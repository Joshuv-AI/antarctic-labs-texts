Abstract
. Combining the tools of geometric analysis with properties of Jordan angles and angle space distributions, we derive a spherical and a Euclidean Bernstein theorem for minimal submanifolds of arbitrary dimension and codimension, under the condition that the Gauss image is contained in some geometrically defined closed region of a Grassmannian manifold. The proof depends on the subharmoncity of an auxiliary function, the Codazzi equations and geometric measure theory.

1.
Introduction

This paper is a part of our systematic approach to the Bernstein problem in higher codimension. The Bernstein problem has a spherical and a Euclidean version, and the two are tightly related and essentially equivalent, as is well known and as we shall explain in a moment in more detail.

The Euclidean version says that a complete
n
-dimensional minimal submanifold
M
of
{\tenmsb R}^{n+m}
, that is, of codimension
m
, has to be an affine subspace if its Gauss image is contained in a sufficiently small subset of the Grassmann manifold
\mathbb{G}_{m,n}
. Equivalently, it is affine when all of its normal spaces
N
satisfies
\langle N,Q_{0}\rangle>c_{0}

for a fixed reference space
Q_{0}
and some positive constant

c_{0}
. In either formulation, we are assuming that the tangent, or
equivalently, the normal spaces do not change their direction too
much when we move across
M
. That some such condition is necessary
follows from an example of Lawson-Osserman
[
14
]
with
\langle N,Q_{0}\rangle=1/9
. And that example tells us, more precisely, that the
condition has to be stricter for
m>1
than in the codimension 1 case, the setting of the classical Bernstein theorem
[
3
]
and its extensions by Fleming
[
7
]
, de Giorgi
[
5
]
, Almgren

[
2
]
, Simons

[
17
]
, Moser
[
15
]
, and others.

The spherical Bernstein theorem concerns compact
(n-1)
-dimensional minimal submanifolds of the sphere
S^{n+m-1}
, and analogously, the aim is to prove that they are totally geodesic (i.e. equatorial) subspheres when their normal planes do not change their directions too much.

As indicated, we are interested here in the case
m>1
, and we ask what the optimal quantitative condition is. In previous work, we have shown

Theorem 1.1
.

[
11
]

Let
M
be an
(n-1)
-dimensional compact minimal submanifold in
S^{n+m-1}
. Suppose that there is a fixed
oriented
m
-plane
Q_{0}
and a number
c_{0}>1/3
, such that
\langle N,Q_{0}\rangle\geq c_{0}
holds for all
normal
m
-planes
N
of
M
. Then
M
is totally geodesic.

Our question here is whether this is optimal, that is, whether there exists a counterexample for
c_{0}=1/3
, or whether one can move beyond. In this paper, we show that the result continues to hold for
c_{0}=1/3
, that is,

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
M
be an
(n-1)
-dimensional compact minimal submanifold in
S^{n+m-1}
. Suppose that there is a fixed
oriented
m
-plane
Q_{0}
, such that
\langle N,Q_{0}\rangle\geq 1/3
holds for all
normal
m
-planes
N
of
M
. Then
M
is totally geodesic.

This might look like a small and insignificant step, but as in many examples of geometric analysis, limiting cases often are much harder than those involving strict inequalities. The reason is that one needs additional tools to analyze possible limit configurations before one can deduce that they can’t exist after all. Typically, in such cases, the analytical estimates need to be supplemented by considerations of a more algebraic nature. That is also the case here. We shall carefully utilize the information contained in the Codazzi equations.
Still, there exists a quantitative gap between the above positive results and the counterexample of Lawson-Osserman, as the latter corresponds to the value
c_{0}=1/9
instead of the
1/3
that we can currently achieve. Nevertheless, we believe that since we can now rule out a counterexample for
c_{0}=1/3
, we expect that one can go even beyond that value. Whether
c_{0}=1/9
is the largest value with a counterexample or whether a counterexample different from the Lawson-Osserman one exists for some value between
1/9
and
1/3
, we currently do not know.

Let us now recall the relation between the spherical and the Euclidean Bernstein problem and then state our results for the latter. Fleming’s idea
[
7
]
was that by rescaling a nontrivial minimal graph in Euclidean space, one obtains a nonflat minimal cone, and the intersection of that cone with the unit sphere then is a compact minimal submanifold of the latter. Therefore, conditions ruling out the latter can be translated into conditions ruling out the former. Because of the noncompact nature of minimal graphs in Euclidean space, as an important technical ingredient, one needs to invoke Allard’s regularity theory.

We then obtain the following result concerning the Euclidean version
of the higher codimensional Bernstein problem (see Theorem
4.2
), which is an improvement of Theorem 1.1 in
[
11
]
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
f:=(f^{1},\cdots,f^{m})
be a smooth
{\tenmsb R}^{m}
-valued function defined everywhere on
{\tenmsb R}^{n}
. If its graph

M:=\text{graph }f=\{(x,f(x)):x\in{\tenmsb R}^{m}\}
is a minimal submanifold in
{\tenmsb R}^{n+m}
, and

(1.1)

\Delta_{f}:=\Big[\det\Big(\delta_{ij}+\sum_{\alpha}\frac{\partial f^{\alpha}}{\partial x^{i}}\frac{\partial f^{\alpha}}{\partial x^{j}}\Big)\Big]^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq 3,

then
f^{1},\cdots,f^{m}
have to be affine linear, that is, represent an affine
n
-plane in
{\tenmsb R}^{n+m}
.

The 3 here corresponds to the
1/3
in the preceding results as will become clear below when we describe the geometry.

In geometric terms, we approach the Bernstein problem via the normal
Gauss map
\gamma
of a minimal submanifold
M
which takes values in the
Grassmannian manifold
\mathbb{G}_{m,n}
. The strategy which was first
applied in
[
9
]
then is to show that
\gamma
is constant. This,
of course, is equivalent to
M
being affine. The main point here is
that this Gauss map is harmonic. Therefore, the strategy then leads
to showing that under appropriate geometric conditions, a harmonic map
into a Grassmannian
\mathbb{G}_{m,n}
has to be constant. This strategy
could be successfully applied in increasing generality, in

[
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
]
. Essentially, one tries to translate a
geometric restriction on the size of the Gauss image
\gamma(M)
into the
subharmonicity of a composition function
\phi\circ\gamma
where

\phi:\gamma(M)\to{\tenmsb R}
is a suitable scalar function, for instance a
strictly convex function. When we are in the spherical setting, the
domain
M
is compact, and so, such a subharmonic function
\phi\circ\gamma
then has to be constant by the maximum principle. One then
utilizes this to deduce that
\gamma
is constant itself. In the Euclidean
setting,
\phi\circ\gamma
has to be subjected to clever and subtle
estimates to eventually reach the same conclusion. As already
mentioned, for the case
c_{0}=1/3
which we are treating in the present
paper, this strategy by itself is not yet powerful enough and needs to
be supplemented by detailed algebraic considerations. In more precise
terms, we consider eigenspaces for Jordan angles between the tangent
or normal space of our minimal submanifold
M
and some fixed reference
space. Invoking a theorem of Nomizu
[
16
]
, we find that,
generically, they yield smooth subbundles of the tangent or normal
bundle of
M
. This will provide us with decompositions that can be
algebraically exploited.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, the basic geometric concepts on Grassmannian manifolds, those of Jordan angles, angle spaces, multiplicities, anti-involutive automorphisms and
angle space distributions, are introduced, which will play an important role in our statement, and the connection between the

w
-function on Grassmannian manifolds and the Jordan angles is revealed. Let
Q_{0}
be a fixed point on
\mathbb{G}_{m,n}
,
then the composition of
w(\cdot,Q_{0})
and the normal Gauss map yields a smooth function on
M
, an arbitrary submanifold
in
{\tenmsb R}^{n+m}
, which is called the
w
-function. Based on
[
6
]
,
[
11
]
and the properties of Jordan angles, one can prove
v:=w^{-1}
is
a subharmonic function whenever
M
has parallel mean curvature and
v\leq 3
. We also explore the second fundamental form of
M
provided that
v\leq 3
,
\Delta v\equiv 0
and
|B|^{2}\neq 0

everywhere, and discover that
M
is a simple austere submanfold, a notion introduced by Harvey-Lawson
[
8
]
and further studied by Bryant
[
4
]
. These are the main points
of Section
3
. Finally in Section
4
, combining the Codazzi equations and basic properties of angle spaces, we derive
a spherical Bernstein theorem, and also the corresponding Euclidean Bernstein theorem.

of

Now, we give a slightly different description for the Jordan angles, which will be useful later.

Denote by

{\tenmsb R}^{n+m}
onto

the orthogonal projection of
{\tenmsb R}^{n+m}

(2.1)

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{P}_{0}u,\varepsilon\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\mathcal{P}_{0}u+(u-\mathcal{P}_{0}u),\varepsilon\rangle=\langle u,\varepsilon\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle u,\mathcal{P}\varepsilon+(\varepsilon-\mathcal{P}\varepsilon)\rangle=\langle u,\mathcal{P}\varepsilon\rangle.

\mathcal{P}
is adjoint to
\mathcal{P}_{0}

(2.2)

\langle(\mathcal{P}\circ\mathcal{P}_{0})u,v\rangle=\langle\mathcal{P}_{0}u,\mathcal{P}_{0}v\rangle=\langle u,(\mathcal{P}\circ\mathcal{P}_{0})v\rangle

For every nonzero

of

\displaystyle=\langle u_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge u_{m},\varepsilon_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\varepsilon_{m}\rangle

\displaystyle=\left|\begin{array}[]{ccc}\langle u_{1},\varepsilon_{1}\rangle&\cdots&\langle u_{1},\varepsilon_{m}\rangle\\
&\cdots&\\
\langle u_{m},\varepsilon_{1}\rangle&\cdots&\langle u_{m},\varepsilon_{m}\rangle\end{array}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\begin{array}[]{ccc}\langle\mathcal{P}_{0}u_{1},\varepsilon_{1}\rangle&\cdots&\langle\mathcal{P}_{0}u_{1},\varepsilon_{m}\rangle\\
&\cdots&\\
\langle\mathcal{P}_{0}u_{m},\varepsilon_{1}\rangle&\cdots&\langle\mathcal{P}_{0}u_{m},\varepsilon_{m}\rangle\end{array}\right|

\displaystyle=\langle\mathcal{P}_{0}u_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathcal{P}_{0}u_{m},\varepsilon_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\varepsilon_{m}\rangle.

As shown above, one can choose an orthonormal basis

,

. Hence

Otherwise, by (

\displaystyle w(P,Q_{0})

\displaystyle=\langle\mathcal{P}_{0}u_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathcal{P}_{0}u_{m},\varepsilon^{\prime}_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\varepsilon^{\prime}_{m}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\cos\theta_{1}\varepsilon^{\prime}_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\cos\theta_{m}\varepsilon^{\prime}_{m},\varepsilon^{\prime}_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\varepsilon^{\prime}_{m}\rangle

\displaystyle=\prod_{\alpha}\cos\theta_{\alpha}\langle\varepsilon^{\prime}_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\varepsilon^{\prime}_{m},\varepsilon^{\prime}_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\varepsilon^{\prime}_{m}\rangle=\prod_{\alpha}\cos\theta_{\alpha};

3.
Subharmonic functions

Let
\bar{M}^{n+m}
be a Riemannian manifold, and
M^{n}\rightarrow\bar{M}^{n+m}
be an isometric immersion.
The second fundamental form
B
is a pointwise symmetric bilinear
form on
T_{p}M
(
p\in M
) with values in
N_{p}M
defined by

B_{XY}=(\overline{\nabla}_{X}Y)^{N}

with
\overline{\nabla}
the Levi-Civita connection on
\bar{M}
. The
induced connections on
TM
and
NM
are defined by

\nabla_{X}Y=(\overline{\nabla}_{X}Y)^{T},\qquad\nabla_{X}\nu=(\overline{\nabla}_{X}\nu)^{N}.

Here
X,Y
are smooth sections of
TM
and
\nu
denotes a smooth
sections of
NM
. The second fundamental form, the curvature tensor
of the submanifold, the curvature tensor of the normal bundle and
the curvature tensor of the ambient manifold satisfy the Gauss
equations, the Codazzi equations and the Ricci equations (see

[
20
]
for details).

The trace of the second fundamental form gives a normal vector field

H
on
M
, which is called the mean curvature vector field. If
\nabla H\equiv 0
, then we say
M
has parallel mean curvature. Moreover if

H\equiv 0
,
M
is called a minimal submanifold in
\bar{M}
.

Now we consider an
n
-dimensional oriented submanifold
M
in
{\tenmsb R}^{n+m}
.
Let
\{e_{1},\cdots,e_{n}\}
be a local orthonormal tangent frame field,

\{\nu_{1},\cdots,\nu_{m}\}
be a local orthonormal normal frame field
on

, and

be the coefficients of the second fundamental form

. We shall use
the summation convention and agree on the ranges of indices

The normal Gauss map

\gamma:M\rightarrow\mathbb{G}_{m,n}

\gamma(p)=N_{p}M\in\mathbb{G}_{m,n}

via parallel translation in
{\tenmsb R}^{n+m}
for every

.
Let
Q_{0}
be a fixed point in
\mathbb{G}_{m,n}
and
define

(3.1)

\displaystyle w:=w(\cdot,Q_{0})\circ\gamma=\langle\nu_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\nu_{m},Q_{0}\rangle.

By the Codazzi equations,

j=1,k=2
in (
4.17
) gives

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{2i}^{n}B_{e_{n}e_{1}}-\Gamma_{i2}^{n}B_{e_{n}e_{1}}-\Gamma_{i1}^{n}B_{e_{2}e_{n}}

\displaystyle=h(\Gamma_{2i}^{n}-\Gamma_{i2}^{n})\nu_{2}+h\Gamma_{i1}^{n}\nu_{1}

and hence
\Gamma_{2i}^{n}-\Gamma_{i2}^{n}=\Gamma_{i1}^{n}=0
. Similarly, choosing
j=2,k=1
yields
\Gamma_{1i}^{n}-\Gamma_{i1}^{n}=\Gamma_{i2}^{n}=0
.
Therefore
\Gamma_{1i}^{n}=\Gamma_{2i}^{n}=\Gamma_{i1}^{n}=\Gamma_{i2}^{n}=0
. Now we put
i=j=1
,
k=2
, then (
4.17
) tells us

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{21}^{n}B_{e_{n}e_{1}}+\Gamma_{21}^{n}B_{e_{1}e_{n}}-\Gamma_{12}^{n}B_{e_{n}e_{1}}-\Gamma_{11}^{n}B_{e_{2}e_{n}}

\displaystyle=h(2\Gamma_{21}^{n}-\Gamma_{12}^{n})\nu_{2}+h\Gamma_{11}^{n}\nu_{1}

and hence
2\Gamma_{21}^{n}-\Gamma_{12}^{n}=\Gamma_{11}^{n}=0
. Similarly, putting
i=j=2
and
k=1
in (
4.17
) yields

2\Gamma_{12}^{n}-\Gamma_{21}^{n}=\Gamma_{22}^{n}=0
. Therefore
\Gamma_{11}^{n}=\Gamma_{12}^{n}=\Gamma_{21}^{n}=\Gamma_{22}^{n}=0
. In summary

(4.18)

\langle\nabla_{e_{i}}e_{j},e_{n}\rangle=\Gamma_{ij}^{n}=0\qquad\forall 1\leq i,j\leq n-1.

Thus

(4.19)

\displaystyle\overline{\nabla}_{e_{1}}e_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq j\leq n-1}\langle\nabla_{e_{1}}e_{n},e_{j}\rangle e_{j}+\langle\nabla_{e_{1}}e_{n},e_{n}\rangle+B_{e_{1}e_{n}}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{1\leq j\leq n-1}\langle\nabla_{e_{1}}e_{j},e_{n}\rangle e_{j}+B_{e_{1}e_{n}}

\displaystyle=h\nu_{2}

and then by (
4.13
),
\nu_{2}
is orthogonal to
Q_{0}
, i.e.
\mathcal{P}_{0}\nu_{2}=0
. But on the other hand,
\nu_{2}\in N_{\theta_{0}}U

implies
|\mathcal{P}_{0}\nu_{2}|=\cos\theta_{0}
, which is a contradiction.

Therefore
U=\emptyset
and
CM_{\varepsilon}
is totally geodesic in
{\tenmsb R}^{n+m}
. Hence
M
has to be a totally geodesic subsphere.

∎

With the results of geometric measure theory, we can also prove a Euclidean Bernstein type theorem.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
f:=(f^{1},\cdots,f^{m})
be a smooth
{\tenmsb R}^{m}
-valued function defined everywhere on
{\tenmsb R}^{n}
. Suppose its graph

M:=\text{graph }f=\{(x,f(x)):x\in{\tenmsb R}^{m}\}
is a minimal submanifold in
{\tenmsb R}^{n+m}
, and

(4.20)

\Delta_{f}:=\Big[\det\Big(\delta_{ij}+\sum_{\alpha}\frac{\partial f^{\alpha}}{\partial x^{i}}\frac{\partial f^{\alpha}}{\partial x^{j}}\Big)\Big]^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq 3,

then
f^{1},\cdots,f^{m}
has to be affine linear, representing an affine
n
-plane in
{\tenmsb R}^{n+m}
.

Proof.

As shown in
[
11
]
, the condition (
4.20
) says

v:=w^{-1}(\cdot,Q_{0})\circ\gamma\leq 3.

Here
\gamma
denotes the normal Gauss map of
M
into
\mathbb{G}_{m,n}
, and
Q_{0}:=\varepsilon_{n+1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\varepsilon_{n+m}
,
where
\varepsilon_{1},\cdots,\varepsilon_{n+m}
denotes the Cartesian coordinate vectors in
{\tenmsb R}^{n+m}
. In other words, the Gauss image
of
M
is contained in a closed region

\overline{{\tenmsb V}}:=\{P\in\mathbb{G}_{m,n}:w(P,Q_{0})\geq 1/3\}.

Now we consider the tangent cone of
M
at
\infty
, which is the limit of a one-parameter family of minimal submanifolds
in
{\tenmsb R}^{n+m}
; each one is obtained by a contracting procedure. More precisely, let

(4.21)

f_{t}=\frac{1}{t}f(tx),\qquad\forall t\in{\tenmsb R}^{+},

then it is easy to check that
f_{t}
satisfies the minimal surface equations and hence
\{M_{t}=\text{graph }f_{t}:t\in{\tenmsb R}^{+}\}

defines a family of minimal submanifolds in
{\tenmsb R}^{n+m}
. Based on (
4.20
), one can proceed as in
[
6
]
§5 to show that

\{f_{t}:t\in{\tenmsb R}^{+}\}
is an equicontinuous family on any compact subset of
{\tenmsb R}^{n}
. Thus the Arzela-Ascoli Theorem implies that
there exists a subsequence
\{f_{t_{i}}:i\in{\tenmsb Z}^{+}\}
such that
\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}t_{i}=+\infty
and

\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}f_{t_{i}}=h
, which is a Lipschitz function. One can
prove that
h
is a weak solution to the minimal surface equations
and its graph
C(M,\infty):=\{(x,h(x)):x\in{\tenmsb R}^{n}\}
is a cone (see
[
13
]
), which is called the tangent cone of
M
at

\infty
. In the framework of geometric measure theory, we can prove
that
C(M,\infty)
is regular except at
0
, as in
[
6
]
.
Hence the intersection of
C(M,\infty)
and the unit sphere gives an
(n-1)
-dimensional embedded minimal submanifold in

S^{n+m-1}
, which is denoted by
M^{\prime}
. The image of the normal Gauss map of
M^{\prime}
is still contained in
\overline{{\tenmsb V}}
,
i.e.
\langle N,Q_{0}\rangle\geq 1/3
for all normal
m
-planes of
M^{\prime}
(the proof is the same as in
[
20
]
§7.3). By Theorem

4.1
,
M^{\prime}
has to be a totally geodesic subsphere. Then Allard’s regularity estimate
[
1
]
implies
f
is affine linear
and
M
is an affine
n
-plane.
∎
