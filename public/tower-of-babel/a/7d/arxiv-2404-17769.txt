Two-stage Risk Control with Application to Ranked Retrieval
Yunpeng Xu, Mufang Ying ∗, Wenge Guo, Zhi Wei

Abstract
Practical machine learning systems often operate in multiple sequential stages, as seen in ranking and recommendation systems, which typically include a retrieval phase followed by a ranking phase. Effectively assessing prediction uncertainty and ensuring effective risk control in such systems pose significant challenges due to their inherent complexity. To address these challenges, we developed two-stage risk control methods based on the recently proposed learn-then-test (LTT) and conformal risk control (CRC) frameworks. Unlike the methods in prior work that address multiple risks, our approach leverages the sequential nature of the problem, resulting in reduced computational burden. We provide theoretical guarantees for our proposed methods and design novel loss functions tailored for ranked retrieval tasks. The effectiveness of our approach is validated through experiments on two large-scale, widely-used datasets: MSLR-Web and Yahoo LTRC.



1
Introduction

As machine learning models become more integrated into our daily lives, the need for transparency and reliability in their predictions is crucial. Moving beyond black-box approaches, the ability to understand and quantify uncertainty in these models is increasingly important to ensure their effectiveness in real-world applications. Conformal prediction, a distribution-free and statistically valid approach that is straightforward to integrate with existing models, has emerged as a promising solution for quantifying uncertainty in machine learning
(
Vovk et al., 2005
)
. Its primary objective is to generate uncertainty sets for model predictions while ensuring a specified coverage level. Recently, the conformal risk control framework
(
Angelopoulos et al., 2024
)
has expanded upon traditional miscoverage control by enabling control over the expected value of any loss function. This extension greatly enhances its applicability across a wider range of contexts.

Most existing research on conformal prediction focuses on a single-stage process, where the machine learning system processes the input and generates the prediction in a single step. However, this assumption does not hold for many real-world systems, which often involve two or more concatenated stages. One notable example is ranked retrieval systems, such as search engines, where the task is to retrieve and rank documents based on their relevance to a user’s query.
These systems typically involve two sequential stages: (1) the retrieval stage, which identifies a set of candidate documents from a large repository, and (2) the ranking stage, which refines and orders these candidates to produce the final ranked list presented to the user
(
Yin and et al, 2016
;
Khattab et al., 2020
)
. This two-stage approach is necessary because the massive volume of documents often exceeds the capacity of a single-stage ranking model, particularly when employing computationally intensive methods. In such two-stage problems, each stage is designed with distinct optimization objectives, and errors from one stage can propagate to the next. Consequently, the two-stage process introduces additional complexity, making it more challenging to accurately quantify and control uncertainty.

To address these challenges, we propose
two-stage
conformal prediction methods to quantify and control the uncertainty inherent in such problems. Specifically, we apply the learn-then-test framework
(
Angelopoulos et al., 2021a
)
and extend the recently developed single-stage conformal risk control framework
(
Angelopoulos et al., 2024
)
to a two-stage setup, where each stage has its own distinct risk control requirement. Risk control is achieved by identifying parameters that jointly satisfy the risk constraints for both stages. Furthermore, to address the specific purpose of the two stages in ranked retrieval problems, we introduce the
retrieval risk
and the
ranking risk
, respectively, and then apply our proposed two-stage risk control methods to derive their corresponding prediction sets while controlling both risks at the pre-specified levels. Our proposed methods are model-agnostic and can be seamlessly integrated into existing ranked retrieval systems.

Related work

Conformal prediction

Conformal prediction, originally developed by Vovk and collaborators, has recently emerged as a prominent method for uncertainty quantification in statistical machine learning
(
Vovk et al., 1999
;
Papadopoulos et al., 2002
;
Vovk et al., 2005
;
Lei et al., 2015
)
. A recent survey by Angelopoulos and Bates outlines the significance and wide applications of the topic
(

)
. Our work builds upon the learn-then-test framework
(

)
and the recently developed conformal risk control (CRC) framework

. The application discussed in our work shares similarities with

)
, which uses the LTT technique

(

)
differ in both objectives and methodologies.

Ranked retrieval

Ranked retrieval has been extensively studied, with models evolving from traditional IR approaches like BM25
(
Baeza-Yates and Ribeiro-Neto, 1999
;
Stephen and K., 1976
)
to modern learning-to-rank algorithms
(

)

Problem setup

Formally, in the first stage, consider an i.i.d collection of non-increasing, right-continuous random functions
L^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}_{i}:\Lambda\to[0,1]
,
i=1,\ldots,n+1
, representing the associated losses. We denote by
\lambda
the tuning parameter in this stage. In the second stage, we consider another i.i.d collection of random functions,
L^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}_{i}:\Lambda\times\Gamma\to[0,1]
,
i=1,\ldots,n+1
, to represent the associated losses in the second stage, incorporating an additional tuning parameter
\gamma
. Here,
L^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}_{i}(\lambda,\gamma)
is assumed to be non-increasing and right continuous in each coordinate. Furthermore, the following conditions hold:
L^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}_{i}(0)=1
,
L^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}_{i}(1)=0
,
L^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}_{i}(0,0)=1
, and
L^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}_{i}(1,1)=0
. We use
R^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}(\lambda)
and
R^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}(\lambda,\gamma)
to denote the expected risk functions at each stage with fixed tuning parameter
\lambda
and
\gamma
, i.e.,
R^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}(\lambda)={\mathbb{E}}L^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}_{n+1}(\lambda)
and
R^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}(\lambda,\gamma)={\mathbb{E}}L^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}_{n+1}(\lambda,\gamma)
, and use
\widehat{R}_{n}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}(\lambda)
and
\widehat{R}_{n}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}(\lambda,\gamma)
to denote the empirical risk functions, i.e.,
\widehat{R}_{n}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}L^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}_{i}(\lambda)
and
\widehat{R}_{n}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}(\lambda,\gamma)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}L^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}_{i}(\lambda,\gamma)
. Without loss of generality, we assume that the parameter
\lambda
is chosen from a finite set
\Lambda=\left\{\lambda_{i}:i\in[m]\right\}
, and the parameter
\gamma
is chosen from a finite set
\Gamma=\left\{\gamma_{i}:i\in[m]\right\}
. We assume the values are ordered such that
0\leq\lambda_{1}<

\lambda_{2}<\cdots<\lambda_{m}\leq 1
and
0\leq\gamma_{1}<\gamma_{2}<\cdots<\gamma_{m}\leq 1
.

For pre-specified risk levels
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}
, we aim to utilize random functions
\{L^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}_{i}(\lambda)\}_{i=1}^{n}
and
\{L^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}_{i}(\lambda,\gamma)\}_{i=1}^{n}
to identify data-dependent tuning parameter pairs
(\lambda,\gamma)
that satisfies the expected risk control guarantee

{\mathbb{E}}R^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}(\lambda)\leq\alpha_{1}\quad\text{and}\quad{\mathbb{E}}R^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}(\lambda,\gamma)\leq\alpha_{2}.

(1)

When there exists a set of feasible tuning parameter pairs,
\mathcal{R}
, that satisfy (
1
), we are also interested in a uniform expected risk control guarantee

\begin{split}&{\mathbb{E}}\sup_{(\lambda,\gamma)\in\mathcal{R}}R^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}(\lambda)\leq\alpha_{1},\quad\text{and}\\
&{\mathbb{E}}\sup_{(\lambda,\gamma)\in\mathcal{R}}R^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}(\lambda,\gamma)\leq\alpha_{2},\end{split}

(2)

as this would allow us to select tuning parameter pair from
\mathcal{R}
based on certain objective function with valid expected risk control guarantee. Note that the selection of
\lambda
and
\gamma
encodes the prediction set sizes. Throughout the paper, we assume
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}
are fixed to be in the interval
[0,1]
, which implies feasible risk control.

With regard to two-stage risk control, one might wonder: if we can manage risk effectively in the second stage, why invest effort in controlling it in the first stage? The reason is that controlling risk in the first stage is foundational to the entire process. In the case of the ranked retrieval problem, retrieving all documents in the first stage imposes a significant computational burden on the ranking stage. Conversely, retrieving too few relevant documents in the first stage undermines the feasibility of second-stage risk control and compromises ranking quality. Thus, this paper focuses on simultaneously controlling risks at both stages.

2.1
Data structure in ranked retrieval problem

Before discussing how to achieve risk control as specified in equations (
1
) and (
2
), we first outline the structure of the data for the ranked retrieval problem. Consider a set of i.i.d calibration data points
\{(X_{i},Y_{i},Z_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
, with
(X_{i},Y_{i},Z_{i})\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Z}
, where
Y_{i}
and
Z_{i}
are the labeled responses of feature vector
X_{i}
corresponding to the two stages respectively. In the ranked retrieval problem,
X_{i}
represents a query along with its associated candidate documents. We let
X_{i}=\{q_{i},\{d_{i,j}\}_{j=1}^{N_{i}}\}
, where
q_{i}
denotes the user query and
\{d_{i,j}\}_{j=1}^{N_{i}}
denotes its associated documents with size
N_{i}
.
Each pair of
q_{i}
and
d_{i,j}
is associated with a ground truth relevance score
r_{i,j}\in\{0,1,\ldots,R\}
, where a higher value of
r_{i.j}
indicates a higher relevance of
d_{i,j}
to
q_{i}
. This score is only observable for the training data and is hidden for the test data. Here we denote the relevant documents with a relevance score great than
0
in the retrieval results of
q_{i}
by
Y_{i}=\{d_{i,j}:r_{i,j}>0\}
. In the ranking stage, with a focus on the ranking quality of documents with a ground truth relevance level
r_{0}\in[R]
or above, the set of
r_{0}
-relevant documents for
q_{i}
:
\{d_{i,j}:r_{i,j}\geq r_{0}\}
is considered. Then,
Z_{i}
denotes the ordered set of the
r_{0}
-relevant documents, sorted in descending order based on the ground truth relevance scores with ties broken arbitrarily:
Z_{i}=\{d_{i,(1)},d_{i,(2)},\ldots\}
.

Without loss of generality, we assume that each stage is associated with a model learned on the training data for all queries, denoted by
\mathcal{M}_{\text{retrieval}}
for the retrieval model and by
\mathcal{M}_{\text{rank}}
for the ranking model, respectively. Note that the form of the retrieval model is flexible; it can range from a simple Okapi BM25 model, which counts word occurrences, to a more complex large language model that generates embeddings for embedding-based retrieval. Typically, the model used in the retrieval stage is more efficient but less powerful than the one in the ranking stage.
For both stages, by leveraging the pre-trained model and calibration data, we can construct prediction sets
\hat{\mathcal{C}}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}(x;\lambda)
for the unknown response
y\in\mathcal{Y}
and
\hat{\mathcal{C}}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}(x;\lambda,\gamma)
for the unknown response
z\in\mathcal{Z}
, given a test data point
(x,y,z)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Z}
. By employing the loss functions
l^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}
and
l^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}
for the first and second stages, respectively, we obtain:

\displaystyle L^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}_{i}(\lambda)=l^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}(\hat{\mathcal{C}}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}(X_{i};\lambda),Y_{i}),\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle L^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}_{i}(\lambda,\gamma)=l^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}(\hat{\mathcal{C}}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}(X_{i};\lambda,\gamma),Z_{i}).

We will specify the choice of
\hat{\mathcal{C}}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}(\cdot;\lambda)
,
\hat{\mathcal{C}}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}(\cdot;\lambda,\gamma)
,
l^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}
, and
l^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}
for ranked retrieval problem in Section
4
.

3
Two-stage risk control

In this section, we discuss two approaches to achieve expected risk control guarantee. The first approach leverages the LTT framework
(
Angelopoulos et al., 2021b
)
, as high-probability risk control provides a stronger guarantee compared to expected risk control. The second approach extends the conformal risk control framework
(
Angelopoulos et al., 2024
)
to accommodate the two-stage setup.

3.1
LTT framework

In the first stage, the value of
\lambda
is determined by evaluating its risk function
R^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}(\lambda)
through the following hypothesis tests for each
\lambda_{i}\in\Lambda
:

\mathcal{H}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}_{i}:R^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}(\lambda_{i})>\alpha_{1}~~\text{ vs. }~~\mathcal{H^{\prime}}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}_{i}:R^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}(\lambda_{i})\leq\alpha_{1}.

(3)

Given
\lambda=\lambda_{i}\in\Lambda
, the value of
\gamma
is selected in the second stage by using the corresponding risk function
R^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}(\lambda_{i},\gamma)
. For each
\gamma_{j}\in\Gamma
, the following hypothesis tests are performed:

\mathcal{H}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}_{i,j}:R^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}(\lambda_{i},\gamma_{j})>\alpha_{2}~~\text{ vs. }~~\mathcal{H^{\prime}}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}_{i,j}:R^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}(\lambda_{i},\gamma_{j})\leq\alpha_{2}.

(4)

Let
\mathcal{F}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}=\left\{\mathcal{H}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}_{i}:i=1,\ldots,m\right\}
denote the collection of all hypotheses tested in the first stage. Given
\lambda=\lambda_{i}\in\Lambda
, let
\mathcal{F}_{i}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}=\left\{\mathcal{H}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}_{i,j}:j=1,\ldots,m\right\}
denote the collection of all hypotheses tested in the second stage for a fixed
\lambda_{i}
. Finally, let
\mathcal{F}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}\mathcal{F}_{i}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}
represent the complete collection of all hypotheses tested in the second stage. To conduct hypothesis testing across both stages, we aim to control the global family-wise error rate (FWER), defined as the probability of making at least one Type I error across the two families
\mathcal{F}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}
and
\mathcal{F}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}
.
For each individual hypothesis
\mathcal{H}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}_{i}\in\mathcal{F}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}
in the first stage and
\mathcal{H}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}_{i,j}\in\mathcal{F}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}
in the second stage, we use the Hoeffding-Bentkus inequality to compute
p
-values
p_{i}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}
and
p_{i,j}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}
, as introduced in
(
Angelopoulos et al., 2021a
)
. These
p
-values are valid under their respective null hypotheses and are defined as follows:

\displaystyle p_{i}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}=

\displaystyle\min(\exp\{-nh(\widehat{R}_{n}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}(\lambda_{i})\wedge\alpha_{1},\alpha_{1})\},

\displaystyle\qquad e\mathbb{P}(\operatorname{Bin}(n,\alpha_{1})\leq\lceil n\widehat{R}_{n}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}(\lambda_{i})\rceil)),

\displaystyle p_{i,j}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}=

\displaystyle\min(\exp\{-nh(\widehat{R}_{n}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}(\lambda_{i},\gamma_{j})\wedge\alpha_{2},\alpha_{2})\},

\displaystyle\qquad e\mathbb{P}(\operatorname{Bin}(n,\alpha_{2})\leq\lceil n\widehat{R}_{n}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}(\lambda_{i},\gamma_{j})\rceil)),

where the function
h(a,b)
is given by:

h(a,b)=a\log\left(\frac{a}{b}\right)+(1-a)\log\left(\frac{1-a}{1-b}\right).

Graphical ordering of hypothesis tests

To sequentially test the families
\mathcal{F}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}
and
\mathcal{F}_{i}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}
for each
i\in[1,m]
, one can employ the FWER controlling algorithm to obtain the tuning parameter pairs
(
Angelopoulos et al., 2021a
)
. Given that the loss function in the first stage,
L^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}_{i}(\lambda)
, is non-increasing in
\lambda
, and the loss function in the second stage,
L^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}_{i}(\lambda,\gamma)
, is non-increasing in both
\lambda
and
\gamma
, the fixed-sequence procedure tests the hypotheses in these families in reverse order. Specifically, for
\mathcal{F}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}
, the
m
hypotheses are tested sequentially in the order
\mathcal{H}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}_{m},\mathcal{H}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}_{m-1},\ldots,\mathcal{H}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}_{1}
. Similarly, for
\mathcal{F}_{i}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}
, the hypotheses are tested sequentially in the order
\mathcal{H}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}_{i,m},\mathcal{H}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}_{i,m-1},\ldots,\mathcal{H}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}_{i,1}
. Building on these considerations, we propose the following procedure for simultaneously testing
\mathcal{F}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}\cup\mathcal{F}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}
, ensuring control of the global FWER at the pre-specified level
\delta
:

Procedure:

1.

Test
\mathcal{F}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}
using the Bonferroni procedure:

Apply the Bonferroni procedure to the
p
-values
p_{i}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}
to simultaneously test
\mathcal{F}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}
at level
\delta
. Let
\mathcal{R}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}
denote the index set of the rejected hypotheses:

\mathcal{R}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}=\left\{i\in[m]:p_{i}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}\leq\delta/m\right\}.

2.

Test
\mathcal{F}_{i}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}
using the fixed-sequence procedure:

For each
i\in\mathcal{R}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}
, use the fixed-sequence procedure on the
p
-values
p_{i,j}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}
to simultaneously test
\mathcal{F}_{i}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}
at level
\delta/m
. Let
\mathcal{R}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}_{i}
denote the index set of the rejected hypotheses:

\mathcal{R}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}_{i}=\left\{j\in[m]:p^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}_{i,j^{\prime}}\leq\delta/m\quad\forall j^{\prime}\in[j,m]\right\}.

3.

Determine the final set of rejected hypothesis pairs:

The set
\mathcal{R}
of tuning parameter pairs that correspond to the rejected pairs of hypotheses
\left(\mathcal{H}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}_{i},\mathcal{H}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}_{i,j}\right)
is:

\mathcal{R}=\left\{(\lambda_{i},\gamma_{j}):i\in\mathcal{R}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}},j\in\mathcal{R}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}_{i}\right\}.

The procedure described above can be viewed as a special case of the sequential graphical approach for multiple testing
(
Bretz et al., 2009
;
Angelopoulos et al., 2021a
)
. Consequently, it strongly controls the global FWER at the pre-specified level
\delta
. We note that some other alternative global FWER-controlling procedures can be applied; a few examples are provided in the appendix. However, determining optimal procedure for a specific setting remains an open problem. In the following, we present the expected risk control guarantee for general FWER-controlling procedure.

Theorem 1

Let
\mathcal{R}
denote the collection of tuning parameter pairs returned from a FWER controlling algorithm testing
\mathcal{F}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}\cup\mathcal{F}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}
at level
\delta
. Then, we have

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\forall(\lambda,\gamma)\in\mathcal{R}:R^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}(\lambda)\leq\alpha_{1},R^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}(\lambda,\gamma)\leq\alpha_{2}\right)\geq 1-\delta.

(5)

Therefore, we have

\begin{split}&{\mathbb{E}}\sup_{(\lambda,\gamma)\in\mathcal{R}}R^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}(\lambda)\leq\alpha_{1}+\delta,\quad\text{and}\\
&{\mathbb{E}}\sup_{(\lambda,\gamma)\in\mathcal{R}}R^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}(\lambda,\gamma)\leq\alpha_{2}+\delta.\end{split}

(6)

A few comments are in order regarding the comparison with multiple risks in
Angelopoulos et al. (2021a)
. The authors therein discuss a similar setting involving multiple risk functions. Specifically, consider the case where the sets of tuning parameters have size
m
, i.e.,
|\Lambda|=|\Gamma|=m
. Their procedure uses the set of all parameter pairs
\{(\lambda,\gamma):\lambda\in\Lambda,\gamma\in\Gamma\}
, as input for running FWER controlling algorithm. In contrast, our approach leverages the sequential nature of the problem and the monotonicity of the risk functions, which reduces the computational burden and enhances overall effectiveness.

3.2
Expected risk control

In this section, we describe methods that leverage conformal risk control framework. To proceed, we consider
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in(\frac{1}{n+1},1]
and introduce a few additional notations. For the purpose of risk control in the first stage, define:

\hat{\lambda}_{0}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}=\inf\left\{\lambda\in\Lambda:\sum_{i=1}^{n}L^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}_{i}(\lambda)\leq(n+1)\alpha_{1}-1\right\}.

(7)

Similarly, to ensure the feasibility of risk control in the second stage, define:

\hat{\lambda}_{0}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}=\inf\left\{\lambda\in\Lambda:\sum_{i=1}^{n}L^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}_{i}(\lambda,1)\leq(n+1)\alpha_{2}-1\right\}.

(8)

Lastly, for any fixed
\lambda\in\Lambda
, we define:

\hat{\gamma}_{0}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}(\lambda)=\inf\left\{\gamma\in\Gamma:\sum_{i=1}^{n}L^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}_{i}(\lambda,\gamma)\leq(n+1)\alpha_{2}-1\right\}.

(9)

When the set in equation (
9
) is empty, we set
\hat{\gamma}_{0}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}(\lambda)=1
.
To ensure risk control in both stages, we set the value of
\lambda
as a linear combination of
\hat{\lambda}_{0}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}\vee\hat{\lambda}_{0}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}
and 1:

\hat{\lambda}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}(t):=\left\lceil t(\hat{\lambda}_{0}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}\vee\hat{\lambda}_{0}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}})+(1-t)\right\rceil_{\Lambda}.

(10)

Here
\hat{\lambda}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}(t)
is defined to be the ceiling of
t(\hat{\lambda}_{0}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}\vee\hat{\lambda}_{0}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}})+(1-t)
, restricted to values in
\Lambda
, and
t\in[0,1]
is a tuning parameter.
This formulation ensures that
\hat{\lambda}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}(t)\geq\hat{\lambda}_{0}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}\vee\hat{\lambda}_{0}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}
for any
t
. Using
\hat{\lambda}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}(t)
, we determine the value of
\gamma
by defining

\hat{\gamma}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}(t):=\hat{\gamma}_{0}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}\left(\hat{\lambda}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}(t)\right).

(11)

With the above definitions, we are ready to state our result for expected risk control.

Theorem 2

For any
t\in

[0,1]
, tuning parameter pair
(\hat{\lambda}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}(t),\hat{\gamma}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}(t))
achieves first-stage risk control at level
\alpha_{1}
with a finite-sample guarantee and achieves second-stage risk control at level
\alpha_{2}
asymptotically, i.e.,

\begin{split}&{\mathbb{E}}R^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}(\hat{\lambda}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}(t))\leq\alpha_{1},\quad\text{and}\\
&\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{E}}R^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}(\hat{\lambda}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}(t),\hat{\gamma}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}(t))\leq\alpha_{2}.\end{split}

Since
\Lambda
and
\Gamma
are finite sets, we further have the following corollary.

Corollary 1

Uniform risk control for both stages can be achieved in the set

\mathcal{R}=\left\{(\lambda,\gamma)\in\Lambda\times\Gamma:\lambda=\hat{\lambda}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}(t),\gamma\geq\hat{\gamma}_{0}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}(\lambda),t\in[0,1]\right\},

i.e.,

\begin{split}&{\mathbb{E}}\sup_{(\lambda,\gamma)\in\mathcal{R}}R^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}(\lambda)\leq\alpha_{1},\quad\text{and}\\
&\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{E}}\sup_{(\lambda,\gamma)\in\mathcal{R}}R^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}(\lambda,\gamma)\leq\alpha_{2}.\end{split}

3.2.1
Finite-sample second-stage risk control

To ensure finite-sample guarantee for the second stage, a data-splitting approach can be employed. Let calibration data be divided into two non-overlapping parts with index sets
\mathcal{I}_{1}
and
\mathcal{I}_{2}
. Let
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in(1/(1+|\mathcal{I}_{1}|),1]
. Define

\displaystyle\tilde{\lambda}_{0}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}=\inf\left\{\lambda\in\Lambda:\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{1}}L^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}_{i}(\lambda)\leq(|\mathcal{I}_{1}|+1)\alpha_{1}-1\right\}.

To proceed, we need to impose the following additional assumption to ensure finite-sample risk control in the second stage:

Assumption 1

There exists a known constant
\lambda_{0}\leq 1
such that
L^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}_{i}(\lambda_{0},1)\leq\alpha_{2}
for
i\in[n+1]
.

The above Assumption
1
enables feasibility of finite-sample second-stage risk control. Next, for
\lambda\in\Lambda\cap[\lambda_{0},1]
define

\tilde{\gamma}_{0}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}(\lambda)=\inf\left\{\gamma\in\Gamma:\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{2}}L^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}_{i}(\lambda,\gamma)\leq(|\mathcal{I}_{2}|+1)\alpha_{2}-1\right\}.

We define
\tilde{\gamma}_{0}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}(\lambda)=1
when the set is empty. For
t\in[0,1]
, we then define:

\begin{split}&\tilde{\lambda}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}(t):=\left\lceil t(\tilde{\lambda}_{0}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}\vee\lambda_{0})+(1-t)\right\rceil_{\Lambda},\quad\text{and}\\
&\tilde{\gamma}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}(t)=\tilde{\gamma}_{0}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}(\tilde{\lambda}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}(t)).\end{split}

(12)

Theorem 3

For any
t\in

[0,1]
, tuning parameter pair
(\tilde{\lambda}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}(t),\tilde{\gamma}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}(t))
achieves finite-sample first-stage and second-stage risk control at level
\alpha_{1}
,
\alpha_{2}
, respectively, i.e.,

{\mathbb{E}}R^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}(\hat{\lambda}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}(t))\leq\alpha_{1}\quad\text{and}\quad{\mathbb{E}}R^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}(\hat{\lambda}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}(t),\hat{\gamma}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}(t))\leq\alpha_{2}.

Note that for any
t\in[0,1]
, we have
{\mathbb{E}}R^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}(\hat{\lambda}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}(t),\hat{\gamma}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}(t))\leq\alpha_{2}
. However, there is no guarantee that

{\mathbb{E}}\sup_{t\in[0,1]}R^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}(\hat{\lambda}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}(t),\hat{\gamma}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}(t))\leq\alpha_{2},

as no monotonic relationship exists. Therefore, to obtain a finite-sample uniform risk control in the second stage, we define

\bar{\gamma}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}=\inf\left\{\gamma\in\Gamma:\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{2}}L^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}_{i}(\tilde{\lambda}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}(1),\gamma)\leq(|\mathcal{I}_{2}|+1)\alpha_{2}-1\right\}.

Corollary 2

Uniform risk control for both stages can be achieved in the set

\mathcal{R}=\left\{(\lambda,\gamma)\in\Lambda\times\Gamma:\lambda=\tilde{\lambda}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}(t),\gamma\geq\bar{\gamma}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}},t\in[0,1]\right\},

i.e.,

{\mathbb{E}}\sup_{(\lambda,\gamma)\in\mathcal{R}}R^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}(\lambda)\leq\alpha_{1}\quad\text{and}\quad{\mathbb{E}}\sup_{(\lambda,\gamma)\in\mathcal{R}}R^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}(\lambda,\gamma)\leq\alpha_{2}.

We remark that our data-splitting approach, which ensures a uniform finite-sample risk control guarantee, can be regarded as a special case of the method proposed in Section 4.3 of
Angelopoulos et al. (2024)
.

4
Application to ranked retrieval

In this section, we apply our proposed methods to ranked retrieval problem. We begin by introducing the loss functions defined for each stage.

4.1
Loss function for retrieval stage

As defined in the Section
2
,
Y_{i}
is the set of relevant documents with respect to the query
q_{i}
, i.e., the set of documents with ground truth relevance scores greater than
0
. To represent the set of documents fetched by
\mathcal{M}_{\text{retrieval}}
used in the retrieval stage, we define the retrieved document set
\hat{\mathcal{C}}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}(X_{i};\lambda)
for the retrieval stage as:

\hat{\mathcal{C}}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}(X_{i};\lambda)=\{d_{i,j}:\mathcal{M}_{\text{retrieval}}(q_{i},d_{i,j})\geq 1-\lambda\},

(13)

where
\lambda\in[0,1]
and
\mathcal{M}_{\text{retrieval}}(q_{i},d_{i,j})
denotes the model score for the query-document pair
(q_{i},d_{i,j})
, as computed by the retrieval model
\mathcal{M}_{\text{retrieval}}
. Note that a good retrieved document set
\hat{\mathcal{C}}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}(X_{i};\lambda)
associated with the query
q_{i}
should aim to cover as many relevant documents in
Y_{i}
as possible. To measure the miscoverage of
Y_{i}
by
\hat{\mathcal{C}}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}(X_{i};\lambda)
, we define retrieval loss as:

L^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}_{i}(\lambda)=1-\frac{|Y_{i}\cap\hat{\mathcal{C}}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}(X_{i};\lambda)|}{|Y_{i}|}.

(14)

Note that the loss function defined in equation (
14
) is non-increasing, right-continuous in
\lambda
, and bounded within
[0,1]
. This form of loss function quantifies the missed fraction of relevant documents retrieved by model
\mathcal{M}_{\text{retrieval}}
. By choosing an appropriate
\lambda
, we aim to control the risk at level
\alpha_{1}
.

4.2
Loss function for ranking stage

Similarly, in the ranking stage, we first define the prediction set
\hat{\mathcal{C}}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}(X_{i};\lambda,\gamma)
as:

\hat{\mathcal{C}}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}(X_{i};\lambda,\gamma)=\{d_{i,j}:\mathcal{M}_{\text{rank}}(q_{i},d_{i,j})\geq 1-\gamma\}\cap\hat{\mathcal{C}}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}(X_{i};\lambda).

(15)

In contexts like information retrieval, search engines, and recommendation systems, nDCG
(
Järvelin and Kekäläinen, 2000
)
is widely used to evaluate the ranking quality of algorithms or systems. Motivated by the intuition from nDCG that a ranked list can be evaluated by rewarding relevance while considering ranking positions, we slightly modify the definition of nDCG to suit our setting, and define:

\text{DCG}_{\mathrm{mod}}\left(\hat{\mathcal{C}}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}(X_{i};\lambda,\gamma),Z_{i}\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{|Z_{i}|}\frac{\mathbf{1}_{\{d_{i,{(j)}}\in\hat{\mathcal{C}}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}(X_{i};\lambda,\gamma)\}}}{\log(j+1)}.

Notably, when
\hat{\mathcal{C}}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}(X_{i};\lambda,\gamma)
contains all the
r_{0}
-relevant documents,
\text{DCG}_{\mathrm{mod}}
attains its maximum value. To normalize the
\text{DCG}_{\mathrm{mod}}
, we define the modified Ideal Discounted Cumulative Gain (
\text{iDCG}_{\mathrm{mod}}
) for query
q_{i}
as:

\text{iDCG}_{\mathrm{mod}}\left(Z_{i}\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{|Z_{i}|}\frac{1}{\log(j+1)}.

Correspondingly, we define the modified Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain (
\text{nDCG}_{\mathrm{mod}}
) as

\text{nDCG}_{\mathrm{mod}}\left(\hat{\mathcal{C}}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}(X_{i};\lambda,\gamma),Z_{i}\right)=\frac{\text{DCG}_{\mathrm{mod}}\left(\hat{\mathcal{C}}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}(X_{i};\lambda,\gamma),Z_{i}\right)}{\text{iDCG}_{\mathrm{mod}}(Z_{i})}.

The loss function for the ranking stage is then defined as

L^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}_{i}(\lambda,\gamma)=1-\text{nDCG}_{\mathrm{mod}}(\hat{\mathcal{C}}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}(X_{i};\lambda,\gamma),Z_{i}).

(16)

The proposed loss function addresses the ranking order within the ground truth set
Z_{i}
. When selecting an appropriate
\gamma
that satisfies risk control guarantee, greater weight is assigned to documents in
Z_{i}
with higher ranking positions, ensuring that the most relevant documents are prioritized for inclusion in the prediction set. Note that, given
\lambda
specified in the first stage, the ranking loss function is non-increasing, right-continuous in
\gamma
, and bounded within
[0,1]
. Our goal is to determine
\gamma
to control the ranking loss at specified level
\alpha_{2}
.

4.3
Parameter pair selection via empirical set sizes minimization

Given a collection of tuning parameter pairs that achieve risk control at both stages, we determine the tuning parameter pair
(\hat{\lambda},\hat{\gamma})
through optimization with a objective function
\mathcal{L}
:

(\hat{\lambda},\hat{\gamma})=\argmin_{(\lambda,\gamma)\in\mathcal{R}}\mathcal{L}\left(\lambda,\gamma;\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\right).

(17)

In many applications (recommendation in mobile devices etc.), it is desirable to produce a smaller prediction set in the second stage. Therefore, in this paper, we consider objective function

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\left(\lambda,\gamma;\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\right)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\hat{\mathcal{C}}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}(X_{i};\lambda,\gamma)\}|.

We comment that, the choice of the loss function should be driven by the specific problem at hand. For instance, in scenarios where the objective is to reduce computational cost during the ranking stage, a smaller prediction set size in the retrieval stage reduces the number of documents evaluated by the computationally intensive ranking model. To address this, the term
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\hat{\mathcal{C}}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}(X_{i};\lambda)|
can be incorporated into the loss function
\mathcal{L}
, thereby systematically mitigating the computational burden.

5
Conclusion & Discussion

In this paper, we study expected risk control under a two-stage setup. We propose methods aimed at simultaneously controlling risk, and establish theoretical guarantees for these methods. The effectiveness of our approach is validated through experiments on two large-scale, widely used datasets: MSLR-Web and Yahoo LTRC. Below, we discuss several extensions of the current framework.

Non-monotone risk function

When risk function is non-monotone, the following monotonization procedure can be applied. At the first stage, the loss function is modified as follows:

\tilde{L}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}_{i}(\lambda)=\sup_{\lambda^{\prime}\in[\lambda,1]\cap\Lambda}L^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}_{i}(\lambda^{\prime}).

At the second stage, we define the modified loss function as

\tilde{L}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}_{i}(\lambda,\gamma)=\sup_{\small\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda^{\prime}\in[\lambda,1]\cap\Lambda,\\
\gamma^{\prime}\in[\gamma,1]\cap\Gamma\end{subarray}}L^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}_{i}(\lambda^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime}).

With these modifications to the loss functions, the results from the previous sections can be directly applied.

Multiple-stages

We extend the problem to the scenario where there are
K>2
stages. Let
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
denote the parameter of interest. At the
k
-th stage, the loss function for sample
i
is denoted as
L^{\scalebox{0.6}{(k)}}_{i}(\theta_{1:k})
, where
\theta_{1:k}
is shorthand for
(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{k})
. The sequential nature of the problem determines that loss function at stage
k
only involves parameters
\theta_{1:k}
. The goal is to determine parameter set
\theta
that satisfies

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\forall k\in[K],L^{\scalebox{0.6}{(k)}}_{i}(\theta_{1:k})\leq\alpha_{k},\right)\geq 1-\delta,

or alternatively,

{\mathbb{E}}L^{\scalebox{0.6}{(k)}}_{i}(\theta_{1:k})\leq\alpha_{k}\quad\text{for all $k\in[K]$}.

When loss functions at different stages are monotonic in each parameter
\theta_{i}
with
i\in[K]
, one can develop analogous approaches to achieve risk control with high probability and expected risk control.

A.1
Proof of Theorem 2

In Section 3.2, we define
\hat{\lambda}_{0}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}
,
\hat{\lambda}_{0}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}
and
\hat{\gamma}_{0}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}
. Here, we introduce the corresponding population-level quantities. We first define

\begin{split}&\lambda_{0}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}=\inf\left\{\lambda\in\Lambda:R^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}(\lambda)\leq\alpha_{1}\right\},\quad\text{and}\\
&\lambda_{0}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}=\inf\left\{\lambda\in\Lambda:R^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}(\lambda,1)\leq\alpha_{2}\right\}.\end{split}

For any fixed
\lambda\in\Lambda
, define

\gamma_{0}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}(\lambda)=\inf\left\{\gamma\in\Gamma:R^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}(\lambda,\gamma)\leq\alpha_{2}\right\}.

We set
\gamma_{0}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}(\lambda)=1
when the set is empty. For any
t\in[0,1]
, define

\lambda^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}(t):=\lceil t(\lambda_{0}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}\vee\lambda_{0}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}})+(1-t)\rceil_{\Lambda}.

Proof.

By the strong law of large numbers, for any
\lambda\in\Lambda
, we have:

\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}L^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}_{i}(\lambda)+\frac{1}{n+1}\xrightarrow{\text{ a.s. }}R^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}(\lambda).

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbf{1}_{\{\hat{\lambda}_{0}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}\geq\lambda_{0}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}\}}=1\quad\text{a.s.}

(18)

Similarly, we obtain

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbf{1}_{\{\hat{\lambda}_{0}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}\geq\lambda_{0}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}\}}=1\quad\text{a.s.}

(19)

And, for any
\lambda\in\Lambda\cap[\lambda_{0}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}},1]

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbf{1}_{\{\hat{\gamma}_{0}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}(\lambda)\geq\gamma_{0}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}(\lambda)\}}=1\quad\text{a.s.}

(20)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbf{1}_{\left\{\inf_{\lambda\in\Lambda\cap[\lambda_{0}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}},1]}\left(\hat{\gamma}_{0}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}(\lambda)-\gamma_{0}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}(\lambda)\right)\geq 0\right\}}=1\quad\text{a.s.}

(21)

For the first stage, risk control is straightforward: since
\hat{\lambda}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}(t)\geq\hat{\lambda}_{0}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}
for all

,

For the second stage risk control, following equations (
18
), (
19
), and (
21
), we have that when
n
is sufficiently large,

R^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}\left(\hat{\lambda}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}(t),\hat{\gamma}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}(t)\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(i)}}{{\leq}}R^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}\left(\hat{\lambda}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}(t),\gamma_{0}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}(\hat{\lambda}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}(t))\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(ii)}}{{\leq}}\alpha_{2}.

With a similar proof as in the proof of Theorem 2, we complete the proof of Corollary 1.

Proof of Theorem 3

For the first-stage risk control, we apply Theorem 1 in

{\mathbb{E}}L^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}_{n+1}(\tilde{\lambda}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}(t))\leq{\mathbb{E}}L^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}_{n+1}(\tilde{\lambda}_{0}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}})\leq\alpha_{1}.

For the second-stage risk control, by the tower property of conditional expectation, we have

{\mathbb{E}}L^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}_{n+1}(\tilde{\lambda}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}(t),\tilde{\gamma}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}(t))={\mathbb{E}}\left\{{\mathbb{E}}\left\{L^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}_{n+1}(\tilde{\lambda}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}(t),\tilde{\gamma}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}(t))|\mathcal{D}_{1}\right\}\right\},

where
\mathcal{D}_{1}=\{L^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}_{i}(\lambda)\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{1}}\cup\{L^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}_{i}(\lambda,\gamma)\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{1}}
. Here by conditioning on the
\mathcal{D}_{1}
,
\tilde{\lambda}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}(t)
can be viewed as a constant. Note that, by definition,
\tilde{\lambda}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}(t)\geq\lambda_{0}
and
L^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}_{i}(\tilde{\lambda}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}(t),1)\leq\alpha_{2}
. Therefore, we can apply Theorem 1 in
(
Angelopoulos et al., 2024
)
to the loss functions
\{L^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}_{i}(\tilde{\lambda}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}(t),\gamma)\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{2}}
, and have

{\mathbb{E}}\left\{L^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}_{n+1}(\tilde{\lambda}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}(t),\tilde{\gamma}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}(t))\mid\mathcal{D}_{1}\right\}\leq\alpha_{2}.

Lastly, by taking expectation, we complete the proof of Theorem 3.

A.4
Proof of Corollary 2

Note that

{\mathbb{E}}\sup_{(\lambda,\gamma)\in\mathcal{R}}L^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}_{n+1}(\lambda)={\mathbb{E}}L^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}_{n+1}(\tilde{\lambda}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}(t))\leq\alpha_{1}.

For the second stage, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\sup_{(\lambda,\gamma)\in\mathcal{R}}R^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}\left(\lambda,\gamma\right)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}R^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}(\tilde{\lambda}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}(1),\bar{\gamma}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}})

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}L^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}_{n+1}(\tilde{\lambda}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}(1),\bar{\gamma}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}).

Conditioned on
\mathcal{D}_{1}
, we make use of Theorem 1 in
(
Angelopoulos et al., 2024
)
to analyze function
L^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}_{i}(\tilde{\lambda}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}(1),\gamma)
, and obtain

{\mathbb{E}}L^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}}_{n+1}(\tilde{\lambda}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(1)}}(1),\bar{\gamma}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(2)}})\leq\alpha_{2}.

Lastly, by taking the expectation, we complete the proof.

Appendix C
Experiment details

C.1
Datasets

In this study, we evaluate our proposed methods on two well-known public datasets for ranked retrieval problems: the MSLR-Web dataset

(
)

2
2

2

This data can be downloaded at https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/project/mslr/
and the Yahoo LTRC dataset
3
3

3

This data can be downloaded at https://webscope.sandbox.yahoo.com/catalog.php?datatype=c
. Both datasets consist of feature vectors extracted from query-document pairs along with relevance judgment labels.

The MSLR-WEB dataset
(
Qin and Liu, 2013
)
, a large-scale Learning-to-Rank dataset released by Microsoft Research, is curated through a commercial web search engine (Microsoft Bing). It comprises 10K queries, associated with 1.2 million documents. Each query-document pair is represented by a 136-dimensional feature vector, normalized to be in the range [0, 1]. The relevance judgments are obtained from a retired labeling set of Microsoft Bing, which takes 5 values from 0 (irrelevant) to 4 (perfectly relevant).

The Yahoo! Learning to Rank Challenge dataset
(
Chapelle and Chang, 2011
)
consists of 172,870 documents spanning 6,330 queries, is sampled from query logs of the Yahoo! search engine. Each query-document pair is represented by a 700-dimensional feature vector, normalized to be in the range [0, 1]. Similarly, each document is given a relevance judgment with respect to the query, ranging from from 0 (least relevant) to 4 (most relevant).

For both datasets, we train their retrieval and ranking models on respective holdout training sets, where we do not particularly tune the models for optimal ranking results, as it is not the primary focus of this work. For the retrieval model, we train a three-layer MLP, with 128 and 32 neurons in the hidden layers. For the ranking model, we utilize the LambdaRank model
(
Burges et al., 2006
)
implemented by the open-source Pytorch package PT-Rank
(
Yu, 2020
)
. This choice of models emulates real search engine practices, where the retrieval stage employs a lightweight model for efficiency, while the ranking stage adopts a more sophisticated model for quality.
