On continuous orbit equivalence rigidity for virtually cyclic group actions
Yongle Jiang

Abstract
. We prove that for any two continuous minimal (topologically free) actions of the infinite dihedral group on an infinite compact Hausdorff space, they are continuously orbit equivalent only if they are conjugate. We also show the above fails if we replace the infinite dihedral group with certain other virtually cyclic groups, e.g. the direct product of the integer group with any non-abelian finite simple group.



2010 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary 37A20, Secondary 37B05, 20F65

1.
introduction

Let
G
be a countable discrete group and
X
be a compact Hausdorff space. Denote by
\mathcal{C}
a class of continuous actions of
G
on
X
. A natural and classical topic is to classify members in
\mathcal{C}
up to certain equivalence relation. In classical topological dynamical system, people have studied intensively the classification of
{\mathbb{Z}}
-actions, or amenable group actions in general, up to conjugacy. Historically speaking, the notion of entropy was invented to distinguish Bernoulli shifts on finite-bases with different sizes up to conjugacy (first in the measurable setting). Since one decade ago, more and more attention has been put on a much wider class of acting groups, i.e. sofic groups after the pioneering work of L. Bowen on sofic entropy in the measurable setting
[
3
]
and D. Kerr and H. Li shortly in the topological setting
[
15
]
.

In contrast to the conjugacy relation, X. Li initiated a systematic study of the notion of continuous orbit equivalence (see Definition
2.1
) for topological free actions of a general group
G

[
19
]
during his study of crossed product C
∗
-algebras. Note that this notion and its weaker versions, e.g. topological orbit equivalence have been studied in special cases (see
[
4
,
10
,
27
]
) before Li’s work. In particular, Giordano-Putnam-Skau
[
10
]
and Giordano-Matui-Putnam-Skau
[
8
,
9
]
have proved a series of remarkable results on classification of minimal
{\mathbb{Z}}
-actions on the Cantor set up to topological orbit equivalence.

Although continuous orbit equivalence relation is by definition, a priori, weaker than the conjugacy relation, an interesting phenomenon is that these two notions may coincide in certain cases.
To better describe this, let us say a continuous action
\alpha
of
G
on
X
is
continuous orbit equivalence rigid for
\mathcal{C}
if for any continuous action
\beta\in\mathcal{C}
,
\alpha
is continuously orbit equivalent to
\beta
implies that they are conjugate, denoted by
\alpha\overset{coe}{\sim}\beta\Rightarrow\alpha\overset{conj.}{\sim}\beta
. Note that here, compared with X. Li’s definition
[
19
, Definition 2.5]
, we further assume the acting groups are the same.

Now, we take
\mathcal{C}
to be the class of minimal topologically free actions
and explain two motivations behind this work.

First, continuous orbit equivalence rigidity has been established for various group actions, see e.g.
[
19
,
4
,
25
,
26
,
7
,
5
]
. However, as far as we know, the acting groups are assumed to be torsion-free in all written proofs (see also Remark
2.5
and Remark
4.2
). Thus it is natural to ask whether continuous orbit equivalence rigidity can be established when the acting groups contain non-trivial torsion elements. Second, a well-known result due to Boyle-Tomiyama
[
4
]
says that two topologically transitive, topologically free
{\mathbb{Z}}
-actions (e.g. minimal free
{\mathbb{Z}}
-actions) are continuously orbit equivalent only if they are conjugate. This result has been extended to minimal equicontinuous actions of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
(
d\geq 2
) in
[
7
]
. More precisely, it is shown there that for minimal equicontinuous
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
-systems, continuous orbit equivalence implies that the systems are virtually piecewise conjugate. Moreover, actions of virtually cyclic groups have been considered in the study of dynamic asymptotic dimension
[
1
]
and Matui’s HK conjecture
[
22
,
23
]
recently. Thus, it is natural to study the analogy of Boyle-Tomiyama’s above mentioned result for virtually cyclic group actions, i.e. we ask the following question.

Question 1.1
.

Can we establish continuous orbit equivalence rigidity for minimal topologically free actions of infinite virtually cyclic (but non-cyclic) groups?

In general, we could not hope continuous orbit equivalence rigidity holds true for all infinite virtually cyclic groups. Indeed, we have the following theorem.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
F
be any non-trivial finite group with trivial center, e.g. a non-abelian finite simple group. Then there exist two minimal free actions
\widetilde{\alpha}
and
\widetilde{\alpha}^{\prime}
of
{\mathbb{Z}}\times F
on an infinite compact Hausdorff space
X
such that
\widetilde{\alpha}\overset{coe}{\sim}\widetilde{\alpha}^{\prime}
but
\widetilde{\alpha}\overset{conj.}{\not\sim}\widetilde{\alpha}^{\prime}
.

On the positive side, when dealing with the infinite dihedral group, we do have such a rigidity theorem.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\alpha
and
\beta
be any two minimal (topologically free) actions of the infinite dihedral group
D_{\infty}={\mathbb{Z}}\rtimes{\mathbb{Z}}/{2{\mathbb{Z}}}
on an infinite compact Hausdorff space
X
. Then
\alpha\overset{coe}{\sim}\beta\Rightarrow\alpha\overset{conj.}{\sim}\beta
.

Note that minimal actions of
D_{\infty}
on an infinite compact Hausdorff space is automatically topologically free, see Proposition
2.6
. Moreover,
under the assumptions in the above theorem, we know continuous orbit equivalence implies the two actions have the same topological entropy. This may be compared with
[
17
, Theorem 7.5]
and
[
14
, Theorem D]
, where it is shown that many non-virtually cyclic groups admit actions for which topological entropy is an invariant of continuous orbit equivalence.
For possible generalizations of the theorems to other virtually cyclic groups, see the discussion in Section

5
. Here, we content ourselves with not discussing a general version of the above theorem. Below, we briefly describe strategy for the proofs.

For the proof of Theorem
1.2
, we use the skew product construction to reduce it to a problem of constructing minimal actions admitting continuous cocycles into finite groups which are not coboundaries. On the positive side, one may compare the proof of Theorem
1.3
with the proof of Boyle-Tomiyama
[
4
]
for continuous orbit equivalence rigidity for minimal
{\mathbb{Z}}
-actions. Here, the basic strategy is as follows: by a criterion
[
19
, Proposition 4.4]
due to X. Li, it suffices to show the orbit cocycle
c
is cohomologous to a group isomorphism of
D_{\infty}
.
The starting point for our proof is the characterization of bi-Lipschitz bijections of
{\mathbb{Z}}
due to Benjamini-Shamov
[
2
]
.
After restricting the orbit cocycle
c:{\mathbb{Z}}\times X\to D_{\infty}
on the sub
{\mathbb{Z}}
-action, we may extract from it a defect cocycle
a
which attains only finitely many values in
D_{\infty}
. Here,
a
is defined to measure the defect between
c
and a group homomorphism from
{\mathbb{Z}}
to
D_{\infty}
. By twisting
a
with a suitable coboundary, we can assume the range of
a
is contained in
{\mathbb{Z}}
. If the sub
{\mathbb{Z}}
-action is minimal, one can apply the well-known Gottschalk-Hedlund theorem
[
12
]
for minimal
{\mathbb{Z}}
-actions to deduce
a
is actually a coboudary, which implies the original orbit cocycle
c
is trivial. For the general case, i.e. the sub
{\mathbb{Z}}
-action is not minimal, we can apply Gottschalk-Hedlund theorem to each
{\mathbb{Z}}
-component, now the existence of reflection of
{\mathbb{Z}}
in the group structure of
D_{\infty}
allows us to patch up untwisters for each component to get a global one.

The paper is organized into five sections. In Section
2
, we explain terminologies used in this paper, including cocycles, continuous orbit equivalence, skew product actions and induced actions. We also present examples of continuous actions. Then we give examples of minimal free actions admitting continuous cocycles which are not coboundaries in Section
3
. The proof of Theorem
1.2
(resp. Theorem
1.3
) is given in Subsection
4.1
(resp. Subsection
4.2
). We conclude the paper with several remarks on the possible generalization of the theorems in Section
5
.

In this paper,
G
and
H
usually mean virtually cyclic groups or finitely generated groups, which is clear from the context. Moreover, we denote a target group by
K
. Unless otherwise specified, all cocycles are assumed to be continuous.

2.
preliminaries

2.1.
Cocycles and continuous orbit equivalence

Let
G\curvearrowright X
be a continuous action on a compact Hausdorff space and let
K
be a discrete group. Recall that a continuous map
c:G\times X\to K
is called a
K
-valued (continuous) cocycle
if
c(g_{1}g_{2},x)=c(g_{1},g_{2}x)c(g_{2},x)
for all
g_{1}
,
g_{2}\in G
and all
x\in X
. Two continuous cocycles
c_{1},c_{2}:G\times X\to K
are
(continuously) cohomologous/equivalent
if there exists a continuous map
b:X\to K
such that
c_{1}(g,x)=b(gx)^{-1}c_{2}(g,x)b(x)
for all
g\in G
and all
x\in X
. A continuous cocycle is called
trivial (resp. a cobundary)
if it is equivalent to a group homomorphism
\phi:G\to K
(resp. the trivial group homomorphism
\phi\equiv e_{K}
on
G
), which is treated as a constant cocycle on
X
.

Note that when
G={\mathbb{Z}}
, a
K
-valued cocycle
c
for
{\mathbb{Z}}\curvearrowright X
can be represented as a map
f:X\to K
. Indeed, for one direction, set
f(x)=c(1,x)
; for the other direction, we may define
c(1,x)=f(x)
and use the cocycle identity to define
c(N,x)
for all
N\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and hence get a cocycle
c
. Sometimes, we may abuse our notation by calling
f
a cocycle.

Given two continuous actions
G\curvearrowright X
and
K\curvearrowright Y
, we have the notion of continuous orbit equivalence (written as coe for short) which is systematically studied by X. Li
[
19
, Definition 2.5]
.

Definition 2.1

(coe)
.

G\curvearrowright X
and
K\curvearrowright Y
are continuously orbit equivalent, denoted by
G\curvearrowright X\overset{coe}{\sim}K\curvearrowright Y
, if there exists a homeomorphism
\phi:X\approx Y
with inverse
\psi=\phi^{-1}
and continuous maps
a:G\times X\to K
and
b:K\times Y\to G
such that

\displaystyle\phi(gx)=a(g,x)\phi(x),~\psi(hy)=b(h,y)\psi(y)

for all
g\in G
,
h\in H
,
x\in X
and
y\in Y
.

If both
a
and
b
are further assumed to be group isomorphisms from
G
to
K
and from
K
to
G
respectively, then we call these two actions are
conjugate
, written as
G\curvearrowright X\overset{conj.}{\sim}K\curvearrowright Y
. Clearly, conjugacy implies coe.

We remark that if
K\curvearrowright Y
is
topologically free
(i.e. for each
e\neq h\in K
,
\{y\in Y:hy\neq y\}
is dense in
Y
), then
a
is a continuous cocycle, see
[
19
, Lemma 2.8]
. When both
a
and
b
are cocycles in the above definition, we simply call
b
the inverse cocycle of
a
.

The following fact is proved independently in
[
21
]
and
[
20
]
, we sketch the proof for completeness.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
G
and
K
be finitely generated groups. Let
G\curvearrowright X
and
K\curvearrowright Y
be two topological free actions on compact spaces. Assume they are continuously orbit equivalent and
a:G\times X\to K
is the associated continuous orbit cocycle, then
G\ni g\mapsto a(g,x)\in K
is a bi-Lipschitz bijection for each
x\in X
with respect to the right invariant word metrics on
G
and
K
respectively.

Throughout the paper, a
bi-Lipschitz bijection
means a bijective map
\phi
such that both
\phi
and
\phi^{-1}
are Lipschitz maps in the usual sense.

Proof.

Let
S
and
T
be any symmetric generating set for
G
and
K
respectively. Let
|\cdot|_{S}
and
|\cdot|_{T}
be associated word lengths, and let
d_{S}(\cdot,\cdot)
and
d_{T}(\cdot,\cdot)
be the corresponding right invariant word length metrics on
G
and
K
, i.e.
d_{S}(g_{1},g_{2})=|g_{2}g_{1}^{-1}|_{S}
and
d_{T}(h_{1},h_{2})=|h_{2}h_{1}^{-1}|_{T}
for all
g_{1}
,
g_{2}\in G
and
h_{1}
,
h_{2}\in K
.

Then
d_{T}(a(g_{1},x),a(g_{2},x))=|a(g_{2},x)a(g_{1},x)^{-1}|_{T}=|a(g_{2}g_{1}^{-1},g_{1}x)|_{T}\leq|g_{2}g_{1}^{-1}|_{S}\cdot\sup_{x\in X}\max_{s\in S}|a(s,x)|_{T}=d_{S}(g_{1},g_{2})\cdot\sup_{x\in X}\max_{s\in S}|a(s,x)|_{T}
, where the inequality follows from the cocycle identity. This shows
g\mapsto a(g,x)
is a Lipschitz map.

To see
g\mapsto a(g,x)
is a bijection and its inverse is a Lipschitz map, apply the similar argument to the inverse cocycle using
[
19
, Lemma 2.10]
.
∎

The following lemma is well-known, but we present its proof for the reader’s convenience.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\alpha:{\mathbb{Z}}\curvearrowright X
be a minimal action on a compact space
X
and
K_{0}\subset K
be a closed subgroup in a topological group. Let
c:{\mathbb{Z}}\times X\to K_{0}
be a continuous cocycle such that
c(n,x)=f(\alpha_{n}(x))f(x)^{-1}
for some continuous map
f:X\to K
and all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and
x\in X
. Then there is a continuous map
f^{\prime}:X\to K_{0}
such that
c(n,x)=f^{\prime}(\alpha_{n}(x))f^{\prime}(x)^{-1}
holds for all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and all
x\in X
.

Proof.

Observe that the continuous map

x\in X\to K_{0}f(x)\in K_{0}\backslash K

is
\alpha
-invariant. Hence minimality implies there exists some
k\in K
such that
K_{0}f(x)=K_{0}k
for all
x\in X
, i.e.
f(x)\in K_{0}k
for all
x\in X
. Define
f^{\prime}(x)=f(x)k^{-1}
. We still have
c(n,x)=f^{\prime}(\alpha_{n}(x))f^{\prime}(x)^{-1}
.
∎

2.2.
Skew product actions

We recall the following well-known construction of skew product actions.
Let
\sigma:H\to Aut(K)
be a group homomorphism. Let
c:H\curvearrowright X\to K
be a
skew cocycle
, i.e. for all
h_{1}
,
h_{2}\in H
and all
x\in X
,

c(h_{1}h_{2},x)=c(h_{1},h_{2}x)\sigma_{h_{1}}(c(h_{2},x)).

One may check that
c((h_{1}h_{2})h_{3},x)=c(h_{1}(h_{2}h_{3}),x)
holds for all
h_{i}\in H
,
1\leq i\leq 3
and all
x\in X
. Besides,
c
is a skew cocycle iff the map
c^{\prime}:H\curvearrowright X\to K\rtimes_{\sigma}H
given by
c^{\prime}(h,x)=(c(h,x),h)
is a cocycle in the usual sense.

Given a group homomorphism
\sigma:H\to Aut(K)
, an action
H\curvearrowright X
and a skew cocycle
c
defined as above, we may define the
generalized skew product action
:

\displaystyle G:=K\rtimes_{\sigma}H

\displaystyle\curvearrowright X\times K

\displaystyle(k,h)(x,k^{\prime})

\displaystyle:=(hx,c(h,x)\sigma_{h}(k^{\prime})k^{-1}).

Note that when
\sigma
is trivial, i.e.
\sigma\equiv id_{K}
, skew cocycles (resp. skew product actions) are reduced to the usual cocycles (resp. usual skew product actions) for the group
K\times H
. In this paper, we usually take
H={\mathbb{Z}}
,
K
be a finite group and
\sigma
be trivial.
To emphasize the cocycle
c
, we usually denote
X\times K
by
X\times_{c}K
.

2.3.
Induced actions

Let
H
be a subgroup of
G
. Fix any lift map
L:G/H\to G
such that
L(gH)H=gH
for all
g\in G
. Consider the associated cocycle
c:G\times G/H\to H
given by
c(g,g^{\prime}H)=L(gg^{\prime}H)^{-1}gL(g^{\prime}H)
for all
g,g^{\prime}\in G
. Given any continuous action
H\curvearrowright Y
, then the
induced action

G\curvearrowright G/H\times Y
is defined as follows:

g(g^{\prime}H,y)=(gg^{\prime}H,c(g,g^{\prime}H)y),~\mbox{for all $g,g^{\prime}\in G$ and $y\in Y$.}

2.4.
Examples of continuous actions

Odometer actions

Fix a sequence of strictly increasing positive integers
(n_{i})_{i\geq 1}
such that
n_{i}\mid n_{i+1}
for all
i\geq 1
. Let
\lim\limits_{\leftarrow}{\mathbb{Z}}/{n_{i}{\mathbb{Z}}}=\{(x_{i}+n_{i}{\mathbb{Z}})_{i\geq 1}\in\prod_{i\geq 1}{\mathbb{Z}}/{n_{i}{\mathbb{Z}}}:~n_{i}\mid(x_{i+1}-x_{i}),~\forall~i\geq 1\}
. The
odometer action associated to
(n_{i})
is defined as the continuous action
\alpha:{\mathbb{Z}}\curvearrowright\lim\limits_{\leftarrow}{\mathbb{Z}}/{n_{i}{\mathbb{Z}}}
given by

\alpha_{n}((x_{i}+n_{i}{\mathbb{Z}})_{i})=(n+x_{i}+n_{i}{\mathbb{Z}})_{i},

where
x_{i}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Weakly mixing actions

A continuous action
{\mathbb{Z}}\curvearrowright X
on a compact Hausdorff space is called
(topologically) weakly mixing

[
11
, p.23]
if the product action
{\mathbb{Z}}\curvearrowright X\times X
is
transitive
, i.e. for every pair of non-empty open sets
U
,
V
in
X\times X
there exists some
g\in{\mathbb{Z}}
with
gU\cap V\neq\emptyset
. Examples of free minimal weakly mixing actions include the so-called Chacón system
[
11
, p. 27]
.

It is well-known and easy to check that for a continuous action
{\mathbb{Z}}\curvearrowright X
on a compact Hausdorff space
X
, if
X
has no isolated points and there is a point in
X
with dense orbit, then
{\mathbb{Z}}\curvearrowright X
is transitive. Conversely, for a compact metric space
X
,
{\mathbb{Z}}\curvearrowright X
is transitive implies it admits a point with dense orbit, see e.g.
[
16
, Proposition 7.9]
.

2.5.
Subgroups of
D_{\infty}

Let
D_{\infty}={\mathbb{Z}}\rtimes\frac{{\mathbb{Z}}}{2{\mathbb{Z}}}=\langle s\rangle\rtimes\langle t\rangle=\langle s,t~|~t^{2},tsts\rangle
be the infinite dihedral group. We record a simple lemma on the structure of its subgroups.

Lemma 2.4
.

If
H
is a subgroup of
D_{\infty}
, then either
H=\langle s^{k},s^{i}t\rangle
for some
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and some
0\leq|i|<k
, or
H=\langle s^{k}t\rangle
or
H=\langle s^{k}\rangle
for some
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. Moreover, all subgroups can be realized as
\phi_{i}(H)
for some
i\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and some
H\in\{k{\mathbb{Z}}\rtimes\frac{{\mathbb{Z}}}{2{\mathbb{Z}}},~k{\mathbb{Z}},~\frac{{\mathbb{Z}}}{2{\mathbb{Z}}}:~k\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}
, where,
\phi_{i}\in Aut(G)
is defined by
\phi_{i}(s)=s,\phi_{i}(t)=s^{i}t
.

Proof.

Write
H\cap{\mathbb{Z}}=\langle s^{k}\rangle
for some
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. Note that the set of torsion elements of
G
is precisely
\{s^{i}t:i\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}
.

If
H
contains no non-trivial torsion elements, then
H\subset{\mathbb{Z}}
, i.e.
H=H\cap{\mathbb{Z}}=\langle s^{k}\rangle
.

If
H
contains some non-trivial torsion element, say
s^{i}t\in H
for some
i\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. W.L.O.G., we may assume
|i|
is smallest among all non-trivial torsion elements in
H
.

Case 1:
i=0
. Then clearly,
H=\langle s^{k},t\rangle
.

Case 2:
|i|>0
.

If
k=0
, then
H=\langle s^{i}t\rangle
. Indeed, assume not, then
s^{j}t\in H
for some
j\neq i
, which implies that
(s^{i}t)(s^{j}t)=s^{i-j}\in H\cap{\mathbb{Z}}
, a contradiction.

We may assume
k>0
. Clearly,
0<|i|<k
, then
H=\langle s^{k},s^{i}t\rangle
. Indeed, assume
s^{j}t\in H
, then
(s^{j}t)(s^{i}t)^{-1}=s^{j-i}\in H\cap{\mathbb{Z}}=\langle s^{k}\rangle
, hence
s^{j}t\in\langle s^{k},s^{i}t\rangle
.

The moreover part is now clear.
∎

Remark 2.5
.

Note that this lemma implies that for two odometer actions of
D_{\infty}
, the notion of structurally conjugacy between them, as introduced in
[
7
, Definition 3.1]
, coincides with the usual conjugacy. Thus
[
7
, Theorem 3.3]
implies that for two free odometer actions of
D_{\infty}
, continuous orbit equivalence between them implies they are conjugate.

Next, let us observe the following hold, see also
[
22
, Proposition 2.8]
.

Proposition 2.6
.

If
\alpha:D_{\infty}\curvearrowright X
is a minimal action on an infinite compact Hausdorff space
X
, then it is topologically free.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we may assume
\alpha
is not free. Write
D_{\infty}={\mathbb{Z}}\rtimes\frac{{\mathbb{Z}}}{2{\mathbb{Z}}}=\langle s,t|~t^{2},tsts\rangle=\langle s\rangle\rtimes\langle t\rangle
. Then observe that the sub
{\mathbb{Z}}=\langle s\rangle
-action, i.e.
\alpha|_{{\mathbb{Z}}}:{\mathbb{Z}}\curvearrowright X
is minimal.

Indeed, note that the number of minimal
{\mathbb{Z}}
-components in
X
is either one or two (See the discussion before Case I in the proof of Theorem
1.3
for details). If we have two minimal
{\mathbb{Z}}
-components, say
X_{0}
and
tX_{0}
, then
\alpha|_{{\mathbb{Z}}}
on each component is free and so is
\alpha
, contradicting to our assumption.

Now, as the action is minimal and
X
is infinite, every stabilizer subgroup must be of infinite index in
D_{\infty}
.
By Lemma
2.4
, we deduce that for each
x\in X
, if the stabilizer group
Stab(x)
is not trivial, then
Stab(x)=\{e,s^{n}t\}
for some

n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. To check topological freeness of
\alpha
, we only
need to show that for each
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, the closed set
X_{n}:=\{x\in X:s^{n}tx=x\}
is nowhere dense.

Note that
x\in X_{n}
iff
Stab(x)=\{e,s^{n}t\}
. Now, assume that
\exists~\emptyset\neq U\subsetneq X_{n}
, where
U
is an open set. Then we may pick any
y\in X\setminus U
and use
{\mathbb{Z}}\curvearrowright X
is minimal to deduce that for each
N
,
\exists~|m|>N
such that
s^{m}y\in U
. Hence,
\{e,s^{n}t\}=Stab(s^{m}y)=s^{m}Stab(y)s^{-m}
. Since
Stab(y)=\{e\}
or
\{e,s^{k}t\}
for some
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, we deduce that
2m=n-k
, which gives us a contradiction since
m
can be arbitrarily large.
∎

2.6.
Bi-Lipschitz bijections of
{\mathbb{Z}}

We need the following result in
[
2
]
.

Theorem 2.7

(Benjamini-Shamov)
.

Let
f:{\mathbb{Z}}\to{\mathbb{Z}}
be a bi-Lipschitz bijection (
{\mathbb{Z}}
is equipped with its usual metric, namely
\rho(x,y):=|x-y|
). Then either
\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}}|f(x)-x|<+\infty
or
\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}}|f(x)+x|<+\infty
. More precisely,
f(x)=\pm x+\mbox{const}+r(x)
, where
\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}}|r(x)|<\infty
.

2.7.
Gottschalk-Hedlund theorem

Another ingredient we need is the classical Gottschalk-Hedlund theorem
[
12
, Theorem 14.11]
. We record the following version as presented in
[
13
, P. 102, Theorem 2.9.4]
and explain how to use it in our setting. Note that the proof of
[
13
, P. 102, Theorem 2.9.4]
still works without the metrizability assumption on the space.

Theorem 2.8

(Gottschalk-Hedlund)
.

Let
X
be a compact Hausdorff space,
f:X\to X
a minimal continuous map and
g:X\to\mathbb{R}
continuous such that
\sup_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}|\sum_{i=0}^{n}g\circ f^{i}(x_{0})|<\infty
for some
x_{0}\in X
. Then there is a continuous
\phi:X\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\phi\circ f-\phi=g
.

Now, let
f:X\to X
be a minimal continuous action and
c:{\mathbb{Z}}\times X\to\mathbb{R}
be a continuous cocycle for this action. Set
g(x)=c(1,x)
, then a calculation shows that
\sum_{i=0}^{n}g\circ f^{i}(x_{0})=c(n+1,x_{0})
.
Thus, Gottschalk-Hedlund theorem actually shows that if
c
is a bounded cocycle, i.e.
\sup_{(n,x)\in{\mathbb{Z}}\times X}|c(n,x)|<\infty
, then it is a continuous coboundary map.

3.
existence of cocycles that are not coboundaries

For the proof of Theorem
1.2
, we need to construct free minimal actions such that they admit continuous cocycles which are not coboundaries with values into finite groups. We collect some results along this direction.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
{\mathbb{Z}}\curvearrowright X
be a minimal weakly mixing and continuous action on a compact metric space
X
. Then for each non-trivial group homomorphism
\phi
from
{\mathbb{Z}}
to
F
, where
F
is a finite abelian group,
\phi
is not a coboundary.

Proof.

Assume that
\phi(s)=L(sx)-L(x)
for some continuous map
L:X\to F
and all
s\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and all
x\in X
. Then
L(sx)-L(x)=L(sy)-L(y)
for all
x,y\in X
. Then
L^{\prime}(x,y):=L(x)-L(y)
is a continuous map which is constant on each orbit of any point in
X\times X
. By assumption, we know that
L^{\prime}
is constant since the diagonal action
{\mathbb{Z}}\curvearrowright X\times X
admits a point with dense orbit. Thus
L^{\prime}(x,y)=L^{\prime}(x,x)=0
for all
x,y\in X
, i.e.
L
is a constant function on
X
, hence
\phi(s)=0
for all
s\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, i.e.
\phi
is a trivial homomorphism, a contradiction.
∎

For the next result, we need the following known characterization of coboundaries using essential values for continuous cocycles in
[
18
]
, which was adapted from Schmidt’s notion for measurable cocycles in
[
24
]
.

Let
K
be a locally compact group with the unit element
e
. By
K_{\infty}
we denote the one point compactification of
K
, i.e.
K_{\infty}=K\cup\{\infty\}
. We may extend the group operation form the group
K
onto the set
K_{\infty}
by
g\cdot\infty=\infty
for all
g\in K_{\infty}
. One can check that the operation
K_{\infty}\times K_{\infty}\ni(g_{1},g_{2})\to g_{1}g_{2}\in K_{\infty}
is continuous.

Definition 3.2

(Definition 3.1 in
[
18
]
)
.

Let
T:{\mathbb{Z}}\curvearrowright X
be a continuous action on a compact space
X
. Let
c:{\mathbb{Z}}\times X\to K
be a continuous cocycle. We say that
r\in K_{\infty}
is an
essential value of
\phi
if for each nonempty open
U\subset X
and each neighborhood
V
of
r
there exists
N\in{\mathbb{Z}}
such that

U\cap T^{-N}U\cap\{x\in X:c(N,x)\in V\}\neq\emptyset.

The set of all essential values of
c
is denote by
E_{\infty}(c)
. Put also
E(c)=E_{\infty}(c)\cap K
.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
{\mathbb{Z}}\curvearrowright X
be a minimal action and
K
be a compact abelain group, e.g. a finite abelian group. Let
c:{\mathbb{Z}}\times X\to K
be a continuous cocycle. Then
E(c)=\{e\}
iff
c
is a coboundary, where
e
denotes the neutral element in
K
.

Proof.

\Rightarrow
: this is Lemma 3.1 (ii) in
[
18
]
.
\Leftarrow
: one can check this directly using the definition of
E(c)
.
∎

The following result might be known to experts, since we do not find a reference, we include the proof.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
({\mathbb{Z}},X)
be a minimal odometer action, say
X=\lim\limits_{\leftarrow}{\mathbb{Z}}/{n_{i}{\mathbb{Z}}}
, where
n_{i}\mid n_{j}
for all
i\leq j
and
\lim_{j\to\infty}n_{j}=\infty
. Let
p
be a prime number. Then the following statements are equivalent.

(1) Every continuous map
f:X\to{\mathbb{Z}}/{p{\mathbb{Z}}}
gives rise to a continuous coboundary.

(2)
\sup_{i}ord(p,n_{i})=\infty
, where
ord(p,n_{i}):=\max_{p^{k}\mid n_{i}}k
.

Proof.

(1)
\Rightarrow
(2): By Proposition
3.3
, we know that for all continuous
f:X\to{\mathbb{Z}}/{p{\mathbb{Z}}}
,
E(f)=\{0\}
, i.e. for all
0\neq c\in{\mathbb{Z}}/{p{\mathbb{Z}}}
, there exists some non-empty open set
U\subset X
such that for all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, we have
x\in U\cap T^{-n}U\Rightarrow f(x)+f(Tx)+\cdots+f(T^{n-1}x)\neq c
.

Now, denote by
\pi_{i}:X\to{\mathbb{Z}}/{n_{i}{\mathbb{Z}}}
the natural projection map, i.e.
\pi_{i}((x_{i}+n_{i}{\mathbb{Z}})_{i})=x_{i}+n_{i}{\mathbb{Z}}
. Assume that
\sup_{i}ord(p,n_{i})
is bounded, let us take some
j
such that
ord(p,n_{j})=\sup_{i}ord(p,n_{i})
and we may assume
n_{j}>1
, then we define a map
f:X\to{\mathbb{Z}}/{p{\mathbb{Z}}}
as the composition
X\overset{\pi_{j}}{\to}{\mathbb{Z}}/{n_{j}{\mathbb{Z}}}\overset{f^{\prime}}{\to}{\mathbb{Z}}/{p{\mathbb{Z}}}
, where
f^{\prime}(i+n_{j}{\mathbb{Z}})=\delta_{i,1}\in{\mathbb{Z}}/{p{\mathbb{Z}}}
for all
0\leq i\leq n_{1}-1
. Take
c=1\in{\mathbb{Z}}/{p{\mathbb{Z}}}
, then we find some
U\subset X
as above. We may shrink
U
if necessary to assume
U=\pi_{k}^{-1}(i_{0}+n_{k}{\mathbb{Z}})
for some
k>j
and
i_{0}
. Now, take any
x=(x_{i}+n_{i}{\mathbb{Z}})_{i}\in U
and
n=n_{k}\ell
for some integer
\ell
such that
\frac{n_{k}}{n_{j}}\ell\equiv 1~mod~p
(This is possible since
p\nmid\frac{n_{k}}{n_{j}}
by our choice of
j
and
p
is a prime number). Note that
n_{k}\mid n
implies that
T^{n}x\in U
. Now, a calculation shows that

\displaystyle f(x)+f(Tx)+\cdots+f(T^{n-1}x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=0}^{n-1}f^{\prime}(x_{j}+s+n_{j}{\mathbb{Z}})

\displaystyle=1\cdot(\frac{n}{n_{j}})=\frac{n_{k}\ell}{n_{j}}=1=c\in{\mathbb{Z}}/{p{\mathbb{Z}}}.

This is a contradiction.

(2)
\Rightarrow
(1): the proof is similar as above. By Proposition
3.3
, we aim to show that for all continuous
f:X\to{\mathbb{Z}}/{p{\mathbb{Z}}}
and all
0\neq c\in{\mathbb{Z}}/{p{\mathbb{Z}}}
, there exists some non-empty set
U\subset X
such that for all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
,
x\in U\cap T^{-n}U\Rightarrow f(x)+f(Tx)+\cdots+f(T^{n-1}x)\neq c
.

Pick
j
large enough such that
f
factors through
\pi_{j}:X\to{\mathbb{Z}}/{n_{j}{\mathbb{Z}}}
, i.e.
f=\pi_{j}\circ f^{\prime}
for some map
f^{\prime}:{\mathbb{Z}}/{n_{j}{\mathbb{Z}}}\to{\mathbb{Z}}/{p{\mathbb{Z}}}
. Since
\sup_{i}ord(p,n_{i})=\infty
, we may find
k>j
such that
ord(p,n_{k})>ord(p,n_{j})
. Then let
U=\pi_{k}^{-1}(0+n_{k}{\mathbb{Z}})
. Now, for each
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, if
x\in U\cap T^{-n}U
, then
x_{k}=0=x_{k}+n
in
{\mathbb{Z}}/{n_{k}{\mathbb{Z}}}
, so
n_{k}\mid n
. Now, a calculation shows that

\displaystyle f(x)+f(Tx)+\cdots+f(T^{n-1}x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=0}^{n-1}f^{\prime}(x_{j}+s+n_{j}{\mathbb{Z}})

\displaystyle=(\sum_{s=0}^{n_{j}-1}f^{\prime}(s+n_{j}{\mathbb{Z}}))\frac{n}{n_{j}}

\displaystyle=[(\sum_{s=0}^{n_{j}-1}f^{\prime}(s+n_{j}{\mathbb{Z}}))\frac{n}{n_{k}}]\frac{n_{k}}{n_{j}}\in p{\mathbb{Z}}~\mbox{(since $ord(p,n_{k})>ord(p,n_{j})$)}.

Thus,
0=f(x)+f(Tx)+\cdots+f(T^{n-1}x)\neq c
in
{\mathbb{Z}}/{p{\mathbb{Z}}}
.
∎

From the above proposition, we can deduce that for the free minimal odometer action
{\mathbb{Z}}\curvearrowright\lim\limits_{\leftarrow}\frac{{\mathbb{Z}}}{2^{i}{\mathbb{Z}}}:=X
, it admits a continuous
{\mathbb{Z}}/{3{\mathbb{Z}}}
-valued cocycle that is not a coboundary.

4.1.
Non-rigidity part

Theorem
1.2
is a direct corollary of the following proposition.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
\alpha:{\mathbb{Z}}\curvearrowright X
be a minimal free action on a compact Hausdorff space. Let
c,c^{\prime}:{\mathbb{Z}}\times X\to F
be two continuous cocycles (w.r.t. the action
\alpha
) into a finite non-trivial group
F
. Consider the associated skew product actions
\widetilde{\alpha}:F\times{\mathbb{Z}}\curvearrowright X\times_{c}F
and
\widetilde{\alpha}^{\prime}:F\times{\mathbb{Z}}\curvearrowright X\times_{c^{\prime}}F
as defined in Subsection
2.2
. Then the following hold:

(i) Both
\widetilde{\alpha}
and
\widetilde{\alpha}^{\prime}
are minimal free actions.

(ii)
\widetilde{\alpha}\overset{coe}{\sim}\widetilde{\alpha}^{\prime}
.

(iii) If
c^{\prime}\equiv e\in F
, the neutral element in
F
, then
\widetilde{\alpha}\overset{conj.}{\sim}\widetilde{\alpha}^{\prime}
implies that
c
is cohomologous to a group homomorphism from
{\mathbb{Z}}
into
C(F)
.

(iv) For each
F
with trivial center, there exist
\alpha
,
c
and
c^{\prime}
such that
\widetilde{\alpha}\overset{conj.}{\not\sim}\widetilde{\alpha}^{\prime}
.

Proof.

(i) We leave it as an exercise.

(ii) Recall that
\widetilde{\alpha}:F\times{\mathbb{Z}}\curvearrowright X\times_{c}F
is defined as follows:

\widetilde{\alpha}_{(f,n)}(x,f^{\prime})=(\alpha_{n}(x),c(n,x)f^{\prime}f^{-1})~\mbox{for all $f$, $f^{\prime}\in F$, $n\in{\mathbb{Z}}$ and $x\in X$}.

Define
\theta:(F\times{\mathbb{Z}})\times(X\times_{c}F)\to F\times{\mathbb{Z}}
by setting

\theta((t,n),(x,f))=(tf^{-1}c(n,x)^{-1}c^{\prime}(n,x)f,n)~\mbox{for all $n\in{\mathbb{Z}}$, $t\in F$ and $x\in X$}.

One can check it is a continuous cocycle w.r.t. the skew product action
\widetilde{\alpha}
. Indeed,

\displaystyle\theta((t_{1},n_{1})(t_{2},n_{2}),(x,f))

\displaystyle=\theta((t_{1}t_{2},n_{1}+n_{2}),(x,f))

\displaystyle=(t_{1}t_{2}f^{-1}c(n_{1}+n_{2},x)^{-1}c^{\prime}(n_{1}+n_{2},x)f,n_{1}+n_{2})

\displaystyle\theta((t_{1},n_{1}),\widetilde{\alpha}_{(t_{2},n_{2})}(x,f))\theta((t_{2},n_{2}),(x,f))

\displaystyle=\theta((t_{1},n_{1}),(\alpha_{n_{2}}(x),c(n_{2},x)ft_{2}^{-1}))\theta((t_{2},n_{2}),(x,f))

\displaystyle=(t_{1}t_{2}f^{-1}c(n_{2},x)^{-1}c(n_{1},\alpha_{n_{2}}(x))^{-1}c^{\prime}(n_{1},\alpha_{n_{2}}(x))c(n_{2},x)ft_{2}^{-1},n_{1})\cdot

\displaystyle\quad\quad(t_{2}f^{-1}c(n_{2},x)^{-1}c^{\prime}(n_{2},x)f,n_{2})

\displaystyle=(t_{1}t_{2}f^{-1}c(n_{2},x)^{-1}c(n_{1},\alpha_{n_{2}}(x))^{-1}c^{\prime}(n_{1},\alpha_{n_{2}}(x))c^{\prime}(n_{2},x)f,n_{1}+n_{2})

\displaystyle=(t_{1}t_{2}f^{-1}c(n_{1}+n_{2},x)^{-1}c^{\prime}(n_{1}+n_{2},x)f,n_{1}+n_{2}).

Thus,

\displaystyle\theta((t_{1},n_{1})(t_{2},n_{2}),(x,f))=\theta((t_{1},n_{1}),\widetilde{\alpha}_{(t_{2},n_{2})}(x,f))\theta((t_{2},n_{2}),(x,f)).

Moreover, one can verify that

\widetilde{\alpha}_{(t,n)}(x,f)=\widetilde{\alpha}^{\prime}_{\theta((t,n),(x,f))}(x,f).

By symmetry, one can also define the inverse cocycle for
\theta
, hence
\widetilde{\alpha}\overset{coe}{\sim}\widetilde{\alpha}^{\prime}
.

(iii) First, we observe that for each
\Phi\in Aut(F\times{\mathbb{Z}})
, we have
\Phi(t,1)=(\epsilon(t)g,\pm 1)
for some
\epsilon\in Aut(F)
and
g\in C(F)
, the center of
F
. Indeed, since
F
is finite,
\Phi(F\times\{0\})=F\times\{0\}
, thus we define
\epsilon=\Phi|_{F\times\{0\}}
. Next, we may write
\Phi(e,1)=(g,\pm 1)
for some
g\in F
. From
\Phi((e,1)(t,0))=\Phi((t,0)(e,1))
for all
t\in F
, we deduce that
g\in C(F)
. Note that
\Phi(t,n)=(\epsilon(t)g^{n},\pm n)
for all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and
t\in F
. For such
\Phi
, we simply write
\Phi=(\epsilon(\cdot)g,\pm)
.

Next, assume
\widetilde{\alpha}
is conjugate to
\widetilde{\alpha}^{\prime}
, say via a homeomorphism
\tau:X\times_{c}F\to X\times_{c^{\prime}}F
and a group isomorphism
(\epsilon(\cdot)g,\pm):F\times{\mathbb{Z}}\cong F\times{\mathbb{Z}}
defined in the last paragraph. Then we may write

\tau((x,f^{\prime}))=(\phi(x,f^{\prime}),\psi(x,f^{\prime}))

for some continuous maps
\phi:X\times_{c}F\to X
and
\psi:X\times_{c}F\to F
.

Fix any
(x,f^{\prime})\in X\times_{c}F
and
(f,n)\in F\times{\mathbb{Z}}
, the
F\times{\mathbb{Z}}
-equivariance of
\tau
shows that for all
x\in X
,
f
,
f^{\prime}\in F
and all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, we have

(1)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\alpha_{\pm n}(\phi(x,f^{\prime}))&=\phi(\alpha_{n}(x),c(n,x)f^{\prime}f^{-1})\\
c^{\prime}(\pm n,\phi(x,f^{\prime}))\psi(x,f^{\prime})g^{-n}\epsilon(f)^{-1}&=\psi(\alpha_{n}(x),c(n,x)f^{\prime}f^{-1}).\end{split}

Since
f
is arbitrary in the 1st expression in (
1
), we deduce that
\phi
only depends on its 1st coordinate, so we may directly write
\phi(x,-)=\phi(x)
and hence
\alpha_{\pm n}(\phi(x))=\phi(\alpha_{n}(x))
. Using
\tau
is a homeomorphism, we can check that
\phi|_{X}
is also a homeomorphism.

Now, set
f=c(n,x)
and
f^{\prime}=e
into the 2nd line in (
1
), we get that

c^{\prime}(\pm n,\phi(x))\psi(x,e)g^{-n}\epsilon(c(n,x))^{-1}=\psi(\alpha_{n}(x),e).

Thus,

(2)

\displaystyle c^{\prime}(\pm n,\phi(x))=\psi(\alpha_{n}(x),e)\epsilon(c(n,x))g^{n}\psi(x,e)^{-1}.

Since
\alpha_{\pm n}(\phi(x))=\phi(\alpha_{n}(x))
, (
2
) may be rewritten as

\displaystyle c^{\prime}(\pm n,x)=\psi(\phi^{-1}(\alpha_{\pm n}(x)),e)\epsilon(c(n,\phi^{-1}(x)))g^{n}\psi(\phi^{-1}(x),e)^{-1}.

or simply

(3)

\displaystyle c^{\prime}(n,x)=\psi^{\prime}(\alpha_{n}(x))\epsilon(c(\pm n,\phi^{-1}(x)))g^{\pm n}\psi^{\prime}(x)^{-1}~\mbox{for all $n\in{\mathbb{Z}}$ and $x\in X$,}

where
\psi^{\prime}(x):=\psi(\phi^{-1}(x),e)
.

Now, let
c\equiv e\in F
, the neutral element in
F
, then (
3
) is the same as saying
c^{\prime}
is cohomologous to a group homomorphism
{\mathbb{Z}}\ni n\mapsto g^{\pm n}\in C(F)
.

(iv) Set
c\equiv e
. We are left to construct
\alpha
and
c
such that
\widetilde{\alpha}\overset{conj.}{\not\sim}\widetilde{\alpha}^{\prime}
. It suffices to make sure the above identity (
3
) fails.

Since
C(F)
is trivial, we deduce
g=e
. It suffices to find some
\alpha
and a cocycle
c^{\prime}:{\mathbb{Z}}\times X\to F
(w.r.t.
\alpha
) which is not a coboundary.

By Cauchy theorem, we may find some non-trivial
t\in F
such that
{\mathbb{Z}}/{p{\mathbb{Z}}}\cong\langle t\rangle\subset F
for some prime number
p
. By Lemma
2.3
, it suffices to find some
\alpha
and a continuous cocycle
c^{\prime}:{\mathbb{Z}}\times X\to{\mathbb{Z}}/{p{\mathbb{Z}}}
which is not a coboundary.

We can apply
Proposition
3.1
or Proposition
3.4
to construct a suitable free minimal and weakly mixing or free minimal odometer action
{\mathbb{Z}}\curvearrowright X
and a continuous cocycle
c
which is not a coboundary.
∎

4.2.
Rigidity part

We are ready to prove Theorem
1.3
.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Let
D_{\infty}={\mathbb{Z}}\rtimes\frac{{\mathbb{Z}}}{2{\mathbb{Z}}}=\langle s\rangle\rtimes\langle t\rangle
. Denote by
S
the symmetric generating set
\{s^{\pm},t^{\pm}\}
for
D_{\infty}
. Denote by
|\cdot|_{S}
the word length on
G
with respect to
S
. Note that
|s^{n}t|_{S}=n+1
and
|s^{n}|_{S}=n
. Let
d(\cdot,\cdot)
be the right-invariant word metric on
D_{\infty}
with respect to
S
, i.e.
d(g_{1},g_{2}):=|g_{2}g_{1}^{-1}|_{S}
.

Let
c:D_{\infty}\times X\to D_{\infty}
be the continuous orbit cocycle associated to a given continuous orbit equivalence.

Claim 1:
for all
x\in X
, either
\sup_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}d(c(s^{n},x),s^{n})<\infty
or
\sup_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}d(c(s^{n},x),s^{-n})<\infty
.

Proof of Claim 1.

Define
\pi:{\mathbb{Z}}\to D_{\infty}
by setting
\pi(s^{2n})=s^{n}
and
\pi(s^{2n+1})=ts^{n}
for all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. One can verify that
\pi
is a bi-Lipschitz bijection. In fact,
\frac{|n-m|}{2}\leq d(\pi(s^{n}),\pi(s^{m}))\leq 2|n-m|
for all
n
,
m\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Fix any
x\in X
, write
\phi_{x}(g)=c(g,x)
for all
g\in D_{\infty}
. Define
\phi:{\mathbb{Z}}\to{\mathbb{Z}}
by setting
\phi=\pi^{-1}\circ\phi_{x}\circ\pi
. Clearly,
\phi
is again a bi-Lipschitz bijection by Proposition
2.2
.

From Theorem
2.7
, we deduce that either
\sup_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}|\phi(n)-n|<\infty
or
\sup_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}|\phi(n)+n|<\infty
. This implies Claim 1 holds.
∎

Define
X_{+}=\{x\in X:\sup_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}d(c(s^{n},x),s^{n})<\infty\}
and
X_{-}=\{x\in X:\sup_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}d(c(s^{n},x),s^{-n})<\infty\}
. Clearly,
X_{+}\cap X_{-}=\emptyset
since
s
has infinite order. From Claim 1, we know that
X=X_{+}\sqcup X_{-}
.

Claim 2
: Both
X_{+}
and
X_{-}
are clopen subsets of
X
.

Proof of Claim 2.

We follow the idea while dealing with
{\mathbb{Z}}
-actions in
[
4
]
. For each
r\geq 1
, define
B(r):=\{ts^{i},s^{i}:~|i|\leq r\}\subset D_{\infty}
. Note that
B(r)\nearrow D_{\infty}
as
r\to\infty
.

First, for all
N>0
, there exists
K>0
such that
B(N)\subset c(B(K),x)
for all
x\in X
. Indeed, since
D_{\infty}\ni g\mapsto c(g,x)\in D_{\infty}
is a bijection, we may find
K_{x}>0
such that
B(N)\subset c(B(K_{x}),x)
for all
x\in X
. Moreover, by continuity of
c
, we may find a small open neighborhood
V_{x}\ni x
such that
B(N)\subset c(B(K_{x}),y)
for all
y\in V_{x}
. Since
X=\cup_{x\in X}V_{x}
, compactness of
X
implies we may find a finite subcover
X=\cup_{i=1}^{n}V_{x_{i}}
, set
K=\max_{1\leq i\leq n}K_{x_{i}}
.

Let
N:=\max_{x\in X,g\in S}|c(g,x)|
, and
K
as above for this
N
. Then for all
x\in X
,
either

c(s^{>K},x)\subseteq\{s^{>0},ts^{>0}\}~\mbox{and}~c(s^{<-K},x)\subseteq\{s^{<0},ts^{<0}\}~\mbox{or}

c(s^{>K},x)\subseteq\{s^{<0},ts^{<0}\}~\mbox{and}~c(s^{<-K},x)\subseteq\{s^{>0},ts^{>0}\}.

Here,
c(s^{>K},x):=\{c(s^{i},x):i>K\}
, and
c(s^{-K},x)
is similarly defined;
\{s^{>0},ts^{>0}\}:=\{s^{i},ts^{j}:i>0,~j>0\}
and
\{s^{<0},ts^{<0}\}
is similarly defined.

To see this, for each
|k|>K
, we have
c(s^{k},x)\not\in B(N)
as
c(-,x)
is bijective. Since
|c(s^{\pm 1},s^{k}x)|\leq N
, from

c(s^{\pm 1+k},x)=c(s^{\pm 1},s^{k}x)c(s^{k},x)
, we deduce that
c(s^{\pm 1+k},x)
and
c(s^{k},x)
must have the same sign for the
s
-exponent when writing them as elements in
t{\mathbb{Z}}=t\langle s\rangle
or
{\mathbb{Z}}=\langle s\rangle
. Then apply Claim 1 to see that if
c(s^{>K},x)\subseteq\{s^{>0},ts^{>0}\}
holds, then
x\in X_{+}
and thus
c(s^{-K},x)\subseteq\{s^{<0},ts^{<0}\}
is automatic.

Now, denote by
X_{+}^{\prime}:=\{x\in X:c(s^{>K},x)\subseteq\{s^{>0},ts^{>0}\}~\mbox{and}~c(s^{<-K},x)\subseteq\{s^{<0},ts^{<0}\}\}
and
X_{-}^{\prime}:=\{x\in X:c(s^{>K},x)\subseteq\{s^{<0},ts^{<0}\}~\mbox{and}~c(s^{<-K},x)\subseteq\{s^{>0},ts^{>0}\}\}
.

From above, we have shown
X_{+}^{\prime}\sqcup X_{-}^{\prime}=X=X_{+}\sqcup X_{-}
. It is easy to see that
X_{+}^{\prime}=X_{+}
and
X_{-}^{\prime}=X_{-}
. Hence, to finish the proof, we just need to observe that both
X_{+}^{\prime}
and
X_{-}^{\prime}

Define

by setting

\displaystyle a(s^{n},x)=\begin{cases}c(s^{n},x)s^{-n},&~\mbox{if $x\in X_{+}$}\\
c(s^{n},x)s^{n},&~\mbox{if $x\in X_{-}$}.\end{cases}

Claim 3:

is a continuous cocycle taking finitely many values as

changes.

Proof of Claim 3.

From Claim 1, we know

Fix any

and

. We have

\displaystyle a(s^{m+n},x)=c(s^{m+n},x)s^{-m-n}=c(s^{m},s^{n}x)c(s^{n},x)s^{-n}s^{-m}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}a(s^{m},s^{n}x)s^{m}a(s^{n},x)s^{-m},~&\mbox{if $s^{n}x\in X_{+}$}\\
a(s^{m},s^{n}x)s^{-m}a(s^{n},x)s^{-m},~&\mbox{if $s^{n}x\in X_{-}$}.\end{cases}

Case 1:
s^{n}x\in X_{+}

Since both

is a finite set. Thus, we have

and thus
a(s^{m+n},x)=a(s^{m},s^{n}x)a(s^{n},x)
.

Case 2:
s^{n}x\in X_{-}
.

Since both
a(s^{m+n},x)
and
a(s^{m},s^{n}x)
lie in a finite set as

\{s^{-m}a(s^{n},x)s^{-m}:m\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}
is a finite set. Thus,

. Once again, this implies that

.
To sum up, we have shown that for all

holds for all

n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Similarly, one can show this also holds for all

. We include the details for completeness.

Fix any
x\in X_{-}
and
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. We have

\displaystyle a(s^{m+n},x)=c(s^{m+n},x)s^{m+n}=c(s^{m},s^{n}x)c(s^{n},x)s^{m+n}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}a(s^{m},s^{n}x)s^{m}a(s^{n},x)s^{m},~&\mbox{if $s^{n}x\in X_{+}$}\\
a(s^{m},s^{n}x)s^{-m}a(s^{n},x)s^{m},~&\mbox{if $s^{n}x\in X_{-}$}.\end{cases}

Case i:
s^{n}x\in X_{+}
.

Since

and
a(s^{m},s^{n}x)
take finitely many values as
m
changes, we get that

, which implies that
a(s^{m+n},x)=a(s^{m},s^{n}x)a(s^{n},x)

Case ii:
s^{n}x\in X_{-}
.

This case can be checked similarly.
∎

Define

by

\displaystyle D(x)=\begin{cases}t,&~\mbox{if $x\in X_{-}$}\\
e,&~\mbox{if $x\in X_{+}$.}\end{cases}

From Claim 2, we know

Moreover, one can verify that

for all

.

Indeed, one can check this by discussing four cases depending on

and
s^{n}x\in X_{\pm}

and

By assumption, there exists a finite subset

such that

Thus, the map

{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Take a
{\mathbb{Z}}

Case I:

\displaystyle c(s^{n},x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}L^{\prime}(s^{n}x)^{-1}L^{\prime}(x)s^{n},~&\mbox{if $x\in X_{+}$}\\

Claim 4:

Notice that on the one hand,

\displaystyle c(s^{-n},x)=c(ts^{n}t^{-1},x)=c(t,s^{n}t^{-1}x)c(s^{n},t^{-1}x)c(t^{-1},x)

\displaystyle=c(t,s^{n}t^{-1}x)L^{\prime}(s^{n}t^{-1}x)^{-1}s^{n}L^{\prime}(t^{-1}x)c(t^{-1},x).

Thus,

(4)

\displaystyle s^{-n}(L^{\prime}(x)c(t^{-1},x)^{-1}L^{\prime}(t^{-1}x)^{-1})s^{-n}=L^{\prime}(s^{-n}x)c(t,s^{n}t^{-1}x)L^{\prime}(s^{n}t^{-1}x)^{-1}.

Write

for some

∎

\displaystyle c(s^{n},x)=L^{\prime}(s^{n}x)^{-1}s^{n}L^{\prime}(x),~c(t,x)=L^{\prime}(tx)^{-1}s^{k}tL^{\prime}(x)~\mbox{for all}~n\in{\mathbb{Z}}~\mbox{and all}~x\in X.

Fix any

\displaystyle c(s^{-n},x)

\displaystyle=L^{\prime\prime}(s^{-n}x)^{-1}s^{-n}L^{\prime\prime}(x)

\displaystyle L^{\prime\prime}(x)c(t^{-1},x)^{-1}L^{\prime}(t^{-1}x)^{-1}=L^{\prime\prime}(s^{-n}x)c(t,s^{n}t^{-1}x)L^{\prime}(s^{n}t^{-1}x)^{-1}\in{\mathbb{Z}}t~\mbox{for all $n\in{\mathbb{Z}}$}.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
\alpha,\beta:{\mathbb{Z}}\curvearrowright X_{0}
be two minimal topologically free actions. Let
\tilde{\alpha},\tilde{\beta}:G\curvearrowright X:=G/{{\mathbb{Z}}}\times X_{0}

(1)
\tilde{\alpha}\overset{coe}{\sim}\tilde{\beta}~\mbox{via the identity homeomorphism}\Rightarrow\alpha\overset{coe}{\sim}\beta

(2)
\alpha\overset{coe}{\sim}\beta\Rightarrow\tilde{\alpha}\overset{conj.}{\sim}\tilde{\beta}

Proof.

(1) Let

\tilde{\alpha}

\displaystyle gs{\mathbb{Z}}=c(g,\tilde{x})s{\mathbb{Z}},~~\alpha_{\delta(g,s{\mathbb{Z}})}(x)=\beta_{\delta(c(g,\tilde{x}),s{\mathbb{Z}})}(x),~\forall~g,s\in G,~\forall~x\in X_{0}.

\tilde{x}:=({\mathbb{Z}},x)

\theta

\theta

\displaystyle\theta(s_{1}s_{2},x)=c(s_{1}s_{2},\tilde{x})=c(s_{1},\tilde{\alpha}_{s_{2}}(\tilde{x}))c(s_{2},\tilde{x})

\displaystyle\theta(s_{1},\alpha_{s_{2}}(x))\theta(s_{2},x)=c(s_{1},\widetilde{\alpha_{s_{2}}(x)})c(s_{2},\tilde{x}).

\tilde{\alpha}_{s_{2}}(\tilde{x})=(s_{2}{\mathbb{Z}},\alpha_{\delta(s_{2},{\mathbb{Z}})}x)=({\mathbb{Z}},\alpha_{s_{2}}(x))

\widetilde{\alpha_{s_{2}}(x)}=({\mathbb{Z}},\alpha_{s_{2}}(x))

\displaystyle\alpha_{s}(x)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{\delta(s,{\mathbb{Z}})}(x)=\beta_{\delta(c(s,\tilde{x}),{\mathbb{Z}})}(x)

\displaystyle=\beta_{\theta(s,x)}(x),~\forall~s\in{\mathbb{Z}},~\forall~x\in X_{0}.
