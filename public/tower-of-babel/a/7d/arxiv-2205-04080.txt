Abstract
The purpose of this tutorial is to give a brief introduction to linear quantum control systems. The mathematical model of linear quantum control systems is presented first, then some fundamental control-theoretic notions such as stability, controllability and observability are given, which are closely related to several important concepts in quantum information science such as decoherence-free subsystems, quantum non-demolition variables, and back-action evasion measurements. After that, quantum Gaussian states are introduced, in particular, an information-theoretic uncertainty relation is presented which often gives a better bound for mixed Gaussian states than the well-known Heisenberg uncertainty relation. The quantum Kalman filter is presented for quantum linear systems, which is the quantum analogy of the Kalman filter for classical (namely, non-quantum-mechanical) linear systems. The quantum Kalman canonical decomposition for quantum linear systems is recorded, and its application is illustrated by means of a recent experiment. As single- and multi-photon states are useful resources in quantum information technology, the response of quantum linear systems to these types of input is presented. Finally, coherent feedback control of quantum linear systems is briefly introduced, and a recent experiment is used to demonstrate the effectiveness of quantum linear systems and networks theory.

Introduction

The dynamics of quantum systems are governed by quantum-mechanical laws. The temporal evolution of a quantum system can be described by its state evolution in the Schrödinger picture. Alternatively, it can also be described by the evolution of system variables for example position and momentum of a quantum harmonic oscillator, this is the so-called Heisenberg picture. System variables are operators in a Hilbert space, instead of ordinary functions. Therefore, the operations of these system variables may not commute. Specifically, let
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}
be two system variables (operators),
\mathbf{AB}\neq\mathbf{BA}
may occur. Non-commutativity renders quantum systems fundamentally different from classical systems where system variables are functions of time and two system variables always commute.

Hurwitz stability, controllability and observability are fundamental concepts

\displaystyle\frac{d\langle\mathbf{x}(t)\rangle}{dt}

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{y}(t)\rangle

The quantum linear system (

4.1
An introduction

Define the
displacement operator

\mathbf{D}(\alpha)\triangleq\exp(\breve{\mathbf{a}}^{\dagger}J_{n}\breve{\alpha}),\ \ \forall\alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{n}.

(52)

Define the counterpart of
\breve{\alpha}
in the real domain,

\beta=V_{n}\breve{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n},

(53)

where
V_{n}
is the unitary matrix defined in Eq. (
36
). Then the displacement operator defined in Eq. (
52
) can be re-written as

\mathbf{D}(\alpha)=\exp(\imath\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbb{J}_{n}\beta).

(54)

Given a density matrix
\rho
of a quantum linear system, define its
quantum characteristic function
to be

\chi_{\rho}\triangleq\mathrm{Tr}[\rho\mathbf{D}(\alpha)].

(55)

For Gaussian states, we have,
[
85
, Eq. (3.1)]
,

\chi_{\rho}=\exp\left(-\imath\mu^{\top}\mathbb{J}_{n}\beta-\frac{1}{2}\beta^{\top}\mathbb{V}\beta\right),

(56)

where

\displaystyle\mu=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}[\rho\mathbf{x}]\in\mathbb{R}^{2n},

(57)

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}\{\rho[(\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mu)(\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mu)^{\top}+((\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mu)(\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mu)^{\top}]^{\top}]\}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2n}.

are the mean and covariance, respectively. Define the complex domain counterpart of
\mu
and
\mathbb{V}
to be

\displaystyle\breve{\gamma}\triangleq

\displaystyle V_{n}^{\dagger}\mu,

(58)

\displaystyle\Pi\triangleq

\displaystyle V_{n}^{\dagger}\mathbb{V}V_{n}.

Then the quantum characteristic function in Eq. (
56
) can be re-written as

\chi_{\rho}=\exp\left(-\breve{\gamma}^{\dagger}J_{n}\breve{\alpha}-\frac{1}{2}\breve{\alpha}^{\dagger}\Pi\breve{\alpha}\right),

(59)

Moreover, for
\rho
to be a Gaussian state of a quantum
linear system, it is required that,
[
85
, Theorem 3.1]
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\pm\frac{\imath}{2}\mathbb{J}_{n};

(60)

or equivalently,

\displaystyle\Pi

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\pm\frac{1}{2}J_{n}.

(61)

In terms of the characteristic function
\chi_{\rho}
defined in Eq. (
55
), we can define the
Wigner function
via the multi-dimensional Fourier transform

W_{\rho}(\mathrm{w})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^{2n}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\exp(-\imath\mathrm{w}^{\top}\mathbb{J}\beta)\mathrm{Tr}[\rho\exp(\imath\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbb{J}_{n}\beta)]d\beta,\ \ \forall\mathrm{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}.

(62)

In particular, if
\rho
is a Gaussian state with the characteristic function in Eq. (
56
), the Wigner function is of the form

W_{\rho}(\mathrm{w})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^{2n}\mathrm{det}(\mathbb{V})}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}(\mathrm{w}-\mu)^{\top}\mathbb{V}^{-1}(\mathrm{w}-\mu)\right),

(63)

with the mean
\mu
and covariance matrix
\mathbb{V}
given in Eq. (
57
). In other words, a Gaussian state is uniquely determined by its first and second moments.

Example 4.1

When
n=1
and the system state is the quantum vacuum state
\ket{0}
, then
\rho=\ket{0}\bra{0}
, and

\mu=\mathrm{Tr}(\rho\mathbf{x})=\left[\begin{array}[]{@{}c@{}}0\\
0\end{array}\right],\ \ \mathrm{Tr}(\rho\mathbf{q}^{2})=\mathrm{Tr}(\rho\mathbf{p}^{2})=\frac{1}{2},\ \ \mathrm{Tr}(\rho\mathbf{p}\mathbf{q})=-\frac{\imath}{2}.

(64)

The covariance matrix
\mathbb{V}
in Eq. (
57
) becomes

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left[\begin{array}[]{@{}ll@{}}1&0\\
0&1\end{array}\right].

Thus,

\mathbb{V}\pm\frac{\imath}{2}\mathbb{J}_{n}=\frac{1}{2}\left[\begin{array}[]{@{}ll@{}}1&\pm\imath\\
\mp\imath&1\end{array}\right]\geq 0,

(68)

which verifies Eq. (
60
). Moreover, the Wigner function (
63
) is now

W_{\rho}(w)=\frac{1}{\pi}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}w^{\top}w\right).

(69)

Finally, from Eq. (
64
) we have

\sqrt{\mathrm{Tr}(\rho\mathbf{q}^{2})}\sqrt{\mathrm{Tr}(\rho\mathbf{p}^{2})}=\frac{1}{2}.

(70)

For any state
\rho
and observables
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
, the Heisenberg uncertainty relation is

\sqrt{\mathrm{Tr}(\rho\mathbf{X}^{2})-(\mathrm{Tr}(\rho\mathbf{X}))^{2}}\sqrt{\mathrm{Tr}(\rho\mathbf{Y}^{2})-(\mathrm{Tr}(\rho\mathbf{Y}))^{2}}\geq\frac{1}{2}|\mathrm{Tr}\left(\rho[\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y}]\right)|.

(71)

According to Eq. (
70
), the vacuum state
\ket{0}
saturates the Heisenberg uncertainty relation (
71
) when
\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{q}
and
\mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{p}
. In the literature, states saturating the Heisenberg uncertainty relation (
71
) are often called

. For example, a special type of Gaussian states, coherent states, defined as

\ket{\alpha}\triangleq e^{-\frac{|\alpha|^{2}}{2}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha^{k}}{\sqrt{k!}}\ket{k},\ \ \alpha\in\mathbb{C},

(72)

are minimum uncertainty states as Eq. (
71
) is saturated when
\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{q}
and
\mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{p}
.

Example 4.2

In this example, we show that the vacuum state
\ket{0}
is a Gaussian state. For simplicity, we look at the single-oscillator case (
n=1
). In this case, the displacement operator
\mathbf{D}(\alpha)
defined in Eq. (
52
) becomes

\mathbf{D}(\alpha)=\exp(\alpha\mathbf{a}^{\ast}-\alpha^{\ast}\mathbf{a}).

(73)

If two operators
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
satisfy
[\mathbf{A},[\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}]]=[\mathbf{B},[\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}]]=0
, then the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff formula is

e^{\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{B}}=e^{\mathbf{A}}e^{\mathbf{B}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}[\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}]}=e^{\mathbf{B}}e^{\mathbf{A}}e^{\frac{1}{2}[\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}]}.

(74)

Therefore,

\mathbf{D}(\alpha)=e^{\alpha\mathbf{a}^{\ast}}e^{-\alpha^{\ast}\mathbf{a}}e^{-\frac{|\alpha|^{2}}{2}}.

(75)

As

e^{\alpha\mathbf{a}^{\ast}}e^{-\alpha^{\ast}\mathbf{a}}\ket{0}=e^{\alpha\mathbf{a}^{\ast}}\ket{0}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha^{k}}{\sqrt{k!}}\ket{k},

(76)

we have

\mathbf{D}(\alpha)\ket{0}=e^{-\frac{|\alpha|^{2}}{2}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha^{k}}{\sqrt{k!}}\ket{k}=\ket{\alpha}.

(77)

Consequently, the characteristic function for the vacuum state
\ket{0}
is

\chi=\braket{0|\mathbf{D}(\alpha)|0}=e^{-\frac{|\alpha|^{2}}{2}},

(78)

which is of the form Eq. (
59
) with

\gamma=0,\ \ \Pi=\frac{1}{2}\left[\begin{array}[]{@{}ll@{}}1&0\\
0&1\end{array}\right].

(79)

Clearly,
\Pi
derived above satisfies the inequality (
61
), hence the vacuum state
\ket{0}
is a Gaussian state.

In
[
92
]
,
[
9
, Section 2.7]
and
[
64
]
, another form of
characteristic functions is defined for a Gaussian system state
\rho
,
which is

\mathrm{Tr}[\rho\exp(i\breve{z}^{\dagger}\breve{\mathbf{a}})]=\exp(\imath\breve{z}^{\dagger}\breve{\beta}-\frac{1}{2}\breve{z}^{\dagger}\Sigma\breve{z}),~~\forall z\in\mathbb{C}^{n},

(80)

where
\breve{\gamma}=\mathrm{Tr}[\rho\breve{\mathbf{a}}]
, and

\Sigma=\mathrm{Tr}[\rho(\breve{\mathbf{a}}-\breve{\gamma})(\breve{\mathbf{a}}-\breve{\gamma})^{\dagger}]
is a non-negative Hermitian matrix.
In general,
\Sigma
has the form

\Sigma=\left[\begin{array}[]{@{}cc@{}}I_{n}+N^{T}&M\\
M^{\dagger}&N\end{array}\right].

(81)

In particular, the
ground
or
vacuum
state
\ket{0}
is specified by
\gamma=0
and
\Sigma=\left[\begin{array}[]{@{}cc@{}}I_{n}&0\\
0&0_{n}\end{array}\right]
. Clearly,

\frac{1}{2}\Sigma\geq\frac{\imath}{2}J_{n}.

(82)

However, the following is not true:

\frac{1}{2}\Sigma\geq-\frac{\imath}{2}J_{n}.

(83)

Consequently, to be consistent with
\mathbb{V}
for the real quadrature operator representation, it is better to use the covariance matrix
\Pi
given in Eq. (
58
), instead of
\Sigma
in Eq. (

A Gaussian state is a

\mathbb{S}\mathbb{J}_{n}\mathbb{S}^{\top}=\mathbb{J}_{n}

is symplectic (See the
Notation

4.3
Skew information and information-theoretic uncertainty relation

In addition to Heisenberg’s uncertainty relation (
71
), an
information-theoretic uncertainty relation was proposed in
[
100
]
based
on skew information. In what follows, we use the notation in
[
100
]
.
Given a density operator
\rho
and an observable
\mathbf{X}
, the
Wigner–Yanase skew information
[
101
]
is defined as

I(\rho,\mathbf{X})\triangleq-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}([\sqrt{\rho},\mathbf{X}]^{2}).

(88)

Skew information was originally defined for Hamiltonians of closed (namely,
isolated) quantum systems
[
101
]
, and was later generalized to arbitrary
observables of open quantum systems. Roughly speaking,
I(\rho,\mathbf{X})
measures
the quantum uncertainty of the observable
\mathbf{X}
with respect to the
density operator
\rho
.

The variance of
\mathbf{X}
with respect to the density operator
\rho
is

V(\rho,\mathbf{X})=\mathrm{Tr}(\rho\mathbf{X}^{2})-(\mathrm{Tr}(\rho\mathbf{X}))^{2}.

(89)

When the state is pure, it is easy to see that
I(\rho,\mathbf{X})=V(\rho,\mathbf{X})
. However,

I(\rho,\mathbf{X})\leq V(\rho,\mathbf{X})
when the state is a mixed one. To quantify quantum uncertainty,
the following quantity has been defined in
[
100
]
:

U(\rho,\mathbf{X})\triangleq\sqrt{V^{2}(\rho,\mathbf{X})-(V(\rho,\mathbf{X})-I(\rho,\mathbf{X}))^{2}}.

(90)

The information-theoretic uncertainty relation is
[
100
, Eq. (2)]

U(\rho,\mathbf{X})U(\rho,\mathbf{Y})\geq\frac{1}{4}|\mathrm{Tr}(\rho[\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y}])|^{2}.

(91)

Interestingly, it is proved in
[
102
]
that when
n=1
for the
single-mode oscillator case, all Gaussian states, pure or mixed, are minimum
uncertainty states, i.e., states that saturate (
91
). On
the other hand, minimum uncertainty states are Gaussian states. However, in
general mixed Gaussian states do not saturate the Heisenberg’s uncertainty
relation (
71
). In this sense, the information-theoretic
uncertainty relation (
91
) better characterizes Gaussian
states. More studies on quantum skew-information and information-theoretic
uncertainty relation can be found in,
e.g.,
[
103
,
104
,
105
,
106
,
107
,
108
,
109
,
110
,

,

,

,
102
]

and references therein.

7.1
Continuous-mode single-photon states

In this subsection, we introduce continuous-mode single-photon states of a free traveling light field.

We look at the single-channel (

\ket{1_{t}}=\mathbf{b}_{\rm in}^{\ast}(t)\ket{\Phi_{0}}

\braket{1_{\xi}|1_{\xi}}=1

\ket{1_{\omega}}\triangleq\mathbf{b}_{\rm in}^{\ast}[i\omega]\ket{0}

\ket{1_{\xi}}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\xi[i\omega]\ket{1_{\omega}}d\omega.

(212)

Remark 7.1

Notice

\displaystyle\langle 1_{\xi}|d\mathbf{B}_{\rm in}^{\ast}(t)d\mathbf{B}_{\rm in}(t)|1_{\xi}\rangle

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}drd\tau\xi(r)^{\ast}\xi(\tau)\int_{t}^{t+dt}\int_{t}^{t+dt}dt_{1}dt_{2}\langle\Phi_{0}|\mathbf{b}_{\rm in}(r)\mathbf{b}_{\rm in}^{\ast}(t_{1})\mathbf{b}_{\rm in}(t_{2})\mathbf{b}_{\rm in}^{\ast}(\tau)\Phi_{0}\rangle

9
Conclusion

In this tutorial, we have given a concise introduction to linear quantum systems, for example, their mathematical models, relation between their control-theoretic properties and physical properties, Gaussian states, quantum Kalman filter, Kalman canonical form, and response to continuous-mode single-photon states. Several simple examples are designed to demonstrate some fundamental properties of linear quantum systems. Pointers to more detailed discussions are given in various places. It is hoped that this tutorial is helpful to researchers in the control community who are interested in quantum control of dynamical systems. Finally, an information-theoretic uncertainty relation has been recorded in this tutorial, which describes uncertainties of mixed quantum Gaussian states better than the Heisenberg uncertainty relation. It is an open question whether this uncertainty relation is useful for mixed quantum Gaussian state engineering.
