Efficiency and Robustness of Rosenbaum’s Regression (Un)-Adjusted Rank-based Estimator in Randomized Experiments
Aditya Ghosh, Nabarun Deb, Bikram Karmakar, Bodhisattva Sen

Abstract
Mean-based estimators of the causal effect in a completely randomized experiment may behave poorly if the potential outcomes have a heavy-tail, or contain outliers. We study an alternative estimator by Rosenbaum that estimates the constant additive treatment effect by inverting a randomization test using ranks. By investigating the breakdown point and asymptotic relative efficiency of this rank-based estimator, we show that it is provably robust against outliers and heavy-tailed potential outcomes, and has asymptotic variance at most 1.16 times that of the difference-in-means estimator (and much smaller when the potential outcomes are not light-tailed). We further derive a consistent estimator of the asymptotic standard error for Rosenbaum’s estimator which yields a readily computable confidence interval for the treatment effect. We also study a regression adjusted version of Rosenbaum’s estimator to incorporate additional covariate information in randomization inference. We prove gain in efficiency by this regression adjustment method under a linear regression model. We illustrate through synthetic and real data that, unlike the mean-based estimators, these rank-based estimators (both unadjusted or regression adjusted) are efficient and robust against heavy-tailed distributions, contamination, and model misspecification. Finally, we initiate the study of Rosenbaum’s estimator when the constant treatment effect assumption may be violated.



Keywords:

Breakdown point; Causal inference; Covariate adjustment; Hodges-Lehmann 0.864 lower bound; Local asymptotic normality; Randomization inference; Wilcoxon rank-sum statistic.

1
Introduction

When a treatment is randomly assigned to the units in a study,
Fisher 1935
showed that this randomization can be used to provide valid statistical inference about the treatment effect without making strong assumptions regarding the outcome-generating model. In particular, we can calculate confidence intervals for the treatment effect based on an estimator using its randomization distribution. A standard choice for the estimator is the difference-in-means estimator, which takes the difference of the averages of outcomes for the treated and control units. Conveniently, the usual large sample confidence interval based on two independent samples from infinite populations for the difference in two population means with unequal variances gives an asymptotically valid, conservative confidence
interval under the randomization distribution
(
Splawa-Neyman 1990
;
Freedman
et al. 2007
)
.
Freedman 2008a
establishes its validity under a finite limiting fourth moment assumption on the sequence of potential outcomes, and
Li and Ding 2017
relax the assumption to any finite limiting moment of more than two; also see
Lin 2013
and
Aronow
et al. 2014
for further developments.

Despite these advances, this mean-based confidence interval can be very wide, with volatile standard errors, when the potential outcomes are heavy-tailed, have a few extreme values, or contaminated by outliers.

Athey et al. 2021
note that in many modern randomized experiments, including in experiments in the digital space, heavy-tailed or extreme outcomes are often likely. An important class of estimators used in practice in such situations use the
ranks
of the potential outcomes as opposed to their exact values (see e.g.,
Imbens and
Rubin 2015
). In particular,
Rosenbaum 1993
proposed to use a Hodges-Lehmann type point estimate
(
Hodges and
Lehmann 1963
)
based on the Wilcoxon rank-sum (abbreviated as WRS) test statistic
(
Wilcoxon 1945
)
for estimating a constant additive treatment effect under randomization inference. The constant treatment effect assumption is often a convenient starting point in answering a causal question
(
Rosenbaum 2002a
;
Ho and Imai 2006
;
Athey et al. 2021
)
. Futher, in some situations, the identification of a constant treatment effect has immediate practical use
(see
Rosenbaum 2002b
, Section 2.4.5)
.

In this paper, we undertake a systematic and rigorous study of the robustness and efficiency properties of Rosenbaum’s estimator based on the WRS test statistic,
under randomization and the constant treatment effect assumption. Our asymptotic results have important implications. First, we illustrate that this rank-based estimator has a positive asymptotic breakdown point of at least
1-2^{-1/2}\approx 0.29
, indicating that over 29% of potential outcomes must be corrupt to make the estimator arbitrarily large, quantifying its robustness against outliers. Second, we show that, under suitable assumptions, the asymptotic variance of Rosenbaum’s estimator is always at most 125/108 (
\approx
1.16) times that of the difference-in-means estimator, and is often much smaller (see
Section

2.4
for a detailed discussion). We also provide an analytically computable, asymptotically valid confidence interval for the treatment effect based on this rank-based estimator, which is more robust to heavy-tailed potential outcomes than the mean-based confidence intervals.

Next we consider randomization inference for the treatment effect with regression adjustment. In modern randomized experiments it is increasingly common to collect additional data on covariates. This covariate information is typically used with an ANCOVA model, aiming to improve the unadjusted difference-in-means type estimator
(
Fisher 1935
;
Cox and Reid 2000
;
Freedman 2008a
;
Freedman 2008b
)
.
Lin 2013
showed that including the interaction of the treatment indicator and covariates in the model results in a regression adjusted estimator which is asymptotically at least as efficient as the unadjusted difference-in-means estimator.
Recently, regression adjusted or model assisted estimators have been devised for various study designs
(
Fogarty 2018
;
Li and Ding 2020
;
Liu and Yang 2020
;
Su and Ding 2021
;
Zhao and
Ding 2022
)
. But, these methods based on linear models which typically use robust standard errors
(
Huber 1967
;
White 1980
)
, are still sensitive to heavy-tailed distributions or extreme values of the potential outcomes
(
MacKinnon and
White 1985
;
Young 2018
)
.

We study the asymptotic properties of a regression adjusted version of Rosenbaum’s rank-based estimator, originally suggested by
Rosenbaum 2002a
for observational studies.
To calculate this estimator, we first regress the control potential outcomes under the hypothesis of a specified treatment effect on the covariates using least squares. Then, we calculate the WRS statistic using the residuals of this regression. Finally, we invert this test statistic to compute the estimator. Unlike some of the regression adjustment methods cited above, we do not use the treatment indicator in the regression model; an approach advocated by
Tukey 1993
(also see
Gail
et al. 1988
). Our simulation studies and data analyses illustrate notable gains in efficiency for this regression adjustment method over its unadjusted counterpart when the potential outcomes are not light-tailed.

1.1
Summary of our contributions

All the theoretical results in this paper are derived under the finite population asymptotics framework where the only source of randomization is the completely randomized assignment of the treatment; the potential outcomes are considered fixed.
We formally define Rosenbaum’s rank-based estimator without regression adjustment in (
2.12
), and study its asymptotic properties in Section
2
, under the constant additive treatment effect model (see
Rosenbaum 2002b
). In particular, we illustrate the robustness of Rosenbaum’s rank-based estimator against extreme potential outcomes or contamination using the concept of breakdown point (see
Proposition

2.2
). Next,
Theorem

2.2
gives the asymptotic distribution of this estimator under a completely randomized design as the sample size grows to infinity, under a mild regularity condition, namely
1
(which holds for many heavy-tailed distributions used in practice; see
Remark

2.2
for a detailed discussion). Further,
Corollary

2.1
gives a readily computable, asymptotically valid confidence interval for the treatment effect.

Our proof of
Theorem

2.2
is novel and does not follow from existing results in the literature. As Rosenbaum’s rank-based estimator is computed by (essentially) equating the WRS statistic for the adjusted responses to its expected null value under randomization (see (
2.10
)), following
Hodges and
Lehmann 1963
we require the asymptotic distribution of this statistic under a sequence of
local alternatives
to study the asymptotic properties of this solution. The analysis is complicated by a number of reasons. First, under a completely randomized assignment, the observed outcomes of different units are neither independent nor identically distributed. Second, to study the behavior of the WRS statistic under local alternatives under randomization inference, the classical results from Le Cam’s local asymptotic normality theory (see
van der
Vaart 1998
for details) do not apply.
Recently,
Li and Ding 2017
have established a few useful asymptotic finite population central limit theorems, including the null distribution of the Wilcoxon statistic; see
Proposition

2.1
and the accompanying discussion. Some of the results in
Li and Ding 2017
have been generalized and used by
Li et al. 2018
,
Lei and Ding 2021
and
Wu and Ding 2021
. However, these papers only consider statistics that are linear in the observations, and their results do not apply to rank-based methods (which are highly non-linear). Thus, our first technical innovation in
Section

2
is deriving the local asymptotic normality of the Wilcoxon statistic; see
Theorem

2.1
.

Next, we establish in
Theorem

2.3
the asymptotic efficiency of Rosenbaum’s estimator relative to the difference-in-means estimator (note that both of these estimators target the same estimand under the constant additive treatment effect model). We note, in particular, that when the potential outcomes are realizations from a normal distribution,
Rosenbaum’s estimator has about 95% asymptotic relative efficiency compared to the difference-in-means estimator (which is most efficient in this case). For potential outcome distributions that are not light-tailed, the efficiency gain is often substantial, see
Table

2.1
. In fact, this result is parallel to the lower bound due to
Hodges and
Lehmann 1956
on the asymptotic efficiency of the WRS test relative to the Student’s
t
-test under an infinite population model.

In
Section

3
, we define our regression adjusted rank-based estimator and study its asymptotic properties. In particular,
Theorem

3.3
gives the asymptotic distribution of our regression adjusted rank-based estimator.
Previously, the asymptotic distributions of regression adjusted difference-in-means type estimators were derived under different randomization strategies
(
Fogarty 2018
;
Li and Ding 2020
;
Liu and Yang 2020
;
Su and Ding 2021
;
Zhao and
Ding 2022
)
. These results build on the results of
Li and Ding 2017
and do not apply to rank-based estimators, as already discussed above.
We prove
Theorem

3.3
by deriving the local asymptotic behavior of the regression adjusted rank statistic, which is provided in
Theorem

3.2
. The proof of this theorem is more involved than the proof of the analogous result in the unadjusted case (i.e.,
Theorem

2.2
); we refer the reader to
Appendix

B
for a summary of our proof techniques. We also illustrate that the regression-adjusted estimator has lower limiting variance than its unadjusted counterpart under a correctly specified linear model; see
Theorem

3.4
.

We evaluate the empirical performance of our rank-based confidence intervals through two real applications — one examining the electoral impact of
Progresa
, Mexico’s conditional cash transfer program
(
De La O 2013
;
Imai 2018
)
, and another focused on housing prices
(
Linden and
Rockoff 2008
)
. Additionally, we present extensive simulation studies in
Section

5
. Our simulation studies and data analyses demonstrate that
the performance of the rank-based estimators is never substantially worse than the ANOVA-based estimators, and is considerably better when the potential outcomes are not light-tailed. Moreover, the regression adjusted estimator provides shorter confidence intervals than the unadjusted estimator.

While the main focus of this paper is on the study of Rosenbaum’s estimator(s) under the assumption of constant treatment effect, it is natural to ask how the estimator behaves when this assumption does not hold. In
Section

6
, we initiate a study of this problem by deriving a weak limit of Rosenbaum’s unadjusted estimator without any assumption on the treatment effect; see
Theorem

6.1
. This leads us to a novel estimand of the treatment effect in the context of randomized trials which is robust to outliers and contamination.

The proofs of our main results, additional technical results and discussions are relegated to
Appendices

C
,
D
,
E
and
F
. Implementation of our proposed methods in
R
are available from
https://github.com/ghoshadi/RRE
.

2
Inference without regression adjustment

We assume throughout that the treatment group is formed by choosing
m=m(N)
out of the
N
subjects in a randomized trial by
simple random sampling without replacement
(SRSWOR). We work in the Neyman-Rubin potential outcomes framework
(
Splawa-Neyman 1990
;
Rubin 1974
;
Rubin 1977
;
Imbens and
Rubin 2015
)
. For the
i
-th subject,
let
a_{i}
and
b_{i}
denote the potential outcomes under the treatment and control respectively; the observed response
Y_{i}
is given by

(2.1)

Y_{i}=Z_{i}a_{i}+(1-Z_{i})b_{i},

where
Z_{i}
equals
1
if the
i
-th subject is assigned to the treatment group, and
0
otherwise. In our model
Z_{i}
is the only source of randomness in
Y_{i}
; we assume throughout this paper that
\{a_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
and
\{b_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
are fixed constants. We also make the assumption of a
constant additive treatment effect

(
Rosenbaum 1993
;
Rosenbaum 2002a
;
Athey et al. 2021
)
, i.e.,

(2.2)

a_{i}-b_{i}=\tau,\qquad\text{for each}\quad 1\leq i\leq N,

for some unknown real number
\tau
. We are interested in point estimation and confidence interval for the constant treatment effect
\tau
. In the sequel we will denote vectors by boldface letters, e.g.,
\bm{Z}:=(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{N})^{\top}
,

\bm{b}:=(b_{1},\dots,b_{N})^{\top}
and so forth. To formally state our asymptotic results as the sample size
N
grows to infinity, we will use an additional index
N
in our notation for the later sections. For example, the potential outcomes will be denoted by
\{(a_{N,j},b_{N,j})\}_{1\leq j\leq N}
, the vector of treatment indicators by
\bm{Z}_{N}=(Z_{N,1},\dots,Z_{N,N})^{\top}
, and so on.

We estimate
\tau
by inverting a testing problem. Towards that, consider the problem of testing

(2.3)

H_{0}:\tau=\tau_{0}\qquad\mathrm{versus}\qquad H_{1}:\tau\neq\tau_{0}.

When
H_{0}
is true, the vector
\bm{Y}-\tau_{0}\bm{Z}
, called the vector of
adjusted responses
, equals
\bm{b}
, which is non-random. Hence any statistic
t(\bm{Z},\bm{Y}-\tau_{0}\bm{Z})
, which is a function of the treatment indicators and the adjusted responses, can be used to make randomization inference about
H_{0}
, since the null distribution of
t(\bm{Z},\bm{Y}-\tau_{0}\bm{Z})=t(\bm{Z},\bm{b})
is completely specified by the randomization distribution of
\bm{Z}
.
For example, one can consider the difference-in-means test statistic:

(2.4)

t_{D}(\bm{Z},\bm{v}):=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i:Z_{i}=1}v_{i}-\frac{1}{N-m}\sum_{i:Z_{i}=0}v_{i},

where
\bm{v}
is a vector of observations of length
N
, and
m=\sum_{i=1}^{N}Z_{i}
.
Mean-based test statistics like (
2.4
) above are often not robust to heavy-tailed potential outcomes or the presence of contaminations/outliers.
On the other hand, test statistics that use the
ranks
of the potential outcomes, as opposed to their exact values, are in general less sensitive to thick-tailed or skewed distributions and hence can lead to more powerful tests (see, e.g.,
Imbens and
Rubin 2015
).
There are various popular choices for the rank-based statistic
t(\cdot,\cdot)
;
Rosenbaum 1993
recommended using the WRS statistic
(
Wilcoxon 1945
)
, defined as

(2.5)

t(\bm{Z},\bm{Y}-\tau_{0}\bm{Z}):=\bm{Z}^{\top}\bm{q}^{(\tau_{0})}=\sum_{j:Z_{j}=1}q_{j}^{(\tau_{0})},

where
q_{j}^{(\tau_{0})}
is the rank of
Y_{j}-\tau_{0}Z_{j}
among
\{Y_{i}-\tau_{0}Z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
. Considering the possibility of ties in the data, we take the following definition for the ranks, known as
up-ranks
:

(2.6)

q_{j}^{(\tau_{0})}:=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{1}{\left\{Y_{i}-\tau_{0}Z_{i}\leq Y_{j}-\tau_{0}Z_{j}\right\}},\qquad\mbox{for }\;1\leq j\leq N.

Note, under
H_{0}:\tau=\tau_{0}
, the ranks
\{q_{j}^{(\tau_{0})}\}_{j=1}^{N}
can equivalently be written as

(2.7)

q_{j}^{(\tau_{0})}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{1}{\left\{b_{i}\leq b_{j}\right\}},\quad 1\leq j\leq N.

We present the asymptotic null distribution of the WRS statistic in the following result, which is essentially an adaptation of
Li and Ding 2017
.

Proposition 2.1

(Asymptotic null distribution of
t_{N}
)
.

Let
t_{N}:=t(\bm{Z}_{N},\bm{Y}_{N}-\tau_{0}\bm{Z}_{N})
be the WRS statistic for a sample of size
N
, with
t(\cdot,\cdot)
as defined in (
2.5
). Assume that
{m}/{N}\to\lambda\in(0,1)
as
N\to\infty
, and that none of the ranks in (
2.7
) dominates all the others, that is,

(2.8)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\max_{1\leq j\leq N}\left(q_{j}^{(\tau_{0})}-\overline{q}_{N}^{(\tau_{0})}\right)^{2}}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(q_{j}^{(\tau_{0})}-\overline{q}_{N}^{(\tau_{0})}\right)^{2}}=0,

where
\overline{q}_{N}^{(\tau_{0})}:=N^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N}q_{j}^{(\tau_{0})}
.
Then, under
\tau=\tau_{0}
,

(2.9)

N^{-3/2}\left(t_{N}-m\overline{q}_{N}^{(\tau_{0})}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{N}\left(0,\frac{\lambda(1-\lambda)}{12}\right).

The limiting distribution in (
2.9
) is identical to the asymptotic null distribution of the WRS statistic under the infinite population framework
(
Lehmann 1975
, Section 1.3)
.
Proposition

2.1
thus justifies Rosenbaum’s proposal (see
Rosenbaum 2002b
) of deriving a confidence interval for
\tau
by numerically solving for the hypothesized treatment effects that equate the
standardized
WRS statistic to the
\alpha/2
and
(1-\alpha/2)
quantiles of the standard normal distribution.
However, Rosenbaum’s proposal of constructing confidence sets does not provide much insight about the efficiency of this rank-based approach over the mean-based approach. In the subsequent sections, we develop the theory for a point estimator
\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}}
of
\tau
based on the WRS statistic, and provide a readily computable, asymptotically valid Wald-type confidence interval that makes the efficiency and robustness properties of the rank-based method transparent.

Remark 2.1

(Choice of the tie-breaking method)
.

Instead of using up-ranks, one can also break ties in the control potential outcomes by (i) comparing indices, (ii) randomization, or (iii) using average ranks. Since the ranks for (i) or (ii) form a permutation of
\{1,2,\dots,N\}
, they satisfy (
2.8
), and thus (
2.9
) holds without any assumption. For (iii), the asymptotic null distribution in (
2.9
) holds as long as these ties do not occur in large blocks; see
Lemma

F.1
in
Appendix

F
for a precise result in this direction.

2.1
Rosenbaum’s estimator based on the WRS statistic

To derive a point estimator for
\tau
,
Rosenbaum 2002a
suggested inverting the testing problem (
2.3
) by equating the test statistic to its expectation under the randomization distribution and solving for the hypothesized treatment effect. Thus, the estimator
\widehat{\tau}
advocated by
Rosenbaum 2002a
is a solution of the equation

(2.10)

t(\bm{Z},\bm{Y}-\widehat{\tau}\bm{Z})=\mathbb{E}_{\tau_{0}}t(\bm{Z},\bm{Y}-\tau_{0}\bm{Z}),

where
\mathbb{E}_{\tau_{0}}
denotes the expectation under
\tau=\tau_{0}
.
Note that
\mathbb{E}_{\tau_{0}}t(\bm{Z},\bm{Y}-\tau_{0}\bm{Z})=\mathbb{E}_{\tau_{0}}t(\bm{Z},\bm{b})
, which does not depend on
\tau_{0}
and can be explicitly evaluated for typical choices of
t(\cdot,\cdot)
, as illustrated below.

Example 1
.

Consider the difference-in-means test statistic
t_{D}(\cdot,\cdot)
defined in (
2.4
).
Observing that
Z_{i}Y_{i}=Z_{i}a_{i}
,
(1-Z_{i})Y_{i}=(1-Z_{i})b_{i}
, and
\mathbb{E}Z_{i}=m/N
, we deduce that
\mathbb{E}_{\tau_{0}}t_{D}(\bm{Z},\bm{Y}-\tau_{0}\bm{Z})=0
. Consequently, the solution to (
2.10
) for
\widehat{\tau}
based on the difference-in-means statistic is given by

(2.11)

\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{dm}}:=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{N}Z_{i}Y_{i}-\frac{1}{N-m}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(1-Z_{i})Y_{i},

which is the difference-in-means estimator.

Next we define Rosenbaum’s estimator based on the WRS statistic
t(\cdot,\cdot)
defined in (
2.5
). Assume for the moment that ties are not present in the ranks, so that the right hand side of (
2.10
) becomes

\mu:=\mathbb{E}_{\tau_{0}}t(\bm{Z},\bm{Y}-\tau_{0}\bm{Z})=\mathbb{E}_{\tau_{0}}\bm{Z}^{\top}\bm{q}^{(\tau_{0})}=\frac{m}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}q^{(\tau_{0})}_{j}=\frac{m(N+1)}{2}.

Considering the possibility that (
2.10
) may not have an exact solution (e.g., if
m(N+1)
is odd),
Rosenbaum 2002b
suggested modifying the definition of
\widehat{\tau}
based on the WRS statistic, in the same style as in
Hodges and
Lehmann 1963
, as follows. Define

(2.12)

\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}}:=\frac{1}{2}\left(\sup\left\{\tau^{\prime}:t(\bm{Z},\bm{Y}-\tau^{\prime}\bm{Z})>\mu\right\}+\inf\left\{\tau^{\prime}:t(\bm{Z},\bm{Y}-\tau^{\prime}\bm{Z})<\mu\right\}\right).

We show in the following section that
\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}}
is robust to outliers (unlike the difference-in-means estimator
\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{dm}}
defined in (
2.11
)), and study its asymptotic efficiency relative to
\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{dm}}
in
Section

2.4
.

2.2
Robustness of Rosenbaum’s estimator

In classical nonparametrics, a natural way to quantify the robustness of an estimator is via its (asymptotic) breakdown point (see, e.g.,
Hettmansperger and
McKean 2011
).
As in our setting the indices corresponding to the treatment and control groups are not deterministic but are rather chosen by randomization,
we define the (asymptotic) breakdown point of an estimator of the constant treatment effect
\tau
in the following fashion.

Definition 1

(Breakdown point)
.

Consider any estimator
\widehat{\tau}=\widehat{\tau}(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{N};Z_{1},\dots,Z_{N})
of the constant treatment effect
\tau
. The finite sample breakdown point of
\widehat{\tau}
is defined as:

(2.13)

\mathrm{BP}_{N}(\widehat{\tau}):=\frac{1}{N}\min\bigg\{1\leq k\leq N:\forall\ z_{1},\dots,z_{N}\in\{0,1\}\text{ such that }\sum_{i=1}^{N}z_{i}=m,\ \\
\min_{1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}<\dots<i_{k}\leq N}\sup_{y_{i_{1}},\dots,y_{i_{k}}\in\mathbb{R}}|\widehat{\tau}(y_{1},\dots,y_{N};z_{1},\dots,z_{N})|=\infty\bigg\}.

Further, the asymptotic breakdown point of
\widehat{\tau}
is defined as
\mathrm{ABP}(\widehat{\tau}):=\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathrm{BP}_{N}(\widehat{\tau})
.

Intuitively, the above notion of breakdown point formalizes the following question: “What is the minimum proportion of responses that, if replaced with arbitrarily extreme values, will cause the estimator to be arbitrarily large (in absolute value), for all configurations of treatment assignments?” Note the emphasis on the phrase “for all configurations of treatment assignments”, which is reflected in the minimum taken over
i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{k}
in (
2.13
). Another alternative would have been to replace the minimum by a maximum in (
2.13
), which leads to a different and more adversarial notion of breakdown point. Also note that when we are taking the supremum in (
2.13
) over the responses, we are changing both the potential outcomes, keeping the assumption of constant additive treatment effect intact.

It is easy to see that changing a single response arbitrarily can lead to
\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{dm}}=\pm\infty
irrespective of the treatment assignment, implying that
\text{BP}_{N}(\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{dm}})=1/N
and consequently
\mathrm{ABP}(\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{dm}})=0
. In contrast, the following result illustrates that the asymptotic breakdown point of
\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}}
is strictly positive for all values of the proportion
\lambda\approx m/N
.

Proposition 2.2

(Asymptotic breakdown point of
\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}}
)
.

Let
\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}}
be the Rosenbaum’s estimator based on the WRS statistic, as defined in (
2.12
). Assume that
m/N\to\lambda\in[0,1]
, where
m
is the size of the treatment group. Then the asymptotic breakdown point of
\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}}
is given by

\mathrm{ABP}(\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}})=\begin{cases}(1-\lambda)/2&\text{if }\lambda<1/3\\
1-\sqrt{2\lambda(1-\lambda)}&\text{if }1/3\leq\lambda\leq 2/3\\
\lambda/2&\text{if }\lambda>2/3.\end{cases}

It is well-known (see, for instance,
Hettmansperger and
McKean 2011
) that the asymptotic breakdown point of the one sample Hodges-Lehmann location estimator (
Hodges and
Lehmann 1963
) is
1-2^{-1/2}\approx 0.29
.
Proposition

2.2
illustrates that the asymptotic breakdown point of
\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}}
is never less than
1-2^{-1/2}
. In words, irrespective of the relative size of the treatment group, more than
29\%
of the responses need to be altered to force
\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}}
to be arbitrarily large for any treatment assignment.

2.3
Asymptotic distribution of Rosenbaum’s estimator

In this section, we establish the asymptotic distribution of Rosenbaum’s estimator
\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}}
defined in (
2.12
) based on the WRS statistic introduced in (
2.5
). For notational clarity, we index vectors and matrices by subscript
N
(the total sample size).
Although the unadjusted estimator
\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}}
can be expressed as the median of the pairwise differences between the responses from the treatment and the control pool, we
follow the strategy of
Hodges and
Lehmann 1963
that works in more generality (in particular, for the regression adjustment case that we study later).
Before presenting our main results, we briefly
discuss this method of obtaining the asymptotic distribution of
\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}}
from that of the test statistic
t_{N}:=t(\bm{Z}_{N},\bm{Y}_{N}-\tau_{0}\bm{Z}_{N})
. We consider a sequence of local alternatives
\tau_{N}:=\tau_{0}-hN^{-1/2}
for the testing problem (
2.3
), where
h\in\mathbb{R}
is fixed.
We show in
Lemma

D.1
in
Appendix

D
that if

(2.14)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{P}_{\tau_{N}}\left(N^{-3/2}(t_{N}-\mathbb{E}_{\tau_{0}}t_{N})\leq x\right)=\Phi\left(\frac{x+hB}{A}\right),\quad\text{ for every }\;x\in\mathbb{R},

where
\mathbb{P}_{\tau_{N}}
denotes the law under
\tau=\tau_{N}
,
\Phi(\cdot)
is the standard normal distribution function, and
A,B
are positive constants free of
\tau_{0}
and
h
, then

\sqrt{N}(\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}}-\tau)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{N}(0,A^{2}/B^{2}).

In view of the above, it suffices to establish (
2.14
) for the WRS statistic
t_{N}:=t(\bm{Z}_{N},\bm{Y}_{N}-\tau_{0}\bm{Z}_{N})
, with
t(\cdot,\cdot)
as defined in (
2.5
). Although
Li and Ding 2017
give the asymptotic null distribution of
t_{N}
, their proof technique does not work for finding the asymptotic distribution of
t_{N}
under local alternatives. The classical results from Le Cam’s local asymptotic normality theory (cf.
van der
Vaart 1998
) also do not apply in our fixed design setting.

We present the asymptotic distribution of
t_{N}
under a sequence of local alternatives in
Theorem

2.1
below, and this is indeed one of our major contributions. To concisely state an assumption we need here, we introduce the following notation.
Define, for any
h,x\in\mathbb{R}
,

(2.15)

I_{h,N}(x):=\begin{cases}\bm{1}{\left\{0\leq x<hN^{-1/2}\right\}}&\text{if }h\geq 0,\\
-\bm{1}{\left\{hN^{-1/2}\leq x<0\right\}}&\text{if }h<0,\end{cases}

where
\bm{1}{\left\{\cdot\right\}}
denotes the indicator of a set.

Assumption 1
.

Let
\{b_{N,j}:1\leq j\leq N\}
be the potential control outcomes. We assume that, for
I_{h,N}
as in (
2.15
), the following holds:

(2.16)

\lim_{N\to\infty}N^{-3/2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}I_{h,N}(b_{N,j}-b_{N,i})=h\mathcal{I}_{b},

for some fixed constant
\mathcal{I}_{b}\in(0,\infty)
and for every
h\in\mathbb{R}
.

Remark 2.2

(On
1
)
.

Intuitively,
1
says that the proportion of the pairwise differences
(b_{N,j}-b_{N,i})
falling into small intervals (shrinking at the rate of
N^{-1/2}
) scales with the lengths of those intervals. Put differently, it essentially says that the potential outcomes behave, in the limit, as if they were drawn from an absolutely continuous distribution. Indeed, if
b_{N,j}
’s are realizations from a distribution with density
f_{b}(\cdot)
that satisfies
\int_{\mathbb{R}}f_{b}^{2}(x)dx<\infty
, then
1
holds almost surely with
\mathcal{I}_{b}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f_{b}^{2}(x)dx
(see
Lemma

D.4
in
Appendix

D
for a formal statement).
This condition is satisfied by many heavy-tailed distributions (e.g., Cauchy, Pareto) for which the moment assumptions required for the finite population CLTs of
Li and Ding 2017
do not hold.

The following theorem is pivotal; it provides the local asymptotic normality for
t_{N}
, which will aid us to study the limiting behavior of
\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}}
.

Theorem 2.1

(Local asymptotic normality of
t_{N}
)
.

Let
t_{N}:=t(\bm{Z}_{N},\bm{Y}_{N}-\tau_{0}\bm{Z}_{N})
be the WRS test statistic, with
t(\cdot,\cdot)
as in (
2.5
). Assume that
{m}/{N}\to\lambda\in(0,1)
, and suppose that
1
holds. Fix
h\in\mathbb{R}
and let
\tau_{N}=\tau_{0}-hN^{-1/2}
. Then it follows that under
\tau=\tau_{N}
,

N^{-3/2}\left(t_{N}-\frac{m(N+1)}{2}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{N}\left(-h\lambda(1-\lambda)\mathcal{I}_{b},\frac{\lambda(1-\lambda)}{12}\right),

where
\mathcal{I}_{b}
is defined in
1
.

With the above result, we are now prepared to present the asymptotic distribution of the estimator
\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}}
. This is the content of our next theorem.

Theorem 2.2

(CLT for the estimator
\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}}
)
.

Let
\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}}
be as in (
2.12
), with
t(\cdot,\cdot)
as in (
2.5
). Suppose that
1
holds, and that
{m}/{N}\to\lambda\in(0,1)
. Then,

\sqrt{N}\left(\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}}-\tau\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{N}\left(0,(12\lambda(1-\lambda)\mathcal{I}_{b}^{2})^{-1}\right),

where
\mathcal{I}_{b}
is defined in
1
.

Theorem

2.2
tells us that the problem of estimating the asymptotic variance of
\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}}
reduces to the problem of estimating the limiting quantity
\mathcal{I}_{b}
, which is unknown in general. Naturally, our next target is to estimate
\mathcal{I}_{b}
based on the observed responses
\{Y_{N,j}\}_{1\leq j\leq N}
. This is the content of the following result.

Proposition 2.3

(Consistent estimation of
\mathcal{I}_{b}
)
.

Suppose that
1
holds and
m/{N}\to\lambda\in(0,1)
as
N\to\infty
. Define

(2.17)

\widehat{\mathcal{I}}_{N}:=\left(1-\frac{m}{N}\right)^{-2}N^{-3/2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1,i\neq j}^{N}(1-Z_{N,i})(1-Z_{N,j})\bm{1}{\left\{0\leq Y_{N,j}-Y_{N,i}<N^{-1/2}\right\}}.

Then
\widehat{\mathcal{I}}_{N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{I}_{b}
as
N\to\infty
.

The above theorem in conjunction with
Theorem

2.2
readily yields an asymptotically valid Wald-type confidence interval for
\tau
, which is formally stated below.

Corollary 2.1

(Confidence interval for
\tau
)
.

Let
\widehat{\mathcal{I}}_{N}
be as defined in (
2.17
).
Under the assumptions of
Theorem

2.2
, an approximate
100(1-\alpha)\%
confidence interval for
\tau
is given by

\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}}\pm\frac{z_{\alpha/2}}{\sqrt{N}}\left(12\frac{m}{N}\left(1-\frac{m}{N}\right)\widehat{\mathcal{I}}_{N}^{2}\right)^{-1/2},

where
z_{\alpha}
denotes the upper
\alpha
-th quantile of the standard normal distribution.

2.4
Efficiency of Rosenbaum’s estimator

In this section we aim to assess the asymptotic relative efficiency of Rosenbaum’s estimator
\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}}
with respect to the difference-in-means estimator
\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{dm}}
.
In classical statistics, a framework for comparing two consistent, asymptotically normal estimators is through the ratio of their asymptotic variances.
A precise definition is given below.

Definition 2

(Asymptotic relative efficiency)
.

Let
\widehat{\tau}_{N,1}
and
\widehat{\tau}_{N,2}
be two asymptotically normal estimators of
\tau
, in the sense that there exist positive sequences
\sigma_{N,1}^{2}
and
\sigma_{N,2}^{2}
such that,
\frac{\widehat{\tau}_{N,1}-\tau}{\sigma_{N,1}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{N}(0,1)
and
\frac{\widehat{\tau}_{N,2}-\tau}{\sigma_{N,2}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{N}(0,1).

Then the asymptotic relative efficiency of
\widehat{\tau}_{N,1}
with respect to
\widehat{\tau}_{N,2}
is defined as

\mathrm{eff}(\widehat{\tau}_{N,1},\widehat{\tau}_{N,2}):=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\sigma_{N,2}^{2}}{\sigma_{N,1}^{2}}.

Our next theorem provides a lower bound on the asymptotic relative efficiency of
\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}}
with respect to
\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{dm}}
.

Theorem 2.3

(Efficiency lower bound)
.

Assume that the distribution
F_{N}
putting equal mass on the potential control outcomes
\{b_{N,j}\}_{\{1\leq j\leq N\}}
converges weakly to a distribution
F
with density
f
(w.r.t. the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}
) such that (a)
N^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N}f(b_{N,j})\to\int_{\mathbb{R}}f^{2}(x)dx<\infty
, and (b) for every fixed
h
,

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\sqrt{N}\,\left(F_{N}(x+\frac{h}{\sqrt{N}})-F_{N}(x)\right)-hf(x)\right|\to 0
as
N\to\infty
.
Then,

\sqrt{N}\left(\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}}-\tau\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{N}\left(0,\left(12\lambda(1-\lambda)\right)^{-1}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}f^{2}(x)dx\right)^{-2}\right).

Moreover, if (c)
N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(b_{N,j}-\overline{b}_{N})^{2}\to\sigma^{2}
, and (d)
N^{-1}\max_{1\leq i\leq N}(b_{N,j}-\overline{b}_{N})^{2}\to 0
, the asymptotic efficiency of
\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}}
relative to
\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{dm}}
is given by

(2.18)

\mathrm{eff}(\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}},\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{dm}})=12\sigma^{2}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}f^{2}(x)dx\right)^{2}.

Further, when
\sigma^{2}
is the variance of the limiting distribution
F
, it holds that

\mathrm{eff}(\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}},\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{dm}})\geq 0.864.

Remark 2.3

(On the assumptions of
Theorem

2.3
)
.

When
F_{N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}F
, the assumption (a) holds under mild conditions on
f
, e.g., if
\{f(B_{N})\}_{N\geq 1}
is uniformly integrable where
B_{N}\sim F_{N}
(this holds, in particular, when
f
is bounded). It follows from
Chernozhukov et al. 2014
that the assumption (b) holds when
\{b_{N,j}\}_{\{1\leq j\leq N\}}
are realizations from a distribution with density
f
. Lastly, the conditions (c) and (d) are identical to the assumptions in
Li and Ding 2017
, and provide the necessary asymptotic distribution of
\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{dm}}
.

Theorem

2.3
shows that, the limiting distribution of the rank-based estimator
\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}}
has a variance that is never more than
1.16
times the same for the difference-in-means estimator.
Table

2.1
records the values of the relative efficiency
\mathrm{eff}(\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}},\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{dm}})
for some standard probability distributions. As can be seen from the table, even when the
\{b_{N,j}\}
are realizations from a normal distribution, the rank-based estimator only suffers a
5\%
loss of efficiency. Whereas, for the exponential distribution, the rank-based estimator gives a
2

It is easy to construct examples where the rank-based estimator will exhibit infinite gains over the difference-in-means estimator, e.g., in the Pareto(

as

Remark 2.4

(The
0.864
lower bound)
.

The efficiency lower bound given in
Theorem

2.3
coincides with a celebrated efficiency lower bound due to
Hodges and
Lehmann 1956
in the context of two sample testing under location shift alternatives. They showed that the Pitman efficiency (see, e.g.,
van der
Vaart 1998
) of the WRS test relative to Student’s
t
-test never falls below
0.864
.
Here,
\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}}
is the estimator that inverts the WRS test, and thus, in a way, our
Theorem

2.3
mimics the efficiency result of
Hodges and
Lehmann 1956
.

Definition

2
above provides a principled framework for comparing estimators under a randomization inference. In practice, this framework allows us to use a pilot sample to choose an estimator that is expected to be more efficient in the final study. Pilot sampling is a frequently used tool to plan an empirical study
(
Wittes and
Brittain 1990
)
. In this context, we can estimate
f
using a pilot sample, which may then be used with (
2.18
) to choose between the difference-in-means and Rosenbaum’s estimator by estimating their relative efficiency.

3
Inference with regression adjustment

We now study a method advocated by
Rosenbaum 2002a
for drawing randomization inference on the treatment effect with regression adjustment for pre-treatment covariates, in the setup of completely randomized experiments as in
Section

2
.
Assume that along with the responses
\{Y_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq N}
(as in (
2.1
)) we also collect data on
p
covariates, denoted by
\bm{x}_{i}
for the
i
-th subject. Also denote by
\bm{X}
the
N\times p
matrix of covariates. To avoid notational clutter, we suppress the use of the index
N
in the notation until
Section

3.1
.

To motivate our estimation strategy, consider again the testing problem (
2.3
). The method suggested by Rosenbaum is to apply a randomization test on the residuals, as follows. First we calculate the adjusted responses
\bm{Y}-\tau_{0}\bm{Z}
and regress it on the covariates
\bm{X}
using the least squares criterion. Define the residuals obtained from this fit as:

(3.1)

\bm{e}_{\tau_{0}}:=\widehat{{\varepsilon}}(\bm{Y}-\tau_{0}\bm{Z},\bm{X})=(\bm{I}-\bm{P}_{\bm{X}})(\bm{Y}-\tau_{0}\bm{Z}),

where
\bm{I}
is the identity matrix of order
N\times N
, and
\bm{P}_{\bm{X}}
is the projection matrix onto the column space of
\bm{X}
.
Now we let the residuals
\bm{e}_{\tau_{0}}
play the role of the adjusted responses
\bm{Y}-\tau_{0}\bm{Z}
while performing the randomization test. Define the WRS test statistic based on the residuals
\bm{e}_{\tau_{0}}
as
t_{\mathrm{adj}}:=t(\bm{Z},\bm{e}_{\tau_{0}})=\bm{q}^{\top}\bm{Z}
, with
t(\cdot,\cdot)
as in (
2.5
) and the up-ranks
\bm{q}
in (
2.6
) being calculated on the residuals instead of the adjusted responses. That is,

(3.2)

t_{\mathrm{adj}}:=t(\bm{Z},\bm{e}_{\tau_{0}})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}Z_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\bm{1}{\left\{e_{\tau_{0},j}\leq e_{\tau_{0},i}\right\}}.

To find a point estimator for
\tau
based on the above test procedure,
Rosenbaum 2002a
suggested we invert the test as in
Section

2.1
. We denote Rosenbaum’s regression adjusted estimator by
\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}}_{\text{adj}}
, which is defined using (
2.12
) in the same manner as
\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}}
, only with the difference that here
t(\bm{Z},\bm{e}_{\tau})
plays the role of
t(\bm{Z},\bm{Y}-\tau\bm{Z})
.
We set
\mu_{\mathrm{adj}}:=\mathbb{E}_{\tau}t(\bm{Z},\bm{e}_{\tau})

and define

(3.3)

\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}}_{\mathrm{adj}}:=\frac{\sup\{\tau^{\prime}:t(\bm{Z},\bm{e}_{\tau^{\prime}})>\mu_{\mathrm{adj}}\}+\inf\{\tau^{\prime}:t(\bm{Z},\bm{e}_{\tau^{\prime}})<\mu_{\mathrm{adj}}\}}{2}.

Analyzing
\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{adj}}^{\mathrm{R}}
presents significant new challenges compared to
\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}}
. The chief reason being that
t(\bm{Z},\bm{e}_{\tau^{\prime}})
, which determines
\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{adj}}^{\mathrm{R}}
, involves indicators of the form
\mathbf{1}(e_{\tau^{\prime},j}\leq e_{\tau^{\prime},i})
, and each of these indicators depends on the entire random treatment assignment vector
\bm{Z}
. This is not the situation in the unadjusted case, where the corresponding indicator only depends on
Z_{i}
and
Z_{j}
. We relegate the discussion on how we were able to resolve this technical challenge to
Appendix

B
so as not to impede the flow of the paper.

3.1
Asymptotic distribution of
\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{adj}}^{\mathrm{R}}

Consider now a sequence of completely randomized experiments as in
Section

2.3
.
Denote the residuals obtained from the least squares fitting of
\bm{Y}_{N}-\tau_{0}\bm{Z}_{N}
on the covariates
\bm{X}_{N}
by
\bm{e}_{N}:=\widehat{{\varepsilon}}(\bm{Y}_{N}-\tau_{0}\bm{Z}_{N},\bm{X}_{N})
, with
\widehat{{\varepsilon}}(\cdot,\cdot)
as in (
3.1
), and
define the regression adjusted WRS statistic as

(3.4)

t_{N,\mathrm{adj}}:=t(\bm{Z}_{N},\bm{e}_{N})

with
t(\cdot,\cdot)
as in (
3.2
). Finally, define
\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{adj}}^{\mathrm{R}}
as in (
3.3
) using the statistic
t_{N,\mathrm{adj}}
.

As in the without regression adjustment case (see (
2.14
)), we reduce the problem of deriving the asymptotic distribution of the estimator
\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{adj}}^{\mathrm{R}}
to the problem of finding the asymptotic distribution of the test statistic
t_{N,\mathrm{adj}}
under the sequence of local alternatives
\tau_{N}=\tau_{0}-hN^{-1/2}
for a fixed
h\in\mathbb{R}
. As a precursor to our result on the local asymptotic normality of
t_{N,\mathrm{adj}}
, we first present its limiting null distribution.
Recall the notation
\{b_{N,j}\}
for the potential control outcomes. Define the regression adjusted potential control outcomes as

(3.5)

\widetilde{b}_{N,j}:=b_{N,j}-\bm{p}_{N,j}^{\top}\bm{b}_{N},\quad 1\leq j\leq N,

where
\bm{p}_{N,j}
is the
j
-th column of the projection matrix
\bm{P}_{\bm{X}_{N}}
that projects onto the column space of
\bm{X}_{N}
. Note that under
\tau=\tau_{0}
, these
\widetilde{b}_{N,j}
’s are identical to the residuals obtained by regressing
\bm{Y}_{N}-\tau_{0}\bm{Z}_{N}
on
\bm{X}_{N}
.
The following assumption mimics
1
of
Section

2.3
.

Assumption 2
.

Let
\{\widetilde{b}_{N,j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
be the regression adjusted potential control outcomes as defined in (
3.5
).
We assume that, for
I_{h,N}
as in (
2.15
), the following holds:

\lim_{N\to\infty}N^{-3/2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}I_{h,N}(\widetilde{b}_{N,j}-\widetilde{b}_{N,i})=h\mathcal{J}_{b},

for some fixed constant
\mathcal{J}_{b}\in(0,\infty)
and for every
h\in\mathbb{R}
.

We show in
Lemma

D.8
in
Appendix

D
that
2
holds (in probability) when the sequence of potential control outcomes are sampled according to the regression model
\bm{b}_{N}=\bm{X}_{N}\bm{\beta}_{N}+\bm{{\varepsilon}}_{N}
, where
{\varepsilon}_{N,1},\dots,{\varepsilon}_{N,N}
are i.i.d. from
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
for some
\sigma^{2}>0
. In fact, the limiting quantity
\mathcal{J}_{b}
has a neat formula in this special case, namely,
\mathcal{J}_{b}=(2\sqrt{\pi}\sigma)^{-1}
.
Our next theorem provides the limiting null distribution of the test statistic
t_{N,\mathrm{adj}}
under
2
.

Theorem 3.1

(Asymptotic null distribution of
t_{N,\mathrm{adj}}
)
.

Let
t_{N,\mathrm{adj}}
be the WRS statistic based on the residuals obtained from the least squares fitting of
\bm{Y}_{N}-\tau_{0}\bm{Z}_{N}
on
\bm{X}_{N}
, as defined in (
3.4
). Assume that
{m}/{N}\to\lambda\in(0,1)
, and suppose that
2
holds. Then, under
\tau=\tau_{0}
,

(3.6)

N^{-3/2}\left(t_{N,\mathrm{adj}}-\frac{m(N+1)}{2}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{N}\left(0,\frac{\lambda(1-\lambda)}{12}\right).

It is noteworthy that the limiting null distribution of the regression adjusted statistic
t_{N,\mathrm{adj}}
given in (
3.6
) and that of the unadjusted statistic
t_{N}
given in (
2.9
) are identical. However, this is not unexpected, since both
\bm{b}_{N}
and
\bm{e}_{N}
are deterministic vectors under the null, the ranks always take values in
\{1,2,\dots,N\}
, and
\bm{Z}_{N}
is the only source of randomness.

We next focus on deriving the asymptotic normality of the statistic
t_{N,\mathrm{adj}}
under the sequence of local alternatives
\tau_{N}=\tau_{0}-hN^{-1/2}
as
N\to\infty
. This is addressed in
Theorem

3.2
below, and it immediately yields a CLT for
\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{adj}}^{\mathrm{R}}
, which is presented in
Theorem

3.3
. Although these results are parallel to their unadjusted counterparts (
Theorems

2.1
and
2.2
, respectively), their proofs are substantially different, and more involved. A more detailed description of the technical challenges is provided in
Appendix

B
.

Theorem 3.2

(Local asymptotic normality of
t_{N,\mathrm{adj}}
)
.

Assume the setting of
Theorem

3.1
. Fix
h\in\mathbb{R}
and let
\tau_{N}=\tau_{0}-hN^{-1/2}
. Then, under
\tau=\tau_{N}
,

N^{-3/2}\left(t_{N,\mathrm{adj}}-\frac{m(N+1)}{2}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{N}\left(-h\lambda(1-\lambda)\mathcal{J}_{b},\frac{\lambda(1-\lambda)}{12}\right),

where
\mathcal{J}_{b}
is defined in
2
.

We are now ready to see a central limit theorem for the estimator
\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}}_{\mathrm{adj}}
, which is the content of the following theorem.

Theorem 3.3

(CLT for the estimator
\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{adj}}^{\mathrm{R}}
)
.

Assume that
{m}/{N}\to\lambda\in(0,1)
, and suppose that
2
holds. Then,

\sqrt{N}\left(\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{adj}}^{\mathrm{R}}-\tau\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{N}\left(0,\left(12\lambda(1-\lambda)\mathcal{J}_{b}^{2}\right)^{-1}\right),

where
\mathcal{J}_{b}
is defined in
2
.

The problem of estimating the asymptotic variance of
\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{adj}}^{\mathrm{R}}
is more challenging than in the unadjusted case, and
is deferred until
Appendix

A
(see
Corollary

A.2
for details, and

Section

4
for its empirical performance).

3.2
Efficiency gain by regression adjustment

As discussed in
Section

2
, appropriate randomization inference can be drawn ignoring the information available on the covariates. However, it is a popular belief
(
Rosenbaum 2002a
)
that regression adjustment may increase the efficiency of the inference based on the rank-based statistic, although any formal result supporting this belief was missing. We provide one result in this direction in
Theorem

3.4
below.

In view of
Theorems

2.2
and
3.3
, a comparison between the asymptotic variances of the adjusted estimator
\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{adj}}^{\mathrm{R}}
and the unadjusted estimator
\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}}
is essentially a comparison of the quantities
\mathcal{J}_{b}
and
\mathcal{I}_{b}
. In the following theorem, we show that
\mathrm{eff}(\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{adj}}^{\mathrm{R}},\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}})\geq 1
under the assumption that the regression model is correctly specified (recall the notion of asymptotic relative efficiency from
Section

2.4
). Thus, in this setting, the regression adjustment provides more efficient estimates than in the unadjusted case.

Theorem 3.4

(Efficiency gain by regression adjustment)
.

Assume that the potential control outcome sequence
\bm{b}_{N}
are realizations from the regression model
\bm{b}_{N}=\bm{X}_{N}\bm{\beta}_{N}+\bm{{\varepsilon}}_{N}
, where
{\varepsilon}_{N,i}
’s are i.i.d. from
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
. Then
2
holds, with
\mathcal{J}_{b}=(2\sqrt{\pi}\sigma)^{-1}
.
Further, denote
\bm{v}_{N}:=\bm{X}_{N}\bm{\beta}_{N}
and assume that
\lim_{N\to\infty}N^{-2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\exp\left(-(v_{N,j}-v_{N,i})^{2}/4\sigma^{2}\right)=\ell
. Then,
1
holds with
\mathcal{I}_{b}=\ell\mathcal{J}_{b}
, and consequently,

\mathcal{J}_{b}\geq\mathcal{I}_{b},\quad\text{i.e.,}\quad\mathrm{eff}(\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{adj}}^{\mathrm{R}},\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}})\geq 1.

Moreover, if it holds that

\liminf_{N\to\infty}N^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N}(v_{N,j}-\overline{v}_{N})^{2}>0,

where
\overline{v}_{N}:=N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}v_{N,i}
, then
\mathcal{J}_{b}>\mathcal{I}_{b}
, and consequently
\mathrm{eff}(\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{adj}}^{\mathrm{R}},\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}})
is strictly greater than
1
.

In
Sections

4
and
5
, we illustrate using both real and simulated datasets that the regression adjusted estimators lead to shorter confidence intervals than the unadjusted estimators, while not compromising on the desired significance level. We also observe in our simulations that, even when the regression model is not correctly specified, the regression adjusted estmator
\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{adj}}^{\mathrm{R}}
is at least as efficient as the unadjusted estimator
\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}}
, suggesting that
Theorem

3.4
might hold in a wider generality, akin to a result by
Lin 2013
on the efficiency gain by regression adjustment for ANOVA-based estimators.

6
Rosenbaum’s
\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}}
under treatment heterogeneity

In this section we initiate a study of Rosenbaum’s unadjusted estimator
\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}}
when the constant additive treatment effects assumption (
2.2
) does not hold. Under the framework (
2.1
), we aim to understand the estimand targeted by the estimator
\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}}
without any assumption on the treatment effect. This is a useful investigation since, in the literature, Rosenbaum’s estimator and the corresponding CIs are sometimes used without explicitly arguing for the constant treatment effect assumption
(
Silber et al. 2017
;
Zubizarreta et al. 2013
, see, for example,)
.

The estimator
\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}}
defined in (
2.12
) is well-defined without assuming contant treatment effects. To study the limit of
\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}}
, we define the following quantity:

(6.1)

\displaystyle\mbox{med}_{N}:=\median\{a_{i}-b_{j}:\ 1\leq i\neq j\leq N\}.

In words,
\mbox{med}_{N}
is the median of the pairwise differences in the potential outcomes under treatment and control situations. Note that
\mathrm{med}_{N}
is reminiscent of the popularly used estimand for two sample Wilcoxon statistics from classical nonparametrics
(e.g.,
Lehmann 1975
, Chapter 2.5)
.
The following result shows that under appropriate assumptions,
\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}}-\mbox{med}_{N}\overset{P}{\longrightarrow}0
, i.e., Rosenbaum’s estimator
\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}}
targets the estimand
\mbox{med}_{N}
.

Theorem 6.1
.

Assume there exists
0<c<1/2
free of
m,N
, such that
c<m/N<1-c
. Given
\varepsilon>0
, define

\kappa_{N}^{(1)}(\varepsilon):=\frac{\sum_{1\leq i\neq j}\mathbf{1}(a_{i}-b_{j}\leq\mathrm{med}_{N}+\varepsilon)}{N(N-1)},\qquad\kappa_{N}^{(2)}(\varepsilon):=\frac{\sum_{1\leq i\neq j}\mathbf{1}(a_{i}-b_{j}\geq\mathrm{med}_{N}-\varepsilon)}{N(N-1)}.

Assume further that for any
\varepsilon>0
,

(6.2)

\sqrt{N}\left(\kappa_{N}^{(1)}(\varepsilon)-\frac{1}{2}\right)\to\infty,\qquad\sqrt{N}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\kappa_{N}^{(2)}(\varepsilon)\right)\to\infty.

Then,
\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}}-\mathrm{med}_{N}\overset{P}{\longrightarrow}0
as
N\to\infty
, where the probability is taken with respect to the randomness of the
Z_{i}
s.

In the special case, where the constant treatment effect assumption (
2.2
) holds,
\mbox{med}_{N}=\tau_{0}+\median\{b_{i}-b_{j}:\ 1\leq i\neq j\leq N\}=\tau_{0}
. So, in this setting, as expected,
\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}}\overset{P}{\longrightarrow}\tau_{0}
. A few remarks are now in order.

Remark 6.1

(On assumption (
6.2
))
.

By definition of median,
\kappa_{N}^{(2)}(\varepsilon)\leq 1/2\leq\kappa_{N}^{(1)}(\varepsilon)
. Assumption (
6.2
) can be thought of as a weaker finite sample analogue of the positive density assumption, see, e.g.,
Lehmann and
Romano 2022
. In
Mizera and
Wellner 1998
, the authors establish necessary and sufficient conditions for convergence of the sample median of independent observations. Condition (
6.2
) is analogous to their necessary condition
(
Mizera and
Wellner 1998
, Equation (2.2))
.

Remark 6.2

(Robustness of
\mbox{med}_{N}
)
.

Being the median of pairwise differences between treatment and control outcomes, the causal estimand
\mbox{med}_{N}
is clearly more robust than the average treatment effect. In fact, the same framework as in
Section

2.2
also shows that
\mbox{med}_{N}
has a breakdown point of
1-2^{-{1}/{2}}
.

Theorem

6.1
opens up a number of questions on the behavior of Rosenbaum’s estimator(s) under heterogeneous treatment effects, e.g., deriving the limiting distribution for
\sqrt{N}(\widehat{\tau}_{R}-\mbox{med}_{N})
, or analogue of the same result for the Rosenbaum’s regression adjusted estimator
\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{adj}}^{\mathrm{R}}
(defined in (
3.3
)). We hope to explore such questions in future work.

7
Discussion

Randomization remains the gold standard method in causal inference for balancing covariates across treatment groups. Large randomization experiments are becoming increasingly popular, see recent discussions in
Banerjee
et al. 2016
and
Deaton and
Cartwright 2018
. In this paper, we studied Rosenbaum’s unadjusted and regression adjusted rank-based estimators (see
Rosenbaum 2002a
) for estimating the constant treatment effect under randomization inference. Our results highlight many benefits of these estimators in terms of their robustness properties and efficiency relative to the standard regression (un)-adjusted mean-based estimators. In particular, we showed that the asymptotic relative efficiency of Rosenbaum’s unadjusted estimator is in the worst case only
\sim
15% lower than the unadjusted difference-in-means estimator, but often the efficiency is much higher. We also showed that regression adjustment gives provable efficiency gain for Rosenbaum’s estimator under a linear outcome model. Additionally, our detailed simulation study illustrates the superior performance of these estimators for normal and heavy-tailed errors, and for linear and non-linear models.

It would be interesting to investigate the properties of the rank-based estimators when the constant treatment effect assumption is violated. There are statistical methods for testing this assumption (see e.g.,
Ding
et al. 2016
). When there is evidence for heterogeneity of the treatment effect, additional methods and/or studies are needed to understand how and to whom the treatment is more beneficial.
We may consider relaxing the constant treatment effect assumption in the following ways. First, it may happen that our data or our existing knowledge indicates that the treatment interacts with a covariate. In that case, we can incorporate this information in our inference by: (a) changing the design (e.g., using blocked randomization that blocks on the interacting covariate instead of complete randomization as studied in this paper), or (b) changing the model for the adjusted responses
(see
Rosenbaum 2021
, Section 1.8 for general adjustment)
. Our results may be extended in these situations to construct confidence intervals for the treatment effect after conditioning on covariates. Second,
Caughey
et al. 2023
provide methods to construct confidence intervals for quantiles of individual treatment effects by inverting randomization based tests. Following their approach, it will be interesting to generalize our methods to target the quantiles of individual treatment effects.

Finally, the average treatment effect across the units is a popular choice as a causal estimand. But this estimand may be undesirable, for example, when only a fraction of the units have a large positive treatment effect and others have no effect or negative effect; see, e.g.,
Athey et al. 2021
and
Rosenbaum 2021
. In
Section

6
, we demonstrated that in the absence of a constant treatment effect assumption, Rosenbaum’s estimator
\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}}
targets a novel estimand, which is given by the median of the pairwise differences between treatment and control outcomes. In future work, we plan to study this estimand in more detail under the setting of heterogeneous treatment effects.

Appendix A
Consistent plug-in estimators of standard errors

In
Theorems

2.2
and
3.3
of the main paper, we showed that
\sqrt{N}(\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}}-\tau_{0})
and
\sqrt{N}(\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{adj}}^{\mathrm{R}}-\tau_{0})
both have centered normal limits with appropriate variances. But these limiting variances depend on certain properties of the unobserved (fixed) potential outcomes through the quantities
\mathcal{I}_{b}
and
\mathcal{J}_{b}
(see
1
and
2
) and hence, are unknown. The main challenge in obtaining confidence intervals, therefore, would be to estimate these unknown variances consistently. Unfortunately, our proposed method for estimating
\mathcal{I}_{b}
(see
Proposition

2.3
of the main paper) does not have a natural extension in the regression-adjusted case. In this section, we propose an alternate method that applies to both the unadjusted and the adjusted cases.

A.1
Consistent estimation of the standard error of
\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}}

In this section, we propose a plug-in estimator of
\mathcal{I}_{b}
that uses all the observed data and not just the control outcomes, and can also be generalized to estimate
\mathcal{J}_{b}
in the regression adjusted case. Using the relation
b_{N,j}=Y_{N,j}-\tau_{0}Z_{N,j}
, we define a plug-in estimator of
b_{N,j}
as

(A.1)

\widehat{b}_{N,j}:=Y_{N,j}-\widehat{\tau}_{N}Z_{N,j}=b_{N,j}-(\widehat{\tau}_{N}-\tau_{0})Z_{N,j},

where
\widehat{\tau}_{N}
is an estimator of
\tau
.
At this point, the next intuitive step would be to replace
(b_{N,1},\ldots,b_{N,N})
with
(\widehat{b}_{N,1},\ldots,\widehat{b}_{N,N})
in
1
of the main paper, i.e., replace the term
I_{h,N}(b_{N,j}-b_{N,i})
on the left hand side of (
2.16
)), with
I_{h,N}(\widehat{b}_{N,j}-\widehat{b}_{N,i})
to construct the estimator. However this leads to some technical problems in proving consistency as
I_{h,N}(\cdot)
is a discontinuous function which itself changes with
N
. This requires us to refine our estimation strategy further and impose a slightly stronger condition than that in
1
. Towards this, for
\nu>0
and
N\geq 1
, define

(A.2)

I_{h,N,\nu}(x):=\begin{cases}\bm{1}{\left\{0\leq x<hN^{-\nu}\right\}}&\text{if }h\geq 0,\\
-\bm{1}{\left\{hN^{-\nu}\leq x<0\right\}}&\text{if }h<0.\end{cases}

Assumption 3
.

For
h\in\mathbb{R}
let
I_{h,N,\nu}
be defined in (
A.2
). We assume that there exists
0<\nu<1/2
, the following holds for every
u\in[\nu,1/2]
that

N^{-(2-u)}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}I_{h,N,u}(b_{N,j}-b_{N,i})\to h\mathcal{I}_{b}

for some constant
\mathcal{I}_{b}\in(0,\infty)
, which is the same
\mathcal{I}_{b}
as in
1
.

Remark A.1

(On
3
)
.

Note that if we were to set
\nu=1/2
in
3
, then
I_{h,N,\nu}(\cdot)\equiv I_{h,N,1/2}(\cdot)
is exactly the same function as
I_{h,N}(\cdot)
from (
2.15
), and consequently
3
would then imply
1
. If we define

(A.3)

T_{h,N,u}:=N^{-(2-u)}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}I_{h,N,u}(b_{N,j}-b_{N,i}),

then
1
requires
T_{h,N,1/2}\to h\mathcal{I}_{b}
whereas
3
requires the mildly stronger condition
T_{h,N,u}\to h\mathcal{I}_{b}
for
u
varying in any non-degenerate interval with right endpoint at
1/2
. We firmly believe that this is a reasonable assumption. For instance, one of the ways we justified
1
was by showing that it is satisfied when
b_{N,i}
’s are randomly sampled from an absolutely continuous distribution (see
Remark

2.2
). The same is also true for
3
, see
Lemma

D.16
for a formal result.

Finding a consistent estimator of
\mathcal{I}_{b}
is now quite intuitive. With
T_{h,N,u}
defined as in (
A.3
),
3
implies that
T_{1,N,\nu}
converges to
\mathcal{I}_{b}
, where
\nu
is as in
3
. Consequently, we construct an estimator
\widehat{V}_{N}
from
T_{1,N,\nu}
using the plug-in principle as described earlier in this section, by replacing
b_{N,i}
’s in (
A.3
) with
\widehat{b}_{N,i}
’s (see (
A.1
)). The following result makes it precise.

Theorem A.1

(Consistent estimation of
\mathcal{I}_{b}
)
.

Define
\widehat{b}_{N,j}
as in (
A.1
), with
\widehat{\tau}_{N}\equiv\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}}
. Suppose that
3
holds for some
\nu\in(0,1/2)
, and define

(A.4)

\widehat{V}_{N}:=N^{-(2-\nu)}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{1}{\left\{0\leq\widehat{b}_{N,j}-\widehat{b}_{N,i}<N^{-\nu}\right\}}.

Then
\widehat{V}_{N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{I}_{b}\text{ as }N\to\infty
.

Combining
Theorems

A.1
and
2.2
we get an asymptotically valid confidence interval for
\tau_{0}
. This is formally stated in the following corollary.

Corollary A.1

(Confidence interval for
\tau_{0}
based on
\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}}
)
.

Under
1
and
3
, an approximate
100(1-\alpha)\%
confidence interval for
\tau_{0}
is given by

(A.5)

\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}}\pm\frac{z_{\alpha/2}}{\sqrt{N}}\left(12\frac{m}{N}\left(1-\frac{m}{N}\right)\widehat{V}_{N}^{2}\right)^{-1/2},

where
\widehat{V}_{N}
is defined in (
A.4
).

We refer the reader to
Remark

A.2
below for a discussion on the choice of
\nu
in the above result.

A.2
Consistent estimation of the standard error of
\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{adj}}^{\mathrm{R}}

For estimating
\tau_{0}
(the true value of
\tau
) under regression adjustment, we follow the same road map as in the unadjusted case in the previous section. Once again, due to technical reasons we require a mildly stronger condition than
2
of the main paper to come up with a consistent estimator for
\mathcal{J}_{b}
. To lay the groundwork, define

(A.6)

\widetilde{T}_{h,N,u}:=N^{-(2-u)}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}I_{h,N,u}(\widetilde{b}_{N,j}-\widetilde{b}_{N,i}),

where
\widetilde{b}_{N,j}
’s are defined as in (
3.5
) and
I_{h,N,u}(\cdot)
is defined as in (
A.2
). Observe the direct correspondence between
\widetilde{T}_{h,N,u}
and
T_{h,N,u}
from (
A.3
). The only difference is that the potential control outcomes
b_{N,j}
’s are replaced by the regression adjusted potential control outcomes
\widetilde{b}_{N,j}
’s. Note also that
2
can be restated as
\widetilde{T}_{h,N,1/2}\to h\mathcal{J}_{b}
. With this observation, in the same vein as
3
, we now state our mildly stronger condition (compared to
2
):

Assumption 4
.

There exists
0<\nu<1/2
, such that for every
u\in[\nu,1/2]
and
h\in\mathbb{R}
,
\widetilde{T}_{h,N,u}\to h\mathcal{J}_{b},

for some constant
\mathcal{J}_{b}\in(0,\infty)
, which is the same
\mathcal{J}_{b}
as in
2
.

One can show that
4
holds under the same regression model assumption as in
Theorem

3.4
(see the arguments used in
Lemma

D.8
).

We are now in the position to construct our consistent estimator for
\mathcal{J}_{b}
. Towards this direction, recall the definition of
\widehat{b}_{N,j}
from (
A.1
) and set
\widehat{\bm{b}}_{N}:=(\widehat{b}_{N,1},\ldots,\widehat{b}_{N,N})
. To carry out the plug-in approach, we define the empirical analogues of
\widetilde{b}_{N,j}
’s, as follows:

(A.7)

\widehat{\widetilde{b}}_{N,j}:=\widehat{b}_{N,j}-\bm{p}_{N,j}^{\top}\widehat{\bm{b}}_{N}=Y_{N,j}-\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{adj}}^{\mathrm{R}}Z_{N,j}-\bm{p}_{N,j}^{\top}(\bm{Y}-\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{adj}}^{\mathrm{R}}\bm{Z}),\qquad j=1,2,\ldots,N.

The plug-in (consistent) estimator for
\mathcal{J}_{b}
is now constructed in the same manner as we did for
\mathcal{I}_{b}
, only with the modification that here
\widehat{\widetilde{b}}_{N,j}
’s will play the role of
\widehat{b}_{N,j}
’s. This is the content of the following theorem.

Theorem A.2

(Consistent estimation of
\mathcal{J}_{b}
)
.

Define
\widehat{\widetilde{b}}_{N,j}
as in (
A.7
), and suppose that
4
holds for some
\nu\in(0,1/2)
. Define

(A.8)

\widehat{W}_{N}:=N^{-(2-\nu)}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{1}{\left\{0\leq\widehat{\widetilde{b}}_{N,j}-\widehat{\widetilde{b}}_{N,i}<N^{-\nu}\right\}}.

Then
\widehat{W}_{N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{J}_{b}\text{ as }N\to\infty
.

Theorem

A.2
in conjunction with
Theorem

3.3
readily yields an asymptotically valid confidence interval for
\tau_{0}
based on
\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{adj}}^{\mathrm{R}}
, which is formally stated below.

Corollary A.2

(Confidence interval for
\tau_{0}
based on
\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{adj}}^{\mathrm{R}}
)
.

Under
2
and
4
, an approximate
100(1-\alpha)\%
confidence interval for
\tau_{0}
is given by

(A.9)

\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{adj}}^{\mathrm{R}}\pm\frac{z_{\alpha/2}}{\sqrt{N}}\left(12\frac{m}{N}\left(1-\frac{m}{N}\right)\widehat{W}_{N}^{2}\right)^{-1/2},

where
\widehat{W}_{N}
is defined in (
A.8
).

The following remark highlights that the choice of the tuning parameter
\nu
is inconsequential for both the regression unadjusted and regression adjusted confidence intervals that we proposed above.

Remark A.2

(Choice of
\nu
)
.

Our plug-in estimators proposed in (
A.4
) and (
A.8
) both require a choice of the tuning parameter
\nu\in(0,1/2)
.
We have observed through extensive simulations that the confidence intervals are not very sensitive to the choice of
\nu
. In fact, it seems that the choice
\nu=1/2
also works in practice, although our proof of
Theorem

A.1
precludes that choice.

For an illustration, consider our simulation setting 3a described in
Section

5
(see

). We calculate the plug-in estimator of the asymptotic variances of

and
\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{adj}}^{\mathrm{R}}
for different values of
\nu
, ranging from
1/4
to
1/2
. Then fixing one particular value of
\nu
as a reference, say
\nu=1/3
, we calculate the ratios of these estimates for for
\nu=1/3
to those for the other values of
\nu
. Repeating this
100
times, we draw the boxplots of the ratios thus obtained, which is provided in

.

The plots suggest that the estimates of the asymptotic variance do not differ much with the choice of

. We observed similar phenomenon for the other simulation settings as well. Furthermore, in our simulations we also varied

Remark A.3

(Scaling the outcome variable)
.

Since our method involves counts of differences in the outcome variable (or the fitted residuals) in shrinking intervals, we might not get a reasonable estimate if the responses are typically large in magnitude. This is why we suggest to scale (or modify) the outcome variable appropriately while using our variance estimators in practice. For instance, in

Appendix B
Main technical tools

In this section, we discuss the main tools used in the proofs of our main results in
Sections

2
and
3
of the main paper and
Appendix

A
. We begin with
Proposition

2.1
where
\tau=\tau_{0}
. In this case, it is easy to check that

t_{N}\equiv t_{N}^{0}=m+\sum_{j=1}^{N}Z_{N,j}\sum_{i=1,i\neq j}^{N}\bm{1}{\left\{b_{N,i}\leq b_{N,j}\right\}}.

Note that
t_{N}^{0}
is a weighted linear combination of
Z_{N,j}
’s, where the weights are non-random. We use the classical Hoeffding’s combinatorial CLT (see
Hoeffding 1951
; also see
Section

C.1
for more details) to complete the proof of
Proposition

2.1
. The case of local alternatives, i.e., where
\tau=\tau_{N}:=\tau_{0}-h/\sqrt{N}
, is more challenging. This is the subject of
Theorem

2.1
of the main paper. Note that under
\tau=\tau_{N}
, we have:

t_{N}\equiv t_{N}^{h}=m+\sum_{j=1}^{N}Z_{N,j}\sum_{i=1,i\neq j}^{N}\bm{1}{\left\{b_{N,i}-hN^{-1/2}Z_{N,i}\leq b_{N,j}-hN^{-1/2}Z_{N,j}\right\}}.

When
h\neq 0
,
t_{N}^{h}
is a weighted linear combination of
Z_{N,j}
’s with random weights depending on the
Z_{N,j}
’s themselves (unlike what we observed for
t_{N}^{0}
). Therefore classical combinatorial CLTs do not apply. Further, as we are in the fixed design setting, the models under
\tau=\tau_{0}
and
\tau=\tau_{N}
lack the desirable forms for us to use the standard contiguity arguments due to Le Cam for deriving local asymptotic normality (see
van der
Vaart 1998
for details). We tackle these challenges with the aid of a special decomposition which is the subject of the following proposition (see
Section

C.14
for a proof).

Proposition B.1
.

Let
t_{N}=t_{N}(\bm{Z}_{N},\bm{Y}_{N}-\tau_{0}\bm{Z}_{N})
be the Wilcoxon rank-sum statistic based on any random treatment assignment, where
t(\cdot,\cdot)
is as in (
2.5
) of the main paper. Fix
h\in\mathbb{R}
and let
\tau_{N}=\tau_{0}-hN^{-1/2}
. It holds under
\tau=\tau_{N}
that

t_{N}\equiv t_{N}^{h}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}t_{N}^{0}-\gamma_{N}^{h},\quad\mbox{where}\quad\gamma_{N}^{h}:=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1,i\neq j}^{N}(1-Z_{N,i})Z_{N,j}I_{h,N}(b_{N,j}-b_{N,i})

where
I_{h,N}
is defined in (
2.15
) of the main paper.

Based on
Proposition

B.1
, the proof of
Theorem

2.1
can be completed via Slutsky’s Theorem if we show the following:

N^{-3/2}\left(t_{N}^{0}-\frac{m(N+1)}{2}\right)\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\mathcal{N}\left(0,\frac{\lambda(1-\lambda)}{12}\right)\quad\mbox{and}\quad\gamma_{N}^{h}\overset{P}{\longrightarrow}-h\lambda(1-\lambda)\mathcal{I}_{b},

where
\mathcal{I}_{b}
is defined in
1
of the main paper. The asymptotic fluctuation of
t_{N}^{0}
, in the above display, follows directly from
Proposition

2.1
as already discussed above. To prove the limit of
\gamma_{N}^{h}
, we use the standard Markov’s inequality coupled with
1

(see
Section

C.3
for more details). Once the asymptotic distribution of
t_{N}\equiv t_{N}^{h}
is obtained, the same for
\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}}
as stated in
Theorem

2.2
, follows as a direct consequence of a classical Hodges and Lehmann argument (see
Hodges and
Lehmann 1963
; also see
Theorem

2.1
and
Lemma

D.1
).

The next important question is obtaining asymptotically honest confidence intervals for
\tau_{0}
(as presented in
Theorem

A.1
of the main paper). Based on
Theorem

2.2
, this reduces to obtaining a consistent estimator for
\mathcal{I}_{b}
, which by
3
, is the limit of
u_{N}
where

u_{N}:=N^{-3/2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\mathbf{1}(Y_{N,j}-Y_{N,i}-\tau_{0}(Z_{N,j}-Z_{N,i})\geq 0)\right.\\
-\left.\mathbf{1}(Y_{N,j}-Y_{N,i}-\tau_{0}(Z_{N,j}-Z_{N,i})\geq N^{-\nu})\right).

The natural plug-in estimator can then be constructed by replacing
\tau_{0}
by
\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}}
above, to get a plug-in analogue of
u_{N}
, say
\widehat{u}_{N}^{\mathrm{R}}
. While
\widehat{u}_{N}^{\mathrm{R}}
should intuitively be consistent, there are two technical issues: (a) indicators are not continuous functions, and (b) the sets in the indicators used above are not fixed, but effectively shrinking with
N
(they are of the form
[0,N^{-\nu}]
). These technical issues preclude the possibility of using a continuous mapping type argument to establish consistency. We circumvent this by approximating the indicators in
u_{N}
with Gaussian mollifiers with an appropriately chosen variance parameter. In particular, setting
\Phi(\cdot)
as the standard Gaussian distribution function and
\sigma_{N,\nu}:=\log{N}/N^{\nu}
, define

\hat{u}_{N}^{\mathrm{G}}:=\frac{1}{N^{3/2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\left(\Phi(\sigma_{N,\nu}^{-1}(Y_{N,j}-Y_{N,i}-\hat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}}(Z_{N,j}-Z_{N,i}))\right.\\
-\left.\Phi(\sigma_{N,\nu}^{-1}(Y_{N,j}-Y_{N,i}-\hat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}}(Z_{N,j}-Z_{N,i}-N^{-\nu}))\right).

Using some careful truncation arguments, we then show that
\hat{u}_{N}^{\mathrm{G}}-\hat{u}_{N}^{\mathrm{R}}\overset{P}{\longrightarrow}0
and
\hat{u}_{N}^{\mathrm{G}}-u_{N}\overset{P}{\longrightarrow}0
, which when combined, yield
\hat{u}_{N}^{\mathrm{R}}\overset{P}{\longrightarrow}\mathcal{I}_{b}
.

Our next important result in the unadjusted setting is the efficiency lower bound for
\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}}
when compared with
\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{dm}}
, as presented in
Theorem

2.3
of the main paper. Fortunately, the heavy lifting is done by
Theorem

2.2
,
Li and Ding 2017
, and
Hodges and
Lehmann 1956
; see
Section

C.6
for details.

We now move on to our results from
Section

3
of the main paper. The major technical difference between the adjusted and unadjusted cases is perhaps reflected in the proof of
Theorem

3.2
, which requires substantially different techniques compared to its unadjusted counterpart in
Theorem

2.1
. To understand this difference, recall from (
3.4
) of the main paper that under
\tau=\tau_{0}-hN^{-1/2}
, the regression adjusted Wilcoxon rank-sum statistic is given by:

\displaystyle t_{N,\textrm{adj}}\equiv t_{N,\textrm{adj}}^{h}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}Z_{N,j}\mathbf{1}\left(hN^{-1/2}(\bm{p}_{N,i}-\bm{p}_{N,j})^{\top}\bm{Z}_{N}\leq\tilde{b}_{N,j}-\tilde{b}_{N,i}-hN^{-1/2}\right),

where
\bm{p}_{N,j}
is the
j
-th column of the projection matrix
\bm{P}_{\bm{X}_{N}}
that projects onto the column space of
\bm{X}_{N}
, and
\tilde{b}_{N,i}:=Y_{N,i}-\tau_{0}Z_{N,i}-\bm{p}_{N,i}^{\top}\bm{b}_{N}
. Therefore, for every pair
(i,j)
, the indicators in
t_{N,\textrm{adj}}
depend on the entire random vector
(Z_{N,1},\ldots,Z_{N,N})
. This is in sharp contrast to
t_{N}^{h}
above, where for every pair
(i,j)
, the indicators depend only on
(Z_{N,i},Z_{N,j})
. As a result, even studying the mean and variance of
t_{N,\textrm{adj}}^{h}
raises significantly more technical challenges than those of
t_{N}^{h}
. We circumvent this by leveraging the properties of the projection matrix and showing that the term on the left hand side of the indicators in the above display can be replaced by
0
, in a suitable asymptotic sense. To be specific, define

\widetilde{A}_{N,\textrm{adj}}^{h}:=\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}Z_{N,j}\mathbf{1}\left(0\leq\tilde{b}_{N,j}-\tilde{b}_{N,i}-hN^{-1/2}\right).

Our main contribution lies in showing that

N^{-3/2}|t_{N,\textrm{adj}}^{h}-\widetilde{A}_{N,\textrm{adj}}^{h}|=o_{p}(1).

The proof of this step is technical and we defer the reader to
Appendix

D
for details. However, once the above display is established, it suffices to obtain the asymptotic distribution of
N^{-3/2}\widetilde{A}_{N,\textrm{adj}}
. Note that the indicators in
\widetilde{A}_{N,\textrm{adj}}^{h}
are deterministic and do not depend on
\bm{Z}_{N}
. This puts us in a similar situation to the analysis of
t_{N}^{0}
, which we have already discussed above.

Once
Theorem

3.2
is established, the proofs of
Theorem

3.3
and
Corollary

A.2
follow using ideas similar to the proofs of
Theorem

2.2
and
Corollary

A.1
respectively.

Appendix C
Proofs of the main results

C.1
Proof of
Proposition

2.1

Proof.

We begin by recalling that

t_{N}:=\widehat{\bm{q}}_{N}^{\top}\bm{Z}_{N}
, where

\widehat{q}_{N,j}:=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{1}{\left\{Y_{N,i}-\tau_{0}Z_{N,i}\leq Y_{N,j}-\tau_{0}Z_{N,j}\right\}}\ ,\ 1\leq j\leq N.

It follows under
\tau=\tau_{0}
that

(C.1)

t_{N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\bm{q}_{N}^{\top}\bm{Z}_{N},\quad q_{N,j}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{1}{\left\{b_{N,i}\leq b_{N,j}\right\}}.

Further, under
\tau=\tau_{0}
,

\bm{q}_{N}^{\top}\bm{Z}_{N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}q_{N,\Pi_{N}(i)}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}c_{N,i}\cdot q_{N,\Pi_{N}(i)}

where
c_{N,i}=\bm{1}{\left\{i\leq m\right\}}
and
\Pi_{N}
is a random permutation of
\{1,2,\dots,N\}
. Note,
\bar{c}_{N}:=N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}c_{N,i}=m/N
, and thus

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}N\cdot\frac{\max_{1\leq i\leq N}(c_{N,i}-\bar{c}_{N})^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}(c_{N,i}-\bar{c}_{N})^{2}}=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\max\{\bar{c}_{N}^{2},(1-\bar{c}_{N})^{2}\}}{\frac{m}{N}(1-\bar{c}_{N})^{2}+\left(1-\frac{m}{N}\right)\bar{c}_{N}^{2}}=\frac{\max\{\lambda^{2},(1-\lambda)^{2}\}}{\lambda(1-\lambda)}.

Consequently,

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}N\cdot\frac{\max_{1\leq i\leq N}(c_{N,i}-\bar{c}_{N})^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}(c_{N,i}-\bar{c}_{N})^{2}}\cdot\frac{\max_{1\leq i\leq N}(q_{N,i}-\bar{q}_{N})^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}(q_{N,i}-\bar{q}_{N})^{2}}=0.

In view of the above, Hoeffding’s combinatorial CLT
Hoeffding 1951
implies that under
\tau=\tau_{0},

\frac{t_{N}-\mathbb{E}(t_{N})}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(t_{N})}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{N}(0,1)\text{ as $N\to\infty$.}

Now invoking
Lemma

D.6
, we have
{\textrm{Var}}_{\tau_{0}}(t_{N})\sim\frac{\lambda(1-\lambda)}{12}N^{3}
, which completes the proof.

\square

C.2
Proof of
Proposition

2.2

Proof.

It follows from the algebraic manipulations provided in
Hodges and
Lehmann 1963
that our modified estimator
\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}}
in (
2.12
), with
t(\cdot,\cdot)
as in (
2.5
) of the main paper, is given by

\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}}=\median\{Y_{i}-Y_{j}:Z_{i}=1,Z_{j}=0,1\leq i,j\leq N\}.

Now for any
1\leq k\leq N
, fix any
k
indices
1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}<\dots<i_{k}\leq N
, and any treatment assignment
(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{N})=(z_{1},\dots,z_{N})
. Set
n_{1}:=|\{1\leq j\leq k:z_{i_{j}}=1\}|.
Note that changing the responses
y_{i_{1}},\dots,y_{i_{k}}
arbitrarily can make the pairwise differences
\{y_{i}-y_{j}:z_{i}=1,z_{j}=0\}
blow up to
+\infty
except for
(m-n_{1})(N-m-(k-n_{1}))
many pairs
(i,j)
. As long as this count is greater than or equal to half of the total number of pairwise differences, i.e.,
m(N-m)/2
, the median cannot be made arbitrarily large. Note, this needs to be satisfied for every treatment assignment
z_{1},\dots,z_{N}\in\{0,1\}
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{N}z_{i}=m
. By symmetry, we can assume without loss of generality that
(i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{k})=(1,2,\dots,k)
. Thus, we can make
\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}}(y_{1},\dots,y_{N};z_{1},\dots,z_{N})
arbitrarily large by altering
k
responses only if
k
satisfies the following:

(C.2)

\forall\ z_{1},\dots,z_{N}\in\{0,1\},\ (m-n_{1})(N-m-(k-n_{1}))<\frac{m(N-m)}{2},\ \text{where}\ n_{1}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}z_{j}.

Moreover, if
k
satisfies this condition,
then for any
z_{1},\dots,z_{N}\in\{0,1\}
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{N}z_{i}=m
, and any choice of indices
1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}<\dots<i_{k}\leq N
, we can let
y_{i_{j}}\to\infty
if
z_{i_{j}}=1
and
y_{i_{j}}\to-\infty
if
z_{i_{j}}=0
, which results in
\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}}(y_{1},\dots,y_{N};z_{1},\dots,z_{N})\to\infty
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\text{BP}_{N}(\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\min\left\{1\leq k\leq N:\eqref{star}\ \text{holds}\right\}.

It is elementary to observe that
f(x)=(m-x)(N-m-k+x)
has a global maximum at
x=m-(N-k)/{2}
, and the maximum value is
(N-k)^{2}/4
. However, we want to maximize
f(x)
over
[0,k]
only. Consider the following cases:

•

Case 1:

m>N/3
. Note,
0\leq m-(N-k)/2\leq k\iff k\geq 2m-N
. Thus, for
2m-N\leq k\leq N
,

\eqref{star}\ \text{holds}\iff\frac{(N-k)^{2}}{4}<\frac{m(N-m)}{2}\iff k>N\left(1-\sqrt{2\frac{m}{N}\left(1-\frac{m}{N}\right)}\right)\geq 2m-N.

In other words, for
2m-N\leq k\leq N
, the condition (
C.2
) is automatic. On the other hand, for
1\leq k<2m-N
, the maximum of the function
f(x)=(m-x)(N-m-k+x)
is attained at
x=k
. So, for
1\leq k<2m-N
,

\eqref{star}\ \text{holds}\iff(m-k)(N-m)<\frac{m(N-m)}{2}\iff k>\frac{m}{2}.

Hence we can write

\displaystyle\left\{1\leq k\leq N:\eqref{star}\ \text{holds}\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{1\leq k<2m-N:\eqref{star}\ \text{holds}\right\}\cup\left\{2m-N\leq k<N:\eqref{star}\ \text{holds}\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{1\leq k<2m-N:k>m/2\right\}\cup\left\{2m-N\leq k<N\right\}.

Since in this case
m/2<2m-N
, it follows that for
m>N/2
,

\text{BP}_{N}(\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}})=\frac{1}{N}\left\lceil\frac{m}{2}\right\rceil.

•

Case 2:

m<N/3
. This case is similar to the previous case. In this case we get

\text{BP}_{N}(\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}})=\frac{1}{N}\left\lceil\frac{N-m}{2}\right\rceil.

•

Case 3:

N/3\leq m\leq 2N/3
. First, consider
N/2\leq m\leq 2N/3
. We note that

\displaystyle\left\{1\leq k\leq N:\eqref{star}\ \text{holds}\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{1\leq k<2m-N:\eqref{star}\ \text{holds}\right\}\cup\left\{2m-N\leq k<N:\eqref{star}\ \text{holds}\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{1\leq k<2m-N:\frac{k}{N}>\frac{m}{2N}\right\}\bigcup\left\{2m-N\leq k<N:\frac{k}{N}>1-\sqrt{2\frac{m}{N}\left(1-\frac{m}{N}\right)}\right\}.

Now a little algebra shows that
m/2<N-\sqrt{2m(N-m)}
. Hence in this case we get

\text{BP}_{N}(\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}})=\frac{1}{N}\left\lfloor N\left(1-\sqrt{2\frac{m}{N}\left(1-\frac{m}{N}\right)}\right)\right\rfloor.

Similarly one can deal with the other sub-case, namely,
N/3\leq m\leq N/2
.

Since
m/N\to\lambda\in[0,1]
, the desired conclusion follows.

\square

C.5
Proof of
Proposition

2.3

Proof.

First note that under any value of
\tau,

B_{N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1,i\neq j}^{N}(1-Z_{N,i})(1-Z_{N,j})I_{1,N}(b_{N,j}-b_{N,i}).

Invoking
1
, we get

\mathbb{E}N^{-3/2}B_{N}=\frac{(N-m)(N-m-1)}{N(N-1)}N^{-3/2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1,i\neq j}^{N}I_{1,N}(b_{N,j}-b_{N,i})\to(1-\lambda)^{2}\mathcal{I}_{b}.

Consequently,
\mathbb{E}((1-m/N)^{-2}N^{-3/2}B_{N})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{I}_{b}
. Thus it suffices to show that

{\textrm{Var}}(B_{N})=o(N^{3})
.
For brevity, we denote by
I_{N}(i,j)
the indicator
I_{1,N}(b_{N,j}-b_{N,i})
in the rest of the proof.
Note that

\text{Var}\left(B_{N}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{l=1,l\neq k}^{N}\text{Cov}\left((1-Z_{N,i})(1-Z_{N,j}),(1-Z_{N,k})(1-Z_{N,l})\right)I_{N}(i,j)I_{N}(k,l).

Since
2\leq|\{i,j,k,l\}|\leq 4
, we consider the following cases.

(a)

|\{i,j,k,l\}|=2,
i.e.,
(i,j)=(k,l)
or
(l,k)
. Since
\text{Var}((1-Z_{N,i})(1-Z_{N,j}))=p_{N}(1-p_{N})
where
p_{N}=P(Z_{N,i}=0,Z_{N,j}=0)=\frac{(N-m)(N-m-1)}{N(N-1)}\sim(1-\lambda)^{2},
the contribution of these terms in
\text{Var}(B_{N})
is given by

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{N}2p_{N}(1-p_{N})I^{2}_{N}(i,j)\lesssim 2(1-\lambda)^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{N}I_{N}(i,j)\lesssim N^{3/2}.

(b)

|\{i,j,k,l\}|=4,
i.e., all
4
indices are distinct. Note that in this case

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}\left((1-Z_{N,i})(1-Z_{N,j}),(1-Z_{N,k})(1-Z_{N,l})\right)

\displaystyle=P(Z_{N,i}=0,Z_{N,j}=0,Z_{N,k}=0,Z_{N,l}=0)-p_{N}^{2}

\displaystyle=p_{N}\left(\frac{(N-m-2)(N-m-3)}{(N-2)(N-3)}-\frac{(N-m)(N-m-1)}{N(N-1)}\right)

\displaystyle=-p_{N}\frac{2m(2N^{2}-2mN-6N+3+3m)}{N(N-1)(N-2)(N-3)}\sim-4\lambda(1-\lambda)^{3}N^{-1}.

Hence the contribution
u_{N}
of these terms in
\text{Var}(B_{N})
satisfies the following.

\displaystyle u_{N}

\displaystyle\lesssim N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{l=1,l\neq k}^{N}I_{N}(i,j)I_{N}(k,l)\lesssim N^{-1+3/2+3/2}=N^{2}.

(c)

|\{i,j,k,l\}|=3
. Here we have 4 sub-cases:
i=k,j\neq l
;
i\neq k,j=l
;
i=l,j\neq k
;
i\neq l,j=k
. In each of the subcases,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}\left((1-Z_{N,i})(1-Z_{N,j}),(1-Z_{N,k})(1-Z_{N,l})\right)

\displaystyle=P(Z_{N,1}=Z_{N,2}=Z_{N,3}=0)-p_{N}^{2}

\displaystyle\sim(1-\lambda)^{3}-(1-\lambda)^{4}=\lambda(1-\lambda)^{3}.

Hence, if
v_{n}
be the contribution of these terms in
\text{Var}(B_{N})
, then

\displaystyle v_{N}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{i,j,l\text{ distinct}}I_{N}(i,j)I_{N}(i,l)+\sum_{i,j,k\text{ distinct}}I_{N}(i,j)I_{N}(k,j)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{i,j,k\text{ distinct}}I_{N}(i,j)I_{N}(k,i)+\sum_{i,j,l\text{ distinct}}I_{N}(i,j)I_{N}(j,l)

\displaystyle\leq 4N\sum_{i,j\neq i}I_{N}(i,j)\lesssim N^{1+3/2}=N^{5/2}.

Combining the above cases, we conclude that
N^{-3}\text{Var}(B_{N})\lesssim N^{-3}(N^{3/2}+N^{2}+N^{5/2})=o(1),

which completes the proof.

\square

Proof of
Theorem

3.2

Proof.

Recall the notation

\widetilde{b}_{N,i}=b_{N,i}-\bm{p}_{N,i}^{\top}\bm{b}_{N}
(

) from (

) of the main paper.

t_{N,\mathrm{adj}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathbf{I}_{N}-\mathbf{II}_{N}+C_{N}
,
where

(C.6)

\widetilde{\xi}_{N,i,j}

Thus we decompose
t_{N,\mathrm{adj}}
under
\tau=\tau_{N}
as

(C.8)

t_{N,\mathrm{adj}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\underbrace{\left(\widetilde{\mathbf{I}}_{N}-\widetilde{\mathbf{II}}_{N}+C_{N}\right)}_{\widetilde{A}_{N,\textrm{adj}}^{h}}+\underbrace{(\mathbf{I}_{N}-\widetilde{\mathbf{I}}_{N})}_{D_{n}}-\underbrace{(\mathbf{II}_{N}-\widetilde{\mathbf{II}}_{N})}_{Q_{n}}.

We first focus on
\widetilde{A}_{N,\textrm{adj}}^{h}
. Note that in (
C.5
) we are summing up the ranks of the
m
numbers

\{\widetilde{b}_{N,j}+hN^{-1/2}\bm{p}_{N,j}^{\top}\bm{Z}_{N}:1\leq j\leq N,Z_{N,j}=1\}

within this set. Unfortunately, due to possibility of ties, we cannot directly equate it with
m(m+1)/2
.
However, invoking
2
we can show that
N^{-3/2}(C_{N}-m(m+1)/2)=o_{P}(1)
as
N\to\infty
. Towards that, observe that for any
\delta>0

Proof of
Theorem

3.4

Proof.

Under the assumptions of this theorem,
Lemma

D.8
tells us that
2
holds in probability, with
\mathcal{J}_{b}=(2\sqrt{\pi}\sigma)^{-1}
.
Next, fix any
h\in\mathbb{R}
and define

\mathcal{I}_{h,N}:=N^{-3/2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}I_{h,N}(b_{j}-b_{i}),

where
I_{h,N}
is defined in (
2.15
) of the main paper. Without loss of generality, we take
h>0
(the other case will be similar). For simplicity in notation, we shall omit the index
N
in this proof for
\bm{b}_{N},\bm{X}_{N}
etc. From
\bm{b}=\bm{X}\bm{\beta}+\bm{{\varepsilon}}
we write
b_{j}-b_{i}={\varepsilon}_{j}-{\varepsilon}_{i}+v_{j}-v_{i}
where
v_{i}
denotes the
i
-th coordinate of
\bm{v}=\bm{X}\bm{\beta}
. It then follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathcal{I}_{h,N}

\displaystyle=N^{-3/2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}P(0\leq{\varepsilon}_{j}-{\varepsilon}_{i}+v_{j}-v_{i}<hN^{-1/2})

\displaystyle=N^{-3/2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[\Phi\left(\frac{v_{i}-v_{j}+hN^{-1/2}}{\sqrt{2}\sigma}\right)-\Phi\left(\frac{v_{i}-v_{j}}{\sqrt{2}\sigma}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=N^{-3/2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[\frac{h}{\sqrt{2}\sigma\sqrt{N}}\phi\left(\frac{v_{i}-v_{j}}{\sqrt{2}\sigma}\right)+\frac{h^{2}}{4N\sigma^{2}}\phi^{\prime}(\xi_{i,j})\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{h}{2\sqrt{\pi}\sigma}N^{-2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}e^{-(v_{j}-v_{i})^{2}/4\sigma^{2}}+(4\sigma^{2})^{-1}h^{2}N^{-5/2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\phi^{\prime}(\xi_{i,j}),

where for each
(i,j)
,
\xi_{i,j}
is a number between
v_{i}-v_{j}
and
v_{i}-v_{j}+N^{-1/2}

Since
\phi^{\prime}
is bounded, we can show (by proceeding in the same manner as in the proof of
Lemma

D.8
) that the second sum in the above display is asymptotically negligible. Hence

(C.11)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\left(\mathbb{E}\mathcal{I}_{h,N}-\frac{h}{2\sqrt{\pi}\sigma}N^{-2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}e^{-(v_{j}-v_{i})^{2}/4\sigma^{2}}\right)=0.

Since
0\leq N^{-2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}e^{-(v_{j}-v_{i})^{2}/4\sigma^{2}}\leq 1
, it follows that for any
h>0
,

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\mathcal{I}_{h,N}/h\leq(2\sqrt{\pi}\sigma)^{-1}=\mathcal{J}_{b}.

Next we show that
{\textrm{Var}}(\mathcal{I}_{h,N})\to 0
. Towards that, we first write

\mathbb{E}\mathcal{I}_{h,N}^{2}=N^{-3}\sum_{i,j,k,l}P(0\leq b_{j}-b_{i}<hN^{-1/2},0\leq b_{l}-b_{k}<hN^{-1/2}).

Now, as mentioned earlier,
2
holds in this setting (cf.
Lemma

D.8
); hence appealing to arguments similar to those given in
Section

C.5
, we argue that the contribution from the terms with repeated indices are negligible. So we are left with terms with distinct indices
i,j,k,l
. For such indices, note that we have
P(0\leq b_{j}-b_{i}<hN^{-1/2},0\leq b_{l}-b_{k}<hN^{-1/2})=P(0\leq b_{j}-b_{i}<hN^{-1/2})P(0\leq b_{l}-b_{k}<hN^{-1/2})
, since the errors
{\varepsilon}_{i}
’s are independent and
v_{i}
’s non-stochastic. We can thus write

\mathbb{E}\mathcal{I}_{h,N}^{2}=\left(N^{-3/2}\sum_{i,j\text{ distinct }}P\left(0\leq b_{j}-b_{i}<hN^{-1/2}\right)\right)^{2}+o(1)=(\mathbb{E}\mathcal{I}_{h,N})^{2}+o(1),

from which the conclusion follows.
Moreover, (
C.11
) tells us that
1
holds, with
\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\mathcal{I}_{h,N}/h=\ell(2\sqrt{\pi}\sigma)^{-1}=\ell\mathcal{J}_{b}.

Finally, for any
x\in\mathbb{R},

(C.12)

e^{-x^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{1+x^{2}}\leq\max\left\{1-\frac{x^{2}}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right\}.

Hence

\displaystyle N^{-2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}e^{-(v_{j}-v_{i})^{2}/4\sigma^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\max\left\{1-\frac{1}{2N^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(v_{j}-v_{i})^{2},\frac{1}{2}\right\}

\displaystyle=\max\left\{1-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}(v_{j}-\overline{v})^{2},\frac{1}{2}\right\}.

Therefore, if
\liminf_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}(v_{j}-\overline{v})^{2}>0
, then

\displaystyle\limsup_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\mathcal{I}_{h,N}/h

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}\sigma}\limsup_{N\to\infty}\max\left\{1-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}(v_{j}-\overline{v})^{2},\frac{1}{2}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}\sigma}\max\left\{1-\liminf_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}(v_{j}-\overline{v})^{2},\frac{1}{2}\right\}

\displaystyle<\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}\sigma}=\mathcal{J}_{b}.

Hence the proof is complete.

\square

C.11
Proof of
Theorem

6.1

Proof.

It follows from the algebraic manipulations provided in
Hodges and
Lehmann 1963
that our modified estimator
\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}}
in (
2.12
), with
t(\cdot,\cdot)
as in (
2.5
) of the main paper, is given by

\displaystyle\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}}

\displaystyle=\median\{Y_{i}-Y_{j}:Z_{i}=1,Z_{j}=0,1\leq i,j\leq N\}

\displaystyle=\median\{a_{i}-b_{j}:Z_{i}=1,Z_{j}=0,1\leq i,j\leq N\}.

For simplicity of presentation, we restrict to the case of
m
is odd and
N
is even. Given
(z_{1},z_{2})\in\{0,1\}^{2}
, we define

\phi(z_{1},z_{2}):=\begin{cases}1&\mbox{if}\ z_{1}=1,z_{2}=0,\\
\infty&\mbox{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Furthermore, given any
x\in\mathbb{R}
, define

K_{m,N}(x):=\frac{1}{m(N-m)}\sum_{1\leq i\neq j\leq N}\mathbf{1}((a_{i}-b_{j})\phi(Z_{i},Z_{j})\leq x).

We will first prove the following two facts.

•

For any
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we have:

(C.13)

\mathbb{E}\left[K_{m,N}(x)\right]=\frac{1}{N(N-1)}\sum_{1\leq i\neq j\leq N}\mathbf{1}(a_{i}-b_{j}\leq x).

•

There exists a constant
C>0
(free of
N
) such that

(C.14)

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\mbox{Var}\left[K_{m,N}(x)\right]\leq CN^{-1}.

Proof of (
C.13
).
For
i\neq j
, note that
\mathbb{P}(Z_{i}=1,Z_{j}=0)=\frac{m(N-m)}{N(N-1)}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[K_{m,N}(x)]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m(N-m)}\sum_{1\leq i\neq j\leq N}\mathbf{1}(a_{i}-b_{j}\leq x)\frac{m(N-m)}{N(N-1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N(N-1)}\sum_{1\leq i\neq j\leq N}\mathbf{1}(a_{i}-b_{j}\leq x).

Proof of (
C.14
).

For notational convenience, define
\Theta_{i,j}:=(a_{i}-b_{j})\phi(Z_{i},Z_{j})
. Note that for
i,j,k,l
distinct, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Z_{i}=Z_{k}=1,Z_{j}=Z_{l}=0)=\frac{m(m-1)(N-m)(N-m-1)}{N(N-1)(N-2)(N-3)}.

We also observe that

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\frac{1}{m^{2}(N-m)^{2}}\sum_{(i,j,k,l)\ \mbox{distinct}}\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}(\Theta_{i,j}\leq x)\mathbf{1}(\Theta_{k,l}\leq x)]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m^{2}(N-m)^{2}}\sum_{(i,j,k,l)\ \mbox{distinct}}\mathbf{1}(a_{i}-b_{j}\leq x)\mathbf{1}(a_{k}-b_{l}\leq x)\frac{m(m-1)(N-m)(N-m-1)}{N(N-1)(N-2)(N-3)}

\displaystyle=\frac{(m-1)(N-m-1)}{m(N-m)N(N-1)(N-2)(N-3)}\left(\sum_{1\leq i\neq j\leq N}\mathbf{1}(a_{i}-b_{j}\leq x)\right)^{2}+O(N^{-1})

(C.15)

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbb{E}[K_{m,N}(x)]\right)^{2}+O(N^{-1}).

Here all the

terms are uniform bounds in
x
which holds as the indicators are all uniformly bounded by
1
irrespective of
x
. Note that we need
m/N
to be bounded away from

and
1
for the above to hold.

Next, we observe that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[K^{2}_{m,N}(x)]

\displaystyle=O(N^{-1})+\frac{1}{m^{2}(N-m)^{2}}\sum_{(i,j,k,l)\ \mbox{distinct}}\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}(\Theta_{i,j}\leq x)\mathbf{1}(\Theta_{k,l}\leq x)]

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbb{E}[K_{m,N}(x)]\right)^{2}+O(N^{-1}).

In the last equality above, we have used (
C.11
). This completes the proof of (
C.14
).

We are now in position to complete the proof of
Theorem

6.1
of the main paper.
Recall that
\mbox{med}_{N}=\median\{a_{i}-b_{j}:1\leq i\neq j\leq N\}
(see (
6.1
) of the main paper). Given any
\varepsilon>0
, we then have

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\mathbb{P}(\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}}-\mbox{med}_{N}>\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(K_{m,N}(\mbox{med}_{N}+\varepsilon)\leq\frac{1}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\bigg(\frac{1}{N(N-1)}\sum_{1\leq i\neq j\leq N}\mathbf{1}(a_{i}-b_{j}\leq\mbox{med}_{N}+\varepsilon)-K_{m,N}(\mbox{med}_{N}+\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\geq\frac{1}{N(N-1)}\sum_{1\leq i\neq j\leq N}\mathbf{1}(a_{i}-b_{j}\leq\mbox{med}_{N}+\varepsilon)-\frac{1}{2}\bigg)

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{\leq}\mathbb{P}\left(\bigg|\mathbb{E}[K_{m,N}(\mbox{med}_{N}+\varepsilon)]-K_{m,N}(\mbox{med}_{N}+\varepsilon)\bigg|\geq\frac{\sum_{1\leq i\neq j\leq N}\mathbf{1}(a_{i}-b_{j}\leq\mbox{med}_{N}+\varepsilon)}{N(N-1)}-\frac{1}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\overset{(b)}{\leq}\frac{\mbox{Var}(K_{m,N}(\mbox{med}_{N}+\varepsilon))}{\left(\frac{\sum_{1\leq i\neq j\leq N}\mathbf{1}(a_{i}-b_{j}\leq\mbox{med}_{N}+\varepsilon)}{N(N-1)}-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle\overset{(c)}{\leq}C\left(\sqrt{N}\left(\kappa_{N}^{(1)}(\varepsilon)-\frac{1}{2}\right)\right)^{-2}\to 0.

Here (a) follows from (
C.13
) and the fact that the definition of
\mbox{med}_{N}
implies
\frac{\sum_{1\leq i\neq j\leq N}\mathbf{1}(a_{i}-b_{j}\leq\mbox{med}_{N}+\varepsilon)}{N(N-1)}\geq\frac{1}{2}
, (b) follows from Markov’s inequality, and (c) is immediate from (
C.14
), the definition of
\kappa_{n}^{(1)}(\varepsilon)
, and assumption (
6.2
) of the main paper.
A similar calculation shows that

. This completes the proof.

For

Letting
\delta\to 0

(b)

Next we deal with
\Delta_{2,N},
for which the bound provided by

Recall that

\Delta_{2,N}=N^{-(2-\nu)}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\bm{1}{\left\{r_{N}(i,j)>0\right\}}-\Phi\left(\frac{r_{N}(i,j)}{\sigma_{N}}\right)\right),

The key idea is to use the bound in

E.2

\delta N^{-\nu}

Note that

\displaystyle(i,j)\in\mathcal{S}_{N,\delta}

\displaystyle\implies\delta N^{-\nu}\geq\left|r_{N}(i,j)\right|\geq\left|\left|b_{j}-b_{i}\right|-N^{-\nu}\right|

, and then

E.2

\displaystyle\left|\bm{1}{\left\{r_{N}(i,j)>0\right\}}-\Phi\left(\frac{r_{N}(i,j)}{\sigma_{N}}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sigma_{N}\delta^{-1}N^{\nu}\exp(-\delta^{2}N^{-2\nu}/2\sigma_{N}^{2})

\displaystyle=(\log N)^{-1}\delta^{-1}\exp\left(-\delta^{2}(\log N)^{2}/2\right)

Combining the above bounds, the following chain of inequalities emerges.

\displaystyle\left|\Delta_{2,N}\right|

\displaystyle\leq N^{-(2-\nu)}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left|\bm{1}{\left\{r_{N}(i,j)>0\right\}}-\Phi\left(\frac{r_{N}(i,j)}{\sigma_{N}}\right)\right|

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\left|\Delta_{2,N}\right|\leq 2\limsup_{N\to\infty}N^{-(2-\nu)}\left|\mathcal{S}_{N,\delta}\right|.

Letting
\delta\downarrow 0
and invoking (
C.17

Recall that

\Delta_{3,N}=N^{-(2-\nu)}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\bm{1}{\left\{\widehat{r}_{N}(i,j)>0\right\}}-\Phi\left(\frac{\widehat{r}_{N}(i,j)}{\sigma_{N}}\right)\right).

Proof of the above part is exactly same as what we did in part (b). For the other part, it suffices to show that for any

(C.18)

\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}\limsup_{N\to\infty}P\left(N^{-(2-\nu)}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{1}{\left\{\left|\widehat{b}_{j}-\widehat{b}_{i}\right|\in\left[(1-\delta)N^{-\nu},(1+\delta)N^{-\nu}\right]\right\}}>\varepsilon\right)=0

\displaystyle N^{-(2-\nu)}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{1}{\left\{\left|\widehat{b}_{j}-\widehat{b}_{i}\right|\in\left[(1-\delta)N^{-\nu},(1+\delta)N^{-\nu}\right]\right\}}\bm{1}_{E_{N}^{c}}

\displaystyle\leq N^{-(2-\nu)}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{1}{\left\{(1-\delta)N^{-\nu}\leq\widehat{b}_{j}-\widehat{b}_{i}<(1+\delta)N^{-\nu}\right\}}\bm{1}_{E_{N}^{c}}

\displaystyle N^{-(2-\nu)}\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{T}_{N,\delta}}\left|\Phi\left(\frac{\widehat{r}_{N}(i,j)}{\sigma_{N}}\right)-\Phi\left(\frac{r_{N}(i,j)}{\sigma_{N}}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sigma_{N}^{-1}N^{-(2-\nu)}\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{T}_{N,\delta}}\left|\widehat{r}_{N}(i,j)-r_{N}(i,j)\right|\exp\left(-\frac{r_{N}(i,j)^{2}\wedge\widehat{r}_{N}(i,j)^{2}}{2\sigma_{N}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=N^{2\nu-1/2}\log N\cdot\sqrt{N}\left|\widehat{\tau}_{N}-\tau_{0}\right|\exp\left(-\frac{\delta^{2}}{2}(\log N)^{2}\right).

Since
\widehat{\tau}_{N}-\tau_{0}=O_{P}(N^{-1/2})

\Delta_{4,N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\longrightarrow}}0
as

Combining the above steps, the proof is now complete.

\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}\limsup_{N\to\infty}P\left(N^{-(2-\nu)}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{1}{\left\{\big|\widehat{\widetilde{b}}_{j}-\widehat{\widetilde{b}}_{i}\big|\in\left[(1-\delta)N^{-\nu},(1+\delta)N^{-\nu}\right]\right\}}>\varepsilon\right)=0

because the above implies that

\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}\limsup_{N\to\infty}P\left(N^{-(2-\nu)}\left|\{(i,j):\left|\widehat{\widetilde{r}}_{N}(i,j)\right|\leq\delta N^{-\nu}\}\right|>\varepsilon\right)=0,

which further implies that

\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}\limsup_{N\to\infty}P\left(\left|\widetilde{\Delta}_{3,N}\right|>\varepsilon\right)=0.

In order to prove (
C.20
), we once again use the event
\widetilde{E}_{N,K}
and the trick with
I
and
J
. For brevity, we use the notation

\kappa_{j,i}:=(1+\sqrt{N}\|\bm{p}_{j}-\bm{p}_{i}\|)\left|\widehat{\tau}_{N}-\tau_{0}\right|.

Observe that,

\displaystyle N^{-(2-\nu)}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{1}{\left\{\big|\widehat{\widetilde{b}}_{j}-\widehat{\widetilde{b}}_{i}\big|\in\left[(1-\delta)N^{-\nu},(1+\delta)N^{-\nu}\right]\right\}}\bm{1}_{\widetilde{E}_{N,K}^{c}}

\displaystyle\leq N^{-(2-\nu)}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{1}{\left\{(1-\delta)N^{-\nu}\leq\widehat{\widetilde{b}}_{j}-\widehat{\widetilde{b}}_{i}<(1+\delta)N^{-\nu}\right\}}\bm{1}_{\widetilde{E}_{N,K}^{c}}

\displaystyle=N^{-(2-\nu)}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{1}{\left\{(1-\delta)N^{-\nu}\leq\widetilde{b}_{j}-\widetilde{b}_{i}-(\widehat{\tau}_{N}-\tau_{0})u_{N}(i,j)<(1+\delta)N^{-\nu}\right\}}\bm{1}_{\widetilde{E}_{N,K}^{c}}

\displaystyle\leq N^{-(2-\nu)}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{1}{\left\{-\kappa_{j,i}+(1-\delta)N^{-\nu}\leq\widetilde{b}_{j}-\widetilde{b}_{i}<(1+\delta)N^{-\nu}+\kappa_{j,i}\right\}}\bm{1}_{\widetilde{E}_{N,K}^{c}}

\displaystyle=N^{\nu}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{J,I}\left[\bm{1}{\left\{-\kappa_{J,I}+(1-\delta)N^{-\nu}\leq\widetilde{b}_{J}-\widetilde{b}_{I}<(1+\delta)N^{-\nu}+\kappa_{J,I}\right\}}\bm{1}_{\widetilde{E}_{N,K}^{c}}\right]

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\leq N^{\nu}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{J,I}\left[\mathbf{1}\Big\{-(1+K)\left|\widehat{\tau}_{N}-\tau_{0}\right|+(1-\delta)N^{-\nu}\leq\widetilde{b}_{J}-\widetilde{b}_{I}\right.\\
&\left.\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad<(1+\delta)N^{-\nu}+(1+K)\left|\widehat{\tau}_{N}-\tau_{0}\right|\Big\}\bm{1}_{\widetilde{E}_{N,K}^{c}}\right]\end{split}

\displaystyle\leq N^{-(2-\nu)}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}\Big\{-(1+K)\left|\widehat{\tau}_{N}-\tau_{0}\right|+(1-\delta)N^{-\nu}\leq\widetilde{b}_{j}-\widetilde{b}_{i}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad<(1+\delta)N^{-\nu}+(1+K)\left|\widehat{\tau}_{N}-\tau_{0}\right|\Big\}\bm{1}_{\widetilde{E}_{N,K}^{c}}

\displaystyle\leq N^{-(2-\nu)}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{1}{\left\{-(1+K)N^{-\nu^{\prime}}+(1-\delta)N^{-\nu}\leq\widetilde{b}_{j}-\widetilde{b}_{i}<(1+\delta)N^{-\nu}+(1+K)N^{-\nu^{\prime}}\right\}}.

Since
\nu^{\prime}>\nu,
it holds for all sufficiently large
N
that
(1+K)N^{-\nu^{\prime}}\leq\delta N^{-\nu}
, and consequently

\displaystyle\limsup_{N\to\infty}N^{-(2-\nu)}\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{1}{\left\{\big|\widehat{\widetilde{b}}_{j}-\widehat{\widetilde{b}}_{i}\big|\in\left[(1-\delta)N^{-\nu},(1+\delta)N^{-\nu}\right]\right\}}\bm{1}_{\widetilde{E}_{N,K}^{c}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{N\to\infty}N^{-(2-\nu)}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{1}{\left\{(1-2\delta)N^{-\nu}\leq\widetilde{b}_{j}-\widetilde{b}_{i}<(1+2\delta)N^{-\nu}\right\}}=4\delta\mathcal{J}_{b}.

Letting
\delta\to 0
here, we conclude that

(C.21)

\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}\limsup_{N\to\infty}N^{-(2-\nu)}\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{1}{\left\{\big|\widehat{\widetilde{b}}_{j}-\widehat{\widetilde{b}}_{i}\big|\in\left[(1-\delta)N^{-\nu},(1+\delta)N^{-\nu}\right]\right\}}\bm{1}_{\widetilde{E}_{N,K}^{c}}\right)=0.

Finally, for any
\varepsilon>0,

\displaystyle P\left(N^{-(2-\nu)}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{1}{\left\{\big|\widehat{\widetilde{b}}_{j}-\widehat{\widetilde{b}}_{i}\big|\in\left[(1-\delta)N^{-\nu},(1+\delta)N^{-\nu}\right]\right\}}>\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle\leq P\left(N^{-(2-\nu)}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{1}{\left\{\big|\widehat{\widetilde{b}}_{j}-\widehat{\widetilde{b}}_{i}\big|\in\left[(1-\delta)N^{-\nu},(1+\delta)N^{-\nu}\right]\right\}}\bm{1}_{\widetilde{E}_{N,K}^{c}}>\varepsilon/2\right)+P(\widetilde{E}_{N,K})

\displaystyle\leq(\varepsilon/2)^{-1}N^{-(2-\nu)}\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{1}{\left\{\big|\widehat{\widetilde{b}}_{j}-\widehat{\widetilde{b}}_{i}\big|\in\left[(1-\delta)N^{-\nu},(1+\delta)N^{-\nu}\right]\right\}}\bm{1}_{\widetilde{E}_{N,K}^{c}}\right)+P(\widetilde{E}_{N,K}).

Invoking (
C.21
) and using
\lim_{K\to\infty}\limsup_{N\to\infty}P(\widetilde{E}_{N,K})=0
, we complete the proof of (
C.20
), which, in turn, proves that
\widetilde{\Delta}_{3,N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\longrightarrow}}0
.

For
k=4
or
5,
the proof of
\widetilde{\Delta}_{k,N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\longrightarrow}}0
follows by making similar changes in the proof of
\Delta_{k,N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\longrightarrow}}0
in
Section

C.12
, just as we did for
k=1
or
3
.

\square

C.14
Proof of
Proposition

B.1

Proof.

The statistic
t_{N}
is defined as

t_{N}=\widehat{\bm{q}}_{N}^{\top}\bm{Z}_{N},\quad\widehat{q}_{N}(j)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{1}{\left\{Y_{N,i}-\tau_{0}Z_{N,i}\leq Y_{N,j}-\tau_{0}Z_{N,j}\right\}},\ 1\leq j\leq N.

Note, the distribution of
t_{N}
under
\tau=\tau_{N}
depends on
\tau_{N}
only through the vector
\widehat{\bm{q}}_{N}
. Under
\tau=\tau_{N}
we can write

(Y_{N,1}-\tau_{0}Z_{N,1},\dots,Y_{N,N}-\tau_{0}Z_{N,N})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}(b_{N,1}+(\tau_{N}-\tau_{0})Z_{N,1},\dots,b_{N,N}+(\tau_{N}-\tau_{0})Z_{N,N})

and hence the distribution of
\widehat{\bm{q}}_{N}^{\top}\bm{Z}_{N}
under
\tau=\tau_{N}
is same as the randomization distribution of
\bm{q}_{N}^{\top}\bm{Z}_{N}
where

q_{N,j}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{1}{\left\{b_{N,i}+(\tau_{N}-\tau_{0})Z_{N,i}\leq b_{N,j}+(\tau_{N}-\tau_{0})Z_{N,j}\right\}},\ 1\leq j\leq N.

In order words, we have
t_{N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}t^{*}_{N}
under
\tau=\tau_{N},
where

\displaystyle t^{*}_{N}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}Z_{N,j}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{1}{\left\{b_{N,i}+(\tau_{N}-\tau_{0})Z_{N,i}\leq b_{N,j}+(\tau_{N}-\tau_{0})Z_{N,j}\right\}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}Z_{N,j}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(Z_{N,i}\bm{1}{\left\{b_{N,i}\leq b_{N,j}\right\}}+(1-Z_{N,i})\bm{1}{\left\{hN^{-1/2}\leq b_{N,j}-b_{N,i}\right\}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}Z_{N,j}+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1,i\neq j}^{N}Z_{N,j}Z_{N,i}\bm{1}{\left\{b_{N,i}\leq b_{N,j}\right\}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1,i\neq j}^{N}Z_{N,j}(1-Z_{N,i})\bm{1}{\left\{hN^{-1/2}\leq b_{N,j}-b_{N,i}\right\}}.

Now when
h\geq 0,

\bm{1}{\left\{hN^{-1/2}\leq b_{N,j}-b_{N,i}\right\}}=\bm{1}{\left\{0\leq b_{N,j}-b_{N,i}\right\}}-\bm{1}{\left\{0\leq b_{N,j}-b_{N,i}<hN^{-1/2}\right\}}

and thus

t^{*}_{N}=m+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1,i\neq j}^{N}Z_{N,j}\bm{1}{\left\{b_{N,i}\leq b_{N,j}\right\}}-\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1,i\neq j}^{N}(1-Z_{N,i})Z_{N,j}\bm{1}{\left\{0\leq b_{N,j}-b_{N,i}<hN^{-1/2}\right\}}.

Similarly, for
h<0,

\bm{1}{\left\{hN^{-1/2}\leq b_{N,j}-b_{N,i}\right\}}=\bm{1}{\left\{0\leq b_{N,j}-b_{N,i}\right\}}+\bm{1}{\left\{hN^{-1/2}\leq b_{N,j}-b_{N,i}<0\right\}}

which gives us the desired expression.

\square

Appendix D
Some Technical Lemmas

Lemma D.1
.

Let
t(\bm{Z},\bm{Y}-\tau_{0}\bm{Z})
be a test statistic for testing
H_{0}:\tau=\tau_{0}
and
\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}}
be the estimator of
\tau
based on
t(\cdot,\cdot)
, as defined in (
2.12
) of the main paper. Assume that for all values of
\bm{y}
and
\bm{z}
,
t(\bm{z},\bm{y}-\tau\bm{z})
is a non-increasing function of
\tau
.
Let
\{c_{N}\}_{N\geq 1}
and
\{d_{N}\}_{N\geq 1}
be sequences of positive real numbers and
\mu_{N}
be the mean of
t_{N}=t(\bm{Z}_{N},\bm{Y}_{N}-\tau_{0}\bm{Z}_{N})
under
\tau=\tau_{0}
. Fix
h\in\mathbb{R}
and define
\tau_{N}:=\tau_{0}-h/c_{N}
. Suppose that

(D.1)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{P}_{\tau_{N}}\left(d_{N}(t_{N}-\mu_{N})\leq x\right)=G((x+hB)/A)

holds for every
x\in\mathbb{R}
, where
G
is a distribution function of a continuous random variable with mean
0
and variance
1
, and
A,B>0
are constants. Then it holds that

(D.2)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{P}_{\tau_{0}}\left(c_{N}(\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}}-\tau_{0})\leq h\right)=G(hB/A).

Proof.

Denote
\tau^{\prime}_{N}=\tau_{0}+h/c_{N}
. We use
Lemma

D.2
to write the following:

(D.3)

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}_{\tau_{0}}\left(t_{N}(\bm{Z},\bm{Y}-\tau^{\prime}_{N}\bm{Z})<\mu_{N}\right)&\leq\mathbb{P}_{\tau_{0}}\left(\widehat{\tau}^{*}\leq\tau^{\prime}_{N}\right)\\
&\leq\mathbb{P}_{\tau_{0}}\left(\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}}\leq\tau^{\prime}_{N}\right)\\
&\leq\mathbb{P}_{\tau_{0}}\left(\widehat{\tau}^{**}\leq\tau^{\prime}_{N}\right)\leq\mathbb{P}_{\tau_{0}}\left(t_{N}(\bm{Z},\bm{Y}-\tau^{\prime}_{N}\bm{Z})\leq\mu_{N}\right).\end{split}

In view of
Lemma

D.3
, the distribution of
t_{N}(\bm{Z},\bm{Y}-\tau^{\prime}_{N}\bm{Z})
under
\tau=\tau_{0}
is same as the distribution of
t_{N}=t_{N}(\bm{Z},\bm{Y}-\tau_{0}\bm{Z})
under
\tau=\tau_{N}
, which we denote by
G_{N}
. Then equation (
D.1
) tells us that
G_{N}(x/d_{N}+\mu_{N})\to G((x+hB)/A)
as
n\to\infty,
for every
x\in\mathbb{R}
. In particular, for
x=0
we can say that
G_{N}(\mu_{N})\to G(hB/A)
and that
G_{N}(\mu_{N}-)\to G(hB/A-)
which is also equal to
G(hB/A)
since
G
is continuous. Therefore both the extreme sides of (
D.3
) converge to
G(hB/A)
and thus by sandwich principle, equation (
D.2
) holds.

\square

Lemma D.2
.

Let
t(\cdot,\cdot)
be a test statistic such that for all values of
\bm{y}
and
\bm{z}
,
t(\bm{z},\bm{y}-\tau\bm{z})
is a non-increasing function of
\tau
. Then for any
h\in\mathbb{R},

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}_{\tau}\left(t(\bm{Z},\bm{Y}-a\bm{Z})<\mu\right)\leq\mathbb{P}_{\tau}\left(\widehat{\tau}^{*}\leq a\right)&\leq\mathbb{P}_{\tau}\left(\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}}\leq a\right)\\
&\leq\mathbb{P}_{\tau}\left(\widehat{\tau}^{**}\leq a\right)\leq\mathbb{P}_{\tau}\left(t(\bm{Z},\bm{Y}-a\bm{Z})\leq\mu\right).\end{split}

Proof.

The proof is straightforward from the definitions of
\widehat{\tau}^{*}
and
\widehat{\tau}^{**}
. First observe that
\tau^{*}\leq\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}}\leq\tau^{**}
holds almost surely. Now, if
a
is such that
t(\bm{Z},\bm{Y}-a\bm{Z})<\mu,
then

h\geq\sup\{\tau:t(\bm{Z},\bm{Y}-\tau\bm{Z})<\mu\}=\widehat{\tau}^{**}\implies h\geq\widehat{\tau}^{*},

so the left-most inequality follows. Next,

\widehat{\tau}^{*}\leq a\implies\widehat{\tau}^{\mathrm{R}}\leq a\implies\widehat{\tau}^{**}\leq a.

Finally, suppose that
a
is such that
t(\bm{Z},\bm{Y}-a\bm{Z})>\mu
. We split it into two cases:

(a) when
\widehat{\tau}^{*}<\widehat{\tau}^{**},
we have

t(\bm{Z},\bm{Y}-a\bm{Z})>\mu\implies a\leq\sup\{\tau:t(\bm{Z},\bm{Y}-\tau\bm{Z})>\mu\}=\widehat{\tau}^{*}<\widehat{\tau}^{**}.

(b) When
\widehat{\tau}^{*}=\widehat{\tau}^{**},
we have

t(\bm{Z},\bm{Y}-a\bm{Z})>\mu\implies h<\sup\{\tau:t(\bm{Z},\bm{Y}-\tau\bm{Z})>\mu\}=\widehat{\tau}^{*}=\widehat{\tau}^{**}.

This finishes the proof.

\square

Lemma D.3
.

The distribution of
t(\bm{Z},\bm{Y}-(\tau+\delta)\bm{Z})
under
\tau=\tau_{0}
is identical to the distribution of
t(\bm{Z},\bm{Y}-\tau\bm{Z})
under
\tau=\tau_{0}-\delta
.

Proof.

For any
x\in\mathbb{R},

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\tau_{0}}\left(t(\bm{Z},\bm{Y}-(\tau+\delta)\bm{Z})\leq x\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{\tau_{0}}\left(t(\bm{Z},\tau_{0}\bm{Z}+\bm{b}-(\tau+\delta)\bm{Z})\leq x\right)

\displaystyle=P\left(t(\bm{Z},(\tau_{0}-\delta)\bm{Z}+\bm{b}-\tau\bm{Z})\leq x\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{\tau_{0}-\delta}\left(t(\bm{Z},\bm{Y}-\tau\bm{Z})\leq x\right),

where the second step is due to the fact that the randomization distribution of
\bm{Z}
is free of
\tau
.
\square

Lemma D.4
.

If
\{b_{N,j}:1\leq j\leq N\}
be i.i.d. from a distribution with density
f_{b}(\cdot)
satisfying
\int_{\mathbb{R}}f_{b}^{2}(x)\,dx<\infty
, and
I_{h,N}
be as in (
2.15
) of the main paper, then

N^{-3/2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}I_{h,N}(b_{N,j}-b_{N,i})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{\longrightarrow}}h\int_{\mathbb{R}}f_{b}^{2}(x)\,dx.

Proof.

Let us denote
S_{N}=S_{N}(b_{N,1},\dots,b_{N,N})=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}I_{h,N}(b_{N,j}-b_{N,i})
.
Observe that for any fixed
i\neq j,
and
h>0,

\mathbb{E}\left(I_{h,N}(b_{N,j}-b_{N,i})\right)=P\left(0\leq b_{N,2}-b_{N,1}<hN^{-1/2}\right)=g(hN^{-1/2})-g(0)

where

g(x)=P(b_{N,2}-b_{N,1}\leq x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f_{b}(u+t)f_{b}(u)\,du\,dt,\ x\in\mathbb{R}.

Using the DCT for integrals, we argue that
g^{\prime}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f_{b}(u+x)f_{b}(u)du
. Hence

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}N^{-3/2}\mathbb{E}(S_{N})

\displaystyle=\lim_{N\to\infty}N^{-3/2}N^{2}(g(hN^{-1/2})-g(0))

\displaystyle=\lim_{N\to\infty}h\cdot\frac{g(hN^{-1/2})-g(0)}{hN^{-1/2}}

\displaystyle=hg^{\prime}(0)

(D.4)

\displaystyle=h\int_{\mathbb{R}}f_{b}(u)^{2}du.

Now we bound
\mathbb{E}(S_{N}-\mathbb{E}S_{N})^{2}
using the Efron-Stein inequality
(
Efron and Stein 1981
)
. For each
1\leq k\leq N,
let
b_{N,k}^{\prime}
be an i.i.d. copy of
b_{N,k},
independent of everything else, and define

S_{N}^{(k)}=S_{N}(b_{N,1},\dots,b_{N,k-1},b_{N,k}^{\prime},b_{N,k+1},\dots,b_{N,N}),\ 1\leq k\leq N.

Note that

\displaystyle S_{N}-S_{N}^{(k)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(I_{h,N}(b_{N,j}-b_{N,k})-I_{h,N}(b_{N,j}-b_{N,k}^{\prime})\right)

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(I_{h,N}(b_{N,k}-b_{N,j})-I_{h,N}(b_{N,k}^{\prime}-b_{N,j})\right).

An application of the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality yields the following.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{N}I_{h,N}(b_{N,j}-b_{N,k})-I_{h,N}(b_{N,j}-b_{N,k}^{\prime})\bigg)^{2}\ \Big|\ b_{N,1},\dots,b_{N,N}\right]

\displaystyle\leq N\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(I_{h,N}(b_{N,j}-b_{N,k})-I_{h,N}(b_{N,j}-b_{N,k}^{\prime})\right)^{2}\ \Big|\ b_{N,1},\dots,b_{N,N}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2N\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(I_{h,N}(b_{N,j}-b_{N,k})+I_{h,N}(b_{N,j}-b_{N,k}^{\prime})\right)\ \Big|\ b_{N,1},\dots,b_{N,N}\right]

\displaystyle=2N\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(I_{h,N}(b_{N,j}-b_{N,k})+\mathbb{E}\left[I_{h,N}(b_{N,j}-b_{N,k})\mid b_{N,j}\right]\right).

Applying the same argument to the second part yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{N}I_{h,N}(b_{N,k}-b_{N,j})-I_{h,N}(b_{N,k}^{\prime}-b_{N,j})\bigg)^{2}\ \Big|\ b_{N,1},\dots,b_{N,N}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2N\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(I_{h,N}(b_{N,k}-b_{N,j})+\mathbb{E}\left[I_{h,N}(b_{N,k}-b_{N,j})\mid b_{N,j}\right]\right).

Using the above bounds and a generalized Efron-Stein inequality (see
Proposition

E.1
),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(N^{-3/2}(S_{N}-\mathbb{E}S_{N}))^{6}

\displaystyle\lesssim N^{-9}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[(S_{N}-S_{N}^{(k)})^{2}\ \big|\ b_{N,1},\dots,b_{N,N}\right]\right]^{3}

\displaystyle\lesssim N^{-6}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(I_{h,N}(b_{N,j}-b_{N,k})+\mathbb{E}\left[I_{h,N}(b_{N,j}-b_{N,k})\mid b_{N,j}\right]\right)\right]^{3}

\displaystyle\quad+N^{-6}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(I_{h,N}(b_{N,k}-b_{N,j})+\mathbb{E}\left[I_{h,N}(b_{N,k}-b_{N,j})\mid b_{N,j}\right]\right)\right]^{3}

\displaystyle\lesssim N^{-6}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}I_{h,N}(b_{N,j}-b_{N,k})\right]^{3}+N^{-6}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[I_{h,N}(b_{N,j}-b_{N,k})\mid b_{N,j}\right]\right]^{3}

\displaystyle\quad+N^{-6}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[I_{h,N}(b_{N,k}-b_{N,j})\mid b_{N,j}\right]\right]^{3}.

We now show that the contribution of each of last three sums is at most of the order of
N^{-3/2},
which would complete the proof, with the aid of Borel-Cantelli lemma. We start with

\displaystyle N^{-6}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}I_{h,N}(b_{N,j}-b_{N,k})\right]^{3}

\displaystyle=N^{-6}\sum_{1\leq i_{1},j_{1},i_{2},j_{2},i_{3},j_{3}\leq N}\mathbb{E}\left[I_{h,N}(b_{N,j_{1}}-b_{N,i_{1}})I_{h,N}(b_{N,j_{2}}-b_{N,i_{2}})I_{h,N}(b_{N,j_{3}}-b_{N,i_{3}})\right]

When
|\{i_{1},j_{1},i_{2},j_{2},i_{3},j_{3}\}|\leq 4
, the number of ways to choose such a set of indices would be
O(N^{4}),
and the summands being bounded above by
1,
the contributions from these terms is
O(N^{-2})
. If
|\{i_{1},j_{1},i_{2},j_{2},i_{3},j_{3}\}|=6,
i.e., the indices are all distinct, we use the independence of the
b_{N,i}
’s to split the joint probability as the product of marginal probabilities, and thus the contribution from such terms becomes

\displaystyle N^{-6}\sum_{i_{1},j_{1},i_{2},j_{2},i_{3},j_{3}\text{ distinct}}\mathbb{E}I_{h,N}(b_{N,j_{1}}-b_{N,i_{1}})\mathbb{E}I_{h,N}(b_{N,j_{2}}-b_{N,i_{2}})\mathbb{E}I_{h,N}(b_{N,j_{3}}-b_{N,i_{3}})

\displaystyle\leq N^{-6}\left(\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq N}\mathbb{E}I_{h,N}(b_{N,j}-b_{N,i})\right)^{3}=N^{-3/2}\left(\mathbb{E}N^{-3/2}S_{N}\right)^{3}=O(N^{-3/2}).

Finally, consider the case where
|\{i_{1},j_{1},i_{2},j_{2},i_{3},j_{3}\}|=5,
i.e., exactly one index is repeated. Then at least two of the sets
\{i_{1},j_{1}\},\{i_{2},j_{2}\},\{i_{3},j_{3}\}
will make four distinct indices altogether. If the set left-out is
\{i_{3},j_{3}\}
, we can just upper bound
I_{h,N}(b_{N,j_{3}}-b_{N,i_{3}})
by
1
. Also, the number of ways of choosing the indices
\{i_{3},j_{3}\}
will be
O(N),
since one of them is repeated within
\{i_{1},j_{1},i_{2},j_{2}\}
. Using this idea,

\displaystyle N^{-6}\sum_{|i_{1},j_{1},i_{2},j_{2},i_{3},j_{3}|=5}\mathbb{E}\left[I_{h,N}(b_{N,j_{1}}-b_{N,i_{1}})I_{h,N}(b_{N,j_{2}}-b_{N,i_{2}})I_{h,N}(b_{N,j_{3}}-b_{N,i_{3}})\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim N^{-5}\sum_{i_{1},j_{1},i_{2},j_{2}\text{ distinct}}\mathbb{E}I_{h,N}(b_{N,j_{1}}-b_{N,i_{1}})\mathbb{E}I_{h,N}(b_{N,j_{2}}-b_{N,i_{2}})

\displaystyle\leq N^{-5}\left(\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq N}\mathbb{E}I_{h,N}(b_{N,j}-b_{N,i})\right)^{2}=N^{-2}\left(\mathbb{E}N^{-3/2}S_{N}\right)^{2}=O(N^{-2}).

Combining the above cases we conclude that
N^{-6}\mathbb{E}[\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}I_{h,N}(b_{N,j}-b_{N,k})]^{3}=O(N^{-3/2})
.
We next look at

\displaystyle N^{-6}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[I_{h,N}(b_{N,j}-b_{N,k})\mid b_{N,j}\right]\right]^{3}

\displaystyle=N^{-6}\sum_{1\leq i_{1},j_{1},i_{2},j_{2},i_{3},j_{3}\leq N}\mathbb{E}\left[I^{*}(b_{N,j_{1}},b_{N,i_{1}})I^{*}(b_{N,j_{2}},b_{N,i_{2}})I^{*}(b_{N,j_{3}},b_{N,i_{3}})\right]

where
I^{*}(b_{N,j},b_{N,i}):=\mathbb{E}\left[I_{h,N}(b_{N,j}-b_{N,i})\mid b_{N,j}\right]
, which is really a function of
b_{N,j}
. Thus, when all the
6
indices are distinct, the joint probability is split into marginals, and once again we can see that the contribution of these terms is
O(N^{-3/2})
. When
|\{i_{1},j_{1},i_{2},j_{2},i_{3},j_{3}\}|\leq 4
, the same argument applies as we gave earlier, and tells us that contribution from these terms would be
O(N^{-2})
. Finally, when
|\{i_{1},j_{1},i_{2},j_{2},i_{3},j_{3}\}|=5
we play the same trick applied in the previous case to conclude that contribution from these terms is

\displaystyle N^{-6}\sum_{|i_{1},j_{1},i_{2},j_{2},i_{3},j_{3}|=5}\mathbb{E}\left[I^{*}(b_{N,j_{1}},b_{N,i_{1}})I^{*}(b_{N,j_{2}},b_{N,i_{2}})I^{*}(b_{N,j_{3}},b_{N,i_{3}})\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim N^{-5}\sum_{i_{1},j_{1},i_{2},j_{2}\text{ distinct}}\mathbb{E}\left[I^{*}(b_{N,j_{1}},b_{N,i_{1}})I^{*}(b_{N,j_{2}},b_{N,i_{2}})\right]

\displaystyle=N^{-5}\sum_{i_{1},j_{1},i_{2},j_{2}\text{ distinct}}\mathbb{E}\left[I_{h,N}(b_{N,j_{1}}-b_{N,i_{1}})\right]\mathbb{E}\left[I_{h,N}(b_{N,j_{2}}-b_{N,i_{2}})\right]

\displaystyle\leq N^{-5}\left(\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq N}\mathbb{E}I_{h,N}(b_{N,j}-b_{N,i})\right)^{2}=N^{-2}\left(\mathbb{E}N^{-3/2}S_{N}\right)^{2}=O(N^{-2}).

Thus,
N^{-6}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[I_{h,N}(b_{N,j}-b_{N,k})\mid b_{N,j}\right]\right]^{3}=O(N^{-3/2})
. In a similar manner one can show that
N^{-6}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[I_{h,N}(b_{N,k}-b_{N,j})\mid b_{N,j}\right]\right]^{3}=O(N^{-3/2})
. Hence the proof.

\square

Lemma D.5
.

Suppose that the potential control outcomes
\{b_{N,j}\}_{1\leq j\leq N}
satisfy
1
. Define

q_{N,j}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{1}{\left\{b_{N,i}\leq b_{N,j}\right\}},\quad 1\leq j\leq N.

Then, as
N\to\infty
,

\overline{q}_{N}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}q_{N,j}=\frac{N+1}{2}+o(N^{1/2}),\quad\text{ and }\quad\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(q_{N,j}-\overline{q}_{N}\right)^{2}=\frac{N^{2}-1}{12}+o(N^{2}).

Proof.

We first break the ties in an arbitrary manner, and obtain
b_{N(1)}\leq b_{N(2)}\leq\dots\leq b_{N(N)}
. Define
r_{N,j}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{1}{\left\{b_{N,i}\leq b_{N(j)}\right\}}
. Note that for each
1\leq j\leq N,

\left|r_{N,j}-j\right|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{1}{\left\{b_{N,i}=b_{N(j)}\right\}}.

The last equation in conjunction with
1
yields the following bounds.

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left|r_{N,j}-j\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{1}{\left\{b_{N,i}=b_{N,j}\right\}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{1}{\left\{0\leq b_{N,j}-b_{N,i}<N^{-1/2}\right\}}\lesssim N^{3/2},

and

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(r_{N,j}-j\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{N}N\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{1}{\left\{b_{N,i}=b_{N,j}\right\}}

\displaystyle\leq N\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{1}{\left\{0\leq b_{N,j}-b_{N,i}<N^{-1/2}\right\}}\lesssim N^{5/2}.

Now,

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{i=1}^{N}r_{N,i}^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{N}i^{2}\right|

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}(r_{N,i}-i)^{2}+\left|\sum_{i=1}^{N}2i(r_{N,i}-i)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N}(r_{N,i}-i)^{2}+2\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}i^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(r_{N,i}-i)^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\lesssim N^{5/2}+N^{11/4}=o(N^{3}).

On the other hand,

\displaystyle N\left|\overline{r}_{N}^{2}-((N+1)/2)^{2}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{N}(r_{N,i}-i)\sum_{i=1}^{N}(r_{N,i}+i)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|r_{N,i}-i|\sum_{i=1}^{N}(|r_{N,i}-i|+2i)

\displaystyle\lesssim N^{-1}N^{3/2}\left(N^{3/2}+N^{2}\right)=o(N^{3}).

Combining the above with the fact that
\{r_{N,1},\dots,r_{N,N}\}
is same as
\{q_{N,1},\dots,q_{N,N}\}
,

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(q_{N,i}-\bar{q}_{N}\right)^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}r_{N,i}^{2}-N\bar{r}_{N}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(i-\frac{N+1}{2}\right)^{2}+o(N^{3})=\frac{N(N^{2}-1)}{12}+o(N^{3}).

This finishes the proof of the second assertion. To prove the first assertion, we fix any
\delta>0
and note that

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{j=1}^{N}q_{N,j}-\frac{N(N+1)}{2}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{j=1}^{N}(r_{N,j}-j)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left|r_{N,j}-j\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{1}{\left\{b_{N,i}=b_{N,j}\right\}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{1}{\left\{0\leq b_{N,j}-b_{N,i}<\delta N^{-1/2}\right\}}.

Invoking
1
we can say that

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\left|\sum_{j=1}^{N}q_{N,j}-\frac{N(N+1)}{2}\right|\leq\delta\mathcal{I}_{b}.

Now letting
\delta\to 0
completes the argument.

\square

Lemma D.6
.

Suppose that ranks
\{q_{N,j}\}
satisfy

\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(q_{N,j}-\overline{q}_{N}\right)^{2}=\frac{N(N^{2}-1)}{12}+o(N^{3})
as
N\to\infty
.
Let
t_{N}
be the Wilcoxon rank-sum statistic when the treatments are assigned by an
m
-out-of-
N
SRSWOR sample where
m/{N}\to\lambda\in(0,1)
as
N\to\infty
. Then, under
\tau=\tau_{0}
,

{\textrm{Var}}\left(t_{N}\right)\sim\frac{\lambda(1-\lambda)}{12}N^{3}\text{ as $N\to\infty$.}

Proof.

Recall from (
C.1
) that under
\tau=\tau_{0},
we can write
t_{N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}q_{N,j}Z_{N,j},
where
q_{N,j}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{1}{\left\{b_{N,i}\leq b_{N,j}\right\}}
.
Note that each
Z_{N,j}
is a Bernoulli(
m/N
) random variable, and for
i\neq j
we have

\displaystyle\text{Cov}(Z_{N,i},Z_{N,j})

\displaystyle=P(Z_{N,i}=1,Z_{N,j}=1)-P(Z_{N,i}=1)P(Z_{N,j}=1)

\displaystyle=\frac{m(m-1)}{N(N-1)}-\left(\frac{m}{N}\right)^{2}=-\frac{1}{N-1}\cdot\frac{m}{N}\left(1-\frac{m}{N}\right).

Hence we deduce that, under
\tau=\tau_{0}
,

\displaystyle{\textrm{Var}}\left(t_{N}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\text{Var}\left(q_{N,j}Z_{N,j}\right)+2\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq N}\text{Cov}\left(q_{N,i}Z_{N,i},q_{N,j}Z_{N,j}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}q_{N,j}^{2}\frac{m}{N}\left(1-\frac{m}{N}\right)-2\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq N}q_{N,i}q_{N,j}\frac{m}{N}\left(1-\frac{m}{N}\right)\frac{1}{N-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{m}{N}\left(1-\frac{m}{N}\right)\frac{N}{N-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(q_{N,j}-\overline{q}_{N}\right)^{2}.

In light of the given condition on the ranks, the above implies that

\displaystyle{\textrm{Var}}\left(t_{N}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{m}{N}\left(1-\frac{m}{N}\right)\frac{N}{N-1}\left(\frac{N(N^{2}-1)}{12}+o(N^{3})\right)\sim\lambda(1-\lambda)\frac{N^{3}}{12},

which completes the proof.

\square

Lemma D.7
.

Let
\bm{Z}_{N}
be the vector of treatment indicators when the treatments are assigned by
m
-out-of-
N
SRSWOR where
{m(N)}/{N}\to\lambda\in(0,1)
as
N\to\infty
. Define

S_{N}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1,i\neq j}^{N}(1-Z_{N,i})Z_{N,j}I_{h,N}(b_{N,j}-b_{N,i})

where
I_{h,N}
is defined in (
2.15
) of the main paper. Then under
1
it holds that

N^{-3/2}S_{N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\longrightarrow}}h\lambda(1-\lambda)\mathcal{I}_{b}.

Proof.

We imitate the proof of
Proposition

2.3
. The first step is to note that

N^{-3/2}\mathbb{E}S_{N}=\frac{m}{N}\left(1-\frac{m-1}{N-1}\right)N^{-3/2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1,i\neq j}^{N}I_{h,N}(b_{N,j}-b_{N,i})\to h\lambda(1-\lambda)\mathcal{I}_{b}.

It therefore remains to show that
N^{-3}\text{Var}\left(S_{N}\right)\to 0
.
For brevity, let us abuse the notation
I_{h,N}(b_{N,j}-b_{N,i})
and write
I_{h,N}(i,j)
instead. We have

\text{Var}\left(S_{N}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{l=1,l\neq k}^{N}\text{Cov}\left((1-Z_{N,i})Z_{N,j},(1-Z_{N,k})Z_{N,l}\right)I_{h,N}(i,j)I_{h,N}(k,l).

Note,
2\leq|\{i,j,k,l\}|\leq 4
. Consider the following cases.

(a)

|\{i,j,k,l\}|=2,
i.e.,
(i,j)=(k,l)
. Note that
\text{Var}((1-Z_{N,i})Z_{N,j})=p_{N}(1-p_{N})
where
p_{N}=P(Z_{N,i}=0,Z_{N,j}=1)=\frac{m}{N}\frac{N-m}{N-1}\sim\lambda(1-\lambda)
. Hence the contribution of these terms in
\text{Var}(S_{N})
is given by

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{N}p_{N}(1-p_{N})I^{2}_{h,N}(i,j)\lesssim\lambda(1-\lambda)\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{N}I_{h,N}(i,j)\lesssim N^{3/2}.

(b)

|\{i,j,k,l\}|=4,
i.e., all
4
indices are distinct. Note that in this case

\displaystyle\text{Cov}\left((1-Z_{N,i})Z_{N,j},(1-Z_{N,k})Z_{N,l}\right)

\displaystyle=P(Z_{N,i}=0,Z_{N,j}=1,Z_{N,k}=0,Z_{N,l}=1)-p_{N}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\binom{N-4}{m-2}}{\binom{N}{m}}-\left(\frac{m}{N}\frac{N-m}{N-1}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{m}{N}\frac{N-m}{N-1}\left(\frac{(m-1)(N-m-1)}{(N-2)(N-3)}-\frac{m}{N}\frac{N-m}{N-1}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{m}{N}\frac{N-m}{N-1}\frac{(4mN^{2}-4m^{2}N-N^{3}+6m^{2}-6mN+2N^{2}-N)}{N(N-1)(N-2)(N-3)}

\displaystyle\sim-\lambda(1-\lambda)(1-2\lambda)^{2}N^{-1}.

Hence the contribution
u_{N}
of these terms in
\text{Var}(S_{N})
satisfies the following.

u_{N}\lesssim N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{l=1,l\neq k}^{N}I_{h,N}(i,j)I_{h,N}(k,l)\lesssim N^{-1+3/2+3/2}=N^{2}.

(c)

|\{i,j,k,l\}|=3
. Here we have 4 sub-cases:

sub-case

\mathbb{E}(1-Z_{N,i})Z_{N,j}(1-Z_{N,k})Z_{N,l}

\text{Cov}\left((1-Z_{N,i})Z_{N,j},(1-Z_{N,k})Z_{N,l}\right)

i=k,j\neq l

P(Z_{N,i}=0,Z_{N,j}=1,Z_{N,l}=1)

\frac{m(m-1)(N-m)}{N(N-1)(N-2)}-p_{N}^{2}\sim\lambda^{3}(1-\lambda)

i\neq k,j=l

P(Z_{N,i}=0,Z_{N,j}=1,Z_{N,k}=0)

\frac{m(N-m)(N-m-1)}{N(N-1)(N-2)}-p_{N}^{2}\sim\lambda(1-\lambda)^{3}

i=l,j\neq k

0

-p_{N}^{2}\sim-\lambda^{2}(1-\lambda)^{2}

i\neq l,j=k

0

-p_{N}^{2}\sim-\lambda^{2}(1-\lambda)^{2}

Hence, if
v_{n}
be the contribution these terms in
\text{Var}(S_{N})
, then

\displaystyle v_{N}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{i,j,l\text{ distinct}}I_{h,N}(i,j)I_{h,N}(i,l)+\sum_{i,j,k\text{ distinct}}I_{h,N}(i,j)I_{h,N}(k,j)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{i,j,k\text{ distinct}}I_{h,N}(i,j)I_{h,N}(k,i)+\sum_{i,j,l\text{ distinct}}I_{h,N}(i,j)I_{h,N}(j,l)

\displaystyle\leq 4N\sum_{i,j\neq i}I_{h,N}(i,j)\lesssim N^{1+3/2}=N^{5/2}.

Combining the three cases, we can say that
N^{-3}\text{Var}(S_{N})\lesssim N^{-3}(N^{3/2}+N^{2}+N^{5/2})=o(1)
.

\square

Lemma D.8
.

Suppose that
\bm{b}_{N}=\bm{X}_{N}\bm{\beta}_{N}+\bm{{\varepsilon}}_{N}
, where
{\varepsilon}_{N,1},\dots,{\varepsilon}_{N,N}
are i.i.d
.
from
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
. Define, for any fixed
h
,

(D.5)

J_{N}:=N^{-3/2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}I_{h,N}(\widetilde{b}_{N,j}-\widetilde{b}_{N,i}),

where
\widetilde{b}_{N,j}
is defined in (
3.5
), and
I_{h,N}
is defined in (
2.15
) of the main paper.
Then
\mathbb{E}J_{N}\to h(2\sqrt{\pi}\sigma)^{-1}
, and
{\textrm{Var}}(J_{N})\to 0
, implying that
2
holds in probability, with
\mathcal{J}_{b}=(2\sqrt{\pi}\sigma)^{-1}
.

Proof.

We do the proof for
h>0
, the other case will be similar. For simplicity in notation, we omit the index
N
in
\widetilde{b}_{N,j},Z_{N,j},\bm{p}_{N,j}
, etc. throughout this proof. Observe that under the linear model, we have
\bm{\widetilde{b}}=(\bm{I}-\bm{P}_{\bm{X}})\bm{b}=(\bm{I}-\bm{P}_{\bm{X}})\bm{{\varepsilon}}
. Hence for any pair
(i,j)
of distinct indices,
\widetilde{b}_{j}-\widetilde{b}_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{ij}^{2})
, where
\sigma_{ij}^{2}:=\sigma^{2}(2-\|\bm{p}_{j}-\bm{p}_{i}\|^{2})
(this follows from the fact that
\bm{P}_{\bm{X}}
is a projection matrix).
Thus

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}J_{N}

\displaystyle=N^{-3/2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}P\left(0\leq\widetilde{b}_{j}-\widetilde{b}_{i}<hN^{-1/2}\right)=N^{-3/2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\Phi\left(\sigma_{i,j}^{-1}hN^{-1/2}\right)-\Phi(0)\right).

For any
\delta>0
and
N\in\mathbb{N}
, define

(D.6)

S_{N,\delta}:=\{(i,j):1\leq i,j\leq N,\|\bm{p}_{j}-\bm{p}_{i}\|\leq\delta\}.

Invoking
Lemma

E.1
, we get

|S_{N,\delta}^{c}|=|\{(i,j):1\leq i,j\leq N,\|\bm{p}_{j}-\bm{p}_{i}\|>\delta\}|\leq\delta^{-2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|\bm{p}_{j}-\bm{p}_{i}\|^{2}=O(N).

Consequently,

N^{-3/2}\sum_{(i,j)\in S_{N,\delta}^{c}}\left(\Phi\left(\sigma_{i,j}^{-1}hN^{-1/2}\right)-\Phi(0)\right)=O(N^{-1/2}).

For
(i,j)\in S_{N,\delta}
we apply the Taylor theorem to conclude that there exists
\psi_{i,j}\in(0,\sigma_{i,j}^{-1}hN^{-1/2})
such that

\displaystyle\Phi\left(\sigma_{i,j}^{-1}hN^{-1/2}\right)-\Phi(0)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{i,j}^{-1}hN^{-1/2}\phi\left(0\right)+\sigma_{i,j}^{-2}h^{2}N^{-1}\phi^{\prime}(\psi_{i,j}).

Since
\phi^{\prime}
is bounded on
\mathbb{R},
we can say that

\displaystyle\Delta_{N}:=\left|\mathbb{E}J_{N}-N^{-3/2}\sum_{(i,j)\in S_{N,\delta}}\sigma_{i,j}^{-1}hN^{-1/2}\phi\left(0\right)\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim N^{-1/2}+N^{-3/2}\sum_{(i,j)\in S_{N,\delta}}\sigma_{i,j}^{-2}h^{2}N^{-1}

\displaystyle\lesssim N^{-1/2}+N^{-5/2}\sum_{(i,j)\in S_{N,\delta}}(2-\|\bm{p}_{j}-\bm{p}_{i}\|^{2})^{-1}

\displaystyle\lesssim N^{-1/2}+N^{-5/2}\sum_{(i,j)\in S_{N,\delta}}(2-\delta^{2})^{-1}\lesssim N^{-1/2}.

Hence
\Delta_{N}\to 0
as
N\to\infty
, and thus

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}J_{N}=\limsup_{N\to\infty}N^{-3/2}\sum_{(i,j)\in S_{N,\delta}}\sigma_{i,j}^{-1}hN^{-1/2}\phi\left(0\right)\leq h\sigma^{-1}\phi(0)(2-\delta^{2})^{-1/2}.

Since the above holds for every
\delta>0
, we can say that
\limsup_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}J_{N}\leq\frac{h}{2\sqrt{\pi}\sigma}.

On the other hand, the fact that
\Delta_{N}\to 0
also yields the following:

\displaystyle\liminf_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}J_{N}

\displaystyle=\liminf_{N\to\infty}N^{-3/2}\sum_{(i,j)\in S_{N,\delta}}(2-\|\bm{p}_{j}-\bm{p}_{i}\|)^{-1/2}h\sigma^{-1}N^{-1/2}\phi\left(0\right)

\displaystyle\geq\liminf_{N\to\infty}N^{-2}|S_{N,\delta}|\cdot h\sigma^{-1}\phi(0)\cdot 2^{-1/2}=h\sigma^{-1}\phi(0)\cdot 2^{-1/2},

where in the last step we again used the fact that
|S_{N,\delta}^{c}|=O(N)
. We thus conclude that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}J_{N}=\frac{h}{2\sqrt{\pi}\sigma}.

Next we show that
{\textrm{Var}}(J_{N})\to 0
as
N\to\infty
. First,

\mathbb{E}J_{N}^{2}=N^{-3}\mathbb{E}\sum_{i,j,k,l}\bm{1}{\left\{0\leq\widetilde{b}_{j}-\widetilde{b}_{i}<hN^{-1/2},0\leq\widetilde{b}_{l}-\widetilde{b}_{k}<hN^{-1/2}\right\}}.

Now, as in the proof of
Proposition

2.3
, observe that the contribution of the terms with repeated indices in the above summation is negligible (since
2
holds and we already have shown that
\mathbb{E}J_{N}
converges). To analyze the terms with distinct indices, note that for any
4
distinct indices
i,j,k,l
,

\begin{pmatrix}\widetilde{b}_{j}-\widetilde{b}_{i}\\
\widetilde{b}_{l}-\widetilde{b}_{k}\end{pmatrix}\sim\mathcal{N}_{2}\left(\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\end{pmatrix},\sigma^{2}\begin{pmatrix}2-\|\bm{p}_{j}-\bm{p}_{i}\|^{2}&-(\bm{p}_{j}-\bm{p}_{i})^{\top}(\bm{p}_{l}-\bm{p}_{k})\\
-(\bm{p}_{j}-\bm{p}_{i})^{\top}(\bm{p}_{l}-\bm{p}_{k})&2-\|\bm{p}_{l}-\bm{p}_{k}\|^{2}\end{pmatrix}\right).

Let
\rho_{i,j,k,l}:=\text{corr}(\widetilde{b}_{j}-\widetilde{b}_{i},\widetilde{b}_{l}-\widetilde{b}_{k})
.
Now we again play the trick of splitting the sum into two groups, such that in one group
|\rho_{i,j,k,l}|
is small, whereas for the other group the number of summands is small. Fix any
\delta\in(0,1)
, and consider the set
S_{N,\delta}
defined in (
D.6
). If
(i,j)
or
(k,l)
does not belong to
S_{N,\delta}
, then that brings down the count for such summands. To be precise,

\displaystyle N^{-3}\sum_{(i,j)\in S_{N,\delta}^{c}\text{ or }(k,l)\in S_{N,\delta}^{c}}P(0\leq\widetilde{b}_{j}-\widetilde{b}_{i}\leq hN^{-1/2},0\leq\widetilde{b}_{l}-\widetilde{b}_{k}\leq hN^{-1/2})

\displaystyle\leq 2N^{-3}\sum_{1\leq j,i\leq N}\sum_{(k,l)\in S_{N,\delta}^{c}}P(0\leq\widetilde{b}_{j}-\widetilde{b}_{i}\leq hN^{-1/2})

\displaystyle\lesssim N^{-2}\sum_{1\leq j,i\leq N}P(0\leq\widetilde{b}_{j}-\widetilde{b}_{i}\leq hN^{-1/2})=N^{-1/2}\mathbb{E}J_{N}.

Since
\mathbb{E}J_{N}
converges, the above shows that the contribution from such indices are also negligible. Finally, for
(i,j),(k,l)\in S_{N,\delta}
, we have

|\rho_{i,j,k,l}|=\frac{\left|(\bm{p}_{j}-\bm{p}_{i})^{\top}(\bm{p}_{l}-\bm{p}_{k})\right|}{\sqrt{2-\|\bm{p}_{j}-\bm{p}_{i}\|^{2}}\sqrt{2-\|\bm{p}_{l}-\bm{p}_{k}\|^{2}}}\leq\frac{\delta}{2-\delta^{2}}\leq\delta,

and hence
Lemma

E.3
tells us that

\displaystyle\big|P(0\leq\widetilde{b}_{j}-\widetilde{b}_{i}\leq hN^{-1/2},0\leq\widetilde{b}_{l}-\widetilde{b}_{k}\leq hN^{-1/2})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-P(0\leq\widetilde{b}_{j}-\widetilde{b}_{i}\leq hN^{-1/2})P(0\leq\widetilde{b}_{l}-\widetilde{b}_{k}\leq hN^{-1/2})\big|

\displaystyle\leq|\rho_{i,j,k,l}|\left((1-\rho_{i,j,k,l}^{2})^{-1/2}+(1-|\rho_{i,j,k,l}|)^{-2}\right)h^{2}N^{-2}\sigma_{i,j}^{-1}\sigma_{k,l}^{-1}

\displaystyle\leq h^{2}N^{-2}\sigma^{-2}\delta\left((1-\delta^{2})^{-1/2}+(1-\delta)^{-2}\right).

Thus we have shown that

\begin{split}\left|\mathbb{E}J_{N}^{2}-\left(N^{-3/2}\sum_{(i,j)\in S_{N,\delta}}P\left(0\leq\widetilde{b}_{j}-\widetilde{b}_{i}\leq hN^{-1/2}\right)\right)^{2}\right|\\
\leq h^{2}\sigma^{-2}\delta\left((1-\delta^{2})^{-1/2}+(1-\delta)^{-2}\right)+O(N^{-1/2}).\end{split}

Now for every
\delta\in(0,1)
, we have
|S_{N,\delta}^{c}|=O(N)
, so it follows that

\limsup_{N\to\infty}{\textrm{Var}}(J_{N})\leq h^{2}\sigma^{-2}\delta\left((1-\delta^{2})^{-1/2}+(1-\delta)^{-2}\right).

Since
\delta\in(0,1)
is arbitrary here, letting
\delta\to 0
completes the proof.

\square

Lemma D.13
.

Define
D_{N}
and
Q_{N}
as in (
C.8
). It holds under
2
that
N^{-3/2}D_{N}=o_{P}(1)
and
N^{-3/2}Q_{N}=o_{P}(1)
as

.

Proof.

We first focus on
D_{N}
. In view of
Lemma

D.11
, it suffices to show that

\lim_{N\to\infty}N^{-3/2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sqrt{\theta_{N,i,j}}=0,

where
\theta_{N,i,j}
is defined in (
D.8
).
Fix
h
for the moment, and introduce the notation

r_{N}(i,j):=\widetilde{b}_{N,j}-\widetilde{b}_{N,i}-hN^{-1/2},\ 1\leq i,j\leq N.

Note,

\displaystyle\xi_{N,i,j}-\widetilde{\xi}_{N,i,j}

\displaystyle=\bm{1}{\left\{hN^{-1/2}(\bm{p}_{N,i}-\bm{p}_{N,j})^{\top}\bm{Z}_{N}\leq r_{N}(i,j)\right\}}-\bm{1}{\left\{0\leq r_{N}(i,j)\right\}}

\displaystyle=\bm{1}{\left\{hN^{-1/2}(\bm{p}_{N,i}-\bm{p}_{N,j})^{\top}\bm{Z}_{N}\leq r_{N}(i,j)<0\right\}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\bm{1}{\left\{hN^{-1/2}(\bm{p}_{N,i}-\bm{p}_{N,j})^{\top}\bm{Z}_{N}>r_{N}(i,j)\geq 0\right\}}

\displaystyle=\bm{1}{\left\{hN^{-1/2}\left|(\bm{p}_{N,i}-\bm{p}_{N,j})^{\top}\bm{Z}_{N}\right|\geq\left|r_{N}(i,j)\right|\right\}}\left(\bm{1}{\left\{r_{N}(i,j)<0\right\}}-\bm{1}{\left\{r_{N}(i,j)\geq 0\right\}}\right).

Now applying the Markov inequality, we get

\displaystyle\sqrt{\theta_{N,i,j}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{1/2}\left[|\xi_{N,i,j}-\widetilde{\xi}_{N,i,j}|\ \Big|\ Z_{N,j}=1\right]

\displaystyle=\left[P\left(hN^{-1/2}\left|(\bm{p}_{N,i}-\bm{p}_{N,j})^{\top}\bm{Z}_{N}\right|\ \geq\ \left|r_{N}(i,j)\right|\ \Big|\ Z_{N,j}=1\right)\right]^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq h^{2}N^{-1}r_{N}(i,j)^{-2}\mathbb{E}^{1/2}\left[\left(\left(\bm{p}_{N,i}-\bm{p}_{N,j}\right)^{\top}\bm{Z}_{N}\right)^{4}\ \Big|\ Z_{N,j}=1\right].

Lemma

E.1
.
The key idea is to use this bound only for those
i,j
for which

is at least as large as
\delta N^{-1/2}
. Towards that, fix
\delta>0
and define

Then

\displaystyle(i,j)\not\in\mathcal{S}_{N,\delta}\implies\sqrt{\theta_{N,i,j}}\leq a^{2}\delta^{-2}\mathbb{E}^{1/2}\left[\left(\left(\bm{p}_{N,i}-\bm{p}_{N,j}\right)^{\top}\bm{Z}_{N}\right)^{4}\ \Big|\ Z_{N,j}=1\right].

\displaystyle N^{-3/2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sqrt{\theta_{N,i,j}}

\displaystyle\leq N^{-3/2}\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{S}_{N,\delta}}1+N^{-3/2}\sum_{(i,j)\not\in\mathcal{S}_{N,\delta}}^{N}a^{2}\delta^{-2}\mathbb{E}^{1/2}\left[\left(\left(\bm{p}_{N,i}-\bm{p}_{N,j}\right)^{\top}\bm{Z}_{N}\right)^{4}\ \Big|\ Z_{N,j}=1\right]

\displaystyle\leq N^{-3/2}\left|\mathcal{S}_{N,\delta}\right|+a^{2}\delta^{-2}N^{-3/2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}^{1/2}\left[\left(\left(\bm{p}_{N,i}-\bm{p}_{N,j}\right)^{\top}\bm{Z}_{N}\right)^{4}\ \Big|\ Z_{N,j}=1\right]

\displaystyle=N^{-3/2}\left|\mathcal{S}_{N,\delta}\right|+a^{2}\delta^{-2}(\rank(\bm{X}_{N})-1)\cdot O(N^{-1/2}).

E.1
. Letting
N\to\infty
and then
\delta\downarrow 0
, it follows that

\limsup_{N\to\infty}N^{-3/2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sqrt{\theta_{N,i,j}}\leq\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}\limsup_{N\to\infty}N^{-3/2}\left|\mathcal{S}_{N,\delta}\right|.

D.9

which finishes the proof for

.

Lemma D.16
.

For each
N\geq 1,
let
b_{N,1},\dots,b_{N,N}
be i.i.d. from an unknown distribution
F_{b}
with density
f_{b}(\cdot)
, and
I_{h,N,\nu}
be defined in (
A.2
) of the main paper, then for any
0<\nu\leq 1/2,

N^{-(2-\nu)}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}I_{h,N,\nu}(b_{N,j}-b_{N,i})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\longrightarrow}}h\int_{\mathbb{R}}f_{b}^{2}(x)dx.

Proof.

We proved the above for
\nu=1/2
in
Lemma

D.4
, so assume now that
\nu\in(0,1/2)
. Define

S_{N}:=S_{N}(b_{N,1},\dots,b_{N,N}):=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}I_{h,N,\nu}(b_{N,j}-b_{N,i}).

Observe that for any fixed
i\neq j,
and
h>0,

\mathbb{E}\left(I_{h,N,\nu}(b_{N,j}-b_{N,i})\right)=P\left(0\leq b_{N,2}-b_{N,1}<hN^{-\nu}\right)=g(hN^{-\nu})-g(0)

where

g(x)=P(b_{N,2}-b_{N,1}\leq x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f_{b}(u+t)f_{b}(u)\,du\,dt,\ x\in\mathbb{R}.

Using the DCT for integrals, we argue that

g^{\prime}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f_{b}(u+x)f_{b}(u)du.

Hence

(D.10)

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}N^{-(2-\nu)}\mathbb{E}(S_{N})

\displaystyle=\lim_{N\to\infty}N^{-(2-\nu)}N^{2}(g(hN^{-\nu})-g(0))

\displaystyle=\lim_{N\to\infty}h\cdot\frac{g(hN^{-\nu})-g(0)}{hN^{-\nu}}

\displaystyle=hg^{\prime}(0)

\displaystyle=h\int_{\mathbb{R}}f_{b}(u)^{2}du.

The case
h<0
can be handled similarly, and the case
h=0
is straight-forward.
Next we bound
\mathbb{E}(S_{N}-\mathbb{E}S_{N})^{2}
using the Efron-Stein inequality
(
Efron and Stein 1981
)
. For each
1\leq k\leq N,
let
b_{N,k}^{\prime}
be an i.i.d. copy of
b_{N,k},
independent of everything else, and define

S_{N}^{(k)}=S_{N}(b_{N,1},\dots,b_{N,k-1},b_{N,k}^{\prime},b_{N,k+1},\dots,b_{N,N}),\ 1\leq k\leq N.

Note,

\displaystyle S_{N}-S_{N}^{(k)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(I_{h,N,\nu}(b_{N,j}-b_{N,k})-I_{h,N,\nu}(b_{N,j}-b_{N,k}^{\prime})\right)

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(I_{h,N,\nu}(b_{N,k}-b_{N,j})-I_{h,N,\nu}(b_{N,k}^{\prime}-b_{N,j})\right).

Hence
\big|S_{N}-S_{N}^{(k)}\big|\leq 4N
almost surely. The Efron-Stein inequality tells us that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(N^{-(2-\nu)}(S_{N}-\mathbb{E}S_{N}))^{2}

\displaystyle\leq N^{-(4-2\nu)}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[(S_{N}-S_{N}^{(k)})^{2}\ \big|\ b_{N,1},\dots,b_{N,N}\right]\right]

\displaystyle\leq N^{-(4-2\nu)}\cdot 16N^{3}=16N^{-(1-2\nu)}.

Since
\nu<1/2,
we can now invoke (
D.10
) to get the desired conclusion.

\square

is idempotent, we derive the following identity.

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|\bm{p}_{N,i}-\bm{p}_{N,j}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\bm{P}_{\bm{X}_{N}}(i,i)+\bm{P}_{\bm{X}_{N}}(j,j)-2\bm{P}_{\bm{X}_{N}}(i,j)\right)

\displaystyle=2N\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{P}_{\bm{X}_{N}}(i,i)-2\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{p}_{N,i}^{\top}\bm{1}

(E.4)

\displaystyle=2N\left(\Tr\left(\bm{P}_{\bm{X}_{N}}\right)-1\right)=2N\left(\rank(\bm{X}_{N})-1\right).

Now
\bm{1}
belongs to the column space of
\bm{X}_{N},
so
\mathbb{E}\left[(\bm{p}_{N,i}-\bm{p}_{N,j})^{\top}\bm{Z}_{N}\right]=0
for each
i,j
. Also note that
{\textrm{Var}}(\bm{Z}_{N})
is of the form
\alpha\bm{I}+\beta\bm{1}\bm{1}^{\top}
, where
\alpha=\frac{m}{N}(1-\frac{m}{N})
. Consequently,

\displaystyle{\textrm{Var}}\left[(\bm{p}_{N,i}-\bm{p}_{N,j})^{\top}\bm{Z}_{N}\right]

\displaystyle=(\bm{p}_{N,i}-\bm{p}_{N,j})^{\top}\left(\alpha\bm{I}+\beta\bm{1}\bm{1}^{\top}\right)(\bm{p}_{N,i}-\bm{p}_{N,j})=\frac{m}{N}\left(1-\frac{m}{N}\right)\|\bm{p}_{N,i}-\bm{p}_{N,j}\|^{2}.

Hence

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[\left((\bm{p}_{N,i}-\bm{p}_{N,j})^{\top}\bm{Z}_{N}\right)^{2}\right]\leq\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|\bm{p}_{N,i}-\bm{p}_{N,j}\|^{2}.

Thus, (
E.1
) follows for
r=1
.

To prove it for
r=2,
fix
i
and
j
for the moment and write
\left(\bm{p}_{N,i}-\bm{p}_{N,j}\right)^{\top}\bm{Z}_{N}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}v_{k}Z_{k}
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg(\sum_{k=1}^{N}v_{k}Z_{k}\bigg)^{4}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k_{1}=1}^{N}v_{k_{1}}^{4}\mathbb{E}Z_{1}+\sum_{k_{1},k_{2}\text{ distinct}}(3v_{k_{1}}^{2}v_{k_{2}}^{2}+4v_{k_{1}}^{3}v_{k_{2}})\mathbb{E}Z_{1}Z_{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}\text{ distinct}}6v_{k_{1}}^{2}v_{k_{2}}v_{k_{3}}\mathbb{E}Z_{1}Z_{2}Z_{3}+\sum_{k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},k_{4}\text{ distinct}}v_{k_{1}}v_{k_{2}}v_{k_{3}}v_{k_{4}}\mathbb{E}Z_{1}Z_{2}Z_{3}Z_{4}.

Now
\sum_{k=1}^{N}v_{k}=(\bm{p}_{N,i}-\bm{p}_{N,j})^{\top}\bm{1}=0,
so we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},k_{4}\text{ distinct}}v_{k_{1}}v_{k_{2}}v_{k_{3}}v_{k_{4}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}\text{ distinct}}v_{k_{1}}v_{k_{2}}v_{k_{3}}(-v_{k_{1}}-v_{k_{2}}-v_{k_{3}})

\displaystyle=-3\sum_{k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}\text{ distinct}}v_{k_{1}}^{2}v_{k_{2}}v_{k_{3}},

\displaystyle\sum_{k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}\text{ distinct}}v_{k_{1}}^{2}v_{k_{2}}v_{k_{3}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k_{1},k_{2}\text{ distinct}}v_{k_{1}}^{2}v_{k_{2}}(-v_{k_{1}}-v_{k_{2}})

\displaystyle=-\sum_{k_{1},k_{2}\text{ distinct}}v_{k_{1}}^{3}v_{k_{2}}-\sum_{k_{1},k_{2}\text{ distinct}}v_{k_{1}}^{2}v_{k_{2}}^{2},

\displaystyle\sum_{k_{1},k_{2}\text{ distinct}}v_{k_{1}}^{3}v_{k_{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k_{1}=1}^{N}v_{k_{1}}^{3}(-v_{k_{1}})=-\sum_{k_{1}=1}^{N}v_{k_{1}}^{4}.

Since
\mathbb{E}\left|Z_{1}\cdots Z_{v}\right|\leq 1
for
1\leq v\leq 4
, it follows from the above identities that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{1/2}\left(\left(\bm{p}_{N,i}-\bm{p}_{N,j}\right)^{\top}\bm{Z}_{N}\right)^{4}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{D^{\prime}}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}v_{k}^{4}+\sum_{k\neq l}v_{k}^{2}v_{l}^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2D^{\prime}}\sum_{k=1}^{N}v_{k}^{2}=\sqrt{2D^{\prime}}\|\bm{p}_{N,i}-\bm{p}_{N,j}\|^{2}

for some constant
D^{\prime}>0
(which is free of
i,j
and
N
). The rest follows again from (
E.4
).

Next, we deal with the identities where we condition upon
Z_{N,j}=1
. Observe that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left((\bm{p}_{N,i}-\bm{p}_{N,j})^{\top}\bm{Z}_{N}\right)^{2}\ \Big|\ Z_{N,j}=1\right]

\displaystyle=(\bm{p}_{N,i}-\bm{p}_{N,j})^{\top}{\textrm{Var}}\left(\bm{Z}_{N}\ \big|\ Z_{N,j}=1\right)(\bm{p}_{N,i}-\bm{p}_{N,j}).

For any
k\neq l,

{\textrm{Var}}(Z_{N,k}\mid Z_{N,j}=1)=(\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{1}^{2})\bm{1}{\left\{k\neq j\right\}},
and
\mathrm{Cov}(Z_{N,k},Z_{N,l}\mid Z_{N,j}=1)=(\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{1}^{2})\bm{1}{\left\{k\neq j\right\}},
where
\alpha_{1}=\frac{m-1}{N-1},\ \alpha_{2}=\frac{m-1}{N-1}\cdot\frac{m-2}{N-2}
.
We thus obtain

\mathbb{E}\left[\left((\bm{p}_{N,i}-\bm{p}_{N,j})^{\top}\bm{Z}_{N}\right)^{2}\ \Big|\ Z_{N,j}=1\right]=\Big[\alpha_{1}(1-\alpha_{1})\|\bm{p}_{N,i}-\bm{p}_{N,j}\|^{2}\\
-(\alpha_{1}-2\alpha_{2}+\alpha_{1}^{2})(\bm{P}_{\bm{X}_{N}}(i,j)-\bm{P}_{\bm{X}_{N}}(j,j))^{2}\Big].

But
(\bm{P}_{\bm{X}_{N}}(i,j)-\bm{P}_{\bm{X}_{N}}(j,j))^{2}\leq\|\bm{p}_{N,i}-\bm{p}_{N,j}\|^{2}
. So it follows that

\mathbb{E}\left[\left((\bm{p}_{N,i}-\bm{p}_{N,j})^{\top}\bm{Z}_{N}\right)^{2}\ \Big|\ Z_{N,j}=1\right]\leq D\|\bm{p}_{N,i}-\bm{p}_{N,j}\|^{2}

for some
D>0
which is free of
i,j
and
N
. Invoking (
E.4
) again, we finish the proof of (
E.2
) for
r=1
.
To prove (
E.2
) for
r=2,
note the following.

\displaystyle\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\left(\bm{p}_{N,i}-\bm{p}_{N,j}\right)^{\top}\bm{Z}_{N}\right)^{4}\ \Big|\ Z_{N,j}=1\right]}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{{\textrm{Var}}\left[\left(\left(\bm{p}_{N,i}-\bm{p}_{N,j}\right)^{\top}\bm{Z}_{N}\right)^{2}\ \Big|\ Z_{N,j}=1\right]}+\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\left(\bm{p}_{N,i}-\bm{p}_{N,j}\right)^{\top}\bm{Z}_{N}\right)^{2}\ \Big|\ Z_{N,j}=1\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{N}{m}}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[{\textrm{Var}}\left(\left(\left(\bm{p}_{N,i}-\bm{p}_{N,j}\right)^{\top}\bm{Z}_{N}\right)^{2}\ \Big|\ Z_{N,j}\right)\right]}+\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\left(\bm{p}_{N,i}-\bm{p}_{N,j}\right)^{\top}\bm{Z}_{N}\right)^{2}\ \Big|\ Z_{N,j}=1\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{N}{m}}\sqrt{{\textrm{Var}}\left[\left(\left(\bm{p}_{N,i}-\bm{p}_{N,j}\right)^{\top}\bm{Z}_{N}\right)^{2}\right]}+\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\left(\bm{p}_{N,i}-\bm{p}_{N,j}\right)^{\top}\bm{Z}_{N}\right)^{2}\ \Big|\ Z_{N,j}=1\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{N}{m}}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\left(\bm{p}_{N,i}-\bm{p}_{N,j}\right)^{\top}\bm{Z}_{N}\right)^{4}\right]}+\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\left(\bm{p}_{N,i}-\bm{p}_{N,j}\right)^{\top}\bm{Z}_{N}\right)^{2}\ \Big|\ Z_{N,j}=1\right].

Since
m/N\to\lambda\in(0,1),
the desired conclusion follows from (
E.1
) (with
r=2
) and (
E.2
) (with
r=1
).
The proof of (
E.3
) is completely analogous to the proofs of (
E.1
) and (
E.2
), hence omitted.

\square

Lemma E.2
.

For
x\neq 0
it holds that
\left|\Phi(x/\sigma)-\bm{1}{\left\{x\geq 0\right\}}\right|\leq\sigma\left|x\right|^{-1}\exp(-x^{2}/2\sigma^{2}),

where
\Phi
is the standard Normal CDF.

Proof.

Let
\phi
be the density of standard Normal. For
x>0
, we have

\left|\Phi(x/\sigma)-\bm{1}{\left\{x\geq 0\right\}}\right|=1-\Phi(x/\sigma)\leq\sigma\cdot x^{-1}\phi(x/\sigma),

using a standard inequality. For
x<0
, we can write

\left|\Phi(x/\sigma)-\bm{1}{\left\{x\geq 0\right\}}\right|=\Phi(x/\sigma)=1-\Phi(-x/\sigma)\leq\sigma\cdot(-x)^{-1}\phi(-x/\sigma).

Hence the result.

\square

Lemma E.3
.

Suppose that
(X,Y)
follows the bivariate Normal distribution with
\mathbb{E}X=\mathbb{E}Y=0
,
{\textrm{Var}}(X)={\textrm{Var}}(Y)=1
, and
\text{corr}(X,Y)=\rho\in(-1,1)
. Then for any
h_{1},h_{2}\in(0,1)
, the following bound holds:

\left|P(0\leq X\leq h_{1},0\leq Y\leq h_{2})-P(0\leq X\leq h_{1})P(0\leq Y\leq h_{2})\right|\leq C_{\rho}h_{1}h_{2},

where
C_{\rho}=|\rho|\left((1-\rho^{2})^{-1/2}+(1-|\rho|)^{-2}\right)
.

Proof.

Define

g_{x,y}(\rho):=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2(1-\rho^{2})}(x^{2}+y^{2}-2\rho xy)\right).

Note that for any fixed
x,y\in[0,1]
,

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\partial g_{x,y}(\rho)}{\partial\rho}\right|=g_{x,y}(\rho)\left|\frac{xy}{1-\rho^{2}}-\frac{\rho}{1-\rho^{2}}\frac{x^{2}+y^{2}-2\rho xy}{1-\rho^{2}}\right|\leq\frac{1}{(1-|\rho|)^{2}}.

Hence

\displaystyle\left|P(0\leq X\leq h_{1},0\leq Y\leq h_{2})-P(0\leq X\leq h_{1})P(0\leq Y\leq h_{2})\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{[0,\ h_{1}]\times[0,\ h_{2}]}\left((2\pi\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}})^{-1}g_{x,y}(\rho)-(2\pi)^{-1}g_{x,y}(0)\right)\penalty\ dx\penalty\ dy\right|

\displaystyle\leq(2\pi)^{-1}\left|(1-\rho^{2})^{-1/2}-1\right|\int_{[0,\ h_{1}]\times[0,\ h_{2}]}g_{x,y}(\rho)\penalty\ dx\penalty\ dy

\displaystyle\hskip 142.26378pt+(2\pi)^{-1}\int_{[0,\ h_{1}]\times[0,\ h_{2}]}\left|g_{x,y}(\rho)-g_{x,y}(0)\right|\penalty\ dx\penalty\ dy

\displaystyle\leq\rho^{2}(1-\rho^{2})^{-1/2}h_{1}h_{2}+\int_{[0,\ h_{1}]\times[0,\ h_{2}]}\left|\rho\right|\int_{0}^{1}\left|\frac{\partial g_{x,y}(t\rho)}{\partial\rho}\right|dt\penalty\ dx\penalty\ dy

\displaystyle\leq|\rho|\left((1-\rho^{2})^{-1/2}+(1-|\rho|)^{-2}\right)h_{1}h_{2}.

This completes the proof.

\square

Proposition E.1

(A generalized Efron-Stein inequality)
.

Given a sequence of independent real-valued random variables
W_{1},W_{2},\ldots,W_{n}
and
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a measurable function. For each
1\leq i\leq n,
let
W^{\prime}_{i}
be an independent copy of
W_{i}
, independent of the other
W_{j}
’s. Define
S:=F\left(W_{1},\ldots,W_{n}\right)
and
S_{i}:=F\left(W_{1},\ldots,W_{i-1},W^{\prime}_{i},W_{i+1},\ldots,W_{n}\right)
. Then for all integers
q\geq 2
, there exists a constant
c_{q}
(depending only on
q
) such that

\mathbb{E}\left|S-\mathbb{E}S\right|^{q}\leq c_{q}\mathbb{E}\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(S-S_{i}\right)^{2}\mid\left(W_{1},W_{2},\ldots,W_{n}\right)\right]\right|^{q/2}.

Proof.

See
Boucheron et al. 2005
for a proof.

\square

The special case
q=2
yields the Efron-Stein inequality (see
Efron and Stein 1981
).

Appendix F
Average ranks

In the main paper, we used
up-ranks
to break ties in the responses (see (
2.6
) in the main paper for a definition). We discuss in this section the analogous results when ties are broken using average ranks. Suppose that while sorting
\left\{b_{N,j}:1\leq j\leq N\right\}
in ascending order, the first
t_{1}
many are equal, then the next
t_{2}
many are equal, and so on. Assume that we get
k
such blocks of sizes
t_{1},\dots,t_{k}
, where the
t_{i}
’s can equal to 1 as well. Set
s_{0}=0
and
s_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{j}t_{i}
. Then for each
1\leq j\leq k
, define

q_{N}^{\mathrm{avg}}\left(s_{j-1}+1\right)=q_{N}^{\mathrm{avg}}\left(s_{j-1}+2\right)=\cdots=q_{N}^{\mathrm{avg}}\left(s_{j}\right):=\frac{s_{j-1}+1+s_{j}}{2}=s_{j-1}+\frac{1+t_{j}}{2}

where the last quantity is nothing but the average of the ranks
\left\{s_{j-1}+1,\ldots,s_{j-1}+t_{j}\right\}
. With the above notion of average ranks, we define the Wilcoxon rank-sum statistic as

t_{N}^{\mathrm{avg}}:=\sum_{j=1}^{N}q_{N}^{\mathrm{avg}}(j)Z_{N,j}.

We make the following assumption on the block-sizes, which essentially tells us that the blocks formed by the ties are not too large.

Assumption 5
.

Let
t_{1},\dots,t_{k}
denote the block sizes, as defined above. Then assume that

\max_{1\leq i\leq k}t_{i}=o\left(N\right)
, as
N\to\infty
.

The following lemma shows that if
5
holds, then the set of the average ranks do not differ much from
\{1,2,\dots,N\}
. This implies, in view of
Lemmas

D.5
and
2.1
, that
t_{N}^{\mathrm{avg}}
has the same asymptotic null distribution as the statistic
t_{N}
that we studied in
Section

2
of the main paper.

Lemma F.1
.

It holds under
5
that

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(q_{N}(i)-\bar{q}_{N}\right)^{2}=\frac{N\left(N^{2}-1\right)}{12}+o\left(N^{3}\right),

as
N\rightarrow\infty
, where
\bar{q}_{N}=N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}q_{N}(i)
.

Proof.

Since for each
j,\sum_{i=s_{j-1}+1}^{s_{j}}\left(q_{N}(i)-i\right)=0
, and thus
\bar{q}_{N}=N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}i=(N+1)/2
we obtain the following.

\displaystyle\sum_{i=s_{j-1}+1}^{s_{j}}\left(\left(q_{N}(i)-\bar{q}_{N}\right)^{2}-\left(i-\bar{q}_{N}\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=s_{j-1}+1}^{s_{j}}\left(q_{N}(i)^{2}-2\bar{q}_{N}\left(q_{N}(i)-i\right)-i^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=s_{j-1}+1}^{s_{j}}\left(i^{2}-\left(\frac{s_{j-1}+1+s_{j}}{2}\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=s_{j-1}+1}^{s_{j}}\left(i-\frac{s_{j-1}+1+s_{j}}{2}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{t_{j}\left(t_{j}^{2}-1\right)}{12}.

Therefore

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(q_{N}(i)-\bar{q}_{N}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(i-\bar{q}_{N}\right)^{2}-\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{t_{j}\left(t_{j}^{2}-1\right)}{12}

\displaystyle=\frac{N(N^{2}-1)}{12}-\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{t_{j}\left(t_{j}^{2}-1\right)}{12}.

Notice that

0\leq\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{t_{j}\left(t_{j}^{2}-1\right)}{12}\leq\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{k}t_{i}}{12}\max_{1\leq i\leq k}t_{i}^{2}=\frac{N}{12}\max_{1\leq i\leq k}t_{i}^{2}.

Hence
5
completes the proof.

\square

Our next lemma extends the last result, by showing that if
5
is satisfied, then the statistic
t_{N}^{\mathrm{avg}}
has the same asymptotic distribution as the statistic
t_{N}
, for any value of the parameter
\tau
. In particular, this implies that
Theorem

2.1
also holds if we replace
t_{N}
by
t_{N}^{\mathrm{avg}}
. Consequently, Rosenbaum’s estimator for
\tau
constructed using
t_{N}^{\mathrm{avg}}
instead of
t_{N}
must also satisfy
Theorem

2.2
.

Lemma F.2
.

It holds under
5
that
N^{-3/2}(t_{N}^{\mathrm{avg}}-t_{N})=o_{P}(1)
as
N\to\infty
. Consequently,
t_{N}^{\mathrm{avg}}
has the same asymptotic distribution as
t_{N}
.

Proof.

Recall that
t_{N}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}q_{N}(j)Z_{N,j}
where
q_{N}(j)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{1}{\left\{b_{N,i}\leq b_{N,j}\right\}}.
Observe that when
s_{j-1}<j\leq s_{j},

0\leq q_{N}(j)-q_{N}^{\mathrm{avg}}(j)\leq t_{j}-\frac{t_{j}+1}{2}=\frac{t_{j}-1}{2}.

Hence
t_{N}\geq t_{N}^{\mathrm{avg}}
a.s., and therefore it suffices to show that the following converges to
0
, as
N\to\infty
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}N^{-3/2}(t_{N}-t_{N}^{\mathrm{avg}})=\frac{m}{N}N^{-3/2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}(q_{N}(j)-q_{N}^{\mathrm{avg}}(j))=\frac{m}{N}N^{-3/2}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{t_{i}(t_{i}-1)}{2}.

We split the above sum into two parts, as follows. Fix any
\varepsilon>0
and define
\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}=\{1\leq j\leq k:t_{j}-1\geq\varepsilon N^{1/2}\}
. Then

\frac{m}{N}N^{-3/2}\sum_{i\not\in\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}}\frac{t_{i}(t_{i}-1)}{2}\leq\varepsilon N^{-1}\sum_{i\not\in\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}}t_{i}\leq\varepsilon.

On the other hand, for
i\in\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}
, and
s_{i-1}<j\leq s_{j}
we have
q_{N}(j)-q_{N}^{\mathrm{avg}}(j)=t_{i}(t_{i}-1)/2\geq\varepsilon^{2}N/2.

Hence, if
J
be an index chosen uniformly at random from
\{1,2,\dots,N\}
(independent of everything else), then

\displaystyle N^{-3/2}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}}\frac{t_{i}(t_{i}-1)}{2}

\displaystyle=N^{-3/2}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}}\sum_{j=s_{i-1}+1}^{s_{i}}(q_{N}(j)-q_{N}^{\mathrm{avg}}(j))

\displaystyle=N^{-1/2}P(q_{N}(J)-q_{N}^{\mathrm{avg}}(J)\geq\varepsilon^{2}N/2)

\displaystyle\leq(\varepsilon^{2}/2)^{-2}N^{-1/2}N^{-2}\mathbb{E}(q_{N}(J)-q_{N}^{\mathrm{avg}}(J))^{2}

\displaystyle=(\varepsilon^{2}/2)^{-2}N^{-7/2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}(q_{N}(j)-q_{N}^{\mathrm{avg}}(j))^{2}.

Therefore it suffices to show that as
N\to\infty
,

(F.1)

\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(q_{N}^{\mathrm{avg}}(j)-{q}_{N}(j)\right)^{2}=O\left(N^{3}\right).

Note that for each
1\leq j\leq k
,
\sum_{i=s_{j-1}+1}^{s_{j}}\left(q_{N}^{\mathrm{avg}}(i)-i\right)=0
. Thus
\bar{q}_{N}^{\mathrm{avg}}=N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}i=(N+1)/2
, and we obtain the following.

\displaystyle\sum_{i=s_{j-1}+1}^{s_{j}}\left(\left(q_{N}^{\mathrm{avg}}(i)-\bar{q}_{N}^{\mathrm{avg}}\right)^{2}-\left(i-\bar{q}_{N}^{\mathrm{avg}}\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=s_{j-1}+1}^{s_{j}}\left(q_{N}^{\mathrm{avg}}(i)^{2}-2\bar{q}_{N}^{\mathrm{avg}}\left(q_{N}^{\mathrm{avg}}(i)-i\right)-i^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=s_{j-1}+1}^{s_{j}}\left(i^{2}-\left(\frac{s_{j-1}+1+s_{j}}{2}\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=s_{j-1}+1}^{s_{j}}\left(i-\frac{s_{j-1}+1+s_{j}}{2}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{12}t_{j}\left(t_{j}^{2}-1\right).

Therefore

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(q_{N}^{\mathrm{avg}}(i)-\bar{q}_{N}^{\mathrm{avg}}\right)^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(i-\bar{q}_{N}^{\mathrm{avg}}\right)^{2}-\frac{1}{12}\sum_{j=1}^{k}t_{j}\left(t_{j}^{2}-1\right)

Notice that

0\leq\frac{1}{12}\sum_{j=1}^{k}t_{j}\left(t_{j}^{2}-1\right)\leq\frac{1}{12}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}t_{i}\right)\max_{1\leq i\leq k}t_{i}^{2}=\frac{N}{12}\max_{1\leq i\leq k}t_{i}^{2}

and therefore
5
tells us that

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(q_{N}^{\mathrm{avg}}(i)-\bar{q}_{N}^{\mathrm{avg}}\right)^{2}=\frac{N(N^{2}-1)}{12}+o(N^{3}).

On the other hand, it follows from the proof of
Lemma

D.5
that

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(q_{N}(i)-\frac{N+1}{2}\right)^{2}=\frac{N(N^{2}-1)}{12}+o(N^{3})

Combining these and doing some algebraic manipulations, (
F.1
) follows. Hence the proof.

\square
