Provably Fast Finite Particle Variants of SVGD via Virtual Particle Stochastic Approximation
Aniket Das, Dheeraj Nagaraj

Abstract
Stein Variational Gradient Descent (SVGD) is a popular variational inference algorithm which simulates an interacting particle system to approximately sample from a target distribution, with impressive empirical performance across various domains. Theoretically, its population (i.e, infinite-particle) limit dynamics is well studied but the behavior of SVGD in the finite-particle regime is much less understood. In this work, we design two computationally efficient variants of SVGD, namely VP-SVGD (which is conceptually elegant) and GB-SVGD (which is empirically effective), both of which exhibit provably fast finite-particle convergence rates. By introducing the concept of virtual particles , we develop novel stochastic approximations of population-limit SVGD dynamics in the space of probability measures, which are exactly implementable using only a finite number of particles. Our algorithms can be viewed as specific random-batch approximations of SVGD, which are computationally more efficient than ordinary SVGD. We show that the n n particles output by VP-SVGD and GB-SVGD, run for T T steps with batch-size K K , are at-least as good as i.i.d samples from a distribution whose Kernel Stein Discrepancy to the target is at most O ⁡ ( d 1 / 3 / ( K ​ T ) 1 / 6 ) O(\nicefrac{{d^{1/3}}}{{(KT)^{1/6}}}) under standard assumptions. Our results also hold under a mild growth condition on the potential function, which is much weaker than the isoperimetric (e.g. Poincare Inequality) or information-transport conditions (e.g. Talagrand’s Inequality 𝖳 1 \mathsf{T}_{1} ) generally considered in prior works. As a corollary, we consider the convergence of the empirical measure (of the particles output by VP-SVGD and GB-SVGD) to the target distribution and demonstrate a double exponential improvement over the best known finite-particle analysis of SVGD. Beyond this, our results present the first known oracle complexities for this setting with polynomial dimension dependence , thereby completely eliminating the curse of dimensionality exhibited by previously known finite-particle rates.

Technical Challenges

We resolve the following important conceptual challenges, which may be of independent interest.

Stochastic Approximation in the Space of Probability Measures
Stochastic approximations are widely used in optimization, control and sampling
[
26
,
51
,
22
]
. In the context of sampling, stochastic approximations are generally implemented in path space, e.g., Stochastic Gradient Langevin Dynamics (SGLD)
[
51
]
takes a random batch approximation of the drift term via the update
\mathbf{x}_{t+1}=\mathbf{x}_{t}-\tfrac{\eta}{K}\sum_{j=0}^{K-1}\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{t},\xi_{j})+\sqrt{2\eta}\epsilon_{t},\ \epsilon_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I})
where
\mathbb{E}[f(\mathbf{x}_{t},\xi_{j})|\mathbf{x}_{t}]=F(\mathbf{x}_{t})
. Such stochastic approximations are then analyzed using the theory of stochastic processes over
\mathbb{R}^{d}

[
11
,
39
,
54
,
24
]
. However, when viewed in the space of probability measures (i.e,
\mu_{t}=\mathrm{Law}(\mathbf{x}_{t})
), the time-evolution of these algorithms is deterministic. In contrast, our approach designs
stochastic approximations in the space of probability measures
. In particular, the time-evolution of the law of any particle in VP-SVGD and GB-SVGD are a stochastic approximation of the dynamics of
population-limit
SVGD. We ensure that requires only a finite number of particles for exact implementation.

Tracking KL Divergence in the Finite-Particle Regime
The population limit (
n\to\infty
) ensures that the initial empirical distribution (
\mu_{0}
) of SVGD admits a density (w.r.t the Lebesgue measure). When the KL divergence between
\mu_{0}
and
\pi^{\star}
is finite, prior works on population-limit SVGD analyze the time-evolution of the KL divergence to
\pi^{\star}
. However, this approach cannot be directly used to analyze finite-particle SVGD since the empirical distribution of a finite number of particles does not admit a density, and thus its KL divergence to
\pi^{\star}
is infinite. Our analysis of VP-SVGD and GB-SVGD circumvents this obstacle by considering the dynamics of an infinite number of particles, whose empirical measure then admits a density. However, the careful design ensures that the dynamics of
n
of these particles can be computed exactly, using only a finite total number of (real + virtual) particles. When conditioned on the virtual particles, these particles are i.i.d. and their conditional law is close to the target distribution with high probability.

Background on Population-Limit SVGD

We briefly introduce the analysis of population-limit SVGD using the theory of Wasserstein Gradient Flows and refer the readers to
Korba et al. 2020

The space

. The Wasserstein Gradient of
\mathcal{L}[\mu]
is
\nabla_{\mathcal{W}_{2}}\mathcal{L}[\mu]=\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\log(\tfrac{\mathsf{d}\mu}{\mathsf{d}\pi^{\star}}(\mathbf{x}))

]

The updates of population-limit SVGD can be viewed as Projected Gradient Descent in the Wasserstein space. Recall from Section
2

h_{\mu}(\mathbf{x})=P_{\mu}(\nabla\log(\tfrac{\mathsf{d}\mu}{\mathsf{d}\pi^{\star}}))(\mathbf{x})=\int h(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\mathsf{d}\mu(\mathbf{y})
. Let

5
Assumptions

In this section, we discuss the key assumptions required for our analysis of VP-SVGD and GB-SVGD. Our first assumption is smoothness of
F
, which is standard in optimization and sampling.

Assumption 1

(L-Smoothness)
.

\nabla F
exists and is
L
Lipschitz. Moreover
\|\nabla F(0)\|\leq\sqrt{L}
.

It is easy find a point such that
\|\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{*})\|\leq\sqrt{L}
(e.g., using
\Theta(1)
gradient descent steps
[
36
]
) and center the initialization at
\mu_{0}
at
\mathbf{x}^{*}
. For clarity, we take
\mathbf{x}^{*}=0
without loss of generality. We now impose the following growth condition on
F
.

Assumption 2

(Growth Condition)
.

There exist
\alpha,d_{1},d_{2}>0
such that

F(\mathbf{x})\geq d_{1}\|\mathbf{x}\|^{\alpha}-d_{2}\quad\forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

Note that Assumption
1
ensures
\alpha\leq 2
. Assumption
2
is essentially a tail decay assumption on the target density
\pi^{\star}(\mathbf{x})\propto e^{-F(\mathbf{x})}
. In fact, as we shall show in Appendix
B
, Assumption
1
ensures that the tails of
\pi^{\star}
decay as
\propto e^{-\|\mathbf{x}\|^{\alpha}}
. Consequently, Assumption
2
holds with
\alpha=2
when
\pi^{\star}
is subgaussian and with
\alpha=1
when
\pi^{\star}
is subexponential. Subgaussianity is equivalent to
\pi^{\star}
satisfying the
\mathsf{T}_{1}
inequality
[
48
, Theorem 22.10]
, commonly assumed in prior works on SVGD
[
41
,
42
]
. We also note that subexponentiality is implied when
\pi^{\star}
satisfies the Poincare Inequality
[
4
, Section 4]
, which is considered a mild condition in the sampling literature
[
46
,
7
,
2
,
11
,
6
]
. This makes Assumption
1
significantly weaker than the isoperimetric or information-transport assumptions considered in prior works.

Next, we impose a mild assumption on the RKHS of the kernel
k
, which has been used by several prior works
[
41
,
25
,
44
,
42
]
.

Assumption 3

(Bounded RKHS Norm)
.

For any
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
k(\cdot,\mathbf{y})
satisfies
\|k(\cdot,\mathbf{y})\|_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}\leq B
. Furthermore,
\nabla_{2}k(\cdot,\mathbf{y})\in\mathcal{H}
and
\|\nabla_{2}k(\cdot,\mathbf{y})\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq B

Assumption
3
ensures that the adjoint operator
P_{\mu}
, used in Sections
2
and
3
, is well-defined. We also make the following assumptions on the kernel
k
, which is satisfied by a large class of standard kernels such as Radial Basis Function kernels and Matérn kernels of order
\geq\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}
.

Assumption 4

(Kernel Decay)
.

The kernel
k
satisfies the following for constants
A_{1},A_{2},A_{3}>0
.

\displaystyle 0\leq k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\leq\tfrac{A_{1}}{1+\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2}},\quad\ \ \|\nabla_{2}k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\|\leq A_{2},\quad\ \ \|\nabla_{2}k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\|^{2}\leq A_{3}k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})

Finally, we make the following mild assumption on the initialization.

Assumption 5

(Initialization)
.

The initial distribution
\mu_{0}
is such that
\mathsf{KL}\left(\mu_{0}\boldsymbol{||}\pi^{\star}\right)<\infty
. Furthermore,
\mu_{0}
is supported in
\mathcal{B}(R)
, the
\ell_{2}
ball of radius
R

Since prior works usually assume Gaussian initialization
[
41
,
46
]
, Assumption
5
may seem slightly non-standard. However, this is not a drawback. In fact, whenever
R=\Theta(\sqrt{d}+\mathsf{polylog}(\nicefrac{{n}}{{\delta}}))
, Gaussian initialization can be made indistinguishable from
\mathsf{Uniform}(\mathcal{B}(R))
initialization, with probability at least
1-\delta
, via a coupling argument. To this end, we impose Assumption
5
for ease of exposition and our results can be extended to consider Gaussian initialization. In Appendix
B
we show that taking
R=\sqrt{\nicefrac{{d}}{{L}}}
and
\mu_{0}=\mathsf{Uniform}(\mathcal{B}(R))
suffices to ensure
\mathsf{KL}\left(\mu_{0}\boldsymbol{||}\pi^{\star}\right)=O(d)
.

\mathbb{E}[\mathsf{KSD}_{\pi^{\star}}(\hat{\nu}^{(n)}||\pi^{\star})]=O(\nicefrac{{d^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}}}}{{n^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{12}}}}}+(\nicefrac{{d}}{{n}})^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}})
. As before, this is a
double exponential improvement
over the SOTA
\tilde{O}(\tfrac{\mathsf{poly}(d)}{\sqrt{\log\log n^{\Theta(\nicefrac{{1}}{{d}})}}})
KSD rate obtained by
Shi and Mackey 2022
for SVGD for subgaussian targets. For subexponential
\pi^{\star}
(i.e.
\alpha=1
), the KSD rate of GB-SVGD is
O(\tfrac{d^{\nicefrac{{3}}{{8}}}}{n^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{16}}}}+\tfrac{d}{n^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}})
, which, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first known result of its kind.

Oracle Complexity
As we shall show in Appendix
E.3
, the oracle complexity of GB-SVGD to achieve
\epsilon
-convergence in KSD for subgaussian
\pi^{\star}
is
O(\nicefrac{{d^{6}}}{{\epsilon^{18}}})
, which significantly improves upon the oracle complexity of SVGD as implied by
Shi and Mackey 2022
. Notably, the obtained oracle complexity of GB-SVGD does not suffer from a curse of dimensionality and to the best of our knowledge, is the first known oracle complexity for this problem with polynomial dimension dependence. For subexponential
\pi^{\star}
, the obtained oracle complexity is
O(\nicefrac{{d^{9}}}{{\epsilon^{24}}})
which is the first known result of its kind.

Proof Sketch

We now present a sketch of our analysis. As shown in Section
4
, the particles
(\mathbf{x}^{(l)}_{t})_{l\geq Kt}
are i.i.d conditioned on the filtration
\mathcal{F}_{t}
, and the random measure
\mu_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t}
is the law of
(\mathbf{x}^{(Kt)}_{t})
conditioned on
\mathbf{x}^{(0)}_{0},\dots,\mathbf{x}^{(Kt-1)}_{0}
. Moreover, from equation (
5
), we know that
\mu_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t}
is a stochastic approximation of population limit SVGD dynamics, i.e.,
\mu_{t+1}|\mathcal{F}_{t+1}=(I-\gamma g_{t})_{\#}\mu_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t}
. Lemma
1
(similar to
Salim et al. 2022
and
Korba et al. 2020
) shows that under appropriate conditions, the KL between
\mu_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t}
and
\pi^{\star}
satisfies a (stochastic) descent lemma . Hence
\mu_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t}
admits a density and
\mathsf{KL}\left(\mu_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t}\boldsymbol{||}\pi^{\star}\right)
is almost surely finite.

Lemma 1

(Descent Lemma for
\mu_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t}
)
.

Let Assumptions
1
,
3
and
5
be satisfied and let
\beta>1
be an arbitrary constant. On the event
\gamma\|g_{t}\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq\tfrac{\beta-1}{\beta B}

\displaystyle\mathsf{KL}\left(\mu_{t+1}|\mathcal{F}_{t+1}\boldsymbol{||}\pi^{\star}\right)\leq\mathsf{KL}\left(\mu_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t}\boldsymbol{||}\pi^{\star}\right)-\gamma\left\langle h_{\mu_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t}},g_{t}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}+\frac{\gamma^{2}(\beta^{2}+L)B}{2}\|g_{t}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}

Lemma

(when interpreted as a Gelfand-Pettis integral

and Appendix

below.

Lemma 2

Let Assumptions
1
,
2
,
3

4

5

\displaystyle\|g_{t}\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq\xi=\zeta_{0}+\zeta_{1}(\gamma T)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha}}}+\zeta_{2}(\gamma^{2}T)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha}}}+\zeta_{3}R^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{\alpha}}}

where

Let

cannot grow too large after

Equipped with Lemma

by taking iterated expectations on both sides. To this end we control
\left\langle h_{\mu_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t}},g_{t}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
and
\|g_{t}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}
in expectation, in Lemma

Let Assumptions

,

,

\xi

Conclusion

We develop two computationally efficient variants of SVGD with provably fast convergence guarantees in the finite-particle regime, and present a wide range of improvements over prior work. A promising avenue of future work could be to establish convergence guarantees for SVGD with general non-logconcave targets, as was considered in recent works on LMC and SGLD
[
2
,
11
]
. Other important avenues include establishing minimax lower bounds for SVGD and related particle-based variational inference algorithms. Beyond this, we also conjecture that the rates of GB-SVGD can be improved even in the regime
n\ll KT
. However, we believe this requires new analytic tools.

The following lemma shows that setting the initial distribution
\mu_{0}=\mathsf{Uniform}(\mathcal{B}(R))
with
R=\sqrt{\nicefrac{{d}}{{L}}}
suffices to ensure
\mathsf{KL}\left(\mu_{0}\boldsymbol{||}\pi^{\star}\right)=O(d)
.The proof of this result is similar to that of
Vempala and Wibisono 2019
with the Gaussian initialization replaced by

initialization.

Lemma 4

(
KL Upper Bound for Uniform Initialization
)
.

Let Assumption
1
be satisfied and let
\mu_{0}=\mathsf{Uniform}(\mathcal{B}(R))
with
R=\sqrt{\nicefrac{{d}}{{L}}}
. Then, the following holds:

\displaystyle\mathsf{KL}\left(\mu_{0}\boldsymbol{||}\pi^{\star}\right)\leq\frac{d}{2}\log(\nicefrac{{L}}{{2\pi}})+d+F(0)+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}\leq O(d)

Proof.

For any
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the following holds by Assumption
1

\displaystyle F(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle\leq F(0)+\left\langle\nabla F(0),\mathbf{x}\right\rangle+\tfrac{L}{2}\|\mathbf{x}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq F(0)+\sqrt{L}\|\mathbf{x}\|+\tfrac{L}{2}\|\mathbf{x}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq F(0)+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}+L\|\mathbf{x}\|^{2}

where the second inequality uses
\|\nabla F(0)\|\leq\sqrt{L}
and the Cauchy Schwarz inequality, and the last inequality uses the identity
ab\leq a^{2}+\nicefrac{{b^{2}}}{{4}}
. It follows that,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mu_{0}}[F(\mathbf{x})]\leq F(0)+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}+LR^{2}

By a slight abuse of notation, let
\mu_{0}
denote the density of
\mathsf{Uniform}(\mathcal{B}(R))
. Clearly.
\mu_{0}(\mathbf{x})=\tfrac{1}{\mathsf{vol}(\mathcal{B}(R))}\mathbb{I}_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{B}(R)}
. It follows that,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mu_{0}(\mathbf{x})\ln(\mu_{0}(\mathbf{x}))\mathsf{d}\mathbf{x}=\int_{\mathcal{B}(R)}\tfrac{1}{\mathsf{vol}(\mathcal{B}(R))}\log(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\mathsf{vol}(\mathcal{B}(R))}})\mathsf{d}\mathbf{x}=-\log(\mathsf{vol}(\mathcal{B}(R)))

Now,
\mathsf{vol}(\mathcal{B}(R))=\tfrac{\pi^{\nicefrac{{d}}{{2}}}}{\Gamma(\tfrac{d}{2}+1)}R^{d}
. Furthermore, by Stirling’s Approximation,
(\nicefrac{{x}}{{e}})^{x-1}\leq\Gamma(x)\leq(\nicefrac{{x}}{{2}})^{x-1}
. Hence,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{2}\log\left(\frac{2\pi R^{2}}{\nicefrac{{d}}{{2}}+1}\right)\leq\log(\mathsf{vol}(\mathcal{B}(R)))\leq\frac{d}{2}\log\left(\frac{e\pi R^{2}}{\nicefrac{{d}}{{2}}+1}\right)

Without loss of generality, assume
\pi^{\star}(\mathbf{x})=e^{-F(\mathbf{x})}
(this can be easily ensured by appropriately adjusting
F(0)
upto constant factors). It follows that,

\displaystyle\mathsf{KL}\left(\mu_{0}\boldsymbol{||}\pi^{\star}\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mu_{0}(\mathbf{x})\log(\tfrac{\mu_{0}(\mathbf{x})}{\pi^{\star}(\mathbf{x})})\mathsf{d}\mathbf{x}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mu_{0}(\mathbf{x})\ln(\mu_{0}(\mathbf{x}))\mathsf{d}\mathbf{x}+\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mu_{0}}[F(\mathbf{x})]

\displaystyle\leq-\log(\mathsf{vol}(\mathcal{B}(R)))+F(0)+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}+LR^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{d}{2}\log\left(\frac{\nicefrac{{d}}{{2}}+1}{2\pi R^{2}}\right)+F(0)+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}+LR^{2}

Setting
R=\sqrt{\nicefrac{{d}}{{L}}}
, we conclude that,

\displaystyle\mathsf{KL}\left(\mu_{0}\boldsymbol{||}\pi^{\star}\right)\leq\frac{d}{2}\log(\nicefrac{{L}}{{2\pi}})+d+F(0)+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}\leq O(d)

We now show that the growth condition on

Definition 2

(
\alpha
-Tail Decay)
.

A probability distribution
\nu
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}

\alpha

if there exists some

such that
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\nu}\left[\exp\left(\|\tfrac{\mathbf{x}}{C}\|^{\alpha}\right)\right]<\infty

The

\pi^{\star}

\propto e^{-\|\mathbf{x}\|^{\alpha}}
. In particular,
Vershynin 2018
shows that

\pi^{\star}

\alpha=1

\pi^{\star}
being subexponential.

F

\alpha
. Consequently, Assumption
2

Lemma 5

(Growth Condition and Tail Decay)
.

Let Assumption

\pi^{\star}
satisfies the
\alpha

1

\pi^{\star}

Growth Condition Implies Tail Decay

Gelfand-Pettis Integrals for Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Spaces

The Gelfand-Pettis integral is a generalization of the Lebesgue integral to functions that take values in an arbitrary topological vector space. In this section, we describe the Gelfand-Pettis integral for an arbitrary Hilbert space
(V,\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle_{V})
and refer the readers to
Talagrand 1984
for a more general treatment.

Let
(X,\Sigma,\lambda)
be a measure space and
(V,\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle_{V})
be a Hilbert Space. A function
g:X\to V
is said to be Gelfand-Pettis integrable if there exists a vector
w_{g}\in V
such that
\left\langle u,w_{g}\right\rangle_{V}=\int_{X}\left\langle u,g(x)\right\rangle_{V}\mathsf{d}\lambda(x)\ \forall\ u\in V
. The vector
w_{g}
is called the Gelfand-Pettis integral of
g

We now establish the following lemma for Gelfand-Pettis integrals with respect to the RKHS
\mathcal{H}
, which is a key component of our analysis of VP-SVGD.

Lemma 8
.

Let
\mu
be a probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let
G:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a function such that for every
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
G(.,\mathbf{y})\in\mathcal{H}
with
\|G(.,\mathbf{y})\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq C
holding
\mu
-almost surely. Let
G_{\mu}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}\sim\mu}[G(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})]
. Then, the map
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathcal{H}
defined as
\psi(\mathbf{y})=G(\cdot,\mathbf{y})
is Gelfand-Pettis integrable and
G_{\mu}
is the Gelfand-Pettis integral of
\psi
with respect to
\mu
, i.e.
G_{\mu}\in\mathcal{H}
and for any
f\in\mathcal{H}
,
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}\sim\mu}[\left\langle f,G(.,\mathbf{y})\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}]=\left\langle f,G_{\mu}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}

Proof.

Let
\Phi:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}
denote the map
\Phi(f)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}\sim\mu}[\left\langle f,G(.,\mathbf{y})\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}]\ \forall\ f\in\mathcal{H}
. By linearity of expectations and inner products, we note that
\Phi
is a linear functional on
\mathcal{H}
. Furthermore, since
\|G(.,\mathbf{y})\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq C
holds
\mu
-almost surely, we note that for any
f\in\mathcal{H}
,
|\Phi(f)|\leq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}\sim\mu}[|\left\langle f,G(.,\mathbf{y})\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}|]\leq C\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}}
by Jensen’s inequality and Cauchy Schwarz inequality for
\mathcal{H}
. We conclude that
\Phi
is a bounded linear functional of
\mathcal{H}
. Thus, by Reisz Representation Theorem
[
9
]
, there exists
g\in\mathcal{H}
such that for any
f\in\mathcal{H}
, the following holds

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}\in\mu}[\left\langle f,G(.,\mathbf{y})\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}]=\left\langle f,g\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}

Hence, we conclude that the map
\psi
is Gelfand-Pettis integrable. We now use the reproducing property of
\mathcal{H}
to show that
g=G_{\mu}
, i.e.,
G_{\mu}
is the Gelfand-Pettis integral of
\psi
. To this end, let
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be arbitrary. Setting
f=k(\mathbf{x},.)
and using the fact that
g\in\mathcal{H}
,
G(.,\mathbf{y})\in\mathcal{H}
for any
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\displaystyle g(\mathbf{x})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}\in\mu}[G(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})]=G_{\mu}(\mathbf{x})

Hence,

, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}\sim\mu}[\left\langle f,G(.,\mathbf{y})\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}]=\left\langle f,G_{\mu}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}

∎

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}^{(i)}_{t}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}^{(j)}_{t}}\left[\left\langle h(\cdot,\mathbf{x}^{(i)}_{t}),h(\cdot,\mathbf{x}^{(j)}_{t})\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\bigr|\right]\right]

Appendix F
Literature Review

Initial works on the analysis of SVGD such as
Liu 2017
,
Lu et al. 2019
,
Duncan et al. 2019
,
Chewi et al. 2020
,
Nüsken and Renger 2021
consider the continuous-time population limit, i.e., the limit of infinite particles and vanishing step-sizes. In this regime,
Liu 2017
,
Lu et al. 2019
,
Nüsken and Renger 2021
show that the behavior of SVGD is characterized by a Partial Differential Equation (PDE), and established asymptotic convergence of this PDE to the target distribution. The work of
Duncan et al. 2019
proposes the Stein Logarithmic Sobolev Inequality which ensures exponential convergence of this PDE to the target distribution. However, characterizing the conditions under which this inequality holds is an open problem. The work of
Chewi et al. 2020
show that the PDE governing SVGD in the continuous-time population limit can be interpreted as an approximate Wasserstein gradient flow of the Chi-squared divergence. To this end,
Chewi et al. 2020
shows that the (exact) Wasserstein gradient flow of the Chi-squared divergence exhibits exponential convergence to the target distribution when
\pi^{\star}
satisfies a Poincare Inequality. To the best of our knowledge, the first discrete-time non-asymptotic convergence result for population-limit SVGD was established in
Korba et al. 2020
, where the authors interpreted population-limit SVGD as projected Wasserstein gradient descent. Their result relied on the assumption that the Kernel Stein Discrepancy to the target is uniformly bounded along the trajectory of SVGD, a condition which is hard to verify apriori. This result was significantly improved in
Salim et al. 2022
, which established convergence of population-limit SVGD assuming the potential
F
is smooth the target
\pi^{\star}\propto e^{-F}
satisfies Talagrand’s inequality
\mathsf{T}_{1}
, an assumption which is equivalent to subgaussianity of
\pi^{\star}
. This result was extended in
Sun et al. 2023
to accommodate for potentials
F
that satisfy a more general smoothness condition.

In comparison to prior works on population-limit SVGD, the literature on finite-particle SVGD is relatively sparse. The works of
Liu 2017
and
Gorham et al. 2020
establish that the dynamics of finite-particle SVGD asymptotically converge to that of population-limit SVGD in bounded Lipschitz distance and Wasserstein-1 distance respectively, as the number of particles approaches infinity. Under the stringent condition of bounded
F
(which is violated in various scenarios, e.g. log-strongly concave
\pi^{\star}
),
Korba et al. 2020
derived a non-asymptotic bound between the expected Wasserstein-2 distance between finite-particle SVGD and population-limit SVGD. The work of
Liu et al. 2023
considers the special case of Gaussian initialization and bilinear kernels, and provides a finite-particle convergence guarantee for the first and second moments whenever the target density is also a Gaussian. To the best of our knowledge,
Shi and Mackey 2022
is the only prior work that explicitly establishes a complete non-asymptotic convergence guarantee of finite-particle SVGD for subgaussian targets, and shows that the empirical measure of SVGD run with
n
particles converges to the target density in KSD at a rate of
O\left(\sqrt{\tfrac{\mathsf{poly}(d)}{\log\log n^{\Theta(\nicefrac{{1}}{{d}})}}}\right)
.
