Minimal hypersurfaces in Kähler scalar flat ALE spaces
Claudio Arezzo, Alberto Della Vedova, Samreena

Abstract
. In this paper we give a new general method to describe all Kähler scalar flat metrics on U ⁡ ( n ) U(n) -invariant domains of ℂ n \mathbb{C}^{n} in a way to be able to detect easily whether it can be completed to larger domains and which kind of ends they can have. This new approach makes possible to decide whether the corresponding metrics contain a minimal sphere among the standard euclidean ones. We will show also how to check the stability (i.e. minimizing volume up to second order) of such submanifolds. We apply the present analysis to a comparison between Penrose Inequality ( [ 39 ] , which requires the presence of a stable minimal hypersurface) and Hein-LeBrun’s ( [ 26 ] , which requires the ambient space to be Kähler and predicts the existence of a special divisor), and we will show that Penrose Inequality does indeed hold in any dimension for this class of manifolds, and that the two inequalities are indeed incomparable in that for these manifolds a stable minimal surface and a divisor never coexist.

Introduction

Studying the existence and the geometry of minimal submanifolds of a given riemannian manifold is one of the most classical aspects
of Differential Geometry. The main scope of this paper is to make some progress towards the solution of this problem for
Kähler scalar flat
U(n)
-invariant metrics. Such metrics appear naturally in a variety of geometric and physical problems especially
with the further constraint of having an Asymptotically (Locally) Euclidean end (see for example

The existence of such objects is of great interest in a number of applications, yet they tend to be very rare, so it is natural to ask whether our curvature conditions prevent their existence. In this respect it is worth noticing that, by Simons Theorem
[
42
]
, the stronger assumption of non-negativity of the Ricci curvature would leave space only to special totally geodesic submanifolds, and at the same time volume minimizers in Kähler manifolds are often complex analytic submanifolds (hence never real hypersurfaces), again hinting to a possible negative answer to our problem (see for example

As a general application of our study we compare in Section

Let

In the Kähler setting Hein-LeBrun (

In Section

we will show how our new representation of

•

There are no

-invariant scalar flat ALE manifolds containing both a stable minimal

and a divisor.

•

The Riemannian Penrose Inequality holds in any dimension for all scalar flat Kähler metrics containing a stable minimal

Again we believe it would be of great interest to generalize the above analysis to more general classes of Kähler manifolds to extend this
dicothomy.

is incomplete on

of the blow-up of
\mathbf{C}^{n}
at the origin.
To see this, let

at the origin.
As well known, to any affine open set

\mathbf{CP}^{n-1}
corresponds an open set of

, which is biholomorphic to
\mathbf{C}^{n}

and

In these coordinates one has

\left.+\frac{|\lambda|^{2k}(1+|\zeta|^{2})^{k}}{B+|\lambda|^{2k}(1+|\zeta|^{2})^{k}}\sqrt{-1}\partial\log\left(|\lambda|^{2}(1+|\zeta|^{2})\right)\wedge\bar{\partial}\log\left(|\lambda|^{2}(1+|\zeta|^{2})\right)\right).

Since

is a positive integer and

|\lambda|^{2k}\sqrt{-1}\partial\log\left(|\lambda|^{2}(1+|\zeta|^{2})\right)\wedge\bar{\partial}\log\left(|\lambda|^{2}(1+|\zeta|^{2})\right)=\\
|\lambda|^{2k-2}\sqrt{-1}\left(d\lambda+\lambda\partial\log(1+|\zeta|^{2})\right)\wedge\left(d\bar{\lambda}+\bar{\lambda}\bar{\partial}\log(1+|\zeta|^{2})\right),

\omega
is smooth, but it is degenerate at points
\lambda=0
in the direction of
\lambda
as soon as

.
This is the reason why one has to take the quotient by the cyclic group
\Gamma_{k}
in order to get a Kähler metric on

.
Note that the action of
\Gamma_{k}
on

is defined by

.
The quotient map from

to

maps

to

Therefore, in local coordinates

\left.+\frac{k^{-2}|\mu|^{2}(1+|\zeta|^{2})^{k}}{B+|\mu|^{2}(1+|\zeta|^{2})^{k}}\sqrt{-1}\partial\log\left(|\mu|^{2}(1+|\zeta|^{2})^{k}\right)\wedge\bar{\partial}\log\left(|\mu|^{2}(1+|\zeta|^{2})^{k}\right)\right),

For

As for the second point of the Corollary, it is enough to consider metrics corresponding to the arcs of level sets with
\lambda\in[2n-1,\lambda_{FYZ})
with
y<0
(as the piece with
y<0
of the yellow arc in Figure
5
).

5.
Penrose vs Hein-LeBrun Inequalities

We are now in position to apply our analysis to the comparison between the two Penrose Inequalities available in the Kähler setting
mentioned in the Introduction.

Three objects evidently come into play:

•

the existence and volume
V
of an outermost minimal surface
\Sigma
;

•

the existence and volume of a “canonical” divisor
\mathcal{D}
;

•

the ADM Mass
m_{ADM}
of an Asymptotic Locally Euclidean manifold.

As we observed in the second Section and in Proposition
4.3
, in order to have a stable minimal surfaces and to have an asymptotically euclidean end,
we must look at metrics corresponding to the arcs of level sets with
\lambda\in[2n-1,\lambda_{FYZ})
with
y<0
. On the other hand
as proved in Section
2
, such metrics are defined on
\mathbb{C}^{n}\setminus B_{R_{\lambda}}
for some finite
R_{\lambda}
, which of course does not contain any compact divisor. The existence of a divisor can indeed happen only when and where the level set intersects the line of admissible metrics as we have seen in Subsection
3.1
. Hence the presence of a divisor and of a stable minimal sphere in the family
S^{2n-1}(t)
are mutually exclusive for
U(n)
-invariant scalar flat metrics. It is then natural to conjecture that this phenomenon is in fact more general and does not depend on the extra symmetries we imposed.

Yet, we now want to check the validity of the Penrose Inequality for the metrics we considered. To do so we first observe that the stable minimal surface we found in the previous section is indeed an outermost minimal surface: in fact, it is easy to check that the interior of any euclidean sphere is strongly convex and formula
4.1
shows that all euclidean spheres outside the stable minimal one have mean curvature of the same sign. Hence a straightforward application of the maximum principle prevents the existence of a compact minimal surface outside the stable one. We then need to compute first the volume
V(\Sigma)
of the stable minimal sphere we found. Recall that in order to associate a metric to a (piece of) level set we fix a given point
(x_{0},y_{0})
on it and we impose this to be the
point corresponding to
t=0
, hence getting rid of undefined scaling factors. In what follows we then choose
(x_{0},y_{0})
to be the point corresponding to the unique stable minimal surface we found.

Recall that

(23)

V(S^{2n-1}(t))=\frac{(2\pi)^{n}}{(n-1)!}\left.u_{t}^{n-1}\sqrt{2u_{tt}}\right.\,\,.

Formula (
12
) readily gives
u_{t}(0)=1
and
u_{tt}(0)=1+x_{0}+y_{0}
, hence

(24)

V(\Sigma)=\frac{(2\pi)^{n}}{(n-1)!}\sqrt{2(\frac{1-n+x_{0}}{n}})\,\,,

where
n-1<x_{0}\leq 2n-1

We are then left with the problem of computing the ADM Mass for all scalar flat metrics studied above. In fact we will compute
this quantity for a broader class of metrics, being of natural independent interest.

Recall that for any ALE scalar flat metric one has (see Lemma
7.2
in (
[
1
]
)), in a suitable choice of euclidean coordinates at infinity,

u(t)=e^{t}+m\frac{e^{(2-n)t}-1}{2-n}+a+O(e^{(1-n)}),\qquad t\to+\infty,

and it is well known that
m
is indeed the AMD mass which can then be computed by
m=\lim_{t\to+\infty}\frac{e^{(n-2)t}}{n-1}(u_{t}-u_{tt})
.

It is important to notice that in all our examples, euclidean coordinates are indeed the fixed coordinates
z_{i}
, and in these
coordinates metrics on the appropriate level sets are automatically ALE without need to use any scaling.

We now want to find the relationship between
m
and the initial point
(x_{0},y_{0})
:

(1)

x_{0}=0

In the case studied in Example
3.2
, for
t\to+\infty
we have

u_{t}=\sqrt[n-1]{B+e^{(n-1)t}}=e^{t}+\frac{B}{(n-1)}e^{(2-n)t}+O(e^{(1-n)t}),

where
B=-y_{0}/(1+y_{0})
..
As a consequence

\displaystyle u_{tt}

\displaystyle=e^{(n-1)t}(B+e^{(n-1)t})^{(2-n)/(n-1)},

\displaystyle=e^{t}(1+Be^{(1-n)t})^{(2-n)/(n-1)},

\displaystyle=e^{t}-\frac{B(2-n)}{n-1}e^{(2-n)t}+O(e^{(1-n)t})\qquad t\to+\infty

As a consequence

m=\lim_{t\to+\infty}\frac{e^{(n-2)t}}{n-1}\left(\frac{Be^{(2-n)t}}{n-1}-\frac{B(2-n)e^{(2-n)t}}{n-1}\right)=\frac{B}{n-1}

Note that one has
m=\frac{-y_{0}}{((n-1)(1+y_{0})}
thus metrics related to initial point
(0,y_{0})
with positive
y_{0}
has negative ADM mass.

(2)

y_{0}=0

For example
3.3
, for
t\to+\infty
we have

u_{t}=\sqrt[n]{B+e^{nt}}=e^{t}+\frac{B}{n}e^{(1-n)t}+O(e^{-nt}),

where
B=-x_{0}/(1+x_{0})
.
As a consequence

\displaystyle u_{tt}

\displaystyle=e^{nt}(B+e^{nt})^{(1-n)/n},

\displaystyle=e^{t}(1+Be^{-nt})^{(1-n)/n},

\displaystyle=e^{t}-\frac{B(1-n)}{n}e^{(1-n)t}+O(e^{-nt})\qquad t\to+\infty

As a consequence

m=\lim_{t\to+\infty}\frac{e^{(n-1)t}}{n-1}\left(\frac{Be^{(1-n)t}}{n}-\frac{B(1-n)e^{(1-n)t}}{n}\right)=\frac{B}{n}

Note that one has
m=\frac{-x_{0}}{((n-1)(1+x_{0})}
thus metrics related to initial point
(0,x_{0})
with positive
x_{0}
has negative ADM mass.

(3)

x_{0}y_{0}\neq 0

u_{t}=\left|\frac{x_{0}}{x}\right|^{1/n},\quad u_{tt}=\left|\frac{x_{0}}{x}\right|^{1/n}+x\left|\frac{x_{0}}{x}\right|^{1/n}+y_{0}|x_{0}|^{2/n-1}|x|^{1-2/n}

Since
x\sim x_{0}e^{-nt}
for
t\to+\infty
we have

\displaystyle m

\displaystyle=\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{e^{(n-2)t}}{n-1}\left(-x\left|\frac{x_{0}}{x}\right|^{1/n}-y_{0}|x_{0}|^{2/n-1}|x|^{1-2/n}\right)

\displaystyle=\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{e^{(n-2)t}}{n-1}\left(-x_{0}e^{(1-n)t}-y_{0}e^{(2-n)t}\right)

whence

(25)

m=-\frac{y_{0}}{(n-1)}.

Penrose Inequality (
1.2
) then boils down to ask whether

-\frac{y_{0}}{(n-1)}\geq 1+x_{0}+y_{0}=-\frac{n+y_{0}}{n-1}\,\,,

which is trivially true in every dimension.

Corollary
1.4
is then proved.
