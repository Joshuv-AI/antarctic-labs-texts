Abstract
. The B-operators (abbreviation for Brownian-type operators) are upper triangular 2 × 2 2\times 2 block matrix operators that satisfy certain algebraic constraints. The purpose of this paper is to characterize the weak, the strong and the uniform stability of B-operators, respectively. This is achieved by giving equivalent conditions for the convergence of powers of a B-operator in each of the corresponding topologies. A more subtle characterization is obtained for B-operators with subnormal ( 2 , 2 ) (2,2) entry. The issue of the strong stability of the adjoint of a B-operator is also discussed.

1.
Introduction

The stability of Hilbert (or Banach) space operators,
which can be thought of as a discrete version of the
stability of
C_{0}
-semigroups of operators, means that
the power sequence of a given operator converges to
the zero operator in one of the various possible
topologies. Among the topologies of interest to us are
the operator norm topology (uniform stability), the
strong operator topology (strong stability) and the
weak operator topology (weak stability). Stability in
its various aspects is related to model theory

[
21
]
, the invariant subspace problem

[
27
]
, supercyclicity
[
22
]
,
ergodicity
[
12
]
, etc. Uniform and strong
stability is now well understood. However, this is not
the case for weak stability. Hence, very few results
on weak stability are known (see e.g.,

[
12
, Sec. II.3]
).

The B-operators introduced in
[
6
]

under the name “Brownian-type operators” generalize
the Brownian unitaries
[
1
]
, the Brownian
isometries
[
2
]
and the quasi-Brownian
isometries
[
24
,
6
]
. The B-operators are
upper triangular
2\times 2
block matrix operators
that satisfy certain algebraic constraints (see
Definition
1.1
). It is worth mentioning that
upper triangular
2\times 2
block matrices appear in
various parts of operator theory and functional
analysis (e.g., see

[
14
,
11
,
2
,
25
,
15
,
9
,
17
,
18
,
19
,
7
]
).

Our main goal in this paper is to give criteria for
the convergence of powers of a B-operator relative to
three topologies, namely the weak operator topology
(
wot
), the strong operator topology (
sot
)
and the operator norm topology (see
Theorems
2.1
and
2.3
). As a

However, there are many weakly stable B-operators. We
refer the reader to Section

5.
Strong stability of
T^{*}

We begin with a general criterion for strong stability
of the adjoint of an operator. Note that due to the
uniform boundedness principle the power boundedness of
an operator
T\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H})
is a necessary condition
for
T^{*}
to be strongly stable. Recall that an
operator
T\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H})
is said to be
analytic

if
\bigcap_{n=0}^{\infty}{\mathcal{R}}(T^{n})=\{0\}
.

Lemma 5.1
.

If
T\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H})
is a power bounded operator such
that
\bigcap_{n=0}^{\infty}\overline{{\mathcal{R}}(T^{n})}=\{0\}
, then
T^{*}
is strongly
stable. In particular, this is the case when
T
is
power bounded, left-invertible and analytic.

Proof.

Suppose that
T\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H})
. First, observe that

\displaystyle\bigcap_{n=0}^{\infty}\overline{{\mathcal{R}}(T^{n})}=\Big(\bigvee_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{N}(T^{*n})\Big)^{\perp}.

(5.1)

It easy to see that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\|T^{*n}h\|=0
for every
h\in\mathcal{E}
, where
\mathcal{E}=\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{N}(T^{*n})
(clearly,

\mathcal{E}
is a vector space). If
T
is power
bounded, then
\lim_{n\to\infty}\|T^{*n}h\|=0
for
every
h\in\overline{\mathcal{E}}
. This combined
with (
5.1
) and the fact that powers of
left-invertible operators being left-invertible have
closed range completes the proof.
∎

Before describing
\overline{{\mathcal{R}}(T^{n})}
for a
B-operator
T
, we need the following fact.

Fix a positive integer
n
. If
T\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H})
,
then
T
is left-invertible if and only if
T^{n}
is
left-invertible. Consequently, if
T
is injective,
then
T
has closed range if and only if
T^{n}
has
closed range.

(5.2)

Indeed, the first statement can be proved purely
algebraically. The second statement follows from the
first and the well-known fact that
T
is
left-invertible if and only if
T
is injective and
has closed range.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
T=\big[\begin{smallmatrix}V&E\\
0&X\end{smallmatrix}\big]
be a B-operator relative
to
\mathcal{H}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{2}
and
n\geqslant 1
. Set

S_{n}=\Big[\begin{smallmatrix}I&0\\[1.29167pt]
0&\sqrt{|E_{n}|^{2}+|X^{n}|^{2}}\end{smallmatrix}\Big]
. Then
there exists a unique unitary isomorphism
U\colon\overline{{\mathcal{R}}(T^{n})}\to\overline{{\mathcal{R}}(S_{n})}
such that

UT^{n}=S_{n}
. Moreover, the following are

equivalent
:

(i)

{\mathcal{R}}(T^{n})
is closed,

(ii)

{\mathcal{R}}(\sqrt{|E_{n}|^{2}+|X^{n}|^{2}}\/)
is closed,

(iii)

{\mathcal{R}}(|E_{n}|)+{\mathcal{R}}(|X^{n}|)
is closed,

(iv)

{\mathcal{R}}(E_{n}^{*})+{\mathcal{R}}(X^{*n})
is closed.

Furthermore, if
E
or
X
is injective, then

{\mathcal{R}}(T^{n})
is closed if and only if
{\mathcal{R}}(T)
is
closed, or equivalently if and only if
|E|^{2}+|X|^{2}

is invertible in
\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H}_{2})
.

Proof.

It follows from (
1.1
) and (
2.1
) that

V^{*n}E_{n}=0
, or equivalently that
{\mathcal{R}}(V^{n})\perp{\mathcal{R}}(E_{n})
. This, together with Lemma
3.3
(i)
yields

\displaystyle\|T^{n}(h_{1}\oplus h_{2})\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|V^{n}h_{1}\oplus E_{n}h_{2}\oplus X^{n}h_{2}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|h_{1}\|^{2}+\|E_{n}h_{2}\|^{2}+\|X^{n}h_{2}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|S_{n}(h_{1}\oplus h_{2})\|^{2},\quad h_{1}\in\mathcal{H}_{1},\,h_{2}\in\mathcal{H}_{2}.

Therefore, there exists a unique unitary isomorphism

U\colon\overline{{\mathcal{R}}(T^{n})}\to\overline{{\mathcal{R}}(S_{n})}

such that
UT^{n}=S_{n}
. As a consequence, the conditions
(i) and (ii) are equivalent. Since by the theorem of
T. Crimmins (see
[
13
, Theorem 2.2]
)

{\mathcal{R}}(A)+{\mathcal{R}}(B)={\mathcal{R}}(\sqrt{AA^{*}+BB^{*}})
for
A,B\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H})
, we deduce that the conditions (ii)-(iv)
are equivalent.

Now we prove the “furthermore” part. Suppose that

E
or
X
is injective. Then

is
injective. Hence, by Lemma
3.3
(ii),
T
is
injective. Therefore, by (
5.2
),
T
has
closed range if and only if
T^{n}
has closed range.
Finally,
T
has closed range if and only if
T
is
left-invertible, or equivalently if and only if
T^{*}T

is invertible. By Lemma
3.3
(ii), this is
equivalent to the invertibility of

.
∎

Theorem 5.5

Let

is invertible in

\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H}_{2})

is strongly stable.

Proof.

In view of Lemma
5.1
, to complete the proof,
it suffices to show that
T
is power bounded,
left-invertible, and analytic. It follows from
Lemma
3.3
(i) that
T
is power bounded if and
only if
\sup_{n\geqslant 1}\|E_{n}\|<\infty
and
X
is
power bounded. In turn,
T
is left-invertible if and
only if
T^{*}T
is invertible, or equivalently by
Lemma
3.3
(ii), the sum
|E|^{2}+|X|^{2}
is
invertible. It remains to show that
T
is analytic.
Take
h_{1}\oplus h_{2}\in\bigcap_{n=0}^{\infty}{\mathcal{R}}(T^{n})
, where
h_{1}\in\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
h_{2}\in\mathcal{H}_{2}
.
Then there exist sequences

\{h_{1,n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{1}
and

\{h_{2,n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{2}
such that

h_{1}\oplus h_{2}=T^{n}(h_{1,n}\oplus h_{2,n})
for
every integer
n\geqslant 1
. According to
Lemma
3.3
(i), we have

\displaystyle h_{1}=V^{n}h_{1,n}+E_{n}h_{2,n}\quad\text{and}\quad h_{2}=X^{n}h_{2,n}\quad\text{for every integer $n\geqslant 1$.}

(5.3)

By the analyticity of
X
, we conclude that
h_{2}=0
.
Using (
1.2
) and (
5.3
), we get

\displaystyle X^{n}E^{*}Eh_{2,n}=E^{*}EX^{n}h_{2,n}=E^{*}Eh_{2}=0,\quad n\geqslant 1.

Knowing that
X^{n}
is injective, we see that
Eh_{2,n}=0
. Hence, by Lemma
3.3
(iii),
E_{n}h_{2,n}=0
. This, together with (
5.3
),
implies that
h_{1}=V^{n}h_{1,n}
for all
n\geqslant 1
. It
follows from the Wold decomposition theorem that
V

being completely non-unitary is analytic, so
h_{1}=0
.
Consequently,
T
is analytic.
∎

Remark 5.6
.

Regarding Theorem
5.5
, we note that the
assumptions that
V
is completely non-unitary,

\sup_{n\geqslant 1}\|E_{n}\|<\infty
and
X
is power
bounded are necessary for the adjoint of a B-operator

T=\big[\begin{smallmatrix}V&E\\
0&X\end{smallmatrix}\big]
to be strongly stable.
Indeed, assume that
T^{*}
is strongly stable. Then, by
Lemma
3.3
(i),
T^{*n}=\big[\begin{smallmatrix}V^{*n}&0\\
E_{n}^{*}&X^{*n}\end{smallmatrix}\big]
for
n\geqslant 1
.
Thus, by the uniform boundedness principle,
X
is
power bounded,
\sup_{n\geqslant 1}\|E_{n}\|<\infty
, and

\text{\sc(sot)}\lim_{n\to\infty}V^{*n}=0
. Using
the Wold decomposition theorem, we conclude that
V

is completely non-unitary.

To have an example illustrating
Theorem
5.5
, consider three isometries

V\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H}_{1})
,
W_{1}\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H}_{2},\mathcal{H}_{1})
and

W_{2}\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H}_{2})
such that
V
and
W_{2}
are
completely non-unitary and
{\mathcal{R}}(V)\perp{\mathcal{R}}(W_{1})
. Let

z,w
be two nonzero complex numbers such that

|w|<1
. Set
E=zW_{1}
and
X=wW_{2}
. Then
T=\big[\begin{smallmatrix}V&E\\
0&X\end{smallmatrix}\big]
is a B-operator relative
to
\mathcal{H}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{2}
, which is not strongly stable
(see Corollary
2.4
), which is not
block-diagonal and whose adjoint
T^{*}
is strongly
stable (use the Wold decomposition theorem and
Theorem
5.5
).

\diamondsuit

6.
Examples

Regarding Theorems
2.1
and

2.5
, it is worth mentioning that in general
the power sequence
\{U^{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is not

wot
-convergent. The trivial reason is that if
the sequence
\{U^{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is
wot
-convergent to a nonzero operator (e.g.,
U=I
),
then
\{(-U)^{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is not
wot
-convergent. Non-trivial examples related to the

wot
-convergence of the power sequence

\{U^{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
are given below. We refer the
reader to Corollary
3.7
for the relationship
between the
wot
-convergence of

\{U^{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
and the weak stability of
U

for a unitary
U
.

Example 6.1
.

Fix an integer
m\geqslant 2
and consider a unitary operator
U\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H})
whose spectrum equals
\{z_{0},\ldots,z_{m-1}\}
,
the set of all
m
th roots of
1
. Since the latter set is a
cyclic multiplicative subgroup of
\mathbb{T}
, we can assume that

z_{j}=z_{1}^{j}
for
j=0,\dots,m-1
. Then, by the spectral
theorem
U=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}z_{1}^{j}P_{j}
, where

\{P_{j}\}_{j=0}^{m-1}
are nonzero orthogonal projections such
that
\mathcal{H}=\bigoplus_{j=0}^{m-1}{\mathcal{R}}(P_{j})
. Thus, we have

\displaystyle\langle U^{km+j}f,f\rangle=z_{1}^{j}\|f\|^{2},\quad k\geqslant 0,\,j\in\{0,\ldots,m-1\},\,f\in{\mathcal{R}}(P_{1}).

Hence, if
f
is a nonzero vector in
{\mathcal{R}}(P_{1})
, then
the sequence
\{\langle U^{n}f,f\rangle\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
has

m
distinct cluster points, showing that

\{U^{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is not
wot
-convergent.

It could be even worse, namely, if
U=zI
, where

z=\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}\vartheta}
and
\vartheta
is an irrational
number, then by Jacobi’s theorem (see

[
10
, Theorem I.3.13]
) the closure of the set

\{\langle U^{n}f,f\rangle\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is equal to
\mathbb{T}
for
every normalized vector
f\in\mathcal{H}
.

In turn, it can happen that the sequence

\{U^{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
does converge in the
wot
,
e.g., when the spectral measure
G
of a unitary operator

U\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H})
is absolutely continuous with respect to

\lambda
, the normalized Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{T}
. Indeed,
by the spectral theorem and the Radon-Nikodym theorem,

\displaystyle\langle U^{n}f,f\rangle=\Big\langle\int_{\mathbb{T}}z^{n}G(\mathrm{d\hskip 0.43057pt}z)f,f\Big\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{T}}z^{n}\phi_{f}(z)\lambda(\mathrm{d\hskip 0.43057pt}z),\quad n\geqslant 0,\,f\in\mathcal{H},

where
\phi_{f}\in L^{1}(\lambda)
is the Radon-Nikodym
derivative of
\langle G(\cdot)f,f\rangle
with respect
to
\lambda
. Applying the Riemann-Lebesgue lemma (see

[
29
, Theorem 5.15]
) shows that
U
is weakly
stable. A simple example of a unitary operator with
these properties is the operator of multiplication by
the independent variable “
z
” on

L^{2}(\mathbb{T},\lambda)
. This kind of result is related to
the characterization of weak stability via Rajchman
measures (see
[
12
, Proposition IV.1.5]
). We
also refer the reader to
[
22
]
for more
information on the weak stability of unitary operators
and its relationship to supercyclicity. In particular,
it is shown there that there are singular-continuous
unitary operators that are weakly stable, as well as
singular-continuous unitary operators that are weakly
unstable.

\diamondsuit

Referring to the previous example, it is worth making
the following remark.

Remark 6.2
.

As we know, every completely non-unitary isometry is
weakly stable. In fact, the same property is shared by
completely non-unitary contractions, namely, each such
operator is weakly stable (see

[
21
, Corollary 7.4]
). On this occasion, let us
recall that every completely non-unitary cohyponormal
contraction is strongly stable (see

[
26
, Theorem 3]
; see also

[
23
, Theorem]
). Another result in this
spirit can be found in

[
5
, Corollary 3.6(a)]
.

\diamondsuit

We will now discuss the issue of weak stability of
B-isometries (which are called quasi-Brownian
isometries in
[
3
]
and

\triangle_{T}
-regular
2
-isometries in
[
24
]
).

Example 6.3
.

Take two infinite dimensional Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and

\mathcal{H}_{2}
such that
\dim\mathcal{H}_{1}\geqslant\dim\mathcal{H}_{2}
. Let
V\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H}_{1})
and
X\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H}_{2})
be isometries and

E\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H}_{2},\mathcal{H}_{1})
be such that
{\mathcal{R}}(V)\perp{\mathcal{R}}(E)

and
XE^{*}E=E^{*}EX
. Then
T:=\big[\begin{smallmatrix}V&E\\
0&X\end{smallmatrix}\big]\in\mathscr{I}_{\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}}
. It follows
from Lemma
3.3
(iii) that
E_{n}^{*}E_{n}=nE^{*}E

for every
n\geqslant 1
. This implies that
\sup_{n\geqslant 1}\|E_{n}\|<\infty
if and only
E=0
. Hence, by
Corollary
2.2
,
T
is weakly stable if and
only if
E=0
and the unitary parts of
V
and
X
are
weakly stable (recall that
\|T^{*}T-I\|^{1/2}=\|E\|
is
called the covariance of
T
). In particular, if
T

is Brownian isometry of covariance
\sigma>0
(in
the Agler-Stankus terminology, see

[
2
, Proposition 5.37]
), that is,
V
is
isometric,
X
is unitary and
E=\sigma W
, where

W\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H}_{2},\mathcal{H}_{1})
is an injective contraction
such that
{\mathcal{R}}(V)\perp{\mathcal{R}}(W)
and
XW^{*}W=W^{*}WX
,
then
T
is never weakly stable.

\diamondsuit

Next, we will give an example of a weakly stable
B-normal operator which is not block-diagonal. In this
example,
E
can be chosen so that
E
is injective
and
{\mathcal{R}}(|E|)
is closed.

Example 6.4

(Example
6.3
continued)
.

Let
V,E,X
be as in Example
6.3
. Assume
furthermore that
V
is completely non-unitary,
E
is
injective and
X
is unitary. Let
z
be a complex
number
such that
0<|z|<1
. Then clearly
T_{z}:=\big[\begin{smallmatrix}V&E\\
0&zX\end{smallmatrix}\big]\in\mathscr{N}_{\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}}
. By

[
6
, Proposition 3.10]
,

\{T_{z}^{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\subseteq\mathscr{Q}_{\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}}
. As powers
of normal operators are normal, we deduce from
Lemma
3.3
(i) that
\{T_{z}^{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\subseteq\mathscr{N}_{\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}}
. Knowing that
\|zX\|<1
, the
unitary part
U
of
V
is trivial and
E
is
injective, we conclude from the “moreover” parts of
Lemma
3.4
and Corollary
2.6
that

T_{z}
is weakly stable. Hence,
T_{z}
is a weakly
stable B-normal operator which is not block-diagonal
relative to
\mathcal{H}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{2}
. By choosing
E
as a
non-zero multiple of an isometry
W\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H}_{2},\mathcal{H}_{1})
with range orthogonal to
{\mathcal{R}}(V)
,
we can guarantee that
{\mathcal{R}}(|E|)
is closed.

\diamondsuit

Now we provide a concrete example of a
weakly stable B-normal operator
T=\big[\begin{smallmatrix}V&E\\
0&X\end{smallmatrix}\big]
for which
{\mathcal{R}}(|E|)
is
not closed. This is closely related to
Lemma
3.5
.

Example 6.5
.

If the hypothesis in Lemma
3.5
that
{\mathcal{R}}(|E|)

is closed is deleted, the conclusion is no longer
valid. Indeed, let
\mu
be a probability Borel
measure on
\bar{\mathbb{D}}
, the closed unit disk in

\mathbb{C}
centered at
0
, such that

\displaystyle\mu(\mathbb{T})=0\quad\text{and}\quad\sup\{|z|\colon z\in\mathrm{supp}\,\mu\}=1,

(6.1)

where
\mathrm{supp}\,\mu
stands for the closed support of

\mu
. Set
\mathcal{H}_{1}=\mathcal{H}_{2}=L^{2}(\mu)
. Denote by
M_{\xi}

the operator of multiplication on
L^{2}(\mu)
by a
complex Borel function
\xi
on
\bar{\mathbb{D}}
. Define
the continuous functions
\psi
and
\varphi
on

\bar{\mathbb{D}}
by

\displaystyle\psi(z)=(1-|z|)^{1/2}\quad\text{and}\quad\varphi(z)=|z|^{1/2}\quad\text{for }z\in\bar{\mathbb{D}}.

(6.2)

Clearly,
M_{\psi}
and
M_{\varphi}
are bounded
commuting positive operators. It follows from
(
6.1
) that there exists a sequence

\{z_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\subseteq\mathbb{D}\cap\mathrm{supp}\,\mu

converging to a point in
\mathbb{T}\cap\mathrm{supp}\,\mu
. This
implies that
\dim L^{2}(\mu)=\aleph_{0}
. Thus, there
exist two isometries
V\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H}_{1})
and
W\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H}_{2},\mathcal{H}_{1})
such that
{\mathcal{R}}(V)\perp{\mathcal{R}}(W)
.
Define the operator
E\in\boldsymbol{B}(\mathcal{H}_{2},\mathcal{H}_{1})
by

E=WM_{\psi}
and set
X=M_{\varphi}
. Since

|E|=M_{\psi}
, the operator
T=\big[\begin{smallmatrix}V&E\\
0&X\end{smallmatrix}\big]
is B-normal relative to

\mathcal{H}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{2}
. Hence,
\{T^{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\subseteq\mathscr{N}_{\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}}
(see Example
6.4
). Moreover,
by the first equality in (
6.1
) and
(
6.2
),
|E|
is injective, so
E
is injective
and
\mathcal{H}_{22}=\mathcal{H}_{2}
. We now claim that the sequence

\{\|E_{n}\|\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is bounded. Indeed, it
follows from Lemma
3.3
(iii) that for all

f\in L^{2}(\mu)
and
n\geqslant 1
,

\displaystyle(E_{n}^{*}E_{n}f)(z)

\displaystyle=\Big(\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}X^{*j}X^{j}E^{*}Ef\Big)(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}|z|^{j}(1-|z|)f(z)=(1-|z|^{n})f(z)\quad\text{for $\mu$-a.e. }z\in\bar{\mathbb{D}},

which implies that
\sup_{n\geqslant 1}\|E_{n}\|\leqslant 1
. Thus
the condition (i) of Lemma
3.5
is valid. In
turn, using the second equality in (
6.1
) we
deduce that
r(X)=\|X\|=1
. Hence by
Lemma
3.2
(iv), the conditions (ii) and (iii)
of Lemma
3.5
do not hold (thus
{\mathcal{R}}(|E|)
is
not closed). This together with Lemma
3.2
(iii)
implies that there exists a unit vector
h\in\mathcal{H}_{2}

such that
\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\langle X^{*j}X^{j}h,h\rangle=\infty
. Using the moreover part of
Corollary
2.6
, we conclude that
T
is weakly
stable if and only if the unitary part of
V
is
weakly stable. To have a more concrete example of a
measure with the above properties, consider the
normalized planar Lebesgue measure on
\bar{\mathbb{D}}
.
Straightforward computations show that the condition
(
6.1
) holds and
\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\langle X^{*j}X^{j}h,h\rangle=\infty
, where
h(z)=1
for every

z\in\bar{\mathbb{D}}
.

\diamondsuit

We conclude the paper with the following remark.

Remark 6.6
.

It is worth pointing out that if
T=T_{z}
is the
B-operator as in Example
6.4
and
\mathscr{X}\in\{\mathscr{N},\mathscr{Q},\mathscr{S},\mathscr{H}

\text{\sc(wot)}\lim_{n\to\infty}T^{n}=0
. The same conclusion can be

T=\big[\begin{smallmatrix}V&E\\

appearing in
Example
6.5
.

\diamondsuit
