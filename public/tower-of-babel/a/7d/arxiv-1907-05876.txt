Novikov’s theorem in higher dimensions?
Sushmita Venugopalan

Abstract
. Novikov’s theorem is a rigidity result on the class of taut foliations on three-manifolds. For higher dimensional manifolds, the existence of a strong symplectic form has been proposed as an analogue for tautness in order to achieve similar rigidity. This leads to the natural question of whether strong symplectic foliations satisfy an analogue of Novikov’s theorem. In this paper, we construct a five-dimensional manifold with a strong symplectic foliation that does not satisfy the expected analogue of Novikov’s theorem.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Taut foliations in three-manifolds

A codimension one foliation on a closed three-manifold is
taut

if the manifold has a closed
2
-form inducing an area form
on each leaf of the foliation. Equivalently, by a theorem of Sullivan
[
16
]
, the
foliation is taut if, through every point, there is a loop everywhere
transverse to the leaves. This characterization shows that a taut foliation does not contain any Reeb components.

Reeb components are a source of flexibility in foliation
theory in the following sense. If one is allowed to use Reeb
components, existence questions about foliations can be reduced to
topological questions of existence of distributions and Haefliger
structures. See results of Thurston
[
17
]
, Eynard-Bontemps

[
5
]
. The following theorem of Novikov is a rigidity result for taut foliations.

Proposition 1.1
.

(Novikov’s theorem,

[
14
]
)
Suppose
X
is a compact
3
-manifold and
\mathcal{F}
is a
taut foliation. Then,

(a)

For any leaf
\mathcal{L}
of
\mathcal{F}
, the map
\pi_{1}(\mathcal{L})\to\pi_{1}(X)
is
injective.

(b)

For any loop
\gamma:\mathbb{S}^{1}\to X
transverse to
\mathcal{F}
, the
homotopy class
[\gamma]
is non-trivial in
\pi_{1}(X)
.

In particular, if
X
admits a taut foliation, it must have infinite
fundamental group. For example,
\mathbb{S}^{3}
has a foliation with a Reeb
component, but Novikov’s result implies that it does not have any taut
foliation.
A non-trivial homology/homotopy class in a leaf is called
a
vanishing cycle
if it becomes homotopically trivial when it
is displaced to nearby leaves. By Novikov’s theorem, taut foliations
in dimension
3
possess no vanishing cycles.

1.2.
Rigidity in higher dimensions

What is the right analogue of tautness in higher dimensional manifolds
which imparts a similar kind of rigidity? Existence of a transversal
to the foliation is not a good answer in higher dimensions. Indeed,
Meigniez
[
12
]
showed that for a manifold
M
of dimension
greater than
3
, a pair comprised of a distribution and a Haefliger
structure (both of codimension one) is homotopic to a pair arising
from a taut foliation.

Martinez-Torres
[
9
,
10
]
proposed that the analogue of a taut
foliation in higher dimensions is a strong symplectic foliation. A

(2n+1)
-dimensional manifold with a codimension one foliation
\mathcal{F}
is

strong symplectic
if there is a closed two-form
\omega\in\Omega^{2}(X)
that restricts to a symplectic form on the leaves. This
suggestion has been further explored in
[
11
]
via a study of the
transverse geometry of strong symplectic foliations. This leads us to
the question of whether strong symplectic foliations satisfy an
analogue of Novikov’s theorem. Unfortunately, the answer to this
question is negative, and in this paper, we present a counter-example.

Theorem 1
.

There is a manifold
X^{5}
with a strong symplectic foliation

(\mathcal{F}^{4},\omega)
for which

(a)

the map
\pi_{1}(\mathcal{F})\to\pi_{1}(X)
is not
injective.

(b)

There is a loop which is transverse to the
foliation
\mathcal{F}
and which is contractible in
X
.

In spite of this result, it is still likely that strong symplectic foliations are
fairly rigid objects similar to taut foliations. The example we construct is not simply connected, and the question of
whether a Novikov-type theorem holds for simply connected manifolds
remains open.

In contrast, weak symplectic foliations are more flexible objects. A
codimension one foliation
(X,\mathcal{F})
is
weak symplectic
if there
is a two-form that restricts to a symplectic form on leaves, but is
not required to be closed in
X
. Ibort and Martínez–Torres showed
in
[
8
]
that any finitely presented group can be made to be the
fundamental group of a weak symplectic foliation. Mitsumatsu

[
13
]
showed that, in particular,
\mathbb{S}^{5}
admits a symplectic
foliation. It is even possible that a foliation is simultaneously
taut and weak symplectic, but not strong symplectic. This is the case
of a symplectic mapping torus whose return map is a symplectomorphism
that is smoothly, but not symplectically, isotopic to the
identity. This has been explored in the work of Seidel
[
15
]
.

Foliations with other geometric structures in the leaves have been
studied in the literature. For example, holomorphic foliations on
complex surfaces have been classified by Brunella

[
2
]
. Riemannian foliations are foliations with a transverse
structure of a Riemannian manifold. This condition is very
over-determined, and a manifold with a generic metric doesn’t have
such a foliation. Such foliations exist in specific cases, such as
manifolds with constant curvature and have been studied in

[
7
]
. These examples are more rigid than strong symplectic
foliations.

We present our example in Section
3
, and a slight variation in

4
. In Section
2
, we review results about symplectic
Lefschetz fibrations and adapt them to the foliated setting. Some
of the results about foliations and Lefschetz fibrations that are given
in this paper are standard in the literature. We include these to make
the paper self-contained.

2.
Symplectic Lefschetz fibrations

The building block of our example is a foliated symplectic Lefschetz fibration.
In this section, we construct a strong symplectic form on a Lefschetz
fibration with a foliated base. This is a mild extension of Gompf’s
construction
[
6
]
. All foliations are assumed to have codimension one.

A
topological Lefschetz fibration

consists of an oriented
four-manifold
X
,
and a proper map
\pi:X\to B

to an oriented surface
B
satisfying
the following
property: For any
x_{0}\in X
, either
d\pi_{x_{0}}
is surjective, or
there are coordinates
(z_{1},z_{2})
in a neighbourhood of
x_{0}
that
respect orientation and satisfy
(z_{1},z_{2})(x_{0})=(0,0)
and

f=f(x_{0})+z_{1}^{2}+z_{2}^{2}
. The projection
\pi
is injective on the set
of its critical points.

A
topological Lefschetz fibration on a foliated base manifold
consists of a
five-manifold
X
and a proper map to a foliated three manifold
(B,\mathcal{F}_{B})

\pi:X\to(B,\mathcal{F}_{B})

such that the pullback
\mathcal{F}_{X}:=\pi^{*}\mathcal{F}_{B}
is an oriented foliation on
X
, and each leaf
L
of
\mathcal{F}_{X}
is a Lefschetz fibration over the leaf
\pi(L)
in
B
.
Further, singular points in
X
are circles transverse to the foliation.

Proposition 2.1
.

Suppose
B
is a manifold with a codimension one foliation
\mathcal{F}_{B}

and a strong symplectic form
\omega_{B}
. The manifold
B
can have
boundary that is tangent to the foliation. Suppose
X\to B
is a
topological Lefschetz fibration on
(B,\mathcal{F}_{B})
, such that for any
b\in B
, the homology class of the fiber is non-trivial, i.e.
[F_{b}]\in H_{2}(X)
is non-zero. Then there is a strong symplectic form
\omega\in\Omega^{2}(X)
for which the fibers are symplectic, and have volume

\lambda>0
.

Remark 2.2
.

If the base manifold
B
is three-dimensional, then the existence of
a strong symplectic form is equivalent to the foliation
\mathcal{F}_{B}
being
taut.

Proof of Proposition
2.1
.

We start the construction by choosing two
2
-forms. One is a closed

2
-form
\zeta\in\Omega^{2}(X)
that integrates to
\lambda
on each
fiber. Indeed,
\zeta
exists because the homology class of the
fiber is on-trivial and
H^{2}(X,\mathbb{R})=\operatorname{Hom}(H_{2}(X,\mathbb{R}),\mathbb{R})
. The second
is a fiber symplectic form
\omega_{F}\in\Omega^{2}(F)
that integrates to
\lambda
, where
F
is a
regular fiber of
X\to B
.

For any point
b\in B
, we
will now define a two-form
\omega_{b}
on

\pi^{-1}(U_{b})
for a neighbourhood
U_{b}\subset B
of
b
,
that is symplectic on the fibers and has area
\lambda
on each fiber.
If
b
is a regular value of
\pi
, we can choose a trivialization
\pi^{-1}(U_{b})\simeq U_{b}\times F
and
define
\omega_{b}\in\Omega^{2}(\pi^{-1}(U_{b}))
to be the pullback of a symplectic form

\sigma\in\Omega^{2}(F)
.
If
b\in B
is a singular value of the fibration, we take a
foliated chart
U_{b}\simeq V_{b}\times(-\epsilon,\epsilon)
on
B
such that

b\in V_{b}
and
\{b\}\times(-\epsilon,\epsilon)
is the set of singular
values in
U_{b}
. A symplectic form is constructed on

\pi^{-1}(V_{b})
by Lemma
2.3
, and
\omega_{b}
is defined
to be its pullback to
\pi^{-1}(U_{b})
.

The global form
\omega
is obtained by patching.
On any open set the difference
\omega_{b}-\zeta
is exact, because the fibration
\pi^{-1}(U_{b})
retracts to the fiber

F_{b}
and
\omega_{b}-\zeta
integrates to zero on
F_{b}
. So,
\omega_{b}-\zeta=d\alpha_{b}
for some
\alpha_{b}\in\Omega^{1}(\pi^{-1}(U_{b}))
. The
base
B
can be covered by a finite subset of
\{U_{b}\}_{b}
, denoted
by
\mathcal{U}
. Let
\{\eta_{b}:B\to[0,1]\}_{b\in\mathcal{U}}
be a partition of
unity for the finite cover. Define a form

\omega_{pre}:=\zeta+\sum_{b\in\mathcal{U}}d(\eta_{b}\alpha_{b}).

This form is symplectic on each fiber
F_{b_{0}}
and has the
right volume, because on
F_{b_{0}}
, the form
\omega_{pre}=\sum_{b\in\mathcal{U}}\eta_{b}(b_{0})\omega_{b}
is a convex combination of the forms

\omega_{b}
. Finally, for a large
C>0
,
\omega:=\omega_{pre}+C\pi^{*}\omega_{B}
is a
symplectic form on the leaves of
X
, finishing the proof of the
proposition.
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

(Symplectic form near a singular fiber)

Suppose
X\to B
is a topological Lefschetz fibration over a
two-ball
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
whose regular fiber is a compact Riemann
surface
F
. The fibration has one singular point
p
which projects
to
0\in B
. Then, for any
\lambda>0
,
there is a closed two-form
\omega\in\Omega^{2}(X)
for which the fibers
are symplectic, and have volume
\lambda>0
.

Proof.

We construct the symplectic form by starting with a product
fibration
B\times F
with a product symplectic form. We delete a
neighbourhood of
B\times V
where
V
is the vanishing cycle
corresponding to the singularity
p
. In its place we glue in a
neighborhood of the origin in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
, which models a singular point
in the fibration. The details are as follows.

Figure 1
.
Construction of symplectic form near a singular fiber

Let
X_{triv}:=B\times F
be the product fibration equipped with
the two-form
\omega_{triv}:=\pi_{2}^{*}\omega_{F}
, where
\omega_{F}
is a
symplectic form on
F
that integrates to
\lambda
. For a small
constant
\delta>0
, let
A\subset F
consist of a pair annuli,
both homotopic to the vanishing cycle
V
, each having area

\delta
, and enclosing an annulus of area
\delta
between
them. Denote the region in between by
R\subset F
. See Figure

1
. Let
A_{triv}:=B\times A
,
R_{triv}:=B\times R
be product regions in
X_{triv}
.

The neighbourhood of a singular point has oriented coordinates

(z_{1},z_{2})\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
that map
p
to the origin and such that the
projection map is

\pi_{std}:\mathbb{C}^{2}\to\mathbb{C},\quad(z_{1},z_{2})\mapsto z_{1}z_{2}.

Let
\omega_{std}
be the standard symplectic form on
\mathbb{C}^{2}
. Embed

B
as a ball in the base space
\mathbb{C}
around the origin. Let

A_{std}\subset\pi_{std}^{-1}(B)
be a region whose intersection
with any fiber of
\pi_{std}
consists of a pair of homotopic
non-intersecting annuli, each of area
\delta
, the area between
them is an annulus of area
\delta
for regular fibers, and a pair
of disks for the singular fiber. The region between the two
components of
A_{std}
is denoted by
R_{std}
and is a
neighbourhood of the origin in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
.

There is a map
\phi:A_{triv}\to A_{std}
that commutes with
projection to
B
, and which is area-preserving on the fiber,
i.e.
\phi^{*}\omega_{std}-\omega_{triv}
vanishes in the fiber direction
on
A_{triv}\to B
. Therefore, this form is exact on

A_{triv}
. There is a one-form
\alpha\in\Omega^{1}(A_{triv})
such
that
\phi^{*}\omega_{std}-\omega_{triv}=d\alpha
.

Finally we glue parts of the two fibrations using the identification

\phi
constructed above. Define

X_{0}:=(X_{triv}\backslash R_{triv})\cup_{\phi:A_{triv}\to A_{std}}(A_{std}\cup R_{std}).

The space
X_{0}
fibers over
B
. Define a form
\omega\in\Omega^{2}(X_{0})

as

\omega:=\begin{cases}\omega_{triv},\quad\text{on }X_{triv}\backslash(A_{triv}\cup R_{triv})\\
\omega_{std},\quad\text{ on }R_{std},\\
\omega_{triv}+d(\eta\alpha),\quad\text{ on }A_{triv},\end{cases}

where
\eta:A_{triv}\to[0,1]
is
1
in a neighbourhood of the
inner boundary, and
0
in a neighbourhood of the outer
boundary. The form
\omega
is closed in
X_{0}
and integrates to
\lambda

on the fiber. Finally, we see that
X_{0}\to B
is diffeomorphic to

X
as a fibration.
∎

Lemma 2.4
.

Assume the setting of Proposition
2.1
. Additionally,
assume that the bundle
X\to B
has a section
s
, and that
L_{B}\subset B
is a compact leaf in the base. Then, for any
\lambda_{L}
,

\lambda_{F}>0
, the strong symplectic form
\omega
on
X
can be
constructed so that
\int_{L_{B}}s^{*}\omega=\lambda_{L}
and

\int_{F_{b}}\omega=\lambda_{F}
for any
b\in B
.

Proof.

The section
s
can be thickened to obtain an embedding
\overline{s}:B\times\mathcal{O}\to X
, where
\mathcal{O}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a ball,
\overline{s}(\cdot,0)=s
, and
\overline{s}(b,\cdot)
is tangent to the fiber for any

b\in B
. For any compactly supported non-negative form
\eta\in\Omega^{2}(\mathcal{O})
, and a strong symplectic form
\omega
on
X
, the sum

\omega^{\prime}+\eta
is also a strong symplectic form whose integral on

L_{B}
is same as that
\omega^{\prime}
. Therefore, we can increase the
integral on
F_{b}
without affecting that on
L_{B}
. Together with the
operations of scaling
\omega
by a positive scalar, and adding a
positive multiple of the base form
\omega_{B}
, we can ensure that both
conditions are satisfied.
∎

3.
The example

In this section, we construct the example that proves Theorem
1
. The
following proposition constructs a building block, which satisfies part (
a
) of Theorem
1
, but not (
b
).
Given a Riemann surface
F
and an essential loop
V\subset F
,
the proposition constructs a Lefschetz fibration whose regular fiber is
F
and
V
is a vanishing cycle. The loop
V
is
essential
, by which we mean there is a set of
2g
generators of
\pi_{1}(F)
that contains
V
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
F
be a Riemann surface with genus
g\geq 1
and let
V\subset F
be an essential loop.
There is a
foliation on the three-dimensional torus
B:=\mathbb{T}^{3}
, and a
symplectic Lefschetz fibration
\pi:X\to B
whose regular fiber is
F
, and there is a loop
\gamma
in a fiber of
\pi
that is

(a)

homotopic to
V
,

(b)

is non-contractible in
\mathcal{F}
, and

(c)

is contractible in
X
.

Figure 2
.
Almost horizontal foliation on a strip
[-1,1]\times\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

We use an
almost horizontal foliation
on the base manifold,
which we first describe. Denote the base manifold as a product

B=\mathbb{S}^{1}_{x}\times\mathbb{S}^{1}_{y}\times\mathbb{S}^{1}_{z}
, and let the maps
x
,

y
,
z
be projection to the respective components. The foliation

\mathcal{F}_{B}
is the pullback of a foliation
\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}
on
\mathbb{S}^{1}_{x}\times\mathbb{S}^{1}_{y}
, which in turn is the quotient of a foliation

\mathcal{F}_{strip}
on the strip
[-1,1]\times\mathbb{R}
. The foliation on the
strip
\mathcal{F}_{strip}
has the lines
\{x=\pm 1,0\}
as closed leaves,
and the non-closed leaves are
\{(y+c)\tan(\pi x)=\operatorname{sign}(x)\}_{c\in\mathbb{R}}
, see Figure
2
. This foliation is invariant
under translation in the
\mathbb{R}
-direction, and so, descends to a
foliation
\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}
on the quotient
([-1,1]\times\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})/(-1,y)\sim(1,y)
. Finally,
\mathcal{F}_{B}
is defined by pullback.

The form
\omega_{B}:=dy\wedge dz
is a strong symplectic form on the base
B
.

We next describe the topology of the Lefschetz fibration. As
prescribed, the regular fiber
F
is a genus
g
Riemann surface.
The singular point set of the fibration consist of two loops
P_{\pm}

transverse to the foliation such that
P_{\pm}
is contained in the
region
\{0<\pm x<1\}
and intersects each leaf in that region
once. As a result the compact leaves do not have any singular
points. The fibration is trivial on the compact leaf
\{x=\pm 1\}
. On the leaf
\{x=0\}
, the fibration has trivial monodromy along
the loop
\mathbb{S}^{1}_{y}
, and along the loop
\mathbb{S}^{1}_{z}
the monodromy map
is a
-1
Dehn twist along the essential loop
V\subset F
. Now,
consider non-compact leaves in the region
\{0<x<1\}
. These leaves
project to cylinders in the base
B
whose positive resp. negative
end is asymptotic to the compact leaf
\{x=1\}
resp.
\{x=0\}
, and
are homotopic to the loop
\mathbb{S}^{1}_{z}
. As a result, the fibration is
trivial near the positive end, and on the negative end, there is a

-1
Dehn twist about
V
. This Dehn twist is induced by the
singularity in each leaf at its intersection with the transversal

P_{+}
. See Figure
3
. The fibration is analogous in the
region
\{-1<x<0\}
.

Figure 3
.
Left:
A non-compact leaf
L
in the base
B
. The
monodromy around the singular point is equal to the difference
in the monodromies around
\beta_{u}
and
\beta_{l}
.
Right:

As the loop
\beta_{u}

\beta_{u}^{\prime}

\beta_{l}^{\prime}

. The monodromy map is trivial on

\beta_{u}
,
\beta_{u}^{\prime}
. On the loops
\beta_{l}
,
\beta_{l}^{\prime}
, the
monodromy map is a negative Dehn twist about

.

By Proposition
2.1
, the space
X
can be given a
strong symplectic form. To apply the Proposition, we need to check
that the fiber class is non-trivial in
H_{2}(X)
. Indeed if we remove
a neighbourhood of the vanishing cycle
V
in all the fibers,
the resulting bundle is trivial – it is diffeomorphic to
B\times(F\backslash V)
. Therefore, there is a section
s:B\to X
, which has
a non-zero intersection with the fiber class.

The map
\pi_{1}(\mathcal{F})\to\pi_{1}(X)
is not injective. Indeed consider a
loop
\gamma
in the compact leaf
\{x=1\}
that lies in a fiber of

\pi
and is homotopic to
V
. This loop is non-contractible in the
compact leaf, but becomes contractible when it is displaced to a
nearby non-compact leaf since it is a vanishing cycle of the Lefschetz
fibration in the non compact leaf.
∎

To finish the proof of Theorem
1
, the following definition is required.

Definition 3.2
.

(Holonomy) Suppose
\gamma:\mathbb{S}^{1}\to L
is a loop in
the leaf
L
of a foliated manifold
(X,\mathcal{F})
, where the foliation is
of codimension one. Suppose
\tau:(-\epsilon,\epsilon)\to\mathbb{R}
is a
transversal through
\gamma(0)
. The transversal can be transported
along
\gamma
via foliated charts of
W
, and the return map

h:\tau\to\tau
is a homotopy invariant, called the
holonomy

\pi_{1}(\mathcal{F})\to\operatorname{Homeo}(\tau)
of the loop
\gamma
. See p.48 in Candel-Conlon

[
3
]
.

Proof of Theorem
1
.

By Proposition
3.1

there is a Lefschetz fibration
\pi:X\to B
that satisfies part
(
a
) of Theorem
1
. That is, there is a loop

\gamma
in the leaf
L_{1}:=\{x=1\}
of
X
that is non-contractible
in
L_{1}
but contractible in
X
. The loop
\gamma
has trivial
holonomy because it is contained in a fiber of
\pi
, and the
Lefschetz fibration is trivial in a neighbourhood of the leaf
L_{1}
.

In order to obtain a contractible transversal, we first perturb the
foliation so that the holonomy of the loop
\gamma
becomes
non-trivial. We replace the single compact leaf
L_{1}
by a family
of compact leaves
L_{1}\times[-\epsilon,\epsilon]
via a
C^{\infty}
-small
perturbation of the foliation. Next, we perform a cut and shear
operation (Lemma
3.4
) at the leaf
L_{1}\times\{0\}
to make
the holonomy of
\gamma
non-trivial. This requires a hypersurface

W
in the leaf
L_{1}
that is transverse to
\gamma
and on which
the holonomy is trivial. The region
L_{1}\times[-\epsilon,\epsilon]
has
the product foliation and trivial Lefschetz fibration. Therefore, we
can take
W
to be
\pi(L_{1})\times\gamma^{\prime}
, where
\gamma^{\prime}\subset F
is a loop that intersects
\gamma
transversely once. The cut
and shear operation (Lemma
3.4
) produces a
C^{\infty}
-small
perturbation of the foliation such that
L_{1}\times\{0\}
is still
a leaf, and the loop
\gamma\subset L_{1}\subset\{0\}
has
non-trivial holonomy. We observe that
\omega
continues to be a strong
symplectic form after a
C^{\infty}
-small perturbation of the
foliation.

We now have a loop
\gamma
in the leaf
L_{1}\times\{0\}
with
non-trivial holonomy and which is contractible in
X
. By moving

\gamma
off the leaf, it becomes transverse to the foliation, see
Lemma
3.3
. This proves part (
b
) of the theorem.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Suppose
(X,\mathcal{F})
is a foliated manifold, and
\gamma
is a loop in a
leaf
L
with non-trivial holonomy. Then, there is a loop
\gamma^{\prime}

that is
C^{0}
-close to
\gamma
and transverse to the foliation.

Proof.

The proof is visual. We move the loop
\gamma
in a transverse
direction to obtain an embedded cylinder
[0,\epsilon)\times S^{1}
in

X
. That is, the segments
[0,\epsilon)\times\{pt\}
are transverse
to the foliation. The non-trivial holonomy implies that the
foliation restricted to the cylinder is as in Figure

4
. The loop
\gamma
can be moved to
\gamma^{\prime}

Lemma 3.4
.

(A cut and shear surgery)
Suppose
(X,\mathcal{F})
is a foliated
manifold containing

(a)

a compact leaf
L
and an embedded loop
\gamma:\mathbb{S}^{1}\to L
with trivial holonomy,

(b)

and a compact embedded hypersurface
W
in the leaf
L
that
intersects
\gamma
transversely at a single point. Further, the
restriction of the holonomy of
L
to
W
is trivial.

Then, there exists a
C^{\infty}
-perturbation of the foliation,
called
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
, such that
L
is still in a leaf of
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
and
\gamma

has non-trivial holonomy in the new foliation.

Proof.

Since the holonomy of the leaf
L
is trivial over
W
, there is an
embedding of
(-\epsilon,\epsilon)\times W
into
X
such that for any
w

(0,w)
maps to
w

is a segment

t

\{t\}\times W

We
cut and shear
along
W\times(-\epsilon,\epsilon)
. That is, we
cut the manifold
X
along
W\times(-\epsilon,\epsilon)
and glue by a

\Phi:=(\phi,\operatorname{Id}):(-\epsilon,\epsilon)\times W\to(-\epsilon,\epsilon)\times W

\pm\epsilon

\mathcal{F}
