Bounded dissipation law and profiles of turbulent velocity moments in wall flows
Xi Chen 1 and Katepalli R. Sreenivasan 2

Abstract
Turbulent wall flows offer the most direct means for understanding the effects of boundaries and viscosity on turbulent fluctuations. Available data on mean-square fluctuations in these flows show apparent contradiction with classical scaling based on the mean wall shear stress. We had earlier proposed an alternative model based on the principle of bounded dissipation to describe the data. Despite its putative success, a conclusive outcome requires much higher Reynolds numbers than are available at present, or can be expected to be available in the near future. However, the model can be validated satisfactorily even within the Reynolds number range already available by considering high-order moments and their distributions in the wall-normal direction. Expressions for high-order moments of streamwise velocity fluctuation u u are derived in the form ⟨ u + 2 ​ q ⟩ 1 / q = α q − β q ​ y ∗ 1 / 4 \langle u^{+2q}\rangle^{1/q}=\alpha_{q}-\beta_{q}y^{\ast 1/4} ; here q q is an integer, α q \alpha_{q} and β q \beta_{q} are constants independent of the friction Reynolds number R ​ e τ Re_{\tau} , and y ∗ = y / δ y^{\ast}=y/\delta is the distance away from the wall, normalized by the flow thickness δ \delta ; in particular, α q = μ + σ ​ q \alpha_{q}=\mu+\sigma q according to the ‘linear q-norm Gaussian’ process, where μ \mu and σ \sigma are flow-independent constants. Excellent agreement is found between this formula and the available data in boundary layers, pipes and channels for 1 ≤ q ≤ 5 1\leq q\leq 5 . For fixed y + = y ∗ ​ R ​ e τ y^{+}=y^{*}Re_{\tau} , the present formulation leads to the bounded state ⟨ u + 2 ​ q ⟩ 1 / q = α q \langle u^{+2q}\rangle^{1/q}=\alpha_{q} as R ​ e τ → ∞ Re_{\tau}\rightarrow\infty . This work demonstrates the success of the present model in describing the behavior of fluctuations in wall flows.

Introduction

In the last 90 years or so, an extraordinary amount of information has become available on the dynamics of homogeneous and isotropic turbulence
Frisch (1995)
;
Sreenivasan & Antonia (1997)
, as well as on passive scalars mixed by this form of turbulence
Sreenivasan (2018)
. Similarly, rich information has been obtained in turbulent shear flows over a smooth wall (“wall flows”)
(
Marusic et al., 2010
)
, but it is not clear how precisely the wall influences the behavior of turbulence. Questions persist
(
Townsend, 1976
;
Smits et al., 2011
)
about how the mean velocity and various moments of fluctuations around the mean are distributed with distance from the wall.
The most successful paradigm that has helped organize much of the empirical information is the so-called law-of-the-wall
(
Monin & Yaglom, 1971
)
, which posits that time averages of turbulence quantities near the wall, when suitably scaled on frictional stress at the wall, are invariant with respect to the Reynolds number (“Reynolds number similarity”). This scaling was advanced in the 1930s by
Millikan (1938)
, who put on a firmer ground the Prandtl-Karman “logarithmic law”, according to which the mean velocity varies logarithmically with the height from the wall. Logical extensions of this principle to fluctuations yield specific forms: for example, the peak velocity fluctuation in the direction of the main motion (the so-called “streamwise” fluctuation) is a constant independent of the Reynolds number. The convenient form of the Reynolds number here is the friction Reynolds number
Re_{\tau}\equiv u_{\tau}\delta/\nu
where
u_{\tau}\equiv\sqrt{\tau_{w}}
,
\tau_{w}
being the frictional stress at the wall in kinematic units.

As more data have accumulated in the last two decades from laboratory experiments
(
Hultmark et al., 2012
;
Vincenti et al., 2013
;
Willert et al., 2017
;
Samie et al., 2018
;
Ono et al., 2022
;
Ono et al., 2023
)
and direct numerical simulations of the Navier-Stokes equations (DNS)
(
Wu & Moin, 2009
;
Schlatter & Örlü, 2010
;
Jimenez et al., 2010
;
Lee & Moser, 2015
;
Yamamoto & Tsuji, 2018
;
Hoyas et al., 2022
;
Yao et al., 2023
)
, it has become increasingly clear that the normalized peak streamwise fluctuation intensity is an increasing function of the Reynolds number over its available range, calling into question the suitability of the law-of-the-wall for fluctuations.
An alternative possibility that has been considered is Townsend’s
(
Townsend, 1976
)
attached eddy model, whose basis is that the boundary layer dynamics is governed by eddies that remain permanently attached to the wall. The application of this hypothesis shows that
the peak variance of streamwise velocity fluctuation increases logarithmically with the Reynolds number
(
Marusic et al., 2017
;
Marusic & Monty, 2019
)
.
On the other hand, the recent work of
Chen & Sreenivasan (2021)
;
Chen & Sreenivasan (2022)
;
Chen & Sreenivasan (2023)
has shown that the increase is a finite-Reynolds-number effect, which eventually saturates to a constant, restoring the validity of the law-of-the-wall. The initial increase and eventual saturation were based on the so-called bounded dissipation model (see also
Monkewitz (2022)
), which states that the dissipation at the wall approaches its upper bound value of 1/4 as the Reynolds number becomes very large and that all fluctuating quantities of interest are slaved to this behavior. This principle of bounded dissipation gave verifiable results for peaks of all variances of fluctuations that reside in the region close to the wall (“inner layer”), in agreement with the data.

.

The underpinnings of both models are not foolproof at present (see below for more comments), so agreement with empirical results is a more solid basis for deciding the relative merits of the models. It has been pointed out, rightly, that the available Reynolds number range is not adequate to make definitive claims in favor of one model or the other: comparison of only velocity variances in the available data range is ambiguous in some instances—though independent assessments
(
Nagib et al., 2024
;
Pirozzoli, 2024
)
, besides our own
(
Chen & Sreenivasan, 2021
;
Chen & Sreenivasan, 2022
;
Chen & Sreenivasan, 2023
)
, document support for the bounded law over the attached eddy results.
Thus, a detailed consideration of high-order velocity moments is essential. Here, we consider not only high-order moments but also the wall-normal profiles of such moments up to order 10, and make detailed data comparisons for the two formulations. We show that the bounded dissipation model is better supported by the data, and follow it up with some general conclusions. This defines the scope of this article.

2
Brief theoretical considerations

A. Attached eddy model:
According to attached eddy model, the variance of streamwise velocity
u
follows a logarithmic decay as

\langle u^{+2}\rangle=B_{1}-A_{1}\ln(y^{\ast}).

(1)

Here, the superscript
+
indicates normalization by
u_{\tau}
and
y^{\ast}=y/\delta
.
The brackets
\langle\rangle
indicate temporal averages. This equation is meant to be valid in the overlap region, nominally associated with the logarithmic distribution of the mean velocity profile
(
Hultmark et al., 2012
;
Meneveau & Marusic, 2013
)
. The same reference
Meneveau & Marusic (2013)
generalizes this formula for high-order moments as

\langle u^{+2q}\rangle^{1/q}=B_{q}-A_{q}\ln(y^{\ast}).

(2)

Here
A_{q}
and
B_{q}
are constants independent of
y^{\ast}
and
Re_{\tau}
but depend on the moment order
q
. In going from Eq.
1
to Eq.
2
, a Gaussian distribution of
u
has been assumed, leading to
\langle u^{+2q}\rangle^{1/q}=[(2q-1)!!]^{1/q}\langle u^{+2}\rangle
with
q!!\equiv q(q-2)(q-4)\ldots 1
, though it is known from
Meneveau & Marusic (2013)
that the slopes
A_{q}
deviate significantly from the Gaussian expectation. Analogous to the Karman constant
1
1

1

We are aware that the Karman constant may depend on the flow
(
Nagib et al., 2007
;
Monkewitz & Nagib, 2023
)
.
in the mean velocity distribution, quantities
A_{q}
are called the Townsend-Perry constants
(
Marusic & Monty, 2019
;
Diwan & Morrison, 2019
;
Meneveau & Marusic, 2013
;
Birnir et al., 2021
;
Smits, 2022
)
.

Equation
1
builds on the notion that turbulent eddies are effectively attached to the wall, with their number density varying inversely with
y^{\ast}

(
Marusic & Monty, 2019
)
. Because the identification of a clear eddy structure is fraught with uncertainties
(
Schlatter et al., 2014
)
, the
k^{-1}
velocity spectrum
(
Perry et al., 1986
)
is sometimes thought to be another possible rationale for the same idea; but that, too, has not been observed unambiguously
(
Vallikivi et al., 2015
;
Panton et al., 2017
)
. To avoid these problems,
Hultmark (2012)
simply invoked an overlap argument. Thus, there is considerable
a priori
room for the debate on the logarithmic profile for the fluctuation intensity.

B. Bounded dissipation model:
According to
Chen & Sreenivasan (2021)
;
Chen & Sreenivasan (2022)
;
Chen & Sreenivasan (2023)
, the alternative model based on the boundedness of dissipation yields

\langle u^{+2}\rangle=\alpha_{1}-\beta_{1}(y^{\ast})^{1/4}.

(3)

The procedure is summarized here only briefly because the details can be found in
Chen & Sreenivasan (2023)
. We have shown that an inner expansion of the form
\langle u^{+2}\rangle=f_{0}(y^{+})+f_{1}(y^{+})g(Re_{\tau})
works very well close to the peak fluctuations. Here,
y^{+}=y^{\ast}Re_{\tau}
is the inner viscous scale, and the Gauge function
g(Re_{\tau})=Re^{-1/4}_{\tau}
depicts the finite
Re_{\tau}
dependence. Similarly, the appropriate outer expansion takes the form
\langle u^{+2}\rangle=F_{0}(y^{\ast})
.
Matching the two expansions results in Eq.
3
. Similar matching of inner-outer expansions of a high-order moment
q
leads to

\langle u^{+2q}\rangle^{1/q}=\alpha_{q}-\beta_{q}(y^{\ast})^{1/4},

(4)

where
\alpha_{q}
and
\beta_{q}
are constants independent of
y^{\ast}
and
Re_{\tau}
.

In contrast to Millikan’s matching for the logarithmic mean velocity, the procedure here matches the two-term inner expansion with the full outer scaling. Equation
4
could then be considered a generalization of Eq.
3
by replacing
\langle u^{+2}\rangle
with
\langle u^{+2q}\rangle^{1/q}
. Another way to derive Eq.
4
is to invoke a “linear-q-norm Gaussian” (LQNG) process developed in
Chen & Sreenivasan (2022)
, which is different from the derivation of Eq.
2
on the basis of the Gaussian assumption. This will be explained more later.

Asymptotic behaviors

Different asymptotic behaviors can be deduced for the two proposals. Unlike the unbounded logarithmic growth of variance according to Eq.
1
, a bounded state follows for Eq.
3
, i.e.,
\langle u^{+2}\rangle=\alpha_{1}\approx 10
as
Re_{\tau}\rightarrow\infty
. This feature also appears for higher
q
. It is therefore important to understand how the
A_{q}
and
\alpha_{q}
vary with
q
because they form the leading terms in the two models. They are plotted in Fig. 4 for all three flows.

For
A_{q}
(normalized by
A_{1}
), the data vary with the flow, those for the channel being the lowest. This is in addition to the sensitivity shown by
A_{1}
itself with respect to

(

. Moreover, following the Gaussian model in
Meneveau & Marusic (2013)

A_{q}=A_{1}[(2q-1)!!]^{1/q},

(5)

which is, however, conspicuously higher than the

Birnir et al. (2021)

In contrast, the
\alpha_{q}

q

\langle u^{+2q}\rangle^{1/q}=\alpha_{q}
for
y^{\ast}\rightarrow 0

\alpha_{q}
actually corresponds to a LQNG process of
\phi=(u^{+})^{2}
, which satisfies the operator reflection symmetry
(
Chen & Sreenivasan, 2022
)
as

\mathbf{E}\circ\mathbf{Q}(\phi)=\mathbf{Q}\circ\mathbf{E}(\kappa).

(6)

Here,
\kappa=N(\mu,{2\sigma})
is a Gaussian seed with mean
\mu
and variance
2\sigma
;
\mathbf{E}
is the exponential transform operator, e.g.
\mathbf{E}(\kappa)=e^{\kappa}
; and
\mathbf{Q}
is the operator of
q
-norm estimation, e.g.
\mathbf{Q}(\phi)=\langle\phi^{q}\rangle^{1/q}
with
\langle\cdot\rangle
for the expectation. To see the linearity with
q
, applying
\mathbf{E}^{-1}
(i.e., taking the logarithm) on both sides of Eq.
6
yields

\displaystyle\langle u^{+2q}\rangle^{1/q}=\mathbf{Q}(\phi)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}^{-1}\circ\mathbf{Q}\circ\mathbf{E}(\kappa)

(7)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ln[\langle e^{\kappa q}\rangle^{1/q}]=\mu+\sigma q.

With
\mu=5.5
and
\sigma=5
, Eq.
7
explains the linear behavior of
\alpha_{q}
in Fig. 4b. Note that the present relation
\alpha_{q}=5.5+5q
is very close to
\alpha_{q}=5.5+5.9q
from Eq. (4.10) of
Chen & Sreenivasan (2022)
for the inner peak moments at
y^{+}=15
, indicating that the entire flow shares the same LQNG property.

The LQNG property can now be used to derive Eq.
4
from Eq.
3
. According to Eq.
6
or Eq.
7
, the ratio of moments increases linearly with order, i.e.,
\langle u^{+2q}\rangle^{1/q}/\langle u^{+2}\rangle=(\mu+\sigma q)/(\mu+\sigma)=c_{1}+c_{2}q
where
c_{1}=\mu/(\mu+\sigma)
and
c_{2}=\sigma/(\mu+\sigma)
. Furthermore, with Eq.
3
for
\langle u^{+2}\rangle
, one obtains Eq.
4
for high-order moments. It is evident that LQNG describes Fig. 4b quite consistently.

5
Conclusions

The most important quantity in turbulent wall flows is the mean velocity distribution and the associated gradient at the wall. For these flows with large fluctuations, it is natural that they will affect the mean flow strongly. Thus, there is considerable interest in the behavior of fluctuations in wall flows. For some time now, the attached eddy model has been advanced
(
Marusic & Monty, 2019
)
as a suitable formulation to explain observed behaviors (experiment and DNS). Recent work based on bounded dissipation
(
Chen & Sreenivasan, 2021
;
Chen & Sreenivasan, 2022
;
Chen & Sreenivasan, 2023
)
offers a better alternative as far as it can be judged by comparison with the data. In this paper, we subject the latter alternative to more rigorous tests by comparing high-order moments (instead of restricting simply to the variance) and also the wall-normal profiles of these high-order moments. We find that the bounded dissipation paradigm for high-order moments of wall turbulence is in excellent agreement with the data of boundary layers, pipes and channels. The new expression covers a wide wall-normal range and suggests a constant plateau for the moments that grow linearly with the order of the moment. Compared to the attached eddy model, the current results support the recovery of the classical law-of-the-wall for asymptotically high Reynolds number.

Future work will focus on the connection of the present formulation to the flow geometry.
