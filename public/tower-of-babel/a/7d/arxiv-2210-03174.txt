Prudent walk in dimension six and higher
Markus HEYDENREICH, Lorenzo TAGGI, Niccolò TORRI

Abstract
We study the high-dimensional uniform prudent self-avoiding walk, which assigns equal probability to all nearest-neighbor self-avoiding paths of a fixed length that respect the prudent condition, namely, the path cannot take any step in the direction of a previously visited site. We prove that the prudent self-avoiding walk converges to Brownian motion under diffusive scaling if the dimension is large enough. The same result is true for weakly prudent walk in dimension d > 5 d>5 . A challenging property of the high-dimensional prudent walk is the presence of an infinite-range self-avoidance constraint. Interestingly, as a consequence of such a strong self-avoidance constraint, the upper critical dimension of the prudent walk is five, and thus greater than for the classical self-avoiding walk. Keywords : Prudent walk ⋅ \cdot Self-avoiding random walk ⋅ \cdot Lace Expansion ⋅ \cdot Scaling limit ⋅ \cdot Critical dimension Mathematics Subject Classification : 82B41 ⋅ \cdot 60G50

Introduction

Prudent walk is a class of self-repellent random walks where the walk cannot take increments pointing in the direction of its range. This results in an infinite-range repellence condition.
The prudent walk was originally introduced in
[
26
,
27
]
under the name of
self-directed walk
and in
[
21
]
under the name
outwardly directed self-avoiding walk
as a class of self-avoiding walks which are simple to modelize.
In the last 20 years this walk has attracted the attention of the combinatorics community, see e.g.
[
1
,
5
,
9
]
, and also of the probability community, see e.g.
[
2
,
7
,
18
,
19
]
.

Let us stress that the prudent condition can be defined in two different ways: in its original formulation
[
26
,
27
]
, prudent random walk is a random walk that chooses its direction uniformly among the admissable moves, and is thus a stochastic process. This model is called
kinetic prudent walk
, and was considered in
[
2
]
.
Alternatively, we fix a length
n
and choose uniformly a prudent trajectory of length
n
, we call this the
uniform prudent walk
. This latter model has been considered by the combinatorics community and also investigated probabilistically
[
18
,
19
]
. In this article we consider the uniform prudent walk.

In dimension
d=2
the scaling limit of the prudent random walk was identified in
[
2
,
18
]
.
The present work concentrates on the high-dimensional case. Similar to other walks without self-intersections (most notably self-avoiding walk) there exists an upper critical dimension
d_{c}
such that in dimension
d>d_{c}
prudent walk is macroscopically very similar to simple random walk. This implies, in particular, that in high dimensions the various self-repellent walk models are all very similar. Most interestingly, we find strong evidence that this upper critical dimension for prudent walk is
d_{c}=5
, rather than 4 as for self-avoiding walk; the extra dimension results from the infinite-range repellence condition of prudent walks (see Section
2.6
for further discussion of the upper critical dimension).

Our approach is based on the
lace expansion
. The lace expansion method was introduced by Brydges and Spencer
[
6
]
to study weakly-self avoiding random walk.
The method was widely exploited for the self-avoiding walk, percolation, lattice trees and lattice animals, and the Ising model, see
[
14
,
16
,
20
,
24
]
and references therein.
The present work is the first one where we employ the lace expansion to a model with infinite range interaction.

The lace expansion has also been exploited successfully to investigate critical percolation in high dimension. It might very well be that the methods proposed in this work to allow for an investigation of percolation with infinite-range interactions as studied by Hilario and Sidoravicius
[
15
]
.

1.1
Main results

We let
\mathcal{W}_{n}
be the set of
n
-step nearest-neighbor paths on the hypercubic lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
starting at the origin, see (
2.3
). We call a path
w=(w(0),w(1),\dots,w(n))

prudent
if

w(s)\not\in w(t)+\mathbb{N}_{0}\big(w(t)-w(t-1)\big)

for all
0\leq s<t\leq n
; see Figure
1
for an example.

Figure 1
:
An example of prudent walk path starting at
x
. When traversing the path starting from
x
, the tip of the path at any given moment does not point to the range of the path until that time.

Our main result is convergence of the rescaled prudent walk to Brownian motion in high dimension. To this end, let
\mathbb{D}(A,B)
be the space of functions
f\colon A\to B
that are left continuous and have limits from the right, equipped with the Skorokhod
J_{1}
-topology, see
[
3
]
.

For a prudent walk
w
, we denote the space-time rescaled variable

X_{n}(t):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{Kn}}w\big(\lfloor nt\rfloor\big),\quad t\in[0,1],

(1.1)

for a certain constant
K>0
defined in (
5.22
) below.
We consider
X_{n}
as a
\mathbb{D}([0,1],\mathbb{R})
-valued random variable with respect to the uniform measure
\langle\;\cdot\;\rangle_{n}
on the set of
n
-step prudent walks.

Theorem 1.1

(Convergence to Brownian motion)
.

There exists
d_{0}>5
such that for
d>d_{0}
the following convergence holds.
For any bounded continuous function
f\colon\mathbb{D}([0,1],\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}
, we have that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\big\langle f(X_{n})\big\rangle_{n}=\mathbb{E}\Big[f((W_{s})_{s\in[0,1]})\Big]\,

where
(W_{s})_{s\geq 0}
denotes the standard Brownian motion and
\mathbb{E}
is its expectation.

The result remains true for the weakly prudent walk introduced in Section
2.3
in dimension
d>5
provided that a “strength parameter”
\lambda
for the weakly prudent walk, defined in (
2.15
), is sufficiently small.

We furthermore prove that the
prudent bubble condition
is satisfied if the dimension is large (or
d>5
and
\lambda
is small), see Section
2.4
.

2
Definitions and results

Given a square-sumable function
f
on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, we define its Fourier transform as

\widehat{f}(k)=\sum\limits_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}f(x)e^{ix\cdot k},\qquad k\in[-\pi,\pi]^{d}

(2.1)

and observe that

f(x)=\int_{[-\pi,\pi]^{d}}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}^{d}k}{(2\pi)^{d}}\widehat{f}(k)e^{-ik\cdot x}.

(2.2)

We now recall some basic definitions for the simple random walk and introduce the main definitions for prudent walk and weakly prudent walk.

2.1
Preliminaries on simple random walk

For
x,y\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}:=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
, we write

\begin{split}\mathcal{W}_{n}(x,y):=\Big\{w\colon\{0,\dots,n\}\to\mathbb{Z}^{d}\colon&w(0)=x,w(n)=y,\\
&\text{ and }|w(s)-w(s-1)|=1\text{ for all }s=1,\dots,n\Big\}\end{split}

(2.3)

for the set of
n
-step walks from
x
to
y
. We further write
\mathcal{W}(x,y)=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\mathcal{W}_{n}(x,y)
, and for
w\in\mathcal{W}(x,y)
we write
|w|
for the length of the walk, that is, the unique
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
such that
w\in\mathcal{W}_{n}(x,y)
.
We denote by
C_{z}
the simple random walk Green’s function, that is,

C_{z}(x,y):=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\big|\mathcal{W}_{n}(x,y)\big|z^{n},

(2.4)

and we let
C_{z}(x)=C_{z}(0,x)
.
We recall that the radius of convergence of
z\mapsto\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}C_{z}(x)
equals
\frac{1}{2d}
.
It is convenient to introduce the function

D(x):=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2d}&\quad\mbox{ if $|x|=1$,}\\
0&\quad\mbox{ otherwise},\end{cases}\qquad x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},

(2.5)

with Fourier transform given by

\widehat{D}(k)=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\cos(k_{i})
.
Since
|\mathcal{W}_{n}(0,x)|=(2d)^{n}D^{\ast n}(x)
(where
D^{\ast n}=D\ast\cdots\ast D
denotes
n
-fold convolution), we get that the Fourier transform of
C_{z}(x)
for
|z|<z_{c}
is given by

\widehat{C}_{z}(k)=\frac{1}{1-2dz\widehat{D}(k)},\qquad k\in[-\pi,\pi]^{d}.

(2.6)

For later reference, we note the elementary relations

1-\widehat{D}(k)=\Big(\frac{1}{2d}+o(1)\Big)\,|k|^{2}\quad\text{as $|k|\to 0$}\qquad{and}\qquad\lim_{n\to\infty}n\big(1-\widehat{D}(k/\sqrt{n})\big)=\frac{1}{2d}\,|k|^{2},

(2.7)

where
|\,\cdot\,|
denotes the
L^{2}
norm.

2.2
The prudent walk

We now properly define the prudent walk.

Definition 2.1
.

A walk
w\in\mathcal{W}_{n}(x,y)
satisfies the
prudent condition
if

w(s)\not\in w(t)+\mathbb{N}_{0}\big(w(t)-w(t-1)\big)\qquad\text{for all $0\leq s<t\leq n$.}

(2.8)

We shortly say “
w
is prudent”.
The set of
prudent

n
-step walks from
x
to
y
is denoted by
\mathfrak{P}_{n}(x,y)
and

c_{n}(x,y):=|\mathfrak{P}_{n}(x,y)|
denotes its cardinality. We denote
c_{n}(x):=c_{n}(0,x)
.
The total number of
n
-step prudent walks is
c_{n}:=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}c_{n}(x)
. From sub-additivity it follows that the limit

\mu:=\lim_{n\to+\infty}(c_{n})^{1/n}

(2.9)

exists. The constant
\mu
is called the
connective constant
and satisfies the trivial bounds

d-1\leq\mu\leq 2d-1.

(2.10)

Prudent random walk is the uniform measure on the set of
n
-step prudent walks. Note that the prudent random walk is not a stochastic process, because the resulting family of measures is not consistent.

The
prudent two-point function
is defined as the generating function

G_{z}(x,y)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}(x,y)\,z^{n}

(2.11)

and we let
G_{z}(x)=G_{z}(0,x)
.
We let
z_{c}:=\frac{1}{\mu}
be the radius of convergence of the power series

\chi(z):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}\,z^{n}=\sum\limits_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}G_{z}(x).

(2.12)

We refer to
z_{c}
as
critical point
and
\chi(z)
as
susceptibility
.

2.3
The weakly-prudent walk

We now introduce the weakly-prudent walk, in which each step of the walk which does not fulfill the prudent condition is penalized by a multiplicative parameter
\lambda\in[0,1]
. Hence the case
\lambda=0
corresponds to simple random walk, while the case
\lambda=1
corresponds to prudent walk.
We also rephrase the expression for the prudent two-point function in (
2.11
) so that it is more suited for an expansion.
Given a
n
-step walk
w=(w(0),\dots,w(n))
, and
0\leq s<t\leq n
, we define

U_{st}(w):=\begin{cases}-1,&\quad\text{if}\quad w:w(t)\downarrow w(s),\\
0,&\quad\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(2.13)

where
w(t)\downarrow a
is shorthand for
a-w(t)\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\big(w(t)-w(t-1)\big)
—recall the prudent condition (
2.8
).
If
w
is such that
w(t)\downarrow a
, then we say that
the
t
-th step of
w

sees

a
or, shortly,
w(t)

sees

a
.
The two-point function for the weakly-prudent walk is defined as

G^{\lambda}_{z}(x,y)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\ z^{n}c^{\lambda}_{n}(x,y)

(2.14)

where

c^{\lambda}_{n}(x,y)=\sum_{w\in\mathcal{W}_{n}(x,y)}\varphi^{\lambda}(w)\quad\quad\text{and}\quad\varphi^{\lambda}(w):=\prod_{0\leq s<t\leq n}\big(1+\,\lambda\,U_{st}(w)\big).

(2.15)

We also let
c^{\lambda}_{n}(x)=c^{\lambda}_{n}(0,x)
and
G^{\lambda}_{z}(x)=G^{\lambda}_{z}(0,x)
.

We let
c^{\lambda}_{n}=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}c_{n}^{\lambda}(x)
. Let us observe that
\varphi^{1}(w)\neq 0
if and only if
w
satisfies the prudent condition. To simplify the notation, we denote
\varphi(w)=\varphi^{1}(w)
.

In analogy with (
2.12
), for any
\lambda>0
, we define
z_{c}(\lambda)
as the radius of convergence of the susceptibility

\chi^{\lambda}(z):=\sum\limits_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}G_{z}^{\lambda}(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\ z^{n}\sum_{w\in\mathcal{W}_{n}}\varphi^{\lambda}(w).

(2.16)

Hence it follows that
z_{c}(0)=\frac{1}{2d}
,
z_{c}(1)=\frac{1}{\mu}
,
G^{1}_{z}(x)=G_{z}(x),

and

\chi^{1}(z)=\chi(z)
.

2.4
Results

A central object in our analysis is the
prudent bubble diagram
, which we now introduce.
To this end, we introduce a notion that is weaker than the symbol
w(t)\downarrow a
used in (
2.13
):
for
x,a\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,
a\neq x
, we let

x\bot a\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x-a\in\bigcup_{j=1,\dots,d}e_{j}\,\mathbb{Z}\,,

(2.17)

where
e_{1},\dots,e_{d}
are the coordinate axes (i.e.,
x\bot a
whenever
x
and
a
differ in at most one coordinate).
For any
x,a\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
we define the
modified indicator function

\mathbf{1}_{\{\bot{a}\}}(x):=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{d}&\mbox{ if $x\bot a$ and $x\neq a$}\\
0&\text{ otherwise},\end{cases}

(2.18)

and abbreviate
\mathbf{1}_{\bot}(x)=\mathbf{1}_{\{\bot{0}\}}(x)
. Observe that
\mathbf{1}_{\{\bot{a}\}}(x)=\mathbf{1}_{\bot}(x-a)
.

Definition 2.2

(Prudent Bubble Diagram)
.

We define the prudent bubble diagram as

{\mathsf{B}}^{\lambda}_{z}:=\|G^{\lambda}_{z}\ast G_{z}^{\lambda}\ast\mathbf{1}_{\bot}\|_{\infty}=\sup_{y\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\end{subarray}}G^{\lambda}_{z}(0,x_{1})G_{z}^{\lambda}(x_{1},x_{2})\mathbf{1}_{\{\bot{y}\}}(x_{2})

(2.19)

If
\lambda=1
, we write
{\mathsf{B}}_{z}={\mathsf{B}}^{1}_{z}
.

The next theorem states that the critical prudent bubble diagram is finite if the dimension is sufficiently large and
\lambda=1
or if the dimension is at least
6
and
\lambda
is sufficiently small.

Theorem 2.1

(Prudent Bubble Condition)
.

There exist
d_{0}\geq 5
and
\lambda_{0}>0
such that if either

(a)

d>5
and
\lambda\in(0,\lambda_{0})
, or

(b)

\lambda=1
and
d>d_{0}
,

then the critical prudent bubble diagram
{\mathsf{B}}^{\lambda}_{z_{c}}
is finite. Moreover, there exists a constant
C>0
(independent of
\lambda
and the dimension
d
) such that

{\mathsf{B}}^{\lambda}_{z_{c}}<C/d.

(2.20)

The proof of Theorem
2.1
is intertwined with the asymptotic behaviour of the critical two-point function, which we formulate in the next theorem.

Theorem 2.2

(Critical Two-Point Function)
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
2.1
, there exists
K\in(0,\infty)
,
defined in (
5.22
) below, such that for
k\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\widehat{c}^{\lambda}_{n}\big(k/\sqrt{n}\big)\sim\widehat{c}^{\lambda}_{n}\big(0\big)\exp\{-K\,|k|^{2}\}\qquad\text{ as $n\to\infty$.}

(2.21)

Theorem
2.2
is already a strong indication towards the Brownian motion limit as it establishes a Gaussian limit for the endpoint. We reformulate this convergence in the generalized context of Section
2.3
.

Theorem 2.3

(Convergence to Brownian motion; general version)
.

Consider (weakly or strictly) prudent walk in dimension
d>5
.
There exists
c>0
such that if
\lambda/d<c
, then

\lim_{n\to\infty}\big\langle f(X_{n})\big\rangle_{n}=\mathbb{E}\Big[f((W_{s})_{s\in[0,1]})\Big]\,

for any bounded continuous function
f\colon\mathbb{D}([0,1],\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}
, where
(W_{s})_{s\geq 0}
denotes the standard Brownian motion and
\mathbb{E}
is its expectation.

This theorem generalizes Theorem
1.1
.
In order to get convergence in path space, as claimed in Theorem
2.3
, we need both convergence of finite-dimensional projections as well as tightness. We state and prove these results in Section
6
.

Further results concern asymptotics of the critical Green’s function of prudent walk in Corollary
5.3
and asymptotics of the moment of the endpoint of prudent walk in Proposition
B.1
.

2.5
Discussion

It is instructive to compare our results for prudent walk with analog results for (classical) self-avoiding walk. Indeed, it is known that rescaled self-avoiding walk in sufficiently high dimension converges to Brownian motion. This has been established by Slade
[
22
,
23
]
using memory cutoff, by Hara and Slade
[
12
]
using an alternative method involving fractional derivative estimates, by Heydenreich
[
13
]
for long-range self-avoiding walk and, very recently, by Michta
[
17
]
for weakly self-avoiding walk on the high-dimensional torus.

An interesting feature of our results is that the upper critical dimension, which appears to be
5
for prudent walk, differs from the upper critical dimension of self-avoiding walk, which is
4
.
We give a heuristic explanation for this change in the next subsection.

Our proof of the bubble condition and the critical two-point function requires a novel lace expansion argument. Our proofs are based on the lace expansion, which was pioneered by Brydges and Spencer
[
6
]
and further developed for self-avoiding walk by Hara and Slade. While the actual expansion is the same as for self-avoiding walks, the expression for the
\Pi
-diagrams as defined in (
3.2
) involves both the two-point function
G_{z}^{\lambda}
as well as the (modified) indicator function
\mathbf{1}_{\bot}
(see Figure
3
, where indicators are represented by dotted lines), and interestingly the resulting diagrams are more reminiscent of percolation lace-expansion diagrams rather than the the diagrams appearing for classical self-avoiding walk.
However, in contrast to earlier lace expansions, we obtain inhomogeneous lace expansion diagrams, and dealing with these inhomogeneities as well as the the infinite-range avoidance constraints is the major challenge in the proof. Our analysis therefore has substantial differences with respect to such classical cases.

This heterogeneity is reflected in the definition of the prudent bubble diagram, and dealing with it is the main obstacle in deriving the diagrammatic bounds and in the proof that the expansion converges. We demonstrate these in Sections
3
and
4
, which we consider the most innovative part of the paper. Once the above bounds have been established, we can rely on a well-established machinery to get convergence to Brownian motion following
[
16
, Chapter 6.6]
or
[
13
]
. Therefore, we concentrate in Section
6
and Appendix
B
on the necessary adaptations rather than detailing the argument.

Recently, novel proofs have been found that allow a direct comparison between weakly self-avoiding walk’s Green function and simple random walk’s Green function, cf.
[
4
,
25
]
.
It might be asked whether similar techniques are applicable to weakly prudent walk. However, it appears that the
\Pi
-diagram for (weakly) prudent walk is converging as
|\Pi(x)|\approx|x|^{-(d-2)}
along the coordinate axes, and thus decays too slow for the methods of
[
4
,
25
]
to apply
straightforwardly.
Even though there is presumably faster decay off the axes, this is therefore indicating that methods involving pointwise estimates are not naturally adapted to the prudent walk and it should require additional estimates to fit the prudent walk setting. Our analysis of the lace expansion is based on Fourier transforms.

2.6
The upper critical dimension for the prudent walk

Our results show that the weakly prudent walk converges to Brownian motion under diffusive scaling in dimension
d>5
if the interaction parameter
\lambda
is small enough.
Since the critical dimension is not expected to depend
on the intensity of the repulsion parameter, this strongly suggests
that also the (strictly) prudent walk converges to Brownian motion under diffusive scaling
in any dimension
d>5
(which we prove only if the dimension is large enough),
and, thus, that the critical dimension is at most five.

We conjecture that the upper critical dimension for the prudent walk is precisely five.
This implies that it is
strictly greater
than for the self-avoiding walk, which has upper critical dimension four.
Our conjecture is supported by the following considerations.

It is generally expected that the prudent bubble diagram in Definition
2.2
is finite whenever the corresponding prudent
random walk
bubble diagram
\|C_{1}\ast C_{1}\ast\mathbf{1}_{\bot}\|_{\infty}
is finite. Indeed, the latter is finite if and only if
d>5
, because
(C\ast C)(x)\approx|x|^{4-d}
and this is summable over
x\in e_{1}\mathbb{Z}
whenever
4-d<-1
. This suggests that also the (ordinary) prudent bubble diagram is infinite for
d\leq 5
.

Alternatively, one may study the expected number of times that two simple random walks “see each other”:
Let
(X^{1}(t))_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
,
(X^{2}(t))_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
be two independent simple random walks starting from the origin, and
let
X^{i}_{j}
denote the
j
th coordinate of
X^{i}
. By the Markov property and reversibility,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big(\sum\limits_{s,t>0}^{\infty}\boldsymbol{1}_{\{X^{1}(t)\downarrow X^{2}(s)\}}\Big)=\frac{1}{2d}\mathbb{E}\Big(\sum\limits_{s>0,t>0}^{\infty}\mathbf{1}_{\{\bot{X^{2}(s)}\}}\big(X^{1}(t-1)\big)\Big)=\frac{1}{2d}\mathbb{E}\Big(\sum\limits_{s>0,t\geq 0}^{\infty}\mathbf{1}_{\{\bot{0}\}}\big(X^{1}(t+s)\big)\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{1}{2d}\mathbb{E}\Big(\sum\limits_{t>0}^{\infty}\,t\,\mathbf{1}_{\{\bot{0}\}}\big(X^{1}(t)\big)\Big)=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\Big(\sum\limits_{t>0}^{\infty}\,t\,\boldsymbol{1}_{\big\{X_{j}^{1}(t)=0\forall j\neq d,\,\,X_{d}^{1}(t)\neq 0\big\}}\Big)\sim C\sum\limits_{t=1}^{\infty}t^{-\frac{d-1}{2}+1},

which is finite if and only if
d>5
.

The above calculation suggests that models with
\ell
-dimensional avoidance constraints have upper critical dimension
4+\ell
.
Self-avoiding walk may be viewed as a repellent model with a 0-dimensional avoidance constraint (and has upper critical dimension 4), whereas prudent walk has a one-dimensional avoidance constraint.

We observe a similar shift in the upper critical dimension for oriented percolation (which has upper critical dimension 4+1) but that is caused by a change of the infrared bound (e.g.
[
24
, Thm. 12.1]
), whereas in our model the shift is caused by a change in the bubble diagram.

3
Lace Expansion and diagrammatic estimates

We next derive the lace expansion for prudent walks, and derive
diagrammatic bounds
that give quantitative control over the lace expansion-related quantities.
As we noted above, the presence of non-localized infinite-range avoidance constraints results in a new type of inhomogeneous lace-expansion diagrams, and therefore the lace-expansion analysis varies significantly from classical self-avoiding walk.

3.1
Lace Expansion

We shall next recall the lace expansion for self-intersecting walks. We follow the presentation in Slade’s exposition about self-avoiding walks
[
24
, Chapter 3]
or
[
16
, Chapter 5]
).

Definition 3.1

(Graphs and Laces)
.

•

Given an interval
I=[a,b]
of integers (
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}
,
0\leq a<b
), we refer to a pair

\{s,t\}
,
s<t
of elements of
I
as an edge. To abbreviate the notation, we usually write
st
for
\{s,t\}
.
The length of an edge
st
is
t-s
. A set of edges is called a
graph
.

•

A graph
\Gamma
is said to be
connected
if both
a
and
b
are end-points of edges in
\Gamma
, and if in addition, for any
c\in(a,b)
, there are
s,t\in[a,b]
such that
s<c<t
and
st\in\Gamma
. The set of all graphs on
[a,b]

is denoted by
\mathcal{B}[a,b]
, and the subset consisting of all connected graphs is denoted by
\mathcal{G}[a,b]
.

•

A
lace
is a minimally connected graph, i.e, a connected graph for which the removal of any edge would result in a disconnected graph.
The set of laces on
[a,b]
is denoted by
\mathcal{L}[a,b]

and the set of laces on
[a,b]
consisting of exactly
N
edges
is denoted by
\mathcal{L}_{N}[a,b]
.

See Figure
2
for an illustration of the terms
graph
and
lace
, and
[
24
, Chapter 3]
for further explanation about these quantities.

Given a connected graph
\Gamma
, the following prescription associates to
\Gamma
a unique lace
\mathcal{L}_{\Gamma}
: the lace
\mathcal{L}_{\Gamma}
consists of edges

s_{1}t_{1}
,
s_{2}t_{2}
,
\ldots
where

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} s_{1}&=a,\\
t_{1}&=\max\{t:at\in\Gamma\}\end{aligned}\qquad\text{and for $i\geq 2$}\qquad\begin{aligned} t_{i+1}&=\max\{t:st\in\Gamma,s<t_{i}\}\\
s_{i}&=\min\{s:st_{i}\in\Gamma\}.\end{aligned}

(3.1)

We let
\mathcal{C}(L)
be the set of all edges which are ‘compatible’ with the lace
L
, that is, all edges
st\not\in L
such that
{\mathcal{L}}_{L\cup\{st\}}=L
.
We finally introduce the
lace expansion coefficients
as

\displaystyle\Pi^{\lambda}_{z}(x)=

\displaystyle\sum_{N=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{N}\Pi_{z}^{(N)}(x),

(3.2)

where

\Pi_{z}^{(N)}(x)=\sum_{w\in\mathcal{W}(0,x)}z^{|w|}\sum_{L\in{\mathcal{L}}_{N}[0,|w|]}\,\Big(\prod_{st\in L}-\lambda U_{st}(w)\Big)\,\Big(\prod_{s^{\prime}t^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{C}}(L)}\big(1+\lambda U_{s^{\prime}t^{\prime}}(w)\big)\,\Big),

(3.3)

(the sum is over all -not necessarily prudent- walks).
While
\Pi_{z}^{(N)}(x)
depends on
\lambda
, we suppress this in the notation.

Proposition 3.1

(Lace expansion)
.

Let
\lambda\in[0,1]
,
and
|z|<z_{c}(\lambda)
, then

G^{\lambda}_{z}(0,x)=\delta_{0,x}+z\sum\limits_{u\sim 0}G_{z}^{\lambda}(u,x)+\sum\limits_{v\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\Pi^{\lambda}_{z}(0,v)\,G_{z}^{\lambda}(v,x),

(3.4)

where
u\sim 0
means that
u
and
0
are neighbors (i.e.,
|u|=1
).

This result is well-known in the lace-expansion literature for self-avoiding walks, see
[
16
, Section 5.4]
or
[
24
, Chapter 3]
. Let us stress that the statement of the theorem is independent of the precise interpretation of
U_{st}
, and concerns only the underlying graph structure that we use to decompose the two point function
G_{z}
. The proof is thus analogous to that of
[
16
, Theorem 5.2.3]
.

3.2

In the whole section we fix
\lambda\in\big(0,1\big]
and

We recall the bubble diagram, defined in (

),
and define for all

and

the function

{\mathsf{Y}}^{\lambda}_{z,k}(x):=[1-\cos(k\cdot x)]\,G^{\lambda}_{z}(x).

(3.5)

We then have the following result which estimates
\Pi_{z}^{(N)}
(and thus
\Pi^{\lambda}_{z}
)
in terms of
{\mathsf{B}}^{\lambda}_{z}
and
{\mathsf{Y}}^{\lambda}_{z}
.

Proposition 3.2

(Diagrammatic Bounds)
.

For any
N\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\Pi_{z}^{(N)}(x)

\displaystyle\leq(dz\lambda)^{N}\,({\mathsf{B}}_{z}^{\lambda})^{N},

(3.6)

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}[1-\cos(k\cdot x)]\,\Pi_{z}^{(N)}(x)

\displaystyle\leq\,(dz\lambda)^{N}(3N-1)(2N-1)\|{\mathsf{Y}}^{\lambda}_{z,k}*\mathbf{1}_{\bot}\|_{\infty}({\mathsf{B}}^{\lambda}_{z})^{N-1}

(3.7)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad+(dz\lambda)^{N}(3N-1)N({\mathsf{B}}^{\lambda}_{z})^{N}(1-\widehat{D}(k)).

The rest of the section is dedicated to the proof of Proposition
3.2
, which is based on the representation in (
3.3
).
We first prove the statement for
N=1
, and subsequently for
N\geq 2
.
In order to lighten the notation, we suppress the dependence on
\lambda
and
z
from the notation.

Proof of (
3.6
) and (
3.7
),
case
N=1
.

We start with (
3.6
). We use (
3.3
) for
N=1
, in which case each lace consists of only a single edge from
0
to the last step of the walk
|w|>0
.
This implies that the last step of each walk
w
in (
3.3
) is such that
w(|w|)\downarrow 0
, namely

w(|w|)-w(|w|-1)\in e_{i}\mathbb{Z}
for some

In turn, this implies that the second-last step of the walk lies on one of the Cartesian axes,

w(|w|-1)\bot 0,

and differs from the origin.
If
x=(0,\ldots,x_{i},\ldots,0)
and
x_{i}\neq 0

\Pi^{(1)}(x)
in (
3.3
) we get

\displaystyle\Pi^{(1)}(x)\leq dz\lambda\,G\big(0,x+e_{i}\,\text{sign}(x_{i})\big)\mathbf{1}_{\bot}{(x+e_{i})}.

Moreover,

\displaystyle\Pi^{(1)}(0)\leq z\lambda\,\sum_{y\sim 0}\,G\big(0,y\big)=dz\lambda\,\sum\limits_{y\sim 0}\,G\big(0,y\big)\mathbf{1}_{\bot}(y),

(3.9)

Finally, if
x
does not belong to the Cartesian axes, then
\Pi^{(1)}(x)=0
. By summing over
x
in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, using the inequalities above and finally using
\delta_{0,x}\leq G(x)
, we deduce

\sum\limits_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\Pi^{(1)}_{z}(x)\leq dz\lambda\,(G*\mathbf{1}_{\bot})(0)\leq dz\lambda\,(G*G*\mathbf{1}_{\bot})(0)\leq dz\lambda\,{\mathsf{B}}_{z}^{\lambda},

thus obtaining (
3.6
) when
N=1
.

We now prove (

).
First we use the fact that

\sum\limits_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}[1-\cos(k\cdot x)]\,\Pi_{z}^{(1)}(x)=\lambda z\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\sum\limits_{x\in e_{i}\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}}G(0,x)\big(1-\cos(k\cdot(x-\text{sign}(x_{i})e_{i}))\big).

Here,

corresponds to the second-last point of the walk and

A key ingredient in bounding (

[
10

:
for

,

, and

1-\cos(k\cdot x_{n})\leq n\Big[(1-\cos(k\cdot x_{1}))+(1-\cos(k\cdot(x_{2}-x_{1}))+\\
\cdots+(1-\cos(k\cdot(x_{n}-x_{n-1}))\Big].

(3.11)

We first use (
3.11
) for

and

Inserting this bound into (

), we obtain that

\begin{split}\sum\limits_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}[1-&\cos(k\cdot x)]\,\Pi_{z}^{(1)}(x)\\
&\leq 2z\lambda\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\sum\limits_{x\in e_{i}\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}}G(0,x)(1-\cos(k\cdot x))+2z\lambda\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\sum\limits_{x\in e_{i}\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}}G(0,x)(1-\cos(k_{i}))\\
&\leq 2dz\lambda\,\|{\mathsf{Y}}_{k,z}^{\lambda}\,*\,\mathbf{1}_{\bot}\|_{\infty}\,+\,2dz\lambda\|G_{z}^{\lambda}*\mathbf{1}_{\bot}\|_{\infty}(1-\widehat{D}(k))\,,\end{split}

where in the last inequality we use the symmetry to get that

.
Since

, this concludes the proof of the case

∎

4.1

Since the indicator function

We also define, for any

Lemma 4.1

There exists two positive constants

Since

for any

and
i=1,\ldots,d
,
we fix

and we consider

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{w\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{-\frac{w^{2}}{R}}\,e^{iwt}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{w\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum\limits_{n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{(iwt)^{n}}{n!}e^{-\frac{w^{2}}{R}}=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{(it)^{2n}}{(2n)!}\sum\limits_{w\in\mathbb{Z}}w^{2n}e^{-\frac{w^{2}}{R}},

(4.4)

We note that there exist two positive constants

\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{[-\pi,\pi]^{d}}{\mathrm{d}}k\,\widehat{D}(k)^{n}\,\widehat{\mathbf{1}_{\bot}^{0}}(k)\leq O(n^{-\frac{d-1}{2}}).

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon>0
.
Using symmetry we obtain that,

\displaystyle\int_{[-\pi,\pi]^{d}}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}k}{(2\pi)^{d}}\,\widehat{D}(k)^{n}\,\widehat{\mathbf{1}_{\bot}^{0}}(k)

\displaystyle=d\lim\limits_{R\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{[-\pi,\pi]^{d}}{\mathrm{d}}k\,\frac{1}{d^{n}}\Big(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\cos(k_{i})\Big)^{n}\sqrt{R\pi}e^{-k_{d}^{2}R}

\displaystyle\leq d\int_{[-\pi,\pi]^{d-1}}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}k^{d-1}}{(2\pi)^{d-1}}\,\frac{1}{d^{n}}\Big(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d-1}\cos(k_{i})+1\Big)^{n}

\displaystyle=d\,\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n}\,\binom{n}{k}\,\frac{1}{d^{n-k}}\Big(\frac{d-1}{d}\Big)^{k}\,\int_{[-\pi,\pi]^{d-1}}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}k^{d-1}}{(2\pi)^{d-1}}\,\frac{1}{(d-1)^{k}}\Big(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d-1}\cos(k_{i})\Big)^{k}

\displaystyle\leq Cd\,\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n}\,\binom{n}{k}\,\frac{1}{d^{n-k}}\Big(\frac{d-1}{d}\Big)^{k}k^{-(d-1)/2}

\displaystyle\leq Cd\,{\Big(\frac{2}{d^{1-\varepsilon}}\Big)}^{n}\,+\,\frac{1}{(\varepsilon n)^{\frac{d-1}{2}}}.

where for the second inequality we used (2.61) of
[
13
]
,
C
is some positive constant. The lemma follows by choosing
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
appropriately.
∎

Appendix B
Moment estimates

We prove a technical result about the control of the moments:

Proposition B.1
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
2.1
, there exists constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
such that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and all
r\in(0,2)
,

C_{1}\sqrt{n}\leq\Big(\frac{1}{c_{n}}\sum_{x}|x|^{r}c_{n}(x)\Big)^{1/r}\leq C_{2}\sqrt{n}.

(B.1)

Proof.

The lower bound in (
B.1
) is straightforward: we get from (
2.21
) that
1-\widehat{c}_{n}(k/\sqrt{n})/\widehat{c}_{n}(0)\to 1-\exp{\big(-K|k|^{2}\big)}
, and this limit is strictly positive whenever
k\neq 0
. Thus for
k=(1,0,\dots,0)
, there exists
b>0
such that for all
n
,

b\leq 1-\frac{\widehat{c}_{n}(k/\sqrt{n})}{\widehat{c}_{n}(0)}=\sum_{x}\big[1-\cos(x_{1}/\sqrt{n})\big]\frac{c_{n}(x)}{c_{n}}\leq\sum_{x}\frac{|x_{1}|^{r}}{n^{r/2}}\frac{c_{n}(x)}{c_{n}}\leq\frac{1}{n^{r/2}}\sum_{x}|x|^{r}\frac{c_{n}(x)}{c_{n}}

using
1-\cos t\leq|t|^{r}
whenever
r\leq 2
.

For the upper bound in (
B.1
), we consider the generating function

H_{z,r}:=\sum_{x}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|x_{1}|^{r}c_{n}(x)z^{n}=\sum_{x}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{du}{u^{1+r}}\big[1-\cos(u\cdot x_{1})\big]c_{n}(x)z^{n},

(B.2)

determine its behaviour as
z\nearrow z_{c}
, and then employ a suitable Tauberian theorem.
We split the integral of
u
in (
B.2
) in two parts, one for
u\leq(z-z_{c})^{1/2}
and the other for
u>(z-z_{c})^{1/2}
.
We use that

•

\widehat{G}_{z}(0)=O(z-z_{c})^{-1}

•

\widehat{\Pi}_{z}(0)-\widehat{\Pi}_{z}(u)=(\frac{1}{2d}+o(1))\,(1-\widehat{D}(u))
,

which follows respectively from Lemma
5.3

and Lemma
5.2
.
Then the arguments in
[
13
, Section 3]
give that
|H_{z,r}|\leq(z_{c}-z)^{-1-r/2}
, and a Tauberian theorem (e.g.
[
11
, Theorem 5]
) consequently gives that

\sum_{x}|x_{1}|^{r}c_{n}(x)\leq n^{r/2}z_{c}^{-n},

from which the result follows using Theorem
2.2
.
∎
