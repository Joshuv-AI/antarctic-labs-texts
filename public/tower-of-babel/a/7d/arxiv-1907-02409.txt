On the continuous extension of Kobayashi isometries
Anwoy Maitra

Abstract
. We provide a sufficient condition for the continuous extension of isometries for the Kobayashi distance between bounded convex domains in complex Euclidean spaces having boundaries that are only slightly more regular than 𝒞 1 \mathcal{C}^{1} . This is a generalization of a recent result by A. Zimmer.



2010 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary: 32F45, 32H40; Secondary: 53C22

1.
Introduction

In this paper, we provide a sufficient condition for the continuous extension to
\overline{\Omega}_{1}
of isometries, with respect to the
Kobayashi distances on
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
, between a pair of bounded convex domains
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
in complex
Euclidean spaces (of not necessarily the same dimension). In this setting it is well
known that such isometries
do
exist. A consequence of fundamental work by Lempert
[
9
,
10
]
is that if
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
is a bounded convex domain, then given a pair
of distinct points
z_{1},z_{2}\in\Omega
, there exists a
holomorphic
map
F:\mathbb{D}\to\Omega
that is an isometry
with respect to the Kobayashi distances on
\mathbb{D}
and
\Omega
and such that
z_{1},z_{2}\in F(\mathbb{D})
. We
call such a map a
complex geodesic
of
\Omega
through
z_{1}
and
z_{2}
.

The question of whether a complex
geodesic extends continuously to
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
is not an easy one. The earliest result
in this direction was given by Lempert
[
9
]
, which states that if

\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
is strongly convex with
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-smooth boundary,
k\geqslant 2
, then every
complex geodesic
F:\mathbb{D}\to\Omega
extends to a
\mathcal{C}^{k-2}
-smooth mapping on
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
(by a

\mathcal{C}^{0}
-smooth mapping we mean a continuous one).
Since then, there has been a number of works dealing with the continuous (or smooth) extension of complex geodesics;
see
[
1
,
11
,
2
,
15
]
.

While Lempert’s result might suggest that the boundary regularity of the target convex domain
\Omega
controls the boundary behaviour of a
complex geodesic of
\Omega
, that is not the case — see
[
8
, Remark 1.8]
and
[
2
, Example 1.2]
. The
latter example shows that there exist
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
-smoothly bounded convex domains having complex geodesics that do not extend
continuously to
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
. In view of this, the question of
\mathcal{C}^{0}
-extension of Kobayashi isometries
in general
is
certainly a challenging one.

Before we state the main result of this paper, let us look at the motivations behind it. Our chief motivation is the following recent
result by Zimmer:

2.
Technical preliminaries

In this section we present some results that play a supporting role in the proofs of the main results in Section
4
and,
therefore, of our main theorem. The first result, by S.E. Warschawski, is the principal tool that enables us to deal with the
low regularity of
\partial\Omega_{1}
and
\partial\Omega_{2}

.

To state this result, we need to fix some terminology. Given a rectifiable arc
\Gamma
in
\mathbb{C}
, we say that

\Gamma
has a
a continuously turning tangent
if there is a
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-smooth diffeomorphism

\gamma:I\to\Gamma
, where
I
is an interval. Note, in particular, that
\gamma^{\prime}
is non-vanishing.
Given that
\Gamma
has a continuously turning tangent, a
tangent angle
at any point

\zeta\in\Gamma
refers to the smaller of the two angles determined by the intersection of

T_{\zeta}\Gamma
with a
fixed
line
\ell
in
\mathbb{C}
. While different choices of
\ell
define
different tangent-angle functions on
\Gamma
, the difference between the tangent angles — determined
by some fixed
\ell
— at two points
\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2}\in\Gamma
depends
only
on
\zeta_{1}

and
\zeta_{2}
(and, of course, on
\Gamma
), i.e., is independent of
\ell
. For this reason, in the
following result — and in all applications of it — we shall use the phrase “the tangent angle” without
any further comment. If
\Gamma
is a closed rectifiable Jordan curve in
\mathbb{C}
, analogous observations
can be made about arc length. With these words, we can now state the following:

Result 2.1

(
[
13
, Theorem 1]
)
.

Let
C
be a closed rectifiable Jordan curve in
\mathbb{C}
and let
C
have a continuously turning tangent in a

C
-open neighbourhood of a point
\zeta_{0}\in C
. Suppose that the tangent angle
\tau(s)

as a function of arc length

s
has a modulus of continuity
\omega
at the point
s_{0}
corresponding to
\zeta_{0}
— i.e.,
there exists a constant
\sigma>0
such that

|\tau(s)-\tau(s_{0})|\leqslant\omega(|s-s_{0}|)\text{ whenever }|s-s_{0}|\leqslant\sigma

(2.1)

— that satisfies the following condition:

\int_{0}^{\sigma}\frac{\omega(t)}{t}dt<\infty.

(2.2)

Let
f
be a biholomorphic map of
\mathbb{D}
onto the region
D
enclosed by
C
and let

\zeta_{0}=f(z_{0})
. Then

\lim_{\mathbb{D}\ni z\to z_{0}}\frac{f(z)-f(z_{0})}{z-z_{0}}=\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}f^{\prime}(z_{0})

exists, and

\lim_{S\ni z\to z_{0}}f^{\prime}(z)=f^{\prime}(z_{0})

for any Stolz angle
S
with vertex at
z_{0}
. Furthermore,
f^{\prime}(z_{0})\neq 0
.

We refer the reader to
[
12
, Chapter 1]
for a definition
of a Stolz angle in
\mathbb{D}
.

Remark 2.2
.

Note that, since the
\omega
appearing in the above result is a modulus of continuity,
it is a non-decreasing function on
[0,\sigma]
(and is continuous at
0
). For this reason,
the integrand in (
2.2
) is Lebesgue measurable. Secondly, in the statement
of Result
2.1
, we have tacitly used Carathéodory’s
theorem to conclude that — given that
C
is rectifiable — the map

f:\mathbb{D}\to D
in the above result extends to a homeomorphism of
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
.

The following is an immediate corollary to the above result.

Corollary 2.3
.

In the set-up described in Result
2.1
, if
g
denotes
f^{-1}
then

L\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\lim_{D\ni\zeta\to\zeta_{0}}\frac{g(\zeta)-z_{0}}{\zeta-\zeta_{0}}

(2.3)

exists and is non-zero.

We will also need the following simple lemma involving moduli of continuity.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
f
be a real-valued function defined on a ball
B^{(n)}(a,r)\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Then the modulus of continuity
\omega
of
f
on

B^{(n)}(a,r)
is sub-additive, i.e., for all
s,t\in[0,2r)
such that
s+t<2r
,
\omega(s+t)\leqslant\omega(s)+\omega(t)
.

Proof.

Suppose
x,y\in B^{(n)}(a,r)
and
\|x-y\|\leqslant s+t
. If
\|x-y\|\leqslant s
, then
|f(x)-f(y)|\leqslant\omega(s)\leqslant\ \omega(s)+\omega(t)
. Now suppose that
\|x-y\|>s
. Note that

\left|f(x)-f\left(x+s\frac{y-x}{\|y-x\|}\right)\right|\leqslant\omega(s)

and

\displaystyle\left|f\left(x+s\frac{y-x}{\|y-x\|}\right)-f(y)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|f\left(x+s\frac{y-x}{\|y-x\|}\right)-f\left(x+\|y-x\|\frac{y-x}{\|y-x\|}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\omega(\|y-x\|-s)\leqslant\omega(t).

Consequently, by the triangle inequality,
|f(y)-f(x)|\leqslant\omega(s)+\omega(t)
. Since
x
and
y
were arbitrary
points in
B^{(n)}(a,r)
satisfying
\|x-y\|\leqslant s+t
, it follows
that
\omega(s+t)\leqslant\omega(s)+\omega(t)
.
∎

3.

In this section we record some facts about convex domains in

. The first two were proved by Zimmer in
[
15
]
. All
of them are needed in the proof of Theorem
1.3

arbitrary convex domains. First, some notation: in what follows, given a domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}

\mathsf{k}_{\Omega}
will denote the
Kobayashi pseudodistance on
\Omega

\kappa_{\Omega}
will denote the Kobayashi pseudometric on

Result 3.1

(
[
15
, Lemma 4.2]
)
.

Let
\Omega\varsubsetneq\mathbb{C}^{n}
be a convex domain and
H\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
be a complex affine hyperplane such that
\Omega\cap H=\varnothing
. Then, for every

\mathsf{k}_{\Omega}(z_{1},z_{2})\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\left|\log\left(\frac{\mathrm{dist}(z_{1},H)}{\mathrm{dist}(z_{2},H)}\right)\right|.

(3.1)

The next result is a sharper lower bound for the Kobayashi distance between a pair of points in a bounded convex domain with

\mathcal{C}^{1}
-smooth boundary under an additional assumption. At this point, we wish to state a key clarification about our notation.
Whenever

(

(3.3)

The goal of this section is to prove certain technical results that are essential for extending the scope of an idea

In

Definition 4.1

(Zimmer
[

)

Let

be a bounded domain. For

, by a

-almost geodesic
in
\Omega

(1)

|s-t|-\log(K)\leqslant\mathsf{k}_{\Omega}(\sigma(s),\sigma(t))\leqslant|s-t|+\log(K)\quad\forall\,s,t\in I
, and

(2)

\mathsf{k}_{\Omega}(\sigma(s),\sigma(t))\leqslant K|s-t|\quad\forall\,s,t\in I

The Forstneric–Rosay estimate involves embedding a certain compact planar set

\overline{\mathcal{D}}
with

into
\Omega
so that its image osculates
\partial\Omega

at the image of
0
. Since the domains considered in Theorem

\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
of Theorem
1.3
.

Such a domain (which must contain

in its boundary) must have a defining function that is
\mathcal{C}^{1}
near

whose derivative (while
not necessarily
\alpha
-Hölder-continuous for any

\omega
as in Theorem
1.3
, we define the function
h:(-2r,2r)\to[0,\infty)
as
follows:

h(t)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\begin{cases}\,{\displaystyle\int_{t}^{0}}\omega(-y)dy,&\text{if }t<0,\\
{}&\\
\,{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}}\omega(y)dy,&\text{if }t\geqslant 0.\end{cases}

The following properties of
h
are easily verified:
h(0)=0
;
h
is strictly increasing on
[0,2r)
, and strictly
decreasing on
(-2r,0]
; and
h^{\prime}(0)=0
. Then, for
\alpha,\tau>0
, consider the domain

\mathcal{D}(\alpha,\tau)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\{\zeta=s+it\in\mathbb{C}\mid|t|<\tau,\ \alpha h(t)<s<\tau\}.

The following property of the domains
\mathcal{D}(\alpha,\tau)
is obvious from the definition: if
\zeta=s+it\in\mathcal{D}(\alpha,\tau)
,
then
|t|\leqslant h^{-1}(s/\alpha)
. Near
0
, a defining function for
\mathcal{D}(\alpha,\tau)
is

\varrho(s,t)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\alpha h(t)-s
. Its total derivative at the point
(s,t)
,

(D\varrho)(s,t)
, with respect to the standard basis of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, is

\big[\!-\!1\;\,\alpha h^{\prime}(t)\big].

It is easily checked that the modulus of continuity of
D\varrho
at

is
\alpha\omega
: i.e., for
every

,
\|(D\varrho)(s,t)-(D\varrho)(0)\|=\alpha\omega(|t|)
.

We will use the following fact in our proof below: if
w\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
, then
\mathbb{J}(w)
is orthogonal to
w
with respect
to the standard real inner product on
\mathbb{R}^{2n}\longleftrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{n}
. With this remark, we now state
and prove the following proposition.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
be a bounded convex domain having the properties common to

\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
as stated in Theorem
1.3
.
For

\xi\in\partial\Omega
, let
\Psi_{\xi}:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}^{n}
denote the
\mathbb{C}
-affine map

\Psi_{\xi}(\zeta)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\xi+\zeta\eta^{\xi}\quad\forall\,\zeta\in\mathbb{C}.

Then there exist constants
\alpha,\tau>0
such that, for every
\xi\in\partial\Omega
,
\Psi_{\xi}(\mathcal{D}(\alpha,\tau))\subset\Omega
.

Proof.

We are given a
\mathcal{C}^{1}
defining function
\rho
defined on a neighbourhood
U
of
\partial\Omega
and we are given an
r>0

such that, for every
\xi\in\partial\Omega
, the directional derivative
D_{\mathbb{J}\left(\eta^{\xi}\right)}\rho
has on

B^{(n)}(\xi,r)
modulus of continuity
\omega
.
We shall identify
\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{2n},\mathbb{R})
with
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
via the matrix representation of the elements of

\mathscr{L}(\mathbb{R}^{2n},\mathbb{R})
relative to the standard basis of
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
.
Since
D\rho
does not vanish on
\partial\Omega
, there is an
m>0
such that, for every
\xi\in\partial\Omega
,

\|(D\rho)(\xi)\|\geqslant m
. Furthermore, if we choose a neighbourhood
V
of
\partial\Omega
in
U
such that

\overline{V}
is a compact subset of
U
, then
D\rho
is uniformly continuous on
V
. In particular, there is a

\delta_{0}
,
0<\delta_{0}<r
, such that

\|(D\rho)(\xi)-(D\rho)(\xi^{\prime})\|\leqslant m/2\quad\forall\,\xi,\xi^{\prime}\in V\text{ such that }\|\xi-\xi^{\prime}\|\leqslant\delta_{0}.

(4.1)

Choose
\tau>0
so small that
\sqrt{2}\tau<\delta_{0}
; it then follows that

\{s+it\in\mathbb{C}\mid|t|<\tau,\ 0<s<\tau\}\subset D(0,\delta_{0}).

Then, for any
\alpha>0
,
\mathcal{D}(\alpha,\tau)\subset D(0,\delta_{0})
.
We fix a value of
\alpha\geqslant 1
so large that
2/\alpha\leqslant m/4
. We shall
soon see the reason for this choice. We may also need to shrink
\tau
further.
The precise value of
\tau
that works will be presented below.

For the rest of the proof we fix

\xi\in\partial\Omega
and
\zeta\in\mathcal{D}(\alpha,\tau)
. In what follows,
a+ib

(
a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
) will, for simplicity of notation, denote either a complex vector or the vector

(a_{1},b_{1},\dots,a_{n},b_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
— the intended meaning being clear from the context.
By Taylor’s theorem, and writing
\zeta=s+it
(and
\langle\boldsymbol{\cdot}\,,\boldsymbol{\cdot}\rangle
denoting the
usual inner product on
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
)

\displaystyle\rho(\xi+\zeta\eta^{\xi})=

\displaystyle\ \rho(\xi)+(D\rho)(\xi)(\zeta\eta^{\xi})+\int_{0}^{1}\big((D\rho)(\xi+x\zeta\eta^{\xi})-(D\rho)(\xi)\big)(\zeta\eta^{\xi})dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ s\langle\nabla\rho(\xi),\eta^{\xi}\rangle+t\langle\nabla\rho(\xi),\mathbb{J}(\eta^{\xi})\rangle+s\int_{0}^{1}\big((D\rho)(\xi+x\zeta\eta^{\xi})-(D\rho)(\xi)\big)(\eta^{\xi})dx

\displaystyle+t\int_{0}^{1}\big((D\rho)(\xi+x\zeta\eta^{\xi})-(D\rho)(\xi)\big)(\mathbb{J}(\eta^{\xi}))dx\quad\text{[since $\rho(\xi)=0$]}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\ -sm+s\int_{0}^{1}\big|((D\rho)(\xi+x\zeta\eta^{\xi})-(D\rho)(\xi))(\eta^{\xi})\big|dx

\displaystyle+|t|\int_{0}^{1}\big|\big(D_{\mathbb{J}(\eta^{\xi})}\rho\big)(\xi+x\zeta\eta^{\xi})-\big(D_{\mathbb{J}(\eta^{\xi})}\rho\big)(\xi)\big|dx\quad\text{[since $\mathbb{J}(\eta^{\xi})\perp\nabla\rho(\xi)$].}

(4.2)

Since, for every
\xi\in\partial\Omega
, every
x\in[0,1]
and every
\zeta\in\mathcal{D}(\alpha,\tau)
,

\|(\xi+x\zeta\eta^{\xi})-\xi\|=x|\zeta|<\delta_{0}
,

\|(D\rho)(\xi+x\zeta\eta^{\xi})-(D\rho)(\xi)\|\leqslant\frac{m}{2},

by (
4.1
).
Therefore the second term on the right hand side of (
4.2
) is less than or equal to
sm/2
.
As for the third term, note that for every
x\in[0,1]
,

\big|\big(D_{\mathbb{J}\left(\eta^{\xi}\right)}\rho\big)(\xi+x\zeta\eta^{\xi})-\big(D_{\mathbb{J}\left(\eta^{\xi}\right)}\rho\big)(\xi)\big|\leqslant\omega(\|(\xi+x\zeta\eta^{\xi})-\xi\|)=\omega(x|\zeta|).

So the third term on the right hand side of (
4.2
) is less than or equal to

|t|\int_{0}^{1}\omega(x|\zeta|)dx.

Therefore we get, from (
4.2
),

\rho(\xi+\zeta\eta^{\xi})\leqslant-sm+(sm/2)+|t|\int_{0}^{1}\omega(x|\zeta|)dx.

(4.3)

Since
\zeta\in\mathcal{D}(\alpha,\tau)
,

|\zeta|\leqslant\big(s^{2}+(h^{-1}(s/\alpha))^{2}\big)^{1/2}=h^{-1}(s/\alpha)\left(1+\left(\frac{s}{h^{-1}(s/\alpha)}\right)^{\!\!2}\right)^{1/2}.

(4.4)

Since
h^{\prime}(0)=0
, we have
\lim_{x\to 0+}x/h^{-1}(x)=0
. Therefore, we can
shrink
\tau
so that,

x/h^{-1}(x)\leqslant 1/\alpha\quad\forall x\in(0,\tau).

(4.5)

Now, from (
4.4
), the fact that

0<s/\alpha<\tau
(since
s/\alpha\leqslant s
by our choice of
\alpha
), and
(
4.5
), we have:

1+\left(\frac{s}{h^{-1}(s/\alpha)}\right)^{\!\!2}=1+\alpha^{2}\left(\frac{s/\alpha}{h^{-1}(s/\alpha)}\right)^{\!\!2}\leqslant 2.

From the last inequality and (
4.4
),

|\zeta|\leqslant\sqrt{2}h^{-1}(s/\alpha).

Using the above in (
4.3
) we get that

\displaystyle\rho(\xi+\zeta\eta^{\xi})

\displaystyle\leqslant-(sm/2)+|t|\int_{0}^{1}\omega\left(\sqrt{2}xh^{-1}(s/\alpha)\right)dx

\displaystyle\leqslant-(sm/2)+h^{-1}(s/\alpha)\int_{0}^{1}\omega\left(2xh^{-1}(s/\alpha)\right)dx

\displaystyle\leqslant s\left(-\frac{m}{2}+\frac{2h^{-1}(s/\alpha)}{s}\int_{0}^{1}\omega\left(xh^{-1}(s/\alpha)\right)dx\right)

\displaystyle=s\left(-\frac{m}{2}+\frac{2}{s}\int_{0}^{h^{-1}(s/\alpha)}\omega(u)du\right)\quad\text{[by change of variables]}

\displaystyle=s\left(-\frac{m}{2}+\frac{2}{\alpha}\right)\leqslant-\frac{sm}{4}<0,

by the choice of
\alpha
discussed above. We note here that the third inequality follows
from Lemma
2.4
.
Therefore,

\xi+\zeta\eta^{\xi}\in\Omega
. Since
\xi\in\partial\Omega
and
\zeta\in\mathcal{D}(\alpha,\tau)
were arbitrary, the proof is complete.
∎

The proof of Theorem

— as we shall see — relies crucially on the conclusion

, for the point

It is enough to show that the modulus of continuity of the tangent angle to

|\tan^{-1}(x)|\leqslant|x|\quad\forall\,x\in\mathbb{R}.

(4.6)

We also note that given
\alpha
and

near

is a suitably small

near

\widehat{\theta}(y)=\tan^{-1}(\alpha h^{\prime}(y))\quad\forall\,y\in[-\epsilon_{0},\epsilon_{0}].

(4.7)

(In this instance, the line
\ell
, as introduced in the explanations preceding Result

,
is the imaginary axis of
\mathbb{C}
.)
Now we present the following lemma.

Lemma 4.3
.

The tangent angle
\theta
of
\partial\mathcal{D}(\alpha,\tau)
near
0
, regarded as a function of arc length, has a modulus of continuity that
is dominated by
\alpha\omega
(and therefore satisfies a Dini condition).

Proof.

First we determine the arc length as a function of
y
. We will reckon the (signed) arc length
s
from
0
and
such that
s(x+iy)<0
for
x+iy\in\partial\mathcal{D}(\alpha,\tau)
and
y<0
, and

s(x+iy)>0
for
x+iy\in\partial\mathcal{D}(\alpha,\tau)
and
y>0

(we are only interested in the arc length near
0
). Using the parametrization
\Phi
referred to just prior to
(

), we see that the function that gives the arc length as a function of

,
which we denote by

, is

G(y)=\int_{0}^{y}\|\Phi^{\prime}(t)\|dt=\int_{0}^{y}\big[1+\alpha^{2}\omega(|t|)^{2}\big]^{1/2}dt

(4.8)

for all

.
Clearly,

|G(y)|\geqslant|y|\quad\forall\,y\in(-\epsilon_{0},\epsilon_{0}).

(4.9)

Note that
G
is a strictly increasing odd function on

.
So
G^{-1}
is a function that is defined on
(-G(\epsilon_{0}),G(\epsilon_{0}))
and is strictly increasing.
Taking

,

, in (

|G^{-1}(s)|\leqslant|s|\quad\forall\,s\in(-G(\epsilon_{0}),G(\epsilon_{0})).

(4.10)

Now the function

\theta(s)=\widehat{\theta}(G^{-1}(s))\quad\forall\,s\in(-G(\epsilon_{0}),G(\epsilon_{0})).

Recall that

, and

\displaystyle|\theta(s)|=|\widehat{\theta}(G^{-1}(s))|

\displaystyle=\left|\tan^{-1}\big(\alpha h^{\prime}(G^{-1}(s))\big)\right|

[by (

\displaystyle\leqslant\alpha|h^{\prime}(G^{-1}(s))|

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
\Omega
be an open convex subset of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
having the properties
possessed in common by
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
in the

1.3
.
Then there exist
K,\epsilon>0
such that for every
\xi\in\partial\Omega
,

\sigma_{\xi}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=t\mapsto\xi+\epsilon e^{-2t}\eta^{\xi}:[0,\infty)\to\Omega

(4.11)

is a
K
-almost-geodesic.

Proof.

Our proof will resemble, in
essence
, the proof of Proposition 4.3 in
[
15
]
.
The two proofs will differ in the key detail that we must work with the domains
\mathcal{D}(\alpha,\tau)
, which are
adapted to the domain
\Omega
under consideration.

By Proposition
4.2
there exist
\alpha,\tau>0
such that for every
\xi\in\partial\Omega
,
\xi+\mathcal{D}(\alpha,\tau)\eta^{\xi}\subset\Omega
.
As
\mathcal{D}(\alpha,\tau)
is a bounded open convex subset of
\mathbb{C}
symmetric
about the real axis, there exists a biholomorphism
g:\mathcal{D}(\alpha,\tau)\to\mathbb{D}
such that

g\big(\mathcal{D}(\alpha,\tau)\cap\mathbb{R}\big)=\mathbb{D}\cap\mathbb{R}
. By Carathéodory’s theorem,
g
extends to a
homeomorphism from

\overline{\mathcal{D}(\alpha,\tau)}
to
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
. We may suppose, without loss of generality, that
g(0)=1
.
By the remark following the proof of Lemma
4.3
, we see that we can apply
Corollary
2.3
to
g
to conclude that

\lim_{\mathcal{D}(\alpha,\tau)\ni z\to 0}\frac{g(z)-g(0)}{z}=\lim_{\mathcal{D}(\alpha,\tau)\ni z\to 0}\frac{g(z)-1}{z}

exists (call it
k
) and is non-zero. Therefore
k
is a negative real number. Thus, there exist constants

\epsilon>0
and
\kappa\geqslant 1
such that
t\in\mathcal{D}(\alpha,\tau)
whenever

0<t\leqslant\epsilon
and

0\leqslant 1-\kappa{}t\leqslant g(t)\leqslant 1-\kappa^{-1}t\quad\forall\,t:0<t\leqslant\epsilon.

(4.12)

Then for
t_{1},t_{2}
such that
0<t_{1}<t_{2}\leqslant\epsilon
, we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{k}_{\mathcal{D}(\alpha,\tau)}(t_{1},t_{2})

\displaystyle=\mathsf{k}_{\mathbb{D}}(g(t_{1}),g(t_{2}))=\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{\big(1+g(t_{1})\big)\big(1-g(t_{2})\big)}{\big(1+g(t_{2})\big)\big(1-g(t_{1})\big)}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\log 2+\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{1-g(t_{2})}{1-g(t_{1})}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\log 2+\log(\kappa)+\frac{1}{2}\log(t_{2}/t_{1}),

by (
4.12
). So for
\xi\in\partial\Omega
and

\displaystyle\mathsf{k}_{\Omega}(\sigma_{\xi}(t),\sigma_{\xi}(s))

\displaystyle=\mathsf{k}_{\Omega}\left(\Psi_{\xi}\big(\epsilon e^{-2t}\big),\Psi_{\xi}\big(\epsilon e^{-2s}\big)\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathsf{k}_{\mathcal{D}(\alpha,\tau)}(\epsilon e^{-2t},\epsilon e^{-2s})

\displaystyle\leqslant\log(\sqrt{2}\kappa)+(1/2)\log(\epsilon e^{-2t}/\epsilon e^{-2s})

\displaystyle=\log(\sqrt{2}\kappa)+(s-t),

provided
s\geqslant{}t
, where
\Psi_{\xi}
is as introduced in Proposition
4.2
. In general

\mathsf{k}_{\Omega}(\sigma_{\xi}(t),\sigma_{\xi}(s))\leqslant\log(\sqrt{2}\kappa)+|s-t|\quad\forall\,s,t\in[0,\infty).

(4.13)

Consider the complex affine hyperplane

tangent to
\partial\Omega
at
\xi
. Of course,

\xi+T^{\mathbb{C}}_{\xi}(\partial\Omega)
is a complex affine supporting hyperplane for
\Omega
at
\xi
. For

arbitrary, the distance of
\sigma_{\xi}(t)
from
\xi+T^{\mathbb{C}}_{\xi}(\partial\Omega)

\epsilon e^{-2t}
. Consequently, by

,

\mathsf{k}_{\Omega}(\sigma_{\xi}(t),\sigma_{\xi}(s))\geqslant(1/2)|\log(\epsilon e^{-2t}/\epsilon e^{-2s})|=|s-t|\quad\forall\,s,t\in[0,\infty).

(4.14)

By (
4.13
) and (
4.14
), each
\sigma_{\xi}
is a
(1,\log(\sqrt{2}\kappa))
-quasi-geodesic.
It only remains to prove the Lipschitz nature of
\sigma_{\xi}
.

By the fact that the boundary of
\Omega
is
\mathcal{C}^{1}
, we
can, by shrinking

if necessary, ensure that for every

,
where

Elementary two-dimensional geometry then shows that there is a

would work.)
Therefore,
by Graham’s estimate — i.e., Result

— for
every
\xi\in\partial\Omega
and every
t\in[0,\infty)
,

\kappa_{\Omega}(\sigma_{\xi}(t),\sigma^{\prime}_{\xi}(t))\leqslant\frac{\|\sigma^{\prime}_{\xi}(t)\|}{r_{\Omega}(\sigma_{\xi}(t),\sigma^{\prime}_{\xi}(t))}\leqslant\frac{2\epsilon e^{-2t}}{C\epsilon e^{-2t}}=\frac{2}{C}.

Consequently, for every
\xi\in\partial\Omega
and every
s,t\in[0,\infty)
,

\mathsf{k}_{\Omega}(\sigma_{\xi}(s),\sigma_{\xi}(t))\leqslant(2/C)|s-t|.

(4.15)

Therefore, by (
4.13
), (
4.14
) and (
4.15
), it follows that for
every
\xi\in\partial\Omega
,
\sigma_{\xi}
is a
K
-almost-geodesic, where
K\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\max\{\sqrt{2}\kappa,2/C\}
.
∎

An outcome of one half of our argument for Proposition
4.5
is the following

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
be a bounded convex domain having the properties common
to
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
as in the statement of Theorem
1.3
. Let
z_{0}\in\Omega
. Then, there
exists a constant
C>0
such that

\mathsf{k}_{\Omega}(z_{0},z)\leqslant C+\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{1}{\mathrm{dist}(z,\Omega^{\mathsf{c}})}\right)\quad\forall z\in\Omega.

Remark 4.7
.

Since the domain
\Omega
in the statement of Proposition
1.5
is bounded, whence

\partial\Omega
is compact, it is easy to see that Proposition
1.5
is a special case of the above.

Proof.

We abbreviate
\mathrm{dist}(z,\Omega^{\mathsf{c}})
to
\delta_{\Omega}(z)
. From the argument leading up to
(
4.13
) in the proof of Proposition
4.5
, we conclude
that there exist constants
\epsilon>0
and
K\geqslant 1
such that for every
\xi\in\partial\Omega
, and

\sigma_{\xi}
as in that proposition,

\mathsf{k}_{\Omega}(\sigma_{\xi}(t),\sigma_{\xi}(s))\leqslant|s-t|+\log K\quad\forall s,t\in[0,\infty).

(4.16)

By compactness, there exists a
\delta\in(0,\epsilon)
such that

\mathrm{dist}(\{\sigma_{\xi}(0)\mid\xi\in\partial\Omega\},\Omega^{\mathsf{c}})\geqslant\delta
.
It suffices to show that there exists
C>0
such that
for every
z\in\Omega
with
\delta_{\Omega}(z)<\delta
,
\mathsf{k}_{\Omega}(z_{0},z)\leqslant C+2^{-1}\log(1/\delta_{\Omega}(z))
.
Let
C^{\prime}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\sup_{\xi\in\partial\Omega}\mathsf{k}_{\Omega}(z_{0},\sigma_{\xi}(0))
. So let

z\in\Omega
with
\delta_{\Omega}(z)<\delta
. Now fix a
\xi\in\partial\Omega
such that
\|z-\xi\|=\delta_{\Omega}(z)
. Clearly,
then, there exists
t(z)\in(0,\infty)
such that
z=\sigma_{\xi}(t(z))
. So
\mathsf{k}_{\Omega}(z_{0},z)\leqslant\mathsf{k}_{\Omega}(z_{0},\sigma_{\xi}(0))+\mathsf{k}_{\Omega}\big(\sigma_{\xi}(0),\sigma_{\xi}(t(z))\big)\leqslant C^{\prime}+t(z)+\log K
, by (
4.16
). Since, by definition of

\sigma_{\xi}
,
\delta_{\Omega}(z)=\epsilon e^{-2t(z)}
, a
simple calculation shows that
t(z)\leqslant 2^{-1}\log(1/\delta_{\Omega}(z))
. Therefore
\mathsf{k}_{\Omega}(z_{0},z)\leqslant C+2^{-1}\log(1/\delta_{\Omega}(z))
, where
C\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=C^{\prime}+\log K
. This gives the desired conclusion.
∎

5.
The proof of Theorem
1.3

The proof of Theorem
1.3
requires the following conclusions: if
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
is a domain that has
the properties possessed in common by
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
in the statement of Theorem
1.3
then
(we remind the reader that for
\xi\in\partial\Omega
, the set
(\xi+T^{\mathbb{C}}_{\xi}(\partial\Omega))
is as described in
Section
3
):

(1)

If
\xi\in\partial\Omega
and
(p_{\nu})_{\nu\geqslant 1}
,
(q_{\mu})_{\mu\geqslant 1}
are sequences in
\Omega
converging to

\xi
, then

\lim_{\nu,\mu\to\infty}(p_{\nu}|q_{\mu})_{o}=\infty.

(2)

If
\xi,\xi^{\prime}\in\partial\Omega
and
(p_{\nu})_{\nu\geqslant 1},(q_{\mu})_{\mu\geqslant 1}
are sequences in
\Omega
converging to

\xi
and
\xi^{\prime}
respectively such that

\limsup_{\nu,\mu\to\infty}\;(p_{\nu}|q_{\mu})_{o}=\infty,

then
\xi+T^{\mathbb{C}}_{\xi}(\partial\Omega)=\xi^{\prime}+T^{\mathbb{C}}_{\xi^{\prime}}(\partial\Omega)
.

In the above,
(\cdot\mid\cdot)_{o}
denotes the Gromov product relative to the Kobayashi distance on
\Omega
and with respect to an
arbitrary but fixed base point
o\in\Omega
. It is defined as

(x|y)_{o}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\frac{1}{2}\big(\mathsf{k}_{\Omega}(x,o)+\mathsf{k}_{\Omega}(y,o)-\mathsf{k}_{\Omega}(x,y)\big).

The above conclusions have been demonstrated by Zimmer under the conditions he states in
[
15
, Theorem 4.1]
. We
observe that what has actually been established in
[
15
, Theorem 4.1]
is the following:

Proposition 5.1
.

Suppose
\Omega
is a bounded open convex subset of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
having
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-smooth boundary. Suppose
\Omega
possesses the property that
there exist constants
\epsilon>0,K\geqslant 1
such that, for each
\xi\in\partial\Omega
, the path

\sigma_{\xi}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=t\mapsto\xi+\epsilon e^{-2t}\eta^{\xi}:[0,\infty)\to\Omega

is a
K
-almost-geodesic. Then:

(1)

If
\xi\in\partial\Omega
and
(p_{\nu})_{\nu\geqslant 1}
,
(q_{\mu})_{\mu\geqslant 1}
are
sequences in
\Omega
converging to
\xi
, then

\lim_{\nu,\mu\to\infty}(p_{\nu}|q_{\mu})_{o}=\infty.

(2)

If
\xi,\xi^{\prime}\in\partial\Omega
and
(p_{\nu})_{\nu\geqslant 1},(q_{\mu})_{\mu\geqslant 1}
are
sequences in
\Omega
converging to
\xi
and
\xi^{\prime}
respectively such that

\limsup_{\nu,\mu\to\infty}\;(p_{\nu}|q_{\mu})_{o}=\infty,

then
\xi+T^{\mathbb{C}}_{\xi}(\partial\Omega)=\xi^{\prime}+T^{\mathbb{C}}_{\xi^{\prime}}(\partial\Omega)
.

The condition on
\partial\Omega
in
[
15
, Theorem 4.1]
was required to obtain the property concerning the paths
\{\sigma_{\xi}\mid\xi\in\partial\Omega\}
stated in Proposition
5.1
. Other than this, there is absolutely no
difference between the proofs of
[
15
, Theorem 4.1]
and Proposition
5.1
. We therefore omit
the proof of the latter.

Finally, we give the proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Proof.

First, we show that whenever
\xi\in\partial\Omega_{1}
,
\lim_{z\to\xi}F(z)
exists. Since
F
is an isometry with respect to the Kobayashi
distances, we see, from the definition of the Gromov product above, that, for every
z,w,o\in\Omega_{1}
,

(z|w)_{o}=(F(z)|F(w))_{F(o)}.

First note that if
\xi\in\partial\Omega_{1}
and
(z_{\nu})_{\nu\geqslant 1}
is a sequence in
\Omega_{1}
converging to
\xi
such that

(F(z_{\nu}))_{\nu\geqslant 1}
converges to some point
\zeta\in\overline{\Omega}_{2}
, then
\zeta\in\partial\Omega_{2}
. The reason is that, if
we fix a point
o\in\Omega_{1}
arbitrarily, we see that

\lim_{\nu\to\infty}\mathsf{k}_{\Omega_{2}}(F(z_{\nu}),F(o))=\lim_{\nu\to\infty}\mathsf{k}_{\Omega_{1}}(z_{\nu},o)=\infty,

by Lemma
3.1
. Consequently,
\zeta=\lim_{\nu\to\infty}F(z_{\nu})
must belong to
\partial\Omega_{2}
. Thus, if
\xi\in\partial\Omega_{1}
and
(z_{\nu})_{\nu\geqslant 1},(w_{\nu})_{\nu\geqslant 1}
are sequences in
\Omega_{1}
converging to
\xi

such that
(F(z_{\nu}))_{\nu\geqslant 1}
and
(F(w_{\nu}))_{\nu\geqslant 1}
converge to
\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2}\in\overline{\Omega}_{2}
,
respectively, then
\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2}\in\partial\Omega_{2}
. Moreover,

\lim_{\nu\to\infty}(F(z_{\nu})|F(w_{\nu}))_{F(o)}=\lim_{\nu\to\infty}(z_{\nu}|w_{\nu})_{o}=\infty,

by (
1
) of Proposition
5.1
above. Consequently, by (
2
) of the
same proposition,
\zeta_{1}+T^{\mathbb{C}}_{\zeta_{1}}(\partial\Omega_{2})=\zeta_{2}+T^{\mathbb{C}}_{\zeta_{2}}(\partial\Omega_{2})
. Therefore, since
\Omega_{2}
is

\mathbb{C}
-strictly convex, one has
\zeta_{1}=\zeta_{2}
. Since
\Omega_{2}
is bounded, so that any sequence in it has a convergent subsequence, the
above shows that
\lim_{z\to\xi}F(z)
exists.

Then define
\widetilde{F}:\overline{\Omega}_{1}\to\overline{\Omega}_{2}
by letting
\widetilde{F}
equal
F
on
\Omega_{1}
and by letting

\widetilde{F}(\xi)
, for
\xi\in\partial\Omega_{1}
, be
\lim_{z\to\xi}F(z)
. It is routine to show that
\widetilde{F}
is continuous, in
view of the conclusions of the previous paragraph. This completes the proof. ∎
