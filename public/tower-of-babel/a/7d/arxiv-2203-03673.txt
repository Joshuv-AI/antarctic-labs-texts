AgraSSt: Approximate Graph Stein Statistics for Interpretable Assessment of Implicit Graph Generators
Wenkai Xu, Gesine Reinert

Abstract
We propose and analyse a novel statistical procedure, coined AgraSSt , to assess the quality of graph generators which may not be available in explicit forms. In particular, AgraSSt can be used to determine whether a learned graph generating process is capable of generating graphs which resemble a given input graph. Inspired by Stein operators for random graphs, the key idea of AgraSSt is the construction of a kernel discrepancy based on an operator obtained from the graph generator. AgraSSt can provide interpretable criticisms for a graph generator training procedure and help identify reliable sample batches for downstream tasks. We give theoretical guarantees for a broad class of random graph models. We provide empirical results on both synthetic input graphs with known graph generation procedures, and real-world input graphs that the state-of-the-art (deep) generative models for graphs are trained on.



Keywords.
Synthetic graph generators, goodness-of-fit testing, model criticism, Stein’s method, kernel Stein discrepancies

Introduction

Generative models for graphs have received increasing attention in the statistics and machine learning communities.
Recently, deep neural networks have been utilised to learn rich representations from graph structures and generate graphs

(
Dai et al. 2020
;
Li et al. 2018
;
Liao et al. 2019
;
You et al. 2018
)
.
However, due to the often opaque deep learning procedures, these deep generative models
are usually implicit, which hinders theoretical analysis to assess how
close the generated samples are in their distributional properties to the graph distribution they are meant to be sampled from.

Learning parametric models requires an explicit pre-specified probability distribution class, and
the learned parameters can be used for model assessment. However, parametric models may only capture a fraction of the graph features and have restricted modelling power.
Due to dependency between edges, parameter estimation can be inconsistent
(
Shalizi and Rinaldo 2013
)
even for well-specified models, and may lead to wrong conclusions in model assessments.
Although recent advances in deep generative models for graphs may surpass some of the above-mentioned issues by learning rich graph representations, methods to assess the quality of such implicit graph generators are lacking.
In principle, nonparametric
hypothesis tests can be useful
to assess complex models, such as by the popular kernel-based tests procedures
that utilise functions in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS)
(
Berlinet and Thomas 2004
)
.
When the models are described in the form of explicit probabilities, goodness-of-fit tests
(
Chwialkowski et al. 2016
;
Liu et al. 2016
)
may apply; however, goodness-of-fit testing procedures are generally not applicable for implicit models. Instead, if a large set of samples from the target distribution is observed, one may generate samples from the implicit model and perform a two-sample test such as a maximum mean discrepancy (MMD) test
(
Gretton et al. 2007
)
for model assessment

(
Jitkrittum et al. 2017
;
Xu and Matsuda 2020
;
Xu and Matsuda 2021
)
.

Background: graph kernel Stein statistics

In
(
Xu and Reinert 2021
)
a goodness-of-fit testing procedure called gKSS for exponential random graph models is introduced, which assumes that only a single network may be observed in the sample. As AgraSSt is inspired by gKSS, and gKSS serves as comparison method when the observed network is known to be generated from an exponential random graph model (ERGM), we briefly review gKSS. The notation introduced in this section is used throughout the paper.

The class of
ERGMs is a rich model class which includes
Bernoulli random graphs
and
which is extensively used for social network analysis
(
Wasserman and Faust 1994
;
Holland and Leinhardt 1981
;
Frank and Strauss 1986
)
.
ERGMs model random graphs via a Gibbs measure with respect to a chosen set of network statistics, such as number of edges, 2-stars, and triangles.
Denote by
\mathcal{G}^{lab}_{n}
, the set of vertex-labeled graphs on
n
vertices, with

N=n(n-1)/2
possible undirected edges.
Encode
x\in\mathcal{G}^{lab}_{n}
by an ordered collection of
\{0,1\}
-valued variables
x=(x^{(ij)})_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\in\{0,1\}^{N}
where
x^{(ij)}=1
if and only if
there is an edge between
i
and
j
. We denote an (ordered) vertex-pair index
s=(i,j)
by
s\in[N]:=\{1,\ldots,N\}
.
For a collection of graphs
H_{1},\ldots,H_{k}
on at most
n
vertices, with
H_{1}
denoting a single edge, let
t_{\ell}
denote the number of counts of
H_{\ell}
in the observed subgraph (possibly scaled; for details including a precise definition,
Definition

A.5
, see SI
A.2
),
For
\beta=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{k})^{\top}{\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}

and

t(x)=(t_{1}(x),\dots,t_{k}(x))^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}

we say that

follows the exponential random graph model

if for
\forall x\in\mathcal{G}^{lab}_{n}

q(X=x)=\frac{1}{\kappa_{n}(\beta)}\exp{\left(\sum_{l=1}^{k}\beta_{l}t_{l}(x)\right)}.

(1)

Here

is a normalisation constant. In this model,

Parameter estimation
\hat{\beta}_{l}
for

is

possible when the graphs

are specified a priori; see SI.

In modern graph learning procedures, e.g. deep generative learning, the

of Eq.(

In
(
Reinert and Ross 2019
)
the exponential random graph distribution in Eq.

is characterised by a so-called

, as follows. Let

e_{s}\in\{0,1\}^{N}
be a vector with

in coordinate

and 0 in all others;

x^{(s,1)}=x+(1-x_{s})e_{s}
has the
s

by the value 1, and

has the
s
-entry of
x
replaced by the value 0; moreover,

{x}_{-s}
is the set of edge indicators with entry
s
removed. For a function

h:\{0,1\}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, let

\Delta_{s}h(x)=h(x^{(s,1)})-h(x^{(s,0)}).
Set

q_{X}(x^{(s,1)}|{x_{-s}})=\mathbb{P}(X^{s}=1|{X_{-s}=x_{-s}}).
Define the operator

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\beta,{t}}f(x)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{s\in[N]}\mathcal{A}^{(s)}_{q}f(x),\quad\mathcal{A}^{(s)}_{q}f(x)=q(x^{(s,1)}|{x_{-s}})\Delta_{s}f(x)+\left(f(x^{(s,0)})-f(x)\right).

(2)

Then under mild conditions
Reinert and Ross 2019
show that if

\mathbb{E}_{p}[{\mathcal{A}}_{\beta,t}f]=0
for all smooth test functions
f
, then
p
must be the distribution of ERGM
(\beta,t)
. Thus, this operator characterises ERGM
(\beta,t)
.
For the derivation of AgraSSt it is of interest to see how this operator is obtained. It is indeed the generator of a so-called
Glauber
Markov chain on
\mathcal{G}^{lab}_{n}
with transition probabilities

\mathbb{P}(x\rightarrow x^{(s,1)})=N^{-1}-\mathbb{P}(x\rightarrow x^{(s,0)})=N^{-1}q(x^{(s,1)}|x_{-s}).

Further, as

\Delta_{s}t_{\ell}(x)=t_{\ell}(x^{(s,1)})-t_{\ell}(x^{(s,0)}),

cancelling out common factors,

\displaystyle q(x^{(s,1)}|{x_{-s}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left\{\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\beta_{\ell}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Delta_{s}t(x)}\right\}\times\left(\exp\left\{\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\beta_{\ell}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Delta_{s}t(x)}\right\}+1\right)^{-1}

(3)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle q(x^{{(}s,1{)}}|{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Delta_{s}t(x)})

depends only on
\Delta_{s}t(x)
.
With the ERGM Stein operator in Eq.(
2
) and a rich-enough RKHS test function class

\mathcal{H}
,
Xu and Reinert 2021
propose a graph kernel Stein statistics (gKSS) to perform goodness-of-fit testing on an

ERGM when a single network sample is observed.
With the summand components in Eq.(
2
), the Stein operator
can be seen as taking expectation over vertex-pair variables
S\in[N]
with uniform probability
\mathbb{P}(S=s)\equiv N^{-1}

independently of
x

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{q}f(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\in[N]}\mathbb{P}(S=s)\mathcal{A}^{(s)}_{q}f(x)=:{\mathbb{E}_{S}[\mathcal{A}^{(S)}_{q}f(x)]}.

For a fixed graph
x
,
gKSS is defined as

\displaystyle\operatorname{gKSS}(q;x)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq 1}\Big|\mathbb{E}_{S}[\mathcal{A}^{(S)}_{q}f(x)]\Big|,

(4)

where the function

is chosen to best distinguish
q
from
x

For an RKHS
\mathcal{H}
associated with kernel
K

by the reproducing property of

the squared version of gKSS admits a quadratic form representation

\operatorname{gKSS}^{2}(q;x)=\Big\langle\mathbb{E}_{S}[\mathcal{A}^{(S)}_{q}K(x,\cdot)],\mathbb{E}_{S}[\mathcal{A}^{(S)}_{q}K(x,\cdot)]\Big\rangle,

which can be computed readily.
More background can be found in
Section

3
AgraSSt: Approximate Graph Stein Statistic

An implicit graph generator may not admit a probability distribution in the form of Eq.(
1
); however, the idea of constructing Stein operators based on Glauber dynamics using conditional probability distribution for ERGM is
inspiring.
Here we propose Stein operators for conditional graph distributions, to facilitate an (approximate) characterisation
for implicit random graph models.

3.1
Stein operators for conditional graph distributions

Let

be any distribution with support

Let

(6)

Approximate Stein operators

The

\mathcal{A}_{\widehat{q},t}f(x)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{s\in[N]}\mathcal{A}_{\widehat{q},t}^{(s)}f(x).

AgraSSt for implicit graph generators

The estimated conditional probabilities give an approximate Stein operator for Eq.(

With the appropriately defined Stein operator from an implicit model given in Eq.(

a kernel-based statistic analogous to gKSS in Eq.(

\operatorname{AgraSSt}(\widehat{q},t;x)=\sup_{\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq 1}\Big|N^{-1}\sum_{s}\mathcal{A}^{(s)}_{\widehat{q},t}f(x)\Big|.

In SI.
A
we prove the following result.

Theorem 3.3
.

If the graph is edge-exchangeable, then
as
L\rightarrow\infty
,
{{\rm AgraSSt}}^{2}(\widehat{q},t;x)

is a consistent estimator of

{\rm{gKSS}}^{2}(q;x)=N^{-2}\sum_{s,s^{\prime}\in[N]}\left\langle\mathcal{A}^{(s)}_{q}K(x,\cdot),\mathcal{A}^{(s^{\prime})}_{q}K(\cdot,x)\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}.

(10)

Re-sampling Stein statistic

A computationally efficient operator for large
N

can be derived via re-sampling
vertex-pairs
s
,
which creates a randomised operator.
Let
B
be the fixed size
to be re-sampled. The re-sampled

{\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{\widehat{q},t}^{B}}f(x)=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b\in[B]}\mathcal{A}^{(s_{b})}_{\widehat{q},t}f(x),

where

are vertex-pair samples from

The expectation of

\mathbb{E}_{B}[{\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{\widehat{q},t}^{B}}f(x)]{=\mathbb{E}_{S}[\mathcal{A}_{\widehat{q},t}^{(S)}f(x)]}=\mathcal{A}_{\widehat{q},t}f(x).

The corresponding re-sampled AgraSSt is

\widehat{\operatorname{AgraSSt}}(\widehat{q},t;x)=\sup_{\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq 1}\Big|\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b\in[B]}\mathcal{A}^{(s_{b})}_{\widehat{q},t}f(x)\Big|.

admits a representation in a quadratic form,

\displaystyle\widehat{\operatorname{AgraSSt}}^{2}({\widehat{q},t};x)=B^{-2}\sum_{b,b^{\prime}\in[B]}\widehat{h}_{x}(s_{b},s_{b^{\prime}}),

(11)

where

\widehat{h}_{x}(s,s^{\prime})=\left\langle\mathcal{A}^{(s)}_{\widehat{q},t}K(x,\cdot),\mathcal{A}^{(s^{\prime})}_{\widehat{q},t}K(\cdot,x)\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}.

4
Applications of AgraSSt

4.1
Assessing graph generators

AgraSSt measures the distributional difference between the underlying distribution of an implicit graph generator
G
and an observed graph
x
, which is useful to assess the quality of the generator
G
.
The
hypothesis testing
procedure for the null hypothesis that the observed graph
x
comes from the same distribution that generates the samples, against the general alternative, is shown in Algorithm
2
.
We emphasise two features of this procedure.
Firstly, for a given generator
G
, AgraSSt directly assesses the quality of the implicit model represented via samples from
G
. Secondly, the generator

G
can be trained on the observed graph
x
, for example through a deep neural network generator.
By learning a deep neural network generator with training samples from the same distribution that generate
x
, AgraSSt can assess the quality of the training procedure, i.e. whether the deep neural network is capable of learning the desired distributions. Additional details are discussed in SI.
C
.

4.2
Interpreting trained graph generators

If the procedure in Algorithm
2
rejects the null hypothesis, the generator may not be suitable
for generating samples from the distribution that generates the one observed graph.
Hence, understanding where the misfit comes from can be very useful, especially for models trained from black-box deep neural networks.
AgraSSt
provides an interpretable model criticism by comparing the learned
\widehat{q}_{t}(x^{s,1})
with the underlying
q_{t}(x^{s,1})
when available, such as in synthetic experiments from a specified ERGM. Such an interpretation can be also useful to re-calibrate training procedures.

4.3
Identifying reliable graph samples

If the procedure in Algorithm
2
does not reject the null hypothesis, there is
not enough evidence
to reject the hypothesis that
the generator is capable of generating graphs that resembles the observed graph.
If a generator
G
has passed this hurdle then it can be recommended for generating graph samples of the desired type. AgraSSt can also be put to use for the task of sample batch selection.
In real scientific studies, only a small batch of
representative graph samples may need to be generated for downstream tasks such as
privacy-preserving methods
where users only access a small number of graph
data, or a randomised experimental design for community interaction.
To quantify the quality of sample batches
via

6
Discussions and future directions

In this paper, we propose AgraSSt, a unique general purpose model assessment and criticism procedure for implicit random graph models. As it is based on a kernel Stein statistic, we are able to give theoretical guarantees.
AgraSSt not only solves an important problem but also
opens up a whole set of follow-up
research problems of which we list a few here.
(i).
Currently AgraSSt is only applied to undirected and unweighted graphs. Extensions to more general graphs as well as to time series of graphs will be interesting to explore in follow-up work.
(ii).
AgraSSt could be also helpful to improve design and training of deep graph generative models, e.g. by regularising graph features if there is a misfit.
(iii).
ERGMs allow for exogenous features to be included in the sufficient statistics.
AgraSSt can be based on a variety of statistics
t(x)
; further examples are found in

Section

D.3
.
It would also be possible
to incorporate exogenous features in the
statistics
t(x)
in AgraSSt, for example using ideas from graph attention networks

(
Veličković et al. 2018
)
. Exploring this idea in more detail will be another topic of further research.

As AgraSSt depends on the chosen summary statistic
t(x)
, results have to be interpreted with regards to the respective conditional distributions. Also multiple tests will have the
p
-values to be adjusted to avoid misinterpretation of tests, which could have serious consequences for example in the area of personal health.

i_{H,x}({e})={\{}i_{H,x}(u),i_{H,x}(v){\}}
and
E(i_{H,x})=\cup_{{e}\in E(H)}\{i_{H,x}({e})\}
. Then the number of edge-preserving injections of
{I}(H,x)
is

t(H,x)=\sum_{i_{H,x}\in{I}(H,x)}\prod_{e\in E(H)}{\mathbbm{1}}\bigl(i_{H,x}(e)\in E(x)\bigr).

Let
x/s:=x\setminus\{s\}
denote the collection of edge indicators except
x_{s}
. This is a collection using the same vertex set as
s
,
and for
s\in E({x})
,

\displaystyle\Delta_{s}t(H,x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i_{H,x}\in{I}(H,x)}\left\{\prod_{e\in E(H)}{\mathbbm{1}}\bigl(i_{H,x}(e)\in E(x/s^{s,1})\bigr)-\prod_{e\in H}{\mathbbm{1}}\bigl(i_{H,x}(e)\in E(x/s^{s,0})\bigr)\right\}

(13)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i_{H,x}\in I(H,x)}{\mathbbm{1}}\bigl(s\in E(i_{H,x})\bigr)\prod_{e\in E(H)\setminus{{i_{H,x}^{-1}(s)}}}{\mathbbm{1}}\Bigl(i_{H,x}(e)\in\bigl(E(x)\setminus i_{H,x}(s)\bigr)\Bigr).

We define

\Delta_{u}t(H,x/s)=\sum_{i_{H,x}\in I(H,x)}{\mathbbm{1}}\bigl(u\in E(i_{H,x})\bigr)\prod_{e\in E(H)\setminus{{i_{H,x}^{-1}(s)}}}{\mathbbm{1}}\Bigl(i_{H,x}(e)\in\bigl(E(x)\setminus\{s,i_{H,x}(u)\}\bigr)\Bigr).

Then
\mathbb{1}(Z_{s}=1)
is independent of
\Delta_{u}t(H,Z/s)
.
Moreover,

\displaystyle R(\underline{k},X)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{s\in[N]}(Z_{s}-{a^{*}})\left\{\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{s}t(Z)=\underline{k})\frac{1}{1+\sum_{u:u\neq s}\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{u}t(Z/s)=\underline{k})}\right\}

\displaystyle+\sum_{s\in[N]}(Z_{s}-{a^{*}})\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{s}t(Z)=\underline{k})\left(\frac{1}{1+\sum_{u:u\neq s}\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{u}t(Z)=\underline{k})}-\frac{1}{1+\sum_{u:u\neq s}\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{u}t(Z/s)=\underline{k})}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{s\in[N]}(Z_{s}-{a^{*}})\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{s}t(Z)=\underline{k})\left(\frac{1}{1+\sum_{u:u\neq s}\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{u}t(Z)=\underline{k})}-\frac{1}{1+\sum_{u:u\neq s}\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{u}t(Z/s)=\underline{k})}\right)

\displaystyle+\sum_{s\in[N]}(Z_{s}-{a^{*}})\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{s}t(Z)=\underline{k})\left(\frac{1}{1+\sum_{u:u\neq s}\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{u}t(Z)=\underline{k})}-\frac{1}{1+\sum_{u:u\neq s}\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{u}t(Z/s)=\underline{k})}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{s\in[N]}(Z_{s}-{a^{*}})\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{s}t(Z)=\underline{k})\left(\frac{\sum_{u:u\neq s}\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{u}t(Z/s)=\underline{k})-\sum_{u:u\neq s}\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{u}t(Z)=\underline{k})}{(1+\sum_{w:w\neq s}\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{w}t(Z)=\underline{k}))(1+\sum_{w:w\neq s}\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{w}t(Z/s)=\underline{k})}\right).

Using the product formula

\prod_{j=0}^{n}a_{j}-\prod_{j=0}^{n}b_{j}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left\{\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}a_{j}\right\}(a_{k}-b_{k})\left\{\prod_{j=k+1}^{n}b_{j}\right\},

with
\underline{k}=(k_{1},\ldots,k_{\ell}),

\displaystyle\sum_{u:u\neq s}\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{u}t(Z/s)=\underline{k})-\sum_{u:u\neq s}\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{u}t(Z)=\underline{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{u:u\neq s}\left\{\prod_{\ell=1}^{L}\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{u}t_{\ell}(Z/s)=k_{\ell})-\prod_{\ell=1}^{L}\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{u}t_{\ell}(Z)=k_{\ell})\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{u:u\neq s}\left\{\sum_{d=1}^{L}\prod_{\ell=1}^{d-1}\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{u}t_{\ell}(Z/s)=k_{\ell})(\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{u}t_{d}(Z/s)=k_{d})-\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{u}t_{d}(Z)=k_{d}))\right.

\displaystyle\left.\prod_{\ell=d+1}^{L}\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{u}t_{\ell}(Z)=k_{\ell})\right\}.

By construction,
H_{1}
is the single edge, and
k_{1}=1
is the only possible value. Hence

\displaystyle\sum_{u:u\neq s}\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{u}t(Z/s)=\underline{k})-\sum_{u:u\neq s}\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{u}t(Z)=\underline{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{u:u\neq s}\left\{\sum_{d=2}^{L}\prod_{\ell=1}^{d-1}\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{u}t_{\ell}(Z/s)=k_{\ell})(\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{u}t_{d}(Z/s)=k_{d})-\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{u}t_{d}(Z)=k_{d}))\right.

\displaystyle\left.\prod_{\ell=d+1}^{L}\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{u}t_{\ell}(Z)=k_{\ell})\right\}.

For convergence, it thus suffices to consider
\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{u}t_{\ell}(Z/s)=k_{\ell})(\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{u}t_{d}(Z/s)=k_{d})-\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{u}t_{d}(Z)=k_{d})
for single graphs
H_{d}
with at least 3 vertices.

We first treat the case that there is an
\ell\geq 2
such that
k_{\ell}=0
. Then
H_{\ell}
has at least 3 vertices and at least 2 edges.
As there are of order
n^{v(H_{e}ll)-2}\geq n

possible realisations of
H_{\ell}
in
Z
which contain the edge
s
, we have

\mathbb{P}(\Delta_{s}t_{\ell}(Z)=0)\leq(1-{a^{*}})^{ne(H_{\ell})}.

This quantity tends to 0 exponentially fast. A similar argument holds for
k_{\ell}=1
. Thus, the values of
k_{\ell}
which we consider are such that the unscaled counts grow with
n
.

The next step is a normal approximation for
\Delta_{s}t(Z),
using the notion of a dissociated sum and the result from
Temčinas et al. 2021
. That normal approximation then gives a bound on the probability to be in
kn(n-1)\cdots(n-v(H_{\ell})+3)\pm 1/2
.

A normal approximation

Here we derive a normal approximation for
W=\sum_{i\in I(H,x,s)}Y(i)
with

Y(i)=\frac{X(i)-p_{H}}{\sigma}

where

\sigma^{2}=Var\left(\sum_{i\in I(H,x,s)}X(i)\right).

Then
Y(i)
has mean zero, and

VarY(i)=\frac{p_{H}(1-p_{H})}{\sigma^{2}}.

To assess
\sigma^{2}
we introduce the dependency neighbourhood of
Y(i)
as the set

D_{s}(i)=\{j=j(H,x)\in I(H,x,s):\{E(x)\setminus i_{H,x}(s)\}\cap\{E(x)\setminus j_{H,x}(s)\}\neq\emptyset\}.

Then
W-\sum_{u\in D_{s}(i)}Y(u)
is independent of
Y(i)
. Moreover, for
j\in D_{s}(i)
, the quantity

W-\sum_{u\in D_{s}(i)}Y(u)-\sum_{u\in D_{s}(j)\setminus D_{s}(i)}Y(u)
is independent of
(Y(i),Y(j))
; we have a dissociated decomposition in the sense of
Temčinas et al. 2021
.
Now, as observed in Remark 1.9 of
Reinert and Ross 2019
,
for each injection there are
O(n^{v(H)-3})
injections which share at least one edge, and hence

\sigma^{2}=O(n^{2v(H)-5}).

Employing Theorem 3.3 in
Temčinas et al. 2021
gives the following result.

Lemma A.3
.

Let
Z
be a standard normal variable. Then

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}|\mathbb{P}(\sum_{i\in I(H,x,s)}X(i)\leq x)-\mathbb{P}(Z\leq(x-|{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}I}|p(H))/\sigma)|=O(n^{-\frac{1}{2}}).

Proof of Lemma
A.3
.

Adapting Theorem 3.3 in
Temčinas et al. 2021
to the univariate case gives that
for any centered dissociated sum
W\in\mathbb{R}
with variance
\sigma^{2}>0
and finite third absolute moments we have

\sup_{x}|\mathbb{P}(W\leq x)-\mathbb{P}(\sigma^{\frac{1}{2}}Z\leq x)|\leq 2^{\frac{7}{2}}3^{-\frac{3}{4}}d^{\frac{3}{16}}B^{\frac{1}{4}},

where

B=B_{1}+B_{2}

with

\displaystyle B_{1}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{3}\sum_{s\in{I}}\sum_{t,u\in D(s)}\hskip-3.99994pt\left(\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}|{Y_{s}Y_{t}Y_{u}}|+\mathbb{E}|{Y_{s}Y_{t}}|\mathbb{E}|{Y_{u}}|\right)

\displaystyle B_{2}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{3}\sum_{s\in I}\sum_{t\in D(s)}\sum_{v\in D(t)\setminus D(s)}\hskip-3.99994pt\left(\mathbb{E}|{Y_{s}Y_{t}Y_{v}}|+\mathbb{E}|{Y_{s}Y_{t}}|\mathbb{E}|{Y_{v}}|\right).

As
E(x)\setminus i_{H,x}(s)
has
e(H)-1
elements, we can bound the size of
D_{s}(i)
. To share a potential edge, it may suffice to share one vertex, which could then be connected to
S
.
Thus,

|D_{i}(s)|\leq C(H)n^{v(H)-3}
, where
C(H)
is a constant which relates to the number of automorphisms of
H
.

Now using our bounds on
|D_{i}(s)|
and
\sigma^{2}
and noting that
v(H)\geq 3
, we obtain that

B=O(n^{v(H)-2}(n^{v(H)-3})^{2}(n^{-(v(H)-2.5)})^{3}=O(n^{-\frac{1}{2}}).

Hence we have shown that
W
is asymptotically standard normal.
\hfill\Box

From the standard normal approximation Lemma
A.3
we obtain that

\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i\in I(H,x,s)}X(i)\leq x\right)=\mathbb{P}(W\leq(x-|I|p(H))/\sigma)

so that for
k=|I|p(H)+a\sigma

\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i\in I(H,x,s)}X(i)\leq k\right)=\mathbb{P}(W\leq a).

Hence, for all
\epsilon>0

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i\in I(H,x,s)}X(i)=k\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i\in I(H,x,s)}X(i)\in\left(k-\epsilon,k+\epsilon\right)\right)

\displaystyle\approx\Phi\left(\frac{k-|{I}|p(H)+\epsilon}{\sigma}\right)-\Phi\left(\frac{k-|{I}|p(H)-\epsilon}{\sigma}\right)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\epsilon}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\frac{a^{2}}{2}}

for
k=|I|p(H)+a\sigma
and
with
\Phi
denoting the standard normal c.d.f.
As we can make
\epsilon
as small as desired, this probability tends to 0;
it goes to zero exponentially fast unless
k=|I|p(H)+a\sigma
for a fixed real number
a
.
Thus the only contribution is the approximation error and we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i\in I(H,x,s)}X(i)=k\right)=\mathbb{P}(\Delta_{s}t(H,Z)=k)=O(\sigma^{-1}).

Different asymptotic regimes

The case
Np(H,k)\rightarrow 0.

Consider the case of counts of graphs
H
with
p(H,k)=\mathbb{P}(\Delta_{s}t(Z)=k)\rightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty.
For such
H
, for all
\epsilon>0,
by Markov’s inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\sum_{s\in[N]}\frac{(Z_{s}-{a^{*}})\mathbb{I}(\Delta_{s}t(Z)=k)}{1+\sum_{u\neq s}\mathbb{I}(\Delta_{u}t(Z)=k)}\right|>\epsilon\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\sum_{s\in[N]}(Z_{s}-{a^{*}})\mathbb{I}(\Delta_{s}t(Z)=k)\right|>\epsilon\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}\left|\sum_{s\in[N]}(Z_{s}-{a^{*}})\mathbb{I}(\Delta_{s}t(Z)=k)\right|}{\epsilon}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{Np(H,k)}{\epsilon}\rightarrow 0

as
n\rightarrow\infty.
Thus, for such
H
, asymptotic consistency follows.

In particular if
H
has
v(H)\geq 5
vertices and if
p(H,k)=\Theta(\sigma^{-1})
then as
\sigma=\Theta(n^{v-\frac{5}{2}})

we have
Np(H,k)=\Theta(n^{9/2-v(H)})\rightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty.
Thus, for such
H
, asymptotic consistency follows.

Moreover, as subgraphs are required to be connected, any subgraph on at least 5 vertices, once
s
is fixed, still has at least 3 free vertices. Subgraph counts can be expressed as functions of degrees, as we will exploit below. The degrees are binomially distributed and hence their probability mass is at most of the order
n^{-\frac{1}{2}}
. Having at least 3 free vertices involves at least 3 such binomial distributions, which are only weakly dependent through the weak dependence in the degrees –
\deg(i)
and
\deg(j)
depend on each other only through
Z_{i,j}
. Thus for graphs on at least 5 vertices we have
p(H,k)=n^{-\frac{3}{2}}
and we are in the regime that
Np(H,k)\rightarrow 0
.

The case
Np(H,k)=\Theta(n^{\frac{3}{2}}).
In this case we write

\frac{\sum_{s\in[N]}(Z_{s}-{a^{*}})\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{s}t=\underline{k})}{\sum_{u\in[N]}\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{u}t=\underline{k})}=\frac{\frac{1}{Np(H,k)}\sum_{s\in[N]}(Z_{s}-{a^{*}})\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{s}t=\underline{k})}{\frac{1}{Np(H,k)}\sum_{u\in[N]}\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{u}t=\underline{k})}.

First, by independence,

\frac{1}{Np(H,k)}\mathbb{E}\sum_{s\in[N]}(Z_{s}-{a^{*}})\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{s}t=\underline{k})=0,\quad\frac{1}{Np(H,k)}\mathbb{E}\sum_{u\in[N]}\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{u}t=\underline{k})=1.

For the variance of the nominator,

\displaystyle Var\left(\frac{1}{Np(H,k)}\sum_{s\in[N]}(Z_{s}-{a^{*}})\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{s}t=k)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{Np(H,k)(1-p(H,k)}{N^{2}p(H,k)^{2}}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{N^{2}p(H,k)^{2}}\sum_{s\in[N]}\sum_{u\neq s}\mathbb{E}(Z_{s}-{a^{*}})(Z_{u}-{a^{*}})\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{s}t=k)\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{u}t=k).

For the second summand, let
Z_{-s}
denote the collection of indicators
Z
with
Z_{s}
left out. Then by independence,

\displaystyle\sum_{s\in[N]}\sum_{u\neq s}\mathbb{E}(Z_{s}-{a^{*}})(Z_{u}-{a^{*}})\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{s}t=k)\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{u}t=k)

(14)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{s\in[N]}\sum_{u\neq s}\mathbb{E}(Z_{s}-{a^{*}})(Z_{u}-{a^{*}})\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{s}t=k)\left\{\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{u}t=k)-\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{u}t(Z_{-s})=k)\right\}.

Next we use Slutsky’s Theorem. Starting with (
14
),
the counts
\Delta_{u}t(Z)
and
\Delta_{u}t(Z_{-s})
differ only if there is at least one occurrence of
H
which includes both
s
and
u
. On the event that
\Delta_{s}t=k
, there are at most
(e(H)-1)k
edges which together with
s
create a copy of
H
. Hence,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N^{2}p(H,k)^{2}}\left|\sum_{s\in[N]}\sum_{u\neq s}\mathbb{E}(Z_{s}-{a^{*}})(Z_{u}-{a^{*}})\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{s}t=k)\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{u}t=k)\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N^{2}p(H,k)^{2}}\sum_{s\in[N]}\sum_{u\neq s}\mathbb{E}\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{s}t=k)\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{u}t=k)\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{u}t(Z)\neq\Delta_{u}t(Z_{-s}))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{N(e(H)-1)kp(H,k)}{N^{2}p(H,k)^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(e(H)-1)k}{Np(H,k)}.

Using the normal approximation,
k=O(n)
, and the assumption
Np(H,k)=\Theta(n^{3/2})
,

Var\left(\frac{1}{Np(H,k)}\sum_{s\in[N]}(Z_{s}-{a^{*}})\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{s}t=k)\right)=O(n^{-\frac{1}{2}}).

From Chebychev’s inequality it follows that

\frac{1}{Np(H,k)}\sum_{s\in[N]}(Z_{s}-{a^{*}})\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{s}t=k)\rightarrow 0

in probability.

For the denominator we calculate the different cases for
H
one by one.

The triangle case with
v=3
.
If
H
is a triangle then
\Delta_{s}t(H)
follows a binomial distribution with parameters
n-2
and
({a^{*}})^{2}.
From (
15
) we have that the mode of the distribution has
p(H,k)=\Theta(n^{-\frac{1}{2}})
so that indeed
Np(H,k)=\Theta(n^{\frac{3}{2}})
.
Moreover,
\Delta_{u}t
and
\Delta_{s}t
are independent if
u
and
s
do not share a vertex.
If they share a vertex then if
s=(i,j)
and
u=i,j^{\prime}
, with
\Delta_{u\setminus s}t
denoting the count of
H
including edge
u
with edge
s
excluded. There is at most one triangle which includes both
s
and
u
if edges
s
and
u
are present, and this triangle is present with conditional probability
p
; excluding this triangle, the counts of
u
-triangles and
s
-triangles are independent as they do not share an edge. Hence

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{s}t=k)\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{u}t=k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p\mathbb{P}(\Delta_{s\setminus u}t=k-1)\mathbb{P}(\Delta_{u\setminus s}t=k-1)

\displaystyle+(1-p)\mathbb{P}(\Delta_{s\setminus u}t=k)\mathbb{P}(\Delta_{u\setminus s}t=k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p\mathbb{P}(\Delta_{s\setminus u}t(H,Z)=(k-1)n)\mathbb{P}(\Delta_{u\setminus s}t(H,Z)=(k-1)n)

\displaystyle+(1-p)\mathbb{P}(\Delta_{s\setminus u}t(H,Z)=kn)\mathbb{P}(\Delta_{u\setminus s}t(H,Z)=kn).

From
Balázs and Tóth
we know that

\mathbb{P}(Bin(m,p)=k)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi mp(1-p)}}e^{-\frac{(k-mp)^{2}}{2mp(1-p)}}+O(m^{-1})

(15)

and hence
that for any
k
the probability that a Binomial
(n,p)
distribution takes on the value
0<nk<1
is

p(nk)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}e^{-\frac{n(k-p)^{2}}{2p(1-p)}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi p(1-p)}}(1+o(1)).

This gives that

\mathbb{E}\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{s}t=k)\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{u}t=k)=O(n^{-1})
, of the same order as
p(H,k)^{2}.

Concluding,

\displaystyle Var\left(\frac{1}{Np(H,k)}\sum_{s\in[N]}\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{s}t=\underline{k})\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N^{2}p(H,k)^{2}}\left(Np(H,k)+N(n-2)O(n^{-1})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O\left(\frac{1}{Np(H,k)^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O\left(\frac{n}{N}\right)=O(n^{-1}).

Hence, using Chebychev’s inequality,

\frac{1}{Np(H,k)}\sum_{s\in[N]}\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{s}t=\underline{k})\rightarrow 1

in probability.
This is the last ingredient to show that our estimator is consistent for triangles.

The 2-star case.
For
H
being a 2-star we can employ a similar argument; for any fixed vertex pair
u
, the number
\Delta_{u}t(H,Z)
of 2-stars which would involve
u=(i,j)

is the sum
\deg_{-j}(i)+\deg_{-i}(j)
. Here
\deg_{-j}(i)
is the number of neighbours of
i
excluding the potential neighbour
j
.
By independence,
\deg_{-j}(i)+\deg_{-i}(j)=\sum_{a\neq i,j}Z_{i,a}+\sum_{b\neq i,j}Z_{j,b}
has the Binomial distribution with parameters
2(n-2)
and
{a^{*}}.
Hence for
k
close to the mean,
p(H,k)=\Theta(n^{-\frac{1}{2}})
by (
15
). Moreover, for
s=(i,j)
and
u=(i^{\prime},j)
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\Delta_{u}t=k,\Delta_{s}t=k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\deg_{-j}(i)+\deg_{-i}(j)=k,\deg_{-j^{\prime}}(i)+\deg_{-i}(j^{\prime})=k)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\Delta_{u}t=k)

and the contribution to the variance is less or equal to

\frac{2(n-2)N}{N^{2}p(H,k)^{2}}p(H,k)=O(n^{-\frac{1}{2}}).

For
s\neq u
,
\Delta_{s}
and
\Delta_{u}
are only weakly dependent through their degrees. Again we use Slutsky’s Theorem.
If
s
and
u
share a vertex then we can bound the covariance again as
O(n^{-1})
and obtain an overall contribution to the variance of
O(n^{-1})
as in the triangle case. If
s=(i,j)
and
u=(i^{\prime}.j^{\prime})
do not share a vertex, then
\deg_{-j}(i)=\mathbb{1}(i\sim i^{\prime})+\mathbb{1}(i\sim j^{\prime})+\sum_{a\neq i.j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}Z_{a,i}

where
\sum_{a\neq i.j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}Z_{a,i}
is Binomial
(n-4,p)
and independent of
\deg_{-i}(j),\deg_{-i^{\prime}}(j),
and
\deg_{-j^{\prime}}(i^{\prime}).
Conditioning on the different indicators gives a contribution to the covariance of order
n^{-1}
. However due to the global dependence, a finer argument is needed.

Expanding gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\Delta_{u}t(Z)=k,\Delta_{s}t(Z)=k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\deg_{-j}(i)+\deg_{-i}(j)=nk,\deg_{-j^{\prime}}(i^{\prime})+\deg_{-i}(j^{\prime})=nk)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{1}(i\sim i^{\prime})+\mathbb{1}(i\sim j^{\prime})+\sum_{a\neq i.j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}Z_{a,i}+\mathbb{1}(j\sim i^{\prime})+\mathbb{1}(j\sim j^{\prime})+\sum_{a\neq i.j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}Z_{a,j}=nk,

\displaystyle\mathbb{1}(i\sim i^{\prime})+\mathbb{1}(i^{\prime}\sim j^{\prime})+\sum_{a\neq i.j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}Z_{a,i^{\prime}}+\mathbb{1}(j^{\prime}\sim i^{\prime})+\mathbb{1}(j\sim j^{\prime})+\sum_{a\neq i.j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}Z_{a,j^{\prime}}=nk)

We condition on the different outcomes.

First, if
\mathbb{1}(i\sim i^{\prime})=\mathbb{1}(i^{\prime}\sim j^{\prime})=\mathbb{1}(i\sim j)=\mathbb{1}(i^{\prime}\sim j^{\prime})=1,

\displaystyle{({a^{*}})^{4}\mathbb{P}(\sum_{a\neq i.j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}Z_{a,i}+\sum_{b\neq i.j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}Z_{b,j}=nk-4,\sum_{a\neq i.j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}Z_{a,i^{\prime}}+\sum_{b\neq i.j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}Z_{a,j^{\prime}}=k-4)}

\displaystyle=({a^{*}})^{4}\mathbb{P}(Bin(2n-8,{a^{*}})=nk-4)^{2}.

Here we used the independence of the indicators. We compare this to

\mathbb{P}(Bin(2n-4,{a^{*}})^{2}=nk).
With (
15
) and
m=2n-4,p={a^{*}},

\displaystyle|e^{-\frac{(k-mp)^{2}}{2mp(1-p)}}-e^{-\frac{(k+\epsilon-(m+\delta)p)^{2}}{2(m+\delta)p(1-p)}}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2p(1-p)}\left|\frac{(k+\epsilon-(m+\delta)p)^{2}}{(m+\delta)^{2}}-\frac{(k-mp)^{2}}{m^{2}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2p(1-p)}\frac{|\epsilon m-k\delta}{m(m+\delta)}\left(\frac{k+\epsilon-(m+\delta)p}{m+\delta}+\frac{k-mp}{m}\right).

Here for

, and

, with

and so this expression is

. We can thus bound all probabilities by the same normal p.d.f., and the contribution of each individual term to the covariance is
0+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2(2n-4){a^{*}}(1-{a^{*}})}}O(n^{-1})=O(n^{-3/2}).

The overall contribution to the covariance is
O(\frac{1}{p(H,k)^{2}}O(n^{-3/2})=O(n^{-\frac{1}{2}}).
Using Chebychev’s inequality yields consistency of

The case that

.
In this case,

With (

\displaystyle\sum_{s\in[N]}\sum_{u\neq s}\mathbb{E}(Z_{s}-{a^{*}})(Z_{u}-{a^{*}})\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{s}t=k)\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{u}t=k)

\displaystyle\sum_{s\in[N]}\sum_{u\neq s}\mathbb{E}(Z_{s}-{a^{*}})(Z_{u}-{a^{*}})\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{s}t=k)\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{u}t\neq\Delta_{u}t(Z_{-s}))

\displaystyle\left\{\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{u}t=k)-\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{u}t(Z_{-s})=k)\right\}.

\displaystyle{\sum_{s\in[N]}\sum_{u\neq s,|u\cap s|=1}\mathbb{E}(Z_{s}-{a^{*}})(Z_{u}-{a^{*}})\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{s}t=k)\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{u}t=k)}

\displaystyle Nnp(H,k)

\frac{Nnp(H,k)}{N^{2}p(H,k)^{2}}=\frac{1}{np(H,k)}.

If

If

If

Similarly, for the denominator of our estimator,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{s\in[N]}\sum_{u\neq s,|u\cap s|=1}Cov(\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{s}t=k),\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{u}t=k))

\displaystyle+\sum_{s\in[N]}\sum_{u\neq s,|u\cap s|=0}Cov(\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{s}t=k),\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{u}t=k)).

We can bound again

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{s\in[N]}\sum_{u\neq s,|u\cap s|=1}Cov(\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{s}t=k),\mathbb{1}(\Delta_{u}t=k))\right|\leq 2Nnp(H,k)

The 3-star case.

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left({{\deg_{-j}(i)}\choose 2}+{{\deg_{-i}(j)}\choose 2}=kn(n-1)\right)

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=0}^{\sqrt{k}n}\mathbb{P}\left({{\deg_{-j}(i)}\choose 2}=\ell\right)\mathbb{P}\left({{\deg_{-i}(j)}\choose 2}=kn(n-1)-\ell\right).

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left({\deg_{-j}(i)}=\frac{1}{2}+\sqrt{2(kn(n-1)-\ell+\frac{1}{4}}\right)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi(n-2){a^{*}}(1-{a^{*}})}}e^{-\frac{(\frac{1}{2}+\sqrt{2(kn(n-1)-\ell+\frac{1}{4}}-(n-2){a^{*}})^{2}}{2(n-2){a^{*}}(1-{a^{*}})}}+O(n^{-1})

The case of a line on 4 vertices.

Appendix B
Additional background

In this section, we present additional background to
complement the discussions in the main text.

B.1
Parameter estimation for random graphs

Estimating parameters for parametric models is possible
only
when the parametric family is
explicitly specified
. For instance, in the synthetic example for E2ST model shown in Section
5.1
,

\hat{\beta}_{l}
can be estimated for
\beta_{l}
since the edge, 2Star and triangle statistics are specified. There are various approaches for parameter estimation.

Maximum likelihood

Maximum likelihood is
a popular approach for parameter estimation in random graph models. A complication arises because its probability mass function from
Eq.(
1
),

q(X=x)=\frac{1}{\kappa_{n}(\beta)}\exp{\left(\sum_{l=1}^{k}\beta_{l}t_{l}(x)\right)}.

involves a normalisation constant
\kappa_{n}(\beta)=\sum_{x}\exp\{\sum_{l=1}^{k}\beta_{l}t_{l}(x)\}
which is generally intractable and needs to be estimated for performing MLE. For this task, Markov chain Monte-Carlo maximum likelihood estimation (MCMCMLE) for ERGM has been developed by
Snijders 2002
.
When the network size is large,
accurate estimation for the normalised
\kappa_{n}(\beta)
requires large amount of Monte-Carlo samples and is hence computationally expensive.

Maximum pseudo-likelihood estimator

To alleviate the problem associated with the normalising constant,
Maximum Pseudo-likelihood Estimation (MPLE)
[
Besag 1975
]
has been developed for ERGMs, see
Strauss and Ikeda 1990
and also
Schmid and Desmarais 2017
. MPLE factorises
the conditional edge probability to approximate the exact likelihood,

q(x)=\Pi_{s\in[N]}q(x^{s}|x_{-s}).

(19)

For ERGMs
the conditional distribution
q(x^{s}|x_{-s})
does not involve the normalising constant and can hence be computed more efficiently than the MLE.
However, in general the MPLS is not consistent for ERGMs
as the edges are generally non-independent. The consistency of MPLE for Boltzmann machines is shown in
Hyvärinen 2006
.
A thorough comparison of MCMCMLE and MPLE estimation in ERGMs can be found in
Van Duijn et al. 2009
.

Contrastive divergence

Estimation based on contrastive divergence (CD)
[
Hinton 2002
]
has also been developed for ERGM estimation
[
Hunter and Handcock 2006
]
. Contrastive divergence runs a small number of Markov chains simultaneously for
T
steps and estimates the gradient based on the differences between initial values and values after
T
steps in order to find a maximum. Convergence results
for exponential family models are shown in
Jiang et al. 2018
. CD can provide a useful balance between computationally expensive but accurate MCMCMLE and fast but inconsistent MPLE.

B.2
Kernel Stein discrepancies and kernel-based nonparametric hypothesis testing

The task of hypothesis testing involves the comparison of distributions
p
and
q
that are significantly different with respect to the size of the test, denoted by
\alpha
.
In nonparametric tests, the
distributions
are not assumed to be in any parametric families and test statistics are often based on ranking of observations.
In contrast, parametric tests, such as a Student t-test or a normality test, assume a pre-defined parametric family to be tested against and usually employ a
particular summary statistics such as means or standard deviations.
Recent advances in nonparametric test procedures introduce RKHS functions which can be rich enough to distinguish distributions whenever they differ. Below we detail two instances which are relevant for the main paper.

We start with a terse review of
kernel Stein discrepancy (KSD) for continuous distributions

developed to compare and test distributions
[
Gorham and Mackey 2015
,
Ley et al. 2017
]
.
Let
q
be a smooth probability density on

\mathbb{R}^{d}
that vanishes at the boundary. The operator
\mathcal{A}_{q}:(\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d})\to(\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R})
is called a
Stein operator
if the following
Stein identity
holds:
\mathbb{E}_{q}[{\mathcal{A}}_{q}f]=0
, where
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is any bounded smooth function.
A suitable
function class
{\mathcal{F}}
is such that if

\mathbb{E}_{p}[{\mathcal{A}}_{q}f]=0
for all functions
f{\in{\mathcal{F}}}
, then
p=q
follows.
It is convenient to take

{\mathcal{F}}=B_{1}({\mathcal{H}})
, the
unit ball of a large enough RKHS with bounded kernel
K
.
The kernel Stein discrepancy (KSD) between two densities
p
and
q
based on
\mathcal{A}_{q}
is defined as

\operatorname{KSD}(p\|q,{{\mathcal{H}}})=\sup_{f\in B_{1}(\mathcal{H})}\mathbb{E}_{p}[{\mathcal{A}}_{q}f].

(20)

Under mild regularity conditions, for a particular choice of
\mathcal{A}
called Langevin operator,
\mathrm{KSD}(p\|q,{{\mathcal{H}}})\geq 0
and
\mathrm{KSD}(p\|q,{{\mathcal{H}}})=0
if and only if
p=q

[
Chwialkowski et al. 2016
]
, in which case
KSD
is a
proper
discrepancy measure between probability densities.

The KSD in Eq.(
20
) can be used to test the model goodness-of-fit as follows.
One can show that

\operatorname{KSD}^{2}(p\|q,{{\mathcal{H}}})={\mathbb{E}}_{x,\tilde{x}\sim p}[h_{q}(x,\tilde{x})]
,
where
x
and
\tilde{x}
are independent random variables with density
p
and
h_{q}(x,\tilde{x})
is given in explicit form which does not involve
p
,

\displaystyle h_{q}(x,\tilde{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathcal{A}_{q}K(x,\cdot),\mathcal{A}_{q}K(\cdot,\tilde{x})\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}.

(21)

Given a set of samples
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
from an unknown density
p
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, to test whether

p=q
, the statistic

\mathrm{KSD}^{2}(p\|q,{{\mathcal{H}}})
can be empirically estimated by independent samples from
p
using a
U
- or
V
-statistic.
The critical value is determined by bootstrap based on
weighted chi-square approximations for

U
- or
V
-statistics.
For goodness-of-fit tests of discrete distributions when i.i.d. samples are available,
a kernel discrete Stein discrepancy (KDSD) has been proposed in
Yang et al. 2018
.

Goodness-of-fit Testing
aims to check the null hypothesis
\mathfrak{H}_{0}:p=q
against the general alternative
\mathfrak{H}_{1}:p\neq q
when the target distribution
q
is explicitly specified.
Given sample(s) from the
unknown
distribution
p
and an explicit density
q
,
\mathfrak{H}_{0}
is assessed using a chosen test statistic, usually a discrepancy measure,
D(q\|p)
, between
p
and
q
, which can be estimated empirically.
Kernel-based hypothesis tests on goodness-of-fit for continuous distributions
q
use the kernel Stein discrepancy (KSD) in Section
B.2
as the test statistic.
Given samples
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
from the
unknown
density
p
,
\operatorname{KSD}^{2}(p\|q,\mathcal{H})
in Eq.(
20
) is estimated via the
V
-statistic

\operatorname{\widehat{KSD}}^{2}(p\|q,{{\mathcal{H}}})=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,j}h_{q}(x_{i},{x_{j}});

recall that
h_{q}(x_{i},{x_{j}})=\left\langle\mathcal{A}_{q}K(x_{i},\cdot),\mathcal{A}_{q}K({x_{j}},\cdot)\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
from Eq.(
21
).
The null distribution of this test statistic involves integral operators that are not available in close form; often it is simulated using a
wild-bootstrap procedure
[
Chwialkowski et al. 2014
]
. With the (simulated) null distribution, the critical value of the test can be estimated
to decide whether the null hypothesis is rejected at test level
\alpha
.
In this way, a general method
for nonparametric testing of goodness-of-fit
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is obtained, which is applicable even for models with an intractable normalising constant.

Two-sample Testing
aims to determine whether two sets of samples are drawn from the same distribution, i.e. instead of

q
being available in density form as in the goodness-of-fit setting,
q
is only accessible through samples.
Maximum mean embedding (MMD) test are often used for this two-sample problem
[
Gretton et al. 2007
]
. These tests are
based on the kernel mean embedding of a distribution,

\mu_{p}:=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p}[k(x,\cdot)]=\int_{\mathcal{X}}k(x,\cdot)dp(x)\in\mathcal{H},

(22)

whenever
\mu_{p}
exist. Similar to KSD, MMD takes the supremum over unit ball RKHS functions;

\operatorname{MMD}(p\|q)=\sup_{f\in B_{1}(\mathcal{H})}\big|\mathbb{E}_{p}[f]-\mathbb{E}_{q}[f]\big|=\|\mu_{p}-\mu_{Q}\|_{\mathcal{H}}.

(23)

With samples
x_{1},\dots x_{m}\sim p
and
y_{1},\dots,y_{n}\sim q
, MMD can be estimated empirically via
U
-statistics,

\displaystyle{\widehat{{\operatorname{MMD}^{2}_{u}}}}(p\|q)=\frac{1}{\small m(m-1)}\sum_{i\neq i^{\prime}}k(x_{i},x_{i^{\prime}})+\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{j\neq j^{\prime}}k(y_{j},y_{j^{\prime}})-\frac{2}{mn}\sum_{ij}k(x_{i},y_{j}).

(24)

In such kernel-based two-sample tests, the null distribution can be obtained via a permutation procedure
[
Gretton et al. 2007
]
;
this procedure
can be more robust compared to a wild-bootstrap procedure, especially when the kernels need to be optimised
[
Gretton et al. 2012
,
Jitkrittum et al. 2016
,
Liu et al. 2020
,
Liu et al. 2021
]
.

The two-sample procedure can also be applied to
verify model assumptions when the model is not directly accessible through its distribution but through generated samples. Such a strategy has been considered as benchmark testing procedure in various studies for goodness-of-fit tests

[
Jitkrittum et al. 2017
,
Xu and Matsuda 2020
,
Xu and Matsuda 2021
]
. Despite lower test power compared to the corresponding state-of-the-art KSD-based tests and higher computational cost due to additional empirical estimation for the distribution
q
, the MMD-based tests are competitive with a simpler derivation in complicated testing scenarios
[
Xu and Matsuda 2020
,
Xu and Matsuda 2021
]
, and they can outperform non-kernel based goodness-of-fit tests as discussed in
Xu and Matsuda 2020
.
