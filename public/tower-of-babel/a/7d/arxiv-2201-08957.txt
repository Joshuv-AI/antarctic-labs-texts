Can we recover an integral quadratic form by representing all its subforms? Thanks: This work of the second author was supported by the National Research Foundation of Korea (NRF-2019R1A2C1086347) and (NRF-2020R1A5A1016126).
Wai Kiu Chan, Byeong-Kweon Oh

Abstract
. Let 𝔬 \mathfrak{o} be the ring of integers of a number field. If f f is a quadratic form over 𝔬 \mathfrak{o} and g g is another quadratic form over 𝔬 \mathfrak{o} which represents all proper subforms of f f , does g g represent f f ? We show that if g g is indefinite, then g g indeed represents f f . However, when f f is positive definite and indecomposable, then there exists a g g which represents all proper subforms of f f but not f f itself. Along the way we give a new characterization of positive definite decomposable quadratic forms over 𝔬 \mathfrak{o} and a number-field generalization of the finiteness theorem of representations of quadratic forms by quadratic forms over ℤ {\mathbb{Z}} [ 13 , Theorem 3.3] which asserts that given any infinite set 𝒮 \mathscr{S} of classes of positive definite integral quadratic forms over 𝔬 \mathfrak{o} of a fixed rank, there exists a finite subset 𝒮 0 \mathscr{S}_{0} of 𝒮 \mathscr{S} with the property that a positive definite quadratic form over 𝔬 \mathfrak{o} represents all classes in 𝒮 \mathscr{S} if and only if it represents all classes in 𝒮 0 \mathscr{S}_{0} .

11E12, 11E25

1.
Introduction

Let
f
be an integral quadratic form. A quadratic form
f^{\prime}
is said to be represented by
f
, or
f^{\prime}
is a subform of
f
, if it is obtained from
f
by a linear change of variables over the integers. If the change of variables is invertible over the integers, then
f
and
f^{\prime}
are said to be equivalent. A proper subform of
f
is a subform of
f
which is not equivalent to
f
. Along with their investigation of the minimal universality criterion sets for positive definite integral quadratic forms, the authors of
[
14
]
consider the following natural question:

(*)
Let
f
be a positive definite integral quadratic form. If a positive definite quadratic form
g
represents all the proper subforms of
f
, does
g
represent
f
?

At first sight this question should have an affirmative answer since a lot of the proper subforms of
f
are almost equal to
f
. However, in
[
14
, Section 4]
it was shown that if
f
is a positive definite indecomposable quadratic form in
\leq 4
variables, then there is always a positive definite quadratic form
g
which represents all proper subforms of
f
but not
f
itself. This suggests that indecomposability plays a key role to the answer of the question
(*)
.

In this paper, we consider
(*)
from a broader perspective by allowing the quadratic forms to be indefinite. Our results show a great contrast between the definite and the indefinite cases. In the indefinite case, we show that if
g
is an indefinite quadratic form which represents all the proper subforms of
f
, then
g
must represent
f
(Theorem
3.2
). However, in the definite case, we prove that for any positive definite indecomposable quadratic form
f
, regardless of the number of variables, there must be a positive definite quadratic which represents all the proper subforms of
f
but not
f
itself (Theorem
4.5
). In the terminology of
[
14
, Definition 3.1]
, this means that a positive definite indecomposable quadratic form is not
recoverable
.

Our algebraic and arithmetic approach is flexible enough to let us expand the scope of the discussion to include quadratic forms over the ring of integers of a number field. Furthermore, it will be more convenient to adopt the geometric language of quadratic spaces and lattices. We will recall in the next section some of the notations and terminologies necessary in our discussion. The readers can find a thorough treatment of quadratic spaces and lattices and any unexplained terminologies in
[
17
]
.

For the indefinite case, the main tool is the powerful theory of spinor exceptions. The interested readers are referred to
[
9
]
and
[
20
]
for a more recent account of this important theory concerning representations of indefinite lattices. As for the definite case, there are two main ingredients both of which could be of independent interest to the readers. The first one is the idea of a
nonsplit volume-minimizing flag
in a positive definite indecomposable lattice (Definition
4.1
). This leads to a new characterization of positive definite decomposable
\mathfrak{o}
-lattices (Theorem
4.4
). The second one is the number-field generalization of the finiteness theorem of representation of quadratic forms by quadratic forms in
[
13
, Theorem 3.3]
(Theorem
5.7
). An interesting consequence is the existence of a finite universality criterion set for positive definite quadratic forms over a totally real number field.

2.
Preliminaries

Let
\mathfrak{o}
be a Dedekind domain of characteristic 0, and
F
be its field of fractions. A quadratic space over
F
is a finite dimensional vector space over
F
which is equipped with a quadratic map. For the sake of convenience, the quadratic map on any quadratic space will be denoted by
Q
. Associated to
Q
is the bilinear map
B
which satisfies the identity
2B(\bm{x},\bm{y})=Q(\bm{x}+\bm{y})-Q(\bm{x})-Q(\bm{y})
for all vectors
\bm{x}
and
\bm{y}
in the underlying quadratic space. If
\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{\ell}
are vectors in a quadratic space, the Gram matrix with respect to the ordered set
\{\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{\ell}\}
is the
\ell\times\ell
matrix
(B(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}_{j}))
.

An
\mathfrak{o}
-lattice is a finitely generated
\mathfrak{o}
-module
L
in a quadratic space
V
over
F
. If
FL=V
, we say that
L
is an
\mathfrak{o}
-lattice
on

V
. The
dual
of
L
, denoted
L^{\#}
, is the set
\{\bm{x}\in FL:B(\bm{x},\bm{v})\in\mathfrak{o}\mbox{ for all }\bm{v}\in L\}
which is also an
\mathfrak{o}
-lattice on
FL
. By the
scale

\mathfrak{s}(L)
of
L
we mean the
\mathfrak{o}
-module generated by the subset
B(L,L)
of
F
, and the
norm

\mathfrak{n}(L)
of
L
is the
\mathfrak{o}
-module generated by the subset
Q(L)
of
F
. By their definitions, we see that
\mathfrak{s}(L)\supseteq\mathfrak{n}(L)\supseteq 2\mathfrak{s}(L)
.

We say that
L
is an integral
\mathfrak{o}
-lattice if
\mathfrak{s}(L)\subseteq\mathfrak{o}
. Let
\mathfrak{a}
be a fractional ideal of
\mathfrak{o}
. Then,
L
is called an
\mathfrak{a}
-maximal
\mathfrak{o}
-lattice on
V
if for every
\mathfrak{o}
-lattice
K
on
V
such that
L\subseteq K
and
\mathfrak{n}(K)\subseteq\mathfrak{a}
, then
L=K
.

When
L
and
K
are
\mathfrak{o}
-lattices in a quadratic space,
L+K
is the their sum as
\mathfrak{o}
-modules which is an
\mathfrak{o}
-lattice. If
L\cap K=\bm{0}
, then their sum becomes a direct sum which is denoted by
L\oplus K
. Further, if
L\cap K=\bm{0}
and
B(L,K)=\{0\}
, then their sum becomes an orthogonal sum and will be denoted by
L\perp K
.

As an module over
\mathfrak{o}
,
L
needs not be free. But there are linearly independent vectors
\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{\ell}
in
L
and fractional ideals
\mathfrak{a}_{1},\ldots,\mathfrak{a}_{\ell}
such that
L=\mathfrak{a}_{1}\bm{x}_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathfrak{a}_{\ell}\bm{x}_{\ell}
. The volume of
L
, denoted
\mathfrak{v}(L)
, is defined as

\mathfrak{v}(L)=(\mathfrak{a}_{1}\cdots\mathfrak{a}_{\ell})^{2}\det(B(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}_{j})).

If
L
is integral, then it is nondegenerate if
\mathfrak{v}(L)\neq 0
, and is unimodular if
\mathfrak{v}(L)=\mathfrak{o}
. More generally,
L
is
\mathfrak{a}
-modular if
\mathfrak{s}(L)=\mathfrak{a}
and
\mathfrak{v}(L)=\mathfrak{a}^{\ell}
, which is equivalent to the equality
\mathfrak{a}L^{\#}=L
. Since
(L^{\#})^{\#}=L
, we see that
L
is
\mathfrak{a}
-modular if and only if
L^{\#}
is
\mathfrak{a}^{-1}
-modular.

Let
N
be another
\mathfrak{o}
-lattice. We say that
N
is represented by
L
, denoted
N\longrightarrow L
, if there exists an
\mathfrak{o}
-linear map
\varphi:N\longrightarrow L
such that
Q(\varphi(\bm{x}))=Q(\bm{x})
for all
\bm{x}\in N
. Such a map
\varphi
is called a representation from
N
to
L
. It is called primitive if
\varphi(N)
is a primitive sublattice (or a direct summand) of
L
, which is the same as
\varphi(FN)\cap L=\varphi(N)
. An injective representation from
N
to
L
is called an isometry from
N
into
L
, and
N
and
L
are said to be isometric if there is an isometry from
N
onto
L
. It is known that if
N
is nondegenerate, then a representation
\varphi:N\longrightarrow L
must be an isometry.

The isometry class of
L
, denoted by
[L]
, is the set of
\mathfrak{o}
-lattices that are isometric to
L
. If
N
is represented by
L
, then any lattices in
[N]
will also be represented by any lattices in
[L]
. Therefore, we may speak of an isometry class of lattices being represented by another isometry class of lattices.

We point out in here that when
\mathfrak{o}
is the ring of integers in a number field, the set of isometry class of
\mathfrak{o}
-lattices is countable. Indeed, every
\mathfrak{o}
-lattice of rank
n
must be isometric to an
\mathfrak{o}
-lattice on
F^{n}
equipped with a quadratic map which can be identified with an
n\times n
symmetric matrix
S
with entries in
F
. As is indicated earlier, to specify such an
\mathfrak{o}
-lattice on
F^{n}
all we need is an
n\times n
matrix
A
over
F
whose columns are linearly independent and an ordered
n
-tuple
I
of fractional ideals of
F
. Since
F
is a number field, there are countably many these
S
,
A
, and
I
. Therefore, all together there are only countably many tuples
(S,A,I)
, and hence there are only countably many isometry classes of
\mathfrak{o}
-lattices of rank
n
.

Suppose that
N
is a sublattice of
L
. We say that
N
splits
L
if
N
is an orthogonal summand of
L
. The lattice
L
is called decomposable if
L=N\perp M
for some nonzero sublattices
N
and
M
; otherwise it is indecomposable. It is clear that every
\mathfrak{o}
-lattice
L
is the orthogonal sum
L=L_{1}\perp\cdots\perp L_{t}
of indecomposable sublattices
L_{1},\ldots,L_{t}
. When
\mathfrak{o}
is the ring of integers in a totally real number field and
L
is positive definite, Eichler’s unique decomposition theorem
[
4
,
15
]
says that the indecomposable sublattices
L_{1},\ldots,L_{t}
are uniquely determined by
L
(aside from their order). O’Meara
[
18
]
showed that when
F
is totally real, positive definite indecomposable
\mathfrak{o}
-lattices exist in all ranks, and if the class number of
F
is 1 then there are positive definite indecomposable
\mathfrak{o}
-lattices of any given rank and any given discriminant that is not square-free with exactly three exceptions, all of them over
{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Suppose that
\mathfrak{o}
is the ring of integers in a number field
F
. For every finite prime
\mathfrak{p}
of
F
,
F_{\mathfrak{p}}
and
\mathfrak{o}_{\mathfrak{p}}
denote the completion of
F
and
\mathfrak{o}
at
\mathfrak{p}
, respectively, and
L_{\mathfrak{p}}
denotes the completion of
L
at
\mathfrak{p}
which is an
\mathfrak{o}_{\mathfrak{p}}
-lattice. The genus of an
\mathfrak{o}
-lattice
L
, denoted
\text{gen}(L)
, is the set of
\mathfrak{o}
-lattices
K
on
FL
such that
K_{\mathfrak{p}}
is isometric to
L_{\mathfrak{p}}
for all finite primes
\mathfrak{p}
. An
\mathfrak{o}
-lattice
N
is said to be represented by
\text{gen}(L)
if
N
is represented by some
\mathfrak{o}
-lattice in
\text{gen}(L)
. This is the same as saying that
N_{\mathfrak{p}}
is represented by
L_{\mathfrak{p}}
for all primes
\mathfrak{p}
.

3.
The Indefinite Case

In this section,
F
is a number field and
\mathfrak{o}
is the ring of integers in
F
. All
\mathfrak{o}
-lattices are assumed to be nondegenerate and integral.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
L
be an
\mathfrak{o}
-lattice. Suppose that
M
is an
\mathfrak{o}
-lattice which represents all proper sublattices of
L
. Then
L
is represented by the genus of
M
. If, in addition,
L
and
M
have the same rank, then
L
is represented by
M
.

Proof.

Since
M
represents every proper sublattice of
L
,
F_{\mathfrak{p}}M
represents
F_{\mathfrak{p}}L
for all infinite primes
\mathfrak{p}
of
F
. Now, suppose that
\mathfrak{p}
is a finite prime of
F
. Let
L^{\prime}
be a proper sublattice of
L
such that
L^{\prime}_{\mathfrak{q}}=L_{\mathfrak{q}}
for all finite primes
\mathfrak{q}\neq\mathfrak{p}
. Then,
L^{\prime}
is represented by
M
, and hence
L_{\mathfrak{q}}=L^{\prime}_{\mathfrak{q}}
is represented by
M_{\mathfrak{q}}
for all
\mathfrak{q}\neq\mathfrak{p}
; the existence of such
L^{\prime}
is an immediate consequence of
[
17
, 81:14]
. Applying the same argument with a finite prime different from
\mathfrak{p}
, we may conclude that
L_{\mathfrak{p}}
is represented by
M_{\mathfrak{p}}
as well.

Suppose in addition that
L
and
M
have the same rank. Since
F_{\mathfrak{p}}L
is isometric to
F_{\mathfrak{p}}M
for all primes
\mathfrak{p}
,
FL
is isometric to
FM
by Hasse-Minkowski Principle. Hence, we may assume that
L
and
M
are
\mathfrak{o}
-lattices on the same space. Let
V
be the space underlying both
L
and
M
. Choose a finite nondyadic prime
\mathfrak{p}
such that both
L_{\mathfrak{p}}
and
M_{\mathfrak{p}}
are unimodular. Let
\{\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{\ell}\}
be an orthogonal basis of
L_{\mathfrak{p}}
, and by
[
17
, 81:14]
let
N
be the proper sublattice of
L
such that
N_{\mathfrak{q}}=L_{\mathfrak{q}}
for all
\mathfrak{q}\neq\mathfrak{p}
and
N_{\mathfrak{p}}=\mathfrak{o}_{\mathfrak{p}}\bm{x}_{1}+\cdots+\mathfrak{o}_{\mathfrak{p}}\bm{x}_{\ell-1}+\mathfrak{o}_{\mathfrak{p}}\pi\bm{x}_{\ell}
, where
\pi
is a uniformizer at
\mathfrak{p}
. By the hypothesis,
N
is represented by
M
. We may assume that
N\subseteq M
. Then, both
L_{\mathfrak{p}}
and
M_{\mathfrak{p}}
are unimodular
\mathfrak{o}_{\mathfrak{p}}
-lattices containing the unimodular sublattice
K_{(\mathfrak{p})}:=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell-1}\mathfrak{o}_{\mathfrak{p}}\bm{x}_{i}
of co-rank 1 as an orthogonal summand. The orthogonal complement of
F_{\mathfrak{p}}K_{(\mathfrak{p})}
in
V_{\mathfrak{p}}
is the 1-dimensional quadratic space
F_{\mathfrak{p}}\bm{x}_{\ell}
which contains only one unimodular
\mathfrak{o}_{\mathfrak{p}}
-lattice, namely
\mathfrak{o}_{\mathfrak{p}}\bm{x}_{\ell}
. This implies that
L_{\mathfrak{p}}=M_{\mathfrak{p}}
. For any finite prime
\mathfrak{q}\neq\mathfrak{p}
,
L_{\mathfrak{q}}=N_{\mathfrak{q}}\subseteq M_{\mathfrak{q}}
. Therefore,
L\subseteq M
.
∎

In what follows,
M
is an indefinite
\mathfrak{o}
-lattice of rank
\geq 3
, and
L
is another
\mathfrak{o}
-lattice in
FM
which is represented by the genus of
M
. The theory of spinor exceptions will play an important role in the following discussion. It is known that
L
may not be represented by every class in
\text{gen}(M)
; in that case and when
\textnormal{rank}(M)\geq 3
,
L
is called a spinor exception of
\text{gen}(M)
. Following the notations used in
[
9
]
, associated to
M
and
L
is a subgroup
F^{\times}\theta(G_{M/L})
(or
F^{\times}\theta_{A}(\text{gen}(M):L)
used in
[
20
]
) of the ideles of
F
. Via class field theory,
F^{\times}\theta(G_{M/L})
corresponds to an abelian extension
\Sigma_{M/L}
of
F
which is unramified outside of the set of prime divisors of
2\mathfrak{v}(M)
. When
\textnormal{rank}(M)\geq 3
, whether or not
M
represents
L
largely depends on the module index ideal
[L:L\cap M]
and its image in
\text{Gal}(\Sigma_{M/L}/F)
under the Artin map. The readers are encouraged to consult
[
3
,
9
,
20
]
for more details. We will describe briefly the results used in later discussion.

For any
\mathfrak{o}
-lattices
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
of the same rank such that
L_{1}\subseteq L_{2}
, let
[L_{2}:L_{1}]
be the module index ideal; see
[
6
, page 10]
or
[
7
, page 94]
. It can be shown–for example, using
[
7
, Theorem 14(b)]
–that
[L_{2}:L_{1}]
is the product of the annihilators of the simple factors of a composition series of
L_{2}/L_{1}
. In particular, if
L_{2}/L_{1}\cong\mathfrak{o}/\mathfrak{p}
where
\mathfrak{p}
is a finite prime, then
[L_{2}:L_{1}]=\mathfrak{p}
.

Suppose that
[L:L\cap M]
is relatively prime to
2\mathfrak{v}(M)
. This is not a serious restriction; as a matter of fact, by the weak approximation of the special orthogonal group there is always an
\mathfrak{o}
-lattice
M^{\prime}
in the isometry class of
M
such that
L_{\mathfrak{p}}\subseteq M^{\prime}_{\mathfrak{p}}
for all prime divisors
\mathfrak{p}
of
2\mathfrak{v}(M)
. In any case, it follows from
[
20
, Theorem 5.3]
that
L
is represented by
M
exactly when the image of
[L:L\cap M]
under the Artin map is trivial in
\text{Gal}(\Sigma_{M/L}/F)
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
L
be an
\mathfrak{o}
-lattice. If
M
is an indefinite
\mathfrak{o}
-lattice which represents all proper sublattices of
L
, then
M
represents
L
.

Proof.

For the sake of convenience, let
\ell
be the rank of
L
and
m
be the rank of
M
.

We first assume that
m\geq 3
. If
m-\ell\geq 3
, then
M
represents
L
by
[
9
, page 135]
. Suppose that
m-\ell\leq 2
. We may assume that
FL
is a subspace of
FM
. Let
\mathfrak{p}
be a finite prime which does not divide
2\mathfrak{v}(M)
and splits completely in the extension
\Sigma_{M/L}
. Let
K
be a proper sublattice of
L
such that
L/K\cong\mathfrak{o}/\mathfrak{p}
as an
\mathfrak{o}
-module. Then,
K
is represented by
M
by the hypothesis. We may then assume that
K\subseteq M
. If
L\subseteq M
, then we are done. Otherwise,
L\cap M=K
and
L/L\cap M\cong\mathfrak{o}/\mathfrak{p}
, i.e.
[L:L\cap M]=\mathfrak{p}
. Since
\mathfrak{p}
splits completely in
\Sigma_{M/L}
, its image under the Artin map is trivial in
\text{Gal}(\Sigma_{M/L}/F)
. By
[
20
, Theorem 5.3]
,
L
is represented by
M
.

In view of Proposition
3.1
, we are left with the case when
m=2
and
\ell=1
. So, suppose that
M
is an indefinite binary
\mathfrak{o}
-lattice and
L
is a unary
\mathfrak{o}
-lattice. We first assume that
M
is anisotropic. In this case, let
\mathfrak{p}
be a finite prime such that
\mathfrak{p}\nmid 2\mathfrak{v}(L)\mathfrak{v}(M)
and
M_{\mathfrak{p}}
is anisotropic. Let
N
be a proper sublattice of
L
such that
N_{\mathfrak{q}}=L_{\mathfrak{q}}
for all
\mathfrak{q}\neq\mathfrak{p}
. Then,
N
is represented by
M
and we may assume that
N\subseteq M
. At
\mathfrak{p}
,
L_{\mathfrak{p}}\subseteq M_{\mathfrak{p}}
because
Q(L_{\mathfrak{p}})\subseteq\mathfrak{o}_{\mathfrak{p}}
and
M_{\mathfrak{p}}
is the unique
\mathfrak{o}_{\mathfrak{p}}
-maximal lattice on
F_{\mathfrak{p}}M
. But for all
\mathfrak{q}\neq\mathfrak{p}
,
L_{\mathfrak{q}}=N_{\mathfrak{q}}\subseteq M_{\mathfrak{q}}
; whence
L\subseteq M
.

Now suppose that
M
is a binary isotropic
\mathfrak{o}
-lattice. The underlying quadratic space
FM
is a hyperbolic plane. Let
\bm{e}
and
\bm{f}
be two linearly independent isotropic vectors in
FM
. For almost all finite primes
\mathfrak{p}
,
M_{\mathfrak{p}}=\mathfrak{o}_{\mathfrak{p}}\bm{e}+\mathfrak{o}_{\mathfrak{p}}\bm{f}
and
L_{\mathfrak{p}}=\mathfrak{o}_{\mathfrak{p}}\bm{v}
for some
\bm{v}\in L
. We choose one such
\mathfrak{p}
that is nondyadic and principal, and let
\pi
be a generator of
\mathfrak{p}
. Let
L^{\prime}
be the sublattice of
L
such that
L^{\prime}_{\mathfrak{q}}=L_{\mathfrak{q}}
for all
\mathfrak{q}\neq\mathfrak{p}
and
L^{\prime}_{\mathfrak{p}}=\mathfrak{o}_{\mathfrak{p}}\pi\bm{v}
. It is represented by
M
, and once again we may assume that it is a sublattice of
M
. If
\pi\bm{v}
is not a primitive vector in
M_{\mathfrak{p}}
, then
\bm{v}\in M_{\mathfrak{p}}
and
L\subseteq M
. So, let us assume that
\pi\bm{v}=\alpha\bm{e}+\beta\bm{f}
, where
\alpha
is a unit of
\mathfrak{o}_{\mathfrak{p}}
. Then,
\pi^{2}\mid\beta
because
2\alpha\beta=Q(\pi\bm{v})
and
\pi^{2}\mid Q(\pi\bm{v})
. Let
\varphi
be the isometry on
FM
such that
\varphi(\bm{e})=\pi\bm{e}
and
\varphi(\bm{f})=\pi^{-1}\bm{f}
. Then
\varphi(L^{\prime})\subseteq M
and
\varphi(\pi\bm{v})=\pi\alpha\bm{e}+\pi^{-1}\beta\bm{f}
. So,
\varphi(L)\subseteq M
and we are done.
∎

4.
The Definite Case

In this section,
F
is assumed to be a totally real number field and the ring
\mathfrak{o}
is always the ring of integers in
F
. We denote the norm and trace from
F
to
\mathbb{Q}
by
Nr
and
Tr
, respectively. We continue to assume that all
\mathfrak{o}
-lattices are nondegenerate and integral.

4.1.
Nonsplit volume-minimizing flags

Let
L
be a positive definite
\mathfrak{o}
-lattice of rank
\ell
. For any positive integer
i<\ell
, let
\mathscr{P}^{i}(L)
be the set of primitive sublattices of
L
of rank
i
. Let
N
be another lattice of rank
n<\ell
. The set of primitive representations from
N
to
L
is denoted by
R^{*}(N,L)
. Define

\mathscr{D}(N):=\{M\in\mathscr{P}^{n+1}(L):\exists\,\varphi\in R^{*}(N,L),\varphi(N)\subseteq M\mbox{ does not split $M$}\}.

This set could be empty, even when
N
is a primitive sublattice of
L
. For example, if
N
is a unimodular sublattice of
L
, then
\varphi(N)
must split
L
for every
\varphi\in R^{*}(N,L)
and
\mathscr{D}(N)
is empty. On the other hand, if
L
is indecomposable, then
\mathscr{D}(N)
is never empty for any primitive proper sublattice
N
of
L
. This can be seen as follows. There is a nonzero sublattice
K
of
L
such that
L=N\oplus K
. This sublattice
K
cannot be orthogonal to
N
; otherwise
L
is decomposable which is not the case. Therefore, there must be a vector
\bm{x}\in K
and a fractional ideal
\mathfrak{a}
such that
B(\bm{x},N)\neq\{0\}
and
N\oplus\mathfrak{a}\bm{x}
is a primitive sublattice of
L
of rank
n+1
. This means that
N\oplus\mathfrak{a}\bm{x}
is in
\mathscr{D}(N)
.

Let

\mathscr{M}(N)=\left\{M\in\mathscr{D}(N):\textnormal{Nr}(\mathfrak{v}(M))\leq\textnormal{Nr}(\mathfrak{v}(M^{\prime}))\mbox{ for all }M^{\prime}\in\mathscr{D}(N)\right\}.

We extend this definition to include the case
N=\bm{0}
by setting
\mathscr{M}(\bm{0})
to be the set of rank-1 sublattices
M
of
L
such that
\textnormal{Nr}(\mathfrak{v}(M))
is the smallest. It is easy to see that all sublattices in
\mathscr{M}(\bm{0})
are in
\mathscr{P}^{1}(L)
. Since there are only finitely many sublattices of
L
of a fixed rank and a fixed volume, each
\mathscr{M}(N)
is a finite set. Moreover,
\mathscr{M}(N)=\{L\}
for any sublattice
N
of rank
\ell-1
which does not split
L
. Note that when
L
is indecomposable,
\mathscr{M}(N)
is not empty because
\mathscr{D}(N)
is not empty.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
L
be a positive definite
\mathfrak{o}
-lattice of rank
\ell
, and
k\leq\ell
be a positive integer. A nonsplit volume-minimizing flag of
L
of length
k
is a chain of sublattices of
L

\bm{0}=N_{0}\subsetneq N_{1}\subsetneq N_{2}\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq N_{k}

such that
N_{i}\in\mathscr{M}(N_{i-1})
for
1\leq i\leq k
. Such a flag is called maximal if
k=\ell
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Suppose that
\bm{0}=N_{0}\subsetneq N_{1}\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq N_{k}
is a nonsplit volume-minimizing flag of a positive definite
\mathfrak{o}
-lattice
L
. Then, for
1\leq j\leq k
, every representation of
N_{j}
by
L
must be primitive.

Proof.

It is clear that the assertion holds for
N_{1}
. Suppose that
k>1
and there is a positive integer
j\leq k-1
such that the assertion holds for
N_{1},\ldots,N_{j}
but not for
N_{j+1}
. Then, there is a representation
\psi
of
N_{j+1}
by
L
such that
\psi(N_{j+1})
is not a primitive sublattice of
L
.

Let
\tilde{N}_{j+1}
be the sublattice
\psi(FN_{j+1})\cap L
. It is a primitive sublattice of
L
containing
\psi(N_{j+1})
as a proper sublattice and
\textnormal{Nr}(\mathfrak{v}(\tilde{N}_{j+1}))<\textnormal{Nr}(\mathfrak{v}(N_{j+1}))
as a result. Since the restriction of
\psi
on
N_{j}
must be a primitive representation from
N_{j}
to
L
and
\psi(N_{j})\subset\tilde{N}_{j+1}
, it follows that
\psi(N_{j})
must split
\tilde{N}_{j+1}
. So,

(4.1)

\tilde{N}_{j+1}=\psi(N_{j})\perp\mathfrak{b}\bm{z}

for some
\bm{z}\in L
and fractional ideal
\mathfrak{b}
containing
\mathfrak{o}
. Since
N_{j}
is primitive in
N_{j+1}
, there exist
\bm{v}\in L
and a fractional ideal
\mathfrak{a}
containing
\mathfrak{o}
such that

N_{j+1}=N_{j}\oplus\mathfrak{a}\bm{v}.

Then,
\bm{v}\in N_{j+1}
, and hence there exist
\beta\in\mathfrak{b}
and
\bm{y}\in N_{j}
such that
\psi(\bm{v})=\psi(\bm{y})+\beta\bm{z}
. For any
\alpha\in\mathfrak{a}
,

\psi(\alpha\bm{v})=\alpha\psi(\bm{y})+\alpha\beta\bm{z}=\psi(\alpha\bm{y})+\alpha\beta\bm{z}.

Since
\alpha\bm{v}
is in
N_{j+1}
and
\psi(N_{j+1})\subseteq\tilde{N}_{j+1}
, by (
4.1
) we can write
\psi(\alpha\bm{v})=\bm{e}+\bm{f}
with
\bm{e}\in\psi(N_{j})
and
\bm{f}\in\mathfrak{b}\bm{z}
. Since
F\psi(N_{j})\cap F\bm{z}=\bm{0}
, we must have
\psi(\alpha\bm{y})=\bm{e}\in\psi(N_{j})
and hence
\alpha\beta\bm{z}\in\psi(N_{j+1})
.

Thus, on one hand, we have

\displaystyle\psi(N_{j+1})=\psi(N_{j})\oplus\mathfrak{a}\psi(\bm{v})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\psi(N_{j})\oplus\mathfrak{a}(\psi(\bm{y})+\beta\bm{z})

\displaystyle\subseteq

\displaystyle\psi(N_{j})+\mathfrak{a}\psi(\bm{y})+\mathfrak{a}\beta\bm{z}

\displaystyle\subseteq

\displaystyle\psi(N_{j})\perp\mathfrak{a}\beta\bm{z}.

On the other hand,
\psi(N_{j})\subseteq\psi(N_{j+1})
and
\alpha\beta\bm{z}\in\psi(N_{j+1})
for all
\alpha\in\mathfrak{a}
. Hence

\psi(N_{j+1})=\psi(N_{j})\perp\mathfrak{a}\beta\bm{z}.

This means that
N_{j}
splits
N_{j+1}
which is impossible. This contradiction proves the lemma.
∎

4.2.
Indecomposable lattices

Recall that an
\mathfrak{o}
-lattice is indecomposable if it is not the orthogonal sum of two nonzero proper sublattices.

Proposition 4.3
.

Every positive definite indecomposable
\mathfrak{o}
-lattice has a maximal nonsplit volume-minimizing flag.

Proof.

Let
L
be a positive definite indecomposable
\mathfrak{o}
-lattice of rank
\ell
. We start with building the flag by fixing a choice of
N_{1}
from
\mathscr{M}(\bm{0})
. Suppose that for some positive integer
k\leq\ell-1
, we have constructed a nonsplit volume-minimizing flag of length
k
:

\bm{0}=N_{0}\subsetneq N_{1}\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq N_{k}.

As is mentioned earlier,
\mathscr{M}(N_{k})
is nonempty because
L
is indecomposable. Let
M
be a lattice in
\mathscr{M}(N_{k})
, which must be primitive of rank
k+1
, and
\varphi
be a primitive representation of
N_{k}
by
L
such that
\varphi(N_{k})\subseteq M
and that
\varphi(N_{k})
does not split
M
. It is clear that
\varphi(N_{j})\in\mathscr{M}(\varphi(N_{j-1}))
for
1\leq j\leq k
and
\mathscr{M}(N_{k})=\mathscr{M}(\varphi(N_{k}))
. We then have a nonsplit volume-minimizing flag of length
k+1
:

\bm{0}=N_{0}\subsetneq\varphi(N_{1})\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq\varphi(N_{k})\subsetneq M.

Thus, we may construct inductively a maximal nonsplit volume-minimizing flag of
L
.
∎

Theorem 4.4
.

A positive definite
\mathfrak{o}
-lattice
L
is decomposable if and only if there are proper sublattices
L_{1},\ldots,L_{t}
of
L
such that
L
is represented by
L_{1}\perp\cdots\perp L_{t}
.

Proof.

The “only if” part of the statement is trivial. So, we assume on the contrary that
L
is indecomposable and
\varphi:L\longrightarrow L_{1}\perp\cdots\perp L_{t}
is a representation with proper sublattices
L_{1},\ldots,L_{t}
of
L
. Obviously,
t
is at least 2. Let

\bm{0}=N_{0}\subsetneq N_{1}\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq N_{\ell}=L

be a maximal nonsplit volume-minimizing flag of
L
. We select vectors
\bm{v}_{1},\ldots,\bm{v}_{\ell}
and fractional ideals
\mathfrak{a}_{1},\ldots,\mathfrak{a}_{\ell}
containing
\mathfrak{o}
such that

N_{j}=\mathfrak{a}_{1}\bm{v}_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathfrak{a}_{j}\bm{v}_{j}

for
1\leq j\leq\ell
.

Suppose that
\varphi(N_{1})
is not a sublattice of any of the
L_{i}
. Then,
\varphi(\bm{v}_{1})
is not in any of the
L_{i}
. Without loss of generality, we may assume that
\varphi(\bm{v}_{1})=\bm{e}_{1}+\bm{f}
, where
\bm{0}\neq\bm{e}_{1}\in L_{1}
and
\bm{0}\neq\bm{f}\in L_{2}\perp\cdots\perp L_{t}
. Then, for any
\alpha_{1}\in\mathfrak{a}_{1}
,
\alpha_{1}\bm{v}_{1}\in N_{1}
and
\varphi(\alpha_{1}\bm{v}_{1})=\alpha_{1}\bm{e}_{1}+\alpha_{1}\bm{f}
with
\alpha_{1}\bm{e}_{1}\in FL_{1}
, hence
\alpha_{1}\bm{e}_{1}\in L_{1}\subseteq L
. So,
\mathfrak{a}_{1}\bm{e}_{1}
is a sublattice of
L
and

\displaystyle\textnormal{Nr}(\mathfrak{v}(N_{1}))=\textnormal{Nr}(\mathfrak{a}_{1}^{2})\textnormal{Nr}(Q(\bm{v}_{1}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\textnormal{Nr}(\mathfrak{a}_{1}^{2})\textnormal{Nr}(Q(\bm{e}_{1})+Q(\bm{f}))

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle\textnormal{Nr}(\mathfrak{a}_{1}^{2})\textnormal{Nr}(Q(\bm{e}_{1}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\textnormal{Nr}(\mathfrak{v}(\mathfrak{a}_{1}\bm{e}_{1})).

This contradicts that
N_{1}
is in
\mathscr{M}(\bm{0})
. So, we may assume that
\varphi(\bm{v}_{1})\in L_{1}
and
\varphi(N_{1})
is a sublattice of
L_{1}
.

Clearly,
\varphi(N_{\ell})=\varphi(L)
is not a sublattice of
L_{1}
. Thus, there is a positive integer
k\leq\ell-1
such that
\varphi(N_{j})\subseteq L_{1}
for
1\leq j\leq k
but
\varphi(N_{k+1})\nsubseteq L_{1}
. Then,
\mathfrak{a}_{k+1}\varphi(\bm{v}_{k+1})\nsubseteq L_{1}
. Suppose that
\varphi(\bm{v}_{k+1})\in L_{1}
. For any
\alpha\in\mathfrak{a}_{k+1}
,
\alpha\bm{v}_{k+1}\in N_{k+1}
and
\varphi(N_{k+1})\subseteq L_{1}\perp\cdots\perp L_{t}
,
\alpha\varphi(\bm{v}_{k+1})=\varphi(\alpha\bm{v}_{k+1})
must be inside of
L_{1}
. Therefore,
\mathfrak{a}_{k+1}\varphi(\bm{v}_{k+1})\subseteq L_{1}
which is a contradiction. This means that
\varphi(\bm{v}_{k+1})\not\in L_{1}
.

Thus,

\varphi(\bm{v}_{k+1})=\bm{x}+\bm{y},\quad\bm{x}\in L_{1},\quad\bm{0}\neq\bm{y}\in L_{2}\perp\cdots\perp L_{t}.

For any
\alpha\in\mathfrak{a}_{k+1}
,
\alpha\bm{v}_{k+1}\in N_{k+1}
and

\varphi(\alpha\bm{v}_{k+1})=\alpha\bm{x}+\alpha\bm{y},

with
\alpha\bm{x}\in FL_{1}
and
\alpha\bm{y}\in F(L_{2}\perp\cdots\perp L_{t})
. Therefore,
\alpha\bm{x}\in L_{1}
and
\alpha\bm{y}\in L_{2}\perp\cdots\perp L_{t}
, whence
\mathfrak{a}_{k+1}\bm{x}\subseteq L_{1}
and
\mathfrak{a}_{k+1}\bm{y}\subseteq L_{2}\perp\cdots\perp L_{t}
. Moreover,
\bm{x}\not\in F\varphi(N_{k})
; otherwise
\mathfrak{a}_{k+1}\bm{x}\subseteq F\varphi(N_{k})\cap L_{1}=\varphi(N_{k})
because
\varphi\in R^{*}(N_{k},L)
by Lemma
4.2
, meaning that
\varphi(N_{k+1})=\varphi(N_{k})\perp\mathfrak{a}_{k+1}\bm{y}
which is impossible. Thus,
\varphi(N_{k})\oplus\mathfrak{a}_{k+1}\bm{x}
is a sublattice of
L_{1}
(hence of
L
as well) of rank
k+1
containing
\varphi(N_{k})
. Let

\hat{N}_{k+1}=F(\varphi(N_{k})\oplus\mathfrak{a}_{k+1}\bm{x})\cap L

which is a primitive sublattice of
L
.

Since
B(\varphi(\bm{v}_{i}),\bm{y})=0
for
1\leq i\leq k
, the Gram matrix of the linearly independent set
\{\varphi(\bm{v}_{1}),\ldots,\varphi(\bm{v}_{k}),\varphi(\bm{v}_{k+1})\}
is

(4.2)

\begin{bmatrix}B(\varphi(\bm{v}_{1}),\varphi(\bm{v}_{1}))&\cdots&\cdots&B(\varphi(\bm{v}_{1}),\varphi(\bm{v}_{k}))&B(\varphi(\bm{v}_{1}),\bm{x})\\
\vdots&&&\vdots&\vdots\\
\vdots&&&\vdots&\vdots\\
B(\varphi(\bm{v}_{k}),\varphi(\bm{v}_{1}))&\cdots&\cdots&B(\varphi(\bm{v}_{k}),\varphi(\bm{v}_{k}))&B(\varphi(\bm{v}_{k}),\bm{x})\\
B(\bm{x},\varphi(\bm{v}_{1}))&\cdots&\cdots&B(\bm{x},\varphi(\bm{v}_{k}))&Q(\bm{x})+Q(\bm{y})\end{bmatrix}

Let
\bm{X}
and
\bm{Y}
be the Gram matrices of the two sets of linearly independent vectors
\{\varphi(\bm{v}_{1}),\ldots,\varphi(\bm{v}_{k}),\bm{x}\}
and
\{\varphi(\bm{v}_{1}),\ldots,\varphi(\bm{v}_{k}),\bm{y}\}
, respectively. Both
\bm{X}
and
\bm{Y}
are positive definite. By expanding along the last row, we see that the determinant of the Gram matrix (
4.2
) is
\det(\bm{X})+\det(\bm{Y})
.
Thus,

\displaystyle\textnormal{Nr}(\mathfrak{v}(N_{k+1}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\textnormal{Nr}(\mathfrak{v}(\varphi(N_{k+1})))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\textnormal{Nr}(\mathfrak{a}_{1}^{2}\cdots\mathfrak{a}_{k+1}^{2})\cdot\textnormal{Nr}(\det(\bm{X})+\det(\bm{Y}))

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle\textnormal{Nr}(\mathfrak{a}_{1}^{2}\cdots\mathfrak{a}_{k+1}^{2})\cdot\textnormal{Nr}(\det(\bm{X}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\textnormal{Nr}(\mathfrak{v}(\varphi(N_{k})\oplus\mathfrak{a}_{k+1}\bm{x}))

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\textnormal{Nr}(\mathfrak{v}(\hat{N}_{k+1})).

So,
\varphi(N_{k})
must split
\hat{N}_{k+1}
, that is,
\hat{N}_{k+1}=\varphi(N_{k})\perp\mathfrak{b}\bm{z}
for some
\bm{z}\in L
and fractional ideal
\mathfrak{b}\supseteq\mathfrak{o}
.

Since
\varphi(N_{k})\oplus\mathfrak{a}_{k+1}\bm{x}\subseteq\hat{N}_{k+1}
, we may write

\bm{x}=\varphi(\bm{u})+\beta\bm{z},\quad\bm{u}\in N_{k},\quad\beta\in\mathfrak{b}.

For any
\alpha\in\mathfrak{a}_{k+1}
,
\alpha\bm{x}=\alpha\varphi(\bm{u})+\alpha\beta\bm{z}\in\hat{N}_{k+1}
, while
\alpha\varphi(\bm{u})\in F\varphi(N_{k})
and
\alpha\beta\bm{z}\in F\bm{z}
. Therefore,
\alpha\varphi(\bm{u})\in\varphi(N_{k})
and
\alpha\beta\bm{z}\in\mathfrak{b}\bm{z}
for all
\alpha\in\mathfrak{a}_{k+1}
, whence
\mathfrak{a}_{k+1}\varphi(\bm{u})\subseteq\varphi(N_{k})
and
\mathfrak{a}_{k+1}\beta\bm{z}\subseteq\mathfrak{b}\bm{z}
. Consequently,

\displaystyle\varphi(N_{k+1})=\varphi(N_{k})\oplus\mathfrak{a}_{k+1}\varphi(\bm{v}_{k+1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varphi(N_{k})\oplus\mathfrak{a}_{k+1}(\bm{x}+\bm{y})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varphi(N_{k})\oplus\mathfrak{a}_{k+1}(\varphi(\bm{u})+\beta\bm{z}+\bm{y})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varphi(N_{k})\perp\mathfrak{a}_{k+1}(\beta\bm{z}+\bm{y}).

This is impossible since
N_{k}
does not split
N_{k+1}
. This completes the proof of the theorem.
∎

As a corollary to Theorem
4.4
, we have

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
L
be a positive definite indecomposable
\mathfrak{o}
-lattice. Then, there is a positive definite
\mathfrak{o}
-lattice which represents all proper sublattices of
L
but not
L
itself.

Proof.

By Theorem
5.7
, the number-field version of
[
13
, Theorem 3.3]
which will be proved in the next section, there exist finitely many proper sublattices
L_{1},\ldots,L_{t}
of
L
with the property that if a positive definite
\mathfrak{o}
-lattice represents every
L_{i}
, then it must represent all the proper sublattices of
L
. As a result,
L_{1}\perp\cdots\perp L_{t}
must represent all the proper sublattices of
L
. However, by Theorem
4.4
, it does not represent
L
.
∎

5.
Finite universality criterion sets

We continue to assume that
F
is a totally real number field. Let
\mathscr{S}
be an infinite set of classes of positive definite
\mathfrak{o}
-lattices of a fixed rank
n
. A positive definite
\mathfrak{o}
-lattice is called
\mathscr{S}
-universal if it represents all classes in
\mathscr{S}
. The main result of this section is an extension of
[
13
, Theorem 3.3]
: there exists a finite subset
\mathscr{S}_{0}
of
\mathscr{S}
such that if a positive definite
\mathfrak{o}
-lattice represents all the classes of
\mathfrak{o}
-lattices in
\mathscr{S}_{0}
, then it will represent all the classes of
\mathfrak{o}
-lattices in
\mathscr{S}
. Following
[
2
]
and
[
5
]
, we call such a finite subset
\mathscr{S}_{0}
an
\mathscr{S}
-universality criterion set. Our proof follows the same strategy employed in
[
13
]
. However, extra care and additional effort are needed when we replace Minkowski reduction with Humbert reduction in the argument. Humbert reduction does not yield a “reduced basis” of an
\mathfrak{o}
-lattice even if the class number of
F
is 1; it only gives a set of linearly independent vectors in the
\mathfrak{o}
-lattice which spans a sublattice whose index is bounded above by a constant depending only on
F
and the rank of the
\mathfrak{o}
-lattice. Lifting a representation of this sublattice to the
\mathfrak{o}
-lattice is what we need to overcome.

5.1.
Humbert Reduction

The standard reference for Humbert reduction is Humbert’s original paper
[
10
]
. Let
N
be a positive definite
\mathfrak{o}
-lattice of rank
n
. In
[
10
]
, Humbert constructed linearly independent vectors
\bm{v}_{1},\ldots,\bm{v}_{n}
which play the roles of the vectors attaining the successive minima in Minkowski’s reduction theory. It may be worth mentioning that Humbert worked with matrices, i.e. free
\mathfrak{o}
-lattices. In other words, he considered quadratic forms on
\mathfrak{o}^{n}
, and the vectors
\bm{v}_{1},\ldots,\bm{v}_{n}
his construction yields are vectors in
\mathfrak{o}^{n}
. In that setting, he called the
n\times n
matrix
[\bm{v}_{1}\,\cdots\,\bm{v}_{n}]
a
matrixe auxiliaire
. However, as is remarked in
[
8
, page 139]
, Humbert’s method works equally well with non-free
\mathfrak{o}
-lattices. As a matter of fact, there is always a free sublattice of
N
of the same rank and whose index in
N
is bounded above by a constant depending only on
F
. We may then apply Humbert’s argument to this free sublattice of
N
.

We collect below some useful properties of the set
\{\bm{v}_{1},\ldots,\bm{v}_{n}\}
:

(H1)

By
[
10
, Théorème 1]
, the index of the free sublattice
\sum\mathfrak{o}\bm{v}_{i}
in
N
is bounded above by a constant depending only on
F
and
n
.

(H2)

By the construction given in
[
10
, §3]
,
\textnormal{Tr}(Q(\bm{v}_{1}))=\min\{\textnormal{Tr}(Q(\bm{x})):\bm{0}\neq\bm{x}\in N\}
, and for
i\geq 2
, we have
\textnormal{Tr}(Q(\bm{v}_{i}))\leq\textnormal{Tr}(Q(\bm{x}))
for all
\bm{x}\in N
such that
\bm{v}_{1},\ldots,\bm{v}_{i-1},\bm{x}
are linearly independent. Consequently,

0<\textnormal{Tr}(Q(\bm{v}_{1}))\leq\textnormal{Tr}(Q(\bm{v}_{2}))\leq\cdots\leq\textnormal{Tr}(Q(\bm{v}_{n})).

(H3)

Let
G=(g_{ij})
be the Gram matrix of
\{\bm{v}_{1},\ldots,\bm{v}_{n}\}
. There exist constants
C_{1},C_{2}
, and
C_{3}
, depending only on
n
and
F
, such that for any embeddings
\sigma
and
\tau
of
F
into
\mathbb{R}
,

(a)

g_{ii}^{\sigma}\leq C_{1}\,g_{jj}^{\tau}
for any
1\leq i\leq j\leq n

[
10
, page 288, (4)]
,

(b)

|g_{ik}^{\tau}|\leq C_{2}\,g_{ii}^{\tau}
for any
1\leq i\leq k\leq n
, following from
[
10
, page 279, (1)]
and (a),

(c)

\det(G)^{\tau}\leq g_{11}^{\tau}\cdots g_{nn}^{\tau}\leq C_{3}\,\det(G)^{\tau}

[
10
, Théorème 4]
.

For a lack of a more suitable terminology, we simply call
\{\bm{v}_{1},\ldots,\bm{v}_{n}\}
a Humbert linearly independent set in
N
. The free
\mathfrak{o}
-lattice
\sum\mathfrak{o}\bm{v}_{i}
is called a Humbert sublattice of
N
. As a consequence of (H1) and (H3), there exists a constant
C_{4}
, depending only on
n
and
F
, such that

\textnormal{Nr}(\mathfrak{v}(N))\leq\textnormal{Nr}(Q(\bm{v}_{1}))\cdots\textnormal{Nr}(Q(\bm{v}_{n}))\leq C_{4}\,\textnormal{Nr}(\mathfrak{v}(N)).

Therefore, there are only finitely many other
\mathfrak{o}
-lattices on the same space containing the Humbert linearly independent set
\{\bm{v}_{1},\ldots,\bm{v}_{n}\}
.

For each
i
, there are only finitely many choices of
\bm{v}_{i}
since
\textnormal{Tr}(Q)
is a positive definite quadratic form on
N
as a
{\mathbb{Z}}
-lattice. Thus, there are only finitely many Humbert linearly independent sets in
N
. From now on, we only work with one choice of Humbert linearly independent set for each positive definite integral
\mathfrak{o}
-lattice (and hence one choice of Humbert sublattice for each such lattice). However, we would like to make this choice uniform across the entire isometry class in the following sense. Fix one positive definite
\mathfrak{o}
-lattice
N
in its isometry class and select a choice of Humbert linearly independent set
\{\bm{v}_{1},\ldots,\bm{v}_{n}\}
for
N
. For each lattice
N^{\prime}
in
[N]
, choose an isometry
\varphi:N\longrightarrow N^{\prime}
and choose
\{\varphi(\bm{v}_{1}),\ldots,\varphi(\bm{v}_{n})\}
to be the Humbert linearly independent set for
N^{\prime}
. In this way, the Gram matrix with respect to the chosen Humbert linearly independent set is the same for every
\mathfrak{o}
-lattice in
[N]
. We define
\mu_{i}(N)
to be
\textnormal{Tr}(Q(\bm{v}_{i}))
for
1\leq i\leq n
. These are invariants of the isometry class
[N]
. For the sake of convenience, we also define
\overline{\mu}(N)
to be
\textnormal{Tr}(Q(\bm{v}_{n}))
.

Let
N^{\prime}
be another positive definite
\mathfrak{o}
-lattice of rank
n
with Humbert linearly independent set
\{\bm{v}_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,\bm{v}_{n}^{\prime}\}
. We say that
N
and
N^{\prime}
are
Humbert-isomorphic
if the canonical
\mathfrak{o}
-module isomorphism from
\sum\mathfrak{o}\bm{v}_{i}\longrightarrow\sum\mathfrak{o}\bm{v}_{i}^{\prime}
, which sends
\bm{v}_{i}
to
\bm{v}_{i}^{\prime}
for
1\leq i\leq n
, extends to an
\mathfrak{o}
-module isomorphism from
N
to
N^{\prime}
. This defines an equivalence relation on the set of
\mathfrak{o}
-lattices of rank
n
. Because of our way of fixing the Humbert linearly independent set of an
\mathfrak{o}
-lattice, we see that if
N
and
N^{\prime}
are isometric as
\mathfrak{o}
-lattices, then
N
and
N^{\prime}
are Humbert-isomorphic.

Let

(H4)

t
be the lcm of all the indices
[N^{\prime}:\sum\mathfrak{o}\bm{v}_{i}^{\prime}]
, where
N^{\prime}
runs through all positive definite
\mathfrak{o}
-lattices of rank
n
, and
\{\bm{v}_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,\bm{v}_{n}^{\prime}\}
runs through all Humbert linear independent sets of
N^{\prime}
.

This integer
t
exists by virtue of (H1). Let
\varphi_{N^{\prime}}:FN^{\prime}\longrightarrow F^{n}
be the isomorphism such that
\varphi_{N^{\prime}}(\bm{v}_{i}^{\prime})=\bm{e}_{i}
, where
\{\bm{e}_{1},\ldots,\bm{e}_{n}\}
is the standard basis of
F^{n}
. Then,
\varphi_{N^{\prime}}(N^{\prime})
is an
\mathfrak{o}
-module sitting between
\mathfrak{o}^{n}
and
t^{-1}\mathfrak{o}^{n}
. Thus, there are only finitely many possibilities of
\varphi_{N^{\prime}}(N^{\prime})
. It is clear that
N
and
N^{\prime}
are Humbert-isomorphic if
\varphi_{N}(N)=\varphi_{N^{\prime}}(N^{\prime})
. This implies that there are only finitely many Humbert-isomorphism classes of positive definite
\mathfrak{o}
-lattices of a fixed rank.

5.2.
The Escalation Process

Let
\mathscr{S}
be an infinite set of classes of positive definite
\mathfrak{o}
-integral lattices of rank
n
. As is pointed out in Section 2,
\mathscr{S}
is a countable set; so we may fix an enumeration of the classes in
\mathscr{S}
as
\{[N_{1}],[N_{2}],\ldots\}
. For any positive definite
\mathfrak{o}
-lattice
L
, define

N_{L}:=\begin{cases}N_{j}&\mbox{ if $N_{i}\longrightarrow L$ $\forall\,i<j$ but $N_{j}\not\longrightarrow L$};\\
\bm{0}&\mbox{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

For each
i\geq 1
, define a set of classes of
\mathfrak{o}
-lattices
\mathscr{T}_{i}
inductively as follows. Let
\mathscr{T}_{1}
be the set of classes of positive definite integral lattices of rank
n
which represent
N_{1}
. Clearly,
\mathscr{T}_{1}
is a finite set. For
i\geq 1
, let

\mathscr{U}_{i}=\{[L]\in\mathscr{T}_{i}:N_{L}\neq\bm{0}\}.

If
\mathscr{U}_{i}
is empty (which happens when every class in
\mathscr{T}_{i}
is
\mathscr{S}
-universal), we set
\mathscr{T}_{i+1}=\mathscr{T}_{i}
. Otherwise, for each
[L]\in\mathscr{U}_{i}
, let
\mathscr{U}(L)
be the set of classes of positive definite
\mathfrak{o}
-lattices
M
such that

(i)

M
represents
L
and
N_{L}
, and

(ii)

No sublattice of
M
of strictly smaller rank can represent both
L
and
N_{L}
.

Note that all lattices in any class in
\mathscr{U}(L)
have rank
\leq n+\textnormal{rank}(L)
. Moreover, the ranks of the classes in
\mathscr{U}(L)
is strictly greater than the rank of
L
. Let

\mathscr{T}_{i+1}=\bigcup_{[L]\in\mathscr{U}_{i}}\mathscr{U}(L).

Lemma 5.1
.

For every
i\geq 1
,
\mathscr{T}_{i}
is a finite set and every class of
\mathfrak{o}
-lattices in
\mathscr{T}_{i}
represents
N_{j}
for all
j\leq i
.

Proof.

The second assertion of the lemma follows from the definition of
\mathscr{T}_{i}
. As for the first assertion, we already noted that
\mathscr{T}_{1}
is finite. Suppose that the assertion holds for some
i\geq 1
. We may assume that
\mathscr{U}_{i}
is not empty. Let
[L]\in\mathscr{U}_{i}
and
[M]\in\mathscr{U}(L)
. Suppose that
\overline{\mu}(M)>\max\{\overline{\mu}(N_{L}),\overline{\mu}(L)\}
. Let
K
be the sublattice of
M
generated by all
\bm{x}\in M
such that
\textnormal{Tr}(Q(\bm{x}))<\overline{\mu}(M)
, and let
\tilde{K}=FK\cap M
. Then,
\tilde{K}
is a primitive sublattice of
M
of strictly smaller rank, and
\tilde{K}
represents the Humbert sublattices of
N_{L}
and
L
. So,
\tilde{K}
must represent
N_{L}
and
L
. But this is impossible as the rank of
\tilde{K}
is the same as the rank of
K
which is strictly smaller than the rank of
M
. Therefore,
\overline{\mu}(M)
is bounded above by a constant depending only on
L
. Since there are only finitely many classes of lattices in
\mathscr{U}_{i}
as
\mathscr{T}_{i}
is finite, there are only finitely many classes of
M
in
\mathscr{T}_{i+1}
.
∎

Lemma 5.2
.

Suppose that there exists
r\geq 1
such that every class of lattices in
\mathscr{T}_{r}
is
\mathscr{S}
-universal. Let

\mathscr{S}_{0}=\{[N_{1}]\}\cup\left\{[N_{L}]:[L]\in\bigcup_{i=1}^{r-1}\mathscr{U}_{i}\right\}.

Then, every
\mathscr{S}_{0}
-universal lattice is
\mathscr{S}
-universal.

Proof.

The proof is the same as that of
[
13
, Proposition 3.1]
.
∎

All is left to do is to demonstrate the existence of the integer
r
in this lemma. In what follows,
\mathfrak{p}
always stands for a finite prime of
F
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
\mathscr{S}(\mathfrak{p})
be an infinite set of
\mathfrak{o}_{\mathfrak{p}}
-lattices of rank
n
. Then, there exists a finite subset
\mathscr{S}_{0}(\mathfrak{p})
of
\mathscr{S}(\mathfrak{p})
such that any
\mathfrak{o}_{\mathfrak{p}}
-lattice that represents every
\mathfrak{o}_{\mathfrak{p}}
-lattice in
\mathscr{S}_{0}(\mathfrak{p})
must represent all
\mathfrak{o}_{\mathfrak{p}}
-lattices in
\mathscr{S}(\mathfrak{p})
.

Proof.

The proof is almost identical to
[
8
, Lemma 1.5]
.
∎

The next two lemmas concern representations of
\mathfrak{o}_{\mathfrak{p}}
-lattices. Their proofs reply on the Jordan decompositions of these lattices which are discussed in detail in
[
17
, Chapter IX]
. We recall some of the basic properties of these decompositions used in our discussion.

Let
L
be an
\mathfrak{o}_{\mathfrak{p}}
-lattice. Then,
L
has a Jordan decomposition (or Jordan splitting)
L=L_{1}\perp\cdots\perp L_{t}
in which every orthogonal summand is modular and
\mathfrak{s}(L_{1})\supsetneq\cdots\supsetneq\mathfrak{s}(L_{t})
. For
1\leq i\leq t
,
L_{i}
is called the
\mathfrak{s}(L_{i})
-component of that decomposition. The chain of fractional ideals
\mathfrak{s}(L_{1})\supsetneq\cdots\supsetneq\mathfrak{s}(L_{t})
is uniquely determined by
L
. For convenience, we denote
\mathfrak{s}(L_{t})
, the scale of the last Jordan component, by
\mathfrak{l}(L)
. Since
L^{\#}=L_{t}^{\#}\perp\cdots\perp L_{1}^{\#}
and each
L_{i}^{\#}
is
\mathfrak{s}(L_{i})^{-1}
-modular,
L_{t}^{\#}\perp\cdots\perp L_{1}^{\#}
is a Jordan decomposition of
L^{\#}
. In particular,
\mathfrak{s}(L^{\#})=\mathfrak{l}(L)^{-1}
and
\mathfrak{l}(L)^{-1}\supseteq\mathfrak{n}(L^{\#})\supseteq 2\cdot\mathfrak{l}(L)^{-1}
.

When
\mathfrak{s}(L)=\mathfrak{o}_{\mathfrak{p}}
, the first component in a Jordan decomposition of
L
must be unimodular. We call it the unimodular component of that Jordan decomposition. In general,
L
has more than one Jordan decompositions. But when
\mathfrak{p}
is nondyadic, the isometry classes of the Jordan components are uniquely determined. In particular, when
\mathfrak{p}
is nondyadic and
\mathfrak{s}(L)=\mathfrak{o}_{\mathfrak{p}}
, the unimodular components in all Jordan decompositions of
L
are isometric. In that case, the unimodular component of any Jordan decomposition of
L
is simply referred as the unimodular component of
L
.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
K
be a nondegenerate
\mathfrak{o}_{\mathfrak{p}}
-lattice and
\mathfrak{a}
be an ideal contained in
4\cdot\mathfrak{l}(K)
. Then,
K
contains a full-rank
\mathfrak{a}
-maximal sublattice. Consequently,
K
represents all integral
\mathfrak{o}_{\mathfrak{p}}
-lattices of rank
\leq g
with norm contained in
4\cdot\mathfrak{l}(K)
, provided
\textnormal{rank}(K)\geq g+3
.

Proof.

By
[
19
, Lemma 5.4]
, whose proof is for dyadic
\mathfrak{p}
but remains valid for the nondyadic case,
K
contains an
\mathfrak{a}
-maximal sublattice on
F_{\mathfrak{p}}K
as long as
\mathfrak{a}\subseteq 4\cdot\mathfrak{n}(K^{\#})^{-1}
. Since
\mathfrak{n}(K^{\#})\subseteq\mathfrak{l}(K)^{-1}
, therefore

4\cdot\mathfrak{n}(K^{\#})^{-1}\supseteq 4\cdot\mathfrak{l}(K)
. This proves the first assertion. The rest of the lemma follows from
[
8
, Lemma 1.1]
and the remark on
[
8
, Page 139]
.
∎

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
L
be a nondegenerate integral
\mathfrak{o}_{\mathfrak{p}}
-lattice of rank
\ell\geq 4n+6
and
K
be a primitive sublattice of
L
of rank
\geq 3n+6
. Then,
K
must represent all integral
\mathfrak{o}_{\mathfrak{p}}
-lattices of rank
n
with norm contained in
4\cdot\mathfrak{l}(L)
.

Proof.

Write
K
as
K=K_{0}\perp\cdots\perp K_{t}
, where
\mathfrak{s}(K_{i})=\mathfrak{p}^{i}
or
K_{i}=\bm{0}
. For the sake of convenience, let
m_{0}=\textnormal{ord}_{\mathfrak{p}}(\mathfrak{l}(L))
. As in
[
13
, Lemma 2.2]
, we define
K_{+}
and
K_{-}
as

K_{-}=K_{0}\perp\cdots\perp K_{m_{0}},\quad K_{+}=K_{m_{0}+1}\perp\cdots\perp K_{t}.

Both
K_{-}
and
K_{+}
are primitive sublattices of
L
. Since
\mathfrak{l}(K_{-})\supseteq\mathfrak{l}(L)
, it suffices show that
\textnormal{rank}(K_{-})\geq n+3
by virtue of the last lemma.

Let
r
be the rank of
K_{+}
, and
\{\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{r}\}
be a basis of
K_{+}
. Extend
\{\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{r}\}
to a basis
\{\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{r},\bm{y}_{1},\ldots,\bm{y}_{\ell-r}\}
of
L
.

Assume for the contradiction that
2r>\ell
. Then,
\ell-r<r
and the rows of the
r\times(\ell-r)
matrix
(B(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{y}_{j}))
must be linearly dependent over
F_{\mathfrak{p}}
. Thus, there must be a primitive row vector
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{r})
of
\mathfrak{o}_{\mathfrak{p}}^{r}
such that

(a_{1},\ldots,a_{r})\cdot(B(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{y}_{j}))=\bm{0}.

Let
\bm{v}=a_{1}\bm{x}_{1}+\cdots+a_{r}\bm{x}_{r}
. Then,
B(\bm{v},L)=\{0\}
mod
\mathfrak{p}^{m_{0}+1}
. However, since
\bm{v}
is a primitive vector of
K_{+}
which is a primitive sublattice of
L
,
\bm{v}
is also a primitive vector of
L
. Since
\mathfrak{l}(L)=\mathfrak{p}^{m_{0}}
,
L
has a Jordan decomposition
L=L_{1}\perp\cdots\perp L_{t}
with
\mathfrak{s}(L_{i})\supseteq\mathfrak{p}^{m_{0}}
for all
i
. Then,
\bm{v}=\bm{v}_{1}+\cdots+\bm{v}_{t}
with
\bm{v}_{i}\in L_{i}
for all
i
and
\bm{v}_{j}
primitive in
L_{j}
for at least one
j
. As a result,
B(\bm{v},L)\supseteq B(\bm{v},L_{j})=B(\bm{v}_{j},L_{j})=\mathfrak{s}(L_{j})\supseteq\mathfrak{p}^{m_{0}}
by virtue of
[
17
, 82:17]
. This is a contradiction.

So, we must have
2r\leq\ell
and

\textnormal{rank}(K_{-})=\textnormal{rank}(K)-r\geq\ell-n-r\geq\frac{\ell}{2}-n\geq n+3

which is what we need.
∎

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
L
be a positive definite
\mathfrak{o}
-lattice of rank
\geq 4n+6
, and
t
be a positive integer. There exists an ideal
\mathfrak{q}
of
\mathfrak{o}
, depending only on
tL
with the following properties: let
N
be a lattice of rank
n
,
M
be a lattice which represents both
N
and
L
, and
\tau:L\longrightarrow M
and
\sigma:N\longrightarrow M
be representations. Then the genus of
\sigma(N)^{\perp}\cap\tau(tL)
, where
\sigma(N)^{\perp}
is the orthogonal complement of
\sigma(N)
in
M
, represents all positive definite
\mathfrak{o}
-lattices of rank
n
with norm contained in
\mathfrak{q}
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that both
N
and
L
are sublattices of
M
, and that
\sigma
and
\tau
are the natural inclusions. Let
\textnormal{pr}:FM\longrightarrow FL
be the orthogonal projection from
FM
onto
FL
. Take an nonzero element
\alpha
of
\mathfrak{o}
such that
\tilde{N}:=\textnormal{pr}(\alpha N)
is a sublattice of
tL
. Let
K
be the orthogonal complement of
\tilde{N}
in
tL
, which is a primitive sublattice of
tL
of rank
\geq 3n+6
.

Let
\bm{v}
be a vector in
K
, and
\bm{x}
a vector in
N
. Then,
\bm{y}:=\textnormal{pr}(\alpha\bm{x})
is in
\tilde{N}
, and
\alpha\bm{x}=\bm{y}+\bm{z}
for some
\bm{z}
orthogonal to
FL
. Clearly,
B(\bm{v},\bm{y})=B(\bm{v},\bm{z})=0
. Therefore,
B(\bm{v},\alpha\bm{x})=0
; hence
B(\bm{v},\bm{x})=0
as well. This shows that
K\subseteq N^{\perp}
.

Let

\mathfrak{q}=\prod_{\mathfrak{p}}\mathfrak{p}^{\textnormal{ord}_{\mathfrak{p}}(4\cdot\mathfrak{l}(tL_{\mathfrak{p}}))}.

This ideal
\mathfrak{q}
is well-defined because
\textnormal{ord}_{\mathfrak{p}}(4\cdot\mathfrak{l}(tL_{\mathfrak{p}}))
is 0 for almost all
\mathfrak{p}
. The conclusion of the lemma now follows from the last lemma.
∎

5.3.
The Finiteness Theorem

We are now ready to complete the proof of number-field analog of the main theorem in
[
13
]
.

Theorem 5.7
.

Let
\mathscr{S}
be an infinite set of isometry classes of positive definite
\mathfrak{o}
-lattices of a fixed rank. Then, there exists a finite subset
\mathscr{S}_{0}
of
\mathscr{S}
such that if a positive definite
\mathfrak{o}
-lattice represents all classes of lattices in
\mathscr{S}_{0}
, then it represents all classes of lattices in
\mathscr{S}
.

Proof.

It suffices to show that an integer
r
satisfying the hypothesis of Lemma
5.2
does exist. Assume to the contrary that no such integer exists. Then, for every positive integer
i
, the set
\mathscr{U}_{i}
is nonempty, and the ranks of all the classes in
\mathscr{T}_{i+1}
is strictly greater than the ranks of all the classes in
\mathscr{T}_{i}
. Therefore, there must be a positive integer
s
such that the ranks of all the classes in
\mathscr{T}_{s}
are
\geq n+3
.

Let
P
be the set of all finite primes
\mathfrak{p}
of
F
such that

(i)

P
contains all the dyadic primes and all the prime divisors of
t
, where
t
is the integer defined in (H4) for rank
n
.

(ii)

L_{\mathfrak{p}}
is unimodular for all
\mathfrak{p}\not\in P
and all
L\in\mathscr{T}_{s}
.

Clearly, this
P
is a finite set.

By Lemma
5.3
, there are finitely many classes
[N^{(j)}]
in
\mathscr{S}
such that for each
\mathfrak{p}\in P
, if an
\mathfrak{o}_{\mathfrak{p}}
-lattice represents
N_{\mathfrak{p}}^{(j)}
for all
j
, then it represents
N_{\mathfrak{p}}
for all
[N]\in\mathscr{S}
. We take an integer
s_{1}\geq s
such that

(iii)

the ranks of all the classes of lattices in
\mathscr{T}_{s_{1}}
are
\geq 4n+6
,

(iv)

N_{\mathfrak{p}}\longrightarrow L_{\mathfrak{p}}
for all
\mathfrak{p}\in P
, all
[N]\in\mathscr{S}
, and all
[L]\in\mathscr{T}_{s_{1}}
,

(v)

L_{\mathfrak{p}}
has a unimodular component of rank
\geq n+3
for all
\mathfrak{p}\not\in P
and all
[L]\in\mathscr{T}_{s_{1}}
.

Condition (v) is a consequence of condition (ii) and the fact that every class of lattices in
\mathscr{T}_{s+1}
represents some classes of lattices in
\mathscr{T}_{s}
.

For every finite
\mathfrak{p}\not\in P
,
\mathfrak{p}
is nondyadic and
L_{\mathfrak{p}}
has a unimodular component of rank
\geq n+3
for every
[L]\in\mathscr{T}_{s_{1}}
. By
[
16
, Theorem 1]
, that unimodular component alone represents all
\mathfrak{o}_{\mathfrak{p}}
-lattices of rank
n
. Therefore, conditions (iv) and (v) together ensure that for each finite prime
\mathfrak{p}
of
F
,
N_{\mathfrak{p}}
is represented by
L_{\mathfrak{p}}
for all
[N]\in\mathscr{S}
and all
[L]\in\mathscr{T}_{s_{1}}
. By
[
8
, Theorem 3]
, there exists
c_{1}>0
such that every class of lattices in
\mathscr{T}_{s_{1}}
represents all classes
[N]
in
\mathscr{S}
provided
\mu_{1}(N)>c_{1}
. For each
[L]\in\mathscr{T}_{s_{1}}
, let
\mathfrak{q}(tL)
be the ideal of
\mathfrak{o}
satisfying Lemma
5.6
for the lattice
L
and the integer
t
, and let

\mathfrak{q}:=\prod_{[L]\in\mathscr{T}_{s_{1}}}\mathfrak{q}(tL).

Let
1\leq i\leq n-1
. Suppose that there exists
c_{i}\geq c_{1}
and
s_{i}\geq s_{1}
such that every class of lattices
[M]
in
\mathscr{T}_{s_{i}}
represents all
[N]\in\mathscr{S}
provided
\mu_{i}(N)>c_{i}
. Let

\mathscr{S}_{i}:=\{[N]\in\mathscr{S}:N\not\longrightarrow M\mbox{ for some }[M]\in\mathscr{T}_{s_{i}}\}.

Then, for every
[N]\in\mathscr{S}_{i}
,
\mu_{i}(N)\leq c_{i}
. So, the Gram matrix
(g_{ij})
of the Humbert linearly independent set of
N
satisfies

\textnormal{Tr}(g_{11})\leq\cdots\leq\textnormal{Tr}(g_{ii})\leq c_{i}.

Since every
g_{jj}
is totally positive, we must have only finitely many possibilities of
g_{11},\ldots,g_{ii}
. By (H3), we see that there are also finitely many possibilities of
g_{jk}
for any
1\leq j\leq i
and
1\leq k\leq n
. So,
\mathscr{S}_{i}
is partitioned into finitely many disjoint parts in such a way that if
\{\bm{v}_{1},\ldots,\bm{v}_{n}\}
and
\{\bm{v}_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,\bm{v}_{n}^{\prime}\}
are the Humbert linearly independent sets of two lattices from different classes in the same part, then their Gram matrices will satisfy

B(\bm{v}_{j},\bm{v}_{k})=B(\bm{v}_{j}^{\prime},\bm{v}_{k}^{\prime})\quad\mbox{ for }1\leq j\leq i\mbox{ and }1\leq k\leq n.

Each of these parts is the disjoint union of finitely many subsets such that all the lattices in the classes within any of these subsets are Humbert isomorphic. Let
\mathscr{X}
be one of these subsets. Let
[N]
and
[N^{\prime}]
be two classes in
\mathscr{X}
, and suppose
\{\bm{v}_{1},\ldots,\bm{v}_{n}\}
and
\{\bm{v}_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,\bm{v}_{n}^{\prime}\}
are the Humbert linearly independent sets of
N
and
N^{\prime}
, respectively. We write
[N]\sim[N^{\prime}]
when

B(\bm{v}_{j},\bm{v}_{k})\equiv B(\bm{v}_{j}^{\prime},\bm{v}_{k}^{\prime})\mod\mathfrak{q}\quad\mbox{ for }i+1\leq j\leq k\leq n.

Then,
\sim
is an equivalence relation on
\mathscr{X}
, and the resulting quotient set has only finitely many equivalence classes. Let
\mathscr{W}
be a complete set of representatives of these equivalence classes, which is a finite set, and let
a=a(\mathscr{X})
be an integer
\geq s_{i}
such that every class of lattices in
\mathscr{T}_{a}
represents all classes of lattices in
\mathscr{W}
.

Let
[N]\in\mathscr{X}
and
[\overline{N}]
be the class in
\mathscr{W}
such that
[N]\sim[\overline{N}]
, and let
\{\bm{v}_{1},\ldots,\bm{v}_{n}\}
and
\{\overline{\bm{v}}_{1},\ldots,\overline{\bm{v}}_{n}\}
be the Humbert linearly independent sets of
N
and
\overline{N}
, respectively. Let
A
be the lattice corresponding to the
(n-i)\times(n-i)
symmetric matrix
(B(\bm{v}_{j},\bm{v}_{k}))-(B(\overline{\bm{v}}_{j},\overline{\bm{v}}_{k}))
, where
j,k\in\{i+1,\ldots,n\}
. When
\mu_{i+1}(N)
is sufficiently large,
A
would be positive definite of rank
n-i
.

Let
[M]\in\mathscr{T}_{a}
. Then,
M
represents
\overline{N}
and
L
for all
[L]\in\mathscr{T}_{s_{1}}
. We may assume that both
\overline{N}
and
L
are sublattices of
M
and that the representations of
\overline{N}
and
L
into
M
are the natural inclusions. When
\mu_{i+1}(N)
is sufficiently large,
A
is positive definite and is represented by
\overline{N}^{\perp}\cap tL\subseteq\overline{N}^{\perp}\cap tM
by Lemma
5.6
and
[
8
, Theorem 3]
. Here,
\overline{N}^{\perp}
is the orthogonal complement of
\overline{N}
in
M
. In that case, there exist
\bm{w}_{i+1},\ldots,\bm{w}_{n}
in
\overline{N}^{\perp}\cap tM
such that

(B(\bm{w}_{j},\bm{w}_{k}))=B(\bm{v}_{j},\bm{v}_{k}))-(B(\overline{\bm{v}}_{j},\overline{\bm{v}}_{k})).

We set
\bm{w}_{j}=\bm{0}
for
j=1,\ldots,i
. Then, the map
\varphi:\sum\mathfrak{o}\bm{v}_{j}\longrightarrow M
defined by

\bm{v}_{j}\longmapsto\overline{\bm{v}}_{j}+\bm{w}_{j},\quad j=1,\ldots,n

is a representation from
\sum\mathfrak{o}\bm{v}_{j}
into
M
. Take
\bm{v}\in N
. Then,
t\bm{v}
is in
\sum\mathfrak{o}\bm{v}_{j}
, hence
\varphi(t\bm{v})=\overline{\bm{v}}+\bm{w}
where
\overline{\bm{v}}\in\sum\mathfrak{o}\overline{\bm{v}}_{j}
and
\bm{w}\in\overline{N}^{\perp}\cap tM
. Since
[N]
and
[\overline{N}]
are Humbert-isomorphic,
t^{-1}\overline{\bm{v}}
must be in
\overline{N}
. As for
\bm{w}
, it is in
tM
and hence
t^{-1}\bm{w}
is in
M
. Therefore,
\varphi(t\bm{v})
is in
tM
, rendering
\varphi(\bm{v})
in
M
. Consequently,
\varphi
is a representation from
N
into
M
.

We have demonstrated that there exists a positive integer
c(\mathscr{X})
such that if
