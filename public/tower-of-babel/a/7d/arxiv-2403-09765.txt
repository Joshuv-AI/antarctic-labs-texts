Perturbative description of high- T c T_{c} superconductivity in the Hubbard model via unitary transformation and classical spins
Kirill Alpin

Abstract
A unitary transformation is applied to the Hubbard model, which maps the Hubbard interaction to a single particle term. The resulting Hamiltonian consists of unconstrained fermions, which is then mapped to a Hamiltonian of spinless fermions coupled to pseudospins. The fermions are integrated out using second order perturbation theory in 1 / U 1/U , resulting in an effective spin Hamiltonian. An order parameter is identified, stabilizing d-wave superconductivity. The groundstate energy of classical spin configurations is minimized at a finite value of this order parameter after a critical chemical potential, resulting in d-wave superconductivity at non-zero doping. This suggests, that the onset of high- T c T_{c} superconductivity is governed by the groundstate of a classical spin system.



I
Introduction

The Hubbard model in the strong coupling regime is believed to describe the emergence of high-
T_{c}
superconductivity in cuprates
Anderson 1987
. The pairing in these compounds is further believed to be d-wave
Tsuei et al. 1994
. There have been numerous previous theoretical studies of the Hubbard model
Qin et al. 2022
;
Arovas et al. 2022
;
Scalapino 2012
. This paper shows a perturbative approach to observing this superconductivity in the strongly repulsive Hubbard model at finite doping. The first step, a unitary transformation, is similar to
Chowdhury et al. 2022
, where auxiliary spins are coupled to the original Hamiltonian via a unitary Schrieffer-Wolff transformation. Unlike this approach, here the unitary transformation is applied exactly. This results in a Hamiltonian with unconstrained fermions, unlike the t-J model
Lee et al. 2006
. The interaction terms now scale with
t,\mu
instead of
U
, so perturbative approaches in
1/U
are viable. This is also not given in the t-J model, where interactions are of the same order as the free part of the Hamiltonian, due to the correlated hopping terms. Therefore, in the t-J model, one must resort to uncontrolled slave-boson approaches
Lee et al. 2006
. The Hamiltonian is then mapped to a system of fermions coupled to pseudospins. In this form, unlike
Chowdhury et al. 2022
, the Hilbert space is not artificially expanded.
It is solved using variational second order perturbation theory and spins are replaced with classical spins. Beyond
MacDonald et al. 1988
, this perturbative expansion can be done at finite doping. An order parameter is identified, which stabilizes d-wave superconductivity. The groundstate energy as a function of this order parameter shows a second order phase transition at a critical chemical potential, resulting in the development of d-wave superconductivity. The flow of the approach described in this paper is shown schematically in Fig.
1
, which we also apply to the Hubbard model with
t^{\prime}=-0.3t
, showing characteristics of cuprate superconductivity.

Figure 1:
Flow diagram of the
1/U
perturbative approach described in this paper.

Notable similar approaches are ones based on strong coupling expansions
Pairault et al. 1998
;
Pairault et al. 2000
around the atomic limit of the Hubbard model. Methods like these, in which class for example Dynamical Mean Field Theory (DMFT)
Metzner and Vollhardt 1989
;
Georges and Kotliar 1992
;
Georges et al. 1996
, Self-Energy Functional Theory
Potthoff and Balzer 2007
and Cluster Perturbation Theory
Potthoff and Balzer 2007
;
Sénéchal et al. 2002
belong to, capture the local correlations of a given system, while it is a challenge to incorporate non-local self-energies
Maier et al. 2005
;
Rohringer et al. 2018
. The unitary transformation defined in this manuscript is applied exactly, so no correlations are lost there. The perturbative expansion following can in principle capture any non-locality, since no diagrams are discarded and this series can be carried out to higher order than the one in this paper, as it has been done in studies using DiagMC
Šimkovic et al. 2021
. Although there, the expansion is carried out in the weak coupling regime, while here we offer to do the same at strong coupling (using for example ordinary diagrammatics in the case of the Hamiltonian with unconstrained fermions). Other weak coupling expansions include
Šimkovic et al. 2016
;
Neumayr and Metzner 2003
.

II
Local unitary transformation

The square lattice repulsive Hubbard model with
U>0
and
t<0
is given by

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\bf r},{\bm{\delta}},\sigma}t(c^{\dagger}_{{\bf r},\sigma}c_{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}},\sigma}+c^{\dagger}_{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}},\sigma}c_{{\bf r},\sigma})

\displaystyle+\sum_{{\bf r}}U\left(n_{{{\bf r}},\uparrow}-\frac{1}{2}\right)\left(n_{{{\bf r}},\downarrow}-\frac{1}{2}\right)+\sum_{{\bf r},\sigma}\mu n_{{{\bf r}},\sigma}.

(1)

In this convention half filling is at
\mu=0
and the system is hole doped with
\mu>0
.
{\bm{\delta}}
runs over the two vectors
(1,0)
,
(0,1)
. We apply a unitary transformation

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}=\prod_{{\bf r}}U_{\bf r}

(2)

to the Hamiltonian (
1
), which decomposes into a series of local unitaries
U_{\bf r}
. We consider the following unitary

\displaystyle U_{\bf r}=1-n_{{\bf r},2}-n_{{\bf r},2}\bar{c}_{{\bf r},1}\tilde{c}_{{\bf r},3}

(3)

where we relabeled
c_{{\bf r},1}\equiv c_{{\bf r},\uparrow}
,
c_{{\bf r},2}\equiv c_{{\bf r},\downarrow}
. We have introduced a third fermion species
c_{{\bf r},3}
at every lattice site, which has no contribution in the original Hamiltonian (
1
). It does not contribute to any observables. This auxiliary fermion is necessary to construct a parity conserving unitary operation. Parity conservation is needed to form a local
U_{{\bf r}}
(a non-parity conserving version of this transformation, dropping the third fermion species, can still be used in 1D).

We used the following definitions in Eq. (
3
)

\displaystyle\bar{c}_{{\bf r},n}

\displaystyle\equiv c^{\dagger}_{{\bf r},n}-c_{{\bf r},n},

(4)

\displaystyle\tilde{c}_{{\bf r},n}

\displaystyle\equiv c^{\dagger}_{{\bf r},n}+c_{{\bf r},n}.

(5)

The unitary transformation (
3
) performs the following mapping from the original basis
\ket{\text{fermions},n_3}
to the
\ket{n_1,n_2,n_3}
basis

\displaystyle\ket{0,0}

\displaystyle\shortrightarrow\ket{0,0,0},\quad\ket{\uparrow,0}\shortrightarrow\ket{1,0,0},

(6)

\displaystyle\ket{\downarrow,0}

\displaystyle\shortrightarrow\ket{1,1,1},\quad\ket{\updownarrows,0}\shortrightarrow\ket{0,1,1},

(7)

\displaystyle\ket{0,1}

\displaystyle\shortrightarrow\ket{0,0,1},\quad\ket{\uparrow,1}\shortrightarrow\ket{1,0,1},

(8)

\displaystyle\ket{\downarrow,1}

\displaystyle\shortrightarrow\ket{1,1,0},\quad\ket{\updownarrows,1}\shortrightarrow\ket{0,1,0}.

(9)

Notice, that all, at large
U

0
occupancies by perturbation theory.
With

\displaystyle\bar{c}^{2}_{{\bf r},n}=-1,\quad\tilde{c}^{2}_{{\bf r},n}=1

(10)

we can show that
U^{\dagger}_{\bf r}=U_{\bf r}
and that
U^{\dagger}_{\bf r}U_{\bf r}=1=U^{2}_{\bf r}
. So
\mathcal{U}
is unitary
\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}\mathcal{U}=1
. Because
U_{\bf r}
is parity conserving, it commutes with itself at any other site (and on the same site)
[U_{\bf r},U_{\bf r^{\prime}}]=0
.

Applying

to the Hubbard interaction maps it to a single particle term

\displaystyle U_{\bf r}\left(n_{\uparrow}-\frac{1}{2}\right)\left(n_{\downarrow}-\frac{1}{2}\right)U_{\bf r}=\frac{1}{4}-\frac{1}{2}n_{{\bf r},1}

(11)

where we used
U^{\dagger}_{\bf r}=U_{\bf r}
.
The chemical potential term is mapped to

\displaystyle U_{\bf r}\sum_{\sigma}n_{{\bf r},\sigma}U_{\bf r}=n_{{\bf r},1}+2n_{{\bf r},2}(1-n_{{\bf r},1}).

(12)

Finally, the hopping term is mapped to

\displaystyle U_{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}}}U_{\bf r}\sum_{\sigma}(c^{\dagger}_{{\bf r},\sigma}c_{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}},\sigma}+c^{\dagger}_{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}},\sigma}c_{{\bf r},\sigma})U_{\bf r}U_{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}}}

(13)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle K_{{\bf r},{\bm{\delta}}}c^{\dagger}_{{\bf r},1}c^{\dagger}_{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}},1}+(1+A_{{\bf r},{\bm{\delta}}})c^{\dagger}_{{\bf r},1}c_{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}},1}+\text{h.c.}

(14)

with

\displaystyle K_{{\bf r},{\bm{\delta}}}=

\displaystyle-n_{{\bf r},2}+n_{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}},2}

\displaystyle-(c^{\dagger}_{{\bf r},2}c_{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}},2}+c^{\dagger}_{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}},2}c_{{\bf r},2})\tilde{c}_{{\bf r},3}\tilde{c}_{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}},3},

(15)

\displaystyle A_{{\bf r},{\bm{\delta}}}=

\displaystyle-n_{{\bf r},2}-n_{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}},2}

\displaystyle+(c^{\dagger}_{{\bf r},2}c_{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}},2}+c^{\dagger}_{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}},2}c_{{\bf r},2})\tilde{c}_{{\bf r},3}\tilde{c}_{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}},3}.

(16)

Let’s apply
\mathcal{U}
to the whole Hubbard model (
1
)

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}H\mathcal{U}

\displaystyle=\prod_{{\bf r}^{\prime}}U_{{\bf r}^{\prime}}H\prod_{{\bf r}}U_{\bf r}

(17)

\displaystyle=t\sum_{{\bf r},{\bm{\delta}},\sigma}U_{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}}}U_{\bf r}(c^{\dagger}_{{\bf r},\sigma}c_{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}},\sigma}+c^{\dagger}_{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}},\sigma}c_{{\bf r},\sigma})U_{\bf r}U_{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}}}

\displaystyle+U\sum_{{\bf r}}U_{\bf r}\left(n_{{{\bf r}},\uparrow}-\frac{1}{2}\right)\left(n_{{{\bf r}},\downarrow}-\frac{1}{2}\right)U_{\bf r}

\displaystyle+\mu\sum_{{\bf r},\sigma}U_{\bf r}n_{{{\bf r}},\sigma}U_{\bf r}.

(18)

The order of the application of
U_{{\bf r}}
in the two products above is not important, since
U_{{\bf r}}
commute. We further used that for any bosonic or fermionic operators
X_{\bf a},Y_{{\bf b}}
we have

\displaystyle\prod_{{\bf r}^{\prime}}U_{{\bf r}^{\prime}}X_{\bf a}Y_{{\bf b}}\prod_{{\bf r}}U_{{\bf r}}

(19)

\displaystyle=U_{{\bf b}}U_{\bf a}X_{\bf a}Y_{{\bf b}}U_{\bf a}U_{{\bf b}}

(20)

because
[X_{\bf a}Y_{{\bf b}},U_{\bf r}]=0
as long as
{\bf a}\neq{\bf r}
and
{\bf b}\neq{\bf r}
.

So the transformed Hamiltonian becomes

\displaystyle H^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}H\mathcal{U}

\displaystyle=t\sum_{{\bf r},{\bm{\delta}}}K_{{\bf r},{\bm{\delta}}}c^{\dagger}_{{\bf r},1}c^{\dagger}_{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}},1}+(1+A_{{\bf r},{\bm{\delta}}})c^{\dagger}_{{\bf r},1}c_{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}},1}+\text{h.c.}

\displaystyle+\sum_{{\bf r}}-\frac{U}{2}n_{{\bf r},1}+\mu(n_{{\bf r},1}+2n_{{\bf r},2}(1-n_{{\bf r},1})).

(21)

Since the Hubbard interaction is mapped to just a chemical potential of the fermion species 1, the interactions in
K_{{\bf r},{\bm{\delta}}}
and

now scale with
t
instead of
U
. Also notice, that, unlike the t-J model, Hamiltonian (

\mu\neq 0

Note that

commutes with any bond operator
i\bar{c}_{{\bf r},3}\bar{c}_{{\bf r}^{\prime},3}

Ultimately, we would like to compute observables in this Hamiltonian. To do so, we also need to transform those too. The transformed magnetization is

\displaystyle U_{\bf r}(n_{{\bf r},\uparrow}-n_{{\bf r},\downarrow})U_{\bf r}=n_{{\bf r},1}(1-2n_{{\bf r},2}).

(22)

The transformed particle density is

\displaystyle U_{\bf r}(n_{{\bf r},\uparrow}+n_{{\bf r},\downarrow})U_{\bf r}=2n_{{\bf r},2}(1-n_{{\bf r},1})+n_{{\bf r},1}.

(23)

A two site singlet pair operator transformed into the new basis is

\displaystyle\Delta=U_{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}}}U_{\bf r}\sum_{\sigma}(-1)^{\sigma}(c_{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}},\bar{\sigma}}c_{{\bf r},\sigma})U_{\bf r}U_{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}}}=

(24)

\displaystyle c_{{{\bf r}},1}c_{{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}}},1}(-c_{{{\bf r}},2}\tilde{c}_{{{\bf r}},3}+c_{{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}}},2}\tilde{c}_{{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}}},3}

\displaystyle-n_{{{\bf r}},2}c_{{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}}},2}\tilde{c}_{{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}}},3}+n_{{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}}},2}c_{{{\bf r}},2}\tilde{c}_{{{\bf r}},3})+

\displaystyle c^{\dagger}_{{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}}},1}c_{{{\bf r}},1}(c_{{{\bf r}},2}\tilde{c}_{{{\bf r}},3}+n_{{{\bf r}},2}c_{{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}}},2}\tilde{c}_{{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}}},3}-n_{{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}}},2}c_{{{\bf r}},2}\tilde{c}_{{{\bf r}},3})+

\displaystyle c^{\dagger}_{{{\bf r}},1}c_{{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}}},1}(c_{{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}}},2}\tilde{c}_{{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}}},3}-n_{{{\bf r}},2}c_{{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}}},2}\tilde{c}_{{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}}},3}+n_{{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}}},2}c_{{{\bf r}},2}\tilde{c}_{{{\bf r}},3})+

\displaystyle c^{\dagger}_{{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}}},1}c^{\dagger}_{{{\bf r}},1}(n_{{{\bf r}},2}c_{{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}}},2}\tilde{c}_{{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}}},3}-n_{{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}}},2}c_{{{\bf r}},2}\tilde{c}_{{{\bf r}},3})

with

,

III
Perturbation theory

In the groundstate and at large
U
and
\mu=0
, the fermion species 1 is, due to the

chemical potential, fully occupied. In this limit, we can therefore perform perturbation theory around
\ket{\Psi}=\prod_{{\bf r}}c^{\dagger}_{{{\bf r}},1}\ket{0}

The first order is zero. Second order is

\displaystyle H_{2}=-\frac{1}{U}\bra{\Psi}H^{\prime 2}\ket{\Psi}.

The factor

\displaystyle-\frac{2t^{2}}{U}\sum_{{\bf r},{\bm{\delta}}}n_{{{\bf r}},2}+n_{{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}}},2}-2n_{{{\bf r}},2}n_{{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}}},2}

We find

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle[b^{\dagger}_{{\bf r}},b^{\dagger}_{{\bf r}^{\prime}}]

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle[b_{{\bf r}},b^{\dagger}_{{\bf r}^{\prime}}]

\displaystyle=(1-2b^{\dagger}_{{\bf r}}b_{{{\bf r}}})\delta_{{{\bf r}},{\bf r}^{\prime}}.

n_{{{\bf r}},2}
can be written in

\displaystyle n_{{{\bf r}},2}=

\displaystyle c^{\dagger}_{{{\bf r}},2}c_{{{\bf r}},2}=c^{\dagger}_{{{\bf r}},2}c_{{{\bf r}},2}\tilde{c}^{2}_{{{\bf r}},3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tilde{c}_{{{\bf r}},3}c^{\dagger}_{{{\bf r}},2}c_{{{\bf r}},2}\tilde{c}_{{{\bf r}},3}=b^{\dagger}_{{\bf r}}b_{{\bf r}}=n_{{{\bf r}},b}

where we used

\displaystyle H_{2}=

\displaystyle-\frac{2t^{2}}{U}\sum_{{\bf r},{\bm{\delta}}}n_{{{\bf r}},b}+n_{{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}}},b}-2n_{{{\bf r}},b}n_{{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}}},b}

\displaystyle-b^{\dagger}_{{{\bf r}},2}b_{{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}}},2}-b^{\dagger}_{{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}}},2}b_{{{\bf r}},2}.

\displaystyle S^{+}_{{\bf r}}=

\displaystyle b_{{\bf r}}\quad S^{-}_{{\bf r}}=b^{\dagger}_{{\bf r}}

(35)

\displaystyle S^{z}_{{\bf r}}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}-b^{\dagger}_{{\bf r}}b_{{\bf r}}

\displaystyle S^{x}_{{\bf r}}=

IV
Variational perturbation theory at finite doping

In this section, we perform perturbation theory at finite
\mu
with the goal of calculating the expectation value of
\Delta
. In terms of hardcore bosons, the
\Delta
order parameter in Eq. (
25
) transforms to

\displaystyle\Delta=c_{{{\bf r}}}c_{{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}}}}(-b_{{\bf r}}+b_{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}}}

\displaystyle-n_{{{\bf r}},b}b_{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}}}+n_{{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}}},b}b_{{\bf r}})+

\displaystyle c^{\dagger}_{{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}}}}c_{{{\bf r}}}(b_{{\bf r}}+n_{{{\bf r}},b}b_{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}}}-n_{{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}}},b}b_{{\bf r}})+

\displaystyle c^{\dagger}_{{{\bf r}}}c_{{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}}}}(b_{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}}}-n_{{{\bf r}},b}b_{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}}}+n_{{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}}},b}b_{{\bf r}})+

\displaystyle c^{\dagger}_{{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}}}}c^{\dagger}_{{{\bf r}}}(n_{{{\bf r}},b}b_{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}}}-n_{{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}}},b}b_{{\bf r}}).

(40)

Hardcore bosons can be expressed as spin operators, which in turn reduces the singlet order parameter
\Delta
to a spin wave order parameter. Note, that an uneven number of hardcore boson operators are present in
\Delta
. When the expectation value of
\Delta
is computed, even at first order in perturbation, an uneven number of hardcore boson operators remain. Translated into the spin picture, this means that to have a finite expectation value of
\Delta
, that is superconductivity in any way, pseudospin U(1) symmetry must be broken. This U(1) symmetry breaking can also be seen when
\Delta
is multiplied with a global U(1) gauge of the original fermionic operators
c_{{{\bf r}},\sigma}\rightarrow\exp(i\theta/2)c_{{{\bf r}},\sigma}
. The only place where this phase can consistently be absorbed to are the
b_{{\bf r}}
operators. A phase in

translates to rotations around the z-axis of the pseudospins
\cos(\theta)S_{x}+\sin(\theta)S_{y}
, see Eq. (
35
). So charge
U(1)
symmetry is transformed using the local unitary transformation (
3
) to a pseudospin
U(1)
symmetry, which we expect to be broken in a superconductor. We can transform the Hamiltonian (
21
) into one containing hardcore bosons

\displaystyle H^{\prime}

\displaystyle=t\sum_{{\bf r},{\bm{\delta}}}K_{{\bf r},{\bm{\delta}}}c^{\dagger}_{{\bf r}}c^{\dagger}_{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}}}+(1+A_{{\bf r},{\bm{\delta}}})c^{\dagger}_{{\bf r}}c_{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}}}+\text{h.c.}

\displaystyle+\sum_{{\bf r}}-\frac{U}{2}n_{{\bf r}}+\mu(n_{{\bf r}}+2n_{{\bf r},b}(1-n_{{\bf r}})).

(41)

We dropped the 1 index for the
c_{1}
fermions. The

\displaystyle K_{{{\bf r}},{\bm{\delta}}}

\displaystyle=-n_{{{\bf r}},b}+n_{{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}}},b}-b^{\dagger}_{{\bf r}}b_{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}}}+b^{\dagger}_{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}}}b_{{\bf r}}

(42)

\displaystyle A_{{{\bf r}},{\bm{\delta}}}

\displaystyle=-n_{{{\bf r}},b}-n_{{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}}},b}+b^{\dagger}_{{\bf r}}b_{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}}}-b^{\dagger}_{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}}}b_{{\bf r}}

(43)

Fourier transforming everything, we end up with the following interaction vertices

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\bm{\delta}}}tK_{{\bf r},{\bm{\delta}}}c^{\dagger}_{{\bf r}}c^{\dagger}_{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}}}+\text{h.c.}

(44)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}0&e^{i{{\bf k}}_{4}{\bm{\delta}}}e^{-i{{\bf k}}_{1}{\bm{\delta}}_{1}+i{{\bf k}}_{2}{\bm{\delta}}_{2}}\\

\displaystyle{\bf c}_{{{\bf k}}}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}c_{{{\bf k}}}\\

(48)

We dropped the sum over
{{\bf r}}
and
{{\bf k}}_{n}
and the momentum conserving
\delta({{\bf k}}_{a}+{{\bf k}}_{b}+...)

We continue to perform second order perturbation theory on this Hamiltonian at finite
\mu
, to derive an effective hardcore boson and therefore pseudospin Hamiltonian. We note here, that there is in general an arbitrariness in how one can perform perturbation theory. Usually, perturbations around
H_{0}
only contain the interaction terms
V
of the Hamiltonian
H=H_{0}+V
. In principle it is possible to add and subract a non-interacting term
V_{\lambda}
like so
H=H_{0}-V_{\lambda}+V+V_{\lambda}
. One can define a new
H_{0}^{\prime}=H_{0}-V_{\lambda}
and perturbation around it
V^{\prime}=V+V_{\lambda}
. This way it is possible to describe symmetry broken phases using a perturbative expansion, as long as
V_{\lambda}
is much smaller than
H_{0}
. The right
V_{\lambda}
is chosen variationally. A similar perturbative approach has been applied directly to the Hubbard model at weak coupling in
Neumayr and Metzner 2003
, where it was possible to optimize the introduced fields via a self-consistency equation and where a finite d-wave superconductive field has been found at non-zero doping.

One of the terms we vary between
H_{0}
and
V_{\lambda}
is the hopping term
tc^{\dagger}_{{\bf r}}c_{{\bf r}+{\bm{\delta}}}+\text{h.c.}
Without variational perturbation theory (VPT), it is unclear if one has to put this term in
H_{0}
or
V^{\prime}
, as it is of order
t
while still being a free particle term.

Before going on to the results of VPT, we describe in the following the way VPT is implemented. We first define
V_{\lambda=0}=0
, and
H_{0}^{\prime}=H_{0}(\lambda)
such that
H_{0}(\lambda=0)=H_{0}
. The zero and first order terms are

\displaystyle H_{f}=\bra{\Psi(\lambda)}H_{0}(\lambda=0)+V_{K}+V_{A+\mu}\ket{\Psi(\lambda)}

(49)

where
\ket{\Psi(\lambda)}
is the groundstate of
H_{0}(\lambda)
. In second order we must compute

\displaystyle\bra{\Psi(\lambda)}V\frac{1}{E_{0}(\lambda)-H_{0}(\lambda)}V\ket{\Psi(\lambda)}

(50)

where
V=V_{K}+V_{A+\mu}+V_{\lambda}
and where we only consider connected diagrams, such that we can drop the projection operators to excited states. Due to the structure of all of the present vertices, the computation of the second order terms reduce to the evaluation of

\displaystyle\frac{1}{E_{0}(\lambda)-H_{0}(\lambda)}({\bf A}\cdot{\bf c}^{\dagger}_{{{\bf k}}})({\bf B}\cdot{\bf c}_{{{\bf k}}^{\prime}})\ket{\Psi(\lambda)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{E_{0}(\lambda)-H_{0}(\lambda)}({\bf A}\sigma_{x}\cdot{\bf c}_{-{{\bf k}}})({\bf B}\cdot{\bf c}_{{{\bf k}}^{\prime}})\ket{\Psi(\lambda)}

V
Discussion

In this paper, we propose a new theoretical description of high temperature superconductivity, based on the application of a unitary transformation to the Hubbard model, which maps the Hubbard interaction to a single particle term. This unitary transformation can be applied exactly. The resulting Hamiltonian contains, unlike the t-J model, unconstrained fermions and can be transformed into one with a single fermion species coupled to hardcore bosons/pseudospins, which suggests that this transformation implements a spin charge separation in any dimension, where the fermions carry charge and the pseudospins spin information. The Hamiltonian is solved by variational second order perturbation theory (VPT), resulting in an effective pseudospin Hamiltonian. The problem of fermions coupled to spins generating an effective spin-spin interaction is reminiscent of the RKKY mechanism
Ruderman and Kittel 1954
. The breakdown of the phases found in this paper might be equivalent to a Kondo-RKKY transition. To compute the groundstate energy, the spins are replaced by classical spins and their classical energy is minimized. We find that in zero order, the introduction of a chemical potential
\mu
acts as a magnetic field for the pseudospins, breaking
U(1)
symmetry. Mapping back to the original system, this
U(1)
symmetry breaking corresponds to the
U(1)
symmetry breaking found in a superconducting state, assuming the superconducting state is the one minimizing the free energy. An order parameter is proposed which at the same time fills in holes into the system and breaks
C_{4}
symmetry. The free energy is minimized at a finite value of this order parameter, resulting in a finite expectation value of the d-wave superconductor pairing operator. This perturbative approach is possible, because the fermionic modes that are integrated out are strongly gapped with a gap of
U/2
. We therefore encourage further studies of Hamiltonians (
21
) or (
41
) with non-perturbative calculations (for example DMFT
Metzner and Vollhardt 1989
;
Georges and Kotliar 1992
;
Georges et al. 1996
or DMRG
White 1992
), which should even increase the accuracy of the presented results. Future work also involves exploring similar unitary transformations like (
3
), for example ones that map a whole cluster of sites to single particle terms. It can also be interesting to vary different and more variational parameters than just one in VPT, like the chemical potential. It is also possible to connect auxiliary bath sites with VPT. Exploring the influence of quantum fluctuations around the classical spin groundstate found in this paper should also give more insights. This is especially important for studying magnetism in the models found in this paper, since classical spins can not describe paramagnetic states at
T=0
, which is why we did not explore magnetism here. In light of numerous potential paths for future work, this paper already suggests that the emergence of high-
T_{c}
superconductivity is governed by the ground state of a classical spin system, showcasing promising directions for further research.
