Abstract
. The initial-boundary value problem in a bounded domain with moving boundaries and nonhomogeneous boundary conditions for a higher order nonlinear Schrödinger (HNLS) equation is considered. Existence and uniqueness of global weak solutions are proved as well as the stability of the solution. Additionally, a conservative numerical method of finite differences is introduced that also verifies stability properties with respect to the L 2 L^{2} -norm, and along with proving its convergence, some interesting numerical examples are shown that illustrate the behavior of the solution.



Keywords
: Higher order nonlinear Schrödinger equation, Moving boundary,
Conservative Finite Difference Method.

1.
Introduction

In this work we study the existence of the weak solution as well as the asymptotic behaviour,
and numerical approximation of three-order nonlinear Schrödinger equation (HNLS) in a bounded domain with moving boundary. Indeed, for real
\tau\geq 0,
let
I_{\tau}
be time-moving interval:

I_{\tau}=\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}:\,\alpha(\tau)<\xi<\beta(\tau)\}
and
Q_{\tau}
denote bounded domain with moving boundaries:

Q_{T}=\{(\xi,\,\tau)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\xi\in I_{\tau},\,0<\tau<T\}.
In
Q_{T}
we consider the higher order nonlinear Schrödinger equation

(1.1)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}iv_{\tau}+\gamma v_{\xi\xi}+i\chi v_{\xi\xi\xi}=|v|^{2}v\quad\mbox{in }Q_{T},\\
v(\xi,\,0)=v_{0}(\xi)\quad\mbox{in}\quad I_{0},\\
v(\alpha(\tau),\,\tau)=v(\beta(\tau),\,\tau)=v_{\xi}(\alpha(\tau),\,\tau)=v_{\xi}(\beta(\tau),\,\tau)=0,\\
v_{\xi\xi}(\alpha(\tau),\,\tau)=v_{\xi\xi}(\beta(\tau),\,\tau)=v_{\xi\xi\xi}(\alpha(\tau),\,\tau)=v_{\xi\xi\xi}(\beta(\tau),\,\tau)=0,\\
\end{cases}

where
\gamma
and
\chi
are real positive constants and
i^{2}=-1.

Moreover, we assume that there exists
\alpha_{0}
and
\beta_{0}
such that

(1.2)

\displaystyle 0<\alpha_{0}\leq\beta(\tau)-\alpha(\tau)\leq\beta_{0}<+\infty,\quad\forall\;\tau\geq 0.

The lateral boundary
\Sigma_{\tau}
of
Q_{\tau}
is given by

(1.3)

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\tau}=\bigcup_{0<\tau<T}\left(\alpha(\tau)\times\{\tau\}\right)\cup\left(\beta(\tau)\times\{\tau\}\right).

The moving boundary and initial value problem
(
1.1
)
is motivated by the generalized HNLS Cauchy problem

(1.4)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}iv_{t}+\gamma v_{xx}+i\chi v_{xxx}+\vartheta|v|^{2}v+i\delta|v|^{2}v_{x}+i\varepsilon v^{2}\overline{v}_{x}=0,\quad x,\ t\in\mathbb{R}\\
v(x,\,0)=v_{0}(x)\end{cases}

where
\gamma,

\chi,

\vartheta,
and
\delta
are real numbers with
\chi\neq 0.
This equation was first proposed Hasegawa
et al.

[
21
]
as a model for the propagation of a signal in an optical fiber (see also
[
26
]
). The equation given above (
1.4
) can be reduced to other well-known equations. For instance, setting

\chi=\delta=\varepsilon=0
we have the classical cubic semilinear Schrödinger equation, that is,

(1.5)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}iv_{t}+\gamma v_{xx}+\vartheta|v|^{2}v=0,\quad x,\ t\in\mathbb{R}\\
v(x,\,0)=v_{0}(x).\end{cases}

If
\gamma=1,

\chi=\vartheta=0
we have the derivative Schrödinger equation, that is,

(1.6)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}iv_{t}+v_{xx}+i\delta|v|^{2}v_{x}+i\varepsilon v^{2}\overline{v}_{x}=0,\quad x,\ t\in\mathbb{R}\\
v(x,\,0)=v_{0}(x).\end{cases}

This equation (
1.6
) is encountered in plasma physics (propagation of Alfven and radio waves)
[
13
]
.
Letting
\gamma=\vartheta=\varepsilon=0
the equation that arises is the
Korteweg-de Vries equation, this is,

(1.7)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}v_{t}+\chi v_{xx}+\delta|v|^{2}v_{x}=0\quad x,\ t\in\mathbb{R}\\
v(x,\,0)=v_{0}(x).\end{cases}

The initial value problem for the equations (
1.5
), (
1.6
) and (
1.7
) has been extensively studied in the last few years. See, for instance,
[
4
,
5
,
8
]
and references therein. In 1992, Laurey
[
28
]
considered the equation (
1.4
) and proved local well-posedness of the initial value problem associated for data in
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}),

s>3/4,
and global well-posedness in
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}),

s\geq 3/4.
In 1997, Staffilani
[
32
]
for (
1.4
) established local well-posedness for data in
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}),

s\geq 1/4
improving Laurey’s result. A similar result was given in
[
9
]
with
w(t),\,\gamma(t)
real functions.

Applying the Gauge transform to the HNLS equation on the real line (
1.4
), the
u_{xx}
term can be removed, and the equation becomes a modified complex Korteweg-de Vries
[
1
]
. In the case of our equation with moving boundary (
1.1
), the Gauge transform as a bi-continuous map from
L^{p}([0,\,T]:H^{s}(Q_{\tau})
to
L^{p}([0,\,T]:H^{s}(\widetilde{Q}_{t})
, allows us to obtain a semilinear Airy equation with a complex nonlinear reaction term and boundary conditions in another moving boundary domain, which is not necessarily simpler than the original equation (
1.1
) (see appendix A).
Thus, this one of the motivations to continue this study for the equations (
1.1
).

In this paper, we study initial-boundary value problem for the higher order nonlinear problem equation (
1.1
) in a bounded domain with moving boundaries and nonhomogeneous boundary conditions. Following the idea of
transforming the moving boundary given by Doronin and Larkin
[
16
]

(see also
[
6
,
34
]
),
we study the HNLS equation in domains with moving boundaries where now the function
v
is a complex valued function. In our case, the aim of this work is to establish the well-posedness of the system with moving boundaries as well as the exponential decay of small solutions in asymptotically cylindrical domains accompanied by numerical results. The most common way to treat this kind of problems is to transform the domain with moving boundaries into a cylindrical one. After the transformation of the domain, we obtain a HNLS equation type with coefficients depending on the space and time variables. Then, we study the existence, uniqueness and stability for this new problem and we give some numerical examples. For this, we consider a
Crank-Nicolson
finite difference scheme based on
conservation laws
similar to the one described in

[
10
,
11
,
12
]
.
This method is originally inspired by L2 norm-preserving, stable, and convergent schemes for dispersive equations

[
15
,
31
]
for the KdV and the KdV–Kawahara equations. Our method is different from the one presented in
[
19
,
20
]
where the authors used Fourier transform for the x-variable. The advantage of our method is that it is better adapted to our nonperiodic boundary conditions, and to the treatment of the system with variable coefficients in the cylindrical domain given by the problem with moving boundaries.

Our paper is organized as follows: In Section 2 we transform the moving boundary domain into the cylindrical one. In Section 3 and 4 we obtain the existence and uniqueness of solutions to the model. in Section 5 we analyze the solution of (
1.1
) using suitable multiplier techniques. Finally in Section 6 a numerical scheme is given.

3.
Existence of solutions

In the previous Section we have transformed a domain with moving boundaries into cylindrical one. Now we will apply the Faedo-Galerkin method in the transformed mixed problem.

For this we study the regularized problem with a spatial basis and we prove the existence of global solutions for this regularization. This regularization procedure is classical adding a dissipative term of fourth order for a KdV type equation (see
[

6.
Numerical Scheme

6.1.
Notation.

For the sake of the following analysis, and for a given
M\in\mathbb{N}
, we will introduce the vector space

X_{M}:=\big\{u=[u_{0}\>u_{1}\>\dots\>u_{M}]^{T}\in\mathbb{C}^{M+1}:u_{0}=u_{M-1}=u_{M}=0\big\}

Let us introduce the classical finite differences operators for complex-valued arrays:

\displaystyle\big[\boldsymbol{D}^{+}u\big]_{j}=D^{+}u_{j}

\displaystyle:=\dfrac{u_{j+1}-u_{j}}{\Delta x}

\displaystyle\big[\boldsymbol{D}^{-}u\big]_{j}=D^{-}u_{j}

\displaystyle:=\dfrac{u_{j}-u_{j-1}}{\Delta x}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{D}_{x}u

\displaystyle:=\dfrac{1}{2}\Big(\boldsymbol{D}^{+}u+\boldsymbol{D}^{-}u\Big)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{D}_{x}^{2}u

\displaystyle:=\boldsymbol{D}^{+}\boldsymbol{D}^{-}u

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{D}_{x}^{3}u

\displaystyle:=\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{D}^{+}\boldsymbol{D}^{-}u.

6.2.
Foundations of the Numerical Scheme.

For a given
\Delta t<1
,
t_{n}=n\Delta t
, and for
u^{n}\in X_{M}
, our numerical scheme is inspired in the discretization of
(
3.1
), and it is defined as follows:

i\dfrac{u^{n+1}-u^{n}}{\Delta t}+i\xi\boldsymbol{p}^{3}(t_{n})\boldsymbol{D}_{x}^{3}u^{n+\frac{1}{2}}+\gamma\boldsymbol{p}^{2}(t_{n})\boldsymbol{D}_{x}^{2}u^{n+\frac{1}{2}}+i\boldsymbol{I}^{n}\boldsymbol{D}u^{n}-|u^{n+\frac{1}{2}}|^{2}u^{n+\frac{1}{2}}=i\boldsymbol{J}^{n+\frac{1}{2}},

where
\boldsymbol{J}^{n}\in X_{M}:[\boldsymbol{J}^{n}]_{i}=J(x_{i},t_{n})
, while
\boldsymbol{I}^{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
such that

[\boldsymbol{I}^{n}]_{ij}=\begin{cases}I(x_{i},t_{n}),\quad\text{ if }i=j\\
0\quad\text{ in other case. }\end{cases}

As this is a nonlinear problem, we will find approximate solutions using a Picard Fixed Point iteration. Re-arranging terms and using matrix notation, we get

(6.3)

\begin{matrix}\ \big[\boldsymbol{Id}-2i\Delta t\xi\boldsymbol{p}^{3}(t_{n})\boldsymbol{D}^{3}-2i\Delta t\gamma\boldsymbol{p}^{2}(t_{n})\boldsymbol{D}^{2}\big]u^{n+1}\\
=\big[\boldsymbol{Id}-2i\Delta t\xi\boldsymbol{p}^{3}(t_{n})\boldsymbol{D}^{3}+2i\Delta t\gamma\boldsymbol{p}^{2}(t_{n})\boldsymbol{D}^{2}-2\Delta t\boldsymbol{I}^{n}\boldsymbol{D}\big]u^{n}-2i\Delta t|u^{n+\frac{1}{2}}|^{2}u^{n+\frac{1}{2}}+2\Delta t\boldsymbol{J}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}.\end{matrix}

where
\boldsymbol{D}
,
\boldsymbol{D}^{2}
and
\boldsymbol{D}^{3}
are matrices in
\mathbb{R}^{2M+1\times 2M+1}
operating over complex-valued vectors, while
\boldsymbol{I}
is the identity matrix. Due to the boundary conditions, we have

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{D}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\Delta x}\left[\begin{matrix}0&1&&&\\
-1&0&\ddots&\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\\
&&\ddots&0&1\\
&&&-1&0\end{matrix}\right]

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{D}^{2}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\Delta x^{2}}\left[\begin{matrix}-2&1&&&\\
1&-2&\ddots&\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\\
&&\ddots&-2&1\\
&&&1&-2\end{matrix}\right]

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{D}^{3}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\Delta x^{3}}\left[\begin{matrix}0&-1&\frac{1}{2}&&&&\\
1&0&-1&\frac{1}{2}&&&\\
-\frac{1}{2}&1&0&-1&\frac{1}{2}&\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\\
&&-\frac{1}{2}&1&0&-1&\frac{1}{2}\\
&&&-\frac{1}{2}&1&0&-1\\
&&&&-\frac{1}{2}&1&0\end{matrix}\right]

To compute the numerical solution, we will use a fixed-point method in order to solve equation (
6.3
) for each time-step. As in Delfour, Fortin and Payre, for a
u^{p=1}=u^{n}\in X_{M}
given, we compute a sequence of complex vectors
\{u^{p}\},p=2,3,4,\dots,
until a stopping criteria is verified. The sequence is given by

(6.4)

\begin{matrix}\ \big[\boldsymbol{Id}-2i\Delta t\xi\boldsymbol{p}^{3}(t_{n})\boldsymbol{D}^{3}_{x}-2i\Delta t\gamma\boldsymbol{p}^{2}(t_{n})\boldsymbol{D}^{2}_{x}\big]u^{p}\\
=\big[\boldsymbol{Id}-2i\Delta t\xi\boldsymbol{p}^{3}(t_{n})\boldsymbol{D}^{3}_{x}+2i\Delta t\gamma\boldsymbol{p}^{2}(t_{n})\boldsymbol{D}^{2}_{x}-2\Delta t\boldsymbol{I}^{n}\boldsymbol{D}\big]u^{n}-2i\Delta t\Big|\frac{u^{p}+u^{n}}{2}\Big|^{2}\frac{u^{p}+u^{n}}{2}+2\Delta t\frac{\boldsymbol{J}^{p}+\boldsymbol{J}^{n}}{2}\end{matrix}

In other words, we have to solve a linear system of equations many times per timestep until a stopping criterion is fulfilled, where the matrix to be inverted has a pentadiagonal structure. The fixed point scheme can stop by two reasons: one, if we have

||u^{p}-u^{p-1}||_{2}<\hat{\delta}

where
\hat{\delta}
is given. In our computations:
\hat{\delta}=10^{-14}
. In that case, we do
u^{n+1}=u^{p}
and then proceed to the next timestep. The second reason used to stop the iteration is if (
6.4
) is not a contraction anymore; that is,

\frac{||u^{p}-u^{p-1}||_{2}}{||u^{p-1}-u^{p-2}||_{2}}\geq 1

In that case the scheme cannot guarantee the uniqueness of the numerical solution, and thus, the computation ends with no output. The scheme has linear convergence for
\Delta t
sufficiently small.

The function that transforms
u(x,t)
to
v(\xi,\tau)
is used only when we need to plot the numerical solution.

D_{t}u(x_{k},t_{n})=u_{t}(x_{k},t_{n})+{\mathcal{O}}(\Delta t),\quad D^{2}_{x}u(x_{k},t_{n})=u_{xx}(x_{k},t_{n})+{\mathcal{O}}(\Delta x^{2})

D^{3}_{x}u(x_{k},t_{n})=u_{xxx}(x_{k},t_{n})+{\mathcal{O}}(\Delta x^{2}),\quad u(x_{k},t_{n+\frac{1}{2}})=u(x_{k},t_{n})+{\mathcal{O}}(\Delta t).

Thus, at
x_{k}=x_{0}+k\Delta x
we have

\begin{matrix}iu_{t}(x_{k},t_{n})+i\xi\boldsymbol{p}^{3}(t_{n})u_{xxx}(x_{k},t_{n})+\gamma\boldsymbol{p}^{2}(t_{n})u_{xx}(x_{k},t_{n})+i\boldsymbol{I}^{n}u_{x}(x_{k},t_{n})-|u(x_{k},t_{n+\frac{1}{2}})|^{2}u(x_{k},t_{n+\frac{1}{2}})\\
=i\boldsymbol{J}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}+F_{k}^{n}+{\mathcal{O}}(\Delta t+\Delta x^{2})\end{matrix}

Thus, we get

|F_{k}^{n}|={\mathcal{O}}(\Delta t+\Delta x^{2})

multiplying by
\Delta x
and summing for
k
we get the result.
