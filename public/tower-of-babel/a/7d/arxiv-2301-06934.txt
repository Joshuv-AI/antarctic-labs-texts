Bessel functions on G ​ L ​ ( n ) GL(n) , I
Jack Buttcane

Abstract
. In the context of the Kuznetsov trace formula, we outline the theory of the Bessel functions on G ​ L ​ ( n ) GL(n) as a series of conjectures designed as a blueprint for the construction of Kuznetsov-type formulas with given ramification at infinity. We are able to prove one of the conjectures at full generality on G ​ L ​ ( n ) GL(n) and most of the conjectures in the particular case of the long Weyl element; as with previous papers, we give some unconditional results on Archimedean Whittaker functions, now on G ​ L ​ ( n ) GL(n) with arbitrary weight. We expect the heuristics here to apply at the level of real reductive groups. A forthcoming paper will address the initial conjectures up to Mellin-Barnes integral representations in the case of G ​ L ​ ( 4 ) GL(4) Bessel functions.

1.
Introduction

Bessel functions appeared in a scattering of topics in analysis and physics prior to their systematic study by Bessel
[
47
]
.
Their introduction to number theory also follows several avenues:
The oldest the author is aware of is the work of Voronoi where the Bessel functions
K_{0}
and
Y_{0}
appear in his summation formula
[
45
]
.
The Bessel functions occur in the theory of automorphic forms as the Fourier coefficients of Maass forms for
SL(2,\mathbb{Z})
are classical Whittaker functions and in the spherical case, these are just the
K
-Bessel functions.
Lastly, they are found in the trace formulas of Petersson
[
38
]
and Kuznetsov
[
31
]
.
These functions are characterized either by integral formulas which happen to produce the functions that Bessel studied or by asymptotics and differential equations that can be manipulated into the Bessel differential equation.

The generalization of Maass forms to different groups leads to new meanings of the term “Fourier coefficient”, but the (properly defined) Fourier coefficients of automorphic forms typically still satisfy asymptotics and differential equations whose solutions we also call “Whittaker functions”.
The Voronoi formula can be generalized to any set of generating functions having some sort of functional equation and the kernel functions occurring in such a formula are typically referred to as Bessel functions.
The Petersson and Kuznetsov formulas can be viewed as special cases of a Kuznetsov formula over Maass forms with weight, and this can be generalized to Kuznetsov-type formulas on other groups (or more broadly, to Jacquet’s relative trace formulas
[
27
]
); again, we will refer to the (conjectural) kernel functions in such formulas as Bessel functions.
For automorphic forms on a given group, the spherical Whittaker function should turn out to be among the Kuznetsov kernel functions, as should the Voronoi Bessel functions.
The functions that we propose to study here are the Kuznetsov kernel functions for the group
GL(n,\mathbb{R})
.

Recent developments in number theory frequently use deep asymptotics for the Voronoi and Kuznetsov-type Bessel functions and stationary phase arguments in the integral transforms.
Currently, we have excellent understanding of the Bessel functions on
GL(2)
(i.e. the classical Bessel functions) for example, in Olver’s work
[
37
]
.
Even for
GL(3)
, construction of an Olver-type asymptotic expansion (which holds across a wide range of parameters) is dizzying to think about, but eventually one would like to characterize the behavior of the functions in every region of the parameter space.
However, the author’s work on
GL(3)
does give us some understanding of the Bessel functions on that group, and the purpose of the current paper is to provide a blueprint for extending this to other groups.
In particular, we certainly expect that these ideas continue to hold at the level of real reductive groups, but we restrict our attention to the case of
GL(n,\mathbb{R})
because the author is simply not well-versed in that language.
The paper is reasonably self-contained without reliance on the author’s previous works and effort has been made to isolate the interactions with representation theory.

The process developed by the author for the
GL(3)
Kuznetsov formula was:

1.

Show that the integral transforms are given by kernel integrals; this is the
Interchange of Integrals Conjecture
.

2.

Show that the Bessel functions satisfy certain differential equations and symmetries, have predictable asymptotics and these properties uniquely characterize them; these are the
Differential Equations and Power Series Conjecture
and
Asymptotics Theorem
.

3.

Solve the differential equations to obtain power series solutions and use the power series solutions and/or the defining properties to obtain useful integral representations of the Bessel functions.

One may regard this paper as studying a class of Bessel functions and the
Interchange of Integrals Conjecture
would imply these are the kernel functions in the
GL(n)
Kuznetsov formulas.
Crucially, we won’t prove the
Interchange of Integrals Conjecture
here, but we do prove the basic properties of the Bessel functions, especially in the case of the long Weyl element and in a forthcoming paper, we will construct the
GL(4)
Bessel functions.

The full detail of the conjectures and what we can prove is laid out in
section

4
.
We generally limit the scope to those properties associated with the construction of spectral-type Kuznetsov formulas as in
section

3
, but we do briefly discuss arithmetic-type Kuznetsov formulas as well as integral representations of the Bessel functions and other applications in
section

5
.

One new ingredient in this paper, over the author’s work on
GL(3)
, is the realization that a slightly stronger version of the
Interchange of Integrals Conjecture
would imply the Bessel functions at higher weight (i.e. attached to strict subrepresentations of principal series representations attached to characters of the minimal parabolic) are given by analytic continuation from the principal series representations, and this removes the need to explicitly construct series and integral representations for the higher-weight cases.
The
Strong Interchange of Integrals Conjecture
is somewhat technical to state and is given in
section

10
; along the way we prove some new results about
GL(n)
Whittaker functions in
section

9
, and both of these things follow from matrix decompositions given in
section

8
.
Mathematica code for the decompositions is provided in
appendix

A
.

Assuming the
Interchange of Integrals
and
Differential Equations and Power Series
Conjectures, we are able to determine the “small
y
” asymptotics (i.e. the leading terms of the power series) of the Bessel functions in general; this is the
Asymptotics Theorem
, which is proved in
section

11
.
Finally, under the
Interchange of Integrals Conjecture
, we are able to say quite a bit about the Bessel function attached to the long Weyl element, and these results are proved in
section

12
.

The corresponding theory at the finite places is laid out in general in
[
4
, Section 6]
; the key distinction is that the defining integral converges (or rather, can be made to converge) absolutely for the finite places, but not the Archimedean place.
Baruch and Mao
[
2
]
have tackled the Archimedean case from that perspective on
SL(2,\mathbb{R})
, and more recent work on
GL(n)
at the finite places can be found in
[
34
]
.

1.1.
A technical note

Obviously, one would hope to express the
GL(n)
Bessel function as an integral transform of the corresponding
GL(n-1)
Bessel function via matrix decompositions in the defining integral; certainly this trick works very well for the Whittaker functions.
(In fact, we use that idea in
section

9
.)
There is a technical point that seems to arise in every viewpoint the author has explored, having to do with conjugating an upper triangular matrix past a lower triangular matrix in the presence of characters of both the lower triangular matrix and the
x
-component of the Iwasawa decomposition of the product.
The conjugation results in terms that are complex exponentials of the bottom row of the lower triangular matrix, and for
n>3
this is no longer given by a character, preventing the descent to
GL(n-1)
.
Precisely, this can be seen in the
Strong Interchange of Integrals Conjecture
, which requires additional variables
\alpha
that are zero for the Bessel function, but generally not in the lower-rank integrals of the inductive construction.
If one is looking for the exact step in the matrix decompositions that this occurs, it is the conjugations (
27
) (on the other side of the Weyl element,
x_{a}
would be lower triangular, while
x_{b}^{*}
remains upper triangular) as well as (
28
) which moves a function of the top row into the first super-diagonal; this directly translates to a need for the
\alpha
vector in
section

8.4
.

2.
Some Notation

We take
G=GL(n,\mathbb{R})/\mathbb{R}^{+}
and
K=O(n)
; please note that this is different from previous papers where we used
GL(n,\mathbb{R})/\mathbb{R}^{\times}
and

.
The distinction allows us to better induct on the rank, e.g. even and odd
GL(2)
Maass forms are distinguished by the
\det
representation of

(and hence correspond to different representations of
SO(n)
when embedded into higher rank groups), but both correspond to the trivial representation of
SO(2)
.
We regularly identify representatives of cosets with the cosets themselves; in particular, we treat elements of
G
as matrices instead of cosets modulo the positive scalar matrices.

Take
Y
to be the diagonal matrices in
G
, then characters of
Y
are parameterized by two tuples
\mu\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
and
\delta\in\left\{0,1\right\}^{n}
(or
\delta\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
modulo 2) where
\mu_{1}+\ldots+\mu_{n}=0
(for consistency mod
\mathbb{R}^{+}
) and the characters
p_{\mu}
and
\chi_{\delta}
on the diagonal matrices are given by

p_{\mu}(\diag(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}))=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left|a_{i}\right|^{\mu_{i}},\qquad\chi_{\delta}(\diag(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}))=\prod_{i=1}^{n}{\mathop{\,sgn}}(a_{i})^{\delta_{i}}.

We generally normalize the character
p_{\mu}
by translating to
p_{\rho+\mu}
where
\rho
is the half-sum of the positive roots with coordinates
\rho_{i}=\frac{n+1}{2}-i
,
i=1,\ldots,n
; this places the tempered almost-spherical automorphic forms at
{\mathop{Re}}(\mu)=0
, see the next section.

Set
V=Y\cap K
and take
Y^{+}
to be the positive diagonal matrices in
G
so that
Y=Y^{+}V
.
Note that
p_{\mu}
is really a character of
Y^{+}
and
\chi_{\delta}
a character of
V
.

Take
U(\mathbb{R})
to be the upper-triangular unipotent (i.e. diagonal entries equal to 1) matrices in
G
, then the Iwasawa decomposition of
G
is
G=U(\mathbb{R})Y^{+}K
.
Characters of
U(\mathbb{R})
have the form

\psi_{m}\begin{pmatrix}1&x_{1,2}&&&*\\
&1&x_{2,3}\\
&&\ddots&\ddots\\
&&&1&x_{n-1,n}\\
&&&&1\end{pmatrix}=e\left(m_{1}x_{1,2}+\ldots+m_{n-1}x_{n-1,n}\right),\qquad e\left(t\right)=e^{2\pi it},

for some
m\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
.
We say
\psi_{m}
is non-degenerate if
m_{1}\cdots m_{n-1}\neq 0
.

On
Y
, we take coordinates

(1)

\displaystyle y=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}y_{1}y_{2}\cdots y_{n}\\
&y_{2}\cdots y_{n}\\
&&\ddots\\
&&&y_{n}\end{pmatrix},

and we generally do not distinguish between
y\in Y
and
y=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})\in(\mathbb{R}^{\times})^{n-1}\times\left\{\pm 1\right\}\cong Y
as the multiplication is the same.
In this notation, we have
\psi_{y}(x)=\psi_{I}(yxy^{-1})
.
(Note the change
y_{j}\mapsto y_{n-j}
compared to
[
15
]
, etc.)
We will denote the
n\times n
identity matrix by
I
or
I_{n}
.

We take the Weyl group
W
to be the set of matrices with exactly one 1 on every row and column and the remaining entries all zero.
Of particular interest will be the set
W^{\text{rel}}
of “relevant” Weyl elements (as in
[
27
]
or
[
30
, Section 7.5]
) of the form

(2)

\displaystyle w_{r_{1},\ldots,r_{\ell}}=\begin{pmatrix}&&I_{r_{1}}\\
&\mathinner{\mkern 1.0mu\raise 1.0pt\vbox{\kern 7.0pt\hbox{.}}\mkern 2.0mu\raise 4.0pt\hbox{.}\mkern 2.0mu\raise 7.0pt\hbox{.}\mkern 1.0mu}\\
I_{r_{\ell}}\end{pmatrix},\qquad r_{1}+\ldots+r_{\ell}=n,

and we denote the long Weyl element
w_{1,\ldots,1}
by

.
The Weyl elements can also be thought of as permutation matrices, and we occasionally use cycle notation, e.g. if
n=3
, then

w_{(1\,2\,3)}=w_{2,1}=\begin{pmatrix}&1\\
&&1\\
1\end{pmatrix}.

The Weyl group acts on
\mu
and
\delta
by permutations using

\displaystyle p_{\mu^{w}}(y):=

\displaystyle p_{\mu}(wyw^{-1}),

\displaystyle\chi_{\delta}(y)=\chi_{\delta}(wyw^{-1}),

\displaystyle w\in W,y\in Y,

and in particular,
\mu^{w_{l}}=(\mu_{n},\mu_{n-1},\ldots,\mu_{1})
.
(Note that, e.g.,
p_{(\mu^{w})^{w^{\prime}}}(y)=p_{\mu^{w}}(w^{\prime}y{w^{\prime}}^{-1})=p_{\mu}(w(w^{\prime}y{w^{\prime}}^{-1})w^{-1})=p_{\mu^{ww^{\prime}}}(y)
, so this really is a right action.)
Similarly, we can define the action on the indices; using
\mu^{w}_{i}:=(\mu^{w})_{i}
, we take
\mu_{i^{w}}:=(\mu^{w})_{i}
.

Suppose
w\in W
can be written
w={\left(e_{i_{1}}\,\ldots\,e_{i_{n}}\right)}^{T}
with

the
j
-th standard basis element (as a column vector), so
e_{1}={\left(1\,0\,\ldots\,0\right)}^{T}
.
Then

.
If we define

, then we have
\mu^{w}_{j}=(\mu^{w})_{j}=\mu_{w^{-1}(j)}
, and we defined the right action so that

.
Note that we could very well have defined the left action
p_{w(\mu)}(y)=p_{\mu}(w^{-1}yw)
; this is clearly related to the right action by
\mu^{w}=w^{-1}(\mu)
, and we would have
w(\mu)_{j}=\mu^{w^{-1}}_{j}=\mu_{w(j)}
.

For any

, we have the decomposition
U=U_{w}\,\overline{U}_{w}
where
U_{w}=U\cap w^{-1}Uw
and
\overline{U}_{w}=U\cap w^{-1}{U}^{T}w
.
(These are orthogonal subspaces of

under the logarithm map and the Killing form.)
We will also write
U_{m}(\mathbb{R})
for the upper triangular unipotent matrices in
GL(m,\mathbb{R})
,
1\leq m\leq n
.

Unless stated otherwise, the variable

will denote an element of
G
,

an element of
U(\mathbb{R})
,

an element of

,

an element of

and

an element of

.
We generally embed

in the upper left corner

g\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}g\\
&1\end{pmatrix},

and we use prime notation to distinguish objects of a similar type, not as derivatives.

3.
Spectral Kuznetsov formulas on
GL(n)

We are interested in studying Fourier-Whittaker coefficients/Hecke eigenvalues of automorphic forms (i.e. a spectral basis) in
L^{2}(\Gamma\backslash G)
where
\Gamma\subset G
is a discrete subgroup of finite covolume.
One key tool in this area is the spectral Kuznetsov formula (aka. “relative trace formula”), which equates a spectral and a geometric/arithmetic decomposition of the Fourier coefficient

\int_{U(\mathbb{Z})\cap\Gamma\backslash U(\mathbb{R})}P(xg)\overline{\psi_{\beta}(x)}dx

of a Poincaré series
P
, i.e. a function of the form

P(g)=\sum_{\gamma\in U(\mathbb{Z})\cap\Gamma\backslash\Gamma}F(\gamma g)

with a kernel function
F:G\to\mathbb{C}
that satisfies
F(xg)=\psi_{a}(x)F(g)
for some characters
\psi_{a},\psi_{b}
of
U(\mathbb{R})
.

Automorphic forms can be arranged into representations of
G
according to the decomposition of the right-regular representation on the
L^{2}
-space, and these can be further decomposed into their
K
-types, i.e. representations of
K
.
Automorphic representations can be embedded as subrepresentations of principal series representations, i.e. representations induced from characters of the group of upper triangular matrices, and also collected into families according to the smallest weight
K
-type occurring inside the representation (up to conjugation).
The principal series representations correspond to characters
p_{\rho+\mu}\chi_{\delta}
of
Y
.

Take
\Lambda=\left(\frac{k_{1}-1}{2},-\frac{k_{1}-1}{2},\ldots,\frac{k_{m}-1}{2},-\frac{k_{m}-1}{2},0,\ldots,0\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
2m\leq n
,
2\leq k_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
, and consider the family of automorphic representations occurring as subrepresentations of principal series representations
\pi_{\Lambda,\Lambda+\widetilde{\mu},\delta}
with characters of the form
p_{\rho+\Lambda+\widetilde{\mu}}\chi_{\delta}
for some
\widetilde{\mu},\delta
with
\widetilde{\mu}_{1}+\ldots+\widetilde{\mu}_{n}=0
and
{\mathop{Re}}(\widetilde{\mu})=0
.
These share a minimal
K
-type and are called “tempered” for the condition
{\mathop{Re}}(\widetilde{\mu})=0
.
By abusive of terminology, we refer to either
\Lambda
or the tuple
(k_{1},\ldots,k_{m})
as the weight of the automorphic representation (as opposed to the weight of the representation of
K
) and we can assume that
k_{i+1}\leq k_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,m-1
(up to conjugation of the representation) as well as
\widetilde{\mu}_{2i}=\widetilde{\mu}_{2i-1}
,
i=1,\ldots,m
(for unitary representations).
Let
i\mathfrak{a}_{0}^{*}(\Lambda)
be the set of all such
\Lambda+\widetilde{\mu}
, i.e.

i\mathfrak{a}_{0}^{*}(\Lambda)=\left\{\Lambda+\widetilde{\mu}\,\middle|\,\mu\in\mathbb{C}^{n},{\mathop{Re}}(\widetilde{\mu})=0,\widetilde{\mu}_{1}+\ldots+\widetilde{\mu}_{n}=0,\widetilde{\mu}_{2j}=\widetilde{\mu}_{2j-1},j=1,\ldots,m\right\}.

We note that for a given
\Lambda\neq 0
, not all values of
\delta
are possible – i.e. yield nontrivial representations; we have the parity constraints

(3)

\displaystyle\delta_{2j-1}+\delta_{2j}\equiv k_{i}\pmod{2},\qquad j=1,\ldots,m,

see (
23
) – and not all
\delta
are inequivalent (i.e. if there is some Weyl element which fixes
\Lambda
and also permutes
\delta\to\delta^{\prime}
, then the data
(\Lambda,\delta)
and
(\Lambda,\delta^{\prime})
yield the same set of forms).
(Beware of drawing a line between
\Lambda
and the minimal
K
-type; for
GL(3)
with
\Lambda=(0,0,0)
and
\delta=(1,1,0)
, the minimal
K
-type has dimension 3, i.e. highest weight 1.)

In order to localize the Kuznetsov formula to such a family of automorphic representations, we take the kernel of the Poincaré series to be an inverse Whittaker transform

F(g)=\int_{i\mathfrak{a}_{0}^{*}(\Lambda)}f(\mu)W_{\sigma}(g,\mu,\delta)d_{\text{spec}}\mu,

where
\sigma
is the minimal
K
-type corresponding to the weight
\Lambda
and
W_{\sigma}(g,\mu,\delta)
is the Jacquet-Whittaker function at
K
-type
\sigma
in the principal series representation with character
p_{\rho+\mu}\chi_{\delta}
.
The measure
d_{\text{spec}}\mu
occurring in Wallach’s Whittaker inversion formula
[
46
]

\int_{U(\mathbb{R})\backslash G}\int_{i\mathfrak{a}_{0}^{*}(\Lambda)}f(\mu)W_{\sigma}(g,\mu,\delta)d_{\text{spec}}\mu\,\overline{W_{\sigma}(g,\mu^{\prime},\delta)}dg=f(\mu^{\prime})

can be computed by a method of Goldfeld and Kontorovich
[
19
]
if one has the appropriate generalization of Stade’s formula
[
44
]
, which is a direct computation of

(4)

\displaystyle L(s,\mu,\mu^{\prime},\delta):=

\displaystyle\int_{U(\mathbb{R})\backslash GL(n-1,\mathbb{R})}\Tr\left(W_{\sigma}(g,\mu,\delta){\overline{W_{\sigma}(g,\mu^{\prime},\delta)}}^{T}\right)\det(g)^{s}dg,

and one expects this to be the gamma factors of the Rankin-Selberg
L
-function
[
28
]
.
(There are ways to temporarily get around the use of Stade’s formula, but each has its own downside.)
A solution of this problem in the principal series case can be found in
[
24
]
.

If we write the Langlands spectral expansion
[
32
]
over this family of automorphic forms (we are not assuming the absence of complementary series forms) as

P(g)=\int_{\mathcal{B}(\Lambda,\delta)}\left\langle P,\Xi\right\rangle\Xi(g)\,d\Xi,

then applying Wallach’s Whittaker inversion and using Stade’s formula to get rid of the spare Whittaker function gives the spectral Kuznetsov formula (see
[
15
, Section 8]
)

(5)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{B}(\Lambda,\delta)}\frac{\lambda_{\Xi}(b)\overline{\lambda_{\Xi}(a)}}{L(1,{\mathrm{Ad}}^{2}\Xi)}f(\mu_{\Xi})d_{\text{H}}\Xi=\sum_{w\in W}\sum_{c}\frac{S_{w}(a,b,c)}{p_{-\rho}(c)}H_{w}(f,acwb^{-1}w^{-1}).

On the left side,
f(\mu)
is a reasonably arbitrary test function and we’ve included the Rankin-Selberg constants from converting to Hecke eigenvalues
\lambda_{\Xi}
in the measure
d_{\text{H}}\Xi
.
The right side has a sum over the Weyl group
W
and a sum over tuples of non-zero integers
c\in\mathbb{Z}^{n-1}
which we arrange into matrices of the form

c=\diag(1/c_{1},c_{1}/c_{2},\ldots,c_{n-2}/c_{n-1},c_{n-1}).

(Note the change
c_{j}\mapsto c_{n-j}
compared to
[
15
]
, etc.)
The variables
a
and
b
are tuples of nonzero integers
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}^{n-1}
, which we treat as elements of
Y
.
We have the Kloosterman sum

S_{w}(a,b,c)=\sum_{xcwx^{\prime}\in U(\mathbb{Z})\cap\Gamma/\backslash\overline{U}_{w}(\mathbb{Z})\cap\Gamma}\psi_{a}(x)\psi_{b}(x^{\prime}),

defined in terms of the Bruhat decomposition
G=U(\mathbb{R})YWU(\mathbb{R})
(the Kloosterman sum is taken to be zero when it is not well-defined), and finally, we have the integral transform

(6)

\displaystyle p_{\rho}(y)H_{w}(f,y)=

\displaystyle\int_{U(\mathbb{R})\backslash GL(n-1,\mathbb{R})}\int_{\overline{U}_{w}(\mathbb{R})}\int_{i\mathfrak{a}_{0}^{*}(\Lambda)}\frac{f(\mu)}{L(1,\mu,\mu,\delta)}

\displaystyle\times\Tr\left(W_{\sigma}(ywug,\mu,\delta){\overline{W_{\sigma}(g,\mu,\delta)}}^{T}\right)d_{\text{spec}}\mu\,\overline{\psi_{I}(u)}du\,\det(g)dg.

In this paper, we are interested in the special functions occurring in the integral transform
H_{w}(f,y)
.
The final integral over
g
simply serves to remove a spare Whittaker function, so we will ignore it.
Also, the Whittaker function
\overline{W_{\sigma}(g,\mu,\delta)}
can be expressed in terms of
W_{\sigma}(g,2\Lambda-\mu,\delta)
, which is entire in
\mu
and reasonably bounded, so we can simply include that in the test function
f(\mu)
.
Similarly, the weight
\frac{d_{\text{spec}}\mu}{L(1,\mu,\mu,\delta)}
is not especially relevant as it is (conjecturally) holomorphic near
{\mathop{Re}}(\mu)=0
, so we restrict our attention to the integral transform

(7)

\displaystyle\int_{\overline{U}_{w}(\mathbb{R})}\int_{i\mathfrak{a}_{0}^{*}(\Lambda)}f(\mu)W_{\sigma}(ywug,\mu,\delta)d_{\text{spec}}\mu\,\overline{\psi_{I}(u)}du.

The spectral measure
d_{\text{spec}}\mu
has some zeros on
i\mathfrak{a}_{0}^{*}(\Lambda)
which will be relevant.
Lastly, only the relevant Weyl elements
w\in W^{\text{rel}}
occur in the spectral Kuznetsov formula, by a theorem of Friedberg
[
18
]
.

We should mention two very common, equivalent constructions of the spectral Kuznetsov formulas:

1.

One can take the inner product of two Poincaré series
P_{1},P_{2}
, applying the unfolding to one of the series and proceeding precisely as above; this yields a spare weight function, which can be sent to 1 by a density argument.

2.

One can take the double Fourier coefficient

\int_{U(\mathbb{Z})\cap\Gamma\backslash U(\mathbb{R})}\int_{U(\mathbb{Z})\cap\Gamma\backslash U(\mathbb{R})}F(xy,x^{\prime}y^{\prime})\overline{\psi(x)}dx\,\overline{\psi^{\prime}(x^{\prime})}dx^{\prime}

of the kernel function

F(g,g^{\prime})=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}f(g^{-1}\gamma g).

One then takes
f
such that on the spectral side an application of Harish-Chandra’s (zonal) spherical inversion reduces to some reasonably arbitrary test function
\hat{f}
, while on the arithmetic side, one needs to show that the Fourier transform of a zonal spherical function is a product of two Whittaker functions, but the remainder of the process is identical.

One should likely always handle the convergence issues by starting with an inner product of a Poincaré series with itself and apply Plancherel’s identity, but having some knowledge of the convergence on the spectral side
[
29
]
, it is slightly simpler to directly consider the Fourier coefficient of a Poincaré series.

4.
The Conjectures and Their Consequences

Let
w=w_{r_{1},\ldots,r_{\ell}}\in W^{\text{rel}}
.
We define the “compatibility condition” for

to be
\psi_{y}(wuw^{-1})=\psi_{I}(u)
for all

, and it turns out that the condition for the Kloosterman sum above to be well-defined is exactly that

satisfies the compatibility condition.
Furthermore, if we treat
\overline{U}_{w}
as the quotient
U_{w}\backslash U
, then the compatibility condition is precisely what (

Define
\hat{r}_{i}=r_{1}+\ldots+r_{i}
and

\displaystyle Y_{w}=\left\{\pm\begin{pmatrix}y_{\hat{r}_{1}}\cdots y_{\hat{r}_{\ell-1}}I_{r_{1}}\\
&\ddots\\
&&y_{\hat{r}_{\ell}}I_{r_{\ell-1}}\\

It is a standard fact from complex analysis that for a function having a Mellin transform in addition to a power series (Frobenius series) expansion, the poles of the Mellin transform are simple and occur precisely at the locations corresponding to terms of the power series.
So there is a strong connection between power series and inverse Mellin transforms.
For hypergeometric functions, one can easily go in the opposite direction; given a series

f(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(a_{1}+k)\cdots\Gamma(a_{r}+k)}{\Gamma(b_{1}+k)\cdots\Gamma(b_{\ell}+k)}\frac{(-x)^{k}}{k!},

we have an inverse Mellin integral representation on a “hook contour”

f(x)=\int_{\mathcal{C}_{1}}\frac{\Gamma(a_{1}-s)\cdots\Gamma(a_{r}-s)}{\Gamma(b_{1}-s)\cdots\Gamma(b_{\ell}-s)}\Gamma(s)x^{-s}\frac{ds}{2\pi i},

with
\mathcal{C}_{1}
as in
figure

(the dots there are the poles of

, which correspond to the terms of the power series), provided the poles of the numerator lie outside the contour (and the original series converged).
If the gamma factors in this integral representation have sufficient decay at infinity, we might hope to straighten the contour into something of the form
\mathcal{C}_{2}
where the the contour passes to the left of the poles of the gamma factors
\Gamma(a_{i}-s)
and the real part of the contour (i.e.

5.
Applications

Consider the spectral Kuznetsov formula as in (
5
).
We can get a bound on the Kloosterman sum terms by contour shifting just in the Mellin expansion of the first Whittaker function of
H_{w}(f,y)
and the
\mu
integrals (without using the Bessel functions).
This bound will certainly be weaker than even simply Mellin expanding the Bessel functions since we lose by Mellin expanding the Whittaker function and we don’t save anything when we use absolute convergence on the
\mu

u
and
g
integrals in
H_{w}(f,y)
.
This weaker bound plus the compatibility relation might be good enough for nontrivial results except on the long Weyl element term, just by comparing the distance needed to shift the leading asymptotics of the Whittaker function.
(The shorter Weyl elements come with compatibility relations which affect the region of absolute convergence of the corresponding Kloosterman zeta function and so reduce the distance needed in the contour shifting.)
For the long Weyl element term, we might hope to prove the
Interchange of Integrals Conjecture
at just that Weyl element, then we have everything but a good integral representation, and one would hope that follows by some contour shifting and combinatorics on
6
; this would likely be the largest benefit at the minimal effort.

In this section, we explore some possible applications of the above conjectures (at all Weyl elements), and what additional results would be required.

5.1.
Arithmetically-weighted Weyl laws

Define
\tau_{w}>0
so that the Kloosterman zeta

{\mathop{Re}}(\mu_{i}-\mu_{i+1})>\tau_{w}

5.2.
Bessel expansions

A paper of Baruch and Mao
[
1
]
defines Bessel-like distributions in terms of the expansion of test functions into a basis of the Whittaker model of a representation.
We wish to connect these back to the Bessel functions studied here, using the methods of
[
15
]
.

Conjecture

(Bessel Expansion)
.

Suppose
F:G\to\mathbb{C}
is smooth and compactly supported in the Iwasawa coordinates, then for
g\in G
, the Bessel-like distribution

\widehat{F}(g,\mu,\delta):=\sum_{\sigma}(\dim\sigma)\Tr\left(W_{\sigma}(g,\mu,\delta)\int_{G}F(g^{\prime}){\overline{W_{\sigma}(g^{\prime},-\overline{\mu},\delta)}}^{T}dg^{\prime}\right)

is given by the integral

\widehat{F}(g,\mu,\delta)=\frac{1}{\left|V\right|}\int_{G_{w_{l}}}F(g^{\prime}g)\overline{K_{w_{l}}(g^{\prime},-\overline{\mu},\delta)}dg^{\prime}.

Here we are using the term “Bessel-like distribution” by analogy with
[
1
, Appendix 4]
.
One should compare this to
[
3
, Theorem 2.5]
; if one could connect the Bessel-like distribution as it appears here to the Bessel distribution there, then that theorem would show
\widehat{F}
is given by integration against some element of
\mathcal{K}_{w}(\mu)
, while the result above specifies which Bessel function appears.

Fundamentally, the
Bessel Expansion Conjecture
follows from two facts, the first of which is that the Fourier transform of a zonal spherical function is a product of two Whittaker functions.
This is strongly tied to Wallach’s Whittaker inversion theorem
[
46
]
and Comtat
[
16
]
has given a very general method for recovering this statement from Wallach’s theorem.
Even though this method should certainly apply here, we consider the method of
[
15
]
, which relies on a conjecture, as it is very nearly a special case of the
Strong Interchange of Integrals Conjecture
and it is likely of independent interest:
Informally, the
Jacquet-Whittaker Direct Continuation Conjecture
states
for
\mu
in a tube domain containing
{\mathop{Re}}(\mu)=0
and all
t
, the integral (
16
) defining the Jacquet-Whittaker function
W_{\sigma}(t,\mu,\delta)
can be rearranged through change of variables, integration by parts and a smooth, dyadic partition of unity so that it converges absolutely.

As with the
Strong Interchange of Integrals Conjecture
, the full statement is rather technical, so we postpone it to
section

10
.

The second required fact is a sort of Bessel inversion or Plancherel formula:
For
F(\mu)=F(\Lambda+\widetilde{\mu})
Schwartz-class and holomorphic in
\widetilde{\mu}
on a tube domain containing
{\mathop{Re}}(\widetilde{\mu})=0
, we define

\widetilde{F}_{\delta,\Lambda}(y)=\int_{i\mathfrak{a}^{*}_{0}(\Lambda)}F(\mu)K_{w_{l}}(y,\mu,\delta)d_{\text{spec}}\mu.

If also
F^{\prime}(\mu)
satisfies these properties for
\Lambda^{\prime},\delta^{\prime}
, define

\mathcal{I}_{\delta,\Lambda,\delta^{\prime},\Lambda^{\prime}}(F,F^{\prime}):=\int_{Y}\widetilde{F}_{\delta,\Lambda}(y)\overline{\widetilde{F^{\prime}}_{\delta^{\prime},\Lambda^{\prime}}(y)}dy.

Then by analytic continuation, it is sufficient to show that
\mathcal{I}_{\delta,\Lambda,\delta^{\prime},0}(F,F^{\prime})
is zero unless
\Lambda=0
and
\delta=\delta^{\prime}
, in which case

(13)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\delta,0,\delta,0}(F,F^{\prime})=\left|V\right|\int_{i\mathfrak{a}^{*}_{0}(0)}F(\mu)\overline{F^{\prime}(-\overline{\mu})}d_{\text{spec}}\mu.

To obtain the actual expansion of smooth, compactly supported functions on
Y_{w}
into Bessel functions, we also require

Conjecture

(Test Functions)
.

For
w\in W^{\text{rel}}
, let

be smooth and compactly supported.
Define

to be zero outside

(14)

\displaystyle F(xyy^{\prime}{z}^{T}wx^{\prime})=

\displaystyle\psi_{I}(xx^{\prime})f(y)u(y^{\prime},z,x^{\prime}),

\int_{\overline{U}_{w}(\mathbb{R})}u(I,I,x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}=1.

Then
F
is smooth and compactly supported in the Iwasawa coordinates when the support of

Note that such an

has

T_{w}(F)(y)=f(y),\qquad y\in Y_{w}.

If the
Bessel Expansion
and
Test Functions
Conjectures hold, it follows that for such

Aside: One might be tempted to define a Bessel function on

5.4.
Other applications

Though
[
8
]
encountered some unexpected difficulties with square-root cancellation in the level aspect, one might hope that
[
15
, Theorem 4]
would continue to hold:

Conjecture 9
.

For
f:Y\to\mathbb{R}
smooth and compactly supported,

\displaystyle\sum_{c}\frac{S_{w_{l}}(a,b,c)}{\left|c_{1}c_{2}\cdots c_{n-1}\right|}f(yc)

\displaystyle\ll_{a,b,f,\epsilon}\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}y_{i}^{\theta+\epsilon}+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}y_{i}^{-1-\epsilon},

where

is the Ramanujan-Selberg parameter for

In
[
15
]
this was proved for

In
[
36
]
, Nelson seems to have proven a very general subconvexity bound for

GL(n)
Maass forms in “generic position”, i.e. when all of

and
\left|\mu_{i}\right|\asymp T

The earlier paper
[
5
]
, took a direct approach to subconvexity using the explicit Kuznetsov formula and Bessel functions for
GL(3)

L
-functions on
GL(n)
, provided the Bessel functions and Kloosterman sums are sufficiently well-understood.
The first challenges faced by such a researcher will likely be:

1.

Optimal (square-root cancellation?) bounds for the Kloosterman sums.
There has be some progress on this front (as well as some negative results), e.g. in
[
34
]
and
[
7
]
.

2.

Proving a sharp cutoff for the sum of Kloosterman sums.
That is, given a reasonably nice test function
f
, we expect

(recall (
6
)) to decay rapidly for
c_{i}
large compared to
a,b
; the goal should be to identify the precise boundary of the region where the rapid decay occurs.
This is Lemmas 8 and 9 in
[
5
]
, and similarly, Lemmas 3 and 4 in
[
6
]
.

3.

Computing the Fourier transform of the Bessel functions.
After applying the Kuznetsov formula to a moment or subconvexity problem, a typical next step involves Poisson or Voronoi summation, and hence the Fourier transform of the
H_{w}(f,y)
function.
This is Lemmas 10 and 11 in
[
5
]
and Lemmas 5 and 6 in
[
6
]
.
Sharma
[
41
]
has apparently improved this by applying stationary phase to the double-Bessel integral representations of the
GL(3)
Bessel function.
The author wonders if directly applying the known Fourier transform of the
GL(2)
Bessel functions in the double-Bessel integral representations would result in a better bound and/or a cleaner argument, and might offer some hope of generalization to
GL(n)
.

4.

Diophantine analysis of the collapsed Kloosterman sums.
The Poisson summation step of
[
5
]
caused the Kloosterman sums to mostly collapse to congruences; one then has to have some understanding of the set of points satisfying those relations.

Lastly, It would be very interesting to write out the Kuznetsov formulas in the case where one or both of the characters are degenerate (having one or more indices equal to zero).
Of course, we should be able to build the
GL(n,\mathbb{R})
spectral Kuznetsov formulas at a degenerate character from the Kuznetsov formulas of
GL(m,\mathbb{R})
with
m<n
, but the arithmetic Kuznetsov formulas have the possibility of connecting sums of degenerate
GL(n,\mathbb{R})
Kloosterman sums to sums of
GL(m,\mathbb{R})
Kloosterman sums in unexpected ways.

5.5.
Other integral representations of the Bessel functions

5.5.1.
An explicit direct integral

For any proposed integral representation of the Bessel functions, the conjectures reduce the question to showing it satisfies the appropriate differential equations and computing its first-term asymptotics.
One might then hope for a direct integral representation; for example, that the Jacquet-type integral of the function defined on
G_{w_{l}}
by

I^{*}_{\mu,\delta}\left(xy{x^{\prime}}^{T}\right)=\psi_{I}(x)p_{\rho+\mu}(y)\chi_{\delta}(y).

would give the Bessel function up to a constant; i.e.
K_{w}(y,\mu,\delta)=C(\mu,\delta)\mathcal{I}_{w}(y,\mu,\delta)
where

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{w}(y,\mu,\delta)=

\displaystyle\int_{\overline{U}_{w}(\mathbb{R})}I^{*}_{\mu,\delta}(ywu)\overline{\psi_{I}(u)}du.

Unfortunately, this integral is far from absolutely convergent for any value of
\mu
.
The author is unaware of any direct, absolutely convergent integral for the Bessel functions.

One could formulate a version of the
Strong Interchange of Integrals Conjecture
for this integral and (with a bit more difficulty) argue that it would satisfy the same differential equations as the Bessel function (for the same reasons), leaving us to check the first-term asymptotics as
y\to 0
, which naively appear to be independent of
{\mathop{\,sgn}}(y)
(except for the factor
\chi_{\delta}(y)
), so it is not unreasonable.
The advantage to applying this integral representation would be significantly fewer extraneous integrals and a notable lack of spare Whittaker functions.

5.5.3.
Zagier’s construction

Li’s generalization to
GL(n)

[
22
]
of Zagier’s
GL(2)
construction follows the spherical inversion route of constructing the Kuznetsov formula, so the weight functions on the Kloosterman sum side are of the form

H_{w}(f,y)=\int_{\overline{U}_{w}(\mathbb{R})}\int_{Y^{+}}\int_{U(\mathbb{R})}F\left(t^{-1}x^{-1}ywut\right)\psi_{I}(x)dx\,(\det t)dt\,\overline{\psi_{I}(u)}du,

where
F(g)
is given by an inverse spherical transform

F(g)=\int_{i\mathfrak{a}^{*}_{0}(0)}f(\mu)h_{\mu}(g)d_{\text{SI}}\mu.

One might consider continuing to follow Zagier’s path to find an integral representation of the Bessel function from this perspective; if the
Interchange of Integrals Conjecture
holds, the constructions may be used interchangeably with the same Bessel functions appearing in each, as per the discussion in
section

.

To the author’s knowledge, Zagier’s construction was never published, but it can be found in Xiaoqing Li’s Kloostermania notes
[
33
]
.
In the
GL(2)
long-element case, Zagier substitutes on

to arrive at

H_{w}(f,y)=\int_{\overline{U}_{w}(\mathbb{R})}\int_{Y^{+}}\int_{U(\mathbb{R})}F\left(t^{-1}x^{-1}ywuxt\right)dx\,(\det t)dt\,\overline{\psi_{I}(u)}du,

and this can be done for most Weyl elements on
GL(n)
(but obviously not all; consider

) by carefully choosing
v\in U(\mathbb{R})
and
v^{\prime}\in U_{w}(\mathbb{R})
such that
x^{-1}ywv^{\prime}(yw)^{-1}=v^{-1}
and
v^{\prime}uv\in\overline{U}_{w}(\mathbb{R})
.

Zagier’s next move substitutes on

to diagonalize
ywu
over
\mathbb{C}
; on
GL(n)
, this can be facilitated by a change of variables replacing

of the coordinates of
u
with eigenvalues
\lambda
of
ywu
.
(There is also perhaps some benefit to viewing

as a function of the characteristic polynomial of
g{g}^{T}

Then Zagier faces cases based on the types (complex or real) of the eigenvalues.
When the eigenvalues are real, we can substitute
xt\mapsto tx
, which removes
t
from the argument of
F
so the

x
and
u
(provided one brings the signs
x\mapsto vxv
,
v\in V
with

).

In the complex eigenvalue case, things are somewhat more complicated.
Zagier’s substitution is now a complex fractional linear transformation; multiplication by

reverses the coordinates of

and the author has no intuition as to how to directly implement it on
GL(n)
.
However, one might instead consider to block diagonalize
ywu
so that in the complex case we have elements of
SO(2)
on the diagonal.
In this way, on
GL(2)
, we can take the Cartan coordinates for

and apply the commutativity of
SO(2)
to remove the

t
and
u
.
On
GL(n)
, a combination of converting certain

across should enjoy similar success.

At this point, having reduced the number of integrals, Zagier uses properties of the
GL(2)
spherical transform whose generalization to
GL(n)
are not at all obvious.
In the real eigenvalue case, Zagier is simply writing the spherical transform as the Mellin transform of the Harish-Chandra transform, but in the complex eigenvalue case, the picture is much less clear.

In theory, one could push the argument through simply because the dimension of
U(\mathbb{R})
is growing faster than the degree of the aforementioned characteristic polynomial, but the argument is far from ready for
GL(n)
, and here is where this story ends, at least for now.

5.5.4.
Via

One might consider to replace the Whittaker function

At that point, one can open the Jacquet integrals and push the sum over

One might hope to produce an integral representation of the long-element Bessel function by applying the

6.
Background

6.1.
Measures

We decompose Haar measure on
G
by the Iwasawa decomposition
dg=dx\,dy\,dk
, and we take
dk
to be Haar probability measure.

On
Y^{+}
, we apply the measure

dy:=p_{-2\rho}(y)\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}\frac{dy_{i}}{y_{i}}=\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}\frac{dy_{i}}{y_{i}^{1+i(n-i)}},

which is the measure inherited as a subspace of
G
(not Haar measure).
For the measure on
Y
, we apply counting measure to
V
, so if
d\bar{k}
is probability measure on
V\backslash K
, we have

\int_{Y^{+}}\int_{K}f(yk)dy\,dk=\frac{1}{\left|V\right|}\int_{Y}\int_{V\backslash K}f(y\bar{k})dy\,d\bar{k}.

Haar measure on
U(\mathbb{R})
is simply Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}
:

dx=\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}\prod_{j=i+1}^{n}dx_{i,j}.

We will several times use the fact that the determinant of the Jacobian for the change of variables
x\mapsto yxy^{-1}
on
\overline{U}_{w}(\mathbb{R})
with
y\in Y
is
p_{\rho-\rho^{w}}(y)
.
To see this, note that the coordinate
x_{i,j}
,
j>i
is nonzero in
\overline{U}_{w}(\mathbb{R})
if
i^{w}>j^{w}
, the change of variables sends
x_{i,j}\mapsto y_{i}\cdots y_{j-1}x_{i,j}
, and the power of
y_{k}
in
p_{\rho-\rho^{w}}(y)
is

\sum_{q=1}^{k}\left(q^{w}-q\right)=\sum_{1\leq i\leq k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq j\leq n\\
i^{w}\geq j^{w}\end{subarray}}1-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i,j\leq k\\
i^{w}\geq j^{w}\end{subarray}}1=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i\leq k<j\leq n\\
i^{w}\geq j^{w}\end{subarray}}1.

For
i=1,\ldots,n-1
, define
h_{i}(\theta)\in K
by

h_{i}(\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&&-\sin\theta\\
&I_{i-1}\\
\sin\theta&&\cos\theta\\
&&&I_{n-1-i}\end{pmatrix}=w_{(2\;i+1)}h_{1}(\theta)w_{(2\;i+1)}.

Then one possible arrangement of the hyper-spherical coordinates of
SO(n)
is

h_{1}(\theta_{n,1})h_{2}(\theta_{n,2})\cdots h_{n-1}(\theta_{n,n-1})h_{1}(\theta_{n-1,1})\cdots h_{n-2}(\theta_{n-1,n-2})\cdots h_{1}(\theta_{2,1}),

with
\theta_{i,1}\in[0,2\pi)
,
\theta_{i,j}\in[0,\pi]
,
j>1
.
Haar probability measure on
SO(n)\subset K
is

d_{SO(n)}k=\prod_{i=2}^{n}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{i}{2}\right)}{2\pi^{\frac{n+2}{4}}}\prod_{j=1}^{i-1}\sin^{j-1}\theta_{i,j}\,d\theta_{i,j},

while
K/SO(n)=\left\{SO(n),\diag(1,\ldots,1,-1)SO(n)\right\}
, so Haar probability measure on
K
is
dk=\frac{1}{2}d_{SO(n)}k
if we assign counting measure on
K/SO(n)
.

6.2.
The power function

Set

\displaystyle M_{n}=

\displaystyle\left\{\mu\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\,\middle|\,\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}=0\right\},

\displaystyle M_{n}^{0}=

\displaystyle\left\{\mu\in M_{n}\,\middle|\,{\mathop{Re}}(\mu)=0\right\}.

For elements
\mu
of
M_{n}
, we define the linear transformation
\mu\mapsto\widehat{\mu}\in\mathbb{C}^{n-1}
by

\widehat{\mu}_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{i}\mu_{j},

and we define a coordinate-wise (as in (
1

\hat{p}_{s}(y)=\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}\left|y_{i}\right|^{s_{i}},

then we have
p_{\mu}(y)=\hat{p}_{\hat{\mu}}(y)
.
In particular,
\widehat{\rho}_{i}=\frac{i(n-i)}{2}
, and we apply the conventions
\widehat{\mu}^{w}=\widehat{\mu^{w}}
,
\widehat{\mu}^{w}_{i}=(\widehat{\mu}^{w})_{i}
.
We apply the same construction to
\delta\mapsto\hat{\delta}
.

6.3.
Representations of
K

Throughout the paper,
\sigma
will always refer to an irreducible, unitary representation
\sigma:K\to GL(n,\mathbb{C})
.
Note that
\sigma|_{O(m)}
,
2\leq m<n
is isomorphic to a sum of irreducible, unitary representations of
O(m)
, and we assume equality in place of the isomorphism, while for the last step, we assume
\sigma|_{SO(2)}
is diagonalized.
These assumptions are effectively choosing a representative of the isormorphism class of
\sigma
.

If
\widehat{K}
is the set of all such
\sigma
, then we have the Peter-Weyl Theorem

(15)

\displaystyle f(k)=

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in\widehat{K}}(\dim\sigma)\Tr\left(\sigma(k)\int_{K}f(k^{\prime}){\overline{\sigma(k)}}^{T}dk\right),

for
f\in L^{2}(K)
.

6.4.
Whittaker functions

The Jacquet-Whittaker function arises from applying a certain integral operator to elements of irreducible adminisible representations of
G
.
Since these can always be viewed as subrepresentations of principal series representations and we may decompose
L^{2}(K)
using the Peter-Weyl theorem, it is sufficient to consider matrix-valued Whittaker functions of the form
W_{\sigma}(g,\mu,\delta)=W_{\sigma}(g,w_{l},\mu,\delta,\psi_{I})
where

(16)

\displaystyle W_{\sigma}(g,w,\mu,\delta,\psi):=\int_{\overline{U}_{w}(\mathbb{R})}I_{\mu,\delta,\sigma}(wxg)\overline{\psi(x)}dx,\qquad{\mathop{Re}}(\mu_{i}-\mu_{j})>0,i<j

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} I_{\mu,\delta,\sigma}=&\Sigma_{\delta,\sigma}I_{\mu,\sigma},&I_{\mu,\sigma}(xyk)=&p_{\rho+\mu}(y)\sigma(k),&\Sigma_{\delta,\sigma}=&\frac{1}{\left|V\right|}\sum_{v\in V}\chi_{\delta}(v)\sigma(v),\end{aligned}

for an irreducible, unitary (finite-dimensional) representation
\sigma
of
K
.
Hopefully the reader will forgive the nonstandard definition which gives the transformations

W_{\sigma}(xgk,w,\mu,\delta,\psi)=\psi(x)W_{\sigma}(g,w,\mu,\delta,\psi)\sigma(k),\qquad x\in\overline{U}_{w}(\mathbb{R}),k\in K.

Anyone looking for a left action by
\sigma
can simply replace
\sigma
with
\sigma^{-1}
in the definition above.

We may generally reduce to
\psi=\psi_{I}
through

(17)

\displaystyle W_{\sigma}(g,w,\mu,\delta,\psi_{yt})=

\displaystyle p_{-\rho-\mu^{w}}(y)\chi_{\delta^{w}}(y)W_{\sigma}(yg,w,\mu,\delta,\psi_{t})

The
(\mu,\delta)\mapsto(\mu^{w},\delta^{w})
functional equation of the Whittaker function can be written

(18)

\displaystyle W_{\sigma}(g,\mu,\delta)=

\displaystyle M_{\sigma}(w,\mu,\delta)W_{\sigma}(g,\mu^{w},\delta^{w}),

where we assume
M_{\sigma}(w,\mu,\delta)=M_{\sigma}(w,\mu,\delta)\Sigma_{\delta^{w},\sigma^{w}}
.
Note: More generally, one can drop
\Sigma_{\delta,\sigma}
from the definition of the Whittaker function and write the functional equation as
W_{\sigma}(g,\mu)=M_{\sigma}(w,\mu)W_{\sigma}(g,\mu^{w})
, then
M_{\sigma}(w,\mu,\delta)=M_{\sigma}(w,\mu)\Sigma_{\delta^{w},\sigma^{w}}
.

To make later computations slightly clearer, we also define

(19)

\displaystyle W^{*}_{m,\sigma}(g,\mu,\delta)=

\displaystyle p_{-\rho_{m}}(g)W_{\sigma}(g,w_{l,m},\mu,\delta,\psi_{I});

in case
m\leq n
, this is a block-diagonal matrix of
GL(m)
Whittaker functions (renormalized slightly).

For

8.
Matrix Decompositions

The goal of this section is essentially to compute the Iwasawa decomposition of
wx
for
w\in W^{\text{rel}}
and
x\in\overline{U}_{w}(\mathbb{R})
.
Throughout this section, the letters
a,b,c,d
as subscripts are used to distinguish matrices of a similar type, not as indices.

We proceed in stages and the general procedure for each stage is essentially the same:
(carefully) factor
wx=x_{a}x_{b}
with
x_{a},x_{b}\in{U(\mathbb{R})}^{T}
, compute the Iwasawa decomposition of
x_{b}=x^{*}_{b}y^{*}_{b}k^{*}_{b}
, (somehow) interchange
x_{a}x^{*}_{b}=x^{\dagger}_{b}x^{\dagger}_{a}
with
x^{\dagger}_{b}\in U(\mathbb{R})
and
x^{\dagger}_{a}\in{U(\mathbb{R})}^{T}
, compute the Iwasawa decomposition of
y^{*-1}_{b}x^{\dagger}_{a}y^{*}_{b}=x^{*}_{a}y^{*}_{a}k^{*}_{a}
, then the final Iwasawa decomposition becomes

wx=\left(x^{\dagger}_{b}y^{*}_{b}x^{*}ay^{*-1}_{b}\right)\left(y^{*}_{b}y^{*}_{a}\right)\left(k^{*}_{a}k^{*}_{b}\right).

To simplify the process as much as possible, we perform coordinate substitutions at each stage.

We then apply these matrix decompositions to the integral definitions of the Whittaker and Bessel functions to derive inductive constructions of a sort.
An implementation of the decompositions in Mathematica is given in
appendix

.

9.1.
Jacquet integrals and limits

For the
Asymptotics Theorem

Throughout this section, the letters
a,b
as subscripts are used to distinguish matrices of a similar type, not as indices.

For
y\in Y_{w}
, define

Suppose

\displaystyle W_{\sigma}(yg,\mu,\delta)=

\displaystyle p_{\rho+\mu^{w_{l}}}(y)\chi_{\delta^{w_{l}}}(y)W_{\sigma}(g,w_{l},\mu,\delta,\psi_{y})

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle p_{\rho+\mu^{w_{l}}}(y)\chi_{\delta^{w_{l}}}(y)W_{\sigma}(g,w_{l},\mu,\delta,\psi_{\alpha_{w}}),\qquad\text{ as }y\to 0.

Then we decompose

\displaystyle\int_{U(\mathbb{R})}I_{\mu,\delta,\sigma}(w_{l}uwg)\overline{\psi_{\alpha_{w}}(u)}du

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{U_{w^{-1}}(\mathbb{R})}\int_{\overline{U}_{w^{-1}}(\mathbb{R})}I_{\mu,\delta,\sigma}(w_{l}u_{a}u_{b}wg)\overline{\psi_{I}(u_{b})}du_{a}\,du_{b},

gives

\displaystyle W_{\sigma}(wg,w_{l},\mu,\delta,\psi_{\alpha_{w}})=

\displaystyle\int_{U_{w}(\mathbb{R})}\int_{\overline{U}_{w^{-1}}(\mathbb{R})}I_{\mu,\delta,\sigma}(w_{l}u_{a}wu_{b}g)\overline{\psi_{I}(u_{b})}du_{a}\,du_{b}.

\displaystyle W_{\sigma}(wg,w_{l},\mu,\delta,\psi_{\alpha_{w}})=

\displaystyle\int_{U_{w}(\mathbb{R})}\int_{\overline{U}_{w^{-1}}(\mathbb{R})}I_{\mu,\delta,\sigma}(w^{-1}u_{a}u^{*}y^{*})\overline{\psi_{I}(u_{b})}\sigma(k^{*})du_{a}\,du_{b}

\displaystyle W_{\sigma}(wg,w_{l},\mu,\delta,\psi_{\alpha_{w}})=

\displaystyle W_{\sigma}(I,w^{-1},\mu,\delta,\psi_{0})\int_{U_{w}(\mathbb{R})}I_{\mu^{w^{-1}},\delta^{w^{-1}},\sigma}(w_{l}u_{b}g)\overline{\psi_{I}(u_{b})}du_{b}.

For the
