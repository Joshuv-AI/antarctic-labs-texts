Directing Words of Fuzzy Finite Automata
Magnus Steinby

Abstract
A deterministic finite automaton is directable if it has a directing word which takes the automaton from every state to the same state. These notions have been extended also to other kinds of automata. Thus, B. Imreh and M. Steinby (1999) identified three natural types of directing words, called D1–, D2– and D3–directing words, for nondeterministic finite automata (NFAs). Here we adapt these notions for fuzzy finite automata (FFAs). The D3–directing words obtained this way are precisely the directing words introduced by V. Karthikeyan and M. Rajasekar (2015). With any FFA ℱ {\mathcal{F}} we associate an NFA ℱ 𝐧𝐝 {\mathcal{F}}^{{\mathbf{n}}{\mathbf{d}}} which has the same D i i –directing words as ℱ {\mathcal{F}} . Thus, if these definitions are used, the theory of directable FFAs reduces to that of NFAs. We also introduce three new kinds of directing words of fuzzy automata that we call DD1–, DD2– and DD3–directing words, respectively which depend more on the fuzzy transition degrees between states. We establish some basic properties of the sets D ​ D i ​ ( ℱ ) DD_{i}({\mathcal{F}}) of DD i i –directing words of any given FFA ℱ {\mathcal{F}} . In particular, it is shown that these languages are regular, and that DD i i –directability is decidable. For so-called normal FFA s the languages D ​ D i ​ ( ℱ ) DD_{i}({\mathcal{F}}) are shown to have some special properties. Several relationships between the families of the corresponding sets D ​ D i ​ ( ℱ ) DD_{i}({\mathcal{F}}) of DD i i –directing words are presented. We also determine the complete ∩ \cap -semilattice of the various classes of DD i i –directable FFAs and normal FFAs and their intersections.

Some basic notions

We may write
A:=B
to emphasize that
A
is defined to be equal to
B
. The cardinality of a set
A
is denoted by
|A|
, and the set of all subsets of
A
by
\wp(A)
. For any integer
n\geq 0
, let
{\mathbf{n}}:=\{1,\ldots,n\}
.

In what follows,
X
is always a finite nonempty alphabet. The set of all (finite) words over
X
is denoted by
X^{*}
and the empty word by
\varepsilon
. Furthermore, let
X^{+}:=X^{*}\setminus\{\varepsilon\}
. The length of a word
w\in X^{*}
is denoted by
\mathrm{lg}(w)
. Subsets of
X^{*}
are called
languages
. A
family of languages

{\mathcal{L}}
assigns to each finite nonempty alphabet
X
a set
{\mathcal{L}}(X)
of languages over
X
. We write
{\mathcal{L}}=\{{\mathcal{L}}(X)\}_{X}
.

A
fuzzy language
over
X
is a mapping
\lambda:X^{*}\rightarrow[0,1]
, where the set of
degrees of membership
is the real unit interval
[0,1]
ordered by the usual
\leq
-relation and equipped with the lattice operations
r\vee s=\max(r,s)
and
r\wedge s=\min(r,s)
. Thus we follow Zadeh’s
[
27
]
original definition.
The
support
of
\lambda
, is the language
\mathrm{supp}(\lambda):=\{w\in X^{*}\mid\lambda(w)>0\}
, and its
range
is the set
\mathrm{ran}(\lambda):=\{\lambda(w)\mid w\in X^{*}\}
. If
\mathrm{supp}(\lambda)
is a finite set
\{w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}\}
, we may write

\lambda=\{w_{1}/\lambda(w_{1}),\ldots,w_{n}/\lambda(w_{n})\}.\vskip-3.0pt

If
\mathrm{ran}(\lambda)\subseteq\{0,1\}
, then
\lambda
is said to be
crisp
.

A
deterministic finite automaton
, a
DFA
for short,
\mathcal{A}=(A,X,\delta)
consists of a finite nonempty set
A
of
states
, the
input alphabet

X
, and a
transition function

\delta:A\times X\rightarrow A
which is extended in the usual way to a mapping
\delta^{*}:A\times X^{*}\rightarrow A
.

A
deterministic finite recognizer (DFR)

{\mathbf{A}}=(A,X,\delta,a_{0},F)
is a DFA
\mathcal{A}=(A,X,\delta)
equipped with an
initial state

a_{0}\in A
and a set
F\subseteq A
of
final states
. It
recognizes
the language
L({\mathbf{A}}):=\{w\in X^{*}\mid\delta^{*}(a_{0},w)\in F\}
, and a language
L\subseteq X^{*}
is said to be
recognizable
, or
regular
, if
L=L({\mathbf{A}})
for such a recognizer
{\mathbf{A}}
. The set of regular languages over
X
is denoted by
Rec(X)
.

A
nondeterministic finite automaton (NFA)

\mathcal{N}=(A,X,\alpha)
consists of a finite nonempty set
A
of
states
, the
input alphabet

X
, and a nondeterministic
transition function

\alpha:A\times X\rightarrow\wp(A)
. The transition function is extended to a mapping

\alpha^{*}:\wp(A)\times X^{*}\rightarrow\wp(A)

as follows: for every
H\subseteq A
,

(1)

\alpha^{*}(H,\varepsilon)=H
, and

(2)

\alpha^{*}(H,ux)=\bigcup\{\alpha(b,x)\mid b\in\alpha^{*}(H,u)\}
for any
u\in X^{*}
and
x\in X
.

For any
H\subseteq A
and
w\in X^{*}
,
\alpha^{*}(H,w)
is the set
of states reachable by the input word
w
from some state in
H
. For any
a\in A
and
w\in X^{*}
, we write
\alpha^{*}(a,w)
for
\alpha^{*}(\{a\},w)
.
The NFA
{\mathcal{N}}
is
complete
, if

\alpha(a,x)\not=\emptyset
for all
a\in A
and
x\in X
. Obviously, then
\alpha^{*}(a,w)\not=\emptyset
for all
a\in A
and
w\in X^{*}
.

A
fuzzy finite automaton (FFA)

\mathcal{F}=(A,X,f)
consists of a finite nonempty set
A
of
states
, the
input alphabet

X
, and a fuzzy
transition function

f:A\times X\times A\rightarrow[0,1].\vskip-3.0pt

In concrete examples of an FFA
\mathcal{F}=(A,X,f)
, we specify the transition function
f
by giving the nonzero values
f(a,x,b)
.

The transition function is extended to
f^{*}:A\times X^{*}\times A\rightarrow[0,1]
by setting

(1)

f^{*}(a,\varepsilon,a)=1
, and
f^{*}(a,\varepsilon,b)=0
if
b\neq a
, and

(2)

f^{*}(a,w,b)=\max\{f^{*}(a,v,c)\land f(c,x,b)\}\mid c\in A\}

for
w=vx
with
v\in X^{*}
and
x\in X
,

for all
a,b\in A
. For any
a\in A
and
w\in X^{*}
, let

{\mathcal{F}}(a,w):=\{b\in A\mid f^{*}(a,w,b)>0\}.\vskip-3.0pt

Thus
{\mathcal{F}}(a,w)
is the set of states reachable (to a nonzero degree) from state
a
by the input word
w
.
With any pair
a\in A
and
w\in X^{*}
, we also associate a fuzzy subset

{\mathcal{F}}_{a,w}:A\rightarrow[0,1],\>b\mapsto f^{*}(a,w,b),\vskip-3.0pt

of
A
. Obviously,
\mathrm{supp}({\mathcal{F}}_{a,w})={\mathcal{F}}(a,w)
.

For any
H\subseteq A
and
w\in X^{*}
, let
{\mathcal{F}}(H,w):=\bigcup\{{\mathcal{F}}(a,w)\mid a\in H\}
.

The fuzzy automaton
{\mathcal{F}}
is
complete
if
{\mathcal{F}}(a,x)\neq\emptyset
for all
a\in A
and
x\in X
. If
{\mathcal{F}}
is complete, then clearly
{\mathcal{F}}(a,w)\neq\emptyset
for all
a\in A
and
w\in X^{*}
.

3
Directing words and directable automata

A word
w\in X^{*}
is a
directing word
of a DFA
\mathcal{A}=(A,X,\delta)
if there is a state
c\in A
such that
\delta^{*}(a,w)=c
for every
a\in A
. The set of directing words of
{\mathcal{A}}
is denoted by
DW({\mathcal{A}})
, and
{\mathcal{A}}
is
directable
if
DW({\mathcal{A}})\neq\emptyset
.

In
[
12
]
three kinds of directing words of an NFA
\mathcal{N}=(A,X,\alpha)
were identified. A word
w\in X^{*}
was said to be

(D1)

D1–directing
if
(\exists c\in A)(\forall a\in A)(\alpha^{*}(a,w)=\{c\})
,

(D2)

D2–directing
if
(\forall a,b\in A)(\alpha^{*}(a,w)=\alpha^{*}(b,w)
, and

(D3)

D3–directing
if
(\exists c\in A)(\forall a\in A)(c\in\alpha^{*}(a,w))
.

For each
i=1,2,3
, the set of D
i
–directing words of
{\mathcal{N}}
is denoted by
D_{i}({\mathcal{N}})
, and
{\mathcal{N}}
is called D
i
–
directable
if
D_{i}({\mathcal{N}})\neq\emptyset
.

If we regard a DFA as a special NFA, all three conditions (D
i
) yield the usual directing words. To state this more precisely, we associate with each DFA
\mathcal{A}=(A,X,\delta)
an NFA
{\mathcal{A}}^{{\mathbf{n}}{\mathbf{d}}}=(A,X,\alpha)
, where
\alpha:A\times X\rightarrow\wp(A)
is defined by
\alpha(a,x):=\{\delta(a,x)\}
(
a\in A,x\in X
). It is clear that
\alpha^{*}(a,w)=\{\delta^{*}(a,w)\}
for all
a\in A
and
w\in X^{*}
, which implies the following facts.

Remark 3.1

For any DFA
{\mathcal{A}}
,
DW({\mathcal{A}})=D_{1}({\mathcal{A}}^{{\mathbf{n}}{\mathbf{d}}})=D_{2}({\mathcal{A}}^{{\mathbf{n}}{\mathbf{d}}})=D_{3}({\mathcal{A}}^{{\mathbf{n}}{\mathbf{d}}})
.

Clearly, the directability of fuzzy automata may also be defined in several ways. The definition suggested by
Karthikeyan
and
Rajasekar

[
16
]
may be stated as follows:
w\in X^{*}
is a
directing word
of an FFA
\mathcal{F}=(A,X,f)
if there is a state
c\in A
such that
f^{*}(a,w,c)>0
(i.e.
c\in{\mathcal{F}}(a,w)
) for every
a\in A
, and
{\mathcal{F}}
is said to be
directable
if it has a directing word. Using these notions the authors attempt to present some ideas and results appearing in
[
11
]
in a ‘fuzzified’ form. The discussion of these and related matters can be simplified by the following reduction to nondeterministic automata.

Consider an FFA
\mathcal{F}=(A,X,f)
. A word
w=x_{1}\ldots x_{k}

(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\in X)
is directing for
{\mathcal{F}}
if and only if there is a state
c\in A
such that for every
a\in A
there is a chain of states
a=a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}=c
such that
f(a_{i-1},x_{i},a_{i})>0
for every
i\in{\mathbf{k}}
. The actual values of the degrees
f(a_{i-1},x_{i},a_{i})
do not matter as long as they are positive. Hence, we could assign every positive transition the value 1 without changing the set of directing words. This means that when we are interested just in directing words,
{\mathcal{F}}
may be replaced by the
associated NFA

{\mathcal{F}}^{{\mathbf{n}}{\mathbf{d}}}=(A,X,\alpha)
, where
\alpha
is defined by

\alpha(a,x):=\{b\in A\mid f(a,x,b)>0\}\qquad(x\in X,a\in A).\vskip-3.0pt

Example 3.2

Let
{\mathcal{F}}=(\{a,b,c\},\{x,y\},f)
be the fuzzy automaton, where the non-zero transitions are
f(a,x,b)=0.3
,
f(b,x,c)=0.4
,
f(c,x,b)=0.2
,
f(c,x,c)=0.6
,
f(b,y,b)=0.5
and
f(b,y,c)=0.1
.
Then the transitions of
{\mathcal{F}}^{{\mathbf{n}}{\mathbf{d}}}
are given by
\alpha(a,x)=\{b\}
,
\alpha(b,x)=\{c\}
,
\alpha(c,x)=\{b,c\}
,
\alpha(a,y)=\emptyset
,
\alpha(b,y)=\{b,c\}

\alpha(c,y)=\emptyset
.

It is easy to verify the following lemma by induction on

.

Lemma 3.3

Let
\mathcal{F}=(A,X,f)
be any FFA and

\alpha^{*}(a,w)={\mathcal{F}}(a,w)

Let us also note the following obvious fact.

A fuzzy finite automaton

Remark 3.5

A converse construction is possible, too, i.e., with any NFA

4
Directability with degrees

We shall now introduce three new types of directing words for FFAs that are analogous to the D1–, D2– and D3–directing words but depend more on the transition degrees
f(a,x,b)
of the automaton.

For a given FFA
\mathcal{F}=(A,X,f)
, we say that a word
w\in X^{*}
is

(DD1)

DD1–directing
if
(\exists c\in A)(\exists r\in(0,1])(\forall a\in A)({\mathcal{F}}_{a,w}=\{c/r\})
,

(DD2)

DD2–directing
if
(\forall a,b\in A)({\mathcal{F}}_{a,w}={\mathcal{F}}_{b,w})
, and

(DD3)

DD3–directing
if
(\exists c\in A)(\forall a\in A)({\mathcal{F}}_{a,w}(c)=\max\{{\mathcal{F}}_{a,w}(b)\mid b\in A\}>0)
.

For each
i=1,2,3
, the set of DD
i
–directing words of
{\mathcal{F}}
is denoted by
D\!D_{i}({\mathcal{F}})
, and
{\mathcal{F}}
is said to be
DD
i
–directable
if

D\!D_{i}({\mathcal{F}})\neq\emptyset
.

If condition (DD1) holds, then for every state
a\in A
,
c
is the only state reachable from
a
by the input word
w
, and its reachability degree
{\mathcal{F}}_{a,w}(c)
has the same value
r>0
for every
a
.
That
w
satisfies (DD2) means that the fuzzy set
{\mathcal{F}}_{a,w}
of states reached from a given state
a
by input
w
is the same for every
a
, i.e.,
f^{*}(a,w,c)=f^{*}(b,w,c)
for all
a,b,c\in A
. Finally, condition (DD3) means that there is a state
c
which has the maximal reachability degree
{\mathcal{F}}_{a,w}(c)>0
by the input
w
from any state
a\in A
.

Let us see how these notions are related to each other and the previously considered types of directing words of fuzzy automata.

Proposition 4.1

Let
\mathcal{F}=(A,X,f)
be any FFA.

(a)

D\!D_{1}({\mathcal{F}})\subseteq D_{1}({\mathcal{F}})
,

(b)

D\!D_{1}({\mathcal{F}})\subseteq D\!D_{2}({\mathcal{F}})\subseteq D_{2}({\mathcal{F}})
,

(c)

D\!D_{1}({\mathcal{F}})\subseteq D\!D_{3}({\mathcal{F}})\subseteq D_{3}({\mathcal{F}})
, and

(d)

D\!D_{1}({\mathcal{F}})\subseteq D\!D_{2}({\mathcal{F}})\cap D\!D_{3}({\mathcal{F}})
.

Any one of the inclusions may be proper.

Proof.
The inclusions follow rather easily from the definitions. Let us prove statements (b) and (d).

If
w\in D\!D_{1}({\mathcal{F}})
, and (DD1) holds for
c\in A
and
r\in(0,1]
, then
{\mathcal{F}}_{a,w}=\{c/r\}={\mathcal{F}}_{b,w}
for all
a,b\in A
, and thus
w\in D\!D_{2}({\mathcal{F}})
.

If
w\in D\!D_{2}({\mathcal{F}})
, then
{\mathcal{F}}(a,w)=\mathrm{supp}({\mathcal{F}}_{a,w})=\mathrm{supp}({\mathcal{F}}_{w,b})={\mathcal{F}}(b,w)
for all
a,b\in A
, and hence
w\in D_{2}({\mathcal{F}})
.

To show that the inclusion
D\!D_{1}({\mathcal{F}})\subseteq D\!D_{2}({\mathcal{F}})
may be proper, we consider the FFA
\mathcal{F}=(A,X,f)
, where
A=\{a,b\}
,
X=\{x\}
, and
f
is defined by
f(a,x,a)=f(b,x,a)=f(a,x,b)=f(b,x,b)=1
. The word
x
does not belong to
D\!D_{1}({\mathcal{F}})
because
{\mathcal{F}}_{a,x}=\{a/1,b/1\}
. On the other hand,
x\in D\!D_{2}({\mathcal{F}})
because
{\mathcal{F}}_{a,x}=\{a/1,b/1\}={\mathcal{F}}_{b,x}
.

The inclusion
D\!D_{2}({\mathcal{F}})\subseteq D_{2}({\mathcal{F}})
is proper for
{\mathcal{F}}=(\{a,b\},\{x\},f)
, if
f
is defined by
f(a,x,b)=0.1
and
f(b,x,b)=0.2
. Now
x\notin D\!D_{2}({\mathcal{F}})
because
{\mathcal{F}}_{a,x}(b)=0.1
while
{\mathcal{F}}_{b,x}(b)=0.2
. On the other hand,
x\in D_{2}({\mathcal{F}})
since
{\mathcal{F}}(a,x)=\{b\}={\mathcal{F}}(b,x)
.

The inclusion relation in (d) follows from (b) and (c). That it may be proper, is seen by considering the first FFA defined above in this proof; the word
x
belongs to
D\!D_{2}({\mathcal{F}})\cap D\!D_{3}({\mathcal{F}})
, but not to
D\!D_{1}({\mathcal{F}})

\Box

Let us say that an FFA
\mathcal{F}=(A,X,f)
is
crisp
if
f(a,x,b)\in\{0,1\}
for all
a,b\in A
and
x\in X
. It is easy to see that if
{\mathcal{F}}
is crisp, then for all
a,b\in A
and
w\in X^{*}
,

(1)
f^{*}(a,w,b)\in\{0,1\}
, and

(2)
{\mathcal{F}}_{a,w}=\{a_{1}/1,\ldots,a_{k}/1\}
, where
\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\}={\mathcal{F}}(a,w)
.

From (2) we get the following facts.

Remark 4.2

If
\mathcal{F}=(A,X,f)
is a crisp FFA, then
D\!D_{1}({\mathcal{F}})=D_{1}({\mathcal{F}}^{{\mathbf{n}}{\mathbf{d}}})=D_{1}({\mathcal{F}})
,
D\!D_{2}({\mathcal{F}})=D_{2}({\mathcal{F}}^{{\mathbf{n}}{\mathbf{d}}})=D_{2}({\mathcal{F}})
and
D\!D_{3}({\mathcal{F}})=D_{3}({\mathcal{F}}^{{\mathbf{n}}{\mathbf{d}}})=D_{3}({\mathcal{F}})
.

Proof.
Let us verify the first pair of equalities. For any
w\in X^{*}
,

\displaystyle w\in D\!D_{1}({\mathcal{F}})

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow(\exists c\in A)(\exists r\in(0,1])(\forall a\in A){\mathcal{F}}_{a,w}=\{c/r\}\vskip-3.0pt

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow(\exists c\in A)(\forall a\in A){\mathcal{F}}_{a,w}=\{c/1\}\vskip-3.0pt

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow(\exists c\in A)(\forall a\in A){\mathcal{F}}(a,w)=\{c\}\vskip-3.0pt

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow w\in D_{1}({\mathcal{F}}^{{\mathbf{n}}{\mathbf{d}}}).\vskip-3.0pt

Hence,
D\!D_{1}({\mathcal{F}})=D_{1}({\mathcal{F}}^{{\mathbf{n}}{\mathbf{d}}})
, and
D_{1}({\mathcal{F}}^{{\mathbf{n}}{\mathbf{d}}})=D_{1}({\mathcal{F}})
by Proposition
3.6
.
The other two pairs of equalities have similar straightforward proofs.

\Box

When dealing with fuzzy finite automata it may be convenient to use their matrix representations.
For any
m,n\geq 1
, a
fuzzy
m\times n
-matrix
is an
m\times n
-array
(r_{ij})
in which
r_{ij}\in[0,1]
for all
i\in{\mathbf{m}}
and
j\in{\mathbf{n}}
. The
product
of a fuzzy
m\times k
-matrix
R=(r_{ij})
and a fuzzy
k\times n
-matrix
S=(s_{ij})
is the fuzzy
m\times n
-matrix
RS=(t_{ij})
, where

t_{ij}=(r_{i1}\land s_{1j})\vee\ldots\vee(r_{ik}\land s_{kj})\qquad(i\in{\mathbf{m}},j\in{\mathbf{n}}).\vskip-3.0pt

Let
\mathcal{F}=(A,X,f)
be an
n
-state FFA with
A=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\}
. With any word
w\in X^{*}
, we associate its fuzzy
n\times n

transition matrix

M^{\mathcal{F}}_{w}=M_{w}=(r_{ij}),\text{ where }r_{ij}=f^{*}(a_{i},w,a_{j})\qquad(i,j\in{\mathbf{n}}).

For any words
u,v\in X^{*}
,
M_{uv}=M_{u}M_{v}
because

f(a_{i},uv,a_{j})=\max\{f^{*}(a_{i},u,a_{k})\land f^{*}(a_{k},v,a_{j})\mid k\in{\mathbf{n}}\}\qquad(i,j\in{\mathbf{n}}).\vskip-3.0pt

For any
w\in X^{*}
and
i\in{\mathbf{n}}
, the
i^{th}
row
(r_{i1}\,r_{i2}\,\ldots\,r_{in})
of
M_{w}
represents the fuzzy set
{\mathcal{F}}_{a_{i},w}
, i.e.,

r_{i1}={\mathcal{F}}_{a_{i},w}(a_{1}),\,r_{i2}={\mathcal{F}}_{a_{i},w}(a_{2}),\ldots,r_{in}={\mathcal{F}}_{a_{i},w}(a_{n})\qquad(i\in{\mathbf{n}})\vskip-3.0pt

By this observation, one may deduce from the matrix
M_{w}
whether the word
w
is DD1–, DD2– or DD3–directing, and this will be used in the proof of the following proposition.

Proposition 4.3

For any FFA
\mathcal{F}=(A,X,f)
, the languages
D\!D_{1}({\mathcal{F}})
,
D\!D_{2}({\mathcal{F}})
and
D\!D_{3}({\mathcal{F}})
are recognizable.

Proof.
Let
A=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\}
. The set
B:=\{M_{w}\mid w\in X^{*}\}
is finite because all the matrix entries belong to the finite set
\{f(a_{i},x,a_{j})\mid i,j\in{\mathbf{n}},x\in X\}\cup\{1\}
. To see this, it suffices to notice that no new elements are introduced when matrices are multiplied as the only operations used are the join
\max(r,s)
and meet
\min(r,s)
in the chain
([0,1],\leq)
.

Let
(B,X,\delta)
be the DFA for which
\delta:B\times X\rightarrow B
is defined by
\delta(M_{w},x)=M_{wx}
(
w\in X^{*},x\in X
). It is easy to show that
\delta^{*}(M_{\varepsilon},w)=M_{w}
for every
w\in X^{*}
.
From this observation, it follows that for each
i=1,2,3
, we get a DFR
{\mathbf{B}}_{i}=(B,X,\delta,M_{\varepsilon},F_{i})
recognizing
D\!D_{i}({\mathcal{F}})
by the following choices of the sets
F_{i}
of final states.

1.

M_{w}\in F_{1}
if and only if for some
j\in{\mathbf{n}}
and
r\in(0,1]
,
r_{1j}=\ldots=r_{nj}=r
and
r_{ik}=0
for all
i,k\in{\mathbf{n}}
with
k\neq j
.

2.

M_{w}\in F_{2}
if and only if all the rows in
M_{w}
are identical.

3.

M_{w}\in F_{3}
if and only if for some
j\in{\mathbf{n}}
, in every row, the
j^{th}
element is maximal and positive.
\Box

In Remark
3.8
we noted some equalities satisfied by the
D_{i}({\mathcal{F}})
-languages, but not all of them hold for the
D\!D_{i}({\mathcal{F}})
-languages. For example, let
{\mathcal{F}}=(\{a,b\},\{x,y\},f)
be the complete FFA in which the nonzero transitions are
f(a,x,b)=f(b,x,b)=f(b,y,b)=1
and
f(a,y,a)=0.5
. Then
x\in D\!D_{1}({\mathcal{F}})
because
{\mathcal{F}}_{a,x}={\mathcal{F}}_{b,x}=\{b/1\}
, but
yx\notin D\!D_{1}({\mathcal{F}})
since
{\mathcal{F}}_{a,yx}=\{b/0.5\}
while
{\mathcal{F}}_{b,yx}=\{b/1\}
. Hence,
X^{*}D\!D_{1}({\mathcal{F}})\neq D\!D_{1}({\mathcal{F}})
. Similarly,
X^{*}D\!D_{2}({\mathcal{F}})\neq D\!D_{2}({\mathcal{F}})
because
x
is a D2–directing word of
{\mathcal{F}}
, but
yx
is not.
However, the equality
X^{*}D\!D_{1}({\mathcal{F}})=D\!D_{1}({\mathcal{F}})
, for example, holds if we replace the completeness of an FFA by the stronger property of “normality”.

Let us say that an FFA
\mathcal{F}=(A,X,f)
is
normal
if for every pair
a\in A,\,x\in X
, there is a state
b\in A
for which
f(a,x,b)=1
, i.e.,
{\mathcal{F}}_{a,x}
is a normal fuzzy set.

Lemma 4.4

If
\mathcal{F}=(A,X,f)
is a normal FFA, then for all
a\in A
and
w\in X^{*}
, there is a state
b\in A
such that
f^{*}(a,w,b)=1
. Hence, if
{\mathcal{F}}
is normal and
w\in D\!D_{3}({\mathcal{F}})
, then there is a
c\in A
such that
f^{*}(a,w,c)=1
for every
a\in A
.

Proposition 4.5

For any FFA
\mathcal{F}=(A,X,f)
,

(a)

D\!D_{2}({\mathcal{F}})X^{*}=D\!D_{2}({\mathcal{F}})
.

If
{\mathcal{F}}
is normal, then we also have

(b)

X^{*}D\!D_{1}({\mathcal{F}})=D\!D_{1}({\mathcal{F}})
,

(c)

X^{*}D\!D_{2}({\mathcal{F}})X^{*}=D\!D_{2}({\mathcal{F}})
, and

(d)

X^{*}D\!D_{3}({\mathcal{F}})X^{*}=D\!D_{3}({\mathcal{F}})
.

Proof.
To prove (a), consider any words
w\in D\!D_{2}({\mathcal{F}})
and
u\in X^{*}
. For all
a,b,c\in A
,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}_{a,wu}(c)

\displaystyle=\max\{f^{*}(a,w,d)\land f^{*}(d,u,c)\mid d\in A\}\vskip-3.0pt

\displaystyle=\max\{f^{*}(b,w,d)\land f^{*}(d,u,c)\mid d\in A\}={\mathcal{F}}_{b,wu}(c),\vskip-3.0pt

which shows that
D\!D_{2}({\mathcal{F}})X^{*}\subseteq D\!D_{2}({\mathcal{F}})
. The converse inclusion is obvious.

Assume now that
{\mathcal{F}}
is normal. To prove (b), consider any
w\in D\!D_{1}({\mathcal{F}})
, and let
c\in A
and
r\in(0,1]
be such that
{\mathcal{F}}_{a,w}=\{c/r\}
for every
a\in A
. Take any
u\in X^{*}
and
a\in A
. By Lemma
4.4
, there is a
b\in A
such that
f^{*}(a,u,b)=1
, and therefore

\displaystyle f^{*}(a,uw,c)

\displaystyle=\max\{f^{*}(a,u,d)\land f^{*}(d,w,c)\mid d\in A\}\vskip-3.0pt

\displaystyle=\max\{f^{*}(a,u,d)\mid d\in A\}\land r\vskip-3.0pt

\displaystyle=1\land r=r,\vskip-3.0pt

where we used the fact that
f^{*}(d,w,c)=r
for every
d\in A
. On the other hand, if
c^{\prime}\in A
,
c^{\prime}\neq c
, then for every
a\in A

\displaystyle f^{*}(a,uw,c^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\max\{f^{*}(a,u,d)\land f^{*}(d,w,c^{\prime})\mid d\in A\}\vskip-3.0pt

\displaystyle=\max\{f^{*}(a,u,d)\land 0\mid d\in A\}\vskip-3.0pt=0.\vskip-3.0pt

This shows that
uw\in D\!D_{1}({\mathcal{F}})
, and hence
X^{*}D\!D_{1}({\mathcal{F}})\subseteq D\!D_{1}({\mathcal{F}})
.

As we already have
D\!D_{2}({\mathcal{F}})X^{*}=D\!D_{2}({\mathcal{F}})
, we get (c) by showing that
X^{*}D\!D_{2}({\mathcal{F}})\subseteq D\!D_{2}({\mathcal{F}})
. Let
u\in X^{*}
and
w\in D\!D_{2}({\mathcal{F}})
, and consider any
a,b,c\in A
. Let
a^{\prime},b^{\prime}\in A
be such that
f^{*}(a,u,a^{\prime})=f^{*}(b,u,b^{\prime})=1
. Then

\displaystyle f^{*}(a,uw,c)

\displaystyle=\max\{f^{*}(a,u,d)\land f^{*}(d,w,c)\mid d\in A\}\vskip-3.0pt

\displaystyle=f^{*}(a,u,a^{\prime})\land f^{*}(a^{\prime},w,c)\vskip-3.0pt

\displaystyle=1\land f^{*}(a^{\prime},w,c)=1\land f^{*}(b^{\prime},w,c)\vskip-3.0pt

\displaystyle=\max\{f^{*}(b,u,d)\land f^{*}(d,w,c)\mid d\in A\}\vskip-3.0pt

\displaystyle=f^{*}(b,uw,c),\vskip-3.0pt

where we also used the fact that
f^{*}(a^{\prime},w,c)=f^{*}(d,w,c)=f^{*}(b^{\prime},w,c)
for every
d\in A
. Thus we have shown that
uw\in D\!D_{2}({\mathcal{F}})
.

Finally, to prove (d), consider any
u\in X^{*}
and
w\in D\!D_{3}({\mathcal{F}})
. Let
c\in A
be a state such that, for every
a\in A
,
f^{*}(a,w,c)>0
and
f^{*}(a,w,b)\leq f^{*}(a,w,c)
for every
b\in A
. If
c^{\prime}\in A
is a state such that
f^{*}(c,u,c^{\prime})=1
, then for any
a\in A
,

\displaystyle f^{*}(a,wu,c^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\max\{f^{*}(a,w,d)\land f^{*}(d,u,c^{\prime})\mid d\in A\}\vskip-3.0pt

\displaystyle\geq f^{*}(a,w,c)\land f^{*}(c,u,c^{\prime})\vskip-3.0pt

\displaystyle=f^{*}(a,w,c)>0.

Moreover, for any
b\in A
,

\displaystyle f^{*}(a,wu,b)

\displaystyle=\max\{f^{*}(a,w,d)\land f^{*}(d,u,b)\mid d\in A\}\vskip-3.0pt

\displaystyle\leq f^{*}(a,w,c)\land\max\{f^{*}(d,u,b)\mid d\in A\}\vskip-3.0pt

\displaystyle\leq f^{*}(a,w,c)=f^{*}(a,w,c)\land f^{*}(c,u,c^{\prime})\vskip-3.0pt

\displaystyle\leq f^{*}(a,wu,c^{\prime}),

where we used the fact that

for every
d\in A

and thus

. The converse inclusion

holds trivially.
To complete the proof of (d), we show that
X^{*}D\!D_{3}({\mathcal{F}})\subseteq D\!D_{3}({\mathcal{F}})
. Let
w\in D\!D_{3}({\mathcal{F}})
,
c\in A
and
u\in X^{*}
be as above. Consider any

and let

be a state for which

. Then

\displaystyle f^{*}(a,uw,c)

\displaystyle=\max\{f^{*}(a,u,d)\land f^{*}(d,w,c)\mid d\in A\}\vskip-3.0pt

\displaystyle\geq f^{*}(a,u,a^{\prime})\land f^{*}(a^{\prime},w,c)=f^{*}(a^{\prime},w,c)>0\vskip-3.0pt

Moreover, for any
b\in A
,

\displaystyle f^{*}(a,uw,b)

\displaystyle=\max\{f^{*}(a,u,d)\land f^{*}(d,w,b)\mid d\in A\}\vskip-3.0pt

\displaystyle\leq\max\{f^{*}(a,u,d)\land f^{*}(d,w,c)\mid d\in A\}=f^{*}(a,uw,c),\vskip-3.0pt

and hence

.

\Box

is DD2– and DD3–directable, but it is not normal. The FFA
{\mathcal{G}}=(\{a,b,c\},\{x\},g)
, in which
g
is given by
g(a,x,b)=0.5
and
g(a,x,a)=g(b,x,c)=g(c,x,c)=1
, is normal and DD3–directable. For example,
xx\in D\!D_{3}({\mathcal{G}})
. On the other hand, there are no DD2–directing words as
{\mathcal{G}}_{a,x}=\{a/1,b/0.5\}
,
{\mathcal{G}}_{b,x}={\mathcal{G}}_{c,x}=\{c/1\}
, and
{\mathcal{G}}_{a,w}=\{a/1,c/0.5\}
,
{\mathcal{G}}_{b,w}={\mathcal{G}}_{c,w}=\{c/1\}
for any
w=x^{n}
with
n\geq 2
.

(i)
\mathbf{nDD}(2)\subset\mathbf{DD}(2)\cap\mathbf{DD}(3)
:

Of course,
\mathbf{nDD}(2)\subseteq\mathbf{DD}(2)
, and by using statements (f) and (a), we get
\mathbf{nDD}(2)\subseteq\mathbf{nDD}(3)\subseteq\mathbf{DD}(3)
. Thus
\mathbf{nDD}(2)\subseteq\mathbf{DD}(2)\cap\mathbf{DD}(3)
. The FFA
{\mathcal{F}}
used in the proof of (h) is DD2– and DD3–directable, but it is not normal. Hence, the inclusion is proper.

(j)
\mathbf{DD}(1)\cap\mathbf{nDD}(2)=\mathbf{nDD}(1)
:

The inclusion
\mathbf{nDD}(1)\subseteq\mathbf{DD}(1)\cap\mathbf{nDD}(2)
holds because
\mathbf{nDD}(1)\subseteq\mathbf{DD}(1)
by (a), and
\mathbf{nDD}(1)\subseteq\mathbf{nDD}(2)
by (e). On the other hand, if
{\mathcal{F}}\in\mathbf{DD}(1)\cap\mathbf{nDD}(2)
, then
{\mathcal{F}}
is both DD1–directable and normal, i.e.,
{\mathcal{F}}\in\mathbf{nDD}(1)
.

(k)
(\mathbf{DD}(2)\cap\mathbf{DD}(3))\cap\mathbf{nDD}(3)=\mathbf{nDD}(2)
:

Obviously, (k) is equivalent to the equality
\mathbf{DD}(2)\cap\mathbf{nDD}(3)=\mathbf{nDD}(2)
which has a similar proof as (j).

(l)
\mathbf{Dir}\subset\mathbf{nDD}(1)
:

Any DFA is clearly normal when viewed as an FFA, and by Lemma
5.1
is DD1–directable. On the other hand, the FFA
\mathcal{F}=(A,X,f)
, where
A=\{a,b,c\}
,
X=\{x\}
, and
f
is given by
f(a,x,b)=f(a,x,c)=f(b,x,c)=f(c,x,c)=1
, is DD1–directable and normal, but not a DFA.

\Box

Let us now consider the subautomata, epimorphic images and finite direct products of DD1–, DD2– and DD3–directable FFAs. In what follows,
\mathcal{F}=(A,X,f)
and
\mathcal{G}=(B,X,g)
are any two FFAs.

We call
{\mathcal{G}}
a
subautomaton
of
{\mathcal{F}}
if (1)
B\subseteq A
, (2)
{\mathcal{F}}(b,x)\subseteq B
for all
b\in B
and
x\in X
, and (3)
g(b,x,b^{\prime})=f(b,x,b^{\prime})
for all
b,b^{\prime}\in B
and
x\in X
.

Lemma 6.2

Let
{\mathcal{G}}
be a subautomaton of
{\mathcal{F}}
. For any
b\in B
and
w\in X^{*}
,

(a)

{\mathcal{F}}(b,w)\subseteq B
,

(b)

{\mathcal{G}}(b,w)={\mathcal{F}}(b,w)
, and

(c)

{\mathcal{G}}_{b,w}={\mathcal{F}}_{b,w}|_{B}
(= the restriction of
{\mathcal{F}}_{b,w}
to
B
).

Proof.
All three statements have easy proofs by induction on
\mathrm{lg}(w)
.
\Box

Proposition 6.3

Let
{\mathcal{G}}
be a subautomaton of an FFA
{\mathcal{F}}
. For each
i=1,2,3
,
D\!D_{i}({\mathcal{F}})\subseteq D\!D_{i}({\mathcal{G}})
, and thus any subautomaton of a DD
i
–directable FFA is also DD
i
–directable. Furthermore, if
{\mathcal{F}}
is normal, then so is
{\mathcal{G}}
.

Proof.
If
w\in D\!D_{1}({\mathcal{F}})
, then there exist
c\in A
and
r\in(0,1]
such that
{\mathcal{F}}_{a,w}=\{c/r\}
for every
a\in A
. In particular, for any
b\in B
,
c\in{\mathcal{F}}(b,w)\subseteq B
, and hence
{\mathcal{G}}_{b,w}={\mathcal{F}}_{b,w}|_{B}=\{c/r\}
. This means that
w\in D\!D_{1}({\mathcal{G}})
.

If
w\in D\!D_{2}({\mathcal{F}})
, then
{\mathcal{G}}_{a,w}={\mathcal{F}}_{a,w}|_{B}={\mathcal{F}}_{b,w}|_{B}={\mathcal{G}}_{b,w}
for all
a,b\in B
, i.e.,
w\in D\!D_{2}({\mathcal{G}})
.

Let
w\in D\!D_{3}({\mathcal{F}})
, and let
c\in A
be such that for every
a\in A
,
{\mathcal{F}}_{a,w}(c)>0
and
{\mathcal{F}}_{a,w}
is maximal among all the values of
{\mathcal{F}}_{a,w}
. Since this holds also for every
a\in B
, we have
c\in B
, and therefore
w\in D\!D_{3}({\mathcal{G}})
.

Assume now that
{\mathcal{F}}
is normal, and consider any
b\in B
and
x\in X
. Since
{\mathcal{F}}
is normal, there is a state
c\in A
such that
f(b,x,c)=1
. By Lemma
6.2
,
c\in B
and
g(b,x,c)=f(b,x,c)=1
, which shows that
{\mathcal{G}}
is normal.

\Box

A mapping
\varphi:A\rightarrow B
defines a
homomorphism

\varphi:{\mathcal{F}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{G}}
of FFAs (cf.
[
19
,
20
,
21
]
, for example) if

g(a\varphi,x,b)=\max\{f(a,x,a^{\prime})\mid a^{\prime}\in b\varphi^{-1}\},\vskip-3.0pt

for all
a\in A
,
b\in B
and
x\in X
. A surjective homomorphism is called an
epimorphism
.

Lemma 6.4

If
\varphi:{\mathcal{F}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{G}}
is an epimorphism of FFAs, then for all
a\in A
,
b\in B
and
w\in X^{*}
,

(a)

g^{*}(a\varphi,w,b)=\max\{f^{*}(a,w,a^{\prime})\mid a^{\prime}\in b\varphi^{-1}\}
, and

(b)

{\mathcal{F}}(a,w)\varphi={\mathcal{G}}(a\varphi,w)
.

Proof.
Statement (a) has a straightforward proof by induction on
\mathrm{lg}(w)
.

Let us consider (b). Also here we proceed by induction on
\mathrm{lg}(w)
.

If
a^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{F}}(a,w)
, then

g^{*}(a\varphi,w,a^{\prime}\varphi)=\max\{f^{*}(a,w,a^{\prime\prime})\mid a^{\prime\prime}\in A,a^{\prime\prime}\varphi=a^{\prime}\varphi\}\geq f(a,w,a^{\prime})>0\vskip-3.0pt

implies that
a^{\prime}\varphi\in{\mathcal{G}}(a\varphi,w)
, and hence that
{\mathcal{F}}(a,w)\varphi\subseteq{\mathcal{G}}(a\varphi,w)
. Conversely, if
b\in{\mathcal{G}}(a\varphi,w)
, then

\max\{f^{*}(a,w,a^{\prime})\mid a^{\prime}\in b\varphi^{-1}\}=g^{*}(a\varphi,w,b)>0\vskip-3.0pt

means that
f^{*}(a,w,a^{\prime})>0
, i.e.,
a^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{F}}(a,w)
, for some
a^{\prime}\in b\varphi^{-1}
, and thus
b\in{\mathcal{F}}(a,w)\varphi
.

\Box

Proposition 6.5

If
\varphi:{\mathcal{F}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{G}}
is an epimorphism of FFAs, then
D\!D_{i}({\mathcal{F}})\subseteq D\!D_{i}({\mathcal{G}})
for
i=1,2,3
, and thus all epimorphic images of a DD
i
–directable FFA are DD
i
–directable. Moreover, if
{\mathcal{F}}
is normal, then so is
{\mathcal{G}}
.

Proof.
Let us prove
D\!D_{i}({\mathcal{F}})\subseteq D\!D_{i}({\mathcal{G}})
for each value of
i
.

If
w\in D\!D_{1}({\mathcal{F}})
, then there exist a
c\in A
and an
r\in(0,1]
satisfying
{\mathcal{F}}_{a,w}=\{c/r\}
for every
a\in A
. Consider any
b\in B
. Since
\varphi
is surjective,
b=a\varphi
for some
a\in A
. For any
d\in B
,

g^{*}(b,w,d)=\max\{f^{*}(a,w,a^{\prime})\mid a^{\prime}\in d\varphi^{-1}\}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}r,&\ \mbox{if }d=c\varphi\\
0,&\ \mbox{if }d\neq c\varphi.\end{array}\right.

This means that
{\mathcal{G}}_{b,w}=\{c\varphi/r\}
for every
b\in B
, and thus
w\in D\!D_{1}({\mathcal{G}})
.

If
w\in D\!D_{2}({\mathcal{F}})
, then
f^{*}(a_{1},w,a)=f^{*}(a_{2},w,a)
for all
a_{1},a_{2},a\in A
. Consider any states
b_{1},b_{2}\in B
of
{\mathcal{G}}
, and let
a_{1},a_{2}\in A
be states of
{\mathcal{F}}
for which
b_{1}=a_{1}\varphi
and
b_{2}=a_{2}\varphi
. For any
b\in B
,

\displaystyle g^{*}(b_{1},w,b)

\displaystyle=\max\{f^{*}(a_{1},w,a)\mid a\in b\varphi^{-1}\}\vskip-3.0pt

\displaystyle=\max\{f^{*}(a_{2},w,a)\mid a\in b\varphi^{-1}\}=g^{*}(b_{2},w,b),\vskip-3.0pt

which shows that
w\in D\!D_{2}({\mathcal{G}})
.

Let
w\in D\!D_{3}({\mathcal{F}})
. Then there is a
c\in A
such that, for every
a\in A
,
f^{*}(a,w,c)>0
and
f^{*}(a,w,d)\leq f^{*}(a,w,c)
for every
d\in A
. Consider any
b,b^{\prime}\in B
, and let
a,a^{\prime}\in A
be states for which
b=a\varphi
and
b^{\prime}=a^{\prime}\varphi
. Then

g^{*}(b,w,c\varphi)=\max\{f^{*}(a,w,a^{\prime\prime})\mid a^{\prime\prime}\varphi=c\varphi\}\geq f^{*}(a,w,c)>0,\vskip-3.0pt

and

g^{*}(b,w,b^{\prime})=\max\{f^{*}(a,w,a^{\prime\prime})\mid a^{\prime\prime}\in b^{\prime}\varphi^{-1}\}\leq f^{*}(a,w,c)\leq g^{*}(b,w,c\varphi),

which means that
w\in D\!D_{3}({\mathcal{G}})
.

Assume now that
{\mathcal{F}}
is normal, and consider any
b\in B
and
x\in X
. If
a\in A
and
c\in A
are states for which
a\varphi=b
and
f(a,x,c)=1
, then

g(b,x,c\varphi)=g(a\varphi,x,c\varphi)=\max\{f(a,x,d)\mid d\varphi=c\varphi\}\geq f(a,x,c)=1,

i.e.,
g(b,x,c\varphi)=1
.

\Box

The
direct product
(cf.
[
19
,
20
]
, for example) of two FFAs
{\mathcal{F}}
and
{\mathcal{G}}
is the FFA
{\mathcal{F}}\times{\mathcal{G}}=(A\times B,X,h)
, where
h
is defined by

h((a,b),x,(a^{\prime},b^{\prime}))=f(a,x,a^{\prime})\land g(b,x,b^{\prime})\quad(a,a^{\prime}\in A,b,b^{\prime}\in B,x\in X).\vskip-3.0pt

The following example shows that DD
i
–directability (
i=1,2,3
) is not preserved under this product.

Example 6.6

Let
{\mathcal{F}}=(\{a,b\},\{x,y\},f)
and
{\mathcal{G}}=(\{1,2\},\{x,y\},g)
be FFAs, where
f
and
g
are given by
f(a,x,b)=f(b,x,b)=f(b,y,b)=1
, and
g(1,y,2)=g(2,y,2)=g(2,x,2)=1
. These FFAs belong to all three classes
\mathbf{DD}(1)
,
\mathbf{DD}(2)
and
\mathbf{DD}(3)
because
x\in D\!D_{1}({\mathcal{F}})\cap D\!D_{2}({\mathcal{F}})\cap D\!D_{3}({\mathcal{F}})
and
y\in D\!D_{1}({\mathcal{G}})\cap D\!D_{2}({\mathcal{G}})\cap D\!D_{3}({\mathcal{G}})
.

As
({\mathcal{F}}\times{\mathcal{G}})((a,1),x)=({\mathcal{F}}\times{\mathcal{G}})((a,1),y)=\emptyset
while
({\mathcal{F}}\times{\mathcal{G}})((b,2),x)=({\mathcal{F}}\times{\mathcal{G}})((b,2),y)=\{(b,2)\}\neq\emptyset
, it is clear that
{\mathcal{F}}\times{\mathcal{G}}
has no DD1–, DD2– or DD3–directing words, and
thus
{\mathcal{F}}\times{\mathcal{G}}\notin\mathbf{DD}(1)\cup\mathbf{DD}(2)\cup\mathbf{DD}(3)
.
