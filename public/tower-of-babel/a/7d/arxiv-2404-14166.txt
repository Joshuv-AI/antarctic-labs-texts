Partial algebras and implications of (weak) matrix properties
Michael Hoefnagel, Pierre-Alain Jacqmin

Abstract
Matrix properties are a type of property of categories which includes the ones of being Mal’tsev, arithmetical, majority, unital, strongly unital and subtractive. Recently, an algorithm has been developed to determine implications M ⇒ lex ∗ N \mathrm{M}\Rightarrow_{\mathrm{lex}_{\ast}}\mathrm{N} between them. We show here that this algorithm reduces to construct a partial term corresponding to N \mathrm{N} from a partial term corresponding to M \mathrm{M} . Moreover, we prove that this is further equivalent to the corresponding implication between the weak versions of these properties, i.e., the one where only strong monomorphisms are considered instead of all monomorphisms.

Keywords

Introduction

A pointed variety
\mdmathbb{V}
of universal algebras in said to be
subtractive
in the sense of
[
30
]
if its theory contains a binary term
x-y
(called a
subtraction
) satisfying the equations

\begin{cases}x-0=x\\
x-x=0\end{cases}

(1)

where
0
is the unique constant in the theory of
\mdmathbb{V}
. From such a term, one can construct a ternary term
p(x,y,z)
satisfying the equations

\begin{cases}p(x,x,x)=x\\
p(x,x,0)=0\\
p(0,x,x)=0\end{cases}

(2)

by setting

p(x,y,z)=x-(y-z).

Indeed, one can check that

\begin{cases}p(x,x,x)=x-(x-x)=x-0=x\\
p(x,x,0)=x-(x-0)=x-x=0\\
p(0,x,x)=0-(x-x)=0-0=0.\end{cases}

(3)

In addition, from a subtraction
x-y
, one can also construct a
4
-ary term
q(x,y,z,w)
satisfying the equations

\begin{cases}q(x,0,0,0)=x\\
q(x,x,y,y)=0\\
q(x,y,x,y)=0\end{cases}

(4)

by setting

q(x,y,z,w)=(x-y)-(z-w).

To see this, it suffices to compute

\begin{cases}q(x,0,0,0)=(x-0)-(0-0)=x-0=x\\
q(x,x,y,y)=(x-x)-(y-y)=0-0=0\\
q(x,y,x,y)=(x-y)-(x-y)=0.\end{cases}

(5)

Although these two proofs look very similar, let us now explain a fundamental difference between them which has deep consequences in Category Theory.

The first proof (the construction of the term
p(x,y,z)
from the subtraction
x-y
) can be extended to the context of partial algebras in the following sense, while the second cannot. Let us consider a pointed set
(A,0)
equipped with a
partial subtraction
, i.e., a partial function
-\colon A^{2}\mathrel{\dabar@\dabar@\mathchar 203}A
such that the existential equations

\begin{cases}x-0=^{\mathrm{e}}x\\
x-x=^{\mathrm{e}}0\end{cases}

hold, meaning that, for every
a\in A
, both
a-0
and
a-a
are defined and they are equal to
a-0=a
and
a-a=0
respectively. For such a
partial subtractive algebra

A
, one can construct the ternary partial operation
p\colon A^{3}\mathrel{\dabar@\dabar@\mathchar 203}A
by setting
p(x,y,z)=x-(y-z)
for
x,y,z\in A
for which
y-z
and
x-(y-z)
are defined (and
p(x,y,z)
is not defined for the other triples
(x,y,z)\in A^{3}
). This partial operation satisfies the existential equations

\begin{cases}p(x,x,x)=^{\mathrm{e}}x\\
p(x,x,0)=^{\mathrm{e}}0\\
p(0,x,x)=^{\mathrm{e}}0\end{cases}

which can be seen using a proof completely analogous to the one in (
3

For the second proof (i.e., the construction of
q(x,y,z,w)
from the subtraction
x-y
), this is no longer the case. One can still define the
4
-ary partial operation
q\colon A^{4}\mathrel{\dabar@\dabar@\mathchar 203}A
by setting

exactly for those
x,y,z,w\in A
for which
x-y
,
z-w
and
(x-y)-(z-w)
are defined, but now the existential equation

can no longer be shown to hold in
A
analogously as in (

is in general not defined for all
x,y\in A
.

Let us now explain the consequences that this difference implies in Category Theory. Let us first see how the algebraic condition that a pointed variety of universal algebras has a subtraction in its theory extends to the context of a finitely complete pointed category. The first thing one can think of is to require that any object
X
is naturally equipped with an internal subtraction
s\colon X^{2}\to X
satisfying the internal version of the axioms
s(x,0)=x
and
s(x,x)=0
. However, this is in general too strong of a condition; even the category of
subtractive algebras
(i.e., pointed sets equipped with a subtraction
-
) does not have such an internal operation since

is not a homomorphism in general. The ‘right’ approach has been proposed in
[
21
]
and generalized in
[
22
]
to all Mal’tsev conditions of a similar form to those appearing in (
1
), (
2
) and (
4

1
) line by line, to look at them ‘components by components’. In other words, if we write the equations (
1

\mathrm{Sub}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc|c}x&0&x\\
x&x&0\end{array}\right],

the columns of this matrix give a condition on the relations in a pointed variety
\mdmathbb{V}
. Indeed, writing elements of
A^{2}
as column vectors, the ‘matrix condition’ corresponding to
\mathrm{Sub}
requires that, for any homomorphic binary relation
R\subseteq A^{2}
in
\mdmathbb{V}
, we have

\left\{\left[\begin{array}[]{c}a\\
a\end{array}\right],\,\left[\begin{array}[]{c}0\\
a\end{array}\right]\right\}\subseteq R\quad\implies\quad\left[\begin{array}[]{c}a\\
0\end{array}\right]\in R

subtractive category

[
21
]
. Using a similar technique introduced in

In addition, still according to this theorem, this implication is also equivalent to the implication where the weak versions of these properties are involved, that is, the properties only requiring that the strong relations satisfy the matrix properties. Strong relations (or in general strong monomorphisms) are those which are orthogonal to epimorphisms. We denote this implication by

The proof of Theorem

The rest of the paper is divided in two sections. In the first one, we essentially recall the needed concepts and results from the literature. Among others, we remind the reader with the matrix properties and the algorithms from

Preliminaries

Matrix properties

Given integers
n>0
and
m,k\geqslant 0
, we denote as in
[
13
]
by
\mathrm{matr}_{\ast}(n,m,k)
the set of (extended) matrices

\mathrm{M}=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc|c}x_{11}&\dots&x_{1m}&x_{1\,m+1}\\
\vdots&&\vdots&\vdots\\
x_{n1}&\dots&x_{nm}&x_{n\,m+1}\end{array}\right]

with
n
rows,
m
left columns, one right column and whose entries lie in the set
\{\ast,x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\}
(i.e., the free pointed set on
k
variables
x_{1},\dots,x_{k}
). Each such matrix
\mathrm{M}\in\mathrm{matr}_{\ast}(n,m,k)
determines a property on
n
-ary (internal) relations
r\colon R\rightarrowtail X_{1}\times\cdots\times X_{n}
in finitely complete pointed categories
\mdmathbb{C}
. Such a relation is given by a subobject of the
n
-fold product
X_{1}\times\cdots\times X_{n}
and is represented by a monomorphism
r
. To describe the property determined by
\mathrm{M}
on such relations, we need the concept of row-wise interpretations. Given pointed sets
(S_{1},\ast_{1}),\dots,(S_{n},\ast_{n})
, a
row-wise interpretation
of
\mathrm{M}
of type
((S_{1},\ast_{1}),\dots,(S_{n},\ast_{n}))
is an extended matrix

\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc|c}f_{1}(x_{11})&\dots&f_{1}(x_{1m})&f_{1}(x_{1\,m+1})\\
\vdots&&\vdots&\vdots\\
f_{n}(x_{n1})&\dots&f_{n}(x_{nm})&f_{n}(x_{n\,m+1})\end{array}\right]

whose entries are obtained by applying to the elements of the
i^{\textrm{th}}
row of
\mathrm{M}
(for each
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
) a
pointed function

f_{i}\colon(\{\ast,x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\},\ast)\to(S_{i},\ast_{i})
, i.e., a function
f_{i}\colon\{\ast,x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\}\to S_{i}
such that
f_{i}(\ast)=\ast_{i}
. Viewing each hom-set
\mdmathbb{C}(X,Y)
of a finitely complete pointed category
\mdmathbb{C}
as a pointed set where the distinguished element is the zero morphism
0\colon X\to Y
, we say that an
n
-ary relation
r\colon R\rightarrowtail X_{1}\times\cdots\times X_{n}
in
\mdmathbb{C}
is
strictly
\mathrm{M}
-closed

[
22
]
if, for any object
Z
of
\mdmathbb{C}

\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc|c}g_{11}&\dots&g_{1m}&h_{1}\\
\vdots&&\vdots&\vdots\\
g_{n1}&\dots&g_{nm}&h_{n}\end{array}\right]

of
\mathrm{M}
of type
(\mdmathbb{C}(Z,X_{1}),\dots,\mdmathbb{C}(Z,X_{n}))
is
compatible
with
r
, i.e., if, for each
j\in\{1,\dots,m\}
, the morphism
(g_{1j},\dots,g_{nj})\colon Z\to X_{1}\times\cdots\times X_{n}
induced by the
j^{\textrm{th}}
left column factors through
r
, then the morphism
(h_{1},\dots,h_{n})\colon Z\to X_{1}\times\cdots\times X_{n}
induced by the right column also factors through
r
.

For a pointed set
(S,\ast)
, a (non-row-wise)
interpretation
of
\mathrm{M}
of type
(S,\ast)
is a row-wise interpretation of
\mathrm{M}
of type
((S,\ast),\dots,(S,\ast))
for which
f_{1}=\dots=f_{n}
. We say that a relation
r\colon R\rightarrowtail X^{n}
in
\mdmathbb{C}
is
\mathrm{M}
-closed

[
22
]
if, for any object
Z
of
\mdmathbb{C}
, each interpretation of
\mathrm{M}
of type
\mdmathbb{C}(Z,X)
is compatible with

.

We denote by
\mathrm{matr}(n,m,k)
the subset of
non-pointed matrices
of
\mathrm{matr}_{\ast}(n,m,k)
, i.e., those matrices not containing
\ast

, we define analogously as above the concepts of row-wise interpretations of
\mathrm{M}
of type
(S_{1},\dots,S_{n})

of type

\mathrm{M}
-closed relations

and of
\mathrm{M}
-closed relations

in a (non necessarily pointed) finitely complete category
\mdmathbb{C}
. We also denote by
\mathrm{matr}
and

the unions

\mathrm{matr}=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}n>0\\
m\geqslant 0\\
k\geqslant 0\end{subarray}}\mathrm{matr}(n,m,k)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathrm{matr}_{\ast}=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}n>0\\
m\geqslant 0\\

Given a class of monomorphisms
\mathcal{M}
stable under pullbacks in a finitely complete category

(respectively in a finitely complete pointed category

) and a matrix

-closed (since

-closed.

.

[

Let

Examples

We first focus on examples where
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{all}}
. For the matrix

\mathrm{Mal}=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc|c}x_{1}&x_{2}&x_{2}&x_{1}\\

is

The algorithm for deciding implications of matrix properties

As in
[
13
,
16
]
, we call a
matrix set
a subset of
\mathrm{matr}_{\ast}
. A matrix set
S
is said to be
non-pointed
if
S\subseteq\mathrm{matr}
. Given a matrix set
S\subseteq\mathrm{matr}
(respectively
S\subseteq\mathrm{matr}_{\ast}
), a finitely complete category
\mdmathbb{C}
(respectively a finitely complete pointed category
\mdmathbb{C}
) and a class
\mathcal{M}
of monomorphisms in
\mdmathbb{C}
stable under pullbacks, we say that
\mdmathbb{C}

has
S
-closed
\mathcal{M}
-relations
if
\mdmathbb{C}
has
\mathrm{M}
-closed
\mathcal{M}
-relations for all
\mathrm{M}
in
S
.

Extending the notation established in
[
14
]
, given a non-pointed matrix set
S\subseteq\mathrm{matr}
and a matrix
\mathrm{N}\in\mathrm{matr}
, we write
S\Rightarrow_{\mathrm{lex}}^{\mathrm{all}}\mathrm{N}
, or simply
S\Rightarrow_{\mathrm{lex}}\mathrm{N}
, if any finitely complete category with
S
-closed relations has also
\mathrm{N}
-closed relations. The abbreviation ‘
\mathrm{lex}
’ here stands for ‘left exact’, another name for ‘finitely complete’. Similarly, we write
S\Rightarrow_{\mathrm{lex}}^{\mathrm{strong}}\mathrm{N}
to mean that any finitely complete category with
S
-closed
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{strong}}
-relations has also
\mathrm{N}
-closed
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{strong}}
-relations. In the pointed case, i.e., given
S\subseteq\mathrm{matr}_{\ast}
and
\mathrm{N}\in\mathrm{matr}_{\ast}
, we write
S\Rightarrow_{\mathrm{lex}_{\ast}}^{\mathrm{all}}\mathrm{N}
, or simply
S\Rightarrow_{\mathrm{lex}_{\ast}}\mathrm{N}
, to mean that any finitely complete pointed category with
S
-closed relations has also
\mathrm{N}
-closed relations. Finally, we write
S\Rightarrow_{\mathrm{lex}_{\ast}}^{\mathrm{strong}}\mathrm{N}
if any finitely complete pointed category with
S
-closed
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{strong}}
-relations has also
\mathrm{N}
-closed
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{strong}}
-relations. In all these abbreviations, if
S=\{\mathrm{M}\}
is a singleton, we sometimes write
\mathrm{M}
instead of
\{\mathrm{M}\}
, e.g.,
\mathrm{M}\Rightarrow_{\mathrm{lex}}\mathrm{N}
instead of
\{\mathrm{M}\}\Rightarrow_{\mathrm{lex}}\mathrm{N}
.

In
[
16
]
, an algorithm was presented to decide whether
S\Rightarrow_{\mathrm{lex}}\mathrm{N}
for a non-pointed matrix set
S\subseteq\mathrm{matr}
and a matrix
\mathrm{N}\in\mathrm{matr}
. In
[
13
]
, this algorithm was adapted to the pointed context to decide whether
S\Rightarrow_{\mathrm{lex}_{\ast}}\mathrm{N}
for a matrix set
S\subseteq\mathrm{matr}_{\ast}
and a matrix
\mathrm{N}\in\mathrm{matr}_{\ast}
. Note that, in the original papers
[
13
,
16
]
, it was required that
S
is finite to be able to implement these algorithms on a computer. Since this will not be our concern in the present paper, we can omit this assumption here. Actually, from the results of
[
13
,
16
]
, we can deduce the following (which can also be deduced from the algorithms recalled below since the number of columns that one can possibly add to
\mathrm{N}
is finite).

Proposition 1.3
.

Given
S\subseteq\mathrm{matr}
and
\mathrm{N}\in\mathrm{matr}
, one has
S\Rightarrow_{\mathrm{lex}}\mathrm{N}
if and only if there is a finite subset
S^{\prime}\subseteq S
such that
S^{\prime}\Rightarrow_{\mathrm{lex}}\mathrm{N}
. Analogously, given
S\subseteq\mathrm{matr}_{\ast}
and
\mathrm{N}\in\mathrm{matr}_{\ast}
, one has
S\Rightarrow_{\mathrm{lex}_{\ast}}\mathrm{N}
if and only if there is a finite subset
S^{\prime}\subseteq S
such that
S^{\prime}\Rightarrow_{\mathrm{lex}_{\ast}}\mathrm{N}
.

As they will be needed, let us now recall these algorithms. In each case, the first step of the algorithm is to deal with trivial matrices. A matrix
\mathrm{M}\in\mathrm{matr}
is said to be
trivial

[
16
]
if every finitely complete category with
\mathrm{M}
-closed (
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{all}}
-)relations is a
preorder
, i.e., for any two objects
X,Y
in the category, there is at most one morphism
X\to Y
. Similarly, a matrix
\mathrm{M}\in\mathrm{matr}_{\ast}
is said to be
trivial

[
13
]
if every finitely complete pointed category with
\mathrm{M}
-closed (
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{all}}
-)relations is a preorder (which in that case will contain only one isomorphism class of objects). Since
\mathrm{matr}\subset\mathrm{matr}_{\ast}
, it is sensible to ask whether these two definitions agree if
\mathrm{M}\in\mathrm{matr}
. As proved in
[
13
]
, they indeed do. In
[
13
,
16
]
, it is shown how to efficiently recognize whether a matrix is trivial. As this will not be needed here, we do not enter into the details and refer the interested reader to those papers. However, some theoretical characterizations from these papers are recalled in Theorem
2.1
where new characterizations are also proved. Given a matrix set
S\subseteq\mathrm{matr}_{\ast}
, we say that
S
is
trivial
if it contains a trivial matrix.

Now, given a non-pointed non-trivial matrix set
S\subseteq\mathrm{matr}
and a matrix
\mathrm{N}\in\mathrm{matr}(n,m,k)
for integers
n>0
and
m,k\geqslant 0
, the algorithm from
[
16
]
to decide whether
S\Rightarrow_{\mathrm{lex}}\mathrm{N}
says the following:

Keep expanding the set of left columns of
\mathrm{N}
, until it is not possible anymore, with the right column of a row-wise interpretation
B
of type
(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\},\dots,\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\})
of a matrix
\mathrm{M}^{\prime}\in\mathrm{matr}(n,m^{\prime},k^{\prime})
whose rows are rows of a common matrix
\mathrm{M}\in S
such that the left columns of
B
, but not its right column, can be found among the left columns of the expending
\mathrm{N}
. One has the implication
S\Rightarrow_{\mathrm{lex}}\mathrm{N}
if and only if the right column of
\mathrm{N}
can be found among the left columns of this expanded
\mathrm{N}
.

In the pointed case, given a non-trivial matrix set
S\subseteq\mathrm{matr}_{\ast}
and a matrix
\mathrm{N}\in\mathrm{matr}_{\ast}(n,m,k)
for integers
n>0
and
m,k\geqslant 0
, the algorithm from
[
13
]
to decide whether
S\Rightarrow_{\mathrm{lex}_{\ast}}\mathrm{N}
says the following (where the pointed set

is again considered with
\ast
as distinguished element):

First, add to
\mathrm{N}
a left column of
\ast
’s. Then, keep expanding the set of left columns of
\mathrm{N}
, until it is no more possible, with the right column of a row-wise interpretation
B
of type
(\{\ast,x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\},\dots,\{\ast,x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\})
of a matrix
\mathrm{M}^{\prime}\in\mathrm{matr}_{\ast}(n,m^{\prime},k^{\prime})
whose rows are rows of a common matrix
\mathrm{M}\in S
such that the left columns of
B
, but not its right column, can be found among the left columns of the expanding
\mathrm{N}
. One has the implication
S\Rightarrow_{\mathrm{lex}_{\ast}}\mathrm{N}
if and only if the right column of
\mathrm{N}
can be found among the left columns of this expanded
\mathrm{N}
.

Categories of partial algebras

In Universal Algebra, a
(finitary one-sorted) signature

\Sigma
is a set of operation symbols
\sigma
, each of them being equipped with an integer
n\geqslant 0
called its arity. Given such a signature
\Sigma
and a set of variables
X
, we define the set
\mathrm{T}_{\Sigma}(X)
of
\Sigma
-terms in the variables from
X
inductively as follows:

•

the elements of
X
are terms (i.e.,
X\subseteq\mathrm{T}_{\Sigma}(X)
);

•

given an
n
-ary operation symbol
\sigma\in\Sigma
and
n
terms
t_{1},\dots,t_{n}\in\mathrm{T}_{\Sigma}(X)
, then
\sigma(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})
is also a term in
\mathrm{T}_{\Sigma}(X)
.

Every element of
\mathrm{T}_{\Sigma}(X)
is constructed using only those two rules. A
\Sigma
-equation in the variables from
X
is then a pair
(t,t^{\prime})
of terms in
\mathrm{T}_{\Sigma}(X)
. A
\Sigma
-equation
is then nothing else than a
\Sigma
-equation in the variables from some set
X
.

For a signature
\Sigma
, a
\Sigma
-partial algebra

A
is a set (also denoted
A
by abuse of notation) equipped with, for each
n
-ary operation symbol
\sigma\in\Sigma
, a
partial
function
\sigma^{A}\colon A^{n}\mathrel{\dabar@\dabar@\mathchar 203}A
, i.e., a (total) function from a subset of
A^{n}
to
A
. In a
\Sigma
-partial algebra
A
, each
\Sigma
-term
t
in the variable from a set
X
gives rise to a partial function
t^{A}\colon A^{X}\mathrel{\dabar@\dabar@\mathchar 203}A
defined inductively as follows:

•

if
t\in X
is a variable, then
t^{A}
is a total function given by the corresponding projection
A^{X}\to A\colon(a_{x})_{x\in X}\mapsto a_{t}
;

•

if
t=\sigma(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})
for some
n
-ary
\sigma\in\Sigma
and
\Sigma
-terms
t_{1},\dots,t_{n}\in\mathrm{T}_{\Sigma}(X)
, then
t^{A}
is defined on the element
(a_{x})_{x\in X}\in A^{X}
if and only if
t^{A}_{1},\dots,t^{A}_{n}
are all defined on
(a_{x})_{x\in X}
and
\sigma^{A}
is defined on
(t_{1}^{A}((a_{x})_{x\in X}),\dots,t^{A}_{n}((a_{x})_{x\in X}))
, in which case

t^{A}((a_{x})_{x\in X})=\sigma^{A}(t_{1}^{A}((a_{x})_{x\in X}),\dots,t^{A}_{n}((a_{x})_{x\in X})).

Following the terminology from
[
7
]
, given a
\Sigma
-equation
(t,t^{\prime})
in the variables from
X
, we say that
A
satisfies the
existence equation

t=^{\mathrm{e}}t^{\prime}
(or simply that the equation
t=^{\mathrm{e}}t^{\prime}
holds in
A
) if
t^{A}((a_{x})_{x\in X})
and
t^{\prime A}((a_{x})_{x\in X})
are both defined for any element
(a_{x})_{x\in X}\in A^{X}
and they are equal:

t^{A}((a_{x})_{x\in X})=t^{\prime A}((a_{x})_{x\in X}).

Although there are other kind of equations that can be defined for partial algebras, in this paper we will only consider existence equations.

Given a pair
(\Sigma,E)
where
\Sigma
is a signature and
E
a set of existence
\Sigma
-equations, a
(\Sigma,E)
-partial algebra
is
\Sigma
-partial algebra which satisfies all equations of
E
. A homomorphism
f\colon A\to B
of
(\Sigma,E)
-partial algebras is a (total) function
f\colon A\to B
such that, for each
n
-ary operation symbol
\sigma\in\Sigma
and every
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in A
, if
\sigma^{A}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
is defined in
A
, then
\sigma^{B}(f(a_{1}),\dots,f(a_{n}))
is defined in
B
and the equality

f(\sigma^{A}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n}))=\sigma^{B}(f(a_{1}),\dots,f(a_{n}))

(6)

holds. These form the category
\mathsf{Part}(\Sigma,E)
of
(\Sigma,E)
-partial algebras. All small limits in such a category of partial algebras exist and are computed as in the category
\mathsf{Set}
of sets and functions. More specifically, given a small category
\mdmathbb{J}
and a diagram
D\colon\mdmathbb{J}\to\mathsf{Part}(\Sigma,E)
, the limit of
D
is constructed as the set

L=\{(a_{J}\in D(J))_{J\in\mdmathbb{J}}\,|\,D(j)(a_{J})=a_{J^{\prime}}\,\forall j\colon J\to J^{\prime}\in\mdmathbb{J}\}

and, given any
n
-ary
\sigma\in\Sigma
,

\sigma^{L}((a_{1J})_{J\in\mdmathbb{J}},\dots,(a_{nJ})_{J\in\mdmathbb{J}})

is defined if and only if
\sigma^{D(J)}(a_{1J},\dots,a_{nJ})
is defined for all object
J\in\mdmathbb{J}
, and is equal in that case to

\sigma^{L}((a_{1J})_{J\in\mdmathbb{J}},\dots,(a_{nJ})_{J\in\mdmathbb{J}})=(\sigma^{D(J)}(a_{1J},\dots,a_{nJ}))_{J\in\mdmathbb{J}}.

The legs of the limit are just the projections. Let us also mention that these categories of partial algebras are
essentially algebraic

[
1
,
2
]
, i.e.,
locally presentable

[
11
]

and a matrix
\mathrm{N}\in\mathrm{matr}
(respectively a matrix set

and a matrix
\mathrm{N}\in\mathrm{matr}_{\ast}
), we write
S\Rightarrow_{\mathrm{ess\,\,alg}}^{\mathrm{strong}}\mathrm{N}
(respectively

-relations has also

-closed
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{strong}}
-relations.

Inspired by Theorem

(respectively

(respectively, the signature

,

Given a signature

and a set

of a

-partial algebra

-partial algebra

\mathrm{Ari}\Rightarrow_{\mathrm{lex}}\mathrm{Maj}
it is actually enough to check that, from the equations (
7
), one has the equations

q(x_{1},x_{1},x_{2})=^{\mathrm{e}}p(x_{1},p(x_{2},x_{1},x_{1}),x_{2})=^{\mathrm{e}}p(x_{1},x_{2},x_{2})=^{\mathrm{e}}x_{1},

q(x_{1},x_{2},x_{1})=^{\mathrm{e}}p(x_{2},p(x_{1},x_{1},x_{2}),x_{1})=^{\mathrm{e}}p(x_{2},x_{2},x_{1})=^{\mathrm{e}}x_{1}

and

q(x_{2},x_{1},x_{1})=^{\mathrm{e}}p(x_{1},p(x_{1},x_{2},x_{1}),x_{1})=^{\mathrm{e}}p(x_{1},x_{1},x_{1})=^{\mathrm{e}}x_{1}

corresponding to the matrix
\mathrm{Maj}
.

Since both rows of
\mathrm{Mal}
appear in
\mathrm{Ari}
, it is straightforward to prove
\mathrm{Ari}\Rightarrow_{\mathrm{lex}}\mathrm{Mal}
using the above term proofs. As shown in
[
16
]
, one also has
\{\mathrm{Maj},\mathrm{Mal}\}\Rightarrow_{\mathrm{lex}}\mathrm{Ari}
. In the language of terms, this can be done by considering

q(x,y,z)=p^{\mathrm{Mal}}(x,p^{\mathrm{Maj}}(z,x,y),z)

where
p^{\mathrm{Mal}}
and
p^{\mathrm{Maj}}
are terms satisfying the existence equations from
\mathrm{Mal}
and
\mathrm{Maj}
respectively. This term
q
indeed satisfies the existence equations from
\mathrm{Ari}
since

q(x,y,y)=^{\mathrm{e}}p^{\mathrm{Mal}}(x,p^{\mathrm{Maj}}(y,x,y),y)=^{\mathrm{e}}p^{\mathrm{Mal}}(x,y,y)=^{\mathrm{e}}x

q(y,y,x)=^{\mathrm{e}}p^{\mathrm{Mal}}(y,p^{\mathrm{Maj}}(x,y,y),x)=^{\mathrm{e}}p^{\mathrm{Mal}}(y,y,x)=^{\mathrm{e}}x

q(x,y,x)=^{\mathrm{e}}p^{\mathrm{Mal}}(x,p^{\mathrm{Maj}}(x,x,y),x)=^{\mathrm{e}}p^{\mathrm{Mal}}(x,x,x)=^{\mathrm{e}}x.

We have thus re-proved that an arithmetical category is the same thing as a Mal’tsev majority category. By the equivalence
(8)
\Leftrightarrow
(12)
of Theorem
2.3
, this also shows that a finitely complete category is
weakly arithmetical
(i.e., has
\mathrm{Ari}
-closed
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{strong}}
-relations) if and only if it is
weakly Mal’tsev
(i.e., has
\mathrm{Mal}
-closed
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{strong}}
-relations) and
weakly majority
(i.e., has
\mathrm{Maj}
-closed
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{strong}}
-relations).

To give an example in the pointed case, let us mention that in
[
13
]
we have shown that the matrix
\mathrm{StrUni}
is equivalent to the matrix

\mathrm{StrUni}^{\prime}=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc|c}x_{1}&x_{1}&\ast&x_{1}\\
\ast&\ast&x_{1}&x_{1}\\
x_{1}&\ast&x_{1}&\ast\end{array}\right]

in the sense that
\mathrm{StrUni}\Rightarrow_{\mathrm{lex}_{\ast}}\mathrm{StrUni}^{\prime}
and
\mathrm{StrUni}^{\prime}\Rightarrow_{\mathrm{lex}_{\ast}}\mathrm{StrUni}
. Let us re-prove it here using (partial) terms. The term
p(x,y,z)
corresponding to
\mathrm{StrUni}
is associated to the equations

\begin{cases}p(x,0,0)=^{\mathrm{e}}x\\
p(y,y,x)=^{\mathrm{e}}x\end{cases}

(8)

while the term
q(x,y,z)
corresponding to
\mathrm{StrUni}^{\prime}
is associated to the equations

\begin{cases}q(x,x,0)=^{\mathrm{e}}x\\
q(0,0,x)=^{\mathrm{e}}x\\
q(x,0,x)=^{\mathrm{e}}0.\end{cases}

(9)

From a term
p(x,y,z)
satisfying the equations (
8
), we can construct the term

q(x,y,z)=p(z,p(x,y,0),y)

satisfying the equations (
9
). Indeed,

\begin{cases}q(x,x,0)=^{\mathrm{e}}p(0,p(x,x,0),x)=^{\mathrm{e}}p(0,0,x)=^{\mathrm{e}}x\\
q(0,0,x)=^{\mathrm{e}}p(x,p(0,0,0),0)=^{\mathrm{e}}p(x,0,0)=^{\mathrm{e}}x\\
q(x,0,x)=^{\mathrm{e}}p(x,p(x,0,0),0)=^{\mathrm{e}}p(x,x,0)=^{\mathrm{e}}0\end{cases}

already proving
\mathrm{StrUni}\Rightarrow_{\mathrm{lex}_{\ast}}\mathrm{StrUni}^{\prime}
. For the other implication, from a term
q(x,y,z)
satisfying the equations (
9
), we can construct the term

p(x,y,z)=q(q(y,0,x),q(y,0,x),z)

satisfying the equations (
8
). Indeed,

\begin{cases}p(x,0,0)=^{\mathrm{e}}q(q(0,0,x),q(0,0,x),0)=^{\mathrm{e}}q(x,x,0)=^{\mathrm{e}}x\\
p(y,y,x)=^{\mathrm{e}}q(q(y,0,y),q(y,0,y),x)=^{\mathrm{e}}q(0,0,x)=^{\mathrm{e}}x\end{cases}

proving
\mathrm{StrUni}^{\prime}\Rightarrow_{\mathrm{lex}_{\ast}}\mathrm{StrUni}
.

Remark 2.6
.

Let us make clear that in the above proofs,
partial
terms are considered. It was shown in
[
16
]
that matrix implications are context sensitive. In particular, given matrices
\mathrm{M},\mathrm{N}\in\mathrm{matr}
, if one proves that any variety with
\mathrm{M}
-closed relations has
\mathrm{N}
-closed relations, this does
not
imply that
\mathrm{M}\Rightarrow_{\mathrm{lex}}\mathrm{N}
in general. An example of this phenomenon has already been shown in the Introduction. Let us show here an example in the non-pointed context. We consider the matrices

\mathrm{Cube}^{\Delta^{*}}_{3}=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccccc|c}x_{1}&x_{1}&x_{1}&x_{2}&x_{2}&x_{1}\\
x_{1}&x_{2}&x_{2}&x_{1}&x_{1}&x_{1}\\
x_{2}&x_{1}&x_{2}&x_{1}&x_{2}&x_{1}\end{array}\right]

and

\mathrm{Edge}_{3}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc|c}x_{2}&x_{2}&x_{1}&x_{1}&x_{1}\\
x_{2}&x_{1}&x_{2}&x_{1}&x_{1}\\
x_{1}&x_{1}&x_{1}&x_{2}&x_{1}\end{array}\right].

It has been shown in
[
16
]
(using a computer implementation of the algorithm, see Figure 2 in
[
16
]
) that
\mathrm{Edge}_{3}\Rightarrow_{\mathrm{lex}}\mathrm{Cube}^{\Delta^{*}}_{3}
but
\mathrm{Cube}^{\Delta^{*}}_{3}\nRightarrow_{\mathrm{lex}}\mathrm{Edge}_{3}
(i.e., the implication
\mathrm{Cube}^{\Delta^{*}}_{3}\Rightarrow_{\mathrm{lex}}\mathrm{Edge}_{3}
does
not
hold). However, it was shown in
[
4
]
that varieties with
\mathrm{Cube}^{\Delta^{*}}_{3}
-closed relations also have
\mathrm{Edge}_{3}
-closed relations. According to Theorem
1.2
, a variety
\mdmathbb{V}
has
\mathrm{Cube}^{\Delta^{*}}_{3}
-closed relations if and only if its theory has a
5
-ary term
p
satisfying the equations

\begin{cases}p(x_{1},x_{1},x_{1},x_{2},x_{2})=x_{1}\\
p(x_{1},x_{2},x_{2},x_{1},x_{1})=x_{1}\\
p(x_{2},x_{1},x_{2},x_{1},x_{2})=x_{1}.\end{cases}

Such a term has been called a
\Delta^{*}
-special cube term
in
[
4
]
, explaining the notation
\mathrm{Cube}^{\Delta^{*}}_{3}
. Analogously, a variety
\mdmathbb{V}
has
\mathrm{Edge}_{3}
-closed relations if and only if its theory has a
4
-ary term
q
satisfying the equations

\begin{cases}q(x_{2},x_{2},x_{1},x_{1})=x_{1}\\
q(x_{2},x_{1},x_{2},x_{1})=x_{1}\\
q(x_{1},x_{1},x_{1},x_{2})=x_{1}.\end{cases}

Such a term has been called a
3
-edge term
in
[
4
]
, justifying the notation
\mathrm{Edge}_{3}
. Although
\mathrm{Cube}^{\Delta^{*}}_{3}\nRightarrow_{\mathrm{lex}}\mathrm{Edge}_{3}
, it was shown in
[
4
]
that one can construct a
3
-edge term
q
from a
\Delta^{*}
-special cube term
p
as follows:

q(x,y,z,w)=p(p(y,z,z,w,w),z,p(x,z,z,z,z),w,p(x,w,z,w,z)).

One can indeed compute

\displaystyle q(x_{2},x_{2},x_{1},x_{1})

\displaystyle=p(p(x_{2},x_{1},x_{1},x_{1},x_{1}),x_{1},p(x_{2},x_{1},x_{1},x_{1},x_{1}),x_{1},p(x_{2},x_{1},x_{1},x_{1},x_{1}))

\displaystyle=x_{1},

\displaystyle q(x_{2},x_{1},x_{2},x_{1})

\displaystyle=p(p(x_{1},x_{2},x_{2},x_{1},x_{1}),x_{2},p(x_{2},x_{2},x_{2},x_{2},x_{2}),x_{1},p(x_{2},x_{1},x_{2},x_{1},x_{2}))

\displaystyle=p(x_{1},x_{2},x_{2},x_{1},x_{1})

\displaystyle=x_{1}

and

\displaystyle q(x_{1},x_{1},x_{1},x_{2})

\displaystyle=p(p(x_{1},x_{1},x_{1},x_{2},x_{2}),x_{1},p(x_{1},x_{1},x_{1},x_{1},x_{1}),x_{2},p(x_{1},x_{2},x_{1},x_{2},x_{1}))

\displaystyle=p(x_{1},x_{1},x_{1},x_{2},x_{2})

\displaystyle=x_{1}.

However, this proof does
not
extend to the partial context since in that case
p(x_{2},x_{1},x_{1},x_{1},x_{1})
in the first equation, and so
q(x_{2},x_{2},x_{1},x_{1})
, are not defined in general.

in Theorem

Corollary 2.7
.

For a matrix set
S\subseteq\mathrm{matr}_{\ast}
, the following conditions are equivalent:

(1)

S
is anti-trivial, i.e., all finitely complete pointed categories have
S
-closed relations;

(2)

all matrices
\mathrm{M}
in
S
are anti-trivial in the sense of
[
13
]
, i.e., all finitely complete pointed categories have
\mathrm{M}
-closed relations;

(3)

all finitely complete pointed categories have
S
-closed
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{strong}}
-relations;

(4)

all essentially algebraic pointed categories have
S
-closed
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{strong}}
-relations;

(5)

for every finitely complete pointed category
\mdmathbb{C}
and every class
\mathcal{M}
of monomorphisms in
\mdmathbb{C}
stable under pullbacks,
\mdmathbb{C}
has
S
-closed
\mathcal{M}
-relations;

(6)

\mathsf{Set}_{\ast}
has
S
-closed relations;

(7)

for all matrices
\mathrm{M}\in S
, all the entries of the right column of
\mathrm{M}
are
\ast
’s or this right column of
\mathrm{M}
can be found among its left columns.

Moreover, if
S\subseteq\mathrm{matr}
, these are further equivalent to the following conditions:

(8)

all finitely complete categories have
S
-closed relations;

(9)

all matrices
\mathrm{M}
in
S
are anti-trivial in the sense of
[
16
]
, i.e., all finitely complete categories have
\mathrm{M}
-closed relations;

(10)

all finitely complete categories have
S
-closed
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{strong}}
-relations;

(11)

all essentially algebraic categories have
S
-closed
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{strong}}
-relations;

(12)

for every finitely complete category
\mdmathbb{C}
and every class
\mathcal{M}
of monomorphisms in
\mdmathbb{C}
stable under pullbacks,
\mdmathbb{C}
has
S
-closed
\mathcal{M}
-relations;

(13)

\mathsf{Set}
has
S
-closed relations;

(14)

for all matrices
\mathrm{M}\in S
, the right column of
\mathrm{M}
can be found among its left columns.

Proof.

Let us first note that the equivalences
(2)
\Leftrightarrow
(6)
\Leftrightarrow
(7)
have been proved in
[
13
]
. Moreover, if
S\subseteq\mathrm{matr}
,
(2)
\Leftrightarrow
(9)
has been shown also in
[
13
]
while
(9)
\Leftrightarrow
(13)
\Leftrightarrow
(14)
were proved in
[
16
]
. In addition, we remark that
(1)
\Leftrightarrow
(2)
and
(8)
\Leftrightarrow
(9)
if
S\subseteq\mathrm{matr}
are obvious. In view of that, we can suppose without loss of generality that
S=\{\mathrm{N}\}
for some
\mathrm{N}\in\mathrm{matr}_{\ast}
.

Now, note that if
\mathrm{N}\in\mathrm{matr}
and
\mathrm{N}
has no left columns, all statements in the theorem are obviously false (and thus equivalent), so that we can exclude this case without loss of generality. Now it suffices to apply Theorem
2.3
with
S^{\prime}=\{[x_{1}|x_{1}]\}
; it is straightforward to show that each of its equivalent conditions becomes (equivalent to) the statements listed above. For statements
(6)
(respectively
(13)
), it is easy to see that
\mathsf{Part}_{\ast}^{S^{\prime}}
(respectively
\mathsf{Part}^{S^{\prime}}
) for that
S^{\prime}
is equivalent to
\mathsf{Set}_{\ast}
(respectively to
\mathsf{Set}
