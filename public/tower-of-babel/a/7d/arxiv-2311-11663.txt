On Hardness Assumptions Needed for “Extreme High-End” PRGs and Fast Derandomization
Ronen Shaltiel, Emanuele Viola

Abstract
The hardness vs. randomness paradigm aims to explicitly construct pseudorandom generators G : { 0 , 1 } r → { 0 , 1 } m G:\{0,1\}^{r}\to\{0,1\}^{m} that fool circuits of size m m , assuming the existence of explicit hard functions. A “high-end PRG” with seed length r = O ⁡ ( log ⁡ m ) r=O(\log m) (implying BPP=P) was achieved in a seminal work of Impagliazzo and Wigderson (STOC 1997), assuming the high-end hardness assumption : there exist constants 0 < β < 1 < B 0<\beta<1<B , and functions computable in time 2 B ⋅ n 2^{B\cdot n} that cannot be computed by circuits of size 2 β ⋅ n 2^{\beta\cdot n} . Recently, motivated by fast derandomization of randomized algorithms, Doron et al. (FOCS 2020) and Chen and Tell (STOC 2021), construct “extreme high-end PRGs” with seed length r = ( 1 + o ⁡ ( 1 ) ) ⋅ log ⁡ m r=(1+o(1))\cdot\log m , under qualitatively stronger assumptions. We study whether extreme high-end PRGs can be constructed from the following scaled version of the assumption which we call the extreme high-end hardness assumption , and in which β = 1 − o ⁡ ( 1 ) \beta=1-o(1) and B = 1 + o ⁡ ( 1 ) B=1+o(1) . We give a partial negative answer: • Doron et al. compose a PEG (pseudo-entropy generator) with an extractor. The PEG is based on a hardness assumption for MA-type circuits. We show that black-box PEG constructions from the extreme high-end hardness assumption must have large seed length (and so cannot be used to obtain extreme high-end PRGs by applying an extractor). To prove this, we establish a new property of (general) black-box PRG constructions from hard functions: it is possible to fix many output bits of the construction while fixing few bits of the hard function. This property distinguishes PRG constructions from typical extractor constructions, and this may explain why it is difficult to design PRG constructions. • Chen and Tell compose two PRGs: G 1 : { 0 , 1 } ( 1 + o ⁡ ( 1 ) ) ⋅ log ⁡ m → { 0 , 1 } r 2 = m Ω ⁡ ( 1 ) G_{1}:\{0,1\}^{(1+o(1))\cdot\log m}\to\{0,1\}^{r_{2}=m^{\Omega(1)}} and G 2 : { 0 , 1 } r 2 → { 0 , 1 } m G_{2}:\{0,1\}^{r_{2}}\to\{0,1\}^{m} . The first PRG is based on the extreme high-end hardness assumption , and the second PRG needs to run in time m 1 + o ⁡ ( 1 ) m^{1+o(1)} , and is based on one way functions. We show that in black-box proofs of hardness amplification to 1 2 + 1 / m \frac{1}{2}+1/m , reductions must make Ω ⁡ ( m ) \Omega(m) queries, even in the extreme high-end. Known PRG constructions from hard functions are black-box and use (or imply) hardness amplification, and so cannot be used to construct a PRG G 2 G_{2} from the extreme high-end hardness assumption . The new feature of our hardness amplification result is that it applies even to the extreme high-end setting of parameters, whereas past work does not. Our techniques also improve recent lower bounds of Ron-Zewi, Shaltiel and Varma (ITCS 2021) on the number of queries of local list-decoding algorithms.

Explicit pseudorandom generators have many applications in computer science. The signature application of PRGs is to derandomize randomized algorithms (by running the algorithm using all outputs of the PRG). This is quantitatively specified in the proposition below.

High-end PRGs that imply BPP=P.

Hardness implied by PRGs.

PRGs immediately imply circuit lower bounds that are beyond our current ability.
Consequently, constructing explicit PRGs, requires circuit lower bounds (namely the existence of explicit functions that cannot be computed by small circuits). In particular, high-end PRGs imply the existence of functions
f:\{0,1\}^{\ell}\to\{0,1\}
which cannot be computed by circuits of size
2^{\Omega(\ell)}
, and
\operatorname{Time}(f)=2^{O(\ell)}
.
3
3

3

More specifically, Impagliazzo, Shaltiel and Wigderson
[

\ell=r+1

(defined by checking whether an input of length

m
, and
\operatorname{Time}_{\operatorname{all}}(f)\approx\operatorname{Time}_{\operatorname{all}}(G)

\operatorname{Time}_{\operatorname{all}}(f)=2^{O(\ell)}

\operatorname{Time}(f)=2^{O(\ell)}

1.1.1
The Impagliazzo-Wigderson (high-end) hardness assumption

The goal of the hardness vs. randomness program is to construct PRGs based on lower bounds that are as strong (or almost as strong) as the ones implied by the PRG. A major milestone in this program was achieved by Impagliazzo and Wigderson
[
IW97
]
.

Theorem 1.4

(
[
IW97
]
)
.

A high-end PRG follows from the following assumption:

The high-end hardness assumption
: There exist constants
0<\beta<1<B
, and a function
f:\{0,1\}^{\ell}\to\{0,1\}
that satisfies:

Hardness:
f
cannot be computed by circuits of size
2^{\beta\cdot\ell}
.

Explicitness:
\operatorname{Time}(f)\leq 2^{B\cdot\ell}
.

Theorem
1.4
converts hardness into pseudorandomness at close to the “correct rate” (as in the converse direction) if one does not care about the precise values of the constants
\beta,B
, and the constant hidden in the
O(\cdot)
notation in the seed length of the high-end PRG.

1.1.2
Scaling the Impagliazzo-Wigderson assumption to the extreme high-end

In the case of extreme high-end PRGs, we insist on seed length
r\approx 1\cdot\log m
and the constants
\beta,B
from the
The high-end hardness assumption
do matter. Assuming that we don’t expect to “improve” the hardness of the assumed explicit hard function, we must have
\beta\geq 1-o(1)
and
B\leq 1+o(1)
. Thus, imitating the approach of Impagliazzo and Wigderson
[
IW97
]

Open Problem 1.5
.

: There exists a function
f:\{0,1\}^{\ell}\to\{0,1\}
that satisfies:

Hardness:
f
cannot be computed by circuits of size
2^{(1-o(1))\cdot\ell}
.

Explicitness:
\operatorname{Time}(f)\leq 2^{(1+o(1))\cdot\ell}
.

4

4

In the case of the extreme high-end, it does make sense to distinguish between

The proof techniques of Impagliazzo and Wigderson

Recent work on extreme high-end PRGs.

Recently, Doron et al.
[

Goals of this paper.

In this paper, we investigate the problem of constructing explicit PRGs from explicit hard functions, focusing on open problem
1.5
. More specifically, we investigate the power of “black-box proofs” that convert explicit hard functions into PRGs and related objects. We show limitations on certain recent approaches to solve Open Problem
1.5
, and hope that this may help to point us in the direction of better constructions. A secondary goal of this paper is to survey recent work and point out the relationship between parameters, and potential barriers for improvement.

1.2.1

A black-box proof that converts hard functions into PRGs consists of two parts:

•

A

f:\{0,1\}^{\ell}\to\{0,1\}

\mathsf{Con}(f)

•

A

\mathsf{Red}^{(\cdot)}

\mathsf{Con}_{f}

C

1.2.2
Parameters of black-box proofs

To the best of our knowledge all hardness vs. randomness proofs of PRG constructions are black-box (or rely on components which are black-box).
In a black-box proof, the advice length
a
and the number of queries
q
determine the “hardness loss” in the tradeoff. More specifically:

Proposition 1.8

(Number of queries determines hardness loss in black-box proofs)
.

Let
(\mathsf{Con},\mathsf{Red})
be a black-box
\mathsf{hard}\mbox{-}\mathsf{function}\Rightarrow\mathsf{PRG}
(resp.
\mathsf{hard}\mbox{-}\mathsf{function}\Rightarrow{\rho^{\prime}}\mbox{-}\mathsf{hard}\mbox{-}\mathsf{function}
) proof in which
\mathsf{Red}
makes
q
queries, and has advice length
a
. If we start from a function
f:\{0,1\}^{\ell}\to\{0,1\}
that cannot be computed by circuits of size
s
, and apply the black-box proof, one can at best obtain that
\mathsf{Con}_{f}
is a
\mathsf{PRG}
(resp.
\rho^{\prime}
-
\mathsf{hard}\mbox{-}\mathsf{function}
) for circuits of size
m\leq\frac{s-a}{q}\leq\frac{s}{q}
.

Loosely speaking, Proposition
1.8
follows because, when measuring the size
s
of the circuit
C=\mathsf{Red}^{D}(\cdot,\alpha)
that is implied by the reduction for a circuit
D
of size
m
, then this circuit is of size
q\cdot m+a
. This gives that
s\geq q\cdot m+a
, implying the proposition.

Every function
f:\{0,1\}^{\ell}\to\{0,1\}
has circuits of size
2^{\ell}
. This means that in order for reductions to be useful in transforming hard-functions into PRGs (or average case hard functions) they must make
q\leq\frac{s-a}{m}\leq\frac{2^{\ell}-a}{m}
queries, and are useless for this purpose, if
q\geq 2^{\ell}
. Moreover, in the extreme high-end,
m=2^{(1-o(1))\cdot\ell}
and so it is critical that
q\leq\frac{s}{m}\leq\frac{2^{\ell}}{m}=2^{o(\ell)}=m^{o(1)}\ll m
.

Our notion of black-box does not guarantee explicitness.

We place no limitation on the map
\mathsf{Con}
, and so, the notion of black-box that we use,
does not
enforce that if

can be computed efficiently, then
\mathsf{Con}_{f}
can be computed efficiently. This notion of black-box does not imply the

of the constructed function
\mathsf{Con}_{f}
. We make this choice, because we want to show impossibility results on black-box proofs, and this choice makes our results stronger.

6

6

. See e.g.,
[

]
.
We also remark that the terms ”black-box” and ‘‘non-black-box’’ are used to mean many different things in the literature.

7

7

Parameters for black-box proofs for the extreme high-end.

As a consequence of Proposition

, if we assume
the extreme high-end hardness assumption
, to obtain an extreme high-end PRG (as in Open Problem

) using a black-box
\mathsf{hard}\mbox{-}\mathsf{function}\Rightarrow\mathsf{PRG}

Open Problem 1.9
.

Does there exist a black-box
\mathsf{hard}\mbox{-}\mathsf{function}\Rightarrow\mathsf{PRG}

Seed length:

. (Any black-box proof must have

).

.

is a

, however, it is not a

We do not know whether black-box
\mathsf{hard}\mbox{-}\mathsf{function}\Rightarrow\mathsf{PRG}

8

8

. More specifically, we show that certain approaches cannot yield reductions with few queries. Before we describe our results, we give more background on black-box proofs.

1.2.3
Black-box proofs as codes, extractors, and other applications

Black-boxness is often helpful in PRG constructions (as demonstrated in
[
NW94
,
KvM02
,
MV05
,
SU05
]
) as such proofs readily extend to other computational models (e.g, bounded depth circuits, or nondeterministic circuits). There are other motivations to study black-box proofs (in addition to PRG constructions and hardness amplification). In fact, in the connections and applications below, the “black-boxness” of the proofs is crucial and helpful.

List-decodable codes.

Following Sudan, Trevisan and Vadhan
[
STV01
]
, a Black-box
\mathsf{hard}\mbox{-}\mathsf{function}\Rightarrow{\rho^{\prime}}\mbox{-}\mathsf{hard}\mbox{-}\mathsf{function}
proof
(\mathsf{Con},\mathsf{Red})
yields a “list-decodable code”
E:\{0,1\}^{2^{\ell}}\to\{0,1\}^{2^{\ell^{\prime}}}
, defined by
E(f)_{y}=\mathsf{Con}_{f}(y)
. A consequence of lower bounds on the rate of such codes is that
\ell^{\prime}\geq\ell+2\cdot\log(1/\rho^{\prime})
, and that
a\geq 2\cdot\log(1/\rho^{\prime})
. Viewing the reduction as a “list” of
2^{a}
procedures (one for every advice string
\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{a}
) yields a variant of a “local list-decoding algorithm” for the defined code, with the same number of queries. Techniques developed for black-box proofs
[
SV10
,
GSV18
,
AASY16
]
have been useful in proving lower bounds on the number of queries of such codes
[
RSV21
]
.

Randomness Extractors:

Following Trevisan
[
Tre01
]
, a black-box
\mathsf{hard}\mbox{-}\mathsf{function}\Rightarrow{\epsilon}\mbox{-}\mathsf{PRG}
proof
(\mathsf{Con},\mathsf{Red})
yields a “randomness extractor”
E:\{0,1\}^{2^{\ell}}\times\{0,1\}^{r}\to\{0,1\}^{m}
, defined by
E(f,y)=\mathsf{Con}_{f}(y)
, and extracting randomness from sources with min-entropy
k=a+\log(1/\epsilon)+O(1)
. A consequence of lower bounds on extractors
[
RTS00
]
is that
r\geq\ell+2\cdot\log(1/\epsilon)
, and that
a\geq m+\log(1/\epsilon)-O(1)
. Continuing the analogy of the previous item, the reduction can be viewed as a local list-decoding algorithm for an “extractor-code”
[
TZ04
]
. Local list-decoding algorithms for (standard) codes, and for extractor-codes are closely related to “hard-core bits” for cryptographic primitives (see e.g.
[
RSV21
]
for a discussion).

Other applications.

In recent years, black-box
\mathsf{hard}\mbox{-}\mathsf{function}\Rightarrow\mathsf{PRG}
proofs have found numerous applications in areas that are not directly related to pseudorandomness, and rely on “black-boxness”. Some examples are: Learning and compression algorithms (Carmosino et al.
[
CIKK16
]
and subsequent work), worst-case to average-case reductions within NP (Hirahara
[
Hir18
]
and subsequent work), and Kolmogorov Complexity (Allender et al.
[
ABK+06
]
and subsequent work).

1.2.4
Black-box proofs and the hybrid argument

The hybrid argument cannot be used in the extreme high-end.

Most known black-box
\mathsf{hard}\mbox{-}\mathsf{function}\Rightarrow\mathsf{PRG}
proofs in the literature rely on
hardness amplification
in order to use the
hybrid argument
of
[
Yao82
,
GM84
]
. That is, to achieve an
\epsilon
-PRG, the construction is a sequence of two black-box proofs:
\mathsf{hard}\mbox{-}\mathsf{function}\Rightarrow(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\epsilon}{m})\mbox{-}\mathsf{hard}\mbox{-}\mathsf{function}\Rightarrow\epsilon\mbox{-}\mathsf{PRG}
. Thus, even for constant
\epsilon
, hardness amplification must be performed to
\rho^{\prime}\leq(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{m})
. By the bounds in Section
1.2.3
, in the first step, a function
f_{1}
with input length
\ell
must be transformed into a function
f^{\prime}
with input length
\ell^{\prime}\geq\ell+2\cdot\log\frac{1}{\rho^{\prime}}\geq\ell+2\log m
.
The final PRG construction will have seed length
r\geq\ell^{\prime}\geq\ell+2\log m\geq 3\log m
. This is too large in the extreme high end, where we want
r=(1+o(1))\cdot\log m
.

The hybrid argument suffices for the high-end.

We remark that taking
\ell^{\prime}=O(\ell+\log m)
and
r=O(\ell^{\prime})
does suffice for the (non-extreme) high-end, and this is how known constructions for the high-end
[
IW97
,
STV01
,
SU05
,
Uma03
]
are achieved.
9
9

9

More specifically, hardness amplification can be performed (by a black-box proof) using “local list-decodable codes”
[
STV01
]
, and the second
{(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\epsilon}{m})}\mbox{-}\mathsf{hard}\mbox{-}\mathsf{function}\Rightarrow\mathsf{PRG}
step is done using the Nisan-Wigderson generator
[
NW94
]
, which is a black-box proof. Shaltiel and Umans
[
SU05
]
and Umans
[
Uma03
]
gave an alternative direct transformation from worst-case hard function into PRGs which achieves a better seed length in the “low-end”. However, as it also relies on the hybrid argument (and implies hardness amplification), it cannot achieve the extreme high-end.

1.3.1
Limitations on constructions of black-box
\mathsf{hard}\mbox{-}\mathsf{function}\Rightarrow\mathsf{PRG}
proofs

We show that for any black-box
\mathsf{hard}\mbox{-}\mathsf{function}\Rightarrow\mathsf{PRG}
proof
(\mathsf{Con},\mathsf{Red})
, if
\mathsf{Red}
makes

queries, then
\mathsf{Con}
must be structured in a way that allows “fixing many outputs, with small information cost”. More precisely, we introduce a measure
\operatorname{Fix}_{j}(\mathsf{Con})
defined to be the minimal number
h
, so that when
F
is chosen at random from
\mathcal{F}_{\ell,1}
, it is possible to fix
j
outputs of
\mathsf{Con}_{F}
, while reducing the information about
F
by only
h
bits of information.

Definition 1.10

(The cost of fixing
j
outputs)
.

Given
\mathsf{Con}:\mathcal{F}_{\ell,1}\to\mathcal{F}_{r,m}
we define
\operatorname{Fix}_{j}(\mathsf{Con})
to be the minimal number
h
such that there exist
j

such that:

\Pr_{F\leftarrow\mathcal{F}_{\ell,1}}[\forall i\in[j]:\exists y_{i}\in\{0,1\}^{r}\mbox{ s.t. }\mathsf{Con}_{F}(y_{i})=z_{i}]\geq 2^{-h}.

We show that if
\mathsf{Red}
makes a small number
q

that is not too large,

. Loosely speaking, this means that after a “fixed cost” of
a

\mathsf{Con}_{F}
can be fixed at the cost of roughly
\log q
bits of information about
F
, per

Theorem 1.11
.

There exists a constant
\nu>0
such that
for every
{\rho}\mbox{-}\mathsf{hard}\mbox{-}\mathsf{function}\Rightarrow{\epsilon}\mbox{-}\mathsf{PRG}
proof

with parameters

, if

makes

queries, then for every

,

\operatorname{Fix}_{j}(\mathsf{Con})\leq a+j\cdot(\log q+O(1))\leq a+j\cdot(\ell+O(1)).

Previous limitations on the number of queries for reductions in black-box proofs (of any type) do not apply when

and therefore are unapplicable in the extreme high-end

We stress that Theorem

is unrelated to the “hybrid argument” and applies even for constructions where the correctness of the reduction

Moreover, the result applies for the whole range of parameters, and regardless of the choices of seed length and output length. See Section

for a more general statement and a discussion.

In the next section we use Theorem

to show limitations on the “PEG + extractor” approach of
[

]
.

Distinction between black-box PRGs and typical extractors.

Following Trevisan
[
Tre01
]
(see discussion in Section
1.2.3
) we know that construction maps for black-box
\mathsf{hard}\mbox{-}\mathsf{function}\Rightarrow\mathsf{PRG}
proofs are extractors (regardless of the number of queries used by the reduction). In fact, extractors and black-box proofs are essentially equivalent if we do not restrict the number of queries made by the reduction.

It is standard that if we choose a construction map
\mathsf{Con}
at random, it will be an extractor. Nevertheless, we show that it is unlikely that a random construction map
\mathsf{Con}
will have
\operatorname{Fix}_{j}(\mathsf{Con})\leq a+j\cdot\ell
(for relevant values of
j
). This implies that:

Theorem 1.12

(informal)
.

It is unlikely that a random construction map (which is an extractor w.h.p) will have a “useful” reduction with
q<2^{\ell}
.

More details, and a precise statement is given in Section
3.1.2
. This demonstrates that requiring a construction to have
q<2^{\ell}
and be useful for PRGs (and not just for extractors) places limitations on the structure of the construction.

Our interpretation.

Our interpretation of Theorem
1.11
is that in order to enable the reduction to make few queries, the construction must “create a backdoor” and introduce correlations between different outputs. These correlations are “slightly harmful” to the goal of being an extractor. More precisely, having low
\operatorname{Fix}_{j}(\mathsf{Con})
means that there is a source distribution with min-entropy that is very high (only lacking
\operatorname{Fix}_{j}(\mathsf{Con})
bits of information) on which
j
outputs of the extractor are fixed. This will violate the extractor guarantee if
j
is close to
2^{r}
(but is possible for
j\ll 2^{r}
which is the case in Theorem
1.11
).

Theorems
1.11
and
1.12
suggest that it is more difficult to design PRG constructions than extractors. In Section
3.1.3
we review the known
\mathsf{hard}\mbox{-}\mathsf{function}\Rightarrow\mathsf{PRG}
constructions in the literature (the Nisan-Wigderson PRG
[
NW94
]
and the Shaltiel-Umans PRG
[
SU05
,
Uma03
]
) observing how they achieve low
\operatorname{Fix}_{j}(\mathsf{Con})
in the high-end, and why they do not achieve this in the extreme high-end.
We hope that understanding conditions that
\mathsf{hard}\mbox{-}\mathsf{function}\Rightarrow\mathsf{PRG}
constructions must satisfy, may point us to new constructions that may be applicable in the extreme high-end.

Technique.

We consider a “distinguisher”
D_{f}:\{0,1\}^{m}\to\{0,1\}
that answers one iff its input is an output of
\mathsf{Con}_{f}
. For every function
f\in\mathcal{F}_{\ell,1}
, as
D_{f}

\mathsf{PRG}\mbox{-}\mathsf{breaks}

\mathsf{Con}_{f}
, by Definition
1.7
, there must exist
\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{a}
such that the function
C(x)=\mathsf{Red}^{D_{f}}(x,\alpha)
satisfies
C=f
. However,
D_{f}
only answers one on
2^{r}
out of the possible
2^{m}
queries. If
\mathsf{Red}
does not ask such “interesting queries”, then it obtains no information on
f
, and cannot hope to reconstruct every
f\in\mathcal{F}_{\ell,1}
.

How does
\mathsf{Red}
know to ask interesting queries? The advice string
\alpha
(that depends on
f
) may give
\mathsf{Red}
information about interesting queries. However, the information in the advice string is limited by its length
a
, and we show that if
\mathsf{Red}
is able to find interesting queries for many choices of
f\in\mathcal{F}_{\ell,1}
and
x\in\{0,1\}^{\ell}
, then after this “fixed cost” of
a
bits of information, it is still difficult for
\mathsf{Red}
to find interesting queries, unless the construction
\mathsf{Con}
is set up so that many interesting queries (that is, outputs of
\mathsf{Con}_{f}
) have low information, giving that

for many values of

). The precise details are given in Section
3
.

1.3.2
Limitations on the “PEG + Extractor” approach of
[
DMOZ20
]

Doron et al.
[
DMOZ20
]
showed how to construct extreme high end PRGs from a strengthening of
the extreme high-end hardness assumption
of Open Problem
1.5
. More specifically, rather than only assuming that
f
cannot be computed by circuits of size
2^{(1-o(1))\cdot\ell}
, it is assumed that this holds even for circuits that are allowed to use nondeterminism and randomness (and can be thought of as a nonuniform analog of the class MA). This assumption is significantly stronger then
the extreme high-end hardness assumption
(although, still plausible).

The PEG + extractor approach.

The approach of
[
DMOZ20
]
is to construct a pseudo-entropy generator (PEGs) (for a specific notion of “computational entropy” suggested in
[
BSW03
]
). This type of PEG can be thought of as a weak notion of PRGs, that is only guaranteed to fool tests that accept a very small fraction of the
2^{m}
inputs:

Definition 1.13

(PEGs)
.

A function
G:\{0,1\}^{r}\to\{0,1\}^{m}
is a
(k,\epsilon)
-PEG
for a function
D:\{0,1\}^{m}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
, if

\Pr[D(U_{m})=1]\leq 2^{k-m}
then
\Pr[D(G(U_{r}))=1]-\Pr[D(U_{m})=1]\leq\epsilon
. We say that
D

(k,\epsilon)
-
\mathsf{PEG}\mbox{-}\mathsf{breaks}

G
, if
G
is not an
\epsilon

D
.
10
10

10

We remark that the requirement that
\Pr[D(G(U_{r}))=1]-\Pr[D(U_{m})=1]\leq\epsilon
is sometimes replaced by the stronger requirement that
\Pr[D(G(U_{r}))=1]\leq\epsilon
, or following
[
BSW03
]
, by the requirement that
\Pr[D(G(U_{r}))=1]\leq\Pr[D(U_{m})=1]\cdot 2^{m-k}+\epsilon
which is stronger still, if we replace
\epsilon
by
\epsilon^{\prime}=\epsilon/2
and
k
by
k^{\prime}=k+\log(1/\epsilon^{\prime})
.
We are interested in proving limitations on PEG and so taking a weak definition only makes our results stronger (especially as we are interested in constant
\epsilon
and
k\ll m
and the distinction between

More specifically, when given a function
f:\{0,1\}^{\ell}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
(which is hard against the stronger model of circuits equipped with nondeterminism and randomness) the construction of
[

]
works in two steps:

1.

PEG:
Use the hard function to construct a PEG

.

2.

Extractor:
Use an explicit extractor
\operatorname{EXT}:\{0,1\}^{m_{\operatorname{PEG}}}\times\{0,1\}^{r_{\operatorname{EXT}}=(1+o(1))\cdot\ell}\to\{0,1\}^{m=2^{(1-o(1))\cdot\ell}}
with entropy threshold

(such explicit constructions are known unconditionally).

The final PRG
G:\{0,1\}^{r=r_{\operatorname{PEG}}+r_{\operatorname{EXT}}}\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{m}
is obtained by interpreting a string
y\in\{0,1\}^{r}
as two strings

and

G(y)=\operatorname{EXT}(\operatorname{PEG}(y_{1}),y_{2})

The seed length of a PEG.

The final seed length of
G
is
r=r_{\operatorname{PEG}}+r_{\operatorname{EXT}}
. Using lower bounds on the seed length of extractors
[
RTS00
]

r_{\operatorname{EXT}}\geq\log(m_{\operatorname{PEG}}-k_{\operatorname{PEG}})\geq(1-o(1))\cdot\ell

r_{\operatorname{PEG}}=o(\ell)

r=o(\log m)
, whereas, as noted earlier, PRGs must have
r\geq\log m-O(\log\log m)
).

Summing up, the construction of Doron et al.
[

relies on the observation that PEGs are weaker objects than PRGs (and are therefore easier to construct) and that PEGs may have seed length that is significantly shorter than PRGs, so that summing the two seed lengths can still yield an almost optimal seed length.

The notion of PEGs in Definition

1.3.3
Lower bounds on black-box hardness amplification at the extreme high-end

Grinberg, Shaltiel and Viola
[
GSV18
]
(continuing a line of previous work
[
Vio06
,
SV10
,
AS14
]
) proved a lower bound of
q\geq\Omega(\frac{\ell}{\epsilon^{2}})
on the number of queries in reductions for black-box
\mathsf{hard}\mbox{-}\mathsf{function}\Rightarrow{(\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon)}\mbox{-}\mathsf{hard}\mbox{-}\mathsf{function}
proofs (a.k.a. hardness amplification). By Proposition

, such bounds imply that using black-box proofs to convert a function
f

m

m\leq\frac{s-a}{q}\leq\frac{s}{q}
which means that such transformation “lose a factor
q
in the hardness”.

In this paper we prove a lower bound of
q\geq\Omega(\frac{1}{\epsilon})
, which is quantitatively weaker than that of
[

]
, but unlike
[
GSV18
]
it applies in the extreme high-end. That is, our result allows
a=2^{(1-o(1))\cdot\ell}
whereas
[
GSV18
]
(as well as all previous bounds) only works if
a\leq 2^{\nu\cdot\ell}
for some constant
\nu>0
. (It is open to match the bound of
[

]
for large

).

is the first bound on the number of queries in black-box hardness amplification that applies for
a\geq 2^{\ell/2}
and to the extreme high-end.

Using Proposition
1.8
, Theorem
1.15
implies that even if one starts from
the extreme high-end hardness assumption
, then to obtain a
{(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{m})}\mbox{-}\mathsf{hard}\mbox{-}\mathsf{function}
for circuits of size
m
(and apply the hybrid argument as explained in Section
1.2.4
) there must be a “hardness loss”, and
m\leq\frac{2^{\ell}}{q}\leq\frac{2^{\ell}}{m}
, implying that
m\leq 2^{\ell/2}
.

Note that this limitation applies regardless of the length
\ell^{\prime}
of the input length of
\mathsf{Con}_{f}
. This means that a black-box
\mathsf{hard}\mbox{-}\mathsf{function}\Rightarrow\mathsf{PRG}
proof that relies on hardness amplification and the hybrid argument (that is:
\mathsf{hard}\mbox{-}\mathsf{function}\Rightarrow{(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{m})}\mbox{-}\mathsf{hard}\mbox{-}\mathsf{function}\Rightarrow\mathsf{PRG}
) must have
m\leq 2^{\ell/2}
, and this holds even if the seed length
r
of the PRG is large.

In the next section we show that a similar argument also gives limitations on using hardness amplification together with the “PRG composition” approach of Chen and Tell
[
CT21b
]
.

Technique.

The work of
[
GSV18
]
(as well as previous work in this area) relied on information theoretic techniques that break down for large
a
. Indeed, the proof of Theorem
1.15
uses a different argument. This argument builds on ideas of Applebaum et al.
[
AASY16
]
which connect the number of queries required by a reduction (or in the case of
[
RSV21
]
a local list-decoding algorithm) to the success that small size, constant depth circuits have in solving the “coin problem” (that is distinguishing a sequence of independent tosses of an unbiased coin from a sequence of independent tosses of a slightly biased coin). The proofs of
[
AASY16
,
RSV21
]
do not work for
a\geq 2^{\ell/2}
and our results are obtained by proving tighter bounds on depth 3 circuits for specific versions of the coin problem that come up in the argument. The proof is given in Section
4
. As a consequence, we also improve the bounds of
[
RSV21
]
on the number of queries of local list-decoding algorithms, see Section
5
for details.

1.3.4
The hybrid argument and the “PRG composition” approach of
[
CT21b
]

Chen and Tell
[
CT21b
]
construct extreme high end PRGs if, in addition to
the extreme high-end hardness assumption
of Open Problem
1.5
, one also assumes the existence of one-way functions (OWFs). The existence of OWFs is a standard and widely believed assumption in cryptography. Nevertheless, OWFs (or more generally cryptography) are not known to be implied by extreme high-end PRGs (or other PRGs in complexity theory). Assuming OWFs does not seem necessary.

The PRG composition approach.

Chen and Tell
[
CT21b
]
start from a hard function
f:\{0,1\}^{\ell}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
given by
the extreme high-end hardness assumption
. Their PRG
G:\{0,1\}^{(1+o(1))\cdot\ell}\to\{0,1\}^{2^{(1-o(1))\cdot\ell}}
is obtained by PRG composition, namely
G(y)=G_{2}(G_{1}(y))
where:

1.

G_{1}:\{0,1\}^{(1+o(1))\cdot\ell}\to\{0,1\}^{m_{1}=2^{\Omega(\ell)}}
is a PRG against circuits of size
2^{(1-o(1))\cdot\ell}
that is constructed from
the extreme high-end hardness assumption
using hardness amplification, the Nisan-Wigderson PRG, and the hybrid argument.
13
13

13

More precisely, the cost of the hybrid argument (explained in Section
1.2.4
) is measured in terms of the output length
m
(even if the PRG fools circuits of larger size, as is the case here). This means, that the goal of fooling circuits of size
2^{(1-o(1))\cdot\ell}
can be achieved by known black-box proofs (in the same manner explained in Section
1.2.4
) from the
extreme high-end hardness assumption
for PRGs that output
m_{1}=2^{\Omega(\ell)}
bits, rather than
m=2^{(1-o(1))\cdot\ell}
bits.

2.

G_{2}:\{0,1\}^{m_{1}=2^{\Omega(\ell)}}\to\{0,1\}^{m=2^{(1-o(1))\cdot\ell}}
is a PRG with modest stretch (polynomial rather than exponential) that suffices to push the output length from
m_{1}=2^{\Omega(\ell)}
to
m=2^{(1-o(1))\cdot\ell}
. Nevertheless, for the composition to be a PRG, it is crucial that
G_{2}
can be computed in time
2^{(1-o(1))\cdot\ell}
(that is in almost linear time in its output length
m
).
14
14

14

This requirement is necessary as in the composition one needs to consider a distinguisher for
G_{1}
that runs
G_{2}
as a procedure, and
G_{1}
cannot fool circuits of size larger than
2^{\ell}
. We also remark that in contrast to cryptography, in hardness vs. randomness, efficiency of components is rarely used in proving security of the final primitive, and this is one such rare instance.
Such PRGs indeed follow from the existence of OWFs
[
HILL99
]
.

A natural question is whether it is possible to construct the PRG
G_{2}
from
the extreme high-end hardness assumption
. This will remove the need for OWFs.

PRGs with polynomial stretch follow from this assumption (and even from weaker versions like
the high-end hardness assumption
or “low-end” versions). This is good news, as it shows that hardness amplification and the hybrid argument
can
yield sufficient stretch in this case.

The issue is that the PRGs constructed by these methods do not run in time that is nearly linear in their output length
m
(and instead run in time
m^{c}
where
c>2
). This means that they are unsuitable for the PRG composition approach (and this is why
[
CT21b
]
relies on OWFs).

Our results.

Theorem
1.15
implies that PRGs (even with modest stretch) cannot run in nearly linear time, if they are obtained using black-box hardness amplification and the hybrid argument, assuming
the extreme high-end hardness assumption
. This implies that if we only assume
the extreme high-end hardness assumption
(and do not assume the existence of OWFs) then current techniques cannot yield the PRG
G_{2}
required by the PRG composition.

More precisely, we have already seen in Section
1.3.3
that in order to do a hybrid argument for output length
m
, one needs a hardness amplification result that amplifies to below
\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{m}
, and that Theorem
1.15
implies that
m\leq 2^{\frac{\ell}{2}}
. This holds in the extreme high-end, regardless of the relationship between
r
and
m
. In particular, it also holds when trying to construct PRGs with modest stretch like
G_{2}
, assuming
the extreme high-end hardness assumption
. On the other hand, assuming that computing the average-case hard function
\mathsf{Con}_{f}
takes at least the time it takes to compute the worst-case hard function
f
, and recalling that
f
cannot be computed by circuits of size
2^{(1-o(1))\cdot\ell}
, we conclude that
\mathsf{Con}_{f}
cannot be computed in time
2^{(1-o(1))\cdot\ell}
(which is at least
m^{2-o(1)}
).

Summing up, after performing hardness amplification, there must be at least a quadratic gap between the time it takes to compute
\mathsf{Con}_{f}
, and the circuit size for which it is hard on average. This gap is inherited by the final PRG
G_{2}
. Consequently,
G_{2}
cannot run in time smaller than
m^{2-o(1)}
, and in particular, there is in obstacle for obtaining PRGs that run in time nearly linear in
m
, using these techniques (even if the stretch is modest).

This shows an obstacle for using current techniques (that rely on hardness amplification and the hybrid argument) to apply the PRG composition approach of
[
CT21b
]
assuming only
the extreme high-end hardness assumption
. This partially explains why
[
CT21b
]
need the additional assumption that OWFs exist in order to construct the PRG
G_{2}
.

3.1.1
A general statement of Theorem

Review of the setup of Theorem

.

Theorem

shows that for any black-box
\mathsf{hard}\mbox{-}\mathsf{function}\Rightarrow\mathsf{PRG}
proof
(\mathsf{Con},\mathsf{Red})
, if
\mathsf{Red}
is useful, and makes

queries, then
\mathsf{Con}
must be structured in a way that allows “fixing many outputs, with small information cost”. This is measured by
\operatorname{Fix}_{j}(\mathsf{Con})
from Definition
1.10
, which defines
\operatorname{Fix}_{j}(\mathsf{Con})
to be the minimal number
h
, so that when
F
is chosen at random from
\mathcal{F}_{\ell,1}
, it is possible to fix
j
outputs of
\mathsf{Con}_{F}
, while only reducing
h
bits of information about
F
.

Theorem
1.11
shows that in order for
\mathsf{Red}
to be useful and have

, it must be that for many sufficiently large choices of
j

\operatorname{Fix}_{j}(\mathsf{Con})\leq a+j\cdot(\log q+O(1))
. This means that after a “fixed cost” of
a
bits of information, a large number of outputs of
\mathsf{Con}_{F}
can be fixed at the cost of
\log q+O(1)
bits of information about
F
, per output.

A more general statement.

The following theorem is a generalized version of Theorem
1.11
which also allows the parameter
\rho
(measuring how hard on average is the function we start from) to be very close to
\frac{1}{2}
, and then the amortized cost

, is replaced by
\log\frac{q}{\rho-\frac{1}{2}}
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
(\mathsf{Con},\mathsf{Red})
be a black-box
{\rho}\mbox{-}\mathsf{hard}\mbox{-}\mathsf{function}\Rightarrow{\epsilon}\mbox{-}\mathsf{PRG}
proof for parameters
\ell,r,m,a,\epsilon,\rho
such that
\mathsf{Red}
makes at most

queries. If
\rho=\frac{1}{2}+\eta

\eta\geq 2^{-\ell}
,
\epsilon\leq 1-2^{r-m}
, and

, then for
j_{\max}=\nu\cdot\frac{\eta^{2}\cdot 2^{\ell}}{\ell}
, and every

,

\operatorname{Fix}_{j}(\mathsf{Con})\leq a+j\cdot(\log q+\log\frac{4}{\eta}).

Theorem
1.11

3.1.2
black-box PRGs are different than typical extractors

In this section we prove Theorem
1.12
which loosely states that a random construction
\mathsf{Con}
for a black-box
\mathsf{hard}\mbox{-}\mathsf{function}\Rightarrow\mathsf{PRG}
proof is likely to be an extractor, but unlikely to be useful when transforming hard functions into PRGs.

Formal connection between extractors and black-box
\mathsf{hard}\mbox{-}\mathsf{function}\Rightarrow\mathsf{PRG}
proofs.

It is well known following Trevisan’s breakthrough construction
[
Tre01
]
(and as explained in Section
1.2.3
) that black-box
\mathsf{hard}\mbox{-}\mathsf{function}\Rightarrow\mathsf{PRG}
proofs, are closely related to randomness extractors. Let us formally specify this connection. We start with a formal definition of randomness extractors.

Definition 3.2

(extractors)
.

A function
E:\{0,1\}^{n}\times\{0,1\}^{r}\to\{0,1\}^{m}
is a
(k,\epsilon)
-
extractor
if for every distribution
X
over
\{0,1\}^{n}
, with
H_{\infty}(X)\geq k
,
E(X,U_{r})
is
\epsilon
-close to
U_{m}
.

In order to compare function
E:\{0,1\}^{n}\times\{0,1\}^{r}\to\{0,1\}^{m}
to functions
\mathsf{Con}:\mathcal{F}_{\ell,1}\to\mathcal{F}_{r,m}
we use the following notation.

Definition 3.3

(Constructions and extractors)
.

A string
f\in\{0,1\}^{n}
can be viewed as a function
f:\{0,1\}^{\log n}\to\{0,1\}
by
f(i)=f_{i}
. This also applies in the other direction, allowing a function
f:\{0,1\}^{\ell}\to\{0,1\}
to be viewed as a string
f\in\{0,1\}^{2^{\ell}}
.

Given a function
E:\{0,1\}^{n}\times\{0,1\}^{r}\to\{0,1\}^{m}
, we define
\ell=\log n
, and

\mathsf{Con}^{E}:\mathcal{F}_{\ell,1}\to\mathcal{F}_{r,m}
where
\mathsf{Con}^{E}_{f}:\{0,1\}^{r}\to\{0,1\}^{m}
is defined by
\mathsf{Con}^{E}_{f}(y)=E(f,y)
. This also applies in the other direction, where a function
\mathsf{Con}:\mathcal{F}_{\ell,1}\to\mathcal{F}_{r,m}
induces a function
E:\{0,1\}^{n=2^{\ell}}\times\{0,1\}^{r}\to\{0,1\}^{m}
by

.

With this notation, extractors and black-box
\mathsf{hard}\mbox{-}\mathsf{function}\Rightarrow\mathsf{PRG}
proofs, are essentially equivalent. This is stated in the following standard proposition which shows that the two notions are roughly equivalent for
a\approx k
.

Proposition 3.4

(Extractors are essentially equivalent to black-box
\mathsf{hard}\mbox{-}\mathsf{function}\Rightarrow\mathsf{PRG}
proofs)
.

Let

, and let
\ell=\log n
.

1.

If
\mathsf{Con}^{E}
can be matched with an oracle procedure
\mathsf{Red}^{(\cdot)}
such that the pair

proof with advice length

is a
(k,2\epsilon)
-extractor, for

2.

If

(k,\epsilon)
-extractor then there exists an oracle procedure
\mathsf{Red}^{(\cdot)}
such that the pair
(\mathsf{Con}^{E},\mathsf{Red})
is a black-box
\mathsf{hard}\mbox{-}\mathsf{function}\Rightarrow{\epsilon}\mbox{-}\mathsf{PRG}
proof with advice length
a=k+1
.

Reductions must make few queries to be useful.

Note however, that in order to be useful for PRG construction, a black-box
\mathsf{hard}\mbox{-}\mathsf{function}\Rightarrow\mathsf{PRG}
proof must have a reduction
\mathsf{Red}
that make few queries. In particular, as can be seen in Proposition
1.8
, the reduction is useless if it makes

queries (and even if it makes

The equivalence of Proposition

does not mention the number of queries (which can be as large as

\mathsf{hard}\mbox{-}\mathsf{function}\Rightarrow\mathsf{PRG}
proof is an extractor even when the number of queries is very large, but it must have

in order to be useful when converting hard functions into PRGs.

A typical extractor is not a useful PRG construction.

We now show that for a wide range of parameters
\ell,r,m
and

(and in particular for the parameters that correspond to the high-end and the extreme high-end) if we choose a function
E:\{0,1\}^{n}\times\{0,1\}^{r}\to\{0,1\}^{m}
uniformly at random then:

•

It is unlikely that construction
\mathsf{Con}^{E}
(associated with

\operatorname{Fix}_{j}(\mathsf{Con}^{E})

\mathsf{Con}^{E}
can be matched with a reduction
\mathsf{Red}
that makes

•

However, a standard argument shows that it is likely that

can be matched with a reduction

(although

As explained in Section

and applies even when the stretch is small. Furthermore, the probability in the first item is

which is quite small.
We also note that the requirement that

•

For every

j\cdot m
. We are only requiring that
a+j\cdot\ell\leq j\cdot(m-r)-O(1)
which is quite close to the obvious bound.

•

The total amount of information in
f
is at most

. We are only requiring that
a+j\cdot\ell\leq\frac{2^{\ell}}{2}
which is quite close to the obvious bound.

Proof.

(of Theorem
3.6
)
The second item is a standard calculation which can be found for example in
[
RTS00
]
. Therefore, we will only prove the first item.
Within this proof, unless otherwise specified, all probabilities are regarding the experiment
E\leftarrow\mathsf{RndE}_{\ell,r,m}
.

For a fixed set
S\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}
of size
K=2^{n-(a+j\cdot\ell)}
and a sequence
\bar{z}=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{j})
of distinct elements in
\{0,1\}^{m}
, let
A_{S,\bar{z}}
denote the event that for every

, there exist
y_{1},\ldots,y_{j}\in\{0,1\}^{r}
such that for all

,
E(f,y_{i})=z_{i}
.
This definition is made so that:

\Pr[\operatorname{Fix}_{j}(\mathsf{Con}^{E})\leq a+j\cdot\ell]\leq{2^{n}\choose K}\cdot 2^{m\cdot j}\cdot\Pr[A_{S,\bar{z}}],

where the latter expression is obtained by a union bound over all choices of
S
and
\bar{z}
. For every such

and
\bar{z}
, and for every

denote the event that there exist
y_{1},\ldots,y_{j}\in\{0,1\}^{r}
such that for all
i\in[j]

E(f,y_{i})=z_{i}
. For every
f\in S

,

\Pr[A_{S,\bar{z},f}]\leq 2^{r\cdot j}\cdot 2^{-m\cdot j}.

These events are independent for the
K

\Pr[A_{S,\bar{z}}]\leq\left(2^{r\cdot j}\cdot 2^{-m\cdot j}\right)^{K}=2^{-j\cdot K\cdot(m-r)}.

We conclude that:

\displaystyle\Pr[\operatorname{Fix}_{j}(\mathsf{Con}^{E})\leq a+j\cdot\ell]

\displaystyle\leq{2^{n}\choose K}\cdot 2^{m\cdot j}\cdot 2^{-j\cdot K\cdot(m-r)}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{2^{n}\cdot e}{K}\right)^{K}\cdot 2^{m\cdot j}\cdot 2^{-j\cdot K\cdot(m-r)}

Let

. In order to show that this probability is smaller than

it is sufficient to show that:

1.

\left(\frac{2^{n}\cdot e}{K}\right)^{K}\cdot 2^{-j\cdot K\cdot(m-r)}\leq 2^{-2v}

These two conditions follow by the requirements of the theorem. More specifically, the requirement that

gives that

. This gives that the first condition follows if
a+j\cdot\ell\leq j\cdot(m-r)-O(1)
which is one of the requirements of the theorem.

The second condition follows because by our requirement
j\leq\frac{2^{\ell}}{2}
and
m\leq 2^{\ell}
. Therefore for sufficiently large

,
m\cdot j\leq 2^{2^{\ell}/2}=v

∎

3.1.3
Revisiting the Nisan-Wigderson PRG and the Shaltiel-Umans PRG

As surveyed in Section
1.2.4
there are only two known constructions of black-box
\mathsf{hard}\mbox{-}\mathsf{function}\Rightarrow\mathsf{PRG}
proofs in the literature, where
\mathsf{Red}
is useful and makes
q
queries, for
q
that can be significantly less than
2^{\ell}
. These are the Nisan-Wigderson PRG
[
NW94
]
and an additional construction is due to Shaltiel and Umans
[
SU05
]
and Umans
[
Uma03
]
.

The former is more versatile, and has many additional applications, mainly because its reduction requires less computational resources, and makes less queries.
The latter has advantages over the Nisan-Wigderson PRG as it allows to achieve
r=O(\ell)
even for small values of
a
(like
a=\operatorname{poly}(\ell)
, which corresponds to “low-end” PRGs).

Nevertheless, as explained in Section
1.2.4
, both these approaches rely on hardness amplification and the hybrid argument, preventing them from achieving the extreme high-end.

Theorem
1.11
and
3.1
show that in
any
black-box proof with
q\leq 2^{\ell}
,
even one that does not rely on the hybrid argument
,
\operatorname{Fix}_{j}(\mathsf{Con})
must be small. Let us review how the known PRGs achieve this.

The Nisan-Wigderson PRG.

The Nisan-Wigderson PRG is a black-box
{(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\epsilon}{m})}\mbox{-}\mathsf{hard}\mbox{-}\mathsf{function}\Rightarrow{\epsilon}\mbox{-}\mathsf{PRG}
proof
(\mathsf{Con}^{NW},\mathsf{Red}^{NW})
with parameters
\ell,r,m,a,\rho=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\epsilon}{m}
and
\epsilon
, such that
\mathsf{Red}
makes a
single
query. This means that the Nisan-Wigderson PRG is applicable in the extreme high-end, when starting from a
(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\epsilon}{m})\mbox{-}\mathsf{hard}\mbox{-}\mathsf{function}
. As explained in Section
1.3.3
current hardness amplification techniques are not applicable for
m\geq 2^{\ell/2}
. This means that they cannot be used in the extreme high-end, even if we are willing to allow large seed length
r
.

By Theorem
3.1
, we have that:

\operatorname{Fix}_{j}(\mathsf{Con}^{NW})\leq a+j\cdot(\log m+O(1)),

for constant
\epsilon>0
, and many values of
m
and
j
. Let us review how
\mathsf{Con}^{NW}
achieves this.

The construction
\mathsf{Con}^{NW}
is defined
using a “design”, namely a collection
S_{1},\ldots,S_{m}\subseteq[r]
such that for every
i\in[m]
,
|S_{i}|=\ell
, and then for every
f\in\mathcal{F}_{\ell,1}
,
\mathsf{Con}^{NW}_{f}:\{0,1\}^{r}\to\{0,1\}^{m}
is defined by

\mathsf{Con}^{NW}_{f}(x)=f(x|_{S_{1}}),\ldots,f(x|_{S_{m}}).

Another version that is often considered is a “seed extending” version
\overline{\mathsf{Con}}^{NW}_{f}(x)=x,\mathsf{Con}^{NW}_{f}(x)
that outputs
m+r
bits.
The analysis of
[
NW94
]
shows that the parameter
a
is determined by the sizes of pairwise intersections of sets in the design. More specifically, using an improved analysis by Raz, Reingold and Vadhan
[
RRV02
]
(for a condition called “weak design”) it follows that taking

a=\sum_{i\in[m]}\sum_{i^{\prime}\neq i}2^{|S_{i^{\prime}}\cap S_{i}|}\leq m\cdot 2^{\max_{i\neq i^{\prime}}|S_{i}\cap S_{i^{\prime}}|},

suffices to obtain a reduction
\mathsf{Red}^{NW}
which makes a
single
query.

The weak design property can be used to show that for every
1\leq j\leq 2^{\ell}
,

\operatorname{Fix}_{j}(\overline{\mathsf{Con}}^{NW})\leq a+j.

Loosely speaking, this is because the NW proof shows that for every
i
, after fixing
a
bits of information about
f
, we have that for every value of
x|_{[r]\setminus S_{i}}
, all
m-1
output bits of the form
f(x|_{S_{i^{\prime}}})
for
i^{\prime}\neq i
are completely determined as a function of
x|_{S_{i}}
. This means that for every value of
x|_{S_{i}}
, the only output bit that is not yet fixed is
f(x|_{S_{i}})
, which can be fixed paying only

one bit of information
.

If we were to beat the hybrid argument, and construct a new black-box
\mathsf{hard}\mbox{-}\mathsf{function}\Rightarrow\mathsf{PRG}
proof that works directly from worst-case hard functions, we would have to come close to this behavior, and we hope that understanding this property may point us to new constructions, potentially utilizing the ability to ask a small number of queries (but more than just one query).

The Shaltiel-Umans PRG.

The Shaltiel-Umans PRG is a black-box
\mathsf{hard}\mbox{-}\mathsf{function}\Rightarrow\mathsf{PRG}
proof
(\mathsf{Con}^{SU},\mathsf{Red}^{SU})
with parameters
\ell,r,m,a,\rho=1
and
\epsilon=\frac{1}{10}
, such that
\mathsf{Red}
makes
q=m^{O(1)}
queries.

Unlike the Nisan-Wigderson PRG, the Shaltiel-Umans PRG is stated for
\rho=1
(starting from worst-case hard functions). Nevertheless, the Shaltiel-Umans PRG builds on the hardness amplification techniques of Sudan, Trevisan and Vadhan
[
STV01
]
and makes
q\geq m
queries. Furthermore, it implies hardness amplification to
\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{m}
. This means that it is not applicable at the extreme high-end.

By Theorem
3.1
, we have that:

\operatorname{Fix}_{j}(\mathsf{Con}^{SU})\leq a+j\cdot(O(\log m)),

for many values of
m
and
j
.

Let us review how
\mathsf{Con}^{SU}
achieves this. The construction
\mathsf{Con}^{SU}_{f}
relies on (a carefully chosen) black-box
\mathsf{hard}\mbox{-}\mathsf{function}\Rightarrow{(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{m})}\mbox{-}\mathsf{hard}\mbox{-}\mathsf{function}
proof (which has additional structure). This hardness amplification construction converts the worst-case hard function
f:\{0,1\}^{\ell}\to\{0,1\}
into an average case hard function
f^{\prime}:\{0,1\}^{\ell^{\prime}}\to\{0,1\}
.

The PRG construction is then defined by setting
r=\ell^{\prime}
and defining:

\mathsf{Con}^{SU}_{f}(x)=f^{\prime}(x),f^{\prime}(g(x)),f^{\prime}(g(g(x))),\ldots,f^{\prime}(g^{(m-1)}(x),

where
g:\{0,1\}^{\ell^{\prime}}\to\{0,1\}^{\ell^{\prime}}
is some specific function. Another version that is often considered is a “seed extending” version
\overline{\mathsf{Con}}^{SU}_{f}(x)=x,\mathsf{Con}^{SU}_{f}(x)
that outputs
m+r
bits.

This means that for every seed
x\in\{0,1\}^{\ell^{\prime}}
if we consider the
\ell
“consecutive” seeds,

g(x),g(g(x)),\ldots,g^{(m-1)}(x),

all the outputs of
\mathsf{Con}^{SU}_{f}
on these seeds, depend only on the values of
f^{\prime}
on
g(x),g(g(x)),\ldots,g^{(2m)}(x)
. This means that
m
outputs of
\mathsf{Con}^{SU}_{f}
can be fixed at the cost of fixing
2m
bits of
f^{\prime}
. In particular, for
j=a
we get that
j=a
outputs of
\mathsf{Con}_{f}
can be fixed at the total cost of
2a=a+j\cdot 1
bits of information about
f
. This means that after paying a fixed cost of
a
,
a
outputs can be fixed at amortized cost of one bit of information per output. This can be used to show that:

\operatorname{Fix}_{a}(\overline{\mathsf{Con}}^{SU})\leq a+a\cdot 1.

Again, if we were to beat the hybrid argument, and construct a new black-box
\mathsf{hard}\mbox{-}\mathsf{function}\Rightarrow\mathsf{PRG}
proof that works directly from worst-case hard functions, we may hope to take this understanding, hoping to somehow avoid paying the price of hardness amplification to extremely hard on average functions.

A\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{\ell,1}
if

\Pr_{F\leftarrow A}[D_{F}(z)=1]\geq 2^{-t}.

We will now consider an iterative process in which we will iteratively fix outputs
z
of
\mathsf{Con}
that are weak.

More precisely,
we set
t=\log\frac{4\cdot q}{\eta}
and consider the following iterative process. We start with the set
A_{0}
that we already obtained, and
W_{0}=\emptyset
. We will maintain the following invariant:

Invariant:

At step
j
we maintain that:

1.

\Pr_{F\leftarrow\mathcal{F}_{\ell,1}}[F\in A_{j}]\geq 2^{-(a+jt)}
.

2.

A_{j}\subseteq A_{0}
.

3.

|W_{j}|=j
.

4.

For every
z\in W_{j}
and every
f\in A_{j}
,
D_{f}(z)=1
.

Note that this invariant indeed holds for
j=0
.
At step
0<j<j_{\max}
we do the following:
If there does not exist a
z\not\in W_{j-1}
that is
t
-weak with respect to
A_{j-1}
then the process stops. Otherwise, if there exists
z\not\in W_{j-1}
that is
t
-weak with respect to
A_{j-1}
, we define:

•

W_{j}=W_{j-1}\cup\left\{{z}\right\}
.

•

A_{j}=\left\{{f\in A_{j-1}:D_{f}(z)=1}\right\}
.

We observe that the invariant is indeed kept throughout this process.

Claim 3.15
.

For every
j\leq j_{max}
for which the process has not yet stopped, the invariant above holds.

Proof.

This is obvious for the second, third and fourth items. The first item follows because for every
j
if the process did not stop before step
j
, then
A_{j}
exists, and we have that:

\displaystyle\Pr_{F\leftarrow\mathcal{F}_{\ell,1}}[F\in A_{j}]

\displaystyle=\Pr_{F\leftarrow\mathcal{F}_{\ell,1}}[F\in A_{j}|F\in A_{j-1}]\cdot\Pr_{F\leftarrow\mathcal{F}_{\ell,1}}[F\in A_{j-1}]

\displaystyle\geq\Pr_{F\leftarrow A_{j-1}}[F\in A_{j}]\cdot 2^{-(a+(j-1)\cdot t)}

\displaystyle\geq 2^{-t}\cdot 2^{-(a+(j-1)\cdot t)}

\displaystyle\geq 2^{-(a+j\cdot t)},

where the third line is using
t
-weakness.
∎

Claim 3.16
.

If the process stops at some
j^{*}<j_{max}
, then there exists
A\subseteq A_{0}
such that:

1.

\Pr_{F\leftarrow\mathcal{F}_{\ell,1}}[F\in A]\geq 2^{-(a+j^{*}\cdot t)+1}
.

2.

\Pr_{F\leftarrow A,X\in\{0,1\}^{\ell}}[C(X)=F(X)]\geq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\eta}{2}
.

Proof.

If the process stopped at some
j^{*}<j_{max}
, then there does not exist a
z\not\in W_{j^{*}}
that is
t
-weak with respect to
A_{j^{*}}
. This in particular means that for every
x\in\{0,1\}^{\ell}
,
z^{x}_{1},\ldots,z^{x}_{q}
are not
t
-weak with respect to
A_{j^{*}}
and therefore, by a union bound:

\Pr_{F\leftarrow A_{j^{*}}}[\exists i\in[q]:D_{F}(z^{x}_{i})=1]\leq q\cdot 2^{-t}\leq\frac{\eta}{4},

which means that:

\Pr_{F\leftarrow A_{j^{*}}}[\forall i\in[q]:D_{F}(z^{x}_{i})=0]\geq 1-\frac{\eta}{4},

by Claim
3.13
we conclude that for every
x\in\{0,1\}^{\ell}
,

\Pr_{F\leftarrow A_{j^{*}}}[C(x)=\mathsf{Red}^{D_{F}}(x,\alpha^{\prime})]\geq 1-\frac{\eta}{4}.

For every
x\in\{0,1\}^{\ell}
, let
V_{x}
denote the random variable (over the probability space of
F\leftarrow A_{j^{*}}
) defined by:

V_{x}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1,&C(x)\neq\mathsf{Red}^{D_{F}}(x,\alpha^{\prime})\\
0,&\mbox{otherwise}\end{array}\right.

Let
V=\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{\ell}}V_{x}
. It follows that
\Exp_{F\leftarrow A_{j^{*}}}[V]\leq 2^{\ell}\cdot\frac{\eta}{4}
. By Markov’s inequality we have that:

\Pr_{F\leftarrow A_{j^{*}}}[V>2^{\ell}\cdot\frac{\eta}{2}]<\frac{1}{2}.

Let
A=\left\{{f\in A_{j^{*}}:V\leq 2^{\ell}\cdot\frac{\eta}{2}}\right\}
. It follows that:

\Pr_{F\leftarrow\mathcal{F}_{\ell,1}}[F\in A]\geq\frac{1}{2}\cdot\Pr_{F\leftarrow\mathcal{F}_{\ell,1}}[F\in A_{j^{*}}]\geq 2^{-(a+j^{*}\cdot t)+1}.

For every
f\in A
, we have that the fraction of inputs
x\in\{0,1\}^{\ell}
on which
V_{x}(f)=1
(meaning that
C(x)\neq\mathsf{Red}^{D_{f}}(x,\alpha^{\prime})
) is
\frac{V(f)}{2^{\ell}}\leq\frac{\eta}{2}
. This means that when we choose both
F\leftarrow A
and
X\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{\ell}
independently, we have that:

\Pr_{F\leftarrow A,X\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{\ell}}[C(X)\neq\mathsf{Red}^{D_{F}}(X,\alpha^{\prime})]\leq\frac{\eta}{2}.

On the other hand, as
A\subseteq A_{j^{*}}\subseteq A_{0}
, by Claim
3.11
we have that for every
f\in A
, the function
C_{f}:\{0,1\}^{\ell}\to\{0,1\}
defined by
C_{f}(x)=\mathsf{Red}^{D_{f}}(x,\alpha^{\prime})
, satisfies that
C_{f}

\rho
-
\mathsf{hard}\mbox{-}\mathsf{function}\mbox{-}\mathsf{breaks}
the function
f
. This means that for every
f\in A
,

\Pr_{X\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{\ell}}[\mathsf{Red}^{D_{f}}(X,\alpha^{\prime})=f(X)]\geq\rho.

This means that when we choose both
F\leftarrow A
and
X\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{\ell}
independently, we have that:

\Pr_{F\leftarrow A,X\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{\ell}}[\mathsf{Red}^{D_{F}}(X,\alpha^{\prime})=F(X)]\geq\rho.

Putting things together, we have that:

\Pr_{F\leftarrow A,X\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{\ell}}[C(X)=F(X)]\geq\rho-\frac{\eta}{2}=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\eta}{2}.

∎

However, the next claim shows that if
A
is a large set, it is unlikely that a single function
C
is a good approximation to
F\leftarrow A
.

Claim 3.17
.

For every
A\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{\ell,1}
, and every function
C:\{0,1\}^{\ell}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
, if

\Pr_{F\leftarrow\mathcal{F}_{\ell,1}}[F\in A]\geq 2^{-\Delta}
then

\Pr_{F\leftarrow A,X\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{\ell}}[C(X)=F(X)]=\frac{1}{2}+O\left(\sqrt{\frac{\Delta+\ell}{2^{\ell}}}\right).

Proof.

For the purpose of contradiction we will set
\lambda=\Omega\left(\sqrt{\frac{\Delta+\ell}{2^{\ell}}}\right)
, and assume that

\Pr_{F\leftarrow A,X\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{\ell}}[C(X)=F(X)]\geq\frac{1}{2}+\lambda.

By an averaging argument, it follows that:

\Pr_{F\leftarrow A}\left[\Pr_{X\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{\ell}}[C(X)=F(X)]\geq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\lambda}{2}\right]\geq\frac{\lambda}{2}.

Let
A^{\prime}\subseteq A
be the subset defined as follows:

A^{\prime}=\left\{{f\in A:\Pr_{X\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{\ell}}[C(X)=f(X)]\geq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\lambda}{2}}\right\}.

It follows that
|A^{\prime}|\geq\frac{\lambda}{2}\cdot|A|
.
For every function
C\in\mathcal{F}_{\ell,1}
, the number of
f\in\mathcal{F}_{\ell,1}
such that
\Pr_{X\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{\ell}}[C(X)=f(X)]\geq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\lambda}{2}
is bounded by the size of a Hamming ball in
\{0,1\}^{2^{\ell}}
that is of radius
(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\lambda}{2})\cdot 2^{\ell}
. The latter quantity is bounded by
2^{H(\frac{1}{2}-\lambda/2)\cdot 2^{\ell}}\leq 2^{(1-O(\lambda^{2}))\cdot 2^{\ell}}
where
H(\cdot)
is Shannon’s binary entropy function, and using the fact that
H(\frac{1}{2}-\lambda)=1-O(\lambda^{2})
.
We conclude that there exists a constant
c>1
such that:

|A|\leq\frac{2}{\lambda}\cdot|A^{\prime}|\leq\frac{2}{\lambda}\cdot 2^{(1-c\cdot\lambda^{2})\cdot 2^{\ell}}=2^{\log(\frac{1}{\lambda})+1+(1-c\cdot\lambda^{2})\cdot 2^{\ell}}=2^{2^{\ell}-(c\cdot\lambda^{2}\cdot 2^{\ell}-\log(1/\lambda)-1)}.

We also have that
|A|\geq 2^{2^{\ell}-\Delta}
, and so we conclude that
\Delta>c\cdot\lambda^{2}\cdot 2^{\ell}-\log(1/\lambda)-1
. This gives a contradiction if
\lambda=\Omega\left(\sqrt{\frac{\Delta+\ell}{2^{\ell}}}\right)
.
∎

Putting Claim
3.16
and Claim
3.17
together, we get that:

Claim 3.18
.

The iterative process does not stop until
j=j_{\max}
.

Proof.

By Claim
3.16
and Claim
3.17
, we conclude that if the process stops at some
j^{*}<j_{max}
then there exists a constant
c>1
such that:

\frac{1}{2}+c\cdot\sqrt{\frac{a+j^{*}\cdot t+\ell}{2^{\ell}}}\geq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\eta}{2},

Recalling that
t=\log\frac{4q}{\eta}
this gives that:

\displaystyle q

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\eta}{4}\cdot 2^{\frac{\frac{2^{\ell}\cdot\eta^{2}}{4c}-a-\ell}{j^{*}}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\eta}{4}\cdot 2^{\frac{\frac{2^{\ell}\cdot\eta^{2}}{5c}}{j^{*}}}

\displaystyle>2^{\ell},

where the first inequality follows because we have that
a\leq\nu\cdot\eta^{2}\cdot 2^{\ell}
, and we can choose the constant
\nu>0
to be sufficiently small. The second inequality follows because
\eta\geq 2^{-\ell}
, and
j^{*}\leq j_{\max}\leq\nu\cdot\frac{\eta^{2}\cdot 2^{\ell}}{\ell}
, and we can choose
\nu>0
to be sufficiently small.

Therefore, as we are assuming that
q\leq 2^{\ell}
, it is a contradiction if the process stops at some
j^{*}<j_{\max}
.
∎

As the process did not stop at any
j<j_{\max}
, then for every
j\leq j_{\max}
, the sets
A_{j},W_{j}
are defined, and by Claim
3.15
they maintain the invariant.

By the invariant, for every
j\leq j_{\max}
we conclude that for every
z\in W_{j}
and every
f\in A_{j}
,
D_{f}(z)=1
(meaning that there exists
y\in\{0,1\}^{r}
such that
z
is an output of
\mathsf{Con}_{f})
. By the invariant, we know that
|W_{j}|=j
therefore, if we denote the elements of
W_{j}
by
z_{1},\ldots,z_{j}\in\{0,1\}^{m}
we conclude that
for every
f\in A_{j}
, and every
i\in[j]
there exists
y_{i}\in\{0,1\}^{r}
(that may depend on
f
) such that
\mathsf{Con}_{f}(y_{i})=z_{i}
. From the invariant, we also have that:

\Pr_{F\leftarrow\mathcal{F}_{\ell,1}}[F\in A_{j}]\geq 2^{-(a+j\cdot t)}=2^{-(a+j\cdot(\log q+\log\frac{4}{\eta}))}.

Putting everything together we get that:

\Pr_{F\leftarrow\mathcal{F}_{\ell,1}}[\forall i\in[j]:\exists y_{i}\in\{0,1\}^{r}\mbox{ s.t. }\mathsf{Con}_{F}(y_{i})=z_{i}]\geq 2^{-(a+j\cdot(\log q+\log\frac{4}{\eta}))},

which gives that for every
j\leq j_{\max}
,
\operatorname{Fix}_{j}(\mathsf{Con})\leq a+j\cdot(\log q+\log\frac{4}{\eta})
. This proves Theorem
3.9
.

4
Limitations on black-box hardness amplification at the extreme high-end

In this section we prove Theorem
1.15
showing that reductions for black-box
\mathsf{hard}\mbox{-}\mathsf{function}\Rightarrow{(\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon)}\mbox{-}\mathsf{hard}\mbox{-}\mathsf{function}
proofs must make many queries, even at the extreme high-end.
Theorem
1.15
is a combination of two lower bounds, stated next:

Theorem 4.1

(Lower bound in terms of
\epsilon
)
.

If
(\mathsf{Con},\mathsf{Red})
is a
\mathsf{hard}\mbox{-}\mathsf{function}\Rightarrow{(\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon)}\mbox{-}\mathsf{hard}\mbox{-}\mathsf{function}
proof
with parameters
\ell,\ell^{\prime},a,\rho=1,\rho^{\prime}=\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon
, satisfying
a\leq\frac{2^{\ell}}{10}
and
\ell^{\prime}\geq\log\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}+\Omega(1)
then
\mathsf{Red}
must make at least
q=\Omega(\frac{1}{\epsilon})
queries.

Theorem 4.2

(Lower bound in terms of
\ell
)
.

If
(\mathsf{Con},\mathsf{Red})
is a
\mathsf{hard}\mbox{-}\mathsf{function}\Rightarrow{(\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon)}\mbox{-}\mathsf{hard}\mbox{-}\mathsf{function}
proof
with parameters
\ell,\ell^{\prime},a,\rho=1,\rho^{\prime}=\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon
, satisfying
\epsilon\leq\frac{1}{10}
, and
\ell^{\prime}\geq\log\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}+\Omega(1)
then
\mathsf{Red}
must make at least
q\geq\frac{\ell-\log(2a)}{3}
queries

A quantitatively better lower bound of
q=\Omega(\frac{\ell}{\epsilon^{2}})
was proven by Grinberg, Shaltiel and Viola
[
GSV18
]
for the case that
a\leq 2^{\nu\cdot\ell}
for some constant
\nu>0
. Theorems
4.1
and Theorem
4.2
achieve a smaller bound on
q
, but apply in the extreme high-end (where
a=2^{(1-o(1))\cdot\ell}
) all the way up to
a=\frac{2^{\ell}}{10}
. This is especially significant in the case of Theorem
4.1
which (as we explained in detail in Section
1.3.4
) can be used to show limitations on the PRG composition of Chen and Tell
[
CT21b
]
.

Roadmap for this section.

Both Theorem
4.1
and Theorem
4.2
will be proven by first connecting a black-box reduction to a depth 3 circuit for a version of the “coin problem”. This connection is stated and proven in Section
4.1
.
The proofs of Theorem
4.1
and Theorem
4.2
show that such a depth 3 circuit must have large
q
. These proofs are given in Section
4.2
and Section
4.3
.

4.1
Reductions as depth 3 circuits

The proof of Theorem
4.1
and Theorem
4.2
rely on the following lemma, which is inspired by an argument of Applebaum et al.
[
AASY16
]
(see also
[
RSV21
]
) and relates black-box reductions to constant depth circuits.

Lemma 4.3

(Reduction to circuit)
.

Let
(\mathsf{Con},\mathsf{Red})
be a black-box
\mathsf{hard}\mbox{-}\mathsf{function}\Rightarrow{(\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon)}\mbox{-}\mathsf{hard}\mbox{-}\mathsf{function}
proof with parameters

\ell,\ell^{\prime},a,\rho=1,\rho^{\prime}=\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon
, such that
\mathsf{Red}
makes
q
queries.
For every
\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{a}
, there exists a
q
-CNF
C^{\alpha}
with
2^{q+\ell}
clauses, over
n=2^{\ell^{\prime}}
variables, such that for
C=\bigvee_{\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{a}}C^{\alpha}
, we have that:

•

\Pr[C(U_{n})]\leq 2^{-(2^{\ell}-a)}
.

•

For every
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
such that
\operatorname{weight}(x)\leq(\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon)\cdot n
,
C(x)=1
.

Proof.

We will view a string
z
of length
n=2^{\ell^{\prime}}
, as a function
z\in\mathcal{F}_{\ell^{\prime},1}
and vice-versa, by
z(y)=z_{y}
.
The proof of
[
AASY16
]
(and the proof presented here) makes use of an idea originating in
[
Vio06
,
SV10
]
(and credited to Madhu Sudan) that the reduction must succeed if given oracle access to
\mathsf{Con}_{f}\oplus z
for a string
z
with
\operatorname{weight}(z)\leq n\cdot(\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon)
(as such an oracle
(\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon)
-
\mathsf{hard}\mbox{-}\mathsf{function}\mbox{-}\mathsf{breaks}

\mathsf{Con}_{f}
), but cannot succeed when given oracle access to
\mathsf{Con}_{f}\oplus z
for
z\leftarrow U_{n}
(as
z
“wipes out” the information in
\mathsf{Con}_{f}
). This will translate into the two conditions in the lemma. Details follow:

For every
f\in\mathcal{F}_{\ell,1}
,
\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{a}
and
x\in\{0,1\}^{\ell}
, we define
C_{f,\alpha,x}:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
as follows:

C_{f,\alpha,x}(z)=1\mbox{ iff $\mathsf{Red}^{\mathsf{Con}_{f}\oplus z}(x,\alpha)=f(x)$.}

We have that
\mathsf{Red}
makes at most
q
queries, and this implies that for every
f
and
\alpha
, the function
C_{f,\alpha}
can be computed by a decision tree with height
q
. Therefore, it can be computed by a
q
-CNF with
2^{q}
clauses.
For every
f\in\mathcal{F}_{\ell,1}
, and
\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{a}
we define
C_{f,\alpha}:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
as follows:

C_{f,\alpha}(z)=1\mbox{ iff $\forall x\in\{0,1\}^{\ell}$, $\mathsf{Red}^{\mathsf{Con}_{f}\oplus z}(x,\alpha)=f(x)$.}

By definition for every
f
and
\alpha
,
C_{f,\alpha}(x)=\bigwedge_{x\in\{0,1\}^{\ell}}C_{f,\alpha,x}
. This means that
C_{f,\alpha}
is an AND of
2^{\ell}

q
-CNFs with
2^{q}
clauses, and overall, it can be computed by a
q
-CNF with
2^{q+\ell}
clauses.
For every
f\in\mathcal{F}_{\ell,1}
, we define
C_{f}:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
as follows:

C_{f,\alpha}(z)=1\mbox{ iff $\exists\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{a}$, s.t. $\forall x\in\{0,1\}^{\ell}$, $\mathsf{Red}^{\mathsf{Con}_{f}\oplus z}(x,\alpha)=f(x)$.}

This gives that for every
f\in\mathcal{F}_{\ell,1}
,
C_{f}
is an OR of
2^{a}

q
-CNFs with
2^{q+\ell}
clauses.

Furthermore, by the definition of black-box
\mathsf{hard}\mbox{-}\mathsf{function}\Rightarrow{(\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon)}\mbox{-}\mathsf{hard}\mbox{-}\mathsf{function}
proof, for every
f\in\mathcal{F}_{\ell,1}
, if
\operatorname{weight}(z)\leq(\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon)\cdot n
, then the function
\mathsf{Con}_{f}\oplus z
,
\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon
-
\mathsf{hard}\mbox{-}\mathsf{function}\mbox{-}\mathsf{breaks}
breaks
\mathsf{Con}_{f}
. This in turn implies (by Definition
1.7
) that there exists
\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{a}
such that for every
x\in\{0,1\}^{\ell}
,
\mathsf{Red}^{\mathsf{Con}_{f}\oplus z}(x)=f(x)
, meaning that
C_{f}(z)=1
. This means that for every choice of
f\in\mathcal{F}_{\ell,1}
,
C_{f}
satisfies the second item.

We will now show that there exists an
f\in\mathcal{F}_{\ell,1}
such that
C_{f}
satisfies the first item.
For a uniformly chosen
z\leftarrow U_{n}
, we have that for every
f\in\mathcal{F}_{\ell,1}
,
\mathsf{Con}_{f}\oplus z
is distributed uniformly over
\{0,1\}^{n}
, and contains no information about
f
. It follows that:

\displaystyle\Pr_{f\leftarrow\mathcal{F}_{\ell,1},z\leftarrow U_{n}}[C_{f}(z)=1]

\displaystyle=\Pr_{F\leftarrow\mathcal{F}_{\ell,1},z\leftarrow U_{n}}[\mbox{$\exists\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{a}$, s.t. $\forall x\in\{0,1\}^{\ell}$, $\mathsf{Red}^{\mathsf{Con}_{f}\oplus z}(x,\alpha)=f(x)$}]

\displaystyle=\Pr_{f\leftarrow\mathcal{F}_{\ell,1},z\leftarrow U_{n}}[\mbox{$\exists\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{a}$, s.t. $\forall x\in\{0,1\}^{\ell}$, $\mathsf{Red}^{z}(x,\alpha)=f(x)$}]

\displaystyle\leq Pr_{f\leftarrow\mathcal{F}_{\ell,1}}[\mbox{$\exists\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{a}$, s.t. $\forall x\in\{0,1\}^{\ell}$, $\mathsf{Red}^{z^{*}}(x,\alpha)=f(x)$}],

for some
z^{*}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
which maximizes the success probability. We can therefore continue and obtain that:

\displaystyle\Pr_{f\leftarrow\mathcal{F}_{\ell,1},z\leftarrow U_{n}}[C_{f}(z)=1]

\displaystyle\leq\Pr_{F\leftarrow\mathcal{F}_{\ell,1}}[\mbox{$\exists\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{a}$, s.t. $\forall x\in\{0,1\}^{\ell}$, $\mathsf{Red}^{z^{*}}(x,\alpha)=f(x)$},]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{a}}\Pr_{f\leftarrow\mathcal{F}_{\ell,1}}[\mbox{$\forall x\in\{0,1\}^{\ell}$, $\mathsf{Red}^{z^{*}}(x,\alpha)=f(x)$}]

\displaystyle\leq 2^{a}\cdot\frac{1}{2^{2^{\ell}}},

where the penultimate inequality follows by a union bound, and the last inequality follows because for every
x\in\{0,1\}^{\ell}
,

\Pr_{f\leftarrow\mathcal{F}_{\ell,1}}[\mathsf{Red}^{z^{*}}(x,\alpha)=f(x)]=\frac{1}{2},

and these events are independent for the
2^{\ell}
choices of
x\in\{0,1\}^{\ell}
.
Finally, by averaging, we conclude that there exists
f\in\mathcal{F}_{\ell,1}
such that:

\Pr_{z\leftarrow U_{n}}[C_{f}(z)=1]\leq 2^{-(2^{\ell}-a)}.

The final function
C
will be this function
C_{f}
which indeed satisfies the properties in the conclusion of the lemma.
∎

When choosing

4.3.1
Proof of Lemma

In this section, we prove Lemma

. We first observe that if a

-CNF does not have many clauses that have disjoint variables, then there must be a small “cover” (namely, a set of variables such that each clause contains a variable from the cover).

Proposition 4.11
.

In every
v
-CNF

, if there does not exist
t
clauses with disjoint variables, then there is a subset
S
of
s=(t-1)\cdot v
variables, such that every clause of
D
contains a variable in
S
.

Proof.

We go over the clauses one by one, staring with an empty
S
. Every time the current clause does not contain a variable from
S
, we mark the current clause, and add its variables to
S
. Every time we mark a clause, its variables are disjoint from all variables of previously marked clauses. By our assumption, we mark at most
t-1
clauses, and so when we conclude the size of
S
is at most
(t-1)\cdot v
.
∎

Lemma 4.12
.

For every
v
-CNF
D
over
m
variables, if
\Pr[D(U^{1/3}_{m})=1]\geq 2^{-w}
then
there exists a set
S
of size
s=w\cdot 4^{v}
variables such that every clause of
D
contains at least one variable in
S
.

Proof.

If there does not exists a set
S
with the required properties, then by Proposition
4.11
,
D
has
t=\frac{s}{v}
clauses that have disjoint variables. Under
U^{1/3}_{m}
, each literal of
D
has probability at least
1/3
to evaluate to zero. This gives that each clause has probability at most
1-\left(\frac{1}{3}\right)^{v}
to evaluate to one. Consequently, the probability that
t

\left(1-\left(\frac{1}{3}\right)^{v}\right)^{t}
, implying that:

\Pr[D(U^{1/3}_{m})=1]\leq\left(1-\left(\frac{1}{3}\right)^{v}\right)^{t}\leq e^{-3^{-v}\cdot t}<e^{-4^{-v}\cdot s}\leq 2^{-w}.

and we get a contradiction.
∎

We are ready to prove Lemma
4.10
. By Lemma

there exists a set

of size

variables such that every clause of
D
contains at least one variable in
S
. Let

be the set of all restrictions that fix the variables in

A=\left\{{\rho:[m]\to\left\{{0,1,*}\right\}:\rho(i)\neq*\mbox{ iff }i\in S}\right\}.

We will show that there exists a
\rho\in A
that satisfies the guarantees of Lemma

with positive probability.

The first item of Lemma

holds for every
\rho\in A
. This is because every clause of

has a variable in

and following the restriction, each clause is over at most

The second item of Lemma

also holds for every
\rho\in A
. For every choice

of the restricted bits in
\rho
, let

denote the event
\left\{{X|_{[n]\setminus\operatorname{Select}(\rho)}=y}\right\}

we have that:

\displaystyle\Pr[D_{\rho}[D(U_{m^{\prime}})=1]

\displaystyle=\Pr_{X\leftarrow U_{m}}[D(X)=1|E_{\rho,y}]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\Pr_{X\leftarrow U_{m}}[D(X)=1]}{\Pr_{X\leftarrow U_{m}}[E_{\rho,y}]}

\displaystyle=\Pr[D(U_{m})=1]\cdot 2^{w\cdot 4^{v}}.

The third item follows because by averaging, there exists
\rho\in A
and

\Pr_{X\leftarrow U^{1/3}_{m}}[D(X)=1|E_{\rho,y}]\geq\Pr_{X\leftarrow U^{1/3}_{m}}[D(X)=1],

For this choice of

5
Improved lower bounds for local list-decoding algorithms

In this section we show that the techniques developed in Section
4
can be used to get an improved lower bounds on the number of queries of local list-decoding algorithms. In Section
5.1
we review the definition of locally list-decodable codes, and local decoding algorithms. In Section
5.2
we state our improved bound and compare it to previous work. In Section
5.3
we prove the improved bound.

5.1
Definition of locally list-decodable codes

List-decodable codes are a natural extension of (uniquely decodable) error-correcting codes, as it allows (list) decoding for error regimes where unique decoding is impossible. This is an extensively studied area; see
[
Gur06
]
for a survey. In this paper, we will be interested in list-decoding of binary codes.

Definition 5.1

(List-decodable code)
.

For a function
\operatorname{Enc}:\{0,1\}^{k}\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{n}
, and
w\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, we define

\operatorname{List}^{\operatorname{Enc}}_{\alpha}(w)=\left\{{m\in\{0,1\}^{k}:\mathsf{dist}(\operatorname{Enc}(m),w)\leq\alpha}\right\}.

We say that
\operatorname{Enc}
is
(\alpha,L)
-list-decodable if for every
w\in\{0,1\}^{n}
,
|\operatorname{List}^{\operatorname{Enc}}_{\alpha}(w)|\leq L
.

The task of
algorithmic
list-decoding is to produce the list
\operatorname{List}^{\operatorname{Enc}}_{\alpha}(w)
on input
w\in\{0,1\}^{n}
.

Local
unique decoding algorithms are algorithms that given an index
i\in[k]
, make few oracle queries to
w
, and reproduce the bit
m_{i}
(with high probability over the choice of their random coins), where
m
denotes the unique codeword close to
w
. This notion of
local decoding
has many connections and applications in computer science and mathematics
[
Yek12
]
.

We will be interested in
local
list-decoding algorithms
that receive oracle access to a received word
w\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, as well as inputs
i\in[k]
and
j\in[L]
.
We will require that for every
m\in\operatorname{List}^{\operatorname{Enc}}_{\alpha}(w)
, with high probability, there exists a
j\in[L]
such that for every
i\in[k]
, when
\operatorname{Dec}
receives oracle access to
w
and inputs
i,j
, it produces
m_{i}
with high probability over its choice of random coins. This motivates the next definition.

Definition 5.2

(Randomized local computation)
.

We say that a procedure
P(i,r)

locally computes
a string
m\in\{0,1\}^{k}
with error
\delta
, if for every
i\in[k]
,
\Pr[P(i,R)=m_{i}]\geq 1-\delta
(where the probability is over a uniform choice of the “string of random coins”
R
).

The definition of local list-decoders considers an algorithmic scenario that works in two steps:

•

At the first step (which can be thought of as a preprocessing step) the local list-decoder
\operatorname{Dec}
is given oracle access to
w
and an index
j\in[L]
. It tosses random coins (which we denote by
r^{\operatorname{shared}}
).

•

At the second step, the decoder receives the additional index
i\in[k]
, and tosses additional coins
r
.

•

It is required that for every
w\in\{0,1\}^{n}
and
m\in\operatorname{List}^{\operatorname{Enc}}_{\alpha}(w)
, with probability
2/3
over the choice of the shared coins
r^{\operatorname{shared}}
, there exists
j\in[L]
such that when the local list-decoder receives
j
, it locally computes
m
(using its “non-shared” coins
r
). The definition uses two types of random coins because the coins
r^{\operatorname{shared}}
are “shared” between different choices of
i\in[k]
and allow different
i
’s to “coordinate”. The coins
r
, are chosen independently for different choices of
i\in[k]
.

This is formally stated in the next definition.

Definition 5.3

(Local list-decoder)
.

Let
\operatorname{Enc}:\{0,1\}^{k}\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{n}
be a function. An
(\alpha,L,q,\delta)
-local list-decoder (LLD)
for
\operatorname{Enc}
is an oracle procedure
\operatorname{Dec}^{(\cdot)}
that receives oracle access to a word
w\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, and makes at most
q
calls to the oracle. The procedure
\operatorname{Dec}
also receives inputs:

•

i\in[k]
: The index of the symbol that it needs to decode.

•

j\in[L]
: An index to the list.

•

Two strings
r^{\operatorname{shared}},r
that are used as random coins.

It is required that for every
w\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, and for every
m\in\operatorname{List}^{\operatorname{Enc}}_{\alpha}(w)
, with probability at least
2/3
over choosing a uniform string
r^{\operatorname{shared}}
, there exists
j\in[L]
such that the procedure

P_{w,j,r^{\operatorname{shared}}}(i,r)=\operatorname{Dec}^{w}(i,j,r^{\operatorname{shared}},r)

locally computes
m
with error
\delta
. If we omit
\delta
, then we mean
\delta=1/3
.

See
[
RSV21
]
for a discussion on the generality of this definition, and on past work in this area.

5.2
Our Results

Ron-Zewi, Shaltiel and Varma
[
RSV21
]
showed lower bounds on the number of queries of local list decoders.
We use our improved techniques to prove the following theorem.

Theorem 5.4

(Improved lower bounds for small
\epsilon
)
.

There exist constants
c_{1},c_{2}>1
such that for every
L\leq 2^{\frac{k}{20}}
,
\delta<\frac{1}{3}
,
n\geq\frac{c_{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}
, and every
(\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon,L,q,\delta)
-local list-decoder for
\operatorname{Enc}:\{0,1\}^{k}\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{n}
, we have that
q\geq\frac{1}{c_{1}\cdot\log(k)\cdot\epsilon}
.

The previous bounds of
[
RSV21
]
come in two forms:

•

For
L\leq 2^{k^{0.9}}
and
\epsilon\geq\frac{1}{k^{\Omega(1)}}
,
[
RSV21
]
obtain tight bounds (up to constants), showing that:
q=\Omega(\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\epsilon^{2}})
.

•

If these conditions are not met (and in particular, if
\epsilon
is smaller than
1/k
),
[
RSV21
]
obtain weaker bounds, showing that if
\delta<\frac{1}{3}
,
q=\Omega(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\epsilon}\cdot\log k})-O(\log L)
.

Theorem
5.4
improves upon the second item above (although it does not match the optimal bound of the first item). More specifically, Theorem
5.4
replaces
\sqrt{\epsilon}
with
\epsilon
, and does not have the additive term of “
-O(\log L)
”.

Not surprisingly, these improvements directly correspond to “the extreme high-end”. Making this analogy, (namely, setting
L=2^{a}
, and
k=2^{\ell}
) we have that for
L=2^{k^{1-o(1)}}
, previous work does not give any bound if
\epsilon\geq\frac{1}{k^{1.9}}
, and in particular for
\epsilon\approx\frac{1}{k}
.
In contrast, Theorem
5.4
gives a bound that is
\Omega(\frac{1}{\log k\cdot\epsilon})
, which is not far from the known upper bound of
O(\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}})
, and is polynomially related to the upper bound for
\epsilon\leq\frac{1}{\log^{2}k}
.

5.3
Proof of Theorem
5.4

In this section we prove Theorem
5.4
. The proof uses a similar approach as that of
[
RSV21
]
(which is in turn based on
[
AASY16
]
) to transform an LLD into a depth 3 circuit for a certain problem. (We make stronger requirements on the circuit, and therefore, need to redo the reduction, taking care to obtain these stronger requirements). Once we obtain a depth 3 circuit, we use Lemma
4.5
to show improved lower bounds. Details follow:

We assume that
L\leq 2^{\frac{k}{20}}
, and
n\geq\frac{c_{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}
.
Our goal is to prove lower bounds on the number of queries
q
of
(\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon,L,q,\delta)
-local list-decoders for
\delta<\frac{1}{3}
. It is possible to amplify the error probability
\delta
from

to

as follows: After choosing the random string

, we choose

independent uniform strings

\operatorname{Dec}^{(\cdot)}(i,j,r_{\ell},r^{\operatorname{shared}})
for all choices of

. We then output the majority vote of the individual

(\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon,q^{\prime}=O(q\cdot\log k),L,1/20k)
-LLD for
\operatorname{Enc}:\{0,1\}^{k}\to\{0,1\}^{n}
.

We plan to reduce to Lemma
4.5
, and set
p=\frac{\epsilon}{10}
(as in that lemma).
Our next step is to fix the random coins of the decoder, and obtain a
deterministic
decoder
\overline{Dec}
which succeeds in a specific experiment in which: a message
m\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{k}
is chosen uniformly, and a “noise string”

is chosen by choosing
\rho\leftarrow\mathsf{R}^{n}_{p}
and filling the unrestricted variables with
U^{1/3}_{p\cdot n}
. Finally, a “received word”

is obtained by

. This is defined formally below:

Definition 5.5

(The experiment
\operatorname{RNSY}
)
.

We consider the following experiment (which we denote by
\operatorname{RNSY}
): The experiment works in two steps. The first step (denoted
\operatorname{RNSY}^{1}
) works as follows:

•

A message
m\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{k}
is chosen uniformly.

•

A restriction
\rho\leftarrow R^{n}_{p}
is chosen uniformly.

The second step is defined for a fixed
m\in\{0,1\}^{k}
and
\rho\in R^{n}_{p}
. It is denoted by
\operatorname{RNSY}^{2}(m,\rho)
and works as follows:

•

A string
z
is chosen from
U^{1/3}_{p\cdot n}
.

•

We define
x=\operatorname{Fill}_{\rho}(z)
.

•

We define
w=\operatorname{Enc}(m)\oplus x
.

We use
(m,\rho,z,w)\leftarrow\operatorname{RNSY}
to denote
m,\rho,z,w
which are sampled by the two steps of this experiment.

The next lemma fixes the coins of the local-decoder, making it deterministic. It is similar in spirit to Proposition 3.1 in
[
RSV21
]
, except that the experiment
\operatorname{RNSY}
that we use is different and more complicated than the one used in
[
RSV21
]
.

Lemma 5.6
.

There exists a constant
c>1
such that if
n\geq\frac{c}{\epsilon^{2}}
and
\operatorname{Dec}
is a

(\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon,L,q,\delta)
-local list-decoder for a function
\operatorname{Enc}:\{0,1\}^{k}\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{n}
then there exists an oracle procedure
\overline{Dec}^{(\cdot)}(i,j)
receiving
i\in[k]
,
j\in[L]
, and making
q
queries to a string
w\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, such that with probability at least
0.51
over choosing
(m,\rho)\leftarrow\operatorname{RNSY}^{1}
, we have that with probability at least
0.01
over choosing
(z,w)\leftarrow\operatorname{RNSY}^{2}(m,\rho)
,
there exists
j\in[L]
such that

\Pr_{i\leftarrow[k]}[\overline{Dec}^{w}(i,j)=m_{i}]\geq 1-10\cdot\delta.

Proof.

Let
\operatorname{Dec}
denote an LLD for
\operatorname{Enc}
.
When choosing
\rho\leftarrow\mathsf{R}^{n}_{p}
we expect that
\frac{1}{n}\cdot|\left\{{i:\rho(i)=1}\right\}|=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{p}{2}
. Therefore, by a Chernoff bound:

\Pr_{\rho\leftarrow\mathsf{R}^{n}_{p}}[\frac{1}{n}\cdot|\left\{{i:\rho(i)=1}\right\}|\geq\frac{1}{2}-0.49\cdot p]\cdot\leq 2^{-\Omega(p^{2}\cdot n)},

which is smaller than
0.01
, by our assumption that
n
is sufficiently larger than
1/\epsilon^{2}
.
If this event occurs, then for every
z\in\{0,1\}^{n\cdot p}
,

\operatorname{weight}(\operatorname{Fill}_{\rho}(z))\leq(\frac{1}{2}-0.49\cdot p)\cdot n+\operatorname{weight}(z).

This means that if
\operatorname{weight}(z)\leq 0.34\cdot p\cdot n
, then

\operatorname{weight}(\operatorname{Fill}_{\rho}(z))\leq n\cdot(\frac{1}{2}-(0.49-0.34)\cdot p)\leq n\cdot(\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon),

By our assumption that
p\leq\frac{\epsilon}{10}
. Applying a Chernoff bound, we conclude that:

\Pr_{z\leftarrow U^{1/3}_{n\cdot p}}[\operatorname{weight}(z)\geq 0.34\cdot p\cdot n]\leq 2^{-\Omega(pn)},

which is smaller than
0.01
by the assumption that
n
is sufficiently larger than
1/\epsilon^{2}
.

Overall, this means that for
\gamma=0.02
, with probability at least
1-\gamma
over choosing
(m,\rho,z,w)\leftarrow\operatorname{RNSY}
, we have that
\mathsf{dist}(\operatorname{Enc}(m),w)\leq\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon
, meaning that
m\in\operatorname{List}^{\operatorname{Enc}}_{\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon}(w)
. By the definition of LLD, this gives that whenever this occurs, with probability at least
2/3
over the choice of
r^{\operatorname{shared}}
, there exists
j\in[L]
such that the procedure
P_{w,j,r^{\operatorname{shared}}}(i,r)=\operatorname{Dec}^{w}(i,j,r^{\operatorname{shared}},r)
locally computes
m
with error
\delta
.

Let
E_{1}
be the experiment in which
(m,\rho,z,w)\leftarrow\operatorname{RNSY}
and
r^{\operatorname{shared}}
be an independent uniform string. It follows that:

\Pr_{E_{1}}[\exists j\in[L]:\mbox{$P_{w,j,r^{shared}}$ locally computes $m$ with error $\delta$}]\geq\frac{2}{3}-\gamma.

By averaging, there exists a fixed string
\hat{r}^{\operatorname{shared}}
such that:

\Pr_{\operatorname{RNSY}}[\exists j\in[L]:\mbox{$P_{w,j,\hat{r}^{shared}}$ locally computes $m$ with error $\delta$}]\geq\frac{2}{3}-\gamma.

Let
S
denote the set of quadruples
(m,\rho,z,w)
in the support of
\operatorname{RNSY}
for which the event above occurs. For every such quadruple, we have that there exists a
j\in[L]
for which
P_{w,j,\hat{r}^{shared}}
locally computes
m
with error
\delta
. Let
f
be a mapping that given a quadruple
(m,\rho,z,w)\in S
, produces such a
j\in[L]
. This means that:

\Pr_{\operatorname{RNSY}}[\mbox{$P_{w,f(m,\rho,z,w),\hat{r}^{shared}}$ locally computes $m$ with error $\delta$}]\geq\frac{2}{3}-\gamma.

Let
\operatorname{RNSY}^{\prime}
be the experiment in which
(m,\rho,z,w)\leftarrow(\operatorname{RNSY}|(m,\rho,z,w)\in S)
. Namely, we choose
(m,\rho,z,w)
from the experiment
\operatorname{RNSY}
, conditioned on the event that
(m,\rho,z,w)\in S
.

Let
E_{2}
be the experiment in which we choose independently a random string
r
,
i\leftarrow[k]
and
(m,\rho,z,w)\leftarrow\operatorname{RNSY}^{\prime}
.
We obtain that:

\Pr_{E_{2}}[\operatorname{Dec}^{w}(i,f(m,\rho,z,w),\hat{r}^{shared},r)=m_{i}]\geq 1-\delta,

since
P_{w,f(m,\rho,z,w),\hat{r}^{shared}}
computes correctly each coordinate
m_{i}
with probability at least
1-\delta
over the choice of
r
.

By averaging, there exists a fixed string
\hat{r}
such that:

\Pr_{(m,\rho,z,w)\leftarrow\operatorname{RNSY}^{\prime},i\leftarrow[k]}[\operatorname{Dec}^{w}(i,f(m,\rho,z,w),\hat{r}^{shared},\hat{r})=m_{i}]\geq 1-\delta.

By Markov’s inequality:

\Pr_{(m,\rho,z,w)\leftarrow\operatorname{RNSY}^{\prime}}\left[\Pr_{i\leftarrow[k]}[\operatorname{Dec}^{w}(i,f(m,\rho,z,w),\hat{r}^{shared},\hat{r})\neq m_{i}]\geq 10\delta\right]\leq\frac{1}{10}.

Let
\overline{\operatorname{Dec}}^{w}(i,j)=\operatorname{Dec}^{w}(i,j,\hat{r}^{shared},\hat{r})
. We obtain that:

\Pr_{(m,\rho,z,w)\leftarrow\operatorname{RNSY}^{\prime}}[\Pr_{i\leftarrow[k]}[\overline{\operatorname{Dec}}^{w}(i,f(m,\rho,z,w))=m_{i}]>1-10\delta]>\frac{9}{10}.

Which gives that:

\Pr_{(m,\rho,z,w)\leftarrow\operatorname{RNSY}}\left[\Pr_{i\leftarrow[k]}[\overline{\operatorname{Dec}}^{w}(i,f(m,\rho,z,w))=m_{i}]>1-10\delta\right]>\left(\frac{2}{3}-\gamma\right)\cdot\frac{9}{10}>0.55.

Let
A
denote the event:

A=\left\{{\Pr_{i\leftarrow[k]}[\overline{\operatorname{Dec}}^{w}(i,f(m,\rho,z,w))=m_{i}]>1-10\delta}\right\},

so that we have that:

\Pr_{(m,\rho,z,w)\leftarrow\operatorname{RNSY}}[A]>0.55.

By the definition of the two steps of
\operatorname{RNSY}
, and an averaging argument this gives that:

\Pr_{(m,\rho)\leftarrow\operatorname{RNSY}^{1}}\left[\Pr_{(z,w)\leftarrow\operatorname{RNSY}^{2}(m,w)}[A]>0.51]>0.01\right].

This concludes the proof.
∎

Applying Lemma
5.6
on the
(\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon,q^{\prime}=O(q\log k),L,1/20k)
-LLD that we have previously obtained, gives the following corollary:

Claim 5.7
.

There exists an oracle procedure
\overline{Dec}^{(\cdot)}(i,j)
receiving
i\in[k]
,
j\in[L]
, and making
q^{\prime}=O(q\cdot\log k)
queries to a string
w\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, such that with probability at least
0.51
over choosing
(m,\rho)\leftarrow\operatorname{RNSY}^{1}
, we have that with probability at least
0.01
over choosing
(z,w)\leftarrow\operatorname{RNSY}^{2}(m,\rho)
,
there exists
j\in[L]
such that for every
i\leftarrow[k]
,
\overline{Dec}^{w}(i,j)=m_{i}
.

Proof.

This follows directly from Lemma
5.6
, noticing that a
statement of the form:

\Pr_{i\leftarrow[k]}[\overline{Dec}^{w}(i,j)=m_{i}]\geq 1-\frac{1}{2k},

implies that for every
i\leftarrow[k]
,
\overline{Dec}^{w}(i,j)=m_{i}
.
∎

We can use this (in a similar way to Lemma
4.3
) to argue that:

Claim 5.8
.

Let
a=\log L+100
and let
\ell=\log k
.
For every
\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{a}
, there exists a
q
-CNF
C^{\alpha}
over
n
variables, such that for
C=\bigvee_{\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{a}}C^{\alpha}
, and
p=\frac{\epsilon}{10}
:

•

\Pr[C(U_{n})=1]\leq 2^{-(2^{\ell}-a)}
.

•

\Pr_{\rho\leftarrow\mathsf{R}^{n}_{p}}[\Pr_{z\leftarrow U^{1/3}_{n\cdot p}}[C(\operatorname{Fill}_{\rho}(z))=1]\geq 0.01]\geq 0.01
.

Proof.

The proof is essentially identical to that of Lemma
4.3
. More precisely, the message
m\in\{0,1\}^{k}
plays the role of
f\in\{0,1\}^{2^{\ell}}
, the index
i\in[k]
plays the role of
x\in\{0,1\}^{\ell}
, and the index
j\in[L]
, plays the role of
\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{a}
. The argument in the proof of Lemma
4.3
shows that for every
m\in\{0,1\}^{k}
, there exists a circuit
C_{m}
of the required form such that:

•

\Pr_{m\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{k},x\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{n}}[C_{m}(x)=1]\leq 2^{-(2^{\ell}-a)}
.

•

With probability at least
0.51
over choosing
(m,\rho)\leftarrow\operatorname{RNSY}^{1}
, we have that with probability at least
0.01
over choosing
(z,w)\leftarrow\operatorname{RNSY}^{2}(m,\rho)
, we have that
C_{m}(x)=1
, for
x=\operatorname{Fill}_{\rho}(z)
(as in experiment
\operatorname{RNSY}
).

By applying Markov’s inequality on each one of the two items, we can obtain that:

•

\Pr_{m\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{k}}\left[\Pr_{x\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{n}}[C_{m}(x)=1]\leq 2^{-(2^{\ell}-a-100)}\right]\geq 1-2^{-100}
.

•

With probability at least
0.1
over choosing
m\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{k}
, we have that with probability at least
0.01
over choosing
\rho\leftarrow\mathsf{R}^{n}_{p}
, we have that with probability at least
0.01
over choosing
(z,w)\leftarrow\operatorname{RNSY}^{2}(m,\rho)
, we have that
C_{m}(x)=1
, for
x=\operatorname{Fill}_{\rho}(z)
.

This allows to do a union bound, and obtain that there exists
m\in\{0,1\}^{k}
such that setting
C=C_{m}
, meets the conclusion of Claim
5.8
.
∎

We are finally ready to prove Theorem
5.4
. Using Lemma
4.5
we conclude that assuming
a+100\leq\frac{2^{\ell}}{10}
(which for sufficiently large
k
, follows by our assumption that
L\leq 2^{k/20}
) we get that
q^{\prime}\geq\frac{1000}{\epsilon}
which gives that
q=\Omega(\frac{1}{\epsilon\cdot\log k})
, as required.

6
Conclusion and Open Problems

The most interesting open problem is Open Problem
1.5
. We hope that Theorem
1.11
may help to point us to new constructions.

However, it is possible that the answer to Open Problem
1.9
is negative, showing that black-box proofs cannot be used to solve Open Problem
1.5
. Is this the case? If it is, can we show this?

Easier problems towards showing a negative answer to Open Problem
1.9
are:

•

Is it true that in any black-box
\mathsf{hard}\mbox{-}\mathsf{function}\Rightarrow\mathsf{PRG}
proof, the number of queries
q\geq m
or even
q\geq m^{2}
? A positive answer will show that the cost of the hybrid argument (in terms of the number queries used by the reduction) is unavoidable in black-box
\mathsf{hard}\mbox{-}\mathsf{function}\Rightarrow\mathsf{PRG}
proofs.

•

We don’t know whether a super-constant number of queries is necessary for constant
\epsilon
. Can we show a super-constant lower bound on the number of queries of a reduction for a black-box
\mathsf{hard}\mbox{-}\mathsf{function}\Rightarrow\mathsf{PRG}
proof?

•

In fact, we don’t even know to show a
q>1
lower bound for black-box
{\rho}\mbox{-}\mathsf{hard}\mbox{-}\mathsf{function}\Rightarrow{\epsilon}\mbox{-}\mathsf{PRG}
proof for constant
\epsilon
, and
\rho=\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon
. Can we show this?

•

In all cases above, the question is open even for small values of
a
(that do not apply in the extreme high-end).

Another approach to hardness vs. randomness was very recently suggested by Chen and Tell
[
CT21a
]
. They use an assumption which is less standard, and incomparable to
the extreme high-end hardness assumption
to construct “target PRGs” which are weaker than PRGs, but suffice for fast derandomization of randomized algorithms. It is interesting to investigate the power of this approach. For more details on this approach and exciting recent developments in this area, see the survey paper
[
CT23
]
.

Finally, another open problem is to further improve our lower bounds on the number of queries for reductions for black-box hardness amplification. More specifically, our improved lower bounds for hardness amplification apply in the extreme high-end, but are not tight. We only get
q\geq\max(\Omega(\ell),\Omega(\frac{1}{\epsilon}))
, whereas the known upper bounds give
q=O(\frac{\ell}{\epsilon^{2}})
. Can we prove a matching lower bound that applies in the extreme high-end? (namely, for
a=2^{(1-o(1))\cdot\ell}
). Such lower bounds were given by
[
GSV18
]
for the high-end, namely for
a\leq 2^{\nu\cdot\ell}
for a constant
\nu>0
.
