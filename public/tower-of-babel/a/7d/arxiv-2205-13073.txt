Malcev Completions, Hodge Theory, and Motives
Emil Jacobsen

Abstract
We prove that, on a smooth, connected variety in characteristic zero admitting a rational point, local systems of geometric origin are stable under extension in the category of all local systems. As a consequence of this, we obtain a (Nori) motivic strengthening of Hain’s theorem on Malcev completions of monodromy representations. Our methods are Tannakian, and rely on an abstract criterion for “Malcev completeness”, which is proved in the first part of the paper. A couple of secondary applications of this criterion are given: an alternative proof of D’Addezio–Esnault’s theorem, which says that local systems of Hodge origin are stable under extension in the category of all local systems; a generalization of the theorem of Hain, mentioned above, which also affirms a conjecture of Arapura; and an alternative proof of a theorem of Lazda, which under suitable assumptions gives an isomorphism between the relative unipotent de Rham fundamental group and the unipotent de Rham fundamental group of the special fibre.

Introduction

The main result of this paper can be stated as:
local systems of geometric origin are stable under extension.
Let us have a closer look at what is meant by this.

Fix a subfield
k
of
\mathbb{C}
.
For a
k
-variety
X
,
Ivorra and Morel (
[
IM24
]
) define the category of
perverse motives
on
X
,
here denoted by
\PM(X)
.
It is a
\mathbb{Q}
-linear abelian category and comes with a faithful exact

Betti realization functor

\PM(X)\to\mathcal{P}(X)
landing in the category
\mathcal{P}(X)
of perverse sheaves (with rational coefficients) on
X
.
Over the base field
k
,
perverse motives are the same as Nori motives.

Now let
X
be a smooth, connected
k
-variety.
Then, the category of local systems (with rational coefficients)
\Loc(X)
on
X

forms an abelian subcategory of
\mathcal{P}(X)
(after shifting).
This lets us define the category of
motivic local systems

\MLS(X)

as the full subcategory of perverse motives on
X
generated by those whose Betti realizations are (shifted) local systems.
We get an induced Betti realization functor
b_{X}^{*}\colon\MLS(X)\to\Loc(X)
.

Remark 1.1
.

Apart from the results of Ivorra–Morel,
the main reason that motivic local systems are well-behaved enough for our purposes
is the work of Terenzi in
[
Ter24
]

to which we return later in the introduction.

Definition 1.2
.

A local system on
X
is
of geometric origin
, or
comes from geometry

if it is a subquotient of one in the image of the Betti realization
b_{X}^{*}\colon\MLS(X)\to\Loc(X)

Our main result can then be stated as follows:

Theorem 1.3

(Thm.
7.7
.1)
.

Let

be a smooth and connected
k
-variety with a rational point.
Then local systems of geometric origin
are stable under extension in
\Loc(X)
.
In other words,
if

\displaystyle 0\to L^{\prime}\to L\to L^{\prime\prime}\to 0

is a short exact sequence in
\Loc(X)
such that
L^{\prime}
and
L^{\prime\prime}
are of geometric origin,
then so is

.

Remark 1.4
.

There are multiple other definitions of local systems of geometric origin in the literature.
For some recent examples, see
[
Ayo21

[

, 685]

Further back,

[

(using the terminology of a local system being “motivic”, rather than “of geometric origin”),
and we believe our definition is in the spirit of this one.
As a consequence of our main theorem (Thm.

1.2
The motivic fundamental group

We keep

, and

Let

Background

1.4.2
Motivic structures on fundamental groups

The singular homology functors
H_{i}(\text{--},\mathbb{Q})
on algebraic varieties over
\mathbb{C}

factor through the category of mixed Hodge structures,
by the seminal work of Deligne.
Conjecturally,
they factor further,
through a (Tannakian) category of motives,
enjoying many useful properties.
By Hurewicz,
the functor
\pi_{1}(\text{--},\text{--})^{\mathrm{ab}}\otimes\mathbb{Q}
on pointed algebraic
\mathbb{C}
-varieties
is isomorphic to
H_{1}(\text{--},\mathbb{Q})
,
so it too should factor through motives.
Put differently,
the abelianization of the fundamental group of an algebraic variety is canonically a motive.
It’s natural to investigate whether a larger part of the fundamental group is a motive,
as passing to the abelianization is rather destructive.
In particular,
one can ask to what extent Hodge theory can be extended to the fundamental group,
i.e., what part of the fundamental group carries a natural mixed Hodge structure.

In this direction,
and improving upon
[
Mor78
]
,
Hain showed in
[
Hai87
]
that
the unipotent completion of the fundamental group carries a natural mixed Hodge structure
(see Rmk.
1.10
).
Hain then went further in
[
Hai98
]

and proved the following:

Theorem 1.11

(
[
Hai98
, Thm. 13.1]
)
.

Let
X
be a smooth connected variety over
\mathbb{C}
,
and let
x
be a complex point of
X
.
Let
V
be a polarizable variation of Hodge structure on
X
,
such that the image of the monodromy representation
\pi_{1}(X,x)\to\GL(V_{x})

is Zariski dense in
\Aut(V_{x},\langle,\rangle)
.
Then the Malcev completion of this monodromy representation
carries a canonical (real) mixed Hodge structure.

Motivic strengthenings of Hain’s unipotent theorem have been established in some cases.
For instance,
Deligne (
[
Del89
]
) equipped the unipotent completion of the fundamental group of a rational variety
with the structure of a compatible system of realizations, which is not too far from that of a motive.
This was strengthened in
[
DG05
]
,
where the unipotent completion of the fundamental group of a unirational variety defined over a number field
was equipped with the structure of a mixed Tate motive,
in Voevodsky’s sense.

A motivic strengthening of Hain’s theorem on Malcev completions is achieved in
Thm.
1.5
.
Moreover,
the second part of the Hodge theoretic result Thm.
1.9

generalizes Hain’s theorem,
by allowing arbitrary admissible variations.

1.5
The Malcev completeness criterion

The proofs of these three theorems are based on a general criterion
for a homomorphism to be Malcev complete.
All the groups we consider are affine group schemes over a fixed field.
Let
P\to K
be a group homomorphism,
and denote the restriction functor of (finite-dimensional)
K
-representations along this morphism by

\Res^{K}_{P}\colon\Rep K\to\Rep P
.
It’s an easy observation that

P\to K
is Malcev complete if and only if

(i)

P\to K
is surjective, and

(ii)

the image of
\Res^{K}_{P}
is stable under extension in
\Rep P
.

Let
K\twoheadrightarrow S
be a quotient by a pro-unipotent normal subgroup.
We write
\Gamma(K,\text{--})
and
\Gamma(P,\text{--})
for
K
- and
P
-invariants respectively.
It is then not difficult to see that Malcev completeness of
P\to K
is equivalent to

(iii)

the composition
P\to K\to S
is surjective, and

(iv)

the natural maps

\displaystyle R^{i}\Gamma(K,\text{--})\to R^{i}\Gamma(P,\text{--})\circ\Res^{K}_{P},

are isomorphisms for
i=0,1
.

In applications, the following theorem offers a condition which is easier to check than (iv):

Theorem 1.12

(Malcev completeness criterion, Thm.
5.1
)
.

Let
G=K\rtimes H
be a semi-direct product
and consider a homomorphism
P\to K
.
Let
K\twoheadrightarrow S
be quotient by a pro-unipotent normal subgroup.
Then
P\to K
is Malcev complete if

P\to S
is surjective and the natural maps

\displaystyle R^{i}\Gamma(K,\text{--})\circ\Res^{G}_{K}\to R^{i}\Gamma(P,\text{--})\circ\Res^{G}_{P}

between functors
\Rep G\to\VecOp
are isomorphisms for
i=0,1
.

In other words,
when checking condition (iv) above,
it’s enough to work with objects in the image of
\Res^{G}_{K}
.
Note that the group
H
and its action on
K
can be chosen completely arbitrary,
and therein lies the strength of the theorem.
The proof of Thm.
1.12
works by reducing to the following special case:

Theorem 1.13

(Unipotent isomorphism criterion, Thm.
3.3
)
.

Let
G=K\rtimes H
be a semi-direct product
and consider a homomorphism
P\to K
.
Then
P\to K
is an isomorphism if

P
is pro-unipotent and the natural maps

\displaystyle R^{i}\Gamma(K,\text{--})\circ\Res^{G}_{K}\to R^{i}\Gamma(P,\text{--})\circ\Res^{G}_{P}

between functors
\Rep G\to\VecOp
are isomorphisms for
i=0,1
.

Remark 1.14
.

Using Tannaka duality, it’s easy to see that,
when
P
is pro-unipotent,

P\to K
is an isomorphism if and only if condition (iv) above holds.
Moreover, the assumptions in Thm.
1.13
easily imply that
K
too is pro-unipotent,
so that
S
is trivial and the surjectivity of
K\to S
is automatic,
really making it a special case of Thm.
1.12
.

The following direct corollary to Thm.
1.12
has been suggested by Marco D’Addezio:

Corollary 1.15
.

Suppose
\charac F=0
.
Let
P\to G
be a homomorphism
whose image is contained in the pro-unipotent radical
R_{\mathrm{u}}G
,
and suppose that
P
is pro-unipotent.
Then
P\to R_{\mathrm{u}}G
is an isomorphism if and only if
the natural maps

\displaystyle\Res^{G_{\mathrm{red}}}_{1}\circ\,R^{i}\Ind_{G}^{G_{\mathrm{red}}}\to R^{i}\Gamma(P,\text{--})\circ\Res^{G}_{P}

are isomorphisms for
i=0,1
.

Remark 1.16
.

Keep the setup as in Thm.
1.12
.
The theorem takes three assumptions:
the cohomological assumptions for
i=0,1
,
and the surjectivity of
P\to S
.
The result is the Malcev completeness of
P\to K
,
which essentially has two parts:
the surjectivity of
P\to K
(listed as (i) above),
and condition (ii) on stability under extension.
If one only cares about the first consequence,
it is shown in
[
DAd23
]
that the
i=1
condition isn’t needed.
More precisely,
D’Addezio–Esnault
show in ibid. how to use results from
[
DE22
, Appendix A]

to lift surjectivity of
P\to S
to surjectivity of
P\to K

when
\Gamma(K,\text{--})\circ\Res^{G}_{K}\to\Gamma(P,\text{--})\circ\Res^{G}_{P}
is an isomorphism.

and the surjectivity assumption is established using Prop.
6.15

This gives the Malcev completeness of the relevant map,
and the second part follows as a corollary,
see Prop.
6.16

In the motivic context of Thm.
1.3

Part I
Affine group schemes over a field

2
Preliminaries

We fix a field
F
and assume all additive categories to be
F
-linear.
The category of finite-dimensional
F
-vector spaces will be denoted by
\VecOp
.
We write
\bm{1}
for the unit object in a monoidal category.
When there are several monoidal categories around,
it will be understood from context which unit object we mean.

By
pro-algebraic group
we mean an affine group scheme over
F
.

We will use the term
tensor category
to mean an
F
-linear symmetric monoidal category.
That is, an
F
-linear category
\mathcal{C}
equipped with an
F
-bilinear functor

(\text{--}\otimes\text{--})\colon\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{C}
,
a unit object
\bm{1}
,
as well as compatible natural isomorphisms encoding associativity, commutativity,
and the unit relations,
that satisfy the usual axioms.
We will say a functor
f
between tensor categories
is
monoidal
when it’s equipped with an isomorphism
f(\bm{1})\simeq\bm{1}

and a natural isomorphism
f(X\otimes Y)\simeq f(X)\otimes f(Y)
,
both compatible with the associator and commutator isomorphisms of the two categories.
Finally, a natural transformation
\phi\colon f\to g
between monoidal functors
f
and
g

is
monoidal
when it’s compatible with the monoidal structures of
f
and
g
.

An object
X
in a tensor category
\mathcal{C}
is
(strongly) dualizable

if there exists an object
X^{\vee}
(called a
dual
of
X
)
and maps

\ev\colon X\otimes X^{\vee}\to\bm{1}
,

\coev\colon\bm{1}\to X^{\vee}\otimes X

that turn

X^{\vee}\otimes(\text{--})

into a right adjoint of

X\otimes(\text{--})
.
Moreover,
\mathcal{C}
is
rigid
if every object in
\mathcal{C}
is dualizable.
Duality is reflexive, in the sense that
X
is a dual of
X^{\vee}
.
Monoidal functors preserve duals.

2.1
Unipotency

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an abelian category and
f\colon\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{A}
a functor.
Say an object
U
in
\mathcal{A}
is
f
-unipotent
if
it can be built in a finite number of steps from the essential image of
f
,
using extensions.
More precisely, if there exists a filtration

\displaystyle 0=U_{-1}\subseteq U_{0}\subseteq\dotsc\subseteq U_{n}=U,

such that the quotients
U_{i}/U_{i-1}
are in the essential image of
f
.
We call such a filtration an
f
-unipotent filtration
of
U
, of
length

n
.
If every object of
\mathcal{A}
is
f
-unipotent,
then
\mathcal{A}
is said to be
f
-unipotent

(or sometimes
relatively unipotent over
\mathcal{C}
).

Definition 2.2
.

Let
U
be an
f
-unipotent object, as above.
Let
n
be the smallest integer such that
U
admits an
f
-unipotent filtration of length
n
.
We say the
f
-unipotency index
of
U
is
n
.

Remark 2.3
.

The usefulness of unipotency is that it allows for
unipotent induction
,
that is,
induction on the unipotency index.
This technique lets us prove many statements
by checking that they hold for a smaller class of objects,
and that they behave well with respect to extension.

Lemma 2.4
.

Unipotency is stable under extension.

Proof.

Let
f\colon\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{A}
be as above.
Consider an extension

\displaystyle 0\to U\to A\to W\to 0,

where
U
and
W
are both
f
-unipotent.
In the case when
W=f(C)
,

A
is unipotent by definition.
We now proceed by induction on the unipotency index of
W
.
Let

\displaystyle 0\to W^{\prime}\to W\to f(C)\to 0,

such that
W^{\prime}
is of a lower unipotency index than
W
.
By induction, any extension of
W^{\prime}
by something
f
-unipotent is unipotent.
The pullback
A^{\prime}
of
A\to W
and
W^{\prime}\to W
is therefore unipotent,
and
A
is an extension

\displaystyle 0\to A^{\prime}\to A\to f(C)\to 0,

by standard arguments. ∎

Definition 2.5
.

With
f\colon\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{A}
as above,
we say that
\mathcal{A}
is
weakly
f
-unipotent

if every non-zero object of
\mathcal{A}
admits a non-zero subobject in the essential image of
f
.

Unipotency immediately implies weak unipotency,
but the converse is only true under a finiteness condition.

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
f\colon\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{A}
be as above.

(i)

If
\mathcal{A}
is
f
-unipotent,
then it is weakly
f
-unipotent.

(ii)

If
\mathcal{A}
is weakly
f
-unipotent,
then any object in
\mathcal{A}
of finite length is
f
-unipotent.

Proof.

(i) Let
A\in\mathcal{A}
be non-zero.
Fix an
f
-unipotent filtration
A_{i}
of
A
as above.
Then the minimal
i
such that
A_{i}
is non-zero gives the desired subobject.

(ii) Let
A
be non-zero of finite length.
We get a short exact sequence

\displaystyle 0\to f(C)\to A\to A_{1}\to 0.

Then
A_{1}
has a non-zero subobject
f(C_{1})
,
and we iterate this until,
by finiteness of the length of
A
,
we get
A_{n}=f(C_{n})
.
Using that unipotency is closed under extension (Lemma
2.4
),
we conclude that the
A_{i}
and finally
A
are all
f
-unipotent.
∎

Remark 2.7
.

When
\mathcal{A}
is monoidal,
\mathcal{C}=\VecOp
, and
f\colon\VecOp\to\mathcal{A}
is monoidal,
then
f
is uniquely determined, up to monoidal natural isomorphism
(cf. Rmk.
2.9
).
In this case, we usually just say that
\mathcal{A}
is
unipotent
,
without reference to
f
.

2.2
Neutral Tannakian categories

The notion of a (neutral) Tannakian category is central in the sequel.
A
neutral Tannakian category
(over
F
) is
a rigid abelian tensor category
(\mathcal{A},\otimes,\bm{1})

such that
\End(\bm{1})=F
,
and which admits a faithful, exact, monoidal functor
\mathcal{A}\to\VecOp
:
such a functor is called a
(neutral) fibre functor
.
A
neutralized Tannakian category
is a Tannakian category together with a neutral fibre functor.

Remark 2.8
.

The tensor product is required to be
F
-bilinear,
and it is automatically exact in both arguments (
[
DM82
, Prop. 1.17]
).
The rigidity implies existence of an internal Hom functor,
which is (right and left) adjoint to the tensor product,
and which is hence also exact in both arguments.
We denote it by
\iHom(\text{--},\text{--})
.

By
morphism
of neutral Tannakian categories
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}

we shall mean an exact, faithful, monoidal functor.
We will always write such a functor decorated with a raised asterisk,
e.g.
p^{*}\colon\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{B}
.
These functors always have ind-right adjoints,
i.e., functors
p_{*}\colon\Ind\mathcal{B}\to\Ind\mathcal{A}
,
for which we use the same letters but lowered asterisks.
These ind-right adjoints are left exact
and can always be right derived,
as the ind-category of a neutral Tannakian category has enough injectives
(see, e.g.,
[
Jan03
, Prop. I.3.9]
).
By
morphism
of neutralized Tannakian categories
(\mathcal{A},f^{*})
and
(\mathcal{B},\varphi^{*})

we shall mean a morphism
p^{*}\colon\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{B}
together with a monoidal isomorphism
\varphi^{*}\circ p^{*}\simeq f^{*}
.

This gives 2-categories
\mathrm{nTan}
and
\mathrm{nTan}_{*}
of neutral and neutralized Tannakian categories, respectively,
after we define what the 2-morphisms are.
In
\mathrm{nTan}
, they are the monoidal, invertible natural transformations.
In
\mathrm{nTan}_{*}
, they are the same, with the additional requirement
that they be compatible with the extra data in the morphisms (the monoidal isomorphisms above).

Remark 2.9

(On sections of fibre functors.)
.

A fibre functor
f^{*}
of a Tannakian category
\mathcal{B}
always admits a section in the 2-category above.
Fix an equivalence of
\VecOp
with its skeleton, consisting of objects
\bm{1}^{\oplus n}
,
n\geq 0
.
On these objects, we simply define the section
s^{*}
in the only way we can:

\bm{1}
has to go to
\bm{1}
,
and direct sums must be respected.
This is clearly a section,
since
f^{*}
is monoidal and respects direct sums.
Moreover, we can explicitly describe the ind-right adjoint
s_{*}
,
which turns out to restrict to an honest right adjoint of
s^{*}
.
Indeed, there’s a natural isomorphism
s_{*}\simeq\Hom_{\mathcal{A}}(\bm{1},\text{--})
.
This follows immediately from the fact that
\iHom_{\VecOp}(\bm{1},\text{--})

is the identity functor on
\VecOp
.

Given a neutralized Tannakian category
(\mathcal{B},f^{*})
,
Tannaka duality produces a pro-algebraic group,
which we denote
\mathscr{G}(f^{*})
,
or
\mathscr{G}(\mathcal{B},f^{*})
, or
\mathscr{G}(\mathcal{B})
, depending on what we want to emphasize.
We call this group the
Tannaka dual
of
(\mathcal{B},f^{*})
.
This group is such that
f^{*}
naturally factors
through the forgetful functor
\Rep\mathscr{G}(f^{*})\to\VecOp
,
and such that
\mathcal{B}\to\Rep\mathscr{G}(f^{*})
is a (monoidal)
F
-linear equivalence.
We also have a canonical isomorphism
\mathscr{G}(\Rep G)\simeq G
for all
G
.

Remark 2.10

(On Tannakian categories and unipotency)
.

➺

A pro-algebraic group
G
is pro-unipotent if and only if

\Rep G
is unipotent.

➺

All objects in a neutral Tannakian category are of finite length,
so for these categories, weak unipotency and unipotency coincide.

➺

If
V
is a unipotent
G
-representation, then any subquotient of
V
in
\Rep G

is unipotent. (Proof: Enough to check for subobjects of
V
, by rigidity.
Given a unipotent filtration on
V
,
the induced filtration on a subobject is also a unipotent filtration,
by the fact that any subobject of
\bm{1}^{n}
is of the form
\bm{1}^{m}
.)∎

Tannaka duality is functorial:
given a morphism
p^{*}
of neutralized Tannakian categories as above,
we get an induced group-scheme homomorphism

p\colon\mathscr{G}(\varphi^{*})\to\mathscr{G}(f^{*})
,
which we always write using the same symbol,
but without the asterisk.
Conversely,
given a group homomorphism
p\colon G\to H
,
we get a morphism of Tannakian categories
p^{*}\colon\Rep H\to\Rep G
,
namely restriction along
p
.
The ind-right adjoint
p_{*}
is in this context given by induction along
p

(cf. Section
2.3
).

The following proposition is an example of how properties of group homomorphisms
are reflected on the Tannakian side:

Proposition 2.11

(
[
DM82
, Prop. 2.21]
)
.

Let
p\colon G\to H
be a homomorphism of pro-algebraic groups.
Then,

1.

p
is a closed immersion (injective) if and only if
every object in
\Rep G
is isomorphic to a subquotient of an object in the image of
p^{*}
,
and

2.

p
is faithfully flat (surjective) if and only if
for every subobject

X\hookrightarrow p^{*}Y
in
\Rep G
,
there exists a subobject
Y^{\prime}
of
Y
such that
p^{*}Y^{\prime}\simeq X
.

Proof.

This is essentially shown in
[
DM82
, Prop. 2.21]
,
the only difference being that the second part also includes the condition that
p^{*}
is full.
However,
fullness follows from having a subobject stable image.
Indeed,
let
Y,Y^{\prime}
be representations of
H
,
and let
\varphi\colon p^{*}Y\to p^{*}Y^{\prime}
be a morphism.
It corresponds to a subobject

\Gamma_{\varphi}\subseteq p^{*}Y\oplus p^{*}Y^{\prime}\simeq p^{*}(Y\oplus Y^{\prime})
,
namely its graph.
Lifting this to a subobject of
Y\oplus Y^{\prime}

amounts exactly to
lifting
\varphi
to a map
Y\to Y^{\prime}
.
∎

With the second part of this proposition in mind,
we make the following definition:

Definition 2.12
.

A
Tannakian subcategory
of a Tannakian category is
a full abelian subcategory closed under direct sums, tensor products, taking duals, and taking subquotients.

Given a homomorphism
G\to H
of pro-algebraic groups,
we sometimes denote by

\Res^{H}_{G}\colon\Rep H\to\Rep G

the restriction functor from
H
-representations to
G
-representations.

Lemma 2.13
.

Given a homomorphism
p\colon G\to H
,
the smallest Tannakian subcategory of
\Rep G
containing
\im(p^{*})

is the essential image of
\Res^{\im(p)}_{G}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{T}\subseteq\Rep(G)
denote the smallest Tannakian subcategory containing
\im(p^{*})
.
Note that we have a factorization

G\to\mathscr{G}(\mathcal{T})\to H

of
p
,
and that the first map is surjective by Prop.
2.11
.
2
.
Next,
we check that

\mathscr{G}(\mathcal{T})\to H

is injective by Prop.
2.11
.
1
.
Thus,
we need to show that every object in
\mathcal{T}

is a subquotient of an object in the essential image of
p^{*}
.
But this follows from the following elementary fact:
if you take a full, monoidal subcategory of a Tannakian category,
closed under taking duals,
then the closure of this subcategory under taking subquotients is a Tannakian category.
Using this fact for the monoidal subcategory
\im(p^{*})
of
\Rep G
,
we get that
\mathcal{T}
is its closure under forming subquotients,
by the minimality of
\mathcal{T}
.
∎

Lemma 2.14
.

A homomorphism
p\colon G\to H
has pro-unipotent kernel
if and only if
the category
\Rep G
is
\Res^{\im(p)}_{G}
-unipotent.
(Equivalently,

\Rep G
is generated under extensions and subquotients by its full subcategory
\im(p^{*})
.)

Proof.

Assume that
\Rep G
is
\Res^{\im(p)}_{G}
-unipotent,
and let
K:=\ker(p)
.
Then it is clear that whatever is in the image of
\Res^{G}_{K}
,
is (absolutely) unipotent.
By Prop.
2.11
.
1
,
every
K
-representation is then a subquotient of a unipotent
K
-representation.
But such a representation must also be unipotent (Rmk.
2.10
).

Now assume that
\ker(p)=:K
is pro-unipotent.
Then, given a non-zero
G
-representation
V
,
the
K
-invariants
V^{K}
are non-zero and form a sub-
G
-representation
(since
K
is normal in
G
).
Moreover,
V^{K}
is actually the restriction of an

\im(p)
-representation,
since
\im(p)=\coker(K\to G)
.
This shows
\Res^{\im(p)}_{G}
-unipotency of
\Rep G
,
by Rmk.
2.10
.
∎

One perspective on Tannaka duality is as follows.
Let
f^{*}
be a fibre functor on a neutral Tannakian category
\mathcal{B}
as above.
Then, using the 2-section
s^{*}
of
f^{*}
described in Rmk.
2.9
,
one can prove that
f^{*}f_{*}\bm{1}
is naturally a Hopf algebra,
and we denote it by
\mathscr{H}(f^{*}):=f^{*}f_{*}\bm{1}
.
The Tannaka dual group is then
\mathscr{G}(f^{*}):=\Spec\mathscr{H}(f^{*})
.
Note that
\mathscr{G}(f^{*})
-representations are the same as
\mathscr{H}(f^{*})
-comodules.

Remark 2.15
.

It’s worth noting at this point,
that if
f^{*}
denotes the canonical fibre functor on
\Rep G
,
then
f^{*}f_{*}\bm{1}
is
\mathcal{O}(G)
,
and
f_{*}\bm{1}
is the regular representation of
G
.
Moreover,
f_{*}\bm{1}
is naturally an algebra object in
\Ind\Rep G
.

2.3
Restriction, induction, and invariants

Let
p\colon H\to G
be a morphism of pro-algebraic groups.
We denote the restriction functor
\Rep G\to\Rep H
by
p^{*}
,
or sometimes
\Res^{G}_{H}
(usually when the morphism is unlabelled).
In the literature,
induction is often only defined for
p
injective.
We depart from this convention and
call the ind-right adjoint
p_{*}\colon\Ind\Rep H\to\Ind\Rep G
of
p^{*}

induction

for arbitrary
p
.
For this reason,
we sometimes denote
p_{*}
by
\Ind_{H}^{G}

(again, usually when the morphism is unlabelled).
Note that we can decompose any
p
as a surjection

p_{0}\colon H\to\im(p)

followed by an injection
p_{1}\colon\im(p)\to G
.
Our induction functor
p_{*}
is then given
by the composition of
the functor
\Gamma(\ker(p_{0}),\text{--})=(\text{--})^{\ker(p_{0})}

taking invariants under the action of the kernel of
p_{0}
,
and the more traditional induction functor
\Ind_{\im(p)}^{G}
along the injective homomorphism
p_{1}
.

Remark 2.16
.

The invariants functor
\Gamma(G,\text{--})=(\text{--})^{G}
on
\Rep G
is given by “induction” along
G\to 1
,
and the trivial representation functor
\VecOp\to\Rep G
is given by restriction along
G\to 1
.

An important fact is that the projection morphism for the restriction-induction adjunction is an isomorphism.

Proposition 2.17

(The restriction-induction projection formula)
.

Let
p
be a homomorphism of pro-algebraic groups.
Then there is an isomorphism
p_{*}(A\otimes p^{*}B)\simeq p_{*}A\otimes B
,
natural in
A
and
B
.
In particular, setting
A=\bm{1}
gives a natural isomorphism

p_{*}p^{*}\simeq p_{*}\bm{1}\otimes(\text{--})
.

Proof.

For
p
injective, this is
[
Jan03
, I.3.6]
.
For
p
surjective, it is obvious.
Combining these two cases, we can conclude for arbitrary
p
.
∎

We give two trivial consequences of this result, since they will be useful in the sequel.
They both concern morphisms
p
that admit a retraction
r
,
and that are therefore, in particular, injective.
The first result gives a description of
p^{*}
in terms of
p_{*}
and
r_{*}
.
The second says that,
when tensoring with
p_{*}\bm{1}
,
one may first change the
\ker(r)
-action to be trivial,
and then tensor with
p_{*}\bm{1}
,
and get the same result.

Lemma 2.18
.

Let
p\colon H\to G
be a homomorphism of pro-algebraic groups,
and let
r\colon G\to H
be a retraction of
p
.
Then we have a natural isomorphism
p^{*}\simeq r_{*}(p_{*}\bm{1}\otimes\text{--})
.

Proof.

By Prop.
2.17
,
we have an isomorphism

p_{*}p^{*}\simeq p_{*}\bm{1}\otimes(\text{--})
.
To this isomorphism, we apply
r_{*}
,
then use that
r_{*}p_{*}=\mathrm{id}
, and get

p^{*}\simeq r_{*}(p_{*}\bm{1}\otimes\text{--})
.
∎

Lemma 2.19
.

Let
p\colon H\to G
be a homomorphism of pro-algebraic groups,
and let
r\colon G\to H
be a retraction of
p
.
Then we have a natural isomorphism
p_{*}\bm{1}\otimes(\text{--})\simeq p_{*}\bm{1}\otimes r^{*}p^{*}(\text{--})
.

Proof.

Using the projection formula,
2.17
,
we compute

\displaystyle p_{*}\bm{1}\otimes(\text{--})\simeq p_{*}p^{*}(\text{--})\simeq p_{*}(p^{*}r^{*})p^{*}(\text{--})\simeq p_{*}\bm{1}\otimes r^{*}p^{*}(\text{--}).

∎

2.4
Group isomorphisms

Consider a homomorphism of pro-algebraic groups

p\colon G\to H
.
We have a corresponding morphism of Tannakian categories

p^{*}\colon\Rep H\to\Rep G
(restriction along
p
).
Let
f^{*}
and
\varphi^{*}
be the canonical fibre functors of
\Rep H
and
\Rep G
, respectively.
Then
p^{*}
induces a morphism of algebra objects

p_{\mathrm{alg}}\colon p^{*}f_{*}\bm{1}\to\varphi_{*}\bm{1}
,
from the restriction along
p
of the regular representation of
H
to the regular representation of
G

(cf. Rmk.
2.15
).
This map is simply the algebra homomorphism
\mathcal{O}(H)\to\mathcal{O}(G)
induced by
p
,
which respects the
\mathcal{O}(G)
-coactions,
making it a morphism of algebra objects in
\Ind\Rep G
.

Another way of viewing this morphism,
is via the universal property of
\varphi_{*}\bm{1}
.
We have a natural isomorphism
\Hom(\text{--},\varphi_{*}\bm{1})\simeq\Hom(\varphi^{*}(\text{--}),\bm{1})
.
So, to give a map
p^{*}f_{*}\bm{1}\to\varphi_{*}\bm{1}
,
is the same as to give a map from
\varphi^{*}p^{*}f_{*}\bm{1}
to
\bm{1}
.
But
\varphi^{*}p^{*}f_{*}\bm{1}\simeq f^{*}f_{*}\bm{1}
has a natural map to
\bm{1}
:
the counit it’s equipped with as a Hopf algebra.
In this way, we recover the algebra homomorphism

p_{\mathrm{alg}}\colon p^{*}f_{*}\bm{1}\to\varphi_{*}\bm{1}

above.
We can see this by applying
\varphi^{*}
to
p_{\mathrm{alg}}
,
and noting that the result is a morphism of Hopf algebras,
and is hence compatible with the two counits.
We get the following proposition:

Proposition 2.20
.

A homomorphism
p\colon G\to H
of pro-algebraic groups is an isomorphism
if and only if the algebra morphism
p^{*}\mathcal{O}(H)\to\mathcal{O}(G)
in
\Ind\Rep G
,
induced by the counit of
\mathcal{O}(H)
under the universal property of
\mathcal{O}(G)
,
is an isomorphism.

2.5
Categories of modules

Consider an abelian tensor category
\mathcal{A}
with all filtered colimits,
in which the tensor product commutes with finite colimits,
and let
\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{cpt}}
denote the full subcategory of compact objects:
objects
C\in\mathcal{A}
such that
\Hom(C,\text{--})
commutes with filtered colimits.
Take an algebra object
A
in
\mathcal{A}
,
and consider the category
\Mod(A)
of (left)
A
-modules in
\mathcal{A}
,
i.e., objects
M\in\mathcal{A}
together with an
A
-action
A\otimes M\to M
satisfying the usual axioms.
The category
\Mod(A)
is an abelian tensor category (under
\otimes_{A}
).

Definition 2.21
.

Let
M
be an
A
-module, as above. Then,

➺

M
is
strictly free of finite rank

Malcev completion gives a functor from the category of homomorphisms of pro-algebraic groups,

6.1
Checking the cohomological assumption

Our ultimate goal is to apply Thm.
5.1
,
and we thus need a way to establish the cohomological assumption,
namely,
that the canonical maps

b^{*}R^{i}s_{*}\to R^{i}\sigma_{*}B^{*}
, for
i=0,1
, are isomorphisms.
The idea is that a theory of enriched local systems usually lives inside some ambient theory of (perverse or constructible) sheaves,
where an analogue of the cohomological assumption holds.
A motivating example to have in mind is
the way admissible variations of mixed Hodge structures
sit in the larger category of mixed Hodge modules.

Notation 6.3
.

Let

be the derived category

of bounded complexes of sheaves of

Remark 6.4
.

Given a morphism

However

Definition 6.5

\displaystyle Rp_{*}^{\mathcal{E}}\to p_{*}^{\mathcal{A}}\circ\mathrm{inc}_{\mathcal{A}},\quad b^{*}\circ\,p_{*}^{\mathcal{A}}\to Rp_{*}\circ\,b^{*}_{\mathcal{A}}.

.
∎

)

6.2
Checking the surjectivity assumption

We begin with a definition:

Definition 6.14
.

Say a weak theory
\mathcal{E}
of enriched local systems

respects semi-simplicity

if for every
X
in
\SmVar_{k}
,
we have that

b^{*}\colon\mathcal{E}(X)\to\Loc(X)

sends semisimple objects to semisimple objects.

It turns out that this property is all we need for the surjectivity assumption:

Proposition 6.15
.

Assume the characteristic of
F
is zero.
Suppose
\mathcal{E}
respects semi-simplicity.
Then, for any
X
in
\SmVar_{k}
admitting an ambient theory
and any
k
-point
x
,
the map

\bar{\varrho}\colon\pi_{1}(X,x)\to S(X,x)

has Zariski dense image.

Proof.

We show it on the Tannakian side,
i.e., we work with

\bar{\varrho}^{*}\colon\Rep S(X,x)\to\Loc(X).

By Prop.
2.11
,
we need to prove that the image of
\bar{\varrho}^{*}
is stable under taking subobjects in
\Loc(X)
.
Recall that we have a commutative diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{E}(X)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Loc(X)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Rep K(X,x)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Rep S(X,x).}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\iota^{*}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces b^{*}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varrho^{*}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\bar{\varrho}^{*}}

Step 1.

Let
\mathcal{E}(X)_{\mathrm{ss}}
denote the full subcategory of semisimple objects in
\mathcal{E}(X)
.
We note that under
\iota^{*}
,
\mathcal{E}(X)_{\mathrm{ss}}
lands in the full subcategory

\Rep S(X,x)\subseteq\Rep K(X,x)
.
Let
G(X,x)_{\mathrm{red}}
be the Tannaka dual of
\mathcal{E}(X)_{\mathrm{ss}}
.
It’s the maximal pro-reductive quotient of
G(X,x)
, as
F
is of characteristic zero.
As the pro-unipotent radical
R_{\mathrm{u}}K(X,x)
is a characteristic subgroup of
K(X,x)
,
it is normal in the larger group
G(X,x)
.
Since
R_{\mathrm{u}}G(X,x)
is the largest connected, pro-unipotent, normal subgroup of
G(X,x)
,
it must contain
R_{\mathrm{u}}K(X,x)
.
Thus,
K(X,x)\to G(X,x)_{\mathrm{red}}
factors uniquely through
K(X,x)\to S(X,x)
.

Step 2.

Next,
we note that the map
S(X,x)\to G(X,x)_{\mathrm{red}}
is injective.
Indeed, this follows from

K(X,x)\cap R_{\mathrm{u}}G(X,x)=R_{\mathrm{u}}K(X,x)
,
which holds true by the previous step and the definition of the right-hand side.
In particular, for every object
A\in\Rep S(X,x)
,
there exists
M\in\mathcal{E}(X)_{\mathrm{ss}}

such that
A
is a subquotient of
\iota^{*}M
, in
\Rep S(X,x)
.
By the semi-simplicity of
\Rep S(X,x)
,

A
can actually be taken to be a subobject of
\iota^{*}M
.

Step 3.
We now show that

\bar{\varrho}^{*}
is full.
Take two objects
M
and
N
in
\mathcal{E}(X)
.
Then,
writing
\iota^{*}\colon\mathcal{E}(X)\to\Rep K
, as before,
we have that

\displaystyle\Hom(\iota^{*}M,\iota^{*}N)=b^{*}s_{*}\iHom(M,N)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{*}\iHom(b_{X}^{*}M,b_{X}^{*}N)

\displaystyle=\Hom(\varrho^{*}\iota^{*}M,\varrho^{*}\iota^{*}N),

using the
i=0
part of Prop.
6.11
;
so we have fullness (for
\varrho^{*}
and hence also
\bar{\varrho}^{*}
) on objects coming from
\mathcal{E}(X)
.

By the previous step,
every object in
\Rep S(X,x)
is a direct summand of an object coming from
\mathcal{E}(X)_{\mathrm{ss}}
.
Let two
S(X,x)
-representations
A
and
B
be direct summands of
\iota^{*}M
and
\iota^{*}N
,
and cut out by projectors
e
and
f
, respectively.
Then,

\displaystyle\Hom(A,B)=f\circ\Hom(\iota^{*}M,\iota^{*}N)\circ e=\varrho^{*}(f)\circ\Hom(\varrho^{*}\iota^{*}M,\varrho^{*}\iota^{*}N)\circ\varrho^{*}(e)

\displaystyle=\Hom(\varrho^{*}A,\varrho^{*}B).

Note finally, that the fullness of
\bar{\varrho}^{*}

implies that its image is stable under taking direct summands
(as fullness lets us lift projectors).

Step 4.

Now we are ready to conclude.
Let
A\in\Rep S(X,x)
and
L\subseteq\varrho^{*}A
.
By Step 2,
there is
M\in\mathcal{E}(X)_{\mathrm{ss}}

such that
A\subseteq\iota^{*}M
.
Thus,
L\subseteq\bar{\varrho}^{*}\iota^{*}M=b^{*}M
,
whence
L
is a direct factor of the right-hand side, as
\mathcal{E}
respects semi-simplicity,
and we are done by Step 3.
∎

6.3
A corollary to Malcev completeness

Fix an enriched local system
M
in
\mathcal{E}(X)

on some
X\in\SmVar_{k}
with a rational point
x
.
Denote by
M_{x}
the vector space
x^{*}b^{*}M
.
We may consider the associated monodromy representation
\pi_{1}(X,x)\to\GL(M_{x})
.
Take the Malcev completion of this morphism,
and denote it by
\hat{\pi}_{1}^{M}(X,x)
.

Proposition 6.16
.

Suppose that
\pi_{1}(X,x)\to K(X,x)
is Malcev complete.
Then the group
\hat{\pi}_{1}^{M}(X,x)
is canonically enriched in
\mathcal{E}(\mathrm{pt})
,
in the sense that
\mathcal{O}(\hat{\pi}_{1}^{M}(X,x))
is naturally a Hopf algebra object in
\Ind\mathcal{E}(\mathrm{pt})
,
or equivalently that there is a natural action by
G(k)
on
\hat{\pi}_{1}^{M}(X,x)
.

Proof.

Consider the full subcategory
\langle M,\mathcal{E}(\mathrm{pt})\rangle^{\otimes}
tensor-generated by
M
and
\mathcal{E}(\mathrm{pt})
in
\mathcal{E}(X)
,
i.e., consisting of subquotients of sums of tensor products of
M
,
M^{\vee}
and constant objects
(i.e., objects coming from
\mathcal{E}
).
We obtain a commutative diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 1}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathscr{G}(\langle\iota^{*}M\rangle^{\otimes})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathscr{G}(\langle M,\mathcal{E}(\mathrm{pt})\rangle^{\otimes})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces G(k)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 1}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 1}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{1}^{\mathcal{E}}(X,x)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces G(X,x)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces G(k)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 1.}

We have seen that the bottom row is (split) exact,
and the (split) exactness of the top row follows.

We have a factorization

\displaystyle\pi_{1}(X,x)\to\mathscr{G}(\langle\iota^{*}M\rangle^{\otimes})\hookrightarrow\GL(M_{x}),

of the monodromy representation.
By Lemma
4.2
,
the Malcev completion of
\pi_{1}(X,x)\to\GL(M_{x})

only depends on the image in
\GL(M_{x})
,
which by this factorization lies in the subgroup

\mathscr{G}(\langle\iota^{*}M\rangle^{\otimes})
.
Thus,
\hat{\pi}_{1}^{M}(X,x)
is the Malcev completion of

\pi_{1}(X,x)\to\mathscr{G}(\langle\iota^{*}M\rangle^{\otimes})
.

Next,
\hat{\pi}_{1}^{M}(X,x)
can be obtained as the Malcev completion of

\pi_{1}^{\mathcal{E}}(X,x)\to\mathscr{G}(\langle\iota^{*}M\rangle^{\otimes})
,
by Lemma
4.10
.
Note that
G(k)
acts on this morphism,
and by functoriality of Malcev completions (Prop.
4.17
),
it acts on the Malcev completion,
\hat{\pi}_{1}^{M}(X,x)
.
This finishes the proof.
∎

Hodge theory and motives

In this section,
we present our two main applications of the Malcev completeness criterion (Thm.
5.1
).
By the previous section,
for each choice of theory of enriched local systems,
we simply need to check the existence of an ambient theory (Def.
6.5
)
and that semi-simplicity is satisfied (Def.
6.14
).
For the entirety of this section,
the field of coefficients

will be
\mathbb{Q}
.
We extensively use the notation and terminology introduced in the previous section.

7.1
Variations of Hodge structures

We start with weak theory of enriched local systems

\mathcal{E}(\text{--})=\MHS(\text{--})

given by
admissible variations of mixed Hodge structures
.
(See, for example,
[
PS08
, Ch. 10,14]

Let
X\in\SmVar_{k}
and let

.
5
5

5

In this setting,

\mathcal{E}(\Spec k)=\mathcal{E}(\Spec\mathbb{C})

In this context,

\pi_{1}^{\mathcal{E}}(X,x)

Over the point, we get the category
\MHS

.

Lemma 7.1
.

(the derived category of) mixed Hodge modules

Proof.

Let

.
We write

for the category of mixed Hodge modules on

,
and
\mathcal{A}(X)
for the bounded derived category

.
The functor

is the composition of the usual inclusion

,

,
and then a shift by

.
(This is because local systems embed via
\mathcal{D}(X)
into
\mathscr{P}(X)
after a shift by

.)
Condition

)
is then clear,
and condition
(

)
is in
[

, Thm. 0.1]
.
For the realization functor

),

(denoted by

in
[

]

The isomorphism in

)
follows.

Condition

)

, Thm. 0.2]

and the definition of smooth mixed Hodge modules that precedes it.
Condition
(

)
is part of

, Thm. 0.1]
.
Finally,

has perverse cohomological amplitude

∎

Note that

.
Thus, plugging Propositions

and

.

.

]

7.2
Motivic local systems

Ivorra and Morel have constructed and studied categories of
perverse (Nori) motives
in
[
IM24
]
,
and we will briefly review their construction.
Given an additive category
\mathcal{Q}

and an additive functor
T\colon\mathcal{Q}\to\mathcal{A}

to an abelian category
\mathcal{A}
,
they construct a universal abelian category
\mathbf{A}^{\mathrm{ad}}(\mathcal{Q},T)

factoring this functor along an exact and faithful functor to
\mathcal{A}
.
Below, this universal category
\mathbf{A}^{\mathrm{ad}}(\mathcal{Q},T)
will be called
the
Nori category
of
T\colon\mathcal{Q}\to\mathcal{A}
.

Given a
k
-variety (separated and of finite type)

,
they consider the additive functor

\displaystyle{}^{p}H^{0}\circ\Bti^{*}\colon\DA_{\mathrm{ct}}(X)\to\mathcal{P}(X),

from the (triangulated) category of constructible étale motivic sheaves (with
\mathbb{Q}
-coefficients) on
X

to (
\mathbb{Q}
-linear) perverse sheaves on
X
.
We denote the Nori category of this functor by
\PM(X)
:
it is the category of
perverse motives
considered in
[
IM24
]
.
6
6

6

They denote it by
\mathscr{M}(X)
.

Here,
\Bti^{*}
is the Betti realization functor constructed by Ayoub in
[
Ayo10
]
.
We note that the category
\PM(k)
of perverse motives over the field
k

is equivalent to the classical category
\NM(k)
of Nori motives
(
[
IM24
, Prop. 2.11]
).

Note that
\NM(k)
is neutral Tannakian,
and that its Tannaka dual
\mathscr{G}^{\mathrm{mot}}(k)
is the
motivic Galois group
of
k
.
It’s a pro-algebraic group (over
\mathbb{Q}
).

Perverse motives come equipped with a realization functor
\PM(X)\to\mathcal{P}(X)
to perverse sheaves
which we denote by
b_{X}^{*}
.
Ivorra and Morel develop a four-functor formalism for perverse motives.
In
[
Ter24
]
,
this is extended to a full six-functor formalism,
with the construction of a tensor product on

.

Definition 7.4
.

Let
X
be a smooth, geometrically connected
k
-variety.
Denote the full subcategory of
\PM(X)

on objects that are sent into

It’s the category of

on

.

,

.

.
In conclusion,

(Def.

.

.

,

,

.

In summary, for

a geometrically connected, smooth

-variety
with a
k
-point

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 1}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces K(X,x)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces G(X,x)}

.

.

We follow the notation in Def.

) is true by

In
[

, Section 6]
,

This lets us show the following:

Lemma 7.6
.

The theory
\mathcal{E}(\text{--})=\MLS(\text{--})
respects semi-simplicity
(Def.

).

We need to show that

But thanks to
[

, Thm. 0.1]
,
perverse motives admit a Hodge realization,

∎

Thus,

Theorem 7.7
.

Let

with a rational point

.

The homomorphism

is Malcev complete.

and

of geometric origin is stable under extension.

every unipotent local system on

is of geometric origin.

2.

For a motivic local system
M
on

,
the Malcev completion
\hat{\pi}_{1}^{M}(X,x)

of the associated monodromy representation

∎

The second part is a (Nori) motivic refinement
of Hain’s theorem.

8
De Rham fundamental groups

We round out the paper with a slightly different kind of application.
The goal of this section is to use
the unipotent isomorphism criterion (Thm.
3.3
)
to give a quick proof of Lazda’s theorem.
The theorem says that,
for a nice enough family,
the relative unipotent de Rham fundamental group of the family
and the unipotent fundamental group of the special fibre
are isomorphic.

Let
k=F
be a field of characteristic zero,
let
S
be a smooth
k
-scheme,
let

be a smooth
S
-scheme with an
S
-point

,
let

be a closed point of

,
and write

also for the closed point of

given by

.
Denote the fibre of
X
over
s
by
X_{s}
,
and the canonical inclusion by

.
For
Y
a smooth
k
-scheme,
let

denote the category of vector bundles on

with regular integrable connection:
in short, the category of

(see for example
[
HTT08
, Def. 1.2.2, Def. 5.3.2]
).

-linear Tannakian category.

Given a morphism of smooth

-schemes

,
we have an induced functor

.
