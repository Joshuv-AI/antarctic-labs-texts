On q q -Counting of Noncrossing Chains and Parking Functions
Yen-Jen Cheng, Sen-Peng Eu, Tung-Shan Fu, Jyun-Cheng Yao

Abstract
. For a finite Coxeter group W W , Josuat-Vergès derived a q q -polynomial counting the maximal chains in the lattice of noncrossing partitions of W W by weighting some of the covering relations, which we call bad edges, in these chains with a parameter q q . We study the connection of these weighted chains with parking functions of type A A ( B B , respectively) from the perspective of the q q -polynomial. The q q -polynomial turns out to be the generating function for parking functions (of either type) with respect to the number of cars that do not park at their preferred spaces. In either case, we present a bijective result that carries bad edges to unlucky cars while preserving their relative order. Using this, we give an interpretation of the γ \gamma -positivity of the q q -polynomial in the case that W W is the hyperoctahedral group.

1.
Introduction

For a finite Coxeter group
W
of rank
n
, Josuat-Vergès
[
18
]
derived a polynomial
M(W,q)
(as shown in Theorem
1.1
)
which enumerates the maximal chains in the lattice of noncrossing partitions of
W
by weighting some of the covering relations, called
bad edges
(as defined below), in these chains with a parameter
q
.
When the polynomial specialized at
q=1
, the value in the case of symmetric group
\mathfrak{S}_{n+1}
(hyperoctahedral group

, respectively) is
(n+1)^{n-1}
(
n^{n}
, respectively), which is known to be the number of parking functions of type
A
(
B
, respectively) of size
n
. The polynomial turns out to be the generating function which counts parking functions (of either type) by the number of cars that do not park at their preferred spaces. Biane
[
6
]
established a bijection between the maximal chains in the lattice of noncrossing partitions of
\mathfrak{S}_{n+1}
(
B_{n}
, respectively) and parking functions of type
A
(
B
, respectively). However, these two bijections are not established with respect to the polynomial. In either case, we come up with a bijection that not only carries the bad edges of a maximal chain to the unlucky cars of the corresponding parking function but also preserves their relative order (Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
). A highlight of our results is that in the case of
W=B_{n}
we prove the
\gamma
-positivity of the polynomial, in terms of words over an alphabet with
n
letters (Theorem
5.1
), and give interpretations of the
\gamma
-coefficients using parking functions and the weighted chains (Theorem

).

1.1.
Noncrossing lattices of
W

Let
S
be the set of simple generators of
W
. Let
T=\{\omega s\omega^{-1}:\omega\in W,s\in S\}
, whose elements are the
reflections
in
W
with respect to roots. For any
\omega\in W
, the
length

\ell_{S}(\omega)
of
\omega
is the minimum integer
r
such that there exists an expression
\omega=s_{1}s_{2}\cdots s_{r}
with
s_{i}\in S
. Write
\omega\rightarrow\omega^{\prime}
if
\omega^{\prime}=\omega t
for some
t\in T
with
\ell_{S}(\omega)<\ell_{S}(\omega^{\prime})
.
The (
strong
)
Bruhat order

\leq_{B}
on
W
is defined by setting
\omega<_{B}\omega^{\prime}
if there is a sequence
\omega=\omega_{0}\rightarrow\omega_{1}\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow\omega_{m}=\omega^{\prime}
.
On the other hand, the
reflection length

\ell_{T}(\omega)
of
\omega
is the minimum integer
r
such that there exists an expression
\omega=t_{1}t_{2}\cdots t_{r}
with
t_{i}\in T
. The
absolute order

\leq
on
W
is defined by setting
\omega<\omega^{\prime}
if
\ell_{T}(\omega^{\prime})=\ell_{T}(\omega)+\ell_{T}(\omega^{-1}\omega^{\prime})
.

Relative to a Coxeter element
c\in W
, the
c
-
noncrossing partition lattice

{\mathrm{NC}}(W,c)
is defined as the interval in the group
W
between the identity element
e
and the Coxeter element
c
under the absolute order (e.g.
[
4
]
,
[
10
]
,
[
11
]
),

{\mathrm{NC}}(W,c):=\{\omega\in W:e\leq\omega\leq c\}.

(1.1)

The structure of
{\mathrm{NC}}(W,c)
is independent of
c
(e.g.
[
20
]
). Deligne
[
15
]
obtained the formula

\frac{n!h^{n}}{|W|},

(1.2)

where
h
is the Coxeter number of
W
, counting the number of factorizations of
c
into
n
reflections. Chapoton
[
12
, Proposition 9]
rediscovered the expression (
1.2
) by counting the maximal chains in the lattice
{\mathrm{NC}}(W,c)
. Recently, Chapuy and Douvropoulos
[
13
]
gave a case-free Coxeter-theoretic derivation of this chain number formula.

An
edge
of
{\mathrm{NC}}(W,c)
is a pair
(\mu,\nu)
of elements in
W
such that
\nu

covers

\mu
(i.e.,
\mu<\nu
and no element
\omega
with
\mu<\omega<\nu
). An edge
(\mu,\nu)
is said to be
good
(
bad
, respectively) if
\mu<_{B}\nu
(
\mu>_{B}\nu
, respectively). The notion of good and bad edges comes from
[
18
, Definition 3.1]
; see also
[
7
, Definition 4.10]
.

Given a maximal chain
{\mathbf{w}}:e=\omega_{0}<\omega_{1}<\cdots<\omega_{n}=c
in
{\mathrm{NC}}(W,c)
, let
{\mathsf{bad}}({\mathbf{w}})
denote the number of bad edges in
{\mathbf{w}}
, i.e.,
{\mathsf{bad}}({\mathbf{w}}):=\#\{(\omega_{i-1},\omega_{i}):\omega_{i-1}>_{B}\omega_{i},1\leq i\leq n\}
. Define

M(W,q):=\sum_{{\mathbf{w}}}q^{{\mathsf{bad}}({\mathbf{w}})},

(1.3)

summed over all maximal chains
{\mathbf{w}}
in
{\mathrm{NC}}(W,c)
. As a refinement of (
1.2
), Josuat-Vergès
[
18
, Proposition 3.6]
obtained the following result.

Theorem 1.1

(Josuat-Vergès)
.

We have

M(W,q)=\frac{n!}{|W|}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\big(d_{i}+q(h-d_{i})\big),

where the integers
d_{1},d_{2},\dots,d_{n}
are the fundamental degrees of
W
.

We shall study the connection of parking functions of type
A
(
B
, respectively) with the maximal chains in
{\mathrm{NC}}(W,c)
by the polynomial
M(\mathfrak{S}_{n+1},q)
(
M(B_{n},q)
, respectively).

1.2.
Parking functions of type
A

Consider a one-way street with
n
parking spaces numbered from 1 to
n
and a line of
n
cars
C_{1},\dots,C_{n}
with preferred parking spaces
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
, accordingly.
The cars arrive sequentially and each car attempts to park at its preferred space. If that space is occupied, the car moves on and parks at the next available space.
If there is no such parking space, the car leaves the street without parking.
The preference list
\alpha=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
is called a
parking function
(of type
A
) of
size

n
if all cars park successfully. For each
i
, assume that the
i
th car
C_{i}
parks at space
p_{i}
. The
n
-tuple
\pi=(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})
is called the
outcome
of
\alpha
. A car
C_{i}
is said to be
lucky
(
reluctant
, respectively) if
p_{i}=a_{i}
(
p_{i}\neq a_{i}
, respectively). Let
{\mathsf{lucky}}(\alpha)
(
{\mathsf{reluc}}(\alpha)
, respectively) denote the number of lucky (reluctant, respectively) cars of
\alpha
. Note that
{\mathsf{lucky}}(\alpha)+{\mathsf{reluc}}(\alpha)=n
. The
{\mathsf{lucky}}
statistic for parking functions was studied by Gessel and Seo
[
16
]
. We refer the readers to
[
23
]
for a survey on various aspects of parking functions.

Let
{\mathrm{PF}}^{A}_{n}
denote the set of parking functions of size
n
. In the case of
W=\mathfrak{S}_{n+1}
, we have

M(\mathfrak{S}_{n+1},q)=\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}\big(n-k+1+kq\big),

(1.4)

which coincides with the generating function for parking functions with respect to their reluctant cars (cf.
[
16
, Theorem 10.1]
), i.e.,

M(\mathfrak{S}_{n+1},q)=\sum_{\alpha\in{\mathrm{PF}}^{A}_{n}}q^{{\mathsf{reluc}}(\alpha)}.

(1.5)

Biane
[
6
]
established a bijection between the maximal chains
{\mathbf{w}}
in
{\mathrm{NC}}(\mathfrak{S}_{n+1},c)
and the parking functions
\alpha
in
{\mathrm{PF}}^{A}_{n}
. This bijection turns out to be equivalent to a result by Stanley
[
22
]
. However, this bijection does not carry the bad edges in
{\mathbf{w}}
to the reluctant cars of
\alpha
. One of our main results is the following bijection.

Theorem 1.2
.

There is a bijection
\varphi_{A}:\alpha\mapsto{\mathbf{w}}:e=\omega_{0}<\omega_{1}<\cdots<\omega_{n}=c
between the parking functions in
{\mathrm{PF}}^{A}_{n}
and the maximal chains in
{\mathrm{NC}}(\mathfrak{S}_{n+1},c)
such that the
i
th car of
\alpha
is reluctant if and only if
(\omega_{i-1},\omega_{i})
is a bad edge.

1.3.
Parking functions of type
B

Now, arrange the
n
parking spaces in a circle. There are still
n
cars with preferred parking spaces
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
, accordingly. Starting just before space 1, each car in turn moves around the circle to park at its preferred space if possible; otherwise, it moves on to the next available space. Each car will eventually park. An
n
-tuple
\alpha=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
of positive integers is a
parking function of type
B
of
size

n
if
a_{i}\leq n
for all
i
. The outcome of
\alpha
and the statistics
{\mathsf{lucky}}(\alpha)
and
{\mathsf{reluc}}(\alpha)
are defined in the same manner as that of a type-
A
parking function.
Let
{\mathrm{PF}}^{B}_{n}
denote the set of parking functions of type
B
of size
n
.

Let
[n]:=\{1,\dots,n\}
and let
[\pm n]:=\{1,2,\dots,n,-1,-2,\dots,-n\}
. A
signed permutation
of the set
[\pm n]
is a bijection
\omega
of the set onto itself such that
\omega(-i)=-\omega(i)
for all
i\in[n]
.
The
hyperoctahedral group

B_{n}
is the group of signed permutations of
[\pm n]
.
In the case of
W=B_{n}
, we have

M(B_{n},q)=\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}\big(n-k+kq\big).

(1.6)

It is easy to see that this polynomial is the generating function for parking functions of type
B
with respect to their reluctant cars, i.e.,

M(B_{n},q)=\sum_{\alpha\in{\mathrm{PF}}^{B}_{n}}q^{{\mathsf{reluc}}(\alpha)}.

(1.7)

Biane
[
6
]
gave an edge labeling of
{\mathrm{NC}}(B_{n},c)
that establishes a bijection between the maximal chains
{\mathbf{w}}
in
{\mathrm{NC}}(B_{n},c)
and the parking functions
\alpha
in
{\mathrm{PF}}^{B}_{n}
. However, this bijection is not in respect of the statistics
{\mathsf{bad}}({\mathbf{w}})
and
{\mathsf{reluc}}(\alpha)
, either. We present a bijection that carries the bad edges in
{\mathbf{w}}
to the reluctant cars of
\alpha
while preserving their relative order.

Theorem 1.3
.

There is a bijection
\varphi_{B}:\alpha\mapsto{\mathbf{w}}:e=\omega_{0}<\omega_{1}<\cdots<\omega_{n}=c
between the parking functions in
{\mathrm{PF}}^{B}_{n}
and the maximal chains in
{\mathrm{NC}}(B_{n},c)
such that the
i
th car of
\alpha
is reluctant if and only if
(\omega_{i-1},\omega_{i})
is a bad edge.

1.4.
Gamma-positivity of
M(B_{n},q)

A polynomial
C(q)=c_{0}+c_{1}q+\cdots+c_{n}q^{n}
with palindromic coefficients (i.e.,
c_{n-i}=c_{i}
) can be written as a sum of polynomials of the form
q^{j}(1+q)^{n-2j}
,

C(q)=\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\gamma_{j}\,q^{j}(1+q)^{n-2j}.

The palindromic polynomial
C(q)
is said to be
\gamma
-
positive
if
\gamma_{j}\geq 0
for all
j
.
Several of the initial polynomials of
M(B_{n},q)
are listed below:

\displaystyle M(B_{1},q)

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle M(B_{2},q)

\displaystyle=2+2q,

\displaystyle M(B_{3},q)

\displaystyle=6+15q+6q^{2},

\displaystyle M(B_{4},q)

\displaystyle=24+104q+104q^{2}+24q^{3},

\displaystyle M(B_{5},q)

\displaystyle=120+770q+1345q^{2}+770q^{3}+120q^{4}.

These terms appear in an OEIS sequence
[
21
, A071208]
. Notice that these palindromic polynomials can be expanded as follows.

\displaystyle M(B_{1},q)

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle M(B_{2},q)

\displaystyle=2(1+q),

\displaystyle M(B_{3},q)

\displaystyle=6(1+q)^{2}+3q,

\displaystyle M(B_{4},q)

\displaystyle=24(1+q)^{3}+32q(1+q),

\displaystyle M(B_{5},q)

\displaystyle=120(1+q)^{4}+290q(1+q)^{2}+45q^{2}.

For
0\leq k\leq\lfloor\frac{n-1}{2}\rfloor
, let
B(n,k)
be the set of
k
-tuples
(b_{1},b_{2},\dots,b_{k})
of integers such that
1<b_{1}<b_{2}<\cdots<b_{k}<n
and
b_{i+1}-b_{i}>1
for each
i\in[k-1]
. We present the following interpretation of
\gamma
-positivity of the polynomial
M(B_{n},q)
.

Theorem 1.4
.

For all
n\geq 1
, we have

M(B_{n},q)=\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor(n-1)/2\rfloor}\gamma_{n,j}q^{j}(1+q)^{n-1-2j},

where
\gamma_{n,j}
counts the following objects:

(i)

the parking functions
\alpha
in
{\mathrm{PF}}^{B}_{n}
with
{\mathsf{reluc}}(\alpha)=j
such that there are no two consecutive reluctant cars, and the last car of
\alpha
is lucky;

(ii)

the maximal chains
{\mathbf{w}}:e=\omega_{0}<\omega_{1}<\cdots<\omega_{n}=c
in
{\mathrm{NC}}(B_{n},c)
with
{\mathsf{bad}}({\mathbf{w}})=j
such that there are no two consecutive bad edges, and
(\omega_{n-1},\omega_{n})
is a good edge.

Moreover,
\gamma_{n,j}
can be calculated by

\gamma_{n,j}=n!\cdot\sum_{(b_{1},b_{2},\cdots,b_{j})\in B(n,j)}\prod_{i=1}^{j}\left(\frac{n-b_{i}}{b_{i}}\right).

(1.8)

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. We establish the bijections in Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
in Sections 2 and 3, respectively. To prove the
\gamma
-positivity of
M(B_{n},q)
, we prepare a bijective result, with words on an alphabet with
n
letters, for the enumeration of
\gamma
-coefficients in Section 4. The proof of Theorem
1.4
is given in Section 5.

2.
Noncrossing partitions and parking functions of type
A

2.1.
Noncrossing partitions of type
A

A
noncrossing partition
of
[n]
is a set partition with the condition that there are no
a,b,c,d
with
1\leq a<b<c<d\leq n
,
\{a,c\}
contained in some block of the partition, and
\{b,d\}
contained in some other block. The set of noncrossing partitions of
[n]
forms a lattice
{\mathrm{NC}}_{n}
, ordered by reverse refinement, with maximum element
\{\{1,2,\dots,n\}\}
and minimum element
\{\{1\},\{2\},\dots,\{n\}\}
.

Fix the symmetric group
\mathfrak{S}_{n+1}
on
[n+1]
and the Coxeter element
c=(1,2,\dots,n+1)
. If we associate every permutation
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n+1}
with the partition of
[n+1]
given by the cycle structure of
\sigma
, this defines a poset isomorphism from
{\mathrm{NC}}(\mathfrak{S}_{n+1},c)
to
{\mathrm{NC}}_{n+1}

[
5
]
. The reflections of
\mathfrak{S}_{n+1}
are the transpositions, i.e.,
T=\{(i,j):1\leq i<j\leq n+1\}
. A maximal chain
{\mathbf{w}}:e=\omega_{0}<\omega_{1}<\cdots<\omega_{n}=c
in
{\mathrm{NC}}(\mathfrak{S}_{n+1},c)
corresponds to a factorization
t_{1}t_{2}\cdots t_{n}
of
c
into
n
reflections, where
\omega^{-1}_{i-1}\omega_{i}=t_{i}\in T
.

Suppose
\delta=(d_{1},\dots,d_{m})
is a cycle of
\sigma
and
t
is a reflection with
t\leq\delta
(in absolute order), say
t=(d_{i},d_{j})
, where
i<j
. If we perform
t
and then
\sigma
, observe that
\delta
is split into two cycles
(d_{1},\dots,d_{i},d_{j+1},\dots,d_{m})
and
(d_{i+1},\dots,d_{j})
of
\sigma t
. Let
{\mathsf{cyc}}(\sigma)
denote the number of
cycles
in
\sigma
. Notice that (e.g.
[
2
, Lemma 4.1.5]
)

\ell_{T}(\sigma)=n+1-{\mathsf{cyc}}(\sigma).

(2.1)

Let
\sigma=\sigma(1)\sigma(2)\cdots\sigma(n+1)
. An
inversion
of
\sigma
is a pair
(\sigma(i),\sigma(j))
such that
\sigma(i)>\sigma(j)
,
1\leq i<j\leq n+1
. The
inversion number

{\mathsf{inv}}(\sigma)
of
\sigma
is defined to be the number of inversions of
\sigma
. It is known
[
9
, Proposition 1.5.2]
that

\ell_{S}(\sigma)={\mathsf{inv}}(\sigma).

(2.2)

Recall that
(\mu,\nu)
is a good (bad, respectively) edge in
{\mathrm{NC}}(W,c)
if
\ell_{S}(\mu)<\ell_{S}(\nu)
(
\ell_{S}(\mu)>\ell_{S}(\nu)
, respectively). By (
2.1
) and (
2.2
), we have the following observations.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
(\mu,\nu)
be an edge in
{\mathrm{NC}}(\mathfrak{S}_{n+1},c)
with
\mu^{-1}\nu=(i,j)
. We have

(i)

(\mu,\nu)
is a good edge if
{\mathsf{inv}}(\mu)<{\mathsf{inv}}(\nu)
, and a bad edge if
{\mathsf{inv}}(\mu)>{\mathsf{inv}}(\nu)
.

(ii)

The entries
i
and
j
occur in the same cycle of
\nu
, and in two different cycles of
\mu
.

Example 2.2
.

Let
\nu=2\,3\,4\,5\,6\,7\,8\,1
, written in cycle notation as
\nu=(1,2,\dots,8)
. Consider the transposition
t=(5,8)
. We obtain
\mu=\nu t=2\,3\,4\,5\,1\,7\,8\,6
, written in cycle notation as
\mu=(1,2,3,4,5)(6,7,8)
. Since
{\mathsf{inv}}(\nu)=7
and
{\mathsf{inv}}(\mu)=6
,
(\mu,\nu)
is a good edge.

The following observation will be used in Proposition
2.4
to distinguish between good and bad edges in
{\mathrm{NC}}(\mathfrak{S}_{n+1},c)
.

Lemma 2.3
.

If
\mu^{-1}\nu=(i,j)
and
i<j
, we have
{\mathsf{inv}}(\mu)>{\mathsf{inv}}(\nu)
if and only if
\nu(i)<\nu(j)
.

Proof.

Note that
\mu(i)=\nu(j)
,
\mu(j)=\nu(i)
and
\mu(k)=\nu(k)
for
k\neq i,j
. The result follows from the definition of inversion number of a permutation.
∎

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
(\mu,\nu)
be an edge of
{\mathrm{NC}}(\mathfrak{S}_{n+1},c)
with
\mu^{-1}\nu=(i,j)
, where
i<j
. Suppose
(d_{1},d_{2},\dots,d_{k})
is the cycle of
\nu
containing
i
and
j
for some integers
d_{1}<d_{2}<\cdots<d_{k}
. We have

(i)

(\mu,\nu)
is a bad edge if
i,j\in\{d_{1},\dots,d_{k-1}\}
.

(ii)

(\mu,\nu)
is a good edge if
i\in\{d_{1},\dots,d_{k-1}\}
and
j=d_{k}
.

Proof.

Note that
\nu(d_{k})=d_{1}
and
\nu(d_{r})=d_{r+1}
for each
r\in[k-1]
. By Lemmas
2.1
and
2.3
,
(\mu,\nu)
is a bad (good, respectively) edge if
\nu(i)<\nu(j)
(
\nu(i)>\nu(j)
, respectively). In the former case, we have
i,j\in\{d_{1},\dots,d_{k-1}\}
, and in the latter case
i\in\{d_{1},\dots,d_{k-1}\}
and
j=d_{k}
.
∎

Example 2.5
.

Suppose
\delta=(1,2,3,7,8)
is a cycle of an element
\nu\in{\mathrm{NC}}(\mathfrak{S}_{8},c)
and
(\mu,\nu)
is an edge with
\mu^{-1}\nu\leq\delta
.
Note that
(\mu,\nu)
is a bad edge if
\mu^{-1}\nu\in\{(1,2),(2,3),(3,7),(1,3)
,
(2,7),(1,7)\}
, and a good edge if
\mu^{-1}\nu\in\{(1,8),(2,8),(3,8),(7,8)\}
.

By Proposition
2.4
, note that there are
\binom{k}{2}
edges
(\mu,\nu)
with transposition
\mu^{-1}\nu\leq\delta
, where
\delta=(d_{1},d_{2},\dots,d_{k})
is a cycle of
\nu
. Among them,
k-1
edges are good. In the following result, these edges are partitioned into
k-1
sets with exactly one good edge in each set.

Lemma 2.6
.

Suppose
\delta=(d_{1},\dots,d_{k})
is a cycle of an element
\nu\in{\mathrm{NC}}(\mathfrak{S}_{n+1},c)
for some integers
d_{1}<\cdots<d_{k}
, let
E_{1},\dots,E_{k-1}
be a partition of the edges
(\mu,\nu)
with
\mu^{-1}\nu\leq\delta
, defined by

E_{j}:=\{(\mu,\nu):\mu^{-1}\nu=(d_{i},d_{i+j}),1\leq i\leq k-j\}.

(2.3)

Then the following properties hold.

(i)

|E_{j}|=k-j
.

(ii)

Each
E_{j}
contains exactly one good edge.

(iii)

For any
(\mu,\nu)\in E_{j}
, the cycle
\delta
is split by the transposition
\mu^{-1}\nu
into a
(k-j)
-cycle and a
j
-cycle.

Proof.

(i) That
|E_{j}|=k-j
is clear. (ii) By Proposition
2.4
, the edge
(\mu,\nu)
with
\mu^{-1}\nu=(d_{k-j},d_{k})
is the only good edge in
E_{j}
. (iii) For any
(\mu,\nu)\in E_{j}
, say
\mu^{-1}\nu=(d_{i},d_{i+j})
, we observe that
(d_{1},\dots,d_{i},d_{i+j+1},\dots,d_{k})
and
(d_{i+1},\dots,d_{i+j})
are two cycles of
\mu
.
∎

2.2.
The bijection
\varphi_{A}

In what follows we shall establish a bijection
\varphi_{A}
between the parking functions in
{\mathrm{PF}}^{A}
and the maximal chains in
{\mathrm{NC}}(\mathfrak{S}_{n+1},c)
.

Let
\alpha=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\in{\mathrm{PF}}^{A}_{n}
with outcome
\pi=(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})
. The following result shows that every entry
p_{i}
determines a decomposition of the prefix
a_{1},\dots,a_{i-1}
of
\alpha
into two parking functions of certain sizes.

Lemma 2.7
.

For all
j<i

3.
Noncrossing partitions and parking functions of type

3.1.
Noncrossing partitions of type
B

A set partition
{\mathcal{P}}
of
[\pm n]
is
invariant
under sign change if
P
is a block of
{\mathcal{P}}
then
-P
is also a block of
{\mathcal{P}}
, where
-P=\{-x:x\in P\}
. We identify
{\mathcal{P}}
with a partition of
[2n]
by sending
i\mapsto i
for
i\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}
and
i\mapsto n-i
for
i\in\{-1,-2,\dots,-n\}
.
As introduced by Reiner
[
19
]
, the lattice
{\mathrm{NC}}^{B}_{n}
of noncrossing partitions of type
B
is defined to be the sublattice of
{\mathrm{NC}}_{2n}
consisting of partitions which are invariant under sign change. An invariant block
P=-P
of a partition is called
zero block
. By the noncrossing condition, there is at most one zero block in
{\mathcal{P}}
and the nonzero blocks come in pairs.

Consider the group
B_{n}
of signed permutations. Every permutation
\omega\in B_{n}
maps to the partition of
[\pm n]
given by the cycle structure of
\omega
. With respect to the Coxeter element

c=(1,2,\dots,n,-1,-2,\dots,-n),

this map defines a poset isomorphism from
{\mathrm{NC}}(B_{n},c)
to
{\mathrm{NC}}^{B}_{n}
(see
[
4
]
,
[
10
]
,
[
11
]
).
In one-line notation, we write
\omega=\omega(1)\omega(2)\cdots\omega(n)
with bars to indicate negative numbers. We also write
\omega
in disjoint cycles. For example, if
\omega
is determined by
\omega(1)=-3
,
\omega(2)=-2
,
\omega(3)=5
,
\omega(4)=4
,
\omega(5)=6
and
\omega(6)=-1
, we write

\omega=\overline{3}\,\overline{2}\,5\,4\,6\,\overline{1}=(1,-3,-5,-6)(-1,3,5,6)(2,-2)(4)(-4).

Note that
\omega
maps to
\{\{1,-3,-5,-6\}
,
\{-1,3,5,6\}
,
\{2,-2\}
,
\{4\},\{-4\}\}
, which is a noncrossing partition of type
B
with zero block
\{2,-2\}
.

The reflections of
B_{n}
are signed permutations of the following three kinds
[
9
, Proposition 8.1.5]
:

\displaystyle(i,-i)

\displaystyle\mbox{for all $1\leq i\leq n$,}

(3.1)

\displaystyle(i,j)(-i,-j)

\displaystyle\mbox{for all $1\leq i<j\leq n$,}

\displaystyle(i,-j)(-i,j)

\displaystyle\mbox{for all $1\leq i<j\leq n$.}

A maximal chain
{\mathbf{w}}:e=\omega_{0}<\omega_{1}<\cdots<\omega_{n}=c
in
{\mathrm{NC}}(B_{n},c)
corresponds to a factorization
t_{1}t_{2}\cdots t_{n}
of
c
into
n

t_{i}=\omega^{-1}_{i-1}\omega_{i}
.

For any
\omega\in{\mathrm{NC}}(B_{n},c)
, the cycle structure of
\omega
consists of at most one cycle of the form
\delta_{0}=(d_{1},\dots,d_{k},-d_{1},\dots,-d_{k})
and some pairs of the form

\delta_{1}=(d_{1},\dots,d_{\ell},-d_{\ell+1},\dots,-d_{k})(-d_{1},\dots,-d_{\ell},d_{\ell+1},\dots,d_{k}),

(3.2)

where

and

.
Following
[

]

(

(

, respectively). Note that a reflection

if

or

for some

if

for some

Let

denote the number of paired cycles of

(3.3)

We recall some facts about signed permutations; see

for details. The inversion number of

{\mathsf{inv}}_{B}(\omega):={\mathsf{inv}}(\omega(1)\cdots\omega(n))+\sum_{\{j\in[n]\,:\,\omega(j)<0\}}|\omega(j)|.

(3.4)

For example, if

For any edge

has been created. There will be a unique empty space. In the second stage, we make an adjustment so that the parking space 0 is empty.

Algorithm E2

(i) Let

. The initial vertex labeling is given by

for all

.

•

For

, say

. If

then set
p^{\prime}_{k}=t
. Otherwise,

and set
p^{\prime}_{k}
to be the next available space, i.e., find the least positive integer
s
such that

and

p^{\prime}_{k}=\ell
.

•

Set

and relabel the vertices

in increasing order by the integers
\{0,1,\dots,n\}\setminus\{g(n-k+1),\dots,g(n)\}
.

(ii) Find the unique element in
\{0,1,\dots,n\}\setminus\{p^{\prime}_{1},\dots,p^{\prime}_{n}\}
, say
b>0
. Then the corresponding parking function
\psi^{-1}_{A}({\mathbf{x}})=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
is obtained by setting

(4.2)

Theorem 4.5
.

\psi_{B}:\alpha\mapsto{\mathbf{x}}=x_{1}\cdots x_{n}
of

(ii)

is the

. Otherwise,

for some

.

5.
Gamma-positivity of
M(B_{n},q)

In this section we prove the
\gamma
-positivity of the polynomial
M(B_{n},q)
in terms of excedance number of the words in
{\mathcal{X}}^{B}_{n}
.

Theorem 5.1
.

For all
n\geq 1
, we have

M(B_{n},q)=\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor(n-1)/2\rfloor}\gamma_{n,j}q^{j}(1+q)^{n-1-2j},

(5.1)

where
\gamma_{n,j}
is the number of words
{\mathbf{x}}=x_{1}\cdots x_{n}\in{\mathcal{X}}^{B}_{n}
with
{\mathsf{exc}}({\mathbf{x}})=j
such that
x_{1}=1
and
{\mathbf{x}}
contains no two consecutive excedances.

The generating function for the
\gamma
-coefficients
\gamma_{n,j}
can be expressed as follows.

Lemma 5.2
.

We have

\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor(n-1)/2\rfloor}\gamma_{n,j}q^{j}=\begin{cases}{\displaystyle n\cdot\prod_{k=1}^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\big(k(n-k)+(n-2k)^{2}q\big)}&\mbox{if $n$ is odd;}\\[12.91663pt]
{\displaystyle\frac{n^{2}}{2}\cdot\prod_{k=1}^{\frac{n-2}{2}}\big(k(n-k)+(n-2k)^{2}q\big)}&\mbox{if $n$ is even.}\end{cases}

(5.2)

Proof.

Using (
1.6
), express
M(B_{n},q)
as a product of the following factors

\displaystyle\big((n-k)+kq\big)\big(k+(n-k)q\big)

\displaystyle=k(n-k)+\big(k^{2}+(n-k)^{2}\big)q+k(n-k)q^{2}

\displaystyle=k(n-k)(1+q)^{2}+(n-2k)^{2}q.

We have

\displaystyle M(B_{n},q)

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}\big((n-k)+kq\big)

\displaystyle=n\cdot\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}\big((n-k)+kq\big)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}{\displaystyle n\cdot\prod_{k=1}^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\big((n-k)+kq\big)\big(k+(n-k)q\big)}&\mbox{if $n$ is odd;}\\[8.61108pt]
{\displaystyle n\left(\frac{n}{2}+\frac{nq}{2}\right)\prod_{k=1}^{\frac{n-2}{2}}\big((n-k)+kq\big)\big(k+(n-k)q\big)}&\mbox{if $n$ is even.}\end{cases}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}{\displaystyle n\cdot\prod_{k=1}^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\big(k(n-k)(1+q)^{2}+(n-2k)^{2}q\big)}&\mbox{if $n$ is odd;}\\[8.61108pt]
{\displaystyle\frac{n^{2}}{2}(1+q)\prod_{k=1}^{\frac{n-2}{2}}\big(k(n-k)(1+q)^{2}+(n-2k)^{2}q\big)}&\mbox{if $n$ is even.}\end{cases}

Thus, the result follows from collecting the terms with
q^{j}(1+q)^{n-1-2j}
.
∎

Let
{\mathcal{F}}_{n}\subset{\mathcal{X}}^{B}_{n}
be the set of words
{\mathbf{x}}=x_{1}\cdots x_{n}
such that
x_{1}=1
and
{\mathbf{x}}
contains no two consecutive excedances. We present a determinantal expression of the generating function for the words in
{\mathcal{X}}^{B}_{n}
(
{\mathcal{F}}_{n}
, respectively) with respect to excedance numbers, using a method in
[
17
, Eq. (12.4)]
.

Lemma 5.3
.

For
1\leq j\leq n-1
, let
m_{j}=(n-j)q/j
. The following results hold.

(i)

We have

M(B_{n},q)=n!\cdot\det\begin{bmatrix}m_{1}&m_{2}&m_{3}&\cdots&m_{n-1}&1\\
-1&m_{2}&m_{3}&\cdots&m_{n-1}&1\\
&-1&m_{3}&\cdots&m_{n-1}&1\\
&&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
&&&-1&m_{n-1}&1\\
&&&&-1&1\end{bmatrix}.

(5.3)

(ii)

We have

\sum_{{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{n}}q^{{\mathsf{exc}}({\mathbf{x}})}=n!\cdot\det\begin{bmatrix}0&m_{2}&m_{3}&\cdots&m_{n-1}&1\\
-1&0&m_{3}&\cdots&m_{n-1}&1\\
&-1&0&\cdots&m_{n-1}&1\\
&&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
&&&-1&0&1\\
&&&&-1&1\end{bmatrix}.

(5.4)

Proof.

(i) For any
A\subset[n-1]
, let
f(A)
be the number of words
{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathcal{X}}^{B}_{n}
with excedance set
A
. If
A=\{b_{1}<b_{2}<\cdots<b_{\ell}\}
, we observe that

f(A)=n!\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}\left(\frac{n-b_{j}}{b_{j}}\right).

(5.5)

Suppose that
M=(m_{ij})
is an
n\times n
matrix for which
m_{ij}=0
if
j<i-1
. By the permutation expansion for determinants, if
\prod_{i=1}^{n}m_{i,\sigma(i)}\neq 0
then every cycle of
\sigma
is of the form
(b,b-1,\dots,a+1,a)
. Moreover, if
m_{i,i-1}=1
for
2\leq i\leq n
then the contribution to
\det M
from the permutation
\sigma=(b_{1},b_{1}-1,\dots,2,1)(b_{2},\dots,b_{1}+1)\cdots(b_{\ell+1},\dots,b_{\ell}+1)
, where
1\leq b_{1}<b_{2}<\cdots<b_{\ell+1}=n
, is
(-1)^{n-\ell-1}m_{1,b_{1}}m_{b_{1}+1,b_{2}}\cdots m_{b_{\ell}+1,n}
. Thus, if we set
m_{i,i-1}=-1
for
2\leq i\leq n
,
m_{ij}=(n-j)q/j
for
1\leq i\leq j\leq n-1
and
m_{i,n}=1
for
1\leq i\leq n
, we derive

\displaystyle n!\cdot\det M

\displaystyle=\sum_{A\subset[n-1]}f(A)q^{|A|}

(5.6)

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathcal{X}}^{B}_{n}}q^{{\mathsf{exc}}({\mathbf{x}})}.

By (
4.2
) and (
5.6
), the assertion (i) follows.

(ii) If we set further
m_{i,i}=0
for
1\leq i\leq n-1
then the excedance sets
A=\{b_{1}<b_{2}<\cdots<b_{\ell}\}\subset[n-1]
that contribute
m_{1,b_{1}}m_{b_{1}+1,b_{2}}\cdots m_{b_{\ell}+1,n}\neq 0
to
\det M
satisfy
b_{1}>1
and
b_{j+1}-b_{j}>1
for each
j\in[\ell-1]
. Thus, the assertion (ii) follows.
∎

To evaluate the determinant in (
5.4
), we make use of Chu’s formula for the determinants of a family of tridiagonal matrices
[
14
]
.

Theorem 5.4

(Chu)
.

Let

f_{n}(x,y;u,v):=\det\begin{bmatrix}x&u&0&0&\cdots&0&0&0\\
nv&x+y&2u&0&\cdots&0&0&0\\
0&(n-1)v&x+2y&3u&\cdots&0&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&0&\cdots&2v&x+(n-1)y&nu\\
0&0&0&0&\cdots&0&v&x+ny\end{bmatrix}.

(5.7)

We have

f_{n}(x,y;u,v)=\prod_{k=0}^{n}\left(x+\frac{ny}{2}+\frac{n-2k}{2}\sqrt{y^{2}+4uv}\right).

(5.8)

To prove Theorem
5.1
, it suffices to show that the generating function for
\gamma_{n,j}
in Lemma
5.2
coincides with the polynomial in Lemma
5.3
(ii).

Proof of Theorem
5.1
.
By Lemma
5.3
(ii), we have

\displaystyle\sum_{{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{n}}q^{{\mathsf{exc}}({\mathbf{x}})}

\displaystyle=n!\cdot\det\begin{bmatrix}1&m_{2}&&&&\\
-1&1&m_{3}&&&\\
&-1&1&\ddots&&\\
&&-1&\ddots&m_{n-2}&\\
&&&\ddots&1&m_{n-1}\\
&&&&-1&1\end{bmatrix}_{(n-1)\times(n-1)}

(5.9)

\displaystyle=n\cdot\det\begin{bmatrix}1&(n-2)q&&&&\\
-1&2&(n-3)q&&&\\
&-2&3&\ddots&&\\
&&-3&\ddots&2q&\\
&&&\ddots&n-2&q\\
&&&&-(n-2)&n-1\end{bmatrix}.

(5.10)

The matrix in (
5.9
) is obtained from the matrix in (
5.4
) by subtracting the
(i+1)
th row from the
i
th row for each
i\in[n-1]
and expanding the determinant along the last column. The matrix in (
5.10
) is obtained from the matrix in (
5.9
) by multiplying the
j
th column by
j
for each
j\in[n-1]
.
Note that the transpose of the matrix in (
5.10
) coincides with the tridiagonal matrix in (
5.7
), with determinant
f_{n-2}(x,y;u,v)
, specialized at
x=1
,
y=1
,
u=-1
and
v=q
. Thus, by Theorem
5.4
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{n}}q^{{\mathsf{exc}}({\mathbf{x}})}

\displaystyle=n\cdot f_{n-2}(1,1;-1,q)

\displaystyle=n\cdot\prod_{k=0}^{n-2}\left(1+\frac{n-2}{2}+\frac{n-2-2k}{2}\sqrt{1-4q}\right)

\displaystyle=n\cdot\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}\left(\frac{n}{2}+\frac{n-2k}{2}\sqrt{1-4q}\right)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}{\displaystyle n\cdot\prod_{k=1}^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\left(\frac{n}{2}+\frac{n-2k}{2}\sqrt{1-4q}\right)\left(\frac{n}{2}-\frac{n-2k}{2}\sqrt{1-4q}\right)}&\mbox{if $n$ is odd;}\\[12.91663pt]
{\displaystyle\frac{n^{2}}{2}\cdot\prod_{k=1}^{\frac{n-2}{2}}\left(\frac{n}{2}+\frac{n-2k}{2}\sqrt{1-4q}\right)\left(\frac{n}{2}-\frac{n-2k}{2}\sqrt{1-4q}\right)}&\mbox{if $n$ is even.}\end{cases}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}{\displaystyle n\cdot\prod_{k=1}^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\big(k(n-k)+(n-2k)^{2}q\big)}&\mbox{if $n$ is odd;}\\[12.91663pt]
{\displaystyle\frac{n^{2}}{2}\cdot\prod_{k=1}^{\frac{n-2}{2}}\big(k(n-k)+(n-2k)^{2}q\big)}&\mbox{if $n$ is even.}\end{cases}

By Lemma
5.2
, we obtain the relation

\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{n}}q^{{\mathsf{exc}}({\mathbf{x}})}=\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor(n-1)/2\rfloor}\gamma_{n,j}q^{j}.

It follows that
\gamma_{n,j}
is the number of words
{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{n}
with
{\mathsf{exc}}({\mathbf{x}})=j
. Thus, the proof of Theorem
5.1
is completed. ∎

1.3
,
4.5
and

6.
Concluding Remarks

In this paper we establish a connection of parking functions of types
A
and
B
with the maximal chains in the lattice of noncrossing partitions of
W
by the polynomials
M(\mathfrak{S}_{n+1},q)
and
M(B_{n},q)
.
Some potential follow-up questions may arise. There are interesting bijections between the regions of Shi arrangement and parking functions
[
1
]
, and between the regions and the maximal chains in the lattice of noncrossing partitions
[
22
]
.
There are other objects counted by the number
(n+1)^{n-1}
, such as Cayley trees with
n+1
vertices, the critical states of the abelian sandpile model on the complete graph
K_{n+1}
, the regions in the Ish arrangement in
\mathbb{R}^{n}

[
3
]
, etc.
It would be interesting to investigate the interrelationship among these combinatorial structures from the perspective of the polynomial
M(\mathfrak{S}_{n+1},q)
.

In a study of interval partitions, Biane and Josuat-Vergés
[
8
]
generalized the polynomial
M(\mathfrak{S}_{n+1},q)
to a multivariate generating function

\sum_{{\mathbf{w}}}{\mathsf{wt}}({\mathbf{w}})=\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}\big(n-k+1+kX_{k}\big),

summed over all maximal chains
{\mathbf{w}}
in
{\mathrm{NC}}(\mathfrak{S}_{n+1},c)
, where the weight
{\mathsf{wt}}
is a monomial in the
X_{k}
. As quoted from
[
8
]
, they said “it would be interesting to investigate the existence of similar formulas for other types.”
