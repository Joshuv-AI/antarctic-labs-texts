Abstract
We propose a model to describe the adaptation of a phenotypically structured population in a H H -patch environment connected by migration, with each patch associated with a different phenotypic optimum, and we perform a rigorous mathematical analysis of this model. We show that the large-time behaviour of the solution (persistence or extinction) depends on the sign of a principal eigenvalue, λ H \lambda_{H} , and we study the dependency of λ H \lambda_{H} with respect to H H . This analysis sheds new light on the effect of increasing the number of patches on the persistence of a population, which has implications in agroecology and for understanding zoonoses; in such cases we consider a pathogenic population and the patches correspond to different host species. The occurrence of a springboard effect, where the addition of a patch contributes to persistence, or on the contrary the emergence of a detrimental effect by increasing the number of patches on the persistence, depends in a rather complex way on the respective positions in the phenotypic space of the optimal phenotypes associated with each patch. From a mathematical point of view, an important part of the difficulty in dealing with H ≥ 3 H\geq 3 , compared to H = 1 H=1 or H = 2 H=2 , comes from the lack of symmetry. Our results, which are based on a fixed point theorem, comparison principles, integral estimates, variational arguments, rearrangement techniques, and numerical simulations, provide a better understanding of these dependencies. In particular, we propose a precise characterisation of the situations where the addition of a third patch increases or decreases the chances of persistence, compared to a situation with only two patches.

Introduction

In
[
13
]
, we analysed PDE systems describing the dynamics of adaptation of a phenotypically structured population, under the effects of mutation, selection and migration in a two-patch environment, each patch being associated with a different phenotypic optimum. Consistently with current literature
[
9
,
18
,
21
]
in evolutionary biology, our analysis showed that migration between the two patches leads to a locally reduced fitness. This reduction in fitness is known as a
migration load

[
12
]
and implies decreased chances of persistence of the global population in a two-patch system compared to a single patch environment.

From an epidemiological viewpoint, the two patches can be interpreted as two different types of
hosts
(different species, or different genetic variants).
Thus, the above result means that it is more difficult for a pathogen to adapt and establish in a two-patch environment connected by migration than in a single patch environment. This observation is in agreement with one of the fundamental principles of agroecology
[
5
,
11
]
, which is that host species diversification should lead to a higher resilience of agroecosystems.

Nevertheless, as discussed in
[
16
]
, what is right for two patches may not be necessarily right for three patches or more. The presence of a third host may indeed cause a
springboard
effect, leading to higher chances of persistence of the pathogen, compared to an environment with two hosts. This is a common pattern in zoonoses. For instance the main reservoirs of influenza A virus are the aquatic birds, but it is widely accepted that these viruses need to adapt in an intermediate host (such as pig or poultry) before they lead to an outbreak in human populations
[
22
,
26
]
. Coronaviruses, including SARS-CoV-1 and SARS-CoV-2, are also zoonotic, with bats as presumed main reservoir. Intermediate hosts are also suspected to have played an important role in the 2003 and 2019 outbreaks
[
14
,
15
]
.

Up to our knowledge, there is no rigorous mathematical framework to study the effect of host diversification on the adaptation of a pathogen, when there are three hosts or more. Here, using the same assumptions as in
[
13
]
, we study a system for a phenotypically structured population, under the effects of mutation, selection and migration between several hosts. Our main goal is to check whether the introduction of a third host leads to the above-mentioned springboard effect, or whether, on the contrary, it reduces the chances of persistence of the global population compared to a situation with two hosts. Thus, we mostly focus on the case of three hosts, described by the following system:

\mathcal{A}
.

3
Effect of the main parameters on persistence

In this section, we investigate the dependence of
\lambda_{H}
with respect to the parameters
\delta>0
,
\alpha>0
,
\mu>0
, and the optima
(\mathbf{O}_{i})_{1\leq i\leq H}\in(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{H}
. It is useful to start with the “reference case”, namely
H=1
.

3.1
The reference case with a single host

In that case of a single host at position
\mathbf{O}_{1}
, there is no transfer rate, that is, (
8
) reduces to a single equation with
\delta=0
. The principal eigenpair
(\lambda_{1},G_{1})
solving

\displaystyle-\frac{\mu^{2}}{2}\Delta G_{1}(\mathbf{x})-r_{1}(\mathbf{x})\,G_{1}(\mathbf{x})=\lambda_{1}\,G_{1}(\mathbf{x})\ \hbox{ in }\mathbb{R}^{n},

(14)

is explicitly given by

\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{1}(\alpha,\mu)=\frac{\mu n\sqrt{\alpha}}{2}-r_{\max},\quad G_{1}(\mathbf{x})=G(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{O}_{1}),\quad G(\mathbf{y}):=C_{G}\,e^{-\sqrt{\alpha}\|\mathbf{y}\|^{2}/(2\mu)},

(15)

where
C_{G}>0
is a normalising positive constant that guarantees that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}G^{2}(\mathbf{y})d\mathbf{y}=1
. Notice that, as the problem (
14
) is left invariant by any translation of the phenotypic space, the eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}
does not depend on the position of the optimum
\mathbf{O}_{1}
.

3.2
The case of
H
hosts with
H\geq 2

In this subsection, we assume
H\geq 2
and denote
\lambda_{H}=\lambda_{H}(\delta,\alpha,\mu,\mathbf{O}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{O}_{H})
the principal eigenvalue of
\mathcal{A}
in
(H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap L^{2}_{w}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))^{H}
, depending on
\delta>0
,
\alpha>0
,
\mu>0
, and
(\mathbf{O}_{i})_{1\leq i\leq H}
. As above, the corresponding normalised eigenvector is denoted
\Phi=(\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{H})
. Observe that, if we consider (
8

\delta=0

H

\lambda_{H}(0,\alpha,\mu,\mathbf{O}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{O}_{H}):=\lambda_{1}(\alpha,\mu)=\frac{\mu n\sqrt{\alpha}}{2}-r_{\max}.

(16)

For later use, we define, for
1\leq i\leq H
,

G_{i}(\mathbf{x}):=G(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{O}_{i})=C_{G}\,e^{-\sqrt{\alpha}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{O}_{i}\|^{2}/(2\mu)}

and it is convenient to straightforwardly compute

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}G_{i}(\mathbf{x})\,G_{j}(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=e^{-\sqrt{\alpha}\,\|\mathbf{O}_{i}-\mathbf{O}_{j}\|^{2}/(4\mu)}.

(17)

The following first result of this section asserts that the principal eigenvalue depends continuously on the parameters, and that an increase of the migration rate
\delta>0
, or of the intensity of selection
\alpha>0
, or of the mutational parameter
\mu>0

Proposition 3.1

(Continuity and monotonicity w.r.t. parameters)
.

Let
H\geq 2
be given. The function
(\delta,\alpha,\mu,\mathbf{O}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{O}_{H})\mapsto\lambda_{H}(\delta,\alpha,\mu,\mathbf{O}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{O}_{H})

[0,+\infty)\times(0,+\infty)^{2}\times(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{H}

(\delta,\alpha,\mu)\in[0,+\infty)\times(0,+\infty)^{2}
. Furthermore,
\lambda_{H}(\delta,\alpha,\mu,\mathbf{O}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{O}_{H})
is increasing with respect to
\alpha>0
and to
\mu>0
. Lastly, either the optima
\mathbf{O}_{i}
are all identical and
\lambda_{H}(\delta,\alpha,\mu,\mathbf{O}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{O}_{H})=\lambda_{1}(\alpha,\mu)=\mu\,n\,\sqrt{\alpha}/2-r_{\max}
for all
\delta\geq 0
, or
\lambda_{H}(\delta,\alpha,\mu,\mathbf{O}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{O}_{H})
is increasing with respect to
\delta\geq 0
.

The following bounds on
\lambda_{H}
shows, in particular, that the case with many hosts is always less favourable for the population than the case with a single host.

Proposition 3.2

(Bounds on

w.r.t.

Let

Proposition 3.4

(Large migration rate)
.

Let

,

\lambda_{H}(\delta,\alpha,\mu,\mathbf{O}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{O}_{H})\nearrow\lambda_{H,\infty}(\alpha,\mu,\mathbf{O}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{O}_{H})

-\frac{\mu^{2}}{2}\Delta\varphi(\mathbf{x})-R_{H}(\mathbf{x})\,\varphi(\mathbf{x})=\lambda_{H,\infty}(\alpha,\mu,\mathbf{O}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{O}_{H})\,\varphi(\mathbf{x}),\quad R_{H}(\mathbf{x}):=\frac{1}{H}\sum_{i=1}^{H}r_{i}(\mathbf{x}),

with principal eigenfunction

\mathbf{M}:=\frac{1}{H}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{H}\mathbf{O}_{i},

\lambda_{H,\infty}(\alpha,\mu,\mathbf{O}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{O}_{H})=\lambda_{1}(\alpha,\mu)-R_{H}(\mathbf{M})+r_{\max}.

(21)

4
Effect of a third host

As seen in Proposition
3.2
, the inequality
\lambda_{1}(\alpha,\mu)=\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}=\lambda_{2}(\delta,\alpha,\mu,\mathbf{O}_{1},\mathbf{O}_{2})
is always true (as is the inequality
\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{H}
for any
H\geq 2
), meaning that the presence of a second host always penalises the population. In this section, given
\delta>0
,
\alpha>0
,
\mu>0
, we investigate how the introduction of a third host affects the chances of survival of the population, compared to the case of only two hosts. To do so, we denote
\lambda_{2}=\lambda_{2}(\mathbf{O}_{1},\mathbf{O}_{2})
the principal eigenvalue for two hosts with optima
\mathbf{O}_{1}
and
\mathbf{O}_{2}
as in (
19
), and
\lambda_{3}=\lambda_{3}(\mathbf{O}_{1},\mathbf{O}_{2},\mathbf{O}_{3})
the principal eigenvalue for three hosts with optima
\mathbf{O}_{1}
,
\mathbf{O}_{2}
and
\mathbf{O}_{3}
. Our goal is to compare
\lambda_{2}(\mathbf{O}_{1},\mathbf{O}_{2})
and
\lambda_{3}(\mathbf{O}_{1},\mathbf{O}_{2},\mathbf{O}_{3})
. As revealed in the following, the situation is very rich and the outcomes dramatically depend not only on the different parameters but also the position of the third optimum
\mathbf{O}_{3}
. In the following, we denote

\Lambda_{\delta}:=\big\{\mathbf{O}_{3}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\lambda_{3}(\mathbf{O}_{1},\mathbf{O}_{2},\mathbf{O}_{3})<\lambda_{2}(\mathbf{O}_{1},\mathbf{O}_{2})\big\}

(22)

the region in the phenotypical space where the presence of a third host corresponding to an optimum
\mathbf{O}_{3}
causes a springboard effect, leading to higher chances of persistence of the pathogen, compared to an environment with the two hosts with optima
\mathbf{O}_{1}
and
\mathbf{O}_{2}
.

We first deal in Section
4.1
with the case of small or large migration regimes (
\delta
small or large), while Section
4.2
provides some comparisons which hold for any given
\delta>0
. Some complementing numerical simulations are presented in Section
4.3
.

4.1
Large and small migration regimes

Large migration regime

Let us first consider the regime corresponding to the limit case
\delta\to+\infty
. We denote

\Lambda_{\infty}:=\{\mathbf{O}_{3}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\lambda_{3,\infty}(\mathbf{O}_{1},\mathbf{O}_{2},\mathbf{O}_{3})<\lambda_{2,\infty}(\mathbf{O}_{1},\mathbf{O}_{2})\},

(23)

where the quantities
\lambda_{H,\infty}
are defined in Proposition
3.4
(there, the dependence of
\lambda_{H,\infty}
on
\alpha
and
\mu
was also emphasised; here
\alpha>0
and
\mu>0
are fixed, and we are mainly interested in the dependence with respect to the position of the optima, this is why we use the notations
\lambda_{3,\infty}(\mathbf{O}_{1},\mathbf{O}_{2},\mathbf{O}_{3})
and
\lambda_{2,\infty}(\mathbf{O}_{1},\mathbf{O}_{2})
). We also recall that
\lambda_{1}=\mu n\sqrt{\alpha}/2-r_{\max}
is independent of
\delta
.

For two hosts, the center of mass is
\mathbf{M}_{2}=(\mathbf{O}_{1}+\mathbf{O}_{2})/2=(0,\ldots,0)=\mathcal{O}
, and the effective fitness defined in Proposition
3.4
is
R_{2}(\mathbf{x})=(r_{1}(\mathbf{x})+r_{2}(\mathbf{x}))/2=r_{\max}-\alpha\|\mathbf{x}\|^{2}/2-\alpha\beta^{2}/2
. As a result

\lambda_{2,\infty}(\mathbf{O}_{1},\mathbf{O}_{2})=\lambda_{1}-R_{2}(\mathbf{M}_{2})+r_{\max}=\lambda_{1}+\frac{\alpha\,\beta^{2}}{2}.

For three hosts, the third optimum is denoted
\mathbf{O}_{3}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})
, and the effective fitness of Proposition
3.4
is

R_{3}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{r_{1}(\mathbf{x})+r_{2}(\mathbf{x})+r_{3}(\mathbf{x})}{3}=r_{\max}-\frac{\alpha}{2}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{M}_{3}\|^{2}-\frac{\alpha}{2}\left(\frac{2}{3}\beta^{2}+\frac{2}{9}(a_{1}^{2}+\cdots+a_{n}^{2})\right),

where
\mathbf{M}_{3}:=\frac{1}{3}\mathbf{O}_{3}
is the center of mass of the triangle
\mathbf{O}_{1}\mathbf{O}_{2}\mathbf{O}_{3}
. As a result

\lambda_{3,\infty}(\mathbf{O}_{1},\mathbf{O}_{2},\mathbf{O}_{3})=\lambda_{1}-R_{3}(\mathbf{M}_{3})+r_{\max}=\lambda_{1}+\frac{\alpha}{2}\left(\frac{2}{3}\beta^{2}+\frac{2}{9}(a_{1}^{2}+\cdots+a_{n}^{2})\right).

From this, we immediately identify the asymptotic region (as
\delta\to+\infty
) where the third host increases the chances of persistence (see right column of Figure
1
).

Proposition 4.1

(Region leading to higher chances of persistence for large migration rate)
.

In the phenotypical space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the region
\Lambda_{\infty}
defined by (
23
) is the open Euclidean ball
B(\mathcal{O},\sqrt{3/2}\,\beta)
centered at the origin
\mathcal{O}
and of radius
\sqrt{3/2}\,\beta
.

Notice that both
\mathbf{O}_{1}
and
\mathbf{O}_{2}
belong to
\Lambda_{\infty}
. On the other hand,
\mathbf{O}_{3}=(0,\pm\sqrt{3}\beta,0,\ldots,0)
, which makes
\mathbf{O}_{1}\mathbf{O}_{2}\mathbf{O}_{3}
an equilateral triangle, does not belong to
\Lambda_{\infty}
. This, as revealed below (see Proposition
4.4
), is actually true for all
\delta>0
.

Remark 4.2
.

Consider the “host in the middle” situation, that is
\mathbf{O}_{3}=\mathcal{O}=(0,\ldots,0)
so that
\lambda_{3,\infty}(\mathbf{O}_{1},\mathbf{O}_{2},\mathcal{O})=\lambda_{1}+\alpha\beta^{2}/3
. We now introduce a fourth host whose optimum is denoted
\mathbf{O}_{4}=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})
. As above we compute

\lambda_{4,\infty}(\mathbf{O}_{1},\mathbf{O}_{2},\mathcal{O},\mathbf{O}_{4})=\lambda_{1}+\frac{\alpha}{2}\left(\frac{1}{2}\beta^{2}+\frac{3}{16}(b_{1}^{2}+\cdots+b_{n}^{2})\right).

Then the region where
\lambda_{4,\infty}(\mathbf{O}_{1},\mathbf{O}_{2},\mathcal{O},\mathbf{O}_{4})<\lambda_{3,\infty}(\mathbf{O}_{1},\mathbf{O}_{2},\mathcal{O})
is the ball
B(\mathcal{O},\sqrt{8/9}\,\beta)
and, this time,
\mathbf{O}_{1}
and
\mathbf{O}_{2}
do not belong to this ball.

\alpha>0
,
\mu>0
,
\beta>0
,
\mathbf{O}_{1}

\mathbf{O}_{2}

The general case

\mathbf{O}_{1}

\mathbf{O}_{2}

\mathbf{O}_{3}=(0,\pm\sqrt{3}\beta,0,\ldots,0)

Proposition 4.4

(Equilateral triangle configuration)
.

Assume that
\delta>0
,
\alpha>0
,
\mu>0
are fixed, together with
\mathbf{O}_{1}

\mathbf{O}_{2}

) with

\mathbf{O}_{3}=(0,\sqrt{3}\beta,0,\ldots,0)

\mathbf{O}_{3}=(0,-\sqrt{3}\beta,0,\ldots,0)

\lambda_{3}(\mathbf{O}_{1},\mathbf{O}_{2},\mathbf{O}_{3})>\lambda_{2}(\mathbf{O}_{1},\mathbf{O}_{2})

\mathbf{O}_{3}\not\in\overline{\Lambda_{\delta}}
.

Now, still for any fixed

,

\mathbf{O}_{1}

\mathbf{O}_{2}

\widetilde{\lambda}_{2}(\mathbf{O}_{1},\mathbf{O}_{2})
satisfying

-\frac{\mu^{2}}{2}\Delta\widetilde{\varphi}_{i}(\mathbf{x})-r_{i}(\mathbf{x})\,\widetilde{\varphi}_{i}-\delta\left(\frac{1}{2}\widetilde{\varphi}_{j}(\mathbf{x})-\widetilde{\varphi}_{i}(\mathbf{x})\right)=\widetilde{\lambda}_{2}(\mathbf{O}_{1},\mathbf{O}_{2})\,\widetilde{\varphi}_{i},

(26)

for
(i,j)=(1,2)
and
(i,j)=(2,1)
with
(\widetilde{\varphi}_{1},\widetilde{\varphi}_{2})\in\big(H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap L^{2}_{w}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\big)^{2}
the corresponding normalised principal eigenvector. Note the factor
1/2
in front of

which describes the migration loss.

Before going further on, we note that an evaluation of the Rayleigh quotient associated with
\widetilde{\lambda}_{2}(\mathbf{O}_{1},\mathbf{O}_{2})

(with

the principal eigenfunction associated with

and
\widetilde{\varphi}_{1}>0

\widetilde{\varphi}_{2}>0

\lambda_{2}(\mathbf{O}_{1},\mathbf{O}_{2})<\widetilde{\lambda}_{2}(\mathbf{O}_{1},\mathbf{O}_{2})\leq\lambda_{1}+\delta.

(27)

Proposition 4.5

(Far away third optimum)
.

5
Discussion

We proposed a model to describe the adaptation dynamics of a phenotypically structured population in a
H
-patch environment, with each patch associated with a different phenotypic optimum, and performed a rigorous mathematical analysis of this model. This analysis sheds new light on the effect of increasing the number of hosts on the persistence of a (say, pathogenic) population. As is already known with two hosts, a pathogen population will always have more difficulty persisting on multiple hosts connected by migration than on a single host (Proposition
3.2
). Furthermore, increasing the migration rate
\delta>0
further reduces the chances of population persistence (Proposition
3.1
). However, in some configurations where pathogen persistence is not possible with two hosts, adding a third host achieves persistence. In such case, the presence of a third host causes a springboard effect, by modifying the geometric configuration of the phenotypic space. Thus, as we already know experimentally with the examples of zoonoses mentioned in the Introduction, the increase in the number of hosts does not necessarily mean a decrease in the chances of persistence as long as the initial number of hosts is at least equal to 2. However, the occurrence of this springboard effect, or on the contrary of a detrimental effect of increasing the number of hosts on the persistence of the pathogen, depends in a rather complex way on the respective positions of the optimal phenotypes associated with each host. Our results, which are based on a fixed point theorem, comparison principles, integral estimates, variational arguments and rearrangement techniques, provide a better understanding of these dependencies.

In the asymptotic regimes of small and large migration rates, we were able to characterise very precisely the situations where the addition of a third host increases or decreases the chances of persistence of the pathogen, compared to a situation with only two hosts. Proposition
3.4
shows that when the migration rate is large, the system with
H
host behaves as a single population, living in a single host and with “effective” fitness equal to the mean of the
H
original fitness functions. In this case, where the population can be qualified as “generalist”, we were able to show that the addition of a third host is favourable to persistence if and only if the optimum associated with this host is inside a certain ball containing the two other optima (Proposition
4.1
). When the migration rate is low, as shown in Proposition
4.3
(and Figure
1
), the shape of the region
\Lambda_{\delta}
in the phenotypical space where the presence of a third host leads to higher chances of persistence of the pathogen is more complex and depends on the parameters. In particular, we see that this region is not necessarily convex especially if the two initial optima are far from each other. Moreover, while the addition of a third host always decreases the chances of persistence when the optima associated with the three hosts form an equilateral triangle in the phenotypic space (Proposition
4.4
), we observe that when migration is low this equilateral configuration is very close to the
\Lambda_{\delta}
region. Of course, some of these observations are only possible if the dimension of the phenotype space is at least 2. This illustrates the importance of taking pleiotropy into account (in the sense that a single mutation can affect multiple phenotypes), as already noted in
[
25
]
in the case
H=1
(but with other assumptions on the mutational operator).

In the general case (i.e., with an arbitrary migration rate
\delta
), and when the positions
\mathbf{O}_{1}
and
\mathbf{O}_{2}
of the two other hosts are fixed, we proved the existence of a position in the phenotypic space of the optimum associated with the third host maximizing the chances of pathogen persistence (Corollary
4.9
). This position is necessarily on the line
(\mathbf{O}_{1}\,\mathbf{O}_{2})
(Proposition
4.8
). However, contrary to a natural intuition, it is not necessarily located in the exact mean position between
\mathbf{O}_{1}
and
\mathbf{O}_{2}
(Proposition
4.7
). In particular, if the two initial optima
\mathbf{O}_{1}
and
\mathbf{O}_{2}
are far apart, it is more beneficial for the persistence of the pathogen to introduce a third host associated with an optimum
\mathbf{O}_{3}
that is in a neighbourhood of
\mathbf{O}_{1}
or
\mathbf{O}_{2}
. This result which is consistent with the simulations in Figure
1
shows the richness of the outcomes.

From a mathematical point of view, an important part of the difficulty in dealing with
H\geq 3
hosts, compared to
H=1
or
H=2
hosts, comes from the lack of symmetry. In the
H=2
case, provided that the initial conditions are themselves symmetric about
(\mathbf{O}_{1}+\mathbf{O}_{2})/2
, the solutions
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
remain symmetric about this point. This type of symmetry argument was used in
[
13
]
to reduce the study of the nonlinear system (
1
) to that of a linear scalar equation. Such arguments cannot be applied in the general case
H\geq 3
where we had to treat the complete nonlinear and nonlocal system. This has led to very different proofs, starting with the well-posedness of the Cauchy problem (Theorem
2.1
). The stationary states of the system illustrate well the complexity induced by the absence of symmetry: when there is persistence and
H=1,\,2
, the stationary states are proportional to the principal eigenfunctions, but this is no longer true in general in the case
H\geq 3
, because of a possible symmetry breaking (Proposition
2.3
). Again because of the lack of symmetry, the populations at equilibrium have no reason to be equal in each host. Even when
H=2
, the eigenfunctions are not radially symmetric (they are biased towards the other optimum, see formula 46 in
[
13
]
). When
H\geq 3
, the qualitative description of their profile is even more involved, leading to new difficulties in the comparison between different geometric configurations of the positions of the optima. Although we were able to show that the “best position” of
\mathbf{O}_{3}
when
\mathbf{O}_{1}
and
\mathbf{O}_{2}
are fixed should belong to the line
(\mathbf{O}_{1}\,\mathbf{O}_{2})
, the rearrangement techniques
that we used for this result did not allow to determine the best position exactly. This is because the rearrangement result is only true in the directions where
\mathbf{O}_{1}
and
\mathbf{O}_{2}
are stable by rearrangement (and thus not in the direction
x_{1}
with our conventions; otherwise the position
\mathbf{O}_{3}=(\mathbf{O}_{1}+\mathbf{O}_{2})/2
would always be optimal, which would contradict the result of the Proposition
4.7
). Note that the rearrangement inequalities used in the proof of Proposition
4.8
are not standard, because of the unbounded domain and of the sign changes of
r_{i}
.

As mentioned in the Introduction, one of the fundamental principles in agroecology is to promote diversified agroecosystems rather than uniform cultures. Our results show, with a rigorous mathematical analysis, that what is true when moving from a monoculture to a varietal mixture is not always true when moving from a varietal mixture with
H\geq 2
species to a mixture with
H+1
species. In such a situation, it is necessary to know the respective positions of the optima associated with the different hosts, from the point of view of the pathogen, to predict the positive or negative impact of diversification. From an experimental point of view, it would therefore be necessary to be able to estimate these positions, by comparing, for example, via cross-inoculation experiments, the effect of pre-adaptation of a pathogen on each host
i
before its introduction on a host
j
, for each pair
i,j
. Of course, the vision here, where each host is described by a unique optimum (FGM model) and where the different parameters do not depend on the host
i
is very schematic, and the comparison with quantitative data would require more complex approaches. However, the model (
1
) has the advantage of being mathematically tractable while capturing the antagonistic effects of host diversification.

and since

satisfies the same properties as the restriction of

to this set, one gets that these two restrictions are equal. Therefore, the function
\mathbf{u}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{H})
extended by

in

(provided this set is not empty), is then of class

for all
(t,\mathbf{x})\in[0,T^{\prime}_{2}]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
1\leq i\leq H
, and the functions

are in

. Since this argument holds for every

\mathbf{u}
can then be extended in
[0,T_{2})\times\mathbb{R}^{n}

T_{2}-T_{1}=\big(A_{1}+A_{2}Ke^{(r_{\max}+1)T_{1}}+A_{3}K^{2}e^{2(r_{\max}+1)T_{1}}\big)^{-1},

and the above properties hold with
[0,T^{\prime}_{2}]
and
(0,T^{\prime}_{2}]
replaced by
[0,T_{2})
and
(0,T_{2})

By an immediate induction, there exists a sequence

of positive real numbers such that, for every

T_{m+1}-T_{m}=\big(A_{1}+A_{2}Ke^{(r_{\max}+1)T_{m}}+A_{3}K^{2}e^{2(r_{\max}+1)T_{m}}\big)^{-1}

(46)

and

can be extended as a

0\leq u_{i}(t,x)\leq K\,e^{(r_{\max}+1)t-\theta\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert}
for all
(t,\mathbf{x})\in[0,T_{m})\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
1\leq i\leq H
, and the functions

are in

would converge to

T_{m}\to+\infty
and the function
\mathbf{u}
is then defined in

2.1
.

Finally, if
\mathbf{v}

listed in Theorem
2.1

\displaystyle\qquad+\delta\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}b^{2}\varphi_{2}^{2}+a^{2}\varphi_{1}\varphi_{2}-ab\varphi_{1}\varphi_{2}-ab\varphi_{2}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a^{2}\lambda_{2}+b^{2}\left(\lambda_{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi_{2}^{2}+\delta\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2})\varphi_{2}\right)+b^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(r_{2}-r_{3})\varphi_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+\delta\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}b^{2}\varphi_{2}^{2}+a^{2}\varphi_{1}\varphi_{2}-ab\varphi_{1}\varphi_{2}-ab\varphi_{2}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda_{2}+\delta\left((b^{2}+a^{2}-ab)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi_{1}\varphi_{2}-ab\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi_{2}^{2}\right)+b^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(r_{2}-r_{3})\varphi_{2}^{2}.

We now select
a=b=\sqrt{2/3}
and thus obtain

\lambda_{3}-\lambda_{2}\leq\frac{2\delta}{3}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi_{1}\varphi_{2}-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi_{2}^{2}\right)+\frac{2}{3}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(r_{2}-r_{3})\varphi_{2}^{2}.

Since the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality yields
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi_{1}\varphi_{2}-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi_{2}^{2}<0
(
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
are not colinear because
\beta\neq 0
) and since the dominated convergence theorem implies that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(r_{2}-r_{3})\varphi_{2}^{2}\to 0
as
\rho\to 0
, we have
\lambda_{3}-\lambda_{2}<0
if
\rho>0
is small enough.
∎

Proof of Proposition
4.7
.

First, from Proposition
3.2
and Corollary
3.3
, we know that, whatever the position of
\mathbf{O}_{3}
, we have

\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{1}+\delta\left(1-\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq 3}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi_{i}(\mathbf{x})\varphi_{j}(\mathbf{x})d\mathbf{x}\right)\leq\lambda_{3}\leq\lambda_{1}+\delta\left(1-\frac{e^{-\sqrt{\alpha}\beta^{2}/\mu}}{3}\right).

(73)

Set
\psi_{i}=\varphi_{i}(\cdot+\mathbf{O}_{i}).
We have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\lambda_{3}&\!\!\!\!=&\!\displaystyle-r_{\max}\!+\!\sum_{i=1}^{3}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\!\!\left(\!\frac{\mu^{2}}{2}\|\nabla\psi_{i}(\mathbf{x})\|^{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2}\,\|\mathbf{x}\|^{2}(\psi_{i}(\mathbf{x}))^{2}\!\right)\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\!+\!\delta\!-\!\delta\!\!\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq 3}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\!\!\varphi_{i}(\mathbf{x})\varphi_{j}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\\
&\!\!\!\!\geq&\displaystyle-r_{\max}+\sum_{i=1}^{3}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\!\!\left(\frac{\mu^{2}}{2}\|\nabla\psi_{i}(\mathbf{x})\|^{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2}\,\|\mathbf{x}\|^{2}\,(\psi_{i}(\mathbf{x}))^{2}\right)\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}.\end{array}

Thus, from (
73
), we obtain that

\sum_{i=1}^{3}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{\alpha}{2}\,\|\mathbf{x}\|^{2}\,(\psi_{i}(\mathbf{x}))^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\leq r_{\max}+\lambda_{1}+\delta\left(1-\frac{e^{-\sqrt{\alpha}\beta^{2}/\mu}}{3}\right).

Considering any radius
R>0
, the last inequality implies that

\|\psi_{i}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B(\mathcal{O},R))}^{2}\leq\frac{2\,(r_{\max}+\lambda_{1}+\delta)}{\alpha\,R^{2}}

for every
\beta>0
and
1\leq i\leq 3
.

Now, consider the particular case where
\mathbf{O}_{3}=\mathcal{O}=(0,\ldots,0)
, so that the Euclidean distance between two optima is at least
\beta
. In this case,
\psi_{3}=\varphi_{3}
and we have, for every
R>0
and
\beta\geq 2R
,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi_{1}(\mathbf{x})\,\varphi_{3}(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\psi_{1}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{O}_{1})\,\psi_{3}(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\int_{B(\mathcal{O},R)}\psi_{1}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{O}_{1})\,\psi_{3}(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B(\mathcal{O},R)}\psi_{1}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{O}_{1})\,\psi_{3}(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle\leq\|\psi_{1}\|_{L^{2}(B(\mathbf{O}_{1},R))}\|\psi_{3}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+\|\psi_{1}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\,\|\psi_{3}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B(\mathcal{O},R))}

\displaystyle\leq\|\psi_{1}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B(\mathcal{O},R))}\|\psi_{3}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+\|\psi_{1}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\,\|\psi_{3}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B(\mathcal{O},R))}

\displaystyle\leq\|\psi_{1}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B(\mathcal{O},R))}+\|\psi_{3}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B(\mathcal{O},R))}

since
B(\mathbf{O}_{1},R)\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B(\mathcal{O},R)
and
\|\psi_{i}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=\|\varphi_{i}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq 1
for each
1\leq i\leq 3
. As a result

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi_{1}(\mathbf{x})\varphi_{3}(\mathbf{x})d\mathbf{x}\leq\frac{\sqrt{8\,(r_{\max}+\lambda_{1}+\delta)}}{R\,\sqrt{\alpha}}.

Applying the same arguments, we finally get

\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq 3}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi_{i}(\mathbf{x})\,\varphi_{j}(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\leq\frac{\sqrt{72\,(r_{\max}+\lambda_{1}+\delta)}}{R\,\sqrt{\alpha}}.

Together with (
73
), this shows that

\lambda_{3}(\mathbf{O}_{1},\mathbf{O}_{2},\mathcal{O})\to\lambda_{1}+\delta\hbox{ as }\beta\to+\infty.

Consider now the case
\mathbf{O}_{3}=\mathbf{O}_{1}
. We note that
\lambda_{3}(\mathbf{O}_{1},\mathbf{O}_{2},\mathbf{O}_{1})=\lambda_{3}(\mathbf{O}_{1},\mathbf{O}_{1},\mathbf{O}_{2})
. Then, using the result of Proposition
4.5
, we obtain that
\lambda_{3}(\mathbf{O}_{1},\mathbf{O}_{1},\mathbf{O}_{2})\to\widetilde{\lambda}_{2}(\mathbf{O}_{1},\mathbf{O}_{1})
as
\beta\to+\infty
, where
\widetilde{\lambda}_{2}(\mathbf{O}_{1},\mathbf{O}_{1})
is defined by (
26
), in the particular case where the two optima are at the same position. In such case,
\widetilde{\varphi}_{1}=\widetilde{\varphi}_{2}
by uniqueness and therefore, from (
26
),

-\frac{\mu^{2}}{2}\Delta\widetilde{\varphi}_{1}-r_{1}(\mathbf{x})\widetilde{\varphi}_{1}+\frac{\delta}{2}\,\widetilde{\varphi}_{1}=\widetilde{\lambda}_{2}(\mathbf{O}_{1},\mathbf{O}_{1})\,\widetilde{\varphi}_{1},

so that
\widetilde{\lambda}_{2}(\mathbf{O}_{1},\mathbf{O}_{1})=\lambda_{1}+\delta/2
, and we are done.
∎

Proof of Proposition
4.8
.

We need to recall the notion of Steiner symmetrisation of a function with respect to the variable
x_{k}
. Consider first a measurable function
h:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
,
x\mapsto h(x)
, which is either nonnegative and belongs to
L^{p}(\mathbb{R})
for some
1\leq p<\infty
, or which is such that
h(x)\to\inf_{\mathbb{R}}h\in[-\infty,+\infty)
as
|x|\to+\infty
. Then there exists a unique (in the class of functions which are identical almost everywhere) function
h^{\sharp}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
,
x\mapsto h^{\sharp}(x)
, such that:
(i)

h^{\sharp}
is symmetric with respect to
x=0
and nonincreasing in
[0,+\infty)
, i.e., for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
,
h^{\sharp}(x)\geq h^{\sharp}(y)
if
|x|\leq|y|
;
(ii)

h^{\sharp}
has the same distribution function as
h
, that is,

\hbox{meas }\{x\in\mathbb{R}:h^{\sharp}(x)>\alpha\}=\hbox{meas }\{x\in\mathbb{R}:h(x)>\alpha\}

for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, where meas denotes the one-dimensional Lebesgue measure.

Consider now a measurable function
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
,
\mathbf{x}\mapsto h(\mathbf{x})
, which is either nonnegative and belongs to
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for some
1\leq p<\infty
, or which is such that
h(\mathbf{x})\to\inf_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h\in[-\infty,+\infty)
as
\|\mathbf{x}\|\to+\infty
. For
1\leq k\leq n
and
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k-1},x_{k+1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
, the measurable function
x_{k}\mapsto h(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k},\ldots,x_{n})
is either nonnegative and belongs to
L^{p}(\mathbb{R})
(for almost every
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k-1},x_{k+1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
), or is such that
h(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k},\ldots,x_{n})\to\inf_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h=\inf_{\mathbb{R}}h(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k-1},\cdot,x_{k+1},\ldots,x_{n})\in[-\infty,+\infty)
as
|x_{k}|\to+\infty
. We can then rearrange the function
x_{k}\mapsto h(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k},\ldots,x_{n})
as above. This corresponds to the Steiner rearrangement of
h
with respect to the variable
x_{k}
, and we denote it by
h^{\sharp_{k}}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
,
\mathbf{x}\mapsto h^{\sharp_{k}}(\mathbf{x})
.

In the sequel, we use some known properties of this rearrangement, which we briefly recall. Firstly, for any nonnegative function
h\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
1\leq p<\infty
, the nonnegative function
h^{\sharp_{k}}
belongs to
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
too, and

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h^{p}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(h^{\sharp_{k}})^{p}.

(74)

Secondly, for any nonnegative
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
functions
h
and
j
, the Hardy-Littlewood inequality asserts that

0\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h\,j\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h^{\sharp_{k}}\,j^{\sharp_{k}}.

(75)

Thirdly, the Pólya-Szegö inequality says that, for any nonnegative function
h\in W^{1,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
1\leq p<\infty
, the function
h^{\sharp_{k}}
belongs to
W^{1,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
too, and

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\|\nabla h\|^{p}\geq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\|\nabla h^{\sharp_{k}}\|^{p}.

(76)

Equipped with this, we are in position to prove the result in Proposition
4.8
. For the proof, without loss of generality; using the notation (
19
) and the fact that the map
\mathbf{O}_{3}\mapsto\lambda_{3}(\mathbf{O}_{1},\mathbf{O}_{2},\mathbf{O}_{3})
is invariant by rotation around the axis
\mathbb{R}\times\{0\}^{n-1}
, we can assume that
\mathbf{O}_{3}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\{0\}^{n-2}
. Let
(\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2},\varphi_{3})\in(H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap L^{2}_{w}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))^{3}
be the normalised positive eigenvector associated with
\lambda_{3}=\lambda_{3}(\mathbf{O}_{1},\mathbf{O}_{2},\mathbf{O}_{3})
. Remember from (
9
) that

\lambda_{3}=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(\frac{\mu^{2}}{2}\|\nabla\varphi_{i}\|^{2}-r_{i}\,\varphi_{i}^{2}\right)+\delta-\delta\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\varphi_{1}\,\varphi_{2}+\varphi_{2}\,\varphi_{3}+\varphi_{1}\,\varphi_{3}).

(77)

Consider
(\varphi_{1}^{\sharp_{2}},\varphi_{2}^{\sharp_{2}},\varphi_{3}^{\sharp_{2}})
the Steiner symmetrisations of the eigenfunctions with respect to the variable
x_{2}
. From (
74
), we know that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\varphi_{1}^{\sharp_{2}})^{2}+(\varphi_{2}^{\sharp_{2}})^{2}+(\varphi_{3}^{\sharp_{2}})^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi_{1}^{2}+\varphi_{2}^{2}+\varphi_{3}^{2}=1.

(78)

Additionally, from (
75
), we have

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\varphi_{1}\,\varphi_{2}+\varphi_{2}\,\varphi_{3}+\varphi_{1}\,\varphi_{3})\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\varphi_{1}^{\sharp_{2}}\,\varphi_{2}^{\sharp_{2}}+\varphi_{2}^{\sharp_{2}}\,\varphi_{3}^{\sharp_{2}}+\varphi_{1}^{\sharp_{2}}\,\varphi_{3}^{\sharp_{2}}).

(79)

Now, we have to prove that, for each
i=1,2,3
, the function
\varphi_{i}^{\sharp_{2}}
belongs to
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap L^{2}_{w}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. First of all, it belongs to
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, from (
74
) and (
76
). Let us now show that the integral
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\|\mathbf{x}\|^{2}\,(\varphi_{i}^{\sharp_{2}}(\mathbf{x}))^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}
converges. To do so, for any
R>0
, call
b_{R}
the nonnegative
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
function defined by
b_{R}(\mathbf{x}):=\max(R^{2}-\|\mathbf{x}\|^{2},0)
and observe that

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\min(\|\mathbf{x}\|^{2},R^{2})\,(\varphi_{i}(\mathbf{x}))^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}&=&\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}R^{2}\,(\varphi_{i}(\mathbf{x}))^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}b_{R}(\mathbf{x})\,(\varphi_{i}(\mathbf{x}))^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\\
&\geq&\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}R^{2}\,(\varphi_{i}^{\sharp_{2}}(\mathbf{x}))^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}b_{R}^{\sharp_{2}}(\mathbf{x})\,(\varphi_{i}^{\sharp_{2}}(\mathbf{x}))^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\end{array}

from (
74
)-(
75
) (we here use the fact that
\varphi_{i}^{2}
belongs to
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, since it is continuous and decays to
0
faster than exponentially as
\|\mathbf{x}\|\to+\infty
). Since
b_{R}^{\sharp_{2}}=b_{R}
, one then gets that

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\|\mathbf{x}\|^{2}\,(\varphi_{i}(\mathbf{x}))^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}&\!\!\!\geq&\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\min(\|\mathbf{x}\|^{2},R^{2})\,(\varphi_{i}(\mathbf{x}))^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\\
&\!\!\!\geq&\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(R^{2}-b_{R}(\mathbf{x}))\,(\varphi_{i}^{\sharp_{2}}(\mathbf{x}))^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\min(\|\mathbf{x}\|^{2},R^{2})\,(\varphi_{i}^{\sharp_{2}}(\mathbf{x}))^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}.\end{array}

The monotone convergence theorem then yields the convergence of
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\|\mathbf{x}\|^{2}\,(\varphi_{i}^{\sharp_{2}}(\mathbf{x}))^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}
. Thus,
\varphi_{i}^{\sharp_{2}}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap L^{2}_{w}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, for each
i=1,2,3
.

Next, we claim that, for each
i=1,2,3
, the Hardy-Littlewood inequality can be applied to the couple
(r_{i},\varphi_{i}^{2})
, although
r_{i}
does not have a constant sign in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, namely we claim that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}r_{i}\,\varphi_{i}^{2}\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}r_{i}^{\sharp_{2}}\,(\varphi_{i}^{2})^{\sharp_{2}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}r_{i}^{\sharp_{2}}\,(\varphi_{i}^{\sharp_{2}})^{2}.

(80)

Notice first that all integrals in (
80
) converge since the functions
\varphi_{i}
and
\varphi_{i}^{\sharp_{2}}
belong to

and since
(r_{1}^{\sharp_{2}},r_{2}^{\sharp_{2}},r_{3}^{\sharp_{2}})=(r_{1},r_{2},r_{\mathbf{O}_{3}^{\sharp}})
, where

r_{\mathbf{O}_{3}^{\sharp}}(\mathbf{x})=r_{\max}-\alpha\,\frac{\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{O}_{3}^{\sharp}\|^{2}}{2}

(the equality
r_{3}^{\sharp_{2}}=r_{\mathbf{O}_{3}^{\sharp}}

r_{i}^{K}(\mathbf{x}):=r_{i}(\mathbf{x})+K
if
r_{i}(\mathbf{x})+K>0
and
r_{i}^{K}(\mathbf{x}):=0
otherwise, that is,
r_{i}^{K}(\mathbf{x})=\max(r_{i}(\mathbf{x})+K,0)
. The function
r_{i}^{K}
is continuous and compactly supported, hence it is in

. From (
75
) applied to the nonnegative
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
functions
r_{i}^{K}
and
\varphi_{i}^{2}
, together with the property

As
(r_{i}^{K})^{\sharp_{2}}=\max(r_{i}^{\sharp_{2}}+K,0)
by definition of

in (

\int_{r_{i}+K>0}r_{i}\,\varphi_{i}^{2}+K\int_{r_{i}+K>0}\varphi_{i}^{2}-K\int_{r_{i}^{\sharp_{2}}+K>0}(\varphi_{i}^{\sharp_{2}})^{2}\leq\int_{r_{i}^{\sharp_{2}}+K>0}r_{i}^{\sharp_{2}}\,(\varphi_{i}^{\sharp_{2}})^{2}.

(81)

It also follows from (

Next, we note that there are some positive constants

\sum_{i=1}^{3}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\|\nabla\varphi_{i}\|^{2}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{3}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\|\nabla\varphi_{i}^{\sharp_{2}}\|^{2}.

(84)
