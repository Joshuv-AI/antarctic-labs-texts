A remark on the Whitney Broken Circuit Theorem
Paula M. S. Fialho, Emanuel Juliano, Aldo Procacci

Abstract
In the present note we show, via the connection between chromatic polynomial and Potts model, that the Whitney Broken circuit theorem is in fact a special case of a more general identity relating the chromatic polynomial of a graph 𝔾 = ( 𝕍 , 𝔼 ) {\mathbb{G}}=({\mathbb{V}},{\mathbb{E}}) to sums over forests of 𝔾 {\mathbb{G}} associated to some partition scheme in 𝔾 {\mathbb{G}} .



1
Introduction

Along this paper,
{\mathbb{G}}=({\mathbb{V}},{\mathbb{E}})
represents a fixed simple graph with vertex set
{\mathbb{V}}
and edge set
{\mathbb{E}}
. For any subset
R\subset{\mathbb{V}}
, we denote by
{\mathbb{G}}|_{R}
the restriction of
{\mathbb{G}}
to
R
, i.e.
{\mathbb{G}}|_{R}=(R,{\mathbb{E}}|_{R})
where
{\mathbb{E}}|_{R}=\{\{x,y\}\subset R:\{x,y\}\in{\mathbb{E}}\}
.
A graph
{\mathbb{G}}^{\prime}=({\mathbb{V}}^{\prime},{\mathbb{E}}^{\prime})
is a subgraph of
{\mathbb{G}}=({\mathbb{V}},{\mathbb{E}})
if
{\mathbb{V}}^{\prime}\subseteq{\mathbb{V}}
and
{\mathbb{E}}^{\prime}\subseteq{\mathbb{E}}
. A subgraph
{\mathbb{G}}^{\prime}

of
{\mathbb{G}}
is called spanning if
{\mathbb{V}}^{\prime}={\mathbb{V}}
. A graph
{\mathbb{G}}=({\mathbb{V}},{\mathbb{E}})

is
connected

if for any pair
B,C
of subsets of
{\mathbb{V}}
such that

B\cup C={\mathbb{V}}
and
B\cap C=\emptyset
, there is at least an edge
e\in{\mathbb{E}}
such
that
e\cap B\neq\emptyset
and
e\cap C\neq\emptyset
. A tree is a connected graph with no cycles. A subset
R\subset{\mathbb{V}}
is said to be connected if
{\mathbb{G}}|_{R}
is connected; we denote by
\mathrm{C}_{\mathbb{V}}
the set of all connected subsets of
{\mathbb{G}}
with cardinality greater than one, i.e.

\mathrm{C}_{\mathbb{V}}=\{R\subset{\mathbb{V}}:\;\mbox{${\mathbb{G}}|_{R}$ is connected and $|R|\geq 2$}\}.

(1.1)

Given
R\in\mathrm{C}_{\mathbb{V}}
, let us represent by
\mathcal{C}_{{\mathbb{G}}|_{R}}
the set of all connected spanning subgraphs of
{{\mathbb{G}}|_{R}}
and by

\mathcal{T}_{{\mathbb{G}}|_{R}}\subset\mathcal{C}_{{\mathbb{G}}|_{R}}
the set of all spanning trees of
{\mathbb{G}}|_{R}
.

Given
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we set
[n]=\{1,2,\dots,n\}
and given a finite set
X
, we let
|X|
denote its cardinality. A coloring of
the vertex set
{\mathbb{V}}
of a graph
{\mathbb{G}}=({\mathbb{V}},{\mathbb{E}})
using
q\in\mathbb{N}
colors is a function
\kappa:{\mathbb{V}}\to[q]
and we denote by
{\mathcal{K}}_{\mathbb{V}}(q)
the set of all colorings of
\mathbb{V}
with
q
colors. Clearly
\left|{\mathcal{K}}_{\mathbb{V}}(q)\right|=q^{\left|{\mathbb{V}}\right|}
. A coloring
\kappa\in{\mathcal{K}}_{\mathbb{V}}(q)
is called
proper
if for any edge
\{x,y\}\in{\mathbb{E}}
it holds that

\kappa(x)\neq\kappa(y)
. We represent by
{\mathcal{K}}^{*}_{\mathbb{V}}(q)
the set of all proper colorings of
{\mathbb{V}}
with
q
colors.
The quantity
P_{\mathbb{G}}(q):=|{\mathcal{K}}^{*}_{\mathbb{V}}(q)|
, i.e. the number of proper colorings with
q
colors of the graph
\mathbb{G}
is, as a function of
q
, a polynomial known as the
chromatic polynomial
of
{\mathbb{G}}
. The classical representation of the chromatic polynomial
P_{\mathbb{G}}(q)
of a graph
{\mathbb{G}}=({\mathbb{V}},{\mathbb{E}})
is as follows.

P_{\mathbb{G}}(q)=\sum_{E\subset{\mathbb{E}}}(-1)^{|E|}q^{k(E)}

(1.2)

where
k(E)
is the number of connected components of the graph
({\mathbb{V}},E)
(isolated vertices are considered trivial connected components, therefore are included).

An alternative representation of
P_{\mathbb{G}}(q)
, known under the name of Whitney’s broken circuit theorem, was derived by Whitney
[
15
]
. To state this theorem we need to introduce some notations regarding forests of a graph
{\mathbb{G}}
.

A connected component of a graph is said non trivial if the cardinality of its vertex set is greater than one, i.e., it contains at least one edge.
A forest in
{\mathbb{G}}=({\mathbb{V}},{\mathbb{E}})
is a subset
F\subset{\mathbb{E}}
such that all non trivial connected components of the graph
({\mathbb{V}},F)
are trees.
We denote by
\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{G}}
the set of
all forests that are subgraphs of
{\mathbb{G}}
and we agree that the empty set belongs to
\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{G}}
, namely
({\mathbb{V}},\emptyset)
is seen as a forest in
{\mathbb{G}}
.
Fix now an ordering on the edge set
{\mathbb{E}}
of the graph
{\mathbb{G}}
. A circuit
C
of
{\mathbb{G}}
is a sequence of edges

\{e_{1},\dots,e_{n}\}
such that
e_{n}
and
e_{1}
are adjacent, as well as
e_{i}
and
e_{i+1}
for all
i\in[n-1]
. A circuit can also be seen as a subset of
{\mathbb{E}}
. A circuit
C=\{e_{1},\dots,e_{n}\}
is called simple if it does not contain any repeated edges or vertices, except for the vertex
v=e_{n}\cap e_{1}
.
Given a simple circuit

C=\{e_{1},\dots,e_{n}\}
, the set
C\setminus e
is called a broken circuit of
{\mathbb{G}}
if
e=\max\{e_{1},\dots,e_{n}\}
(where the maximum is in respect to the ordering of
{\mathbb{E}}
previously fixed). A forest
F\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{G}}
is said to be
broken circuit free
if it does not contain any broken circuit. Let us represent by
\mathcal{F}^{*}_{\mathbb{G}}
the set of all broken circuit free forests of
{\mathbb{G}}
, then the Whitney’s broken circuit theorem can now be stated as follows.

Theorem 1.1
(Whitney
[
15
]
)

Given a graph
{\mathbb{G}}=({\mathbb{V}},{\mathbb{E}})
, we have

P_{\mathbb{G}}(q)=q^{|{\mathbb{V}}|}\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}^{*}_{\mathbb{G}}}\left(-{1\over q}\right)^{|F|}.

(1.3)

Whitney’s broken circuit theorem is relevant because it provides
a simple combinatorial interpretation of the coefficients of the chromatic polynomial. Many papers are based on this theorem and several generalization (to hypergraphs, matroids, lattices etc.) have been given (see e.g.
[
3
]
and references therein). In particular,
Theorem
1.1
has recently been used in
[
14
]
to prove that the number of list-colorings of a graph
{\mathbb{G}}=({\mathbb{V}},{\mathbb{E}})
is not less than that of ordinary
q
-colorings of
{\mathbb{G}}
provided that
q>1.135|{\mathbb{E}}|
. Even more recently, the broken circuit theorem has been used in

[
7
]
to obtain the best bounds for the zero-free region of the chromatic polynomial. In
[
7
]
the authors show
that for any
graph
{\mathbb{G}}
with maximum degree
\Delta
, the chromatic polynomial
P_{\mathbb{G}}(q)

is free of zeros outside the complex disk
|q|<5.94\Delta
. Their result improves the previous bounds given in
[
13
]
and
[
5
]
which
were obtained instead via the connection between
P_{\mathbb{G}}(q)
and the partition function of the antiferromagnetic Potts model at zero temperature.

In this note we show that the broken circuit theorem, i.e. Theorem
1.1
, is in fact a special case of a more general representation of
P_{\mathbb{G}}(q)
in terms of sum over suitable forest of
{\mathbb{G}}
.
To state our theorem, we need to introduce a so-called
partition scheme in
{\mathbb{G}}
(see e.g. Sec. 4 of
[
6
]
and references therein) according to
the following definition.

Definition 1.2

Given
R\in\mathrm{C}_{\mathbb{V}}
, a partition scheme in
{\mathbb{G}}|_{R}
is a map
{\mathrm{m}_{R}}:\mathcal{T}_{{\mathbb{G}}|_{R}}\to\mathcal{C}_{{\mathbb{G}}|_{R}}

such that for all
\tau\in\mathcal{T}_{{\mathbb{G}}|_{R}}
, it holds that

\tau\subset{\mathrm{m}_{R}}(\tau)
and

\mathcal{C}_{{\mathbb{G}}|_{R}}=\biguplus_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}_{{\mathbb{G}}|_{R}}}[\tau,\mathrm{m}_{R}(\tau)]
, where
\biguplus
means disjoint union
and
[\tau,\mathrm{m}_{R}(\tau)]=\{g\in\mathcal{C}_{{\mathbb{G}}|_{R}}:\tau\subseteq g\subseteq{\mathrm{m}_{R}(\tau)}\}
. Having established, for any
R\subset\mathrm{C}_{\mathbb{V}}
, a partition scheme
\mathrm{m}_{R}
on
{\cal C}_{{\mathbb{G}}|_{R}}
, the family
{\mathrm{\mathbf{m}}}=\{\mathrm{m}_{R}\}_{R\in\mathrm{C}_{\mathbb{V}}}
is said to be a partition scheme in
{\mathbb{G}}
and, for any non trivial
\tau
subtree of
{\mathbb{G}}
, we will denote shortly

\mathrm{\mathbf{m}}(\tau)=\mathrm{m}_{V_{\tau}}(\tau)
(here
V_{\tau}
denotes the vertex set of
\tau
).

We are now in the position to state the main result of this note.

Theorem 1.3

Let
{\mathbb{G}}=({\mathbb{V}},{\mathbb{E}})
be a graph and let
P_{\mathbb{G}}(q)
be the chromatic polynomial of
{\mathbb{G}}
. Let
\mathrm{\mathbf{m}}
be a partition scheme in
{\mathbb{G}}
according to Definition
1.2
.
Then

P_{\mathbb{G}}(q)=q^{|{\mathbb{V}}|}\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}^{\bf m}_{\mathbb{G}}}\left(-{1\over q}\right)^{|F|}

(1.4)

where
\mathcal{F}^{\bf m}_{\mathbb{G}}
is the subset of
\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{G}}
formed by the empty set and by all non trivial forests
F=\{\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{k}\}
in which
\mathrm{\mathbf{m}}(\tau_{i})=\tau_{i}
for each tree
\tau_{i}
,
i\in[k]
.

Remark
. It is noteworthy to observe that identity (
1.4
) holds independently of the partition scheme
{\bf m}
in
{\mathbb{G}}
used.
Even more impressive is that despite the fact that the set
\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{G}}^{\mathrm{\mathbf{m}}}
depends strongly on the partition scheme
\mathrm{\mathbf{m}}

, i.e. in general
\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{G}}^{\mathrm{\mathbf{m}}}\neq\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{G}}^{\mathrm{\mathbf{m}}^{\prime}}
if
\mathrm{\mathbf{m}}\neq\mathrm{\mathbf{m}}^{\prime}
, the coefficients of
1.4
, namely

|\{F\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{G}}^{\mathrm{\mathbf{m}}}:|F|=k\}|
, does not depend on
\mathrm{\mathbf{m}}
.
Various partition schemes are available (see e.g.
[
6
]
, Sec. 4 and references therein). In particular, in statistical mechanics, as far as we know, two partition schemes have been used: the first one is the so called Penrose partition scheme, originally presented in
[
9
]
and used in
[
4
]
to improve the Dobrushin criterion
[
2
]
for the convergence of the cluster expansion on the abstract polymer gas; the second one is the
minimal-tree partition scheme
, described e.g. in the proof of Lemma 2.2. in
[
12
]
and used in
[
10
]
to improve the lower bound of the convergence radius of the Mayer Series on a gas of
classical particles in the continuum interacting via a stable and regular pair potential. The Penrose partition scheme has also been
used in combinatorics, e.g. in
[
6
]
it was a key tool to find zero-free regions in the complex plane at large
|q|
for the
multivariate Tutte polynomial (see there Proposition 4.4.and Lemma 4.5). Last but not least,
the improvement of the Lovász local lemma given in
[
1
]
also relies crucially on the Penrose partition scheme.

To conclude the introduction, we show as promised that the broken circuit theorem, i.e. Theorem
1.1
is in fact
a special case of Theorem (
1.3
). Namely, the broken circuit theorem is in fact Theorem
1.1
when the partition scheme
\mathrm{\mathbf{m}}

is the so-called
minimal-tree partition scheme
.
To see this, let us construct explicitly the above mentioned partition scheme in
{\mathbb{G}}
.

Minimal-tree partition scheme.
We start by choosing a total order
\succ
in the set of edges
{\mathbb{E}}
of
{\mathbb{G}}
. Now, for any
R\in\mathrm{C}_{\mathbb{V}}
, let
{\mu_{R}}:\mathcal{T}_{{\mathbb{G}}|_{R}}\to\mathcal{C}_{{\mathbb{G}}|_{R}}
be the map that associates to the
tree
\tau=(R,E_{\tau})\in\mathcal{T}_{{\mathbb{G}}|_{R}}
the graph
{\mu_{R}}(\tau)\in\mathcal{C}_{{\mathbb{G}}|_{R}}

whose edge set is
E_{{\mu_{R}}(\tau)}=E_{\tau}\cup E_{\tau}^{*}
, where
E_{\tau}^{*}
is composed by all edges
\{x,y\}\subset{\mathbb{E}}|_{R}\setminus E_{\tau}

such that
\{x,y\}\succ\{z,u\}
for every edge
\{z,u\}\in E_{\tau}
belonging to the path from
x
to
y
through
\tau
.

It is easy to show that
{\mu_{R}}
is, for any
R\in C_{\mathbb{V}}
, a partition scheme in
\mathcal{C}_{{\mathbb{G}}|_{R}}
(see e.g.
[
10
]
,
[
11
]
,
[
12
]
) and thus the family
{\bm{\mu}}=\{\mu_{R}\}_{R\in\mathrm{C}_{\mathbb{V}}}
is a partition scheme in
{\mathbb{G}}
. Then, according to Theorem
1.3
, we can write

P_{\mathbb{G}}(q)=q^{|{\mathbb{V}}|}\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}^{\bm{\mu}}_{\mathbb{G}}}\left(-{1\over q}\right)^{|F|},

(1.5)

where
\bm{\mu}
is the minimal-tree partition scheme and
\mathcal{F}^{\bm{\mu}}_{\mathbb{G}}
is the set of all forests
F
in
{\mathbb{G}}
whose non trivial trees
\tau\in F
are such that

{\bm{\mu}}(\tau)=\tau
.
Observe now that
for any non trivial tree
\tau\subset{\mathbb{G}}
the condition
\bm{\mu}(\tau)=\tau
simply means that for any
\{x,y\}\in{\mathbb{E}}|_{R}\setminus E_{\tau}
,
the path
p^{\tau}_{xy}
in
\tau
from
x
to
y
is such that
\{x,y\}
is never the largest edge in the set

\{\{x,y\}\cup\{e\}_{e\in p^{\tau}_{xy}}\}
.
Therefore, the condition
{\bm{\mu}}(\tau)=\tau
for any tree in a forest
F\in\mathcal{F}^{\bm{\mu}}_{{\mathbb{G}}}
can be rephrased by saying that
F
does not contain broken circuits. That is to say,

\mathcal{F}^{\bm{\mu}}_{\mathbb{G}}=\mathcal{F}^{*}_{\mathbb{G}}
and the sum r.h.s. of (
1.5
) coincides term by term with the sum of the r.h.s. of (
1.3
).

2
Proof of Theorem
1.3

It is long known (see
[
8
]
and references therein) that
P_{\mathbb{G}}(q)
also
coincides with the partition function of the antiferromagnetic
Potts model with
q
states on
\mathbb{G}
at zero temperature.
The Potts model with
q
states on a finite graph
\mathbb{G}=(\mathbb{V},\mathbb{E})

(a heavily studied spin system in statistical mechanics) is defined by a set
of random variables (a.k.a. “spins”)
\{\sigma_{x}\}_{x\in{\mathbb{V}}}
. For any vertex
x\in{\mathbb{V}}
, the spin
\sigma_{x}

takes values in the set of integers

[q]
and a
configuration

\bm{\sigma}
of the system is a function
\bm{\sigma}:{\mathbb{V}}\to[q]
. Interpreting
[q]
as a set of
q
colors, a spin configuration

\bm{\sigma}
can be seen as an element of
{\mathcal{K}}_{\mathbb{V}}(q)
.
The
energy
of a spin configuration
\bm{\sigma}\in{\mathcal{K}}_{\mathbb{V}}(q)
is defined as

H_{{\mathbb{G}}}(\bm{\sigma})=-J\sum_{\{x,y\}\in\mathbb{E}}\delta_{\sigma_{x}\sigma_{y}}

where
J\in\mathbb{R}
and

\delta_{\sigma_{x}\sigma_{y}}
is the Kronecker symbol which is equal to one when
\sigma_{x}=\sigma_{y}
and
zero otherwise.
When
J>0
the model is called

ferromagnetic
, while if
J<0
the model is said to be
antiferromagnetic
.

The probability
to find the system in the configuration
\bm{\sigma}
is defined as

{\rm Prob}(\bm{\sigma})=\frac{e^{-\beta H_{{\mathbb{G}}}(\bm{\sigma})}}{Z_{{\mathbb{G}}}(q,\beta)}

(2.1)

where
\beta\geq 0
is the inverse temperature. The normalization constant
Z_{{\mathbb{G}}}(q,\beta)
in the denominator
is called the
partition function
and is explicitly given by

Z_{{\mathbb{G}}}(q,\beta)=\sum_{\bm{\sigma}\in{\mathcal{K}}_{\mathbb{V}}(q)}e^{-\beta H_{{\mathbb{G}}}(\bm{\sigma})}.

(2.2)

The case
J<0
and
\beta=+\infty
is the
antiferromagnetic
and

zero temperature
Potts model with
q
-states. In this case,
when a product
0\cdot\infty
appears in the factor
\beta H_{{\mathbb{G}}}(\bm{\sigma})
,
it must be interpreted as zero. With this convention, the only configurations
\bm{\sigma}
with non zero probability are those for which

\delta_{\sigma_{x}\sigma_{y}}=0
for all
\{x,y\}\in{\mathbb{E}}
, i.e. those configurations in
which adjacent spins have different values (or colors).
In other words, the
antiferromagnetic Potts model at zero temperature is such that
{\rm Prob}(\bm{\sigma})\neq 0
if and only if the configuration
\bm{\sigma}
is a proper coloring (i.e.
\bm{\sigma}\in{\mathcal{K}}^{*}_{\mathbb{V}}(q)
).
Clearly, for any
\bm{\sigma}\in{\mathcal{K}}^{*}_{\mathbb{V}}(q)
we have that

\exp\{-\beta H_{{\mathbb{G}}}(\bm{\sigma}\}|_{\beta=+\infty,J<0}=1
, so that, according to (
2.1
), all colorings in
{\mathcal{K}}^{*}_{\mathbb{V}}(q)
have the same probability

1/Z_{\mathbb{G}}(q,\beta)
, and thus the normalization constant
Z_{\mathbb{G}}(q,\beta)
coincides with
|{\mathcal{K}}^{*}_{\mathbb{V}}(q)|
, i.e. we get that

Z_{\mathbb{G}}(q,\beta)|_{\beta=+\infty,J<0}=P_{\mathbb{G}}(q).

(2.3)

The connection between chromatic polynomial and antiferromagnetic Potts model at zero temperature shown by Formula (
2.3
)
has been explored in recent times to study the location of the zeros of the
P_{\mathbb{G}}(q)
in the complex plane
(see
[
13
,
5
]
).

Let us enunciate two rather standard results in statistical mechanics. The first is basically related to the well known
high-temperature expansion for the partition function of a general discrete spin system while the second is a long known identity discovered by Oliver Penrose
[
9
]
during with the study of
the mathematical properties of continuous particle systems in the high-temperature and low-density regime.

Proposition 2.1

let
Z_{\mathbb{G}}(q,\beta)|_{\beta=+\infty,J<0}
be the partition function of the antiferromagnetic Potts model with
q
state at zero temperature. Then

Z_{\mathbb{G}}(q,\beta)|_{\beta=+\infty,J<0}=q^{|{\mathbb{V}}|}\Xi_{\mathbb{G}}(q),

(2.4)

where

\Xi_{\mathbb{G}}(q)=1+\sum_{k\geq 1}\sum_{\{R_{1},\dots,R_{k}\}\atop R_{i}\in\mathrm{C}_{\mathbb{V}},~R_{i}\cap R_{j}=\emptyset}\prod_{i=1}^{k}z(R_{i},q)

(2.5)

is the grand canonical partition function of a “polymer gas”, in which the polymers are connected subsets
R\subset\mathbb{V}
, with cardinality
|R|\geq 2
(i.e. they are the elements of the set

\mathrm{C}_{\mathbb{V}}
defined in (
1.1
)), subjected to a non-intersection constraint (hard-core interaction) and with weights (a.k.a. activities)
z(R,q)
given by

z(R,q)={1\over q^{|R|-1}}\sum_{g\in\mathcal{C}_{{\mathbb{G}}|_{R}}}(-1)^{|E_{g}|}

(2.6)

where
\mathcal{C}_{{\mathbb{G}}|_{R}}
denotes the set of all connected spanning subgraphs of
{\mathbb{G}}|_{R}
.

A sketch of the proof of Proposition
2.1
can be found e.g. in
[
13
]
and in
[
6
]
.
However, since this representation of
Z_{\mathbb{G}}(q,\beta)|_{\beta=+\infty,J<0}
is crucial for the proof of
Theorem
1.3
, we will provide a detailed proof of it in the appendix.

Proposition 2.2

Let
{\mathrm{\mathbf{m}}}
be a partition scheme in
{\mathbb{G}}=({\mathbb{V}},{\mathbb{E}})
according to Definition
1.2
, and let
\{w_{e}\}_{e\in{\mathbb{E}}}
be
any collection of real numbers, then, for any
R\in\mathrm{C}_{\mathbb{V}}
, we have

\sum_{g\in\mathcal{C}_{{\mathbb{G}}|_{R}}}\prod_{e\in E_{g}}w_{e}=\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}_{{\mathbb{G}}|_{R}}}\prod_{e\in E_{\tau}}w_{e}\prod_{e\in E_{{\mathrm{\mathbf{m}}}(\tau)}\setminus E_{\tau}}(1+w_{e}),

(2.7)

where
E_{g}
,
E_{\tau}
, and
E_{{\mathrm{\mathbf{m}}}(\tau)}
represent the edge sets of
g
,
\tau
, and
{\mathrm{\mathbf{m}}}(\tau)
respectively.

The proof of identity (
2.7
) is straightforward and can be seen e.g. in Sec. 4.1 of
[
4
]
.

We are now in the position to prove Theorem
1.3
.
Let us turn initially
our attention to the Penrose identity (
2.7
) and observe that when
w_{e}=-1
for all
e\in{\mathbb{E}}|_{R}
,
the l.h.s. of (
2.7
) becomes
\sum_{g\in\mathcal{C}_{{\mathbb{G}}|_{R}}}(-1)^{|E_{g}|}
, which, recalling the definition of
z(q,R)
given in (
2.6
) is exactly the sum in the r.h.s. of (
2.6
). On the other hand in the r.h.s. of the Penrose identity (
2.7
), when
w_{e}=-1
for all
e\in{\mathbb{E}}|_{R}
, the last product kills the contribution of any tree
\tau
such that
E_{{\mathrm{\mathbf{m}}}(\tau)}\setminus E_{\tau}\neq\emptyset
, so that the only contributions on the sum over trees in
\mathcal{T}_{{\mathbb{G}}|_{R}}
come from those trees
\tau
such that
{\mathrm{\mathbf{m}}}(\tau)=\tau
. Since
for any
\tau\in\mathcal{T}_{{\mathbb{G}}|_{R}}
we have that
|\tau|=|R|-1
, as far as the case
w_{e}=-1
for all
e\in{\mathbb{E}}|_{R}
is concerned, the Penrose identity (
2.7
) can be written as

\sum_{g\in\mathcal{C}_{{\mathbb{G}}|_{R}}}(-1)^{|E_{g}|}=(-1)^{|R|-1}\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}_{{\mathbb{G}}|_{R}}\atop{\mathrm{\mathbf{m}}}(\tau)=\tau}1.

(2.8)

Hence we can rewrite
the activity
z(R,q)
of a polymer
R
, given in (
2.6
), as

z(R,q)=\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}_{{\mathbb{G}}|_{R}}\atop{\mathrm{\mathbf{m}}}(\tau)=\tau}\left(-{1\over q}\right)^{|\tau|},

(2.9)

where
{\mathrm{\mathbf{m}}}
is any partition scheme on
{\mathbb{G}}
.
Then, recalling (
2.5
) we get

\Xi_{\mathbb{G}}(q)=1+\sum_{k\geq 1}\sum_{\{R_{1},\dots,R_{k}\}\atop R_{i}\in\mathrm{C}_{\mathbb{V}},~R_{i}\cap R_{j}=\emptyset}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{\tau_{i}\in\mathcal{T}_{{\mathbb{G}}|_{R_{i}}}\atop{\mathrm{\mathbf{m}}}(\tau_{i})=\tau_{i}}\left(-{1\over q}\right)^{|\tau|}=\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{\mathbf{m}}}_{\mathbb{G}}}\left(-{1\over q}\right)^{|F|},

(2.10)

where
\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{\mathbf{m}}}_{\mathbb{G}}
is the subset of
\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{G}}
formed by the empty set
(contributing with the factor “1” in the sum in the r.h.s. of (
2.10
))
and by all non trivial forests
F=\{\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{k}\}
where each tree
\tau_{i}
,
i\in[k]
, is such that
\mathrm{\mathbf{m}}(\tau_{i})=\tau_{i}
.
Inserting finally (
2.10
) into (
2.4
) and recalling (
2.3
), we get (
1.4
).

Appendix. Proof of Proposition
2.1

We start by rewriting
Z_{\mathbb{G}}(q)|_{\beta=+\infty,J<0}
as follows.

Z_{\mathbb{G}}(q)|_{\beta=+\infty,J<0}=\sum_{\bm{\sigma}\in{\mathcal{K}}_{\mathbb{V}}(q)}\exp\Big\{-\sum_{\{x,y\}\subset{\mathbb{V}}}J_{xy}\delta_{\sigma_{x}\sigma_{y}}\Big\}

(A.1)

where

J_{xy}=\begin{cases}+\infty&{\rm if}~\{x,y\}\in\mathbb{E}\\
0&{\rm otherwise}\end{cases}

(A.2)

reminding that a product
0\cdot\infty
in the r.h.s. of (
A.1
) must be interpreted as zero.

Using the so-called Mayer trick, we can write

\displaystyle\exp\Big\{-\sum_{\{x,y\}\subset{\mathbb{V}}}J_{xy}\delta_{\sigma_{x}\sigma_{y}}\Big\}

\displaystyle=\prod_{\{x,y\}\subset{\mathbb{V}}}\Big[(e^{-J_{xy}\delta_{\sigma_{x}\sigma_{y}}}-1)+1]

\displaystyle=\sum_{g\in{\cal G}_{\mathbb{V}}}\prod_{\{x,y\}\in E_{g}}(e^{-J_{xy}\delta_{\sigma_{x}\sigma_{y}}}-1)

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{k\geq 1}\sum_{\{R_{1},\dots,R_{k}\}\subset{\mathbb{V}}\atop R_{i}\cap R_{j}=\emptyset,~|R_{i}|\geq 2}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{g\in G_{R_{i}}}\prod_{\{x,y\}\in E_{g}}(e^{-J_{xy}\delta_{\sigma_{x}\sigma_{y}}}-1)

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{k\geq 1}\sum_{\{R_{1},\dots,R_{k}\}\subset{\mathbb{V}}\atop R_{i}\cap R_{j}=\emptyset,~|R_{i}|\geq 2}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\rho(R_{i},\bm{\sigma}_{R_{i}})

where in the second line we have denoted by
{\cal G}_{\mathbb{V}}
the set of all graphs whose vertex set is
{\mathbb{V}}
and, for any
g\in{\cal G}_{\mathbb{V}}
,
E_{g}
denotes the edge set of
g
. In the third line, for any
R\subset{\mathbb{V}}
such that
|R|\geq 2
,
G_{R}
denotes the set of all connected graphs with vertex set
R
. Finally, in the fourth line
we have set, for any
R\subset{\mathbb{V}}
such that
|R|\geq 2
,

\rho(R,\bm{\sigma}_{R})=\sum_{g\in G_{R}}\prod_{\{x,y\}\in E_{g}}(e^{-J_{xy}\delta_{\sigma_{x}\sigma_{y}}}-1)

(A.3)

where for any
R\subset{\mathbb{V}}
,
\bm{\sigma}_{R}
is the restriction of
\bm{\sigma}
to
R
.

Recalling (
2.3
), we have that the chromatic polynomial
P_{\mathbb{G}}(q)
can be written as

\displaystyle P_{\mathbb{G}}(q)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{\sigma}\in{\mathcal{K}}_{\mathbb{V}}(q)}\Bigg[1+\sum_{k\geq 1}\sum_{\{R_{1},\dots,R_{k}\}\subset{\mathbb{V}}\atop R_{i}\cap R_{j}=\emptyset,~|R_{i}|\geq 2}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\rho(R_{i},\sigma_{R_{i}})\Bigg]

\displaystyle=q^{|{\mathbb{V}}|}+\sum_{k\geq 1}\sum_{\{R_{1},\dots,R_{k}\}\subset{\mathbb{V}}\atop R_{i}\cap R_{j}=\emptyset,~|R_{i}|\geq 2}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{\bm{\sigma}_{R_{i}}\in{\mathcal{K}}_{R_{i}}(q)}\rho(R_{i},\bm{\sigma}_{R_{i}})q^{|{\mathbb{V}}|-\sum_{i=1}^{k}|R_{i}|}

According to the last equality above we can rewrite
P_{{\mathbb{G}}}(q)
as follows.

P_{{\mathbb{G}}}(q)=q^{|{\mathbb{V}}|}\Bigg(1+\sum_{k\geq 1}\sum_{\{R_{1},\dots,R_{k}\}\subset{\mathbb{V}}\atop R_{i}\cap R_{j}=\emptyset,~|R_{i}|\geq 2}\prod_{i=1}^{k}{1\over q^{|R_{i}|}}\sum_{\bm{\sigma}_{R_{i}}\in{\mathcal{K}}_{R_{i}}(q)}\rho(R_{i},\bm{\sigma}_{R_{i}})\Bigg)

(A.4)

Now observe that, given
R\subset{\mathbb{V}}
such that
|R|\geq 2
we have that

\sum_{\bm{\sigma}_{R}\in{\mathcal{K}}_{R}(q)}\rho(R,\bm{\sigma}_{R})=\begin{cases}\displaystyle{q\sum\limits_{g\in\mathcal{C}_{{\mathbb{G}}|_{R}}}}(-1)^{|E_{g}|}&\mbox{if ${\mathbb{G}}|_{R}$ is connected}\\
0&{\rm otherwise}\end{cases}

(A.5)

where
\mathcal{C}_{{\mathbb{G}}|_{R}}
denotes the set of all connected spanning subgraphs of
{\mathbb{G}}|_{R}
.
Indeed, recalling (
A.3
), we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\bm{\sigma}_{R}\in{\mathcal{K}}_{R}(q)}\rho(R,\bm{\sigma}_{R})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{\sigma}_{R}\in{\mathcal{K}}_{R}(q)}\sum_{g\in G_{R}}\prod_{\{x,y\}\in E_{g}}(e^{-J_{xy}\delta_{\sigma_{x}\sigma_{y}}}-1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{\sigma}_{R}\in{\mathcal{K}}_{R}(q)}\sum_{g\in G_{R}}\prod_{\{x,y\}\in E_{g}}\delta_{\sigma_{x}\sigma_{y}}(e^{-J_{xy}}-1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{g\in G_{R}}\left[\sum\limits_{\bm{\sigma}_{R}\in{\mathcal{K}}_{R}(q)}\prod\limits_{\{x,y\}\in E_{g}}\delta_{\sigma_{x}\sigma_{y}}\right]\prod_{\{x,y\}\in E_{g}}(e^{-J_{xy}}-1)

\displaystyle=q\sum_{g\in G_{R}}\prod_{\{x,y\}\in E_{g}}(e^{-J_{xy}}-1)

where the last equality above holds because, for any
g\in G_{R}
,

\sum\limits_{\bm{\sigma}_{R}\in{\mathcal{K}}_{R}(q)}\prod\limits_{\{x,y\}\in E_{g}}\delta_{\sigma_{x}\sigma_{y}}=q

Finally, recalling the definition of
J_{xy}
given in (
A.2
), we have that

\sum_{g\in G_{R}}\prod_{\{x,y\}\in E_{g}}(e^{-J_{xy}}-1)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle{\sum\limits_{g\in\mathcal{C}_{{\mathbb{G}}|_{R}}}}(-1)^{|E_{g}|}&\mbox{if ${\mathbb{G}}|_{R}$ is connected}\\
0&{\rm otherwise}\end{cases}

Therefore inserting (
A.5
) in (
A.4
) and recalling that

\mathrm{C}_{\mathbb{V}}=\{R\subset{\mathbb{V}}:|R|\geq 2~{\rm and}~{\mathbb{G}}|_{R}~{\rm is}~{\rm connected}\}
(i.e.
\mathrm{C}_{\mathbb{V}}
is the set of all connected subsets of
{\mathbb{V}}
with cardinality greater than one), we get

P_{\mathbb{G}}(q)=q^{|{\mathbb{V}}|}\Xi_{\mathbb{G}}(q)

where

\Xi_{\mathbb{G}}(q)=1+\sum_{k\geq 1}\sum_{\{R_{1},\dots,R_{k}\}\atop R_{i}\in\mathrm{C}_{\mathbb{V}},~R_{i}\cap R_{j}=\emptyset}\prod_{i=1}^{k}z(R_{i},q)

with

z(R,q)={1\over q^{|R|-1}}\sum_{g\in\mathcal{C}_{{\mathbb{G}}|_{R}}}(-1)^{|E_{g}|}

This concludes the proof of Proposition
2.1
.
