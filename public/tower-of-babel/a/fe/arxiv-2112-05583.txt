Validation design I: construction of validation designs via kernel herding
Luc Pronzato & Maria-João Rendas

Abstract
We construct validation designs 𝐙 m \mathbf{Z}_{m} aimed at estimating the integrated squared prediction error of a given design 𝐗 n \mathbf{X}_{n} . Our approach is based on the minimization of a maximum mean discrepancy for a particular kernel, conditional on 𝐗 n \mathbf{X}_{n} , so that sequences of nested validation designs can be constructed incrementally by kernel herding. Numerical experiments show that key features for a good validation design are its space-filling properties, in order to fill the holes left by 𝐗 n \mathbf{X}_{n} and properly explore the whole design space, and the suitable weighting of its points, since evaluations far from 𝐗 n \mathbf{X}_{n} tend to overestimate the global error. A dedicated weighting method, based on a particular kernel, is proposed. Numerical simulations with random functions show the superiority the method over more traditional validation based on random designs, low-discrepancy sequences, or leave-one-out cross validation.

validation; design of experiments; computer experiments; discrepancy; space-filling design; greedy algorithm

Introduction and motivation

This paper proposes methods to define designs enabling good estimation of the prediction performance of a given non-parametric model, which has been adjusted to a known training dataset.
More precisely, we suppose that a design
\mathbf{X}_{n}=\{\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n}\}
with
n
points in
{\mathscr{X}}=[0,1]^{d}
has been been used to build a predictor of the value of an unknown function
f
on
{\mathscr{X}}
. We denote
by
\mathbf{y}_{n}=[f(\mathbf{x}_{1}),\ldots,f(\mathbf{x}_{n})]^{\top}
the vector collecting the
n
evaluations of
f
at the
\mathbf{x}_{i}
and by
\eta_{n}(\mathbf{x})=\eta_{[\mathbf{X}_{n},\mathbf{y}_{n}]}(\mathbf{x})
the corresponding prediction of
f(\mathbf{x})
.
The Integrated Squared Error (ISE) over
{\mathscr{X}}
is then

\displaystyle\mathsf{ISE}(\mathbf{X}_{n})=\int_{\mathscr{X}}\left[\eta_{n}(\mathbf{x})-f(\mathbf{x})\right]^{2}\,\mu(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x})\,.

(1)

Above, the measure
\mu
codes the user preferences, penalizing regions of
{\mathscr{X}}
which are of particular interest or importance. In the paper we will always consider that
\mu
is the Lebesgue measure on
{\mathscr{X}}
, the extension to non-uniform
\mu
requiring only minor modifications. Note that we slightly abuse notation here, as the dependency of
\mathsf{ISE}(\mathbf{X}_{n})
on
\mathbf{X}_{n}
is hidden in
\eta_{n}(\cdot)
, which is adapted to the training set
\mathbf{X}_{n}
. The same shortcut is used throughout the paper.

In practice, the integral in definition (
1
) is approximated by a discrete sum, which is equivalent to letting
\mu
be a discrete measure with finite support
\mathbf{Z}_{m}=\{\mathbf{z}_{1},\ldots\mathbf{z}_{m}\}\subset{\mathscr{X}}
, at which
\eta_{n}
and
f
are effectively evaluated.
The objective of the paper is to propose methods for the construction of
validation designs

\mathbf{Z}_{m}
and investigate the properties of the corresponding estimates of
\mathsf{ISE}(\mathbf{X}_{n})

In our study, we consider that the design

. We are interested in particular in situations where

2
An ISE-based criterion for validation design

2.1
A Gaussian process model

As mentioned above,
f
is unknown and we cannot choose
\mathbf{Z}_{m}
by minimizing
|\widehat{\mathsf{ISE}}(\mathbf{Z}_{m},\mathbf{X}_{n})-\mathsf{ISE}(\mathbf{X}_{n})|
directly. Assumptions on the behavior of
f
must be made. Considering the worst case for
f
in a given class of functions would be an option. Here we shall follow another, simpler, route and assume that
f
is the realization of a Gaussian Process (GP), or Gaussian Random Field,
\mathcal{F}_{x}
indexed by
{\mathscr{X}}
, with given second-order characteristics.

For the sake of simplicity, we suppose that
\mathsf{E}\{\mathcal{F}_{x}\}=0
for all
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathscr{X}}
. Extension to the case of a linearly parameterized mean, with
\mathsf{E}\{\mathcal{F}_{x}\}=\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{x})
for a vector
\boldsymbol{\beta}
of unknown parameters and a vector
\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{x})=[h_{1}(\mathbf{x}),\ldots,h_{p}(\mathbf{x})]^{\top}
of
p
known functions of
\mathbf{x}
(including the constant) is possible via some adaptation. We also suppose that
\mathsf{E}\{\mathcal{F}_{x}\mathcal{F}_{x^{\prime}}\}=K(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
, a known covariance function.
In practice,
K
may be known up to a (variance) scaling coefficient
\sigma^{2}
and parameterized by some parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, setting in particular the correlation lengths
and the smoothness of the functions that belong to the Reproducing Hilbert Space (RKHS)
\mathcal{H}_{K}
associated with
K
. Both
\sigma^{2}
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}
can be estimated from the data
\mathscr{F}_{n}=\{\mathbf{X}_{n},\mathbf{y}_{n}\}
, e.g. by maximum likelihood, see for instance
Santner et al., 2003
, or cross validation; see
Bachoc, 2013
and Section
5.2
for an example.

The GP assumption defines a prior distribution for
f
, which can be updated given
\mathscr{F}_{n}
into a posterior distribution, with mean
\mathsf{E}\{\mathcal{F}_{x}|\mathscr{F}_{n}\}=\mathbf{k}_{n}^{\top}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{K}_{n}^{-1}\mathbf{y}_{n}
and covariance

\displaystyle\mathsf{E}\{\mathcal{F}_{x}\mathcal{F}_{x^{\prime}}|\mathscr{F}_{n}\}=K_{|n}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=K(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})-\mathbf{k}_{n}^{\top}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{K}_{n}^{-1}\mathbf{k}_{n}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\geq 0\,,

(2)

for any
\mathbf{x}
,
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
in
{\mathscr{X}}
, where

\displaystyle\mathbf{k}_{n}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[K(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_{1})\,\ldots,K(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_{n})\right]^{\top}\,,

\displaystyle\{\mathbf{K}_{n}\}_{i,j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle K(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j})\,,\ i,j=1,\ldots,n\,,

the
n\times n
matrix
\mathbf{K}_{n}
being positive definite. The Integrated Mean Squared Error (IMSE)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathscr{X}}\mathsf{E}\left\{\left[\eta_{n}(\mathbf{x})-f(\mathbf{x})\right]^{2}|\mathscr{F}_{n}\right\}\,\mu(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathscr{X}}\mathsf{E}\left\{\left[\eta_{n}(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{k}_{n}^{\top}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{K}_{n}^{-1}\mathbf{y}_{n}\right]^{2}|\mathscr{F}_{n}\right\}\,\mu(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle+\int_{\mathscr{X}}K_{|n}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x})\,\mu(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x})

is minimum when the prediction
\eta_{n}(\mathbf{x})
equals the posterior mean
\mathbf{k}_{n}(\mathbf{x})^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{n}^{-1}\mathbf{y}_{n}
, which yields

\displaystyle\mathsf{IMSE}(\mathbf{X}_{n})=\int_{\mathscr{X}}K_{|n}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x})\,\mu(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x})\,.

(3)

Note that
K_{|n}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_{i})=0
for any design point
\mathbf{x}_{i}
and any
\mathbf{x}
in
{\mathscr{X}}
and that
\eta_{n}
interpolates the observations
\mathbf{y}_{n}
. The extension to the case where
\eta_{n}
is not a interpolator does not raise particular difficulties, only yielding a kernel
\overline{K}_{|n}
different from (
5
), having a slightly more complicated expression where the errors
f(\mathbf{x}_{i})-\eta_{m}(\mathbf{x}_{i})
intervene.

2.2
Validation designs minimizing the expected squared
\mathsf{ISE}
difference

Replacing
f(\mathbf{x})
by a realization
\mathcal{F}_{x}
of the GP model of Section
2.1
in
\mathsf{ISE}(\mathbf{X}_{n})
and
\widehat{\mathsf{ISE}}(\mathbf{Z}_{m},\mathbf{X}_{n})
, we define

\displaystyle\overline{\Delta}^{2}(\mathbf{Z}_{m},\mathbf{X}_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathsf{E}\left\{\left[\mathsf{ISE}(\mathbf{X}_{n})-\widehat{\mathsf{ISE}}(\mathbf{Z}_{m},\mathbf{X}_{n})\right]^{2}|\mathscr{F}_{n}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathsf{E}\left\{\left[\int_{\mathscr{X}}[\mathcal{F}_{x}-\eta_{n}(\mathbf{x})]^{2}\,(\zeta_{m}-\mu)(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x})\right]^{2}|\mathscr{F}_{n}\right\}\,,

the mean squared error of the ISE estimator
\widehat{\mathsf{ISE}}(\mathbf{Z}_{m},\mathbf{X}_{n})
, and where
\zeta_{m}
denotes the discrete measure
\zeta_{m}=(1/m)\,\sum_{i=1}^{m}\delta_{\mathbf{z}_{i}}
, with
\delta_{\mathbf{z}}
the delta measure at
\mathbf{z}

(
\zeta_{m}-\mu
is thus a signed measure with total mass 0).

For any positive definite kernel
C(\cdot,\cdot)
and probability measures
\xi
and
\nu
, denote by
\gamma_{C}(\xi,\nu)
the Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) between
\xi
and
\nu
, defined by

\displaystyle\gamma_{C}^{2}(\xi,\nu)=\int_{{\mathscr{X}}^{2}}C(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\,(\xi-\nu)(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x})(\xi-\nu)(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\,;

see
(
Sejdinovic et al., 2013
, Def. 10)
. The minimization of
\gamma_{C}(\xi,\nu)
with respect to
\xi
for a given
\nu
can be performed by kernel herding (Section
3
), yielding a sequence of finitely supported measures
\xi^{(t)}
such that
\gamma_{C}(\xi^{(t)},\nu)\rightarrow 0
as
t\rightarrow\infty
.

When
\eta_{n}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{k}_{n}(\mathbf{x})^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{n}^{-1}\mathbf{y}_{n}
, direct calculation gives

\displaystyle\overline{\Delta}^{2}(\mathbf{Z}_{m},\mathbf{X}_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathscr{X}}^{2}}\mathsf{E}\left\{[\mathcal{F}_{x}-\eta_{n}(\mathbf{x})]^{2}[\mathcal{F}_{x^{\prime}}-\eta_{n}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})]^{2}|\mathscr{F}_{n}\right\}\,(\zeta_{m}-\mu)(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x})(\zeta_{m}-\mu)(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}^{\prime})

(4)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathscr{X}}^{2}}\overline{K}_{|n}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\,(\zeta_{m}-\mu)(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x})(\zeta_{m}-\mu)(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=\gamma_{\overline{K}_{|n}}^{2}(\zeta_{m},\mu)

where, for all
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
in
{\mathscr{X}}
, we denote

\displaystyle\overline{K}_{|n}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=2\,K_{|n}^{2}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})+K_{|n}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x})K_{|n}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\,,

(5)

with
K_{|n}
defined by (
2
).
Note that
\overline{K}_{|n}
is positive definite (but not strictly positive definite, see Appendix A).
Indeed, the Hadamard product
\mathbf{C}_{n}^{\circ 2}
with elements
\{\mathbf{C}_{n}^{\circ 2}\}_{i,j}=C^{2}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j})
,
i,j=1,\ldots,n
, is positive definite when the matrix
\mathbf{C}_{n}
with elements
\{\mathbf{C}_{n}\}_{i,j}=C(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j})
is positive definite. Hence,
K_{|n}^{2}
is positive definite since
K_{|n}
is positive definite, which implies that
\overline{K}_{|n}
is positive definite.
The fact that
\overline{\Delta}^{2}(\mathbf{Z}_{m},\mathbf{X}_{n})
does not depend on
\mathbf{y}_{n}
although it relies on conditioning on
\mathscr{F}_{n}
is a direct consequence of using a GP model.

3
Kernel herding for validation designs

3.1
A summary of kernel herding

Let
C
denote a positive definite kernel. For any signed measure
\xi
on
{\mathscr{X}}
, let

\displaystyle{\mathscr{E}}_{C}(\xi)=\int_{{\mathscr{X}}^{2}}C(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\,\xi(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x})\xi(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\geq 0

(6)

denote the energy of
\xi
for
C
, so that
\gamma_{\overline{K}_{|n}}^{2}(\zeta_{m},\mu)={\mathscr{E}}_{\overline{K}_{|n}}(\zeta_{m}-\mu)
in (
4
).
A kernel
C
is called
characteristic
when
\gamma_{C}(\cdot,\cdot)
defines a metric on the set of probability measures on
{\mathscr{X}}
, implying in particular that, for two probability measures
\zeta
and
\mu
,
\gamma_{C}(\zeta,\mu)=0
if and only if
\zeta=\mu
. The kernel
\overline{K}_{|n}
is not characteristic, see Appendix A, but we can nevertheless consider the minimization of
\gamma_{\overline{K}_{|n}}^{2}(\zeta_{m},\mu)
.

For any
\alpha\in[0,1]
, we have

(1-\alpha)\,{\mathscr{E}}_{C}(\xi)+\alpha\,{\mathscr{E}}_{C}(\nu)-{\mathscr{E}}_{C}[(1-\alpha)\xi+\alpha\nu]=\alpha(1-\alpha)\,{\mathscr{E}}_{C}(\xi-\nu)\geq 0
, showing that
{\mathscr{E}}_{C}(\cdot)
is convex; see
Pronzato and Zhigljavsky, 2020
, and we can minimize the squared MMD criterion
\gamma_{C}^{2}(\xi,\mu)={\mathscr{E}}_{C}(\xi-\mu)
with respect to
\xi
by a simple descent algorithm.

Denote by
F_{C,\mu}(\xi;\nu)
the directional derivative of
\gamma_{C}^{2}(\cdot,\mu)
at
\xi
in the direction
\nu
,

\displaystyle F_{C,\mu}(\xi;\nu)=\lim_{\alpha\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{{\mathscr{E}}_{C}[(1-\alpha)\xi+\alpha\nu-\mu]-{\mathscr{E}}_{C}(\xi-\mu)}{\alpha}\,.

Straightforward calculation gives

\displaystyle F_{C,\mu}(\xi;\nu)=2\left[\int_{{\mathscr{X}}^{2}}C(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\,(\nu-\mu)(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x})(\xi-\mu)(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}^{\prime})-{\mathscr{E}}_{C}(\xi-\mu)\right]\,.

In particular, for
\nu=\delta_{\mathbf{x}}
, we get

\displaystyle F_{C,\mu}(\xi;\delta_{\mathbf{x}})=2\left[P_{C,\xi}(\mathbf{x})-P_{C,\mu}(\mathbf{x})-{\mathscr{E}}_{C}(\xi)+{\mathscr{E}}_{C}(\xi,\mu)\right]\,,

(7)

where
{\mathscr{E}}_{C}(\xi,\nu)=\int_{{\mathscr{X}}^{2}}C(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\,\xi(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x})\nu(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
and

\displaystyle P_{C,\xi}(\mathbf{x})=\int_{\mathscr{X}}C(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\,\xi(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}^{\prime})

(respectively,
P_{C,\mu}(\mathbf{x})=\int_{\mathscr{X}}C(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\,\mu(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
) is called the potential of
\xi
(respectively, of
\mu
), at
\mathbf{x}
, associated with
C
.
P_{C,\mu}(\cdot)
is also called the kernel embedding of
\mu
is the RKHS associated with
C

(
Sejdinovic et al., 2013
, Def. 9)
.
Standard kernel-herding corresponds to the Frank-Wolfe conditional gradient algorithm
(
Bach et al., 2012
)
, that is, to the vertex-direction method with predefined step-length, commonly used in optimal experimental design since the pioneering work of
Wynn, 1970
and
Fedorov, 1972
.

The general form of the algorithm, with step length
\alpha_{k}\in(0,1)
at iteration
k
, is as follows: starting with some probability measure
\zeta^{(k_{1})}
on
{\mathscr{X}}
, we take, for all
k\geq k_{1}
,

\displaystyle\zeta^{(k+1)}=(1-\alpha_{k})\,\zeta^{(k)}+\alpha_{k}\,\delta_{\mathbf{z}_{k+1}}

(8)

where
\mathbf{z}_{k+1}\in\mathrm{Arg}\min_{\mathbf{z}\in{\mathscr{X}}}F_{C,\mu}(\zeta^{(k)};\delta_{\mathbf{x}})
. From (
7
), this is equivalent to

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}_{k+1}\in\mathrm{Arg}\min_{\mathbf{z}\in{\mathscr{X}}}P_{C,\zeta^{(k)}}(\mathbf{z})-P_{C,\mu}(\mathbf{z})\,.

(9)

If the initial measure
\zeta^{(k_{1})}
is finitely supported on a set
{\mathscr{S}}^{(k_{1})}
, then
\zeta^{(k)}
remains finitely supported for all
k
. Selecting the optimal
\alpha_{k}
at each iteration corresponds to Fedorov’s algorithm (1972) used in optimal design for parametric models. If
{\mathscr{S}}^{(k_{1})}
has
k_{1}
elements,
{\mathscr{S}}^{(k_{1})}=\mathbf{Z}^{(k_{1})}=\{\mathbf{z}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{z}_{k_{1}}\}
, and
\zeta^{(k_{1})}=(1/k_{1})\sum_{i=1}^{k_{1}}\delta_{\mathbf{z}_{i}}
is uniform on
\mathbf{Z}^{(k_{1})}
, by choosing
\alpha_{k}=1/(k+1)
for all
k\geq k_{1}
we obtain that

\zeta^{(k)}=(1/k)\sum_{i=1}^{k}\delta_{\mathbf{z}_{i}}
for all
k
, and

\displaystyle P_{C,\zeta^{(k)}}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}C(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}_{i})\,.

In particular, we can take
\alpha_{k}=1/(k+1)
,
\zeta^{(1)}=\delta_{\mathbf{z}_{1}}
for some
\mathbf{z}_{1}\in{\mathscr{X}}
, and
\mathbf{z}_{1}
can be chosen by maximizing
P_{C,\mu}(\mathbf{z})
. For stationary kernels such that
C(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
only depends on
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x}
, it amounts at taking
\mathbf{z}_{1}
at the center of
{\mathscr{X}}
.

We shall denote by
\mathbf{Z}_{k}=\mathsf{KH}(\mathbf{Z}_{k_{1}},C,k)
the
k
-point design design obtained in this way, after
k
iterations of kernel herding initialized at
\mathbf{Z}_{k_{1}}
containing
k_{1}
elements, with
\alpha_{k}=1/(k+k_{1})
for all
k\geq 1
;
\mathsf{KH}(\emptyset,C,k)
selects
\mathbf{z}_{1}
by maximization of
P_{C,\mu}
.

In practice, the search for
\mathbf{z}_{k+1}
in (
9
) is generally made within a finite subset
{\mathscr{X}}_{Q}
of
{\mathscr{X}}
, with
Q
elements.
The cost of the determination of
\mathbf{z}_{k+1}
in (
9
) is
\mathcal{O}(Q)
if we compute
C(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}_{k})
for all
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathscr{X}}_{Q}
and update the sum
\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}C(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}_{i})
; the cost for
k
iterations then scales as
\mathcal{O}(kQ)
, including the initial cost for the computation of
P_{C,\mu}(\mathbf{x})
for all
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathscr{X}}_{Q}
. Another option when
\mu
is approximated by the uniform measure
\mu_{Q}
on
{\mathscr{X}}_{Q}
and
C(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
only depends on
\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|
, is to compute in advance the
Q(Q-1)/2
distances between all pairs of points in
{\mathscr{X}}_{Q}
(feasible only if
Q
is not too large).

The minimum-norm variant of
Bach et al., 2012
replaces
\zeta^{(k)}
in (
9
) by the measure having the same support
{\mathscr{S}}^{(k)}
but optimal weights, positive and summing to one; these optimal weights are solution of a convex quadratic programming problem. Here we shall consider a simplified version where
\zeta^{(k)}
is replaced by
\hat{\zeta}^{(k)}
having weights
\hat{w}_{i}^{(k)}
summing to one and such that such that
{\mathscr{E}}_{C}(\hat{\zeta}^{(k)}-\mu)
is minimal. For a measure
\zeta_{k}
with support
{\mathscr{S}}^{(k)}
and weights
\mathbf{w}^{(k)}
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathscr{E}}_{C}(\zeta_{k}-\mu)={\mathbf{w}^{(k)}}^{\top}\mathbf{C}_{k}\mathbf{w}^{(k)}-2\,{\mathbf{w}^{(k)}}^{\top}\mathbf{p}_{C,k}(\mu)+{\mathscr{E}}_{C}(\mu)\,,

(10)

where
\{\mathbf{C}_{k}\}_{i,j}=C(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{j})
,
i,j=1\ldots,k
and
\mathbf{p}_{C,k}(\mu)=\left[P_{C,\mu}(\mathbf{z}_{1}),\ldots,P_{C,\mu}(\mathbf{z}_{k})\right]^{\top}
.
Its minimization under the constraint
\mathbf{1}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{w}^{(k)}=1
, with
\mathbf{1}_{k}
the
k
-dimensional vector with all components equal to one, gives the optimal weights

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{w}}^{(k)}=(\hat{w}_{1}^{(k)},\ldots,\hat{w}_{k}^{(k)})^{\top}=\left(\mathbf{C}_{k}^{-1}-\frac{\mathbf{C}_{k}^{-1}\mathbf{1}_{k}\mathbf{1}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{C}_{k}^{-1}}{\mathbf{1}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{C}_{k}^{-1}\mathbf{1}_{k}}\right)\mathbf{p}_{C,k}(\mu)+\frac{\mathbf{C}_{k}^{-1}\mathbf{1}_{k}}{\mathbf{1}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{C}_{k}^{-1}\mathbf{1}_{k}}\,.

(11)

By construction,
\hat{\zeta}^{(k)}
minimizes
{\mathscr{E}}_{C}(\zeta_{k}-\mu)
with respect to measures
\zeta_{k}
of total mass one supported on
{\mathscr{S}}^{(k)}
, and one can show
(
Pronzato and Zhigljavsky, 2020
)
that its potential
P_{C,\hat{\zeta}^{(k)}}(x)
satisfies

\displaystyle P_{C,\hat{\zeta}^{(k)}}(x)-P_{C,\mu}(x)-{\mathscr{E}}_{C}(\hat{\zeta}^{(k)},\mu)+{\mathscr{E}}_{C}(\mu)=0\,,\ \forall x\in{\mathscr{S}}^{(k)}\,,

showing that
P_{C,\hat{\zeta}^{(k)}}(x)-P_{C,\mu}(x)
is constant on
{\mathscr{S}}^{(k)}
.

When
{\mathscr{X}}
is discretized into
{\mathscr{X}}_{Q}
, the substitution of
\hat{\zeta}^{(k)}
for
\zeta^{(k)}
requires the storage of all
C(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}_{i})
,
i=1,\ldots,k
,
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathscr{X}}_{Q}
, in order to compute
P_{C,\hat{\zeta}^{(k)}}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\hat{w}_{i}^{(k)}C(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_{i})
in (
9
). At iteration
k
, the computation of
\hat{\mathbf{w}}^{(k)}
by (
11
) also induces an additional computational cost of
\mathcal{O}(k^{3})
(reduced to
\mathcal{O}(k^{2})
if rank-one updating is used to compute
\mathbf{C}_{k}^{-1}
);
\gamma_{C}^{2}(\hat{\zeta}^{(k)},\mu)
decreases faster than
\gamma_{C}^{2}(\zeta^{(k)},\mu)
; see
Pronzato, 2021

(

When it happens that

(

(

\displaystyle\breve{\mathbf{w}}^{(k)}=(\breve{w}^{(k)}_{1},\ldots,\breve{w}^{(k)}_{k})^{\top}=\mathbf{C}_{k}^{-1}\mathbf{p}_{C,k}(\mu)\,.

Theorem 1
.

For any positive definite kernel
K
, any design
\mathbf{X}_{n}
and any
k\geq 1
, there exist choices for
\mathbf{z}_{i+1}
,
i=0,\ldots,k
in (
9

\mathsf{MN}_{2}(\mathbf{X}_{n},K,k)=\mathsf{MN}_{2}(\emptyset,K_{|n},k)
, where
K_{|n}
is defined by (
2

Proof.
Consider first the case
k=1
. On the one hand,
\mathbf{z}_{1}=\mathsf{MN}_{2}(\mathbf{X}_{n},K,1)
minimizes
\mathbf{k}_{n}^{\top}(\mathbf{z})\mathbf{K}_{n}^{-1}\mathbf{p}_{K,n}(\mu)-P_{K,\mu}(\mathbf{z})
, see (
15
); on the other hand,
\mathbf{z}^{\prime}_{1}=\mathsf{MN}_{2}(\emptyset,K_{|n},1)
maximizes
P_{K_{|n},\mu}(\mathbf{z})=P_{K,\mu}(\mathbf{z})-\mathbf{k}_{n}^{\top}(\mathbf{z})\mathbf{K}_{n}^{-1}\mathbf{p}_{K,n}(\mu)
, see (
14
). One can therefore choose
\mathbf{z}_{1}=\mathbf{z}^{\prime}_{1}
.

The identity of the two constructions at any
k>1
is a consequence of the conditioning property of GP: at step
k
, they both use the kernel
K_{|n+k}
. More precisely,
\mathbf{z}_{k+1}
for the construction of
\mathsf{MN}_{2}(\mathbf{X}_{n},K,k+1)
minimizes

J(\mathbf{z})=\mathbf{k}_{n+k}^{\top}(\mathbf{z})\mathbf{K}_{n+k}^{-1}\mathbf{p}_{K,n+k}(\mu)-P_{K,\mu}(\mathbf{z})
, where

\displaystyle\mathbf{k}_{n+k}(\mathbf{z})=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{k}_{n}(\mathbf{z})\\
\mathbf{k}_{k}(\mathbf{z})\\
\end{array}\right)\,,\ \mathbf{p}_{K,n+k}(\mu)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{p}_{K,n}(\mu)\\
\mathbf{p}_{K,k}(\mu)\\
\end{array}\right)\,,\ \mathbf{K}_{n+k}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathbf{K}_{n}&\mathbf{K}_{n,k}\\
\mathbf{K}_{k,n}&\mathbf{K}_{k}\\
\end{array}\right)\,,

while
\mathbf{z}^{\prime}_{k+1}
for
\mathsf{MN}_{2}(\emptyset,K_{|n},k+1)
minimizes
J^{\prime}(\mathbf{z})={\mathbf{k}_{|n}}_{k}^{\top}(\mathbf{z}){\mathbf{K}_{|n}}_{k}^{-1}\mathbf{p}_{K_{|n},k}(\mu)-P_{K_{|n},\mu}(\mathbf{z})
, where

\displaystyle{\mathbf{k}_{|n}}_{k}(\mathbf{z})=\mathbf{k}_{k}(\mathbf{z})-\mathbf{K}_{k,n}\mathbf{K}_{n}^{-1}\mathbf{k}_{n}(\mathbf{z})\,,\ \mathbf{p}_{K_{|n},k}(\mu)=\mathbf{p}_{K,k}(\mu)-\mathbf{K}_{k,n}\mathbf{K}_{n}^{-1}\mathbf{p}_{K,n}(\mu)

and
{\mathbf{K}_{|n}}_{k}=\mathbf{K}_{k}-\mathbf{K}_{k,n}\mathbf{K}_{n}^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n,k}
. Direct application of Woodbury identity for matrix inversion and inversion of a block matrix shows that
J(\mathbf{z})=J^{\prime}(\mathbf{z})
; we can thus choose the same
\mathbf{z}_{k+1}
in both constructions in case multiple choices are possible.

5.1
Separable kernels

The substitution of a finite set
{\mathscr{X}}_{Q}
for
{\mathscr{X}}
and of the uniform measure on
{\mathscr{X}}_{Q}
for
\mu
yields a drastic simplification of calculations in the evaluation of the MMD
\gamma_{\overline{K}_{|n}}(\zeta_{m},\mu)
and in the algorithmic construction of designs by kernel herding and its variants. However, for large
d
we need to take
Q
very large to make
{\mathscr{X}}_{Q}
dense enough in
{\mathscr{X}}
, and another approach is required if we want to maintain a reasonable accuracy.

A bottleneck in the application of kernel herding is the need to calculate
P_{\overline{K}_{|n},\mu}(\mathbf{z})
for many
\mathbf{z}
in order to choose
\mathbf{z}_{k+1}
in (
9
). An additional difficulty for the evaluation of
\gamma_{\overline{K}_{|n}}(\zeta_{m},\mu)
is the need to compute
{\mathscr{E}}_{\overline{K}_{|n}}(\mu)
, see (
10
). However, when
K
is a separable (tensor-product) kernel, both
P_{\overline{K}_{|n},\mu}
and
{\mathscr{E}}_{\overline{K}_{|n}}(\mu)
can be calculated explicitly.

Since
\mu
is uniform on
{\mathscr{X}}=[0,1]^{2}
, we can write
\mu(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x})=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\mu_{1}(\mathrm{d}x_{i})
with
\mu_{1}
the uniform measure on
[0,1]
and
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})^{\top}
. For a separable (or tensor-product) kernel
K
, such that

\displaystyle K(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=\prod_{i=1}^{d}K_{i}(x_{i},x^{\prime}_{i})\,,

where
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})^{\top}
and
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=(x^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,x^{\prime}_{d})^{\top}
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathscr{E}}_{K}(\mu)=\prod_{i=1}^{d}{\mathscr{E}}_{K_{i}}(\mu_{1})\mbox{ and }P_{K,\mu}(\mathbf{x})=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\int_{{\mathscr{X}}_{i}}K_{i}(x_{i},x^{\prime}_{i})\,\mu_{1}(\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}_{i})=\prod_{i=1}^{d}P_{K_{i},\mu_{1}}(x_{i})\,.

One may refer to
Szabó and Sriperumbudur, 2018
for connections between positive-definiteness properties of the
K_{i}
and those of
K
.
The expressions of
{\mathscr{E}}_{K_{i}}(\mu_{1})
and
P_{K_{i},\mu_{1}}(\cdot)
are available for many kernels
K_{i}
; see
Pronzato and Zhigljavsky, 2020
and the references therein.

Before deriving the expressions of
P_{\overline{K}_{|n},\mu}(\mathbf{x})
and
{\mathscr{E}}_{\overline{K}_{|n}}(\mu)
, we introduce some notation.
Denote by
\overline{\mathbf{\Omega}}_{K,n}
and
\overline{\mathbf{\Gamma}}_{K,n}
the
n\times n
matrices with respective elements

\displaystyle\{\overline{\mathbf{\Omega}}_{K,n}\}_{j,k}=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\beta_{K_{i}}({x_{j}}_{i},{x_{k}}_{i})\mbox{ and }\{\overline{\mathbf{\Gamma}}_{K,n}\}_{j,k}=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\gamma_{K_{i}}({x_{j}}_{i},{x_{k}}_{i})\,,

and by
\overline{\mathbf{\omega}}_{K,n}(\mathbf{x})
the vector with
j
-th component

\displaystyle\{\overline{\mathbf{\omega}}_{K,n}(\mathbf{x})\}_{j}=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\beta_{K_{i}}({x_{j}}_{i},x_{i})\,,

where
{x_{j}}_{i}
(respectively,
{x_{k}}_{i}
) is the
i
-th component of
\mathbf{x}_{j}
(respectively,
\mathbf{x}_{k}
), and

\displaystyle\beta_{K_{i}}(r,s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathscr{X}}K_{i}(r,t)K_{i}(s,t)\,\mu_{1}(\mathrm{d}t)\,,\ i=1,\ldots,d\,,

\displaystyle\gamma_{K_{i}}(r,s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathscr{X}}^{2}}K_{i}(r,t)K_{i}(s,u)K_{i}(t,u)\,\mu_{1}(\mathrm{d}t)\mu_{1}(\mathrm{d}u)\,,\ i=1,\ldots,d\,.

Then, using (
5
), direct calculation gives

\displaystyle P_{\overline{K}_{|n},\mu}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\,P_{K^{2},\mu}(\mathbf{x})-4\,\mathbf{k}_{n}^{\top}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{K}_{n}^{-1}\overline{\mathbf{\omega}}_{K,n}(\mathbf{x})+2\,\mathbf{k}_{n}^{\top}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{K}_{n}^{-1}\overline{\mathbf{\Omega}}_{K,n}\mathbf{K}_{n}^{-1}\mathbf{k}_{n}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle+\left[1-\mathbf{k}_{n}^{\top}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{K}_{n}^{-1}\mathbf{k}_{n}(\mathbf{x})\right]\left[1-\mathrm{trace}(\mathbf{K}_{n}^{-1}\overline{\mathbf{\Omega}}_{K,n})\right]\,,

\displaystyle{\mathscr{E}}_{\overline{K}_{|n}}(\mu)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\,{\mathscr{E}}_{K^{2}}(\mu)-4\,\mathrm{trace}(\mathbf{K}_{n}^{-1}\overline{\mathbf{\Gamma}}_{K,n})+2\,\mathrm{trace}\left[(\mathbf{K}_{n}^{-1}\overline{\mathbf{\Gamma}}_{K,n})^{2}\right]+\left[1-\mathrm{trace}(\mathbf{K}_{n}^{-1}\overline{\mathbf{\Omega}}_{K,n})\right]^{2}\,.

The expressions of
P_{K^{2},\mu_{1}}(x)
,
{\mathscr{E}}_{K^{2}}(\mu_{1})
,
\beta_{K}(u,v)
and
\gamma_{K}(u,v)
,
x,u,v\in[0,1]
, for
\mu_{1}
uniform on
[0,1]
and
K_{i}(x,x^{\prime})
a Matérn 3/2 kernel (
12
) are given in Appendix B, making the expressions of
P_{\overline{K}_{|n},\mu}(\mathbf{x})
and
{\mathscr{E}}_{\overline{K}_{|n}}(\mu)
available in closed form when
K(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
is the product of uni-dimensional Matérn 3/2 kernels and
\mu
is uniform on
{\mathscr{X}}=[0,1]^{d}
. Similar calculations can be conducted for other kernels.

6
Conclusions

The construction of a validation design
\mathbf{Z}_{m}
aimed at estimating
\mathsf{ISE}(\mathbf{X}_{n})
for a given
\mathbf{X}_{n}
can be casted as the choice of a design minimizing a maximum mean discrepancy for a particular kernel, conditional of
\mathbf{X}_{n}
. A sequence of nested validation designs can be obtained by incremental construction via kernel herding. Numerical experiments indicate that the most important characteristics of a good validation design are its space-filling properties (it should populate the holes left by
\mathbf{X}_{n}
to properly explore the design space) and the weighting of its points (since evaluations far from the design points tend to overestimate the global error).
What one would expect is that some combination of both is needed: if the validation points would sample the error well, no
weighting would be needed; if the points were space-filling, and the design very regular, a contant weight smaller than one would be almost optimal. In fact these factors play in antagonistic directions and some compromise is needed.
A dedicated weighting method, based on a particular kernel, conditional on
\mathbf{X}_{n}
, has been proposed. Numerical simulations with random functions show the effectiveness of this weight reduction when it is applied to random or usual low-discrepancy designs. Performances are still better when the weight reduction is associated with a space-filling design that minimizes a kernel discrepancy: they are significantly better than those obtained with leave-one-out cross validation, which strongly overestimates
\mathsf{ISE}(\mathbf{X}_{n})
.

Appendix A: Characteristic kernels

A characteristic kernel
C
defines a metric on the set of probability measures on
{\mathscr{X}}
.
It is called
Integrally Strictly Positive Definite
(ISPD) when
{\mathscr{E}}_{C}(\nu)>0
for any nonzero signed measure
\nu
on
{\mathscr{X}}
, see (
6
), and
Conditionally Integrally Strictly Positive Definite
(CISPD) when
{\mathscr{E}}_{C}(\nu)>0
for all nonzero signed measures
\nu
on
{\mathscr{X}}
with total mass
\nu({\mathscr{X}})=0
. An ISPD kernel is CISPD; a bounded ISPD kernel is SPD and defines an RKHS. When
C
is uniformly bounded, it is characteristic if and only if it is CISPD; see
(
Sriperumbudur et al., 2010
, Lemma 8)
. For instance, the isotropic squared exponential, Matérn and generalized multiquadric kernels are ISPD.

The kernel
\overline{K}_{|n}
considered in this paper is positive definite but not strictly positive definite (and thus not ISPD). Indeed,
K_{|n}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_{i})=\overline{K}_{|n}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_{i})=0
for all
\mathbf{x}
and all
\mathbf{x}_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,n
, implying that
\gamma_{\overline{K}_{|n}}(\zeta,\xi)=0
for any measures
\zeta
and
\xi
supported on
\mathbf{X}_{n}
.
Since
\mu
is uniform and not supported on
\mathbf{X}_{n}
, it nevertheless makes sense to minimize
\gamma_{\overline{K}_{|n}}(\zeta_{m},\mu)
.
The investigation of conditions under which
\gamma_{\overline{K}_{|n}}(\zeta,\mu)=0
would imply
\zeta=\mu
, exploiting for instance the notion of universal kernel
(
Sriperumbudur et al., 2011
)
, is beyond the scope of this paper and we simply mention the following two points, concerning respectively
K_{|n}
and
\overline{K}_{|n}
.

(
i
)

Suppose that
K
is ISPD. For any signed measure
\xi
on
{\mathscr{X}}
and
\mathbf{w}\in\mathds{R}^{n}
, define
\xi^{[\mathbf{w}]}=\xi+\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\,\delta_{\mathbf{x}_{i}}
. Then, using the notation of Section
3.1
,
{\mathscr{E}}_{K}(\xi^{[\mathbf{w}]}-\mu)={\mathscr{E}}_{K}(\xi-\mu)+\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{n}\mathbf{w}+2\,\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{p}_{K,n}(\xi-\mu)\geq 0
, with equality if and only if
\xi^{[\mathbf{w}]}=\mu
since
K
is ISPD. Direct calculation gives

\min_{\mathbf{w}}{\mathscr{E}}_{K}(\xi^{[\mathbf{w}]}-\mu)={\mathscr{E}}_{K_{|n}}(\xi-\mu)
, and therefore
{\mathscr{E}}_{K_{|n}}(\xi-\mu)\geq 0
with equality if and only if
\xi^{[\mathbf{w}]}=\mu
. As
\mu
has no discrete component,
\xi^{[\mathbf{w}]}=\mu
implies
\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{0}
, and we get
\xi=\mu
.

(
ii
)

Suppose now that
K
is ISPD and continuous on
{\mathscr{X}}
and consider its Mercer decomposition:
K(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=\sum_{i\geq 1}\lambda_{i}\,\varphi_{i}(\mathbf{x})\varphi_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
,
\lambda_{i}>0
. It yields the following decomposition for
K^{2}
:
K^{2}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=\sum_{i,j\geq 1}\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}\,\varphi_{i}(\mathbf{x})\varphi_{j}(\mathbf{x})\varphi_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\varphi_{j}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
, and
{\mathscr{E}}_{K^{2}}(\nu)=0
for some signed measure
\nu
on
{\mathscr{X}}
implies that
\int_{\mathscr{X}}\varphi_{i}(\mathbf{x})\varphi_{j}(\mathbf{x})\,\nu(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x})=0
for all
i
and
j
. When the constant
1
belongs to the
RKHS
\mathcal{H}_{K}
associated with
K
,
there exists constants
\alpha_{i}
,
i\geq 1
, such that
\sum_{i\geq 1}\alpha_{i}\,\varphi_{i}(\mathbf{x})=1
for all
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathscr{X}}
, and
{\mathscr{E}}_{K^{2}}(\nu)=0
implies that
\int_{\mathscr{X}}\varphi_{j}(\mathbf{x})\,\nu(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x})=0
for all
j\geq 1
. Therefore,
{\mathscr{E}}_{K}(\nu)=0
, and
\nu=0
since
K
is ISPD. However, this argumentation cannot be combined with (
i
) above to show that
{\mathscr{E}}_{K_{|n}^{2}}(\xi-\mu)=0
implies that
\xi=\mu
since
1\not\in\mathcal{H}_{K_{|n}}
: indeed,
f(\mathbf{x}_{i})=0
for any
f\in\mathcal{H}_{K_{|n}}
and any
\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbf{X}_{n}
.

Consider the family of Legendre polynomials, orthonormal for the uniform measure
\mu_{1}
on
{\mathscr{X}}_{1}=[0,1]

\displaystyle P_{0}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle P_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{3}\,(2\,x-1)

\displaystyle P_{2}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{5}\,(6\,x^{2}-6\,x+1)

\displaystyle P_{3}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{7}\,(20\,x^{3}-30\,x^{2}+12\,x-1)

\displaystyle P_{4}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 3\,(70\,x^{4}-140\,x^{3}+90\,x^{2}-20\,x+1)

Denote by
\mathds{L}

\mathds{N}^{d}

. The multivariate polynomials we consider have the form

\displaystyle P(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{\underline{\ell}\in\mathds{L}}\beta_{\underline{\ell}}\Psi_{\underline{\ell}}(\mathbf{x})\,,
