Pythagorean Theorem in Elamite Mathematics
Nasser Heydari, Kazuo Muroi

Abstract
This article studies the application of the Pythagorean theorem in the Susa Mathematical Texts ( SMT ) and we discuss those texts whose problems and related calculations demonstrate its use. Among these texts, SMT No. 1 might be the most important as it contains a geometric application of the Pythagorean theorem.

Pythagorean Theorem

2.1
Statement

One of the oldest elementary mathematical theorems taught to students in middle or high school is the
Pythagorean theorem
. Geometrically, this theorem states that for any given right triangle, the area of the square whose side is the hypotenuse (that opposite the right angle) is equal to the sum of the areas of the two squares whose sides form the other two legs of the right triangle. A pictorial representation of this theorem is given in
Fig.

1
in which the total area of the two smaller green squares is equal to the area of the bigger orange one.

Figure 1:
Pythagorean theorem

The best-known statement of this theorem is usually given as an algebraic equation with respect to the lengths of sides of a right triangle. Consider a right triangle
\triangle ABC
such that its angle
\angle ACB=90^{\circ}
and the lengths of its two legs are given by
\overline{BC}=a
,
\overline{AC}=b
and that of its hypotenuse by
\overline{AB}=c
. Then the Pythagorean theorem simply states that

c^{2}=a^{2}+b^{2}.

(1)

This is usually called the
Pythagorean rule
or
Pythagorean formula
. It should be noted that the converse of the Pythagorean theorem is also true in that if the lengths of three sides of a triangle satisfy
Eq.

1
, then the triangle has a right angle.

2.3
Applications

The Pythagorean theorem has different applications in mathematics. Here, we only list some of the most important applications.

Construction of Incommensurable Lengths

One of the earliest applications of this theorem is to construct lengths like
\sqrt{2},\sqrt{3},\sqrt{5}
and so on. Two non-zero real numbers
a
and
b
are called
incommensurable
if their ratio is not a rational number. For example, any pair
(\sqrt{n},1)
, where
n
is not a perfect square, is incommensurable.

Note that for any natural number
n
, we always can write

\sqrt{n}=\sqrt{n-1+1}=\sqrt{(\sqrt{n-1})^{2}+1^{2}}.

This says that the length
\sqrt{n}
is constructible by straightedge and compass, if we can construct
\sqrt{n-1}
. In that case, all we need to do is to construct a right triangle with legs
1
and
\sqrt{n-1}
. The hypotenuse is then the square root
\sqrt{n}
(see
Fig.

4
).

Figure 4:
Incommensurable lengths

Euclidean Distance

Perhaps one of the main applications of this theorem appears in Euclidean geometry where it serves as a basis for the definition of the distance between two points in the plane. The distance
d(A,B)
between two points
A=(x_{1},y_{1})
and
B=(x_{2},y_{2})
in the
xy
-coordinate system is defined as

d(A,B)=\sqrt{(x_{2}-x_{1})^{2}+(y_{2}-y_{1})^{2}}.

(3)

Note that a similar formula is also used for Euclidean spaces of higher dimensions.

It is clear from
Fig.

5
that the distance
d
is the length of the hypotenuse
AB
in the blue right triangle
\triangle AHB
whose legs are of lengths
\overline{AH}=x_{2}-x_{1}
and
\overline{BH}=y_{2}-y_{1}
. Formula
Eq.

3
is a direct consequence of formula
Eq.

1
.

Figure 5:

Trigonometry

Trigonometry is based on right triangles and the relation between their sides and angles. The two main trigonometric relations, i.e., the
sine
and the
cosine
of an acute angle
\theta
in a right triangle with legs
a,b
and hypotenuse
c
are defined as
\sin(\theta)=\frac{a}{c}
and
\cos(\theta)=\frac{b}{c}
(see
Fig.

6
). The Pythagorean theorem simply implies the most famous trigonometric identity:

\sin^{2}(\theta)+\cos^{2}(\theta)=1.

In a sense, this identity and the Pythagorean rule are equivalent.

Complex Numbers

Complex numbers are defined as
z=x+iy
where
x,y
are real numbers and
i=\sqrt{-1}
is the
imaginary unit
. Any complex number

can be identified with the point
(x,y)
in the two-dimensional coordinate system (see
Fig.

of

r^{2}=x^{2}+y^{2}
.

m>n>0

(m^{2}-n^{2},2mn,m^{2}+n^{2})

(m^{2}+n^{2})^{2}=(m^{2}-n^{2})^{2}+(2mn)^{2}.

The inverse of this statement is also true: for any primitive Pythagorean triple

2.6
Generalization

It is interesting that if we replace the squares in the Pythagorean theorem with similar shapes, the relation between their areas still holds. In other words, if we build similar figures on the sides of a right triangle, then the area of the figure on the hypotenuse is equal to the sum of those of the two other figures on the legs.

Bruins’ Interpretation:

He considered the constant

for a circle and suggested the following approximate formula for the area

c

S\approx(0;57,36)\times\frac{c^{2}}{12}.

Since the area of a circle with circumference

it follows that

\frac{c^{2}}{4\pi}\approx\frac{c^{2}}{12}\times\frac{24}{25},

which implies that

\pi\approx\frac{25}{8}=3.125.

Neugebauer’s Interpretation:

4
Conclusion

The implicit and explicit applications of the Pythagorean theorem found in the
SMT
show that the Elamite scribes–like their Babylonian counterparts–were fully aware of this basic theorem. They freely used the Pythagorean rule whenever their calculations involved computing a side of a right triangle.

Besides the algebraic applications of this theorem in the
SMT
, the scribe of
SMT No. 1
has presented a geometric application of the theorem. Although the scribe has only given the numerical data on the tablet, its mathematical interpretation clearly confirms that obtaining these numbers requires the application of the Pythagorean theorem. In fact, this text and the Babylonian text
YBC 7289
might be the only geometric applications of the Pythagorean theorem in Elamite and Babylonian mathematics.
