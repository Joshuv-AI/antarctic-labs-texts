Gravitational lens on a static optical constant-curvature background: Its application to Weyl gravity model
Keita Takizawa, Hideki Asada

Abstract
This paper extends the de-Sitter/anti-de Sitter (dS/AdS) background method based on the optical metric for gravitational lens [Phys. Rev. D 105 , 084022 (2022)] to a static optical constant-curvature (SOCC) background. It is shown that the exact lens equation on the SOCC background can be written in the same form as that for either Minkowski, dS or AdS background in terms of flat, spherical or hyperbolic trigonometry, depending on the Gaussian curvature of the equatorial plane in the SOCC background. To exemplify the SOCC method, we consider the gravitational lens in Mannheim-Kazanas (MK) solution of Weyl gravity, which includes Rindler and de Sitter terms. In the zero mass limit, the deflection angle of light for the MK solution in the literature diverges to infinity. This is because there is a self-contradiction in their perturbative approximations of the MK metric and the orbit equation. The SOCC method incorporates the long-distance curvature effect into the background. Thereby the SOCC expression for the deflection angle of light in the MK solution is finite also in the zero mass limit.

II
Background and lens

II.1
Background spacetime metric

We examine the light deflection due to a localized lens object. We denote lens parameters as
p_{i}
where
i
labels each parameter of the lens object (e.g. mass, spin parameter, electric charge, and so on). The zero point of
p_{i}\,(p_{i}=0)
is chosen when the lens object does not exist. There can be global parameters other than the localized lens parameters, which are denoted by
q_{i}
(e.g. the cosmological constant
\Lambda
). If
q_{i}\neq 0
, the background is not Minkowskian.

The spacetime metric can be written as

\displaystyle ds^{2}=g_{\mu\nu}(p_{i},q_{i})\,dx^{\mu}dx^{\nu}\,\,.

(1)

We can thus define a background metric as

\displaystyle\bar{g}_{\mu\nu}\equiv g_{\mu\nu}(p_{i},q_{i})|_{p_{i}=0}\,\,,

(2)

where an overbar denotes a quantity in the background.

II.2
Background optical metric

In this paper, we focus on a static and spherically symmetric spacetime.
The line element can be written as

\displaystyle ds^{2}=-A(r)dt^{2}+B(r)dr^{2}+C(r)d\Omega^{2}\,\,,

(3)

where
d\Omega^{2}\equiv d\theta^{2}+\sin^{2}\theta\,d\phi^{2}
. For this metric, we treat the optical metric followed by the null condition
ds^{2}=0
, which determines the light propagation e.g.
Ishihara2016
;
GW
;
AK2000
.
The optical metric describes a three-dimensional Riemannian space, where the distance between two points is given by
d\ell^{2}\equiv dt^{2}
.

Since the spacetime is spherically symmetric, the photon orbital plane can be chosen as the equatorial plane without the loss of generality. Hence the hypersurface which is defined by the optical metric becomes a two-dimensional Riemannian space.
It is a hypersurface at
\theta=\pi/2
.

We can write the optical metric for this two-dimensional space as

\displaystyle d\ell^{2}

\displaystyle\equiv\gamma_{IJ}dx^{I}dx^{J}

\displaystyle=\frac{B(r)}{A(r)}dr^{2}+\frac{C(r)}{A(r)}d\phi^{2}\,\,,

(4)

where subscripts denote the spatial coordinates
r
and
\phi
.
According to the definition of the background, a background optical metric is defined as

\displaystyle\bar{\gamma}_{IJ}

\displaystyle\equiv\gamma_{IJ}|_{p_{i}=0}\,\,.

(5)

For later convenience, we define the circumference radius on the optical metric as

\displaystyle\tilde{r}

\displaystyle\equiv\sqrt{\frac{\bar{C}(r)}{\bar{A}(r)}}\,\,.

(6)

We define also a function which represents the inverse of the radial component of the optical metric with respect to
\tilde{r}
as

\displaystyle\bar{F}(\tilde{r})

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{\bar{\gamma}_{\tilde{r}\tilde{r}}}\,\,.

(7)

Thereby, the line element of the background space can be written in terms of
\tilde{r}
as

\displaystyle d\bar{\ell}^{2}=\frac{d\tilde{r}^{2}}{\bar{F}(\tilde{r})}+\tilde{r}^{2}d\phi^{2}\,\,.

(8)

III
SOCC approach

III.1
SOCC background

In the above definitions of the background and the gravitational lens, the background is not necessarily flat.
In a non-flat background space, a conventional gravitational lens equation based on the Euclidean geometry is not valid. Deviations may be significant at large distance

Takizawa2022
.

Furthermore, the present paper focuses on a background with a constant Gaussian curvature. In differential geometry, a
N
-dimensional constant-curvature space is isometric (under the assumptions of simple connectivity and completeness) to either Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, sphere
S^{N}
, or hyperbolic space
H^{N}
space.

Hence a geometry of the background must be either Euclidean, spherical, or hyperbolic, depending on the sign of the constant Gaussian curvature of the background.

The Gaussian curvature for Eq. (
8
) can be calculated as
Werner2012

\displaystyle\bar{K}^{\mathrm{opt}}

\displaystyle=\frac{R_{\tilde{r}\phi\tilde{r}\phi}}{\mathrm{det}(\bar{\gamma}_{IJ})}\,\,.

(9)

Since the metric depends only on the radial coordinate, the condition for being the SOCC background is

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bar{K}^{\mathrm{opt}}}{\partial r}=0\,\,.

(10)

We impose the regularity condition
\bar{F}(\tilde{r})\neq 0
and the normalization
\bar{F}(\tilde{r})|_{\tilde{r}=0}=1
.
Then, a general solution of Eq. (
10
) is obtained as

\displaystyle\bar{F}(\tilde{r})=1+\kappa\tilde{r}^{2}\,\,,

(11)

where
\kappa
is an integration constant.

Therefore, the optical metric for the background space for Eq. (
11
) describes a constant-curvature space
CCS
.
The Gaussian curvature for Eq. (
11
) is related to
\kappa
as

\displaystyle\bar{K}^{\mathrm{opt}}=-\kappa\,\,.

(12)

Flat trigonometry based on the Euclidean geometry can be used for flat SOCC backgrounds
(\bar{K}^{\mathrm{opt}}=0)
, while spherical and hyperbolic trigonometry are used for positive SOCC backgrounds
(\bar{K}^{\mathrm{opt}}>0)
and negative SOCC backgrounds
(\bar{K}^{\mathrm{opt}}<0)
, respectively.

Any metric function quadratic in
r
can satisfy Eq. (
11
),
after the origin of
r
is appropriately shifted.
One example is de Sitter (or AdS) metric. This has been recently investigated
Takizawa2022
. Another interesting example is the massless case of the Mannheim-Kazanas (MK) metric in conformal Weyl gravity
MK
. This case is discussed in Section
\mathrm{IV}
.

III.3
Iterative solutions

In order to solve the lens equation analytically,
we consider an iterative method with assuming small angles. From the relation between an impact parameter of a light ray
b
and an image position
b=D_{L}\sin\theta
, the angular position of a lensed image can be approximated as
\theta\sim b/D_{L}
. Since this ratio is sufficiently smaller than unity under the small angle approximation, let
b/D_{L}
be
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
, where
\varepsilon
is a non-dimensional small parameter.

Hence we consider a Taylor-series
expansion of
\theta
as

\displaystyle\theta

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{n}\theta_{(n)}\,\,,

(15)

where the expansion parameter
\varepsilon
plays a role as a book-keeping.
As we shall discuss below,
if we assume
m\ll b
, the ratio
m/b
is much smaller than unity, where
m
denotes a lens mass. In this weak deflection approximation, therefore,
\alpha\sim m/b=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
. In the weak deflection and small angle approximations, we assume
\varepsilon/\epsilon=\mathcal{O}(1)
. Hence,
\alpha
can be expanded in terms of
\varepsilon
as

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{n}\alpha_{(n)}\,\,.

(16)

As shown below,

\alpha\sim m/b\sim m/D_{L}\theta
.
From
m/D_{L}\propto\varepsilon^{2}
and
\theta=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
,
\alpha
is
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
in our expansion method.
\beta
is given by hand. We examine a case that multiple images occur, for which
\beta\sim\theta_{E}\sim\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
.
This is because
\theta_{E}\sim\sqrt{m/D_{L}}=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
.
Therefore, we take

\beta=\varepsilon\beta_{(1)}
.

The solutions of Eq. (
III.2
) are obtained up to the fourth order in
\varepsilon
as

\displaystyle\theta_{(1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{D_{S}}{D_{L}+D_{LS}}\beta_{(1)}+\frac{D_{LS}}{D_{L}+D_{LS}}\alpha_{(1)}\,\,,

(17)

\displaystyle\theta_{(2)}

\displaystyle=\frac{D_{LS}}{D_{L}+D_{LS}}\alpha_{(2)}\,\,,

(18)

and

\displaystyle\theta_{(3)}=

\displaystyle\frac{D_{LS}}{D_{L}+D_{LS}}\alpha_{(3)}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{3}\frac{D_{S}}{D_{L}+D_{LS}}\left[1-\left(\frac{D_{L}}{D_{LS}}\right)^{2}\right]\beta_{(1)}^{3}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{D_{S}}{D_{LS}}\right)^{2}\frac{D_{L}(1-\bar{K}^{\mathrm{opt}}D_{LS}^{2})}{D_{L}+D_{LS}}\beta_{(1)}^{2}\theta_{(1)}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{6}\frac{D_{L}}{D_{L}+D_{LS}}\left[1-\left(\frac{D_{L}}{D_{LS}}\right)^{2}\right]\theta_{(1)}^{3}\,\,.

(19)

In order to fully consider the constant-curvature effect of the background, the background dependence of the deflection angle of light should be clarified. This clarification will be done by using a concrete example in Section

IV.2
Metric splitting

We have discussed how to split a full metric into a background part and a lens part

In Eq. (

\displaystyle x^{\mu}=(t,r,\theta,\phi)\longrightarrow\hat{x}^{\mu}=(\hat{t},r,\hat{\theta},\hat{\phi})\,\,,

where the new coordinates with the hat are

Therefore, the MK metric in the coordinate system

\displaystyle=g_{\mu\nu}(\hat{x})d\hat{x}^{\mu}d\hat{x}^{\nu}

\displaystyle=-\frac{f(r)}{a^{2}}d\hat{t}^{2}+\frac{1}{f(r)}dr^{2}+\frac{r^{2}}{a}d\hat{\Omega}^{2}\,\,,

Next, we consider the following conformal transformation as

After these transformations, we obtain

\displaystyle d\hat{s}^{2}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{f(r)}{a}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{2\hat{m}}{r}+\hat{\gamma}r-\hat{k}r^{2}\,\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(r)_{\mathrm{bg}}

\displaystyle=1+\hat{\gamma}r-\hat{k}r^{2}\,\,,

(35)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(r)_{\mathrm{lens}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2\hat{m}}{r}\,\,.

IV.4
Deflection angle of light

Here we follow Reference
Ishihara2016
to adopt the definition of
the deflection angle of light in the optical metric.
The definition is valid even in an asymptotically non-flat spacetime

Takizawa2020a

\displaystyle\alpha\equiv\Psi_{R}-\Psi_{S}+\phi_{RS}\,\,,

(62)

where
\Psi_{R}
and
\Psi_{S}
denote the angle between the unit vector along the radial direction from the center of the lens object and a unit tangential vector along the light ray at a receiver and a source, respectively.

\phi_{RS}
denotes the coordinate separation angle between a source and a receiver.
Note that Reference
Takizawa2020a
focuses only on the flat background case.
The present paper investigates a more general background with a constant curvature.

Thus,
\alpha
is a function of the parameters
p_{i}
and
q_{j}
.

\displaystyle\alpha=\alpha(p_{i},q_{j})\,\,.

(63)

We wish to consider only the contributions due to the lens objects in a consistent manner with the SOCC background.
\alpha(p_{i}=0,q_{j})
is the contribution purely from the background and it should be subtracted from
\alpha(p_{i},q_{j})
. Namely, the deflection angle in the SOCC approach
should be

\displaystyle\alpha(p_{i})\equiv\alpha(p_{i},q_{j})-\alpha(p_{i}=0,q_{j})\,\,.

(64)

In a simple case that the metric has only the cosmological constant as the background parameter, Eq. (
64
) recovers Eq. (77) in Reference
Takizawa2022
.

To obtain the expression for
\phi_{RS}
, we discuss null geodesics in the rescaled MK metric Eq. (
IV.2
). The constants of motion of light rays are found by noting Killing vector fields in
\hat{t}
and
\hat{\phi}
directions as

\displaystyle\hat{E}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}(r)_{\mathrm{bg}}\frac{d\hat{t}}{d\hat{\lambda}}\,\,,

(65)

\displaystyle\hat{J}

\displaystyle=r^{2}\frac{d\hat{\phi}}{d\hat{\lambda}}\,\,,

(66)

where
\hat{\lambda}
is an affine parameter in the coordinate system
\hat{x}^{\mu}
. Then, the impact parameter of light is defined by

\displaystyle\hat{b}\equiv\frac{\hat{J}}{\hat{E}}=\frac{r^{2}}{\mathcal{F}(r)_{\mathrm{bg}}}\frac{d\hat{\phi}}{d\hat{t}}\,\,.

(67)

From the null condition, the orbit equation for light rays are obtained in terms of
u\equiv 1/r
as

\displaystyle\left(\frac{du}{d\hat{\phi}}\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{\hat{b}^{2}}-u^{2}-\hat{\gamma}u+\hat{k}+2\hat{m}u^{3}\,\,.

(68)

Eq. (
68
) is rewritten under the transformation
U\equiv u+\hat{\gamma}/2
as

\displaystyle\left(\frac{dU}{d\hat{\phi}}\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{\hat{B}^{2}}-U^{2}+2\hat{m}\left(U-\frac{\hat{\gamma}}{2}\right)^{3}\,\,,

(69)

where the Gaussian curvature term can be absorbed as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\hat{B}^{2}}=\frac{1}{\hat{b}^{2}}-\hat{K}^{\mathrm{opt}}\,\,.

(70)

Note that the negative
\hat{\gamma}
contributes to a photon sphere, where the radius is
r_{\mathrm{PS}}=-2/\hat{\gamma}
. Thus the transformation means
U=u-u_{\mathrm{PS}}
, where
u_{\mathrm{P}S}\equiv 1/r_{\mathrm{P}S}
. Since
U>0
, this transformation implies for the light ray following Eq. (
69
),
r<r_{\mathrm{PS}}
.

Since the
\hat{m}
-independent terms in the orbit equation are derived from

\mathcal{F}(r)_{\mathrm{bg}}
,
we substitute the iterative solution

\displaystyle U(\hat{\phi})=U_{\mathrm{bg}}+\hat{m}U_{\hat{m}}+\mathcal{O}(\hat{m}^{2})\,\,,

(71)

into Eq. (
69
). By direct calculations, the first term (background part) in Eq. (
71
) is obtained as

\displaystyle U_{\mathrm{bg}}=\frac{\sin(\hat{\phi}+C_{\mathrm{bg}})}{\hat{B}}\,\,,

(72)

where
C_{\mathrm{bg}}
is an integration constant.

Since the spacetime is spherically symmetric,
the light ray has reflection symmetry with respect to angular coordinate value

\hat{\phi}=\pi\sqrt{a}/2

that corresponds to the closest approach. Therefore, the integration constant is determined as

\displaystyle C_{\mathrm{bg}}=\frac{\pi}{2}(1-\sqrt{a})\,\,.

(73)

Hence the zeroth-order solution is

\displaystyle U_{\mathrm{bg}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sin\hat{\varphi}}{\hat{B}}=\frac{\sqrt{1-\hat{b^{2}}\hat{K}^{\mathrm{opt}}}}{\hat{b}}\sin\hat{\varphi}\,\,,

(74)

where the argument of the sine function is
\hat{\varphi}\equiv\hat{\phi}+C_{\mathrm{bg}}
. The transformations
u\longrightarrow U
and
\hat{\phi}\longrightarrow\hat{\varphi}
do not change the orbit equation nor its solution because
dU=du
and
d\hat{\phi}=d\hat{\varphi}
, respectively.
Note that the square root in Eq. (
74
) for
\hat{K}^{\mathrm{opt}}>0
leads to the upper bound on the impact parameter as

\displaystyle\hat{b}<\frac{1}{\sqrt{\hat{K}^{\mathrm{opt}}}}\,\,.

(75)

Namely, in the spherical SOCC background, the light rays with the impact parameter greater than the SOCC curvature radius are not allowed for
r<r_{\mathrm{PS}}
. Indeed, from Eq. (
68
) excluding
\hat{m}
term, it can be confirmed that
\hat{b}=1/\sqrt{\hat{K}^{\mathrm{opt}}}
when
r=r_{\mathrm{PS}}
.

By using Eq. (
74
), we obtain the linear term in either
\hat{m}
or
\hat{\gamma}
of Eq. (
71
) as

\displaystyle U_{\hat{m}}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\hat{B}^{2}}(1+\cos^{2}\hat{\varphi})

\displaystyle-\frac{3\hat{\gamma}}{2\hat{B}}\left[\sin\hat{\varphi}-\left(\hat{\varphi}-\frac{\pi}{2}\right)\cos\hat{\varphi}\right]\,\,.

(76)

Therefore, the iterative solution to the orbit equation in terms of
1/\hat{B}
is

\displaystyle U(\hat{\varphi})=

\displaystyle\frac{\sin\hat{\varphi}}{\hat{B}}+\frac{\hat{m}}{\hat{B}^{2}}(1+\cos^{2}\hat{\varphi})

\displaystyle-\frac{3\hat{m}\hat{\gamma}}{2\hat{B}}\left[\sin\hat{\varphi}-\left(\hat{\varphi}-\frac{\pi}{2}\right)\cos\hat{\varphi}\right]\,\,+\mathcal{O}(\hat{m}^{2}).

(77)

By using Eq. (
IV.4
), we obtain the angle as

\displaystyle\hat{\varphi}(U)=

\displaystyle\arcsin(\hat{B}U)-\frac{\hat{m}(2-\hat{B}^{2}U)}{\hat{B}\sqrt{1-\hat{B}^{2}U^{2}}}

\displaystyle-\frac{3\hat{m}\hat{\gamma}\hat{B}U}{2\sqrt{1-\hat{B}^{2}U^{2}}}-\frac{3}{2}\hat{m}\hat{\gamma}\left[\arcsin(\hat{B}U)-\frac{\pi}{2}\right]\,\,

\displaystyle+\mathcal{O}(\hat{m}^{2}).

(78)

Therefore, the separation angle in
\hat{\varphi}
is

\displaystyle\hat{\varphi}_{RS}

\displaystyle=\hat{\varphi}_{R}-\hat{\varphi}_{S}

\displaystyle=\pi-\hat{\varphi}(U_{R})-\hat{\varphi}(U_{S})\,\,.

(79)

Next, let us calculate the
\Psi
part in Eq. (
62
). From the definition,

\displaystyle\Psi=\arcsin\left[\hat{b}u\sqrt{\mathcal{F}(u)}\right]\,\,.

(80)

We can obtain the expression for
\Psi
by using
\delta\equiv\mathcal{F}(u)_{\mathrm{lens}}/\mathcal{F}(u)_{\mathrm{bg}}\ll 1
as

\displaystyle\Psi=\arcsin\left[\hat{b}u\sqrt{\mathcal{F}(u)_{\mathrm{bg}}}\left(1+\frac{\delta}{2}\right)\right]+\mathcal{O}\left(\delta^{2}\right)\,\,.

(81)

In Eq. (
81
), we can use the relation between
u
and
U
as

\displaystyle u\sqrt{\mathcal{F}(u)_{\mathrm{bg}}}=\sqrt{U^{2}+\hat{K}^{\mathrm{opt}}}\,\,.

(82)

The expression for
\Psi
in terms of
U
is thus obtained as

\displaystyle\Psi(U)=

\displaystyle\arcsin\left(\hat{b}U\sqrt{1+\frac{\hat{K}^{\mathrm{opt}}}{U^{2}}}\right)

\displaystyle-\frac{\hat{m}\hat{b}U^{2}}{\sqrt{1-\hat{b}^{2}U^{2}+\hat{b}^{2}\hat{K}^{\mathrm{opt}}}}\left(1-\frac{\hat{\gamma}}{2U}\right)^{3}\left(1+\frac{\hat{K}}{U^{2}}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle+\mathcal{O}(\hat{m}^{2})\,\,.

(83)

Hence the
\Psi
part is obtained as

\displaystyle\Psi_{R}-\Psi_{S}=\Psi(U_{R})+\Psi(U_{S})-\pi\,\,.

(84)

IV.5
Weak deflection of light by a small black hole

In order to clarify which parameter contributes perturbatively to the deflection angle of light, we assume the weak field of a small black hole as
|\hat{b}^{2}\hat{K}^{\mathrm{opt}}|\ll 1
and
|\hat{K}^{\mathrm{opt}}/U^{2}|\ll 1
. Therefore, the deflection angle in the rescaled MK metric is expressed as

\displaystyle\alpha_{\mathrm{MK}}=

\displaystyle\frac{\hat{b}\hat{K}^{\mathrm{opt}}}{2}\left(\frac{\sqrt{1-\hat{b}^{2}U_{S}^{2}}}{U_{S}}+\frac{\sqrt{1-\hat{b}^{2}U_{R}^{2}}}{U_{R}}\right)

\displaystyle-\frac{\hat{m}\hat{b}\hat{K}^{\mathrm{opt}}}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\hat{b}^{2}U_{S}^{2}}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\hat{b}^{2}U_{R}^{2}}}\right)

\displaystyle+3\hat{m}\hat{\gamma}\left(\frac{\hat{b}U_{S}}{\sqrt{1-\hat{b}^{2}U_{S}^{2}}}+\frac{\hat{b}U_{R}}{\sqrt{1-\hat{b}^{2}U_{R}^{2}}}\right)

\displaystyle-\frac{3}{2}\hat{m}\hat{\gamma}\left[\arcsin(\hat{b}U_{S})+\arcsin(\hat{b}U_{R})-\pi\right]

\displaystyle+\frac{2\hat{m}}{\hat{b}}\left(\sqrt{1-\hat{b}^{2}U_{S}^{2}}+\sqrt{1-\hat{b}^{2}U_{R}^{2}}\right)+\mathcal{O}(\hat{m}^{2})\,\,.

(85)

The first term (derived from
\mathcal{F}(r)_{\mathrm{bg}}
) in the right-hand side of Eq. (
IV.5
) is independent of
\hat{m}
. This means the SOCC background effect, while effects due to the lens object come from the other terms.

IV.6
Self-contradiction in perturbative approximations in the literature

First of all,
let us point out there is a self-contradiction in perturbative approximations
in
Sultana
;
Cattani
;
Bhattacharya2010
;
Bhattacharya2011
;
Sultana2013
,
which seems to have been unnoticed.

On deriving the deflection angle of light
in
Sultana
;
Cattani
;
Bhattacharya2010
;
Bhattacharya2011
;
Sultana2013
,
the MK metric is expanded around the Minkowski background.
This means

|\gamma r|\ll 1
in the metric expansion.

On the other hand,
the equation for a photon trajectory is expanded,
where the zeroth-order trajectory equation is assumed to
be in the form as

Sultana
;
Cattani
;
Bhattacharya2010
;
Bhattacharya2011
;
Sultana2013

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}u}{d\phi^{2}}=-u-\frac{\gamma}{2},

(86)

and
u\equiv 1/r
.
This zeroth-order choice is a root of the problem.
We shall discuss this problem below.

If
|\gamma r|\ll 1
is used for the zeroth-order equation,
the magnitude of the second term
-\gamma/2
of the right-hand side
in Eq. (
86
) is much less than that of the first term
\sim 1/r
.
To be correct, therefore,
the second term should be moved from the zeroth-order equation
to the next-order equation.

Namely, the use of the particular form of the zeroth-order equation
as Eq. (
86
)
implicitly needs another assumption as
\gamma r=O(1)
.
Therefore,
there is a self-contradiction between
(1)
|\gamma r|\ll 1
for the metric perturbation
and (2)
|\gamma r|=O(1)
at the zeroth-order trajectory equation.
This inconsistency makes the previous results unconvincing.

One such example of the unconvincing results is that
the deflection angle of light in

Sultana
;
Cattani
;
Bhattacharya2010
;
Bhattacharya2011
;
Sultana2013

includes an inverse-mass term.
See e.g.
-kb^{3}/(2\beta)
of Eq. (15) in
Sultana
,

-kr_{0}^{3}/(2m)
of Eq. (17) in
Cattani
,

-kR^{3}/(2m)
of Eq. (21) in
Bhattacharya2010
,
and

-kR^{3}/(2m)
in
Sultana2013

where
R=r_{0}
,
This inverse-mass term
diverges to infinity in the limit of the small lens mass (
m\to 0
).
If the inverse-mass term existed,
an infinitesimal mass could cause
a huge amount of the deflection of light.
However, this behavior makes no sense.
The unreasonable behavior is due to the inverse-mass term
as a consequence of the self-contradictory assumptions in the literature.

See Eq. (
IV.5
) for the deflection angle in the present paper.
There is no inverse-mass term in the deflection angle.
The present form of the deflection angle remains finite
in the zero mass limit (
m\to 0
).
Eq. (
IV.5
) is thus convincing.
This improvement is mainly because the SOCC method
incorporates the constant curvature
\hat{K}^{\mathrm{opt}}

into background
and hence the iterative calculations are done self-consistently.
See e.g. Eqs. (
69
) and (
70
).

in Eq. (

V
Conclusion

This paper extended the dS/AdS background method
based on the optical metric
to a SOCC background.
It was shown also that
the exact lens equation on the SOCC background
can be written in the same form as that for either Minkowski,
dS or AdS background
in terms of flat, spherical or hyperbolic trigonometry,
depending on the Gaussian curvature
of the equatorial plane in the SOCC background.

To exemplify the SOCC method,
we studied the gravitational lens in MK solution of Weyl gravity.
In the zero mass limit,
the deflection angle of light for the MK solution in the literature
diverges to infinity,
because there is a self-contradiction in perturbative approximations
of the MK metric and the orbit equation.
The SOCC method incorporates the long-distance curvature effect into the background.
Thereby the SOCC expression for the deflection angle of light in the MK solution
is finite also in the zero mass limit.

By solving the exact gravitational lens equation iteratively,
we found analytical solutions to the lens equation (i.e. image positions) which differ from previous studies assuming the flat background.
For instance, coupling effects of
\gamma
parameter on image positions
were found at the second order.
If
\hat{\gamma}
parameter has a value such that
\hat{\gamma}D_{L}=\mathcal{O}(1)
,
the new term can
affect the Einstein ring radius at the order of 0.1 milli arcseconds in the galactic scale.

It is left for future to discuss the SOCC approach for a less symmetric case such as an axisymmetric spacetime.
