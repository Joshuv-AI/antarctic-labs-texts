Experimental study on the relationship between extensional and shear rheology of low-viscosity power-law fluids
Yuzuki Matsumoto, Misa Kawaguchi, Yoshiyuki Tagawa

Abstract
This paper investigates the relationship between extensional and shear viscosity of low-viscosity power-law fluids. We show the first experimental evidence of the conditions satisfying the same power exponents for extensional and shear viscosity, as indicated by the Carreau model. The extensional and shear viscosity are respectively measured by capillary breakup extensional rheometry dripping-onto-substrate (CaBER-DoS) and by a shear rheometer for various Ohnesorge number O ​ h Oh . The viscosity ranges measured are about O ⁡ ( 10 0 ) O(10^{0}) to O ⁡ ( 10 4 ) O(10^{4}) mPa ⋅ \cdot s for shear viscosity and O ⁡ ( 10 1 ) O(10^{1}) to O ⁡ ( 10 3 ) O(10^{3}) mPa ⋅ \cdot s for apparent extensional viscosity. Our experimental results show that, at least for the range of O ​ h > 1 Oh>1 , the power-law expression for the liquid filament radius, apparent extensional viscosity, and shear viscosity holds, even for low-viscosity fluids under our experimental conditions .

Recently, CaBER-DoS has been used to measure solutions with even lower viscosities and shorter relaxation times than permitted by the ROJER.
While CaBER utilize the self-thinning of a stretched liquid bridge formed by applying a discrete step strain to a drop between two parallel plates, CaBER-DoS utilize the simple dripping system

Therefore, it is necessary to conduct extensional rheology measurements on low-viscosity solutions and analyze their extensional rheological characteristics

The purpose of this paper is to experimentally clarify the applicable conditions of the relationship between the extensional and shear rheology in power-law fluids. The extensional viscosity is measured using CaBER-DoS and the shear viscosity is measured using a shear rheometer.
The theoretical relationships between the extensional and shear viscosities given by the Carreau model

A previous numerical study

We experimentally clarify the range of

To investigate the rheology under different values of

CaBER-DoS

In CaBER-DoS
[

By considering the resistance force on the liquid filament against the surface tension, the equation for the time evolution of the filament radius

In the inertia-capillary regime, where the

\displaystyle R/R_{0}\propto\left(\frac{\gamma}{\rho{R_{0}}^{3}}\right)^{1/3}(t_{c}-t)^{2/3},

(1)

where

Equation (

l acceleration

In the visco-capillary regime, where the viscous force is dominant as a resisting force against the surface tension, the filament radius decays linearly with time as follows
[
24
]

\displaystyle R/R_{0}=\frac{2\chi-1}{6}\frac{\gamma}{\eta_{S}R_{0}}(t_{c}-t).

(2)

Equation (
2

t=0

The parameter

\chi(t)=\frac{F_{z}(t)}{2\pi\gamma R(t)},

(3)

where

When

The tension force

As the filament approaches a cylindrical shape,

\lim_{R\to 0}F_{z}(t)=2\pi\gamma R(t).

(4)

For a power-law fluid, the decrease in local effective viscosity at the necking point leads to necking rate acceleration. T

\displaystyle R/R_{0}\propto(t_{c}-t)^{n},

(5)

where

From the CaBER-DoS experiments, the extensional rate
\dot{\varepsilon}
, Hencky strain

\dot{\varepsilon}=-\frac{2}{R}\frac{dR}{dt},\ \ \varepsilon=2\ln{\frac{R_{0}}{R}},\ \ \eta_{E}=-\frac{\gamma}{2(dR/dt)}.

(6)

The

In the case of power-law fluids, the actual liquid filament is not cylindrical, but the filament radius changes along the

\frac{\gamma}{R}-\frac{F_{z}(t)}{\pi R(t)^{2}}=\eta_{E}\dot{\varepsilon}.

(7)

Using

In

The inertial

Therefore, numerical calculations were used to obtain a universal similarity solution that balances inertial, capillary, and viscous effects

This is supported by McKinley and Tripathi

Recently,

2.2
Extensional viscosity and shear viscosity in three-parameter Carreau model

In this subsection, the
expressions for
both the shear and extensional viscosity are derived using the three-parameter Carreau model
[
10
]
.
The three-parameter Carreau model is an effective method for expressing the shear-thinning of fluids.
The Carreau model is one of the Generalised Newtonian Fluid (GNF) models. GNF models assume that the viscosity of non-Newtonian fluid is independent of deformation history and it is modeled inelastically as a function of the rate of strain.
As shown below, the GNF models are a function of the magnitude of the strain rate tensor, which is related to the second principal invariant of this tensor.

Yildirim
et al.

[
21
]
and Doshi
et al.

[
29
]
have used this model to consider the viscosity in uniaxial extension.
In the three-parameter Carreau model, viscosity is expressed as

\displaystyle\eta={{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta_{0}}}(1-\beta)[1+(\alpha\hat{\dot{\gamma}})^{2}]^{(n-1)/2}+\beta{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\eta_{0}}},

(9)

where
{\eta}
is the apparent viscosity,
\eta_{0}
is the zero-strain-rate viscosity,

\beta
\eta_{0}
is the viscosity in the limit as the deformation rate goes to infinity (
0<\beta\leq 1
),
\alpha
is a constant with the dimensions of time,
\hat{\dot{\gamma}}
is deformation rate, and
n
is the power-law index.
A smaller characteristic shear rate
\alpha^{-1}
makes it easier to maintain a high viscosity as the strain rate increases.

First, we derive the shear viscosity by considering that
\hat{\dot{\gamma}}^{2}
in Eq. (
9
) is expressed through the second invariant.
In the Cartesian coordinate system, the velocity gradient tensor
\bf{\nabla}\bm{\upsilon}
in shear flow is

\displaystyle\bf{\nabla}\bm{\upsilon}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&\displaystyle\frac{\partial\upsilon_{x}}{\partial y}&0\\
&\\
\displaystyle\frac{\partial\upsilon_{y}}{\partial x}&0&0\\
&\\
0&0&0\end{array}\right).

The second invariant of the rate of deformation tensor

\displaystyle{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\text{II}_{S}}}=\frac{1}{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}2}}}\left(\frac{\partial\upsilon_{x}}{\partial y}+\frac{\partial\upsilon_{y}}{\partial x}\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}2}}}{\dot{\gamma}}^{2},

(16)

where
\dot{\gamma}
is the strain rate.
Note that the definition of the second invariant of the tensor is taken from the textbook
[

and their notation for

In GNF model

\displaystyle\bm{\tau}=\eta(\bf{\nabla}\bf{\upsilon}+\bf{\nabla}\bf{\upsilon}^{T})\equiv\eta\bm{\dot{\gamma}},

(17)

(\bf{\nabla}\bf{\upsilon}+\bf{\nabla}\bf{\upsilon}^{T})

The non-Newtonian viscosity

(19)

Filament radius

Using the image sequences, the minimum filament radius

At higher concentrations, the time

At lower concentrations (

In the case of suspensions, the viscosity decreases in the thinned filaments just before pinch-off, which accelerates the necking and results in faster breakage compared with Newtonian liquids of the same initial viscosity
[
25

.

McIlroy et al.
[
37

reported that a transition to the accelerated thinning regime starts at the point where the filament radius is approximately five times the size of the suspended particles. This acceleration is attributed to the geometry of the filament. At this regime, the filament has high curvature. Therefore, thinning is accelerated in order to conserve volume
[
37
]
. As the necking radius approaches the particle size, the transition occurs from the mid-term to the final regime.

dominates. This result suggests that the behavior of test fluids at 60–70% is clearly different from that of power-law fluids.

4.4
Relationship between extensional and shear rheology: power-law exponents

From our experiments, the power-law exponents
n
,
l
, and
m
for the filament radius,
apparent
extensional viscosity, and shear viscosity were obtained.
In the following, we explain the
Oh
conditions under which Eqs.
(
39
) and (
45
)
hold.
Figure
6
shows the values of
n-l
and
m/l
.
It is clear that
n-l=1
for
Oh>1
in the range of
\pm
29
%.
Our experimental results indicate that the relation between the power-law exponents of the
apparent
extensional viscosity and the radius of the liquid filament for
Oh>1
is valid, as shown numerically by Suryo
and Basaran

[
22
]
.
For
Oh>1
, the power-law exponents for the
apparent
extensional viscosity and shear viscosity obey the relation
m/l=1
in the range of
\pm
31
%, which is in agreement with the power-law expression based on the Carreau model.
Below 65 wt%,
n-l
becomes larger than 1 and
m/l<1
.

From the above results, it is clear that
Oh>1
is a condition for the applicability of the viscous power-law expression with respect to the liquid filament radius, apparent extensional viscosity, and shear viscosity.
This result may be useful in predicting the
apparent
extensional viscosity from shear viscosity measurements when the relationship between the shear and extensional viscosities follows the Carreau model in the viscosity-dominated region.
As shear viscosity measurements are easier than extensional viscosity measurements, any discussion of the extensional viscosity will be easier if we can predict the
apparent
extensional viscosity from measured values of the shear viscosity.
For example, because the liquid jet pinches off after the extension of the filament, the results of this study will be useful for controlling the injection volume of the jet, which has practical applications in on-demand printing technologies.

5
Conclusion

This study has experimentally investigated the relationship between the shear and extensional rheology of power-law fluids.
A previous study based on numerical simulations by Suryo and Basaran
[
22
]
suggested that the power-law relationship of extensional rheology is applicable when
Oh>1
, but this condition was not experimentally investigated.
In addition, this previous study only simulated the necking behavior, and did not discuss the shear viscosity.
We experimentally investigated the relationship between the power-law exponents relating to the shear and extensional rheology.
For the extensional rheology measurements, we used CaBER-DoS, which is capable of measuring fluids with lower viscosity than other measurement methods.
The shear rheology was measured using a shear rheometer.
The applicable conditions were investigated by changing the
dilutions
of the power-law fluids.
The measured
apparent
extensional and shear viscosities were compared with the theoretical relationships given by the Carreau model.

Our experimental results revealed that the power-law expression for the filament radius, apparent extensional viscosity, and shear viscosity is applicable in the case of at least
Oh>1
. In this condition, the exponents for shear and extensional viscosity become same.
In addition, the difference between exponents of filament radius and that of apparent extensional viscosity become constant value of 1 for
Oh>1
.

The results presented in this paper will be useful in predicting the
apparent
extensional viscosity from the shear viscosity, and will contribute to industrial applications such as on-demand printing.

Our results provide experimental evidence for the criteria of
Oh
, which were also suggested numerically by Suryo and Basaran
[
22
]
. However, this study is limited to a single fluid, industrial car paint, modeled as a power-law fluid. Therefore, future work should examine other fluids, such as aqueous solutions of xanthan gum.
