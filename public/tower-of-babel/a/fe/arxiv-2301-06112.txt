Homology growth, hyperbolization, and fibering
Grigori Avramidi, Boris Okun, Kevin Schreve

Abstract
. We introduce a hyperbolic reflection group trick which builds closed aspherical manifolds out of compact ones and preserves hyperbolicity, residual finiteness, and—for almost all primes p p — 𝔽 p \mathbb{F}_{p} -homology growth above the middle dimension. We use this trick, embedding theory and manifold topology to construct Gromov hyperbolic 7 7 -manifolds that do not virtually fiber over a circle out of graph products of large finite groups.

Introduction

By the Chern–Gauss–Bonnet theorem, an even dimensional hyperbolic manifold always has non-zero Euler characteristic.
One geometric consequence of this is that such manifolds cannot fiber over the circle.
On the other hand, all odd-dimensional manifolds have vanishing Euler characteristic, so, at least in principle, odd-dimensional hyperbolic manifolds can fiber.
A remarkable discovery of the last few decades is that the later possibility is actually realized in dimension three: All closed, hyperbolic
3
-manifolds have a finite cover that fibers over a circle

3

There has been some recent progress towards determining whether or not some analogue of this phenomenon persists in higher dimensions.
In a geometric direction, Italiano, Martelli, and Migliorini constructed the first finite volume hyperbolic

[
42

These examples are non-compact and no closed
5

In a more algebraic direction, Kielak

For

Homology growth and virtual fibering

In the examples we produce for Theorem

To keep track of it, fix a field

\beta^{\inf}_{k}(\mathcal{M};\mathbb{F}):=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{M}^{\prime}\to\mathcal{M},\\
\lvert\mathcal{M}^{\prime}\to\mathcal{M}\rvert<\infty\end{subarray}}\frac{b_{k}(\mathcal{M}^{\prime};\mathbb{F})}{\lvert\mathcal{M}^{\prime}\to\mathcal{M}\rvert}

and note that virtually fibering

So, positivity of this number obstructs virtual fibering.

-Betti numbers

Pinching homology growth

A hyperbolic reflection group trick

To obtain a closed manifold we do a combination of the Davis reflection group trick and the Charney–Davis strict hyperbolization.
This hyperbolic reflection group trick works in any dimension, and may be of independent interest, because it preserves hyperbolicity, residual finiteness and other pleasant features.

Upper and lower homology growth

Our first goal is to understand the normalized Betti numbers of finite covers of a complex
X
and how they vary as we pass to further covers.
Somewhat surprisingly, a number of basic but useful properties of this can be established by thinking of the normalized Betti numbers as a function on the partially ordered
4
4

4

The partial order on covers of
X
is defined by
X^{\prime\prime}>X^{\prime}
whenever
X^{\prime\prime}
is a cover of
X^{\prime}
.

set of all finite covers, looking at upper and lower limits over this set, and using the fact that any two finite covers have a further finite cover lying above both of them.
In this section we record the basic properties of such limits.

Limits over directed posets

Let
(C,<)
be a partially ordered set.
Suppose it is directed (for any
x,y\in C
there exists
z\in C
such that
x<z
and
y<z
).
A subset
D
is
cofinal
if for any
x\in C
there exists
y\in D
such that
x\leq y
.
We want to define various notions of limits of bounded real-valued functions on
C
.
The basic building blocks are taking
\inf
or
\sup
over a subset.
There are two basic observations: smaller subsets produce smaller
\sup
and if the function is decreasing then
\inf
can be computed over any cofinal subset.

Applying
\sup
to tails (subsets of the form
C_{\geq x}=\{y\in C\mid y\geq x\}
) defines an operation on bounded functions
f\mapsto f^{\sup}
:

f^{\sup}(x)=\sup_{C_{\geq x}}f.

This converts any function to a decreasing one.

Define
f^{\inf}:=-(-f)^{\sup}
.
The following is immediate from the observations:

Lemma 2.1
.

\overline{f}:=(f^{\sup})^{\inf}
and
\underline{f}:=(f^{\inf})^{\sup}
are constant functions.

We will call
\overline{f}
and
\underline{f}
the upper and lower limits of
f
.

We collect in the following lemma all the properties of upper and lower limits that we need.
They will be primarily used in obtaining the Mayer–Vietoris inequalities in Section
9
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
f,g
be bounded functions.

(1)

If
f\leq g
, then
\underline{f}\leq\underline{g}
and
\overline{f}\leq\overline{g}
.

(2)

(Almost additivity)

\underline{f}+\underline{g}\leq\underline{f+g}\leq\underline{f}+\overline{g}\leq\overline{f+g}\leq\overline{f}+\overline{g}.

(3)

(Restriction)
Let
D
be a cofinal subset of
C
.
Then

\underline{f}\leq\underline{f|_{D}}\leq\overline{f|_{D}}\leq\overline{f}.

(4)

Let
T
be a tail of
C
.
Then

\underline{f}=\underline{f|_{T}}\leq\overline{f|_{T}}=\overline{f}.

Proof.

(1) is immediate.
The last inequality in (2) follows from general subadditivity of
\sup
and additivity of
\inf
for decreasing functions.
Using
\underline{f}=-\overline{(-f)}
the following trick then proves the third inequality in (2):

\underline{f}+\overline{g}=\overline{f+g-f}-\overline{(-f)}\leq\overline{f+g}+\overline{(-f)}-\overline{(-f)}=\overline{f+g}.

Note that setting
g=0
in this inequality gives
\underline{f}\leq\overline{f}
, which justifies the upper and lower notation and proves the middle inequalities in (3) and (4).

Since for a subset
D
of
C
,
D_{\geq x}=C_{\geq x}\cap D
, the basic observations give the last inequality in (3).
If
T
is itself a tail of
C
, then its tails are tails of
C
, hence

(f|_{T})^{\sup}=f^{\sup}|_{T},

and we obtain the last equality in (4).
The remaining (in)equalities are obtained from these by flipping signs.
∎

Normalized Betti numbers as a function on a set of covers

For a complex
X
, let
C_{X}
denote the poset of finite covers of
X
.
The normalized
k
-th
\mathbb{F}
-Betti numbers of such covers define a function on this poset, which we will denote by
\beta_{k}(X;\mathbb{F})
, i.e. if
X^{\prime}\to X
is a finite cover, then

\beta_{k}(X;\mathbb{F})(X^{\prime}):=\frac{b_{k}(X^{\prime};\mathbb{F})}{\lvert X^{\prime}\to X\rvert}.

For much of this section,
k
and
\mathbb{F}
will be unimportant, and then we will omit one (or both) of them from the notation.
The function
\beta_{k}(X;\mathbb{F})
is bounded by the number of
k
-cells in
X
.

The
upper
and
lower
\mathbb{F}
-homology growth
,
\overline{\beta}_{k}(X;\mathbb{F})
and
\underline{\beta}_{k}(X;\mathbb{F})
are the upper and lower limits over
C_{X}
of this function, more explicitly:

\displaystyle\overline{\beta}_{k}(X;\mathbb{F})

\displaystyle:=\inf_{X^{\prime}\to X}\left(\sup_{X^{\prime\prime}\to X^{\prime}}\frac{b_{k}(X^{\prime\prime};\mathbb{F})}{\lvert X^{\prime\prime}\to X\rvert}\right),

\displaystyle\underline{\beta}_{k}(X;\mathbb{F})

\displaystyle:=\sup_{X^{\prime}\to X}\left(\inf_{X^{\prime\prime}\to X^{\prime}}\frac{b_{k}(X^{\prime\prime};\mathbb{F})}{\lvert X^{\prime\prime}\to X\rvert}\right).

More generally, given a map
h:X\to Y
, and a finite cover
\pi:Y^{\prime}\rightarrow Y
, the pullback

h^{*}(Y^{\prime})=\{(x,y^{\prime}):h(x)=\pi(y^{\prime})\}\subset X\times Y^{\prime}

is a finite cover of
X
of the same degree.
We define the
restricted
homology growths of
X
,
\overline{\beta}_{k}^{Y}(X;\mathbb{F})
and
\underline{\beta}_{k}^{Y}(X;\mathbb{F})
, by taking the above limits over the subset of covers pulled back from
Y
.
Note that for
Y=X
,
\overline{\beta}^{X}(X)=\overline{\beta}(X)
and
\underline{\beta}^{X}(X)=\underline{\beta}(X)
.

Rational homology growth and
\delta
-good covers

We next describe a
\delta
-pinching theorem for normalized rational Betti numbers.
It is a consequence of the proof of Lück’s approximation theorem in
[
58
]
and can be thought of as a quantitative variant of that theorem.
Other quantitative versions of Lück approximation also appear in
[
19
]
and
[
55
]
.

We need the following basic linear algebraic lemma.

Lemma 2.3

(
[
58
]
*Theorem 3.4(1))
.

Suppose
\Delta
is an
N\times N
matrix with integer entries.
Let
N_{\epsilon}
be the number of eigenvalues
\lambda
with
\lvert\lambda\rvert\in(0,\epsilon]
, counted with multiplicity.
If
\epsilon<1
then

\frac{N_{\epsilon}}{N}\leq\frac{\log\lvert\Delta\rvert}{\log(\epsilon^{-1})}

Proof.

Look at the characteristic polynomial
\det(t-\Delta)=t^{b}q(t)
where
q(t)=\prod_{0<\mu_{i}\leq\epsilon}(t-\mu_{i})\prod_{\epsilon<\mu_{i}\leq\lvert\Delta\rvert}(t-\mu_{i})
.
Since
\Delta
has integer entries, the number
q(0)
is a non-zero
integer
, hence

1\leq\lvert q(0)\rvert\leq\epsilon^{N_{\epsilon}}\lvert\Delta\rvert^{N}.

Rearranging to
(\epsilon^{-1})^{N_{\epsilon}}\leq\lvert\Delta\lvert^{N}
and taking logs gives
N_{\epsilon}\log(\epsilon^{-1})\leq N\log\rvert\Delta\rvert
.
∎

Theorem 2.4

(
\delta
-pinching
\mathbb{Q}
-homology growth)
.

Let
X
be a finite complex.
Given
\delta>0
there is a finite cover
X_{\delta}
such that the function
\beta_{k}(X;\mathbb{Q})
is
\delta
-pinched above
X_{\delta}
.
More explicitly, if
X^{\prime}
and
X^{\prime\prime}
are finite covers of
X_{\delta}
then

\left\lvert\frac{b_{k}(X^{\prime};\mathbb{Q})}{\lvert X^{\prime}\to X\rvert}-\frac{b_{k}(X^{\prime\prime};\mathbb{Q})}{\lvert X^{\prime\prime}\to X\rvert}\right\rvert\leq\delta.

Proof.

Let
N
be the number of
k
-cells of
X
.
There is a finite constant
D
such that the norm of the combinatorial Laplacian
\Delta^{\prime}
acting on
C_{k}(X^{\prime};\mathbb{Q})
of finite covers of
X
is uniformly bounded by
D
(see
[
58
]
*Lemma 2.5) independent of the cover.
Choose
0<\epsilon<1
satisfying

\epsilon N<\delta/2,\qquad\epsilon+\frac{\log D}{\log(\epsilon^{-1})}<\delta/N.

Next choose
r
so that polynomial
f(x)=(1-x/D)^{r}
satisfies

f(\epsilon)<\epsilon.

Then, since
f
is monotone decreasing on
[0,D]
,

\chi_{0}\leq f\leq\chi_{[0,\epsilon]}+\epsilon\text{ on $[0,D]$}.

So, for any finite cover

we have

b_{k}(X^{\prime};\mathbb{Q})=\tr\chi_{0}(\Delta^{\prime})\leq\tr f(\Delta^{\prime})\leq\tr(\chi_{[0,\epsilon]}+\epsilon)(\Delta^{\prime})=b_{k}(X^{\prime};\mathbb{Q})+N^{\prime}_{\epsilon}+\epsilon N^{\prime},

where
N^{\prime}
is the number of
k
-cells of
X^{\prime}
, and
N^{\prime}_{\epsilon}
is the number of eigenvalues in the interval
(0,\epsilon]
of the Laplacian
\Delta^{\prime}
acting on
C_{k}(X^{\prime};\mathbb{Q})
.
Since this combinatorial Laplacian has integer entries, Lemma
2.3
implies

N^{\prime}_{\epsilon}\leq\frac{\log D}{\log(\epsilon^{-1})}N^{\prime}.

Hence,

b_{k}(X^{\prime};\mathbb{Q})\leq\tr(f(\Delta^{\prime}))\leq b_{k}(X^{\prime};\mathbb{Q})+\delta\lvert X^{\prime}\to X\rvert.

Since
f
is a polynomial, there is a radius
R
such that the support of
f(\Delta^{\prime})e
is in the
R

e
for each cell
e
in
X^{\prime}
.

Let
\hat{X}\to X
denote the universal residually finite cover and choose its finite quotient
X_{\delta}
, so that
\hat{X}\to X_{\delta}
is injective on
R

Then any finite cover
X^{\prime}\to X_{\delta}
is also injective on
R

\tr f(\Delta^{\prime})=\tr(f(\Delta_{\delta}))\lvert X^{\prime}\to X_{\delta}\rvert.

Combining this with the above inequality shows that for any such cover

the normalized Betti numbers lie in the
\delta

\left[\frac{\tr(f(\Delta_{\delta}))}{\lvert X_{\delta}\to X\rvert}-\delta,\frac{\tr(f(\Delta_{\delta}))}{\lvert X_{\delta}\to X\rvert}\right]
, which proves the claim.
∎

For any finite complex

\underline{\beta}_{*}(X;\mathbb{Q})=\overline{\beta}_{*}(X;\mathbb{Q})

If we fix lifts

In particular, if

Connectedness

If
X
is disconnected, then its homology growth is the sum of the homology growth of its components, as the following lemma shows.

Lemma 2.7
.

If
X=Y\amalg Z
, then

\underline{\beta}(X)=\underline{\beta}(Y)+\underline{\beta}(Z),

\overline{\beta}(X)=\overline{\beta}(Y)+\overline{\beta}(Z).

Proof.

Clearly, the normalized Betti numbers of a finite cover of
X
is the sum of the normalized Betti numbers of its restrictions to
Y
and
Z
.
The issue is that in general finite covers
Y^{\prime}\to Y
and
Z^{\prime}\to Z
do not combine to a cover of
X
unless they have same degree, as our definition requires the degree to be constant.
However, we can equalize degrees by replacing
Y^{\prime}
with
\lvert Z^{\prime}\to Z\rvert
disjoint copies of
Y^{\prime}
and replacing
Z^{\prime}
with
\lvert Y^{\prime}\to Y\rvert
disjoint copies of
Z^{\prime}
.
This replacement does not change the normalized Betti numbers, and the Lemma follows.
∎

It is sometimes useful to keep in mind that we can compute homology growth of a connected finite complex either using all covers, or just the connected ones.
We record this observation here as a lemma.

Lemma 2.8
.

For a connected finite complex
X
, the upper and lower homology growth can be computed using connected covers.

Proof.

We give the proof for upper homology growth.
Suppose
X
is a connected complex, let
X^{\prime}\to X
be a finite cover and denote by
X_{i}^{\prime}
its components.
Then the normalized Betti numbers of this cover are a convex combination

\beta(X)(X^{\prime})=\sum\frac{|X_{i}^{\prime}\to X|}{|X^{\prime}\to X|}\beta(X)(X_{i}^{\prime})

of the normalized Betti numbers of the components.
Since the coefficients sum to one, we conclude that
\beta(X)(X^{\prime})\leq\beta(X)(X^{\prime}_{i})
for some
i
.
So
\beta(X)^{\sup}
can be computed over connected covers of
X
.
Since
\beta(X)^{\sup}
is decreasing, its
\inf
can be computed over any cofinal subset.
In particular, it can be computed over covers that have identical components.
Clearly, the answer of this computation is the same as that for any of the components.
Therefore
\overline{\beta}(X)
of a connected complex can be computed using only connected covers.
∎

Remark
.

The regular covers of
X
form a cofinal subset of
C_{X}
, so Lemma
2.2
(
3
) provides bounds for homology growth in terms of the limits over regular covers.
We don’t know whether regular covers give an exact computation.

Finiteness

Since connected covers correspond to subgroups of the fundamental group, we can relax finiteness assumptions on
X
.
Recall that a connected complex
X
is of type
FP_{n}(\mathbb{F})
if the chain complex of the universal cover
C_{*}(\widetilde{X};\mathbb{F})
is
\mathbb{F}\pi_{1}X
-chain homotopy equivalent to a complex
P_{*}
of free
\mathbb{F}\pi_{1}X
-modules which have finite rank in degrees
\leq n
.
The following lemma shows that homology growth is well defined and finite in degrees
\leq n
for such complexes.

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
X
be a connected complex so that the chain complex of the universal cover
C_{*}(\widetilde{X};\mathbb{F})
is
\mathbb{F}\pi_{1}X
-chain homotopy equivalent to a complex
P_{*}
of
\mathbb{F}\pi_{1}X
modules, where
P_{k}
is a free module of finite rank.
Then the normalized Betti function
\beta_{k}(X;\mathbb{F})
is bounded:

\beta_{k}(X;\mathbb{F})\leq\rk_{\mathbb{F}\pi_{1}X}P_{k}.

Proof.

By the proof of the previous Lemma it is enough to check the inequality for connected covers.
Denote
G:=\pi_{1}X
.
Let
X^{\prime}\to X
be a finite connected cover, and let
G^{\prime}<G
be the corresponding subgroup.
Then we have

\beta_{k}(X;\mathbb{F})(X^{\prime})=\frac{\dim_{\mathbb{F}}H_{k}(X;\mathbb{F}[G/G^{\prime}])}{[G:G^{\prime}]}=\frac{\dim_{\mathbb{F}}H_{k}(P_{*}\otimes_{\mathbb{F}G}\mathbb{F}[G/G^{\prime}])}{[G:G^{\prime}]}\leq\rk_{\mathbb{F}G}(P_{k}).

∎

Multiplicativity

If
X^{\prime}\to X
is a finite cover, then
C_{X^{\prime}}
is naturally identified with the tail
(C_{X})_{\geq X^{\prime}}
of
C_{X}
, and on this tail we have
\beta(X)\lvert X^{\prime}\to X\rvert=\beta(X^{\prime})
.
Therefore, since by Lemma
2.2
(4) the limits can be computed over tails, the homology growth is multiplicative in covers of
X
:

\displaystyle\overline{\beta}(X^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\overline{\beta}(X)\lvert X^{\prime}\to X\rvert,

\displaystyle\underline{\beta}(X^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\underline{\beta}(X)\lvert X^{\prime}\to X\rvert.

Homology growth as a fibering obstruction

Multiplicativity implies a variant of Lück’s mapping torus theorem
[

]
for homology growth.

Theorem 2.10

(
\mathbb{F}
-homology mapping torus theorem for
\overline{\beta}
)
.

Let
X
be a complex of type
FP_{n}(\mathbb{F})
,

a self-homotopy equivalence and

its mapping torus.
Then for
k\leq n

\overline{\beta}_{k}(T_{f};\mathbb{F})=0.

Proof.

We can assume that
f
is a cellular map.
Let
K=\pi_{1}(X)
and let
G=\pi_{1}(T_{f})=K\rtimes\mathbb{Z}
.
The assumption means that the chain complex
C:=C_{*}(\widetilde{X};\mathbb{F})
is
\mathbb{F}K
-chain homotopy equivalent to a complex of free
\mathbb{F}K
-modules
P
which have finite rank in degrees
\leq n
.
Let
h:C\to P
and
l:P\to C
be the chain homotopy equivalence and its inverse.
Consider the map
g=f^{m}
for some positive integer
m
.
The map
g
induces a chain map which we will also call
g:C\to C
.

Let
\hat{g}=hfl:P\to P
.
The algebraic mapping telescope
\widetilde{T}_{\hat{g}}
of
\hat{g}
is
\mathbb{F}G
-chain homotopy equivalent to
C_{*}(\widetilde{T}_{g};\mathbb{F})
.
Thus, by Lemma

\beta_{k}(T_{g};\mathbb{F})\leq\rk_{\mathbb{F}G}(\widetilde{T}_{\hat{g}})_{k}=\rk_{\mathbb{F}K}(P_{k}\oplus P_{k-1}).

The mapping torus

is homotopy equivalent to a degree

, so by multiplicativity

m\overline{\beta}_{k}(T_{f};\mathbb{F})=\overline{\beta}_{k}(T_{g};\mathbb{F})\leq\rk_{\mathbb{F}K}(P_{k}\oplus P_{k-1}).

Since

can be picked arbitrarily large, we are done.
∎

3.
\beta^{\inf}
via skew fields

In this section we will give a skew field description of
\beta^{\inf}
for complexes with residually torsion-free nilpotent fundamental group.
The proof goes by first approximating the residually torsion-free nilpotent group by torsion-free nilpotent groups, and then approximating those by finite groups.
Since groups are central to this argument, we will use equivariant notation to highlight the role of the groups involved, rather than relegating it to a subscript in a coefficient module.

Skew field Betti numbers

Let
G
be a group, let
Y
be a free cocompact
G
-CW complex, and suppose we have a homomorphism
\phi:\mathbb{Z}G\to D
to a skew field.
The homomorphism makes
D
into a
\mathbb{Z}G
-bimodule, so we can take equivariant homology of
Y
with coefficients in
D

H_{*}^{G}(Y;D)=H_{*}(D\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}G}C_{*}(Y)),

and define the
equivariant Betti numbers with coefficients in
D
of
Y
by taking its dimension over
D
:

b^{G}_{*}(Y;D)=\dim_{D}H_{*}^{G}(Y;D).

More explicitly,

b^{G}_{i}(Y;D)=\lvert Y^{(i)}\rvert-\big(\rk_{D}\phi(\partial_{i})+\rk_{D}\phi(\partial_{i+1})\big),

where
\phi(\partial_{i})
denotes the image of the matrix of the differential in
D
and
\lvert Y^{(i)}\rvert
is the number of
G
-orbits of
i
-cells in
Y
.

Remark
.

When
Y
is the universal cover of a connected finite complex
Y/G
with fundamental group
G
, then this definition coincides with the usual (unequivariant) homology of
Y/G
with local coefficients in the
\mathbb{Z}G
-module
D
,

H_{*}^{G}(Y;D)=H_{*}(Y/G;D).

On the level of skew field Betti numbers,
b_{*}^{G}(Y;D)=b_{*}(Y/G;D)
.

Local homomorphisms

A nontrivial homomorphism between skew fields is necessarily injective, however there is a more general notion of morphisms between skew fields.
It leads to an inequality between Betti numbers.

A
local homomorphism
(or
subhomomorphism
) between two skew fields
D
and
D^{\prime}
is a homomorphism from a subring
L
of
D
to
D^{\prime}
,
f:L\to D^{\prime}
whose kernel is precisely the set of non-units of
L
.
It follows that
L
is a local ring,
J:=\ker f
is its unique maximal ideal, and
J\backslash L
is a sub-skew field of
D^{\prime}
.
If
M
is a finitely generated
L
-module, then by Nakayama’s lemma, cf.
[
30
]
*Corollary 2.13, any lift of a basis of
J\backslash L\otimes_{L}M=JM\backslash M
to
M
is a generating set for
M
, therefore
\dim_{D^{\prime}}D^{\prime}\otimes_{L}M\geq\dim_{D}D\otimes_{L}M
.
In terms of ranks we have an opposite inequality, for any
L
-matrix
A
,
\rk_{D^{\prime}}f(A)\leq\rk_{D}A
.

Thus we have the following: suppose
\mathbb{Z}G\to D^{\prime}
extends to a local homomorphism from
D
to
D^{\prime}
, then

b^{G}_{*}(Y;D)\leq b^{G}_{*}(Y;D^{\prime}).

If
D
is a sub-skew field of
D^{\prime}
, then we have obvious local homomorphisms from
D
to
D^{\prime}
and vice versa extending the map
\mathbb{Z}G\to D
, which gives us

Change of coefficients
.

If
D
is contained in another skew field
D^{\prime}
, then

b^{G}_{*}(Y;D)=b^{G}_{*}(Y;D^{\prime}),

where the latter is computed using the composition
\mathbb{Z}G\to D\hookrightarrow D^{\prime}
.
(This is also a consequence of
C_{*}(Y)\otimes_{\mathbb{F}G}D^{\prime}=(C_{*}(Y)\otimes_{\mathbb{F}G}D)\otimes_{D}D^{\prime}
.)

In particular, if
\phi:\mathbb{Z}G\to D
, we can always replace
D
with the skew field generated by the
\phi(\mathbb{Z}G)
, i.e. the
division closure
of
\phi(\mathbb{Z}G)
, without changing Betti numbers.

Epic
\mathbb{F}G
fields

There are two classical constructions (for certain amenable groups, and for bi-orderable groups) of
\mathbb{Z}G
skew fields.
Both constructions depend on the choice of a base field
\mathbb{F}
(we will be mostly concerned with
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{Q}
or
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{F}_{p}
) and produce canonical epic embeddings
\mathbb{F}G\hookrightarrow D_{\mathbb{F}G}
.
(A homomorphism of
\mathbb{F}G
into a skew field
D
is
epic
if the image of
\mathbb{F}G
generates
D
.) Moreover, both constructions behave nicely with respect to subgroups.
If
H<G
then the division closure of
\mathbb{F}H
in
D_{\mathbb{F}G}
coincides with
D_{\mathbb{F}H}
.
If
H
happens to be finite index in
G
, then in both cases
D_{\mathbb{F}G}\cong\oplus_{[G:H]}D_{\mathbb{F}H}
, which implies a multiplicativity formula for the corresponding Betti numbers.
We shall use the same notation for both constructions, and let the context distinguish them.
This does not lead to confusion, as the constructions agree when both are defined.

Of course the existence of such an embedding requires
\mathbb{F}G
to have no zero divisors.
Conjecturally,
\mathbb{F}G
has no zero divisors for any torsion-free group.
It is known for many classes of groups, in particular for left-orderable groups and for torsion-free elementary amenable groups
[
50
]
*Theorem 1.4.

We now discuss both constructions.

Amenable groups

Suppose
R
is a ring without zero divisors, and
S
is a multiplicatively closed subset of nonzero elements.
The pair
(R,S)
satisfies the (right) Ore condition if for each
r\in R
and
s\in S
there are
r^{\prime}\in R
and
s^{\prime}\in S
with

rs^{\prime}=sr^{\prime}.

If the pair
(R,S)
satisfies the Ore condition, then one can form a ring called the Ore localization
RS^{-1}
.
The elements of
RS^{-1}
are equivalence classes of fractions
r/s
,
r\in R
,
s\in S
; the Ore condition allows one to add and multiply these expressions.
There is a natural injection
R\to RS^{-1}
, given by
r\mapsto r/1
.
If
S
is the set of all nonzero elements of
R
, then
RS^{-1}
is a skew field, and we get an epic embedding
R\hookrightarrow RS^{-1}
.
This embedding is a unique epic embedding, since any other embedding factors through it.

For a group ring,
R=\mathbb{F}G
without zero divisors, the pair
(\mathbb{F}G;\mathbb{F}G-\{0\})
satisfies the Ore condition if and only if
G
is amenable
[
9
]
*Theorem A.1.
In this case we shall denote the localization by
D_{\mathbb{F}G}
.
So, to summarize, for amenable
G
with
\mathbb{F}G
having no zero divisors we have a unique epic embedding
\mathbb{F}G\hookrightarrow D_{\mathbb{F}G}
.
If
G
is amenable and
\mathbb{F}G
has no zero divisors, then this also holds for all subgroups of
G
and their group rings.
Furthermore, if
H<G
then we can identify
D_{\mathbb{F}H}
with the division closure of
\mathbb{F}H
inside of
D_{\mathbb{F}G}
.
If
G
is in addition residually finite, then we also have a version of Lück’s Approximation theorem, which is the main result of Linnell, Lück, and Sauer
[
54
]
*Theorem 0.2.

Theorem 3.1

(
[
54
]
*Theorem 0.2)
.

Suppose
G
is amenable and
\mathbb{F}G
has no zero divisors.
Let
G_{i}\lhd G
be a residual sequence of finite index normal subgroups of
G
.
Then for any cocompact free
G
-CW complex
Y

b^{G}_{*}(Y;D_{\mathbb{F}G})=\lim_{i\to\infty}\frac{b_{*}(Y/G_{i};\mathbb{F})}{[G:G_{i}]}.

Bi-orderable groups

As another example, suppose the group
G
is bi–orderable, and fix a particular bi-invariant total order on
G
.
The Malcev–Neumann series are infinite linear combinations of elements in
G
with
\mathbb{F}
coefficients, whose support is well ordered in the induced order.
They form a skew field, into which the group ring
\mathbb{F}G
naturally embeds.
Let
D_{\mathbb{F}G}
denote the division closure of
\mathbb{F}G
in this skew field.

One can easily see from the construction of inverses that if
a
is a non-zero Malcev–Neumann series then the support of
a^{-1}
is contained in the subgroup generated by the support of
a
.

Therefore, for a subgroup
H
the set of elements of
D_{\mathbb{F}G}
supported on
H
is a sub-skew field, and it follows that the division closure of
\mathbb{F}H
in
D_{\mathbb{F}G}
is naturally identified with
D_{\mathbb{F}H}
, coming from the induced order on
H
.

Since the action of
G
on cosets preserves the induced order, a similar picture holds for the set of elements of
D_{\mathbb{F}G}
supported on a single coset, it has a natural structure of a vector space over
D_{\mathbb{F}H}
.
This gives an injective homomorphism of
D_{\mathbb{F}H}
-vector spaces:
\bigoplus_{G/H}D_{\mathbb{F}H}\to D_{\mathbb{F}G}
.

The injectivity of this homomorphism is a (very strong) form of the so called Hughes-free condition, so the embedding
\mathbb{F}G\hookrightarrow D_{\mathbb{F}G}
is Hughes-free.
Hughes
[
41
]
proved that for a given
\mathbb{F}
, Hughes-free epic embeddings are unique up to an isomorphism over
\mathbb{F}G
, thus
D_{\mathbb{F}G}
does not depend on the choice of the order, and we obtain well-defined Betti numbers
b^{G}_{*}(Y;D_{\mathbb{F}G})
.

We also have an approximation theorem of a different flavor.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
K_{i}
be a nested residual sequence of normal subgroups in a group
G
.
Suppose we have bi-invariant orderings on
G
and on the quotients
G_{i}=G/K_{i}
such that each quotient map
p_{i}:G\to G_{i}
is order-preserving.
Then for any cocompact free
G
-complex
Y
there exist
i_{0}
such that for any
i\geq i_{0}
,

b^{G}_{*}(Y;D_{\mathbb{F}G})=b^{G_{i}}_{*}(Y/K_{i};D_{\mathbb{F}G_{i}}).

Proof.

Following
[
28
,
52
]
define a sequence of subrings
\{S_{i}\}
of
D_{\mathbb{F}G}
with
S_{i}\subset S_{i+1}
consisting of elements whose support has finite intersection with
K_{i}
-cosets.
A slight generalization of the Malcev–Neumann argument, cf.
[
52
]
*Proposition 7.1, shows that
\bigcup S_{i}
is a skew field.
Since each
S_{i}
contains
\mathbb{F}G
, we have
\bigcup S_{i}=D_{\mathbb{F}G}
.
Note that the maps
p_{i}
obviously extend to maps
p_{i}:S_{i}\to D_{\mathbb{F}G_{i}}
.

Given a matrix
A
over
\mathbb{F}G
, we can diagonalize
A
over
D_{\mathbb{F}G}
by performing column and row operations: there exist
D_{\mathbb{F}G}
-matrices
C
and
C^{\prime}
, such that
CAC^{\prime}=I_{\rk_{D_{\mathbb{F}G}}A}
.
The entries of
C
and
C^{\prime}
are a finite collection of elements of
D_{\mathbb{F}G}
, so they are all contained in
S_{i_{0}}
for some
i_{0}
.
Then for
i\geq i_{0}
, applying
p_{i}
to the above diagonalization shows that
\rk_{D_{\mathbb{F}G}}A=\rk_{D_{\mathbb{F}G_{i}}}p_{i}(A)
.
Choosing
i_{0}
so that the above works for all differentials in
Y
finishes the proof.
∎

Remark
.

The skew field
\bigcup S_{i}
has been recently used by Sikorav in
[
69
]
to give a new fibering criterion for closed aspherical
3
-manifolds.

Finite index subgroups

It turns out that in both cases the equivariant Betti numbers have an additional nice property satisfied by the usual
L^{2}
-Betti numbers, namely multiplicativity for finite index subgroups.

Lemma 3.3
.

Suppose
G
is bi-orderable or amenable with
\mathbb{F}G
having no zero-divisors, and
H<G
is a finite index subgroup.
Then for any
G
-complex
Y

b^{H}_{*}(Y;D_{\mathbb{F}H})=[G:H]b^{G}_{*}(Y;D_{\mathbb{F}G}).

Proof.

For bi-orderable groups, if
H<G
has finite index, then the full Malcev–Neumann skew field of
G
is a vector space of dimension
[G:H]
over the full Malcev–Neumann skew field of
H
, and these skew fields give the same Betti numbers as
D_{\mathbb{F}H}
and
D_{\mathbb{F}G}
.
In particular, it follows that
D_{\mathbb{F}G}\cong D_{\mathbb{F}H}^{[G:H]}
as
D_{\mathbb{F}H}
-vector spaces.

This also holds in the amenable case, see e.g.

[
54
]
*Equation 5.2 for a more general statement.
For convenience, we give the argument here assuming that there are no zero-divisors.
It is enough to consider normal subgroups.
The main point is that the pair
(\mathbb{F}G,S=\mathbb{F}H-\{0\})
satisfies the Ore condition
[
66
]
* Lemma 13.3.5 (ii), so we can form the localization
(\mathbb{F}G)S^{-1}
.
This is a
D_{\mathbb{F}H}
-vector space of dimension
[G:H]
which naturally injects into
D_{\mathbb{F}G}
.
We claim that this is onto; it suffices to show that each nonzero
t
in
\mathbb{F}G
is invertible in
(\mathbb{F}G)S^{-1}
.
Since
t
is not a zero divisor in
D_{\mathbb{F}G}
, it is not a zero divisor in
(\mathbb{F}G)S^{-1}
, hence the left multiplication by
t
induces an injective linear self-map of
(\mathbb{F}G)S^{-1}
.
Therefore, this multiplication is an isomorphism, and the preimage of 1 is the inverse of
t
.
∎

Finite generation

Note that we did not assume that the group
G
is finitely generated.
We now show that in both constructions we can always reduce the computation of
b_{k}^{G}(Y;D_{\mathbb{F}G})
to the case of finitely generated
G
.

First, we need the following observation.

Induced representation
.

If
H<G
is a subgroup and
Y_{0}
is an
H
-complex, then for
Y=G\times_{H}Y_{0}
we have

b^{G}_{*}(Y;D)=b^{H}_{*}(Y_{0};D),

where the latter is computed using the composition
\mathbb{Z}H\hookrightarrow\mathbb{Z}G\to D
, since the chain complexes used to compute the two homologies are identical:

C_{*}(Y_{0})\otimes_{\mathbb{F}H}D=C_{*}(Y_{0})\otimes_{\mathbb{F}H}\mathbb{F}G\otimes_{\mathbb{F}G}D=C_{*}(Y)\otimes_{\mathbb{F}G}D.

Lemma 3.4
.

Suppose
G
is a bi-orderable or amenable group with
\mathbb{F}G
having no zero-divisors, and
Y
is a cocompact, free
G
-complex.
For each connected component of
Y/G
choose its lift
Y_{i}
to
Y
, and let
G_{i}
denote the stabilizer of
Y_{i}
in
G
.
Then each
G_{i}
is finitely generated and

b_{k}^{G}(Y;D_{\mathbb{F}G})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{k}^{G_{i}}(Y_{i};D_{\mathbb{F}G_{i}}).

Proof.

Since each
Y_{i}
is a connected cocompact free
G_{i}
-complex, each
G_{i}
is finitely generated.

Y
is a disjoint union of
G
-orbits of
Y_{i}
, i.e.
Y=\coprod_{i=1}^{n}G\times_{G_{i}}Y_{i}
.
In both constructions the division closure of each
\mathbb{F}G_{i}
in
D_{\mathbb{F}G}
is
D_{\mathbb{F}G_{i}}
.
Therefore

b_{k}^{G}(Y;D_{\mathbb{F}G})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{k}^{G_{i}}(Y_{i};D_{\mathbb{F}G})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{k}^{G_{i}}(Y_{i};D_{\mathbb{F}G_{i}}).

∎

Torsion-free nilpotent groups

Let
N
be a torsion-free nilpotent group.
Then
N
is both bi-orderable and amenable, and both constructions produce the same skew field
D_{\mathbb{F}N}
.
It will be most useful here to think of
D_{\mathbb{F}N}
as an Ore localization.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
N
be a torsion-free nilpotent group.
Then for any free, cocompact
N
-CW complex
Y

b^{N}_{*}(Y;D_{\mathbb{F}N})\leq b_{*}(Y/N;\mathbb{F}).

Proof.

By Lemma
3.4
we can assume that
N
is finitely generated.
Let
C=\langle t\rangle
be a normal, infinite cyclic subgroup of
N
with
H=N/C
torsion-free nilpotent.
The existence of such a subgroup follows from the fact that the center
Z(N)
of
N
is infinite and
N/Z(N)
is torsion-free nilpotent
[
66
]
*Lemma 11.1.3, p. 470.

The quotient map
N\to H
induces a map
p:\mathbb{F}N\to\mathbb{F}H
and
\ker p
is the two-sided principal ideal generated by
(1-t)
.
We claim that
p
extends to a local homomorphism
D_{\mathbb{F}N}\to D_{\mathbb{F}H}
where the domain consists of elements which have a representation with denominator not in
\ker p
.
The only nontrivial part of this claim is that the domain is a subring of
D_{\mathbb{F}N}
, or equivalently, that
(\mathbb{F}N,S:=\mathbb{F}N-\ker p)
satisfies the Ore condition.

To see this, take
r\in\mathbb{F}N
and
s\in S
.
Since
(\mathbb{F}N;\mathbb{F}N-\{0\})
satisfies the Ore condition, there are
r^{\prime},s^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}N
with
rs^{\prime}=sr^{\prime}
, and we need to show that
s^{\prime}
can be chosen in
S
.

The key point is that there is a bound on the powers of
(1-t)
that divide
s^{\prime}
.
Indeed, take a coset of
C
which intersects the support of
s^{\prime}
nontrivially; for a suitable choice of
g\in N
the restriction of
s^{\prime}
to this coset has the form
gP(t)
where
P(t)
is a polynomial in
t
.
The right multiplication by
(1-t)
preserves the coset decomposition, hence the power of
(1-t)
dividing
s^{\prime}
on the right is bounded above by the degree of
P(t)
.

Now, if
s^{\prime}\in\ker p
, then
s^{\prime}r=sr^{\prime}\in\ker p
, and hence
r^{\prime}\in\ker p
as
\mathbb{F}H
has no zero divisors.
Therefore, both
s^{\prime}
and
r^{\prime}
are divisible on the right by
(1-t)
, and we can cancel to get a new
s^{\prime}
and
r^{\prime}
.
So, we can keep cancelling powers of
(1-t)
until
s^{\prime}\in S
.

Applying the same procedure to the quotient group
H
in place of
N
and composing local homomorphisms eventually produces a trivial quotient and therefore a local homomorphism
D_{\mathbb{F}N}\to\mathbb{F}
extending the augmentation map.
∎

Residually torsion-free nilpotent groups

From now on, we suppose that the group
G
is residually torsion-free nilpotent.
Then
G
is bi-orderable, and in fact as explained in
[
28
]
*Corollary to Lemma 4.1, any torsion-free nilpotent approximation can be made into an bi-orderable one by a suitable choice of orders.
Then the equivariant Betti numbers of a
G
-complex
Y
are approximated by the equivariant Betti numbers of the torsion-free nilpotent quotients, which in turn are approximated by normalized usual Betti numbers of finite quotients.
This leads to equivalence between the skew field definition and the infimum definition for simply connected
Y
.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
G
be a residually torsion-free nilpotent group and let
Y
be a free cocompact
G
-complex.
Then

b^{G}_{*}(Y;D_{\mathbb{F}G})=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}H<G\\
[G:H]<\infty\end{subarray}}\frac{b_{*}(Y/H;\mathbb{F})}{[G:H]}.

Proof.

Let
H<G
be a finite index subgroup of
G
.
Choose a torsion free nilpotent approximation
K_{i}\lhd H\twoheadrightarrow N_{i}
.
Then, using Theorem
3.2
and Lemma
3.5
, for sufficiently large
i
we have,

b^{H}_{*}(Y;D_{\mathbb{F}H})=b^{N_{i}}_{*}(Y/K_{i};D_{\mathbb{F}N_{i}})\leq b_{*}(Y/H;\mathbb{F}).

The multiplicativity now implies
\leq
inequality in the desired formula.

For the opposite inequality we first find, similar to the above, a torsion free nilpotent quotient
K\lhd G\twoheadrightarrow N
with
b^{G}_{*}(Y;D_{\mathbb{F}G})=b^{N}_{*}(Y/K;D_{\mathbb{F}N})
and then apply Theorem
3.1
to the
N
-complex
Y/K
to find a further finite quotient so that
b^{N}_{*}(Y/K;D_{\mathbb{F}N})
is approximated within any given
\epsilon
by the normalized usual Betti numbers.
∎

Simply connected components

When the components of the
G
-complex
Y
are simply connected, then we can use Theorem
3.6
to relate skew field Betti numbers to homology growth of the quotient
Y/G
(note that if
Y
is not simply connected then the right-hand term in Theorem
3.6
is generally not equal to
\beta^{\inf}_{\ast}(Y/G)
).

Corollary 3.7
.

Suppose
G
is a residually torsion-free nilpotent group and
Y
is a cocompact, free
G
-complex with simply connected components.
Then

b_{k}^{G}(Y;D_{\mathbb{F}G})=\beta^{\inf}_{k}(Y/G;\mathbb{F}).

Proof.

By Lemma
3.4

b_{k}^{G}(Y;D_{\mathbb{F}G})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{k}^{G_{i}}(Y_{i};D_{\mathbb{F}G_{i}}).

where each
Y_{i}
is a connected component of
Y
and
G_{i}
is its stabilizer.
By assumption each
Y_{i}
is simply connected, so finite connected covers of
Y_{i}/G_{i}
correspond to finite index subgroups of
G_{i}
.

Therefore, Theorem
3.6
and Lemma
2.8
imply that right hand side equals
\sum_{i}\beta^{\inf}_{k}(Y_{i}/G_{i};\mathbb{F})
.
Finally, additivity of
\beta^{\inf}
in disjoint unions identifies this with
\beta^{\inf}_{k}(Y/G;\mathbb{F})
.
∎

Remark
.

Droms
[
26
]
and Duchamp–Krob
[

Since this property passes to subgroups, the fundamental group of any compact special cube complex in Haglund and Wise’s sense is residually torsion-free nilpotent.

Remarks
.

Many of the results in this section (and stronger versions) were previously known.
The fact that for a torsion-free nilpotent group
N
there is a local homomorphism
D_{\mathbb{F}N}\to\mathbb{F}
extending the augmentation map follows from
[
70
]
*Theorem 2.2, which implies that the complement of the augmentation ideal in
\mathbb{F}N
satisfies the Ore condition.
The local ring in this case then consists of fractions with representatives
f/g
where
g
is not in the augmentation ideal.

More generally, a skew field
D
containing and generated by
\mathbb{F}G
is called
universal
if any homomorphism
\alpha:\mathbb{F}G\to D^{\prime}
can be extended to a local homomorphism
\alpha:D\to D^{\prime}
.
Of course, if
D
is universal, there is a local homomorphism
D\to\mathbb{F}
extending the augmentation map, so we obtain the same statement as in Lemma
3.5
.
For instance, Passman
[
65
]
showed that if
G
is poly-
\mathbb{Z}
, then
D_{\mathbb{F}G}
is a universal division ring for
\mathbb{F}G
.

Jaikin-Zapirain
[
45
]
*Corollary 1.3 proved that a residually (amenable locally indicable) group has a Hughes-free epic embedding, and this embedding is universal.
The core of his construction is a very general form of the approximation theorem by locally indicable quotients.
He also observed
[
45
]
*Proposition 2.2 that a recent result of Gräter
[
36
]
*Corollary 8.3 that Hughes-free embeddings are strongly Hughes-free implies multiplicativity of the equivariant Betti numbers, see also
[
32
]
*Lemma 6.3 for more details.
Since the Linnell–Lück–Sauer theorem applies to amenable locally indicable groups, it follows that Theorem
3.6
holds for residually (amenable locally indicable) groups.
For these groups, Fisher, Hughes and Leary in a recent paper independently proved one inequality in Theorem

Theorem 5.1
.

Let

be a graph product of finite groups.
Suppose

\lvert b_{i}(\Gamma;\mathbb{F})/n-\tilde{b}_{i-1}(L;\mathbb{F})\rvert\leq 2\lvert\partial K_{{L}}\rvert/\min\lvert G_{s}\rvert

where

b_{i}(\Gamma;\mathbb{F})/n\leq b_{i-1}(L;\mathbb{F})

Proof.

Since all

Let

X

For a cube

In particular, the cubes in

A hyperbolic reflection group trick

In this section, we describe how to build a closed, aspherical manifold

The idea is to combine the Davis reflection group trick with the strict hyperbolization procedure of Charney–Davis

The output is very similar to the relative strict hyperbolization procedure of Belegradek

Our hyperbolic reflection group trick has one major difference with Belegradek’s procedure: our output has many disjoint copies of the input

Virtual specialness of Charney–Davis pieces

We start by recalling that the arithmetic manifold
A^{n}
, and hence

, has virtually special fundamental group.
The manifold
A^{n}
is constructed via arithmetic methods, and involves some number of choices.
We can take it to be the quotient of the hyperbolic space

by a congruence subgroup of the orthogonal group of the quadratic form

-\frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}x_{0}^{2}+x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+\dots+x_{n}^{2}

over the ring
\mathbb{Z}\left[\frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}\right]

This is a uniform arithmetic lattice of simplest type (we give a definition and discuss some properties of such lattices in Section

Moreover, as pointed out in
[
17
]
, for

the orthogonal group is virtually a Coxeter group.
The manifolds

that we cut

along are images under the covering projection of hyperplanes in
\mathbb{H}^{n}
given by setting
x_{i}=0
for

Charney and Davis showed that if

is any torsion-free congruence subgroup of the orthogonal group, then all the conditions of Theorem

hold.
In
[

]
, Haglund and Wise showed virtual specialness for such lattices, see also
[

]
for an alternative approach.

Theorem 8.1

(
[
39
]
,
[

]
)

Uniform arithmetic lattices in
\mathbb{H}^{n}
of simplest type are virtually special.

The group
\pi_{1}(CD^{n})

is a quasiconvex subgroup of

Since quasiconvex subgroups of hyperbolic virtually special groups are virtually special, we have

The fundamental group of a Charney–Davis piece is virtually special.

Our manifold

We begin by showing that the fundamental groups of strictly hyperbolized cones are virtually special, and then do the resolved case.

Let

be a flag complex, and let

be the corresponding hyperbolized cone.
If

have quite complicated topology.

Note that if
v
is a vertex of

is different than the mirror
h(K_{v})
used to reflect the hyperbolized Davis chamber around in the previous section.
Indeed, that subcomplex was a hyperbolization of the cubical star of the vertex
[v]
in
\partial K_{L}
, whereas this is the hyperbolization of the subcomplex of cubes containing the cone point and corresponding to simplices in

Theorem 8.3
.

Let

be a finite

-dimensional flag complex, and suppose that

is obtained by applying the

have virtually special fundamental group.

To prove this, we use the following result of Groves and Manning, which generalizes Wise’s notion of a quasiconvex hierarchy for virtually special groups.

Theorem 8.4

(Groves–Manning
[
37
]
*Theorems A and D)

Suppose that
G
is a hyperbolic group acting cocompactly on a CAT
(0)
cube complex

so that vertex stabilizers are quasi-convex and virtually special.

is virtually special.

Proof of Theorem
8.3
.

We induct on the number of vertices of
L
.
If
L
is a simplex, then
K_{{L}}
is a single cube.
Therefore, the fundamental group of each component of
hK_{{L}}
is a Charney–Davis piece (or face of such), and so by assumption is virtually special.
Otherwise, we can find a vertex

is not equal to

and hence a decomposition of

Now, take a connected component of

This is a union of components of
hK_{{\St(s)}}
and

meeting along components of
hK_{{\Lk(s)}}

This union gives us a graph of groups decomposition with vertex groups the fundamental groups of components of

and components of

Let

Theorem

If

Virtual retractions

The same argument applied to
hK_{{L}}
in place of

shows that the fundamental group of each

is hyperbolic and virtually special.
We record a corollary of the fact that the
hP_{L}
have virtually special fundamental groups and work of Haglund–Wise
[
38
]
*Sections 6 and 7 that we will use in the next section in order to prove Mayer–Vietoris inequalities for homology growth of manifolds obtained by the hyperbolic reflection group trick.

We need a few simple observations, which are true for general complexes.
Given a map
f:Z\to X
we say that

is a

of

if there exist a finite cover
p:X^{r}\to X
, an injective lift
g:Z\hookrightarrow X^{r}
of

, and a retraction

such that

.

Lemma 8.9
.

Suppose
f:Z\to X
and
Z
consists of finitely many components
Z_{i}
.
If each
Z_{i}
is a virtual retract of
X
, then
Z
is a virtual retract of
X
.

Proof.

By assumption, there are finite covers
X_{i}\to X
, lifts
g_{i}:Z_{i}\hookrightarrow X_{i}
and retractions
r_{i}:X_{i}\to Z_{i}
.
These assemble to a lift
Z=\coprod Z_{i}\hookrightarrow\coprod X_{i}=:X^{r}
and a retraction
X^{r}\to Z
.
∎

Lemma 8.10
.

Suppose
f:Z\rightarrow X
is a virtual retract.
Then, in the notation of the above definition:

(1)

If
q:X^{\prime}\to X
is a finite cover of
X
, then the pullback
q^{*}(Z)=f^{*}(X^{\prime})
is a virtual retract of
X^{\prime}
.

(2)

If
t:Z^{\prime}\rightarrow Z
is a finite cover of
Z
, then
Z^{\prime}
is a virtual retract of
X^{r}
.

(3)

f^{*}(C_{X})
is a cofinal subset of
C_{Z}
.

(4)

If
f:Z\hookrightarrow X
is a retract of
X
, then
f^{*}(C_{X})=C_{Z}
.

Proof.

Under the assumptions of (1), the pullback
q^{*}(X^{r})=p^{*}(X^{\prime})
is a cover of
X^{\prime}
which retracts onto
q^{*}(Z)
via
q^{*}(r)
.
This proves (1).

Under the assumptions of (2), the pullback
t^{*}(X^{r})=r^{*}(Z^{\prime})
is a finite cover of
X^{r}
which retracts onto
Z^{\prime}
via
t^{*}(r)
.
This proves (2).

Starting with a finite cover
Z^{\prime}
of

, we can think of

as a finite cover of

.
Let

be a further regular cover of

.
Since

is regular and covers

.
Since

, regarding

as a cover of

is a cover of

which covers

.
Therefore,

as well.

If

is a retract of

, then

of

.

Our goal in this section is to control the homology growth of a space

Mayer–Vietoris inequalities for homology growth

Lemma 9.1

(Restricted Mayer–Vietoris inequalities)
.

Suppose
X=A_{1}\cup_{B}A_{2}
.

(1)

If
\overline{\beta}_{k-1}^{X}(B)=0
then

\overline{\beta}_{k}(X)\leq\overline{\beta}_{k}^{X}(A_{1})+\overline{\beta}_{k}^{X}(A_{2}).

(2)

If
\overline{\beta}_{k}^{X}(B)=0
then

\displaystyle\underline{\beta}_{k}^{X}(A_{i})

\displaystyle\leq\underline{\beta}_{k}(X),

\displaystyle\overline{\beta}_{k}^{X}(A_{i})

\displaystyle\leq\overline{\beta}_{k}(X).

Proof.

For any cover
X^{\prime}\to X
denote the induced covers by
′
.
The Mayer–Vietoris sequence

\dots\to H_{k}(B^{\prime})\to H_{k}(A_{1}^{\prime})\oplus H_{k}(A_{2}^{\prime})\to H_{k}(X^{\prime})\to H_{k-1}(B^{\prime})\to\dots

implies that on the poset
C_{X}
the normalized Betti functions satisfy

\beta_{k}(X)\leq\beta_{k}(A_{1})+\beta_{k}(A_{2})+\beta_{k-1}(B).

Taking the upper limit of this inequality over
C_{X}
and noting that
\overline{\beta}_{k-1}^{X}(B)=0
proves part (1) of the lemma.
The same Mayer–Vietoris sequence implies that on
C_{X}

\beta_{k}(A_{1})+\beta_{k}(A_{2})\leq\beta_{k}(B)+\beta_{k}(X).

Taking the upper or lower limit of this inequality over
C_{X}
and using
\overline{\beta}_{k}^{X}(B)=0
and subadditivity Lemma
2.2
(
2
) proves (2).
∎

, obtain an immersion with zero intersection vector and finally apply Proposition
III.2
to get an embedding.
This finishes the proof of Theorem
III.1
.

2
-dimensional octahedralizations

One might wonder if there is a more natural way to embed
OL
in
S^{2d}
.
Recently
Tâm Nguyễn Phan
and the first author showed that the

restriction in Theorem

cannot be removed.

Theorem III.7

(
[
7
]
)
.

For a sufficiently fine flag triangulation of the
2
-complex
D^{2}\cup_{3}S^{1}
, the octahedralization
O(D^{2}\cup_{3}S^{1})
does not
PL

Immersions of
OL
via perturbation

The goal of the remainder of this appendix is to show that we don’t really need orderings or the moment map to obtain a generic immersion satisfying the invariance property (
3
).
A small, generic, linear perturbation of a generic linear immersion of
L
will do!

Suppose
L
is linearly immersed in
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
.
For each vertex
v
pick a vector
X_{v}
in
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
.
For a simplex
\sigma
and subset of its vertices
A
, we will denote by
\sigma_{\epsilon A}
the linear simplex in
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
obtained from the image of
\sigma
by moving each vertex
v
in
A
by the vector
\epsilon X_{v}
.
Denote by
\overline{\sigma_{\epsilon A}}
the plane spanned by this simplex.

The collection of vectors
\{X_{v}\}
is a
generic linear perturbation
of
L\to\mathbb{R}^{2d}
if for every pair of
d
-simplices
\sigma
and
\tau
in
L
and partition containing their common vertices

L^{(0)}\supset A\coprod B\supset(\sigma\cap\tau)^{(0)}

there is an
\epsilon^{\prime}>0
such that for each
0<\epsilon\leq\epsilon^{\prime}
the following two conditions hold.

•

\overline{\sigma_{\epsilon A}}
and
\overline{\tau_{\epsilon B}}
are
d
-planes that intersect in a single point
p_{\epsilon}
, and

•

p_{\epsilon}
does not lie on the boundaries of the simplicies:
p_{\epsilon}\notin\partial\sigma_{\epsilon A}\cup\partial\tau_{\epsilon B}
.

In particular, for a pair of simplices
\sigma
and
\tau
that are disjoint in
L
, this is saying that their intersection in
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
is transverse.

It is easy to see that generic linear perturbations of generic immersions exist, and in fact form an open dense subset of all possible choices of vectors
\{X_{v}\}
.

Genericity has several important consequences.

(1)

The intersections form a half-open linear interval
p_{[\epsilon^{\prime},0)}
(because everything defining it is linear) converging to a point
p_{s}\to p_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
.
In other words, there is a vector
W
in
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
such that for
0<\epsilon\leq\epsilon^{\prime}
we have
p_{\epsilon}=p_{0}+\epsilon W
.
Next, pick a vertex
v
of
\tau
that is not contained in
A\coprod B
and look at what happens to the intersection as we vary
\tau_{\epsilon B}
by moving the vertex
v
in the direction
tX_{v}
.
This varies the vector
W
linearly in
t
, i.e. we have for small enough
t
that

\overline{\sigma_{\epsilon A}}\cap\overline{\tau_{tv\cup\epsilon B}}=p_{0}+\epsilon(W+tV)=p_{\epsilon}+\epsilon tV

for some vector
V
which can be expressed infinitesimally as

V:=\frac{1}{\epsilon}\frac{d(\overline{\sigma_{\epsilon A}}\cap\overline{\tau_{tv\cup\epsilon B}})}{dt}\big\rvert_{t=0}.

In particular, for small enough
\epsilon
, the intersection between the planes spanned by
\sigma_{\epsilon A}
and
\tau_{\epsilon(v\cup B)}
occurs at
p_{\epsilon}+\epsilon^{2}V
.

(2)

There is linear isomorphism
\pi:\overline{\sigma_{\epsilon A}}\cong\overline{\sigma}
that identifies
\sigma_{\epsilon A}
with
\sigma
and is bilipschitz, with bilipschitz constant tending to one as
\epsilon\to 0
.
The image
\pi(p_{[\epsilon^{\prime},0)})
is a linear family in
\overline{\sigma}
that does not meet
\partial\sigma
, which implies there is a constant
C^{\prime}
such that
d(\pi(p_{\epsilon}),\partial\sigma)>C^{\prime}\epsilon
for all
0<\epsilon\leq\epsilon^{\prime}
.
The same statement holds with
\sigma_{\epsilon A}
and
\sigma
replaced by
\tau_{\epsilon B}
and
\tau
.
Therefore, there is a positive constant
C
such that

(4)

d(p_{\epsilon},\partial\sigma_{\epsilon A}\cup\partial\tau_{\epsilon B})>C\epsilon.

Now, let
P:=P^{d+1}
be the
(d+1)
-plane spanned by
\tau_{\epsilon B}
and
\tau_{\epsilon(v\cup B)}
, (or, equivalently, by
\tau_{\epsilon B}
and
X_{v}
).
Parametrize
P\cap\overline{\sigma_{\epsilon A}}
as a line
L(s)
with
L(0)=p_{\epsilon}
and
L(1)=p_{\epsilon}+\epsilon^{2}V
.
Note that

•

for small enough
\epsilon
it follows from (
4
) that
L(0)\in\sigma_{\epsilon A}
if and only if
L(1)\in\sigma_{\epsilon A}
.

We will need an analogous statement involving
\tau_{\epsilon B}
and
\tau_{\epsilon(v\cup B)}
.
To that end, note that
P
has linear retractions

\overline{\tau_{\epsilon B}}\xleftarrow{r_{1}}P_{\epsilon}\xrightarrow{r_{2}}\overline{\tau_{\epsilon(v\cup B)}}

whose Lipschitz constants are uniformly bounded as
\epsilon\to 0
and which restrict to inverse isomorphisms
r_{2}|_{\overline{\tau_{\epsilon B}}}=r_{1}^{-1}|_{\overline{\tau_{\epsilon(v\cup B)}}}
identifying
\tau_{\epsilon B}
with
\tau_{\epsilon(v\cup B)}
.

Lemma III.8
.

For small enough
\epsilon>0
, we have
L(0)\in\tau_{\epsilon B}
if and only if
L(1)\in\tau_{\epsilon(v\cup B)}
.

Proof.

Note that
p_{\epsilon}=L(0)=r_{1}(L(0))
and that
d(r_{1}(L(0)),r_{1}(L(1)))\leq\lvert r_{1}\lvert\lvert V\rvert\epsilon^{2}
.
Since
\lvert r_{1}\rvert\rvert V\rvert
is uniformly bounded independent of
\epsilon
, (
4
) implies for small enough
\epsilon
that
p_{\epsilon}
is in
\tau_{\epsilon B}
if and only if
r_{1}(L(1))
is in
\tau_{\epsilon B}
.
This happens if and only if
r_{2}r_{1}(L(1))=L(1)
is in
\tau_{\epsilon(v\cup B)}
.
So, we are done.
∎

Corollary III.9
.

For small enough
\epsilon>0
,
\sigma_{\epsilon A}
intersects
\tau_{\epsilon B}
if and only if
\sigma_{\epsilon A}
intersects
\tau_{\epsilon(B\cup v)}
.
Moreover, the signs of the intersections are the same.

Proof.

The bullet point and lemma above imply that for small enough
\epsilon
we have
L(0)\in\sigma_{\epsilon A}\cap\tau_{\epsilon B}
if and only if
L(1)\in\sigma_{\epsilon A}\cap\tau_{\epsilon(v\cup B)}
, which proves the first part.
The second statement is clear because (for small enough
\epsilon
and
0\leq t\leq\epsilon
) the signed intersection number
\overline{\sigma_{\epsilon A}}\cap\overline{\tau_{tv\cup\epsilon B}}
is defined and independent of
t
.
∎

Proposition III.10

(Invariance property)
.

Suppose
f:L\to\mathbb{R}^{2d}
is a generic linear immersion.
Then for any generic linear perturbation
\{X_{v}\}
there is an
\varepsilon>0
such that the linear immersion
f_{\varepsilon}:OL\to\mathbb{R}^{2d}
defined on vertices by
v^{+}\mapsto f(v),v^{-}\mapsto f(v)+\epsilon X_{v}
is generic and its signed intersection numbers satisfy

f_{\epsilon}(\sigma)\cap f_{\epsilon}(\tau)=f_{\epsilon}(\sigma)\cap f_{\epsilon}(\tau^{\prime})

whenever
\sigma,\tau
and
\tau^{\prime}
are
d
-simplices,
\tau
and
\tau^{\prime}
are disjoint from
\sigma
and
\pi(\tau)=\pi(\tau^{\prime})
.

Proof.

Given
\sigma
,
\tau
and
\tau^{\prime}
as in the statement of the proposition,
f_{\epsilon}(\sigma)=\sigma_{\epsilon A},

f_{\epsilon}(\tau)=\tau_{\epsilon B}
and
f_{\epsilon}(\tau^{\prime})=\tau_{\epsilon B^{\prime}}
for some
A\coprod B\supset(\sigma\cap\tau)^{(0)}\subset A\coprod B^{\prime}
.
Repeatedly using the Corollary we conclude that for small enough
\epsilon
there are equalities of signed intersection numbers

\sigma_{\epsilon A}\cap\tau_{\epsilon B}=\sigma_{\epsilon A}\cap\tau_{\epsilon(B\cup B^{\prime})}=\sigma_{\epsilon A}\cap\tau_{\epsilon B^{\prime}}

which proves the proposition.
∎

Remark
.

The embedding
OL\hookrightarrow S^{2d}
produced by the methods of this appendix appears quite exotic from a metric perspective, because it starts with an immersion of
OL
obtained by perturbing an immersion of
L
.
In this immersion, two vertices
v^{+}
and
v^{-}
that correspond to the positive and negative directions of the loop
S^{1}_{v}
are put very close together, as opposed to being antipodal in the sphere
S^{2d}
.
