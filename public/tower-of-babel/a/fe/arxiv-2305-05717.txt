Necessary and Sufficient Conditions for Kolmogorov’s Flux Laws on 𝕋 2 {\mathbb{T}}^{2} and 𝕋 3 {\mathbb{T}}^{3} .
Ethan Dudley

Abstract
Necessary and sufficient conditions for the third order Kolmogorov universal scaling flux laws are derived for the stochastically forced incompressible Navier-Stokes equations on the torus in 2d and 3d. This paper rigorously generalizes the result of [ 6 ] to functions which are heavy-tailed in Fourier space or have local finite time singularities in the inviscid limit. In other words we have rigorously derived the well known physical relationship the direct cascade is a local process and is non-trivial if and only if energy moves toward the small scales or singularities have occurred. Similarly, an inverse cascade occurs if and only if energy moves towards the k = 0 k=0 Fourier mode in the invisicid limit.



1
Introduction

In this paper, we consider the stochastically forced incompressible Navier-Stokes equations on the Torus
{\mathbb{T}}_{\lambda}^{d}=[0,\lambda)^{d}
for
d=2,3
:

\displaystyle du^{\nu}+(u^{\nu}\cdot\nabla u^{\nu}+\nabla p^{\nu})\;dt

\displaystyle=\nu\Delta u^{\nu}\;dt+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}f_{j}\;dW_{t}^{j}

(1)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot u^{\nu}

\displaystyle=0

Here
\nu
is the kinematic viscosity and represents the inverse Reynolds number,
u^{\nu}
is the viscous velocity, and
p^{\nu}
is the associated pressure. The forcing is assumed to be white-in-time and coloured-in-space Gaussian process represented by

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}f_{j}\;dW_{t}^{j}

where
\{W_{t}^{j}\}
are a family of independent one-dimensional Brownian motions supported on a common canonical filtered probability space denoted as
(\Omega,{\mathcal{F}},({\mathcal{F}}_{t}),{\mathbb{P}})
, and the
\{f_{j}\}
are smooth, divergence free vector fields on
{\mathbb{T}}_{\lambda}^{d}
satisfying the colouring condition

\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\|f_{j}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\varepsilon<\infty.

Weak solutions to Equation (
1
) are called
martingale solutions
and are the probabilistic analog of Leray-Hopf solutions. The existence of such solutions was first proven in
[
7
]
. See Section
2.1
for their definition and further information. As these solutions are random variables, they exhibit random fluctuations in the flow and a form of stochastic roughness. Furthermore, the Lagrangian trajectories are chaotic due to Stochastic Navier Stokes Equations possessing a positive Lyapunov exponent
[
3
]
. Solutions do not conserve kinetic energy along particular trajectories (i.e. in a deterministic sense), however, due to the martingale structure of the solutions, kinetic energy is conserved on average and for statistically stationary solutions there is a lack of dependence on the initial conditions. These kinds of features are all typically found in turbulent flows
[
19
]
, which is why Equation (
1
) has been studied as a model for fully developed turbulence in multiple situations. See
[
21
,
34
,
29
,
6
,
8
]
and the references within for examples.

Of interest in this paper is the dynamics of energy in the invisicid limit. In 1922, Richardson
[
35
]
conjectured that kinetic energy is not conserved in the invisicid limit and that there is a cascade of energy from the large scales where it is injected into the system to small scales where it may be dissipated by the viscosity. Kolmogorov provided a heuristic argument for the rate at which energy is transferred to the small scales for statistically stationary, isotropic flows in a series of papers in 1941 now known as K41 theory
[
25
,
23
,
24
]
. Since then his theory has been extended to 2D flows by Batchelor
[
2
]
, Kraichnan
[
26
]
, and Fjørtoft
[
17
]
and numerous experimental and simulation studies have agreed with their conjectures. Onsanger
[
32
]
expounded upon the ideas of Kolmogorov to provide an upper bound on the regularity of the velocity for which such an energy cascade can exist (See Section
1.1.2
for more details)

Within the mathematical literature, most of the attention surrounding the energy dynamics has been related to Onsanger’s conjecture (see
[
10
,
15
,
14
,
12
,
13
]
and the references within for a sample). Only relatively recently have mathematicians began to rigorously cement K41 theory in mathematical analysis. In the deterministic setting, in a landmark paper, Duchon and Robert
[
14
]
found a connection between Kolmogorov’s 4/5 law (see Section
1.1.1
for details) and possible local singularities in the velocity. Similar results were found for passive scalar turbulent flux laws in
[
40
,
4
,
38
]
. In the stochastic setting, Bedrossian et. al. provided sufficient conditions for Kolmogorov’s 4/3 and 4/5 laws on
{\mathbb{T}}^{3}
for continuous (in space) solutions
[
6
]
and in a later paper they provide similar sufficient conditions for the third order flux laws on
{\mathbb{T}}^{2}
when there is a friction term to collect energy at large scales
[
5
]
. However, their analysis only proves existence in the case that
all
of the energy is dissipated at the smallest length scales and when the solutions are continuous in space. However, recent studies on thin flows shows the existence of split cascades
[
31
,
20
]
, where some energy moves towards the smallest scales to be dissipated and the rest moves towards scales near the size of the system. Similarly in 2D soap films, energy will move to the small scales as part of the enstrophy while the rest will move to large scales to create eddies
[
36
,
26
]
. For these reasons, the author provides a rigorous mathematical characterization of Kolmogorov’s third order flux laws to classify them even when energy remains at medium and large length scales.

1.1
3D Incompressible Flows

Since the dynamics of the problem are slightly different when working in 2D vs 3D, we will examine them seperately. We begin with the 3D case as this has been historically the case due to it being slightly easier to analyze.

1.1.1
Kolmogorov’s Flux Laws

In the 1930s and 40s, the physical literature on turbulence was revolutionized by Taylor, Richardson, Kolmogorov, and Obukhov to name a few. In order to experimentally measure the fluctuation in the velocity, Taylor considered the difference in velocity at two difference points:
\delta_{h}u^{\nu}(x)=u^{\nu}(x+h)-u^{\nu}(x)
for all
h\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
, and averaged together multiple of these differences together
[
19
]
. These combinations are refered to as structure functions. Using three axioms about the velocity field:

1.

the flow is homogeneous and isotropic away from the boundary i.e.
\delta_{h}u^{\nu}
is invariant under translations and rotations away from the boundary (in the case of a bounded domain)

2.

the flow has mono-fractal scaling, i.e. the system is self similar with a single fractal scaling exponent
s\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that
\delta_{\lambda\ell}u^{\nu}=\lambda^{s}\delta_{\ell}u^{\nu}
for all
\lambda>0
and the increments
\lambda,\lambda\ell\ll\ell_{inj}
where
\ell_{inj}
is the scale of energy injection
[
37
]

3.

there is an finite non-zero mean rate of dissipation
\varepsilon
per unit mass independent of
\nu

Kolmogorov provided a heuristic argument that there is an inertial range
\ell_{\nu}\ll\ell\ll\ell_{inj}
such that

S_{vel,p}^{\parallel}(\ell):=\langle(\delta_{\ell n}u^{\nu}\cdot n)^{p}\rangle\sim C_{p}\varepsilon^{p/3}\ell^{p/3}

Here
C_{p}
is a fixed diffusion constant depending on the dimension and the power of
p
(and independent of
\nu
),
\langle\cdot\rangle
is some combination of ensemble, spatial, rotational, and temporal averaging and
S_{vel,p}^{\parallel}
is the
p
th order universal longitudinal velocity structure function. Landau famously objected to Kolmogorov’s second assumption of mono-fractal scaling due to intermittency effects, i.e. rare events and non-uniformity in the roughness of the velocity field,
[
19
]
. Indeed, both of the first two assumptions have been found to be generally false due to such intermittency effects
[
19
,
13
,
6
,
22
]
However such errors can be corrected for by including an intermittency corrector
\tau(p)
to the exponent on the right hand side of the law. See
[
22
]
and the references within for more details regarding intermittency. Nevertheless, experimental results show that the
p=3
case correlates exceptionally close to Kolmogorov’s prediction to the point that it is considered the only exact law in the physics literature
[
13
]
. This is because the
p=3
case doesn’t rely on the statistical assumptions of isotropy and mono-fractal scaling, but can be derived directly from the Navier Stokes equations via the Karman-Howarth-Monin (KHM) relations (see Section
2.2
for more details).

When
p=3
Kolmogorov’s prediction is known as the
4/5
-law due to its diffusion coefficient:

\langle(\delta_{\ell n}u^{\nu}\cdot n)^{3}\rangle\sim-\frac{4}{5}\varepsilon\ell\quad\quad\ell_{\nu}\ll\ell\ll\ell_{inj}

(2)

where
n
it a unit vector, and the dissipation scale is given by
\ell_{\nu}=(\nu^{3}/\varepsilon)^{1/4}
is the scale at which dissipation effects begin to dominate the flow. There is also a closely related
4/3
-law originally derived by Yaglom
[
41
]
for passive scalars:

\langle|\delta_{\ell n}u^{\nu}|^{2}\delta_{\ell n}u^{\nu}\cdot n\rangle\sim\frac{-4}{3}\varepsilon\ell\quad\ell_{\nu}\ll\ell\ll\ell_{inj}

(3)

In both instances, the negative sign on the flux indicates that energy is moving from large scales to small scales and one can see that the rate of the flux decreases proportional to the scale.

1.1.2
Anomalous Dissipation of Kinetic Energy

Finally, we consider Kolmogorov’s third “axiom” which is also known as the anomalous dissipation of kinetic energy. Experimental evidence for such behaviour was first observed by Taylor
[
39
]
and can be thought of as a loss at large Reynold’s numbers type assumption. The cause of this anomalous behaviour is thought to be due to the nonlinear term
(u^{\nu}\cdot\nabla)u^{\nu}
, which is accelerating the movement of energy to small scales in such a way that
u^{\nu}
losses its derivative in the invisicid limit.

To phrase this mathematically, first consider the determinstic case:
It is well known that Leray-Hopf solutions to the Navier-Stoke’s equation satisfy an energy inequality of the form (see
[
13
]
for example)

\|u^{\nu}(T)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\nu\int_{0}^{T}\|\nabla u^{\nu}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\;dt\leq\|u^{\nu}(0)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\langle f_{j},u^{\nu}(t)\rangle\;dt

Then typically for such deterministic solutions, anomalous dissipation is defined as there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that

\liminf_{\nu\to 0}\nu\int_{0}^{T}\|\nabla u^{\nu}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\;dt\geq\varepsilon>0

Thus anomalous dissipation can be seen as a loss in regularity type assumption where the
L^{2}(0,T,\dot{H}^{1})
norm of
u^{\nu}
must blow up like
1/\nu
. In a landmark paper by Duchon and Robert
[
14
]
it was shown that this inequality may be transformed into an equality if one includes a Radon measure
D(u^{\nu})
which is defined locally in terms of (possible) finite time singularities. Then one may consider a larger class of solutions
u^{\nu}
where
\nu\int_{0}^{T}\|\nabla u^{\nu}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\;dt\to 0
but the invisicid limit consists of singularities on a set of positive measure.

Similarly, for statistically stationary martingale solutions one can form the energy balance via Ito’s lemma

\displaystyle\nu\mathbf{E}\fint_{0}^{T}\|\nabla u^{\nu}(s)\|_{\lambda}^{2}\;ds+\mathbf{E}D(u^{\nu})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j}\|f_{j}\|_{\lambda}^{2}=\varepsilon

(4)

See the Appendix for the details for how to derive the energy balance (
4
). Additionally if the forcing has a deterministic component one can establish a similar bound, see
[
9
]
for details.
We note that while the right hand side of the energy balance is independent of the viscosity, the deterministic regime’s definition of anomalous dissipation does not apply here when
u^{\nu}
is sufficiently regular so that
\mathbf{E}D(u^{\nu})\equiv 0
. This is because there is nothing inherently nonlinear nor anomalous in the statement:

\nu\mathbf{E}\fint_{0}^{T}\|\nabla u^{\nu}(s)\|_{\lambda}^{2}\;ds=\varepsilon

as the same energy balance can be achieved by the stochastic Heat equation
[
6
]
. Thus in the stochastic regime we define anomalous dissipation as

Definition 1.1
.

We say that a sequence
\{u^{\nu}\}_{\nu\geq 0}
of stationary martingale solutions satisfies weak anomalous dissipation if either

\liminf_{\nu\to 0}\mathbf{E}D(u^{\nu})>0

(5)

or there exists
N_{\nu}\to\infty
such that

\liminf_{\nu\to 0}\nu\sum_{|k|\geq N_{\nu}}\mathbf{E}\fint_{0}^{T}|\widehat{\nabla u^{\nu}}(k)|^{2}>0

(6)

Notice that condition (
6
) can be restated as in the invisicid limit, the family of solutions
u^{\nu}
moves a sufficient amount of energy to small scales for
u^{\nu}
to lose its
H^{1}
regularity.

Remark 1.2
.

Previously, the authors in
[
6
]
and
[
33
]
defined a weak anomalous dissipation condition in terms of the Taylor micro-scale

\lim_{\nu\to 0}\nu\mathbf{E}\|u^{\nu}(t)\|_{\lambda}^{2}=0\quad\forall\;t

Such a condition is sufficient to guarantee the existence of a sequence
N_{\nu}
such that (
6
) holds. For example, if
N_{\nu}^{-2}=o(\nu\mathbf{E}\|u^{\nu}\|_{L^{2}}^{2})
then

\limsup_{\nu\to 0}\nu\sum_{|k|\leq N_{\nu}}\mathbf{E}\fint_{0}^{T}|\widehat{\nabla u^{\nu}}(k)|^{2}\leq\limsup_{\nu\to 0}N_{\nu}^{2}\nu\sum_{|k|\leq N_{\nu}}\mathbf{E}|\widehat{u^{\nu}}|^{2}\leq\limsup_{\nu\to 0}N_{\nu}^{2}\nu\mathbf{E}\|u^{\nu}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=0

Therefore

\liminf_{\nu\to 0}\nu\sum_{|k|\geq N_{\nu}}\mathbf{E}\fint_{0}^{T}|\widehat{\nabla u^{\nu}}(k)|^{2}=\varepsilon-\limsup_{\nu\to 0}\nu\sum_{|k|\leq N_{\nu}}\mathbf{E}\fint_{0}^{T}|\widehat{\nabla u^{\nu}}(k)|^{2}=\varepsilon>0

It is an interesting question whether the condition in (
5
) is true for some flows. Sufficient conditions for
\displaystyle\liminf_{\nu\to 0}D(u^{\nu})\equiv 0
have been found in the deterministic case, see
[
28
]
for work in this direction. To the authors no such analysis has been carried out in the stochastic regime, but similar results should hold. Additionally, one could ask under what regularity do solutions satisfy condition (
6
) and converge to a solution to the stochastic Euler equation. While this is merely the stochastic version of Onsanger’s conjecture, we remark that to the best of our knowledge no such analysis has been done (in the stochastic case) but most likely the answer relies on a translation of the work by Eyink
[
15
]
and Constantin et.al.
[
10
]
to the stochastic framework.

1.2
2D Incompressible Flows

Now we consider the problem’s dynamics when restricted to two dimensions.
In two dimensions one could extend the three dimensional results directly for particularly rough solutions. However, if the solution is nice enough that the initial velocity has finite expected enstrophy:

\mathbf{E}\|\omega^{\nu}(0)\|_{\lambda}^{2}<\infty\quad\omega^{\nu}(t)=\partial_{1}u_{2}^{\nu}(t)-\partial_{2}u_{1}^{\nu}(t)

then the 2D dynamics are largely disparate from the 3D dynamics. The main culprit for this difference is the lack of vortex stretching in 2D resulting in a well-behaved vorticity equation.

Consider the vorticity equation in two dimensions:

\displaystyle d\omega^{\nu}+(u^{\nu}\cdot\nabla)\omega^{\nu}\;dt

\displaystyle=\nu\Delta\omega^{\nu}\;dt+\sum_{j}{\mathrm{c}url}f_{j}\;dW_{t}^{j}

(7)

\displaystyle u^{\nu}

\displaystyle=\nabla^{\perp}(-\Delta)^{-1}\omega^{\nu}

Here
\nabla^{\perp}:=(-\partial_{2},\partial_{1})
and we call the second relation
u^{\nu}=\nabla^{\perp}(-\Delta)^{-1}\omega^{\nu}
the Biot-Savart law. As there is no vortex stretching, the vorticity equation is an advection-diffusion equation which is fairly well behaved. First, it is known that one can construct mild solutions by pushing the initial condition forward in time via a semi-group operator (see Section
2.1.1
for the definition and futher details).
Second, the total enstrophy is conserved for all time. This results in a second invariant for the system which
should
be conserved in the invisicid limit in some sense. In order to guarantee the existance of stationary measures we make th following compactness assumption:

Assumption 1.3
.

There exists a constant
C>0
such that

\displaystyle\sup_{\lambda\in(1,\infty)}\sum_{j}\|\nabla^{3}f_{j}\|_{\lambda}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C

This is a purely mathematical assumption used to ensure the existence of stationary measures for the 2D vorticity equation.

When extending Kolmogorov’s K41 theory to 2D, Kraichnan
[
26
]
, Batchelor
[
2
]
, Leith , and Fjortoft
[
17
]
independently observed two different inertial zones, one for each conserved quantity.
It was observed that now energy moves toward large scales in an inverse cascade, while enstrophy moves to small scales via a direct cascade. Such situations are frequently called dual cascades and are generally known to exist in systems with two conserved quantities
[
5
]
. Specifically the direct cascade flux laws for the velocity (and the vorticity as predicted by Eyink
[
16
]
) can be informally stated as there exists a dissipation scale
\ell_{\nu}
such that over the inertial range
\ell_{\nu}\ll\ell\ll\ell_{inj}

\displaystyle\langle|\delta_{\ell n}\omega^{\nu}|^{2}\delta_{\ell n}u^{\nu}\cdot n\;\rangle\sim-2\eta\ell

\displaystyle\langle|\delta_{\ell n}u^{\nu}|^{2}\delta_{\ell n}u^{\nu}\cdot n\rangle\sim\frac{1}{4}\eta\ell^{3}

\displaystyle\langle(\delta_{\ell n}u^{\nu}\cdot n)^{3}\rangle\sim\frac{1}{8}\eta\ell^{3}

Moreover, there is a
second
inertial range
\ell_{inj}\ll\ell\ll\tilde{\ell}_{nu}\leq\lambda
for the inverse cascade such that

\displaystyle\langle|\delta_{\ell n}u^{\nu}|^{2}\delta_{\ell n}u^{\nu}\cdot n\rangle\sim 2\varepsilon\ell

\displaystyle\langle(\delta_{\ell n}u^{\nu}\cdot n)^{3}\rangle\sim\frac{3}{2}\varepsilon\ell

A simple heuristic argument for the existence of two separate inertial ranges with different flux coefficients can be seen from the two associated balance laws for the system:

\displaystyle\nu\mathbf{E}\int_{0}^{T}\|\nabla\omega^{\nu}\|_{\lambda}^{2}\;dt=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\|{\mathrm{c}url}f_{j}\|_{\lambda}^{2}=:\eta<\infty

\displaystyle\nu\mathbf{E}\int_{0}^{T}\|\omega^{\nu}\|_{\lambda}^{2}\;dt=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\|f_{j}\|_{\lambda}^{2}=\varepsilon<\infty

The first comes from Ito’s lemma and computing the energy balance for stationary solutions to the vorticity equation, while the second one is the energy balance for stationary solutions to the Navier-Stokes equations after we applied the Biot-Savart Law. As neither of the right hand sides depend on the viscosity, it can be seen that at least one wave-number of
\omega^{\nu}
must become singular. Moreover, if there are two singular wave-numbers then flux of energy to these scales must occur at different rates as one is moving like
|k|^{2}\mathbf{E}|\widehat{\omega^{\nu}}(k)|^{2}
and the other is moving like
\mathbf{E}|\widehat{\omega^{\nu}}(k)|^{2}
.

While the existence of both cascades simultaneously had been conjectured for a while, only recently in
[
8
]
did researchers observe such a phenomena in soap films. This is because the inverse cascade is somewhat unstable as it is due to the difference in the amount of energy at large scales and how much energy the force pushes towards this end of the spectrum. Moreover, as we show in Corollary
if the enstrophy is does not blow up fast enough at small wave-numbers then there is no inverse cascade. For these reasons most works regarding inverse cascades include a friction force to dampen the large wave-number terms and collect the energy at small wave-numbers or a hyper/hypo-viscosity term to heighten the effects of the viscous dissipation. See
[
36
,
34
]
and the references within for more information. Within the mathematics literature far fewer works have been produced compared to the three dimensional case. However, we note that a major inspiration for this paper was the work by Bedrossian et.al.
[
5
]
where they showed sufficient conditions for the existence of a dual cascade to the Navier-Stoke’s equations with a frictional term of the form:
\alpha(-\Delta)^{-2\gamma}u^{\nu}
. In this paper, we generalize their argument to the case without friction.

1.3
Main Results

Inspired by the ideas in
[
6
]
,
[
5
]
, and
[
14
]
we characterize the direct cascades in terms of the (possible) finite time singularities and the amount of the energy which moves out to infinite wave-numbers. In contrast we characterize an inverse cascade as the amount of energy moving towards the wave-numbers near 0 due to the forcing. On top of reestablishing the results in each of the highlighted works, we can also provide necessary and sufficient conditions for split cascades and isolated cascades without frictional forces in both 2D and 3D.

First we characterize a direct cascade in both
{\mathbb{T}}^{2}
and
{\mathbb{T}}^{3}
separately due to their differences covered before.

Theorem 1.4

(3D Direct Cascade Characterization)
.

Suppose
\{u\}_{\nu>0}
is a sequence of statistically stationary solutions to the statistically forced Navier-Stokes equations (
1
) on
{\mathbb{T}}_{\lambda}^{3}
.
There exists
N_{\nu}\geq 1
such that
\displaystyle\lim_{\nu\to 0}N_{\nu}=\infty
and

\liminf_{\nu\to 0}\Big(\nu\sum_{|k|\geq N_{\nu}}\mathbf{E}\fint_{0}^{T}|\widehat{\nabla u}|^{2}+\mathbf{E}D(u^{\nu})\Big)=\varepsilon^{*}

if and only if there exists
\ell_{\nu}\in(0,1)
such that
\displaystyle\lim_{\nu\to 0}\ell_{\nu}=0
and

\displaystyle\lim_{\ell_{I}\to 0}\limsup_{\nu\to 0}\sup_{\ell\in[\ell_{\nu},\ell_{I}]}\Big|\frac{1}{\ell}\mathbf{E}\fint_{S^{2}}\fint_{0}^{T}\fint_{{\mathbb{T}}_{\lambda}^{3}}|\delta_{\ell n}u^{\nu}|^{2}(\delta_{\ell n}u^{\nu}\cdot n)\;dxdtdS(n)+\frac{4}{3}\varepsilon^{*}\Big|=0

(8)

\displaystyle\lim_{\ell_{I}\to 0}\limsup_{\nu\to 0}\sup_{\ell\in[\ell_{\nu},\ell_{I}]}\Big|\frac{1}{\ell}\mathbf{E}\fint_{S^{2}}\fint_{0}^{T}\fint_{{\mathbb{T}}_{\lambda}^{3}}(\delta_{\ell n}u^{\nu}\cdot n)^{3}\;dxdtdS(n)+\frac{4}{5}\varepsilon^{*}\Big|=0

(9)

Similarly in 2D:

Theorem 1.5

(2D Direct Cascade Characterization)
.

Let
\{u\}_{\nu>0}
be a sequence of statistically stationary solutions to the stochastically forced Navier-Stokes equations(
1
) and the 2D vorticity equation (
7
) on
{\mathbb{T}}_{\lambda}^{2}
.
There exists
N_{\nu}\geq 1
such that
\displaystyle\lim_{\nu\to 0}N_{\nu}=\infty
and

\liminf_{\nu\to 0}\nu\sum_{|k|\geq N_{\nu}}\mathbf{E}|\widehat{\nabla\omega^{\nu}}|^{2}=\eta^{*}

if and only if there exists
\ell_{\nu}\in(0,1)
such that
\displaystyle\lim_{\nu\to 0}\ell_{\nu}=0
and

\displaystyle\lim_{\ell_{I}\to 0}\limsup_{\nu\to 0}\sup_{\ell\in[\ell_{\nu},\ell_{I}]}\Big|\frac{1}{\ell^{3}}\mathbf{E}\fint_{S^{1}}\fint_{0}^{T}\fint_{{\mathbb{T}}_{\lambda}^{2}}|\delta_{\ell n}\omega^{\nu}|^{2}(\delta_{\ell n}u^{\nu}\cdot n)\;dxdtdS(n)+2\eta^{*}\Big|=0

(10)

\displaystyle\lim_{\ell_{I}\to 0}\limsup_{\nu\to 0}\sup_{\ell\in[\ell_{\nu},\ell_{I}]}\Big|\frac{1}{\ell^{3}}\mathbf{E}\fint_{S^{1}}\fint_{0}^{T}\fint_{{\mathbb{T}}_{\lambda}^{2}}|\delta_{\ell n}u^{\nu}|^{2}(\delta_{\ell n}u^{\nu}\cdot n)\;dxdtdS(n)-\frac{1}{4}\eta^{*}\Big|=0

(11)

\displaystyle\lim_{\ell_{I}\to 0}\limsup_{\nu\to 0}\sup_{\ell\in[\ell_{\nu},\ell_{I}]}\Big|\frac{1}{\ell^{3}}\mathbf{E}\fint_{S^{1}}\fint_{0}^{T}\fint_{{\mathbb{T}}_{\lambda}^{2}}(\delta_{\ell n}u^{\nu}\cdot n)^{3}\;dxdtdS(n)-\frac{1}{8}\eta^{*}\Big|=0

(12)

Here the limit
\ell_{I}\to 0
should
not
be confused with injection scale of energy moving to small scales. Instead
\ell_{I}
is acting as a dummy variable signifying when the scales go to 0. This is similar to the limits shown in
[
19
,
6
,
5
,
33
]
.

Remark 1.6
.

In both 2D and 3D, the size of the torus
\lambda
is insignificant. This is because the direct cascade limit localizes the structure function. For this reason, one can also extend these ideas to domains with boundaries by localizing each of the quantities. See
[
33
]
for details.

While the direct cascade, is significantly different between
{\mathbb{T}}^{2}
and
{\mathbb{T}}^{3}
, the existence of the inverse cascade is nearly identical in both dimensions. Moreover, the inverse cascade is very sensitive to the size of the domain and requires
\lambda\to\infty
. This is because while the direct cascade localizes the structure functions, the inverse cascade globalizes them.

Theorem 1.7

(Inverse Cascade Characterization)
.

Suppose that
\lambda=\lambda(\nu)<\infty
is a continuous monotone increasing function such that
\lim_{\nu\to 0}\lambda=\infty
. Let
\{u\}_{\nu>0}
be a sequence of statistically stationary solutions to (
1
) with mean-zero, divergence free forcing correlations
f_{j}
.
There exists a decreasing sequence
M_{\nu}
satisfying
\displaystyle\lim_{\nu\to 0}M_{\nu}=0
such that

\liminf_{\nu\to 0}\nu\sum_{|k|\leq M_{\nu}}\mathbf{E}\fint_{0}^{T}|\widehat{\nabla u^{\nu}}|^{2}=\varepsilon^{*}

if and only if there exists an increasing sequence
\tilde{\ell}_{\nu}\in(1,\lambda)
satisfying
\displaystyle\lim_{\nu\to 0}\tilde{\ell}_{\nu}=\infty
such that:

\displaystyle\lim_{\ell_{I}\to\infty}\limsup_{\nu\to 0}\sup_{\ell\in[\ell_{I},\tilde{\ell}_{\nu}]}\Big|\frac{1}{\ell}\mathbf{E}\fint_{S^{d-1}}\fint_{0}^{T}\fint_{{\mathbb{T}}_{\lambda}^{d}}|\delta_{\ell n}u^{\nu}|^{2}(\delta_{\ell n}u^{\nu}\cdot n)\;dxdtdS(n)-\gamma_{d}\varepsilon^{*}\Big|=0

(13)

\displaystyle\lim_{\ell_{I}\to\infty}\limsup_{\nu\to 0}\sup_{\ell\in[\ell_{I},\tilde{\ell}_{\nu}]}\Big|\frac{1}{\ell}\mathbf{E}\fint_{S^{d-1}}\fint_{0}^{T}\fint_{{\mathbb{T}}_{\lambda}^{d}}(\delta_{\ell n}u^{\nu}\cdot n)^{3}\;dxdtdS(n)-\kappa_{d}\varepsilon^{*}\Big|=0

(14)

where the coefficients are given by

\gamma_{d}=\begin{cases}2&d=2\\
4/3&d=3\end{cases}\quad\quad\kappa_{d}=\begin{cases}3/2&d=2\\
4/5&d=3\end{cases}

Remark 1.8
.

Notice that the difference in coefficients
\gamma_{d}
and
\kappa_{d}
when
d=2,3
are due to the difference in dimension but the proof is otherwise exactly the same.

Preliminaries

2.1
Stationary Martingale Solutions

As previously mentioned, one can construct weak solutions to the Navier Stokes equations (
1
) called martingale solutions. They are the probabilistic analog of Leray-Hopf solutions which also happens to be a square-integrable martingale. By this we mean that a martingale solutions consist of a
d
-dimensional stochastic basis
(\Omega,({\mathcal{F}}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]},{\mathbb{P}},\{W_{k}\})
and a process
u^{\nu}:\Omega\times[0,T]\to L_{{\mathrm{d}iv}}^{2}
which satisfies the Navier Stokes Equations in the sense of distributions and for all
s,t\in[0,T]

\mathbf{E}[u^{\nu}(t)\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{s})]=u^{\nu}(s)\quad\quad s\leq t

and
\mathbf{E}\|u^{\nu}(t)\|_{L^{2}}<\infty
. It is worth noting that what makes this probabilistically weak is that the stochastic basis must be solved for simultaneously to the process
u^{\nu}
which means that the forcing

\sum_{j}f_{j}\;dW_{t}^{j}

is an
output
rather than an input to the system. Hence in order to prescribe a particular forcing on the system we must do so through the choice of the spatial correlation vector fields
f_{j}
, which is slightly different from the deterministic setting.
Other remarks to be aware of is that two martingale solutions to (
1
) may exist on different stochastic basis and therefore have different trajectories for a particular
\omega\in\Omega
. Hence uniqueness is usually considered in the sense of the law of the process
u^{\nu}
.

The existence of martingale solutions to the stochastically forced Navier-Stokes Equations
(
1
) have been known since the 1970s with the work of Bensoussan
[
7
]
. Such solutions are rigorously defined as:

Definition 2.1
.

A
d
dimensional stochastic basis
(\Omega,({\mathcal{F}}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]},{\mathbb{P}},\{W_{k}(t)\}_{t\in[0,T]})
along with a complete right-continuous filtration and an
{\mathcal{F}}_{t}
-progressively measureable stochastic process
u^{\nu}:\Omega\times[0,T]\to L_{{\mathrm{d}iv}}^{2}({\mathbb{T}}_{\lambda}^{d})
is called a martingale solution to (
1
) on
[0,T]
if

•

the trajectories of
u^{\nu}
(i.e. the sample paths
u^{\nu}(\cdot,t)
for
t\in[0,T]
) belong to

C_{t}\dot{H}_{x}^{\alpha}({\mathbb{T}}_{\lambda}^{d})\cap L_{t}^{\infty}L_{\mathrm{d}iv}^{2}({\mathbb{T}}_{\lambda}^{d})\cap L_{t}^{2}H_{\mathrm{d}iv}^{1}({\mathbb{T}}_{\lambda}^{d}),\quad\text{ for some $\alpha<0$;}

•

for all
t\in[0,T]
and all

the following identity holds
{\mathbb{P}}
almost surely:

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}u^{\nu}(t)\cdot\phi\;dx

\displaystyle+\nu\int_{0}^{t}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}\nabla u^{\nu}(x):\nabla\phi\;dxds-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}u^{\nu}(s)\cdot(u^{\nu}(s)\cdot\nabla)\phi\;dxds

(15)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}u^{\nu}(0)\cdot\phi\;dx+\sum_{j}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}f_{j}\cdot\phi\;dxdW^{j}(s)

In this paper, we will call a process
u^{\nu}
a martingale solution without reference to its stochastic basis unless there is a risk of confusion.

Similar to the deterministic literature, several years after the existence of Leray-Hopf solutions did the existence of global in time weak solutions began to emerge. In the context of martingale solutions these global in time solutions are called statistically stationary martingale solutions as the law is invariant under time translations. They were first constructed in 1995 in the landmark paper by Flandoli and Gatarek
[
18
]
where they are defined as

Definition 2.2
.

A
d
-dimensional stochastic basis
(\Omega,({\mathcal{F}}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]},{\mathbb{P}},\{W_{k}(t)\}_{t\in[0,\infty)})
along with a complete right-continuous filtration and an
{\mathcal{F}}_{t}
-progressively measureable stochastic process
u^{\nu}:\Omega\times[0,\infty)\to L_{{\mathrm{d}iv}}^{2}({\mathbb{T}}_{\lambda}^{d})
is called a martingale solution to (
1
) on
[0,\infty)
if

•

for each
T\geq 0

u^{\nu}|_{[0,T]}
belong to

C_{t}\dot{H}_{x}^{\alpha}({\mathbb{T}}_{\lambda}^{d})\cap L_{t}^{\infty}L_{\mathrm{d}iv}^{2}({\mathbb{T}}_{\lambda}^{d})\cap L_{t}^{2}H_{\mathrm{d}iv}^{1}({\mathbb{T}}_{\lambda}^{d}),\quad\text{ for some $\alpha<0$;}

•

the path of
u(\cdot+s)=u(\cdot)
in law for all
s\geq 0
;

•

for all
t\geq 0
and all
\phi\in C_{\mathrm{d}iv}^{\infty}
identity (
15
) holds
{\mathbb{P}}
almost surely.

Again, we will simply call a process
u^{\nu}
a stationary martingale solution and omit reference to the associated stochastic basis.

Next, we consider the energy in the system. Specifically, we consider the total kinetic energy:

\frac{1}{2}\|u^{\nu}\|_{\lambda}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\fint_{{\mathbb{T}}_{\lambda}^{d}}|u^{\nu}|^{2}\;dx

and note that for stationary martingale solutions, the average kinetic energy is conserved for all time:

\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{E}\|u^{\nu}(t)\|_{\lambda}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{E}\|u^{\nu}(s)\|_{\lambda}^{2}\quad\forall\;t,s\in[0,\infty)

Using standard results regarding Leray-Hopf solutions, one can quickly extend the ideas in
[
18
]
to prove an energy inequality where the average energy dissipation due to viscosity is bounded by the total energy injected into the system by the force. However, this inequality can be made into an equality if one accounts for the dissipation of energy due to (possible) local singularities in the flow (such as shocks)
[
14
]
.

Theorem 2.3
.

Suppose

is a statistically stationary martingale solution to (

. Then the following energy equality holds:

\nu\mathbf{E}\fint_{0}^{T}\|\nabla u^{\nu}(s)\|_{\lambda}^{2}\;ds+\mathbf{E}D(u^{\nu})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j}\|f_{j}\|_{\lambda}^{2}=\varepsilon

This result is relatively well-known in the deterministic setting
[

but the author could find no reference to it within the stochastic literature. Therefore, the proof of this result can be found in the Appendix.

Such mild solutions are defined as

2.2

In this section we cover one of the key ideas behind the derivation of the third order flux laws: the Karman-Howarth-Monin (KHM) relations. These relations relate the various structure functions to two point correlations of the velocity or vorticity dissipation through the Navier-Stokes Equations. They were first derived for classical solutions to the determinstic Navier Stokes Equations by Karman and Howarth for the 4/5th law (

[

[
30

and Eyink
[

used an analogous KHM relation to derive Yaglom’s law (

{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
for
d=2,3

[

5

2.3.1
Expression for isotropic tensors

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
\{i_{j}\}_{j=1}^{2k}\subset\{1,2,\dots,d\}
. We define
\mathcal{S}_{k}
to be the space of all pairwise combinations of the elements
\{i_{j}\}
and let
n\in S^{d-1}
for
d=2,3
and
dS(n)
be the surface measure of the unit sphere
S^{d-1}
. If
\sigma(p)
is the
p
th particular set of pairings of
\{i_{j}\}
(i.e.
\sigma(p)\in\mathcal{S}_{k}
, with
1\leq p\leq|\mathcal{S}_{k}|
), then the following identity holds:

\fint_{S^{d-1}}\prod_{j=1}^{2k}n_{i_{j}}\;dS(n)=\beta_{d}(k)\sum_{j=1}^{|\mathcal{S}_{k}|}\prod_{(a,b)\in\sigma(j)}\delta_{a,b}

(21)

where

\beta_{d}(k)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2^{k}k!}&d=2\\
\frac{2^{k}k!}{(2k+1)!}&d=3\end{cases}

Proof.

Due to the odd symmetry of each of the components
n_{i}
over the unit sphere
S^{d-1}
, the integral

\fint_{S^{d-1}}\prod_{j=1}^{2k}n_{i_{j}}\;dS(n)>0

if and only if there is an even number of each of the components. Moreover from trigonometric identities, one can deduce that it is equivalent to a linear combination of kronecker delta tensors symbolizing all of the possible pairings of the elements in
\{i_{j}\}
. Moreover as the ordering of the
\{i_{j}\}
are interchangeable they must all occur with the same factor. Therefore,

\fint_{S^{d-1}}\prod_{j=1}^{2k}n_{i_{j}}\;dS(n)=\beta_{d}(k)\sum_{j=1}^{|\mathcal{S}_{k}|}\prod_{(a,b)\in\sigma(j)}\delta_{a,b}

where
\mathcal{S}_{k}
is the collection of all pairwise combinations of
\{i_{j}\}
and
\sigma(p)\in\mathcal{S}_{k}
is the
p
th collection of pairings. For example when
k=2
we have

\mathcal{S}_{2}=\big\{\underbrace{\{(i_{1},i_{2}),(i_{3},i_{4})\}}_{\sigma(1)},\underbrace{\{(i_{1},i_{3}),(i_{2},i_{4})\}}_{\sigma(2)},\underbrace{\{(i_{1},i_{4}),(i_{2},i_{3})\}}_{\sigma(3)}\big\}

It is a well known combinatorial result that the number of pairings is
|\mathcal{S}_{k}|=\frac{(2k)!}{2^{k}k!}
.

Now all that is left is to compute the constant
\beta_{d}(k).
As all possible pairings have the same factor we consider the case with
i_{1}=i_{2}=\dots=i_{2k}
. As none of the terms on the left hand side vanish and we get

|\mathcal{S}_{k}|\beta_{d}(k)=\fint_{S^{d-1}}n_{i_{1}}^{2k}\;dS(n).

The right hand side depends on the dimension so we consider each case separately. When
d=2
we use the reduction formula to deduce:

\fint_{S^{1}}(n_{i_{1}})^{2k}\;dS(n)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\cos^{2k}\theta\;d\theta=\frac{(2k-1)!!}{(2k)!!}=\frac{(2k-1)!}{(2k-2)!!(2k)!!}=\frac{2(2k-1)!}{4^{k}(k-1)!k!}

where we have used the identity
(2k)!!=\prod_{j=1}^{k}2j=2^{k}k!
. Thus

\beta_{2}(k)=\frac{2(2k-1)!}{4^{k}(k-1)!k!}\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}_{k}|}=\frac{1}{2^{k}k!}

Whereas when
d=3
:

\fint_{S^{2}}(n_{i_{1}})^{2k}\;dS(n)=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\cos^{2k}\phi\sin\phi\;d\phi d\theta=\frac{1}{2k+1}

so

\beta_{3}(k)=\frac{1}{2k+1}\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}_{k}|}=\frac{2^{k}k!}{(2k+1)!}

∎

Two direct consequences of Lemma
2.9
are the following lemmas:

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
r\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
p\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\}
, and
n,k\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
such that
|n|=1
. Then

\Re\Big(\fint_{S^{d-1}}(n\cdot k)^{2p}e^{-ir(n\cdot k)}\;dS(n)\Big)=|k|^{2p}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\beta_{d}(m+p)\frac{(-1)^{m}(r|k|)^{2m}(2m+2p)!}{2^{m+p}(m+p)!(2m)!}

Proof.

We use the power series expansion of
e^{x}
and the fact that it is an entire function to consider the integration term-wise:

\displaystyle\Re\Big(\fint_{S^{d-1}}(n\cdot k)^{2p}e^{-ir(n\cdot k)}\;dS(n)\Big)

\displaystyle=\Re\Big(\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-ir)^{m}}{m!}\fint_{S^{d-1}}(n\cdot k)^{m+2p}\;dS(n)\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{m}r^{2m}}{(2m)!}\fint_{S^{d-1}}\prod_{j=1}^{2(m+p)}k_{i_{j}}n_{i_{j}}\;dS(n)

where
k_{i_{j}}n_{i_{j}}
is the
j
th copy of the inner product
k_{i}n_{i}=k\cdot n
. Then it follows from Lemma
2.9
that

\displaystyle\fint_{S^{d-1}}\prod_{j=1}^{2(m+p)}k_{i_{j}}n_{i_{j}}\;dS(n)

\displaystyle=\beta_{d}(m+p)\prod_{j=1}^{2(m+p)}k_{i_{j}}\sum_{q=1}^{|\mathcal{S}_{m+p}|}\prod_{(a,b)\in\sigma(q)}\delta_{ab}

\displaystyle=\beta_{d}(m+p)\sum_{q=1}^{|\mathcal{S}_{m+p}|}|k|^{2m+2p}

\displaystyle=\beta_{d}(m+p)|\mathcal{S}_{m+p}||k|^{2m+2p}

\displaystyle=\beta_{d}(m+p)\frac{(2m+2p)!}{2^{m+p}(m+p)!}|k|^{2m+2p}

Now we substitute this back into the sum to conclude the proof.
∎

Lemma 2.11
.

Let
u
be a divergence free random vector field and let
p\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\}
and
r\in{\mathbb{R}}
, then

\Re\Big(\fint_{S^{d-1}}n_{a}n_{b}(n\cdot k)^{2p}e^{-irn\cdot k}\;dS(n)\mathbf{E}\widehat{u_{a}}(k)\widehat{u_{b}}(k)\Big)=|k|^{2p}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\beta_{d}(m+p+1)\frac{(-1)^{m}(r|k|)^{2m}(2m+2p)!}{2^{m+p}(m+p)!(2m)!}\mathbf{E}|\widehat{u}|^{2}

Proof.

Similar to the proof of Lemma
2.10
, we use the power series expansion of
e^{x}
and the fact that it is an entire function to consider the integration term-wise:

\displaystyle\Re\Big(\fint_{S^{d-1}}n_{a}n_{b}(n\cdot k)^{2p}e^{-irn\cdot k}\;dS(n)\mathbf{E}\widehat{u_{a}}\widehat{u_{b}}\Big)

\displaystyle=\Re\Big(\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-ir)^{m}}{m!}\fint_{S^{d-1}}n_{a}n_{b}(n\cdot k)^{m+2p}\;dS(n)\mathbf{E}\widehat{u_{a}}\widehat{u_{b}}\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{m}r^{2m}}{(2m)!}\fint_{S^{d-1}}n_{a}n_{b}\prod_{j=1}^{2(m+p)}k_{i_{j}}n_{i_{j}}\;dS(n)\mathbf{E}\widehat{u_{a}}\widehat{u_{b}}

Note that on the Fourier side, the divergence free condition can be written as
k_{j}\widehat{u_{j}}=0
. Now, as there are
2n+2p+2

n_{j}
when we apply Lemma
2.9
we get

\displaystyle\fint_{S^{d-1}}n_{a}n_{b}\prod_{j=1}^{2(m+p)}k_{i_{j}}n_{i_{j}}\;dS(n)\mathbf{E}\widehat{u_{a}}\widehat{u_{b}}

\displaystyle=\beta_{d}(m+p+1)\prod_{j=1}^{2(m+p)}k_{i_{j}}\Big(\sum_{q=1}^{|\mathcal{S}_{m+p}|}\delta_{ab}\prod_{(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\in\sigma(q)}\delta_{a^{\prime}b^{\prime}}\mathbf{E}\widehat{u_{a}}\widehat{u_{b}}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{q=1}^{|\mathcal{S}_{m+p}|}\sum_{(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\in\sigma(q)}(\delta_{ab^{\prime}}\delta_{a^{\prime}b}+\delta_{a^{\prime}a}\delta_{b^{\prime}b})\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}(c^{\prime},d^{\prime})\in\sigma(q)\\
(c^{\prime},d^{\prime})\neq(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\end{subarray}}\delta_{c^{\prime}d^{\prime}}\mathbf{E}\widehat{u_{a}}\widehat{u_{b}}\Big)

\displaystyle=\beta_{d}(m+p+1)\sum_{q=1}^{|\mathcal{S}_{m+p}|}|k|^{2m+2p}\mathbf{E}|\widehat{u}|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+2\beta_{d}(m+p+1)\sum_{q=1}^{|\mathcal{S}_{m+p}|}|k|^{2m+2p-2}\mathbf{E}k_{a}\widehat{u}_{a}k_{b}\widehat{u}_{b}

\displaystyle=\beta_{d}(m+p+1)|\mathcal{S}_{m+p}||k|^{2m+2p}\mathbf{E}|\widehat{u}|^{2}

\displaystyle=\beta_{d}(m+p+1)\frac{(2m+2p)!}{2^{m+p}(m+p)!}|k|^{2m+2p}\mathbf{E}|\widehat{u}|^{2}

Substituting this back into the sum concludes the proof.
∎

2.3.2
Filtration Limits

In this section, we will present the two main lemmas which will provide the characterization of the direct and inverse cascades. First we consider the case when small scale limit i.e. when
\ell\to 0
and characterize the existence of a cascade as when there is a sequence of wave-numbers for the energy to “escape out to infinity”.

Lemma 2.12

(Small Scale Limit)
.

Let
c(\ell,k)
be a bounded sequence of real numbers such that
\displaystyle\lim_{\ell|k|\to 0}c(\ell,k)=0
and
\displaystyle\lim_{\ell|k|\to\infty}c(\ell,k)=L
with
L\neq 0
. Let
f^{\nu}
is a sequence of
L^{2}
random variables such that

\limsup_{\nu\to 0}\mathbf{E}\|f^{\nu}\|_{\lambda}^{2}<\infty

Then there exists
\ell_{\nu}\to 0
such that

\lim_{\ell_{I}\to 0}\liminf_{\nu\to 0}\inf_{\ell\in(\ell_{\nu},\ell_{I})}\sum_{k}c(\ell,k)\mathbf{E}|\widehat{f^{\nu}}(k)|^{2}=L\delta

if and only if there exists
N_{\nu}\to\infty
such that

\liminf_{\nu\to 0}\sum_{|k|\geq N_{\nu}}\mathbf{E}|\widehat{f^{\nu}}|^{2}=\delta

Proof.

(\Rightarrow)
Suppose there exists
\ell_{\nu}\to 0
such that

\lim_{\ell_{I}\to 0}\liminf_{\nu\to 0}\inf_{\ell\in(\ell_{\nu},\ell_{I})}\sum_{k}c(\ell,k)\mathbf{E}|\widehat{f^{\nu}}(k)|^{2}=L\delta

Pick
N_{\nu}\to\infty
such that
\displaystyle\lim_{\nu\to 0}\ell_{\nu}N_{\nu}=\infty
and consider the sums

\sum_{k}c(\ell,k)\mathbf{E}|\widehat{f^{\nu}}(k)|^{2}=\sum_{|k|<N_{\nu}}c(\ell,k)\mathbf{E}|\widehat{f^{\nu}}(k)|^{2}+\sum_{|k|\geq N_{\nu}}c(\ell,k)\mathbf{E}|\widehat{f^{\nu}}(k)|^{2}=:I_{1}+I_{2}

First consider
I_{2}
and observe

\inf_{|k|\geq N_{\nu}}c(\ell,k)\sum_{|k|\geq N_{\nu}}\mathbf{E}|\widehat{f^{\nu}}(k)|^{2}\leq I_{2}\leq\sup_{|k|\geq N_{\nu}}\sum_{|k|\geq N_{\nu}}c(\ell,k)\mathbf{E}|\widehat{f^{\nu}}(k)|^{2}

Apply the inf in
\ell
to each side of the inequality:

\inf_{\ell|k|\geq\ell_{\nu}N_{\nu}}c(\ell,k)\sum_{|k|\geq N_{\nu}}\mathbf{E}|\widehat{f^{\nu}}(k)|^{2}\leq\inf_{\ell\in(\ell_{\nu},\ell_{I})}I_{2}\leq\sup_{\ell|k|\geq\ell_{\nu}N_{\nu}}c(\ell,k)\sum_{|k|\geq N_{\nu}}\mathbf{E}|\widehat{f^{\nu}}(k)|^{2}

Next we take the limits in
\nu
and
\ell_{I}
. Note that by our choice of
N_{\nu}
and the fact that
\displaystyle\lim_{\ell|k|\to\infty}c(\ell,k)=L
it follows that

\displaystyle\lim_{\ell_{I}\to 0}\liminf_{\nu\to 0}\inf_{\ell|k|\geq\ell_{\nu}N_{\nu}}c(\ell,k)\sum_{|k|\geq N_{\nu}}\mathbf{E}|\widehat{f^{\nu}}(k)|^{2}

\displaystyle=L\liminf_{\nu\to 0}\sum_{|k|\geq N_{\nu}}\mathbf{E}|\widehat{f^{\nu}}(k)|^{2}

\displaystyle\lim_{\ell_{I}\to 0}\liminf_{\nu\to 0}\sup_{\ell|k|\geq\ell_{\nu}N_{\nu}}c(\ell,k)\sum_{|k|\geq N_{\nu}}\mathbf{E}|\widehat{f^{\nu}}(k)|^{2}

\displaystyle=L\liminf_{\nu\to 0}\sum_{|k|\geq N_{\nu}}\mathbf{E}|\widehat{f^{\nu}}(k)|^{2}

Hence by the Squeeze theorem

\lim_{\ell_{I}\to 0}\liminf_{\nu\to 0}\inf_{\ell|k|\geq\ell_{\nu}N_{\nu}}I_{2}=L\liminf_{\nu\to 0}\sum_{|k|\geq N_{\nu}}\mathbf{E}|\widehat{f^{\nu}}(k)|^{2}

Next we claim that

\lim_{\ell_{I}\to 0}\liminf_{\nu\to 0}\inf_{\ell\in(\ell_{\nu},\ell_{I})}|I_{1}|=0

For the sake of contradiction suppose that

\lim_{\ell_{I}\to 0}\liminf_{\nu\to 0}\inf_{\ell\in(\ell_{\nu},\ell_{I})}|I_{1}|>0

Then there exists
|k|<\infty
and
\varepsilon_{0}
such that for all
\nu>0,\ell_{I}>0
there exists
\nu^{*}<\nu
and
\ell_{I}^{*}<\ell_{I}

\varepsilon_{0}<\inf_{\ell\in(0,\ell_{I}^{*})}|c(\ell,k)|\mathbf{E}|\widehat{f^{\nu^{*}}}(k)|^{2}\leq C\sup_{\ell\in 0,\ell_{I})}|c(\ell,k)|

However, as
\displaystyle\lim_{\ell|k|\to 0}c(\ell,k)=0
, we can find
\ell_{I}
small enough such that

\sup_{\ell\in(0,\ell_{I})}|c(\ell,k)|<\varepsilon_{0}/C

a contradiction. Thus
|I_{1}|\to 0
as
\ell_{I}\to 0
and
\nu\to 0
.
Putting it all together now we get

\displaystyle\liminf_{\nu\to 0}\sum_{|k|\geq N_{\nu}}\mathbf{E}|\widehat{f^{\nu}}(k)|^{2}=\frac{1}{L}\lim_{\ell_{I}\to 0}\liminf_{\nu\to 0}\inf_{\ell\in(\ell_{\nu},\ell_{I})}\sum_{k}c(\ell,k)\mathbf{E}|\widehat{f^{\nu}}(k)|^{2}=\delta

(
\Leftarrow
) Now we assume that there exists
N_{\nu}\to\infty
such that

\liminf_{\nu\to 0}\sum_{|k|\geq N_{\nu}}\mathbf{E}|\widehat{f^{\nu}}|^{2}=\delta

Pick
\ell_{\nu}\to 0
such that
\displaystyle\lim_{\nu\to 0}\ell_{\nu}N_{\nu}=\infty
. Repeat the steps above to conclude that

\displaystyle\lim_{\ell_{I}\to 0}\liminf_{\nu\to 0}\inf_{\ell\in(\ell_{\nu},\ell_{I})}I_{1}=0

\displaystyle\lim_{\ell_{I}\to 0}\liminf_{\nu\to 0}\inf_{\ell\in(\ell_{\nu},\ell_{I})}I_{2}=L\delta

∎

Notice that the small scale limit filters out everything except what happens to the random variables at infinite wave-numbers. As such we will sometimes refer to Lemma
2.12
as the high-frequency filtration limit. Similarly we can characterize the large scale limit as filtering out everything except what happens at low frequencies.

Lemma 2.13

(Large Scale Limit)
.

Let
c(\ell,k)
be a sequence of real numbers such that
\displaystyle\lim_{\ell|k|\to 0}c(\ell,k)=0
and
\displaystyle\lim_{\ell|k|\to\infty}c(\ell,k)=L
with
L\neq 0
. Let
f^{\nu}
is a sequence of
L^{2}
random variables such that

\limsup_{\nu\to 0}\mathbf{E}\|f^{\nu}\|_{\lambda}^{2}=\Delta<\infty.

Then there exists
\tilde{\ell}_{\nu}\to\infty
such that

\lim_{\ell_{I}\to\infty}\limsup_{\nu\to 0}\sup_{\ell\in(\ell_{I},\tilde{\ell}_{\nu})}\sum_{k}c(\ell,k)\mathbf{E}|\widehat{f^{\nu}}(k)|^{2}=L(\Delta-\delta)

if and only if there exists
M_{\nu}\to 0
such that

\liminf_{\nu\to 0}\sum_{|k|\leq M_{\nu}}\mathbf{E}|\widehat{f^{\nu}}|^{2}=\delta

Proof.

The proof is very similar to the proof of Lemma
2.12
.

(
\Rightarrow
) Suppose there exists
\tilde{\ell}_{\nu}
such that

\lim_{\ell_{I}\to\infty}\limsup_{\nu\to 0}\sup_{\ell\in(\ell_{I},\tilde{\ell}_{\nu})}\sum_{k}c(\ell,k)\mathbf{E}|\widehat{f^{\nu}}(k)|^{2}=L(\Delta-\delta)

Pick
M_{\nu}\to 0
such that
M_{\nu}=O(\tilde{\ell}_{\nu}^{-1})
. By assumption
c(\ell,0)=0
for all
\ell
.
Hence

\displaystyle\sum_{k}c(\ell,k)\mathbf{E}|\widehat{f^{\nu}}(k)|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{|k|\geq 1}c(\ell,k)\mathbf{E}|\widehat{f^{\nu}}(k)|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq|k|\leq M_{\nu}}c(\ell,k)\mathbf{E}|\widehat{f^{\nu}}(k)|^{2}+\sum_{|k|\geq\max\{M_{\nu},1\}}c(\ell,k)\mathbf{E}|\widehat{f^{\nu}}(k)|^{2}

\displaystyle=:I_{1}+I_{2}

As the sequence
c(\ell,k)
is convergent, it is also bounded uniformly in
\ell
and
k
. Moreover, from our choice of
M_{\nu}
it follows that

\lim_{\ell_{I}\to\infty}\limsup_{\nu\to 0}\sup_{\ell\in(\ell_{I},\tilde{\ell}_{\nu})}|I_{1}|\leq C\limsup_{\nu\to 0}\sum_{1\leq|k|\leq M_{\nu}}\mathbf{E}|\widehat{f^{\nu}}|^{2}=0

Next consider the term
I_{2}
. Define
M_{\nu}^{*}=\max\{M_{\nu},1\}
then

\inf_{|k|\geq M_{\nu}^{*}}c(\ell,k)\sum_{|k|\geq M_{\nu}^{*}}\mathbf{E}|\widehat{f^{\nu}}|^{2}\leq I_{2}\leq\sup_{|k|\geq M_{\nu}^{*}}c(\ell,k)\sum_{|k|\geq M_{\nu}^{*}}\mathbf{E}|\widehat{f^{\nu}}|^{2}

Apply the sup in
\ell
to each side of the inequality:

\inf_{|k|\ell\geq\ell_{I}M_{\nu}^{*}}c(\ell,k)\sum_{|k|\geq M_{\nu}^{*}}\mathbf{E}|\widehat{f^{\nu}}|^{2}\leq\sup_{\ell\in(\ell_{I},\tilde{\ell}_{I})}I_{2}\leq\sup_{|k|\ell\geq\ell_{I}M_{\nu}^{*}}c(\ell,k)\sum_{|k|\geq M_{\nu}^{*}}\mathbf{E}|\widehat{f^{\nu}}|^{2}

Finally take the limit in
\nu
and
\ell_{I}
and use the fact that
\displaystyle\lim_{\ell|k|\to\infty}c(\ell,k)=L
:

\displaystyle\lim_{\ell_{I}\to\infty}\limsup_{\nu\to 0}\inf_{\ell|k|\geq\ell_{I}M_{\nu}^{*}}c(\ell,k)\sum_{|k|\geq M_{\nu}^{*}}\mathbf{E}|\widehat{f^{\nu}}(k)|^{2}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\ell_{I}\to\infty}\inf_{\ell|k|\geq\ell_{I}}c(\ell,k)\limsup_{\nu\to 0}\sum_{|k|\geq M_{\nu}^{*}}\mathbf{E}|\widehat{f^{\nu}}(k)|^{2}

\displaystyle=L\limsup_{\nu\to 0}\sum_{|k|\geq M_{\nu}^{*}}\mathbf{E}|\widehat{f^{\nu}}(k)|^{2}

\displaystyle\lim_{\ell_{I}\to\infty}\limsup_{\nu\to 0}\sup_{\ell|k|\geq\ell_{I}M_{\nu}^{*}}c(\ell,k)\sum_{|k|\geq M_{\nu}^{*}}\mathbf{E}|\widehat{f^{\nu}}(k)|^{2}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\ell_{I}\to\infty}\sup_{\ell|k|\geq\ell_{I}}c(\ell,k)\limsup_{\nu\to 0}\sum_{|k|\geq M_{\nu}^{*}}\mathbf{E}|\widehat{f^{\nu}}(k)|^{2}

\displaystyle=L\limsup_{\nu\to 0}\sum_{|k|\geq M_{\nu}^{*}}\mathbf{E}|\widehat{f^{\nu}}(k)|^{2}

Hence by the Squeeze theorem

\lim_{\ell_{I}\to\infty}\limsup_{\nu\to 0}\sup_{\ell\in(\ell_{I},\tilde{\ell}_{\nu}}I_{2}=L\limsup_{\nu\to 0}\sum_{|k|\geq M_{\nu}^{*}}\mathbf{E}|\widehat{f^{\nu}}(k)|^{2}

Putting it all together results in

\displaystyle\liminf_{\nu\to 0}\sum_{|k|\leq M_{\nu}}\mathbf{E}|\widehat{f^{\nu}}(k)|^{2}

\displaystyle=\Delta-\limsup_{\nu\to 0}\sum_{|k|\geq M_{\nu}^{*}}\mathbf{E}|\widehat{f^{\nu}}(k)|^{2}

\displaystyle=\Delta-\frac{1}{L}\lim_{\ell_{I}\to\infty}\limsup_{\nu\to 0}\sup_{\ell\in(\ell_{I},\tilde{\ell}_{\nu}}\sum_{k}c(\ell,k)\mathbf{E}|\widehat{f^{\nu}}(k)|^{2}

\displaystyle=\delta

(
\Leftarrow
) Now assume that there exists
M_{\nu}\to 0
such that

\delta=\liminf_{\nu\to 0}\sum_{|k|\leq M_{\nu}}\mathbf{E}|\widehat{f^{\nu}}|^{2}=\Delta-\limsup_{\nu\to 0}\sum_{|k|\geq\max\{M_{\nu},1\}}\mathbf{E}|\widehat{f^{\nu}}|^{2}

Simplifying, we have
\displaystyle\limsup_{\nu\to 0}\sum_{|k|\geq\max\{M_{\nu},1\}}\mathbf{E}|\widehat{f^{\nu}}|^{2}=\Delta-\delta
.

Choose
\tilde{\ell}_{\nu}=O(M_{\nu}^{-1})
and repeat the steps above to conclude that

\displaystyle\lim_{\ell_{I}\to 0}\limsup_{\nu\to 0}\sup_{\ell\in(\ell_{\nu},\ell_{I})}I_{1}=0

\displaystyle\lim_{\ell_{I}\to 0}\limsup_{\nu\to 0}\sup_{\ell\in(\ell_{\nu},\ell_{I})}I_{2}=L(\Delta-\delta)

∎

The 3D Direct Cascade

In this section we establish the characterization of the direct cascade flux laws in 3D.

Theorem 3.1

(3D Direct Cascade Characterization)
.

Suppose
\{u\}_{\nu>0}
is a sequence of statistically stationary solutions.
There exists
N_{\nu}\geq 1
such that
\displaystyle\lim_{\nu\to 0}N_{\nu}=\infty
and

\liminf_{\nu\to 0}\Big(\nu\sum_{|k|\geq N_{\nu}}\mathbf{E}|\widehat{\nabla u}|^{2}+\mathbf{E}D(u^{\nu})\Big)=\varepsilon^{*}

if and only if there exists
\ell_{\nu}\in(0,1)
such that
\displaystyle\lim_{\nu\to 0}\ell_{\nu}=0
and

\displaystyle\lim_{\ell_{I}\to 0}\limsup_{\nu\to 0}\sup_{\ell\in[\ell_{\nu},\ell_{I}]}\Big|\frac{S_{vel}(\ell)}{\ell^{3}}+\frac{4}{3}\varepsilon^{*}\Big|=0

(22)

\displaystyle\lim_{\ell_{I}\to 0}\limsup_{\nu\to 0}\sup_{\ell\in[\ell_{\nu},\ell_{I}]}\Big|\frac{S_{vel}^{\parallel}(\ell)}{\ell}+\frac{4}{5}\varepsilon^{*}\Big|=0

(23)

3.1
Proof of (
22
)

First, recall the KHM equation for
S_{vel}
in three dimensions:

\frac{S_{vel}}{\ell}=-\frac{4\nu}{\ell}\Gamma_{vel}^{\prime}(\ell)-\frac{4}{\ell^{3}}\int_{0}^{\ell}r^{2}a_{vel}(r)\;dr

Step 1:
Beginning with the forcing term, we show that

\frac{-4}{\ell^{3}}\int_{0}^{\ell}r^{2}a_{vel}(r)\;dr=-\frac{4}{3}\varepsilon+o_{\ell\to 0}(1)

Note that

a_{vel}(0)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j}\mathbf{E}\fint_{{\mathbb{T}}_{\lambda}^{3}}|f_{j}(x)|^{2}\;dx=\varepsilon.

Furthermore, as the
f_{j}
are smooth, we Taylor expand around
r=0
using the Peano formulation of the error (as we do not need a rigorous analysis of the error in this case).

\displaystyle\frac{-4}{\ell^{3}}\int_{0}^{\ell}r^{2}a_{vel}(r)\;dr

\displaystyle=\frac{-2}{\ell^{3}}\int_{0}^{\ell}r^{2}\mathbf{E}\fint_{S^{2}}\sum_{j}\fint_{{\mathbb{T}}_{\lambda}^{3}}f_{j}\cdot T_{rn}f_{j}\;dxdS(n)dr

\displaystyle=\frac{-2}{\ell^{3}}\int_{0}^{\ell}r^{2}\mathbf{E}\fint_{S^{2}}\sum_{j}\fint_{{\mathbb{T}}_{\lambda}^{3}}|f_{j}|^{2}\;dxdS(n)+r^{2}h_{0}(r)dr

\displaystyle=\frac{-2}{3}\sum_{j}\fint_{{\mathbb{T}}_{\lambda}^{3}}\mathbf{E}|f_{j}|^{2}\;dx+\frac{-2}{\ell^{3}}\int_{0}^{\ell}r^{2}h_{0}(r)\;dr

\displaystyle=\frac{-4}{3}\varepsilon+\frac{-2}{\ell^{3}}\int_{0}^{\ell}r^{2}h_{0}(r)\;dr

where
h_{0}(r)
is a function such that
\displaystyle\lim_{r\to 0}h_{0}(r)=0
. Hence the error disappears as
\ell\to 0
or in other words
\frac{-2}{\ell^{3}}\int_{0}^{\ell}r^{2}h_{0}(r)\;dr=o_{\ell\to 0}(1)
.

Step 2:
Next we show that

\frac{-4\nu}{\ell}\Gamma_{vel}^{\prime}(\ell)=\frac{4}{3}\varepsilon-\frac{4}{3}\mathbf{E}D(u^{\nu})-\sum_{k}c(k,\ell)\mathbf{E}\fint_{0}^{T}|\widehat{\nabla u^{\nu}}|^{2}

where
\displaystyle\lim_{\ell|k|\to 0}c(\ell,k)=0
and
\displaystyle\lim_{\ell|k|\to\infty}c(\ell,k)=\frac{4}{3}
.

From Proposition
2.7
it is known that
\Gamma_{vel}\in C^{2}
, so if we Taylor expand
\Gamma_{vel}^{\prime}
about 0 and use the Lagrange formulation of the error to get:

\frac{-4\nu}{\ell}\Gamma_{vel}^{\prime}(\ell)=\frac{-4\nu}{\ell}\Gamma_{vel}^{\prime}(0)+\frac{-4\nu}{\ell}\int_{0}^{\ell}\Gamma_{vel}^{\prime\prime}(r)\;dr

By integration by parts

\displaystyle\Gamma_{vel}^{\prime}(0)

\displaystyle=-\mathbf{E}\fint_{S^{2}}\fint_{{\mathbb{T}}_{\lambda}^{2}}n_{i}u_{m}^{\nu}\partial_{i}u_{m}^{\nu}\;dxdS(n)=0

Next, consider the error term, which we will note is real valued. We use Plancheral’s theorem to rewrite the
L^{2}
inner product in
x
into a Fourier series. Then we apply Fubini’s theorem and Lemma
2.10
when
p=1
to get

\displaystyle\frac{-4\nu}{\ell}\int_{0}^{\ell}\Gamma_{vel}^{\prime\prime}(r)\;dr

\displaystyle=\frac{4\nu}{\ell}\int_{0}^{\ell}\mathbf{E}\fint_{S^{2}}\fint_{{\mathbb{T}}_{\lambda}^{3}}n_{i}n_{j}\partial_{i}u_{m}^{\nu}\partial_{j}T_{rn}u_{m}^{\nu}\;dxdS(n)dr

\displaystyle=\frac{-4\nu}{\ell}\sum_{k}\int_{0}^{\ell}\fint_{S^{2}}(n\cdot k)^{2}e^{-irn\cdot k}\mathbf{E}|\widehat{u^{\nu}}(k)|^{2}\;dS(n)dr

\displaystyle=\frac{-4\nu}{\ell}\sum_{k}\int_{0}^{\ell}|k|^{2}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{m}(r|k|)^{2m}}{(2m)!(2m+3)}\;dr\mathbf{E}|\widehat{u^{\nu}}(k)|^{2}

\displaystyle=4\nu\sum_{k}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{m}(\ell|k|)^{2m}}{(2m+1)!(2m+3)}\mathbf{E}\fint_{0}^{T}|\widehat{\nabla u^{\nu}}(k)|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{4\nu}{3}\sum_{k}\mathbf{E}\fint_{0}^{T}|\widehat{\nabla u^{\nu}}|^{2}-4\nu\sum_{k}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{m+1}(\ell|k|)^{2m}}{(2m+1)!(2m+3)}\mathbf{E}\fint_{0}^{T}|\widehat{\nabla u^{\nu}}(k)|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{3}\varepsilon-\frac{4}{3}\mathbf{E}D(u^{\nu})-\sum_{k}c(\ell,k)\mathbf{E}\fint_{0}^{T}|\widehat{\nabla u^{\nu}}|^{2}

Notice that when applying Lemma
2.10
we substituted in for the value of
\beta_{3}(m+1)
and simplified the coefficient in the power series in
m
. Also the ultimate equality comes from the energy balance (
16
) to write the first term as
\frac{4}{3}\varepsilon.

From the definition of
c(\ell,k)
it is readily seen that
\displaystyle\lim_{\ell|k|\to 0}c(\ell,k)=0
.

\displaystyle\Big|\frac{4}{3}-c(\ell,k)\Big|

\displaystyle=\Big|4\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{m}(\ell|k|)^{2m}}{(2m+1)!(2m+3)}\Big|

.

Step 3:

).

5
The Inverse Cascade

In this section, we characterize the existence of an inverse cascade in both 2D and 3D.

Theorem 5.1

(Characterization of the Inverse Cascade)
.

Suppose that
\lambda=\lambda(\nu)<\infty
is a continuous monotone increasing function such that
\displaystyle\lim_{\nu\to 0}\lambda=\infty
. Let
\{u\}_{\nu>0}
be a sequence of statistically stationary solutions to the Navier-Stokes equations (
1
) on
{\mathbb{T}}_{\lambda}^{d}
with mean-zero, divergent free forcing correlations
f_{j}
. Then there exists a decreasing sequence
M_{\nu}
such that
\displaystyle\lim_{\nu\to 0}M_{\nu}=0
and

\liminf_{\nu\to 0}\nu\sum_{|k|\leq M_{\nu}}\mathbf{E}|\widehat{\nabla u^{\nu}}(k)|^{2}=\varepsilon^{*}

if and only if there exists
\tilde{\ell}_{\nu}\in(1,\lambda)
such that
\displaystyle\lim_{\nu\to 0}\tilde{\ell}_{\nu}=\infty
and

\displaystyle\lim_{\ell_{I}\to\infty}\limsup_{\nu\to 0}\sup_{\ell\in[\ell_{I},\tilde{\ell}_{\nu}]}\Big|\frac{S_{vel}(\ell)}{\ell}-4\beta_{d}(1)\varepsilon^{*}\Big|=0

(27)

\displaystyle\lim_{\ell_{I}\to\infty}\limsup_{\nu\to 0}\sup_{\ell\in[\ell_{I},\tilde{\ell}_{\nu}]}\Big|\frac{S_{vel}^{\parallel}(\ell)}{\ell}-\big(4\beta_{d}(2)+\frac{8\beta_{d}(1)}{d+2}\big)\varepsilon^{*}\Big|=0

(28)

Explicitly the coefficients can be computed as

4\beta_{d}(1)=\begin{cases}2&d=2\\
4/3&d=3\end{cases}\quad 4\beta_{d}(2)+\frac{8\beta_{d}(1)}{d+2}=\begin{cases}3/2&d=2\\
4/5&d=3\end{cases}

The proof of Theorem
