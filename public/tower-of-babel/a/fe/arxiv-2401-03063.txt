Variance Bounds: Some Old and Some New
Clément Deslandes, Christian Houdré

Abstract
For functions of independent random variables, various upper and lower variance bounds are revisited in diverse settings. These are then specialized to the Bernoulli, Gaussian, infinitely divisible cases and to Banach space valued random variables. Frameworks and techniques vary from jackknives through semigroups and beyond. Some new applications are presented, in particular, recovering and improving upon all the known estimates on the variance of the length of the longest common subsequences of two random words.



1
Introduction and preliminary results

We revisit below various lower and upper bounds on the variance of functions of independent random variables.
Throughout and unless otherwise noted,
X_{1},\dots,X_{n},X^{\prime}_{1},\dots,X^{\prime}_{n}
are independent random variables such that for
all
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
,
X_{k}
and
X^{\prime}_{k}
are identically distributed, while
S:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is a
Borel function such that
\mathds{E}S(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})^{2}<+\infty
.
Next, and if
S
is short for
S(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
, for any
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, let
S^{k}:=S(X_{1},\dots,X_{k-1},X^{\prime}_{k},X_{k+1},\dots,X_{n})
and
more generally if
\alpha\subset\{1,\dots,n\}
, let
S^{\alpha}
be defined as
S(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
but with
X_{k}
replaced by
X^{\prime}_{k}
for all
k\in\alpha
, and so
S^{\emptyset}=S
, while
S^{\{1,\dots,n\}}
is an independent copy of
S
.
With the help of these preliminary notations, we next recall the definitions of various quantities which will play an important role in the sequel.

Following
[
2
]
, for
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, let

B_{k}:=\mathds{E}\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{i\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}S(S^{i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1}}-S^{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}}),

(1.1)

where
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
is the symmetric group of degree
n
and where for
k=1,S^{i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1}}=S
. As the following sum is telescopic:

\sum_{k=1}^{n}B_{k}=\mathds{E}\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{i\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}S(S-S^{i_{1},\dots,i_{n}})={\textrm{Var}}\,S.

One key fact motivating the definition of the
B_{k}
’s is that they can be rewritten as:

B_{k}=\mathds{E}\frac{1}{2n!}\sum_{i\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}(S-S^{i_{k}})(S^{i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1}}-S^{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}}).

(1.2)

Indeed, if
\alpha,\beta\subset\{1,\dots,n\}
,

\mathds{E}\left(S^{\alpha}S^{\beta}\right)=\mathds{E}\left(SS^{\alpha\Delta\beta}\right),

(1.3)

where as usual
\Delta
denotes the symmetric difference operator, so

.\mathds{E}(S-S^{i_{k}})(S^{i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1}}-S^{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}})=2\mathds{E}\left(SS^{i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1}}-SS^{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}}\right)

(1.4)

Next, for all
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, let
\Delta_{k}S:=S-S^{k}
, and for
k\neq{\ell}
, let
\Delta_{k,{\ell}}S:=\Delta_{k}(\Delta_{\ell}S)=S-S^{k}-S^{\ell}+S^{k,{\ell}}
(note the commutativity property:
\Delta_{k}(\Delta_{\ell}S)=\Delta_{\ell}(\Delta_{k}S)
). Iterating this process, let
\Delta_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}}S:=\Delta_{k}(\Delta_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1}}S)
. Using this notation, we have

B_{k}=\mathds{E}\frac{1}{2n!}\sum_{i\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}(\Delta_{i_{k}}S)(\Delta_{i_{k}}S)^{i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1}},

(1.5)

and so
B_{k}\geq 0
since if
U,U^{\prime}
and
V
are independent with
U
and
U^{\prime}
identically distributed, then for any function
F
such that
F(U,V)
is integrable,
\mathbb{E}\left(F(U,V)F(U^{\prime},V)\right)=\mathbb{E}\left(\mathbb{E}\left(F(U,V)|V\right)^{2}\right)\geq 0
. We are now ready to generalize the approach used to go from (
1.1
) to (
1.5
), leading to novel properties of the
B_{k}^{\prime}s
.

Lemma 1.1
.

Let
\alpha\neq\emptyset
and
\beta
be two disjoint subsets of
\{1,\dots,n\}
. Then,

\mathbb{E}\left(S(\Delta_{\alpha}S)^{\beta}\right)=\frac{1}{2^{|\alpha|}}\mathbb{E}\left(\Delta_{\alpha}S(\Delta_{\alpha}S)^{\beta}\right).

(1.6)

Proof.

First, if
\beta=\emptyset
, then the above identity reduces to (
1.4
). Next, by a straightforward induction on the cardinality
k:=|\alpha|
, note that
\Delta_{\alpha}S=\sum_{\alpha^{\prime}\subset\alpha}(-1)^{|\alpha^{\prime}|}S^{\alpha^{\prime}}
. Then, for any
\alpha^{\prime}\subset\alpha
,

(-1)^{|\alpha^{\prime}|}S^{\alpha^{\prime}}(\Delta_{\alpha}S)^{\beta}=\sum_{\alpha^{\prime\prime}\subset\alpha}(-1)^{|\alpha^{\prime}|+|\alpha^{\prime\prime}|}S^{\alpha^{\prime}}S^{\alpha^{\prime\prime}\cup\beta},

and so using (
1.3
) (
\alpha
and
\beta
are disjoint and
\alpha^{\prime}\subset\alpha
so
\alpha^{\prime}\Delta(\alpha\cup\beta)=(\alpha^{\prime}\Delta\alpha)\cup\beta
),

\mathds{E}\left((-1)^{|\alpha^{\prime}|}S^{\alpha^{\prime}}(\Delta_{\alpha}S)^{\beta}\right)=\mathds{E}\left(\sum_{\alpha^{\prime\prime}\subset\alpha}(-1)^{|\alpha^{\prime}|+|\alpha^{\prime\prime}|}SS^{(\alpha^{\prime}\Delta\alpha^{\prime\prime})\cup\beta}\right).

Since
\alpha^{\prime\prime}\mapsto\alpha^{\prime}\Delta\alpha^{\prime\prime}
is just a permutation of the subsets of
\alpha
and
(-1)^{\alpha^{\prime}\Delta\alpha^{\prime\prime}}=(-1)^{|\alpha^{\prime}|+|\alpha^{\prime\prime}|}
,

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left((-1)^{|\alpha^{\prime}|}S^{\alpha^{\prime}}(\Delta_{\alpha}S)^{\beta}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}\left(\sum_{\alpha^{\prime\prime}\subset\alpha}(-1)^{|\alpha^{\prime\prime}|}SS^{\alpha^{\prime\prime}\cup\beta}\right)=\mathbb{E}\left(S(\Delta_{\alpha}S)^{\beta}\right),

and so

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{|\alpha|}}\mathbb{E}\left(\Delta_{\alpha}S(\Delta_{\alpha}S)^{\beta}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{|\alpha|}}\sum_{\alpha^{\prime}\subset\alpha}\mathds{E}\left((-1)^{|\alpha^{\prime}|}S^{\alpha^{\prime}}(\Delta_{\alpha}S)^{\beta}\right)=\mathbb{E}\left(S(\Delta_{\alpha}S)^{\beta}\right).

∎

Let
T
be the forward shift operator, i.e., for
k\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
, let
TB_{k}:=B_{k+1}
and let
D
be the backward discrete derivative:
D:=Id-T
(so for
k\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
,
DB_{k}=B_{k}-B_{k+1}
), and denote by
D^{\ell}
(
\ell\geq 1
) its
\ell
-th iteration, with
D^{0}=Id
. It is known (see
[
2
]
) that the finite sequence
(B_{k})_{1\leq k\leq n}
is non-increasing. More can be said.

Theorem 1.2
.

For all
\ell\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}
and
k\in\{1,\dots,n-\ell\}
,

D^{\ell}B_{k}=\mathds{E}\frac{1}{2^{\ell+1}n!}\sum_{i\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}(\Delta_{i_{1},\dots,i_{\ell+1}}S)(\Delta_{i_{1},\dots,i_{\ell+1}}S)^{i_{\ell+2},\dots,i_{k+\ell}}.

(1.7)

In particular,
D^{\ell}B_{k}\geq 0
, i.e.,
(B_{k})_{1\leq k\leq n}
is completely monotone (recall that
D=Id-T
).

Proof.

With the previous lemma, it is enough to prove that for all
\ell\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}
and
k\in\{1,\dots,n-\ell\}
,

D^{\ell}B_{k}=\mathds{E}\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{i\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}S(\Delta_{i_{1},\dots,i_{\ell+1}}S)^{i_{\ell+2},\dots,i_{k+\ell}}.

(1.8)

This is done by induction on
\ell
. When
\ell=0
, (
1.8
) is just the very definition of
B_{k}
. Assume next that (
1.8
) holds for
\ell\in\{0,\dots,n-2\}
. Let
k\in\{1,\dots,n-(\ell+1)\}
. Then,

\displaystyle D^{\ell+1}B_{k}

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{i\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}S(\Delta_{i_{1},\dots,i_{\ell+1}}S)^{i_{\ell+2},\dots,i_{k+\ell}}-\mathds{E}\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{i\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}S(\Delta_{i_{1},\dots,i_{\ell+1}}S)^{i_{\ell+2},\dots,i_{k+1+\ell}}

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{i\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}S(\Delta_{i_{1},\dots,i_{\ell+1}}S)^{i_{\ell+3},\dots,i_{k+1+\ell}}-\mathds{E}\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{i\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}S(\Delta_{i_{1},\dots,i_{\ell+1}}S)^{i_{\ell+2},\dots,i_{k+1+\ell}}

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{i\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}S(\Delta_{i_{1},\dots,i_{\ell+2}}S)^{i_{\ell+3},\dots,i_{k+1+\ell}},

where in getting the second equality, the terms are reindexed.
∎

We wish now to study potential connections between the
B_{k}
’s and jackknives operators
J_{k}
and
K_{k}
previously studied in
[
4
]
. For
Y\in\sigma(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
, i.e., Y measurable with respect to the
\sigma
-field generated by
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
and
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, let
\mathds{E}^{(i)}Y:=\mathds{E}(Y|X_{1},\dots,X_{i-1},X_{i+1},\dots,X_{n})
(with the convention
\mathds{E}^{(0)}Y:=Y
) and more generally for any subset
\alpha
of
\{1,\dots,n\}
, let

\mathds{E}^{\alpha}Y:=\mathds{E}(Y|(X_{j})_{j\notin\alpha}),

with
\mathds{E}^{\emptyset}Y=Y
, and
\mathds{E}^{\{1,\dots,n\}}Y=\mathds{E}Y
. Next,
for
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, let

{\textrm{Var}}\,^{(i)}Y:=\mathds{E}^{(i)}Y^{2}-(\mathds{E}^{(i)}Y)^{2}

while iterating, for
i\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, and
k\geq 1
, let

{\textrm{Var}}\,^{(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})}Y:=\mathds{E}^{(i_{1})}({\textrm{Var}}\,^{(i_{2},\dots,i_{k})}Y)-{\textrm{Var}}\,^{(i_{2},\dots,i_{k})}(\mathds{E}^{(i_{1})}Y),

and so (using the commutation property of conditional expectations) for any
\alpha=\{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\}
,

{\textrm{Var}}\,^{\alpha}Y
is well defined.

For
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, let

J_{k}:=\mathds{E}\sum_{i_{1}\neq i_{2}\dots\neq i_{k}}{\textrm{Var}}\,^{(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})}S,

(1.9)

and

K_{k}:=\mathds{E}\sum_{i_{1}\neq i_{2}\dots\neq i_{k}}{\textrm{Var}}\,^{(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})}\mathds{E}^{\overline{(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})}}S,

(1.10)

where
\overline{(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})}=(i_{k+1},\dots,i_{n})
.
For ease of notation, set also
J^{\prime}_{k}:=J_{k}/k!
and
K^{\prime}_{k}:=K_{k}/k!
. The next lemma provides relationships between these quantities and the
B_{k}
’s, it allows to easily get, and in a unified fashion, many of the known expressions involving the variance, along with some new ones.

Lemma 1.3
.

Let
\alpha\neq\emptyset
and
\beta
be two disjoint subsets of
\{1,\dots,n\}
. Then

\mathds{E}\left({\textrm{Var}}\,^{\alpha}\mathds{E}^{\beta}S\right)=\mathds{E}\left(S(\Delta_{\alpha}S)^{\beta}\right).

(1.11)

Proof.

If
\beta=\emptyset
, then the result is clear. Next, the proof is done by induction on the cardinality of
\alpha
. For the base case, let
|\alpha|=1
, i.e., let
\alpha=\{i\}
, for some
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left({\textrm{Var}}\,^{(i)}\mathds{E}^{\beta}S\right)

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}\left(\mathds{E}^{(i)}(\mathds{E}^{\beta}S)^{2}-(\mathds{E}^{(i)}\mathds{E}^{\beta}S)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}(\mathds{E}^{\beta}S)^{2}-\mathds{E}(\mathds{E}^{\beta,i}S)^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}(SS^{\beta})-\mathds{E}(SS^{\beta,i})

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}(S(\Delta_{i}S)^{\beta}),

where to get the next to last equality we use
\mathds{E}(SS^{\beta})=\mathds{E}(\mathds{E}^{\beta}(SS^{\beta}))=\mathds{E}(\mathds{E}^{\beta}S)^{2}
, which follows from the independence assumption. Now, let us assume the validity of the lemma for any set of cardinality
k
and let
\alpha=\{i_{1},\dots,i_{k+1}\}
. Then, since

\alpha
and
\beta
are disjoint, and by independence,

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left({\textrm{Var}}\,^{\alpha}\mathds{E}^{\beta}S\right)

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}\left(\mathds{E}^{(i_{1})}({\textrm{Var}}\,^{(i_{2},\dots,i_{k+1})}\mathds{E}^{\beta}S)-{\textrm{Var}}\,^{(i_{2},\dots,i_{k+1})}(\mathds{E}^{(i_{1})}\mathds{E}^{\beta}S)\right)

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}\left({\textrm{Var}}\,^{(i_{2},\dots,i_{k+1})}\mathds{E}^{\beta}S\right)-\mathds{E}\left({\textrm{Var}}\,^{(i_{2},\dots,i_{k+1})}\mathds{E}^{(i)}S\mathds{E}^{\beta}S\right)

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}\left(S(\Delta_{(i_{2},\dots,i_{k+1})}S)^{\beta}\right)-\mathds{E}\left(S(\Delta_{(i_{2},\dots,i_{k+1})}S)^{\beta,i}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}(S(\Delta_{\alpha}S)^{\beta}),

where in the middle equality we have used the induction hypothesis.

∎

Recalling (
1.8
), we get from (
1.11
) that for all
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
,

J^{\prime}_{k}={{n}\choose{k}}D^{k-1}B_{1}\quad\text{and}\quad K^{\prime}_{k}={n\choose k}D^{k-1}B_{n-k+1}.

(1.12)

It is easy to check that for any finite sequence
(a_{k})_{1\leq k\leq n}
and any positive integers
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
,

a_{k}=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}(-1)^{j}{k-1\choose j}D^{j}a_{1}=\sum_{j=0}^{n-k}{n-k\choose j}D^{j}a_{n-j}.

(1.13)

In particular, for all
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
,

\displaystyle B_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}(-1)^{j}\frac{{k-1\choose j}}{{n\choose j+1}}J^{\prime}_{j+1},

(1.14)

\displaystyle B_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{n-k}\frac{{n-k\choose j}}{{n\choose j+1}}K^{\prime}_{j+1}.

(1.15)

We can now connect the
J^{\prime}_{k}
’s and
K^{\prime}_{k}
’s to the variance.

Lemma 1.4
.

For all
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
,

{\textrm{Var}}\,S-J^{\prime}_{1}+J^{\prime}_{2}-\dots+(-1)^{k}J^{\prime}_{k}=(-1)^{k}\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\dots<i_{k+1}\leq n}D^{k}B_{i_{1}},

(1.16)

{\textrm{Var}}\,S-K^{\prime}_{1}-K^{\prime}_{2}-\dots-K^{\prime}_{k}=\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\dots<i_{k+1}\leq n}D^{k}B_{i_{k+1}}.

(1.17)

Proof.

Let us prove (
1.16
) by induction on
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. For the base case:

{\textrm{Var}}\,S-J^{\prime}_{1}=B_{1}+\dots+B_{n}-nB_{1}=\sum_{j=2}^{n}(B_{j}-B_{1})=-\sum_{j=2}^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}DB_{i}.

For the inductive step: assume it is true for
k\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
. Then,

\displaystyle{\textrm{Var}}\,S-J^{\prime}_{1}+J^{\prime}_{2}-\dots+(-1)^{k}J^{\prime}_{k}+(-1)^{k+1}J^{\prime}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k}\left(\left(\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\dots<i_{k+1}\leq n}D^{k}B_{i_{1}}\right)-J^{\prime}_{k+1}\right)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k}\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\dots<i_{k+1}\leq n}\left(D^{k}B_{i_{1}}-D^{k}B_{1}\right)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k}\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\dots<i_{k+1}\leq n}\sum_{1\leq i_{0}<i_{1}}-D^{k+1}B_{i_{0}}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k+1}\sum_{1\leq i_{0}<i_{1}<\dots<i_{k+1}\leq n}D^{k+1}B_{i_{0}}.

The proof of (
1.17
) is very similar and so it is omitted. The following proposition recovers and extends some of the results obtained in
[
4
]
.
∎

Proposition 1.5
.

{\textrm{Var}}\,S=J^{\prime}_{1}-J^{\prime}_{2}+\dots+(-1)^{n-1}J^{\prime}_{n}=K^{\prime}_{1}+K^{\prime}_{2}+\dots+K^{\prime}_{n},

(1.18)

and for all
k\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
,

K^{\prime}_{k+1}\leq(-1)^{k}\left({\textrm{Var}}\,S-J^{\prime}_{1}+J^{\prime}_{2}-\dots+(-1)^{k}J^{\prime}_{k}\right)\leq J^{\prime}_{k+1},

(1.19)

K^{\prime}_{k+1}\leq{\textrm{Var}}\,S-K^{\prime}_{1}-K^{\prime}_{2}-\dots-K^{\prime}_{k}\leq J^{\prime}_{k+1}.

(1.20)

{\textrm{Var}}\,S=J^{\prime}_{1}-J^{\prime}_{2}+\dots+(-1)^{k-1}J^{\prime}_{k}+(-1)^{k}\sum_{j=k+1}^{n}{j-1\choose k}K^{\prime}_{j}.

(1.21)

{\textrm{Var}}\,S=\frac{{k\choose 1}}{{n\choose 1}}J^{\prime}_{1}-\frac{{k\choose 2}}{{n\choose 2}}J^{\prime}_{2}+\dots+(-1)^{k-1}\frac{{k\choose k}}{{n\choose k}}J^{\prime}_{k}+\frac{{n-k\choose 1}}{{n\choose 1}}K^{\prime}_{1}+\frac{{n-k\choose 2}}{{n\choose 2}}K^{\prime}_{2}+\dots+\frac{{n-k\choose n-k}}{{n\choose n-k}}K^{\prime}_{n-k}.

(1.22)

Proof.

Above, the first two equalities simply follow from the fact that the right-hand terms in Lemma
1.4
are zero when
k=n
.
Then, the first two inequalities follow from Lemma
1.4
and the complete monotonicity of the
B_{k}
’s: for
1\leq i_{1}\leq n-k
,
D^{k}B_{n-k}\leq D^{k}B_{i_{1}}\leq D^{k}B_{1}
. Let us turn to the identity (
1.21
).

Applying the inversion formula (
1.13
) to
(D^{k}B_{i})_{1\leq i\leq n-k}
, with
i\leq n-k
, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\dots<i_{k+1}\leq n}D^{k}B_{i_{1}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n-k}{n-i\choose k}D^{k}B_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n-k}\sum_{j=0}^{n-k-i}{n-i\choose k}{n-k-i\choose j}D^{k+j}B_{n-k-j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n-k}\sum_{j=0}^{n-k-i}{n-i\choose k}{n-k-i\choose j}\frac{K^{\prime}_{k+j+1}}{{n\choose k+j+1}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n-k-j}{n-i\choose k+j}{k+j\choose k}\frac{K^{\prime}_{k+j+1}}{{n\choose k+j+1}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}{k+j\choose k}K^{\prime}_{k+j+1},

where the last equality stems from the hockey-stick formula and reindexing.

To finish, let us prove (
1.22
) which will follow from
{\textrm{Var}}\,S=B_{1}+\dots+B_{k}+B_{k+1}+\dots+B_{n}
. Indeed, the equality (
1.18
) remains valid for any sequence
(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}
, namely, the same proof shows that

a_{1}+\dots+a_{n}={n\choose 1}D^{0}a_{1}-{n\choose 2}D^{1}a_{1}+\dots+(-1)^{n-1}{n\choose n}D^{n-1}a_{1}.

(1.23)

In particular,

\displaystyle B_{1}+\dots+B_{k}

\displaystyle={k\choose 1}D^{0}B_{1}-{k\choose 2}D^{1}B_{1}+\dots+(-1)^{k-1}{k\choose k}D^{k-1}B_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{{k\choose 1}}{{n\choose 1}}J^{\prime}_{1}-\frac{{k\choose 2}}{{n\choose 2}}J^{\prime}_{2}+\dots+(-1)^{k-1}\frac{{k\choose k}}{{n\choose k}}J^{\prime}_{k}.

The second part,
B_{k+1}+\dots+B_{n}
, is treated similarly.
∎

The equality (
1.22
) could be of use to find the order of
{\textrm{Var}}\,S
as
n
tends to infinity. For example, if there is a constant
C>1
(independent of
n
) such that
J^{\prime}_{2}(n)\leq CJ^{\prime}_{1}(n)
, then, taking
k=\lfloor\frac{n}{2C}\rfloor
will lead to

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{{\textrm{Var}}\,S(n)}{J^{\prime}_{1}(n)}\geq\frac{1}{4C}.

We have proved that the finite sequence
(B_{k})_{1\leq k\leq n}
is completely monotone and we already knew from
[
2
]
that it is non-increasing, so it is natural to wonder if one could find further properties of the
B_{k}
’s. On the other hand, one may also wonder whether or not
(K_{k})_{1\leq k\leq n}
does satisfy any further property except, of course, from being non-negative. Both answers appear to be negative:

Proposition 1.6
.

For any
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\geq 0
, there exists
S:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
a Borel function such that for all
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
,
K_{k}=a_{k}
.

Corollary 1.7
.

If
(b_{k})_{1\leq k\leq n}
is completely monotone, then there exists
S:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
a Borel function such that for all
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
,
B_{k}=b_{k}
.

Proof of the Corollary.

It is easy to see that
(b_{k})_{1\leq k\leq n}
is completely monotone if and only if for all
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
,
D^{k-1}b_{n-k+1}\geq 0
. From the statement of the proposition, there exists
S:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
a Borel function such that for all
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
,
K_{k}=\frac{n!}{(n-k)!}D^{k-1}b_{n-k+1}
, and, recalling (
1.12
), since there is no choice for the
B_{k}
’s knowing the
K_{k}
’s,
B_{k}=b_{k}
.
∎

Proof of the proposition.

This follows from using the link with the Hoeffding decomposition observed in
[
4
]
. Consider for example
A_{1},\dots,A_{n}\geq 0
and
S(X_{1},\dots,X_{n}):=A_{1}\sum_{1\leq i_{1}\leq n}(X_{i_{1}}-\mathds{E}X_{i_{1}})+A_{2}\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}\leq n}(X_{i_{1}}-\mathds{E}X_{i_{1}})(X_{i_{2}}-\mathds{E}X_{i_{2}})+\dots+A_{n}\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\dots<i_{n}\leq n}(X_{i_{1}}-\mathds{E}X_{i_{1}})\dots(X_{i_{n}}-\mathds{E}X_{i_{n}})
. Then, from
[
4
]
,

\displaystyle K_{k}

\displaystyle=A_{k}^{2}\,k!\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\dots<i_{k}\leq n}{\textrm{Var}}\,(X_{i_{1}}-\mathds{E}X_{i_{1}})\dots(X_{i_{n}}-\mathds{E}X_{i_{k}})

\displaystyle=A_{k}^{2}\,k!\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\dots<i_{k}\leq n}{\textrm{Var}}\,(X_{i_{1}})\dots{\textrm{Var}}\,(X_{i_{k}}),

so it is possible to adjust the
A_{k}
’s to have the
K_{k}
’s as wanted.
∎

One could expect the
J_{k}
’s to behave like the
K_{k}
’s and to also be able to take any values, but this is unfortunately not the case, for example
{2}J_{2}/n=(n-1)(B_{1}-B_{2})\leq nB_{1}=J_{1}
.

To conclude this section, we connect the
B_{k}
’s and the quantities
T_{A}
introduced in
[
5
]
.
For any subset
A
of
\{1,\dots,n\}
, including
A=\emptyset
,
T_{A}
is defined as

T_{A}=\sum_{j\notin A}\Delta_{j}S(\Delta_{j}S)^{A},

(1.24)

and then
T
is defined as

T=\sum_{A\subsetneq\{1,\dots,n\}}\frac{T_{A}}{2(n-|A|){n\choose|A|}}.

(1.25)

It is easy to check that for all
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
,

B_{k}=\sum_{A:|A|=k-1}\frac{\mathds{E}(T_{A})}{2(n-|A|){n\choose|A|}},

hence
\mathds{E}T=\sum_{k=1}^{n}B_{k}={\textrm{Var}}\,S
(as expected).

Remark 1.8
.

(i)

One might wonder if the above variance results can be transferred to
the
\Phi
-entropy.
Let
\Phi
be a convex function of the real variable such that
\mathds{E}|\Phi(S)|<+\infty
, and let the
\Phi
-entropy
H_{\Phi}
of
S
(e.g., see
[
3
]
) be defined as:

H_{\Phi}(S)=\mathds{E}\Phi(S)-\Phi(\mathds{E}S).

Following
[
4
]
, for
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, let

H^{(i)}_{\Phi}(S)=\mathds{E}^{(i)}\Phi(S)-\Phi(\mathds{E}^{(i)}(S)),

while for
i\neq j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
,

H^{(j,i)}_{\Phi}(S):=\mathds{E}^{(j)}H^{(i)}_{\Phi}(S)-H^{(i)}_{\Phi}(\mathds{E}^{(j)}S)=H^{(i,j)}_{\Phi}(S).

Still iterating, for
i_{1}\neq\dots\neq i_{k}\in\{1,\dots,n\}
,

H^{(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})}_{\Phi}(S):=\mathds{E}^{(i_{1})}H^{(i_{2},\dots,i_{k})}_{\Phi}(S)-H^{(i_{2},\dots,i_{k})}_{\Phi}(\mathds{E}^{(i_{1})}S).

Define the corresponding
B_{k}
’s as,

B_{k}:=\mathds{E}\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{i\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}H^{(i_{k})}_{\Phi}(\mathds{E}^{(i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1})}S),

for all
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. Once again the sum is telescopic:

\sum_{k=1}^{n}B_{k}=\mathds{E}\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{i\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\mathds{E}^{(i_{k})}\Phi(\mathds{E}^{(i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1})}S)-\Phi(\mathds{E}^{(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})}S)=H_{\Phi}S.

By the conditional Jensen inequality, the
B_{k}
’s are non-negative. Just like in the variance case, it is clear by induction that for all
\ell\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}
,

D^{\ell}B_{k}=\mathds{E}\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{i\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}H^{(i_{1},\dots,i_{\ell+1})}_{\Phi}(\mathds{E}^{(i_{\ell+2},\dots,i_{k+\ell})}S).

Let us now look for the class of convex functions
\Phi
such that for any
S
and
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
satisfying the basic independence and integrability assumptions,
(B_{k})_{1\leq k\leq n}
is non-increasing. In particular, for any random variable
Z
defined on a product space
\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2}
satisfying the integrability conditions, choosing
S
and
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
such that
S=Z
(
S=f(X_{1},X_{2})
for some function
f
), we have that

\displaystyle D^{1}B_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n!}\mathds{E}\sum_{i\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}H^{(i_{1},i_{2})}_{\Phi}(\mathds{E}^{(i_{3},\dots,i_{k+1})}S)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{n!}\mathds{E}H^{(1,2)}_{\Phi}(S)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{n!}\mathds{E}\left(\Phi(Z)-\Phi(\mathds{E}^{(1)}Z)-\Phi(\mathds{E}^{(2)}Z)+\Phi(\mathds{E}^{(1,2)}Z)\right),

so
\mathds{E}\left(\Phi(Z)-\Phi(\mathds{E}^{(1)}Z)-\Phi(\mathds{E}^{(2)}Z)+\Phi(\mathds{E}^{(1,2)}Z)\right)\geq 0
. Reciprocally, if for any random variable
Y
defined on a product space
\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2}
satisfying the integrability conditions,

\mathds{E}\left(\Phi(Y)-\Phi(\mathds{E}^{(1)}Y)-\Phi(\mathds{E}^{(2)}Y)+\Phi(\mathds{E}^{(1,2)}Y)\right)\geq 0,

then clearly
D^{1}B_{k}\geq 0
for all
k\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
. Theorem 1 in
[
23
]
tells us that this happens if and only if
\Phi
is affine or is twice differentiable with
\Phi^{\prime\prime}>0
and
1/\Phi^{\prime\prime}
concave.

(ii)

One may further wonder what conditions on
\Phi
would guarantee
(B_{k})_{1\leq k\leq n}
to be completely monotone, or, at least, to have
D^{2}B_{k}\geq 0
for all
k\in\{1,\dots,n-2\}
. Unfortunately, the variance is basically the only case for which this holds true. Indeed, if the condition
D^{2}B_{k}\geq 0
is satisfied for all
S
, then, as before, choosing
S=f(X_{1},X_{2},X_{3})
, we get

\displaystyle D^{1}B_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n!}\mathds{E}\sum_{i\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}H^{(i_{1},i_{2},i_{3})}_{\Phi}(\mathds{E}^{(i_{3},\dots,i_{k+2})}S)

\displaystyle=\frac{6}{n!}\mathds{E}H^{(1,2,3)}_{\Phi}(S)

\displaystyle=\frac{6}{n!}\mathds{E}\sum_{\alpha\subset\{1,2,3\}}(-1)^{|\alpha|}\Phi(\mathds{E}^{\alpha}S).

Therefore, for any random variable
Y
defined on a product space
\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2}\times\Omega_{3}
satisfying the integrability conditions,
\sum_{\alpha\subset\{1,2,3\}}(-1)^{|\alpha|}\Phi(\mathds{E}^{\alpha}Y)\geq 0
. Reciprocally, this guarantees the non-negativity of
D^{2}B_{k}
, for any
k\in\{1,\dots,n-2\}
and any
S
. According to
[
23
, Theorem 2]
, this happens if and only if there exist
a,b,c\in\mathbb{R}
with
a\geq 0
and
\Phi:x\mapsto ax^{2}+bx+c
. So for any function
\Phi
that is not of this form, the
K_{k}
’s and the
J_{k}
’s (defined as the variations of
B_{k}
’s) are not always non-negative: for some functions
S
they are negative.

(iii)

It is tempting to use the representation of completely monotone functions for the
B_{k}^{\prime}s
. Unfortunately, a completely monotone finite sequence may not be the restriction of a completely monotone function.

(iv)

From the decomposition of the variance one easily gets the decomposition of the covariance of square integrable
S
and
T
:
\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, as a polarization identity gives:

{\textrm{Cov}}\,(S,T)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}B_{k}(S,T),

where

\displaystyle B_{k}(S,T)=\mathds{E}\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{i\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}S(T^{i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1}}-T^{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}})

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}\frac{1}{2n!}\sum_{i\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}(S-S^{i_{k}})(T^{i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1}}-T^{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}})

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}\frac{1}{2n!}\sum_{i\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}(T-T^{i_{k}})(S^{i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1}}-S^{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}}).

To get a symmetrical formula of the variance, let for any
i_{1},\dots i_{k}\in\{1,\dots,n\}
,
S^{\widetilde{i_{1}},\dots,\widetilde{i_{k}}}
be as
S^{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}}
, but with
X^{\prime\prime}_{k}
in place of
X^{\prime}_{k}
, where the
X^{\prime\prime}_{k}
’s have same distribution as the
X_{k}
’s and are independant of all previous random variables. Then,

\displaystyle B_{k}(S,T)

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}\frac{1}{2n!}\sum_{i\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\Delta_{i_{k}}(T^{\widetilde{i_{1}},\dots,\widetilde{i_{k}-1}})\Delta_{i_{k}}(S^{i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1}}).

2
Connections with decompositions of the variance

2.1
Connection with a more general decomposition of the variance

Let
U_{1},\dots,U_{n}
be random variables taking values in
(0,1)
and independent of
X_{1},\dots,X_{n},X^{\prime}_{1},\dots,X^{\prime}_{n}
. For any
\alpha\in[0,1]
, let
X^{(\alpha)}
be the vector with coordinates
X^{(\alpha)}_{i}:=\mathds{1}_{\alpha\leq U_{i}}X_{i}+\mathds{1}_{\alpha>U_{i}}X^{\prime}_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq n
. Then,

{\textrm{Var}}\,S=\mathds{E}\left(S(X^{(0)})\left(S(X^{(0)})-S(X^{(1)})\right)\right),

(2.1)

and it is tempting to rewrite this last term as an integral. Let us assume that each
U_{i}
has a density
\nu_{i}
. For any
0\leq\alpha<\alpha^{\prime}\leq 1
, denote by
A_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}
the random set of indices
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
such that
\alpha\leq U_{i}<\alpha^{\prime}
. Then, conditioning on the cardinality of
A_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}
, applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and an inductive argument lead to:

\displaystyle\left|\mathds{E}\left(S(X^{(0)})S(X^{(\alpha^{\prime})})\right)-\mathds{E}\left(S(X^{(0)})S(X^{(\alpha)})\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathds{E}(S^{2})\mathds{P}\left(|A_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}|>0\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathds{E}(S^{2})\mathds{E}|A_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}|

\displaystyle=2\mathds{E}(S^{2})\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\alpha}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\mathrm{d}\nu_{i}.

Therefore,
\alpha\mapsto\mathds{E}\left(S(X^{(0)})S(X^{(\alpha)})\right)
is absolutely continuous, its derivative is well defined almost everywhere, integrable, and

\displaystyle{\textrm{Var}}\,S=\mathds{E}\left(S(X^{(0)})S(X^{(0)})\right)-\mathds{E}\left(S(X^{(0)})S(X^{(1)})\right)=-\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\alpha}\mathds{E}\left(S(X^{(0)})S(X^{(\alpha)})\right)\mathrm{d}\alpha.

(2.2)

In order to compute the derivative in (
2.2
), fix
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1-\alpha)
. Conditioning on
A_{\alpha,\alpha+\varepsilon}
and letting

\Delta_{\alpha,\varepsilon}:=\frac{\mathds{E}\left(S(X^{(0)})S(X^{(\alpha+\varepsilon)})\right)-\mathds{E}\left(S(X^{(0)})S(X^{(\alpha)})\right)}{\varepsilon},

we get

\Delta_{\alpha,\varepsilon}=\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\dots<i_{k}\leq n,k\leq n}\frac{\mathds{E}\left(S(X^{(0)})\left(S(X^{(\alpha+\varepsilon)})-S(X^{(\alpha)})\right)|A_{\alpha,\alpha+\varepsilon}=\{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\}\right)}{\varepsilon}\mathds{P}\left(A_{\alpha,\alpha+\varepsilon}=\{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\}\right),

so for almost every
\alpha
,

\Delta_{\alpha,\varepsilon}\xrightarrow[\varepsilon\to 0]{}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{E}\left(S(X^{(0)})(S(X^{(\alpha),\hat{i}})-S(X^{(\alpha),i}))\right)\nu_{i}(\alpha),

where
X^{(\alpha),i}
is defined like
X^{(\alpha)}
but with
X_{i}
for its
i
-th coordinate, and
X^{(\alpha),\hat{i}}
is defined like
X^{(\alpha)}
but with
X^{\prime}_{i}
for its
i
-th coordinate. So we get finally:

{\textrm{Var}}\,S=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{1}\mathds{E}\left(S(X^{(0)})(S(X^{(\alpha),i})-S(X^{(\alpha),\hat{i}}))\right)\mathrm{d}\nu_{i}(\alpha).

(2.3)

Let us further define, for
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
and any
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
d_{i}S
via,

d_{i}S(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}):=S(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})-\mathds{E}S(x_{1},\dots,x_{i-1},X_{i},x_{i+1},\dots,x_{n}).

(2.4)

Note that if
Z_{i}
is independent of all the other random variables and has same distribution as
X_{i}
, we have

d_{i}S(X)=\mathds{E}_{Z_{i}}\left(S(X)-S(X_{1},\dots,X_{i-1},Z_{i},X_{i+1},\dots,X_{n})\right).

(2.5)

Therefore we notice, conditioning on
U_{i}
, that

\mathds{E}\left(d_{i}S(X^{(0)})d_{i}S(X^{(\alpha)})\right)=\mathds{P}(\alpha\leq U_{i})\mathds{E}\left(S(X^{(0)})(S(X^{(\alpha),i})-S(X^{(\alpha),\hat{i}}))\right).

(2.6)

We can rewrite the variance as

\displaystyle{\textrm{Var}}\,S

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{1}\mathds{E}\left(d_{i}S(X^{(0)})d_{i}S(X^{(\alpha)})\right)\frac{1}{\int_{\alpha}^{1}\mathrm{d}\nu_{i}(\alpha)}\mathrm{d}\nu_{i}(\alpha).

(2.7)

Note that in the special case where
U_{i}
are uniformly distributed on
[0,1]
,

\displaystyle{\textrm{Var}}\,S

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{1}\mathds{E}\left(d_{i}S(X^{(0)})d_{i}S(X^{(\alpha)})\right)\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\mathrm{d}\alpha,

(2.8)

and a simple change of variables allows us to recover again (
2.7
).
Therefore, we will focus on the uniformly distributed case.

Next, from (
2.3
),

\displaystyle{\textrm{Var}}\,S

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{1}\mathds{E}\left(S(X^{(0)})(S(X^{(\alpha),i})-S(X^{(\alpha),\hat{i}}))\right)\mathrm{d}\alpha

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\mathds{E}\left(S(X^{(0)})(S(X^{(\alpha),i})-S(X^{(\alpha),\hat{i}}))\right)\mathds{1}_{|A_{0,\alpha}\setminus\{i\}|=k}\mathrm{d}\alpha

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}n\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{E}\left(S\left(\Delta_{i}S\right)^{\beta_{k,i}}\right)\mathds{P}\left(|A_{0,\alpha}\setminus\{i\}|=k\right)\mathrm{d}\alpha,

where
\beta_{k,i}
is a random set of
k
elements chosen in
\{1,\dots,n\}\setminus\{i\}
. Clearly
\mathds{P}\left(|A_{0,\alpha}\setminus\{i\}|=k\right)={n-1\choose k}\alpha^{k}(1-\alpha)^{n-1-k}
, and from the representations (
1.5
) and (
1.6
), we get for any
k\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}
,

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{E}\left(S\left(\Delta_{i}S\right)^{\beta_{k,i}}\right)=B_{k+1}.

Hence,

\displaystyle{\textrm{Var}}\,S

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\int_{0}^{1}n{n-1\choose k}\alpha^{k}(1-\alpha)^{n-1-k}B_{k+1}\mathrm{d}\alpha=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}B_{k+1}.

2.2
Connection with a semigroup approach

The semigroup approach, as developed in
[
18
]
for the hypercube, boils down to the same integration along
\alpha
trick. We need first to rewrite our results in a more general setup: we assume the
X_{i}
’s to be i.i.d. discrete variables, taking a finite number of values and this time,
S
takes values in a Banach space
(E,\|\cdot\|_{E})
. We also consider a continuous convex function
\Phi:E\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
, so instead of considering
{\textrm{Var}}\,S=\mathds{E}\|S-\mathds{E}S\|_{E}^{2}=\|S-\mathds{E}S\|_{E,2}^{2}
, we consider
\mathds{E}\left(\Phi(S-\mathds{E}S)\right)
. The price to pay is a suboptimal constant, as seen next, and the lack of connection with the
B_{k}
’s, which do not seem to have any equivalent in this setup. We hope that making this connection casts a new light on the breakthrough
[
18
]
, but also gives prospects to generalize it: indeed, while it is not clear what would be the adequate semigroup when the
X_{i}
’s are not binary variables, our theorem works for all discrete distributions with finite support (and it is straightforward to generalize to all discrete distributions or even bounded continuous distributions). It should also be pointed that, motivated by geometric applications, the case of the biased cube was also recently studied by different methods in
[
7
]
.

Theorem 2.1
.

For any
\alpha\in(0,1)
, let
\varepsilon_{1}(\alpha),\dots,\varepsilon_{n}(\alpha)
be i.i.d. random variables such that
\mathds{P}(\xi_{i}(\alpha)=1)=1-\alpha,\mathds{P}(\xi_{i}(\alpha)=-1)=\alpha
, and let
\delta_{i}(\alpha)=({\xi_{i}(\alpha)-\mathds{E}\xi_{i}(\alpha)})/{\sqrt{{\textrm{Var}}\,\xi_{i}(\alpha)}}
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathds{E}(\Phi(S-\mathds{E}S))

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}\mathds{E}\Phi\left(\pi\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{i}(\alpha)d_{i}S(X)\right)\frac{\mathrm{d}\alpha}{\pi\sqrt{\alpha(1-\alpha)}}.

(2.9)

Proof.

Firstly, without loss of generality, we may assume
\mathds{E}S=0
(one may check all
the following results are true when one adds a constant to
S
). Following
[
18
]
, denoting by
\Phi^{*}
the convex conjugate
of
\Phi
, we note that for any
x\in E
,

\Phi(x)=\sup_{y\in E^{*}}\left(\langle y,x\rangle-\Phi^{*}(y)\right),

(2.10)

and therefore, since the
X_{i}
’s only take a finite number of values,

\mathds{E}(\Phi(S-\mathds{E}S))=\sup_{T\text{ is }\sigma(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})-\text{measurable, taking values in }E^{*}}\mathds{E}(\langle T,S\rangle-\Phi^{*}(T)).

(2.11)

We now bound the term
\mathds{E}(\langle T,S\rangle-\Phi^{*}(T))
. As in (
2.2
),

\displaystyle\mathds{E}(\langle T,S\rangle-\Phi^{*}(T))=-\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\alpha}\left(\mathds{E}(\langle T,S(X^{(\alpha)})\rangle\right)\mathrm{d}\alpha-\mathds{E}\Phi^{*}(T)),

(2.12)

and just like in obtaining (
2.3
), we get

\mathds{E}(\langle T,S\rangle-\Phi^{*}(T))=\int_{0}^{1}\mathds{E}(\langle T,\sum_{i=1}^{n}S(X^{(\alpha),i})-S(X^{(\alpha),\hat{i}}))\rangle\mathrm{d}\alpha-\mathds{E}\Phi^{*}(T).

(2.13)

Note that

S(X^{(\alpha),i})-S(X^{(\alpha),\hat{i}})=d_{i}S(X^{(\alpha),i})-d_{i}S(X^{(\alpha),\hat{i}}),

(2.14)

and by independence,

\displaystyle\mathds{E}(\langle T,d_{i}S(X^{(\alpha),\hat{i}})\rangle)

\displaystyle=0,

(2.15)

so

\mathds{E}(\langle T,S\rangle-\Phi^{*}(T))=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{1}\mathds{E}(\langle T,d_{i}S(X^{(\alpha),i})\rangle\mathrm{d}\alpha-\mathds{E}\Phi^{*}(T).

(2.16)

Now, let

\delta_{i}(\alpha):=\frac{\mathds{1}_{U_{i}\geq\alpha}-(1-\alpha)}{\sqrt{\alpha(1-\alpha)}}=\frac{2(\mathds{1}_{U_{i}\geq\alpha}-1/2)-(1-2\alpha)}{2\sqrt{\alpha(1-\alpha)}},

(2.17)

where the last equality is here to show that this is just a renormalized random variable taking values in
\{-1,1\}
, much like the
\xi_{i}(t)
’s, random variables with
\mathds{P}(\xi_{i}(t)=1)=(1+\mathrm{e}^{-t})/2
and
\mathds{P}(\xi_{i}(t)=-1)=(1-\mathrm{e}^{-t})/2
introduced in
[
18
]
. We have:

\displaystyle\mathds{E}(\langle T,\delta_{i}(\alpha)d_{i}S(X^{(\alpha)})\rangle)

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}\left(\langle T,\frac{\mathds{1}_{U_{i}\geq\alpha}d_{i}S(X^{(\alpha),i})-(1-\alpha)(\mathds{1}_{U_{i}\geq\alpha}d_{i}S(X^{(\alpha),i})+\mathds{1}_{U_{i}<\alpha}d_{i}S(X^{(\alpha),\hat{i}}))}{\sqrt{\alpha(1-\alpha)}}\rangle\right)

(2.18)

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}\left(\langle T,\frac{\alpha\mathds{1}_{U_{i}\geq\alpha}d_{i}S(X^{(\alpha),i})}{\sqrt{\alpha(1-\alpha)}}\rangle\right)

(2.19)

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}\left(\langle T,\sqrt{\alpha(1-\alpha)}d_{i}S(X^{(\alpha),i})\rangle\right),

(2.20)

hence, with (
2.16
) we get:

\displaystyle\mathds{E}(\langle T,S\rangle-\Phi^{*}(T))

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\mathds{E}(\langle T,\delta_{i}(\alpha)d_{i}S(X^{(\alpha)})\rangle)}{\sqrt{\alpha(1-\alpha)}}\mathrm{d}\alpha-\mathds{E}\Phi^{*}(T)

(2.21)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\mathds{E}(\langle T,\pi\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{i}(\alpha)d_{i}S(X^{(\alpha)})\rangle)-\mathds{E}\Phi^{*}(T)\frac{\mathrm{d}\alpha}{\pi\sqrt{\alpha(1-\alpha)}}

(2.22)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}\mathds{E}\Phi\left(\pi\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{i}(\alpha)d_{i}S(X^{(\alpha)})\right)\frac{\mathrm{d}\alpha}{\pi\sqrt{\alpha(1-\alpha)}}.

(2.23)

Note that
(U_{1},\dots,U_{n},X^{(\alpha)}_{1},\dots,X^{(\alpha)}_{n})
has the same distribution as
(U_{1},\dots,U_{n},X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
, so

\displaystyle\mathds{E}(\langle T,S\rangle-\Phi^{*}(T))

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}\mathds{E}\Phi\left(\pi\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{i}(\alpha)d_{i}S(X)\right)\frac{\mathrm{d}\alpha}{\pi\sqrt{\alpha(1-\alpha)}}.

(2.24)

Recalling (
2.11
), the result follows.
∎

Let us see how the above allows to link our approach with the main results of
[
18
]
for Rademacher random variables. Recalling the notation of
[
18
]
: for
x\in\{-1,1\}^{n}
, let

D_{i}S(x):=\frac{S(x_{1},\dots,x_{i},\dots,x_{n})-S(x_{1},\dots,-x_{i},\dots,x_{n})}{2}.

(2.25)

We can now state the corollary, in the Rademacher case:

Corollary 2.2
.

Let
X_{i}
’s be i.i.d. Rademacher random variables,

\displaystyle\mathds{E}(\Phi(S-\mathds{E}S))

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}\mathds{E}\Phi\left(\pi\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{i}(\alpha)D_{i}S(X)\right)\frac{\mathrm{d}\alpha}{\pi\sqrt{\alpha(1-\alpha)}},

(2.26)

i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathds{E}(\Phi(S-\mathds{E}S))

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{+\infty}\mathds{E}\Phi\left(\pi\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{i}(\mathrm{e}^{-2t})D_{i}S(X)\right)\frac{2\mathrm{d}t}{\pi\sqrt{\mathrm{e}^{2t}-1}}.

(2.27)

Proof.

Since
\mathds{E}^{(i)}S
does not depend on
x_{i}
,

\displaystyle D_{i}S(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{(S-\mathds{E}^{(i)}S)(x_{1},\dots,x_{i},\dots,x_{n})-(S-\mathds{E}^{(i)}S)(x_{1},\dots,-x_{i},\dots,x_{n})}{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{d_{i}S(x_{1},\dots,x_{i},\dots,x_{n})-d_{i}S(x_{1},\dots,-x_{i},\dots,x_{n})}{2}

\displaystyle=d_{i}S(x),

where, above, the last equality follows
d_{i}S(x_{1},\dots,1,\dots,x_{n})=-d_{i}S(x_{1},\dots,-1,\dots,x_{n})
since
\mathds{E}^{(i)}(d_{i}S(X))=0
.

∎

The above implies a slightly weaker
[
18
, Theorem 1.2]
, i.e., with a different absolute constant, but the fact that Enflo type and Rademacher type coincide still follows from Theorem
2.1
just as it follows from
[
18

with, as indicated there, a routine symmetrization argument.

To make the connection complete, recall the additional notations in

]

\Delta
is defined by

\Delta:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}D^{i},

(2.28)

and the semigroup
P_{t}
is defined as

P_{t}:=\mathrm{e}^{-t\Delta}.

(2.29)

When the
X_{i}
’s are Rademacher random variables, the crucial observation in
[
18
]
is that (we denote by
\xi^{\prime}
,
\delta^{\prime}
the variables
\xi
,

\delta
previously defined):

-\frac{\mathrm{d}P_{t}S}{\mathrm{d}t}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathrm{e}^{2t}-1}}\mathds{E}_{\xi^{\prime}(t)}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta^{\prime}_{i}(t)D_{i}S(\xi^{\prime}(t)X)\right),

(2.30)

where
\xi^{\prime}(t)X
is defined as
(\xi^{\prime}_{1}(t)X_{1},\dots,\xi^{\prime}_{n}(t)X_{n})
.

Something similar holds in a more general framework (when the
X_{i}^{\prime}s
are random variables taking a finite number of values):

Theorem 2.3
.

With the same assumptions as in Theorem
2.1
,

-\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathds{E}_{X^{\prime},U}S(X^{(\alpha)})}{\mathrm{d}\alpha}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha(1-\alpha)}}\mathds{E}_{X^{\prime},U}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{i}(\alpha)d_{i}S(X^{(\alpha)})\right).

(2.31)

Proof.

This is essentially the same proof as the proof of Theorem
2.1
.
∎

We conclude this section with a remark on the Talagrand
L_{1}-L_{2}
inequality in Banach spaces of Rademacher type 2.

As noted in
[
6
]
, it is natural, to try to understand for which Banach spaces
(E,\|\cdot\|_{E})
there exists
C=C(E)>0
such that for any function
S
of i.i.d. Rademacher
random variables
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
taking values in
E
,

\|S-\mathds{E}S\|_{E,2}^{2}\leq C\sigma(S)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\|D_{i}S\|_{E,2}^{2}}{1+\log\left(\frac{\|D_{i}S\|_{E,2}}{\|D_{i}S\|_{E,1}}\right)},

(2.32)

where
\|\cdot\|_{E,k}=\left(\mathds{E}\|\cdot\|_{E}^{k}\right)^{1/k}
,
which is a generalization of Talagrand’s
L_{1}-L_{2}
inequality (see Theorem
3.7
) to Banach spaces.

Clearly, if a Banach space satisfies (
2.32
), it must be of Rademacher type 2. It is still unknown whether or not the converse is true. To date, the best result is
[
6
, Theorem 1]
:

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
(E,\|\cdot\|_{E})
be a Banach space of Rademacher type 2. Then there there exists
C=C(E)>0
such that for any function
S
of the i.i.d. Rademacher random variables
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
taking values in
E
,

\|S-\mathds{E}S\|_{E,2}^{2}\leq C\sigma(S)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\|D_{i}S\|_{E,2}^{2}}{1+\log\left(\frac{\|D_{i}S\|_{E,2}}{\|D_{i}S\|_{E,1}}\right)},

where
\sigma(S)=\max_{i\in\{1,\dots,n\}}\log\left(1+\log\left(\frac{\|D_{i}S\|_{E,2}}{\|D_{i}S\|_{E,1}}\right)\right)
.

It is still unclear whether or not the logarithmic term
\sigma(S)
is needed, but we now show how hypercontractivity can come short to removing it.

As noted in
[
6
]
, one may apply (
2.30
) to
P_{t}S
instead of
S
(for a fixed
t
), while the chain rule and semigroup properties give:

-\frac{\mathrm{d}P_{2t}S}{\mathrm{d}t}=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\mathrm{e}^{2t}-1}}\mathds{E}_{\xi^{\prime}(t)}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta^{\prime}_{i}(t)D_{i}P_{t}S(\xi^{\prime}(t)X)\right).

(2.33)

Hence, since
E
is of Rademacher type 2, denoting by
K
its constant, we get (see e.g.
[
6
, (57)]
):

\|S-\mathds{E}S\|_{E,2}\leq 4K\int_{0}^{+\infty}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|D_{i}P_{t}S\|_{E,2}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{\sqrt{\mathrm{e}^{2t}-1}}.

(2.34)

We now show that in some cases hypercontractivity may not be enough to get rid of the factor
\sigma(S)
. (A different (related?) approach for Boolean functions is presented in
[
17
]
.) More precisely, let

I=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|D_{i}S\|_{E,2}^{2}\left(\frac{\|D_{i}S\|_{E,1}}{\|D_{i}S\|_{E,2}}\right)^{2\frac{1-\mathrm{e}^{-2t}}{1+\mathrm{e}^{-2t}}}\right)^{1/2}\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{\sqrt{\mathrm{e}^{2t}-1}},

(2.35)

which is the upper bound on the right term of (
2.34
) one gets using hypercontractivity.

We let
L_{i}=\log\left(\mathrm{e}\frac{\|D_{i}S\|_{E,2}}{\|D_{i}S\|_{E,1}}\right)
,
d_{i}=\|D_{i}S\|_{E,2}
and
\theta(t)=\frac{1-\mathrm{e}^{-2t}}{1+\mathrm{e}^{-2t}}
, so

I\sim\int_{0}^{+\infty}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-2L_{i}\theta(t)}\right)^{1/2}\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{\sqrt{\mathrm{e}^{2t}-1}}.

(2.36)

With a change of variables,

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle\sim\int_{0}^{1}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-2L_{i}\theta}\right)^{1/2}\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{\sqrt{\theta(1-\theta)}}

\displaystyle\sim\int_{0}^{1/2}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-2L_{i}\theta}\right)^{1/2}\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{\sqrt{\theta}}+\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-1}}{\sqrt{2}}\int_{1/2}^{1}\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{\sqrt{1-\theta}}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{d_{i}^{2}}{L_{i}}},

so bounding
\frac{I^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}^{2}/L_{i}}
(we already know it is bounded by
\sigma(S)
) is equivalent to bounding

R:=\frac{\int_{0}^{1/2}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-2L_{i}\theta}\right)^{1/2}\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{\sqrt{\theta}}}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}^{2}/L_{i}}}.

(2.37)

Letting
\lambda_{i}:=\frac{d_{i}^{2}/L_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}^{2}/L_{i}}
, we get

R=\sqrt{2}\int_{0}^{1/2}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}L_{i}\mathrm{e}^{-L_{i}\theta}\right)^{1/2}\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{\sqrt{\theta}}.

(2.38)

Assume
L_{i}=2^{i-1}
,
\lambda_{i}=1/n
. Then for any
\theta\in(1/2^{n},1)
, there exists
i_{0}\in\{1,\dots,n\}
such that
1/2^{i_{0}}\leq\theta\leq 1/2^{i_{0}-1}
, and

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}L_{i}\mathrm{e}^{-L_{i}\theta}\right)^{1/2}\geq\left(\lambda_{i_{0}}L_{i_{0}}\mathrm{e}^{-L_{i_{0}}\theta}\right)^{1/2}\geq\left(\frac{L_{i_{0}}\theta\mathrm{e}^{-L_{i_{0}}\theta}}{n\theta}\right)^{1/2}\geq\frac{\sqrt{2\mathrm{e}^{-2}}}{\sqrt{n\theta}},

so

\displaystyle R\geq\frac{2\sqrt{\mathrm{e}^{-2}}}{\sqrt{n}}\int_{1/2^{n}}^{1}\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{\theta}\geq 2\sqrt{\mathrm{e}^{-2}}\log(2)\sqrt{n}\geq\frac{2\sqrt{\mathrm{e}^{-2}}}{\log(2)}\sqrt{\max_{i\in\{1,\dots,n\}}L_{i}}.

Thus in this case,
\frac{I^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}^{2}/L_{i}}
is lower bounded by
C\sigma(S)
, for some constant
C>0
.

3
Further applications to some generic inequalities

3.1
Iterated gradients and Gaussian (in)equalities

It is well known that one can transfer the finite samples results of the previous section to functions of normal random variables, somehow reversing the analogies between iterated jackknives and iterated gradients first unveiled in
[
9
]
. This transfer is then followed by a study of the infinitely divisible framework and by the semigroup approach to these inequalities.

Let
Z
be a standard random variable and
G
be an absolutely continuous function. As well known, the Gaussian Poincaré inequality asserts that

{\textrm{Var}}\,G(Z)\leq\mathds{E}\left(G^{\prime}(Z)^{2}\right),

(3.1)

while in
[
11
]
, this inequality is generalized with higher order gradients.
Lemma
1.4
and Proposition
1.5
allows us to quickly recover Gaussian results. Indeed, e.g., see
[
3
]
in the case
k=1
, one can infer from the discrete decomposition of the variance a decomposition for
{\textrm{Var}}\,G(Z)
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
G
be a real-valued
m
-times continuously differentiable function, such that
\mathds{E}\left(G^{(k)}(Z)^{2}\right)<+\infty
,
k=0,\dots,m
. Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n},X^{\prime}_{1},\dots,X^{\prime}_{n}
be independent Rademacher random variables and let
S(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})\!:=G\left(\frac{X_{1}+\dots+X_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)
. Then for all
k\in\{1,\dots,m\}
,

J_{k}(n)\xrightarrow[n\to+\infty]{}\mathds{E}\left(G^{(k)}(Z)^{2}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad K_{k}(n)\xrightarrow[n\to+\infty]{}\left(\mathds{E}\left(G^{(k)}(Z)\right)\right)^{2}.

(3.2)

Proof.

It is enough to prove the theorem for
G

m+1
-times continuously differentiable with compact support. From (
1.12
), we have

J_{k}=k!{n\choose k}D^{k-1}B_{1},

so (using (
1.7
)),

J_{k}=k!{n\choose k}\mathds{E}\frac{1}{2^{k}n!}\sum_{i\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\left(\Delta_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}}S\right)^{2}.

By symmetry of the function
S(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})=G\left(\frac{X_{1}+\dots+X_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)
, this simplifies to

J_{k}=k!{n\choose k}\mathds{E}\frac{1}{2^{k}}\left(\Delta_{1,\dots,k}S\right)^{2}.

(3.3)

For any
i\in\{1,\dots,k\}
,

\Delta_{i}S=(D_{i}S)2\mathds{1}_{X_{i}=X^{\prime}_{i}}

with

D_{i}S(x):=\frac{S(x_{1},\dots,x_{i},\dots,x_{n})-S(x_{1},\dots,-x_{i},\dots,x_{n})}{2}.

Iterating,

\Delta_{1,\dots,k}S=(D_{1,\dots,k}S)2^{k}\mathds{1}_{X_{1}=X^{\prime}_{1},\dots,X_{k}=X^{\prime}_{k}},

hence

\mathds{E}\frac{1}{2^{k}}\left(\Delta_{1,\dots,k}S\right)^{2}=\mathds{E}\left(D_{1,\dots,k}S\right)^{2}.

(3.4)

We now expand, for any
x\in\{-1,1\}^{n}
,
D_{1,\dots,k}S(x)
. Let us denote for
A\subset\{1,\dots,k\}
,

x^{A}:=(2\mathds{1}_{1\in A}-1,\dots,2\mathds{1}_{k\in A}-1,x_{k+1},\dots,x_{n}).

It is straightforward to prove by induction that

D_{1,\dots,k}S(x)=(-1)^{|i\in\{1,\dots,k\}:x_{i}=1|}\frac{1}{2^{k}}\sum_{A\subset\{1,\dots,k\}}(-1)^{|A|}S(x^{A}),

which simplifies to

D_{1,\dots,k}S(x)=(-1)^{|i\in\{1,\dots,k\}:x_{i}=1|}\frac{1}{2^{k}}\sum_{i=0}^{k}{k\choose i}(-1)^{i}G\left(\frac{2i-k+x_{k+1}+\dots+x_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}\right).

By Taylor’s formula, and using the fact that
\sum_{i=0}^{k}{k\choose i}(-1)^{i}i^{\ell}/\ell!=(-1)^{k}\mathds{1}_{\ell=k}
for any
\ell\in\{0,\dots,k\}
, we get that

\left|D_{1,\dots,k}S(x)\right|=\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}^{k}}G^{(k)}\left(\frac{-k+x_{k+1}+\dots+x_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\right|+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}^{k+1}}\right),

with
\mathcal{O}
uniform in
x
(thanks to the compact support assumption). This leads to

\mathds{E}n^{k}\left(D_{1,\dots,k}S\right)^{2}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\mathds{E}\left(G^{(k)}(Z)\right)^{2},

and using (
3.3
) and (
3.4
), we get the desired result

\mathds{E}J_{k}(n)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\mathds{E}\left(G^{(k)}(Z)\right)^{2}.

The other limit in the theorem is obtained in a very similar fashion.
∎

We now see, using (
1.14
) and (
1.15
), that for any fixed
k\geq 1
,

B_{k}(n)\sim_{n\to+\infty}\frac{1}{n}\mathds{E}\left(G^{\prime}(Z)^{2}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad B_{n-k}(n)\sim_{n\to+\infty}\frac{1}{n}\left(\mathds{E}\left(G^{\prime}(Z)\right)\right)^{2}.

More generally, for any
a\in(0,1)
,

B_{\lfloor an\rfloor}(n)\sim_{n\to+\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{a^{i}(-1)^{i}}{i!}\mathds{E}\left(G^{(i+1)}(Z)^{2}\right).

Note that

\int_{0}^{1}B_{\lfloor an\rfloor}(n)\mathrm{d}a\xrightarrow[n\to+\infty]{}\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{i}}{(i+1)!}\mathds{E}\left(G^{(i+1)}(Z)^{2}\right)={\textrm{Var}}\,G(Z),

as one could expect.

Proposition 3.2
.

Under similar assumptions on
G
, for all
k\in\{1,\dots,m\}
,

\frac{\left(\mathds{E}\left(G^{(k+1)}(Z)\right)\right)^{2}}{(k+1)!}\leq(-1)^{k}\left({\textrm{Var}}\,G(Z)-\mathds{E}\left(G^{\prime}(Z)^{2}\right)+\dots+(-1)^{k}\frac{\mathds{E}\left(G^{(k)}(Z)^{2}\right)}{k!}\right)\leq\frac{\mathds{E}\left(G^{(k+1)}(Z)^{2}\right)}{(k+1)!}.

\frac{\left(\mathds{E}\left(G^{(k+1)}(Z)\right)\right)^{2}}{(k+1)!}\leq{\textrm{Var}}\,G(Z)-\left(\mathds{E}\left(G^{\prime}(Z)\right)\right)^{2}-\frac{\left(\mathds{E}\left(G^{\prime\prime}(Z)\right)\right)^{2}}{2}-\dots-\frac{\left(\mathds{E}\left(G^{(k)}(Z)\right)\right)^{2}}{k!}\leq\frac{\mathds{E}\left(G^{(k+1)}(Z)^{2}\right)}{(k+1)!}.

The above indicates that the difference between the variance and each partial sum is
squeezed between the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. We may also get equalities, when
G
is infinitely differentiable, with additional conditions. Indeed,

Corollary 3.3
.

Let
G
be a real-valued infinitely-differentiable function, such that, for all
k\geq 0
,

\mathds{E}(G^{(k)}(Z))^{2}<+\infty
. Then,

{\textrm{Var}}\,G(Z)=\sum_{i=1}^{+\infty}(-1)^{i-1}\frac{\mathds{E}\left(G^{(i)}(Z)^{2}\right)}{i!},

if and only if
\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathds{E}(G^{(k)}(Z))^{2}/k!=0
, and under such a condition,

{\textrm{Var}}\,G(Z)=\sum_{i=1}^{+\infty}\frac{\left(\mathds{E}\left(G^{(i)}(Z)\right)\right)^{2}}{i!}.

For any
k\geq 1
,

VarG(Z)=\mathds{E}\left(G^{\prime}(Z)^{2}\right)-\frac{\mathds{E}\left(G^{\prime\prime}(Z)^{2}\right)}{2}+\dots+(-1)^{k-1}\frac{\mathds{E}\left(G^{(k)}(Z)^{2}\right)}{k!}+(-1)^{k}\sum_{j=k+1}^{\infty}{j-1\choose k}\frac{\left(\mathds{E}\left(G^{(j)}(Z)\right)\right)^{2}}{j!}.

(3.5)

For any
a\in[0,1]
,

{\textrm{Var}}\,G(Z)=\sum_{i=1}^{+\infty}\left((-1)^{i-1}a^{i}\frac{\mathds{E}\left(G^{(i)}(Z)^{2}\right)}{i!}+(1-a)^{i}\frac{\left(\mathds{E}\left(G^{(i)}(Z)\right)\right)^{2}}{i!}\right).

(3.6)

Proof.

This is nothing but Lemma
3.1
together with Proposition
1.5
. To get the last equality, apply (
1.22
) to
k=\lfloor an\rfloor
.
∎

The equality (
3.5
) is a generalization of the equality in
[
4
]
, where
k=1
. Note that (
3.6
) can be rewritten as

{\textrm{Var}}\,G(Z)=\sum_{i=1}^{+\infty}\frac{\left(\mathds{E}\left(G^{(i)}(Z)\right)\right)^{2}}{i!}+\sum_{i=1}^{+\infty}\left((-1)^{i-1}\frac{\mathds{E}\left(G^{(i)}(Z)^{2}\right)}{i!}+\sum_{j\geq i}(-1)^{i}{j\choose i}\frac{\left(\mathds{E}\left(G^{(j)}(Z)\right)\right)^{2}}{j!}\right)a^{i},

which gives us the additional equality: for all
i\geq 1
,

\frac{\mathds{E}\left(G^{(i)}(Z)^{2}\right)}{i!}=\sum_{j\geq i}{j\choose i}\frac{\left(\mathds{E}\left(G^{(j)}(Z)\right)\right)^{2}}{j!}.

This gives an alternative way to find (
3.1
) again:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=k+1}^{+\infty}(-1)^{i-1}\frac{\mathds{E}\left(G^{(i)}(Z)^{2}\right)}{i!}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\geq i\geq k+1}(-1)^{i-1}{j\choose i}\frac{\left(\mathds{E}\left(G^{(j)}(Z)\right)\right)^{2}}{j!}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=k+1}^{+\infty}\left(\sum_{i=k+1}^{j}(-1)^{i-1}{j\choose i}\right)\frac{\left(\mathds{E}\left(G^{(j)}(Z)\right)\right)^{2}}{j!}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=k+1}^{+\infty}{j-1\choose k}\frac{\left(\mathds{E}\left(G^{(j)}(Z)\right)\right)^{2}}{j!}.

Multivariable versions of the above results remain true, and in fact, so do infinite-dimensional ones on Wiener space or Poisson space or even Fock space. In each case, what is needed is a proper definition of the gradient, e.g., see
[
15
]
for some infinite dimensional setting (Wiener and Poisson spaces). In the multivariate setting here is a small sample of results which can be easily obtained via the techniques developed to this point: Let
m\geq 1
, let
G:\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a smooth function (for the sake of simplicity, just assume differentiability up to the correct order, as above), and let
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{m}
be i.i.d. standard normal random variables. Now, for
k\geq 1
, let

\theta_{k}=\sum_{1\leq i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\leq m}\left(\mathds{E}\left(\frac{\partial^{k}G}{\partial x_{i_{1}}\dots x_{i_{k}}}(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{m})\right)\right)^{2},

and let

\eta_{k}=\sum_{1\leq i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\leq m}\left(\mathds{E}\left(\frac{\partial^{k}G}{\partial x_{i_{1}}\dots x_{i_{k}}}(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{m})\right)^{2}\right).

Let further
(X_{i,j})_{i\in\{1,\dots,m\},j\in\{1,\dots,n\}}
be independent Rademacher random variables and let

S(X_{1,1},\dots,X_{m,n}):=G\left(\frac{X_{1,1}+\dots+X_{1,n}}{\sqrt{n}},\dots,\frac{X_{m,1}+\dots+X_{m,n}}{\sqrt{n}}\right).

Then for all
k\geq 1
,

J_{k}(n)\xrightarrow[n\to+\infty]{}\eta_{k}\quad\text{and}\quad K_{k}(n)\xrightarrow[n\to+\infty]{}\theta_{k}.

Moreover, for all
k\geq 1
,

\frac{\theta_{k+1}}{(k+1)!}\leq(-1)^{k}\left({\textrm{Var}}\,G(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{m})-\eta_{1}+\frac{\eta_{2}}{2}-\dots+(-1)^{k}\frac{\eta_{k}}{k!}\right)\leq\frac{\eta_{k+1}}{(k+1)!}.

\frac{\theta_{k+1}}{(k+1)!}\leq{\textrm{Var}}\,G(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{m})-\theta_{1}-\frac{\theta_{2}}{2}-\dots-\frac{\theta_{k}}{k!}\leq\frac{\eta_{k+1}}{(k+1)!}.

Remark 3.4
.

It is well known that if
Z_{1},Z_{2},\dots,Z_{m}
are iid standard normal random variables, and if

\|Z\|_{2}^{2}:=\sum_{k=1}^{m}Z_{k}^{2}
, then
\left(Z_{1}/\|Z\|_{2},\dots,Z_{m}/\|Z\|_{2}\right)
is uniformly distributed on the
m-1
-dimensional unit sphere. Therefore, the above multivariate Gaussian case allows to recover and extend various variance bounds and covariance representations on the high-dimensional sphere.

3.2
The infinitely divisible case

Let
Y
be an infinitely divisible real-valued random variable, and
G:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a smooth function such that its derivatives of all order are well defined and
\mathds{E}\left(G^{(k)}(Y)\right)^{2}<+\infty
for all
k\geq 0
. We are interested in the decomposition of the variance of
G(Y)
.

We let
(Y_{t})_{t\geq 1}
be the corresponding Lévy process (i.e.
Y_{1}
has the same distribution as
Y
), we denote by
(b,\sigma,\nu)
its generator (from the Lévy–Khintchine representation), and let
(Y^{\prime}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
,
(Y^{\prime\prime}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be independent copies of
(Y_{t})_{t\geq 0}
. For
1\leq{\ell},m
, let

\displaystyle X_{{\ell},m}

\displaystyle=Y_{{\ell}/m}-Y_{({\ell}-1)/m},

(3.7)

\displaystyle X^{\prime}_{{\ell},m}

\displaystyle=Y^{\prime}_{{\ell}/m}-Y^{\prime}_{({\ell}-1)/m},

(3.8)

and let

S_{n}=G(X_{1,n}+\dots+X_{n,n}).

(3.9)

We now study, for any fixed
\alpha\in(0,1)
, the limit when
m
goes to infinity of
nB_{\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor}
(the
B_{k}
’s of
S_{n}
) where
n=2^{m}+1
, which allows us to recover in another way the representation of the variance from
[
16
]
.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Then with the notations above,

2^{m}B_{\lfloor\alpha(2^{m})\rfloor+1}\xrightarrow[m\to\infty]{}\mathds{E}\left(\sigma G^{\prime}(Y_{\alpha}+Y^{\prime\prime}_{1-\alpha})G^{\prime}(Y^{\prime}_{\alpha}+Y^{\prime\prime}_{1-\alpha})+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Delta_{u}G(Y_{\alpha}+Y^{\prime\prime}_{1-\alpha})\Delta_{u}G(Y^{\prime}_{\alpha}+Y^{\prime\prime}_{1-\alpha})\mathrm{d}\nu\right),

(3.10)

where
\Delta_{u}G(x)=G(x+u)-G(x)
.

Proof.

We first prove this fact for
\alpha
a dyadic rational number,
\alpha=a/2^{b}\in(0,1)
. Let
m\geq b
and
n=2^{m}
, with
m\geq b
. The proof is more convenient to write for a slightly different function: instead of computing the
B_{k}
’s of
S_{n}
(
n=2^{m}
), we compute the
B_{k}
’s of

T_{n}:=G(X_{1,n}+\dots+X_{n,n}+X_{n+1,n}).

(3.11)

Since the difference between the former and latest has order
\mathcal{O}(1/n)
, this is enough to get the desired result. We have

\displaystyle B_{\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor+1}

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}\resizebox{20348790}{}{$\left(G\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}X_{i,n}\right)\left(G\left(X_{1}+\sum_{i=2}^{2^{m-b}a+1}X^{\prime}_{i,n}+\sum_{i=2^{m-b}a+2}^{n+1}X_{i,n}\right)-G\left(\sum_{i=1}^{2^{m-b}a+1}X^{\prime}_{i,n}+\sum_{i=2^{m-b}a+2}^{n+1}X_{i,n}\right)\right)\right)$}

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}\left(G(Z_{1/n}+Y^{\prime}_{\alpha}+Y^{\prime\prime}_{1-\alpha})\left(G(Z_{1/n}+Y_{\alpha}+Y^{\prime\prime}_{1-\alpha})-G(Z^{\prime}_{1/n}+Y^{\prime}_{\alpha}+Y^{\prime\prime}_{1-\alpha})\right)\right),

where
Z
and
Z^{\prime}
are two independent copies of
Y
, since
(Z_{1/n},Z^{\prime}_{1/n},Y_{\alpha},Y^{\prime}_{\alpha},Y^{\prime\prime}_{1-\alpha})
has the same distribution as
(X_{1},X^{\prime}_{1},\sum_{i=2}^{2^{m-b}a+1}X_{i,n},\sum_{i=2}^{2^{m-b}a+1}X^{\prime}_{i,n},\sum_{i=2^{m-b}a+2}^{n+1}X_{i,n})
.

Let
G_{\alpha,1}(\cdot)=\mathds{E}(G(\cdot+Y_{\alpha}+Y^{\prime\prime}_{\alpha})|Y_{\alpha},Y^{\prime\prime}_{1-\alpha})
and
G_{\alpha,2}(\cdot)=\mathds{E}(G(\cdot+Y_{\alpha}+Y^{\prime\prime}_{\alpha})|Y^{\prime}_{\alpha},Y^{\prime\prime}_{1-\alpha})
, we then have

\displaystyle B_{\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor+1}

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}\left(G_{\alpha,1}(Z_{1/n})G_{\alpha,2}(Z_{1/n})-G_{\alpha,1}(0)G_{\alpha,2}(0)-(G_{\alpha,1}(Z_{1/n})G_{\alpha,2}(Z^{\prime}_{1/n})-G_{\alpha,1}(0)G_{\alpha,2}(0))\right),

so if
A_{1}
be the infinitesimal generator of
(Y_{t},Y_{t})_{t\geq 0}
and
A_{0}
is the infinitesimal generator of
(Y_{t},Y^{\prime}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, then

\displaystyle nB_{\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor+1}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}

\displaystyle\,\mathds{E}\left((A_{1}-A_{0})G_{\alpha,1}\otimes G_{\alpha,2}(0,0)\right)

(3.12)

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}\left(\sigma G^{\prime}(Y_{\alpha}+Y^{\prime\prime}_{1-\alpha})G^{\prime}(Y^{\prime}_{\alpha}+Y^{\prime\prime}_{1-\alpha})+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Delta_{u}G(Y_{\alpha}+Y^{\prime\prime}_{1-\alpha})\Delta_{u}G(Y^{\prime}_{\alpha}+Y^{\prime\prime}_{1-\alpha})\mathrm{d}\nu\right),

(3.13)

since
(A_{1}-A_{0})(f\otimes g)(0,0)=\sigma f^{\prime}(0)g^{\prime}(0)+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Delta_{u}f(0)\Delta_{u}g(0)\mathrm{d}\nu
. (This computation is in
[
16
, Proposition 2]
.)

Since the finite sequence
B_{k}
is non-decreasing, a routine density argument shows that for any
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
nB_{\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor}
has limit (
3.13
), which is the desired result.
∎

Corollary 3.6
.

{\textrm{Var}}\,G(Y)=\int_{0}^{1}\mathds{E}\left(\sigma G^{\prime}(Y_{\alpha}+Y^{\prime\prime}_{1-\alpha})G^{\prime}(Y^{\prime}_{\alpha}+Y^{\prime\prime}_{1-\alpha})+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Delta_{u}G(Y_{\alpha}+Y^{\prime\prime}_{1-\alpha})\Delta_{u}G(Y^{\prime}_{\alpha}+Y^{\prime\prime}_{1-\alpha})\mathrm{d}\nu\right)\mathrm{d}\alpha.

(3.14)

This above representation of the variance stems from the decomposition
{\textrm{Var}}\,G(Y)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}B_{k}
and it can also be found, with a different approach, in

[
16
]
. Although we have only been concerned with representations of the variance, similar representations continue to hold for covariances in the spirit of the work just cited.

For example, we note that in the Poisson case, the limit (
3.13
) is simply

\mathds{E}\left(DG(Y_{\alpha}+Y^{\prime\prime}_{1-\alpha})DG(Y^{\prime}_{\alpha}+Y^{\prime\prime}_{1-\alpha})\right),

(3.15)

where
DG(x)=G(x+1)-G(x)
,
Y_{\alpha},Y^{\prime}_{\alpha},Y^{\prime\prime}_{1-\alpha}
are Poisson distributed independent random variables (with respective
parameter
\alpha
,
\alpha
, and
1-\alpha
). In the Gaussian case, it is

\mathds{E}\left(G^{\prime}(Z_{1,\alpha})G^{\prime}(Z_{2,\alpha})\right),

(3.16)

where
Z_{1,\alpha}
,
Z_{2,\alpha}
are Gaussian random variables centered with variance one and covariance
1-\alpha
.

3.3
A weaker Talagrand
L_{1}-L_{2}
inequality

Let us focus on the special case where the
X_{i}
’s are Bernoulli with parameter
1/2
. For
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, let

\tau_{i}S(X_{1},\dots,X_{n}):=S(X_{1},\dots,X_{i-1},0,X_{i+1},\dots,X_{n})-S(X_{1},\dots,X_{i-1},1,X_{i+1},\dots,X_{n})

(so this does not depend on
X_{i}
). Then, Talagrand’s
L_{1}-L_{2}
inequality (
[
22
, Theorem 1.5]
) can be stated as follows:

Theorem 3.7
.

There exists
C>0
such that for any function
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, the following inequality holds

{\textrm{Var}}\,S\leq C\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\|\tau_{i}S\|_{2}^{2}}{1+\log\left(\frac{\|\tau_{i}S\|_{2}}{\|\tau_{i}S\|_{1}}\right)},

(3.17)

where
S=f(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
.

We now prove a weaker form of this inequality using the
B_{k}^{\prime}s
, in the special case where there exists
a>0
such that for all
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
,
|\tau_{i}S|\in\{0,a\}
. We can further assume without loss of generality by rescaling that
a=1
. Note that this particular case includes
LC_{n}
(changing a letter can only change
LC_{n}
by at most one).

Firstly, conditioning on whether
X_{i_{k}}=X^{\prime}_{i_{k}}
or
X_{i_{k}}\neq X^{\prime}_{i_{k}}
, we can rewrite (
1.5
) as

B_{k}=\mathds{E}\frac{1}{4n!}\sum_{i\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}(\tau_{i_{k}}S)(\tau_{i_{k}}S)^{i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1}},

(3.18)

so

\displaystyle{\textrm{Var}}\,S

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathds{E}\frac{1}{4n!}\sum_{i\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}(\tau_{i_{k}}S)(\tau_{i_{k}}S)^{i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4n!}\sum_{i\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathds{E}(\tau_{i_{1}}S)(\tau_{i_{1}}S)^{i_{2},\dots,i_{k}}.

Let us fix
i\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
and bound
\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathds{E}(\tau_{i_{1}}S)(\tau_{i_{1}}S)^{i_{2},\dots,i_{k}}
. For ease of notation, by reindexing the
X_{i}
’s, we may assume
i=Id
, also, let us write
X:=(X_{2},\dots,X_{n})
. Since, by assumption,
\tau_{1}S
is boolean, there exists
m\leq 2^{n-1}
and
x^{1},\dots,x^{m}\in\{0,1\}^{n-1}
pairwise distinct such that
|\tau_{1}S|=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathds{1}_{X=x^{i}}
.
Let, for
\alpha\subset\{2,\dots,n\}
,
N(\alpha):=|\{(i,j)\in\{1,\dots,m\}^{2}:\forall k\in\alpha,x^{i}_{k}=x^{j}_{k}\}|
. We have

\mathds{E}(\tau_{1}S)(\tau_{1}S)^{2,\dots,k}\leq\mathds{E}|\tau_{1}S||\tau_{1}S|^{2,\dots,k}=\frac{N(\{k+1,\dots,n\})}{2^{n+k-2}}.

(3.19)

Let
{\ell}\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
be such that
2^{{\ell}-1}\leq m\leq 2^{\ell}
(we may exclude the trivial case
m=0
). Using that for any
k\in\{1,\dots,{\ell}\}
,
N(\{k+1,\dots,n\})\leq m2^{k-1}
, and the trivial bound
N(\{k+1,\dots,n\})\leq m^{2}
when
k>{\ell}
, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathds{E}(\tau_{1}S)(\tau_{1}S)^{2,\dots,k}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\frac{m}{2^{n-1}}+\sum_{k=1+1}^{n}\frac{m^{2}}{2^{n+k-2}}

\displaystyle\leq{\ell}\frac{m}{2^{n-1}}+2\frac{m^{2}}{2^{n-1+{\ell}}}

\displaystyle\leq({\ell}+2)\frac{m}{2^{n-1}}=({\ell}+2)\|\tau_{1}S\|_{2}^{2}.

Note that
\log\left(\frac{\|\tau_{1}S\|_{2}}{\|\tau_{1}S\|_{1}}\right)=\log\left(\sqrt{\frac{2^{n-1}}{m}}\right)={\log(2)}(n-1-\log_{2}(m))/2
so
{\ell}+2\leq n+2-\frac{2}{\log(2)}\log\left(\frac{\|\tau_{1}S\|_{2}}{\|\tau_{1}S\|_{1}}\right)
hence

\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathds{E}(\tau_{1}S)(\tau_{1}S)^{2,\dots,k}\leq\left(n+2-\frac{2}{\log(2)}\log\left(\frac{\|\tau_{1}S\|_{2}}{\|\tau_{1}S\|_{1}}\right)\right)\|\tau_{1}S\|_{2}^{2}.

(3.20)

Finally,

\displaystyle{\textrm{Var}}\,S

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4n!}\sum_{i\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathds{E}(\tau_{i_{1}}S)(\tau_{i_{1}}S)^{i_{2},\dots,i_{k}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4n!}\sum_{i\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\left(n+2-\frac{2}{\log(2)}\log\left(\frac{\|\tau_{i_{1}}S\|_{2}}{\|\tau_{i_{1}}S\|_{1}}\right)\right)\|\tau_{i_{1}}S\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(1+\frac{2}{n}-\frac{2}{n\log(2)}\log\left(\frac{\|\tau_{j}S\|_{2}}{\|\tau_{j}S\|_{1}}\right)\right)\|\tau_{j}S\|_{2}^{2}.

To see that it is weaker than Talagrand’s
L_{1}-L_{2}
inequality, consider for example
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
independent Bernoulli variables of parameter
1/2
, and
S
defined on
\{0,1\}^{n}
by
S(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}):=x_{1}\dots x_{n/2}
(assuming
n
is even). Then, for any
j\in\{1,\dots,n/2\}
,
\|\tau_{j}S\|_{1}=(1/2)^{\frac{n}{2}-1}
and
\|\tau_{j}S\|_{2}=\sqrt{\|\tau_{j}S\|_{1}}
. So on the one hand, Talagrand’s inequality gives a bound of order
(1/2)^{\frac{n}{2}-1}
, which is optimal, while on the other hand, our weaker bound gives an upper bound of order
n(1/2)^{\frac{n}{2}-1}
.

4
On the variance of the length of longest common subsequences

To finish these notes, we present some applications of the above inequalities to subsequences problems, in particular to lower-bounding the variance of the length of the longest common subsequences between two random words. For
(x_{1},\dots,x_{s})
,
(y_{1},\dots,y_{t})
two sequences taking values in a finite set
\mathcal{A}
, we denote by
LCS(x_{1}\cdots x_{s};y_{1}\cdots y_{t})
the largest integer
k
such that there exists
1\leq i_{1}<\dots<i_{k}\leq s
,
1\leq j_{1}<\dots<j_{k}\leq t
satisfying
a_{i_{1}}=b_{j_{1}},\dots,a_{i_{k}}=b_{j_{k}}
, or
0
if there is no such integer. In the sequel, we take
\mathcal{A}=\{1,\dots,m\}
(for some
m
we specify in each case),
X_{1},\dots,X_{n},\dots Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n},\dots
i.i.d. random variables taking values in
\mathcal{A}
(according to a distribution we specify), and consider the length of the longest common subsequences of these two random words, written
LCS(X_{1}\cdots X_{n};Y_{1}\cdots Y_{n})
or simply
LC_{n}
.

4.1
A generic upper bound

Via the Efron-Stein inequality, the upper bound
{\textrm{Var}}\,S\leq nB_{1}
was already obtained
[
21
]
. Let us study the variance of
LC_{n}
with our approach.

Let now
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{2n}
be
i.i.d.
Bernoulli random variables of parameter
1/2
, and consider the
B_{k}
’s of the function
S(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{2n}):=LCS(Z_{1}\cdots Z_{2n})=LC_{n}
. We know that
{\textrm{Var}}\,LC_{n}\leq 2nB_{1}(2n)
. Using (
3.18
),
B_{1}(2n)\leq 1/4
so
{\textrm{Var}}\,LC_{n}\leq n/2
(at result already known to
[
21
]
. But this last bound can be slightly improved: note that by symmetry of the zeros and ones in
LC_{n}
(that is, if
\bar{Z}_{i}:=1-Z_{i},i\in\{1,\dots,2n\}
,
S(Z)=S(\bar{Z})
),
\mathds{E}\tau_{i}S
=0 so
B_{2n}(2n)=0
. By convexity,
B_{1}(2n)+\dots+B_{2n}(2n)\leq 2n(B_{1}(2n)+B_{2n}(2n))/2
, so
{\textrm{Var}}\,LC_{n}\leq n/4
.

In case of an alphabet
\{1,\dots,m\}
, conditioning on
X_{i}\neq X^{\prime}_{i}
we get
B_{1}(2n)\leq\left(1-\sum_{k=1}^{m}p_{k}^{2}\right)/2
, and when additionally
B_{2n}(2n)=0
, then
{\textrm{Var}}\,LC_{n}\leq\left(1-\sum_{k=1}^{m}p_{k}^{2}\right)n/2
, which improves, by a factor of two, on the upper bound obtained in
[
21
]
(see also
[
13
]
). The condition
B_{2n}(2n)=0
is realized, for instance, when
p_{1}=\dots=p_{m}=1/m
(by symmetry).

In the remaining part of this manuscript, we focus on various lower bounds on the variance of
LC_{n}
.
Since by Theorem
1.2
,
(B_{k})_{1\leq k\leq 2n}
is, in particular, non-decreasing,

{\textrm{Var}}\,LC_{n}\geq 2nB_{2n},

(4.1)

and this last inequality we will of use throughout this section. As a first instance,
[
19
, Theorem 2.1]
provides a lower bound on
the variance of
LC_{n}
, in the Bernoulli case, proving that when
p
is smaller than some universal (but unspecified and extremely small) constant, the variance is of order
n
, see also
[
13
]
for more explicit bounds. To obtain this results, the authors of
[
19
]
first show Theorem 2.2 there, and then prove that it implies a variance lower bound of order
n
. The proof of this implication is long and we aim to show that the jackknives tools we developed greatly simplifies it. (This methodology also simplifies, in the quadratic case, the implication of
[
13
, Theorem 2.1]
towards the linear lower bound.) With our approach, we also generalize results for the case where one letter is omitted. We also proceed to prove, in the binary case, another slightly weaker bound: for some
p_{1}\in(0.096,0.5)
(so not as small as in
[
19
]
or
[
13
]
), or
p_{2}\in(0.5,0.904)
,
\limsup_{n\to+\infty}{\textrm{Var}}\,LC_{n}/n>0
. Finally, we give further partial results on the order of the variance in the uniform case.

4.2
On the order of the variance under a hypothesis on a modification of
LC_{n}

In this section we prove how Theorem 2.2 in
[
19
]
or Theorem 2.1 in
[
13
]
imply their main theorem, namely the linear lower order of the variance. This shows how the use
of the
B_{k}
’s greatly simplify some proofs, and it is of interest to infer, more generally, a lower bound on the variance from a random perturbation that has an effect on the expectation. More specifically, here, the random perturbation is to pick, in the binary case, a random
1
from the letters (if there is at least one), and to turn it into a
0
. The original letters are denoted by
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{2n}
, the new letters (with a
1
turned into a
0
) by
\tilde{Z_{1}},\dots,{\tilde{Z}}_{2n}
. We refer to
[
19
]
and
[
13
]
for a more formal definition of
\tilde{Z}
. Theorem 2.2/Theorem 2.1 there implies, in particular, that for any
\delta\in(0,\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2})
, where
\alpha_{1}
,
\alpha_{2}
are constants defined there such that
\alpha_{1}>\alpha_{2}
, for
n
large enough,

\mathds{E}\left(LCS(Z)-LCS(\tilde{Z})\right)\geq\delta.

From this, it is natural to try to prove that
B_{2n}(2n)
is greater than some absolute constant, to infer that the variance has linear order. Let, for all
z\in\{0,1\}^{2n}
,
x\in\{0,1\}
and
k\in\{1,\dots,2n\}
,
z^{k,x}:=(z_{1},\dots,z_{k-1},x,z_{k+1},\dots,z_{2n})
. Consider the modifications of
Z
,
Z^{N,1}
and
Z^{N,0}
, with
N
picked in
\{1,\dots,n\}
uniformly. Intuitively, this is "almost" like the previous pair
(Z,\tilde{Z})
. But it is easier to write
B_{2n}(2n)
in terms of
\mathds{E}\left(LCS(Z^{N,1})-LCS(Z^{N,0}\right)
. Indeed, we have

\displaystyle B_{2n}(2n)

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}\frac{1}{2(2n)!}\sum_{i\in\mathfrak{S}_{2n}}(S-S^{i_{2n}})(S^{i_{1},\dots,i_{2n-1}}-S^{i_{1},\dots,i_{2n}})

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}\frac{1}{2(2n)}\sum_{k=1}^{2n}(S-S^{k})(S^{\{1,\dots,2n\}\setminus\{k\}}-S^{\{1,\dots,2n\}}),

conditioning on
(Z_{i_{2n}},Z^{\prime}_{i_{2n}})
(first term when its
(0,1)
, second term
(1,0)
, the other terms are null) we get

\displaystyle B_{2n}(2n)

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}\frac{1}{2(2n)}\sum_{k=1}^{2n}\left(LCS(Z^{k,0})-LCS(Z^{k,1})\right)\left(LCS(Z^{\prime k,0})-LCS(Z^{\prime k,1})\right)p(1-p)

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\mathds{E}\frac{1}{2(2n)}\sum_{k=1}^{2n}\left(LCS(Z^{k,1})-LCS(Z^{k,0})\right)\left(LCS(Z^{\prime k,1})-LCS(Z^{\prime k,0})\right)p(1-p),

and by independence,

\displaystyle B_{2n}(2n)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{k=1}^{2n}\left(\mathds{E}\left(LCS(Z^{k,1})-LCS(Z^{k,0})\right)\right)^{2}p(1-p),

so by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

B_{2n}(2n)\geq\left(\mathds{E}\left(LCS(Z^{N,1})-LCS(Z^{N,0})\right)\right)^{2}p(1-p).

(4.2)

We next provide a lower bound on
\mathds{E}\left(LCS(Z^{N,0})-LCS(Z^{N,1})\right)
. First note that if
N_{1}
denotes the number of
ones, for any
{\ell}\in\{1,\dots,2n\}
,
(Z^{N,1},Z^{N,0})
conditionally on
N_{1}(Z^{N,1})={\ell}
has the same distribution as
(Z,\tilde{Z})
conditionally
on
N_{1}(Z)={\ell}
. Indeed, this is the uniform distribution on all the possible pairs of
2n
bits, the first one having
k
ones and the second one being identical
except exactly for a
1
turned into a
0
. To simplify the notations, for
{\ell}\in\{0,\dots,2n\}
, let

f({\ell}):=\mathds{E}\left(LCS(Z)-LCS(\tilde{Z})|N_{1}(Z)\right).

We have

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left(LCS(Z)-LCS(\tilde{Z})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\ell}=1}^{2n}f({\ell})\mathds{P}(N_{1}(Z)={\ell})=\mathds{E}\left(f(N_{1}(Z))\right),

while, since
f(0)=0
,

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left(LCS(Z^{N,1})-LCS(Z^{N,0})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\ell}=1}^{2n}\mathds{E}\left(LCS(Z^{N,1})-LCS(Z^{N,0})|N_{1}(Z)\right)\mathds{P}(N_{1}(Z^{N,1})={\ell})

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\ell}=1}^{2n}f({\ell})p^{{\ell}-1}(1-p)^{n-{\ell}}{n-1\choose{\ell}-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\ell}=1}^{2n}f({\ell})\frac{{\ell}}{pn}\mathds{P}(N_{1}(Z)={\ell})

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}\left(f(N_{1}(Z))\frac{N_{1}(Z)}{pn}\right),

so by dominated convergence,

\mathds{E}\left(LCS(Z^{N,1})-LCS(Z^{N,0})\right)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\mathds{E}\left(LCS(Z)-LCS(\tilde{Z})\right).

Therefore, for any
\delta\in(0,\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2})
, for
n
large enough,

\mathds{E}\left(LCS(Z^{N,1})-LCS(Z^{N,0})\right)\geq\delta,

and using (
4.1
) and (
4.2
),

\frac{{\textrm{Var}}\,LC_{n}}{n}\geq 2p(1-p)\delta^{2}.

4.3
On the order of the variance when one letter is omitted

Let the letters
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
be drawn from an alphabet
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m+1}
and
the letters
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
drawn from an alphabet
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m}
: so
\alpha_{m+1}
is an omitted letter, not belonging to
any longest common subsequence. (Let
m>1
, as the case
m=1
is trivial and may be dealt with separately.) Let
p:=\mathds{P}(X_{i}=\alpha_{m+1})>0
, and in contrast to the binary-ternary case
[
1
]
or
[
12
]
, no longer assume that the other letters are equiprobable and let

p_{X,1}:=\mathds{P}(X_{i}=\alpha_{1}),\dots,p_{X,m}:=\mathds{P}(X_{i}=\alpha_{m}),p_{Y,1}:=\mathds{P}(Y_{i}=\alpha_{1}),\dots,p_{Y,m}:=\mathds{P}(Y_{i}=\alpha_{m})
. Since

{\textrm{Var}}\,LC_{n}\geq 2nB_{2n}(2n),

(4.3)

it is therefore enough to find a constant lower bound on
B_{2n}(2n)
. Firstly, by conditioning,

\displaystyle B_{2n}(2n)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4n}\sum_{j=1}^{2n}\mathds{E}\left(\Delta_{j}LC_{n}(\Delta_{j}LC_{n})^{1,\dots,j-1,j+1,\dots,n}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4n}\sum_{j=1}^{2n}\sum_{i,i^{\prime}=1}^{m}\left(\mathds{E}\Delta_{j}LC_{n}^{Z_{j}=\alpha_{i},Z^{\prime}_{j}=\alpha_{i^{\prime}}}\right)^{2}\mathds{P}(Z_{j}=\alpha_{i})\mathds{P}(Z^{\prime}_{j}=\alpha_{i^{\prime}})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{4n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\mathds{E}\Delta_{j}LC_{n}^{X_{j}=\alpha_{i},X^{\prime}_{j}=\alpha_{m+1}}\right)^{2}p_{X,i}p

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{4n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathds{E}\Delta_{j}LC_{n}^{X_{j}=\alpha_{i},X^{\prime}_{j}=\alpha_{m+1}}p_{X,i}\right)^{2}p.

Letting
LC_{n-1,n}:=LCS(X_{1}\cdots X_{n-1};Y_{1}\cdots Y_{n})
, we have for any
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
,

\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathds{E}\Delta_{j}LC_{n}^{X_{j}=\alpha_{i},X^{\prime}_{j}=\alpha_{m+1}}p_{X,i}=\mathds{E}(LC_{n})-\mathds{E}(LC_{n-1,n}),

and so,

B_{2n}(2n)\geq\frac{1}{4}\left(\mathds{E}(LC_{n})-\mathds{E}(LC_{n-1,n})\right)p.

(4.4)

Let
(\pi,\eta)
be the alignment of
(X_{1},\dots,X_{n-1}),(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n})
which is minimal for the lexicographic order, so
(\pi,\eta)
is well defined as a (measurable) function of
X_{1},\dots,X_{n-1},Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
. Let
F_{n}
be the event "
\eta_{LC_{n}}<n
", in other words,
Y_{n}
does not contribute to the longest common subsequences, then
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{n}LC_{n}^{X_{n}=\alpha_{i},X^{\prime}_{n}=\alpha_{m+1}}\geq\mathds{1}_{F_{n}}
, hence

\mathds{E}(LC_{n})-\mathds{E}(LC_{n-1,n})\geq p_{X,\min}\mathds{P}(F_{n}),

(4.5)

where
p_{X,\min}:=\min_{1\leq i\leq m}p_{X,i}
.

We now combine this bound with some elements already present in
[
12
]
(with its notations). Let
V_{1}=\pi_{1}-1,V_{2}=\pi_{2}-\pi_{1}-1,\dots,V_{LC_{n}}=\pi_{LC_{n}}-\pi_{LC_{n}-1}-1
, and let
M
be the number of indices
i
such that
V_{i}>0
. In terms of
[
12
]
,
M
is the number of nonempty matches (except that there is also the term
V_{1}
). We denote by
I_{i,j}
the event: "inserting
\alpha_{i}
at the
j
-th position in
(X_{1},\dots,X_{n-1}),(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n})
increases the longest common subsequence". Observe that

\displaystyle\mathds{E}(LC_{n})-\mathds{E}(LC_{n-1,n})

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}\left(LCS(X_{1}\cdots X_{j-1}X^{\prime}_{1}X_{j}\cdots X_{n-1};Y_{1}\cdots,Y_{n})-LCS(X_{1}\cdots X_{n-1};Y_{1}\cdots Y_{n})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{X,i}\mathds{P}(I_{i,j})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{X,i}\mathds{P}(I_{i,j})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{p_{X,\min}}{n}\mathds{E}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{m}I_{i,j}

\displaystyle\geq p_{X,\min}\frac{\mathds{E}M}{n}.

(4.6)

From (
4.5
) and (
4.3
), we get

\mathds{E}(LC_{n})-\mathds{E}(LC_{n-1,n})\geq\frac{p_{X,\min}}{2}\left(\mathds{P}(F_{n})+\frac{\mathds{E}M}{n}\right).

(4.7)

Let
\gamma^{*}
be the limit of
\mathds{E}(LC_{n})/n
, we have
\gamma^{*}\leq 1-p<1
, i.e.,
0<p<1-\gamma^{*}
. Fix
k_{0}>0
such that

\sum_{k>k_{0}}mk(1-p_{Y,\min})^{k}\leq\frac{1-\gamma^{*}}{2}.

When
F_{n}
does not hold, that is, when
\pi_{n}=LC_{n}
, we have

\sum_{i=1}^{LC_{n}}V_{i}=n-LC_{n},

so

\mathds{E}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{LC_{n}}V_{i}\right)\geq\mathds{E}\left((n-LC_{n})\mathds{1}_{F_{n}^{c}}\right)\geq\mathds{E}\left(n-LC_{n}\right)-\mathds{P}(F_{n})n\geq(1-\gamma^{*})n-\mathds{P}(F_{n})n.

Moreover,

k_{0}\mathds{E}M\geq\mathds{E}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{LC_{n}}V_{i}\mathds{1}_{V_{i}\leq k_{0}}\right).

On the other hand,
(\pi,\eta)
is minimal, so any unmatched gap has (at least) a letter of the alphabet
which is not used, namely, the letter used in the next match.
Therefore the average number of indices
i
such that
V_{i}=k
is no more than
nm(1-p_{Y,\min})^{k}
, and

\mathds{E}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{LC_{n}}V_{i}\mathds{1}_{V_{i}>k_{0}}\right)\leq n\sum_{k>k_{0}}mk(1-p_{Y,\min})^{k}\leq\frac{1-\gamma^{*}}{2}n.

Finally we get

k_{0}\mathds{E}M\geq\frac{1-\gamma^{*}}{2}n-\mathds{P}(F_{n})n,

and

\mathds{P}(F_{n})+\frac{\mathds{E}M}{n}\geq\frac{k_{0}\mathds{E}M+\mathds{P}(F_{n})n}{k_{0}n}\geq\frac{1-\gamma^{*}}{2k_{0}},

so putting it together with (
4.3
), (
4.4
) and (
4.7
), we get

{\textrm{Var}}\,LC_{n}\geq\frac{pp_{X,\min}(1-\gamma^{*})}{8k_{0}}n.

4.4
A weaker kind of lower bound

Let us return to the Bernoulli framework with parameter
0<p<1
, and let
\gamma_{n}(p)=\mathds{E}LC_{n}/n
and
\gamma(p)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\gamma_{n}(p)
. It seems reasonable to expect that
{\textrm{Var}}\,LC_{n}/n
converges when
n
tends to infinity, but, so far, unfortunately a proof of this result has been elusive. To the best of our knowledge, it is still an open problem to determine whether or not, in the uniform case, the variance tends to infinity. The function
\gamma
is clearly symmetric around
1/2
, and it is expected to be strictly convex with a minimum at
1/2
, but besides numerical simulations there is no proof of this fact yet. The goal of this section is to prove:

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
p_{0}\in(0,1/2)
be such that
\gamma(p_{0})>\gamma(1/2)
. Then there exists
p_{1}\in(p_{0},1/2)
such that when
p=p_{1}
,

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{{\textrm{Var}}\,LC_{n}}{n}\geq 2p_{0}(1-p_{0})\left(\frac{\gamma(p_{0})-\gamma(1/2)}{1/2-p_{0}}\right)^{2}.

Remark 4.2
.

Using the bound
\gamma(1/2)<0.8263
from
[
20
]
, and since
\gamma(p)\geq p^{2}+(1-p)^{2}
, we can apply the above
theorem with
p_{0}=0.096
, to get for some
p_{1}\in(0.096,0.5),\limsup_{n\to\infty}{\textrm{Var}}\,LC_{n}/n\geq 1.8/10^{8}
. Clearly, by symmetry, this limsup result is also valid for some
p_{2}=1-p_{1}\in(0.5,0.904)
.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\gamma_{n}(p_{0})-\gamma_{n}(1/2)=-\int_{p_{0}}^{1/2}\frac{\mathrm{d}\gamma_{n}}{\mathrm{d}p}(p)\mathrm{d}p=\int_{p_{0}}^{1/2}\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{k=1}^{2n}\mathds{E}_{p}\left(LC_{n}^{k,0}-LC_{n}^{k,1}\right)\mathrm{d}p,

using a Russo-Margulis type formula. This is not strictly the Russo-Margulis lemma since
LC_{n}
is not monotone, but the proof of this version is
elementary: as in
[
8
]
, we rewrite
\gamma_{n}
as a function of
2n
parameters, the parameters of each letter (Bernoulli random variables):

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\gamma_{n}}{\mathrm{d}p}(p)=\frac{\mathrm{d}\gamma_{n}}{\mathrm{d}p}(p,p,\dots,p)=\sum_{k=1}^{2n}\frac{\mathrm{d}\gamma_{n}}{\mathrm{d}p_{k}}(p,p,\dots,p),

which yields the result. Hence,

\displaystyle\gamma(p_{0})-\gamma(1/2)

\displaystyle=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\gamma_{n}(p_{0})-\gamma_{n}(1/2)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{p_{0}}^{1/2}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{k=1}^{2n}\mathds{E}_{p}\left(LC_{n}^{k,0}-LC_{n}^{k,1}\right)\mathrm{d}p,

so there exists
p_{1}\in(p_{0},1/2)
such that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{k=1}^{2n}\mathds{E}_{p_{1}}\left(LC_{n}^{k,0}-LC_{n}^{k,1}\right)\geq\frac{\gamma(p_{0})-\gamma(1/2)}{1/2-p_{0}}.

Let us fix
p=p_{1}
. As seen previously,

\displaystyle B_{2n}(2n)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{k=1}^{2n}\left(\mathds{E}\left(LC_{n}(Z^{k,0})-LC_{n}(Z^{k,1})\right)\right)^{2}p_{1}(1-p_{1}),

so

\displaystyle{\textrm{Var}}\,LC_{n}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{k=1}^{2n}\left(\mathds{E}\left(LC_{n}(Z^{k,0})-LC_{n}(Z^{k,1})\right)\right)^{2}p_{0}(1-p_{0})

\displaystyle\geq 2n\left(\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{k=1}^{2n}\mathds{E}_{p_{1}}\left(LC_{n}^{k,0}-LC_{n}^{k,1}\right)\right)^{2}p_{0}(1-p_{0}),

and finally

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{{\textrm{Var}}\,LC_{n}}{n}

\displaystyle\geq 2p_{0}(1-p_{0})\left(\frac{\gamma(p_{0})-\gamma(1/2)}{1/2-p_{0}}\right)^{2}.

∎

Remark 4.3
.

As already mentioned, it is expected that the function
\gamma
is strictly convex, but even
proving that
\gamma
is non-increasing on
[0,1/2]
and non-decreasing on
[1/2,1]
seems to be lacking. It also seems reasonable that for a fixed alphabet, say binary uniform, the sequence
(\mathds{E}LC_{n}/n)_{n\geq 1}
is non-decreasing, but again a proof is lacking.

4.5
On the order of the variance in the binary uniform case

A long-standing open problem is to find the order of the variance of
LC_{n}
when the distribution is uniform. In this section, we focus on the uniform binary case, so
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathds{E}LC_{n}/n=\gamma(1/2):=\gamma_{2}
. We recall, from
[
14
]
, the definition of the function
\widetilde{\gamma}
: for any
p>0
,

\widetilde{\gamma}(p):=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathds{E}\left(LCS(X_{1}\cdots X_{n};Y_{1}\cdots Y_{\lfloor np\rfloor})\right)}{n(1+p)/2}.

(4.8)

By a superadditivity argument, this limit is well defined and
\widetilde{\gamma}
is concave, non-decreasing on
[0,1]
and non-increasing on
[1,+\infty)
(for the details, and further properties of
\widetilde{\gamma}
, we refer the reader to
[
14
]
).

By symmetry, in this case,
B_{2n}(2n)=0
. However, letting
Z_{1}=(X_{1},Y_{1}),Z_{2}=(X_{2},Y_{2}),\dots,Z_{n}=(X_{n},Y_{n})
, then we may see the last
B_{k}
is
B_{n}(n)
(
LC_{n}=LC(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n})
), which can be written as, by conditioning,

\displaystyle B_{n}(n)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathds{E}\left(\Delta_{j}LC_{n}(\Delta_{j}LC_{n})^{1,\dots,j-1,j+1,\dots,n}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{2}}\left(\mathds{E}\Delta_{j}LC_{n}^{Z_{j}=\varepsilon,Z^{\prime}_{j}=\varepsilon^{\prime}}\right)^{2}\mathds{P}(Z_{j}=\varepsilon)\mathds{P}(Z^{\prime}_{j}=\varepsilon^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}8\left(\mathds{E}\Delta_{j}LC_{n}^{Z_{j}=(0,0),Z^{\prime}_{j}=(0,1)}\right)^{2}\mathds{P}(Z_{j}=(0,0))\mathds{P}(Z^{\prime}_{j}=(0,1),

where we used symmetry and the fact that when
\varepsilon^{\prime}=\varepsilon
or
\varepsilon^{\prime}=(1,1)-\varepsilon
,
\mathds{E}\Delta_{j}LC_{n}^{Z_{j}=\varepsilon,Z^{\prime}_{j}=\varepsilon^{\prime}}=0
. Finally,

\displaystyle B_{n}(n)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\mathds{E}\Delta_{j}LC_{n}^{Z_{j}=(0,0),Z^{\prime}_{j}=(0,1)}\right)^{2}

which may also be written

B_{n}(n)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{4}\left(\mathds{E}\left(LCS(Z^{j,(0,0)})-LCS(Z^{j,(0,1)})\right)\right)^{2}.

(4.9)

So it is enough to find a lower bound on this quantity, which is doable for the terms on the edge (
1
or
n
) but seems tricky for the terms in the middle.

We may also fix
b\geq 2
and let
Z_{1}=X_{1},\dots,X_{b}
,
Z_{2}=X_{b+1},\dots,X_{2b}
,
\dots
. In this case, one gets that lower bounding
B_{n}(n)
amounts to finding
w_{1},w_{2}\in\{0,1\}^{b}
and
\delta>0
such that for all
n\geq 1
,

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\mathds{E}\left(LCS(Z^{j,w_{1}})-LCS(Z^{j,w_{2}})\right)\right)^{2}\geq\delta.

(4.10)

For example, intuitively, it is likely to get a larger LCS with
w_{1}=(1,0)
than with
w_{2}=(1,1)
, and with
w_{1}=(1,0,1,0,1)
than with
w_{2}=(1,1,1,1,1)
. Running simulations in Python, Figure
1
seems to indicate that
B_{n}(n)
is lower bounded by a strictly positive constant (which would yield the linearity of the variance).

Figure 1:
B_{n}(n)
for
w_{1}=(1,0),w_{2}=(1,1)
(left), and
w_{1}=(1,0,1,0,1)
,
w_{2}=(1,1,1,1,1)
(right), with the empirical measure over
1000
simulations.

We now pick again
Z_{1}=X_{1},Z_{2}=X_{2},\dots,Z_{2n}=Y_{n}
, and study
B_{1}(2n)
. Note that if
B_{1}(2n)
was converging to zero, this would rule out the possibility of a linear lower bound on the variance. In the following, we study
B_{1}(2n)
, and find that it is lower bounded by a constant.

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n},Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
be independent Bernoulli random variables with parameter
1/2
, and let
\upsilon\leq n
. We may assume, for ease of notations, that
n=\upsilon m
is a multiple of
\upsilon
, but it is not hard to adapt all the following proofs to the general case. Let
\mathcal{R}:=\{\overrightarrow{r}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{m}:1=r_{0}\leq r_{1}\leq\dots\leq r_{m}=n\}
, and, for any
\overrightarrow{r}\in\mathcal{R}
, let

LC_{n}(\overrightarrow{r})=\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}LCS\left(P_{i}\right),

(4.11)

where
P_{i}:=\left((X_{\upsilon i+1},\dots,X_{(\upsilon+1)i}),(Y_{r_{i}},\dots,Y_{r_{i+1}-1})\right)
(with the convention
(Y_{r_{i}},\dots,Y_{r_{i+1}-1})=\emptyset
if
r_{i}=r_{i+1}
). For any
\overrightarrow{r}\in\mathcal{R}
, call
\overrightarrow{r}
an alignment if
LC_{n}=LC_{n}(\overrightarrow{r})
.

Denote by
N_{i}
the number of letters in the cell
P_{i}
, that is
\upsilon+r_{i+1}-r_{i}
. For any
\overrightarrow{r}\in\mathcal{R}
, let
I_{p_{1},p_{2}}(\overrightarrow{r})=\{i\in\{0,\dots,m-1\};r_{i+1}-r_{i}\in[\upsilon p_{1},\upsilon p_{2}]\}
, and
(I_{p_{1},p_{2}}(\overrightarrow{r}))^{c}
its complement in
\{0,\dots,m-1\}
. Next, let
B^{n}_{\varepsilon,p_{1},p_{2}}
be the event that: for any alignment
\overrightarrow{r}
,

\sum_{i\in I_{p_{1},p_{2}}(\overrightarrow{r})}N_{i}\geq\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)2n.

Note that, recalling the notation
A^{n}_{\varepsilon,p_{1},p_{2}}
as defined in
[
14
]
, we have
B^{n}_{\varepsilon,p_{1},p_{2}}\subset A^{n}_{\varepsilon,p_{1},p_{2}}
. Indeed, if
A^{n}_{\varepsilon,p_{1},p_{2}}
is not satisfied, there is an alignment
\overrightarrow{r}
(in
[
14
]
, the definition of an alignment is with strict inequalities rather than our non-strict inequalities, therefore an alignment as defined in
[
14
]
is necessarily also an alignment as defined here) such that the cardinality of
I_{p_{1},p_{2}}(\overrightarrow{r})
is strictly greater than
\varepsilon m
, which implies

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in(I_{p_{1},p_{2}}(\overrightarrow{r}))^{c}}N_{i}

\displaystyle>\nu\varepsilon m

\displaystyle>\varepsilon n,

hence
\sum_{i\in I_{p_{1},p_{2}}(\overrightarrow{r})}N_{i}<2n-\varepsilon n
, indicating that
B^{n}_{\varepsilon,p_{1},p_{2}}
is not satisfied. Hence
B^{n}_{\varepsilon,p_{1},p_{2}}\subset A^{n}_{\varepsilon,p_{1},p_{2}}
, and
\mathds{P}\left(B^{n}_{\varepsilon,p_{1},p_{2}}\right)\leq\mathds{P}\left(A^{n}_{\varepsilon,p_{1},p_{2}}\right)
. Therefore, the following is a strengthening of
[
14
, Theorem 2.2]
(which is the same statement but with
A^{n}_{\varepsilon,p_{1},p_{2}}
in place of
B^{n}_{\varepsilon,p_{1},p_{2}}
).

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
. Let
0<p_{1}<1<p_{2}
be such that
\widetilde{\gamma}(p_{1})<\widetilde{\gamma}(1)={\gamma_{2}}
and
\widetilde{\gamma}(p_{2})<{\gamma_{2}}
and let
\delta\in\left(0,\min({\gamma_{2}}-\widetilde{\gamma}(p_{1}),{\gamma_{2}}-\widetilde{\gamma}(p_{2}))\right)
.
Fix the integer
\upsilon
to be such that
(1+\ln(1+\upsilon))/\upsilon\leq\delta^{2}\varepsilon^{2}/16
, then

\mathds{P}\left(B^{n}_{\varepsilon,p_{1},p_{2}}\right)\geq 1-\exp\left(-n\left(\frac{\delta^{2}\varepsilon^{2}}{16}-\frac{1+\ln(1+\upsilon)}{\upsilon}\right)\right),

for all
n
large enough.

Proof.

Let
\overrightarrow{r}\in\mathcal{R}
be such that
\sum_{i\in(I_{p_{1},p_{2}}(\overrightarrow{r}))^{c}}N_{i}>\varepsilon n
. We first prove that

\mathds{E}\left(LC_{n}(\overrightarrow{r})-LC_{n}\right)\leq-\frac{\delta\varepsilon n}{2},

for all
n
large enough.
We follow the proof of
[
14
, Lemma 3.1]
. Let
\delta^{*}=\min({\gamma_{2}}-\widetilde{\gamma}(p_{1}),{\gamma_{2}}-\widetilde{\gamma}(p_{2}))
. Using the superadditivity of
\widetilde{\gamma}
, we get

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left(LC_{n}(\overrightarrow{r})\right)

\displaystyle\leq{\gamma_{2}}\left(\sum_{i\in I_{p_{1},p_{2}}(\overrightarrow{r})}\frac{N_{i}}{2}\right)+({\gamma_{2}}-\delta^{*})\left(\sum_{i\in(I_{p_{1},p_{2}}(\overrightarrow{r}))^{c}}\frac{N_{i}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left({\gamma_{2}}-\frac{\delta^{*}\varepsilon}{2}\right)n.

Moreover, for
n
large enough,

-\mathds{E}\left(LC_{n}\right)\leq-\left({\gamma_{2}}-\frac{(\delta^{*}-\delta)\varepsilon}{2}\right)n,

so combining together these two inequalities, we get the desired result:

\mathds{E}\left(LC_{n}(\overrightarrow{r})-LC_{n}\right)\leq-\frac{\delta\varepsilon n}{2}.

The end of the proof is exactly as in
[
14
]
, the only difference is, as pointed out in
[
10
, Remark 2.2]
, that the cardinality of
\mathcal{R}
is now
{n+\upsilon\choose\upsilon}
instead of
{n\choose\upsilon}
so
\ln\upsilon
becomes
\ln(1+\upsilon)
.

∎

Theorem 4.5
.

There exists
C>0
such that for all
n
large enough,
B_{1}(2n)\geq C
.

Proof.

For any
\overrightarrow{r}\in\mathcal{R}
, let
S(\overrightarrow{r})=\{i\in\{0,\dots,m-1\};LCS(P_{i})=\min(\upsilon,r_{i+1}-r_{i})\}
the set of the indices of "saturated" cells, meaning that
LCS(P_{i})
is maximal given the size of the cell. We first show that for some
\varepsilon>0
, with high probability, for any alignment
\overrightarrow{r}
,
|S(\overrightarrow{r})|\leq(1-\varepsilon)m
(still using the notation
|.|=\text{Card}(.)
). The idea behind this fact is that the
\varepsilon m
non-saturated cells will guarantee the lower bound on
B_{1}(2n)
, as changing their coordinates might increase
LC_{n}
.
Let
x=0.28,p_{1}=1-x,p_{2}=1/p_{1}
, we know from
[
14
]
that
\widetilde{\gamma}(p_{1})<{\gamma_{2}}
and
\widetilde{\gamma}(p_{2})<{\gamma_{2}}
. Let
\eta=\frac{2(1-x)}{2-x}-{\gamma_{2}}
, from the upper bound
{\gamma_{2}}\leq 0.8263
, see
[
20
]
, it that
\eta>0
. Let
\varepsilon\in\left(0,\frac{\eta}{2({\gamma_{2}}+\eta)}\right)
, and, lastly, let
\delta\in\left(0,\min({\gamma_{2}}-\widetilde{\gamma}(p_{1}),{\gamma_{2}}-\widetilde{\gamma}(p_{2}))\right)
and fix
\upsilon
to be such that
(1+\ln(1+\upsilon))/\upsilon<\delta^{2}\varepsilon^{2}/16
.

Let
C^{n}_{\varepsilon}
be the event: for any alignment
\overrightarrow{r}
,
|S(\overrightarrow{r})|\leq(1-\varepsilon)m
. If
(C^{n}_{\varepsilon})^{c}\cap B^{n}_{\varepsilon,p_{1},p_{2}}
is realized, then there is some alignment
\overrightarrow{r}
such that
|S(\overrightarrow{r})|>(1-\varepsilon)m
, and

LC_{n}\geq\sum_{i\in S(\overrightarrow{r})\cap I_{p_{1},p_{2}}(\overrightarrow{r})}\frac{N_{i}}{2}\frac{\min(\upsilon,r_{i+1}-r_{i})}{\frac{N_{i}}{2}}.

(4.12)

For any
i\in I_{p_{1},p_{2}}(\overrightarrow{r})
,
r_{i+1}-r_{i}\in[\upsilon p_{1},\upsilon p_{2}]
so

\frac{\min(\upsilon,r_{i+1}-r_{i})}{\frac{N_{i}}{2}}\geq\frac{2}{1+p_{2}}=\frac{2p_{1}}{1+p_{1}}=\frac{2(1-x)}{2-x}={\gamma_{2}}+\eta,

so

LC_{n}\geq\sum_{i\in S(\overrightarrow{r})\cap I_{p_{1},p_{2}}(\overrightarrow{r})}\frac{N_{i}}{2}({\gamma_{2}}+\eta).

(4.13)

Moreover,

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in(S(\overrightarrow{r})\cap I_{p_{1},p_{2}}(\overrightarrow{r}))^{c}}\frac{N_{i}}{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in(S(\overrightarrow{r}))^{c}\cap I_{p_{1},p_{2}}(\overrightarrow{r})}\frac{N_{i}}{2}+\sum_{i\in(I_{p_{1},p_{2}}(\overrightarrow{r}))^{c}}\frac{N_{i}}{2}

\displaystyle\leq\upsilon\frac{1+p_{2}}{2}\varepsilon m+\frac{\varepsilon n}{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\varepsilon n,

and therefore,

LC_{n}\geq(1-2\varepsilon)({\gamma_{2}}+\eta)n.

(4.14)

Given the choice of
\varepsilon
,
(1-2\varepsilon)({\gamma_{2}}+\eta)>{\gamma_{2}}
, so by concentration, this has probability exponentially small to happen.
Therefore,
\mathds{P}\left((C^{n}_{\varepsilon})^{c}\right)\leq\mathds{P}\left((C^{n}_{\varepsilon})^{c}\cap B^{n}_{\varepsilon,p_{1},p_{2}}\right)+\mathds{P}\left((B^{n}_{\varepsilon,p_{1},p_{2}})^{c}\right)
goes to zero (exponentially fast) as
n
goes to infinity.

Now for
i\in\{1,\dots,m\}
, let

V_{i}=\max_{x\in\{0,1\}^{\upsilon}}\left|LCS(X_{1}\cdots X_{\upsilon(i-1)}x_{1}\cdots x_{\upsilon}X_{\upsilon i+1}\cdots X_{n};Y_{1}\cdots Y_{n})-LCS(X_{1}\cdots X_{n};Y_{1}\dots Y_{n})\right|.

(4.15)

But, for any
i\in(S(\overrightarrow{r}))^{c}
, it follows that
V_{i}\geq 1
, and so

\mathds{E}\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}V_{i}^{2}\right)>\varepsilon\mathds{P}(C^{n}_{\varepsilon}).

(4.16)

Now, for
x\in\{0,1\}^{\upsilon}
and
j\in\{\upsilon(i-1)+1,\dots,\upsilon i\}
, let

\displaystyle\delta_{j}(x)=

\displaystyle LCS(X_{1}\cdots X_{\upsilon(i-1)}x_{1}\cdots x_{j-\upsilon(i-1)}X_{j+1}\cdots X_{n};Y_{1}\cdots Y_{n})

\displaystyle-LCS(X_{1}\cdots X_{\upsilon(i-1)}x_{1}\cdots x_{j-\upsilon(i-1)-1}X_{j}\cdots X_{n};Y_{1}\cdots Y_{n}),

so that,

\displaystyle V_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle=\max_{x\in\{0,1\}^{\upsilon}}\left|\sum_{j=\upsilon(i-1)+1}^{\upsilon i}\delta_{j}(x)\right|^{2}\leq\max_{x\in\{0,1\}^{\upsilon}}\upsilon\sum_{j=\upsilon(i-1)+1}^{\upsilon i}\delta_{j}(x)^{2}\leq\upsilon\sum_{j=\upsilon(i-1)+1}^{\upsilon i}\max_{x\in\{0,1\}^{\upsilon}}\delta_{j}(x)^{2}.

Note that
\mathds{E}\Delta_{j}^{2}=\mathds{E}\delta_{j}(X^{\prime}_{1},\dots,X^{\prime}_{\upsilon})^{2}
(where
\Delta_{j}
is the difference in length between the original LCS and the LCS modified, via an independent copy of the variable
at spot
j
, (see the next section for the precise definition of
\Delta_{j}
)), and

\mathds{E}_{X^{\prime}_{1},\dots,X^{\prime}_{\upsilon}}\Delta_{j}^{2}\geq\frac{1}{2^{\upsilon}}\max_{x\in\{0,1\}^{\upsilon}}\delta_{j}(x)^{2}.

Hence,
\mathds{E}\Delta_{j}^{2}\geq\mathds{E}\max_{x\in\{0,1\}^{\upsilon}}\delta_{j}(x)^{2}/{2^{\upsilon}}
, and
so
V_{i}^{2}\leq\upsilon 2^{\upsilon}\sum_{j=\upsilon(i-1)+1}^{\upsilon i}\mathds{E}\Delta_{j}^{2}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}V_{i}^{2}\right)\leq\frac{\upsilon 2^{\upsilon}}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathds{E}\Delta_{j}^{2},

finally,

\displaystyle\varepsilon\mathds{P}(C^{n}_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle<\upsilon^{2}2^{\upsilon}B_{1}(2n),

and thus, for
n
large enough,
B_{1}(2n)>{\varepsilon}/{2\upsilon^{2}2^{\upsilon}}
.
∎

Remark 4.6
.

The above result is a necessary condition (certainly not sufficient, though) to have
{\textrm{Var}}\,LC_{n}
asymptotically linear.
This implies that there exists
C^{\prime}>0
, such that for all
n
,
B_{1}(2n)\geq C^{\prime}
, as for all
n
,
B_{1}(2n)>0
.

4.6
A note on a potential implication of
[
10
]

In this section,
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
\upsilon=n^{\alpha}
, and
\overrightarrow{r}
is a random alignment.
Let
X^{\prime}_{1},\dots,X^{\prime}_{n},Y^{\prime}_{1},\dots,Y^{\prime}_{n}
be independent Bernoulli variables with parameter
1/2
, independent from all the previous variables. As previously, we write
Z=(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{2n}):=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n},Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n})
, and as in
[
10
]
, for
j\in\{1,\dots,2n\}
, let

\displaystyle\Delta_{j}

\displaystyle:=LCS(Z)-LCS(Z_{1}\cdots Z^{\prime}_{j}\cdots Z_{2n})

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Delta_{j}}

\displaystyle:=LCS\left(P_{i}\right)-LCS\left(P^{\prime}_{i}\right)

where
P_{i}
is the cell of length
\upsilon

Z_{j}
and

Z^{\prime}_{j}
instead of
Z_{j}
.
We also write for
j,k\in\{1,\dots,m\}
:

\displaystyle LC_{n}^{j}

\displaystyle:=LCS(Z_{1}\cdots Z^{\prime}_{j}\cdots Z_{2n})

\displaystyle LC_{n}^{j,k}

\displaystyle:=LCS(Z_{1}\cdots Z^{\prime}_{j}\cdots Z^{\prime}_{k}\cdots Z_{2n})

\displaystyle\Delta_{j,k}

\displaystyle:=LC_{n}-LC_{n}^{j}-LC_{n}^{j}+LC_{n}^{j,k}.

It is claimed in
[
10
]
that
\mathds{E}\left|\widetilde{\Delta_{j}}-\Delta_{j}\right|=\mathds{E}\left(\widetilde{\Delta_{j}}-\Delta_{j}\right)
is exponentially small in
n
. The equality comes from the fact that
\widetilde{\Delta_{j}}-\Delta_{j}\geq 0
(as explained in
[
10
]
). Furthermore,
\mathds{E}\Delta_{j}=0
, so the problem boils to controlling
\mathds{E}\widetilde{\Delta_{j}}
. Let us assume, in this section, that
\mathds{E}\widetilde{\Delta_{j}}\leq\exp(-tn)
for some
t>0
not depending on
j,n
, and let us denote by
A_{j}
the event
\widetilde{\Delta_{j}}-\Delta_{j}=0
. Of course,
\mathds{P}(A_{j}^{c})\leq\exp(-tn)
. Finally, let
C_{j,k}
be the event "
Z_{j}
and
Z_{k}
are not in the same cell". Let
j,k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
and suppose
A_{j}
,
A_{k}
and
C_{j,k}
are all realized, then when
X_{j}
is flipped to
X^{\prime}_{j}
, the alignment
\overrightarrow{r}=\overrightarrow{r}(Z)
is still an alignment for
(Z_{1},\dots,Z^{\prime}_{j},\dots,Z_{2n})
, so

LC_{n}^{j,k}-LC_{n}^{j}\geq-\widetilde{\Delta_{k}}=LC_{n}^{k}-LC_{n}

(4.17)

so, in other terms,

\Delta_{j,k}\mathds{1}_{A_{j}}\mathds{1}_{A_{k}}\mathds{1}_{C_{j,k}}\geq 0.

(4.18)

Let us write
\Delta_{j,k}=\Delta_{j,k}^{+}-\Delta_{j,k}^{-}
(the positive and negative parts), using the bounds
|\Delta_{j,k}|\leq 2
and (
4.18
) we get

\Delta_{j,k}^{-}\leq 2(1-\mathds{1}_{A_{j}}\mathds{1}_{A_{k}}\mathds{1}_{C_{j,k}})

(4.19)

so
(\Delta_{j,k}^{-})^{2}\leq 4(1-\mathds{1}_{A_{j}}\mathds{1}_{A_{k}}\mathds{1}_{C_{j,k}})
, and

\mathds{E}(\Delta_{j,k}^{-})^{2}\leq 4\left(\mathds{P}(A_{j}^{c})+\mathds{P}(A_{k}^{c})+\mathds{P}(C_{j,k}^{c})\right),

(4.20)

\mathds{E}(\Delta_{j,k}^{+})^{2}\leq 2\mathds{E}\Delta_{j,k}^{+}=2\mathds{E}\Delta_{j,k}^{-}\leq 4\left(\mathds{P}(A_{j}^{c})+\mathds{P}(A_{k}^{c})+\mathds{P}(C_{j,k}^{c})\right),

(4.21)

hence

\mathds{E}(\Delta_{j,k})^{2}\leq 8\left(\mathds{P}(A_{j}^{c})+\mathds{P}(A_{k}^{c})+\mathds{P}(C_{j,k}^{c})\right).

(4.22)

We may now give an upper bound on
B_{1}(2n)-B_{2}(2n)
:

\displaystyle B_{1}(2n)-B_{2}(2n)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4(2n)(2n-1)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\neq k\\
j,k\in\{1,\dots,2n\}\end{subarray}}\mathds{E}(\Delta_{j,k})^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{4(2n)(2n-1)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\neq k\\
j\in\{1,\dots,n\},k\in\{1,\dots,2n\}\end{subarray}}\mathds{E}(\Delta_{j,k})^{2}\quad\text{(by symmetry)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{n(2n-1)}\mathds{E}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\neq k\\
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}\end{subarray}}\mathds{1}_{C_{j,k}^{c}}\right)+\frac{2}{n(2n-1)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\neq k\\
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}\end{subarray}}\left(\mathds{P}(A_{j}^{c})+\mathds{P}(A_{k}^{c})\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{n(2n-1)}(2n\upsilon-n)+2\exp(-tn).

So when
n
is large enough,

B_{1}(2n)-B_{2}(2n)\leq\frac{2\upsilon}{n},

(4.23)

and from the convexity of
B
, and using the lower bound
0<C\leq B_{1}(2n)
(see Theorem
4.5
),

{\textrm{Var}}\,LC_{n}=B_{1}(2n)+\dots+B_{2n}(2n)\geq\sum_{i=1}^{\frac{Cn}{2\upsilon}}C-\frac{2\upsilon(i-1)}{n},

which is equivalent to
C^{2}n/(4\upsilon)
. So for some constant
C^{\prime}>0
,

{\textrm{Var}}\,LC_{n}\geq C^{\prime}n^{1-\alpha}.

(4.24)

Once again, this is under the assumption that
\mathds{E}\widetilde{\Delta_{j}}\leq\exp(-tn)
. If, additionally, this assumption holds for some
\alpha<1/10
, then by
[
10
]
there is convergence of the properly rescaled
LC_{n}
to a Gaussian.

There is also a somewhat weaker assumption that would guarantee the linearity of the variance.
Recalling the percolation interpretation of the LCS, we denote by
Geo
the (random) set of geodesics, and for any
a,b\in\{1,\dots,2n\}
,
\text{Geo}^{a}
the set of geodesics when the
Z_{a}
is turned into
Z^{\prime}_{a}
, and
\text{Geo}^{a,b}
the set of geodesics when
Z_{a}
is turned into
Z^{\prime}_{a}
and
Z_{b}
is turned into
Z^{\prime}_{b}
. For
j,k\in\{1,\dots,m\}
, let
A_{j,k}
be the event: there exists
(p,q)
such that
j<p<k
and there exist
(g_{1},g_{2},g_{3},g_{4})\in\text{Geo}\cap\text{Geo}^{j}\cap\text{Geo}^{k}\cap\text{Geo}^{j,k}
such that
(p,q)\in g_{1}\cap g_{2}\cap g_{3}\cap g_{4}
. In words, this is the event that it is possible to find
X_{p}
aligned with
Y_{q}
no matter the values of
X_{j}
and
X_{k}
. Similarly, let
B_{j,k}
be the event: there exists
(p,q)
such that
j<p
and
k>q
or
j>p
and
k<q
and there exists
(g_{1},g_{2},g_{3},g_{4})\in\text{Geo}\cap\text{Geo}^{j}\cap\text{Geo}^{k+n}\cap\text{Geo}^{j,k+n}
such that
(p,q)\in g_{1}\cap g_{2}\cap g_{3}\cap g_{4}
. In words, this is the event that it is possible to find
X_{p}
aligned with
Y_{q}
no matter the values of
X_{j}
and
X_{k}
, and such that
X_{j},Y_{k}
are not both "on the same side". Now suppose that
\mathds{P}(A_{j,k}^{c}),\mathds{P}(B_{j,k}^{c})\leq\exp(\alpha|k-j|)
for some constant
\alpha>0
. Then an adaptation of the proof above shows that the variance is lower bounded by
C^{\prime}n
for some constant
C^{\prime}>0
.
