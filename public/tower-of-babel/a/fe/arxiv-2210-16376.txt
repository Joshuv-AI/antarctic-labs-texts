A Heintze-Karcher inequality with free boundaries and applications to capillarity theory
Matias G. Delgadino, Daniel Weser

Abstract
. In this paper we analyze the shape of a droplet inside a smooth container. To characterize their shape in the capillarity regime, we obtain a new form of the Heintze–Karcher inequality for mean convex hypersurfaces with boundary lying on curved substrates. MSC 2020: 53C24, 35J25, 53C21 Keywords: Heintze-Karcher’s inequality, capillary hypersurface, CMC hyper- surface, Alexandrov’s theorem.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
The Heintze-Karcher inequality and Alexandrov’s theorem

Given a bounded open connected set
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
with smooth boundary
\partial\Omega
and positive scalar mean curvature
H_{\Omega}
, the
Heintze-Karcher inequality
states that

(n+1)|\Omega|\leq\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{n}{H_{\Omega}}\,,

(1.1)

with equality if and only if
\Omega
is a Euclidean ball. Here
|\Omega|
denotes the volume of
\Omega
, while
H_{\Omega}
is computed with respect to the outer unit normal
\nu_{\Omega}
to
\Omega
.

The Heintze-Karcher inequality is closely related to Alexandrov’s theorem, which states that if
H_{\Omega}
is constant (and
\Omega
is bounded and connected), then
\Omega
is a Euclidean ball. Indeed, if
H_{\Omega}
is constant, then by applying the divergence theorem twice (once in
\Omega
and once tangentially to
\partial\Omega
) to the identity vector field one finds

\displaystyle(n+1)|\Omega|=\int_{\Omega}{\rm div}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega}(x\cdot\nu_{\Omega})=\frac{1}{H_{\partial\Omega}}\int_{\partial\Omega}(x\cdot\nu_{\Omega})H_{\partial\Omega}

(1.2)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{H_{\partial\Omega}}\int_{\partial\Omega}{\rm div}^{\partial\Omega}(x)=\frac{n\,\mathcal{H}^{n}(\partial\Omega)}{H_{\partial\Omega}}=\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{n}{H_{\partial\Omega}}\,,

where
\mathcal{H}^{n}
denotes the
n
-dimensional Hausdorff measure in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
and
{\rm div}^{\partial\Omega}
the tangential divergence with respect to
\partial\Omega
. Thus the relation between (
1.1
) and Alexandrov’s theorem is that one can prove the latter by characterzing the equality cases in (
1.1
).

This interesting connection has motivated many authors to investigate the Heintze-Karcher inequality (and variants of it) for proving rigidity theorems. Two proofs of the Heintze-Karcher inequality that are particularly relevant for the present study are the one by Montiel and Ros
[
MR91
]
(see Lemma
6.1
and Remark
6.2
below; this proof is also related to the argument recently used by Brendle
[
Bre13
]
to extend Alexandrov theorem to a large class of warped product manifolds) and the one by Ros
[
Ros87
]
. Ros’ argument is based on the study of the torsion potential
u
of
\Omega
, namely, the solution to the problem

\left\{\begin{split}-\Delta u=1&\qquad\mbox{in $\Omega$}\,,\\
u=0&\qquad\mbox{on $\partial\Omega$}\,.\end{split}\right.

(1.3)

As a by-product of this proof, one sees that when equality holds in (
1.1
), then
-\nabla^{2}u=(n+1)^{-1}\,{\rm Id}\,
in
\Omega
and there exists
\lambda>0
such that
|\nabla u|=-\nabla u\cdot\nu_{\Omega}=\lambda
on
\partial\Omega
. In particular, the connected components of
\Omega
are balls of the same radius, which can be computed in terms of
\lambda
by exploiting the Dirichlet condition on
u
.

The analysis of this inequality found an application in a basic question of capillarity theory, namely, the geometric descriptions of critical points of the Gauss energy of a liquid droplet in the capillarity regime. Indeed, using the Heintze-Karcher inequality, the pioneering work of
[
CM17
]
showed that if a smooth set has mean curvature uniformly close to a constant, then the set is almost a finite union of tangent balls of equal radius. In
[
DM19
]
, Alexandrov’s theorem was revisited for non smooth sets to account for bubbling phenomenon. More specifically, sets of finite perimeter with distributional mean curvature equal to a constant are shown to be the union of disjoint and tangent balls of equal radius. This result was later quantified for smooth sets in
[
JN20
]
with the closeness of the mean curvature to a constant measured in
L^{n}(\partial\Omega)
, which allowed the authors to show exponential convergence of the volume preserving mean curvature flow to a union of balls in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. For the anisotropic generalization of some of these results, see
[
DMMN18
,
DRKS20
]
.

1.2.
The Heintze-Karcher inequality on an ideal substrate

Motivated by the study of droplets on a substrate, we look for a natural extension of the Heintze-Karcher inequality from the case of smooth sets in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
to that of subsets
\Omega
of the half-space
H=\{x_{n+1}>0\}
. As depicted in Figure
1.1
,

Figure 1.1.
The setting for the Heintze-Karcher inequality on an ideal substrate.

we consider sets
\Omega\subseteq\{x_{n+1}>0\}
such that
M=\overline{\partial\Omega\cap\{x_{n+1}>0\}}
is a smooth hypersurface with boundary, whose set of boundary points
{\rm bd}(M)
satisfies
{\rm bd}(M)=M\cap\{x_{n+1}=0\}
. We endow
M
with the orientation
\nu_{M}
defined by
\nu_{\Omega}
, and, after defining for each
x\in{\rm bd}(M)
the angle
\theta(x)
through

\nu_{M}(x)\cdot\nu_{H}=\cos\theta(x)\,,

(1.4)

we assume the non-degeneracy condition (related to (
1.10
)) that
\theta(x)\in(0,\pi)
for every
x\in{\rm bd}(M)
. In particular, the wetted region
\Sigma=\partial\Omega\cap\{x_{n+1}=0\}
is a smooth set with
{\rm bd}(\Sigma)={\rm bd}(M)
, and one can decompose

\nu_{M}(x)=\cos\theta(x)\nu_{H}+\sin\theta(x)\,\nu_{\Sigma}(x)\qquad\forall x\in{\rm bd}(M)\,,

where
\nu_{\Sigma}
denotes the outer unit normal to
\Sigma
in
\{x_{n+1}=0\}
.

A basic difficulty here is understanding what form the new Heintze-Karcher inequality should take; indeed, the right-hand side of (
1.1
) makes no sense, as
H_{\Omega}
becomes a measure on
{\rm bd}(M)
. The answer is found by looking at the Montiel-Ros proof
[
MR91
]
. Exploited in this setting, their argument rather naturally suggests the discussion of the problem under the additional assumption that

either
\theta(x)\in(0,\pi/2]
or
\theta(x)\in[\pi/2,\pi)
for every
x\in{\rm bd}(M)
.

Although this may look a bit artificial, this kind of restriction is actually natural from the physical point of view, as it amounts to consider the case of
hydrophobic
(
0<\theta\leq\pi/2
) or
hydrophilic
(
\pi/2\leq\theta<\pi
) substrates. It is perhaps not surprising, then, that one finds
two different generalizations
of the Heintze-Karcher inequality (
1.1
), which we collectively call the
Heintze-Karcher inequalities on an ideal substrate
:

Figure 1.2.
In the hydrophobic case, the “pyramid”
\Lambda
is contained in
H
. Notice that
\Lambda
may not be contained in
\Omega
. When
\Omega
is a ball which intersects
\partial H
below its equator (picture on the top right corner), then
\Lambda
is the cone with vertex at the center of the ball, spanned over the wetted region.

Figure 1.3.
In the hydrophilic case,
\Lambda
is contained in
\{x_{n+1}<0\}
, and thus it is automatically disjoint from
\Omega
. In this regime the optimal case is when
\Omega
is a ball intersecting
\partial H
above its equator. As in the hydrophobic case,
\Lambda
can then be described as the cone with vertex at the center of the ball, spanned over the wetted region.

Theorem 1.1

(Heintze-Karcher inequality on an ideal substrate)
.

If
\Omega\subseteq H=\{x_{n+1}>0\}
is a bounded connected open set such that
M=\overline{H\cap\partial\Omega}
is a hypersurface with boundary satisfying the hypotheses
(h1)
–
(h4)
from Section
2
, then

\begin{split}&(n+1)\big(|\Omega|-|\Lambda|\big)\leq\int_{M}\frac{n}{H_{M}}\qquad\mbox{in the hydrophobic case}\,,\\
&(n+1)\big(|\Omega|+|\Lambda|\big)\leq\int_{M}\frac{n}{H_{M}}\qquad\mbox{in the hydrophilic case}\,,\end{split}

(1.5)

where in the hydrophobic case

\Lambda=\Big\{(z,t)\in\Sigma\times[0,\infty):0\leq t<d(z,\partial\Sigma)\tan\theta(P_{\partial\Sigma}(z))\Big\}\,,

(1.6)

and in the hydrophilic case

\Lambda=\Big\{(z,t)\in\Sigma\times(-\infty,0):d(z,\partial\Sigma)\tan\theta(P_{\partial\Sigma}(z))\leq t\leq 0\Big\}\,,

(1.7)

where
P_{\partial\Sigma}:\Sigma\to\partial\Sigma
is the projection onto the boundary.

In both cases, equality holds in (
1.5
) if and only if
\Omega=B_{r}(t\,e_{n+1})\cap H
for some
r>0
and
|t|<r
.

The sets
\Lambda
are depicted in Figures
1.2
and
1.3
, which illustrate the hydrophobic and hydrophilic cases, respectively. The Heintze-Karcher inequalities on a substrate are both sharp, and only a ball meeting
\partial H
orthogonally (
\theta\equiv\pi/2
) is an equality case for
both
inequalities.

Comparing Theorem
1.1
to other recent results in this direction, we first mention the work of Jia, Xia and Zhang
[
JXZ23
]
, in which the authors prove an alternative Heintze-Karcher inequality for sets over an ideal substrate or contained inside a half-ball and for fixed angles. A strengthened quantitative version of their result for slightly curved substrates and variable angles can be found in Proposition
3.4
, which is used for our compactness result. In follow up work, the above authors together with G. Wang, lifted this restriction to variable angles, see
[
JWXZ22
, Theorem 1.1]
. In comparison with Theorem
1.1
,
[
JWXZ22
, Theorem 1.1]
considers the worst case scenario in terms of the angle of the “pyramids” we construct, see
1.3
and
1.2
.

1.3.
Droplets in a container

We now describe the main focus of this paper, the sessile or pendant droplet in capillarity theory. For more details, we refer any interested reader to
[
Fin86
]
for a classical overview of this problem. We consider the Gauss energy of a droplet of fixed volume
m
sitting in a container: if
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
an open set with
C^{2}
-boundary denotes the container and the open set
\Omega\subseteq K
denotes the region occupied by the droplet, then the Gauss energy of
\Omega
is given by

\mathcal{F}(\Omega)=P(\Omega;K)+\int_{\partial\Omega\cap\partial K}\sigma\,d\mathcal{H}^{n}+\int_{\Omega}\,g(x)\,dx\qquad\mbox{with $|\Omega|=m$ fixed.}

(1.8)

where
m>0
is a given volume parameter, the function
g:K\to\mathbb{R}
is the
potential energy density
, and
\sigma:\partial K\to[-1,1]
is the
relative adhesion coefficient
, which measures the relative mismatch between the surface tension of the liquid/air interface
K\cap\partial\Omega
and the liquid/solid interface
\partial\Omega\cap\partial K
. Typically, one sets
g(x)=g_{0}\,\rho\,x_{n+1}
, where
g_{0}
is Earth’s gravity and
\rho
the density of on the liquid. Capillarity phenomena are characterized by the dominance of the surface tension energy, that is of
\mathcal{H}^{n}(K\cap\partial\Omega)+\int_{\partial\Omega\cap\partial K}\sigma
over the potential energy
\int_{\Omega}g
. This is definitely the case when the volume parameter
m
is small enough in terms of
g
, see (
5.9
) for the expression of the energy after scaling by the volume. Although for mathematical simplicity the relative adhesion coefficient
\sigma
is sometimes assumed to be constant, several relevant physical phenomena such as hysteresis and contact line pinning can be explained by local inhomogeneities of
\sigma
. Beyond the classical reference
[
HM77
]
, we refer the reader to
[
CM07a
,
CM07b
]
for a modern homogenization approach to rapidly oscillating
\sigma
.

In this work, we address the description of the critical points of
\mathcal{F}
when
m
is small. This problem leads to a question related to the one considered in
[
CM17
]
. To explain this point, let us say that a bounded set
\Omega
is a critical point of (
1.8
). If
M=\overline{K\cap\partial\Omega}
is a smooth hypersurface with boundary, then the Euler-Lagrange equations are the following:

H_{\Omega}(x)+g(x)=\lambda\,\,\qquad\mbox{for every $x\in{\rm int}\,(M)$}\,,

(1.9)

\nu_{M}(x)\cdot\nu_{K}=\sigma(x)\qquad\mbox{for every $x\in{\rm bd}(M)$}\,.

(1.10)

where
{\rm bd}(M)=M\cap\partial K
and
{\rm int}\,(M)=\partial\Omega\cap K
. Condition (
1.10
) is known as
Young’s law
, and it has been formulated here by taking
\nu_{M}
to be the orientation of
M
obtained by continuously extending
\nu_{\Omega}
from
{\rm int}\,(M)
to
{\rm bd}(M)
. Condition (
1.9
) is the Euler-Lagrange equation of the perimeter, and
\lambda
is a Lagrange multiplier that arises from the volume constraint.

In the ideal case when
g=0
and
\sigma\equiv\sigma_{0}
, then
M
is a constant mean curvature hypersurface contained in
K
whose boundary meets
\partial K
at a constant angle.
Wente’s theorem

[
Wen80
]
, which was originally obtained by exploiting the moving planes method, shows that when
\partial K
is flat then
M
is a spherical cap. We also mention
[
HHW20
]
for the state of the art on the moving planes method with minimal regularity requirements. When
g
is non-trivial and
|\Omega|
is small, we are back to a problem similar to the one considered in
[
CM17
]
, with the added difficulties of having to deal with a surface with boundary and almost constant contact angle. In
[
MM16
]
, this problem was addressed for the case of global minimizers, showing that as
m\to 0^{+}
global minimizers converge, up to a re-scaling, to a ball intersected with a half-space. In this work, we extend this result to
local
minimizers of (
1.8
), which we define as follows:

Definition 1.2
.

A set of locally finite perimeter
\Omega\subseteq K
of volume
|\Omega|=m
is said to be a
volume constrained local minimizer (VCLM)
with diameter
\varepsilon_{0}>0
of the Gauss capillarity energy if

\mathcal{F}_{K,\sigma,g}(\Omega)\leq\mathcal{F}_{K,\sigma,g}(\tilde{\Omega})

for every
\tilde{\Omega}\subseteq K
satisfying
|\tilde{\Omega}|=m
and
diam(\tilde{\Omega}\triangle\Omega)<\varepsilon_{0}m^{\frac{1}{n+1}}
.

Analogous to the global minimizer case, our result is that as
m\to 0^{+}
, up to re-scaling, local minimizers converge to a single ball intersected with the half-space
\{x_{n+1}>0\}
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Consider a fixed compact container
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
with
C^{2}
-boundary and a Lipschitz regular hydrophilic adhesion coefficient
\sigma:\partial K\to(-1,0)
. Given constants
C>1
and
\varepsilon_{0}>0
, for each
m>0
, we consider
\mathcal{G}_{m,\varepsilon_{0},C}
a subset of smooth connected sets, which are volume constrained local minimizers of
\mathcal{F}_{K,\sigma,g}
with diameter
\varepsilon_{0}
and volume
m
. Moreover, we assume that any
\Omega_{m}\in\mathcal{G}_{m,\varepsilon_{0},C}
satisfies the uniform non-degeneracy conditions for perimeter, diameter and wetter region:

•

P(\Omega_{m};K)\leq Cm^{\frac{n}{n+1}}
,

•

{\rm diam}(\Omega_{m})\leq Cm^{\frac{1}{n+1}}
,

•

\mathcal{H}^{n}(\Sigma_{m})\geq C^{-1}m^{\frac{n}{n+1}}
.

Then, we have that as
m\to 0^{+}
any element sequence converges to a spherical cap:

\lim_{m\to 0^{+}}\sup_{\Omega_{m}\in\mathcal{G}_{m,\varepsilon_{0},C}}\inf_{x_{0},Q,\theta_{*}}m^{-1}|\Omega_{m}\triangle(m^{\frac{1}{n+1}}(x_{0}+Q[B_{\theta_{*}}]))|=0,

(1.11)

where
B_{\theta_{*}}
is the ball which intersects
\{x_{n+1}=0\}
with angle
\theta_{*}\in(\pi/2,\pi)
and has volume
|B_{\theta_{*}}\cap\{x_{n+1}>0\}|=1
, and
Q[B_{\theta_{*}}]
is a rigid motion of
B_{\theta_{*}}
.

Remark 1.4
.

We note that for
n=2
local minimizers are regular, see
[
Tay77
,
PM15
]
.

Remark 1.5
.

The convergence in (
1.11
) is actually stronger, see (
4.13
) in Theorem
1.6
.

To obtain this characterization, we prove a compactness result for Wente’s theorem. The non-degeneracy conditions in the statement of theorem are the minimal ones necessary to be able to rescale the problem appropriately. The proof of the compactness theorem occupies the bulk of this paper.

1.4.
Compactness

To prove Theorem
1.3
, we will re-scale the problem so that the re-scaled sets have unit volume. Due to the
C^{2}
-regularity of the container, the re-scaled boundary will become flat in the limit. Consequently, our compactness theorem takes the following (simplified) form (see Section
4
for the precise statement):

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
\{K_{l}\}_{l\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of connected open sets with connected
C^{2}
-boundaries which converge to the half space
\{x_{n+1}>0\}
in the following sense:

\|A_{l}\|_{C^{0}_{loc}(\partial K_{l})}\to 0,\qquad\|e_{n+1}\cdot\nu_{K_{l}}\|_{C^{0}_{loc}(\partial K_{l})}\to 0\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\|x\cdot\nu_{K_{l}}\|_{C^{0}_{loc}(\partial K_{l})}\to 0\,,

(1.12)

where
A_{l}
is the second fundamental form of
\partial K_{l}
.

Let
\{\Omega_{l}\}_{l\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of connected open sets such that their boundaries
\{M_{l}\}_{l\in\mathbb{N}}
are smooth (see hypotheses
(h1)
-
(h4)
and notation in Section
2
).
If there exists a positive constant
\lambda_{0}>0
such that

\left\|H_{l}-\lambda_{0}\right\|_{C^{0}(M_{l})}\to 0\,,

(1.13)

a fixed angle
\theta_{0}\in(\pi/2,\pi)
such that

\|\theta_{l}-\theta_{0}\|_{C^{0}({\rm bd}(M_{l}))}\to 0\,,

(1.14)

and a bounded set of finite perimeter
\Omega_{\infty}\subseteq\{x_{n+1}>0\}
such that

P(\Omega_{l};K_{l})\to P(\Omega_{\infty};\{x_{n+1}>0\})\qquad\textnormal{and}\qquad|\Omega_{l}\Delta\Omega_{\infty}|\to 0,

(1.15)

then
\Omega_{\infty}
is equal to the finite union of disjoint tangent balls of radius
n/\lambda_{0}
intersected with
\{x_{n+1}>0\}
and is such the that any ball in
\Omega_{\infty}
which intersects the hyperplane
\{x_{n+1}=0\}
does so with contact angle
\theta_{0}
. Moreover, the wetted regions converge in the following sense:

\lim_{l\to\infty}|\mathcal{H}^{n}(\Sigma_{l})-\mathcal{H}^{n}(\Sigma_{\infty})|+|\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial\Sigma_{l})-\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial\Sigma_{\infty})|\;=\;0\,.

(1.16)

The proof of Theorem
1.6
follows from deriving a Heintze-Karcher-like inequality (see Proposition
3.3
)

\displaystyle\frac{|\Omega|-\gamma\mathcal{H}^{n}(\Sigma)}{n+1}\left(\int_{M}\frac{n}{H_{M}}d\mathcal{H}^{n}-(n+1)\big(|\Omega|-\gamma\mathcal{H}^{n}(\Sigma)\big)\right)

(1.17)

\displaystyle\quad+2(n+1)\varepsilon\bigg\{\int_{M}\frac{d\mathcal{H}^{n}}{H_{M}}\left(\frac{|\Omega|}{n+1}+4(d_{\Omega}^{2}+\gamma^{2})(|\Omega|+\gamma\mathcal{H}^{n}(\Sigma))\right)+\max\frac{1}{H_{M}}\left(|\Omega|-\gamma\mathcal{H}^{n}(\Sigma)\right)^{2}\bigg\}\geq 0

(1.18)

by utilizing an appropriately modified torsion potential

\begin{cases}\hfill-\Delta u=1&\mbox{ in $\Omega$}\\
\hfill u=0&\mbox{ on $M$}\\
\hfill\partial_{\nu_{K}}u=\gamma&\mbox{ on $\Sigma$}\,,\end{cases}

(1.19)

where

\gamma=-\frac{n}{n+1}\frac{\mathcal{H}^{n}(\Sigma)}{\int_{\partial\Sigma}\tan\theta(x)\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x)}\,.

(1.20)

Although this is the guiding principle of the proof in
[
CM17
]
, this is where the similarities with our proof end. The mixed boundary conditions in (
3.9
) made obtaining uniform Lipschitz estimates impossible at the present time. In fact, the need to restrict to the hydrophilic regime
\theta\in(\pi/2,\pi)
is due to the lack of
C^{1}
-regularity in the hydrophobic case, see Remark
3.1
and Lemma
3.2
. We sidestep these difficulties by utilizing varifold convergence of the boundaries and density estimates to imply Hausdorff convergence of the boundaries. The characterization of the limit set as a union balls follows from showing that the case of equality in the Heintze-Karcher-like inequality (
1.18
) is obtained in the limit. In fact, we can show the following quantitative inequality:

\displaystyle\left|(n+1)(|\Omega|-\gamma\mathcal{H}^{n}(\Sigma))-\int_{M}\frac{n}{H_{M}}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n}\right|

(1.21)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq C\left(\varepsilon+\|\theta-\theta_{0}\|_{C^{0}(\partial\Sigma)}+\frac{\|H_{M}-\lambda\|_{C_{0}(M)}}{\lambda}\right)\left(d_{\Omega}+\frac{1}{\lambda}\right)\mathcal{H}^{n}(M),

(1.22)

where
\gamma
is defined in (
3.4
).

2.
Hypotheses and Notation

Let
n\geq 2
, and denote by
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
an open set with
C^{2}
-boundary. We will consider non-empty bounded connected open sets
\Omega\subseteq K
with finite perimeter,
and we will denote by
\nu_{\Omega}
the measure-theoretic outer unit normal to
\Omega
(which agrees with the classical one if the
\partial\Omega
is of class
C^{1}
). We assume that

M=\overline{\partial\Omega\cap K}

is a smooth hypersurface with boundary in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. We denote by
{\rm bd}(M)
and by
{\rm int}\,(M)
the boundary and the interior points of
M
, respectively, and make the following assumptions:

(h1):

{\rm bd}(M)
is non-empty and contained in
\partial K
, that is

{\rm bd}(M)=M\cap\partial K\qquad{\rm int}\,(M)=M\cap K=\partial\Omega\cap K\,.

In particular,
\nu_{\Omega}
is classically defined on
M\cap K=K\cap\partial\Omega
, and an orientation
\nu_{M}
of
M
can be defined by setting

\begin{split}\nu_{M}(x)=\nu_{\Omega}(x)&\qquad\forall x\in{\rm int}\,(M)\,,\\
\nu_{M}(x)=\lim_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle y\in M\cap K}}{{y\to x}}}\nu_{\Omega}(y)&\qquad\forall x\in{\rm bd}(M)\,.\end{split}

(h2):
if we denote by
H_{M}
the scalar mean curvature of
M
with respect to
\nu_{M}
, then

H_{M}>0\qquad\mbox{on $M$}\,.

(h3):
if we define
\theta:{\rm bd}(M)\to[0,\pi]
by setting

\cos\theta(y)=\nu_{M}(y)\cdot\nu_{K}\qquad\forall x\in{\rm bd}(M)\,,

then

\theta(x)\in(0,\pi)\qquad\forall x\in{\rm bd}(M)\,.

In particular,
\partial\Omega\cap\partial K
is a smooth hypersurface in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
,
\Sigma:={\rm int}\,(\partial\Omega\cap\partial K)
is an open set with smooth boundary in
\partial H=\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\{0\}
, and

{\rm bd}(M)=\partial\Sigma\,,

where
\partial\Sigma
denotes boundary of
\Sigma
in
\partial H
, whose outer unit normal is denoted by
\nu_{\Sigma}
.

(h4):
denoting by
\theta_{min}
and
\theta_{max}
the extreme values of
\theta
on
{\rm bd}(M)
, we shall assume that

\begin{split}&\mbox{either $[\theta_{min},\theta_{max}]\subseteq(0,\pi/2)$ (hydrophobic substrate)}\,,\\
&\mbox{or \hskip 14.22636pt $[\theta_{min},\theta_{max}]\subseteq(\pi/2,\pi)$ (hydrophilic substrate)}\,.\end{split}

(2.1)

With the above notation in force, for every
x\in{\rm bd}(M)
one has

\displaystyle\nu_{M}(x)\;

\displaystyle=\;\cos\theta(x)\nu_{K}+\sin\theta(x)\nu_{\Sigma}(x)\,,

(2.2)

\displaystyle\nu_{{\rm bd}(M)}(x)\;

\displaystyle=\;\sin\theta(x)\nu_{K}-\cos\theta(x)\nu_{\Sigma}(x)\,,

(2.3)

where
\nu_{{\rm bd}(M)}
denotes the outer normal to
{\rm bd}(M)
with respect to
M
.

3.
Torsion potential with a substrate

In this section, we will assume hypotheses
(h1)
–
(h4)
from Section
2
and, per the discussion in the Introduction, we will assume further that the substrate is hydrophilic and the container is almost flat. The precise definitions are as follows:

Hydrophilic:
Denoting by
\theta_{min}
and
\theta_{max}
the extreme values of
\theta
on
{\rm bd}(M)
, we shall assume that there exists
\delta_{0}>0
such that

\begin{split}&\mbox{$[\theta_{min},\theta_{max}]\subseteq(\pi/2+\delta_{0},\pi)$.}\end{split}

(3.1)

Almost Flatness:
There exists
\varepsilon\ll 1
such that, up to rotation and translation

\|A_{\partial K}\|_{C^{0}(\Sigma)}<\varepsilon,\quad\|1+e_{n+1}\cdot\nu_{K}\|_{C^{0}(\Sigma)}<\varepsilon,\quad\mbox{and}\quad\|x\cdot\nu_{K}\|_{C^{0}(\Sigma)}<\varepsilon|x|.

(3.2)

Remark 3.1
.

While
Almost Flatness
naturally arises from the proof of Theorem
1.3
, we need to assume we are in the
Hydrophilic
regime due to the fact that the torsion potential fails to be
C^{1}(\overline{\Omega})
in the hydrophobic regime, see Lemma
3.2
below. The regularity of the torsion potential is essential to justify Reilly’s identity, see Lemma
3.5
below.

We define the
torsion potential of
\Omega
to be the unique solution
u\in C^{2}(\Omega\cup\Sigma)\cap C^{0}(\overline{\Omega})
to the mixed boundary value problem

\begin{cases}\hfill-\Delta u=1&\mbox{ in $\Omega$}\\
\hfill u=0&\mbox{ on $M$}\\
\hfill\partial_{\nu_{K}}u=\gamma&\mbox{ on $\Sigma$},\end{cases}

(3.3)

where

\gamma=-\frac{n}{n+1}\frac{\mathcal{H}^{n}(\Sigma)}{\int_{\partial\Sigma}\tan\theta(x)\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x)}\,.

(3.4)

Existence, uniqueness, and regularity follow from
[
Lie86
, Theorem 1]
. Next, we show that in the
Hydrophilic
regime one can prove additional regularity of the torsion potential and that in the
Almost Flatness
regime one can control its
L^{\infty}
-norm.

Lemma 3.2
.

Under the hypothesis of Section
2
, assume further that the substrate is
Hydrophilic
. Then, the unique solution
u\in C^{2}(\Omega\cup\Sigma)\cap C^{0}(\overline{\Omega})
to (
3.9
) additionally satisfies

u\in C^{1}(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\Omega\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu)\quad\textnormal{and}\quad u>0\;\;\textnormal{in }\Omega\,.

(3.5)

Moreover, if
\Sigma
satisfies
Almost Flatness
with
\varepsilon
small enough then

\|u\|_{C^{0}(\Omega)}\;\leq\;4(d^{2}_{\Omega}+\gamma^{2})\,.

(3.6)

Proof.

The
C^{1}
-regularity of
u
follows directly from
[
AK81
, Thm. 1]
after using a standard argument to locally straighten the boundary of
\partial\Omega
.

We now show that
u\geq 0
by showing that its minimum is achieved on
\partial\Omega\cap K
, where
u
vanishes. Indeed, the minimum can not be achieved in the interior by super harmonicity, and the minimum of
u
cannot be achieved on
\Sigma
by applying Hopf’s lemma, since
Hydrophilic
implies

\partial_{\nu}u(x)\;=\;\gamma\;\geq\;0\,.

(3.7)

To show (
3.6
), consider
v\in C^{2}(\Omega\cup\Sigma)\cap C^{1}(\overline{\Omega})
given by

v(x):=u(x)+\frac{|x|^{2}}{2(n+1)}+\frac{\gamma+\frac{\varepsilon}{n+1}d_{\Omega}}{1-\varepsilon}x_{n+1}\,,

(3.8)

where
\varepsilon
is given by the
Almost Flatness
hypothesis.
Then,
v
is the unique solution to

\begin{cases}\hskip 12.80365pt-\Delta v=0&\mbox{ in $\Omega$}\\
\hskip 15.93347ptv(x)=\frac{|x|^{2}}{2(n+1)}+\frac{\gamma+\frac{\varepsilon}{n+1}d_{\Omega}}{1-\varepsilon}x_{n+1}&\mbox{ on $M$}\\
\partial_{\nu_{K}}v=\gamma+\frac{1}{n+1}x\cdot\nu_{K}+\frac{\gamma+\frac{\varepsilon}{n+1}d_{\Omega}}{1-\varepsilon}e_{n+1}\cdot\nu_{K}&\mbox{ on $\Sigma$},\end{cases}

(3.9)

By the
Almost Flatness
hypothesis, we have that

|x\cdot\nu_{K}|\leq\varepsilon d_{\Omega}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad e_{n+1}\cdot\nu_{K}\leq-(1-\varepsilon),

which implies that

\partial_{\nu_{K}}v\leq 0\qquad\mbox{ on $\Sigma$.}

By harmonicity and Hopf’s lemma, we have that
v
satisfies

\sup_{\overline{\Omega}}u-2(d_{\Omega}^{2}+\gamma^{2})\leq\sup_{\overline{\Omega}}v\,\leq\,\sup_{M}v\,=\,\sup_{x\in M}\Big(\frac{|x|^{2}}{2(n+1)}+\frac{\gamma+\frac{\varepsilon}{n+1}d_{\Omega}}{1-\varepsilon}x_{n+1}\Big)\,\leq\,2(d_{\Omega}^{2}+\gamma^{2})\,.

(3.10)

Thus, (
3.6
) holds.
∎

The main point of this section is to prove Propositions
3.3
and
3.4
, which will be fundamental in the proof of our main compactness theorem.

Proposition 3.3
.

Under the hypothesis of Section
2
, assume further that the substrate is
Hydrophilic
, that
\Sigma
satisfies
Almost Flatness
with
\varepsilon
small enough, and that the mean curvature
H_{M}
is bounded away from zero. Then,

\displaystyle\frac{|\Omega|-\gamma\mathcal{H}^{n}(\Sigma)}{n+1}\left(\int_{M}\frac{n}{H_{M}}d\mathcal{H}^{n}-(n+1)\big(|\Omega|-\gamma\mathcal{H}^{n}(\Sigma)\big)\right)

(3.11)

\displaystyle\qquad+2(n+1)\varepsilon\left(\int_{M}\frac{d\mathcal{H}^{n}}{H_{M}}\left(\frac{|\Omega|}{n+1}+4(d_{\Omega}^{2}+\gamma^{2})(|\Omega|+\gamma\mathcal{H}^{n}(\Sigma))\right)+\max\frac{1}{H_{M}}\left(|\Omega|-\gamma\mathcal{H}^{n}(\Sigma)\right)^{2}\right)

(3.12)

\displaystyle\geq(1-2(n+1)\varepsilon)\int_{M}\frac{d\mathcal{H}^{n}}{H_{M}}\int_{\Omega}\left(|\nabla^{2}u|^{2}-\frac{(\Delta u)^{2}}{n+1}\right)\;dx

(3.13)

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{M}\frac{d\mathcal{H}^{n}}{H_{M}}\int_{M}|\nabla u|^{2}(H_{M}-2(n+1)\varepsilon)d\mathcal{H}^{n}-\left(1-2(n+1)\varepsilon\max\frac{1}{H_{M}}\right)\left(\int_{M}|\nabla u|d\mathcal{H}^{n}\right)^{2}

(3.14)

\displaystyle\geq 0\,.

(3.15)

Proof of Proposition
3.3
.

By Lemma
3.5
and Lemma
3.6
we have that

\displaystyle\frac{|\Omega|-\gamma\mathcal{H}^{n}(\Sigma)}{n+1}\left(\int_{M}\frac{n}{H_{M}}d\mathcal{H}^{n}-(n+1)\big(|\Omega|-\gamma\mathcal{H}^{n}(\Sigma)\big)\right)

(3.16)

\displaystyle\geq\int_{M}\frac{d\mathcal{H}^{n}}{H_{M}}\int_{\Omega}\left((1-2(n+1)\varepsilon)|\nabla^{2}u|^{2}-\frac{(\Delta u)^{2}}{n+1}\right)\;dx

(3.17)

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{M}\frac{d\mathcal{H}^{n}}{H_{M}}\int_{M}|\nabla u|^{2}(H_{M}-2(n+1)\varepsilon)d\mathcal{H}^{n}-\left(\int_{M}|\nabla u|d\mathcal{H}^{n}\right)^{2}.

(3.18)

The first inequaliy in (
3.15
) now follows by using that
(\Delta u)^{2}=1
and the divergence Theorem to obtain

\left(\int_{M}|\nabla u|\right)^{2}=(|\Omega|-\gamma\mathcal{H}^{n}(\Sigma))^{2}.

To obtain the positivity we notice that by Cauchy-Schwarz

\inf\frac{H_{M}-C(n)\varepsilon}{H_{M}}\left(\int_{M}|\nabla u|\right)^{2}\leq\left(\int_{M}|\nabla u|\frac{\sqrt{H_{M}-C(n)\varepsilon}}{\sqrt{H_{M}}}\right)^{2}\leq\int_{M}\frac{1}{H_{M}}\int_{M}|\nabla u|^{2}(H_{M}-C(n)\varepsilon),

with
C(n)=2(n+1)
.
∎

Proposition 3.4
.

Under the hypothesis of Section
2
, assume further that the substrate is
Hydrophilic
with constant
\delta_{0}
, that
\Sigma
satisfies
Almost Flatness
, and that
\varepsilon
,
\|\theta-\theta_{0}\|_{C^{0}}
and
\frac{|H_{M}-\lambda|_{C_{0}}}{\lambda}
are small enough. Then, we have the bound

\displaystyle\left|(n+1)(|\Omega|-\gamma\mathcal{H}^{n}(\Sigma))-\int_{M}\frac{n}{H}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n}\right|

(3.19)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq C(\delta_{0})\left(\varepsilon+\|\theta-\theta_{0}\|_{C^{0}(\partial\Sigma)}+\frac{|H_{M}-\lambda|_{C_{0}}}{\lambda}\right)\left(d_{\Omega}+\frac{1}{\lambda}\right)\mathcal{H}^{n}(M)\,,

(3.20)

where
\gamma
is as in (
3.4
).

Proof of Proposition
3.4
.

We consider the vector field
X=x
, and we apply the divergence theorem to obtain

(n+1)|\Omega|\;=\;\int_{M}x\cdot\nu\,d\mathcal{H}^{n}\,+\,\int_{\Sigma}x\cdot\nu_{V}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n}\,.

(3.21)

By
Almost Flatness
, we have

\left|\int_{\Sigma}x\cdot\nu_{V}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n}\right|\leq\varepsilon d_{\Omega}\mathcal{H}^{n}(\Sigma)\,,

which will become a part of the remainder. For the first term in (
3.21
), the tangential divergence theorem yields the following identity:

\int_{M}x\cdot\nu\;d\mathcal{H}^{n}=\frac{n}{\lambda}\mathcal{H}^{n}(M)+\frac{1}{\lambda}\int_{\partial\Sigma}x\cdot\nu_{co}^{M}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}+\int_{M}\left(1-\frac{H_{M}}{\lambda}\right)(x\cdot\nu)\;d\mathcal{H}^{n}\,.

(3.22)

We notice the last term of (
3.22
) can be lumped into the remainder

\left|\int_{M}\left(1-\frac{H_{M}}{\lambda}\right)(x\cdot\nu)\;d\mathcal{H}^{n}\right|\leq d_{\Omega}\mathcal{H}^{n}(M)\frac{|H_{M}-\lambda|_{C_{0}}}{\lambda}\,.

(3.23)

For the second term of (
3.22
), we use the almost constant angle condition to obtain the identity

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Sigma}x\cdot\nu_{co}^{M}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}

\displaystyle=-\cos(\theta_{0})\int_{\partial\Sigma}x\cdot\nu_{\Sigma}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}+\int_{\partial\Sigma}(\cos(\theta_{0})-\cos(\theta))x\cdot\nu_{\Sigma}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}

(3.24)

\displaystyle\hskip 140.84105pt+\int_{\partial\Sigma}\sin(\theta)x\cdot\nu_{V}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\,.

(3.25)

The last two terms can be lumped into the remainder by the bounds

\left|\int_{\partial\Sigma}(\cos(\theta_{0})-\cos(\theta))x\cdot\nu_{\Sigma}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\right|\leq\|\theta-\theta_{0}\|_{C^{0}(\partial\Sigma)}d_{\Omega}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial\Sigma)

and

\left|\int_{\partial\Sigma}\sin(\theta)x\cdot\nu_{V}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\right|\leq\varepsilon d_{\Omega}H^{n-1}(\partial\Sigma).

For the first term in (
3.25
), we can use the tangential divergence theorem on
\Sigma
to obtain

\int_{\partial\Sigma}x\cdot\nu_{\Sigma}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}=n\mathcal{H}^{n}(\Sigma)-\int_{\Sigma}H_{\partial K}x\cdot\nu_{V}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n},

and we can bound

\left|\int_{\Sigma}H_{\partial K}x\cdot\nu_{V}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n}\right|\leq\varepsilon^{2}d_{\Omega}\mathcal{H}^{n}(\Sigma)

Putting all the previous identities together, we obtain that there exists a constant depending on
d_{\Omega}
,
\mathcal{H}^{n}(M)
,
\|H_{\Sigma}\|_{C^{0}}
and
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial\Sigma)
, such that

\displaystyle\left|(n+1)|\Omega|+\frac{\cos(\theta_{0})n\mathcal{H}^{n}(\Sigma)}{\lambda}-\frac{n\mathcal{H}^{n}(M)}{\lambda}\right|

(3.26)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\varepsilon d_{\Omega}\left(2\mathcal{H}^{n}(\Sigma)+\frac{\mathcal{H}^{n}(\partial\Sigma)}{\lambda}\right)+d_{\Omega}\mathcal{H}^{n}(M)\frac{|H_{M}-\lambda|_{C_{0}}}{\lambda}+\|\theta-\theta_{0}\|_{C^{0}(\partial\Sigma)}d_{\Omega}\frac{H^{n-1}(\partial\Sigma)}{\lambda}\,.

(3.27)

Next, using the mean curvature deficit we can bound the difference

\left|\frac{n\mathcal{H}^{n}(M)}{\lambda}-\int_{M}\frac{n}{H}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n}\right|\;\leq\;\frac{\|H-\lambda\|_{C^{0}}}{\lambda}\int_{M}\frac{n}{H}\;dH^{n}\;\leq\;\frac{\|H-\lambda\|_{C^{0}}}{\lambda}2n\frac{\mathcal{H}^{n}(M)}{\lambda}\,,

where we have assumed that the mean curvature deficit is small enough.

Using Lemma
3.7
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\cos(\theta_{0})n\mathcal{H}^{n}(\Sigma)}{\lambda}-(n+1)\gamma_{0}\mathcal{H}^{n}(\Sigma)\right|

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\cot\theta_{0}\left(\frac{6}{\sin(\theta_{0})}(\varepsilon+\|\theta-\theta_{0}\|_{C^{0}})+\frac{\|H-\lambda\|_{C^{0}}}{\lambda}\right)\frac{\mathcal{H}^{n}(M)\mathcal{H}^{n}(\Sigma)}{\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial\Sigma)},

where

\gamma_{0}=\frac{n}{n+1}\frac{\mathcal{H}^{n}(\Sigma)}{\tan(\theta_{0})\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial\Sigma)}.

Using that
\theta
is bounded away from
\pi/2
by the hypothesis
Hydrophilic
, we have the bound

|(n+1)\gamma_{0}\mathcal{H}^{n}(\Sigma)-(n+1)\gamma|\leq C(\delta_{0})\frac{\mathcal{H}^{n}(\Sigma)^{2}}{\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial\Sigma)}\|\theta-\theta_{0}\|_{C^{0}}.

Finally, combining the above inequalities with the bound on
\mathcal{H}^{n}(\partial\Sigma)
from Lemma
3.7
, we obtain the bound in the statement of the Proposition.
∎

In this section, we develop the lemmas used in the proofs of Propositions
3.3
and
3.4
. We start by exploiting the boundary regularity of the torsion potential
u
in the hydrophilic regime (
3.5
) to prove a Reilly’s-type identity.

Lemma 3.5
.

Under the hypothesis of Section
2
, assume further that the substrate is
Hydrophilic
. Then,
u
satisfies the identities

\frac{n}{n+1}(|\Omega|-\gamma\mathcal{H}^{n}(\Sigma))+\int_{\Omega}\frac{(\Delta u)^{2}}{n+1}-|D^{2}u|^{2}=\int_{M}(u_{\nu})^{2}H+\int_{\Sigma}\gamma^{2}H_{\partial K}+A_{\partial K}[\nabla^{\Sigma}u,\nabla^{\Sigma}u]\,,

(3.28)

and

\displaystyle\frac{|\Omega|-\gamma\mathcal{H}^{n}(\Sigma)}{n+1}

\displaystyle\left(\int_{M}\frac{n}{H}-(n+1)\big(|\Omega|-\gamma\mathcal{H}^{n}(\Sigma)\big)\right)

(3.29)

\displaystyle=\int_{M}\frac{1}{H}\int_{\Omega}\left(|\nabla^{2}u|^{2}-\frac{(\Delta u)^{2}}{n+1}\right)-\left(\int_{M}|\nabla u|\right)^{2}

(3.30)

\displaystyle\hskip 31.2982pt+\int_{M}\frac{1}{H}\left(\int_{M}|\nabla u|^{2}H+\int_{\Sigma}\gamma^{2}H_{\partial K}+A_{\partial K}[\nabla^{\Sigma}u,\nabla^{\Sigma}u]\right)\,.\;\;\;

(3.31)

Proof.

We re-write the following expression as a divergence:

(\Delta u)^{2}-|D^{2}u|^{2}=\nabla\cdot(\nabla u\Delta u-D^{2}u\cdot\nabla u)\,.

(3.32)

Hence, by using
-\Delta u=1
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{n}{n+1}|\Omega|+\int_{\Omega}\frac{(\Delta u)^{2}}{n+1}-|D^{2}u|^{2}=\int_{\partial\Omega}u_{\nu}\Delta u-D^{2}u[\nabla u,\nu]\,.

(3.33)

On
M
, we can use Reilly’s identity. Indeed, because
u=0
on
M
and
\nabla u=u_{\nu}\nu
, we have that pointwise on
M

u_{\nu}\Delta u-D^{2}u[\nabla u,\nu]=u_{\nu}\sum_{i=1}^{n}D^{2}u[\tau_{i},\tau_{i}]=u_{\nu}\Delta_{M}u+H(u_{\nu})^{2}=H(u_{\nu})^{2}\,,

(3.34)

where
\{\tau_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
is a basis of the tangent space and
\Delta_{M}
is the Laplace-Beltrami operator on
M
. Therefore,

\int_{M}u_{\nu}\Delta u-D^{2}u[\nabla u,\nu]=\int_{M}H(u_{\nu})^{2}\,.

(3.35)

Next, given a point
p\in\Sigma
and a basis
\{\tau_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
for
T_{p}\Sigma
we find pointwise

\displaystyle u_{\nu}

\displaystyle\Delta u-D^{2}u[\nabla u,\nu]

(3.36)

\displaystyle=\;u_{\nu}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}D^{2}u[\tau_{i},\tau_{i}]+D^{2}u[\nu,\nu]\right\}\;-\;\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle\nabla u,\tau_{i}\rangle\,D^{2}u[\tau_{i},\nu]+\langle\nabla u,\nu\rangle\,D^{2}u[\nu,\nu]\right\}

(3.37)

\displaystyle=\;u_{\nu}\,\sum_{i=1}^{n}D^{2}u[\tau_{i},\tau_{i}]\;-\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle\nabla u,\tau_{i}\rangle\,D^{2}u[\tau_{i},\nu]

(3.38)

\displaystyle=\;u_{\nu}\,\Delta_{\Sigma}u\,+\,H\,(u_{\nu})^{2}\;-\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle\nabla u,\tau_{i}\rangle\,D^{2}u[\tau_{i},\nu]\,.

(3.39)

We now compute the Hessian term in the sum to be

\displaystyle D^{2}u[\tau_{i},\nu]\;

\displaystyle=\;\langle D_{\tau_{i}}\nabla u,\nu\rangle

(3.40)

\displaystyle=\;D_{\tau_{i}}\langle\nabla u,\nu\rangle\,-\,\langle\nabla u,D_{\tau_{i}}\nu\rangle

(3.41)

\displaystyle=\;0\,-\,\langle\nabla^{\Sigma}u,D_{\tau_{i}}\nu\rangle\,-\,u_{\nu}\langle\nu,D_{\tau_{i}}\nu\rangle

(3.42)

\displaystyle=\;\,-\,A_{\Sigma}[\nabla^{\Sigma}u,\tau_{i}]\,,

(3.43)

where we used the facts that
u_{\nu}
is constant on
\Sigma
(and hence
D_{\tau_{i}}u_{\nu}\equiv 0
) and that

\langle\nu,D_{\tau_{i}}\nu\rangle=D_{\tau_{i}}\langle\nu,\nu\rangle-\langle D_{\tau_{i}}\nu,\nu\rangle=-\langle D_{\tau_{i}}\nu,\nu\rangle\,,

(3.44)

which directly implies that
\langle\nu,D_{\tau_{i}}\nu\rangle\equiv 0
. Thus, we can rewrite (
3.39
) to be

\displaystyle u_{\nu}\Delta u-D^{2}u[\nabla u,\nu]\;

\displaystyle=\;u_{\nu}\,\Delta_{\Sigma}u\,+\,H(u_{\nu})^{2}\;-\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle\nabla u,\tau_{i}\rangle\,D^{2}u[\tau_{i},\nu]

(3.45)

\displaystyle=\;u_{\nu}\,\Delta_{\Sigma}u\,+\,H(u_{\nu})^{2}\,+\,\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle\nabla u,\tau_{i}\rangle\,A_{\Sigma}[\nabla^{\Sigma}u,\tau_{i}]

(3.46)

\displaystyle=\;u_{\nu}\,\Delta_{\Sigma}u\,+\,H(u_{\nu})^{2}\,+\,A_{\Sigma}[\nabla^{\Sigma}u,\nabla^{\Sigma}u]

(3.47)

Hence, by the divergence theorem on
\Sigma
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}u_{\nu}\Delta u-D^{2}u[\nabla u,\nu]\;

\displaystyle=\;\gamma\int_{\Sigma}\Delta_{\Sigma}u+\gamma^{2}H_{\Sigma}+A_{\Sigma}[\nabla^{\Sigma}u,\nabla^{\Sigma}u]

(3.48)

\displaystyle=\;\gamma\int_{\partial\Sigma}\nabla u\cdot\nu_{\Sigma}\,+\,\int_{\Sigma}\gamma^{2}H_{\Sigma}+A_{\Sigma}[\nabla^{\Sigma}u,\nabla^{\Sigma}u]\,.

(3.49)

We now use the definition of the angle to write the co-normal of
M
on
\partial\Sigma
as

\nu_{co}=-\cos(\theta)\nu_{\Sigma}+\sin(\theta)\nu_{K}\,.

(3.50)

As noted above, we have that
\nabla u=u_{\nu}\nu
on
M
, and hence on
\partial M=\partial\Sigma
by the
C^{1}
regularity on
\overline{\Omega}
. So, we find that pointwise on
\partial\Sigma

0=\nabla u\cdot\nu_{co}=-\cos(\theta)\nabla u\cdot\nu_{\Sigma}+\gamma\sin(\theta)\,.

(3.51)

We thus arrive at the pointwise identity

\nabla u\cdot\nu_{\Sigma}=\gamma\frac{\sin(\theta)}{\cos(\theta)}\quad\textnormal{on }\partial\Sigma\,,

(3.52)

which we substitute into (
3.49
) to find

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}u_{\nu}\Delta u-D^{2}u[\nabla u,\nu]\;

\displaystyle=\;\gamma^{2}\int_{\partial\Sigma}\frac{\sin(\theta)}{\cos(\theta)}\,+\,\int_{\Sigma}\gamma^{2}H_{\Sigma}+A[\nabla^{\Sigma}u,\nabla^{\Sigma}u]

(3.53)

\displaystyle=\;\gamma\frac{n}{n+1}\mathcal{H}^{n}(\Sigma)\,+\,\int_{\Sigma}\gamma^{2}H_{\Sigma}+A[\nabla^{\Sigma}u,\nabla^{\Sigma}u]\,,

(3.54)

where the last equality follows from the definition of
\gamma
. The identity (
3.28
) then follows by putting together (
3.33
), (
3.35
) and (
3.54
).

By the divergence theorem one has

|\Omega|-\gamma\mathcal{H}^{n}(\Sigma)=\int_{M}|\nabla u|\,,

(3.55)

so by using this identity and (
3.28
) we can directly expand the right-hand-side of (
3.31
) to find

\displaystyle\int_{M}\frac{1}{H}\int_{\Omega}\left(|\nabla^{2}u|-\frac{(\Delta u)^{2}}{n+1}\right)+\int_{M}\frac{1}{H}\left(\int_{M}|\nabla u|^{2}H+\int_{\Sigma}\gamma^{2}H+A[\nabla^{\Sigma}u,\nabla^{\Sigma}u]\right)-\left(\int_{M}|\nabla u|\right)^{2}

(3.56)

\displaystyle=\int_{M}\frac{1}{H}\left(\int_{\Omega}|\nabla^{2}u|-\frac{(\Delta u)^{2}}{n+1}+\int_{M}|\nabla u|^{2}H+\int_{\Sigma}\gamma^{2}H+A[\nabla^{\Sigma}u,\nabla^{\Sigma}u]\right)-\left(\int_{M}|\nabla u|\right)^{2}

(3.57)

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{M}\frac{1}{H}\right)\frac{n}{n+1}\big(|\Omega|-\gamma\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\Sigma)\big)\;-\;\big(|\Omega|-\gamma\mathcal{H}^{n}(\Sigma)\big)^{2}

(3.58)

\displaystyle=\frac{|\Omega|-\gamma\mathcal{H}^{n}(\Sigma)}{n+1}\left(\int_{M}\frac{n}{H}-(n+1)\big(|\Omega|-\gamma\mathcal{H}^{n}(\Sigma)\big)\right)\,,

(3.59)

which is (
3.31
).
∎

Next, we utilize the
Almost Flatness
hypothesis to control the extra the terms in the wetted region.

Lemma 3.6
.

Under the hypothesis of Section
2
, assume further that the substrate is
Hydrophilic
,
\Sigma
satisfies
Almost Flatness
with parameter
\varepsilon<1/2
, then

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\Sigma}\big(\gamma^{2}H_{\Sigma}+A_{\Sigma}[\nabla^{\Sigma}u,\nabla^{\Sigma}u]\big)\,d\mathcal{H}^{n}\right|

(3.60)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\;2(n+1)\varepsilon\left(\int_{\Omega}\big(|D^{2}u|^{2}+|\nabla u|^{2}\big)dx+\int_{M}|\nabla u|^{2}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n}\right)

(3.61)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\;2(n+1)\varepsilon\left(4(d_{\Omega}^{2}+\gamma^{2})(|\Omega|+\gamma\mathcal{H}^{n}(\Sigma))+\int_{\Omega}|D^{2}u|^{2}\;dx+\int_{M}|\nabla u|^{2}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n}\right)\,,\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;

(3.62)

where
u\in C^{2}(\Omega\cup\Sigma)\cap C^{1}(\overline{\Omega})
is the unique solution to (
3.9
).

Proof.

We consider the vector field
-|\nabla u|^{2}e_{n+1}
, and apply the divergence theorem to obtain

\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla u|^{2}(-e_{n+1}\cdot\nu_{\Sigma})\,d\mathcal{H}^{n}=\int_{M}|\nabla u|^{2}e_{n+1}\cdot\nu_{M}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n}+2\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\nabla\partial_{n+1}u\;dx

From the
Almost Flatness
hypothesis, we obtain

(1-\varepsilon)\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla u|^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n}\leq\int_{M}|\nabla u|^{2}\;d\mathcal{H}^{n}+\int_{\Omega}|D^{2}u|^{2}+|\nabla u|^{2}\;dx\,.

(3.63)

Using the previous inequality with
\varepsilon<1/2
, we can directly bound

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\Sigma}\big(\gamma^{2}H_{\Sigma}+A_{\Sigma}[\nabla u,\nabla u]\big)\,d\mathcal{H}^{n}\right|\;

\displaystyle\leq\;(n+1)\,\|A_{\Sigma}\|_{C^{0}(\Sigma)}\,\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla u|^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n}

(3.64)

\displaystyle\leq\;2(n+1)\,\|A_{\Sigma}\|_{C^{0}(\Sigma)}\,\left(\int_{\Omega}\big(|D^{2}u|^{2}+|\nabla u|^{2}\big)dx+\int_{M}|\nabla u|^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n}\right)\,.

(3.65)

The first inequality in (
3.62
) then follows from the

hypothesis

. For the second inequality, we integrate by parts and use (
3.9
) to obtain

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}\;dx=\int_{\Omega}u\;dx+\gamma\int_{\Sigma}u\;d\mathcal{H}^{n}\leq\|u\|_{\infty}(|\Omega|+\gamma\mathcal{H}^{n}(\Sigma))\,,

and the desired inequality now follows from applying the

-estimate (
3.6
).

Finally, we use the

Lemma 3.7

\displaystyle\delta V_{\infty}[X]

\displaystyle=\lim_{h\to\infty}\int_{M_{h}}{\rm div}_{\tau}X\,d\mathcal{H}^{n}

(4.37)

\displaystyle=\lim_{h\to\infty}n\int_{M_{h}}X\cdot\nu\,d\mathcal{H}^{n}

(4.38)

\displaystyle=\lim_{h\to\infty}n\int_{\Omega_{h}}{\rm div}_{n+1}X\,dx

(4.39)

\displaystyle=n\int_{\Omega_{\infty}}{\rm div}_{n+1}X\,dx

(4.40)

\displaystyle=n\int_{\partial\Omega_{\infty}}X\cdot\nu\,d\mathcal{H}^{n}

(4.41)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in I}n\int_{\partial B_{r_{i}}(y_{i})}X\cdot\nu\,d\mathcal{H}^{n}\,,

(4.42)

Hence we may directly conclude that
r_{i}
is constantly equal to
\Xi_{1}
in each spherical cap
\partial B_{i}
, and the fact that
r_{i}=\Xi_{1}=1\pmod{2}
follows from Theorem
4.1
.

Step five.
In this step we show that for all the radii
r_{i}
are all equal to
1
, and to do so we will use a sliding argument. Fix an index
i\in I
. If
r_{i}=1
, then we are of course done, so suppose for sake of contradiction that
r_{i}\neq 1
. By the previous step, we have that
r_{i}\geq 3
. If
B_{r_{i}}(y_{i})
does not intersect the plane
\{x_{n+1}=0\}
, the proof that
r_{i}=1
follows from obvious modifications of the argument below. Thus, we will assume that
B_{r_{i}}(y_{i})
intersects the plane
\{x_{n+1}=0\}
with angle
\theta_{i}

\theta_{i}\in(0,\pi/2)
and
\theta_{i}\in[\pi/2,\pi)
separately.

Case I:

\theta_{i}\in(0,\pi/2)
. By the Hausdorff convergence of the boundary we have that for any
\varepsilon>0
and any
\eta>0
there exists
l
large enough such that
\Sigma_{l}\subset\{x_{n+1}<\eta/2\}
and

B_{r_{i}-\varepsilon}(y_{i})\cap\{x_{n+1}>\eta\}\subseteq\Omega_{l}\cap\{x_{n+1}>\eta\}\,.

(4.43)

Using that
\theta_{i}\in(0,\pi/2)
, if we translate
\partial B_{r_{i}-\varepsilon}(y_{i})\cap\{x_{n+1}>\eta\}
vertically then the first touching point with
\partial\Omega_{l}
can not happen at
\{x_{n+1}=\eta\}
for
\eta

\frac{n}{r_{i}-\varepsilon}\geq H_{\partial\Omega_{l}}>n-\delta
. Using that
r_{i}\geq 3
, we get a contradiction by taking
\varepsilon
and
\eta
small enough.

Case II:

\theta_{i}\in[\pi/2,1)
. We first define the height

h_{i}:=-y_{i}\cdot e_{n+1}\,,

(4.44)

which is the distance of the center of the ball
B_{1}(y_{i})
from the plane
\{x_{n+1}=0\}
(note the convention
h_{i}\geq 0
).
We will now let
\varepsilon>0
be such that

\varepsilon\;<\;\min\left\{\frac{1}{2r_{i}}\,,\;\frac{r_{i}-h_{i}}{r_{i}+h_{i}}\right\}

(4.45)

By the local Hausdorff convergence of the boundary (setting
\eta=\varepsilon
for simplicity), for
l

\emptyset\neq M_{l}\cap B_{r_{i}+\varepsilon}(y_{i})\cap\{x_{n+1}>\varepsilon\}\;\subseteq\;B_{r_{i}-\varepsilon}(y_{i})^{c}\cap\{x_{n+1}>\varepsilon\}\,.

(4.46)

If necessary, we will take
l
larger so that
\Sigma_{l}\subset\{x_{n+1}<\varepsilon\}
. For
t\in(0,1+\varepsilon)
the mapping

t\;\mapsto\;B_{r_{i}-1}(y_{i}+te_{n+1})\cap\{x_{n+1}>\varepsilon\}

(4.47)

corresponds to vertically sliding the ball
B_{r_{i}-1}(y_{i})
until it touches the boundary
\partial B_{r_{i}+\varepsilon}(y_{i})
, which serves as an upper for the furthest one would need to translate in order to touch
M_{l}
. If the sliding ball (
4.47
) does not intersect the boundary of the disk
B_{r_{i}-\varepsilon}(y_{i})\cap\{x_{n+1}=\varepsilon\}
for any

, then the first point of contact with

will be tangential, and the same argument will hold as in

, thereby allowing us to conclude that
r_{i}=1
. Thus, we are reduced to showing that

\min_{t\in[0,1+\varepsilon]}{\rm dist}\Big(\partial B_{r_{i}-1}(y_{i}+te_{n+1})\cap\{x_{n+1}=\varepsilon\}\,,\,\partial B_{r_{i}-\varepsilon}(y_{i})\cap\{x_{n+1}=\varepsilon\}\Big)\;>\;0\,.

(4.48)

We note that both of the sets considered in (
4.48
) are disks with the same center, hence to check the desired property we need to show that for

Computationally, we break the proof into two subcases,
I
when the minimum is achieved for some

, and
II
when it is achieved at

. To alleviate the notation, without loss of generality we consider the case that

is on the

Subcase I:
There exists a time

such that

(4.49)

which implies that we have the inequality

h_{i}=t_{0}-\varepsilon<1,

(4.50)

as

.
Using the Pythagorean theorem, we see that the desired inequality (

{\rm radius}\big(B_{r_{i}-\varepsilon}(-(h_{i}+\varepsilon)e_{n+1})\cap\{x_{n+1}=0\}\big)=\sqrt{(r_{i}-\varepsilon)^{2}-(h_{i}+\varepsilon)^{2}}\geq r_{i}-1\,,

which directly follows from the fact that
r_{i}\geq 3
,
h_{i}<1
by (
4.50

\varepsilon<\frac{1}{2r_{i}}
by (
4.45
).

Subcase II:
Assuming (
4.49

{\rm radius}\Big(B_{r_{i}-\varepsilon}\big(-(h_{i}+\varepsilon)e_{n+1}\big)\cap\{x_{n+1}=0\}\Big)\geq{\rm radius}\Big(B_{r_{i}-1}\big((-h_{i}+1)e_{n+1}\big)\{x_{n+1}=0\}\Big)\,.

(4.52)

Using the Pythagorean theorem and squaring both sides, this is equivalent to

(r_{i}-\varepsilon)^{2}-(h_{i}+\varepsilon)^{2}\geq(r_{i}-1)^{2}-(-h_{i}+1)^{2}\,

which follows from the choice of
\varepsilon<\frac{r_{i}-h_{i}}{r_{i}+h_{i}}
in (
4.45
).

Step six.
In this step we will show that the angles with which the components of
\Omega_{\infty}
intersect the plane
\{x_{n+1}=0\}
must all be equal to
\theta_{0}
. More specifically, if we let

denote the subset of indices such that

for all

. As a byproduct of the proof, we will also show that

.

We consider a test vector field

such that
X\cdot e_{n+1}=0
on
\{x_{n+1}=0\}
, for which it holds that

\delta V_{l}[X]=\int_{M_{l}}H_{l}X\cdot\nu_{\Omega_{l}}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n}+\int_{\partial\Sigma_{l}}\Big(\sin(\theta_{l})X\cdot\nu_{K_{l}}-\cos(\theta_{l})X\cdot\nu_{\Sigma_{l}}\Big)\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\,.

(4.53)

Using the varifold convergence
V_{l}\rightharpoonup V_{\infty}
, the convergence of both the mean curvature (
4.9
) and angle (
4.10
), the convergence of
\nu_{K_{l}}\to e_{n+1}
by (

from Lemma

(4.54)

which in turn implies by (
4.15
) and (
4.17
) that

\delta V_{\infty}[X]=n\int_{\Omega_{\infty}}{\rm div}_{n+1}X\,dx-\cos(\theta_{0})\int_{\Sigma_{\infty}}{\rm div}_{n}X\,d\mathcal{H}^{n}\,.

(4.55)

Since the boundary of
\Omega_{\infty}=\bigcup_{i\in I}B_{1}(y_{i})\cap\{x_{n+1}>0\}
has constant mean curvature equal to
n
, we can apply the divergence theorem again to obtain that

\displaystyle\delta V_{\infty}[X]\;

\displaystyle=\;\sum_{i\in I}\int_{\partial B_{1}(y_{i})\cap H}{\rm div}_{\tau}X\,d\mathcal{H}^{n}

(4.56)

\displaystyle\hskip 19.91684pt+\;\sum_{i\in I^{\prime}}\cos(\theta_{i})\int_{B_{1}(y_{i})\cap\{x_{n+1=0}\}}{\rm div}_{n}X\,d\mathcal{H}^{n}-\cos(\theta_{0})\int_{\Sigma_{\infty}}{\rm div}_{n}X\,d\mathcal{H}^{n}\,,\,.

(4.57)

At the same time, we claim that we can write
\delta V_{\infty}
as

\delta V_{\infty}[X]\;=\;\sum_{i\in I}\int_{\partial B_{1}(y_{i})\cap H}{\rm div}_{\tau}Xd\mathcal{H}^{n}\,+\,\int_{\Omega_{\infty}\cap\{x_{n+1}=0\}\setminus\Sigma_{\infty}}{\rm div}_{n}X\,\Xi_{1}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n}\,+\,\int_{S}{\rm div}_{n}X\,\Xi_{0}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n}\,,

for

n
-rectifiable subset of the plane

disjoint from

, and
\Xi_{0}
and

and
\Xi_{1}=1\pmod{2}
. Indeed, for

defined as in Theorem

, we can use (

) and
Step five
to write

V_{\infty}\;=\;W_{\infty}-d\mathcal{H}^{n}|_{\Sigma_{\infty}}\;=\;d\mathcal{H}^{n}|_{\partial\Omega_{\infty}\cap\{x_{n+1}>0\}}+\Xi_{1}d\mathcal{H}^{n}|_{\Omega_{\infty}\cap\{x_{n+1}=0\}\setminus\Sigma_{\infty}}+\Xi_{0}d\mathcal{H}^{n}|_{S}\,,

(4.59)

where

is the set from Theorem

with multiplicity greater than

We now wish to show that

To this end, comparing (

\sum_{i\in I^{\prime}}\cos(\theta_{i})\chi_{B_{1}(y_{i})\cap\{x_{n+1}=0\}}-\cos(\theta_{0})\chi_{\Sigma_{\infty}}=\Xi_{0}\chi_{S}+\Xi_{1}\chi_{\Omega_{\infty}\cap\{x_{n+1}=0\}\setminus\Sigma_{\infty}}\,.

(4.60)

Given

, if the set

is not contained in

, then by (

\Xi_{1}=\cos(\theta_{i})
on

. The condition

\Xi_{0}\;=\;\cos(\theta_{i})-\cos(\theta_{0})\;=\;0\pmod{2},

which can only be satisfied if

for every

Step seven.
We claim that
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}|\partial\Sigma_{l}\rightharpoonup\mathcal{H}^{n-1}|\partial\Sigma_{\infty}

(4.65)

\displaystyle\lim_{l\to\infty}\int_{M_{l}}{\rm div}_{\tau}(\phi e_{n+1})

5.
Convergence of local minimizers

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

We argue by contradiction. Assume that
\{\Omega_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of smooth VCLMs in the hydrophilic regime with diameter
\varepsilon_{0}
and volume
|\Omega_{j}|=m_{j}\to 0
satisfying

•

\Omega_{j}
is connected,

•

P(\Omega_{j};K)\leq Cm_{j}^{\frac{n}{n+1}}
,

•

\Omega_{j}\subseteq Cm_{j}^{\frac{1}{n+1}}B_{1}(x_{j})
for some
x_{j}\in\partial K
,

•

\mathcal{H}^{n}(\Sigma_{j})>C^{-1}m_{j}^{\frac{n}{n+1}}
,

and such that for any angle
\theta

\lim_{j\to 0^{+}}\inf_{x,Q,\theta_{*}}m^{-1}|\Omega_{j}\triangle(m^{\frac{1}{n+1}}(x_{0}+Q[B_{\theta}]))|>0,

(5.1)

where
Q[B_{\theta}]
is a rigid transformation of a ball that intersects the
\{x_{n+1}=0\}
with angle
\theta
.

By the compactness of the container, up to a subsequence we can assume that
x_{j}\to x_{0}\in\partial K
. Then by re-centering and re-scaling, we have that (up to a rotation) the sequence

K_{j}:=\frac{K-x_{0}}{m_{j}}\;\to\;\{x_{n+1}>0\}.

Moreover, using that the boundary of
K
is
C^{2}
, we have that the re-scalings
K_{j}
converge to the half-space in the sense of (
4.7
), which is of course is one of the hypotheses of Theorem
1.6
.

To analogously re-scale and re-center our sets, we define

\tilde{E}_{j}=\frac{E_{j}-x_{0}}{m_{j}}\,,

which now satisfy

•

\tilde{\Omega}_{j}
is connected,

•

P(\tilde{\Omega}_{j};K_{j})\leq C
,

•

\tilde{\Omega}_{j}\subseteq B_{2C}(0)
,

•

\mathcal{H}^{n}(\tilde{\Sigma}_{j})>C^{-1}
.

Moreover, using the local minimality condition (properly re-scaled), we can derive the mean curvature equation

H_{\partial\tilde{\Omega}_{j}}+m^{\frac{1}{n+1}}g=\lambda_{j}\qquad\mbox{on $\partial\tilde{E}_{j}$}

and Young’s law

\nu_{\tilde{\Omega}_{j}}(y)\cdot\nu_{K^{j}}(y)=\sigma(m_{j}y+x_{0})\qquad\mbox{on $\tilde{\Sigma}_{j}\subset\partial K_{j}$}.

Hence, we have that

\|H_{\partial\tilde{\Omega}_{j}}-\lambda_{j}\|_{C^{0}(\partial\tilde{\Omega}_{j})}\leq m^{\frac{1}{n+1}}\|g\|_{\infty}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\|\theta(x)-\theta_{0}\|_{C^{0}(\tilde{\Sigma}_{j})}\leq C(\theta_{0})\|\sigma\|_{Lip(\partial K)}m_{j},

(5.2)

where we have used the Lipschitz regularity of the adhesion coefficient in the second inequality.

By Proposition
3.4
and Lemma
3.7
, we can control the perimeter of the wetted region

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{n}(\partial\tilde{\Sigma}_{j})\leq C(\theta_{0})\lambda_{j}\left(\mathcal{H}^{n}(\tilde{\Sigma}_{j})+m_{j}^{\frac{1}{n+1}}\|g\|_{C^{0}}P(\partial\tilde{\Omega}_{j};K_{j})\right).

(5.3)

Moreover, using that
|\tilde{\Omega}_{j}|=1
we can identify the limit of the Lagrange multiplier

\displaystyle\left|(n+1)\lambda_{j}+n\cos\theta_{0}\mathcal{H}^{n}(\tilde{\Sigma}_{j})-nP(\partial\tilde{\Omega}_{j};K_{j})\right|

(5.4)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq C(\theta_{0})d_{\tilde{\Omega}_{j}}(\varepsilon+\|\theta(x)-\theta_{0}\|_{C^{0}(\tilde{\Sigma}_{j})})\mathcal{H}^{n}(\tilde{\Sigma}_{j})\lambda_{j}+m_{j}^{\frac{1}{n+1}}\|g\|_{C^{0}}d_{\tilde{\Omega}_{j}}P(\partial\tilde{\Omega}_{j};K_{j})\,,

(5.5)

where
C(\theta_{0})
is the constant from (
3.27
) depending on
\theta_{0}
and
\varepsilon>0
is the parameter in
Almost Flatness
which can be taken as small as we like (for
j
large enough). Using upper and lower bounds on perimeter, we can conclude that, up to a subsequence, there exists
\lambda_{0}>0
such that

\lim_{j\to\infty}\lambda_{j}=\lambda_{0}>0\,,

and hence (
5.2
) may be recast as

\|H_{\partial\tilde{\Omega}_{j}}-\lambda_{0}\|_{C^{0}(\partial\tilde{\Omega}_{j})}\to 0\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\|\theta(x)-\theta_{0}\|_{C^{0}(\tilde{\partial\Sigma}_{j})}\to 0.

(5.6)

The desired convergence will follow from applying Theorem
1.6
to the sequence of sets
\{\tilde{\Omega}_{j}\}
; however, we still need to check the technical condition on the convergence of perimeter. To show this, we will exploit the local minimality property and apply
[
PM15
, Theorem 2.9]
to show that the perimeter of
\tilde{E}_{j}
converges strongly in the limit
j\to\infty
.

First, as the hypotheses of
[
PM15
, Theorem 2.9]
require the container to be exactly the half space, we need to rectify the boundary. For a small enough radius
r>0
, we can consider the inverse mapping that locally rectifies the boundary of
K
around
x_{0}
; more specifically, we take the
C^{1}
-diffeomorphism
f:\{x_{n+1}>0\}\cap B_{r}\to K\cap B_{r}(x_{0})
that, up to rotation, satisfies

\nabla f(x)={\rm id}+O(|x|)\,.

We next scale
f
by the sequence of length scales
m_{j}
to obtain the sequence of mappings

f_{j}(x)=m_{j}^{-\frac{1}{n+1}}\Big(f\big(m_{j}^{\frac{1}{n+1}}x\big)-x_{0}\Big)\,,

which are defined

f_{j}\,:\,m_{j}^{-\frac{1}{n+1}}\Big(\{x_{n+1}>0\}\cap B_{r}(x_{0})\Big)\;\to\;m_{j}^{-\frac{1}{n+1}}\Big(K\cap B_{r}-x_{0}\Big)\,.

(5.7)

Note that for
j
large enough each
f_{j}
is a small deformation of the identity on compact sets, i.e.

\nabla f_{j}(x)={\rm id}+O\big(m_{j}^{\frac{1}{n+1}}|x|\big)\,.

For
j
large enough, one has the inclusion
\tilde{E_{j}}\subset m_{j}^{-\frac{1}{n+1}}(K\cap B_{r}-x_{0})
, and hence we consider

G_{j}:=f_{j}^{-1}(\tilde{\Omega}_{j}),

which satisfies
G_{j}\subseteq\{x_{n+1}>0\}\cap B_{3C}
.
Translating the local minimality property of
\Omega_{j}
to these new sets, we have that for
j
large enough the set
G_{j}
is a local minimizer of the following anisotropic perimeter functional for variations of diameter less than
\varepsilon_{0}/2
:

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{F}}[G]=\int_{\partial^{*}G}|{\rm cof}(\nabla f_{j}(y))\nu_{F}(y)|d\mathcal{H}^{n}(y)+\int_{G\cap\partial H}\sigma(f_{j}(z))J^{\partial H}f_{j}(z)\;d\mathcal{H}^{n}(z)

(5.8)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+m_{j}^{\frac{1}{n+1}}\int_{G}g(f_{j}(x))Jf_{j}(x)\;dx\,,

(5.9)

where the

is the co-factor matrix of
\nabla f_{j}(y)
.

Next, to apply the compactness result
[
PM15
, Theorem 2.9]
, we need to transform the wetting energy into a surface integral. Following the idea in
[
PM15
, Lemma 6.1]
, we consider the vector field

T_{j}(x)=\sigma(f_{j}(\hat{x}))J^{\partial H}f_{j}(\hat{x})e_{n+1}\,,

where
\hat{x}
denotes the projection onto the boundary of the half-space
\{x_{n+1}=0\}
, and we notice that for a set of finite perimeter

we have by the divergence theorem

\int_{F\cap\partial H}\sigma(f_{j}(z))J^{\partial H}f_{j}(z)\;d\mathcal{H}^{n}(z)=-\int_{\partial^{*}F\cap H}T_{j}(y)\cdot\nu_{F}(y)\;d\mathcal{H}^{n}(y)+\int_{F\cap H}{\rm div}\,T_{j}(x)\;dx\,.

Using that
G_{j}
is a VCLM of (
5.9
), we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\partial^{*}G_{j}}|{\rm cof}(\nabla f_{j}(y))\nu_{G_{j}}(y)|-T_{j}(y)\cdot\nu_{G_{j}}(y)\ d\mathcal{H}^{n}(y)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\int_{\partial^{*}G}|{\rm cof}(\nabla f_{j}(y))\nu_{G}(y)|-T_{j}(y)\cdot\nu_{G}(y)\ d\mathcal{H}^{n}(y)+(\sup|{\rm div}\,T_{j}|+m_{j}^{\frac{1}{n+1}}\sup g)|G_{j}\triangle G|

for any set

satisfying
{\rm diam}(G\triangle G_{j})<\varepsilon_{0}/2
. Therefore,
G_{j}
is an almost minimizer of the pure anisotropic perimeter with elliptic integrand

\Phi_{j}(y,\nu)=|{\rm cof}(\nabla f_{j}(y))\nu|-T_{j}(y)\cdot\nu\,.

For
j
large enough
\Phi_{j}
are convex, positive one-homogeneous, and satisfy the requirements of
[
PM15
, Theorem 2.9]
.

Therefore, there exists a limiting set
G_{0}
such that
G_{j}\to G_{0}
in
L^{1}
and such that on
\{x_{n+1}>0\}
the norm of the Gauss-Green measures converge
\mu_{G_{j}}\to\mu_{G_{0}}
. Using that up to a rigid motion
f_{j}
converges in
C^{1}
to the identity map on compact sets, we obtain that the original scaled set also converges
\tilde{\Omega}_{j}\to G_{0}
in
L^{1}
and
P(\tilde{\Omega}_{j};K_{j})\to P(G_{0};\{x_{n+1}>0\})
in perimeter. Therefore,
G_{0}
is a union of balls of equal radius intersecting with constant angle
\theta_{0}
. By the local minimality of
\tilde{E}_{j}
, we obtain that
G_{0}
is also a local minima of the Gauss energy
\mathcal{F}_{K,\sigma,0}
, where
K=\{x_{n+1}>0\}
and the adhesion coefficient
\sigma=\cos\theta_{0}
is constant. Lemma
5.1
below implies that
G_{0}
is a single ball that intersects the hyperspace
\{x_{n+1}=0\}
with angle
\theta_{0}
, which contradicts (
5.1
). This concludes the proof of the theorem.
∎

Lemma 5.1
.

Consider the case when
K=\{x_{n+1}>0\}
and the adhesion coefficient
\sigma
is constant. Then, within the class of unions of tangent balls, volume constrained local minimizers of
\mathcal{F}_{K,\sigma,0}
are a single ball.

Proof.

Suppose for sake of contradiction that
G
is the union of at least two tangent balls. Then, we first note the Euler-Lagrange equations implies that each of them have correct associated contact angle with the plane
\{x_{n+1}=0\}
.

If the point of tangency of any two balls occurs in the interior
\{x_{n+1}>0\}
, then by classical upper and lower density arguments, see for instance
[
PM15
, Lemma 2.8]
, yield the desired contradiction. Next, we assume that there exists a point of tangency
x_{0}
lying in the plane
\{x_{n+1}=0\}
. Blowing up this configuration at
x_{0}
, we obtain (up to a rotation) the wedge

\tilde{G}=\left\{x\;:\;0<x_{n+1}<\frac{|x_{1}|}{|\sigma|}\right\}.

Using the local minimality property of
G
, we can deduce that
\tilde{G}
must also be a local minimizer of the functional

\mathcal{F}(E)=P(E;\{x_{n+1}>0\})+\sigma\mathcal{H}^{n}(E\cap\{x_{n+1}=0\}).

We will achieve the desired contradiction by constructing an appropriate competitor.

For a parameter
l>0
to be determined later, we define the prism set

S=\left\{x:|x_{i}|\leq l\;\mbox{for $2\leq i\leq n$}\right\}\,.

(5.10)

On
S
, we alter the wedge
\tilde{G}
to obtain a new set
\tilde{F}
in the following way:

\tilde{F}:=\tilde{G}\cap S^{c}\;\bigcup\;\left\{x\;:\;0<x_{n+1}<r\;\mbox{for $x\in S$ and $|x_{1}|<|\sigma|r$}\right\}.

We first notice that the wetted regions for
\tilde{G}
and
\tilde{F}
are the same; hence to contradict the minimality of
\tilde{G}
we only need to compute their difference in perimeter. Due to the simple geometry of the competitor, we can compute explicitly

\mathcal{F}(\tilde{G})-\mathcal{F}(\tilde{F})\;=\;2l^{n-1}r(\sqrt{1+\sigma^{2}}-|\sigma|)-2|\sigma|(n-1)l^{n-2}r^{2}

By using that
\sqrt{1+\sigma^{2}}-|\sigma|>0
for
|\sigma|<1
and by taking
r\ll l
and
r
small enough, we obtain that

\mathcal{F}(\tilde{G})-\mathcal{F}(\tilde{F})>0,

which contradicts the local minimality property for
\tilde{G}
. This concludes the proof of the lemma.
∎

6.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

The next lemma contains the core of the Montiel-Ros argument for proving the classical Heintze-Karcher inequality. The argument is detailed for the sake of clarity.

Lemma 6.1
.

If
N
is an orientable hypersurface with boundary in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
with finite area and positive mean curvature
H_{N}
with respect to the normal vector field
\nu_{N}
, then

(n+1)|\psi(\Gamma)|\leq\int_{N}\,\frac{n}{H_{N}}

(6.1)

where
\psi(x,t)=x-t\,\nu_{N}(x)
for every
(x,t)\in N\times\mathbb{R}
,

\Gamma=\Big\{(x,t)\in N\times\mathbb{R}:0<t\leq\frac{1}{\kappa_{n}(x)}\Big\}\,,

and
\kappa_{1}\leq\kappa_{2}\leq\cdots\leq\kappa_{n}
denote the principal curvatures of
N
.

Moreover, denoting by
\{N_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
the connected components of
N
, we see that equality holds in (
6.1
) if and only if each
N_{i}
is contained in a sphere
\partial B_{r}(x_{i})
and the cones over
N_{i}
with vertex at
x_{i}
are mutually disjoint.

Remark 6.2
.

When
N=\partial\Omega
we are in the case considered by Montiel and Ros
[
MR91
]
, who complete the proof of (
1.1
) by showing that
\Omega\subseteq\psi(\Gamma)
. Indeed, pick
y\in\Omega
and let
x_{0}\in\partial\Omega
such that
|x_{0}-y|={\rm dist}(x_{0},\partial\Omega)
. Then
\nu_{\Omega}(x_{0})=(x_{0}-y)/|x_{0}-y|
and thus
\kappa_{n}(x_{0})\geq|x_{0}-y|^{-1}
. In particular,
y=\psi(x_{0},|x_{0}-y|)
for
|x_{0}-y|\leq 1/\kappa_{n}(x)
, and thus
\Omega\subseteq\psi(\Gamma)
.

Proof of Lemma
6.1
.

The tangential Jacobian of
\psi
along
N\times\mathbb{R}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n+2}
is given by

J\psi(x,t)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\big(1-t\,\kappa_{i}(x)\big)\qquad\forall(x,t)\in N\times\mathbb{R}\,.

If
t\leq 1/\kappa_{n}(x)
, then each factor in this formula is non-negative, and thus one can apply the arithmetic-geometric mean inequality to infer that

J\psi(x,t)\leq\Big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(1-t\,\kappa_{i}(x))\Big)^{n}=\Big(1-t\,\frac{H_{N}(x)}{n}\Big)^{n}\,,\qquad\forall(x,t)\in\Gamma\,.

By the area formula we thus get

|\psi(\Gamma)|\leq\int_{\psi(\Gamma)}\mathcal{H}^{0}(\psi^{-1}(y))\,dy=\int_{\Gamma}J\psi\,d\mathcal{H}^{n+1}\leq\int_{\Gamma}\Big(1-t\,\frac{H_{N}}{n}\Big)^{n}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n+1}

where
\mathcal{H}^{n+1}
is the
(n+1)
-dimensional measure in
\mathbb{R}^{n+2}
. By the coarea formula and since
H_{N}/n\leq\kappa_{n}
,

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}\Big(1-t\,\frac{H_{N}}{n}\Big)^{n}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{N}d\mathcal{H}^{n}\int_{0}^{1/\kappa_{n}(x)}\Big(1-t\,\frac{H_{N}}{n}\Big)^{n}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{N}d\mathcal{H}^{n}\int_{0}^{n/H_{N}}\Big(1-t\,\frac{H_{N}}{n}\Big)^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n+1}\int_{N}\,\frac{n}{H_{N}}\,.

This proves (
6.1
). If equality holds, then (i)
N
is umbilical, in the sense that
\kappa_{i}(x)=H_{N}(x)/n
for every
x\in M
and
i=1,...,n
; (ii)
\psi
is injective on
\Gamma
. By (i), each connected component
N_{i}
of
N
is either contained in a sphere or in a hyperplane, the latter case being actually ruled out by
H_{N}>0
. Denote by
x_{i}
the center of the sphere containing
N_{i}
, and by
K_{i}
the cone with vertex
x_{i}
spanned by
N_{i}
. By (ii), the cones
K_{i}
are mutually disjoint.
∎

Now we are ready to prove Theorem
1.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

We apply Lemma
6.1
with
N=M
and find

(n+1)|\psi(\Gamma)|\leq\int_{M}\frac{n}{H_{M}}

(6.2)

where
\psi(x,t)=x-t\,\nu_{\Omega}(x)
,
\Gamma=\{(x,t):x\in M\,,0<t\leq 1/\kappa_{n}(x)\}
and
\kappa_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\kappa_{n}
are the principal curvatures of
M
. We notice that

A\subseteq\psi(\Gamma)

(6.3)

if
A
denotes the set of points
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
such that

\mbox{${\rm dist}(y,M)=|x-y|$ for some $x\in M\cap H$ with $\frac{x-y}{|x-y|}=\nu_{\Omega}(x)$}\,.

(6.4)

Indeed, this is the same argument outlined in Remark
6.2
: if
y\in A
then the sphere
\partial B_{|x-y|}(y)
is touching
M
at
x
from the inside of
\Omega
. Hence

\frac{1}{|x-y|}\geq\kappa_{n}(x)

which in turn implies
y=\psi(x,|x-y|)
for
(x,|x-y|)\in\Gamma
.

Step one
: We show that if
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
is such that
{\rm dist}(y,M)=|x_{0}-y|
for some
x_{0}\in{\rm bd}(M)
, then

\frac{x_{0}-y}{|x_{0}-y|}=\cos\alpha\,\nu_{H}+\sin\alpha\,\nu_{\Sigma}(x_{0})\qquad\sin(\theta(x_{0})-\alpha)\leq 0\,,

(6.5)

Indeed, by differentiating
x\mapsto|x-y|^{2}
at
x_{0}
along a direction
v\in T_{x_{0}}(\partial\Sigma)
we find that

(x_{0}-y)\cdot v=0\qquad\forall v\in T_{x_{0}}(\partial\Sigma)=\nu_{\Sigma}(x_{0})^{\perp}\cap\nu_{H}^{\perp}\,.

This implies that
x_{0}-y
lies in the plane generated by
\nu_{H}
and
\nu_{\Sigma}(x_{0})
, thus the first relation in (
6.5
). Next, by differentiating
x\mapsto|x-y|^{2}
at
x_{0}
in the direction
-\nu_{{\rm bd}(M)}^{M}(x_{0})
(which points inwards
M
) we find that

(x_{0}-y)\cdot\big(-\nu_{{\rm bd}(M)}^{M}(x_{0})\big)\geq 0

which thanks to (
2.3
) and the first relation in (
6.5
) gives

0\leq-\cos\alpha\sin\theta(x_{0})+\sin\alpha\cos(\theta(x_{0}))=\sin(\alpha-\theta(x_{0}))\,.

This proves (
6.5
).

Step two
: We assume to be in the hydrophobic case where
0<\theta_{min}\leq\theta_{max}\leq\pi/2
. Setting
\Lambda
as in (
1.6
), it will suffice to prove

\Omega\setminus\overline{\Lambda}\subseteq A\,.

(6.6)

Indeed, by combining (
6.2
) and (
6.3
) with (
6.6
) we find

\frac{1}{n+1}\int_{M}\frac{n}{H_{M}}\geq|\psi(\Gamma)|\geq|A|\geq|\Omega\setminus\Lambda|\geq|\Omega|-|\Lambda|\,,

that is the hydrophobic case of (
1.5
).

We now prove (
6.6
). Let
y\in\Omega\setminus\overline{\Lambda}
, and let
x_{0}\in M
be such that
|x_{0}-y|={\rm dist}(y,M)
. If
x_{0}\in M\cap H
, then according to (
6.4
) we immediately find
y\in A
, as desired. We conclude by showing that if
x_{0}\in{\rm bd}(M)
, then
y\in\overline{\Lambda}
. Indeed, if this is the case, then (
6.5
) holds. Since
y_{n+1}>0
, in (
6.5
) we have
\alpha\in(-\pi/2,\pi/2)
. By
\theta(x_{0})\in(0,\pi/2]
, we have
\theta(x_{0})-\alpha\in(-\pi/2,\pi)
, so that
\sin(\theta(x_{0})-\alpha)\leq 0
implies

-\frac{\pi}{2}<\theta(x_{0})-\alpha\leq 0\,.

Summarizing,

0<\theta_{min}\leq\alpha<\frac{\pi}{2}\,.

(6.7)

Denoting
y=(z,t)
and multiplying (
6.5
) by
x_{0}-y
, we obtain

0\leq|x_{0}-y|\leq-\cos\alpha t+\sin\alpha|z-x_{0}|

which implies that

0\leq t\leq\tan\alpha\;d(P_{\partial H}y,\partial\Sigma),

which is the definition of
y\in\Lambda
.

Step three
: We address the hydrophilic case
\pi/2\leq\theta_{min}\leq\theta_{max}<\pi
by showing that

\Omega\cup\Lambda\subseteq A\,,

(6.8)

where now
\Lambda
is as in (
1.7
). As before, we just need to exclude the existence of
y\in\Omega\cup\Lambda
such that
{\rm dist}(y,M)=|x_{0}-y|
for some
x_{0}\in{\rm bd}(M)
. Looking at (
6.5
), if
y\in\Omega
, then
y_{n+1}>0
, which implies
\alpha<\pi/2
. If
y\in\Omega
, but
\mathbf{p}y\notin\Sigma
, then the closest point has to happen at
M
. Therefore, we can assume that
\mathbf{p}y\in\Sigma
and thus
\alpha>0
. Since
\alpha\in(0,\pi/2)
, and
\theta(x_{0})\in[\pi/2,\pi)
, we conclude that
\theta(x_{0})-\alpha\in(0,\pi)
,
\sin(\theta(x_{0})-\alpha)>0
, and thus find a contradiction with the second condition in (
6.5
). This proves that
\Omega\subseteq A
. Now let us consider the case of
y\in\Lambda
. By construction,
0\leq\alpha<\theta_{min}
so that

0<\theta_{min}-\alpha\leq\theta(x_{0})-\alpha\leq\theta(x_{0})<\pi

which, again, contradicts
\sin(\theta(x_{0})-\alpha)\leq 0
.

Step four
: Let us now assume that
\Omega
satisfies (
1.5
) as an equality. This means that (
6.2
) holds as an equality. By Lemma
6.1
and since
\Omega
is connected,
M\subseteq\partial B_{r}(x)
for some
r>0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. The facts that
{\rm bd}(M)=M\cap H
and
\theta(x)\in(0,\pi)
for every
x\in{\rm bd}(M)
force
{\rm bd}(M)
to be a non-degenerate sphere and
\theta
to be constant along
{\rm bd}(M)
.

Let us show, conversely, that if
\Omega
is the intersection of an Euclidean ball with
H
in such a way that
\theta=\theta_{min}=\theta_{max}\in(0,\pi)
, then equality holds in (
1.5
). Let us notice that, independently from the value of
\theta
, we always have that

\Omega=H\cap B_{r}(r\cos(\theta)\,e_{n+1})\,.

(6.9)

Since
M
is always a spherical cap of radius
r
, we have

\int_{M}\frac{n}{H_{M}}=r\,\mathcal{H}^{n}(M)\,.

Notice also that
\Lambda
is always the cone with center at
r\cos(\theta)\,e_{n+1}
spanned by
B_{r}(r\cos(\theta)\,e_{n+1})\cap\partial H
. In the hydrophobic case,

\Lambda\subseteq\Omega\qquad\mbox{with}\qquad\Omega\setminus\Lambda=\bigcup_{0<\lambda<1}\lambda\,M\,;

in the hydrophilic case,

\Lambda\cap\Omega=\emptyset\qquad\mbox{with}\qquad\Omega\cup\Lambda=\bigcup_{0<\lambda<1}\lambda\,M\,.

Since in both cases

\Big|\bigcup_{0<\lambda<1}\lambda\,M\Big|=\int_{0}^{r}\,\mathcal{H}^{n}((\rho/r)M)\,d\rho=\int_{0}^{r}\,(\rho/r)^{n}\,d\rho\,\mathcal{H}^{n}(M)=\frac{r\,\mathcal{H}^{n}(M)}{n+1}

we conclude that (
1.5
) always holds as an equality.
∎
