Abstract
. We introduce a novel method, called Swarm-based Simulated Annealing (SSA), for non-convex optimization which is at the interface between the swarm-based gradient-descent (SBGD) [ 21 , 29 ] , and Simulated Annealing (SA) [ 5 , 18 , 11 ] . Similar to SBGD, we introduce a swarm of agents, each identified with a position, 𝐱 \mathbf{x} and mass m m , to explore the ambient space. Similar to SA, the agents proceed in the gradient descent direction, and are subject to Brownian motion. The annealing rate, however, is dictated by a decreasing function of their mass. As a consequence, instead of the SA protocol for time-decreasing temperature, here the swarm decides how to ‘cool down’ agents, depending on their own accumulated mass. The dynamics of masses is coupled with the dynamics of positions: agents at higher ground transfer (part of) their mass to those at lower ground. Consequently, the resulting SSA optimizer is dynamically divided between heavier, cooler agents viewed as ‘leaders’ and lighter, warmer agents viewed as ‘explorers’. Mean-field convergence analysis and benchmark optimizations demonstrate the effectiveness of the SSA method as a multi-dimensional global optimizer.

Why provisional minimum?
We refer to the mass-weighted average

(1.1)

\overline{F}^{N}_{t}:=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{N}m^{j}_{t}F(\mathbf{x}^{j}_{t})}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}m^{j}_{t}}

\sum_{j}\textnormal{d}m^{j}_{t}/\textnormal{d}t=0

\sigma^{j}_{t}

\sigma^{j}_{t}=\sigma(m^{j}_{t}):(0,\infty)\mapsto{\mathbb{R}}_{+},\qquad j=1,2,\ldots,N.

Given this adjustment of mass-dependent annealing,

N\gg 1

1.1

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\overline{F}^{N}_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle t\rightarrow\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}F_{*}:=F(\mathbf{x}_{*}).

Indeed, our main result states that, under the appropriate assumptions (in particular, the assumption of uniqueness of global minimizer made in Assumption

The method described above lies at the interface of the deterministic SBGD approach and the stochastic-based SA.
We recall that the SBGD is governed by a deterministic system which governs the swarm of
N

j=1,2,\ldots,N

(SBGD)

\frac{\textnormal{d}}{\textnormal{d}t}\mathbf{x}^{j}(t)=-\alpha(m^{j}(t))\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{j}(t)),\quad\frac{\textnormal{d}}{\textnormal{d}t}m^{j}(t)=-\big(F(\mathbf{x}^{j}(t))-\overline{F}^{N}(t)\big)m^{j}(t)\,.

The key feature advocated in (

{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
. The additional dimension of mass serves as a platform for

was taken as the minimum of the crowd at the given time,
\overline{F}^{N}(t)=\min_{j}F(\mathbf{x}^{j}(t))

The same swarm-based methodology is used in this work, except for two distinct features. First, the provisional minimum is given here by (

(
On the choice of provisional minimum).

The SBGD method (

1.1.
In-swarm communication combined with stochastic search

Application of deterministic swarm-based methods reveals that such methods
succeed in the accuracy sense but fail in the probability sense
, — namely when these methods succeed, then they find the global optimizer, yet there is non-zero probability for the methods to fail to do so.
In contrast, stochastic methods
succeed in the probability sense but fail in the accuracy sense
, — namely such methods can always find the global basin, but they do not necessarily return a true global optimizer.
In this context, our swarm-based optimization with adjusted annealing rate  takes advantage of randomness in (
SSA
)
1
combined with communication-based swarming in (
SSA
)
2
, with the aim of succeeding in both the probability and the accuracy sense. This is achieved by a dynamic process that combines swarm communication and randomness, as described below.

The essential role of communication in SBGD was already emphasized in
[
21
,
29
]
. This is amplified in the present context, upon setting
\sigma(\cdot)\equiv 0
in (
SSA
)
1
. One ends up with a crowd of
N
independent agents driven by gradient descent,

\dot{\mathbf{x}}^{j}(t)=-\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{j}(t)),\qquad j=1,2,\ldots,N,.

The success of such a non-communicating crowd in exploring the ambient landscape is
significantly worse
than that of a communication-based crowd. In Section
4
, we show that the method fails in the probability sense.

Communication is encoded in the provisional minimum, where agents ‘communicate’ their height relative to
\overline{F}^{N}_{t}
. In our swarm-based optimization with adjusted annealing rate  (
SSA
),
\overline{F}^{N}_{t}
is taken as the weighted average height, weighted by the different masses. Consequently, there is a dynamic distinction between agents above the provisional minimum and agents below or at the level of the provisional minimum. Agents above the provisional minimum carry lighter mass and therefore explore the ambient landscape with a relatively large Brownian motion; these are viewed as the
explorers
of the crowd. The heavier agents — those that are below or at the level of the provisional minimum, evolve in the gradient direction with a smaller Brownian motion and are viewed as
leaders
of the crowd. Of course, once an exploring light agent ‘hits’ a new lower ground at, say,
\mathbf{x}^{j}_{t}
where
F(\mathbf{x}^{j}_{t})\ll-\overline{F}^{N}_{t}
, it is expected to accumulate more mass from the higher agents, to cool down, and eventually to become a leader, driving the crowd to a new lower point.

We note that by fixing a constant annealing rate, (
SSA
)
1
with
\sigma(m^{j}_{t})\equiv\sigma>0
, we also encounter a problem. In this case, we recover Langevin Monte Carlo

\textnormal{d}\mathbf{x}^{j}_{t}=-\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{j}_{t})\textnormal{d}t+\sqrt{2\sigma}\textnormal{d}W^{j}_{t},\qquad j=1,2,\ldots,N,

whose long time invariant measure suggests
\mathbf{x}\propto e^{-F(\mathbf{x})/\sigma}
. It admits the proper convergence in the zero-variance limit,
\mathbf{x}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sigma\rightarrow 0}}{{\longrightarrow}}\argmin_{\mathbf{x}}F(\mathbf{x})
, but, as we show in Section
4
below, the resulting method fails in the accuracy sense for any finite
\sigma
.
Thus, the dynamic process of relabeling explorers and leaders, coupled through a mass-dependent annealing rate,
\sigma^{j}_{t}=\sigma(m^{j}_{t})
, is at the heart of the matter.

In Section
5
we prove global convergence of (
SSA
) in both, the sense of probability and the sense of accuracy. This is achieved in a two-step argument: first, we conduct a large-crowd, mean-field analysis summarized in Theorem
2.1
below, which allows us to pass from finitely many Stochastic-ODEs governing the particle system (
SSA
), to the limiting dynamics for the probability measure,
\mu_{t}(\mathbf{x},m)
, governed by the limiting PDE (
2.4
); and second, the large-time behavior of the latter, summarized in Theorem
2.2
, implies that in this mean-field limit, the PDE in long time returns the the provisional minimum as the global minimum. Combining these results we conclude in Theorem
2.3
the claimed convergence
\displaystyle\mathop{\liminf}_{t\rightarrow\infty}\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\overline{F}^{N}_{t}=F_{*}
with a precise convergence rate of polynomial type. Further, in Section
3
we derive the corresponding macroscopic description of the system.

1.2.
The swarm-based optimization with adjusted annealing rate

The exchange of mass encoded in (
SSA
) assigns smaller masses to agents with higher values, while the decreasing property of
\sigma(m)

\sum_{j=1}^{N}m^{j}_{n+1}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}m^{j}_{n}-h\sum_{j=1}^{N}m^{j}_{n}\left(F(\mathbf{x}^{j}_{n})-\overline{F}_{n}\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}m^{j}_{n}

2.1.
From empirical distribution to mean-field

We define the

\mu^{N}_{t}

\mu^{N}_{t}(\mathbf{x},m)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\delta_{\mathbf{x}^{j}_{t}}(\mathbf{x})\otimes\delta_{m^{j}_{t}}(m)\,.

Recall that the provisional minimum is given by the weighted average in (
1.1

(2.2)

\overline{F}^{N}_{t}=\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}m^{j}_{t}F(\mathbf{x}^{j}_{t})}{\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}m^{j}_{t}}=\frac{\displaystyle\iint mF(\mathbf{x})\hskip 0.56917pt\textnormal{d}\hskip-0.85355pt\mu^{N}_{t}(\mathbf{x},m)}{\displaystyle\iint m\hskip 0.56917pt\textnormal{d}\hskip-0.85355pt\mu^{N}_{t}(\mathbf{x},m)}.

To formally derive the mean-field limit, we use a smooth test function

Differentiating, recalling Itô’s formula, and using (

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\textnormal{d}\phi(\mathbf{x}^{j}_{t},m^{j}_{t})\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\phi(\mathbf{x}^{j}_{t},m^{j}_{t})\cdot\textnormal{d}\mathbf{x}^{j}_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\langle D^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}\phi,2\sigma(m^{j}_{t})\rangle_{\text{Tr}}\textnormal{d}t+\partial_{m}\phi(\mathbf{x}^{j}_{t},m^{j}_{t})\textnormal{d}m^{j}_{t}\right]

3.
From mean-field to macroscopic description

We shall briefly comment on the macroscopic description of the swarm-based optimization with adjusted annealing rate, furnished in terms of the first two moments —
the density,
\rho:{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\mapsto{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, and momentum,
\rho{\sf M}:{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\mapsto{\mathbb{R}}
, (observe that time-dependence is denoted as usual for the density
\rho=\rho(t,\mathbf{x})
, macroscopic mass,
{\sf M}={\sf M}(t,\mathbf{x})
etc.)

\rho(t,\mathbf{x}):=\int\mu_{t}(\mathbf{x},m)\textnormal{d}m,\qquad\rho{\sf M}(t,\mathbf{x}):=\int m\mu_{t}(\mathbf{x},m)\textnormal{d}m.

Taking the first two moments of the mean-field (
2.4
), we find

(3.1)

\left\{\begin{split}\rho_{t}-\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\cdot(\rho\nabla F)&=\Delta_{\mathbf{x}}\int\sigma(m)\mu_{t}(\mathbf{x},m)\textnormal{d}m,\\
(\rho{\sf M})_{t}-\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\cdot(\rho{\sf M}\nabla F)&=\big(\overline{F}(t)-F(\mathbf{x})\big)\rho{\sf M}+\Delta_{\mathbf{x}}\int m\sigma(m)\mu_{t}(\mathbf{x},m)\textnormal{d}m.\end{split}\right.

We normalize the total mass and momentum,
\displaystyle\int\rho(t,\mathbf{x})\textnormal{d}\mathbf{x}\equiv 1
and
\displaystyle\int\rho{\sf M}(t,\mathbf{x})\textnormal{d}\mathbf{x}\equiv 1
, which leaves us with a provisional minimum,
\overline{F}(t)=\overline{\mathscr{F}}^{\mu}_{t}
, given by

\overline{F}(t)=\int F(\mathbf{x})\rho{\sf M}(t,\mathbf{x})\textnormal{d}\mathbf{x}.

To unlock a closed form of the diffusion on the right, we assume the simplest closure based on a mono-kinetic pseudo-Maxwellian ,
\mu_{t}(\mathbf{x},m)=\rho(t,\mathbf{x})\delta(m-{\sf M}(t,\mathbf{x}))
. This yields

(3.2a)

\displaystyle\rho_{t}-\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\cdot(\rho\nabla F)

\displaystyle=\Delta_{\mathbf{x}}(\sigma(t,\mathbf{x})\rho(t,\mathbf{x})),\qquad\sigma(t,\mathbf{x}):=\sigma({\sf M}(t,\mathbf{x}))

(3.2b)

\displaystyle(\rho{\sf M})_{t}-\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\cdot(\rho{\sf M}\nabla F)

\displaystyle=\big(\overline{F}(t)-F(\mathbf{x})\big)\rho{\sf M}+\Delta_{\mathbf{x}}\big(\sigma(t,\mathbf{x})\rho{\sf M}(t,\mathbf{x})\big).

The momentum (
3.2b
) can be converted into a drift-diffusion
equation for the velocity
{\sf M}
,

(3.3a)

\displaystyle\rho_{t}-\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\cdot(\rho\nabla F)

\displaystyle=\Delta_{\mathbf{x}}(\sigma(t,\mathbf{x})\rho(t,\mathbf{x})),

(3.3b)

\displaystyle{\sf M}_{t}-\nabla F\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}{\sf M}

\displaystyle=\big(\overline{F}(t)-F(\mathbf{x})\big){\sf M}+\frac{1}{\rho}\Delta_{\mathbf{x}}\big(\sigma(t,\mathbf{x})\rho{\sf M}\big)-\frac{1}{\rho}\Delta_{\mathbf{x}}\big(\sigma(t,\mathbf{x})\rho\big){\sf M},\quad\mathbf{x}\in\textnormal{supp}\{\rho(t,\cdot)\}.

We shall not dwell on a detailed study of (
3.3

\frac{\textnormal{d}}{\textnormal{d}t}\int(F(\mathbf{x})-F_{*})\rho(t,\mathbf{x})\textnormal{d}\mathbf{x}=-\int|\nabla F(\mathbf{x})|^{2}\rho(t,\mathbf{x})\textnormal{d}\mathbf{x}+\int\Delta_{\mathbf{x}}F(\mathbf{x})\sigma(t,\mathbf{x})\rho(t,\mathbf{x})\textnormal{d}\mathbf{x},

with

long-time behavior
\rho(t,\mathbf{x})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle t\rightarrow\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}\delta(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{*})
and

4.
Pitfalls

We examine the swarm-based optimization with adjusted annealing rate  in two extreme cases — when
\sigma(m)\equiv 0
and
\sigma(m)\equiv 1
. The former case turns off stochasticity and amounts to a deterministic system of
N
independent gradient
descending agents; we show that their convergence fails in probability sense. The latter turns on stochasticity uniformly for all agents, which amounts to a stochastic system of
N
independent gradient descending agents; we show that their convergence fails in accuracy sense.

We note that in both cases, the resulting systems are passive systems in the sense that the weights of agents do
not
affect their trajectories. As a consequence, agents roam the landscape with equal randomness with no distinction between leaders and explorers. The convergence of the provisional minimum
\overline{\mathscr{F}}^{\mu}_{t}
is naturally worse than in the communicating swarm-based dynamics.

4.1.
Swarming with no communication

What is the effect of Brownian motion in our swarm-based optimization with adjusted annealing rate? If we turn off the amplitude,
\sigma(m)\equiv 0
, then (
SSA
) is reduced to a deterministic system of
non-interacting
agents with no Brownian motion (and again we switch notations of time-dependence in deterministic quantities,
\mathbf{x}^{j}(t),m^{j}(t),\overline{F}^{N}(t)
etc.)

(4.1)

\left\{\begin{split}\frac{\textnormal{d}}{\textnormal{d}t}\mathbf{x}^{j}(t)&=-\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{j}(t))\\
\frac{\textnormal{d}}{\textnormal{d}t}m^{j}(t)&=-m^{j}(t)\big(F(\mathbf{x}^{j}(t))-\overline{F}^{N}(t)\big),\quad\end{split}\right.j=1,...,N.

We still assume random initial configurations: the initial drawing is independent, with
\{\mathbf{x}^{j}(0)\}^{N}_{j=1}
drawn from an initial distribution
\rho_{0}
, and
m^{j}(0)=\frac{1}{N}
. Since the total mass is conserved, we can be
M(t)=1
, so that provisional minimum is reduced to the usual average
\overline{F}^{N}(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}m^{j}(t)F(\mathbf{x}^{j}(t))
. The main feature
missing
in (
4.1
) is communication. Different agents at different positions proceed along gradient decent, independently of each other: since communication through provisional minimum is missing, the dynamics lacks the collective engagement of the agents as a self-organized swarm.

The essential role of communication was already highlighted in the context of SBGD method,
[
21
]
: once we decouple the step-size and mass, setting a
uniform
step-size in (
SBGD
), that is,
\alpha(m)\equiv 1
,
then the success rate of the resulting non-communicating swarm-based method decreases dramatically.
The system (
4.1
) provides yet another realization of the essential role of communication. We note that since all particles are “descending,” it is also straightforward to show that the collective mean objective
\overline{F}^{N}(t)
is always decreasing:
\frac{\textnormal{d}}{\textnormal{d}t}\overline{F}^{N}(t)\leqslant 0
. See Section
A.1
for the proof.
However, even though
\overline{F}^{N}(t)
continues to decay in time, it does not guarantee the desired convergence to the global minimum,
\displaystyle\liminf_{t\rightarrow\infty}\overline{F}^{N}(t)=\min_{\mathbf{x}}F(\mathbf{x})
. This is due to the passive nature of the gradient descent of the agents. In particular, it is likely that all agents will become trapped at local minima or saddle points, especially when the number of particles is not sufficiently large. Turning on the communication term that adjusts strength of Brownian motion, as done in (
SSA
), allows particles situated at local minima to experience increased randomness. This increased randomness facilitates their escape from local basins. With the communication and the randomness turned off, we have the following:

Theorem 4.1

(
Lack of communication and failure in probability).

Assume
F
has a unique global
minimum
\mathbf{x}_{*}
inside an open set
\Omega
so that
F_{*}=F(\mathbf{x}_{*})
. Moreover,

\nabla F(\mathbf{x})=0
on
\partial\Omega

If we let
\{\mathbf{x}^{j}(0)\}
drawn from
\rho_{0}
,

(4.2)

\mathbb{P}\left(\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\overline{F}^{N}(t)=F_{*}\right)\leqslant 1-\left(1-\int_{\Omega}\textnormal{d}\rho_{0}\right)^{N}\,.

In particular, if
\mathrm{supp}_{\mathbf{x}}(\rho_{0})\cap\Omega=\emptyset
, the success rate is
0
, meaning
\mathbb{P}\left(\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\overline{F}^{N}(t)=F_{*}\right)=0
for all
N
.

Proof
. To prove (
4.2
), we first notice that without the Brownian motion, the deterministic system drives the samples to the local minima of the local basin in which the samples are located at
t=0
, so the event of
\left\{\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\overline{F}^{N}(t)=\min F\right\}\subset\left\{\exists\mathbf{x}^{i}(0)\in\Omega\right\}
. This implies

\mathbb{P}\left(\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\overline{F}^{N}(t)=\min F\right)\leqslant\mathbb{P}\left(\left\{\exists\mathbf{x}^{j}(0)\in\Omega\right\}\right)=1-\left(1-\int_{\Omega}\textnormal{d}\rho_{0}\right)^{N}\,.

When

\mathbb{P}\left(\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\overline{F}^{N}(t)=\min F\right)=0\,,

Since
\overline{F}^{N}(t)
is a decreasing in

\mathbb{P}\left(\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\overline{F}^{N}(t)>\min F\right)=1.\hfill\square

This theorem explicitly spells out the “probability failure”, so there is a nonzero probability that the mean objectives obtained by the particles cannot ever achieve the global optimizer.

4.2.
Stochastic system

The limitation of (
4.1
) clearly originates from the fact that particle evolution closely resembles classical gradient descent, lacking a mechanism for domain exploration and avoiding entrapment in local minima. The other end of the spectrum is to introduce “too much” exploration. Indeed, setting

(4.3)

5.
Proofs of the main results

In this section we prove Theorem
2.1
on the convergence to the mean-field limit, and Theorem
2.2
on the large-time behavior of the limiting equation.

5.1.
Convergence to the mean-field limit

Theorem
2.1
states that the empirical distribution
\mu^{N}_{t}
that assembles all samples that follow the coupled SDE system (
SSA
), is close to the limiting distribution
\mu
that solves the PDE (
2.4a
).

There are a few different but related approaches for justifying mean-field limits, including the coupling method, the energy/entropy estimates, the utilization of BBGKY (Bogoliubov–Born–Green–Kirkwood–Yvon hierarchy) hierarchies and running the tightness argument to confine a sequence of measures for the convergence; see references
[
22
,
28
]
. Here we employ the coupling method, introduced in
[
28
]
. The coupling method places a stronger requirement on the field, but the machinery is rather simple to use, and it has the advantage of providing precise convergence rate in terms of the number of particles,
N
.

To apply the coupling method, one usually designs an auxiliary system that is pushed forward by the underlying field. This auxiliary system serves as a bridge linking the discrete SDE system and the limiting mean-field PDE. On the one hand, it is driven by the underlying field and thus its ensemble closely resembles
\rho
, and on the other hand, this system adopts the SDE structure and can be easily compared with the original self-consistent SDE system. In our context, we define such auxiliary system as
\left\{\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}^{j}_{t}\,,\widetilde{m}^{j}_{t}\right\}_{j=1}^{N}
, that are governed by:

(5.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}\textnormal{d}\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}^{j}_{t}&=&-\nabla F\left(\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}^{j}_{t}\right)\textnormal{d}t+\sqrt{2\sigma\left(\widetilde{m}^{j}_{t}\right)}\textnormal{d}W^{j}_{t}\\
\textnormal{d}\widetilde{m}^{j}_{t}&=&-\widetilde{m}^{j}_{t}(F\left(\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}^{j}_{t}\right)-\overline{\mathscr{F}}^{\mu}_{t})\textnormal{d}t,\end{array}\right.

with the initial data set to be
\{\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}^{j}_{0}=\mathbf{x}^{j}_{0}\,,\widetilde{m}^{j}_{0}=m^{j}_{0}\}
, and
\overline{\mathscr{F}}^{\mu}=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[mF(\mathbf{x})]/\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[m]
was defined in (
2.4b
). For this system, we define the corresponding empirical distribution and the provisional minimum:

(5.2)

\widetilde{\mu}^{N}_{t}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}\delta_{\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}^{j}_{t}}(\mathbf{x})\otimes\delta_{\widetilde{m}^{j}_{t}}(m)\,,\quad\text{and}\quad\widetilde{F}^{N}_{t}=\frac{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j}\widetilde{m}^{j}_{t}F\left(\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}^{j}_{t}\right)}{\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[m]}\,.

We note that this system has a clear similarity with the original SDE (
SSA
) but is passive, in the sense that the dynamics of
\widetilde{m}^{j}_{t}
is driven by
\overline{\mathscr{F}}^{\mu}
instead of
\widetilde{F}^{N}_{t}
. While the dynamics of
\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}_{t}
agrees with that of
\mathbf{x}_{t}
, the mass changes call for different rates. In particular, in (

), we compare

with
\overline{F}^{N}_{t}
that is self-generated in (

is compared with

defined from the underlying PDE system (

This design of the new auxiliary system prompts two lemmas.

\mu_{t}

\frac{\textnormal{d}}{\textnormal{d}t}G(t)\leqslant C(t)G(t)+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{N}\right)\quad\text{with}\quad G(0)=0\,.

Finally, calling Grönwall’s inequality, we have

G(t)\leqslant\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{N}\right),

which completes the proof.
∎

Interacting vs. non-interacting agents

In this appendix, we investigate the effect of communication, and study the behavior of two systems that produce pitfalls. In subsection

) with mass communication passively adjusted according to
\mathbf{x}
-values, and prove Theorem
4.2
.

A.1.
Properties of the deterministic system (non interacting agents).

Proposition A.1
.

Given the
N-
particle system defined in (
4.1
), we have the provisional minimum
\overline{F}^{N}(t)
always decay in time
7
7

7

Note that the time dependent quantities in the deterministic case are denotes
\square(t,\cdots)
, e.g.,
\mathbf{x}^{j}(t),\overline{F}^{N}(t),\nu^{N}(t,\mathbf{x})
etc.
:

(A.1)

\frac{\textnormal{d}}{\textnormal{d}t}\overline{F}^{N}(t)\leqslant 0\,,\quad\forall t\geqslant 0\,\qquad\overline{F}^{N}(t)=\frac{1}{M(0)}\sum_{j=1}^{N}m^{j}(t)F(\mathbf{x}^{j}(t)).

Proof.

This comes from direct computation:

\displaystyle\frac{\textnormal{d}}{\textnormal{d}t}\overline{F}^{N}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M(0)}\left(\sum^{N}_{j=1}\left(\frac{\textnormal{d}}{\textnormal{d}t}m^{j}(t)\right)F(\mathbf{x}^{j}(t))+m^{j}(t)\frac{\textnormal{d}}{\textnormal{d}t}F(\mathbf{x}^{j}(t))\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M(0)}\left(-\sum^{N}_{j=1}m^{j}_{t}\left(F(\mathbf{x}^{j}(t))-\overline{F}^{N}(t)\right)F(\mathbf{x}^{j}(t))-m^{j}(t)\left|\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{j}(t))\right|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{M(0)}\sum^{N}_{j=1}m^{j}_{t}\left(F(\mathbf{x}^{j}(t))-\overline{F}^{N}(t)\right)^{2}-\frac{1}{M(0)}\sum^{N}_{j=1}m^{j}_{t}\left|\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{j}(t))\right|^{2}\leqslant 0\,.

This concludes the proof.
∎

Similar to its stochastic counterpart, we can also derive the mean-field limiting equation in the deterministic case. This is expressed in terms of the limiting distribution
\nu(t,\mathbf{x})
. To be specific, we have the following proposition:

Proposition A.2
.

Let
\{\mathbf{x}^{j}(t),m^{j}(t)\}_{j=1}^{N}
denote the crowd of agents satisfying the deterministic system (
4.1
) subject to initial data
\{\mathbf{x}^{j}(0),m^{j}(0)=\frac{1}{N}\}^{N}_{j=1}
, independently drawn from random distribution
\rho_{0}
, and let
\nu^{N}(t,\mathbf{x})
denote its empirical distribution

(A.2)

\nu^{N}(t,\mathbf{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{N}m^{j}(t)\delta_{\mathbf{x}^{j}(t)}(\mathbf{x}).

Then its mean-field limit of the ensemble distribution,
\nu^{N}(t,\mathbf{x})\rightarrow\nu(t,\mathbf{x})
, satisfies

(A.3)

\partial_{t}\nu(t,\mathbf{x})=\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\cdot(\nu(t,\mathbf{x})\nabla F(\mathbf{x}))-(F(\mathbf{x})-\overline{\mathscr{F}}^{\nu}(t))\nu(t,\mathbf{x}),

subject to
\nu(0,\cdot)=\rho_{0}(\cdot)
. Here
\overline{\mathscr{F}}^{\nu}(t)
is the weighted average is:

(A.4)

\overline{\mathscr{F}}^{\nu}(t)=\frac{\displaystyle\int F(\mathbf{x})\textnormal{d}\nu(t,\mathbf{x})}{\displaystyle\int\textnormal{d}\nu(t,\mathbf{x})}\,.

Proof.

To derive the mean-field limit, we test the system with a test smooth function
\phi

\mathbb{E}_{\nu(t)}[\phi]=\int\phi(\mathbf{x})\textnormal{d}\nu(t,\mathbf{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{N}m^{j}(t)\phi(\mathbf{x}^{j}(t))\,.

We take the time derivative on the two sides of the equation. The left hand side provides:

(A.5)

\frac{\textnormal{d}}{\textnormal{d}t}\mathbb{E}_{\nu(t)}[\phi]=\int\partial_{t}\nu(t,\mathbf{x})\phi(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}.

Here expectation is with respect to the randomly drawn initial data.
The time derivative of the right hand side yields

(A.6)

\begin{split}\frac{\textnormal{d}}{\textnormal{d}t}\Bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{N}&m^{j}(t)\phi(\mathbf{x}^{j}(t))\Bigg)=\\
&-\sum_{j=1}^{N}m^{j}_{t}\underbrace{\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\phi(\mathbf{x}^{j}(t))\cdot\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{j}(t))}_{\dot{\mathbf{x}}=-\nabla F(\mathbf{x}(t))}-\sum_{j=1}^{N}\phi(\mathbf{x}^{j}(t))\underbrace{m^{j}(t)(F(\mathbf{x}^{j}(t))-\overline{F}(t))}_{\dot{m}=-m(t)(F(\mathbf{x}(t)-\overline{\mathscr{F}}^{\nu}(t))},\end{split}

where we integrated the ODE (
4.1

-\sum_{j=1}^{N}m^{j}(t)\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\phi(\mathbf{x}^{j}(t))\cdot\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{j}(t))=-\mathbb{E}_{\nu(t)}[\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\phi\cdot\nabla F]=\int\phi(\mathbf{x})\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\cdot(\nu(t,\mathbf{x})\nabla F(\mathbf{x}))\textnormal{d}\mathbf{x}

using integration-by-parts, and the second term of (

) can be written as

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}\phi(\mathbf{x}^{j}(t))m^{j}(t)(F(\mathbf{x}^{j}(t))-\overline{\mathscr{F}}^{\nu}(t))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\nu(t)}[\phi F]-\mathbb{E}_{\nu(t)}[\phi]\mathbb{E}_{\nu(t)}[F]

\displaystyle=\int\nu(t,\mathbf{x})\phi(\mathbf{x})F(\mathbf{x})\textnormal{d}\mathbf{x}-\overline{\mathscr{F}}^{\nu}(t)\int\phi(\mathbf{x})\nu(\mathbf{x})\textnormal{d}\mathbf{x}.

Plugging the latter into (

A.2.
Proprieties of the stochastic system (interacting agents)

We turn to the proof of Theorem
4.2
.

Proof.

We may assume without loss of generality that
F_{*}=0
, and that the total mass is normalized

\iint m\hskip 0.56917pt\textnormal{d}\hskip-0.85355pt\mu_{0}(\mathbf{x},m)=1,

so that
\displaystyle\rho_{t}(\cdot)=\int m\hskip 0.56917pt\textnormal{d}\hskip-0.85355pt\mu_{t}(\cdot,m)
can be viewed as a probability density function in
\mathbf{x}
.
Multiplying (
4.4
) by
mF(\mathbf{x})
and integrate, we obtain that, with a few integration by parts:

\frac{\textnormal{d}}{\textnormal{d}t}\overline{\mathscr{F}}^{\mu}_{t}=-\iint m|\nabla F|^{2}\hskip 0.56917pt\textnormal{d}\hskip-0.85355pt\mu_{t}(\mathbf{x},m)-\iint m(F-\overline{\mathscr{F}}^{\mu}_{t})^{2}\hskip 0.56917pt\textnormal{d}\hskip-0.85355pt\mu_{t}(\mathbf{x},m)+\iint m\Delta F\hskip 0.56917pt\textnormal{d}\hskip-0.85355pt\mu_{t}(\mathbf{x},m)\,,

and rewriting it using
\rho
,

\frac{\textnormal{d}}{\textnormal{d}t}\overline{\mathscr{F}}^{\mu}_{t}=-\mathbb{E}_{\rho}(|\nabla F|^{2})-\mathrm{Var}_{\rho}(F)+\mathbb{E}_{\rho}(\Delta F)\,.

Because
\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}F
is
L
-Lipschitz according to the first condition in Assumption
2.1
and
F_{*}=0
, we obtain

|\nabla F|^{2}\leqslant 2LF(\mathbf{x})\,.

This implies the first term can be lower bounded by the expectation of
F(\mathbf{x})
:

(A.7)

-\mathbb{E}_{\rho}(|\nabla F|^{2})\geqslant-2L\overline{\mathscr{F}}^{\mu}_{t}\,.

Since
\mathbf{x}_{\ast}
is the unique global minimal, there exists
\epsilon^{\prime}>0
such that

F^{-1}([0,\epsilon^{\prime}])\subset B_{R}(\mathbf{x}_{\ast})\,.

Now, we lower bound the last two terms:

•

Because
F\leqslant D
according to the second condition in Assumption
2.1
and
F_{*}=0
, we obtain

\mathrm{Var}_{\rho}(F)=\mathbb{E}_{\rho}(F^{2})-(\overline{\mathscr{F}}^{\mu}_{t})^{2}\leqslant D\overline{\mathscr{F}}^{\mu}_{t}\left(1-\frac{\overline{\mathscr{F}}^{\mu}_{t}}{D}\right)\leqslant D\overline{\mathscr{F}}^{\mu}_{t}\,,

where we use Assumption
2.1
,
0\leqslant F\leqslant D
in the inequalities. This implies

(A.8)

-\mathrm{Var}_{\rho}(F)\geqslant-D\overline{\mathscr{F}}^{\mu}_{t}\,.

•

Because
F(x)
is
\xi
-strongly convex in the ball of
B_{R}(\mathbf{x}_{*})
, we obtain that

\Delta F\geqslant\xi d,\quad\forall\mathbf{x}\in B_{R}(\mathbf{x}_{*})

and because
\nabla F
is
L
-Lipschitz, we have:

\left|\Delta F\right|\leqslant dL\,,\forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,.

To lower bound the last term, we first deploy Markov inequality:

\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{x}\in B_{R}(\mathbf{x}_{\ast}))\geqslant\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{x}\in F^{-1}([0,\epsilon^{\prime}]))=1-\mathbb{P}(F(\mathbf{x})\geqslant\epsilon^{\prime})\geqslant 1-\frac{\overline{\mathscr{F}}^{\mu}_{t}}{\epsilon^{\prime}}\,.

Then, we have
