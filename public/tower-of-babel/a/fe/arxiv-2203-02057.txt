Interpretable Latent Variables in Deep State Space Models
Haoxuan Wu, David S. Matteson, Martin T. Wells

Abstract
We introduce a new version of deep state-space models (DSSMs) that combines a recurrent neural network with a state-space framework to forecast time series data. The model estimates the observed series as functions of latent variables that evolve non-linearly through time. Due to the complexity and non-linearity inherent in DSSMs, previous works on DSSMs typically produced latent variables that are very difficult to interpret. Our paper focus on producing interpretable latent parameters with two key modifications. First, we simplify the predictive decoder by restricting the response variables to be a linear transformation of the latent variables plus some noise. Second, we utilize shrinkage priors on the latent variables to reduce redundancy and improve robustness. These changes make the latent variables much easier to understand and allow us to interpret the resulting latent variables as random effects in a linear mixed model. We show through two public benchmark datasets the resulting model improves forecasting performances.



1
Introduction

Time series forecasting has been an important area of research for long periods of time. With the advancements in technology and ever-looming presence of big data, the importance of producing accurate and interpretable forecasts for large, noisy datasets is higher than ever before.

A wide variety of methods have previously been utilized for time series forecasting. State-space models (SSMs) have been commonly used for this task
[
Hyndman and Khandakar 2008
,
Aoki 2013
,
Hyndman et al. 2002
]
. SSMs produce interpretable forecasting results and have shown to be every effective at forecasting low-dimensional time-series data
[
Durbin and Koopman 2012
]
. However, they tend to struggle in high dimensional settings, be inefficient with large datasets and are prone to high error propagation for long-term forecasting
[
Daum 2005
]
. As a result, machine learning based methods such recurrent neural networks (RNN) have been frequently used as alternatives
[
Oancea and Ciucu 2014
,
Connor et al. 1994
]
. RNNs have shown to effective at forecasting high dimensional time series. However, a RNN by itself tends to be not enough to learn more intricate patterns in large datasets.

Deep state-space models (DSSMs) incorporate recurrent neural networks into a state-space framework to provide non-linear mappings of latent variables
[
Gedon et al. 2021
]
. In comparison to SSMs, DSSMs provide additionally flexibility in the estimation of the latent variables while maintaining the same general structure. By utilizing recurrent neural networks to learn global patterns across many series, DSSMs have proven to be effective at forecasting large datasets
[
Rangapuram et al. 2018
]
.

At high level, existing machine learning methods are model-agnostic methods or innately interpretable models. The goal of model-agnostic methods is to explain predictions by a black-box approach. Alternatively, directly interpretable models such as generalized linear models (GLMs)
[
McCullagh and Nelder 2019
]
are widely and successfully applied. Despite their effectiveness, one weakness of DSSMs is the lack of interpretability for the latent variables. For many of the existing work
[
Fraccaro et al. 2016
,
Chung et al. 2015
,
Krishnan et al. 2017
]
, the observed data is assumed to follow a certain probability distribution whose sufficient statistics are calculated by a non-linear, black-box transformation of the latent variables. As a result, it becomes very difficult to explain the relationship between the observed and the latent variables. In this paper, we propose a new version of DSSM that produces interpretable latent variables while improving the forecasting performances. The details of our model will be shown in Section
3
. We highlight two key contributions as follows.

First, our model restricts the mean of the observed variables to be a linear function of the latent variables. Previous concept, previous works on deep state-space models, especially all those dealing with time series data, have focused on using a neural network for the decoder. We aim to show that we can achieve similar level of results with more interpretability by switching to a linear decoder. With a normal likelihood for the observed variables, our set-up allows the latent variables to be interpreted as random effects in a (generalized) linear mixed model with an exotic variance component
[
McCulloch and Searle 2004
]
.

Second, we incorporate shrinkage priors into the latent space to reduce redundancy and further enhance interpretability. Shrinkage priors have previous shown to be effective in SSMs
[
Cadonna et al. 2020
,
Kowal et al. 2019
]
and in other deep machine learning models
[
Bhadra et al. 2020
,
Louizos et al. 2017
,
Ghosh and Doshi-Velez 2017
]
. However, shrinkage priors, in general, have not been used in deep state-space models, in particular for dealing with latent variables. Our work is a first adaption of these ideas to the much broader deep state-space framework in modeling the latent states. We utilize inverse gamma-gamma shrinkage prior
[
Bai and Ghosh 2017
]
for the latent variables in order to induce more shrinkage toward zero. The resulting increased sparsity makes latent variables easier to understand and interpret. As we will show through two real-world benchmark datasets, incorporating shrinkage priors leads to more robustness and better forecasting performances.

2
Related Work

Variational autoencoder, introduced by
Kingma and Welling 2013
, produced an efficient method to estimate latent variables that are representative of the observed variables using variational inference. Since then, many works has been done expanding the structure and the application of the framework
[
Bayer and Osendorfer 2014
,
Xue et al. 2020
,
Krishnan et al. 2017
,
Karl et al. 2016
]
.
Chung et al. 2015
introduced variational recurrent neural network which utilizes high level latent variables to learn structure in sequential data.
Fraccaro et al. 2016
extended the framework by incorporating a backward recurrent neural network to learn information from future observed variables.
Krishnan et al. 2015
developed Deep Kalman Filter which utilized a non-linear autoregressive structure in the estimation of the latent variables. However, in most of above models, the observed variables are modeled as a non-linear transformation of the latent variables parameterized by neural networks. This set-up makes it very difficult to interpret the meaning of the latent variables.

To induce learning of global structure in the latent variables, three key areas of improvements have been made in recent works. First, simplifying the decoder in the generative model have shown to lead to more information stored in the latent
[
Gulrajani et al. 2016
]
. For example,
Chen et al. 2016
utilized noisy version of historical data in the generative process. Second, the loss can be modified to induce more structures in the latent variables.
Bowman et al. 2015
utilized KL-annealing to induce lower weight on the KL-divergence term in the loss function to allow for better learning in the training process.
Goyal et al. 2017
added auxiliary variables into the network to ensure the observed can be reconstructed through the latent variables. Third,
Rezende and Mohamed 2015
introduced the idea of normalizing flow which induces a more flexible, scalable class of posterior distributions.
Kingma et al. 2016
and
Louizos and Welling 2017
expanded on this idea for better approximate posteriors. Most of these works have been focused on the area of NLP or image recognition. We adopt some of ideas for time series analysis and take it one step further. In addition to ensuring the latent variables contain useful information, our model also ensures these variables are interpretable.

Within the class of deep state-space models, arguably the most interpretable version is the model introduced by
Rangapuram et al. 2018
. In their model, a RNN estimated the coefficients of a Gaussian state-space model and Kalman Filtering was used to update the latent variables. By utilizing the RNN only for modeling of the coefficients, the model avoided the need for variational inference. While this model maintained a state-space structure, the coefficients themselves may freely change over-time. This makes it difficult to understand the true underlying relationship between the observed and the latent variables. The other work in a DSSM context focused on interpretability is the model introduced by
[
Li et al. 2019
]
. The paper utilized automatic relevance determination network to select relevant covariates for each time-step. Unlike their work which focused on interpretability in covariate selection, our work focuses on interpretability in terms of latent variables.

Methodology

3.1
Cost Function

Suppose we have observed time series
\boldsymbol{y}_{1:T}
with
\{\boldsymbol{y}_{t}\in R^{M}\}_{t=1}^{T}
and a set of covariates
\boldsymbol{u}_{1:T}
with
\{\boldsymbol{u}_{t}\in R^{N}\}_{t=1}^{T}
. For our version of the deep state-space model, we assume the observed variables are functions of latent variables
\boldsymbol{z}_{1:T}
with
\{\boldsymbol{z}_{t}\in R^{Q}\}_{t=1}^{T}
. Suppose further the latent variables depend on a recurrent neural network with output variables
\boldsymbol{h}_{1:T}
, a set of global variables
\boldsymbol{g}
and a set of local variables
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1:T}
. We will detail each of these variables in Subsection
3.2
. For clarity, we will drop subscripts from notation (e.g.
\boldsymbol{y}\equiv\boldsymbol{y}_{1:T}
). Let
\boldsymbol{\theta}
denote all parameters associated with the DSSM such as weights for the neural networks. We can write the loss as follows:

\displaystyle L(\boldsymbol{\theta};\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{u})=L(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle\equiv\log\!\int\!p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{h},\boldsymbol{g},\boldsymbol{\lambda}|\boldsymbol{u})d\boldsymbol{z}\,d\boldsymbol{h}\,d\boldsymbol{g}\,d\boldsymbol{\lambda}

(1)

Due to the complexity of the model, this integral is intractable. As a result, we will use an inference model to estimate an approximate posterior
q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{h},\boldsymbol{g},\boldsymbol{\lambda}|\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{u})
with a set of inference parameters
\boldsymbol{\phi}
. The inference model is utilized to approximate the true posterior
p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{h},\boldsymbol{g},\boldsymbol{\lambda}|\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{u})
. Using Jensen’s inequality, Equation
1
can be bounded by:

\displaystyle L(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle\geq E_{q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\cdot)}[\log\frac{p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{h},\boldsymbol{g},\boldsymbol{\lambda}|\boldsymbol{u})}{q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{h},\boldsymbol{g},\boldsymbol{\lambda}|\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{u})}]

\displaystyle=E_{q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\cdot)}[\log p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{h},\boldsymbol{g},\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{u})]-\text{KL}(q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{h},\boldsymbol{g},\boldsymbol{\lambda}|\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{u})\,||\,p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{h},\boldsymbol{g},\boldsymbol{\lambda}|\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{u}))

(2)

where
q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\cdot)=q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{h},\boldsymbol{g},\boldsymbol{\lambda}|\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{u})
. Equation
3.1
is also known as the Variational Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO). The first term is a reconstruction loss composed of the expected log-likelihood. This term encourages the approximate posterior from the inference model to properly explain the observed variables. The second term is the KL-divergence between the prior and the approximate posterior. This term encourages the approximate posterior to be close to the prior. The resulting model can be decomposed into two parts: a generative model to estimate the joint distribution and an inference model to estimate the posterior for the latent variables.

To ensure the ELBO can be estimated effectively, we design the model (seen in Figure
1
) to allow for closed-form estimation of the KL-divergence term. A closed form solution avoids sampling from
p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{h},\boldsymbol{g},\boldsymbol{\lambda}|\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{u})
during training and speeds up estimation of the loss. Our choices for the generative/inference portions of the model will be detailed in Subsections
3.2
and
3.3
. A more detailed explanation for the variational inference steps are shown in the Appendix.

The generative portion of the model can be seen in Figure
1
, Part a. The joint distribution can be factorized as follows:

\displaystyle p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{h},\boldsymbol{g},\boldsymbol{\lambda}|\boldsymbol{u})=p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{g})\prod_{t=1}^{T}

\displaystyle p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{y}_{t}|\boldsymbol{z}_{t})p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{t}|\boldsymbol{h}_{t},\boldsymbol{z}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t},\boldsymbol{g})p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{h}_{t}|\boldsymbol{h}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{y}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{u}_{t})p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t})

The generative model composed of estimating two global distributions and four time-specific distributions. The global part consists of estimating

while the time-specific part consists of estimating

3.2.1
Generate Latent Variables

To allow for more efficient posterior sampling
[
Ingraham and Marks 2016
]
, we write

p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{t}|\boldsymbol{h}_{t},\boldsymbol{z}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t},\boldsymbol{g})
in non-centered parameterization form as follows:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{z}_{t}\sim\boldsymbol{z}_{t}^{*}\boldsymbol{\tau}_{t}^{*}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t}.

(4)

The prior for
\boldsymbol{z}_{t}
can be decomposed into two parts: a normal prior given by
\boldsymbol{z}_{t}^{*}
and shrinkage components given by
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{t}^{*}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t}
. Note that
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{t}^{*}
is a function of both
\boldsymbol{g}
and
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t}
, details will be shown in Equation
8
.

Note that
\boldsymbol{z}_{t}^{*}
is a function of the output of the recurrent neural network from the current time-step and the latent variables from the previous time-step.

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{z}_{t}^{*}\sim N(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{z}}(\boldsymbol{h}_{t},\boldsymbol{z}_{t-1}),\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{z}}(\boldsymbol{h}_{t},\boldsymbol{z}_{t-1}))

(5)

where
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{z}}(\boldsymbol{h}_{t},\boldsymbol{z}_{t-1})=NN_{\boldsymbol{\theta},1}(\boldsymbol{h}_{t},\boldsymbol{z}_{t-1})
,
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{z}}(\boldsymbol{h}_{t},\boldsymbol{z}_{t-1})
=
\text{SoftPlus}(NN_{\boldsymbol{\theta},2}(\boldsymbol{h}_{t},\boldsymbol{z}_{t-1}))
in which
NN
denote a feed-forward neural network. Note we use different numbered subscripts to denote that the feed-forward neural networks are different.

3.2.2
Generate Shrinkage Variables

The shrinkage components
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{t}^{*}
and
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t}
are the regularized global and local components of the shrinkage prior placed on the latent variables. The purpose of these terms is to reduce redundancy and add additional robustness. We chose inverse gamma-gamma (IG-G) prior
[
Bai and Ghosh 2017
]
because it has strong posterior contraction properties and has previously shown to be effective in a variational inference setting
[
Ghosh et al. 2018
]
. The IG-G prior falls under the class of global-local shrinkage priors with a global component to track overall shrinkage and local components to track shrinkage at each time-step. This set-up allows for strong global shrinkage while still maintaining localized adaptivity. We utilize a regularized version of the prior to achieve an bound on the upper tail
[
Piironen and Vehtari 2017
]
. We will discuss our choice for the local components
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t}
first followed by the global component.

Suppose there are
Q
latent variables estimated at each time-step, the prior models each of the variables
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t}=[\lambda_{t,1},...,\lambda_{t,Q}]
independently using a standard half-Cauchy distribution denoted by
C^{+}(0,1)
. Since approximating the posterior directly from a half-Cauchy distribution can be difficult, we will use the decomposition to write the half-Cauchy as a combination of a Gamma and an inverse-Gamma distribution
[
Neville et al. 2014
]
.

\displaystyle\lambda_{t,i}^{2}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{t,i}\beta_{t,i}

\displaystyle\text{for \; $i$}=1,...,Q

\displaystyle\alpha_{t,i}

\displaystyle\sim G(0.5,1)

\displaystyle\beta_{t,i}\sim IG(0.5,1)

(6)

where
G(a,b)
and
IG(a,b)
is the Gamma and inverse-Gamma distribution with shape parameter
a
and scale parameter
b
.

The global component of the model consists of two variables:
\boldsymbol{g}=(\tau,c)
.
\tau
estimates global amount of shrinkage across all time-steps and
c
controls the upper bound for the shrinkage components. Details for these components will be given in Equation
3.2.2
and
9
. A similar decomposition can be used for
p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\tau)
. In a IG-G prior,
\tau\sim C^{+}(0,\tau_{0})
where
\tau_{0}
is a hyperparameter.

\displaystyle\tau^{2}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{\tau}\beta_{\tau}

\displaystyle\alpha_{\tau}

\displaystyle\sim G(0.5,\tau_{0}^{2})

\displaystyle\beta_{\tau}\sim IG(0.5,1).

(7)

One potential issue of the half-Cauchy distribution is the fat tail which may lead to undesirable, large values for the latent variables. To bound the upper limit of the shrinkage prior, we write
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{t}^{*}=[\tau_{t,1}^{*},...,\tau_{t,Q}^{*}]
as follows:

\displaystyle\tau_{t,i}^{*2}

\displaystyle=\frac{c^{2}\tau^{2}}{c^{2}+\tau^{2}\lambda_{t,i}^{2}}

\displaystyle\text{for \; $i$}=1,...,Q

(8)

where
c
is a weight decay variable for controlling the upper-bound. With this set-up, in time-steps when
\tau^{2}\lambda_{t,i}^{2}\gg c^{2}
,
\tau_{t,i}^{*2}\lambda_{t,i}^{2}\rightarrow c^{2}
. In time-steps when
\tau^{2}\lambda_{t,i}^{2}\ll c^{2}
,
\tau_{t,i}^{*2}\lambda_{t,i}^{2}\rightarrow\tau^{2}\lambda_{t,i}^{2}
, which is the standard horseshoe. As a result, the regularized version maintains the strong posterior contraction of the horseshoe prior while avoiding arbitrarily high values for the latent variables. As recommended by
Piironen and Vehtari 2017
, we place an inverse gamma prior on
c^{2}
with two hyperparameters
c_{0}
and
c_{1}
:

\displaystyle c^{2}\sim IG(c_{0},c_{1})

(9)

3.2.3
Generate Response Variables

As previous discussed in Section 1, we restrict
\{\boldsymbol{y}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
to be a linear transformation of the latent variables plus some noise component. Assuming
\boldsymbol{y}_{t}
follows normal distribution,
p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{y}_{t}|\boldsymbol{z}_{t})
can be written as follows:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{y}_{t}\sim N(\boldsymbol{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\boldsymbol{z}_{t},\sigma_{\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{y}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{t}))

(10)

where
\sigma_{\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{y}}(\cdot)=\text{SoftPlus}(NN_{\boldsymbol{\theta},3}(\boldsymbol{z}_{t}))
and
\boldsymbol{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is a linear transformation. We adopt the SoftPlus transformation, previously seen in
Li et al. 2019
, to ensure that the standard deviation is non-negative. We allow the standard deviation to be a non-linear transformation of the latent variables to give additional flexibility for complex noises. Setting
\boldsymbol{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
as non-time-varying makes the relationship between the predicted response and the latent variables easier to understand. As we will discuss in Section
4
, this set-up allows
\{\boldsymbol{z}_{t}\}
to be interpreted as random effects in a linear mixed model.

3.3
Inference Model

The inference portion of the model can be seen in Figure
1
, Part b. We approximate the true posterior with the following factorization:

\displaystyle q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{h},\boldsymbol{g},\boldsymbol{\lambda}|\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{u})=

\displaystyle q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{g}|\boldsymbol{y})\prod_{t=1}^{T}q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{t}|\boldsymbol{z}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{y}_{t},\boldsymbol{h}_{t},\boldsymbol{g},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t})

\displaystyle q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t}|\boldsymbol{z}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{h}_{t})p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{h}_{t}|\boldsymbol{h}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{y}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{u}_{t})

(11)

For the inference portion, we estimate the posterior for the global variables
\boldsymbol{g}
using all available observed variables. Two posterior distributions are calculated at each time-step: an estimation for posterior of
\boldsymbol{z}_{t}
and an estimation for the posterior of
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t}
. Since
\boldsymbol{h}_{t}
is deterministic, we use the same network as the generative portion. The key to the inference step is finding an appropriate family for the posterior of each variable to allow for estimation of intractable posterior seen in Equation
1
. We will explain the choice and reasoning for each of the posteriors below, starting with
q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{t}|\boldsymbol{z}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{y}_{t},\boldsymbol{h}_{t},\tau,c,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t})
.

3.3.1
Inference of Latent Variables

Due to the non-centered parameterization for the generative model seen in Equation
4
, we adopt a similar parameterization for the approximate posterior. Note we use tilde notation to indicate the posterior estimates for the variables from the inference network.

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\widetilde{z}}_{t}

\displaystyle\sim\boldsymbol{\widetilde{z}}_{t}^{*}\boldsymbol{\widetilde{\tau}}_{t}^{*}\boldsymbol{\widetilde{\lambda}}_{t}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\widetilde{z}}_{t}^{*}

\displaystyle\sim N(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\widetilde{z}}}(\boldsymbol{\widetilde{z}}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{y}_{t},\boldsymbol{h}_{t}),\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\widetilde{z}}}(\boldsymbol{\widetilde{z}}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{y}_{t},\boldsymbol{h}_{t}))

(12)

where
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\widetilde{z}}}(\boldsymbol{\widetilde{z}}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{y}_{t},\boldsymbol{h}_{t})=NN_{\boldsymbol{\phi},1}(\boldsymbol{\widetilde{z}}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{y}_{t},\boldsymbol{h}_{t})
, (
\boldsymbol{\widetilde{\tau}}_{t}^{*}
,
\boldsymbol{\widetilde{\lambda}}_{t}
) denote the approximate posteriors for the shrinkage variables and
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\widetilde{z}}}(\boldsymbol{\widetilde{z}}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{y}_{t},\boldsymbol{h}_{t})=\text{SoftPlus}(NN_{\boldsymbol{\phi},2}(\boldsymbol{\widetilde{z}}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{y}_{t},\boldsymbol{h}_{t}))
. With this set-up, the posterior for the latent variables can be expressed as

\boldsymbol{\widetilde{z}}_{t}\sim N(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\widetilde{z}}}(\boldsymbol{\widetilde{z}}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{y}_{t},\boldsymbol{h}_{t})\boldsymbol{\widetilde{\tau}}_{t}^{*}\boldsymbol{\widetilde{\lambda}}_{t},\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\widetilde{z}}}(\boldsymbol{\widetilde{z}}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{y}_{t},\boldsymbol{h}_{t})\boldsymbol{\widetilde{\tau}}_{t}^{*}\boldsymbol{\widetilde{\lambda}}_{t})

This allows
\boldsymbol{\widetilde{z}}_{t}
to be viewed as a scale mixture of normals with scales
\boldsymbol{\widetilde{\tau}}_{t}^{*}\boldsymbol{\widetilde{\lambda}}_{t}
.

3.3.2
Inference of Shrinkage Variables

From Equation
3.2.2
, we see that each of the
\boldsymbol{\widetilde{\lambda}}_{t}=[\widetilde{\lambda}_{t,1},...,\widetilde{\lambda}_{t,Q}]
is modeled independently by a gamma and an inverse gamma distribution. We approximate each using a log-normal distribution. We choose log-normal distribution as it has a closed form KL-divergence with Gamma/Inverse-Gamma prior
[
Louizos and Welling 2017
]
and allows for efficient sampling
[
Ghosh and Doshi-Velez 2017
]
. The approximate posterior looks as follows:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\lambda}_{t,i}^{2}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\alpha}_{t,i}\widetilde{\beta}_{t,i}\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\text{for $i$}=1,...,Q

\displaystyle\widetilde{\alpha}_{t,i}

\displaystyle\sim LN(\mu_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\alpha,t,i}(\boldsymbol{z}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{h}_{t}),\sigma_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\alpha,t,i}(\boldsymbol{z}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{h}_{t}))

\displaystyle\widetilde{\beta}_{t,i}

\displaystyle\sim LN(\mu_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\beta,t,i}(\boldsymbol{z}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{h}_{t}),\sigma_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\beta,t,i}(\boldsymbol{z}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{h}_{t}))

(13)

where
LN(\cdot,\cdot)
represents a log-normal distribution. The posterior parameters

(\mu_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\alpha,t,i}(\boldsymbol{z}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{h}_{t}),\sigma_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\alpha,t,i}(\boldsymbol{z}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{h}_{t})
,
\mu_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\beta,t,i}(\boldsymbol{z}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{h}_{t})
,
\sigma_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\beta,t,i}(\boldsymbol{z}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{h}_{t}))
are each estimated

through a feed-forward neural network. The posterior for the global portion is shown in the Appendix.

4
Latent Variables Interpretability

As previously mentioned, directly interpretable models such as generalized linear models (GLMs)
[
McCullagh and Nelder 2019
]
have been widely and successfully applied. Equation
10
shows the relationship between the response and the latent variables. From the set-up, it’s clear that
\{\boldsymbol{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\boldsymbol{z}_{t}\}
is the linear predictor component of the normal likelihood. By utilizing a linear layer rather than a deep neural network in the generative framework, the model makes the relationship between the response and the latent variables much easier to understand, therefore enhancing the interpretability of the latent variables. Furthermore, shrinkage priors are utilized to borrow strength across the variables
[
Fourdrinier et al. 2018
,
Seto et al. 2021
]
in order to reduce redundancy in the latent variables. In particular, from Equations
4
-
10
one can see that the latent variables can be interpreted as random effects in a linear mixed model with an exotic variance component.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we introduced a deep state-space model (DSSM-SH) with the ability to produce interpretable latent variables. Our two key modifications to the DSSM framework are restricting the relationship between the response and the latent variables to be a linear function plus some noise component, and incorporating inverse gamma-gamma priors for the latent variables. As we have shown through two real world benchmark datasets, these modifications lead to more interpretable latent results and improved forecasting performances. Our model outperforms both ARIMA (a state-space model) and DeepAR (a RNN based model), showing the effectiveness of the DSSM framework in combining the two parts. In comparison to DSSM, a deep state-space model without shrinkage, our model performs better in forecasting metrics. This shows that add shrinkage priors reduce redundancy and improve robustness.

Model Details

Generate RNN Variables

The variables
\{\boldsymbol{h}_{t}\}
are estimated in a deterministic manner via a recurrent neural network with gated recurrent units. Recurrent neural network unfolds along the temporal horizon, making it a natural choice for the time series modeling. We write the update equation as follows:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{h}_{t}

\displaystyle=\delta(\text{GRU}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{h}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{u}_{t},\boldsymbol{y}_{t-1}))

where GRU
(\cdot)
is gated recurrent unit update and
\delta(\cdot)
is the delta-Dirac function.

Inference of Global Shrinkage Variables

The posterior for the global portion of the shrinkage prior
\boldsymbol{g}=(\tau,c)
are estimated conditional on the entirety of the observed data. We adopt two log-normal distributions for the approximate posterior of
\tau
. An additional log-normal will be used as the posterior of
c
.

\displaystyle\widetilde{\tau}^{2}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\alpha}_{\tau}\widetilde{\beta}_{\tau}

\displaystyle\widetilde{c}^{2}\sim LN(\mu_{\boldsymbol{\phi},c}(\boldsymbol{y}_{1:T}),\sigma_{\boldsymbol{\phi},c}(\boldsymbol{y}_{1:T}))

\displaystyle\widetilde{\alpha}_{\tau}

\displaystyle\sim LN(\mu_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\alpha,\tau}(\boldsymbol{y}_{1:T}),\sigma_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\alpha,\tau}(\boldsymbol{y}_{1:T}))

\displaystyle\widetilde{\beta}_{\tau}\sim LN(\mu_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\beta,\tau}(\boldsymbol{y}_{1:T}),\sigma_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\beta,\tau}(\boldsymbol{y}_{1:T}))

where once again
(\mu_{\boldsymbol{\phi},c}(\boldsymbol{y}_{1:T})
,
\sigma_{\boldsymbol{\phi},c}(\boldsymbol{y}_{1:T})
,
\mu_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\alpha,\tau}(\boldsymbol{y}_{1:T})
,
\sigma_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\alpha,\tau}(\boldsymbol{y}_{1:T})
,
\mu_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\beta,\tau}(\boldsymbol{y}_{1:T})
,
\sigma_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\beta,\tau}(\boldsymbol{y}_{1:T}))
are all feed-forward neural networks. These neural networks takes average of all observed variables as inputs to allow for varying length sequences. Conditional on
(\widetilde{\tau},\widetilde{c},\boldsymbol{\widetilde{\lambda}}_{t})
,
\boldsymbol{\widetilde{\tau}}_{t}^{*}
can be estimated as follows:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\widetilde{\tau}}_{t}^{*2}=\frac{\widetilde{c}^{2}\widetilde{\tau}^{2}}{\widetilde{c}^{2}+\widetilde{\tau}^{2}\boldsymbol{\widetilde{\lambda}}_{t}^{2}}.

Variational Inference / Forecasting Procedures

Estimating the expectation term in the inference Equation remains a difficult task. To reduce the variance of the estimator, we utilize Stochastic Gradient Variational Bayes
[
Kingma and Welling 2013
,
Rezende et al. 2014
]
and the reparameterization trick. At each time step, we sample
q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\tau|\boldsymbol{y}_{1:T})
,
q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(c|\boldsymbol{y}_{1:T})
and
q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t}|\boldsymbol{z}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{h}_{t})
from the log-normal posterior distributions. However, instead of directly sampling from
q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{t}|\boldsymbol{z}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{y}_{t},\boldsymbol{h}_{t},\tau,c,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t})
, we sample from auxiliary random variables
\boldsymbol{\widetilde{\epsilon}}_{t}\sim N(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{I})
where
\boldsymbol{I}
is the identity matrix. We then obtain samples of the posterior through a transformation the auxiliary variables as follows:
\boldsymbol{\widetilde{z}}_{t}=\boldsymbol{\widetilde{\epsilon}}_{t}\odot\boldsymbol{\widetilde{\sigma}}_{\boldsymbol{z},t}+\boldsymbol{\widetilde{\mu}}_{\boldsymbol{z},t}
where
\odot
is element-wise product,
\boldsymbol{\widetilde{\mu}}_{\boldsymbol{z},t}=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\widetilde{z}}}(\boldsymbol{\widetilde{z}}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{y}_{t},\boldsymbol{h}_{t})\boldsymbol{\widetilde{\tau}}_{t}^{*}\boldsymbol{\widetilde{\lambda}}_{t}
and

\boldsymbol{\widetilde{\sigma}}_{\boldsymbol{z},t}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\widetilde{z}}}(\boldsymbol{\widetilde{z}}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{y}_{t},\boldsymbol{h}_{t})\boldsymbol{\widetilde{\tau}}_{t}^{*}\boldsymbol{\widetilde{\lambda}}_{t}
. By utilizing auxiliary random variables, we separate the source of randomness from the posterior parameters in which the gradients are required. This, in turn, reduces the variance of the gradient estimators.

During testing time, Monte Carlo samples will be utilized to generate forecasts from the model. Suppose the goal is to forecast
p(\boldsymbol{y}_{(T+1):(T+p)}|\boldsymbol{y}_{1:T},\boldsymbol{u}_{1:(T+p)})
with
p
denoting the forecasting horizon. First, we sample global variables from their approximate posterior
q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(c,\tau|\boldsymbol{y}_{1:T})
using the sequence of known observed values. The sampled values will be used in all time-steps in the forecasting horizon.

Next, for each time-step from
1
to
T
, we iteratively estimate all time-dependent variables using both the generative and the inference portions of the model. The latent variables will be sampled from the posterior distribution
q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{t}|\boldsymbol{z}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{y}_{t},\boldsymbol{h}_{t},\tau,c,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t})
until the last known time-step. For each time-step
t
in the forecasting horizon from
T+1
to
T+p
, we iteratively update the model to obtain updates as follows:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\widetilde{y}}_{T+t}

\displaystyle\sim N(\boldsymbol{A}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\boldsymbol{z}_{T+t},\sigma_{\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{y}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{t}))

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{z}_{T+t}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{z}_{T+t}^{*}\boldsymbol{\widetilde{\tau}}_{T+t}^{*}\boldsymbol{\widetilde{\lambda}}_{T+t}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{z}_{T+t}^{*}

\displaystyle\sim N(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\theta,\boldsymbol{z}}(\boldsymbol{h}_{T+t},\boldsymbol{z}_{T+t-1}),\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\theta,\boldsymbol{z}}(\boldsymbol{h}_{T+t},\boldsymbol{z}_{T+t-1}))

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{h}_{T+t}

\displaystyle=\delta(\text{GRU}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{h}_{T+t-1},\boldsymbol{u}_{T+t},\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}}_{T+t-1}))

where
\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}}_{T+t-1}
is the sampled response from the previous time-step.
\boldsymbol{\widetilde{\lambda}}_{T+t}
is sampled using the generator model and
\boldsymbol{\widetilde{\tau}}_{T+t}^{*}
is calculated using the inference model.

One note to highlight is that we sample the variables associated with the shrinkage prior from the inference model and the latent variables from the generative model. This is due to the fact that in the inference model, estimation of
\boldsymbol{\widetilde{z}_{t}}^{*}
requires
\boldsymbol{y}_{t}
which makes it infeasible during testing time. In contrast, we specifically designed the estimation for the approximate posterior of the shrinkage variables to not rely on
\boldsymbol{y}_{t}
. This choice allows the shrinkage variables to be sampled from their approximate posteriors which provide more information.

Variational Inference Details

In this section, we will give more details concerning the estimation of the cost function for our model. Given the details about the generative and the inference portion of the models seen in Section 3, we start with the ELBO as follows:

\displaystyle L(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\phi})

\displaystyle\equiv E_{q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{1:T},\boldsymbol{h}_{1:T},\tau,c,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1:T}|\boldsymbol{y}_{1:T},\boldsymbol{u}_{1:T})}[\log p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{y}_{1:T}|\boldsymbol{z}_{1:T},\boldsymbol{h}_{1:T},\tau,c,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1:T},\boldsymbol{u}_{1:T})]-

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\text{KL}(q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{1:T},\boldsymbol{h}_{1:T},\tau,c,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1:T}|\boldsymbol{y}_{1:T},\boldsymbol{u}_{1:T})||p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{1:T},\boldsymbol{h}_{1:T},\tau,c,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1:T}|\boldsymbol{y}_{1:T},\boldsymbol{u}_{1:T}))

\displaystyle\equiv L_{E}-L_{KL}

This loss has two terms which we will discuss individually. For clarity and ease of notation, we will denote the first term as
L_{E}
and the second term as
L_{KL}
. We will start with a discussion for the KL term followed by discussion of the expectation term. We first perform the following factorization for
L_{KL}
:

\displaystyle L_{KL}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}E_{q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\tau,c,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t}|\boldsymbol{y}_{t},\boldsymbol{h}_{t},\boldsymbol{u}_{t})}[

\displaystyle KL(q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{t}|\boldsymbol{z}_{t-1},\tau,c,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t},\boldsymbol{y}_{t},\boldsymbol{h}_{t},\boldsymbol{u}_{1:T})||

\displaystyle p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{t}|\boldsymbol{z}_{t-1},\tau,c,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t},\boldsymbol{y}_{t},\boldsymbol{h}_{t},\boldsymbol{u}_{t}))]

\displaystyle+KL(q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\tau,c,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t}|\boldsymbol{z}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{y}_{1:T},\boldsymbol{u}_{t},\boldsymbol{h}_{t})||p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\tau,c,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t}))

This factorization allow KL-divergence terms to be written as sum of individual KL-terms across time. The reason this factorization works is due to the structure we chose for the generative and inference models. Note that
\boldsymbol{h}_{1:T}
is deterministic and uses the same RNN for both the generative and inference portions. This choice leads to a KL-divergence of 0 for
\boldsymbol{h}_{1:T}
which simplify the derivation.

We will now break down each term in
L_{KL}
. First, in the generative model, the distributions
(\tau,c,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t})
do not conditional on any other variables and are independent of one another. This allow us to drop the conditional for this term. In the inference model, conditional on
(\boldsymbol{z}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{y}_{1:T},\boldsymbol{u}_{t},\boldsymbol{h}_{t})
, the approximate posterior of
(\tau,c,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t})
are independent of one another. This allow us to fully factorize
KL(q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\tau,c,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t}|\boldsymbol{z}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{y}_{1:T},\boldsymbol{u}_{t},\boldsymbol{h}_{t})||p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\tau,c,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t}))
into separate terms and evaluated analytically. From the posterior and prior choices given in Section 3, this KL term has a closed form solution. This closed form solution relies the closed form KL-divergence formula between log-normal random variables and Gamma/inverse-Gamma random variables derived in
Louizos et al. 2017
.

Second, conditional on
(\boldsymbol{z}_{t-1},\tau,c,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t},\boldsymbol{y}_{t},\boldsymbol{h}_{t},\boldsymbol{u}_{1:T})
, the distribution of
\boldsymbol{z}_{t}
is normal for both the generative and inference portion of the model. The conditional can be written as follows:

\displaystyle q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{t}|\boldsymbol{z}_{t-1},\tau,c,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t},\boldsymbol{y}_{t},\boldsymbol{h}_{t},\boldsymbol{u}_{t})

\displaystyle\sim N(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{z}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{y}_{t},\boldsymbol{h}_{t})\boldsymbol{\tau}_{t}^{*}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t},\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{z}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{y}_{t},\boldsymbol{h}_{t})\boldsymbol{\tau}_{t}^{*}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t})

\displaystyle p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{t}|\boldsymbol{z}_{t-1},\tau,c,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t},\boldsymbol{y}_{t},\boldsymbol{h}_{t},\boldsymbol{u}_{t})

\displaystyle\sim N(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{z}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{y}_{t},\boldsymbol{h}_{t})\boldsymbol{\tau}_{t}^{*}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t},\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{z}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{y}_{t},\boldsymbol{h}_{t})\boldsymbol{\tau}_{t}^{*}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t})

Suppose
\boldsymbol{z}_{t}\in R^{Q}
where
\boldsymbol{z}_{t}=(z_{t,1},...,z_{t,Q})
, the KL-divergence between the two terms can be calculated as follows:

\displaystyle KL(q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{t}|\boldsymbol{z}_{t-1},\tau,c,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t},\boldsymbol{y}_{t},\boldsymbol{h}_{t},\boldsymbol{u}_{t})||p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{t}|\boldsymbol{z}_{t-1},\tau,c,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t},\boldsymbol{y}_{t},\boldsymbol{h}_{t},\boldsymbol{u}_{t}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{Q}\log\frac{\sigma_{\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{z},i}(\boldsymbol{z}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{y}_{t},\boldsymbol{h}_{t})\tau_{t,i}^{*}\lambda_{t,i}}{\sigma_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{z},i}(\boldsymbol{z}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{y}_{t},\boldsymbol{h}_{t})\tau_{t,i}^{*}\lambda_{t,i}}-\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;+\frac{\sigma_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{z},i}^{2}(\boldsymbol{z}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{y}_{t},\boldsymbol{h}_{t})\tau_{t,i}^{2*}\lambda_{t,i}^{2}+(\mu_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{z},i}(\boldsymbol{z}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{y}_{t},\boldsymbol{h}_{t})\tau_{t}^{*}\lambda_{t}-\mu_{\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{z},i}(\boldsymbol{z}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{y}_{t},\boldsymbol{h}_{t})\tau_{t}^{*}\lambda_{t})^{2}}{2\sigma_{\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{z},i}^{2}(\boldsymbol{z}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{y}_{t},\boldsymbol{h}_{t})\tau_{t,i}^{2*}\lambda_{t,i}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{Q}\log\frac{\sigma_{\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{z},i}(\boldsymbol{z}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{y}_{t},\boldsymbol{h}_{t})}{\sigma_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{z},i}(\boldsymbol{z}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{y}_{t},\boldsymbol{h}_{t})}

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;+\frac{\sigma_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{z},i}^{2}(\boldsymbol{z}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{y}_{t},\boldsymbol{h}_{t})+(\mu_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{z},i}(\boldsymbol{z}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{y}_{t},\boldsymbol{h}_{t})-\mu_{\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{z},i}(\boldsymbol{z}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{y}_{t},\boldsymbol{h}_{t}))^{2}}{2\sigma_{\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{z},i}^{2}(\boldsymbol{z}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{y}_{t},\boldsymbol{h}_{t})}-\frac{1}{2}

As seen, all terms involving the shrinkage variables
(\tau,c,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1:T})
cancels out in the KL-divergence. As a result,

\displaystyle E_{q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\tau,c,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t}|\boldsymbol{y}_{t},\boldsymbol{h}_{t},\boldsymbol{u}_{t})}

\displaystyle[KL(q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{t}|\boldsymbol{z}_{t-1},\tau,c,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t},\boldsymbol{y}_{t},\boldsymbol{h}_{t},\boldsymbol{u}_{1:T})||p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{t}|\boldsymbol{z}_{t-1},\tau,c,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t},\boldsymbol{y}_{t},\boldsymbol{h}_{t},\boldsymbol{u}_{t}))]

\displaystyle=KL(q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{t}|\boldsymbol{z}_{t-1},\tau,c,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t},\boldsymbol{y}_{t},\boldsymbol{h}_{t},\boldsymbol{u}_{1:T})||p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{t}|\boldsymbol{z}_{t-1},\tau,c,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t},\boldsymbol{y}_{t},\boldsymbol{h}_{t},\boldsymbol{u}_{t}))

This once again allows for a closed-form solution which allows for effective evaluation of the KL-divergence. This solution is one of the key reasons behind many of the design choices for the generative/inference portions of the model. By choosing approximate conditional dependencies among variables, we allow for a closed-form solution for the KL-divergence in the loss. A closed-form solution leads to more accurate gradients and faster evaluations.

Given a closed form solution for
L_{KL}
, we will now focus our attention to the first part of the loss:
L_{E}
. Similar to the first step in factorizing
L_{KL}
, we adopt the following factorization for
L_{E}
:

\displaystyle L_{E}

\displaystyle=E_{q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{1:T},\boldsymbol{h}_{1:T},\tau,c,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1:T}|\boldsymbol{y}_{1:T},\boldsymbol{u}_{1:T})}[\log p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{y}_{1:T}|\boldsymbol{z}_{1:T},\boldsymbol{h}_{1:T},\tau,c,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1:T},\boldsymbol{u}_{1:T})]

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}E_{q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{t}|\boldsymbol{z}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{h}_{t},\tau,c,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t},\boldsymbol{y}_{t},\boldsymbol{u}_{t})}[\log p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{y}_{t}|\boldsymbol{z}_{t})]

This factorization results directly from the choices we made in the generative model. Conditional on
\boldsymbol{z}_{t}
, the generative distribution for
\boldsymbol{y}_{t}
is independent of over other variables in the model. To evaluate this expectation, we obtain samples from the approximate posterior
q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{t}|\boldsymbol{z}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{h}_{t},\tau,c,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t},\boldsymbol{y}_{t},\boldsymbol{u}_{t})
and evaluate the log predictive likelihood.

Through our choices for the generative and inference portions of the model, we are able to obtain a time-based factorization of the loss. This allows the loss function to be calculated one-step at a time during training. With the closed-form KL-divergence terms, we do not need to sample any variables from the generative model in the training phase. We only need to sample from
q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{t}|\boldsymbol{z}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{h}_{t},\tau,c,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t},\boldsymbol{y}_{t},\boldsymbol{u}_{t})
at each time-step
t
of the training process. For sampling, we utilize reparameterization trick as detailed above.
