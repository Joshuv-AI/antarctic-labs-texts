Almost sure convergence rates of adaptive increasingly rare Markov chain Monte Carlo
Julian Hofstadler, Krzysztof Łatuszyński, Gareth O. Roberts, Daniel Rudolf

Abstract
We consider adaptive increasingly rare Markov chain Monte Carlo (AIR MCMC), which is an adaptive MCMC method, where the adaptation concerning the “past” happens less and less frequently over time. Under a contraction assumption for a Wasserstein-like function we deduce upper bounds of the convergence rate of Monte Carlo sums taking a renormalisation factor into account that is close to the one that appears in a law of the iterated logarithm. We demonstrate the applicability of our results by considering different settings, among which are those of simultaneous geometric and uniform ergodicity. All proofs are carried out on an augmented state space, including the classical non-augmented setting as a special case. In contrast to other adaptive MCMC limit theory, some technical assumptions, like diminishing adaptation, are not needed.



Keywords:
adaptive Markov chain Monte Carlo, almost sure convergence rate, Wasserstein contraction

65C05; 65C20; 60J22

Adaptive MCMC allows to update the single step transition mechanism by taking the whole history into account and therefore leads to non-Markovian processes (in general).

2
Preliminaries

In this section we provide all required objects to establish our limit theorems later.
We start with some general notation and assumptions.

Following
[
PHŁ20
]

\mathcal{Y}=\mathcal{X}\times\Phi

To avoid problems concerning measurability, all involved spaces are assumed to be Polish with countable generated

Given some measurable space

1)

a family of probability distributions

a family of transition kernels

To be more precise, by 2) we mean that for any
\gamma\in\mathcal{I}
the mapping
{P}_{\gamma}\colon\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{Y}}\to[0,1]
is a transition kernel. We assume that
{\pi}_{\gamma}
has marginal
\nu
for any
\gamma\in\mathcal{I}
, that is,
{\pi_{\gamma}}(B\times\Phi)=\nu(B)
for any
B\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{X}}
and is the invariant distribution of
{P}_{\gamma}({y},\cdot)
(for any
{y}\in\mathcal{Y}
).
Moreover, we assume that any kernel
P_{\gamma}
is
\psi
-irreducible and aperiodic, see
[
MT12
]
for definitions.

2.1
Adaptive and AIR MCMC

We consider an adaptive MCMC chain, say
(Y_{n},\Gamma_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
, evolving in the space
(\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{I})
, where
(\Gamma_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
denotes the sequence of (possibly random) adaptation parameters.
In other words,
\mathcal{Y}
is the state space of
(Y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
and
\mathcal{I}
that of
(\Gamma_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
.
We assume that there exists some underlying (sufficiently regular) probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
and set
\mathcal{F}_{n}=\sigma\left(Y_{0},\dots,Y_{n},\Gamma_{0},\dots,\Gamma_{n}\right)
, such that
(\mathcal{F}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is the natural filtration of
(Y_{n},\Gamma_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
.
We assume that
(Y_{0},\Gamma_{0})\equiv(y_{0},\gamma_{0})
for some initial values
y_{0}\in\mathcal{Y}
and
\gamma_{0}\in\mathcal{I}
.

The one step transition probabilities of
(Y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
are specified via

\mathbb{P}\left[Y_{n+1}\in B|Y_{n}={y},\Gamma_{n}=\gamma\right]={P}_{\gamma}(y,B),

(3)

where
B\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{Y}}
.
Moreover, the adaptation parameter
\Gamma_{n}
is chosen according to some update rule
2
2

2

The functions
U_{n}
are assumed to be measurable.

U_{n}\colon\mathcal{Y}^{n+1}\times\mathcal{I}^{n}\to\mathcal{I}
, allowing to “improve” the kernel of
Y_{n+1}
based on the already known values of
Y_{0},\dots,Y_{n}
and
\Gamma_{0},\dots,\Gamma_{n-1}
.
That is
\Gamma_{n}=U_{n}(Y_{0},\dots,Y_{n},\Gamma_{0},\dots,\Gamma_{n-1})
.

Using the regularity of all involved spaces and standard techniques of measure theory we have that
\mathbb{P}
-almost surely

\mathbb{E}\left[g(Y_{n+1})|\mathcal{F}_{n}\right]=P_{\Gamma_{n}}g(Y_{n}),

(4)

where
g\colon\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
is an arbitrary measurable function such that
P_{\gamma}g(y)<\infty
for any
\gamma,y
.

We assume that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
the mapping
\omega\mapsto\pi_{\Gamma_{n}(\omega)}(g)
is measurable for any (measurable) function
g\colon\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\pi_{\gamma}(|g|)<\infty
for all
\gamma\in\mathcal{I}
.

Since
(Y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
evolves in the augmented space
\mathcal{Y}=\mathcal{X}\times\Phi
, each state has the form
Y_{n}=(X_{n},\phi_{n})
, with an
\mathcal{X}
-valued component
X_{n}
and a
\Phi
-valued component
\phi_{n}
.
Given some measurable function of interest
f\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
with
\nu(|f|)<\infty
, we set
h=h_{f}=f\cdot\mathds{1}_{\Phi}
.
Using the fact that
\pi_{\gamma}
has marginal
\nu
we obtain the following

S_{n}f-\nu(f)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}f(X_{j})-\nu(f)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(h(Y_{j})-{\pi}_{\Gamma_{j-1}}(h)\right),

(5)

holding
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

In this paper we focus on AIR MCMC algorithms, as introduced in
[
CŁR18
]
.
This class of methods allows
\Gamma_{n}
to be updated only at specific times, in contrast to purely adaptive schemes where
\Gamma_{n}
may be changed after each step.
Given
\beta>0
we set
k_{j}=\lceil j^{\beta}\rceil
and allow adaptation only at the times

T_{m}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}k_{j}.

That is, for
i_{1},i_{2}\in\{T_{j},\dots,T_{j+1}-1\}
(with
j\in\mathbb{N}
) we have
\Gamma_{i_{1}}=\Gamma_{i_{2}}
.

A simple, yet important observation is that for any
\beta>0
there exist constants
c_{\beta},C_{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

c_{\beta}m^{1+\beta}\leq T_{m}\leq C_{\beta}m^{1+\beta}.

(6)

In particular, this means that we adapt less and less frequently.

2.2
Martingale approximation

Our results shall be proven using a martingale approximation technique based on solutions of Poisson’s equation.
Providing a powerful tool for the analysis of general state space Markov chains, see e.g.
[
MT12
,
DMPS18
]
, Poisson’s equation was later also used in the adaptive setting, e.g. in
[
AM06
,
SV10
,
ALR13
]
. See also
[
AF10
,
AF12
]
for further generalisations.

For a function of interest
h\colon\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
as well as
\gamma\in\mathcal{I}
we say
u_{\gamma}
is a solution of Poisson’s equation if

u_{\gamma}(y)-P_{\gamma}u_{\gamma}(y)=h(y)-{\pi}_{\gamma}(h)

for any
y\in\mathcal{Y}
.
Provided such an
u_{\gamma}
exists for any
\gamma\in\Gamma
we obtain the following decomposition for the Monte Carlo sum of an adaptive MCMC method driven by (
3
):

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(h(Y_{j})-{\pi}_{\Gamma_{j-1}}(h)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left({u}_{\Gamma_{j-1}}(Y_{j})-{P}_{\Gamma_{j-1}}{u}_{\Gamma_{j-1}}(Y_{j-1})\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left({u}_{\Gamma_{j}}(Y_{j})-{u}_{\Gamma_{j-1}}(Y_{j})\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+h(Y_{n})-h(Y_{0})+{u}_{\Gamma_{0}}(Y_{0})-{u}_{\Gamma_{n}}(Y_{n}).

(7)

Recall that for an AIR scheme adaptation only happens at certain times
\{T_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
with
T_{0}:=0
.
Assume
T_{m}\leq n+1<T_{m+1}
,
then, since
u_{\Gamma_{i+1}}(Y_{i+1})-u_{\Gamma_{i}}(Y_{i+1})=0
for any
i\in\{T_{j},\dots,T_{j+1}-2\}
, equation (
2.2
) simplifies to

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(h(Y_{j})-{\pi}_{\Gamma_{j-1}}(h)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left({u}_{\Gamma_{j-1}}(Y_{j})-{P}_{\Gamma_{j-1}}{u}_{\Gamma_{j-1}}(Y_{j-1})\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\left(u_{\Gamma_{T_{j+1}}}(Y_{T_{j+1}})-u_{\Gamma_{T_{j}}}(Y_{T_{j+1}})\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+h(Y_{n})-h(Y_{0})+{u}_{\Gamma_{0}}(Y_{0})-{u}_{\Gamma_{n}}(Y_{n}).

(8)

Here “simplification” refers to the fact that (
2.2
) contains less summands of the form
{u}_{\Gamma_{j}}(Y_{j})-{u}_{\Gamma_{j-1}}(Y_{j})
compared to (
2.2
).

Let us define the following quantities:

\displaystyle M_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left({u}_{\Gamma_{j-1}}(Y_{j})-{P}_{\Gamma_{j-1}}{u}_{\Gamma_{j-1}}(Y_{j-1})\right);

\displaystyle\Delta_{j}

\displaystyle:={u}_{\Gamma_{j-1}}(Y_{j})-{P}_{\Gamma_{j-1}}{u}_{\Gamma_{j-1}}(Y_{j-1});

\displaystyle\text{and}\qquad R_{m}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\left(u_{\Gamma_{T_{j+1}}}(Y_{T_{j+1}})-u_{\Gamma_{T_{j}}}(Y_{T_{j+1}})\right).

Note that we have
\mathbb{E}[M_{n}|\mathcal{F}_{n-1}]=M_{n-1}
almost surely, see (

).

2.3
Distance-like and Wasserstein-like functions

We consider a measurable space
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{A}})
, which is Polish w.r.t. some metric
d_{\mathcal{A}}
and has a countably generated
\sigma
-algebra.

In the spirit of
[
HMS11
]
, we call a lower semi-continuous function
d\colon\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}

distance-like
if

a)

For any
x,y\in\mathcal{A}
holds
d(x,y)=d(y,x)
.

b)

We have
d(x,y)=0\;\Longleftrightarrow\;x=y
.

Roughly speaking, a distance-like function is a metric without triangle inequality and
clearly, any metric is distance-like.
As for the case where
d
is a metric, we call a function
\psi\colon\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}
Lipschitz if

|\psi(x)-\psi(y)|\leq L\cdot d(x,y),

for any
x,y\in\mathcal{A}
with some real constant
L>0
.

Given some distance-like function
d
on
\mathcal{A}
and probability measures
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}
on
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{A}})
we define

\mathcal{W}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2}):=\inf_{\xi\in\mathcal{C}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})}\int_{\mathcal{A}^{2}}d(x,y)\xi({\rm d}x,{\rm d}y),

where
\mathcal{C}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})
is the set of all couplings of
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}
.
As is argued in
[
HMS11
]
below Definition 4.3,
\mathcal{W}
is a reasonable tool for quantifying the distance between probability measures.
Following
[
HJ23
]
we call
\mathcal{W}
a
Wasserstein-like
function (w.r.t.
d
).
However, if
d
is a metric, then
\mathcal{W}
corresponds to the usual
1
-Wasserstein distance.

We write
\text{Lip}_{1}
for the set of all functions that are Lipschitz (w.r.t.
d
) with constant
1
.

Lemma 2.1
.

For
\mathcal{W}
w.r.t. distance-like function
d
holds

\mathcal{W}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})\geq\sup_{\psi\in\text{Lip}_{1}}\left|\int_{\mathcal{A}}\psi(x)\mu_{1}({\rm d}x)-\int_{\mathcal{A}}\psi(y)\mu_{2}({\rm d}y)\right|.

Proof.

Let
\psi\in\text{Lip}_{1}
.
By lower semi-continuity we have that there exists an optimal coupling
\xi
, i.e.
\mathcal{W}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})=\int d(x,y)\xi({\rm d}x,{\rm d}y)
.
Thus

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\mathcal{A}}\psi(x)\mu_{1}({\rm d}x)-\int_{\mathcal{A}}\psi(y)\mu_{2}({\rm d}y)\right|=\left|\int_{\mathcal{A}}\int_{\mathcal{A}}\psi(x)-\psi(y)\xi({\rm d}x,{\rm d}y)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathcal{A}}\int_{\mathcal{A}}d(x,y)\xi({\rm d}x,{\rm d}y)=\mathcal{W}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2}).

∎

The well known Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality states that if
d
is a metric, then one would have equality in Lemma
2.1
.

1)

Given

3.1
Solving Poisson’s equation

The upcoming result shows that under Assumption
3.1
Poisson’s equation has a solution which is bounded by the eccentricity.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
\gamma\in\mathcal{I}
,
f\colon\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
be
\pi_{\gamma}
integrable and Lipschitz with constant
L\in(0,\infty)
and let
E_{\gamma}(y)<\infty
for any
y\in\mathcal{Y}
.
If Assumption
3.1
is true, then the function
u_{\gamma}\colon\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
given via

u_{\gamma}(y)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}P_{\gamma}^{\ell}f(y)-\pi_{\gamma}(f)

is well defined and we have

i)

|u_{\gamma}(y)|\leq\frac{k_{0}LM^{k_{0}}E_{\gamma}(y)}{1-\tau}
for any
y\in\mathcal{Y}
,

ii)

u_{\gamma}(y)-P_{\gamma}u(y)=f(y)-\pi_{\gamma}(f)
for any
y\in\mathcal{Y}
.

Proof.

Let
\ell=tk_{0}+r\in\mathbb{N}
with
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
1\leq r<k_{0}
as well as
y\in\mathcal{Y}
.
By Lemma
2.1

\displaystyle\left|P_{\gamma}^{\ell}f(y)-\pi_{\gamma}(f)\right|

\displaystyle\leq L\sup_{\psi\in\text{Lip}_{1}}\left|P^{\ell}\psi(y)-\pi_{\gamma}(\psi)\right|\leq L\mathcal{W}(P_{\gamma}^{\ell}(y,\cdot),\pi_{\gamma})

\displaystyle\leq L\tau(P_{\gamma}^{r})\tau(P_{\gamma}^{tk_{0}})\mathcal{W}(\delta_{y},\pi_{\gamma})\leq LM^{r}\tau^{t}\mathcal{W}(\delta_{y},\pi_{\gamma})

\displaystyle\leq LM^{k_{0}}\tau^{t}E_{\gamma}(y)<\infty.

Using this bound we obtain

\displaystyle|u_{\gamma}(y)|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}|P_{\gamma}^{\ell}f(y)-\pi_{\gamma}(f)|=\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\sum_{j=tk_{0}+1}^{(t+1)k_{0}}|P_{\gamma}^{j}f(y)-\pi_{\gamma}(f)|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\sum_{j=tk_{0}+1}^{(t+1)k_{0}}LM^{k_{0}}\tau^{t}E_{\gamma}(y)=\frac{k_{0}LM^{k_{0}}E_{\gamma}(y)}{1-\tau}

This shows that
u_{\gamma}
is well defined and also that i) holds.

It remains to show ii):
It is no restriction to assume that
\pi_{\gamma}(f)=0

g=f-\pi_{\gamma}(f)

For any

\psi_{n}^{+}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}P^{\ell}_{\gamma}f^{+}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\psi_{n}^{-}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}P_{\gamma}^{\ell}f^{-},

where

and

are the positive and negative part of

.
For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
holds

P_{\gamma}\psi_{n}^{+}=\psi_{n+1}^{+}-f^{+}\qquad\text{and}\qquad P_{\gamma}\psi_{n}^{-}=\psi_{n+1}^{-}-f^{-}.

By monotone convergence for any
y_{0}\in\mathcal{Y}
holds

\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(P_{\gamma}\psi_{n}^{+}(y_{0})\right)=P_{\gamma}\left(\lim_{n\to\infty}\psi_{n}^{+}\right)(y_{0}).

By the same arguments a similar statement for
\psi_{n}^{-}
is deduced.
Clearly,
u_{\gamma}=\lim_{n\to\infty}(\psi_{n}^{+}-\psi_{n}^{-})
and hence by linearity

\displaystyle P_{\gamma}u_{\gamma}=P_{\gamma}\left(\lim_{n\to\infty}(\psi_{n}^{+}-\psi_{n}^{-})\right)=\lim_{n\to\infty}P_{\gamma}\psi_{n}^{+}-\lim_{n\to\infty}P_{\gamma}\psi_{n}^{-}

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\psi_{n+1}^{+}-\psi_{n+1}^{-}\right)-f=u_{\gamma}-f.

This shows ii) and therefore completes the proof.
∎

3.2
Limit theorems

This section contains our main results based on Assumption
3.1
together with either, a bounded eccentricity condition, or, the existence of a Lyapunov function.

3.2.1
Uniformly bounded eccentricity

The first two of our main results are for the setting where the eccentricities
E_{\gamma}
are uniformly bounded.

Assumption 3.4
.

There is some
K\in(0,\infty)
such that for any
\gamma\in\mathcal{I}
and
y\in\mathcal{Y}
holds

E_{\gamma}(y)\leq K.

Our first main result is for the case
\beta\geq 1
in the AIR scheme.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
f\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
be a function such that
h=h_{f}=f\cdot\mathds{1}_{\Phi}
is Lipschitz with constant
L
(w.r.t.
d
) and bounded.
Let Assumptions
3.1
and
3.4
be true.
Then, for any
\beta\geq 1
we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}(\log n)^{1/2+\eps}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}f(X_{j})-\nu(f)\right)=0,

almost surely for arbitrary
\eps>0
.

Before we turn to the proof we state our result for
\beta\in(0,1)
, where the rate gets worse as
\beta
approaches
0
.
This does not mean that small values of
\beta
or purely adaptive algorithms are worse, rather this phenomena is due to our proof technique, which collapses as
\beta\to 0
.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
f\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
be a function such that
h=h_{f}=f\cdot\mathds{1}_{\Phi}
is Lipschitz with constant
L
(w.r.t.
d
) and bounded.
Let Assumptions
3.1
and
3.4
be true.
Then, for any
\beta\in(0,1)
and
\eps>\frac{1}{1+\beta}-\frac{1}{2}
we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n^{1/2+\eps}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}f(X_{j})-\nu(f)\right)=0,

almost surely.

To prove Theorems
3.5
and
3.6
we start with two auxiliary results.

Lemma 3.7
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
3.5
(or Theorem
3.6
) we have that
(M_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a
(\mathcal{F}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
martingale and for its differences holds

|\Delta_{j}|\leq 2\frac{k_{0}LM^{k_{0}}K}{1-\tau},

almost surely, with
k_{0},L,M,\tau
and
K
as in Assumptions
3.1
and
3.4
.

Proof.

We know from Section
2
that
\mathbb{E}[M_{n}|\mathcal{F}_{n-1}]=M_{n-1}
almost surely.
Now we establish the bound for the differences which also implies
\mathbb{E}|M_{n}|<\infty
for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, showing that
M_{n}
is a martingale.

Thereto we observe that

\sup_{y\in\mathcal{Y}\,;\,\gamma\in\mathcal{I}}|u_{\gamma}(y)|\leq\frac{k_{0}LM^{k_{0}}K}{1-\tau},

where we used Proposition
3.3
as well as Assumption
3.4
.
With the same arguments we deduce that

\sup_{y\in\mathcal{Y}\,;\,\gamma\in\mathcal{I}}|P_{\gamma}u_{\gamma}(y)|\leq\frac{k_{0}LM^{k_{0}}K}{1-\tau},

and thus
|\Delta_{j}|\leq 2\frac{k_{0}LM^{k_{0}}K}{1-\tau}
almost surely.
∎

Next we bound the remainder term
R_{m}
.
Recall that the times of adaptation are
T_{\ell}=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}k_{j}
, where
k_{j}
are the “windows” between adaptation times
T_{0}:=0
.
Moreover, given
n\in\mathbb{N}
we assume that
m=m(n)
is such that
T_{m}\leq n+1<T_{m+1}
.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
h\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
be a function such that
f=f_{h}=h\cdot\mathds{1}_{\Phi}
is Lipschitz with constant
L
(w.r.t.
d
).
Let Assumptions
3.1
and
3.4
be true.
Then, for any
\beta>0
holds

|R_{m}|\leq n^{1/(1+\beta)}\cdot(2c_{\beta})^{1/(1+\beta)}\frac{k_{0}LM^{k_{0}}K}{1-\tau},

almost surely.

Proof.

By Proposition
3.3
and Assumption
3.4
we have

\sup_{y\in\mathcal{Y}\,;\,\gamma\in\mathcal{I}}|u_{\gamma}(y)|\leq\frac{k_{0}LM^{k_{0}}K}{1-\tau},

such that
|R_{m}|\leq m\cdot\frac{LM^{k_{0}}K}{1-\tau^{k_{0}}}
almost surely.
According to (
6
) and since
T_{m}\leq n+1
it follows

m\leq c_{\beta}^{1/(1+\beta)}T_{m}^{1/(1+\beta)}\leq c_{\beta}^{1/(1+\beta)}(n+1)^{1/(1+\beta)}\leq(2c_{\beta})^{1/(1+\beta)}n^{1/(1+\beta)},

which finishes the proof.
∎

Now we are ready to show our main results of this section.

Proof of Theorem
3.5
.

We set
r(n)=\sqrt{n}(\log n)^{1/2+\eps}
.
By Lemma
3.7

M_{n}
is a martingale with uniformly bounded increments.
Hence an application of Lemma
A.1
shows

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{r(n)}M_{n}=0

almost surely.

Since
\beta\geq 1
and by Lemma
3.8
we moreover deduce that

\frac{1}{r(n)}|R_{m}|\leq\frac{n^{1/(1+\beta)}}{r(n)}\widetilde{C}\leq\frac{n^{1/2}}{r(n)}\widetilde{C},

with
\widetilde{C}=(2c_{\beta})^{1/(1+\beta)}\frac{k_{0}LM^{k_{0}}K}{1-\tau}
.
Thus
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{r(n)}|R_{m}|=0
a.s.

Finally, by setting

g_{n}=h(Y_{n})-h(Y_{0})+{u}_{\Gamma_{0}}(Y_{0})-{u}_{\Gamma_{n}}(Y_{n})

we have a sequence of uniformly (in
n
) bounded random variables.
Thus
\frac{1}{r(n)}g_{n}\to 0
almost surely.

Now the result is a consequence of equations (
5
) and (
2.2
).
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.6
.

We set
r(n)=n^{1/2+\eps}
.
Note that our choice of
\eps
implies that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n^{1/(1+\beta)}}{r(n)}=0.

The proof is finished similar as in the proof of Theorem
3.5
.
∎

3.2.2
Lyapunov condition

In this section derive a limit result based on the existence of a Lyapunov function, which satisfies an inequality w.r.t.
d
.

Recall that a function
V\colon\mathcal{Y}\to[1,\infty)
is called a Lyapunov function (for some transition kernel
P
on
(\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{Y}})
) if there exist constants
\kappa\in(0,1)
and
b\geq 0
such that

PV(y)\leq\kappa V(y)+b.

Note that if
\pi
is the invariant distribution of
P
, then
\pi(V)\leq\frac{b}{1-\tau}
, see e.g.
[
Hai18
, Proposition 4.24]
.

Assumption 3.9
.

There exists a function
V\colon\mathcal{Y}\to[1,\infty)
and constants
\kappa\in(0,1)
and
b\in(0,\infty)
such that for any
\gamma\in\mathcal{I}
and
y\in\mathcal{Y}
holds

P_{\gamma}V(y)\leq\kappa V(y)+b.

If
V
is a Lyapunov function then
V^{q}
is also a Lyapunov function for any value of
q\in(0,1)
.
This is well known from the literature (see e.g.
[
MT12
, Lemma 15.2.9]
for the case
q=1/2
).
For the sake of completeness we included a proof in the appendix, cf. Lemma
A.2
.

The second assumption relates the function
V^{1/p}
to the distance-like function
d
, where
V
is from Assumption
3.9
and
p>2
.

Assumption 3.10
.

There are constants
p>2
and
K\in(0,\infty)
such that for the function
V
from Assumption
3.9
and any
y_{1},y_{2}\in\mathcal{Y}
we have

d(y_{1},y_{2})\leq K(V^{1/p}(y_{1})+V^{1/p}(y_{2})).

Theorem 3.11
.

Let
f\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
be a function such that
h=h_{f}=f\cdot\mathds{1}_{\Phi}
is Lipschitz with constant
L
(w.r.t.
d
) and
let Assumptions
3.1
,
3.9
and
3.10
hold true.
If
|h|\leq V^{1/p}
, then for any
\beta\in(0,\infty)
and
\eps>\max\left\{0,\frac{1}{1+\beta}+\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2}\right\}
we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n^{1/2+\eps}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}f(X_{j})-\nu(f)\right)=0,

almost surely.

We establish two auxiliary lemmas before proving the main result.

Lemma 3.12
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
3.11

(M_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a
(\mathcal{F}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
martingale and there exists some constant
C_{1}\in(0,\infty)
such that for any
j\in\mathbb{N}
holds

\mathbb{E}|\Delta_{j}|^{2}\leq C_{1}.

Proof.

From Section
2
we know
\mathbb{E}[M_{n}|\mathcal{F}_{n-1}]=M_{n-1}
almost surely.
Next we establish the claimed bound on the differences which implies
\mathbb{E}|M_{n}|<\infty
for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, showing that
(M_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a martingale.

For arbitrary
j,k\in\mathbb{N}
Proposition
3.3
, our assumptions about the Lyapunov functions
V
and
V^{1/p}
as well as the bound
\pi_{\gamma}(V)\leq\frac{b}{1-\kappa}
, valid for any
\gamma\in\mathcal{I}
(compare
[
Hai18
, Proposition 4.24]
), yield

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|u_{\Gamma_{j}}(Y_{k})|^{p}]

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{k_{0}LM^{k_{0}}}{1-\tau}\right)^{p}\mathbb{E}[E_{\Gamma_{j}}(Y_{k})^{p}]

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{k_{0}LM^{k_{0}}}{1-\tau}\right)^{p}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\mathcal{X}}d(Y_{k},y)^{p}\pi_{\Gamma_{j}}({\rm d}y)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{Kk_{0}LM^{k_{0}}}{1-\tau}\right)^{p}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\mathcal{X}}\left(V^{1/p}(Y_{k})+V^{1/p}(y)\right)^{p}\pi_{\Gamma_{j}}({\rm d}y)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{2Kk_{0}LM^{k_{0}}}{1-\tau}\right)^{p}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\mathcal{X}}V(Y_{k})+V(y)\pi_{\Gamma_{j}}({\rm d}y)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{2Kk_{0}LM^{k_{0}}}{1-\tau}\right)^{p}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[V(Y_{k})\right]+\frac{b}{1-\kappa}\right).

Applying the Lyapunov property multiple times we see

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[V(Y_{k})\right]=\mathbb{E}[P_{\Gamma_{k_{j}}}V(Y_{k-1})]\leq\kappa\mathbb{E}[V(Y_{k-1})+b]\leq\cdots

\displaystyle\leq\kappa^{k}\mathbb{E}[V(Y_{0})]+\frac{b}{1-\kappa}\leq\mathbb{E}[V(Y_{0})]+\frac{b}{1-\kappa}=V(y_{0})+\frac{b}{1-\kappa}.

Thus by an application of Jensen’s inequality we deduce that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[

\displaystyle|u_{\Gamma_{j}}(Y_{k})|^{2}]\leq\mathbb{E}[|u_{\Gamma_{j}}(Y_{k})|^{p}]^{2/p}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{2Kk_{0}LM^{k_{0}}}{1-\tau}\right)^{2}\left(V(y_{0})+\frac{2b}{1-\kappa}\right)^{2/p}<\infty.

Employing Proposition
3.3
ii) we deduce

|P_{\Gamma_{j}}u_{\Gamma_{j}}(Y_{k})|\leq|u_{\Gamma_{j}}(Y_{k})|+|V^{1/p}(Y_{k})|+\pi_{\Gamma_{j}}(|V^{1/p}|).

Using the same arguments as above, one obtains a bound for
|P_{\Gamma_{j}}u_{\Gamma_{j}}(Y_{k})|^{p}
from which the desired result follows.
∎

Next we bound the remainder term
R_{m}
in a suitable way.

Lemma 3.13
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
3.11
there exists some constant
C_{2}\in(0,\infty)
such that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
m=m(n)
holds

\mathbb{E}|R_{m}|^{p}\leq m^{p}\cdot C_{2}.

Proof.

From the proof of Lemma
3.12
we know

\mathbb{E}[|u_{\Gamma_{j}}(Y_{k})|^{p}]\leq\left(\frac{2Kk_{0}LM^{k_{0}}}{1-\tau}\right)^{p}\left(V(y_{0})+\frac{2b}{1-\kappa}\right),

for any
j,k\in\mathbb{N}
.
Using this we can bound each summand of
R_{m}
and the desired result follows.
∎

Now we turn to the proof of Theorem
3.11
.

Proof of Theorem
3.11
.

We show that all terms in the martingale decomposition (
2.2
) go to
0
after being normalised with
r(n)=n^{1/2+\eps}
.

We begin with the martingale part
M_{n}
which has differences uniformly bounded in
L^{2}
, see Lemma
3.12
.
Thus Lemma
A.1
yields

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{r(n)}M_{n}=0

almost surely.

To
R_{m}
: For any
\delta>0
we have by Lemma
3.13
and (
6
) that

\mathbb{P}\left[\left|R_{m}\right|>\delta r(n)\right]\leq\frac{m^{p}}{r(n)^{p}}C_{2}\delta^{-p}\leq\frac{n^{p/(1+\beta)}}{r(n)^{p}}C_{2}(2c_{\beta})^{1/(1+\beta)}\delta^{-p}.

By our assumptions on
\eps>0
follows

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{n^{p/(1+\beta)}}{r(n)^{p}}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{n^{p/(1+\beta)}}{n^{p(1/2+\eps)}}<\infty,

such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{r(n)}R_{m}=0
almost surely by the Borel-Cantelli lemma.

The remaining part that needs to be controlled is:

\frac{1}{r(n)}\left(h(Y_{n})-h(Y_{0})+{u}_{\Gamma_{0}}(Y_{0})-{u}_{\Gamma_{n}}(Y_{n})\right).

Using the estimate
\mathbb{E}|u_{\Gamma_{j}}(Y_{k})|^{p}\leq C
, which was shown in Lemma
3.12
with some finite constant
C
, it follows by Markov’s inequality and Borel-Cantelli lemma that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{r(n)}{u}_{\Gamma_{0}}(Y_{0})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{r(n)}{u}_{\Gamma_{n}}(Y_{n})=0

almost surely.
Finally using the fact that
|h|\leq V^{1/p}\leq V
and by multiple applications of the Lyapunov condition we deduce the existence of some
\widetilde{C}<\infty
such that
\mathbb{E}|h(Y_{k})|^{p}\leq\widetilde{C}
for any
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
Another Borel-Cantelli argument finishes the proof.
∎

4
Examples

In this section we apply our theoretical results to three different settings, depending on the ergodicity properties of the family
\{P_{\gamma}\}_{\gamma\in\mathcal{I}}
.
We start with the case where all kernels are uniformly ergodic,
then, we investigate an AIR scheme relying on geometric ergodic kernels.
Finally, we study algorithms where all the kernels satisfy conditions similar to those shown in
[
HMS11
]
.

4.1
Uniform Ergodicity

Here we use the trivial metric on
\mathcal{Y}
, i.e., we set
d(y_{1},y_{2})=\mathds{1}_{\{y_{1}\neq y_{2}\}}
.
The corresponding
1
-Wasserstein distance
\mathcal{W}
coincides with the total variation distance, that is,

\mathcal{W}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})=\left\|\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}\right\|_{tv},

for any two probability measures
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}
on
(\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{Y}})
.

To show our results we assume the following
simultaneous uniform ergodicity
condition.

Assumption 4.1
.

There exists some
\tau\in[0,1)
such that for any
\gamma\in\mathcal{I}
,
y\in\mathcal{Y}
and
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
holds

\left\|P_{\gamma}^{\ell}(y,\cdot)-\pi_{\gamma}\right\|_{tv}\leq\tau^{\ell}.

Remark 4.2
.

It is well known from (adaptive) MCMC literature that one usually requires compact state spaces to have uniform ergodicity.
However, this still includes different interesting examples, see e.g.
[
RR07
]
for a discussion.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
f\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
be bounded and Assumption
4.1
be true.
If
\beta\geq 1
, then for any
\eps>0
we have that almost surely

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}(\log n)^{1/2+\eps}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(f(X_{j})-\nu(f)\right)=0.

Proof.

Assumption
3.1
is true since total variation distance and
\mathcal{W}
coincide under the assumptions in this section.
Moreover, by definition of
E_{\gamma}(y)
we have

E_{\gamma}(y)=\int_{\mathcal{Y}}d(y,y^{\prime})\pi_{\gamma}({\rm d}y^{\prime})\leq\int_{\mathcal{Y}}1\;\pi_{\gamma}({\rm d}y^{\prime})=1,

for any
\gamma\in\mathcal{I}
.
Hence also Assumption
3.4
is true.
Since
f
is bounded it is also Lipschitz w.r.t.
d
, so all requirements to apply Theorem
3.5
are fulfilled.
∎

Corollary 4.4
.

Let
f\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
be bounded, Assumption
4.1
be true,
\beta\geq 1
and
\eps>0
.
Then, there exists some (measurable)
C\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
such that for almost all
\omega\in\Omega
and any
n\in\mathbb{N}
holds

\left|\frac{1}{{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(f(X_{j}(\omega))-\nu(f)\right)\right|\leq\frac{(\log n)^{1/2+\eps}}{\sqrt{n}}C(\omega)

We end this example by providing a result for
\beta\in(0,1)
.
The proof is along the lines of Theorem
4.3
and is therefore omitted.
Moreover, a similar statement as in Corollary
4.4
could be deduced.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
f\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
be bounded and Assumption
4.1
be true.
If
\beta\in(0,1)
, then for any
\eps>\max\left\{0,\frac{1}{1+\beta}-\frac{1}{2}\right\}
we have that almost surely

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n^{1/2+\eps}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(f(X_{j})-\nu(f)\right)=0.

4.2
Geometric ergodicity

Next we study the case where we have a (simultaneous) drift and minorisation condition.
Assumptions of this form are standard in adaptive MCMC theory, e.g.
[
AM06
,
RR07
,
FMP11
,
CŁR18
]
.
Recall that there is some metric
d_{\mathcal{Y}}
on
\mathcal{Y}
such that the space is Polish.
If we speak of (semi) continuity we always refer to that metric.

Assumption 4.6
.

There exists
C\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{Y}}
, a lower semi-continuous measurable function
V\colon\mathcal{Y}\to[1,\infty)
as well as numbers
\kappa\in(0,1)
,
\delta>0
,
b<\infty
such that
\sup_{C}V=v<\infty
and for any
\gamma\in\mathcal{I}
the following holds:

i)

P_{\gamma}V\leq\kappa V+b

ii)

There exists a probability measure
\eta_{\gamma}
on
C
such that for any
x\in C
we have
P_{\gamma}(x,\cdot)\geq\delta\eta_{\gamma}
.

With
V
from Assumption
4.6
let us introduce the following family of lower semi-continuous metrics

d_{q}(y_{1},y_{2})=\mathds{1}_{\{y_{1}\neq y_{2}\}}\left(V^{q}(y_{1})+V^{q}(y_{2})\right),

where
q\in(0,1]
.
The set of functions which are Lipschitz with constant
1
w.r.t.
d_{q}
is denoted by
\text{Lip}_{q,1}
and the
1
-Wasserstein distance corresponding to
d_{q}
by
\mathcal{W}_{q}
.

Theorem 4.7
.

Let Assumption
4.6
be true and
f\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
be such that
h=h_{f}=\mathds{1}_{\Phi}f
satisfies
|h|\leq V^{1/p}
, with
V
from Assumption
4.6
and some
p>2
.
Then, for any
\beta>0
and
\eps>\max\left\{0,\frac{1}{1+\beta}+\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2}\right\}
holds almost surely

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n^{1/2+\eps}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(f(X_{i})-\nu(f)\right)=0.

Proof.

Set
q=1/p\in(0,1]
.
The goal is to apply Theorem
3.11
for the metric
d_{q}
.
In the same fashion as
[
HM11
, Lemma 2.1]
one can prove

\mathcal{W}_{q}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})=\sup_{|\psi|\leq V^{q}}\left|\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\psi\;{\rm d}\mu_{1}-\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\psi\;{\rm d}\mu_{2}\right|,

where
V
is the function from Assumption
4.6
and
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}
are arbitrary probability measures on
(\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{Y}})
.
Furthermore, Assumption
4.6
implies simultaneous geometric ergodicity (note that also for
V^{q}
we have drift and minorisation): There exist
\alpha_{q}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\tau_{q}\in(0,1)
only depending on
\delta,\kappa,b,v,q
such that for any
\gamma\in\mathcal{I}
,
y\in\mathcal{Y}
holds

\sup_{|\psi|\leq V^{q}}\left|\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\psi(y^{\prime})P_{\gamma}^{\ell}(y,{\rm d}y^{\prime})-\pi_{\gamma}(\psi)\right|\leq\alpha_{q}V^{q}(y)\tau_{q}^{\ell},

for any
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
.
This follows from
[
Bax05
]
, see also
[
AM06
, Theorem 2]

[
RR07
, Section 7]
.

Hence (by an application of triangle inequality) for any
y_{1},y_{2}\in\mathcal{Y}
and any
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
holds

\mathcal{W}_{q}(P^{\ell}_{\gamma}(y_{1},\cdot),P^{\ell}_{\gamma}(y_{2},\cdot))\leq\alpha_{q}\tau_{q}^{\ell}(V^{q}(y_{1})+V^{q}(y_{2})).

This shows that Assumption
3.1
holds (for the metric
d_{q}
).
Moreover, Assumptions
3.9
and
3.10
(both w.r.t. the metric
d_{q}
) are immediate consequences of Assumption
4.6
.

Finally we note that since
|h|\leq V^{q}
it follows that
h
is Lipschitz (with constant
1
) w.r.t. the metric
d_{q}
.
Thus all requirements to apply Theorem
3.11
are met and the claimed result follows.
∎

Corollary 4.8
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
3.11
for any
\beta\in(0,\infty)
and
\eps>\max\left\{0,\frac{1}{1+\beta}+\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2}\right\}
there exists some (measurable)
C\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
such that for almost all
\omega\in\Omega
and any
n\in\mathbb{N}
holds

\left|\frac{1}{{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(f(X_{j}(\omega))-\nu(f)\right)\right|\leq\frac{1}{n^{1/2-\eps}}C(\omega).

Clearly, we want to have
\eps>0
as small as possible in Corollary

to obtain a good rate of convergence.
For fixed
\beta

\eps

This can be interpreted as follows: For large

4.3
Weak Harris ergodicity

In this section we investigate kernels which satisfy a contraction similar to the one which one obtains from the
weak Harris theorem
of
[
HMS11
]
.
For certain (classical) MCMC algorithms contraction results, similar to those required in this section, were shown in
[
HSV14
,
HJ23
]
.
In the adaptive setting, however, we are not aware of convergence results which are based on this kind of contraction assumptions.

Throughout the section we assume that
d\colon\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Y}\to[0,1]
is a distance-like function.

Assumption 4.9
.

There exists a lower semi-continuous function
V\colon\mathcal{Y}\to[1,\infty)
which is a Lyapunov function for all
P_{\gamma}
simultaneously.

Following
[
HMS11
]
we define the following distance-like function(s)

\widetilde{d}_{q}(y_{1},y_{2})=\sqrt{d(y_{1},y_{2})(1+V^{q}(y_{1})+V^{q}(y_{2}))},

where
q\in(0,1)
and
V
is from Assumption
4.9
.
The corresponding Wasserstein-like function is denoted by
\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}_{q}
and the contraction coefficient by
\widetilde{\tau}_{q}
.
The second assumption we need in this section is that one has a contraction for one of these distances.

Assumption 4.10
.

There exist numbers
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
,
\tau\in[0,1)
,
M\in(0,\infty)
and
p>2
such that for any
\gamma\in\mathcal{I}
holds

\widetilde{\tau}_{1/p}(P_{\gamma}^{k_{0}})\leq\tau\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\widetilde{\tau}_{1/p}(P_{\gamma})\leq M.

Remark 4.11
.

A contraction w.r.t.
\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}_{q}
can be checked by using the weak Harris theorem of
[
HMS11
]
.
It is worth noting that the mentioned result is suitable for infinite dimensional settings, see also
[
HSV14
,
HJ23
]
.

Theorem 4.12
.

Let Assumptions
4.9
and
4.10
be true and
f\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
be such that

is Lipschitz w.r.t.
\widetilde{d}_{1/p}
and
|h|\leq V^{1/p}
, where
V,p
are as in Assumption
4.10
.
Then, for any
\beta>0
and
\eps>\max\left\{0,\frac{1}{1+\beta}+\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2}\right\}
holds almost surely

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n^{1/2+\eps}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(f(X_{i})-\nu(f)\right)=0.

Proof.

Clearly, Assumptions
3.1
and
3.9
are true.
Moreover, by definition of
\widetilde{d}_{1/p}
also Assumption
3.10
(w.r.t.
\widetilde{d}_{1/p}
) holds.

Since
h
is Lipschitz and bounded by

all requirements to apply Theorem
3.11
are met and the result follows.
∎

Clearly, also a result similar to Corollary
