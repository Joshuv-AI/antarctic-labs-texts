Computing the Gromov–Hausdorff distance using gradient methods
Vladyslav Oles

Abstract
The Gromov–Hausdorff distance measures the difference in shape between metric spaces and poses a notoriously difficult problem in combinatorial optimization. We introduce its quadratic relaxation over a convex polytope whose solutions provably deliver the Gromov–Hausdorff distance. The optimality guarantee is enabled by the fact that the search space of our approach is not constrained to a generalization of bijections, unlike in other relaxations such as the Gromov–Wasserstein distance. We suggest conditional gradient descent for solving the relaxation in O ⁡ ( n 3 ) O(n^{3}) -time per iteration, and demonstrate its performance on metric spaces of hundreds of points. In particular, we use it to obtain a new bound of the Gromov–Hausdorff distance between the unit circle and the unit hemisphere equipped with Euclidean metric. Our approach is implemented as a Python package dgh .

Introduction

The Gromov–Hausdroff distance
[
GKPS99
]
measures the difference in shape between geometric objects, and is a natural metric on the space of (isometry classes of) compact metric spaces. It is widely used in Riemannian geometry and appears in other areas of modern mathematics such as computational topology and graph theory (see e.g.
[
GKPS99
,
Lat01
,
CCSG+09
,
Tuz20
]
). Starting from the late 2000s, it has become increasingly popular in data science as a model for dissimilarity measures between shapes such as point clouds
[
Mém07
,
CCSG+09
,
VBBW16
,
BBK+10
]
and graphs
[
LKC+12
,
CLS+17
,
FVFM18
]
).

Formally, the Gromov–Hausdorff distance between a pair of compact metric spaces
X,Y
minimizes the distortion of distances between the points of
X
and
Y
and their images under some bi-directional mapping pair
(f,g)\in(X\to Y)\times(Y\to X)
. Solving this combinatorial minimization is an NP-hard problem, and in fact even approximating it up to a multiplicative factor of
\sqrt{n}
(where
n\defeq\max\{|X|,|Y|\}
) in the general case remains an intractable task
[
Sch17
]
. High computational cost of the distance has motivated its relaxations such as the modified Gromov–Hausdorff distance
[
Mém12
]
, the Gromov–Wasserstein distance (which became a broadly used metric between metric measure spaces in its own right)
[
Mém11
]
, and GHMatch
[
VBBW16
]
. Other studies have proposed more feasible algorithms for approximating the Gromov–Hausdorff distance for the special cases of subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{1}

[
MVW23
]
, metric trees
[
AFN+18
,
TW18
]
, and ultrametric spaces
[
MSW21
]
.

An example with
|X|=|Y|=4
for which the mapping pairs minimizing the distortion are not bijective. The dotted lines represent one of such mapping pairs. The distances in
X
and
Y
take only two distinct values, whose ratio exceeds 2.

While the Gromov–Wasserstein distance and GHMatch are similar to our proposed approach in leveraging gradient methods to obtain approximate solutions, both of these relaxations are constrained to some generalization of bijections between
X
and
Y
.
Assuming
|X|=|Y|
(and, for the Gromov–Wasserstein distance, equal measure assigned to each point), they can retrieve only those solutions
(f,g)
to the original minimization that satisfy
f=g^{-1}
. However, the existence of such solutions is not guaranteed, as is illustrated by the example from Figure
1
. In particular, the search space of the Gromov–Wasserstein distance between the depicted
X
and
Y
under the uniform probability measure does not contain any of the original solutions. Moreover, the example demonstrates that the minimum distortion over all mapping pairs (which is the “short” distance) can be arbitrarily smaller than the minimum distortion over bijections (which is the difference between the “long” and the “short” distances).
This is perhaps unsurprising considering that
(X\to Y)\times(Y\to X)
grows super-exponentially faster than the set of bijections between
X
and
Y
. From a practical standpoint, such an expressivity of the Gromov–Hausdorff distance can be useful for aligning metric spaces of irregular density, e.g. those obtained by uneven sampling from the underlying objects.

In the below Section
2
, we propose a parametrized relaxation of the Gromov–Hausdorff distance over the same search space
(X\to Y)\times(Y\to X)
. We derive a threshold value for the parameter ensuring that any solution to the relaxed problem minimizes the distortion and therefore delivers the Gromov–Hausdorff distance. Section
3
describes solving the relaxation using conditional gradient descent and discusses the associated computational complexity and optimization landscape. We detail on our implementation and demonstrate its performance in numerical experiments in Section
4
. As a byproduct, we tighten the upper bound on the Gromov–Hausdorff distance between a unit circle
S^{1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
and a unit hemisphere
H^{2}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
(Section
4.2
). For brevity, technical proofs are relegated to the appendix.

Relaxing the Gromov–Hausdorff distance

2.1
Matrix reformulation

Let
X=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
and
Y=\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{m}\}
be metric spaces whose finite cardinalities satisfy
n\geq m>1
, and let
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and
\mathbf{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
denote their distance matrices, e.g.
X_{ij}=d_{X}(x_{i},x_{j})
. Recall that the Gromov–Hausdorff distance can be formulated as a combinatorial minimization over the bi-directional mapping pairs
(X\to Y)\times(Y\to X)

d_{\mathrm{GH}}(X,Y)=\frac{1}{2}\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}f:X\to Y,\\
g:Y\to X\end{subarray}}\dis\left(\left\{\left(x,f(x)\right):x\in X\right\}\cup\left\{\left(g(y),y\right):y\in Y\right\}\right),\quad\cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{kalton1999distances}{}{}]}

where the distortion of some relation
R\subseteq X\times Y
is defined as the largest absolute difference in distances it incurs:

\dis R\defeq\max_{\vbox{\Let@\restore@math@cr\default@tag\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptstyle#$&$\m@th\scriptstyle{}#$\hfil\cr(x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in R\crcr}}}\left|d_{X}(x,x^{\prime})-d_{Y}(y,y^{\prime})\right|.

For some
f:X\to Y
, consider its “one-hot encoded” matrix representation
\mathbf{F}\in\{0,1\}^{n\times m}
s.t.
F_{ij}=\begin{cases}1,&f(x_{i})=y_{j}\\
0,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}
and note that
\left(\mathbf{F}\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{F}^{T}\right)_{ij}=d_{Y}\left(f(x_{i}),f(x_{j})\right)
. It follows that

\max_{x,x^{\prime}\in X}\Big|d_{X}(x,x^{\prime})-d_{Y}\left(f(x),f(x^{\prime})\right)\Big|=\big\|\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{F}\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{F}^{T}\big\|_{\infty}.

From the analogous construction of
\mathbf{G}\in\{0,1\}^{m\times n}
for an arbitrary
g:Y\to X
,

\max_{y,y^{\prime}\in Y}\left|d_{X}\left(g(y),g(y^{\prime})\right)-d_{Y}(y,y^{\prime})\right|=\big\|\mathbf{Y}-\mathbf{G}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{G}^{T}\big\|_{\infty}.

Because
(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{G}^{T})_{ij}=d_{X}\left(g(y_{j}),x_{i}\right)
and
(\mathbf{F}\mathbf{Y})_{ij}=d_{Y}\left(f(x_{i}),y_{j}\right)
,

\max_{x\in X,y\in Y}\left|d_{X}\left(x,g(y)\right)-d_{Y}\left(f(x),y\right)\right|=\big\|\mathbf{X}\mathbf{G}^{T}-\mathbf{F}\mathbf{Y}\big\|_{\infty}.

Denote
R=R(f,g)\defeq\left\{\left(x,f(x)\right):x\in X\right\}\cup\left\{\left(g(y),y\right):y\in Y\right\}
. Any pair
(x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in R
satisfies

\displaystyle\left|d_{X}(x,x^{\prime})-d_{Y}(y,y^{\prime})\right|\in\bigg\{

\displaystyle\left|d_{X}(x,x^{\prime})-d_{Y}\left(f(x),f(x^{\prime})\right)\right|,

\displaystyle\left|d_{X}\left(g(y),g(y^{\prime})\right)-d_{Y}(y,y^{\prime})\right|,

\displaystyle\left|d_{X}\left(x,g(y)\right)-d_{Y}\left(f(x),y\right)\right|,

\displaystyle\left|d_{X}\left(x^{\prime},g(y^{\prime})\right)-d_{Y}\left(f(x^{\prime}),y^{\prime}\right)\right|\bigg\},

and therefore

\displaystyle\dis R

\displaystyle=\max\bigg\{\begin{aligned} &\max_{x,x^{\prime}\in X}\left|d_{X}(x,x^{\prime})-d_{Y}\left(f(x),f(x^{\prime})\right)\right|,\\
&\max_{y,y^{\prime}\in Y}\left|d_{X}\left(g(y),g(y^{\prime})\right)-d_{Y}(y,y^{\prime})\right|,\\
&\max_{x\in X,y\in Y}\left|d_{X}\left(x,g(y)\right)-d_{Y}\left(f(x),y\right)\right|\bigg\}\end{aligned}

\displaystyle=\left\|\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{F}\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{F}^{T}&\mathbf{X}\mathbf{G}^{T}-\mathbf{F}\mathbf{Y}\\
\mathbf{G}\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{F}^{T}&\mathbf{Y}-\mathbf{G}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{G}^{T}\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\big\|\mathbf{V}-\mathbf{R}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{R}^{T}+\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}-\mathbf{W}^{T}\mathbf{R}^{T}\big\|_{\infty},

where
\mathbf{R}\defeq\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{F}&\\
&\mathbf{G}\end{bmatrix}
is the matrix representation of
R
and
\mathbf{V}\defeq\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{X}&\\
&\mathbf{Y}\end{bmatrix}
,
\mathbf{U}\defeq\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{Y}&\\
&\mathbf{X}\end{bmatrix}
,
\mathbf{W}\defeq\begin{bmatrix}&\mathbf{Y}\\
\mathbf{X}&\end{bmatrix}
. The presence of a redundant block
\mathbf{G}\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{F}^{T}
in the distance difference matrix
\mathbf{V}-\mathbf{R}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{R}^{T}+\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}-\mathbf{W}^{T}\mathbf{R}^{T}
is motivated by matrix symmetry.

By construction,
\mathbf{R}\in\{0,1\}^{(n+m)\times(n+m)}
is row-stochastic and has
m\times m
and
n\times n
blocks of zeros in the upper right and lower left, respectively. Let
\mathcal{R}\subset\{0,1\}^{(n+m)\times(n+m)}
denote the set of all such matrices, which is in a 1-to-1 correspondence with the bi-directional mapping pairs
(X\to Y)\times(Y\to X)
. We will write
\dis\mathbf{R}\defeq\left\|\mathbf{V}-\mathbf{R}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{R}^{T}+\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}-\mathbf{W}^{T}\mathbf{R}^{T}\right\|_{\infty}
to denote the matrix-based formulation of distortion, and assume that the distinction between
\dis:\mathcal{R}\to\mathbb{R}
and
\dis:\mathscr{P}(X\times Y)\to\mathbb{R}
is clear from the context. Then an equivalent formulation of the Gromov–Hausdorff distance can be given as

d_{\mathrm{GH}}(X,Y)=\frac{1}{2}\min_{\mathbf{R}\in\mathcal{R}}\dis\mathbf{R}.

Solving the relaxation

3.1
Conditional gradient descent

(

⋆
⁣
⋆

⋆

‣
2.3
) is an indefinite quadratic minimization with affine constraints and a Lipschitz gradient

\bm{\nabla}\sigma_{c}(\mathbf{S})=2\left(c^{\mathbf{V}}\mathbf{S}c^{-\mathbf{U}}+c^{-\mathbf{V}}\mathbf{S}c^{\mathbf{U}}+\big(c^{\mathbf{W}}\mathbf{S}c^{-\mathbf{W}}+c^{-\mathbf{W}}\mathbf{S}c^{\mathbf{W}}\big)^{T}\right).

While finding its global minimum remains an NP-hard problem, approximate solutions can be efficiently obtained by the Frank–Wolfe algorithm
[
FW56
]
, also known as conditional gradient descent.

The iterative algorithm starts at some
\mathbf{S}_{0}\in\mathcal{S}
. At every iteration, it finds the descent direction as a point in
\mathcal{S}
that minimizes the cosine similarity with the gradient at the current point
\mathbf{S}_{i}
,

\mathbf{R}_{i}\in\argmin_{\mathbf{S}\in\mathcal{S}}\left\langle\mathbf{S},\bm{\nabla}\sigma_{c}(\mathbf{S}_{i})\right\rangle.

The descent direction
\mathbf{R}_{i}
chosen by the algorithm is always a vertex of
\mathcal{S}
(note that the minimum must be attained at some vertex due to the linearity of the problem).
The algorithm then finds a point on the line segment
\overline{\mathbf{S}_{i}\mathbf{R}_{i}}
that minimizes
\sigma_{c}
,

\mathbf{S}_{i+1}\in\argmin_{\gamma\in[0,1]}\sigma_{c}\left(\gamma\mathbf{S}_{i}+(1-\gamma)\mathbf{R}_{i}\right),

which concludes the
i
-th iteration.

The algorithm’s convergence is measured as the
Frank–Wolfe gap

\left\langle\mathbf{S}_{i}-\mathbf{R}_{i},\bm{\nabla}\sigma_{c}(\mathbf{S}_{i})\right\rangle\geq 0
, which is zero if and only if
\mathbf{S}_{i}
is a stationary point.
The algorithm terminates when the Frank–Wolfe gap becomes sufficiently small (or after reaching the iteration limit). It takes the Frank–Wolfe algorithm
O(\epsilon^{-2})
iterations to approach a stationary point with the gap of
\epsilon

[
LJ16
]
, each iteration requiring
O(n^{3})
time.

3.3
Non-convexity and the choice of
c

The Hessian of
\sigma_{c}
is a constant
(n+m)^{2}\times(n+m)^{2}
matrix

\mathbf{H}\defeq\bm{\nabla}^{2}\sigma_{c}(\cdot)=2\left(c^{\mathbf{V}}\otimes c^{-\mathbf{U}}+c^{-\mathbf{V}}\otimes c^{\mathbf{U}}+\left(c^{\mathbf{W}}\otimes c^{-\mathbf{W}^{T}}+c^{-\mathbf{W}}\otimes c^{\mathbf{W}^{T}}\right)\mathbf{K}\right),

where
\otimes
is the Kronecker product and
\mathbf{K}\in\{0,1\}^{(n+m)^{2}\times(n+m)^{2}}
denotes the commutation matrix. The standard approach to navigating the non-convexity of
\sigma_{c}
is to consider the space spanned by the eigenvectors corresponding to negative eigenvalues of
\mathbf{H}
. However, doing so has
O(n^{6})
-time and
O(n^{4})
-memory complexity and is impractical for most cases. As a cheaper substitute, we assess the extent of non-convexity of
\sigma_{c}
using the normalized nuclear norm-induced distance from
\mathbf{H}
to the set of positive semidefinite matrices
\mathcal{M}^{+}
:

\mathrm{nconv}(\sigma_{c})\defeq\inf_{\mathbf{M}\in\mathcal{M}^{+}}\frac{\|\mathbf{H}-\mathbf{M}\|_{*}}{\|\mathbf{H}\|_{*}}=\frac{\lambda^{-}}{\lambda^{+}+\lambda^{-}}\in[0,1],

where
\|\cdot\|_{*}
denotes the nuclear norm and
\lambda^{-}
and
\lambda^{+}
are the total magnitudes of respectively negative and positive eigenvalues of
\mathbf{H}

[
DMZ19
]
. In particular, this distance is equal to 0 (or 1) if and only if the function is convex (or concave).

Note that
\lambda^{+}-\lambda^{-}=\tr\mathbf{H}=8(n+m)^{2}
due to the zero main diagonals of
\mathbf{V}
and
\mathbf{U}
and

\displaystyle\tr\left(\left(c^{\mathbf{W}}\otimes c^{-\mathbf{W}^{T}}\right)\mathbf{K}\right)=\left\langle\mathbf{K},c^{\mathbf{W}}\otimes c^{-\mathbf{W}^{T}}\right\rangle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n+m}c^{W_{ij}}c^{(-\mathbf{W}^{T})_{ij}}=(n+m)^{2}=\tr\left(\left(c^{-\mathbf{W}}\otimes c^{\mathbf{W}^{T}}\right)\mathbf{K}\right),

and therefore

\mathrm{nconv}(\sigma_{c})=\frac{\lambda^{+}-8(n+m)^{2}}{2\lambda^{+}-8(n+m)^{2}}\in[0,\frac{1}{2}).

Let
\lambda_{\max}>0
denote the dominant eigenvalue of
\mathbf{H}
as per the Perron–Frobenius theorem. In the degenerate case of
c=1
this is the only non-zero eigenvalue of the rank-1 Hessian
\mathbf{H}=8\left(\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{T}\right)
, which makes
\sigma_{c}
convex (though uninformative). On the other hand, the Courant–Fischer theorem yields

\lambda_{\max}\geq\frac{\mathbf{1}^{T}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{1}}{\mathbf{1}^{T}\mathbf{1}}=\frac{\|\mathbf{H}\|_{1}}{(n+m)^{2}}\xrightarrow[c\to\infty]{}\infty,

and

\lambda^{+}\geq\lambda_{\max}

then entails

\mathrm{nconv}(\sigma_{c})\xrightarrow[c\to\infty]{}\frac{1}{2}.

We conclude that the landscape of (

⋆
⁣
⋆

⋆

‣
2.3
) starts flat at
c=1
and becomes increasingly non-convex as
c
grows. In particular, the following result provides a tractable (
O(n^{2})
-time and -memory) bound on its non-convexity based on the value of
c
.

Theorem 4
.

Let
\alpha\in\left[0,\frac{1}{2}\right)
and
c\geq 1
satisfy

\frac{2\alpha}{\frac{1}{2}-\alpha}=\frac{\sqrt{16(n+m)^{4}+\left(c^{d_{\max}}+c^{-d_{\max}}-2\right)p_{\max}-\frac{16}{(n+m)^{4}}\left\|c^{\mathbf{W}}\right\|_{1}^{2}\left\|c^{-\mathbf{W}}\right\|_{1}^{2}}}{n+m},

where
d_{\max}\defeq\max\{\diam X,\diam Y\}
and
p_{\max}\defeq\frac{\left(2\sqrt{2}+4\right)(n+m)^{2}\sqrt{(n+m)^{2}\|\mathbf{W}\|_{2}^{2}-\|\mathbf{W}\|_{1}^{2}}}{d_{\max}}+6
. Then
\mathrm{nconv}(\sigma_{c})\leq\alpha
.

The choice of
c
is therefore a balancing act between increasing the fraction of solutions (i.e. global minima) delivering the Gromov–Hausdorff distance and decreasing the probability of discovering a solution by the Frank–Wolfe algorithm. As
c\to\infty
, the fraction stops growing after attaining 1 at some
c=c^{*}
, while the chances of discovering a solution continue to decay due to the increasingly non-convex optimization landscape. Because Theorem
1
implies
c^{*}\leq\left(\frac{(n+m)^{2}-n-m}{2}\right)^{1/\rho}
, it is never optimal to choose
c
above this bound.

4
Numerical experiments

We demonstrate the method’s performance using our implementation from a Python package
dgh
.
Given a choice of
c
and a budget of Frank–Wolfe iterations, it starts from a random
\mathbf{S}_{0}\in\mathcal{S}
and iterates until the Frank–Wolfe gap is at most

(or there are no iterations left), after which the last
\mathbf{S}_{i}
is projected to the nearest
\mathbf{R}\in\mathcal{R}

In a bid to save on redundant computations,

also compares every new best
\frac{1}{2}\dis\mathbf{R}
against the trivial lower bound

and terminates whenever they match, which means that the Gromov–Hausdorff distance was found exactly. Prior to the computations,
dgh
normalizes all distances in the input metric spaces so that
\max\{\diam X,\diam Y\}=1
to avoid floating-point arithmetic overflow, and scales the resulting
\dis\mathbf{R}
back afterwards.

Let

denote the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{H}
. We already established using the Courant-Fischer theorem that

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\|\mathbf{H}\|_{1}}{(n+m)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{(n+m)^{2}}(\big\|c^{\mathbf{U}}\big\|_{1}\big\|c^{-\mathbf{V}}\big\|_{1}+\big\|c^{-\mathbf{U}}\big\|_{1}\big\|c^{\mathbf{V}}\big\|_{1}+\big\|c^{\mathbf{W}}\big\|_{1}\big\|c^{-\mathbf{W}^{T}}\big\|_{1}+\big\|c^{-\mathbf{W}}\big\|_{1}\big\|c^{\mathbf{W}^{T}}\big\|_{1})

\displaystyle=\frac{8}{(n+m)^{2}}\big\|c^{\mathbf{W}}\big\|_{1}\big\|c^{-\mathbf{W}}\big\|_{1}.

To bound
\lambda_{-}\defeq\sum_{\lambda_{i}<0}|\lambda_{i}|
from above, we will first introduce two new notations for convenience. For any two matrices
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
, let
\mathbf{A}\ominus\mathbf{B}
denote a matrix operation analogous to the Kronecker product but with subtraction in place of multiplication. In addition, define
\mathrm{ch}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
as
\mathrm{ch}(a)\defeq c^{a}+c^{-a}
and its entrywise counterpart for matrices
\mathrm{ch}:\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}\to\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}
, so that we are able to compactly write

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{H}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{ch}(\mathbf{U}\ominus\mathbf{V})+\mathrm{ch}(\mathbf{W}\ominus\mathbf{W}^{T})\mathbf{K}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{ch}(\mathbf{U}\ominus\mathbf{V})+\mathrm{ch}\big((\mathbf{W}\ominus\mathbf{W}^{T})\mathbf{K}\big).

Note that
\big\langle\mathrm{ch}(\mathbf{A}),\mathrm{ch}(\mathbf{B})\big\rangle=\big\|\mathrm{ch}(\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{B})\big\|_{1}+\big\|\mathrm{ch}(\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{B})\big\|_{1}
. Furthermore, if
\|\mathbf{A}\|_{1}
and
\|\mathbf{A}\|_{\infty}
are fixed,
\big\|\mathrm{ch}(\mathbf{A})\big\|_{1}
is maximized by the highest possible count of entries of
\mathbf{A}
equal to
\big\|\mathbf{A}\big\|_{\infty}
due to the superadditivity of
\mathrm{ch}
(see also a proof based on Lagrange multipliers under “
upper bound on sum of exponential functions
” on Mathematics Stack Exchange). As a consequence, for
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}

\displaystyle\big\|\mathrm{ch}(\mathbf{A})\big\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lceil\frac{\|\mathbf{A}\|_{1}}{\|\mathbf{A}\|_{\infty}}\right\rceil\mathrm{ch}\big(\|\mathbf{A}\|_{\infty}\big)+\left\lfloor pq-\frac{\|\mathbf{A}\|_{1}}{\|\mathbf{A}\|_{\infty}}\right\rfloor\mathrm{ch}(0)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{\|\mathbf{A}\|_{1}}{\|\mathbf{A}\|_{\infty}}+1\right)\left(\mathrm{ch}\big(\|\mathbf{A}\|_{\infty}\big)-2\right)+2pq

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sqrt{pq}\frac{\|\mathbf{A}\|_{2}}{\|\mathbf{A}\|_{\infty}}+1\right)\left(\mathrm{ch}\big(\|\mathbf{A}\|_{\infty}\big)-2\right)+2pq.

The looser bound in terms of the Frobenius norm allows for its tractable computation when
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{B}\ominus\mathbf{C}
for some
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times q},\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times s}
with non-negative entries, as then

\|\mathbf{A}\|_{2}^{2}=\|\mathbf{B}\ominus\mathbf{C}\|_{2}^{2}=pq\|\mathbf{B}\|_{2}^{2}+rs\|\mathbf{C}\|_{2}^{2}-2\|\mathbf{B}\|_{1}\|\mathbf{C}\|_{1}.

\displaystyle\big\|\mathbf{U}\ominus\mathbf{V}\pm(\mathbf{W}\ominus\mathbf{W}^{T})\mathbf{K}\big\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\|\mathbf{U}\ominus\mathbf{V}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\mathbf{W}\ominus\mathbf{W}^{T}\|_{2}^{2}\pm 2\big\langle(\mathbf{W}\ominus\mathbf{W}^{T})\mathbf{K},\mathbf{U}\ominus\mathbf{V}\big\rangle}
