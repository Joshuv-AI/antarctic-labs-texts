Mode analysis for the linearized Einstein equations on the Kerr metric : the large 𝔞 \mathfrak{a} case.
Lars Andersson, Dietrich Häfner, Bernard F. Whiting

Abstract
. We give a complete analysis of mode solutions for the linearized Einstein equations and the 1 − 1- form wave operator on the Kerr metric in the large 𝔞 \mathfrak{a} case. By mode solutions we mean solutions of the form e − i ​ t ∗ ​ σ ​ h ~ ​ ( r , θ , φ ) e^{-it_{*}\sigma}\tilde{h}(r,\theta,\varphi) where t ∗ t_{*} is a suitable time variable. The corresponding Fourier transformed 1 − 1- form wave operator and linearized Einstein operator are shown to be Fredholm between suitable function spaces and h ~ \tilde{h} has to lie in the domain of these operators. These spaces are constructed following the general framework of Vasy [ Va1 ] , [ Va3 ] - [ Va4 ] along the lines of [ HHV ] . No mode solutions exist for ℑ ⁡ σ ≥ 0 , σ ≠ 0 {\Im}\,\sigma\geq 0,\,\sigma\neq 0 . For σ = 0 \sigma=0 mode solutions are Coulomb solutions for the 1 − 1- form wave operator and linearized Kerr solutions plus pure gauge terms in the case of the linearized Einstein equations. If we fix a De Turck/wave map gauge, then the zero mode solutions for the linearized Einstein equations lie in a fixed 7 − 7- dimensional space. The proof relies on the absence of modes for the Teukolsky equation shown by the third author in [ Wh ] and a complete classification of the gauge invariants of linearized gravity on the Kerr spacetime, see [ AB1 , AABKW ] .

We now give an informal statement of our main theorem. Recall that the Kerr black hole is a 2
-

Theorem 1.1

(Mode stability for linearized gravity)
.

Consider the gauge fixed linearized Einstein equation on a subextreme Kerr spacetime. Let
\Im\sigma\geq 0
, and let
\dot{g}_{ab}
be a mode solution of the gauge fixed linearized Einstein equation with frequency
\sigma
. Then
\dot{g}_{ab}
is, modulo gauge, a perturbation of the Kerr metric with respect to the Kerr parameters.

7

In contrast to the Fredholm analysis, the proof of mode stability requires the precise form of the equations and in most cases uses separation of variables and a delicate analysis of the separated equations. Mode stability can fail in prominent examples such as the Klein-Gordon equation on the Kerr spacetime
[
SR
]
or the charged Klein-Gordon field on the De Sitter-Reissner-Nordström metric
[
BH
]

The general strategy for the mode analysis is to first analyse the modes for the linearized Einstein equations without gauge fixing. However to establish linear stability, it will be important to understand the mode solutions for the gauge fixed Einstein equations. In the wave map/de Turck gauge, the
1-
form wave operator plays the role of a gauge propagation operator; this explains why the mode analysis for this operator is important in this context. It should however be pointed out that already in the small
\mathfrak{a}
case, quadratically growing generalized zero modes appear in the usual wave map/de Turck gauge. At least in the small
\mathfrak{a}
case, these modes can be eliminated by constraint damping via a perturbation argument
[
HHV
]
.

Summarizing the complete analysis of mode solutions divides into the following points:

(1)

An analysis of mode solutions of the linearized Einstein operator.

(2)

An analysis of mode solutions of the
1-
form wave operator.

(3)

Implementation of constraint damping.

(4)

Non-degenerate control of generalized zero energy states.

We address points
(1)
and
(2)
in this paper,
(3),\,(4)
are postponed to future work.

Mode analysis for perturbations of the Kerr metric has a long history and as already mentioned can be linked to the mode analysis of the Teukolsky equation. The central breakthrough in this context was the paper by Whiting in 1989 showing absence of modes with positive imaginary part for the Teukolsky equation for all
\mathfrak{a}
subextreme
[
Wh
]
. We also refer to
[
CTC
]
for a new proof of this result. Later, Andersson, Ma, Paganini and Whiting
[
AMPW
]
showed the absence of modes also on the real axis for non zero spectral parameter. In the present paper we establish the absence of suitably defined zero frequency modes.
We also refer to
[
CTC
]
,
[
Hi2
]
for partial mode stability results in the De Sitter Kerr case.

The
1-
form wave equation in Kerr is not separable, but divergence free solutions of this equation give rise to solutions of the Maxwell equations. When considering solutions of the Maxwell equations or the linearized Einstein equations, one can compute the so called Teukolsky scalars which are solutions of the Teukolsky equation. Whereas this equation doesn’t have any mode solutions, this is true neither for the linearized Einstein equation nor for the
1-
form wave equation. Indeed the linearized Einstein equation will have zero modes consisting of a linearized Kerr metric plus a pure gauge solution, and the
1-
form wave equation will have solutions which correspond to Coulomb solutions for the Maxwell field. However the vanishing of the Teukolsky scalars will be the central information to show that the only mode solutions encountered are the expected ones. Whereas one obtains the mode solutions for the
1-
form wave equation more or less by direct integration, the situation is more complicated for the Einstein case.

In the Einstein case, the gauge invariants of linearized gravity on the Kerr spacetime will play a crucial role in our proof. They have been completely classified in
[
AB1
,
AABKW
]
. It is a remarkable fact that these gauge invariants have, for vanishing extreme Teukolsky scalars, exactly the form that they have for the Plebanski-Demianski family of line elements, parametrized by
\mathfrak{m},\,\mathfrak{a},\,\mathfrak{c}
and
\mathfrak{n}
, see
[
AB3
]
. The set of gauge invariants is complete, see
[
AABKW
]
. It follows that locally the perturbation to consider is up to gauge a linearized Plebanski-Demianski line element. We make this argument global by using the setting of
[
AABKW
]
and the Poincaré lemma. We then have to show that the nut parameter perturbation
\dot{\mathfrak{n}}
and the acceleration parameter perturbation
\dot{\mathfrak{c}}
are zero to argue that the solution is a linearized Kerr solution plus a pure gauge term. For a solution that decays like
\mathcal{O}(r^{-1})
at infinity, it follows directly from decay considerations for the gauge invariants that
\dot{\mathfrak{n}}
and
\dot{\mathfrak{c}}
are zero. However, this fall-off is more than what we want to impose, a priori, in our functional setting. The remedy is a normal operator argument that gives a certain polyhomogeneous expansion of our mode solution. This argument however can’t be applied directly to the linearized Einstein equation but only for the gauged fixed one. We therefore first correct the solution of the linearized Einstein equation without gauge fixing to a solution of the gauge fixed linearized Einstein equation by adding a linearized Kerr metric plus gauge term.

3.
Geometric background

In this section we introduce the Kerr family of metrics and some of the geometrical structures we will use in later sections.

3.1.
The Kerr family

We shall consider the Kerr family of solutions to the vacuum Einstein equation, parametrized by
b=(\mathfrak{m},\mathfrak{a})\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}
, denoting mass and angular momentum per unit mass, respectively. We restrict to the subextreme case
|\mathfrak{a}|<\mathfrak{m}
. Let

r_{\pm}:=\mathfrak{m}\pm\sqrt{\mathfrak{m}^{2}-\mathfrak{a}^{2}}.

For given
b=(\mathfrak{m},\mathfrak{a})
, we put
b_{0}=(\mathfrak{m},0)
.
Let
(\theta,\phi)\in[0,\pi]\times[0,2\pi]
be coordinates on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
. The Kerr metric in Boyer-Lindquist coordinates
(t,r,\theta,\varphi)\in\mathbb{R}\times(r_{+},\infty)\times\mathbb{S}^{2}
is

\displaystyle g_{b}^{\rm BL}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta_{b}}{\varrho_{b}^{2}}(dt-\mathfrak{a}\sin^{2}\theta\,d\varphi)^{2}-\varrho_{b}^{2}\Bigl(\frac{dr^{2}}{\Delta_{b}}+d\theta^{2}\Bigr)-\frac{\sin^{2}\theta}{\varrho_{b}^{2}}\bigl(\mathfrak{a}\,dt-(r^{2}+\mathfrak{a}^{2})d\varphi\bigr)^{2},

(3.1a)

with inverse

\displaystyle G_{b}^{\rm BL}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Delta_{b}\varrho_{b}^{2}}\bigl((r^{2}+\mathfrak{a}^{2})\partial_{t}+\mathfrak{a}\partial_{\varphi}\bigr)^{2}-\frac{\Delta_{b}}{\varrho_{b}^{2}}\partial_{r}^{2}-\frac{1}{\varrho_{b}^{2}}\partial_{\theta}^{2}-\frac{1}{\varrho_{b}^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}(\partial_{\varphi}+\mathfrak{a}\sin^{2}\theta\,\partial_{t})^{2},

(3.1b)

\displaystyle\quad\Delta_{b}

\displaystyle=r^{2}-2\mathfrak{m}r+\mathfrak{a}^{2},\ \ \varrho_{b}^{2}=r^{2}+\mathfrak{a}^{2}\cos^{2}\theta.

(3.1c)

Then
g_{b}^{\rm BL}
is the metric of an isolated, rotating, stationary black hole with event horizon
\{r=r_{+}\}
, and domain of outer communications is
\{r>r_{+}\}
. Further,
\{r=r_{-}\}
is the inner horizon.
We have
\sqrt{|\det g_{b}|}=\varrho_{b}^{2}\sin\theta
. The Kerr metric is a solution of the Einstein vacuum equation:

\mathop{\rm Ric}\bigl(g_{b}^{\rm BL}\bigr)=0.

(3.2)

The form (
3.1
) of the metric breaks down at
r_{\pm}
which are the roots of
\Delta_{b}
. Let

r_{*}=\int\frac{r^{2}+\mathfrak{a}^{2}}{\Delta_{b}}dr\quad\mbox{with}\quad r_{*}(3\mathfrak{m})=0.

Consider coordinates
(t_{*},r,\varphi_{*},\theta)
, where
t_{*}(t,r)=t+F(r)
and
\varphi_{*}(t,r)=\varphi+T(r)
are smooth functions such that

\displaystyle F(r)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}r_{*}\quad\mbox{for}\,r\leq 3\mathfrak{m},\\
-r_{*}\quad\mbox{for}\,r\geq 4\mathfrak{m},\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle T(r)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\int\frac{a}{\Delta_{b}}\quad\mbox{for}\,r\leq 3\mathfrak{m},\,T(3\mathfrak{m})=0,\\
0\quad\mbox{for}\,r\geq 4\mathfrak{m}.\end{array}\right.

For
r\leq 3\mathfrak{m}
the metric then takes the following form

g_{b,*}=\frac{\Delta_{b}}{\varrho_{b}^{2}}(dt_{*}-\mathfrak{a}\sin^{2}\theta d\varphi_{*})^{2}-2(dt_{*}-\mathfrak{a}\sin^{2}\theta d\varphi_{*})dr-\varrho_{b}^{2}d\theta^{2}-\frac{\sin^{2}\theta}{\varrho_{b}^{2}}(\mathfrak{a}dt_{*}-(r^{2}+\mathfrak{a}^{2})d\varphi_{*})^{2},

which is clearly smooth up to
r_{+}
.

Given

r_{-}<r_{0}<r_{+},

(3.5)

let

X^{0}_{b}=[r_{0},\infty)\times\mathbb{S}^{2}

and consider

\displaystyle M^{\circ}_{b}=\mathbb{R}\times X^{0}_{b},

(3.6)

with coordinates
t_{*},r,\theta,\varphi_{*}
.
We compactify
X^{0}_{b}
as follows: recalling the definition of
\overline{\mathbb{R}^{3}}
from (
2.2
), we set

X:=\overline{X^{0}_{b}}\subset\overline{\mathbb{R}^{3}},\,\rho:=\frac{1}{r}.

Thus,
X=\overline{\{r\geq r_{0}\}}
, and we let
\partial_{-}X=r^{-1}(r_{0})
,
\partial_{+}X=\partial\overline{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\subset X
. Within
X
, the topological boundary of
\mathcal{X}=(r_{+},\infty)\times\mathbb{S}
has two components,

\partial\mathcal{X}=\partial_{-}\mathcal{X}\sqcup\partial_{+}\mathcal{X},\qquad\partial_{-}\mathcal{X}:=r^{-1}(r_{+}),\quad\partial_{+}\mathcal{X}:=\rho^{-1}(0)=\partial_{+}X.

(3.7)

Note that
\partial_{-}X
is distinct from
\partial_{-}\mathcal{X}
, and is indeed a hypersurface lying
beyond
the event horizon. Note that
\mathbb{R}\times\partial X
has two components,

\Sigma_{\rm fin}:=\mathbb{R}\times\partial_{-}X

(3.8)

(which is a spacelike hypersurface inside of the black hole) and
\mathbb{R}_{t_{*}}\times\partial_{+}X=\mathbb{R}_{t_{*}}\times\partial_{+}\mathcal{X}
(which is future null infinity, typically denoted
\mathscr{I}^{+}
); moreover, the future event horizon,
\mathcal{H}^{+}
, is
\mathbb{R}_{t_{*}}\times\partial_{-}\mathcal{X}
.

Concerning the inverse metric we obtain for
r\leq 3\mathfrak{m}

\displaystyle G

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mathfrak{a}^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}{\varrho_{b}^{2}}\partial_{t_{*}}^{2}-2\frac{\mathfrak{a}}{\varrho_{b}^{2}}\partial_{\varphi_{*}}\partial_{t_{*}}-2\frac{r^{2}+\mathfrak{a}^{2}}{\varrho_{b}^{2}}\partial_{r}\partial_{t_{*}}-2\frac{\mathfrak{a}}{\varrho_{b}^{2}}\partial_{\varphi_{*}}\partial_{r}-\frac{1}{\varrho_{b}^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}\partial_{\varphi_{*}}^{2}

\displaystyle-\frac{\Delta_{b}}{\varrho_{b}^{2}}\partial_{r}^{2}-\frac{1}{\varrho_{b}^{2}}\partial_{\theta}^{2}.

For
r\geq 4\mathfrak{m}
we obtain

\displaystyle G

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mathfrak{a}^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}{\varrho_{b}^{2}}\partial_{t_{*}}^{2}+4\frac{a\mathfrak{m}r}{\varrho_{b}^{2}\Delta_{b}}\partial_{\varphi_{*}}\partial_{t_{*}}-\frac{\Delta_{b}-\mathfrak{a}^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}{\varrho_{b}^{2}\Delta_{b}\sin^{2}\theta}\partial_{\varphi_{*}}^{2}-2\rho^{2}\frac{r^{2}+\mathfrak{a}^{2}}{\varrho_{b}^{2}}\partial_{\rho}\partial_{t_{*}}

\displaystyle-\frac{\rho^{4}}{\varrho_{b}^{2}}\Delta_{b}\partial_{\rho}^{2}-\frac{1}{\varrho_{b}^{2}}\partial_{\theta}^{2}.

We will also have to consider linearized Kerr metrics which are given by

\dot{g}_{b}(\dot{b})=\left.\frac{d}{ds}\right|_{s=0}g_{b+s\dot{b}},\,\dot{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

(3.9)

They fulfill the linearized Einstein equations

\displaystyle D_{g_{b}}Ric(\dot{g}_{b}(\dot{b}))=0.

Remark 3.1
.

The explicit forms of the Kerr metric introduced above represent a rotating, isolated black hole at rest at the center of coordinates, with axis of rotation aligned with the axial Killing field
\partial_{\varphi}
. Acting on the Kerr black hole with global Poincaré transformations, corresponding to a change of reference frame, leads to a family of translated, boosted and rotated black holes. By analogy with a relativistic, massive, spinning particle, the linear and angular momenta of the Kerr black hole take values in a coadjoint orbit of the Poincaré group, determined by the parameters
\mathfrak{m},\mathfrak{a}
. Linearized perturbations of the Kerr metric are solutions of the linearized vacuum Einstein equation on the Kerr background,

\displaystyle D_{g_{b}}\mathop{\rm Ric}\dot{g}=0.

(3.10)

In view of the above remarks, we see that
\ker D_{g_{b}}\mathop{\rm Ric}
contains a subspace corresponding to the infinitesmal action of the Poincare group on the Kerr black hole, i.e. to the tangent space of the coadjoint orbit. In particular,
\ker D_{g_{b}}\mathop{\rm Ric}
contains perturbations that change the axis of rotation of the black hole. However, as we shall see in sections
6
,
7
below, these are pure gauge, except for perturbations
\dot{\mathfrak{m}},\dot{\mathfrak{a}}
of the two Kerr parameters.

Remark 3.2
.

All pairings will be defined with respect to the natural inner product on
L^{2}(X^{0}_{b})
, i.e.

\langle f,g\rangle:=\int_{X^{0}_{b}}fg\varrho_{b}^{2}\sin\theta drd\theta d\varphi_{*}

for functions and

\langle\omega,\nu\rangle:=\int_{X^{0}_{b}}\omega^{a}\nu_{a}\varrho_{b}^{2}\sin\theta drd\theta d\varphi_{*}

for
1-
forms.

3.2.
Stationarity, vector bundles, and geometric operators

In the notation (
3.6
), denote the projection to the spatial manifold by

\pi_{X}\colon M^{\circ}_{b}\to X^{\circ}_{b}.

Suppose
E_{1}\to X^{\circ}_{b}
is a vector bundle; then differentiation along
\partial_{t_{*}}
is a well-defined operation on sections of the pullback bundle
\pi_{X}^{*}E_{1}
. The tangent bundle of
M^{\circ}_{b}
is an important example of such a pullback bundle, as

TM^{\circ}_{b}\cong\pi_{X}^{*}(T_{t_{*}^{-1}(0)}M^{\circ}_{b}),

likewise for the cotangent bundle and other tensor bundles.

Let
E_{2}\to X^{\circ}_{b}
be another vector bundle, and suppose
\widehat{L}(0)\in\mathrm{Diff}(X^{\circ}_{b};E_{1},E_{2})
is a differential operator; fixing
\mathfrak{t}=t_{*}+F
,
F\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(X^{\circ}_{b})
, we can then define its
stationary extension
by assigning to
u\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(M^{\circ};\pi_{X}^{*}E_{1})
the section
(Lu)(\mathfrak{t},-):=\widehat{L}(0)(u(\mathfrak{t},-))
of
\pi_{X}^{*}E_{2}
; this extension
does
depend on the choice of
\mathfrak{t}
. The action of
L
on stationary functions on the other hand is independent of the choice of
\mathfrak{t}
since

L\pi_{X}^{*}=\pi_{X}^{*}\widehat{L}(0).

(3.11)

Via stationary extension, one can consider
\mathrm{Diff}_{\mathrm{b}}(X;E)
(for

a smooth vector bundle down to
\partial_{+}X
) to be a subalgebra of
\mathrm{Diff}(M^{\circ}_{b};\pi_{X}^{*}E)
; likewise
\mathrm{Diff}_{\mathrm{sc}}(X;E)\hookrightarrow\mathrm{Diff}(M^{\circ}_{b};\pi_{X}^{*}E)
.

Conversely, if
L\in\mathrm{Diff}(M^{\circ}_{b};\pi_{X}^{*}E_{1},\pi_{X}^{*}E_{2})
is
stationary
, i.e. commutes with
\partial_{t_{*}}
, there exists a unique (independent of the choice of
\mathfrak{t}
) operator
\widehat{L}(0)\in\mathrm{Diff}(X^{\circ}_{b};E_{1},E_{2})
such that the relation (
3.11
) holds. More generally, we can consider the formal conjugation of
L
by the Fourier transform in
\mathfrak{t}
,

\widehat{L}(\sigma):=e^{i\sigma\mathfrak{t}}Le^{-i\sigma\mathfrak{t}}\in\mathrm{Diff}(X^{\circ}_{b};E_{1},E_{2}),

where we identify the stationary operator
e^{i\sigma\mathfrak{t}}Le^{-i\sigma\mathfrak{t}}
with an operator on
X^{\circ}_{b}
. Switching from
\mathfrak{t}
to another time function,
\mathfrak{t}+F^{\prime}
,
F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(X^{\circ})
, amounts to conjugating
\widehat{L}(\sigma)
by
e^{i\sigma F^{\prime}}
.

In order to describe the uniform behavior of geometric operators at
\partial_{+}X
concisely, we need to define a suitable extension of
T^{*}M^{\circ}_{b}
to ‘infinity’. To accomplish this, note that the product decomposition (
3.6
) induces a splitting

T^{*}M^{\circ}_{b}\cong\pi_{T}^{*}(T^{*}\mathbb{R}_{t_{*}})\oplus\pi_{X}^{*}(T^{*}X^{\circ}_{b}),

where
\pi_{T}\colon M^{\circ}_{b}\to\mathbb{R}_{t_{*}}
is the projection. We therefore define the
extended scattering cotangent bundle
of
X
by

\widetilde{}^{{\mathrm{sc}}}T{}^{*}X:=\mathbb{R}dt_{*}\oplus{}^{{\mathrm{sc}}}T^{*}X.

(3.12)

At this point,
dt_{*}
is merely a name for the basis of a trivial real rank
1
line bundle over
X
; considering the pullback bundle
\pi_{X}^{*}\widetilde{}^{{\mathrm{sc}}}T{}^{*}X\to M^{\circ}_{b}
, we identify it with the differential of
t_{*}\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(M^{\circ}_{b})
, giving an isomorphism

(\pi_{X}^{*}\widetilde{}^{{\mathrm{sc}}}T{}^{*}X)|_{M^{\circ}_{b}}\cong T^{*}M^{\circ}_{b}.

(3.13)

Smooth sections of
\widetilde{{}^{{\mathrm{sc}}}T^{*}}X\to X
are linear combinations, with
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(X)
coefficients, of
dt_{*}
and the 1-forms
dx^{i}
, where
(x^{1},x^{2},x^{3})
are standard coordinates on
X^{\circ}_{b}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
For a stationary metric
g
on
M^{\circ}_{b}
, there exists a unique
g^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(X^{\circ}_{b};S^{2}\,\widetilde{}^{{\mathrm{sc}}}T{}^{*}X)
such that
\pi_{X}^{*}g^{\prime}=g
, namely
g^{\prime}
is the restriction (as a section of
S^{2}\,\widetilde{}^{{\mathrm{sc}}}T{}^{*}X
) of
g
to any transversal of
\pi_{X}
, such as level sets of
t_{*}
. Identifying
g
with
g^{\prime}
and applying this to the Kerr family, we then have

g_{b}\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(X;S^{2}\,\widetilde{}^{{\mathrm{sc}}}T{}^{*}X);

(3.14)

it is non-degenerate down to
\partial_{+}X
. Let
\not{g}{}
be the standard metric on the sphere. Then we have

\begin{split}&g_{b_{0}}-\underaccent{\bar}{g}\in\rho\mathcal{C}^{\infty},\quad\underaccent{\bar}{g}:=dt^{2}-dr^{2}-r^{2}\not{g}{},\\
&g_{(\mathfrak{m},\mathfrak{a})}-g_{(\mathfrak{m},0)}\in\rho^{2}\mathcal{C}^{\infty},\end{split}

(3.15)

i.e. a Kerr metric equals the Minkowski metric

We proceed to discuss basic geometric operators on Kerr spacetimes. We write

4.
The Teukolsky master equation

Perturbations of Kerr are significantly more complicated to analyze than perturbations of Schwarzschild. Real progress became possible when Teukolsky
[
Te
]
wrote down his master equation describing gauge invariant perturbations of various spin. We begin by setting up the framework required for this formulation and proceed to discuss the absence of mode solutions.

4.1.
The Teukolsky operator

We briefly review the construction of the Teukolsky operator, we refer e.g. to
[
Ch
]
,
[
GHP
]
,
[
Te
]
for details of the construction and to
[
M1
]
for a summary of the geometric background of the Teukolsky equation.
Since
\mathcal{M}
is a non-compact, oriented and time oriented Lorentzian 4-manifold, it admits a spin structure, i.e. an
\mathop{\rm SL}(2,\mathbb{C})
principal bundle double covering the orthonormal frame bundle
\mathop{\rm SO}(\mathcal{M})
.
Let
\mathcal{S},\bar{\mathcal{S}}
be the spinor bundle and its conjugate with fibers
\mathbb{C}^{2}
and
\bar{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
, respectively. The isomorphism
\mathcal{S}\otimes\bar{\mathcal{S}}\to T_{\mathbb{C}}\mathcal{M}
yields a correspondence between tensors and spinors. Sections of
\mathcal{S}
are denoted with capital latin indices, eg.
\kappa^{A}
, while sections of
\bar{\mathcal{S}}
are denoted with primed indices, eg.
\varpi^{A^{\prime}}
.
The action of
\mathop{\rm SL}(2,\mathbb{C})
on
\mathbb{C}^{2}
leaves a symplectic structure on
\mathcal{S}
invariant. The spin-metric
\epsilon_{AB}=\epsilon_{[AB]}
is given by normalizing this symplectic form so that
g_{ab}=\epsilon_{AB}\epsilon_{A^{\prime}B^{\prime}}
. The spin-metric
\epsilon_{AB}
and its inverse
\epsilon^{AB}
, where
\epsilon_{AB}\epsilon^{CB}=\delta_{A}{}^{C}
, are used to raise and lower spinor indices, eg.
\kappa^{A}\epsilon_{AB}=\kappa_{B}
. A spin-frame is a dyad
o^{A},\iota^{A}
normalized such that

\displaystyle o_{A}\iota^{A}=1.

(4.1)

This corresponds to a complex null tetrad

\displaystyle\ell^{a}=o^{A}o^{A^{\prime}},\quad n^{a}=\iota^{A}\iota^{A^{\prime}},\quad m^{a}=o^{A}\iota^{A^{\prime}},\quad\bar{m}^{a}=\iota^{A}o^{A^{\prime}},

(4.2)

with

\displaystyle g_{ab}=2(\ell_{(a}n_{b)}-m_{(a}\bar{m}_{b)}).

(4.3)

The Newman-Penrose (NP) and Geroch-Held-Penrose (GHP) formalisms represent spinor and tensor fields in terms of scalar dyad and tetrad components. Note that (
4.1
) is left invariant under rescalings

\displaystyle o^{A}\to\lambda o^{A},\quad\iota^{A}\to\lambda^{-1}\iota^{A}

(4.4)

for a non-vanishing, complex scalar field
\lambda
on
\mathcal{M}
. A scalar
\phi
constructed by projecting on a dyad or tetrad then transforms as

\displaystyle\phi\to|\lambda|^{2w}\left(\frac{\lambda}{\bar{\lambda}}\right)^{s}\phi

(4.5)

where
w,s
are the boost- and spin-weights of
\phi
, respectively. Scalars transforming as in (
4.5
) are termed properly weighted. Properly weighted scalars can be viewed as sections of complex line bundles
\mathcal{B}(s,w)
with structure group
\mathbb{C}_{*}=\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
. A spin dyad (or a complex null tetrad), provides a trivialization of
\mathcal{B}(s,w)
and in this trivialization, sections of
\mathcal{B}(s,w)
correspond to complex functions on
\mathcal{M}
.
It is an important fact that the irreducible representations of
\mathop{\rm SL}(2,\mathbb{C})
are given by symmetric spinors. Using the spinor-tensor correspondence already mentioned above, one may expand each tensor field in terms of symmetric spinors and
\epsilon
factors. For example, the electromagnetic field strength tensor is a real 2-form
\mathrm{F}_{ab}=\mathrm{F}_{[ab]}
and we have (see (3.4.20) in
[
PR1
]

4.5.
Asymptotic behavior

To analyze the asymptotic behavior of the solutions of the Teukolsky equation, we will use normal operator arguments. An important role will be played by the Mellin transform and its inverse, see e.g.
[
Va1
, Section 3.1]
.
Let
M_{I}=[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{S}^{2}
. The Mellin transform is defined by

\displaystyle({\mathcal{M}}\phi)(\lambda,\omega)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\rho^{-i\lambda}\phi(\rho,\omega)\frac{d\rho}{\rho}

and it gives an isometric isomorphism of
L^{2}(M_{I};\frac{d\rho}{\rho}d\omega)
with
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{\lambda};L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2};d\omega))
. Noting that
L^{2}(M_{I};\frac{d\rho}{\rho}d\omega)=\rho^{-3/2}L^{2}(M_{I};\frac{d\rho}{\rho^{4}}d\omega)
we obtain an isomorphism of
\rho^{-3/2}L^{2}(M_{I};\frac{d\rho}{\rho^{4}}d\omega)
with
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{\lambda};L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2};d\omega))
. The inverse Mellin transform is given by

\displaystyle({\mathcal{M}^{-1}}\psi)(\rho,\omega)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho^{i\lambda}\psi(\lambda,\omega)d\lambda.

More generally for
\phi\in\rho^{q}L^{2}_{b}(M_{I};\frac{d\rho}{\rho^{4}}d\omega)=\rho^{q+3/2}L^{2}_{b}(M_{I};\frac{d\rho}{\rho}d\omega)
,
\mathcal{M}\phi(\bullet-i(q+3/2))
is well defined as an element of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{\lambda};L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2}d\omega))
and its inverse Mellin transform becomes

\displaystyle({\mathcal{M}^{-1}}\psi)(\rho,\omega)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty-i(q+3/2)}^{\infty-i(q+3/2)}\rho^{i\lambda}\psi(\lambda,\omega)d\lambda.

If
\phi\in\rho^{q}L^{2}_{b}(M_{I};\frac{d\rho}{\rho^{4}}d\omega)
has compact support in
\rho
, the Mellin transform
\mathcal{M}\phi
extends to a holomorphic function in
{\Im}\lambda>-(q+3/2)
with values in
L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2};d\omega)
, satisfying

\displaystyle\sup_{-(q+3/2)<\alpha<C}\|\mathcal{M}\phi(\bullet+i\alpha)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{\lambda};L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2}))}<\infty

for all
C<\infty
, and
\mathcal{M}\phi(\bullet+i\alpha)
extends continuously to
\alpha=-(q+3/2)
in the topology of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{\lambda};L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
. Since
\mathcal{M}
intertwines differentiation
\rho D_{\rho}
and multiplication by
\lambda
, we obtain similar statements for weighted

\displaystyle\bar{H}_{{\mathrm{b}}}^{b,q}(M_{I};\frac{d\rho}{\rho^{4}}d\omega)\ni\phi\mapsto\mathcal{M}\phi(\bullet-i(q+3/2))\in\bigcap_{j=0}^{k}\langle\lambda\rangle^{-j}L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{\lambda};H^{k-j}(\mathbb{S}^{2})).

F_{\mu\nu}=4({\rm Re}\Phi_{0}\,n_{[\mu}l_{\nu]}+i\Im\Phi_{0}\,m_{[\mu}\bar{m}_{\nu]}).

\omega
is a potential for the Coulomb solution and it fulfills the Lorenz gauge (
5.4
). Therefore
\omega=C(\omega_{0}+d\tilde{f})
with

\omega_{0}=\frac{r}{\varrho_{b}^{2}}(dt-a\sin^{2}\theta\,d\varphi).

We will suppose
C=1
in the following. The
1-
form
\omega_{0}
is singular at the horizon, we therefore have to correct this behavior by a gauge term. Concretely we have for

:

\displaystyle\omega_{0}=\frac{r}{\varrho_{b}^{2}}(dt_{*}-a\sin^{2}\theta d\varphi_{*})-\frac{r}{\Delta_{b}}dr

and for

\displaystyle\omega_{0}=\frac{r}{\varrho_{b}^{2}}(dt_{*}-a\sin^{2}\theta d\varphi_{*})+\frac{(r^{2}+a^{2})r}{\varrho_{b}^{2}\Delta_{b}}dr.

(5.13)

Therefore we put

\displaystyle\omega_{b,s_{0}}=\omega_{0}+\frac{r_{+}}{\Delta_{b}}dr.

Note that
\omega_{b,s_{0}}\in\bar{H}_{{\mathrm{b}}}^{\infty,q}
. An explicit calculation gives

\displaystyle\Box_{g_{b},1}\omega_{b,s_{0}}=0.

By the same argument as before we find

\displaystyle\delta_{g_{b}}\omega_{b,s_{0}}=0.

Let now
f=\tilde{f}-\int\frac{r_{+}}{\Delta_{b}}
. We find

\displaystyle\Box_{g_{b},0}f

\displaystyle=\delta_{g_{b}}d\tilde{f}-\delta_{g_{b}}\frac{r_{+}}{\Delta_{b}}dr=\delta_{g_{b}}\omega-\delta_{g_{b}}\omega_{b}=0.

By Theorem
4.7

f=const
.
It remains to show that
\omega_{b}^{*}
is in the cokernel. We first observe that
\omega^{*}_{b,s_{0}}=d(H(r-r_{+}))
and then compute

\hat{\Box}^{*}_{g_{b},1}(0)d(H(r-r_{+}))=d\hat{\Box}^{*}_{g_{b},0}(0)H(r-r_{+})=0.

∎

6.2.
Link to the gauge fixed linearized Einstein operator

Let

\displaystyle L_{g_{b}}:=2(D_{g_{b}}{\rm Ric}+\delta^{*}_{g_{b}}\delta_{g_{b}}G_{g_{b}})

be the linearized Einstein operator around the Kerr metric in the wave map/De Turck gauge. Here

denotes the trace reversal operator in
4
spacetime dimensions. We start with the following

Proposition 6.2
.

Suppose
\dot{g}\in\bar{H}_{{\mathrm{b}}}^{\infty,q}(X),\,q\in(-3/2;-1/2)
. If
\widehat{L_{g_{b}}}(0)\dot{g}=0
, then there exists

such that

.

Proof.

The proof is in principle the same as the proof of
[
HHV
, Proposition 4.4]
, we refer to this proof for details. We note that in the full subextreme range of
\mathfrak{a}
the normal operator of
\widehat{L_{g_{b}}}(0)
is the negative euclidian Laplacian tensored with the
10\times 10
identity matrix when working in the standard coordinate trivialization. The boundary spectrum of the scalar euclidian Laplacian is, by definition, the divisor of
\widehat{\rho^{-2}\Delta}(\lambda)^{-1},
where the hat stands for Mellin transform in
\rho
and
-\Delta=\rho^{2}\rho D_{\rho}\rho D_{\rho}+i\rho^{3}D_{\rho}-\rho^{2}\not{\Delta}{}
is the positive euclidean Laplacian. Decomposing functions on
\partial X
into spherical harmonics, and denoting by
\mathbb{S}_{l}

a degree
l\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
spherical harmonic, we have

\rho^{-i\lambda}(-\rho^{-2}\Delta)(\rho^{i\lambda}\mathbb{S}_{l})=(\lambda(\lambda+i)+l(l+1))\mathbb{S}_{l},

which vanishes for
\lambda=il,\,\lambda=-i(l+1)
. With respect to the choice of our Sobolev spaces only the
\lambda=-i(l+1)
are relevant for the expansion, the
l=0
spherical harmonics gives the
\rho

∎

Lemma 6.3
.

(1)

(6.3)

To arrange that the R.H.S. lies in the image of

we need

\displaystyle\langle-\delta_{g_{b}}G_{g_{b}}\dot{g}+\delta_{g_{b}}G_{g_{b}}\dot{g}_{b}(\lambda(\dot{\mathfrak{a}}),\dot{\mathfrak{a}}),\omega_{b,s_{0}}^{*}\rangle=0.

If

\displaystyle\dot{g}_{b}(1,0)

\displaystyle=-\frac{2r}{\varrho_{b}^{2}}(dt_{*}-a\sin^{2}\theta d\varphi_{*})^{2}.

\displaystyle\hat{n}_{\mu}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(dt_{*}-a\sin^{2}\theta d\varphi_{*}).

(2)

4.5
