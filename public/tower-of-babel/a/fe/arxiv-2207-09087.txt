Is Vertical Logistic Regression Privacy-Preserving? A Comprehensive Privacy Analysis and Beyond Conference: ACM Conference on Computer and Communications Security; Due 15 May 2019; London, TBD CCS: Security and privacy Privacy-preserving protocols CCS: Security and privacy Privacy protections CCS: Computing methodologies Machine learning
Yuzheng Hu, Tianle Cai, Jinyong Shan, Shange Tang, Chaochao Cai, Ethan Song, Bo Li, Dawn Song

Abstract
. We consider vertical logistic regression (VLR) trained with mini-batch gradient descent — a setting which has attracted growing interest among industries and proven to be useful in a wide range of applications including finance and medical research. We provide a comprehensive and rigorous privacy analysis of VLR in a class of open-source Federated Learning frameworks, where the protocols might differ between one another, yet a procedure of obtaining local gradients is implicitly shared. We first consider the honest-but-curious threat model, in which the detailed implementation of protocol is neglected and only the shared procedure is assumed, which we abstract as an oracle. We find that even under this general setting, single-dimension feature and label can still be recovered from the other party under suitable constraints of batch size, thus demonstrating the potential vulnerability of all frameworks following the same philosophy. Then we look into a popular instantiation of the protocol based on Homomorphic Encryption (HE). We propose an active attack that significantly weaken the constraints on batch size in the previous analysis via generating and compressing auxiliary ciphertext. To address the privacy leakage within the HE-based protocol, we develop a simple-yet-effective countermeasure based on Differential Privacy (DP), and provide both utility and privacy guarantees for the updated algorithm. Finally, we empirically verify the effectiveness of our attack and defense on benchmark datasets. Altogether, our findings suggest that all vertical federated learning frameworks that solely depend on HE might contain severe privacy risks, and DP, which has already demonstrated its power in horizontal federated learning, can also play a crucial role in the vertical setting, especially when coupled with HE or secure multi-party computation (MPC) techniques.



1.
Introduction

Recent years have witnessed the advancement of Artificial Intelligence (AI) in a wide range of tasks, such as image recognition
(
He
et al. 2016
)
, natural language processing
(
Devlin
et al. 2018
)
, recommendation
(
Gharibshah et al. 2020
)
and game
(
Silver et al. 2016
)
. These achievements crucially rely on the availability of massive amount of labeled data. Across various industries, however, data are usually distributed over multiple places in different forms, and most organizations only possess partial or limited amount of
unlabeled
data due to the heavy cost of annotation. Further, it has been increasingly difficult for organizations to share and cooperatively use their data from a legislative perspective owing to regulations like General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR)
(
Voigt and Von dem
Bussche 2017
)
and California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA)
(
CCPA 2018
)
, which enforce strict requirements on the use of data in order to protect individuals’ privacy.

To address this issue, the concept of federated learning (FL) was proposed by Google
(
Konečnỳ et al. 2016a
;
Konečnỳ et al. 2016b
;
McMahan
et al. 2016
)
in 2016, aiming to build machine learning models across multiple decentralized parties without revealing their raw data. Over the past few years, there is an increasing concern towards privacy and security within both academia and industry, and FL has been widely adopted in finance
(
Liu
et al. 2020a
)
, medical research
(
Jochems et al. 2016
)
, smart phones
(
Hard et al. 2018
)
and so on. Meanwhile, several open source FL systems have been developed, including TensorFlow Federated (TFF)
(
Authors 2019d
)
by Google, Federated AI Technology Enabler (FATE)
(
Authors 2019a
)
by Tencent Webank, PySyft
(
Ryffel et al. 2018
)
by Openmined, LEAF
(
Caldas et al. 2018
)
by CMU and PaddleFL
(
Authors 2019c
)
by Baidu. The convenience brought by these frameworks makes FL the mainstream paradigm for proprietary entities to learn jointly across their geographical boundaries.

Based on the distribution characteristics of data, FL can be categorized into horizontal federated learning (HFL) and vertical federated learning (VFL)
(
Yang
et al. 2019
)
. HFL is a system in which all participants share the same feature space, but the sample space could be significantly different. For example, a set of domestic banks may share a similar financial profile, however very few common customers. In such case, HFL can be applied to agglomerate models learnt in each individual banks. In VFL (a.k.a. split learning), participants first align their user ID to ensure a common sample space, then jointly train a model based on the aggregation of different features. For example, banks and fund companies may own different perspectives of a customer’s financial status, so they can perform better risk management leveraging the information from multiple dimensions.

While FL came up as a promising solution to preserve users’ privacy, potential risk of data leakage has been recognized under this new paradigm, which may hinder its widespread adoption. The main observation is that, although the raw data stays untouched, the exchange of intermediate values within the execution of FL framework, might reveal much private information
even with cryptography algorithm
. Zhu and Han
(
Zhu and Han 2020
)
show that it is possible to
learn from gradients
and recover the original input as long as the batch size is small; Zhao et al.
(
Zhao
et al. 2020
)
further show that the label can be directly inferred from the gradients. Other forms of attack include
membership inference

(
Nasr
et al. 2019
)
, in which the adversary aims to learn whether a given sample belongs to another participant;
property inference

(
Melis et al. 2019
)
, where the adversary intends to learn data properties that are independent of the main task; and
model poisoning/backdoor attack

(
Bhagoji et al. 2019
;
Bagdasaryan et al. 2020
)
, with the goal of causing the global model to misclassify a set of chosen inputs. As FL is gaining more popularity within the AI community, privacy analysis of the current FL frameworks has become one of the most important research directions, which could potentially lead to the improvement over existing algorithms and protocols.

It has come to our attention that a major study of the privacy analysis of FL focuses on HFL, whereas the corresponding work on VFL lacks, albeit VFL is being widely used in industry
(
Wang 2019
;
Feng and Yu 2020
;
Gu
et al. 2020
;
Webank 2019a
;
Webank 2019b
)
. As far as we know, there are only a few exceptions: Weng et al.
(
Weng
et al. 2020
)
propose
reverse multiplication attack
and
reverse sum attack
for logistic regression and Secure XGBoost
(
Cheng et al. 2021
)
in the VFL setting and demonstrate the privacy leakage of feature and partial order; Luo et al.
(
Luo
et al. 2021
)
study feature attack with similar models in the prediction phase. However, the theoretical analyses are either not rigorous or based on unrealistic assumptions; further, none of these works consider the privacy of
labels
of training instances, which is a crucial part of the raw data. Recently, Liu et al.
(
Liu et al. 2020b
)
point out that the label can be inferred from the sign of gradients, but this is just a simple extension of
(
Zhao
et al. 2020
)
in the vertical setting and does not hold when using an approximate version of the cross-entropy loss. Li et al.
(
Li et al. 2022
)
consider the model-agnostic split learning, and propose a norm-based approach and a direction-based approach to identify the labels based on the information of per-sample gradient during training; however, their analyses relies on certain heuristics regarding the behavior of the model and is restricted to the two-party case.

Aiming to provide an in-depth understanding of a
specific
model, we focus on a popular setting in VFL, where two (or multiple) parties collaboratively train a logistic regression model using mini-batch gradient descent, with separated features and labels owned by only one of them
(
Hardy et al. 2017
)
. We refer to the setting as vertical logistic regression (VLR). Though being simple, VLR has attracted growing interest among industries and proven to be useful in many practical settings such as risk control
(
Chen et al. 2021
)
, credit scoring,
(

et al. 2020

(

. Also, while this setting has been studied by
(

)

Specifically, starting from the two-party scenario and assuming an oracle of obtaining local gradients which is actually being deployed in practice

)
, we provide a fine-grained privacy analysis for both parties, and propose attacks on both feature and label levels under suitable conditions of batch size. For feature attack we come up with a corresponding hardness result, and for label recovery attack we show it naturally generalizes to the multi-party scenario. We then take into account the protocol of obtaining gradients based on Homomorphic Encryption (HE), and propose an active attack via generating and compressing auxiliary ciphertext, which allows us to relax the constraints of batch size. We proceed to develop a countermeasure based on Differential Privacy (DP) against the proposed attack, and provide utility and privacy guarantees for the updated algorithm. Finally, we verify the effectiveness of our attack and defense on benchmark datasets, indicating the fundamental weakness in existing frameworks as well as the power of coupling DP with HE techniques in VLR.

The main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows:

•

We provide a fine-grained privacy analysis of VLR in the honest-but-curious setting: under suitable conditions of batch size, 1) for Party
A
, we construct a feature attack when
B
has single-dimension feature and provide a hardness result for the rest case; 2) for Party
B
, we identify a label recovery attack when the initialization is small. We also show that the label recovery attack can naturally generalize when there are multiple parties. Our attack is strong in the sense that we (i) assume no
prior knowledge of the data
; (ii) make no assumptions on the
model’s accuracy
; (iii) do no resort to external tools such as
learning-based algorithm
; it holds (iv) independently of the
existence of the third party
and (v) can be performed at
any time
during training. It only requires the knowledge of intermediate gradients, and is formalized via a careful dissection of the linear system;

•

We then consider the active adversarial model: by taking into account the specific implementation of protocol based on Homomorphic Encryption (HE), we propose an active attack via generating and compressing auxiliary ciphertext, which allows us to relax the constraints of batch size and greatly extend the range of applications;

•

We develop a simple-yet-effective countermeasure for the proposed attack based on Differential Privacy (DP), which is realized by injecting Gaussian noise to the HE-based protocol. While DP appears as a standard building block in HFL
(
Geyer
et al. 2017
;
Truex et al. 2019
;
Wei et al. 2020
)
, to the best of our knowledge, we are the first to couple it with HE techniques in the vertical setting. We also provide both utility and privacy guarantees for the updated algorithm;

•

Experiment results on benchmark datasets verify the effectiveness of our proposed attack and defense, and showcase both the potential weakness of general VFL frameworks that solely rely on HE, as well as the capability of coupling DP with HE or MPC techniques to achieve better performance in terms of data privacy.

General Defense Strategies.

•

Homomorphic Encryption.
Homomorphic Encryption (HE)
(
Rivest
et al. 1978
)
is a form of encryption which allows operations on the encrypted data without access to the secret key. Based on the utility of the cryptosystem, HE can be categorized into partially homomorphic encryption (PHE), somewhat homomorphic
encryption (SWHE) and fully homomorphic encryption (FHE)
(
Acar
et al. 2018
)
. While FHE enjoys more desirable properties and has been deployed in practical settings such as training with genomic data
(
Chen et al. 2018
)
, we resort to the Paillier cryptosystem for efficiency, which is a standard form of additively homomorphic encryption within PHE and has been used in VLR in many previous works
(
Aono
et al. 2016
;
Aono et al. 2017
;
Hardy et al. 2017
)
.

•

Differential Privacy.
Differential Privacy (DP)
(
Dwork 2006
)
offers a probabilistic framework to
quantify
privacy and has become a standard building block in machine learning community ever since its birth. DP ensures that the outcome of an algorithm will not be impacted by the presence or absence of a single instance, and is usually realized via
randomization
: making the algorithm stochastic by adding noise. Based on the structure of noise, there are several types of mechanism to achieve DP: Laplace, Exponential and Gaussian Mechanism
(
Dwork
et al. 2014
)
. See Section
6
for formal definitions.

DP has been applied to a wide range of learning tasks to ensure privacy
(
Chaudhuri
et al. 2011
;
Song
et al. 2013
;
Bassily
et al. 2014
;
Abadi et al. 2016
)
, in which the noise is injected to the parameter after a step of update. For FL, DP is mostly used in the horizontal setting
(
Geyer
et al. 2017
;
Truex et al. 2019
;
Wei et al. 2020
)
. On the contrary, we apply DP to the VFL framework, carefully couple the noise-injection mechanism with the HE-based protocol and inject noise to the sensitive information before communication.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
(Vertical) Logistic Regression

In binary logistic regression, the goal is to learn a linear model
\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
that correctly maps the sample
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
to its corresponding label
y\in\{-1,1\}
. To achieve this, we use the training set
D=\{(\bm{x}_{i},y_{i})\}_{{i\in[n]}}
with
[n]=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
, and consider the following unregularized empirical risk minimization:

(1)

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{\theta}}\ell_{D}(\bm{\theta}):=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log S(y_{i}\bm{\theta}^{\top}\bm{x}_{i}),

where
S(z)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}
is the sigmoid function. In practice, we usually resort to mini-batch gradient descent, which is an effective algorithm of solving Eq. (
1
).

Mini-batch Gradient Descent (MGD).

The parameter is initialized as
\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{0}
. At
t
-th iteration, a mini-batch
\mathcal{B}_{t}
of the indices of training examples with fixed batch size
s
is sampled from
D
, and the mini-batch gradient, which is the average gradient over
\mathcal{B}_{t}
, is computed as follows:

(2)

\displaystyle\nabla\ell_{\mathcal{B}_{t}}(\bm{\theta}_{t-1})=\frac{1}{s}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{t}}\left(S(y_{i}\bm{\theta}_{t-1}^{\top}\bm{x}_{i})-1\right)y_{i}\bm{x}_{i}.

The model is then updated as

(3)

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}_{t}=\bm{\theta}_{t-1}-\eta_{t}\nabla\ell_{\mathcal{B}_{t}}(\bm{\theta}_{t-1}),

where
t\geq 1
and
\eta_{t}
represents the learning rate. We assume
s
divides
n
, so there are
m:=n/s
iterations in one epoch. The algorithm is executed for
e
epochs and there will be
T:=me
iterations in total.

First-order Taylor Approximation.

Since the sigmoid function is non-polynomial, and the operations of exponential and logarithmic functions are cryptographically computational-inefficient, a natural solution is to use simple functions to approximate the gradient. There are several standard methods to approximate Eq. (
2
), such as first-order Taylor approximation
(
Aono
et al. 2016
;
Hardy et al. 2017
)
, minimax approximation
(
Chen et al. 2018
)
and piecewise approximation
(
Mohassel and
Zhang 2017
)
. It turns out that the specific choice of approximation scheme has minimal effect on the privacy analysis. Therefore, we will focus on the first one throughout the main body of this paper, and leave the analyses of other approximation methods to Appendix
E
and
F
.

Now, doing Taylor’s expansion of
S(z)
at
z=0
, we have

\displaystyle S(z)=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{4}z+O(z^{2}).

Therefore, the gradient in Eq. (
2
) can be approximated as

(4)

\displaystyle\nabla\ell_{\mathcal{B}_{t}}(\bm{\theta}_{t-1})\approx\frac{1}{4s}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{t}}f_{i,t}\bm{x}_{i},

where we define the coefficients

(5)

\displaystyle f_{i,t}:=\bm{\theta}^{\top}_{t-1}\bm{x}_{i}-2y_{i},\ \forall t{\in[T]},\ \forall i\in[n].

Vertical Logistic Regression.

We start with two-party vertical logistic regression (the multi-party case will be discussed at the end of Section
4
). Suppose
A
and
B
vertically share a group of data — formally, given a sample
(\bm{x},y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\{-1,1\}
, the feature
\bm{x}
is split into
\bm{x}^{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{A}}
and
\bm{x}^{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{B}}
, where
\bm{x}=\left(\begin{array}[]{@{}c@{}}\bm{x}^{A}\\
\hline\cr\bm{x}^{B}\end{array}\right)
,
d=d_{A}+d_{B}
,
(\bm{x}^{A},y)
belongs to Party
A
, and
\bm{x}^{B}
belongs to Party
B
. Now, both parties want to jointly optimize a binary logistic regression model using mini-batch gradient descent. Since we are considering a linear model, the separation in feature naturally induces a separation in parameter, i.e. the model parameter
\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is also split into
\bm{\theta}^{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{A}}
and
\bm{\theta}^{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{B}}
, belonging to Party
A
and Party
B
respectively. The gradients of both parties at each iteration then write as

(6)

\displaystyle\nabla^{A}_{t}=\frac{1}{4s}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{t}}f_{i,t}\bm{x}^{A}_{{i}}\quad\text{and}\quad\nabla^{B}_{t}=\frac{1}{4s}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{t}}f_{i,t}\bm{x}^{B}_{{i}}.

Data Assumption.

We assume that the data
\{\bm{x}_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
are sampled from a probability distribution which is continuous w.r.t. the Lebesgue measure over
\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}
, and that
\|\bm{x}_{i}\|_{2}\leq 1,\ \forall i\in[n]
. These assumptions are canonical in the machine learning literature.

2.2.
Threat Models

In this subsection, we formally state the key ingredients of our threat models. We use two parties
A
and
B
for illustration and consider both the
passive
adversarial model (see Section
3
and
4
) and the
active
adversarial model (see Section
5
).

Adversary’s objective.

At a time during training, the main goal of the label party (Party
A
) is to identify the feature of the non-label party (Party
B
); whereas the main goal of a non-label Party is to recover the hidden label from the label party.

Passive Adversarial Model.

In the passive adversarial model (a.k.a. the honest-but-curious model), a corrupted party (Party
A
or Party
B
) correctly follows the protocol specification and the adversary obtains the input and internal state of the corrupted party
(
Hazay and Lindell 2010
)
. We make two key assumptions within this threat model: 1) first of all, each party is capable of obtaining his own share of gradient (
\nabla^{A}_{t}
or
\nabla^{B}_{t}
) at each iteration — we abstract such ability as an oracle and ignore the details of implementation for now; 2) second, the non-label party holds the information about the initialization scheme (not necessarily the precise value), i.e. how
\bm{\theta}_{0}
is initialized.

Active Adversarial Model.

In the active adversarial model, a corrupted party can arbitrarily deviate from the protocol specification, according to the adversary’s instructions
(
Hazay and Lindell 2010
)
. In this adversarial model, we focus on an
instantiation
of computing the gradients
\nabla^{A}_{t}
and
\nabla^{B}_{t}
based on HE
(
Authors 2019b
;
Hardy et al. 2017
)
, and show how the adversary can obtain more information by modifying the original protocol. Such active attack is expected to be stronger than the ones proposed in the passive adversarial model, as will be seen in Section
5
.

Publicly-available information.

The
mini-batch
and the
learning rate
used in each iteration are known to all participants — combined with the assumption in the passive adversarial model, this ensures that both parties can do local updates on their own share of model parameters.

3.
Privacy Analysis for Party
B

As an adversary, Party
A
seeks to obtain information about the feature
\bm{x}_{B}
. At
t
-th iteration,
A
receives his own gradient

(7)

\displaystyle\nabla^{A}_{t}=\frac{1}{4s}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{t}}f_{i,t}\bm{x}^{A}_{i},

which is the linear combination on his own share of feature. We will restrict our discussion to
s\leq d_{A}
, and resort to a useful result in linear algebra.

Theorem 3.1.

Suppose
\{\bm{z}_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
is randomly sampled from a probability distribution which is continuous w.r.t. the Lebesgue measure over
\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}\times n}
and we want to solve
a_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
, then
\forall I\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}},

\forall S\subseteq D
such that
|S|=s
, the linear system

(8)

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in S}a_{i}\bm{z}_{i}=\bm{I}

has at most one solution with probability one if
s\leq d^{\prime}
.

The proof is mainly based on the fact that degenerate matrix has zero Lebesgue measure, and the details are deferred to Appendix
A
. As a direct consequence, we immediately have the following corollary.

Corollary 3.2.

If
s\leq d_{A}
, then
\forall t
, Party
A
can uniquely determine the values of
f_{i,t}
for all
i\in{\mathcal{B}}_{t}
with probability one.

Proof.

The uniqueness follows from Theorem
3.1
, and the fact that
\{\bm{x}_{A,i}\}_{i\in[n]}
also follows a continuous probability distribution. The existence is trivial.
∎

Saying in another way, Corollary
3.2
allows Party
A
to determine the coefficients of the linear system Eq. (
7
). Now denote

(9)

\displaystyle g_{i,t}:=\bm{\theta}_{t-1}^{\top}\bm{x}_{i}

and decompose

(10)

\displaystyle g_{i,t}=\underbrace{(\bm{\theta}_{t-1}^{A})^{\top}\bm{x}^{A}_{i}}_{g^{A}_{i,t}}+\underbrace{(\bm{\theta}_{t-1}^{B})^{\top}\bm{x}^{B}_{i}}_{g^{B}_{i,t}}.

After obtaining the coefficient

\displaystyle f_{i,t}=g_{i,t}-2y_{i},

Party
A
can extract the information known to himself (which is
g^{A}_{i,t}-2y_{i}
), and obtain the values of
g_{i,t}^{B}
, which is exactly the inner product of the data
\bm{x}
and the parameter
\bm{\theta}
on
B
’s feature. Therefore, in the special case
d_{B}=1
, Party
A
can determine the
order
of the data according to the
x_{B}
feature in each batch.

Lemma 3.3.

Suppose
s\leq d_{A}
and
d_{B}=1
, then Party
A
can learn the ratio of the data in
{\mathcal{B}}_{t}
in terms of feature
x^{B}
.

Proof.

For
i,j\in{\mathcal{B}}_{t}
, Party
A
can compute the ratio of
x_{B,i}
and
x_{B,j}
via calculating
g_{i,t}^{B}/g_{j,t}^{B}
. Therefore, he will be able to sort the data in
{\mathcal{B}}_{t}
in terms of feature
x^{B}
.
∎

There are two special cases of Lemma
3.3
: when
s=n
, Party
A
directly obtains the order of the full dataset
D
; when
s=1
, there is essentially
no
sorting and thus Party
A
can obtain
no
information about the feature
x_{B}
. When
2\leq s\leq n-1
, in order to obtain the full order, we need a few epochs to make different batches ‘intertwine’ with each other, as shown in the following lemma.

Lemma 3.4.

Under the same condition as in Lemma
3.3
, and we further assume
s\geq 2
. Then Party
A
can learn the ratio of the dataset
D
in terms of feature
x_{B}
in at most
\lfloor\log_{2}(m)\rfloor+1
epochs with probability at least
1-\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{m^{s-1}})
.

The proof of Lemma
3.4
is deferred to Appendix
A
. Now, after
\lfloor\log_{2}(m)\rfloor+1
epochs, Party
A
will be able to learn the ratio between different data points on
B
’s feature:

(11)

\displaystyle\frac{x^{B}_{i}}{x^{B}_{1}}:=r_{i},\ 2\leq i\leq n.

Without loss of generality, assume
1\in\mathcal{B}_{1}
and
1\in\mathcal{B}_{k}
for some
m+1\leq k\leq 2m
. By comparing
g_{1,k}
and
g_{1,1}
— the inner products of the same feature
x_{1}^{B}
and
B
’s parameter
\theta^{B}
at different iterations, we immediately have the following theorem.

Theorem 3.5.

Suppose
s\leq d_{A}
and
d_{B}=1
, then Party
A
can learn the precise value of the data in terms of feature
x^{B}
(up to a difference in sign) in at most
\max\{\lfloor\log_{2}(m)\rfloor+1,2\}
epochs with probability at least
1-\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{m^{s-1}})
.

Proof.

By the update formula of
\theta^{B}_{k}
, we have

\displaystyle g_{1,k}-g_{1,1}

\displaystyle=\theta^{B}_{k-1}x^{B}_{1}-\theta^{B}_{0}x^{B}_{1}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{t=1}^{k-1}\frac{\eta_{t}}{4s}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{B}_{t}}(\theta_{t-1}{x}_{j}-2y_{j})x^{B}_{j}x^{B}_{1}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{t=1}^{k-1}\frac{\eta_{t}}{4s}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{B}_{t}}f_{j,t}r_{j}(x^{B}_{1})^{2}

(12)

\displaystyle=-\left[\sum_{t=1}^{k-1}\left(\frac{\eta_{t}}{4s}\sum_{i\in D_{t}}f_{j,t}r_{j}\right)\right](x^{B}_{1})^{2}.

Therefore,

(13)

\displaystyle x^{B}_{1}=\pm\sqrt{\frac{4s(g_{1,1}-g_{1,k})}{\sum\limits_{t=1}^{k-1}\sum\limits_{j\in\mathcal{B}_{t}}f_{j,t}r_{j}\eta_{t}}}

and
x^{B}_{i}=r_{i}x^{B}_{1}
, where
r_{i}
is defined in Eq. (
11
).
∎

When
d_{B}\geq 2
, we provide a hardness result: by simply observing
\{g_{i,t}^{B}\}_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{t},t\in[T]}
, Party
A
cannot learn
B
’s feature in general. The main observation is that an orthogonal transformation would not change the coefficients obtained during training.

Theorem 3.6.

Assume Party
A
does not know the precise value of
\bm{\theta}^{B}_{0}
and
d_{B}\geq 2
. Fix the mini-batch and learning rate used in each iteration, then there are infinitely many choices of
\{\bm{x}_{i}^{B}\}_{i\in[n]}
that yield the same set of
\{g_{i,t}^{B}\}_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{t},t\in[T]}
through the optimization process. As a consequence,
A
cannot determine the precise value of
B
’s feature.

Proof.

Denote
O(d_{B},\mathbb{R})
as the orthogonal group in dimension
d_{B}
. Since
A
does not hold the information of
\bm{\theta}_{0}^{B}
, we can pick an arbitrary
\bm{Q}\in O(d_{B},\mathbb{R})
and consider the following transformation

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}_{t-1}^{B}\mapsto\bm{Q}\bm{\theta}_{t-1}^{B},\ \forall t\in[T]\ \ \text{and}\ \ \bm{x}^{B}_{i}\mapsto\bm{Q}\bm{x}^{B}_{i},\ \forall i\in[n].

By the update formula on
\bm{\theta}^{B}
, we have

(14)

\displaystyle\bm{Q}\bm{\theta}_{t}^{B}=\bm{Q}\bm{\theta}_{t-1}^{B}-\frac{1}{4s}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{t}}(\bm{\theta}_{t-1}^{\top}\bm{x}_{i}-2y_{i})\bm{Q}\bm{x}_{i}^{B},

which is equivalent to

\displaystyle\bm{Q}\bm{\theta}_{t}^{B}=\bm{Q}\bm{\theta}_{t-1}^{B}-\frac{1}{4s}

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{t}}\big((\bm{Q}\bm{\theta}_{t-1}^{B})^{\top}(\bm{Q}\bm{x}_{i}^{B})

(15)

\displaystyle+(\bm{\theta}_{t-1}^{A})^{\top}\bm{x}_{i}^{A}-2y_{i}\big)\bm{Q}\bm{x}_{i}^{B},

i.e. the mini-batch gradient descent acting on a new dataset with
\hat{\bm{x}}_{i}=\left(\begin{array}[]{@{}c@{}}\bm{x}_{i}^{A}\\
\hline\cr\bm{Q}\bm{x}_{i}^{B}\end{array}\right)
and starting from
\bm{Q}\bm{\theta}_{0}
.
Party
A
cannot distinguish the two processes by observing
g_{i,t}^{B}
, since the inner product

(16)

\displaystyle\left<\bm{Q}\bm{\theta}_{t-1}^{B},\bm{Q}\bm{x}^{B}_{i}\right>=\left<\bm{\theta}_{t-1}^{B},\bm{x}_{i}^{B}\right>

remains unchanged. Combining the fact that
O(d_{B},\mathbb{R})
has infinite cardinality when
d_{B}\geq 2
gives the result as desired.
∎

4.
Privacy Analysis for Party
A

We shall see that Party
A
and
B
are symmetric except that
B
does not hold the label. Therefore, the main goal of an adversarial
B
is to obtain information about the label
y
. At
t
-th iteration,
B
receives his own gradient

(17)

\displaystyle\nabla^{B}_{t}=\frac{1}{4s}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{t}}f_{i,t}\bm{x}^{B}_{i}

which is still the linear combination on his own share of feature. Similar to the previous section, we will restrict our discussion to
s\leq d_{B}
, implying that the linear system given by Eq. (
17
) is not underdetermined. As a corollary of Theorem
3.1
, Party
B
also holds the value of

(18)

\displaystyle f_{i,t}=(\bm{\theta}_{t-1}^{A})^{\top}\bm{x}^{A}_{i}+(\bm{\theta}_{t-1}^{B})^{\top}\bm{x}^{B}_{i}-2y_{i}

for
t\in[T]
and
i\in\mathcal{B}_{t}
. In the right hand side, Party
B
does not know about the first and the third term, so at first sight it seems unlikely for him to gain information about the label. However, we will show in the following analysis that label attack would still be possible under certain circumstances.

We first consider a special case where Party
A

only
holds the label, meaning that Party
B
have full control of the model.

Theorem 4.1.

Suppose
d_{A}=0
, then Party
B
can uniquely determine the label
\{y_{i}\}_{i\in S_{t}}
for arbitrary sampled batch
\mathcal{B}_{t}
with probability one.

The proof of Theorem
4.1
is deferred to Appendix
B
. Some might argue for a simpler attack in this special case: since
B
holds both the data and the model, he can directly feed the data to the trained model and use the output to ‘guess’ the label. However, there are two drawbacks of this approach: 1) it heavily relies on the model’s training accuracy, and do not have any
guarantees
on the predictions; 2) the attack can only be performed after (or at the end of) training.

Now we will consider the more realistic case
d_{A}\geq 1
. The intuition here is that
|2y_{i}|=2
, so if Party
B
can convince himself that the full inner product satisfies
|\bm{\theta}_{t-1}^{\top}\bm{x}_{i}|<2
, then

(19)

\displaystyle\operatorname{sign}(f_{i,t})=\operatorname{sign}(-2y_{i})=-y_{i},

so he can use the opposite sign of
f_{i,t}
to determine the value of
y_{i}
in the
t
-th iteration. This can be formulated into the following criterion.

Criterion 4.2.

For
t=1,\cdots,T^{*}
, Party
B
uses
-\operatorname{sign}(f_{i,t})
to determine
y_{i},\ \forall i\in\mathcal{B}_{t}
.

Remark 4.3.

If Criterion
4.2
is valid for
T^{*}\geq m
, then Party
B
can obtain the label of the full dataset
D
.

Now the analysis naturally breaks into two parts:

•

Show when Criterion
4.2
is valid for
T^{*}=1
;

•

Determine the largest
T^{*}
that we can
safely
apply the criterion.

We will first deal with the second part. Define
h_{t}=\max\limits_{i}\{|\bm{\theta}_{t-1}^{\top}\bm{x}_{i}|+2\}
and we will establish a recursive relation of
h_{t}
in the following lemma.

Lemma 4.4.

Suppose we use a constant learning rate
\eta_{t}=\eta
in mini-batch gradient descent. Then we have

(20)

\displaystyle h_{t+1}\leq\left(1+\frac{\eta}{4}\right)h_{t}.

Proof.

By the update formula Eq. (
3
), for
1\leq j\leq n
we have

(21)

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\top}\bm{x}_{j}=\bm{\theta}_{t-1}^{\top}\bm{x}_{j}-\frac{\eta}{4s}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{t}}(\bm{\theta}_{t-1}^{\top}\bm{x}_{i}-2y_{i})\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{x}_{j}.

Applying Cauchy-Schwarz and triangle inequality, we have

(22)

\displaystyle\big|\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\top}\bm{x}_{j}\big|\leq\big|\bm{\theta}_{t-1}^{\top}\bm{x}_{j}\big|+\frac{\eta}{4}\cdot\frac{1}{s}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{t}}\left(\big|\bm{\theta}_{t-1}^{\top}\bm{x}_{i}\big|+2\right),

implying

(23)

\displaystyle\left(\big|\bm{\theta}_{t}^{\top}\bm{x}_{j}\big|+2\right)\leq\left(\big|\bm{\theta}_{t-1}^{\top}\bm{x}_{j}\big|+2\right)+\frac{\eta}{4s}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{t}}\left(\big|\bm{\theta}_{t-1}^{\top}\bm{x}_{i}\big|+2\right).

Taking maximum over
1\leq j\leq n
on both side, we immediately have

(24)

\displaystyle h_{t+1}\leq\left(1+\frac{\eta}{4}\right)h_{t}.

∎

As a corollary, we immediately obtain a possible choice of
T
that will be safe for the implementation of the criterion.

Corollary 4.5.

Suppose
\max\limits_{i}\{|\bm{\theta}_{0}^{\top}\bm{x}_{i}|\}=\epsilon<2
. Then Criterion
4.2
is valid for

(25)

\displaystyle T_{\epsilon}=\bigg\lceil\log_{\left(1+\frac{\eta}{4}\right)}\left(\frac{4}{2+\epsilon}\right)\bigg\rceil.

Proof.

We have
h_{1}=2+\epsilon
and the criterion fails with threshold
4
. By Lemma
4.4
, the criterion is safe for the first
\bigg\lceil\log_{\left(1+\frac{\eta}{4}\right)}\left(\frac{4}{2+\epsilon}\right)\bigg\rceil
iterations.
∎

We now come back to the first part. Party
B
need to determine whether Criterion
4.2
is valid for
t=1
,
i.e.

\max\limits_{i}\{|\bm{\theta}_{0}^{\top}\bm{x}_{i}|\}<2
based on his knowledge of the initialization scheme. We will discuss several possible choices of initialization.

Deterministic Initialization.

Suppose
\bm{\theta}_{0}
is initialized to be a fixed vector. If

(26)

\displaystyle\max\limits_{i}\{|\bm{\theta}_{0}^{\top}\bm{x}_{i}|\}=\epsilon<2,

then by Corollary
4.5
, we can set
T^{*}=T_{\epsilon}
in Criterion
4.2
. As a special case, we have

Theorem 4.6.

If we apply Zero Initialization
(
Tripathi
et al. 2018
)
to
\bm{\theta}_{0}
, then Criterion
4.2
is valid for

(27)

\displaystyle T_{0}=\bigg\lceil\log_{\left(1+\frac{\eta}{4}\right)}2\bigg\rceil.

Small Random Initialization.

This is a common strategy in modern machine learning problems to ensure an
O(1)
output, so that optimization can be significantly boosted. Common initialization schemes such as
Xavier Initialization

(
Glorot and Bengio 2010
)
and
Kaiming Initialization

(

all fall into this category. Formally, since

i.e.

\mathbb{E}\theta_{0,j}^{2}=O\left(\frac{1}{d}\right),\ \ \forall j\in[d]
. To achieve this, we set
\gamma
to be a mean-zero sub-exponential random variableand initialized each element of
\bm{\theta}_{0}

i.i.d.
through
\gamma/\sqrt{d}
. We then have the following lemma.

Lemma 4.7.

Suppose
\|\gamma\|_{\psi_{1}}=l
, then with probability at least

(28)

\displaystyle\big|\bm{\theta}_{0}^{\top}\bm{x}_{i}\big|<\frac{l}{c}d^{-\frac{1}{4}},\ \ \ \ \ \forall i\in[n],

where

is an absolute constant.

Proof.

For a fixed
i\in[n]

\|\bm{x}_{i}\|_{2}\leq 1
and
\|\bm{x}_{i}\|_{\infty}\leq 1

(

), we have

\displaystyle\ \ \ \Pr\left(\bigg|\sum_{j=1}^{d}\theta_{0,j}x_{i,j}\bigg|\geq\epsilon\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left[-c\min\left(\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{L^{2}\|\bm{x}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}},\frac{\epsilon}{L\|\bm{x}_{i}\|_{\infty}}\right)\right]

(29)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left[-c\min\left(\frac{d\epsilon^{2}}{l^{2}},\frac{\sqrt{d}\epsilon}{l}\right)\right].

Setting
\epsilon=\frac{l}{c}d^{-\frac{1}{4}}
, we have with probability at most

(30)

\displaystyle|\bm{\theta}_{0}^{\top}\bm{x}_{i}|\geq\frac{l}{c}d^{-\frac{1}{4}}.

Taking a union bound over

yields the result as desired.
∎

Together with Corollary
4.5
, we immediately have

Theorem 4.8.

Suppose each element of
\bm{\theta}_{0}

\displaystyle T_{l}=\bigg\lceil\log_{\left(1+\frac{\eta}{4}\right)}\left(\frac{4c}{2c+ld^{-\frac{1}{4}}}\right)\bigg\rceil,

Remark 4.9.

Suppose Party

Large Random Initialization.

The strategy is to initialize each element of
\bm{\theta}_{0}
to be

e.g.\

Suppose

\displaystyle\max_{i}\{|\bm{\theta}_{0}^{\top}\bm{x}_{i}|\}=\epsilon<2,

\displaystyle T_{\epsilon}=\bigg\lceil\log_{\left(1+\frac{\eta}{4}\right)}\left(\frac{4}{2+\epsilon}\right)\bigg\rceil

5.
Protocol-aware Active Attack

For the privacy analysis in Section
3
and
4
, we assume the existence of an oracle such that both parties can obtain their local gradients, and ignore the details of practical implementation. In this section, we will describe the protocol through which both parties can cooperatively learn their own share of gradient, without violating the privacy of individual instance
directly
. Further, by modifying the protocol maliciously, both parties can relax the constraints of batch size as appears in the previous sections, and therefore are able to perform the same attack in more general scenarios. We refer to this as ‘
active attack
’.

5.1.
Protocol of Obtaining Gradient

We will discuss how both parties collaboratively obtain
\nabla^{A}_{t}
, and the procedure for computing
\nabla^{B}_{t}
shares a similar philosophy. Aside from the information known to himself, Party
A
needs to compute

via the assistance from Party

. Also, Party
B
cannot directly send the value of
g_{i,t}^{B}
to Party
A
as it will violate the privacy of individual
i
.

Note
\bm{H}^{A}_{t}
is essentially a linear combination of the
\bm{x}^{A}
feature, with the coefficients being unknown. Therefore, a natural solution to the above problem is (additively) homomorphic encryption, such as Paillier
(
Paillier 1999
)
and OU
(
Okamoto and
Uchiyama 1998
)
. Here we will resort to the former one. In Paillier cryptosystem, there exists a pair of keys
pk_{B}
and

, where both parties can use

to encrypt a plaintext

as its corresponding ciphertext
[\![a]\!]
, but only Party

can use

to decrypt
[\![a]\!]
and get
a
, which we denote as

. For vector
\bm{v}
, we will use
[\![\bm{v}]\!]
to denote element-wise encryption and similarly
\bm{D}
for decryption.

The most notable property of
[\![\cdot]\!]
is additively homomorphic, i.e.
[\![a]\!]+[\![b]\!]=[\![a+b]\!]
and

and

While constructing attacks in Section

6.1.
DP-Paillier-MGD

Let us begin with the formal definition of Differential Privacy (DP).

Definition 6.1 (Differential Privacy).

A randomized algorithm
\mathcal{A}
with domain
\mathcal{D}
and range
\mathcal{R}
is
(\epsilon,\delta)
-differentially private if for all
\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{R}
and
for all
D_{1},D_{2}\in\mathcal{D}
that differ by at most one instance,

\displaystyle\Pr(\mathcal{A}(D_{1})\in\mathcal{S})\leq\exp{(\epsilon)}\Pr(\mathcal{A}(D_{2})\in\mathcal{S})+\delta.

DP is usually realized via
randomization
: making the output undeterministic by adding noise. Here we set the noise vector to be Gaussian, which has been widely adopted in practice
(
Abadi et al. 2016
;
Bassily
et al. 2014
)
.

Definition 6.2 (Gaussian Mechanism).

Given
f:\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the Gaussian Mechanism with parameter
\sigma
is defined as:

(40)

\displaystyle M_{G}\left(D,f(\cdot),\sigma\right):=f(D)+(Y_{1},\cdots,Y_{d})^{\top},

where
D\in\mathcal{D}
and
Y_{i}
are i.i.d. random variables drawn from
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
.

An important caveat here is that we will not resort to the original definition of DP — which is interpreted as a privacy guarantee regarding the
membership inference attack
— because Party
A
and
B
need to align their user ID before initializing the procedure of VFL and hence hold the data records in the training set. Instead, we seek to leverage the effect of
obfuscation
brought along by the noise-adding mechanism, to ensure that DP also serves as a good countermeasure for the attacks proposed in the previous sections.

Specifically, the strategy for Party
B
is to mask the sensitive information, and therefore Party
A
cannot learn the
precise
value of
g_{i,t}^{B}
after solving the linear system. Similarly, Party

will also mask the sensitive information

before encryption. Denote

6.2.1.
Utility Analysis

We begin ourselves on the utility side and provide a convergence analysis for Algorithm
3
under the noise level
\sigma_{A}
and
\sigma_{B}
. Note that under the first-order approximation scheme, the original update on

can be treated as mini-batch gradient descent of the following loss function:

(43)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta}):=\frac{1}{8n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\bm{\theta}^{\top}\bm{x}_{i}-2y_{i})^{2},

with the mini-batch gradient
\nabla\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}_{t}}(\bm{\theta}_{t-1})
identical to the one defined in Eq. (
4
). After injecting noise in Algorithm
3
, the update on
\bm{\theta}
becomes

(44)

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}_{t}=\bm{\theta}_{t-1}-\eta_{t}\left(\nabla\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}_{t}}(\bm{\theta}_{t-1})+{\bm{e}_{t}}\right),

where the gradient error is

\displaystyle\bm{e}_{t}:=\frac{1}{4s}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\bm{X}_{\mathcal{B}_{t}}^{A}\bm{Z}^{B}\\
\bm{X}_{\mathcal{B}_{t}}^{B}\bm{Z}^{A}\end{array}\right).

Our first step is to control the magnitude of
\bm{e}_{t}
, which is mainly done by a standard concentration inequality and deferred to Appendix

Lemma 6.3.

Denote
\sigma:=\max\{\sigma_{A},\sigma_{B}\}
. Given
k\geq 1
, we have
\|\bm{e}_{t}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{\frac{k}{s}}\sigma
for
t\in[T]
with probability at least

, where

is an absolute constant.

With Lemma
6.3
at hand, we are now ready to state the utility guarantee of Algorithm
3
. It is basically a combination of the standard mirror descent argument, as well as a martingale trick to control the stacking of noise over the training horizon.

Theorem 6.4.

Let
\bm{\theta}^{*}
be an arbitrary reference point. Assume

(47)

\displaystyle\max\limits_{t\in[T]}\|\bm{\theta}_{t-1}-\bm{\theta}^{*}\|_{2}\leq C,

then with step size
\eta={\mathcal{O}}(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T\ln T}})
, Algorithm
3
satisfies

(48)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t\in[T]}\bm{\theta}_{t-1}\right)\leq\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta}^{*})+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{u+1}{\sqrt{T}}\right)

with probability at least
1-2\delta
, where
u:=\sqrt{\frac{\ln(2T/\delta)}{cs}}\sigma
.

Proof.

Let
\delta=2Te^{-ck}
in Lemma
6.3
, we have with failure probability at most
\delta
,

(49)

\displaystyle\|\bm{e}_{t}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{\frac{k}{s}}\sigma=\sqrt{\frac{\ln(2T/\delta)}{cs}}=u

for
t\in[T]
. Now denote

(50)

\displaystyle\bm{g}_{t}:=\nabla\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}_{t}}(\bm{\theta}_{t-1})\ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ \bm{G}_{t}:=\nabla\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta}_{t-1}).

Since
\|\nabla\mathcal{L}_{i}(\bm{\theta}_{t-1})\|_{2}\leq G
, we have
\|\bm{g}_{t}\|_{2},\|\bm{G}_{t}\|_{2}\leq G
by triangle inequality. Now for
t\in[T]
, consider

(51)

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \|\bm{\theta}_{t}-\bm{\theta}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\bm{\theta}_{t-1}-\bm{\theta}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}+2\eta\langle\bm{\theta}^{*}-\bm{\theta}_{t-1},\bm{g}_{t}+\bm{e}_{t}-\bm{G}_{t}\rangle

(52)

\displaystyle\ \ \ +2\eta\langle\bm{\theta}^{*}-\bm{\theta}_{t-1},\bm{G}_{t}\rangle+\eta^{2}\|\bm{g}_{t}+\bm{e}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|\bm{\theta}_{t-1}-\bm{\theta}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}+2\eta\underbrace{\langle\bm{\theta}^{*}-\bm{\theta}_{t-1},\bm{g}_{t}+\bm{e}_{t}-\bm{G}_{t}\rangle}_{\epsilon_{t}}

(53)

\displaystyle\ \ \ +2\eta(\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta}^{*})-\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta}_{t-1}))+2\eta^{2}\left(G+\|\bm{e}_{t}\|_{2}\right)^{2},

where we use the convexity of
\mathcal{L}
in the first inequality. Rearranging both side, we have

(54)

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta}_{t-1})

(55)

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta}^{*})+\frac{\|\bm{\theta}_{t-1}-\bm{\theta}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}-\|\bm{\theta}_{t}-\bm{\theta}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}}{2\eta}+\frac{\eta}{2}\left(G+u\right)^{2}+\epsilon_{t}.

Now apply
\frac{1}{T}\sum\limits_{t\in[T]}
to both sides, and note the convexity of
\mathcal{L}
again, we have

(56)

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \mathcal{L}\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t\in[T]}\bm{\theta}_{t-1}\right)

(57)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{T}\sum\limits_{t\in[T]}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta}_{t-1})

(58)

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta}^{*})+\frac{\|\bm{\theta}_{0}-\bm{\theta}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}}{2\eta T}+\frac{\eta}{2}\left(G+u\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t\in[T]}\epsilon_{t}.

Setting
\eta=\frac{\|\bm{\theta}_{0}-\bm{\theta}^{*}\|_{2}}{(G+u)\sqrt{T}}
, we have

(59)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t\in[T]}\bm{\theta}_{t-1}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta}^{*})+\frac{(G+u)C}{\sqrt{T}}+\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t\in[T]}\epsilon_{t}.

Finally, it suffices to control the sum of error term
\sum_{t\in[T]}\epsilon_{t}
. Note

(60)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\epsilon_{t}\mid\bm{\theta}_{<t}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\langle\bm{\theta}^{*}-\bm{\theta}_{t-1},\bm{g}_{t}+\bm{e}_{t}-\bm{G}_{t}\rangle\mid\bm{\theta}_{<t}]

(61)

\displaystyle=\big<\mathbb{E}[\bm{g}_{t}+\bm{e}_{t}-\bm{G}_{t}\mid\bm{\theta}_{<t}],\bm{\theta}^{*}-\bm{\theta}_{t-1}\big>

(62)

\displaystyle=0,

so
\{\epsilon_{t}\}_{t\in[T]}
is a martingale difference sequence. Further, by Cauchy-Schwarz and triangle inequality we have

(63)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|\epsilon_{t}|

\displaystyle\leq\|\bm{\theta}^{*}-\bm{\theta}_{t-1}\|_{2}(\|\bm{g}_{t}\|_{2}+\|\bm{G}_{t}\|_{2}+\|\bm{e}_{t}\|_{2})

(64)

\displaystyle\leq(2G+u)C.

Therefore, by Azuma-Hoeffding inequality (Corollary 2.20 in
(
Wainwright 2019
)
) we have

(65)

\displaystyle\sum_{t\in[T]}\epsilon_{t}\leq(2G+u)C\sqrt{2T\ln(1/\delta)}

with probability at least
1-\delta
. Plugging Eq. (
65
) into Eq. (
59
) gives the result as desired.
∎

Remark 6.5.

The error in the right hand side consists of two parts: the first term is

6.2.2.
Privacy Analysis

We will now turn to the privacy side. As mentioned at the beginning of this section, we will not argue that Algorithm
3
is
(\epsilon,\delta)
-differentially private, mainly because this is usually seen as a privacy guarantee regarding membership inference attack. Therefore, we choose the upper bound on

We focus on the label recovery attack as the feature attack is expected to fail, since it relies on the precise value of the sensitive information and therefore can be easily broken by noise, whereas the label recovery attack only requires the sensitive information to fall in a certain range. Note such upper bound might not accurately reflect practice as we are actually considering the

In this section, we implement vertical logistic regression on practical problems including classifying digits 0 and 1 in MNIST dataset

Data.

MNIST is a dataset of the images of handwritten digits from 0 to 9. For evaluating vertical logistic regression, we take all the 0, 1 from MNIST and construct a binary classification problem. We assign label -1 to digit 0 and label 1 to digit 1 in the binary classification problem. There are 5923 training samples and 980 test samples for digit 0; 6742 training samples and 1135 test samples for digit 1, respectively. UCL default of credit card clients Data Set (abbreviated as credit) contains information on default payments, demographic factors, credit data, history of payment, and bill statements of credit card clients in Taiwan from April 2005 to September 2005. There are 30000 samples and we randomly split them into a training set of 27000 samples and a test set of 3000 samples. Each sample has a 23-dimensional feature and a binary label. We standardize the features of the credit dataset before processing logistic regression.

Learning Setting.

For both datasets, we consider the vertical logistic regression where Party A can access the first half of the features and Party B can access the last half. The batch size is set to 256. Note here we focus on the validity of Criterion
4.2
, which only involves the value of the full inner product, hence independent of the choices of batch size or the splitting ratio. We mildly tune the learning rate from

Experiment results on credit dataset with a highlight on the comparison between different initialization schemes. The success rate of label attack is less than 1 for Xavier Initialization on credit dataset because the feature dimension is small (23 dimensions) so that the scale of Xavier Initialization is large.

8.
Conclusion

In this paper, we provide a formal privacy analysis for VLR, under an oracle of obtaining local gradients at each iteration within the honest-but-curious threat model. When the linear system is uniquely solvable, i.e.
s\leq d_{A}
or
s\leq d_{B}
, we 1) identify feature attack for Party
A
when
d_{B}=1
and provide hardness result when
d_{B}\geq 2
; 2) construct label recovery attack for Party
B
when the initialization is small. By taking into account the protocol of obtaining gradient, which is mainly based on Homomorphic Encryption, we propose an active attack via generating and compressing auxiliary ciphertext and thereby relax the constraints of batch size. Since increasing the batch size might not be adequate to addressing the privacy leakage, we adopt a simple-yet-effective countermeasure based on DP, which injects noise to the sensitive information before communication. We provide both utility and privacy guarantees for the updated algorithm. Experiment results on benchmark datasets demonstrate the effectiveness of our attack and defense, indicating the vulnerability of existing frameworks as well as the power of coupling DP with HE techniques.

To be fair, other forms of attacks certainly exist (either simpler or being much more sophisticated) beyond those proposed in this paper. For example, it is possible that when the feature dimension is small, inference attacks using model’s predictions could reveal partial information about the data. It is also possible that some advanced techniques (such as gradient inversion
(
Yin et al. 2021
)
) could allow the adversary to recover much more information about the data. However, our goal in this paper is to analyze the existence of privacy leakage in a
provable, mathematically-rigorous
manner. A desirable answer should either be ‘Yes’, as suggested in Theorem
3.5
,
4.1
and
4.8
, or ‘No’, as pointed out by Theorem
3.6
. Saying in another way, we are trying to uncover the most intrinsic and fundamental vulnerability of the VLR systems that resort to the gradient oracle. We believe this is exactly what separates our paper from previous works.

In all, our work suggests that all VFL frameworks that solely rely on HE might contain severe privacy risks, and DP, being an important building block of horizontal federated learning, can also exert its power in the vertical setting. We hypothesize that a
hybrid
protocol that makes use of both HE (or MPC) and DP would lead to the best practical performance.
From a broader perspective, we hope this work would inspire more works to focus on the (rigorous) privacy analysis of general VFL beyond logistic regression, which could lead to substantial improvement over existing algorithms and protocols.

Appendix F
Discussion of Piecewise Approximation

Another commonly used approximation method of the sigmoid function is the piecewise linear approximation
(
Mohassel and
Zhang 2017
)
:

(141)

S(z)\approx\begin{cases}0,&z\leq-0.5\\
z+0.5,&|z|<0.5\\
1,&z\geq 0.5\end{cases}.

The mini-batch gradient can then be approximated as

(142)

\displaystyle\nabla\ell_{\mathcal{B}_{t}}(\bm{\theta}_{t-1})\approx\frac{1}{s}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{t}}f_{i,t}\bm{x}_{i}

at
t
-th iteration, where the coefficient is defined through

\displaystyle f_{i,t}:=

\displaystyle\mathbb{1}[|\bm{\theta}_{t-1}^{\top}\bm{x}_{i}|<0.5](\bm{\theta}_{t-1}^{\top}\bm{x}_{i}-0.5y_{i})

(143)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\mathbb{1}[y_{i}\bm{\theta}_{t-1}^{\top}\bm{x}_{i}\leq-0.5](-y_{i}).

We assume
s\leq\min\{d_{A},d_{B}\}
in the following analyses, so that both parties can learn the precise value of
f_{i,t}
with probability one by Theorem
3.1
.

F.1.
Privacy Analysis for Party
B

Note that

(144)

|f_{i,t}|=\begin{cases}1&\ \text{if}\ \ y_{i}\bm{\theta}_{t-1}^{\top}\bm{x}_{i}\leq-0.5\\
|\bm{\theta}_{t-1}^{\top}\bm{x}_{i}-0.5y_{i}|\in(0,1)&\ \text{if}\ \ |\bm{\theta}_{t-1}^{\top}\bm{x}_{i}|<0.5\\
0&\ \text{if}\ \ y_{i}\bm{\theta}_{t-1}^{\top}\bm{x}_{i}\geq 0.5\end{cases}

Decompose

(145)

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}_{t-1}^{\top}\bm{x}_{i}=\underbrace{\left(\bm{\theta}^{A}_{t-1}\right)^{\top}\bm{x}^{A}_{i}}_{g^{A}_{i,t}}+\underbrace{\left(\bm{\theta}^{B}_{t-1}\right)^{\top}\bm{x}^{B}_{i}}_{g^{B}_{i,t}}.

then there are three possible cases:

(1)

|f_{\bm{i},t}|=1.
Then Party
A
can learn the
range
of
g_{i,t}^{B}

(146)

g_{i,t}^{B}\begin{cases}\leq-0.5-g_{i,t}^{A},&\ \ \text{if}\ \ y=1,\\
\geq 0.5-g_{i,t}^{A},&\ \ \text{if}\ \ y=-1.\end{cases}

(2)

0<|f_{i,t}|<1.
Then Party
A
can learn the
precise value
of
g_{i,t}^{B}=f_{i,t}+0.5y_{i}-g_{i,t}^{A}
.

(3)

f_{i,t}=0.
Then Party
A
can learn the
range
of
g_{i,t}^{B}

(147)

g_{i,t}^{B}\begin{cases}\geq 0.5-g_{i,t}^{A},&\ \ \text{if}\ \ y=1,\\
\leq-0.5-g_{i,t}^{A},&\ \ \text{if}\ \ y=-1.\end{cases}

Therefore, if Party
A
collect those instance which falls into the second case:

(148)

\displaystyle D_{t}=\{i\in\mathcal{B}_{t}:0<|f_{i,t}|<1\},

then under
d_{B}=1
, he can sort the data in
D_{t}
according to the
x_{B}
feature. Also, similar to the previous method, we have the following theorem.

Theorem F.1.

In the piecewise approximation scheme, suppose
s\leq d_{A}
and
d_{B}=1
, and
D
becomes sortable in
R
epochs. Then Party
A
can learn the precise value of the data in terms of feature
x_{B}
(up to a difference in sign) in
\max\{R,2\}

F.2.
Privacy Analysis for Party
A

In analogy to Theorem
4.1
, Party
B
