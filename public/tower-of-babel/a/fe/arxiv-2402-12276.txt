Explain then Rank: Scale Calibration of Neural Rankers Using Natural Language Explanations from LLMs
Puxuan Yu, Daniel Cohen, Hemank Lamba, Joel Tetreault, Alex Jaimes

Abstract
In search settings, calibrating the scores during the ranking process to quantities such as click-through rates or relevance levels enhances a system’s usefulness and trustworthiness for downstream users. While previous research has improved this notion of calibration for low complexity learning-to-rank models, the larger data demands and parameter count specific to modern neural text rankers produce unique obstacles that hamper the efficacy of methods intended for the learning-to-rank setting. This paper proposes exploiting large language models (LLMs) to provide relevance and uncertainty signals for these neural text rankers to produce scale-calibrated scores through Monte Carlo sampling of natural language explanations (NLEs). Our approach transforms the neural ranking task from ranking textual query-document pairs to ranking corresponding synthesized NLEs. Comprehensive experiments on two popular document ranking datasets show that the NLE-based calibration approach consistently outperforms past calibration methods and LLM-based methods for ranking, calibration, and query performance prediction tasks.

Introduction

Figure 1:
The key idea of this study: Neural ranking models struggle to produce
meaningful
ranking scores when encountering complex query-document pairs. We investigate the integration of natural language explanations as inputs to neural rankers, aiming to simplify the scale-calibrated ranking task for these rankers.

Neural ranking models act as the core component of many search systems, often producing the final document scores. However, these scores are usually treated as transient information and only the relative orderings are preserved to produce a ranking. While this approach results in well-performing systems with respect to common retrieval metrics, such as nDCG and MAP, it ignores vital information that could be used by end users and downstream applications with real-world impacts, such as fair ranking
(
Zerveas et al. 2022
)
, ranked list truncation
(
Bahri et al. 2020
)
, and query performance prediction
(
Zhou and Croft 2007
;
Shtok et al. 2012
;
Faggioli et al. 2023b
)
.

The common decision to discard the model scores comes from the fact that almost all neural ranking models, even probabilistic approaches, are trained to optimize relative orderings of documents as opposed to their absolute level of relevance. Aligning these ranking scores to a fixed scale is particularly difficult due to the nature of each query requiring different amounts of information to satisfy relevance, and it is much easier to determine if a document is more relevant than another rather than determining whether the information is sufficient for complex queries
Khattab and Zaharia 2020
;
Santhanam et al. 2021
;
Zhuang et al. 2023b
;
Laitz et al. 2024
.

This concept, that output scores should have meaningful real-world interpretations, is known as
calibration
. Having this property ensures that a model’s predictions reliably reflects the “true score.” The most commonly recognized form of this is, where, for example, a classification model with a confidence of
p=0.47
should be correct 47% of the time. Building upon this idea of grounded scores,
scale calibration
extends this setting to values that do not have a direct probabilistic interpretation, such as document dwell time
(
Smucker and Clarke 2012
)
and multiple levels of relevance
(
Yan et al. 2022
;
Bai et al. 2023
)
which can exist beyond the [0,1] range.

Recent studies by
Yan et al. 2022
and
Bai et al. 2023
, have highlighted a conflict between the objectives of the scale calibration and the ranking task, where optimizing for one may compromise the other. They propose new training objectives to improve scale calibration in learning-to-ranking (LTR) models, which are low in parameter count due to relying on these LTR datasets containing solely preprocessed features like length and query-term overlap as inputs.
Applying these methods to larger neural rankers over raw text remains a challenge.
As opposed to LTR datasets, text-based collections with graded relevance judgements remain limited and orders of magnitude smaller. Further, the drastically higher parameter count of neural text rankers
(
Craswell et al. 2021
;
Nogueira et al. 2019b
;
Nogueira et al. 2020
;
Zhuang et al. 2023b
;
Ma et al. 2023a
)
make them even more data-intensive and reduces the efficacy of listwise loss functions due to hardware constraints and data sparsity.

In light of these challenges, we propose an alternative approach to improve scale calibration and score usability in these large text ranking models that does not rely on the loss functions of past works specific to the LTR settings. We instead ground the candidate query-document pairs via external knowledge, i.e., specifically leveraging the recent advances in generative and autoregressive large language models (LLMs) and their emerging capabilities for general language tasks with minimal supervision
(
Wei et al. 2022a
)
.
By producing natural language explanations (NLEs) of relevance with this additional knowledge, the ranking model will be able to leverage this information and produce better calibrated scores. As current LLMs have stability issues when generating text and can produce varying responses given the same input
(
Ye and Durrett 2022
;
Turpin et al. 2023
;
Chen et al. 2023
)
, we hypothesize that the confidence and stability of a NLE generated from a zero-shot LLM
explaining
the relevance of a query-document pair correlates well with a calibrated ranking score. We validate this hypothesis by developing a method that aggregates the Monte Carlo (MC) generated NLEs of query-document relevance that serves as input into a traditional neural ranking model.

This work makes the following three contributions:

1.

Introduce MC-aggregate NLEs to improve calibration error and downstream usability of document rankings.

2.

Significantly enhance the scale calibration of neural text rankers up to 25% and downstream usability while maintaining or improving ranking effectiveness.

3.

Conduct a wide set of experiments on two benchmark text ranking datasets to validate performance and properties.

2
Related Work

Scale Calibration of LTR Models.

Previous approaches to calibrating ranking models primarily involved post-processing methods
(
Tagami et al. 2013
;
Chaudhuri et al. 2017
)
, where a function maps the outputs for the frozen ranking model
(
Platt 2000
;
Zadrozny and Elkan 2002
)
. Recent studies, such as those by
Yan et al. 2022
and
Bai et al. 2023
, have identified the issue that commonly used pairwise and listwise ranking objectives theoretically lead to uncalibrated scores and advocate for calibrated ranking objectives as a solution. While effective for the LTR setting that uses extracted features and small models, the limitations imposed by large text ranking models makes their effective application non-trivial.

Uncertainty Calibration of Neural Ranking Models.
To achieve a notion of calibration,
Cohen et al. 2021
and
Penha and Hauff 2021
suggest leveraging a Bayesian interpretation of neural rankers to capture a distribution of possible scores for each document based on the internal uncertainty of the model. However, these approaches fail to ground the scores on any scale as such uncertainty information only makes sense when comparing documents, and is distinct from the type of calibration discussed in this paper.

Natural Language Explanations.
NLEs are an emerging point of research in NLP and IR. These explanations can be presented directly to users to elucidate the rationale behind system decisions, such as in recommendation systems
(
Li et al. 2020
)
and search results
(
Yu et al. 2022
)
. More pertinently to this study, NLEs have been employed as intermediaries to enhance task efficacy
(
Camburu et al. 2018
;
Wei et al. 2022b
;
Ferraretto et al. 2023
)
.
Ye and Durrett 2022
use the term overlap ratio between NLEs and their corresponding inputs as a feature to calibrate LLM predictions.
Wadhwa et al. 2023
investigated explanation-based rescaling, converting coarse numeric scales into finer gradations. This technique leverages
annotator-written
NLEs and scoring rubrics to prompt LLMs in generating rescaled outputs. Our research diverges by concentrating on NLEs
autonomously
generated by LLMs, which we leverage as inputs for neural rankers to facilitate scale calibration.

Calibration of Large Language Models.
Various recent studies have focused on eliciting well-calibrated confidence scores from LLM responses. Some works treat LLMs as black boxes and use various prompting-based techniques to generate confidence scores
Lin et al. 2022
;
Kadavath et al. 2022
. Other works rely on training external models to associate confidence with LLM responses
Kuhn et al. 2022
;
Kadavath et al. 2022
;
Mielke et al. 2022
.
Tian et al. 2023
conducted systematic evaluations over a wide range of methods to obtain confidence scores from LLMs and discovered that LLM-verbalized probabilities are better calibrated than conditional probabilities across several black-box RLHF-tuned LLMs. This framework was later extended to include hybrid methods (i.e., a mix of self-consistency and verbalized confidence) and evaluated the calibration performance on five different types of datasets
(
Xiong et al. 2023
)
. In Section
4.3
, we introduce two methods that take advantage of the calibration capabilities of LLMs to elicit scale calibrated ranking scores.

3
Methodology

In this section, we formalize the definition of scale calibration of neural ranking models, following which we introduce the proposed method of leveraging natural language explanations (NLEs) for obtaining scale calibrated ranking scores.

3.1
Problem Statement and Motivation

In ad-hoc ranking, we define a scoring function
\phi
for a given query
q
and its
n
associated candidate documents
\{d^{q}\}_{1}^{n}
. This function, denoted as
\phi_{\Phi}(q,\{d^{q}\})
produces a score for each query-document pair under the given retrieval model parameterized by
\Phi
. The ideal parameters of
\Phi
are obtained by optimizing an empirical loss on a query-grouped training dataset
\mathcal{D}=\{(\{d^{q}\},\{y^{q}\})\,|\,q\in Q\}
. Here,
Q
represents the set of training queries, and
\{y_{q}\}
is the corresponding set of labels of documents
\{d^{q}\}
. The empirical loss is defined as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\Phi)=\frac{1}{|Q|}\sum_{q\in Q}l^{\text{rank}}(\{y^{q}\},\phi_{\Phi}(q,\{d^{q}\}))

(1)

where
l^{\text{rank}}
is a ranking loss function for an individual query. In the case of neural ranking, the scoring function
\phi
is defined by a backbone neural language model. For instance, using a pretrained BERT checkpoint as
\Phi
, concatenating query and each candidate document with a [
SEP
] token in between as the inputs, and leveraging cross entropy loss or pairwise cross entropy loss as
l^{\text{rank}}
leads to the development of the widely known monoBERT or duoBERT models
(
Nogueira et al. 2019a
)
, respectively. However, it has been observed that popular pairwise and listwise ranking losses are not scale calibrated due to their translation-invariant property
(
Yan et al. 2022
)
1
1

1

Although the cross entropy loss used in monoBERT is scale-calibrated, but it assumes only binary labels; which is something not observed in real-world datasets (e.g., multiple levels of relevance
(
Craswell et al. 2020
;
Mao et al. 2019
)
).
; adding a constant to all outputs of
\phi
does not alter the loss value.

Although calibrated ranking losses works well for LTR models
(
Yan et al. 2022
;
Bai et al. 2023
)
,
the application of these approaches to neural text rankers is non-trivial. In LTR datasets with graded relevance judgements
(
Chapelle and Chang 2011
;
Qin and Liu 2013
;
Dato et al. 2017
)
,
|Q|
is orders of magnitude larger than that of text based collections, which often only have less than
100
queries. Further,
|\Phi|
is much larger in neural text ranking models, limiting the use of listwise loss functions due to GPU VRAM constraints.
Addressing the scale calibration problem in neural rankers therefore requires a multifaceted approach that extends beyond the mere application of calibrated ranking loss.

3.2
Scale Calibration via Natural Language Explanations

We propose a novel two-step approach to obtain a scale-calibrated numerical score from the textual query and document: map the query-document to a NLE and then score this NLE using a standard neural ranking model.
This overall strategy is grounded in the recent success of LLMs to establish the relevance of query-document pairs
(
Ferraretto et al. 2023
)
and the demonstrated efficacy of LLM-generated explanations in various reasoning tasks
(
Wei et al. 2022b
)
.
We use an LLM, denoted as
g(\cdot)
, to generate NLEs
\{e^{q}\}
for query-document pairs
\{d^{q}\}
, and then leverage a neural ranker over
only
the NLEs to predict relevance scores. This process can be formally represented as a decomposition of
\phi
into:

\displaystyle\phi_{\Phi}(q,\{d^{q}\})

\displaystyle=f_{\Theta}(g_{\Psi}(q,\{d^{q}\}))

(2)

\displaystyle=f_{\Theta}(\{e^{q}\})

(3)

where
\Psi
represents the parameters of the LLM, and
\Theta
encapsulates the parameters of the neural ranker. The neural ranker
f(\cdot)
in this setup is adapted to take the NLEs of the original inputs as its new inputs. Note that when using Eq.
1
to optimize parameters
\Phi=\{\Theta,\Psi\}
, we can optimize the parameters of the LLM and the neural ranker. While it is possible to perform full or partial fine-tuning on the LLM parameters
\Psi
, for simplicity and considering the limited amount of training data, we choose to freeze
\Psi
and only optimize parameters of the neural ranker
\Theta
.

3.3
Acquiring NLEs via LLM Prompting

Given the impact of prompting, we investigate two distinct methods for acquiring NLEs with varying characteristics.

3.3.1
Literal Explanation

We present the query-document pair to the LLM and ask for both a relevance prediction (either “relevant” or “non-relevant”) and an accompanying explanation. The exact prompt we use to generate such literal explanations is listed in Appendix
A
.

A notable limitation of this approach is its susceptibility to inaccuracies stemming from the LLM’s prediction errors. To mitigate this, we employ a strategy involving Monte Carlo (MC) sampling of multiple NLEs for the same input and then forming a single meta NLE via an aggregation function
AGGR
(detailed in Section
3.4
):

\displaystyle e^{q}=\texttt{AGGR}(\{y_{i}\sim g_{\Psi}(y|q,d^{q})\})

(4)

which aims to diminish the influence of erroneous predictions in the preference of the most likely generation.

3.3.2
Conditional Explanation

To address instances where the LLM persistently errs in judging the relevance of an input, we also explore a different explanation generation strategy termed
conditional explanation
. Motivated by the work of sampling multiple reasoning paths to enhance the self-consistency capabilities of LLMs
(
Wang et al. 2023
)
, this method involves prompting the LLM to generate rationales supporting both the relevance and non-relevance of a given query-document pair. The exact prompt we use to generate conditional explanations is also listed in Appendix
A
.
Both conditional explanations are then used by the neural ranker
f(\cdot)
to produce a scale calibrated ranking score (Eq.
3
).

3.4
Aggregating Multiple NLEs

As previously discussed, relying solely on the most probable output generated by the LLM, particularly in the literal explanation approach, could result in incorrect and overconfident explanations
Ye and Durrett 2022
. To mitigate this issue, we propose sampling multiple generations from the LLMs. This technique has the advantage to introduce additional information, which may either support the initial judgment with varied reasoning or present contrasting viewpoints. We consider both outcomes to be advantageous: in the former scenario, it leads to a more robust and multi-faceted argument supporting a specific prediction; in the latter scenario, the generation of conflicting information by the LLM partially reveals its uncertainty in comprehending and assessing the relevance of the input query-document pair. We hypothesize that this uncertainty is indicative of predicting a more calibrated ranking score.

4.2
Metrics

Our goal is to devise methods that not only enhance the calibration of neural rankers but also maintain their ranking effectiveness. Accordingly, we evaluate both calibration and ranking aspects.

Ranking Metrics:
We employ the normalized discounted cumulative gain (
nDCG
), which accounts for multiple levels of relevance judgment. We report
nDCG
and
nDCG@10
.

Calibration Metrics:
For calibration effectiveness, mean square error (
MSE
), as well as the expected calibration error (
ECE
)
(
Guo et al. 2017
)
, a metric initially developed for
classification
calibration, are commonly used in ranking literature
Yan et al. 2022
;
Bai et al. 2023
;
Penha and Hauff 2021
. ECE measures the discrepancy between a model’s predictions and gold labels in a binning approach via

ECE=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\frac{|B_{m}|}{n}\left|acc(B_{m})-conf(B_{m})\right|,

(5)

where
M
is the number of bins
B
. However, given the highly skewed and unbalanced label distribution in our test set, as indicated in Table
1
, MSE and ECE are biased. Opting for scores that are near the most frequent label results in lower errors while having no real world usability. This bias undermines these metrics’ effectiveness in the ranking context, and is further discussed in Appendix
C
. To achieve a metric that more closely aligns with real world usability for the search task, we propose the adoption of a class-balanced version of ECE, called
CB-ECE
, which allocates equal significance to all candidate scale values. The CB-ECE is computed by initially calculating the ECE for each individual class, and subsequently taking the mean,

\text{CB-ECE}=\frac{1}{C}\sum_{c=1}^{C}ECE_{c},

(6)

with
ECE_{c}
as the error for each class,
c
.

Query Performance Prediction (QPP):
As the motivation of this work comes from the desire of usable scores, we also evaluate the methods using the QPP task. We evaluate the effectiveness of each approach by using
Pearson’s
\rho
and
Kendall’s
\tau

4.3
Baselines

Due to the scarcity of relevant work in scale calibrated search, we have developed several methods to establish meaningful baselines in addition to the past relevant work of
Yan et al. 2022
. For consistency, we use the same neural ranking architecture, when applicable, for all approaches. Additional details are in Appendix
B
.

No calibration (NC)
. We use monoBERT
(
Nogueira and Cho 2019
)
, a BERT-based ranker that has been previously finetuned with the MS MARCO passage ranking training set.

Post-hoc calibration (PC)
. We apply Platt scaling
(
Platt 2000
)
adapted for regression calibration to the uncalibrated monoBERT. It is important to note that the parameters of the ranker remain fixed under this approach.

Full calibration (FC)
. We use the training data to finetune a BERT-based ranker using calibrated softmax loss
(
Yan et al. 2022
)
, which represents a straight adoption of the approach from LTR to neural ranking. As discussed, due to substantially larger size of the neural ranker and reduced training data, we are forced to only use a small fraction of negatives for each query compared to under the LTR setting.

LLM prompting with rubrics (PR)
. Inspired by studies on using LLMs for rendering relevance judgments
(
Zhuang et al. 2023a
;
Thomas et al. 2023
;
Faggioli et al. 2023a
)
and rescaling
(
Wadhwa et al. 2023
)
, we explore leveraging the scoring rubrics as a contextual guide for LLM prompting. We present each query-document pair, along with the rubrics from each dataset that were initially used to instruct human annotators, to the LLM and take the scalar predictions.

. Based on prior studies that LLM confidence scores are well-calibrated
(
Kadavath et al. 2022
;
Tian et al. 2023
)
, we use the LLM as a zero-shot binary classifier and take the average predictions over 20 samples as the confidence score. We then use Platt scaling
(
Platt 2000
)
to map these scores to the target scale.

Full calibration with NLEs (NLE)
. We first acquire meta NLEs for candidate query-document pairs and then fine-tune a BERT model

4.4
Downstream Performance: QPP

The ultimate goal of a calibrated model is interpretable scores by end users and algorithms. Therefore we evaluate the scores produced from each approach using the query performance prediction (QPP) task. The goal of QPP, as discussed by
Arabzadeh et al. 2024
, is to estimate the performance of individual queries such that hard-to-satisfy queries can be better addressed by search systems. We use two popular post-hoc QPP methods—Weighted Information Gain (WIG)
(
Zhou and Croft 2007
)
and Normalized Query Commitment (NQC)
(
Shtok et al. 2012
)
—to explore whether the proposed NLE based approaches yield more usable scores. Evaluations are conducted on the test set of TREC, using the top-10 documents to calculate WIG and NQC.

4.6
Results and Analysis

RQ1: How do LLM-generated natural language explanations impact the calibration and ranking performance of neural rankers?
We find in Table
2
that NLE-based methods yield statistically significant improvements for both aspects. For scale calibration, NLEs exhibit lower CB-ECE compared to both post-hoc calibration (PC) and full calibration (FC) across both datasets, with literal NLEs performing better on TREC while conditional NLEs perform slightly better on NTCIR. Regarding ranking, the proposed approach significantly surpasses NC and FC baselines, which shares the same fine-tuning data and initial weights, with improvements of up to 11.4% in nDCG and 63.3% in nDCG@10 in the TREC dataset. This indicates that LLM-generated NLEs provide valuable insights for document differentiation beyond the scope of calibration.

RQ2: Is there a consistent improvement across different training objectives when using these explanations?

Yan et al. 2022
and
Bai et al. 2023
advocate for addressing the scale calibration of LTR models through calibrated loss functions. As a significant motivator for this work are the challenges of applying LTR approaches to larger text models, we investigate the efficacy of naively applying these objectives to the neural ranking task.
To this end, we experiment with four fine-tuning objectives commonly used in this setting: MSE as a pointwise regression loss, uncalibrated listwise softmax (Softmax), a multi-objective combination of MSE and Softmax (MultiObj) from
Bai et al. 2023
, and the calibrated listwise softmax (Calibrated Softmax) from
Yan et al. 2022
. The FC baseline is also trained with each of these objectives. As seen in Fig.
2
, the NLE-based approaches consistently surpass the performance of neural models that process raw text queries and documents across all four optimization methods. 5
Conclusions

This study tackled the challenge of scale calibration for neural rankers.
Our method leverages zero-shot LLMs’ inherent understanding of textual data through NLEs to enhance scale calibration while maintaining or improving ranking performance. We showed that our approach surpasses established baselines in ranking and calibration metrics, demonstrates consistency across various training objectives, and is effective for downstream post-hoc QPP applications.

6
Limitations

This work prototypes the idea of leveraging LLM-generated NLEs of query-document pairs to improve the scale calibration of neural ranking models. Like many recent works that incorporate LLMs as an integral part of the document ranking process
(
Ma et al. 2023b
;
Qin et al. 2023
;
Zhuang et al. 2024
)
, our approach is not yet efficient enough to be directly applied to an online ranking system with high latency requirements. Having validated the feasibility of NLEs for calibration, future work could incorporate techniques such as distillation to improve run-time efficiency.

Another limitation stems from the unreliability and inconsistency of NLEs from LLMs
(
Ye and Durrett 2022
)
. Although we paradoxically leverage the inconsistency of NLEs to further improve the scale calibration of neural ranking models, the potential effects of addressing unreliability or inconsistency on the calibration task remain unclear. Moreover, while we aim for the scale-calibrated ranking
scores
to be useful for explainability purposes as they correspond to certain criteria, the NLEs themselves are not meant to be, nor should they be directly displayed to users for interpretability purposes in the current form.

A notable performance disparity is observed between our current aggregate MC approach and the oracle explanation setting in Table
3
. This gap highlights a significant potential for improvement in NLE-based neural rankers contingent upon better alignment of LLM judgments with human annotators on a
binary
scale. Although we experimented with various prompts for generating NLEs in some pilot studies, this research is designed to explore the feasibility of LLM-generated NLEs for a specific important task. Consequently, various directions that could potentially improve the LLMs in terms of accuracy of predictions and quality of explanations—including but not limited to few-shot prompting, prompt tuning and optimization, LLM fine-tuning, and leveraging better (even closed-source) LLMs—are beyond the interest and scope of this study.

7
Potential Risks

Although calibrated ranking scores can be leveraged in certain ways to mitigate bias for fairer document ranking
(
Heuss et al. 2023
)
, the use of NLEs acts as an information transformation, filtering parts of the candidate document while adding the LLM’s own biases. Certain topics, demographics, and perspectives could be impacted and consequently change the proposed document rankings. While traditional
ad-hoc
web search is transitioning to retrieval augmented generation, the consequence remains the same. These systems act as information brokers for the general public, and can easily introduce or reinforce biases into the population based on the retrieved information. With this in mind, we elected to use an off-the-shelf text ranking paradigm as the final scorer. This facilitates the drop in use of the many techniques already developed to improve fairness in ranking
(
Zehlike et al. 2023a
;
Zehlike et al. 2023b
)
.

Appendix B
Details of Competing Methods

Here we provide more details and explanations for competing methods evaluated in the experiments.

No calibration (NC)
.
Our approach here employs a BERT-based ranker that has been previously finetuned using the MS MARCO dataset
(
Bajaj et al. 2018
)
. Despite its strong performance in ranking tasks on TREC (in-domain) and NTCIR (out-of-domain), this ranker lacks scale calibration. This is attributed to the binary relevance labels in MS MARCO, which differ from the multi-level relevance judgments used in TREC and NTCIR.

Post-hoc calibration (PC)
.
In this method, we adjust the output scores of the finetuned rankers using a learnable function. Following the approach of
Yan et al. 2022
, we apply Platt scaling
(
Platt 2000
)
, adapted for regression calibration. Given the output ranking scores of the model
\mathbf{s}
, the calibrated scores are computed as
\mathbf{s^{\prime}}=\exp{(w\mathbf{s}+b)}/2
, where
w
and
b
are learnable parameters. It is important to note that under this method, the parameters of the finetuned rankers remain fixed; only the parameters
w
and
b
are optimized using the scale calibration data. This approach maintains the original ranking performance of the ranker, provided the final value of
w
is positive.

Full calibration (FC)
. This strategy involves directly fine-tuning a BERT-based ranker with “query-[
SEP
]-document” style inputs on scale calibration data. Utilizing the monoBERT checkpoint allows for a direct comparison with PC methods - they use the same initial checkpoint and training data, but optimize different parameters. Starting with a general-purpose BERT checkpoint sets up a direct comparison with our NLE-based calibration approaches - they share the same initial weights and training data, but the format of their input data differs significantly.

LLM prompting with rubrics (PR)
. The underlying concept is that the LLM should be able to comprehend the given rubrics and automatically produced calibrated scores that align with the rubrics, thereby removing the need for any post-hoc calibration steps. It is important to note that in this method, the LLM itself essentially functions as the ranker, without the integration of an additional neural ranking model. For the TREC dataset, we adopt the prompt method used by
Thomas et al. 2023
. Conversely, for NTCIR, we adapt the approach to incorporate the specific rubric outlined in the task description of NTCIR-14 WWW-2
(
Mao et al. 2019
)
. It is noteworthy that the NTCIR rubric is defined based on scores provided by two annotators (e.g., “relevance=3: One annotator rated as highly relevant, one as relevant”), focusing on the quantitative synthesis of annotations. In contrast, the TREC rubric is more qualitatively oriented, emphasizing the explanation of query-document relationships.

Post-hoc calibrating LLM predictions (PL)
.
In this method, we use the LLM as a zero-shot classifier to determine whether a given query and document pair is relevant (denoted as 1) or not (denoted as 0). To mitigate the bias inherent in the most probable generation and to minimize instances of tied scores, which complicate the derivation of rankings, we sample the LLM’s responses 20 times for each input and calculate the average of these scores. Subsequently, we employ Platt scaling to these averaged scores and refine the parameters using the training set. The prompt we use is similar to that of
Zhuang et al. 2023a
, with one significant modification: we instruct the LLM to output either “relevant” or “nonrelevant” in lieu of “yes” or “no”. This alteration stems from our observation that the LLM exhibits a strong prior towards generating affirmative responses such as “Yes, I can help you with this request…”, which could potentially skew the predictions. By specifying the terms “relevant” and “nonrelevant”, we aim to reduce this bias and achieve more accurate relevance predictions.

Full calibration with NLEs (NLE)
. Building upon our methods for generating and aggregating natural language explanations (NLEs) for query-document pairs, as discussed in Section
3
, we proceed to finetune a BERT model (not finetuned for retrieval) to process meta NLEs and yield scale-calibrated ranking scores. In the scenario of the conditional explanation approach (Section
3.3.2
), where each input is represented with two meta NLEs, one for relevance and one for non-relevance, our method involves an additional processing step. Specifically, we concatenate the
[CLS]
hidden states obtained from encoding both NLEs. This concatenated representation then feeds into an additional linear layer, which is responsible for transforming these combined hidden states into a final ranking score. This approach allows the model to integrate insights from both relevance perspectives.

Appendix C
Class-balanced ECE and the limitations of ECE

We discuss in Section
4.2
the limitations inherent in using the expected calibration error (ECE)
(
Guo et al. 2017
)
for scale calibration data, especially when faced with imbalanced label distributions. To counter this challenge, we propose a straightforward modification: the adoption of class-balanced ECE (CB-ECE). This section uses model outputs on TREC as illustrative examples to provide detailed insights and empirical support for our approach.

Figure 3:
Reliability diagrams for two models on TREC: The left diagram shows a model with ranking scores densely concentrated on the lower part of the scale, which exhibits better ECE performance due to ECE’s failure to account for prediction coverage across the target scale. On the right, the CB-ECE penalizes this undesirable behavior, indicating that the model providing better coverage across the scale is more effectively calibrated.

We employ reliability diagrams
(
Murphy and Winkler 1977
)
, a widely-used technique in calibration, to visualize calibration performance. In ECE calculations, samples are sorted by their predicted values and divided into several buckets. The calibration error for each bucket is determined by the absolute difference between the mean labels and mean predictions of samples within that bucket, further
weighted by
the number of samples falling in the bucket. Reliability diagrams concretely visualize this concept, with each bucket represented by a bar. The mean predictions are indicated on the x-axis, while the mean labels (i.e., scale) are represented by the height of the bars. In an ideal scenario, a perfectly calibrated model would yield a reliability diagram aligning with the diagonal line, indicating that mean predictions correspond accurately to the labels for every bucket. However, ECE overlooks a critical aspect of scale calibration:
coverage
. This refers to the model’s ability to adequately span the target scale range. Figure
3
illustrates this point with two diagrams. The left diagram shows a model whose outputs are narrowly concentrated around lower values on TREC’s 0-3 scale. The right diagram, conversely, exhibits outputs covering a broader range on the target scale, also closely aligned with the diagonal line. Relying solely on ECE values would misleadingly suggest that the first model is better calibrated (ECE: 0.123 < 0.134; lower is better). However, we argue that a model with predictions confined to a limited portion of the desired scale, particularly skewed towards certain values popular in the evaluation set, cannot be considered well-calibrated. CB-ECE offers a solution by re-adjusting the significance of each target scale. In the example shown in Fig.
3
, CB-ECE more accurately indicates that the second model exhibits better scale calibration (CB-ECE: 1.137 > 1.084; lower is better), which is more in line with our intuitive understanding of model calibration.
