Physics Inspired Neural Networks Enable Efficient Graph Coloring
Lorenzo Colantonio, Andrea Cacioppo, Federico Scarpati, Maria Chiara Angelini, Federico Ricci-Tersenghi, Stefano Giagu

Abstract
Combinatorial optimization problems near algorithmic phase transitions represent a fundamental challenge for both classical algorithms and machine learning approaches. Among them, graph coloring is a prototypical constraint satisfaction problem characterized by phase transitions that fragment the solution space into distinct clusters, making the search for valid solutions challenging. Here we introduce a physics-inspired neural framework that learns to solve large-scale graph coloring instances by combining graph neural networks with statistical-mechanics principles. Our approach integrates a planting-based supervised signal, symmetry-breaking regularization, and iterative noise-annealed neural dynamics to navigate complex solution landscapes. When the number of iterations scales quadratically with graph size, the learned solver successfully tackles increasingly challenging instances, even in regimes where finding valid solutions becomes particularly difficult, demonstrating its ability to extract meaningful information from the graph structure. The model generalizes from small training graphs to instances up to 20 times larger, demonstrating that neural architectures can learn scalable algorithmic strategies that remain effective in hard connectivity regions. These results establish a general framework for learning neural solvers that operate near fundamental phase boundaries in combinatorial optimization and inference.

Introduction

The graph coloring problem (GCP) is a paradigmatic constrained optimization problem that lies at the core of combinatorial complexity theory. It consists of assigning one among
q
possible colors to each vertex of an undirected graph, so that no two adjacent vertices share the same color.
The problem of deciding whether a graph can be fairly colored with
q
colors is NP-complete
[
18
]
, while the optimization of a graph’s coloring to minimize conflicts is NP-hard
[
13
]
.
Beyond its theoretical relevance, graph coloring arises in a wide range of practical applications, including scheduling
[
24
]
, timetabling
[
1
]
and even puzzle solving such as Sudoku
[
39
]
, moreover it can be reduced in polynomial time to all other NP-hard problems
[
18
]
as the travelling salesman problem, the clique problem or k-SAT
[
13
]
.
The intrinsic difficulty of the GCP is deeply connected to the widely held belief that
P\neq NP

[
13
]

[
10
]
, making the GCP both difficult and useful to solve.
In large random graphs, the structure of the solution space undergoes sharp phase transitions as the average connectivity varies. These transitions dramatically affect the performance of algorithms, creating regimes in which solutions exist but are algorithmically hard to find. Understanding and overcoming these barriers remains a central challenge in combinatorial optimization.
Classical approaches to graph coloring typically rely on local search in configuration space
[
12
]
. Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods such as Simulated Annealing
[
7
]

[
17
]

[
3
]
, TabuCol
[
15
]
, and evolutionary algorithms
[
11
,
26
,
31
]
, can achieve strong performance, but may require running times that grow exponentially with graph size in hard regimes. Greedy heuristics such as Greedy coloring
[
27
]
or DSatur
[
6
]
, avoid the problem of time scaling but provide sub-optimal solutions. In recent years, graph neural networks (GNNs) have emerged as a promising alternative, leveraging the connectivity structure to process graph-structured data. Several works have applied GNNs to graph coloring, using reinforcement learning, direct classification of colorability, or physics-inspired energy minimization strategies. While these approaches demonstrate encouraging results, their performance often degrades near critical connectivity thresholds, precisely where the problem becomes structurally hardest.

In this work, we introduce a physics-inspired neural framework that explicitly integrates statistical-mechanics insights into the training and inference dynamics of a GNN-based solver.
By scaling the number of inference iterations with the problem size,
we demonstrate that the learned solver remains effective in challenging regimes where the solution space becomes increasingly difficult. Furthermore, it can recover the planted solution
[
22
]
, a valid coloring embedded during graph generation, even when the underlying structure is difficult to detect.. Moreover, the model generalizes from training on relatively small graphs to solving instances up to 20 times larger, highlighting the potential of neural architectures to learn scalable algorithmic strategies rather than memorizing instance-specific patterns.
This paper is structured as follows. In section
2
, we provide a high-level overview of existing methods based on GNNs. In Sec.
3
we address the GCP from a statistical mechanics point of view, highlighting its connections with the Potts model. We describe the two types of graphs used in this work, namely Erdős–Rényi and planted graphs, describing the procedure for generating them. In Sec.
6
we detail the dataset used in this work, we present a GNN-based architecture, introduce an effective supervised training strategy, and describe the coloring procedure. In Sec.
4
we presents the performances of our method in terms of energy and at different connectivities, comparing with the best state-of-the-art graph coloring algorithms.

Related Work

There is a vast and diverse list of algorithms used to solve NP-hard problems. In this section, we focus on algorithms that primarily use GNNs for solving the GCP.

In
[
16
]
, improved heuristics for graph coloring are sought using reinforcement learning, leveraging a deep neural network architecture that has access to the entire graph structure. In
[
23
]
, a GNN is used to determine whether a graph can be colored using
q
colors or not. Additionally, it is shown how node embeddings can be used to assign coloring to the graph. In
[
25
]
, the performances of aggregation-combine GNNs are studied. The work highlights the features that limit the expressive power of these models in solving node-classification problems and proposes solutions to improve performances.

In
[
35
]
, the authors use the architecture of a physics-inspired GNN (PI-GNN) for graph coloring. Specifically, the neural network is optimized in order to minimize the Potts energy separately for each graph instance. This approach has been adopted in several subsequent works. In
[
41
]
, an alternative to PI-GNN is proposed, introducing negative-message-passing, achieving numerical improvements. Additionally, an entropic term is introduced in the loss function to accelerate convergence during the model training. Finally, in
[
46
]
, a graph isomorphism network model, using a physics-inspired approach, is proposed. The output of the neural network, is post-processed using TabuCol to reduce the number of conflicts.
Our work builds upon the same continuous Potts formulation and a related message-passing architecture, but differs fundamentally in the learning and inference framework. Rather than optimizing the model for each graph, we train a
single
GNN on a distribution of graphs. The trained architecture is then used to solve unseen graphs through an iterative noise-annealed dynamics.
Our approach combines three key ingredients: (i) a planting procedure that can produce large, hard instances with associated labels, suitable for supervised training of the network, while preserving, in relevant regimes, the same statistical properties as random graph ensembles (ii) a semi-supervised loss that blends a differentiable Potts energy with symmetry-breaking overlap terms; and (iii) an iterative noise-annealed inference procedure that enables the model to escape metastable configurations in clustered solution landscapes.

3
Background

In this section, we formally introduce the GCP and its connection to the Potts model, furthermore the graphs ensembles used in this work are presented and the algorithm used to generate them is described.

3.1
Graph coloring

The GCP is a very well-known constraint satisfaction problem (CSP). Formally, we consider an undirected graph
{\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})}
where
{\mathcal{V}=\{1,...,N\}}
is the set of vertices and
{\mathcal{E}=\{(i,j)|i,j\in\mathcal{V}\}}
is the set of edges.
The objective is to assign an integer variable (or color)
{s_{\nu}\in\{1,...,q\}}
to each node
\nu\in\mathcal{V}
, such that nodes sharing the same edge have different colors, namely

s_{i}\neq s_{j},\ \forall(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}

(1)

If a graph admits such a configuration
\mathbf{s}
, the graph is said
q
-colorable. The smallest value of
q
for which the graph is colorable is the chromatic number
\chi(\mathcal{G})
.

3.2
Potts model

Searching graph coloring solutions is equivalent to studying the energy minima of the anti-ferromagnetic Potts model. The Potts model is the generalization of an Ising model, where the spins can take
q
different values
[
44
]
. The energy of the anti-ferromagnetic Potts model is given by

H_{\text{Potts}}(\mathbf{s})=\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}}\delta_{s_{i},s_{j}}\;,

(2)

and counts the number of conflicting edges in the associated GCP.
For a given graph
\mathcal{G}
in equilibrium at temperature
T
, the nodes configurations
\mathbf{s}
follows the Gibbs-Boltzmann probability distribution

P_{\mathcal{G}}(\mathbf{s})=\frac{e^{-H(\mathbf{s})/T}}{\mathcal{Z}}\;,

(3)

where the partition function
\mathcal{Z}
is the normalization factor.
In the limit
T\to 0
, the measure is dominated by configurations with the minimum energy
E_{0}
. If
E_{0}=0
, the graph is
q
-colorable. Sampling the
T=0
distribution solves the GCP
[
29
]
and underlies algorithms such as simulated annealing (SA)
[
20
]
and belief propagation (BP)
[
32
]
.
Nevertheless, sampling the Gibbs distribution is a considerably stronger requirement than finding a single valid coloring. In practice, SA and BP-based strategies—such as BP with decimation or reinforcement—are both unable to sample beyond the so-called dynamical threshold
[
14
]
, and BP-based strategies fails even before it
[
33
]
. Our method therefore addresses only the latter: finding one valid coloring.

It is well known that the solution space of GCP for random graphs such as Random Regular Graphs (RRG)
[
5
]
and Erdős–Rényi Graphs (ERG)
[
9
]
undergoes multiple phase transitions
[
45
]
.

Figure 1:

Schematic illustration of the evolution of the solution-space landscape
with increasing average connectivity
c
. The red cross represent the
planted solution. At low connectivity, the planted solution belongs to a
large connected region of solutions. Above the dynamical transition
c_{d}
, the solution space fragments into an exponential number of clusters. At the condensation transition
c_{c}
, the solution measure concentrates onto a subexponential number of dominant clusters, one of which contains the planted solution. The satisfiability threshold
c_{s}
marks the transition beyond which typical random instances become unsatisfiable. The Kesten–Stigum threshold
c_{\mathrm{KS}}
marks the detectability of the planted structure. Blue regions denote solutions, whereas orange regions represent local minima.

The success of sampling and the time required to perform it strongly depend on the average connectivity of the graph under consideration and the number of colors
q
. In particular, for sufficiently small connectivity values
c<c_{d}(q)
, most solutions form a single connected cluster, where any two solutions differ by only a sub-extensive number of node variables. In this phase, finding solutions is particularly easy for most algorithms. In the
q>3
case, at
c_{d}(q)
a dynamic phase transition takes place, such that for
c>c_{d}(q)
the solutions are shattered among exponentially many (in
N
) clusters; this structure of solutions generally slows down the dynamics of algorithms.
In the range
c_{c}(q)<c<c_{s}(q)
, the number of clusters becomes sub-exponential, making feasible colorings difficult to find using polynomial-time algorithms. Finally, in the region with high connectivity values
c>c_{s}(q)
, the probability that a random graph is
q
-colorable vanishes in the large
N
limit. We stress that the above scenario holds only in the thermodynamic limit, while for finite-size graphs, there are exeption to this behavior. The
q\leq 3
case is less significant, as the
c_{d}(q)
and
c_{c}(q)
critical thresholds coincide, resulting in a situation that is generally easier to tackle for algorithms.

3.3
Planted Erdős–Rényi random graphs

As we have discussed in Secs.
1
and
3.2
, finding a perfect coloring for a generic graph can be very resource-expensive. To this end, in the present paragraph, we briefly discuss a simple and effective method that allows us to generate graphs for which a solution
\mathbf{s}^{*}
is already known
[
22
]
.
The procedure is explained in Algorithm A.1 in the Supplementary Information.
Notably, this algorithm can generate fairly colored graphs at any connectivity, even beyond the satisfiability threshold (
c>c_{s}
).
It can be proved that planted random graphs are indistinguishable from random graphs as long as
c<c_{c}

[
22
]
.
For this reason, in that region, the procedure is called quiet-planting.
When
c>c_{c}
, the entropy of the planted state (i.e., the set of solutions around the planted one) becomes larger than the entropy of all other random solutions, making the planted random graph distinguishable from a standard (non-planted) random graph.
However, the model that we introduce in Sec.
6
does not aim to sample graphs from the Gibbs distribution, but to find a solution that minimizes the energy.
Therefore, the solutions obtained by the planting procedure continue to be suitable for the purpose of training.

In the planted model, the planted solution can be detected only if the value of
c
is large enough. At present, the best detection algorithm is Belief Propagation, which is successful for
c>c_{\text{KS}}
, where
c_{\text{KS}}
is the Kesten-Stigum bound. Originally derived by Kesten and Stigum in the study of the asymptotic growth of multitype branching processes
[
19
]
, the KS criterion was later connected to reconstruction problems on trees
[
30
,
28
]
.
The KS threshold corresponds to the instability of the uninformative BP fixed point toward the planted solution and its role in inference problems has been discussed in detailed in the literature
[
22
,
34
]
.
For the GCP the KS threshold marks the onset of detectability for polynomial-time algorithms.

we report a visual representation of the solution space in each phase.

4
Experiments

In this section, we show the performance of our algorithm in coloring graphs.
Throughout this work, we focus on the representative case of
q=5
. As a matter of fact, the qualitative phase diagram is the same for all
q\geq 5
, with the clustering, condensation, and satisfiability transitions occurring at different critical connectivities but exhibiting the same underlying behaviour. The situation is different for
q<5
: there, some transitions change order or collapse together
[
45
]
, and the solution space is less fragmented, making the search problem substantially simpler
[
37
]
. Our choice of
q=5
is therefore motivated by the fact that it is the smallest number of colors for which the problem exhibits the full complexity of the hard regime.
We consider two configurations targeting distinct regions of the phase diagram: the Dynamical Transition Regime (DTR), which addresses random-graph coloring near the dynamical transition, and the Kesten–Stigum Regime (KSR), which focuses on planted inference near the Kesten–Stigum threshold.

Models setup

In the following, we provide details on the training datasets and architectures of the two models. Additional information on the model configuration, training procedure, and inference hyperparameters is provided in Table 1 and Table 2 of the Supplementary Information.

To train the DTR Model, we have used a dataset of
4000
planted graphs for each connectivity in
[12.5,12.7,12.9,\dddot{},13.5,13.7]
, and sizes
N=[1024,2048,4096]
for a total of 84k graphs. This dataset has been proposed as a hard benchmark to test neural-network-based solvers
[
37
]
.

To train the KSR Model, we have used a dataset of
10000
planted graphs for each connectivity in
[15.5,16.0,16.5,17.0]
, and size
N=1000
for a total of 40k graphs.

For each model,
90\%
of the dataset is used for training and the remaining
10\%
for validation. The models are evaluated on independently generated graph instances.

In all models, we have used
q=5
colors and an additional input feature to encode the node’s degree. We have observed that this additional feature increases the model performance with a negligible computational cost.

5
Discussion

We have introduced an algorithm that leverages physics-inspired graph neural networks to solve efficiently the graph coloring problem, which provides hard instances of both optimization and inference problems depending on the random graph connectivity.
Our algorithm clearly outperforms any machine-learning–based method previously available (see the recent comparative study in Ref.
[
37
]
) and it is competitive with other known algorithms: it ranks second, behind Focused Metropolis Search (an impressive local search algorithm) in the optimization problem, and it exhibits a detection transition close to the Kesten–Stigum threshold in the planted inference experiments.

A key conceptual contribution of this work is the integration of statistical-mechanics principles into the learning process. Impressive performances have been achieved implementing an approach that uses planted solutions to introduce supervised signals without sacrificing the statistical structure of hard random ensembles, while symmetry breaking and noise-annealed iteration enable controlled navigation of clustered energy landscapes, avoiding the common problem of over-smoothing in GNNs. It is also very important to stress that the reported performance near the phase-transition thresholds is obtained using a heuristic inference schedule in which the number of iterations scales quadratically with graph size, inspired by a recent work
[
2
]
.

Our results strongly suggest that further research in this direction is not only promising but necessary in order to push the current limits of machine learning methods used in high-dimensional combinatorial optimization and inference. Our study shows that, for certain connectivity regimes, the performance of the algorithm does not significantly vary across different graph sizes. The ability of the trained model to generalize from relatively small graphs to instances up to 20 times larger highlights another important perspective. Instead of learning instance-specific mappings, the network appears to internalize structural regularities of the problem class.

The results presented in this work point toward a broader research direction beyond graph coloring: the development of neural solvers designed to operate in regimes close to algorithmic phase transitions. Many hard combinatorial optimization and inference problems, including SAT, Max-Cut, and error-correcting codes, exhibit structural transitions separating relatively easy from algorithmically challenging regimes.
While the present study is restricted to graph coloring, our results suggest that combining learned local update rules with problem-specific structural guidance and iterative search dynamics may provide a strategy for tackling similarly challenging problems. Extending the framework to different CSPs would, however, require problem-specific representations, differentiable constraint-violation objectives, and known solutions. Establishing whether the same advantages persist beyond graph coloring therefore remains an important direction for future work.

From an applied standpoint, scalable neural solvers for large combinatorial instances could impact domains such as communication networks, distributed computing, logistics, circuit design, and scheduling, where large-scale graph-structured constraints are ubiquitous.

Several open directions emerge from this study. First, a deeper understanding of why the relationship between quadratic iteration scaling and the observed performance near the dynamical and Kesten–Stigum thresholds remains to be understood theoretically. Second, extending this framework to heterogeneous graph ensembles, weighted constraints, or real-world network topologies would test its robustness beyond random models. Third, integrating learned neural dynamics with classical message-passing or local-search algorithms could yield adaptive hybrid solvers that combine interpretability with learned flexibility.
Finally, an important direction is to reduce the reliance on labeled solutions during training, which currently represents a limitation of the proposed framework.

Ultimately, this work contributes to a growing perspective in machine learning: that learning can complement complexity theory and statistical physics in exploring the algorithmic limits of hard problems. By explicitly targeting phase boundaries and structural bottlenecks, neural solvers may help bridge the gap between theoretical optimality and practical scalability in combinatorial optimization and inference.

6
Methods

In this section, we describe the model architecture and dataset. The loss function and training method are then introduced. Finally, the algorithm used for searching the solution is presented and motivated.

6.1
Model

We are interested in learning a map
f(\cdot;\mathcal{G})
from a feature space
\mathcal{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times N}
to a color space
\{1,\dddot{},q\}^{N}
such that:

H_{\text{Potts}}\big[f(\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{V}};\mathcal{G})\big]=0

(4)

In our setting, each node is represented by a feature vector of dimension
k=q+1
, where the first
q
components encode the color information and the last component encodes the node degree.
We choose to parameterize this map with a GNN model, implementing the transformation
f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\cdot;\mathcal{G})
.
GNNs are designed to leverage the invariance properties of graph structures, making them effective for learning representations that respect the underlying graph topology.

In recent years, a plethora of architectures have been presented in the context of GNNs
[
47
]
. For our purposes, we have focused on one of the most general and expressive models, described in
[
4
]
, and already used with promising results in solving various tasks
[
38
,
43
,
42
]
.
The model used in this work is based on a message passing network composed by
L
layers. At each layer nodes features are updated as follows: at first for each edge an ingoing and an outgoing message are computed, (each node sends and receives a message from its neighbors) using the formula

\textbf{m}_{ij}^{(l)}=\phi^{(l)}_{\theta_{1}^{(l)}}\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{(l-1)},\mathbf{x}_{j}^{(l-1)}\right)\;,

(5)

where
\textbf{m}_{ij}^{(l)}
represents the message sent by the
j^{\text{th}}
node and gathered by the
i^{\text{th}}
node in the
l^{\text{th}}
layer, while
\mathbf{x}_{i}^{(l-1)}
and
\mathbf{x}_{j}^{(l-1)}
represent the node features at the previous layer.
Next, the messages are aggregated using any permutation invariant function (e.g. sum, average, max). Together with the node features from the previous step
\mathbf{x}_{i}^{(l-1)}
, the aggregated messages are used to update the node features:

\mathbf{x}_{i}^{(l)}=\gamma^{(l)}_{\theta_{2}^{(l)}}\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{(l-1)},\bigoplus_{j\in\mathcal{N}(i)}\textbf{m}_{ij}^{(l)}\right)\;,

(6)

where
\mathcal{N}(i)
is the set of neighbors of node
i
in the graph.
The functions
\phi^{(l)}_{\theta_{1}^{(l)}}
and
\gamma^{(l)}_{\theta_{2}^{(l)}}
, used to compute the messages and the node features respectively, are parameterized by two multi layer perceptrons (MLP).
After the
L^{\text{th}}
layer, all hidden and input features are passed to a third MLP
\Gamma_{\theta_{3}}
to compute the final output of the node:

\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\text{output}}=\text{softmax}\Big[\Gamma_{\theta_{3}}(\{\mathbf{x}^{(l)}_{i}\}_{l=0,\dots,L})\Big]

(7)

The softmax activation makes the output feature vector normalized. In this way the
a^{\text{th}}
component of
\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\text{output}}
can be interpreted as a node’s probability of having the
a^{\text{th}}
color.
We denote the overall function implemented by the parametric model with
f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\big(\{\mathbf{x}_{i}^{(0)}\}_{i\in\mathcal{V}}\big)
, where
\boldsymbol{\theta}
represents the set of all trainable parameters and we denote the input feature vector associated to the
i^{\text{th}}
node with
\mathbf{x}_{i}^{(0)}
.
We highlight that by construction, the hidden features
\mathbf{x}_{i}^{(l)}
contain information about all neighbors at distance
l
from the
i^{\text{th}}
node. Moreover the output function
\Gamma_{\theta_{3}}
is able to use all intermediate features at once, effectively learning to process messages gathered at different graph distances.

6.2
Dataset

We are interested in training our algorithm using both supervised and unsupervised loss terms. To do so, we need a dataset of graphs satisfying the GCP.
It is already well known that finding a good strategy to train neural networks to solve problems for which it is hard to have a solution is a challenging point. In this paper we overcome this problem, using the efficient planting procedure to obtain a random graph together with a solution of the GCP that could be however hard to find even for very smart algorithms
[
22
]
. The ensemble of graphs generated with this planting procedure is usually referred to as the planted ensemble and, in some region of the parameters, is contiguous to the random Erdős–Rényi ensemble of graphs.

6.3
Training and loss function

In this work, we are interested in training a model that, given a colored graph with conflicting edges, produces a perfect coloring. Hence we are interested in finding a map

f:\{1,\dddot{},q\}^{N}\longrightarrow\{1,\dddot{},q\}^{N}

(8)

To this end, the model has been trained with a semi-supervised loss function, including an energy term and a term which measures the closeness of the output with the perfectly colored solution. The training is performed using a gradient-descent-based algorithm, therefore the loss function must be differentiable with respect to the model’s parameters. This is not possible by using the Potts energy directly, as it is not differentiable. For this reason, regarding the energy term in the loss function, we have employed a continuous version of the Potts energy, introduced in
[
35
]
:

H_{\text{Potts}}^{c}\big[\{\mathbf{y}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{V}}\big]=\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}}\langle\textbf{y}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{j}\rangle\;,

(9)

where the
a^{\text{th}}
component of
\mathbf{y}_{i}
, indicated as
y_{i}^{a}
, is the probability of the
a^{\text{th}}
color to be assigned to the
i^{\text{th}}
node. For this reason, both spaces described in Eq. (
8
) are extended to
\mathbb{R}^{q\times N}
.

Regarding the supervised term in the loss function, this is inspired by a denoising autoencoder
[
40
]
, which forces the model to map corrupted inputs by adding noise (thus moving them away from the solution clusters) into inputs belonging to the solution manifold. In our case, the model is trained by taking a planted solution
\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{V}}
, written in the extended space, to which noise
\{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{V}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\hat{I})
is added

\{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{V}}=\sqrt{\alpha}\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{V}}+\sqrt{1-\alpha}\{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{V}}

(10)

and including in the loss function a term which measures the closeness of the input planted graph to the output of the model.
At each training iteration,
\alpha
is sampled from the uniform distribution
\mathrm{Uniform}(\alpha_{\min},\alpha_{\max})
.

A third contribution to the loss is given by an entropy term, measuring the average output node entropy. This term is used only during the initial steps of training to help the algorithm escape the region of parameter space that corresponds to the paramagnetic state
\Big(\big[f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\{\textbf{x}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{V}})\big]_{j}^{a}=\frac{1}{q},\forall j\in\mathcal{V},\forall a\in\{1,\dddot{},q\}\Big)
. We have empirically observed that this term does not lead to a final lower energy but increases the convergence speed.
The final loss function is then given by

L(\{\textbf{x}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{V}},\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{V}};\boldsymbol{\theta})=\\
\mathbb{E}_{\{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{V}}}\big[h\left[f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\{\textbf{x}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{V}})\right]\big]\\
+\eta_{1}\,\mathbb{E}_{\{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{V}}}\big[S\left[f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\{\textbf{x}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{V}})\right]\big]\\
-\eta_{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{\{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{V}}}\big[O\left[f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\{\textbf{x}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{V}}),\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{V}}\right]\big]

where
\eta_{1}
and
\eta_{2}
are relative weights and the three contributions are respectively an
energy term
, namely the percentage of conflicting edges, the
features entropy
and the
overlap
with the planted solution. These terms are given by

\displaystyle h\left[f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\{\textbf{x}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{V}})\right]=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}}\langle f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\{\boldsymbol{x}\})_{i},f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\{\boldsymbol{x}\})_{j}\rangle

\displaystyle S\left[f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\{\textbf{x}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{V}})\right]=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}}f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\{\boldsymbol{x}\})_{i}\cdot\log_{2}f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\{\boldsymbol{x}\})_{i}

\displaystyle O\left[f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\{\textbf{x}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{V}}),\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}\right]=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{V}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}}\langle f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\{\boldsymbol{x}\})_{i},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}\rangle

where
A_{ij}
is the adjacency matrix and

h\left[\{\textbf{x}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{V}}\right]=\frac{1}{M}H_{\text{Potts}}^{c}\big[\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{V}}\big]

(11)

The overlap term is necessary to break the permutation symmetry of the colors. In fact, the energy and entropy terms in the loss are invariant for color permutations. Consequently the loss landscape contains
q!
identical regions, each corresponding to equivalent, permuted colorings. Breaking this symmetry simplifies the map that the model needs to learn, as the loss landscape is modified so that only the planted solution has the lowest loss, guiding the optimizer more efficiently.

The training phase of our model is detailed in Algorithm A.2 in the Supplementary Information.

6.4
Coloring

In this paragraph, we detail the coloring process using our trained model
f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\big(\cdot\big)
.
For a sufficiently expressive model, we would expect to find the perfect coloring with a single forward pass (
N_{\text{iter}}=1
).
For our model, however, we have empirically observed that this is not the case, as the output energy is further reduced with subsequent applications of the same trained model.
We call
N_{\text{iter}}
the number of iterations, that is, subsequent applications of the model.
Additionally, we have observed that the algorithm used is characterized by fixed points that do not correspond to the solutions of the GCP. This causes the algorithm to get stuck in sub-optimal configurations. We have verified that performances are greatly improved by adding noise after each forward step, as described in Eq.
10
.
We experimented with different choices for noise scheduling.
During this noise-annealing procedure, the neural network gradually reduces the energy of the input graph. If it is performed for a large enough number of iterations, this procedure eventually leads the graph into the attraction basin of a zero-energy solution. The scheduling of noise from large to smaller values has the meaning of letting the model explore a larger portion of the color space in the initial steps, while making the model collapse towards the zero-energy solution in the final steps. We have empirically observed how this procedure produces very similar results even when a solution is not guaranteed to exist.
A more detailed discussion of how noise modifies the forward step, together with a detailed description of the coloring procedure, is provided Section A.3 of the Supplementary Information.
