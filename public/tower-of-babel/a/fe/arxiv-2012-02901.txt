Near-Optimal Procedures for Model Discrimination with Non-Disclosure Properties
Dmitrii M. Ostrovskii Mohamed Ndaoud Adel Javanmard Meisam Razaviyayn

Abstract
Let θ 0 , θ 1 ∈ ℝ d \theta_{0},\theta_{1}\in\mathds{R}^{d} be the population risk minimizers associated to some loss ℓ : ℝ d × 𝒵 → ℝ \ell:\mathds{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{Z}\to\mathds{R} and two distributions ℙ 0 , ℙ 1 \mathds{P}_{0},\mathds{P}_{1} on 𝒵 \mathcal{Z} . The models θ 0 \theta_{0} and θ 1 \theta_{1} are unknown and the distributions ℙ 0 , ℙ 1 \mathds{P}_{0},\mathds{P}_{1} can be accessed by drawing i.i.d samples from them. Our work is motivated by the following model discrimination question: Given i.i.d. samples from ℙ 0 \mathds{P}_{0} and ℙ 1 \mathds{P}_{1} , what sample sizes are sufficient and necessary to distinguish between the two hypotheses θ ∗ = θ 0 \theta^{*}=\theta_{0} and θ ∗ = θ 1 \theta^{*}=\theta_{1} for given θ ∗ ∈ { θ 0 , θ 1 } \theta^{*}\in\{\theta_{0},\theta_{1}\} ? Making the first steps towards answering this question in full generality, we first consider the case of a well-specified linear model with squared loss. Here we provide matching upper and lower bounds on the sample complexity as given by min ⁡ { 1 / Δ 2 , r / Δ } \min\{1/\Delta^{2},\sqrt{r}/\Delta\} up to a constant factor; here Δ \Delta is a measure of separation between ℙ 0 \mathds{P}_{0} and ℙ 1 \mathds{P}_{1} and r r is the rank of the design covariance matrix. This bound is dimension-independent, and rank-independent for large enough separation. We then extend this result in two directions: (i) for the general parametric setup in asymptotic regime; (ii) for generalized linear models in the small-sample regime n ⩽ r n\leqslant r and under weak moment assumptions. In both cases we derive sample complexity bounds of a similar form while allowing for model misspecification. In fact, our testing procedures only access θ ∗ \theta^{*} through a certain functional of empirical risk. In addition, the number of observations that allows to reach statistical confidence in our tests does not allow to “resolve” the two models – that is, recover θ 0 , θ 1 \theta_{0},\theta_{1} up to O ⁡ ( Δ ) O(\Delta) prediction accuracy. These two properties allow to use our framework in applied tasks where one would like to identify a prediction model, which can be proprietary, while guaranteeing that the model cannot be actually inferred by the agent performing identification.

Introduction

Statistical theory teaches us that testing is generally easier than estimation or prediction.
This reasoning is exploited, for example, when proving minimax lower bounds in parametric estimation. Indeed, the minimax risk in such problems can often be lower-bounded in terms of the testing error in a hypothesis testing problem associated to the estimated signal or parameter
[
1
,
2
]
.
More interestingly, in some situations one observes
quantifiable gaps
between the sample complexity of an estimation or prediction problem and that of the testing problem associated to the initial problem in the natural sense.
1
1

1

For this expository discussion, we define the sample complexity of a (binary) testing problem as the size of an i.i.d. sample for which there exists a test with testing errors of both types at most
0.05
.
For example, such a situation arises in certain detection-type problems where the goal is to detect the presence of a ground-truth signal in the background noise.
Signal detection can be relatively easy because it does not necessarily entail localizing the signal or estimating its direction; thus one does not have to deal with the complexity of the associated hypotheses spaces.
Along these lines, statistical and computational gaps between signal detection and estimation have been observed, for example, in sparse linear regression
[
3
,
4
]
and in the spiked covariance model
[
5
]
.

In this paper, we identify and study a class of testing problems
in which the “testing-easier-than-estimation” phenomenon can be
used for practical purposes
, by relying on the following observation:

One might
test hypotheses
about a parametric predictive model by observing its output, with provable guarantees of not being able to actually
recover the model
,
whenever the number of observations necessary for recovery is larger than the one sufficient for testing.

(
\boldsymbol{\star}
)

Before we make this high-level observation more concrete, let us define the class of testing problems that we study in this paper.
This class is natural and interesting in its own right, not merely in the context of (

Much to our surprise, it seems to not have been studied in the literature so far.

Problem formulation.

Let
z
be a random observation in some space
\mathcal{Z}
, and let
\ell(\cdot,z):\mathds{R}^{d}\to\mathds{R}
be a random loss function associated to
z
.
Our task is to distinguish between the two hypotheses

\mathcal{H}_{0}:\{\theta^{*}=\theta_{0}\},\quad\mathcal{H}_{1}:\{\theta^{*}=\theta_{1}\}

(1)

where
\theta_{0},\theta_{1}
minimize the population risks
L_{0}(\cdot),L_{1}(\cdot)
associated with two distributions
\mathds{P}_{0},\mathds{P}_{1}
of
z
:

\theta_{k}=\argmin_{\theta\in\mathds{R}^{d}}\left\{L_{k}(\theta):=\mathds{E}_{z\sim\mathds{P}_{k}}[\ell(\theta,z)]\right\}\quad(k\in\{0,1\}).

(2)

In (
1
),
\theta^{*}\in\mathds{R}^{d}
is known; thus, hypotheses (
1
) can be understood as those about the unknown
\theta_{0},\theta_{1}
.
The loss
\ell(\cdot,z):\mathds{R}^{d}\to\mathds{R}
is also known, and is assumed strictly convex in
\theta
, which guarantees the uniqueness of
\theta_{0},\theta_{1}
.
To construct a test, the statistician can generate i.i.d. samples from
\mathds{P}_{0}
and
\mathds{P}_{1}
:

Z^{(0)}:=\left(z^{(0)}_{1},...,z^{(0)}_{\vphantom{0}n_{0}}\right)\sim\mathds{P}_{0}^{\otimes n_{0}},\quad Z^{(1)}:=\left(z^{(1)}_{1},...,z^{(1)}_{\vphantom{1}n_{1}}\right)\sim\mathds{P}_{1}^{\otimes n_{1}}.

(3)

The performance of a test
\widehat{T}:(\theta^{*},Z^{(0)},Z^{(1)},\ell)\mapsto\{0,1\}
can be measured by the largest of two error probabilities
\mathds{P}_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}[\widehat{T}=1],\mathds{P}_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}[\widehat{T}=0]
.
We characterize the sample complexity for this problem in terms of
n_{0},n_{1}
that are necessary and sufficient to guarantee fixed error probabilities of both types.

At first glance, the problem formulation in (
1
)–(
3

However, the testing procedures we develop to address it turn out to be useful in a number of practical tasks
where, conforming to (

1

identifying
a statistical prediction model while at the same time providing a guarantee that the model cannot be
recovered
by a testing agent with due accuracy.

Such guarantees are central for our testing procedures when looking at them from the viewpoint of applications.
We shall refer to such guarantees as the
non-disclosure property

1.1
Testing protocol and non-disclosure property

Access through empirical prediction scores.

The mechanism behind the non-disclosure property of our testing procedures relies on the following fact:

While in the formulation (
1
)–(
3
) the data
(\theta^{*},Z^{(0)},Z^{(1)},\ell)
is formally assumed to be known to the testing agent,
our
actual testing procedures
do not require direct access to these data.
Rather, the data is accessed through
a pair of
empirical prediction scores
.

Formally, the empirical prediction scores of
\theta^{*}
for two samples
Z^{(0)},Z^{(1)}
are the local Newton decrements (see
[
6
]
) of the empirical risks

\widehat{L}_{0}(\theta):=\frac{1}{n_{0}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{0}}\ell(\theta,z_{i}^{(0)}),\quad\widehat{L}_{1}(\theta):=\frac{1}{n_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\ell(\theta,z_{i}^{(1)})

at
\theta^{*}
, that is

\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{H}}_{0}(\theta^{*})^{{\dagger}/{2}}\nabla\widehat{L}_{0}(\theta^{*})\|^{2},\quad\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{H}}_{1}(\theta^{*})^{{\dagger}/{2}}\nabla\widehat{L}_{1}(\theta^{*})\|^{2}

(4)

where
\widehat{\boldsymbol{H}}_{k}(\theta)^{{\dagger}}
(
k\in\{0,1\}
) is the generalized inverse of the empirical risk Hessian
\widehat{\boldsymbol{H}}_{k}(\theta):=\nabla^{2}\widehat{L}_{k}(\theta)
, and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{H}}_{k}(\theta)^{{\dagger/2}}
is the (unique) positive-semidefinite square root of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{H}}_{k}(\theta)^{{\dagger}}
.
The Newton decrements (
4
) quantify how well
\theta^{*}
fits each of the two samples in terms of the local affine-invariant gradient norm.
Our testing procedures can be run without directly communicating the data
(\theta^{*},Z^{(0)},Z^{(1)},\ell)
to the testing agent, but only granting access to the Newton decrements.
3
3

3

This is a simplification for the initial discussion. In fact, in every setting of interest we start with a basic test that requires additional parameters of
\mathds{P}_{0},\mathds{P}_{1}
such as noise variances or effective ranks; we then construct an
adaptive
test that uses estimates obtained from
Z^{(0)},Z^{(1)}
or hold-out samples.
We shall address these considerations later on.

It is especially important here that
\theta^{*}
does not have to be directly communicated: as we shall see in Section
1.2
, in many applications
\theta^{*}
specifies the “proprietary” prediction model which must remain undisclosed to the testing agent.

Testing protocol.

The testing protocol which shall be presented next is motivated by the following considerations.
In applications to be discussed in Section
1.2
,
\theta^{*}
specifies a prediction model trained on a large dataset. Direct access to such model, which allows to compute the empirical prediction scores (
4
), is a prerogative of its owner, possibly a private corporation.
We shall refer to this entity as
Learner
.
On the other hand, testing the hypotheses in (
1
) is a task of a different entity, called
Auditor
, who cannot directly access
\theta^{*}
. We discuss several applications in details in Section
1.2
, but it would be useful to briefly consider an example here to motivate the testing protocol. A natural right for users of a platform is the “right to be forgotten”. The users may request to remove all or part of their data from the platform database e.g. due to privacy concerns. Nonetheless, the platform may have incentive to ignore such requests e.g., for a better predictive model or to avoid retraining its model. In this example,
\theta_{0}
and
\theta_{1}
correspond to the models trained with and without the datapoints to be removed, and an auditor would like to test which model is actually used by the platform.
Motivated by such applications, we require two natural constraints on the testing protocol:

(
a)

We would like to protect
Learner
’s model
\theta^{*}
from recovery by
Auditor
(in the exact sense to be defined in the next section) while still allowing
Auditor
to run the test and distinguish between the two hypotheses in (
1
), that is, conclude which of the two samples
Z^{(0)},Z^{(1)}
was generated by the distribution corresponding to
\theta^{*}
.

(
b)

In addition, we would like to protect the complementary model

\bar{\theta}:=\theta_{0}+\theta_{1}-\theta^{*}\vskip-5.69046pt

from recovery by either
Learner
or
Auditor
.

One way of respecting both these two requirements is by granting
Auditor
access only to the bare minimum of information that suffices to run the test, namely the two prediction scores (
4
) (this guarantees
(a)
) and by choosing the sample sizes
n_{0},n_{1}
small enough for not allowing to recover
\theta_{0},\theta_{1}
from the data
(Z^{(0)},Z^{(1)},\ell)
(this allows to guarantee
(b)
by exploiting (

⋆

‣
1
)).
This approach can be implemented in a formal
testing protocol
that involves three parties:
Learner
(owner of
\theta^{*}
),
Auditor
(or testing agent), and
sampling oracles
for
\mathds{P}_{0},\mathds{P}_{1}
(see Figure
1
):

Non-disclosure mechanism.

Above we described our preferred variant of the testing protocol with minimal access modality for
Auditor
.
In practice,
Auditor
might enjoy a more favorable access modality, say, have access to
(Z^{(0)},Z^{(1)},\ell)
in addition to
\widehat{S}_{0}(\theta^{*}),\widehat{S}_{1}(\theta^{*})
, or even have access to the full data
(\theta^{*},Z^{(0)},Z^{(1)},\ell)
if
Auditor
and
Learner
are united into a single entity (say, as two divisions in a single technology company).
Observe, however, that we can still fulfill both requirements
(a)
,
(b)
in the first of these cases, and requirement
(b)
in the second case, if we guarantee that the data

\left(Z^{(0)},Z^{(1)},\ell,\widehat{S}_{0}(\theta^{*}),\widehat{S}_{1}(\theta^{*})\right)

allows to recover neither
\theta_{0}
nor
\theta_{1}
.
Furthermore, intuitively these data contain only a little more statistical information about
\theta_{0}
and
\theta_{1}
than the data

\left(Z^{(0)},Z^{(1)},\ell\right)

—after all,
\widehat{S}_{0}(\theta^{*}),\widehat{S}_{1}(\theta^{*})
are just two random measurements, whereas each sample
Z^{(k)}
contains
n_{k}
measurements. (We refer to Appendix
F
for a formal discussion through the Fisher information.)
Let us now complete the description of the non-disclosure mechanism by specifying the precise meaning of “impossibility to recover
\theta_{0},\theta_{1}
from the data
\left(Z^{(0)},Z^{(1)},\ell\right)
.”

To this end, assume that
\theta_{0},\theta_{1}
are separated “in terms of excess risk,” namely

\displaystyle\Delta:=\min\left\{L_{0}(\theta_{1})-L_{0}(\theta_{0}),L_{1}(\theta_{0})-L_{1}(\theta_{1})\right\}>0.

(5)

In Section
1.3
, we derive sample complexity estimates for confident testing in (
1
)–(
3
), namely the required sample size such that both types of testing error are bounded by arbitrarily small, fixed value say 0.05. As we will see the derived sample complexity bounds for confident testing are controlled by the separation
\Delta
, and are orderwise (in terms of
\Delta
) smaller than the complexity of estimating
\theta_{0},\theta_{1}
from the data
(Z^{(0)},Z^{(1)},\ell)
up to excess risk
O(\Delta)
, i.e., the required sample size so that

\min\left\{\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}_{0}}[L_{0}(\widehat{\theta}_{0})]-L_{0}(\theta_{0}),\;\mathds{E}_{\mathds{P}_{1}}[L_{1}(\widehat{\theta}_{1})]-L_{1}(\theta_{1})\right\}=O(\Delta).

(6)

Therefore, one can choose the sample sizes
n_{0},n_{1}
that suffice for distinguishing between the two hypotheses in (
1
), but do
not
allow to recover
\theta_{0}
or
\theta_{1}
with the prediction accuracy better than
\Delta
, i.e., better than the one we get by simply using
\theta_{1}
instead of
\theta_{0}
and vice versa.

As a result, given full data
(\theta^{*},Z^{(0)},Z^{(1)},\ell)
,
Learner
cannot recover the complementary model
\bar{\theta}

with “prediction accuracy” better than
\Delta
.
Similarly, even when granted access to
(Z^{(0)},Z^{(1)},\ell)
,
Auditor
cannot recover
Learner
’s model
\theta^{*}
with prediction accuracy better than
\Delta
.

Remark 1
.

Our testing framework and the protocol in Figure
1
can be easily extended to the multiple-testing setup, where one has to distinguish between
m
hypotheses corresponding to
\mathds{P}_{0},...,\mathds{P}_{m-1}
. To this end, it suffices to increase the number of sampling oracles and replace the decision rule by
\widehat{T}\in\argmin_{\;k\in\{0,...,m-1\}}\widehat{S}_{k}(\theta^{*})
. Our theory can be easily extended to this multiple testing setup.

Next we discuss some practical applications of our testing framework.

1.2
Applications

Applications related to “the right to be forgotten.”

Ubiquitous collection and storage of large volumes of user data by internet corporations poses societal risks such as potential for unfair/discriminatory outcomes and privacy violations.
To confront such tendencies, governments and consumer protection agencies have established guidelines and regulations such as
[
7
,
8
,
9
]
.
In a nutshell, these documents require from organizations to provide users with ‘‘the right to be forgotten,’’ i.e., removal of users’ data upon request.
In the simplest scenario, this means removal of the part of a big dataset pertaining to a single user or a group of users.
However, the corporate entity might be incentivized to ignore or violate such a removal request for various reasons, such as potential degradation of the model performance after the data removal procedure or a high computational cost of retraining the model.
4
4

4

While techniques for removing the impact of data points from a model without retraining it have been proposed
[
10
]
, their analysis is only tight in simple cases, and lacks generalization guarantees.

Thus, users might be interested in verifying whether the corporate entity complied with their deletion requests. Ideally, such verification is to be done by a third party, without compromising users’ data nor revealing the trained model to the third party.

The testing protocol in Figure
1
can be used in such an application.
Here we view
\mathds{P}_{0}
as the original dataset and
\mathds{P}_{1}
the dataset after receiving (potentially many) data removal requests.
(Both datasets are assumed to be large, hence are modeled by population distributions.)
The respective population risk minimizers
\theta_{0},\theta_{1}
correspond to the model trained with or without the datapoints to be removed.
Under
\mathcal{H}_{1}
,
Learner
indeed removed the data, whereas
\mathcal{H}_{0}
corresponds to the request violation; in both cases,
\theta^{*}
is the
actual
model trained by
Learner
.
Auditor
, which can be a third party or an internal agent in a technological platform, can verify if the data removal indeed took place by asking
Learner
to evaluate the empirical prediction scores
\theta^{*}
on the “snapshots”
Z^{(0)},Z^{(1)}
of the full datasets, with and without the datapoints requested for deletion.
Such verification does not require to provide
Auditor
with direct access to neither the trained model
\theta^{*}
nor the full datasets
\mathds{P}_{0},\mathds{P}_{1}
, and protects
\theta^{*}
from being learned by
Auditor
as discussed in Section
1.1
.

Note that in this application, it would be problematic to assume that both sampling oracles are readily available.
Instead, it is reasonable to assume that
Auditor
has access to the dataset of deletion requests (let us call it
Q
), and can also ask
Learner
for a subsample of their data.
Now, we claim that this allows
Auditor
to emulate both sample oracles (for
\mathds{P}_{0}
and
\mathds{P}_{1}
) assuming that
Learner
behaves rationally, i.e., is interested in demonstrating
compliance with deletion requests
.

•

Indeed, first observe that
\mathds{P}_{0}=(1-\delta)\mathds{P}_{1}+\delta Q
, where
\delta
is the share of deletion requests; thus, assuming the knowledge of
\delta
, a sampling oracle for
\mathds{P}_{0}
can be emulated given those for
\mathds{P}_{1}
and
Q
. Observe also that
Auditor
has full access to
Q
, and thus can also sample from it.

•

On the other hand, a rational
Learner
, in the sense defined above, will sample from
\mathds{P}_{1}
under either hypothesis: clearly, this is the case under
\mathcal{H}_{1}
, but this is also the case under
\mathcal{H}_{0}
, since in this case
Learner
would like to feign the execution of deletion requests.
5
5

5

In fact, if any datapoint from
Q
is included into
Learner
’s subsample,
Auditor
can immediately detect
Learner
’s non-compliance
by finding a duplicate of this datapoint in
Q
. This is possible thanks to
Auditor
’s full access to
Q
.
This grants
Auditor
a sampling oracle for
\mathds{P}_{1}
, and thus for
\mathds{P}_{0}
.

Verification of fair representation of minority groups in training dataset.

In the context of fair machine learning
[
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
]
, a common task is to ensure that a prediction model does not lead to discriminatory outcomes against minority groups.
Such discriminatory outcomes may be caused by an imbalanced representation of sub-populations in the dataset used in training;
for example, the minority sub-populations will suffer higher test error due to their small share in the training dataset.
In this context, our testing protocol can be used to verify whether the training data has equal representation of sub-populations.
To this end, a data platform plays the role of
Learner
and has access to the trained model
\theta^{*}
while
Auditor
functions as a verification agent;
the sampling oracles can be implemented by collecting (small) datasets among separate subpopulations and appropriately mixing them.
More precisely, assume there are two different subpopulations that we shall call Democrats and Republicans.
The null-hypothesis corresponds to a balanced dataset with an equal representation of both of them, and the alternative is an imbalanced dataset skewed by a margin of
p-1/2>0

Denoting
\mathds{P}_{\textup{dem}}
and
\mathds{P}_{\textup{rep}}
the two populations, we have that
\mathds{P}_{0}=\tfrac{1}{2}\mathds{P}_{\textup{dem}}+\tfrac{1}{2}\mathds{P}_{\textup{rep}}
and
\mathds{P}_{1}=p\mathds{P}_{\textup{dem}}+(1-p)\mathds{P}_{\textup{rep}}
. The sampling oracles can then be implemented by sampling from
\mathds{P}_{\textup{dem}}
and
\mathds{P}_{\textup{rep}}
, tossing a fair coin for
\mathds{P}_{0}
, and a biased coin for
\mathds{P}_{1}
.

proposed in
[
15

16

17
]

1.3
Overview of technical contributions, literature and paper organization

We make important first steps towards solving the general case of the problem summarized in (
1
)–(
3
).

In Section
2
, we focus on the case of well-specified linear regression with random design, where we have an almost complete picture.
In other words, here we assume that
z=(x,y)
with
x\in\mathds{R}^{d}
and
y\in\mathds{R}
, and the distributions
\mathds{P}_{0},\mathds{P}_{1}
are given by

\mathds{P}_{k}:x\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}),\;y|x\sim\mathcal{N}(x^{\top}\theta_{k}^{\vphantom{2}},\sigma_{k}^{2})\;\;\text{for}\;\;k\in\{0,1\}.

(7)

We first assume the noise variances to be known, which is equivalent to
\sigma_{0}^{2}=\sigma_{1}^{2}=1
by rescaling; however, other parameters
\theta_{0},\theta_{1},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}
are assumed unknown.
Writing the two samples in (
3
) in a concise form as
(X^{(0)},Y^{(0)})
and
(X^{(1)},Y^{(1)})
with
X^{(k)}\in\mathds{R}^{n_{k}\times d}
,
Y^{(k)}\in\mathds{R}^{n_{k}}
for
k\in\{0,1\}
,
our approach is based on the high-level idea of comparing the squared norms of residuals at
\theta^{*}
for the two samples, that is
\|Y^{(0)}-X^{(0)}\theta^{*}\|^{2}
and
\|Y^{(1)}-X^{(1)}\theta^{*}\|^{2}
. We further adjust this idea as follows:

•

Instead of using the residuals directly, we first project them onto the respective “signal spaces”, i.e., the column spaces of
X^{(0)}
and
X^{(1)}
, thus passing to
\|\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{X^{(0)}}[Y^{(0)}-X^{(0)}\theta^{*}]\|^{2}
and
\|\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{X^{(1)}}[Y^{(1)}-X^{(1)}\theta^{*}]\|^{2}
. (Here
\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{A}:=A(A^{\top}A)^{\dagger}A^{\top}
is the projector onto
\textup{col}(A)
, the column space of
A
.)

•

Instead of comparing the squared norms of projected residuals, we compare the
deviations
of the squared norms from their conditional expectations under
\mathds{P}_{0},\mathds{P}_{1}
.
We exploit the fact that these expectations are observed and given by the ranks of empirical covariance matrices
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{0},\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{1}
.

Assuming
n_{0}=n_{1}
for the sake of simplicity, these adjustments result in a test with sample complexity

O\bigg(\min\bigg\{\frac{1}{\Delta^{2}},\frac{\sqrt{\max\{r_{0},r_{1}\}}}{\Delta}\bigg\}\bigg),

(8)

where
r_{k}=\textup{rank}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})
and
\Delta=\min\{\Delta_{0},\Delta_{1}\}
is the smallest of the two (squared) prediction distances

\Delta_{k}:=\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}^{1/2}(\theta_{1}-\theta_{0})\|^{2}\quad(k\in\{0,1\}).

Note that the sample complexity bound (
8
) depends on
\max\{r_{0},r_{1}\}
rather than the ambient dimension
d
, and even this dependency vanishes when
\Delta\geqslant 1/\sqrt{\max\{r_{0},r_{1}\}}
.
We later show that bound (
8
) is near-optimal in the minimax sense, up to the replacement of
\max\{r_{0},r_{1}\}
by
\min\{r_{0},r_{1}\}
.

Finally, we consider the case of unknown and unequal noise variances.
The challenge here is that the conditional expectations of
\|\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{X^{(0)}}[Y^{(0)}-X^{(0)}\theta^{*}]\|^{2}
and
\|\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{X^{(1)}}[Y^{(1)}-X^{(1)}\theta^{*}]\|^{2}
are not given explicitly anymore, and have to be inferred from observations.
Fortunately, this turns out to be possible: we construct an
adaptive
test that performs essentially the same as if
\sigma_{0}^{2},\sigma_{1}^{2}
were known.

In Section
3
, we revisit the general
M
-estimation setup.
Here we adjust the test introduced in the linear model setup by replacing the quantities
\|\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{X^{(0)}}[Y^{(0)}-X^{(0)}\theta^{*}]\|^{2}
and
\|\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{X^{(1)}}[Y^{(1)}-X^{(1)}\theta^{*}]\|^{2}
with
empirical prediction scores
, i.e., the Newton decrements (
4
) of empirical risks
\widehat{L}_{0}(\theta^{*}),\widehat{L}_{1}(\theta^{*})
over the two samples.
As the case of a linear model falls under this more general scenario, the lower bound on the sample complexity established in that case, and nearly matching (
8
), still applies.
However, deriving a matching upper bound proves to be challenging in the general case.
Hence, we focus on the asymptotic regime of large sample sizes and small prediction distances, namely
n_{0},n_{1}\to\infty
and
\Delta_{0},\Delta_{1}\to 0
with
finite
n_{k}\Delta_{k}\to\lambda_{k}
.
In this regime, a local Gaussian approximation applies
[
18
]
, and we are in a position to use the central limit theorem in our analysis.
In terms of the derived dependency of the testing risk from
\lambda
, the obtained results nearly match the lower bound, but also take into account the effect of model misspecification.
We also demonstrate that the second-order information plays a critical role: the risk for a natural test in which one measures the gradients of empirical risks without using their Hessians can be
arbitrarily larger
than for our test.

In Section
4
, we extend our theory in another direction, now focusing on the
small-sample regime

n_{k}\leqslant r_{k}\quad(k\in\{0,1\}).

(9)

While estimation is clearly impossible in this regime unless
\Delta\gg 1
, the testing problem is still well-posed.
Indeed, according to (
8
), in the simple case
n_{0}=n_{1}
the sample complexity is at most
O(\max\{r_{0},r_{1}\})
unless
\Delta
is very small (more precisely, unless
\Delta\ll 1/\sqrt{\max\{r_{0},r_{1}\}}
). Thus, for linear models condition (
9
) does not restrict us from constructing tests with near-optimal complexity bounds, provided that
r_{0},r_{1}
are of the same order and
\Delta
is not too small.
A similar conclusion for generalized linear models (GLMs) follows directly from the upper bound on sample complexity derived in Section
4
.
Namely, for GLMs under (
9
) we again use a Newton-decrement based statistic, and prove an analogue of bound (
8
) under weak moment assumptions and moderate level of misspecification.
As a byproduct, we extend the results of Sec.
2
to linear models with heavy-tailed noise distribution.
From a technical viewpoint, small sample size leads to the diagonalization of the Newton decrements, with two advantages as a result: (i) simpler statistical analysis; (ii) faster computation of the test.

In Section
5
, we present the results of numerical experiments.
They show that our approach has better empirical performance compared to those that do not use the second-order information and are based only on the values or gradients of the empirical risk.

Related work.

Our testing problem, as formulated in (
1
)–(
3
), appears to be new.
The closest line of research concerns hypothesis testing in linear regression. Related literature is very rich and it would be hard to thoroughly overview it here.
Still, one important direction is signal testing in linear regression
[
19
,
20
,
21
]
. Variants of this problem were considered when the signal is sparse and the noise either known
[
22
]
or unknown
[
23
]
. Other works test, in the same flavor, sparsity of the signal
[
24
]
or some component of the signal
[
25
]
.
We encourage an interested reader to study these recent works and references therein for further details.

Let us, however, emphasize the differences between the classical setup of parametric testing and our setup in (
1
)-(
3
).
In the classical parametric testing setup, one is given a sample from a parametric distribution with unknown mean
\theta^{*}
and we are asked to choose
\theta^{*}
from
\{\theta_{0},\theta_{1}\}
.
In contrast, in (
1
)-(
3
) one is given a parameter
\theta^{*}
and two samples with respective means
\{\theta_{0},\theta_{1}\}
to choose from. While we only have access to one of the two parameters, we have access to more samples than a single one.
Thus, the classical setup is sample-focused, while ours is parameter-focused.

Finally, it should be mentioned that our problem shares some similarities with the general two-sample testing problems (see, e.g.,
[
26
,
27
,
28
]
and references therein), where the statistician is given two independent samples and is asked to determine whether they come from the same distribution—in other words, to test
\mathcal{H}_{0}:\mathds{P}_{0}=\mathds{P}_{1}
against
\mathcal{H}_{1}:\mathds{P}_{0}\neq\mathds{P}_{1}
. Here the main distinction from our setup is the absence of the knowledge of
\theta^{*}\in\{\theta_{0},\theta_{1}\}
. This knowledge turns out to be instrumental in the model discrimination problem, allowing to circumvent the estimation of
\theta_{0},\theta_{1}
and thus ultimately leading to confident testing im the small-sample regime (
9
). To the best of our knowledge, analogous phenomena have not been observed in the context of two-sample testing.

Well-specified linear models

In this section, we consider well-specified linear models – in other words, the case of linear prediction (as defined in Sec.
1
) with
\ell_{z}(\theta)=(y-x^{\top}\theta)^{2}
and Gaussian conditional distributions of
y|x
such that

y=x^{\top}\theta_{k}+\varepsilon,\;\;\varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)\quad(k\in\{0,1\})

(10)

with
\varepsilon
independent from
x
.
For convenience, let us denote the corresponding conditional distribution of
y|x
(i.e.,
\mathcal{N}(x^{\top}\theta_{k},1)
) by
\mathds{P}_{k}[\cdot|x]
. In order to highlight the intuition behind our approach, we begin with a fixed-design scenario, so that all randomness of
y
stems from the noise term
\varepsilon
.
Later on, we address the random-design scenario, thus returning to our canonical problem formulation as in Sec.
1
.
This requires an additional step of marginalizing over
x
in the analysis, and results in the appearance of the ranks
r_{0},r_{1}
of
population covariances

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}
in the error bound and sample complexity estimate.
Another simplification in (
10
) is the implicit assumption of the uniform noise variance under both models, i.e.,
\varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
instead of
\varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{k}^{2})
for
k\in\{0,1\}
. When variances
\sigma_{0}^{2},\sigma_{1}^{2}
are different but
known
, we can simply rescale the two samples dividing
(X^{(k)},Y^{(k)})
by
\sigma_{k}
;
however, this is impossible without knowledge of
\sigma_{0}
and
\sigma_{1}
.
Therefore, in Section
2.3
we shall construct a test which is adaptive to the unknown noise variances.

2.1
Basic test and statistical guarantee

Let
(x_{i}{}^{(k)},y_{i}{}^{(k)}),
for
k\in\{0,1\}
and
i\in[n_{k}]
, be two samples with
y_{1}{}^{(k)},...,y_{n_{k}}{}^{(k)}
distributed independently according to
\mathds{P}_{k}[\cdot|x_{i}{}^{(k)}]
as in (
10
).
Writing them in a matrix form as
X^{(k)}\in\mathds{R}^{n_{k}\times d}
and
Y^{(k)}\in\mathds{R}^{n_{k}}
,
we first consider the test

\widehat{T}=\mathds{1}\left\{\|Y^{(0)}-X^{(0)}\theta^{*}\|^{2}_{X^{(0)}}-\textup{rank}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{0})\geqslant\|Y^{(1)}-X^{(1)}\theta^{*}\|^{2}_{X^{(1)}}-\textup{rank}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{1})\right\}.

(
Lin
)

(Recall that
\|u\|_{A}:=\|\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{A}u\|^{2}
for
u\in\mathds{R}^{n}
, where
\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{A}:=A(A^{\top}A)^{\dagger}A^{\top}
is the projector onto the column space
\textup{col}(A)
of
A\in\mathds{R}^{n\times d}
.)
Test (
Lin
) combines two ideas.
First, the residuals are projected onto their respective signal subspaces
\textup{col}(X^{(0)})
and
\textup{col}(X^{(1)})
.
This reduces the noise variance without affecting the signal magnitude.
Second, instead of directly comparing the squared norms of (projected) residuals, we compare their deviations from the corresponding sample covariance ranks.
A direct calculation shows that the sample covariance rank is precisely the conditional (on the design) expectation of the corresponding residual under the matching hypothesis.
Thus, subtracting the ranks debiases the squared norms and improves statistical performance of the resulting test.

In order to quantify the statistical performance of (
Lin
), we introduce two measures of separation:

\widehat{\Delta}_{k}=\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{k}^{1/2}(\theta_{1}-\theta_{0})\|^{2}\quad(k\in\{0,1\})

(11)

where
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{k}=\tfrac{1}{n_{k}}{X^{(k)\top}}X^{(k)}
is the corresponding sample covariance matrix.
Essentially,
\widehat{\Delta}_{k}
controls the separation between
\mathds{P}_{0}
and
\mathds{P}_{1}
from the viewpoint of the
k
-th model.
Now our first result follows.

Proposition 1
.

The type I error probability of test (
Lin
) is bounded as

\mathds{P}_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}[\widehat{T}=1|X^{(0)},X^{(1)}]\leqslant C\exp\bigg(-cn_{1}\widehat{\Delta}_{1}\min\bigg\{1,\frac{n_{1}\widehat{\Delta}_{1}}{\max\{\textup{rank}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{0}),\textup{rank}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{1})\}}\bigg\}\bigg)\,,

(12)

for some constants
c,C>0
. The type II error admits a similar bound with the replacement
1\mapsto 0
.

We now sketch the proof of this result. The full proof, as well as the proofs of subsequent results, is deferred to appendix.
Denote
\widehat{r}_{k}:=\textup{rank}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{k})
, and let
\xi^{(0)},\xi^{(1)}
be the additive noise vectors:

\xi^{(k)}:=Y^{(k)}-X^{(k)}\theta_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\boldsymbol{I}_{n_{k}}).

Observing that
\|X^{(1)}(\theta_{0}-\theta_{1})\|^{2}=n_{1}\widehat{\Delta}_{1}
we bound the type-I error probability as follows:

\displaystyle\mathds{P}_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}[\widehat{T}=1|X^{(0)},X^{(1)}]

\displaystyle=\mathds{P}\big[\|\xi^{(0)}\|^{2}_{X^{(0)}}-\widehat{r}_{0}+\widehat{r}_{1}-\|\xi^{(1)}\|^{2}_{X^{(1)}}+2\langle X^{(1)}(\theta_{0}-\theta_{1}),\xi^{(1)}\rangle\geqslant n_{1}\widehat{\Delta}_{1}\big]

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathds{P}\bigg[\|\xi^{(0)}\|^{2}_{X^{(0)}}-\widehat{r}_{0}\hskip-1.9919pt\geqslant\hskip-1.9919pt\frac{n_{1}\widehat{\Delta}_{1}}{3}\bigg]+\mathds{P}\bigg[\widehat{r}_{1}-\|\xi^{(1)}\|^{2}_{X^{(1)}}\hskip-1.9919pt\geqslant\hskip-1.9919pt\frac{n_{1}\widehat{\Delta}_{1}}{3}\bigg]+\mathds{P}\bigg[\langle X^{(1)}(\theta_{0}-\theta_{1}),\xi^{(1)}\rangle\geqslant\frac{n_{1}\widehat{\Delta}_{1}}{6}\bigg].

The first two terms are controlled through the standard chi-squared tail bounds (see, e.g.,
[
29
, Lem. 1]
).
Moreover,
\langle X^{(1)}(\theta_{0}-\theta_{1}),\xi^{(1)}\rangle\sim\mathcal{N}(0,n_{1}\widehat{\Delta}_{1})
conditionally on
X^{(1)}
,
and the last deviation probability can be controlled via the standard Gaussian tail bound.
Combining these results yields (
12
).
∎

Remark 2
.

Proposition
1
can be extended to the case where the additive noise is subgaussian. We here avoid such an extension, as in Section
4
we establish more general results that hold for generalized linear models under weak moment assumptions, and allow for model misspecification.

Our next goal is to extend Proposition
1
to the random-design setup, conforming to the scenario of repeated i.i.d. observations in Section
1
.
We specify two distributions
\mathds{P}_{0},\mathds{P}_{1}
of
(x,y)
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathds{P}_{k}:x\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}),\;y|x

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}(x^{\top}\theta_{k},1)\quad(k\in\{0,1\}).

(13)

Furthermore, we let
(X^{(k)},Y^{(k)})
represent the i.i.d. sample from
\mathds{P}_{k}
with size
n_{k}
(
k\in\{0,1\}
).
Finally, we let
r_{k}=\textup{rank}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})
for
k\in\{0,1\}
.
Our test is still (
Lin
), but we now additionally observe that

\widehat{r}_{k}=\textup{rank}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{k})=\min\{n_{k},r_{k}\}\quad\text{a.s.}

(More generally, this is the case when the marginal distribution of
x
has density on its support).
Next we characterize the sample complexity of reliable testing in terms of the
population separations

\Delta_{0},\Delta_{1}
,

\Delta_{k}:=\mathds{E}_{k}[\widehat{\Delta}_{k}]=\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}^{1/2}(\theta_{1}-\theta_{0})\|^{2}\quad(k\in\{0,1\}),

(14)

i.e., the squared Mahalanobis distances between
\theta_{0},\theta_{1}
associated with the two covariance matrices.

Theorem 1
.

In the random-design setup specified above, the type I error of test (
Lin
) satisfies

\mathds{P}_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}[\widehat{T}=1]\leqslant C\exp\bigg(-cn_{1}\Delta_{1}\min\bigg\{1,\frac{n_{1}\Delta_{1}}{\bar{r}}\bigg\}\bigg)+C\exp(-cn_{1}),

(15)

\text{where}\;\;\bar{r}:=\max\{\min\{n_{0},r_{0}\},\min\{n_{1},r_{1}\}\}.

(16)

The type II error probability admits a similar bound with the replacement
1\mapsto 0
.

In the proof of Theorem
1
, we first condition on
X^{(0)},X^{(1)}
and repeat the analysis leading to Proposition
1
.
Then we add a marginalization step, which leads to the result.
The term
\exp(-cn_{1})
in (
15
) appears in this step.
When
\Delta_{1}\leqslant c
, this term is dominated, and the guarantee is essentially the same as in the fixed-design scenario, except that
\widehat{\Delta}_{1}
gets replaced with its expectation
\Delta_{k}
.

Next we examine Theorem
1
in more detail and discuss its implications.
For the sake of simplicity, until the end of this section we assume
n_{0}=n_{1}[:=n]
and
\max\{\Delta_{0},\Delta_{1}\}\leqslant 1
.
Clearly, the latter assumption is rather mild as it only excludes very simple problems which are of little interest anyway.
We also define
r:=\max\{r_{0},r_{1}\}
, so that
\bar{r}=\min\{r,n\}
.

First, with some algebra we translate (
15
)
into the sample complexity bound (
8
) announced in Section
1.3
.
More precisely, we establish the following result.

Corollary 1
.

Assuming
\max\{\Delta_{0},\Delta_{1}\}\leqslant 1
, the sample complexity of distinguishing between two linear models, i.e.,
n=n_{0}=n_{1}
that guarantees
\max\{\mathds{P}_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}[\widehat{T}=1],\mathds{P}_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}[\widehat{T}=0]\}\leqslant\delta<1/2
, is

n=O\left(\min\left\{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\Delta^{2}},\frac{\sqrt{r\log(1/\delta)}+\log(1/\delta)}{\Delta}\right\}\right),

(17)

where
\Delta=\min\{\Delta_{0},\Delta_{1}\}
and
r=\max\{r_{0},r_{1}\}
. This reduction is tight: conversely, when
\max\{\Delta_{0},\Delta_{1}\}\leqslant 1
, bounding the testing errors of both types simultaneously by
\delta
, as per (
15
), requires
n
to be at least as in (
17
).

Corollary
1
is proved in Appendix
A
.
From this result we see that the sample complexity of testing with fixed confidence is
O(\min\{1/\Delta^{2},\sqrt{r}/\Delta\})
.
In particular, in the
well-separated regime

\Delta=\Omega\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{r}}\right),

(18)

the sample complexity becomes rank-independent, scaling as
n=\Omega(1/\Delta^{2})
.
An intuitive explanation of this result is that testing does
not
require to estimate the population risk minimizers
\theta_{0},\theta_{1}
—which would inevitably result in a rank-dependent complexity term.
In fact, our analysis in the proofs of Proposition
1
and Theorem
1
shows that the
O(1/\Delta^{2})
complexity term appears because of the random fluctuations of a centered chi-squared noise around its mean
\bar{r}=\min\{r,n\}
, which does not exceed the population rank
r
.
On the procedural level, we achieve this effect in (
Lin
) by projecting the residuals onto the column spaces
\textup{col}(X^{(0)}),\textup{col}(X^{(1)})
with dimensions
\widehat{r}_{k}\leqslant r_{k}
,
k\in\{0,1\}
.

In what follows next, we discuss the sample complexity result (
17
) in a somewhat broader context.

2.2
Discussion of implications

Key implication: model non-disclosure.

In the context of the testing protocol discussed in Section
1.2
,
test (
Lin
) provides a guarantee that the model
\theta^{*}
trained by
Learner
cannot be inferred by
Auditor
(in the variant of the protocol where the two entities are separated).
Indeed, as we discussed in Section
1.2
, we cannot hope to estimate
\theta^{*}
up to prediction error
\Delta
(in the covariance matrix corresponding to the true hypothesis) without estimating at least one of the two parameters
\theta_{k}
(
k\in\{0,1\}
) with prediction error
\Delta_{k}
, i.e., such that
\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}{}^{1/2}(\widehat{\theta}_{k}-\theta_{k})\|^{2}\leqslant\Delta_{k}
.
When
\theta_{k}
is arbitrary, this is known to require
\Omega(r_{k}/\Delta_{k})
sample size for any estimator (see, e.g.,
[
1
]
).
In the “typical” situation where
\Delta_{0},\Delta_{1}
, as well as
r_{0},r_{1}
, are of the same order, this lower bound on sample complexity reduces to
\Omega(r/\Delta)
, which orderwise (in terms of
\Delta
) dominates the upper bound
\min\{1/\Delta^{2},\sqrt{r}/\Delta\}
in Corollary
1
.
Thus, one can choose the sample sizes that suffice for confident testing, yet do not allow to recover
\theta_{0},\theta_{1}
up to
O(\Delta)
prediction accuracy.

In fact, this conclusion extends beyond the case of well-specified linear models to other scenarios.
Indeed, for general
M
-estimators, the lower bound for the complexity of estimating
\theta_{k}
(with respect to the excess risk, i.e., such that
L_{k}(\widehat{\theta}_{k})-L_{k}(\theta_{k})\leqslant\Delta_{k}
) is generally as bad as
\Omega(r_{k}/\Delta_{k})
: this is already so in the case of linear models, in which
L_{k}(\widehat{\theta}_{k})-L_{k}(\theta_{k})=\tfrac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}^{1/2}(\widehat{\theta}_{k}-\theta_{k})\|^{2}
.
In particular, estimation with prediction accuracy
\Delta_{k}\leqslant 1
is generally impossible when
n_{k}\leqslant r_{k}
. On the other hand, in Section
4
we show that the rank-independent bound on the sample complexity of testing,

O\left(\frac{1}{\Delta^{2}}\right),

extends to generalized linear models under (
9
) provided that
\Delta=\Omega(1/\sqrt{\min\{r_{0},r_{1}\}})
(see the discussion after Theorem
4
). In this regime
1/\Delta^{2}\ll r/\Delta
, i.e., testing is way easier than recovery.
Complementary to this result, in Section
3
we consider the case of general
M
-estimators in the large sample size regime (with
n\to\infty,\Delta\to 0
, and fixed ranks)
and show that testing is possible whenever

n=O\left(\frac{\sqrt{r_{\textsf{eff}}}}{\Delta}\right)

where
r_{\textsf{eff}}
is the largest of the two effective ranks (under
\mathds{P}_{0}
and
\mathds{P}_{1}
) reducing to
r
in the case of well-specified models (see Proposition
3
for the exact statement).
Typically, we expect
r_{\textsf{eff}}=O(r)
, and the sample complexity of testing is
O(\sqrt{r}/\Delta)
, again way smaller than the complexity of recovery.

“Plug-in” interpretation of test (
Lin
).

There is an interesting alternative interpretation of test (
Lin
). Observe that the normal equation for the least-squares estimate
\widehat{\theta}_{k}
of
\theta_{k}
reads

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{k}\widehat{\theta}_{k}=\tfrac{1}{n_{k}}X^{(k)\top}Y^{(k)}.

(19)

This allows to rewrite the statistic in (
Lin
): omitting the subscript
k
and superscript
(k)
for brevity,

\displaystyle\|Y-X\theta^{*}\|^{2}_{X}=(Y-X\theta^{*})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{X}(Y-X\theta^{*})

\displaystyle=(X^{\top}Y-X^{\top}X\theta^{*})^{\top}(X^{\top}X)^{\dagger}(X^{\top}Y-X^{\top}X\theta^{*})

\displaystyle=n^{2}(\widehat{\theta}-\theta^{*})^{\top}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}(X^{\top}X)^{\dagger}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}(\widehat{\theta}-\theta^{*})

\displaystyle=n(\widehat{\theta}-\theta^{*})^{\top}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{\dagger}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}(\widehat{\theta}-\theta^{*})

\displaystyle=n(\widehat{\theta}-\theta^{*})^{\top}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}(\widehat{\theta}-\theta^{*})

\displaystyle=n\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{1/2}(\widehat{\theta}-\theta^{*})\|^{2}.

(20)

As a result, we recast test (
Lin
) as

\widehat{T}=\mathds{1}\big\{n_{0}\big\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{0}^{1/2}(\theta^{*}-\widehat{\theta}_{0})\big\|^{2}-\widehat{r}_{0}\geqslant n_{1}\big\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{1}^{1/2}(\theta^{*}-\widehat{\theta}_{1})\big\|^{2}-\widehat{r}_{1}\big\}.

(21)

Now, assume (w.l.o.g.) that
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}
are both full-rank, and let
n_{k}\geqslant r_{k}
(
k\in\{0,1\}
) so that
\widehat{\theta}_{0},\widehat{\theta}_{1}
are defined unambiguously as the unique solutions to (
19
).
In this regime, we can interpret (
21
) as a test that constructs plug-in estimates of the squared empirical prediction distances
\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{0}{}^{1/2}(\theta^{*}-\theta_{0})\|^{2}
and
\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{1}{}^{1/2}(\theta^{*}-\theta_{1})\|^{2}
of
\theta^{*}
to
\theta_{0}
and to
\theta_{1}
, then rescales and debiases these estimates conditionally on
X^{(0)},X^{(1)}
under the matching hypothesis, and compares the results.
However, equations (
19
) characterize the solutions
\widehat{\theta}_{0},\widehat{\theta}_{1}
to the corresponding least-squares problems even in the small-sample regime, where these solutions are not unique, let alone concentrate around
\theta_{0},\theta_{1}
.
Yet, the squared norms used in (
21
) are still well-defined due to (
20
).
In this sense, the “plug-in” interpretation (cf. (
21
)) of test (
Lin
) is limited and can be misleading.

However, the “plug-in” interpretation (
21
) is still useful since it provides an alternative explanation of the terms
\widehat{r}_{0},\widehat{r}_{1}
appearing in (
21
).
Indeed, instead of (
21
) one could think of using a simpler test

\widehat{T}=\mathds{1}\big\{\big\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{0}^{1/2}(\theta^{*}-\widehat{\theta}_{0})\big\|^{2}\geqslant\big\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{1}^{1/2}(\theta^{*}-\widehat{\theta}_{1})\big\|^{2}\big\},

(22)

i.e., directly compare the plug-in estimates of the squared prediction distances
\big\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}^{1/2}(\theta^{*}-\theta_{k})\big\|^{2}
(
k\in\{0,1\}
) one of which vanishes under the matching hypothesis.
However, this approach does not take into account the additional noise arising due to the random fluctuations of
\widehat{\theta}_{k}
around
\theta_{k}
.
To illustrate this issue, it suffices to consider an idealized test with the population covariances, namely

\widehat{T}=\mathds{1}\big\{\big\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0}^{1/2}(\theta^{*}-\widehat{\theta}_{0})\big\|^{2}\geqslant\big\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}^{1/2}(\theta^{*}-\widehat{\theta}_{1})\big\|^{2}\big\}.

(23)

Note that
\mathds{E}[\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}^{1/2}(\widehat{\theta}_{k}-\theta_{k})\|^{2}]={r_{k}}/{n_{k}}
; thus, under
\mathcal{H}_{0}
the expectation of the left-hand side in (
23
) is
r_{0}/n_{0}
and of the right-hand side is
\Delta_{1}+r_{1}/n_{1}
.
As a result, in the case
n_{0}=n_{1}[=n]
, the type I error for (
23
) cannot be controlled when
n<(r_{0}-r_{1})/\Delta_{1}
; similarly, the type II error cannot be controlled when
n<(r_{1}-r_{0})/\Delta_{0}
; overall, we cannot control at least one of these errors when
n<|r_{1}-r_{0}|/\Delta
.
Hence, when the ranks
r_{0},r_{1}
are significantly different, and when
n_{0}=n_{1}
, the sample complexity for (
23
) is at least
\Omega(r/\Delta)
– way larger than the upper bound
O(\min\{\sqrt{r}/\Delta,1/\Delta^{2}\})
for (
Lin
).

It is also clear that the above-described issue cannot be solved by choosing the sample sizes proportionally to the ranks (when this is allowed, and when the ranks are known) whenever
\min\{r_{0},r_{1}\}
is way smaller than
\max\{r_{0},r_{1}\}
. Indeed, while such an approach would guarantee that
r_{0}/n_{0}=r_{1}/n_{1}
and thus correct for the additional bias due to
r_{0}\neq r_{1}
, it would also result in increased magnitude of fluctuations, compared to the choice
n_{0}=n_{1}
, as the sample size corresponding to the smallest rank must also be very small.
Finally, when
r_{0}=r_{1}
and
n_{0}=n_{1}
, there is no issue with additional bias to begin with, and in this case (
22
) reduces to (
Lin
).

Necessity of projection.

Consider the small sample regime
n\leqslant\min\{r_{0},r_{1}\}
(cf. (
9
)).
In this regime,
X^{(0)}
and
X^{(1)}
have full column ranks (
n_{0}
and
n_{1}
respectively)
and test (
Lin
) simplifies to

\widehat{T}=\mathds{1}\{\|Y^{(0)}-X^{(0)}\theta^{*}\|^{2}-n_{0}\geqslant\|Y^{(1)}-X^{(1)}\theta^{*}\|^{2}-n_{1}\}.

(24)

This simple observation has several implications.

•

In the well-separated regime (cf. (
18
)), the sample complexity upper bound for (
Lin
), cf. (
17
), falls into the range
n\leqslant r
.
When
r_{0}
and
r_{1}
are of the same order, confident testing in the well-separated regime thus only requires the sample size
n=O(\min\{r_{0},r_{1}\})
.
One can then run (
Lin
) with such sample sizes to begin with, thus effectively reducing it to (
24
) and avoid the computational burden of performing projections.

•

On the other hand, the projection step is crucial for
ill-separated
problems, i.e., when
\Delta\ll 1/\sqrt{r}
.
Indeed, in this case the sample complexity bound in (
17
) falls
beyond
the range
n\leqslant r
and test (
24
), which does not project onto
\textup{col}(X^{(0)})
and
\textup{col}(X^{(1)})
, becomes suboptimal.
More precisely, one can easily verify (by mimicking the decomposition in the proof of Proposition
1
) that the sample complexity for test (
24
)
is
O(\max\{1/\Delta^{2},1/\Delta\})
, i.e.,
O(1/\Delta^{2})
unless
\Delta\gg 1
, whereas for (
Lin
) it is
O(\sqrt{r}/\Delta)\ll 1/\Delta^{2}
.

•

In the general
M
-estimation setup (as defined in (
1
)-(
3
)),
we naturally generalize test (
24
) to

\widehat{T}=\mathds{1}\big\{n_{0}\widehat{L}_{0}(\theta^{*})-n_{0}L_{0}(\theta_{0})\geqslant n_{1}\widehat{L}_{1}(\theta^{*})-n_{1}L_{1}(\theta_{1})\big\},

(
Val
)

where
\widehat{L}_{k}(\theta)
and
L_{k}(\theta)
are, respectively, the empirical and population risks (
k\in\{0,1\}
).
An immediate problem with this generalization is that the population risks
L_{k}(\theta_{k})
are generally unobservable, unless in the exceptional case of a well-specified linear model where
L_{k}(\theta_{k})=1
.
Thus, test (
Val
) generally cannot be implemented.

Meanwhile, in Sections
3
-
4
we shall see that test (
Lin
) does generalize beyond the setup of a well-specified linear model,
as the corresponding debiasing terms (replacing
\widehat{r}_{0},\widehat{r}_{1}
in (
Lin
)) can be estimated from observations. Moreover, the reduction of (
Lin
) to (
Val
) for small sample sizes is also limited to the case of a well-specified linear model.
This is because the terms generalizing
\|Y^{(0)}-X^{(0)}\theta^{*}\|^{2}_{X^{(0)}}
and
\|Y^{(1)}-X^{(1)}\theta^{*}\|^{2}_{X^{(1)}}
in (
Lin
) will stem from the second-order Taylor approximation of empirical risks rather than the empirical risks themselves.

In the next section, we consider the “heterogeneous” scenario in which the noise variance depends on the hypothesis and is unknown.
We construct an adaptive test that performs essentially as good as in the case of known variances, albeit this requires an extra assumption in small-sample regime.

2.3
Adaptation to unknown noise levels

Recall that test (
Lin
), in its precise form, is designed for the case of unit variance of additive noise under both hypotheses, cf. (
10
).
If this assumption is not met, i.e.,
\mathds{P}_{k}[y|x]
reads

y=x^{\top}\theta_{k}+\sigma_{k}\varepsilon,\;\;\varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)\quad(k\in\{0,1\}),

(25)

instead of (
10
), then test (
Lin
) can be replaced with the following one:

\widehat{T}=\mathds{1}\left\{\frac{1}{\sigma_{0}^{2}}\|Y^{(0)}-X^{(0)}\theta^{*}\|^{2}_{X^{(0)}}-\textup{rank}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{0})\geqslant\frac{1}{\sigma_{1}^{2}}\|Y^{(1)}-X^{(1)}\theta^{*}\|^{2}_{X^{(1)}}-\textup{rank}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{1})\right\}.

(
Lin-Var
)

Since (
Lin-Var
) reduces to (
Lin
) when each sample is rescaled by
1/\sigma_{k}
, the results in Section
2.1
generalize for this test, with
\widehat{\Delta}_{k}
(and correspondingly
\Delta_{k}
) rescaled by
1/\sigma_{k}^{2}
.
However, such a reduction requires the knowledge of both noise variances
\sigma_{0}^{2},\sigma_{1}^{2}
.
Next we construct an adaptive counterpart of test (
Lin-Var
) that admits similar statistical guarantees without such knowledge.
To that end, we construct some estimates
\widehat{\sigma}_{0}^{2},\widehat{\sigma}_{1}^{2}
of
\sigma_{0}^{2},\sigma_{1}^{2}
and plug them in (
Lin-Var
), thus arriving at

\widehat{T}=\mathds{1}\left\{\frac{1}{\widehat{\sigma}_{0}^{2}}\|Y^{(0)}-X^{(0)}\theta^{*}\|^{2}_{X^{(0)}}-\textup{rank}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{0})\geqslant\frac{1}{\widehat{\sigma}_{1}^{2}}Y^{(1)}-X^{(1)}\theta^{*}\|^{2}_{X^{(1)}}-\textup{rank}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{1})\right\}.

(
Lin-Var
+
)

Assuming
n_{0}=n_{1}=n
for the sake of simplicity, we construct
\widehat{\sigma}_{k}^{2}
(
k\in\{0,1\}
) in two different ways, depending on how large is
n
compared to
\widehat{r}_{k}=\textup{rank}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{k})
.
Namely, if
n
is large enough, then we construct estimates with sufficient accuracy without additional assumptions;
however, in the small-sample regime we in addition assume that
y
can be resampled conditionally on
x
(once).

Assumption 1
.

One can sample
\widetilde{Y}^{(k)}
such that
\widetilde{Y}^{(k)},Y^{(k)}
are i.i.d. conditionally on
X^{(k)}
(
k\in\{0,1\}
).

Admittedly, this assumption is rather strong. However, we only needed it in the small-sample regime.
We now present the variance estimates (we omit the subscript in
X^{(k)}
and
Y^{(k)}
for brevity).

•

Case
n\geqslant 2\widehat{r}_{k}
(note that this is equivalent to
n\geqslant 2r_{k}
since
\widehat{r}_{k}=\min\{n,r_{k}\}

a.s
).
Here we put

\widehat{\sigma}_{k}^{2}=\frac{\|Y-X\theta^{*}\|^{2}-\|Y-X\theta^{*}\|^{2}_{X}}{n-\widehat{r}_{k}}.

(26)

The idea is to estimate the noise variance from the component of the residual which is orthogonal to the signal subspace and thus only contains the noise.

•

Case
n\leqslant 2\widehat{r}_{k}
(similarly, this is equivalent to
n\leqslant 2r_{k}
).
Here we leverage Assumption
1

and let

\widehat{\sigma}_{k}^{2}=\frac{\|{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{k}}^{\dagger/2}X^{\top}[Y-\widetilde{Y}]\|^{2}}{2n\widehat{r}_{k}}.

(27)

Here the idea is to project onto the signal subspace while eliminating the signal via resampling.
Note that whenever
n\leqslant r_{k}
, estimate (
27
) reduces to
\tfrac{1}{2n}\|Y-\widetilde{Y}\|^{2}
, as in this case
\widehat{r}_{k}=n
and

\frac{\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{k}^{\dagger/2}X^{\top}(Y-\widetilde{Y})\|^{2}}{2n\widehat{r}_{k}}=\frac{\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{k}^{\dagger/2}X^{\top}(Y-\widetilde{Y})\|^{2}}{2n^{2}}=\frac{\|\Pi_{X}[Y-\widetilde{Y}]\|^{2}}{2n}=\frac{\|Y-\widetilde{Y}\|^{2}}{2n}

where the last step uses that
X
has full column rank.
Note also that, strictly speaking, we do not need access to
\widetilde{Y}
itself – rather, to the right-hand side of (
27
) or to
\|Y-\widetilde{Y}\|^{2}
when
n\leqslant r_{k}
.

The key property in both these constructions is that the normalized estimate
\widehat{\tau}_{k}:=\widehat{\sigma}_{k}^{2}/\sigma_{k}^{2}
satisfies

\mathds{P}\left[\left|\widehat{\tau}_{k}-1\middle|X\right|\geqslant t\right]\leqslant 2\exp\left(-cn\min\{t^{2},t\}\right).

(28)

Indeed, for (
26
) the conditional distribution of
\widehat{\tau}_{k}
given
X^{(k)}
is
\chi_{n-\widehat{r}_{k}}^{2}/(n-\widehat{r}_{k})
, hence by the standard
\chi^{2}
tail bound (see, e.g., (
53a
)–(
53b
) in the appendix) we have

\mathds{P}\left[\left|\widehat{\tau}_{k}-1\right|\geqslant t\middle|X^{(k)}\right]\leqslant 2\exp\left(-2c(n-\widehat{r}_{k})\min\{t^{2},t\}\right)\leqslant 2\exp\left(-cn\min\{t^{2},t\}\right).

On the other hand, in the case of (
27
) we have
\widehat{\tau}_{k}\sim\chi_{\widehat{r}_{k}}^{2}/\widehat{r}_{k}
.
Since
2\widehat{r}_{k}>n
, we again arrive at (
28
).

Applying (
28
) to
\widehat{\tau}_{k}
for
k\in\{0,1\}
we can show that the resulting test (
Lin-Var+
) admits similar statistical guarantees to those for the oracle test (
Lin-Var
).
To this end, we first extend Proposition
1
.

Proposition 2
.

The type I error probability of test (
Lin-Var+
) is bounded as

\mathds{P}_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}[\widehat{T}=1|X^{(0)},X^{(1)}]\leqslant C\exp\bigg(-\frac{cn\widehat{\Delta}_{1}}{\sigma_{1}^{2}}\min\bigg\{1,\frac{n\widehat{\Delta}_{1}}{\sigma_{1}^{2}\widehat{r}}\bigg\}\bigg)+C\exp(-cn)

(29)

where
\widehat{r}=\max\{\textup{rank}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{0}),\textup{rank}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{1})\}
.
The type II error admits the bound with the replacement
1\mapsto 0
.

The full proof of this result is deferred to the appendix, and here we sketch it.
We have

\displaystyle\;\mathds{P}_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}[\widehat{T}=1|X^{(0)},X^{(1)}]

\displaystyle=\mathds{P}\bigg[\frac{1}{\widehat{\tau}_{0}}\|\xi^{(0)}\|^{2}_{X^{(0)}}-\widehat{r}_{0}+\widehat{r}_{1}-\frac{1}{\widehat{\tau}_{1}}\|\xi^{(1)}\|^{2}_{X^{(1)}}+\frac{2}{\widehat{\tau}_{1}\sigma_{1}}\langle X^{(1)}(\theta_{1}-\theta_{0}),\xi^{(1)}\rangle\geqslant\frac{n\widehat{\Delta}_{1}}{\widehat{\tau}_{1}\sigma_{1}^{2}}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathds{P}\bigg[\frac{\widehat{\tau}_{1}}{\widehat{\tau}_{0}}\|\xi^{(0)}\|^{2}_{X^{(0)}}-\widehat{\tau}_{1}\widehat{r}_{0}+\widehat{\tau}_{1}\widehat{r}_{1}-\|\xi^{(1)}\|^{2}_{X^{(1)}}+\frac{2}{\sigma_{1}}\langle X^{(1)}(\theta_{1}-\theta_{0}),\xi^{(1)}\rangle\geqslant\frac{n\widehat{\Delta}_{1}}{\sigma_{1}^{2}}\bigg]

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathds{P}\bigg[\widehat{r}_{1}-\|\xi^{(1)}\|^{2}_{X^{(1)}}\geqslant\frac{n\widehat{\Delta}_{1}}{5\sigma_{1}^{2}}\bigg]+\mathds{P}\bigg[\frac{1}{\sigma_{1}}\langle X^{(1)}(\theta_{1}-\theta_{0}),\xi^{(1)}\rangle\geqslant\frac{n\widehat{\Delta}_{1}}{10\sigma_{1}^{2}}\bigg]

\displaystyle\quad+\mathds{P}\bigg[\frac{\widehat{\tau}_{1}}{\widehat{\tau}_{0}}\left(\|\xi^{(0)}\|^{2}_{X^{(0)}}-\widehat{r}_{0}\right)\geqslant\frac{n\widehat{\Delta}_{1}}{5\sigma_{1}^{2}}\bigg]+\mathds{P}\bigg[(\widehat{\tau}_{1}-1)\widehat{r}_{1}\geqslant\frac{n\widehat{\Delta}_{1}}{5\sigma_{1}^{2}}\bigg]+\mathds{P}\bigg[\widehat{\tau}_{1}\bigg(\frac{1}{\widehat{\tau}_{0}}-1\bigg)\widehat{r}_{0}\geqslant\frac{n\widehat{\Delta}_{1}}{5\sigma_{1}^{2}}\bigg]

where all probabilities are conditional on
X^{(0)},X^{(1)}
.
The first two terms in the right-hand side can be bounded via the standard chi-squared and Gaussian tail bounds as in the proof of Proposition
1
since these terms do not contain
\widehat{\tau}_{k}
.
Now, it turns out that the remaining three terms also admit the bound from the right-hand side of (
29
).
To obtain this result, we separate the factors containing
\widehat{\tau}_{0},\widehat{\tau}_{1}
and use (
28
) to bound by
C\exp(-cn)
the probability for these factors to deviate from a constant.
The resulting
(\widehat{\tau}_{0},\widehat{\tau}_{1})
-free deviations are bounded as in Proposition
1
for the first term in the last line,
and by Theorem
1
for the last two terms.
∎

Finally, repeating the steps in the proof of Theorem
1
– namely, conditioning over design and using that
\mathds{P}\left[n_{1}\widehat{\Delta}_{1}\leqslant{n_{1}\Delta_{1}}/{2}\right]\leqslant\exp(-cn_{1})
– we extend Proposition
2
to the random design setup.

Corollary 2
.

Let
\mathds{P}_{k}
(
k\in\{0,1\}
) correspond to
x\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
and
y|x\sim\mathcal{N}(x^{\top}\theta^{\vphantom{2}}_{k},\sigma_{k}^{2})
as in the premise of Theorem
1
.
Then test (
Lin-Var+
) satisfies

\mathds{P}_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}[\widehat{T}=1]\leqslant C\exp\bigg(-\frac{cn\Delta_{1}}{\sigma_{1}^{2}}\min\bigg\{1,\frac{n\Delta_{1}}{\bar{r}\sigma_{1}^{2}}\bigg\}\bigg)+C\exp(-cn),

(30)

where
\bar{r}
is as in Theorem
1
(cf. (
16
)).
The type II error admits the bound with the replacement
1\mapsto 0
.

2.4
Lower bound

Our next result demonstrates that test (
Lin
) is near-optimal in the minimax sense.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\Delta^{2}>0
and integers
r_{0},r_{1},n\geqslant 28
be given.
Consider distributions
\mathds{P}_{0},\mathds{P}_{1}
given by

\displaystyle\mathds{P}_{k}:x\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}),\;y|x\sim\mathcal{N}(x^{\top}\theta_{k},1)\quad(k\in\{0,1\}),

parametrized by unknown
\theta_{0},\theta_{1}\in\mathds{R}^{d}
with
d\geqslant\max\{r_{0},r_{1}\}
,
with covariances
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0}=\boldsymbol{I}_{r_{0}}
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}=\boldsymbol{I}_{r_{1}}
.
Observing
\theta^{*}\in\{\theta_{0},\theta_{1}\}
and two independent samples
(X^{(0)},Y^{(0)})\sim\mathds{P}_{0}^{\otimes n}
and
(X^{(1)},Y^{(1)})\sim\mathds{P}_{1}^{\otimes n}
,
consider testing
\mathcal{H}_{0}:\theta_{0}=\theta^{*}
against
\mathcal{H}_{1}:\theta_{1}=\theta^{*}
.
Then

\underset{\widehat{T}}{\inf}\underset{(\theta_{0},\theta_{1})\in\Theta(\Delta^{2})}{\sup}\left\{\mathds{P}_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}[\widehat{T}=1]+\mathds{P}_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}[\widehat{T}=0]\right\}\geqslant C\exp\bigg(-cn\Delta\min\bigg\{1,\frac{n\Delta}{\textup{{r}}}\bigg\}\bigg),

(31)

where the infimum is over all measurable maps
T:(\theta^{*},X^{(0)},Y^{(0)},X^{(1)},Y^{(1)})\to\{0,1\}
, the supremum is over the set

\Theta(\Delta):=\{(\theta_{0},\theta_{1})\in\mathds{R}^{2d}:\max\{\Delta_{0},\Delta_{1}\}\geqslant\Delta\}

with
\Delta_{0},\Delta_{1}
as defined in (
14
),
and

\textup{{r}}=\min\{n,r_{0},r_{1}\}.

Theorems
1
and
2
together show that test (
Lin
) is optimal up to the replacement of
\min\{\Delta_{0},\Delta_{1}\}
with
\max\{\Delta_{0},\Delta_{1}\}

and
\max\{r_{0},r_{1}\}
with
\min\{r_{0},r_{1}\}
. Removing this gap remains an open problem.

The main technical challenge in the proof of Theorem
2
stems from not observing the residuals at the complementary to
\theta^{*}
model

\bar{\theta}=\theta_{0}+\theta_{1}-\theta^{*}.

This leads to a
composite
hypothesis testing problem.
To circumvent this challenge, we first consider the simplified situation where one observes the residuals corresponding to
both
\theta^{*}
and
\bar{\theta}
, rather than only
\theta^{*}
, but one is not told “which one is which.” In other words, one observes the
unordered
pair of residuals.
The testing problem then reduces to inferring the right ordering of
\bar{\theta}
and
\theta^{*}
, i.e., testing two
simple
hypotheses
(\theta^{*},\bar{\theta})=(\theta_{0},\theta_{1})
and
(\theta^{*},\bar{\theta})=(\theta_{1},\theta_{0})
. In this situation, the Neyman-Pearson lemma
[
30
]
ensures that the likelihood ratio test is optimal. It turns out that this reduction already suffices to match the
r
-independent first term under exponent in (
31
).
However, it fails to capture the dependency on
r
which is reflected in the second term.
In order to do that, we rely on a constrained Bayesian approach, putting a truncated Gaussian prior on
\bar{\theta}
in the same spirit as in
[
31
,
32
]
.

In the next section, we generalize our approach to the general
M
-estimation scenario described in Section
1
.
The key insight is that the projected residuals used in test (
Lin
) are in fact instantiations of the Newton decrements of empirical risks over the two samples.
This allows to generalize the test by using the Newton decrements in the general case.
In addition, we have to appropriately modify the debiasing terms with which the Newton decrements are compared.
This is done by replacing the covariance rank with the
local effective rank
– the trace of the Hessian-standardized local Fisher matrix,
which is equal to identity in the well-specified case (see the preliminary part of Section
3
for details).
On the positive side, such modification allows to effortlessly handle misspecified models: the argument to bound the error probability remains similar, and we only pay for model misspecification through a (controlled) deterioration of the derived error bounds.
(This occurs due to the modified Fisher matrix not being identity in the misspecified case, see the statement of Proposition
3
for more details.)
However, unlike the actual model ranks, the effective ranks are usually unknown, and our testing procedure must be adaptive to them to begin with. We address this challenge via an estimator of the effective rank, which might be interesting in a wider context.

3
General asymptotic theory

Reminder of the setup.

We now revisit the general
M
-estimation setup introduced in Section
1
.
Our goal is to characterize the sample complexity of confident testing in the asymptotic large-sample regime, with prediction distances vanishing as
n_{0},n_{1}\to\infty
.
Before we specify the regime and present the proposed testing procedures, let us recap the setup.
Hypothesis
\mathcal{H}_{k}
(
k\in\{0,1\}
) specifies the (unique) unconstrained minimizer
\theta_{k}
of the population risk
L_{k}(\theta):=\mathds{E}_{k}[\ell(\theta,z)]
via the distribution
\mathds{P}_{k}
of
z
, where we use the shorthand
\mathds{E}_{k}[\cdot]:=\mathds{E}_{z\sim\mathds{P}_{k}}[\cdot]
. In what follows, we require the loss function
\ell(\cdot,z)
to be strictly convex and twice continuously differentiable for all possible values of
z
.
Given two samples
Z^{(0)}\sim\mathds{P}_{0}^{\otimes n_{0}},Z^{(1)}\sim\mathds{P}_{1}^{\otimes n_{1}}
(cf. (
3

\widehat{L}_{0}(\theta):=\frac{1}{n_{0}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{0}}\ell(\theta,z_{i}^{(0)}),\quad\widehat{L}_{1}(\theta):=\frac{1}{n_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\ell(\theta,z_{i}^{(1)}).

(32)

In order to facilitate the reader, we shall now remind the main notions related to
M
-estimators.
We use a simplified notation:
L(\theta)
, without any subscript, is the population risk corresponding to an abstract distribution
\mathds{P}
(later on
\mathds{P}
will be either
\mathds{P}_{0}
or
\mathds{P}_{1}
).
We similarly omit the subscript for all matrix functionals of
L(\theta)
(see (
33
)–(
35
) below).
Finally, we let
\theta^{\circ}
be a minimizer of
L(\cdot)
unique due to the strict convexity of
\ell_{z}(\cdot)
. In other words, under
\mathcal{H}_{k}
we have
L(\theta)\equiv L_{k}(\theta)
and
\theta^{\circ}=\theta_{k}
.

Background on
M
-estimators.

In the context of
M
-estimators (see, e.g.,
[
33
,
34
,
35
]
),
it is convenient to define the local Fisher information matrix and the population risk Hessian:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{G}(\theta)

\displaystyle:=\textup{Cov}[\nabla\ell(\theta,z)],\quad\boldsymbol{H}(\theta):=\mathds{E}[\nabla^{2}\ell(\theta,z)]=\nabla^{2}L(\theta).

(33)

Hereinafter the loss is differentiated in the first argument.

Recall the well-known fact (see, e.g.,
[

]

\boldsymbol{G}(\theta^{\circ})=\boldsymbol{H}(\theta^{\circ}),

, holds in the

,
i.e., when
\ell_{z}(\theta)=-\log P_{\theta}(z)
with
P_{\theta}\in\mathcal{P}:=\{P_{\theta}(\cdot),\;\theta\in\mathds{R}^{d}\}
,
where
\mathcal{P}
is a family of probability densities
P_{\theta}(\cdot)
that is sufficiently regular (see, e.g.,
[
36
]
for more details on regularity), and

is generated by a distribution with density from
\mathcal{P}
; this density is then necessarily
P_{\theta^{\circ}}

a.s.
by the non-negativity of the Kullback-Leibler divergence.
6
6

6

\theta\in\Theta

Note also that this was the case in the setup of Section

, with

•

Now, under (

\boldsymbol{J}(\theta):=\boldsymbol{H}(\theta)^{\dagger/2}\,\boldsymbol{G}(\theta)\,\boldsymbol{H}(\theta)^{\dagger/2},

satisfies
\boldsymbol{J}(\theta^{\circ})=\boldsymbol{I}_{r^{\circ}}
, where

.
In fact, we can redefine

\boldsymbol{H}(\theta)\equiv\boldsymbol{\Sigma}

\boldsymbol{H}_{k}^{\vphantom{2}}(\theta)\equiv\sigma_{k}^{2}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}^{\vphantom{2}}

3.1
Basic test via Newton decrements

The key insight allowing to generalize test (
Lin
) to non-linear models is as follows.
Omitting
k\in\{0,1\}
for brevity, test (
Lin
) was based on the quantities
\|Y-X\theta^{*}\|^{2}_{X}
with
Z=(X,Y)\sim\mathds{P}^{\otimes n}
. Moreover, observe that in the setup of Section

one has
\nabla\widehat{L}(\theta)=\tfrac{1}{n}X^{\top}(Y-X\theta)
and

\widehat{\boldsymbol{H}}(\theta)=\tfrac{1}{n}X^{\top}X.

As such, we can express
\|Y-X\theta^{*}\|^{2}_{X}
in terms of the gradient
\nabla\widehat{L}(\theta^{*})
and Hessian
\widehat{\boldsymbol{H}}(\theta^{*})

\|Y-X\theta^{*}\|^{2}_{X}=\|\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{X}(Y-X\theta^{*})\|^{2}=\|(X^{\top}X)^{\dagger/2}X^{\top}(Y-X\theta^{*})\|^{2}=n\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{H}}(\theta^{*})^{\dagger/2}\nabla\widehat{L}(\theta^{*})\|^{2}.

The quantity in the right-hand side is the rescaled Newton decrement of empirical risk
\widehat{L}(\cdot)
at
\theta^{*}
.
This naturally leads us to replacing the quadratic terms in (
Lin

\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{H}}_{k}(\theta^{*})^{\dagger/2}\nabla\widehat{L}_{k}(\theta^{*})\|^{2}
over the two samples (
k\in\{0,1\}

However, we also have to adjust the debiasing terms.
To this end, examining the proof of Proposition
1

\widehat{r}_{k}=\textup{rank}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{k})
in (
Lin

\|Y^{(k)}-X^{(k)}\theta^{*}\|_{X^{(k)}}^{2}
under the matching hypothesis
\mathcal{H}_{k}

In the setup of Sec.

,
\textup{rank}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{k})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{\to}}\textup{rank}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})
as

\textup{rank}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})
is the total expectation of the squared norm of the projected residual over the whole observation
z=(x,y)

Returning to the

n_{0},n_{1}\to\infty

\widehat{T}=\mathds{1}\big\{n_{0}\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{H}}_{0}(\theta^{*})^{{\dagger}/{2}}\nabla\widehat{L}_{0}(\theta^{*})\|^{2}-\textup{Tr}[\boldsymbol{J}_{0}(\theta^{*})]\geqslant n_{1}\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{H}}_{1}(\theta^{*})^{{\dagger}/{2}}\nabla\widehat{L}_{1}(\theta^{*})\|^{2}-\textup{Tr}[\boldsymbol{J}_{1}(\theta^{*})]\big\}.

(
Asymp

We now explain the mechanism behind this test in more detail.
First, one can easily check that

\textup{Tr}[\boldsymbol{J}_{k}(\theta^{*})]=n_{k}\mathds{E}\big[\big\|\boldsymbol{H}_{k}(\theta^{*})^{{\dagger}/{2}}\big[\nabla\widehat{L}_{k}(\theta^{*})-\nabla L_{k}(\theta^{*})\big]\big\|^{2}\big],

(36)

and

Then Slutsky’s theorem (see, e.g.,

More precisely, the latter two expectations are given correspondingly by

\displaystyle:=\|\boldsymbol{H}_{0}(\theta_{1})^{\dagger/2}\nabla L_{0}(\theta_{1})\|^{2},\quad\bar{\Delta}_{1}

Remark 3
.

Under each hypothesis, one of the two population risks vanishes at

Remark 4
.

It is instructive to apply the result of Proposition

Moment assumptions.

The result presented next holds under weak moment assumptions about

as claimed.
∎

A.3
Proof of Corollary
1

For simplicity we assume
\Delta_{0}=\Delta_{1}=\Delta
; the general case is similar.
Recall that
\Delta^{2}\leqslant 1
.
Recall also that
r:=\max\{r_{0},r_{1}\}
and
\bar{r}:=\min\{r,n\}
.

\boldsymbol{{1}^{o}}
. We first look at the reverse direction: assume that

cn\Delta\min\left\{1,\frac{n\Delta}{\bar{r}}\right\}\geqslant\log(1/\delta)

(54)

which, by (
15
), corresponds to
\max\{\mathds{P}_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}[\widehat{T}=1],\mathds{P}_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}[\widehat{T}=0]\}\leqslant 2C\delta=O(\delta)
.
Choosing the second term in the minimum in (
54
), we get

n\Delta\geqslant\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)\bar{r}/c}=\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)\min\{r,n\}/c},

whence

n=\Omega\left(\min\left\{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\Delta^{2}},\frac{\sqrt{r\log(1/\delta)}}{\Delta}\right\}\right).

(55)

On the other hand, choosing the first term in the minimum in (
54
) yields
n=\Omega(\log(1/\delta)/\Delta)
, which corresponds to the missing (so far) term in the sample complexity bound (
17
) (recall that
\Delta\leqslant 1
).
Thus, (
17
) is indeed
necessary
to guarantee that
\max\{\mathds{P}_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}[\widehat{T}=1],\mathds{P}_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}[\widehat{T}=0]\}=O(\delta)
as per (
15
).

\boldsymbol{{2}^{o}}
.
Conversely, assume that

cn\Delta\geqslant\min\left\{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\Delta},\sqrt{r\log(1/\delta)}+\log(1/\delta)\right\},

(56)

which corresponds to the sufficient sample size as per (
17
).
Meanwhile, (
54
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle cn\Delta\geqslant\max\left\{1,\frac{\bar{r}}{n\Delta}\right\}\log(1/\delta)

\displaystyle=\max\left\{1,\min\left\{\frac{1}{\Delta},\frac{r}{n\Delta}\right\}\right\}\log(1/\delta)

(57)

\displaystyle=\min\left\{\frac{1}{\Delta},\max\left\{1,\frac{r}{n\Delta}\right\}\right\}\log(1/\delta),

where in the last step we used that

Clearly, the first case for the minimum in (

) is identical to the first case for the minimum in (

).
On the other hand, the second case for the minimum in (

∎

A.4
Proof of Proposition
2

Recall the decomposition of the type I error (all probabilities are conditional on
X^{(0)},X^{(1)}
):

\displaystyle\;\mathds{P}_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}[\widehat{T}=1|X^{(0)},X^{(1)}]

\displaystyle=\mathds{P}\bigg[\frac{1}{\widehat{\tau}_{0}}\|\xi^{(0)}\|^{2}_{X^{(0)}}-\widehat{r}_{0}+\widehat{r}_{1}-\frac{1}{\widehat{\tau}_{1}}\|\xi^{(1)}\|^{2}_{X^{(1)}}+\frac{2}{\widehat{\tau}_{1}\sigma_{1}}\langle X^{(1)}(\theta_{1}-\theta_{0}),\xi^{(1)}\rangle\geqslant\frac{n\widehat{\Delta}_{1}}{\widehat{\tau}_{1}\sigma_{1}^{2}}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathds{P}\bigg[\frac{\widehat{\tau}_{1}}{\widehat{\tau}_{0}}\|\xi^{(0)}\|^{2}_{X^{(0)}}-\widehat{\tau}_{1}\widehat{r}_{0}+\widehat{\tau}_{1}\widehat{r}_{1}-\|\xi^{(1)}\|^{2}_{X^{(1)}}+\frac{2}{\sigma_{1}}\langle X^{(1)}(\theta_{1}-\theta_{0}),\xi^{(1)}\rangle\geqslant\frac{n\widehat{\Delta}_{1}}{\sigma_{1}^{2}}\bigg]

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathds{P}\bigg[\widehat{r}_{1}-\|\xi^{(1)}\|^{2}_{X^{(1)}}\geqslant\frac{n\widehat{\Delta}_{1}}{5\sigma_{1}^{2}}\bigg]+\mathds{P}\bigg[\frac{1}{\sigma_{1}}\langle X^{(1)}(\theta_{1}-\theta_{0}),\xi^{(1)}\rangle\geqslant\frac{n\widehat{\Delta}_{1}}{10\sigma_{1}^{2}}\bigg]

\displaystyle\quad+\mathds{P}\bigg[\frac{\widehat{\tau}_{1}}{\widehat{\tau}_{0}}\left(\|\xi^{(0)}\|^{2}_{X^{(0)}}-\widehat{r}_{0}\right)\geqslant\frac{n\widehat{\Delta}_{1}}{5\sigma_{1}^{2}}\bigg]+\mathds{P}\bigg[(\widehat{\tau}_{1}-1)\widehat{r}_{1}\geqslant\frac{n\widehat{\Delta}_{1}}{5\sigma_{1}^{2}}\bigg]+\mathds{P}\bigg[\widehat{\tau}_{1}\bigg(\frac{1}{\widehat{\tau}_{0}}-1\bigg)\widehat{r}_{0}\geqslant\frac{n\widehat{\Delta}_{1}}{5\sigma_{1}^{2}}\bigg].

(58)

As in the proof of Proposition
1
, the sum of the terms in the penultimate line of (
58
) is bounded by

2\exp\bigg(-\frac{cn\widehat{\Delta}_{1}}{\sigma_{1}^{2}}\min\bigg\{1,\frac{n\widehat{\Delta}_{1}}{\widehat{r}_{1}\sigma_{1}^{2}}\bigg\}\bigg).

Using that
\widehat{\tau}_{0},

\widehat{\tau}_{1}
and
\|\xi^{(0)}\|^{2}_{X^{(0)}}-\widehat{r}_{0}
are conditionally independent and
\mathds{P}[\widehat{\tau}_{k}\notin[\tfrac{1}{2},2]]\leqslant C\exp(-cn)
for
k\in\{0,1\}
due to (
28
), applying (
53
) and the union bound, we bound the first term in the last line of (
58
) by

\displaystyle\mathds{P}\bigg[\|\xi^{(0)}\|^{2}_{X^{(0)}}-\widehat{r}_{0}\geqslant\frac{n\widehat{\Delta}_{1}}{5\sigma_{1}^{2}}\bigg]+C\exp(-cn)\leqslant\exp\bigg(-\frac{cn\widehat{\Delta}_{1}}{\sigma_{1}^{2}}\min\bigg\{1,\frac{n\widehat{\Delta}_{1}}{\widehat{r}_{0}\sigma_{1}^{2}}\bigg\}\bigg)+C\exp(-cn).

Moreover, applying (
28
) to
\widehat{\tau}_{1}
and noting that
\widehat{r}_{1}\leqslant n

a.s.
, we bound the next term as

\mathds{P}\bigg[(\widehat{\tau}_{1}-1)\geqslant\frac{n\widehat{\Delta}_{1}}{5\widehat{r}_{1}\sigma_{1}^{2}}\bigg]\leqslant 2\exp\bigg(-\frac{c_{3}n^{2}\widehat{\Delta}_{1}}{\widehat{r}_{1}\sigma_{1}^{2}}\min\bigg\{1,\frac{n\widehat{\Delta}_{1}}{\widehat{r}_{1}\sigma_{1}^{2}}\bigg\}\bigg)\leqslant 2\exp\bigg(-\frac{c_{3}n\widehat{\Delta}_{1}}{\sigma_{1}^{2}}\min\bigg\{1,\frac{n\widehat{\Delta}_{1}}{\widehat{r}_{1}\sigma_{1}^{2}}\bigg\}\bigg).

In order to estimate the last term in the right-hand side of (
58
), we use that
\mathds{P}[\widehat{\tau}_{1}\geqslant 2]\leqslant 2\exp(-cn)
by (
28
) and that
\widehat{\tau}_{0}
and
\widehat{\tau}_{1}
are conditionally independent.
We then have

\displaystyle\mathds{P}\bigg[\widehat{\tau}_{1}\bigg(\frac{1}{\widehat{\tau}_{0}}-1\bigg)\geqslant\frac{n\widehat{\Delta}_{1}}{5\widehat{r}_{0}\sigma_{1}^{2}}\bigg]

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\exp(-cn)+\mathds{P}\bigg[\bigg(\frac{1}{\widehat{\tau}_{0}}-1\bigg)\geqslant\frac{n\widehat{\Delta}_{1}}{10\widehat{r}_{0}\sigma_{1}^{2}}\bigg]

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\exp(-cn)+\mathds{P}\bigg[\widehat{\tau}_{0}\leqslant 1-\frac{1}{2}\min\bigg\{\frac{n\widehat{\Delta}_{1}}{10\widehat{r}_{0}\sigma_{1}^{2}},1\bigg\}\bigg]

\displaystyle\leqslant 4\exp(-c_{4}n)+2\exp\bigg(-\frac{c_{4}n\widehat{\Delta}_{1}}{\sigma_{1}^{2}}\min\bigg\{1,\frac{n\widehat{\Delta}_{1}}{\widehat{r}_{0}\sigma_{1}^{2}}\bigg\}\bigg)

where in the second step we used that
\tfrac{1}{1+t}\leqslant 1-\tfrac{1}{2}\min[t,1]
for
t\geqslant 0
, and in the end we used (
28
).
∎

Proof of Theorem

Our goal in this section is to prove Theorem

Reduction to fixed-design setup

(59)

Here the infimum is over all measurable maps
(\theta^{*},X,Y,X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})\to\{0,1\}

with

\widehat{\texttt{r}}:=\min\{\textup{rank}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{0}),\textup{rank}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{1})\}

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{k}=\frac{1}{n}X^{(k)\top}X^{(k)}
. Moreover, when proving (
59

, and recall that

Finally, from now on we assume that

\lambda_{\min}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{0,\mathsf{\Gamma}}),\lambda_{\max}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{1,\mathsf{\Gamma}})\in\left[\frac{1}{16},\frac{49}{16}\right],

This is a non-restrictive assumption: indeed, in Section

\mathds{P}\left\{\lambda_{\min}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{0,\mathsf{\Gamma}}),\lambda_{\max}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{1,\mathsf{\Gamma}})\in\left[\frac{1}{16},\frac{49}{16}\right]\right\}\geqslant\frac{1}{8}.

(62)

When combined with (

)
will readily yield (

\widehat{\Delta}_{0}\leqslant 32\Delta_{0}\;\;\text{and}\;\;\widehat{\Delta}_{1}\leqslant 32\Delta_{1}

Under the joint event (and so w.p.

\displaystyle\underset{\widehat{T}}{\inf}\underset{(\theta_{0},\theta_{1})\in\Theta(\Delta)}{\sup}\left\{\mathds{P}_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}[\widehat{T}=1]+\mathds{P}_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}[\widehat{T}=0]\right\}

which is (

Thus, we can indeed focus on proving (

) with fixed design matrices

).
This is our next goal; Secs.

–
B.3

we shall verify (

).

What is ahead.

Note that (
59
) comprises two bounds simultaneously: the rank-independent bound
\exp(-cn\Delta)
and the second bound which depends on

.
These two bounds shall be proved correspondingly in Sections
B.2
and
B.3
.
It turns out that for the first bound it suffices to consider a further simplified testing problem, where one observes
both models

\{\theta^{*},\bar{\theta}\}
rather than just
\theta^{*}
,
where

\bar{\theta}:=\theta_{0}+\theta_{1}-\theta^{*}

is the complementary to
\theta^{*}
model in
\{\theta_{0},\theta_{1}\}
.
The problem then reduces to discriminating between the two
simple
hypotheses
(\theta_{0},\theta_{1})=(\theta^{*},\bar{\theta})
and
(\theta_{0},\theta_{1})=(\bar{\theta},\theta^{*})
.
Intuitively, the absence of the rank in the corresponding sample complexity bound can be explained by the fact that the testing problem reduces to a one-dimensional one since
\theta_{1}-\theta_{0}
is known.
On the other hand, capturing the dependency on
r
requires to take into account that the complementary model
\bar{\theta}
actually is
not
observed. We capture this by putting a Gaussian prior on
\bar{\theta}
and carefully bounding the Bayes risk.

B.2
Rank-independent bound

Let
\bar{\theta}:=\theta_{0}+\theta_{1}-\theta^{*}
, so that
\{\theta^{*},\bar{\theta}\}=\{\theta_{0},\theta_{1}\}
as the two sets regardless of the hypothesis.
Now, observe that the two hypotheses
\mathcal{H}_{0}:\theta_{0}=\theta^{*}
and
\mathcal{H}_{1}:\theta_{1}=\theta^{*}
can be reformulated as the hypotheses
(\theta_{0},\theta_{1})=(\theta^{*},\bar{\theta})

and
(\theta_{0},\theta_{1})=(\bar{\theta},\theta^{*})

about the aggregated parameter
(\theta_{0},\theta_{1})
.
Since
\bar{\theta}
is unknown, we are dealing with a composite testing problem, and thus cannot apply the Neyman-Pearson lemma. To put us in the two-point hypothesis testing situation, we can simply fix a value of
\bar{\theta}
, and assume that it is also known – in other words, consider the following testing problem:

\boxed{\begin{aligned} \textit{Given the data}\;n,\widehat{r}_{0},\widehat{r}_{1},(\theta^{*},\bar{\theta})\in\widehat{\Theta},\;(X,Y)\in\mathds{P}_{0}^{\otimes n},\;(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})\sim\mathds{P}_{1}^{\otimes n},\\
\textit{test}\;\;\bar{\mathcal{H}}_{0}:\;(\theta^{*},\bar{\theta})=(\theta_{0},\theta_{1})\;\text{against}\;\;\bar{\mathcal{H}}_{1}:(\theta^{*},\bar{\theta})=(\theta_{1},\theta_{0}).\end{aligned}}

(
2-point
)

Clearly, (
2-point
) is a problem of testing between two
simple
hypotheses about the unknown parameter
(\theta_{0},\theta_{1})\in\widehat{\Theta}
.
By the Neyman-Pearson lemma, the sum of type I and II error probabilities for any test in (
2-point
) is lower-bounded by that for the likelihood-ratio test, and this test writes

\widehat{T}_{\textsf{LR}}=\mathds{1}\left\{\|Y-X\theta^{*}\|^{2}+\|Y^{\prime}-X^{\prime}\bar{\theta}\|^{2}\geqslant\|Y-X\bar{\theta}\|^{2}+\|Y^{\prime}-X^{\prime}\theta^{*}\|^{2}\right\}.

(63)

On the other hand, (
2-point
) cannot be harder than the initial testing problem considered in (
59
).
Indeed, any test admissible in (
59
)
is a mapping
(\theta^{*},\bar{\theta},X,Y,X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})\to\{0,1\}
that does
not
depend on
\bar{\theta}
, whereas in (
2-point
) one is also allowed to use the tests that do depend on
\bar{\theta}
and thus are not
(\theta^{*},X,Y,X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})
-measurable.
As such, the left-hand side of (
59
) admits the bound

\displaystyle\underset{\widehat{T}:(\theta^{*},X,Y,X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})\to\{0,1\}}{\inf}

\displaystyle\underset{(\theta_{0},\theta_{1})\in\widehat{\Theta}}{\sup}\left\{\mathds{P}_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}[\widehat{T}=1|X,X^{\prime}]+\mathds{P}_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}[\widehat{T}=0|X,X^{\prime}]\right\}

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle\underset{(\theta^{*},\bar{\theta})\in\widehat{\Theta}}{\sup}\left\{\mathds{P}_{\bar{\mathcal{H}}_{0}}[\widehat{T}_{\textsf{LR}}=1|X,X^{\prime}]+\mathds{P}_{\bar{\mathcal{H}}_{1}}[\widehat{T}_{\textsf{LR}}=0|X,X^{\prime}]\right\},

with
\widehat{T}_{\textsf{LR}}
being the test given by (
63
).
Fixing
(\theta^{*},\bar{\theta})\in\widehat{\Theta}=\widehat{\Theta}(32\Delta)
, under
\bar{\mathcal{H}}_{0}
we get

\displaystyle\mathds{P}_{\bar{\mathcal{H}}_{0}}[\widehat{T}_{\textsf{LR}}=1|X,X^{\prime}]

\displaystyle\geqslant\mathds{P}\left\{\|Y-X\theta_{0}\|^{2}+\|Y^{\prime}-X^{\prime}\theta_{1}\|^{2}\geqslant\|Y-X\theta_{1}\|^{2}+\|Y^{\prime}-X^{\prime}\theta_{0}\|^{2}\;\middle|\;X,X^{\prime}\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathds{P}\left\{2\langle\xi,X(\theta_{0}-\theta_{1})\rangle+2\langle\xi^{\prime},X^{\prime}(\theta_{0}-\theta_{1})\rangle\geqslant\|X(\theta_{0}-\theta_{1})\|^{2}+\|X^{\prime}(\theta_{0}-\theta_{1})\|^{2}\middle|X,X^{\prime}\right\}

\displaystyle\geqslant C\exp\big(-c\max\{\|X(\theta_{0}-\theta_{1})\|^{2},\|X^{\prime}(\theta_{0}-\theta_{1})\|^{2}\}\big)=C\exp\big(-cn\max\{\widehat{\Delta}_{0},\widehat{\Delta}_{1}\}\big).

Here we first used that
\langle\xi^{(k)},X^{(k)}(\theta_{0}-\theta_{1})\rangle\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\|X^{(k)}(\theta_{0}-\theta_{1})\|^{2})
for
k\in\{0,1\}

conditionally on
(X,X^{\prime})
, and then applied the lower bound for the Gaussian tails (see
[
41
, Eq. 7.1.13]
):

\mathds{P}[\mathcal{N}(0,1)\geqslant t]\geqslant C\exp(-ct^{2}),\quad\forall t\geqslant 0.

By symmetry, we also have the same bound for
\mathds{P}_{\bar{\mathcal{H}}_{1}}[\widehat{T}_{\textsf{LR}}=0|X,X^{\prime}]
.
Now, taking
(\theta_{*},\bar{\theta})
on the boundary of
\widehat{\Theta}(32\Delta)
, i.e., such that
\max[\widehat{\Delta}_{0},\widehat{\Delta}_{1}]=32\Delta
, we arrive at

\displaystyle\underset{\widehat{T}:(\theta^{*},X,Y,X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})\to\{0,1\}}{\inf}\;\underset{(\theta_{0},\theta_{1})\in\widehat{\Theta}}{\sup}\left\{\mathds{P}_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}[\widehat{T}=1|X,X^{\prime}]+\mathds{P}_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}[\widehat{T}=0|X,X^{\prime}]\right\}\geqslant C\exp(-c^{\prime}n\Delta).

(64)

This is precisely the rank-independent part of (
59
).
Next we deal with the rank-dependent part.

B.3
Rank-dependent bound

We first observe that in the cases
n\Delta\leqslant 1
and
n\Delta\geqslant\texttt{r}/4
,
the bound (
59
) follows from (
64
) up to a change in constants. Hence, it suffices to focus on the range

1\leqslant n\Delta\leqslant{\texttt{r}/4}.

(65)

Instead of fixing the pair
(\theta^{*},\bar{\theta})
, we now only fix
\theta^{*}

and put on
\bar{\theta}
a Gaussian prior centered at
\theta^{*}
with covariance corresponding to the constraint
(\theta^{*},\bar{\theta})\in\widehat{\Theta}
(cf. (
68
)).
Note that, by symmetry, this is equivalent to putting the same prior on
(\theta_{0},\theta_{1})
, or the centered prior with the same covariance on
\theta_{1}-\theta_{0}
(cf. (
69
), but the exact expression is not important for what follows in step
\boldsymbol{{1}^{o}}

Technically, such prior violates the constraint
(\theta_{0},\theta_{1})\in\widehat{\Theta}
, so we have to verify that the event

\mathcal{C}:=\{(\theta_{0},\theta_{1})\in\widehat{\Theta}\}

(66)

has sufficient mass under this prior. (Note that this
concentration step
is common in the literature on Bayesian lower bounds, see, e.g.,
[
42

To that end, we further proceed in three steps.

\boldsymbol{{1}^{o}}
:
Reduction to the unconstrained Bayes risk.

We let
\pi
be any prior on
(\theta_{0},\theta_{1})\in\mathds{R}^{d}
, and let
\pi_{\mathcal{C}}
be the conditioning of
\pi
to the event
\mathcal{C}
in (
66

\pi_{\mathcal{C}}(\mathcal{E})=\frac{\pi(\mathcal{\mathcal{E}}\cap\mathcal{C})}{\pi(\mathcal{C})},\quad\forall\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathds{R}^{2d}.

In what follows, we identify events over
(\theta_{0},\theta_{1})
with subsets of
\mathds{R}^{2d}
; in particular,
\mathcal{C}
is identified with
\widehat{\Theta}=\widehat{\Theta}(32\Delta)
.
Now, since
\pi_{\mathcal{C}}
is supported on
\mathcal{C}
, we can bound the left-hand side of (
59
) as

\displaystyle\underset{\widehat{T}}{\inf}\underset{(\theta_{0},\theta_{1})\in\widehat{\Theta}}{\sup}\mathds{P}_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}[\widehat{T}=1|X,X^{\prime}]+\mathds{P}_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}[\widehat{T}=0|X,X^{\prime}]\geqslant\underset{\widehat{T}}{\inf}\;\mathds{E}_{\pi_{\mathcal{C}}}\Big[\mathds{P}_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}[\widehat{T}=1|X,X^{\prime}]+\mathds{P}_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}[\widehat{T}=0|X,X^{\prime}]\Big]

where
\mathds{E}_{\pi_{\mathcal{C}}}
is the expectation over
(\theta_{0},\theta_{1})\sim\pi_{\mathcal{C}}
, and the infimum on the right is over the same set of admissible tests
(\theta^{*},X,Y,X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})\to\{0,1\}
as on the left.
Now, let
\widehat{T}_{\pi_{\mathcal{C}}}
be the corresponding Bayes test (i.e., the one on which the Bayes risk is attained):

\displaystyle\underset{\widehat{T}}{\inf}\;\mathds{E}_{\pi_{\mathcal{C}}}\Big[\mathds{P}_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}[\widehat{T}=1|X,X^{\prime}]+\mathds{P}_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}[\widehat{T}=0|X,X^{\prime}]\Big]=\mathds{E}_{\pi_{\mathcal{C}}}\left[\mathds{P}_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}[\widehat{T}_{\pi_{\mathcal{C}}}=1|X,X^{\prime}]+\mathds{P}_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}[\widehat{T}_{\pi_{\mathcal{C}}}=0|X,X^{\prime}]\right].

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\mathds{E}_{\pi}\left[\mathds{P}_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}[\widehat{T}_{\pi_{\mathcal{C}}}=1|X,X^{\prime}]+\mathds{P}_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}[\widehat{T}_{\pi_{\mathcal{C}}}=0|X,X^{\prime}]\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathds{E}_{\pi_{\mathcal{C}}}\left[\mathds{P}_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}[\widehat{T}_{\pi_{\mathcal{C}}}=1|X,X^{\prime}]+\mathds{P}_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}[\widehat{T}_{\pi_{\mathcal{C}}}=0|X,X^{\prime}]\right]

\displaystyle\quad+2\pi(\mathds{R}^{2d}\setminus\mathcal{C}).

Finally, since
\widehat{T}_{\pi_{\mathcal{C}}}
is generally not the Bayes test for
\pi
, we have

\displaystyle\underset{\widehat{T}}{\inf}\;\mathds{E}_{\pi}\Big[\mathds{P}_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}[\widehat{T}=1|X,X^{\prime}]+\mathds{P}_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}[\widehat{T}=0|X,X^{\prime}]\Big]\leqslant\mathds{E}_{\pi}\left[\mathds{P}_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}[\widehat{T}_{\pi_{\mathcal{C}}}=1|X,X^{\prime}]+\mathds{P}_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}[\widehat{T}_{\pi_{\mathcal{C}}}=0|X,X^{\prime}]\right].

We conclude that

\displaystyle\underset{\widehat{T}}{\inf}\underset{(\theta_{0},\theta_{1})\in\widehat{\Theta}}{\sup}\mathds{P}_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}[\widehat{T}=1|X,X^{\prime}]+\mathds{P}_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}[\widehat{T}=0|X,X^{\prime}]

\displaystyle\geqslant\underset{\widehat{T}}{\inf}\,\mathds{E}_{\pi}\left[\mathds{P}_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}[\widehat{T}=1|X,X^{\prime}]+\mathds{P}_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}[\widehat{T}=0|X,X^{\prime}]\right]

(67)

\displaystyle-2\pi(\mathds{R}^{2d}\setminus\mathcal{C});

thus, it suffices to bound the Bayes risk associated with
\pi
from below and
\pi(\mathds{R}^{2d}\setminus\mathcal{C})
from above.

\boldsymbol{{2}^{o}}
:
Choosing a prior and showing concentration.

Let us choose
\pi
as follows:
\theta^{*}
is fixed at an arbitrary value, and
\theta_{1}-\theta_{0}
is a vector supported on
{\mathcal{I}}:=\{1,\lfloor r/4\rfloor\}
and such that

(\theta_{1}-\theta_{0})_{{\mathcal{I}}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\rho(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{0,\mathsf{\Gamma}})^{-1})\;\;\text{with}\;\;\rho:=\frac{64\Delta}{\lfloor\texttt{r}/4\rfloor},

(68)

where
(u)_{{\mathcal{I}}}=[u_{1};...;u_{\lfloor r/4\rfloor}]
. By writing
\mathcal{C}=\widehat{\Theta}(32\Delta)
, cf. (
60
), as
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}_{0}\cup\mathcal{C}_{1}
with
\mathcal{C}_{k}=\{\widehat{\Delta}_{k}\geqslant 32\Delta\}
for
k\in\{0,1\}
, and recalling that
\widehat{\Delta}_{0}=\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{0}^{1/2}(\theta_{1}-\theta_{0})\|^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\|X(\theta_{1}-\theta_{0})\|^{2}
, we observe that

We now show that the additional fluctuation term
|\widehat{V}_{1}-\widehat{V}_{0}|

|\widehat{U}_{1}-\widehat{U}_{0}|

\displaystyle\mathds{P}\left[|\widehat{V}_{0}|\geqslant\frac{n\Delta}{c}\right]\leqslant\frac{c^{2}\textup{Var}[n\widehat{\nu}_{0}(\theta^{*})]}{n^{2}\Delta^{2}}=\frac{c^{2}}{n\Delta^{2}}\textup{Var}\left[\frac{(y-\widetilde{y})^{2}}{2a^{\prime\prime}(\eta_{x}(\theta^{*}))}\right]=\frac{c^{2}}{n\Delta^{2}}\left(\mathds{E}\left[\frac{(y-\widetilde{y})^{4}}{4a^{\prime\prime}(\eta_{x}(\theta^{*}))^{2}}\right]-\nu_{0}(\theta^{*})\right);

\widetilde{y}

y

1

\mathds{E}\left[\frac{(y-\widetilde{y})^{4}}{4a^{\prime\prime}(\eta_{x}(\theta^{*}))^{2}}\right]=\mathds{E}\left[\frac{(y-\mathds{E}[y|x]-(\widetilde{y}-\mathds{E}[\widetilde{y}|x]))^{4}}{4a^{\prime\prime}(\eta_{x}(\theta^{*}))^{2}}\right].

Appendix E
Suboptimality of test (
Grad
)

For simplicity, we let
n_{0}=n_{1}=n
and consider the setup of a well-specified linear model with fixed design (cf. Sec.
2
); the example can easily be extended for random design. We use the abridged notation
(X,Y)=(X^{(0)},Y^{(0)})
and
(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})=(X^{(1)},Y^{(1)})
for the two samples, and we also let
\xi=Y-X\theta_{0}
and
\xi^{\prime}=Y^{\prime}-X^{\prime}\theta_{1}
.
Recall that, by Proposition
1
, our proposed test in (
Lin
) has type I error probability

\mathds{P}_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}[\widehat{T}_{\textsf{{Lin}}}=1|X,X^{\prime}]\leqslant C\exp\left(-cn_{1}\widehat{\Delta}_{1}\min\left\{1,\frac{n_{1}\widehat{\Delta}_{1}}{\widehat{r}}\right\}\right)

and the complementary bound for type II error (with the replacement
1\mapsto 0
of the subscript).
Here the (empirical) separation measures
\widehat{\Delta}_{0},\widehat{\Delta}_{1}
are given by

\widehat{\Delta}_{0}=\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{0}^{1/2}(\theta_{1}-\theta_{0})\|^{2},\quad\widehat{\Delta}_{1}=\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{1}^{1/2}(\theta_{1}-\theta_{0})\|^{2},

and
\widehat{r}=\max\{\widehat{r}_{0},\widehat{r}_{1}\}
, where
\widehat{r}_{k}=\textup{rank}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{k})
.
We now construct a problem instance in which the gradient test
\widehat{T}_{\textsf{{Grad}}}
, given by (
Grad
), is suboptimal.
To this end, let
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{0}
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{1}
have the same rank
\widehat{r}
, smallest non-zero eigenvalue
1/\kappa
and other eigenvalues equal to
1
, for
\kappa>0
to be fixed later.
Now, let us take
\theta_{1}-\theta_{0}
as the unit vector in the direction corresponding to the smallest eigenvalue of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{1}
.
Then
\widehat{\Delta}_{1}={1}/{\kappa}\leqslant\widehat{\Delta}_{0}.

On the other hand, the quantities
\widehat{\delta}_{k}:=\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{k}(\theta_{1}-\theta_{0})\|^{2}
,
k\in\{0,1\}
, satisfy

\widehat{\delta}_{1}=\frac{\widehat{\Delta}_{1}}{\kappa}=\frac{1}{\kappa^{2}}\leqslant\widehat{\delta}_{0}.

Now, observe that
\widehat{\delta}_{1}
controls the sum of errors of the gradient norm test (
Grad
) in a similar manner as
\widehat{\Delta}_{1}
controls that for test (
Lin
). Indeed, proceeding as in the proof of Proposition
1
, we have

\displaystyle\mathds{P}_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}[\widehat{T}_{\textsf{{Grad}}}=1|X,X^{\prime}]

\displaystyle=\mathds{P}\Big[\underbrace{\tfrac{1}{n}\|X^{\top}\xi\|^{2}-\mathds{E}[\tfrac{1}{n}\|X^{\top}\xi\|^{2}|X]}_{U}+\underbrace{\mathds{E}[\tfrac{1}{n}\|(X^{\prime})^{\top}\xi^{\prime}\|^{2}|X^{\prime}]-\tfrac{1}{n}\|(X^{\prime})^{\top}\xi^{\prime}\|^{2}}_{V}+\underbrace{2\langle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{1}(\theta_{1}-\theta_{0}),(X^{\prime})^{\top}\xi^{\prime}\rangle}_{W}

\displaystyle\geqslant n\widehat{\delta}_{1}\Big].

As our goal is to show suboptimality, let us bound
\mathds{P}_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}[\widehat{T}_{\textsf{{Grad}}}=1|X,X^{\prime}]
from below. Since
\tfrac{1}{n}XX^{\top}
has the same eigenvalues as
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{0}=\frac{1}{n}X^{\top}X
, and similarly for
\frac{1}{n}X^{\prime}(X^{\prime})^{\top}
, we have that
\tfrac{1}{n}\|X^{\top}\xi\|^{2}
is distributed as
\frac{1}{\kappa}\zeta^{2}+\chi_{\widehat{r}-1}^{2}
, where
\chi_{\widehat{r}-1}^{2}
is chi-squared with
\widehat{r}-1
degrees of freedom, and
\zeta\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
is independent from
\chi_{\widehat{r}-1}^{2}.

Thus, we estimate the (conditional to
X
) variance of
U
above as

\textup{Var}[U|X]\leqslant\widehat{r}(\widehat{r}-1)+3\widehat{r}-(\widehat{r}-1)^{2}\leqslant 4\widehat{r},

where we used that
\mathds{E}[\zeta^{4}]=3
. Similarly,
\textup{Var}[V|X^{\prime}]\leqslant 4\widehat{r}
. Finally, by the definition of
\theta_{1}-\theta_{0}
we find

\textup{Var}[W|X^{\prime}]=4\big\|X^{\prime}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{1}(\theta_{1}-\theta_{0})\big\|^{2}=\frac{4}{\kappa^{2}}\|X^{\prime}(\theta_{1}-\theta_{0})\|^{2}=\frac{4n\widehat{\Delta}_{1}}{\kappa^{2}}=\frac{4n\widehat{\delta}_{1}}{\kappa}.

Thus,

\textup{Var}[\tfrac{1}{\kappa}\zeta^{2}-\tfrac{1}{\kappa}+V+W]\leqslant\textup{Var}[U+V+W]\leqslant 3\left(8\widehat{r}+\frac{4n\widehat{\delta}_{1}}{\kappa}\right).

By Chebyshev’s inequality, any centered random variable
Z
satisfies

\mathds{P}[Z\leqslant 2\sqrt{\textup{Var}[Z]}]\geqslant 1/2.

Applying this to
Z=\tfrac{1}{\kappa}\zeta^{2}-\tfrac{1}{\kappa}+V+W
, we arrive at

\displaystyle\mathds{P}_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}[\widehat{T}_{\textsf{{Grad}}}=1|X,X^{\prime}]

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\mathds{P}\left[\chi_{\widehat{r}-1}^{2}-(\widehat{r}-1)\geqslant n\widehat{\delta}_{1}-10\left(\sqrt{\widehat{r}+\frac{n\widehat{\delta}_{1}}{\kappa}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\mathds{P}\left[\chi_{\widehat{r}-1}^{2}-(\widehat{r}-1)\geqslant\frac{1}{\kappa}\left(n\widehat{\Delta}_{1}-10\sqrt{\kappa^{2}\widehat{r}+n\widehat{\Delta}_{1}}\right)\right]

Now, for
\widehat{r}
large enough, consider the regime

40\sqrt{\widehat{r}}\leqslant\frac{n\widehat{\Delta}_{1}}{\kappa}\leqslant 3\widehat{r}\quad\iff\quad 40\sqrt{\widehat{r}}\leqslant\frac{n}{\kappa^{2}}\leqslant 3\widehat{r}.

(82)

By simple algebra, in this regime we have that
10\sqrt{\kappa^{2}\widehat{r}+n\widehat{\Delta}_{1}}\leqslant n\widehat{\Delta}_{1}/2
, thus

\mathds{P}_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}[\widehat{T}_{\textsf{{Grad}}}=1|X,X^{\prime}]\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\mathds{P}\left[\chi_{\widehat{r}-1}^{2}-(\widehat{r}-1)\geqslant\frac{n\widehat{\Delta}_{1}}{2\kappa}\right].

On the other hand, in this regime we can apply the lower bound for the right tail probability of the chi-squared distribution (cf. (
73
)), which gives

\mathds{P}_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}[\widehat{T}_{\textsf{{Grad}}}=1|X,X^{\prime}]\geqslant c\exp\left(-\frac{C}{\kappa^{2}}\frac{n^{2}\widehat{\Delta}_{1}^{2}}{\widehat{r}}\right).

Now, allowing
\widehat{r}
to grow, let us put
\kappa=\sqrt{\widehat{r}}
and
n=40\widehat{r}^{3/2}

(this is on the “left edge” of (
82
)). Then

\mathds{P}_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}[\widehat{T}_{\textsf{{Grad}}}=1|X,X^{\prime}]\geqslant c.

On the other hand, in this regime we have
n\widehat{\Delta}_{1}\asymp\widehat{r}
up to a constant, whence, returning to test (
Lin
),

\mathds{P}_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}[\widehat{T}_{\textsf{{Lin}}}=1|X,X^{\prime}]+\mathds{P}_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}[\widehat{T}_{\textsf{{Lin}}}=0|X,X^{\prime}]\leqslant C\exp\bigg(-\frac{cn^{2}\widehat{\Delta}_{1}^{2}}{\widehat{r}}\bigg)\leqslant C\exp(-c\widehat{r}).

Thus, we see that there is a widening gap – as
\widehat{r}
grows – between the error bounds for the tests
\widehat{T}_{\textsf{{Lin}}}
and
\widehat{T}_{\textsf{{Grad}}}
(and moreover,
\widehat{T}_{\textsf{{Grad}}}
is not even consistent in this regime, whereas
\widehat{T}_{\textsf{{Lin}}}
still is).
∎

v\otimes u=vu^{{\sf T}}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\widehat{T}_{k}(\mu_{0})}
is the log-likelihood of
\widehat{T}_{k}(\mu_{0})
.
The functional

\textup{Tr}[\mathfrak{I}_{\widehat{T}_{k}(\mu_{0})}(\mu)]=\mathds{E}\left[\Big\|\nabla\mathcal{L}_{\widehat{T}_{k}(\mu_{0})}(\mu)\Big\|^{2}\right]

evaluated at
\mu=\mu_{0}
quantifies the amount of information about
\mu^{*}=\mu_{0}
contained in
\widehat{T}_{k}(\mu_{0})
, and can be compared with
\textup{Tr}[\mathfrak{I}_{\widehat{Z}_{k}}(\mu_{0})]
, where
\mathfrak{I}_{\widehat{Z}_{k}}(\mu)
corresponds to the likelihood of
\widehat{Z}_{k}
.
In addition, by rotational invariance of Gaussian distribution we have

{\widehat{T}_{k}}(\mu)=\|\omega^{(k)}+\|\mu-\mu_{k}\|\boldsymbol{e}_{1}\|^{2},

where
\boldsymbol{e}_{1}
is the first canonical vector in
\mathds{R}^{\widehat{r}}
, and
\omega^{(k)}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\boldsymbol{I}_{\widehat{r}})
.
Now, by the monotonicity property of Fisher information matrices (see, e.g.,
[
47
, Theorem 2.86]
) we have that

\mathfrak{I}_{\widehat{T}_{k}(\mu_{0})}(\mu)\preccurlyeq\mathfrak{I}_{\omega^{(k)}+\|\mu_{0}-\mu_{k}\|\boldsymbol{e}_{1}}(\mu).

Furthermore,
\omega^{(k)}+\|\mu-\mu_{k}\|\boldsymbol{e}_{1}
has independent entries, of which only the first one depends on
\mu
, so

\mathfrak{I}_{\omega^{(k)}+\|\mu_{0}-\mu_{k}\|\boldsymbol{e}_{1}}(\mu)=\mathfrak{I}_{\omega_{1}^{(k)}+\|\mu_{0}-\mu_{k}\|}(\mu).

Thus,
\textup{Tr}[\mathfrak{I}_{\widehat{T}_{k}(\mu_{0})}(\mu)]\leqslant\textup{Tr}[\mathfrak{I}_{\omega_{1}+\|\mu_{0}-\mu_{k}\|}(\mu)]
, where
\omega_{1}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.
Finally, the latter trace can be computed explicitly.
Indeed, the log-likelihood of the observation
\omega_{1}+\|\mu_{0}-\mu_{k}\|\sim\mathcal{N}(\|\mu_{0}-\mu_{k}\|,1)
at arbitrary
\mu
(i.e., with the actual data-generating distribution corresponding to
\mu=\mu_{0}
) is given by

\mathcal{L}_{\omega_{1}+\|\mu_{0}-\mu_{k}\|}(\mu)=-\tfrac{1}{2}(\omega_{1}+\|\mu_{0}-\mu_{k}\|-\|\mu-\mu_{k}\|)^{2}.

Taking the gradient (in
\mu
) through the composition formula, we get

\displaystyle\nabla\mathcal{L}_{\omega_{1}+\|\mu_{0}-\mu_{k}\|}(\mu)

\displaystyle=(\omega_{1}+\|\mu_{0}-\mu_{k}\|-\|\mu-\mu_{k}\|)\,\nabla(\|\mu-\mu_{k}\|)

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega_{1}+\|\mu_{0}-\mu_{k}\|-\|\mu-\mu_{k}\|}{\|\mu-\mu_{k}\|}(\mu-\mu_{k})=(\omega_{1}+\|\mu_{0}-\mu_{k}\|-\|\mu-\mu_{k}\|)\,\boldsymbol{e}_{\mu-\mu_{k}},

where
\boldsymbol{e}_{\mu-\mu_{k}}
is the unit-norm vector in the direction of
\mu-\mu_{k}
.
In particular, at
\mu=\mu_{0}
we get
\nabla\mathcal{L}_{\omega_{1}+\|\mu_{0}-\mu_{k}\|}(\mu_{0})=\omega_{1}\boldsymbol{e}_{\mu_{0}-\mu_{k}}
, thus
\|\nabla\mathcal{L}_{\omega_{1}+\|\mu_{0}-\mu_{k}\|}(\mu_{0})\|^{2}=\omega_{1}^{2}
and

\textup{Tr}[\mathfrak{I}_{\omega_{1}+\|\mu_{0}-\mu_{k}\|}(\mu_{0})]=1
.
We conclude that

\textup{Tr}[\mathfrak{I}_{\widehat{T}_{k}(\mu_{0})}(\mu_{0})]\leqslant 1,\quad k\in\{0,1\}.

Let us now contrast this with the amount of information about
\mu_{0}
that is already contained in the samples themselves. On one hand,
Y^{(1)}
does not reveal any information about
\mu_{0}
as it only depends on
\mu_{1}
; hence,
\textup{Tr}[\mathfrak{I}_{Y^{(1)}}(\mu_{0})]=0
. On the other hand,
\textup{Tr}[\mathfrak{I}_{Y^{(0)}}(\mu_{0})]=n
by a straightforward calculation in the Gaussian sequence model.
Thus, we conclude that each prediction scores contains information about
\mu^{*}
roughly corresponding to a single additional data point – just as we claimed.
∎
