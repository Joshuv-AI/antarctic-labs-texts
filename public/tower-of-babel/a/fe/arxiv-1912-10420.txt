Abstract
With the recent developments on opening the THz ( THz ) spectrum for experimental purposes by the FCC , transceivers operating in the range of 0.1 0.1 THz - 10 10 THz , which are known as THz bands, will enable ultra-high throughput wireless communications. However, actual implementation of the high-speed and high reliability THz band communication systems should start with providing extensive knowledge in regards to the propagation channel characteristics. Considering the huge bandwidth and the rapid changes in the characteristics of THz wireless channels, ray tracing and one-shot statistical modeling are not adequate to define an accurate channel model. In this work, we propose Gamma mixture based channel modeling for the THz band via the EM ( EM ) algorithm. First, MLE ( MLE ) is applied to characterize the Gamma mixture model parameters, and then EM algorithm is used to compute MLE of the unknown parameters of the measurement data. The accuracy of the proposed model is investigated by using the WMRD ( WMRD ) error metrics, KL ( KL )-divergence, and KS ( KS ) test to show the difference between the proposed model and the actual PDF that are obtained via the designed test environment. To efficiently evaluate the performance of the proposed method in more realistic scenarios, all the analysis is done by examining measurement data from a measurement campaign in the 240 240 GHz to 300 300 GHz frequency range, using a well-isolated anechoic chamber. According to WMRD error metrics, KL -divergence, and KS test results, PDF generated by the mixture of Gamma distributions fit to the actual histogram of the measurement data. It is shown that instead of taking pseudo-average characteristics of sub-bands in the wide band, using the mixture models allows for determining channel parameters more precisely.

Signal Model

\displaystyle r(t)=\mathrm{Re}\big\{[x_{I}(t)+jx_{Q}(t)]e^{j2\pi f_{c}t}\big\},

(1)

where

denotes the unit imaginary number and

is the real part of the complex number.
x_{I}(t)
and
x_{Q}(t)
are
I/Q
(
I/Q
) parts of the complex baseband signal.
f_{c}
stands for the carrier frequency of the signal.

The multipath channel at passband with different delays and attenuation levels can be given as:

\displaystyle h(t)=\sum_{l=0}^{L-1}a_{l}\delta(t-t_{l}),

(2)

where
L
is the number of multipath components.
a_{l}
and
t_{l}
denote the attenuation and delay factors for the
l
th path, respectively. The complex baseband representation of Eq. (
2
) is

\displaystyle h(t)=\sum_{l=0}^{L-1}a_{l}\delta(t-t_{l})e^{-j2\pi f_{c}t_{l}}.

(3)

If the channel consists of only
LOS
component,
L
in Eq. (
3
) is equal to 1. Then,
LOS
channel is given as:

\displaystyle h(t)=a_{0}\delta(t-t_{0})e^{-j2\pi f_{c}t_{0}},

(4)

where
a_{0}
and
2\pi f_{c}t_{0}
denote amplitude and phase of channel, respectively.
t_{0}
is propagation delay given with

\displaystyle t_{0}=\frac{d}{c},

(5)

where

Anechoic chambers, as used in our measurements, do not allow

(

a_{0}

\displaystyle P_{RX}=P_{TX}-10n\mathrm{log}(d)+M.

(6)

The received power
P_{RX}

II-B

Gamma Distribution

The Gamma function,
\Gamma(a)
, is defined as
[
37
]
:

\displaystyle\Gamma(a)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-x}x^{a-1}dx,\;a>0.

(7)

By using integration by parts,
\Gamma(a)=(a-1)!
when
a
is a positive integer. Consider the random variable
G
which is a mixture of
m
Gamma distributions and defined as:

f_{G}(x)=\sum_{l=1}^{m}\rho_{l}f_{l}(x;\alpha_{l},\beta_{l}),~l=1,2,\ldots,m,~~x>0,~~\rho_{l}>0

(8)

where
f_{l}(x;\alpha_{l},\beta_{l})=\frac{1}{\beta_{l}^{\alpha_{l}}\Gamma(\alpha_{l})}x^{\alpha_{l}-1}e^{-x/\beta_{l}}
;
\alpha_{l}>0
and
\beta_{l}>0
are the shape and scale parameters of the
l
th component of the mixture distribution;
\rho_{l}
denotes mixture proportions or weights that satisfy the conditions

(a)

0<\rho_{l}<1,~\forall l=1,2,\ldots,m
and

(b)

\sum_{l=1}^{m}\rho_{l}=1

. Please note that,
m
denotes the number of components in the mixture. The main reasons for using a mixture of Gamma distributions in the paper are:

(i)

the tractability of its
CDF
(
CDF
) and
MGF
(
MGF
),

(ii)

giving an approximation for small-scale fading channels
[
33
]
, and

(iii)

high accuracy by properly adjusting parameters

.

II-C

Maximum Likelihood Estimation

The
MLE
technique is provided to obtain the parameters of the gamma mixture from the actual channel
PDF
. Let assume that
X_{1},\cdots,X_{n}
are random variables with Gamma distribution (with unknown parameters
\alpha>0
and
\beta>0
). The likelihood function is given as:

\displaystyle L(x;\alpha,\beta)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{x_{i}^{\alpha-1}e^{-\frac{x_{i}}{\beta}}}{\Gamma\left(\alpha\right)\beta^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=\left\{\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\right\}^{-1}\left\{\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\right\}^{a}e^{-\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}}{\beta}}\beta^{-n\alpha}\Gamma^{-n}\left(\alpha\right)

The uninformative factor,
\left\{\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\right\}^{-1}
, is discarded

\displaystyle=\left\{\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\right\}^{a}e^{-\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}}{\beta}}\beta^{-n\alpha}\Gamma^{-n}\left(\alpha\right)

\displaystyle=\beta^{-n\alpha}\Gamma^{-n}\left(\alpha\right)\left\{\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\right\}^{a}e^{-\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}}{\beta}}

(9)

The corresponding log likelihood function of Eq. (
II-C
) leads to:

\displaystyle\ln\left(L\right)

\displaystyle=-n\alpha\ln\left(\beta\right)-n\ln\left(\Gamma(a)\right)+\alpha\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ln\left(x_{i}\right)-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{x_{i}}{\beta}.

(10)

Maximum likelihood estimates can be found for
\alpha
and
\beta
by taking partial derivatives of Eq. (
10
) with respect to
\alpha
and
\beta
, then we obtain:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\ln\left(L\right)}{\partial\alpha}

\displaystyle=-n\ln\left(\beta\right)-n\frac{\partial\ln\left(\Gamma(a)\right)}{\partial\alpha}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ln\left(x_{i}\right)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\ln\left(L\right)}{\partial\beta}

\displaystyle=-n\alpha\frac{1}{\beta}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{x_{i}}{\beta^{2}}

(11)

Because of the diGamma and logarithm functions in Eq. (
II-C
), a closed-form solution could not be provided
[
37
]
. Numerical methods such as Newton-Raphson can be applied to find the values for
\alpha
and
\beta
which is not the scope of this study.

II-D

Expectation Maximization

We have a training set
\mathbf{r}=\left(r_{1},r_{2},\cdots,r_{m}\right)
consisting of
m
independent observations captured by considering each measurement data at different transmitter-receiver separation distances such as
d
=
20
cm,
30
cm,
40
cm,
60
cm,
80
cm. Our goal is to fit the Gamma distribution parameters by utilizing the
EM
algorithm.
EM
algorithm, which is a machine learning technique
[
38
]
, provides a simplification to
MLE
problems, which are mostly seen in mixture models
[
35
]
. The
EM
algorithm consists of two steps, namely, the
expE
(
expE
)-step and the
maxM
(
maxM
)-step. The reader is referred to
[
39
]
for more detailed explanations about the
EM
algorithm.

The
EM
algorithm requires number of mixtures as a priori. Initially, the parameters are randomly chosen for the mixture model parameters
\theta_{1:M}=(\theta_{1},\cdots,\theta_{M})
. Then, the parameters are updated in each iteration until the convergence criteria hold. E-step calculates membership coefficients for all data point (
i=1,\cdots,L
) and mixture components (
k=1,\cdots,M
) by utilizing the current parameters
\theta_{1:M}

[
35
,
40
]

\displaystyle\phi_{ik}=\frac{\pi_{k}p_{k}(x_{i}|\theta_{k})}{\sum_{k=1}^{M}\pi_{k}p_{k}(x_{i}|\theta_{k})},

(12)

where
x_{i}
is the data in the
k
th mixture;
\pi_{k}
denotes the mixing proportion. It is obvious that
\sum_{k=1}^{M}\phi_{ik}=1
. Then, the parameter values and the mixing proportions for each mixture components are updated to maximize the likelihood probability in the M-step. In the M-step, the membership coefficients calculated in E-step are used to find parameters and mixing proportions as:

\displaystyle\pi_{k}^{new}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{L}\phi_{ik}}{L}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{k}]^{new}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{L}\phi_{ik}x_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{L}\phi_{ik}}=\alpha\beta

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}[X_{k}]^{new}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{L}\phi_{ik}(x_{i}-\mathbb{E}[X_{k}]^{new})^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{L}\phi_{ik}}=\alpha\beta^{2}.

(13)

The parameters (
\alpha,\beta
) for each Gamma mixture can be found by using Eq. (
13
).

II-E

Error Metrics

In this subsection, we provide an overview of the possible error metrics to determine the goodness-of-fit for the proposed model.

II-E
1
Weighted Mean Relative Difference

The proposed models are quantified by using
WMRD
, which gives a measurement for the difference between the model and actual
PDF
. It is defined as
[
35
]
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{WMRD}=\frac{\sum_{\rho}|y_{\rho}-\hat{y}_{\rho}|}{\sum_{\rho}(y_{\rho}+\hat{y}_{\rho})\times 0.5},

(14)

where
\rho
represents the received power and
y_{\rho}
is the number of
\rho
value observations in the received power set. As well as,
\hat{y}_{\rho}
is related to the estimated model.

II-E
2
Kolmogorov-Smirnov Test

KS
test is a non-parametric goodness-of-fit test, namely it does not make an assumption of any distribution. In addition to vector norm based error technique, the
KS
test is employed as goodness-of-fit test with the confidence level
p=0.05
to compare the actual
PDF
with the estimated mixture models.

II-E
3
Kullback–-Leibler Divergence

KL
distance or divergence is interpreted as the distance between the actual probability distribution,
P_{act}
and the estimated probability distribution,
P_{est}
. Let
P_{act}=\left\{p_{1},p_{2},\cdots,p_{n}\right\}
and
P_{est}=\left\{q_{1},q_{2},\cdots,q_{n}\right\}
, then
KL
-divergence is defined as

\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{act}\|P_{est})=-\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}P_{act}(x)\log\left(\frac{P_{est}(x)}{P_{act}(x)}\right).

(15)

In this paper,
KL
-divergence is utilized to compare the actual distribution and the estimated models via the
EM
algorithm.
KL
-divergence gets a higher value when two distributions have less similarities.

In Fig.

MLE
gets hampered to fit since it assumes that there is no serious change in the channel behavior through the transmission band due to the characteristics of the molecules in the propagation environment. Even though this assumption can be reasonable for the cellular communication bands below
60

THz
band. As shown in Fig.
4
, the mixture models provide more adequate
PDF
than
MLE
for the tx-rx separation between
20
cm and
80
cm. Fig.
4
shows that three mixtures and four mixtures of Gamma distribution have almost the same performance to fit the actual histograms. It is seen in Fig.
4
that the mean of the received signal power decreases for increasing distance, as expected. Furthermore, Fig.
4

30
cm, which is consistent with
[
2
,
4
]
.

Moreover,

-divergence also confirm that mixture models converge to the actual histogram better than

distance, and

IV-B

Comparison with Weibull and Gaussian Distributions

Although it is shown that Gamma mixture distribution is able to describe THz channels with ultra broadband in the Section

Firstly,
20

V
Concluding Remarks and Future Works

In this paper, we investigate the channel model for the
THz
band in between
240
GHz-
300
GHz by using Gamma mixture models. To find the mixture parameters,
EM
algorithm is utilized. It is visible that the mixture models are better to fit the measurement histogram for all measurements compared to
MLE
. The comparison between the mixture models and the actual
PDF
is carried out by
WMRD
,
KS
and
KL
-divergence metrics. The metrics adminiculate that the mixture of Gamma distributions can accurately model the
THz
channels.

Since the average channel capacity, the outage probability, and the symbol error rate are derived for mixture Gamma wireless channels, the analytical analyse can be carried by using mixture parameters given in this study. As known, the
EM
algorithm requires the number of mixtures as a priori information. However, to determine the number of mixtures, the Dirichlet process mixture model and Bayesian information criterion can be utilized.

Due to limitations of the measurement setup in this study, any mobility could not be considered. However, measurement campaigns which enable to consider mobility in THz band should be carried out. Furthermore, in-vivo channel characteristics are heavily dependent on the density of the materials in the tissue; therefore, the in-vivo channels have greater changes in their behaviors. It can be thought that mixture models are appropriate for also in-vivo channels. As a future work, in-vivo channel can be investigated by utilizing mixture models.
