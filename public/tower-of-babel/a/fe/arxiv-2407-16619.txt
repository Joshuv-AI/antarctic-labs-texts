Trapped Stresses in ‘Simple’ Yield Stress Materials Influence Their Rheology and Cause Delayed Yielding Preprint: APS/123-QED
Kasra Farain and Daniel Bonn

Abstract
Simple yield stress materials are composed of soft particles, bubbles, or droplets with purely repulsive forces. The constituent elements are typically too large to undergo thermal fluctuations, suggesting that the material internal structure, and therefore its rheology, should not change over time. Here, we explore the rheology of Carbopol, a prototypical simple yield stress material, and reveal that gradual, spontaneous relaxation of residual stresses—induced when loading the sample into rheometer—results in a small yet significant reduction in the material dynamic yield stress. This relaxation process can lead to a non-monotonic creep deformation rate under constant stress, culminating in delayed fluidization of the material. These findings highlight that yield stress is not merely a static material property but a function of the material internal stress state.

In this Letter, we demonstrate that trapped stresses, which usually emerge when the material is loaded into the rheometer, and their spontaneous slow relaxation significantly influence the rheology of Carbopol and can account for most of the puzzling observations above. Specifically, we show that the relaxation of these trapped stresses leads to a gradual reduction in the dynamic yield stress (
F_{SS}
) of Carbopol in time, which in turn can result in its delayed yielding under a constant near-threshold stress. While the stored stress in the material is usually ignored or attempts are made to remove it by a pre-shearing treatment, our results indicate that it is an integral component of the rheology of yield stress materials and should be considered in their physical description if it is present. Our data suggest that these residual stresses may be the primary source of discrepancies in the experimentally measured yield stress for a given material.

To demonstrate stress relaxation in our Carbopol sample, the material is loaded onto the bottom plate and compressed by the upper cone to fill the gap between the cone and plate as is usual in rheology experiments. During compression, the small surplus of material flows out of the gap, and the normal force measured by the rheometer increases as the yield stress material resists flowing. When this compression stops and the surplus material around the cone edge is carefully removed, a significant normal force persists, often measuring a few Newtons for our 50-mm cone. This elastic force then slowly decreases over an extended period (Fig. 2a), without any flow imposed by the rheometer. As the material is incompressible and the gap is fixed, the decrease in normal stress is due to spontaneous slow stress relaxation within the material.

Stress relaxation can also be observed in the shear stress. To do so, we rapidly apply a small shear strain in a step-wise manner to the sample, creating an initial shear stress. The shear stress is then measured as a function of time at fixed strain (Fig. 2b). While the normal stress measurements are not completely reproducible, at least partly due to variations in sample volume and edge effects, the shear stress always relaxes logarithmically with the same time scale for different preparations of the sample and applied shear strain. It should be noted that before each shear stress relaxation experiment, we allow the sample to relax so that the normal force is close to zero. In general, such logarithmic relaxation involves plastic events that lead to dissipation of elastic energy stored within non-ideal, non-crystalline elastic materials. This phenomenon therefore appears to be quite general. Our study, however, focuses on the consequences of such local microscopic relaxation processes on the global and macroscopic rheology of the yield stress material.

It is observed that while the normal stress slowly relaxes, the material is unable to attain a steady state, with the shear stress continuing to decrease gradually. Long-lasting unsteady flows in Carbopol under constant shear have also been attributed to ‘structuration’ mechanisms while the material was at rest

The observation that in the presence of relaxation,
\Sigma

\Sigma(t)
should closely approximate the instantaneous dynamic yield stress of the material at time t, corresponding to its specific trapped stresses and relaxation state at that moment. Defining a time-dependent yield stress is reasonable and useful because the rate of changes induced by relaxation in the material is considerably slower than the time scale of typical stress-imposed experiments where the material’s stability or yielding is studied.

However, the decrease in the minimum stress for steady Carbopol flow, or its dynamic yield stress, during stress relaxation can be directly observed in stress-imposed tests conducted over long periods. Figure 3a illustrates the normal force relaxation for a Carbopol sample squeezed in the cone-plate geometry. During the time interval marked by a red stripe in this figure, a constant shear stress of 38 Pa is applied to the sample. The shear rate arising in the material is shown by red triangles in Fig. 3b. Although the material initially exhibits a small shear rate, it soon approaches zero. This is a transient creep flow in response to the suddenly applied stress, characteristic of yield stress materials under stresses below the yield point
22
;
24
. After 90 minutes more of spontaneous relaxation, the exact same shear stress is applied to the material again during the time interval indicated by a blue stripe in Fig. 3a. This results in a shear rate shown by blue circles in Fig. 3b. As observed, this time, the material stabilizes at a finite constant shear rate under 38 Pa. This observation clearly indicates that the dynamic yield stress of the material has decreased from above 38 Pa to below 38 Pa between the two tests.

All this suggests that the material’s yield stress is affected by the trapped stress. Could this account for the elusive observation of delayed yielding in these systems under a constant imposed stress? In Fig. 4, we plot the shear rate as a function of time for a Carbopol sample undergoing stress relaxation under an imposed shear stress of 42 Pa. Initially, the shear rate decreases, seemingly going to zero, indicating that the applied stress is below the instantaneous yield stress. However, as the trapped stresses relax and the yield stress decreases, a sudden acceleration of the flow is observed; the system begins to yield and flow.
This delayed yielding has attracted much attention in the past, and various interpretations have been proposed

;
23
;
24
;
26

24

\Sigma_{t}

\gamma

\gamma

Summarizing, we have demonstrated that the rheology of an archetypical ‘simple’ yield stress material, Carbopol is influenced by the presence of trapped stresses and their subsequent spontaneous relaxation. Although such effects are often avoided in standard rheology tests through pre-shearing treatments and might not be immediately evident in experimental results from samples prepared in a consistent manner, they are critically important for the universal quantitative description of these systems. Additionally, these results denote that pre-shearing treatments should focus on the release of trapped stresses, especially considering that very high rate shearing can itself lead to increase of stresses within the material. Furthermore, the relaxation phenomenon becomes crucial in scenarios where standard pre-treatments under a rheometer are not possible. An example arises in simple experiments involving a ball falling through a Carbopol sample
37
;
38
. In a classical study, the speed of a falling ball passing through Carbopol was measured, and two subsequent experiments yielded ball speeds varying by a factor of two
39
. Our measurements show that such perplexing observations might be understood in terms of stress relaxation of the system, and that they can be avoided by allowing the system to rest long enough to relax all the trapped stresses arising from the preparation processes and previous falling ball experiment(s).
