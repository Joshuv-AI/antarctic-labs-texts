Wong–Zakai approximations with convergence rate for stochastic differential equations with regime switching
Giang T. Nguyen, Oscar Peralta

Abstract
We construct Wong–Zakai approximations of time–inhomogeneous stochastic differential equations with regime switching (RSSDEs), and provide a convergence rate. In the proposed approximations, the standard Brownian motion driving the time-inhomogeneous RSSDEs is replaced by a family of finite–variation processes { ℱ λ } λ > 0 \{\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}\}_{\lambda>0} . We show that if ℱ λ \mathcal{F}^{\lambda} strongly converges to ℬ \mathcal{B} at rate δ ⁡ ( λ ) \delta(\lambda) , then the Wong–Zakai approximation strongly converges to the original solution of the time–inhomogeneous RSSDE at rate δ ⁡ ( λ ) ​ λ ε \delta(\lambda)\lambda^{\varepsilon} , for any ε > 0 \varepsilon>0 . This is the first paper on Wong–Zakai approximations for time–inhomogeneous RSSDEs, and significantly extends the counterparts for time–homogeneous SDEs without regime switching in Römisch and Wakolbinger [ 24 ] .

Introduction

Let
\mathcal{B}=\{B_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
be a standard Brownian motion and let
\mathcal{J}=\{J_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
be a càdlàg jump process with finite state space
\mathcal{E}
. Suppose that
\mathcal{J}
is independent of
\mathcal{B}
and has finite jump activity on compact intervals. For
i\in\mathcal{E}
, let
\mu_{i}:\mathds{R}_{+}\times\mathds{R}\mapsto\mathds{R}
and
\sigma_{i}:\mathds{R}_{+}\times\mathds{R}\mapsto\mathds{R}
, where
\mathds{R}_{+}=[0,\infty)
. A
regime–switching time-inhomogeneous stochastic differential equation
(RSSDE) takes the form

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}X_{t}

\displaystyle=\mu_{J_{t}}(t,X_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\sigma_{J_{t}}(t,X_{t})\mathrm{d}B_{t},\quad{X_{0}=x_{0}\in\mathds{R}}.

(1.1)

RSSDEs are used to model systems in which randomness arise from two sources: continuous (diffusive) and discrete (environmental). These processes are widely used in areas such as economics, finance, ecology, and automatic control
[
30
]
. Although (
1.1
) is well–defined in the context of Itô stochastic differential equations (SDE), most of its properties such as existence and uniqueness of solutions and stability do not follow from standard SDE theory and thus need to be properly verified; see
[
18
]
for the particular case when
\mathcal{J}
is Markovian.

Suppose that there exists a unique continuous solution
\mathcal{X}=\{X_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
of (
1.1
). Our goals in paper are twofold. First, we construct a family of the so–called Wong–Zakai approximations
\{\mathcal{X}^{\lambda}\}_{\lambda>0}=\{\{X^{\lambda}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}\}_{\lambda>0}
. On a fixed time interval
[0,T]
,
T<\infty
, we replace the standard Brownian motion
\mathcal{B}
in (
1.1
) by a family of finite-variation processes
\{\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}\}_{\lambda>0}=\{\{F^{\lambda}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}\}_{\lambda>0}
that strongly converges to
\mathcal{B}
; that is,

\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|B_{t}-F^{\lambda}_{t}\right|=0\quad\mbox{almost surely.}

For each
\lambda>0
, consider the Wong–Zakai approximation
\mathcal{X}^{\lambda}
that is the solution of the following regime-switching SDE

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}X_{t}^{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\left(\mu_{J_{t}}(t,X_{t}^{\lambda})-\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{J_{t}}(t,X^{\lambda}_{t})\partial_{2}\left[\sigma_{J_{t}}(t,X^{\lambda}_{t})\right]\right)\mathrm{d}t+\sigma_{J_{t}}(t,X_{t}^{\lambda})\mathrm{d}F_{t}^{\lambda},

(1.2)

where
\partial_{k}
denotes the partial differential operator with respect to the
k
th entry. We show in Proposition
4.1
that this solution is unique.

Second, under certain regularity conditions on
\mu_{i}
and
\sigma_{i}
, and boundedness on
\mathcal{J}
, we show that the Wong–Zakai approximations
\{\mathcal{X}^{\lambda}\}_{\lambda>0}
converge to the solution
\mathcal{X}
of (
1.1
). More precisely, we obtain the rate of strong convergence of
\mathcal{X}^{\lambda}
to
\mathcal{X}
, and show that it is
almost the same
as that of
\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}
to
\mathcal{B}
. The precise rate of strong convergence and the conditions under which this holds are stated in Theorem
1.1
, our main result.

Theorem 1.1
.

Under Assumptions
1
–
4
, suppose that there exists a family of finite–variation processes
\{\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}\}_{\lambda>0}
such that for some
\delta:\mathds{R}_{+}\mapsto\mathds{R}_{+}
with
\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}\delta(\lambda)=0
, we have that for all
q>0

\displaystyle\mathds{P}\left(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|F^{\lambda}_{t}-B_{t}|\geq\alpha\delta(\lambda)\right)=o(\lambda^{-q}),

where
\alpha=\alpha(q,T)>0
is some constant dependent on
q
and
T
only. For
\lambda>0
, let
\mathcal{X}^{\lambda}
be the unique solution
\mathcal{X}^{\lambda}
to the regime-switching SDE

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}X_{t}^{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\left(\mu_{J_{t}}(t,X_{t}^{\lambda})-\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{J_{t}}(t,X^{\lambda}_{t})\partial_{2}\left[\sigma_{J_{t}}(t,X^{\lambda}_{t})\right]\right)\mathrm{d}t+\sigma_{J_{t}}\left(t,X_{t}^{\lambda}\right)\mathrm{d}F_{t}^{\lambda},

(1.3)

\displaystyle X^{\lambda}_{0}

\displaystyle=x_{0}.

(1.4)

Then,

(i)

the family of processes
\{\mathcal{X}^{\lambda}\}_{\lambda>0}
converges strongly to the unique solution
\mathcal{X}
of the regime–switching time–inhomogeneous SDE (
1.1
)

(ii)

moreover, for all
q,T>0
there exists
\gamma=\gamma(q,T)>0
such that for all
\varepsilon>0

\displaystyle\mathds{P}\left(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|X^{\lambda}_{t}-X_{t}|\geq\gamma\delta(\lambda)\lambda^{\varepsilon}\right)=o(\lambda^{-q}).

(1.5)

Thus, Theorem
1.1
states that if
\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}
strongly converges to
\mathcal{B}
at rate
\delta(\lambda)
, then the Wong–Zakai approximation
\mathcal{X}^{\lambda}
strongly converges to
\mathcal{X}
at rate
\delta(\lambda)\lambda^{\varepsilon}
, for any
\varepsilon>0
.

Details of Assumptions
1
–
4
can be found in later sections. Briefly speaking, Assumption
1
is about linear growth and local Lipschitz continuity, and is standard for ensuring existence and uniqueness of solutions of SDEs. Assumption
2
provides bounds for the diffusion coefficient
\sigma
and its derivative, and is standard for Wong–Zakai approximations. Assumption
3
is needed specifically because of the time–inhomogeneity feature,
and does not automatically follow from the properties of
\mu
and
\sigma
described in Assumptions
1
and
2
.
Assumption
4
arises due to the regime switching feature, and bounds the tail of the jump distribution of
\mathcal{J}
over a compact time interval. This assumption allows us to obtain the convergence rate in the regime–switching case, without being overly restrictive: it holds whenever
\mathcal{J}
has a bounded jump intensity. Cases where Assumption
4
is satisfied include, but are not limited to, time–homogeneous Markovian, time–inhomogeneous Markovian, and semi–Markovian processes.

Most related to this paper is the work of Römisch and Wakolbinger
[
24
]
, which determined the rate of strong convergence of the Wong–Zakai approximation for time-homogeneous SDEs without regime switching, under Assumptions
1
and
2
reduced to the time–homogeneous setting. While our strong approximation is an extension of

[
24
]
, our methods significantly differ from theirs in order to handle challenges that the regime–switching and time–inhomongeneity generalizations bring to the problem.
A key technique in our analysis is applying the Lamperti transform
[
16
]
to regime–switching SDEs (
1.1
). This was previously applied in Pettersson
[
22
]
and Zhang
[
32
]
in the simpler case of time–homogeneous SDEs without regime switching.
The Lamperti transformation leads to clear expressions in the approximation and an insight on how to bound approximation errors at jump epochs of
\mathcal{J}
.

1.1
Related literature

Wong and Zakai
[
28
,
29
]
were the first to consider almost-sure approximations to solutions of SDEs. Let
\pi
be a finite partition of
[a,b]
, where
\pi=\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{k+1}\}
,
a=t_{1}<t_{2}<\ldots<t_{k+1}=b
, and let
\mathcal{B}^{n}=\{B^{n}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
be a process whose the sample paths coincide with those of
\mathcal{B}
at
t_{j}
and are linear between them. Then, as the mesh of
\pi
, i.e.
\max_{j}|t_{j+1}-t_{j}|
, tends to
0
, the solutions
\mathcal{Y}^{n}=\{Y^{n}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
of the equation

\displaystyle Y^{n}_{t}=y_{0}+\int_{a}^{t}\mu(s,Y^{n}_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{a}^{t}\sigma(s,Y^{n}_{s})\,\mathrm{d}B^{n}_{s},\quad t\in[a,b],

(1.6)

converge in quadratic mean to
\mathcal{Y}=\{Y_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}

[
29
]
, which is a solution of

\displaystyle Y_{t}=y_{0}+\int_{a}^{t}\mu(s,Y_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{a}^{t}\sigma(s,Y_{s})\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{a}^{t}\sigma(s,Y_{s})\partial_{2}[\sigma(s,Y_{s})]\,\mathrm{d}s.

(1.7)

The last term on the RHS of (
1.7
) is known as the
Wong-Zakai correction term
, and the solution to (
1.7
) is also known as
the Stratonovich solution to the SDE
. In
[
28
]
, Wong and Zakai extended their previous result to show that, given any piece-wise smooth approximation
\mathcal{B}^{n}
for
\mathcal{B}
, the convergence from
\mathcal{Y}^{n}
to
\mathcal{Y}
holds almost surely.

Subsequently, approximations following this framework are generally referred to as of the
Wong-Zakai type
; see the comprehensive survey by Twardowska
[
27
]
and references therein. We describe briefly three major directions of generalization of Wong–Zakai results. First, the Wong–Zakai framework has been extended to SDEs driven by higher–dimensional Brownian motion processes. Stroock and Varadhan
[
26
]
proposed piecewise–linear approximations and showed convergence in probability; Ikeda
et al.

[
13
]
and Ikeda and Watanabe
[
14
]
proved, respectively, convergence in meansquare and uniform convergence over finite time interval. Second, Wong–Zakai approximations have also been obtained for reflected SDEs. Pettersson
[
22
]
analysed SDEs reflected on the boundary of a convex set, replacing the Brownian motion process with its polygonal approximation, and obtained uniform convergence. Evans and Stroock
[
6
]
approximated SDEs reflected in general domains and obtained convergence in law. Zhang
[
31
]
showed strong convergence of reflected SDE in multi–dimensional general domains. Third, Wong–Zakai approximations have been generalized to stochastic partial differential equations, such as the Wong-Zakai theorem obtained by Hairer and Pardoux
[
12
]
for one–dimensional parabolic nonlinear stochastic PDEs driven by space–time white noise.

Results on convergence rates in Wong-Zakai approximations are much rarer; we briefly highlight a few. Brzeniak and Flandoli
[
3
]
proved almost sure convergence for parabolic and hyperbolic evolution equations and provided estimates for the rate of convergence. Later, Brzeniak and Carroll
[
2
]
obtained convergence rate for approximations of SDEs in M–type
2
Banach spaces. More recently, Nakayama and Tappe
[
19
]
obtained convergence rate for semilinear stochastic partial differential equation, and Ammou and Lanconelli
[
1
]
for Stratonovich and Itô quasi–linear SDEs.

1.2
Paper structure

We outline in Section
2
some results of stochastic calculus for regime-switching processes which will be useful later. In
3.1
, we introduce the Lamperti transformation,
\mathcal{L}
, of a solution of a regime-switching SDE,
\mathcal{X}
, and prove its properties. In
3.2
, we construct the transformation pathwise and prove its uniqueness. In Section
4
, we apply the Lamperti transformation to the approximating process

, given in (

), to obtain
\mathcal{L}^{\lambda}
and compute its convergence rate to

. We show that, in fact,
\mathcal{L}^{\lambda}
is the unique càdlàg solution to a regime-switching SDE driven by
\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}
with
jumps
. Lipschitz continuity helps us obtain the almost sure convergence, and subsequently the convergence rate, from
\mathcal{X}^{\lambda}
to
\mathcal{X}
, giving rise to Theorem
1.1
. Finally, in Section
6
we state a direct implication of Theorem
1.1
, with respect to recent approximations of Markov-modulated Brownian motion
[
17
,
21
]
.

2
Preliminaries and notation

Here, we briefly summarize key results in stochastic calculus for regime-switching processes needed for this paper. We write
\partial_{k\ell}\equiv\partial_{k}\partial_{\ell}
to denote the partial differential operator first w.r.t. to the
k
th entry and then w.r.t. the
\ell
th entry. Furthermore,
\mathcal{C}^{i,j}
denotes the class of continuous
\mathds{R}^{2}\mapsto\mathds{R}
functions with continuous
i
th derivative with respect to the first parameter and continuous
j
th derivative with respect to the second parameter.

Let
\mathcal{Z}=\{Z_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
be an almost surely continuous stochastic process; we write
\{[Z]_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
to denote the quadratic variation process of
\mathcal{Z}
.

Theorem 2.1

(Itô’s formula)
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{C}^{1,2}
. Then, for all
t\geq 0

\displaystyle f(t,Z_{t})

\displaystyle=f(0,Z_{0})+\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{1}[f(s,Z_{s})]\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{2}[f(s,Z_{s})]\mathrm{d}Z_{s}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{22}[f(s,Z_{s})]\mathrm{d}[Z]_{s}\quad\mbox{a.s.}

Remark 2.2
.

If
\mathcal{Z}
is has finite variation, then
[Z]\equiv 0
. If
\mathcal{Z}=\mathcal{B}
, then
[Z]\equiv\mbox{Id}
.

Theorem 2.3

(Itô’s formula for regime-switching processes)
.

For
i\in\mathcal{E}
, let
f_{i}\in\mathcal{C}^{1,2}
. Then, for all
t\geq 0

\displaystyle f_{J_{t}}(t,Z_{t})

\displaystyle=f_{J_{0}}(0,Z_{0})+\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{1}[f_{J_{s}}(s,Z_{s})]\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{2}[f_{J_{s}}(s,Z_{s})]\mathrm{d}Z_{s}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{22}[f_{J_{s}}(s,Z_{s})]\mathrm{d}[Z]_{s}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{s\leq t:J_{s}\neq J_{s-}}\left(f_{J_{s}}(s,Z_{s})-f_{J_{s-}}(s,Z_{s})\right)\quad\mbox{a.s.}

(2.1)

Proof.

This follows by concatenation. Let
t_{1}<t_{2}<\dots<t_{n}
denote the jump epochs of
\mathcal{J}
on
(0,t]
, and let
t_{0}:=0
,
t_{n+1}:=t
. Let
j_{i}=J_{t_{i}}
,
0\leq i\leq n
. Notice that

\displaystyle f_{J_{t}}(t,Z_{t})-f_{J_{0}}(0,Z_{0})=f_{J_{n}}(t_{n+1},Z_{t_{n+1}})-f_{j_{0}}(t_{0},Z_{t_{0}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left(f_{j_{k}}(t_{k+1},Z_{t_{k+1}})-f_{j_{k}}(t_{k},Z_{t_{k}})\right)+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(f_{j_{k}}(t_{k},Z_{t_{k}})-f_{j_{k-1}}(t_{k},Z_{t_{k}})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left(f_{j_{k}}(t_{k+1},Z_{t_{k+1}})-f_{j_{k}}(t_{k},Z_{t_{k}})\right)+\sum_{s\leq t:J_{s}\neq J_{s-}}\left(f_{J_{s}}(s,Z_{s})-f_{J_{s-}}(s,Z_{s})\right).

(2.2)

Then, (
2.1
) follows by applying Itô’s formula to each term in the first sum of (
2.2
).
∎

Note that the concatenation method used in the proof of Theorem
2.3
holds for both Markovian and non-Markovian jump processes. This technique can be found in the proof of Lemma 3 in
[
25
, Section II.2.1]
, where an alternative form of Itô’s formula is provided for the particular case of regime-switching diffusions with an underlying Markovian jump mechanism; see
[
30
, Equation (2.8)]
for a related formula.

Remark 2.4
.

Since
\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}
converges strongly to
\mathcal{B}
, for each
t\geq 0
we have

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}f_{J_{t}}(t,F^{\lambda}_{t})-f_{J_{t}}(t,B_{t})=0\quad\mbox{a.s.}

(2.3)

Applying Theorem
2.3
to both terms in (
2.3
)
we obtain

\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{2}[f_{J_{s}}(s,F^{\lambda}_{s})]\mathrm{d}F^{\lambda}_{s}=\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{2}[f_{J_{s}}(s,B_{s})]\mathrm{d}B_{s}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{22}[f_{J_{s}}(s,B_{s})]\mathrm{d}s\quad\mbox{a.s.}

In particular, if
\psi_{i}\in\mathcal{C}^{1,1}
, then by taking
f_{i}(t,x):=\int_{0}^{x}\psi_{i}(t,y)\mathrm{d}y

we have

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}\int_{0}^{t}\psi_{J_{s}}(s,F^{\lambda}_{s})\mathrm{d}F^{\lambda}_{s}=\int_{0}^{t}\psi_{J_{s}}(s,B_{s})\mathrm{d}B_{s}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{2}[\psi_{J_{s}}(s,B_{s})]\mathrm{d}s\quad\mbox{a.s.}

(2.4)

As seen in (
2.4
), whenever we compute the stochastic integral w.r.t. a finite-variation process with regime switching, the limit as
\lambda\rightarrow\infty
is equal to the same stochastic integral w.r.t. the Brownian motion
plus
a correction term.

Now, consider the regime-switching SDE given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}Y_{t}

\displaystyle=\mu_{J_{t}}(t,Y_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\sigma_{J_{t}}(t,Y_{t})\mathrm{d}Z_{t},\quad Y_{0}=x_{0}.

(2.5)

In order to guarantee the existence of a unique càdlàg solution of (
2.5
), we need the following concepts. Let
g:\mathds{R}_{+}\times\mathds{R}\mapsto\mathds{R}
. We say that the function
g
is:

•

linearly growing
if there exists
E>0
such that

g(t,x)\leq E(1+t+|x|)\quad\forall t\geq 0,x\in\mathds{R};

•

locally Lipschitz continuous
if for each compact set
\mathcal{K}\subset\mathds{R}_{+}\times\mathds{R}
, there exists
M_{\mathcal{K}}>0
such that

\displaystyle\Big|g(t_{1},x_{1})-g(t_{2},x_{2})\Big|

\displaystyle\leq M_{\mathcal{K}}\left(|t_{1}-t_{2}|+|x_{1}-x_{2}|\right)\quad\forall(t_{1},x_{1}),(t_{2},x_{2})\in\mathcal{K};

•

Lipschitz continuous on its second entry
if there exists
M>0
such that

\displaystyle\Big|g(t,x_{1})-g(t,x_{2})\Big|

\displaystyle\leq M|x_{1}-x_{2}|\quad\forall t\geq 0,x\in\mathds{R}.

Note that a function being Lipschitz continuous on its second entry does not necessarily imply linear growth nor local Lipschitz continuity (e.g.
g(t,x)=\sqrt{t}+t^{2}
).

Assumption 1
.

For all
i\in\mathcal{E}
,
t\geq 0
and
x\in\mathds{R}
:

(i)

\mu_{i}
is in
\mathcal{C}^{0,0}
, linearly growing and locally Lipschitz continuous,

(ii)

\sigma_{i}
is in
\mathcal{C}^{1,1}
, linearly growing, locally Lipschitz continuous, and Lipschitz continuous on its second entry,

(iii)

\partial_{1}[\sigma_{i}]
and
\partial_{2}[\sigma_{i}]
are locally Lipschitz continuous.

Assumption
1
corresponds to classic conditions of existence and uniqueness of the solution of an SDE without regime switching
[
23
, Section V.12]
. For regime-switching SDEs, existence and uniqueness of solutions between jump times of
\mathcal{J}
, together with a continuous concatenation of paths, yields the existence and uniqueness of
\mathcal{Y}=\{Y_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
. In Section
3.2
, we make explicit this concatenation technique for the Lamperti transform of the solution of the regime–switching SDEs (

).

3
Lamperti transform for regime-switching SDEs

In general,
Lamperti transform
refers a class of transformations of solutions of SDEs that lead to the diffusion term in the resulting processes being independent of the state
[
16
]
.

3.1
Functional construction

Here, we introduce a Lamperti transformation for a regime-switching SDE. Denote by
\{t_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
the jump times of
\mathcal{J}
, with
t_{0}:=0
, and we write
J_{k}
for
J_{t_{k}}
for
k\geq 0
whenever there is no ambiguity. Let
\mathcal{X}
be the unique solution to (
1.1
). To construct the transformation, we need an extra assumption which is also in classic papers dealing with Wong-Zakai approximations
[
28
,
24
]
.

Assumption 2
.

For
i\in\mathcal{E}
,

(i)

there exist constants
v
and
V
such that
0<v<\sigma_{i}<V<\infty
;

(ii)

there exists a constant
K>0
such that
|\partial_{1}[\sigma_{i}]|/\sigma_{i}^{2}\leq K
.

Theorem 3.1

(Lamperti transform for regime-switching SDEs)
.

Suppose Assumptions
1
and
2
hold. For
t\geq 0
and
i\in\mathcal{E}
, define

\displaystyle h_{i}(t,x):=\int_{x_{0}}^{x}\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}(t,y)}\mathrm{d}y,\quad x\in\mathds{R},

(3.1)

and
L_{t}:=h_{J_{t}}(t,X_{t})
. Then, the process
\mathcal{L}=\{L_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
satisfies the regime-switching stochastic differential equation with jumps given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}L_{t}

\displaystyle=\mu^{*}_{J_{t}}(t,L_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{d}B_{t}+\sum_{s\leq t:J_{s}\neq J_{s^{-}}}\left(h_{J_{s}}\left(s,h^{-1}_{J_{s^{-}}}(s,L_{s^{-}})\right)-L_{s^{-}}\right),

(3.2)

\displaystyle L_{0}

\displaystyle=h_{J_{0}}(0,x_{0}),

where, for each fixed
t
,
h_{i}^{-1}(t,\cdot):\mathds{R}\mapsto\mathds{R}
denotes the inverse of
h_{i}(t,\cdot):\mathds{R}\mapsto\mathds{R}
and

\displaystyle\mu^{*}_{i}(t,\ell):=\partial_{1}\left[h_{i}\left(t,h_{i}^{-1}(t,\ell)\right)\right]+\frac{\mu_{i}\left(t,h_{i}^{-1}(t,\ell)\right)}{\sigma_{i}\left(t,h_{i}^{-1}(t,\ell)\right)}-\frac{1}{2}\partial_{2}\left[\sigma_{i}\left(t,h_{i}^{-1}(t,\ell)\right)\right].

(3.3)

Even though
\mathcal{L}
has discontinuities at
\{t_{k}\}_{k>0}
, the important characteristic is that it has a unit diffusion coefficient; it is identically
1
and independent of
J_{t}
,
t
, and
L_{t}
.

Proof.

Since
1/V<1/\sigma_{i}(t,y)<1/v
, the function
h_{i}(t,\cdot)
is non-decreasing and thus bijective with a continuous inverse. The boundedness and
\mathcal{C}^{1,1}
properties of
\sigma_{i}
imply that
h_{i}(\cdot,\cdot)\in\mathcal{C}^{1,2}
. Applying Theorem
2.3
gives

\displaystyle L_{t}-L_{0}

\displaystyle=h_{J_{t}}(t,X_{t})-h_{J_{0}}(0,x_{0})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{1}\left[h_{J_{s}}(s,X_{s})\right]\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{2}\left[h_{J_{s}}(s,X_{s})\right]\mathrm{d}X_{s}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{22}\left[h_{J_{s}}(s,X_{s})\right]\mathrm{d}[X]_{s}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{s\leq t:J_{s}\neq J_{s-}}\left(h_{J_{s}}(s,X_{s})-h_{J_{s-}}(s,X_{s})\right)

Since
\mathrm{d}[X]_{t}=\sigma_{J_{t}}^{2}(t,X_{t})\mathrm{d}[B]_{t}=\sigma_{J_{t}}^{2}(t,X_{t})\mathrm{d}t,
we have

\displaystyle L_{t}-L_{0}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\left(\partial_{1}\left[h_{J_{s}}(s,X_{s})\right]+\partial_{2}\left[h_{{J_{s}}}(s,X_{s})\right]\mu_{J_{{s}}}({s},X_{s})+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{22}\left[h_{{J_{s}}}(s,X_{s})\right]\sigma_{J_{{s}}}^{2}({s},X_{{s}})\right)\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{2}[h_{{J_{s}}}(s,X_{s})]\sigma_{J_{{s}}}({s},X_{{s}})\mathrm{d}B_{s}+\sum_{s\leq t:J_{s}\neq J_{s-}}\left(h_{J_{s}}(s,X_{s})-h_{J_{s-}}(s,X_{s})\right).

Since
\partial_{2}\left[h_{{J_{t}}}(t,X_{t})\right]=1/\sigma_{J_{t}}(t,X_{t}),
we have

\partial_{2}\left[h_{{J_{s}}}(s,X_{s})\right]\sigma_{J_{{s}}}({s},X_{{s}})\mathrm{d}B_{s}=\mathrm{d}B_{s}.

Furthermore, as
\mathcal{X}
is continuous, it follows that

\displaystyle h_{J_{s}}(s,X_{s})

\displaystyle=h_{J_{s}}(s,X_{s^{-}})=h_{J_{s}}\left(s,h^{-1}_{J_{s^{-}}}(s,L_{s^{-}})\right),\quad\mbox{and}\quad

\displaystyle h_{J_{s-}}(s,X_{s})

\displaystyle=h_{J_{s-}}(s,X_{s^{-}})=L_{s^{-}},

Finally, (
3.3
) follows by noticing that

\displaystyle\partial_{22}\left[h_{{J_{t}}}(t,X_{t})\right]=-\frac{\partial_{2}\left[\sigma_{J_{t}}(t,X_{t})\right]}{\sigma_{J_{t}}^{2}(t,X_{t})}\quad\mbox{and}\quad X_{t}=h_{{J_{t}}}^{-1}(t,L_{t}),

and thus
\mathcal{L}
solves (
3.2
).
∎

Remark 3.2
.

Since
h_{i}(t,\cdot)
is invertible, the Lamperti transform is injective. Thus, if we find the solution
\mathcal{L}
to (
3.2
) and prove its uniqueness, then we can recover the solution
\mathcal{X}
to (
1.1
) by taking
X_{t}:=h_{J_{t}}^{-1}(t,L_{t})
for all
t\geq 0
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Under Assumptions
1
and
2
, for each
i\in\mathcal{E}
the function
\mu_{i}^{*}
, defined as in (
3.3
), is locally Lipschitz continuous and has linear growth.

Proof.

Note that
\mu^{*}_{i}
is formed by sums and compositions of
\partial_{1}[h_{i}]
,
h_{i}^{-1}(t,\cdot)
,
\mu_{i}/\sigma_{i}
and
\partial_{2}[\sigma_{i}]
, all locally Lipschitz continuous and linearly growing: the first three by Lemma
7.1
and the last one by Assumption
1
(ii, iii); the function
\partial_{2}[\sigma_{i}]
is in fact bounded by the Lipschitz continuity on the second entry of
\sigma_{i}
. This implies that
\mu^{*}_{i}
, too, is locally Lipschitz and linearly growing.
∎

3.2
Pathwise construction and uniqueness

By a concatenation argument, Lemma
3.3
implies that under Assumptions
1
and
2
a unique càdlàg solution
\mathcal{L}
to the regime-switching SDE (
3.2
) exists. Given its importance, we give a proof of this in this section.

Here, we explicitly consider
\mathcal{L}
as a path functional of
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{J}
.
Since
\mathcal{L}
has a unit diffusion, the pathwise construction of
\mathcal{L}
between jumps is considerably easier than in
[
5
,
8
,
24
]
, as shown next.
For fixed
i\in\mathcal{E},b\in\mathds{R},r\geq 0
consider the process
\mathcal{Y}_{i,b,r}=\{Y_{i,b,r}(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
where

\displaystyle Y_{i,b,r}(t)

\displaystyle:=b+\int_{0}^{t}\mu_{i}^{*}\left(r+u,Y_{i,b,r}(u)+B_{r+u}-B_{r}\right)\mathrm{d}u,

(3.4)

where
\mu^{*}_{i}
is given in (
3.3
). Here, the parameter
b
acts as the starting point, and
r
a displacement in time with respect to
\mathcal{B}
. By Theorem
3.3
, for any given realization of
\mathcal{B}
, a unique solution
\mathcal{Y}_{i,b,r}
to the system exists
[
23
, Section V.2]
.

Next, define the process
\mathcal{S}=\{S_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
as follows. Let
S_{0}=x_{0}
, and recursively over
n\geq 0
let

\displaystyle S_{t}

\displaystyle:=Y_{J_{n},S_{t_{n}},t_{n}}(t-t_{n})+B_{t}-B_{t_{n}}\quad\mbox{for}\quad t\in(t_{n},t_{n+1}),

(3.5)

\displaystyle S_{t_{n}}

\displaystyle:=h_{J_{n}}\left(t_{n},h_{J_{n-1}}^{-1}(t_{n},S_{t_{n}^{-}})\right),\quad n\geq 1.

(3.6)

Theorem 3.4
.

The process
\mathcal{S}
is the unique solution to the regime-switching stochastic differential equation with jumps given by (
3.2
).

Proof.

Clearly, the process
\mathcal{S}
is continuous on
[0,\infty)\setminus\{t_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
. Furthermore, since
\lim_{s\downarrow 0}Y_{i,b,r}(s)=b
for
i\in\mathcal{E},b\in\mathds{R},r\geq 0
,
we have
\lim_{s\downarrow t_{n}}S_{s}=S_{t_{n}}
for
n\geq 0
. In other words,
\mathcal{S}
is càdlàg.

Next, for
t\in(t_{n},t_{n+1})

\displaystyle S_{t}-S_{t_{n}}

\displaystyle=\left(Y_{J_{n},S_{t_{n}},t_{n}}(t-t_{n})+B_{t}-B_{t_{n}}\right)-S_{t_{n}}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t-t_{n}}\mu_{J_{n}}^{*}\left(u+t_{n},Y_{J_{n},S_{t_{n}},t_{n}}(u)+B_{u+t_{n}}-B_{t_{n}}\right)\mathrm{d}u+B_{t}-B_{t_{n}}

\displaystyle=\int_{t_{n}}^{t}\mu^{*}_{J_{n}}\left(s,Y_{J_{n},S_{t_{n}},t_{n}}(u-t_{n})+B_{u}-B_{t_{n}}\right)\mathrm{d}u+B_{t}-B_{t_{n}}

\displaystyle=\int_{t_{n}}^{t}\mu_{J_{n}}^{*}(u,S_{u})\mathrm{d}u+B_{t}-B_{t_{n}}.

Thus,
\mathcal{S}
indeed evolves according to (
3.2
) in its continuity points. Since for
n\geq 1

\displaystyle S_{t_{n}}-S_{t_{n}^{-}}=h_{J_{n}}\left(t_{n},h_{J_{n-1}}^{-1}(t_{n},S_{t_{n}^{-}})\right)-S_{t_{n}^{-}},

the discontinuities of
\mathcal{S}
, too, coincide with those given in (
3.2
).

Finally, the uniqueness of
\mathcal{S}
follows by the uniqueness of
\mathcal{Y}_{i,b,r}
for
i\in\mathcal{E},b\in\mathds{R},r\geq 0
, and by the fact that each point of discontinuity, say
S_{t_{n}}-S_{t_{n}^{-}}
, is completely determined by
\mathcal{J}
and
\{S_{t}:t\in[0,t_{n})\}
.
∎

Note that the Lamperti transformation introduced in Theorem
3.1
can be applied to solutions of general stochastic differential equations driven by continuous processes that are not standard Brownian motion. In that case, the pathwise construction above can be generalised to build, and show the uniqueness of, those solutions. In fact, this is what we shall need in the next section.

4
Rate of convergence for transformed processes

Consider a Wong-Zakai approximation to
\mathcal{X}
, denoted by
\mathcal{X}^{\lambda}
, as given in Eqn (

).

Proposition 4.1
.

The process
\mathcal{X}^{\lambda}
defined by (

) is the unique solution of

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}X_{t}^{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\left(\mu_{J_{t}}(t,X_{t}^{\lambda})-\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{J_{t}}(t,X^{\lambda}_{t})\partial_{2}\left[\sigma_{J_{t}}(t,X^{\lambda}_{t})\right]\right)\mathrm{d}t+\sigma_{J_{t}}(t,X_{t}^{\lambda})\mathrm{d}F_{t}^{\lambda},

(4.1)

\displaystyle X_{0}^{\lambda}

\displaystyle=x_{0}.

(4.2)

Proof.

By Assumption
1
,
\sigma_{i}\partial_{2}[\sigma_{i}]
is a product of two locally Lipschitz functions, so it is itself locally Lipschitz. Furthermore,

\sigma_{i}\partial_{2}[\sigma_{i}]
is the product of a bounded function and one with linear growth, so that
\sigma_{i}\partial_{2}[\sigma_{i}]
has linear growth. This implies uniqueness of the solution to the RSSDE (
4.1
).
∎

To show strong convergence from
\mathcal{X}^{\lambda}
to
\mathcal{X}
, we first apply the Lamperti transformation given in Theorem
3.1
to
\mathcal{X}^{\lambda}
,
\mathcal{L}^{\lambda}=\{L^{\lambda}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}=\{h_{J_{t}}(t,X^{\lambda}_{t})\}_{t\geq 0}
, which satisfies the SDE

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}L^{\lambda}_{t}

\displaystyle=\mu^{*}_{J_{t}}(t,L^{\lambda}_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{d}F^{\lambda}_{t}+\sum_{s\leq t:J_{s}\neq J_{s^{-}}}\left(h_{J_{s}}\left(s,h^{-1}_{J_{s^{-}}}(s,L^{\lambda}_{s^{-}})\right)-L^{\lambda}_{s^{-}}\right),

(4.3)

\displaystyle L^{\lambda}_{0}

\displaystyle=h_{J_{0}}(0,x_{0}).

By similar arguments to those in Section
3.2
, we have that the unique solution to the above SDE is satisfied by
\mathcal{S}^{\lambda}
constructed as follows. Let
\mathcal{Y}^{\lambda}_{i,b,r}=\{Y^{\lambda}_{i,b,r}(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
be given by

\displaystyle Y^{\lambda}_{i,b,r}(t)

\displaystyle:=b+\int_{0}^{t}\mu_{i}^{*}\left(r+u,Y^{\lambda}_{i,b,r}(u)+F^{\lambda}_{r+u}-F^{\lambda}_{r}\right)\mathrm{d}u.

(4.4)

Next, define
\mathcal{S}^{\lambda}=\{S^{\lambda}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
, with

, as follows

\displaystyle S^{\lambda}_{t}

\displaystyle:=Y^{\lambda}_{J_{n},S^{\lambda}_{t_{n}},t_{n}}(t-t_{n})+F^{\lambda}_{t}-F^{\lambda}_{t_{n}}\quad\mbox{for }t\in(t_{n},t_{n+1}),n\geq 0,

(4.5)

\displaystyle S_{t_{n}}^{\lambda}

\displaystyle:=h_{J_{n}}\left(t_{n},h_{J_{n-1}}^{-1}\left(t_{n},S^{\lambda}_{t_{n}^{-}}\right)\right),\quad n\geq 1.

(4.6)

Because of uniqueness,
\mathcal{S}
is the same as
\mathcal{L}
, and similarly
\mathcal{S}^{\lambda}
as
\mathcal{L}^{\lambda}
. To emphasize their pathwise constructions, we use the notation

and

for the rest of the paper. Here, we assess the rate of strong convergence of

to
\mathcal{S}
; in Section

to
\mathcal{L}
.

To prove that

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|S_{t}-S^{\lambda}_{t}\right|=0\quad\mbox{ a.s. }\quad\mbox{ for all }T>0,

and to compute its rate of convergence, without loss of generality we assume

.

4.1
Two additional assumptions

In addition to Assumptions
1
and
2
, we need the following:

Assumption 3
.

For
i\in\mathcal{E}
, the function
\mu_{i}^{*}
defined in (
3.3
) is Lipschitz continuous on its second entry:

\displaystyle\Big|\mu_{i}^{*}(t,x_{1})-\mu_{i}^{*}(t,x_{2})\Big|

\displaystyle\leq M^{*}_{i}|x_{1}-x_{2}|\quad\forall t\geq 0,x\in\mathds{R}.

The following is an important special case for which Assumption
3
holds, and shows that the time–inhomogeneous case is thus not a “direct” extension of the time–homogeneous case, in the sense that some extra conditions may be needed.

Lemma 4.2
.

If
\mu_{i}/\sigma_{i}
and
\partial_{2}[\sigma_{i}]
are Lipschitz continuous on its second entry, then
\mu_{i}^{*}
is Lipschitz continuous on its second entry.

Proof.

In the case
\mu_{i}/\sigma_{i}
and
\partial_{2}[\sigma_{i}]
are Lipschitz continuous on its second entry, then
\mu^{*}
is formed by sums and second-entry compositions of functions which are Lipschitz continuous on its second entry, implying that
\mu^{*}
is Lipschitz continuous on its second entry.
∎

From here on, we assume Assumption
3
, not necessarily in the context of Lemma
4.2
. Next, define

\displaystyle N:=\#\{s\in(0,1]:J_{s}\neq J_{s^{-}}\},

(4.7)

the number of jumps of
\mathcal{J}
on the interval
(0,1]
. We also assume the following.

Assumption 4
.

There exists some
\gamma_{0}>0
such that
\mathds{P}(N>n)=o\left(e^{-n(\log n-\gamma_{0})}\right)
.

Assumption
4
trivially holds if
\mathcal{J}
is deterministic. The following lemma shows how Assumption
4
arises naturally in common scenarios for random jump processes.

Lemma 4.3
.

Suppose that
N
is stochastically dominated by
N^{*}\sim
Poisson
(c)
, where
c<\infty
. Then, Assumption
4
holds with
\gamma_{0}=\log c+1
.

Proof.

By definition, stochastic domination means
\mathds{P}(N\geq n)\leq\mathds{P}(N^{*}\geq n)
for
n\geq 0
. The asymptotic tail behaviour of Poisson distribution
[
9
, Cor. 1]
implies that
\mathds{P}(N^{*}\geq n)=O\left(c^{n}/n!\right)
. By Stirling’s formula
[
7
, p. 52]
,

\mathds{P}(N^{*}\geq n)=O\left(e^{n\log c-(n+1/2)\log n+n}\right)=O\left(n^{-1/2}e^{-n(\log n-(\log c+1))}\right),

which completes the proof.
∎

Lemma
4.3
implies that Assumption
4
holds whenever
\mathcal{J}
has a bounded jump intensity. This follows by choosing a sufficiently large
c>0
such that each holding time of
\mathcal{J}
stochastically dominates an exponential variable with rate
c
, implying that
N^{*}\sim\mbox{Poisson}(c)
stochastically dominates
N
. Thus, Lemma
4.3
allows us to work with jump processes that are not necessarily time–homogeneous Markovian, such as time–inhomogeneous Markovian or semi–Markovian processes.

4.2
From
\mathcal{S}^{\lambda}
to
\mathcal{S}

We write
\|\mathcal{U}\|_{\infty}
to denote
\sup_{t\in[0,1]}|U_{t}|
for a process
\mathcal{U}=\{U_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}\in\mathcal{D}(0,\infty)
.
The following lemma bounds the difference between two processes
\mathcal{Y}^{\lambda}_{i,a,r}
and
\mathcal{Y}_{i,b,r}
over the time interval
[0,1]
, given that the two processes have different starting points.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
a,b\in\mathds{R}
and
r\in[0,1]
. Then, for all
i\in\mathcal{E}
,

\displaystyle\Big|Y^{\lambda}_{i,a,r}(t)-Y_{i,b,r}(t)\Big|\leq K_{1}\left(\|\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}-\mathcal{B}\|_{\infty}+|a-b|\right)\quad\forall t\in[0,1-r],

(4.8)

where
K_{1}:=(2\overline{M}\vee 1)\exp(\overline{M})
and
\overline{M}:=\max_{i\in\mathcal{E}}M^{*}_{i}
.

Proof.

For
t\in[0,1-r]
,

\displaystyle\Big|Y^{\lambda}_{i,a,r}(t)-Y_{i,b,r}(t)\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{t}\Big|\mu_{i}^{*}\left(r+s,Y^{\lambda}_{i,a,r}(s)+F^{\lambda}_{r+s}-F^{\lambda}_{r}\right)-\mu_{i}^{*}\left(r+s,Y^{\lambda}_{i,b,r}(s)+B_{r+s}-B_{r}\right)\Big|\mathrm{d}s+|a-b|

\displaystyle\leq\overline{M}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\left|Y^{\lambda}_{i,a,r}(s)-Y_{i,b,r}(s)\right|+\left|\left(F^{\lambda}_{r+s}-F^{\lambda}_{r}\right)-\left(B_{r+s}-B_{r}\right)\right|\mathrm{d}s\right)+|a-b|

\displaystyle\leq\overline{M}\int_{0}^{t}\left|Y^{\lambda}_{i,a,r}(s)-Y_{i,b,r}(s)\right|\mathrm{d}s+2\overline{M}\|\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}-\mathcal{B}\|_{\infty}+|a-b|.

Gronwall’s Inequality implies

\displaystyle\Big|Y^{\lambda}_{i,a,r}(t)-Y_{i,b,r}(t)\Big|\leq\left(2\overline{M}\|\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}-\mathcal{B}\|_{\infty}+|a-b|\right)\exp(\overline{M}).

Take
K_{1}:=(2\overline{M}\vee 1)\exp(\overline{M})
and (
4.8
) follows.
∎

In general,
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{S}^{\lambda}
do not coincide at jump epochs
\{t_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
; Lemma
4.4
helps bounding the error
accumulated
at each jump time up to
T=1
, as it is shown next.

Theorem 4.5
.

On the event
\{N=u\}
, for
u\in\mathds{N}
,

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{S}^{\lambda}-\mathcal{S}\|_{\infty}\leq K_{2}K_{3}^{u}\|\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}-\mathcal{B}\|_{\infty},

(4.9)

where
N
is the number of jumps of
\mathcal{J}
on the time interval
[0,1]
given by (
4.7
), and
K_{2}:=K_{3}(2+K_{1})/(K_{3}-1)
and
K_{3}:=K_{1}V/v
.

Proof.

Let
n\in\{0,1,\dots,u\}
and fix
t\in[t_{n},t_{n+1}\wedge 1)
. Then,

\displaystyle\left|S^{\lambda}_{t}-S_{t}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|Y^{\lambda}_{J_{n},S^{\lambda}_{t_{n}},t_{n}}(t-t_{n})+F^{\lambda}_{t}-F^{\lambda}_{t_{n}}-\left(Y_{J_{n},S_{t_{n}},t_{n}}(t-t_{n})+B_{t}-B_{t_{n}}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|Y^{\lambda}_{J_{n},S^{\lambda}_{t_{n}},t_{n}}(t-t_{n})-Y_{J_{n},S_{t_{n}},t_{n}}(t-t_{n})\right|+2\|\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}-\mathcal{B}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq K_{1}\left(\|\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}-\mathcal{B}\|_{\infty}+\left|S^{\lambda}_{t_{n}}-S_{t_{n}}\right|\right)+2\|\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}-\mathcal{B}\|_{\infty},

(4.10)

by Lemma
4.4
. By definition,

\displaystyle\left|S^{\lambda}_{t_{n}}-S_{t_{n}}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|h_{J_{n}}\left(t_{n},h_{J_{n-1}}^{-1}(t_{n},S^{\lambda}_{t_{n}^{-}})\right)-h_{J_{n}}\left(t_{n},h_{J_{n-1}}^{-1}(t_{n},S_{t_{n}^{-}})\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{v}\left|h_{J_{n-1}}^{-1}\big(t_{n},S^{\lambda}_{t_{n}^{-}}\big)-h_{J_{n-1}}^{-1}\left(t_{n},S_{t_{n}^{-}}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{V}{v}\left|S^{\lambda}_{t_{n}^{-}}-S_{t_{n}^{-}}\right|,

(4.11)

where for the last two inequalities we used the Lipschitz continuity on the second entry of
h_{i}
and
h_{i}^{-1}
, respectively, which is proven in Lemma
7.1
(i).

Let
\rho:=K_{1}V/v
. Substituting (

) into (
4.10
), applying the same steps recursively for other jump epochs, and using
S^{\lambda}_{0}-S_{0}=0
, we have

\displaystyle\left|S^{\lambda}_{t}-{S}_{t}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=0}^{n}\rho^{k}(2+K_{1})\|\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}-\mathcal{B}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho^{u+1}-1}{\rho-1}(2+K_{1})\|\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}-\mathcal{B}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\left[\frac{\rho(2+K_{1})}{\rho-1}\right]\rho^{u}\|\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}-\mathcal{B}\|_{\infty},

so that (

) follows.
∎

Theorem
4.5
implies that the error bound increases geometrically with respect to the number of jump occurrences in
[0,1]
. In general this would be problematic because the error keeps building up with each jump of
\mathcal{J}
—this is the main challenge when dealing with regime switching. However, as will be seen in Theorem
4.6
, if the tail distribution of
N
is
light enough
, the error bound given by (

) will still be controllable. Recall that Assumption
4
gives the property of the tail distribution being light enough.

Now we are ready to assess the rate of convergence of
\mathcal{S}^{\lambda}
to
\mathcal{S}
.

Theorem 4.6
.

Under Assumptions
1
–
4
, suppose that there exists
\delta:\mathds{R}_{+}\mapsto\mathds{R}_{+}
with
\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}\delta(\lambda)=0

\displaystyle\mathds{P}\left(\|\mathcal{S}^{\lambda}-\mathcal{S}\|_{\infty}\geq\beta\delta(\lambda)\lambda^{\varepsilon}\right)=o\left(\lambda^{-q}\right).

(4.12)

Theorem
4.6
implies that under Assumptions
1
–
4
, as
\lambda\rightarrow\infty
the unique solution

of the regime-switching, time-inhomogeneous SDE (

Proof.

We will determine the value of
\beta=\beta(q)

f_{\gamma_{0},q}:=\exp(x(\log x-\gamma)/q)
and let
A_{\lambda}:=f_{\gamma_{0},q}^{-1}(\lambda)
, where
\gamma_{0}
is from Assumption
4
. Let
W
denote
the Lambert

z\mapsto ze^{z}
. By Lemma
7.9
in the Appendix,

\displaystyle A_{\lambda}=e^{\gamma_{0}}\exp\left\{W(qe^{-\gamma_{0}}\log\lambda)\right\},

which implies
e^{A_{\lambda}((\log A_{\lambda})-\gamma_{0})/q}=\lambda
. By Assumption

,

\displaystyle\mathds{P}(N>A_{\lambda})=o\left(e^{-A_{\lambda}((\log A_{\lambda})-\gamma_{0})}\right)=o(\lambda^{-q}).

(4.13)

Next, fix
\varepsilon>0
. By Theorem

,

\displaystyle\mathds{P}

\displaystyle\left(\|\mathcal{S}^{\lambda}-\mathcal{S}\|_{\infty}\geq\beta\delta(\lambda)\lambda^{\varepsilon}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathds{P}\left(N\leq A_{\lambda},\;\|\mathcal{S}^{\lambda}-\mathcal{S}\|_{\infty}\geq\beta\delta(\lambda)\lambda^{\varepsilon}\right)+\mathds{P}\left(N>A_{\lambda}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathds{P}\left(K_{2}K_{3}^{A_{\lambda}}\|\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}-\mathcal{B}\|_{\infty}\geq\beta\delta(\lambda)\lambda^{\varepsilon}\right)+\mathds{P}\left(N>A_{\lambda}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathds{P}\left(K_{2}K_{3}^{A_{\lambda}}\lambda^{-\varepsilon}\|\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}-\mathcal{B}\|_{\infty}\geq\beta\delta(\lambda)\right)+\mathds{P}\left(N>A_{\lambda}\right).

Note that

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}K_{3}^{A_{\lambda}}\lambda^{-\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}\exp\left(A_{\lambda}\log K_{3}-\varepsilon\log\lambda\right).

(4.15)

By the asymptotic decomposition of Lambert W functions

as

we obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}A_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}e^{\gamma_{0}}\exp\left\{\log\left(qe^{-\gamma_{0}}\log\lambda\right)-\log\left(\log\left(qe^{-\gamma_{0}}\log\lambda\right)\right)+o(1)\right\}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}e^{\gamma_{0}}\frac{qe^{-\gamma_{0}}\log\lambda}{\log\left(qe^{-\gamma_{0}}\log\lambda\right)}.

(4.16)

Substituting (
4.16
) into (
4.15
) gives

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}

\displaystyle K_{3}^{A_{\lambda}}\lambda^{-\varepsilon}=\exp\left(e^{\gamma_{0}}\log K_{3}\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}\left\{\frac{q\log K_{3}}{\log\left(qe^{-\gamma_{0}}\log\lambda\right)}-\varepsilon\right\}\log\lambda\right)=0.

Let
\lambda_{0}=\lambda_{0}(\varepsilon)>0
be such that
K_{3}^{A_{\lambda}}\lambda^{-\varepsilon}\leq 1
for all
\lambda\geq\lambda_{0}
. Take
\beta:=K_{2}K_{1}\alpha
, then for all
\lambda\geq\lambda_{0}
,

\displaystyle\mathds{P}\left(K_{2}K_{3}^{A_{\lambda}}\lambda^{-\varepsilon}\|\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}-\mathcal{B}\|_{\infty}\geq\beta\delta(\lambda)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathds{P}\left(\|\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}-\mathcal{B}\|_{\infty}\geq\alpha\delta(\lambda)\right)

\displaystyle=o(\lambda^{-q})\quad\mbox{as}\quad\lambda\rightarrow\infty,

and so by (
4.13
) and (
4.14
) we complete the proof.
∎

5
Rate of strong convergence from
\mathcal{X}^{\lambda}
to
\mathcal{X}

Now that we have obtained a rate of convergence of
\|\mathcal{S}^{\lambda}-\mathcal{S}\|_{\infty}
, we return to the original setting: approximating the solution
\mathcal{X}
to the regime-switching time-inhomogeneous stochastic differential equation (
1.1
).

In the following we assess the distance between
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{X}^{\lambda}
of (
4.1
) in terms of the distance between their respective transformations,
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{S}^{\lambda}
.

Proposition 5.1
.

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{X}^{\lambda}-\mathcal{X}\|_{\infty}\leq V\|\mathcal{S}^{\lambda}-\mathcal{S}\|_{\infty},

(5.1)

where
V
is the uniform upper bound for
\sigma_{i}
,
i\in\mathcal{E}
.

Proof.

As
X_{t}=h_{J_{t}}^{-1}(t,S_{t})
and
X_{t}^{\lambda}=h_{J_{t}}^{-1}(t,S^{\lambda}_{t})
, Inequality (
5.1
) follows from the Lipschitz continuity on the second entry of
h_{i}
, as shown in Lemma
7.1
(i).
∎

Theorem
1.1
then follows directly from Theorems
4.6
and Proposition
5.1
.

Remark 5.2
.

Even though Theorem
4.6
and Proposition
5.1
concern the case
T=1
, their generalization to arbitrary
T>0
is straightforward and thus Theorem
1.1
indeed follows.

6
Special case: Markov-modulated Brownian motion process

Here, we focus on the special case where the finite–variation process
\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}=\{F^{\lambda}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
is a
uniform transport process

[
11
]
(also known as the
telegraph process

[
15
]
or
flip-flop process

[
17
]
):

F^{\lambda}_{t}:=\sqrt{\lambda}\int_{0}^{t}(-1)^{M_{s}^{\lambda}}\mathrm{d}s,

where
\{M_{t}^{\lambda}\}_{t\geq 0}
is a Poisson process of intensity
\lambda>0
. Through two different constructions, Gorostiza and Griego
[
10
]
and Nguyen and Peralta
[
21
]
showed that there exists a probability space on which
\mathcal{F}^{\lambda}
converges strongly to
\mathcal{B}
, at rates
\delta_{1}(\lambda)=\lambda^{-1/4}(\log\lambda)^{5/2}
and
\delta_{2}(\lambda)=\lambda^{-1/4}\log\lambda
, respectively.

Now, for
i\in\mathcal{E}
, let
\mu_{i}
and
\sigma_{i}
be fixed constants. Then,
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{X}^{\lambda}
respectively take the form

\displaystyle X_{t}

\displaystyle=x_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\mu_{J_{s}}\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{J_{s}}\mathrm{d}B_{s},\quad\mbox{and}

\displaystyle X_{t}^{\lambda}

\displaystyle=x_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\mu_{J_{s}}\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{J_{s}}\mathrm{d}F_{s}^{\lambda}.

When
\mathcal{J}
is a time-homogeneous Markovian jump process independent of
\mathcal{B}
, the process
\mathcal{X}
is called a
Markov-modulated Brownian motion
. In this context, Latouche and Nguyen
[
17
]
proved that
\mathcal{X}^{\lambda}
converges weakly to
\mathcal{X}
. Theorem
1.1
, together with Theorem 3.1 in
[
20
]
, implies that such convergence also holds in a strong sense.

Significantly, Theorem
1.1
is applicable in much more general settings, not only in terms of the drift and diffusion coefficients but also in terms of the jump process
\mathcal{J}
; the latter can be, for example,
time–inhomogeneous
or
semi–Markov
.

7.1
Properties of the Lamperti transformation

The following are some standalone results needed throughout the main text.

Lemma 7.1
.

Suppose Assumptions
1
and
2
hold. Then, for
i\in\mathcal{E}
,

(i)

h_{i}
,
h_{i}^{-1}
and
\partial_{1}[h_{i}]
are Lipschitz continuous on their second entry,

(ii)

h_{i}

is locally Lipschitz continuous and of linear growth,

(iii)

h^{-1}

is locally Lipschitz continuous and of linear growth, where
h^{-1}(t,y):=x
such that
h(t,x)=y
.

(iv)

\partial_{1}[h_{i}]
is locally Lipschitz continuous and of linear growth,

(v)

\mu_{i}/\sigma_{i}
is locally Lipschitz continuous and of linear growth.

Proof.

(i)
By Assumption 2(i), there exist
V<\infty,v>0
such that
v<\sigma_{i}<V
.
Then,

\displaystyle\Big|h_{i}(t,x_{1})-h_{i}(t,x_{2})\Big|=\left|\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}(t,y)}\mathrm{d}y\right|\leq\frac{1}{v}|x_{1}-x_{2}|,\quad t\geq 0,\,x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathds{R},

which implies
h_{i}
is Lipschitz continuous on its second entry.

Next,

\displaystyle\Big|h_{i}(t,x_{1})-h_{i}(t,x_{2})\Big|=\left|\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}(t,y)}\mathrm{d}y\right|\geq\frac{1}{V}|x_{1}-x_{2}|,\quad t\geq 0,\,x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathds{R}.

(7.1)

Substituting
x_{1}:=h_{i}^{-1}(t,y_{1})
and
x_{2}:=h_{i}^{-1}(t,y_{2})
into (
7.1
) implies that

\displaystyle\Big|h^{-1}_{i}(t,y_{1})-h^{-1}_{i}(t,y_{2})\Big|\leq V|y_{1}-y_{2}|,\quad t\geq 0,\,y_{1},\,y_{2}\in\mathds{R},

meaning
h_{i}^{-1}
is Lipschitz continuous on its second entry.

By Assumption
2
(ii),

\displaystyle\Big|\partial_{1}\left[h_{i}(t,x_{1})\right]-\partial_{1}\left[h_{i}(t,x_{2})\right]\Big|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}\frac{\partial_{1}\left[\sigma_{i}(t,y)\right]}{\sigma_{i}^{2}(t,y)}\mathrm{d}y\right|

\displaystyle\leq K|x_{1}-x_{2}|,\quad t\geq 0,\,x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathds{R},

(7.2)

implying that
\partial_{1}\left[h_{i}\right]
is Lipschitz continuous on its second entry.

For Parts (ii)–(v), without loss of generality we focus on a compact set
\mathcal{K}=[t_{1},t_{2}]\times[y_{1},y_{2}]
, where
0\leq t_{1}\leq t_{2}
,
y_{1}\leq x_{0}\leq y_{2}
, and let
(s_{1},x_{1}),(s_{2},x_{2})\in\mathcal{K}
.

(ii)
As
h_{i}
is Lipschitz continuous on its second entry,

\displaystyle\Big|h_{i}(s_{1},x_{1})-h_{i}(s_{2},x_{2})\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\Big|h_{i}(s_{1},x_{1})-h_{i}(s_{1},x_{2})\Big|+\Big|h_{i}(s_{1},x_{2})-h_{i}(s_{2},x_{2})\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{v}|x_{1}-x_{2}|+\Big|h_{i}(s_{1},x_{2})-h_{i}(s_{2},x_{2})\Big|.

Since
|\partial_{1}[\sigma_{i}]|/\sigma_{i}^{2}\leq K
by Assumption
2
(ii),

\displaystyle\Big|\partial_{1}[h_{i}(t,x)]\Big|=\left|\int_{x_{0}}^{x}\frac{\partial_{1}[\sigma_{i}(t,y)]}{\sigma_{i}^{2}(t,y)}\mathrm{d}y\right|\leq K|x-x_{0}|.

(7.3)

The mean value theorem gives
|h_{i}(s_{1},x_{2})-h_{i}(s_{2},x_{2})|\leq K|x_{2}-x_{0}||s_{1}-s_{2}|,

which leads to

\displaystyle\Big|h_{i}(s_{1},x_{1})-h_{i}(s_{2},x_{2})\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{v}|x_{1}-x_{2}|+K|x_{2}-x_{0}||s_{1}-s_{2}|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{v}|x_{1}-x_{2}|+K|y_{1}-y_{2}||s_{1}-s_{2}|

(7.4)

and thus
h_{i}
is locally Lipschitz. The Lipschitz continuity on the second entry of
h_{i}
implies that

\Big|h_{i}(t,x)\Big|\leq\Big|h_{i}(t,x_{0})\Big|+\frac{1}{v}|x-x_{0}|=\frac{1}{v}|x-x_{0}|,

so that
h_{i}
has linear growth.

(iii)
We have

\displaystyle\Big|h_{i}^{-1}(s_{1},x_{1})-h_{i}^{-1}(s_{2},x_{2})\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\Big|h_{i}^{-1}(s_{1},x_{1})-h_{i}^{-1}(s_{1},x_{2})\Big|+\Big|h_{i}^{-1}(s_{1},x_{2})-h_{i}^{-1}(s_{2},x_{2})\Big|

\displaystyle\leq V\left|x_{1}-x_{2}\right|+\Big|h_{i}^{-1}(s_{1},x_{2})-h_{i}^{-1}(s_{2},x_{2})\Big|.

(7.5)

Note that
h_{i}^{-1}(t,0)=x_{0}
; furthermore,

\displaystyle\partial_{2}\left[h_{i}^{-1}(t,x)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\partial_{2}\left[h_{i}\left(t,h_{i}^{-1}(t,x)\right)\right]}=\sigma_{i}\left(t,h_{i}^{-1}(t,x)\right),

Thus,

\displaystyle h_{i}^{-1}(t,x)=x_{0}+\int_{0}^{x}\sigma_{i}\left(t,h_{i}^{-1}(t,y)\right)\mathrm{d}y.

(7.6)

Without loss of generality, suppose that
x_{2}\geq 0
. Then

\displaystyle\Big|h_{i}^{-1}(s_{1},x_{2})-h_{i}^{-1}(s_{2},x_{2})\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{x_{2}}\Big|\sigma_{i}\left(s_{1},h_{i}^{-1}(s_{1},y)\right)-\sigma_{i}\left(s_{2},h_{i}^{-1}(s_{2},y)\right)\Big|\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{x_{2}}G_{\mathcal{K}}\left(\left|s_{1}-s_{2}\right|+\Big|h_{i}^{-1}(s_{1},y)-h_{i}^{-1}(s_{2},y)\Big|\right)\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leq G_{\mathcal{K}}x_{2}|s_{1}-s_{2}|+G_{\mathcal{K}}\int_{0}^{x_{2}}\Big|h_{i}^{-1}(s_{1},y)-h_{i}^{-1}(s_{2},y)\Big|\mathrm{d}y,

where, by Assumption
1
(ii),
G_{\mathcal{K}}
is the local Lipschitz constant of
\sigma_{i}
on
\mathcal{K}
.

Gronwall’s Inequality implies that

\displaystyle\Big|h_{i}^{-1}(s_{1},x_{2})-h_{i}^{-1}(s_{2},x_{2})\Big|\leq G_{\mathcal{K}}x_{2}|s_{1}-s_{2}|\exp(G_{\mathcal{K}}x_{2}).

(7.7)

Substituting (
7.7
) into (
7.5
) gives

\displaystyle\Big|h_{i}^{-1}(s_{1},x_{1})-h_{i}^{-1}(s_{2},x_{2})\Big|

\displaystyle\leq V|x_{1}-x_{2}|+G_{\mathcal{K}}x_{2}|s_{1}-s_{2}|\exp(G_{\mathcal{K}}x_{2})

\displaystyle\leq V|x_{1}-x_{2}|+G_{\mathcal{K}}y_{2}|s_{1}-s_{2}|\exp(G_{\mathcal{K}}y_{2})

(7.8)

and so
h_{i}^{-1}
is locally Lipschitz continuous.

The linear growth of
h_{i}^{-1}
is implied by the inequality
\Big|h_{i}^{-1}(t,x)\Big|\leq|x_{0}|+V|x|.

(iv)
Note that, by (
7.2
),

\displaystyle\Big|\partial_{1}\left[h_{i}(s_{1},x_{1})\right]-\partial_{1}\left[h_{i}(s_{2},x_{2})\right]\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\Big|\partial_{1}\left[h_{i}(s_{1},x_{1})\right]-\partial_{1}\left[h_{i}(s_{1},x_{2})\right]\Big|+\Big|\partial_{1}\left[h_{i}(s_{1},x_{2})\right]-\partial_{1}\left[h_{i}(s_{2},x_{2})\right]\Big|

\displaystyle\leq K|x_{1}-x_{2}|+\Big|\partial_{1}\left[h_{i}(s_{1},x_{2})\right]-\partial_{1}\left[h_{i}(s_{2},x_{2})\right]\Big|.

As
v\leq\sigma_{i}\leq V
and
\sigma_{i}
is locally Lipschitz,
1/\sigma_{i}
is also locally Lipschitz. Since the product of locally Lipschitz is locally Lipschitz, by Assumption
1
(ii, iii) the function
\partial_{1}[\sigma_{i}]/\sigma_{i}^{2}
, too, is locally Lipschitz. This implies the existence of
Q_{\mathcal{K}}>0
such that

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\partial_{1}\left[\sigma_{i}(s_{1},x_{1})\right]}{\sigma_{i}^{2}(s_{1},x_{1})}-\frac{\partial_{1}\left[\sigma_{i}(s_{1},x_{1})\right]}{\sigma_{i}^{2}(s_{1},x_{1})}\right|\leq Q_{\mathcal{K}}\left(|s_{1}-s_{2}|+|x_{1}-x_{2}|\right)

Thus,

\displaystyle\Big|\partial_{1}\left[h_{i}(s_{1},x_{2})\right]-\partial_{1}\left[h_{i}(s_{2},x_{2})\right]\Big|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{x_{0}}^{x_{2}}\left\{\frac{\partial_{1}[\sigma_{i}(s_{1},y)]}{\sigma_{i}^{2}(s_{1},y)}-\frac{\partial_{1}[\sigma_{i}(s_{2},y)]}{\sigma_{i}^{2}(s_{2},y)}\right\}\mathrm{d}y\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\int_{x_{0}}^{x_{2}}Q_{\mathcal{K}}|s_{1}-s_{2}|\mathrm{d}y\right|

\displaystyle=Q_{\mathcal{K}}|x_{0}-x_{2}||s_{1}-s_{2}|

\displaystyle\leq Q_{\mathcal{K}}(y_{2}-y_{1})|s_{1}-s_{2}|.

Thus,

\displaystyle\Big|\partial_{1}\left[h_{i}(s_{1},x_{1})\right]-\partial_{1}\left[h_{i}(s_{2},x_{2})\right]\Big|

\displaystyle\leq K|x_{1}-x_{2}|+Q_{\mathcal{K}}(y_{2}-y_{1})|s_{1}-s_{2}|,

which means
\partial_{1}h_{i}
is locally Lipschitz continuous. The linear growth condition follows from (
7.3
).

(v)
As
\mu_{i}/\sigma_{i}
is the product of two locally Lipschitz continuous functions, it is locally Lipschitz. Its linear growth follows from the linear growth of
\mu_{i}
and the boundedness of
\sigma_{i}
.
∎

7.2
Inverse of function
f_{\gamma,q}

Lemma 7.2
.

For
\gamma,q>0
, let
f_{\gamma,q}:[e^{\gamma},\infty)\mapsto[1,\infty)
be defined by

\displaystyle f_{\gamma,q}(x):=\exp(x(\log x-\gamma)/q).

(7.9)

Then
f_{\gamma,q}
is bijective with inverse given by

\displaystyle f_{\gamma,q}^{-1}(y)=e^{\gamma}\exp\left(W(qe^{-\gamma}\log y)\right),

(7.10)

where
W
denotes
the Lambert
W
function
, the inverse of the mapping
z\mapsto ze^{z}
.

Proof.

The bijectivity of
f_{\gamma,q}
follows from the fact that it is strictly increasing, with
\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}f_{\gamma,q}(x)=\infty
. Let
y
be in the image of
f_{\gamma,q}
, so that it is of the form

y=\exp(x(\log x-\gamma)/q)\quad\mbox{for some }x\geq e^{\gamma}.

Taking the log of both sides and multiplying
qe^{-\gamma}
across gives

\displaystyle qe^{-\gamma}\log y=e^{-\gamma}x(\log x-\gamma).

(7.11)

Note that
e^{-\gamma}x(\log x-\gamma)=e^{-\gamma}x(\log x+\log(e^{-\gamma}))=\log(xe^{-\gamma})e^{\log(xe^{-\gamma})}
. Let
z:=\log(xe^{-\gamma})
, we have that (
7.11
) is equivalent to
qe^{-\gamma}\log y=ze^{z}.

Thus,

\displaystyle\log(xe^{-\gamma})=W\left(qe^{-\gamma}\log y\right).

(7.12)

Solving (
7.12
) for
x
gives (
7.10
).
∎
