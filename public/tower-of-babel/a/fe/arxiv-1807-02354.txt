The stepping stone model in a random environment and the effect of local heterogneities on isolation by distance patterns
Raphaël Forien

Abstract
We study a one-dimensional spatial population model where the population sizes of the subpopulations are chosen according to a translation invariant and ergodic distribution and are uniformly bounded away from 0 and infinity. We suppose that the frequencies of a particular genetic type in the colonies evolve according to a system of interacting diffusions, following the stepping stone model of Kimura. We show that, over large spatial and temporal scales, this model behaves like the solution to a stochastic heat equation with Wright-Fisher noise with constant coefficients. These coefficients are the effective diffusion rate of genes within the population and the effective local population density. We find that, in our model, the local heterogeneity leads to a slower effective diffusion rate and a larger effective population density than in a uniform population. Our proof relies on duality techniques, an invariance principle for reversible random walks in a random environment and a convergence result for a system of coalescing random walks in a random environment.

Introduction

Most species occupy a spatially extended habitat, where each individual produces some quantity of offspring which disperse around them.
If this dispersion is limited compared to the whole range of the population, spatial patterns of genetic diversity can build up over many generations, and individuals living far apart tend to be more genetically different than individuals living close to one another.

If sufficiently many individuals from different geographic locations are sampled and genotyped, one can estimate the parameters governing the dispersal and reproduction of individuals within the studied population
[
31
,
4
,
29
]
.
In particular, one is interested in the diffusion coefficient of genes inside the population, corresponding to the mean squared distance between parents and their offspring, and in the effective population density, which is the mean density of reproducing individuals per generation
[
3
]
.
This estimation relies mainly on the comparison of the observed geographical decrease of genetic relatedness to theoretical models of isolation by distance
[
36
,
25
,
21
]
.

To derive analytical formulae, these models often assume that the population density is uniform in space and constant in time
[
32
,
33
]
.
Natural populations, however, are hardly likely to satisfy this assumption, either because of external conditions (
e.g.
climate variations or fluctuating resources) or because of demographic fluctuations.
These heterogeneities are almost certain to affect the estimates of the demographic parameters, either increasing or decreasing the effective rate of diffusion and the effective population density.

In this paper, we investigate the effect of local heterogeneities in the population density on the large scale genetic composition of the population and on the corresponding demographic parameters.
We assume that the population density varies in space according to a translation invariant distribution and that it is constant in time.
In our model, we find that these heterogeneities lead to a slower diffusion of genes and a larger effective population density than in a population with uniform density.
According to this, significant local heterogeneities could lead to an underestimation of the migration levels and an overestimation of the population densities in natural populations.

This result relies on a study of the stepping stone model introduced by Kimura in 1953
[
19
]
.
In this model, the population is divided into subpopulations, or demes, sitting at the vertices of a graph (
e.g.

\mathbb{Z}
or
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
), and in each generation, new individuals are chosen at random to form the new generation in each deme, some of which are issued from parents within the same deme while others descend from individuals in neighbouring demes.
If the number of individuals in each deme is large enough and if the migration probabilities are small enough, the evolution of the frequency of a given genetic type can be described by a system of interacting diffusions solving

\displaystyle dp_{t}(x)=\sum_{y\sim x}m_{xy}(p_{t}(y)-p_{t}(x))dt+\sqrt{\frac{1}{N(x)}p_{t}(x)(1-p_{t}(x))}dB^{x}_{t},

12

]

Kimura and Weiss

, followed by Sawyer
[

showed how the correlations in the genetic composition of demes decrease with the distance between them.
In particular, in a one-dimensional space, they showed that the correlation coefficient between the genetic composition of two demes separated by a distance

This model can also be extended to a (one-dimensional) continuous geographical space in the following way

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\bm{p}_{t}=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\partial_{xx}\bm{p}_{t}+\sqrt{\gamma\bm{p}_{t}(1-\bm{p}_{t})}\dot{W},

A related convergence result was proved for the long range voter model in

A corresponding convergence result regarding the underlying genealogies was also proved in

To study the impact of local heterogeneities in the population size, we consider a stepping stone model where the population sizes are drawn at random from a translation invariant and ergodic distribution and remain fixed in time.
We then suppose that, with probability
1-m
, individuals in deme
x
descend from an individual in the same deme, and with probability
m

\{x-1,x,x+1\}
.
The probability of having an ancestor in a neighbouring deme is thus proportional to the population size in this deme.
More precisely, an individual in deme
x
has its parent in deme
y
with probability

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\text{ if }\left|x-y\right|>1,

(4)

\displaystyle m\frac{N(y)}{N(x-1)+N(x)+N(x+1)}

\displaystyle\text{ if }\left|x-y\right|=1

\displaystyle 1-m\frac{N(x-1)+N(x+1)}{N(x-1)+N(x)+N(x-1)}

\displaystyle\text{ if }x=y.

We then define (see Definition
2

1

2

(
3

The two parameters

and

In particular, we find that (see Remark
5

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}\leq\frac{2}{3}m,

\displaystyle\gamma\leq\frac{1}{\langle N\rangle},

where

would be the expected diffusion coefficient if the population sizes were uniform.
As a result, local demographic heterogeneities appear to reduce the effective diffusion of genes within the population and to increase the effective population size.
This is due to the fact that, in our model, the ancestors of current individuals are more likely to have lived in more crowded demes, from which the diffusion is slower and in which the perceived population size is larger.

This bias should in particular be taken into account when estimating levels of gene flow and effective population sizes from large scale genetic patterns.
One should be careful, however, that these biases are a direct consequence of the choice made in (
4

7

The proof of the main result relies on a duality relation between the stepping stone model (
1
) and a system of coalescing random walks which describes the genealogy of a random sample of individuals in the population
[
12
,
34
]
.
In our model, this dual takes the form of a system of coalesing random walks in a random environment, whose jump and coalescence rates are both affected by the environment (the random walkers - or ancestral lineages - are more likely to jump to more crowded demes and coalesce more quickly if they meet in less crowded demes).

It turns out that these random walks admit a reversible measure on
\mathbb{Z}
. This allows us to prove an invariance principle for the trajectory of each of these random walks
[
20
,
9
,
23
,
8
]
.
To characterize the coalescence time of a pair of lineages, we introduce an auxiliary process which records the sequence of demes where the two lineages meet, and we show that this process also admits a reversible measure (which happens to be the square of the previous one, see Proposition
19
).
We thus show that the rescaled dual process converges to a system of Brownian motions which coalesce at a rate proportional to their local time together.
In turn, this system is known to be dual to a weak solution to equation (
3
), which allows us to uniquely characterise the possible limits of the rescaled stepping stone model.
We then conclude the proof of the main result by proving the tightness of this sequence.

Related models have been studied before, for example
[
16
]
introduce a system of interacting Wright-Fisher diffusions in a space-time random environment and study its long time behaviour.
Moreover, in
[
2
]
, the authors introduce a random walk on the
backbone of an oriented percolation cluster.
Given a realisation of a discrete-time contact process on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,
(\eta_{n},n\in\mathbb{Z})
, taking values in
\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
, they consider a random walk describing the position of the ancestor of a particle at time 0.
This random walk
(X_{n},n\geq 0)
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left.X_{n+1}=y\>\right|\>X_{n}=x,(\eta_{n},n\in\mathbb{Z})\right)\propto\eta_{-n-1}(y)\mathds{1}_{\left\{\|x-y\|=1\right\}}.

The authors then prove a law of large numbers and a quenched and annealed central limit theorem for this random walk in a dynamical random environment.
Their proof also covers the case were one puts (random) weights on the available sites in the contact process by replacing
(\eta_{n}(x),x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},n\in\mathbb{Z})
with
(\eta_{n}(x)K(x,n),x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},n\in\mathbb{Z})
with
(K(x,n),x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},n\in\mathbb{Z})

i.i.d.

\mathbb{N}^{*}
-valued random variables.
The random field
K
can then be seen as a carrying capacity which fluctuates in space and time, superimposed on the contact process
\eta
which determines the availability of sites.
This was then generalised by K. Miller in
[
27
]
, where
K
can be an arbitrary mixing field taking values in
(0,\infty)
.
Finally, Birkner et al.
[
1
]
extended this to more general settings where
\eta
is a stationary Markovian particle system whose evolution can be described by ‘local rules.’

Our setting is simpler than in these works in that we consider an environment (the deme sizes) which is fixed in time, and uniformly elliptic (
i.e.
bounded away from zero and infinity), but we are interested in the asymptotic behaviour of more than one ancestral lineage in the population.
For this we have to study the coalescence of pairs of random walks in a random environment.
This was done in a particular setting in
[
5
]
, where the authors consider a family of random walks following the model in
[
2
]
which coalesce whenever they find themselves in the same site.
They show that, adequately rescaled, the resulting process converges in distribution to the Brownian web (
i.e.
a family of Brownian motions which coalesce instantly upon meeting, see for example
[
14
]
or
[
35
]
).
Here, however, we explore the regime of so-called
delayed coalescence
, where lineages must spend a positive amount of time together before they can coalesce (see Theorem
15
).
This adds an additional dependence on the law of the environment in the limiting process since the rate of coalescence depends on the local population density.

Another related work is
[
15
]
, where a large population is divided in colonies labelled by the hierarchical group of order
N
, and migration and reproduction can take place within each hierarchical block, and where the rates of these events for each block is random.

The paper is laid out as follows.
In the first section, we define the stepping stone model in a random environment and state our main result, namely the convergence of the sequence of rescaled stepping stone models to a weak solution to equation (
3
).
In Section
2
, we define the dual of the stepping stone model in a random environment and we state the related convergence results, first for individual lineages and then for the whole dual process.
These results concerning the dual are then proved in Section
3
.
In Section
4
, we prove our main result, using estimates on the heat kernel associated to the random environment.
These estimates are proved in Appendix
A
.
Appendix
B
is then devoted to the proof of a local central limit theorem for the random walk in a random environment which appears in the dual process.

1
The stepping stone model in a random environment

1.1
Definition of the model

We define a model for the evolution of a population living in discrete colonies or demes located on the one-dimensional integer lattice
\mathbb{Z}
.
Moreover, we wish to draw the deme sizes (
i.e.
the effective number of individuals living in each colony) at random from some translation invariant, ergodic distribution.

For
x\in\mathbb{Z}
, let
T^{x}
be the translation operator acting on functions from
\mathbb{Z}
to
\mathbb{R}
defined by

\displaystyle T^{x}f(z)=f(x+z),\quad\forall z\in\mathbb{Z}.

Thus let
N=\{N(x),x\in\mathbb{Z}\}
be an
\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{Z}}
-valued random variable such that

i)

(translation invariance) for all
x\in\mathbb{Z}
,
T^{x}N\overset{d}{=}N
, where
\overset{d}{=}
stands for equality in distribution,

ii)

(ergodicity) for all
f:\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{Z}}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
T^{x}f=f
for all
x\in\mathbb{Z}
,
f(N)
is deterministic (or almost surely constant),

iii)

(uniform ellipticity) there exists
K>0
such that, almost surely, for all
x\in\mathbb{Z}
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{K}\leq N(x)\leq K.

(
\mathcal{U.E.}
)

In words, we assume that the distribution of the population sizes is invariant by translation, that any translation invariant statistic is deterministic and that they are uniformly bounded away from 0 and infinity, almost surely.

Such a random variable can be defined using the usual formalism of random walks in random environments in the following way. Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{B},\mu)
be a probability space on which is defined a family of measurable maps
(T^{x})_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}

a)

b)

\displaystyle\mu(T^{x}A)=\mu(A)\quad\forall A\in\mathcal{B},

c)

the family

is ergodic with respect to the measure
\mu
,
i.e.

\displaystyle T^{x}A=A\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{Z}\implies\mu(A)\in\{0,1\}.

Now let
N:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
be a random variable on
\Omega
such that there exists
K>0

\displaystyle\frac{1}{K}\leq N(\omega)\leq K\qquad\mu(d\omega)\text{-almost surely}.

For
x\in\mathbb{Z}

\displaystyle N(x)=N(\omega,x)=N(T^{x}\omega).

Then
\{N(x),x\in\mathbb{Z}\}
satisfies assumptions

above.
The parameter
\omega\in\Omega
determines the environment in which the population evolves, and the whole process will be defined on a larger (unspecified) probability space.
All our results will be

they will hold conditionally on
\omega

\mu

\omega

\Omega

Note that

1.2
Main result - rescaling limit of the stepping stone model in a random environment

Individuals are related when they share at least one common ancestor some time in the past.
If two individuals are sampled at a distance
\sqrt{n}
of each other, they need to look back at least
n
generations in the past to expect to have ancestors living in the same deme.
Over time scales of the order of
n
generations, individuals sampled from the same deme will have ancestors living in the same deme around
\sqrt{n}
generations.
Each time they do, they have a probability
1/N
of having a common genealogical ancestor in the previous generation.
Hence if
N
is of the order of
\sqrt{n}
, a positive proportion of individuals living in these demes should be related after a time of the order of
n
generations.

For this reason, we shall study the behaviour of
(p_{t}(\omega,\cdot),t\geq 0)
on spatial scales of the order of
\sqrt{n}
and at times of the order of
n
with
\lambda=\sqrt{n}
as
n
tends to infinity, for a fixed environment
\omega
.
When the deme sizes are uniform, it is well known that the stepping stone model rescaled in this way converges in distribution to a weak solution of the stochastic heat equation with Wright-Fisher noise (
3
)
[
28
,
34
]
.
Our main result states that on these spatial and temporal scales, the process forgets the details of the environment and evolves as an effective population with uniform demographic parameters.

For
n\geq 1
, let
p^{0}_{n}\in\mathcal{F}(\mathbb{Z},[0,1])
and set
\lambda_{n}=\sqrt{n}
.
Let
(p^{n}_{t}(\omega,\cdot),t\geq 0)

\displaystyle\bm{p}^{n}_{t}(\omega,x)=p_{nt}^{n}(\omega,\sqrt{n}x),\quad x\in\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\mathbb{Z}.

\displaystyle\bm{p}^{n}_{t}(\omega,dx)=\sum_{y\in\mathbb{Z}}p^{n}_{nt}(\omega,y)\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\delta_{y/\sqrt{n}}(dx).

Let
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})

For

Remark 7

Alternative migration mechanisms can readily be considered instead of the one chosen in Definition

2
Coalescing random walks in a random environment

It is well known that the stepping stone model of Definition
2
admits a moment dual in the form of a system of coalescing random walks
[
34
]
.
These random walks describe the positions of the ancestors of a random sample of individuals in the population.
Each pair of random walks (also called ancestral lineages) coalesces at the first time in the past when the two sampled individuals have a common ancestor.
The lineages are affected by the heterogeneity of the environment in two ways: they are more likely to jump to more crowded demes, and they coalesce more quickly in less crowded demes.

In this section, we first define the system of coalescing random walks in a random environment which is dual to the stepping stone model of Definition
2
.
We then state several results on this dual which will be used to identify the limit in the proof of Theorem
4
.
First we state the convergence of the rescaled random walk to Brownian motion (Theorem
10
) and a local central limit theorem for this random walk (Theorem
11
).
Then we state the convergence of the whole dual process to a system of coalescing Brownian motions (Theorem
15
).

2.1
The dual of the stepping stone model in a random environment

Definition 8

3.2
The environment viewed from the two random walks

We now introduce an auxiliary process which records the environment viewed by the two independent random walks when they meet.
Let
(\xi^{1}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
and
(\xi^{2}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be two independent random walks on
\mathbb{Z}
with transition rates given by (
11
) and started from
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
respectively.
Define, for
t\geq 0
, the time spent together by the two random walks up to time
t
,

\displaystyle L_{0}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{1}_{\left\{\xi^{1}_{s}=\xi^{2}_{s}\right\}}ds.

(27)

Also let
L_{0}^{-1}(\cdot)
be the right-continuous inverse of
L_{0}
,
i.e.

\displaystyle L_{0}^{-1}(t)=\inf\left\{s\geq 0:L_{0}(s)>t\right\}.

Note that

\displaystyle\{L_{0}^{-1}(t),t\geq 0\}=\{t\geq 0:\xi^{1}_{t}=\xi^{2}_{t}\}.

(28)

As in (
22
), let
(Z(t),t\geq 0)
be the environment viewed from the first random walk,
i.e.

\displaystyle Z(t)=T^{\xi^{1}_{t}}\omega.

In words, each time the first random walk jumps, we translate the whole environment in order to always keep the random walk at the origin.
We now want to keep track of the environment viewed from the two random walks.
Of course, when the two random walks are in different locations, this does not make sense, so whenever the two random walks are not together, we "fast-forward" to the next time when they are in the same location in
\mathbb{Z}
, and we translate the whole environment so that they are both at the origin, until one of them jumps again, and we go to the next time when they rejoin,
etc
.
This process is then a time change of
(Z(t),t\geq 0)
, and in view of (
28
), the corresponding time change is exactly given by
L_{0}^{-1}(\cdot)
.
This leads to the following definition of the environment viewed from the two random walks:

\displaystyle Y(t)=Z(L_{0}^{-1}(t))\quad t\geq 0.

The main result of this subsection is the following.
It gives a description of the typical environment seen by the two random walks whenever they are at the same location, and will be crucial to determine their overall coalescence rate.

Proposition 19
.

The process
(Y(t))_{t\geq 0}
, started from the initial distribution

\displaystyle\frac{\pi(\omega)^{2}}{\int\pi^{2}d\mu}\mu(d\omega),

(29)

is a stationary and ergodic Markov process.

Proof.

Let
\tau_{0}
denote the first time at which the two random walks
\xi^{1}
and
\xi^{2}
meet,
i.e.

\displaystyle\tau_{0}=\inf\{t\geq 0:\xi^{1}_{t}=\xi^{2}_{t}\}.

Let
\mathcal{F}_{0}(\mathbb{Z})
be the space of real-valued functions on
\mathbb{Z}
which are zero on all but a finite number of points.
For
f\in\mathcal{F}_{0}(\mathbb{Z})
, define

\displaystyle E^{\omega}f(x,y)=\mathbb{E}^{\omega}_{x,y}\left[f(\xi^{1}_{\tau_{0}})\right].

For
x\in\mathbb{Z}
,
z\in\{-1,1\}
and
\omega\in\Omega
, let

\displaystyle j(\omega,x,z)=m\frac{N(\omega,x+z)}{N_{3}(\omega,x)}

(30)

be the rate at which
\xi^{i}
jumps from
x
to
x+z
.
The process
(\xi^{1}_{L_{0}^{-1}(t)},t\geq 0)
then jumps from
x
to
y
at rate

\displaystyle q(\omega,x,y)=2\sum_{z\in\{-1,1\}}j(\omega,x,z)E^{\omega}\delta_{y}(x,x+z),

where
\delta_{y}(x)=1
if
x=y
and 0 otherwise.
Setting
q(\omega,y)=q(\omega,0,y)
, the generator of
(Y(t))_{t\geq 0}
takes the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{Y}f(\omega)=\sum_{y\in\mathbb{Z}}q(\omega,y)\left(f(T^{y}\omega)-f(\omega)\right).

Proposition
19
will then be proved if we show that for
\mu
almost every
\omega\in\Omega
and every
y\in\mathbb{Z}
,

\displaystyle\pi(\omega)^{2}q(\omega,y)=\pi(T^{y}\omega)^{2}q(T^{y}\omega,-y).

(31)

Indeed the stationarity of the measure (
29
) follows directly from (
31
) and ergodicity is a consequence of assumption
c
and

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}f(\omega)(-\mathcal{L}^{Y})f(\omega)\pi(\omega)^{2}\mu(d\omega)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{y\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{\Omega}q(\omega,y)\left(f(T^{y}\omega)-f(\omega)\right)^{2}\pi(\omega)^{2}\mu(d\omega).

We now prove (
31
).
To avoid writing infinite sums, we first restrict ourselves to random walks on a bounded region
\{-A,\ldots,A\}
.
For
A\geq 1
, define

j^{A}(\omega,x,z)=\left\{\begin{aligned} j(\omega,x,z)\hphantom{0}&\text{ if }\left|x\right|\leq A\text{ and }\left|x+z\right|\leq A,\\
0\hphantom{p(x,z)}&\text{ otherwise.}\end{aligned}\right.

Let
(\xi^{A,1}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
and
(\xi^{A,2}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be two independent random walks on
\{-A,\ldots,A\}
which jump from
x
to
x+z
at rate
j^{A}(\omega,x,z)
(
i.e.
they behave as
\xi^{i}
but the jumps leading outside
\{-A,\ldots,A\}
are suppressed).
Let
\tau_{0}^{A}
be the first time at which
\xi^{A,1}
and
\xi^{A,2}
meet and define, for
f\in\mathcal{F}_{0}(\mathbb{Z})
,
x,y\in\{-A,\ldots,A\}
,

\displaystyle E^{A,\omega}f(x,y)=\mathbb{E}^{\omega}_{x,y}\left[f(\xi^{A,i}_{\tau_{0}^{A}})\right].

Then, since

\displaystyle\pi(\omega,x)j^{A}(\omega,x,z)=\pi(\omega,x+z)j^{A}(\omega,x+z,-z),

for any
f,g\in\mathcal{F}_{0}(\mathbb{Z})
,

\sum_{x=-A}^{A}\sum_{z\in\{-1,1\}}\pi(\omega,x)j^{A}(\omega,x,z)E^{A,\omega}f(x+z,y)E^{A,\omega}g(x,y)\\
=\sum_{x=-A}^{A}\sum_{z\in\{-1,1\}}\pi(\omega,x)j^{A}(\omega,x,z)E^{A,\omega}f(x,y)E^{A,\omega}g(x+z,y).

As a result,

\sum_{x,y\in[-A,A]}\sum_{z\in\{-1,1\}}\pi(\omega,x)\pi(\omega,y)\Big[j^{A}(\omega,x,z)\left(E^{A,\omega}f(x+z,y)-E^{A,\omega}f(x,y)\right)\\
+j^{A}(\omega,y,z)\left(E^{A,\omega}f(x,y+z)-E^{A,\omega}f(x,y)\right)\Big]E^{A,\omega}g(x,y)\\[13.00005pt]
=\sum_{x,y\in[-A,A]}\sum_{z\in\{-1,1\}}\pi(\omega,x)\pi(\omega,y)E^{A,\omega}f(x,y)\Big[j^{A}(\omega,x,z)\left(E^{A,\omega}g(x+z,y)-E^{A,\omega}g(x,y)\right)\\
+j^{A}(\omega,y,z)\left(E^{A,\omega}g(x,y+z)-E^{A,\omega}g(x,y)\right)\Big].

(32)

But, by the Markov property and the definition of
E^{\omega}
, for any
x\neq y\in\{-A,\ldots,A\}
and for any
f\in\mathcal{F}_{0}(\mathbb{Z})
,

\sum_{z\in\{-1,1\}}\Big[j^{A}(\omega,x,z)\left(E^{A,\omega}f(x+z,y)-E^{A,\omega}f(x,y)\right)\\
+j^{A}(\omega,y,z)\left(E^{A,\omega}f(x,y+z)-E^{A,\omega}f(x,y)\right)\Big]=0.

As a result, the only non-zero terms in the sums appearing in (
32
) are those for which
x=y
.
We thus obtain that, for any
f,g\in\mathcal{F}_{0}(\mathbb{Z})
,

\sum_{x=-A}^{A}\sum_{z\in\{-1,1\}}\pi(\omega,x)^{2}j^{A}(\omega,x,z)g(x)\left(E^{A,\omega}f(x+z,x)-f(x)\right)\\
=\sum_{x=-A}^{A}\sum_{z\in\{-1,1\}}\pi(\omega,x)^{2}j^{A}(\omega,x,z)f(x)\left(E^{A,\omega}g(x+z,x)-g(x)\right).

Taking
f=\delta_{y}
and
g=\delta_{x}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\pi(\omega,x)^{2}\sum_{z\in\{-1,1\}}j^{A}(\omega,x,z)E^{A,\omega}\delta_{y}(x+z,x)=\pi(\omega,y)^{2}\sum_{z\in\{-1,1\}}j^{A}(\omega,y,z)E^{A,\omega}\delta_{x}(y+z,y).

We now wish to let
A\to\infty
in order to obtain (
31
).
Clearly, for
A
large enough,
j^{A}(\omega,x,z)=j(\omega,x,z)
.
Let
T_{A}
be such that

\displaystyle T_{A}=\inf\{t\geq 0:\left|\xi^{i}_{t}\right|>A\text{ for some }i\in\{1,2\}\}.

Then, since
T_{A}\to\infty
almost surely as
A\to\infty
,
\xi^{A,i}_{\tau_{0}^{A}}
converges in distribution to
\xi^{i}_{\tau_{0}}
.
Hence
E^{A,\omega}\delta_{y}(x,x+z)\to E^{\omega}\delta_{y}(x,x+z)
and (
31
) is proved.
∎

\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{k}\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\omega}_{\bm{p}^{n}_{0}}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{k}\left\langle\bm{p}^{n}_{t},\phi_{i}\right\rangle\right]=\frac{1}{n^{k/2}}\sum_{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}}\mathbb{E}^{\omega}_{\left\{\frac{x_{1}}{\sqrt{n}},\ldots,\frac{x_{k}}{\sqrt{n}}\right\}}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{N_{t}}\bm{p}_{0}^{n}(\xi^{i,n}_{t})\right]\phi_{1}\left(\frac{x_{1}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\ldots\phi_{k}\left(\frac{x_{k}}{\sqrt{n}}\right),

(33)

where
\bm{\mathcal{A}}^{n}_{t}=\{\xi^{1,n}_{t},\ldots,\xi^{N_{t},n}_{t}\}
.
Furthermore, for all
n\geq 1
,
t\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\left|\langle\bm{p}^{n}_{t},\phi\rangle\right|\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}\left|\phi\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\right|.

(34)

Since the right hand side is bounded uniformly in
n
for any
\phi\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
, the set

\displaystyle\left\{p\in\Xi:\left|\left\langle p,\phi\right\rangle\right|\leq\sup_{k\geq 1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}\left|\phi\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{k}}\right)\right|,\forall\phi\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})\right\}.

(35)

is strongly bounded, hence compact in
\Xi
for the vague topology by the Banach-Alaoglu theorem.
As a consequence
\bm{p}^{\infty}
also takes values in this subset.
Since
p\mapsto\left\langle p,\phi\right\rangle
is continuous and bounded on this subset for any smooth and bounded
\phi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, we can let
n=n_{j}\to\infty
in the left hand side of (
33
).
The right hand side can also be written

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}\mathbb{E}^{\omega}_{\left\{\frac{\lfloor\sqrt{n}x_{1}\rfloor}{\sqrt{n}},\ldots,\frac{\lfloor\sqrt{n}x_{k}\rfloor}{\sqrt{n}}\right\}}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{N_{t}}\bm{p}_{0}^{n}(\xi^{i,n}_{t})\right]\phi_{1}\left(\frac{\lfloor\sqrt{n}x_{1}\rfloor}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\ldots\phi_{k}\left(\frac{\lfloor\sqrt{n}x_{k}\rfloor}{\sqrt{n}}\right)dx_{1}\ldots dx_{k}.

Since
\prod_{i=1}^{N_{t}}\bm{p}_{0}^{n}(\xi^{i,n}_{t})\leq 1
, we can use Theorem
15
and the dominated convergence theorem for
k\in\{1,2\}
to obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\omega}_{\bm{p}_{0}}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{k}\left\langle\bm{p}^{\infty}_{t},\phi_{i}\right\rangle\right]=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}\mathbb{E}^{\omega}_{\{x_{1},x_{k}\}}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{N_{t}}\bm{p}_{0}(X^{i}_{t})\right]\phi_{1}(x_{1})\phi_{k}(x_{k})dx_{1}dx_{k},

(36)

where
\mathcal{D}_{t}=\{X^{1}_{t},X^{N_{t}}_{t}\}
is the Brownian flow with delayed coalescence with parameters
\sigma^{2}
and
\gamma
, started from either
\{x_{1}\}
if
k=1
or
\{x_{1},x_{2}\}
if
k=2
.
Note that, passing to the limit in (
34
) and since
p^{n}_{t}\geq 0
, we have
0\leq\langle\bm{p}^{\infty}_{t},\phi\rangle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi(x)dx
for all
\phi\geq 0
.
We have thus proved that
(\bm{p}^{\infty}_{t},t\geq 0)
satisfies the conditions of Proposition
14
, hence it is a solution to the martingale problem (
8
) and the proof of Theorem
4
is complete.
∎

4.2
Tightness of the sequence

Since the sequence of processes
(\bm{p}^{n}_{t}(\omega,\cdot),t\in[0,T])
takes values in a compact subspace of
\Xi
almost surely (see (
35
) above), to prove that it is tight in
\mathcal{C}([0,T],\Xi)
it is enough to show that for any
\phi\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
and
f\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, the sequence of real-valued processes
(f(\left\langle\bm{p}^{n}_{t},\phi\right\rangle),t\in[0,T])
is tight in
\mathcal{C}([0,T],\mathbb{R})

[
11
, Theorem 3.9.1]
.
To do so, we want to use the Aldous-Rebolledo criterion and show that both the finite variation part and the quadratic variation part of
(f(\left\langle\bm{p}^{n}_{t},\phi\right\rangle),t\in[0,T])
are tight.
In fact, we show that this is true for another process
(f(\left\langle\tilde{p}^{n}_{t},\phi\right\rangle),t\in[0,T])
, defined below, and we show that the difference between the two processes vanishes as
n\to\infty
.
Let us first state two lemmas.

Lemma 22
.

There exists
\beta\in(0,1)
such that for all
T>0
there exist random variables
(C_{n},n\geq 1)
such that, for all
n\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\sup_{x,y\in\mathbb{Z}}n^{\beta/2}\frac{\left|p^{n}_{nt}(\omega,x)-p^{n}_{nt}(\omega,y)\right|}{\left|x-y\right|^{\beta}}\leq C_{n}\quad\text{ almost surely,}

and there exists
C>0
such that, for all
n\geq 1
,
\mathbb{E}^{\omega}\left[C_{n}^{2}\right]\leq C
,
\mu(d\omega)
-almost surely.

Lemma
22
is proved in Subsection
4.3
.
Recall the definition of
\pi
in (
13
).
For
n\geq 1
,
\delta>0
and
x\in\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\mathbb{Z}
, set

\displaystyle\Pi^{n,\delta}(\omega,x)=\frac{1}{2\delta\sqrt{n}}\sum_{k\in B(\sqrt{n}x,\delta\sqrt{n})\cap\mathbb{Z}}\pi(\omega,k)

(37)

and extend
x\mapsto\Pi^{n,\delta}(\omega,x)
to
\mathbb{R}
by linear interpolation.

Lemma 23
.

For any
\delta>0
,
\Pi^{n,\delta}
converges to 1 uniformly on compact sets as
n\to\infty
,
\mu(d\omega)
-almost surely.

Proof.

By the pointwise ergodic theorem, for each
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
\delta>0
,

\displaystyle\Pi^{n,\delta}(\omega,x)\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}1\quad\mu(d\omega)-\text{almost surely.}

Moreover, for any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
, by (

U
.
E

.

),

\displaystyle\left|\Pi^{n,\delta}(\omega,x)-\Pi^{n,\delta}(\omega,y)\right|\leq\frac{K^{4}}{\delta}\left|x-y\right|.

Hence
x\mapsto\Pi^{n,\delta}(\omega,x)
is equicontinuous
\mu(d\omega)
-almost surely.
By Arzela-Ascoli’s theorem, it converges uniformly on compact sets.
∎

We can now prove the tightness of the sequence.

Proof of Lemma
21
.

For
n\geq 1
, define a
\Xi
-valued process
(\tilde{p}^{n}_{t},t\geq 0)
by

\displaystyle\left\langle\tilde{p}^{n}_{t},\phi\right\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}\,\pi(\omega,x)\,p^{n}_{nt}(\omega,x)\,\phi\left(\frac{F(\omega,x)}{c\sqrt{n}}\right),

where
F(\omega,x)
was defined in (
17
) and
c
was defined just above (
23
).
We first show that for any
\phi\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
and
f\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
the sequence
(f(\left\langle\tilde{p}^{n}_{t},\phi\right\rangle),t\in[0,T])
is tight and then we show that the difference between
\left\langle\bm{p}^{n}_{t},\phi\right\rangle
and
\left\langle\tilde{p}^{n}_{t},\phi\right\rangle
vanishes as
n\to\infty
.

Tightness of
(f(\left\langle\tilde{p}^{n}_{t},\phi\right\rangle),t\in[0,T])

By the definition of
p^{n}
and (
5
),

d\left\langle\tilde{p}^{n}_{t},\phi\right\rangle=n\frac{m}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{z\in\{-1,1\}}\pi(\omega,x)j(\omega,x,z)\left(p^{n}_{nt}(x+z)-p^{n}_{nt}(x)\right)\phi\left(\frac{F(\omega,x)}{c\sqrt{n}}\right)dt\\
+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}\pi(\omega,x)\phi\left(\frac{F(\omega,x)}{c\sqrt{n}}\right)\sqrt{\frac{\sqrt{n}}{N(\omega,x)}p^{n}_{nt}(x)(1-p^{n}_{nt}(x))}dB^{x}_{t},

where
j(\omega,x,z)
was defined in (
30
) and
(B^{x},x\in\mathbb{Z})
is a family if independent Brownian motions.
Note that, by the reversibility of
\pi
,

n\frac{m}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{z\in\{-1,1\}}\pi(\omega,x)j(\omega,x,z)\left(p^{n}_{nt}(x+z)-p^{n}_{nt}(x)\right)\phi\left(\frac{F(\omega,x)}{c\sqrt{n}}\right)\\
=n\frac{m}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{z\in\{-1,1\}}\pi(\omega,x)j(\omega,x,z)p^{n}_{nt}(x)\left(\phi\left(\frac{F(\omega,x+z)}{c\sqrt{n}}\right)-\phi\left(\frac{F(\omega,x)}{c\sqrt{n}}\right)\right).

(38)

By Taylor’s theorem,

\phi\left(\frac{F(\omega,x+z)}{c\sqrt{n}}\right)-\phi\left(\frac{F(\omega,x)}{c\sqrt{n}}\right)=\phi^{\prime}\left(\frac{F(\omega,x)}{c\sqrt{n}}\right)\frac{F(\omega,x+z)-F(\omega,x)}{c\sqrt{n}}\\
+R\left(\frac{F(\omega,x)}{c\sqrt{n}},\frac{F(\omega,x+z)}{c\sqrt{n}}\right)\frac{(F(\omega,x+z)-F(\omega,x))^{2}}{c^{2}n},

where

\displaystyle R(x,y)=\int_{0}^{1}(1-t)\phi^{\prime\prime}(x+t(y-x))dt.

In view of (
20
), equation (
38
) equals

\displaystyle\frac{m}{c^{2}\sqrt{n}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathbb{Z}\\
z\in\{-1,1\}\end{subarray}}\pi(\omega,x)j(\omega,x,z)p^{n}_{nt}(x)R\left(\frac{F(\omega,x)}{c\sqrt{n}},\frac{F(\omega,x+z)}{c\sqrt{n}}\right)(F(\omega,x+z)-F(\omega,x))^{2}.

Since
\left|p^{n}_{nt}(x)\right|\leq 1
and by assumption (

U
.
E

.

), this is smaller than

\displaystyle\frac{C}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{z\in\{-1,1\}}\left|R\left(\frac{F(\omega,x)}{c\sqrt{n}},\frac{F(\omega,x+z)}{c\sqrt{n}}\right)\right|

for some constant
C>0
.
In turn, the above expression is uniformly bounded as
n\to\infty
since
\phi\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
(also note (
25
)).
We have thus proved the existence of a constant
C_{1}>0
such that, for all
n\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\left|n\frac{m}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{z\in\{-1,1\}}\pi(\omega,x)j(\omega,x,z)\left(p^{n}_{nt}\left(x+z\right)-p^{n}_{nt}\left(x\right)\right)\phi\left(\frac{F(\omega,x)}{c\sqrt{n}}\right)\right|\leq C_{1}.

(39)

We now turn to the quadratic variation part of
\left\langle\tilde{p}^{n}_{t},\phi\right\rangle
.
Since the Brownian motions
(B^{x},x\in\mathbb{Z})
are independent, it is

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}\pi(\omega,x)^{2}\phi\left(\frac{F(\omega,x)}{c\sqrt{n}}\right)^{2}\frac{1}{N(\omega,x)}p^{n}_{ns}(x)(1-p^{n}_{ns}(x))ds.

Then, by (

U
.
E

.

) and since
\phi\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
there exists a constant
C_{2}>0
such that

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}\pi(\omega,x)^{2}\phi\left(\frac{F(\omega,x)}{c\sqrt{n}}\right)^{2}\frac{1}{N(\omega,x)}p^{n}_{ns}(x)(1-p^{n}_{ns}(x))\right|\leq C_{2}.

(40)

We then write
FV_{t}(f,\phi)
and
QV_{t}(f,\phi)
for the finite variation part and the quadratic variation part of
f(\left\langle\tilde{p}^{n}_{t},\phi\right\rangle)
.
Then, by (
39
) and (
40
) and by the Itô formula,

\displaystyle\left|FV_{t}(f,\phi)-FV_{s}(f,\phi)\right|\leq\left(\sup\left|f^{\prime}\right|C_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\sup\left|f^{\prime\prime}\right|C_{2}\right)\left|t-s\right|

\displaystyle\left|QV_{t}(f,\phi)-QV_{t}(f,\phi)\right|\leq\sup\left|f^{\prime}\right|^{2}C_{2}\left|t-s\right|,

where the supremum is taken over a suitable compact set (depending on
\phi
).
Hence both
(FV_{t}(f,\phi),t\in[0,T])
and
(QV_{t}(f,\phi),t\in[0,T])
are tight, and by the Aldous-Rebolledo criterion,
(f(\left\langle\tilde{p}^{n}_{t},\phi\right\rangle),t\in[0,T])
is tight in
\mathrm{D}([0,T],\Xi)
.
Since for all
n\geq 1
,
t\mapsto f(\langle\tilde{p}^{n}_{t},\phi\rangle)
is continuous almost surely, so are its potential limit points, hence the sequence is tight in
\mathcal{C}([0,T],\Xi)
.

Conclusion

Let us now show that the difference between
\left\langle\tilde{p}^{n}_{t},\phi\right\rangle
and
\left\langle p^{n}_{t},\phi\right\rangle
vanishes as
n\to\infty
.
First note that, by (
25
), for any
\varepsilon>0
,

\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}\pi(\omega,x)p^{n}_{nt}(x)\phi\left(\frac{F(\omega,x)}{c\sqrt{n}}\right)-\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}\pi(\omega,x)p^{n}_{nt}(x)\phi\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\right|\\
\leq\frac{4}{\sqrt{n}}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\phi(x)\right|M_{\varepsilon}K^{4}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathbb{Z}\\
\left|x\right|>M_{\varepsilon}\end{subarray}}K^{4}\sup_{\left|y-x/\sqrt{n}\right|\leq\varepsilon\frac{\left|x\right|}{c\sqrt{n}}}\left|\phi^{\prime}(y)\right|\varepsilon\frac{\left|x\right|}{c\sqrt{n}}.

The first term on the right hand side vanishes as
n\to\infty
for any fixed
\varepsilon
while the second term can be made arbitrarily small for all
n\geq 1
by choosing
\varepsilon
small enough.
Hence

\displaystyle\sup_{t\geq 0}\left|\left\langle\tilde{p}^{n}_{t},\phi\right\rangle-\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}\pi(\omega,x)p^{n}_{nt}(x)\phi\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\right|\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}0.

To conclude, we write, for
\delta>0
,

\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}p^{n}_{nt}(x)\phi(x/\sqrt{n})-\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}\pi(\omega,x)p^{n}_{nt}(x)\phi(x/\sqrt{n})\right|\\
\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}\left|\phi(x/\sqrt{n})\right|\left|1-\frac{1}{2\delta\sqrt{n}}\sum_{y\in B(x,\delta\sqrt{n})\cap\mathbb{Z}}\pi(\omega,y)\right|\\
+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{1}{2\delta\sqrt{n}}\sum_{y\in B(x,\delta\sqrt{n})\cap\mathbb{Z}}\pi(\omega,y)\left|p^{n}_{nt}(x)\phi(x/\sqrt{n})-p^{n}_{nt}(y)\phi(y/\sqrt{n})\right|.

The first term on the right hand side vanishes as
n\to\infty
for any
\delta>0
by Lemma
23
(recall that
\phi
is compactly supported).
For the second term, by Lemma
22
, for
t\in[0,T]
and
\left|x-y\right|\leq\delta\sqrt{n}
,

\displaystyle\left|p^{n}_{nt}(x)\phi(x/\sqrt{n})-p^{n}_{nt}(y)\phi(y/\sqrt{n})\right|\leq\sup_{\left|z-x/\sqrt{n}\right|\leq\delta}\left|\phi^{\prime}(z)\right|\delta+\left|\phi(x/\sqrt{n})\right|C_{n}\delta^{\beta}.

Hence the second term can be made arbitrarily small for all
n\geq 1
by choosing
\delta
small enough.
As a result,

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|\left\langle\tilde{p}^{n}_{t},\phi\right\rangle-\left\langle\bm{p}^{n}_{t},\phi\right\rangle\right|\overset{\mathbb{P}^{\omega}}{\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}}0.

It follows that
(f(\left\langle\bm{p}^{n}_{t},\phi\right\rangle),t\in[0,T])
is tight in
\mathcal{C}([0,T],\mathbb{R})
for any
\phi\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
and
f\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.
By Theorem 3.9.1 in
[
11
]
, the sequence
(\bm{p}^{n}_{t},t\in[0,T])
is tight in
\mathcal{C}([0,T],\Xi)
.
∎

4.3
Continuity estimate

To prove Lemma
22
, we need the following bounds, which will be proved in Appendix
A
.
Recall the definition of
g^{\omega}_{t}(x,y)
in (
14
).

Lemma 24
.

There exist constants
C,C^{\prime}>0
such that, for
\mu
-almost every
\omega\in\Omega
, for all
n\geq 1
,
t\geq 0
and
x,y\in\mathbb{Z}
,

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{0}^{t}\left(g^{\omega}_{ns}(x,z)-g^{\omega}_{ns}(y,z)\right)^{2}ds\leq Ct^{1/4}\left|\frac{x-y}{\sqrt{n}}\right|^{1/2}

\displaystyle\sup_{z\in\mathbb{Z}}\left|g^{\omega}_{t}(x,z)-g^{\omega}_{t}(y,z)\right|\leq C^{\prime}t^{-3/4}\left|x-y\right|^{1/2}.

Proof of Lemma
22
.

We begin by noting that
p^{n}_{nt}
has the integral form representation

\displaystyle p^{n}_{nt}(\omega,x)=\sum_{y\in\mathbb{Z}}g^{\omega}_{nt}(x,y)p^{n}_{0}(y)+\sum_{y\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{0}^{t}g^{\omega}_{ns}(x,y)\sqrt{\frac{\sqrt{n}}{N(\omega,y)}p^{n}_{ns}(\omega,y)(1-p^{n}_{ns}(\omega,y))}dB^{y}_{s}.

Then, for
k\geq 1
,

\mathbb{E}^{\omega}\left[\left|p^{n}_{nt}(\omega,x)-p^{n}_{nt}(\omega,y)\right|^{2k}\right]\leq 2^{2k-1}\left|\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}}g^{\omega}_{nt}(x,z)p^{n}_{0}(z)-\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}}g^{\omega}_{nt}(y,z)p^{n}_{0}(z)\right|^{2k}\\
+2^{2k-1}\mathbb{E}^{\omega}\left[\left|\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{0}^{t}\left(g^{\omega}_{ns}(x,z)-g^{\omega}_{ns}(y,z)\right)\sqrt{\frac{\sqrt{n}}{N(\omega,z)}p^{n}_{ns}(\omega,z)(1-p^{n}_{ns}(\omega,z))}dB^{z}_{s}\right|^{2k}\right].

(41)

We then bound each term on the right hand side separately, starting with the second one.
By the Burkholder-Davis-Gundy inequality, (

U
.
E

.

) and since
0\leq p^{n}_{nt}(\omega,z)\leq 1
, for some
C>0
,

\mathbb{E}^{\omega}\left[\left|\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{0}^{t}\left(g^{\omega}_{ns}(x,z)-g^{\omega}_{ns}(y,z)\right)\sqrt{\frac{\sqrt{n}}{N(\omega,z)}p^{n}_{ns}(\omega,z)(1-p^{n}_{ns}(\omega,z))}dB^{z}_{s}\right|^{2k}\right]\\
\begin{aligned} &\leq C\left(\sqrt{n}\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{0}^{t}\left(g^{\omega}_{ns}(x,z)-g^{\omega}_{ns}(y,z)\right)^{2}ds\right)^{k}\\
&\leq Ct^{k/4}\left|\frac{x-y}{\sqrt{n}}\right|^{k/2},\end{aligned}

(42)

where we used Lemma
24
in the last line.
For the first term, we combine two bounds.
First note that, for all
R>0
,
x,y\in\mathbb{Z}

\left|\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}}g^{\omega}_{nt}(x,z)p^{n}_{0}(z)-\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}}g^{\omega}_{nt}(y,z)p^{n}_{0}(z)\right|\leq\sum_{z\in B\left(\frac{x+y}{2},\frac{\left|x-y\right|}{2}+R\sqrt{n}\right)\cap\mathbb{Z}}\left|g^{\omega}_{nt}(x,z)-g^{\omega}_{nt}(y,z)\right|\\
+\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus B(x,R\sqrt{n})}g^{\omega}_{nt}(x,z)+\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus B(y,R\sqrt{n})}g^{\omega}_{nt}(y,z).

By Lemma
18
, the last two terms are both bounded by
\frac{C}{R^{2}}
for some constant
C>0
, for all
n\geq 1
.
Then by Lemma
24
,

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}}g^{\omega}_{nt}(x,z)p^{n}_{0}(z)-\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}}g^{\omega}_{nt}(y,z)p^{n}_{0}(z)\right|\leq C\left(R+\frac{\left|x-y\right|}{2\sqrt{n}}\right)\sqrt{n}(nt)^{-3/4}\left|x-y\right|^{1/2}+\frac{2C}{R^{2}}.

Choosing
R=\left|\frac{x-y}{\sqrt{n}}\right|^{-1/6}
, we obtain that for all
A>0
there exists a constant
C>0
such that, for all
x,y\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
\left|\frac{x-y}{\sqrt{n}}\right|\leq A
,

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}}g^{\omega}_{nt}(x,z)p^{n}_{0}(z)-\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}}g^{\omega}_{nt}(y,z)p^{n}_{0}(z)\right|\leq C(1+t^{-3/4})\left|\frac{x-y}{\sqrt{n}}\right|^{1/3}.

(43)

This bound doesn’t behave well when
t
is too small. To remedy this, we note that by assumption (
7
),

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}}g^{\omega}_{nt}(x,z)p^{n}_{0}(z)-p^{n}_{0}(x)\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}}g^{\omega}_{nt}(x,z)\left|\frac{x-y}{\sqrt{n}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\,\mathbb{E}^{\omega}_{x}\left[\left|\frac{\xi_{nt}-x}{\sqrt{n}}\right|^{2}\right]^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq C\sqrt{t},

where we have used Jensen’s inequality in the second line and Lemma
18
in the last line.
Hence, using assumption (
7
) again,

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}}g^{\omega}_{nt}(x,z)p^{n}_{0}(z)-\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}}g^{\omega}_{nt}(y,z)p^{n}_{0}(z)\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2C\sqrt{t}+\left|p^{n}_{0}(x)-p^{n}_{0}(y)\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2C\sqrt{t}+C\left|\frac{x-y}{\sqrt{n}}\right|.

(44)

The above inequality behaves well when
t
is not too large.
Hence, using (
43
) when
t\geq\left|\frac{x-y}{\sqrt{n}}\right|^{4/15}
and (
44
) when
t\leq\left|\frac{x-y}{\sqrt{n}}\right|^{4/15}
, we conclude that there exists a constant
C>0
such that, for all
t\geq 0
and every
x,y\in\mathbb{Z}
,

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}}g^{\omega}_{nt}(x,z)p^{n}_{0}(z)-\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}}g^{\omega}_{nt}(y,z)p^{n}_{0}(z)\right|\leq C\left|\frac{x-y}{\sqrt{n}}\right|^{2/15}.

(45)

Finally, plugging (
42
) and (
45
) into (
41
) and noting that the left hand side stays bounded even when
\left|x-y\right|
grows, we obtain that there exists a constant
C>0
depending on
k\geq 1
such that, for
t\in[0,T]
, for all
x,y\in\mathbb{Z}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\omega}\left[\left|p^{n}_{nt}(\omega,x)-p^{n}_{nt}(\omega,y)\right|^{2k}\right]\leq C\left|\frac{x-y}{\sqrt{n}}\right|^{4k/15}\quad\mu(d\omega)-a.s.

By Kolmogorov’s continuity theorem, this implies that for any
\beta\in(0,2/15)
there exist random variables
(C_{n},n\geq 1)
such that, for
n\geq 1
and
T>0
, and every
x,y\in\mathbb{Z}
,

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|p^{n}_{nt}(\omega,x)-p^{n}_{nt}(\omega,y)\right|\leq C_{n}\left|\frac{x-y}{\sqrt{n}}\right|^{\beta}

almost surely and
\mathbb{E}^{\omega}\left[C_{n}^{2}\right]\leq C
for all
n\geq 1
,
\mu(d\omega)
-almost surely.
This concludes the proof of Lemma
22
.
∎

To prove Lemma

\displaystyle h^{\omega}_{t}(x,y)=\frac{g^{\omega}_{t}(x,y)}{\pi(\omega,y)}.

We follow a similar approach to that of

, using (

.

Lemma 25

Proof of Lemma
25
.

For
f\in\mathcal{F}_{0}(\mathbb{Z})
, we set

\displaystyle Q^{\omega}(f)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{z\in\{-1,1\}}\pi(\omega,x)j(\omega,x,z)\left(f(x+z)-f(x)\right)^{2}.

Using Bernstein’s representation theorem as in
[
8
]
, one shows that for all
x\in\mathbb{Z}
,
t>0
,

\displaystyle Q^{\omega}(h^{\omega}_{t}(x,\cdot))\leq\frac{e^{-1}}{t}h^{\omega}_{t}(x,x).

(46)

We then proceed as in the proof of Theorem 3.3 in
[
8
]
.
Fix
x\in\mathbb{Z}
,
t>0
and
k\geq 1
(to be chosen later on).
Let
y_{0}\in B(x,k)\cap\mathbb{Z}
be such that

\displaystyle h^{\omega}_{t}(x,y_{0})=\min_{y\in B(x,k)\cap\mathbb{Z}}h^{\omega}_{t}(x,y).

Then

\displaystyle h^{\omega}_{t}(x,y_{0})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sum_{y\in B(x,k)\cap\mathbb{Z}}\pi(\omega,y)}\sum_{y\in B(x,k)\cap\mathbb{Z}}h^{\omega}_{t}(x,y)\pi(\omega,y)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{y\in B(x,k)\cap\mathbb{Z}}\pi(\omega,y)\right)^{-1},

since
\sum_{y\in\mathbb{Z}}h^{\omega}_{t}(x,y)\pi(\omega,y)=1
.
Hence, assuming without loss of generality that
y_{0}\geq x
,

\displaystyle h^{\omega}_{t}(x,x)-\left(\sum_{y\in B(x,k)\cap\mathbb{Z}}\pi(\omega,y)\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle\leq h^{\omega}_{t}(x,x)-h^{\omega}_{t}(x,y_{0})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{l=x}^{y_{0}-1}\left|h^{\omega}_{t}(x,l)-h^{\omega}_{t}(x,l+1)\right|.

By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, the right hand side is bounded by

\left(\sum_{l=x}^{y_{0}-1}\pi(\omega,l)j(\omega,l,1)\left(h^{\omega}_{t}(x,l+1)-h^{\omega}_{t}(x,l)\right)^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left(\sum_{l=x}^{y_{0}-1}\frac{1}{\pi(\omega,l)j(\omega,l,1)}\right)^{1/2}\\
\leq\sqrt{2}Q^{\omega}(h^{\omega}_{t}(x,\cdot))^{1/2}\left(\sum_{l\in B(x,k)\cap\mathbb{Z}}\frac{1}{\pi(\omega,l)j(\omega,l,1)}\right)^{1/2}.

By (
46
) and (

U
.
E

.

), recalling the expression of
\pi(\omega,l)
and
j(\omega,l,1)
,

\displaystyle h^{\omega}_{t}(x,x)-\left(\sum_{y\in B(x,k)\cap\mathbb{Z}}\pi(\omega,y)\right)^{-1}\leq 2\sqrt{3}K^{2}\sqrt{k}\frac{e^{-1/2}}{\sqrt{t}}h^{\omega}_{t}(x,x)^{1/2}.

(47)

Since the random walk
(\xi_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is null recurrent,
h^{\omega}_{t}(x,x)\to 0
as
t\to\infty
for all
x\in\mathbb{Z}
.
In addition,
h^{\omega}_{t}(x,x)\leq\pi(\omega,x)^{-1}
, hence we can always choose
k\geq 1
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{y\in B(x,k-1)\cap\mathbb{Z}}\pi(\omega,y)\leq\frac{3}{h^{\omega}_{t}(x,x)}\leq\sum_{y\in B(x,k)\cap\mathbb{Z}}\pi(\omega,y).

(48)

Using such a
k
in (
47
) results in

\displaystyle\frac{2}{3}h^{\omega}_{t}(x,x)

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{3}K^{2}\frac{e^{-1/2}}{\sqrt{t}}\left(kh^{\omega}_{t}(x,x)\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq 6K^{2}\frac{e^{-1/2}}{\sqrt{t}}\left(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{y\in B(x,k-1)\cap\mathbb{Z}}\pi(\omega,y)\right)^{-1/2}.

Finally, by (

U
.
E

.

),

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k}\sum_{y\in B(x,k-1)\cap\mathbb{Z}}\pi(\omega,y)\geq\frac{1}{K^{4}}\frac{2k-1}{k}\geq\frac{1}{K^{4}}.

As a result, for all
x\in\mathbb{Z}
,
t>0
,

\displaystyle\sqrt{t}h^{\omega}_{t}(x,x)\leq 9K^{4}e^{-1/2}\quad\mu(d\omega)-a.s.

(49)

This proves the result when
x=y
, let us now extend it for all
x,y\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Reasoning as above, we obtain for
k\geq 1
,

\displaystyle h^{\omega}_{t}(x,y)-\left(\sum_{z\in B(y,k)\cap\mathbb{Z}}\pi(\omega,z)\right)^{-1}\leq 2\sqrt{3k}K^{2}Q^{\omega}(h^{\omega}_{t}(x,\cdot))^{1/2}.

By (
46
) and (
49
),

\displaystyle Q^{\omega}(h^{\omega}_{t}(x,\cdot))^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{e^{-1/2}}{\sqrt{t}}h^{\omega}_{t}(x,x)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{t^{3/4}},

(50)

for some constant
C>0
.
We then choose
k\geq 1
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{z\in B(y,k-1)\cap\mathbb{Z}}\pi(\omega,z)\leq\frac{3}{h^{\omega}_{t}(x,y)}\leq\sum_{z\in B(y,k)\cap\mathbb{Z}}\pi(\omega,z).

Noting that

\displaystyle 2k-1\leq K^{4}\frac{3}{h^{\omega}_{t}(x,y)},

we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{2}{3}h^{\omega}_{t}(x,y)\leq 2\sqrt{3}K^{2}\frac{C}{t^{3/4}}\left(\frac{3}{2}\frac{K^{4}}{h^{\omega}_{t}(x,y)}+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{1/2}.

Multiplying by
t^{3/4}\sqrt{h^{\omega}_{t}(x,y)}
on both sides, we obtain the result after noting that
h^{\omega}_{t}(x,y)\leq K^{4}
.
∎

We now turn to the proof of Lemma

.

Proof of Lemma

For

Proof of Lemma

We first note that the second part of the statement follows at once from Lemma

Appendix B
The local central limit theorem for reversible random walks in a random environment

Armed with Lemma
25
and Lemma
26
, we can prove the local central limit theorem for the random walk
(\xi_{t})_{t\geq 0}
(Theorem
11
).
Again, we follow the method used in
[
8
]
.

Proof of Theorem
11
.

For
\delta>0
and
x,y\in\mathbb{Z}
, we write

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\frac{g^{\omega}_{nt}(x,y)}{\pi(\omega,y)}-G_{t}

\displaystyle\left(\frac{x-y}{\sqrt{n}}\right)=\sqrt{n}h^{\omega}_{nt}(x,y)\left(1-\frac{1}{2\delta\sqrt{n}}\sum_{k\in B(y,\delta\sqrt{n})\cap\mathbb{Z}}\pi(\omega,k)\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2\delta}\sum_{k\in B(y,\delta\sqrt{n})\cap\mathbb{Z}}\pi(\omega,k)\left(h^{\omega}_{nt}(x,y)-h^{\omega}_{nt}(x,k)\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2\delta}\left(\mathbb{P}^{\omega}_{x}\left(\xi_{nt}\in B(y,\delta\sqrt{n})\cap\mathbb{Z}\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{N}(x/\sqrt{n},\sigma^{2}t)\in B(y/\sqrt{n},\delta)\right)\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2\delta}\int_{y/\sqrt{n}-\delta}^{y/\sqrt{n}+\delta}G_{t}\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{n}}-z\right)dz-G_{t}\left(\frac{x-y}{\sqrt{n}}\right)

\displaystyle=A^{\delta}_{n}+B^{\delta}_{n}+C^{\delta}_{n}+D^{\delta}_{n}.

We then bound each term separately.

Bound on
A^{\delta}_{n}

By Lemma
25
,

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in B(0,R\sqrt{n})\cap\mathbb{Z}\\
y\in B(0,R\sqrt{n})\cap\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}\left|A^{\delta}_{n}\right|\leq C\max_{y\in B(0,r\sqrt{n})\cap\mathbb{Z}}\left|1-\Pi^{n,\delta}\left(\omega,\frac{y}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\right|,

(51)

where
\Pi^{n,\delta}
was defined in (
37
).
By Lemma
23
, the right hand side converges to 0 as
n\to\infty
for any
\delta>0
,
\mu(d\omega)
-almost surely.

Bound on
B^{\delta}_{n}

From Lemma
26
and (

U
.
E

.

),

\displaystyle\left|B^{\delta}_{n}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{K^{4}}{2\delta}\sum_{k\in B(y,\delta\sqrt{n})\cap\mathbb{Z}}C(nt)^{-3/4}\left|y-k\right|^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq C\frac{K^{4}}{t^{3/4}}\delta^{1/2}

Hence for all
\delta>0
and
0<\varepsilon<T
,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{t\in[\varepsilon,T]}\max_{x,y\in\mathbb{Z}}\left|B^{\delta}_{n}\right|=0\quad\mu(d\omega)-a.s.

(52)

Bound on
C^{\delta}_{n}

For
t\geq 0
set

\displaystyle F_{n}^{\omega}(t,x,y)=\mathbb{P}^{\omega}_{\lfloor\sqrt{n}x\rfloor}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\xi_{nt}\leq y\right)

\displaystyle F(t,x,y)=\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{N}(x,\sigma^{2}t)\leq y\right).

By the central limit theorem,
F_{n}^{\omega}
converges pointwise to
F
as
n\to\infty
, for
\mu
almost every
\omega\in\Omega
.
Moreover, by the functional central limit theorem (Theorem
10
),
F_{n}^{\omega}(\cdot,x,y)
converges to
F(\cdot,x,y)
uniformly on
[0,T]
for each
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
(using Fatou’s lemma).
Also,
x\mapsto F_{n}^{\omega}(t,x,y)
and
y\mapsto F_{n}^{\omega}(t,x,y)
are both monotone (and so are
x\mapsto F(t,x,y)
and
y\mapsto F(t,x,y)
), and
F
is continuous.
It then follows that
F_{n}^{\omega}
converges to
F
uniformly on compact sets of
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
\mu(d\omega)
almost surely.

Now note that

C^{\delta}_{n}=\frac{1}{2\delta}\left[\left(F_{n}^{\omega}\left(t,\frac{x}{\sqrt{n}},\frac{y}{\sqrt{n}}+\delta\right)-F\left(t,\frac{x}{\sqrt{n}},\frac{y}{\sqrt{n}}+\delta\right)\right)\right.\\
\left.-\left(F_{n}^{\omega}\left(t,\frac{x}{\sqrt{n}},\frac{y}{\sqrt{n}}-\delta\right)-F\left(t,\frac{x}{\sqrt{n}},\frac{y}{\sqrt{n}}-\delta\right)\right)\right].

Hence

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in B(0,R\sqrt{n})\cap\mathbb{Z}\\
y\in B(0,R\sqrt{n})\cap\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}\left|C^{\delta}_{n}\right|\leq\frac{1}{\delta}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in B(0,R)\\
y\in B(0,R+\delta)\end{subarray}}\left|F_{n}^{\omega}(t,x,y)-F(t,x,y)\right|.

By the uniform convergence of
F_{n}^{\omega}
on compact sets, we thus have, for any
\delta>0
,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in B(0,R\sqrt{n})\cap\mathbb{Z}\\
y\in B(0,R\sqrt{n})\cap\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}\left|C^{\delta}_{n}\right|=0\quad\mu(d\omega)-a.s.

(53)

Bound on
D^{\delta}_{n}

Since

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\partial_{x}G_{t}(x)\right|=\frac{e^{-1/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma^{2}t},

we have, for all
0<\varepsilon<T
and
\delta>0
,

\displaystyle\sup_{n\geq 1}\sup_{t\in[\varepsilon,T]}\sup_{x,y\in\mathbb{R}}\left|D^{\delta}_{n}\right|\leq\frac{e^{-1/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma^{2}\varepsilon}\delta.

(54)

Combining (
51
), (
52
), (
53
) and (
54
), we see that
\delta
can be chosen so that
\left|B^{\delta}_{n}\right|
and
\left|D^{\delta}_{n}\right|
are arbitrarily small for all
n\geq 1
, and then we can choose
n_{0}
such that for all
n\geq n_{0}
,
\left|A^{\delta}_{n}\right|
and
\left|C_{n}^{\delta}\right|
are arbitrarily small.
This concludes the proof of Theorem
11
.
∎

Appendix C
Proof of Lemma
6

Proof of Lemma
6
.

First of all, we can assume without loss of generality that
\mathbb{E}\left[Y\right]=1
.
Then there exists a random variable
\tilde{X}
taking values in
\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that, for any measurable non-negative function
f
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[f(\tilde{X})]=\mathbb{E}\left[f(X)Y\right].

Let us show that there exists a coupling of
X
and
\tilde{X}
such that
\tilde{X}\geq X
almost surely.

Let
\mathcal{P}_{X}
denote the law of the random variable
X
and set, for all
x\in[0,+\infty]
,

\displaystyle F(x)=\int_{0}^{x}\mathcal{P}_{X}(du),

\displaystyle G(x)=\int_{0}^{x}g(u)\mathcal{P}_{X}(du).

We prove that for all
x\geq 0
,

\displaystyle G(x)\leq F(x).

(55)

Define
x_{0}\in[0,+\infty]
by

\displaystyle x_{0}=\inf\{x\geq 0:g(x)>1\}.

Then (
55
) clearly holds for all
x<x_{0}
.
If
x_{0}=+\infty
, then (
55
) is proved.
Otherwise, suppose that there exists
x\geq x_{0}
such that

\displaystyle G(x)>F(x).

Since
g
is non-decreasing,
g(u)\geq 1
for all
u>x_{0}
.
If
g(x_{0})<1
, (
55
) holds for
x=x_{0}
and we can assume that
x>x_{0}
, otherwise we have
g(u)\geq 1
for all
u\geq x_{0}
.
As a result,

\displaystyle\int_{x}^{+\infty}g(u)\mathcal{P}_{X}(du)\geq\int_{x}^{+\infty}\mathcal{P}_{X}(du).

Adding this to the previous inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle G(+\infty)>F(+\infty).

This is a contradiction because we have assumed
\mathbb{E}\left[Y\right]=G(+\infty)=1=F(+\infty)
.
We have thus proved (
55
) for all
x\geq 0
.
Since
F
(resp.
G
) is the cumulative distribution function of
X
(resp. of
\tilde{X}
), there exists a coupling of
X
and
\tilde{X}
such that
\tilde{X}\geq X
almost surely (for example let
U
be a uniform random variable on
[0,1]
and set
X=F^{-1}(U)
and
\tilde{X}=G^{-1}(U)
, where
F^{-1}
and
G^{-1}
are the right continuous generalised inverse functions of
F
and
G
).
It then follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[XY\right]=\mathbb{E}[\tilde{X}]\geq\mathbb{E}\left[X\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[X\right]\mathbb{E}\left[Y\right],

and the Lemma is proved.
∎
