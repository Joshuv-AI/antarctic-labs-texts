Pseudo-Kähler Geometry of Properly Convex Projective Structures on the torus
Nicholas Rungi, Andrea Tamburelli

Abstract
. In this paper we prove the existence of a pseudo-Kähler structure on the deformation space ℬ 0 ​ ( T 2 ) \mathcal{B}_{0}(T^{2}) of properly convex ℝ ​ ℙ 2 \mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2} -structures over the torus. In particular, the pseudo-Riemannian metric and the symplectic form are compatible with the complex structure inherited from the identification of ℬ 0 ​ ( T 2 ) \mathcal{B}_{0}(T^{2}) with the complement of the zero section of the total space of the bundle of cubic holomorphic differentials over the Teichmüller space. We show that the S 1 S^{1} -action on ℬ 0 ​ ( T 2 ) \mathcal{B}_{0}(T^{2}) , given by rotation of the fibers, is Hamiltonian and it preserves both the metric and the symplectic form. Finally, we prove the existence of a moment map for the SL ⁡ ( 2 , ℝ ) \SL(2,\mathbb{R}) -action over ℬ 0 ​ ( T 2 ) \mathcal{B}_{0}(T^{2}) .



1.
Introduction

The purpose of this paper is to study the geometry of the deformation space of properly convex
\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}
-structures over the torus. This work is inspired by
[
MST21
]
, where the authors developed the techniques to study the geometry of the deformation space of MGHC anti-de Sitter structures over
\Sigma_{g}\times\mathbb{R}
, and a similar approach is used in our case.

1.1.
Motivation

The study of
\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{n}
-structures on a smooth manifold started in the 30s with the work of Ehresmann. The case
n=2
arose great interest among the mathematical community, to the point that between the 50s and the 60s the first results were obtained for the classification of (convex)
\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}
-structures on closed surfaces. See for example
[
Kui53
]
and
[
Ben60
]
. About 30 years later, Goldmann
[
Gol90a
]
showed that the deformation space of convex
\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}
-structures
\mathcal{B}(\Sigma_{g})
over a closed surface
\Sigma_{g}
, with
g\geq 2
, is a cell of real dimension
16g-16
. He proved a more general result for compact surfaces with
k
boundary components and he also described the geometry of the deformation space of not necessarily convex
\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}
-structures. On the other hand, the space
\mathcal{B}(\Sigma_{g})
is intimately related to the Teichmüller space
\mathcal{T}^{c}(\Sigma_{g})
. In fact, using the Klein-Beltrami model for hyperbolic geometry, every hyperbolic structure on
\Sigma_{g}
induces a convex
\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}
-structure, giving an embedding of
\mathcal{T}^{c}(\Sigma_{g})
inside
\mathcal{B}(\Sigma_{g})
.

Loftin proved in
[
Lof01
]
, using the theory of hyperbolic affine spheres, that the space
\mathcal{B}(\Sigma_{g})
can be endowed with a complex structure coming from the identification with the total space of the holomorphic bundle of cubic differentials over
\mathcal{T}^{c}(\Sigma_{g})
. It has been conjectured that this complex structure is compatible with a natural symplectic form defined on
\mathcal{B}(\Sigma_{g})
, which restricts to a multiple of the Weil-Petersson Kähler form on
\mathcal{T}^{c}(\Sigma_{g})
, see
[
Gol90b
]
. It is not yet known whether these two structures matched together give a Riemannian metric. Recently, it has been shown (
[
KZ17
]
) that
\mathcal{B}(\Sigma_{g})
admits an invariant mapping class group Kähler metric, but it is not clear how this is related to the natural complex structure introduced by Loftin.

In the case of genus one, the holomorphic vector bundle of
k
-differentials
Q^{k}(\mathcal{T}^{c}(T^{2}))
has complex rank equal to one. Thus, its total space can be identified with
\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{C}
, where here
\mathbb{H}^{2}
has to be interpreted as a copy of
\mathcal{T}^{c}(T^{2})
. Recently, it has been shown (
[
Tra18
]
) that the total space of the disc bundle inside
Q^{k}(\mathcal{T}^{c}(T^{2}))
admits a family of symplectic forms parametrized by smooth functions
f:[0,1)\to[0,1)
, which have nice geometric descriptions if the functions are chosen carefully. This result extends the Donaldson’s construction of the Feix-Kaledin hyperKähler metric (the case
k=2
and
f(t)=1-\sqrt{1-t}
) defined on a neighborhood of the zero section of
T^{*}\mathbb{H}^{2}
(
[
Don03
]
), where
T^{*}\mathbb{H}^{2}
is identified with the holomorphic bundle of quadratic differentials over
\mathcal{T}^{c}(T^{2})
.

The aim of this paper is to show that, in the case
k=3
, as long as the condition of being positive-definite on the metric is relaxed, it is possible to find a family of symplectic forms
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{f}
and pseudo-Riemannian metrics
\mathbf{g}_{f}
, depending on a choice of a function
f:[0,\infty)\to(-\infty,0]
with some properties, on the whole
Q^{3}(\mathcal{T}^{c}(T^{2}))
. In particular, they are compatible with the natural complex structure on
Q^{3}(\mathcal{T}^{c}(T^{2}))
, thus giving a family of pseudo-Kähler structures to
Q^{3}(\mathcal{T}^{c}(T^{2}))
. We will see how the pseudo-Kähler structure on the space of properly convex
\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}
-structures over the torus will be induced from the one on
Q^{3}(\mathcal{T}^{c}(T^{2}))
, and we will give a geometric description of this process in terms of hyperbolic affine spheres.

We now introduce the fundamental notions and we state our main theorems.

1.2.
Deformation space of convex
\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}
-structures

Here we give the standard definitions of
convex

\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}
-
structures
over a smooth surface
S
and we introduce the deformation space of such structures. Most of the notions presented here can be found in
[
Gol90a
]
,
[
CG97
]
and
[
Gol18
]
.

An
\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}
-
structure
on a smooth connected surface
S
is a maximal
\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}
-atlas, namely an atlas in which the local charts take value in the real projective plane and the transition functions restrict to projective transformations on each connected component of the subset where defined. Once a maximal
\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}
-atlas is given, we say that
S
has an
\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}
-
structure
. By unravelling the definition it is easy to see that if
S
is an
\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}
-surface and
p:\widetilde{S}\to S
is its universal cover, then
\widetilde{S}
inherits an
\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}
-structure from the one of
S
.

A domain (open and connected)
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}
is said to be
convex
if there exists a projective line
l
disjoint from
\Omega
such that
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}\setminus l\cong\mathbb{A}^{2}
is convex in the usual sense. By definition
\mathbb{R}^{2}
is convex but
\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}
is not. It is not difficult to show that this notion of convexity in the real projective plane does not add new convex sets with respect to those usual in affine spaces, see
[
APS+04
, §1]
.

Definition 1.1
.

An
\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}
-surface
S
is
convex
if it is projectively isomorphic to a quotient
\Omega/_{\Gamma}
, where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}
is a convex domain and
\Gamma\subset\Proj(\Omega)\subset\SL(3,\mathbb{R})
is a discrete group of projective transformations preserving
\Omega
acting freely and properly discontinuously on
\Omega
. The surface
S
is
\emph{properlyconvex}
if
\Omega
is bounded in some affine space.

There is a well-known equivalent way of defining a convex
\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}
-surface in terms of the existence of a pair of maps with special properties. This is the following:

Theorem 1.2

(Development Theorem)
.

Let
S
be an
\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}
-surface, then the following are equivalent:

(1)

S
is convex

(2)

There exists a pair
(\mathrm{dev},h)
, where
\mathrm{dev}:\widetilde{S}\to\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}
is a diffeomorphism onto a convex domain in
\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}
called the developing map and
h:\pi_{1}(S)\to\SL(3,\mathbb{R})
is a group homomorphism called the holonomy homomorphism, such that the following diagram commutes:

(1.1)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\widetilde{S}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}}

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{dev}}

Moreover, if

\widetilde{\mathrm{dev}}=g\circ\mathrm{dev},\qquad\widetilde{h}(\gamma)=g\circ h(\gamma)\circ g^{-1},\ \forall\gamma\in\pi_{1}(S)\ .

It is clear from the statement that if

is convex, then its universal cover
\widetilde{S}
can be identified with a convex domain

via the developing map and the discrete subgroup
\Gamma
can be identified with
\pi_{1}(S)
via the holonomy homomorphism. From this point on we will focus only on the case in which the surface is closed and orientable, hence it will be denoted with
\Sigma_{g}

Definition 1.3
.

Let
\Sigma_{g}
be a smooth, closed and orientable surface. A
(properly) convex

\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}
-
structure
on
\Sigma_{g}
is a pair
(f,M)
, where
f:\Sigma_{g}\to M
is a diffeomorphism (called the

) and
M\cong\Omega/_{\Gamma}
is a (properly) convex
\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}
-surface.

One can define an equivalence relation on such pairs, namely
(f_{1},M_{1})\sim(f_{2},M_{2})
if and only if there exists a projective isomorphism
\Psi:M_{1}\to M_{2}
such that the new marking
\Psi\circ f_{1}
is isotopic to
f_{2}
. Now we are ready to introduce the main space that we are going to study in this article:
the deformation space of (properly) convex
\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}
-structures

(1.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(\Sigma_{g}):=\{(f,M)\ \text{convex}\ \mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}-\text{structure on}\ \Sigma_{g}\}_{/_{\sim}}

(1.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{0}(\Sigma_{g}):=\{(f,M)\ \text{properly convex}\ \mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}-\text{structure on}\ \Sigma_{g}\}_{/_{\sim}}

Remark 1.4
.

The reader familiar with Teichmüller theory may have noticed the remarkable similarity of the construction of this space with
\mathcal{T}^{c}(\Sigma_{g})
, when
g\geq 2
. In fact, as stated before, if
\Sigma_{g}\cong\mathbb{H}^{2}/_{\Gamma}
with

, using the Klein-Beltrami model for hyperbolic geometry and noting that
\mathbb{P}\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})\cong\Iso^{+}(\mathbb{H}^{2})
and
\mathbb{P}\mathrm{SL}(3,\mathbb{R})\cong\Aut(\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2})
, we can identify
\Gamma
with a discrete subgroup of
\mathbb{P}\mathrm{SL}(3,\mathbb{R})
, contained in
\Proj(\Omega)
, acting freely and properly discontinuously on
\Omega\equiv\mathbb{H}^{2}
. Thus, giving an inclusion
\mathcal{T}^{c}(\Sigma_{g})\subset\mathcal{B}(\Sigma_{g})
at least as sets.

The behavior of this space depends highly on the genus of the surface and, as one can imagine, there are notable differences between the flat case (genus one) and the hyperbolic one (
g\geq 2
). The first result in this direction was given by Kuiper and Benzecrí (
[
Kui53
]
,
[
Ben60
]
) which we now recall.

Proposition 1.5
.

If
\Sigma_{g}
is a convex
\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}
-surface with
g\geq 2
, then it must be properly convex. Moreover, the boundary
\partial\Omega
is always strictly convex and
C^{1}
, and it must be either and ellipse or a Jordan curve which is nowhere
C^{2}
. In particular, there is an identification
\mathcal{B}(\Sigma_{g})\equiv\mathcal{B}_{0}(\Sigma_{g})
.

In the case of the torus this is no longer true, for instance there are many convex
\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}
-structures which are not properly convex: affine and Euclidean ones. They can not be properly convex since the developing map identifies the universal cover of
T^{2}
with a copy of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
inside
\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}
, which is convex but not bounded, see
[
Gol18
, §8.5]
.

The most important results about this space, when
g\geq 2
, are summarized in these two theorems:

Theorem 1.6

(Goldman
[
Gol90a
]
and Goldman-Choi
[
CG93
]
)
.

The deformation space
\mathcal{B}(\Sigma_{g})
is a smooth manifold diffeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{16g-16}
. Moreover, it is isomorphic to one of the three connected components of the
\SL(3,\mathbb{R})
-character variety, called the Hitchin component.

Theorem 1.7

(Loftin
[
Lof01
]
)
.

The data of a compact Riemann surface
(\Sigma_{g},J)
, with
g\geq 2
, together with a holomorphic section of the tri-canonical bundle
K^{3}
is equivalent to the data of a smooth, oriented closed surface
S
homeomorphic to
\Sigma_{g}
with a convex
\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}
-structure.

In other words, there exists an homeomorphism

\displaystyle\Psi

\displaystyle:\mathcal{B}(\Sigma_{g})\to Q^{3}(\mathcal{T}^{c}(\Sigma_{g}))

\displaystyle[(f,\Omega/_{\Gamma})]\mapsto(J,q)

where
q\in H^{0}(\Sigma_{g},K^{3})
. From this,
\mathcal{B}(\Sigma_{g})
inherits the structure of a holomorphic vector bundle over
\mathcal{T}^{c}(\Sigma_{g})
, in particular it inherits a natural complex structure which is invariant under the action of the mapping class group
MCG(\Sigma_{g})
. It must be noted that Loftin’s theorem recovers Goldman’s result about the contractility of the space and that
\mathcal{T}^{c}(\Sigma_{g})
now embeds into
\mathcal{B}(\Sigma_{g})
as the zero section.

1.3.
Pseudo-Kähler geometry

We now introduce the notion of
pseudo-Kähler structure
and we state our main theorem.

Recall that a
pseudo-Riemannian
metric
\mathbf{g}
on a smooth
n
-manifold
M
is an everywhere non-degenerate, smooth, symmetric
(0,2)
-tensor. The
\emph{index}
of
\mathbf{g}
is the maximal rank
k
of the smooth distribution where it is negative-definite. For instance, if
k=0
then
\mathbf{g}
is a Riemannian metric. Now let
\mathbf{I}
be a complex structure on
M
, then
(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{I})
is a
pseudo-Hermitian structure
if

\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{I}X,\mathbf{I}Y)=\mathbf{g}(X,Y),\qquad\forall X,Y\in T_{p}M,p\in M

Notice that, due to this last condition, the index of
\mathbf{g}
in this case is always even
k=2s
, where
s
is called the
complex index
and it satisfies
1\leq s\leq m=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}M
. The
fundamental 2-form

\boldsymbol{\omega}
of a pseudo-Hermitian manifold
(M,\mathbf{g},\mathbf{I})
is defined by:

\boldsymbol{\omega}(X,Y):=\mathbf{g}(X,\mathbf{I}Y),\qquad\forall X,Y\in T_{p}M,p\in M

Definition 1.8
.

A pseudo-Hermitian manifold
(M,\mathbf{g},\mathbf{I},\boldsymbol{\omega})
is called
pseudo-Kähler
if the fundamental
2
-form is closed, namely if
\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\omega}=0
. In this case the corresponding metric is called
pseudo-Kähler
.

Theorem A
.

Let
f:[0,+\infty)\to(-\infty,0]
be a smooth function such that:

(i)

f^{\prime}(t)<0,\ \forall t\geq 0

(ii)

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to+\infty}f(t)=-\infty
.

Then the space
\mathcal{B}_{0}(T^{2})
admits a
MCG(T^{2})
-invariant pseudo-Kähler structure
(\mathbf{g}_{f},\boldsymbol{\omega}_{f},\mathbf{I})
. The pseudo-Riemannian metric
\mathbf{g}_{f}
and the symplectic form
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{f}
both depend on
f
and
\mathbf{I}
is the complex structure coming from the identification of
\mathcal{B}_{0}(T^{2})
with the complement of the zero section in
Q^{3}(\mathcal{T}^{c}(T^{2}))
.

In what follows we will denote the pseudo-Riemannian metric with
\mathbf{g}
and the symplectic form with
\boldsymbol{\omega}
, since their dependence on
f
will be clear from the definition (see Definition
3.9
).

1.4.
The circle action and the
\SL(2,\mathbb{R})
moment map

We now move on to the study of two natural actions on the space
\mathcal{B}_{0}(T^{2})
. Let us consider the complement of the zero section in the bundle of cubic holomorphic differentials:

Q^{3}_{0}(\mathcal{T}^{c}(T^{2})):=Q^{3}(\mathcal{T}^{c}(T^{2}))\setminus\big(\mathcal{T}^{c}(T^{2})\times\{0\}\big)\ .

There is an isomorphism
\chi:\mathcal{B}_{0}(T^{2})\to Q^{3}_{0}(\mathcal{T}^{c}(T^{2}))
(see Corollary
2.17
) which allows us to pull the natural circle action on
Q^{3}(\mathcal{T}^{c}(T^{2}))
, given by
e^{i\theta}\cdot(J,q)\mapsto(J,e^{-i\theta}q),\ \theta\in\mathbb{R}
, back to
\mathcal{B}_{0}(T^{2})
(notice that this circle action preserves the zero section). Let
\Psi_{\theta}:\mathcal{B}_{0}(T^{2})\to\mathcal{B}_{0}(T^{2})
be the induced
S^{1}
-action, then we have the following:

Theorem B
.

The
S^{1}
-action on
\mathcal{B}_{0}(T^{2})
is Hamiltonian with respect to
\boldsymbol{\omega}
and it satisfies

(1.4)

\Psi_{\theta}^{*}\boldsymbol{\omega}=\boldsymbol{\omega},\qquad\Psi_{\theta}^{*}\mathbf{g}=\mathbf{g}

There is also a natural
\SL(2,\mathbb{R})
-action on

preserving the zero section, namely if
P\in\SL(2,\mathbb{R})
then

\displaystyle\Phi_{P}:

\displaystyle Q^{3}(\mathcal{T}^{c}(T^{2}))\to Q^{3}(\mathcal{T}^{c}(T^{2}))

\displaystyle(J,q)\mapsto(PJP^{-1},(P^{-1})^{*}q)

where the action on

is by pull-back. By abuse of notation we still denote with
\Phi_{P}
the
\SL(2,\mathbb{R})
-action on
\mathcal{B}_{0}(T^{2})
, then we have the following:

Theorem C
.

The
\SL(2,\mathbb{R})
-action on
\mathcal{B}_{0}(T^{2})
is Hamiltonian with respect to
\boldsymbol{\omega}
and with moment map
\mu:\mathcal{B}_{0}(T^{2})\to\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})^{*}
given by

(1.5)

\langle\mu(J,q),X\rangle=\bigg(1-f\big(||q||^{2}_{J}\big)\bigg)\tr(JX),\quad X\in\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})

where if

for

||q||_{J}^{2}=|c|^{2}e^{-3\phi}

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Hyperbolic affine spheres and cubic tensors

Here we briefly introduce the theory of hyperbolic affine spheres in general dimension, recalling also the most important result regarding their global geometry as hypersurfaces of
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. Finally, we will focus on the case
n=2
, showing that there is a close relationship between these geometric objects and the cubic holomorphic differentials defined on the surface. We will follow closely the introduction presented in
[
LM13
]
. For a more specific discussion, see
[
NS94
]
.

Affine differential geometry is the study of the differential-geometric properties of hypersurfaces in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
which are invariant under unimodular affine transformations, namely those affine maps
T:\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\to\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
such that
T(x)=Ax+b
, with
\det A=1
and
b\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. The affine invariants are not obvious at first glance, as the usual notions of length and angle in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
are not valid. One way of accessing the theory is the so called
affine normal
, which is a transverse vector field to a smooth hypersurface in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. In order to define the affine normal, we first discuss the affine geometry a transverse vector field generates on a hypersurface in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. Let
f\!:M\to\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
be an immersion and
\widetilde{\xi}\!:M\to\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
a transverse vector field to
f(M)
. This means that for all
x\in M
we have a splitting:

T_{f(x)}\mathbb{R}^{n+1}=f_{*}T_{x}M+\mathbb{R}\widetilde{\xi}_{x}\ .

Now, for any smooth vector fields
X,Y
on
M
, push them forward by
f_{*}
and locally extend
f_{*}X
,
f_{*}Y
and
\widetilde{\xi}
to smooth vector fields on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. Then, if
D
is the standard (flat) connection on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
we get:

\displaystyle D_{f_{*}X}f_{*}Y

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f_{*}(\widetilde{\nabla}_{X}Y)+\widetilde{h}(X,Y)\widetilde{\xi},

\displaystyle D_{f_{*}X}\widetilde{\xi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-f_{*}(\widetilde{S}(X))+\widetilde{\tau}(X)\widetilde{\xi}.

One can easily check that
\widetilde{\nabla}
is a torsion-free connection on
f(M)
,
\widetilde{h}
is a symmetric
(0,2)
-tensor,
\widetilde{S}
is an endomorphism of
TM
and
\widetilde{\tau}
is a one-form on
f(M)
, and that all the elements are independent of the extensions. Note that
D
depends only on the affine structure on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. Below we suppress the
f_{*}
from the notation.

Now assume
\widetilde{h}
is defined for every transverse vector field
\widetilde{\xi}
, then the affine normal
\xi
can be defined by the following requirements:

(1)

h:=\widetilde{h}
is positive definite.

(2)

\widetilde{\tau}=0
.

(3)

For each frame of tangent vectors
\{X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\}
to
M
,

\det_{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}}(X_{1},\dots,X_{n},\xi)^{2}=\det_{1\leq i,j\leq n}h(X_{i},X_{j}).

The first condition means that
\xi
points inward (so that
M
and
\xi
lie on the same side of the tangent plane). Moreover, if
\tilde{h}
is not definite but non-degenerate, we can still define the affine normal up to a choice of orientation in a similar manner. The affine structure equations are then

(2.1)

\displaystyle D_{X}Y

\displaystyle=\nabla_{X}Y+h(X,Y)\xi

\displaystyle D_{X}\xi

\displaystyle=-S(X)

In this case,
\nabla
is called the
Blaschke connection
,
h
is the
Blaschke metric
and
S
is the
affine shape operator
.

Now that we have introduced the basics of affine differential geometry we can define the affine spheres. There are several ways to do this and the one we will use will be through the affine shape operator, which is the most convenient for our discussion.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
M
be an immersed hypersurface in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
with structure equations given by (
2.1
), then it is called an
affine sphere
if
S=\lambda\cdot\mathrm{Id}_{TM}
, where
\lambda
is a real-valued smooth function defined on
M
.

It is not hard to see that the function
\lambda
has to be constant and hence, by rescaling, we can assume
\lambda\in\{-1,0,1\}
. We say that an affine sphere is
parabolic
if
\lambda=0
,
elliptic
if
\lambda=1
and
hyperbolic
if
\lambda=-1
. Moreover, in the last two cases we can translate the affine spheres so that
\xi=-\lambda f
, where
f:M\to\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
is the immersion. For parabolic affine spheres we may apply a linear map to ensure that
\xi
is the last coordinate vector.

Remark 2.2
.

In general, it is not easy to write down affine spheres except in very special cases. The simplest examples are the following: the elliptic paraboloid for
\lambda=0
, the round sphere and the ellipsoids for
\lambda=1
and one sheet of a hyperbolic paraboloid for
\lambda=-1
. Moreover, for the parabolic and elliptic case those are the only examples of such affine spheres which are also closed subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
.

[
Cal72
]
) and proved by Cheng-Yau (
[
CY77
]
,
[
CY86
]
) and Calabi-Nirenberg (with clarifications by Gigena (
[
Gig81
]
) and Li (
[
Li90
]

2.2.
Wang’s equation

For convex affine spheres, the affine metric

2.3.
The genus one case

In this section we aim to study equation (

3.
The main results

The purpose of this section is to present and prove the three results of this paper. First, we define a symplectic form and a complex structure on
\mathcal{B}_{0}(T^{2})
compatible with a pseudo-Riemannian metric. We then move on to show the existence of Hamiltonian actions of
S^{1}
and
\SL(2,\mathbb{R})
on
\mathcal{B}_{0}(T^{2})
and we finally compute their moment maps.

3.1.
The pseudo-Kähler structure on
\mathcal{B}_{0}(T^{2})

We begin by defining the main objects we will use during this section. Let
\rho:=\mathrm{d}x_{0}\wedge\mathrm{d}y_{0}
be the standard area form on
\mathbb{R}^{2}

Definition 3.1
.

The space
\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
of
\rho
-compatible linear-complex structures on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
is defined as

\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{R}^{2}):=\{J\in\End(\mathbb{R}^{2})\ |\ J^{2}=-\mathds{1},\ \rho(v,Jv)>0\ \text{for some}\ v\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\}\}\ .

This space is a
2
-dimensional manifold and it is easy to see that
\forall J\in\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, the tensor
g_{J}(\cdot,\cdot):=\rho(\cdot,J\cdot)
is a Riemannian metric on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, with respect to which
J
is an orthogonal endomorphism. By differentiating the identity
J^{2}=-\mathds{1}
, it follows that

T_{J}\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{R}^{2})=\{\dot{J}\in\End(\mathbb{R}^{2})\ |\ J\dot{J}+\dot{J}J=0\}\ .

Equivalently, the space
T_{J}\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
can be identified with the trace-less and
g_{J}
-symmetric endomorphisms of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. It carries a natural (almost) complex structure given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}:T_{J}

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\to T_{J}\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{R}^{2})

\displaystyle\dot{J}\mapsto-J\dot{J}\ .

There is a natural scalar product defined on each tangent space

\langle\dot{J},\dot{J}^{\prime}\rangle_{J}:=\frac{1}{2}\tr(\dot J\dot J')

for each
\dot{J},\dot{J}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. It is easy to check that
\mathcal{I}
preserves this scalar product.

Lemma 3.2

(
[
MST21
, Lemma 3.11]
)
.

There is a diffeomorphism between
\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and
\mathcal{T}^{c}(T^{2})
, which is equivariant with respect to the action of
MCG(T^{2})\cong\SL(2,\mathbb{Z})

Corollary 3.3
.

The holomorphic vector bundle
Q^{3}(\mathcal{T}^{c}(T^{2}))
can be identified with the following

(3.1)

D^{3}(\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{R}^{2})):=\{(J,C)\in\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\times S_{3}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\ |\ C(J\cdot,J\cdot,J\cdot)=-C(J\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)\}

where
S_{3}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
is the space of totally-symmetric tri-linear forms on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Moreover if
(J,C)\in D^{3}(\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))
, then

(3.2)

C(J\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)=C(\cdot,J\cdot,\cdot)=C(\cdot,\cdot,J\cdot)\ .

Proof.

If
J\in\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and
q
is a cubic
J
-holomorphic differential, then
C=\mathcal{R}e(q)
is a totally-symmetric tri-linear form on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
by Theorem
2.8
. In particular,

C(X,Y,Z)=g_{J}(A(X)Y,Z),\qquad\forall X,Y,Z\in\Gamma(T\mathbb{R}^{2})

where
A\in\End(\mathbb{R}^{2})\otimes T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{2}
is the Pick form and it satisfies the properties in Proposition
2.5
. Hence, for all
X,Y,Z\in\Gamma(T\mathbb{R}^{2})
, we have

(
A(JX)\in T_{J}\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
)

\displaystyle C(JX,JY,JZ)

\displaystyle=-g_{J}(JA(JX)Y,JZ)

(
J\ \text{is}\ g_{J}-\text{orthogonal}
)

\displaystyle=-g_{J}(A(JX)Y,Z)

\displaystyle=-C(JX,Y,Z)\ .

We conclude that
(J,C=\mathcal{R}e(q))\in D^{3}(\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))
. Conversely, if
(J,C)\in D^{3}(\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))
, then
q=C(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)-iC(J\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)
defines a cubic holomorphic differential by Theorem
2.8
. Finally,

\displaystyle C(JX,Y,Z)

\displaystyle=g_{J}(A(JX)Y,Z)

(
A(JX)\in T_{J}\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
)

\displaystyle=-g_{J}(A(JX)JY,JZ)

(Proposition
2.5
)

\displaystyle=-g_{J}(A(JY)JX,JZ)

\displaystyle=g_{J}(JA(JY)X,JZ)

\displaystyle=g_{J}(A(X)JY,Z)

\displaystyle=C(X,JY,Z)

for all
X,Y,Z\in\Gamma(T\mathbb{R}^{2})
. A similar computation shows that
C(\cdot,J\cdot,\cdot)=C(\cdot,\cdot,J\cdot)
.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

Notice that, thanks to relation (
2.2
), the space
D^{3}(\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))
can be interpreted in terms of the Pick form
A
, namely it is formed by all possible pair
(J,A)
with
J\in\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and
A\in\End(\mathbb{R}^{2})\otimes T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that:

(a)

A(X)Y=A(Y)X,\quad\forall X,Y\in\Gamma(T\mathbb{R}^{2})
;

(b)

the endomorphism

is
g_{J}
-symmetric and trace-less for all vector field

. In particular,
A(X)\in T_{J}\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{R}^{2})

We will make repetitive use of this correspondence, using the Pick tensor or the Pick form whichever is more convenient.

It is clear that
D^{3}(\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))
can be interpreted as a vector bundle over
\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, whose fiber at a point

is a two dimensional real vector space, denoted by

. Hence, we can introduce a scalar product on this vector space by

(3.3)

\langle A,B\rangle_{J}:=\int_{T^{2}}\tr(A\wedge\ast B)

where
\ast
is the Hodge-star operator with respect to
g_{J}
. We can give a more precise description of this scalar product: let
J_{0}\in\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
be the standard linear complex structure, namely

J_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}

then,

is the Euclidean metric on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and the standard basis

is such that
J_{0}(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{0}})=\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{0}},J_{0}(\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{0}})=-\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{0}}
. Similarly, for any other

, we can find a
g_{J}
-orthonormal basis
\{e_{1},e_{2}\}
such that
Je_{1}=e_{2},\ Je_{2}=-e_{1}
. Then, if

is the dual basis and

, we have
A=A_{1}e_{1}^{*}+A_{2}e_{2}^{*},\ B=B_{1}e_{1}^{*}+B_{2}e_{2}^{*}
for some
2\times 2
matrices
A_{i}
and
B_{i}
. Hence
\ast B=B_{1}e_{2}^{*}-B_{2}e_{1}^{*}
. In the end, the scalar product is given by

\langle A,B\rangle_{J}=\int_{T^{2}}\tr(A_1B_1+A_2B_2)e_{1}^{*}\wedge e_{2}^{*}\ .

Remark 3.5
.

We will be assuming that the area of the torus for the flat metric

is equal to

, in other words

\int_{T^{2}}e_{1}^{*}\wedge e_{2}^{*}=1\ .

Indeed there is an equivalent description of

presented in Lemma

. The space

can be interpreted as the space of all orientation preserving linear maps

up to rotation and/or dilation. This is equivalent to classify all possible marked lattices in

up to Euclidean isometries and homotheties. Since we can always, up to homotheties, choose a marked lattice of unit area, it follows we can always find a

with the above property. For a more detailed discussion see

Lemma 3.6
.

Let

First notice that

Then, since

and the endomorphisms

are trace-free for each vector field

Using equation (

∎

The group

Lemma 3.7
.

There is an
\SL(2,\mathbb{R})

P\cdot(J,A):=(PJP^{-1},PA(P^{-1}\cdot)P^{-1})

In Section 1.4 we introduced an

Lemma 3.8
.

For every

and

\displaystyle\langle P\cdot\dot{J},P\cdot\dot{J}^{\prime}\rangle_{P\cdot J}=\langle\dot{J},\dot{J}^{\prime}\rangle_{J}

.

Proof.

For the action on

, we have

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\tr(P\dot J\dot J'P^{-1})

)

\displaystyle=\langle\dot{J},\dot{J}^{\prime}\rangle_{J}\ .

\displaystyle\langle P\cdot A,P\cdot B\rangle_{P\cdot J}

Lemma 3.11

(2)

(1) By definition

and the vector

can be written as a linear combination of

. Then, it is sufficient to prove that

we get

, but since the basis

is
g_{J}
-orthonormal we can write

\beta_{11}=g_{J}(A_{1}\cdot e_{1},e_{2})=C(e_{1},e_{1},Je_{1})

and

\displaystyle\alpha_{21}

\displaystyle=g_{J}(JA_{2}\cdot e_{1},e_{1})

(
J\ \text{is}\ g_{J}-\text{orthogonal}
)

\displaystyle=g_{J}(A(e_{2})\cdot e_{1},e_{1})

\displaystyle=C(Je_{1},e_{1},e_{1})

(
C(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)\ \text{is totally-symmetric}
)

\displaystyle=\beta_{11}

With the same argument one can prove that
-\beta_{21}=\alpha_{11}
and that
A_{1}\cdot e_{2}=JA_{2}\cdot e_{2}
, obtaining the thesis.

(2) By using the decomposition
\dot{A}=\dot{A}_{0}+(\dot{A})_{\tr}
we get

(3.10)

-\dot{A}J=-(\dot{A}_{1})_{0}Je_{1}^{*}-(\dot{A}_{2})_{0}Je_{2}^{*}-\frac{1}{2}\tr(\dot A_1)Je_{1}^{*}-\frac{1}{2}\tr(\dot A_2)Je_{2}^{*}\ .

The same happens for the tensor
A
, hence, using Equation (
3.9
),

\displaystyle-A\dot{J}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\tr(A_1\dot J)\mathds{1}e_{1}^{*}-\frac{1}{2}\tr(A_2\dot J)\mathds{1}e_{2}^{*}+\frac{1}{2}\tr(JA_1\dot J)Je_{1}^{*}+\frac{1}{2}\tr(JA_2\dot J)Je_{2}^{*}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\tr(\dot A_2)\mathds{1}e_{1}^{*}+\frac{1}{2}\tr(\dot A_1)\mathds{1}e_{2}^{*}+\frac{1}{2}\tr(\dot A_1)Je_{1}^{*}+\frac{1}{2}\tr(\dot A_2)Je_{2}^{*}

where in the last equality we used (
3.4
) and

. It is now clear that adding the two terms

and

we get the desired formula in the statement.
∎

Theorem 3.13
.

\bullet

\mathbf{I}^{2}=-\mathds{1}

\displaystyle\mathbf{I}_{(J,A)}^{2}(\dot{J},\dot{A})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{I}_{(J,A)}\big(-J\dot{J},-\dot{A}J-A\dot{J}\big)

\displaystyle=(J^{2}\dot{J},-(-\dot{A}J-A\dot{J})J+AJ\dot{J})

\displaystyle\mathbf{g}_{(J,A)}\big(\mathbf{I}_{(J,A)}(\dot{J},\dot{A});\ \mathbf{I}_{(J,A)}(\dot{J}^{\prime},\dot{A}^{\prime})\big)

\displaystyle=\big(1-f(||A||_{0}^{2})\big)\langle-J\dot{J},-J\dot{J}^{\prime}\rangle

Lemma 3.19
.

\mathbf{g}_{(z,w)}
is non-degenerate and the form
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{(z,w)}

(z,w)\in\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{C}

The tensor
\mathbf{g}
can be written as:

\mathbf{g}_{(z,w)}=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\

where

A=\frac{1}{y^{2}}\big(1-f+3y^{3}(u^{2}+v^{2})f^{\prime}\big)\mathds{1}_{2\times 2},\qquad\qquad D=\frac{4}{3}y^{3}f^{\prime}\mathds{1}_{2\times 2}\ .

\det(\g_{(z,w)})=\det(AD-BC)

The right hand side of the last equation is always non-zero thanks to the property of the function

(z,w)\in\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{C}

It only remains to prove that

•

∎

Thanks to the expression in coordinates, it is easy to see that
\mathbf{g}
is indeed a pseudo-Riemannian metric on
\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{C}
(hence on
\mathcal{B}_{0}(T^{2})
) since it is negative-definite when restricted to
\{0\}\times\mathbb{C}
and it coincides with
g_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}
on
\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\{0\}
.

In the following we will give an idea on how to compute
\mathbf{g}_{(i,w)}
by using Definition
3.9
and the expression of the tensors in coordinates. Finally, by using the
\SL(2,\mathbb{R})
-invariance we briefly sketch how to compute the tensor
\mathbf{g}
at an arbitrary point of
\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{C}
.

In order to simplify the computation we will give the expression of the associated quadratic form. The first part of the quadratic form in the tensor formalism is
(1-f)\frac{1}{2}\tr(\dot J^2)
, hence at the point
(i,w)
we have:

J=J_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\dot{J}=\mathrm{d}_{i}j(\dot{x},\dot{y})=\begin{pmatrix}\dot{x}&-\dot{y}\\
-\dot{y}&-\dot{x}\end{pmatrix}\ .

Thus,
(1-f)\frac{1}{2}\tr(\dot J^2)=(1-f)\big(\dot{x}^{2}+\dot{y}^{2}\big)
. Moreover, using the expression in coordinates of
(\dot{A}_{0})_{(i,w)}
and
(\dot{A}_{\tr})_{(i,w)}
we get:

\displaystyle(\ast\dot{A}_{0})_{(i,w)}=\begin{pmatrix}\dot{u}+u\dot{y}+v\dot{x}&-u\dot{x}+\dot{v}+v\dot{y}\\
-u\dot{x}+\dot{v}+v\dot{y}&-\dot{u}-u\dot{y}-v\dot{x}\end{pmatrix}\mathrm{d}y_{0}-\begin{pmatrix}\dot{v}+2(v\dot{y}-u\dot{x})&-\dot{u}-2(u\dot{y}+v\dot{x})\\
-\dot{u}-2(u\dot{y}+v\dot{x})&-\dot{v}+2(u\dot{x}-v\dot{y})\end{pmatrix}\mathrm{d}x_{0},

\displaystyle(\ast\dot{A}_{\tr})_{(i,w)}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}-u\dot{y}-v\dot{x}&0\\
0&-u\dot{y}-v\dot{x}\end{pmatrix}\mathrm{d}y_{0}-\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}u\dot{x}-v\dot{y}&0\\
0&u\dot{x}-v\dot{y}\end{pmatrix}\mathrm{d}x_{0}\ .

Hence,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{3}\tr(\dot A_0\wedge\ast\dot A_0)=\frac{4}{3}(\dot{u}^{2}+\dot{v}^{2})+\frac{10}{3}(u^{2}+v^{2})(\dot{x}^{2}+\dot{y}^{2})+4\big(u(\dot{u}\dot{y}-\dot{x}\dot{v})+v(\dot{y}\dot{v}+\dot{u}\dot{x})\big)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{6}\tr(\dot A_{\tr}\wedge\ast\dot A_{\tr})=\frac{1}{3}(u^{2}+v^{2})(\dot{x}^{2}+\dot{y}^{2})\ .

The final expression for the quadratic form associated with
\mathbf{g}
and computed at
(i,w)
is thus

(3.14)

\big(1-f+3f^{\prime}(u^{2}+v^{2})\big)\big(\dot{x}^{2}+\dot{y}^{2}\big)+\frac{4}{3}f^{\prime}(\dot{u}^{2}+\dot{v}^{2})+4f^{\prime}\big(u(\dot{u}\dot{y}-\dot{x}\dot{v})+v(\dot{y}\dot{v}+\dot{u}\dot{x})\big)

It is now clear how to recover the above expression of
\mathbf{g}_{(i,w)}
from (
3.14
). In order to give the precise expression of
\mathbf{g}
at an arbitrary point
(z,\widetilde{w})\in\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{C}
we need to use the
\SL(2,\mathbb{R})
-invariance of
\mathbf{g}
and the fact that the
\SL(2,\mathbb{R})
-action on
\mathbb{H}^{2}
is transitive. In fact, we can find a
P\in\SL(2,\mathbb{R})
such that
P\cdot z=i
for
z\in\mathbb{H}^{2}
, where
P\cdot z
is the action via Möbius transformations. This matrix
P
is explicitly given by

P=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{\sqrt{y}}&-\frac{x}{\sqrt{y}}\\
0&\sqrt{y}\end{pmatrix}\ .

In particular, the point
\widetilde{w}=\widetilde{u}+i\widetilde{v}\in\mathbb{C}
is determined by
P\cdot(z,\widetilde{w})=(i,w)
. In fact,

(3.15)

u^{2}+v^{2}=y^{3}(\widetilde{u}^{2}+\widetilde{v}^{2})\ .

By using the
\SL(2,\mathbb{R})
-invariance we get

\mathbf{g}_{(z,\widetilde{w})}(\cdot,\cdot)=(P^{*}\mathbf{g})_{(z,\widetilde{w})}(\cdot,\cdot)=\mathbf{g}_{(i,w)}(\mathrm{d}_{(z,\widetilde{w})}P\cdot,\mathrm{d}_{(z,\widetilde{w})}P\cdot),

where the differential of
P
at
(z,\widetilde{w})
is given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{(z,\widetilde{w})}P\bigg(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\bigg)=\frac{1}{y}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\quad

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{(z,\widetilde{w})}P\bigg(\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{y}\frac{\partial}{\partial y}

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{(z,\widetilde{w})}P\bigg(\frac{\partial}{\partial u}\bigg)=y^{\frac{3}{2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial u}\quad

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{(z,\widetilde{w})}P\bigg(\frac{\partial}{\partial v}\bigg)

\displaystyle=y^{\frac{3}{2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial v}\ .

Now we have all the tools to compute
\mathbf{g}
at a point
(z,\widetilde{w})
. For instance,

\displaystyle\mathbf{g}_{(z,\widetilde{w})}\bigg(\frac{\partial}{\partial x},\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{g}_{(i,w)}\bigg(\mathrm{d}_{(z,\widetilde{w})}P\bigg(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\bigg),\mathrm{d}_{(z,\widetilde{w})}P\bigg(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\bigg)\bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{y^{2}}\mathbf{g}_{(i,w)}\bigg(\frac{\partial}{\partial x},\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{y^{2}}\bigg(1-f+3f^{\prime}(u^{2}+v^{2})\bigg)

(Equation
3.15
)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{y^{2}}\bigg(1-f+3y^{3}f^{\prime}(\widetilde{u}^{2}+\widetilde{v}^{2})\bigg)\

With a similar computation one can recover all the entries of the tensor
\mathbf{g}
at every
(z,w)\in\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{C}
.

3.2.
The circle action and the
\SL(2,\mathbb{R})
-moment map

In this section we study the behavior of the circle and
\SL(2,\mathbb{R})
-action on
\mathcal{B}_{0}(T^{2})
with respect to the pseudo-Kähler structure
(\mathbf{g},\mathbf{I},\boldsymbol{\omega})
. Before analyzing the two actions in detail let us recall some basic definitions regarding symplectic actions and moment maps.

Definition 3.20
.

Let
(M,\omega)
be a symplectic manifold and let
G
be a Lie group acting on
M
. Let
\psi_{g}:M\to M
be the map
\psi_{g}(p):=g\cdot p
, then we say the group
G
acts by symplectomorphisms on
(M,\omega)
if
\psi_{g}^{*}\omega=\omega
for all
g\in G
.

Definition 3.21
.

Let
G
be a Lie group, with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
, acting on a symplectic manifold
(M,\omega)
by symplectomorphisms. We say the action is
Hamiltonian
if there exists a smooth function
\mu:M\to\mathfrak{g}^{*}
satisfying the following properties:

(i)

The function
\mu
is equivariant with respect to the
G
-action on
M
and the co-adjoint action on
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
, namely

(3.16)

\mu_{g\cdot p}=\Ad^{*}(g)(\mu_{p}):=\mu_{p}\circ\Ad(g^{-1})\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}\ .

(ii)

Given
\xi\in\mathfrak{g}
, let
X_{\xi}
be the vector field on
M
generating the action of the
1
-parameter subgroup generated by
\xi
, i.e.
X_{\xi}=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\text{exp}(t\xi)\cdot p|_{t=0}
. Then, for every
\xi\in\mathfrak{g}
we have

(3.17)

\mathrm{d}\mu^{\xi}=\iota_{X_{\xi}}\omega=\omega(X_{\xi},\cdot)

where
\mu^{\xi}:M\to\mathbb{R}
is the function
\mu^{\xi}(p):=\mu_{p}(\xi)
.

A map
\mu
satisfying the two properties above is called a
moment map
for the Hamiltonian action.

Let us start with the study of the
S^{1}
-action on
\mathcal{B}_{0}(T^{2})
. First of all, we need to understand how the circle action
q\mapsto e^{-i\theta}q
on
Q^{3}_{0}(\mathcal{T}^{c}(T^{2}))
changes under the bijection with
D^{3}(\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))
(see Corollary
3.3
). In other words, if
C
is the Pick tensor associated with the
J
-holomorphic cubic differential
q
, namely
C=\mathcal{R}e(q)
, then we need to find the expression of the new Pick form
\widetilde{A}
associated with
\widetilde{C}=\mathcal{R}e(e^{-i\theta}q)
. We have
q=C(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)-iC(\cdot,J\cdot,\cdot)
since
(J,C)\in D^{3}(\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))
by definition. In particular, the expression

e^{-i\theta}q=\cos\theta C(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)+\sin\theta C(\cdot,J\cdot,\cdot)+i\bigg(\cos\theta C(\cdot,J\cdot,\cdot)-\sin\theta C(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)\bigg)

implies that
\widetilde{C}(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)=\cos\theta C(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)+\sin\theta C(\cdot,J\cdot,\cdot)
and the new Pick form is

\widetilde{A}(\cdot)=g_{J}^{-1}\widetilde{C}=\cos\theta A(\cdot)-\sin\theta A(\cdot)J\ .

The last equation gives an induced action on
D^{3}(\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))
by setting

\displaystyle\Psi_{\theta}\ :

\displaystyle\ D^{3}(\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))\longrightarrow D^{3}(\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))

\displaystyle(J,A)\mapsto(J,\cos\theta A(\cdot)-\sin\theta A(\cdot)J)\ .\

It is clear from the definition that
\Psi_{\theta}
preserves the
0
-section in
D^{3}(\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))
(seen as a vector bundle over
\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
), hence it induces an
S^{1}
-action on
\mathcal{B}_{0}(T^{2})
which will still be denoted by
\Psi_{\theta}
by abuse of notation. Before stating and proving the main result, we need a technical lemma regarding the derivative of the norm of the Pick form.

Lemma 3.22
.

Let
(J,A)\in D^{3}(\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))
, then

(3.18)

\big(||A||_{J}^{2}\big)^{\prime}=2\langle A,\dot{A}_{0}\rangle\ .

Proof.

During the proof of this lemma we use the notation of the previous section, namely
A=A_{1}e_{1}^{*}+A_{2}e_{2}^{*}
and
\dot{A}=\dot{A}_{1}e_{1}^{*}+\dot{A}_{2}e_{2}^{*}
, with
\{e_{1},e_{2}\}
a
g_{J}
-orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
\{e_{1}^{*},e_{2}^{*}\}
its dual basis. Recall that the relation between the Pick form
A
and the Pick tensor
C
is
A=g_{J}^{-1}C
, hence

\displaystyle A^{\prime}

\displaystyle=(g_{J}^{-1}C)^{\prime}

\displaystyle=-g_{j}^{-1}\dot{g}_{J}g_{J}^{-1}C+g_{J}^{-1}\dot{C}

(
\dot{g}_{J}(\cdot,\cdot)=-g_{J}(\cdot,J\dot{J}\cdot)
)

\displaystyle=J\dot{J}A+\dot{A}

In particular,
(A_{i})^{\prime}=J\dot{J}A_{i}+\dot{A}_{i}
, for each
i=1,2
. Thus,

\displaystyle\big(||A||_{J}^{2}\big)^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\int_{T^{2}}\bigg(\tr(A'\wedge\ast A)+\tr(A\wedge\ast A')\bigg)

\displaystyle=\int_{T^{2}}\bigg(2\tr(A_1\dot A_1+A_2\dot A_2)+\tr(J\dot J((A_1)^2+(A_2)^2)+A_1J\dot JA_1+A_2J\dot JA_2)\bigg)e_{1}^{*}\wedge e_{2}^{*}

\displaystyle=2\int_{T^{2}}\tr(A_1\dot A_1+A_2\dot A_2)e_{1}^{*}\wedge e_{2}^{*}\ ,

where in the second line we used the fact that, since both
J\dot{J}A_{i}A_{i}
and
A_{i}J\dot{J}A_{i}
anticommute with
J
for each
i=1,2
, the terms
\tr(J\dot JA_iA_i)=\tr(A_iJ\dot JA_i)
vanish for each
i=1,2
. Thus, we get

\big(||A||_{J}^{2}\big)^{\prime}=2\langle A,\dot{A}\rangle\ .

Finally, by writing
\dot{A}=\dot{A}_{0}+\dot{A}_{\tr}
where

\dot{A}_{\tr}=\frac{1}{2}\tr(\dot A_1)\mathds{1}e_{1}^{*}+\frac{1}{2}\tr(\dot A_2)\mathds{1}e_{2}^{*}

we obtain

\langle A,\dot{A}_{\tr}\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{T^{2}}\tr(\tr(\dot A_1)A_1+\tr(\dot A_2)A_2)e_{1}^{*}\wedge e_{2}^{*}

and this last term is equal to zero since the
A_{i}
’s are trace-less endomorphisms.
∎

Theorem B
.

The
S^{1}
-action on
\mathcal{B}_{0}(T^{2})
is Hamiltonian with respect to
\boldsymbol{\omega}
and it satisfies

\Psi_{\theta}^{*}\boldsymbol{\omega}=\boldsymbol{\omega}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Psi_{\theta}^{*}\mathbf{g}=\mathbf{g}\ .

The Hamiltonian function is given by
H\big(||A||_{0}^{2}\big)=\frac{2}{3}f\big(||A||_{0}^{2}\big).

Proof.

The infinitesimal generator of the action is

X_{(J,A)}=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\theta}_{\big|_{\theta=0}}\Psi_{\theta}(J,A)=(0,-AJ).

Hence,

\displaystyle(\iota_{X}\boldsymbol{\omega})_{(J,A)}(\dot{J},\dot{A})

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\omega}_{(J,A)}\big((\dot{J},\dot{A}),(0,AJ)\big)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{g}_{(J,A)}\big((\dot{J},\dot{A}),\mathbf{I}(0,AJ)\big)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{g}_{(J,A)}\big((\dot{J},\dot{A}),(0,-AJ^{2})\big)

(
A\ \text{is}\ g_{J}-\text{traceless}
)

\displaystyle=\frac{f^{\prime}}{3}\langle\dot{A}_{0},A\rangle_{J}\

Now we compute the differential of
H\big(||A||_{J}^{2}\big)=\frac{2}{3}f\big(||A||_{0}^{2}\big)
. This is given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{(J,A)}H(\dot{J},\dot{A})

\displaystyle=\frac{f^{\prime}}{6}\big(||A||_{J}^{2}\big)^{\prime}

(
Lemma

3.22
)

\displaystyle=\frac{f^{\prime}}{3}\langle A,\dot{A}_{0}\rangle_{J}

Thus, the
S^{1}
-action is Hamiltonian. The symplectic form
\boldsymbol{\omega}
is preserved by this action since its Lie derivative along the infinitesimal generator
X
is zero:

\mathcal{L}_{X}\boldsymbol{\omega}=\mathrm{d}(\iota_{X}\boldsymbol{\omega})+\iota_{X}\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\omega}=\mathrm{d}^{2}H=0

by Cartan’s magic formula.

It only remains to prove that
\Psi_{\theta}
is an isometry for
\mathbf{g}
. First of all we compute the differential of the action:

\mathrm{d}_{(J,A)}\Psi_{\theta}(\dot{J},\dot{A})=\big(\dot{J},\cos\theta\dot{A}(\cdot)-\sin\theta(\dot{A}(\cdot)J+A(\cdot)\dot{J})\big)\ .

Then, we notice that the circle action preserves the norm of the Pick form, namely
||\cos\theta A-\sin\theta AJ||^{2}_{J}=||A||_{J}^{2}
. In fact,

(3.19)

||\cos\theta A-\sin\theta AJ||^{2}_{J}=\cos^{2}\theta||A||_{J}^{2}+\sin^{2}\theta\underbrace{||AJ||_{J}^{2}}_{(a)}-2\cos\theta\sin\theta\underbrace{\langle A,AJ\rangle_{J}}_{(b)}\ .

The term
(a)
is

\displaystyle||AJ||_{J}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{T^{2}}\tr(A_1JA_1J+A_2JA_2J)\ e_{1}^{*}\wedge e_{2}^{*}

(
A_{i}\in T_{J}\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\ \text{and}\ J^{2}=-\mathds{1}
)

\displaystyle=\int_{T^{2}}\tr(A_1A_1+A_2A_2)\ e_{1}^{*}\wedge e_{2}^{*}

\displaystyle=||A||_{J}^{2}\ .

The term
(b)
is

\displaystyle\langle A,AJ\rangle_{J}=\int_{T^{2}}\tr(A_1A_1J+A_2A_2J)\ e_{1}^{*}\wedge e_{2}^{*}

but
\tr(A_iJA_i)=\tr(JA_iA_i)=\tr(A_iA_iJ)=-\tr(A_iJA_i)
for
i=1,2
, hence the term
(b)
is zero. In the first two equalities we used the trace symmetry and in the third one the fact that
A_{i}\in T_{J}\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. The circle action
\Psi_{\theta}
preserves the pseudo-Riemannian metric
\mathbf{g}
if and only if the following holds

\mathbf{g}_{(J,A)}\big((\dot{J},\dot{A});(\dot{J}^{\prime},\dot{A}^{\prime})\big)=\mathbf{g}_{\Psi_{\theta}(J,A)}\big(\mathrm{d}_{(J,A)}\Psi_{\theta}(\dot{J},\dot{A});\mathrm{d}_{(J,A)}\Psi_{\theta}(\dot{J}^{\prime},\dot{A}^{\prime})\big)\ .

Let us define
\psi_{\theta}
to be the second component of the differential of the circle action, namely

\psi_{\theta}(\dot{J},\dot{A}):=\cos\theta\dot{A}-\sin\theta(\dot{A}J+A\dot{J}).

Then, in order to conclude the proof, we need to show

(1)

\langle\psi_{\theta}(\dot{J},\dot{A})_{0},\psi_{\theta}(\dot{J}^{\prime},\dot{A}^{\prime})_{0}\rangle=\langle\dot{A}_{0},\dot{A}_{0}^{\prime}\rangle;

(2)

\langle\psi_{\theta}(\dot{J},\dot{A})_{\tr},\psi_{\theta}(\dot{J}^{\prime},\dot{A}^{\prime})_{\tr}\rangle=\langle\dot{A}_{\tr},\dot{A}_{\tr}^{\prime}\rangle.

The left hand side term of
(1)
can be written as

\displaystyle\cos^{2}\theta\langle\dot{A}_{0},\dot{A}_{0}^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle+\sin^{2}\theta\langle(\dot{A}J+A\dot{J})_{0},(\dot{A}^{\prime}J^{\prime}+A^{\prime}\dot{J}^{\prime})_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle+\cos\theta\sin\theta\big(\langle\dot{A}_{0},-(\dot{A}^{\prime}J+A\dot{J}^{\prime})_{0}\rangle+\langle-(\dot{A}J+A\dot{J})_{0},\dot{A}_{0}^{\prime}\rangle\big)\ .

The coefficient of
\sin^{2}\theta
has already been calculated (see proof of Theorem
3.13
) and it is equal to
\langle\dot{A}_{0},\dot{A}_{0}^{\prime}\rangle
. The coefficient of
\cos\theta\sin\theta
is equal to

\displaystyle\int_{T^{2}}\tr(-(\dot A_1)_0(\dot A_1')_0J-(\dot A_2)_0(\dot A_2')_0J-(\dot A_1)_0J(\dot A_1')_0-(\dot A_2)_0J(\dot A_2')_0)\ e_{1}^{*}\wedge e_{2}^{*}

and it vanishes since
(\dot{A}_{i})_{0},(\dot{A}_{i}^{\prime})_{0}\in T_{J}\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
for each
i=1,2
.

The left hand side term of
(2)
can be written as

\displaystyle\cos^{2}\theta\langle\dot{A}_{\tr},\dot{A}_{\tr}^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle+\sin^{2}\theta\langle(\dot{A}J+A\dot{J})_{\tr},(\dot{A}^{\prime}J^{\prime}+A^{\prime}\dot{J}^{\prime})_{\tr}\rangle

\displaystyle+\cos\theta\sin\theta\big(\langle\dot{A}_{\tr},-(\dot{A}^{\prime}J+A\dot{J}^{\prime})_{\tr}\rangle+\langle-(\dot{A}J+A\dot{J})_{\tr},\dot{A}_{\tr}^{\prime}\rangle\big)\ .

The coefficient of
\sin^{2}\theta
has already been calculated (see proof of Theorem
3.13
) and it is equal to
\langle\dot{A}_{\tr},\dot{A}_{\tr}^{\prime}\rangle
. By using Lemma
3.12
, the coefficient of
\cos\theta\sin\theta
can be written as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{T^{2}}\tr(-(\dot A_1)_{\tr}\tr(\dot A_2')\dsone+(\dot A_2)_{\tr}\tr(\dot A_1')\dsone-(\dot A_1')_{\tr}\tr(\dot A_2)\dsone+(\dot A_2')_{\tr}\tr(\dot A_1)\dsone)e_{1}^{*}\wedge e_{2}^{*}\ .

Since
(\dot{A}_{i})_{\tr}=\frac{1}{2}\tr(\dot A_i)\mathds{1}
and
(\dot{A}_{i}^{\prime})_{\tr}=\frac{1}{2}\tr(\dot A_i')\mathds{1}
for each
i=1,2
, the first term of the above expression cancels out with the last one and the same happens for the second and third one. Finally, the term of
\cos\theta\sin\theta
vanishes and we obtain the thesis.
∎

Now we will study the
\SL(2,\mathbb{R})
-action and its moment map. Recall that if
P\in\SL(2,\mathbb{R})
and
(J,A)\in D^{3}(\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))
, then

P\cdot(J,A)=(PJP^{-1},PA(P^{-1}\cdot)P^{-1})\ .

In particular, this action preserves the
0
-section in
D^{3}(\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))
(seen as a vector bundle over
\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
), hence it induces an
\SL(2,\mathbb{R})
-action on
\mathcal{B}_{0}(T^{2})
, which will be denoted by
\Phi_{P}:\mathcal{B}_{0}(T^{2})\to\mathcal{B}_{0}(T^{2})
. Thanks to Lemma
3.8
, it is clear that
\Phi_{P}^{*}\boldsymbol{\omega}=\boldsymbol{\omega}
, i.e.
\SL(2,\mathbb{R})
acts by symplectomorphisms on
\mathcal{B}_{0}(T^{2})
. Thus, it makes sense to ask if this action is Hamiltonian and, if this is the case, to give the expression of the moment map. The Lie algebra of
\SL(2,\mathbb{R})
is given by
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})=\{X\in\End(\mathbb{R}^{2})\ |\ \tr(X)=0\}
with Lie bracket
[X,Y]=XY-YX
. In particular any
X\in\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})
can be decomposed as
X=X^{a}+X^{s}
, where
X^{s}
is a trace-less
g_{J}
-symmetric matrix and
X^{a}
is a trace-less
g_{J}
-skew-symmetric matrix. In particular,
X^{s}\in T_{J}(\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))
and
X^{a}=-\frac{1}{2}\tr(JX)J
, since it commutes with
J
.

Theorem C
.

The
\SL(2,\mathbb{R})
-action on
\mathcal{B}_{0}(T^{2})
is Hamiltonian with respect to
\boldsymbol{\omega}
with moment map
\mu:\mathcal{B}_{0}(T^{2})\to\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})^{*}
given by

(3.20)

\mu_{(J,A)}(X)=\bigg(1-f\big(||A||_{0}^{2}\big)\bigg)\tr(JX)

for all
X\in\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})
.

Proof.

\bullet

The infinitesimal generator of the action
. Let
X\in\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})
and let

V_{X}(J,A)=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(e^{tX}Je^{-tX},(e^{-tX})^{*}C)|_{t=0}

be its infinitesimal generator. The first component is equal to
XJ-JX=[X,J]
. For the second component define
P_{t}:=e^{tX}
, then we need to compute the Pick form corresponding to

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}C((P_{t})^{-1}\cdot,(P_{t})^{-1}\cdot,(P_{t})^{-1}\cdot)|_{t=0}=-C(X\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)-C(\cdot,X\cdot,\cdot)-C(\cdot,\cdot,X\cdot)\ .

If
\widetilde{C}(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)
is defined as the right hand side term of the equation above, then the new Pick form
\widetilde{A}
satisfies

\displaystyle g_{J}(\widetilde{A}(Y)Z,W)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{C}(Y,Z,W)

\displaystyle=-C(X\cdot Y,Z,W)-C(Y,X\cdot Z,W)-C(Y,Z,X\cdot W)

\displaystyle=-g_{J}(A(X\cdot Y)Z,W)-g_{J}(A(Y)X\cdot Z,W)-g_{J}(A(Y)Z,X\cdot W)

\displaystyle=-g_{J}(A(X\cdot Y)Z+A(Y)X\cdot Z+X^{*}\cdot A(Y)Z,W)

for all
Y,Z,W\in\Gamma(T\mathbb{R}^{2})
, where
X^{*}
denotes the adjoint of
X
with respect to
g_{J}
. Hence, we have

\widetilde{A}(\cdot)=-A(X\cdot)-AX-X^{*}A\ .

By using the decomposition
X=X^{s}+X^{a}
in its symmetric and skew-symmetric part, we can write the second component of
V_{X}(J,A)
as:

(3.21)

\underbrace{-A(X^{a}\cdot)-A(X^{s}\cdot)+[X^{a},A]}_{\text{trace-less part}}-\underbrace{(AX^{s}+X^{s}A)}_{\text{trace part}}\ .

\bullet

\mu

is equivariant
. Let
P\in\SL(2,\mathbb{R})
and
X\in\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})
, then

\displaystyle\mu_{P\cdot(J,A)}(X)

\displaystyle=\bigg(1-f\bigg(\frac{1}{4}||P\cdot A||_{P\cdot J}^{2}\bigg)\bigg)\tr(PJP^{-1}X)

\displaystyle=\bigg(1-f\big(||A||_{0}^{2}\big)\bigg)\tr(JP^{-1}XP)

\displaystyle=\mu_{(J,A)}\circ\Ad(P^{-1})(X)

\displaystyle=\Ad^{*}(P)(\mu_{(J,A)})(X)

where in the second equality we used Lemma
3.8
and the trace symmetry.

\bullet

\mu

satisfies property (ii) in Definition

3.21
.
Let
\mu^{X}:\mathcal{B}_{0}(T^{2})\to\mathbb{R}
be the map

\mu^{X}(J,A)=\bigg(1-f\big(||A||_{0}^{2}\big)\bigg)\tr(JX)\ ,

then

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{(J,A)}\mu^{X}(\dot{J},\dot{A})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4}\bigg(||A||^{2}_{J}\bigg)^{\prime}f^{\prime}\big(||A||_{0}^{2}\big)\tr(JX)+\bigg(1-f\big(||A||_{0}^{2}\big)\bigg)\tr(\dot JX)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\langle A,\dot{A}_{0}\rangle f^{\prime}\big(||A||_{0}^{2}\big)\tr(JX)+\bigg(1-f\big(||A||_{0}^{2}\big)\bigg)\tr(\dot JX)

where we used Lemma
3.22
in the second equality. Now let
V_{X}
be the infinitesimal generator of
X
, then

(3.22)

\displaystyle\iota_{V_{X}}\boldsymbol{\omega}(\dot{J},\dot{A})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{g}(V_{X}(J,A),\mathbf{I}(\dot{J},\dot{A}))

\displaystyle=\frac{f-1}{2}\tr([X,J]J\dot J)+\frac{f^{\prime}}{3}\langle[X^{a},A]-A(X\cdot),(-\dot{A}J-A\dot{J})_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle-\frac{f^{\prime}}{6}\langle AX^{s}+X^{s}A,(\dot{A}J+A\dot{J})_{\tr}\rangle

where we used the decomposition in (
3.21
). The first term of
\iota_{V_{X}}\boldsymbol{\omega}(\dot{J},\dot{A})
is

\displaystyle\frac{1-f}{2}\tr(\dot JX+JXJ\dot J)=(1-f)\tr(\dot J X)

by trace symmetry and
\dot{J}J+J\dot{J}=0
. It only remains to show that the sum of the second and third term of
\iota_{V_{X}}\boldsymbol{\omega}(\dot{J},\dot{A})
is equal to
-\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime}\langle A,\dot{A}_{0}\rangle\tr(JX)
. The coefficient of
\frac{f^{\prime}}{3}
in (
3.22
) can be written as

\underbrace{\langle A(X^{s}\cdot),(\dot{A}J+A\dot{J})_{0}\rangle}_{(a)}+\underbrace{\langle[X^{a},A]-A(X^{a}\cdot),(-\dot{A}J-A\dot{J})_{0}\rangle}_{(b)}\ .

Moreover, by using Lemma
3.12
the term with
[X^{a},A]
in
(b)
becomes

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{T^{2}}\tr(JX)\tr(JA_1(\dot A_1)_0J+JA_2(\dot A_2)_0J-A_1J(\dot A_1)_0J-A_2J(\dot A_2)_0J)e_{1}^{*}\wedge e_{2}^{*}\ .

Using that
A_{i},(\dot{A}_{i})\in T_{J}\mathcal{J}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
for each
i=1,2
, the above term reduces to
-\tr(JX)\langle A,\dot{A}_{0}\rangle
. Notice that
-A(X^{a}\cdot)=\frac{1}{2}\tr(JX)A(\cdot)J
, since
C(J\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)=C(\cdot,J\cdot,\cdot)
. Hence, the term with
-A(X^{a}\cdot)
in
(b)
becomes

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\int_{T^{2}}\tr(JX)\tr(A_1J(\dot A_1)_0J+A_2J(\dot A_2)_0J)e_{1}^{*}\wedge e_{2}^{*}=-\frac{1}{2}\tr(JX)\langle A,\dot{A}_{0}\rangle\ .

Finally, the term
(b)
multiplied by
\frac{f^{\prime}}{3}
is equal to

-\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime}\tr(JX)\langle A,\dot{A}_{0}\rangle\ .

Hence, it only remains to show that

(3.23)

\frac{f^{\prime}}{3}\underbrace{\langle A(X^{s}\cdot),(\dot{A}J+A\dot{J})_{0}\rangle}_{(a)}-\frac{f^{\prime}}{6}\underbrace{\langle AX^{s}+X^{s}A,(\dot{A}J+A\dot{J})_{\tr}\rangle}_{(c)}=0\ .

To do so, we will use a basis of
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})
, namely we can write
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})=\Span_{\mathbb{R}}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\xi_{3}\}
where

\xi_{1}=J_{0},\qquad\xi_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix},\qquad\xi_{3}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\ .

The only symmetric matrices of this basis are
\xi_{2}
and
\xi_{3}
, hence it is sufficient to prove equation (
3.23
) when
X^{s}=\xi_{2}
and
X^{s}=\xi_{3}
, since all the elements are linear in
X\in\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})
. In both cases we use the description in coordinates
z=x+iy
for
\mathbb{H}^{2}
and
w=u+iv
for
\mathbb{C}
, of the Pick form
A
and its variation
\dot{A}
, as we did at the end of Section 3.1. In particular we can do the computation in
(z,w)=(i,w)
by
\SL(2,\mathbb{R})
-invariance.

(i)

X^{s}=\xi_{2}
.

In this case if
\{\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{0}},\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{0}}\}
is a
g_{J_{0}}
-orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, then
X^{s}\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{0}}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{0}}
and
X^{s}\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{0}}=-\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{0}}
, hence
A(X^{s}\cdot)=A_{1}\mathrm{d}x_{0}-A_{2}\mathrm{d}y_{0}
.
In particular,

\displaystyle\tr(A_1(\dot A_1)_0J_0)

\displaystyle=2(-|w|^{2}\dot{x}-v\dot{u}+u\dot{v})

\displaystyle\tr(A_2(\dot A_2)_0J_0)

\displaystyle=2(-2|w|^{2}\dot{x}+u\dot{v}-v\dot{u})\ .

Hence,

\displaystyle\frac{f^{\prime}}{3}\langle A(X^{s}\cdot),(\dot{A}J+A\dot{J})_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{f^{\prime}}{3}\int_{T^{2}}\tr(A_1(\dot A_1)_0J-A_2(\dot A_2)_0J)\mathrm{d}x_{0}\wedge\mathrm{d}y_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{3}f^{\prime}|w|^{2}\dot{x}\ .

On the other hand, since

\displaystyle\tr(\dot A_1)=-2(u\dot{y}+v\dot{x})\qquad\tr(A_1X^s)=2u

\displaystyle\tr(\dot A_2)=2(u\dot{x}-v\dot{y})\qquad\tr(A_2X^s)=2v,

we get

\displaystyle-\frac{f^{\prime}}{6}\langle AX^{s}+

\displaystyle X^{s}A,(\dot{A}J+A\dot{J})_{\tr}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\frac{f^{\prime}}{6}\int_{T^{2}}\bigg(\tr(A_1X^s)\tr(\dot A_2)-\tr(A_2X^s)\tr(\dot A_1)\bigg)\mathrm{d}x_{0}\wedge\mathrm{d}y_{0}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{3}f^{\prime}|w|^{2}\dot{x}

and equation (
3.23
) is clearly satisfied.

(ii)

X^{s}=\xi_{3}.

With the same notation as in case
(i)
we have
X^{s}\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{0}}=\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{0}}
and
X^{s}\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{0}}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{0}}
, hence
A(X^{s}\cdot)=A_{2}\mathrm{d}x_{0}+A_{1}\mathrm{d}y_{0}
. Using that

\displaystyle(\dot{A}_{1})_{0}+(\dot{A}_{2})_{0}J=\begin{pmatrix}-u\dot{y}-v\dot{x}&u\dot{x}-v\dot{y}\\
u\dot{x}-v\dot{y}&u\dot{y}+v\dot{x}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle\tr(A_1\big((\dot A_1)_0+(\dot A_2)_0J\big))=-2|w|^{2}\dot{y}

the term
(a)
multiplied by
\frac{f^{\prime}}{3}
is

\displaystyle\frac{f^{\prime}}{3}\langle A(X^{s}\cdot),(\dot{A}J+A\dot{J})_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{f^{\prime}}{3}\int_{T^{2}}\tr(A_1\big((\dot A_1)_0+(\dot A_2)_0J\big))\mathrm{d}x_{0}\wedge\mathrm{d}y_{0}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{3}f^{\prime}|w|^{2}\dot{y}\ .

On the other hand, since

\displaystyle\tr(\dot A_1)=-2(u\dot{y}+v\dot{x})\qquad\tr(A_1X^s)=2v

\displaystyle\tr(\dot A_2)=2(u\dot{x}-v\dot{y})\qquad\tr(A_2X^s)=-2u,

the term
(c)
multiplied by
-\frac{f^{\prime}}{6}
is

\displaystyle-\frac{f^{\prime}}{6}\int_{T^{2}}\bigg(\tr(A_1X^s)\tr(\dot A_2)-\tr(A_2X^s)\tr(\dot A_1)\bigg)\mathrm{d}x_{0}\wedge\mathrm{d}y_{0}=\frac{2}{3}f^{\prime}|w|^{2}\dot{y}\ .

Thus, equation (
3.23
) is proved and the theorem as well.
∎
