Thresholds for patterns in random permutations with a given number of inversions
David Bevan, Dan Threlfall

Abstract
We explore how the asymptotic structure of a random permutation of [ n ] [n] with m m inversions evolves, as m m increases, establishing thresholds for the appearance and disappearance of any classical, consecutive or vincular pattern. The threshold for the appearance of a classical pattern depends on the greatest number of inversions in any of its sum indecomposable components.

Introduction

We consider permutations
from an evolutionary perspective, in an analogous manner to the Gilbert–Erdős–Rényi random graph
[
28
,
29
,
32
]
.
Our model, which we call the
uniform random permutation
, and denote
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n,m}
, is a permutation drawn uniformly from the set of permutations of
[n]
with exactly
m
inversions.
We are interested in how, for large
n
, the structure of
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n,m}
evolves as the number of its inversions
m
increases from zero to
\binom{n}{2}
.
Specifically, for any classical, consecutive or vincular pattern
\pi
, we establish thresholds for the
appearance and disappearance of
\pi
in
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n,m}
.
These results build on our previous work
[
11
]
on thresholds for patterns in random compositions.

A
permutation
or
n-permutation
is considered to be simply an arrangement of the numbers
[n]:=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
for some positive
n
.
Let
\mathcal{S}_{n}
denote the set of all
n
-permutations.
We often display an
n
-permutation
\sigma
using its
plot
, the set of points
(i,\sigma(i))
in the Euclidean plane, for
i=1,\dots,n
.
Sometimes we identify a permutation with its plot.
If
\sigma
is an
n
-permutation, we define its
complement
, denoted
\overline{\sigma}
, to be the permutation such that
\overline{\sigma}(i)=n+1-\sigma(i)
for every
i\in[n]
.
Thus the plot of
\overline{\sigma}
is the reflection of the plot of
\sigma
about a horizontal axis.
See Figure
1
for the plots of an
8
-permutation and its complement.

Figure 1:
The permutation
\sigma=23175468
and its complement
\overline{\sigma}=76824531

An
inversion
in a permutation
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
is a pair
i,j\in[n]
such that
i<j
and
\sigma(i)>\sigma(j)
.
We use
\inv(\sigma)
to denote the total number of inversions in the permutation
\sigma
,
and use

\mathcal{S}_{n,m}\mathchoice{\;=\;}{=}{=}{=}\{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{n}\>:\>\inv(\sigma)=m\}

to denote the set of
n
-permutations with exactly
m
inversions.
Thus
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n,m}
is a permutation chosen uniformly from
\mathcal{S}_{n,m}
.
The greatest possible number of inversions that can occur in an
n
-permutation is
\binom{n}{2}
.
Note that
\inv(\overline{\sigma})=\binom{n}{2}-\inv(\sigma)
.
Thus,
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n,\binom{n}{2}-m}
has the same distribution as
\overline{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n,m}}
.

We consider three different forms of permutation pattern containment.
For a very brief introduction to permutation patterns, see
[
7
]
; for more extended expositions, see either Bóna
[
15
]
or Kitaev
[
39
]
.

A
k
-permutation
\pi
occurs as a
consecutive pattern
at position
j
in a permutation
\sigma
if the consecutive subsequence
\sigma(j)\ldots\sigma(j+k-1)
has the same relative ordering as
\pi
.
For example, the consecutive pattern
132
occurs three times in the permutation at the left of Figure
1
, at positions 1, 3 and 7.
A permutation that doesn’t contain a pattern is said to
avoid
it.
See
[
25
,
26
,
27
]
for investigations of permutations avoiding consecutive patterns.

A permutation
\pi
occurs as a
classical pattern
in
\sigma
if
\sigma
has a (not necessarily consecutive) subsequence whose terms have the same relative ordering as
\pi
.
For example, the classical pattern
312
occurs twice in the permutation at the left of Figure
1
,
one occurrence consisting of the points
at positions 4, 5 and 7, and the other consisting of the points
at positions 4, 6 and 7.
For an extensive survey on classical patterns, see Vatter
[
48
]
.

Finally, in a
vincular pattern
(see
[
2
,
5
,
10
,
17
,
18
,
24
,
35
]
) only some terms are required to be adjacent.
Consecutive terms in a vincular pattern that must be adjacent are underlined.
For example, the vincular patterns
\underline{31}2
and
3\underline{12}
each occur once in the permutation at the left of Figure
1
,
the former consisting of the points
at positions 4, 5 and 7, and the latter consisting of the points
at positions 4, 6 and 7.

We take a dynamic (or evolutionary) view by considering a process on
n
-permutations, namely a sequence of permutations
\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\dots,\sigma_{\binom{n}{2}}
, where
\sigma_{t+1}
is obtained from
\sigma_{t}
, by the addition of one inversion.
In this context, a striking phenomenon is the abrupt appearance and disappearance of substructures.
To quantify this, we introduce the concept of a threshold.
A function
m^{\star}=m^{\star}(n)
is a threshold in
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n,m}
for
(the appearance of)
a property
\mathcal{Q}
of permutations if

\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\big[\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n,m}\text{ satisfies }\mathcal{Q}\big]\mathchoice{\;=\;}{=}{=}{=}\begin{cases}0&\text{if~ $m\ll m^{\star}$,}\\
1&\text{if~ $m^{\star}\ll m\ll m^{+}$,}\end{cases}

for some function
m^{+}\gg m^{\star}
,
where, throughout this work,
we write
f\ll g
to denote that
\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}f/g=0
.
We also write
f\sim g
if
\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}f/g=1
.

We say that a property holds
asymptotically almost surely
(a.a.s) if asymptotically the probability that it holds equals
1
.
Thus, above its threshold, a.a.s.
\mathcal{Q}
holds,
whereas below its threshold, a.a.s.
\mathcal{Q}
does not hold.

With a slight abuse of terminology, we also say that
\binom{n}{2}-m\sim m^{\star}
is a threshold in
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n,m}
for
the
disappearance
of
a property
\mathcal{Q}
if

\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\big[\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n,m}\text{ satisfies }\mathcal{Q}\big]\mathchoice{\;=\;}{=}{=}{=}\begin{cases}1&\text{if~ $m^{\star}\ll\binom{n}{2}-m\ll m^{+}$,}\\
0&\text{if~ $\binom{n}{2}-m\ll m^{\star}$,}\end{cases}

for some function
m^{+}\gg m^{\star}
.
We determine the thresholds for the appearance and disappearance of patterns in
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n,m}
.

Consecutive patterns

For a
consecutive
pattern, these thresholds depend on the number of inversions in the pattern, and on the number of inversions in its complement, respectively.
Specifically, we have the following result.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\pi
be any consecutive permutation pattern of length
k
.
If
s=\inv(\pi)
and
s^{\prime}=\inv(\overline{\pi})
, then for any positive constant
a
,

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\big[\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n,m}\text{ contains }\pi\big]

\displaystyle\mathchoice{\;=\;}{=}{=}{=}\begin{cases}0&\quad\text{if~ }m\ll n^{1-1/s},\\
1-e^{-a^{s}}&\quad\text{if~ $m\sim an^{1-1/s}$}\text{~and~}s>1,\\
1&\quad\text{if~ }m\sim a\text{~and~}s=1,\\
1&\quad\text{if~ }n^{1-1/s}\ll m\ll n^{1+1/k},\end{cases}

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\big[\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n,m}\text{ contains }\pi\big]

\displaystyle\mathchoice{\;=\;}{=}{=}{=}\begin{cases}1&\quad\text{if~ }n^{1+1/k}\gg\binom{n}{2}-m\gg n^{1-1/s^{\prime}},\\
1&\quad\text{if~ }\binom{n}{2}-m\sim a\text{~and~}s=1,\\
1-e^{-a^{s^{\prime}}}&\quad\text{if~ $\binom{n}{2}-m\sim an^{1-1/s^{\prime}}$}\text{~and~}s>1,\\
0&\quad\text{if~ }\binom{n}{2}-m\ll n^{1-1/s^{\prime}},\end{cases}

as long as
s>0
and
s^{\prime}>0
, respectively.

For example, the threshold for the appearance of consecutive pattern
2143
in
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n,m}
is
m\sim\sqrt{n}
, and the threshold for its disappearance is
\binom{n}{2}-m\sim n^{3/4}
.

Unfortunately, our methods do not enable us to show that
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n,m}
a.a.s. contains a given pattern for all values of
m
between the thresholds for its appearance and disappearance.
We defer further discussion of this challenge to Section
5
.

Classical patterns

Given an
n
-permutation
\sigma
, we say that it is
decomposable
if there exists some
k<n
such that

\{\sigma(1),\sigma(2),\dots,\sigma(k)\}\mathchoice{\;=\;}{=}{=}{=}\{1,2,\dots,k\}.

If a permutation is not decomposable, we say it is
indecomposable
.
For example, Figure
2
displays the plot of a decomposable permutation at the left and the plot of an indecomposable permutation at the right.
Any permutation that is decomposable can be expressed as the combination of two or more shorter permutations. Given two permutations
\sigma
and
\tau
with lengths
k
and
\ell
respectively, their
direct sum

\sigma\oplus\tau
is the permutation of length
k+\ell
consisting of
\sigma
followed by a shifted copy of
\tau
:

(\sigma\oplus\tau)(i)\mathchoice{\;=\;}{=}{=}{=}\begin{cases}\sigma(i)\qquad&\text{ if~ }i\leqslant k,\\
k+\tau(i-k)&\text{ if~ }k+1\leqslant i\leqslant k+\ell.\end{cases}

For example, the permutation at the left of Figure
2
is
231\oplus 4213\oplus 1
.
Every permutation has a unique representation as the direct sum of a sequence of one or more indecomposable permutations, which we call its
components
.
This representation is known as its
sum decomposition
.
Note that the complement of a decomposable permutation (with more than one component) is indecomposable (having only one component),
as illustrated in Figure
2
:
the permutation at the right is the complement of the permutation at the left.

The threshold for the appearance of a
classical
pattern depends on the greatest number of inversions in one of its components, and
the threshold for its disappearance depends on the greatest number of inversions in a component of its complement.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\pi
be any classical permutation pattern.
If
s
is the greatest number of inversions in a component of
\pi
,
and
s^{\prime}
is the greatest number of inversions in a component of
\overline{\pi}
,
then

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\big[\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n,m}\text{ contains }\pi\big]

\displaystyle\mathchoice{\;=\;}{=}{=}{=}\begin{cases}0&\quad\text{if~ }m\ll n^{1-1/s},\\
1&\quad\text{if~ }n^{1-1/s}\ll m\ll n,\end{cases}

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\big[\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n,m}\text{ contains }\pi\big]

\displaystyle\mathchoice{\;=\;}{=}{=}{=}\begin{cases}1&\quad\text{if~ }n\gg\binom{n}{2}-m\gg n^{1-1/s^{\prime}},\\
0&\quad\text{if~ }\binom{n}{2}-m\ll n^{1-1/s^{\prime}},\end{cases}

as long as
s>0
and
s^{\prime}>0
, respectively.

For example, the threshold for the appearance of classical pattern
23175468=231\oplus 4213\oplus 1
(shown at the left of Figure
2
) in
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n,m}
is
m=n^{3/4}
, since component 4213 has four inversions and neither of the other two components have more.

Vincular patterns

A
vincular
pattern with sum decomposition
\alpha_{1}\oplus\ldots\oplus\alpha_{k}
,
has a unique (possibly coarser) representation as a direct sum
\beta_{1}\oplus\ldots\oplus\beta_{\ell}
for some
\ell\leqslant k
, such that

(a)

each
\beta_{j}=\alpha_{i_{j}}\oplus\alpha_{i_{j}+1}\oplus\ldots\oplus\alpha_{i_{j}+r_{j}}
for some
i_{j}
and
r_{j}
, and

(b)

\alpha_{i}
and
\alpha_{i+1}
are components of the same
\beta_{j}
only if the last term of
\alpha_{i}
is required to be adjacent to the first term of
\alpha_{i+1}
.

and
2\underline{31754}68

We say that
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{\ell}
are the pattern’s
supercomponents
.
For example,
considering the permutation shown at the left of Figure
2
,
the vincular pattern

231\underline{75}4\underline{68}
has supercomponent decomposition
231\oplus 42135
, whereas
2\underline{31754}68
decomposes as
2317546\oplus 1
.
See Figure
3
for an illustration, in which the adjacency criteria are shown by shading.

The threshold for the appearance of a vincular pattern depends on the greatest number of inversions in one of its supercomponents.

Theorem 3
.

Let
\pi
be any vincular permutation pattern.
If
s
is the greatest number of inversions in a supercomponent of
\pi
,
and
s^{\prime}
is the greatest number of inversions in a supercomponent of
\overline{\pi}
,
then

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\big[\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n,m}\text{ contains }\pi\big]

\displaystyle\mathchoice{\;=\;}{=}{=}{=}\begin{cases}0&\quad\text{if }m\ll n^{1-1/s},\\
1&\quad\text{if }n^{1-1/s}\ll m\ll n,\end{cases}

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\big[\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n,m}\text{ contains }\pi\big]

\displaystyle\mathchoice{\;=\;}{=}{=}{=}\begin{cases}1&\quad\text{if }n\gg\binom{n}{2}-m\gg n^{1-1/s^{\prime}},\\
0&\quad\text{if }\binom{n}{2}-m\ll n^{1-1/s^{\prime}},\end{cases}

as long as
s>0
and
s^{\prime}>0
, respectively.

Background

There has not been a great deal of previous study of the structure of permutations with a given number of inversions.
Even the magnitude of
|\mathcal{S}_{n,m}|
appears not to have been established for all ranges of
m
.
Comtet
[
20
, Section 7.4]
proves that the number of inversions in a random
n
-permutation satisfies a central limit theorem
(see also
[
6
,
31
]
).
Asymptotics for
|\mathcal{S}_{n,m}|
have been determined when
m\leqslant n
(see
[
42
]
), when
m\sim an
(see
[
19
,
40
]
), and when
m\sim an^{2}
(see
[
40
]
).
However, the gap
n\ll m\ll n^{2}
seems not have been investigated.

Apart from the flawed preprint
[
8
]
, the only prior work specifically on
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n,m}

of which we are aware
is that of Acan and Pittel
[
1
]
.
Their primary result is a determination of the (sharp) threshold at which
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n,m}
becomes indecomposable — at
m\sim(6/\pi^{2})n\log n
. They make use of an implicitly defined Markov process that produces
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n,m+1}
from
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n,m}
. No explicit model of this evolutionary process is known.
Kenyon, Král’, Radin and Winkler
[
38
]
compute the limit shapes of permutations when
m\sim an^{2}
, thus making it possible to determine the expected density of any classical pattern in
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n,m}
in this range.

The structure of a random permutation
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}
drawn uniformly from
\mathcal{S}_{n}
has been rather better studied.
We mention just a few results.
Janson, Nakamura and Zeilberger
[
37
]
establish asymptotic normality for the distribution of any classical pattern, a result which has been extended to every vincular pattern by Hofer
[
35
]
.
Perarnau
[
44
]
investigates consecutive pattern avoidance in
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}
.
Bhattacharya and Mukherjee
[
13
]
determine the number of inversions involving a given point of
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}
, proving convergence to a uniform distribution (over a range dependent on the position) except in the case of the central point (which satisfies a central limit theorem).
Probably the most celebrated result in this context is the establishment by Baik, Deift and Johansson
[
3
]
of the limiting distribution of the length of the longest increasing subsequence in a random
n
-permutation. See
[
47
]
for an extended exposition.

Outline

In Section
2
, we consider inversion sequences of permutations, relating the presence of a consecutive pattern in a permutation to the inversion sequences of the pattern and of the host permutation.
In Section
3
, we apply our work on patterns in random compositions
[
11
]
to inversion sequences and prove Theorem
1
on the thresholds for consecutive patterns.
Section
4
builds on this to prove Theorems
2
and
3
giving the thresholds for classical and vincular patterns.
Various consequences of these theorems are discussed.
Finally, in Section
5
, we present several open questions, including considering the challenge of bridging the gap between the thresholds for a pattern’s appearance and its disappearance, and briefly discussing the relationship between
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n,m}
and Mallows permutations.

.

3
Compositions and inversion sequences

In this section, we introduce two models of random integer compositions.
We then leverage results from
[
11
]
concerning patterns in compositions to find the thresholds for consecutive patterns in inversion sequences, and hence also for consecutive patterns in permutations.

An
n
-term weak composition of
m
, or just an
n
-composition of
m
, is a sequence of
n
nonnegative integers that sum to
m
. See Figure
7
for an example.
If
C
is a composition, then we use
C(i)
to denote its
i
th term and
\|C\|
to denote the sum of its terms (or
weight
).
Let
\mathcal{C}_{n}
denote the (infinite) set of all
n
-compositions, and let
\mathcal{C}_{n,m}
denote the set of all
n
-compositions of
m
.
By a simple “stars and bars” argument, it can be seen that the total number of distinct
n
-compositions of
m
is equal to
\binom{m+n-1}{m}
.

We say that a composition
c
of length
k
occurs as an
exact pattern
at position
j
in another composition
C
if

C(j-1+i)=c(i)
for each
i\in[k]
.
Equivalently,
c
occurs at position
j
in
C
if
C[j,j+k-1]=c
, where
C[i,j]
denotes the sub-composition
C(i),\ldots,C(j)
.
For example, the exact pattern 3112 occurs twice in the composition in Figure
7
, at positions 6 and 19.

We now present two models of random compositions.
The first is the
uniform random composition

\mathbf{C}_{n,m}
, chosen uniformly from
\mathcal{C}_{n,m}
.
For any
C\in\mathcal{C}_{n,m}
, we have

\mathbb{P}\big[\mathbf{C}_{n,m}=C\big]\mathchoice{\;=\;}{=}{=}{=}\binom{m+n-1}{m}^{\!\!-1}.

It is easy to see that the distribution of any exact pattern in
\mathbf{C}_{n,m}
is independent of its position.

Proposition 9
.

Let
c
be any exact composition pattern of length
k
. Then,
for any

i,j\in[n+1-k]
,

\mathbb{P}\big[c\text{ occurs at }i\text{ in }\mathbf{C}_{n,m}\big]\mathchoice{\;=\;}{=}{=}{=}\mathbb{P}\big[c\text{ occurs at }j\text{ in }\mathbf{C}_{n,m}\big].

Proof.

The probability of
c
appearing at position
i
in
\mathbf{C}_{n,m}
is equal to

{\binom{(m-\|c\|)+(n-k)-1}{m-\|c\|}}\times{\binom{m+n-1}{m}}^{\!\!-1},

which doesn’t depend on
i
.
∎

Our second model, which is significantly easier to analyse, is the
geometric random composition

\mathbf{C}_{n,p}
.
If
p\in[0,1)
, then
\mathbf{C}_{n,p}
is distributed over
\mathcal{C}_{n}
so that for each
C\in\mathcal{C}_{n}
, we have

\mathbb{P}\big[\mathbf{C}_{n,p}=C\big]\mathchoice{\;=\;}{=}{=}{=}q^{n}p^{\|C\|},

where
q=1-p
.
Each term of
\mathbf{C}_{n,p}
is sampled independently from the geometric distribution with parameter
q
, that is,
\mathbb{P}\big[C_{n,p}(i)=k\big]=qp^{k}
for each
k\geqslant 0
and
i\in[n]
.
Note that
\mathbb{E}\big[\|\mathbf{C}_{n,p}\|\big]=np/q
.
To avoid unnecessary repetition, when considering
\mathbf{C}_{n,p}
in this work,
q
always denotes
1-p
.

Thresholds are defined in our composition models in an analogous manner to
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n,m}
.
A function
m^{\star}=m^{\star}(n)
is a threshold for a property
\mathcal{Q}
in the uniform random composition
\mathbf{C}_{n,m}
if

\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\big[\mathbf{C}_{n,m}\text{ satisfies }\mathcal{Q}\big]\mathchoice{\;=\;}{=}{=}{=}\begin{cases}0&\text{if~ $m\ll m^{\star}$,}\\
1&\text{if~ $m\gg m^{\star}$.}\end{cases}

Similarly, a function
p^{\star}=p^{\star}(n)
is a threshold for a property
\mathcal{Q}
in the geometric random composition
\mathbf{C}_{n,p}
if

\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\big[\mathbf{C}_{n,p}\text{ satisfies }\mathcal{Q}\big]\mathchoice{\;=\;}{=}{=}{=}\begin{cases}0&\text{if~ $p/q\ll p^{\star}/q^{\star}$,}\\
1&\text{if~ $p/q\gg p^{\star}/q^{\star}$,}\end{cases}

where
q^{\star}=1-p^{\star}
.

In our previous work
[
11
]
on the evolution of random compositions, we investigate the appearance of patterns in compositions.
We use these results to determine the threshold for the appearance of an exact pattern in
\mathbf{C}_{n,m}
.

Proposition 10
.

If
c
is a non-zero exact composition pattern of length
k
with
\|c\|=s
, then for any positive constant
a
,

\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\big[\mathbf{C}_{n,m}\text{ contains }c\big]\mathchoice{\;=\;}{=}{=}{=}\begin{cases}0&\quad\text{if~ }m\ll n^{1-1/s},\\
1-e^{-a^{s}}&\quad\text{if~ }m\sim an^{1-1/s}\text{~and~}s>1,\\
1&\quad\text{if~ }m\sim a\text{~and~}s=1,\\
1&\quad\text{if~ }n^{1-1/s}\ll m\ll n^{1+1/k}.\end{cases}

Proof.

This follows from two results in
[
11
]
,
Proposition 4.1:

\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\big[\mathbf{C}_{n,p}\text{ contains }c\big]\mathchoice{\;=\;}{=}{=}{=}\begin{cases}0&\quad\text{if~ }p\ll n^{-1/s},\\
1-e^{-a^{s}}&\quad\text{if~ }p\sim an^{-1/s},\\
1&\quad\text{if~ }n^{-1/s}\ll p\text{ and }q\gg n^{-1/k},\end{cases}

and Proposition 4.2:

\text{If~ $m\sim np/q\gg 1$ ~then~~}\mathbb{P}\big[\mathbf{C}_{n,m}\text{ contains }c\big]\sim\mathbb{P}\big[\mathbf{C}_{n,p}\text{ contains }c\big].

If
m<s
, then
\mathbf{C}_{n,m}
doesn’t contain
c
.
If
m
is bounded and
m\geqslant s>1
, then

\mathbb{P}\big[\mathbf{C}_{n,m}\text{ contains }c\big]\;<\;n\,\frac{\binom{m-s+n-k-1}{m-s}}{\binom{m+n-1}{m}}\;\sim\;\frac{m!}{(m-s)!}\,n^{1-s}\;\ll\;1.

If
m\sim a
and
s=1
, then a.a.s.
\mathbf{C}_{n,m}
contains exactly
a
occurrences of
c
,
this being the same as
having the first few and last few terms equal to zero, and
avoiding a finite number of patterns,
each of weight greater than one,
whose non-zero terms are close together.
∎

Clearly an inversion sequence is a special type of composition.
We would like to leverage our results on patterns in compositions in order to establish results concerning inversion sequences, and hence permutations.
To this end we determine when
\mathbf{C}_{n,m}
is a.a.s. an inversion sequence.

Proposition 11
.

The threshold for
\mathbf{C}_{n,m}
to be an inversion sequence is given by

\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\big[\mathbf{C}_{n,m}\in\mathcal{E}_{n,m}\big]\mathchoice{\;=\;}{=}{=}{=}\begin{cases}1&\quad\text{if~ }m\ll n,\\
0&\quad\text{if~ }m\gg n.\end{cases}

The proof of this result requires the notion of an
increasing
property.
We say that a property
\mathcal{Q}
of compositions is increasing if
C
satisfying
\mathcal{Q}
implies that
C^{+j}
satisfies
\mathcal{Q}
, for every
j\in[n]
, where
C^{+j}
denotes the composition obtained from
C
by the addition of
1
to its
j
th term.

Proof.

We first establish the threshold for the geometric random composition
\mathbf{C}_{n,p}
to be an inversion sequence.
Recall that
C\in\mathcal{E}_{n,m}
if
C(i)<i
for each
i\in[n]
.

Now,

\mathbb{P}\big[\mathbf{C}_{n,p}(i)<i\big]\mathchoice{\;=\;}{=}{=}{=}1-p^{i}.

So

\mathbb{P}\big[\text{$\mathbf{C}_{n,p}$ is an inversion sequence}\big]\mathchoice{\;=\;}{=}{=}{=}\prod^{n}_{i=1}\left(1-p^{i}\right).

By Euler’s Pentagonal Number Theorem (see
[
33
]
),

\prod^{\infty}_{i=1}\left(1-p^{i}\right)\mathchoice{\;=\;}{=}{=}{=}1+\sum^{\infty}_{k=1}(-1)^{k}\left(p^{k(3k+1)/2}+p^{k(3k-1)/2}\right)\mathchoice{\;=\;}{=}{=}{=}1-p-p^{2}+p^{5}+p^{7}-\dots.

If
p\ll 1
, then this converges to 1 as
n
tends to infinity, and so a.a.s.
\mathbf{C}_{n,p}
is an inversion sequence.

On the other hand,

\mathbb{P}\big[\mathbf{C}_{n,p}\text{ is an inversion sequence}\big]\mathchoice{\;=\;}{=}{=}{=}q\prod^{n}_{i=2}\left(1-p^{i}\right)\;\leqslant\;q.

If
q\ll 1
, then this converges to 0 as
n
tends to infinity, and so a.a.s.
\mathbf{C}_{n,p}
is not an inversion sequence.

Not being an inversion sequence is an increasing property.
This enables us to transfer the threshold from
\mathbf{C}_{n,p}
to
\mathbf{C}_{n,m}
by using
[
11
, Proposition 2.8]
:

If
\mathcal{Q}
is an increasing property that has a threshold
p^{\star}\geqslant n^{-1}
in
\mathbf{C}_{n,p}
, then
np^{\star}/q^{\star}
is a threshold for
\mathcal{Q}
in
\mathbf{C}_{n,m}
, where
q^{\star}=1-p^{\star}
.

The result follows.
∎

This enables us to handle values of
m\ll n
.
To extend our results to slightly greater
m
, we require the following bound from
[
11
]

on the largest term in
\mathbf{C}_{n,m}
.
If
\max(C)
is the largest term in composition
C
, then a.a.s.
\max(\mathbf{C}_{n,m})
does not grow faster than
\frac{m}{n}\log n
.

Proposition 12

(
[
11
, Propositions 4.9–4.11 and 2.8]
)
.

\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\big[\max(\mathbf{C}_{n,m})\gg\frac{m}{n}\log n\big]\mathchoice{\;=\;}{=}{=}{=}0.

Using this bound, we can establish that, under suitable conditions, if a pattern a.a.s. occurs in
\mathbf{C}_{n,m}
then it also a.a.s. occurs in (a suffix of)
\mathbf{e}_{n,m}
.

Proposition 13
.

Suppose
c
is an exact composition pattern,
and that

m^{-}\gg 1
and
m^{+}\ll n^{2}/\log^{2}n
are such that
a.a.s.
\mathbf{C}_{n,m}
contains

whenever
m^{-}\ll m\ll m^{+}

Then,
a.a.s.
\mathbf{e}_{n,m}
also contains

under the same conditions on

.

Proof.

Suppose
m\ll n^{2}/\log^{2}n
. Then,

\frac{m}{n}\log n\;\ll\;\sqrt{m}\frac{n}{\log n}\frac{\log n}{n}\mathchoice{\;=\;}{=}{=}{=}\sqrt{m}\;\ll\;\frac{n}{\log n}\;\ll\;n.

Let

satisfy

.
Then, by Proposition
12
, a.a.s. no term of
\mathbf{C}_{n,m}
is greater than

.

Suppose
s\ll m
.
Then
m^{-}(n)\ll m\ll m^{+}(n)
implies
m^{-}(n-k)\ll m-s\ll m^{+}(n-k)
.
So,
if a.a.s.
\mathbf{C}_{n,m}
contains

whenever
m^{-}\ll m\ll m^{+}
,
then it is also the case that a.a.s.
\mathbf{C}_{n-k,m-s}
contains

whenever
m^{-}\ll m\ll m^{+}

Now consider the suffix

of
\mathbf{e}_{n,m}

Clearly,
\mathbf{e}^{\prime}(i)<k+i
for each

and
m-\binom{k}{2}\leqslant\|\mathbf{e}^{\prime}\|\leqslant m

Hence,

a.a.s.
\mathbf{C}_{n,m}
contains

whenever
m^{-}\ll m\ll m^{+}

\displaystyle\;\implies\;

a.a.s.
\mathbf{C}_{n-k,\|\mathbf{e}^{\prime}\|}
contains

whenever
m^{-}\ll m\ll m^{+}

\displaystyle\;\implies\;

a.a.s.
\mathbf{e}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\;\implies\;

\displaystyle\text{a.a.s. $\mathbf{e}_{n,m}$ contains $c$ whenever $m^{-}\ll m\ll m^{+}$},

that has the same relative order as
\sigma(i),\ldots,\sigma(j)
.

We begin with two propositions concerning occurrences of indecomposable classical patterns.

Proposition 14
.

Suppose
\alpha
is an indecomposable classical pattern of length
k\geqslant 2
.
If
\alpha
occurs at
[i,j]
in a permutation
\sigma
, with width
w=j+1-i
, then
\inv(\sigma[i,j])\geqslant\inv(\alpha)+w-k
.

Proof.

If
i<\ell<j
then
\sigma(\ell)
forms an inversion with some term in the occurrence of
\alpha
.
Otherwise we would have
\alpha=\beta\oplus\gamma
, with
\beta
lying to the left and below
\sigma(\ell)
and
\gamma
lying to the right and above
\sigma(\ell)
.
But
\alpha
is indecomposable.
Thus each of the
w-k
terms of
\sigma[i,j]
not in the occurrence of
\alpha
contributes at least 1 to the number of inversions in
\sigma[i,j]
.
∎

With this in hand, we prove that containment of an indecomposable classical pattern implies containment of a consecutive pattern with as many inversions whose length is bounded.
No attempt has been made to optimise the bound on the length.

Proposition 15
.

Suppose
\alpha
is an indecomposable classical pattern of length
k\geqslant 2
with
s
inversions.
If
\alpha
occurs in a permutation
\sigma
, then
\sigma
contains a consecutive pattern with at least
s
inversions of length at most
ks
.

Proof.

Suppose
\alpha
occurs at
[i,j]
in
\sigma
with width
w=j+1-i\geqslant k
.
Let
t=\inv(\sigma[i,j])
.
By Proposition
14
, we have
t\geqslant s+w-k
.
Note that
t\geqslant s\geqslant 1
.

Let
d=\left\lfloor t/s\right\rfloor
and partition
e_{\sigma[i,j]}
into
d
consecutive blocks of almost equal length,
each block having length either
\left\lfloor w/d\right\rfloor
or
\left\lceil w/d\right\rceil
.
Since

t/d\geqslant s
,
by the pigeonhole principle, there is a block
b
with
\|b\|\geqslant s
.

Now,
w\leqslant k+t-s
, and

d\mathchoice{\;=\;}{=}{=}{=}\left\lfloor\frac{t}{s}\right\rfloor\;\geqslant\;\frac{t}{s}-1+\frac{1}{s}\mathchoice{\;=\;}{=}{=}{=}\frac{1+t-s}{s}.

So the length of each block is bounded above by

\displaystyle\left\lceil\frac{w}{d}\right\rceil\;<\;\frac{w}{d}+1

\displaystyle\;\leqslant\;1+\frac{(k+t-s)s}{1+t-s}

\displaystyle\mathchoice{\;=\;}{=}{=}{=}1+s+\frac{(k-1)s}{1+t-s}\;\leqslant\;1+s+(k-1)s\mathchoice{\;=\;}{=}{=}{=}1+ks.

Thus, since this is a strict inequality, there is a consecutive subsequence of
e_{\sigma[i,j]}
of length no more than
ks
whose terms total at least
s
, and so

\sigma
contains a consecutive pattern with at least
s
inversions of length at most
ks
.
∎

We are now in a position to establish the thresholds for classical patterns in
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n,m}
.

Theorem
2
.

Let
\pi
be any classical permutation pattern.
If
s
is the greatest number of inversions in a component of
\pi
,
and
s^{\prime}
is the greatest number of inversions in a component of
\overline{\pi}
,
then for any positive constant
a
,

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\big[\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n,m}\text{ contains }\pi\big]

\displaystyle\mathchoice{\;=\;}{=}{=}{=}\begin{cases}0&\quad\text{if~ }m\ll n^{1-1/s},\\
1&\quad\text{if~ }n^{1-1/s}\ll m\ll n,\end{cases}

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\big[\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n,m}\text{ contains }\pi\big]

\displaystyle\mathchoice{\;=\;}{=}{=}{=}\begin{cases}1&\quad\text{if~ }n\gg\binom{n}{2}-m\gg n^{1-1/s^{\prime}},\\
0&\quad\text{if~ }\binom{n}{2}-m\ll n^{1-1/s^{\prime}},\end{cases}

as long as
s>0
and
s^{\prime}>0
, respectively.

Proof.

We first prove that below the threshold a.a.s.
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n,m}
avoids
\pi
.
Indeed, a.a.s. it contains no indecomposable pattern with
s
inversions.

By Proposition
15
, if
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n,m}
were to contain an indecomposable pattern
\alpha
of length
k
then it would also contain some consecutive pattern of length at most
ks
with at least
s
inversions.
There are only finitely many such consecutive patterns.
Now suppose that
m\ll n^{1-1/s}
.
From Theorem
1
, we know that a.a.s.
\mathbf{C}_{n,m}
contains no fixed finite set of consecutive patterns with
s
or more inversions.
Thus
\mathbf{C}_{n,m}
avoids
\alpha
, and hence also avoids
\pi
.

We now prove that above the threshold a.a.s.
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n,m}
contains
\pi
.
Suppose
\pi
has sum decomposition
\pi=\alpha_{1}\oplus\ldots\oplus\alpha_{r}
.

Let
\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{C}_{n,m}
.
For
0\leqslant j\leqslant r
, let
i_{j}=\left\lfloor jn/r\right\rfloor
, and, for each
j\in[r]
, let
\mathbf{C}_{j}=\mathbf{C}[i_{j-1}+1,i_{j}]
.
Thus,
\mathbf{C}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{C}_{r}
is a partition of the terms of
\mathbf{C}
, each
\mathbf{C}_{j}
having length
n_{j}\in\big\{\!\left\lfloor n/r\right\rfloor,\left\lceil n/r\right\rceil\!\big\}
.
Let
m_{j}=\|\mathbf{C}_{j}\|
.

Since
\|\mathbf{C}\|
is constant,
the covariance between any two distinct terms of
\mathbf{C}
is negative.
Indeed, straightforward calculations show that

\mathrm{Var}\big[\mathbf{C}(i)\big]\mathchoice{\;=\;}{=}{=}{=}\frac{(n-1)m(m+n)}{n^{2}(n+1)},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathsf{Cov}\big[\mathbf{C}(i_{1}),\mathbf{C}(i_{2})\big]\mathchoice{\;=\;}{=}{=}{=}-\frac{m(m+n)}{n^{2}(n+1)}\text{~~if $i_{1}\neq i_{2}$}.

Hence,

\mathrm{Var}\big[{m_{j}}/{n_{j}}\big]\mathchoice{\;=\;}{=}{=}{=}{\mathrm{Var}\big[m_{j}\big]}/{n_{j}^{2}}\;<\;\,n_{j}\,\mathrm{Var}\big[\mathbf{C}(i)\big]/n_{j}^{2}\;\sim\;\frac{rm(m+n)}{n^{3}},

which tends to zero as long as
m\ll n^{3/2}
.
Thus (by Chebyshev’s inequality),
for this range of values for
m

the sum of terms in each
\mathbf{C}_{j}
satisfies a law of large numbers.

Thus, for each
j
and any
\varepsilon>0
, a.a.s. we have

m_{j}>(1-\varepsilon)m/r
.
Therefore, if

m\gg n^{1-1/s}
,
then

m_{j}\gg n_{j}^{1-1/s}
for each
j\in[r]
.

Thus, if

n^{1-1/s}\ll m\ll n
, for each
j\in[r]
, we have the following sequence of implications:

\bullet

By Proposition
10
, a.a.s.
\mathbf{C}_{n_{j},m_{j}}
contains a consecutive occurrence of
e_{\alpha_{j}}
.

\bullet

Thus a.a.s.
\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{C}_{n,m}
contains consecutive occurrences of
e_{\alpha_{1}},\ldots e_{\alpha_{r}}
in that order.

\bullet

Since, by Proposition
11
,
\mathbf{C}_{n,m}
is a.a.s. an inversion sequence, a.a.s.
\mathbf{e}_{n,m}
contains consecutive occurrences of
e_{\alpha_{1}},\ldots e_{\alpha_{r}}
in that order.

\bullet

By Proposition
7
, these correspond to occurrences of
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{r}
as consecutive patterns in
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n,m}
, such that no point of
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n,m}
is to the upper left of any point in any of these occurrences.

\bullet

Thus a.a.s.
\pi=\alpha_{1}\oplus\ldots\oplus\alpha_{r}
occurs in
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n,m}
.

The threshold for the disappearance of
\pi
then follows
because

\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n,\binom{n}{2}-m}
has the same distribution as
\overline{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n,m}}
.
∎

We can say a little more about the appearance of classical patterns.
As the random permutation
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n,m}
evolves, indecomposable patterns appear first as consecutive patterns.
Let us say that a component
\alpha
of a classical pattern

is

if no other component of

has more inversions than

.
Then for an arbitrary classical pattern, we have the following behaviour.

Proposition 16
.

Let
\pi
be any classical permutation pattern whose dominant components have
s
inversions.
If
n^{1-1/s}\ll m\ll n^{1-1/(s+1)}
, then
asymptotically almost surely, in every occurrence of
\pi
in
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n,m}
, each dominant component of
\pi
occurs consecutively.

Proof.

Suppose
\alpha
is a dominant component of
\pi
.
By Propositions
14
and
15
, if there is a nonconsecutive occurrence of
\alpha
in
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n,m}
, then there is an occurrence of some consecutive pattern with at least
s+1
inversions.
If
m\ll n^{1-1/(s+1)}
than a.a.s. this does not occur.
∎

A direct application of Theorems
1
and
2
yields the threshold for the longest decreasing subsequence in
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n,m}
to have a given length.

Corollary 17
.

If
\ell\geqslant 2
, then

\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\big[\text{$\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n,m}$ contains $\ell\ldots 21$}\big]\mathchoice{\;=\;}{=}{=}{=}\begin{cases}0&\quad\text{if~ }m\ll n^{1-1/\binom{\ell}{2}},\\
1&\quad\text{if~ }n^{1-1/\binom{\ell}{2}}\ll m\ll n^{1+1/\ell}.\end{cases}

Thus, if
0<\gamma<1
and
m\sim n^{\gamma}
, then the length
\ell
of the longest decreasing subsequence in
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n,m}
depends on the value of the solution of the equation
\gamma=1-1/\binom{d}{2}
:

d\mathchoice{\;=\;}{=}{=}{=}\frac{1}{2}\!\left(\!1+\sqrt{\frac{9-\gamma}{1-\gamma}}\,\right)\!.

If
d
is not an integer, then a.a.s.
\ell=\left\lfloor d\right\rfloor
, and every longest decreasing subsequence occurs consecutively.
If
d
is an integer, then a.a.s.
\ell
takes one of the two values
d-1
or
d
.

We can also determine the threshold for the occurrence of an inversion with a given width.

Corollary 18
.

If
w\geqslant 2
, then

\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\big[\text{$\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n,m}$ contains an inversion of width $w$}\big]\mathchoice{\;=\;}{=}{=}{=}\begin{cases}0&\quad\text{if~ }m\ll n^{1-1/(w-1)},\\
1&\quad\text{if~ }n^{1-1/(w-1)}\ll m\ll n^{1+1/w}.\end{cases}

Proof.

By Proposition
14
, if an inversion of width
w
occurs at
[i,j]
in
\sigma
then
\sigma[i,j]
has at least
w-1
inversions.
So, by Theorem
1
, below the threshold a.a.s. there is no occurrence of an inversion of width
w
in
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n,m}
.
On the other hand, the first and last terms of consecutive pattern
\pi=234\ldots w1
form an inversion of width
w
. Since
\inv(\pi)=w-1
, by Theorem
1
, above the threshold a.a.s.
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n,m}
contains an occurrence of
\pi
and hence also contains an inversion of width
w
.
∎

Another consequence of Theorem
2
is that if

grows sufficiently fast (e.g.

), then a.a.s.
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n,m}
contains
any
given classical pattern, or equivalently a.a.s.
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n,m}
is not contained in any permutation class

.

Corollary 19
.

If
n^{1-\delta}\ll m\ll n
for every
\delta>0
, and
\pi
is any classical permutation pattern, then asymptotically almost surely
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n,m}
contains
\pi
.

.

Theorem

.

Let
\pi
be any vincular permutation pattern.
If

is the greatest number of inversions in a supercomponent of

,

is the greatest number of inversions in a supercomponent of

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\big[\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n,m}\text{ contains }\pi\big]

\displaystyle\mathchoice{\;=\;}{=}{=}{=}\begin{cases}0&\quad\text{if }m\ll n^{1-1/s},\\

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\big[\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n,m}\text{ contains }\pi\big]

\displaystyle\mathchoice{\;=\;}{=}{=}{=}\begin{cases}1&\quad\text{if }n\gg\binom{n}{2}-m\gg n^{1-1/s^{\prime}},\\

Open questions

As noted in the introduction, our methods do not enable us to show that
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n,m}
a.a.s. contains a given pattern for all values of
m
between the thresholds for its appearance and disappearance.
This is because our approach builds on results concerning random compositions,
and the threshold for the disappearance of an exact pattern of length
k
in
\mathbf{C}_{n,m}
is

(Proposition
10
).
We also remarked that even the asymptotics of
|\mathcal{S}_{n,m}|
appear not to have been established when
n\ll m\ll n^{2}
.
When
m\sim an^{2}
, we do know a bit more.
Specifically, the permuton approach (see
[
38
]
) is sufficient to establish that any
classical
pattern is present a.a.s.
However, permutons tell us nothing about the local structure (see
[
9
,
16
]
), so cannot help with consecutive patterns.

Proposition 20
.

Let
\pi
be any classical permutation pattern, and suppose
m\sim an^{2}
for some constant

.
Then
\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\big[\text{$\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n,m}$ contains $\pi$}\big]=1
.

Nevertheless, there seems no reason to doubt that any pattern is a.a.s. present in
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n,m}
between its two thresholds.
Somewhat surprisingly, there appears to be no simple strategy for proving this.

Conjecture 21
.

Let
\pi
be any consecutive permutation pattern,
and let
s=\inv(\pi)
and
s^{\prime}=\inv(\overline{\pi})
.
If
n^{1-1/s}\ll m
and
\binom{n}{2}-m\gg n^{1-1/s^{\prime}}
,

\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\big[\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n,m}\text{ contains }\pi\big]=1
.

Furthermore, it is natural to suppose that the expected number of occurrences in
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n,m}
of a given pattern is a unimodal function of
m
, first increasing and then decreasing.
Given a permutation pattern
\pi
, let
\mathbb{E}_{n,m}(\pi)
denote the expected number of occurrences of
\pi
in
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n,m}
.

Conjecture 22
.

Let
\pi
be any consecutive permutation pattern.
If
n\geqslant|\pi|+2
, then the sequence

\mathbb{E}_{n,0}(\pi),\,\mathbb{E}_{n,1}(\pi),\,\ldots,\,\mathbb{E}_{n,{\binom{n}{2}}}(\pi)

is unimodal.

One model that may help in addressing such questions is the

[

The Mallows distribution on
\mathcal{S}_{n}
with parameter

— which we denote
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n,p}

\mathbb{P}\big[\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n,p}=\sigma\big]\mathchoice{\;=\;}{=}{=}{=}\frac{(1-p)^{n}p^{\inv(\sigma)}}{\prod_{j=1}^{n}(1-p^{j})}.

1,&\text{if~ $p=0$ and $k=0$},\\[3.0pt]
0,&\text{if~ $p=0$ and $k>0$},\\[5.0pt]
1/j,&\text{if~ $p=1$}.\end{cases}

Proposition 29

If

and

\mathbb{E}\big[\inv(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n,p})\big]\;\sim\;\begin{cases}\frac{p}{1-p}n,&\text{if~ $1-p\gg n^{-1}$},\\[5.0pt]

\frac{1}{4}n^{2},&\text{if~ $1-p\ll n^{-1}$},\\[5.0pt]
\end{cases}

with

c(a)\mathchoice{\;=\;}{=}{=}{=}\frac{1}{a}-\frac{1}{a^{2}}\mathrm{Li}_{2}(1-e^{-a}),

where
\mathrm{Li}_{2}(z)
is the dilogarithm function. The constant

\lim\limits_{a\to 0}c(a)=\frac{1}{4}

Proof.

Let

Suppose first that

\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbf{e}_{n,p}(j)\big]\mathchoice{\;=\;}{=}{=}{=}\sum_{k=0}^{j-1}\dfrac{kqp^{k}}{1-p^{j}}\mathchoice{\;=\;}{=}{=}{=}\frac{p}{q}-\frac{jp^{j}}{1-p^{j}}\;\geqslant\;\frac{p}{q}-\frac{np^{j}}{1-p^{j}}\;>\;\frac{p}{q}-p^{2}.

Thus,
\big(1-o(1)\big)np/q<\mathbb{E}\big[\|\mathbf{e}_{n,p}\|\big]<np/q

Now suppose that

Suppose also that

Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbf{e}_{n,p}(j)\big]\mathchoice{\;=\;}{=}{=}{=}\sum_{k=0}^{j-1}\dfrac{kqp^{k}}{1-p^{j}}

\displaystyle\mathchoice{\;=\;}{=}{=}{=}q^{-1}-1-j\left(\frac{1}{1-(1-q)^{j}}-1\right)

\displaystyle\;\geqslant\;q^{-1}-1-n\left(\frac{1}{1-(1-q)^{j}}-1\right)\;>\;q^{-1}-1-\frac{q^{-1}}{\omega}.

\big(1-o(1)\big)n/q<\mathbb{E}\big[\|\mathbf{e}_{n,p}\|\big]<n/q
