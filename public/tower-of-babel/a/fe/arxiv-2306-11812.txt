The Relativistic Vlasov-Maxwell system Local smooth solvability for a weak topology
Christophe Cheverry & Slim Ibrahim

Abstract




Abstract
.
This article is devoted to the Relativistic Vlasov-Maxwell system in
space dimension three. We prove the local existence and uniqueness of solutions for initial data
({\rm f}_{0},{\rm E}_{0},{\rm B}_{0})\in L^{\infty}\times H^{1}\times H^{1}
, with
{\rm f}_{0}
compactly supported in momentum.
This result is the consequence of the local smooth solvability for the “weak" topology associated with

L^{\infty}\times H^{1}\times H^{1}
. It is derived from a representation formula decoding how the momentum
spreads and revealing that the domain of influence in momentum is controlled by mild information (involving
only conserved quantities). We do so by developing a Radon Fourier analysis on the RVM system, leading to
the study of a class of singular weighted integrals. In parallel, we implement our method to construct smooth
solutions to the RVM system in the regime of dense, hot and strongly magnetized plasmas.

Keywords
. Relativistic Vlasov-Maxwell system ; Local
well-posedness ; Continuation criteria ; Radon transform ; Kinetic equations ; Phase space analysis ;
Strongly magnetized plasmas ; turbulence.

1.
Main results

Let
C^{n}_{c}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})
be the class of compactly supported

n
times continuously differentiable functions on
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, endowed with the norm

\parallel{\rm U}\parallel_{n}:=\sup\ \bigl\{\parallel\partial^{\alpha}{\rm U}\parallel_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{m})}\,;\,|\alpha|\leq n\bigr\}\,,\hskip 18.49988pt\parallel\cdot\parallel_{0}\equiv\parallel\cdot\parallel_{L^{\infty}}\,,\hskip 18.49988pt(n,m)\in{\mathbb{N}}^{2}.

Consider a Cauchy problem which is locally well-posed in
C^{n}_{c}
with
n\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
. Given some
initial data
{\rm U}_{0}\in C^{n}_{c}
, denote by
T({\rm U}_{0})\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{*}\cup\{+\infty\}
the lifespan
of the associated smooth (say
C^{1}
) solution. Select a subspace
\mathfrak{N}\subset C^{n}_{c}
which
is equipped with a « small » norm
{\mathcal{N}}
, weaker than
\parallel\cdot\parallel_{n}
meaning that
{\mathcal{N}}\lesssim\parallel\cdot\parallel_{n}
on
\mathfrak{N}
. From there, for all
{\rm S}_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, we can introduce
the lower bound

(1.1)

T(\mathfrak{N},{\mathcal{N}};{\rm S}_{0}):=\inf\ \bigl\{T({\rm U}_{0})\,;\,{\rm U}_{0}\in\mathfrak{N}\text{ and }{\mathcal{N}}({\rm U}_{0})\leq{\rm S}_{0}\bigr\}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\cup\{+\infty\}.

Definition 1

(Smooth solvability of initial value problems)
.

We say that a Cauchy
problem is locally smoothly solvable (LSS) for
(\mathfrak{N},{\mathcal{N}})
when
T(\mathfrak{N},{\mathcal{N}};{\rm S}_{0})

is a positive number for all
{\rm S}_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
. It is globally smoothly solvable (GSS) for
(\mathfrak{N},{\mathcal{N}})
when
T(\mathfrak{N},{\mathcal{N}};{\rm S}_{0})=+\infty
for all
{\rm S}_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
.

The notion of smooth solvability for
(\mathfrak{N},{\mathcal{N}})
focuses on the persistence of regularity.
It must be clearly distinguished from the classical concept of « well-posedness », which is usually understood
in the sense of Hadamard that is with existence, uniqueness and also stability related to the normed space
associated with
{\mathcal{N}}
. It is less demanding in the sense that it does not necessarily require the continuous
dependence with respect to the « small » norm
{\mathcal{N}}
. Such an alleviation may have an important implication
to analyze systems in turbulent regimes.

For a large class of first-order quasilinear symmetric hyperbolic systems (QSH systems) whose
prototypes are Burgers’ equation and the 3D compressible Euler equations (away from the vacuum), the Cauchy
problem is known to be well posed in
(C^{n}_{c},\parallel\cdot\parallel_{n})
as long as
n
is large enough.
But, due to the finite-time singularity formation, it is certainly not globally smoothly solvable for such

(C^{n}_{c},\parallel\cdot\parallel_{n})
. A simple scaling argument indicates that it cannnot be LSS for

(C^{n}_{c},\parallel\cdot\parallel_{0})
.

For another whole range of (three-dimensional) nonlinear equations including the incompressible
Euler equations, the Navier-Stokes equations and the Relativistic Vlasov-Maxwell system (RVM system in
abbreviated form), we have the local well-posedness (at least for adequate « large » norms), but
the global well-posedness is still open. Standard results involving norms
\parallel\cdot\parallel_{n}
with

n
large provide with a lower bound for
T({\rm U}_{0})
which is typically of the form
\parallel{\rm U}_{0}\parallel_{n}^{-1}
. Starting from there, the local smooth solvability for weaker norms
{\mathcal{N}}
(with
{\mathcal{N}}\lesssim\parallel\cdot\parallel_{n}
) may not be granted. It is recognized that the challenge behind this
issue (and behind GSS) lies in the development of turbulence.

This text is devoted to the three-dimensional RVM system. The local Hadamard well-posedness for
the norm
C^{1}\times C^{2}\times C^{2}
is known
[
18
,
19
,
37
]
. The same
applies to the global weak existence
[
10
,
32
]
(without uniqueness and stability) for data in

L^{\infty}\times L^{2}\times L^{2}
(with finite energy). Our goal here is to make a significant progress at the
interface. It is to show the local smooth solvability for the norm
L^{\infty}\times H^{1}\times H^{1}
. As a
consequence, we can keep the local existence with the regularity
L^{\infty}\times H^{1}\times H^{1}
while
conserving the uniqueness (Corollary
12
).

The present section summarizes our outcomes. In Subsection
1.1
, we
specify our result (Theorem
2
) on LSS. In Subsection
1.2
, the focus is on dense,
hot, collisionless and strongly magnetized plasmas. As a by-product of our analysis, we are able to construct a
framework within which the local uniform existence of smooth solutions may be achieved while a large magnetic
field is applied (Theorem
5
). This furnishes mathematical tools for a better understanding of
plasmas during their confinement (like in fusion devices), including the several complicated behaviors that can occur.

1.1.
On the Cauchy problem for the RVM system in a strong-weak setting

The unknowns are the distribution function
{\rm f}
and the electromagnetic field
({\rm E},{\rm B})

depending on the time

The speed of light is normalized to one. We deal with the relativistic velocity

(1.3a)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\partial_{t}{\rm E}-\nabla_{x}\times{\rm B}={\rm J}:=-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\nu(\xi)\ {\rm f}(t,x,\xi)\,d\xi,

\displaystyle\nabla_{x}\cdot{\rm E}=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}{\rm f}(t,x,\xi)\,d\xi-\rho,

(1.3b)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\partial_{t}{\rm B}+\nabla_{x}\times{\rm E}=0,

\displaystyle\nabla_{x}\cdot{\rm B}=0.\hskip 18.49988pt\hskip 9.24994pt\hskip 18.49988pt\hskip 18.49988pt\

The system (

(1.5)

\nabla_{x}\cdot{\rm E}_{0}=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}{\rm f}_{0}(x,\xi)\,d\xi-\rho\,,\hskip 18.49988pt\nabla_{x}\cdot{\rm B}_{0}=0.

At the level of (

This is because there are also notable differences separating the RVM system from common QSH systems.

In kinetic theory, the time evolution

1.3

Property (

range of applications. It is related to precise quantitative information on the lifespan, and therefore it is well suited to the study

large magnetic fields. As a matter of fact, such models constitute a real challenge in plasma physics (Paragraph
1.2.1
)
and provide means (Paragraph

1.2.1.
The physical model

In many important applications such as planetary magnetospheres
(like the Van Allen Belts in the Earth situation) or fusion devices (like tokamaks), a large inhomogeneous external magnetic field

\varepsilon^{-1}\,{\rm B}_{e}(x)
is applied. Here, the number
\varepsilon
is the inverse of the electron gyrofrequency ; it is a small
dimensionless parameter which is typically in the order of

of

(1.12)

By this way, the given external magnetic field

1.5

underlying physical geometries (such as toroidal shapes). Physical plasmas are generally comprised of
a dominant part which stays at the thermodynamic equilibrium. In the relativistic framework, the reference

a steady state. This may be represented by a distribution profile for

),
Definition

gives access to large magnetic fields
{\rm B}_{a}^{\varepsilon}
.
That is, however, not the only aspect. Implementing

or

is also a way to generate rapid fluctuations,
and then to measure their quantitative impact. In Theorem

) on the remainder

and
(

) on the initial data

. This is really not demanding in comparison with the common

-estimates and so on) in nonlinear geometric optics
[

,
30
]
,
in comparison with the already improved contexts of
[
8
]
or
[

]
, and in comparison with the
strong conditions imposed in
[

and recently in
[

]
.

The regime of dense, hot and strongly magnetized plasmas is of practical interest. For this reason, it has been
intensively studied in physics. Up to now, the mathematical advances

,
10
]
are based on weak

achieves that goal with smooth solutions on a pertinent observation time (

The construction of solutions to the RVM system has a long history going back to the pioneering works of Wollman

2.1.
Historical background

We give here a quick overview of previous contributions.

2.1.1.
Local and global smooth well-posedness

The existence of local stable classical solutions dates back to
[
37
]
. The issue of global existence was
then raised in
[
18
,
19
]
. Then, much has been done to make progress on the global smooth solvability.
The advances concern small initial data
[
17
]
(much smaller than in Theorem
5
), reduced
dimensions
[
14
,
16
]
(less than the six dimensions of the actual phase space), symmetry conditions

[
35
]
(broken by the inhomogeneities of the external field
{\rm B}_{e}
), stability properties
[
31
]

(under stronger topologies), and so on. For further information, we can refer to the survey article
[
15
]
.
The most elaborate result in relation with our theme is perhaps the latest article
[
36
]
of Wei-Yang which
(among other things) can include a large electromagnetic field but which also requires (in compensation) a very small
density distribution. In view of
[
36
]
-Proposition 4.8, interpreted in our framework, this density should be of size
less that
\varepsilon^{8}\,|\ln\varepsilon|^{-11}
. This is far from the regime under consideration in Theorem
5
.
Now, for general data, many continuation criteria have been established, see
[
25
,
28
,
29
]

and the references therein. However, the global existence of classical solutions remains open. This is a longstanding and still
active problem. On the other hand, failing to reach global existence, in connection with concrete applications, we can seek
for more precise quantitative information on the life-span of smooth solutions. That is the position of the present text.

2.1.2.
About nonlinear geometric optics

The construction of oscillating solutions for
quasilinear systems of conservation laws is the core subject of nonlinear geometric optics. However, the general results

[
26
,
30
]
do not furnish optimal information when applied to the RVM system. There are several reasons
for this. The transport part (the Vlasov equation) and the integral source term (the electric courant) make things easier.
Also, importantly, the variable coefficients with
\varepsilon^{-1}
in factor (generated by
{\rm B}_{e}
) can be handled through
different arguments than the usual quite restrictive conditions.

With the goal of better exploiting the specificities of (
1.2
)-(
1.3
) and also
motivated by the applications, there has been some progress to remedy this situation. The cold configuration
for which
|\xi|\lesssim\varepsilon
, or
{\rm M}^{\varepsilon}\equiv M(|\xi|/\varepsilon)
with
M\in C^{1}_{c}({\mathbb{R}}_{+};{\mathbb{R}}_{+})
, is examined in
[
8
]
. The hot but dilute situation, for which
{\rm M}^{\varepsilon}\equiv\varepsilon\,M(\xi)
with
M\in C^{1}_{c}({\mathbb{R}}^{3};{\mathbb{R}}_{+})
and
(f_{0}^{\varepsilon},E_{0}^{\varepsilon},B_{0}^{\varepsilon})
is small in Lipschitz
norm (of size
\varepsilon
), is investigated in
[
9
]
. In
[
9
]
, large amplitude profiles

{\rm M}^{\varepsilon}\approx 1
as well as weak norms of
U^{\varepsilon}_{0}
are excluded for deeper reasons related to the
method used.

To our knowledge, Theorem
5
is completely new. Implemented in the dense, hot and strongly
magnetized framework, that is when
{\rm M}\approx 1
,
{\rm P}_{0}\approx 1
and
|{\rm B}_{e}|\approx 1
, the criteria
yielding global existence are clearly not applicable. On the other hand, all preceding approaches seem to furnish a life-span

{\mathcal{T}}^{\varepsilon}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{*}
that shrinks very rapidly to
0
when
\varepsilon
goes to
0
.

2.1.3.
Global weak existence

As is well-known, weak global solutions
are available. This has been shown by DiPerna and Lions in the seminal contribution
[
10
]
, just after

[
18
,
19
]
in the late 1980s. But such solutions are obtained by compactness methods, and
much information has not yet been provided. In the context of (
1.2
)-(
1.3
),
little can currently be said about the uniqueness, the stability, the regularity, or the (oscillating) form of these solutions.
Note however that uniqueness and stability can be addressed from the perspective of the renormalization property

[
11
]
. We refer to
[
21
]
-Paragraph 1.3 for recent developments (in a decoupled situation)
and a nice presentation of results in this direction. Recall also that weak solutions have been considered in the
framework (similar to Subsection
1.2
) of large magnetic fields in order to give partial information,
develop asymptotic models or enrich gyrokinetic theory (see for instance
[
3
]
). In view of
Theorem
5
, some strong control (related to the smoothness) on the solutions does persist. But
how is this possible, and why
? The first step (Subsection
2.2
) is to identify the underlying
difficulties ; the second step (Subsection
2.3
) is to complement our presentation by recalling the
previous strategies ; the third step (Subsection
2.4
) is to present our plan and to explain our approach.

2.2.
The impact of localizations and oscillations

Plasmas out of equilibrium can exhibit
a wide variety of complicated behaviors, which are manifested by oscillating coherent structures (in subdomains of the phase
space
[
6
,
7
]
) or by chaotic motions (shearing due to the sensitivity of characteristics under
changes of initial data, especially near separatrices). As a consequence, the Lipschitz norm of
f^{\varepsilon}
is in practice often very
large. Our aim in this subsection is not to inventory all the phenomena that can occur. Instead, we want to indicate why the
distribution function
f^{\varepsilon}
should undergo large rapid variations (when
{\rm B}_{e}\not\equiv 0
). For the sake of simplicity,
we do not (always) mention the dependence on the parameter
\varepsilon
at the level of

{\rm U}
,
U
,
f
,
E
and
B
.

In the simplified framework (
1.17
), emphasis may be placed on the role
of
f_{0}^{\varepsilon}
, and its impact on
f
and on the self-consistent electromagnetic field
(E,B)
. The presence of some

f_{0}^{\varepsilon}\not\equiv 0
has many consequences, among which :

i)

the onset of anisotropic features (in momentum variable
\xi
). A spatial localization of
f_{0}^{\varepsilon}

necessarily implies that
\nabla_{x}f\not\equiv 0
. Then, the part
\nu(\xi)\cdot\nabla_{x}f\not\equiv 0
inside
(
1.2
) is switched on, and it cannot be only a function of
|\xi|
. The same applies for
f
.

ii)

the emergence of fast oscillations. The Lorentz force
{\rm F}
can be decomposed into

(2.1)

{\rm F}=F+\varepsilon^{-1}\,\nu(\xi)\times{\rm B_{e}}(x)\,,\hskip 18.49988ptF(t,x,\xi):=E(t,x)+\nu(\xi)\times B(t,x).

Due to the anisotropy of
f
, when computing the contribution
(\xi\times{\rm B_{e}})\cdot\nabla_{\xi}{\rm f}
, the above fast
rotating term is certainly activated. This means that, as allowed by (
1.9c
),
\parallel f\parallel_{W^{1,\infty}}

is designed to be not uniformly bounded with respect to
\varepsilon\in]0,1]
. The condition (
1.9c
) does not preclude the
choice of initial data
f_{0}^{\varepsilon}
having arbitrarily large Lipschitz norms (as compared to
{\rm P}_{0}
and
{\rm S}_{0}
).

The above considerations deserve to be highlighted by a concrete example (more elaborate models can be found in

[
6
,
7
,
12
]
). Let
\phi:{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
be a global diffeomorphism. Select

f_{0}^{\varepsilon}(x,\xi):={\mathcal{P}}\bigl(x,\varepsilon^{-1}\,\phi(x),\xi\bigr)\,,\hskip 18.49988pt{\mathcal{P}}\in C^{1}_{c}({\mathbb{R}}^{3}_{x}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{3}_{\xi};{\mathbb{R}}).

The profile
{\mathcal{P}}
may be periodic or (partially) compactly supported with respect to its second variable (to represent a localized
input of electrons for the initialization of an electron beam). For adequate choices of
{\mathcal{P}}
and
\phi
, we have clearly access
to (
5.18
), and Theorem
5
does apply. Now, we can get a preview of what happens at least when
neglecting most of the terms inside (
1.2
). With this in mind, we can consider the elementary transport equation

(2.2)

\partial_{t}f+\varepsilon^{-1}\ \bigl(\nu(\xi)\times{\rm B}_{e}(x)\bigr)\cdot\nabla_{\xi}f=0,\hskip 18.49988pt{\rm B}_{e}(x)=b_{e}(x_{3})\ {}^{t}(0,0,1).

In (
2.2
), the field
{\rm B}_{e}
has a fixed (vertical) direction and a varying amplitude
b_{e}
(depending only on
x_{3}
to satisfy

\nabla\cdot B_{e}=0,\;=\nabla\times B_{e}=0
) . In the cylindrical coordinate system for
\xi
, with

\xi=(r\,\cos\theta,r\,\sin\theta,\xi_{3}),\hskip 9.24994pt\ r^{2}=\xi_{1}^{2}+\xi_{2}^{2},\hskip 9.24994pt\ \theta\in{\mathbb{T}},\hskip 9.24994pt\ \tilde{\mathcal{P}}(\cdot,r,\theta,\xi_{3}):={\mathcal{P}}(\cdot,r\,\cos\theta,r\,\sin\theta,\xi_{3}),

the solution is simply given by

(2.3)

\tilde{f}(x,r,\theta,\xi_{3}):=f(t,x,r\,\cos\theta,r\,\sin\theta,\xi_{3})=\tilde{\mathcal{P}}\Bigl(x,\frac{\phi(x)}{\varepsilon},r,\theta+\frac{t\,b_{e}(x_{3})}{\varepsilon\,\sqrt{1+r^{2}}},\xi_{3}\Bigr).

The transport part
\nu(\xi)\cdot\nabla_{x}f
of (
1.2
) has been removed at the level of (
2.2
).
This suppresses the first effect i). But still we are faced with ii). Indeed, when
\partial_{\theta}\tilde{\mathcal{P}}\not\equiv 0
, the first order
derivatives in almost all directions (time, space and momentum) of the expression
\tilde{f}
given by (
2.3
) are of
large size
\varepsilon^{-1}
. As a consequence, uniform Lipschitz estimates (with respect to
\varepsilon
) are certainly not available. The same is
sure to apply to the solutions provided by Theorem
5
.

In the dilute situation
[
9
]
, the expression
{\rm U}^{\varepsilon}_{a}
and the perturbation
f^{\varepsilon}_{a}

are of small amplitude
\varepsilon
. This weight
\varepsilon
which can be put in factor of the data can be used to absorb the oscillations
at the frequency
\varepsilon^{-1}
. This boils down to a sort of weakly nonlinear regime. Then, uniform Lipschitz estimates may become
available and, by this way, oscillating approximate solutions to (
1.2
) can be constructed and justified by standard
arguments. There is nothing like this in the framework of Theorem
5
.

In fact, the main reason why it is complicated to achieve
{\mathcal{T}}^{\varepsilon}\sim 1
is the following. So far, the methods which
have been implemented require, at a moment or another, to compute derivatives of
U
. But this is proving to be very costly in
terms of negative powers of
\varepsilon
because these derivatives are - in all reasonable norms - at least of size
\varepsilon^{-1}
,
compromising the existence of a uniform life-span. By contrast, we are able here to avoid this problem.

2.3.
Previous approaches for smooth solutions

Estimates on
{\rm P}
(or on similar quantities) have been a central part in the study of the RVM system because
singularities do not develop as long as the momentum support of
{\rm f}(t,\cdot)
remains bounded. This is Glassey-Strauss’
conditional theorem
[
18
]
which has inspired many works and which has been revisited in
[
4
]
. Such bounds
have been achieved by pursuing three noteworthy lines of research.

2.3.1.
The historical procedure

As already mentioned, the standard method has been
initiated in
[
17
,
18
,
19
]
. It is based on a representation formula for
({\rm E},{\rm B})
, see

[
18
]
-Theorem 3 and
[
16
]
for its simplified two-dimensional version. In this line, the stability under
(very) small perturbations in Lipschitz norm of smooth solutions (such as
{\rm U}^{\varepsilon}_{a}
) has been investigated in
[
31
]
.

2.3.2.
An alternative path

The second way is to proceed similarly to what has been
done in Klainerman-Staffilani
[
24
]
or even simpler in Bouchut-Golse-Pallard
[
4
,
5
]
. These
authors have obtained with an economy of means Lipschitz estimates on the field
({\rm E},{\rm B})
. To this end, they have
implemented three principal arguments :

a) commuting vector field techniques
[
24
]
for the wave equation related to
Maxwell’s equations ;

b) a non-resonant smoothing property
[
5
]
stating that
1\pm\nu(\xi)\cdot\omega

with
\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
remains away from zero. This property of ellipticity deteriorates when
|\xi|

grows up, and this is one of the difficulties ;

c) a division lemma, see
[
4
]
-Lemma 3.1, whose aim is to convert transversal derivatives
to the light cone into the derivative
\partial_{t}+\nu(\xi)\cdot\nabla_{x}
(appearing in the transport part of RVM).

2.3.3.
Global existence through sharp decay estimates

The third method
is to implement (by way of a fixed-point iteration) sup-norm controls and decay estimates (related to the dispersion) on

({\rm E},{\rm B})
, as was achieved by Wei-Yang in
[
36
]
. But this requires controls in
C^{1}\times C^{2}\times C^{2}
which are at the origin of the strong smallness condition (
\leq\varepsilon^{8}\,|\ln\varepsilon|^{-11}
)
on
f^{\varepsilon}_{0}
noted above.

The three approaches
2.3.1
,
2.3.2
and
2.3.3

are based on different assumptions ; they resort to distinct techniques ; they produce complementary information ; and they are
good starting points. But, to achieve our goal, they need to be supplemented by novel procedures. We will adapt these
methods by following at some points a different logic, yielding other consequences.

2.4.
Plan of the text, and main ideas

This article is organized around three sections
(
3
,
4
and
5
) whose contents are detailed in below.

2.4.1.
Content of Section
3

In Section
3
, we deal with smooth compactly supported solutions to the RVM system
(
1.2
)-(
1.3
). We derive a representation formula (Proposition
7
)
which is reminiscent of
[
18
]
. In contrast to
[
4
,
18
]
, we do not look at
({\rm E},{\rm B})
.
Instead, we focus on the
momentum increment
{\rm D}(t,y,\eta)
, see Definition
6
. The construction of

{\rm D}(t,y,\eta)
is related to the characteristic
(X,\Xi)(t,y,\eta)
emanating from a point
(y,\eta)

and associated with the Vlasov equation (
1.2
). Equivalently,
{\rm D}(t,y,\eta)
is the difference

\langle\Xi(t,y,\eta)\rangle-\langle\eta\rangle
which is built from the solution
(X,\Xi)
to the ordinary differential
equation (
3.1
). The quantity
{\rm D}
depends on
t
. It is a dynamical quantity which allows to control
the size
{\rm P}(t)
of the momentum support. Obviously, when
{\rm E}\equiv 0
, we find that
{\rm D}
is invariant,
that is
{\rm D}(t)={\rm D}(0)
for all time
t
. But certainly this becomes not true in the presence of interactions. This
raises an interesting question :

Question 1.
Can we identify the factors that alter
{\rm D}\,
? And evaluate their quantitative effects
?

The determination of
{\rm D}
is based on
|\Xi|
, not on
\Xi
. This remark is important because, in the
regime under consideration, the directions of
\Xi
may be strongly oscillating with respect to the parameter
\varepsilon\in]0,1]
,
see for instance (
2.3
),
and therefore they would be impractical to control. In fact, resorting on
{\rm D}
is a way to avoid rapid fluctuations of

\Xi/|\Xi|
, and to dispense with having to implement the (possibly large) momentum speed of propagation.
The quantity
{\rm D}
is recovered after some integration (with underlying cancellation effects), while still informing
about the expanse of the momentum support.

To evaluate
{\rm D}
, a first option is to consider (
3.10
), and to directly extract the sup-norm of
E
.
But this would imply pointwise estimates (like the estimates of von Wahl
[
34
]
or the Strichartz inequalities

[
22
,
33
]
) at the level of a three-dimensional wave equation with source term
\partial_{t}{\rm J}
.
This would take us back to the control of derivatives with again important losses in terms of negative powers of
\varepsilon
.
This is why we adopt an alternative strategy which is partly inspired by
[
4
,
28
]
. As in
[
4
]
,
in Paragraph
3.1.1
, we interpret the RVM system in terms of the Lienard-Wiechert potential

{\rm u}(t,x,\xi)
. As in
[
28
]
, we aim at substituting for (
3.10
) integral expressions which are
not based on
dt
but instead which integrate with respect to the time, the space and the velocity. But, to this end, we
exploit other arguments. Our main innovation to prove (
3.4
) is :

- to perform a Radon transform (with respect to the position variable
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
). The Radon transform (see the book

[
20
]
and Paragraph
3.1.2
) allows to convert
{\rm f}(t,x,\xi)
into
{\rm g}(t,\omega,p,\xi)
and

{\rm u}(t,x,\xi)
into
{\rm v}(t,\omega,p,\xi)
. The equation (
3.23
) for
{\rm g}(\cdot,\omega,\cdot,\xi)

becomes (with respect to

and

) a one-dimensional transport equation ; the equation (
3.22
) for

{\rm v}(\cdot,\omega,\cdot,\xi)
becomes (with respect to
t
and
p
) a one-dimensional wave equation. These
one-dimensional features entail many simplifications. Indeed, we recover a simple one dimensional mean field equation
(comparable to the model studied in
[
13
]
) with all the three dimensional geometry encoded in the sphere
(with the angle
\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
serving as a parameter).

- to interpret
{\rm D}
as an oscillatory integral. By this way, the geometry of propagation is driven by the phase
\pm|X(s)-x|+s-r
which reveals very well the joint properties of the transport equation (
1.2
)
and of the wave equation inside (

It follows that :

a) the commutating vector fields considerations of
[

are bypassed ;

b) the non-resonant smoothing property of
[

]
reduces to the manipulation of a non-zero
coefficient (vanishing to

when

tends to
+\infty
) occurring in a non-stationary phase ;

c) the division lemma of
[
4

is replaced by a straightforward argument (there is no more three
spatial directions but instead only one direction

with a parameter
\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
).

On the other hand, the return through the inverse Radon transform to the original spatial configuration
(in

and

The content of (

{\rm f}
,

{\rm E}
or
{\rm B}

{\rm f}
,
{\rm E}
or
{\rm B}

it relies on integral computations with respect to the volume element

Henceforth, we obtain a connection between the value of
{\rm D}(t,y,\eta)

\text{\small$\boldsymbol{\mathscr{E}}$}(t):=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\langle\xi\rangle\ {\rm f}(t,x,\xi)\ dx\,d\xi+\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}|{\rm E}(t,x)|^{2}\ dx+\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}|{\rm B}(t,x)|^{2}\ dx,

\text{\small$\boldsymbol{\mathscr{E}}$}(t)=\text{\small$\boldsymbol{\mathscr{E}}$}_{\!0}:=\text{\small$\boldsymbol{\mathscr{E}}$}(0)\,,\hskip 18.49988ptt\geq 0.

Thus, the search for pointwise estimates on

In that sense, we can again assert that our approach is at the junction between results about weak solutions (which rely
on the sole preservation of

computation of derivatives.

Amongst other things, the formula (

Effect of nonlinear interactions (the quadratic terms in the Vlasov equation) together with the coupling (electric current in Maxwell’s

3.1.2.
Reminders on the Radon transform

The Radon transform
[
20
]
is the map defined by

\begin{array}[]{rcl}R:C^{0}_{c}({\mathbb{R}}^{3};{\mathbb{C}})&\longrightarrow&C^{0}_{c}(\mathbb{S}^{2}\times{\mathbb{R}};{\mathbb{C}})\\
{\rm f}(x)&\longmapsto&R{\rm f}(\omega,p),\end{array}

where

(3.12)

{\rm g}(\omega,p):=R{\rm f}(\omega,p):=\int_{H_{\omega,p}}{\rm f}(x)\,dm(x)={\rm g}(-\omega,-p),

and
dm
is the Euclidean measure on the hyperplane
H_{\omega,p}:=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\,;\,\omega\cdot x=p\}
.
Recall that
R{\rm f}
is linked to the Fourier transform
\hat{\rm f}
through the relation

\hat{\rm f}(\rho\,\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}e^{-i\rho p}\,R{\rm f}(\omega,p)\,dp,\hskip 18.49988pt\rho\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}.

It follows that

(3.13)

R(\partial_{x_{i}}{\rm f})(\omega,p)=\omega_{i}\ \partial_{p}{\rm g}(\omega,p)\,,\hskip 18.49988ptR(\Delta{\rm f})(\omega,p)=\partial^{2}_{pp}{\rm g}(\omega,p).

As explained for instance in line (38)-p. 19 of
[
20
]
, with
\eta=\rho\,\omega\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
, we
obtain that

(3.14)

\begin{array}[]{rl}{\rm f}(x)&\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}e^{ix\cdot\eta}\,\hat{\rm f}(\eta)\,d\eta=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\!\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{i\rho(\omega\cdot x)}\,\hat{\rm f}(\rho\,\omega)\,\rho^{2}\,d\rho\,d\omega\\
&\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\,\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\Bigl\{\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\rho^{2}\,e^{i\rho(\omega\cdot x)}\Bigl[\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}e^{-i\rho p}\,R{\rm f}(\omega,p)\,dp\Bigr]\,d\rho\Bigr\}\,d\omega,\end{array}

where the change of variables
(\omega,\rho)
into
(-\omega,-\rho)
has been exploited to pass from the first to the second line.
This yields the (three dimensional) inversion formula

(3.15)

{\rm f}(x)=R^{-1}{\rm g}(x):=-\frac{1}{2}\,\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\partial^{2}_{pp}{\rm g}(\omega,\omega\cdot x)\,d\omega.

Also observe that

(3.16)

\forall n\,\in{\mathbb{N}},\hskip 18.49988pt(i\,\rho)^{n}\ \hat{\rm f}(\rho\omega)={\mathcal{F}}_{p}\bigl((\partial^{n}_{p}R{\rm f})(\omega,\cdot)\bigr)(\rho).

As a consequence, we have

(3.17)

\forall n\,\in{\mathbb{N}},\hskip 18.49988pt(\partial^{n}_{p}R{\rm f})(\omega,p)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}e^{i\rho(p-\omega\cdot x)}\,(i\,\rho)^{n}\,{\rm f}(x)\,d\rho\,dx.

The relation (
3.13
) indicates that a derivative in
x
is on the Radon side a derivative in
p
. But
the Radon transform is clearly associated with a smoothing effect (due to the integration). And thereby, its inverse can consume
derivatives of
{\rm g}
, as demonstrated on (
3.15
). This means that gains of derivatives (in
p
) are hidden
behind the integration with respect to
\omega
. This phenomenon is detected in Subsection
3.2
and exploited
in Subsection
3.3
.

3.2.
Two oscillatory integrals

As in the case of the Fourier transform, the study of partial
differential equations with the Radon transform may reveal the role of oscillatory integrals. In the present context, these integrals
are of two types : the first (Paragraph
3.2.1
) involves a compact surface (the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}
) ; the
second (Paragraph
3.2.2
) implies the whole space
{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
. In both cases, the aim is to take
advantage of cancellation properties induced by oscillations.

3.2.1.
Oscillatory integrals on the sphere

In view of (
3.16
), a derivative
\partial_{p}

on the Radon side costs a multiplication by
\rho
. On the other hand, as can be inferred from (
3.17
), this loss may be
associated with oscillatory integrals implying phases looking like
\rho\,(\omega\cdot{\rm X}+\tau)
. It is compensated at the
level of (
3.15
) by an integration with respect to
\omega
. Thus, there is some underlying spatial averaging effect
(on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
). This is highlighted below.

Lemma 8

(Gain of one derivative)
.

Fix
{\rm X}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\setminus\{0\}
and
(\rho,\tau)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. We have

(3.18)

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}e^{i\rho\,(\omega\cdot{\rm X}+\tau)}\ (i\rho)\ d\omega=\frac{2\pi}{|{\rm X}|}\,\sum_{\pm}\pm e^{i\rho\,(\pm|{\rm X}|+\tau)}=4\pi\,i\,e^{i\rho\,\tau}\,\frac{\sin(\rho\,|{\rm X}|)}{|{\rm X}|}.

Proof.
Let
{\mathcal{R}}
be a rotation such that
{\mathcal{R}}\,({\rm X}/|{\rm X}|)={}^{t}(0,0,1)
. We can change
\omega
into

\upomega:={\mathcal{R}}\omega
and then work in spherical coordinates, that is with

(3.19)

\hskip 9.24994pt\upomega=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\cos\varphi\,\sin\vartheta\\
\sin\varphi\,\sin\vartheta\\
\cos\vartheta\end{array}\right),\hskip 18.49988pt\varphi\in[0,2\pi],\hskip 18.49988pt\vartheta\in[0,\pi],\hskip 18.49988ptd\upomega=\sin\vartheta\,d\vartheta\,d\varphi.

Since
{\mathcal{R}}^{-1}\upomega\cdot{\rm X}=\upomega\cdot{\mathcal{R}}{\rm X}
, this furnishes

\begin{array}[]{rl}\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}&\displaystyle e^{i\rho\,(\omega\cdot{\rm X}+\tau)}\ (i\rho)\ d\omega=\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}e^{i\rho\,({\mathcal{R}}^{-1}\upomega\cdot{\rm X}+\tau)}\ (i\rho)\ d\upomega=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}e^{i\rho\,\bigl(|X|\,\upomega\cdot{\mathcal{R}}({\rm X}/|{\rm X}|)+\tau\bigr)}\ (i\rho)\ d\upomega\\
&\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{i\rho\,(|{\rm X}|\cos\vartheta+\tau)}\,(i\rho)\ \sin\vartheta\,d\vartheta\,d\varphi=-\frac{2\pi}{|{\rm X}|}\int_{0}^{\pi}\partial_{\vartheta}\Bigl\{e^{i\rho\,[|{\rm X}|\cos\vartheta+\tau)}\Bigr\}\ d\vartheta\\
&\displaystyle=\displaystyle\frac{2\pi}{|{\rm X}|}\,\Bigl\{e^{i\rho[+|{\rm X}|+\tau]}-e^{i\rho[-|{\rm X}|+\tau]}\Bigr\},\end{array}

which is exactly (
3.18
).

\Box

Let
\nu\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
. Applying the differential operator
\nu\cdot\nabla_{\rm X}
to the identity (
3.18
),
we end up with

(3.20)

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\nu\cdot\omega\ e^{i\rho\,(\omega\cdot{\rm X}+\tau)}\ \rho^{2}\ d\omega=2\pi\ \frac{\nu\cdot{\rm X}}{|{\rm X}|^{3}}\,\sum_{\pm}(\pm 1-i\,\rho\,|{\rm X}|)\ e^{i\rho\,(\pm|{\rm X}|+\tau)}.

3.2.2.
Oscillatory integrals on the whole space

Replace
{\rm X}
by

Looking at the right hand side of (

Lemma 9

(Passage from singular weights to traces)
.

Let
{\rm f}:{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
be
a compactly supported function of class
C^{1}
. Let
{\rm K}:\mathbb{S}^{2}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
be a bounded function.
Fix
{\rm X}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
,
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
\tau\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Then, for
\alpha\leq 2
, we have

(3.21)

\begin{array}[]{rl}\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}&\displaystyle e^{i\rho[\pm|{\rm X}-x|+\tau]}\ {\rm K}\Bigl(\frac{x-{\rm X}}{|x-{\rm X}|}\Bigr)\ \frac{{\rm f}(x)}{|{\rm X}-x|^{\alpha}}\ d\rho\,dx\\
&\displaystyle=2\pi\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}|\tau|^{2-\alpha}\ H(\mp\tau)\ {\rm K}(\omega)\ {\rm f}(X+|\tau|\,\omega)\ d\omega,\end{array}

where
H
is the Heaviside function (in the half-maximum convention which is important at least when
\alpha=2

and
\tau=0
), namely

H(\tau):=\frac{1}{2}\bigl[\mathbb{1}_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}(\tau-)+\mathbb{1}_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}(\tau+)\bigr]=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcl}0&\text{ if }&\tau<0,\\
1/2&\text{ if }&\tau=0,\\
1&\text{ if }&\tau>0.\\
\end{array}\right.

Proof.
In spherical coordinates
r\,\omega
for
x-{\rm X}
, we have to deal with

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\rm K}(\omega)\,\Bigl(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}r^{2-\alpha}\ {\rm f}({\rm X}+r\,\omega)\ e^{-i\rho\,(\mp r-\tau)}\,dr\Bigr)\,d\rho\,d\omega\\
\displaystyle\hskip 18.49988pt=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}{\rm K}(\omega)\,\Bigl\{\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\Bigl(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\psi_{\mp}({\rm r})\,e^{-i\rho\,{\rm r}}\ d{\rm r}\Bigr)\,d\rho\Bigr\}\,d\omega=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}{\rm K}(\omega)\,\Bigl\{\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}{\mathcal{F}}(\psi_{\mp})(\rho)\,d\rho\Bigr\}\,d\omega\,,\end{array}

where we have changed
r
into
{\rm r}:=\mp r-\tau
, and where we have introduced the function
\psi_{\mp}:{\mathbb{R}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
(depending on
\omega
) given by

\psi_{\mp}({\rm r}):=|{\rm r}+\tau|^{2-\alpha}\ \mathbb{1}_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\bigl(\mp({\rm r}+\tau)\bigr)\ {\rm f}\bigl({\rm X}+|{\rm r}+\tau|\,\omega).

The function
\psi_{\mp}
is compactly supported. It is bounded and piecewise
C^{1}
as long as
\alpha\leq 2
with only one
possible discontinuity when
\alpha=2
(located at
{\rm r}=-\tau
). Thus, we can apply the Dirichlet condition
for inversion of Fourier integrals which furnishes

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}{\mathcal{F}}(\psi_{\mp})(\rho)\,d\rho=2\pi\ ({\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\circ{\mathcal{F}})(\psi_{\mp})(0)=\pi\,\bigl(\psi_{\mp}(0+)+\psi_{\mp}(0-)\bigr).

After substitution, we find (
3.21
).

\Box

3.3.
Proof of Proposition
7

The demonstration is done in three stages.

3.3.1.
The Radon side picture

Introduce

{\rm v}(t,\omega,p,\xi):=R\bigl[{\rm u}(t,\cdot,\xi)\bigr](\omega,p),\hskip 18.49988pt{\rm g}(t,\omega,p,\xi):=R\bigl[{\rm f}(t,\cdot,\xi)\bigr](\omega,p).

Under the action of the Radon transform, the three-dimensional wave equation is transformed into a

{\rm v}_{1}\equiv 0

(3.22)

(\partial^{2}_{tt}-\partial_{pp}^{2}){\rm v}={\rm g},\hskip 18.49988pt{\rm v}_{\mid t=0}=0,\hskip 18.49988pt\partial_{t}{\rm v}_{\mid t=0}={\rm v}_{1}.

On the other hand, from the Vlasov equation, we can deduce that

(3.23)

\partial_{t}{\rm g}+\nu(\xi)\cdot\omega\ \partial_{p}{\rm g}+\text{\rm div}_{\xi}\,R({\rm f}\,{\rm F})=0,\hskip 18.49988pt{\rm g}_{\mid t=0}={\rm g}_{0}:=R\bigl[{\rm f}_{0}(\cdot,\xi)\bigr].

At the level of (
3.11
), the integral
\breve{\rm D}
is built with four types of derivative :
\partial_{s}{\rm u}
and
\partial_{x_{i}}{\rm u}

with
i\in\{1,2,3\}
. In Subparagraph a), we show that
\partial_{s}{\rm u}
can be expressed as a function of
\partial^{2}_{pp}{\rm g}
. In Subaragraph b), we do the same for
\nabla_{x}{\rm u}
. By this way, in Subparagraph c), we can extract a reformulation
of
\breve{\rm D}
in terms of one type of derivative, namely
\partial^{2}_{pp}{\rm g}
. By this way, contrary to the division lemma

[
4
,
24
]
, looking at
\breve{\rm D}
as depending on
{\rm g}
instead of
{\rm u}
allows to reduce the kinds
of derivatives which are needed : we can concentrate on
\partial_{p}
only.

\bullet
a) Computation of
\partial_{s}{\rm u}
.
The solution to the one-dimensional wave equation (
3.22
) can be obtained through Duhamel’s formula

(3.24)

{\rm v}(t,\omega,p,\xi)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{p-t}^{p+t}{\rm v}_{1}(\omega,y,\xi)\,dy+\frac{1}{2}\int\!\!\int_{\triangle(t,p)}{\rm g}(s,\omega,y,\xi)\,ds\,dy,

where
\triangle(t,p)
is the triangle

\triangle(t,p):=\bigl\{\,(s,y)\,;\,s\in[0,t]\ ,\ p-(t-s)\leq y\leq p+(t-s)\,\bigr\}.

From (
3.22
) with
{\rm v}_{1}\equiv 0
, we find also

(\partial^{2}_{tt}-\partial_{pp}^{2})(\partial_{t}{\rm v})=\partial_{t}{\rm g},\hskip 18.49988pt\partial_{t}{\rm v}_{\mid t=0}=0,

as well as

\partial_{t}(\partial_{t}{\rm v})_{\mid t=0}={\rm g}_{\mid t=0}+(\partial_{pp}^{2}{\rm v})_{\mid t=0}={\rm g}_{0}.

Applying (
3.24
) to the above equation, we get

(3.25)

\begin{array}[]{rl}\partial_{t}{\rm v}(t,\omega,p,\xi)&\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{p-t}^{p+t}{\rm g}_{0}(y,\xi)\,dy+\frac{1}{2}\int\!\!\int_{\triangle(t,p)}\partial_{s}{\rm g}(s,\omega,y,\xi)\,ds\,dy\\
&\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{p-t}^{p+t}{\rm g}_{0}(y,\xi)\,dy+\frac{1}{2}\Bigl\{\int_{p-t}^{p}\Bigl(\int_{0}^{y-p+t}\partial_{s}{\rm g}(s,\omega,y,\xi)\,ds\Bigr)\,dy\\
&\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt+\int^{p+t}_{p}\Bigl(\int_{0}^{-y+p+t}\partial_{s}{\rm g}(s,\omega,y,\xi)\,ds\Bigr)\,dy\Bigr\}\\
&\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\bigl[{\rm g}(s,\omega,p+t-s,\xi)+{\rm g}(s,\omega,p-t+s,\xi)\bigr]\,ds.\end{array}

From (
3.25
), we can infer that

(3.26)

\begin{array}[]{rl}\displaystyle\partial_{s}{\rm u}(s,x,\xi)&\displaystyle=R^{-1}(\partial_{s}{\rm v})(s,x,\xi)=-\frac{1}{2}\,\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}(\partial^{2}_{pp}\partial_{s}{\rm v})(s,\omega,\omega\cdot x,\xi)\,d\omega\\
&\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4}\,\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,\sum_{\pm}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\Bigl\{\int_{0}^{s}\partial^{2}_{pp}{\rm g}\bigl(r,\omega,\omega\cdot x\pm(s-r),\xi\bigr)\,dr\Bigr\}\,d\omega.\end{array}

Since
\partial^{2}_{pp}{\rm g}(r,\cdot,\xi)
is in view of (
3.12
) an even function, we know that

(3.27)

\partial^{2}_{pp}{\rm g}\bigl(r,-\omega,-\omega\cdot x-(s-r),\xi\bigr)=\partial^{2}_{pp}{\rm g}\bigl(r,\omega,\omega\cdot x+(s-r),\xi\bigr),

and therefore, changing
\omega
into
-\omega
, we can deduce that

(3.28)

\partial_{s}{\rm u}(s,x,\xi)=-\frac{1}{2}\,\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\Bigl\{\int_{0}^{s}\partial^{2}_{pp}{\rm g}\bigl(r,\omega,\omega\cdot x+(s-r),\xi\bigr)\,dr\Bigr\}\,d\omega.

\bullet
b) Computation of
\nabla_{x}{\rm u}
. From (
3.22
) with
{\rm v}_{1}\equiv 0
, we can extract

(3.29)

(\partial^{2}_{tt}-\partial_{pp}^{2})(\partial_{p}{\rm v})=\partial_{p}{\rm g},\hskip 18.49988pt(\partial_{p}{\rm v})_{\mid t=0}=0,\hskip 18.49988pt\partial_{t}(\partial_{p}{\rm v})_{\mid t=0}=0.

Applying (
3.24
), this yields

(3.30)

\begin{array}[]{rl}\partial_{p}{\rm v}(t,\omega,p,\xi)&\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int\!\!\int_{\triangle(t,p)}\partial_{p}{\rm g}(s,\omega,y,\xi)\,ds\,dy\\
&\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\bigl[{\rm g}(s,\omega,p+t-s,\xi)-{\rm g}(s,\omega,p-t+s,\xi)\bigr]\,ds.\end{array}

On the other hand, from (
3.13
), we have

R\bigl[\nu\circ\Xi(s)\cdot\nabla_{x}{\rm u}\bigr](s,\omega,p,\xi)=\nu\circ\Xi(s)\cdot\omega\ \partial_{p}{\rm v}(s,\omega,p,\xi),

and therefore, using (
3.27
) again and (
3.30
), we obtain that

(3.31)

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\nu\circ\Xi(s)\cdot\nabla_{x}{\rm u}(s,x,\xi)=R^{-1}\bigl[\nu\circ\Xi(s)\cdot\omega\ \partial_{p}{\rm v}\bigr](s,x,\xi)\\
\hskip 18.49988pt\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\,\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\nu\circ\Xi(s)\cdot\omega\ \partial^{2}_{pp}(\partial_{p}{\rm v})(s,\omega,\omega\cdot x,\xi)\,d\omega\\
\hskip 18.49988pt\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\,\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\Bigl\{\int_{0}^{s}\nu\circ\Xi(s)\cdot\omega\ \partial^{2}_{pp}{\rm g}(r,\omega,\omega\cdot x+s-r,\xi)\,dr\Bigr\}\,d\omega.\end{array}

\bullet
c) Summary. It suffices to plug (
3.28
) and (
3.31
) inside (
3.11
)
to get a representation formula for
\breve{\rm D}
on the Radon side

\breve{\rm D}=\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}\Bigl\{\int_{0}^{s}\nu\circ\Xi(s)\cdot\bigl(\omega+\nu(\xi)\bigr)\ \partial^{2}_{pp}{\rm g}\bigl(r,\omega,\omega\cdot X(s)+s-r,\xi\bigr)\,dr\Bigr\}\,ds\,d\omega\,d\xi.

Recall (
3.13
) which says that
\partial^{2}_{pp}=R\Delta
. Thus, the computation of
\breve{\rm D}
seems
to consume two derivatives of
{\rm f}
. The aim of the next paragraph is to show that this is not the case. The goal
is to remove the presence of
\partial^{2}_{pp}
inside
{\rm D}
.

3.3.2.
Analysis through oscillatory integrals

To better understand the content of
\breve{\rm D}
, we can apply (
3.17
) with
n=2
to exhibit the following
oscillatory integral

\begin{array}[]{rl}\displaystyle\breve{\rm D}=-\frac{1}{16\pi^{3}}\int_{0}^{t}\!\int_{0}^{s}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}}\!\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\!\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\Bigl(\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\nu\circ\Xi(s)\cdot\bigl(\omega+\nu(\xi)\bigr)&\displaystyle e^{i\rho[\omega\cdot X(s)-\omega\cdot x+s-r]}\,\rho^{2}\ d\omega\Bigr)\\
&\displaystyle\times\,{\rm f}(r,x,\xi)\ ds\,dr\,d\rho\,dx\,d\xi.\end{array}\hskip 18.49988pt\hskip 9.24994pt

We can apply Lemma
8
and (
3.20
) with
{\rm X}=X(s)-x
and
\tau=s-r
to get

\begin{array}[]{rl}\displaystyle\breve{\rm D}=&\displaystyle\sum_{\pm}\mp\,\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}\!\int_{0}^{s}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}}\!\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\!\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\frac{\nu\circ\Xi(s)\cdot\bigl(X(s)-x\bigr)}{|X(s)-x|^{3}}\ {\rm f}(r,x,\xi)\ e^{i\rho\,[\pm|X(s)-x|+s-r]}\ ds\,dr\,d\rho\,dx\,d\xi\\
+&\displaystyle\sum_{\pm}\pm\,\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}\!\int_{0}^{s}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}}\!\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\!\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\Bigl(\frac{\nu\circ\Xi(s)\cdot\nu(\xi)}{|X(s)-x|}\pm\frac{\nu\circ\Xi(s)\cdot\bigl(X(s)-x\bigr)}{|X(s)-x|^{2}}\Bigr)\\
&\hskip 18.49988pt\hskip 18.49988pt\hskip 18.49988pt\hskip 18.49988pt\hskip 18.49988pt\hskip 9.24994pt\ \times\,{\rm f}(r,x,\xi)\ (i\rho)\ e^{i\rho\,[\pm|X(s)-x|+s-r]}\ ds\,dr\,d\rho\,dx\,d\xi.\end{array}

In the second sum, there is still
\rho
in factor (which corresponds to the lost of one derivative). The next idea is to eliminate
this weight
\rho
by exploiting the underlying presence of oscillations. This is the principle of non-stationary phase. To this end,
the strategy is to perform an integration by parts with respect to
r
and
x
. As usual, this operation costs time and spatial
derivatives of the symbol
{\rm f}
. It must be done without introducing unmanageable derivatives of
{\rm f}
. In practice,
taking into account the Vlasov equation, we can convert the derivative
\partial_{r}+\nu(\xi)\cdot\nabla_{x}
into derivatives with
respect to
\xi
. With this in mind, we look at

(3.32)

\begin{array}[]{rl}\displaystyle\bigl(\partial_{r}+\nu(\xi)\cdot\nabla_{x}\bigr)&\displaystyle\Bigl\{e^{i\rho\,[\pm|X(s)-x|+s-r]}\Bigr\}\\
&\displaystyle=\Bigl(-1\pm\frac{x-X(s)}{|x-X(s)|}\cdot\nu(\xi)\Bigr)\ (i\rho)\ e^{i\rho\,[\pm|X(s)-x|+s-r]}.\end{array}

Since
|\nu(\xi)|<1
, the multiplicative factor in the right hand side is negative, and therefore it can be inverted.
This argument exploits a microlocal ellipticity property. As mentioned in b) of Paragraph
2.3.2
, it is
referred to as a non-resonant smoothing property
[
5
]
.

The identity (
3.32
) connects
\partial_{r}+\nu(\xi)\cdot\nabla_{x}
to the multiplication by
\rho
. In view of
(
3.16
), this amounts to apply the derivative
\partial_{p}
. Now, recall that
R(\partial_{x_{i}}{\rm f})=\omega_{i}\,\partial_{p}(R{\rm f})
, and thereby
\omega_{i}\,\partial_{p}
may be viewed on the Radon side as a condensed version
of the spatial derivatives
\partial_{x_{i}}
. Thus, a link is established between
\partial_{r}+\nu(\xi)\cdot\nabla_{x}
and

\partial_{x_{i}}
.

Historically
[
4
,
24
]
, this was done by converting the derivative
\partial_{x_{i}}{\rm u}

inside (
3.11
) into
\partial_{r}{\rm f}+\nu(\xi)\cdot\nabla_{x}{\rm f}
. Here, there is no need of such division lemma,
alinea c) of Paragraph
2.3.2
. We just observe that the light cones (related to the wave equation for the
electromagnetic fields), which may be viewed as the level surfaces in
{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
of the phase functions
\pm|X(s)-x|+s-r
, are transversal to the derivative
\partial_{r}+\nu(\xi)\cdot\nabla_{x}
(which is related to the time-spatial
transport in the Vlasov equation). With the help of (
3.32
), in the second sum defining
\breve{\rm D}
, we
can interpret the coefficient which is in factor of
{\rm f}
according to

\begin{array}[]{rl}\displaystyle\Bigl(\frac{\nu\circ\Xi(s)\cdot\nu(\xi)}{|X(s)-x|}&\displaystyle\pm\frac{\nu\circ\Xi(s)\cdot\bigl(X(s)-x\bigr)}{|X(s)-x|^{2}}\Bigr)\ (i\rho)\ e^{i\rho\,[\pm|X(s)-x|+s-r]}\\
=&\displaystyle-k_{\mp}(s,x,\xi)\ \bigl(\partial_{r}+\nu(\xi)\cdot\nabla_{x}\bigr)\Bigl\{e^{i\rho\,[\pm|X(s)-x|+s-r]}\Bigr\},\end{array}

with

k_{\mp}(s,x,\xi):=\frac{1}{|X(s)-x|}\ K_{\mp}\Bigl(s,\frac{x-X(s)}{|x-X(s)|},\xi\Bigr)\,,\hskip 18.49988ptK_{\mp}(s,\omega,\xi):=\frac{\nu\circ\Xi(s)\cdot\bigl(\nu(\xi)\mp\omega\bigr)}{1\mp\omega\cdot\nu(\xi)}.

Let
T_{k}
be the multiplicative operator by the function
k
, that is

(3.33)

T_{k}({\rm f})(r,s,x,\xi):=k(s,x,\xi)\,{\rm f}(r,x,\xi).

Now, we can integrate by parts with respect to
r
and
x
to obtain

\begin{array}[]{rl}\displaystyle\breve{\rm D}=&\displaystyle\sum_{\pm}\mp\,\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}\!\int_{0}^{s}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}}\!\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\!\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\frac{\nu\circ\Xi(s)\cdot\bigl(X(s)-x\bigr)}{|X(s)-x|^{3}}\ {\rm f}(r,x,\xi)\ e^{i\rho\,[\pm|X(s)-x|+s-r]}\ ds\,dr\,d\rho\,dx\,d\xi\\
+&\displaystyle\sum_{\pm}\pm\,\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\Bigl\{\int_{0}^{s}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\bigl(\partial_{r}+\nu(\xi)\cdot\nabla_{x}\bigr)\bigl(T_{k_{\mp}}({\rm f})\bigr)\ e^{i\rho\,[\pm|X(s)-x|+s-r]}\ dx\,dr\Bigr\}\ d\xi\,d\rho\,ds\\
-&\displaystyle\sum_{\pm}\pm\,\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\Bigl[\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}T_{k_{\mp}}({\rm f})\ e^{i\rho\,[\pm|X(s)-x|+s-r]}\ dx\Bigr]_{0}^{s}\ d\xi\,d\rho\,ds.\end{array}

This yields two boundary terms (
{\rm D}_{b0}
and
{\rm D}_{bs}
issued respectively from
r=0
and
r=s
),
a term
{\rm D}_{l}
including the integrands which have
{\rm f}
in factor, as well as a contribution
{\rm D}_{n}
which
implies derivatives of
{\rm f}
. More precisely, we have
\breve{\rm D}={\rm D}_{b0}+{\rm D}_{bs}+{\rm D}_{l}+{\rm D}_{n}

with

{\rm D}_{b0}=\sum_{\pm}\pm{\rm D}_{b0}^{\pm}\,,\hskip 18.49988pt{\rm D}_{bs}=\sum_{\pm}\mp{\rm D}_{bs}^{\pm}\,,\hskip 18.49988pt{\rm D}_{l}=\sum_{\pm}\pm{\rm D}_{l}^{\pm}\,,\hskip 18.49988pt{\rm D}_{n}=\sum_{\pm}\pm{\rm D}_{n}^{\pm}\,,

where

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\rm D}_{b0}^{\pm}:=\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}T_{k_{\mp}}({\rm f})(0,s,x,\xi)\ e^{i\rho\,[\pm|X(s)-x|+s]}\ ds\,d\rho\,dx\,d\xi,

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\rm D}_{bs}^{\pm}:=\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}T_{k_{\mp}}({\rm f})(s,s,x,\xi)\ e^{\pm i\rho\,|X(s)-x|}\ ds\,d\rho\,dx\,d\xi,

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\rm D}_{l}^{\pm}:=\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{s}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}T_{\tilde{k}_{\mp}}({\rm f})\ e^{i\rho\,[\pm|X(s)-x|+s-r]}\ ds\,dr\,d\rho\,dx\,d\xi,

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\rm D}_{n}^{\pm}:=\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{s}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}T_{k_{\mp}}\bigl(\partial_{r}{\rm f}+\nu(\xi)\cdot\nabla_{x}{\rm f}\bigr)\ e^{i\rho\,[\pm|X(s)-x|+s-r]}\ ds\,dr\,d\rho\,dx\,d\xi.

By construction, we find

\tilde{k}_{\mp}:=-\frac{\nu\circ\Xi(s)\cdot\bigl(X(s)-x\bigr)}{|X(s)-x|^{3}}+\nu(\xi)\cdot\nabla_{x}k_{\mp}=\frac{1}{|X(s)-x|^{2}}\ \tilde{K}_{\mp}\Bigl(s,\frac{x-X(s)}{|x-X(s)|},\xi\Bigr),

with

\tilde{K}_{\mp}(s,\omega,\xi):=\frac{1}{\langle\xi\rangle^{2}}\ \frac{\nu\circ\Xi(s)\cdot\bigl(\omega\mp\nu(\xi)\bigr)}{\bigl(1\mp\omega\cdot\nu(\xi)\bigr)^{2}}.

Now, the time spatial derivative
\partial_{r}{\rm f}+\nu(\xi)\cdot\nabla_{x}{\rm f}
can be exchanged with velocity
derivatives. Indeed, using the Vlasov equation, it can be converted into derivatives with respect to
\xi
(which are harmless
because the coefficients are smooth in
\xi
and because the phase does not depend on
\xi
). We find that

{\rm D}_{n}^{\pm}=\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{s}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\nabla_{\xi}k_{\mp}\cdot{\rm F}\ {\rm f}\ e^{i\rho\,[\pm|X(s)-x|+s-r]}\ ds\,dr\,d\rho\,dx\,d\xi.

On the other hand, in
{\rm D}^{\pm}_{l}
and
{\rm D}^{\pm}_{n}
, we can switch the order of integrations according to

\int_{0}^{t}\Bigl(\int_{0}^{s}\cdots\,dr\Bigr)\,ds=\int_{0}^{t}\Bigl(\int_{r}^{t}\cdots\,ds\Bigr)\,dr.

3.3.3.
Epilog

In general, the Fourier analysis as well as the Radon analysis of nonlinear
partial differential equations lead to complex formulas. And indeed, the above oscillatory integrals seem complicated.
Surprisingly, they can be significantly simplified by applying Lemma
9
. As a matter of fact, all the
computations can be made explicit :

-

Study of
{\rm D}_{b0}
. We take
{\rm K}=K_{\mp}
,
{\rm X}=X(s)
,
\alpha=1
and
\tau=s\geq 0
.
We find that
{\rm D}_{b0}^{+}=0
and
{\rm D}_{b0}=-{\rm D}_{b0}^{-}
. With
{\rm W}_{0}=-s\,K_{+}/(4\pi)
, we
can recognize
{\rm D}_{0}
as in (
3.4a
).

-

Study of
{\rm D}_{bs}
. We take
{\rm K}=K_{\mp}
,
{\rm X}=X(s)
,
\alpha=1
and
\tau=0
to see that

{\rm D}_{bs}^{\pm}=0
.

-

Study of
{\rm D}_{l}
. We take
{\rm K}=\tilde{K}_{\mp}
,
{\rm X}=X(s)
,
\alpha=2
and
\tau=s-r\geq 0

to obtain
\mathcal{D}_{l}^{+}=0
so that
{\rm D}_{l}=-{\rm D}_{l}^{-}
. This is coherent with (
3.4c
)
where
{\rm W}_{l}=-\tilde{K}_{+}/(4\pi)
as in (
3.5b
).

-

Study of
{\rm D}_{n}
. We deal with a vector valued version of (
3.21
) where
{\rm f}
is replaced by

{\rm F}\,{\rm f}
. We take
{\rm K}=\nabla_{\xi}K_{\mp}
,

{\rm X}=X(s)
,
\alpha=1
and
\tau=s-r\geq 0
to see that
\mathcal{D}_{n}^{+}=0
and therefore that
{\rm D}_{n}=-{\rm D}_{n}^{-}
. By this way, with
{\rm W}_{n}=-(s-r)\,\nabla_{\xi}K_{+}/(4\pi)
, we find that
{\rm D}_{n}
is given by (
3.4
).

The proof of Proposition
7
is now complete.

4.
The control of the momentum spread

In this section, we consider a solution
{\rm U}
to the Cauchy problem (
1.2
)-(
1.3
)-(
1.4
),
assumed to be smooth, compactly supported (in
\xi
), and defined on
[0,T[
where
T\equiv T({\rm U}_{0})\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{*}\cup\{+\infty\}

is the maximum life-span of this smooth solution. From (
3.2
), we know that the support of
{\rm f}(t,\cdot)

is the image by the map
(X,\Xi)(t,\cdot)
of the support of
{\rm f}_{0}
. Given
t\in[0,T[
, we can define the
maximal size
{\rm Q}(t)
of the spatial support of
{\rm f}(t,\cdot)
, which is

(4.1)

\hskip 18.49988pt{\rm Q}(t):=\inf\ \bigl\{R\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\,;\,{\rm f}(t,x,\xi)=0\ \text{for all}\ \xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\ \text{and for all}\ x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\ \text{with}\ R\leq|x|\bigr\},

as well as the maximal size
{\rm P}(t)
of the momentum support of
{\rm f}(t,\cdot)
, which is

(4.2)

\hskip 18.49988pt{\rm P}(t):=\inf\ \bigl\{R\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\,;\,{\rm f}(t,x,\xi)=0\ \text{for all}\ x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\ \text{and for all}\ \xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\ \text{with}\ R\leq|\xi|\bigr\}.

By construction, we have

\text{supp}\ {\rm f}\subset\bigl\{(t,x,\xi)\in[0,T[\times{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\,;\,|x|\leq{\rm Q}(t)\,,\,|\xi|\leq{\rm P}(t)\,\bigr\}.

The quantity
{\rm Q}
yields a control on the size of the spatial domain of influence. In view of the first equation of (
3.1
),
the spatial speed of propagation is bounded by one, so that

(4.3)

{\rm Q}(t)\leq{\rm Q}_{0}+t\,,\hskip 18.49988pt{\rm Q}_{0}:={\rm Q}(0).

Both Vlasov and Maxwell’s equations have a finite spatial speed of propagation (bounded by
1
). Thus, exploiting the notion
of region of influence, to prove the local smooth solvability, it suffices to work with solutions that are compactly supported with
respect to the space variable
x
. To simplify, we can localize the spatial and momentum support in the same ball (say of
size
{\rm P}_{0}
). With this in mind, we replace the condition inside (
1.6
) on
\text{\rm supp}\ {\rm f}_{0}
by

(4.4)

\text{\rm supp}\ {\rm f}_{0}\subset B(0,{\rm P}_{0}]\times B(0,{\rm P}_{0}].

On the other hand, the quantity
{\rm P}
gives a bound on the extent of the momentum domain of influence. It provides insight
into the momentum spread. Without a control involving the sup-norm of
{\rm F}
(that is equivalently of
{\rm E}
and

{\rm B})
, the second equation of (
3.1
) does not provide with a bound for the momentum speed of propagation. This is
usually resolved by looking at Lipschitz bounds on
{\rm U}=({\rm f},{\rm E},{\rm B})
. From the pioneering works

[
17
,
18
,
19
,
37
]
on smooth solutions, we know that there exists a time
{\mathcal{T}}_{s}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{*}

and a continuous function
{\mathcal{F}}_{s}:[0,{\mathcal{T}}_{s}]\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
(the subscript
s
stands for
s
mooth) depending both
on the Lipschitz norm of
{\rm U}_{0}
such that

(4.5)

{\rm P}(t)\leq{\mathcal{F}}_{s}(t)\,,\hskip 18.49988pt\forall\,t\in[0,{\mathcal{T}}_{s}]\,,\hskip 18.49988pt0<{\mathcal{T}}_{s}\leq T.

A key issue is whether (
4.5
) remains true under less restrictive criteria on the initial data
{\rm U}_{0}
. Our purpose
here is to remove the Lipschitz condition on
{\rm f}_{0}
and to relax the cost of two derivatives concerning
({\rm E}_{0},{\rm B}_{0})
.

Proposition 10

(Control on the momentum spread by mild information)
.

Fix
{\rm P}_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, and
consider the corresponding subspace
\mathfrak{N}
adjusted as in (
1.6
). Given any
{\rm S}_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{*}
,
select initial data
{\rm U}_{0}\in\mathfrak{N}
satisfying
{\mathcal{N}}({\rm U}_{0})\leq{\rm S}_{0}
with
{\mathcal{N}}
as in (
1.7
).
Then, there exist a time
{\mathcal{T}}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{*}
and a continuous increasing function
{\mathcal{F}}:[0,{\mathcal{T}}]\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, both
depending only on
{\rm S}_{0}
so that
{\mathcal{T}}\equiv{\mathcal{T}}({\rm S}_{0})
and
{\mathcal{F}}\equiv{\mathcal{F}}({\rm S}_{0};\cdot)
, such that

(4.6)

\hskip 18.49988pt{\rm P}(t)\leq{\mathcal{F}}({\rm S}_{0};t),\hskip 18.49988pt\forall\,t\in\bigl[0,\min\bigl(T({\rm U}_{0});{\mathcal{T}}({\rm S}_{0})\bigr)\bigr[.

Moreover, under (
4.4
), the behavior of

4.1.2.
Comparison of measures, microlocal pictures and related difficulties

All the integrals

(except for

It is worth noting that this

-

the part

-

the part

By assembling

. This combination of the variables

and
\omega
emerges also at the level of (

(note that

in case of

). This furnishes a link to the Lebesgue measure
dx
. To this end, we can (for instance)
exploit Lemma 2.2 of
[
28
]
which is recalled below for the sake of completeness.

Lemma 13
.

[by C. Pallard] Let

. The map

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{\mathcal{X}}\,:\,[r,t]\times{\mathbb{S}^{2}}&\longrightarrow&{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\\
(s,\omega)&\longmapsto&{\mathcal{X}}(s,\omega):=X(s)+(s-r)\,\omega\end{array}

is a
C^{1}
-diffeomorphism onto some region of

. Its Jacobian

is given by

(4.16)

J(r,s,\omega):=(s-r)^{2}\ \bigl(1+\omega\cdot\nu\circ\Xi(s)\bigr)\,,\hskip 18.49988ptJ\ ds\,d\omega=dx.

Thus, the pushforward (by
{\mathcal{X}}
) of the measure
ds\,d\omega
is absolutely continuous with respect
to the Lebesgue measure : it is just
J^{-1}\,dx
. Now, we can extend this argument at a microlocal level. The Radon
transform is an integral (hence non local) operator and, as such, it is not suitable for transferring localizations. However,
what counts inside the
{\rm D}^{\rm a}_{\star}
is
{\mathcal{X}}
, which can effectively translates (microlocal) localizations in terms
of
(s,\omega,\xi)
into (microlocal) localizations in terms of

changing. The advantage is that working with
(s,\omega,\xi)
instead of
(x,\xi)
is much easier.

This transfer of microlocal localizations is achieved by the map
{\mathcal{X}}
which is built (through
X
) on a
complete nonlinear evolution (on a special solution to the RVM system) and on the choice of a specific characteristic
(namely
X
for a special selection of

and
\eta_{0}
). Passing to the phase space, we have to deal with the

One of the main interests of Proposition

,
{\rm E}
and

4.2.
Weighted integrals

The goal of this subsection is to estimate the contributions
provided by the
{\rm D}^{\rm a}_{\star}
with
\star\in\{0,h,l,ne,nb\}
. This will be done in separate paragraphs, one
for each
{\rm D}^{\rm a}_{\star}
. Before starting, we would like to accurately define the scope of our discussion. Indeed,
different courses of action are possible when studying the
{\rm D}^{\rm a}_{\star}
.

In the perspective of continuation criteria, one might attempt to minimize the powers of
\langle{\rm P}\rangle_{\infty}

needed to control the
{\rm D}^{\rm a}_{\star}
. As in
[
25
,
28
,
29
]
, it seems that additional
conditions (to be identified) are needed to recover the global existence. This interesting option is not pursued here.

On the other hand, to fit in with Proposition
10
, one can insist on the role of
{\rm S}_{0}
when
looking at the momentum increment. This is the path that we follow below. However, when doing this, a cautionary note
is in order. This is because the functional
{\mathcal{N}}
(and thereby
{\rm S}_{0}
) involves different types of norms : first, the
sup-norm concerning
{\rm f}_{0}
; and secondly, the Sobolev norm
W^{1,\bar{p}}
in the case of
({\rm E}_{0},{\rm B}_{0})
.
Let us explain the origin of this distinction :

-

The handling of the
{\rm D}^{\rm a}_{\star}
with
\star\in\{0,l,ne,nb\}
does not prove to be demanding in terms
of regularity. It will only require the use of
\parallel f_{0}\parallel_{0}
and
\text{\small$\boldsymbol{\mathscr{E}}$}_{\!0}
.

-

The manipulation of
{\rm D}^{\rm a}_{h}
could be based just on a sup-norm estimate concerning
{\rm E}_{h}
. It is
the transcription of such uniform bound in terms of the initial data
({\rm E}_{0},{\rm B}_{0})
that generates the implementation
of
W^{1,\bar{p}}
. By the way, note that the use of
\bar{p}\in]3/2,3[
instead of
\bar{p}=+\infty
(or even higher levels of
regularity) is a subtle refinement that will be clarified in Paragraph
4.2.2
.

In other words, the focus is on the minimal regularity required on the complete initial data
{\rm U}_{0}

in order to control the quantities
{\rm D}^{\rm a}_{\star}
. In so doing, for the sake of simplicity, we have highlighted in
Proposition
10
the role of the sole parameter
{\rm S}_{0}
, which serves in fact to cover different aspects.
For instance, from (
1.9
), we get that

(4.23)

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\langle\xi\rangle\ {\rm f}_{0}(x,\xi)\ dx\,d\xi\lesssim\langle{\rm P}\rangle_{0}^{7}\ {\rm S}_{0}\,,\hskip 18.49988pt\langle{\rm P}\rangle_{0}:=(1+{\rm P}_{0}^{2})^{1/2},

where the symbol
\lesssim
is for
\leq C
with some (universal) constant
C
not depending on
{\rm P}_{0}

or
{\rm S}_{0}
. On the other hand, the Sobolev embedding theorem (which holds since
\bar{p}<3
) ensures that

{\rm E}_{0}\in L^{\tilde{p}}
for some
\tilde{p}>3
. By interpolation, we get that

.
Now, since

, we have

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}|{\rm E}_{0}(x)|^{2}\ dx+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}|{\rm B}_{0}(x)|^{2}\ dx\lesssim{\rm S}_{0}^{2}.

Coming back to (

) and using (

and small

\text{\small$\boldsymbol{\mathscr{E}}$}_{\!0}\lesssim\langle{\rm P}\rangle_{0}^{7}\ {\rm S}_{0}+{\rm S}_{0}^{2}\lesssim{\rm S}_{0}.

It is clear that the
{\rm S}_{0}
of (

{\rm S}_{0}
of (

-norm of
({\rm E}_{0},{\rm B}_{0})
. These
contributions are mixed (with different powers) at the level of (

much material, our decision is to not make the various origins of
{\rm S}_{0}
apparent in the final statement.
But the interested reader can easily trace
{\rm S}_{0}
in the forthcoming analysis.

[Control of

] We have

\hskip 18.49988pt{\rm D}_{h}^{\rm a}\lesssim{\rm S}_{0}\ (t+1)\ \langle{\rm P}\rangle_{\infty}(t)^{2/3}.
