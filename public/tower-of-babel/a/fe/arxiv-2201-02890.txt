Lazy Lagrangians with Predictions for Online Learning
Daron Anderson, George Iosifidis, Douglas J. Leith

Abstract
We consider the general problem of online convex optimization with time-varying additive constraints in the presence of predictions for the next cost and constraint functions. A novel primal-dual algorithm is designed by combining a Follow-The-Regularized-Leader iteration with prediction-adaptive dynamic steps. The algorithm achieves 𝒪 ⁡ ( T 3 − β 4 ) \mathcal{O}(T^{\frac{3-\beta}{4}}) regret and 𝒪 ⁡ ( T 1 + β 2 ) \mathcal{O}(T^{\frac{1+\beta}{2}}) constraint violation bounds that are tunable via parameter β ∈ [ 1 / 2 , 1 ) \beta\!\in\![1/2,1) and have constant factors that shrink with the predictions quality, achieving eventually 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 ) \mathcal{O}(1) regret for perfect predictions. Our work extends the FTRL framework for this constrained OCO setting and outperforms the respective state-of-the-art greedy-based solutions, without imposing conditions on the quality of predictions, the cost functions or the geometry of constraints, beyond convexity.

Introduction

The online convex optimization (OCO) framework introduced in
[
1
]
and
[
2
]
is employed to solve various learning problems ranging from spam filtering to portfolio selection and network routing, cf.
[
3
]
. At each round
t
an algorithm selects an action
x_{t}
from a convex set
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
and incurs cost
f_{t}(x_{t})
, where the convex function
f_{t}:\mathcal{X}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is revealed after
x_{t}
is decided. The algorithm’s performance is measured using the metric of

\mathcal{R}_{T}=\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\big(f_{t}(x_{t})-f_{t}(x^{\star})\big),

(1)

which quantifies the difference of the total cost from that of the best action selected with hindsight
x^{\star}\!\in\!\arg\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{t}(x)

\{x_{t}\}
that ensure sublinear regret, i.e.,
\mathcal{R}_{T}=o(T)
.

A practical extension of this setting is the
constrained
OCO framework, where the actions must satisfy long-term constraints of time-varying functions:

g_{t}(x)\triangleq\big(g_{t}^{(1)}(x),g_{t}^{(2)}(x),\ldots,g_{t}^{(d)}(x)\big)\preceq 0,

which are unknown when
x_{t}
is decided. In this case we are additionally interested in achieving sublinear total constraint violation,
\mathcal{V}_{T}=o(T)
, where:

\mathcal{V}_{T}=\Big\|\left[\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}g_{t}(x_{t})\right]_{+}\Big\|.

(2)

Constrained OCO algorithms have applications in advertising with budget constraints, control of capacitated communication systems, queuing problems
[
4
]
, etc. Nevertheless, they are notoriously hard to tackle. In particular,
[
5
]

x^{\star}\in\mathcal{X}_{T}^{\text{max}}=\Big\{x\in\mathcal{X}\,\Big|\,\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}g_{t}(x)\preceq 0\Big\}.

Subsequent works considered either benchmarks that respect the constraints for short time windows
[
6
]
; dynamic benchmarks
\{x_{t}^{\star}\}
that satisfy separately each
t
-round constraint
g_{t}(x_{t}^{\star})\!\preceq\!0

[
7
]
,
[
8
]
; or benchmarks
[
9
]
,
[
10
]
restricted in set:

\mathcal{X}_{T}=\left\{x\in\mathcal{X}\,\Big|\,g_{t}(x)\preceq 0,\,\forall t\leq T\right\}.

Special cases of
\mathcal{X}_{T}
are considered in
[
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
]
where
g_{t}(x)=g(x)
,
\forall t

[
15

An aspect that has received less attention, however, is whether constrained OCO algorithms can be assisted by predictions for the next-round functions
f_{t}
and
g_{t}

Leveraging predictions to improve learning algorithms is attracting increasing interest and has many practical applications, e.g., in data caching
[
16
]

[
17

how much can predictions improve the performance of constrained OCO algorithms and how can we accrue these benefits in the presence of inaccurate, potentially even adversarial, predictions?

Discussion
. We can see from Theorem
1

\mathcal{R}_{T}
and
\mathcal{V}_{T}
, which diminish proportionally to their accuracy. The bound of
\mathcal{R}_{T}
is further reduced when
\sigma\!=\!\sqrt{L_{f}}/D
and it settles to zero for perfect predictions, i.e. when
x_{t}=z_{t},\forall t
, and:

\epsilon_{t}=0,\,\,\,\,\,\delta_{t}=0,\,\,\,\,\,\xi_{t}=0,\,\,\,\forall t\leq T,

while the same is not true for
\mathcal{V}_{T}
. Moreover, this theorem reveals the
tension
between
\mathcal{R}_{T}
and
\mathcal{V}_{T}
. Indeed, observe that
-\mathcal{R}_{T}
appears in the bound of
\mathcal{V}_{T}
which means that when
\{x_{t}\}
outperforms
x^{\star}
, we might incur higher constraint violation.

From Algorithm
1
we can see that LLP requires predictions for the next gradient
\tilde{c}_{t}\!=\!\nabla\tilde{f}_{t}(\tilde{x}_{t})
, next cost function
\tilde{g}_{t}(\cdot)
, and next constraint point
\tilde{g}_{t}(\tilde{x}_{t})
. It is important here to note the timing of these predictions. Namely, updating
x_{t}
requires
\tilde{c}_{t}
and
\tilde{g}_{t}(\cdot)
and access to regularizers
r_{0:t-1}
which are calculated using the prediction errors up to slot
t-1
. Knowledge of
\tilde{c}_{t}
is the standard prediction that all prior works employ, e.g., see
[
22
]
,
[
20
]
and references therein. On the other hand, since we have not linearized the constraint function, the respective predictions involve function
\tilde{g}_{t}(\cdot)
and its next-round value
\tilde{g}_{t}(\tilde{x}_{t})
. In Section
IV
we present a version of LLP where we linearize the constraints and hence use only gradient predictions for the constraints – similarly to cost functions.

The complexity of LLP is analogous to its greedy-based counterparts — sans the additional prescient update (
5

IV
Less Computations And Predictions

We discuss next how to reduce the computation and memory requirements of LLP by using non-proximal primal regularizers; and the impact of linearizing the constraint functions on the required predictions.

IV-A

LLP with Non-proximal Regularizers

In this new algorithm, called LLP2, we use the same dual regularizer (
8
) and update (
6
), but the general primal regularizer:

\displaystyle r_{0}(x)=\mathbf{I}_{\mathcal{X}}(x)\,\,\text{and}\,\,r_{t}(x)=\sigma_{t}\|x\|^{2}/2,\,\,\forall t\geq 1,\,\text{with:}

\displaystyle\sigma_{t}=\sigma\left(\sqrt{h_{1:t}+\mu_{t+1}}-\sqrt{h_{1:t-1}+\mu_{t}}\right)

(24)

where
\mu_{t}\!\triangleq\!E_{m}\!+\!a_{t-1}Gt\Delta_{m}
. The new updates are:

\displaystyle\!\!x_{t}\!=\!\arg\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\textstyle\left\{\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}\mathcal{L}_{i}(x,\lambda_{i})\!+\tilde{c}_{t}^{\top}x\!+\lambda_{t}^{\top}\tilde{g}_{t}(x)\right\},\!

(25)

\displaystyle\!\!z_{t}\!=\!\arg\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\textstyle\left\{\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}\mathcal{L}_{i}(x,\lambda_{i})\!+c_{t}^{\top}x\!+\lambda_{t}^{\top}g_{t}(x)\right\},\!\!

(26)

where
\mathcal{L}_{t}(x,\lambda)
is defined using
r_{t}(x)
from (
24
), and note the off-by-one regularizer of (
26
) compared to (
5
).

These non-proximal regularizers facilitate solving for
\{x_{t}\}
and
\{z_{t}\}
since
r_{1:t}(x)\!=\!\sigma_{1:t}\|x\|^{2}/2
involves only one quadratic term and can be represented in constant memory space, unlike
r_{1:t}(x)\!=\!\sum_{i=1}^{t}\sigma_{i}\|x\!-x_{i}\|^{2}/2
that expands with time. On the other hand, non-proximal updates yield looser bounds, cf.
[
28
]
, and require a new saddle-point analysis. Interestingly, they do not worsen the growth of LLP2, but do prevent it from achieving
\mathcal{R}_{T}=\!\mathcal{O}(1)
for perfect predictions.

Theorem 3
.

Under Assumptions (

)-(

and

satisfying (

), LLP2 ensures for every

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{T}\!\leq\!\widehat{B}_{T}\!\triangleq\!2\Big(\!\sigma D^{2}\!+\!\frac{L_{f}}{\sigma}\!\Big)\!\sqrt{h_{1:T}\!+\!\mu_{T+1}}\!+\!\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\xi_{t}^{2}}{\phi_{0:t-1}}\!\!\!

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{T}\!\leq\!\sqrt{2\phi_{0:T-1}(\widehat{B}_{T}\!-\!\mathcal{R}_{T})}\!+\!\frac{2L_{g}}{\sigma}\!\sqrt{h_{1:T}\!+\!\mu_{T+1}}\!\!\!

Corollary 2

LLP2 achieves the same regret bounds as those described in Theorem

for LLP in the general case, and under perfect predictions it ensures:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{T}\!=\!\mathcal{O}\Big(T^{\frac{1-\beta}{2}}\Big),\,\,\,\,\mathcal{V}_{T}\!=\mathcal{O}\Big(T^{\frac{1+\beta}{2}}\Big),\,\,\,\,\,\,\beta\!\in\![0,1).

For example, LLP2 with perfect predictions and

IV-B

LLP with Linearized Constraints

Another way to reduce the computation load of LLP is to linearize the constraint function. This, however, is not trivial since we cannot recover
\mathcal{V}_{T}
by simply using the convexity of
\{g_{t}\}
, as we do with
\mathcal{R}_{T}
and the linearization of
\{f_{t}\}
. Hence, we use linear proxies for the constraint and its prediction in each round
t
:

\displaystyle g_{t}^{\ell}(x)=g_{t}(x_{t})+\nabla g_{t}(x_{t})^{\top}(x-x_{t}),

(27)

\displaystyle\tilde{g}_{t}^{\ell}(x)=\tilde{g}_{t}(\tilde{x}_{t})+\nabla\tilde{g}_{t}(\tilde{x}_{t})^{\top}(x-\tilde{x}_{t}).

(28)

LLP with linearized constraints runs similarly to Algorithm
1
, but uses predictions
\tilde{c}_{t}
,
\nabla\tilde{g}_{t}(\tilde{x}_{t})
and
\tilde{g}_{t+1}(\tilde{x}_{t+1})
, i.e., does not need to predict the entire constraint function — nor
\tilde{x}_{t}
, despite appearing in (
28
). Interestingly, this does not affect its performance.

Lemma 3
.

LLP with linearized constraints and predictions given by (
27
)-(
28
), achieves the
\mathcal{R}_{T}
,
\mathcal{V}_{T}
bounds and convergence rates in Theorems
1
and
2
, respectively.

The proof of the Lemma and the details of the linearized LLP can be found in the Appendix. Whether it is more challenging to predict the next constraint gradient or the next constraint function, is a question pertaining to the problem at hand, and one can select the version of LLP that is more suitable for that.

V

In this section we consider the special type of constraints that are linearly-perturbed, which was studied first in
[

. In detail, in this case the constraints and their predictions are:

\displaystyle g_{t}(x)=g(x)+b_{t}\quad\text{and}\quad\tilde{g}_{t}(x)=g(x)+\tilde{b}_{t}.

(29)

where
b_{t},\tilde{b}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are the unknown per-slot perturbations that are added to the fixed (and known) function component

. This simplification has important ramifications for the analysis and, eventually, improves the bounds of LLP as follows:

Lemma 4
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
1

29

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{T}\leq 0,\,\,\,\mathcal{V}_{T}=\mathcal{O}(T^{(1+\beta)/2}),\,\,\,\,\,\,\text{perfect predict.},\beta\!\in\![0,1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{T}=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{T}),\,\,\,\mathcal{V}_{T}=\mathcal{O}(T^{5/8}),\,\,\text{worst predictions},\beta\!=\!1/2

This result improves the bounds for the case of general constraints of the previous section, and yields only

[

– which however cannot benefit from predictions. On the contrary, we see that with LLP, due to its prediction-adaptive steps, we get no regret for perfect predictions and, interestingly, the constant factors of the bounds shrink commensurately with the predictions’ accuracy. This becomes clear if we express the bounds as:

\displaystyle\!\!\mathcal{R}_{T}\!\leq B_{T}\!\triangleq\!A_{1}\sqrt{h_{1:T}}+\min\left\{\!2a\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\xi_{t}^{2}},\,A_{2}T^{1-\beta}\right\}

and note that in this case the quantity

VI
Conclusions

LLP differs from related algorithms since the primal and dual updates are
lazy
. This allows an FTRL-based analysis, which is widely used in fixed-constraints OCO algorithms (
x\in\mathcal{X}
) but is new in the context of time-varying constrained OCO (
x\in\mathcal{X}_{T}
). The LLP order bounds, even with worst-case or no predictions, are competitive with existing algorithms while dropping several restrictive and often impractical assumptions these are using. Indeed, prior algorithms with time-varying constraints require strongly convex cost and constraint functions or linearly-perturbed fixed constraints, and rely on the Slater condition. Other proposals assume time-invariant constraints, still rely on the Slater condition and on a fixed and known time horizon
T
(which most often is not available); and need access to all Lipschitz constants and constraint bounds. Crucially, these prior works do not benefit from the availability of (potentially inaccurate) predictions. When the latter are perfect, LLP achieves
\mathcal{R}_{T}=\mathcal{O}(1)
and
\mathcal{V}_{T}=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{T})
. Last but not least, our framework is unified as it and can run without predictions (setting them zero) since we impose no assumptions on their quality, and can be applied to problems with time-invariant constraints.

Lemma 6
.

Proof.
