Quantum walk for SU(1,1)
Liwei Duan

Abstract
We propose a scheme to implement the quantum walk for SU(1,1) in the phase space, which generalizes those associated with the Heisenberg-Weyl group. The movement of the walker described by the SU(1,1) coherent states can be visualized on the hyperboloid or the Poincaré disk. In both one-mode and two-mode realizations, we introduce the corresponding coin-flip and conditional-shift operators for the SU(1,1) group, whose relations with those for Heisenberg-Weyl group are analyzed. The probability distribution, standard deviation and the von Neumann entropy are employed to describe the walking process. The nonorthogonality of the SU(1,1) coherent states precludes the quantum walk for SU(1,1) from the idealized one. However, the overlap between different SU(1,1) coherent states can be reduced by increasing the Bargmann index k k , which indicates that the two-mode realization provides more possibilities to simulate the idealized quantum walk.

Introduction

The classical random walk has been employed as a framework to understand and utilize the stochastic processes in a wide spectrum of scientific disciplines
Barber et al. 1970
. The superposition and entanglement inherent in the quantum theory introduce some novel perspectives. The combination of quantum theory with the classical random walk provides a new framework,
i.e.
quantum walks
Aharonov et al. 1993
;
Kempe 2003

Venegas-Andraca 2012

Kempe 2003
;
Venegas-Andraca 2012
. It is also noteworthy that the quantum entanglement is inherent in the quantum walk
Venegas-Andraca 2012
;
Carneiro et al. 2005
;
Abal et al. 2006
, which distinguishes it from its classical counterpart.

Quantum walks can be regarded as one of the most fruitful outcomes of quantum information theory
Kadian et al. 2021
, as they underlie the development for other models of computation and help in designing quantum algorithms with speed-ups over the classical ones
AMBAINIS 2003
;
Portugal 2013
;
Farhi and Gutmann 1998
. Quantum walks also pave the way towards the development of universal quantum computation
Childs 2009
;
Childs et al. 2013
;
Lovett et al. 2010
. In addition to the potential applications in quantum information and quantum computation, quantum walks also serve as a powerful tool to simulate various physical phenomena, such as the Landau-Zener transition

Depending on the timing used to perform evolution operators, quantum walks are generally classified into two categories
Venegas-Andraca 2012
: discrete and continuous quantum walks. For the discrete quantum walk, the evolution operator of the system is performed only in discrete time steps
Aharonov et al. 1993
, while for the continuous quantum walk, the corresponding evolution operator can be performed with no timing restrictions at all
Farhi and Gutmann 1998
. In this paper, we mainly focus on the former case. The implementation of discrete quantum walks generally requires two basic operations
Kempe 2003
;
Venegas-Andraca 2012
: coin-flip operation which determines the state of the coin, and the conditional-shift operation which determines the movement of the walker.

The phase space provides a platform to perform the quantum walk
Travaglione and Milburn 2002
;
Omanakuttan and Lakshminarayan 2018
;
Schmitz et al. 2009
.
Previous studies mainly focus on the phase plane associating with the Heisenberg-Weyl group. Based on the harmonic oscillator, the quantum walks over a circle
Sanders et al. 2003
;
Xue and Sanders 2008
;
Ramasesh et al. 2017
or a line
Schmitz et al. 2009
;
Matjeschk et al. 2012
on the phase plane have attracted persistent attention. The coherent state is feasible in the experiments
Zhang et al. 1990
, which can be chosen to describe the walker. The number of steps of quantum walk is limited by the nonorthogonality of the coherent states
Sanders et al. 2003
;
Schmitz et al. 2009
;
Matjeschk et al. 2012
.
There exist various phase spaces, which are intimately connected with the dynamical group of each physical problem
Zhang et al. 1990
. Recently, the quantum walk in the phase space has been extended to the Bloch sphere, which is closely related to the SU(2) group
Duan 2022
. A spin cluster described by the spin coherent state serves as the walker. An additional spin plays the role of a coin, who interacts with the spin cluster and determines its rotation on the Bloch sphere.

In this paper, we generalize the quantum walk to the SU(1,1) group. The SU(1,1) group has been employed in various branches, such as quantum optics
Wodkiewicz and Eberly 1985
;
Gerry 2001
, Bose-Einstein condensates
Lv et al. 2020
;
Lyu et al. 2020
;
Chen et al. 2020
;
Cheng and Shi 2021
and quantum metrology
Yurke et al. 1986
;
Ou and Li 2020
, etc.
With regard to the quantum walk for SU(1,1), the hyperboloid or the Poincaré disk provides a platform to visualize the walking process.
The corresponding condition-shift operator is closely related to that of the quantum walk for Heisenberg-Weyl group, which indicates that one can extend the well-studied experimental setups for Heisenberg-Weyl group to that for SU(1,1). The SU(1,1) coherent state can be used to describe walker’s location, whose superpositions can be generated during the walking process and have potential applications in quantum sensing
Cardoso et al. 2021
;
Akhtar et al. 2022
.
The paper is structured as follows. In Sec.
II
, we introduce the Heisenberg-Weyl group, and demonstrate the corresponding quantum walk over a circle on the phase plane. The conditional-shift operator and coin-flip operator are introduced, which will be employed to construct corresponding operators for SU(1,1). In Sec.
III
, we first revisit the basic properties of the SU(1,1) group. The phase space for SU(1,1) corresponds to a hyperboloid or a Poincaré disk. Then, the quantum walk on the hyperboloid or the Poincaré disk is proposed for both one-mode and two-mode realizations of the SU(1,1) group. The walker is described by the SU(1,1) coherent states. The probability distribution and the standard deviation are calculated, from which we confirm the quadratically growing variance. The influence of nonorthogonality is also discussed. In Sec.
IV
, we introduce the von Neumann entropy to describe the quantum entanglement between the coin and the walker. Finally, a brief summary is given in Sec.
V
.

II
Quantum walk related to the Heisenberg-Weyl group

The quantum walk in phase spaces has been widely studied.
We begin by briefly reviewing the quantum walk over a circle on the phase plane

;
Ramasesh et al. 2017

The phase plane consists of all possible values of position and momentum variables, and the walker’s location on the phase plane can be described by the coherent state. The coin-flip and the conditional-shift operators for the Heisenberg-Weyl group are introduced, which will be generalized to those in the SU(1,1) group in Sec.
III
.

II.1
Heisenberg-Weyl group

One usually introduce the bosonic annihilation operator
\hat{a}
and creation operator
\hat{a}^{\dagger}
to describe the harmonic oscillator, which satisfy

\displaystyle\left[\hat{a},\hat{a}^{\dagger}\right]=\hat{I}_{\text{a}},\qquad\left[\hat{a},\hat{I}_{\text{a}}\right]=\left[\hat{a}^{\dagger},\hat{I}_{\text{a}}\right]=0,

(1)

with
\hat{I}_{\text{a}}
the identity operator. The above commutation relations are described by the Heisenberg-Weyl group, and the corresponding operators can be regarded as the generators of the Heisenberg-Weyl algebra
Weyl 1928
.

The coherent state related to the Heisenberg-Weyl group is defined as

\displaystyle\left|{}\alpha\right>

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left(\alpha\hat{a}^{\dagger}-\alpha^{*}\hat{a}\right)\left|{}0\right>,

(2)

with
\alpha=|\alpha|\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}
. For clarity, we just call
\left|{}\alpha\right>
the HW coherent state. The position and momentum operators can be defined as

\displaystyle\hat{x}=\frac{\hat{a}^{\dagger}+\hat{a}}{\sqrt{2}},\qquad\hat{p}=\mathrm{i}\frac{\hat{a}^{\dagger}-\hat{a}}{\sqrt{2}}.

(3)

The harmonic oscillator described by
\left|{}\alpha\right>
is centered at

\displaystyle\left(x,p\right)=\sqrt{2}|\alpha|\left(\cos\theta,\sin\theta\right),

(4)

with
A=\left<{}\alpha\right|\hat{A}\left|{}\alpha\right>
(
A=x,p
). As long as
|\alpha|
is fixed,
(x,y)
is located on a circle with radius
\sqrt{2}|\alpha|
in the phase plane, as shown in Fig.
1
.

II.2
Quantum walk for Heisenberg-Weyl group

The quantum walk over a circle on the phase plane arises naturally for a harmonic oscillator.
The harmonic oscillator serves as the walker whose location is determined by the HW coherent state.
As shown in Fig.
1
, a set of equally displacing points on the circle can be expressed as
\left\{\left|{}\alpha_{n}\right>\right\}
, with
\alpha_{n}=|\alpha|\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta_{n}}
,
\theta_{n}=n\delta\theta
,
n\in\left[-\frac{L}{2},\frac{L}{2}\right]
and
\delta\theta=2\pi/L
.
L
is the total number of sites on the phase plane.

In addition to the walker, a typical discrete quantum walk also consists of a coin which can be an arbitrary two-level system. For example, a spin can be regarded as the coin, with spin-up (
\left|{}\uparrow\right>
) and spin-down (
\left|{}\downarrow\right>
) states corresponding to the head and tail of the coin respectively. In each step of the quantum walk, one needs to flip the coin at first. This process can be described by the coin-flip operator
\hat{C}
. In this paper, we employ the widely-used Hadamard gate
\hat{H}
to perform the coin-flip operation.
\hat{H}
can be written as a
2\times 2
matrix in the bases of spin-up and spin-down states as follows,

\displaystyle\hat{H}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&1\\
1&-1\end{array}\right).

with which the coin-flip operator can be expressed as

\displaystyle\hat{C}=\hat{I}_{\text{a}}\otimes\hat{H}.

(8)

Subsequently, the walker shifts its location according to the state of the flipped coin. This process is described by the conditional-shift operator
\hat{S}_{\text{a}}(\delta\theta)
, which can be written as

\displaystyle\hat{S}_{\text{a}}(\delta\theta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left(\mathrm{i}\delta\theta\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}\otimes\hat{\sigma}_{z}\right)

(9)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{+\mathrm{i}\delta\theta\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}}\otimes\left|{}\uparrow\right>\left<{}\uparrow\right|+\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\delta\theta\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}}\otimes\left|{}\downarrow\right>\left<{}\downarrow\right|,

with
\hat{\sigma}_{z}
the Pauli matrix. The conditional-shift operator
\hat{S}_{\text{a}}(\delta\theta)
leads to a counter-clockwise rotating by angle
\delta\theta
when the coin is on the spin-up state, and vice versa, as follows,

\displaystyle\hat{S}_{\text{a}}(\delta\theta)\left|{}\alpha_{n}\right>\otimes\left|{}\uparrow\right>

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|{}\alpha_{n+1}\right>\otimes\left|{}\uparrow\right>,

(10a)

\displaystyle\hat{S}_{\text{a}}(\delta\theta)\left|{}\alpha_{n}\right>\otimes\left|{}\downarrow\right>

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|{}\alpha_{n-1}\right>\otimes\left|{}\downarrow\right>.

(10b)

Given that the walker and the coin are initially at
\left|{}\alpha_{0}\right>
and
\left|{}\uparrow\right>
respectively, after
l
steps the state of the whole system will become

\displaystyle\left|{}\psi(l)\right>=\left(\hat{S}_{\text{a}}(\delta\theta)\cdot\hat{C}\right)^{l}\left|{}\psi(0)\right>,

(11)

with
\left|{}\psi(0)\right>=\left|{}\alpha_{0}\right>\otimes\left|{}\uparrow\right>
. For an idealized quantum walk, the walker’s states corresponding to different sites are orthogonal. In other words, there is no overlap between different states. However, the coherent states are not orthogonal due to

\displaystyle\langle{}\alpha_{m}|{}\alpha_{n}\rangle=\exp\left[-|\alpha|^{2}\left(1-e^{i(n-m)\delta\theta}\right)\right],

(12)

which leads to a limitation on the number of steps for the quantum walk
Sanders et al. 2003
;
Schmitz et al. 2009
;
Matjeschk et al. 2012
.

III.1
SU(1,1) group

The SU(1,1) group is non-compact. The generators associated with SU(1,1) group satisfy the following commutation relations,

\displaystyle\left[\hat{K}_{0},\hat{K}_{\pm}\right]=\pm\hat{K}_{\pm},\qquad\left[\hat{K}_{+},\hat{K}_{-}\right]=-2\hat{K}_{0}.

(13)

The Casimir
\hat{C}
operator commutes with all the generators of the su(1,1) Lie algebra, which can be written as

\displaystyle\hat{C}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{K}_{0}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\left(\hat{K}_{+}\hat{K}_{-}+\hat{K}_{-}\hat{K}_{+}\right).

(14)

One can choose the basis
\left|{}k,m\right>
, which satisfies

\displaystyle\hat{K}_{0}\left|{}k,m\right>

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(k+m)\left|{}k,m\right>,

(15a)

\displaystyle\hat{K}_{+}\left|{}k,m\right>

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{(m+1)(m+2k)}\left|{}k,m+1\right>,

(15b)

\displaystyle\hat{K}_{-}\left|{}k,m\right>

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{m(m+2k-1)}\left|{}k,m-1\right>,

(15c)

\displaystyle\hat{C}\left|{}k,m\right>

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle k(k-1)\left|{}k,m\right>,

(15d)

with
m=0,1,2,\dots
The number
k
is known as the Bargmann index which separates different irreducible representations.

The HW coherent state defined in the preceding section can be generalized to any Lie group. The SU(1,1) coherent state is defined as
Perelomov 1986

\displaystyle\left|{}k,\zeta\right>

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left(\zeta^{*}\hat{K}_{+}-\zeta\hat{K}_{-}\right)\left|{}k,0\right>,

(16)

with
\zeta=r\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}
. It’s easy to prove that

\left(K_{0},K_{1},K_{2}\right)=k\left(\cosh 2r,\sinh 2r\cos\theta,\sinh 2r\sin\theta\right),

(17)

with
K_{i}=\left<{}k,\zeta\right|\hat{K}_{i}\left|{}k,\zeta\right>
(
i=0,1,2
) and

\displaystyle\hat{K}_{1}=\frac{\hat{K}_{+}+\hat{K}_{-}}{2},\quad\hat{K}_{2}=\frac{\hat{K}_{+}-\hat{K}_{-}}{2\mathrm{i}}.

(18)

Therefore,
K_{0}^{2}-K_{1}^{2}-K_{2}^{2}=k^{2}
indicates that
\left|{}k,\zeta\right>
is centered at
\left(K_{1},K_{2},K_{0}\right)
on the hyperboloid, as shown in Fig.
2
. Alternately, one can choose the Poincaré disk to geometrize each SU(1,1) coherent state. As long as
r
doesn’t change,
K_{0}
is fixed whereas
K_{1}^{2}+K_{2}^{2}=k^{2}\sinh^{2}2r
forms a circle on the hyperboloid or the Poincaré disk. A set of equally displacing sites on the circle can be expressed as
\left\{\left|{}k,\zeta_{n}\right>\right\}
, with
\zeta_{n}=re^{i\theta_{n}}
.

III.2
Quantum walk for SU(1,1)

To perform the quantum walk over a circle on the hyperboloid, one can choose the same coin-flip operator as Eq. (
8
). With regard to the conditional-shift operator
\hat{S}(\delta\theta)
, it can be expressed as

\displaystyle\hat{S}(\delta\theta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\mathrm{i}\delta\theta\hat{K}_{0}\otimes\sigma_{z}\right)

(19)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-\mathrm{i}\delta\theta\hat{K}_{0}}\otimes\left|{}\uparrow\right>\left<{}\uparrow\right|+e^{+\mathrm{i}\delta\theta\hat{K}_{0}}\otimes\left|{}\downarrow\right>\left<{}\downarrow\right|,

which leads to

\displaystyle\hat{S}(\delta\theta)\left|{}k,\zeta_{n}\right>\otimes\left|{}\uparrow\right>

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|{}k,\zeta_{n+1}\right>\otimes\left|{}\uparrow\right>,

(20a)

\displaystyle\hat{S}(\delta\theta)\left|{}k,\zeta_{n}\right>\otimes\left|{}\downarrow\right>

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|{}k,\zeta_{n-1}\right>\otimes\left|{}\downarrow\right>.

(20b)

It should be noted that the SU(1,1) coherent states are also not orthogonal due to

\displaystyle\langle{}k,\zeta_{m}|{}k,\zeta_{n}\rangle=\left[\cosh^{2}r-e^{i\left(\theta_{m}-\theta_{n}\right)}\sinh^{2}r\right]^{-2k}.

(21)

However, we can decrease the overlap
\left|\langle{}k,\zeta_{m}|{}k,\zeta_{n}\rangle\right|
by increasing
k
and
r
.

III.2.1
One-mode realization

Depending on the underlying physical system, we can choose different realizations of the SU(1,1) group. The generators of su(1,1) Lie algebra with one-mode realization
Perelomov 1986
can be written as

\displaystyle\hat{K}_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}+\frac{1}{2}\right),

(22a)

\displaystyle\hat{K}_{+}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\hat{a}^{\dagger}\right)^{2},

(22b)

\displaystyle\hat{K}_{-}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\hat{a}^{2}.

(22c)

The corresponding Bargmann index is
k=\frac{1}{4}
or
\frac{3}{4}
. Given the Fock states
\left|{}n\right>_{\text{a}}
(
\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}\left|{}n\right>_{\text{a}}=n\left|{}n\right>_{\text{a}}
with
n=0,1,2\dots
),
\left|{}k,m\right>
can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\left|{}k,m\right>=\left|{}2\left(m+k-\frac{1}{4}\right)\right>_{\text{a}}.

(23)

Accordingly, the SU(1,1) coherent state is nothing but the squeezed vacuum state for
k=\frac{1}{4}
and squeezed one-photon state for
k=\frac{3}{4}
, due to

\displaystyle\left|{}k,\zeta\right>

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left[\frac{\zeta^{*}}{2}\left(\hat{a}^{\dagger}\right)^{2}-\frac{\zeta}{2}\hat{a}^{2}\right]\left|{}2\left(k-\frac{1}{4}\right)\right>_{\text{a}}.

(24)

Both of them can be produced in the experiments by engineering the interactions between a quantum system (such as a trapped ion) and the environment
Kienzler et al. 2015
;
Kienzler et al. 2017
.
It should be noted that the SU(1,1) coherent state [Eq.
24
] is always centered at the origin of the phase plane, namely,
\left(x,p\right)=(0,0)
. Therefore, the circular movement of walker cannot be visualized on the phase plane but rather on the hyperboloid.

In the one-mode realization, the conditional-shift operator
\hat{S}_{1}(\delta\theta)
can be written as

\displaystyle\hat{S}_{1}(\delta\theta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\mathrm{i}\delta\theta\hat{K}_{0}\otimes\sigma_{z}\right)

(25)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{S}_{\text{a}}\left(-\frac{\delta\theta}{2}\right)\cdot\left[\hat{I}_{\text{a}}\otimes\exp\left(-\mathrm{i}\frac{\delta\theta}{4}\hat{\sigma}_{z}\right)\right].

Therefore, the conditional-shift operator
\hat{S}_{1}(\delta\theta)
in the one-mode realization is equivalent to the coin operation
\hat{I}_{\text{a}}\otimes\exp\left(-\mathrm{i}\delta\theta\hat{\sigma}_{z}/4\right)
followed by the conditional-shift operator
\hat{S}_{\text{a}}\left(-\frac{\delta\theta}{2}\right)
for the HW coherent state.

In a word, the quantum walk for Heisenberg-Weyl group can be easily extended to that for SU(1,1) in the one-mode realization. The latter only introduces an extra coin operation before performing the conditional shift for the walker. Besides, the squeezed vacuum or one-photon state should be introduced as the initial state.

III.2.2
Two-mode realization

The generators of su(1,1) Lie algebra with two-mode realization
Perelomov 1986
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\hat{K}_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}+\hat{b}^{\dagger}\hat{b}+1\right),

(26a)

\displaystyle\hat{K}_{+}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{b}^{\dagger},

(26b)

\displaystyle\hat{K}_{-}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{a}\hat{b}.

(26c)

Given the Fock states
\left|{}n\right>_{s}
(
s=a,b
),
\left|{}k,m\right>
can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\left|{}k,m\right>=\left|{}m+2k-1\right>_{\text{a}}\otimes\left|{}m\right>_{\text{b}},

(27)

with the Bargmann index
k=\frac{1}{2},~1,~\frac{3}{2},~\dots

From Eqs. (
16
) and (
26
), the corresponding SU(1,1) coherent state is

\displaystyle\left|{}k,\zeta\right>=\exp\left(\zeta^{*}\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{b}^{\dagger}-\zeta\hat{a}\hat{b}\right)\left|{}2k-1\right>_{\text{a}}\otimes\left|{}0\right>_{\text{b}},

(28)

which represents the two-mode squeezed operator acting on the Fock states.

In the two-mode realization, the conditional-shift operator
\hat{S}_{2}(\delta\theta)
can be written as

\displaystyle\hat{S}_{2}(\delta\theta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\mathrm{i}\delta\theta\hat{K}_{0}\otimes\sigma_{z}\right)

(29)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{S}_{\text{a}}\left(-\frac{\delta\theta}{2}\right)\cdot\hat{S}_{\text{b}}\left(-\frac{\delta\theta}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\cdot\left[\hat{I}_{\text{a}}\otimes\hat{I}_{\text{b}}\otimes\exp\left(-\mathrm{i}\frac{\delta\theta}{2}\hat{\sigma}_{z}\right)\right],

which corresponds to a coin operation followed by the conditional-shift operators for mode a and b respectively.

III.3
Probability distribution and standard deviation

The quantum walk is a quantum mechanical extension of the classical random walk. Due to the quantum interference effect, the quantum walk presents a ballistic spread quadratically faster than the classical one which yields a diffusive spread. The quadratic enhancement can be well captured by the probability distribution and the corresponding standard deviation. The probability to find the walker at site
n
after
l
steps
Schmitz et al. 2009
;
Matjeschk et al. 2012
can be approximately given by

\displaystyle P_{n}(l)=\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}}\left<{}k,\zeta_{n}\right|\hat{\rho}_{\text{w}}(l)\left|{}k,\zeta_{n}\right>,

(30)

where
\hat{\rho}_{\text{w}}(l)=\mathrm{tr}_{{}\text{c}}\left[{}\left|{}\psi(l)\right>\left<{}\psi(l)\right|\right]
is the reduced density matrix for the walker and
\mathcal{N}
is introduced to normalize the whole probabilities. The approximation is due to the nonorthogonality of the SU(1,1) coherent states, as given in Eq. (
21
).

IV
Quantum Entanglement

The quantum entanglement is a key ingredient in quantum information and quantum computation. Entanglement between the coin and the walker is one of the unique characters owned by the discrete quantum walk, which cannot be exactly reproduced by its classical counterpart
Venegas-Andraca 2012
;
Carneiro et al. 2005
;
Abal et al. 2006
.

The von Neumann entropy, also known as the entropy of entanglement, can be employed to quantify the entanglement between the coin and the walker
Carneiro et al. 2005
;
Abal et al. 2006
. Generally, one needs to calculate the reduced density matrix of the coin or the walker to obtain the von Neumann entropy. Since the coin is a two-level system, we can employ an alternate approach
Costi and McKenzie 2003
, defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{E}=-p_{+}\log_{2}p_{+}-p_{-}\log_{2}p_{-},

(32)

with

\displaystyle p_{\pm}=\frac{1}{2}\left(1\pm\sqrt{M_{x}^{2}+M_{y}^{2}+M_{z}^{2}}\right),

(33)

and
M_{i}=\left<{}\psi(l)\right|\hat{\sigma}_{i}\left|{}\psi(l)\right>
(
i=x,y,z
). For the Hadamard gate we considered, Carneiro et al. numerically studied the long-time asymptotic behavior of entanglement, and they conjectured that the entanglement is 0.872
for arbitrary coin initial states
Carneiro et al. 2005
. Soon after that, it was proved by Abal et al. through an analytically approach in the Fourier representation
Abal et al. 2006
.

V
Conclusions

The quantum walk can be visualized by choosing proper phase spaces. Persistent attention has been drawn to the quantum walk over a circle on the phase plane, which associates with the Heisenberg-Weyl group. In this paper, we propose a new possibility to perform the quantum walk in the experiments, which generalize it to the SU(1,1) group. The walker is described by the SU(1,1) coherent states, whose movement during the walking process can be visualized on the hyperboloid or the Poincaré disk.

We consider two different realizations of the SU(1,1) group. In the one-mode realization, the conditional-shift operators for the Heisenberg-Weyl and the SU(1,1) groups share similar structures. One only needs to be careful about the initial state of the walker, as the SU(1,1) coherent states correspond to the squeezed vacuum or the one-photon states. In the two-mode realization, the conditional-shift operations for both modes should be performed separately.

The nonorthogonality of the SU(1,1) coherent states precludes the quantum walk for SU(1,1) from idealized quantum walk. Increasing the Bargmann index
k
can decrease the overlap between different SU(1,1) coherent states. From this perspective, the two-mode realization is able to provide a larger Bargmann index
k
required to simulate the idealized quantum walk.

The quantum walk is known for its ballistic spread, which is confirmed by the quadratically growing variance with the number of steps, namely,
\sigma^{2}\propto l^{2}
. The entanglement between the coin and the walker is also a characteristic feature of the discrete quantum walk, whose long-time asymptotic behavior depends on the coin’s initial state if the nonorthogonality of the SU(1,1) coherent states is not negligible. The decoherence can destroy the quantum interference and entanglement, which leads to the classical random walk with
\sigma^{2}\propto l

Brun et al. 2003a
;
Brun et al. 2003b
. The dependence of the quantum walk for SU(1,1) on the decoherence deserves further studies, which is left to future research.
