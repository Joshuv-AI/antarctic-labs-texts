Efficient Calculation the Number of Partitions of the Set { 1 , 2 , … , 3 ​ n } \{1,2,\ldots,3n\} into Subsets { x , y , z } \{x,y,z\} Satisfying x + y = z x+y=z


Abstract
Consider the set { 1 , 2 , … , 3 ​ n } \{1,2,\ldots,3n\} . We are interested in the number of partitions of this set into subsets of three elements each, where the sum of two of them equals the third. We give some criteria such a partition has to fulfill, which can be used for efficient pruning in the search for these partitions. In particular, we enumerate all such partitions for n = 16 n=16 and n = 17 n=17 adding new terms to the series A108235 in the Online Encyclopedia of Integer Sequences.

Introduction

The enumeration of set partitions with different constraints as pattern avoiding is a long-studied field of problems; see Mansour
[
2
]
for an overview.

We are interested in partitions of the set
\{1,\ldots,3n\}
for a positive integer
n
into subsets of size 3 each with the additional property that in every such subset the sum of the two smaller elements gives the third one.

This problem has wide connections to cyclic Steiner triple systems, modified Nim games or arranging colored blocks; see Nowakowski
[
4
]
for an overview.

E.g., Nickerson asks in
[
3
]
for an arrangement of two copies of all integers
i\in\{1,\ldots,2n\}
such that the two copies of
i
are separated by exactly
i-1
other terms. Thus,
11423243
is a Nickerson sequence for
n=4
. If
a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{2n}
is such a Nickerson sequence, then let
p_{1}(i)
be the index of the first occurrence of the number
i
and
p_{2}(i)
the index of the second one. Then the sets
\{p_{1}(i),p_{2}(i)\}
are partitioning the set
\{1,\ldots,2n\}
, and each difference
p_{2}(i)-p_{1}(i)=i
occurs exactly once. Thus, the sets
\{i,p_{1}(i)+n,p_{2}(i)+n\}
are a partition of
\{1,\ldots,3n\}
. Hence, every solution to Nickerson’s problem leads to such a partition. (Note that the revers is not true.)

But the task of finding the number of such partitions is also a good riddle; see e.g.
[
6
]
. That was the context in which the authors of this paper had first contact with this problem.

It is well-known that such a partition can only exist if
n\equiv 0\ \mbox{\rm(mod}\ 4\mbox{\rm)}
or
n\equiv 1\ \mbox{\rm(mod}\ 4\mbox{\rm)}
. The proof of this statement is straightforward: Suppose we have a partition of
\{1,\ldots,3n\}
into such subsets
\{x_{i},y_{i},z_{i}\}
with
x_{i}+y_{i}=z_{i}
. Then, clearly, we have

\sum_{i}(x_{i}+y_{i})=\sum_{i}z_{i}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{3n}k=\frac{3n(3n+1)}{4}.

So
4\mid 3n(3n+1)
. Since
3n
and
3n-1
are relatively prime it follows
4\mid 3n
or
4\mid 3n+1
, and hence, we get
n\equiv 0\ \mbox{\rm(mod}\ 4\mbox{\rm)}
or
n\equiv 1\ \mbox{\rm(mod}\ 4\mbox{\rm)}
,
\Box
.

If a positive integer
n
is of the necessary form, then such partitions of
\{1,\ldots,3n\}
always exist. Examples of general constructions are given by Nowakowski
[
4
]
.

The question of how many such partitions for a given
n
exist is not so easy to answer. In the next sections, we give tools for an efficient computation of these numbers.

Algorithm 1

Naive Recursive Algorithm.

For a much faster calculation, we want to know as early as possible if a complete such partition is not possible anymore. The first and most used constraint is given in the next theorem.

Theorem 1
.

Let
m
be a positive integer, and
S:=\{b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{3m}\}
a set of positive integers with
b_{1}<b_{2}<\cdots<b_{3m}
which can be partitioned into subsets
\{x_{i},y_{i},z_{i}\}
,
i=1,\ldots,m
with
x_{i}+y_{i}=z_{i}
. Furthermore, let
S_{1}:=\sum_{k=1}^{2m}b_{k}
and
S_{2}:=\sum_{k=2m+1}^{3m}b_{k}
. Then

S_{1}\leq S_{2}.

Proof.

For a given set
S=\{b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{3m}\}
with
b_{1}<b_{2}<\cdots<b_{3m}
let
\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}\{x_{i},y_{i},z_{i}\}=S
and
x_{i}+y_{i}=z_{i}
for all
i=1,\ldots,m
. Then we have

S_{1}=\sum_{k=1}^{2m}b_{k}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}(x_{i}+y_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{m}z_{i}\leq\sum_{k=2m+1}^{3m}b_{k}=S_{2}.

∎

Algorithm 2

Basic Algorithm.

Furthermore, we have some extra conditions such a partition must have:

Theorem 2
.

In the same setting as in Theorem
1
the following conditions hold:

1.

If and only if
S_{1}=S_{2}
, then
\{x_{i}\mid 1\leq i\leq n\}\cup\{y_{i}\mid 1\leq i\leq n\}=\{b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{2n}\}
and
\{z_{i}\mid 1\leq i\leq n\}=\{b_{2n+1},b_{2n+2},\ldots,b_{3n}\}
.

2.

It is
b_{1}+b_{2n}\leq b_{3n}
.

3.

If equality holds in
2
, then
\{x_{i},y_{i},z_{i}\}=\{b_{1},b_{2n},b_{3n}\}
for some
i
and
S_{1}=S_{2}
.

4.

If
b_{1}+b_{2n+1}>b_{3n}
, then
S_{1}=S_{2}
.

Proof.

We use the same notation as in the proof of Theorem
1
.

1.

If equality holds in Theorem
1
, then it must hold on all places in the chain of inequalities given in the last item. If
S_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_{i}+y_{i})
, then
\{x_{i},y_{i}\mid 1\leq i\leq n\}=\{b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{2n}\}
. And if
\sum_{i=1}^{n}z_{i}=S_{2}
, we have
\{z_{i}\mid 1\leq i\leq n\}=\{b_{2n+1},b_{2n+2},\ldots,b_{3n}\}
. And if the sets are as described, equality holds in Theorem
1
.

2.

Clearly, we have
x_{i}\geq b_{2n}
or
y_{i}\geq b_{2n}
for some
i
. W.l.o.g. let the second case be true. Then we have
b_{1}+b_{2n}\leq x_{i}+y_{i}=z_{i}\leq b_{3n}
.

3.

If equality holds in Theorem
1
, then from the chain of inequalities in the previous item we get
x_{i}=b_{1}
,
y_{i}=b_{2n}
, and
z_{i}=b_{3n}
, which proves the first part of this claim. Furthermore, if there were an index
j
with
x_{j}<y_{j}
and
y_{j}>b_{2n}
, we would have
b_{1}+b_{2n}<x_{j}+y_{j}=z_{j}\leq b_{3n}
, contradicting the assumed equality in Theorem
1
. Therefore, all
x_{i}
and
y_{i}
are smaller than or equal to
b_{2n}
, which proves the second part of this claim.

4.

Let
j\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
, and w.l.o.g. let
x_{j}<y_{j}
. If
b_{1}+b_{2n+1}>b_{3n}
, we have
b_{3n}\geq z_{j}=x_{j}+y_{j}\geq b_{1}+y_{j}>b_{3n}-b_{2n+1}+y_{j}
. Thus, it is
b_{2n+1}>y_{j}>x_{j}
, and we get equality in Theorem
1
with the second direction of the equivalence given in Condition
1
.

∎

This leads to some significant improvements of Algorithm
2
:

•

If at some point in the recursive search the equality
S_{1}=S_{2}
holds, then any triple has to consist of two elements of
\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{2m}\}
and one of
\{b_{2m+1},\ldots,b_{3m}\}
. Thus, we have
2\leq i\leq 2m
and
2m+1\leq k\leq 3m
, which narrows the search space for triples. Also, if this equality is reached at one point, it has to hold in all subsequent recursion steps. Therefore, in this case, the sums
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
need not be computed anymore. This will be done in a reduced version of the given function.

•

If
b_{1}+b_{i}\geq b_{k}
, then
b_{1}+b_{i+1}>b_{k}
. Therefore, after the search for triples with
b_{1}
and
b_{i}
up to
b_{k}
, the search for triples with
b_{1}
and
b_{i+1}
can start with the next value after
b_{k}
.

•

If for some
i
one has
b_{1}+b_{i}>b_{3m}
, then one can end the search not only for this
i
but also for all higher values of
i
in the
for
loop.

•

If
b_{1}+b_{i}>b_{3m}
for some
i\geq 2m+1
, then by Condition
4
of Theorem
2
, we can use the reduced function for further calculating, since we have
S_{1}=S_{2}
.

•

If
b_{1}+b_{2m}=b_{3m}
, we have found one triple
\{b_{1},b_{2m},b_{3m}\}
, and the next step of the recursion can be done with the reduced function, since we have
S_{1}=S_{2}
in this case as well.

With this we improved the program by a fair bit; see Tables

Further Ideas

Theorems
1
and
2
as well as the algorithms we provided do not make use of the fact that the set
S
to be partitioned is a subset of
\{1,\ldots,3n\}
. It could be any set of positive reals. Thus, we can use them to answer some slight variation of the given problem, too:

Sedláček
[
5
]
asks if, for every
n\not\equiv 6\ \mbox{\rm(mod}\ 12\mbox{\rm)}
and
n\not\equiv 9\ \mbox{\rm(mod}\ 12\mbox{\rm)}
in the set
\{1,\ldots,n\}
, there are
\lfloor\tfrac{n}{3}\rfloor
pairwise disjoint subset
\{x,y,z\}
with
x+y=z
. (For
n\equiv 6\ \mbox{\rm(mod}\ 12\mbox{\rm)}
or
n\equiv 9\ \mbox{\rm(mod}\ 12\mbox{\rm)}
the parity argument made in the introduction proves that there cannot be
\tfrac{n}{3}
such triples.) Guy
[
1
]
gives a general proof of existence for all remaining
n
and asks how many different such maximal collections
of disjoint subsets with the desired property exist. The sequence
A002849
in OEIS gives these numbers. (For
n\equiv 6\ \mbox{\rm(mod}\ 12\mbox{\rm)}
or
n\equiv 9\ \mbox{\rm(mod}\ 12\mbox{\rm)}
the maximal number of disjoint subsets only is
\tfrac{n}{3}-1
.)

By excluding the right quantity of numbers from
\{1,\ldots,n\}
such that the remaining set has a multiple of 3 elements and their sum is divisible by 4, we construct subproblems which can be solved with our methods. In this way, we calculated the next hitherto unknown elements in this series:

\displaystyle a_{43}

\displaystyle=16852166906\text{ and}

\displaystyle a_{44}

\displaystyle=270947059160.

But, if we constrain our search to the task of partitioning the set
\{1,\ldots,3n\}
(with
n\equiv 0\ \mbox{\rm(mod}\ 4\mbox{\rm)}
or
n\equiv 1\ \mbox{\rm(mod}\ 4\mbox{\rm)}
as given in the introduction), then we can use this as a meta information. Thus, we know that all elements
b_{1},\ldots,b_{3m}
of the considered set are positive integers and at most
3n
. Additionally, by our strategy of always putting the smallest available element in the next triple, we know
b_{1}\geq n-m+1
, since, previously, at least the smallest
n-m
elements have already been chosen for the first
n-m
triples.

Theorem 3
.

In the recursive search for partitions of
\{1,\ldots,3n\}
into subsets
\{x,y,z\}
satisfying
x+y=z
, where in every step the smallest available number is chosen, suppose that only be six elements remain. Thus, we have
m=2
. Let
S=\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{6}\}
with
b_{1}<\cdots<b_{6}
,
S_{1}=b_{1}+\cdots+b_{4}
, and
S_{2}=b_{5}+b_{6}
. If there is such a partition, then
S_{1}=S_{2}
.

Proof.

Since
m=2
, we look at the second to last step in the recursion: Only six elements
b_{1}<\cdots<b_{6}
are remaining; and we know
b_{1}+b_{2}+b_{3}+b_{4}\leq b_{5}+b_{6}
. From the previous paragraph, we additionally have
n-1\leq b_{1}
,
n\leq b_{2}
,
\ldots
and
b_{6}\leq 3n
.

Now we can make an analysis by case which partitions are still possible. In the following, we distinguish the cases by naming the “partners” of
b_{1}
in its triple. The other triple is then well-defined:

•

b_{1}+b_{2}=b_{3}
: Then
b_{3}\geq(n-1)+n=2n-1
and
b_{4}\geq 2n
. But this leads to the contradiction
3n\geq b_{6}=b_{5}+b_{4}>2n+2n=4n
.

•

b_{1}+b_{2}=b_{4}
: Then
b_{4}\geq 2n-1
, and
b_{5}\geq 2n
. This yields the contradiction
n+1\leq b_{3}\leq b_{6}-b_{5}\leq 3n-2n=n
.

•

b_{1}+b_{2}=b_{5}
: A possible solution.

•

b_{1}+b_{2}=b_{6}
: This cannot be the case, since
b_{1}+b_{2}<b_{3}+b_{4}=b_{5}<b_{6}
.

•

b_{1}+b_{3}=b_{4}
: Then
b_{4}\geq(n-1)+(n+1)=2n
and
b_{5}\geq 2n+1
. Because of
n+1\leq b_{2}=b_{6}-b_{5}\leq 3n-(2n+1)=n-1
, this leads to a contradiction, too.

•

b_{1}+b_{3}=b_{5}
: A possible solution.

•

b_{1}+b_{3}=b_{6}
: This cannot be the case, since
b_{1}+b_{3}<b_{2}+b_{4}=b_{5}<b_{6}
.

•

b_{1}+b_{4}=b_{5}
: A possible solution.

•

b_{1}+b_{4}=b_{6}
: A possible solution.

•

b_{1}+b_{5}=b_{6}
: Then we have
b_{4}=b_{2}+b_{3}\geq n+(n+1)=2n+1
and, therefore,
b_{5}\geq 2n+2
. This gives the contradiction
3n\geq b_{6}=b_{1}+b_{5}\geq(n-1)+(2n+2)=3n+1
.

In all four remaining cases, we have
b_{1}+b_{2}+b_{3}+b_{4}=b_{5}+b_{6}
, hence
S_{1}=S_{2}
.
∎

If this equality does not hold, we can discard the set directly. Otherwise, we only need to check the four possible solutions from above. This speeds up the process, a little bit further.
