𝒦 {\mathcal{K}} -Lorentzian Polynomials
Grigoriy Blekherman and Papri Dey

Abstract
. Lorentzian polynomials are a fascinating class of real polynomials with many applications. Their definition is specific to the nonnegative orthant. Following recent work, we examine Lorentzian polynomials on proper convex cones. For a self-dual cone 𝒦 {\mathcal{K}} we find a connection between 𝒦 {\mathcal{K}} -Lorentzian polynomials and 𝒦 {\mathcal{K}} -positive linear maps, which were studied in the context of the generalized Perron-Frobenius theorem. We find that as the cone 𝒦 {\mathcal{K}} varies, even the set of quadratic 𝒦 {\mathcal{K}} -Lorentzian polynomials can be difficult to understand algorithmically. We also show that, just as in the case of the nonnegative orthant, 𝒦 {\mathcal{K}} -Lorentzian and 𝒦 {\mathcal{K}} -completely log-concave polynomials coincide.

14P99, 52A20

1.
Introduction

Lorentzian polynomials were introduced by Brändén and Huh in
[
BH20
]
and simultaneously by Anari, Oveis Gharan and Vinzant
[
AGV21
]
(under the name of completely log-concave polynomials) as a generalization of hyperbolic polynomials that retain their log-concavity properties. They have already seen a multitude of applications
[
ALOGV24a
,
ALOGV24b
,
AD20
]
. Lorentzian polynomials are specific to the nonnegative orthant
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq 0}
. Lorentzian polynomials with respect to a full-dimensional convex cone
{\mathcal{K}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
were introduced by Brändén and Leake in
[
BL23
]
and by the second author.

We consider what interesting properties of Lorentzian polynomials are maintained when we switch from the nonnegtave orthant to more general cones. The cone
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{\geq 0}
is self-dual, and for any self-dual cone
{\mathcal{K}}
there is an immediate connection between
{\mathcal{K}}
-Lorentzian polynomials and well-studied concept of
{\mathcal{K}}
-nonnegative linear maps
[
Bv20
]
. This allows us to also consider properties of
{\mathcal{K}}
-irreducibility and
{\mathcal{K}}
-positivity, which have been studied in the context of generalizing the Perron-Frobenius theorem
[
Gan98
,
Van68
]
.

We take a detailed look at
{\mathcal{K}}
-Lorentzian quadratic forms for various self-dual cones
{\mathcal{K}}
. Quadratic Lorentzian polynomials are simple to understand, and so are quadratic forms Lorentzian on the second order cone
\mathcal{L}_{n}
which can be characterized via a simple semidefinite program. However, quadratic forms Lorenztian on the cone of positive semidefinite matrices
\mathcal{S}^{n}_{+}
are more difficult. We show that a natural linear projection of
\mathcal{S}^{n}_{+}
-Lorentzian quadratic forms is the convex cone of degree
4
globally nonnegative polynomials. From this we see that testing whether a quadratic form is
\mathcal{S}^{n}_{+}
-Lorentzian is NP-hard.

In analogy with results of
[
BH20
]
and
[
ALOGV24b
]
we show that
{\mathcal{K}}
-Lorentzian polynomials are equivalently completely log-concave polynomials (CLC) on
{\mathcal{K}}
. We also find some interesting properties of Hessians of
{\mathcal{K}}
-Lorentzian polynomials related to
{\mathcal{K}}
-nonnegativity and
{\mathcal{K}}
-irreducibility. We provide a different proof of a result of
[
BL23
]
that testing whether a polynomial is
{\mathcal{K}}
-Lorentzian can be reduced to taking directional derivatives with respect to extreme rays of
{\mathcal{K}}
and one interior point of
{\mathcal{K}}
, and examine the interior of the set of
{\mathcal{K}}
-Lorentzian polynomials.
The rest of the paper is structured as follows: in
Section

2
we set notation and main definitions.
Section

3
is devoted to a detailed examination of quadratic
{\mathcal{K}}
-Lorentzian polynomials; we also make a first connection to
{\mathcal{K}}
-nonnegative and
{\mathcal{K}}
-irreducible matrices. In
Section

4
we examine the interior of the set of
{\mathcal{K}}
-Lorentzian polynomials, show equivalence between
{\mathcal{K}}
-Lorentzian and
{\mathcal{K}}
-completely log-concave polynomials and provide a sufficient condition for a sum of two
{\mathcal{K}}
-Lorentzian polynomials to be
{\mathcal{K}}
-Lorentzian. In
Section

5
we expand on the connection to
{\mathcal{K}}
-irreducibility and
{\mathcal{K}}
-nonnegativity and show a simplified characterization of
{\mathcal{K}}
-Lorentzian polynomials.

2.
Definitions and Notation

A nonempty convex set
{\mathcal{K}}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is said to be a cone if
c{\mathcal{K}}\subseteq{\mathcal{K}}
for all
c\geq 0
. A cone
{\mathcal{K}}
is called proper if it is closed (in the Euclidean topology on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
), pointed (i.e.,

{\mathcal{K}}\cap(-{\mathcal{K}})=\{0\}
), and solid (i.e., the topological interior of
{\mathcal{K}}
, denoted as
\inter{\mathcal{K}}
, is nonempty).

The vector space of real homogeneous polynomials (forms) in
n
variables of degree
d
is denoted by
{\mathbb{R}}[x]^{d}_{n}
. A form
f\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]_{n}^{d}
is said to be
log-concave
at a point
a\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
if
f(a)>0
and
\log f
is a convex function at
a
, i.e. the Hessian of
\log f
is negative semidefinite at
a
.
A form
f
is
strictly log-concave
at
a
if the Hessian of
\log f
at
a
is negative definite. A form
f\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]_{n}^{d}
is log-concave on a proper cone
{\mathcal{K}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
if
f
is log-concave at every point of the interior of
{\mathcal{K}}
. By convention, the zero polynomial is log-concave (but not strictly log-concave) at all points of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

We will denote quadratic forms by a lower case letter, and the matrix of the quadratic form by the corresponding upper case letter, i.e. if
q
is a quadratic form, then its matrix is
Q
and
q(x)=x^{t}Qx
.

For a point
a\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and
f\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]_{n}^{d}
we use
D_{a}f
to denote the directional derivative of
f
in direction
a
:
D_{a}f=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}
. We now define
{\mathcal{K}}
-completely log-concave and
{\mathcal{K}}
-Lorentzian forms.

Definition 2.1
.

A form
f\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]_{n}^{d}
is called a
{\mathcal{K}}
-completely log-concave (
{\mathcal{K}}
-CLC) on a proper convex cone
{\mathcal{K}}
if for any choice of
a_{1},\dots,a_{m}\in{\mathcal{K}}
, with
m\leq d
, we have that
D_{a_{1}}\dots D_{a_{m}}f
is log-concave on
\inter{\mathcal{K}}
.
A form
f\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]_{n}^{d}
is strictly
{\mathcal{K}}
-CLC if for any choice of
a_{1},\dots,a_{m}\in{\mathcal{K}}
, with
m\leq d
,
D_{a_{1}}\dots D_{a_{m}}f
is strictly log-concave on all points of
{\mathcal{K}}
. We use
\CL^{d}({\mathcal{K}})
to denote the set of
{\mathcal{K}}
-CLC forms in
{\mathbb{R}}[x]_{n}^{d}
.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
{\mathcal{K}}
be a proper convex cone. A form
f\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]_{n}^{d}
of degree
d\geq 2
is said to be
{\mathcal{K}}
-Lorentzian if for any
a_{1},\dots,a_{d-2}\in\inter{\mathcal{K}}
the quadratic form
q=D_{a_{1}}\dots D_{a_{d-2}}f
satisfies the following conditions:

(1)

The matrix
Q
of
q
has exactly one positive eigenvalue.

(2)

For any
x,y\in\inter{\mathcal{K}}
we have
y^{t}Qx=\langle y,Qx\rangle>0
.

For degree
d\leq 1
a form is
{\mathcal{K}}
-Lorentzian if it is nonnegative on
{\mathcal{K}}
. We use
\SL^{d}({\mathcal{K}})
to denote the set of
{\mathcal{K}}
-Lorentzian forms in
{\mathbb{R}}[x]_{n}^{d}
.

Remark 2.3
.

Definition

2.2
is equivalent to the definition in
[
BL23
]
, although we state it in a slightly different way. For a quadratic form
q
with matrix
Q
and for all
a,b\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
we have
D_{b}D_{a}q=2b^{t}Qa
. Therefore, we can also express condition (2) as
D_{b}D_{a}q>0
for all
a,b\in\inter{\mathcal{K}}
. Combining with the fact that we have the freedom to choose directions of derivatives
a_{1},\dots,a_{d-2}\in\inter{\mathcal{K}}
we can express condition (2) as
D_{a_{1}}\dots D_{a_{d}}f>0
for all

a_{1},\dots,a_{d}\in\inter{\mathcal{K}}
, as was done in
[
BL23
]
.

We can also write conditions (1) and (2) above without relying on writing the matrix of
q
in coordinates. Condition (1) can be stated via the variational definition of eigenvalues: there exists a point
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
such that
q(x)>0
, and for any two dimensional subspace
V
of
R^{n}
, there exists
v\in V
,
v\neq 0
such that
q(v)\leq 0
. Condition (2) can also be stated in terms of the naturally associated bilinear form
B(x,y)
of
q(x)
: there exists a unique bilinear form
B(x,y)
such that
q(x)=B(x,x)
for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. We can equivalently say that
B(x,y)>0
for all
x,y\in\inter{\mathcal{K}}
.

3.
Quadratic
{\mathcal{K}}
-Lorentzian polynomials

In this section we analyze properties of quadratic
{\mathcal{K}}
-Lorentzian polynomials. Although these observations are mostly elementary, they point to interesting connections with other well-studied notions. We first describe several equivalent formulations for nonnegativity condition (2) in
Definition

2.2
, which may be easier to verify.

Lemma 3.1
.

A quadratic form
q(x)\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]_{n}^{2}
is
{\mathcal{K}}
-Lorentzian on a proper convex cone
{\mathcal{K}}
if and only if the matrix
Q
of
q
has exactly one positive eigenvalue and one of the following conditions holds:

(a)

for all
x\in{\mathcal{K}}
we have
Qx\in{\mathcal{K}}^{*}
, i.e.
Q({\mathcal{K}})\subseteq{{\mathcal{K}}^{*}}
.

(b)

y^{t}Qx\geq 0
for all
x,y
spanning extreme rays of
{\mathcal{K}}
.

(c)

x^{t}Qx>0
for all
x\in\inter{\mathcal{K}}
.

Remark 3.2
.

Note that conditions (b) and (c) of Lemma
3.1
are relaxations of Condition (2) from
Definition

2.2
.

Proof.

(a) By the Biduality Theorem
[
Bar02
, Chapter-IV]

\langle{y,Qx}\rangle\geq 0
for all
y\in{\mathcal{K}}
if and only if
Qx\in{\mathcal{K}}^{*}
. If
Q({\mathcal{K}})=\{0\}
, then
Q
is the zero matrix and
q
is
{\mathcal{K}}
-Lorentzian. If
Q
is not the zero matrix and
Qx=0
for
x\in\inter{\mathcal{K}}
, then
Q({\mathcal{K}})
contains a linear subspace. Since
Q({\mathcal{K}})\subseteq{\mathcal{K}}^{*}
we see that
{\mathcal{K}}
is not full-dimensional, which is a contradiction. Therefore
Qx\neq 0
for any
x\in\inter{\mathcal{K}}
. Then for any
y\in\inter K
we have
\langle y,Qx\rangle>0
.

(b) Since
{\mathcal{K}}
is a proper convex cone, by the conical Minkowksi Theorem
[
Bar02
, Chapter II]
, it is the conical hull of its extreme rays. Therefore
Q({\mathcal{K}})\subseteq{\mathcal{K}}^{*}
if and only if for all
x\in{\mathcal{K}}
spanning extreme rays of
{\mathcal{K}}
we have
Qx\subset{\mathcal{K}}^{*}
. Additionally, we have
Qx\in{\mathcal{K}}^{*}
if and only if for all
y
spanning extreme rays of
{\mathcal{K}}
we have
y^{t}Qx\geq 0
.

(c) Let
v=(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})\in\inter{\mathcal{K}}
. Let
C
be the connected component of the set
\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\,\,\mid\,\,q(x)>0\}
which contains
v
. Then
C
is an open convex cone
[
G5̇9
]
,
[
G9̈7
]
(which is the hyperbolicity cone of
q
), and its closure
\bar{C}
is a closed, convex cone. Note that we have
{\mathcal{K}}\subset\bar{C}
, and we need to show that
Q({\mathcal{K}})\subseteq{\mathcal{K}}^{*}
. Since duality reverses inclusion, we have
\bar{C}^{*}\subseteq{\mathcal{K}}^{*}
. Therefore it suffices to show that
Q(\bar{C})\subseteq\bar{C}^{*}
.

Since
Q
has exactly one positive eigenvalue, then up to an orthogonal change of variables, we can write
Q(x)=\lambda_{1}x_{1}^{2}-\lambda_{2}x_{2}^{2}-\dots-\lambda_{k}x_{k}^{2}
with
k\leq n
and
\lambda_{i}>0
. Furthermore we may assume
v_{1}>0
. Let
q^{\prime}
be the quadratic form
q^{\prime}(x)=\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}}x_{1}^{2}-\frac{1}{\lambda_{2}}x_{2}^{2}-\dots-\frac{1}{\lambda_{k}}x_{k}^{2}
. We have

\displaystyle\bar{C}=\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\,\,\mid\,\,x_{1}\geq 0,\,q(x)\geq 0\},

\displaystyle\bar{C}^{*}=\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\,\,\mid\,\,x_{1}\geq 0,\,q^{\prime}(x)\geq 0,\,x_{k+1}=\dots=x_{n}=0\}.

Take
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\bar{C}
. Let
y=Qx=(\lambda_{1}x_{1},-\lambda_{2}x_{2},\dots,-\lambda_{k}x_{k},0,\dots,0)
. Observe that
y_{1}\geq 0
,
y_{k+1}=\dots=y_{n}=0
and
q^{\prime}(y)=q(x)\geq 0
. Therefore
y\in\bar{C}^{*}
and the Lemma follows.

∎

Next we show that nonnegativity condition (2) from
Definition

2.2
can be even further relaxed to just testing nonnegativity of
q
on the extreme rays of
{\mathcal{K}}
in case the matrix
Q
is nonsingular, and the set of extreme rays of
{\mathcal{K}}
is path-connected.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
q
be a a quadratic form on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
such that its matrix
Q
is nonsingular and has exactly one positive eigenvalue. Let
{\mathcal{K}}
be a proper convex cone in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
such that the set of vectors spanning an extremal ray of
{\mathcal{K}}
is path-connected. Then
q\in\SL^{2}({\mathcal{K}})
if and only if
q(v)\geq 0
for all
v
spanning an extremal ray of
K
.

Remark 3.4
.

The reduction to extreme rays is not possible for the nonnegative orthant
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{\geq 0}
(the standard setting for Lorentzian polynomials), or for any other polyhedral cone
{\mathcal{K}}
. In these cases, the set of vectors spanning extreme rays of
{\mathcal{K}}
is not connected.

Proof.

Up to a change of coordinates we have
q(x)=x_{1}^{2}-x_{2}^{2}-\dots-x_{n}^{2}
. Let
v,w
be two points spanning distinct extreme rays of
{\mathcal{K}}
. We see that the first coordinates
v_{1}
,
w_{1}
must have the same sign, since there is a path connecting
v
and
w
which does not pass through the origin, such that for any point
t
on the path we have
q(t)\geq 0
. Without loss of generality we may assume that for any
v
spanning an extremal ray of
{\mathcal{K}}
we have
v_{1}>0
. It follows that all extreme rays of
{\mathcal{K}}
are contained in the proper convex cone
\bar{C}=\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\,\,\mid\,\,x_{1}\geq 0,\,q(x)\geq 0\}
. Therefore, the interior of
{\mathcal{K}}
is contained in the interior of
\bar{C}
and
q
is strictly positive on the interior of
\bar{C}
. The Lemma now follows by Lemma
3.1
.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

The hypothesis that
Q
is nonsingular cannot be eliminated. Let
\ell
be any linear form such that
\ell(v)=0
for some
v\in\inter{\mathcal{K}}
, and let
q=\ell(v)^{2}
. Then
Q
has exactly one positive eigenvalue, and
q
is nonnegative on all the extreme rays of
{\mathcal{K}}
, but
q
is not
{\mathcal{K}}
-Lorentzian.

Let
{\mathcal{K}}
be a self-dual cone, i.e.
{\mathcal{K}}={\mathcal{K}}^{*}
. We can use Condition (a) of
Lemma

3.1
to make a connection between
{\mathcal{K}}
-Lorentzian quadratic forms and
{\mathcal{K}}
-nonnegative matrices (or equivalently linear maps).

Definition 3.6
.

Let
{\mathcal{K}}
be a proper convex cone in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. A
n\times n
matrix
A
is
{\mathcal{K}}
-nonnegative if
Ax\in{\mathcal{K}}
for any
x\in{\mathcal{K}}
, i.e.
A({\mathcal{K}})\subseteq{\mathcal{K}}
. A matrix
A
is
{\mathcal{K}}
-positive if
Ax\in\inter{\mathcal{K}}
for any nonzero
x\in{\mathcal{K}}
. A
{\mathcal{K}}
-nonnegative matrix
A
is called
{\mathcal{K}}
-irreducible if
A
leaves no (proper) face
of
{\mathcal{K}}
invariant. A
{\mathcal{K}}
-nonnegative matrix which is not
{\mathcal{K}}
-irreducible is called
{\mathcal{K}}
-reducible, cf.
[
Gan98
]
,
[
Van68
]
.

Here we record some equivalent characterizations to
{\mathcal{K}}
-irreducibility which will be used in the sequel
[
Van68
]
.

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
A
be a
{\mathcal{K}}
-nonnegative matrix for a solid (with nonempty interior) cone
{\mathcal{K}}
. The following statements are equivalent:

(1)

A
is
{\mathcal{K}}
-irreducible

(2)

no eigenvector of
A
lies on the boundary
\partial{\mathcal{K}}
of
{\mathcal{K}}
.

(3)

A
has exactly one eigenvector in
{\mathcal{K}}
, and this eigenvector is in
\inter{\mathcal{K}}
.

From
Lemma

3.1
(a) we immediately see the following:

Corollary 3.8
.

Let
{\mathcal{K}}
be a self-dual cone. Then
q\in\SL^{2}({\mathcal{K}})
if and only if the matrix
Q
of
q
has exactly one positive eigenvalue and
Q
is
{\mathcal{K}}
-nonnegative.

We now show that if a matrix
Q
of
q\in\SL^{2}({\mathcal{K}})
is nonsingular and the cone
{\mathcal{K}}
is self-dual, then
Q
must be
{\mathcal{K}}
-irreducible.

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
{\mathcal{K}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be a self-dual cone and
q\in\SL^{2}({\mathcal{K}})
with matrix
Q
. If
Q
is nonsingular, then
Q
is
{\mathcal{K}}
-irreducible.

Proof.

It suffices to show that a symmetric, nonsingular
{\mathcal{K}}
-nonnegative matrix
Q
with exactly one positive eigenvalue must be
{\mathcal{K}}
-irreducible.
Let
v
be the eigenvector associated with the positive eigenvalue
\alpha
of
Q
. Suppose that neither
v
nor
-v
lie in the interior of
{\mathcal{K}}
. Since
{\mathcal{K}}
is self-dual, there exists a nonzero vector
w\in{\mathcal{K}}
such that
\langle v,w\rangle=0
. Since
Q
is nonsingular, we see that the quadratic form
q
is negative definite on the orthogonal complement of
v
, and in particular, we have
q(w)<0
. This is a contradiction, since
q
is nonnegative on
{\mathcal{K}}
. The Lemma now follows by
Theorem

3.7
. ∎

Combining
Corollary

3.8
and
Lemma

3.9
we immediately obtain the following:

Corollary 3.10
.

Let
{\mathcal{K}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be a self-dual cone. Then
q\in\SL^{2}({\mathcal{K}})
if and only if the matrix
Q
of
q
has exactly one positive eigenvalue and
Q
is either nonsingular and
{\mathcal{K}}
-irreducible, or singular and
{\mathcal{K}}
-nonnegative.

3.1.
Quadratic Lorentzian Polynomials on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{\geq 0}
and the Second order cone.

Quadratic forms Lorentzian on the nonnegative orthant
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{\geq 0}
(the usual Lorenztian polynomials) are simply quadratic forms with nonnegative coefficients and exactly one positive eigenvalue (see e.g.
Lemma

3.1
(b)). Therefore it is easy to test whether a quadratic form is
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{\geq 0}
-Lorentzian. It was recently shown in
[
Chi24
]
that while testing whether a polynomial is Lorentzian is easy in any degree, testing whether a polynomial is stable (hyperbolic on the nonnegative orthant) is a diffciult computational problems starting in degree
3
.

The second order cone
\mathcal{L}_{n}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is defined by

\mathcal{L}_{n}=\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\,\,\mid\,\,x_{1}^{2}\geq x_{2}^{2}+\dots+x_{n}^{2}\}.

Let
B
be the diagonal matrix
n\times n
matrix with
1
in the
(1,1)
-entry and
-1
in all other diagonal entries. It is well-known that by the S-Lemma
[
PT07
]
,
[
LS75
]
a quadratic form
q
with matrix
Q
is nonnegative on
\mathcal{L}_{n}
if and only if there exists a nonnegative
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that
Q-\lambda B
is positive semidefinite. Note that
\lambda>0
would imply that
q
is strictly positive on the interior of
\mathcal{L}_{n}
, and therefore if
q
has exactly one positive eigenvalue, then
q
is
\mathcal{L}_{n}
-Lorentzian (
Lemma

3.1
(c)). If
\lambda=0
, then
Q
is positive semidefinite, and if
Q
has exactly one positive eigenvalue, then
q=\langle v,x\rangle^{2}
for some
v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. In this case
q
is
\mathcal{L}_{n}
-Lorentzian if and only if
v\in\mathcal{L}_{n}
, since the second order cone is self-dual. Therefore we can test whether a quadratic form is
\mathcal{L}_{n}
-Lorentzian by semidefinite programming and establishing the signature of
Q
.

However, as we will see below, there are self-dual cones
{\mathcal{K}}
for which the set of
{\mathcal{K}}
-Lorenztian quadratic forms is complicated. We now turn to the cone of positive semidefinite matrices
\mathcal{S}^{n}_{+}
.

3.2.
Quadratic Lorentzian Polynomials on
\mathcal{S}^{n}_{+}

We consider quadratic forms Lorentzian on the cone
\mathcal{S}^{n}_{+}
of
n\times n
positive semidefinite symmetric matrices. Let
\mathcal{S}^{n}
denote the vector space of real symmetric
n\times n
matrices equipped with the standard inner product
\langle A,B\rangle=\operatorname{trace}AB
. It is well-known that the cone
\mathcal{S}^{n}_{+}
is self-dual with the respect to the standard inner product
[
Bar02
, Chapter II.12]
. Linear maps that are
S^{n}_{+}
-nonnegative are known as
positive linear maps
, and they are also related to nonnegative biquadratic forms on
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We describe this connection now.

Let
V
be the vector space of linear maps from
\mathcal{S}^{n}
to itself.
Note that here we are not restricting the matrices of linear transformations to be symmetric, and we have
\dim V=\binom{n}{2}^{2}
. A linear map
L:\mathcal{S}^{n}\rightarrow\mathcal{S}^{n}
is called
a positive linear map
if
L(\mathcal{S}^{n}_{+})\subseteq\mathcal{S}^{n}_{+}
. It follows from
Corollary

3.8
that quadratic
\mathcal{S}^{n}_{+}
-Lorentzian polynomials correspond to positive linear maps whose matrix is symmetric and has exactly one positive eigenvalue.

Let
W
be the vector of real biquadaratic forms on
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
, i.e. homogeneous polynomials in variables
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
,
(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})
which are quadratic in
x
and
y
variables. Note that
\dim W=\binom{n}{2}^{2}
. We can define a linear map
\Psi
from
V
to
W
.

\Psi:V\rightarrow W,\,\,\,\text{via}\,\,\,\Psi(L)=\langle yy^{t},L(xx^{t})\rangle.

It is well-known that
\Psi
is a linear isomorphism and a map
L:\mathcal{S}^{n}\rightarrow\mathcal{S}^{n}
is positive if and only if the associated biquadratic form
\Psi(L)
is nonnegative on
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}

[
Bv20
]
.

Let
V^{\prime}
be the vector space of quadratic forms on
\mathcal{S}^{n}
, which we can equivalently see as a subspace of
V
consisting of linear maps
\mathcal{S}^{n}\rightarrow\mathcal{S}^{n}
with a symmetric matrix. It follows that
\Psi(V^{\prime})
is the vector space
W^{\prime}
of biquadratic forms which are symmetric under swapping
x
and
y
. We see from the above discussion that the image of
\SL^{2}(\mathcal{S}^{n}_{+})
under
\Psi
is a non-convex subset of the cone of nonnegative biquadratic forms in
W^{\prime}
. It is not clear to us if the signature condition of exactly one positive eigenvalue has a natural translation in terms of biquadratic forms.

However, we can use the criterion of Lemma
3.3
to provide a simpler criterion for nonsingular quadratic forms to be
\mathcal{S}^{n}_{+}
-Lorentzian , which will give us a different linear map, where we will have full understanding of the image of
\SL^{2}(\mathcal{S}^{n}_{+})

Corollary 3.11
.

Let
q
be a quadratic form on
\mathcal{S}^{n}
whose matrix
Q
is nonsingular and has exactly one positive eigenvalue. Then
q
is
\mathcal{S}^{n}_{+}
-Lorentzian if and only if the associated quartic polynomial
q(xx^{t})
is nonnegative for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Proof.

The extreme rays of the cone
\mathcal{S}^{n}_{+}
are well-known to be spanned by rank
1
matrices of the form
xx^{t}
for
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}

[
Bar02
, Chapter II.12]
. Now we apply
Lemma

3.3
.
∎

Motivated by the above result we define the linear map
\Phi:V^{\prime}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}[x]_{n}^{4}
given by
\Phi(q)=q(xx^{t})
. We can define a natural linear projection from the vector space
W
of biquadratic forms on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
to the vectors space of quartic polynomials
{\mathbb{R}}[x]_{n}^{4}
by sending a biquadratic form
T(x,y)
to the quartic polynomial
T(x,x)
. We can see that

\Phi=\pi\circ\Psi,

i.e. the quartic polynomial
q(xx^{t})
can be obtained by plugging in
y=x
in the associated biquadratic form
\Psi(q)
. Interestingly, the image of
\SL^{2}(\mathcal{S}_{+}^{n})
under
\Phi
is the entire cone
P_{n,4}
of nonnegative quartics on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, and in particular it is convex.

Theorem 3.12
.

We have

\Phi(\SL^{2}(\mathcal{S}^{n}_{+}))=P_{n,4},

and moreover the same holds if we replace
\SL^{2}(\mathcal{S}^{n}_{+})
with nondegenerate quadratic forms in
\SL^{2}(\mathcal{S}^{n}_{+})
.

Proof.

It follows from the above discussion that the image of
\SL^{2}(\mathcal{S}^{n}_{+})
under
\Phi
lies inside
P_{n,4}
.

Let
r
be the following quadratic form on
\mathcal{S}^{n}
:

r(X)=\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}x_{ii}x_{jj}-x^{2}_{ij},

i.e.
r
is the sum of all
2\times 2
principal minors of a matrix
X\in\mathcal{S}^{n}
. It follows that
\Phi(r)=r(xx^{t})=0
. If we coordinatize
\mathcal{S}^{n}
by first listing diagonal elements (in any order) and then off-diagonal elements (in any order), then the matrix
R
of
r
becomes:

R=\begin{pmatrix}\begin{matrix}0&1/2&\dots&1/2&\\
1/2&0&\dots&1/2\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
1/2&1/2&\dots&0\end{matrix}&\pic@makebox@{\makebox}{}(0.0,0.0){\text{\huge 0}}\\
\pic@makebox@{\makebox}{}(0.0,0.0){\text{\huge 0}}&\begin{matrix}-1&0&\dots&0\\
0&-1&\dots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\dots&-1\end{matrix}\end{pmatrix}.

We see that the matrix
R
is nonsingular with exactly one positive eigenvalue. Since the map
\Phi
is onto
{\mathbb{R}}[x]_{n}^{4}
, given a polynomial
p\in P_{n,4}
, there exists a quadratic form
a\in V^{\prime}
, with matirx
A
, such that
\Phi(a)=p
. Now let
A^{\prime}=T+tR
and let
a^{\prime}
be the associated quadratic form. For a sufficiently large
t
we see that
A^{\prime}
is nonsingular with exactly one positive eigenvalue, and therefore by
Corollary

3.11
we have that
a^{\prime}\in\SL^{2}(\mathcal{S}^{n}_{+})
and
\Phi(a^{\prime})=\Phi(a)=p
.
∎

It quickly follows from the above result that testing whether a quadratic form is
\mathcal{S}^{n}_{+}
-Lorentzian is NP-hard.

Corollary 3.13
.

It is NP-hard to test whether a quadratic form is
\mathcal{S}^{n}_{+}
-Lorentzian.

Proof.

Note that the projection
\Phi
sending a quadratic form
q
to
q(xx^{t})
simply acts by substituting
x_{ij}=x_{i}x_{j}
into
q
. Under this map, several quadratic monomials
x_{ij}x_{k\ell}
may map to the same degree 4 monomial, since for example,
x_{1}^{2}\cdot x_{2}^{2}=(x_{1}x_{2})^{2}
. Therefore, in coordinates,
\Phi
simply adds several coefficients of monomials of
q
to produce a coefficent of the quartic polynomial
\Phi(q)
, and these sums are taken in disjoint groups.

Given a polynomial
p\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]_{n}^{4}
we can define a canonical preimage
m\in\Phi^{-1}(p)
as follows. Let
x^{a}
be a degree
4
monomial in
p
with coefficient
c_{a}
. Define
d_{a}
to be the number of possible quadrartic monomial
x_{ij}x_{k\ell}
that produce
x^{a}
under the substitution
x_{ij}=x_{i}x_{j}
. Then coefficient of
x_{ij}x_{k\ell}
in
m
is simply
\frac{c_{a}}{d_{a}}
where
a=\Phi(x_{ij}x_{k\ell})
. By construction we have
\Phi(m)=p
.

Then, as in proof of
Theorem

3.12
we consider
M^{\prime}=M+tR
. It is not hard to estimate
t
such that
M^{\prime}
has exactly one positive eigenvalue. Therefore, if we can test in polynomial time whether quadratic form
m^{\prime}
associated to
M^{\prime}
is
\mathcal{S}^{n}_{+}
-Lorentzian, then we can test whether
p\in P_{n,4}
by
Lemma

3.3
. However, it is known that testing nonnegativity of degree 4 polynomials is NP-Hard
[
BCSS98
, Part 1]
.
∎

4.
Completely
{\mathcal{K}}
-log-concave and
{\mathcal{K}}
-Lorentzian polynomials

We now investigate properties of the set
\SL^{d}({\mathcal{K}})
of
{\mathcal{K}}
-Lorentzian polynomials of degree
d
. We consider its interior, and we also show equivalence between
{\mathcal{K}}
-Lorentzian and
{\mathcal{K}}
-CLC polynomials.

4.1.
Log-concavity over proper convex cones

We show several equivalent characterizations of forms log-concave on a proper convex cone
\mathcal{K}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We employ the same reasoning as outlined in
[
AGV21
]
.
Additionally, we utilize the following version of Cauchy’s interlacing Theorem.

Lemma 4.1

(Cauchy’s interlacing Theorem I)
.

[
HJ13
, Corollary
4.3.9
]

Let
A
be a symmetric matrix and vector
v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
. Then, the eigenvalues of
A-vv^{t}
interlace the eigenvalues of
A
, i.e., if
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}
are eigenvalues of
A
and
\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{n}

are eigenvalues of
A-vv^{T}
, then

\beta_{1}\leq\alpha_{1}\leq\dots\beta_{n}\leq\alpha_{n}

Therefore, we have the following result.

Lemma 4.2
.

[
AGV21
, Lemma 2.4]

Let
Q\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
be a symmetric matrix and let
P\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n}
. If
Q
has exactly one positive eigenvalue, then
PQP^{t}
has at most one positive eigenvalue.

Remark 4.3
.

Let
Q
be a symmetric matrix. Then
Q
has exactly one positive eigenvalue if and only if
Q
has at most one positive eigenvalue and there exists
a\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
such that
a^{t}Qa>0
.

The following lemma can be found in
[
HW17
]
and
[
AGV21
]
. However, for the sake of completeness, we provide a proof here to keep the paper self-contained.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
Q
be a symmetric matrix and
a\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
such that
a^{t}Qa>0
. Then the following statements are equivalent:

(a)

Q
has exactly one positive eigenvalue

(b)

the matrix
(a^{t}Qa)\cdot Q-t(Qa)(Qa)^{t}
is negative semidefinite for all
t\geq 1
.

(c)

Q
is negative semidefinite on the hyperplane
(Qa)^{t}
.

Proof.

(a\Rightarrow b)
Since the matrix
(Qa)(Qa)^{t}
is positive semidefinite, it suffices to prove part (2) for
t=1
.
Suppose
b\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is not a scalar multiple of
a
. Consider the
2\times n
matrix
P
with rows
a^{t}
and
b^{t}
. Then

(1)

PQP^{t}=\begin{bmatrix}a^{t}Qa&a^{t}Qb\\
b^{t}Qa&b^{t}Qb&\end{bmatrix}

By
Lemma

4.2
, the matrix
PQP^{t}
has at most one positive eigenvalue. Then,
\det(PQP^{t})=a^{t}Qa\cdot b^{t}Qb-a^{t}Qb\cdot b^{t}Qa\leq 0
. Thus,

b^{t}\left((a^{t}Qa)\cdot Q-(Qa)(Qa)^{t}\right)b\leq 0

for all
b\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

(
b\Rightarrow c
) Since
a^{t}Qa\cdot Q-(Qa)(Qa)^{t}
is negative semidefinite and
a^{t}Qa>0
, we have

b^{t}\left((a^{t}Qa)\cdot Q-(Qa)(Qa)^{t}\right)b\leq 0

for all
b\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. Therefore
a^{t}Qa\cdot b^{t}Qb-a^{t}Qb\cdot b^{t}Qa\leq 0
. Thus,
Q
is negative semidefinite on the hyperplane
\{b\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\,\mid\,b^{t}Qa=0\}=(Qa)^{\perp}
.

(
c\Rightarrow a
) Since
Q
is negative semidefinite on the hyperplane
\{b\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\,\mid\,b^{T}Qa=0\}=(Qa)^{\perp}
and
a^{t}Qa>0
, we see that
Q
has exactly one positive eigenvalue.
∎

We now present several equivalent conditions for log-concavity of a form
f\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]_{n}^{d}
on a proper convex cone
\mathcal{K}
, which are quite similar to the conditions described in
[
AGV21
]
and
[
BH20
]
for the nonnegative orthant.

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
f\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]_{n}^{d}
be a form of degree
d\geq 2
and
{\mathcal{K}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be a proper convex cone. Suppose that
f(a)>0
for all
a\in\inter{\mathcal{K}}
, and
let
Q_{a}
denote the Hessian matrix of
f
evaluated at a:
Q_{a}=H_{f}(a)
. The following statements are equivalent:

(a)

f
is log-concave on
{\mathcal{K}}
.

(b)

Q_{a}
has exactly one positive eigenvalue for all
a\in\inter{\mathcal{K}}
.

(c)

The quadratic form
x^{t}Q_{a}x
is negative semidefinite on the hyperplane
(Q_{a}a)^{\perp}
for all
a\in\inter{\mathcal{K}}
.

(d)

For all
a\in\inter{\mathcal{K}}
, the matrix
(a^{t}Q_{a}a)Q_{a}-(Q_{a}a)(Q_{a}a)^{t}
is negative semidefinite.

Proof.

Euler’s Identity states that for a homogeneous polynomial
f
of degree
d
,
\langle\nabla f(x),x\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\partial_{i}f(x)=d\cdot f(x)
. Using this on
f
and
\partial_{i}f
we easily get the following identities:

Q_{a}a=(d-1)\cdot\nabla f(a),\ a^{t}Q_{a}a=d(d-1)\cdot f(a).

Additionally, the Hessian of
\log(f)
at
x=a
equals

(2)

H_{\log f}(a)=\left(\frac{f\cdot H_{f}-\nabla f\nabla f^{t}}{f^{2}}\right)|_{{\mathbf{x}}={\mathbf{a}}}=d(d-1)\frac{a^{t}Q_{a}a\cdot Q_{a}-\frac{d}{d-1}(Q_{a}a)(Q_{a}a)^{t}}{(a^{t}Qa)^{2}}.

(
a\Rightarrow b
) Since
f
is a non-zero form which is log-concave on all points of the interior of
{\mathcal{K}}
we see that
f(a)>0
for all
a\in\inter{\mathcal{K}}
, and the
Hessian of
\log(f)
at
a
is negative semidefinite. Since
a^{t}Q_{a}a>0
we see from (
2
) that
Q_{a}
has at most
1
positive eigenvalue. However,
Q_{a}
cannot be negative definite, since
a^{t}Q_{a}a>0
. Thus
Q_{a}
has exactly one positive eigenvalue.

(
b\Leftrightarrow c\Leftrightarrow d
)
The proof follows by
Lemma

4.4
.
∎

We also have analogous conditions for strict log-concavity.

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
f\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]_{n}^{d}
be a form of degree
d\geq 2
and
{\mathcal{K}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be a proper convex cone. Suppose that
f(a)>0
for all
a\in{\mathcal{K}}
, and
let
Q_{a}
denote the Hessian matrix of
f
evaluated at a:
Q_{a}=H_{f}(a)
. The following statements are equivalent:

(a)

f
is strictly log-concave on
{\mathcal{K}}
.

(b)

For all
a\in{\mathcal{K}}
, the quadratic form
x^{t}Q_{a}x
is negative definite on the hyperplane
(Q_{a}a)^{\perp}
.

(c)

The matrix
Q_{a}
is nonsingular and has exactly one positive eigenvalue for all
a\in{\mathcal{K}}
.

Proof.

(
a\Rightarrow b
) The Hessian of
\log(f(x))
at
x=a
is negative definite. Then by Equation (
2
) the matrix
M_{a}:=(a^{t}Q_{a}a)\cdot Q_{a}-\frac{d}{d-1}(Q_{a}a)(Q_{a}a)^{t}
is negative definite. Consider the restriction of the quadratic form
x^{t}M_{a}x
to the hyperplane
(Q_{a}a)^{\perp}
. For
x
in this hyperplane we have
x^{t}M_{a}x=(a^{t}Q_{a}a)\cdot x^{t}Q_{a}x
, and so
x^{t}Q_{a}x
is negative definite on
(Q_{a}a)^{\perp}
.

(
b\Rightarrow c
) Since
x^{t}Q_{a}x
is negative definite on the hyperplane
(Q_{a}a)^{\perp}
, we see that
Q
has at least
n-1
negative eigenvalues. But, since

d(d-1)\cdot f(a)=a^{t}Q_{a}a>0
we see that
Q_{a}
must also have a positive eigenvalue.

(
c\Rightarrow a
) We need to establish that the matrix
a^{t}Q_{a}a\cdot Q_{a}-\frac{d}{d-1}(Q_{a}a)(Q_{a}a)^{t}
is negative definite using the fact that
Q_{a}
is nonsingular. The proof is exactly the same as the proof of
(a\Rightarrow b)
in
Lemma

4.4
.

∎

The following result shows that
f
is log-concave at
a
if and only if its directional derivative in the direction
a
is log-concave at
a
, cf.
[
ALOGV24b
, Lemma 2.1]
.

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
f\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]_{n}^{d}
be a homogeneous polynomial of degree
d\geq 3
with
f(a)>0
at some point
a\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. Then
f
is log-concave at
a
if and only if
D_{a}(f)
is log-concave at
a
.

Proof.

By using Euler’s identity for a homogeneous polynomial of degree
d
, we can express
d\cdot f(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}=D_{x}(f(x))
. Therefore,
f(a)>0
if and only if
D_{a}f(a)>0
. Moreover,
H_{D_{a}f}(a)=(d-2)H_{f}(a)
. Then at the point
x=a
, signature of
H_{f}(a)
and
H_{D_{a}f}(a)
are the same. Then the claim follows from
Proposition

4.5
. ∎

We also show that log-concavity at a point is preserved under invertible linear transformations.

Lemma 4.8
.

If
f(x)\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]_{n}^{d}

is log-concave at
a
, then
f(Ax)
is log-concave at
A^{-1}a
for any nonsingular
n\times n
matrix
A
.

Proof.

The Hessian of
f(Ax)
at
A^{-1}a
is equal to
A^{t}H_{f}(a)A
. Therefore the Hessian of
f(Ax)
at
A^{-1}a
has exactly one positive eigenvalue and log-concavity of
f(Ax)
at
A^{-1}a
follows by
Proposition

4.5
.
∎

4.2.
Interior

Brändén and Leake
[
BL23
]
proved that
\SL^{d}({\mathcal{K}})
is a closed set, and it is the closure of its interior.
We use their result to describe the interior of
\SL^{d}({\mathcal{K}})
. We define the set
\iSL^{d}({\mathcal{K}})
as the set consisting forms in
\SL^{d}({\mathcal{K}})
which additionally satisfy
the following properties: for all
a_{1},\dots,a_{d-2}\in{\mathcal{K}}
the matrix
Q
of
q=D_{a_{1}}\dots D_{a_{d-2}}f
is nonsingular and
x^{t}Qy>0
for all
x,y\in{\mathcal{K}}
, i.e. strict positivity in
Definition

2.2
holds on all of
{\mathcal{K}}
, and not just in the interior of
{\mathcal{K}}
. Next we show that
\iSL^{d}({\mathcal{K}})
is the interior of
\SL^{d}({\mathcal{K}})
.

Theorem 4.9
.

Let
{\mathcal{K}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be a proper convex cone. Then
\iSL^{d}_{n}({\mathcal{K}})
is the interior of
\SL^{d}_{n}({\mathcal{K}})
, and

\displaystyle\SL^{d}_{n}({\mathcal{K}})=\overline{\iSL^{d}_{n}({\mathcal{K}})}

Proof.

It suffices to show that (1) the set
\iSL^{d}_{n}({\mathcal{K}})
is open, and (2) any form
f\in\SL^{d}_{n}({\mathcal{K}})\setminus\iSL^{d}_{n}({\mathcal{K}})
does not lie in the interior of
\SL^{d}_{n}({\mathcal{K}})
.

Openness of
\SL^{d}({\mathcal{K}})
is clear for
d\leq 1
. For
d\geq 2
, let
f\in\iSL^{d}({\mathcal{K}})
and let
B
be the intersection of
{\mathcal{K}}
with the unit sphere in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
:
B=\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\cap{\mathcal{K}}
. Let
\alpha
be the infimum of
x^{t}Qy
where
q=D_{a_{1}}\cdots D_{a_{d-2}}f
with
a_{i},x,y\in B
. Since we are taking the infimum of a continuous function on a compact set
B^{d}
(
d-2
choices for
a_{i}
and one each for
x
and
y
), we see that
\alpha>0
. Otherwise the minimum of
0
is attained on some point of
B^{d}
, which contradicts
f\in\iSL^{d}({\mathcal{K}})
. Similarly let
\beta
be the minimum of
|\det Q|
over all choices of
a_{1},\dots,a_{d-2}\in B
. Again we see that
\beta>0
.
Therefore for any
g\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]_{n}^{d}
there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that
f+\varepsilon g\in\iSL^{d}({\mathcal{K}})
. Thus we see that
\iSL^{d}({\mathcal{K}})
is open.

If a form
f
lies in
\SL^{d}({\mathcal{K}})\setminus\iSL^{d}({\mathcal{K}})
, then for some choice of
a_{1},\dots,a_{d-2},x,y\in B
we have
x^{t}Qy=0
, or
Q
is singular, where
q=D_{a_{1}}\cdots D_{a_{d-2}}f
. In the first case, take
g\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]_{n}^{d}
such that
x^{t}Q^{\prime}y<0
where
q^{\prime}=D_{a_{1}}\cdots D_{a_{d-2}}g
. Then for any
\varepsilon>0
we have
f+\varepsilon g\notin\SL^{d}({\mathcal{K}})
, and thus
f\notin\inter\SL^{d}({\mathcal{K}})
. In the second case take
g\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]_{n}^{d}
such that
q^{\prime}=D_{a_{1}}\cdots D_{a_{d-2}}g
is strictly positive on the kernel of
Q
. Then for sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
we have
f+\varepsilon g\notin\SL^{d}({\mathcal{K}})
, and thus
f\notin\inter\SL^{d}({\mathcal{K}})
.
∎

4.3.
Equivalence of
{\mathcal{K}}
-Lorentzian and
{\mathcal{K}}
-CLC forms

In this subsection, we will often utilize the equivalent defintion of
{\mathcal{K}}
-Lorentzian polynomials using positivity of
D_{a_{1}}\dots D_{a_{d}}f
for all
a_{1},\dots,a_{d}\in\inter{\mathcal{K}}
, as explained in
Remark

2.3
.

Theorem 4.10
.

Let
{\mathcal{K}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be a proper cone. A form
f\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]_{n}^{d}
is
{\mathcal{K}}
-Lorentzian if and only if
f
is
{\mathcal{K}}
-CLC. Equivalently,
\SL^{d}({\mathcal{K}})=\mathrm{CLC}^{d}({\mathcal{K}})
.

Proof.

\Leftarrow

For
f\in\mathrm{CLC}^{d}({\mathcal{K}})
, the form
f
and all its repeated directional derivatives are log-concave on
\inter{\mathcal{K}}
. Thus we see that, the conditions for a polynomial to lie in
\SL^{d}({\mathcal{K}})
are a subset of the conditions for a polynomial to lie in
\mathrm{CLC}^{d}({\mathcal{K}})
.

\Rightarrow

According to
Definition

2.2
, nonnegative constants and linear forms nonnegative on
{\mathcal{K}}
are
{\mathcal{K}}
-Lorentzian polynomials. We establish this result by induction on the degree
d
of the forms. For the base case
d=2
, let
f\in\SL^{2}({\mathcal{K}})
. The positivity condition (2) in
Definition

2.2
and
Remark

2.3
ensure log-concavity for directional derivatives of degree
1
and
0
for
f
. The signature condition (1) in
Definition

2.2
implies that
f
is log-concave on
\inter{\mathcal{K}}
by
Proposition

4.5
.

Now we assume that the result holds for forms in
\SL^{d-1}({\mathcal{K}})
.
By the induction hypothesis,
D_{a}f
is
{\mathcal{K}}
-CLC for any
a\in\inter{\mathcal{K}}
, and since the set
\SL^{d-1}({\mathcal{K}})=\CL^{d-1}({\mathcal{K}})
is closed, we see that
D_{a}f
is
{\mathcal{K}}
-CLC for any
a\in{\mathcal{K}}
. Thus it suffices to show that
f
is log-concave on
\inter{\mathcal{K}}
. Similarly to the base case, the positivity condition (2) in
Definition

2.2
implies
f(a)>0
for all
a\in\inter{\mathcal{K}}
.
By the induction hypothesis,
D_{a}f
is log-concave at any
a\in\inter{\mathcal{K}}
, and we also have
H_{D_{a}f}(a)=(d-2)H_{f}(a)
. Therefore, by
Proposition

4.7
we see that
f
is log-concave at
a
.
Thus,
f\in\CL^{d}({\mathcal{K}})
.
∎

Remark 4.11
.

By
Theorem

4.10
and
Theorem

4.9
we have shown that
\CL^{d}({\mathcal{K}})
is the closure of its interior, denoted as
\inter\CL^{d}({\mathcal{K}})
, consisting of strictly
{\mathcal{K}}
-CLC polynomials.

4.4.
{\mathcal{K}}
-CLC forms over proper convex cones

We now derive some consequences for the structure of the set
\CL^{d}({\mathcal{K}})=\SL^{d}({\mathcal{K}})
. We use
H_{f}
to denote the Hessian matrix of a form
f
.

Lemma 4.12
.

Let
f\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]^{d}_{n}
be a nonzero polynomial and
{\mathcal{K}}
be a proper convex cone.

(a)

If
f\in\CL^{d}({\mathcal{K}})
, then
H_{f}(a)
has exactly one positive eigenvalue for all
a\in\inter{\mathcal{K}}
.

(b)

If
f\in\inter\CL^{d}({\mathcal{K}})
, then
H_{f}(a)
is nonsingular and has exactly one positive eigenvalue for all
a\in{\mathcal{K}}
.

Proof.

(a)
follows from
Proposition

4.5
.

(
b
)
f\in\inter\CL^{d}({\mathcal{K}})
implies that
f
is strictly log-concave on all points of
{\mathcal{K}}
. The rest follows from
Proposition

4.6
.
∎

Proposition 4.13
.

Let
f\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]_{n}^{d}
be a nonzero form of degree
d\geq 2
,
{\mathcal{K}}
be a proper convex cone in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, and
Q_{a}=H_{f}(a)
.

(a)

Let
f\in\inter\CL^{d}({\mathcal{K}})
. Then quadratic form
x^{t}Q_{a}x
is negative definite on
(Q_{a}b)^{\perp}
for every
a,b\in{\mathcal{K}}
such that
Q_{a}b\neq 0
.

(b)

Let
f\in\CL^{d}({\mathcal{K}})
. Then quadratic form
x^{t}Q_{a}x
is negative semidefinite on
(Q_{a}b)^{\perp}
for every
a\in\inter{\mathcal{K}}
, and
b\in{\mathcal{K}}
such that
Q_{a}b\neq 0
.

Proof.

(a) Given that
f\in\inter\CL^{d}({\mathcal{K}})
and
a\in{\mathcal{K}}
, we know that
Q_{a}
is nonsingular has exactly one positive eigenvalue by
Lemma

4.12
. Additionally, since
b\in{\mathcal{K}}
, so it follows from the
Definition

2.1
that
b^{t}Q_{a}b=D_{b}D_{b}f(a)>0
. Then the result follows from
Lemma

4.4
.

(b) This follows from (a) by taking limits and using
Remark

4.11
.
∎

Lemma 4.14
.

Let
f\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]_{n}^{d}
be a nonzero polynomial,
{\mathcal{K}}
be a proper convex cone in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, and
Q_{a}:=H_{f}(a)
for
a\in{\mathcal{K}}
.

(a)

If
f\in\inter\CL^{d}({\mathcal{K}})
, the restriction of
Q_{a}
on a plane spanned by
b,c\in{\mathcal{K}}
is a positive matrix with negative determinant.

(b)

If
f\in\CL^{d}({\mathcal{K}})
, the restriction of
Q_{a}
on a plane spanned by
b,c\in{\mathcal{K}}
is a nonnegative matrix with nonpositive determinant.

Proof.

(a)
By
Lemma

4.12
,
Q:=H_{f}(a)
is nonsingular and has exactly one positive eigenvalue. Then the rest follows from
Remark

2.3
and the restriction of the Hessian to the plane spanned by
b,c\in{\mathcal{K}}
, outlined in (
1
).

(b)
Since
\CL^{d}({\mathcal{K}})
is the closure of its interior (See
Remark

4.11
), the result follows from part
(a)
by taking limits.
∎

4.5.
The sum of two Lorentzian polynomials

The sum of two log-concave polynomials may not be log-concave.
The subsequent result provides a sufficient condition for the sum of two
{\mathcal{K}}
-Lorentzian polynomials to also be
{\mathcal{K}}
-Lorentzian. This condition is analogous to the one provided in
[
ALOGV24b
, Lemma 3.3]
for the sum of two log-concave polynomials with nonnegative coefficients to be log-concave on the nonnegative orthant.

Theorem 4.15
.

Let
f,g\in\SL^{d}({\mathcal{K}})
.
If there exist vectors
b,c\in{\mathcal{K}}
such that
D_{b}f=D_{c}g\not\equiv 0
, then
f+g
is
{\mathcal{K}}
-Lorentzian.

Proof.

We prove this result by induction on the degree
d
. If
d=1
, then
(f+g)(a)=f(a)+g(a)>0
for any
a\in\inter{\mathcal{K}}
. Thus,
f+g
is
{\mathcal{K}}
-Lorentzian.

Now, suppose
d\geq 2
and fix
a\in\inter{\mathcal{K}}
. Let
f_{1}=D_{a}f
and
g_{1}=D_{a}g
. Note that
f_{1}
and
g_{1}
are
{\mathcal{K}}
-Lorentzian polynomials, and additionally
D_{b}f_{1}=D_{b}D_{a}f=D_{a}D_{b}f=D_{a}D_{c}g=D_{c}D_{a}g=D_{c}g_{1}
. If we can show that
D_{b}f_{1}=D_{c}g_{1}\not\equiv 0
for all
a\in\inter{\mathcal{K}}
, then by the induction assumption we have
f_{1}+g_{1}=D_{a}(f+g)
is
{\mathcal{K}}
-Lorentzian for all
a\in\inter{\mathcal{K}}
and therefore
f+g
is
{\mathcal{K}}
-Lorentzian.

Since the set
\SL^{d-1}({\mathcal{K}})
is closed it follows that
D_{b}f
is a nonzero polynomial in
\SL^{d-1}({\mathcal{K}})
. In particular,
D_{b}f
is strictly positive on
\inter K
, and therefore
D_{a}D_{b}f(a)=(d-1)(D_{b}f)(a)>0
. Thus we see that
D_{b}f_{1}=D_{b}D_{a}f=D_{a}D_{b}f
is not identically zero.
∎

5.
{\mathcal{K}}
-Lorentzian polynomials over self-dual cones

In this section, we outline an interesting connection
{\mathcal{K}}
-Lorentzian polynomials on a self-dual cone
{\mathcal{K}}
and the generalized Perron-Frobenius theorem. We refer the reader to
[
GL23
]
for various classes of self-dual cones other than the nonnegative orthant, the second order cone, and the cone of positive semidefnite matrices.

5.1.
{\mathcal{K}}
-irreducibility

We now show an alternative characterization of the set of Lorentzian polynomials
\SL^{d}({\mathcal{K}})
for a self-dual cone
{\mathcal{K}}
. We note that for a quadratic form
q
, the Hessian matrix
H_{q}
is equal to twice the matrix
Q
of
q
. Therefore our results can be equivalently stated in terms of the matrix
H_{q}
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
f\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]_{n}^{d}
be a form of degree
d\geq 2
and
{\mathcal{K}}
be a self-dual cone. Then
f\in\SL^{d}({\mathcal{K}})
if and only if for all
a_{1},\dots,a_{d-2}\in\inter{\mathcal{K}}
the matrix
Q
of the quadratic form
q=D_{a_{1}}\dots D_{a_{d-2}}f
satisfies the following two conditions:

(a)

Q
either nonsingular and
{\mathcal{K}}
-irreducible, or singular and
{\mathcal{K}}
-nonnegative.

(b)

Q
has exactly one positive eigenvalue.

Proof.

This follows from
Definition

2.2
and
Corollary

3.10
. ∎

Therefore, we have the following characterization of Lorentzian polynomials or, equivalently, CLC polynomials on the nonnegative orthant.

Corollary 5.2
.

Let
f\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]_{n}^{d}
be a form of degree
d\geq 2
. Then
f
is Lorentzian or CLC polynomial on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{\geq 0}
if and only if for all
a_{1},\dots,a_{d-2}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{>0}
, the matirx
Q
of the quadratic form
q=D_{a_{1}}\dots D_{a_{d-2}}f
satisfies the following two conditions:

(1)

Q
is either nonsingular and irreducible, or singular and nonnegative.

(2)

Q
has exactly one positive eigenvalue.

We now provide an alternative characterization of the interior
\iSL^{d}({\mathcal{K}})
of the set of
{\mathcal{K}}
-Lorentzian polynomials on a self-dual cone
{\mathcal{K}}
using
{\mathcal{K}}
-positivity.

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
f\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]_{n}^{d}
be a homogeneous polynomial of degree
d\geq 2
and
{\mathcal{K}}
be a self-dual cone. Then
f\in\iSL^{d}({\mathcal{K}})
if and only if for any
a_{1},\dots,a_{d-2}\in{\mathcal{K}}
, the matrix
Q
of the quadratic form
q=D_{a_{1}}\dots D_{a_{d-2}}f
satisfies the following two conditions:

(1)

Q
is
{\mathcal{K}}
-positive and nonsingular.

(2)

Q
has exactly one positive eigenvalue.

Proof.

This follows from
Theorem

4.9
(see also observations in
Lemma

3.1
and
Corollary

3.8
).
∎

5.2.
Extreme Rays

Let
{\mathcal{K}}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be a self-dual cone and
f\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]_{n}^{d}
be a homogeneous polynomial of degree
d
in
n
variables. By the conical Minkowski theorem, a proper convex cone
{\mathcal{K}}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is generated by its extreme rays. We recall that
{\mathcal{K}}
-CLC polynomials are a closed set in
{\mathbb{R}}_{n}^{d}[x]
, which is the closure of its interior, which consists of strictly
{\mathcal{K}}
-CLC polynomials. Therefore, we may ask whether taking directional derivatives with respect to extreme rays is sufficient to guarantee that a polynomial is
{\mathcal{K}}
-CLC. First we demonstrate that in general, testing
{\mathcal{K}}
-CLC conditions on extreme rays of
{\mathcal{K}}
is not sufficient.

Example 5.4
.

Consider the polynomial
f=x_{1}(x_{1}x_{2}+x_{1}x_{3}+x_{2}x_{3})+x_{4}^{3}
. Then over the nonnegative orthant
D_{e_{i}}f
are
{\mathcal{K}}
-Lorentzian polynomials, but
D_{{\mathbf{1}}}f
is not where
{\mathbf{1}}
denotes all-ones vector. Consequently,
f
is not a
{\mathcal{K}}
-Lorentzian polynomial over the nonnegative orthant.

However, with additional assumptions on the polynomial
f\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]_{n}^{d}
, testing
{\mathcal{K}}
-CLC conditions on the extreme rays of a self-dual cone
{\mathcal{K}}
will be sufficient.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
f\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]_{n}^{d}
be a form of degree
d\geq 2
. Let
{\mathcal{K}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be a self-dual cone and
E_{{\mathcal{K}}}
denote the set of extreme rays of cone
{\mathcal{K}}
. If the Hessian matrix
H_{f}(v)
is
{\mathcal{K}}
-positive for all
v\in E_{{\mathcal{K}}}
, then
D_{v_{1}}D_{v_{2}}f
is not identically zero for all
v_{1},v_{2}\in E_{{\mathcal{K}}}
.

Proof.

If
D_{v_{1}}D_{v_{2}}f\equiv 0
then we have
v_{2}^{t}H_{f}(x)v_{1}=0
for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. This contradicts
{\mathcal{K}}
-positivity of
H_{f}(v_{i})
.
∎

Remark 5.6
.

The above result does not hold if we only require that
H_{f}(v)
is
{\mathcal{K}}
-irreducible for all
v\in E_{k}
. For instance, consider
f=x_{1}x_{2}+x_{1}x_{3}
with
{\mathcal{K}}
the nonnegative orthant in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
. While the Hessian matrices are nonsingular and irreducible, we have
\partial_{2}\partial_{3}f
is identically zero.

Theorem 5.7
.

Let
f\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]_{n}^{d}
be a form of degree
d\geq 3
, and
{\mathcal{K}}
be a self-dual cone where
E_{{\mathcal{K}}}
denotes the set of its extreme rays. Suppose that the Hessian
H_{f}(v)
is nonsingular and
{\mathcal{K}}
-positive for all
v\in E_{{\mathcal{K}}}
. If
D_{v}f
is
{\mathcal{K}}
-CLC for all
v\in E_{{\mathcal{K}}}
, then
f
is
{\mathcal{K}}
-CLC.

Proof.

Let
a\in{\mathcal{K}}
and
write
a=\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}v_{i}
for real positive
c_{i}
and
v_{i}\in E_{\mathcal{K}}
. Then we have
D_{a}f=\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}D_{v_{i}}f
, and
D_{v_{j}}D_{v_{i}}f=D_{v_{i}}D_{v_{j}}f
. Now by
Lemma

5.5
all
D_{v_{j}}D_{v_{i}}f
are nonzero polynomials and we can apply
Theorem

4.15
to see that
D_{a}f
is
{\mathcal{K}}
-CLC for all
a\in{\mathcal{K}}
. This implies that
f
is
{\mathcal{K}}
-CLC by the equivalence between
{\mathcal{K}}
-Lorentzian and
{\mathcal{K}}
-CLC polynomials.
∎

Finally, we provide an alternative proof of another necessary and sufficient condition first proved in
[
BL23
, Theorem 8.15]
for
f\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]_{n}^{d}
to be a
{\mathcal{K}}
-Lorentzian polynomial. We demonstrate that testing conditions in
Definition

2.2
for directional derivatives along the extreme rays and one more additional point in the interior actually suffices.

Lemma 5.8
.

Let
f\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]_{n}^{d}
be a form of degree
d\geq 2
, and let
{\mathcal{K}}
be a proper convex cone where
E_{{\mathcal{K}}}
denotes the set of its extreme rays, and
a\in\inter{\mathcal{K}}
. If
D_{a}f
and
D_{v_{i}}f
are
{\mathcal{K}}
-CLC, then the sum
D_{a+v}f
for any
v\in E_{{\mathcal{K}}}
is also
{\mathcal{K}}
-CLC.

Proof.

We observe that for any non-zero
{\mathcal{K}}
-CLC polynomial
g
we have that
D_{a}g
is a nonzero polynomial for all
a\in\inter{\mathcal{K}}
.
If
D_{v}f
is identically zero, then the statement follows, so we may assume that
D_{v}f
is a nonzero polynomial. Since
D_{v}f
is
{\mathcal{K}}
-CLC, we see that
D_{v}D_{a}f=D_{a}D_{v}f
is a nonzero polynomial. We now conclude that the sum
D_{v+a}f=D_{v}f+D_{a}f
is
{\mathcal{K}}
-CLC by
Theorem

4.15
.
∎

Theorem 5.9
.

Let
f\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]_{n}^{d}
be a form of degree
d\geq 2
, and let
{\mathcal{K}}
be a proper convex cone where
E_{{\mathcal{K}}}
denotes the set of its extreme rays, and
a\in\inter{\mathcal{K}}
. Then
f
is
{\mathcal{K}}
-Lorentzian if and only if for all
v_{1},\dots,v_{d-2}\in E_{{\mathcal{K}}}
and all
0\leq k\leq d-2
, the quadratic form
D_{v_{1}}D_{v_{2}}\dots D_{v_{k}}D_{a}^{d-2-k}f
is
{\mathcal{K}}
-Lorentzian.

Proof.

\Rightarrow
Since
\SL^{m}({\mathcal{K}})
is closed for all
m\geq 0
we see that conditions on directional derivatives in directions of extreme rays and
a
are just a subset of conditions defining
{\mathcal{K}}
-Lorentzian polynomials.

\Leftarrow

We induct on the degree
d
of
f
. The base case
d=2
is trivially true since we do not take any directional derivatives.

For
d\geq 3
we assume that the result is true up to degree
d-1
. It suffices to show that
D_{b}f\in\SL^{d-1}({\mathcal{K}})
for all
b\in\inter{\mathcal{K}}
. We can write any
b\in\inter{\mathcal{K}}
as a positive linear combination of
a
and extreme rays
v_{i}
, where the coeficient of
a
is strictly positive:
b=\lambda a+\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}v_{i}
with
\lambda,c_{i}>0
, and
v_{i}\in E_{{\mathcal{K}}}
.

Now we iteratively apply
Lemma

5.8
to
\lambda a+c_{1}v_{1}
,
(\lambda a+c_{1}v_{1})+c_{2}v_{2}
,
(\lambda a+c_{1}v_{1}+c_{2}v_{2})+c_{3}v_{3}
and so on, since
\lambda a+\sum c_{i}v_{i}
always lies in the interior of
{\mathcal{K}}
.

∎

An immediate corollary provides a characterization of Lorentzian polynomials which is first proved in
[
BL23
, Corollary 8.16]
.

Corollary 5.10
.

Let
f\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]_{n}^{d}
be a form of degree
d\geq 2
and
{\mathbf{1}}
denote the all-ones vector. Then
f
is Lorentzian if and only if for all
i_{1},\dots,i_{d-2}\in[n]
and all
0\leq k\leq d-2
, the quadratic polynomial
\partial_{i_{1}}\dots\partial_{i_{k}}D^{d-2-k}_{{\mathbf{1}}}f
is Lorentzian.

For a self-dual cone
{\mathcal{K}}
, using
Theorem

5.1
, we have the following result.

Theorem 5.11
.

Let
f\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]_{n}^{d}
be a form of degree
d\geq 2
and
{\mathcal{K}}
be a self-dual cone where
E_{{\mathcal{K}}}
denotes the set of its extreme rays. Fix
a\in\inter{\mathcal{K}}
. Then
f
is
{\mathcal{K}}
-Lorentzian if and only if for all
v_{1},\dots,v_{k}\in E_{{\mathcal{K}}}
where
0\leq k\leq d-2
, the matrix
Q
of the quadratic form
q=D_{v_{1}}D_{v_{2}}\dots D_{v_{k}}D_{a}^{d-2-k}f
satisfies the following two conditions:

(1)

Q
either nonsingular and
{\mathcal{K}}
-irreducible, or singular and
{\mathcal{K}}
-nonnegative.

(2)

Q
has exactly one positive eigenvalue.

As a consequence we provide another alternative necessary and sufficient conditions for Lorentzian polynomials.

Corollary 5.12
.

Let
{\mathbf{1}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{>0}
denote the all-ones vector.

f
is Lorentzian over
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{\geq 0}
if and only if for all
i_{1},\dots,i_{d-2}\in[n]
and
a\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{>0},0\leq k\leq d-2
, the matrix
Q
of the quadratic form
q=\partial_{i_{1}}\dots\partial_{i_{k}}D_{{\mathbf{1}}}^{d-2-k}f
satisfies the following two conditions:

(1)

Q
is either nonsingular and irreducible, or singular and nonnegative.

(2)

Q
has exactly one positive eigenvalue.

Note that it is easy to check irreducibility of a nonnegative matrix due to the following result, see
[
Bru79
]
.

Theorem 5.13
.

An
n\times n
nonnegative matrix
A
is permutation equivalent to an irreducible matrix if and only if
A
contains no zero row or zero column.
