Repro Samples Method for Finite- and Large-Sample Inferences
Min-ge Xie, Peng Wang

Abstract
This article presents a novel, general, and effective simulation-inspired approach, called repro samples method , to conduct statistical inference. The approach studies the performance of artificial samples, referred to as repro samples , obtained by mimicking the true observed sample to achieve uncertainty quantification and construct confidence sets for parameters of interest with guaranteed coverage rates. Both exact and asymptotic inferences are developed. An attractive feature of the general framework developed is that it does not rely on the large sample central limit theorem and is likelihood-free. As such, it is thus effective for complicated inference problems which we can not solve using the large sample central limit theorem. The proposed method is applicable to a wide range of problems, including many open questions where solutions were previously unavailable, for example, those involving discrete or non-numerical parameters. To reduce the large computational cost of such inference problems, we develop a unique matching scheme to obtain a data-driven candidate set. Moreover, we show the advantages of the proposed framework over the classical Neyman-Pearson framework. We demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed approach on various models throughout the paper and provide a case study that addresses an open inference question on how to quantify the uncertainty for the unknown number of components in a normal mixture model. To evaluate the empirical performance of our repro samples method, we conduct simulations and study real data examples with comparisons to existing approaches. Although the development pertains to the settings where the large sample central limit theorem does not apply, it also has direct extensions to the cases where the central limit theorem does hold.

Introduction

Statistical inference includes quantifying uncertainty inherited from sampling based on an observed copy of data.
The approach of repeatedly creating similar artificial (Monte-Carlo) data
to help assess the uncertainty has proved in the literature to be an effective method.
Examples include bootstrap, approximate Bayesian computing (ABC), generalized fiducial inference (GFI) algorithm,
permutation, among others; see, e.g.,
Efron and Tibshirani 1993
;
Fishman 1996
;
Robert 2016
;
Hannig et al. 2016
,
and references therein.
Most of these works, however, rely on the large sample central limit theorem (CLT) or approximations
to justify their validity of statistical inference.
Their applicability to some complicated
inference problems, especially those to which the large sample CLT does not apply, is limited. In this article, we develop a novel and wide reaching artificial-sample-based inferential framework, which we support with both finite and large sample theories.
The proposed framework has the advantage of providing valid inference without relying on likelihood functions or large sample theories.
It is especially effective for difficult inference problems such as those involving discrete and/or non-numerical parameters (e.g., the unknown number of clusters, model A, B, C, etc.) among others.
Although the repro samples method pertains to settings in which the large sample CLT does not apply, it also has direct extensions to the cases where the large sample theorem does hold.

Suppose that
the sample data
\bm{Y}=(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n})^{\top}\in{\cal Y}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}

are generated from a model

\bm{Y}|{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits}_{0}\sim M_{{\bf\theta}_{0}}(\cdot),
where

{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits}_{0}\in\Theta
is the true value of the model parameters that can be either numerical or nonnumerical or a mixture of both.
We assume (and assume only) that we know how to simulate data from
M_{\bf\theta}
, given
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits
. Often,
\bm{Y}|{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits}_{0}\sim M_{{\bf\theta}_{0}}(\cdot)

can be re-expressed in a structure equation form (also referred to as an “algorithmic model”):

\bm{Y}=G({\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits}_{0},\bm{U}),

(1)

where
G(\cdot,\cdot)
is a known mapping from
\Theta\times{\cal U}\mapsto{\cal Y}
and
\bm{U}=(U_{1},\ldots U_{r})^{\top}\in{\cal U}\subset\mathbb{R}^{r}
,
r\geq n
, is a random vector whose distribution is known or from which it can be simulated.
The reason that we adopt the formulation of (
1
) is that this model specification is very general.
For example, it includes the traditional statistical model specification using a density function as its special case:
Let
Y\sim f_{\theta_{0}}(y)
be a sample from a density function
f_{\theta}(y)
with true parameter value
\theta_{0}
.
We then express
Y
in the form of (
1
) as
Y=G(\theta_{0},U)
where
G(\theta,U)=F_{\theta}^{-1}(U)
,
U\sim U(0,1)
and

F_{\theta}(y)
is the cumulative distribution function of
f_{\theta}(y)
.
Additional examples, including more complicated ones,
will be provided throughout the paper.

The repro samples method uses a simple yet fundamental idea: study the performance of artificial samples that are obtained by mimicking the sampling mechanism
of the observed data; the
artificial samples then help to
quantify the uncertainty in estimation of the associated model and parameters. Specifically, let

\bm{y}_{obs}=G({\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits}_{0},\bm{u}^{rel})

be the realized observations, where
\bm{u}^{rel}
is the corresponding unobserved (thus unknown) realization of
\bm{U}
.
For any
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits\in\Theta
and
a copy of
\bm{u}^{*}\sim\bm{U}
, we can obtain
a copy of artificial sample
\bm{y}^{*}=G({\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits},\bm{u}^{*})
,
which we refer to in this paper as
repro samples
.
Note that, when
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits=\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{0}
and
\bm{u}^{*}
matches
\bm{u}^{rel}
, the corresponding repro sample
\bm{y}^{*}=G(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits,\bm{u}^{*})

will match
\bm{y}_{obs}
.
However, both
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{0}
and
\bm{u}^{rel}
are unknown.
Since
\bm{u}^{rel}
and
\bm{u}^{*}
are both realizations from the same
\bm{U}
distribution, we use this fact, together with a so-called
nuclear mapping function
on
{\cal U}\times\Theta\to\mathbb{R}^{q}
,

q\leq n
, to quantify how likely a
\bm{u}^{*}
is a potential match for
\bm{u}^{rel}
. For the collection of
\bm{u}^{*}
’s that are deemed as “likely” matches, we look for the corresponding
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits
that produces a matching repro sample
\bm{y}^{*}=G(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits,\bm{u}^{*})
with
\bm{y}^{*}=\bm{y}_{obs}
.
We use these
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits
to help recover
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{0}
.
We formally formulate the procedure
and show that a subset of
\Theta
constructed by this procedure has a desired frequency coverage rate and thus is a confidence set for
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{0}
.

In Section 2, we formally describe the repro samples framework. For a given potential value of
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits
, we construct a fixed level-
\alpha
Borel set of
\bm{u}^{*}
through a nuclear mapping function, and
analyze whether any
\bm{u}^{*}
in the Borel set produces repro samples
\bm{y}^{*}=G({\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits},{\bm{u}}^{*})
that match
\bm{y}_{obs}
. By controlling

\bm{u}^{*}
through the fixed Borel set and seeking for the matching, we obtain a theoretically guaranteed level-
\alpha
confidence set for
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{0}
. The use of nuclear mapping functions is new and general, and it greatly increases the flexibility and utility of the repro samples method. If the distribution of the nuclear mapping has an explicit form, so does the confidence set. When the the confidence set cannot be explicitly expressed,
we provide a generic algorithm. Moreover, the repro samples method can also be used to obtain p-values to conduct hypothesis testing
for a wide range of problems.
In addition, we further extended the framework beyond the parametric model (
1
), where the data generating model
G(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits,\bm{U})
is not completely known.
Although the repro samples method shares many insights with some Bayesian and fiducial procedures,
its development is purely under the frequentist paradigm. It
sidesteps the need to specify a likelihood or a loss function, and also the need to use large sample theories or the Dumpster-Shafer calculus.
It has a wide range of applications,
including problems involving any type of parameter spaces, exact and asymptotic inferences, parametric and nonparametric models, among others.

Section 3 provides
additional developments we use to facilitate our understanding and broaden the application of the repro samples method.
In Section 3.1, we explore
the relationship of our repro samples method with the classical Neyman-Pearson hypothesis testing procedure
by considering a special case in which we choose a test statistic as our nuclear mapping function. In general,
a nuclear mapping function does not need to be a test statistics. Even in special cases in which the nuclear mapping is defined by a typical test statistics, we show that a confidence set obtained by the repro samples method is either the same or smaller than the one obtained by inverting the classical Neyman-Pearson hypothesis testing procedure.

We also obtain a set of optimality results.
Furthermore, we develop in Section 3.2 a general technique to handle nuisance parameters through profiling.
The method
maintains the desired
coverage rates for confidence sets constructed for target parameters, even in
finite sample settings.
Finally in Section 3.3, we improve the computational efficiency by investigating the use of data-dependent candidate sets to greatly reduce the search region in the parameter spaces. Using a candidate set is particularly effective when the target parameter of the inference is discrete, since we can take advantage of a many-to-one mapping that is uniquely inherent in a repro samples procedure. We provide a Monte-Carlo approach to obtain the candidate set and supporting theories to ensure the coverage rate of the final confidence set.

To demonstrate the utility and performance of the repro samples method, we use a normal mixture model as a case study example in Section 4.
Accounting for uncertainty in an estimation of the unknown number of components in a mixture model, say
\tau_{0}
, is a
notoriously difficult and unsolved
inference problem
(
Roeder 1994
;
Chen et al. 2012
, e.g.,)
, partly because the parameter space of
\tau_{0}
is discrete and the large sample CLT does not apply on any point estimator
\hat{\tau}
of
\tau_{0}
.
Here, we
construct a finite-sample confidence set for the number of components
\tau_{0}
in the normal mixture model.
We demonstrate that the repro samples method can effectively solve this open inference problem while handling a large and varying number of nuisance parameters.
We perform simulation and real data analyses, and provide comparisons to existing frequentist and Bayesian approaches. The analyses is revealing. First, the regular criterion-based point estimators (even consistent estimators such as the BIC estimators) are often biased for smaller
\tau
and they rarely recover the true
\tau_{0}
. Second, existing (frequentist) likelihood-based procedures do not provide any upper bounds, and they have low power and work only asymptotically. Third, Bayesian credible sets obtained using the posteriors of
\tau
perform poorly in terms of covering
\tau_{0}
in repeated runs, even when a uniform flat prior of
\tau
(over a range including the true value
\tau_{0}
) is used. In addition, credible sets for
\tau_{0}
are very sensitive to the prior choices of both the target parameter
\tau
and also (surprisingly) the nuisance parameters. By contrast, the proposed repro sample confidence sets always recover
\tau_{0}
with the desired coverage rate. They also provide a full picture of the sampling uncertainty pertaining to the estimation of
\tau_{0}
.

The repro samples method stems
from
several existing artificial-sample-based inference procedures across Bayesian, frequentist and fiducial (BFF) paradigms, particularly, approximate Bayesian computing (ABC), generalized fiducial inference (GFI) and bootstrap methods, in which artificial samples are used to help quantify inference uncertainty. It also extends a key idea of the inferential model (IM)
(
Martin and Liu 2015
)
that the inference uncertainty is quantified through the auxiliary
\bm{U}
.
We view the repro samples approach as an improvement over the current existing inference methods and provide comparisons throughout the paper when the topic arises,
At the outset, we emphasize that the repro samples method side-steps many
restrictions encountered in the aforementioned BFF approaches. First, bootstrap, GFI and ABC methods rely on large sample CLT or some version of Bernstein von Mises-type theorem
(thus CLT) to justify their (frequentist) frequency performance, therefore does not guarantee Small sample frequency performance. Second, both ABC and GFI methods are approximation methods that require a predetermined threshold
\epsilon
to judge whether an artificial sample is sufficiently close to the observed sample. Their supporting theories need
\epsilon\to 0
at a fast rate as the sample size
n\to\infty
. However, when
\epsilon\to 0
at a fast rate, the sample rejection rate
in their algorithms
tends to
1
. Thus, how to choose an appropriate
\epsilon
to balance the procedures’ computational efficiency and inference validity
is an unsolved problem
(
Li and Fearnhead 2018
)
.
Finally, IM method is designed for epistemic probabilistic inference, and its complexity and reliance on Dempster-Shafer calculus make it hard to implement under complex settings. The IM method is not easily adaptable for asymptotic inference, and the restriction of using an ancillary to quantify uncertainty also limits its use in applications.
The repro samples method, developed fully following the frequentist setting, addresses or sidesteps all these issues.

The remainder of this article is arranged as follows. Section 2 develops the general framework of the repro samples method with supporting theories and associated algorithms. Sections 3 considers several important aspects that further support the general development, including connection to the
Neyman-Pearson procedure, handling of nuisance parameters, and a unique approach of getting a data-driven candidate set to improve computational efficiency. Section 4 is a case study on a normal mixture model that addresses a long standing open problem in statistical inference. Section 5 contains further remarks and discussions.

A general inference framework by repro samples

Let
(\Omega,{\cal F},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space and
\bm{U}\in{\cal U}
is a measurable
r\times 1
random vector on
\Omega
with
\{\bm{U}\leq\bm{u}\}=\{\omega\in\Omega:\bm{U}(\omega)\leq\bm{u}\}
.
Suppose
\bm{Y}
is the sample data from (
1
) and the

m\times 1
vector
\bm{Z}={\bf m}(\bm{Y})
,
m\leq n
, is a function (or a summary) of the sample data
\bm{Y}
.
A slightly more general model than (
1
) is to assume that
\bm{Z}
is directly generated from

\bm{Z}=G_{z}({\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits}_{0},\bm{U}),

(2)

where
G_{z}(\cdot,\cdot)={\bf m}(G(\cdot,\cdot))
is a given mapping from
\Theta\times{\cal U}\to{\cal Z}\subset R^{m}
.
In the special case that
{\bf m}(\cdot)
is the identity mapping function,
then
G_{z}=G
,
\bm{Z}=\bm{Y}
and model (
2
) reduces to model (
1
); thus (
1
) is a special case of (
2
).

Let
\bm{z}_{obs}
be the observed
\bm{Z}
and
\bm{u}^{rel}
be the (unknown) realization of
\bm{U}
associated with
\bm{z}_{obs}
. By (
2
), we have

\bm{z}_{obs}=G_{z}({\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits}_{0},\bm{u}^{rel}).

For any given
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits^{*}
and a simulated
\bm{u}^{*}\sim\bm{U}
, we can thereafter construct an artificial (‘fake’) copy of
\bm{z}_{obs}
:

\bm{z}^{*}=G_{z}({\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits}^{*},\bm{u}^{*}).

We call
\bm{z}^{*}
a
repro sample
of
\bm{z}_{obs}
and
({\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits}^{*},\bm{u}^{*})
a
repro copy
of
({\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits}_{0},\bm{u}^{rel})
.

The goal of Section 2.1-2.3 is to present how to use repro samples to construct a confidence set, based on the observed
\bm{z}_{obs}
, for the unknown parameter
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{0}
with a guaranteed coverage rate. In Section 2.1 we consider the case of giving the algorithmic model (
2
) where
G_{z}(\cdot,\cdot)
is completely known. Section 2.2 contains a generic algorithm
when an explicit mathematical expression of the confidence set is not available.
Section 2.3 extends the developments to a more general case in which the algorithmic model
G_{z}(\cdot,\cdot)
is not completely given.

2.1
A general formulation of a basic repro samples method

Under the algorithmic model (
2
) with a fixed
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{0}
, the sampling uncertainty of
\bm{Z}
is solely determined by the uncertainty of
\bm{U}
, whose distribution is free of the unknown model parameters
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{0}
.
Let us first focus on the easier task
of quantifying the uncertainty in
\bm{U}
.
Specifically, let
B_{\alpha}\subset{\cal U}
be a level-
\alpha
Borel set such that
\mathbb{P}\{\bm{U}\in B_{\alpha}\}\geq\alpha.

Although
\bm{u}^{rel}
is unknown, we have more than
\alpha
-level confidence that
\bm{u}^{rel}\in B_{\alpha}
. If
\bm{u}^{*}
is confined within the same set
B_{\alpha}
(i.e., requiring
\bm{u}^{*}\in B_{\alpha}
) and we
collect any potential
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits
values that can create a repro sample
\bm{z}^{*}=G_{z}(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits,\bm{u}^{*})
that matches
\bm{z}^{*}=\bm{z}_{obs}
, then it leads to a subset on
\Theta
:

\Gamma_{\alpha}(\bm{z}_{obs})=\big\{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits:\exists\,\bm{u}^{*}\in B_{\alpha}\mbox{ s.t. }\bm{z}_{obs}=G_{z}({\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits},\bm{u}^{*})\big\}\subset\bm{\Theta},

(3)

where s.t. is short for such that. In another words, for a potential value
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits
, if there exists a
\bm{u}^{*}\in B_{\alpha}
such that the repro sample
{\bm{z}}^{*}=G({\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits},{\bm{u}}^{*})
matches
\bm{z}_{obs}
with
\bm{z}^{*}=\bm{z}_{obs}
, then we keep this
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits
in the set.
Since
\bm{z}_{obs}=G_{z}({\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits}_{0},\bm{u}^{rel})
, if

\bm{u}^{rel}\in B_{\alpha}
then
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{0}\in\Gamma_{\alpha}(\bm{z}_{obs})
.
Similarly,
under the model
\bm{Z}=G_{z}({\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits}_{0},\bm{U})
, we have
\left\{\bm{U}\in B_{\alpha}\right\}\subseteq\left\{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{0}\in\Gamma_{\alpha}(\bm{Z})\right\}
. Thus, by construction,

\mathbb{P}\left\{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{0}\in\Gamma_{\alpha}(\bm{Z})\right\}\geq\mathbb{P}\big\{\bm{U}\in B_{\alpha}\big\}\geq\alpha.

The set
\Gamma_{\alpha}(\bm{z}_{obs})
constructed in (
3
)
is a
100\alpha\%
confidence set for
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{0}
. Here,
B_{\alpha}
is any single fixed set. As long as
\mathbb{P}(\bm{U}\in B_{\alpha})\geq\alpha
, the validity statement holds.

To expand the scope of this development,
the requirement
\mathbb{P}(\bm{U}\in B_{\alpha})\geq\alpha
is replaced by

\mathbb{P}\left\{T(\bm{U},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)\in B_{\alpha}(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)\right\}\geq\alpha,

(4)

for any
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits
, where
T(\cdot,\cdot)
is a mapping function from
{\cal U}\times\Theta\to

{\cal T}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{q}
, for some
q\leq n
.
Then, the constructed confidence set in (
3
) becomes

\Gamma_{\alpha}(\bm{z}_{obs})=\big\{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits:\exists\,\bm{u}^{*}\in{\cal U}\mbox{ s.t. }\bm{z}_{obs}=G_{z}({\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits},\bm{u}^{*}),\,T(\bm{u}^{*},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)\in B_{\alpha}(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)\big\}\subset\Theta.

(5)

The function
T(\cdot,\cdot)
, which we refer to as a
nuclear mapping
, adds much flexibility to our repro-sampling method, as we will show throughout the paper. For the moment, we assume that the nuclear mapping
T
is given; further discussions are provided in later sections.

From now on the set
\Gamma_{\alpha}(\bm{z}_{obs})
refers to the general expression of (
5
) instead of the special case in (
3
), unless specified otherwise.
In the construction of
\Gamma_{\alpha}(\bm{z}_{obs})
in (
5
), for any potential value
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits
, we impose two constraints: (a) The repro error
\bm{u}^{*}
is confined by the restriction
T(\bm{u}^{*},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)\in B_{\alpha}(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
; (b) The repro sample
\bm{z}^{*}(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)=G_{z}({\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits},\bm{u}^{*})
generated using
\bm{\theta}
matches the observed sample, i.e.,
\bm{z}_{obs}=\bm{z}^{*}(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
. The quantification of inference uncertainty is achieved only through constraint (a) requiring
T(\bm{u}^{*},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)\in B_{\alpha}(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
for the given
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits
,
and it does not involve the unknown true
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{0}
that generates
\bm{z}_{obs}
.
The input of the observed sample
\bm{z}_{obs}
is discernible by matching the artificial repro sample
\bm{z}^{*}(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)=G_{z}({\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits},\bm{u}^{*})
with
\bm{z}_{obs}
in constraint (b).

Although the repro samples method is developed following the frequentist principles, thus making it a completely frequentist approach, it is also closely related to several existing approaches across the Bayesian, fiducial, and frequentist (BFF) paradigms.
In Remarks 2.1 - 2.3 below, we provide discussions to connect and compare these approaches. See also Appendix II for further and more elaborated discussions.

Remark 2.1
(comparison with Neyman-Pearson testing method). Corollary
1

suggests that we can conduct hypothesis testing using the repro samples method.
Section
3.1

further investigates the relationship between the repro samples method and the classical Neyman-Pearson (N-P) hypothesis testing procedure
under a special case where a nuclear mapping
T(\bm{u},\theta)
in (
5
) is chosen through a test statistic, i.e., we set
T(\bm{u},\theta)=\widetilde{T}(\bm{z}^{\prime},\theta)
where
\bm{z}^{\prime}=G_{z}(\bm{u},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
is a copy of data
generated by the given
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits
and
\widetilde{T}(\bm{z}^{\prime},\theta)
is a test statistic of a hypothesis testing problem.
In general, the repro samples approach is broader than the classical N-P procedure.
In particular, the nuclear mapping
T(\bm{u},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
does not need to be a test statistic since it is not necessarily a function of data
\bm{z}^{\prime}.

Allowing the nuclear mapping to be a function of
\bm{u}
provides more flexibility for developing inference procedures than the conventional testing approach.
We illustrate this point using
a simple example where a single
\text{Bernoulli}(\theta)
observation
y_{obs}
is generated by
Y=I(U<\theta_{0})
,
U\sim\text{Uniform}(0,1).

To get a confidence set for
\theta_{0}
, we can let

T(u,\theta)=u
,
and define
B_{\alpha}(\theta)=\begin{cases}[1-\alpha,1]&\theta>0.5\\
[\frac{1-\alpha}{2},\frac{1+\alpha}{2}]&\theta=0.5\\
[0,\alpha]&\theta<0.5\end{cases}.

Then, following (
5
), we construct the repro samples confidence set

\Gamma_{\alpha}(y_{obs})=\begin{cases}[1-\alpha,1]&y_{obs}=1\\
[0,\alpha]&y_{obs}=0\end{cases}.

Apparently,
U
is not a function of
Y
, since it is impossible to solve for
U
in the structure equation
Y=I(U<\theta)
when given
(Y,\theta)
.
In this case,
T(U,\theta)=U
is not a test statistic.
Section
3.1
provides further discussion and additional examples comparing the repro samples methods and the classical N-P method. We show that the repro samples method can always produce an either equal or smaller confidence set than the traditional N-P method when the nuclear mapping
T(\bm{u},\theta)
is chosen through a test statistic. There are also options in which the nuclear mapping function
T(\bm{u},\theta)
is not a test statistic.

Remark 2.2
(comparison with GFI and ABC methods) In modern statistical practice, R.A. Fisher’s fiducial method is understood as an inversion method
that solves the structural equation
\bm{z}_{obs}=G_{z}({\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits},\bm{u}^{*})
for parameter
{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits}
with any
\bm{u}^{*}\sim\bm{U}

(
Brenner et al. 1983
;
Hannig et al. 2016
;
Thornton and Xie 2022
, cf.,)
. The matching equation
\bm{z}_{obs}=G_{z}({\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits},\bm{u}^{*})
in (
5
) plays a key role in the GFI development

(
Hannig et al. 2016
)
.

Hannig et al. 2016
and
Thornton and Xie 2022
have also explored the connection of GFI to the Bayesian ABC method, noting that trying to match
\bm{z}_{obs}
with
\bm{z}^{*}(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)=G_{z}({\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits},\bm{u}^{*})
is a key step in both GFI and ABC procedures. However, as stated in

Hannig et al. 2016
, an
exact matching of
\bm{z}_{obs}=\bm{z}^{*}(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
for any given
\bm{u}^{*}
is difficult and sometime even impossible. Both GFI and ABC methods adopts a tuning threshold value
\epsilon
to judge whether an artificial sample
\bm{z}^{*}=G_{z}({\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits},\bm{u}^{*})

is close to the observed
\bm{z}_{obs}
.
Unfortunately, the use of approximating threshold
\epsilon
has piratical issues.
For instance, as shown in

Li and Fearnhead 2018
,
the validity of the ABC method requires that
\epsilon\to 0
at a fast rate, but this fast rate in turn leads to inefficient sampling with a high (often 100%) rejection rate.
How to choose an appropriate
\epsilon
to balance inferential validity and computational efficiency
is still an unaddressed question in both ABC and GFI.
Our repro sample approach avoids this issue of using a threshold
\epsilon
by directly working with a set of
\bm{u}^{*}\in\{T(\bm{u}^{*},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)\in B_{\alpha}(\theta)\}
and multiple copies of
\bm{z}^{*}=G_{z}({\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits},\bm{u}^{*})
for each given
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits
.
As further explained in Appendix II
the repro samples method in effect compares
\bm{z}_{obs}
with multiple copies of
\bm{z}^{*}=G_{z}({\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits},\bm{u}^{*})
at each given
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits
and thus enabling us to use a confidence (significant) level
\alpha
to replace the arbitrary
\epsilon
.
In addition, the repro sample method can provide finite sample inference whereas GFI does not. Finally, ABC is a Bayesian procedure that not only requires additional prior assumption, but also requires a sufficient summary statistic in its algorithm.
The repro samples approach compares favorably in that it does not have these constraints.

Remark 2.3
(comparison with
IM method).
Inferential model (IM)
(
Martin and Liu 2015
)
also works
directly on
\bm{U}
to quantify inference uncertainty, but contains notable differences from the proposed repro samples method. First and foremost, the IM method exists to provide an epistemic probabilistic inference for
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{0}
, a task not considered under the current frequentest framework; cf.,
Martin and Liu 2015
. The repro samples on the other hand is a frquentist approach developed fully within the frequentist framework. Also importantly, the IM method uses
random sets to quantify the uncertainty of the auxiliary term
\bm{U}
. But the repro samples method quantifies inference uncertainty through a nuclear mapping function
T(\bm{U},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
that involves a potential parameter
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits
and an associated Borel set. Only one fixed level-
\alpha
Borel set is needed. Although IM can sometimes potentially provide some finer details in special cases (since Dempster-Shefer calculus automatically includes both upper and lower probabilities; i.e., plausible and belief functions), with additional constraints, the application of IM is more limited than repro samples method, especially under more complicated settings. Moreover, using a nuclear mapping function involving
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits
greatly extends the scope and flexibility of the repro samples method. As a special case, we are able to directly work on auxiliary
\bm{U}
by setting
T(\bm{U},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)=\bm{U}
. To the best of our knowledge, the framework of IM method cannot be easily extended to incorporate varying
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits
. Furthermore, IM development requires Dempster-Shefer calculus and asymptotic inference is not available under the development. When both apply, the IM and repro samples methods provide either the same or comparable inference conclusions.
Finally, the repro samples development
affords us the opportunity to explore the unique aspect of matching to obtain a data-driven candidate set and greatly reduce computational burden;
details are provided in Section 3.3. On the other hand, how to incorporate a data-driven candidate set with the IM method to reduce computational cost is not apparent.

2.2
A general Monte-Carlo algorithm

In Example 1, the distribution of
T({\bm{U}},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
has an explicit form, enabling us to express the level-
\alpha
confidence set
\Gamma_{\alpha}(\bm{y}_{obs})
in (
5
) explicitly. In cases when the distribution of
T({\bm{U}},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
is not explicit, we can use a Monte-Carlo method to obtain a level-
\alpha
Borel set
B_{\alpha}(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
in (
4
) and then construct the level-
\alpha
confidence set
\Gamma_{\alpha}(\bm{y}_{obs})
in (
5
) as stated in Algorithm 1 below:

Algorithm 1

Monte-Carlo algorithm (general case)

1:

Simulate many copies of
{\bm{u}^{s}}\sim\bm{U}
; the collection of
{\bm{u}^{s}}
forms a set
{\cal V}
.

2:

For each given (grid) value of
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits\in\Theta
,

(a) Compute
\{T({\bm{u}^{s}},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits),{\bm{u}^{s}}\in{\cal V}\}
, from which obtain an empirical distribution of
T(\bm{U},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
. Based on the empirical distribution, we get a level-
\alpha
Borel set
B_{\alpha}(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
.

(b) Check whether there exists a
\bm{u}^{*}\in{\cal U}
such that

{\bm{z}_{obs}}=G_{z}({\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits},\bm{u}^{*})

and
T(\bm{u}^{*},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)\in B_{\alpha}(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
. If both of the above criteria are satisfied, keep the
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits
.

3:

Collect all kept
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits
to form the set
\Gamma_{\alpha}(\bm{y}_{obs})
.

This general algorithm,
grid search algorithm, searches the space of
\Theta
and is
used only when an explicit expression of
T(\bm{U},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
’s distribution
is not available.
Our remarks on the implementation of the algorithm are as follows.

Remark 2.4

To obtain the level-
\alpha
Borel set
B_{\alpha}({\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits})
for a given
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits
in Step 2(a), w often need to compute the (empirical) distribution of
T(\bm{U},{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits})
based on the set of Monte-Carlo points
{{\cal S}_{\theta}}=\{T(\bm{u}^{s},{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits})
,
\bm{u}^{s}\in{\cal V}\}
. Then according to the empirical distribution, we can always numerically calculate
B_{\alpha}({\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits})
. If
T(\bm{u},{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits})
is a scalar, we just directly compute its empirical distribution
F_{\cal V}(t)=

\sum_{\bm{u}^{s}\in{\cal V}}I\big(T(\bm{u}^{s},{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits})\leq t\big)\big/|{\cal V}|
and use the (upper/lower) quantiles of
F_{\cal V}(t)
to obtain a level-
\alpha
interval as the Borel set
B_{\alpha}({\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits})
. When
T(\bm{u},{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits})
is a vector,
say in
{\cal T}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{q}
,
we can use data depth
and follow
Liu et al. 2021

to construct
the Borel set
B_{\alpha}({\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits})
. Specifically, we let
D_{{\cal S}_{\theta}}(\bm{t})
be the empirical depth function for any
\bm{t}\in\cal T
that is computed based on the Monte-Carlo points in
{\cal S}_{\theta}=\{T(\bm{u},{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits})
,
\bm{u}\in{\cal V}\}
and
F_{{\cal V}|D}(t)=

\sum_{\bm{u}^{s}\in{\cal V}}I\big(D_{{\cal S}_{\theta}}(T(\bm{u}^{s},{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits}))\leq t\big)\big/|{\cal V}|
be the empirical CDF of
D_{{\cal S}_{\theta}}(T(\bm{u}^{s},{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits})).

Then, following Lemma 1 (a) of
Liu et al. 2021
, the level-
\alpha
(empirical) central region

B_{\alpha}({\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits})=\{\bm{t}:F_{{\cal V}|D}(D_{{\cal S}_{\theta}}(\bm{t}))\geq 1-\alpha\}
is a level-
\alpha
Borel set on
{\cal T}
. See also Example 2 (B) of Section 2.3 for a numerical example, where the empirical depth function
D_{{\cal S}_{\theta}}(\cdot)
is computed by R package
ddalpha

(
Pokotylo et al. 2019
)
.

Remark 2.5
The algorithm needs to calculate the empirical distribution of
T(\bm{U},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
for each
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits
.
To improve the computing efficiency (at the cost of potentially losing the flexibility of the repro samples method), we can sometimes choose a nuclear mapping function
T(\bm{U},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
whose distribution of is free (or approximately free) of
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits
.
In this case,
B_{\alpha}(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
in (
4
) is free (or approximately free) of
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits
with
B_{\alpha}(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)\equiv B_{\alpha}
.
A benefit of using this type of nuclear mapping function
is that we can modify Algorithm
1
to save computing time:
in step 2(a), we only need to obtain
B_{\alpha}(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
for a single value of
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits
instead of for each grid value of
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits\in\Theta.

Remark 2.6

Later in Section 3.3, we will discuss the technique of using a data-driven candidate set to significantly reduce the search space. From the outset, we would like to comment that
the search algorithm works in most cases that one commonly encounter.
In many statistical analysis, for instance the commonly used likelihood-based inference, the regular conditions on the parameter space include that
\Theta
is a continuous space and the test statistic is also continuous (and often differentiable) in its inner space
\Theta^{o}
. Similarly,
if the nuclear mapping function
T(y_{obs},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
is continuous in
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits
and
\Theta
is a continuous space, one can in principle use a grid search method to carry out an analysis in this situation. What is new in the repro samples development is that
\Theta
can either be a discrete space or a set of non-numerical subjects. In this case, even if
\Theta
is (countable) infinite, we provide in Section 3.3 a way to take advantage of a many-to-one mapping structure inherited in the repro samples procedure to create a finite candidate set and carry out an efficient grid search method. See Section 3.3 for further details.

2.3
A further extension to more general cases

In this section, we further extend the framework developed above to settings where the matching equation (
2
) of the algorithmic model is not available. This includes some nonparametric inference problems that are not covered by the existing ABC or GFI methods.
Consider the example of making inference for a population quantile of a completely unknown distribution
F
, say,
\theta_{0}=F^{-1}(\zeta)
, the
\zeta
-th quantile parameter of
F
.
Since
I(Y<\theta_{0})\sim\text{Bernoulli}(\zeta)
for
Y\sim F
, we have equation

I(Y<\theta_{0})=U
, where
U\sim\text{Bernoulli}(\zeta)
. Now suppose we observe data
\bm{y}_{obs}=(y_{1}\ldots,y_{n})^{\top}
, for a fixed
n
, then the equation becomes

\sum_{i=1}^{n}I(Y_{i}-\theta_{0}<0)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}U_{i}

(8)

where
Y_{i}\overset{iid}{\sim}F
and
U_{i}\overset{iid}{\sim}\text{Bernoulli}(\zeta)
.
The corresponding realized version is

\sum_{i=1}^{n}I(y_{i}-\theta_{0}<0)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}^{rel}
. Equation (
8
) can not be expressed in the form of either (
1
) or (
2
). In fact, one cannot generate a repro sample
\bm{y}^{*}
based on (
8
) that is directly comparable to the observed sample
\bm{y}_{obs}
. However, we can still use a repo sampling approach to provide an exact inference for such a nonparametrc inference problem.

In particular, we
consider the following generalized data generating equation:

g(\bm{Z},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{0},\bm{U})=\bm{0},

(9)

where
g
is a given mapping function from
{\cal Z}\times\Theta\times{\cal U}\to\mathbb{R}^{s}
and
\bm{Z}={\bf m}(\bm{Y})
is a
m\times 1
vector as defined at the start of Section 2.
The corresponding realization version is

g(\bm{z}_{obs},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{0},\bm{u}^{rel})=\bm{0}
.
Equation (
8
)
is a special case of (
9
) with
g(\bm{Y},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{0},\bm{U})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}I(Y_{i}-\theta_{0}<0)-\sum_{i=1}^{n}U_{i}
.

The level-
\alpha
confidence set in (
5
) is modified to be

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\alpha}(\bm{z}_{obs})=\big\{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits:\exists\,\bm{u}^{*}\in{\cal U}\mbox{ s.t. }g(\bm{z}_{obs},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits,\bm{u}^{*})=0,T(\bm{u}^{*},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)\in B_{\alpha}(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)\big\}\subset\Theta.

(10)

We have the following theorem for the set
\Gamma_{\alpha}(\bm{z}_{obs})
defined in (
10
). The proof is similar to that of Theorem
1
and is briefly described in Appendix I.

Theorem 2
.

Assume the generalized model equation (
9
) holds. If the inequality (
4
) holds exactly, then for
\Gamma_{\alpha}(\bm{z}_{obs})
defined in (
10
) the following inequality holds exactly,

\mathbb{P}\left\{{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits}_{0}\in\Gamma_{\alpha}(\bm{Z})\right\}\geq\alpha\quad\hbox{for $0<\alpha<1$.}

(11)

Furthermore, if (
4
) holds approximately
with
\mathbb{P}\left\{T(\bm{U},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)\in B_{\alpha}(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)\right\}\geq\alpha\{1+o(\delta^{{}^{\prime}})\}
, then (
11
) holds approximately
with
\mathbb{P}\left\{{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits}_{0}\in\Gamma_{\alpha}(\bm{Z})\right\}\geq\alpha\{1+o(\delta^{{}^{\prime}})\}
, for
0<\alpha<1
, where
\delta^{{}^{\prime}}>0
is
a small value
that may
depend on sample size
n
.

As discussed in Sections 2.2,
when the distribution of
T(\bm{u}^{*},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
cannot be explicitly expressed, we may use a Monte-Carlo algorithm to carry out the construction of
\Gamma_{\alpha}(\bm{y}_{obs})
. Specifically, we may still use Algorithm
1
to get
\Gamma_{\alpha}(\bm{y}_{obs})
but replace the matching equation
{\bm{z}_{obs}}=G_{z}({\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits},\bm{u}^{*})
in the algorithm with
g(\bm{z}_{obs},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits,\bm{u}^{*})=0
.

The example below provides an illustration of this extended framework. Part (A) is on nonparametric quantile inference and part (B) is on
censored quantile regression where making a finite-sample (semi-parametric) inference for model parameters is new and previously unavailable in the literature. Both parts demonstrate that
the repro samples method performs well and is compared favorably to the corresponding large sample bootstrap methods.

Example 2

(Nonparametric and semi-parametric inference of quantiles)
.

(A)
Assume
\bm{y}_{obs}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})^{\top}
are from an unknown distribution
F
. We are interested in making inference about the population quantile
\theta_{0}=F^{-1}(\zeta)
, for a given
0<\zeta<1
.
Write

g(\bm{Y},\theta,\bm{U})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}I(Y_{i}\leq\theta)-\sum_{i=1}^{n}U_{i}
, where
Y_{i}\sim F
and
U_{i}\sim\text{Bernoulli}(\zeta)
.
We define
T(\bm{U},\theta)=T(\bm{U})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}U_{i}
. It follows that
T(\bm{U})\sim\text{Binomial}(n,\zeta)
and
B_{\alpha}=[a_{L}(\zeta),a_{U}(\zeta)]
, where

3
Test statistic, nuisance parameters and candidate set

For any nuclear mapping function
T
, as long as we have a set
B_{\alpha}(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
such that (
4
) holds,
the set
\Gamma_{\alpha}(\bm{z}_{obs})
constructed accordingly is a level-
\alpha
confidence set for
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits
. In other words, it is possible that one might have multiple choices of
T
that all lead to valid (and perhaps different) confidence sets.
Therefore, the role of
T
under the repro samples framework is similar to that of a test statistic under the classical (Neyman-Pearson) hypothesis testing framework.
Accordingly, a good choice for
T
is problem specific and we do not have a unique solution to all situations.
In this section, we investigate the choice of the nuclear mapping function
T
in two specific yet common settings.
Specifically, in Section 3.1, we consider the special case of using a test statistic, including
an (approximate) pivot quantity such as a likelihood ratio test statistic, as a nuclear mapping function. We provide comparisons to Neyman-Pearson testing methods.
In Section 3.2, we consider
the situation of existing nuisance parameters and provide a method to define a nuclear mapping function while minimizing the impact of the nuisance parameters.

To improve the efficiency of
computing Algorithms
1
,
we also discuss the use of a pre-screening candidate set in Section 3.3.
We pay special attention to
discrete target parameters, a unique and also important development under the repro samples framework.

3.1
Define a nuclear mapping through a test statistic

In a classical testing problem
H_{0}:\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{0}=\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits
versus
H_{1}:\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{0}\not=\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits
, one often constructs a test statistic, say
\widetilde{T}(\bm{z}_{obs},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
, and assume that we know the distribution of
\widetilde{T}(\bm{Z}^{\prime},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
for
\bm{Z}^{\prime}=G_{z}(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits,\bm{U})
generated under
H_{0}:\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{0}=\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits
.
Under the Neyman-Pearson framework, we can derive
a level-
\alpha
acceptance region
A_{\alpha}(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)=\big\{\bm{z}_{obs}\big|\widetilde{T}(\bm{z}_{obs},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)\in B_{\alpha}(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)\big\}
, where the set
B_{\alpha}(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
satisfies
\mathbb{P}\big\{\widetilde{T}(\bm{Z}^{\prime},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)\in B_{\alpha}(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)\big\}\geq\alpha
. By the property of test duality, a level-
\alpha
confidence set is

\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\alpha}(\bm{z}_{obs})=\left\{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits:\bm{z}_{obs}\in A_{\alpha}(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)\right\}=\left\{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits:\widetilde{T}(\bm{z}_{obs},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)\in B_{\alpha}(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)\right\}.

Suppose the nuclear mapping function
T(\bm{u},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
is defined through this test statistic:

T(\bm{U},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)=\widetilde{T}(\bm{Z}^{\prime},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)=\widetilde{T}(G_{z}({\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits},\bm{U}),\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits),

(12)

where
\bm{Z}^{\prime}=G_{z}(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits,\bm{U})
. Since
\mathbb{P}\left\{T(\bm{U},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)\in B_{\alpha}(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)\right\}=\mathbb{P}\big\{\widetilde{T}(\bm{Z}^{\prime},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)\in B_{\alpha}(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)\big\}\geq\alpha
for the same
B_{\alpha}(\cdot)
, a level-
\alpha
confidence set of (
5
) by the repro samples method is

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\alpha}(\bm{z}_{obs})=\left\{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits:\exists\bm{u}^{*}\in{\cal U}\mbox{ s.t. }\bm{z}_{obs}=G_{z}({\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits},\bm{u}^{*}),\widetilde{T}(G_{z}({\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits},\bm{u}^{*}),\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)\in B_{\alpha}(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)\right\}.

We have the following lemma suggesting
\Gamma_{\alpha}(\bm{z}_{obs})\subseteq\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\alpha}(\bm{z}_{obs})
. See Appendix I for a proof.

Lemma 1
.

If the nuclear mapping function is defined through a test statistic as in (
12
), then we have
\Gamma_{\alpha}(\bm{z}_{obs})\subseteq\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\alpha}(\bm{z}_{obs})
. The two sets are equal

\Gamma_{\alpha}(\bm{z}_{obs})=\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\alpha}(\bm{z}_{obs})
, when
\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\alpha}(\bm{z}_{obs})\subseteq\big\{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits:\bm{z}_{obs}=G_{z}({\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits},\bm{u}^{*}),\exists\,\bm{u}^{*}\in{\cal U}\big\}
.

It is possible that
\Gamma_{\alpha}(\bm{z}_{obs})
is strictly smaller than

\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\alpha}(\bm{z}_{obs})
with
\Gamma_{\alpha}(\bm{z}_{obs})\subsetneq\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\alpha}(\bm{z}_{obs})
. The following is an example in which
\Gamma_{\alpha}(\bm{z}_{obs})
is strictly smaller than

\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\alpha}(\bm{z}_{obs})
.

Example 3
.

Suppose
\bm{y}_{obs}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})^{\top}
is a set of sample from the model
Y_{i}=\theta_{0}+U_{i}
,
U_{i}\sim U(-1,1)
,
i=1,\ldots,n
. Here,
\Theta=(-\infty,+\infty)
,
{\cal Y}=(-\infty,\infty)
.
The point estimator
\bar{Y}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}
is unbiased (and also
\sqrt{n}
-consistent). Let’s
use the test statistic
\widetilde{T}(\bm{y}_{obs},\theta)=\bar{y}-\theta
. Since
n\{(\bar{Y}-\theta)+1\}/2=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{U_{i}+1}{2}
follows a Irwin-Hall distribution when
\theta
is the true value
\theta_{0}

by the classical testing method a level-

\widetilde{\Gamma}_{.95}(\bm{y}_{obs})=(\bar{y}-\frac{2}{n}q_{.975}+1,\bar{y}+\frac{2}{n}q_{.975}-1)
. Here,

is the

quantile of the Irwin-Hall distribution.
If we use the repro samples method, our confidence set is then

\Gamma_{.95}(\bm{y}_{obs})=\big\{\theta:y_{i}=\theta+u_{i}^{*},i=1,\ldots,n;\,\bar{y}\in(\theta-\frac{2}{n}q_{.975}+1,\theta+\frac{2}{n}q_{.975}-1);\exists\bm{u}^{*}\in(-1,1)^{n}\big\}=\left\{\theta:\theta\in\cap_{i=1}^{n}(y_{i}-1,y_{i}+1)\right\}\cap\widetilde{\Gamma}_{.95}(\bm{y}_{obs})
. Since
the constraint
\{\theta\in\cap_{i=1}^{n}(y_{i}-1,y_{i}+1)\}
is non-trivial,
the strictly smaller statement
\Gamma(\bm{y}_{obs})\subsetneq\widetilde{\Gamma}(\bm{y}_{obs})
holds for non-trivially many realizations of
\bm{y}_{obs}
.

As an illustration, consider the case
\theta=0
and
n=3
, in which
q_{.975}=2.4687
. Suppose we have a sample realization
\bm{y}_{obs}=(-0.430,0.049,0.371)^{\top}
. By a direct calculation,

\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\alpha}(\bm{z}_{obs})=(-.6458,.6458)
and
\Gamma_{\alpha}(\bm{y}_{obs})=\big\{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits:y_{obs,i}=\theta+u_{i}^{*},i=1,2,3;\,\exists(u_{1}^{*},u_{2}^{*},u_{3}^{*})\in(-1,1)\times(-1,1)\times(-1,1)\big\}\cap\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\alpha}(\bm{y}_{obs})=\big\{(-1.430,.570)\cap(-.951,1.049)\cap(-.629,1.371)\big\}\cap(-.6458,.6458)=(-.629,.570)
. Clearly,
\Gamma_{\alpha}(\bm{z}_{obs})\subsetneq\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\alpha}(\bm{z}_{obs})
.
We repeated the experiment
1,000
times. The coverage rates of
\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\alpha}(\bm{y}_{obs})
and
\Gamma_{\alpha}(\bm{y}_{obs})
are both
95.1\%
. Among the
1,000

219
times

\Gamma_{\alpha}(\bm{y}_{obs})=\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\alpha}(\bm{y}_{obs})
and

781
times
\Gamma_{\alpha}(\bm{y}_{obs})\subsetneq\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\alpha}(\bm{y}_{obs})
. The average lengths of
\Gamma_{\alpha}(\bm{y}_{obs})
and
\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\alpha}(\bm{y}_{obs})

As mentioned in Remark 2.2, a nuclear mapping function does not need to be a test statistic.
However, if it is defined through a test statistics by (
12
), there are some immediate implications from Lemma 1.
First, the confidence set by the repro samples method is either smaller or the same as the one obtained using the test statistic by the classical testing approach. So the set
\Gamma(\bm{z}_{obs})
by the repro samples method is more desirable. Second, if the test statistic is optimal (in the sense that it leads to a powerful test), then the confidence set constructed by the corresponding nuclear mapping function is also optimal. In particular, we have the following corollary.
Here, a level-
\alpha

uniformly most accurate
(UMA) confidence set (also called
Neyman shortest
) refers to a level-
\alpha
confidence set that minimizes the probability of false coverage (i.e., probability of covering a wrong parameter value) over a class of level-
\alpha
confidence sets; cf.,
Casella and Berger 1990
. See Appendix I for a proof of the corollary.

Corollary 2
.

(a) If the test statistic
\widetilde{T}(\bm{z}_{obs},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
corresponds to the uniformly most powerful test and
\widetilde{\Gamma}(\bm{z}_{obs})
is a level-
\alpha
UMA confidence set, then the set
\Gamma(\bm{z}_{obs})
by the corresponding repro sample method is also a level-
\alpha
UMA confidence set.

(b) If the test statistic
\widetilde{T}(\bm{z}_{obs},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
corresponds to the uniformly most powerful unbiased test and
\widetilde{\Gamma}(\bm{z}_{obs})
is a level-
\alpha
UMA unbiased confidence set, then the confidence set
\Gamma(\bm{z}_{obs})
by the corresponding repro sample method is also a level-
\alpha
UMA unbiased confidence set.

Finally, we can define the nuclear mapping through a pivot or an approximately pivot quantity, which is often used as a test statistic. The probability distribution of a pivot quantity does not depend on the unknown parameters. Hence it is convenient to define the nuclear mapping function through a pivot or an approximately pivot quantity, i.e., set
T(\bm{u},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)=\widetilde{T}(\bm{z}^{\prime},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
,
where
\widetilde{T}(\bm{z}^{\prime},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
is
a
pivot quantity
whose distribution satisfies
\mathbb{P}\{\widetilde{T}(\bm{Z}^{\prime},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)\in B\}=\int_{{\bf t}\in B}f({\bf t})d{\bf t}

or an
approximately pivot quantity
whose distribution satisfies

\mathbb{P}\{\widetilde{T}(\bm{Z}^{\prime},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)\in B\}=\int_{{\bf t}\in B}f({\bf t})d{\bf t}\,\{1+o(\delta^{{}^{\prime}})\}.
Here,
f({\bf t})
is a density function that is free of the unknown parameter
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits
and
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is any Borel set, and
\delta^{{}^{\prime}}>0
is
a small number as defined in Theorems
1
and
2
.
In this case,
we have simplified in (
4
) that the set
B_{\alpha}(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)\equiv B_{\alpha}
is free or approximately free of
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits
.

Let’s consider the example of a likelihood ratio test (LRT) statistic, which is often regarded as the most commonly used asymptotic pivot quantity;

confidence set is
\Gamma_{\alpha}(\bm{y}_{obs})=\{\theta:y_{(n)}-1<\theta<y_{(1)}+1;

y_{(1)}-\theta<-c_{\alpha},y_{(n)}-\theta>c_{\alpha}\}=\big(\max\big\{y_{(1)}+c_{\alpha},y_{(n)}-1\big\},\min\big\{y_{(n)}-c_{\alpha},y_{(1)}+1\big\}\big)
. We have conducted a numerical study with true
\theta_{0}=0
and
n=5,20,200
, respectively. In all cases, the coverage rates of both confidence intervals are right on target around
95\%
in 3000 repetitions.
The intervals by the repro sample method are consistently shorter than those obtained using the LRT method across all sample sizes, as evidenced in Figure
2
. Although a LRT is uniformly most powerful for a simple-versus-simple test by the Neyman-Pearson Lemma, it is not the case for the two-sided test in this example. we are able to explore and use the repro samples method to obtain a better confidence interval.

3.2
Define a nuclear mapping and control nuisance parameters

Frequently researchers are
interested in making
inference only for a certain parameter, say
\eta
, and the remaining parameters
\mathop{\xi\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\xi$}}\nolimits
in
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits=(\eta,\mathop{\xi\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\xi$}}\nolimits^{\top})^{\top}
are considered nuisance parameters.
There is a large number of literature on this topic where
profiling and conditional inference remain to be two major techniques that handle nuisance parameters
(
Cox and Reid 1987
;
Davison 2003
, and references therein)
.
These techniques typically
lead to test statistics, say
\widetilde{T}(\bm{z},\eta)
, that do not involve the nuisance parameter
\mathop{\xi\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\xi$}}\nolimits
in expressions.
Often times however the distributions of these test statistics
\widetilde{T}(\bm{z},\eta)=\widetilde{T}(G_{z}((\eta,\mathop{\xi\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\xi$}}\nolimits^{\top})^{\top},\bm{u}),\eta)
still depend on the unknown nuisance parameter
\mathop{\xi\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\xi$}}\nolimits
and thus
not readily available. To obtain the distributions of
\widetilde{T}(\bm{z},\eta)
, researchers often invoke large samples
and assume that there exists a consistent plug-in estimator of the nuisance parameter; e.g.,
Reid and Cox 2015
;
Chuang and Lai 2000
.
In this subsection, however, we use a similar profiling idea to provide an alternative approach. The advantage of our approach is that it can give us a confidence set with a guaranteed finite-sample coverage rate.

Suppose
T_{a}(\bm{u},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
is a mapping function from
{\cal U}\times\Theta\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
For instance, we can often make

T_{a}(\bm{u},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)=\widetilde{T}(\bm{z},\eta)
, a test statistic mentioned above using either a profile or a conditional approach. Or, more generally
T_{a}(\bm{u},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
can be any nuclear mapping function
T(\bm{u},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
used to make a joint inference about
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits=(\eta,\mathop{\xi\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\xi$}}\nolimits^{\top})^{\top}
. Suppose we have an artificial data point
\bm{z}^{\prime}=G_{z}(\bm{u},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
and let
\Gamma_{\alpha^{\prime}}(\bm{z}^{\prime})
be a level-
\alpha^{\prime}
confidence set for
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits
, where
\Gamma_{\alpha^{\prime}}(\bm{z})

has the expression of (
5
) or (
10
) but with

\bm{z}_{obs}
replaced by the artificial data
\bm{z}^{\prime}=G_{z}(\bm{u},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
and the nuclear mapping function replaced by
T_{a}(\bm{u},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
. Now, beside
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits=(\eta,\mathop{\xi\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\xi$}}\nolimits^{\top})^{\top}
, let us consider another copy of parameter vector
\widetilde{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}}\nolimits=(\eta,\widetilde{\mathop{\xi\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\xi$}}}\nolimits^{\top})^{\top}\in\Theta
that has the same
\eta
but perhaps a different
\widetilde{\mathop{\xi\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\xi$}}}\nolimits\not=\mathop{\xi\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\xi$}}\nolimits
. To explore the impact of a potentially wrong nuisance parameter
\widetilde{\mathop{\xi\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\xi$}}}\nolimits\not=\mathop{\xi\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\xi$}}\nolimits
, we define

\nu(\bm{u};\eta,\mathop{\xi\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\xi$}}\nolimits,\widetilde{\mathop{\xi\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\xi$}}}\nolimits)=\inf_{\alpha^{\prime}}\{\alpha^{\prime}:\widetilde{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}}\nolimits\in\Gamma_{\alpha^{\prime}}(\bm{z})\}=\inf_{\alpha^{\prime}}\{\alpha^{\prime}:\widetilde{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}}\nolimits\in\Gamma_{\alpha^{\prime}}(G_{z}(\bm{u},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits))\}.

(13)

From
Corollary
1
, we can interpret
1-\nu(\bm{u};\eta,\mathop{\xi\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\xi$}}\nolimits,\widetilde{\mathop{\xi\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\xi$}}}\nolimits)
as a ‘
p
-value’ when we test the null hypothesis that the data
\bm{z}
is from
\widetilde{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}}\nolimits=(\eta,\widetilde{\mathop{\xi\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\xi$}}}\nolimits^{\top})^{\top}
but in fact
\bm{z}=G_{z}(\bm{u},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
with
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits=(\eta,\mathop{\xi\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\xi$}}\nolimits^{\top})^{\top}
.
Our proposed
profile nuclear mapping function

for the target parameter
\eta
is defined as

\displaystyle T^{P}(\bm{u},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)=\min_{\widetilde{\xi}}\,\,\nu(\bm{u};\eta,\mathop{\xi\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\xi$}}\nolimits,\widetilde{\mathop{\xi\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\xi$}}}\nolimits).

(14)

Although
T^{P}(\bm{u},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
still depends on
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits=(\eta,\mathop{\xi\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\xi$}}\nolimits^{\top})^{\top}
, including the unknown nuisance parameter
\mathop{\xi\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\xi$}}\nolimits
, it
is dominated by a
U(0,1)
random variable as stated in the lemma below. See Appendix I for a proof of the lemma.

Lemma 2
.

Suppose
T^{P}(\bm{u},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
is a profile nuclear mapping function from
{\cal U}\times\Theta\to[0,1]
defined above. Then, we have
\mathbb{P}\left\{T^{P}(\bm{U},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)\leq\alpha\right\}\geq\alpha.

Based on the above lemma and following the construction outlined in Section 1, we define

\displaystyle\Xi_{\alpha}(\bm{z}_{obs})=\bigg\{\eta:\exists\,\bm{u}^{*}\in{\cal U}\,\text{and}\,\mathop{\xi\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\xi$}}\nolimits,

\displaystyle\qquad\text{ s.t. }\begin{pmatrix}\eta\\
\mathop{\xi\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\xi$}}\nolimits\end{pmatrix}\in\Theta,\bm{z}_{obs}=G_{z}\left(\begin{pmatrix}\eta\\
\mathop{\xi\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\xi$}}\nolimits\end{pmatrix},\bm{u}^{*}\right),\,T^{P}\left(\bm{u}^{*},\begin{pmatrix}\eta\\
\mathop{\xi\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\xi$}}\nolimits\end{pmatrix}\right)\leq\alpha\bigg\}.

(15)

The next theorem states that
\Xi_{\alpha}(\bm{z}_{obs})

is a level-
\alpha
confidence set of the target parameter
\eta
. A proof is provided in Appendix.

Theorem 3
.

Suppose that
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{0}=(\eta_{0},\mathop{\xi\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\xi$}}\nolimits_{0}^{\top})^{\top}
is the true parameter with
\bm{Z}=G_{z}(\bm{U},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{0})
and
\Xi_{\alpha}(\cdot)
is defined in (
3.2
), we have
\mathbb{P}\left\{\eta_{0}\in\Xi_{\alpha}(\bm{Z})\right\}\geq\alpha
.

In cases when the distribution of
T_{a}(\bm{U},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
is known, the function
\nu(\cdot;\eta,\mathop{\xi\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\xi$}}\nolimits,\widetilde{\mathop{\xi\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\xi$}}}\nolimits)
in (
13
) and the nuclear mapping function
T^{P}(\bm{u},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
have explicit formulas.
When the distribution of
T_{a}(\bm{U},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
is not available, we can use a Monte-Carlo method to get
\nu(\cdot;\eta,\mathop{\xi\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\xi$}}\nolimits,\widetilde{\mathop{\xi\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\xi$}}}\nolimits)
for each given
(\eta,\mathop{\xi\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\xi$}}\nolimits,\widetilde{\mathop{\xi\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\xi$}}}\nolimits)
as described in the lemma below. See Appendix I for a proof.

Lemma 3
.

Let
D_{{\cal S}_{\theta}}(\bm{t})
be the empirical depth function that is computed based on the Monte-Carlo points in
{\cal S}_{\theta}
, where

{{\cal S}_{\theta}}=\{T_{a}(\bm{u}^{s},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits),\bm{u}^{s}\in{\cal V}\}
is the Monte-Carlo set defined in Algorithm 1 for a given
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits=(\eta,\mathop{\xi\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\xi$}}\nolimits^{\top})^{\top}
. Let
F_{\mathcal{V}|D}(s)=\frac{1}{|{\cal V}|}\sum_{\bm{u}^{s}\in{\cal V}}I\big\{D_{{\cal S}_{\theta}}\big(T_{a}(\bm{u}^{s},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)\big)\leq s\big\}
be the empirical CDF of
D_{{\cal S}_{\theta}}\big(T_{a}(\bm{u}^{s},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)\big),
then

\nu(\bm{u};\eta,\mathop{\xi\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\xi$}}\nolimits,\widetilde{\mathop{\xi\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\xi$}}}\nolimits)=\inf_{\{\bm{u}^{*}:G_{z}(\bm{u}^{*},\tilde{\theta})=G_{z}(\bm{u},\tilde{\theta})\}}\left\{1-F_{\mathcal{V}|D}\left(D_{{\cal S}_{\tilde{\theta}}}\big(T_{a}(\bm{u}^{*},\tilde{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}}\nolimits)\big)\right)\right\},

where
\tilde{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}}\nolimits=(\eta,\widetilde{\mathop{\xi\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\xi$}}}\nolimits^{\top})^{\top}.

Using Lemma
3
, we can evaluate
T^{P}(\bm{u},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
via (
14
), and then obtain the confidence set in (
3.2
). Note that, for any given
(\bm{u},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
, we can express
\nu(\bm{u};\eta,\mathop{\xi\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\xi$}}\nolimits,\widetilde{\mathop{\xi\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\xi$}}}\nolimits)
as a function of
\widetilde{\mathop{\xi\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\xi$}}}\nolimits
only, so we can use a build-in optimization function in R (or another computing package) to obtain
T^{P}(\bm{u},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
in (
14
).

3.3
Finding candidate set for target parameter

Sometimes when an explicit expression of the confidence set
\Gamma_{\alpha}(\bm{z}_{obs})
is not available,
one can use Algorithm 1 to search through the parameter space
\Theta
and construct
\Gamma_{\alpha}(\bm{z}_{obs})
. Finding a suitable candidate set to limit the search can greatly improve the computing efficiency of the algorithm. Here, a candidate set, say
\widehat{\Theta}=\widehat{\Theta}(\bm{z}_{obs})
refers to a subset of the parameter space
\Theta

that is pre-screened using the observed data
\bm{z}_{obs}
.
In this section, we first provide a general result of the impact of using a data-dependent candidate set on the coverage. We then discuss the special case of a discrete target parameter in which we can take advantage of a many-to-one mapping that is unique in a repro samples setup
to create an effective pre-screen candidate set to significantly reduce our computing cost.

The candidate set
\widehat{\Theta}=\widehat{\Theta}(\bm{z}_{obs})
may be obtained using different
pre-screening methods.
The key is that we need to overcome the issue of a “double usage” of the data but still ensure an at least approximate coverage of the final output confidence set

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\alpha}^{\prime}(\bm{z}_{obs})=\widehat{\Theta}(\bm{z}_{obs})\cap\Gamma_{\alpha}(\bm{z}_{obs})

\displaystyle\qquad=\big\{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits:\exists\,\bm{u}^{*}\in{\cal U},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits\in\widehat{\Theta},\,\hbox{s.t.}\,\bm{z}_{obs}=G_{z}({\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits},\bm{u}^{*}),\,T(\bm{u}^{*},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)\in B_{\alpha}(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)\big\},

(16)

we impose a condition that

\mathbb{P}\big\{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{0}\in\widehat{\Theta}(\bm{Z})\big\}=1-o(\delta^{\prime}),

(17)

where
\delta^{\prime}>0
is a small number as described in Theorem
1
. Here, the “double usage” refers to the fact that we use the observed data in constructing both the candidate set
\widehat{\Theta}(\bm{z}_{obs})
and confidence set
\Gamma_{\alpha}(\bm{z}_{obs})
.
The corollary below contains a result showing that the output set
\Gamma_{\alpha}^{\prime}(\bm{z}_{obs})

is still an (approximate) level-
\alpha
confidence set.
See Appendix I for a proof of the corollary.

Corollary 3
.

Let
\bm{Z}=G_{z}(\bm{U},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{0})
and
\widehat{\Theta}=\widehat{\Theta}(\bm{Z})
is a candidate set that satisfies (
17
).
Then, we have

\mathbb{P}\big\{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{0}\in\Gamma_{\alpha}^{\prime}(\bm{Z})\big\}\geq\alpha-o(\delta^{\prime}).

When the parameter space
\Theta
is continuous and the mapping function
T(\bm{u},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
is continuous in
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits
, we may use a grid search method to cover the entire
\Theta
, as discussed in Remark 2.6. A data-dependent candidate set
\widehat{\Theta}

can help further narrow down to the grid search; cf., Example
Example 2 (Continuation)
and also
Michael et al. 2019
.

An intriguing yet unique situation in the repro samples development is when the parameter space
\Theta
is discrete.
We can take advantage of a “many-to-one” mapping that often exists in the setup to get a data-dependent candidate set
\widehat{\Theta}
that is much smaller than
\Theta
.
We describe the above key idea and a
generic approach below. Section 4 includes a case study with with greater details to provide a specific illustration on how to obtain this kind of candidate set
\widehat{\Theta}.

For convenience of discussion, we rewrite (
2
) as
\bm{Z}=G_{z}(\kappa_{0},\mathop{\xi\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\xi$}}\nolimits_{0},\bm{U})
, where
\kappa_{0}
is the true value of a discrete parameter with a parameter space
{\it\Upsilon}
and, if applicable,
\mathop{\xi\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\xi$}}\nolimits_{0}
are the values of other (nuisance) parameters. Write
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits=(\kappa,\mathop{\xi\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\xi$}}\nolimits^{\top})^{\top}
.
Our goal is to find a candidate set
\widehat{\it\Upsilon}=\widehat{\it\Upsilon}(\bm{z}_{obs})
such that
\widehat{\it\Upsilon}(\bm{z}_{obs})
is significantly smaller than
{\it\Upsilon}
and
\widehat{\it\Upsilon}
satisfies (
17
).

Let us first examine a “many-to-one” mapping often inherited in a repro sample setup involving a discrete parameter
\kappa
. In particular, based on the equation
\bm{z}_{obs}=G_{z}(\kappa_{0},\mathop{\xi\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\xi$}}\nolimits_{0},\bm{u}^{rel}),
we can rewrite
\kappa_{0}
as a solution of an optimization problem

\kappa_{0}=\arg\min\limits_{\kappa}\min\limits_{\xi}\|\bm{z}_{obs}-G_{z}(\kappa,\mathop{\xi\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\xi$}}\nolimits,\bm{u}^{rel})\|
or, more generally,

\kappa_{0}=\arg\min\limits_{\kappa}\min\limits_{\xi}L\big(\bm{z}_{obs},G_{z}(\kappa,\mathop{\xi\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\xi$}}\nolimits,\bm{u}^{rel})\big),

(18)

where
L\big(\bm{z},\bm{z}^{\prime}\big)
is a continuous loss function that measures the difference between two copies of data
\bm{z}
and
\bm{z}^{\prime}
. However, we do not know (observe)
\bm{u}^{rel}
. By replacing
\bm{u}^{rel}
with a repro copy
\bm{u}^{*}
in (
18
), we get

\kappa^{*}=\arg\min\limits_{\kappa}\min\limits_{\xi}L\big(\bm{z}_{obs},G_{z}(\kappa,\mathop{\xi\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\xi$}}\nolimits,\bm{u}^{*})\big),

(19)

which maps a value
\bm{u}^{*}\in{\cal U}
to a
\kappa^{*}\in{\it\Upsilon}
. Typically,
{\cal U}
is uncountable and
{\it\Upsilon}
is countable, so the mapping in (
19
) is “many-to-one”; That is, many
\bm{u}^{*}\in{\cal U}
correspond to one
\kappa^{*}\in{\it\Upsilon}
. We are particularly interested in the
subset
S=\big\{\bm{u}^{*}:\kappa_{0}=\arg\min\limits_{\kappa}\min\limits_{\xi}L\big(\bm{z}_{obs},G_{z}(\kappa,\mathop{\xi\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\xi$}}\nolimits,\bm{u}^{*})\big)\big\}

\subset{\cal U}
, where for any
\bm{u}^{*}\in S
the mapping in (
19
) produces
\kappa^{*}=\kappa_{0}
. Since

\bm{u}^{rel}\in S
,
S\not=\emptyset
. We assume
S\subset{\cal U}
is a nontrivial set containing many elements that can not be ignored.

Now suppose
\mathbb{P}\big\{\bm{U}\in S\big|\bm{z}_{obs}\big\}>0
has a nontrivial probability.
Then, we can use a Monte-Carlo method to obtain a candidate set
\widehat{\Upsilon}=\widehat{\Upsilon}(\bm{z}_{obs})

such that
\mathbb{P}\big\{\kappa_{0}\not\in\widehat{\it\Upsilon}(\bm{Z})\big\}=o(\delta^{\prime})
.
The idea is to simulate a sequence of
\bm{u}^{c}\sim\bm{U}
, say,

{\cal V}_{c}=\big\{\bm{u}_{1}^{c},\ldots,\bm{u}_{N}^{c}\big\}
. When
N=|{\cal V}_{c}|
is large enough,
{\cal V}_{c}\cap S\not=\emptyset
with a high (Monte-Carlo) probability.
That is
\kappa_{0}\in\widehat{\it\Upsilon}_{{\cal V}_{c}}(\bm{z}_{obs})
with a high (Monte-Carlo) probability, where
\widehat{\it\Upsilon}_{{\cal V}_{c}}(\bm{z}_{obs})
is our candidate set

\widehat{\it\Upsilon}_{{\cal V}_{c}}(\bm{z}_{obs})=\left\{\kappa^{*}=\arg\min\limits_{\kappa}\min\limits_{\xi}L\big(\bm{z}_{obs},G_{z}(\kappa,\mathop{\xi\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\xi$}}\nolimits,\bm{u}^{c})\big)\,\,\big|\,\,\bm{u}^{c}\in{\cal V}_{c}\right\}.

(20)

We show next that

\mathbb{P}\big\{\kappa_{0}\in\widehat{\it\Upsilon}_{{\cal V}_{c}}(\bm{Z})\big\})=1-o(\delta^{\prime})
, where
\delta^{\prime}=\delta^{\prime}({\footnotesize|{\cal V}_{c}|})\to 0
as
|{\cal V}_{c}|\to\infty
.

Let

\bm{U}^{*}
be an independent copy of
\bm{U}
. Formally, we assume
the following condition:

(N1)

For
any
\bm{u}^{rel}\in\mathcal{U}
, there exists a
neighborhood of
\bm{u}^{rel}
, say
S_{\cal N}(\bm{u}^{rel})
,
such that

S_{\cal N}(\bm{u}^{rel})\subseteq S=\big\{\bm{u}^{*}:\kappa_{0}=\arg\min\limits_{\kappa}\min\limits_{\theta}L\big(G_{z}(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{0},\kappa_{0},\bm{u}^{rel}),G_{z}(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits,\kappa,\bm{u}^{*})\big)\big\}

(21)

and
\mathbb{P}_{(\bm{U}^{*},\bm{U})}\left\{\bm{U}^{*}\in S_{\cal N}(\bm{U})\right\}\geq P_{\cal N}>0
,
for a positive number
P_{\cal N}
.

Although the neighborhood
S_{\cal N}
and the number
P_{\cal N}
are problem specific, Condition (N1) is typically satisfied (or nearly satisfied)
when
L(\cdot,\cdot)
is a continuous loss function and
|{\cal U}|
is uncountable.
The following lemma suggests that,
under Condition (N1) and as
|{\cal V}_{c}|\to 0
,

\widehat{\it\Upsilon}_{{\cal V}_{c}}(\bm{z}_{obs})

in (
20
) satisfy condition (
17
). Here, the probability
\mathbb{P}_{\bm{U},{\cal V}_{c}}(\cdot)
refers to the joint distribution of
\bm{U}
and
|{\cal V}_{c}|
Monte-Carlo
\bm{U}
’s in
{\cal V}_{c}
. See Appendix I for a proof of the lemma.

Lemma 4
.

Suppose Condition (N1) holds and
\widehat{\it\Upsilon}(\bm{z}_{obs})
is defined in (
20
).
Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\bm{U},{\cal V}_{c}}\left\{\kappa_{0}\not\in\widehat{\it\Upsilon}_{{\cal V}_{c}}(\bm{Z})\right\}\leq(1-P_{\cal N})^{|{\cal V}_{c}|}.

Based on the lemma, we propose the following theorem. Here, the probability
\mathbb{P}_{\bm{U}|{\cal V}_{c}}(\cdot)
refers to the condition distribution of
\bm{U}
given the set
{\cal V}_{c}
. See Appendix I for a proof.

Theorem 4
.

Suppose Condition (N1) holds and
\widehat{\it\Upsilon}(\bm{z}_{obs})
is defined in (
20
). Then, there exists a positive number
c
such that we have, in probability,
(a)
\mathbb{P}_{\bm{U}|{\cal V}_{c}}\left\{\kappa_{0}\in\widehat{\it\Upsilon}_{{\cal V}_{c}}(\bm{Z})\right\}=1-o_{p}\left(e^{-c|{\cal V}_{c}|}\right)

and (b)
\mathbb{P}_{\bm{U}|{\cal V}_{c}}\big\{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{0}\in\Gamma_{\alpha}^{\prime}(\bm{Z})\big\}\geq\alpha-o_{p}\left(e^{-c|{\cal V}_{c}|}\right)
.

In the theorem, the small
o_{p}\left(e^{-c|{\cal V}_{c}|}\right)
term tends to
0
as the size of the Monte-Carlo set
|{\cal V}_{c}|\to\infty
, and the result holds for any fixed (data) sample size
n<\infty
. So the theorem is a small sample result. Also, the “in probability” statement regards the Monte-Carlo randomness of
{\cal V}_{c}
. Based on Theorem 4, we have a clean
interpretation for the frequentist
coverage statement:
if we use the proposed Monte-Carlo method to obtain the candidate set
\widehat{\it\Upsilon}_{{\cal V}_{c}}(\bm{Z})
, we have a near
100\%
chance (i.e., in probability statement in
{\cal V}_{c}
) that the coverage provability of the obtained set
\Gamma_{\alpha}^{\prime}(\bm{Z})
is
no less than
\alpha
(i.e., frequency statement in
\bm{U}
).

We will further discuss and provide an extension of Condition (N1) and Theorem
4
in Section 4.3.

4
Case study example: normal mixture with unknown number of components

Mixture distributions are a commonly used model in data science.
However, a challenging task
remains to be
quantifying the uncertainty and making inference for the unknown number of components, say
\tau_{0}
, in a finite mixture distribution.
Although several existing procedures provide point estimates for
\tau_{0}
, the question of how to assess the uncertainty of estimation of
\tau_{0}
is still open.
This difficulty is due in large part to the fact that the unknown
\tau_{0}
is a discrete natural number
and typical tools such as the large sample CLT are no long applicable to the point estimators of
\tau_{0}
.
In this section, we demonstrate how to use the repro samples method to solve this open question in a normal mixture model by constructing a finite sample level-
\alpha
confidence set for the unknown
\tau_{0}
.
This case study example also
sheds light on how to address inference problems of discrete parameters and handle nuisance parameters in general cases.

4.1
Notations: normal mixture model

Suppose
Y_{i}\sim N\big(\mu^{(0)}_{k},(\sigma^{(0)}_{k})^{2}\big)
, one of the
\tau_{0}
models in
{\cal M}=\{N\big(\mu^{(0)}_{k},(\sigma^{(0)}_{k})^{2}\big),

k\in\{1,\ldots,\tau_{0}\}\}
, for
i=1,\ldots,n.
Write
{\bm{Y}}=(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n})^{\top}
. Here, the model parameters are
{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits}=(\tau,\bm{M}_{\tau},{\bm{\mu}}_{\tau},\bm{\sigma}_{\tau}^{2})^{\top}
, where
{\bm{M}}_{\tau}=({\bm{m}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{m}}_{n})^{\top}
is the unknown (unobserved)
n\times\tau
membership assignment matrix, where
{\bm{m}}_{i}=(m_{i1},\ldots,m_{i\tau})^{\top}
and
m_{ik}
is the indicator of whether
Y_{i}
is from the
k
th distribution
N(\mu_{k},\sigma^{2})
,
1\leq i\leq n
,
1\leq k\leq\tau
. We denote by the corresponding true values
\tau_{0}
,
\bm{M}_{0}=({\bm{m}}^{(0)}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{m}}^{(0)}_{n})^{\top}

=

\big((m^{(0}_{11},\ldots,m^{(0)}_{1\tau})^{\top},\dots,(m^{(0)}_{n1},

\ldots,

m^{(0)}_{n\tau})^{\top}\big)^{\top}
,

{\bm{\mu}}_{0}=(\mu_{1}^{(0)},\ldots,\mu_{{}_{\tau_{0}}}^{(0)})^{\top}
,
\bm{\sigma}_{0}=(\sigma^{(0)}_{1},\dots,\sigma^{(0)}_{\tau_{0}})^{\top}
and
{\bm{\theta}}_{0}

=

(\tau_{0},\bm{M}_{0},{\bm{\mu}}_{0},

\bm{\sigma}_{0})^{\top}
, respectively. Because we are interested in inference of the unknown number of components
\tau<n,

{\bm{\mu}}_{\tau}=(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{\tau})^{\top}
and
\bm{\sigma}_{\tau}=(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{\tau})^{\top}
are therefore unknown nuisance parameters of the
\tau
components’ means and variances.

We can re-express
Y_{i}=\sum_{k=1}^{\tau}m^{(0)}_{ik}(\mu_{k}^{(0)}+\sigma^{(0)}_{k}U_{i})
, where
U_{i}\sim N(0,1)
, in a vector form,

\displaystyle{\bm{Y}}={\bm{M}}_{0}{\bm{\mu}}_{0}+\hbox{diag}(\bm{U})\bm{M}_{0}{\bm{\sigma}_{0}}={\bm{M}}_{0}{\bm{\mu}}_{0}+\hbox{diag}(\bm{M}_{0}{\bm{\sigma}_{0}})\bm{U},

(22)

where
\bm{U}=(U_{1},\ldots,U_{n})^{\top}
.
The realized version of (
22
) is

{\bm{y}_{obs}}={\bm{M}}_{0}{\bm{\mu}}_{0}+\hbox{diag}({\bm{u}}^{rel}){\bm{M}}_{0}\bm{\sigma}_{0},

where
\bm{M}_{0}
is the realized membership assignment matrix that generates
\bm{y}_{obs}
.
A typical
setup
Y_{i}\sim\sum_{k=1}^{\tau}p_{k}N(\mu_{k},\sigma_{k}^{2})
further assumes that
{\bm{m}}_{i}=(m_{i1},\ldots,m_{i\tau})^{\top}
is a random draw from multinomial distribution
\text{Multinomial}(\tau;p_{1},\ldots,p_{\tau})
with unknown proportion parameters
(p_{1},\ldots,p_{\tau})
,
\sum_{k=1}^{\tau}p_{k}=1
.

The literature confirms the identifiability issue in a mixture model that multiple sets of parameters could possibly generate the same data
(
Liu and Shao 2003
)
. To account for this identifiability issue, we re-define the true parameters as those that correspond to the ones with the smallest
\tau
, i.e.,

\displaystyle(\tau_{0},\bm{M}_{0},{\bm{\mu}}_{0},\bm{\sigma}_{0}^{2})=\underset{\{(\tau,\bm{M}_{\tau},{\bm{\mu}}_{\tau},\bm{\sigma}^{2})|{\bm{M}}_{\tau}{\bm{\mu}}_{\tau}={\bm{M}}_{0}{\bm{\mu}}_{0},\,{\bm{M}}_{\tau}{\bm{\sigma}}_{\tau}={\bm{M}}_{0}{\bm{\sigma}}_{0}\}}{\arg\min}\tau,

(23)

when the identifiability issue arises.
Here,
\bm{M}_{0}
and
{\bm{\mu}}_{0}
on the right hand side are the true parameter values that generates
\bm{y}^{obs}.
With a slight abuse of notation, we still use the same notations on the left hand side to denote the ones
with the smallest
\tau
.
We further assume that
(\tau_{0},\bm{M}_{0},{\bm{\mu}}_{0},\bm{\sigma}_{0}^{2})
defined in (
23
)
is unique. We also assume that

0<\sigma_{\min}\leq\sigma_{\max}<\infty,

where
\sigma_{\min}=\|\bm{\sigma}_{0}\|_{0}
and
\sigma_{\max}=\|\bm{\sigma}_{0}\|_{\infty}.

That is, among the
\tau_{0}
components, none of the variance
\sigma_{k}^{2}
can completely dominate another
\sigma_{k^{\prime}}^{2}
, for any
k\not=k^{\prime}
. The inference we develop is on the set of parameters defined in (
23
) with the smallest
\tau_{0}
.

The inference problem for the target parameter
\tau_{0}
is complicated. Here, we have both types of nuisance parameters of varying sizes: discrete nuisance parameters
\bm{M}_{\tau}
that are potentially high-dimensional and continuous nuisance parameters
(\bm{\mu}_{\tau},\bm{\sigma}_{\tau})
whose lengths change with
\tau
.
To have an effective repro samples approach that is computationally efficient,
we handle the two types of nuisance parameters in two different ways. For the discrete nuisance parameter
\bm{M}_{\tau}
we take advantage of
the many-to-one mapping described in Section 3.3 to obtain a manageable candidate set. For the continuous nuisance parameters
(\bm{\mu}_{\tau},\bm{\sigma}_{\tau})
, we utilize a specific feature of the normal mixture distribution, namely that, given
\tau
, we can find sufficient statistics for
(\bm{\mu}_{\tau},\bm{\sigma}_{\tau})

and avoid searching through all possible values of
(\bm{\mu}_{\tau},\bm{\sigma}_{\tau})
. In Section 4.2, we define a nuclear mapping function
T^{P}(\bm{u},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
that minimizes the impact of the nuisance parameters.
In Section 4.3, we construct an effective candidate set of
(\tau,\bm{M}_{\tau})
over the corresponding discrete parameter space to greatly improve our computing efficiency.

4.2
Nuclear mapping function
for
given
(\tau,\bm{M}_{\tau})

In most derivations of this subsection,
\tau
is fixed. For notation simplicity, we suppress the subscripts in
(\bm{\mu}_{\tau},\bm{\sigma}_{\tau})
and simply write
(\bm{\mu},\bm{\sigma})
. Note that the vectors
\bm{\mu}
and
\bm{\sigma}
have the length of
\tau
that changes when
\tau
varies. Also, let
\bm{H}_{\tau}={\bm{M}}_{\tau}({\bm{M}}_{\tau}^{\top}{\bm{M}}_{\tau})^{-1}{\bm{M}}_{\tau}^{\top}
,
\bm{H}_{\tau}^{(k)}=\bm{H}_{\tau}[\mathcal{I}_{k},\mathcal{I}_{k}]
be the submatrix of
\bm{H}_{\tau}
corresponding to the
k
th component with
\mathcal{I}_{k}=\{i:m_{ik}=1\}
being the index set corresponding component
k
, and
\bm{Y}^{(k)}=\bm{Y}[\mathcal{I}_{k}]
be the subvector of
\bm{Y}
that is corresponding to the
k
th component.

Corresponding to

\bm{Y}=\bm{M}_{0}\bm{\mu}_{0}+\hbox{diag}(\bm{M}_{0}{\bm{\sigma}}_{0})\bm{U}
in (
22

\bm{Y}^{\prime}

\bm{Y}^{\prime}=\bm{M}_{\tau}\bm{\mu}+\hbox{diag}(\bm{M}_{\tau}{\bm{\sigma}})\bm{U}=\bm{M}_{\tau}\mathbb{A}_{\tau}(\bm{Y}^{\prime})+\hbox{diag}\left\{\bm{M}_{\tau}\mathbb{B}_{\tau}(\bm{Y}^{\prime})\right\}\mathbb{C}_{\tau}(\bm{Y}^{\prime}),

where
\mathbb{A}_{\tau}(\bm{Y}^{\prime})=(\bm{M}_{\tau}^{\top}\bm{M}_{\tau})^{-1}\bm{M}_{\tau}^{\top}\bm{Y}^{\prime}

\mathbb{B}_{\tau}(\bm{Y}^{\prime})=\begin{pmatrix}\|(I-\bm{H}_{\tau}^{(1)})\bm{Y}^{{}^{\prime}(1)}\|\\
\vdots\\
\|(I-\bm{H}_{\tau}^{(\tau)})\bm{Y}^{{}^{\prime}(\tau)}\|\end{pmatrix}\,\,\hbox{and}\,\,\mathbb{C}_{\tau}(\bm{Y}^{\prime})=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{(I-\bm{H}_{\tau}^{(1)})\bm{Y}^{{}^{\prime}(1)}}{\|(I-\bm{H}_{\tau}^{(1)})\bm{Y}^{{}^{\prime}(1)}\|}\\
\vdots\\
\frac{(I-\bm{H}_{\tau}^{(\tau)})\bm{Y}^{{}^{\prime}(\tau)}}{\|(I-\bm{H}_{\tau}^{(\tau)})\bm{Y}^{{}^{\prime}(\tau)}\|}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{(I-\bm{H}_{\tau}^{(1)})\bm{U}^{(1)}}{\|(I-\bm{H}_{\tau}^{(1)})\bm{U}^{(1)}\|}\\

\frac{(I-\bm{H}_{\tau}^{(\tau)})\bm{U}^{(\tau)}}{\|(I-\bm{H}_{\tau}^{(\tau)})\bm{U}^{(\tau)}\|}\end{pmatrix}.

For the given
(\tau,\bm{M}_{\tau})
,

(\bm{\mu},\bm{\sigma})
and
\mathbb{C}_{\tau}(\bm{Y}^{\prime})

(\bm{\mu},\bm{\sigma})
.
By its expression,
\mathbb{C}(\bm{Y}^{\prime})
depends only on
\bm{U}
, so we also write
\mathbb{C}(\bm{Y}^{\prime})=\mathbb{C}(\bm{U})
.
To develop a nuclear mapping for
\tau
while removing the impact of the nuisance parameters
(\bm{\mu},\bm{\sigma})
, we consider below a conditional probability given
\{\mathbb{A}_{\tau}(\bm{Y}^{\prime}),\mathbb{B}_{\tau}(\bm{Y}^{\prime})\}
, the sufficient statistics for
(\bm{\mu},\bm{\sigma})
.

Let
\hat{\tau}(\bm{Y}^{\prime})
be any reasonable estimates of
\tau
based on
\bm{Y}^{\prime}.

For instance, we can use a BIC-based estimator

\displaystyle\hat{\tau}(\bm{Y}^{\prime})=\arg\min_{\tau\in{\it\Upsilon}}\left[\left\{-2\max_{(\bm{M}_{\tau},{\bm{\mu}},\bm{\sigma})}\log\ell(\bm{M}_{\tau},{\bm{\mu}},\bm{\sigma}|\bm{Y}^{\prime})\right\}+2\tau\log(n)\right],

where
\ell(\bm{M}_{\tau},{\bm{\mu}},\bm{\tau}|\bm{Y}^{\prime})
is the log-likelihood function of
(\bm{M}_{\tau},{\bm{\mu}},\bm{\tau})
given
\bm{Y}^{\prime}.

We also denote the conditional probability
\mathcal{P}(k|\bm{a},\bm{b})=\mathbb{P}\big\{\hat{\tau}(\bm{Y}^{\prime})=k\big|\mathbb{A}_{\tau}(\bm{Y}^{\prime})=\bm{a},\mathbb{B}_{\tau}(\bm{Y}^{\prime})=\bm{b}\big\}
and let

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\theta}(w|\bm{a},\bm{b})=\sum_{\{k:\mathcal{P}(k|\bm{a},\bm{b})>\mathcal{P}(w|\bm{a},\bm{b})\}}\mathcal{P}(k|\bm{a},\bm{b}).

Conditional on
\big\{A_{\tau}(\bm{Y}^{\prime})=\bm{a},B_{\tau}(\bm{Y}^{\prime})=\bm{b}\big\}

Lemma 5
.

The function defined above

is free of
(\bm{\mu},\bm{\sigma})
and so we can express
\mathcal{F}_{\theta}(w|\bm{a},\bm{b})=\mathcal{F}_{(\tau,M_{\tau})}(w|\bm{a},\bm{b}).

Next we follow Section 3.2 to remove the impact of the remaining latent discrete nuisance parameter

.
Specifically, let
T_{a}(\bm{u},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)=(\mathbb{A}_{\tau}(\bm{y}^{\prime}),\mathbb{B}_{\tau}(\bm{y}^{\prime}),

\mathbb{C}_{\tau}(\bm{y}^{\prime})),

\bm{y}^{\prime}=\bm{M}_{\tau}\bm{\mu}+\hbox{diag}(\bm{M}_{\tau}{\bm{\sigma}})\bm{u}

\displaystyle B_{\theta}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\left\{(\bm{a},\bm{b},\bm{c}):\mathcal{F}_{\theta}(\hat{\tau}(\bm{y}^{*})|\bm{a},\bm{b})\leq\alpha\,\,\text{for}\,\,\bm{y}^{*}=\bm{M}_{\tau}\bm{a}+\hbox{diag}\{\bm{M}_{\tau}\bm{b}\}\bm{c}\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{(\bm{a},\bm{b},\bm{c}):\mathcal{F}_{(\tau,\bm{M}_{\tau})}(\hat{\tau}(\bm{y}^{*})|\bm{a},\bm{b})\leq\alpha\,\,\text{for}\,\,\bm{y}^{*}=\bm{M}_{\tau}\bm{a}+\hbox{diag}\{\bm{M}_{\tau}\bm{b}\}\bm{c}\right\}

below states that

Theorem 5
.

For any

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\{T_{a}(\bm{U},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)\in B_{(\tau.\bm{M}_{\tau})}(\alpha)|\mathbb{A}_{\tau}(\bm{Y}^{\prime})=\bm{a},\mathbb{B}_{\tau}(\bm{Y}^{\prime})=\bm{b}\}\geq\alpha.

Therefore,

\mathbb{P}\{T_{a}(\bm{U},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)\in B_{(\tau.\bm{M}_{\tau})}(\alpha)\}\geq\alpha.

For

\displaystyle\nu(\bm{u},\tau,\bm{M}_{\tau},\bm{\mu},\bm{\sigma},\widetilde{\bm{M}_{\tau}})=\tilde{\nu}(\bm{y}^{\prime},\tau,\bm{M}_{\tau},\widetilde{\bm{M}_{\tau}})=\inf_{\alpha^{\prime}}\{\alpha^{\prime}:(\tau,\widetilde{\bm{M}}_{\tau})\in\Gamma_{\alpha^{\prime}}(\bm{y}^{\prime})\},

\displaystyle T^{P}(\bm{u},\tau,\bm{M}_{\tau},\bm{\mu},\bm{\sigma})=\widetilde{T}^{P}(\bm{y}^{\prime},\tau)=\min_{\widetilde{\bm{M}}_{\tau}}\tilde{\nu}(\bm{y}^{\prime},\tau,\bm{M}_{\tau},\widetilde{\bm{M}_{\tau}}).

According to Lemma

\displaystyle\Xi_{\alpha}(\bm{y}_{obs})

\displaystyle=\big\{\tau:\exists\bm{u}^{*}\in{\cal U}\mbox{ and $(\bm{M}_{\tau},\bm{\mu},\bm{\sigma})$ s.t. }\bm{y}_{obs}=\bm{\mu}+\hbox{diag}\{\bm{M}_{\tau}\bm{\sigma}\}\bm{u}^{*},

The set defined in (

We follow Section

Then based on (

) and (

),
we define the confidence set

\Xi^{\prime}_{\alpha}(\bm{y}_{obs})=\left\{\tau:\exists\widetilde{\bm{M}}_{\tau},\mbox{s.t. }(\tau,\widetilde{\bm{M}}_{\tau})\in\widehat{\it\Upsilon}_{{\cal V}_{c}}(\bm{y}_{obs}),\mathcal{F}_{\tau,\widetilde{\bm{M}}_{\tau}}(\hat{\tau}(\bm{y}^{obs})|\widetilde{\mathbb{A}}_{\tau}(\bm{y}_{obs}),\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}_{\tilde{\tau}}(\bm{y}_{obs}))\leq\alpha\right\}.

(28)

One complication under the mixture model setting is that the equation (
21
)
in (N1) may not hold when
\bm{u}^{rel}
hits certain directions (i.e., the two vectors
\hbox{diag}(\bm{M}_{0}\sigma_{0})\bm{u}^{rel}

,
\tau^{\prime}\leq\tau_{0}

5.1
Real data analysis: red blood cell sodium-lithium countertransport (SLC) data

In this section we consider a data set that consists of Red blood cell sodium-lithium countertransport (SLC) activity measurements from 190 individuals
(
Dudley et al. 1991
)

SLC measurement is known to be correlated with blood pressure, and perceived as an essential cause of hypertension by some researchers. Moreover, SLC is usually easier to study than blood pressure that could be influenced by numerous environmental and genetic factors
(
Dudley et al. 1991

.
Roeder 1994
and
Chen et al. 2012
both analyzed the SLC data set of 190 individuals using a normal mixture model, and perform a hypothesis testing on the number of components
H_{0}:\tau_{0}=k
vs
H_{1}:\tau_{0}>k
for a small integer
k.

Roeder 1994
concluded that
\tau_{0}=3
is the smallest value of
\tau_{0}
not rejected by the data assuming variance of each component is the same.

Chen et al. 2012
argued instead that dropping the assumption of equal variances, the hypothesis of
\tau_{0}=2
would not be rejected and
thus a good fit for the data.

We use the repro samples approach developed in Section 4
to re-analyze the SLC data. Following
Roeder 1994
;
Chen et al. 2012
, we assume that the SLC measurements follow a normal mixture distribution with unknown number of components
\tau_{0}
and obtain a 95% confidence set for
\tau_{0}
that is
\{2,3,4\}.
After that, we fit the normal mixture model with
\tau=2,3,4
on the SLC data with an EM algorithm, respectively. We provide the estimated mean, standard deviation and weight of each component in Table

\tau_{0}=2
and
\tau_{0}=3

(
Roeder 1994
)
,
evidently we can not rule out either of the two competing genetic models based on the SLC data alone.

\tau_{0}
supported by the data set, whereas inverting one-sided tests proposed by

To further demonstrate the performance of the proposed confidence set, we carry out simulation studies in which the simulated data are generated using the estimated normal mixture model with

It appears that for both settings of
\tau_{0}=3
and
\tau_{0}=4

\tau_{0}
achieves coverage rates of 99% and 96.5% respectively, both greater than 95%. Note that the parameter space for
\tau_{0}
is discrete, therefore, unlike confidence intervals for continuous parameters, it is not always possible to achieve an exact confidence level of 95%. For simulated data with
\tau_{0}=3,

We compare the performance of the existing methods under the same simulation settings. First, the classical BIC method recovers the true value of
\tau_{0}=3
only 13 times (6.5%) out of the 200 repetitions.
The number of times
drops to
0
(out of 200 repetitions) when the true

The results are not surprising, since with penalty terms point estimators of

are often biased towards smaller

These two extremely low rates of correctly recovering the true cluster sizes highlights the challenge of the traditional point estimation method in this simulation example, and more generally the risk of using just a point estimation in a normal mixture model. This result also suggests the necessity of quantifying the uncertainty of the estimation using a confidence set with desired coverage rate.

Additionally, when it comes to make an inference for
\tau_{0}
, we also illustrate the advantage of our proposed confidence set over the existing one-sided hypothesis testing approach in the literature. In practice, researchers would usually favor the smallest model that can not be rejected, as discussed in

and

versus
H_{1}:\tau_{0}=4

\infty
. In conclusion, we believe that the proposed confidence set by the repro samples method is a more intact approach of conducting inference on
\tau_{0}
compared to the one that uses only hypothesis testing.

6
Further comments and discussion

This article develops a general and effective framework, named repro samples method, to making inference for a wide range of problems, including many difficult open questions for which solutions were previously unavailable or could not be easily
obtained.
It utilizes artificial sample sets generated
by mimicking the observed data to quantify inference uncertainty.
Our development has explicit mathematical solutions in many settings, while others it uses a Monte-Carlo algorithm to achieve numerical solutions. The repro samples method
has several theoretical guarantees. Theoretical discussions in the article include guaranteed frequentist coverage in various situations in both finite and large sample settings, relationship and advantages to the classical hypothesis testing procedure and associated optimality results, effective handling of nuisance parameters, and use of a data-driven candidate set to improve computing efficiency, etc.
We also discuss relationships to other
artificial sample based approaches across Bayesian, fiducial and frequentist inferences and to that of the probabilistic IM method, with additional details provided in Appendix II.
A distinct advantage of the proposed framework is that it does not need to rely on likelihood or large sample CLT to develop inference. It is particularly effective for a broad collection of
challenging inference problems, especially those involving discrete target parameters.

The repro samples method is broadly applicable. This article includes a number of examples to illustrate several different aspects of the development.
It further includes a case study example to construct a finite-sample confidence set for the unknown number of components in a mixed normal model, which is a well-known unsolved problem in statistical inference. Although it appears simple, the case study example requires a number of procedures to deal with the discrete target parameter and also a large number of nuisance parameters of varying lengths.
Beside the examples in this article, we have also applied the repro samples method to solve another difficult problem on post-selection inference in high dimensional models where the model selection uncertainty are quantified and also incorporated in the inference of regression coefficients. Due to space limitation, we summarize the results of the post-selection inference problem in a separate paper, in
Wang et al. 2022
.
Additional research involving discrete parameters (e.g., identification of root node(s) of a network, network membership, unknown number of clusters/mixtures, etc.) and also rare events data are currently underway. The research results will be reported in separate articles.

Indeed, the repro samples method can greatly extend the scope and reach of statistical inference by considering broad types of parameters and models.
Besides the regular type of parameter space
\Theta
that is continuous, the parameter space
\Theta
in a repro samples method can be discrete or even mixed types. It sidesteps some regularity conditions commonly imposed in a traditional approach (e.g., the true
\bm{\theta}_{0}
is an inner point of parameter space
\Theta
;
Casella and Berger 1990
, Chapters 7 and 8
).
Furthermore, the algorithmic model (
1
) and its extension (
9
) are very general. In addition to covering the traditional Fisherian model specifications of using density functions (as illustrated throughout the paper),
they cover complex models such as those specified in a sequence of differential equations in population genetics, astronomy, geology, etc., in which one can simulate a data set from the model given the model parameter
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits
.
With the fast growth of data science, there is a pressing need to develop and expand the theoretical foundation for statistical inference and prediction. The development of the repro samples method is trans-formative and we anticipate that it will
serve as a stepping stone to establish theoretical foundations for statistical inference and prediction
in data science.

Appendix II: A Brief Review and Comparison with Existing and Relevant Inference Procedures

The repro samples method is an entirely frequentist approach, but
its development inherits some key ideas from several
existing inferential procedures
across Bayesian, fiducial and frequentist (BFF) paradigms.
For readers’ convenience, we first provide in Subsection II-A a brief review of several relevant BFF inferential approaches. We then provide in Subsection II-B a comparative discussion to highlight the strengths and uniqueness of the proposed repro samples method, and also describe a role that the samples method can play in bridging across BFF inferences.

II-A. A Brief Review of Existing and
Relevant BFF procedures

The repro samples method utilizes artificial sample data sets for inference. There are several relevant artificial sample-based inferential approaches across the BFF paradigms.

(i) Approximate Bayesian Computing (ABC)

refers to a family of techniques used to approximate posterior densities of
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits
by bypassing direct likelihood evaluations
(
Rubin 1984
;
Tavaré et al. 1997
;
Peters et al. 2012
, cf.)
.
A basic version of the so-called
rejection ABC algorithm
has the following [a] - [c] steps:

[a] Simulate an artificial model parameter
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits^{*}
from a prior
\pi(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
and, given
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits^{*}
, simulate an artificial dataset
{\bf y}^{*}

(e.g., set
{\bf y}^{*}=G(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits^{*},{\bf u}^{*})
for a simulated
{\bf u}^{*}
);
[b] If

{\bf y}^{*}\approx{\bf y}_{obs}
,
we collect the artificial parameter
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits^{*}
;
[c] Repeat steps [a] & [b] to obtain a large set of
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits^{*}
.

ABC is in fact
a Bayesian inversion method,
since the match
{\bf y}^{*}\approx{\bf y}_{obs}
is equivalent to solving
G(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits,{\bf u}^{*})\approx{\bf y}_{obs}
for
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits^{*}
.

Operationally,
because it is difficult to match
{\bf y}^{*}\approx{\bf y}_{obs}
, ABC instead matches

|S({\bf y}_{obs})-S({\bf y}^{*})|\leq\epsilon
for a pre-chosen summary statistic
S(\cdot)
and tolerance
\epsilon>0
. The kept
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits^{*}
form

an ABC posterior
,
p_{\epsilon}(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits|\bm{y}_{obs})
.
If
S(\cdot)

is a
sufficient statistic
, then
p_{\epsilon}(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits|\bm{y}_{obs})\to p(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits|\bm{y}_{obs})
,
the target posterior distribution, as
\epsilon\to 0
at a fast rate
(
Barber et al. 2015
;
Li and Fearnhead 2018
)
.
To improve computing efficiency, this basic ABC algorithm has been extended to, for instance, more complex ABC-Markov Chain Monte Carlo (ABC-MCMC) type of algorithms, but the key matching idea remains.
Note that, the ABC method seeks to match each single of artificial sample copy
\bm{y}^{*}
with the observed data
\bm{y}_{obs}
through a summary statistic and a preset tolerance level
\epsilon
to form a rejection rule.

Bayesian literature has
pointed out two remaining issues in ABC
(
Li and Fearnhead 2018
;
Thornton et al. 2018
, cf.,)
. First, When
S(\cdot)

is not sufficient,
p_{\epsilon}(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits|\bm{y}_{obs})\not\to p(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits|\bm{y}_{obs})
.
In this case,
there is no guarantee that an ABC posterior is a Bayesian posterior thus
the interpretation of the inferential result by the ABC posterior is unclear.
However,
deriving a sufficient statistic is not possible when the likelihood is intractable, thus it places a limitation on the practical use of ABC as a likelihood-free inference approach.
Second, an ABC method requires the preset threshold
\epsilon\to 0
at a fast rate,
leading to a degeneracy of computing efficiency
(since the acceptance probability in Step [b] goes to
0
for a very small
\epsilon\to 0
).
In practice, there is no clear-cut choice for an appropriate
\epsilon
to balance the procedures’ computational efficiency and inference validity
is an unsolved question
(
Li and Fearnhead 2018
, e.g.,)
.

(ii) Generalized fiducial inference (GFI)
is a generalization of Fisher’s fiducial method, which is understood in contemporary statistics as an inversion method to solve a pivot equation for model parameter
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits

(
Zabell 1992
;
Hannig et al. 2016
)
.
GFI extends the inversion from a pivot to actual data; i.e., solves for
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits^{*}
from

{\bf y}_{obs}=G(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits,{\bf u}^{*})
for artificially generated
{\bf u}^{*}
’s. But, since a solution is not always possible, thus GFI
considers an optimization under an
\epsilon
-approximation:

\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{\epsilon}^{*}={\rm argmin}_{\theta\in\{\theta:\,\,||{\bf y}_{obs}-G(\theta,{\bf u}^{*})||^{2}\leq\epsilon\}}||{\bf y}_{obs}-G({\theta},{\bf u}^{*})||^{2}

(40)

and let
\epsilon\to 0
at a fast rate
(
Hannig et al. 2016
)
.
GLI often has a guaranteed statistical (frequentist) performance, provided
n\to\infty
and
G
is smooth/differentiable in
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits

(
Hannig 2009
;
Hannig et al. 2016
)
. Different than the ABC that uses rejection sample, the GFI instead uses optimizations to approximately solve the equation
\bm{y}_{obs}=G(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits,\bm{u}^{*})
within a
\epsilon
-constrained set in
\Theta
.
Operationally,
GLI suffers the same computational issue as in ABC on the choice of the pre-specified
\epsilon
since,
as
\epsilon\to\infty
at a fast rate, equation (
40
) may not have a solution.

(iii) Efron’s bootstrap and other related artificial sampling methods
, in which many copies of artificial data are generated, are a popular way to help quantify uncertainty in complicate estimation problems in statistics. Let
\hat{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}}\nolimits
be a point estimator of
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits
and
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits^{*}
be the corresponding bootstrap estimator
The key justification relies on the so-called
bootstrap central limit theorem
(CLT)
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits^{*}-\hat{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}}\nolimits|\bm{y}_{obs}\sim\hat{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}}\nolimits-\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits|\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits

(
Singh 1981
;
Bickel and Freedman 1981
, e.g.,)
, in which the (multinomial distributed) uncertainty in resampled artificial data asymptotically matches the (often non-multinomial distributed) uncertainty in
\hat{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}}\nolimits
inherited from the sampling population.
When CLT does not apply (e.g., the parameter space
\Theta
is a discrete set), the methods are not supported by theory and often perform poorly.

In addition to the above artificial-sample-based approaches, repro samples method is also related the so called
Inferential Model
(IM) that seeks to produce (epistemic) probabilistic inference for
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{0}
:

(iv) Inferential model (IM)
is an attempt to develop a general framework for “prior-free exact probabilistic inference”
(
Martin and Liu 2015
)
. As described in
Martin and Liu 2015
and under the model assumption (
1
), an IM procedure include three steps:

[A-step] Associate
(\bm{Y},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
with the unobserved auxiliary statistic
\bm{U}
, i.e.,
\bm{Y}=G({\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits},{\bm{U}})
;
[P-Step] Predict the unobserved auxiliary statistic
\bm{U}
with a random set
{\cal S}
with distribution
{\mathbb{P}}_{\cal S}
;
[C-step] Combine observed
\bm{y}_{obs}
with random set
{\cal S}
into a new data-dependent random set

\Theta_{\bm{y}_{obs}}({\cal S})=\bigcup_{\bm{u}\in{\cal S}}\left\{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits:\bm{y}_{obs}=G(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits,\bm{u})\right\}
.

A novel aspect of the IM development that is beyond the typical fiducial procedures is that the uncertainty quantification of
\bm{Y}
is through the parameter free
\bm{U}
using a random set in [P-step]. This separation removes the impact of parameter
\bm{\theta}
and makes the task of uncertainty quantification easier.
In order to fully express the outcomes into probabilistic forms, IM development needs to use a system of imprecise probability known as
Dempster-Shafer Calculus
.
The outcomes of an IM algorithm are the so-called plausible and belief functions (or lower and upper probability functions) for
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{0}
, which can be used for frequentist inference. The plausible and belief functions are akin to the so-called
upper or lower confidence distributions

(
Thornton and Xie 2022
, e.g.,)
, although the IM method is not considered by most (including Martin and Liu) as a frequentist development.

II-B. Repro samples method and Connections to BFF Inferential Approaches

The repro samples method borrows two important ideas from the aforementioned BFF procedures. First, the repro samples method utilizes artificial data samples to help quantify the uncertainty of a statistical inference. This idea has been used in bootstrap, ABC, GFI, and Monte-Carlo hypothesis testing procedures, among others. A key aspect of utilizing artificial data samples for inference in these procedures is the common attempt to match (in a certain way) the artificial data with the observed sample. This matching is done by directly comparing either the value or distribution of the artificial
\bm{y}
or its summary statistic with that of
\bm{y}_{obs}
. The proposed repro samples method fully utilizes the artificial samples and the matching idea to both develop inference and solve computational questions. Second, the repro samples method borrows an idea from IM that we can quantify the inference uncertainty by first assessing the uncertainty of the unobserved
\bm{u}^{rel}
. This allows us to develop both finite and asymptotic inferences under the frequentist framework without the need to use any likelihood or decision theoretical criteria.
We consider that the repro samples method is a further development (generalization and refinements) of these existing BFF procedures. We provide some comparative discussions below.

Repro samples method versus the classical Monte-Carlo hypothesis testing.
In a few plain statements, the Monte-Carlo Algorithm 1 for the repro samples method can be described as follows: For any potential value
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits\in\Theta
, we generate multiple copies of
\bm{u}^{s}
(denote the set of their collection
{\cal V}
) and compute corresponding
T(\bm{u}^{s},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
’s. If there exists an
\bm{u}^{*}\in{\cal U}
such that
\bm{y}_{obs}=G(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits,\bm{u}^{*})
and the value
T(\bm{u}^{*},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)

is
conformal
with the multiple copies of
T(\bm{u}^{s},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
,
\bm{u}^{s}\in{\cal V}
, at the level
\alpha
, then we keep the
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits
in
\Gamma_{\alpha}(\bm{y}_{obs})
. Here, the matching of a single copy of
T(\bm{u}^{*},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
with the multiple copies of
T(\bm{u}^{s},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
’s is evaluated by a
conformal measure
at level-
\alpha

is
borrowed from recent developments of

(
Vovk et al. 2005

In the special case when the nuclear mapping is defined through a test statistic
T(\bm{u},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)=\tilde{T}(\bm{y},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
where
\bm{y}=G(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits,\bm{u})

\bm{y}_{obs}=G(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits,\bm{u}^{*})
for any
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits

\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits\in\Theta

\bm{y}^{s}=G(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits,\bm{u}^{s})
and compute the corresponding
\tilde{T}(\bm{y}^{s},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)

This simplified algorithm is exactly an Monte-Carlo implementation of the classical hypothesis test of
H_{0}:\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{0}=\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits

H_{0}

\bm{y}^{s}

Repro samples method versus ABC and GFI.

Both GFI and repro samples developments highlight the matching of equation
\bm{y}_{obs}=G(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits,\bm{u}^{*})
and the fact that, when
\bm{u}^{*}
matches or is close to
\bm{u}^{rel}

\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits
from the equation is equal or close to the true
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{0}
. The difference is that the GFI approach compares each single copy of artificial sample
\bm{y}^{*}=G(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits,\bm{u}^{*})
with the single copy of the observed
\bm{y}_{obs}
, so does the ABC method, while the repro samples method compares the single copy of the observed
\bm{y}_{obs}
with multiple copies of
\bm{y}^{s}=G(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits,\bm{u}^{s})

(or, more accurately, the realized
T(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits,\bm{u}^{rel})
with multiple copies of
T(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits,\bm{u}^{s})
’s) for
\bm{u}^{s}\in{\cal V}
. By a single comparing observed sample
\bm{y}_{obs}
(or realized
\bm{u}^{rel}
) with many copies of artificiallt generated
\bm{y}^{s}
(or
\bm{u}^{s}\in{\cal V}
), the repro samples method has the advantage to use a level

to calibrate the uncertain and side-step the difficult problem on how to appropriately preset the
threshold

Repro samples method versus IM.

The repro samples and IM methods both promote to first quantify the uncertainty of
\bm{U}
and use it to help address the overall uncertainty inherited in the sampling data. In order to produce a level
\alpha
confidence set, the repro sample method use a single fixed Borel set and a
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits
-dependent nuclear mapping function to help quantify the uncertainty in
\bm{U}
. The repro samples method is a fully frequentist approach
developed using only the standard probability tool. The IM on the other hand attempts to achieve a higher goal of producing a prior-free probabilistic inference for the unknown parameter
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits
. By doing so, it requires to use random sets and a complex imprecise probability system of Dempster-Shafer calculus.
Furthermore, IM focuses on finite sample inference, while the repro samples can be used for both finite and large sample inferences.
Finally, the repro samples method stresses the actual matching of simulated artificial
\bm{u}^{*}
with the (unknown) realized
\bm{u}^{rel}
as well. The development of candidate set for discrete parameter
\kappa
in Section 3.3 is rooted on the fact that, when
\bm{u}^{*}
is equal or in a neighborhood of
\bm{u}^{rel}
can give us the same
\kappa_{0}
in the many-to-one mapping function (
19
). This cannot be foreseen or easily adapted in an IM approach.

Artificial-sample-based inference and a bridge of BFF paradigms
. There have been several recent developments on the foundation of statistical inference across Bayeisan, fiducial and frequentist (BFF) paradigms.
Reid 2022
and
Thornton and Xie 2022
provide comprehensive overviews on several recent BFF research developments at the foundation level. Based on the development of confidence distribution,
Thornton and Xie 2022
also explores the aspect of making inference though matching artificial samples with observed data across BFF procedures. It argues that the matching of simulated artificial randomness with the sampling randomness inherited form a statistical model provides a unified
bridge to connect BFF inference procedures.
Here, the artificial randomness includes, for examples, bootstrap randomness in bootstrap, MCMMC randomness in a Bayesian analysis, the randomness of
\bm{U}^{*}
in fiducial procedures such as
GLM and IM.
The repro samples method is another development that seeks to align simulated artificial randomness with the sampling randomness. By doing so we can effective measure and quantify the uncertainty in our statistical inferential statements.
The development can be used to address many difficult inference problems, especially those involving discrete parameter space and also those where large sample CLT does not apply. It provides a further advancement on the foundation of statistical inference to meet the growing need for ever-emerging data science.
