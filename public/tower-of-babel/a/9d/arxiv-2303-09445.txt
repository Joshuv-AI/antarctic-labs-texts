Weakly reversible deficiency one realizations of polynomial dynamical systems: an algorithmic perspective
Gheorghe Craciun, Abhishek Deshpande, Jiaxin Jin

Abstract
Given a dynamical system with polynomial right-hand side, can it be generated by a reaction network that possesses certain properties? This question is important because some network properties may guarantee specific dynamical properties, such as existence or uniqueness of equilibria, persistence, permanence, or global stability. Here we focus on this problem in the context of weakly reversible deficiency one networks . In particular, we describe an algorithm for deciding if a polynomial dynamical system admits a weakly reversible deficiency one realization, and identifying one if it does exist. In addition, we show that weakly reversible deficiency one realizations can be partitioned into mutually exclusive Type I and Type II realizations, where Type I realizations guarantee existence and uniqueness of positive steady states, while Type II realizations are related to stoichiometric generators, and therefore to multistability.

Introduction

By a
polynomial dynamical system
we mean a dynamical system of the form

\begin{split}\frac{dx_{1}}{dt}&=p_{1}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}),\\
\frac{dx_{2}}{dt}&=p_{2}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}),\\
&\qquad\quad\vdots\\
\frac{dx_{n}}{dt}&=p_{n}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}),\\
\end{split}

(1)

where each
p_{i}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
is a polynomial in the variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
.
Such systems can exhibit exotic behaviors like multistability, presence of oscillations, and chaos due to the underlying nonlinearities.
We are especially interested in the dynamics of these systems when restricted to the
positive orthant
, because such systems are very common models of biological interaction networks, population dynamics models, or models for the transmission of infectious diseases
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
]
.

Very often, polynomial dynamical systems are generated by
reaction networks
. It is often convenient to study the graphical structure of these networks to make inference about their dynamics. It is also possible to study the
inverse problem
, i.e., for some given polynomial dynamical systems, ask what reaction networks can generate them. Due to the phenomenon of
dynamical equivalence

[
5
,
6
]
, such a network may not be unique, i.e., there exist multiple reaction networks that generate the same dynamics.

A key quantity in the study of these networks is its
deficiency
. In particular, networks possessing low deficiency have been studied in reaction network theory using the Deficiency Zero and Deficiency One theorems
[
2
,
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
]
. In particular, the Deficiency One Theorem
[
2
,
10
]
guarantees
uniqueness
of the steady state within each linear invariant subspace; this, together with the
existence
result in
[
11
]
completely characterizes the steady states of weakly reversible networks that satisfy the hypotheses of the Deficiency One Theorem. Further, weakly reversible networks (i.e., networks where each reaction is part of a cycle) are related to dynamical properties like persistence, permanence, and the existence of a globally attracting steady state
[
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
]
.

The problem of identifying weakly reversible deficiency
zero
realizations has been addressed in
[
16
]
. Here we analyze the realizability problem for weakly reversible reaction networks with deficiency
one
. In particular, given a polynomial dynamical system, we describe an algorithm to identify if there exists a weakly reversible deficiency one reaction network that generates this dynamical system.
Moreover, if weakly reversible deficiency one realizations do exist, our algorithm uses the geometry of some convex cones generated using the net reaction vectors to construct one such realization explicitly.

Definition 2.1

A

In this paper, there are neither self-loops nor isolated vertices in

(a)

Let
V=\{\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{y}_{m}\}
, and denote the
number of vertices
in
G
by
m
.

(b)

We denote a directed edge by
(\boldsymbol{y}_{i},\boldsymbol{y}_{j})\in E
, which represents a reaction in the network. Here
\boldsymbol{y}_{i}
and
\boldsymbol{y}_{j}
are called the
source vertex
and

respectively.
Moreover, we denote the

associated with the edge
\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{y}_{j}

\boldsymbol{y}_{j}-\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

Definition 2.2
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a Euclidean embedded graph.
The
stoichiometric subspace of
G
is the vector space spanned by the reaction vectors as follows:

S=\text{span}\{\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{y}\,|\,\boldsymbol{y}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\in E\}.

Given a subset of vertices

, the

S(V_{0})=\text{span}\{\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{y}\,|\,\boldsymbol{y}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\in E\ \text{and}\ \boldsymbol{y}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{y}\in V_{0}\}.

Furthermore, given a positive vector
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{n}
, the polyhedron
(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}+S)\cap\mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0}
is called the

of
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}
.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a Euclidean embedded graph.

(a)

The set of vertices
V
is partitioned by its connected components, also called
linkage classes
.
Every connected component is denoted by the set of vertices belonging to it.

(b)

A connected component

is

, if every edge is part of an oriented cycle.
Moreover, a strongly connected component
L\subseteq V
is

, if for every vertex
\boldsymbol{y}\in L

\boldsymbol{y}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\in E

\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\in L
.

(c)

G=(V,E)

, if all connected components are strongly connected.

Remark 2.4
.

For any weakly reversible reaction network

, every vertex

is a source and a target vertex.
Moreover, every connected component of
G
is strongly connected, and terminal strongly connected.

Definition 2.5
.

Let

Definition 2.6
.

Let

. Furthermore, the

(3)

A point

\sum_{\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{y}_{j}\in E}k_{\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{y}_{j}}(\boldsymbol{x}^{*})^{\boldsymbol{y}_{i}}(\boldsymbol{y}_{j}-\boldsymbol{y}_{i})=\mathbf{0}.

From
[

]
, it is known that every mass-action system admits the following matrix decomposition:

\frac{d\boldsymbol{x}}{dt}=\boldsymbol{Y}\boldsymbol{A}_{\boldsymbol{k}}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\boldsymbol{Y}},

(5)

where
\boldsymbol{Y}
is called the

, whose columns are the vertices, defined as

\boldsymbol{Y}=(\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\ \boldsymbol{y}_{2},\ \ldots,\ \boldsymbol{y}_{m}),

{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\boldsymbol{Y}}
is the vector
of monomials given by

{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\boldsymbol{Y}}=({\boldsymbol{x}}^{\boldsymbol{y}_{1}},{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\boldsymbol{y}_{2}},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\boldsymbol{y}_{m}})^{\intercal},

and

, defined as

[\boldsymbol{A}_{\boldsymbol{k}}]_{ji}=\begin{cases}k_{\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{y}_{j}},&\text{if }\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{y}_{j}\in E,\\[5.0pt]
-\sum\limits_{\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{y}_{j}\in E}k_{\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{y}_{j}},&\text{if }i=j,\\[5.0pt]
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

2.2
Net reaction vectors and dynamical equivalence

Inspired by the matrix decomposition in (
5
), we introduce the key concept: net reaction vector, and illustrate a new matrix decomposition in terms of net reaction vectors.

Definition 2.9
.

Let
(G,\boldsymbol{k})
be a mass-action system, and
V_{s}=\{\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\boldsymbol{y}_{2},\ldots,\boldsymbol{y}_{m_{s}}\}\subseteq V
be
the set of source vertices of
G
. For each source vertex
\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\in V_{s}
, we denote the
net reaction vector

\boldsymbol{w}_{i}
corresponding to
\boldsymbol{y}_{i}
by

\boldsymbol{w}_{i}=\sum\limits_{\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{y}_{j}\in E}k_{\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{y}_{j}}(\boldsymbol{y}_{j}-\boldsymbol{y}_{i}).

(6)

Further, we denote the
matrix of net reaction vectors of
G
as follows:

\boldsymbol{W}=\left(\boldsymbol{w}_{1},\boldsymbol{w}_{2},\ldots,\boldsymbol{w}_{m_{s}}\right).

(7)

It is convenient to refer to
\boldsymbol{w}_{j}
even when
\boldsymbol{y}_{j}\not\in V_{s}
, in which case we consider
\boldsymbol{w}_{j}
represents an empty sum, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{w}_{j}=\mathbf{0}

From Definition

corresponding to

\boldsymbol{w}_{i}=\sum\limits_{\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{y}_{j}\in E}k_{\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{y}_{j}}\boldsymbol{y}_{j}-\bigg(\sum\limits_{\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{y}_{j}\in E}k_{\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{y}_{j}}\bigg)\boldsymbol{y}_{i}.

(8)

Using a direct computation, we rewrite the mass-action system in (
3

\frac{d\boldsymbol{x}}{dt}=\boldsymbol{W}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\boldsymbol{Y}_{s}},

(9)

where
\boldsymbol{Y}_{s}
is called the
matrix of source vertices
, whose columns are source vertices, defined as

\boldsymbol{Y}_{s}=(\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\boldsymbol{y}_{2},\ldots,\boldsymbol{y}_{m_{s}}),

and
{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\boldsymbol{Y}_{s}}
is the vector
of monomials given by

{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\boldsymbol{Y}_{s}}=({\boldsymbol{x}}^{\boldsymbol{y}_{1}},{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\boldsymbol{y}_{2}},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\boldsymbol{y}_{m_{s}}})^{\intercal}.

Note that for any weakly reversible mass-action system

Moreover, we derive that

Definition 2.10
.

Let
(G,\boldsymbol{k})
and
(\bar{G},\bar{\boldsymbol{k}})
be two mass-action systems. Then

(10)

Remark 2.11
.

From Equation (

for every vertex

(11)

From Definition

(12)

Note that if either

Recall that a set

is a
polyhedral cone
if
X=\{\boldsymbol{x}:M\boldsymbol{x}\leq\textbf{0}\text{ for some matrix }M\}
. Such a cone is convex. It is

if it does not contain a positive dimensional linear subspace. A pointed polyhedral cone admits a unique (up to scalar multiple) minimal set of generators, and these generating vectors are called

[

]
.

Lemma 3.6
.

Consider a mass-action system
(G,\boldsymbol{k})
with vertices
\{\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
. Let
\boldsymbol{W}
be the matrix of net reaction vectors of
G
, then we have:

(a)

\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W})\cap\mathbb{R}^{m}_{\geq 0}
is a pointed polyhedral cone.

(b)

There exists the minimal set of generators for
\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W})\cap\mathbb{R}^{m}_{\geq 0}
.

Proof.

(a)

It is clear that the set
\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W})\cap\mathbb{R}^{m}_{\geq 0}
is the solution to
\boldsymbol{W}\nu\geq\textbf{0},-\boldsymbol{W}\nu\geq\textbf{0}
, and
\boldsymbol{I}_{m}\nu\geq\textbf{0}
, and the set is a polyhedral cone. From the definition, a cone contained in the positive orthant
\mathbb{R}^{m}_{\geq 0}
is always pointed.
Therefore, we deduce that
\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W})\cap\mathbb{R}^{m}_{\geq 0}
is a pointed polyhedral cone.

(b)

Since
\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W})\cap\mathbb{R}^{m}_{\geq 0}
is a pointed cone, by the Minkowski-Weyl theorem
[
24
,
25
]
, we have

\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W})\cap\mathbb{R}^{m}_{\geq 0}=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{r}\zeta_{i}\boldsymbol{d}_{i},

(15)

where
\{\boldsymbol{d}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{r}
is the
unique
(up to scalar multiple) minimal set of generators of the cone
\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W})\cap\mathbb{R}^{m}_{\geq 0}
.

∎

Furthermore, using the rank-nullity theorem, we have

\text{dim}(\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W}))=\text{dim}(\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{A}_{\boldsymbol{k}}))+\text{dim}(\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{Y})\cap\text{Im}(\boldsymbol{A}_{\boldsymbol{k}})).

Thus the minimal set of generators can be divided into two groups. The next definition illustrates this point.

Definition 3.7

(
[
26
]
)
.

Consider a mass-action system
(G,\boldsymbol{k})
and let
\boldsymbol{W}
be the matrix of net reaction vectors of
G
. An extreme vector
\boldsymbol{d}_{i}
of the cone
\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W})\cap\mathbb{R}^{m}_{\geq 0}
is called

1.

a
cyclic generator
, if
\boldsymbol{d}_{i}\in\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{A}_{\boldsymbol{k}})
.

2.

a
stoichiometric generator
, if
\boldsymbol{A}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\boldsymbol{d}_{i}\in\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{Y})\backslash\{\mathbf{0}\}
.

\delta=1
). Moreover, the net reaction vector matrix follows:

\boldsymbol{W}=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&-2&1&-1\\
1&-1&0&0&0\end{pmatrix},

(16)

and

\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W}))=\text{span}\big\{(1,1,1,0,0)^{\intercal},\ (1,1,0,-2,0)^{\intercal},\ (1,1,0,0,2)^{\intercal}\big\}.

(17)

Therefore, we can compute the minimal set of generators of
\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W})\cap\mathbb{R}^{5}_{\geq 0}
:

(i)

Cyclic generators:

\boldsymbol{d}_{1}=(1,1,1,0,0)^{\intercal},\ \ \boldsymbol{d}_{2}=(0,0,0,1,1)^{\intercal}.

(18)

(ii)

Stoichiometric generators:

\boldsymbol{d}_{3}=(1,1,0,0,2)^{\intercal},\ \ \boldsymbol{d}_{4}=(0,0,1,2,0)^{\intercal}.

(19)

Figure 3:
The mass-action system corresponds to Example
3.8
, which has both cyclic and stoichiometric generators.

In general, both cyclic and stoichiometric generators can be studied by flux mode analysis.
Further, Conradi et al.
[
26
]
defined subnetworks generated by stoichiometric generators, and showed that under some conditions if these subnetworks exhibit multistationarity, then so does the original network.

As remarked before, weakly reversible deficiency one realizations of Type I satisfy the conditions of the Deficiency One Theorem. This implies that there exists a unique equilibrium within each stoichiometric compatibility class for all values of the rate constants
\boldsymbol{k}
of these realizations. Weakly reversible deficiency one realizations of Type II are also important since the subnetworks generated by the stoichiometric generators can help answer questions about multistationarity. It is therefore important to identify and analyze weakly reversible deficiency one realizations.

4
The pointed cone
\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W})\cap\mathbb{R}^{m}_{\geq 0}

The goal of this section is to analyze the pointed cone
\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W})\cap\mathbb{R}^{m}_{\geq 0}
for weakly reversible deficiency one reaction networks. Specifically, we focus on the extreme vectors of
\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W})\cap\mathbb{R}^{m}_{\geq 0}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Consider a weakly reversible mass-action system
(G,\boldsymbol{k})
with vertices
\{\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
.
Let
\boldsymbol{W}
be the matrix of net reaction vectors of
G
, and
\{\boldsymbol{d}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{d}_{r}\}
be the minimal set of generators of
\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W})\cap\mathbb{R}^{m}_{\geq 0}
, then

\bigcup\limits_{i=1}^{r}\text{supp}(\boldsymbol{d}_{i})=[m].

(20)

Proof.

For contradiction, assume there exists
j\in[m]
, such that
j\notin\bigcup\limits_{i=1}^{r}\text{supp}(\boldsymbol{d}_{i})
. Then for any
\boldsymbol{v}=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{m})^{\intercal}\in\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W})\cap\mathbb{R}^{m}_{\geq 0}
, we obtain that

\boldsymbol{v}_{j}=0.

(21)

Since
(G,\boldsymbol{k})
is a weakly reversible mass-action system, by Proposition
2.8
there exists a positive vector in the kernel of the Kirchoff matrix
\boldsymbol{A}_{\boldsymbol{k}}
.
Note that weakly reversibility indicates
\boldsymbol{W}=\boldsymbol{Y}\boldsymbol{A}_{\boldsymbol{k}}
. Thus we have

\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{A}_{\boldsymbol{k}})\subseteq\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{Y}\boldsymbol{A}_{\boldsymbol{k}})=\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W}).

This implies the existence of a positive vector in
\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W})\cap\mathbb{R}^{m}_{\geq 0}
, contradicting Equation (
21
).
∎

Lemma 4.2

(
[
27
]
)
.

Consider a weakly reversible mass-action system
(G,\boldsymbol{k})
with vertices
\{\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
and stoichiometric subspace
S
. Let

\boldsymbol{W}
be the matrix of net reaction vectors of
G
, then

\text{Im}(\boldsymbol{W})=S.

(22)

The following lemma concerns the dimension of
\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W})

in various cases.

Lemma 4.3
.

Consider a weakly reversible mass-action system
(G,\boldsymbol{k})
with vertices
\{\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
and stoichiometric subspace
S
. Let
\boldsymbol{W}
be the matrix of net reaction vectors of
G
.

(a)

If
G
has deficiency
\delta
and a single linkage class (i.e.
\ell=1
), we have

\text{dim}(\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W}))=\delta+1.

(23)

Moreover, if
\delta=0
, then for any
\boldsymbol{z}\in\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W})\backslash\{\mathbf{0}\}
,
\text{supp}(\boldsymbol{z})=[m]
.

(b)

If
G
has deficiency one and
\ell\geq 1
linkage classes, we have

\text{dim}(\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W}))=\ell+1.

(24)

Proof.

(a)

Since
G
has deficiency
\delta
and one linkage class, we have

\dim(S)=s=m-(\delta+1).

By Lemma
4.2
,
\text{rank}(\boldsymbol{W})=\dim(\text{Im}(\boldsymbol{W}))=s
. Using the rank-nullity theorem, we obtain

\text{dim}(\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W}))=\delta+1.

Furthermore, if
\delta=0
, we deduce that

\text{dim}(\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W}))=1.

(25)

As a weakly reversible mass-action system,
(G,\boldsymbol{k})
possesses a strictly positive steady state
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0}
by Theorem
3.4
.
Using Equation (
3
), we get

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{j\in[m]}{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}^{\boldsymbol{y}_{j}}\sum\limits_{\boldsymbol{y}_{j}\to\boldsymbol{y}_{j}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}}k_{\boldsymbol{y}_{j}\to\boldsymbol{y}_{j}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{y}_{j}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{y}_{j})=\sum\limits_{j\in[m]}{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}^{\boldsymbol{y}_{j}}\boldsymbol{w}_{j}=0.

(26)

Note that
({\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}^{\boldsymbol{y}_{1}},{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}^{\boldsymbol{y}_{2}},\ldots,{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}^{\boldsymbol{y}_{m}})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{>0}
, and it spans
\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W})
due to Equation (
25
).

(b)

Since the deficiency of
G
is one, we get

\dim(S)=s=m-\ell-\delta=m-(\ell+1).

From Lemma
4.2
, we conclude that

\text{dim}(\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W}))=m-\dim(\text{Im}(\boldsymbol{W}))=m-\dim(S)=\ell+1.

∎

Here we start with the minimal set of generators of
\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W})\cap\mathbb{R}^{m}_{\geq 0}
, when weakly reversible mass-action systems contain a single linkage class.

Lemma 4.4
.

Consider a weakly reversible mass-action system
(G,\boldsymbol{k})
that has deficiency
\delta
and a single linkage class
L=\{\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{y}_{m}\}
.
Let
\boldsymbol{W}
be the matrix of net reaction vectors of
G
, and
\{\boldsymbol{d}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{d}_{r}\}
be the minimal set of generators of
\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W})\cap\mathbb{R}^{m}_{\geq 0}
, then

r\geq\delta+1.

(27)

Moreover, if
\delta=1
, then
r=2
.
Assume
\{\boldsymbol{d}_{1},\boldsymbol{d}_{2}\}
is the minimal set of generators, then

\text{supp}(\boldsymbol{d}_{1})\subsetneq[m],\ \ \text{supp}(\boldsymbol{d}_{2})\subsetneq[m],\ \ \text{supp}(\boldsymbol{d}_{1})\cup\text{supp}(\boldsymbol{d}_{2})=[m].

(28)

Proof.

Since
G
has deficiency
\delta
and one linkage class, from Lemma
4.3
.
(a)

we obtain

\text{dim}(\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W}))=\delta+1.

Using Equation (
26
) in Lemma
4.3
, we set
\boldsymbol{d}=(\boldsymbol{x}^{\boldsymbol{y}_{1}},\boldsymbol{x}^{\boldsymbol{y}_{2}},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}^{\boldsymbol{y}_{m}})
where
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0}
is a steady state for the system, and obtain
\boldsymbol{d}\in\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W})\cap\mathbb{R}^{m}_{\geq 0}
.
Then there exists a basis of
\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W})
that contains
\boldsymbol{d}
as follows.

B=\{\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{e}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{e}_{\delta}\}.

Since
\boldsymbol{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{>0}
, for any weights

\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{\delta}
, we can always find a sufficiently large
\lambda>0
, such that

\sum\limits^{\delta}_{i=1}\lambda_{i}\boldsymbol{e}_{i}+\lambda\boldsymbol{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{>0}.

Thus, we conclude

r\geq\text{dim}(\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W}))=\delta+1.

Furthermore, if the system has deficiency one (i.e.
\delta=1
), we derive that

\text{dim}(\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W}))=\delta+1=2,

and thus
\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W})\cap\mathbb{R}^{m}_{\geq 0}
is a two-dimensional pointed cone.
Therefore, the cone
\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W})\cap\mathbb{R}^{m}_{\geq 0}
must have two generators, i.e.,
r=2
.

Now assume
\{\boldsymbol{d}_{1},\boldsymbol{d}_{2}\}
is the minimal set of generators of
\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W})\cap\mathbb{R}^{m}_{\geq 0}
when the system has deficiency one. Using
\boldsymbol{d}=(\boldsymbol{x}^{\boldsymbol{y}_{1}},\boldsymbol{x}^{\boldsymbol{y}_{2}},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}^{\boldsymbol{y}_{m}})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{>0}
, we derive that

\text{supp}(\boldsymbol{d}_{1})\cup\text{supp}(\boldsymbol{d}_{2})=[m].

Suppose
\text{supp}(\boldsymbol{d}_{1})=[m]
, thus
\boldsymbol{d}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{>0}
. Then we can find a sufficiently large
\lambda>0
, such that

\boldsymbol{v}=\lambda\boldsymbol{d}_{1}-\boldsymbol{d}_{2}\in\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W})\cap\mathbb{R}^{m}_{>0}.

Note that
\boldsymbol{d}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{d}_{2}
are linearly independent, this contradicts with
\{\boldsymbol{d}_{1},\boldsymbol{d}_{2}\}
being the generating set of
\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W})\cap\mathbb{R}^{m}_{\geq 0}
. Thus, we derive that
\text{supp}(\boldsymbol{d}_{1})\subsetneq[m]
. Similarly, we can show
\text{supp}(\boldsymbol{d}_{2})\subsetneq[m]
, and conclude (
28
).
∎

Lemma 4.5
.

Consider a weakly reversible mass-action system
(G,\boldsymbol{k})
with a single linkage class
L=\{\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{y}_{m}\}
, and let
\boldsymbol{W}
be the matrix of net reaction vectors of

\boldsymbol{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{\geq 0}
generating the cone
\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W})\cap\mathbb{R}^{m}_{\geq 0}
if and only if the system has deficiency zero.

Proof.

First, suppose the system has deficiency zero.
From Equation (
26
) in Lemma
4.3
, we set
\boldsymbol{d}=(\boldsymbol{x}^{\boldsymbol{y}_{1}},\boldsymbol{x}^{\boldsymbol{y}_{2}},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}^{\boldsymbol{y}_{m}})
where
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0}
is a steady state for the system
(G,\boldsymbol{k})
, and obtain

\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W})=\text{span}\{\boldsymbol{d}\}.

One can check
\boldsymbol{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{>0}
, and hence
\boldsymbol{d}
generates
\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W})\cap\mathbb{R}^{m}_{\geq 0}
.

On the other hand, consider a vector
\boldsymbol{d}
that generates
\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W})\cap\mathbb{R}^{m}_{\geq 0}
. Using (
27
) and deficiency is non-negative, we get that

0\leq\delta\leq 1-1=0.

(29)

Thus, we conclude the deficiency of the system is zero.
∎

and
4.5
.

Remark 4.6
.

Consider a weakly reversible mass-action system
(G,\boldsymbol{k})
with a single linkage class

.
Let
\boldsymbol{W}
be the matrix of net reaction vectors of

.
Suppose two vectors

Next, we work on the minimal set of generators of

when the weakly reversible deficiency one networks have multiple linkage classes.

Lemma 4.7
.

Consider a weakly reversible deficiency one mass-action system
(G,\boldsymbol{k})
of Type I that has
\ell>1
linkage classes, denoted by
L_{1},\ldots,L_{\ell}
. Let
\boldsymbol{W}
be the matrix of net reaction vectors of
G
, and
\{\boldsymbol{W}_{p}\}^{\ell}_{p=1}
be the matrix of net vectors corresponding to linkage classes
\{L_{p}\}^{\ell}_{p=1}
, then

(a)

\text{dim}(\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W}_{1}))+\cdots+\text{dim}(\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W}_{\ell}))=\text{dim}(\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W}))=\ell+1,

(30)

where

\text{dim}(\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W}_{i}))=\begin{cases}1,&\text{for }1\leq i\leq\ell-1,\\[5.0pt]
2,&\text{for }i=\ell.\end{cases}

Moreover, for any
1\leq i\leq\ell-1
and
\boldsymbol{z}\in\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W}_{i})\backslash\{\mathbf{0}\}
, we have
\text{supp}(\boldsymbol{z})=L_{i}
.

(b)

There exist
\ell+1
vectors
\boldsymbol{d}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{d}_{\ell+1}
, which form the minimal set of generators of the cone
\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W})\cap\mathbb{R}^{m}_{\geq 0}
, such that

\text{supp}(\boldsymbol{d}_{i})=L_{i},\ \text{for }1\leq i\leq\ell-1,

(31)

and

\text{supp}(\boldsymbol{d}_{\ell})\subsetneq L_{\ell},\ \ \text{supp}(\boldsymbol{d}_{\ell+1})\subsetneq L_{\ell},\ \ \text{supp}(\boldsymbol{d}_{\ell})\cup\text{supp}(\boldsymbol{d}_{\ell+1})=L_{\ell}.

(32)

Proof.

(a)
From the assumption, the system
G
is of Type I with
\delta_{1}=\cdots=\delta_{\ell-1}=0
and
\delta_{\ell}=1
.
Using Lemma
4.3
, we get

\text{dim}(\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W}_{\ell}))=\delta_{\ell}+1=2,\ \ \text{dim}(\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W}_{i}))=\delta_{i}+1=1,\ \text{for }1\leq i\leq\ell-1.

Further, for any
1\leq i\leq\ell-1
and
\boldsymbol{z}\in\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W}_{i})\backslash\{\mathbf{0}\}
,

\text{supp}(\boldsymbol{z})=L_{i}.

Note that
G
has deficiency one, thus

\text{dim}(\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W}))=\ell+1
,
and we derive (
30
).

(b)

Now we construct the minimal set of generators of
\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W})\cap\mathbb{R}^{m}_{\geq 0}
, denoted by
\{\boldsymbol{d}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{d}_{r}\}
. It will follow from the construction that
r=\ell+1=\text{dim}(\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W}))
.

Since
(G,\boldsymbol{k})
is a weakly reversible mass-action system, it possesses a strictly positive steady state
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0}
by Theorem
3.4
. Following Equation (
26
) in Lemma
4.3
, we can build
\ell-1
vectors
\boldsymbol{d}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{d}_{\ell-1}
. We define
\boldsymbol{d}_{1}=(\boldsymbol{d}_{1,1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{d}_{1,m})\in\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W})\cap\mathbb{R}^{m}_{\geq 0}
, such that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{d}_{1,i}=\begin{cases}\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\boldsymbol{y}_{i}},&\text{for }i\in L_{1},\\
0,&\text{for }i\notin L_{1}.\end{cases}

(33)

It is clear that
\text{supp}(\boldsymbol{d}_{1})=L_{1}
. Analogously, for
i=1,\ldots,\ell-1
, we can define
\boldsymbol{d}_{i}
corresponding to the linkage classes
L_{i}
with
\text{supp}(\boldsymbol{d}_{i})=L_{i}
.

Note that
G
is of Type I and the linkage class
L_{\ell}
has deficiency one.
Let
L_{\ell}=\big\{\boldsymbol{y}_{\ell_{i}}\big\}^{m_{\ell}}_{i=1}
with
m_{\ell}=|L_{\ell}|
. From Lemma
4.4
, the cone
\text{dim}(\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W}_{\ell}))\cap\mathbb{R}^{m_{\ell}}_{\geq 0}
has two generators, denoted by
\{\boldsymbol{g}_{1},\boldsymbol{g}_{2}\}
. Suppose
\boldsymbol{g}_{1}=\big(\boldsymbol{g}_{1,i}\big)_{i\in L_{\ell}}
and
\boldsymbol{g}_{2}=\big(\boldsymbol{g}_{2,i}\big)_{i\in L_{\ell}}
, then we define
\boldsymbol{d}_{\ell}=(\boldsymbol{d}_{\ell,1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{d}_{\ell,m})
,
\boldsymbol{d}_{\ell+1}=(\boldsymbol{d}_{\ell+1,1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{d}_{\ell+1,m})
as

\begin{split}\boldsymbol{d}_{\ell,i}=\begin{cases}\boldsymbol{g}_{1,i},&\text{for }i\in L_{\ell},\\
0,&\text{for }i\notin L_{\ell}.\end{cases}\ \ \text{and }\ \boldsymbol{d}_{\ell+1,i}=\begin{cases}\boldsymbol{g}_{2,i},&\text{for }i\in L_{\ell},\\
0,&\text{for }i\notin L_{\ell}.\end{cases}\end{split}

(34)

Note that both
\boldsymbol{d}_{\ell},\boldsymbol{d}_{\ell+1}\in\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W})\cap\mathbb{R}^{m}_{\geq 0}
, and satisfy Equation (
32
).

We claim that the vectors
\boldsymbol{d}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{d}_{\ell}
form a set of generators for
\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W})\cap\mathbb{R}^{m}_{\geq 0}
.
From Equations (
33
) and (
34
), we deduce that the vectors
\{\boldsymbol{d}_{i}\}^{\ell+1}_{i=1}
are linearly independent. Together with
\text{dim}(\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W}))=\ell+1
, we derive that the set
\{\boldsymbol{d}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{d}_{\ell+1}\}
is a basis for
\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W})
. Thus, any vector
\boldsymbol{v}\in\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W})\cap\mathbb{R}^{m}_{\geq 0}
can be expressed as

\boldsymbol{v}=a_{1}\boldsymbol{d}_{1}+a_{2}\boldsymbol{d}_{2}+\cdots+a_{\ell+1}\boldsymbol{d}_{\ell+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{\geq 0},

(35)

where
a_{1},\ldots,a_{\ell+1}\in\mathbb{R}
.
So it suffices to prove all
\{a_{i}\}^{\ell+1}_{i=1}
are non-negative.
Recall
\{L_{i}\}^{\ell}_{i=1}
are linkage classes with
\text{supp}(\boldsymbol{d}_{i})=L_{i}
, and
\text{supp}(\boldsymbol{d}_{\ell})
,
\text{supp}(\boldsymbol{d}_{\ell+1})\subseteq L_{\ell}
, then we obtain

a_{i}\geq 0,\ \text{for }i=1,\ldots,\ell-1.

Moreover, we set
\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}=a_{\ell}\boldsymbol{g}_{1}+a_{\ell+1}\boldsymbol{g}_{2}
. From
\sum\limits^{\ell}_{i=1}\text{dim}(\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W}_{i}))=\text{dim}(\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W}))
, we derive

\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}\in\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W}_{\ell})\cap\mathbb{R}^{m_{\ell}}_{\geq 0}.

Since
\boldsymbol{g}_{1},\boldsymbol{g}_{2}
form the generators of the cone
\text{dim}(\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W}_{\ell}))\cap\mathbb{R}^{m_{\ell}}_{\geq 0}
, we have

a_{\ell}\geq 0,\ \ a_{\ell+1}\geq 0.

Therefore, we prove the claim.

Finally, we show
\{\boldsymbol{d}_{1},\boldsymbol{d}_{2},\ldots,\boldsymbol{d}_{\ell+1}\}
is the minimal set of generators for
\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W})\cap\mathbb{R}^{m}_{\geq 0}
.
Note from Equations (
33
) and (
34
),
\boldsymbol{d}_{\ell+1}
cannot be generated by
\{\boldsymbol{d}_{i}\}^{\ell}_{i=1}
, thus it suffices to show
\{\boldsymbol{d}_{1},\boldsymbol{d}_{2},\ldots,\boldsymbol{d}_{\ell}\}
are all extreme vectors.

Suppose not, there exists
1\leq j\leq\ell
, such that
\boldsymbol{d}_{j}
is not an extreme vector.
Then we can find two vectors
\gamma,\theta\in\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W})\cap\mathbb{R}^{m}_{\geq 0}
and
0<\lambda<1
, such that

\lambda\gamma+(1-\lambda)\theta=\boldsymbol{d}_{j},

(36)

where
\gamma\neq\nu\theta
for any constant
\nu
.
From Equation (
35
), we can express
\gamma
and
\theta
as the conical combination of
\{\boldsymbol{d}_{1},\boldsymbol{d}_{2},\ldots,\boldsymbol{d}_{\ell+1}\}
as

\gamma=\sum\limits^{\ell+1}_{i=1}\gamma_{i}\boldsymbol{d}_{i}\ \ \text{and }\ \theta=\sum\limits^{\ell+1}_{i=1}\theta_{i}\boldsymbol{d}_{i},

where
\gamma_{i},\ \theta_{i}\geq 0
, for
i=1,\ldots,\ell+1
.

If
j\neq\ell
, from
\text{supp}(\boldsymbol{d}_{j})=L_{j}
and Equation (
36
), we derive that
\gamma_{i}=\theta_{i}=0
for
1\leq i\leq\ell+1,i\neq j
.
This implies
\gamma=\gamma_{j}d_{j}
and
\theta=\theta_{j}d_{j}
, which contradicts with
\gamma\neq\nu\theta
.

If
j=\ell
, we deduce that
\gamma_{i}=\theta_{i}=0
for
1\leq i\leq\ell+1
such that
i\neq j
in a similar way. This implies
\gamma=\gamma_{\ell}\boldsymbol{d}_{\ell}
and
\theta=\theta_{\ell}\boldsymbol{d}_{\ell}
, which also contradicts with
\gamma\neq\nu\theta
.
Therefore we conclude that
\{\boldsymbol{d}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{d}_{\ell+1}\}
is the minimal set of generators of the cone
\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W})\cap\mathbb{R}^{m}_{\geq 0}
.
∎

Lemma 4.9
.

Consider a weakly reversible deficiency one mass-action system
(G,\boldsymbol{k})
of Type II that has

linkage classes denoted by
L_{1},\ldots,L_{\ell}
. Let
\boldsymbol{W}
be the matrix of net reaction vectors of
G
, and
\{\boldsymbol{W}_{p}\}^{\ell}_{p=1}
be the matrix of net reaction vectors corresponding to linkage classes
\{L_{p}\}^{\ell}_{p=1}
, then

\text{dim}(\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W}_{1}))+\cdots+\text{dim}(\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W}_{\ell}))=\text{dim}(\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W}))-1=\ell,

To conclude this section, we show that given a mass-action system that admits weakly reversible deficiency one realizations, then these realizations must be of the same type.
First, we recall a special result from
[

:

Theorem 4.11

(
[
28
, Theorem 6.3]
)
.

Consider two weakly reversible mass-action systems
(G,\boldsymbol{k})
and
(G^{\prime},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})
having a deficiency of one and the same number of linkage classes. Let
G
be of Type I and
G^{\prime}
be of Type II. Then
(G,\boldsymbol{k})
and
(G^{\prime},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})
cannot be dynamically equivalent.

After Theorem
4.11
, we are ready to prove the more general result as follows:

Theorem 4.12
.

Given two weakly reversible deficiency one mass-action systems:
(G,\boldsymbol{k})
of Type I and
(G^{\prime},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})
of Type II, then
(G,\boldsymbol{k})
and
(G^{\prime},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})
cannot be dynamically equivalent.

Proof.

For contradiction, assume that the two weakly reversible deficiency one mass-action systems
(G=(V,E),\boldsymbol{k})
of Type I, and
(G^{\prime}=(V^{\prime},E^{\prime}),\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})
of Type II are dynamically equivalent. By Remark
2.11
, they have the same set of non-zero net reaction vectors. Using
\text{Im}(\boldsymbol{W})=S
from Lemma
4.2
, we get that
(G,\boldsymbol{k})
and
(G^{\prime},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})
share the same stoichiometric subspace.

Now we claim that
(G,\boldsymbol{k})
and
(G^{\prime},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})
have the same number of vertices. For contradiction, suppose there exists a vertex
\boldsymbol{y}\in V^{\prime}
such that
\boldsymbol{y}\notin V
. Let
\boldsymbol{w}_{\boldsymbol{y}}
and
\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{y}}
represent the net reaction vectors corresponding to the vertex
\boldsymbol{y}
in
G
and
G^{\prime}
. From Remark
2.11
, we deduce that

\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{y}}=\sum\limits_{\boldsymbol{y}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{y}_{j}\in E^{\prime}}k^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{y}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{y}_{j}}(\boldsymbol{y}_{j}-\boldsymbol{y})=\mathbf{0}.

(67)

Since the network
G^{\prime}
is of Type II, each linkage of
G^{\prime}
has deficiency zero. By Proposition
3.5
, we get that its vertices are affinely independent within each linkage class. This implies that the reaction vectors
\{\boldsymbol{y}_{i}-\boldsymbol{y}\}_{\boldsymbol{y}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\in E^{\prime}}
are linearly independent, contradicting Equation (
67

Assume that there exists a vertex
\boldsymbol{y}\in L\subseteq V
such that
\boldsymbol{y}\notin V^{\prime}
(where

is some linkage class in
G
). Following the steps in the first part, we have that for any
\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\in V^{\prime}

\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{y}}\neq\mathbf{0}
. This shows that
V^{\prime}\subsetneq V
.
Moreover, from
\boldsymbol{y}\notin V^{\prime}

This implies that the vertices in the linkage class

are not affinely independent. Therefore the deficiency of linkage class

is one. Since
(G,\boldsymbol{k})
and
(G^{\prime},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})
have the same stoichiometric subspace and deficiency, we deduce that

5
Main results

This section aims to present the main algorithm of this paper, which checks the existence of a weakly reversible deficiency one realization and outputs one if it exists.
In this algorithm, the inputs are the matrices of source vertices and net reaction vectors via

\boldsymbol{Y}_{s}=(\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\boldsymbol{y}_{2},\ldots,\boldsymbol{y}_{m})\ \ \text{and }\ \boldsymbol{W}=(\boldsymbol{w}_{1},\boldsymbol{w}_{2},\ldots,\boldsymbol{w}_{m})

respectively.
For the sake of simplicity, we temporarily let

denote the minimal set of generators of
\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W})\cap\mathbb{R}^{m}_{\geq 0}
in this section.

To build the main algorithm, we need an algorithm to search for a weakly reversible realization with a single linkage class.
We use the algorithm in
[
27
]
and summarize its main idea as follows.

First, the algorithm in
[
27
]
checks whether there exists a reaction network realization that generates the given dynamical system such that all the target vertices are among the source vertices,
without
imposing the restrictions that
(i)
the network should be
weakly reversible
, and
(ii)
there should be only
one
linkage class. Next, if such a realization exists, the algorithm greedily searches for a maximal realization (a realization containing the maximum number of reactions) that generates the same dynamical system, while still imposing the restriction that all target vertices are among the source vertices.
The algorithm uses the fact that if the initial realization was weakly reversible and consisted of a single linkage class, then the maximal realization found using this procedure preserves weakly reversibility and a single linkage class.
Finally, based on this maximal realization, the algorithm constructs a Kirchoff matrix
Q
and checks whether
\dim(\text{ker}(Q))=1
and
\text{supp}(\text{ker}(Q))=\{1,2,3,\ldots,m\}
.
If both conditions are satisfied, then the maximal realization is weakly reversible and consists of a single linkage class. Otherwise, there is no such realization that generates the given polynomial dynamical system.

For more details on this algorithm and its implementation and complexity, please see
[
27
]
. In what follows, we will refer to the algorithm in
[

as

ℓ=1

5.2
Implementation of Algorithm
5.1

In this section, we discuss how to implement Algorithm
5.1
.
The algorithm is designed to find a weakly reversible deficiency one realization that generates the dynamical system
\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\boldsymbol{x}^{\boldsymbol{y}_{i}}\boldsymbol{w}_{i}
, and it has three key steps:

1.

Compute
\dim(\ker(\boldsymbol{W}))
and
\dim(\ker(\boldsymbol{W}_{i}))
.

2.

Find the extreme vectors of the cone
\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W})\cap\mathbb{R}^{m}_{\geq 0}
.

3.

Pass pairs of the matrices

\boldsymbol{Y}_{s},\boldsymbol{W}
or
\boldsymbol{Y}_{i},\boldsymbol{W}_{i}
through
Alg-WR
ℓ=1
.

In Step 1, the implementation needs a rank-revealing factorization; we need to find a basis of
\boldsymbol{W}
or
\boldsymbol{W}_{i}
, and then we can check the number of vectors in this basis.
This is equivalent to solving a linear programming problem.

In Step 2, we note that by the Minkowski-Weyl theorem
[
24
,
25
]
, there exists two representations of a polyhedral cone
C
given by:

(a)

H-representation
:
There exists a matrix
A
, such that the cone
C
can be written as

C=\{Ax\leq 0\}.

(b)

V-representation
:
The cone
C
has the minimal set of generators
\{d_{i}\}
, such that

C=\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}d_{i},

where
\lambda_{i}\geq 0
.

To find the extreme vectors of the cone
\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W})\cap\mathbb{R}^{m}_{\geq 0}
,
we need a way to convert from the H-representation to the V-representation. There are two popular ways of performing this conversion:

(a)

Double description method
: This is an example of an
incremental
method, where the conversion from H-representation to V-representation is performed assuming that the solution to a smaller problem is already known
[
29
]
. In particular, let
C(A):=\{Ax\leq 0\}
. Let
J
be a subset of the row indices of
A
. We will denote by
A_{J}
the submatrix of
A
obtained by selecting the
J
rows of
A
. Let us assume that we have found the minimal set of generators for the cone
C(A_{J})
. We will denote by
E
the generating matrix whose columns are the extreme vectors of
C(A_{J})
. The double description algorithm selects an index
h
that is not present in
J
and constructs the generating matrix
E^{\prime}
that corresponds to the
A_{J+h}
. This is repeated for several iterations until the generating matrix for
C(A)
is found. This algorithm is useful in cases where the inputs are degenerate and the dimension of the cone is small.

(b)

Pivoting methods
: In this method, the extreme vectors of the cone are found by the
reverse search
technique, where the simplex algorithm (that uses pivots iteratively) is run in reverse for the linear programming problem
Ax\leq 0
. The reverse search method determines the extreme vectors of the cone by building a tree in a depth-first-search fashion. This method was developed by Avis and Fukuda
[
30
]
. It is particularly useful for non-degenerate inputs where it runs in time polynomial of the input size.

In Step 3, we apply
Alg-WR
ℓ=1
, and this step can be done by solving a sequence of linear programming problems. More details can be found in section 4.4 in
[
27
]
.

6
Discussion

Weakly reversible deficiency one networks are ubiquitous in biochemistry, and are known to have the capacity to exhibit sophisticated dynamics. Some notable examples include the Edelstein network, as in Example
3.1
.
To better understand their dynamics, we divide them into two categories: (i) Type I networks, where all linkage classes have deficiency zero except one linkage class having deficiency one, and (ii) Type II networks, where all linkage classes have deficiency zero. The crucial quantity in the analysis of such networks is the pointed cone
\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W})\cap\mathbb{R}^{m}_{\geq 0}
, where
\boldsymbol{W}
is the matrix formed by the net reaction vectors. In particular, the extreme vectors of this cone can be divided into two classes: cyclic generators and stoichiometric generators. Networks of Type I possess only cyclic generators and satisfy the conditions of the Deficiency One Theorem. Consequently, for Type I networks, there exists a unique steady state within every stoichiometric compatibility class. For Type II networks, the set of stoichiometric generators is not empty. The stoichiometric generators define subnetworks, such that if these subnetworks possess multiple steady states, then the original network also allows multiple steady states
[
26
]
.

In addition, we show that networks of different types cannot be dynamically equivalent. Theorem
4.12
establishes this fact, and this implies that any mass-action system, at most, has one type of weakly reversible deficiency one realization, either Type I or Type II. In Section
4
we analyze in depth the extreme vectors of the cone
\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W})\cap\mathbb{R}^{m}_{\geq 0}
for weakly reversible deficiency one networks. In particular, we show that for Type I networks with
\ell
linkage classes, there exist
\ell+1
generators, while for Type II networks with
\ell
linkage classes, there exist
\ell+2
generators. Lemmas
4.7
and
4.9
establish these facts.

In Section
5
we describe our main result: the construction and the proof of correctness of Algorithm
5.1
.
This algorithm takes as input a matrix of source vertices and the corresponding matrix of net reaction vectors.
Algorithm
5.1
uses

Alg-WR
ℓ=1

as a subroutine and determines whether or not there exists a weakly reversible deficiency one realization for this input.
It is interesting to put this algorithm in the context of existing algorithms in the literature. There has been seminal work in this direction
[
31
,
32
,
33
,
34
,
35
,
36
,
37
]

based mostly on optimization methods that rely on mixed integer linear programming to determine the existence of realizations of a certain type.
The algorithm in this paper uses a novel and straightforward geometric approach by focusing on the extreme vectors of the cone
\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W})\cap\mathbb{R}^{m}_{\geq 0}
, instead of posing it as a constrained optimization problem. Algorithm
5.1
uses

Alg-WR
ℓ=1

\text{ker}(\boldsymbol{W})\cap\mathbb{R}^{m}_{\geq 0}

One could also explore the existence of mutually exclusive types of weakly reversible realizations for networks of higher deficiency.
