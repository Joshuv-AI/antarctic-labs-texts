The Famous American Economist H. Markowitz and Mathematical Overview of his Portfolio Selection Theory
Ignas Gasparavičius, Andrius Grigutis

Abstract
This survey article is dedicated to the life of the famous American economist H. Markowitz (1927–2023). We do revisit the main statements of the portfolio selection theory in terms of mathematical completeness including all the necessary auxiliary details.

Introduction

On June 22, 2023, the famous American economist H. Markowitz (aged 95) passed away. In this survey article, we revisit the main statements of the portfolio selection theory (also known as modern portfolio theory, mean-variance analysis, or just Markowitz theory) left by this prominent man, see

We also briefly touch on the influence that Markowitz’s theory made on other fields such as the rise of the capital asset pricing model, utility theory, arbitrage pricing theory, post-modernistic theory, etc.; see the end of the next section.

To read the presented text, it is sufficient to be familiar with fundamental mathematical courses that are usually taught during the first two years in many universities of natural sciences. We ask readers to be familiar with the Lagrange multiplier method, quadratic forms, differentials, linear algebra, probability theory, and mathematical statistics.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and denote

\displaystyle\mathbb{W}:=(w_{1},\,w_{2},\,\ldots,\,w_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\,\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}=1.

We call the vector
\mathbb{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

portfolio
, whose every component
w_{i},\,i=1,\,2,\,\ldots,\,n
shall be understood as the proportion (weight) of asset
i
in total "pool" of investments. In other words, if
A_{1},\,A_{2},\,\ldots,\,A_{n}
denote certain financial firms, the portfolio informally can be represented as
"w_{1}A_{1}+w_{2}A_{2}+\ldots+w_{n}A_{n}"
, where
w_{i}
denotes the proportion of capital allocated to the financial firm
A_{i}
,
i=1,\,2,\,\ldots,\,n
. If
w_{i}<0
for some
i=1,\,2,\,\ldots,\,n
, we say the asset
i
of the financial firm
A_{i}
is in a "short position", which means that it is borrowed, sold, and the attained income invested in some other assets out of those represented by financial firms
A_{1},\,A_{2},\,\ldots,\,A_{n}
. Let
{\boldsymbol{R}}:=(r_{1},\,r_{2},\,\ldots,\,r_{n})
be the collection of real-valued random variables defined on the same probability space. We suppose that the random vector
{\boldsymbol{R}}
represents the random returns "paid" by the financial firms
A_{1},\,A_{2},\,\ldots,\,A_{n}
. Of course, we quote the word "paid" here due to the negative return cases. Let
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}:=(\mu_{1},\,\mu_{2},\,\ldots,\,\mu_{n})
and
\sigma^{2}_{1},\,\sigma^{2}_{2},\,\ldots,\,\sigma^{2}_{n}
denote the expected values and variances of
r_{1},\,r_{2},\,\ldots,\,r_{n}
correspondingly. In addition, let
\mathrm{cov}(r_{i},\,r_{j})
denote the covariance between the random variables
r_{i}
and
r_{j}
, for all
i,\,j=1,\,2,\,\ldots,\,n
and

\displaystyle\Sigma:=\begin{pmatrix}\sigma^{2}_{1}&\mathrm{cov}(r_{1},\,r_{2})&\ldots&\mathrm{cov}(r_{1},\,r_{n})\\
\mathrm{cov}(r_{1},\,r_{2})&\sigma^{2}_{2}&\ldots&\mathrm{cov}(r_{2},\,r_{n})\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\mathrm{cov}(r_{1},\,r_{n})&\mathrm{cov}(r_{2},\,r_{n})&\ldots&\sigma^{2}_{n}\end{pmatrix}

be the covariance matrix. In practice, the collections of numerical characteristics
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
and
\Sigma
can be obtained variously: according to some historical observations, estimates, predictions, etc, see
[
37
]
,
[
35
]
,
[
2
]
. We next denote the random variable
P:=w_{1}r_{1}+w_{2}r_{2}+\ldots+w_{n}r_{n}
whose expected value and variance are:

\displaystyle\mu_{p}:={\boldsymbol{\mu}}\mathbb{W}^{T},

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{P}:=\mathbb{W}\Sigma\mathbb{W}^{T}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}w^{2}_{i}\sigma^{2}_{i}+2\sum_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant n}w_{i}w_{j}\mathrm{cov}(r_{i},\,r_{j}).

The notation
P
here is of course associated with the portfolio as it represents the random return of sum of weighted random returns
r_{1},\,r_{2},\,\ldots,\,r_{n}
of single investments.

The famous portfolio selection theory asks what weights
\mathbb{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
shall be chosen so that
\sigma_{P}^{2}
and
\mu_{P}
satisfy certain conditions (see the next section for more precise formulations). The core assumption of portfolio selection theory is that investors behave rationally: they prefer the lower-risk (variance) portfolios under the same level of the expected return. Technically, the numerical characteristics
\sigma_{P}^{2}
and
\mu_{P}
are nothing but functions of
n
variables
w_{1},\,w_{2},\,\ldots,\,w_{n}
under constraint
\mathbbm{1}\mathbb{W}^{T}=1
, where
\mathbbm{1}:=(1,\,1,\ldots,\,1)_{1\times n}
.

In the next section, we reformulate the core statements of the Markowitz portfolio selection theory. As we will see, the portfolio selection is based on the inverse
\Sigma^{-1}
of the covariance matrix
\Sigma
. Because of that, we need the following two little auxiliary statements on the existence of
\Sigma^{-1}
and some of its properties.

Lemma 1
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{0}}:=(0,\,0,\ldots,\,0)_{1\times n}
. The covariance matrix
\Sigma
is singular iff
\mathbb{P}(w_{1}r_{1}+w_{2}r_{2}+\ldots+w_{n}r_{n}=conts.)=1
for some
\mathbb{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{{\boldsymbol{0}}\}
.

Proof.

I
f
\Sigma
is singular then

\displaystyle\Sigma{\mathbb{W}}^{T}={\boldsymbol{0}}^{T}

(1)

for some
\mathbb{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{{\boldsymbol{0}}\}
. Consequently, eq. (
1
) implies

\displaystyle\sigma_{P}^{2}=\mathbb{W}\Sigma{\mathbb{W}}^{T}=\mathbb{W}{\boldsymbol{0}}^{T}=0,

and that means
\mathbb{P}(w_{1}r_{1}+w_{2}r_{2}+\ldots+w_{n}r_{n}=conts.)=1
since the variance of the random variable
w_{1}r_{1}+w_{2}r_{2}+\ldots+w_{n}r_{n}
is zero.

Conversely,
\mathbb{P}(w_{1}r_{1}+w_{2}r_{2}+\ldots+w_{n}r_{n}=conts.)=1
implies

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{P}=\mathbb{W}\Sigma\mathbb{W}^{T}=0=\mathbb{W}{\boldsymbol{0}}^{T}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\Sigma\mathbb{W}^{T}={\boldsymbol{0}}^{T},

which implies the singularity of
\Sigma
.
∎

Notice that the condition
\mathbb{P}(w_{1}r_{1}+w_{2}r_{2}+\ldots+w_{n}r_{n}=conts.)=1
in Lemma
1
is equivalent to the condition that all the realizations
(x_{1},\,x_{2},\,\ldots,\,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
of the random vector
{\boldsymbol{R}}
lie on the hyperplane
w_{1}x_{1}+w_{2}x_{2}+\ldots+w_{n}x_{n}=conts.
almost surely. See
[
5
]
as a related topic of mean-variance analysis under the singular covariance matrix.

Lemma 2
.

Let
A
be the symmetric and positively defined matrix such that its inverse
A^{-1}
exists. Then
A^{-1}
is also symmetric and positively defined.

Proof.

L
et
I
be the identity matrix. Then
AA^{-1}=A^{-1}A=I
and consequently

\displaystyle\left(AA^{-1}\right)^{T}=I^{T}\Rightarrow\left(A^{-1}\right)^{T}A^{T}=I\Rightarrow\left(A^{-1}\right)^{T}A=I\Rightarrow\left(A^{-1}\right)^{T}=A^{-1}.

If
A
is positively defined matrix, then
\mathbb{W}A\mathbb{W}^{T}>0
with any
\mathbb{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{{\boldsymbol{0}}\}
. If
\mathbb{W}=\tilde{\mathbb{W}}A^{-1}
then

\displaystyle\mathbb{W}A\mathbb{W}^{T}=\tilde{\mathbb{W}}A^{-1}AA^{-1}\tilde{\mathbb{W}}^{T}=\tilde{\mathbb{W}}A^{-1}\tilde{\mathbb{W}}^{T}>0

with any
\tilde{\mathbb{W}}=\mathbb{W}A\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{{\boldsymbol{0}}\}
.
∎

2
Propositions and their implications

As stated, in this section we formulate the essential statements of portfolio selection theory. These statements allow us to choose portfolios with the lowest variance, and the highest Sharpe ratio, draw the efficient frontier, and illustrate the fund separation theorem.

Proposition 1

(The minimal variance portfolio)
.

Let
P={\boldsymbol{R}}\mathbb{W}^{T}
be the random variable, where
{\boldsymbol{R}}
denotes the random vector whose covariance matrix
\Sigma
is non-singular, and
\mathbb{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The variance
\sigma_{P}^{2}=\mathbb{W}\Sigma\mathbb{W}^{T}
, under constraint
\mathbbm{1}\mathbb{W}^{T}=1
, is minimal when

\displaystyle\mathbb{W}^{T}=\frac{\Sigma^{-1}\mathbbm{1}^{T}}{\mathbbm{1}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbbm{1}^{T}}.

(2)

Moreover,

\displaystyle\min\sigma_{P}^{2}=\frac{1}{\mathbbm{1}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbbm{1}^{T}}.

The next statement provides what weighs
\mathbb{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
maximize the Sharpe ratio. First, let us denote
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}:=(\mu_{1}-r_{f},\,\mu_{2}-r_{f},\,\ldots,\,\mu_{n}-r_{f})
, where
r_{f}=conts.
denotes the risk-free return rate.

Proposition 2

(The maximal Sharpe ratio portfolio)
.

Let
P={\boldsymbol{R}}\mathbb{W}^{T}
be the random variable, where
{\boldsymbol{R}}
denotes the random vector whose covariance matrix
\Sigma
is non-singular,
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\neq{\boldsymbol{0}}
, and
\mathbb{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The Sharpe ratio

\displaystyle S_{P}(\mathbb{W})=\frac{\mathbb{W}\tilde{\bm{\mu}}^{T}}{\sqrt{\mathbb{W}\Sigma\mathbb{W}^{T}}},

under constraint
\mathbbm{1}\mathbb{W}^{T}=1
, is maximal when

\displaystyle\mathbb{W}^{T}=\frac{\Sigma^{-1}\tilde{\bm{\mu}}^{T}}{\mathbbm{1}\Sigma^{-1}\tilde{\bm{\mu}}^{T}}.

(3)

Moreover,

\displaystyle\max S_{P}=\sqrt{\tilde{\bm{\mu}}\Sigma^{-1}\tilde{\bm{\mu}}^{T}}\cdot\frac{\left|\mathbbm{1}\Sigma^{-1}\tilde{\bm{\mu}}^{T}\right|}{\mathbbm{1}\Sigma^{-1}\tilde{\bm{\mu}}^{T}}.

(4)

Proposition 3

(The minimal variance portfolio under the given return)
.

Let
P={\boldsymbol{R}}\mathbb{W}^{T}
be the random variable, where
{\boldsymbol{R}}
denotes the random vector whose covariance matrix
\Sigma
is non-singular,
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\neq{\boldsymbol{0}}
, and
\mathbb{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The variance
\sigma_{P}^{2}=\mathbb{W}\Sigma\mathbb{W}^{T}
, under constraints
\mathbbm{1}\mathbb{W}^{T}=1
and
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\mathbb{W}^{T}=\mu_{0}=
conts., is minimal when

\displaystyle\mathbb{W}^{T}=\left(\Sigma^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T},\,\Sigma^{-1}\mathbbm{1}^{T}\right)\begin{pmatrix}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\Sigma^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}&{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbbm{1}^{T}\\
\mathbbm{1}\Sigma^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}&\mathbbm{1}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbbm{1}^{T}\end{pmatrix}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}\mu_{0}\\
1\end{pmatrix}.

(5)

Proposition
3
plays the key role in drawing the curve in
(\sigma,\,\mu)
plane which is called the
efficient frontier
. The efficient frontier denotes the set of portfolios where no other portfolio exists with a higher expected return
\mu_{P}
under the same standard deviation
\sigma_{P}
(risk) of return. Let us denote
\mu_{\sigma\min}
the expected return of portfolio (
2
), i.e.

\displaystyle\mu_{\sigma\min}:=\frac{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbbm{1}^{T}}{\mathbbm{1}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbbm{1}^{T}}.

Proposition 4

(Efficient frontier of risky assets only)
.

An efficient frontier when the portfolio consists of risky assets only is

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}=a\mu^{2}+b\mu+c,\,\mu\geqslant\mu_{\min\sigma},

(6)

where

\displaystyle a=\frac{\mathbbm{1}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbbm{1}^{T}}{d},\,b=-2\cdot\frac{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbbm{1}^{T}}{d},\,c=\frac{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\Sigma^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}}{d},

\displaystyle d=\left({\boldsymbol{\mu}}\Sigma^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}\right)\left(\mathbbm{1}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbbm{1}^{T}\right)-\left({\boldsymbol{\mu}}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbbm{1}^{T}\right)^{2}>0\text{ if }{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\neq{\boldsymbol{0}}.

Notice the condition
\mu\geqslant\mu_{\min\sigma}
in (
6
) defines the upper branch of the hyperbola in the plane
(\mu,\,\sigma)
. By letting
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
in (
6
), we get the total hyperbola which is often referred to as the
minimum variance frontier
. We call the portfolios, lying on the efficient frontier,
efficient
. According to the core assumption of the Markowitz theory, efficient portfolios are always preferred by rational investors.

Proposition 5

(Efficient frontier including the risk-free asset)
.

An efficient frontier when the portfolio consists of risky assets and the risk-free asset is

\displaystyle\mu=\sqrt{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\Sigma^{-1}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}}\cdot\sigma+r_{f},\,\sigma\geqslant 0.

We call the portfolio (
3
)
optimal
and denote it by
M
. In addition, by
\sigma_{M}
and
\mu_{M}
we denote the standard deviation and the expected return of the optimal portfolio correspondingly.

Proposition 6

(Tangent line of the efficient frontier at optimal portfolio)
.

The tangent line of the efficient frontier given in (
6
) at the optimal portfolio
M
is

\displaystyle\mu=\frac{\mu_{M}-r_{f}}{\sigma_{M}}\cdot\sigma+r_{f},\,\sigma\geqslant 0.

Notice that the slope of the semi-line in Proposition
6
is the Sharpe ratio of the optimal portfolio. Thus,
(\mu_{M}-r_{f})/\sigma_{M}
equals to the maximal Sharpe ratio given in (
4
). Moreover, the slope of the semi-line in Proposition
5
equals the maximal Sharpe ratio too when
\mu_{M}-r_{f}>0
. In addition, the semi-line in Proposition
6
can be easily rewritten in its parametric form:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\mu=w\mu_{M}+(1-w)r_{f}\\
\sigma=w\sigma_{M}\end{cases},\,w\geqslant 0.

Thus, portfolios of the efficient frontier, when there is risk-free investment included, are just the combinations of optimal portfolio
M
and risk-free asset
F
, i.e. "
wM+(1-w)F,\,w\geqslant 0
".

Proposition 7

(Mutual fund separation theorem)
.

Let
\mathbb{W}_{1},\,\mathbb{W}_{2},\,\ldots,\,\mathbb{W}_{m}
, where
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
\mathbb{W}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
for all
i=1,\,2,\,\ldots,\,m
, be the efficient portfolios whose expected returns are
\mu_{0,\,1},\,\mu_{0,\,2},\,\ldots,\,\mu_{0,\,m}
respectively. Let

\mathbb{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
be such that

\displaystyle\begin{cases}w_{1}+w_{2}+\ldots+w_{m}=1\\
w_{1}\mu_{0,\,1}+w_{2}\mu_{0,\,2}+\ldots+w_{m}\mu_{0,\,m}=\tilde{\mu}_{0}\geqslant\mu_{\sigma\min}\end{cases}.

(7)

Then the portfolio
w_{1}\mathbb{W}_{1}+w_{2}\mathbb{W}_{2}+\ldots+w_{m}\mathbb{W}_{m}
is also efficient.

If
m=2
in Proposition
7
and
\mu_{0,\,1}\neq\mu_{0,\,2}
, the system (
7
) implies

\displaystyle(w_{1},\,w_{2})=\left(\frac{\tilde{\mu}_{0}-\mu_{0,\,2}}{\mu_{0,\,1}-\mu_{0,\,2}},\,\frac{\mu_{0,\,1}-\tilde{\mu}_{0}}{\mu_{0,\,1}-\mu_{0,\,2}}\right).

(8)

These proportions (
8
) are met in the so-called two-fund separation theorem. Notice that the second equation in (
7
) is nothing but the expected return of the portfolio
w_{1}\mathbb{W}_{1}+w_{2}\mathbb{W}_{2}+\ldots+w_{m}\mathbb{W}_{m}
. In practice, the mutual fund separation theorem is valuable because under some favorable circumstances it is more convenient or rational, in terms of transaction costs, to acquire the portfolios (funds)
\mathbb{W}_{1},\,\mathbb{W}_{2},\,\ldots,\,\mathbb{W}_{m}
and form the desired efficient portfolio out of them rather than to acquire the single assets of the financial firms
A_{1},\,A_{2},\,\ldots,\,A_{n}
; see
[
36
]
,
[
6
]
,
[
33
]
.

The listed Propositions

7
consist of the essence of the portfolio selection theory. As mentioned, Markowit’s works had a significant influence on many other fields or views of the surrounding phenomena. For instance, the shape of the efficient frontier tells us that ”extra benefits are not free”. In other words, the higher expected return corresponds to the higher standard deviation (risk), and the level of risk tolerance is up to the investors themselves. That encouraged developments in
risk aversion
and
utility theory
, see
[
24
]
,
[
3
]
. Also, the understanding of risk was extended realizing that the standard deviation as such does not tell the whole story. As a measure of returns’ scattering around its expectation, the standard deviation and similar measures provide only the so-called own (idiosyncratic) risk of the asset that generates the return. The
capital asset pricing model
(see
[
32
]
,
[
13
]
,
[

]
,
[

]
,
[

, p. 132]
,
[

]
) states that under some ideal-word assumptions, the expected return of any asset
A
is implied by the equation

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}r_{A}=r_{f}+\beta_{i}(\mathbb{E}r_{S}-r_{f}),\,\beta_{i}=\frac{\mathrm{cov}(r_{A},\,r_{S})}{\sigma^{2}_{S}},

(9)

where
S
in (
9
) denotes the systemic (market) portfolio. The coefficient
\beta_{i}
in (
9
), the slope of this linear equation, is interpreted as a measure of systemic risk that is raised by the systemic portfolio
S
. Based on (
9
), in
(\beta,\,\mu)
plane one may define the so-called
security market line

\displaystyle\mu=r_{f}+\beta(\mathbb{E}r_{S}-r_{f}),\,\beta\in\mathbb{R}.

(10)

The line (
10
) is a basic tool providing the equilibrium of the surrounding returns, see
[
9
]
,
[
20
]
. In
[
14
, p. 65]
one may find a certain related instance where the foundations of the capital asset pricing model appear. It is said that a certain asset is fairly evaluated if its expected return belongs to the line (
10
). On the other hand, assets below the line (

) are known as overestimated, while the above ones are called underestimated.
Based on that, there was built the
arbitrage pricing theory
, see
[

,
[

post-modernistic theory
. The post-modernistic theory optimizes plenty of other numerical characteristics (quantiles, etc.) in a similar way that Markowitz’s theory does the job in mean-variance optimization, see

]
,

Proofs

Proof of Proposition
1
.

We shall find the point
\mathbb{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that the function
\sigma_{P}^{2}=\mathbb{W}\Sigma\mathbb{W}^{T}
attains its minimum under constraint
\mathbbm{1}\mathbb{W}^{T}=1
. Let us setup the Lagrangian function
L(\mathbb{W},\,\lambda)=\mathbb{W}\Sigma\mathbb{W}^{T}-\lambda(\mathbbm{1}\mathbb{W}^{T}-1)
. By calculating the partial derivatives of
L(\mathbb{W},\,\lambda)
with respect to
w_{1},\,w_{2},\,\ldots,\,w_{n}
and
\lambda
, we obtain the following system of linear equations

\displaystyle\begin{cases}2\Sigma\mathbb{W}^{T}-\lambda\mathbbm{1}^{T}={\boldsymbol{0}}^{T},\\
\mathbbm{1}\mathbb{W}^{T}=1.\end{cases}

(11)

From the first equation in (
11
) we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{W}^{T}=\frac{\lambda}{2}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbbm{1}^{T}.

This and the last equation in (
11
) yields

\displaystyle\mathbbm{1}\frac{\lambda}{2}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbbm{1}^{T}=1\quad\Rightarrow\quad\frac{\lambda}{2}=\frac{1}{\mathbbm{1}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbbm{1}^{T}}.

Thus,

\displaystyle\mathbb{W}^{T}=\frac{\Sigma^{-1}\mathbbm{1}^{T}}{\mathbbm{1}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbbm{1}^{T}}.

(12)

To prove that the point (
12
) is minimum, we observe that the Hessian matrix

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}L(\mathbb{W},\,\lambda)}{\partial w_{i}\partial w_{j}}=\frac{\partial\left(2\Sigma\mathbb{W}^{T}-\lambda\mathbbm{1}^{T}\right)}{\partial w_{i}}=2\Sigma,

for all
i,\,j=1,\,2,\,\ldots,\,n
. This yields the positive second differential:

\displaystyle d^{2}\sigma_{P}^{2}(\mathbb{W})=(\Delta w_{1},\,\Delta w_{2},\,\ldots,\Delta w_{n})2\Sigma(\Delta w_{1},\,\Delta w_{2},\,\ldots,\Delta w_{n})^{T}>0,

for all
(\Delta w_{1},\,\Delta w_{2},\,\ldots,\,\Delta w_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}\}
.

By inserting the point (
12
) into
\sigma_{P}^{2}(\mathbb{W})=\mathbb{W}\Sigma\mathbb{W}^{T}
we get

\displaystyle\min\sigma^{2}=\frac{\mathbbm{1}\Sigma^{-1}}{\mathbbm{1}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbbm{1}^{T}}\Sigma\frac{\Sigma^{-1}\mathbbm{1}^{T}}{\mathbbm{1}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbbm{1}^{T}}=\frac{1}{\mathbbm{1}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbbm{1}^{T}}.

Here we have used the symmetry of
\Sigma^{-1}
, see Lemma
2
.
∎

Proof of Proposition
2
.

Let us set up the Lagrangian function

\displaystyle L(\mathbb{W},\lambda)=\frac{\mathbb{W}\bm{\mu}^{T}-r_{f}}{\sqrt{\mathbb{W}\Sigma\mathbb{W}^{T}}}-\lambda(\mathbbm{1}\mathbb{W}^{T}-1).

Its partial derivatives with respect to
w_{1},\,w_{2},\,\ldots,\,w_{n}
are

\displaystyle\frac{\partial L(\mathbb{W},\,\lambda)}{\partial w_{i}}=\frac{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}\sqrt{\mathbb{W}\Sigma\mathbb{W}^{T}}-(\mathbb{W}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}-r_{f})\frac{\Sigma\mathbb{W}^{T}}{\sqrt{\mathbb{W}\Sigma\mathbb{W}^{T}}}}{\mathbb{W}\Sigma\mathbb{W}^{T}}-\lambda\mathbbm{1}^{T},

for all
i=1,\,2,\,\ldots,\,n
. This and the partial derivative of
L(\mathbb{W},\,\lambda)
with respect to
\lambda
, yields the following system of linear equations

\displaystyle\begin{cases}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}\left(\mathbb{W}\Sigma\mathbb{W}^{T}\right)-\left(\mathbb{W}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}-r_{f}\right)\Sigma\mathbb{W}^{T}-\lambda\mathbbm{1}^{T}\left(\mathbb{W}\Sigma\mathbb{W}^{T}\right)^{3/2}={\boldsymbol{0}}^{T},\\
\mathbbm{1}\mathbb{W}^{T}=1.\end{cases}

(13)

By multiplying the both sides of the first equation in (
13
) by
\mathbb{W}
and simplifying, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{W}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}-(\mathbb{W}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}-r_{f})-\lambda\mathbb{W}\mathbbm{1}^{T}\left(\mathbb{W}\Sigma\mathbb{W}^{T}\right)^{1/2}=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad\lambda=\frac{r_{f}}{\left(\mathbb{W}\Sigma\mathbb{W}^{T}\right)^{1/2}}.

By inserting this expression of
\lambda
into the first equation of (
13
), we obtain

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}\left(\mathbb{W}\Sigma\mathbb{W}^{T}\right)-\left(\mathbb{W}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}-r_{f}\right)\Sigma\mathbb{W}^{T}-r_{f}\mathbbm{1}^{T}\left(\mathbb{W}\Sigma\mathbb{W}^{T}\right)={\boldsymbol{0}}^{T}

(14)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\,\left(\mathbb{W}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}-r_{f}\right)\Sigma\mathbb{W}^{T}=\left(\mathbb{W}\Sigma\mathbb{W}^{T}\right)({\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}-r_{f}\mathbbm{1}^{T})

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\,\mathbb{W}^{T}=\frac{\mathbb{W}\Sigma\mathbb{W}^{T}}{\mathbb{W}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}-r_{f}}\Sigma^{-1}({\boldsymbol{\mu}}-r_{f}\mathbbm{1})^{T},\,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\neq{\boldsymbol{0}}.

By inserting this into the last equation of (
13
), under the requirement
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\neq{\boldsymbol{0}}
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbbm{1}\frac{\mathbb{W}\Sigma\mathbb{W}^{T}}{\mathbb{W}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}-r_{f}}\Sigma^{-1}({\boldsymbol{\mu}}-r_{f}\mathbbm{1})^{T}=1\quad\Rightarrow\quad\frac{\mathbb{W}\Sigma\mathbb{W}^{T}}{\mathbb{W}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}-r_{f}}=\frac{1}{\mathbbm{1}\Sigma^{-1}({\boldsymbol{\mu}}-r_{f}\mathbbm{1})^{T}}.

Thus,

\displaystyle\mathbb{W}^{T}=\frac{\Sigma^{-1}({\boldsymbol{\mu}}-r_{f}\mathbbm{1})^{T}}{\mathbbm{1}\Sigma^{-1}({\boldsymbol{\mu}}-r_{f}\mathbbm{1})^{T}}=\frac{\Sigma^{-1}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}^{T}}{\mathbbm{1}\Sigma^{-1}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}^{T}}.

(15)

To prove that the point (
15
) is maximum, we first compute the Sharpe ratio in this point:

\displaystyle S_{P}(\mathbb{W})

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{W}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}^{T}}{\sqrt{\mathbb{W}\Sigma\mathbb{W}^{T}}}=\frac{\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}\Sigma^{-1}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}^{T}}{\mathbbm{1}\Sigma^{-1}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}^{T}}\Bigg/\sqrt{\frac{\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}\Sigma^{-1}}{\mathbbm{1}\Sigma^{-1}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}}\cdot\Sigma\cdot\frac{\Sigma^{-1}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}^{T}}{\mathbbm{1}\Sigma^{-1}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}^{T}}}=\sqrt{\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}\Sigma^{-1}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}^{T}}\cdot\frac{\left|\mathbbm{1}\Sigma^{-1}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}^{T}\right|}{\mathbbm{1}\Sigma^{-1}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}^{T}}.

Let us take some other point in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
than (
15
)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbb{W}}^{T}=\frac{\Sigma^{-1}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}^{T}}{\mathbbm{1}\Sigma^{-1}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}^{T}}+{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}^{T},

(16)

where
{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}:=(\delta_{1},\,\ldots,\,\delta_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then the Sharpe ratio in (
16
) is

\displaystyle S_{P}(\tilde{\mathbb{W}})=\left(\frac{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\Sigma^{-1}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}}{\mathbbm{1}\Sigma^{-1}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}}+{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}\right)\Bigg/\left(\sqrt{\frac{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\Sigma^{-1}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}}{\left(\mathbbm{1}\Sigma^{-1}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}\right)^{2}}+\frac{2\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}^{T}}{\mathbbm{1}\Sigma^{-1}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}}+{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}\Sigma{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}^{T}}\right).

Thus, the desired statement follows if we can show

\displaystyle S_{P}(\tilde{\mathbb{W}})<S_{P}({\mathbb{W}})

(17)

for all
{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}=(\delta_{1},\,\ldots,\,\delta_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{{\boldsymbol{0}}\}
.

Inequality (
17
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\frac{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\Sigma^{-1}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}}{\mathbbm{1}\Sigma^{-1}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}}+{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}<\sqrt{\left(\frac{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\Sigma^{-1}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}}{\mathbbm{1}\Sigma^{-1}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}}+{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}\right)^{2}-\left({\boldsymbol{\Delta}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}\right)^{2}+{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}\Sigma{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}^{T}\cdot\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\Sigma^{-1}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}}.

(18)

Let us observe that the inequality

\displaystyle x<\sqrt{x^{2}+\varepsilon},\,x\in\mathbb{R}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\frac{x}{|x|}<\sqrt{1+\frac{\varepsilon}{x^{2}}},\,x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}

is correct for all
\varepsilon>0
. Thus, inequality (
18
) follows if we can show

\displaystyle\left({\boldsymbol{\Delta}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}\right)^{2}<{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}\Sigma{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}^{T}\cdot\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\Sigma^{-1}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}.

(19)

Due to the positively defined covariance matrix, we can write

\displaystyle\left(\mathbbm{1}\Sigma^{-1}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}\right)^{2}\tilde{\mathbb{W}}\Sigma\tilde{\mathbb{W}}^{T}={\boldsymbol{\Delta}}\Sigma{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}^{T}\left(\mathbbm{1}\Sigma^{-1}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}\right)^{2}+2{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}\left(\mathbbm{1}\Sigma^{-1}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}\right)+\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\Sigma^{-1}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}>0.

This implies (
19
) because
ax^{2}+bx+c>0
being valid for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
implies
b^{2}-4ac<0
.

Proof of Proposition
3
.

Let us set up the Lagrangian function

\displaystyle L(\mathbb{W},\,\lambda_{1},\,\lambda_{2})=\mathbb{W}\Sigma\mathbb{W}^{T}-\lambda_{1}\left({\boldsymbol{\mu}}\mathbb{W}^{T}-\mu_{0}\right)-\lambda_{2}\left(\mathbbm{1}\mathbb{W}^{T}-1\right).

By calculating its partial derivatives with respect to
w_{1},\,w_{2},\,\ldots,\,w_{n}
,
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
, we get

\displaystyle\begin{cases}&2\Sigma\mathbb{W}^{T}-\lambda_{1}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}-\lambda_{2}\mathbbm{1}^{T}={\boldsymbol{0}}\\
&{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\mathbb{W}^{T}=\mu_{0}\\
&\mathbbm{1}\mathbb{W}^{T}=1\end{cases}.

(20)

The first equation of (
20
) yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{W}^{T}=\frac{\lambda_{1}}{2}\Sigma^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}+\frac{\lambda_{2}}{2}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbbm{1}^{T}=\left(\Sigma^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T},\,\Sigma^{-1}\mathbbm{1}^{T}\right)\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1}/2\\
\lambda_{2}/2\end{pmatrix}.

(21)

We then multiply the both sides of (
21
) by
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
and
\mathbbm{1}
respectively and, due to the last two equations in (
20
), obtain

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\frac{\lambda_{1}}{2}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\Sigma^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}+\frac{\lambda_{2}}{2}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbbm{1}^{T}=\mu_{0}\\
\frac{\lambda_{1}}{2}\mathbbm{1}\Sigma^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}+\frac{\lambda_{2}}{2}\mathbbm{1}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbbm{1}^{T}=1\end{cases}\Leftrightarrow\begin{pmatrix}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\Sigma^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}&{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbbm{1}^{T}\\
\mathbbm{1}\Sigma^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}&\mathbbm{1}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbbm{1}^{T}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\lambda_{1}}{2}\\
\frac{\lambda_{2}}{2}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\mu_{0}\\
1\end{pmatrix}.

Thus,

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1}/2\\
\lambda_{2}/2\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\Sigma^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}&{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbbm{1}^{T}\\
\mathbbm{1}\Sigma^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}&\mathbbm{1}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbbm{1}^{T}\end{pmatrix}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}\mu_{0}\\
1\end{pmatrix}

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{W}^{T}=\left(\Sigma^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T},\,\Sigma^{-1}\mathbbm{1}^{T}\right)\begin{pmatrix}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\Sigma^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}&{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbbm{1}^{T}\\
\mathbbm{1}\Sigma^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}&\mathbbm{1}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbbm{1}^{T}\end{pmatrix}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}\mu_{0}\\
1\end{pmatrix}.

Let us observe that the matrix

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\Sigma^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}&{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbbm{1}^{T}\\
\mathbbm{1}\Sigma^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}&\mathbbm{1}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbbm{1}^{T}\end{pmatrix}

is never singular as its determinant

\displaystyle\left({\boldsymbol{\mu}}\Sigma^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}\right)\left(\mathbbm{1}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbbm{1}^{T}\right)-\left({\boldsymbol{\mu}}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbbm{1}^{T}\right)^{2}>0,\,{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\neq{\boldsymbol{0}}

due to the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality.

Argumentation that the attained point is the minimum, is analogous to the one given in the proof of Proposition
1
.
∎

Proof of Proposition
4
.

It is easy to compute that

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\Sigma^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}&{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbbm{1}^{T}\\
\mathbbm{1}\Sigma^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}&\mathbbm{1}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbbm{1}^{T}\end{pmatrix}^{-1}=\frac{1}{d}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbbm{1}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbbm{1}^{T}&-{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbbm{1}^{T}\\
-{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbbm{1}^{T}&{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\Sigma^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}\end{pmatrix},

where
d=\left({\boldsymbol{\mu}}\Sigma^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}\right)\left(\mathbbm{1}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbbm{1}^{T}\right)-\left({\boldsymbol{\mu}}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbbm{1}^{T}\right)^{2}>0
if
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\neq{\boldsymbol{0}}
. Then, by inserting
\mathbb{W}
from Proposition
3
into
\sigma^{2}_{P}=\mathbb{W}\Sigma\mathbb{W}^{T}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{P}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d^{2}}\left(\mu_{0},\,1\right)\begin{pmatrix}\mathbbm{1}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbbm{1}^{T}&-{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbbm{1}^{T}\\
-{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbbm{1}^{T}&{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\Sigma^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\Sigma^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T},\,\Sigma^{-1}{\mathbbm{1}}^{T}\end{pmatrix}^{T}\left({\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T},\,\mathbbm{1}^{T}\right)\begin{pmatrix}\mathbbm{1}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbbm{1}^{T}&-{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbbm{1}^{T}\\
-{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbbm{1}^{T}&{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\Sigma^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mu_{0}\\
1\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\left(\mu_{0},\,1\right)\begin{pmatrix}\mathbbm{1}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbbm{1}^{T}&-{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbbm{1}^{T}\\
-{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbbm{1}^{T}&{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\Sigma^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mu_{0}\\
1\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\left(\left(\mathbbm{1}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbbm{1}^{T}\right)^{2}\mu_{0}^{2}-2\left({\boldsymbol{\mu}}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbbm{1}^{T}\right)\mu_{0}+{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\Sigma^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}\right).

Proof of Proposition
5
.

Let
P=w_{1}r_{1}+\ldots+w_{n}r_{n}+w_{f}r_{f}
be the random variable, where
w_{1}+w_{2}+\ldots+w_{n}+w_{f}=1
,
(w_{1},\,w_{2},\,\ldots,\,w_{n},\,w_{f})\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, the real-valued random variables
r_{1},\,r_{2},\,\ldots,\,r_{n}
have positive variances and represent the returns "paid" by the financial firms
A_{1},\,A_{2},\,\ldots,\,A_{n}
;
r_{f}=const.
denotes the risk-free return rate of asset
F
, and
w_{f}
denotes the proportion invested to
F
. Then

\displaystyle\mu_{P}

\displaystyle=w_{1}\mu_{1}+w_{2}\mu_{2}+\ldots+w_{n}\mu_{n}+w_{f}\,r_{f}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\mu}\mathbb{W}^{T}+w_{f}\,r_{f}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\mu}\mathbb{W}^{T}+r_{f}(1-\mathbbm{1}\mathbb{W}^{T})

or

\displaystyle\mu_{P}-r_{f}=(\boldsymbol{\mu}-r_{f}\mathbbm{1})\mathbb{W}^{T}\Rightarrow\tilde{\mu}_{P}=\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\mathbb{W}^{T},

where
\tilde{\mu}_{P}=\mu_{P}-r_{f}
and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}=\boldsymbol{\mu}-r_{f}\mathbbm{1}
. It is evident that the variance of the random variable
P=w_{1}r_{1}+\ldots+w_{n}r_{n}+w_{f}r_{f}
is
\sigma_{P}^{2}=\mathbb{W}\Sigma\mathbb{W}^{T}
,
\mathbb{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Thus, we shall minimize the variance
\sigma_{P}^{2}=\mathbb{W}\Sigma\mathbb{W}^{T}
under constraint
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\mathbb{W}^{T}=\mu_{0}-r_{f}=\tilde{\mu}_{0}=conts
. Let us set up the Lagrangian function

\displaystyle L(\mathbb{W},\,\lambda)=\mathbb{W}\Sigma\mathbb{W}^{T}-\lambda(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\mathbb{W}^{T}-\tilde{\mu}_{0}),

whose partial derivatives with respect to
w_{1},\,w_{2},\,\ldots,\,w_{n},\,w_{f}
and
\lambda
implies the system of linear equations

\displaystyle\begin{cases}2\Sigma\mathbb{W}^{T}-\lambda\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}={\boldsymbol{0}}^{T}\\
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\mathbb{W}^{T}-\tilde{\mu}_{0}=0\end{cases},

and its solution is

\displaystyle\mathbb{W}^{T}=(\mu_{0}-r_{f})\frac{\Sigma^{-1}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}}{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\Sigma^{-1}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}},\,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\neq{\boldsymbol{0}}.

(22)

Justification that the attained point is minimum, is implied due to the Hessian matrix of
L(\mathbb{W},\,\lambda)
being
2\Sigma
.
Then

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{P}=\mathbb{W}\Sigma\mathbb{W}^{T}=(\mu_{0}-r_{f})^{2}\frac{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}\Sigma^{-1}}{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\Sigma^{-1}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}}\Sigma\frac{\Sigma^{-1}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}}{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\Sigma^{-1}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}}=\frac{(\mu_{0}-r_{f})^{2}}{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\Sigma^{-1}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}},\,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\neq{\boldsymbol{0}}

or

\displaystyle\mu-r_{f}=\pm\sqrt{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\Sigma^{-1}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}}\,\sigma,\,\sigma\geqslant 0.

∎
