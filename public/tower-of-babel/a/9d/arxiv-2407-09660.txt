Abstract
. We prove an abstract convergence result for a family of dual-mesh based quadrature rules on tensor products of simplical meshes. In the context of the multilinear tensor-product finite element discretization of reaction-drift-diffusion equations, our quadrature rule generalizes the mass-lump rule, retaining its most useful properties; for a nonnegative reaction coefficient, it gives an O ⁡ ( h 2 ) O(h^{2}) -accurate, nonnegative diagonalization of the reaction operator. The major advantage of our scheme in comparison with the standard mass lumping scheme is that, under mild conditions, it produces an O ⁡ ( h 2 ) O(h^{2}) consistency error even when the integrand has a jump discontinuity. The finite-volume-type quadrature rule has been stated in a less general form and applied to systems of reaction-diffusion equations related to particle-based stochastic reaction-diffusion simulations (PBSRD); in this context, the reaction operator is required to be an M M -matrix and a standard model for bimolecular reactions has a discontinuous reaction coefficient. We apply our convergence results to a finite element discretization of scalar drift-diffusion-reaction model problem related to PBSRD systems, and provide new numerical convergence studies confirming the theory.

A distinguishing property of our quadrature scheme is that neither the primal nor the dual mesh needs to have any explicit relation to

Again, our target application is the CRDME for which the high dimensionality of the PIDE system prevents us from fitting the mesh to the interface. Another possible application includes evolving level-set problems for which it may be more efficient to recompute, or approximately recompute, the discrete reaction operator near the evolving interface

[
26

The majority of the paper (Sections

2.6

29

The estimates on the interface are more delicate and are closely related to some results from the papers
[
30

18

Many problems have singularities living on low-dimensional manifolds (as in the current paper), or require modified, lower-order discretizations near a subdomain boundary. Therefore, the arguments in this paper hint at a general principle that we hope to apply in future work.

The remainder of our paper is organized as follows: In Section

2.
Setup and basic convergence results

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a polygonal domain, and let
\mathcal{T}_{h}

\Omega

(i)

(ii)

\mathcal{T}_{h}^{(j)}

\Omega_{j}
, and

\mathcal{T}_{h}=\left\{\prod_{j=1}^{J}T_{j}:T_{j}\in\mathcal{T}_{h}^{(j)}\right\}.

Given our assumptions on
\mathcal{T}_{h}
, we observe that each element

has the same number of nodes

N_{T}=\prod_{j=1}^{J}(d_{j}+1).

We denote the vertices of
\mathcal{T}_{h}
by
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, and note that

x_{i}=(x^{(1)}_{i_{1}},x^{(2)}_{i_{2}},...,x^{(J)}_{i_{J}}),

where

\mathcal{T}_{h}^{(j)}
.

For the purposes of this paper, we will refer to a function

on
\Omega
as
multilinear
on an element
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
if it is linear in each variable

. For concreteness and clarity of exposition, we will let

be the space of piecewise multilinear functions on
\mathcal{T}_{h}
, although the results are applicable in other cases as well. That is,

V_{h}=\Span\{\phi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N},

where each

is defined as

\phi_{i}(x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{J})=\prod_{j=1}^{J}\phi^{(j)}_{i_{j}}(x_{j}),\qquad x_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{j}}

for

.

Next, we associate to each vertex

We call the resulting subdivision

Each voxel

2.

(3)

\displaystyle|V_{i}\cap T|=\frac{|T|}{N_{T}}1_{x_{i}\in T}\quad\text{so that}\quad|V_{i}|=\sum_{T\ni x_{i}}\frac{|T|}{N_{T}}.

We note that these two properties imply that

. Moreover, for any function

For any

2.1.
Function spaces and quadrature errors

Through the remainder of the paper, for any open set
D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
we will denote by
W^{k,p}(D)
the usual Sobolev space consisting of functions whose derivatives of order
\leq k
are in
L^{p}(D)
, with
H^{k}(D)=W^{k,2}(D)
. We use
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{k,p,D}
to denote the usual Sobolev space norm on
W^{k,p}(D)
and
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{k,D}
the corresponding norm on
H^{k}(D)
. For the Sobolev space seminorm associated with
W^{k,p}(D)
or
H^{k}(D)
, i.e.,

|f|_{k,p,K}=\left(\sum_{|\alpha|=k}\int_{D}|\partial^{\alpha}f|^{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}},

we write
|\cdot|_{k,p,D}
and
|\cdot|_{k,D}
. Note, the definitions of the norms also apply for
k<0
, in which case they refer to a dual norm or seminorm. We use
\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>_{D}
to denote the
L^{2}
-inner product on
D
. Where there is no ambiguity in any of the above notations, we will occasionally drop the dependence on the domain
D
. The spaces
C^{k}(D)
denote spaces of functions with continuous derivatives up to order
k
on
D
.

For any approximation (e.g., a quadrature scheme)
\mathcal{Q}(f)
or
\mathcal{Q}(f,g)
of a linear or bilinear functional (e.g., an integral), we can associate an error functional
\mathcal{E}(f)
or bilinear form
\mathcal{E}(f,g)
representing the difference between the exact value of the functional and the approximation. For convenience, we will occasionally overload notation, such that
\mathcal{E}(fg)
and
\mathcal{E}(f,g)
refer to the same error functional depending on whether we want to view
\mathcal{E}
as a linear functional of
fg
or a bilinear functional of
f
and
g
. We also drop arguments of
\mathcal{E}
that we want to view as fixed parameters. When necessary, we will use the notation
\mathcal{E}_{D}
to denote the restriction of the error functional to a particular subset
D
of the original domain of its arguments; for example, for the mass lump rule (
4
) we would have

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(f)

\displaystyle=\bar{\mathcal{M}}_{h}(f)-\mathcal{M}_{h}(f)=\int_{\Omega}f(x)dx-\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\sum_{x_{i}\in\mathcal{T}}\frac{|T|}{N_{T}}f_{i},

and, for any
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{T}(f)

\displaystyle=\int_{T}f(x)dx-\sum_{x_{i}\in T}\frac{|T|}{N_{T}}f_{i}.

For
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
, we refer to
\mathcal{E}_{T}(f)
and
\mathcal{E}(f)
as the local and global error functionals, respectively. The initial error estimates of Section
2.2
will use the typical procedure of first bounding the local error on a
reference element

\hat{T}
of unit size in terms of Sobolev space norms of its arguments. The reference element is chosen so that for each
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
, there exists an multi-affine (defined here in analogy with multilinear) transformation
G_{T}:\hat{T}\to T
. We refer to the reference element functional or bilinear form as
\hat{\mathcal{E}}
. To map a local error functional
\mathcal{E}_{T}
onto the reference error functional
\hat{\mathcal{E}}
, we employ a change of variables which involves composing arguments
f
of
\mathcal{E}
with
G_{T}
. We denote a transformed function
f\circ G_{T}
by
\hat{f}
. Since
G_{T}
is multi-affine, if
f
is multilinear on
T
then
\hat{f}
is multilinear on
\hat{T}
. By a scaling argument, if
f\in W^{k,p}(T)
we have that

(5)

|\hat{f}|_{k,p,\hat{T}}\leq Ch^{k}|f|_{k,p,T}.

Therefore, we gain powers of
h
in our error estimates by applying the Bramble-Hilbert lemma on the reference element to change Sobolev space norms into semi-norms, and then scaling back to the original element. For convenience, we state a version of the Bramble-Hilbert lemma below which applies to both linear and bilinear forms.

Lemma 2.3

(Bramble-Hilbert for bilinear forms)
.

Let
D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
have Lipschitz-continuous boundary. For some integer
k\geq 0
and some
p\in[1,\infty]
, let
G
be a continuous bilinear functional on
W^{k,p}(D)\times W^{\ell,q}(D)
such that

\displaystyle G(f,\cdot)

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{for all $f\in P_{k}$},

\displaystyle G(\cdot,g)

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{for all $g\in P_{\ell}$}.

where
P_{k}
denotes the set of polynomials of degree
\leq k
. Then there exists a constant
C(D)
such that

|G(f,g)|\leq C(D,G)|f|_{k+1,p,D}|g|_{\ell+1,q,D}\quad\text{for all $f\in W^{k,p}(D),g\in W^{\ell,q}(D)$}.

More details and background on the type of argument summarized above can be found in many classical finite element method references, including
[
1
]
, Chapter 3 and 4.

We note that, while our quadrature error estimates do not require any mesh restrictions, the finite element convergence theorems proven in Section
4
require the existence of an interpolation operator
I_{h}:H^{k}(\Omega)\to V_{h}
with error estimate

(6)

\left(\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}|v-I_{h}v|_{m,p,T}^{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\leq Ch^{k-m}|v|_{k,p,\Omega}.

For this one typically requires a nondegeneracy condition
[
24
]
:

(7)

\sup_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\frac{\diam T}{\rho_{T}}\leq\gamma_{0},

where
\gamma_{0}
is independent of
h
and
\rho_{T}
is the radius of the largest ball contained in
T
. The bound (
7
) implies the existence of a constant
C
such that the operator norm of the inverse transformation
G_{T}^{-1}
is bounded by
Ch^{-1}
. The constant in (
6
) depends on
\gamma_{0}
, among other quantities.

2.2.
Error estimates

For any
u_{h},v_{h}\in V_{h}
, define

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(u_{h},v_{h};\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},K)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\kappa(x)u_{h}(x)v_{h}(x)dx-\mathcal{Q}(u_{h},v_{h}),

where
\mathcal{Q}
is as in (
1
).

The proof of our first
O(h)
consistency error estimate uses a relatively straightforward application of the Bramble-Hilbert lemma, the basic idea being that the quadrature is exact if
\lambda u_{h}v_{h}
is a constant on each element
T
. We include a detailed version here for completeness. In later proofs, which follow a similar line of argument, we will omit some of the details included here.

Lemma 2.4
.

Assume that
\lambda_{1}\in W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)
and
\lambda_{2}\in L^{\infty}(K)
. Then, there exists a constant
C
such that for any
u_{h},v_{h}\in V_{h}
,

(i)

For each
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
, the local quadrature error satisfies

(8)

|\mathcal{E}_{T}(\kappa u_{h}v_{h})|\leq Ch\left\lVert\lambda_{1}\right\rVert_{1,\infty,T}\left\lVert\lambda_{2}\right\rVert_{0,\infty,T}\left\lVert u_{h}v_{h}\right\rVert_{1,1,T},

(ii)

The global quadrature error satisfies

(9)

|\mathcal{E}(\kappa u_{h}v_{h})|\leq Ch\left\lVert\lambda_{1}\right\rVert_{1,\infty}\left\lVert\lambda_{2}\right\rVert_{0,\infty}\left\lVert u_{h}\right\rVert_{1}\left\lVert v_{h}\right\rVert_{1}.

Proof.

We first restrict to the local error functional for
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
:

\mathcal{E}_{T}(u_{h}v_{h})=\int_{T}\kappa(x)u_{h}(x)v_{h}(x)dx-\sum_{x_{i}\in T}u_{i}v_{i}\lambda_{1}(x_{i})\int_{V_{i}\cap T}\lambda_{2}(x)1_{K}(x)dx,

and map to the reference element:

\hat{\mathcal{E}}(\hat{\lambda}_{1}\hat{u}_{h}\hat{v}_{h})=\int_{\hat{T}}\hat{\kappa}(x)\hat{u}_{h}(x)\hat{v}_{h}(x)dx-\sum_{x_{i}\in T}u_{i}v_{i}\lambda_{1}(x_{i})\int_{\widehat{V_{i}\cap T}}\hat{\lambda}_{2}(x)\hat{1}_{K}(x)dx.

Now, evidently we have the bound

|\hat{\mathcal{E}}(f)|\leq C\left\lVert\lambda_{2}\right\rVert_{0,\infty,\hat{T}}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{1,\infty,\hat{T}}

for any
f\in W^{1,\infty}(\hat{T})
. Moreover,
\hat{\mathcal{E}}(f)=0
if
f
is constant. Therefore, we can apply the Bramble-Hilbert lemma to obtain

\displaystyle|\hat{\mathcal{E}}(\hat{\lambda}_{1}\hat{u}_{h}\hat{v}_{h})|

\displaystyle\leq C\left\lVert\lambda_{2}\right\rVert_{0,\infty,T}|\hat{\lambda}_{1}\hat{u}_{h}\hat{v}_{h}|_{1,\infty,T}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\lVert\lambda_{2}\right\rVert_{0,\infty,T}(|\hat{\lambda}_{1}|_{0,\infty}|\hat{u}_{h}\hat{v}_{h}|_{1,\infty}+|\hat{\lambda}_{1}|_{1,\infty}|\hat{u}_{h}\hat{v}_{h}|_{0,\infty})

\displaystyle\leq C\left\lVert\lambda_{2}\right\rVert_{0,\infty,T}(|\hat{\lambda}_{1}|_{0,\infty}|\hat{u}_{h}\hat{v}_{h}|_{1,1}+|\hat{\lambda}_{1}|_{1,\infty}|\hat{u}_{h}\hat{v}_{h}|_{0,1})

where in the last inequality we have used the equivalence of norms on a finite-dimensional space. Thus, mapping back to
T
we apply (
5
) to obtain

\displaystyle|\mathcal{E}_{T}(u_{h}v_{h})|

\displaystyle\leq C|T||\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{1}(\hat{u}_{h}\hat{v}_{h})|

\displaystyle\leq C|T|\left\lVert\lambda_{2}\right\rVert_{0,\infty,T}(|\hat{\lambda}_{1}|_{0,\infty}|\hat{u}_{h}\hat{v}_{h}|_{1,1}+|\hat{\lambda}_{1}|_{1,\infty}|\hat{u}_{h}\hat{v}_{h}|_{0,1})

\displaystyle\leq Ch\left\lVert\lambda_{2}\right\rVert_{0,\infty,T}(|\lambda_{1}|_{0,\infty,T}|u_{h}v_{h}|_{1,1,T}+|\lambda_{1}|_{1,\infty,T}|u_{h}v_{h}|_{0,1,T})

\displaystyle\leq Ch\left\lVert\lambda_{2}\right\rVert_{0,\infty,T}\left\lVert\lambda_{1}\right\rVert_{1,\infty,T}\left\lVert u_{h}v_{h}\right\rVert_{1,1,T}

which establishes (
8
). Summing over the elements then yields the desired global error bound (
9
):

\displaystyle|\mathcal{E}(u_{h}v_{h})|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\mathcal{E}_{T}(u_{h}v_{h})\right|\leq Ch\left\lVert\lambda_{2}\right\rVert_{0,\infty,\Omega}\left\lVert\lambda_{1}\right\rVert_{1,\infty,\Omega}\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}|u_{h}v_{h}|_{1,1,T}

\displaystyle=Ch\left\lVert\lambda_{2}\right\rVert_{0,\infty,\Omega}\left\lVert\lambda_{1}\right\rVert_{1,\infty,\Omega}|u_{h}v_{h}|_{1,1,\Omega}

\displaystyle\leq Ch\left\lVert\lambda_{2}\right\rVert_{0,\infty,\Omega}\left\lVert\lambda_{1}\right\rVert_{1,\infty,\Omega}\left\lVert u_{h}\right\rVert_{1,\Omega}\left\lVert v_{h}\right\rVert_{1,\Omega}.

∎

Our next result, Lemma
2.5
, establishes a stronger local error estimate on elements that do not intersect the region of discontinuity.

Lemma 2.5
.

Assume that
\lambda\in W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)
. Then, there exists a constant
C
such that on each element
T
such that
\interior T\cap\partial K=\emptyset
,

(10)

|\mathcal{E}_{T}(\kappa u_{h}v_{h})|\leq Ch^{2}\left\lVert\lambda_{2}\right\rVert_{1,\infty,T}\left\lVert\lambda_{1}\right\rVert_{2,\infty,T}\left\lVert u_{h}\right\rVert_{1,T}\left\lVert v_{h}\right\rVert_{1,T}\quad\text{ for all }u_{h},v_{h}\in V_{h}.

Proof.

We observe that since
\interior T\cap\partial K=\emptyset
,
1_{K}(x)\equiv 0
or
1_{K}(x)\equiv 1
on
T
, i.e.,
K\cap T=\emptyset
or
T\subseteq K
. Evidently the error is zero if
K\cap T=\emptyset
, so we only consider the case
T\subseteq K
. We break the error functional
\mathcal{E}_{T}
into three parts:

\mathcal{E}_{T}(\kappa u_{h}v_{h})=\mathcal{E}_{T}^{1}(\lambda_{2},\lambda_{1}u_{h}v_{h})+\mathcal{E}_{T}^{2}(\lambda_{1}u_{h}v_{h};\lambda_{2})+\mathcal{E}_{T}^{3}(\lambda_{2},\lambda_{1}u_{h}v_{h}),

where

(11)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{T}^{1}(\lambda_{2},\lambda_{1}u_{h}v_{h})

\displaystyle=\int_{T}\lambda(x)u_{h}(x)v_{h}(x)dx

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{|T|}\left(\int_{T}\lambda_{2}(x)dx\right)\left(\int_{T}(\lambda_{1}u_{h}v_{h})(x)dx\right)

(12)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{T}^{2}(\lambda_{1}u_{h}v_{h})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|T|}\left(\int_{T}\lambda_{2}(x)dx\right)\left(\int_{T}(\lambda_{1}u_{h}v_{h})(x)dx\right)

\displaystyle-\left(\int_{T}\lambda_{2}(x)dx\right)\left(\frac{1}{N_{T}}\sum_{x_{i}\in T}\lambda_{1}(x_{i})u_{i}v_{i}\right)

(13)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{T}^{3}(\lambda_{2},\lambda_{1}u_{h}v_{h})

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{T}\lambda_{2}(x)dx\right)\left(\frac{1}{N_{T}}\sum_{x_{i}\in T}\lambda_{1}(x_{i})u_{i}v_{i}\right)

\displaystyle-\sum_{x_{i}\in T}\lambda_{1}(x_{i})u_{i}v_{i}\int_{V_{i}\cap T}\lambda_{2}(x)dx.

1.

To estimate
\mathcal{E}_{T}^{1}
, we map to the reference element
\hat{T}
to obtain the reference element error functional

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{1}(\hat{\lambda}_{2},\hat{\lambda}_{1}\hat{u}_{h}\hat{v}_{h})

\displaystyle=\int_{\hat{T}}\hat{\lambda}(x)\hat{u}_{h}(x)\hat{v}_{h}(x)dx

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\frac{1}{|\hat{T}|}\left(\int_{\hat{T}}\hat{\lambda}_{2}(x)dx\right)\left(\int_{\hat{T}}(\hat{\lambda}_{1}\hat{u}_{h}\hat{v}_{h})(x)dx\right).

Now, we observe that
\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{1}
is a bounded bilinear form on
W^{1,\infty}(\hat{T})\times W^{1,\infty}(\hat{T})
, since for any
f,g\in W^{1,\infty}(\hat{T})

\left|\hat{\mathcal{E}^{1}}(f,g)\right|\leq C\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{1,\infty,\hat{T}}\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{1,\infty,\hat{T}}.

Moreover,
\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{1}(f,g)=0
whenever either
f
or
g
is constant. It follows by the Bramble-Hilbert lemma for bilinear forms (Lemma
2.3
) that there exists a constant
C=C(\hat{T})
such that

(14)

\left|\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{1}(\hat{\lambda}_{2},\hat{\lambda}_{1}\hat{u}_{h}\hat{v}_{h})\right|\leq C|\hat{\lambda}_{2}|_{1,\infty,\hat{T}}|\hat{\lambda}_{1}\hat{u}_{h}\hat{v}_{h}|_{1,\infty,\hat{T}}.

Now, applying the product rule and the equivalence of norms on a finite-dimensional space, we have that

|\hat{\lambda}_{1}\hat{u}_{h}\hat{v}_{h}|_{1,\infty,\hat{T}}\leq C(|\hat{u}_{h}\hat{v}_{h}|_{0,1,\hat{T}}|\hat{\lambda}_{1}|_{1,\infty,\hat{T}}+|\hat{u}_{h}\hat{v}_{h}|_{1,1,\hat{T}}|\hat{\lambda}_{1}|_{0,\infty,\hat{T}}).

Substituting this bound into the estimate (
14
) and scaling between
T
and the reference element, we have

\displaystyle|\mathcal{E}_{T}^{1}(\lambda_{2},\lambda_{1}u_{h}v_{h})|

\displaystyle\leq C|T||\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{1}(\hat{\lambda}_{2},\hat{\lambda}_{1}\hat{u}_{h}\hat{v}_{h})|

\displaystyle\leq C|T||\hat{\lambda_{2}}|_{1,\infty,\hat{T}}(|\hat{u}_{h}\hat{v}_{h}|_{0,1,\hat{T}}|\hat{\lambda}_{1}|_{1,\infty,\hat{T}}+|\hat{u}_{h}\hat{v}_{h}|_{1,1,\hat{T}}|\hat{\lambda}_{1}|_{0,\infty,\hat{T}})

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{2}|\lambda_{2}|_{1,\infty,T}\left\lVert\lambda_{1}\right\rVert_{1,\infty,T}\left\lVert u_{h}v_{h}\right\rVert_{1,1,T}.

2.

The quadrature used on this step is a straightforward application of the mass-lump quadrature rule
\mathcal{M}_{h,T}
to to evaluate
\int_{T}(\lambda_{1}u_{h}v_{h})(x)dx
. It follows from standard estimates (e.g.,
[
23
]
), following a similar argument to the one from the previous lemma, that

\displaystyle|\mathcal{E}^{2}(\lambda_{1}u_{h}v_{h};\lambda_{2})|

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{2}\left\lVert\lambda_{2}\right\rVert_{0,\infty,T}\left\lVert\lambda_{1}\right\rVert_{2,\infty,T}[\left\lVert u_{h}v_{h}\right\rVert_{1,1,T}+|\diag\nabla^{2}(u_{h}v_{h})|_{0,1,T}]

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{2}\left\lVert\lambda_{2}\right\rVert_{0,\infty,T}\left\lVert\lambda_{1}\right\rVert_{2,\infty,T}\left\lVert u_{h}\right\rVert_{1,T}\left\lVert v_{h}\right\rVert_{1,T},

where
\diag\nabla^{2}(u_{h}v_{h})
indicates the diagonal part of the matrix of second derivatives of
u_{h}v_{h}
. The second inequality uses the fact that
\diag\nabla^{2}u_{h}=\diag\nabla^{2}v_{h}=0
, which is only true if
u_{h}
and
v_{h}
are multilinear.

3.

To estimate (
13
), we again map to the reference element:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{3}(\hat{\lambda}_{2},\hat{\lambda}_{1}\hat{u}_{h}\hat{v}_{h})

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{\hat{T}}\hat{\lambda}_{2}(x)dx\right)\left(\frac{1}{N_{T}}\sum_{x_{i}\in T}\lambda_{1}(x_{i})u_{i}v_{i}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\sum_{x_{i}\in T}\lambda_{1}(x_{i})u_{i}v_{i}\int_{\widehat{V_{i}\cap T}}\hat{\lambda}_{2}(x)dx,

where we note that
|\widehat{V_{i}\cap T}|=\frac{\hat{T}}{N_{T}}
by multilinearity of the volume transformation.

Again, we have for any
f,g\in W^{1,\infty}(\hat{T})
, that

\displaystyle|\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{3}(f,g)|\leq C\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{1,\infty,\hat{T}}\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{1,\infty,\hat{T}}.

Moreover,
\hat{\mathcal{E}}^{3}(f,g)
vanishes if
f
or
g
is constant, since if
g\equiv\bar{g}
we have

\displaystyle\left(\int_{\hat{T}}f(x)dx\right)\left(\frac{1}{N_{T}}\sum_{x_{i}\in T}g_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\bar{g}\int_{\hat{T}}f(x)dx

\displaystyle=\sum_{x_{i}\in T}\bar{g}\int_{\widehat{T\cap V_{i}}}f(x)dx=\sum_{x_{i}\in T}g_{i}\int_{\widehat{T\cap V_{i}}}f(x)dx,

and on the other hand if
f\equiv\bar{f}
,

\displaystyle\left(\int_{\hat{T}}f(x)dx\right)\left(\frac{1}{N_{T}}\sum_{x_{i}\in T}g_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x_{i}\in T}g_{i}\left(\bar{f}\frac{|\hat{T}|}{N_{T}}\right)=\sum_{x_{i}\in T}g_{i}\left(\int_{\widehat{T\cap V_{i}}}f(x)dx\right).

The last equality above follows from the fact that
|\widehat{T\cap V_{i}}|=\frac{|\hat{T}|}{N_{T}}.
Thus, following the same steps as in the estimate of (
11
), we obtain

\displaystyle|\mathcal{E}_{T}(\lambda_{2},\lambda_{1}u_{h}v_{h})|\leq Ch^{2}|\lambda_{2}|_{1,\infty,T}\left\lVert\lambda_{1}\right\rVert_{1,\infty,T}\left\lVert u_{h}\right\rVert_{1,T}\left\lVert v_{h}\right\rVert_{1,T}.

Finally, to finish the proof we sum the error estimates to prove (
10
).
∎

Remark 2.6
.

Proofs of various local consistency error estimates (in the
\lambda_{1}\equiv 1
case) related to Lemma
2.5
have appeared in the finite-volume-element literature. We first mention
[
10
]
, who uses a dual mesh condition (Equation (2.4.1f) in
[
10
]
) similar to
3
to estimate the difference between a finite-volume-element discretization of (
2
) and the corresponding
P^{1}
-finite element discretization in 2D. The proofs of the relevant estimates (Lemmas and Lemma 3.3.3) are valid only in two dimensions. A new proof appears in
[
20
]
(and similarly
[
27
]
), and although the authors assume that
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
their proof apparently can generalize to multiple dimensions as well and is similar in spirit to our proof of Lemma
2.5
(we mention also the related work
[
21
]
, which studies the parabolic reaction-diffusion equation). Finally, consistency estimates for the finite-volume-element discretization of the diffusion equation were explicitly generalized to multiple dimensions in
[
29
]
(see also
[
5
]
). The argument in
[
21
]
and
[
29
]
goes as follows: if
f\in W^{1,p}(\Omega)
and
v_{h}\in V_{h}
, then

\displaystyle\left|\int_{T}f(x)v_{h}(x)dx-\sum_{x_{i}\in T}v_{h}(x_{i})\int_{V_{i}\cap T}f(x)dx\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{T}[f(x)-\bar{f}_{T}]v_{h}(x)dx-\sum_{x_{i}\in T}v_{h}(x_{i})\int_{V_{i}\cap T}[f(x)-\bar{f}_{T}]dx\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|f-\bar{f}_{T}\right|_{p,T}\left|v_{h}-\sum_{x_{i}\in T}v_{h}(x_{i})1_{x\in V_{i}}\right|_{q,T}\leq Ch^{2}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{1,p,T}\left\lVert v_{h}\right\rVert_{1,q,T},

where
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=1
and
\bar{f}_{T}
is the average value of
f
on
T
. Lemma
2.5
admits a similar proof, after estimating the interpolation error for
u_{h}v_{h}\lambda_{2}(x)
onto
V_{h}
. For details we refer the reader to
[
21
,
29
]
.

Remark 2.7
.

In addition to its relationship with finite volume methods, the general idea of interpolating nonsmooth functions by averaging is related to the Clément and Scott-Zhang finite element interpolation operators
[
3
,
24
]
, which compute the projection
f_{h}
of a given function
f
onto a finite element space
V_{h}
by first computing local
L^{2}
-projections
f_{h,i}
of
f
onto
V_{h}
on a neighborhood
S_{i}
of each node
x_{i}
, and then set
f_{h}(x_{i})=f_{h,i}(x_{i})
. We see that one can think of (
1
) as just one example from a family of local averaging methods for approximating integrals of nonsmooth functions.

To take this idea further, we note that a common choice for
S_{i}
above is the nodal support of
x_{i}
,

(15)

S_{i}=\bigcup_{T\ni x_{i}}T.

If we choose
V_{i}=S_{i}
from (
15
), then the voxels now overlap (and (
3
) is not satisfied), but we can define a consistent quadrature scheme

(16)

\int_{\Omega}u_{h}(x)v_{h}(x)\lambda_{1}(x)\lambda_{2}(x)1_{K}(x)dx\approx\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{u_{i}v_{i}}{N_{T}}\lambda_{1}(x_{i})\int_{V_{i}}\lambda_{2}(x)1_{K}(x)dx,

where the factor of
N_{T}
reflects the fact that

\sum_{T\ni x_{i}}\int_{V_{i}\cap T}\lambda_{2}(x)1_{K}(x)dx=N_{T}\int_{T}\lambda_{2}(x)1_{K}(x)dx.

With trivial modifications to our paper (especially Lemma
2.5
) one could replace (
1
) with (
16
) and arrive at the same convergence results. Although we only directly consider the barycentric dual here, it may prove useful in the future to consider other averaging schemes such as the one suggested above. Indeed, (
16
) may be attractive if only because the nodal support is typically easily accessible in an unstructured mesh finite element implementation, and so the barrier to implementation is lower than for (
1
).

Remark 2.8
.

The error bounds of Lemma
2.4
and
2.5
involve
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
and
W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)
norms of
\lambda_{2}
, respectively, compared with
W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)
and
W^{2,\infty}(\Omega)
norms of
\lambda_{2}
. Therefore, we would expect that, all else being equal, choosing
\lambda_{1}\equiv 1
in (
1
) (which we refer to later as the averaging method) should result in smaller quadrature errors compared with
\lambda_{2}\equiv 1
(referred to as the lumping method), especially as the regularity of
\lambda
deteriorates. We briefly investigate this idea numerically in Section
5
, but do not observe significant differences in the errors of the two methods. While we do not claim the error bounds computed here are optimal, it does seem reasonable to expect that the averaging approximation of
\lambda_{1}
would require less regularity than the pointwise evaluation required by the lumping method, particularly in higher dimensions. We leave a more thorough investigation of this aspect of the method to future work.

3.
Refined error bounds on the interface

Let

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{K,h}

\displaystyle=\{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}:\interior T\cap K\neq\emptyset\},

\displaystyle K_{h}

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{T\in\mathcal{X}_{K,h}}T.

Then combining the local error estimates from Lemma
2.4
and
2.5
, we obtain the following global error estimate:

Corollary 3.1
.

For any
u_{h},v_{h}\in V_{h}
,

(17)

\displaystyle|\mathcal{E}(u_{h}v_{h})|

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{2}\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\setminus\mathcal{X}_{K,h}}Ch^{2}\left\lVert u_{h}\right\rVert_{2,T}\left\lVert v_{h}\right\rVert_{2,T}+Ch\sum_{T\in\mathcal{X}_{K,h}}Ch|u_{h}v_{h}|_{1,1,T}

\displaystyle=Ch^{2}\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\setminus\mathcal{X}_{K,h}}\left\lVert u_{h}\right\rVert_{2,T}\left\lVert v_{h}\right\rVert_{2,T}+Ch|u_{h}v_{h}|_{1,1,K_{h}}.

Our object in this section will be to improve upon (
17
) by studying the term
|u_{h}v_{h}|_{1,1,K_{h}}
. The
O(h)
estimate of Lemma
2.4
for the quadrature errors is the best possible on each element
T\in\mathcal{X}_{h}
, since without a mesh restriction the intersection of
K
with such elements can be arbitrary. On the other hand, if we assume that
|K_{h}|=O(h)
, then

(18)

|u_{h}v_{h}|_{1,1,K_{h}}\leq C|K_{h}|\left\lVert u_{h}\right\rVert_{1,\infty}\left\lVert v_{h}\right\rVert_{1,\infty}\leq Ch\left\lVert u_{h}\right\rVert_{1,\infty}\left\lVert v_{h}\right\rVert_{1,\infty}.

Heuristically, the idea is that the larger
O(h)
errors local to the discontinuity contribute relatively little to the global error. However, (
18
) is not very useful for finite element error analysis, which normally requires an integral norm on
v_{h}
. The following related error estimate will be used to prove a supercloseness result for reaction-drift-diffusion equations:

Theorem 3.2
.

Assume that
|K_{h}|=O(h)
. Then, for any
2\leq p\leq\infty
there exists a constant
C
such that

|\mathcal{E}(u_{h}v_{h})|\leq Ch^{\frac{3p-2}{2p}}\left\lVert u_{h}\right\rVert_{1,p}\left\lVert v_{h}\right\rVert_{1}.

Next, we turn our attention to
O(h^{2})
error estimates involving integral norms of
v_{h}
. In particular, the remainder of this section will be devoted to replacing the
W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)
norms in (
18
) with
H^{2}(\Omega)
norms. The analysis is based on the following mild assumption about the smoothness of
K
(which, incidentally, also implies
|K_{h}|=O(h)
):

Assumption 1
.

There is a Lipschitz domain
\hat{K}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
(\partial K\cap\Omega)\subseteq\partial\hat{K}
. By Lipschitz domain, we mean that
\hat{K}
is open and that for each
x\in\partial\hat{K}
, there exists an open hypercube
V\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
, a Lipschitz function
\varphi:V\to\mathbb{R}
, two constants
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\alpha<\varphi(y)<\beta
for all
y\in V
, and a linear rotation map
Q:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

(1)

Qx\in V\times[\alpha,\beta]
and

(2)

Q\hat{K}\cap[V\times(\alpha,\beta)]=\{(y,w):y\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1},\alpha<w<\varphi(y)\}
.

In other words, there is a rotation of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
described by
Q
such that
\partial\hat{K}
can be locally written as the graph of a Lipschitz function
\varphi
on some open cube
V
.

Remark 3.3
.

Assumption
1
essentially requires that the portion of
\partial K
interior to
\Omega
is a Lipschitz curve. We chose this slightly more general statement in lieu of the more straightforward assumption that
K
itself is Lipschitz in part to handle the case where
K
is
defined
as the intersection of a larger set
\hat{K}
with
\Omega
, where
\hat{K}
has a Lipschitz boundary. The distinction is necessary, since even if
\Omega
and
\hat{K}
each have smooth boundaries,
K=\hat{K}\cap\Omega
can still have cusps. The more general assumption may also be useful in the case where
\partial K
is defined by a Lipschitz level set function that can intersect
\partial\Omega
, but information about that intersection is not known a priori.

Of course, if
K
itself is known to be Lipschitz, the natural choice is to take
\hat{K}=K
(in particular, if
K
is compactly contained in
\Omega
). We note, however, that even if
K
is Lipschitz, if it shares part its boundary with
\Omega
then the constants in the error analysis may improve by choosing
\hat{K}
to be different from
K
(the extreme example being
K=\Omega
,
\hat{K}=\emptyset
).

We start our analysis by defining the distance function
d_{D}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
for any set
D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
by

d_{D}(x)=d(x,D)=\inf_{y\in D}|x-y|.

Our object will be to obtain uniform (in
h
) control over the traces of
H^{1}(\Omega)
functions on the
d_{K}
and
d_{K^{c}}
-level sets (here
K^{c}
is the complement of
K
taken relative to
\Omega
)

\Gamma_{\varepsilon}=\{x\in K:d_{K^{c}}(x)=\varepsilon\}\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad\tilde{\Gamma}_{\varepsilon}=\{x\in K^{c}:d_{K}(x)=\varepsilon\}.

In Subsection
3.1
below, we use the differentiability properties of
d_{\hat{K}}
and
d_{\hat{K}^{c}}
to characterize the normal vectors of their level sets, which contain
\Gamma_{h}
and
\tilde{\Gamma}_{h}
.

3.1.
Uniform trace inequalities

In this subsection, we will establish several facts about general Lipschitz domains
D
, that we later apply to
\hat{K}
. First, the normal vectors of
D
can be directly related to the constant appearing in the trace inequality for
D
via the following elementary lemma, appearing in
[
9
]
:

Lemma 3.4

(Characterization of trace constant)
.

Let
D
be a Lipschitz domain. Then

(1)

There exists a vector field
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{D}\in W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
and constant
\delta_{D}>0
such that

(19)

\boldsymbol{\mu}_{D}(x)\cdot\mathbf{n}_{D}(x)>\delta_{D}\quad\textrm{a.e. $x\in\partial D$},

where
\mathbf{n}_{D}
is the outward pointing normal on
\partial D
.

(2)

The constant
C_{D}
in the trace inequality for
D
is bounded by
(\delta_{D})^{-1}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\mu}_{D}\right\rVert_{1,\infty,D}
for any vector field
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{D}\in W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfying (
19
). That is, there is a constant
C(p)>0
independent of
D
such that for any
f\in W^{1,p}(D)
, we have

\delta_{D}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{0,p,\partial D}\leq C(p)\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\mu}_{D}\right\rVert_{1,\infty,D}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{1,p,D}.

Proof.

This is essentially Lemmas 1.5.1.9 and 1.5.1.10 from
[
9
]
, except that we replace their
C^{\infty}(D;\mathbb{R}^{d})
vector field with a
W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
extension.
∎

To state the next lemma, we introduce the projection
P_{D}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\overline{D}
as

P_{D}(x)=\argmin_{y\in\overline{D}}|x-y|.

Note that
P_{D}
is only well-defined at almost every point
x\in\overline{D}^{c}
, since the set
\{y\in\overline{D}:|x-y|=d_{D}(x)\}
may have more than one member. At such
x
, we will show that the direction vector
x-P_{D}(x)
from
x
to
P_{D}(x)
can be written as a nonnegative linear combination of normal vectors
\mathbf{n}_{D}
of
D
infinitesimally close to
P_{D}(x)
. Crucially, this relationship holds even when
\mathbf{n}_{D}
is not defined at

— although
\mathbf{n}_{D}

P_{D}(x)
on an open subset of

. If
D
is a

domain, then
P_{D}
is well-defined for
\varepsilon
sufficiently small and
\frac{x-P_{D}(x)}{|x-P_{D}(x)|}=\mathbf{n}_{D}(P_{D}(x))
, a result that has already been proven in many different sources (see, e.g.,
[

]
, Theorem 14.16). The novelty of Lemma

stems from the weakening of the

hypothesis.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
D
be a Lipschitz domain with outward pointing normal
\mathbf{n}_{D}
. If
x\notin\overline{D}
and the projection
P_{D}(x)
of

is well-defined, then

\frac{x-P_{D}(x)}{|x-P_{D}(x)|}=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{m}\alpha_{j}\lim_{i\to\infty}\mathbf{n}_{D}(x_{i}^{(j)})}{\left|\sum_{j=1}^{m}\alpha_{j}\lim_{i\to\infty}\mathbf{n}_{D}(x_{i}^{(j)})\right|}

where
\alpha_{j}\geq 0
and each
x_{i}^{(j)}\in\partial D
is a sequence such that
x_{i}^{(j)}\to P_{D}(x)
and
\mathbf{n}_{D}(x_{i}^{(j)})
converges as

.

Proof.

See
Appendix
.
∎

Before proving the uniform trace inequality, we collect a few more basic properties of
d_{D}
, which help tie Lemmas
3.4
and
3.5
together by characterizing the normal vectors
\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon}(x)
to the
\varepsilon
-level sets of
d_{D}
as being almost-everywhere parallel to the direction vector
x-P_{D}(x)
. Define the signed distance function
\rho:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
by

\rho(x)=\begin{cases}d_{D}(x)&x\in D^{c}\\
-d_{D^{c}}(x)&x\in D.\end{cases}

Further define, for all
\varepsilon>0
,

U_{\varepsilon}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\rho(x)<\varepsilon\},\qquad L_{\varepsilon}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\rho(x)<-\varepsilon\}

so that

\partial U_{\varepsilon}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\rho(x)=\varepsilon\},\qquad\partial L_{\varepsilon}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\rho(x)=-\varepsilon\}.

According to
[
4
]
, Theorem 4.1,
U_{\varepsilon}
and
L_{\varepsilon}
are Lipschitz domains for
\varepsilon
sufficiently small, and therefore have a.e. well-defined normal vectors. First considering
U_{\varepsilon}
, we can characterize those normal vectors as follows:

(1)

\rho
is differentiable at
x\in\overline{D}^{c}
if and only if the projection
P_{D}(x)
is single-valued (see, e.g.,
[
6
]
); at any such point
x
we have
\nabla d_{D}(x)=\frac{x-P_{D}(x)}{|x-P_{D}(x)|}
.

(2)

Since
d_{D}
is Lipschitz, the set of points where
d_{D}
is not differentiable is measure zero in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
; it follows that for almost every
\varepsilon>0
,
d_{D}
is differentiable almost everywhere on the
\varepsilon
level set of
\rho
,
\partial U_{\varepsilon}
.

(3)

At such
\varepsilon
, we conclude from (1) and (2) that the outward pointing normal vector
\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon}(x)
for
U_{\varepsilon}
coincides almost everywhere with
\frac{x-P_{D}(x)}{|x-P_{D}(x)|}
.

Following a similar argument, we can show that the outward pointing normal vector of
L_{\varepsilon}
coincides almost everywhere with
\frac{P_{D^{c}}(x)-x}{|P_{D^{c}}(x)-x|}
.

Lemma 3.6

(Uniform trace inequality)
.

There exists
\varepsilon_{0}>0
, such that for almost every
\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}
, the constants
C_{U_{\varepsilon}}
and
C_{L_{\varepsilon}}
in the trace inequalities

\left(\int_{\partial U_{\varepsilon}}|f|^{p}dS(x)\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\leq C_{U_{\varepsilon}}(p)\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{1,p,U_{\varepsilon}}\qquad\left(\int_{\partial L_{\varepsilon}}|f|^{p}dS(x)\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\leq C_{L_{\varepsilon}}(p)\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{1,p,L_{\varepsilon}}

can be chosen independent of
\varepsilon
. That is, there exists
C_{\varepsilon_{0}}(p)
such that
C_{U_{\varepsilon}},C_{L_{\varepsilon}}\leq C_{\varepsilon_{0}}(p)
for almost every
\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}
. Moreover, by choosing
\varepsilon_{0}
sufficiently small,
C_{\varepsilon_{0}}
can be chosen arbitrarily close to
\frac{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\mu}\right\rVert_{1,\infty,\mathbb{R}^{d}}}{\delta}
, where
\boldsymbol{\mu}=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{D}
and
\delta=\delta_{D}
are any vector field and positive constant satisfying (
19
) from Lemma
3.4
.

Proof.

We will prove the inequality for
U_{\varepsilon}
, since the proof for
L_{\varepsilon}
is completely analogous using the result of Lemma
3.4
as below and applying Lemma
3.5
to
\overline{D}^{c}
.

To prove the uniform trace theorem for
U_{\varepsilon}
, we will use Lemma
3.4
to show that there exists
\varepsilon_{0}
such that, for almost every
\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}
,
\boldsymbol{\mu}
and
\delta
satisfy the inequality from (
19
) for almost every
x\in U_{\varepsilon}
. To that end, we choose
\varepsilon_{0}
such that
U_{\varepsilon}
is Lipschitz for
\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}
and so that
\varepsilon_{0}<\frac{\delta}{|\boldsymbol{\mu}|_{1,\infty,\mathbb{R}^{d}}}
.

Let
\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}
be such that
\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon}(x)=\frac{x-P_{D}(x)}{|x-P_{D}(x)|}
for almost every
x\in\partial U_{\varepsilon}
, and let
x\in\partial U_{\varepsilon}
be such a point. Then by Lemma
3.5
, there exist sequences
x^{(j)}_{i}\to P_{D}(x)
and constants
\alpha_{j}\geq 0
,
j=1,2,...,m
, such that

\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon}(x)=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{m}\alpha_{j}\lim_{i\to\infty}\mathbf{n}_{D}(x_{i}^{(j)})}{\left|\sum_{j=1}^{m}\alpha_{j}\lim_{i\to\infty}\mathbf{n}_{D}(x_{i}^{(j)})\right|},

Using the continuity of
\boldsymbol{\mu}
, we have that
\lim_{i\to\infty}\boldsymbol{\mu}(P_{D}(x))\cdot\mathbf{n}_{D}(x_{i}^{(j)})>\delta
, so the triangle inequality implies

\boldsymbol{\mu}(P_{D}(x))\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon}(x)=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{m}\alpha_{j}\lim_{i\to\infty}\boldsymbol{\mu}(P_{D}(x))\cdot\mathbf{n}_{D}(x_{i}^{(j)})}{\left|\sum_{j=1}^{m}\alpha_{j}\lim_{i\to\infty}\mathbf{n}_{D}(x_{i}^{(j)})\right|}>\delta.

Thus finally

\displaystyle\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon}(x)\cdot\boldsymbol{\mu}(x)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon}(x)\cdot\boldsymbol{\mu}(P_{D}(x))+\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon}(x)\cdot[\boldsymbol{\mu}(x)-\boldsymbol{\mu}(P_{D}(x))]

\displaystyle>\delta-\varepsilon_{0}|\boldsymbol{\mu}|_{1,\infty,\mathbb{R}^{d}}>0.

Hence, applying Lemma
3.4
the constant in the trace inequality can be chosen uniformly as
C_{\varepsilon_{0}}(p)=C(p)\frac{|\boldsymbol{\mu}|_{1,\infty,\mathbb{R}^{d}}}{\delta-\varepsilon_{0}|\boldsymbol{\mu}|_{1,\infty,\mathbb{R}^{d}}}
for
\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}
. As
\varepsilon_{0}\to 0
, the upper bound
C(p)\frac{|\boldsymbol{\mu}|_{1,\infty,\mathbb{R}^{d}}}{\delta-\varepsilon_{0}|\boldsymbol{\mu}|_{1,\infty,\mathbb{R}^{d}}}
converges to
C(p)\frac{|\boldsymbol{\mu}|_{1,\infty,\mathbb{R}^{d}}}{\delta},
as claimed.
∎

3.2.
A refined error bound

Finally, with the help of Lemma
3.6
, we can now improve the error bound near
\partial K
in the situation when
u_{h}
and
v_{h}
are
V_{h}
approximations of functions in

. First, we show how the Hölder inequality employed for Theorem

can be formally replaced with a trace inequality, trading off derivatives instead of powers of
p
.

Proposition 3.8
.

Assume that
f\in W^{1,p}(\Omega)
. Then, there exists
h_{0}
and
C=C(h_{0},p)>0
such that for all
h<h_{0}
,

|f|_{0,p,K_{h}}\leq Ch^{\frac{1}{p}}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{1,p,\Omega}.

Proof.

In the following, we define
\rho
,
U_{\varepsilon}
, and
L_{\varepsilon}
as in the previous subsection with
D=\hat{K}
. Observe that for each
h
,

K_{h}\subseteq\bigcup_{-h<\varepsilon<h}\{\rho(x)=\varepsilon\}=\bigcup_{0\leq\varepsilon<h}\partial U_{\varepsilon}\cup\partial L_{\varepsilon}=:X_{h}.

Let
h_{0}
be a constant such that the uniform trace inequality of Lemma
3.6
applies on
U_{\varepsilon}
and
L_{\varepsilon}
for
h<h_{0}
, and let
{\Omega}_{\textrm{ext}}=\Omega\cup X_{h_{0}}
. By the Sobolev extension theorem, there exists an extension
{f}_{\textrm{ext}}\in W^{1,p}({\Omega}_{\textrm{ext}})
of
f
such that
\left\lVert{f}_{\textrm{ext}}\right\rVert_{1,p,{\Omega}_{\textrm{ext}}}\leq C\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{1,p,\Omega}
, where
C
is independent of
f
. Therefore, noting that
\rho
is Lipschitz with constant 1, the co-area formula implies that

|f|_{0,p,K_{h}}\leq|{f}_{\textrm{ext}}|_{0,p,X_{h}}=\left(\int_{-h}^{h}\int_{\{\rho=\varepsilon\}}|{f}_{\textrm{ext}}|^{p}dS_{\varepsilon}d\varepsilon\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}.

Applying the uniform trace inequality on
\{\rho=\varepsilon\}
makes the integrand independent of
h
, so that

\left(\int_{-h}^{h}\int_{\{\rho=\varepsilon\}}|{f}_{\textrm{ext}}|^{p}dS_{\varepsilon}d\varepsilon\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\leq C(h_{0},p)\left(\int_{-h}^{h}\left\lVert{f}_{\textrm{ext}}\right\rVert_{1,p,{\Omega}_{\textrm{ext}}}^{p}d\varepsilon\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\leq Ch^{\frac{1}{p}}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{1,p,\Omega},

as needed.
∎

Remark 3.9
.

We mention here a few results related to Proposition

Theorem 3.10
.

Assume that
K
satisfies Assumption

. Then, there exists

\displaystyle|u_{h}w_{h}|_{1,1,K_{h}}\leq C[\left\lVert u-u_{h}\right\rVert_{1,K_{h}}\left\lVert w-w_{h}\right\rVert_{1,K_{h}}

\displaystyle+h^{\frac{1}{2}}\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{2,\Omega}\left\lVert w-w_{h}\right\rVert_{1,K_{h}}

\displaystyle+h^{\frac{1}{2}}\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{2,\Omega}\left\lVert u-u_{h}\right\rVert_{1,K_{h}}+h\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{2,\Omega}\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{2,\Omega}].

The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality implies that

(20)

\displaystyle\left\lVert u_{h}\right\rVert_{1,K_{h}}\left\lVert v_{h}\right\rVert_{1,K_{h}}

For

3.8
to

for

4.
Convergence results and monotonicity for reaction-drift-diffusion equations

Starting from the weak form of (
2
), we denote the exact solution
u\in H^{1}_{D}(\Omega)
as the unique solution to

(23)

\displaystyle a(u,v)

\displaystyle=-r(u,v)+\left<f,v\right>,\quad\text{for all }v\in H^{1}_{D}(\Omega),

where
H^{1}_{D}(\Omega)
is the subspace of functions in
H^{1}(\Omega)
with zero trace on
\Gamma_{D}
. We assume for simplicity homogeneous boundary conditions (i.e.,
g_{D},g_{N}\equiv 0
).

We will assume that the mesh
\mathcal{T}_{h}
is aligned with
\Gamma_{D}
and
\Gamma_{N}
, so that we can incorporate the Dirichlet conditions into
V_{h}
by removing the basis functions with nodes touching the Dirichlet boundary. Then, we can define the Galerkin finite element solution
u_{h}^{G}
associated with
V_{h}
as the solution of reaction-diffusion equation

(24)

\displaystyle a(u_{h}^{G},v_{h})

\displaystyle=-r(u_{h}^{G},v_{h})+\left<f,v_{h}\right>,\quad\text{for all }v_{h}\in V_{h},

where
\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>
is the
L^{2}
inner product on
\Omega
.

Finally, our quadrature approximation to (
2
) is given by

(25)

\displaystyle a_{h}(u_{h},v_{h})

\displaystyle=-r_{h}(u_{h},v_{h})+\left<f,v_{h}\right>,\quad\text{for all }v_{h}\in V_{h}

where
a_{h}(u_{h},v_{h})
is a quadrature approximation to the drift-diffusion operator. To state error estimates for the quadrature approximation (
2
), we will need to assume that the discrete drift-diffusion operator
a_{h}
satisfies similar approximation properties to
r_{h}
. In particular, we assume that

(26)

\displaystyle|a(w_{h},v_{h})-a_{h}(w_{h},v_{h})|

\displaystyle\leq Ch\left\lVert w_{h}\right\rVert_{1}\left\lVert v_{h}\right\rVert_{1}\quad

\displaystyle\text{for all $w_{h},v_{h}\in V_{h}$},

\displaystyle|a(w_{h},v_{h})-a_{h}(w_{h},v_{h})|

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{\frac{3p-2}{2p}}\left\lVert w_{h}\right\rVert_{1,p}\left\lVert v_{h}\right\rVert_{1}\quad

\displaystyle\text{for all $w_{h},v_{h}\in V_{h}$},

\displaystyle|a(u_{h},w_{h})-a_{h}(u_{h},w_{h})|

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{2}\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{2},

where in the last line
u_{h}
and
w_{h}
again satisfy the asymptotic error estimates (
21
).

We also assume that
\alpha
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}
are continuous on
\overline{\Omega}
, and in addition that
\alpha
is positive definite. These conditions are not necessarily optimal, and in future work we hope to relax our assumptions somewhat to allow discontinuous or singular coefficients
\boldsymbol{\beta}
. However, for our purposes in this work the current assumptions are enough to imply the coercivity condition for
w_{h}\in V_{h}
:

(27)

\displaystyle\sup_{v_{h}\in V_{h}}\frac{a_{h}(w_{h},v_{h})+r_{h}(w,v_{h})}{\left\lVert v_{h}\right\rVert_{1}}>c_{0}\left\lVert w_{h}\right\rVert_{1}

where
c_{0}>0
is independent of
h
. Therefore, there is a unique solution each problem associated with (
25
).

Using the convergence estimates of Corollaries
3.1
and
3.11
, the error analysis for (
25
) is fairly routine. We give convergence and superconvergence results in the
H^{1}(\Omega)
norm in Theorem
4.1
, and an
L^{2}(\Omega)
convergence proof in Theorem
4.3
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Given Assumption
1
and the inf-sup condition
27
, there exists
C>0
such that

\left\lVert u^{G}_{h}-u_{h}\right\rVert_{1}\leq Ch\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{1},\qquad\left\lVert u-u_{h}\right\rVert_{1}\leq C\inf_{v_{h}\in V_{h}}\left\lVert v_{h}-u\right\rVert_{1}+Ch\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{1}.

If
u\in W^{1,p}(\Omega)
for some
2<p\leq\infty
and there exists an interpolation operator
I_{h}
satisfying (
6
), then we have the strengthened supercloseness estimate

\left\lVert u^{G}_{h}-u_{h}\right\rVert_{1}\leq Ch^{\frac{3p-2}{2p}}\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{1,p}.

Proof.

The first result essentially follows from the first Strang lemma combined with Lemma
2.4
and (
26

\displaystyle c_{0}\left\lVert u_{h}^{G}-u_{h}\right\rVert_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{v_{h}\in V_{h}}\frac{a_{h}(u_{h}-u_{h}^{G},v_{h})+r_{h}(u_{h}-u_{h}^{G},v_{h})}{\left\lVert v_{h}\right\rVert_{1}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{v_{h}\in V_{h}}\frac{\left<f,v_{h}\right>-a_{h}(u_{h}^{G},v_{h})-r_{h}(u_{h}^{G},v_{h})}{\left\lVert v_{h}\right\rVert_{1}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{v_{h}\in V_{h}}\frac{a(u_{h}^{G},v_{h})+r(u_{h}^{G},v_{h})-a_{h}(u_{h}^{G},v_{h})-r_{h}(u_{h}^{G},v_{h})}{\left\lVert v_{h}\right\rVert_{1}}

\displaystyle\leq Ch\left\lVert u_{h}^{G}\right\rVert_{1}.

Since standard estimates imply
\left\lVert u_{h}^{G}-u\right\rVert_{1}\leq C\inf_{v_{h}\in V_{h}}\left\lVert v_{h}-u\right\rVert_{1}
, the first result follows.

To prove the strengthened estimates, we simply modify the last line of the above computation by applying Theorem

and (

\displaystyle c_{0}\left\lVert u_{h}^{G}-u_{h}\right\rVert_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{v_{h}\in V_{h}}\frac{a(u_{h}^{G},v_{h})+r(u_{h}^{G},v_{h})-a_{h}(u_{h}^{G},v_{h})-r_{h}(u_{h}^{G},v_{h})}{\left\lVert v_{h}\right\rVert_{1}}

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{\frac{3p-2}{2p}}\left\lVert u_{h}^{G}\right\rVert_{1,p}\leq Ch^{\frac{3p-2}{2p}}\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{1,p},

where the last inequality again follows from the standard estimate
\left\lVert u_{h}^{G}\right\rVert_{1,p}\leq C\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{1,p}
for the Galerkin solution.

Remark 4.2
.

Clearly, if the Galerkin solution is superconvergent — that is, there is a constant
C(u)
such that
\left\lVert u^{G}_{h}-I_{h}u\right\rVert_{1}\leq C(u)h^{1+s}
for some
s>0
— then Theorem

implies that

is also superconvergent up to a maximum order of

when

. If the reaction coefficient is smooth instead of discontinuous, then we obtain the optimal result
\left\lVert u_{h}^{G}-u_{h}\right\rVert_{1}\leq Ch^{2}\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{1}
. Therefore, Theorem
4.1
reveals that we expect to lose at least half an order of accuracy in the discontinuous case if our result is sharp. We provide numerical evidence to support the theoretical
O(h^{\frac{3}{2}})
rate in Section
5
.

The next theorem, which gives an
L^{2}
-estimate, further requires that the solution
u\in H^{2}(\Omega)
, and that the adjoint problem

a(v,w_{\chi})=\left<\chi,v\right>\qquad\text{for all }v\in H^{1}_{D}(\Omega)

is
L^{2}
-regular in the sense that if
\chi\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, then
w_{\chi}\in H^{2}(\Omega)
and there exists a constant
C>0
such that
\left\lVert w_{\chi}\right\rVert_{2}\leq C|\chi|_{0}
. We will also assume the existence of an interpolation operator
I_{h}
satisfying (
6

Theorem 4.3
.

Assume the hypotheses stated above, i.e., that
u\in H^{2}(\Omega)
and that the adjoint problem is
L^{2}
-regular. Then, there exists
C>0
such that

|u-u_{h}|_{0}\leq Ch^{2}\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{2}.

Proof.

Let
w_{\chi}
be the solution of the adjoint problem for
\chi
. Then we have

\displaystyle\left<u_{h}-u,\chi\right>

\displaystyle=a(u_{h}-u,w_{\chi})

\displaystyle=a(u_{h}-u,w_{\chi}-I_{h}w_{\chi})+a(u_{h}-u,I_{h}w_{\chi})

\displaystyle=E_{1}+a(u_{h}-u,I_{h}w_{\chi})-a_{h}(u_{h},I_{h}w_{\chi})+a(u,I_{h}w_{\chi})

\displaystyle=E_{1}+a(u_{h},I_{h}w_{\chi})-a_{h}(u_{h},I_{h}w_{\chi})

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert u-u_{h}\right\rVert_{1}\left\lVert w-I_{h}w_{\chi}\right\rVert_{1}+Ch^{2}\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{2}\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{2}.

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{2}\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{2}|\chi|_{0}.

∎

4.1.
Tensor products and a fully monotone scheme

After their introduction at the beginning of Section
2
, the fact that the discretized domain is a tensor product of simplical meshes instead of a single simplical mesh has mostly faded into the background, because the essential properties (e.g., (
3
)) satisfied by the dual mesh on the tensor product mesh are inherited from its components. In the same way, our goal in this section will be to formally show how to produce a quadrature rule for the drift-diffusion operator from quadrature rules defined on each tensor product component, such that the tensor product discretization is monotone (or more narrowly, produces an
M
-matrix) whenever the component discretizations are. Our prime example is the edge-average finite element (EAFE) method. We will keep our explanations brief and narrowly focused on describing the extension of EAFE and other monotone schemes to tensor product meshes. We refer the reader to
[
28
]
and the references therein for details on EAFE and the concept of monotonicity.

A monotone scheme for discretizing the reaction-drift-diffusion operator produces a stiffness matrix
A
such that
A^{-1}\mathbf{f}
is a vector with nonnegative entries whenever
\mathbf{f}
has nonnegative entries. A sufficient condition for monotonicity is that
A
is an
M
-matrix. The finite element Laplacian matrix for
P^{1}
simplical elements is an
M
-matrix, and therefore monotone, whenever its off-diagonals are nonnegative, leading to the mesh condition described in
[
28
]
. If the finite element Laplacian is an
M
-matrix, then the EAFE approximation of the drift-diffusion operator is also an
M
-matrix, and it remains so when perturbed by a nonnegative diagonal approximation of the reaction operator. In
[
28
]
, the nonnegative diagonalization of the reaction operator is done using mass lumping, whereas here we have introduced our new quadrature rule (
1
) which can be applied for piecewise smooth functions.

Remark 4.4
.

The mesh condition mentioned above is more restrictive in 3D than in 2D, since for the latter it suffices for the triangulation to be Delaunay. A subject for future research will be to investigate combining meshing techniques (e.g., using cut cells and related ideas) with modifications to EAFE to more easily produce an
M
-matrix even in the 3D case.

We now extend the monotonicity property of the drift-diffusion operator to tensor products. To illustrate the basic idea, we return to the notation of Section
2
and assume that the diffusion coefficient has the diagonal form

\alpha(x)=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{1}(x)I_{d_{1}}&&&\\
&\alpha_{2}(x)I_{d_{2}}&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&&&\alpha_{J}(x)I_{d_{J}}\end{pmatrix}\qquad\boldsymbol{\beta}=\left[\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}(x),\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}(x),...,\boldsymbol{\beta}_{J}(x)\right],

where
I_{d_{j}}
is the
d_{j}\times d_{j}
identity matrix and
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{j}}
. For PBSRD systems, each
\alpha_{j}
would represent the diffusivity for a particular particle. The Galerkin finite element discretization of the drift-diffusion operator reads:

A=\sum_{j=1}^{J}M^{1}\otimes M^{2}\otimes...\otimes M^{j-1}\otimes A^{j}\otimes M^{j+1}\otimes...\otimes M^{J},

where
\otimes
represents a Kronecker product,
A^{j}
discretizes the drift-diffusion operator
\nabla_{x^{(j)}}\cdot[\alpha_{j}(x)\nabla_{x^{(j)}}u+u\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}(x)]
on
\mathcal{T}_{h}^{(j)}
, and
M^{j}
is the
P^{1}
finite element mass matrix on
\mathcal{T}_{h}^{(j)}
.

To produce a monotone scheme, we simply replace each
M^{i}
with its lumped counterpart,
\tilde{M}^{i}
, and replace the drift-diffusion operators
A^{j}
with a quadrature approximation producing inverse-positive approximations
\tilde{A}^{j}
of
A^{j}
. In terms of quadrature rules, the procedure described above involves expanding the drift-diffusion operator into a sum over component operators, and then applying mass lumping on each variable except those whose derivatives appear in that term. Below we give an example of such a derivation.

Example 4.5
.

When
J=2
, for example, we start with a two term expansion and then mass-lump each term in the variables which are not being differentiated:

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2}}

\displaystyle[\alpha(x,y)\nabla u_{h}(x,y)+u_{h}(x,y)\boldsymbol{\beta}(x,y)]\cdot\nabla v_{h}(x,y)dxdy

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2}}[\alpha_{1}(x,y)\nabla_{x}u_{h}(x,y)+u_{h}(x,y)\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}(x,y)]\cdot\nabla_{x}v_{h}(x,y)dxdy

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2}}[\alpha_{2}(x,y)\nabla_{y}u_{h}(x,y)+u_{h}(x,y)\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}(x,y)]\cdot\nabla_{y}(x,y)dxdy

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{i=1}^{N_{2}}|V_{i}^{(2)}|\int_{\Omega_{1}}[\alpha_{1}(x,x_{i}^{(2)})\nabla_{x}u_{h}(x,x_{i}^{(2)})+u_{h}(x,x_{i}^{(2)})\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}(x,x_{i}^{(2)})]\cdot\nabla_{x}v_{h}(x,x^{(2)}_{i})dx

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega_{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{1}}|V_{i}^{(1)}|[\alpha_{2}(x_{i}^{(1)},y)\nabla_{y}u_{h}(x_{i}^{(1)},y)+u_{h}(x_{i}^{(1)},y)\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}(x_{i}^{(1)},y)]\cdot\nabla_{y}v_{h}(x_{i}^{(1)},y)dy

Then, we apply mesh-dependent monotone discretizations
a^{(1)}_{h}
and
a^{(2)}_{h}
to the resulting component drift-diffusion operators:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N_{2}}|V_{i}^{(2)}|\int_{\Omega_{1}}[\alpha_{1}(x,x_{i}^{(2)})\nabla_{x}u_{h}(x,x_{i}^{(2)})+u_{h}(x,x_{i}^{(2)})\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}(x,x_{i}^{(2)})]\cdot\nabla_{x}v_{h}(x,x^{(2)}_{i})dx

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega_{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{1}}|V_{i}^{(1)}|[\alpha_{2}(x_{i}^{(1)},y)\nabla_{y}u_{h}(x_{i}^{(1)},y)+u_{h}(x_{i}^{(1)},y)\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}(x_{i}^{(1)},y)]\cdot\nabla_{y}v_{h}(x_{i}^{(1)},y)dy

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{i=1}^{N_{2}}|V_{i}^{(2)}|a_{h}^{(1)}\left(u_{h}(\cdot,x_{i}^{(2)}),v_{h}(\cdot,x_{i}^{(2)})\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{N_{1}}|V_{i}^{(1)}|a_{h}^{(2)}\left(u_{h}(x_{(i)}^{(1)},\cdot),v_{h}(x_{i}^{(1)},\cdot)\right).

Note that a similar derivation to the one here appears in
[
11
]
in the context of PBSRD simulation.

Our discretization for the drift-diffusion operator is easily shown to be monotone, since it produces a block-diagonal matrix with monotone blocks. The stiffness matrix is also an
M
-matrix if the blocks are
M
-matrices. One can also easily show that if the block discretizations
a_{h}^{(i)}
satisfy appropriate consistency error estimates (
26
), then so do the bilinear forms associated with the tensor-product problem.

For the EAFE method that we use for our numerical examples in Section
5
, only the first estimate from (
26
) has been proven in arbitrary dimensions
[
28
]
. Other works (e.g.,
[
7
]
) have given numerical evidence that the EAFE method is second order accurate in the
L^{2}
-norm in two dimensions when the advection coefficient
\boldsymbol{\beta}
is a potential field, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{\beta}=\nabla\psi
for some
\psi\in H^{1}(\Omega)
. We also mention here that in our own numerical experiments, when
\boldsymbol{\beta}=\nabla\psi
the local errors (quadrature errors on each element) are
O(h)
, which is the result shown in
[
22
,
28
]
. The global errors, however, are empirically
O(h^{2})
. Finally, in one dimension one can actually prove stronger convergence results for EAFE along the lines of (
26
)
[
13
,
19
]
. This implies that EAFE is second-order accurate when applied dimension-by-dimension on a structured grid as described above, which extends the results of, e.g.,
[
13
,
19
]
for one- and two-dimensional problems on structured grids. We leave it to future work to show that, in the general case, EAFE satisfies all of the estimates (
26
).

6.
Conclusion

We have proven convergence estimates for a quadrature scheme for discontinuous integrands, which we applied to construct a monotone finite-element discretization of scalar reaction-drift-diffusion equations with discontinuous coefficients. The quadrature scheme was originally intended and successfully used for discretizing systems of reaction-drift-diffusion equations related to PBSRD simulation
[
16
,
14
,
15
,
11
]
, and our work justifies theoretically the empirical
O(h^{2})
convergence estimates observed in those works. More generally, our work shows that incorporating averaging-type quadrature schemes into low-order finite-element methods for PDEs with irregular coefficients can recover additional orders of convergence beyond what can be recovered using a traditional element-by-element analysis of the quadrature errors.

Several possible modifications and extensions of our work, some of which are mentioned earlier in the paper, include:

•

Rigorous comparisons of the averaging and lumping schemes.

•

Exploration of alternative definitions of the dual mesh such as (
15
).

•

Proof of second-order accuracy for the EAFE discretization.

•

Incorporation of our results into CRDME convergence proofs (i.e., convergence of the discretization described in
[
11
]
).

•

Extension of our results to include irregularities in other coefficients, such as the drift coefficient
\boldsymbol{\beta}
.

Proof of Lemma
3.5
.

Up to a rotation of the domain, there exists a Lipschitz continuous parametrization
(y,\varphi(y))
,
y\in V\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
of
\partial K
around
P_{K}(x)
such that
V\cap\Omega=\{(y,y_{n}):y_{n}<\varphi(y)\}
. We have that
P_{K}(x)=(y^{*},\varphi(y^{*}))
, where

y^{*}=\argmin_{y\in V}\Phi(y):=\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\left<y,\varphi(y)\right>-x\right\rVert^{2}.

Since
\Phi
is Lipschitz continuous and
y^{*}
is a critical point, we have that
0\in\partial\Phi(y^{*})
, where

\displaystyle\partial\Phi(y^{*})=\conv\left\{\lim_{i\to\infty}\nabla\Phi(y_{i}):\text{$(y_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}\subseteq W$, $y_{i}\to y^{*}$, $\nabla\Phi(y_{i})$ converges}\right\}

is the Clarke subdifferential of
\Phi
at
y^{*}
with
W
denoting the set of points in
V
where
\Phi
is differentiable (see
[
2
]
, p. 27 for the definition of the Clarke subdifferential, Theorem 2.5.1 for the relevant characterization, and Proposition 2.3.2 for the relationship to local extrema). Now, at any
y
such that
\varphi
is differentiable, the gradient of
\Phi
is given by

\nabla\Phi(y)=y-\tilde{x}+\nabla\varphi(y)(\varphi(y)-x_{n}),

where we use
\tilde{x}
to denote the projection of
x
into
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
. Using this formula for the gradient and the characterization of
\partial\Phi(y^{*})
given above, the inclusion
0\in\partial\Phi(y^{*})
can be rewritten as

\tilde{x}-y^{*}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\beta_{j}(\lim_{i\to\infty}-\nabla\varphi(y_{i}))(x_{n}-\varphi(y^{*}))

for some scalars
\beta_{j}\geq 0
with
\sum_{j=1}^{m}\beta_{j}=1
. Using the fact that
n_{K}(y_{i},\varphi(y_{i}))=\frac{\left<-\nabla\varphi(y_{i}),1\right>}{\sqrt{\left\lVert\nabla\varphi(y_{i})\right\rVert^{2}+1}}
, the above is evidently equivalent to

x-P_{K}(x)=(x_{n}-\varphi(y^{*}))\sum_{j=1}^{m}\beta_{j}\lim_{i\to\infty}\left<-\nabla\varphi(y_{i}),1\right>=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\beta_{j}c_{j}\lim_{i\to\infty}n_{K}(y_{i},\varphi(y_{i})),

where
c_{j}=\lim_{i\to\infty}\sqrt{\left\lVert\nabla\varphi(y_{i})\right\rVert^{2}+1}
. That the righthand side is nonzero follows from the fact that
x-P_{K}(x)\neq 0
. Therefore, setting
\alpha_{j}=\beta_{j}c_{j}
and normalizing both sides yields the result.
∎
