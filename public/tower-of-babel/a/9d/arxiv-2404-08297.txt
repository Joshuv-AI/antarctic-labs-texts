Towards a representer theorem for identification of passive systems
B. M. Shali, and, H. J. van Waarde

Abstract
A major problem in system identification is the incorporation of prior knowledge about the physical properties of the given system, such as stability, positivity and passivity. In this paper, we present first steps towards tackling this problem for passive systems. In particular, using ideas from the theory of reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces, we solve the problem of identifying a nonnegative input-output operator from data consisting of input-output trajectories of the system. We prove a representer theorem for this problem in the case where the input space is finite-dimensional. This provides a computationally tractable solution, which we show can be obtained by solving an associated semidefinite program.



I
Introduction

System identification is the process of obtaining a mathematical model that accurately describes the behaviour of a dynamical system based on empirical observations. System identification has a rich history in the field of systems and control. The classical approach is to search within a given class of finite-dimensional parametric models, e.g., in autoregressive or state-space form. Recently, inspired by developments in machine learning, a new paradigm for system identification has emerged
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
]
. The latter uses the theory of reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces
[
5
,
6
]
and formulates system identification as function (operator) estimation in a possibly infinite-dimensional reproducing kernel Hilbert space. A key aspect of this paradigm is the
representer
theorem, which allows for a computationally tractable solution to this estimation problem.

A major challenge in system identification is the incorporation of prior knowledge about the physical properties of the system, such as stability
[
7
]
, positivity
[
8
,
9
]
and (incremental) dissipativity
[
10
,
11
]
. The incorporation of such properties can enhance the reliability and robustness of the identified model by ensuring that the simulated behaviour aligns with the real-world behaviour of the system. This is particularly relevant when the model is used to simulate trajectories far away from the data set used for identification. For example, it has been shown
[
12
,
13
]
that incorporating stability in the identified model results in better long-term predictions and generalizations to new data sets.

In this paper, we focus on the identification of passive systems, which can be seen as a special case of dissipative systems. Identification of dissipative systems has been studied in the case where the underlying model class consists of linear systems
[
10
]
. On the other hand, identification of incrementally dissipative (nonlinear) systems has been studied in the case where the underlying model class is a reproducing kernel Hilbert space of operators
[
11
]
. The results in the latter are obtained by making use of the so-called
scattering transform
, which allows to reformulate the original problem as the problem of identifying a system with a small incremental gain. While these results can be used to identify systems with non-incremental properties, i.e., systems that are simply dissipative, the reliance on the scattering transform presents some limitations. Most notably, the scattering transform is generally not computable in the non-incremental case.

Motivated by this, we consider the problem of identifying a passive input-output model for a (nonlinear) system from data on its input and output trajectories. We consider an input-output model defined as an operator from the space of inputs to itself. In this case, passivity of the system corresponds to nonnegativity of the corresponding input-output operator, under the assumption that the latter is causal. Therefore, as a first step towards identifying a passive input-output model, we focus on the problem of identifying a nonnegative input-output operator. Since nonnegativity is not preserved under arbitrary linear combinations, we cannot directly apply the theory of reproducing Hilbert spaces and use the representer theorem. Instead, we take inspiration from the sum of squares idea for nonnegative function estimation recently developed in
[
14
]
.

In particular, we consider a class of input-output operators defined via a feature map and a nonnegative bounded linear operator in such a way that nonnegativity of the input-output operator is guaranteed. Then, we formulate the identification problem as an estimation problem for the aforementioned linear operator. As our main result, we obtain a representer theorem for this estimation problem in the case where the input space is finite-dimensional. Then, we show that a solution of the latter can be obtained by solving an associated semidefinite program. Finally, by means of an example, we demonstrate how these results can be used in practice, even when the input space is not finite-dimensional.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. We introduce notation and preliminaries in Section
II
. We formulate the identification problem in Section
III
and we discuss the relevant theory of reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces in Section
IV
. We solve the identification problem in Section
V
and we show how the solution can be obtained by solving an associated semidefinite program in Section
VI
. Finally, we demonstrate our results with an example in Section
VII
and we provide concluding remarks in Section
VIII
.

II
Notation and prelimnaries

We denote the set of positive integers by
\mathbb{N}
. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we denote the set
\{1,\dots,n\}
by
[n]
. We denote the Cartesian product of the sets
X
and
Y
by
X\times Y
. Given a set
X
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we write
X^{n}
to denote the Cartesian product

X^{n}=\underbrace{X\times X\times\cdots\times X}_{n\text{ times}}.

(1)

Given a Hilbert space
\mathcal{U}
, we denote the corresponding inner product by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{U}}
and the induced norm by
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\mathcal{U}}
. We say that an operator
G:\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{U}
is
nonnegative
if
\langle Gu,u\rangle_{\mathcal{U}}\geq 0
for all
u\in\mathcal{U}
. Given another Hilbert space
\mathcal{W}
, we denote the Banach space of bounded linear operators from
\mathcal{U}
to
\mathcal{W}
by
\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{W})
and the operator norm by
\lVert\cdot\rVert
. We use the shorthand notation
\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{U})=\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{U})
and

\mathscr{B}^{+}(\mathcal{U})=\left\{G\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{U})\,\left|\,\vphantom{G\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{U})}G\text{ is nonnegative}\right.\right\}.

(2)

We denote the adjoint of
B\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{W})
by
B^{*}\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{W},\mathcal{U})
. We identify the vector
w\in\mathcal{W}
with the operator
\alpha\mapsto\alpha w
, which belongs to
\mathscr{B}(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{W})
. Consequently,
w^{*}\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{W},\mathbb{R})
is such that
w^{*}z=\langle w,z\rangle_{\mathcal{W}}
for all
z\in\mathcal{W}
. In particular, given
w_{1},w_{2}\in\mathcal{W}
, we have that
w_{1}w_{2}^{*}\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{W})
is such that
w_{1}w_{2}^{*}z=\langle w_{2},z\rangle_{\mathcal{W}}w_{1}
for all
z\in\mathcal{W}
.

Note that
\mathcal{U}^{n}
is a Hilbert space with inner-product

\langle(u_{1},\dots,u_{n}),(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})\rangle_{\mathcal{U}^{n}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle u_{i},v_{i}\rangle_{\mathcal{U}}.

(3)

Given operators
M_{ij}\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{U})
,
i,j\in[n]
, we define

M=\begin{bmatrix}M_{11}&\cdots&M_{1n}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
M_{n1}&\cdots&M_{nn}\end{bmatrix}

(4)

as the operator in
\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{U}^{n})
such that

M(u_{1},\dots,u_{n})=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}M_{1i}u_{i},\dots,\sum_{i=1}^{n}M_{ni}u_{i}\right).

(5)

It is easily seen that any
M\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{U}^{n})
can be written in the form (
4
) for suitably chosen
M_{ij}\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{U})
,
i,j\in[n]
.

II-A

Signal spaces

Let
\mathbb{R}^{+}
denote the set of nonnegative real numbers and let
\mathcal{R}
be a finite-dimensional Hilbert space, e.g.,
\mathbb{R}^{m}
with the Euclidian inner-product. The set of
square integrable functions

L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{+},\mathcal{R})
consists of all measurable functions
u:\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathcal{R}
such that
\int_{0}^{\infty}\lVert u(t)\rVert_{\mathcal{R}}\textrm{d}t<\infty
. If we identify functions that differ only on a set of Lebesgue measure zero, then
L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{+},\mathcal{R})
becomes a Hilbert space with inner-product

\langle u,v\rangle_{L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{+},\mathcal{R})}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\langle u(t),v(t)\rangle_{\mathcal{R}}\textrm{d}t,

(6)

The Hilbert space
L_{2}([0,T],\mathcal{R})
, where
T>0
, can be defined similarly by replacing the upper bound with
T
.

Let
\mathbb{Z}^{+}
denote the set of nonnegative integers. The set of
square summable sequences

\ell_{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{+},\mathcal{R})
consists of all functions
u:\mathbb{Z}^{+}\to\mathcal{R}
such that
\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\lVert u(t)\rVert_{\mathcal{R}}<\infty.
The latter is a Hilbert space with inner-product

\langle u,v\rangle_{\ell_{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{+},\mathcal{R})}=\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\langle u(t),v(t)\rangle_{\mathcal{R}}.

(7)

The Hilbert space
\ell_{2}(\{0,1\dots,T\},\mathcal{R})
, where
T\in\mathbb{N}
, can be defined similarly.

We will collectively refer to the spaces above as
signal spaces
. Thus, a signal space consists of functions
u:\mathbb{T}\to\mathcal{R}
, where
\mathcal{R}
is a Hilbert space and
\mathbb{T}
is a time axis given by one of
\mathbb{R}^{+}
,
[0,T]
,
\mathbb{Z}^{+}
or
\{0,1,\dots,T\}
.

II-B

Passivity and causality

Next, we define the notions of causality and passivity, see
[
15
, Chapter VI]
for details. Let
\mathcal{U}
be a signal space. For
\tau\in\mathbb{T}
, we define the truncation operator
P_{\tau}:\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{U}
as

(P_{\tau}u)(t)=\begin{cases}u(t)&$t\leq\tau$,\\
0&$t>\tau$.\end{cases}

(8)

Note that
P_{\tau}
is linear,
P_{\tau}^{2}=P_{\tau}
and
P_{\tau}^{*}=P_{\tau}
, that is,
P_{\tau}
is an orthogonal projection for all
\tau\in\mathbb{T}
. We say that an operator
G:\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{U}
is:

1.

passive
if

\langle P_{\tau}u,P_{\tau}Gu\rangle_{\mathcal{U}}\geq 0

(9)

for all
u\in\mathcal{U}
and
\tau\in\mathbb{T}
;

2.

causal
if

P_{\tau}Gu=P_{\tau}GP_{\tau}u,

(10)

for all
u\in\mathcal{U}
and
\tau\in\mathbb{T}
.

In other words,
G
is causal if the output at each time instant depends only on the past of the input. Note that
G
is passive only if it is nonnegative (take
\tau\to\infty
in (
9
)). If
G
is causal, then
G
is passive if and only if
G
is nonnegative since

\langle P_{\tau}u,P_{\tau}Gu\rangle_{\mathcal{U}}=\langle P_{\tau}u,P_{\tau}GP_{\tau}u\rangle_{\mathcal{U}}=\langle P_{\tau}u,GP_{\tau}u\rangle_{\mathcal{U}},

for all
u\in\mathcal{U}
and
\tau\in\mathbb{T}
, where the second equality follows from the fact that
P_{\tau}
is an orthogonal projection.

III
Problem formulation

Let
\mathcal{U}
be a signal space. Suppose that we are given a data set consisting of
n\in\mathbb{N}
input-output signals

(u_{i},y_{i})\in\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{U},\quad i\in[n].

(11)

At a high-level, we are interested in finding a passive operator
G:\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{U}
that fits the data well, i.e., such that the
data misfit

L(G)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lVert Gu_{i}-y_{i}\rVert_{\mathcal{U}}^{2}

(12)

is small. Note that a passive operator is necessarily nonnegative. Therefore, as a first step towards solving this high-level problem, in this paper, we focus on the following problem.

Problem 1

Given the data in (
11
), find a nonnegative operator
G:\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{U}
such that
L(G)
is small.

We will formalize what “small” means by introducing an appropriate cost functional in Section
V
. Then, we will solve Problem
1
in the case where
\mathcal{U}
is finite-dimensional. Note that the only finite dimensional signal space that we consider is
\ell_{2}(\{0,\dots,T\},\mathcal{R})
, where
T\in\mathbb{N}
and
\mathcal{R}
is a Hilbert space. Nevertheless, since nonnegativity is defined for operators on arbitrary Hilbert spaces, the following results are equally applicable to any finite-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{U}
. In particular, they are applicable to any finite dimensional subspace of a signal space. We will demonstrate this with an example in Section
VII
.

IV
Reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces

In this section, we will introduce part of the theory on reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces that is relevant to this paper. We refer to
[
6
]
for a detailed treatment of the case where the kernel is scalar-valued, and to
[
16
]
for the extension to the general case. We also refer to the technical report by
[
17
]
. In the context of this paper, we restrict the discussion to the case where the input and output spaces are identical.

Consider a Hilbert space
\mathcal{U}
. A Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
of operators from
\mathcal{U}
to
\mathcal{U}
is a
reproducing kernel Hilbert space
if it admits a reproducing kernel, defined below.

Definition 1

A map
k:\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{U}\to\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{U})
is a
reproducing kernel
for
\mathcal{H}
if
k(\cdot,u)v:\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{U}
is a member of
\mathcal{H}
for all
u,v\in\mathcal{U}
, and

\langle v,Hu\rangle_{\mathcal{U}}=\langle H,k(\cdot,u)v\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}

(13)

for all
u,v\in\mathcal{U}
and
H\in\mathcal{H}
.

Property (
13
) is referred to as the
reproducing property
. The class of reproducing kernels is completely characterized by two properties, namely, symmetry and positive semidefiniteness, defined below.

Definition 2

The map
k:\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{U}\to\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{U})
is
symmetric
if
k(u,v)^{*}=k(v,u)
for all
u,v\in\mathcal{U},
.

Definition 3

The map
k:\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{U}\to\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{U})
is
positive semidefinite
if

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle v_{i},k(u_{i},u_{j})v_{j}\rangle\geq 0

(14)

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
u_{1},\dots,u_{n}\in\mathcal{U}
,
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\in\mathcal{U}
.

It is straightforward to show that a reproducing kernel needs to be symmetric and positive semidefinite due to the reproducing property. More notably, the converse also holds and is known as the Moore-Aronszajn theorem
[
5
]
, see
[
16
]
for the extension to the general case.

IV-A

Gram operator

The properties of symmetry and positive semidefiniteness of the map
k:\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{U}\to\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{U})
can be expressed using the associated
Gram operator
. Given
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
u_{1},\dots,u_{n}\in\mathcal{U}
, we define the Gram operator
K\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{U}^{n})
as

K=\begin{bmatrix}k(u_{1},u_{1})&\cdots&k(u_{1},u_{n})\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
k(u_{n},u_{1})&\cdots&k(u_{n},u_{n})\end{bmatrix},

(15)

Then, (
14
) can be written as

\langle K(v_{1},\dots,v_{n}),(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})\rangle_{\mathcal{U}_{n}}\geq 0.

(16)

Therefore, the map
k
is symmetric and positive semidefinite if and only if, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
u_{1},\dots,u_{n}\in\mathcal{U}
, the corresponding Gram operator
K
in (
15
) is self-adjoint and nonnegative.

IV-B

Feature map

Symmetric positive definite maps
k:\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{U}\to\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{U})
can be defined via an auxiliary Hilbert space
\mathcal{W}
and a map
\phi:\mathcal{U}\to\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{W})
. In particular, it can be shown that the map
k(u,v)=\phi(u)^{*}\phi(v)
is symmetric and positive semidefinite. The space
\mathcal{W}
is typically referred to as a
feature space
and the map
\phi
as a
feature map
. There is a close relationship between symmetric positive definite maps
k
and their associated feature maps
\phi
. This is captured in the following proposition.

Proposition 1

The map
k:\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{U}\to\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{U})
is symmetric and positive semidefinite if and only if there exists a Hilbert space
\mathcal{W}
and a feature map
\phi:\mathcal{U}\to\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{W})
such that
k(u,v)=\phi(u)^{*}\phi(v)
.

The feature map
\phi
is central in developing kernel-based algorithms and can allow for simpler analysis. In particular, it can be shown that the reproducing kernel Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
with reproducing kernel
k(u,v)=\phi(u)^{*}\phi(v)
consists of operators of the form

Hu=\phi(u)^{*}w,

(17)

where
w\in\bar{\mathcal{W}}
and
\bar{\mathcal{W}}\subset\mathcal{W}
is the closed linear span of the set
\left\{\phi(v_{1})v_{2}\,\left|\,\vphantom{\phi(v_{1})v_{2}}v_{1},v_{2}\in\mathcal{U}\right.\right\}
. We refer to
[
17
, Theorem 5 and Theorem 6]
for a proof in the case where the kernel is scalar-valued. The proof in the general case follows similar reasoning.

IV-C

Representer theorem

One of the most attractive features of the theory of reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces is that operator estimation problems have computationally tractable solutions. Here, we focus on the problem stated in Section
III
but without the nonnegativity requirement. In particular, we consider the problem of identifying an operator
G:\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{U}
such that the data misfit (
12
) is small. Let
\mathcal{H}
be a reproducing kernel Hilbert space of operators from
\mathcal{U}
to
\mathcal{U}
. We restrict our search to operators
G
that belong to
\mathcal{H}
. In general, under mild conditions, the data
(u_{i},y_{i})\in\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{U}
,
i\in[n]
, can be interpolated exactly, i.e., there exists an operator
G\in\mathcal{H}
such that
Gu_{i}=y_{i}
for all
i\in[n]
and, thus,
L(G)=0
. However, we typically expect the data to be noisy, in which case exact interpolation is undesirable. In such cases, a well-established identification approach is to instead consider the
regularized least squares
problem

\min_{G\in\mathcal{H}}\quad L(G)+\gamma\lVert G\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}.

(18)

Here, the first term promotes small
L(G)
, i.e., a good fit of the data, whereas the second term promotes small
\lVert G\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}
, which is interpreted as low complexity. The parameter
\gamma>0
regulates the balance between these two terms.

The representer theorem, stated in the following proposition, shows that (
18
) has a unique solution for any reproducing Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, any parameter
\gamma>0
and any data set
(u_{i},y_{i})\in\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{U}
,
i\in[n]
.

Proposition 2

There exists a unique solution
\hat{G}
of (
18
), which is given by

\hat{G}u=\sum_{i=1}^{n}k(u,u_{i})c_{i},

(19)

with
c_{i}\in\mathcal{U}
,
i\in[n]
, given by the unique solution of

(K+\gamma I)(c_{1},\dots,c_{n})=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n}),

(20)

where
K\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{U}^{n})
is the Gram operator associated with
k
and
u_{1},\dots,u_{n}
, and
I\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{U}^{n})
is the identity operator.

Note that, if
\mathcal{U}
is finite-dimensional, then
\mathcal{U}^{n}
is finite-dimensional and, thus, solving (
20
) amounts to solving a finite-dimensional system of linear equations.

As already mentioned in the previous subsection, there exists feature map
\phi:\mathcal{U}\to\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{W})
such that the reproducing kernel for
\mathcal{H}
is given by
k(u,v)=\phi(u)^{*}\phi(v)
. Furthermore, every
G\in\mathcal{H}
is of the form

Gu=\phi(u)^{*}w,

(21)

where
w\in\bar{\mathcal{W}}\subset\mathcal{W}
. With a minor abuse of notation, we can treat the data misfit (
12
) as a function of
w
, such that

L(w)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lVert\phi(u_{i})^{*}w-y_{i}\rVert_{\mathcal{U}}^{2}.

(22)

Since
\lVert G\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}=\lVert w\rVert_{\mathcal{W}}
, we can rewrite (
18
) as

\min_{w\in\bar{\mathcal{W}}}\quad L(w)+\gamma\lVert w\rVert_{\mathcal{W}}.

(23)

Then, Proposition
2
tells us that (
23
) has a unique solution
\hat{w}\in\bar{\mathcal{W}}
given by

\hat{w}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi(u_{i})c_{i},

(24)

with
c_{i}\in\mathcal{U}
,
i\in[n]
, given by the unique solution of (
20
).

V
Representer theorem for nonnegative operators

In this section, we will provide a solution to Problem
1
. To do this, we take inspiration from the theory of reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces and the sum of squares idea developed in
[
14
]
. We will first motivate our approach by considering a simple example.

Example 1

Consider a continuous function
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
that is differentiable at zero. We view
f
as an operator from
\mathbb{R}
to
\mathbb{R}
. As such,
f
is nonnegative if and only if
f(x)x\geq 0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
. In particular, this means that
f(0)=0
, hence we can write
f(x)=g(x)x
, where the function
g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
given by

g(x)=\begin{cases}\frac{f(x)}{x}&if $x\neq 0$,\\
f^{\prime}(0)&if $x=0$.\end{cases}

(25)

is continuous. Since
f(x)
is nonnegative as an operator, it follows that
g(x)\geq 0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
, i.e.,
g
is nonnegative as a function. Conversely, if
f(x)=g(x)x
for some nonnegative function
g
, then
f
is nonnegative as an operator. Now, suppose that we have a data set consisting of
n\in\mathbb{N}
pairs
(x_{i},f_{i})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
,
i\in[n]
, and consider the problem of finding a nonnegative operator
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
that fits the data well, i.e.,
f(x_{i})\approx f_{i}
for all
i\in[n]
. If we further require that
f
is continuous and differentiable at
0
, then, without loss of generality, we can assume that
f(x)=g(x)x
for some continuous nonnegative function
g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
. Consequently, the problem reduces to finding a nonnegative
g
such that
g(x_{i})\approx f_{i}/x_{i}
for all
i\in[n]
. This can be solved using the sum-of-squares approach in
[
14
]
. In particular, given a Hilbert space of features
\mathcal{W}
and a feature map
\phi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathcal{W}
, we can model a nonnegative function as
g(x)=\phi(x)^{*}Q\phi(x)
, where the operator
Q\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{W})
is nonnegative. Note that this yields

f(x)=\phi(x)^{*}Q\phi(x)x

(26)

As shown in
[
14
]
, the problem can be reformulated as a regularized least squares problem with an appropriately chosen cost functional. Furthermore, the authors of
[
14
]
obtain a representer theorem for this problem that provides a computationally tractable solution.

With Example
1
in mind, we take the following approach to solve Problem
1
. Let
\mathcal{W}
be a Hilbert space of features and
\phi:\mathcal{U}\to\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{W})
a feature map. In view of (
26
), we consider input-output operators
G:\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{U}
of the form

Gu=\phi(u)^{*}Q\phi(u)u,

(27)

where
Q\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{W})
. Since

\langle Gu,u\rangle_{\mathcal{U}}=\langle\phi(u)^{*}Q\phi(u)u,u\rangle_{\mathcal{U}}=\langle Q\phi(u)u,\phi(u)u\rangle_{\mathcal{W}},

it follows that that
G
is nonnegative if
Q
is nonnegative. With a minor abuse of notation, we can treat the data misfit (
12
) as a function of
Q
, such that

L(Q)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lVert\phi(u_{i})^{*}Q\phi(u_{i})u_{i}-y_{i}\rVert_{\mathcal{U}}^{2}.

(28)

We can now reformulate Problem
1
as the regularized least squares problem

\min_{Q\in\mathscr{B}^{+}(\mathcal{W})}L(Q)+\gamma\lVert Q\rVert,

(29)

where
\lVert\cdot\rVert
is the operator norm and
\gamma>0
. We can obtain a representer theorem for this problem under the condition that
\mathcal{U}
is finite-dimensional. Therefore, for the remainder of this paper, we will assume that
\mathcal{U}
is finite-dimensional.

Consider the subspace
\hat{\mathcal{W}}\subset\mathcal{W}
given by

\hat{\mathcal{W}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\im\phi(u_{i})

(30)

Since
\mathcal{U}
is finite-dimensional, it follows that
\hat{\mathcal{W}}
is also finite-dimensional. Let
\Pi\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{W})
be the orthogonal projection onto
\hat{\mathcal{W}}
. This means that
\im\Pi=\hat{\mathcal{W}}
,
\Pi^{2}=\Pi
and
\Pi^{*}=\Pi
. Consider the following lemma.

Lemma 1

The following statements hold:

1.

L(\Pi Q\Pi)=L(Q)
for all
Q\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{W})
;

2.

\lVert\Pi Q\Pi\rVert\leq\lVert Q\rVert
for all
Q\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{W})
;

3.

\Pi Q\Pi\in\mathscr{B}^{+}(\mathcal{W})
for all
Q\in\mathscr{B}^{+}(\mathcal{W})
.

Proof:

Statement 1) holds because
\Pi\phi(u_{i})=\phi(u_{i})
and
\phi(u_{i})^{*}\Pi=\phi(u_{i})^{*}
for all
i\in[n]
. We also have that

\langle\Pi Q\Pi w,w\rangle_{\mathcal{W}}=\langle Q\Pi w,\Pi w\rangle_{\mathcal{W}}

(31)

for all
Q\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{W})
and
w\in\mathcal{W}
, hence statement 3) holds. To show that statement 2) holds as well, let
Q\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{W})
and note that
\lVert\Pi w\rVert_{\mathcal{W}}\leq\lVert w\rVert_{\mathcal{W}}
for all
w\in\mathcal{W}
. Therefore, we have that
\lVert\Pi Q\Pi w\rVert_{\mathcal{W}}\leq\lVert Q\Pi w\rVert_{\mathcal{W}}
and
\lVert w\rVert_{\mathcal{W}}\leq 1
implies
\lVert\Pi w\rVert_{\mathcal{W}}\leq 1
for all
w\in\mathcal{W}
. This implies that

\displaystyle\lVert\Pi Q\Pi\rVert

\displaystyle=\sup_{\lVert w\rVert\leq 1}\lVert\Pi Q\Pi w\rVert_{\mathcal{W}}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\lVert w\rVert\leq 1}\lVert Q\Pi w\rVert_{\mathcal{W}}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\lVert\Pi w\rVert\leq 1}\lVert Q\Pi w\rVert_{\mathcal{W}}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert Q\rVert

and, thus, statement 3) holds.
∎

The first two statements of Lemma
1
tell us that the cost in (
29
) does not increase when
Q
is replaced by
\Pi Q\Pi
. Together with the third statement, this implies that

\min_{Q\in\mathscr{B}^{+}(\mathcal{W})}L(Q)+\gamma\lVert Q\rVert=\min_{\hat{Q}\in\hat{\mathcal{Q}}}L(\hat{Q})+\gamma\lVert\hat{Q}\rVert,

(32)

where
\hat{\mathcal{Q}}=\left\{\Pi Q\Pi\,\left|\,\vphantom{\Pi Q\Pi}Q\in\mathscr{B}^{+}(\mathcal{W})\right.\right\}\subset\mathscr{B}^{+}(\mathcal{W})
. In the following, we will show that
\hat{\mathcal{Q}}
has a finite parametrization. To this end, let the operator
\Phi\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{U}^{n},\mathcal{W})
be given by

\Phi=\begin{bmatrix}\phi(u_{1})&\cdots&\phi(u_{n})\end{bmatrix}

(33)

and consider the following lemma.

Lemma 2

There exist a self-adjoint operator
N\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{U}^{n})
such that
\Pi=\Phi N\Phi^{*}
.

Proof:

Suppose that the dimension of
\hat{\mathcal{W}}
is
K\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\{w_{1},\dots,w_{K}\}
be an orthonormal basis for
\hat{\mathcal{W}}
. It is well-known
[
18
, Proposition 4.7]
that
\Pi=\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{i}w_{i}^{*}
. Since
w_{k}\in\hat{\mathcal{W}}
for all
k\in[K]
, there exist
v_{k,i}\in\mathcal{U}
,

, such that
w_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi(u_{i})v_{k,i}
. Consequently, we obtain

\Pi=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\phi(u_{i})v_{k,i}v_{k,j}^{*}\phi(u_{j})^{*}

(34)

hence the operators
N_{ij}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}v_{k,i}v_{k,j}^{*}
,
i,j\in[n]
, are such that
\Pi=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\phi(u_{i})N_{ij}\phi(u_{j})^{*}
. In other words, we have that
\Pi=\Phi N\Phi^{*}
with
N\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{U}^{n})
given by

N=\begin{bmatrix}N_{11}&\cdots&N_{1n}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
N_{n1}&\cdots&N_{nn}\end{bmatrix}

(35)

Since
N_{ij}^{*}=N_{ji}
for all
i,j\in[n]
, it follows that
N
is self-adjoint, which concludes the proof.
∎The following lemma is a direct consequence of Lemma
2
.

Lemma 3

We have that
\hat{\mathcal{Q}}=\left\{\Phi M\Phi^{*}\,\left|\,\vphantom{\Phi M\Phi^{*}}M\in\mathscr{B}^{+}(\mathcal{U}^{n})\right.\right\}
.

Proof:

Let
\hat{Q}=\Phi M\Phi^{*}
for some
M\in\mathscr{B}^{+}(\mathcal{U}^{n})
. Since
\Pi\phi(u_{i})=\phi(u_{i})
for all
i\in[n]
, it follows that
\hat{Q}=\Pi\hat{Q}\Pi
. Furthermore, since
M
is nonnegative, it follows that

\langle\hat{Q}w,w\rangle_{\mathcal{W}}=\langle\Phi M\Phi^{*}w,w\rangle_{\mathcal{W}}=\langle M\Phi^{*}w,\Phi^{*}w\rangle_{\mathcal{U}^{n}}\geq 0

for all
w\in\mathcal{W}
, hence
\hat{Q}\in\mathscr{B}^{+}(\mathcal{W})
and, thus,
\hat{Q}\in\hat{\mathcal{Q}}
. For the converse, let
\hat{Q}\in\hat{\mathcal{Q}}
, i.e.,
\hat{Q}=\Pi Q\Pi
with

. Due to Lemma

, there exists a self-adjoint

such that

, hence
\hat{Q}=\Phi M\Phi^{*}
with
M\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{U}^{n})
given by
M=N\Phi^{*}Q\Phi N

is nonnegative and

M\in\mathscr{B}^{+}(\mathcal{U}^{n})
,
∎

Note that

(36)

Theorem 1

Suppose that
\mathcal{U}
is finite-dimensional. A solution of (

\hat{Q}=\Phi M\Phi^{*},

(37)

where
M\in\mathscr{B}^{+}(\mathcal{U}^{n})

36
).

Proof:

Suppose that

is a solution of (
29

, it follows that
\Pi Q\Pi

L(\Pi Q\Pi)+\lVert\Pi Q\Pi\rVert\leq L(Q)+\lVert Q\rVert.

(38)

29
). Due to Lemma

\hat{Q}=\Phi M\Phi^{*}

\hat{Q}

for all

for all

M

is a solution of (
29

and note that (

. Furthermore, due to Lemma

M^{\prime}\in\mathscr{B}^{+}(\mathcal{U}^{n})
such that
\Pi Q\Pi=\Phi M^{\prime}\Phi^{*}
. Now, (
40

is a solution of (

L(\Phi M\Phi^{*})+\lVert\Phi M\Phi^{*}\rVert\leq L(Q)+\lVert Q\rVert

(41)

Since the latter holds for arbitrary

\hat{Q}=\Phi M\Phi^{*}
is a solution of (

∎

Recall that any
M\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{U}^{n})
is of the form (
4
), where
M_{ij}\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{U})
,
i,j\in[n]
. Therefore, we can rewrite (

\hat{Q}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\phi(u_{i})M_{ij}\phi(u_{j})^{*}

(42)

Note the parallel between (
23
) and (

24

42

2

1
does not tell us that a solution of (

paves the way to a computationally tractable solution of Problem

in the case where
\mathcal{U}
is finite-dimensional. Indeed, in the next section we will show that a solution of Problem
1
can be obtained by solving an associated semidefinite program.

Before we do that, we note that the operator
G:\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{U}
corresponding to (
37

Gu=\kappa(u)^{*}M\kappa(u)u,

(43)

where
\kappa(u)\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{U}^{n})
is given by

\kappa(u)=\Phi^{*}\phi(u)=\begin{bmatrix}k(u_{1},u)\\
\vdots\\
k(u_{n},u)\end{bmatrix},

(44)

and
k:\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{U}\to\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{U})
is the reproducing kernel corresponding to the the feature map
\phi
, i.e.,
k(v_{1},v_{2})=\phi(v_{1})^{*}\phi(v_{2})
. This means that evaluation of
G
does not require evaluation of the feature map
\phi
, but only of the kernel
k
corresponding to
\phi
. In fact, it can be shown that solving (
36
) also does not require evaluation of
\phi
. To this end, note that

L(\Phi M\Phi^{*})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lVert K_{i}^{*}MK_{i}u_{i}-y_{i}\rVert^{2}_{\mathcal{U}},

(45)

where
K_{i}=\kappa(u_{i})\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{U}^{n},\mathcal{U})
for all
i\in[n]
. The Gram operator associated with
k
and
u_{1},\dots,u_{n},
is given by

K=\begin{bmatrix}K_{1}&\cdots&K_{n}\end{bmatrix}=\Phi^{*}\Phi.

(46)

Since
K\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{U}^{n})
is self-adjoint and nonnegative, there exists a self-adjoint and nonnegative
K^{\frac{1}{2}}\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{U}^{n})
such that
K^{\frac{1}{2}}K^{\frac{1}{2}}=K
, and we have the following lemma.

Lemma 4

We have that

\lVert\Phi M\Phi^{*}\rVert=\|K^{\frac{1}{2}}MK^{\frac{1}{2}}\|

(47)

for all
M\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{U}^{n})
.

Proof:

As
\mathcal{U}
is finite-dimensional and
\Phi\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{U}^{n},\mathcal{W})
, it follows that
\im\Phi
is finite-dimensional. Consequently, for all
w\in\mathcal{W}
, we can write
w=\Phi x+z
, where
x\in\mathcal{U}^{n}
and
z\in(\im\Phi)^{\perp}=\ker\Phi^{*}
. Using this, we obtain

\lVert\Phi M\Phi^{*}w\rVert^{2}_{\mathcal{W}}=\lVert\Phi MKx\rVert^{2}_{\mathcal{W}},

(48)

where we used the fact that
K=\Phi^{*}\Phi
. This implies that

\lVert\Phi M\Phi^{*}\rVert^{2}=\sup_{\lVert\Phi x\rVert_{\mathcal{W}}\leq 1}\lVert\Phi MKx\rVert^{2}_{\mathcal{W}}.

(49)

Since
K^{\frac{1}{2}}K^{\frac{1}{2}}=\Phi^{*}\Phi
and
K^{\frac{1}{2}}
is self-adjoint, we have that

\lVert\Phi x\rVert_{\mathcal{W}}^{2}=\langle\Phi x,\Phi x\rangle_{\mathcal{W}}=\|{K^{\frac{1}{2}}x}\|_{\mathcal{U}^{n}}^{2},

(50)

We also have that
\lVert\Phi MKx\rVert^{2}_{\mathcal{W}}=\|{K^{\frac{1}{2}}MKx}\|_{\mathcal{U}^{n}}^{2}
and, thus,

\lVert\Phi M\Phi^{*}\rVert^{2}=\sup_{\|{K^{\frac{1}{2}}x}\|_{\mathcal{U}^{n}}\leq 1}\|{K^{\frac{1}{2}}MKx}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{U}^{n}}.

(51)

Since
\im K^{\frac{1}{2}}
is finite-dimensional,
z\in\mathcal{U}^{n}
can be written as
z=K^{\frac{1}{2}}x+z^{\prime}
with
x\in\mathcal{U}^{n}
and
z^{\prime}\in\im(K^{\frac{1}{2}})^{\perp}=\ker K^{\frac{1}{2}}
. Then, (
51
) implies that

\lVert\Phi M\Phi^{*}\rVert^{2}=\sup_{\|{z}\|_{\mathcal{U}^{n}}\leq 1}\|{K^{\frac{1}{2}}MK^{\frac{1}{2}}z}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{U}^{n}}=\|{K^{\frac{1}{2}}MK^{\frac{1}{2}}}\|^{2},

and, thus, (
47
) holds.
∎

As an immediate consequence of (
45
) and (
47
), we obtain the following corollary of Theorem
1
.

Corollary 1

If it exists, a solution of (
29
) is given by (
37
), where
M\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{U}^{n})
is a solution of

\min_{M\in\mathscr{B}^{+}(\mathcal{U}^{n})}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lVert K_{i}^{*}MK_{i}u_{i}-y_{i}\rVert^{2}_{\mathcal{U}}+\lVert K^{\frac{1}{2}}MK^{\frac{1}{2}}\rVert.

(52)

Moreover, the operator
G:\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{U}
corresponding to this solution is given by (
43
).

Corollary
1
shows that a solution of Problem
1
can be obtained by solving (
52
). Note that all operators in (
52
) belong to
\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{U}^{n})
, which is finite-dimensional, hence Corollary
1
provides a computationally tractable solution of Problem
1
. In the next section, we will show how this solution can be obtained by solving an associated semidefinite program.

VI
Computional aspects

In this section, we will show how to obtain a solution of Problem
1
by solving an associated semidefinite program. To this end, since
\mathcal{U}
is finite-dimensional, it is isometrically isomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{m}
for some
m\in\mathbb{N}
. Therefore without loss of generality, we can assume that
\mathcal{U}=\mathbb{R}^{m}
with the Euclidian inner-product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{2}
and induced norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{2}
. Now, consider (
52
). Note that

\mathscr{B}^{+}(\mathcal{U}^{n})
consists of matrices
M\in\mathbb{R}^{nm\times nm}
such that
M+M^{\top}
is symmetric positive semidefinite, which we denote by
M+M^{\top}\geq 0
. Furthermore, we have that

K_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{mn\times m},\quad K^{\frac{1}{2}}\in\mathbb{R}^{nm\times nm},\quad K^{\frac{1}{2}}\in\mathbb{R}^{nm\times nm}

(53)

where
K\geq 0
and
K^{\frac{1}{2}}\geq 0
. It is well known, see, e.g.,
[
19
]
, that
\lVert K_{i}^{\top}MK_{i}u_{i}-y_{i}\rVert^{2}_{2}\leq p_{i}
,
i\in[n]
, if and only if

\begin{bmatrix}I&K_{i}^{\top}MK_{i}u_{i}-y_{i}\\
u_{i}^{\top}K_{i}^{\top}M^{\top}K_{i}-y_{i}^{\top}&p_{i}\end{bmatrix}\geq 0.

(54)

Similarly,
\lVert K^{\frac{1}{2}}MK^{\frac{1}{2}}\rVert\leq p_{0}
if and only if

\begin{bmatrix}p_{0}I&K^{\frac{1}{2}}MK^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
K^{\frac{1}{2}}M^{\top}K^{\frac{1}{2}}&p_{0}I\end{bmatrix}\geq 0.

(55)

It can be shown that the latter is equivalent to

\begin{bmatrix}p_{0}K&KMK\\
KM^{\top}K&p_{0}K\end{bmatrix}\geq 0,

(56)

whose evaluation does not require the computation of the matrix
K^{\frac{1}{2}}
. Indeed, (
55
) holds if and only if

p_{0}(x_{1}^{\top}x_{1}+x_{2}^{\top}x_{2})+2x_{1}^{\top}K^{\frac{1}{2}}MK^{\frac{1}{2}}x_{2}\geq 0

(57)

for all
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{mn}
. Since every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{mn}
can be written as
x=K^{\frac{1}{2}}\bar{x}+z
, where
\bar{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{mn}
and
z\in\ker K^{\frac{1}{2}}
, it follows that (
55
) holds if and only if

p_{0}(\bar{x}_{1}^{\top}K\bar{x}_{1}+\bar{x}_{2}^{\top}K\bar{x}_{2}+z_{1}^{\top}z_{1}+z_{2}^{\top}z_{2})+2\bar{x}_{1}^{\top}KMK\bar{x}_{2}\geq 0

for all
\bar{x}_{1},\bar{x}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{mn}
and
z_{1},z_{2}\in\ker K^{\frac{1}{2}}
. Since
z_{1}^{\top}z_{1}\geq 0
and
z_{2}^{\top}z_{2}\geq 0
for all
z_{1},z_{2}\in\ker K^{\frac{1}{2}}
, it follows that (
55
) holds if and only if

p_{0}(\bar{x}_{1}^{\top}K\bar{x}_{1}+\bar{x}_{2}^{\top}K\bar{x}_{2})+2\bar{x}_{1}^{\top}KMK\bar{x}_{2}\geq 0,

(58)

for all
\bar{x}_{1},\bar{x}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{mn}
, that is, (
56
) holds.
With this in mind, the following corollary of Theorem
1
is an immediate consequence of Corollary
1
.

Corollary 2

Suppose that
\mathcal{U}=\mathbb{R}^{m}
. If it exists, a solution of (
52
) can be obtained by solving the semidefinite program

\displaystyle\min

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}+\gamma p_{0}

s.t.

\displaystyle M+M^{\top}\geq 0\text{ and }\eqref{eq:SDP_pi},\eqref{eq:SDP_p0_re}\text{ hold for all }i\in[n]

Moreover, the operator
G:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
corresponding to this solution is given by (

).

VII
Illustrative example

In this section, we will demonstrate how our results can be used in practice. Consider the nonlinear system

\displaystyle 2\ddot{q}+\ddot{\theta}\cos(\theta)

\displaystyle=\dot{\theta}^{2}\sin(\theta)-q

(59)

\displaystyle 2\ddot{\theta}+\ddot{q}\cos(\theta)

\displaystyle=u

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle=\dot{\theta}

The latter describes the motion of a rotational/translational proof mass actuator
[
20
]
. For simplicity, we have neglected disturbances and taken all parameters to be equal to 1.

Let
T>0
and
\mathcal{U}=L_{2}([0,T],\mathbb{R})
. We can view (
59
) as an operator
G_{\textup{true}}:\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{U}
that maps the input signal
u\in\mathcal{U}
to the corresponding output signal
G_{\textup{true}}u\in\mathcal{U}
generated by (
59
), where the system is initially at rest, i.e.,

q(0)=0,\quad\dot{q}(0)=0,\quad\theta(0)=0,\quad\dot{\theta}(0)=0.

(60)

It can be shown that
G_{\textup{true}}
is passive, hence, in particular,
G_{\textup{true}}
is nonnegative. Now, suppose that we are given a data set consisting of
n\in\mathbb{N}
input-output signals

(u_{i},y_{i})\in\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{U},\quad i\in[n],

(61)

generated by the system (
59
). We are interested in identifying a nonnegative operator
G:\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{U}
that fits the data well, i.e.,
Gu_{i}\approx y_{i}
for all
i\in[n]
. Since
\mathcal{U}
is infinite-dimensional, we cannot use our results directly. We circumvent this issue by restricting to a finite-dimensional subspace of
\mathcal{U}
.

To this end, we can approximate signals in
\mathcal{U}
to arbitrary precision by a finite-degree polynomial. In particular, we can approximate signals using the first
m
Legendre polynomials
[
21
]
, where
m
is as large as necessary to achieve a desired accuracy of the approximation. We refer the reader to
[
22
]
for an example of the use of Legendre polynomials in control.

Recall
[
21
, Section 2.3.2]
that the Legendre polynomials on the interval
[-1,1]
are given by
L_{1}(t)=1
,
L_{2}(t)=t
, and the recurrence relation

\frac{\textrm{d}}{\textrm{d}t}L_{i+2}=\frac{\textrm{d}}{\textrm{d}t}L_{i}+(2i+2)L_{i+1},\quad i\in\mathbb{N}.

(62)

The Legendre polynomials on the interval
[0,T]
can be obtained via the transformation
t\mapsto\frac{T}{2}(t+1)
. We assume that each Legendre polynomial is scaled so that
\lVert L_{i}\rVert_{\mathcal{U}}=1
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
. As such, the Legendre polynomials form an orthonormal basis for
\mathcal{U}
, i.e., every
u\in\mathcal{U}
can be written as
u=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}c_{i}L_{i}
for some coefficients
c_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
,
i\in\mathbb{N}
, which we call the
Legendre coefficients
of
u
. Therefore,
G_{\textup{true}}
is associated with an operator
\tilde{G}_{\textup{true}}
that maps the Legendre coefficients of
u
to the Legendre coefficients of
G_{\textup{true}}u
. Since the Legendre polynomials are orthonormal,
G_{\textup{true}}
is nonnegative if and only if
\tilde{G}_{\textup{true}}
is nonnegative.

With this in mind, let
m\in\mathbb{N}
be such that the first
m
Legendre polynomials can be used to accurately approximate the data set (
61
). Given a desired precision, an appropriate
m
can be determined by using existing error bounds, see
[
21
, Section 5.4]
for details. The approximation yields a data set consisting of
n\in\mathbb{N}
coefficient vectors

(\tilde{u}_{i},\tilde{y}_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{m},\quad i\in[n],

(63)

such that
u_{i}\approx\sum_{j=1}^{m}\tilde{u}_{ij}L_{i}
and
y_{i}\approx\sum_{j=1}^{m}\tilde{y}_{ij}L_{i}
, where
\tilde{u}_{ij}
and
\tilde{y}_{ij}
are the
j
’th entries of
\tilde{u}_{i}
and
\tilde{y}_{i}
, respectively. Now, we are interested in identifying a nonnegative operator
\tilde{G}:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
that fits the data well, i.e.,
\tilde{G}\tilde{u}_{i}\approx\tilde{y}_{i}
for all
i\in[n]
. This would correspond to a nonnegative operator
G:\tilde{\mathcal{U}}\to\tilde{\mathcal{U}}
, where
\tilde{\mathcal{U}}\subset\mathcal{U}
is given by the linear span of the first
m
Legendre polynomials. This operator
G
is expected to be a good approximation of
G_{\textup{true}}
when the input signal is restricted (or close) to
\tilde{\mathcal{U}}
.

To illustrate the effectiveness of this approach, we consider the following specific example. Let
T=20
,
n=9
and consider the data set (
61
), where

u_{i}=\begin{cases}L_{i}&i\in\{1,\dots,5\},\\
L_{i-5}+L_{i-4}&i\in\{6,\dots,9\}.\end{cases}

(64)

This means that the first 5 Legendre polynomials can be used to exactly represent the input signals
u_{i}
,
i\in[9]
. Approximating the corresponding output signals
y_{i}
,
i\in[9]
, using the first 10 Legendre polynomials yields relative errors less than
10^{-4}
. This is small enough for the purposes of this example, hence we take
m=10
. Next, we solve the semidefinite program in Corollary
2
, where
\gamma=10^{-3}
and the data set is given by the corresponding Legendre coefficient vectors (
63
). Furthermore, we use a simple Gaussian kernel

k(\tilde{v}_{1},\tilde{v}_{2})=e^{\frac{-\lVert\tilde{v}_{1}-\tilde{v}_{2}\rVert^{2}_{2}}{100^{2}}}I,

(65)

where

VIII
Conclusion

In this paper, we treated the problem of identifying a nonnegative operator from given input-output trajectories. We considered a class of input-output operators defined via a feature map and an arbitrary nonnegative bounded linear operator in a “sum of squares” fashion, such that nonnegativity of the input-output operator is guaranteed. Then, we cast the identification problem as a regularized least squares problem over a cone of nonnegative bounded linear operators. As our main result, we proved a representer theorem for this problem in the case where the input space is finite-dimensional. We also showed that the latter provides a computationally tractable solution which can be obtained by solving an associated semidefinite program. For future research, in addition to nonnegativity, we will investigate the problem of imposing causality of the identified input-output operator.
