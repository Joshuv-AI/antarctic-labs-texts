Eigenfunctions localised on a defect in high-contrast random media SIAM Journal on Mathematical Analysis 55 :6 (2023), 7449–7489 Authors’ typesetting and spelling conventions may differ from the final journal version.
Matteo Capoferri, Mikhail Cherdantsev, Igor Velčić

Abstract
We study the properties of eigenvalues and corresponding eigenfunctions generated by a defect in the gaps of the spectrum of a high-contrast random operator. We consider a family of elliptic operators 𝒜 ε \mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon} in divergence form whose coefficients are random, possess double porosity type scaling, and are perturbed on a fixed-size compact domain (a defect) . Working in the gaps of the limiting spectrum of the unperturbed operator 𝒜 ^ ε \widehat{\mathcal{A}}^{\varepsilon} , we show that the point spectrum of 𝒜 ε \mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon} converges in the sense of Hausdorff to the point spectrum of the limiting two-scale operator 𝒜 hom \mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{hom}} as ε → 0 \varepsilon\to 0 . Furthermore, we prove that the eigenfunctions of 𝒜 ε \mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon} decay exponentially at infinity uniformly for sufficiently small ε \varepsilon . This, in turn, yields strong stochastic two-scale convergence of such eigenfunctions to eigenfunctions of 𝒜 hom \mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{hom}} . Keywords: high contrast media, random media, stochastic homogenisation, defect modes, localised eigenfunctions. 2020 MSC classes: primary 74S25, 74A40; secondary 35B27, 74Q15, 35B40, 60H15, 35P05.

Introduction

We consider a high-contrast two-phase random medium, comprising a moderately “stiff” material — the
matrix

[

,
[

[

4
,
15

. The goal of the paper is to study localised modes generated by a defect.

The existence of localised modes, i.e. finite energy wave-like solutions of equations of physics — typically of electromagnetic or elastic nature — such that almost all their energy remains in a bounded region at all times, is a classical and well-studied problem.
This phenomenon is particularly important in applications, in that it can confer advantageous and desirable physical properties on the material in question. It is responsible, for instance, for the behaviour of photonic and phononic fibres
[
24
,
20
,
26
]
.

In mathematical terms, the study of localised modes typically reduces to the analysis of an elliptic spectral problem on the cross-section of the material.
The prototypical example is that of a composite medium described by an elliptic operator in divergence form with periodic coefficients perturbed on a relatively compact set. The spectral theory of operators with periodic coefficients, both in the presence and in the absence of a defect, has received considerable attention over the years. We refer the reader to
[
21
]
for a review of the subject, which focuses, in particular, on the mathematics of photonic band-gap optical materials.

In the current work we are interested in high-contrast media, namely, materials described by an elliptic operator in divergence form whose coefficients possess the so-called double porosity type scaling (see (
2.8
) below). Defect modes for high-contrast
periodic

[
7

and Kamotski–Smyshlyaev
[
19
]
in dimension

[
8
]
, see also
[
11

, although it is worth mentioning that one-dimensional setting is very special, and the nature of the band-gap spectrum there is different from the one in higher dimensions.

Even in the absence of high-contrast in the constituents, by carefully choosing the frequency of a Bloch wave type solution in relation to the wavenumber along the fibres one can
artificially
obtain a high-contrast operator on the cross-section of the material, see

The mathematical properties of high-contrast

The goal of the current paper is to perform a detailed analysis of localised modes in high-contrast random media,

Let us discuss our problem in more detail.

We begin by recalling a classical result of Figotin and Klein
[
15
]
, which applies, in particular, to a general class of elliptic self-adjoint operators of the form
-\nabla\cdot A\nabla
. Figotin and Klein showed that if the operator in question has a gap in its spectrum, then one can create an eigenvalue within any specific subinterval of the gap, by an appropriate compact perturbation of the coefficients
A
; furthermore, the corresponding eigenfunction decays exponentially with the rate of decay proportional, loosely speaking, to the distance from the eigenvalue to the edges of the gap, with proportionality factor depending on the ellipticity constant.

The basic starting point for creating localised modes — the existence of spectral gaps — is, in general, a non-trivial problem. While for periodic operators the spectrum has a band-gap structure, the existence of gaps is not guaranteed, since all the bands may overlap. In particular, this question is open for general elliptic operators of the form
-\nabla\cdot A\nabla
with periodic coefficients. However, in specific physically relevant examples, the existence of gaps can be shown, see, e.g.,
[
16
]
, and
[
22
]
for a general overview of the topic. It is well known that in homogenisation setting in the case of uniformly elliptic coefficients
A
, both periodic and random, the limit homogenised operator has no gaps in the spectrum. The picture is drastically different for the high-contrast setting with infinitely many gaps opening in homogenisation limit, which makes it particularly interesting for the study of localisation phenomena. One more observation we make here is that the type of the spectrum surrounding the gap is irrelevant for the basic argument one employs to argue the existence of localised modes induced by a defect, at least in the high-contrast homogenisation setting. Thus, while the problem of characterising the spectrum of an operator
-\nabla\cdot A\nabla
with random coefficients
A
is wide open to date, the answer to this question has no implications for our analysis.

In order to proceed we need to preliminarily introduce some notation; rigorous definitions will be given in the next section. We denote by
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}^{\varepsilon}
the unperturbed (i.e., without defect) high-contrast operator, where
\varepsilon
is a small parameter characterising the size of the microstructure, and by
\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon}
its perturbation by a finite size defect;
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}^{\rm hom}
and
\mathcal{A}^{\rm hom}
will denote the corresponding limiting two-scale operators.

For
periodic
high-contrast operators Zhikov
[
30
,
31
]
showed that the spectrum of
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}^{\rm hom}
, characterised by a certain nonlinear function of the spectral parameter
\beta(\lambda)
, has infinitely many gaps. Furthermore, one has convergence of spectra
\sigma(\widehat{\mathcal{A}}^{\varepsilon})\to\sigma(\widehat{\mathcal{A}}^{\rm hom})
in the sense of Hausdorff, so that, for sufficiently small
\varepsilon
gaps open in the spectrum of
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}^{\varepsilon}
. Therefore, according to
[
15
]
, by introducing a compact defect one can generate discrete spectrum in the gaps of
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}^{\varepsilon}
. It can be shown that one can induce localised eigenvalues in the gaps of
\sigma(\widehat{\mathcal{A}}^{\rm hom})
via an argument analogous to that of
[
15
]
. In particular, Kamotski and Smyshlyaev in
[
19
]
provided an explicit example of a localised eigenfunction of
\mathcal{A}^{\rm hom}
induced by a defect under an additional assumption of isotropy of the homogenised medium. Their main result, however, consists in showing that for a defect eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}
in a gap of the essential spectrum of
\mathcal{A}^{\rm hom}
, there exists a defect eigenvalue
\lambda_{\varepsilon}
of
\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon}
converging to
\lambda_{0}
with a rate of order
\varepsilon^{1/2}
. Moreover, the distance (in an appropriate sense) between the localised eigenfunction
u_{0}
corresponding to
\lambda_{0}
and the eigenspace of
\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon}
corresponding to its eigenvalues located in the vicinity of
\lambda_{0}
is also of order
\varepsilon^{1/2}
. In order to prove the converse statement, i.e. that for a sequence of defect eigenvalues
\lambda_{\varepsilon}
of
\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon}
converging to a point
\lambda_{0}
in a gap of the essential spectrum of
\mathcal{A}^{\rm hom}
it holds that
\lambda_{0}
is an eigenvalue of
\mathcal{A}^{\rm hom}
, one needs a compactness statement for the corresponding sequence of eigenfunctions
u_{\varepsilon}
. This was obtained in
[
7
]
, where the author proved a uniform exponential decay of
u_{\varepsilon}
independent of
\varepsilon
. Note that the general results of
[
15
]
do not allow one to obtain compactness, since there the decay rate depends on the ellipticity constant, which vanishes in the high-contrast setting as
\varepsilon\to 0
. Combining the results of
[
7
]
and
[
19
]
one also gets an asymptotic one-to-one correspondence between the defect modes of
\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon}
and
\mathcal{A}^{\rm hom}
. The final remark we make about the high-contrast periodic setting is that both
u_{0}
and
u_{\varepsilon}
(for small
\varepsilon
) decay exponentially as
e^{-\alpha|x|}
, where
\alpha
is of order
\sqrt{|\beta(\lambda_{0})|}
with proportionality factor depending on the ellipticity constant of the homogenised coefficients. Note that the quantity
\sqrt{|\beta(\lambda_{0})|}
blows up as
\lambda_{0}
to approach the left end of a spectral gap; therefore, the latter provides a much better rate of decay in the high-contrast setting than the more general result
[
15
]
of Figotin and Klein.

Whilst for
stochastic
operators the picture is to a certain extent similar, at the same time there is a number of significant technical and phenomenological differences. The spectrum of
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}^{\rm hom}
can be characterised by a stochastic analogue of Zhikov’s function
\beta(\lambda)
— see
[
10
]
and Section
2
below — however, the limit of the spectra of
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}^{\varepsilon}
is, in general, strictly larger than
\sigma(\widehat{\mathcal{A}}^{\rm hom})
(in fact, in some examples
\sigma(\widehat{\mathcal{A}}^{\varepsilon})=\mathbb{R}_{+}

[
10
, § 5.6]
). In
[
10
]
the authors show that
\sigma(\mathcal{A}^{\rm hom})\subset\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\sigma(\widehat{\mathcal{A}}^{\varepsilon})\subset\mathcal{G}
, where the set
\mathcal{G}
is determined by a function
\beta_{\infty}(\lambda)
— a modification of the function
\beta(\lambda)
which carries an information about areas with “non-typical” distribution of inclusions in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In realistic examples, e.g. the random parking model
[
10
, § 5.6]
, the set
\mathcal{G}
can be shown to have gaps, and hence so does
\sigma(\widehat{\mathcal{A}}^{\varepsilon})
for small enough
\varepsilon
.

In this work we adapt the general strategy of
[
7
]
and
[
19
]
to the stochastic setting. While postponing a detailed description of our results to later section, let us briefly elaborate on the novelty and challenges posed by the random setting.

We restrict our attention to the gaps of
\mathcal{G}
(assuming that they exist), rather than the gaps of
\sigma(\widehat{\mathcal{A}}^{\rm hom})
. Indeed, we have to do this in order to avoid the limit set of
\sigma(\widehat{\mathcal{A}}^{\varepsilon})
(note that under the assumption of finite range of dependence of the distribution of inclusions in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
one has
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\sigma(\widehat{\mathcal{A}}^{\varepsilon})=\mathcal{G}
, see
[
10
, Theorem 5.5]
).
The two-scale nature of the limiting operator
\mathcal{A}^{\rm hom}
captures the micro- and macroscopic scales of
\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon}
. We perform a delicate analysis, incorporating the subtle relation between the multiple scales of the operators
\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon}
and
\mathcal{A}^{\rm hom}
, in order to establish an asymptotic one-to-one correspondence between the defect modes of the operators
\mathcal{A}^{\rm hom}
and
\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon}
with the eigenvalues in the gaps of the set
\mathcal{G}
.

Another manifestation of the technical difficulties arising from the stochastic nature of the problem is that while for a defect eigenfunction of
\mathcal{A}^{\rm hom}
the exponential decay rate is determined by the stochastic version of
\beta(\lambda_{0})
(in direct analogy to the periodic setting), for the defect eigenfunctions of
\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon}
we were only able to characterise the exponential decay in terms of
\beta_{\infty}(\lambda_{0})
(the function that carries information about the limit of the spectra of
\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon}
) satisfying

\beta(\lambda_{0})\leq\beta_{\infty}(\lambda_{0}),

thus obtaining a slightly worse decay rate than one would hope for. At this stage, it is not entirely clear whether this result is optimal.

Remark 2.8

Remark 2.10

We have

(i)

(ii)

[

. For the sake of completeness, in Appendix

Therefore, the presence of a defect only affects the discrete spectrum of the operators in question. In particular, if the spectra of
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}^{\varepsilon}
and
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathrm{hom}}
have gaps, eigenvalues in the gaps — often known as

It is natural to ask the following questions.

Question 1.
Suppose we have an eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}\in\sigma_{d}(\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{hom}})
in a gap of
\mathcal{G}
, hence due to the defect. Is it true that there exist eigenvalues
\lambda_{\varepsilon}\in\sigma_{d}(\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon})
such that
\lambda_{\varepsilon}\to\lambda_{0}
as
\varepsilon\to 0
?

Question 2.
Suppose we have a sequence of eigenvalues
\lambda_{\varepsilon}\in\sigma_{d}(\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon})
converging to some
\lambda_{0}
in a gap of
\mathcal{G}
as
\varepsilon\to 0
. Is it true that
\lambda_{0}\in\sigma_{d}(\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{hom}})
?

Question 3.
Suppose that the answer to Question 1 or 2 is affirmative. What can we say about the convergence of the corresponding eigenfunctions?

The main goal of this paper is to provide a rigorous answer to Questions 1, 2 and 3.

\lambda_{\varepsilon}

\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon}
such that
\lambda_{\varepsilon}\to\lambda_{0}
as
\varepsilon\to 0
? It was shown in
[

, Proposition 5.11]
that
\beta_{\infty}(\lambda)
is continuous and strictly increasing on every interval contained in
\mathbb{R}^{+}\setminus\sigma(-\Delta_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Omega}})
. This implies that the part of the set

contained in the gaps of
\sigma(\widehat{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathrm{hom}})
is a union of intervals of positive length (i.e. it contains no isolated points). Let us assume for simplicity that the equality (

) holds. Then for
\lambda_{0}\in\mathcal{G}\setminus\sigma(\widehat{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathrm{hom}})
and
\delta>0
one obviously has that

\operatorname{dim}\operatorname{ran}\widehat{E}^{\varepsilon}_{(\lambda_{0}-\delta,\lambda_{0}+\delta)}\to+\infty\quad\text{as}\quad\varepsilon\to 0,

where
\widehat{E}^{\varepsilon}_{I}
denotes the spectral projection onto the interval

associated with the unperturbed operator
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}^{\varepsilon}
.
Therefore, the usual strategy of seeking defect modes with standard tools of functional analysis utilised in the present work would no longer be useful in the set
\mathcal{G}\setminus\sigma(\widehat{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathrm{hom}})
. Indeed, such problem is related to or — when
(\lambda_{0}-\delta,\lambda_{0}+\delta)\cap\sigma(\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon})=(\lambda_{0}-\delta,\lambda_{0}+\delta)
— is the problem of embedded eigenvalues, whose analysis is extremely challenging in general. For this reason, we refrain from studying defect modes in
\mathcal{G}\setminus\sigma(\widehat{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathrm{hom}})
here, with the plan to perform this delicate analysis elsewhere.

3
Main results

In what follows we always assume that the set
\mathcal{G}
defined in accordance with (
2.16
) has gaps, namely, it does not coincide with the whole positive real line. Note that this assumption does not yield an empty set of operators, as there are explicit examples — e.g., the random parking model or randomly scaled inclusions — for which it is satisfied, see
[
10
, § 5.6]
. More generally, consider a model with identical inclusions such that for a fixed (large enough)

the number of inclusions contained in any cube

is at least

. Then it is not difficult to see from the definition of
\beta_{\infty}(\lambda)
, taking into account the spectral decomposition (

), that
\mathcal{G}
has infinitely many gaps.

Our main results can be summarised in the form of three theorems stated in this section.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\lambda_{0}\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathcal{G}
be an eigenvalue of
\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{hom}}
.
Then almost surely

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\operatorname{dist}(\lambda_{0},\sigma_{d}(\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon}))=0.

(3.1)

In order to state our second theorem, we need to recall the notion of two-scale convergence, adapted to the stochastic setting.

Definition 3.2

(Stochastic two-scale convergence
[
32
]
)
.

Let
\{u^{\varepsilon}\}
be a bounded sequence in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. We say that
\{u^{\varepsilon}\}
weakly stochastically two-scale converges to
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\overline{u}}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\Omega)
(for a given
\omega_{0}\in\Omega
) and write
u^{\varepsilon}\overset{2}{\rightharpoonup}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\overline{u}}
if

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u^{\varepsilon}(x)\,f(x,x/\varepsilon,\omega_{0})\,dx={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\overline{u}\overline{f}\right]}\quad\forall{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\overline{f}}\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\otimes C^{\infty}(\Omega).

(3.2)

We say that
\{u^{\varepsilon}\}
strongly stochastically two-scale converges to
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\overline{u}}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\Omega)
and write
u^{\varepsilon}\overset{2}{\to}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\overline{u}}
if it satisfies (
3.2
) and

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\|u^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}=\|{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\overline{u}}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\Omega)}.

Some properties of stochastic two-scale convergence are provided in Appendix
B
.

The statement of the following theorem is true almost surely.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
\{\lambda_{\varepsilon}\}
,

\lambda_{\varepsilon}\in\sigma_{d}(\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon})\cap\left(\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathcal{G}\right),

be a sequence of eigenvalues of
\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon}
in the gaps of the limiting spectrum such that

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\lambda_{\varepsilon}=\lambda_{0}\not\in\mathcal{G}.

(3.3)

Denote by
\{u^{\varepsilon}\}
a sequence of corresponding normalised eigenfunctions,

\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon}u^{\varepsilon}=\lambda_{\varepsilon}\,u^{\varepsilon},\qquad\|u^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}=1.

(3.4)

Then we have the following.

(a)

The eigenfunctions
u^{\varepsilon}
are uniformly exponentially decaying at infinity, namely, for every

0<\alpha<\sqrt{\frac{|\beta_{\infty}(\lambda_{0})|}{\gamma}}

(3.5)

there exists
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that for every
0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}

we have

\|e^{\alpha\,|x|}u^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq C,

where
C
is a constant uniform in
\varepsilon
, the quantity
\gamma:=\max_{\lambda\in\sigma(A_{1})}\lambda
is the largest eigenvalue of the matrix
A_{1}
, and
\beta_{\infty}
is the function introduced in Definition
2.21
.

(b)

The limit
\lambda_{0}
is an (isolated) eigenvalue of
\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{hom}}
,

\lambda_{0}\in\sigma_{d}(\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{hom}}).

Furthermore, possibly up to extracting a subsequence, the sequence
\{u^{\varepsilon}\}
strongly stochastically two-scale converges to an eigenfunction
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\overline{u}}^{0}
of
\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{hom}}
corresponding to the eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}
.

Remark 3.4
.

We prove the exponential decay for the eigenfunctions whose eigenvalues converge to a point in a gap of the set
\mathcal{G}
. Note that the gaps of
\mathcal{G}
are given by
\{\lambda:\,\beta_{\infty}(\lambda)<0\}
. In fact, we expect that the range of admissible
\alpha

) to extend (similarly to the periodic setting
[
7
]
) up until

; recall that

characterises the gaps of

as
\{\lambda:\,\beta(\lambda)<0\}
. This conjecture is supported by the notions of statistically relevant and irrelevant spectra introduced in
[

]
. Indeed, the statistically relevant limiting spectrum of

coincides with
\sigma(\widehat{\mathcal{A}}^{\rm hom})
, while the quasimodes of
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}^{\varepsilon}
corresponding to the spectrum contained in the gaps of

have most of their energy “far away” from the origin (for small enough

localised on the defect should not “feel” the statistically irrelevant spectrum.
Unfortunately, by means of the techniques developed in the current paper we were unable to replace

with

in (

.

The next theorem, whose statement is true almost surely, establishes an “asymptotic” one-to-one correspondence between the defect eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of
\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon}
and

.

(a)

Suppose there exists a sequence of positive real numbers

\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{hom}}
of multiplicity at least

.

(b)

Let
\lambda_{0}\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathcal{G}
be an eigenvalue of

Remark 3.6
.

In the deterministic periodic setting

[

]

In the current paper, we are unable to formulate similar results, because a quantitative theory for the homogenisation corrector and other mathematical quantities appearing in (

This notwithstanding, as soon as one is able to quantitatively describe the convergence of the objects on the right-hand side of (

Remark 3.7
.

An explicit example was constructed in
[

]
in the case of the defect being a ball,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}=B_{R}(0)

\|\varepsilon\nabla u^{\varepsilon}_{0}\|_{L^{2}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{I}}^{\varepsilon})}+\|u^{\varepsilon}_{0}\|_{L^{2}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{I}}^{\varepsilon})}\leq C\|\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{I}}^{\varepsilon})}.

(5.30)

We are ready to estimate (
5.25
). Utilising (
5.30
), we have

\begin{split}\mathcal{I}_{0}&\leq C\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{\varepsilon}(\omega)}\|\nabla(u^{\varepsilon}_{0}+\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon})\|_{L^{2}(\varepsilon{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{\omega}^{k}})}\|\nabla(\psi_{R}\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon})\|_{L^{2}(\varepsilon{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{\omega}^{k}})}\\
&{\leq}C\varepsilon\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{\varepsilon}(\omega)}\left(\|\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\varepsilon{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{\omega}^{k}})}+\varepsilon\|\nabla\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\varepsilon{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{\omega}^{k}})}\right)\|\nabla(\psi_{R}\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon})\|_{L^{2}(\varepsilon{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{\omega}^{k}})}.\end{split}

(5.31)

Applying Lemma
5.5
to the right-hand side of
(

) we obtain

\mathcal{I}_{0}\leq C\varepsilon\left(\|\psi_{R}^{1/2}\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}+\|\nabla(\psi_{R}^{1/2}\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon})\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{I}}^{\varepsilon})}^{2}\right).

(5.32)

Let us move on to
\mathcal{I}_{1}
.
Substituting (
5.27
) into (
5.26
) we obtain

\mathcal{I}_{1}=\lambda_{\varepsilon}\int_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{I}}^{\varepsilon}\cup{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}^{\varepsilon}}\psi_{R}\,(\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon})^{2}+\lambda_{\varepsilon}\int_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{I}}^{\varepsilon}}u^{\varepsilon}_{0}\psi_{R}\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon}.

(5.33)

We further decompose

as
u^{\varepsilon}_{0}=u^{\varepsilon}_{0,1}+u^{\varepsilon}_{0,2}
,
where
u^{\varepsilon}_{0,1}
and
u^{\varepsilon}_{0,2}
are defined to be the solutions of

-\Delta_{y}u^{\varepsilon}_{0,1}(\varepsilon y)-\lambda_{\varepsilon}u^{\varepsilon}_{0,1}(\varepsilon y)=\lambda_{\varepsilon}\langle\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon y)\rangle_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{\omega}^{k}}},\qquad y\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{\omega}^{k}},\ k\in\mathbb{N}^{\varepsilon}(\omega),

(5.34)

and

-\Delta_{y}u^{\varepsilon}_{0,2}(\varepsilon y)-\lambda_{\varepsilon}u^{\varepsilon}_{0,2}(\varepsilon y)=\lambda_{\varepsilon}\left[\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon y)-\langle\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon y)\rangle_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{\omega}^{k}}}\right],\qquad y\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{\omega}^{k}},\ k\in\mathbb{N}^{\varepsilon}(\omega),

(5.35)

respectively.
Formula (
5.35
), combined with the Poincaré inequality and the uniform (in
\varepsilon
) boundedness of the resolvent
(-\Delta_{y}-\lambda_{\varepsilon})^{-1}
, implies

\|u^{\varepsilon}_{0,2}(\varepsilon y)\|_{L^{2}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega^{k}})}\leq C\|\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon y)-\langle\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon y)\rangle_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{\omega}^{k}}}\|_{L^{2}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{\omega}^{k}})}\leq C\|\nabla_{y}\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon y)\|_{L^{2}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{\omega}^{k}})},

\|u^{\varepsilon}_{0,2}\|_{L^{2}(\varepsilon{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{\omega}^{k}})}\leq C\varepsilon\|\nabla\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\varepsilon{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{\omega}^{k}})}.

Arguing as above and resorting to Lemma
5.5
, we thus obtain

\begin{split}\Big|\int_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{I}}^{\varepsilon}}u^{\varepsilon}_{0,2}\psi_{R}\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon}\Big|\leq C\varepsilon\left(\|\psi_{R}^{1/2}\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}+\|\nabla(\psi_{R}^{1/2}\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon})\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{I}}^{\varepsilon})}^{2}\right).\end{split}

(5.36)

Furthermore, formula (
5.34
) implies

u^{\varepsilon}_{0,1}=\lambda_{\varepsilon}\,b^{\varepsilon}_{\lambda_{\varepsilon}}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{\varepsilon}(\omega)}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbbm{1}}_{\varepsilon{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega^{k}}}\,\langle\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon}\rangle_{\varepsilon{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega^{k}}}.

(5.37)

Therefore, on account of (
5.36
) and (
5.37
), we can estimate (
5.33
) as

\begin{split}\mathcal{I}_{1}\leq\int_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{I}}^{\varepsilon}\cup{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}^{\varepsilon}}(\lambda_{\varepsilon}+\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{2}\,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbbm{1}}_{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{out}}\,b^{\varepsilon}_{\lambda_{\varepsilon}})\psi_{R}(\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon})^{2}&+C\varepsilon\left(\|\psi_{R}^{1/2}\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}+\|\nabla(\psi_{R}^{1/2}\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon})\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{I}}^{\varepsilon})}^{2}\right)\\
&+\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{2}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{\varepsilon}(\omega)}\int_{\varepsilon{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega^{k}}}b^{\varepsilon}_{\lambda_{\varepsilon}}\left(\langle\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon}\rangle_{\varepsilon{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega^{k}}}-\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon}\right)\psi_{R}\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon},\end{split}

(5.38)

where

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbbm{1}}_{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{out}}:=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{\varepsilon}(\omega)}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbbm{1}}_{\varepsilon{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega^{k}}}

is the characteristic function of the set of
\varepsilon
-scaled inclusions disjoint from
\overline{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}}
.

By means of the bound (
5.7
), the Poincaré inequality and Lemma
5.5
, we get

\Bigg|\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{2}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{\varepsilon}(\omega)}\int_{\varepsilon{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega^{k}}}b^{\varepsilon}_{\lambda_{\varepsilon}}\left(\langle\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon}\rangle_{\varepsilon{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega^{k}}}-\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon}\right)\psi_{R}\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon}\Bigg|\leq C\varepsilon\left(\|\psi_{R}^{1/2}\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}+\|\nabla(\psi_{R}^{1/2}\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon})\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{I}}^{\varepsilon})}^{2}\right),

so that (
5.38
) turns into

\begin{split}\mathcal{I}_{1}\leq\int_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{I}}^{\varepsilon}\cup{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}^{\varepsilon}}(\lambda_{\varepsilon}+\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{2}\,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbbm{1}}_{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{out}}\,b^{\varepsilon}_{\lambda_{\varepsilon}})\psi_{R}(\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon})^{2}+C\varepsilon\left(\|\psi_{R}^{1/2}\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}+\|\nabla(\psi_{R}^{1/2}\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon})\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{I}}^{\varepsilon})}^{2}\right).\end{split}

(5.39)

Next we analyse the integral on the right-hand side of (
5.39
). Suppose

\alpha\in(0,\sqrt{|\beta_{\infty}(\lambda_{0})|/\gamma}).

Let us tile
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with hypercubes of size
L
,

\mathbb{R}^{d}=\bigcup_{\xi\in L\,\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\square_{\xi}^{L},

where the value of
L
is chosen in accordance with Lemma
5.2
for some
\delta
satisfying

\alpha<\sqrt{\dfrac{|\beta_{\infty}(\lambda_{0})|-\delta}{\gamma}},

(5.40)

and define

J_{\mathrm{bd}}(\varepsilon)=\{\xi\in L\,\mathbb{Z}^{d}\ |\ \varepsilon\square_{\xi}^{L}\cap\partial{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}\neq\emptyset\ \ \text{or}\ \ \varepsilon\square_{\xi}^{L}\cap(\varepsilon{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}\setminus{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{I}}^{\varepsilon})\cap(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}})\neq\emptyset\},

J_{\mathrm{out}}(\varepsilon)=\{\xi\in L\,\mathbb{Z}^{d}\ |\ \varepsilon\square_{\xi}^{L}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}},\quad\xi\not\in J_{\mathrm{bd}}(\varepsilon)\}.

The set
J_{\mathrm{bd}}(\varepsilon)
labels hypercubes that either intersect the boundary of
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}
or have non-empty intersection with one of the inclusions that we have discarded at the very beginning, on the grounds that they had non-empty intersection with
\overline{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}}
, cf. Remark
2.10
.

To begin with, let us estimate the contribution from hypercubes labelled by
J_{\mathrm{bd}}(\varepsilon)
. In light of Corollary
5.4
, we have

\Bigg|\sum_{\xi\in J_{\mathrm{bd}}(\varepsilon)}\int_{({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{I}}^{\varepsilon}\cup{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}^{\varepsilon})\cap\varepsilon\square_{\xi}^{L}}(\lambda_{\varepsilon}+\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{2}\,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbbm{1}}_{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{out}}\,b^{\varepsilon}_{\lambda_{\varepsilon}})\psi_{R}(\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon})^{2}\Bigg|\leq C\sum_{\xi\in J_{\mathrm{bd}}(\varepsilon)}\int_{\varepsilon\square_{\xi}^{L}}(\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon})^{2}.

(5.41)

From (
5.18
) we have that
\{\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon}\}
is uniformly bounded in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, hence weakly compact, namely, up to a subsequence,

\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup u_{0}\quad\text{in}\quad H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

(5.42)

for some
u_{0}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Note that at this stage we are not yet in a position (nor need) to show that
u_{0}
is the macroscopic component of an eigenfunction of
\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{hom}}
corresponding to
\lambda_{0}
; this fact will be established in Section
6
. Let
U
be an open bounded set such that
\overline{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}}\subset U
. By the Rellich–Kondrachov theorem, up to a subsequence,

\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon}\to u_{0}\quad\text{in}\quad L^{2}(U).

(5.43)

For small enough
\varepsilon
we have

\bigcup_{\xi\in J_{\mathrm{bd}}(\varepsilon)}\varepsilon\square_{\xi}^{L}\subset U,

and

\Bigg|\bigcup_{\xi\in J_{\mathrm{bd}}(\varepsilon)}\varepsilon\square_{\xi}^{L}\Bigg|=o(1)\quad\text{as}\quad\varepsilon\to 0.

(5.44)

Then from (
5.43
) and (
5.44
) one easily gets

\sum_{\xi\in J_{\mathrm{bd}}(\varepsilon)}\int_{\varepsilon\square_{\xi}^{L}}(\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon})^{2}=o(1)\quad\text{as}\quad\varepsilon\to 0.

Let us then estimate the contribution to the integral on the right-hand side of (
5.39
) from hypercubes disjoint from
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}
and labelled by
J_{\mathrm{out}}(\varepsilon)
. Resorting to Corollary
5.4
, the Poincaré inequality, Lemma
5.5
, and Lemma
5.2
, we get

\begin{split}\sum_{\xi\in J_{\mathrm{out}}(\varepsilon)}\int_{({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{I}}^{\varepsilon}\cup{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}^{\varepsilon})\cap\varepsilon\square_{\xi}^{L}}(\lambda_{\varepsilon}&+\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{2}\,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbbm{1}}_{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{out}}\,b^{\varepsilon}_{\lambda_{\varepsilon}})\psi_{R}(\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon})^{2}=\sum_{\xi\in J_{\mathrm{out}}(\varepsilon)}\int_{\varepsilon\square_{\xi}^{L}}(\lambda_{\varepsilon}+\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{2}\,b^{\varepsilon}_{\lambda_{\varepsilon}})\psi_{R}(\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon})^{2}\\
&=\sum_{\xi\in J_{\mathrm{out}}(\varepsilon)}\int_{\varepsilon\square_{\xi}^{L}}\left(\lambda_{\varepsilon}+\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{2}\,b^{\varepsilon}_{\lambda_{\varepsilon}}\right)\psi_{R}^{1/2}\,\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon}\left(\psi_{R}^{1/2}\,\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon}-\langle\psi_{R}^{1/2}\,\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon}\rangle_{\varepsilon\square_{\xi}^{L}}\right)\\
&+\sum_{\xi\in J_{\mathrm{out}}(\varepsilon)}\int_{\varepsilon\square_{\xi}^{L}}\left(\lambda_{\varepsilon}+\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{2}\,b^{\varepsilon}_{\lambda_{\varepsilon}}\right)\langle\psi_{R}^{1/2}\,\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon}\rangle_{\varepsilon\square_{\xi}^{L}}\left(\psi_{R}^{1/2}\,\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon}-\langle\psi_{R}^{1/2}\,\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon}\rangle_{\varepsilon\square_{\xi}^{L}}\right)\\
&+\sum_{\xi\in J_{\mathrm{out}}(\varepsilon)}\int_{\varepsilon\square_{\xi}^{L}}\left(\lambda_{\varepsilon}+\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{2}\,b^{\varepsilon}_{\lambda_{\varepsilon}}\right)\langle\psi_{R}^{1/2}\,\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon}\rangle_{\varepsilon\square_{L}(\xi)}\langle\psi_{R}^{1/2}\,\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon}\rangle_{\varepsilon\square_{\xi}^{L}}\\
&\leq C\sum_{\xi\in J_{\mathrm{out}}(\varepsilon)}\|\psi_{R}^{1/2}\,\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\varepsilon\square_{\xi}^{L})}\|\varepsilon\nabla(\psi_{R}^{1/2}\,\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon})\|_{L^{2}(\varepsilon\square_{\xi}^{L})}\\
&+\sum_{\xi\in J_{\mathrm{out}}(\varepsilon)}\langle\lambda_{\varepsilon}+\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{2}\,b^{\varepsilon}_{\lambda_{\varepsilon}}\rangle_{\varepsilon\square_{\xi}^{L}}\langle\psi_{R}^{1/2}\,\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon}\rangle_{\varepsilon\square_{\xi}^{L}}\int_{\varepsilon\square_{\xi}^{L}}\psi_{R}^{1/2}\,\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon}\\
&\leq C\varepsilon\left(\|\psi_{R}^{1/2}\,\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}+\|\nabla(\psi_{R}^{1/2}\,\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon})\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{I}}^{\varepsilon})}^{2}\right)\\
&-(|\beta_{\infty}(\lambda_{0})|-\delta)\sum_{\xi\in J_{\mathrm{out}}(\varepsilon)}\int_{\varepsilon\square_{\xi}^{L}}\psi_{R}^{1/2}\,\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon}\langle\psi_{R}^{1/2}\,\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon}\rangle_{\varepsilon\square_{\xi}^{L}}\\
&\leq(C\varepsilon-|\beta_{\infty}(\lambda_{0})|+\delta)\|\psi_{R}^{1/2}\,\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}+C\varepsilon\|\nabla(\psi_{R}^{1/2}\,\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon})\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{I}}^{\varepsilon})}^{2}\,.\end{split}

(5.45)

Combining (
5.41
) and (
5.45
), we can estimate the integral on the RHS of (
5.39
) to obtain

\begin{split}\mathcal{I}_{1}&\leq(C\varepsilon-|\beta_{\infty}(\lambda_{0})|+\delta)\|\psi_{R}^{1/2}\,\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}+C\varepsilon\|\nabla(\psi_{R}^{1/2}\,\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon})\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{I}}^{\varepsilon})}^{2}+o(1)\,.\end{split}

(5.46)

Finally, substituting (
5.25
), (
5.32
) and (
5.26
), (
5.46
) into (
5.24
), we arrive at

(c-C\varepsilon)\,\|\nabla(\psi_{R}^{1/2}\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon})\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{I}}^{\varepsilon})}^{2}+(|\beta_{\infty}(\lambda_{0})|-\delta-\gamma\alpha^{2}-C\varepsilon)\|\psi_{R}^{1/2}\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}\leq C+o(1).

Hence,
in view of (
5.40
), for sufficiently small
\varepsilon
we get

\|\nabla(\psi_{R}^{1/2}\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon})\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{I}}^{\varepsilon})}^{2}+\|\psi_{R}^{1/2}\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}\leq C,

(5.47)

where
C
is a constant independent of
\varepsilon
and
R
.

Now, formulae (
5.47
) and (
5.1b
) imply

\|e^{\alpha|x|}\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon}\|_{H^{1}(B_{R}(0))}\leq C,

uniformly in
\varepsilon
and

. Letting

tend to

we obtain
(
5.3
).

It remains only to translate (

) into an

estimate for our original family of eigenfunctions

.
In view of the decomposition
u^{\varepsilon}=\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon}+u^{\varepsilon}_{0}
, we have

\begin{split}\|e^{\alpha|x|}u^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}&\leq\|e^{\alpha|x|}\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+\|e^{\alpha|x|}u^{\varepsilon}_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\\
&\leq\|e^{\alpha|x|}\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{\varepsilon}(\omega)}\|e^{\alpha|x|}u^{\varepsilon}_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\varepsilon{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega^{k}})}.\end{split}

(5.48)

The right-hand side of (
5.48
) can then be estimated by resorting to
(
5.3
),
(

) and (

(
5.2
).

6
Strong stochastic two-scale convergence

Using the results of Section
5
, we will show in this section that our sequence of eigenfunctions
\{u^{\varepsilon}\}
, up to a subsequence, strongly stochastically two-scale converges to an eigenfunction
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\overline{u}}^{0}\in H
of the operator
\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{hom}}
, thus completing the proof of Theorem
3.3
.

We will do this in two steps: first we will show that the sequence
\{u^{\varepsilon}\}
strongly stochastically two-scale converges, up to a subsequence, to some limit function
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\overline{u}}^{0}(x,\omega)=u_{0}(x)+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\overline{u}}_{1}(x,\omega)\in H
, and then we will argue that
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\overline{u}}^{0}
is an eigenfunction of
\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{hom}}
corresponding to
\lambda_{0}
.

Recall the decomposition
u^{\varepsilon}=\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon}+u^{\varepsilon}_{0}
,
where
\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon}
is the extension from Theorem
5.1

and
u^{\varepsilon}_{0}
is defined in accordance with (
5.27
).

We have that
\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon}
weakly converges in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
to some function
u_{0}
, see (
5.42
).
Because
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is not bounded, one cannot immediately argue strong convergence in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. However, this can be achieved with the help of Theorem
5.1
.

Lemma 6.1
.

We have

\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon}\to u_{0}\quad\text{in}\quad L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

(6.1)

Proof.

For a given
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have that
\{\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon}\}
is weakly compact in
H^{1}(B_{n}(0))
, hence strongly compact in
L^{2}(B_{n}(0))
. A standard diagonalisation argument gives us that, up to a subsequence,
\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon}\to u_{0}
in
L^{2}(B_{n}(0))
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
. The
H^{1}
-exponential decay (
5.3
) of
\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon}
implies

\|\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon}\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{n}(0))}\leq Ce^{-n\alpha}.

For every
\delta>0
, one can choose
n
sufficiently large and
\varepsilon
sufficiently small so that

\|\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{n}(0))}\leq\delta,\quad\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{n}(0))}\leq\delta,\quad\|\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon}-u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(B_{n}(0))}\leq\delta.

Hence

\|\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon}-u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq\|\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon}-u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(B_{n}(0))}+\|\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{n}(0))}+\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{n}(0))}\leq 3\delta,

which infers (
6.1
).
∎

Lemma 6.2
.

There exists
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\overline{u}}_{1}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)})
such that

u^{\varepsilon}_{0}\overset{2}{\longrightarrow}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\overline{u}}_{1}.

Furthermore, the function
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\overline{u}}_{1}
satisfies

\mathbb{E}\left[(\nabla_{y}u_{1}\cdot\nabla_{y}\psi-\lambda_{0}\,u_{1}\,\psi)\,1_{\Omega}\right]=\lambda_{0}(1-\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{D}})u_{0}\,\mathbb{E}\left[{\psi}\,1_{\Omega}\right],\qquad\forall\overline{\psi}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega),\,\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(6.2)

Proof.

Weak convergence
u^{\varepsilon}_{0}\overset{2}{\rightharpoonup}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\overline{u}}_{1}
to a function satisfying (
6.2
) follows from a straightforward adaptation of
[
9
, Proposition 4.1]
to the case at hand,
with account of the fact that
u^{\varepsilon}_{0}
satisfies

\varepsilon^{2}\int_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{I}}^{\varepsilon}}\nabla u^{\varepsilon}_{0}\cdot\nabla\varphi-\lambda_{\varepsilon}\int_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{I}}^{\varepsilon}}u^{\varepsilon}_{0}\varphi=\lambda_{\varepsilon}\int_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{I}}^{\varepsilon}}\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon}\varphi\qquad\forall\varphi\in H^{1}_{0}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{I}}^{\varepsilon}),

(6.3)

and that
\lambda_{\varepsilon}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbbm{1}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{I}}^{\varepsilon}}\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon}\overset{2}{\rightharpoonup}\lambda_{0}\,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1_{\Omega}\,\mathbbm{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\mathcal{D}}}\,u_{0}
. (Clearly,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\overline{u}}_{1}=0
in
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}}\times\Omega
.)

That the convergence is, in fact, strong can be established by means of a general argument due to Zhikov
[
30
,
31
]
. Namely, let
z^{\varepsilon}\in H^{1}_{0}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{I}}^{\varepsilon})
be the solution of

\varepsilon^{2}\int_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{I}}^{\varepsilon}}\nabla z^{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla\varphi-\lambda_{\varepsilon}\int_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{I}}^{\varepsilon}}z^{\varepsilon}\varphi=\lambda_{\varepsilon}\int_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{I}}^{\varepsilon}}u^{\varepsilon}_{0}\varphi\qquad\forall\varphi\in H^{1}_{0}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{I}}^{\varepsilon}).

(6.4)

Then, the same adaptation of
[
9
, Proposition 4.1]
gives us

z^{\varepsilon}\overset{2}{\rightharpoonup}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\overline{z}}(x,\omega)\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}),

where
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\overline{z}}
solves

\mathbb{E}[(\nabla_{y}z\cdot\nabla_{y}\psi-\lambda_{0}\,z\psi)\,1_{\Omega}]=\lambda_{0}\mathbb{E}[\overline{u}_{1}\overline{\psi}\,\overline{1_{\Omega}}]\qquad\forall\overline{\psi}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega).

(6.5)

By using
z^{\varepsilon}
and
u^{\varepsilon}_{0}
as test functions in (
6.3
) and (
6.4
), respectively, and equating the right-hand sides, we get

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\mathcal{I}^{\varepsilon}}(u^{\varepsilon}_{0})^{2}=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\mathcal{I}^{\varepsilon}}\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon}z^{\varepsilon}=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u_{0}\,\overline{z}\,dx\right].

(6.6)

Finally, by using
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\overline{z}}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\overline{u}}_{1}
as test functions in (
6.2
) and (
6.5
), respectively, we arrive at

\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u_{0}\,\overline{z}\,dx\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\overline{u}_{1}^{2}\,dx\right].

(6.7)

Formulae (
6.6
) and (
6.7
) give us strong stochastic two-scale convergence of
u_{0}^{\varepsilon}
.
∎

Lemma 6.3
.

The function
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\overline{u}}^{0}=u_{0}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\overline{u}}_{1}
is an eigenfunction of
\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{hom}}
corresponding to the eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}
, i.e. it satisfies
(
4.1a
)–(
4.1b
).

Proof.

The strategy of the proof is quite standard and consists in choosing appropriate test functions in (
5.15
) and passing to the limit. Recall that the functions
u^{\varepsilon}
,
\widetilde{u}^{\varepsilon}
and
u^{\varepsilon}_{0}
satisfy the estimates
(
5.1b
),
(
5.1c
),
(
5.16
)
and
(
5.30
).

Lemmata
6.1
and
6.2
, together with Theorem
B.1
, imply that, up to extracting subsequences, we have

(6.8)

for some

In conclusion, adapting an argument from

]
, we show that strong stochastic two-scale convergence of eigenfunctions implies that the multiplicity of eigenvalues is preserved in the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
, thus proving Theorem
3.5
. The latter establishes an asymptotic one-to-one correspondence between eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of
\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon}
and
\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{hom}}
as
\varepsilon\to 0
.

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
E^{\varepsilon}_{I}
be the spectral projection onto the interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}
associated with the operator
\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon}
. Suppose that

,
\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}=1
, satisfies

for some

. Then for every

The claim follows immediately from the Spectral Theorem.

Proof of Theorem
3.5
.

(a) For each
\varepsilon_{n}
let
u^{\varepsilon_{n}}_{j}
,

, a family of orthonormal eigenfunctions associated with
\lambda_{\varepsilon_{n},j}
,

. Theorem
3.3
tells us that
\lambda_{0}\in\sigma_{d}(\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{hom}})

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\overline{u}}^{0}_{j}
,

\delta_{jk}=(u^{\varepsilon_{n}}_{j},u^{\varepsilon_{n}}_{k})_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\to({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\overline{u}}^{0}_{j},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\overline{u}}^{0}_{k})_{H}\quad\text{as}\quad n\to+\infty,

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\overline{u}}^{0}_{j}

Appendix A
Stability of the essential spectrum

Theorem A.1
.

Let
T_{i},i=1,2,
be the non-negative self-adjoint operators on
L^{2}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{R}}^{d})
uniquely defined by the densely defined bilinear forms

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}a_{i}\nabla u\cdot\nabla v,\quad u,v\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),

where
a_{i}
,
i=1,2
, are measurable matrix-valued functions satisfying

0<\nu|\xi|^{2}\leq a_{i}\xi\cdot\xi\leq\nu^{-1}|\xi|^{2}\quad\forall\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

(A.1)

for some constant
\nu
. Assume that
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
differ only in a relatively compact set, i.e.
{\rm supp}(a_{1}-a_{2})\subset B_{R}
for sufficiently large
R
. Then

\sigma_{\rm ess}(T_{1})=\sigma_{\rm ess}(T_{2}).

Proof.

Let
\lambda\in\sigma_{\rm ess}(T_{1})
and
\varphi_{n}
be a corresponding Weyl sequence for
T_{1}
. Namely,
\|\varphi_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}=1
,
\varphi_{n}\rightharpoonup 0
weakly in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and

T_{1}\varphi_{n}=\lambda\varphi_{n}+h_{n},

(A.2)

where
\|h_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. From (
A.1
) it follows that
\varphi_{n}
is bounded in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and, hence, converges to zero weakly in
H^{1}(B_{R})
and strongly in
L^{2}(B_{R})
for any
R>0
. Multiplying (
A.2
) by
\eta_{2R}\varphi_{n}
and integrating by parts, we obtain

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\eta_{2R}\,a_{1}\nabla\varphi_{n}\cdot\nabla\varphi_{n}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi_{n}a_{1}\nabla\eta_{2R}\cdot\nabla\varphi_{n}=\lambda\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\eta_{2R}|\varphi_{n}|^{2}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}h_{n}\eta_{2R}\varphi_{n}.

From the latter and (
A.1
) one easily concludes that

\|\nabla\varphi_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(B_{R})}\leq\frac{C}{R}\|\varphi_{n}\|_{L^{2}(B_{2R})}\|\nabla\varphi_{n}\|_{L^{2}(B_{2R})}+\lambda\|\varphi_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(B_{2R})}+\|h_{n}\|_{L^{2}(B_{2R})}\|\varphi_{n}\|_{L^{2}(B_{2R})},

and, therefore,
\|\nabla\varphi_{n}\|_{L^{2}(B_{R})}\to 0
for any
R>0
. It follows that there exists a subsequence
n_{k}
such that

\|\varphi_{n_{k}}\|_{H^{1}(B_{k})}\to 0\mbox{ as }k\to\infty.

(A.3)

Next we follow the general argument utilised in Section
4
(and initially devised in
[
19
]
). Define
\psi_{k}:=(1-\eta_{k})\varphi_{n_{k}}
and
\widehat{\psi}_{k}:=(\lambda+1)(T_{2}+I)^{-1}\psi_{k}
. We claim that
\widehat{\psi}_{k}
is a Weyl sequence for the operator
T_{2}
corresponding to
\lambda
. Clearly,

(T_{2}-\lambda)\widehat{\psi}_{k}=(\lambda+1)(\psi_{k}-\widehat{\psi}_{k}).

Thus, in order to prove the claim, it is sufficient to show that
\|\psi_{k}-\widehat{\psi}_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}
vanishes in the limit. (Indeed, this would also imply via (
A.3
) that
\|\widehat{\psi}_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\to 1
and
\widehat{\psi}_{k}\rightharpoonup 0
weakly in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.) After observing that

\|\psi_{k}-\widehat{\psi}_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}\leq\tau_{2}(\psi_{k}-\widehat{\psi}_{k},\psi_{k}-\widehat{\psi}_{k}),

(A.4)

where
\tau_{i},i=1,2,
denotes the bilinear form of the operator
T_{i}+I
, we focus on the right hand side of the latter.

For an arbitrary
v\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
from the definition of
\widehat{\psi}_{k}
we have

\tau_{2}(\psi_{k}-\widehat{\psi}_{k},v)=\tau_{2}(\psi_{k},v)-(\lambda+1)(\psi_{k},v).

Since for sufficiently large
k
the defect
{\rm supp}(a_{1}-a_{2})
is contained in
B_{k/2}
, one has

\displaystyle\tau_{2}(\psi_{k},v)

\displaystyle-(\lambda+1)(\psi_{k},v)=\tau_{1}(\varphi_{n_{k}},v)-(\lambda+1)(\varphi_{n_{k}},v)

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\eta_{k}a_{1}\nabla\varphi_{n_{k}}\cdot\nabla v-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi_{n_{k}}a_{1}\nabla\eta_{k}\cdot\nabla v+\lambda\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\eta_{k}\varphi_{n_{k}}v.

We estimate the last three terms as follows:

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\eta_{k}a_{1}\nabla\varphi_{n_{k}}\cdot\nabla v\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\|\nabla\varphi_{n_{k}}\|_{L^{2}(B_{k})}\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}(B_{k})}\,,

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}a_{1}\varphi_{n_{k}}\nabla\eta_{k}\cdot\nabla v\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{k}\|\varphi_{n_{k}}\|_{L^{2}(B_{k})}\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}(B_{k})}\,,

\displaystyle\left|\lambda\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\eta_{k}\varphi_{n_{k}}v\right|

\displaystyle\leq\|\varphi_{n_{k}}\|_{L^{2}(B_{k})}\|v\|_{L^{2}(B_{k})}\,.

We conclude that

|\tau_{2}(\psi_{k}-\widehat{\psi}_{k},v)|\leq C\|\varphi_{n_{k}}\|_{H^{1}(B_{k})}\|v\|_{H^{1}(B_{k})}\leq C\|\varphi_{n_{k}}\|_{H^{1}(B_{k})}\sqrt{\tau_{2}(v,v)}\,,

(A.5)

where the last inequality follows from (
A.1
). Finally, replacing
v
with
\psi_{k}-\widehat{\psi}_{k}
in (
A.5
) and taking into account (
A.3
), we conclude from
(
A.4
) that

\|\psi_{k}-\widehat{\psi}_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\to 0.

The theorem is proved.
∎

Appendix B
Properties of two-scale convergence

In this appendix we summarise, in the form of a theorem, some properties of stochastic two-scale convergence used in our paper. We refer the reader to
[
32
]
for further details, see also
[
9
]
.

Theorem B.1
.

Stochastic two-scale convergence enjoys the following properties.

(i)

Let
\{u^{\varepsilon}\}
be a bounded sequence in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Then there exists
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\overline{u}}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\Omega)
such that, up to extracting a subsequence,

u^{\varepsilon}\overset{2}{\rightharpoonup}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\overline{u}}.

(ii)

If
u^{\varepsilon}\overset{2}{\rightharpoonup}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\overline{u}}
, then
\|{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\overline{u}}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\Omega)}\leq\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\|u^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}
.

(iii)

If
u^{\varepsilon}\to{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\overline{u}}
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, then
u^{\varepsilon}\overset{2}{\rightharpoonup}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\overline{u}}
.

(iv)

Let
\{v^{\varepsilon}\}
be a uniformly bounded sequence in
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that
v^{\varepsilon}\to v
in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
\|v\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}<+\infty
. Suppose that
\{u^{\varepsilon}\}
is a bounded sequence in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that

u^{\varepsilon}\overset{2}{\rightharpoonup}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\overline{u}}
for some
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\overline{u}}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\Omega)
. Then
v^{\varepsilon}u^{\varepsilon}\overset{2}{\rightharpoonup}v{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\overline{u}}
.

(v)

Let
\{u^{\varepsilon}\}
be a bounded sequence in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Then, there exist
u_{0}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\overline{p}}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{V}^{2}_{\rm pot}(\Omega)})
such that, up to extracting a subsequence,

u^{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup u_{0}\quad\text{in}\quad H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),

\nabla u^{\varepsilon}\overset{2}{\rightharpoonup}\nabla u_{0}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\overline{p}}.

(vi)

Let
\{u^{\varepsilon}\}
be a bounded sequence in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that
\{\varepsilon\nabla u^{\varepsilon}\}
is also bounded in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Then there exists
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\overline{u}}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};H^{1}(\Omega))
such that, up to extracting a subsequence,

u^{\varepsilon}\overset{2}{\rightharpoonup}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\overline{u}},

\varepsilon\nabla u^{\varepsilon}\overset{2}{\rightharpoonup}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\overline{\nabla_{y}u}}.
