Darcy’s law of yield stress fluids on a treelike network
Vincenzo Maria Schimmenti, Federico Lanza, Alex Hansen, Silvio Franz, Alberto Rosso, Laurent Talon, Andrea De Luca

Abstract
Understanding the flow of yield stress fluids in porous media is a major challenge. In particular, experiments and extensive numerical simulations report a non-linear Darcy law as a function of the pressure gradient. In this letter, we consider a tree-like porous structure for which the problem of the flow can be resolved exactly thanks to a mapping with the directed polymer (DP) with disordered bond energies on the Cayley tree. Our results confirm the non-linear behavior of the flow and expresses its full pressure-dependence via the density of low-energy paths of DP restricted to vanishing overlap. These universal predictions are confirmed by extensive numerical simulations.



In a series of experiments during the nineteenth century, Henry Darcy studied the flow of water in a cylinder filled with sand
Darcy 1856
and established the empirical law for the flow rate
Q
as a function of the pressure difference
P
between the two ends of the cylinder

Q=\kappa R^{2}P/(\eta L)

(1)

here
R
and
L
are respectively the radius and the length of the cylinder,
\eta
is the viscosity of the fluid. The permeability,
\kappa
, has the dimension of a surface and measures the ability of a given porous medium
Bear 1988
;
Sahimi 2011
;
Blunt 2017
;
Feder et al. 2022
to transmit a fluid. Darcy gave an interpretation to the permeability assuming that, in a medium, the flow is possible only along non-intersecting thin channels, each of radius
~R_{c}\ll R
. The flow along a single channel is given by the Poiseuille’s law which holds for empty cylinders and the total flow can be written as
Q=\pi R^{2}n^{\rm ch}\pi R_{c}^{4}P/(8\eta L)
, with
n^{\rm ch}
the number of channels per unit surface. Hence, the permeability can be identified as
\kappa=\pi n^{\rm ch}R_{c}^{4}/8
. The network of the channels of a real porous medium is more complex: channels have heterogeneous shape and can intersects. However the Darcy law is valid as far as the number of channels remains pressure-independent. This is not the case for yield stress fluids, such as suspensions
Fall et al. 2009
, gels
Piau 2007
, heavy oil
Pascal 1981
, slurries or cement
Coussot 2005
for which a minimum yield stress ,
\sigma_{Y}
, is needed to flow
Bingham 1922
. Hence, at low pressure gradient, these yield stress fluids behave like a solid and no flow is measured. However, increasing the pressure gradient, they start flow along more and more channels. Experiments
Al-Fariss and Pinder 1987
;
Chase and Dachavijit 2005
and numerical simulations
Lopez et al. 2003
;
Balhoff and Thompson 2004
;
Chen et al. 2005
indicated that the Darcy law is modified: below a threshold pressure
P_{0}
no flow occurs while, above it, the flow grows non-linearly with
P
. Three flowing regimes are observed
Talon and Bauer 2013
;
Chevalier and Talon 2015
: i) initially the flow grows linearly in
P-P_{0}
, but with an effective permeability which is very small ii) for larger pressure the flow grows non-linearly as
(P-P_{0})^{\beta}
(with
\beta\approx 2
)
Roux and Herrmann 1987
;
Liu et al. 2019
. iii) only above a saturation pressure
P_{\rm sat}\gg P_{0}
, the flow recovers the linear growth with the standard permeability
\kappa

Barnes 2000
.

Despite these detailed observations, a theoretical explanation for the non-linearity is still lacking. In this letter, we propose both an explanation and provide an explicit prediction for the modified Darcy law. We consider a porous structure with the geometry of a binary Cayley tree with
t
levels (see Fig.
1
). This geometry is the simplest with intersecting channels and is realistic for several biological networks (e.g. alveoli system in lungs
Mauroy et al. 2004
or leaf veins). In this work, we establish a precise mapping between the Darcy problem for yield stress fluids and the directed polymer on Cayley tree, a model displaying one-step replica symmetry breaking (1-RSB)
Mézard et al. 1987
;
Derrida and Spohn 1988
. Each individual channel in the porous structure has a pressure threshold which we identify with the energy of the directed polymer represented by that channel. We show that the first channels where flow occurs correspond to those low-energies directed polymers with small overlaps (i.e. they share a short common path). Next we modified the Kolmogorov Petrovsky Piskunov (KPP) approach proposed in
Brunet and Derrida 2011
to determine analytically their number,
n^{\rm ch}
, as a function of
x=P-P_{0}
(see Eq. (
13
)). Finally, we determine explicitly the low pressure behaviour, described by Eq. (
14
), the linear high pressure regime of Eq. (
15
) as well as the saturation pressure
P_{\rm sat}
(see Eq. (
16
)). At this pressure, the fluid flows along
\sim t
channels almost non-overlapping. By further increasing the pressure, new channels open without significantly changing the permeability of the network. This predictions are confirmed by direct numerical simulations.

Mapping to the directed polymer and large-
t
limit. —

Our model is a Cayley tree pore network filled by a Bingham fluid. A pressure
P
is applied on the root pore and a zero pressure at the leaves. In this model large open pores with a well-defined pressure are connected by tubes of random radius and length. The modified Poiseuille law for a Bingham fluid is an open problem, but in the limit
P\gg\tau
takes a simple for
Q_{\rm Pois}(P)=\frac{\pi R^{4}}{8\eta L}(P-\tau)_{+}
(see
Chen et al. 2005
). Here we denote
(x)_{+}=\max(0,x)
and
\tau=L\sigma_{Y}/R
and consider
P>0
.
We consider the simplified case in which only the thresholds fluctuate and the flow in a tube between the pore
i
, at the pressure
P_{i}
, and the pore
j
, at pressure
P_{j}<P_{i}
, reads:

Q_{ij}=(P_{i}-P_{j}-\tau_{ij})_{+}

(2)

The threshold
\tau_{ij}
is a random variable, drawn from a distribution
p(\tau)
. Hence a tube is open if
P_{i}-P_{j}>\tau_{ij}
.
A pore has one incoming tube and two outgoing tubes and inside it Kirchhoff’s law holds: the incoming flow must be equal to the sum of the outgoing ones. It follows that, given an open incoming tube, there must be at least one outgoing open tube. Thus along a channel from the root to a leaf flow occurs if all its
t
tubes are open.

As a consequence, the pressure
P_{0}
at which the first channel opens is given by:

P_{0}=\min_{\alpha}\sum_{(ij)\in\alpha}\tau_{ij}

(3)

where
\alpha
labels the
2^{t-1}
directed path connecting the root to a leaf.
As observed in
Liu et al. 2019
, the threshold pressure
P_{0}
identifies with the ground state energy of the associated directed polymer (DP) model. We define
\epsilon_{\alpha}
the energy of a directed path
\alpha
as the sum of the thresholds along
\alpha
:

\epsilon_{\alpha}=\sum_{(ij)\in\alpha}\tau_{ij}

(4)

It follows that
P_{0}=\min_{\alpha}\epsilon_{\alpha}
.
The key point of this letter is that also the pressures
P_{1},P_{2},\dots
at which a new channel opens are related to the low-energy levels of the DP. It is useful to label the directed paths
\alpha
by ordering the energies as
\epsilon_{0}<\epsilon_{1}<\epsilon_{2}<\ldots
.

Using Kirchhoff’s law (see section A of
Note1
) we compute the explicit expression for
P_{1}
:

P_{1}=\epsilon_{0}+\min_{\alpha\neq 0}\frac{\epsilon_{\alpha}-\epsilon_{0}}{1-\hat{q}_{0\alpha}/t}=\epsilon_{0}+\frac{\epsilon_{\alpha_{1}}-\epsilon_{0}}{1-\hat{q}_{0\alpha_{1}}/t}

(5)

Here
\hat{q}_{0\alpha}
stands for the overlap between the
\alpha
channel and the ground state, namely the number of common tubes between the two channels. The path
\alpha_{1}
realises the minimum and it is the channel that opens just above
P_{1}
.
It is crucial to remark that the minimization involves two terms: the term
\epsilon_{\alpha}-\epsilon_{0}
favors low-energy paths while the term
1/(1-\hat{q}_{0\alpha}/t)
selects the ones with a small overlap with the ground state.
The directed polymers on Cayley tree display one step replica symmetry breaking 1-RSB. This means that in the limit
t\to\infty
the overlap among any two low-energy directed paths is either
O(1)
or
\sim t

Mézard et al. 1987
(finite
t
corrections are also known
Derrida and Mottishaw 2016
;
Cao et al. 2017
). Hence, in this large-
t
limit, the channel
\alpha_{1}
corresponds to a path with low-energy and low-overlap (
\hat{q}_{0\alpha_{1}}=O(1)
), so that
\lim_{t\to\infty}1/(1-\hat{q}_{0\alpha_{1}}/t)=1
and
P_{1}=\epsilon_{\alpha_{1}}
. Moreover, the total flow reduces to the sum of the contribution from each channel, namely
Q_{t}(P)=(P-P_{0})/t+(P-P_{1})/t
for
P\in[P_{1},P_{2}]
(see section A of

The same property holds for higher pressures (see section B of

): the channels
\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3},\dots
correspond to paths with the low-energy and low-overlap and
P_{2},P_{3},\dots
coincide with the energies
\epsilon_{\alpha_{2}},\epsilon_{\alpha_{3}},\dots
(see sorted energies of Fig.

Similarly the total flow reduces to the sum of the contribution from each channel.

As a consequence the computation of the flow problem reduces to determine the growth of the number of open channels
n^{\rm ch}_{t}(x)
as a function of
x=P-P_{0}
. This number identifies with the number of low-energy levels of the directed polymer, provided they have low overlap among them. We compute this number adapting the tools introduced in
Brunet and Derrida 2011
and based on the mapping to the discrete Kolmogorov-Petrovsky-Piskunov (KPP) equation
Derrida and Spohn 1988
;
Majumdar and Krapivsky 2003
;
Majumdar and Krapivsky 2000
;
Majumdar and Krapivsky 2002
;
Arguin et al. 2011
;
Ramola et al. 2015
.

KPP approach. —

To begin, we introduce the number of energy levels with energy smaller than
\epsilon_{0}+x
, namely
m^{(\rm full)}_{t}(x)=\sum_{\alpha}\vartheta(x-(\epsilon_{\alpha}-\epsilon_{0}))

(
\vartheta(x)
is the Heaviside theta function). In
Brunet and Derrida 2011
(for a self-contained derivation see section B of
Note1
)

\overline{m^{(\rm full)}_{t}(x)}
(the overbar stands for the average over the random thresholds) is expressed as
\overline{m^{(\rm full)}_{t}(x)}=\int dx^{\prime}\,r_{t}(x^{\prime};x)
. The function
r_{t}(x^{\prime};x)
satisfies the following recursive equation:

\displaystyle r_{t+1}(x^{\prime};x)=2\int d\tau p(\tau)\Omega_{t}(x^{\prime}-\tau)r_{t}(x^{\prime}-\tau;x)

(6)

\displaystyle\Omega_{t+1}(x)=\int d\tau p(\tau)\Omega_{t}(x-\tau)^{2}

(7)

Here
p(\tau)
the thresholds distribution and the initial conditions read
r_{1}(x^{\prime};x)=p(x+x^{\prime})
and
\Omega_{1}(x)=\int_{x}^{\infty}d\tau p(\tau)
.
Equation (
7
) is the discrete KPP equation and the function
\Omega_{t}(x)
is the probability that the ground state energy of the DP on a Cayley tree of
t
levels is larger than
x
. Hence equation (
7
) corresponds to growing a
t+1
-level tree starting from two
t
-level trees
Derrida and Spohn 1988
.

. The corresponding value for
\beta_{c}

To determine the Darcy flow we are interested in a subset of low-energy paths, namely the ones that contribute to
n^{\rm ch}_{t}(x)=\sum_{i}\vartheta(x-(P_{i}-P_{0}))
with
i=1\dots 2^{t-1}-1
.
As discussed above, in the limit
t\to\infty
, the open channels coincide with the low-energy paths with vanishing overlap and the pressures
P_{1},P_{2},\dots
with the corresponding path energies
\epsilon_{\alpha_{1}},\epsilon_{\alpha_{2}},\dots
.
To make progress we introduce the quantity
m_{\hat{q}}(x)
which counts, in an infinite tree, the number of paths with energy
\epsilon_{\alpha}\leq P_{0}+x
and maximum overlap between them
\hat{q}
. With this prescription, we immediately take limit
t\to\infty
and only consider low-energy paths with vanishing overlap,
\hat{q}\ll t
. This remains valid even in the limit
\hat{q}\to\infty
, so that

n^{\rm ch}_{\infty}(x)=\lim_{\hat{q}\to\infty}m_{\hat{q}}(x)\;.

(8)

To compute
\overline{m_{\hat{q}}(x)}
, we modify the KPP approach introduced for
\overline{m^{(\rm full)}_{t}(x)}
.
For this, we introduce a pruning procedure (see fig.
2
): at the level
\hat{q}
of the full Cayley tree, there are
2^{\hat{q}}
subtrees labelled by
a=1,\ldots,2^{\hat{q}}
. We replace each of these subtrees with a single tube with a minimum energy
\epsilon^{(a)}_{0}
. This way we obtain a tree of
\hat{q}
levels containing the
2^{\hat{q}}
low energies paths with maximal overlap
\hat{q}
.
This procedure is equivalent to growing a tree with
\hat{q}
levels where the leaves thresholds are drawn from the distribution of the minimum of an infinite tree. The probability
w_{\rm min}(x)
that the minimal energy of an infinite tree is larger than
x
is obtained from the fixed-point travelling wave solution of equation (
7
):

w_{\rm min}(x+c(\beta_{c}))=\int d\tau p(\tau)w_{\rm min}^{2}(x-\tau)

(9)

where
c(\beta_{c})
is the minimal value for which (
9
) has a solution and its value can be obtained as:

\displaystyle\beta_{c}=\arg\min_{\beta}c(\beta)

(10a)

\displaystyle c(\beta)=\frac{1}{\beta}\log\left(2\int d\tau p(\tau)e^{-\beta\tau}\right)

(10b)

The function
w_{\rm min}(x)
is a sigmoid with
w_{\rm min}(x)\simeq 1-x\exp{(\beta_{c}x)}
for
x\to-\infty
and
w_{\rm min}(x)\simeq 0
for
x\to\infty
. The solution of eq. (
9
) is defined up to an arbitrary shift that we set to zero for simplicity. For large but finite
t
, it has been proven
Bramson 1983
that :

P_{0}=\epsilon_{0}=-c(\beta_{c})t+\frac{3}{2\beta_{c}}\log t+\chi_{0},

(11)

where the first two terms are deterministic while
\chi_{0}
is a random variable of order
1
distributed according to
-w^{\prime}_{min}(\chi_{0})
.
In our problem, the limit
t\to\infty
can be safely taken as the divergent deterministic part is unimportant, being the same for all leaves.

Thus, to compute
\overline{m_{\hat{q}}(x)}
, one has to implement the recurrence in
\hat{q}
instead of
t
, replacing
r_{t}(x^{\prime};x)
with
r_{\hat{q}}(x^{\prime};x)
.
Moreover, in equation (
6
),
\Omega_{t}(x)
is replaced with
w_{\rm min}(x+c(\beta_{c})\hat{q})
and the initial condition is

r_{\hat{q}=1}(x^{\prime};x)=-w_{\rm min}^{\prime}(x+x^{\prime})
.
Finally, as before,
\overline{m_{\hat{q}}(x)}=\int dx^{\prime}\,r_{\hat{q}}(x^{\prime};x)
.

In the limit
t\to\infty
a closed-form expression for
\overline{m^{(\rm full)}_{\infty}(x)}
is not known
Cao et al. 2016
however in
Brunet and Derrida 2011
it was shown numerically that the following asymptotic holds:

\overline{m^{(\rm full)}_{\infty}(x)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle x\gg 1}}{{=}}Axe^{\beta_{c}x}

(12)

with
A
a non-universal
O(1)
constant.
On the contrary, direct numerical integration of
r_{\hat{q}}(x^{\prime};x)
(Fig.
3
(right)) show that, when
\hat{q}\to\infty
, the expression of
n^{\rm ch}_{\infty}(x)
is:

n^{\rm ch}_{\infty}(x)=\lim_{\hat{q}\to\infty}\overline{m_{\hat{q}}(x)}=e^{\beta_{c}x}.

(13)

In
Note1
, we also provide an analytical argument to support this result.

Determination of the flow. —

At low pressure, for a fixed
x=P-P_{0}
, there is are finite number of channels, sharing low overlap and each supporting a flow
(x-x^{\prime})/t
, being
P_{0}+x^{\prime}
its opening pressure. In this regime the total flow reduces the sum of independent contributions
Q_{t}(P_{0}+x)=\int_{0}^{x}dx^{\prime}\,n^{\rm ch}_{t}(x-x^{\prime})/t
. For large
t
this leads to

\overline{Q_{t}(P_{0}+x)}=\frac{e^{\beta_{c}x}-1}{\beta_{c}t}\;\quad\textrm{with }P\gtrsim P_{0}

(14)

This expression captures the first two regimes of the flow: when
P\to P_{0}
the flow is linear
Q(P)=(P-P_{0})/t
, for larger pressure a non linear regime takes over.
At very high pressure instead all channels are open and we recover the second linear behavior with:

Q_{t}(P)=\kappa(P-P^{*})\quad\textrm{with }P\to\infty

(15)

For large
t
,
\kappa=1/2
and
P^{*}=\overline{\tau}t
(with
\overline{\tau}=\int\tau p(\tau)d\tau
. See section C of
Note1
).

The crossover between the non-linear regime of equation (
14
) and the linear regime of equation (
15
) occurs at the pressure
P_{\rm sat}
. An estimation of
P_{\rm sat}
is obtained by matching the effective permeability at low pressure,
\overline{\kappa_{\rm eff}}=d\overline{Q_{t}}/dP\sim e^{\beta_{c}(P-P_{0})}/t
, with the value
\kappa=1/2
at high pressure:

P_{\rm sat}=P_{0}+(1/\beta_{c})\ln t

(16)

As consequence, at the saturation pressure, the number of channels obtained by eq. (
13
) is
\sim t
. Let us comment about this result.
When the pressure is slightly above the minimal value
P_{0}
, only a single channel is open and
\kappa_{\rm eff}
is
\sim 1/t
. Increasing the pressure slightly more (
\sim\ln t
), it is enough to have
\sim t
channels with very small overlap between them and to reach the total permeability
\kappa
. Note that this number is very small compare to
2^{t-1}
, the total number of directed paths. At even larger pressure, the fluid flows indeed in more and more channel, but this does not affect much the permeability of the network. To check these results we carried out exact numerical simulations on the Cayley tree with moderate
t
. For a given finite tree the flow curve as function of the pressure is a piece-wise linear function, with breakpoints at
P_{0},P_{1},\ldots,P_{n^{\rm ch}{}}
, the pressures at which a new channel opens. For each segment the flow reads
Q(P)=\kappa_{\rm eff}(P-P_{\rm eff}^{*}).
The first parameter,
\kappa_{\rm eff}
, is the permeability of the set of open channels, while
P_{\rm eff}^{*}
depends on the threshold along them. In Fig.
3
, we study their behavior as a function of
n^{\rm ch}{}
. In Fig.
3
middle, we observe that the permeability grows quickly with
n^{\rm ch}{}
and after
n_{\rm SAT}^{\rm ch}\sim t
reach the value
\kappa=1/2
(see inset of Fig.
3
middle). The converse is not true for
P_{\rm eff}^{*}
which grows slowly as shown in Fig.
3
right.

Conclusions. —

In this work, we show that the Darcy problem with a yield stress fluid is closely related to the associated directed polymer. In particular, in the limit of large trees, a direct mapping emerges between
n^{\rm ch}
and low-energy directed paths with zero-overlap. Thanks to this identification, we derive a simple universal expression for the flow as a function of the applied pressure. Equation (
14
) is independent of most microscopic details. However, the threshold distribution and the tree branching ratio set the parameter
\beta_{c}
.

The next big challenge would be to solve the problem of the flow in finite dimension. In particular, it would be interesting to understand the role of the low-energy and low-overlap energy levels. Those low-overlap excitations are abundant in mean-field glassy disordered systems but their number is suppressed in finite dimension.
For this reason, their role in realistic set-ups has always been controversial. However, in the Darcy problem, excitations with high overlap are strongly penalized and they are inessential in increasing the flow, independently of the spatial dimension. For this reason, the Cayley tree solution of the flow can give important insights on finite dimensional porous media. In particular the high pressure behaviour of Eq.(
15
) holds in all dimensions and we expect that the scenario depicted in Fig.
3
middle and right holds as well. In a real porous medium, we predict that the effective permeability grows initially fast and saturates to
\kappa
, while
P^{*}_{eff}
evolves slowly to
P^{*}=t\bar{\tau}
.
Moreover, from the Cayley tree solution, we know that the permeability of the network of flowing channels is governed by a small number of independent channels.
It would be interesting to understand if this remains true in finite dimensions.

Q_{1,t}(P)
:

P^{\prime}(P)=\frac{\hat{q}_{0\alpha}(\epsilon_{0}+\epsilon_{\alpha})+(t-\hat{q}_{0\alpha})P}{t+\hat{q}_{0\alpha}}-\epsilon_{0\alpha}\;,\qquad Q_{1,t}(P)=\frac{2}{t+\hat{q}_{0\alpha}}\left(P-\frac{\epsilon_{0}+\epsilon_{\alpha}}{2}\right)

(S5)

We can now determine the pressure
\tilde{P}_{1}
such that
Q_{0,t}(\tilde{P}_{1})=Q_{1,t}(\tilde{P}_{1})
, namely

\tilde{P}_{1}=\epsilon_{0}+\frac{t}{t-\hat{q}_{0\alpha}}(\epsilon_{\alpha}-\epsilon_{0})

(S6)

For pressure
P\in(P_{0},\tilde{P}_{1})
we have
Q_{0,t}(\tilde{P}_{1})>Q_{1,t}(\tilde{P}_{1})
, this means that the fluid cannot flow in the second channel and the flow rate is given by
Q_{0,t}(P)
. Above
\tilde{P}_{1}
the second channel is open and the flow rate is given by
Q_{1,t}(\tilde{P}_{1})
. The criterion to select the first excited channel that opens above
P_{0}
, is that the pressure
P_{1}
is the smallest among all the
\tilde{P}_{1}
computed for all possible two-channel geometries. This translates into

P_{1}=\min_{\tilde{P}_{1}}\tilde{P}_{1}=\epsilon_{0}+\min_{\alpha\neq 0}\frac{t}{t-\hat{q}_{0\alpha}}(\epsilon_{\alpha}-\epsilon_{0})

(S7)

The channel satisfying the minimum condition is denoted by
\alpha_{1}
.

I.2
The three channels problem

There are three possible configurations for the position of the second excited channel with respect to the ground state and the first one. They each lead to a slightly different expression for the pressure
P_{2}
, but all simplify to
P_{2}=\epsilon_{\alpha_{2}}
in the limit
t\to\infty
.

The first case is the simplest: the second channel opens with a common overlap

\hat{q}_{0\alpha_{2}}<\hat{q}_{0\alpha_{1}}

\hat{q}_{0\alpha_{2}}<\hat{q}_{0\alpha_{1}}

\hat{q}_{0\alpha_{2}}=O(1)

The second case corresponds to the opening of the second excited channel from the ground state with an overlap

This expression is identical to equation (

P_{2}=\epsilon_{0}+\frac{t}{t-\hat{q}_{0\alpha_{1}}}(\epsilon_{\alpha_{1}}-\epsilon_{0})+\min_{\alpha_{2}\neq\{\alpha_{1},0\}}\frac{t+\hat{q}_{0\alpha_{1}}}{t-\hat{q}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}}(\epsilon_{\alpha_{2}}-\epsilon_{\alpha_{1}})

II
B. KPP equation

The problem of a directed polymer on the Cayley tree can be studied using the formalism of the discrete KPP equation (see
Derrida and Spohn 1988
;
Brunet and Derrida 2011
for the original literature). A binary Cayley tree of
t+1
levels has
2^{t}
distinct paths denoted by
\alpha
. It can be constructed by considering two independent trees with
t
levels and joining them together by adding a bond with threshold
\tau
. This hierarchical structure can be exploited when we want to study functions of the energies with a multiplicative form

G_{t}(x)=\overline{\prod_{\alpha}g(x-\epsilon_{\alpha}))}

(S13)

for some
g(x)
. Indeed
G_{t+1}(x)
and
G_{t}(x)
can be related by the following discrete KPP recursive equation:

G_{t+1}(x)=\int d\tau p(\tau)G^{2}_{t}(x-\tau)

(S14)

where
p(\tau)
is the probability distribution of a single threshold and the initial conditions reads
G_{t=0}(x)=g(x)
.

The discrete KPP equation (
S14
) allows for travelling wave solutions of the form

G_{t}(x)=w(x+ct)\qquad\textrm{when}\,\,t\to\infty

(S15)

where
c
is the velocity of the traveling wave. When the initial conditions
G_{0}(x)=g(x)
satisfy
\lim_{x\to\infty}g(x)=0
and
\lim_{x\to-\infty}g(x)=1
,
c
is positive and the front moves backward (when the limits are reversed
c
is negative and the front propagates in the forward direction). The form of the front
w(x)
satisfies the following fixed point equation:

w(x+c)=\int d\tau p(\tau)w^{2}(x-\tau)

(S16)

We consider initial conditions of the form
g(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle x\to-\infty}}{{=}}1-e^{\beta x}
. By substituting this form into equation (
S16
) and expanding at first order in
e^{\beta x}
one obtains an equation for
c
as a function of
\beta
:

c(\beta)=\frac{1}{\beta}\log\left(2\int d\tau p(\tau)e^{-\beta\tau}\right)

(S17)

The velocity is shown, as a function of
\beta
, in figure (
S3
) and presents a minimum at
\beta_{c}
. It was proven
Kolmogorov et al. 1937
;
Bramson 1983
;
Majumdar and Krapivsky 2003
that the increasing branch of
c(\beta)
is unstable and that for
\beta>\beta_{c}
both the velocity and the front shape freeze, namely
c(\beta>\beta_{c})=c(\beta_{c})
and the front shape takes the form
w_{\rm min}(x)
satisfying the fixed point equation:

w_{\rm min}(x+c(\beta_{c}))=\int d\tau p(\tau)w_{\rm min}^{2}(x-\tau)

(S18)

This equation corresponds to equation (9) of the main text. Indeed for
g(x)=\vartheta(-x)
, which corresponds to
\beta\to\infty
We have

\Omega_{t}(x)=\overline{\prod_{\alpha}\vartheta(\epsilon_{\alpha}-x)}=\overline{\vartheta(\epsilon_{0}-x)}\;,\qquad\epsilon_{0}=\min_{\alpha}\epsilon_{\alpha}

(S19)

where
1-\Omega_{t}(x)
is the cumulative distribution of the minimum energy of a directed polymer of
t
levels. Hence the directed polymer with minimal energy on a Cayley tree with
t
levels for large
t
is

P_{0}=\epsilon_{0}=-c(\beta_{c})t+\frac{3}{2\beta_{c}}\log t+\chi_{0}

(S20)

The extensive part of the minimal energy,
-c(\beta_{c})t
, comes from the velocity selection criterion. The subextensive logarithmic correction cannot be obtain by our first order analysis of equation (
S16
) but was proven by Bramson
Bramson 1983
. The term
\chi_{0}
is a random variable of order
1
whose cumulative distribution is
1-w_{\rm min}(x)
.

Left panel: the dispersion relation of the velocity (black solid line) as a function of

c(\beta)=\ln 2/\beta+\beta\sigma^{2}/2

\Omega_{t}(x)
approaches the stationary solution
w_{\rm min}(x)
(red line), up to a
t

Note that
w_{\rm min}(x)
moves exactly at velocity
c(\beta_{c})
while the location of the front
\Omega_{t}(x)
is
-c(\beta_{c})t
up to logarithmic corrections (
S20
).

II.1
Average number of energy levels above the minimum

Using this KPP formalism, we can also study the number of energy levels with an energy less or equal than
x
:

n_{t}(x)=\sum_{\alpha}\vartheta(x-\epsilon_{\alpha})

(S21)

Indeed by choosing as initial condition
g(x)=1+\vartheta(x)(\lambda-1)
we can obtain the generating function of
n_{t}(x)
, namely
\Psi_{t}(x;\lambda)=\overline{\lambda^{n_{t}(x)}}
.
However, we are interested in the average number of energy levels having an energy
\epsilon_{\alpha}
bigger than the minimal energy
\epsilon_{0}
of an amount
x
. We denote this quantity by:

m^{(\rm full)}_{t}(x)=\overline{\sum_{\alpha}\vartheta(x+\epsilon_{0}-\epsilon_{\alpha})}=\overline{n_{t}(\epsilon_{0}+x)}

(S22)

This quantity is more complex than
n_{t}(x)
since it involves the minimal energy
\epsilon_{0}
. However, following an approach introduced in
Brunet and Derrida 2011
, it is possible to derive an equation for
m^{(\rm full)}_{t}(x)
that we can solve numerically.
We sketch here the derivation. The reader uninterested to the technical details can skip this section.

The first step is to introduce the generating function of
m_{t}^{(\rm full)}(x)
with
|\lambda|<1
:

\chi_{t}(x;\lambda)=\overline{\lambda^{m_{t}^{(\rm full)}(x)}}

(S23)

This function satisfies the following identity:

\chi_{t}(x;\lambda)=\int dx^{\prime}\;\overline{\lambda^{n_{t}(x^{\prime}+x)}\delta(x^{\prime}-\epsilon_{0})}=1+\partial_{x}\int dx^{\prime}\;\overline{\lambda^{n_{t}(x^{\prime}+x)}\vartheta(\epsilon_{0}-x^{\prime})}

(S24)

To prove the validity of (
S24
) we used
(\partial_{x}\lambda^{n})\vartheta=(\partial_{x^{\prime}}\lambda^{n})\vartheta=\partial_{x^{\prime}}(\lambda^{n}\vartheta)-\lambda^{n}(\partial_{x^{\prime}}\vartheta)
.
The integrand of equation (
S24
) satisfies a discrete KPP equation (
S14
) since it can be recast into a multiplicative form:

\lambda^{n_{t}(x^{\prime}+x)}\vartheta(\epsilon_{0}-x^{\prime})=\prod_{\alpha}\lambda^{\vartheta(x^{\prime}+x-\epsilon_{\alpha})}\vartheta(\epsilon_{\alpha}-x^{\prime})

(S25)

In this case, the initial condition reads
g(x^{\prime};x,\lambda)=\lambda^{\vartheta(x^{\prime}+x)}\vartheta(-x^{\prime})
.
The average number of energy levels above the minimum can be written as:

\overline{m^{(\rm full)}_{t}(x)}=\left.\partial_{\lambda}\chi_{t}(x;\lambda)\right|_{\lambda=1}

(S26)

We thus expand for
\lambda=1-\varepsilon

\overline{\lambda^{n_{t}(x^{\prime}+x)}\vartheta(\epsilon_{0}-x^{\prime})}=\Omega_{t}(x^{\prime})-\varepsilon R_{t}(x^{\prime};x)+O(\epsilon^{2})

(S27)

The function
\Omega_{t}(x)
is defined in (
S19
).
This expansion leads to:

\overline{m^{(\rm full)}_{t}(x)}=\int dx^{\prime}\;\partial_{z}R_{t}(x^{\prime};x)=\int dx^{\prime}\;r_{t}(x^{\prime};x)

(S28)

where we set
r_{t}(x^{\prime};x)=\partial_{x}R_{t}(x^{\prime};x)
.
Now, by plugging the expansion (
S27
) in (
S14
) and taking the first order in
\varepsilon
, we obtain a linear equation for
R_{t}(x^{\prime};x)

R_{t+1}(x^{\prime};x)=2\int d\tau\;p(\tau)\Omega_{t}(x^{\prime}-\tau)R_{t}(x^{\prime}-\tau;x)\;,\qquad R_{t=0}(x^{\prime};x)=\frac{1}{2}\vartheta(x^{\prime}+x)\vartheta(-x^{\prime})\;.

(S29)

Moreover because of the linearity of (
S29
),
r_{t}(x;z)
satisfies

r_{t+1}(x^{\prime};x)=2\int d\tau\;p(\tau)\Omega_{t}(x^{\prime}-\tau)r_{t}(x^{\prime}-\tau;x)\;,\qquad r_{t=0}(x^{\prime};x)=\frac{1}{2}\delta(x^{\prime}+x)

(S30)

By numerically solving simultaneously the equation for
\Omega_{t}(x)
and (
S30
), one has direct access to the
t
dependence of
\overline{m_{t}^{(\rm full)}(x)}
(
S28
).

As reported in the main text, the same equations hold for the determination of
m_{\hat{q}}(x)
. Indeed equation (
S30
) changes as:

r_{\hat{q}+1}(x^{\prime};x)=2\int d\tau\;p(\tau)w_{\rm min}(x^{\prime}-\tau+c(\beta_{c}))r_{\hat{q}}(x^{\prime}-\tau;x)\;,\qquad r_{\hat{q}=0}(x^{\prime};x)=-w^{\prime}_{\rm min}(x^{\prime}+x)

(S31)

Yielding:

m_{\hat{q}}(x)=\int dx^{\prime}\,r_{\hat{q}}(x^{\prime};x)\;.

(S32)

II.2
Numerical integration of discrete KPP equation

The directed numerical integration of
\Omega_{t}(x)
(
S19
) is not feasible at large
t
as the position of the front moves in the backward direction. In order to maintain the integration limits fixed in a window
[x_{\rm min},x_{\rm max}]
, one needs to shift the front by fixing its position as
t
grows. Moreover to ensure a better numerical stability we evolve
H_{t}(x)=e^{-\beta_{c}x}(1-\Omega_{t}(x))
then the equation for (
S19
) becomes:

H_{t+1}(x)=\int d\tau\tilde{p}(\tau)[2H_{t}(x-\tau)-e^{\beta_{c}(x-\tau)}H_{t}(x-\tau)]

(S33)

with
\tilde{p}(\tau)=p(\tau)e^{-\beta_{c}\tau}
and initial condition
H_{0}(x)=\vartheta(x)e^{-\beta_{c}x}
. To fix the position of
H_{t}(x)
we compute numerically (Riemann sum)
b_{t}=\int dxH_{t}(x)e^{\beta_{c}x}
. At each step
t
if
b_{t}-b_{0}>0
we make the substitution
H_{t}(x)\to H_{t}(x-(b_{t}-b_{0}))
. As we shift we should assign a value of
H_{t}(x)
to the points in
[x_{\rm min},x_{\rm min}+b_{t}-b_{0}]
; to do so interpolate the last
10
points using a spline.
We employed
p(\tau)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-x^{2}/2}
and discretized our integration limits
[x_{\rm min},x_{\rm max}]
in
N=150000
points with
x_{\rm min}=-10000
and
x_{\rm max}=30
. The equation for
r_{t}(x;z)
(
S30
) is solved in parallel to
H_{t}(x)
taking into account the aforementioned translations. We use the same procedure to solve for
r_{\hat{q}}(x;z)
.

II.3
Large
\hat{q}
limit of maximal overlap energy levels

In Eq. (
13
) of the main text, we report the convergence of
\overline{m_{\hat{q}}(x)}
to
e^{\beta_{c}x}
as
\hat{q}\to\infty
. To get further insights about the large
\hat{q}
behavior of
\overline{m_{\hat{q}}(x)}
, we further analyze Eq. (
S31
). As it is obtained from the linearization of the full KPP equation (
S14
) which supports travelling waves, it still induces a translation at velocity
c(\beta_{c})
. In order to get rid of this translation effect, we introduce the shifted quantity
r_{\hat{q}}^{(s)}(x^{\prime};x)=r_{\hat{q}}(x^{\prime}-c(\beta_{c})\hat{q};x)
. In this way, Eq. (
S31
) becomes

r_{\hat{q}+1}^{(s)}(x^{\prime};x)=2\int d\tau\;p(\tau)w_{\rm min}(x^{\prime}-\tau-c(\beta_{c}))r_{\hat{q}}^{(s)}(x^{\prime}-\tau-c(\beta_{c});x)=\int dx^{\prime\prime}\mathcal{L}(x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime})r_{\hat{q}}^{(s)}(x^{\prime\prime};x)

(S34)

where in the last equality we changed the integration variable setting
x^{\prime\prime}=x^{\prime}-\tau-c(\beta_{c})
and implicitly defined the linear operator
\mathcal{L}
. Thus, one can pass from
\hat{q}\to\hat{q}+1
by applying a fixed matrix
\mathcal{L}(x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime})
2
2

2

We use the expression
matrix
, although compact linear operator would be more appropriate in the mathematical jargon.
. Since the matrix
\mathcal{L}
is independent of
\hat{q}
, iterating this equation
\hat{q}
times, we can express
r_{\hat{q}}^{(s)}
in terms of the initial condition at
\hat{q}=0

r_{\hat{q}}^{(s)}(x^{\prime};x)=\int dx^{\prime\prime}\mathcal{L}^{\hat{q}}(x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime})r_{0}^{(s)}(x^{\prime\prime};x)=-\int dx^{\prime\prime}\mathcal{L}^{\hat{q}}(x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime})w^{\prime}_{\rm min}(x^{\prime\prime}+x)

(S35)

where we denoted as
\mathcal{L}^{\hat{q}}
the
\hat{q}
-th matrix power of
\mathcal{L}
and in the last equality we used (
S31
).
In the limit
\hat{q}\to\infty
,
\mathcal{L}
acts as a projector on the eigenspace with largest eigenvalues. Taking the derivative of Eq. (

) with respect to

, we obtain

w_{\rm min}^{\prime}(x^{\prime})=\int dx^{\prime\prime}\mathcal{L}(x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime})w^{\prime}_{\rm min}(x^{\prime\prime})\;.

which shows that
w_{\rm min}^{\prime}(x^{\prime})

.
We also have

1
. Therefore, without further knowledge of the full spectrum of

, we know that the remaining eigenvectors lie inside the unit circle. We can thus expand

r_{0}^{(s)}(x^{\prime};x)\equiv-w^{\prime}_{\rm min}(x^{\prime}+x)=-A(x)w^{\prime}_{\rm min}(x^{\prime})+R(x^{\prime};x)

where the first term contains the components on the leading eigenvector and

\mathcal{L}

so that we can express the coefficient

A(x)=\frac{\int dx^{\prime}\ell_{\rm min}(x^{\prime})w_{\rm min}^{\prime}(x^{\prime}+x)}{\int dx^{\prime}\ell_{\rm min}(x^{\prime})w_{\rm min}^{\prime}(x^{\prime})}

(S40)

Computing the adjoint of
\mathcal{L}
, we see that
\ell_{\rm min}(x^{\prime})

\ell_{\rm min}(x^{\prime})=2w_{\rm min}(x^{\prime})\int dx^{\prime\prime}\;\ell_{\rm min}(x^{\prime\prime})p(x^{\prime\prime}-x^{\prime}-c(\beta_{c}))

(S41)

\ell_{\rm min}(x)

\hat{q}

2\int dx^{\prime}\;e^{-\tilde{\beta}x^{\prime}}p(x^{\prime}-x-c(\beta_{c}))=e^{-\tilde{\beta}x}

and after the change of variables

Since

13

which gives the expected exponential behavior.

III
C. Determination of the flow

III.1
Large Pressure limit

The hierarchical structure of the tree allows to write a recursive equation in
t
for the flow:

\displaystyle Q_{t}(P)=(P-P^{\prime}-\tau)_{+}

(S44)

\displaystyle(P-P^{\prime}-\tau)_{+}=Q_{t-1}^{(1)}(P^{\prime})+Q_{t-1}^{(2)}(P^{\prime})

(S45)

the superscripts above indicates two different branches of the tree. The second equation come from Kirchhoff law applied at the node joining a new tube with two different sub-trees. This node is at pressure
P^{\prime}
which must be found self-consistently as a function of
P
. As reported in the main text, this is a non-feasible task for generic
t
. However in the
P\to\infty
regime all the channels are open, the flow takes a simple linear form:

Q_{t}(P)=\kappa_{t}(P-P_{t}^{*})

(S46)

Inserting this ansatz in the second equation we get:

P^{\prime}=\frac{P-\tau+\kappa_{t-1}(P_{t,1}^{*}+P_{t,2}^{*})}{1+2\kappa_{t-1}}

(S47)

A recursive equation for the permeability,

\kappa_{t}=\frac{2\kappa_{t-1}}{1+2\kappa_{t-1}}

(S48)

together with
\kappa_{1}=1
allows to find the solution :

\kappa_{t}=\frac{2^{t-1}}{2^{t}-1}

(S49)

For large trees
\kappa_{t}\approx 1/2
. As we found
\kappa_{t}
, we can find the formula for
P_{t}^{*}
:

\displaystyle P_{t}^{*}=\tau+\frac{1}{2}(P_{t,1}^{*}+P_{t,2}^{*})

(S50)

As all the
P_{t,1}^{*}
and
P_{t,2}^{*}
are independent random variables, coming from separate sub-trees, the average
P_{t}^{*}
reads:

\overline{P_{t}^{*}}=\overline{\tau}+\overline{P_{t-1}^{*}}=t\overline{\tau}

(S51)

III.2
Exact numerical solution of the flow for the Cayley tree

The Darcy flow on the Cayley tree can be exactly numerically solved for moderate
t

Sketch of the algorithm used for the exact solution of the flow in the Cayley tree.

The solution consists in finding the pressures
P_{0},P_{1},P_{2},\dots
at which each new channel opens. The algorithm we use to produce the Fig. 3 middle and the Fig. 3 right of the main text is a simplified version of the general algorithm valid for any directed network and discussed in the supplementary material of
Liu et al. 2019
. In practice, the critical pressure
P_{0}
is the ground state of the associated directed polymer model. The other pressures are found following an iterative procedure. At each step, we denote by
\mathcal{C}_{s}
the subtree made of open channels exclusively for
P\in[P_{s},P_{s+1}]
(where
P_{s+1}
is not yet determined). Then,

1.

Using Kirchhoff’s equations, we determine the pressure at each node of
\mathcal{C}_{s}
as a function of the applied pressure
P
(these functions are linear in
P
).

2.

We find all the subtrees connected to a node in
\mathcal{C}_{s}
which are still closed. In figure
S4
they are highlighted by ovals.

3.

For each subtree, we find its ground state, denoted by
\mathcal{E}_{s}^{(1)},\mathcal{E}_{s}^{(2)},\dots
. They are indicated with dashed lines in figure
S4
.

4.

We find the minimal applied pressure
\tilde{P}_{s}^{(1)},\tilde{P}_{s}^{(2)},\dots
at which the ground state of each subtree opens. This is done by setting the node pressure at the root of each subtree (determined in step (1) using Kirchhoff’s equation) equal to
\mathcal{E}_{s}^{(1)},\mathcal{E}_{s}^{(2)},\dots
.

5.

We pick as a new channel the one with minimal opening pressure, namely
P_{s}=\min_{\alpha}\tilde{P}_{s}^{(\alpha)}
.

In numerical simulations, we stop at
t=23
due to the exponential growth in
t
of the number of configurations on the Cayley tree.
