Introduction

Let
G<\GL_{d}
be an algebraic group defined over
\mathbb{Q}
whose real points
G(\mathbb{R})
is a semisimple Lie group of noncompact type. It is a basic problem to understand the (co)homology of the arithmetic group
G(\mathbb{Z})
and its principal congruence subgroups

\Gamma(s):=\ker\big[G(\mathbb{Z})\rightarrow G(\mathbb{Z}/s\mathbb{Z})\big].

Problem 1.1
.

For a fixed prime
p
and
k\geq 1
, determine the rate of growth of the homology groups
H_{k}(\Gamma(p^{\ell});\mathbb{Q})
as
\ell
increases.

Let
Y(p^{\ell})=\Gamma(p^{\ell})\backslash G(\mathbb{R})/K
denote the locally symmetric space for
\Gamma(p^{\ell})
. Then

H_{*}(\Gamma(p^{\ell});\mathbb{Q})\cong H_{*}(Y(p^{\ell});\mathbb{Q}),

and by the theory of
L^{2}
-Betti numbers, if
k\neq\frac{1}{2}\dim Y(p^{\ell})
, then
H_{k}(Y(p^{\ell});\mathbb{Q})
grows sublinearly in terms of the volume
\vol(Y(p^{\ell}))
, i.e.

\lim_{\ell\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\dim H_{k}(Y(p^{\ell});\mathbb{Q})}{\vol(Y(p^{\ell}))}=0.

(1)

See e.g.
[
L0̈2
, §0.6,§5.2]
and
[
Gab02
]
. When
k=\frac{1}{2}\dim Y(p^{\ell})
then
H_{k}(Y(p^{\ell});\mathbb{Q})
grows linearly in the volume for certain
G

[
L0̈2
, op. cit.]
.

No other general results are known about the growth of
H_{k}(Y(p^{\ell});\mathbb{Q})
outside of Borel’s stable range. Some sporadic results are known for
G(\mathbb{R})=\SO(n,1)

[
Xue92
]
and
G(\mathbb{R})=U(n,m)

[
Mar14
,
MS19
,
GG20
,
Sto21
]
.

Our main results provide lower bounds on the rate of growth of
H_{n}(Y(p^{\ell});\mathbb{Q})
for certain
G
, where
n
is the real rank of
G
. Precise statements follow.

Theorem A
.

Fix
n\geq 2
and let
G=\SL_{n+1}
. Denoting
\Gamma(s)<\SL_{n+1}(\mathbb{Z})
and
Y(s)=\Gamma(s)\backslash\SL_{n+1}(\mathbb{R})/\SO(n)
as above,
there are infinitely many primes
p
with the following property. There exist constants
\mathcal{C},\mathcal{L}>0
such that

\dim H_{n}(Y(p^{\ell});\mathbb{Q})\geq\mathcal{C}\cdot\vol(Y(p^{\ell}))^{\frac{n+1}{n^{2}+2n}}

for each
\ell\geq\mathcal{L}
.

Theorem B
.

For
n=2m\geq 2
, let
Q_{n}
be the
unimodular, even integral symmetric bilinear form with signature
(n,n)
. Let
G=\SO(Q_{n})
be its isometry group, and define
\Gamma(s)<\SO(Q_{n};\mathbb{Z})
and
Y(s)=\Gamma(s)\backslash G(\mathbb{R})/K
as above.
For any
\epsilon>0
, there are infinitely many primes
p
with the following property. There exist constants
\mathcal{C},\mathcal{L}>0
such that

\dim H_{n}(Y(p^{\ell});\mathbb{Q})\geq\mathcal{C}\cdot\vol(Y(p^{\ell}))^{\frac{n-1-\epsilon}{n^{2}}}

for each
\ell\geq\mathcal{L}
.

Theorems
A
and
B
are proved by giving a lower bound on the dimension of the subspace of
H_{n}(Y(p^{\ell});\mathbb{Q})
spanned by
flat cycles
, i.e. compact, totally geodesic, flat submanifolds of
Y(p^{\ell})
of maximal dimension
n
. Using the same technique we can prove a similar result for Hilbert modular varieties.

Theorem C
.

Fix
n\geq 2
and fix a degree-
n
totally real number field
F/\mathbb{Q}
with ring of integers
\mathcal{O}_{F}
. For a nonzero
ideal
\mathfrak{s}\subset\mathcal{O}_{F}
, consider the congruence subgroup

\Gamma(\mathfrak{s})=\ker\big[\SL_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{F})\rightarrow\SL_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{F}/\mathfrak{s})\big]

and let
Y(\mathfrak{s})=\Gamma(\mathfrak{s})\backslash(\mathbb{H}^{2})^{\times n}
. There are infinitely many prime ideas
\mathfrak{p}\subset\mathcal{O}_{F}
with the following property. There exist constants
\mathcal{C},\mathcal{L}>0
such that if
\ell\geq\mathcal{L}
, then the subspace of
H_{n}(Y(\mathfrak{p}^{\ell});\mathbb{Q})
spanned by flat cycles has dimension at least
\mathcal{C}\cdot\vol(Y(\mathfrak{p}^{\ell}))^{1/3}
.

In Theorem
C
, the real rank
n
is equal to half the dimension of
Y(\mathfrak{p}^{\ell})
, and it is known that
H_{n}(Y(\mathfrak{p}^{\ell});\mathbb{Q})
grows linearly in the volume, again by results in
L^{2}
-cohomology
[
L0̈2

[

Avramidi–Nguyen-Phan
[
ANP15
, §9]

Remark 1.2
.

Problem

is related to a conjecture of Sarnak–Xue. Recall that the cohomology of an arithmetic group can be described representation-theoretically by Matsushima’s formula, and computing

of certain representations

[
SX91
]
conjectured upper bounds on the growth of these multiplicities. Ultimately, this has consequences for the growth of Betti numbers, but this conjecture has been approached in only a small number of cases. See, for example, the work of Marshall, Marshall–Shin, and Gerbelli-Gauthier
[
Mar14
,
MS19
,
GG20
]
, where the case
G=U(n,m)
is studied using the theory of endoscopy.

Topological argument for homology growth in finite covers

The main goal of this section is to explain a simple topological argument of Xue
[
Xue92
, §3]
that we use to prove our main theorems. Xue explains the argument in the context of arithmetic groups and congruence covers, but the argument applies more generally. We explain this below. It could be of independent interest.

Fix an oriented manifold
Y
(possibly noncompact) with oriented, embedded submanifolds
Y_{1},Y_{2}\subset Y
of complementary dimension
n_{1}+n_{2}=\dim Y
. Assume that
Y_{1}
is compact and that
Y_{1},Y_{2}
intersect transversely with nonzero algebraic intersection number
Y_{1}\cdot Y_{2}\neq 0
.

The submanifold
Y_{1}\subset Y
defines a homology class
[Y_{1}]\in H_{n_{1}}(Y;\mathbb{Q})
. Since
Y_{2}
is not assumed to be compact, it only defines a homology class in Borel–Moore homology with closed supports
H_{n_{2}}^{BM}(Y;\mathbb{Q})
. By Poincaré duality, the intersection pairing between
H_{n_{1}}(Y;\mathbb{Q})
and
H_{n_{2}}^{BM}(Y;\mathbb{Q})
is a perfect pairing. Note in particular, that
[Y_{1}]\neq 0
in
H_{n_{1}}(Y;\mathbb{Q})
because
Y_{1}\cdot Y_{2}\neq 0
.

Fix a basepoint
y\in Y_{1}\cap Y_{2}
. Fix a finite quotient
\rho:\pi_{1}(Y)\twoheadrightarrow W
, and let
\pi:\widehat{Y}\rightarrow Y
be the corresponding covering space. Choose a component
\widehat{Y}_{i}
of
\pi^{-1}(Y_{i})
for
i=1,2
, and set
W_{i}=\rho(\pi_{1}(Y_{i}))
. In particular
\widehat{Y}_{i}\rightarrow Y_{i}
is a regular cover with deck group
W_{i}
.

The following result gives a lower bound on how many
W
-translates of
\widehat{Y}_{1}
are linearly independent in
H_{n_{1}}(\widehat{Y};\mathbb{Q})
.

Proposition 2.1

(Xue)
.

With the preceding setup, the
W
-translates of
[\widehat{Y}_{1}]\in H_{n_{1}}(\widehat{Y};\mathbb{Q})
span a subspace of dimension at least
\frac{1}{|Y_{1}\cap Y_{2}|}\cdot\frac{|W|}{|W_{1}|\cdot|W_{2}|}
.

The proof is an easy consequence of the following linear algebra fact, proved in
[
Xue92
, Lem. 6]
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
V
be a vector space with dual
V^{*}
, and let
\phi\cdot u
denote the pairing between
\phi\in V^{*}
and
u\in V
. Let
S\subset V
and
S^{*}\subset V^{*}
be finite sets and assume that
(1)
for each
\phi\in S^{*}
there exists
u\in S
such that
\phi\cdot u\neq 0
and
(2)
there exists
t\geq 1
such that for each
u\in S
, there are at most
t
elements in
S^{*}
such that
\phi\cdot u\neq 0
. Then

\dim_{\mathbb{Q}}\text{\emph{span}}(S)\geq\frac{|S^{*}|}{t}

Proof of Proposition
2.1
.

First we bound the number of translates of
\widehat{Y}_{2}
that have nontrivial algebraic intersection with
\widehat{Y}_{1}
.

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\#\{w(\widehat{Y}_{2}):\widehat{Y}_{1}\cdot w(\widehat{Y}_{2})\neq 0\}&\leq&\#\{w(\widehat{Y}_{2}):\widehat{Y}_{1}\cap w(\widehat{Y}_{2})\neq\vn\}\\[5.69054pt]
&\leq&|\widehat{Y}_{1}\cap\pi^{-1}(Y_{2})|\\[5.69054pt]
&=&|Y_{1}\cap Y_{2}|\cdot|W_{1}|.\end{array}

The first inequality holds because disjoint submanifolds have zero algebraic intersection number. The second inequality says that the number of components of
\pi^{-1}(Y_{2})
that intersect
\widehat{Y}_{1}
is bounded by the total number of intersections (so there is equality precisely when each component of
\pi^{-1}(Y_{2})
intersects
\widehat{Y}_{1}
at most once). For the last equality, observe that
\widehat{Y}_{1}\cap\pi^{-1}(Y_{2})
is the preimage of
Y_{1}\cap Y_{2}
under the covering map
\widehat{Y}_{1}\rightarrow Y_{1}
.

An identical bound holds for
\#\{w(\widehat{Y}_{2}):u(\widehat{Y}_{1})\cdot w(\widehat{Y}_{2})\neq 0\}
for any
u\in W
.

The proposition now follows immediately by applying Lemma
2.2
to
V=H_{n_{1}}(\widehat{Y};\mathbb{Q})
,
V^{*}=H_{n_{2}}^{BM}(\widehat{Y};\mathbb{Q})
, and
S
(resp.
S^{*}
) equal to the
W
-translates of
[\widehat{Y}_{1}]
(resp.
[\widehat{Y}_{2}]
).
∎

Algebraic groups and geometric cycles

Here we explain the general setup for studying geometric cycles in the homology of arithmetic locally symmetric manifolds, following methods of Millson
[
Mil76
]
and Millson–Raghunathan
[
MR80
]
. A good reference is
[
Sch10
]
.

Let
G<\GL_{d}
be an algebraic subgroup defined over
\mathbb{Q}
such that
G(\mathbb{R})
is a semisimple Lie group.
Fix reductive
\mathbb{Q}
-subgroups
G_{1},G_{2}<G
.

For any subring
R\subset\mathbb{C}
, we define
G(R):=G\cap\GL_{d}(R)
, and similarly for
G_{1},G_{2}
. If
G
is actually defined over
\mathbb{Z}
(as will hold for some of our examples), then for any ring
R
(e.g.
R=\mathbb{Z}/t\mathbb{Z}
), we can define
G(R)=G\cap\GL_{d}(R)
. More generally, since
G
is defined over
\mathbb{Q}
, the group
G(\mathbb{Z}/t\mathbb{Z})
is defined as long as
t
is relatively prime to the (finitely many) denominators appearing in the defining equations of
G
.

For
r\geq 1
, let
\Gamma(r)<G(\mathbb{Z})
denote the congruence subgroup

\Gamma(r)=\ker\big[G(\mathbb{Z})\hookrightarrow\GL_{d}(\mathbb{Z})\rightarrow\GL_{d}(\mathbb{Z}/r\mathbb{Z})\big],

and set
\Gamma_{i}(r)=G_{i}(\mathbb{Z})\cap\Gamma(r)
. When
G
is defined over
\mathbb{Z}
, we can equivalently define
\Gamma(r)
as the kernel of the reduction map
G(\mathbb{Z})\rightarrow G(\mathbb{Z}/r\mathbb{Z})
.

Suppose
K\subset G(\mathbb{R})
is a maximal compact subgroup such that
K_{i}:=G_{i}(\mathbb{R})\cap K
is a maximal compact subgroup of
G_{i}(\mathbb{R})
for
i=1,2
. Then the orbit map
G_{i}(\mathbb{R})\rightarrow G(\mathbb{R})/K
defined by
g\mapsto gK
descends to a totally geodesic embedding of
X_{i}:=G_{i}(\mathbb{R})/K_{i}
into
X:=G(\mathbb{R})/K
. The embedding
X_{i}\hookrightarrow X
descends to an immersion of
Y_{i}(r):=\Gamma_{i}(r)\backslash G_{i}(\mathbb{R})/K_{i}
into
Y(r):=\Gamma(r)\backslash G(\mathbb{R})/K
for
i=1,2
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
G\leq\GL_{n}
and
G_{1},G_{2}\leq G
be as above. For a maximal compact subgroup
K\subset G(\mathbb{R})
and integer
r\geq 1
, say that
(G,G_{1},G_{2},K,r)
is a
Millson–Raghunathan tuple
if, using the above notation, the following conditions hold:

(A1)

G_{1}(\mathbb{Z})
is cocompact in
G_{1}(\mathbb{R})
.

(A2)

K_{i}\leq G_{i}(\mathbb{R})
is a maximal compact subgroup for
i=1,2
.

(A3)

\dim X_{1}+\dim X_{2}=\dim X
and
X_{1}\cap X_{2}=\{eK\}
.

(A4)

Y_{i}(r)
is orientable, and
Y_{i}(r)\rightarrow Y(r)
is an embedding.

(A5)

Y_{1}(r),Y_{2}(r)
intersect transversely and each intersection has the same sign.

Given a Millson–Raghunathan tuple
(G,G_{1},G_{2},K,r)
, for any natural number
s
that is a multiple of
r
, each component of the lift of
Y_{1}(r)
over the covering map
Y(s)\to Y(r)
represents a nonzero element of homology. In particular, following the results of §
2
we have the following bound on the dimension of homology groups of covers of locally symmetric spaces arising from Millson–Raghunathan tuples.

Lemma 3.2
.

Suppose
(G,G_{1},G_{2},K,r)
is a Millson–Raghunathan tuple and let
d=\dim(X_{1})
. Given
s
any multiple of
r
, let
W(r,s):=\Gamma(r)/\Gamma(s)
and
W_{i}(r,s)=\Gamma_{i}(r)/\Gamma_{i}(s)
for
i=1,2
. Then there is a constant
\mathcal{C}>0
such that

\dim(H_{d}(Y(s);\mathbb{Q}))\geq\mathcal{C}\cdot\frac{|W(r,s)|}{|W_{1}(r,s)|\cdot|W_{2}(r,s)|}

Proof.

As discussed in §
2
, condition (
A5
) guarantees that the homology class
[Y_{1}(r)]
in
H_{d}(Y(r);\mathbb{Q})
is nonzero, because it pairs nontrivially with the class
[Y_{2}(r)]
in the Borel–Moore homology of
Y(r)
with closed supports.

The group
W(r,s)
is the deck group of the cover
Y(s)\rightarrow Y(r)
. By Proposition
2.1
, the number of linearly independent translates of
[Y_{1}(s)]\in H_{d}(Y(s);\mathbb{Q})
under
W(r,s)
is at least

\frac{1}{|Y_{1}(r)\cap Y_{2}(r)|}\cdot\frac{|W(r,s)|}{|W_{1}(r,s)|\cdot|W_{2}(r,s)|}

∎

(2)

and the group

4
Explicit construction of periodic maximal flats

Let
X=G(\mathbb{R})/K
be a symmetric space of noncompact type, and let
\Gamma<G(\mathbb{R})
be a lattice. A
flat
is a geodesically embedded Euclidean space
\mathbb{E}^{i}\hookrightarrow X
. The maximal dimension of a flat is the real rank of
G(\mathbb{R})
, which we denote by
n
. A maximal flat is preserved by a Cartan subgroup
H
of
G(\mathbb{R})
, and we call the flat
periodic
if
\Gamma\cap H
is a lattice in
H
. In this case,
(\Gamma\cap H)\backslash H
is a compact flat manifold and there is an immersion
(\Gamma\cap H)\backslash H\looparrowright\Gamma\backslash X
.

There are general results of Prasad–Raghunathan
[
PR72
]
that guarantee the existence of periodic maximal flats. These results, however, are not constructive and are consequently difficult to apply in our setting. The explicit construction we give below will facilitate the computation in §
6
for the proofs of Theorems
A
–
C
.

To construct periodic maximal flats in our cases, it suffices to find a
\mathbb{Q}
-subgroup
G_{1}<G
such that
G_{1}(\mathbb{R})\cong(\mathbb{R}^{\times})^{n}
and
G_{1}(\mathbb{Z})<G_{1}(\mathbb{R})
is cocompact (equivalently,
G_{1}(\mathbb{Z})
contains a free abelian group of rank
n
). For then
G_{1}(\mathbb{R})
has maximal compact subgroup
K_{1}\cong\{\pm 1\}^{n}
and
G_{1}(\mathbb{R})/K_{1}\cong\mathbb{E}^{n}
embeds in
G(\mathbb{R})/K
as a maximal flat for any maximal compact subgroup
K\subset G_{(}\mathbb{R})
, which is periodic because
G_{1}(\mathbb{Z})<G_{1}(\mathbb{R})
is cocompact.

Remark 4.1
.

For
G=\SL_{n+1}
, it is easy to find
G_{1}
. Fix a totally real number field
E/\mathbb{Q}
of degree
n+1
, and let
G_{1}=R_{E/\mathbb{Q}}^{1}(G_{m})
be the norm torus in the restriction of scalars group
R_{E/\mathbb{Q}}(G_{m})
. The group
G_{1}(\mathbb{Z})\doteq\mathcal{O}_{E}
is cocompact in
G_{1}(\mathbb{R})\cong(\mathbb{R}^{\times})^{n}
by Dirichlet’s unit theorem. For the computation in §
6
, it is helpful to assume further that the ring of integers
\mathcal{O}_{E}
contains a unit
\lambda\in\mathcal{O}_{E}^{\times}
that is also a primitive element, i.e.
E=\mathbb{Q}(\lambda)
. It may be well known that such fields exist for each
n
, but we did not find a reference, so we give a construction below. We also note that this simple construction for
G=\SL_{n+1}
does not seem to work as well for
G=\SO(Q_{n})
.

In each case, we define
G_{1}
as the centralizer of a certain matrix
A
. There are two basic constructions that will be useful for showing that
G_{1}(\mathbb{Z})<G_{1}(\mathbb{R})
is cocompact. Let
F/\mathbb{Q}
be a number field with ring of integers
\mathcal{O}_{F}
.

•

Fix a polynomial
\xi\in\mathcal{O}_{F}[x]
that is irreducible in
F[x]
, and let
\lambda
be a root of
\xi
. View the number field
E=F[x]/(\xi)\cong F(\lambda)
as an
F
vector space with basis
1,\lambda,\ldots,\lambda^{d-1}
, where
d=\deg(\xi)
. Multiplication by
E
on itself defines a ring homomorphism

\rho:E\rightarrow M_{d}(F).

The matrix
\rho(\lambda)
belongs to
M_{d}(\mathcal{O}_{F})
and has characteristic polynomial
\xi
(this is the so-called
companion matrix
of
\xi
). If
\xi(0)=1
, then
\rho(\lambda)\in\SL_{d}(\mathcal{O}_{F})
. In this case
\mathcal{O}_{F}[\lambda]^{\times}
maps into the centralizer of
\rho(\lambda)
in
\GL_{d}(\mathcal{O}_{F})
.

•

Let
A\in\SL_{d}(\mathcal{O}_{F})
be a matrix whose characteristic polynomial
\chi
is irreducible in
F[x]
, and let
\lambda
be a root of
\chi
(i.e. an eigenvalue of
A
). The field
F[x]/(\chi)
can be identified with

(1)

the subalgebra of
M_{d}(F)
generated by
A
(the map
F[x]\rightarrow M_{d}(F)
defined by
x\mapsto A
factors through
(\chi)
by the Cayley–Hamilton theorem, and this ideal is the kernel because
\chi
is the minimal polynomial of
A
), and

(2)

the vector space
V=F^{d}
viewed as a
F[x]
-module with
x
acting by
A
(for any nonzero
v\in V
, the vectors
v,Av,\ldots,A^{d-1}v
are linearly independent –
A
acts irreducibly on
V
because
\chi
is irreducible).

4.1
Periodic maximal flats for
\SL_{n+1}

Fix a polynomial

\xi=x^{n+1}+a_{n}x^{n}+\cdots+a_{1}x+1

with integer coefficients
a_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
, and assume that
\xi
is irreducible in
\mathbb{Q}[x]
and has only real roots. Consider the companion matrix

A=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccc}0&0&\cdots&0&-1\\
1&0&\cdots&0&-a_{1}\\
0&1&\cdots&0&-a_{2}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&1&-a_{n}\end{array}\right)\in\SL_{n+1}(\mathbb{Z}).

Lemma 4.2
.

For
\xi
and
A
as above, define
G_{1}<\SL_{n+1}
be the centralizer of
A
. Then
G_{1}(\mathbb{R})\cong(\mathbb{R}^{\times})^{n}
, and
G_{1}(\mathbb{Z})
is cocompact in
G_{1}(\mathbb{R})
.

Proof.

Since
A
has irreducible characteristic polynomial and real roots,
A
is diagonalizable over
\mathbb{R}
. This implies that
G_{1}(\mathbb{R})\cong(\mathbb{R}^{\times})^{n}
.

To show
G_{1}(\mathbb{Z})
is cocompact in
G_{1}(\mathbb{R})
, we show that
G_{1}(\mathbb{Z})
contains an abelian subgroup of rank
n
. Consider the subalgebra
\mathbb{Q}[A]\subset M_{n+1}(\mathbb{Q})
generated by
A
. As mentioned above,
\mathbb{Q}[A]
is isomorphic to the field
E=\mathbb{Q}[x]/(\xi)
. The group
\mathbb{Z}[A]^{\times}
is contained in
\GL_{n+1}(\mathbb{Z})
and centralizes
A
. The group
\mathbb{Z}[A]^{\times}\cap\SL_{n+1}(\mathbb{Z})
has finite index in
\mathbb{Z}[A]^{\times}
and is contained in
G_{1}(\mathbb{Z})
, so it suffices to show
\mathbb{Z}[A]^{\times}
has rank
n
. For this, note that
\mathbb{Z}[A]^{\times}\cong\mathbb{Z}[x]/(\xi)
has the same rank as
\mathcal{O}_{E}^{\times}
, which is
n
by Dirichlet’s unit theorem; cf.
[
Neu99
, Ch. I, Thms. 7.4 and 12.12]
.
∎

Remark 4.3

(Irreducible is necessary)
.

If
\xi
is reducible and square-free with real roots, then again
G_{1}(\mathbb{R})=(\mathbb{R}^{\times})^{n}
, but now
G_{1}(\mathbb{Z})
has rank
<n
and hence is not cocompact. For example, suppose
A
is a block matrix
B\oplus C
, where
B,C
have irreducible characteristic polynomials which are relatively prime. Then
A
can be diagonalized over
\mathbb{R}
using a block diagonal matrix, and this implies that its centralizer is also block diagonal. Now one computes
\rank G_{1}(\mathbb{Z})<n
using Dirichlet’s unit theorem similar to the proof of Lemma
4.2
.

Next we show that for each
n\geq 0
, there is a degree-
(n+1)
monic polynomial
\xi\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
that is irreducible in
\mathbb{Q}[x]
, has only real roots, and satisfies
\xi(0)=1
.
There are likely several arguments for this, but we were not able to find a reference. Below we make a few remarks, and then give explicit examples in Lemma
4.7
.

Remark 4.4
.

The characteristic polynomial of a “random” element in
\SL_{n+1}(\mathbb{Z})
is irreducible by Rivin
[
Riv08
]
, but typically one expects the characteristic polynomial will have non-real roots. It would be nice to show that a random symmetric matrix in
\SL_{n+1}(\mathbb{Z})
has irreducible characteristic polynomial (since then the roots are necessarily real by the spectral theorem). There seem to be difficulties to adapting Rivin’s argument
[
Riv08
, Thm. 5.2]
to show this.

Remark 4.5
.

If
E/\mathbb{Q}
is a number field and
\alpha\in\mathcal{O}_{E}^{\times}
is a unit and also
\alpha
is a primitive element (
E=\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)
), then the minimal polynomial of
\alpha
has the desired properties. However, not every number field has a primitive element that’s also a unit
[
ABZ16
]
.

Remark 4.6
.

Kedlaya
[
Ked12
]
constructs for each
n=r+2s
, monic, irreducible polynomials
\xi\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
so that the number field
F=\mathbb{Q}[x]/(\xi)
has
r
real embeddings and
s
complex embeddings, and such that
\mathcal{O}_{F}=\mathbb{Z}[x]/(\xi)
. The polynomials
\xi
are somewhat complicated, and we did not check if Kedlaya’s construction includes examples with
\xi(0)=1
. In any case, the examples we give below are quite simple.

Lemma 4.7
.

Fix
n\geq 6
, and let
a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
be integers such that

0=a_{0}<a_{1}<a_{2}<\cdots<a_{n}\>\>\>\text{ and }\>\>\>a_{i+1}-a_{i}>2.

Then the degree-
(n+1)
polynomial

\xi=1+x(x-a_{1})\cdots(x-a_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}[x]

(3)

has only real roots and is irreducible in
\mathbb{Q}[x]
.

Lemma
4.7
only gives examples for
n\geq 6
, but it is easy to construct ad hoc examples for
n\leq 5
.
The proof of Lemma
4.7
uses the following irreducibility criterion, which can be found in
[
Pra04
, Thm. 2.2.8]
.

Theorem 4.8

(Polya irreducibility criterion)
.

Let
\xi
be a degree-
d
polynomial and let
k=\lfloor\frac{d+1}{2}\rfloor
. If there are distinct integers
b_{0},\ldots,b_{d-1}
such that

0<|\xi(b_{i})|<\frac{k!}{2^{k}}\>\>\>\text{ for }\>\>\>0\leq i\leq d-1,

then
\xi
is irreducible in
\mathbb{Q}[x]
.

Proof of Lemma
4.7
.

We sketch the argument. To see that
\xi
has
n+1
real roots, let
m_{0}=-2
,
m_{i}=\frac{a_{i-1}+a_{i}}{2}
for
i=1,\dotsc,n
, and
m_{n+1}=a_{n}+2
. Then the assumption that
a_{i+1}-a_{i}>2
implies that the sign of
\xi(m_{i})
is
(-1)^{n-i}
, and so
\xi
has
n+1
real roots by the intermediate value theorem.

Next we explain why
\xi
is irreducible. Observe that
\xi(0)=\xi(a_{1})=\cdots=\xi(a_{n})=1
. Since
1<\frac{k!}{2^{k}}
for
k\geq 4
, we can apply Polya’s Theorem
4.8
as long as
\lfloor\frac{n+2}{2}\rfloor\geq 4
, i.e.
n\geq 6
, to conclude that
\xi
is irreducible.
∎

4.2
Periodic maximal flats for
R_{F/\mathbb{Q}}(\SL_{2})

This case can be treated similar to the
\SL_{n+1}
case. Let
F/\mathbb{Q}
be a totally real number field of degree
n\geq 2
.

Let
\xi=x^{2}+ax+1
be a polynomial in
\mathcal{O}_{F}[x]
that is irreducible in
F[x]
and has real roots (equivalently,
a^{2}-4
is positive and is not a square in
F
). Define

A=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&-1\\
1&-a\end{array}\right)\in\SL_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{F}),

which has characteristic polynomial
\xi
.

Consider
\mathcal{H}=\SL_{2}
viewed as an algebraic group over
F
, and define
\mathcal{H}_{1}<\mathcal{H}
to be the centralizer of
A
. Set
G=R_{F/\mathbb{Q}}(\mathcal{H})
and
G_{1}=R_{F/\mathbb{Q}}(\mathcal{H}_{1})
.

Lemma 4.9
.

The group
G_{1}(\mathbb{R})
is isomorphic to
(\mathbb{R}^{\times})^{n}
and is embedded diagonally in
G(\mathbb{R})=\big(\SL_{2}(\mathbb{R})\big)^{n}
. Also,
G_{1}(\mathbb{Z})<G_{1}(\mathbb{R})
is cocompact.

Proof.

The proof is similar to the proof of Lemma
4.2
. Since
A
is diagonalizable in
\mathcal{H}_{1}(\mathbb{R})
, its centralizer is isomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{\times}
. Applying restriction of scalars gives the first statement.

For the second statement, we prove that
G_{1}(\mathbb{Z})
contains a rank-
n
abelian group. The groups
G_{1}(\mathbb{Z})
and
\mathcal{H}_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{F})
are commensurable, so it suffices to work with
\mathcal{H}_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{F})
.

Similar to the proof of Lemma
4.2
, consider the subalgebra
F[A]\subset M_{2}(F)
generated by
A
, which is isomorphic to the field
E=F[x]/(\xi)
. The group
(\mathcal{O}_{F}[A])^{\times}
is contained in
\GL_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{F})
and centralizes
A
. Consequently,
\mathcal{H}_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{F})
contains the kernel of the composition

\big(\mathcal{O}_{F}[A])^{\times}\rightarrow\GL_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{F})\xrightarrow{\det}\mathcal{O}_{F}^{\times}.

This kernel has the same rank as the kernel of the norm map

\mathcal{O}_{E}^{\times}\rightarrow\GL_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{F})\xrightarrow{\det}\mathcal{O}_{F}^{\times},

which is
n=(2n-1)-(n-1)
by Dirichlet’s unit theorem. (Note that the norm map restricted to
\mathcal{O}_{F}^{\times}
is the map
\lambda\mapsto\lambda^{2}
, so the map
\mathcal{O}_{E}^{\times}\rightarrow\mathcal{O}_{F}^{\times}
is virtually surjective.)
∎

4.3
Periodic maximal flats in
\SO(Q_{n})

Define

Q_{n}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&I_{n}\\
I_{n}&0\end{array}\right)

(4)

where
I_{n}
is the
n\times n
identity matrix. The bilinear form defined by
Q_{n}
is the unique unimodular, even integral
symmetric bilinear form with signature
(n,n)
. For a ring
R
, we define

\SO(Q_{n};R)=\{g\in\SL_{2n}(R):g^{t}Q_{n}g=Q_{n}\}.

Explicitly constructing periodic maximal flats in this case is more subtle than the preceding cases because there is no known simple characterization of the characteristic polynomials of elements in
\SO(Q_{n})
. See e.g.
[
GM02
]
and
[
BF15
]
. The main result we use follows.

Lemma 4.11
.

Fix
n\geq 2
, and fix a rational
symmetric bilinear form
Q
of signature
(n,n)
. Assume that
A\in\SO(Q;\mathbb{Z})
has characteristic polynomial
\chi
that’s irreducible in
\mathbb{Q}[x]
and has only real roots. Let
G_{1}<\SO(Q)
be the centralizer of
A
. Then
G_{1}(\mathbb{R})\cong(\mathbb{R}^{\times})^{n}
and
G_{1}(\mathbb{Z})
is cocompact in
G_{1}(\mathbb{R})
.

Remark 4.12
.

We do not actually know if there always exists
A\in\SO(Q;\mathbb{Z})
with irreducible characteristic polynomial and only real roots. Generically one expects that the characteristic polynomial is irreducible by an argument similar to
[
Riv08
, Thm. 5.2]
. (For
Q=Q_{n}
, one can use
[
GM02
]
to prove that the map from (orthogonal) matrices to characteristic polynomials is dominant as a map of varieties.) But similar to Remark
4.4
, we are unaware of an argument that ensures that there are examples with only real roots.

Remark 4.13
.

From the proof we will see that we do not need to assume that
\chi
is irreducible. For example, we have the same conclusion if
\chi=\chi_{1}\cdots\chi_{j}
is a product of irreducible polynomials with real roots. We will use this observation to construct explicit examples in each
\SO(Q_{n})
after the proof of Lemma
4.11
.

Remark 4.14
.

The statement of Lemma
4.11
can be generalized to
Q
of signature
(n,m)
with minor changes. For example, if
Q^{\prime}=Q\oplus P
where
Q
has signature
(n,n)
and
P
is positive (or negative) definite, then a conclusion similar to that of Lemma
4.11
holds for
A\in\SO(Q^{\prime})
of the form
A=A^{\prime}\oplus\id
, where
A^{\prime}\in\SO(Q)
has irreducible characteristic polynomial with real roots. In this case the characteristic polynomial is not irreducible and the centralizer of

is not a Cartan subgroup, rather
G_{1}(\mathbb{R})
is the product of

with the compact group

.

Proof of Lemma
4.11
.

By assumption, the action of
A
on
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
has
2n
distinct real eigenvalues, which occur in
(\lambda,1/\lambda)
pairs with
\lambda\neq\pm 1
. From this one deduces that there is an eigenbasis
u_{1},\ldots,u_{n},v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
consisting of isotropic vectors whose intersection matrix is
Q_{n}
. Then it is not hard to see that
G_{1}(\mathbb{R})
is isomorphic to
\SO(1,1)^{n}\cong(\mathbb{R}^{\times})^{n}
.

Now we show that
G_{1}(\mathbb{Z})
is cocompact in
G_{1}(\mathbb{R})
by showing that
G_{1}(\mathbb{Z})
contains a rank-
n
free abelian group. For this, we use some structural results of Milnor
[
Mil69
]
. Let
V\cong\mathbb{Q}^{2n}
denote the standard representation of
\SO(Q;\mathbb{Q})
. For any nonzero vector
v\in V
, the vectors
v,Av,\ldots,A^{2n-1}v
are linearly independent (otherwise the action of
A
on
V
would be reducible, contradicting the fact that
\chi
is irreducible). This allows us to identify
E:=\mathbb{Q}[x]/(\chi)
with
V
as
\mathbb{Q}[x]
-modules, with multiplication by
x
on
\mathbb{Q}[x]/(\chi)
corresponding to the action of
A
on
V
. According to
[
Mil69
, Lem. 1.1]
, there exists a unique
bilinear form
h:E\times E\rightarrow E
that is Hermitian with respect to the involution
e\mapsto\bar{e}
on
E
defined by
\bar{x}=x^{-1}
and such that

(u,v)\mapsto\Tr_{E/\mathbb{Q}}(h(u,v))

is equal to the given form
Q
on
V
(with respect to the identification of
E
and
V
). From this we conclude that the action of
e\in E
on
V
preserves the form
Q
if and only if
e\bar{e}=1
.

Let
\lambda
be a root of
\chi
. Under the map
E\cong\mathbb{Q}(\lambda)\rightarrow M_{2n}(\mathbb{Q})
sending
\lambda
to
A
, the subgroup of
\mathbb{Z}[\lambda]^{\times}
of elements satisfying
e\bar{e}=1
maps into
G_{1}(\mathbb{Z})
. We will show this subgroup has rank
n
. Since
\mathbb{Z}[\lambda]^{\times}
has finite index in
\mathcal{O}_{E}^{\times}
, it suffices to prove the same statement for
\mathcal{O}_{E}^{\times}
.

Define
\Omega^{\pm}=\{e\in\mathcal{O}_{E}^{\times}:\bar{e}=e^{\pm 1}\}
, each of which is a subgroup of
\mathcal{O}_{E}^{\times}
. We want to compute the rank of
\Omega^{-}
. The product
\Omega^{+}\times\Omega^{-}
is a finite-index subgroup of
\mathcal{O}_{E}^{\times}
(this is a general fact about integral representations of
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
). The group
\Omega^{+}
coincides with
\mathcal{O}_{F}
, where
F=\{e\in E:\bar{e}=e\}
is the fixed field of the involution. Now
F/\mathbb{Q}
has degree
n
because
E/F
has degree 2.
Then
\rank\Omega^{+}=\rank\mathcal{O}_{F}^{\times}=n-1
. Since
\rank\mathcal{O}_{E}^{\times}=2n-1
, this implies that
\rank\Omega^{-}=n
. This completes the proof.
∎

Proposition 4.15
.

For
\chi=1-18x+43x^{2}-18x^{3}+x^{4}
and
E=\mathbb{Q}[x]/(\chi)
, there are infinitely many primes
p\equiv 3\pmod{4}
that split completely in
E
.

Proof.

First we note that
E=\mathbb{Q}[x]/(\chi)
is a Galois extension of
\mathbb{Q}
and has Galois group
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
. We checked this using Sage.

By the Kronecker–Weber theorem
[
Neu99
, Ch. V, Thm. 1.10]
, every abelian extension of
\mathbb{Q}
is contained in a cyclotomic field
\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{m})
, where
\zeta_{m}
a primitive
m
-th root of unity. In fact,
E\subset\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{40})
. To see this, first compute that the roots of
\chi
are

\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{1}{2}\big(9-6\sqrt{2}-3\sqrt{5}+2\sqrt{10}\big)\\[5.69054pt]
\frac{1}{2}\big(9-6\sqrt{2}+3\sqrt{5}-2\sqrt{10}\big)\\[5.69054pt]
\frac{1}{2}\big(9+6\sqrt{2}-3\sqrt{5}-2\sqrt{10}\big)\\[5.69054pt]
\frac{1}{2}\big(9+6\sqrt{2}+3\sqrt{5}+2\sqrt{10}\big)\end{array}

This implies that
E\cong\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2},\sqrt{5})
, and since

\sqrt{2}=\zeta_{8}+\zeta_{8}^{-1}\>\>\>\text{ and }\>\>\>\sqrt{5}=(\zeta_{5}+\zeta_{5}^{-1})-(\zeta_{5}^{2}+\zeta_{5}^{-2})

we conclude that
E\subset\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{5},\zeta_{8})\subset\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{40})
.

The primes
p\nmid 40
that split completely in
E
are characterized as follows. The Galois group of
\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{40})/\mathbb{Q}
is
(\mathbb{Z}/40\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
and contains
\Gal(\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{40})/E)
as a subgroup. If
p\nmid 40
then
p
splits completely if and only if the image of
p
in
(\mathbb{Z}/40\mathbb{Z})^{\times}\cong\Gal(\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{40})/\mathbb{Q})
lies in
\Gal(\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{40})/E)
;
[
Neu99
, Ch. I, §10 and Ch. VII, Lem. 13.5]
. One computes

\Gal(\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{40})/E)=\{1,9,31,39\}\subset(\mathbb{Z}/40\mathbb{Z})^{\times}.

Therefore,
\chi
splits completely in
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
whenever
p
is a prime in one of the following arithmetic progressions.

40x+1,\>\>\>40x+9,\>\>\>40x+31,\>\>\>40x+39

By Dirichlet’s theorem, each of these arithmetic progressions contain infinitely many primes. In particular, since
40x+31\equiv 3\pmod{4}
, there are infinitely many primes congruent to
3\pmod{4}
so that
\chi

Proof.

First we recall a few facts which make the lemma easy.

Parameterizing the space of flats.
Any flat

Now we prove the lemma. Choose a flat

6
Size of Lie groups over finite rings

The Lie groups of finite type that are of interest in this paper fall into two groups: (1)
\SL_{d}(\mathcal{O}_{F}/\mathfrak{p}^{j})
, where
F
is a number field and
\mathfrak{p}\subset\mathcal{O}_{F}
is a prime ideal, and (2) the orthogonal groups
O(Q;\mathbb{Z}/p^{j}\mathbb{Z})
for a prime number
p\neq 2
. In this section we will provide estimates of the size of these groups as functions of
j
, considering all other variables fixed.

Given two functions
f,g
of
j
, we write
f\sim g
if there is a constant
C
(independent of
j
) such that
\frac{1}{C}\cdot g(j)\leq f(j)\leq C\cdot g(j)
for all
j\geq 1
.

6.1
Size of
\SL_{d}(\mathcal{O}_{F}/\mathfrak{p}^{j})

Fix a number field
F/\mathbb{Q}
. Throughout this section we consider an ideal
\mathfrak{t}\subset\mathcal{O}_{F}
of the form
\mathfrak{t}=\mathfrak{p}^{j}
for some prime ideal
\mathfrak{p}
. Set
\mathbb{F}=\mathcal{O}_{F}/\mathfrak{p}
.

Proposition 6.1
.

Fix
j\geq 1
, and let
\mathfrak{t}=\mathfrak{p}^{j}
, where
\mathfrak{p}\subset\mathcal{O}_{F}
is a prime ideal. For
d\geq 2
,

|\SL_{d}(\mathcal{O}_{F}/\mathfrak{t})|\sim N(\mathfrak{t})^{d^{2}-1},

where
N(\mathfrak{t})=|\mathcal{O}_{F}/\mathfrak{t}|
is the ideal norm.

Proposition
6.1
is surely well known. We include a proof for completeness and also because it is a good warm-up for the orthogonal case in the next section.

Proof of Proposition
6.1
.

Set
R=\mathcal{O}_{F}/\mathfrak{t}
. Given the short exact sequence

1\rightarrow\SL_{d}(R)\rightarrow\GL_{d}(R)\rightarrow R^{\times}\rightarrow 1,

we may break up our computation into computing
|\GL_{d}(R)|
and
|R^{\times}|
.

Computing
|R^{\times}|
.

Let
M\subset R
be the unique maximal ideal, which is the image of
\mathfrak{p}\subset\mathcal{O}_{F}
under
\mathcal{O}_{F}\rightarrow\mathcal{O}_{F}/\mathfrak{p}^{j}\equiv R
. Note that
R/M\cong\mathbb{F}
.

Claim
1
.
The natural map
R^{\times}\rightarrow(R/M)^{\times}=\mathbb{F}^{\times}
gives a short exact sequence

1\rightarrow(1+M)\rightarrow R^{\times}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}^{\times}\rightarrow 1.

(9)

Given the claim, we conclude that

|R^{\times}|=|M|\cdot|\mathbb{F}^{\times}|=N(\mathfrak{t})\>\frac{|\mathbb{F}^{\times}|}{|\mathbb{F}|}.

(10)

Here we use that
|M|=\frac{|R|}{|R/M|}
and
R/M=\mathbb{F}
.

Proof of Claim
1
.
We need to show that
R^{\times}\rightarrow(R/M)^{\times}
is surjective and has kernel
1+M
.

The kernel of
R^{\times}\rightarrow(R/M)^{\times}
is equal to
(1+M)\cap R^{\times}
. In fact,
1+M
is contained in
R^{\times}
: for each
m\in M
, the element
1+m
is invertible in
R
because the ideal
M\subset R
is nilpotent (
M^{j}=0
). Therefore,
1+M
is the kernel of
R^{\times}\rightarrow(R/M)^{\times}
.

To show that
R^{\times}\rightarrow(R/M)^{\times}
is surjective, fix
x\in R
whose image in
R/M
is invertible. To prove surjectivity, it suffices to show that
x
is invertible. By assumption there exists
y\in R
such that
xy=1+m
for some
m\in M
. Since
1+m
is invertible, this implies
xy
is invertible; hence
x
is also invertible, as desired.

Computing
|\GL_{d}(R)|
.

Similar to the preceding computation, we reduce to the following claim.

Claim
2
.
The natural map
\GL_{d}(R)\rightarrow\GL_{d}(R/M)=\GL_{d}(\mathbb{F})
gives a short exact sequence

1\rightarrow\big[I+M_{d}(M)\big]\rightarrow\GL_{d}(R)\rightarrow\GL_{d}(\mathbb{F})\rightarrow 1,

where
I
is the
2\times 2
identity matrix and
M_{d}(M)
is the group of
d\times d
matrices with entries in
M
.

The claim implies that

|\GL_{d}(R)|=|M_{d}(M)|\cdot|\GL_{d}(\mathbb{F})|=N(\mathfrak{t})^{d^{2}}\>\frac{|\GL_{d}(\mathbb{F})|}{|\mathbb{F}|^{d^{2}}}.

(11)

Combining Equations (
10
) and (
11
), we conclude that

|\SL_{d}(R)|=\frac{|\GL_{d}(R)|}{|R^{\times}|}=N(\mathfrak{t})^{d^{2}-1}\cdot\frac{|\GL_{d}(\mathbb{F})|}{|\mathbb{F}^{\times}|\cdot|\mathbb{F}|^{d^{2}-1}},

which proves the proposition. Therefore, it remains only to prove Claim 2.

Proof of Claim
2
.
The kernel of
\GL_{d}(R)\rightarrow\GL_{d}(R/M)
is the intersection of
I+M_{d}(M)
with
\GL_{d}(R)

is contained in

is nilpotent (

This finishes the proof of Claim 2 and the proof of the proposition.

6.2
Size of finite orthogonal groups

Fix a prime
p\neq 2
and
d\geq 2
. Let
Q\in M_{d}(\mathbb{Z})
be the matrix of a nondegenerate symmetric bilinear form
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle:\mathbb{Z}^{d}\times\mathbb{Z}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}
, and assume that

remains nondegenerate after reducing mod

, i.e.
\det(Q)\not\equiv 0\pmod{p}
. Define the orthogonal group

O(Q;\mathbb{Z}/p^{j}\mathbb{Z})=\{g\in\GL_{d}(\mathbb{Z}/p^{j}\mathbb{Z}):g^{t}Qg=Q\}.

The reduction map

\rho_{j}:\mathbb{Z}/p^{j}\mathbb{Z}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}/p^{j-1}\mathbb{Z}

induces a homomorphism

\pi_{j}:O(Q;\mathbb{Z}/p^{j}\mathbb{Z})\rightarrow O(Q;\mathbb{Z}/p^{j-1}\mathbb{Z}),

which we will use to inductively compute

. For each
i\geq 2
, set

, and define

S_{i}(Q)=\{\mu\in M_{d}(M_{i}):\mu^{t}Q+Q\mu=0\}.

Proposition 6.2
.

With the preceding setup,

|O(Q;\mathbb{Z}/p^{j}\mathbb{Z})|=|O(Q;\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})|\cdot|S_{2}(Q)|\cdots|S_{j}(Q)|.

Proof.

First we show that
\pi_{j}
is surjective for each
j\geq 2
. For this, we use that
O(Q;\mathbb{Z}/p^{j}\mathbb{Z})
is generated by reflections

r_{x}:v\mapsto v-2\frac{\langle v,x\rangle}{\langle x,x\rangle}\>x,

where

is such that
\langle x,x\rangle
is a unit in
\mathbb{Z}/p^{j}\mathbb{Z}
; see
[
MH73
, Ch. I, Cor. 4.3]
. Given this, it is straightforward to check that if

is a unit and

is a lift of

, then
\langle\hat{x},\hat{x}\rangle
is a unit in
\mathbb{Z}/p^{j}\mathbb{Z}
, and
\pi_{j}(r_{\hat{x}})=r_{x}
. Thus
\pi_{j}
is surjective.

It remains to understand

. Similar to the proof of Proposition
6.1
, there is a short exact sequence

1\rightarrow\big[I+M_{d}(M_{j})\big]\rightarrow\GL_{d}(\mathbb{Z}/p^{j}\mathbb{Z})\rightarrow\GL_{d}(\mathbb{Z}/p^{j-1}\mathbb{Z})\rightarrow 1,

so
\ker(\pi_{j})
is the intersection of

with
O(Q;\mathbb{Z}/p^{j}\mathbb{Z})
. If

belongs to

and
O(Q;\mathbb{Z}/p^{j}\mathbb{Z})
, then
(1+\mu)^{t}Q(1+\mu)=Q
, which implies that
\mu^{t}Q+Q\mu=0
(note that

is 0 because

in
\mathbb{Z}/p^{j}\mathbb{Z}
). Therefore
|\ker(\pi_{j})|=|S_{j}(Q)|
, and the Proposition follows by induction on

.
∎

6.3
Finite tori

For the proof of the main theorems, we will need to know the size of the centralizer of certain elements in certain finite groups of Lie type.

Lemma 6.5
.

Let

be a number field. Fix

and assume that its characteristic polynomial
\chi
is irreducible in

(i)

(ii)

For the last statement, compare with (

Remark 6.6
.

There is reason to be cautious when trying to diagonalize a matrix

when

is a commutative ring that is not a field because in this generality, (1) a given eigenspace

need not have a basis, (2) the sum of eigenspaces need not be a direct sum, and (3) the sum of eigenspaces need not span

. These phenomena are illustrated in
[

]
. These issues can be avoided if the characteristic polynomial of

is square-free and splits completely in

, as will be explained in the proof of Lemma
6.5
.

Proof of Lemma
6.5
.

We begin with (i). Fix a root
\lambda

, and let

.

[

Next we prove (ii). Fixing

is diagonalizable in

has a basis of eigenvectors. Let

, let
\mathfrak{r}=\mathfrak{p}^{k}
for some fixed
k\gg 0
large enough that
Corollary
5.6
applies. For any
\ell>k
, let
\mathfrak{s}=\mathfrak{p}^{\ell}
. Next we estimate the size of the groups
W(\mathfrak{r},\mathfrak{s})=\Gamma(\mathfrak{r})/\Gamma(\mathfrak{s})
and
W_{i}(\mathfrak{r},\mathfrak{s})=\Gamma_{i}(\mathfrak{r})/\Gamma_{i}(\mathfrak{s})
for
i=1,2
as functions of
\ell
for infinitely many primes
\mathfrak{p}
.

Size of
W(\mathfrak{r},\mathfrak{s})
.

First we show that
|W(\mathfrak{r},\mathfrak{s})|\sim N(\mathfrak{s})^{3}
,
where
N(\mathfrak{t})=|\mathcal{O}_{F}/\mathfrak{t}|
is the ideal norm. Similar to (
2
), we have a short exact sequence

1\rightarrow W(\mathfrak{r},\mathfrak{s})\rightarrow\SL_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{F})/\Gamma(\mathfrak{s})\rightarrow\SL_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{F})/\Gamma(\mathfrak{r})\rightarrow 1.

By
[
HO89
, Thm. 4.3.9]
, the reduction map
\SL_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{F})\rightarrow\SL_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{F}/\mathfrak{t})
is surjective, so there is a short exact sequence

1\rightarrow\Gamma(\mathfrak{t})\rightarrow\SL_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{F})\rightarrow\SL_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{F}/\mathfrak{t})\rightarrow 1.

(15)

Using (
15
) and Proposition
6.1
, we conclude that

W(\mathfrak{r},\mathfrak{s})=\frac{|\SL_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{F}/\mathfrak{s})|}{|\SL_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{F}/\mathfrak{r})|}\sim N(\mathfrak{s})^{3}.

Size of
W_{i}(\mathfrak{r},\mathfrak{s})
.

Recall that
\mathcal{H}_{i}
is the centralizer of
A_{i}\in\SL_{2}(F)
, and we have assumed that
A_{1},A_{2}
are conjugate in
\SL_{2}(F)
. As such, the computation of
|W_{i}(\mathfrak{r},\mathfrak{s})|
is essentially the same for
i=1,2
. To simplify exposition, we present the proof for
i=1
and show that
|W_{1}(\mathfrak{r},\mathfrak{s})|\sim N(\mathfrak{s})
.

We start by assuming
\mathfrak{p}
is not one of the finitely many factors of denominators of entries of
A_{1}
. This allows us to consider
A_{1}
as an element of
\SL_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{F}/\mathfrak{p}^{k})
for each
k\geq 1
.

By Lemma
6.5
, there are infinitely many prime ideals
\mathfrak{p}\subset\mathcal{O}_{F}
such that the characteristic polynomial of
A_{1}
splits completely in
(\mathcal{O}_{F}/\mathfrak{p}^{j})[x]
and
A_{1}
is diagonalizable in
\GL_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{F}/\mathfrak{p}^{j})
for each
j\geq 1
. Fixing such a prime, the centralizer of
A_{1}
in
\GL_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{F}/\mathfrak{p}^{j})
is conjugate to the group of diagonal matrices, so the centralizer of
A_{1}
in
\SL_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{F}/\mathfrak{p}^{j})
is isomorphic to
(\mathcal{O}_{F}/\mathfrak{p}^{j})^{\times}
. Denoting
\mathfrak{t}=\mathfrak{p}^{j}
, this group has order
N(\mathfrak{t})\cdot\frac{N(\mathfrak{p})-1}{N(\mathfrak{p})}
, cf. Equation (
10
).

Next we show that
\mathcal{H}_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{F})
surjects to the centralizer of
A_{1}
in
\SL_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{F}/\mathfrak{p}^{j})
. The centralizer of
A_{1}
in
M_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{F})
contains a subring isomorphic to
\mathcal{O}_{F}[x]/(\chi)
, where
\chi
is the characteristic polynomial. The same statement holds with
\mathcal{O}_{F}
replaced by
R:=\mathcal{O}_{F}/\mathfrak{p}^{j}
. Since
\chi
splits completely in
R
, there is a ring isomorphism
R[x]/(\chi)\cong R^{2}
. Up to conjugation the map
R[t]/(\chi)\rightarrow M_{2}(R)
has image equal to the diagonal matrices. From this we deduce that the invertible elements of
R[t]/(\chi)
surject onto the diagonal subgroup of
\GL_{2}(R)
and that
\mathcal{H}_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{F})
surjects onto the centralizer of
A_{1}
in
\SL_{2}(R)
.

From this we conclude that
|W_{1}(\mathfrak{r},\mathfrak{s})|\sim N(\mathfrak{s})
. By the same argument, we have
|W_{2}(\mathfrak{r},\mathfrak{s})|\sim N(\mathfrak{s})
.

An expression in terms of volume.

So far, we have shown that

\frac{|W(\mathfrak{r},\mathfrak{s})|}{|W_{1}(\mathfrak{r},\mathfrak{s})|\cdot|W_{2}(\mathfrak{r},\mathfrak{s})|}\sim\frac{N(\mathfrak{s})^{3}}{N(\mathfrak{s})\cdot N(\mathfrak{s})}=N(\mathfrak{s})

for infinitely many primes
p
.

Since the volume satisfies
\vol(Y(\mathfrak{s}))=|W(\mathfrak{r},\mathfrak{s})|\cdot\vol(Y(\mathfrak{r}))
, we can write
\vol(Y(s))\sim N(\mathfrak{s})^{3}
. Therefore,
\frac{|W(\mathfrak{r},\mathfrak{s})|}{|W_{1}(\mathfrak{r},\mathfrak{s})|\cdot|W_{2}(\mathfrak{r},\mathfrak{s})|}\sim\vol(Y(\mathfrak{s}))^{1/3}
.
Combined with Lemma
3.2
and Corollary
5.6
, this proves Theorem
C
. ∎

7.3
Orthogonal case (Theorem
B
)

In this section we prove Theorem
B
. Fix
n\geq 2
and let
G
denote the algebraic group

\SO(Q_{n})=\{g\in\SL_{2n}:g^{t}Q_{n}g=Q_{n}\},

where
Q_{n}
is the matrix defined in (
4
).

Take
G_{1},G_{2}<G
as in §
5.3
. For any prime
p\equiv 3\pmod{4}
, let
r=p^{k}
for some fixed
k\gg 0
large enough that
Corollary
5.8
applies. For any
\ell>k
, let
s=p^{\ell}
. Below we estimate the size of the groups
W(r,s)=\Gamma(r)/\Gamma(s)
and
W_{i}(r,s)=\Gamma_{i}(r)/\Gamma_{i}(s)
for
i=1,2
as functions of
\ell
for infinitely many primes
p
.

Size of
W(r,s)
.

First show that
|W(r,s)|\sim\left(p^{n^{2}}\right)^{\ell}
. Recall the following short exact sequence (
2
) from §
3
:

1\rightarrow W(r,s)\rightarrow G(\mathbb{Z})/\Gamma(s)\rightarrow G(\mathbb{Z})/\Gamma(r)\rightarrow 1.

For
t=p^{j}
, the group
G(\mathbb{Z})/\Gamma(t)
is isomorphic to the image of

\SO(Q_{n};\mathbb{Z})\rightarrow\SO(Q_{n};\mathbb{Z}/t\mathbb{Z}).

By strong approximation and Hensel’s lemma, the image of this map contains the group
\Omega(Q_{n};\mathbb{Z}/t\mathbb{Z})
defined in (
12
) for almost every prime
p
. This implies that
|W(r,s)|\sim\left(p^{n^{2}}+2p^{n(n-1)/2}\right)^{\ell}
by Corollary
6.3
.

Our application of these results is routine, so we only give a brief explanation. Similar to §
7.1
, strong approximation is used to conclude that the image of the inclusion
G(\mathbb{Z})\hookrightarrow G(\mathbb{Z}_{p})
is dense. One difference is that the algebraic group
\SO(Q_{n})
is not simply connected, so in order to apply strong approximation, one needs to first pass to the 2-fold spin cover; see
[
LS03
, Window 9, §1 and §4]
and
[
Pin00
]
. Hensel’s lemma in algebraic geometry implies that the reduction map
G(\mathbb{Z}_{p})\rightarrow G(\mathbb{Z}/p^{j}\mathbb{Z})
is surjective for each
j\geq 1
; see
[
Gro67
, §18.5]
and
[
Stu20
, Thm. 4.17]
. In order to apply Hensel, one needs
G
to be a smooth
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-scheme. The smoothness of
G=\SO(Q_{n})
as a group scheme is discussed in
[
Con14
, C.1.5]
.

Size of
W_{2}(r,s)
.

For all but finitely many primes
p
(depending on the choice of the negative vector), the group
G_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/t\mathbb{Z})
is isomorphic to
\SO(\widetilde{Q}_{n-1},\mathbb{Z}/t\mathbb{Z})
, where
\widetilde{Q}_{n}
is defined in (
13
). Again by strong approximation, the image of
G_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\rightarrow G_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/t\mathbb{Z})
is isomorphic to
\Omega(\widetilde{Q}_{n-1};\mathbb{Z}/t\mathbb{Z})
for almost every prime
p
. This implies that
|W_{2}(r,s)|\sim\left(p^{(n-1)^{2}}+2p^{(n-1)(n-2)/2+p^{n-1}}\right)^{\ell}
by Corollary
6.4
.

Size of
W_{1}(r,s)
.

We show that
|W_{1}(r,s)|\sim\left(p^{n}\right)^{\ell}
.

By the definition of
G_{1}
, the group
G_{1}(R)
is the centralizer of
A_{1}
in
\SO(Q_{n};R)
, for any ring
R
. Let
\chi\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
be the characteristic polynomial of
A_{1}
.

By Proposition
4.15
and Lemma
6.7
, there are infinitely many primes
p\equiv 3\pmod{4}
so that
\chi
splits completely in
(\mathbb{Z}/p^{j}\mathbb{Z})[x]
and
A_{1}
is diagonalizable in
\SO(Q_{n};\mathbb{Z}/p^{j}\mathbb{Z})
for each
j\geq 1
. Fix such a prime
p
, and set
R=\mathbb{Z}/p^{j}\mathbb{Z}
. Since
A_{1}
is diagonalizable with distinct eigenvalues, the centralizer of
A_{1}
in
\SO(Q_{n};R)
is conjugate to the group of diagonal matrices in
\SO(Q_{n};R)
; hence this centralizer has size

|(\mathbb{Z}/p^{j}\mathbb{Z})^{\times}|^{n}\sim(p^{j})^{n}.

It remains to show that
G_{1}(\mathbb{Z})
surjects to the centralizer of
A_{1}
in
\SO(Q_{n};\mathbb{Z}/p^{j}\mathbb{Z})
. The centralizer of
A_{1}
in
M_{2n}(\mathbb{Z})
contains a subring isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}[t]/(\chi)
(see §
4
). The same statement holds with
\mathbb{Z}
replaced by
R
. Since
\chi
splits completely in
R
by our choice of
p
, there is a ring isomorphism
R[x]/(\chi)\cong R^{n+1}
. Up to conjugation the map
R[x]/(\chi)\rightarrow M_{2n}(R)
has image equal to the diagonal matrices. From this we deduce that the invertible elements of
R[x]/(\chi)
surject onto the diagonal subgroup of
\GL_{2n}(R)
and that
G_{1}(\mathbb{Z})
surjects onto the centralizer of
A_{1}
in
\SO(Q_{n};\mathbb{Z}/p^{j}\mathbb{Z})
. From this we conclude that
|W_{1}(r,s)|\sim\left(p^{n}\right)^{\ell}
(for an isometry, the diagonal entries occur in
(\lambda,\frac{1}{\lambda})
pairs).

An expression in terms of volume.

Combining the above estimates, we have

\frac{|W(r,s)|}{|W_{1}(r,s)|\cdot|W_{2}(r,s)|}\sim\left(\frac{p^{n^{2}}+p^{n(n-1)/2}}{p^{n^{2}-n+1}+2p^{(n^{2}-n+2)/2}+p^{2n-1}}\right)^{\ell}

for infinitely many primes
p\equiv 3\pmod{4}
.

Since the volume satisfies
\vol(Y(s))=|W(r,s)|\cdot\vol(Y(r))
, we can write
\vol(Y(s))\sim\left(p^{n^{2}}+p^{n(n-1)/2}\right)^{\ell}

Therefore,

\frac{|W(r,s)|}{|W_{1}(r,s)|\cdot|W_{2}(r,s)|}\sim\vol(Y(s))^{\kappa},

where

\kappa=1-\frac{\log(p^{n^{2}-n+1}+2p^{(n^{2}-n+2)/2}+p^{2n-1})}{\log(p^{n^{2}}+p^{n(n-1)/2})}.

For given
p
and
n
, the numerator of the above fraction is bounded above by
\log(3p^{n^{2}-n+1})
while the denominator is bounded below by
\log(p^{n^{2}})
, so there is an inequality

\kappa\geq\frac{n-1-\log_{p}(3)}{n^{2}}.

Combined with Lemma
3.2
and Corollary
5.8
, this proves Theorem
B
. ∎
