Reverse mathematics and Weihrauch analysis motivated by finite complexity theory
Zack BeMent, Jeffry Hirst, Asuka Wallace

Abstract
We extend a study by Lempp and Hirst of infinite versions of some problems from finite complexity theory, using an intuitionistic version of reverse mathematics and techniques of Weihrauch analysis.



An early article of Hirst and Lempp
[
hl
]
was motivated by the following example.
The problem of determining if a finite graph has a Hamiltonian path is NP complete,
while the problem for Eulerian paths is in the class P. In reverse mathematics,
the problem of selecting which infinite graphs in an infinite sequence have Hamiltonian
paths is equivalent to
{\Pi^{1}_{1}{\text{-}}{\sf{CA}}_{0}}
, while the corresponding problem for Eulerian graphs
is equivalent to
{\sf ACA}_{0}
. While very evocative, Hirst and Lempp showed that this
parallel does not generally hold. In conjunction with an AMS special session in
honor of Lempp’s birthday, we revisited these early results using the tools
of Weihrauch analysis in two ways.

In the next section we concentrate on formalized Weihrauch reductions for
graph theoretic problems. This approach is limited to problems that can be
expressed with particularly simple formulas, having certain subformulas that
are
\exists
-free. The process yields both reverse mathematical and Weihrauch
reducibility results from a single argument.
More importantly, it facilitates the use of techniques from Weihrauch analysis,
like parallelization, in reverse mathematics proofs.
Additionally, the intuitionistic formal systems
used admit proof mining
[
kbook
]
, so terms corresponding to the reduction functionals could
be extracted from formal proofs. If we view the proofs as a form of verification,
formal Weihrauch reduction could offer a framework for finding verified extensions
of a trusted library of routines.

The final section addresses Weihrauch analysis of stronger results, with parallelizations
at the
{\Pi^{1}_{1}{\text{-}}{\sf{CA}}_{0}}
level. The formulas describing the problems are too complicated for
formal analysis techniques, so we turn to traditional Weihrauch analysis. The results
extend the currently small catalog of Weihrauch problems at this level, for example like those
in
[
leaf
]
. Weihrauch analysis at the level of
{\Pi^{1}_{1}{\text{-}}{\sf{CA}}_{0}}
appears to parallel
familiar reverse mathematics closely, in contrast to recent results related to

{\sf ATR}_{0}
by Kihara, Marcone, and Pauly
[
kmp
]
.

1
Formalized Weihrauch analysis

The following development of formalized Weihrauch reducibility draws from
work of Hirst and Mummert
[
hm
]
. Like them, we work in
i{\sf RCA}_{0}^{\omega}
, Kohlenbach’s
[
k2001
]

higher order axiomatization for reverse mathematics, restricted to intuitionistic logic.
Informally, we may view
i{\sf RCA}_{0}^{\omega}
as an axiomatization of intuitionistic arithmetic that can
prove the existence of computable functions and computable functionals. For more
details of the axiomatization, see
[
hm
]
.

We formalize a Weihrauch problem with a formula

\forall x(p_{1}(x)\to\exists y\,p_{2}(x,y))
where
p_{1}(x)
indicates that
x
is an accepted input
and
p_{2}(x,y)
indicates that
y
is a solution of
x
. Here
y
may be a function, set, or number,
depending on the problem. For total problems, that is, those problems accepting all sets as inputs,
we can use the more simple representation
\forall x\exists y\,p(x,y)
.

Some of the results connecting provability and formalized Weihrauch reducibility are restricted
to a family of formulas called
\Gamma_{1}
by Troelstra
[
troelstra
]
.
A formula is
\exists
-free
if it is built from atomic formulas using only universal quantification
and the connectives
\land
and
\to
.
(Troelstra includes
\bot
as an atomic formula, so
\neg P
abbreviates
P\to\bot
.)
The collection
\Gamma_{1}
consists
of those formulas defined inductively by the following:

\bullet

All atomic formulas are elements of
\Gamma_{1}
.

\bullet

If
A
and
B
are in
\Gamma_{1}
, then so are
A\land B
,
A\lor B
,

\forall xA
, and
\exists xA
.

\bullet

If
A
is
\exists
-free and
B
is in
\Gamma_{1}
then
\exists xA\to B
is in
\Gamma_{1}
,
where
\exists x
denotes a block of zero or more existential quantifiers.

Definition
.

Suppose
{\sf P}{\rm:}\forall x(p_{1}(x)\to\exists y\,p_{2}(x,y))
and
{\sf Q}{\rm:}\forall u(q_{1}(u)\to\exists v\,q_{2}(u,v))
are problems. The reduction prenex formula for
{\sf Q}\leq_{W}{\sf P}

is the formula:

S^{\sf P}_{\sf Q}:\forall u\exists x\forall y\exists v(q_{1}(u)\to(p_{1}(x)\land(p_{2}(x,y)\to q_{2}(u,v))))

If the problems are total, we can write
\sf P
as

\forall x\exists y\,p(x,y)
,
\sf Q
as
\forall u\exists v\,q(u,v)
and
S^{\sf P}_{\sf Q}
as

\forall u\exists x\forall y\exists v(p(x,y)\to q(u,v))
.

The implication
S^{\sf P}_{\sf Q}\to({\sf P}\to{\sf Q})
is provable in intuitionistic predicate calculus,
though the proof of the converse requires classical logic. If the constituent formulas

p_{1}
,
p_{2}
,
q_{1}
, and
q_{2}
are all
\exists
-free, then the matrix of
S^{\sf P}_{\sf Q}

is also
\exists
-free and
S^{\sf P}_{\sf Q}
is in
\Gamma_{1}
. This also holds for total problems.

If
\sf P
and
\sf Q
are Weihrauch problems, we can formalize
{\sf Q}\leq_{W}{\sf P}
by asserting
the existence of Skolem functions for the existential quantifiers of
S^{\sf P}_{\sf Q}
.
Using
\sf P
and
\sf Q
as in the definition, the relation
{\sf Q}\leq_{W}{\sf P}
can be
translated into the language of
i{\sf RCA}_{0}^{\omega}
as:

\exists\Phi\exists\Psi\forall u\forall y(q_{1}(u)\to(p_{1}(\Phi(u))\land(p_{2}(\Phi(u),y)\to q_{2}(u,\Psi(y)))))

Here the functionals
\Phi
and
\Psi
are of type
1\to 1
, which is our primary motivation
for working in higher order subsystems.

Using the preceding notation, we can present the following slightly modified version
of Theorem 1 of Hirst and Mummert
[
hm
]
.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose
\sf P
and
\sf Q
are problems and
the reduction prenex formula

S^{\sf P}_{\sf Q}
is in
\Gamma_{1}
.
Then
i{\sf RCA}_{0}^{\omega}\vdash S^{\sf P}_{\sf Q}
if and only if

i{\sf RCA}_{0}^{\omega}\vdash{\sf Q}\leq_{W}{\sf P}
.

Proof.

The result follows from the application of Kohlenbach’s
[
kbook
]
proof mining technology
to extract terms corresponding to the Skolem functions.
Only one modification to the proof of Theorem 1 of Hirst
and Mummert
[
hm
]
is needed. The hypothesis of their theorem specifies that
the matrix of
S^{\sf P}_{\sf Q}
is in
\Gamma_{1}
, which by the definition of

\Gamma_{1}
is equivalent to
S^{\sf P}_{\sf Q}\in\Gamma_{1}
.
∎

The following theorem is the intuitionistic analog of a conservation result
of Kohlenbach
[
k2001
]
.

Theorem 2
.

The system
i{\sf RCA}_{0}^{\omega}
is conservative over
i{\sf RCA}_{0}
for second order formulas.

Proof.

Kohlenbach’s conservation result for
{\sf RCA}_{0}^{\omega}
over
{\sf RCA}_{0}
appears
as Proposition 3.1 in his article on higher order reverse mathematics
[
k2001
]
.
Kohlenbach’s proof is based on the formalization of the extensional model
of the hereditarily continuous functionals (ECF) as presented in
Section 2.6.5 of Troelstra’s book
[
troelstra
]
. The arguments of
Troelstra (e.g. 2.6.12 and 2.6.20 of
[
troelstra
]
) are carried out
in intutionistic systems. Similarly, Kohlenbach’s proof holds for
i{\sf RCA}_{0}^{\omega}
and
i{\sf RCA}_{0}
.
For other related discussion, see Theorem 2.7
and Theorem 2.8 of Hirst and Mummert
[
hmunif
]
.
∎

1.1
Local graph coloring

The following encoding of graphs is useful in exploring graph coloring problems.
We can view a countable infinite graph as having
\mathbb{N}
as its vertex set.
Fix a primitive recursive bijective pairing function,
p
, mapping
\mathbb{N}
onto
\{(a,b)\mid a<b\}
, the
subset of
\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}
consisting of increasing pairs. Both
p
and
p^{-1}
can be
defined in such a way that
i{\sf RCA}_{0}^{\omega}
proves their existence and salient properties.
Using this pairing function, any function
e:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
can be viewed as a characteristic
function for the edge set of a graph
G
. If
e(n)\neq 0
then the edge
p(n)
is in
G
,
and if
e(n)=0
then
p(n)
is not in
G
. We will conflate
G
with the set of
codes for the edges of
G
and write
(a,b)\in G
as a shorthand for

e(p^{-1}(a,b))\neq 0
.

For any graph
G
and any
m
,
let
G_{m}
denote the finite subgraph
with vertices
\{0,\dots,m\}
and edge set

\{(a,b)\mid a<b\leq m\land(a,b)\in G\}
.
We say that
G_{m}
has a
k
-coloring if there is a finite function

f:m\to k
such that for all
a<b\leq m
,
(a,b)\in G
implies
f(a)\neq f(b)
.
Informally, vertices connect by an edge must have distinct colors.
The existence of
k
-colorings for (initial) finite subgraphs can be
formulated as a problem.

LG
k

(Local
k
-coloring for graphs): Fix
k
.
For a graph
G
(encoded by a characteristic function for its edge set), there
is a value
{\sf LG}{k}(G)
such that
{\sf LG}{k}(G)=0
implies that for every
m
the subgraph

G_{m}
has a
k
-coloring, and
{\sf LG}{k}(G)=m>0
implies that

G_{m}
has no
k
-coloring and
G_{m-1}
has a
k
-coloring.

Let
c(G,m)
denote a primitive recursive function such that

c(G,m)=1
if
G_{m}
has a
k
-coloring and
c(G,m)=0
if
G_{m}
has no

k
-coloring. Using this function, we can formalize the predicate
{\sf LG}{k}(G)=n

as the following
\exists
-free formula,

\begin{split}(n=0\to\forall m(c(G,m)=1))\land&\\
(n>1\to(c(G,n)=&0\land\forall t(t<n\to c(G,t)=1)))\end{split}

and note that
{\sf LG}{k}
is a total problem of the form

\forall G\exists n({\sf LG}{k}(G)=n)
.
We will compare
{\sf LG}{k}
to a version of the limited principle of omniscience.

LPO

(Limited principle of omniscience): For every
p:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
there is a value

{\sf{LPO}}(p)
such that
{\sf{LPO}}(p)=0
implies that
\forall k~p(k)>0
, and

{\sf{LPO}}(p)=m>0
implies that
p(m-1)=0
and for all
t<(m-1)
,
p(t)>0
.

We can formalize the predicate
{\sf{LPO}}(p)=n
as the following
\exists
-free formula,

(n=0\to\forall k(p(k)>0))\land(n>0\to(p(n-1)=0\land\forall t(t<(n-1)\to p(t)>0)))

and note that
{\sf{LPO}}
is a total problem of the form
\forall p\exists n({\sf{LPO}}(p)=n)
.
This version of
{\sf{LPO}}
differs from that presented in the survey by
Brattka, Gherardi, and Pauly
[
bgp
]
. It is Weihrauch equivalent to their version,
but not strongly Weihrauch equivalent, as the range of their version includes only
\{0,1\}

and the range of this version includes all of
\mathbb{N}
. However, for our purposes it is desirable
to have the underlying predicate be
\exists
-free.

Lemma 3
.

(i{\sf RCA}_{0}^{\omega})

For each
k\geq 1
, both
S^{{\sf LG}{k}}_{\sf{LPO}}
and
S^{\sf{LPO}}_{{\sf LG}{k}}
hold.

Proof.

Because both
{\sf{LPO}}
and
{\sf LG}{k}
are total, we can use the simple form of the
reduction prenex formula. Thus
S^{{\sf LG}{k}}_{\sf{LPO}}
is

\forall p\exists G\forall y\exists v({\sf LG}{k}(G)=y\to{\sf{LPO}}(p)=v).

To prove this in
i{\sf RCA}_{0}^{\omega}
, fix an instance
p
of
{\sf{LPO}}
. Define the graph
G
as follows.
For each
m
, if
p(m)=0
, add the edges

\{(s,t)\mid m\leq s<t\leq m+k\}
to
G
. These edges form a complete subgraph on

k+1
vertices, precluding any
k
-coloring of
G_{m+k}
. If
p(m)\neq 0
, add no new edges
to
G
. By this construction, if
{\sf LG}{k}(G)=0
, then for all
m
,
p(m)>0
, so
{\sf{LPO}}(p)=0
.
On the other hand, if
{\sf LG}{k}(G)=m>0
, then
m=n+k
for some
n\geq 0
, and
p(n)=0
,
so
{\sf{LPO}}(p)=n+1=m-k+1
.

To show that
S^{{\sf{LPO}}}_{{\sf LG}{k}}
, fix a graph
G
. Define an instance
p
of
{\sf{LPO}}
as follows.
For each
m
, if the subgraph
G_{m}
has a
k
-coloring, let
p(m)=1
. If
G_{m}
has no
k
-coloring,
let
p(m)=0
. The truncated subtraction
{\sf{LPO}}(p)\frac{\cdot}{~~}1
yields a correct output for
{\sf LG}{k}(G)
.
∎

By formalizing the transitivity of Weihrauch reduction we can extract additional results from the preceding lemma.

Lemma 4
.

(i{\sf RCA}_{0}^{\omega})
If
{\sf P}\leq_{W}{\sf Q}
and
{\sf Q}\leq_{W}{\sf R}
, then
{\sf P}\leq_{W}{\sf R}
.

Proof.

This is a formalization of the well-known property for Weihrauch reductions. The
system
i{\sf RCA}_{0}^{\omega}
can prove that compositions of given functionals exist, so the usual
proof holds.
∎

Theorem 5
.

(i{\sf RCA}_{0}^{\omega})
For any
j\geq k\geq 1
,
{\sf{LPO}}\equiv_{W}{\sf LG}{k}\equiv_{W}{\sf LG}{j}
.

Proof.

By our formalization of
{\sf{LPO}}
and
{\sf LG}{k}
, the corresponding predicates are
\exists
-free.
Consequently, both
S^{{\sf LG}{k}}_{\sf{LPO}}
and
S^{{\sf{LPO}}}_{{\sf LG}{k}}
are in
\Gamma_{1}
.
Applying Theorem
1
to the implications of Lemma
3
yields proofs in

i{\sf RCA}_{0}^{\omega}
that
{\sf{LPO}}\leq_{W}{\sf LG}{k}
,
{\sf LG}{k}\leq_{W}{\sf{LPO}}
, and so
{\sf{LPO}}\equiv_{W}{\sf LG}{k}
.
This holds for all
j
and
k
, so by Lemma
4
we have
{\sf LG}{k}\equiv_{W}{\sf LG}{j}
.
∎

One noteworthy consequence of the preceding theorem is the provability of the Weihrauch equivalence
of
{\sf LG}{2}
and
{\sf LG}{3}
in
i{\sf RCA}_{0}^{\omega}
. The finite combinatorial analogs of these problems are
2-colorability and 3-colorability of finite graphs, which are respectively polynomial time computable
and NP complete. As was the case in the traditional reverse mathematics and computability
theoretic analysis of Hirst and Lempp
[
hl
]
, formal Weihrauch
analysis of the related infinite problems does not distinguish between these problems.
The current setting does allow us to apply techniques of Weihrauch analysis, like the
application of Lemma
4
in the preceding proof to obtain formal equivalences,
and then to translate these into proofs of implications in weak subsystems, as in the following
corollary.

Corollary 6
.

(i{\sf RCA}_{0}^{\omega})
For every pair of problems
\sf P
and
\sf Q
from the list in Theorem
5
,

S^{\sf P}_{\sf Q}
holds. Furthermore, the implication
{\sf P}\to{\sf Q}
holds.

Proof.

As noted above, the formulas
S^{\sf P}_{\sf Q}
of Theorem
5

are in
\Gamma_{1}
. Applying the reverse implication Theorem
1
to the equivalences of Theorem
5

proves
S^{\sf P}_{\sf Q}
. To justify the final sentence, note that these are total problems and can be written
as
\Pi^{1}_{1}
formulas. Over intuitionistic predicate calculus,
S^{\sf P}_{\sf Q}
implies
{\sf P}\to{\sf Q}
.
∎

If
\sf P
is a problem, the parallelization of
\sf P
, denoted by
\widehat{\sf P}
is the problem
that accepts as input any infinite sequence of inputs for
\sf P
, and outputs the associated infinite
sequence of solutions for the input instances. The following lemma is a formalization of a portion
of part 3
of Proposition 3.6 of Brattka, Gherardi, and Pauly
[
bgp
]
.

Lemma 7
.

(i{\sf RCA}_{0}^{\omega})

{\sf P}\leq_{W}{\sf Q}
implies
\widehat{\sf{P}}\leq_{W}\widehat{\sf{Q}}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
\Phi
and
\Psi
are the functionals witnessing
{\sf P}\leq_{W}{\sf Q}
.
If
\vec{u}=\langle u_{i}\rangle_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is an input for
\widehat{\sf Q}
, define

\hat{\Phi}
by
\hat{\Phi}(\vec{u})=\langle\Phi(u_{i})\rangle_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
. Define

\hat{\Psi}
similarly. Note that
i{\sf RCA}_{0}^{\omega}
suffices to prove the existence of
\hat{\Phi}

and
\hat{\Psi}
, and also proves that they witness
\widehat{\sf{P}}\leq_{W}\widehat{\sf{Q}}
.
∎

Theorem 8
.

(i{\sf RCA}_{0}^{\omega})
For every
k\geq 1
,
\widehat{\sf{LPO}}\equiv_{W}\widehat{{\sf LG}{k}}
.

Proof.

Apply Lemma
7
to Theorem
5
.
∎

If
\langle f_{i}\rangle_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of input functions for
\widehat{\sf{LPO}}
, we can
define a type 1 function on (codes of pairs of) natural numbers by
f(i,n)=f_{i}(n)
and think of
f
as a type 1 input for
\widehat{\sf{LPO}}
. This modification
allows us to think of
\widehat{\sf{LPO}}
as a
\Pi^{1}_{2}
formula of second order arithmetic.
Similarly, we will conflate
\widehat{{\sf LG}{k}}
with its second order analog.

Corollary 9
.

(i{\sf RCA}_{0})
The following are equivalent:

(1)

{\sf ACA}_{0}
.

(2)

\widehat{{\sf{LPO}}}

(3)

\widehat{{\sf LG}{k}}
, where
k\geq 1
.

Proof.

The equivalence of parts (
2
) and (
3
) follows from
the application of Theorem
1
to the Weihrauch reductions included in Theorem
8
, and the
fact that
S^{\sf P}_{\sf Q}\to({\sf P}\to{\sf Q})
. To show that
(
1
) implies (
2
), let
\langle f_{i}\rangle_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be
an input for
\widehat{{\sf{LPO}}}
. Arithmetical comprehension asserts the
existence of the set
\{i\mid\forall n\,f_{i}(n)\neq 0\}
and the
corresponding characteristic function, which is a solution of the instance of
\widehat{{\sf{LPO}}}
.

To show that (
2
) implies (
1
), we adapt the familiar
application of Lemma III.1.3 of Simpson
[
simpson
]
. In
i{\sf RCA}_{0}
it suffices to
find the range of an arbitrary function that is injective, except for possibly repeatedly
taking the value
0
. (This broader class of almost injective functions avoids a use of classical
logic in the proof of Lemma II.3.7, used by Simpson
[
simpson
]
to prove the reversal.)
Let
f
be such a function.
Define the sequence of functions
\langle f_{i}\rangle_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
by

f_{i}(m)=1
if
\forall t<m(f(t)\neq i)
and
f_{i}(m)=0
otherwise.
The solution to the
\widehat{{\sf{LPO}}}
problem
\langle f_{i}\rangle_{i\in\mathbb{N}}

.

are all second order, so by Theorem

the equivalences are provable in the second order system

.

1.2
Colorings as outputs

We can reformulate the graph coloring problems so that the outputs are infinite graph colorings.
We will continue to use the notation of the previous subsection. In particular,
G_{m}
denotes the finite
subgraph of
G
consisting of the first
m+1
vertices and their edges in
G
.

GC
k

(
k
-coloring of graphs):
If
e:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
codes the edge set for a graph
G
and every finite
subgraph
G_{m}
has a
k
-coloring, then there is a function
f:\mathbb{N}\to k

that is a
k
-coloring of
G
.

The predicate asserting that
G_{m}
is
k
-colorable can be written using bounded quantifiers, with

G
,
k
, and
m
as parameters. The system
i{\sf RCA}_{0}^{\omega}
proves the existence of the primitive recursive
characteristic functional for this predicate, so the assertion that every
G_{m}
has a
k
-coloring can
be formalized with a
\exists
-free formula. Note that
f
is a
k
-coloring of
G
if and only if
for each
i\neq j
, if
(i,j)
is an edge in
G
then
f(i)\neq f(j)
, which is also
\exists
-free.
Thus we can write an
\exists
-free formula
{\sf{GC}}k(G,f)
asserting that
f
is the solution
to the
{\sf{GC}}k
problem for
G
.

In our study of problems involving infinite trees, we will use the the following encoding.
Fix a suitable bijective coding function
{\sf seq}
mapping
\mathbb{N}
onto
2^{<\mathbb{N}}
, the set
of finite sequences of zeros and ones. Any function
t:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
can be viewed as
a code for a binary tree
T
using the following convention. The finite sequence
{\sf seq}(n)
is
in
T
if and only if
t(n)>0
and for every initial segment
\sigma
of
{\sf seq}(n)
,

t({\sf seq}^{-1}(\sigma))>0
. Informally, the function
t
is the characteristic function for a
set that includes the sequences of
T
and omits the immediate successors of any leaves of
T
.
Using this encoding, we have the following formalization of Weak Königs’s Lemma as a total
problem.

WKL

(Weak König’s Lemma):
If
T
is a binary tree and for every
m
there is a sequence
\sigma\in T
of length
m
,
then there is a function
p
that codes an infinite path through
T
.

In the previous formalization,
p
can be viewed as
a code for a subtree
P
of
T
such that each node in
P
has precisely one immediate successor.
Using our standard techniques for representing primitive recursive functionals, it is easy to
formalize the assertion that for every
m
the tree
T
contains a binary sequence of length

m
as an
\exists
-free formula. Note that the bound on the labels is essential for this.
The claim that
p
is an infinite path is a universal statement, so we can write an
\exists
-free
formula
{\sf{WKL}}(T,f)
asserting that
f
is a solution to the
\sf{WKL}
problem for
T
.

For values of
n
greater than
2
, we can use a bijective coding
{\sf seq}_{n}
of sequences of numbers
less than
n
, and formulate a corresponding version of
{\sf WKL}
.

WKL
n

(Weak König’s Lemma for trees in
n^{<\mathbb{N}}
):
If
T
is a tree with nodes labeled with numbers less than
n
,
and for every
m
there is a sequence
\sigma\in T
of length
m
,
then there is a function
p
that codes an infinite path through
T
.

Note that
{\sf WKL}
and
{\sf WKL}{2}
denote the same problem. As with
{\sf WKL}

{\sf S}_{F}(G)=1
, then the search for the least
m
such that
p(m)=0
will
succeed and the appropriate value of
{\sf{LPO}}(p)
can be output.

The case when
F
is completely disconnected is similar, using complementary graphs. All possible edges
are added to
G
until a zero of
p
is discovered, at which point no additional edges are added.
∎

The fact that
{\sf S}_{F}\equiv_{W}{\sf{LPO}}
and
{\sf S}_{L}\equiv_{W}{\sf WF}
motivates the following question: Is there a graph

H
such that
{\sf{LPO}}<_{W}{\sf S}_{H}<_{W}{\sf WF}
?
Arno Pauly says yes, if we switch from isomorphism to subgraph embedding (
SE
).
For the graph
H
consisting of infinitely many copies of K3,
{\sf SE}_{H}\equiv_{\sf W}{\sf{LPO}}^{\prime}

[
pauly
]
.
Switching to embeddings does not change Theorem
24
, for example. Reed Solomon has
asked what other reductions differ in strength for various notions of embedding.

We can also consider the problem
{\sf S}^{G}
for a fixed graph
G
, asking whether or not an input graph is isomorphic
to a subgraph of
G
. For the complete graph
K
and the totally disconnected graph
D
it is easy to show that

{\sf S}^{K}
and
{\sf S}^{D}
are both Weihrauch equivalent to
{\sf{LPO}}
. Theorem 3.2 of
[
hl
]
shows that there is a
computable graph
G
such that the set of indices of computable graphs that are isomorphic to a subgraph of
G
is

\Sigma^{1}_{1}
complete. This prompts us to ask: Is there a graph
G
such that
{\sf S}^{G}\equiv_{sW}{\sf WF}
?
Indeed, is there even a graph
G
such that
{\sf{LPO}}<_{W}{\sf S}^{G}
?

Theorem 2.6 of
[
hl
]
shows a connection between sequential versions of the following problems.
For a graph
G
with vertex set
V
, we say
c:V\to\mathbb{N}
is a coloring of
G
if whenever
(v_{1},v_{2})
is
an edge of
G
,
c(v_{1})\neq c(v_{2})
.

\sf{RC}

(Repeated color):
Given a graph
G
as an input, output
1
if there is a coloring of
G
that uses one color infinitely often and
output
0
if there is no such coloring.

\sf{D}

(Disconnected subgraph):
Given a graph
G
as an input, output
1
if
G
has an infinite completely disconnected subgraph and output

0
otherwise.

Theorem 28
.

{\sf RC}\equiv_{sW}{\sf D}\equiv_{sW}{\sf WF}
.

Proof.

Any graph
G
has a coloring as required by
\sf RC
if and only if it has a subgraph as in
\sf D
. Thus

{\sf RC}\equiv_{sW}{\sf D}
. Overloading notation and using
D
to denote the totally disconnected graph,
we have
{\sf D}\equiv_{sW}{\sf S}_{D}
. The problem
{\sf S}_{D}
is a special case of
{\sf S}
, so

{\sf D}\leq_{sW}{\sf S}\equiv_{sW}{\sf WF}
.

To show that
{\sf WF}\leq_{sW}{\sf S}_{D}
, given a tree
T
as input, define a graph
G
as follows. The vertices of

G
will consist of the nodes of
T
. A pair
(v_{1},v_{2})
is an edge in
G
if and only if the corresponding
nodes of
T
are incomparable. (Nodes of
T
are comparable if one extends the other in the tree.)
G
is
uniformly computable from
T
. The completely disconnected graph
D
is isomorphic to a subgraph of

G
if and only if
T
contains an infinite collection of pairwise comparable nodes, that is, if and only if

T
is not well founded. For
T
and
G
as described above,
{\sf WF}(T)=1-{\sf S}_{D}(G)
, so

{\sf WF}\leq_{sW}{\sf S}_{D}
, as desired.
∎

Appending a triangle to each tree node in the previous construction yields a proof that
{\sf WF}
is Weihrauch
equivalent to
{\sf S}_{G}
for the graph
G
consisting of infinitely many disconnected triangles.
(This was proven independently by Arno Pauly
[
pauly
]
.) Thus
the graph from Pauly’s embedding observation does not answer our question about isomorphic subgraphs.
Parallelizing the previous theorem yields Weihrauch equivalences mirroring the reverse mathematics of Theorem 2.6 of
[
hl
]
.

Corollary 29
.

\widehat{\sf RC}\equiv_{sW}\widehat{\sf D}\equiv_{sW}\widehat{\sf{WF}}
.

Proof.

Immediate from Theorem
28
by Proposition 3.6 part 3 of
[
bgp
]
.
∎

The application of Weihrauch analysis to the problems of this section yields no new
insights into the corresponding finite complexity theoretic relationships. However, it does raise
some new Weihrauch analysis questions. Analyzing infinite versions of other statements from
finite complexity theory might lead to additional interesting Weihrauch reductions.
