Introduction

The present paper deals with univariate real polynomials

Q:=\sum_{j=0}^{d}a_{j}x^{j}
,
a_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
,
a_{d}\neq 0
. The classical
Descartes’ rule of signs states that the number
\ell_{+}
of positive roots
of such a polynomial (counted with multiplicity) is majorized by the number

\tilde{c}
of sign changes in the sequence of its coefficients and the
difference
\tilde{c}-\ell_{+}
is even, see
[
1
]
,
[
2
]
,
[
3
]
,

[
4
]
,
[
5
]
,
[
6
]
,
[
7
]
,
[
8
]
,
[
15
]
or

[
16
]
. (When
defining the number
\tilde{c}
one has to erase the zero coefficients.)
We are mainly concerned with
hyperbolic
polynomials, i. e. ones with all
roots real. If all coefficients of a hyperbolic polynomial are
non-vanishing, then
\ell_{+}=\tilde{c}
and
\ell_{-}=\tilde{p}
, where
\ell_{-}
is
the number of negative roots and
\tilde{p}
is the number of sign
preservations; clearly
\ell_{+}+\ell_{-}=\tilde{c}+\tilde{p}=d
.

We are interested in a problem closely related to Descartes’ rule of signs.

Definition 1
.

A
sign pattern
of length
d+1
is a string of
d+1
signs

+
and/or
-
. If all coefficients
a_{j}
are non-zero, then we say
that the polynomial
Q
defines the sign pattern

\sigma(Q):=({\rm sgn}(a_{d}),{\rm sgn}(a_{d-1})
,

\ldots
,
{\rm sgn}(a_{0}))
.
If there is at least one vanishing coefficient, then we say that

\sigma(Q)
is a
generalized sign pattern
and its components
can be equal to
+
,
-
or
0
. For a given sign pattern, we define
its corresponding
change-preservation pattern
by writing the letter

c

when there are two consecutive signs
(+,-)
or
(-,+)
and the letter

p
if
there are signs
(+,+)
or
(-,-)
. Thus the change-preservation pattern
corresponding to the sign pattern
(+,+,-,-,-,+,-,+)
is
pcppccc
.
We consider mainly monic polynomials, so the correspondence between sign
and change-preservation patterns is bijective.

One can consider the moduli of the roots of
Q
. We limit ourselves to the
case when all these moduli are distinct. The definition of the

order
of these moduli should be clear from the following example.

Example 1
.

Suppose that
d=8
and the polynomial
Q
has positive roots

\alpha_{1}<\alpha_{2}<\alpha_{3}
and negative roots
-\gamma_{j}
,
j=1
,

\ldots
,
5
, such that

\gamma_{1}<\alpha_{1}<\gamma_{2}<\gamma_{3}<\gamma_{4}<\alpha_{2}<\gamma_{5}<\alpha_{3}~.

Then we say that the order of moduli defined by the roots of
Q
is

NPNNNPNP
, i. e. the letters
P
and
N
indicate the relative positions
of the moduli of positive and negative roots.

Definition 2
.

(1) A couple (sign pattern, order of moduli) is
compatible
if the
number of sign changes (resp. sign preservations) of the sign pattern is
equal to the number of letters
P
(resp.
N
) of the order of moduli.
In what follows we consider only compatible couples
and we write “couples” for short.

(2) A couple is
realizable
if there
exists a hyperbolic polynomial which defines the sign pattern of the couple
and the moduli of whose roots define the given order. We can also say that
the given sign pattern is realizable with the given order of moduli or
vice versa.

In the present paper we consider the following problem:

Problem 1
.

For a given degree
d
, which couples are realizable?

The problem is settled for
d\leq 6
, see
[
11
]
and
[
12
]
.
We remind now some results towards the resolution of Problem
1

Definition 3
.

Given a sign pattern
\sigma^{*}
one defines its corresponding

canonical
order of moduli as follows: one reads
\sigma^{*}

from the right and to each sign change (resp. sign preservation) one puts
into correspondence the letter
P
(resp.
N
). Example: the canonical
order corresponding to the sign pattern
(+,-,-,-,+,+,-,+,-,-,+)
is

PNPPPNPNNP
.

Remarks 1
.

(1) Each couple (sign pattern, corresponding canonical order)
is realizable,
see
[
13
, Proposition 1]
. However there exist sign patterns
(called also
canonical
, see
[
10
]
) which are realizable

only
with
their corresponding canonical orders. They are characterized by the property
not to contain four consecutive signs
(+,+,-,-)
,
(-,-,+,+)
,

(+,-,-,+)
or
(-,+,+,-)
, so the corresponding change-preservation patterns
have no triple
cpc
or
pcp
, i. e. no isolated sign change and no
isolated sign preservation except at the two ends.

(2) There exist also orders of moduli (called
rigid
) which are realizable
with a single sign pattern. Except the
trivial case
when all roots are
of the same sign and the change-preservation pattern is
cc\ldots c
or

pp\ldots p
, rigid are the orders
\ldots NPNPNP
and
\ldots PNPNPN
,
realizable with the sign patterns
(+,-,-,+,+,-,-,+,+,\ldots)
and

(+,+,-,-,+,+,-,-,+,+,\ldots)
respectively, see
[
14
]
.

The present paper explores the inverse phenomenon, i. e.
sign patterns realizable with all compatible orders of moduli.

Remark 1
.

Excluding the trivial case, orders of moduli realizable
with all compatible sign patterns do not exist. Indeed, if such an order

\Omega
exists (with
\tilde{c}>0
letters
P
and
\tilde{p}>0

letters
N
), then it should be realizable with each of the two
change-preservation patterns,
cc\ldots cpp\ldots p
and

pp\ldots pcc\ldots c
, each with
\tilde{c}
letters
c
and
\tilde{p}

letters
p
. However these sign patterns are canonical, see part (1) of
Remarks
1
, and their corresponding canonical orders of
moduli are different, see Definition
3
,
so
\Omega
cannot be equal to each of them.

Definition 4
.

A sign pattern is called
universal
if it is realizable with all
compatible orders of moduli.

There exist natural candidates for the role of universal sign patterns.

Notation 1
.

(1) We set
P_{m,n}:=(x-1)^{m}(x+1)^{n}
,
m
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
. We denote
by
\sigma_{j}(P_{m,n})
the sign
+
,
-
or
0
of the coefficient of

of the polynomial
P_{m,n}
.
It is clear that the polynomial

is

self-reciprocal

x^{m+n}P_{m,n}(1/x)=(-1)^{m}P_{m,n}(x)
. Hence the
sign changes and sign preservations in
\sigma(P_{m,n})
are situated
symmetrically w.r.t. the middle of
\sigma(P_{m,n})

As
P_{m,n}(-x)=(-1)^{m+n}P_{n,m}(x)
, it suffices to study the polynomials

for

.

(2) We denote by
\Sigma_{k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},\ldots}
a sign pattern beginning with

k_{1}
signs

followed by
k_{2}

signs
-
followed by
k_{3}
signs
+
etc.
For a generalized sign pattern we use a similar notation including zeros at the
places between two numbers

where zero coefficients occur, see part (1)
of Lemma
1
. Thus the
generalized sign pattern
(+,+,0,-,-,-,+,0,-,-)
will be denoted also by

\Sigma_{2,0,3,1,0,2}
.

Remark 2
.

For
d\geq 4
, a sign pattern
\Sigma_{k_{1},k_{2},\ldots k_{s}}
is canonical
if and only if
k_{i}\neq 2
for
i=2
,
\ldots
,
s-1
. For

,
not canonical are exactly
\Sigma_{1,2,1}
and

.

Theorem 1
.

(1) When the polynomial
P_{m,n}
has no vanishing coefficients, the sign
pattern
\sigma(P_{m,n})
is universal.

(2) For
d=2m+1
, a sign pattern with
m
sign changes and
m+1
sign
preservations is universal if and only if this is the sign pattern

\sigma(P_{m,m+1})=\Sigma_{2,2,\ldots,2}
.

(3) For
m
even, one has
\sigma(P_{m,m+2})=\Sigma_{2,\ldots,2,3,2,\ldots,2}

(
m/2
times
2
, one
3
and again
m/2
times
2
). For
m

\sigma(P_{m,m+2})=\Sigma_{2,2,\ldots,2,0,2,2,\ldots,2}

is a generalized sign pattern
consisting of
(m+1)/2
couples of equal signs (the signs of two consecutive
couples being opposite), one
0
and again
(m+1)/2
couples of equal signs.

We give comments on the theorem in the next section and its proof in
Section
4
. While the proof of part (1) is short and needs no
preliminary results, the proofs of parts (2) and (3) rely on
Section
3
in which we study the question when the polynomial
P_{m,n}

can have zero coefficients.

2.
Comments

If a hyperbolic polynomial
Q
has at least one vanishing coefficient, then
it is not true that
\ell_{-}=\tilde{p}
, but only that
\ell_{-}
is equal to
the number of sign changes in the sequence of coefficients of the polynomial

Q(-x)
. Example: for
Q:=x^{2}-1
, one has

\ell_{+}=\ell_{-}=\tilde{c}=1\neq\tilde{p}=0
; see also Remark
4
.
Bearing this difference in mind,
one can define realizability of
couples (generalized sign pattern,
order of moduli) as this is done for sign patterns in
part (2) of Definition
2
,
and then define universal generalized sign patterns by analogy with
Definition
4
.

For
d\leq 6
, the following two statements hold true
(this can be directly checked using the results
in
[
11
]
and
[
12
]
):

•

All universal sign patterns are of the form
\sigma(P_{m,n})
for
some
m
and
n
.

•

When
\sigma(P_{m,n})
is a generalized sign pattern, not
a sign pattern, then there exists no universal sign pattern with

\tilde{c}=m
and
\tilde{p}=n
. Hence for
d\leq 6
,
no generalized sign pattern is
universal, otherwise all sign patterns obtained from it by replacing its
zeros by
+
or
-
should also be universal. (One can perturb the
coefficients of
P_{m,n}
to make them all non-zero without changing
the order of moduli.)

It would be interesting to prove or disprove that these two statements hold
true for any
d\in\mathbb{N}
.

In the next section we deal with the situation when
\sigma(P_{m,n})
is not
a sign pattern, but a generalized sign pattern, so part (1) of
Theorem
1
cannot be applied. For
n>m
and as
m\rightarrow\infty
,
in asymptotically
1/4
of the cases there is a vanishing coefficient in

P_{m,n}
. These are the cases when
m
and
n
are odd and this is the middle
coefficient, see part (1) of Proposition
1
. Zero coefficients
not in the middle also occur, but they seem to appear less and less frequently
as
m\rightarrow\infty
, see Propositions
1

and
3
, Remarks
2

and Remarks
3
.

Proof of Proposition
1
.

The first two claims of part (1) follow from the self-reciprocity of
P_{m,n}
.
In particular, when
m
and
n
are odd, one has

x^{m+n}P_{m,n}(1/x)=-P_{m,n}(x)
and
the middle coefficient of
P_{m,n}
is equal to its opposite, so it is
0
.
For
m=1
, one has
P_{m,n}=\sum_{j=0}^{n}({n\choose j-1}-{n\choose j})x^{j}
, so

P_{m,n}
has a zero coefficient if and only if
n
is odd and
j=(n+1)/2
.
If
m
and
n
are even and the middle coefficient vanishes, then by
part (2) of Lemma
1
there is a sign change in the middle of the sign
pattern
\sigma(P_{m,n})
. By self-reciprocity the remaining sign changes
are symmetrically situated w.r.t. the middle of
\sigma(P_{m,n})
, so
the total number of sign changes is odd which contradicts the parity of
m
.

Part (2). For
m=2
, one has

\begin{array}[]{cccccc}P_{2,n}&=&(x^{2}-2x+1)\sum_{j=0}^{n}{n\choose j}x^{j}&=&\sum_{j=0}^{n+2}b_{n,j}x^{j}~,&{\rm where}\\
\\
b_{n,j}&:=&{n\choose j}-2{n\choose j-1}+{n\choose j-2}~.&&&\end{array}

The condition
b_{n,j}=0
reads

\begin{array}[]{lccc}(n-j+2)(n-j+1)-2(n-j+2)j+j(j-1)&=&0~,&{\rm i.~e.}\\
\\
4j^{2}-(4n+8)j+(n+2)(n+1)&=&0~,&\end{array}

so one obtains
j=\tilde{j}_{\pm}:=(n+2\pm\sqrt{n+2})/2
.
These values of
j
are integer
exactly when
n=\nu^{2}-2
,
\nu\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
,
\nu\geq 2
,
from which the first two claims of part (2) follow.

Suppose that
\nu^{2}-2<n<(\nu+1)^{2}-2
, i. e.

\nu^{2}<n+2<(\nu+1)^{2}~~~\,{\rm and}~~~\,(n+1-\nu)/2<\tilde{j}_{-}<(n+2-\nu)/2~.

If
n-\nu
is even, then

[(n-\nu)/2]<[(n-\nu)/2]+1/2<\tilde{j}_{-}<[(n-\nu)/2]+1

(where
[.]
stands
for the integer part). If
n-\nu
is odd, then

[(n-\nu)/2]+1<\tilde{j}_{-}<[(n-\nu)/2]+3/2<[(n-\nu)/2]+2

from which the rest of
part (2) follows.

Part (3). For
m=3
, one has

\begin{array}[]{l}P_{3,n}=(x^{3}-3x^{2}+3x-1)\sum_{j=0}^{n}{n\choose j}x^{j}=\sum_{j=0}^{n+2}c_{n,j}x^{j}~,~~~\,{\rm where}\\
\\
c_{n,j}:={n\choose j}-3{n\choose j-1}+3{n\choose j-2}-{n\choose j-3}~.\end{array}

The condition
c_{n,j}=0
yields

\begin{array}[]{l}(n-j+3)(n-j+2)(n-j+1)-3(n-j+3)(n-j+2)j\\
\\
+3(n-j+3)j(j-1)-j(j-1)(j-2)=0~,~~~\,{\rm i.~e.}\\
\\
2(j-(n+3)/2)(4j^{2}-(4n+12)j+(n+2)(n+1))=0~.\end{array}

The first factor defines an integer solution
j=(n+3)/2
exactly when
n
is
odd. This proves part (3)(a).

The second factor is
0
for

j=j^{*}_{\pm}:=(n+3\pm\sqrt{3n+7})/2

and
j^{*}_{\pm}
are
integer only if
n=(\mu^{2}-7)/3
,
\mu\in\mathbb{N}
, in which case

n
and
\mu
are of opposite parity and

j^{*}_{\pm}=(\mu\pm 1)(\mu\pm 2)/6
. Both these values are integer exactly
when
\mu
is not divisible by

. This proves the first displayed formula
of part (3)(b).

It is clear that if
j^{*}_{\pm}
are
not integer, then
\rho=[j^{*}_{-}]+1
. The condition

(\mu^{2}-7)/3<n<((\mu+1)^{2}-7)/3

can be given the equivalent form

-\mu-1<-\sqrt{3n+7}<-\mu~,

therefore

(n+2-\mu)/2<j^{*}_{-}<(n+3-\mu)/2~,~~~\,{\rm i.~e.}

\begin{array}[]{cl}(n-\mu)/2+1<j^{*}_{-}<(n-\mu)/2+3/2<(n-\mu+4)/2&{\rm for}~~n-\mu~~{\rm even}~,\\
\\
(n-\mu-1)/2+3/2<j^{*}_{-}<(n-\mu+3)/2&{\rm for}~~n-\mu~~{\rm odd}~.\end{array}

When
n
and
\mu
are of the same parity, then
n-\mu
is even and

\rho=(n-\mu+4)/2
. When they are of different parity, then

n-\mu
is odd and
\rho=(n-\mu+3)/2
.

It remains to find
\kappa

To this end one has to notice that when
n
is odd, then the length
of the sign pattern is odd and

, so

\kappa=\mu/2
for

\kappa=(\mu-1)/2
for
\mu

When
n
is even, then the length
of the sign pattern is even and

, thus

\kappa=\mu/2
for
\mu

\kappa=(\mu+1)/2
for
\mu

This proves the second displayed formula of part (3)(b).

Part (4). The first claim of part (4) results from
P_{m,m}=(x^{2}-1)^{m}

The following lemma (see

(2) If

Remark 4
