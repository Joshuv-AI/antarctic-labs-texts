2.
Induction on entropy distance

Fix, for the rest of the paper, the group

and the variables

Our overall strategy can be thought of as a kind of induction on (a slight modification of) the distance

For the rest of the paper,

\tau[X_{1};X_{2}]\coloneqq{\operatorname{d}[X_{1};X_{2}]}+\eta{\operatorname{d}[X^{0}_{1};X_{1}]}+\eta{\operatorname{d}[X^{0}_{2};X_{2}]}.

Note that this functional depends only on the distributions

(or

Here is the main result from which we will deduce

Proposition 2.1

Let

be two

{\operatorname{d}[X_{1};X_{2}]}>0

\tau[X^{\prime}_{1};X^{\prime}_{2}]<\tau[X_{1};X_{2}].

If we ignore the two penalty terms involving

asserts that if two random variables have a positive Ruzsa distance, then we can ‘improve’ them, finding two new variables

. To take care of the penalty terms, we will also need the new variables

to be ‘related’ to the old variables

in some sense, so that both sets of variables have a comparable Ruzsa distance to the reference variables

In order to deduce

Suppose that

{\operatorname{d}[X_{1};X_{2}]}=0

{\operatorname{d}[X_{1};U_{H}]}={\operatorname{d}[X_{2};U_{H}]}=0

Let

To prove

To describe the choices we will try, consider a four-tuple of independent random variables

are copies of

will be sums

(3.2)

What we find in this case is that if neither choice (
3.1

\tilde{X}_{1}+X_{2}
are almost independent conditioned on
X_{1}+X_{2}+\tilde{X}_{1}+\tilde{X}_{2}
.
Similarly, if neither choice (
3.2
), (
3.4
) works to prove (
2.2
), we obtain a bound

(3.6)

I_{2}\coloneqq\mathbf{I}\big[X_{1}+X_{2}:X_{1}+\tilde{X}_{1}|X_{1}+X_{2}+\tilde{X}_{1}+\tilde{X}_{2}\big]=O(\eta k).

Finally,

(3.7)

I_{3}\coloneqq\mathbf{I}\big[\tilde{X}_{1}+X_{2}:X_{1}+\tilde{X}_{1}|X_{1}+X_{2}+\tilde{X}_{1}+\tilde{X}_{2}\big]=O(\eta k),

since in fact we have
I_{2}=I_{3}
, as may be seen by interchanging the names of
\tilde{X}_{1}
and
X_{1}
.

If none of the choices (
3.1
), (
3.2
), (
3.3
), (
3.4
) works to prove (
2.2
), all three estimates (
3.5
), (
3.6
) and (
3.7
) hold.
In this case, we proceed to a part of the argument we refer to as the ‘endgame’.

Suppose first for simplicity that the mutual informations in (
3.5
), (
3.6
) and (
3.7
) were zero rather than merely small.
It follows that for any
s
in the support of
X_{1}+X_{2}+\tilde{X}_{1}+\tilde{X}_{2}
, the three random variables

\displaystyle T_{1}

\displaystyle=(X_{1}+X_{2}|X_{1}+X_{2}+\tilde{X}_{1}+\tilde{X}_{2}=s),

\displaystyle T_{2}

\displaystyle=(X_{1}+\tilde{X}_{1}|X_{1}+X_{2}+\tilde{X}_{1}+\tilde{X}_{2}=s),

(3.8)

\displaystyle T_{3}

\displaystyle=(\tilde{X}_{1}+X_{2}|X_{1}+X_{2}+\tilde{X}_{1}+\tilde{X}_{2}=s)

are pairwise independent.
We also note that
T_{1}+T_{2}+T_{3}
is constant (in fact it is identically zero).
However, for any trio
(T_{1},T_{2},T_{3})
of pairwise independent random variables such that
T_{1}+T_{2}+T_{3}
is constant, a short calculation shows that

{\operatorname{d}[T_{1};T_{2}]}=\mathbf{H}[T_{3}]-\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{H}[T_{1}]-\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{H}[T_{2}]

and similarly for cyclic permutations, hence

(3.9)

{\operatorname{d}[T_{1};T_{2}]}+{\operatorname{d}[T_{1};T_{3}]}+{\operatorname{d}[T_{2};T_{3}]}=0

and so, taking
X^{\prime}_{1}
and
X^{\prime}_{2}
to be some pair of
T_{1},T_{2},T_{3}
, we have
{\operatorname{d}[X_{1}^{\prime};X_{2}^{\prime}]}=0
.
This is a very strong conclusion, and with some further estimation, it allows us to establish (
2.2
). (The choice of
X^{\prime}_{1},X^{\prime}_{2}
here is inspired, albeit rather indirectly, by a step in an argument of Katz and Koester
[
14
]
.)

If the three variables in (
3.8
) are merely ‘almost pairwise independent’, as in (
3.5
) and (
3.6
), then things are less straightforward.
Very roughly, sums of non-independent random variables are to sums of independent random variables as
partial
sumsets
A+_{E}B
along a bipartite graph
E
are to full sumsets
A+B
.
Hence, it is natural to apply a variant of the entropic Balog–Szemerédi–Gowers lemma due to the fourth author
[
36
, Lemma 3.3]
(reproduced as
Lemma

A.2
here) to pass from the ‘almost independent’ variables
(T_{1},T_{2})
as above, for which
\mathbf{H}[T_{1}+T_{2}]-\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{H}[T_{1}]-\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{H}[T_{2}]
is small (or even negative), to variables
X_{1}^{\prime},X_{2}^{\prime}
with
{\operatorname{d}[X_{1}^{\prime};X_{2}^{\prime}]}=O(\eta k)
.
These variables
X^{\prime}_{1},X^{\prime}_{2}
, which are given to us by
Lemma

A.2
, now establish (
2.2
).

Examining the proof of
Lemma

A.2
carefully, the final choice of
X^{\prime}_{1}
that comes out of this argument is of the form

(3.10)

(X_{1}+X_{2}\,|\,\tilde{X}_{1}+X_{2}=t,X_{1}+\tilde{X}_{2}=s+t)

or a related quantity obtained by permuting the variables, and similarly for
X^{\prime}_{2}
(using the same
s,t
for both
X_{1}^{\prime}
and
X_{2}^{\prime}
).

Motivating examples

To understand the strategy, it is helpful to consider some cases of the form
X_{1}=U_{A_{1}}
,
X_{2}=U_{A_{2}}
for various sets
A_{1},A_{2}\subseteq\mathbf{F}_{2}^{n}
, and to discuss which choices of
X^{\prime}_{1},X^{\prime}_{2}
give the desired estimate (
2.2
). In this discussion is convenient to write
K\coloneqq e^{k}
.

Consider first the case in which
A_{1}=A_{2}=A
and
A
is a random subset of some subgroup
H\leqslant\mathbf{F}_{2}^{n}
with density
K^{-1}
in
H
.
Then (almost surely)
{\operatorname{d}[X_{1};X_{2}]}\approx k
.
In this case the choice (
3.2
), that is to say
X^{\prime}_{1}=X_{1}+\tilde{X}_{1}
and
X^{\prime}_{2}=X_{2}+\tilde{X}_{2}
, immediately establishes (
2.2
). Indeed both
X^{\prime}_{1}
and
X^{\prime}_{2}
are close to the uniform distribution
U_{H}
on
H
, so
{\operatorname{d}[X^{\prime}_{1};X^{\prime}_{2}]}\approx 0
and
{\operatorname{d}[X^{\prime}_{i};X_{i}]}\approx k/2
for
i=1,2
, and (
2.2
) follows, with room to spare, by the triangle inequality.

Consider next the case in which
A_{1}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}(x_{i}+H)
and
A_{2}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}(y_{i}+H)
, where
H<\mathbf{F}_{2}^{n}
is a subgroup and the
x_{i}+H
and
y_{i}+H
are linearly independent in
G/H
. Setting
m\coloneqq K
gives
{\operatorname{d}[X_{1};X_{2}]}\approx k
.
In this case one can obtain (
2.2
) with the choice (
3.3
), that is to say
X^{\prime}_{1}=(X_{1}|X_{1}+\tilde{X}_{2}=g)
and
X^{\prime}_{2}=(X_{2}|X_{2}+\tilde{X}_{1}=g^{\prime})
for any
g,g^{\prime}\in A_{1}+A_{2}
.
Indeed,
X^{\prime}_{1}
is the uniform distribution on
A_{1}\cap(A_{2}+g)
, which if
g\in x_{i}+y_{j}+H
is exactly the coset
x_{i}+H
of
H
(by linear independence). Similarly,
X^{\prime}_{2}
is uniform on a coset
y_{j}^{\prime}+H
, so
{\operatorname{d}[X^{\prime}_{1};X^{\prime}_{2}]}\approx 0
and again (
2.2
) follows.
(Using the fibres in (
3.4
) also gives a contradiction in this case, but the analysis is slightly more involved, since
A_{1}\cap(A_{1}+g)
is typically a union of two cosets of
H
.)

In both of the above examples, one of the choices (
3.1
), (
3.2
), (
3.3
) or (
3.4
) already gives the estimate (
2.2
) and so it is not necessary to proceed to the ‘endgame’.

Here is a third example, a sort of combination of the previous two, for which the choices (
3.1
), (
3.2
), (
3.3
) and (
3.4
) all fail to work.
Let
A_{1}^{\prime},A_{2}^{\prime}
be (independent) random subsets of density
1/m
of the sets
A_{1},A_{2}
from the previous example, where now
m=\sqrt{K}
.
One may then check that
X_{1}+\tilde{X}_{1},X_{2}+\tilde{X}_{2}
resemble the uniform distribution on the union of
\approx K/2
cosets of
H
, so
{\operatorname{d}[X^{\prime}_{1};X^{\prime}_{2}]}\approx k-O(1)
and we do not obtain (
2.2
) (if
k
is large) with the choice (
3.2
). The case of (
3.1
) is similar.

On the other hand, the variables
(X_{1}|X_{1}+\tilde{X}_{2}=g)
,
(X_{2}|X_{2}+\tilde{X}_{1}=g^{\prime})
will be uniform on
A_{1}\cap(A_{2}+g)
and
A_{2}\cap(A_{1}+g^{\prime})
respectively, and such sets will typically (when non-empty) resemble random subsets of a coset of
H
, of density
1/m^{2}\approx 1/K
. For these variables we also have
{\operatorname{d}[X^{\prime}_{1};X^{\prime}_{2}]}\approx k
, and so we do not obtain (
2.2
) with the choice (
3.3
). The case of (
3.4
) is broadly similar.

We therefore proceed to the endgame and consider the three variables in (
3.8
).
Since
X_{1}+X_{2}
is roughly uniform on
B\coloneqq\bigcup_{i,j}(x_{i}+y_{j}+H)
, a similar analysis to the second example shows that
(X_{1}+X_{2}|X_{1}+X_{2}+\tilde{X}_{1}+\tilde{X}_{2}=s)
is roughly uniform on
B\cap(B+s)
, which is typically a union of four cosets of
H
, and similarly for
(\tilde{X}_{1}+X_{2}|X_{1}+X_{2}+\tilde{X}_{1}+\tilde{X}_{2}=s)
.
Moreover, one can check that these two variables are ‘50% independent’ (in that knowing one of them narrows the choice of the other to two cosets of
H
).

If we then apply
Lemma

A.2
, we find variables
X^{\prime}_{1}
,
X^{\prime}_{2}
of the form given in (
3.10
).
In other words,
X^{\prime}_{1}
is a sum of two variables of the form
(X_{1}|X_{1}+\tilde{X}_{2}=a)
and
(X_{2}|X_{2}+\tilde{X}_{1}=b)
. Each will be uniform on a random subset of a coset of
H
, and adding them together will give something close to uniform on a coset of
H
. The case of
X^{\prime}_{2}
is similar, so we get
{\operatorname{d}[X^{\prime}_{1};X^{\prime}_{2}]}\approx 0
and (
2.2
) follows.
(Again, permuting the variables may give slighly different conclusions.)

Finally, we consider a further example over
G=\mathbf{Z}
instead of
\mathbf{F}_{2}^{n}
.
Take
X_{1},X_{2}
to have a ‘discrete Gaussian’ distribution
p(x)=C_{0}e^{-x^{2}/2r^{2}}
with some large ‘width’
r
and a normalizing constant
C_{0}
.
By
[
36
, Theorem 1.13]
, the distance
{\operatorname{d}[X_{1};X_{2}]}\approx\frac{1}{2}\log 2
is roughly minimal among random variables on
\mathbf{Z}
with large entropy, and hence
Proposition

2.1
cannot hold over
\mathbf{Z}
in the form stated.

Concretely, one can show that sums
X_{i}+\tilde{X}_{j}
as in (
3.1
), (
3.2
) are (roughly) discrete Gaussians of width
\sqrt{2}r
, and fibres
(X_{i}|X_{i}+\tilde{X}_{j})
as in (
3.3
), (
3.4
) are (shifted) discrete Gaussians of width about
r/\sqrt{2}
. Hence, all these have essentially the same value of
{\operatorname{d}[-;-]}
, and there is no way of ‘improving’ these variables with any of the choices considered in
Sections

5
and
6
.
One could also see directly that the conditional near-independence statements such as

\mathbf{I}[X_{1}+X_{2}:X_{1}+\tilde{X}_{1}|X_{1}+X_{2}+\tilde{X}_{1}+\tilde{X}_{2}]\approx 0

do indeed hold for discrete Gaussians.

In the above one-dimensional example, the entropic doubling constant is bounded, and so this example does not, by itself, present a strong obstruction to the entire approach. However, by considering discrete gaussian examples in a high-dimensional lattice
\mathbf{Z}^{d}
, the entropic doubling constant is now close to
\frac{d}{2}\log 2
, which is unbounded, and all the moves considered previously do not significantly reduce this constant.

Even though discrete Gaussians over
\mathbf{F}_{2}
do not exist, this example places significant constraints on what we can hope to prove in general. In particular, it shows that the endgame argument must ‘see’ the finite characteristic in an essential way.

The contrapositive formulation

The above discussion and the motivating examples which followed it were framed in terms of proving
Proposition

2.1
directly, by making various choices of
X^{\prime}_{1}
and
X^{\prime}_{2}
, and the authors found this most natural when thinking about the problem.

However, when it comes to recording the proofs, it turns out to be notationally simpler to argue in the contrapositive.
Thus, suppose henceforth that we have a pair
(X_{1},X_{2})
of
G
-valued random variables with
{\operatorname{d}[X_{1};X_{2}]}=k
for some
k
, and suppose that

(3.11)

\tau[X^{\prime}_{1};X^{\prime}_{2}]\geqslant\tau[X_{1};X_{2}]

for every pair of
G
-valued random variables
(X^{\prime}_{1},X^{\prime}_{2})
. Using the definition of
\tau
(see (
2.1
)) we may rewrite (
3.11
) as

(3.12)

{\operatorname{d}[X^{\prime}_{1};X^{\prime}_{2}]}\geqslant k-\eta\bigl({\operatorname{d}[X_{1}^{0};X^{\prime}_{1}]}-{\operatorname{d}[X_{1}^{0};X_{1}]}\bigr)-\eta\bigl({\operatorname{d}[X_{2}^{0};X^{\prime}_{2}]}-{\operatorname{d}[X_{2}^{0};X_{2}]}\bigr).

As in the discussion above, the idea now is to test (
3.12
) with various choices of
(X^{\prime}_{1},X^{\prime}_{2})
generated from
(X_{1},X_{2})
, the aim being to deduce from this that
k=0
.

Testing (
3.12
) with the choices (
3.1
), (
3.3
) will lead us to the conclusion that

(3.13)

I_{1}\coloneqq\mathbf{I}\big[X_{1}+X_{2}:\tilde{X}_{1}+X_{2}|X_{1}+X_{2}+\tilde{X}_{1}+\tilde{X}_{2}\big]\leqslant 2\eta k

(that is, a more precise version of (
3.5
)). The details of this deduction are provided in
Section

5
. Testing (
3.12
) with the choices (
3.2
), (
3.4
) leads to

(3.14)

I_{2}\coloneqq\mathbf{I}\big[X_{1}+X_{2}:X_{1}+\tilde{X}_{1}|X_{1}+X_{2}+\tilde{X}_{1}+\tilde{X}_{2}\big]\leqslant 2\eta k+\frac{2\eta(2\eta k-I_{1})}{1-\eta}

(a more precise version of (
3.6
)), and of course the same estimate holds for
I_{3}
, since in fact
I_{2}=I_{3}
. The details of this deduction are provided in
Section

6
.

Finally, armed with the knowledge that (
3.13
), (
3.14
) hold, we proceed to the endgame, using choices of
X^{\prime}_{1},X_{2}^{\prime}
arising from (
3.8
) and entropic Balog–Szemerédi–Gowers (i.e., of forms such as (
3.10
)) to finally show that
k=0
.
The details are given in
Section

7
.

The main reason for arguing by contradiction is that it greatly simplifies our discussions of conditioned random variables.
Note that (
3.12
) implies a conditioned version of itself:
for any random variables
(X_{1}^{\prime},Y_{1})
and
(X_{2}^{\prime},Y_{2})
we may apply (
3.12
) with
X^{\prime}_{1},X^{\prime}_{2}
replaced by each of the conditioned random variables
(X^{\prime}_{1}|Y_{1}=y_{1}),(X^{\prime}_{2}|Y_{2}=y_{2})
, obtaining

\displaystyle{\operatorname{d}[(X^{\prime}_{1}|Y_{1}=y_{1});(X^{\prime}_{2}|Y_{2}=y_{2})]}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\geqslant k\begin{aligned} &-\eta\bigl({\operatorname{d}[X_{1}^{0};(X^{\prime}_{1}|Y_{1}=y_{1})]}-{\operatorname{d}[X_{1}^{0};X_{1}]}\bigr)\\
&-\eta\bigl({\operatorname{d}[X_{2}^{0};(X^{\prime}_{2}|Y_{2}=y_{2})]}-{\operatorname{d}[X_{2}^{0};X_{2}]}\bigr).\end{aligned}

Multiplying by
p_{Y_{1}}(y_{1})p_{Y_{2}}(y_{2})
and summing, we have

\displaystyle{\operatorname{d}[X^{\prime}_{1}|Y_{1};X^{\prime}_{2}|Y_{2}]}\geqslant k

\displaystyle-\eta\bigl({\operatorname{d}[X_{1}^{0};X^{\prime}_{1}|Y_{1}]}-{\operatorname{d}[X_{1}^{0};X_{1}]}\bigr)

(3.15)

\displaystyle-\eta\bigl({\operatorname{d}[X_{2}^{0};X^{\prime}_{2}|Y_{2}]}-{\operatorname{d}[X_{2}^{0};X_{2}]}\bigr).

More discussion of the notion of conditional distance may be found at (
A.14
).

To make the same argument in the direct (non-contrapositive) direction we would pick some particular
(y_{1},y_{2})
such that

\displaystyle{\operatorname{d}[(X_{1}^{\prime}|Y_{1}=y_{1});(X_{2}^{\prime}|Y_{2}=y_{2})]}

\displaystyle+\eta\bigl({\operatorname{d}[X_{1}^{0};(X^{\prime}_{1}|Y_{1}=y_{1})]}-{\operatorname{d}[X_{1}^{0};X_{1}]}\bigr)

\displaystyle+\eta\bigl({\operatorname{d}[X_{2}^{0};(X^{\prime}_{2}|Y_{2}=y_{2})]}-{\operatorname{d}[X_{2}^{0};X_{2}]}\bigr)

is at most the (weighted) average value of the same quantity over all
(y_{1},y_{2})
.
If we have to perform many such steps in sequence, this becomes notationally very taxing.
However, this is purely a matter of preference: for instance, in
Lemma

7.2
and its proof we instead choose to argue directly.

Remarks on odd
p

We will handle the case
p
odd in our forthcoming paper

.
All of the above steps go through, except for the final contradiction in the ‘endgame’ where we used an argument specific to characteristic
2
.
To get a contradiction in characteristic
p
, it is necessary to run a variant of the argument with a
p
-partite ‘distance’ function
\operatorname{D}[X_{1};\dots;X_{p}]
replacing
{\operatorname{d}[X_{1};X_{2}]}
as the main term in the functional to be minimized.
This introduces additional notational complexity: for instance we now have an array
(X_{ij})_{i,j\in\mathbf{F}_{p}}
of random variables, and the fibring lemma must be generalized to this
p
-partite distance and applied
p-1

4.
Fibring lemma

Here we record the fibring lemma, that is to say
[
11
, Proposition 1.4]
, with an explicit error term. (The latter was mentioned in
[
11
]
, but only as a casual remark.)

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
\pi\colon H\to H^{\prime}
be a homomorphism between abelian groups and let
Z_{1},Z_{2}
be
H
-valued random variables. Then we have

{\operatorname{d}[Z_{1};Z_{2}]}\geqslant{\operatorname{d}[\pi(Z_{1});\pi(Z_{2})]}+{\operatorname{d}[Z_{1}|\pi(Z_{1});Z_{2}|\pi(Z_{2})]}

where the notation is as in (
A.14
).
Moreover, if
Z_{1},Z_{2}
are taken to be independent, then the difference between the two sides is

(4.1)

\mathbf{I}\big[Z_{1}-Z_{2}:(\pi(Z_{1}),\pi(Z_{2}))\;|\;\pi(Z_{1}-Z_{2})\big].

Proof.

Let
Z_{1},Z_{2}
be independent throughout. We have

\displaystyle{\operatorname{d}\bigl[Z_{1}|\pi(Z_{1});Z_{2}|\pi(Z_{2})\bigr]}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{H}\big[Z_{1}-Z_{2}|\pi(Z_{1}),\pi(Z_{2})\big]-\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{H}[Z_{1}|\pi(Z_{1})]-\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{H}[Z_{2}|\pi(Z_{2})]

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbf{H}\big[Z_{1}-Z_{2}|\pi(Z_{1}-Z_{2})\big]-\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{H}[Z_{1}|\pi(Z_{1})]-\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{H}[Z_{2}|\pi(Z_{2})]

\displaystyle={\operatorname{d}[Z_{1};Z_{2}]}-{\operatorname{d}[\pi(Z_{1});\pi(Z_{2})]}.

In the middle step, we used submodularity of entropy, and in the last step we used the fact that

\mathbf{H}\big[Z_{1}-Z_{2}|\pi(Z_{1}-Z_{2})\big]=\mathbf{H}[Z_{1}-Z_{2}]-\mathbf{H}[\pi(Z_{1}-Z_{2})]

(since
Z_{1}-Z_{2}
determines
\pi(Z_{1}-Z_{2})
) and that

\mathbf{H}[Z_{i}|\pi(Z_{i})]=\mathbf{H}[Z_{i}]-\mathbf{H}[\pi(Z_{i})]

(since
Z_{i}
determines
\pi(Z_{i})
).
This gives the claimed inequality. The difference between the two sides is precisely

\mathbf{H}\big[Z_{1}-Z_{2}|\pi(Z_{1}-Z_{2})\big]-\mathbf{H}\big[Z_{1}-Z_{2}|\pi(Z_{1}),\pi(Z_{2})\big].

To rewrite this in terms of (conditional) mutual information, we use the identity

\mathbf{H}[A|B]-\mathbf{H}[A|B,C]=\mathbf{I}[A:C|B],

taking

A\coloneqq Z_{1}-Z_{2}
,
B\coloneqq\pi(Z_{1}-Z_{2})
and
C\coloneqq(\pi(Z_{1}),\pi(Z_{2}))
, and noting that in this case
\mathbf{H}[A|B,C]=\mathbf{H}[A|C]
since
C
uniquely determines
B
.
This completes the proof.
∎

We extract the specific special case of this result that we will need in our arguments.

Corollary 4.2
.

Let
Y_{1},Y_{2},Y_{3}
and
Y_{4}
be independent random variables taking values in some abelian group
G
.
Then

\displaystyle{\operatorname{d}[Y_{1}-Y_{3};Y_{2}-Y_{4}]}+{\operatorname{d}\bigl[Y_{1}|Y_{1}-Y_{3};Y_{2}|Y_{2}-Y_{4}\bigr]}

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathbf{I}\big[Y_{1}-Y_{2}:Y_{2}-Y_{4}|Y_{1}-Y_{2}-Y_{3}+Y_{4}\big]={\operatorname{d}[Y_{1};Y_{2}]}+{\operatorname{d}[Y_{3};Y_{4}]}.

Proof.

We apply
Proposition

4.1
with
H\coloneqq G\times G
,
H^{\prime}\coloneqq G
,
\pi
the subtraction homomorphism
\pi(x,y)\coloneqq x-y
, and with the random variables
Z_{1}\coloneqq(Y_{1},Y_{3})
and
Z_{2}\coloneqq(Y_{2},Y_{4})
. Then by independence we easily calculate

{\operatorname{d}[Z_{1};Z_{2}]}={\operatorname{d}[Y_{1};Y_{2}]}+{\operatorname{d}[Y_{3};Y_{4}]}

while by definition

{\operatorname{d}[\pi(Z_{1});\pi(Z_{2})]}={\operatorname{d}[Y_{1}-Y_{3};Y_{2}-Y_{4}]}.

Furthermore,

{\operatorname{d}[Z_{1}|\pi(Z_{1});Z_{2}|\pi(Z_{2})]}={\operatorname{d}[Y_{1}|Y_{1}-Y_{3};Y_{2}|Y_{2}-Y_{4}]},

since
Z_{1}=(Y_{1},Y_{3})
and
Y_{1}
are linked by an invertible affine transformation once
\pi(Z_{1})=Y_{1}-Y_{3}
is fixed, and similarly for
Z_{2}
and
Y_{2}
.
Finally, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{I}\big[Z_{1}-Z_{2}:(\pi(Z_{1}),\pi(Z_{2}))\,|\,\pi(Z_{1})+\pi(Z_{2})\big]

\displaystyle\ =\mathbf{I}\big[(Y_{1}-Y_{2},Y_{3}-Y_{4}):(Y_{1}-Y_{3},Y_{2}-Y_{4})\,|\,Y_{1}-Y_{2}-Y_{3}+Y_{4}\big]

\displaystyle\ =\mathbf{I}\big[Y_{1}-Y_{2}:Y_{2}-Y_{4}\,|\,Y_{1}-Y_{2}-Y_{3}+Y_{4}\big]

where in the last line we used the fact that
(Y_{1}-Y_{2},Y_{1}-Y_{2}-Y_{3}+Y_{4})
uniquely determine
Y_{3}-Y_{4}
and similarly

(Y_{2}-Y_{4},Y_{1}-Y_{2}-Y_{3}+Y_{4})
uniquely determine
Y_{1}-Y_{3}
.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

Of course, in our main characteristic
2
setting, the minus signs in
Propositions

4.1
and
4.2
may be replaced with plus signs.

First estimate

Recall that in this and subsequent sections we are working on the assumption that (

X_{1}
and
X_{2},\tilde{X}_{2}

In this section we establish the upper bound (

I_{1}\coloneqq\mathbf{I}\big[X_{1}+X_{2}:\tilde{X}_{1}+X_{2}|X_{1}+X_{2}+\tilde{X}_{1}+\tilde{X}_{2}\big]\leqslant 2\eta k.

We apply
Corollary

4.2
(and Remark

) with the choice

(Y_{1},Y_{2},Y_{3},Y_{4})\coloneqq(X_{1},X_{2},\tilde{X}_{2},\tilde{X}_{1}).

Appendix A
Entropy and additive combinatorics

In this appendix we record some standard inequalities regarding Shannon entropy, as well as the standard ‘entropic Ruzsa calculus’ concerning entropies of sums of random variables.

First we remark that if
X
takes values in a set
S
then, by Jensen’s inequality,

(A.1)

\mathbf{H}[X]\leqslant\log|S|.

Also, denoting by
p_{X}
the density function of
X
,

\mathbf{H}[X]=\sum_{x}p_{X}(x)\log\frac{1}{p_{X}(x)}\geqslant\min_{x:p_{X}(x)>0}\log\frac{1}{p_{X}(x)},

and therefore

(A.2)

\max_{x}p_{X}(x)\geqslant e^{-\mathbf{H}[X]}.

Given a pair
(X,Y)
of random variables, the
conditional entropy

\mathbf{H}[X|Y]
is defined by the formula

\mathbf{H}[X|Y]\coloneqq\sum_{y}p_{Y}(y)\mathbf{H}[X|Y=y]

where
y
ranges over the support of
p_{Y}
, and
X|Y=y
denotes the random variable
X
conditioned on the event
Y=y
. We have the fundamental
chain rule

(A.3)

\mathbf{H}[X,Y]=\mathbf{H}[X|Y]+\mathbf{H}[Y].

Here we abbreviate
\mathbf{H}[(X,Y)]
as
\mathbf{H}[X,Y]
, and will make similar abbreviations regarding other information-theoretic quantities in this paper without further comment; for instance,
\mathbf{H}[(X,Y)|(Z,W)]
becomes
\mathbf{H}[X,Y|Z,W]
. Note that (
A.3
) implies a conditional generalization

\mathbf{H}[X,Y|Z]=\mathbf{H}[X|Y,Z]+\mathbf{H}[Y|Z].

for all random variables
X,Y,Z
.

The
mutual information

\mathbf{I}[X:Y]
is defined by the formula

\begin{split}\mathbf{I}[X:Y]&=\mathbf{H}[X]+\mathbf{H}[Y]-\mathbf{H}[X,Y]\\
&=\mathbf{H}[X]-\mathbf{H}[X|Y]\\
&=\mathbf{H}[Y]-\mathbf{H}[Y|X],\end{split}

and is non-negative by a standard application of Jensen’s inequality, vanishing precisely when
X,Y
are independent; in particular

(A.4)

\mathbf{H}[X,Y]=\mathbf{H}[X]+\mathbf{H}[Y]

if and only if
X,Y
are independent, and

(A.5)

\mathbf{H}[X|Y]\leqslant\mathbf{H}[X]

always.

Suppose now that
(X,Y,Z)
is a triple of random variables. Applying (
A.5
) to
(X|Z=z)
and summing over
z
(weighted by
p_{Z}(z)
) gives

(A.6)

\mathbf{H}[X|Y,Z]\leqslant\mathbf{H}[X|Z],

which is known as
submodularity
. It may equivalently be written as

(A.7)

\mathbf{H}[X,Y,Z]+\mathbf{H}[Z]\leqslant\mathbf{H}[X,Z]+\mathbf{H}[Y,Z].

The
conditional mutual information

\mathbf{I}[X:Y|Z]
is defined by

\mathbf{I}[X:Y|Z]\coloneqq\sum_{z}p_{Z}(z)\mathbf{I}[(X|Z=z):(Y|Z=z)].

Submodularity is equivalent to the statement that

(A.8)

\mathbf{I}[X:Y|Z]\geqslant 0,

since

(A.9)

\mathbf{I}[X:Y|Z]=\mathbf{H}[X,Z]+\mathbf{H}[Y,Z]-\mathbf{H}[X,Y,Z]-\mathbf{H}[Z].

Equality occurs in (
A.8
) (and hence in (
A.7
)) if and only if
X,Y
are conditionally independent relative to
Z
.

G
-valued random variables

We turn now to additional properties enjoyed by random variables taking values in an additive group
G
.

Whenever
X,Y
are
G
-valued random variables, we have
that

\mathbf{H}[X\pm Y]\geqslant\mathbf{H}[X\pm Y|Y]=\mathbf{H}[X|Y]=\mathbf{H}[X]-\mathbf{I}[X:Y]

and similarly with the roles of
X,Y
reversed, thus

(A.10)

\max(\mathbf{H}[X],\mathbf{H}[Y])-\mathbf{I}[X:Y]\leqslant\mathbf{H}[X\pm Y].

We note also the conditional variant of (
A.10
), namely

\max(\mathbf{H}[X|Z],\mathbf{H}[Y|Z])-\mathbf{I}[X:Y|Z]\leqslant\mathbf{H}[X\pm Y|Z],

which follows from (
A.10
) by conditioning on
Z=z
and summing over
z
(weighted by
p_{Z}(z)
).

A particular consequence of (
A.10
) is that

(A.11)

\max(\mathbf{H}[X],\mathbf{H}[Y])\leqslant\mathbf{H}[X\pm Y]

when
X,Y
are independent.

Continuing to suppose that
X,Y
are independent, recall the definition (
1.1
) of the Ruzsa distance
{\operatorname{d}[X;Y]}
which, since
X
and
Y
are independent, is that

{\operatorname{d}[X;Y]}=\mathbf{H}[X-Y]-\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{H}[X]-\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{H}[Y].

Comparing this with (
A.11
) we see that

(A.12)

|\mathbf{H}[X]-\mathbf{H}[Y]|\leqslant 2{\operatorname{d}[X;Y]}.

We may also deduce that

\mathbf{H}[X-Y]-\mathbf{H}[X],\mathbf{H}[X-Y]-\mathbf{H}[Y]\leqslant 2{\operatorname{d}[X;Y]}.

The most important property of the Ruzsa distance is the (Ruzsa) triangle inequality

{\operatorname{d}[X;Y]}\leqslant{\operatorname{d}[X;Z]}+{\operatorname{d}[Z;Y]}.

This was shown in
[
30
]
and
[
36
, (16)]
; we recall a proof for completeness.
This is equivalent to establishing

(A.13)

\mathbf{H}[X-Y]\leqslant\mathbf{H}[X-Z]+\mathbf{H}[Z-Y]-\mathbf{H}[Z]

whenever
X,Y,Z
are independent. To prove this, apply (
A.8
) with a change of variables to get
\mathbf{I}[X-Z:Y|X-Y]\geqslant 0
which, when written out in full, gives

\mathbf{H}[X-Z,X-Y]+\mathbf{H}[Y,X-Y]\geqslant\mathbf{H}[X-Z,Y,X-Y]+\mathbf{H}[X-Y].

Using

\mathbf{H}[X-Z,X-Y]=\mathbf{H}[X-Z,Y-Z]\leqslant\mathbf{H}[X-Z]+\mathbf{H}[Y-Z],

\mathbf{H}[Y,X-Y]=\mathbf{H}[X,Y],

and

\mathbf{H}[X-Z,Y,X-Y]=\mathbf{H}[X,Y,Z]=\mathbf{H}[X,Y]+\mathbf{H}[Z],

and rearranging, we indeed obtain (
A.13
). (As observed in
[
11
]
, we do not in fact use the independence of
X
and
Y
here.)

We will also need conditional variants of the distance.
If
(X,Z)
and
(Y,W)
are random variables (where
X
and
Y
are
G
-valued) we define

(A.14)

{\operatorname{d}[X|Z;Y|W]}\coloneqq\sum_{z,w}p_{Z}(z)p_{W}(w){\operatorname{d}[(X|Z=z);(Y|W=w)]}.

Alternatively, if
(X^{\prime},Z^{\prime}),(Y^{\prime},W^{\prime})
are independent copies of the variables
(X,Z)
,
(Y,W)
,

(A.15)

{\operatorname{d}[X|Z;Y|W]}=\mathbf{H}[X^{\prime}-Y^{\prime}|Z^{\prime},W^{\prime}]-\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{H}[X^{\prime}|Z^{\prime}]-\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{H}[Y^{\prime}|W^{\prime}].

To conclude this appendix we give the proofs of two results from the literature which were used in the main text. The first is an inequality of Madiman
[
22
, Theorem I]
, which is a more general form of an inequality of Kaimanovich and Vershik
[
13
, Proposition 1.3]
.

Lemma A.1
.

Suppose that
X,Y,Z
are independent random variables taking values in an abelian group. Then

\mathbf{H}[X+Y+Z]-\mathbf{H}[X+Y]\leqslant\mathbf{H}[Y+Z]-\mathbf{H}[Y].

Proof.

By (
A.9
) we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{I}[X:Z|X+Y+Z]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{H}[X,X+Y+Z]+\mathbf{H}[Z,X+Y+Z]

\displaystyle\quad-\mathbf{H}[X,Z,X+Y+Z]-\mathbf{H}[X+Y+Z].

However, using (
A.4
) three times we have
\mathbf{H}[X,X+Y+Z]=\mathbf{H}[X,Y+Z]=\mathbf{H}[X]+\mathbf{H}[Y+Z]
,
\mathbf{H}[Z,X+Y+Z]=\mathbf{H}[Z,X+Y]=\mathbf{H}[Z]+\mathbf{H}[X+Y]
and
\mathbf{H}[X,Z,X+Y+Z]=\mathbf{H}[X,Y,Z]=\mathbf{H}[X]+\mathbf{H}[Y]+\mathbf{H}[Z]
.

After a short calculation, we see that the claimed inequality is equivalent to the assertion that
\mathbf{I}[X:Z|X+Y+Z]\geqslant 0
, which of course is an instance of (
A.8
).
∎

The next lemma is not quite in the literature but is very closely related to the entropic version of the Balog–Szemerédi–Gowers lemma due to the fourth author
[
36
, Lemma 3.3]
. Here we provide slightly better constants and a slightly simpler proof.

Lemma A.2
.

Let
(A,B)
be a
G^{2}
-valued random variable, and set
Z\coloneqq A+B
.
Then

(A.16)

\sum_{z}p_{Z}(z){\operatorname{d}[(A|Z=z);(B|Z=z)]}\leqslant 3\mathbf{I}[A:B]+2\mathbf{H}[Z]-\mathbf{H}[A]-\mathbf{H}[B].

We stress that the quantity
2\mathbf{H}[Z]-\mathbf{H}[A]-\mathbf{H}[B]
is
not
the same as
2{\operatorname{d}[A;B]}
, because
(A,B)
are given a joint distribution which may not be independent. In particular,
\mathbf{H}[Z]=\mathbf{H}[A+B]
may not match the entropy of a sum of independent copies of
A
and
B
.

Proof.

In the proof we will need the notion of
conditionally independent trials
of a pair of random variables
(X,Y)
(not necessarily independent). We say that
X_{1},X_{2}
are conditionally independent trials of
X
relative to
Y
by declaring
(X_{1}|Y=y)
and
(X_{2}|Y=y)
to be independent copies of
(X|Y=y)
for all
y
in the range of
Y
.
We then have

\mathbf{H}[(X_{1}|Y=y),(X_{2}|Y=y)]=2\mathbf{H}[X|Y=y]

for all
y
, which upon summing over
y
(weighted by
p_{Y}(y)
) gives

\mathbf{H}[X_{1},X_{2}|Y]=2\mathbf{H}[X|Y]

and hence

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}[X_{1},X_{2},Y]=\mathbf{H}[X_{1},X_{2}|Y]+\mathbf{H}[Y]

\displaystyle=2\mathbf{H}[X|Y]+\mathbf{H}[Y]

(A.17)

\displaystyle=2\mathbf{H}[X,Y]-\mathbf{H}[Y].

Note also that the marginal distributions of
(X_{1},Y)
and
(X_{2},Y)
each match the original distribution
(X,Y)
.

Turning to the proof of
Lemma

A.2
itself, let
(A_{1},B_{1})
and
(A_{2},B_{2})
be conditionally independent trials of
(A,B)
relative to
Z
, thus
(A_{1},B_{1})
and
(A_{2},B_{2})
are coupled through the random variable

, which by abuse of notation we shall also call

.

Observe that the left-hand side of (
A.16
) is

(A.18)

\mathbf{H}[A_{1}-B_{2}|Z]-\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{H}[A_{1}|Z]-\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{H}[B_{2}|Z].

since, crucially,
(A_{1}|Z=z)
and
(B_{2}|Z=z)
are independent for all
z
.

Applying submodularity (
A.7
) gives

\begin{split}&\mathbf{H}[A_{1}-B_{2}]+\mathbf{H}[A_{1}-B_{2},A_{1},B_{1}]\\
&\qquad\leqslant\mathbf{H}[A_{1}-B_{2},A_{1}]+\mathbf{H}[A_{1}-B_{2},B_{1}].\end{split}

We estimate the second, third and fourth terms appearing here.
First note that, by (
A.17
) (noting that the tuple
(A_{1}-B_{2},A_{1},B_{1})
determines the tuple
(A_{1},A_{2},B_{1},B_{2})
since

)

(A.20)

\mathbf{H}[A_{1}-B_{2},A_{1},B_{1}]=\mathbf{H}[A_{1},B_{1},A_{2},B_{2}]=2\mathbf{H}[A,B]-\mathbf{H}[Z].

Next observe that

(A.21)

\mathbf{H}[A_{1}-B_{2},A_{1}]=\mathbf{H}[A_{1},B_{2}]\leqslant\mathbf{H}[A]+\mathbf{H}[B].

Finally, we have

(A.22)

\mathbf{H}[A_{1}-B_{2},B_{1}]=\mathbf{H}[A_{2}-B_{1},B_{1}]=\mathbf{H}[A_{2},B_{1}]\leqslant\mathbf{H}[A]-\mathbf{H}[B].

Substituting (

and so by (

Since

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}[A_{1}|Z]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{H}[A_{1},A_{1}+B_{1}]-\mathbf{H}[Z]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{H}[A,B]-\mathbf{H}[Z]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{H}[A]+\mathbf{H}[B]-\mathbf{I}[A:B]-\mathbf{H}[Z]

and similarly for

, we see that (
