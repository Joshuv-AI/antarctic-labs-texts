Operational Methods Applied to the Spherical Mean and X-Ray Transform
Julius Lehmann

Abstract
We use the framework of operational calculus to derive the operators associated with the spherical mean and related means of a function in n n -dimensional space. These operators are then used to simplify the treatment and discussion of the means, i. e., to determine their power series expansion, to find the series solution to the corresponding inversion problem, to derive the associated differential equations that the means satisfy, or give meaning to fractional spherical means. Finally, we use the framework to derive the inversion formula of the X-ray transform in an operational manner.



1
Spherical Mean and Its Generalizations

The general solution of the wave equation in odd space dimensions is given by Kirchhoff’s formula and derived by using spherical means to reduce the higher dimensional wave equation to the wave equation in
\mathbb{R}
, and then using d’Alembert’s famous formula to solve the reduced system
[
11
]
. Spherical means also appear in the theory of harmonic functions as one of their defining properties, i. e. a function is harmonic if and only if it is its own spherical mean
[
18
]
. The spherical mean of a function
f
in
n
dimensions over a sphere of radius
r
is defined by the integral
[
12
]

\bar{f}_{S}(r,\vec{x})=\frac{1}{S_{n-1}}\int_{\mathclap{\lVert\vec{u}\rVert=1}}\mathrm{d}^{n}u\,f(\vec{x}+r\vec{u}),

(1)

where
S_{n-1}=2\pi^{\frac{n}{2}}/\Gamma(\frac{n}{2})
is the surface area of the
n
-dimensional unit sphere. Using this definition, we can already derive many properties of the spherical mean, i. e. the differential equation it satisfies, approximations for small radii
r
, or the inverse operation. However, imagine we had an operator that we could apply to the function
f(\vec{x})
to find its spherical mean, then its properties would follow from the properties of the operator. In the following, we will utilize operational calculus
[
19
]
to find the associated operator of the spherical mean of a function and use this operator to derive some of the properties of Eq. (
1
). In a nutshell, operational calculus is a method by which we treat operators algebraically and give meaning to functions of operators. Although its origin dates back to the foundations of calculus with Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, the approach was primarily developed during the 19
{}^{\text{th}}
century by British and Irish mathematicians such as Sylvester, Boole, Glaisher, Crofton, and Blizard
[
7
,
9
]
, and only fully refined by Oliver Heaviside in the 1890s
[
17
]
. A well-known example of this approach is the operator we will employ in this paper, which, as suggested by the form of the integral in Eq. (
1
), is the shift operator
[
14
]
and given in one dimension by the exponential of the derivative operator
\hat{D}_{x}
:

\mathrm{e}^{t\hat{D}_{x}}f(x)=f(x+t).

(2)

This expression can be easily generalized to higher dimensions by using the usual properties of the exponential function and the commutativity of the various derivative operators, giving

\mathrm{e}^{\vec{t}\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{\vec{x}}}f(\vec{x})=f(\vec{x}+\vec{t}\,).

(3)

As mentioned above, this relation can be used to find a compact (though somewhat unusual) expression for the spherical mean as an operator. We find by directly applying Eq. (
3
) to equation (
1
):

\bar{f}_{S}(r,\vec{x})=\frac{1}{S_{n-1}}\int_{\mathclap{\lVert\vec{u}\rVert=1}}\mathrm{d}^{n}u\,f(\vec{x}+r\vec{u})=\frac{1}{S_{n-1}}\int_{\mathclap{\lVert\vec{u}\rVert=1}}\mathrm{d}^{n}u\,\mathrm{e}^{r\vec{u}\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{\vec{x}}}f(\vec{x}).

(4)

The integral on the right-hand side is independent of
f(\vec{x})
and can be calculated using standard procedures
[
8
]
, yielding the operator expression

\bar{f}_{S}(r,\vec{x})=\Gamma\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)\bigg(\frac{2}{r\lVert\vec{\nabla}_{\vec{x}}\rVert}\bigg)^{\frac{n}{2}-1}I_{\frac{n}{2}-1}(r\lVert\vec{\nabla}_{\vec{x}}\rVert)f(\vec{x}),

(5)

where
I_{\alpha}(x)
is the modified Bessel function of the first kind
[
1
]
. In fact, we can write this expression even more compact by using generalized hypergeometric functions
[
2
]
,

{}_{p}F_{q}(a_{1},\dots,a_{p};b_{1},\dots,b_{q};x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\prod_{i=1}^{p}\frac{\Gamma(a_{i}+n)}{\Gamma(a_{i})}\prod_{j=1}^{q}\frac{\Gamma(b_{j})}{\Gamma(b_{j}+n)}\frac{x^{n}}{n!},

(6)

or more precisely, the confluent hypergeometric limit function, as

\bar{f}_{S}(r,\vec{x})={}_{0}F_{1}\Big(;\frac{n}{2};\frac{r^{2}\mathop{{}\bigtriangleup}\nolimits\!_{\vec{x}}}{4}\Big)f(\vec{x}).

(7)

This operator is a function of the Laplacian
\mathop{{}\bigtriangleup}\nolimits\!_{\vec{x}}
acting on the function
f(\vec{x})
and contains all relevant information about the spherical mean of that function. To make sense of the operator, we interpret it in terms of the power series of
{}_{0}F_{1}(;\frac{n}{2};x)
at
x=0
:

{}_{0}F_{1}\Big(;\frac{n}{2};\frac{r^{2}\mathop{{}\bigtriangleup}\nolimits\!_{\vec{x}}}{4}\Big)f(\vec{x})=\Big(1+\frac{r^{2}}{2n}\mathop{{}\bigtriangleup}\nolimits\!_{\vec{x}}+\frac{r^{4}}{8n(n+2)}\mathop{{}\bigtriangleup}\nolimits\!_{\vec{x}}^{2}+\dotsi\Big)f(\vec{x}).

(8)

This already gives us a way to efficiently approximate the spherical mean for small radii
r
. The same expression was found using standard methods and Taylor’s theorem in Ref.
[
20
]
, confirming this result is correct. Before we continue, let us emphasize that the operational methods are not rigorous and therefore need to be verified by conventional techniques. However, as shown above, they provide valuable insights and “shortcuts” for finding analytical results that are otherwise more cumbersome to derive. With the help of the spherical mean operator, it is an easy task to write down the inverse operation of spherical averaging by simply diving by
{}_{0}F_{1}(;\frac{n}{2};\frac{r^{2}\mathop{{}\bigtriangleup}\nolimits\!_{\vec{x}}}{4})
on both sides:

f(\vec{x})=\Big[{}_{0}F_{1}\Big(;\frac{n}{2};\frac{r^{2}\mathop{{}\bigtriangleup}\nolimits\!_{\vec{x}}}{4}\Big)\Big]^{-1}\bar{f}_{S}(r,\vec{x}).

(9)

Again, we interpret the operator by its power series and find the series solution to the inversion problem as

f(\vec{x})=\Big(1-\frac{r^{2}}{2n}\mathop{{}\bigtriangleup}\nolimits\!_{\vec{x}}+\frac{(n+4)r^{4}}{8n^{2}(n+2)}\mathop{{}\bigtriangleup}\nolimits\!_{\vec{x}}^{2}+\dotsi\Big)\bar{f}_{S}(r,\vec{x}).

(10)

Furthermore, successive spherical means of the same function can be found by applying the operator (
7
) multiple times to the function. Looking at the
m
-th iterated spherical mean, we find the power series expansion for small
r
:

\Big[{}_{0}F_{1}\Big(;\frac{n}{2};\frac{r^{2}\mathop{{}\bigtriangleup}\nolimits\!_{\vec{x}}}{4}\Big)\Big]^{m}f(\vec{x})=\Big(1+\frac{mr^{2}}{2n}\mathop{{}\bigtriangleup}\nolimits\!_{\vec{x}}+\frac{m(m(n+2)-2)r^{4}}{8n^{2}(n+2)}\mathop{{}\bigtriangleup}\nolimits\!_{\vec{x}}^{2}+\dotsi\Big)f(\vec{x}).

(11)

This formula also is valid for
m<0
and therefore generalizes Eq. (
9
). By continuation, this expression also provides a way to compute and give meaning to fractional spherical means, such as the “
\frac{1}{2}
-th spherical mean” of
f(\vec{x})
. From the operator form it is also clear that the successive spherical means of different radii
r_{1},r_{2}
of a function commute, i. e.

{}_{0}F_{1}\Big(;\frac{n}{2};\frac{r_{1}^{2}\mathop{{}\bigtriangleup}\nolimits\!_{\vec{x}}}{4}\Big){}_{0}F_{1}\Big(;\frac{n}{2};\frac{r_{2}^{2}\mathop{{}\bigtriangleup}\nolimits\!_{\vec{x}}}{4}\Big)f(\vec{x})={}_{0}F_{1}\Big(;\frac{n}{2};\frac{r_{2}^{2}\mathop{{}\bigtriangleup}\nolimits\!_{\vec{x}}}{4}\Big){}_{0}F_{1}\Big(;\frac{n}{2};\frac{r_{1}^{2}\mathop{{}\bigtriangleup}\nolimits\!_{\vec{x}}}{4}\Big)f(\vec{x}),

(12)

since the operators commute. Nonetheless, spherical means are not additive in the sense that
{}_{0}F_{1}\big(;\frac{n}{2};\frac{r_{2}^{2}\mathop{{}\bigtriangleup}\nolimits\!_{\vec{x}}}{4}\big)\bar{f}(r_{1},\vec{x})\neq\bar{f}(r_{1}+r_{2},\vec{x})
. However, they admit an addition formula in terms of the Gaussian hypergeometric function
{}_{2}F_{1}
:
[
5
]

\displaystyle{}_{0}F_{1}\Big(;\frac{n}{2};\frac{r_{1}^{2}\mathop{{}\bigtriangleup}\nolimits\!_{\vec{x}}}{4}\Big)

\displaystyle{}_{0}F_{1}\Big(;\frac{n}{2};\frac{r_{2}^{2}\mathop{{}\bigtriangleup}\nolimits\!_{\vec{x}}}{4}\Big)f(\vec{x})

(13)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{r_{1}^{2k}\Gamma(\frac{n}{2})}{4^{k}k!\Gamma(k+\frac{n}{2})}{}_{2}F_{1}\Big(1-\frac{n}{2}-k,-k;\frac{n}{2};\Big(\frac{r_{2}}{r_{1}}\Big)^{2}\Big)\mathop{{}\bigtriangleup}\nolimits\!_{\vec{x}}^{k}f(\vec{x}).

Note that for
r_{1}=r_{2}=r
, two consecutive spherical means of equal radius, we get the rather simple hypergeometric expression
[
4
]

\Big[{}_{0}F_{1}\Big(;\frac{n}{2};\frac{r^{2}\mathop{{}\bigtriangleup}\nolimits\!_{\vec{x}}}{4}\Big)\Big]^{2}f(\vec{x})={}_{1}F_{2}\Big(\frac{n-1}{2};\frac{n}{2},n-1;r^{2}\mathop{{}\bigtriangleup}\nolimits\!_{\vec{x}}\Big)f(\vec{x}).

(14)

) (solid red line) with the spherical mean by the operator Eq. (

) (dashed red line) for the same radius of an arbitrary function
f(x)
. The mean of the operator shows very good agreement with the exact expression from the integral definition.

and

.

For the next paragraph, let us remember another property of harmonic functions. These functions, which are their own spherical mean, are known to satisfy Laplace’s equation:

\mathop{{}\bigtriangleup}\nolimits\!_{\vec{x}}h(\vec{x})=0,\quad\vec{x}\in\Omega,

(15)

for appropriate boundary conditions on
\Omega

[
3
]
. While this is not true for the spherical mean of a function in general, it does satisfy a modified version of Laplace’s equation where we include additional source terms. To derive the corresponding differential equation, recall that the confluent hypergeometric limit functions
w\equiv{}_{0}F_{1}(;n;x)
satisfy the following differential equation
[
2
]
:

x\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}w}{\mathrm{d}{x}^{2}}+n\frac{\mathrm{d}w}{\mathrm{d}x}-w=0.

(16)

To bring this into the form of our operator, we perform the change of variables
x\to ax^{2}
and write
s\equiv ax^{2}
, which gives us

s\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}w(s)}{\mathrm{d}{s}^{2}}+n\frac{\mathrm{d}w(s)}{\mathrm{d}s}-w(s)=0.

(17)

Applying the chain rule to the differential operators, we continue to find for the first derivative:

\frac{\mathrm{d}w(ax^{2})}{\mathrm{d}(ax^{2})}=\frac{1}{2ax}\frac{\mathrm{d}w(ax^{2})}{\mathrm{d}x},

(18)

and for the second derivative:

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}(ax^{2})}\frac{\mathrm{d}w(ax^{2})}{\mathrm{d}(ax^{2})}=\frac{1}{2ax}\left(\frac{1}{2ax}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}w(ax^{2})}{\mathrm{d}{x}^{2}}-\frac{1}{2ax^{2}}\frac{\mathrm{d}w(ax^{2})}{\mathrm{d}x}\right).

(19)

Therefore, the differential equation (
16
) under the change of variables becomes

\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}w(ax^{2})}{\mathrm{d}{x}^{2}}+\frac{2n-1}{x}\frac{\mathrm{d}w(ax^{2})}{\mathrm{d}x}-4aw(ax^{2})=0,

(20)

and by treating the Laplacian as a simple number, we can identify
a\to\mathop{{}\bigtriangleup}\nolimits\!_{\vec{x}}/4
, which implies that the spherical mean operator “satisfies” the partial differential equation

\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial r^{2}}{}_{0}F_{1}\Big(;\frac{n}{2};\frac{r^{2}\mathop{{}\bigtriangleup}\nolimits\!_{\vec{x}}}{4}\Big)+\frac{n-1}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}{}_{0}F_{1}\Big(;\frac{n}{2};\frac{r^{2}\mathop{{}\bigtriangleup}\nolimits\!_{\vec{x}}}{4}\Big)=\mathop{{}\bigtriangleup}\nolimits\!_{\vec{x}}{}_{0}F_{1}\Big(;\frac{n}{2};\frac{r^{2}\mathop{{}\bigtriangleup}\nolimits\!_{\vec{x}}}{4}\Big).

(21)

By applying this equation from the left to the function
f(\vec{x})
and combining the operator and the function to the spherical mean, we obtain the well-known Euler-Poisson-Darboux equation
[
10
,
23
]
:

\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial r^{2}}\bar{f}_{S}(r,\vec{x})+\frac{n-1}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\bar{f}_{S}(r,\vec{x})=\mathop{{}\bigtriangleup}\nolimits\!_{\vec{x}}\bar{f}_{S}(r,\vec{x}).

(22)

Now that we have seen the effectiveness and use of operational calculus and the operator associated with an integral transformation, let us consider other examples. As a matter of fact, the spherical mean can be seen as a special member of a broader class of averaging operations on a function within a spherical region of space:

\bar{f}_{K}(r,\vec{x})=\frac{1}{C_{K}}\int_{\mathclap{\lVert\vec{u}\rVert\leq 1}}\mathrm{d}^{n}u\,K(\vec{u})f(\vec{x}+r\vec{u}),

(23)

where
K(\vec{u})
is the kernel or weight of the mean, which we assume to be radially symmetric, and
C_{K}
,

C_{K}=\int_{\mathclap{\lVert\vec{u}\rVert\leq 1}}\mathrm{d}^{n}u\,K(\vec{u})=S_{n-1}\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}u\!\mathop{}u^{n-1}K(u),

(24)

is a normalization constant such that the average of the identity returns the identity. In this notation, the usual spherical mean is given by the choice of kernel
K(\vec{u})=\delta(\lVert\vec{u}\rVert-1)
. To find the associated operator of Eq. (
23
), recall the expression for the Fourier transform of radially symmetric functions
[
13
]
:

\mathcal{F}\{f(\lVert\vec{x}\rVert)\}(k)=\frac{(2\pi)^{\frac{n}{2}}}{k^{\frac{n}{2}-1}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}u\!\mathop{}J_{\frac{n}{2}-1}(ku)u^{\frac{n}{2}}f(u),

(25)

using the shift operator (
3
) on our integral,

\bar{f}_{K}(r,\vec{x})=\frac{1}{C_{K}}\int_{\mathclap{\lVert\vec{u}\rVert\leq 1}}\mathrm{d}^{n}u\,K(\vec{u})f(\vec{x}+r\vec{u})=\frac{1}{C_{K}}\int_{\mathclap{\lVert\vec{u}\rVert\leq 1}}\mathrm{d}^{n}u\,K(\vec{u})\mathrm{e}^{r\vec{u}\cdot\vec{\nabla}_{\vec{x}}}f(\vec{x}),

(26)

the right-hand side is in essence the Fourier transform of
K(\lVert\vec{u}\rVert)\Theta(1-\lVert\vec{u}\rVert)
after a “Wick rotation” in frequency space
k\to\mathrm{i}r
. Inserting the expression for the Fourier transform for the integral, we find

\bar{f}_{K}(r,\vec{x})=\frac{(2\pi)^{\frac{n}{2}}}{C_{K}(r\lVert\vec{\nabla}_{\vec{x}}\rVert)^{\frac{n}{2}-1}}\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}u\!\mathop{}I_{\frac{n}{2}-1}(ru\lVert\vec{\nabla}_{\vec{x}}\rVert)K(u)u^{\frac{n}{2}}f(\vec{x}),

(27)

from which we can further recognize the term
(r\lVert\vec{\nabla}_{\vec{x}}\rVert)^{-\frac{n}{2}+1}I_{\frac{n}{2}-1}(ru\lVert\vec{\nabla}_{\vec{x}}\rVert)
from the ordinary spherical mean. Therefore, the integral could be equivalently written in terms of the confluent hypergeometric limit function:

\bar{f}_{K}(r,\vec{x})=\frac{2\pi^{\frac{n}{2}}}{C_{K}\Gamma(\frac{n}{2})}\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}u\!\mathop{}K(u)u^{n-1}{}_{0}F_{1}\Big(;\frac{n}{2};\frac{(ru)^{2}\mathop{{}\bigtriangleup}\nolimits\!_{\vec{x}}}{4}\Big)f(\vec{x})

(28)

and so it is easy to see that the generalized spherical mean for some kernel
K(u)
is just a weighted integral of the spherical mean of
f(\vec{x})
:

\bar{f}_{K}(r,\vec{x})=\frac{2\pi^{\frac{n}{2}}}{C_{K}\Gamma(\frac{n}{2})}\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}u\!\mathop{}K(u)u^{n-1}\bar{f}_{S}(ru,\vec{x}).

(29)

As a first simple example, consider the constant kernel
K(\vec{u})=1
; this gives the average of a function over the entire
n
-ball instead of just its surface. By equation (
27
) we find the following operator:

\bar{f}_{B}(r,\vec{x})={}_{0}F_{1}\Big(;\frac{n}{2}+1;\frac{r^{2}\mathop{{}\bigtriangleup}\nolimits\!_{\vec{x}}}{4}\Big)f(\vec{x}).

(30)

Again, this operator can be used to find small
r
approximations or the series solution to the inversion problem. Furthermore, since its form is almost identical to the spherical mean operator, we can immediately write down the corresponding partial differential equation the
n
-ball mean satisfies:

\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial r^{2}}\bar{f}_{B}(r,\vec{x})+\frac{n+1}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\bar{f}_{B}(r,\vec{x})=\mathop{{}\bigtriangleup}\nolimits\!_{\vec{x}}\bar{f}_{B}(r,\vec{x}).

(31)

A more interesting example is the general kernel
K(\vec{u})=(1-\lVert\vec{u}\rVert^{\beta})^{\alpha}
with normalization constant
C_{K}=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+1)\Gamma(\frac{n}{\beta})}{\beta\Gamma(\frac{n}{\beta}+\alpha+1)}S_{n-1}
. Let us consider two special cases. First, the bell-shaped
\beta=2
. Inserting the kernel into equation (
27
) we obtain the familiar looking operator

\bar{f}_{K}(r,\vec{x})={}_{0}F_{1}\Big(;\frac{n}{2}+\alpha+1;\frac{r^{2}\mathop{{}\bigtriangleup}\nolimits\!_{\vec{x}}}{4}\Big)f(\vec{x}),

(32)

which satisfies the differential equation

\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial r^{2}}\bar{f}_{K}(r,\vec{x})+\frac{2\alpha+n+1}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\bar{f}_{K}(r,\vec{x})=\mathop{{}\bigtriangleup}\nolimits\!_{\vec{x}}\bar{f}_{K}(r,\vec{x}).

(33)

Both expressions converge to the
n
-ball mean for
\alpha\to 0
, as expected. Note that using the property of hypergeometric function that
{}_{0}F_{1}(;a;x)=\frac{x}{(a+1)a}{}_{0}F_{1}(;a+2;x)+{}_{0}F_{1}(;a+1;x)
, we arrive at the peculiar relationship between the average over a sphere, the average over a ball and the average over a shifted parabola (
\alpha=1
):

\displaystyle\bar{f}_{S}(r,\vec{x})=\frac{r^{2}}{n(n+2)}\mathop{{}\bigtriangleup}\nolimits\!_{\vec{x}}\bar{f}_{K}(r,\vec{x})+\bar{f}_{B}(r,\vec{x}).

(34)

The second case,

\beta=1
, is a generalization of the triangular weight and results in the operator

\bar{f}_{K}(r,\vec{x})={}_{1}F_{2}\Big(\frac{n+1}{2};\frac{n+\alpha+1}{2},\frac{n+\alpha}{2}+1;\frac{r^{2}\mathop{{}\bigtriangleup}\nolimits\!_{\vec{x}}}{4}\Big)f(\vec{x}).

(35)

Since the partial differential equation is more involved here, we will refrain from showing it. Again, these expressions converge to the
n
-ball mean for
\alpha\to 0
.

Before we move on to the next section, let us briefly consider two more examples. The first is the one-dimensional form of Eq. (
30
), which coincides with the moving average of a function over a window of width
r
:

\bar{f}(r,x)=\frac{1}{2r}\int_{\mathclap{-r}}^{r}\mathrm{d}u\,f(x+u)=\frac{\sinh(r\hat{D}_{x})}{r\hat{D}_{x}}f(x),

(36)

where by use of the two integral identities
[
6
]

\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}u\!\mathop{}\cos(\alpha u)\mathrm{e}^{-su}=\frac{s}{(s^{2}+\alpha^{2})},\qquad\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}u\!\mathop{}\lvert\cos(\alpha u)\rvert\mathrm{e}^{-su}=\frac{s+\alpha\operatorname{csch}(\frac{\pi}{2\alpha}s)}{(s^{2}+\alpha^{2})},

(37)

we find for the inverse operation
f(x)=\frac{r\hat{D}_{x}}{\sinh(r\hat{D}_{x})}\bar{f}(r,x)
that recovers the original function from the moving average:

f(x)=\frac{2r^{2}}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}u\!\mathop{}\Big(\Big\lvert\cos\Big(\frac{\pi}{2r}u\Big)\Big\rvert-\cos\Big(\frac{\pi}{2r}u\Big)\Big)\Big(\bar{f}^{\prime\prime\prime}(r,x-u)+\Big(\frac{\pi}{2r}\Big)^{2}\bar{f}^{\prime}(r,x-u)\Big),

(38)

for suitable conditions on
\bar{f}(r,x)
.
The second example is related to the two-dimensional spherical mean,

\bar{f}_{S}(r,\vec{x})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathclap{\lVert\vec{u}\rVert=1}}\mathrm{d}^{2}u\,f(\vec{x}+r\vec{u}),

(39)

by “complexifying” the
y
-component of
\vec{u}
, yielding the integral

\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{d}\theta\!\mathop{}f(z+r\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}).

(40)

However, applying the shift operator to the integrand shows that

\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{d}\theta\!\mathop{}\mathrm{e}^{r\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}\hat{D}_{z}}f(z)=f(z),

(41)

since
\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{d}\theta\!\mathop{}\mathrm{e}^{r\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}}=2\pi
for
r\neq 0
. So the “complex” 2D spherical mean of a function is the function itself; this is known as Gauss’s mean value theorem
[
22
]
. Obviously this is not a valid proof, since we have omitted the necessary conditions on
f
, but it is nevertheless a simple way of arriving at this conclusion.

2
X-Ray Transform

In this short section, we apply the associated operator of the spherical mean to derive the inversion formula for the X-ray transform. This integral transform is very closely related to the Radon transform, and coincides with it in two dimensions. In general, the X-ray transform of a function
f
is defined by integrating over lines in space; parameterizing the line as
L=\vec{x}+\ell\vec{\theta}
for an initial point
\vec{x}
and a direction
\vec{\theta}
of the line, this can be written as

X\{f\}(\vec{x},\vec{\theta})=\int_{L}f(L)=\int_{\mathclap{-\infty}}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\ell\,f(\vec{x}+\ell\vec{\theta}).

(42)

To derive the inversion formula, we integrate the left-hand side over the surface of the unit sphere,

\frac{1}{S_{n-1}}\int_{\mathclap{\lVert\vec{\theta}\rVert=1}}\mathrm{d}^{n}\theta\,X\{f\}(\vec{x},\vec{\theta}),

(43)

and exchange the order of integration between the surface integral and the X-ray transform:

\frac{1}{S_{n-1}}\int_{\mathclap{\lVert\vec{\theta}\rVert=1}}\mathrm{d}^{n}\theta\int_{\mathclap{-\infty}}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\ell\,f(\vec{x}+\ell\vec{\theta})=\int_{\mathclap{-\infty}}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\ell\,\frac{1}{S_{n-1}}\int_{\mathclap{\lVert\vec{\theta}\rVert=1}}\mathrm{d}^{n}\theta\,f(\vec{x}+\ell\vec{\theta}).

(44)

The right integral is simply the spherical mean of
f(\vec{x})
, thus we can rewrite it with the operator (
7
) to get

\int_{\mathclap{-\infty}}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\ell\,\frac{1}{S_{n-1}}\int_{\mathclap{\lVert\vec{\theta}\rVert=1}}\mathrm{d}^{n}\theta\,f(\vec{x}+\ell\vec{\theta})=\int_{\mathclap{-\infty}}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\ell\,{}_{0}F_{1}\Big(;\frac{n}{2};\frac{\ell^{2}\mathop{{}\bigtriangleup}\nolimits\!_{\vec{x}}}{4}\Big)f(\vec{x}),

(45)

which evaluates to the operator

\int_{\mathclap{-\infty}}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\ell\,{}_{0}F_{1}\Big(;\frac{n}{2};\frac{\ell^{2}\mathop{{}\bigtriangleup}\nolimits\!_{\vec{x}}}{4}\Big)f(\vec{x})=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{n}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{n-1}{2})}\sqrt{-\frac{4\pi}{\mathop{{}\bigtriangleup}\nolimits\!_{\vec{x}}}}f(\vec{x}).

(46)

By rearranging this expression for
f(\vec{x})
, we find the following formal inversion formula:

f(\vec{x})=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{n-1}{2})}{\sqrt{4\pi}\Gamma(\frac{n}{2})}\sqrt{-\mathop{{}\bigtriangleup}\nolimits\!_{\vec{x}}}\frac{1}{S_{n-1}}\int_{\mathclap{\lVert\vec{\theta}\rVert=1}}\mathrm{d}^{n}\theta\,X\{f\}(\vec{x},\vec{\theta}).

(47)

Of course, this formula depends on the interpretation of the operator
\sqrt{-\mathop{{}\bigtriangleup}\nolimits\!_{\vec{x}}}
and is of little use without specifying it. To make sense of the square root of the Laplacian, we multiply the numerator and denominator by
\sqrt{-\mathop{{}\bigtriangleup}\nolimits\!_{\vec{x}}}
:

f(\vec{x})=-\frac{\Gamma(\frac{n-1}{2})}{\sqrt{4\pi}\Gamma(\frac{n}{2})}\frac{\mathop{{}\bigtriangleup}\nolimits\!_{\vec{x}}}{\sqrt{-\mathop{{}\bigtriangleup}\nolimits\!_{\vec{x}}}}\frac{1}{S_{n-1}}\int_{\mathclap{\lVert\vec{\theta}\rVert=1}}\mathrm{d}^{n}\theta\,X\{f\}(\vec{x},\vec{\theta}).

(48)
