Provable and Efficient Continual Representation Learning
Yingcong Li yli692@ucr.edu, Mingchen Li mli176@ucr.edu, M. Salman Asif sasif@ece.ucr.edu, Samet Oymak oymak@ece.ucr.edu

Abstract
In continual learning (CL), the goal is to design models that can learn a sequence of tasks without catastrophic forgetting. Despite rich set of CL techniques, relatively little understanding exists on how representations built by previous tasks benefit new tasks that are added to the network. To this aim, we study continual representation learning (CRL) problem where we learn an evolving representation as new tasks arrive. Focusing on zero-forgetting methods where tasks are embedded in subnetworks, we first provide experiments demonstrating CRL can significantly boost sample-efficiency when learning new tasks. To explain this, we establish sample complexity and generalization error bounds for new tasks which formalize the benefits of previously-learned representations. Our analysis and experiments show that CL benefits more when the initial tasks have large sample size and high representation diversity. Diversity ensures that adding new tasks incurs small representation mismatch while requiring few additional trainable weights. Finally, we study computational-efficiency of CRL and propose a novel variant of the PackNet algorithm that employs joint channel & weight pruning. Our method embeds tasks in channel-sparse subnets requiring up to 80% less FLOPs to compute while approximately retaining accuracy and is shown to be competitive with various baselines.

Introduction

Continual learning
(CL) or lifelong learning aims to build a model for a non-stationary and never-ending sequence of tasks, without access to previous or future data (
Thrun 1998
;
Chen and Liu 2018
;
Parisi et al. 2019
). The main challenge in CL is that a standard learning procedure usually results in performance reduction on previously trained tasks if their data is not available. The phenomenon is termed as
catastrophic forgetting
(
McCloskey and Cohen 1989
;
Kirkpatrick et al. 2017
;
Pfülb and Gepperth 2019
).

Importance of continual learning in real-life inference and decision making scenarios led to a rich set of CL techniques (
Delange et al. 2021
;
Van de Ven and Tolias 2019
). However, the theoretical principles of continual learning is relatively less understood and the progress is under-whelming compared to the algorithmic advances despite recent progress (see
Bennani et al. 2020
;
Doan et al. 2021
;
Lee et al. 2021
). In this work, for the first time, we investigate the problem of
Continual Representation Learning
(CRL) to answer

What are the statistical benefits of previous feature representations for learning a new task? Can we build an insightful theory explaining empirical performance?

Our key contribution is addressing both questions affirmatively. We develop our algorithm and theory for architecture-based zero-forgetting CL which includes PackNet (
Mallya and Lazebnik 2018
), CPG (
Hung et al. 2019
), and RMN (
Wu et al. 2020
). These methods eliminate forgetting by training a sub-network for each task and freezing the trained parameters. At a high level, all of these methods inherently have the potential of continual representation learning by allowing new tasks to reuse the frozen feature representations built for earlier tasks. However, quantifying the empirical benefits/performance of these representations and building the associated theory have been elusive. We overcome this via innovations in experiment design, theory, and algorithms:

\bullet

Theoretical and empirical benefits of continual representations.
We establish theoretical results and sample complexity bounds for CRL by using tools from empirical process theory. Within our model, a new task uses frozen feature map
\phi_{\text{frz}}
of previous tasks and learns an additional task-specific representation
\phi_{\text{new}}
. For PackNet (
Mallya and Lazebnik 2018
),
\phi_{\text{frz}}
corresponds to all the nonzero weights so far and
\phi_{\text{new}}
is the nonzeros allocated to the new task, thus
\phi_{\text{frz}}
requires a lot more data to learn. Our theory (see Section
4.2
) explains (1) how the new task can reuse the frozen feature map to greatly reduce the sample size and (2) how to quantify the representational compatibility between old and new tasks. Specifically, we fully avoid the statistical cost of learning
\phi_{\text{frz}}
and replace it with a
“representational mismatch”
term between the new task and frozen features.
Additionally, inspired by
Srebro et al. 2010
;
Bartlett et al. 2005
, we obtain faster rates using a localization argument for our CRL problem (see Section
4.2
).
We also extend our results from a single task to learning a sequence of tasks by quantifying the aggregate impact of finite data on representation
\phi_{\text{frz}}
which evolves as new tasks arrive (see Section
6
).

An important conclusion is that the ideal frozen representation
\phi_{\text{frz}}
should contain
diverse and high-quality features
so that it has small representational
mismatch
with new tasks. This conclusion is depicted in Figure
1
where we highlight the impact of diversity and sample size on task accuracy. It is also in line with the intuitions from multitask representation learning (
Maurer et al. 2016
;
Tripuraneni et al. 2020b
) and motivates the following CL principle supported by our experiments:

First learn diverse and large-data tasks so that their representations help upcoming tasks.

Indeed, we show in Fig.
3(a)
that a new task with small data achieves significantly higher accuracy if it is added later in the task sequence (so that
\phi_{\text{frz}}
becomes more diverse). Then, we show in Fig.
3(b)
that choosing the first task to be diverse (such as ImageNet) helps all the downstream tasks. Finally, we show in Fig.
3(c)
that it is better to first learn tasks with large sample sizes to ensure high-quality features.
Our results on the importance of task order also relate to curriculum learning (
Bengio et al. 2009
) where the agent gets to choose the order tasks are learned. However, instead of curriculum learning, which aims to learn one task from easy to hard, our work is based on a more general continual learning setup. Our conclusion on task diversity provides a new perspective on the learning order of curriculum learning that we should first learn more representative tasks instead of the easier tasks.

\bullet

Algorithms for inference-efficient CRL.
Zero-forgetting CL methods often need a large neural network (dubbed as supernetwork) to load numerous tasks into subnetworks, or dynamically expand the model to avoid forgetting (
Delange et al. 2021
). Thus, CL/CRL may incur a large computational cost during inference. This leads us to ask whether one can retain the accuracy benefits of CRL while ensuring that each new task utilizes an
inference-efficient
sub-network, thus achieving the best of both worlds. We quantify inference-efficiency via floating point operations (FLOPs) required to compute the task subnetwork. To this end, we propose Efficient Sparse PackNet (ESPN) algorithm (Fig.
2
). In a nutshell, ESPN guarantees inference-efficiency by incorporating a channel-pruning stage within PackNet-style approaches. Via extensive evaluations, we find that, ESPN incurs minimal loss of accuracy while greatly reducing FLOPs (as much as 80% in our SplitCIFAR-100 experiments, Table
1
).

In summary, this work makes key contributions to continual representation learning from empirical, theoretical, and algorithmic perspectives. We remark that our main work focuses on the task-incremental CL setting with parameter-isolation. However, we anticipate that the high-level principles arising from our findings will inform alternative continual learning strategies as well. In the remainder of the paper, we discuss the related work, detail our empirical and theoretical findings on CRL, and present the ESPN algorithm and its evaluations.

2
Related Work

Our contributions are most closely related to the continual learning literature. Our theory and algorithm also connect to representation learning and neural network pruning.

Continual learning.

A number of methods for continual and lifelong learning have been proposed to tackle the problem of catastrophic forgetting and existing approaches can be broadly categorized into three groups (
Delange et al. 2021
): replay-based (
Lopez-Paz and Ranzato 2017
;
Rebuffi et al. 2017
;
Rolnick et al. 2018
;
Buzzega et al. 2021
;
Aljundi et al. 2019
;
Borsos et al. 2020
), regularization-based (
Kirkpatrick et al. 2017
;
Zenke et al. 2017
;
Li and Hoiem 2017
;
Jung et al. 2020
), and architecture-based methods (
Fernando et al. 2017
;
Yoon et al. 2017
;
Mallya et al. 2018
;
Rusu et al. 2016
).
Lubana et al. 2021
shows that the quadratic regularization technique plays a rule in preventing catastrophic forgetting.
Recent work
Ramesh and Chaudhari 2021
explores statistical challenges associated with continual learning in terms of the relatedness across tasks through replay-based strategies. In comparison, we focus on the benefits of representation learning and establish how representation built for previous tasks can drastically reduce the sample complexity on new tasks in terms of representation mismatch without access to past data. Another key difference is that our analysis allows for learning multiple sequential tasks rather than a single task. In our work, consistent with our theory, we focus on zero-forgetting CL (
Mallya and Lazebnik 2018
;
Hung et al. 2019
;
Mallya et al. 2018
;
Wortsman et al. 2020
;
Kaushik et al. 2021
;
Tu et al. 2020
), which is a sub-branch of architecture-based methods and completely eliminates forgetting. PackNet (
Mallya and Lazebnik 2018
), CPG (
Hung et al. 2019
), and RMN (
Kaushik et al. 2021
) train a sub-network for each task and implement zero-forgetting by freezing the trained parameters, while Piggyback (
Mallya et al. 2018
) trains masks only over pretrained model. Inspired by
Ramanujan et al. 2020
, SupSup (
Wortsman et al. 2020
) trains a binary mask for each task while keeping the underlying model fixed at initialization. However, finding of
Frankle and Carbin 2018
shows that a network can reduce by over 90% of parameters without performance reduction. This inspires our weight-allocation strategy to adapt PackNet to more sparse sub-networks. Unlike PackNet which prunes the network by keeping the largest absolute weights in each layer and reuses all the frozen weights, CPG and RMN apply real-valued mask to each fixed entry and prune by keeping the largest values of the mask. SupSup is motivated by
Ramanujan et al. 2020
;
Zhou et al. 2019
;
Malach et al. 2020
which show that a sufficiently over-parameterized random network contains a sub-network with roughly the same accuracy as the target network without training. In essence, it aims to find masks only over random network, and it is adaptable to infinite tasks. However, it leads to inefficient inference (due to using the full network) and potentially large memory requirements (as one has to store a mask as large as supernet rather than a subnet).

In CL, in order to embed the super-network with many tasks or to achieve acceptable performance over a masked random network, sufficiently large networks are needed, which naturally leads to inefficiency during inference-time without proper safeguards. Addressing this challenge appears to be an unexplored avenue as far as we are aware. While in this work, we present Efficient Sparse PackNet (ESPN) that implements zero-forgetting CL and achieves state-of-the-art accuracy with less computational demand.

We also emphasize that there are several interesting works on the theory of continual learning such as
Lee et al. 2021
;
Doan et al. 2021
;
Bennani et al. 2020
;
Mirzadeh et al. 2020
;
Yin et al. 2020
. These works focus on NTK-based analysis for deep nets, theoretical investigation of orthogonal gradient descent (
Farajtabar et al. 2020
), and task-similarity. However, to the best of our knowledge, ours is the first work on the representation learning ability and the associated data-efficiency.

Representation learning theory.
The rise of deep learning motivated a growing interest in theoretical principles behind representation learning. Similar in spirit to this project,
Maurer et al. 2016
provides generalization bounds for representation-based transfer learning in terms of the Rademacher complexities associated with the source and target tasks. Some of the earliest works towards this goal include
Baxter 2000
and linear settings of
Lounici et al. 2011
;
Pontil and Maurer 2013
;
Wang et al. 2016
;
Cavallanti et al. 2010
. More recent works (
Hanneke and Kpotufe 2020
;
Lu et al. 2021
;
Kong et al. 2020
;
Qin et al. 2022
;
Wu et al. 2020
;
Garg and Liang 2020
;
Gulluk et al. 2021
;
Xu and Tewari 2021b
;
Du et al. 2021
;
Tripuraneni et al. 2020b
;
Qin et al. 2022
;
Tripuraneni et al. 2020a
;
Sun et al. 2021
;
Maurer et al. 2016
;
Arora et al. 2019
;
Chen et al. 2021
;
Sun et al. 2021
) consider variations beyond supervised learning, concrete settings or established more refined upper/lower performance bounds. There is also a long line of works related to model agnostic meta-learning (
Finn et al. 2017
;
Denevi et al. 2019
;
Balcan et al. 2019
;
Khodak et al. 2019
). Unlike these works, we consider the CL setting where tasks arrive sequentially and establish how the representation learned by earlier tasks provably helps learning the new tasks with fewer samples and better accuracy.
Similar to
Bartlett et al. 2005
;
Srebro et al. 2010
, we also obtain faster rate bounds using a localization argument but for continual learning problem with multiple tasks. To the best of our knowledge, existing works on multitask learning do not achieve fast rates except for linear representations (
Tripuraneni et al. 2020a
;
Sun et al. 2021
) which is a distinguishing feature of our bound.

Neural network pruning.
Our work is naturally related to neural network pruning methods and compression techniques as we embed tasks into sub-networks. Large model sizes in deep learning have led to a substantial interest in model pruning/quantization (
Han et al. 2015a
;
Hassibi and Stork 1993
;
LeCun et al. 1990
). DNN pruning has a diverse literature with various architectural, algorithmic, and hardware considerations (
Sze et al. 2017
;
Han et al. 2015b
). Here, we mention the ones related to our work.
Frankle and Carbin 2018
empirically shows that a large DNN contains a small subset of favorable weights (for pruning), which can achieve similar performance to the original network when trained with the same initialization.
Zhou et al. 2019
;
Malach et al. 2020
;
Pensia et al. 2020
demonstrate that there are subsets with good test performance even without any training and provide theoretical guarantees. In relation,
Chang et al. 2021
establishes the theoretical benefits of training large over-parameterized networks to improve downstream pruning performance.

Although weight pruning is proven to be a good way to reduce model parameters and maintain performance, practically, it does not lead to compute efficiency except for some dedicated hardwares (
Han et al. 2016
). Unlike weight pruning, structured/channel pruning prunes the model at the channel level which results in a slim sub-network carrying much fewer FLOPs than the original dense model (
Liu et al. 2017
;
Zhuang et al. 2020
;
Wen et al. 2016
;
Ye et al. 2018
). For example
Wen et al. 2016
;
Zhou et al. 2016
;
Alvarez and Salzmann 2016
;
Lebedev and Lempitsky 2016
;
He et al. 2017
prune models by adding a sparse regularization over model weights whereas
Liu et al. 2017
;
Zhuang et al. 2020
only add regularization over channel factors, and prune channels with lower scaling factors. However, these prior works do not focus on the scenario where almost all of FLOPs pruned, for example with only 1% of original FLOPs remained. To achieve this goal, we present an innovative channel pruning method based on FLOP-aware penalization. Our technique is inspired from
Liu et al. 2017
;
Zhuang et al. 2020
(they use sparsity regularization over BatchNorm weights only). However, as demonstrated in Appendix
A
, our pruning method outperforms both of them in our evaluations.

Efficient Sparse PackNet (ESPN) Algorithm

We will use PackNet and ESPN throughout the paper to study continual representation learning. Thus, we first introduce the high-level idea of our ESPN algorithm which augments PackNet. Suppose we have a single model referred to as supernetwork and a sequence of tasks. Our goal is to train and find optimal sparse sub-networks within the supernet that satisfy both FLOPs and sparsity restrictions without any performance reduction or forgetting of earlier tasks. Figure
2
illustrates our proposed algorithm. We propose a joint channel and weight pruning strategy in which FLOPs constraint (in channel pruning) is important for efficient inference and sparsity constraint (in weight pruning) is important for
packing
all tasks into the network even for a large number of tasks
T
. In essence, ESPN equips PackNet-type methods with inference-efficiency using an innovative FLOP-aware channel pruning strategy. Section
7
and appendix provide implementation details and inference-time evaluations on ESPN.

4
Empirical and Theoretical Insights for Continual Representation Learning

In this section, we discuss continual learning from the representation learning perspective. We first present our experimental insights which show that (1) features learned from previous tasks help reduce the sample complexity of new tasks and (2) the order of task sequence (in terms of diversity and sample size) is critical for the success of CRL. In Section
4.2
, we present our theoretical framework and a rigorous analysis in support of our experimental findings.

4.1
Empirical Investigation of CRL

We further elucidate upon Figure
3
and discuss the role of sample size (for both past and new tasks) and task diversity.

Investigating data efficiency (Fig
3(a)
)
A good test to assess benefit of CRL is by constructing settings where new tasks have fewer samples. Consider SplitCIFAR100 for which
100

20

{\cal{D}}_{cl}=\{\mathcal{T}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{T}_{15}\}
and a test set
{\cal{D}}_{t}=\{\mathcal{T}_{16},\dots,\mathcal{T}_{20}\}
. Test set is used to assess data efficiency and therefore, (intentionally) contains only 10% of the original sample size (250 samples per task instead of 2500). We first train the network sequentially using
{\cal{D}}_{cl}
via ESPN/PackNet and create checkpoints of the supernetwork at different task IDs: At time

, we get a supernet trained with
\mathcal{T}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{T}_{t}
, for
t\leq 15
.

on it. Fig.

displays the test accuracy of tasks in

where we used SplitCIFAR100 setting detailed in Sec.
7.1

Importance of sample size.

Finally, we show that the sample size is also critical for CRL because one can build higher-quality (less noisy) features with more data. To this end, we devise another experiment based on SplitCIFAR100 dataset. Instead of using original tasks each with 5 classes and 2,500 samples, we train the first task (task ID 1 in Fig.
3(c)
) with 2,500 samples, then decrease the sample size for all the following tasks using the rule
\lfloor 2,500\times(1/20)^{\text{(ID-1)}/19}\rfloor
until the last task (task ID 20 in Fig.
3(c)
) has only 125 training samples. Figure
3(c)
presents the results, where solid curves are obtained by training Task ID 1 to 20 with decreasing sample size, dashed curves are for training Task ID 20 to 1 with increasing sample size, and dotted curves are for individual training where task order does not matter. The accuracy curves are smoothed with a moving average and displayed in the decreasing order from Task ID 1 to 20. The results support our intuition that training large sample tasks first (decreasing order) performs better, as larger tasks build high quality representations that benefit generalization for future small tasks with less data. More strikingly, the dotted Individual line falls strictly between solid and dashed curves. This means that increasing order actually
hurts accuracy
whereas decreasing order
helps accuracy
compared to training from scratch (i.e. no representation). Specifically, decreasing helps (solid
>
dotted) on the right side of the figure where tasks are small (thanks to good initial representations) whereas increasing hurts on the left side where tasks are large. The latter is likely due to the fact that, adding a large task requires a larger/better subnetwork to achieve high accuracy, however, since we train small tasks first, supernetwork runs out of sufficient free weights for a large subnetwork.

4.2
Theoretical Analysis and Performance Bounds for Continual Representation Learning

In this section, we provide theoretical analysis to explain how CRL provably promotes sample efficiency and benefits from initial tasks with large diversity and sample size.

Denote
[n]=\{1,2,\dots,n\}
. We use
{\widetilde{\cal{O}}}(\cdot)
to denote equality up to a factor involving at most logarithmic terms. Following our experiments as well as
Maurer et al. 2016
, a realistic model for deep representation learning is the compositional hypothesis
f=h\circ\phi
where
h\in{\bm{H}}
is the classifier head and
\phi\in{\bm{\Phi}}
is the shared backend feature extractor. In practice,
\phi
has many more parameters than
h
. To model continual learning, let us assume that we already trained a frozen feature extractor
\phi_{\text{frz}}\in{\bm{\Phi}}_{\text{frz}}
. Here
{\bm{H}},{\bm{\Phi}},{\bm{\Phi}}_{\text{frz}}
are the hypothesis sets to learn from. Suppose we are now given a set of
T
new tasks represented by independent datasets
\mathcal{S}_{t}=\{(\bm{x}_{ti},y_{ti})\}_{i=1}^{N}{\subset\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}
, each drawn i.i.d. from different distributions
{\cal{D}}_{t}
for
1\leq t\leq T
. Our goal is to build the hypotheses
(f_{t})_{t=1}^{T}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
for these new tasks with small sample size
N
while leveraging
\phi_{\text{frz}}\in{\bm{\Phi}}_{\text{frz}}
.

CRL setting.
In a realistic CL setting the new tasks are allowed to learn new features. We will capture this with an
incremental
feature extractor
\phi_{\text{new}}\in{\bm{\Phi}}_{\text{new}}
, and represent the hypothesis of each task via the composition
f_{t}=h_{t}\circ\phi
where
\phi=\phi_{\text{new}}+\phi_{\text{frz}}
. For PackNet/ESPN,
{\bm{\Phi}}_{\text{new}}
corresponds to the free/trainable weights allocated to the new task,
\phi_{\text{frz}}
corresponds to the trained weights of the earlier tasks and
\phi
corresponds to the eventual task subnetwork and its weights. We will evaluate the quality of
\phi
(which lies in the Minkowski sum
{\bm{\Phi}}_{\text{new}}+{\bm{\Phi}}_{\text{frz}}
) with respect to a global representation space
{\bm{\Phi}}
which is chosen to be a superset:
{\bm{\Phi}}_{\text{new}}+{\bm{\Phi}}_{\text{frz}}\subseteq{\bm{\Phi}}
. For instance, in PackNet,
{\bm{\Phi}}_{\text{new}}+{\bm{\Phi}}_{\text{frz}}
denotes the sparse sub-networks allocated to the tasks whereas
{\bm{\Phi}}
corresponds to the full supernet.

This motivates us to pose a CRL problem that builds a continual representation by searching for
\phi_{\text{new}}
and combining with
\phi_{\text{frz}}
. Let
{\bm{h}}=(h_{t})_{t=1}^{T}\in{\bm{H}}^{T}
denote all
T
task-specific classifier heads, we solve

\displaystyle\underset{\phi=\phi_{\text{new}}+\phi_{\text{frz}}}{\underset{{\bm{h}}\in{\bm{H}}^{T},\phi_{\text{new}}\in{\bm{\Phi}}_{\text{new}}}{\arg\min}}

\displaystyle{\widehat{\cal{L}}}({\bm{h}},\phi):=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}{\widehat{\cal{L}}}_{\mathcal{S}_{t}}(h_{t}\circ\phi)

WHERE

\displaystyle{\widehat{\cal{L}}}_{\mathcal{S}_{t}}(f):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ell(y_{ti},f(\bm{x}_{ti})).

(CRL)

Intuition:
(
CRL
) aims to learn the task-specific headers
{\bm{h}}
and the shared incremental representation
\phi_{\text{new}}
. Let
\mathcal{C}(\cdot)
be a complexity measure for a function class (e.g. VC-dimension). Intuitions from the MTL literature would advocate that when the total sample size obeys
N\times T\gtrsim T\mathcal{C}({\bm{H}})+\mathcal{C}({\bm{\Phi}}_{\text{new}})
, then (
CRL
) would return generalizable solutions
{\bm{\hat{h}}},\hat{\phi}_{\text{new}}
. This is desirable as in practice
\phi_{\text{frz}}
is a much more complex hypothesis obtained by training on many earlier tasks. Thus, from continual learning perspective, theoretical goals are:

1.

The sample size should only depend on the complexity

of the incremental representation rather than the combined complexity that can potentially be much larger (
\mathcal{C}({\bm{\Phi}}_{\text{new}})+\mathcal{C}({\bm{\Phi}}_{\text{frz}})\gg\mathcal{C}({\bm{\Phi}}_{\text{new}})

2.

To explain Figure

\phi_{\text{frz}}
can provably help accuracy. Ideally, thanks to
\phi_{\text{frz}}

{\bm{\Phi}}

3.

Finally, we emphasize that, we add the
T

Before stating our technical results, we need to introduce a few definitions. To quantify the complexities of the search spaces
{\bm{\Phi}}_{\text{new}},{\bm{H}}
, we introduce
metric dimension
(
Mendelson 2003

Vapnik and Chervonenkis 2015

Definition 1

(Metric dimension)
.

Let
{\bm{G}}:\mathcal{Z}\rightarrow\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}
be a set of functions. Let
\bar{C}_{\mathcal{Z}}>0
be a scalar that is allowed to depend on
\mathcal{Z}
. Let
{\bm{G}}_{\varepsilon}
be a minimal-size
\varepsilon
-cover of
{\bm{G}}
such that for any
g\in{\bm{G}}
there exists
g^{\prime}\in{\bm{G}}_{\varepsilon}
that ensures
\sup_{{\bm{z}}\in\mathcal{Z}}\|{g({\bm{z}})-g^{\prime}({\bm{z}})}\|_{\ell_{2}}\leq\varepsilon
. The metric dimension
\mathcal{C}({\bm{G}})
is the smallest number that satisfies
\log|{\bm{G}}_{\varepsilon}|\leq\mathcal{C}({\bm{G}})\log(\bar{C}_{\mathcal{Z}}/\varepsilon)
for all
\varepsilon>0
.

\bar{C}_{\mathcal{Z}}
typically depends only logarithmically on the Euclidean radius of the feature space under mild Lipschitzness conditions, thus,
\bar{C}_{\mathcal{Z}}
dependence will be dropped for cleaner exposition. In practice, for neural networks or other parametric hypothesis, metric dimension is bounded by the number of trainable weights up to logarithmic factors (
Barron and Klusowski 2018
).

Metric dimension will help us characterize the sample complexity. However, we also would like to understand when
{\bm{\Phi}}_{\text{frz}}
can help. To this end, we introduce definitions that capture the population loss (infinite data limit) of new tasks and the
feature compatibility
between the new tasks and
\phi_{\text{frz}}
of old tasks. These definitions help decouple the finite sample size
N
and the distribution of the new tasks.

Definition 2

(Distributional quantities)
.

Define the population (infinite-sample) risk as
{\cal{L}}({\bm{h}},\phi)=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[{\widehat{\cal{L}}}({\bm{h}},\phi)]
. Define the optimal risk over representation
{\bm{\Phi}}
as
{\cal{L}}^{\star}=\min_{{\bm{h}}\in{\bm{H}}^{T},\phi\in{\bm{\Phi}}}{\cal{L}}({\bm{h}},\phi)
. Note that the optimal risk can always choose the best representations within
{\bm{\Phi}}_{\text{new}}
and
{\bm{\Phi}}_{\text{frz}}
since
{\bm{\Phi}}_{\text{new}}+{\bm{\Phi}}_{\text{frz}}\subseteq{\bm{\Phi}}
. Finally, define the optimal population risk using frozen
\phi_{\text{frz}}

{\cal{L}}^{\star}_{{\phi}_{\text{frz}}}=\min_{{\bm{h}}\in{\bm{H}}^{T},\phi_{\text{new}}\in{\bm{\Phi}}_{\text{new}}}{\cal{L}}({\bm{h}},\phi)
s.t.
\phi=\phi_{\text{new}}+\phi_{\text{frz}}
.

Following this, the
representation mismatch
introduced below assesses the suboptimality of
\phi_{\text{frz}}
for the new task distributions compared to the optimal hypothesis within
{\bm{\Phi}}
.

Definition 3

(New & old tasks mismatch)
.

The representation mismatch between the frozen features
\phi_{\text{frz}}
and the new tasks is defined as

\displaystyle\text{MM}_{\text{frz}}={\cal{L}}^{\star}_{\phi_{\text{frz}}}-{\cal{L}}^{\star}.

By construction,
\text{MM}_{\text{frz}}
is guaranteed to be non-negative. Additionally,
\text{MM}_{\text{frz}}=0
if we choose global space to be
{\bm{\Phi}}={\bm{\Phi}}_{\text{new}}+{\bm{\Phi}}_{\text{frz}}
and

{\bm{\Phi}}_{\text{frz}}

Theorem 4

Let
{\bm{h}},{\bm{\hat{h}}}

\phi_{\text{frz}}

obeys

\displaystyle{\cal{L}}({\bm{\hat{h}}},\hat{\phi})

\displaystyle\leq{\cal{L}}^{\star}_{\phi_{\text{frz}}}+\sqrt{\frac{{\cal{L}}^{\star}_{\phi_{\text{frz}}}\cdot{\widetilde{\cal{O}}}_{\tau}({\bm{H}},{\bm{\Phi}}_{\text{new}})}{TN}}+\frac{{\widetilde{\cal{O}}}_{\tau}({\bm{H}},{\bm{\Phi}}_{\text{new}})}{TN},

Proof sketch:
Our proof uses a covering argument following Definition
1

and
{\bm{\Phi}}_{\text{new}}

and
{\bm{\Phi}}_{\text{new},\varepsilon}

such that

, where

,

, and we assume

, and derive that with probability at least

. Here

captures dependence on dimension and specific hypothesis, namely, the bound is localized and smaller

leads to a tighter bound. Finally, Theorem

. Further proof details are deferred to Appendix

In words, this theorem shows that as soon as the total sample complexity obeys

from scratch

An intuitive way is to repeatedly apply Theorem

With enough samples, representations learned from finite sample tasks are almost as useful as representations learned from infinite sample tasks.

Assumption 1

Discussion.
Here are a few remarks in place. First, we state the sum of test errors rather than the average. Secondly, observe that (
6
) compares the test errors to the optimal possible errors
{\cal{L}}^{\star,t}
. On the right hand side there are two terms: “representation mismatch” and “cost of finite sample learning”.

“Representation mismatch” quantifies the population-level error that arises even if each task had access to infinite samples. This is because, even if each task solved (
CRL-SEQ
) perfectly, the resulting sequence of representations does not have to be optimal for the next task and
\text{MM}_{\text{seq}}^{\star,t}
precisely captures this suboptimality. Recall that this population-level gap arises from Definition
6
. This also emphasizes that we should train diverse tasks firstly, since diverse features learned from previous tasks help reduce
\text{MM}_{\text{seq}}^{\star,t}
due to its highly relevant representation.

The “cost of finite sample learning” term originally captures the finite sample effects, and it is proportional to the statistical error rate of solving (
CRL-SEQ
) for the first task-only i.e.
\sqrt{\frac{\mathcal{C}({\bm{H}})+\mathcal{C}^{{\bm{\Phi}}}_{\text{new}}}{N}}
. Here, recall from (
2
) that
\mathcal{C}^{{\bm{\Phi}}}_{\text{new}}
is an upper bound to the complexities of
{\bm{\Phi}}_{\text{new}}^{1},\dots,{\bm{\Phi}}_{\text{new}}^{T}
. Perhaps unexpected dependence is the quadratic growth in
T
. This is in contrast to linear growth one would get from adding tasks simultaneously as in Theorem
4
. This quadratic growth arises from the accumulation of the finite-sample representation suboptimalities as we add more tasks. Specifically, Assumption
1
helps guarantee that feature-extractors of tasks
1,\dots,t-1
are useful for task
t
; however, as more tasks are added they incur more divergence from
(\phi^{\star,t})_{t=1}^{T}
. Each new task has a finite sample size and contributes to this divergence and our analysis leads to
{\cal{O}}(T^{2})
upper bound on the error.
\varepsilon_{0}
is an additional mismatch term that makes Assumption
1
significantly more flexible (albeit ideally, it is close to zero). Finally, it would be interesting to explore the tightness of these bounds for concrete analytical settings (e.g.
CRL-SEQ
with linear models or neural nets), run more experiments and further study the role of finite sample effects & representation divergences.

7
Inference-efficient Continual Representations via ESPN

In this section, we introduce more implementation and evaluation details of our ESPN algorithm. We use the phrases
mask
and
sub-network
interchangeably because we obtain the task sub-network by masking weights of the supernet. This sub-network is the nonzero support of the task, that is, the task-specific model is obtained by setting other weights to zero. We assume a sequence of tasks
\{\mathcal{T}_{t}
,
1\leq t\leq T\}
is received during training time, where
t
is task identifier,
T
is the number of tasks, and
\mathcal{T}_{t}
has training dataset
\mathcal{S}_{t}=\{(\bm{x}_{ti},y_{ti})\}_{i=1}^{N_{t}}
(In experiments,
(N_{t})_{t=1}^{T}
do not need to be the same.).
Given task sequence and a single model, our goal is to find optimal sparse sub-networks that satisfy both FLOPs and sparsity restrictions without performance reduction and knowledge forgetting. FLOPs constraint is important for efficient inference whereas sparsity constraint is important for adding all tasks into the network even for large number of tasks
T
. To this end, we use joint channel and weight pruning strategy. Let
\ell
be a loss function,
f
be a hypothesis (prediction function) and

\bm{m}^{\text{frz}}_{1}=\emptyset

\bm{m}

Define the loss

\bullet

Trainable task-specific BatchNorm.

We train separate BatchNorm layers for each task. This has multiple synergistic benefits. First, our algorithm trains faster and generalizes better than PackNet which does not train BatchNorm weights (see Table
1
). Specifically, training BatchNorm weights allows ESPN to re-purpose the (frozen) weights of the earlier tasks with negligible memory cost. BatchNorm weights also guide our channel pruning scheme described next.

\bullet

FLOP-aware pruning.
Many of the prior works on channel pruning (
Liu et al. 2017
;
Zhuang et al. 2020
) focus on reducing the number of channels rather than the computation/FLOPs cost of the channels which varies across layers. As shown in Fig.
5(b)
in appendix, in order to satisfy FLOPs constraint
\gamma
, prior methods require numerous channels to be pruned. This results in unsatisfactory performance especially under aggressive FLOPs constraint (shown in Fig.
5(a)
). However in CL setup, in order to train a single model with many tasks, a significantly larger supernet with high capacity is needed compared to a single task requirement, and we aim to find a sub-network with very few FLOPs without compromising performance. To fit our specific needs for channel pruning, in this paper we present an innovative channel pruning algorithm called FLOP-aware channel pruning that preserves the performance up to 80% FLOPs reduction in our SplitCIFAR100 experiments (Table
1
). Following
Liu et al. 2017
, we consider BatchNorm weights as trainable saliency scores for convolutional channels and prune all channels with scores lower than a certain threshold by setting them to zero. Let
{\bm{\Gamma}}
be the BatchNorm vectors. Given dataset
\{(\bm{x}_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
, in practice, rather than solving the constrained problem (
ESPN-OPT
), Algorithm
1
minimizes a regularized objective
{\cal{L}}_{\mathcal{S}_{t}}({\bm{\theta}}\odot\bm{m})+\mathcal{R}({\bm{\Gamma}},\bm{m})
. Here
\mathcal{R}
is a regularization term to promote channel sparsity in (
8
).

For an
L
layer network, use
{\bm{\Gamma}}_{l}
to denote the
l
’th BatchNorm weights for
l\in[L]
. Intuitively, we wish to use
\ell_{1}
-regularization on
{\bm{\Gamma}}
. However, since layers of the network show variation, layerwise regularization parameters
(\lambda_{l})_{l=1}^{L}
are needed. Instead of designing
\lambda_{l}
by trial-and-error which is time-consuming and expert-dependent, we introduce a method that chooses
\lambda_{l}
automatically, fine-tunes
\lambda_{l}
during gradual pruning and adapts to the global FLOPs constraint. Specifically,
\lambda_{l}
is chosen based on the FLOPs load of a channel determined by its input feature dimensions and operations. Here an implicit goal is pruning as few channels as possible while achieving maximum FLOPs reduction. To achieve these goals, we use the following FLOP-weighted sparsity regularization

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}({\bm{\Gamma}},\bm{m})

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{L}\lambda_{l}\|{\bm{\Gamma}}_{l}\|_{1},~~\text{where}~~\lambda_{l}=g(\text{FLOP}_{l}(\bm{m})).

(8)

Here
\text{FLOP}_{l}(\bm{m})
calculates the FLOPs load for a channel in the
l
’th layer of the subnet, and

is a monotonically increasing function. Notably, channels in the same layer load the same number of FLOPs. We use
\ell_{1}
-penalty to enforce unimportant elements to zeros and prune the channels with the smallest weights over all layers. Since
g(\cdot)
is increasing, channels costing more FLOPs are assigned with larger
\lambda_{l}
and are pushed towards zero, thus they are easier to be pruned. Additionally, since
\text{FLOP}_{l}(\cdot)
is based on subnet
\bm{m}
,
\lambda_{l}
is automatically tuned while we use gradual pruning (Line
10

1
). In our experiments, we use
g(x)=C\sqrt{x}
for a proper scaling choice
C>0
. Here
C
can be seen as a normalized term and in detail we have

\displaystyle\lambda_{l}=\frac{\sqrt{\text{FLOP}_{l}(\bm{m})}}{\sum_{i=1}^{L}\sqrt{\text{FLOP}_{i}(\bm{m})}}.

\bullet

Weight allocation.
Since we do not modify the supernet architecture, without care, supernet might run out of free weights if there is a huge number of tasks. While original PackNet paper (
Mallya and Lazebnik 2018
) also uses weight pruning, since they consider relatively fewer tasks, they don’t develop an algorithmic strategy for allocating the free weights to new tasks. In our experiments, we introduce a simple weight allocation scheme to assign
\overline{\text{NNZ}}_{t}
depending on the number of remaining free weights. Let
p
be the total number of weights,
p_{t}
be the total number of weights used by tasks
1
to
t
and
p_{0}=0
. We set

\displaystyle\overline{\text{NNZ}}_{t}=\lceil(p-p_{t-1})\cdot\alpha\rceil\quad\text{for some}\quad 0<\alpha<1.

(9)

Here
\alpha
is the
weight-allocation
level and a new task gets to use
\alpha
fraction of all unused weights in the supernet. We emphasize that weight allocation controls the number of new nonzeros allocated to a task. A new task is allowed to use all of the (frozen) nonzeros that are allocated to the previous tasks (as long as FLOPs constraint is not violated).

7.1
Experimental Setup

We evaluate the performance of our proposed ESPN in terms of accuracy and efficiency metrics on three datasets: SplitCIFAR100, RotatedMNIST, and PermutedMNIST. We compare ESPN to numerous baselines, which include training each task individually, multitask learning (MTL), PackNet (
Mallya and Lazebnik 2018
), CPG (
Hung et al. 2019
), RMN (
Kaushik et al. 2021

To the best of our knowledge, none of the existing methods consider the inference-efficiency of individual tasks within the CL setting. In our experiments, in addition to embedding each task to a sparse subnet, we also enforce each subnet to be computation-efficient via our channel pruning technique while minimally harming accuracy.

) into 20 tasks where each task contains

500

36
tasks with

36

8
Conclusion and Discussion

To summarize, our work elucidates the benefit of continual representation learning theoretically and empirically and sheds light on the role of task ordering, diversity and sample size. We also propose a new CL algorithm to learn good representations subject to inference considerations. Extensive experimental evaluations on the proposed Efficient Sparse PackNet demonstrate its ability to achieve good accuracy as well as fast inference.

Limitations and future directions.

Although we highlight the importance of task order in CRL, the task sequence is not always under our control. It would be desirable to develop adaptive learning schemes that can better identify an exploit diverse tasks and discover semantic connections across task pairs even for a predetermined task sequence.
Another potential direction is to develop similar inference-efficient continual learning schemes for other architectures by appropriately adapting our joint weight and channel pruning strategy. An example is transformer-based models where computation and memory efficiency is particularly critical.

(16)

Setting
\varepsilon=\frac{1}{\Gamma L_{+}NT}
, for an updated constant
C>0
, we find

\displaystyle|{\cal{L}}(\bm{{\bm{h}}},\phi)-{\widehat{\cal{L}}}(\bm{{\bm{h}}},\phi)|\leq\sqrt{\frac{2{\cal{L}}({\bm{h}},\phi)B(D+\tau)}{NT}}+\frac{CB(D+\tau)}{NT},

(17)

where
D=(\mathcal{C}({\bm{\Phi}}_{\text{new}})+T\mathcal{C}({\bm{H}}))\log(\bar{C}\Gamma L_{+}NT)
following the above definition of
D_{\varepsilon}
.

Note that, the uniform concentration above also implies the identical bound for the minimizer of the empirical risk. Let
(\hat{\bm{{\bm{h}}}},\hat{\bm{\phi}})
be the minimizer of the empirical risk. Specifically, let
(\bm{{\bm{h}}}^{\star,\phi_{\text{frz}}},\phi_{\text{new}}^{\star,\phi_{\text{frz}}})
be the optimal hypothesis in
({\bm{H}},{\bm{\Phi}}_{\text{new}})
minimizing the population risk subject to using frozen feature extractor
\phi_{\text{frz}}
, that is,
{\cal{L}}(\bm{{\bm{h}}}^{\star,\phi_{\text{frz}}},\phi_{\text{new}}^{\star,\phi_{\text{frz}}}+\phi_{\text{frz}})={\cal{L}}^{\star}_{\phi_{\text{frz}}}
. Then, we note that

{\cal{L}}(\hat{\bm{{\bm{h}}}},\hat{\bm{\phi}})-{\widehat{\cal{L}}}(\hat{\bm{{\bm{h}}}},\hat{\bm{\phi}})\leq\sqrt{\frac{2{\cal{L}}(\hat{\bm{h}},\hat{\phi})B(D+\tau)}{NT}}+\frac{CB(D+\tau)}{NT},

{\widehat{\cal{L}}}(\bm{{\bm{h}}}^{\star,\phi_{\text{frz}}},\phi_{\text{new}}^{\star,\phi_{\text{frz}}}+\phi_{\text{frz}})-{\cal{L}}^{\star}_{\phi_{\text{frz}}}\leq\sqrt{\frac{2{\cal{L}}^{\star}_{\phi_{\text{frz}}}\cdot B(D+\tau)}{NT}}+\frac{CB(D+\tau)}{NT}.

Since
(\hat{\bm{h}},\hat{\phi})
minimizes the empirical risk,
{\widehat{\cal{L}}}(\hat{\bm{{\bm{h}}}},\hat{\bm{\phi}})\leq{\widehat{\cal{L}}}(\bm{{\bm{h}}}^{\star,\phi_{\text{frz}}},\phi_{\text{new}}^{\star,\phi_{\text{frz}}}+\phi_{\text{frz}})
. Then, we can obtain

{\cal{L}}(\hat{\bm{{\bm{h}}}},\hat{\bm{\phi}})-{\cal{L}}^{\star}_{\phi_{\text{frz}}}\leq\sqrt{\frac{2{\cal{L}}(\hat{\bm{h}},\hat{\phi})B(D+\tau)}{NT}}+\sqrt{\frac{2{\cal{L}}^{\star}_{\phi_{\text{frz}}}\cdot B(D+\tau)}{NT}}+\frac{2CB(D+\tau)}{NT}.

This in turn implies, for an updated constant
C^{\prime}=2C+0.5

\displaystyle\left(\sqrt{{\cal{L}}(\hat{\bm{{\bm{h}}}},\hat{\bm{\phi}})}-\sqrt{\frac{B(D+\tau)}{2NT}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq{\cal{L}}^{\star}_{\phi_{\text{frz}}}+\sqrt{\frac{2{\cal{L}}^{\star}_{\phi_{\text{frz}}}\cdot B(D+\tau)}{NT}}+\frac{C^{\prime}B(D+\tau)}{NT}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sqrt{{\cal{L}}^{\star}_{\phi_{\text{frz}}}}+\sqrt{\frac{C^{\prime}B(D+\tau)}{NT}}\right)^{2}.

Now, observe that if
{\cal{L}}(\hat{\bm{{\bm{h}}}},\hat{\bm{\phi}})\leq\frac{B(D+\tau)}{2NT}
, the statement of the theorem holds. Otherwise,
\sqrt{{\cal{L}}(\hat{\bm{{\bm{h}}}},\hat{\bm{\phi}})}-\sqrt{\frac{B(D+\tau)}{2NT}}\geq 0
and we can obtain
\sqrt{{\cal{L}}(\hat{\bm{{\bm{h}}}},\hat{\bm{\phi}})}\leq\sqrt{{\cal{L}}^{\star}_{\phi_{\text{frz}}}}+\left(\sqrt{C^{\prime}}+1\right)\sqrt{\frac{B(D+\tau)}{NT}}
. Taking the square of both sides concludes the proof of the main statement (first line). The second statement follows directly from the definition of mismatch, that is, using the fact that
\text{MM}_{\text{frz}}={\cal{L}}^{\star}_{\phi_{\text{frz}}}-{\cal{L}}^{\star}
.
