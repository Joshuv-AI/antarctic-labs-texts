Abstract
In [ 19 ] , Taslaman, Tisseur, and Zaballa show that any regular matrix polynomial P ⁡ ( λ ) P(\lambda) over an algebraically closed field is spectrally equivalent to a triangular matrix polynomial of the same degree. When P ⁡ ( λ ) P(\lambda) is real and regular, they also show that there is a real quasi-triangular matrix polynomial of the same degree that is spectrally equivalent to P ⁡ ( λ ) P(\lambda) , in which the diagonal blocks are of size at most 2 × 2 2\times 2 . This paper generalizes these results to regular matrix polynomials P ⁡ ( λ ) P(\lambda) over arbitrary fields 𝔽 \mathbb{F} , showing that any such P ⁡ ( λ ) P(\lambda) can be quasi-triangularized to a spectrally equivalent matrix polynomial over 𝔽 \mathbb{F} of the same degree, in which the largest diagonal block size is bounded by the highest degree appearing among all of the 𝔽 \mathbb{F} -irreducible factors in the Smith form for P ⁡ ( λ ) P(\lambda) .



Key words.
matrix polynomials, triangularization, arbitrary field, majorization, inverse problem, Mobius transformation.

1
Introduction

Triangularizations of matrix polynomials
that preserve degree as well as the finite and infinite spectral structure
via unimodular equivalence
are essentially Schur-like forms for matrices
whose entries are polynomials.
These have been achieved
over algebraically closed fields
for regular quadratic matrix polynomials in
[
20
]
,
and for regular matrix polynomials of arbitrary degree in
[
19
]
.
There are also some results in
[
19
]

on singular matrix polynomials, but these will not be addressed in this paper.
Also in
[
19
,
20
]
,
when the underlying field is
\mathbb{R}
,
the authors show how to produce quasi-triangularizations
with diagonal blocks of size at most 2
\times
2.

The goal of this paper is similar:
given a regular matrix polynomial
P(\lambda)
,
to show how to construct a regular quasi-triangular matrix polynomial
with the same finite and infinite spectral structure as
P(\lambda)
,
i.e., is spectrally equivalent to
P(\lambda)
,
and has the same degree as
P(\lambda)
.
However, this work is an extension of
[
19
]

in that the results presented here are for matrix polynomials over an
arbitrary field
.
In order to achieve this generalization, though, the possibility of diagonal blocks
of sizes even larger than 2
\times
2 must be allowed.
We show that a quasi-triangularization can always be constructed in which the sizes of the diagonal blocks
do not exceed
k\times k
,
where
k
is the highest degree
among all of the irreducible factors of the invariant polynomials
in the Smith form of the given polynomial matrix
P(\lambda)
.
Note that the term
quasi-triangular
is used throughout this paper
to refer to square matrices
that are block upper (or lower) triangular
with square blocks along the main diagonal,
at least one of which has size 2
\times
2 or larger.
A matrix is
k
-quasi-triangular

if the diagonal blocks are no larger than
k\times k
.

Here is a brief overview of the paper.
After some preliminary discussion of concepts, notation, and terminology
in Section
2
,
we begin in Section
3

by solving the quasi-triangular
realization problem

for finite spectral data over an arbitrary field
\mathbb{F}
.
That is, we take as starting point
a collection of finite spectral data rather than a matrix polynomial,
and show how to construct a strictly regular

k
-quasi-triangular matrix polynomial over
\mathbb{F}

having exactly the given spectral data;
here
k
is the largest degree
among the irreducible divisors of the given data.
The solution of this inverse problem
is the main technical result of the paper;
all other results depend on and follow from this.
The central idea of the proof is to take the given spectral data,
form the corresponding Smith form,
and then systematically “un-diagonalize” in a way that moves
the matrix polynomial toward the desired degree,
while maintaining quasi-triangularity.
This is a proof technique used in
[
8
]
,
and then developed by
[
19
]
;
some antecedents of this technique
can also be found in
[
13
]
.
We develop it further here,
adapting it to the arbitrary field setting.
Indeed, a number of nontrivial ingredients
go into proving this quasi-triangular realization result by this method —
majorization plays a role,
as well as a new combinatorial lemma
on the partitioning of integer multisets.
In Section
4

we extend this realization result
to include elementary divisors at
\infty

in the given spectral data.
In order to achieve this extension,
we use the well-known tool of Möbius transformations
[
12
]
,
although once again we will need to do a significant amount of work to adapt them
to work smoothly for matrix polynomials over arbitrary fields.
At this point the signature result of the paper —
the quasi-triangularization of any regular matrix polynomial
over an arbitrary field in Theorem
4.9
—
now follows easily.
Finally, in Section
5

we investigate conditions for describing when exact triangularization is possible
in the arbitrary field setting;
we also display several families of examples,
some that illustrate the sharpness of
k

as a general upper bound on the size of the diagonal blocks in quasi-triangularizations,
and others that show that this upper bound
k

can sometimes be a huge overestimate of the diagonal block size
that is actually attainable.

2
Preliminaries

In this paper, we deal with
polynomial matrices

(also referred to as
matrix polynomials
) over an arbitrary field
\mathbb{F}
,
i.e., matrices whose entries are polynomials with coefficients from
\mathbb{F}
.
In particular, we will be working only with
regular
matrix polynomials,
that is, square matrix polynomials with determinant
different from the zero polynomial.
A polynomial matrix is
unimodular
if it is regular
and has a (nonzero) constant determinant. Throughout the paper the set of natural numbers are denoted by
\mathbb{N}
,
and includes zero;

\mathbb{N}^{+}
is then the set of positive integers.

The Smith form is a canonical representation of matrix polynomials
under
unimodular equivalence
,
i.e., obtained by left and right multiplication
by unimodular matrix polynomials.
This form was first defined for integer matrices
[
18
]
.
We will use the extension given in
[
6
]
for matrix polynomials:

Theorem 2.1

(Smith form)
.

Let
P(\lambda)
be an
m\times n
matrix polynomial over an arbitrary field
\mathbb{F}
.
Then there exists
r\in\mathbb{N}
, and unimodular matrix polynomials
U(\lambda)
and
V(\lambda)

such that

U(\lambda)P(\lambda)V(\lambda)={\rm diag}(s_{1}(\lambda),\dots,s_{\mathop{\rm min}\{m,n\}}(\lambda))=:S(\lambda)

where
s_{i}(\lambda)\in\mathbb{F}[\lambda]
, for
i=1,\dots,\mathop{\rm min}\{m,n\}
,
s_{1}(\lambda),\dots,s_{r}(\lambda)
are monic,
s_{r+1}(\lambda),\dots,s_{\mathop{\rm min}\{m,n\}}(\lambda)
are identically-zero,
and
s_{i}(\lambda)
is a divisor of
s_{i+1}(\lambda)
for
i=1,\dots,r-1
.
Moreover, the number
r
is equal to the rank of
P
,
and the diagonal entries of the
m\times n
matrix polynomial
S(\lambda)

are uniquely determined by the multiplicative relations

s_{1}(\lambda)s_{2}(\lambda)\dotsb s_{j}(\lambda)\;=\;\gcd\bigl\{\,\text{all }j\times j\text{ minors of $P(\lambda)$\,}\bigr\}\,,\;\text{ for }\;j=1,\dotsc,r\,.

(2.1)

When
P(\lambda)
is regular, then
r=m=n
,
and
S(\lambda)
is a nonsingular diagonal matrix.

The diagonal matrix
S(\lambda)
featuring in this theorem is called the
Smith form
of
P(\lambda)
.
The nonzero diagonal entries of
S(\lambda)
are called the
invariant polynomials
of
P(\lambda)
,
and their zeros are the
finite eigenvalues
of
P(\lambda)
.
An invariant polynomial will be called
trivial

if it is identically equal to 1 and
nontrivial
otherwise.

A non-constant irreducible polynomial
\chi(\lambda)\in\mathbb{F}[\lambda]

that divides some invariant polynomial of
P(\lambda)

will be called an
irreducible divisor
of
P(\lambda)
.
This new concept and terminology is adopted in this work
because of the central role that will be played here by these objects.
Given an invariant polynomial
s_{i}(\lambda)
and an irreducible divisor
\chi(\lambda)
,
or indeed any irreducible polynomial
\chi(\lambda)
,
there is a unique natural number
\alpha_{i}
(perhaps zero) such that

s_{i}(\lambda)\,=\,\chi(\lambda)^{\alpha_{i}}\mkern 1.0mu\mkern 1.0mu\widehat{s}_{i}(\lambda)\,,

with
\widehat{s}_{i}(\lambda)
not divisible by
\chi(\lambda)
.
Any factor
\chi(\lambda)^{\alpha_{i}}
with
\alpha_{i}>0
is traditionally called
an
elementary divisor

[
7
]
of
P(\lambda)
.
The number
\alpha_{i}
, whether it is zero or nonzero,
is called the
partial multiplicity
of the irreducible
\chi(\lambda)

with respect to the invariant polynomial
s_{i}(\lambda)
,
while the sequence
(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dotsc,\alpha_{r})
,
with
0\leq\alpha_{1}\leq\alpha_{2}\leq\dotsb\leq\alpha_{r}
,
is called the
partial multiplicity sequence
of
P(\lambda)
at
\chi(\lambda)
,
which we will denote by

{\cal P}\!{\cal M}(P,\chi):=\,(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dotsc,\alpha_{r})\,.

(2.2)

Note that
{\cal P}\!{\cal M}(P,\chi)
may consist of all zeroes,
and
\chi
is an irreducible divisor of
P
exactly when some
\alpha_{i}
is nonzero.
The partial multiplicity sequence of a degree one irreducible divisor
\chi(\lambda)=\lambda-\lambda_{0}

is also sometimes referred to as the partial multiplicity sequence of the eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}
.
An abbreviated notation,
{\cal P}\!{\cal M}(\chi)
, will also be used for the partial multiplicity sequence associated to
\chi(\lambda)
.
This may be used when the underlying matrix polynomial is understood,
but more commonly it will be employed when there is
no
matrix polynomial
in the background at all,
and the sequence being specified (and its association with
\chi
)
is part of a collection of given spectral data
that is yet to be realized.
To emphasize this role of being input data for a realization problem,
we will sometimes instead write
{\cal P}\!{\cal M}_{\text{given}}(\chi)
.

In using the notation (
2.2
),
it is very useful to
abandon
a standard convention,
i.e., the convention that only
monic
irreducibles should be allowed or considered.
As far as partial multiplicities are concerned,
there is no important difference between an irreducible
\chi(\lambda)

and any nonzero scalar multiple
c\chi(\lambda)
.
In particular, since for any invariant polynomial
s_{i}(\lambda)
we have

s_{i}(\lambda)\,=\,\chi(\lambda)^{\alpha_{i}}\,\mkern 1.0mu\widehat{s}_{i}(\lambda)\,=\,\bigl(c\chi(\lambda)\bigr)^{\alpha_{i}}\,\mkern 1.0mu\frac{\,\widehat{s}_{i}(\lambda)\,}{c^{\alpha_{i}}}\,=\,\bigl(c\chi(\lambda)\bigr)^{\alpha_{i}}\,\mkern 1.0mu\widetilde{s}_{i}(\lambda)

where
c\chi
and
\widetilde{s_{i}}
are coprime,
we see that the definition of the partial multiplicity sequence

(\alpha_{1},\dotsc,\alpha_{r})

is unaffected by allowing non-monic irreducibles.
Thus we may consistently say that

{\cal P}\!{\cal M}(P,c\chi)\;=\;{\cal P}\!{\cal M}(P,\chi)

(2.3)

for any nonzero scalar
c\in\mathbb{F}
,
and thereby simply ignore whether an irreducible is monic or not.
In line with this,
we will say that two
\mathbb{F}
-irreducible polynomials are

distinct irreducibles

if neither is a nonzero scalar multiple of the other.

Remark 2.2
.

In effect we are implicitly defining an equivalence relation on the set
of
\mathbb{F}
-irreducible polynomials.
That is, two irreducibles are equivalent
(with respect to partial multiplicity sequences)
if one is a nonzero scalar multiple of the other.
Distinct irreducibles are then just representatives
of different equivalence classes under this relation.

Remark 2.3
.

It is important to keep in mind that there are fields
\mathbb{F}

that support the presence of
\mathbb{F}
-irreducible polynomials
of arbitrarily high degree.
A simple example of this is the field
\mathbb{Q}
of rational numbers.
Using the Eisenstein criterion
[
2
]
,
it is easy to see that the polynomial
\,x^{n}+p\,

is
\mathbb{Q}
-irreducible for any prime number
p\geq 2
and any
n\geq 1
.

Definition 2.4
.

The
finite spectral structure
of
P(\lambda)

refers to any of the following equivalent (and easily inter-convertible) collections of data:

(a)

the set of all distinct irreducible divisors of
P(\lambda)
,
each equipped with their partial multiplicity sequence,

(b)

the multiset of all elementary divisors of
P(\lambda)
,
together with the number
\mathop{\rm rank}P
,

(c)

the multiset of all invariant polynomials of
P(\lambda)
, including the trivial ones.

(Recall that a multiset is like a set, but with repetitions allowed,
i.e., a “set with multiplicities”
[
10
]
.)

Remark 2.5
.

Note that the rank of a matrix polynomial is encoded
in each partial multiplicity sequence by its length.

Some regular matrix polynomials have structure that
is not completely captured by their finite spectral structure alone.
To get the full story for these matrix polynomials,
it is also necessary to include their “spectral structure at
\infty
”.
For this,
some additional terminology is needed.
The
grade
of a matrix polynomial
P(\lambda)
is a natural number
g

such that

P(\lambda)\,=\,P_{0}+P_{1}\lambda+\dotsb+P_{g}\lambda^{g}\,,

with each
P_{j}\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times n}
.
Note, however, that unlike in the definition of the degree
d
of
P(\lambda)
,
there is no requirement here for the leading coefficient
P_{g}
to be nonzero.
Thus we see that
g\geq d
always holds,
no matter what the choice of grade might be.
And we emphasize that, in contrast with degree, grade is indeed a choice,
although a very common choice is for
g
to be taken to be equal to
d
.
The
grade
g
reversal
of
P(\lambda)
is the matrix polynomial

(\textstyle{\mathop{\rm rev}_{g}}P)(\lambda)\,:=\;\lambda^{g}P(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\lambda}}),

and
P(\lambda)
has an
eigenvalue at infinity

if
(\mathop{\rm rev}_{g}P)(\lambda)
has an eigenvalue at zero.
Moreover, the partial multiplicity sequence
of the eigenvalue at infinity for
P(\lambda)

is, by definition,
identical to the partial multiplicity sequence
of the eigenvalue zero for
(\mathop{\rm rev}_{g}P)(\lambda)
.
We will use
{\cal P}\!{\cal M}(P,\infty)
as temporary notation
for the partial multiplicity sequence of
P
at
\infty
,
so that this definition can be expressed as

{\cal P}\!{\cal M}(P,\infty)\,:=\;{\cal P}\!{\cal M}(\textstyle{\mathop{\rm rev}_{g}}P,\lambda)\,.

However, later in Section
4

we will have reason to change this notation
to something that is more consistent with the notation in (
2.2
),
and also works more smoothly with Möbius transformations
and their properties.

Definition 2.6
.

The
infinite spectral structure
of
P(\lambda)

refers to the eigenvalue at infinity (if it exists),
together with its partial multiplicity sequence.

The relationship of grade to degree
(e.g., whether
g=d
or
g>d
)
is reflected in the infinite spectral structure,
in particular in the first partial multiplicity at
\infty
.
Note that some of the following result
appeared previously in
[
3
, Lemma 2.17]
,
but without proof.

Lemma 2.7
.

Suppose
P(\lambda)
is any
m\times n
matrix polynomial over a field
\,\mathbb{F}
,
with rank
r
, degree
d
, and grade
g
.
Let
(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dotsc,\alpha_{r})

with
0\leq\alpha_{1}\leq\alpha_{2}\leq\dotsb\leq\alpha_{r}

be the partial multiplicity sequence of
P
at
\infty
.
Then

\alpha_{1}\,=\,g-d\,,

(2.4)

and hence
\,g=d\,
if and only if
\,\alpha_{1}=0
.

(
Equivalently,
g>d
if and only if
\alpha_{1}>0
.
)

Furthermore, suppose
\widetilde{P}(\lambda)
is an
m\times n
matrix polynomial
that is entry-wise identical to
P(\lambda)
,
but its grade has been chosen to be equal to its degree
d
.
Then the partial multiplicity sequences at
\infty
of
P(\lambda)
and
\widetilde{P}(\lambda)

are related by a constant “shift” of
g-d
,
that is,

{\cal P}\!{\cal M}(P,\infty)\;=\;{\cal P}\!{\cal M}(\widetilde{P},\infty)\,+\,(g-d)\cdot(1,1,\dotsc,1)\,.

(2.5)

Proof.

Begin by expressing
P(\lambda)=P_{g}\lambda^{g}+\dotsb+P_{d}\lambda^{d}+\dotsb+P_{1}\lambda+P_{0}
,
so that
\widetilde{P}(\lambda)=P_{d}\lambda^{d}+\dotsb+P_{1}\lambda+P_{0}
,
with
P_{d}\neq 0
and
P_{j}=0
for the
g-d
leading coefficients
with
j=d+1,\dotsc,g
.
Then the reversals of
P
and
\widetilde{P}
are related by

\textstyle{\mathop{\rm rev}_{g}}P\,=\,\lambda^{g}P\bigl(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\lambda}}\bigr)\,=\,\lambda^{g-d}\lambda^{d}P\bigl(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\lambda}}\bigr)\,=\,\lambda^{g-d}\bigl(\textstyle{\mathop{\rm rev}_{d}}\widetilde{P}\bigr)\,.

(2.6)

Note that the constant term of
\mathop{\rm rev}_{d}\widetilde{P}
is the
nonzero
matrix
P_{d}
.

Next observe that for any constant
\mu_{0}\in\mathbb{F}

and any matrix polynomial
Q(\lambda)
over
\mathbb{F}
,
the number of initial
zero
partial multiplicities in the sequence
{\cal P}\!{\cal M}(Q,\chi)

with
\chi(\lambda)=\lambda-\mu_{0}
is equal to
\mathop{\rm rank}Q(\mu_{0})
.
This can be easily seen from the Smith form

U(\lambda)Q(\lambda)V(\lambda)\;=\;\widehat{S}(\lambda)\,

(2.7)

by evaluating (
2.7
) at
\mu_{0}
,
then computing ranks to get

\mathop{\rm rank}\bigl(Q(\mu_{0})\bigr)\,=\,\mathop{\rm rank}\bigl(\widehat{S}(\mu_{0})\bigr)
.
But any diagonal entry of
\widehat{S}(\lambda)
corresponding
to a nonzero partial multiplicity of
\lambda-\mu_{0}

will be zeroed out in
\widehat{S}(\mu_{0})
,
so the remaining nonzero diagonal entries of
\widehat{S}(\mu_{0})
,
there are
\mathop{\rm rank}\widehat{S}(\mu_{0})=\mathop{\rm rank}Q(\mu_{0})
many of them,
will count the zero partial multiplicities of
\lambda-\mu_{0}
for
Q
.
Applying this to
Q(\lambda)=\mathop{\rm rev}_{d}\widetilde{P}
, we see that

\displaystyle(\text{no. of zero partial multiplicities for $\widetilde{P}$ at $\infty$})

\displaystyle\;=\;(\text{no.\ of zero partial multiplicities for $\textstyle{\mathop{\rm rev}_{d}}\widetilde{P}$ at $(\lambda-0)$})

\displaystyle\;=\;\mathop{\rm rank}\bigl(\textstyle{\mathop{\rm rev}_{d}}\widetilde{P}(0)\bigr)\;=\;\mathop{\rm rank}P_{d}\,>\,0\,,

since
P_{d}
is nonzero.
Thus
{\cal P}\!{\cal M}(\widetilde{P},\infty):={\cal P}\!{\cal M}\bigl(\textstyle{\mathop{\rm rev}_{d}}\widetilde{P},(\lambda-0)\bigr)=(0,\dotsc)
.
Hence in the Smith form
\widetilde{S}(\lambda)
for
\mathop{\rm rev}_{d}\widetilde{P}
,
i.e.,
\widetilde{S}(\lambda)=\mathop{\rm diag}(\widetilde{s}_{1}(\lambda),\widetilde{s}_{2}(\lambda),\dotsc)
,
we see that
\widetilde{s}_{1}(\lambda)
must be coprime to

.
Now from (

) it follows that the Smith form

Hence we can immmediately conclude that

,

) holds.

The first major result in this paper will be concerned
with a special class of regular matrix polynomials,
as in the following definition.

Definition 2.8
.

Regular matrix polynomials that have
no
infinite spectral structure at all
will be referred to as
strictly regular
.

For a regular
n\times n
matrix polynomial
P(\lambda)
of grade
g
,
being strictly regular is equivalent
to the leading coefficient
P_{g}
being
nonsingular

(and hence necessarily also that
g=d
),
or equivalently to
\deg\bigl(\mathop{\rm det}P(\lambda)\bigr)
being equal to
gn
.

Definition 2.9
.

The
complete spectral data
of a matrix polynomial
P(\lambda)

is the combination of the finite and infinite spectral structures.
Two regular matrix polynomials with the same complete spectral data
are said to be
spectrally equivalent
.

The notion of spectral equivalence was introduced in
[
3
]
as a way to compare matrix polynomials,
both regular and singular, even if they have different sizes or degrees.
Although the definition given in
[
3
]

is quite different than Definition
2.9
,
it was shown in
[
3
]

that for regular polynomials being spectrally equivalent
is the same as having the same complete spectral data.
Note that in
[
19
]
, having the same complete spectral data
is termed
strongly equivalent
.

It is important to keep firmly in mind the contrast between
unimodular equivalence and spectral equivalence.
Unimodular equivalence preserves all finite spectral structure,
but carries with it the unfortunate possibility of altering
any infinite spectral structure that might be present.
We will see a concrete illustration of this phenomenon
in Example
4.8
,
but a more detailed discussion
of the possible effects of unimodular transformations
on infinite spectral structure
can be found in
[
3
, Sect. 4.3]
.
In this paper the ultimate aim is to produce quasi-triangularizations
that are spectrally equivalent to the given matrix polynomial,
and not just unimodularly equivalent.

The following theorem, dubbed the Index Sum Theorem in
[
3
]
,
appeared for the first time in
[
21
]
for the complex field,
and in
[
16
]
and
[
17
]
for the real field.
This theorem describes the fundamental relationship
between the grade, rank, and structural data
(elementary divisors together with minimal indices)
of
any
matrix polynomial,
and was recently extended to arbitrary fields in
[
3
]
.
Here we state the result only for regular matrix polynomials over an arbitrary field;
the result in its full generality can be found in
[
3
]
.
For more on the history of this theorem,
as well as its connection to other fundamental results, see
[
1
]
.

Theorem 2.10

(Index Sum Theorem for Regular Matrix Polynomials)
.

Let
P(\lambda)
be a regular
n\times n
matrix polynomial of degree
d
and grade
g

having the the complete spectral data:

•

invariant polynomials
p_{j}(\lambda)
of degrees
\delta_{j}
, for
j=1,\dots,n
,

•

infinite partial multiplicities
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{n}
,

where some of the degrees or partial multiplicities can be zero.
Then the index sum
\sigma
satisfies the relation

\sigma\,:=\;\sum_{j=1}^{n}\delta_{j}\,+\,\sum_{j=1}^{n}\gamma_{j}\;=\;gn\,.

(2.8)

If
P(\lambda)
is strictly regular,
so that all of the
\gamma_{i}
’s are zero and
g=d
,
then (
2.8
) simplifies to just the relation

\sigma\;=\;\sum_{j=1}^{n}\delta_{j}\;=\;dn\,.

(2.9)

The index sum theorem plays a key role in the following theorem,
which is a very special case of a much more general result in
[
4
]
.
The corollary immediately following it will be used at a key moment in the proof of our first main result, Theorem

.

,
such that

.
Then there exists a strictly regular

matrix polynomial

\,\mathbb{F}
with degree

and invariant polynomials

,
if and only if (

) holds.

Corollary 2.12
.

Let
\,\mathbb{F}
be an arbitrary field,
and
Q(\lambda)
a regular
n\times n
polynomial matrix over
\,\mathbb{F}
.
Suppose that
\sigma:=\deg\bigl(\mathop{\rm det}Q(\lambda)\bigr)
is divisible by
n
,
in particular that
\sigma=dn
.
Then there is a strictly regular
n\times n
matrix polynomial
P(\lambda)
over
\,\mathbb{F}

of degree
d

that is unimodularly equivalent to
Q(\lambda)
.

Proof.

Let
q_{1}(\lambda),\dots,q_{n}(\lambda)
be the invariant polynomials of
Q(\lambda)
,
with degrees
\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{n}
, respectively.
Then from the Smith form of
Q(\lambda)

we know that
\sum_{j=1}^{n}\delta_{j}=\sigma=dn
,
i.e., that (
2.9
) holds.
Thus by Theorem
2.11

there exists a strictly regular
n\times n
matrix polynomial
P(\lambda)

over
\,\mathbb{F}
with degree
d
and the same invariant polynomials as
Q(\lambda)
.
Now since
P(\lambda)
and
Q(\lambda)
have the same Smith form,
they must be unimodularly equivalent.
∎

An important concept, used in
[
4
]
and
[
19
]

for handling regular matrix polynomials that have nontrivial infinite spectral structure,
is that of Möbius transformations of matrix polynomials.
These transformations and their properties were studied in
[
12
]
,
and will be important for the results in Section
4
.

Definition 2.13

(Möbius transformations of matrix polynomials)
.

Let
P(\lambda)
be a matrix polynomial of grade
g
over the field
\mathbb{F}
,
and suppose
A=\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]\in\mathbb{F}^{2\times 2}

is nonsingular.
The matrix polynomial

\mathbf{M}_{A}(P)(\lambda)\,:=\;(c\lambda+d)^{g}P\left(\frac{a\lambda+b}{c\lambda+d}\right)

(2.10)

is the
Möbius transformation
of
P(\lambda)
with respect to
A
.

Example 2.14
.

Note that the grade
g
reversal operation is an example of a Möbius transformation,
specifically
\,\mathop{\rm rev}_{g}P=\mathbf{M}_{R}(P)\,
for

\,R=\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]
.

For conceptual clarity,
it is important to keep in mind that for any fixed field
\mathbb{F}
and grade
g
,
the formula in (
2.10
)
actually defines a whole
family
of transformations,
one for each matrix size
m\times n
.
Once one has fixed the underlying field
\mathbb{F}
, grade
g
,
input matrix size
m\times n
, and matrix
A
,
then it is known
[
12
]

that the Möbius transformation
\mathbf{M}_{A}
defines a
bijection

on the set of all
m\times n
, grade
g
matrix polynomials over
\mathbb{F}
.
However, even by staying within the confines of one of these bijections,
the transformation
\mathbf{M}_{A}(P)
does
not
in general preserve the degree of the input
P
;
degree may increase, decrease, or remain unchanged
[
12
]
.
This lack of degree preservation happens even for the simplest size matrix polynomials,
i.e., for scalar (
1\times 1
) polynomials.
However, for scalar polynomials the following lemma describes
one simple scenario, important for this paper,
where degree preservation by Möbius transformations is guaranteed.
The proof of this result will be postponed
until Section
4.1
,
where a number of results about the properties of Möbius transformations
and their effect on partial multiplicity sequences will be developed.

Lemma 2.15
.

Suppose
\mathbb{F}
is an arbitrary field,
and
\chi(\lambda)
is any
\mathbb{F}
-irreducible scalar polynomial with
\deg\chi\geq 2
.
Let
\mathbf{M}_{A}
be the Möbius transformation
associated with any

A=\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]\in GL(2,\mathbb{F})
.
Then with
\mathop{\rm grade}(\chi)
taken to be equal to
\deg(\chi)
,
the transformation
\mathbf{M}_{A}
preserves both the degree and the
\mathbb{F}
-irreducibility of
\chi
.
That is,
\mathbf{M}_{A}(\chi)
is
\mathbb{F}
-irreducible,
and
\,\deg\bigl(\mathbf{M}_{A}(\chi)\bigr)=\deg(\chi)
.

3
Quasi-triangular realization of finite spectral data

Our first result is the construction of a quasi-triangular realization
of a given list of finite spectral data,
with a choice of degree that is compatible with the index sum theorem.

Theorem 3.1

(Quasi-Triangular Realization: Strictly Regular Case)
.

Suppose a list of
m
nonconstant monic polynomials
s_{1}(\lambda),\dots,s_{m}(\lambda)

over an arbitrary field
\,\mathbb{F}
is given,
satisfying the divisibility chain condition

s_{1}(\lambda)\,|\,s_{2}(\lambda)\,|\,\dotsb\,|\,s_{m}(\lambda)
.
Let
\sigma:=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\deg\bigl(s_{i}(\lambda)\bigr)
,
and define
k
to be the maximum degree among all of the
\mathbb{F}
-irreducible factors
of the polynomials
s_{i}(\lambda)
for
i=1,\dotsc,m
.
Then for any choice of nonzero
d,n\in\mathbb{N}

such that
n\geq m
and
dn=\sigma
,
there exists an
n\times n
, degree
d
, strictly regular matrix polynomial
Q(\lambda)
over
\mathbb{F}

that is
k
-quasi-triangular,
and has exactly the given polynomials
s_{1}(\lambda),\dots,s_{m}(\lambda)

as its nontrivial invariant polynomials,
together with
n-m
trivial invariant polynomials. In addition,
Q(\lambda)
can always be chosen so that the degree
of every entry in any off-diagonal block of
Q(\lambda)

is strictly less than
d
.

Note that the two conditions in this theorem,
i.e., that
n\geq m
and
dn=\sigma
,
are both necessary conditions for
any
strictly regular realization
of the given data,
whether that realization is quasi-triangular or not.
The restriction
n\geq m
simply says that the realization (and its Smith form)
needs to be big enough to accommodate all of the nontrivial invariant polynomials.
The condition
dn=\sigma
is simply the Index Sum Theorem
in the form (
2.9
) required of any strictly regular realization
of data with the given
\sigma
.

Now before embarking on the extensive technical details
of the proof of Theorem
3.1
,
which will occupy our attention for the rest of Section
3
,
it will be helpful to give a brief idea of the overall strategy
of the argument.
The first step in our process of quasi-triangular realization
is to construct the Smith form corresponding to the given data
and the choice of
n
, i.e.,

S(\lambda)=I_{(n-m)\times(n-m)}\oplus\mathop{\rm diag}\{s_{1}(\lambda),s_{2}(\lambda),\dotsc,s_{m}(\lambda)\}\,.

We take this as our starting point,
and begin changing
S(\lambda)
by unimodular transformations,
“un-diagonalizing” it and
slowly turning it into the desired quasi-triangularization.
The first phase of this un-diagonalization of the Smith form
aims to shift irreducible factors around on the diagonal,
in such a way as to try to make the degrees
of all of the diagonal entries
as close to the target degree
d
as possible. This is a proof idea pioneered in
[
8
]
,
and developed further in
[
19
]
.
Although the diagonal form is sacrificed, in this first stage
at least upper triangularity is maintained.

Now for some collections of spectral data,
this phase may succeed in making the diagonal entries
all have exactly the target degree
d
;
in this case a triangularization is obtained.
As shown in
[
19
]
, this can always be achieved
when the underlying field is algebraically closed.
However, for arbitrary fields we have to be satisfied with something less.
The best that can be achieved in general is to rearrange the irreducible factors
along the diagonal so that no two diagonal entries differ in degree by more than
k

(the maximum degree among all of the irreducible divisors of the given spectral data),
and so that the vector of diagonal entries can be grouped
into contiguous blocks
D_{1},\dotsc,D_{\ell}
for some
\ell\leq n
, each of size at most
k
,
where the
average
degree of the entries in each
D_{j}

is exactly the target degree
d
.

In the second phase of construction,
the upper triangular matrix attained so far
is partitioned into blocks,
with square (upper triangular) blocks down the diagonal
that correspond to the contiguous blocks
D_{j}

of diagonal entries just mentioned.
Each of these diagonal blocks is now “un-triangularized”
so that it has exactly degree
d
.
We now have a quasi-triangular realization
in which all of the diagonal blocks have degree
d
,
but the off-diagonal blocks have been left uncontrolled,
and may still have degree larger than
d
.

The final step, then, is to visit each of the off-diagonal blocks
in a “sweep by super-diagonals pattern”,
using matrix polynomial division
to force the degree of all of the off-diagonal blocks to be strictly less than
d
.
This completes the construction of a
k
-quasi-triangular realization of degree
d

for the given spectral data.

Example 3.2
.

Throughout this paper, we will keep returning to a single illustrative example,
continually developing it as we go. To start, consider the finite spectral data over the two-element field
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{Z}_{2}
,
consisting of the invariant polynomials

s_{1}(\lambda)=\phi\psi\,,\quad s_{2}(\lambda)=\chi\phi\psi\,,\quad s_{3}(\lambda)=\chi^{2}\phi\psi^{2}\,,\quad s_{4}(\lambda)=\chi^{3}\phi\psi^{2}\,,\;\text{ and }\;s_{5}(\lambda)=\chi^{3}\phi^{3}\psi^{4}\,,

where
\chi(\lambda)=\lambda^{4}+\lambda^{3}+1
,
\phi(\lambda)=\lambda^{2}+\lambda+1
,
and
\psi(\lambda)=\lambda
are
\mathbb{F}
-irreducible.
The sum of the degrees of these invariant polynomials is
\sigma=60
,
so we may choose the target degree to be
d=10
.
As a consequence, the size of the realization must be
6\times 6
,
and so the Smith form for our given data is

S(\lambda)=\mathop{\rm diag}\{1,s_{1}(\lambda),s_{2}(\lambda),s_{3}(\lambda),s_{4}(\lambda),s_{5}(\lambda)\}\,.

The irreducible divisors together with their associated partial multiplicity sequences are

\displaystyle{\cal P}\!{\cal M}_{\text{given}}(\chi)

\displaystyle\,=\,(0,0,1,2,3,3)

\displaystyle{\cal P}\!{\cal M}_{\text{given}}(\phi)

\displaystyle\,=\,(0,1,1,1,1,3)

\displaystyle{\cal P}\!{\cal M}_{\text{given}}(\psi)

\displaystyle\,=\,(0,1,1,2,2,4)\,,

which provides an alternative way to present the given finite spectral data.

3.1
Coprime partitions, factor-counting vectors, and the unimodular transfer lemma

In this section we develop the tools needed to implement the first phase
of the construction of a quasi-triangular realization
from given finite spectral data.
That is, we will see how it is possible
to rearrange irreducible factors along the diagonal
via unimodular equivalence,
while at the same time maintaining upper triangularity.
However, before we present these tools,
some additional concepts and terminology will be required.

Let
{\cal M}
be the multiset of all of the
\mathbb{F}
-irreducible factors of a scalar polynomial
p(\lambda)
.
A partition of
{\cal M}
into a disjoint union
{\cal M}={\cal F}\sqcup{\cal G}

of multisets
{\cal F}
and
{\cal G}

is called a
coprime partition
if every
f\in{\cal F}
is coprime to every
g\in{\cal G}
.
This is equivalent to saying that there is no irreducible factor of
p

that appears in both
{\cal F}
and
{\cal G}
.
Given such a coprime partition, we can uniquely factor
p(\lambda)
into

p(\lambda)\;=\;p_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}}}(\lambda)\cdot p_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal G}}}(\lambda)\,,

where
p_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}}}(\lambda)
denotes the product
of all of the
\mathbb{F}
-irreducible factors in
p(\lambda)
from
\mathcal{F}
,
and
p_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal G}}}(\lambda)
denotes the product
of all of the
\mathbb{F}
-irreducible factors in
p(\lambda)
from
\mathcal{G}
.
We also denote by
|p(\lambda)|_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}}}
the total
number
of
\mathbb{F}
-irreducible factors
from
\mathcal{F}
in
p(\lambda)
, with a similar meaning for
|p(\lambda)|_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal G}}}
.
Note that
|p_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}}}(\lambda)|_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}}}=|p(\lambda)|_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}}}
.

If
\mathbf{v}(\lambda)
is a polynomial
n
-vector
and
{\cal F}\sqcup{\cal G}
is a coprime partition of the multiset
of all of the
\mathbb{F}
-irreducible factors of all of the entries of
\mathbf{v}(\lambda)
,
then the integer vector

|\mathbf{v}(\lambda)|_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}}}\,:=\;\bigl(|v_{1}(\lambda)|_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}}},\dotsc,|v_{n}(\lambda)|_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}}}\bigr)

is the
factor-counting vector

of
\mathbf{v}(\lambda)
with respect to
{\cal F}
.
Also useful is the integer
degree vector

\deg\mathbf{v}:=\,\bigl(\deg v_{1}(\lambda),\dotsc,\deg v_{n}(\lambda)\bigr)\,.

Finally, given an
n\times n
matrix polynomial
P(\lambda)
,
its main diagonal vector
\mathbf{p}(\lambda):=\mathop{\rm diag}P(\lambda)
,
and a coprime partition
\mathcal{F}\sqcup\mathcal{G}

of the multiset
\mathcal{M}
of all of the
\mathbb{F}
-irreducible factors
of the entries of
\mathbf{p}(\lambda)
,
we define the
diagonal factor-counting vector
of
P(\lambda)

with respect to
\mathcal{F}
to be the integer vector

\mathbf{d}_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}}}(P)\,:=\;|\mathbf{p}(\lambda)|_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}}}\,.

(3.1)

With these concepts, terminology, and notation in hand,
we can now describe and develop the tool for transferring irreducible factors
along the diagonal of an upper triangular matrix polynomial.

Lemma 3.3

(
2\times 2
Unimodular Transfer Lemma)
.

Let

T(\lambda)\,=\,\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}p(\lambda)&q(\lambda)\\
0&r(\lambda)\end{array}\right]

be a regular
2\times 2
upper triangular matrix polynomial
over an arbitrary field
\mathbb{F}
.
Let
\mathcal{F}\sqcup\mathcal{G}
be any coprime partition
of the multiset of all of the
\mathbb{F}
-irreducible factors
in the product
p(\lambda)r(\lambda)
. Let
m=|p|_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}}}
and
n=|r|_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}}}
so that
\mathbf{d}_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}}}(T)=(m,n)
.
Then for any
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{N}

such that
\alpha+\beta=m+n
and
\mathop{\rm min}\{m,n\}\leq\alpha,\beta\leq\mathop{\rm max}\{m,n\}
,
there exists a regular upper triangular matrix polynomial
\widetilde{T}(\lambda)

of the form

\widetilde{T}(\lambda)=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\widetilde{p}_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}}}(\lambda)\cdot p_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal G}}}(\lambda)&\widetilde{q}(\lambda)\\
0&\widetilde{r}_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}}}(\lambda)\cdot r_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal G}}}(\lambda)\end{array}\right]

(3.2)

with
\mathbf{d}_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}}}(\widetilde{T})=(\alpha,\beta)
,
such that
T(\lambda)
is unimodularly equivalent to
\widetilde{T}(\lambda)
.

Proof.

If
m=n
there is nothing to do, so assume that
m\neq n
.
Let
g(\lambda):=\gcd\bigl\{p(\lambda),q(\lambda),r(\lambda)\bigr\}
,
and factor

p(\lambda)=g(\lambda)\cdot\widehat{p}(\lambda)\mbox{$\quad\mbox{ and }\quad$}r(\lambda)=g(\lambda)\cdot\widehat{r}(\lambda)\,.

(3.3)

Then the Smith form of
T(\lambda)

is
\mathop{\rm diag}\{g(\lambda),g(\lambda)\,\widehat{p}(\lambda)\,\widehat{r}(\lambda)\}
,
so any
\widetilde{T}
as in (
3.2
) that we construct that has this Smith form
will be unimodularly equivalent to
T
.
Here is how to construct many such
\widetilde{T}
’s.

Begin by refining the factorizations of
p
and
r
in (
3.3
),
in a way that is compatible with the given coprime partition
{\cal F}\sqcup{\cal G}
:

\displaystyle p(\lambda)=g(\lambda)\cdot\widehat{p}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\bigl(g_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}}}(\lambda)g_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal G}}}(\lambda)\bigr)\cdot\bigl(\widehat{p}_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}}}(\lambda)\widehat{p}_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal G}}}(\lambda)\bigr)

\displaystyle=\bigl(g_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}}}(\lambda)\widehat{p}_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}}}(\lambda)\bigr)\cdot\bigl(g_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal G}}}(\lambda)\widehat{p}_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal G}}}(\lambda)\bigr)=p_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}}}(\lambda)\cdot p_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal G}}}(\lambda)

(3.4)

and

\displaystyle r(\lambda)=g(\lambda)\cdot\widehat{r}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\bigl(g_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}}}(\lambda)g_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal G}}}(\lambda)\bigr)\cdot\bigl(\widehat{r}_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}}}(\lambda)\widehat{r}_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal G}}}(\lambda)\bigr)

\displaystyle=\bigl(g_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}}}(\lambda)\widehat{r}_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}}}(\lambda)\bigr)\cdot\bigl(g_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal G}}}(\lambda)\widehat{r}_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal G}}}(\lambda)\bigr)=r_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}}}(\lambda)\cdot r_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal G}}}(\lambda).

(3.5)

Since we wish to leave
p_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal G}}}(\lambda)
and
r_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal G}}}(\lambda)

completely undisturbed in going from
T
to
\widetilde{T}
,
and also to have
g(\lambda)
present in both diagonal entries of
\widetilde{T}

in order to preserve the Smith form,
this means that the only room for maneuvering
is with the factors in
\widehat{p}_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}}}(\lambda)
and
\widehat{r}_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}}}(\lambda)
.
So let
a(\lambda)
and
b(\lambda)
be
any
two polynomials
(including possibly
a\equiv 1
or
b\equiv 1
) over
\mathbb{F}

such that

a(\lambda)\cdot b(\lambda)\,=\,\widehat{p}_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}}}(\lambda)\cdot\widehat{r}_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}}}(\lambda)\,,

(3.6)

and consider the polynomial matrix

\widetilde{T}(\lambda)\,=\,\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}g_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}}}(\lambda)a(\lambda)\cdot p_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal G}}}(\lambda)&g(\lambda)\\
0&g_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}}}(\lambda)b(\lambda)\cdot r_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal G}}}(\lambda)\end{array}\right]\,=\,\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}g(\lambda)\cdot a(\lambda)\widehat{p}_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal G}}}(\lambda)&g(\lambda)\\
0&g(\lambda)\cdot b(\lambda)\widehat{r}_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal G}}}(\lambda)\end{array}\right]\,,

(3.7)

of the form in (
3.2
)
with
\widetilde{p}_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}}}(\lambda)=g_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}}}(\lambda)a(\lambda)
,
\widetilde{q}(\lambda)=g(\lambda)
,
and
\widetilde{r}_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}}}(\lambda)=g_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}}}(\lambda)b(\lambda)
.
Then the
\gcd
of the entries of
\widetilde{T}
is easily seen to be
g(\lambda)
,
so the Smith form of
\widetilde{T}(\lambda)
is

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm diag}\left\{g(\lambda),\,g(\lambda)\bigl[a(\lambda)b(\lambda)\bigr]\!\cdot\bigl[\widehat{p}_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal G}}}(\lambda)\widehat{r}_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal G}}}(\lambda)\bigr]\right\}

\displaystyle=\,\mathop{\rm diag}\left\{g(\lambda),\,g(\lambda)\bigl[\widehat{p}_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}}}(\lambda)\widehat{r}_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}}}(\lambda)\bigr]\!\cdot\bigl[\widehat{p}_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal G}}}(\lambda)\widehat{r}_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal G}}}(\lambda)\bigr]\right\}

\displaystyle=\,\mathop{\rm diag}\left\{g(\lambda),\,g(\lambda)\widehat{p}(\lambda)\widehat{r}(\lambda)\right\}\,,

which is identical to the Smith form of
T(\lambda)
.
Thus for any choice of
a(\lambda)
and
b(\lambda)
in (
3.6
),
we have
\widetilde{T}\sim T
.
Letting
\delta=\lvert\,g(\lambda)\,\rvert_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}}}
, we see that
the diagonal factor-counting vector
\mathbf{d}_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}}}(\widetilde{T})

for
\widetilde{T}(\lambda)
in (
3.7
)
can be
(\alpha,\beta)
for any
\alpha+\beta=m+n

with
\delta\leq\alpha,\beta\leq m+n-\delta
.
Since

\delta\,\leq\,\mathop{\rm min}(m,n)\,\leq\,\mathop{\rm max}(m,n)\,\leq\,m+n-\delta\,,

any
(\alpha,\beta)
pair given in the statement of the lemma
is always achievable for
\mathbf{d}_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}}}(\widetilde{T})
.
∎

This lemma for
2\times 2
triangular matrices
can now be used on a triangular matrix of any size
to “transfer” irreducible factors belonging to a family
\mathcal{F}

between any two
adjacent
diagonal entries,
while maintaining triangularity,
and without disturbing any of the factors
that belong to the complementary family
\mathcal{G}
.
This is done by embedding the
2\times 2
unimodular transformations
provided by Lemma
3.3
into larger identity matrices.

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
P(\lambda)
be a regular
n\times n
upper triangular polynomial matrix,
and let
{\cal M}={\cal F}\sqcup{\cal G}
be a coprime partition
of the multiset of all irreducible factors
of the diagonal entries of
P(\lambda)
. Consider any
2\times 2
principal submatrix of
P(\lambda)

with
adjacent
diagonal entries, i.e.,

T(\lambda)=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}p_{ii}(\lambda)&p_{ij}(\lambda)\\
0&p_{jj}(\lambda)\end{array}\right]\quad\text{ with }\quad j=i+1\,,

and let
\mathbf{d}_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}}}(T)=(m,n)
.
Then for any integers
\alpha,\beta
such that
\alpha+\beta=m+n

and
\mathop{\rm min}\{m,n\}\leq\alpha,\beta\leq\mathop{\rm max}\{m,n\}
,
there exists a regular upper triangular matrix polynomial
\widetilde{P}(\lambda)

such that

•

\widetilde{p}_{ii}(\lambda)=[\widetilde{p}_{ii}(\lambda)]_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}}}\cdot p_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal G}}}(\lambda)

with
\lvert\,\widetilde{p}_{ii}(\lambda)\,\rvert_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}}}=\alpha
,

•

\widetilde{p}_{jj}(\lambda)=[\widetilde{p}_{jj}(\lambda)]_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}}}\cdot p_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal G}}}(\lambda)

with
\lvert\,\widetilde{p}_{ii}(\lambda)\,\rvert_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}}}=\beta
,

•

\widetilde{p}_{kk}(\lambda)=p_{kk}(\lambda)
for all
k\neq i,j
,

•

\widetilde{P}(\lambda)
is unimodularly equivalent to
P(\lambda)
.

Proof.

Apply Lemma
3.3
to the submatrix
T

to get two
2\times 2
unimodular matrices
E(\lambda)
and
F(\lambda)

such that

E(\lambda)T(\lambda)F(\lambda)=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}[\widetilde{p}_{ii}(\lambda)]_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}}}\cdot p_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal G}}}(\lambda)&\widetilde{p}_{ij}(\lambda)\\
0&[\widetilde{p}_{jj}(\lambda)]_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}}}\cdot p_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal G}}}(\lambda)\end{array}\right]

with diagonal
{\cal F}
-factor-counting vector
(\alpha,\beta)
.
Then construct the
n\times n
unimodular matrices

\widehat{E}(\lambda)\,=\,\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c|c}I_{i-1}&&\\
\hline\cr\\[-12.0pt]
&\rule{0.0pt}{11.66563pt}E(\lambda)&\\
\hline\cr\\[-12.0pt]
&&\rule{0.0pt}{11.66563pt}I_{n-i-1}\end{array}\right]\quad\text{ and }\quad\widehat{F}(\lambda)\,=\,\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c|c}I_{i-1}&&\\
\hline\cr\\[-12.0pt]
&\rule{0.0pt}{11.66563pt}F(\lambda)&\\
\hline\cr\\[-12.0pt]
&&\rule{0.0pt}{11.66563pt}I_{n-i-1}\end{array}\right]\,.

Transforming
P(\lambda)
using these two unimodular matrices,
we obtain the desired matrix
\widetilde{P}(\lambda)=\widehat{E}(\lambda)P(\lambda)\widehat{F}(\lambda)
.
∎

Example 3.5
.

Consider the Smith form
S(\lambda)
from Example
3.2
.
The multiset
{\cal M}
of all of the irreducible factors in the entries of
S(\lambda)

contains many copies of
\chi
,
\phi
, and
\psi
,
but we can partition it into
{\cal M}={\cal F}_{1}\sqcup{\cal F}_{2}\sqcup{\cal F}_{3}\sqcup{\cal F}_{4}
,
where
{\cal F}_{j}
contains all of the irreducible factors of degree
j
.
Thus we see that
{\cal F}_{1}
contains all of the copies of
\psi
,

{\cal F}_{2}
contains all of the copies of
\phi
,
{\cal F}_{3}
is empty,
and
{\cal F}_{4}
contains all of the copies of
\chi
.
The diagonal factor-counting vectors of
S(\lambda)
then are

\displaystyle\mathbf{d}_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}_{1}}}(S)

\displaystyle\;=\;(0,1,1,2,2,4)\,,\hskip 34.1433pt\mathbf{d}_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}_{3}}}(S)\;=\;(0,0,0,0,0,0)\,,

\displaystyle\mathbf{d}_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}_{2}}}(S)

\displaystyle\;=\;(0,1,1,1,1,3)\,,\hskip 34.1433pt\mathbf{d}_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}_{4}}}(S)\;=\;(0,0,1,2,3,3)\,.

Note that we include
\mathbf{d}_{{\cal F}_{3}}(S)
here not only for the sake of completeness,
but also to show that having any of the partition multisets be empty is allowed.
Later on, we will use Corollary
3.4

to rearrange the irreducible factors along the diagonal
so that these diagonal factor-counting vectors
will have the property of being

,
a concept to be defined in the next section.

Note that these diagonal factor-counting vectors just happen
to match up with the partial multiplicity sequences in this example.
But this is not typical,
and follows from there being at most one irreducible divisor
in each piece of the given coprime partition of
{\cal M}
.
For general coprime partitions, diagonal factor-counting vectors of Smith forms
will each be a
sum
of partial multiplicity sequences.

3.2
Homogenization of natural vectors and un-diagonalizing the Smith form

With the ability to transfer irreducible factors along the diagonal
(Corollary
3.4
) now in our tool box,
it is time to see how to employ that tool to rearrange the diagonal irreducible factors
so as to make the new diagonal entries as close in degree to each other as possible.
This is phase
1
of our quasi-triangular realization construction.
It will be shown that the best that can be done in general
is to make these diagonal entry degrees differ by at most
k
,
where
k
is the highest degree among all of the irreducible factors along the diagonal.
To facilitate the discussion of this process,
we introduce the following two concepts.
Note that vectors whose entries are natural numbers,
in particular diagonal factor-counting vectors,
appear frequently in this discussion;
such vectors will be referred in brief as
natural vectors
.
Keep in mind that in this paper the natural numbers
\mathbb{N}

includes zero
.

Definition 3.6
.

A natural vector
\mathbf{v}=(v_{1},\dots,v_{r})\in\mathbb{N}^{r}
is
k
-homogeneous

if
|v_{i}-v_{j}|\leq k
for any
1\leq i,j\leq r
.

Definition 3.7
.

Let
\mathbf{v}=(v_{1},\dots,v_{r})\in\mathbb{N}^{r}

with component sum
s=v_{1}+v_{2}+\dotsb+v_{r}
,
and divide
s
by
r
to get
s=qr+t
, with
0\leq t<r
.
Then any permutation of the
1
-homogeneous vector

(q,q,\dotsc,q,\underbrace{q+1,q+1,\dotsc,q+1}_{t\,\text{ copies}})\,\in\,\mathbb{N}^{r}

is called a
homogenization
of
\mathbf{v}
.

Before addressing our primary objective, that is,
the phase
1
rearrangement of diagonal irreducible factors,
it will be useful to do a preliminary examination
of the process of homogenizing natural vectors
via two very simple operations
that we will refer to as “interchange” and “compression”.
We will see that these two operations on natural vectors
are closely related to transformations of the diagonal of upper triangular polynomial matrices
achievable by the Unimodular Transfer Corollary
3.4
.
The operations of interchange and compression also bring us
into contact with the classical notion of majorization of vectors,
which we recall next.

Definition 3.8

(Majorization
[
14
]
)
.

For vectors
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
let
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
and
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}

denote the permutations of those vectors
in which the entries have been arranged in decreasing order.
We say that
\mathbf{x}
majorizes
\mathbf{y}
,
or
\mathbf{y}
is majorized by
\mathbf{x}
,
and write
\,\mathbf{x}\succeq\mathbf{y}\,
, if

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}x_{i}^{\prime}\;\geq\;\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}y_{i}^{\prime}\quad\text{ for }\quad\ell=1,2,\dotsc,n\;,

(3.8)

with equality when
\ell=n
.
Clearly this definition also applies without change when restricted
to integer vectors
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
,
or even to natural vectors
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
.

Remark 3.9
.

It is useful to keep in mind the basic intuition about majorization,
i.e., that if
\mathbf{x}
majorizes
\mathbf{y}
,
then the entries of
\mathbf{x}
are more “spread out”
than those of
\mathbf{y}
.
This can be seen, at least in part,
by examining the two extreme components of the ordered vectors

\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
and
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}
.
From the
\ell=1
inequality in (
3.8
)
we have
x^{\prime}_{1}\geq y^{\prime}_{1}
,
and by combining the
\ell=n
and
\ell=n-1
parts of (
3.8
)
we see that
y^{\prime}_{n}\geq x^{\prime}_{n}
.
Thus we have
x^{\prime}_{1}\geq y^{\prime}_{1}\geq\dotsb\geq y^{\prime}_{n}\geq x^{\prime}_{n}
,
displaying some of the greater dispersion of the entries of
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
.

It is a classical result of the study of majorization
(attributed in
[
14
]
to a 1903 article of Muirhead
[
15
]
)
that for natural vectors
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
,

\mathbf{x}
majorizes
\mathbf{y}

(\mathbf{x}\succeq\mathbf{y})

if and only if

\,\mathbf{x}
can be transformed into
\mathbf{y}

by a finite sequence of operations known
variously as
transfers
,
Dalton transfers
,
or more recently (and colorfully) as
Robin Hood transfers

– think “rob from the rich and give to the poor”.
Such an operation takes any two components of a natural vector
\mathbf{v}
,
say
v_{i}
and
v_{j}
, and replaces them by natural numbers
\alpha
and
\beta

that are closer together in size;
more specifically, where
\alpha+\beta=v_{i}+v_{j}

and
|\alpha-\beta|\leq|v_{i}-v_{j}|
.
(Note that in his proof of this result,
Muirhead uses only transfers in which
v_{i}
and
v_{j}

change by exactly
1
, i.e.,
\alpha=v_{i}\pm 1
and
\beta=v_{j}\mp 1
.)

For our purposes, we need to have a Muirhead-like result
for converting a natural vector into a homogenization of itself.
However, since we will ultimately need to implement these conversions
by unimodular transformations that preserve upper triangularity
(see Corollary
3.4
),
it is essential that we limit our operations on natural vectors
to ones that act only on
adjacent entries
of a vector,
which is more restrictive than the Robin Hood transfers used by Muirhead.
Thus we introduce the following two operations
acting on natural vectors:

•

Interchange
of
adjacent
components:

(v_{1},\dots,v_{i},v_{i+1},\dots,v_{r})\leadsto(v_{1},\dots,v_{i+1},v_{i},\dots,v_{r})\in\mathbb{N}^{r}

•

Compression
of
adjacent
components:

(v_{1},\dots,v_{i-1},v_{i},v_{i+1},v_{i+2},\dots,v_{r})\leadsto(v_{1},\dots,v_{i-1},\alpha,\beta,v_{i+2},\dots,v_{r})\in\mathbb{N}^{r}\,,

where
\alpha+\beta=v_{i}+v_{i+1}
and
|\alpha-\beta|<|v_{i}-v_{i+1}|
.

Equivalently, we can instead require that
\alpha+\beta=v_{i}+v_{i+1}

and
\mathop{\rm min}\{v_{i},v_{i+1}\}<\alpha,\beta<\mathop{\rm max}\{v_{i},v_{i+1}\}
.

Note that we use the new terminology “interchange” and “compression” here
for these more limited operations, to try to avoid any confusion
with the more flexible Robin Hood transfers.
We now state and prove the modified version
of the classic Muirhead result,
specialized to the transformation of a natural vector
into a homogenization of itself.
As an immediate consequence of Lemma
3.10

and the Muirhead theorem,
we see that any natural vector
\mathbf{v}

majorizes any homogenization of itself.

Lemma 3.10

(Homogenization Lemma)
.

Consider any
\mathbf{v}=(v_{1},\dots,v_{r})\in\mathbb{N}^{r}
,
with component sum
s=v_{1}+\dots+v_{r}
, and average component value
a=s/r
.
Let
q\in\mathbb{N}
be an integer such that the average
a

is contained in the closed interval
[\mkern 1.0muq,q+1\mkern 1.0mu]
.
Then by a finite sequence of interchanges and at most
r-1
compressions,

\mathbf{v}
can be transformed into a homogenization of itself,
comprised of only
q
and
q+1
components, ordered arbitrarily.
Writing
s=qr+t
with
0\leq t\leq r
,
then in the homogenization of
\mathbf{v}

there are exactly
t
copies of
q+1

and
r-t
copies of
q
.

Proof.

The proof will proceed by a pair of inductions,
each on the length
r
of the vector
\mathbf{v}
.
The first induction considers only vectors
\mathbf{v}

whose average component value
a
is an integer, either
q
or
q+1
.
In this case we will see that the homogenization of
\mathbf{v}

is the vector
(a,a,\dotsc,a)
.
The second induction, which builds on the result of the first,
considers the vectors
\mathbf{v}
where
a
is
not
an integer,
i.e.,
q<a<q+1
.
For this second case the homogenization will have both
q
and
q+1
entries.

Part 1
(
a
is an integer)

The case
r=1
is trivial, and the case
r=2
is also very easy –
just do a single (
r-1=1
) compression to produce the homogenization
(a,a)
.
So now assume that every
k
-vector
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
with
k<r

and integer average
a

can be finitely transformed into a homogenization
(a,a,\dotsc,a)

using at most
k-1
compressions.
Let
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{N}^{r}
be any
r
-vector with average value
a=q
.
If
\mathbf{v}
already has any component equal to
q
,
then finitely many interchanges move this component to the end,
producing
\mathbf{v}\leadsto(\widetilde{\mathbf{v}},q)
.
If not, then one can find at least one component larger than
q

and one component smaller than
q
.
By interchanges make these components adjacent,
and then a compression on these two components can produce at least one
q
component.
Finitely many more interchanges then moves this newly-produced
q
to the end,
again producing
\mathbf{v}\leadsto(\widetilde{\mathbf{v}},q)
.
In either case, the
(r-1)
-vector
\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}

has average component value
\widetilde{a}=(s-q)/(r-1)=(ar-a)/(r-1)=a=q
.
By the induction hypothesis the vector
\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}

can now be transformed by finitely many interchanges and at most
r-2
compressions
into the vector
(a,a,\dotsc,a)
,
and thus we have the desired conclusion for the
r
-vector
\mathbf{v}
.
The same argument works, mutatis mutandis, for vectors
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{N}^{r}

with average value
a=q+1
.

Part 2
(
a
is
not
an integer, i.e.,
q<a<q+1
and
0<t<r
)

Here the base case for the induction cannot be
r=1
;
it must be
r=2
, with
a=q+\frac{1}{\mkern 1.0mu2\mkern 1.0mu}
.
In this
r=2
case, then,
a single compression can produce the vector
(q,q+1)
, as desired.
Again as in Part 1, now assume that every
k
-vector
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
with
k<r

and average
q<a<q+1
can be finitely transformed into a homogenized version containing only
q
and
q+1
components,
using at most
k-1
compressions.
Let
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{N}^{r}
be any
r
-vector with non-integer average value
q<a<q+1
.
If
\mathbf{v}
already has any component equal to
q
or
q+1
,
then finitely many interchanges moves this component to the end,
producing
\mathbf{v}\leadsto(\widetilde{\mathbf{v}},q)
or
(\widetilde{\mathbf{v}},q+1)
.
If no component of
\mathbf{v}
is
q
or
q+1
,
then there must be at least one component larger than
q+1

and one component smaller than
q
.
Make these components adjacent by interchanges,
and compress these two components
to produce at least one
q
or
q+1
entry
(possibly both, or perhaps even two
q
’s or two
q+1
’s).
Move this
q
or
q+1
entry to the end by interchanges,
once again transforming
\mathbf{v}

into the form
(\widetilde{\mathbf{v}},q)
or
(\widetilde{\mathbf{v}},q+1)
.
The
(r-1)
-vector
\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}
now has a component average

\widetilde{a}\,=\,\begin{cases}\frac{\,s-q\,}{r-1}\,=\,\frac{\,qr+t-q\,}{r-1}\,=\,q+\frac{t}{\,r-1\,}\,\leq\,q+1&\text{\,if\; $\mathbf{v}\leadsto(\widetilde{\mathbf{v}},q)$,}\\[5.0pt]
\frac{\,s-(q+1)\,}{r-1}\,=\,\frac{\,qr+t-q-1\,}{r-1}\,=\,q+\frac{t-1}{\,r-1\,}\,\geq\,q&\text{\,if\; $\mathbf{v}\leadsto(\widetilde{\mathbf{v}},q+1)$.}\end{cases}

If
\widetilde{a}
is
q
or
q+1
, then we are done by Part 1.
Otherwise
q<\widetilde{a}<q+1
,
and we are done by the induction hypothesis of Part 2.

This completes the inductive argument
that a homogenization of
\mathbf{v}
can always be achieved.
The expression of the component sum
s
in the form
s=qr+t
with
0\leq t\leq r

then uniquely determines the number of
q
and
q+1
entries
in the homogenization.
A final sequence of interchanges
can put the entries of the homogenization into any desired order,
thus completing the proof of the lemma.
∎

With the Homogenization Lemma in hand,
we return to the first phase of our quasi-triangular realization process,
the “un-diagonalizing” of a Smith form.
The next result addresses the combinatorial essence of this problem,
showing how it is possible to take a multiset of irreducible polynomials
and distribute them among the entries of a vector
in a way that minimizes the degree differences,
and at the same time produces a viable configuration
for the diagonal vector of an upper triangular un-diagonalized Smith form.
The corollary immediately following
shows that the target diagonal produced by Lemma
3.11

is in fact reachable from the Smith form
via the type of triangularity-preserving unimodular transformations
developed in Corollary
3.4
.

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
\,\mathbb{F}
be an arbitrary field,
and consider a finite multiset
{\cal M}
of
\,\mathbb{F}
-irreducible polynomials
of degree less than or equal to
k
.
Also let
n
be any positive integer.
Let
{\cal M}={\cal F}_{1}\sqcup\dotsb\sqcup{\cal F}_{k}

be the coprime partition of
{\cal M}

in which
{\cal F}_{j}
contains all of the
\,\mathbb{F}
-irreducible factors in
{\cal M}
of degree
j
. Then there exists a polynomial
n
-vector
\,\mathbf{p}(\lambda)

with all nonzero entries
such that

•

the multiset of all of the
\,\mathbb{F}
-irreducible factors
of all the entries of
\,\mathbf{p}(\lambda)
is exactly
{\cal M}
,

•

the degree vector

\,\deg\mathbf{p}:=\,\bigl(\deg p_{1}(\lambda),\dotsc,\deg p_{n}(\lambda)\bigr)

is
k
-homogeneous,

•

and each of the factor-counting vectors
|\mathbf{p}(\lambda)|_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}_{j}}}

for
j=1,\dotsc,k
is
1
-homogeneous.

Proof.

For notational convenience, we first append
enough copies of the constant polynomial
1
to the multiset
{\cal M}

so that the total number
m
of elements in
{\cal M}

is a multiple of
n
, i.e.,
|{\cal M}|=:m=(q+1)n
with
q\geq 0
.
The presence of these copies of
1
will have no impact
on the vector
\mathbf{p}
that is constructed,
but the exposition will be simplified by including them.

Next order the elements of
{\cal M}
into a list
{\cal L}

so that the sequence of degrees is decreasing.
That is, let
{\cal L}=\bigl[a_{1}(\lambda),a_{2}(\lambda),\dotsc,a_{m}(\lambda)\bigr]
,
where
\deg a_{\ell}\geq\deg a_{\ell+1}
for every
\ell=1,2,\dotsc,m-1
.
Now partition the list
{\cal L}
into
q+1
contiguous sublists of
n
polynomials each,
and stack them up as in the following diagram.   (Keep in mind that
qn+n=m
.)

\begin{array}[]{cc@{\hspace{5mm}}l@{\hspace{5mm}}lll}{\cal L}_{0}&=\hfil\hskip 14.22636pt&\;[\,a_{1},\hfil\hskip 14.22636pt&\;a_{2},&\dotsc&\;\;,a_{n}]\\
{\cal L}_{1}&=\hfil\hskip 14.22636pt&[\,a_{n+1},\hfil\hskip 14.22636pt&a_{n+2},&\dotsc&\;\,,a_{2n}\,]\\
&\vdots\hfil\hskip 14.22636pt&\hfil\hskip 14.22636pt&&&\\
{\cal L}_{j}&=\hfil\hskip 14.22636pt&\![\,a_{jn+1},\hfil\hskip 14.22636pt&a_{jn+2},&\dotsc&\,,a_{jn+n}\,]\\
&\vdots\hfil\hskip 14.22636pt&\hfil\hskip 14.22636pt&&&\\
{\cal L}_{q}&=\hfil\hskip 14.22636pt&\![\,a_{qn+1},\hfil\hskip 14.22636pt&a_{qn+2},&\dotsc&,a_{qn+n}\,]\end{array}
\color[rgb]{1,0,0}v_{1}(\lambda)
\color[rgb]{1,0,0}v_{2}(\lambda)
\dotsc
\color[rgb]{1,0,0}v_{n}(\lambda)

The
n
component polynomials of the desired vector
\mathbf{p}(\lambda)

are formed by taking the column-wise products indicated by the blue ovals.
For example,

p_{1}(\lambda):=a_{1}(\lambda)a_{n+1}(\lambda)\dotsb a_{qn+1}(\lambda)
,

p_{2}(\lambda):=a_{2}(\lambda)a_{n+2}(\lambda)\dotsb a_{qn+2}(\lambda)
, etc.
It is clear by construction that the multiset
of all of the
\,\mathbb{F}
-irreducible factors
of all the entries of
\,\mathbf{p}(\lambda)
is exactly
{\cal M}
.
All that remains is to see why this
\mathbf{p}(\lambda)

has the other two desired properties.

To see why the degree vector
\deg\mathbf{p}
is
k
-homogeneous,
first observe that the component degrees are decreasing,
i.e.,
\deg p_{1}\geq\deg p_{2}\geq\dotsb\geq\deg p_{n}
.
This follows immediately from the elements of the list
{\cal L}

being in decreasing order.
Consequently the largest degree difference in
\mathbf{p}

will be between the first and last components
p_{1}(\lambda)
and
p_{n}(\lambda)
.
But we have

\displaystyle\deg p_{1}-\deg p_{n}\;

\displaystyle=\;(\deg a_{1}+\deg a_{n+1}+\dotsb+\deg a_{(qn+1)})-(\deg a_{n}+\deg a_{2n}+\dotsb+\deg a_{(qn+n)})

\displaystyle=\;\deg a_{1}-\bigl[(\deg a_{n}-\deg a_{n+1})+(\deg a_{2n}-\deg a_{2n+1})+\dotsb+(\deg a_{qn}-\deg a_{qn+1})+\deg a_{m}\bigr]

\displaystyle\leq\;k\,,

since
\deg a_{1}(\lambda)\leq k
,
and the expression inside the brackets is a sum of non-negative numbers
(due to the list
{\cal L}
being in decreasing-degree order).
Hence the degree vector
\deg\mathbf{p}(\lambda)
is
k
-homogeneous.

Finally, consider the factor-counting vectors
|\mathbf{p}(\lambda)|_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}_{j}}}
.
To see why these vectors are all
1
-homogeneous,
observe that because the irreducible polynomials are listed in
{\cal L}

in order of decreasing degree,
the degree
j
irreducibles in
{\cal L}
form a contiguous segment of
{\cal L}
,
and hence are distributed among consecutive sublists
{\cal L}_{\delta}

in a manner analogous to the green entries in the following diagram.
On the other hand, if
\alpha<\beta
,
then

\begin{array}[]{cclllllll}&\vdots&&&&&&&\\
{\cal L}_{\alpha}&=&\{a_{\alpha n+1}\,,&\dotsc&\,,\boxed{\color[rgb]{0,0.7,0}a_{\alpha n+i}}\,,&\color[rgb]{0,0.7,0}\dotsc&,\,\color[rgb]{0,0.7,0}a_{\alpha n+\ell}\,,&\color[rgb]{0,0.7,0}\dotsc&,\;\color[rgb]{0,0.7,0}a_{\alpha n+n}\}\\
{\cal L}_{\alpha+1}&=&\{\color[rgb]{0,0.7,0}a_{(\alpha+1)n+1},&\color[rgb]{0,0.7,0}\dotsc&\,,\,\color[rgb]{0,0.7,0}a_{(\alpha+1)n+i},&\color[rgb]{0,0.7,0}\dotsc&,\color[rgb]{0,0.7,0}a_{(\alpha+1)n+\ell},&\color[rgb]{0,0.7,0}\dotsc&,\;\color[rgb]{0,0.7,0}a_{(\alpha+1)n+n}\}\\
&\vdots&&&&&&&\\
{\cal L}_{\beta}&=&\{\color[rgb]{0,0.7,0}a_{\beta n+1}\,,&\color[rgb]{0,0.7,0}\dotsc&\,,\,\color[rgb]{0,0.7,0}a_{\beta n+i}\,,&\color[rgb]{0,0.7,0}\dotsc&,\boxed{\color[rgb]{0,0.7,0}a_{\beta n+\ell}}\,,&\dotsc&,\;a_{\beta n+n}\}\\
&\vdots&&&&&&&\\
\end{array}
\color[rgb]{1,0,0}v_{1}
\dotsc
\color[rgb]{1,0,0}v_{i}
\dotsc
\color[rgb]{1,0,0}v_{\ell}
\dotsc
\color[rgb]{1,0,0}v_{n}

The first instance of a degree
j
irreducible
is the boxed entry
a_{\alpha n+i}
with
1\leq i\leq n
in some sublist
{\cal L}_{\alpha}
,
and the last degree
j
irreducible is the boxed entry
a_{\beta n+\ell}
,
where
\alpha\leq\beta
.

If
\alpha=\beta
, then
i\leq\ell
,
and all of the degree
j
irreducibles in
{\cal L}
are in the sublist
{\cal L}_{\alpha}
,
so the factor-counting vector
|\mathbf{p}(\lambda)|_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}_{j}}}

has only
0
and
1
entries;
this certainly constitutes a
1
-homogeneous vector.
On the other hand,
if
\alpha<\beta
then we may have either
\ell<i-1
,
\ell=i-1
, or
\ell>i-1
.
Consider each of these possibilities in turn:

•

\ell<i-1
:
In this case the combined contribution of the degree
j
entries
in
{\cal L}_{\alpha}
and
{\cal L}_{\beta}
to
|\mathbf{p}(\lambda)|_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}_{j}}}
is
the
n
-vector

(1,1,\dotsc,\underbrace{1}_{\ell},0,\dotsc,0,\underbrace{1}_{i},\dotsc,1)\,,

while the contribution of the sublists
{\cal L}_{\delta}

with
\alpha<\delta<\beta

is a constant vector with
n
entries all equal to
\beta-\alpha-1
.
The sum of these two vectors is
|\mathbf{p}(\lambda)|_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}_{j}}}
,
and is clearly
1
-homogeneous.

•

\ell=i-1
:
Now the contribution to
|\mathbf{p}(\lambda)|_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}_{j}}}

from
{\cal L}_{\alpha}
and
{\cal L}_{\beta}
combined is just
the constant
n
-vector
(1,1,\dotsc,1)
,
which together with the contribution from the sublists
between
{\cal L}_{\alpha}
and
{\cal L}_{\beta}

gives a constant vector for
|\mathbf{p}(\lambda)|_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}_{j}}}
,
with entries all equal to
\beta-\alpha
.
This is certainly
1
-homogeneous, indeed even
0
-homogeneous.

•

\ell>i-1
:
In this final case the combined contribution of the degree
j
entries
in
{\cal L}_{\alpha}
and
{\cal L}_{\beta}
to
|\mathbf{p}(\lambda)|_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}_{j}}}
is
the
n
-vector

(1,\dotsc,1,\underbrace{2}_{i},2,\dotsc,\underbrace{2}_{\ell},1,\dotsc,1)\,.

Together with the constant vector contribution
from the sublists between
{\cal L}_{\alpha}
and
{\cal L}_{\beta}
,
we again see that
|\mathbf{p}(\lambda)|_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}_{j}}}
is
1
-homogeneous.

∎

Remark 3.12
.

Note that
k
-homogeneity for
\mathbf{p}(\lambda)
in Lemma
3.11

is the best possible general result here,
as illustrated by the following simple example.
Suppose
{\cal M}
contains
only
irreducible polynomials of degree
k
,
say
\ell
of them, and
n>\ell
.
Then clearly the
n
-vector
\mathbf{p}(\lambda)
that minimizes the degree differences
has
|\mathbf{p}(\lambda)|_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}_{k}}}=(k,k,\dotsc,k,0,\dotsc,0)
,
and
k
-homogeneity cannot be improved upon in this situation.

We return to our running illustration of the results of this paper,
as begun earlier in Examples
3.2
and
3.5
.
The next example demonstrates the application of Lemma
3.11

to this data.

Example 3.13
.

Recall the
\,6\times 6\,
Smith form from Example
3.2
,
i.e.,

S(\lambda)=\mathop{\rm diag}\{1,\phi\psi,\chi\phi\psi,\chi^{2}\phi\psi^{2},\chi^{3}\phi\psi^{2},\chi^{3}\phi^{3}\psi^{4}\}\,,

and consider the coprime partition of the list of irreducible divisors
as in Example
3.5
,
with

and an
{\cal F}_{3}

Following the proof of Lemma
3.11
gives us five sublists,

:

\begin{array}[]{lclccccr}{\cal L}_{0}&=&\{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\chi},&\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\chi},&\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\chi},&\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\chi},&\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\chi},&\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\chi}\}\\

We now have the tools needed to reach the next milestone
in our construction of a quasi-triangular realization
of given finite spectral data.
The following corollary is the main result to carry forward
into the next stages of this construction.

Corollary 3.14

(Un-diagonalizing the Smith form)
.

Let
\,\mathbb{F}
be an arbitrary field,
and consider any regular
n\times n
diagonal polynomial matrix
S(\lambda)
over
\,\mathbb{F}

that is in Smith form.
Let
{\cal M}
be the multiset of all of the
\,\mathbb{F}
-irreducible factors
of all of the invariant polynomials in
S(\lambda)
,
and let
k
be the maximum degree among all elements of
{\cal M}
.
Consider also the coprime partition
{\cal M}={\cal F}_{1}\sqcup\dotsb\sqcup{\cal F}_{k}
,
in which each
{\cal F}_{j}
contains all of the
\,\mathbb{F}
-irreducible factors in
{\cal M}
of degree
j
.
Then there is an upper triangular polynomial matrix
T(\lambda)

that is unimodularly equivalent to
S(\lambda)
,
with diagonal degree vector
\,\deg\bigl(\mathop{\rm diag}T(\lambda)\bigr)

that is
k
-homogeneous,
and such that each diagonal factor-counting vector
\mathbf{d}_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}_{j}}}(T)

is
1
-homogeneous.

Proof.

Use the given multiset
{\cal M}
of
\mathbb{F}
-irreducible polynomials
as input to Lemma
3.11
.
The output vector
\mathbf{p}(\lambda)
from that Lemma
is now the target diagonal for the desired upper triangular
T(\lambda)
.
Since each factor-counting vector
|\mathbf{p}(\lambda)|_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}_{j}}}

is
1
-homogeneous,
the Homogenization Lemma
3.10

guarantees that the transition from the vector
\mathop{\rm diag}S(\lambda)

to the vector
\mathbf{p}(\lambda)

can be achieved using a finite number of compressions and interchanges.
But Corollary
3.4
gives us the means
to implement all of these compressions and interchanges
as unimodular transformations applied to
S(\lambda)
.
Doing this then converts
S(\lambda)

into the desired upper triangular
T(\lambda)
.
∎

3.3
A combinatorial lemma

In this short section we focus on establishing a new combinatorial property of “tightly packed”
integer
multisets,
that is, multisets that contain more, perhaps even many more elements
than the width of the interval into which they are packed.
This property will enable us to permute the diagonal entries
of the
T(\lambda)
from Corollary
3.14

in preparation for the final phase
of our quasi-triangular realization construction.
Note that for ease of expression,
in this section we use the word “list”
as a synonym for multiset;
however, nothing about any ordering of these lists
is of any relevance for the development here.

Lemma 3.15

(Homogeneous Partitioning Property)
.

Let
{\cal I}=[\,j,j+k\,]
be a closed interval with
integer
endpoints and length
k\geq 1
,
and consider a list
\mathcal{L}=\{n_{1},n_{2},\dotsc,n_{m}\}
of
m
integers,
all in
{\cal I}
.
If the average value of all of the entries in
{\cal L}

is an
integer

\mu\in{\cal I}
,
then
{\cal L}
can be partitioned into sublists
{\cal S}_{1},{\cal S}_{2},\dotsc,{\cal S}_{\ell}

such that the number of entries in each
{\cal S}_{i}
does not exceed
k
,
and the average value of each sublist is exactly
\mu
.

To help prove this result,
we need another lemma characterizing the solution set
of a certain diophantine equation in two variables.

Lemma 3.16
.

Let
a
and
b
be positive integers, with
d:=\gcd\{a,b\}
.
Then the set of integer solutions
(x,y)
of the equation
ax=by

consists of all the integer multiples of the pair
(\widetilde{b},\widetilde{a})
,
where
\widetilde{a}=a/d
and
\widetilde{b}=b/d
.

Proof.

We can immediately reduce to the equation
\widetilde{a}x=\widetilde{b}y
,
which has exactly the same solution set as
ax=by
.
Since
\widetilde{a}
and
\widetilde{b}
are relatively prime,
for any solution pair
(x,y)

it must be true that
\widetilde{a}|y
and
\widetilde{b}|x
.
Letting
x=\widetilde{b}k
and
y=\widetilde{a}\ell
,
then
\widetilde{a}\widetilde{b}k=\widetilde{b}\widetilde{a}\ell

implies that
k=\ell
.
Thus the solutions of
\widetilde{a}x=\widetilde{b}y

(and hence also of
ax=by
)
are exactly the integer multiples of
(\widetilde{b},\widetilde{a})
.
∎

We now prove the Homogeneous Partitioning Property.

Proof.

(of Lemma
3.15
)
One strategy to achieve this homogeneous partitioning
is to first translate all the given data
(i.e., the interval
{\cal I}
and the list
{\cal L}
)
by any fixed constant
c\in\mathbb{Z}
, solve the translated problem,
and then “un-translate” the solution back to the original location.
Thus it suffices to solve the problem for the case when
\mu=0

and the interval
{\cal I}
is
[\,e_{\ell},e_{r}\,]
,
with endpoints
e_{\ell}\leq 0
and
e_{r}\geq 0
such that
e_{r}-e_{\ell}=k
.
The goal in this more specialized scenario, then, is to partition
{\cal L}

into sublists of no more than
k
entries each,
such that the
sum
(equals the average)
of the entries of each sublist
{\cal S}_{i}
is zero.

The proof for this special scenario proceeds by an induction on
m
,
the number of elements in the list
{\cal L}
.
(Note that this induction can be easily converted into an algorithm
for computing the desired partition.)
The base case(s) for this induction are all
m
such that
1\leq m\leq k
,
for which the result is trivially true.
So now suppose that the homogeneous partitioning property
holds for all number lists satisfying the hypotheses of Lemma
3.15

with
m\leq h
,
and consider a list
{\cal L}
of
m=h+1
integers
with values in
{\cal I}=[\,e_{\ell},e_{r}\,]
and average value
\mu=0
.
There are now three cases to consider:

(a)

Some
n_{i}\in{\cal L}
is equal to zero.

In this case we can split off the singleton sublist
{\cal S}_{1}=\{n_{i}\}

from
{\cal L}
, leaving a smaller list
\widehat{{\cal L}}
with
m=h
entries,
and average value
\widehat{\mu}=0
.
Applying the inductive hypothesis to
\widehat{{\cal L}}
completes
the homogeneous partitioning of
{\cal L}
.

(b)

No element of
{\cal L}
is zero,
and all elements of
{\cal L}
lie at the endpoints
e_{\ell}
and
e_{r}
of
\,{\cal I}
.

Suppose there are
\alpha
copies of
e_{\ell}

and
\beta
copies of
e_{r}
, so that
\,\alpha+\beta=h+1>k
,
and
\,\alpha e_{\ell}+\beta e_{r}=0
,
or equivalently
(-e_{\ell})\alpha=e_{r}\beta
.
Let
d=\gcd\{-e_{\ell},e_{r}\}
.
Applying Lemma
3.16
to the equation
ax=by

with
a=-e_{\ell}
and
b=e_{r}
,
we see that the solution
(x,y)=(\alpha,\beta)
to
ax=by

is an integer multiple of
(\frac{\,e_{r}\,}{d},\frac{\,-e_{\ell}\,}{d})
.
Thus we can completely partition
{\cal L}
into sublists
{\cal S}_{i}
,
each consisting of
\frac{\,e_{r}\,}{d}
copies of
e_{\ell}

and
\frac{\,-e_{\ell}\,}{d}
copies of
e_{r}
.
Since
\,\frac{\,e_{r}\,}{d}+\frac{\,-e_{\ell}\,}{d}=\frac{\,k\,}{d}
,
each of these sublists has
\frac{\,k\,}{d}\leq k
elements,
and sum zero, as desired.

(c)

No element of
{\cal L}
is zero,
but there is some
n_{i_{1}}
from
{\cal L}

in the
open
interval
\,\widetilde{{\cal I}}=(e_{\ell},e_{r})
.

Begin building a sublist
{\cal S}
with the given (nonzero) element
\,n_{i_{1}}
in
\widetilde{{\cal I}}
.
Pick from among the remaining elements of
{\cal L}

to update
{\cal S}=\{n_{i_{1}},n_{i_{2}},\dotsc,n_{i_{j}},\dotsc\}
,
and keep track of the “partial sums”
\sigma_{j}:=\sum_{\ell=1}^{j}n_{i_{\ell}}

as you go to see if a zero sum has been achieved.

At each stage, the element
n_{i_{j}}
to be appended to
{\cal S}
is chosen
to be any one of the remaining elements of
{\cal L}
that have
a sign
opposite
to that of
\sigma_{j-1}
,
in order to try to drive the partial sum value to zero.
Observe that there must always exist such an “opposite-sign” element
remaining in
{\cal L}
,
since otherwise the sum (hence also the average)
of all of the elements in
{\cal L}
would not be zero.
Another consequence of this opposite-sign strategy
is that the
\sigma_{j}
values can never be equal
to either endpoint
e_{\ell}
or
e_{r}
of the interval
{\cal I}
;
each
\sigma_{j}
must be one of the
k-1
integers
in the interior of
{\cal I}
, i.e. in
\,\widetilde{{\cal I}}
.
To see why this is so, first observe that

\sigma_{1}=n_{i_{1}}
is in
\,\widetilde{{\cal I}}
by construction.
For the passage from
\sigma_{j}
to
\sigma_{j+1}
with
j\geq 1
,
there are three scenarios:

\text{(i)}\;\;e_{\ell}<\sigma_{j}<0\,,\qquad\text{(ii)}\;\;\sigma_{j}=0\,,\quad\text{ or }\quad\text{(iii)}\;\;0<\sigma_{j}<e_{r}\,.

In case (i),
\sigma_{j+1}
can be at most
e_{r}
larger than
\sigma_{j}
,
so
\sigma_{j+1}\in\,\widetilde{{\cal I}}
.
In case (ii), the construction will cease, and there will be no
\sigma_{j+1}
.
And in case (iii),
\sigma_{j+1}
can be at most

\lvert e_{\ell}\rvert
smaller than
\sigma_{j}
,
so once again
\sigma_{j+1}\in\,\widetilde{{\cal I}}
.

Now carry on the building up of the sublist
{\cal S}

using the “opposite-sign” strategy,
until either  (a) a partial sum
\sigma_{j}=0
with
j\leq k-1
is attained,
or  (b) the sublist contains
k-1
elements with
every
partial sum

\sigma_{1},\dotsc,\sigma_{k-1}
being nonzero.
If (a) occurs, then split off the sublist

{\cal S}=\{n_{i_{1}},n_{i_{2}},\dotsc,n_{i_{j}}\}
from
{\cal L}
,
and the remaining sublist
\widehat{{\cal L}}
can be homogeneously partitioned
by the inductive hypothesis.
On the other hand, if (b) occurs,
then the
k-1
nonzero partial sums must have some repetitions,
since there are only
k-2
nonzero integers in the open interval
\,\widetilde{{\cal I}}
.
So suppose that
\sigma_{i_{p}}=\sigma_{i_{j}}
for some
p<j\leq k-1
,
and let
\widehat{{\cal S}}:=\{n_{i_{p+1}},\dotsc,n_{i_{j}}\}
,
with at most
k-2
elements.
Observe that the sum of the elements in
\widehat{{\cal S}}
is

\sum_{\ell=p+1}^{j}n_{i_{\ell}}\;=\;\biggl(\,\sum_{\ell=1}^{j}n_{i_{\ell}}-\sum_{\ell=1}^{p}n_{i_{\ell}}\biggr)\;=\;\sigma_{i_{j}}-\sigma_{i_{p}}\;=\;0\,,

so splitting off the sublist
\widehat{{\cal S}}
from
{\cal L}

starts the homogeneous partitioning,
leaving a remaining sublist
\widehat{{\cal L}}

that can be homogeneously partitioned by the inductive hypothesis.

The result for the general interval
{\cal I}=[\,j,j+k\,]
now follows by translation.
∎

Remark 3.17
.

A closer examination of the proof of the Homogeneous Partitioning property
indicates that the presence of sublists of “full length”
k

in a homogeneous partitioning may be somewhat rare.
In most of the scenarios for splitting off a sublist
{\cal S}
from the main list
{\cal L}
,
the length of the split-off sublist is strictly less than
k
.
In fact, the
only
scenario that can force a sublist to have length
k

is very special;
with all data translated so that
\mu=0
,

all
of the elements of
{\cal L}
must be at the endpoints
e_{\ell}
and
e_{r}
,

and
these endpoints must be relatively prime.

Another feature of the homogeneous partitioning problem
that is hinted at in the proof
is that often these partitions are
not unique
.
Indeed, in the partitioning procedure described in the proof,
in particular for case (c),
there may be many arbitrary choices that can be made,
all of which lead to an admissible partitioning.
In addition, there may be other homogeneous partitionings
that cannot be generated from the procedure in the proof at all,
no matter what choices are made.

Remark 3.18
.

It is worth noting that Lemma
3.15

can be extended to apply to the situation
in which the average
\mu\in{\cal I}
is
not
necessarily an integer.
For this general case the statement looks like the following:

General Homogeneous Partitioning Property

Let
{\cal I}=[\,j,j+k\,]
be a closed interval with
integer
endpoints and length
k\geq 1
,
and consider a list
\mathcal{L}=\{n_{1},n_{2},\dotsc,n_{m}\}
of
m
integers,
all in
{\cal I}
.
Let
\mu\in{\cal I}
denote the average value of all of the entries in
{\cal L}
.
Then
{\cal L}
can be partitioned into sublists
{\cal S}_{1},{\cal S}_{2},\dotsc,{\cal S}_{\ell}

such that the number of entries in each
{\cal S}_{i}
does not exceed
k
,
and the average value of each sublist
is in the closed interval
\bigl[\,\lfloor\mu\rfloor,\lceil\mu\rceil\,\bigr]
.

Note that this more general partitioning result
reduces to exactly Lemma
3.15

when the average
\mu
is an integer.
However, since we will not need the general property for this paper,
we omit the proof.

Example 3.19
.

Recall that in Example
3.13
we took the diagonal vector of a Smith form
S(\lambda)
,
and rearranged the
\mathbb{F}
-irreducible factors via Lemma
3.11

to obtain a vector
\mathbf{p}(\lambda)
with average degree
10

and
4
-homogeneous degree vector
\deg\mathbf{p}=(12,12,11,9,8,8)
.
Lemma
3.15
now guarantees that there is a partitioning
of
\,\deg\mathbf{p}\,

, where

sublist

.

(12,8\mid 12,8\mid 11,9)

3.4
Un-triangularizing
T(\lambda)

One final tool is needed in order to complete the construction
of our
k
-quasi-triangular realization.
After our realization is initially brought into quasi-triangular form,
it may happen that the off-diagonal blocks have increased
in degree beyond the target for the final matrix polynomial,
since no control of these blocks has even been attempted
in the early stages of the construction.
Thus we need some method to bring these degrees
back within the target range.
Furthermore, it is important to do this by unimodular transformations,
so that the desired finite spectral structure will not be spoiled.
Lemma
3.22
, a generalization of Lemma 2.4 from
[
20
]
,
shows how to achieve this goal.

Two preliminaries are needed for Lemma
3.22
.
First it will be helpful to recall the notion of the “diagonals” of a matrix,
and more generally the block-diagonals of a block-partitioned matrix.

Definition 3.20

(Diagonals of a matrix)
.

The
\ell^{th}
-diagonal
of a matrix
A

is the set of entries
a_{ij}
of
A
such that
j-i=\ell
.
(Note that the
0^{th}
-diagonal is conventionally known as the main diagonal of
A
.)
If
A
is block-partitioned with blocks
A_{ij}
,
then the
\ell^{th}
-block-diagonal
of
A

is the collection of blocks
A_{ij}
with
j-i=\ell
.

A second important background fact concerns the division of matrix polynomials.
We recall now this fundamental result for the convenience of the reader.

Lemma 3.21

(Division of matrix polynomials)
.

Suppose
A(\lambda)
is any
m\times n
matrix polynomial over an arbitrary field
\,\mathbb{F}
.
Furthermore, let
B(\lambda)
and
C(\lambda)
be any two

strictly regular
matrix polynomials over
\,\mathbb{F}
,
with size
m\times m
and
n\times n
, respectively.
Then we have:

(a)

Left division by
B
:
There exist unique
m\times n
matrix polynomials
Q_{\ell}(\lambda)
and
R_{\ell}(\lambda)

such that

A(\lambda)\;=\;B(\lambda)Q_{\ell}(\lambda)\,+\,R_{\ell}(\lambda)\,,

and either
R_{\ell}(\lambda)=0
,
or
R_{\ell}(\lambda)
is nonzero with
\deg R_{\ell}<\deg B
.
The matrices
Q_{\ell}(\lambda)
and
R_{\ell}(\lambda)

are called the left quotient and left remainder of
A(\lambda)
, respectively,
upon (left) division by
B(\lambda)
.

(b)

Right division by
C
:
There exist unique
m\times n
matrix polynomials
Q_{r}(\lambda)
and
R_{r}(\lambda)

such that

A(\lambda)\;=\;Q_{r}(\lambda)C(\lambda)\,+\,R_{r}(\lambda)\,,

and either
R_{r}(\lambda)=0
,
or
R_{r}(\lambda)
is nonzero with
\deg R_{r}<\deg C
.
The matrices
Q_{r}(\lambda)
and
R_{r}(\lambda)

are called the right quotient and right remainder of
A(\lambda)
, respectively,
upon (right) division by
C(\lambda)
.

Proof.

See
[
7
, Ch. 4]
,
[
9
, Sect. 6.3]
, or
[
11
, Sect. 7.2]
.
∎

Lemma 3.22

(Degree reduction of off-diagonal blocks)
.

Let
T(\lambda)\in\mathbb{F}[\lambda]^{n\times n}

be a block upper triangular matrix polynomial,
partitioned into blocks such that all of the
s
diagonal blocks

T_{ii}(\lambda)\in\mathbb{F}[\lambda]^{n_{i}\times n_{i}}

are
strictly regular
. Then
T(\lambda)
is unimodularly equivalent to a block upper triangular matrix polynomial

\widetilde{T}(\lambda)
with exactly the same diagonal blocks as
T(\lambda)
,
and with off-diagonal blocks satisfying

\deg\widetilde{T}_{ij}(\lambda)\;<\;\mathop{\rm min}\bigl\{\,\deg T_{ii}(\lambda),\,\deg T_{jj}(\lambda)\,\bigr\}

(3.10)

for
\,1\leq i<j\leq s
.

Proof.

Let us begin by focusing
on a single fixed but arbitrary off-diagonal block
T_{ij}(\lambda)
with
i<j
,
and showing how to reduce its degree by a unimodular transformation
so as to satisfy the condition (
3.10
).
It may be that (
3.10
) is already satisfied;
in this case do nothing.
Otherwise, suppose first that
\deg T_{ii}\leq\deg T_{jj}
.
Then by Lemma
3.21
we can
divide
T_{ij}(\lambda)
by
T_{ii}(\lambda)

on the left

to obtain

T_{ij}(\lambda)\,=\,T_{ii}(\lambda)Q_{ij}(\lambda)+R_{ij}(\lambda)\;\text{ with }\;R_{ij}(\lambda)\,=\,0\;\text{ or }\;\deg R_{ij}(\lambda)<\deg T_{ii}(\lambda)\,,

and hence
T_{ij}(\lambda)-T_{ii}(\lambda)Q_{ij}(\lambda)=R_{ij}(\lambda)
.
Now define the unimodular matrix

V_{ij}(\lambda)\,:=\;\left[\begin{array}[]{ccccc}I_{a}&&&&\\
&I_{n_{i}}&&-Q_{ij}(\lambda)&\\
&&I_{b}&&\\
&&&I_{n_{j}}&\\
&&&&I_{c}\end{array}\right]\,,\;\text{ where }\quad a=\sum_{m=1}^{i-1}n_{m}\,,\;b=\sum_{m=i+1}^{j-1}n_{m}\,,\;c=\sum_{m=j+1}^{s}n_{m}\,.

Multiplying
T(\lambda)
on the right by
V_{ij}(\lambda)

has the effect of an elementary block-column operation,
which replaces the block
T_{ij}(\lambda)
by
\widetilde{T}_{ij}(\lambda)=R_{ij}(\lambda)
,
thus satisfying condition (
3.10
)
at this one location.
In addition we see that the diagonal blocks of
T(\lambda)

remain unchanged by this block-column operation.
Indeed, the
only
other blocks of
T(\lambda)

that may even possibly be affected
are the ones directly above the
(i,j)
-block,
i.e., blocks
T_{\ell j}(\lambda)
with
\ell\leq i
.

On the other hand, if
\deg T_{ii}>\deg T_{jj}

then something analogous can be done.
In this case use Lemma
3.21

to divide
T_{ij}(\lambda)
by
T_{jj}(\lambda)

on the right

to obtain

T_{ij}(\lambda)\,=\,\widehat{Q}_{ij}(\lambda)T_{jj}(\lambda)+\widehat{R}_{ij}(\lambda)\;\text{ with }\;\widehat{R}_{ij}(\lambda)\,=\,0\;\text{ or }\;\deg\widehat{R}_{ij}(\lambda)<\deg T_{jj}(\lambda)\,,

and hence
T_{ij}(\lambda)-\widehat{Q}_{ij}(\lambda)T_{jj}(\lambda)=\widehat{R}_{ij}(\lambda)
.
Define the unimodular matrix

U_{ij}(\lambda)\,:=\;\left[\begin{array}[]{ccccc}I_{a}&&&&\\
&I_{n_{i}}&&-\widehat{Q}_{ij}(\lambda)&\\
&&I_{b}&&\\
&&&I_{n_{j}}&\\
&&&&I_{c}\end{array}\right]\,,\;\text{ where }\quad a=\sum_{m=1}^{i-1}n_{m}\,,\;b=\sum_{m=i+1}^{j-1}n_{m}\,,\;c=\sum_{m=j+1}^{s}n_{m}\,,

and multiply
T(\lambda)
on the left by
U_{ij}(\lambda)
.
This has the effect of an elementary block-row operation,
which replaces the block
T_{ij}(\lambda)
by
\widetilde{T}_{ij}(\lambda)=\widehat{R}_{ij}(\lambda)
,
once again satisfying condition (
3.10
)
at this one location.
In the product
U_{ij}(\lambda)T(\lambda)
,
the only other blocks of
T(\lambda)
that can possibly affected
are those to the right of the
(i,j)
-block,
i.e., blocks
T_{i\ell}(\lambda)
with
\ell\geq j
.
Altogether, then, the only possible collateral damage that can be inflicted
on
T(\lambda)
by this degree reduction of the
(i,j)
-block
is to the blocks of
T(\lambda)
in the L-shaped region marked in (
3.11
).

T(\lambda)\;=\;\left[\begin{array}[]{cccccccc}\diamond&&&&{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\bigl\|}&&&\\[-5.0pt]
&\ddots&&&{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\Bigl\lVert}&&&\\[4.0pt]
&&T_{ii}&&T_{ij}&{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}***}&\hskip-7.11317pt{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}***\,*}&\hskip-7.68222pt{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}***}\\
&&&\ddots&&&&\\
&&&&T_{jj}&&&\\
&&&&&\ddots&&\\
&&&&&&\ddots&\\
&&&&&&&\hskip-8.53581pt\diamond\end{array}\right]

(3.11)

It is important to keep this L-shape firmly in mind
as we see how to order the block degree reductions
so that no individual block’s degree reduction
spoils a block that has already had its degree reduced.

The key observation here is that whenever a block
T_{ij}(\lambda)

has its degree reduced by the procedure described above,
then all of the other blocks in
T(\lambda)
that are affected by that reduction
lie on “higher” diagonals of
T(\lambda)
than the diagonal of
T_{ij}(\lambda)
.
More precisely, if
T_{ij}(\lambda)
lies on the
\ell^{th}
-block-diagonal of
T(\lambda)
,
then all other blocks affected by that degree reduction
lie on an
m^{th}
-block-diagonal with
m>\ell
.
Thus a single
sweep-by-diagonals
through the off-diagonal blocks
will have the desired effect
of achieving condition (
3.10
)
on
all
off-diagonal blocks simultaneously.
To do this kind of sweep,
first target each of the blocks in the
1^{th}
-block-diagonal for degree reduction,
in any order.
After visiting each block in the
1^{th}
-block-diagonal,
go next to the
2^{th}
-block-diagonal and do a degree reduction
on each of these blocks, again in any order.
Because of the key observation,
none of the degree reductions done in the
1^{th}
-block-diagonal
will be spoiled by the degree reductions done in the
2^{th}
-block-diagonal,
and none of the
2^{th}
-block-diagonal degree reductions will spoil each other.
Continue in this manner,
moving up one block-diagonal at a time,
until each off-diagonal block has been visited exactly once.
At this point, the desired matrix polynomial
\widetilde{T}(\lambda)

will have been attained.
\square

We finally have all the tools we will need to “un-triangularize”
T(\lambda)

into a degree-

,
k
-quasi-triangular realization
of the original list of finite spectral data.
For convenience, we recall the statement of Theorem

Theorem

(Quasi-Triangular Realization: Strictly Regular Case)
.

Suppose a list of
m
nonconstant monic polynomials

over an arbitrary field
\,\mathbb{F}

s_{1}(\lambda)\,|\,s_{2}(\lambda)\,|\,\dotsb\,|\,s_{m}(\lambda)
.
Let
\sigma:=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\deg\bigl(s_{i}(\lambda)\bigr)
,
and define

\mathbb{F}
-irreducible factors
of the polynomials
s_{i}(\lambda)
for

.
Then for any choice of nonzero

such that

and

,
there exists an
n\times n
, degree
d
, strictly regular matrix polynomial

over
\mathbb{F}

that is
k
-quasi-triangular,
and has exactly the given polynomials

n-m

Q(\lambda)
can always be chosen so that the degree
of every entry in any off-diagonal block of
Q(\lambda)

is strictly less than
d
.

Proof.

From the given spectral data, begin by constructing the
n\times n
Smith form

S(\lambda)=\mathop{\rm diag}\{\,\,\underbrace{1,\dotsc,1}_{n-m},s_{1}(\lambda),\dotsc,s_{m}(\lambda)\,\}\,.

Now we can use the tools developed in Corollary
3.14
,
Lemma
3.15
, Corollary
3.4
, Corollary
2.12
,
and Lemma
3.22
to build the desired
k
-quasi-triangular realization
of the spectral data contained in
S(\lambda)
,
in the following five steps:

•

Use
S(\lambda)
as input to Corollary
3.14

to generate an upper triangular
T(\lambda)
that is unimodularly equivalent to
S(\lambda)
,
and has a diagonal degree vector
\,\deg\bigl(\mathop{\rm diag}T(\lambda)\bigr)

k
-homogeneous.

•

Use the natural vector
\,\deg\bigl(\mathop{\rm diag}T(\lambda)\bigr)
,
with average value
\mu=d
,
as input to Lemma
3.15
,
and find a homogeneous partitioning of the degrees of the diagonal entries of
T(\lambda)

into
\ell

The corresponding partitioning of the diagonal entries themselves,
with the elements of the entry sublists arranged into
\ell

T(\lambda)
.

•

Implement this rearrangement of the diagonal entries of
T(\lambda)

via the triangularity-preserving unimodular transformations of
T(\lambda)

provided by Corollary
3.4

each of which has the effect of simply performing an interchange of adjacent diagonal entries.
(Of course some of the off-diagonal entries are being changed in the process,
but we do not try to keep any control of them at this stage of the construction.)
Denote the resulting upper triangular matrix by
\widehat{T}(\lambda)
,
and partition this
\widehat{T}(\lambda)
into blocks

\widehat{T}(\lambda)\;=\;\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}\widehat{T}_{11}(\lambda)&\widehat{T}_{12}(\lambda)&\dotsm&\widehat{T}_{1\ell}(\lambda)\\
&\widehat{T}_{22}(\lambda)&\dotsm&\widehat{T}_{2\ell}(\lambda)\\[-2.0pt]
&&\ddots&\vdots\\
&&&\widehat{T}_{\ell\ell}(\lambda)\end{array}\right]\,,

(3.12)

so that each (upper triangular and square) diagonal block
\widehat{T}_{jj}(\lambda)

has diagonal entries that correspond to the sublist
{\cal S}_{j}

of the homogeneous partitioning of
\,\deg\bigl(\mathop{\rm diag}T(\lambda)\bigr)

For each

,
and the average degree of the diagonal entries of

.
So the sum of the degrees of the diagonal entries of

•

By Corollary

P_{jj}(\lambda):=\,\widehat{U}_{jj}(\lambda)\widehat{T}_{jj}(\lambda)\widehat{V}_{jj}(\lambda)\,,

where each
\widehat{U}_{jj}(\lambda)

Now define the

&P_{22}(\lambda)&\dotsm&P_{2\ell}(\lambda)\\
&&\ddots&\vdots\\
&&&P_{\ell\ell}(\lambda)\end{array}\right].

(3.13)

This matrix

.
But

•

The final step brings the degrees of the off-diagonal blocks back under control,

&Q_{22}(\lambda)&\dotsm&Q_{2\ell}(\lambda)\\

&&&Q_{\ell\ell}(\lambda)\end{array}\right]\,.

(3.14)

This polynomial matrix

k
-quasi-triangular,
has degree

,
and is unimodularly equivalent to the original

,
and hence has exactly the given finite spectral data.
To see that

we regard

Since by construction the sum of the degrees

4
Including infinite spectral data in a realization

In this section we extend the range of our quasi-triangular realization construction
to handle spectral data arising from a general regular matrix polynomial
— i.e., data that may now include nontrivial infinite spectral structure.
The main tool that allows us to achieve this extension
are Möbius transformations,
employing a now well-used technique;
see
[
4
]
,
[
12
]
, and
[
19
]
.
In a nutshell, the strategy of this technique is to
use an appropriately chosen Möbius transformation
to translate a realization problem involving a mixture
of finite and infinite spectral data
into one that has
only
finite spectral data,
solve this strictly regular realization problem,
and then translate the solution back to the original spectral data
using the inverse Möbius transformation.

However, before we are able to implement this strategy,
it will be necessary to reexamine one of the fundamental properties
of Möbius transformations from
[
12
]
, and recast it in a way that enables us to work with these transformations
more smoothly in the context of arbitrary fields
\mathbb{F}
,
in particular in the presence of higher degree
\mathbb{F}
-irreducible divisors
in spectral data.
This will be the task of Section
4.1
.
With this reimagined property of Möbius transformations in hand,
in Section
4.2

we then solve the quasi-triangular realization problem
with nontrivial infinite spectral structure.
This gives us the final result needed to now easily prove
in Section
4.3

the featured result of the paper,
the quasi-triangularization of arbitrary regular matrix polynomials.

Before embarking on this final stage, though,
it is worth pointing out several subtle issues
that arise in the course of carrying out this strategy.
The first concerns an extra hypothesis that is only needed
if the field
\mathbb{F}
is finite,
and even then only to exclude very special types of spectral data set.
When
\mathbb{F}
is finite, then it is possible that
every
element of
\mathbb{F}
,
as well as
\infty
, may appear in a given spectral data set as an eigenvalue.
If that happens,
then any possible Möbius transformation
that one might attempt to use
will simply map the set of eigenvalues bijectively to itself,
and thus will not be able to transform the given spectral data
into one that has only finite spectral data.
To exclude this one problematic scenario,
it has been necessary to include an additional hypothesis,
i.e., that there must be some element of
\mathbb{F}

that is
not
an eigenvalue in the given spectral data.
Of course for any infinite field
\mathbb{F}
,
this hypothesis always holds,
and so has no impact on the range of spectral data sets
to which the argument applies.
But for finite fields, having this one additional condition satisfied
is sufficient to make the rest of the argument work smoothly.
(When this condition is violated,
it is not known whether quasi-triangular realizations exist or not.)

A second issue concerns the reduction of off-diagonal degrees
to be less than the target degree, as was done in the strictly regular case.
When there is nontrivial spectral structure at
\infty
,
such a reduction may not be possible.
The difficulty is that this reduction is achieved via unimodular transformations,
which may alter the spectral structure at
\infty
.
Indeed, Example
4.8
in Section
4.2

gives a concrete illustration
of how not only the infinite spectral structure,
but even the overall degree of the realization itself,
can be spoiled by doing the kind of degree reduction
of off-diagonal blocks described in Lemma
3.22
.

4.1
Möbius transformation of spectral structure over an arbitrary field

We begin this section by reviewing the effect of Möbius transformations
on the spectral structure of a matrix polynomial,
in the manner developed in
[
12
]
.
In that paper the emphasis was on scalars in the ambient field
\mathbb{F}

(plus
\infty
) as potential eigenvalues,
and so the development was best adapted to algebraically closed fields.
However, when working with matrix polynomials over arbitrary fields,
with irreducible divisors of higher degree,
the formulation in
[
12
]
can be rather inconvenient,
even a bit clumsy to use.
Thus our aim in this section is to reformulate the relationship
between partial multiplicity sequences and Möbius transformations
in such a way that it works smoothly and efficiently over all fields,
and is well-adapted to our extended notion of partial multiplicity sequences
for irreducible divisors of any degree, as defined in (
2.2
).
We begin, though, with a brief review of the relevant
concepts, notation, and results from
[
12
]
,
in particular Theorem 5.3 from that paper.

If
P(\lambda)
is a matrix polynomial over
\mathbb{F}
,
and
\mu_{0}\in\mathbb{F}_{\infty}
is any scalar in the “extended” field

\mathbb{F}_{\infty}:=\mathbb{F}\cup\{\infty\}
,
then in
[
12
]
the partial multiplicity sequence
associated with
\mu_{0}
was denoted by
{\cal J}(P,\mu_{0})
.
Note that the letter
{\cal J}
was chosen there because
the partial multiplicity sequences for
P
for
all
scalars
\mu_{0}\in\mathbb{F}_{\infty}

was collectively termed the “Jordan characteristic” of
P
.
In the current paper, the partial multiplicity sequence
{\cal J}(P,\mu_{0})

is associated with the degree one irreducible polynomial
\lambda-\mu_{0}
,
rather than with the scalar
\mu_{0}
.
Thus we have the following equivalence between notations:

{\cal J}(P,\mu_{0})\;\equiv\;{\cal P}\!{\cal M}(P,\lambda-\mu_{0})\,.

(4.1)

Continuing the review of
[
12
]
—
to any nonsingular

A=\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]
over
\mathbb{F}

there is an associated
Möbius function

\mathsf{m}_{A}(\lambda):\mathbb{F}_{\infty}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{\infty}
,
which is a bijection of scalars given by the formula

\mathsf{m}_{A}(\lambda)\,:=\;\frac{a\lambda+b}{\,c\lambda+d\,}\,,

and a Möbius transformation on matrix polynomials of grade
g

given by

\mathbf{M}_{A}\bigl(P(\lambda)\bigr)\,:=\;(c\lambda+d)^{g}\,P\bigl(\mathsf{m}_{A}(\lambda)\bigr)\,.

Note that this is exactly the same as in Definition
2.13
.
Now a key result from
[
12
]
establishes a simple relationship
between the Jordan characteristics of
P
and
\mathbf{M}_{A}(P)
.

Theorem 4.1

(Theorem 5.3 from
[
12
]
)
.

Suppose
P(\lambda)
is an
m\times n
matrix polynomial of grade
g
over an arbitrary field
\,\mathbb{F}
,
and
A\in GL(2,\mathbb{F})
is a nonsingular matrix
with associated Möbius function
\mathsf{m}_{A}(\lambda)

and Möbius transformation
\mathbf{M}_{A}
.
Then for any
\mu_{0}\in\mathbb{F}_{\infty}
,

{\cal J}\bigl(\mathbf{M}_{A}(P),\mu_{0}\bigr)\;=\;{\cal J}\bigl(P,\mathsf{m}_{A}(\mu_{0})\bigr)\,,

(4.2)

or equivalently,

{\cal J}\bigl(\mathbf{M}_{A}(P),\mathsf{m}_{A^{-1}}(\mu_{0})\bigr)\;=\;{\cal J}\bigl(P,\mu_{0}\bigr)\,.

(4.3)

The formulas in Theorem
4.1
have one glaring drawback
when trying to capture the effect of a Möbius transformation
on the complete spectral structure of a matrix polynomial
P

over a field
\mathbb{F}
that is
not
algebraically closed.
The problem is that the irreducible divisors of
P

may all have degree
2
or larger,
and thus have
no
eigenvalues in
\mathbb{F}_{\infty}
at all.
In this scenario the formulas in Theorem
4.1

tell us nothing at all about the spectral effects of the Möbius transformation.
At least not directly.
The only way to recover any information about the spectral effects of
\mathbf{M}_{A}

would be to pass to the algebraic closure
\overline{\mathbb{F}}
,
which though feasible,
may become an unwelcome annoyance.
It would be very useful to instead
have some analog of Theorem
4.1

that applies directly to partial multiplicity sequences
associated with irreducible divisors of any degree,
i.e., to the
{\cal P}\!{\cal M}(P,\chi)
defined earlier in (
2.2
).
There is indeed such an analog,
and the goal of the remainder of this section is to establish
this extension of Theorem
4.1
.
We claim that the following relationship holds:

{\cal P}\!{\cal M}\bigl(\mathbf{M}_{A}(P),\mathbf{M}_{A}(\chi)\bigr)\;=\;{\cal P}\!{\cal M}(P,\chi)\,.

(4.4)

To properly interpret this formula, however,
two conventions must be observed:

•

On the left-hand side of (
4.4
),

\mathbf{M}_{A}(P)
is taken with respect to the specified grade for
P
,
but
\mathbf{M}_{A}(\chi)
should always be taken with
\mathop{\rm grade}\chi
equal to
\deg\chi
,
with just the one exception described next.

•

If
\chi(\lambda)=\beta
is any
nonzero
constant,
then
\chi
is to be regarded as a
grade one
polynomial,
i.e., as
\alpha\lambda+\beta
with
\alpha=0
.

Note that the naive intuition underlying this second convention
is that the “root” of
0\lambda+\beta
is
\lambda=-\beta/0=\infty
,
and so
0\lambda+\beta
can play the role of a “polynomial stand-in”
for an eigenvalue at
\infty
.
Thus we will now use the notation
{\cal P}\!{\cal M}(P,0\lambda+\beta)

to replace the earlier temporary notation
{\cal P}\!{\cal M}(P,\infty)

for the partial multiplicity sequence associated with an eigenvalue at
\infty
.
We will see in Theorem
4.6
and its proof
that this now makes the formula (
4.4
)
internally consistent and universally applicable
to all partial multiplicity sequences,
both for finite and for infinite spectral structure.
Some additional motivation and justification for this
(perhaps unexpected?)
second convention is given in Remark
4.2
.

Remark 4.2
.

Let
{\cal G}_{1}
denote the set of all
nonzero
grade one scalar (i.e.,
1\times 1
) polynomials

L(\lambda)=\alpha\lambda+\beta
over
\mathbb{F}
.
It is well known
[
12
]
that any Möbius transformation
\mathbf{M}_{A}

defines a bijection
{\cal G}_{1}\rightarrow{\cal G}_{1}
,
and the associated Möbius function
\mathsf{m}_{A}

defines a bijection
\mathbb{F}_{\infty}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{\infty}

on the extended field of scalars
\mathbb{F}_{\infty}
.
The purpose of this remark is to briefly explore the parallelism
between the bijection
\mathbf{M}_{A}
and the bijection
\mathsf{m}_{A^{-1}}

(
not

\mathsf{m}_{A}
).

What underlies this parallelism is the simple calculation of
\mathbf{M}_{A}(\lambda-\mu_{0})
,
which also plays a central role in the proof of Theorem
4.6
.
Using Definition
2.13
, it is not hard to see that
\mathbf{M}_{A}(\lambda-\mu_{0})

is (usually) a nonzero scalar multiple of
\lambda-\mathsf{m}_{A^{-1}}(\mu_{0})
,
with the one possible exception of being just a nonzero scalar.
Now recall that in the context of discussing spectral data,
we are associating the degree one
\mathbb{F}
-irreducible
(\lambda-\mu_{0})

with the eigenvalue
\mu_{0}
.
So we see that the mapping of degree one irreducibles by
\mathbf{M}_{A}

is mirrored by the mapping of eigenvalues in
\mathbb{F}
by
\mathsf{m}_{A^{-1}}
,
at least for most
\mu_{0}
.

To make this parallelism more precise,
let us define a map
\rho:{\cal G}_{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{\infty}

that sends
\alpha\lambda+\beta\in{\cal G}_{1}
with
\alpha\neq 0

to its root
\frac{\,-\beta\,}{\alpha}\in\mathbb{F}
,
i.e., define
\rho(\alpha\lambda+\beta):=\frac{\,-\beta\,}{\alpha}
.
This specifies
\rho
on most of the desired domain
{\cal G}_{1}
,
and enables us to express the parallelism in the diagram

\begin{CD}{\cal G}_{1}@>{\mathbf{M}_{A}}>{}>{\cal G}_{1}\\
@V{\rho}V{}V@V{}V{\rho}V\\
\mathbb{F}_{\infty}@>{\mathsf{m}_{A^{-1}}}>{}>\mathbb{F}_{\infty}\end{CD}\qquad.

(4.5)

For all elements of
{\cal G}_{1}
with
\alpha\neq 0
(with at most one exception),
we see that the diagram (
4.5
) commutes.
But what about elements of
{\cal G}_{1}
with
\alpha=0

(and hence
\beta\neq 0
), and the one possible exceptional case
when
\mathbf{M}_{A}(\lambda-\mu_{0})
is just a nonzero scalar?
Is there a “natural” way to complete the definition of the map
\rho

to the whole domain
{\cal G}_{1}
,
and to do so in such a way
that (
4.5
) commutes?
There is indeed a unique way to achieve this,
and that is to define
\rho(0\lambda+\beta):=\infty\in\mathbb{F}_{\infty}
,
for any
\beta\neq 0
.
Thus we see from another direction
why it is natural to associate the grade
1
polynomial
0\lambda+\beta

with an eigenvalue at
\infty
.
Note that the commuting of the diagram (
4.5
)
holds for any
A\in GL(2,\mathbb{F})

and its associated Möbius transformation and function.

The simplest (and perhaps most revealing) example of (
4.5
)
uses
\mathbf{M}_{R}
with
R=\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]

i.e.,
\mathbf{M}_{R}=\mathop{\rm rev}_{1}
.
In this case with
\alpha\neq 0
and
\beta\neq 0

we have
\mathbf{M}_{R}(\alpha\lambda+\beta)=\beta\lambda+\alpha

with reciprocal roots
\frac{\,-\beta\,}{\alpha}
and
\frac{\,-\alpha\,}{\beta}
, respectively,
and also
\mathsf{m}_{R^{-1}}(\mu_{0})=\mathsf{m}_{R}(\mu_{0})=\frac{1}{\,\mu_{0}\,}

is the reciprocal map.
So clearly everything in the diagram commutes when
\alpha
and
\beta

are
both
nonzero.
But if
\alpha=0
,
then with our convention we have
\mathbf{M}_{R}(0\lambda+\beta)=\beta\lambda
,
with roots
\infty
and
0
,
which are commonly regarded in the literature as being “reciprocal”.
Thus we see directly when
A=R
that (
4.5
) commutes
for all elements of
{\cal G}_{1}
.

It is also useful to note that (
4.5
) still commutes
if we pass to the equivalence classes discussed
earlier in Remark
2.2
.
That is, let us declare that two
\mathbb{F}
-irreducible polynomials
are equivalent if one is a nonzero scalar multiple of the other,
i.e.,
\chi\sim c\chi
for any nonzero scalar
c\in\mathbb{F}
.
Letting
\widetilde{{\cal G}}_{1}
denote the resulting set of equivalence classes
of grade one polynomials over
\mathbb{F}
,
then both of the maps
\mathbf{M}_{A}
and
\rho

respect these equivalence classes,
and so induce well-defined quotient maps
\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{A}
and
\widetilde{\rho}
,
which are both bijections.
This, then, gives us the following commutative diagram
in which all of the mappings are bijective.

\begin{CD}\widetilde{{\cal G}}_{1}@>{\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{A}}>{}>\widetilde{{\cal G}}_{1}\\
@V{\widetilde{\rho}}V{}V@V{}V{\widetilde{\rho}}V\\
\mathbb{F}_{\infty}@>{\mathsf{m}_{A^{-1}}}>{}>\mathbb{F}_{\infty}\end{CD}

(4.6)

Finally, note that with our conventions, the scenario
\mathbf{M}_{A}(\chi)=\beta

cannot
arise in (
4.4
)
if the
\mathbb{F}
-irreducible
\chi
has
\deg(\chi)\geq 2
;
it can only occur if
\chi\in{\cal G}_{1}
.
This is not obvious, but follows from a property to be proved
in Lemma
4.4
,
namely that for any
\mathbb{F}
-irreducible
\chi
with
\deg(\chi)\geq 2
,
and
\mathbf{M}_{A}(\chi)
taken with respect to degree,
then
\deg\mathbf{M}_{A}(\chi)=\deg(\chi)
.

Before proceeding to prove (
4.4
) in Theorem
4.6
,
we first establish some preliminary lemmas.
The first of these lemmas appeared in
[
12
]
as Corollary 3.24a,
but we recall it here together with its simple proof
for the convenience of the reader.

Lemma 4.3

(Basic product property of Möbius transformations)
.

Let
p,q\in\mathbb{F}[\lambda]
be nonzero scalar polynomials,
and let
A=\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]\in GL(2,\mathbb{F})
.
Letting the grades of
p
,
q
, and
pq
all be chosen to be equal to their degrees,
then we have
\mathbf{M}_{A}(pq)=\mathbf{M}_{A}(p)\mathbf{M}_{A}(q)
.

Proof.

Let
d_{1}=\deg p
and
d_{2}=\deg q
.
Then from Definition
2.13
we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}_{A}(pq)

\displaystyle\;=\;(c\lambda+d)^{d_{1}+d_{2}}\,p\bigl(\mathsf{m}_{A}(\lambda)\bigr)\,q\bigl(\mathsf{m}_{A}(\lambda)\bigr)

\displaystyle\;=\;(c\lambda+d)^{d_{1}}\,p\bigl(\mathsf{m}_{A}(\lambda)\bigr)\cdot(c\lambda+d)^{d_{2}}\,q\bigl(\mathsf{m}_{A}(\lambda)\bigr)\;=\;\mathbf{M}_{A}(p)\,\mathbf{M}_{A}(q)\,.

∎

The next result was mentioned in Section
2

under the name Lemma
2.15
.
We recall it here and provide the proof postponed from earlier.

Lemma 4.4
.

Suppose
\mathbb{F}
is an arbitrary field,
and
\chi(\lambda)
is any
\mathbb{F}
-irreducible scalar polynomial with
\deg\chi\geq 2
.
Let
\mathbf{M}_{A}
be the Möbius transformation
associated with any

A=\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]\in GL(2,\mathbb{F})
.
Then with
\mathop{\rm grade}(\chi)
taken to be equal to
\deg(\chi)
,
the transformation
\mathbf{M}_{A}
preserves both the degree and the
\mathbb{F}
-irreducibility of
\chi
.
That is,
\mathbf{M}_{A}(\chi)
is
\mathbb{F}
-irreducible,
and
\,\deg\bigl(\mathbf{M}_{A}(\chi)\bigr)=\deg(\chi)
.

Proof.

That
\mathbf{M}_{A}
preserves
\mathbb{F}
-irreducibility was shown in
[
12
, Corollary 3.24]
.
To see why degree is preserved, we look at
\chi(\lambda)

over the algebraic closure
\overline{\mathbb{F}}
of
\mathbb{F}
.
Over
\overline{\mathbb{F}}
we may factor
\chi
completely into linear factors,
i.e.,
\,\chi(\lambda)\,=\,k(\lambda-r_{1})(\lambda-r_{2})\dotsb(\lambda-r_{d})
,
where
d=\deg\chi\geq 2
and
k\in\mathbb{F}
.
Now observe that for
\chi
to be
\mathbb{F}
-irreducible,

all
of these roots
r_{i}
must be in
\overline{\mathbb{F}}\setminus\mathbb{F}
,
otherwise
\chi
would be
\mathbb{F}
-reducible.
Computing
\mathbf{M}_{A}(\chi)
with grade equal to degree
d
,
and using the multiplicative property of Möbius transformations
in Lemma
4.3
,
we obtain

\mathbf{M}_{A}(\chi)\;=\;k\,\mathbf{M}_{A}(\lambda-r_{1})\cdot\mathbf{M}_{A}(\lambda-r_{2})\dotsb\mathbf{M}_{A}(\lambda-r_{d})\,,

where each of these transformations
\mathbf{M}_{A}(\lambda-r_{j})
is computed with respect to grade
1
.
Using the definition, we easily see that

\mathbf{M}_{A}(\lambda-r_{j})\,=\,(a-cr_{j})\lambda\,+\,(b-dr_{j})\,.

(4.7)

Now consider two cases:
c=0
and
c\neq 0
.
If
c=0
, then since
A
is nonsingular we must have
a\neq 0
,
so each of the factors
\mathbf{M}_{A}(\lambda-r_{j})
will have degree one,
and
\mathbf{M}_{A}(\chi)
will have degree
d=\deg\chi
.
On the other hand, if
c\neq 0
,
then once again each of these
\mathbf{M}_{A}(\lambda-r_{j})
will have degree one,
and hence
\deg\bigl(\mathbf{M}_{A}(\chi)\bigr)=d=\deg\chi
,
unless one of the roots
r_{j}
satisfies
a-cr_{j}=0
,
i.e.,
r_{j}=\tfrac{a}{c}
.
This, however, is impossible,
since
\tfrac{a}{c}\in\mathbb{F}
, but
r_{j}\in\overline{\mathbb{F}}\setminus\mathbb{F}
.
∎

Lemma 4.5
.

Suppose
\,\mathbb{F}
is an arbitrary field,
and
\,\widehat{\mathbb{F}}\supseteq\mathbb{F}
is any field extension of
\,\mathbb{F}
.
Then polynomials
p,q\in\mathbb{F}[\lambda]
are coprime in
\,\mathbb{F}[\lambda]

if and only if they are coprime in
\,\widehat{\mathbb{F}}[\lambda]
.
More specifically, if
p,q\in\mathbb{F}[\lambda]
are distinct
\mathbb{F}
-irreducible polynomials,
then as polynomials over the algebraic closure
\,\overline{\mathbb{F}}
,

p
and
q
have no roots in common.

Proof.

Suppose
p
and
q
are coprime in
\,\mathbb{F}[\lambda]
.
Then
p
and
q
satisfy the Bezout identity
pr+qt\equiv 1
,
for some polynomials
r,t\in\mathbb{F}[\lambda]
.
This identity still holds for
p,q,r,t
viewed as polynomials over
\widehat{\mathbb{F}}
,
so
p
and
q
are coprime in
\widehat{\mathbb{F}}[\lambda]
.
Conversely, if
p
and
q
are coprime in
\widehat{\mathbb{F}}[\lambda]
,
then they have no nontrivial common factor in
\widehat{\mathbb{F}}[\lambda]
,
and hence no nontrivial common factor in the smaller ring
\mathbb{F}[\lambda]
,
and thus are coprime in
\mathbb{F}[\lambda]
.
The statement about
\mathbb{F}
-irreducible polynomials
p
and
q
now follows immediately,
since
distinct

\mathbb{F}
-irreducibles are necessarily coprime in
\mathbb{F}[\lambda]
.
∎

We are now in a position to prove the main result of this section,
i.e., the formula that describes the effect of Möbius transformations
on spectral structure.
It is important to emphasize that this formula holds in complete generality
for
all
matrix polynomials
(regular or singular, of any size, over arbitrary fields),
for
all
irreducible divisors (of any degree),
and for
all
Möbius transformations
\mathbf{M}_{A}
,
whatever the underlying (nonsingular) matrix
A
.
Note also that the proof makes extensive use
of the previously-known formulas
in Theorem
4.1
.

Theorem 4.6

(Effect of Möbius on spectral structure over arbitrary fields)
.

Let
P(\lambda)
be any grade
g
matrix polynomial over
\,\mathbb{F}
,
where
\mathbb{F}
is an arbitrary field.
Also let
A=\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]\in GL(2,\mathbb{F})

be any nonsingular matrix over
\mathbb{F}
,
with associated Möbius transformation
\mathbf{M}_{A}
.
Then for any
\mathbb{F}
-irreducible scalar polynomial
\chi(\lambda)
,
including the grade one polynomial
(0\mkern 1.0mu\lambda+\beta)
with
\beta\neq 0
,
we have

{\cal P}\!{\cal M}\bigl(\mathbf{M}_{A}(P),\mathbf{M}_{A}(\chi)\bigr)\;=\;{\cal P}\!{\cal M}(P,\chi)\,.

(4.8)

(
Here
\mathbf{M}_{A}(P)
is taken with respect to grade
g
,
while each
\mathbf{M}_{A}(\chi)
is taken with grade equal to
\deg\chi
,
with the sole exception of the grade one

\chi(\lambda)=\mkern 1.0mu0\mkern 1.0mu\lambda+\beta
, with
\beta\neq 0
.
)

Proof.

We consider three cases,
depending on the nature of the
\mathbb{F}
-irreducible
\chi(\lambda)
involved.
In all cases we have

A=\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]\in GL(2,\mathbb{F})
,
and
A^{-1}=\frac{1}{\,\mathop{\rm det}A\,}\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}d&-b\\
-c&a\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]
.
In the following calculations, keep in mind that we are free
(because of (
2.3
))
to alter
\mathbb{F}
-irreducibles by (nonzero) scalar multiples, whenever convenient.
We will also make repeated use of the formula (
4.3
)
from Theorem
4.1
,
as well as the notation conversion formula (
4.1
),
without ever explicitly mentioning that they are being used.

(a)

[
\chi=0\lambda+\beta
, with
\beta\neq 0
and
\mathop{\rm grade}(\chi)=1
]  Given the above conventions, we have

\displaystyle{\cal P}\!{\cal M}(P,\chi)\,=\,{\cal J}(P,\infty)

\displaystyle\,=\,{\cal J}\bigl(\mathbf{M}_{A}(P),\mathsf{m}_{A^{-1}}(\infty)\bigr)

\displaystyle\,=\,{\cal J}\bigl(\mathbf{M}_{A}(P),\tfrac{d}{\,-c\,}\bigr)\,=\,{\cal P}\!{\cal M}\bigl(\mathbf{M}_{A}(P),\,c\lambda+d\bigr)\,=\,{\cal P}\!{\cal M}\bigl(\mathbf{M}_{A}(P),\,\mathbf{M}_{A}(\chi)\bigr)\,,

as desired.

(b)

[
\chi=\lambda-\mu_{0}
, with
\mu_{0}\in\mathbb{F}
and
\mathop{\rm grade}(\chi)=\deg(\chi)=1
]

Recall from (
4.7
)
that
\mathbf{M}_{A}(\lambda-\mu_{0})=(a-c\mu_{0})\lambda\,+\,(b-d\mu_{0})
.
Splitting this into two cases, depending on whether
a-c\mu_{0}
is zero or not,
we have

\mathbf{M}_{A}(\chi)\,=\,\begin{cases}(a-c\mu_{0})\bigl[\,\lambda\,-\,\mathsf{m}_{A^{-1}}(\mu_{0})\,\bigr]&\text{ if $\,a-c\mu_{0}\neq 0$\,},\\
(b-d\mu_{0})&\text{ if $\,a-c\mu_{0}=0$\,}.\end{cases}

So if
a-c\mu_{0}\neq 0
, then

\displaystyle{\cal P}\!{\cal M}(P,\chi)\,=\,{\cal J}(P,\mu_{0})

\displaystyle\,=\,{\cal J}\bigl(\mathbf{M}_{A}(P),\mathsf{m}_{A^{-1}}(\mu_{0})\bigr)

\displaystyle\,=\,{\cal P}\!{\cal M}\bigl(\mathbf{M}_{A}(P),\lambda-\mathsf{m}_{A^{-1}}(\mu_{0})\bigr)\,=\,{\cal P}\!{\cal M}\bigl(\mathbf{M}_{A}(P),\,\mathbf{M}_{A}(\chi)\bigr)\,.

On the other hand, if
a-c\mu_{0}=0
(i.e.,
\mu_{0}=\frac{a}{\,c\,}=\mathsf{m}_{A}(\infty)
),
then
b-d\mu_{0}\neq 0
because of the nonsingularity of
A
,
and we have

\displaystyle{\cal P}\!{\cal M}(P,\chi)\,=\,{\cal J}(P,\mu_{0})

\displaystyle\,=\,{\cal J}\bigl(\mathbf{M}_{A}(P),\mathsf{m}_{A^{-1}}(\mu_{0})\bigr)

\displaystyle\,=\,{\cal J}\bigl(\mathbf{M}_{A}(P),\infty\bigr)\,=\,{\cal P}\!{\cal M}\bigl(\mathbf{M}_{A}(P),\,0\lambda+(b-d\mu_{0})\bigr)\,=\,{\cal P}\!{\cal M}\bigl(\mathbf{M}_{A}(P),\,\mathbf{M}_{A}(\chi)\bigr)\,.

(c)

[
\mathop{\rm grade}(\chi)=\deg(\chi)\geq 2
]

In this argument we will go back and forth between
\mathbb{F}

and its algebraic closure
\overline{\mathbb{F}}
,
exploiting the fact that
P
,
\mathbf{M}_{A}
, and
\chi
can be viewed
either as objects over
\mathbb{F}
or as objects over
\overline{\mathbb{F}}
.

Viewing
\chi(\lambda)
as a polynomial in
\overline{\mathbb{F}}[\lambda]
,
we may factor it completely into linear factors

\chi(\lambda)\,=\,\beta(\lambda-r_{1})^{\delta_{1}}(\lambda-r_{2})^{\delta_{2}}\dotsb(\lambda-r_{k})^{\delta_{k}}\,,\quad 0\neq\beta\in\mathbb{F}\,,

(4.9)

where the roots
r_{i}
are
distinct
, and all in
\overline{\mathbb{F}}\setminus\mathbb{F}
.
(See proof of Lemma
4.4
.)
Denoting the
j^{\text{th}}
partial multiplicity of
\chi
in
P

by
\alpha_{j}:=\bigl[\mkern 1.0mu\mkern 1.0mu{\cal P}\!{\cal M}(P,\chi)\mkern 1.0mu\bigr]_{j}
,
so that the the
j^{\text{th}}
invariant polynomial
s_{j}
of
P

can be expressed as

s_{j}(\lambda)\,=\,\chi^{\alpha_{j}}\widetilde{s}_{j}(\lambda)

(4.10)

with
\chi
coprime to
\widetilde{s}_{j}
,
we can now easily see that over
\overline{\mathbb{F}}
we have

\bigl[\mkern 1.0mu\mkern 1.0mu{\cal P}\!{\cal M}(P,\lambda-r_{i})\mkern 1.0mu\bigr]_{j}=\delta_{i}\alpha_{j}
,
for each
1\leq i\leq k
.
This follows from (
4.9
) and (
4.10
),
together with the fact that
no additional copies
of
(\lambda-r_{i})

can arise from
\widetilde{s}_{j}
,
by Lemma
4.5
.
Applying the result of part (b) of this proof (over the field
\overline{\mathbb{F}}
),
we now have that

\bigl[\mkern 1.0mu\mkern 1.0mu{\cal P}\!{\cal M}(\mathbf{M}_{A}(P),\mathbf{M}_{A}\bigl(\lambda-r_{i}\bigr)\mkern 1.0mu\bigr]_{j}=\bigl[\mkern 1.0mu\mkern 1.0mu{\cal P}\!{\cal M}(P,\lambda-r_{i})\mkern 1.0mu\bigr]_{j}=\delta_{i}\alpha_{j}\,,

(4.11)

for all
1\leq i\leq k
and
1\leq j\leq\mathop{\rm rank}P
.
Note also from part(b) that

\mathbf{M}_{A}\bigl(\lambda-r_{i}\bigr)=(a-cr_{i})\bigl[\,\lambda\,-\,\mathsf{m}_{A^{-1}}(r_{i})\,\bigr]

is always degree one, since
a-cr_{i}\neq 0

(
a,c\in\mathbb{F}
, but
r_{i}\in\overline{\mathbb{F}}\setminus\mathbb{F}
).
(Although it is not strictly needed for this argument,
it is also helpful to keep in mind that the transformation
\mathbf{M}_{A}
is
bijective

on the set of grade one polynomials over
\overline{\mathbb{F}}
,
so that
\mathbf{M}_{A}(\lambda-r_{i})
is distinct from any other
\mathbf{M}_{A}(\lambda-\mu_{0})
.)

Gathering together all of the partial multiplicities in (
4.11
),
we now see that the
j^{\text{th}}
invariant polynomial of
\mathbf{M}_{A}(P)

can be written, over
\overline{\mathbb{F}}
, as

\displaystyle m_{j}(\lambda)

\displaystyle\,=\,\bigl[\mkern 1.0mu\mkern 1.0mu\mathbf{M}_{A}(\lambda-r_{1})\mkern 1.0mu\bigr]^{\delta_{1}\alpha_{j}}\,\bigl[\mkern 1.0mu\mkern 1.0mu\mathbf{M}_{A}(\lambda-r_{2})\mkern 1.0mu\bigr]^{\delta_{2}\alpha_{j}}\dotsb\bigl[\mkern 1.0mu\mkern 1.0mu\mathbf{M}_{A}(\lambda-r_{k})\mkern 1.0mu\bigr]^{\delta_{k}\alpha_{j}}\cdot\widehat{m}_{j}(\lambda)

\displaystyle\,=\,\Bigl[\mkern 1.0mu\mkern 1.0mu\bigl[\mkern 1.0mu\mkern 1.0mu\mathbf{M}_{A}(\lambda-r_{1})\mkern 1.0mu\bigr]^{\delta_{1}}\bigl[\mkern 1.0mu\mkern 1.0mu\mathbf{M}_{A}(\lambda-r_{2})\mkern 1.0mu\bigr]^{\delta_{2}}\dotsb\bigl[\mkern 1.0mu\mkern 1.0mu\mathbf{M}_{A}(\lambda-r_{k})\mkern 1.0mu\bigr]^{\delta_{k}}\Bigr]^{\alpha_{j}}\cdot\widehat{m}_{j}(\lambda)\,,

where
\widehat{m}_{j}(\lambda)
is coprime in
\overline{\mathbb{F}}[\lambda]

to each of the degree one factors
\mathbf{M}_{A}(\lambda-r_{i})
.
But by the product property of Möbius transformations
in Lemma
4.3

we also have

\bigl[\mkern 1.0mu\mkern 1.0mu\mathbf{M}_{A}(\lambda-r_{1})\mkern 1.0mu\bigr]^{\delta_{1}}\bigl[\mkern 1.0mu\mkern 1.0mu\mathbf{M}_{A}(\lambda-r_{2})\mkern 1.0mu\bigr]^{\delta_{2}}\dotsb\bigl[\mkern 1.0mu\mkern 1.0mu\mathbf{M}_{A}(\lambda-r_{k})\mkern 1.0mu\bigr]^{\delta_{k}}\\
\,=\,\mathbf{M}_{A}\bigl[(\lambda-r_{1})^{\delta_{1}}\bigr]\,\mathbf{M}_{A}\bigl[(\lambda-r_{2})^{\delta_{2}}\bigr]\dotsb\mathbf{M}_{A}\bigl[(\lambda-r_{k})^{\delta_{k}}\bigr]\\
\,=\,\mathbf{M}_{A}\bigl[(\lambda-r_{1})^{\delta_{1}}(\lambda-r_{2})^{\delta_{2}}\dotsb(\lambda-r_{k})^{\delta_{k}}\bigr]\,=\,\mathbf{M}_{A}\bigl[\chi(\lambda)\bigr]\;/\;\beta\,.

So the invariant polynomial
m_{j}(\lambda)
for
\mathbf{M}_{A}(P)
,
which is the same over
\mathbb{F}
or over
\overline{\mathbb{F}}

(since Smith forms are invariant under field extensions),
can be expressed as

m_{j}(\lambda)\,=\,\Bigl[\mkern 1.0mu\mathbf{M}_{A}\bigl[\chi(\lambda)\bigr]\mkern 1.0mu\Bigr]^{\alpha_{j}}\widetilde{m}_{j}(\lambda)\,,

where
\mathbf{M}_{A}\bigl[\chi(\lambda)\bigr]
and
\widetilde{m}_{j}(\lambda)

are coprime in
\overline{\mathbb{F}}[\lambda]
.
Since
\mathbf{M}_{A}\bigl[\chi(\lambda)\bigr]
, and hence also
\widetilde{m}_{j}(\lambda)
,
is a polynomial over
\mathbb{F}
,
by Lemma
4.5
they are also coprime in
\mathbb{F}[\lambda]
.
Also we know from Lemma
4.4

and the discussion surrounding it that
\mathbf{M}_{A}\bigl[\chi(\lambda)\bigr]

is an
\mathbb{F}
-irreducible polynomial of the same degree as
\chi(\lambda)
.
Thus it is well-defined to speak of the partial multiplicities
of
\mathbf{M}_{A}(P)
at
\mathbf{M}_{A}(\chi)
,
and we see that over
\mathbb{F}
we have

\bigl[\mkern 1.0mu\mkern 1.0mu{\cal P}\!{\cal M}\bigl(\mathbf{M}_{A}(P),\mathbf{M}_{A}(\chi)\bigr)\mkern 1.0mu\bigr]_{j}\,=\,\alpha_{j}\,=\,\bigl[\mkern 1.0mu\mkern 1.0mu{\cal P}\!{\cal M}(P,\chi)\mkern 1.0mu\bigr]_{j}

for each
1\leq j\leq\mathop{\rm rank}P
, i.e., for each individual partial multiplicity.
Hence we also have the equality

{\cal P}\!{\cal M}\bigl(\mathbf{M}_{A}(P),\mathbf{M}_{A}(\chi)\bigr)\,=\,{\cal P}\!{\cal M}(P,\chi)

for the whole partial multiplicity sequence,
as desired.

∎

4.2
Quasi-triangular realization with infinite spectral structure

With the new tools for working with Möbius transformations
developed in Section
4.1
,
we are now able to show how to construct a quasi-triangular realization
for spectral data that may now include nontrivial structure at
\infty
.
An example to illustrate this construction
follows immediately after the theorem.

Theorem 4.7

(Quasi-Triangular Realization with Eigenvalue at
\infty
)
.

Let
\mathbb{F}
be an arbitrary field,
and suppose a list of
m
invariant polynomials
s_{1}(\lambda),\dots s_{m}(\lambda)
over
\,\mathbb{F}

forming a divisibility chain
s_{1}(\lambda)|s_{2}(\lambda)|\dotsb|s_{m}(\lambda)
is given,
together with a nonempty list of
\ell
nonzero partial multiplicities at infinity

\,\alpha_{1}\leq\alpha_{2}\leq\dotsb\leq\alpha_{\ell}
.
Let

\sigma\,:=\;\sum_{i=1}^{m}\deg\bigl(s_{i}(\lambda)\bigr)\,+\,\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\alpha_{j}

be the index sum for this data,
and define
k
to be the maximum degree
among all of the
\,\mathbb{F}
-irreducible divisors of
s_{m}(\lambda)
.
Suppose also that the field
\,\mathbb{F}
contains some scalar
\,\omega\in\mathbb{F}

such that
s_{m}(\omega)\neq 0
. Then for any choice of nonzero
g,n\in\mathbb{N}

such that
n\geq\mathop{\rm max}\{m,\ell\}
and
gn=\sigma
,
there exists an
n\times n
, grade
g
matrix polynomial
Q(\lambda)
over
\,\mathbb{F}

that is
k
-quasi-triangular,
has exactly the given invariant polynomials

s_{1}(\lambda),\dots,s_{m}(\lambda)

with all other
n-m
invariant polynomials equal to
1
,
and partial multiplicity sequence at infinity

(0,\dotsc,0,\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{\ell})
.
In addition,
\deg Q(\lambda)=g
if and only if
n>\ell
.

Proof.

Begin by taking the given spectral data and converting it
into the “first form” described in Definition
2.4
,
i.e., into a list of
\mathbb{F}
-irreducible divisors
\chi_{j}
(for
j=1,t
),
each equipped with a partial multiplicity sequence
{\cal P}\!{\cal M}_{\text{given}}(\chi_{j})
of length
n
.
Recall that the partial multiplicities at
\infty

are recorded as
{\cal P}\!{\cal M}_{\text{given}}(\beta)
for some (any) nonzero
\beta\in\mathbb{F}
.
Of course, doing this conversion
may require adjoining some additional initial trivial invariant polynomials,
and also perhaps some additional initial zero partial multiplicities at
\infty
,
as needed to fill out the length
n
.

Next we design some Möbius transformations
\mathbf{M}_{A}
that will interchange
the spectral roles of
\infty
and the “special” scalar
\omega\in\mathbb{F}

that has been assumed to exist.
(The “special” property of
\omega
is that
{\cal P}\!{\cal M}(\lambda-\omega)=(0,0,\dotsc,0)
.)
We claim that the
\mathbf{M}_{A}
defined by the nonsingular matrix

A\,=\,\begin{cases}\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}\omega&0\\
1&-\omega\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]&\text{\, if \;$\omega\neq 0$}\,,\\[3.0pt]
\;\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}0&\;1\\
1&\;0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]&\text{\, if \;$\omega=0$}\,,\end{cases}

achieves this goal.
As a mapping on grade one scalar polynomials, this
\mathbf{M}_{A}
is a bijection,
and it is straightforward to check that

\mathbf{M}_{A}(\lambda-\omega)\,=\,\begin{cases}\omega^{2}&\text{\, if \;$\omega\neq 0$}\\
1&\text{\, if \;$\omega=0$}\end{cases}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\mathbf{M}_{A}(\beta)\,=\,\begin{cases}\beta(\lambda-\omega)&\text{\, if \;$\omega\neq 0$}\\
\beta\lambda=\beta(\lambda-\omega)&\text{\, if \;$\omega=0$}\end{cases}\quad,

for any nonzero
\beta\in\mathbb{F}
.
Thus we see that for any
\omega
, the transformation
\mathbf{M}_{A}
effects an interchange of the roles
of
\omega
and
\infty
as eigenvalues,
while all other finite scalars
\mu\in\mathbb{F}

(represented by degree one polynomials
\lambda-\mu
)
are simply permuted in some fashion,
the details of which are irrelevant here.

Using this
\mathbf{M}_{A}
,
we now define a new collection of spectral data that has no eigenvalue at
\infty
.
For each irreducible divisor
\chi_{j}
in the original data,
we replace it by
\mathbf{M}_{A}(\chi_{j})
,
but assign to it the same partial multiplicity sequence
that
\chi_{j}
has in the given spectral data.
(Here each
\mathbf{M}_{A}(\chi_{j})
is taken with grade equal to degree.)
In other words, we declare that

{\cal P}\!{\cal M}\bigl(\mathbf{M}_{A}(\chi_{j})\bigr)\,:=\;{\cal P}\!{\cal M}_{\text{given}}(\chi_{j})\,.

By Lemma
4.4
we know that each
\mathbf{M}_{A}(\chi_{j})

is
\mathbb{F}
-irreducible with
\deg\mathbf{M}_{A}(\chi_{j})=\deg\chi_{j}
.

Similarly, for the eigenvalue at
\infty
in the original given data,
we replace it by
\mathbf{M}_{A}(0\lambda+1)=\lambda-\omega
,
and assign to it the partial multiplicity sequence that
\infty

has in the given spectral data.
(Here
0\lambda+1
is viewed as a grade one polynomial.)
In other words, we declare that

{\cal P}\!{\cal M}(\lambda-\omega)\;=\;{\cal P}\!{\cal M}\bigl(\mathbf{M}_{A}(0\lambda+1)\bigr)\,:=\;{\cal P}\!{\cal M}_{\text{given}}(0\lambda+1)\,.

Thus we have specified a new collection
{\cal C}
of purely finite spectral data,
that is, a list of
\mathbb{F}
-irreducible divisors

\bigl\{\mathbf{M}_{A}(\chi_{1}),\mathbf{M}_{A}(\chi_{2}),\dotsc,\mathbf{M}_{A}(\chi_{t}),\mathbf{M}_{A}(0\lambda+1)=(\lambda-\omega)\bigr\}
,
together with assigned partial multiplicity sequences for each.
Since the irreducible divisors in
{\cal C}
have the same degrees
as their partners from the original spectral data
(the same grade in the case of partners
0\lambda+1
and
\lambda-\omega
),
as well as the same partial multiplicities,
then the index sum
\sigma
is the same for
{\cal C}

as it was for the original data.
Thus we may use the same values of
n
,
g
, and
k
for
{\cal C}

as was used for the original data.

Now by Theorem
3.1
there exists an
n\times n
,
degree
g
matrix polynomial
P(\lambda)
over
\mathbb{F}

that is
k
-quasi-triangular,
and has exactly the spectral data in
{\cal C}
.
In other words,

\displaystyle{\cal P}\!{\cal M}\bigl(P(\lambda),\mathbf{M}_{A}(\chi_{j})\bigr)

\displaystyle\,=\;{\cal P}\!{\cal M}_{\text{given}}(\chi_{j})\,,

(4.12)

\displaystyle\text{and}\qquad{\cal P}\!{\cal M}\bigl(P(\lambda),\mathbf{M}_{A}(0\lambda+1)\bigr)

\displaystyle\,=\;{\cal P}\!{\cal M}_{\text{given}}(0\lambda+1)\,.

(4.13)

Applying Theorem
4.6

using the Möbius transformation
\mathbf{M}_{A^{-1}}
(with respect to grade
g
)
to (
4.12
) and (
4.13
),
and defining
Q(\lambda):=\mathbf{M}_{A^{-1}}(P)
, we have

\displaystyle\hskip 34.1433pt{\cal P}\!{\cal M}\bigl(Q(\lambda),\chi_{j}\bigr)\,=\,{\cal P}\!{\cal M}\bigl(\mathbf{M}_{A^{-1}}(P),\chi_{j}\bigr)

\displaystyle\,=\,{\cal P}\!{\cal M}\Bigl(\mathbf{M}_{A^{-1}}\bigl(P\bigr),\mathbf{M}_{A^{-1}}\bigl(\mathbf{M}_{A}(\chi_{j})\bigr)\Bigr)

\displaystyle\,=\,{\cal P}\!{\cal M}\bigl(P(\lambda),\mathbf{M}_{A}(\chi_{j})\bigr)\,=\;{\cal P}\!{\cal M}_{\text{given}}(\chi_{j})\,,\hskip 42.67912pt

and

{\cal P}\!{\cal M}\bigl(Q(\lambda),(0\lambda+1)\bigr)\,=\,{\cal P}\!{\cal M}\bigl(\mathbf{M}_{A^{-1}}(P),(0\lambda+1)\bigr)\,=\,{\cal P}\!{\cal M}\Bigl(\mathbf{M}_{A^{-1}}\bigl(P\bigr),\mathbf{M}_{A^{-1}}\bigl(\mathbf{M}_{A}(0\lambda+1)\bigr)\Bigr)\\
\,=\,{\cal P}\!{\cal M}\bigl(P(\lambda),\mathbf{M}_{A}(0\lambda+1)\bigr)\,=\;{\cal P}\!{\cal M}_{\text{given}}(0\lambda+1)\,,\hskip 56.9055pt

Thus the matrix polynomial
Q(\lambda)

is the desired quasi-triangular realization of the given spectral data.
The relation between the degree and the grade of
Q(\lambda)
follows
immediately from Lemma
2.7
.
∎

4.3
Quasi-triangularization of regular matrix polynomials

In the following signature result of this paper,
the goal is to quasi-triangularize a given polynomial matrix,
rather than to construct a quasi-triangular realization of some given data.
That is, we will start with an arbitrary regular polynomial matrix
P(\lambda)
,
and show that there must always be a quasi-triangular matrix
Q(\lambda)

with the same degree, grade, and complete spectral data as
P(\lambda)
.

Theorem 4.9

(Quasi-Triangularization)
.

Suppose
P(\lambda)
is a regular
n\times n
matrix polynomial
of grade
g
and degree
d
,
over an arbitrary field
\,\mathbb{F}
.
Define
k
to be the maximum degree among all
of the
\mathbb{F}
-irreducible divisors of
P(\lambda)
.
If
P(\lambda)
is
not
strictly regular,
further suppose that there is some constant
\omega\in\mathbb{F}
such that
s_{n}(\omega)\neq 0
,
where
s_{n}(\lambda)
is the
n^{\text{th}}
invariant polynomial of
P(\lambda)
.

(
I.e., there is some
\omega\in\mathbb{F}
that is
not
in the spectrum of
P
.
)

Then there exists a regular
k
-quasi-triangular matrix polynomial
Q(\lambda)
over
\,\mathbb{F}

that has exactly the same size, grade, degree, and complete spectral data as
P(\lambda)
.
When
P(\lambda)
is strictly regular,
then the
k
-quasi-triangularization
Q(\lambda)
is strictly regular,
and may be chosen to have the additional property
that all off-diagonal blocks have degree strictly less than
d
.

Proof.

From
P(\lambda)
, extract the complete spectral data, size, degree, and grade.
If
P(\lambda)
is strictly regular, then use this data
together with Theorem
3.1
to construct the desired
Q(\lambda)

with
\mathop{\rm grade}Q=\deg Q=\deg P=\mathop{\rm grade}P
.

If
P(\lambda)
is not strictly regular,
then define a new matrix polynomial
\widetilde{P}(\lambda)

that is entrywise identical to
P(\lambda)
,
but with
\mathop{\rm grade}\widetilde{P}
chosen to be equal to
d=\deg P
.
(If
g=d
to begin with, then
\widetilde{P}
is identical to
P
in every way.)
Note that the degrees of
P
and
\widetilde{P}

as well as their finite spectral structures
are the same,
even though their grades may be different.
Now if
{\cal P}\!{\cal M}(P,\,0\lambda+1)=(\alpha_{1},\dotsc,\alpha_{n})

is the partial multiplicity sequence of
P
at
\infty
,
then by Lemma
2.7

the partial multiplicity sequence of
\widetilde{P}
at
\infty

is shifted by
\alpha_{1}=g-d
from that of
P
,
i.e.,

{\cal P}\!{\cal M}(\widetilde{P},\,0\lambda+1)\,=\;{\cal P}\!{\cal M}(P,\,0\lambda+1)-\alpha_{1}\cdot(1,1,\dotsc,1)\,=\,(0,\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{1},\dotsc,\alpha_{n}-\alpha_{1})\,.

Now we can use Theorem
4.7
to construct
a
k
-quasi-triangular realization
\widetilde{Q}(\lambda)

for the complete spectral data, size, degree, and grade of this
\widetilde{P}(\lambda)
,
for which we have

\deg\widetilde{Q}=\mathop{\rm grade}\widetilde{Q}=\mathop{\rm grade}\widetilde{P}=\deg\widetilde{P}=\deg P=d
.
Finally, we define a
k
-quasi-triangular matrix polynomial
Q(\lambda)

that is entrywise identical to
\widetilde{Q}(\lambda)
,
and so has
\deg Q=\deg\widetilde{Q}=d
,
but now has grade chosen to be
g
.
The infinite spectral data of
Q
is shifted by
\alpha_{1}=g-d

from that of
\widetilde{Q}
, so we have

\displaystyle{\cal P}\!{\cal M}(Q,\,0\lambda+1)

\displaystyle=\,{\cal P}\!{\cal M}(\widetilde{Q},\,0\lambda+1)+\alpha_{1}\cdot(1,1,\dotsc,1)

\displaystyle=\,{\cal P}\!{\cal M}(\widetilde{P},\,0\lambda+1)+\alpha_{1}\cdot(1,1,\dotsc,1)\,=\,(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dotsc,\alpha_{n})\,=\,{\cal P}\!{\cal M}(P,\,0\lambda+1)\,.

Thus we see that
Q(\lambda)

has exactly the same size, degree, grade, and complete spectral data as
P(\lambda)
,
and so is the desired quasi-triangularization of
P(\lambda)
.
∎

Remark 4.10
.

It is worth emphasizing that the relationship between
P(\lambda)

and the quasi-triangularization
Q(\lambda)

is stronger than just unimodular equivalence.
Theorem
4.9
guarantees the existence
of a
spectrally equivalent

k
-quasi-triangularization
for any regular matrix polynomial over an arbitrary field.

5
More on diagonal block sizes

In this final section we explore the range of possibilities for diagonal block sizes
in degree-preserving quasi-triangularizations.
We have shown that every regular matrix polynomial
P(\lambda)
over an arbitrary field
admits a spectrally equivalent degree-preserving
k
-quasi-triangularization,
where
k
is the highest degree
among the irreducible divisors of
P(\lambda)
.
But is that really the best possible general result?
Could it be that there is a smaller bound on diagonal block sizes
of quasi-triangularizations that holds for all regular matrix polynomials?
Section
5.1
addresses this issue,
exhibiting a family of examples that shows
that the
k
in Theorem
4.9

is indeed the best possible general bound for diagonal block sizes.

By contrast, in Section
5.2

we probe the opposite end of the size range of diagonal block sizes,
trying to determine when it is possible to achieve diagonal blocks
that are all
1\times 1
,
i.e., when it is possible to just plain
triangularize

in a spectrally equivalent and degree-preserving way.
Although we have not even come close to completely settling this question,
we are at least able to identify some scenarios
where a necessary and sufficient condition for triangularizability can be found,
and some other more general scenarios where a condition
sufficient to guarantee the existence
of a triangularization can be given.

5.1
Sharpness of the upper bound
k

The matrix polynomials described in Example
5.1

show that Theorem
4.9
provides
the best possible general bound on diagonal block sizes.
This is done via an infinite family of examples where it can be proved
that every possible quasi-triangularization
has all of its diagonal blocks of size
k\times k
or larger,
where
k
is the largest degree among all of the irreducible divisors.

Example 5.1
.

Consider any strictly regular
n\times n
matrix polynomial
P(\lambda)
with degree
d\geq 2
,
such that
P(\lambda)
has exactly one irreducible divisor
\chi(\lambda)
,
and
k=\deg(\chi)\geq 2

is coprime to
d
.
Since
mk=dn
for some

by the index sum theorem,
we must then also have
k|n
.

Suppose
\mathbb{F}
is any field that supports
such an
\mathbb{F}
-irreducible polynomial

with

;

has such a

for any

at all.
Then there are infinitely many choices of

and
n

that satisfy the conditions mentioned above,
i.e., that
k
is coprime to
d
and

.
For any of these choices of

, and the field
\mathbb{F}
,
the Fundamental Realization Theorem
2.11

guarantees the existence of a matrix polynomial

over

as described above,
i.e., one that is strictly regular,

,
and with just one irreducible divisor

.
Thus there are infinitely many matrix polynomials
encompassed by the discussion in this Example.

Now suppose that

is
any

;
that is,

is block-upper-triangular, has degree
d
,
and is unimodularly equivalent to

.
Suppose

has
\ell

with sizes

, respectively,
so that

.
Now each block

has degree at most

,
so that

.
Thus we have

dn\,=\,\deg\bigl(\mathop{\rm det}P\bigr)\,=\,\deg\bigl(\mathop{\rm det}Q\bigr)\,=\,\deg\Bigl(\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}\mathop{\rm det}Q_{ii}\Bigr)\,=\,\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\deg\bigl(\mathop{\rm det}Q_{ii}\bigr)\,\leq\,\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}dn_{i}\,=\,dn\,.

But this means that each inequality
\deg\bigl(\mathop{\rm det}Q_{ii}\bigr)\leq dn_{i}

must actually be an equality,
so
\deg\bigl(\mathop{\rm det}Q_{ii}\bigr)=dn_{i}\geq 2
,
and hence
Q_{ii}(\lambda)
is not a constant block.
Since
\mathop{\rm det}Q=\alpha\chi^{m}
for some nonzero scalar
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}
,
that means that
\chi
must divide each
\mathop{\rm det}Q_{ii}(\lambda)
.
Thus
k
divides each
dn_{ii}
,
and hence also divides each
n_{ii}

since
k
and
d
are coprime.
But this means that
k\leq n_{ii}
for each
1\leq i\leq\ell
,
and so
Q(\lambda)
is at best
k
-quasi-triangular.

Finally, note that the discussion in Remark
3.17

is relevant to this example.
In that remark, it was pointed out that there is only one scenario
in which the homogeneous partitioning procedure
forces there to be sublists with exactly
k
elements,
which then later lead to
k\times k
diagonal blocks
in the quasi-triangularization.
It is not hard to show that this example
falls exactly under this scenario,
so our procedure will necessarily produce a quasi-triangularization
with
all
of its diagonal blocks being
k\times k
.
This, of course, is not equivalent to
proving

that no quasi-triangularization with any diagonal block of size
smaller than
k\times k
can exist, as we have done above.
But it certainly is completely consistent with that result.

When
d
and
k
are coprime,
then Example
5.1
has shown that the “best”
quasi-triangularization that can be attained may sometimes
be forced to have all of its diagonal blocks with size
k\times k
.
However, there are many matrix polynomials that have quasi-triangularizations
with much smaller diagonal blocks than the general upper bound of
k\times k
.
Indeed we have seen this already in Example
3.23
,
where we had
k=4
,
but were able to construct a
2
-quasi-triangularization.
The next example gives a whole family of matrix polynomials
that show that the gap between this general upper bound
k

and the actual smallest realizable diagonal block size for quasi-triangularizations
can be arbitrarily large.
The discussion in Section
5.2

provides further examples of this phenomenon.

Example 5.2
.

Consider an arbitrary target degree
d
and irreducible polynomial

\chi(\lambda)
with
k=\deg(\chi)=2d
.
Note that there are many fields
\mathbb{F}

that support the presence of such
high degree
\mathbb{F}
-irreducible polynomials, e.g.,
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{Q}
.
For any such
\mathbb{F}
-irreducible
\chi(\lambda)
of degree
k
,
there is a unique way to express it
in the form
\chi(\lambda)=\lambda^{d}p(\lambda)+q(\lambda)
,
where
\deg p=d
and
\deg q=d-1
.
Then letting

X(\lambda)\;=\;\left[\begin{array}[]{cr}p(\lambda)&-1\\
q(\lambda)&\lambda^{d}\end{array}\right]\,,

we see that
\deg X=d
,
\mathop{\rm det}X=\chi(\lambda)
,
and the Smith form of
X(\lambda)
is just
\mathop{\rm diag}\bigl(1,\chi(\lambda)\bigr)
.
This implies that the Smith form of
X^{6}(\lambda)

must be of the form
\mathop{\rm diag}(\chi^{\ell},\chi^{m})
,
where
\ell+m=6
.

Now let
P(\lambda)
be any strictly regular

12\times 12
matrix polynomial over
\mathbb{F}
with degree
d
,
that has the Smith form
\mathop{\rm diag}(I_{10},\chi^{\ell},\chi^{m})
.
By the Fundamental Realization Theorem
2.11

such matrix polynomials must exist.
Consider next the matrix polynomial

Q(\lambda)\;=\;\left[\begin{array}[]{cccccc}X(\lambda)&I_{2}&&&&\\
&X(\lambda)&I_{2}&&&\\
&&X(\lambda)&I_{2}&&\\
&&&X(\lambda)&I_{2}&\\
&&&&X(\lambda)&I_{2}\\
&&&&&X(\lambda)\end{array}\right]\,\in\;\mathbb{F}[\lambda]^{12\times 12}\,.

This
Q(\lambda)
has degree
d
, and it is not hard to show
that it is unimodularly equivalent to
\mathop{\rm diag}\bigl(I_{10},X^{6}(\lambda)\bigr)
,
and hence also to
P(\lambda)
.
Thus
Q(\lambda)
is a degree-preserving 2-quasi-triangularization of
P(\lambda)
.
This
Q(\lambda)
has very much smaller diagonal blocks
than what is guaranteed by the general result in Theorem
4.9
,
with a gap (of
k-2
) in the size of diagonal blocks
between the general upper bound and those actually occurring in
Q(\lambda)
,
a gap that can be arbitrarily large.

5.2
When triangularizing is possible

A natural question to ask is “When is it possible to triangularize?”,
or in other words, when can we guarantee the existence
of a degree-preserving quasi-triangularization in which all diagonal blocks are 1
\times
1?
To answer this, let’s begin with an example
where all of the irreducible divisors have degree 2 or less,
as in a real matrix polynomial.

Example 5.3
.

Suppose that we are trying to build a
triangularization

of a strictly regular matrix polynomial with degree
d=7
and size
n=8
,
and there are a total of
m_{1}=18
degree-1 irreducible factors
and
m_{2}=19
degree-2 irreducible factors in the Smith form,
for a total degree sum of
56
.
One strategy to build an appropriate target diagonal
is to first spread the degree-2 factors out as much as possible,
and then try to fill in the resulting gaps with the degree-1 factors.
0
1
2
3
4
5
6
7

Let
\mathbf{f}_{1}
and
\mathbf{f}_{2}
denote
the degree-1 and degree-2 factor-counting vectors
of the Smith form, respectively.
If
\mathbf{v}_{2}
is the homogenization of
\mathbf{f}_{2}
,
then we know that it is possible to spread out
the
19
degree-2 factors along the diagonal
to realize this
\mathbf{v}_{2}
via unimodular transformations,
using Lemma
3.10

and Corollary
3.4
.
This homogenization is visualized in the diagram above,
where each column displays the contents of a diagonal entry location,
each box stands for an irreducible factor,
and the height of each box displays the degree of that factor,
in this case a height/degree of
2
.
To achieve a triangularization,
we need to have each diagonal entry have degree
7
,
so our goal is to populate each column in the diagram
with boxes up to exactly height
7
.
The amount of remaining space between the top of the current stack of cyan blocks
and the red dashed line in each column will be called a degree gap,
and the vector containing all of the degree gaps will be called the gap vector
\mathbf{g}
.
In this example the gap vector is
\mathbf{g}=(3,3,3,3,3,1,1,1)
.
So what remains is to try to distribute the
18
degree-1 irreducible factors
(height-1 boxes) so as to exactly fill these gaps.
In other words, we need to try to convert
\mathbf{f}_{1}

into the gap vector
\mathbf{g}
.
Now our only means to move these degree-1 factors around
is to use the tools from Corollary
3.4
,
which correspond to compressions and interchanges
of the entries of the factor-counting vector.
But such actions can only convert
\mathbf{f}_{1}
into a vector that it majorizes.
(Recall the classical Muirhead theorem discussed
right after Remark
3.9
.)
Thus this strategy will succeed in producing
an appropriate diagonal for an achievable triangularization
whenever
\mathbf{f}_{1}\succeq\mathbf{g}
.
Consequently we see that this majorization condition is a sufficient condition
to guarantee the triangularizability of a strictly regular matrix polynomial
whenever all irreducible divisors are of degree at most two.

In fact, though, the condition
\mathbf{f}_{1}\succeq\mathbf{g}

is also a necessary condition for triangularizability,
and so gives a characterization in this scenario,
as will be seen as an immediate consequence of the following development.
We begin with some background lemmas.
The first of these lemmas uses an alternative definition
for majorization of vectors, that is equivalent
to the one given earlier in Definition
3.8
.

Definition 5.4

(Majorization
[
14
]
)
.

For vectors
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
(or in
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
),
let
\mathbf{x}^{\prime\prime}
and
\mathbf{y}^{\prime\prime}

denote the permutations of those vectors
in which the entries have been arranged in
increasing

We say that
\mathbf{x}

\mathbf{y}
,
or
\mathbf{y}
is majorized by
\mathbf{x}
,
and write
\,\mathbf{x}\succeq\mathbf{y}\,
, if

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}x_{i}^{\prime\prime}\,\leq\,\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}y_{i}^{\prime\prime}\quad\text{ for }\quad\ell=1,2,\dotsc,n\;,

(5.1)

with equality when
\ell=n
.

Lemma 5.5
.

Suppose
T(\lambda)
is a regular triangular matrix polynomial
over an arbitrary field
\,\mathbb{F}
,
and has the Smith form
S(\lambda)
.
Let
\mathcal{F}\sqcup\mathcal{G}
be
any
coprime partition
of the multiset of all of the
\mathbb{F}
-irreducible factors
of the invariant polynomials in
S(\lambda)
,
equivalently of all of the
\mathbb{F}
-irreducible factors
of the diagonal entries of
T(\lambda)
.
Then the majorization relations

\mathbf{d}_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}}}(S)\,\succeq\,\mathbf{d}_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}}}(T)

(5.2)

hold for the diagonal factor-counting vectors of
S(\lambda)
and

,
with respect to
every

{\cal F}
from
every
such coprime partition.

Proof.

For convenience, let us introduce some notation to ease the discussion.
Let

\bm{\sigma}\;=\;(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\dotsc,\sigma_{n})\,:=\;\mathbf{d}_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}}}(S)

be an abbreviation for the diagonal factor-counting vector
\mathbf{d}_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}}}(S)
.
Note that
\sigma_{1}\leq\sigma_{2}\leq\dotsb\leq\sigma_{n}

because of the divisibility chain property of invariant polynomials,
but the entries of
\mathbf{d}_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}}}(T)
may not be in any such order.
So let
\bm{\tau}=(\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\dotsc,\tau_{n})

be the permutation of
\mathbf{d}_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}}}(T)

such that
\tau_{1}\leq\tau_{2}\leq\dotsb\leq\tau_{n}
.
Now from (
2.1
)
we know that
s_{11}(\lambda)|\,t_{jj}(\lambda)
for

,
so
|s_{11}(\lambda)|_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}}}\leq|t_{jj}(\lambda)|_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}}}
for

,
and thus
\sigma_{1}\leq\tau_{1}=\mathop{\rm min}_{j}|t_{jj}(\lambda)|_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}}}
.

Using (

) again,
we have that
\bigl[s_{11}(\lambda)s_{22}(\lambda)\bigr]|\,\bigl[t_{ii}(\lambda)t_{jj}(\lambda)\bigr]

for

with

.
Hence

|s_{11}(\lambda)s_{22}(\lambda)|_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}}}\;\leq\;|t_{ii}(\lambda)t_{jj}(\lambda)|_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}}}

for
i,j=1,\dotsc,n
with
i\neq j
,
and consequently
\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{2}\leq\tau_{1}+\tau_{2}
.
In a similar manner, we see from (

) that
for each
1\leq\ell\leq n-1
we have

|s_{11}(\lambda)s_{22}(\lambda)\dotsb s_{\ell\ell}(\lambda)|_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}}}\;\leq\;|t_{{i_{1}}{i_{1}}}(\lambda)t_{{i_{2}}{i_{2}}}(\lambda)\dotsb t_{{i_{\ell}}{i_{\ell}}}(\lambda)|_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}}}

for all

.
Thus we have

\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{2}+\dotsb+\sigma_{\ell}\;\leq\;\tau_{1}+\tau_{2}+\dotsb+\tau_{\ell}

for each
1\leq\ell\leq n-1
.
Finally, since
\mathop{\rm det}S=c\cdot\mathop{\rm det}T
for some nonzero scalar

,
we have

|s_{11}(\lambda)s_{22}(\lambda)\dotsb s_{nn}(\lambda)|_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}}}\;=\;|t_{11}(\lambda)t_{22}(\lambda)\dotsb t_{nn}(\lambda)|_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}}}\,,

\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{2}+\dotsb+\sigma_{n}\;=\;\tau_{1}+\tau_{2}+\dotsb+\tau_{n}\,.

The next result shows that if the irreducible divisors of a matrix polynomial
have
only two different degrees
,
and one of those is degree
1
,
then having a triangularization of any kind
implies that there must also exist a triangularization
in which the highest degree irreducible factors are indeed
”spread out as much as possible” as in Example
5.3
,
i.e., where their factor-counting vector is
1
-homogeneous.

Lemma 5.6
.

Suppose a strictly regular matrix polynomial
P(\lambda)
of degree
d

has a triangularization
Q_{0}(\lambda)
of degree
d
.
Further suppose that the multiset
{\cal M}
of all of the
\,\mathbb{F}
-irreducible factors
in the Smith form for
P(\lambda)
contains
only two
distinct degrees,

1
and
k
, for some
k\geq 2
.
Let
{\cal M}={\cal F}_{1}\sqcup{\cal F}_{k}
be the coprime partition
in which
{\cal F}_{j}
contains all of the irreducible factors in
{\cal M}
of degree
j
for
j=1,k
.
Then
P(\lambda)
has a triangularization
T(\lambda)
of degree
d

in which the diagonal factor-counting vector
\mathbf{d}_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}_{k}}}(T)

is
1
-homogeneous.

Proof.

If
\mathbf{d}_{{{\cal F}}_{k}}(Q_{0})
is already
1
-homogeneous,

and then of course we are done.
So suppose that
\bm{\kappa}=(\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2},\dotsc,\kappa_{n}):=\mathbf{d}_{{{\cal F}}_{k}}(Q_{0})

is not
1
-homogeneous.
The argument will consist of a procedure showing how to convert
Q_{0}(\lambda)

by a finite sequence of triangularizations for
P(\lambda)

into a degree-
d
triangularization
T(\lambda)

that has the desired
1
-homogeneity property.

Let
\kappa_{i}
and
\kappa_{j}
be the minimum and maximum entries in
\bm{\kappa}
,
so
\kappa_{j}-\kappa_{i}\geq 2
.
Now by a finite sequence of interchanges we can arrange
that the corresponding diagonal entries of
Q_{0}(\lambda)
are adjacent,
say in the
(i,i)
and
(i+1,i+1)
locations,
and these interchanges can be implemented by unimodular transformations
using Corollary
3.4
.
This gives us a new degree
d
triangularization
\widetilde{Q}_{0}(\lambda)
for
P(\lambda)
,
with a new
\bm{\widetilde{\kappa}}=\mathbf{d}_{{{\cal F}}_{k}}(\widetilde{Q}_{0})

with
\widetilde{\kappa}_{i}=\kappa_{i}
and
\widetilde{\kappa}_{i+1}=\kappa_{j}
.
Now we do a compression of the degree
k
factors in these two adjacent entries,
again via a unimodular transformation from Corollary
3.4
,
decreasing the maximum
\widetilde{\kappa}_{i+1}
by one
and increasing the minimum
\widetilde{\kappa}_{i}
by one.
This gives us a triangular matrix polynomial
\widehat{Q}_{0}(\lambda)

that is no longer degree
d
, although it is still unimodularly equivalent to
P(\lambda)
;
all diagonal entries have degree
d
, except for the
i^{th}
and
(i+1)^{th}
,
which now have degrees
d+k
and
d-k
, respectively.
We can now restore degree
d
on these adjacent diagonal entries,
by doing a compression of the degree
1
factors,
again using a unimodular transformation from Corollary
3.4
.
The
i^{th}
diagonal entry of
\widehat{Q}_{0}(\lambda)
has
d-k\kappa_{i}
degree-
1
factors,
while the
(i+1)^{th}
diagonal entry has
d-k\kappa_{j}
degree-
1
factors.
Since
(d-k\kappa_{i})>(d-k\kappa_{j})
,
with a degree difference of at least

,
we can do a compression of the degree-
1
factors
where the
i^{th}
diagonal entry loses
k

1
factors,
and the
(i+1)^{th}
diagonal entry gains
k
degree-
1
factors.
This gives a triangular matrix polynomial
\breve{Q}_{0}(\lambda)

that is unimodularly equivalent to
P(\lambda)
,
and has all diagonal entries with degree
d
again,
but the off-diagonal entries may now have degree larger than
d
.
This is remedied by using Lemma
3.22
,
with all diagonal blocks taken to be of size
1\times 1
.
This finally gives us a degree-
d
triangularization
Q_{1}(\lambda)
of
P(\lambda)
,
with
\mathbf{d}_{{{\cal F}}_{k}}(Q_{1})

1
-homogeneous
than
\mathbf{d}_{{{\cal F}}_{k}}(Q_{0})

Of course if
\mathbf{d}_{{{\cal F}}_{k}}(Q_{1})
is now

-homogeneous,

If not, we repeat the above procedure on

to produce a new degree-

with diagonal factor-counting vector
\mathbf{d}_{{{\cal F}}_{k}}(Q_{2})

1
-homogeneous.
Continuing this, we generate a sequence

,
which in finitely many steps must eventually produce

for which
\mathbf{d}_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}_{k}}}(T)

-homogeneous.

Proposition 5.7
.

Let
P(\lambda)
be a strictly regular
n\times n
polynomial matrix
of degree
d
over a field
\,\mathbb{F}
.
Let
S(\lambda)
be the Smith form of
P(\lambda)
,
and assume that all irreducible divisors of
P(\lambda)

are degree
1
or degree
k
, where
k\geq 2
.
Let
{\cal F}_{1}\sqcup{\cal F}_{k}
be the same coprime partition
of the multiset of all
\,\mathbb{F}
-irreducible factors in
S(\lambda)

as in Lemma
5.6
.
Organize the degree-
k

n

\mathbf{q}(\lambda):=\bigl(q_{1}(\lambda),q_{2}(\lambda),\dotsc,q_{n}(\lambda)\bigr)

in the same way as in the proof of Lemma
3.11

(
and as in the diagram for Example
5.3

)
,
i.e., so that
|\mathbf{q}(\lambda)|_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}_{k}}}
is
1
-homogeneous.
Define the degree gaps
g_{i}:=d-\deg(q_{i})
,
and the corresponding gap vector
\mathbf{g}:=(g_{1},g_{2},\dotsc,g_{n})
.

(

may be negative.

Then

has a triangularization of degree

if and only if the majorization condition

\mathbf{d}_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}_{1}}}(S)\,\succeq\,\mathbf{g}
holds.

Proof.

(\Rightarrow)
Suppose
P(\lambda)
has a triangularization of degree
d
.
Then by Lemma
5.6
,

P(\lambda)
has a triangularization
T(\lambda)
of degree
d

in which the diagonal factor-counting vector
\mathbf{d}_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}_{k}}}(T)

is
1
-homogeneous.
Applying Lemma
5.5
to the coprime partition
{\cal F}_{1}\sqcup{\cal F}_{k}
,
we then have that
\mathbf{d}_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}_{1}}}(S)\,\succeq\,\mathbf{d}_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}_{1}}}(T)
.
But in this triangularization
T(\lambda)
,
it is easy to see that
the diagonal factor-counting vector
\mathbf{d}_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}_{1}}}(T)

is exactly the same as the gap vector
\mathbf{g}
.
Thus
\mathbf{d}_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}_{1}}}(S)\,\succeq\,\mathbf{g}
, as desired.

(\Leftarrow)
Now conversely, suppose that the majorization condition

\mathbf{d}_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}_{1}}}(S)\,\succeq\,\mathbf{g}

Starting from
S(\lambda)
,
we know from Corollary
3.4

and Lemma
3.10

that we can spread out the irreducible factors in
{\cal F}_{k}
along the diagonal
via unimodular transformations so as to form

\widetilde{T}(\lambda)

such that
\mathbf{d}_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}_{k}}}(\widetilde{T})=|\mathbf{q}(\lambda)|_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}_{k}}}

-homogeneous,
and
\mathbf{d}_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}_{1}}}(\widetilde{T})=\mathbf{d}_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}_{1}}}(S)
.
Since
\mathbf{d}_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}_{1}}}(S)\,\succeq\,\mathbf{g}
,
there exists a finite sequence of interchanges and compressions of adjacent diagonal entries

\mathbf{d}_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}_{1}}}(S)

\mathbf{g}
.
Implementing this sequence via unimodular transformations from Corollary

will produce a triangular matrix polynomial

such that
\mathbf{d}_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}_{k}}}(T)=\mathbf{d}_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}_{k}}}(\widetilde{T})=|\mathbf{q}(\lambda)|_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}_{k}}}

-homogeneous,

\mathbf{d}_{{}_{\scriptstyle{\cal F}_{1}}}(T)=\mathbf{g}
,

,
and

is unimodularly equivalent to

,

.
In other words,

is the desired degree-

.
∎

∎

The results in Proposition
5.7

and its Corollary
5.9

show that the triangularization question is still somewhat tractable
when there are no more than two different degrees
among all of the irreducible divisors that are present.
However, when irreducible divisors have three or more degrees,
the picture gets much more involved,
with some significant combinatorial complexity now possible.
The strategy guiding Example
5.3
is still viable, though,
and sometimes is able to provide sufficient conditions
for guaranteeing that a triangularization is possible,
although these conditions may no longer be necessary.
To see why this is the case, we consider a few more examples,
this time with irreducible divisors of degrees 1, 2, and 3.

This brings us now to our final result,
which gives a generalized sufficient condition
for guaranteeing the existence of a triangularization over an arbitrary field.

Proposition 5.11
.

Let
P(\lambda)
be a strictly regular
n\times n
polynomial matrix
of degree
d
over a field
\,\mathbb{F}
.
Let
S(\lambda)
be the Smith form of
P(\lambda)
,
and assume that all irreducible divisors are degree
k
or less.
Organize the degree-
2
through degree-
k

into
n

q_{1}(\lambda),q_{2}(\lambda),\dotsc,q_{n}(\lambda)

in the same way as in the proof of Lemma

,
and define the degree gaps
g_{i}:=d-\deg(q_{i})
.

(
Note that some of the

)

If the degree-1 factor-counting vector for the diagonal of
S(\lambda)

majorizes the gap vector
\mathbf{g}:=(g_{1},g_{2},\dotsc,g_{n})
,
then
P(\lambda)
has a triangularization of degree
d
.

Proof.

It is possible to employ the techniques
pictured in Examples
5.3
and
5.10

(i.e., following the pattern of the proof of Lemma
3.11

in distributing all irreducible factors of degree two and higher,
and then filling in the rest of the available spaces
with all of the remaining degree-
1

to design a target diagonal in which all of the entries have degree
d
.
Note that the degree-
\ell

2\leq\ell\leq k

in this target diagonal are all
1

and thus are definitely all realizable by spreading out
the irreducible divisors in the Smith form

3.4

and Lemma

.
The majorization hypothesis

.
Once all of these factor-counting vectors for the target diagonal are realized,

.

In the statement of Proposition
5.11
it was noted
that under the given conditions,
it is possible for the gap vector
\mathbf{g}
to have negative entries.
Whenever this occurs,
then it is impossible for any conceivable degree-1 factor-counting vector
for the Smith form
to majorize
\mathbf{g}
, since all entries of a factor-counting vector are non-negative.
In this scenario, then, Proposition
5.11

tells us nothing about the existence or non-existence of a triangularization.
Other arrangements of the higher degree irreducible factors along the diagonal
may still lead to a triangularization,
as illustrated in Example
5.10
.

Remark 5.12
.

Note that the condition in Proposition
5.11

for ensuring triangularizability
can be adapted to regular matrix polynomials
P(\lambda)

having nontrivial infinite spectral structure.
First apply a Möbius transformation
to transform
P(\lambda)
into a matrix polynomial
Q(\lambda)

with only finite spectral structure,
i.e., into a strictly regular matrix polynomial,
as was done in the proof of Theorem
4.7
.
Since any Möbius transformation preserves the degree
of any irreducible divisor of degree two or higher by Lemma
4.4
,
all of the degree-
\ell
factor-counting vectors of
Q(\lambda)

will be exactly the same as those of
P(\lambda)
,
except for
\ell=1
.
The partial multiplicities at
\infty
for
P(\lambda)

will turn into partial multiplicities for
Q(\lambda)

at some degree-1 irreducible
\lambda-\omega
,
hence the degree-
1
factor-counting vector
for the Smith form of
Q(\lambda)

will be equal to the sum of the degree-
1
factor-counting vector
for the Smith form of
P(\lambda)

together with the partial multiplicity sequence for
P(\lambda)
at
\infty
.
In other words, the infinite partial multiplicities
effectively get included with all of the degree-1 irreducible factors.
We can now apply the majorization condition in Proposition
5.11

(or Proposition
5.7
)
to determine if a triangularization for
Q(\lambda)
is guaranteed.
If it is, then the inverse Möbius transformation
applied to this triangularization for
Q(\lambda)

provides a spectrally equivalent triangularization for
P(\lambda)
.
Note that this generalization to all regular matrix polynomials
appears in
[
19
,
20
]
for real matrix polynomials.

6
Conclusion

This work has shown that any regular matrix polynomial
P(\lambda)

over an arbitrary field
\mathbb{F}

is spectrally equivalent
to a
k
-quasi-triangular matrix polynomial over
\mathbb{F}

of the same size and degree,
where
k
is the largest degree
among all of the irreducible divisors of
P(\lambda)
.
This extends and generalizes the earlier work in
[
19
]
,
which found triangularizations and
2
-quasi-triangularizations
for regular matrix polynomials over algebraically closed fields
and the real field
\mathbb{R}
.
We have also shown that for any field
\mathbb{F}
,
this
k
is the best possible general bound on the diagonal block sizes of quasi-triangularizations
that holds for all regular matrix polynomials over
\mathbb{F}
.

Several new tools and results were developed
in order to achieve this extension to arbitrary fields.
Among these are:

•

a technique to allow the flexible but controlled movement of individual irreducible factors
up and down the diagonal of a triangular polynomial matrix via unimodular transformations,

•

a new homogeneous partitioning property
of “tightly packed” integer multisets,

•

a reformulation of the interaction of Möbius transformations with spectral data,
in a way that makes it easier to work with higher degree irreducible divisors.

A number of issues remain to be settled,
especially ones related to the size of diagonal blocks
in quasi-triangularizations.
Although we know that these block sizes need never be any larger than
k
,
and that sometimes they are all forced to be of size exactly
k
,
very often quasi-triangularizations
can be found with diagonal block sizes much smaller than the upper bound
k
.
For given spectral data, can one predict how small
the diagonal blocks can be made in a quasi-triangularization,
and indeed when these blocks can all be made
1\times 1
,
i.e., when can we actually triangularize?
Some limited results were given along these lines,
but much about this question still remains open.
