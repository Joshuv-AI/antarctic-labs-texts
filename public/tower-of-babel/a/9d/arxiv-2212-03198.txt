A new method to invert InSAR data to resolve stress changes on a fracture embedded in a 3D heterogeneous crust
Oliver Bodart, Valérie Cayol, Farshid Dabaghi

Abstract
We present a new method to invert variable stress changes of fractures from InSAR ground displacements. Fractures can be either faults or magma intrusions, embeded in a 3D heterogeneous crust with prominent topographies. The method is based on a fictituous domain approach using a finite element discretization of XFEM type. A cost function involves the misfit between the solution of the physical problem and the observed data together with the smoothing terms. Regularization parameters are determined by using L-curves. The method is then reformulated to be applied to InSAR data (masked and noisy), projected in Earth-Satellite directions. Synthetic tests confirm the efficiency and effectiveness of our method.



1
Physical and computational model

1.1
Mathematical modelling of the solid

From a mathematical point of view, a volcano is a bounded open set
\Omega\subset\,\mathbb{R}^{3}
, occupied by an elastic solid. This set is assumed to have a smooth boundary
\partial\Omega
which we separate in two (non empty) parts
\partial\Omega:={\Gamma_{\text{D}}}\cup\overline{\Gamma_{\text{N}}}
, with
{\Gamma_{\text{D}}}\cap{\Gamma_{\text{N}}}=\emptyset
. As depicted on Figure
1
, the subset
\Gamma_{\text{N}}
is actually the ground of the volcano and free to move. The subset
\Gamma_{\text{D}}
is an artificial boundary defined in order to work on a finite size object and is assumed to to satisfy a 0 displacement condition.
We assume that the elastic solid occupying
\Omega
is subject to a body force field
\mathbf{f}
.
In the sequel, boldface letters will be used to denote (usually
3
-dimensional) vector fields. Plain letters will represent scalar quantities.

Volcanic domain
\Omega
and its boundaries

We denote by
\mathbf{x}=(x,y,z)
the generic point in
\,\mathbb{R}^{3}
, and by
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x})
the displacement field of the solid
\Omega
. The Cauchy stress
\sigma(\mathbf{u})
and strain
\varepsilon(\mathbf{u})
are given by

\sigma(\mathbf{u})=\lambda\textrm{Tr}\bigl(\varepsilon(\mathbf{u})\bigr)\mathbf{I}_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}+2\mu\varepsilon(\mathbf{u})\quad\text{and}\quad\varepsilon(\mathbf{u})=\frac{1}{2}(\nabla\mathbf{u}+\nabla\mathbf{u}^{\mathsf{T}}),

where
(\lambda,\mu)
are the Lamé coefficients of the material,
\mathbf{I}_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}
the
identity matrix, and
\textrm{Tr}(\cdot)
the matrix trace.

We also assume the presence of a crack in the volcano, which is represented mathematically by a 2-D surface
\Gamma_{\text{C}}\subset\Omega
on which a traction or pressure force will be exerted (see also Figure
1
). In a volcanic context,
\Gamma_{\text{C}}
might represent a magma–filled crack on a fault. As already said in the introduction, the shape and position of the crack are supposed to be known in this work.
The deformation field
\mathbf{u}
of the solid is supposed to satisfy the following elastostatic system:

\displaystyle-\mathrm{div}\penalty\ \sigma({\bf u})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{f}

\displaystyle\textrm{in}\penalty\ \Omega,

(1)

\displaystyle{\bf u}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\textrm{in}\penalty\ \Gamma_{\text{D}},

(2)

\displaystyle{\sigma}({\bf u})\cdot{{\mathbf{n}}}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\textrm{on}\penalty\ \Gamma_{\text{N}},

(3)

\displaystyle{\sigma}({\bf u})\cdot{{\mathbf{n}}^{\pm}}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{t}}^{\pm}

\displaystyle\textrm{on}\penalty\ \Gamma_{\text{C}}.

(4)

The first equation is the equilibrium law describing the (linear) elastic behaviour of the material. Condition (
2
) describes the fact that the displacement vanishes on the underground boundary of the solid. In equation (
3
), and in the sequel,
{\mathbf{n}}
denotes the unit normal vector on the boundary of
\Omega

Finally, equation (

\Gamma_{\text{C}}

and
{\mathbf{n}}^{-}=-{\mathbf{n}}^{+}
are unit normal vectors on the crack
\Gamma_{\text{C}}
(see Figure
2
). The vector functions
{\mathbf{t}}^{\pm}(\mathbf{x})
denote the force exerted on the crack. They are such that
{\mathbf{t}}^{+}=-{\mathbf{t}}^{-}
. When this force acts normally to the crack, that is
{\mathbf{t}}^{\pm}(\mathbf{x})=p(\mathbf{x}){\mathbf{n}}^{\pm}
, the force is called a pressure, otherwise in general it is called a traction force.

This traction force is the actual unknown of our problem. From the mathematical point of view it is supposed to be quadratically integrable as well as its gradient.

In order to derive a finite element approximation of the state equations (
1
)–(
4
), we need a weak formulation of the system. This is done by multiplying the equation (
1
) by a regular vector field
{\bf v}
such that
{\bf v}=0
, and then integrating by parts over
\Omega
. Using the conditions (
2
)–(
4
) then yields:

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\sigma({\bf u}):\varepsilon({\bf v})\,\mathrm{d}{\Omega}=\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{f}\cdot{\bf v}\,\mathrm{d}{\Omega}+\int_{\Gamma_{\text{C}}}({\mathbf{t}}^{\pm})\cdot{\bf v}\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma_{\text{C}},

(5)

where
:
is the double inner product for matrices, i.e. for
A=(a_{ij})_{i,j=1,2,3}
and
B=(b_{ij})_{i,j=1,2,3}
,

A:B=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\sum_{j=1}^{3}a_{ij}b_{ij}.

Notice that this approach allow us to deal with situations where the elastic material is nonhomogeneous and anisotropic, that is when the Lamé coefficients are not constant.

Finite element model

The direct problem (
1

Assuming that

Therefore, the equations (

\displaystyle=\mathbf{f}^{\pm}

(6)

\displaystyle{\bf u}^{\pm}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\textrm{on}\penalty\ \Gamma_{\text{D}}\cap\partial\Omega^{\pm},

\displaystyle({\sigma}({\bf u})\cdot{{\mathbf{n}}})^{\pm}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\textrm{on}\penalty\ \Gamma_{\text{N}}\cap\partial\Omega^{\pm},

\displaystyle[\![{\bf u}]\!]

\displaystyle=0

Let us now describe the discretization of this problem via the finite element method.
A lagrange multiplier
\lambda
defined on
\Gamma_{0}
is introduced to enforce the continuity of the displacement across the

6
)–(
11
) which
unknown is in the form

(see
[

]
for details).

In order to numerically approximate the system, consider a tetrahedral mesh of
\Omega
(as e.g. in Figure
3
). Let
\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{\pm}_{i}
be a finite element basis defined on this mesh. Also defining a mesh of the extended crack, we build finite elements bases
\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j}
on
\Gamma_{0}
and
\Gamma_{\text{C}}
respectively.

Identifying the unknowns
{\bf u}^{+}
,
{\bf u}^{-}
and
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
with there value on the nodes of the mesh, the discretized form of Problem (
6
)–(
11
), then reads

\mathrm{K}{\bf X}={\bf F}.

(12)

The matrix
\mathrm{K}
of this system is built from the stiffness matrices
A^{\pm}
, and the constraint coupling matrices on
\Gamma_{0}
denoted by

. More precisely, we have

\mathrm{K}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}A^{+}&0&{B^{+}}^{T}\\
0&A^{-}&-{B^{-}}^{T}\\
B^{+}&-B^{-}&0\end{array}\right),

where

A^{\pm}:=\left[\int_{\Omega^{\pm}}\sigma(\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{i}^{\pm}):\varepsilon(\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{j}^{\pm})\,\mathrm{d}\Omega^{\pm}\right]_{ij},\qquad B^{\pm}:=\left[\int_{\Gamma_{0}}\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{i}^{\pm}\cdot\boldsymbol{\psi}_{j}\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma_{0}\right]_{ij}

The right hand side vector
{\bf F}^{\pm}
is given by

{\bf F}=\left(\begin{array}[]{l}\mathbf{F}^{+}\\
\mathbf{F}^{-}\\
0\end{array}\right),\qquad\mathbf{F}^{\pm}:=\left[\int_{\Omega^{\pm}}\mathbf{f}^{\pm}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{i}^{\pm}\,\mathrm{d}\Omega^{\pm}+\int_{\Gamma_{\text{C}}}{\mathbf{t}}^{\pm}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{i}^{\pm}\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma_{\text{C}}\right]_{i},

which boils down to the algebraic formulation

{\bf F}=\left(\begin{array}[]{l}\mathbf{F}^{+}\\
\mathbf{F}^{-}\\
0\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}M_{\Omega}^{+}{\bf f}^{+}\\
M_{\Omega}^{-}{\bf f}^{-}\\
\penalty\ \penalty\ 0\end{array}\right)+\left(\begin{array}[]{c}+M_{C}{\mathbf{t}}\\
-M_{C}{\mathbf{t}}\\
\penalty\ \penalty\ 0\end{array}\right),

where the mass matrices
M_{\Omega}^{\pm}
and the coupling matrix on
\Gamma_{\text{C}}
, denoted by
M_{C}
, are given by

\displaystyle\left[M_{\Omega}^{\pm}\right]_{ij}=\int_{\Omega^{\pm}}\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{i}^{\pm}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{j}^{\pm}\,\mathrm{d}\Omega^{\pm},\qquad\displaystyle\left[M_{c}\right]_{ij}=\int_{\Gamma_{\text{C}}}\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{i}^{\pm}\cdot\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j}\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma_{\text{C}}.

Eventually, we can naturally define two matrices

and

such that we have

The matrix

is symmetric and positive definite, which allows the use of powerful classical solvers.
The implementation of this model uses the finite element library GetFem++
[
9
]
. The library provides all the necessary tools to handle various types of PDEs,
and links to classical powerful solvers (conjugate gradient, SuperLU, MUMPS) as well as other routines for mesh management, definition of cracks and boundaries via level set functions)
which make it quite versatile and powerful.

2
Inversion from full ground measurements

2.1
Mathematical formulation of the problem

Let us assume the displacement field of the volcano to be fully measured on the ground
\Gamma_{\text{N}}
, and call it
\mathbf{u}_{d}
. The inversion problem consists in finding a traction vector
{\mathbf{t}}
such that the solution of Problem (
1
)–(
4
) satisfies
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{u}_{d}
on
\Gamma_{\text{N}}
. However this is known to be an ill-posed problem (e.g. the solution is not unique), and moreover the measurements are perturbed by random uncertainties. Therefore we adopt a least squares approach, combined with a regularization (i.e. smoothing) technique to build the inversion process.

Consider the following cost function:

J({\mathbf{t}})=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Gamma_{\text{N}}}({\bf u}-{\bf u}_{d})^{\top}{\mathbf{C}^{-1}}({\bf u}-{\bf u}_{d})\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma_{\text{N}}+\frac{\alpha_{0}}{2}\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma_{\text{C}}}\lvert{\mathbf{t}}\rvert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma_{\text{C}}+\frac{\alpha_{1}}{2}\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma_{\text{C}}}\lvert\nabla{\mathbf{t}}\rvert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma_{\text{C}},

(13)

where
{\mathbf{C}}
denotes the covariance operator of the measurements uncertainties (see e.g.
[
11
]
), and is assumed to be positive definite,
\top
denotes the transpose operator,
and finally, the non–negative constants
\alpha_{0}
and
\alpha_{1}
are the Tikhonov regularization (or smoothing) parameters.

We aim at minimizing this cost function over the space of feasible traction vectors. This space consists in quadratically integrable vectors, which gradient is also quadratically integrable.

In
[
1
,
2
]
, a simplified version of this problem is mathematically studied. The cost function only features the first smoothing term. As it will be shown later on, our tests have proved the necessity of smoothing the gradient, in a practical framework, to obtain a physically admissible solution
{\mathbf{t}}
.
The optimal choices for the values of
\alpha_{0}
and
\alpha_{1}
will be discussed further.

To derive the optimality conditions for the Problem (
13
), we introduce the following Lagrangian:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}({\bf u},{\mathbf{t}},\boldsymbol{\phi})=J({\mathbf{t}})-\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(\sigma({\bf u}):\varepsilon(\boldsymbol{\phi})-f)\cdot\boldsymbol{\phi}\,\mathrm{d}{\Omega}+\int_{\Gamma_{\text{C}}}{{\mathbf{t}}^{\pm}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\phi}\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma_{\text{C}},

where
\boldsymbol{\phi}
is the Lagrange multiplier for the constraint (
1
)–(
4
) in the weak form (
5
).
When
\nabla_{{\bf u}}{\mathcal{L}}=0
and
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}{\mathcal{L}}=0
, we have
\nabla_{{\mathbf{t}}}{\mathcal{L}}=\nabla J
. Cancelling
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}{\mathcal{L}}
gives problem (
1
)–(
4
). Cancelling
\nabla_{{\bf u}}{\mathcal{L}}
and performing integration by parts yields that
\boldsymbol{\phi}
is the solution of

\begin{cases}-\mathrm{div}\penalty\ \sigma(\boldsymbol{\phi})=0&\textrm{in}\penalty\ \Omega,\\
\boldsymbol{\phi}=0&\textrm{in}\penalty\ \Gamma_{\text{D}},\\
{\sigma}(\boldsymbol{\phi})\cdot{{\mathbf{n}}}=\mathrm{C}^{-1}({\bf u}-{\bf u}_{d})&\textrm{on}\penalty\ \Gamma_{\text{N}},\\
\end{cases}

(14)

called the
adjoint state
system. Finally, computing
\nabla_{{\bf u}}{\mathcal{L}}
, for
{\bf u}
and
\boldsymbol{\phi}
satisfying (
1
)–(
4
) and (
14
), gives:

\nabla J({\mathbf{t}})=\alpha_{0}{\mathbf{t}}+\alpha_{1}\nabla{\mathbf{t}}+(\boldsymbol{\phi}\cdot{\mathbf{n}}^{\pm}).

(15)

We refer the reader to
[
1
,
2
]
, for further details.

After splitting the computational domain
\Omega
, the cost function
J
in the optimization problem (
13
) becomes:

\begin{split}J({\mathbf{t}})&=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Gamma_{\text{N}}^{+}}({\bf u}^{+}-{\bf u}_{d}^{+})^{\top}{\mathbf{C}^{-1}}({\bf u}^{+}-{\bf u}_{d}^{+})\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma_{\text{N}}^{+}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Gamma_{\text{N}}^{-}}({\bf u}^{-}-{\bf u}_{d}^{-})^{\top}{\mathbf{C}^{-1}}({\bf u}^{-}-{\bf u}_{d}^{-})\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma_{\text{N}}^{-}\\
&+\frac{\alpha_{0}}{2}\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma_{\text{C}}}\lvert{\mathbf{t}}\rvert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma_{\text{C}}+\frac{\alpha_{1}}{2}\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma_{\text{C}}}\lvert\nabla{\mathbf{t}}\rvert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma_{\text{C}}.\end{split}

(16)

The previous optimality conditions rewrite naturally from this new formulation.

2.2
The Discrete Problem

The next step is to adapt the cost function, the adjoint state and the gradient of the cost function to the discrete system (
12
). To this aim, it has to be noticed that all terms in (
16
) are symmetric. Discretizing the two first terms will involve the introduction of mass matrices on
\Gamma_{\text{N}}^{\pm}
. On needs to keep the symmetry of the discrete form. Since the covariance matrix
\mathbf{C}
is positive definite, it is also the same for its inverse and we can write

\mathbf{C}=QDQ^{-1},\qquad\mathbf{C}^{-1}=QD^{-1}Q^{-1},

where
Q
is the matrix which columns are the eigenvectors of
\mathbf{C}
and
D
the diagonal matrix of the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{C}
. This diagonalized form allows us to write
\mathbf{C}^{-1}=\mathbf{C}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{C}^{-\frac{1}{2}}
, where

\mathbf{C}^{-\frac{1}{2}}=QD^{-\frac{1}{2}}Q^{-1}.

In view of this, we rewrite the continuous cost function
J
as

\begin{split}J({\mathbf{t}})&=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Gamma_{\text{N}}^{+}}({\bf u}^{+}-{\bf u}_{d}^{+})^{\top}{\mathbf{C}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{C}^{-\frac{1}{2}}}({\bf u}^{+}-{\bf u}_{d}^{+})\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma_{\text{N}}^{+}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Gamma_{\text{N}}^{-}}({\bf u}^{-}-{\bf u}_{d}^{-})^{\top}{\mathbf{C}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{C}^{-\frac{1}{2}}}({\bf u}^{-}-{\bf u}_{d}^{-})\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma_{\text{N}}^{-}\\
&+\frac{\alpha_{0}}{2}\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma_{\text{C}}}\lvert{\mathbf{t}}\rvert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma_{\text{C}}+\frac{\alpha_{1}}{2}\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma_{\text{C}}}\lvert\nabla{\mathbf{t}}\rvert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma_{\text{C}}.\end{split}

(17)

As in Section
1.2
, we will identify the previous functions with their values at the mesh nodes. Define a set of finite element basis functions
\boldsymbol{\chi}_{i}^{\pm}
(with 3 degrees of freedom at each mesh node) defined on
\Gamma_{\text{N}}^{\pm}
. Then, define the ground mass matrix

M_{G}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ll}M_{G}^{+}&0\\
0&M_{G}^{-}\end{array}\right),

(18)

with
\displaystyle\left[M_{G}^{\pm}\right]_{ij}=\int_{\Gamma_{\text{N}}}\boldsymbol{\chi}_{i}^{\pm}\cdot\boldsymbol{\chi}_{j}^{\pm}\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma_{\text{N}}
.
Let us also define the matrices
M_{F_{0}}
and
M_{F_{1}}
as

\left[M_{F_{0}}\right]_{ij}=\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma_{\text{C}}}\boldsymbol{\theta_{i}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\theta_{j}}\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma_{\text{C}},\qquad\left[M_{F_{1}}\right]_{ij}=\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma_{\text{C}}}\nabla\boldsymbol{\theta_{i}}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{\theta_{j}}\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma_{\text{C}}.

Recall that
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j}
are the finite element basis functions defined on
\Gamma_{\text{C}}
.

Finally, in view of the domain decomposition method, the mesh nodes located on the ground will be on either side of the crack. Therefore, the measured displacement and the restriction to the ground nodes of the computed displacement (denoted
{\bf u}_{G}
) can be written as

{\bf u}_{d}=\left(\begin{array}[]{l}{\bf u}_{d}^{+}\\
{\bf u}_{d}^{-}\end{array}\right),\qquad{\bf u}_{G}=\left(\begin{array}[]{l}{\bf u}_{G}^{+}\\
{\bf u}_{G}^{-}\end{array}\right).

Consider then the reduction matrix
\mathcal{O}_{R}
such that

\mathcal{O}_{R}{\bf X}={\bf u}_{G},

where
{\bf X}
is the solution of (
12
).

The discrete version of the cost function
J
defined by (
17
) is then

J_{\mathtt{d}}({\mathbf{t}})=\frac{1}{2}(\mathcal{O}_{R}{\bf X}-{\bf u}_{d})^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{C}^{-\frac{1}{2}}M_{G}\mathbf{C}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\mathcal{O}_{R}{\bf X}-{\bf u}_{d})+\frac{\alpha_{0}}{2}({\mathbf{t}}^{\top}M_{F_{0}}{\mathbf{t}})+\frac{\alpha_{1}}{2}({\mathbf{t}}^{\top}M_{F_{1}}{\mathbf{t}}).

(19)

The optimality conditions for the minimizer of the discrete cost function
J_{\mathtt{d}}
are obtained via the characterization of the saddle point of the following (discrete) Lagrangian function:

{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathtt{d}}({\bf X},{\mathbf{t}},\boldsymbol{\phi})=J_{\mathtt{d}}({\mathbf{t}})-\langle\mathrm{K}{\bf X}-(L_{\small\Omega}{\bf f}+L_{C}{\mathbf{t}}),\boldsymbol{\phi}\rangle.

Computing the partial derivative of
{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathtt{d}}
and then cancelling them, lead to the discrete counterpart
of the adjoint problem (
14
) as:

\mathrm{K}\boldsymbol{\phi}=\mathcal{O}_{R}^{\top}\mathbf{C}^{-\frac{1}{2}}M_{G}\mathbf{C}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\mathcal{O}_{R}{\bf X}-{\bf u}_{d}),

which rewrites, denoting
{\bf u}_{G}=\mathcal{O}_{R}{\bf X}
,

\mathrm{K}\boldsymbol{\phi}=\mathcal{O}_{R}^{\top}\mathbf{C}^{-\frac{1}{2}}M_{G}\mathbf{C}^{-\frac{1}{2}}({\bf u}_{G}-{\bf u}_{d}),

(20)

where
K
is the (symmetric) matrix of System (
12
).
The gradient of
J_{\mathtt{d}}
at any point
{\mathbf{t}}
is then:

\nabla J_{\mathtt{d}}({\mathbf{t}})=\alpha_{0}M_{F_{0}}{\mathbf{t}}+\alpha_{1}M_{F_{1}}{\mathbf{t}}+L_{C}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\phi}.

(21)

2.3
Practical minimization algorithm

In a previous work (see
[
2
]
), we presented two methods to minimize a simpler version of our cost function
J_{\mathtt{d}}
: the conjugate gradient algorithm and a quasi-Newton method (low storage BFGS). These two techniques are natural choices due to the quadratic structure of the cost function. They were studied in depth in terms of computational performance and accuracy. The BFGS method appeared to be converging faster when the mesh of the domain
\Omega
is rather coarse. When the mesh becomes finer, the number of unknowns in the problem increases and the two methods tend to give similar results. In the framework of applications which we are interested in, we aim at fast processes, that is we will most of the time consider meshes that are rather coarse except in the neighborhood of the crack
\Gamma_{\text{C}}
. Therefore we will use a BFGS algorithm (see
[
4
,
5
,
7
,
10
]
) in our numerical tests. It involves the construction of a sequence of approximation of the inverse of the Hessian matrix of the cost function
J_{\mathtt{d}}
. We use a limited storage version of the algorithm called L-BFGS (see
[
8
]
). The method is presented in Algorithm

, with the necessary adaptations to our problem.

As usual with this type of methods sequences are built and will be denoted as follows:

•

3
Taking into account the earth-satellite directions

3.1
Mathematical framework and new optimization problem

Before going further, let us first remark that the previous section can easily be adapted to the case where the measurements of the displacement field are performed only on a part of the ground
\widetilde{\Gamma_{\text{N}}}\subset\Gamma_{\text{N}}
.
The cost function to be minimized becomes

J({\mathbf{t}})=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\widetilde{\Gamma_{\text{N}}}}({\bf u}-{\bf u}_{d})^{\top}{\mathbf{C}^{-1}}({\bf u}-{\bf u}_{d})\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma_{\text{N}}+\frac{\alpha_{0}}{2}\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma_{\text{C}}}\lvert{\mathbf{t}}\rvert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma_{\text{C}}+\frac{\alpha_{1}}{2}\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma_{\text{C}}}\lvert\nabla{\mathbf{t}}\rvert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma_{\text{C}}.

Rewriting the optimality conditions and adapting the algorithms is straightforward. This is what is actually implemented in our software.

However, assuming the displacement field to be known in cartesian coordinates is not realistic for the applications. In this section we aim at adapting our work to the case where measurements are provides by satellite radar interferometry
(as e.g. in
[
6
]
). We will first state precisely what measurements are made and then adapt our methods to this new framework.

Let
\mathbf{u}_{d}=(u_{x},u_{y},u_{z})^{\top}
be a displacement field on
\Gamma_{\text{N}}
, written in cartesian coordinates. A satellite will aim at the ground to measure a displacement, and the resulting measurement will be in the form

{\bf p}^{\top}\mathbf{u}_{d}
where the aiming direction (also called earth-satellite direction)
{\bf p}=(p_{x},p_{y},p_{z})
is a unit vector oriented from the ground to the satellite. Let then
N\geq 1
be the number of satellites, each associated to a direction
{\bf p}_{i}=(p_{ix},p_{iy},p_{iz})
for
i=1,\ldots,N
. We can build a matrix
\mathbf{P}:\mathbb{R}^{3}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N}
as
\mathbf{P}=({\bf p}_{1},{\bf p}_{2},\cdots,{\bf p}_{N})^{\top}
, so that the actual measurement is

\mathbf{R}_{d}=\mathbf{P}\mathbf{u}_{d}.

The vector field
\mathbf{R}_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is defined on
\Gamma_{\text{N}}
, and the matrix
\mathbf{P}
also operates on the solutions of System (
1
)–(
4
). Hence, given the field
\mathbf{R}_{d}
, our new optimization problem consists now in finding the traction vector
{\mathbf{t}}
minimizing the following cost function:

J({\mathbf{t}})=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Gamma_{\text{N}}}(\mathbf{P}{\bf u}-\mathbf{R}_{d})^{\top}{{\mathbf{C}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}{\mathbf{C}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}}(\mathbf{P}{\bf u}-\mathbf{R}_{d})\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma_{\text{N}}+\frac{\alpha_{0}}{2}\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma_{\text{C}}}\lvert{\mathbf{t}}\rvert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma_{\text{C}}+\frac{\alpha_{1}}{2}\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma_{\text{C}}}\lvert\nabla{\mathbf{t}}\rvert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma_{\text{C}},

(24)

where
\alpha_{0}>0
and
\alpha_{1}>0
are the regularization parameters.
The optimality conditions for this problem are derived in the same way than in Section
2.1
. We will not go into details about this and focus on the discrete version of the problem.

Using the definitions of Sections
1.2
and
2.2
, and in view of (
19
) and (
24
) the discrete cost function becomes

J_{\mathtt{d}}({\mathbf{t}})=\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{P}\mathcal{O}_{R}{\bf X}-\mathbf{R}_{d})^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{C}^{-\frac{1}{2}}M_{G}\mathbf{C}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbf{P}\mathcal{O}_{R}{\bf X}-\mathbf{R}_{d})+\frac{\alpha_{0}}{2}({\mathbf{t}}^{\top}M_{F_{0}}{\mathbf{t}})+\frac{\alpha_{1}}{2}({\mathbf{t}}^{\top}M_{F_{1}}{\mathbf{t}}).

(25)

where
X
is the solution of the discrete system (
12
). This finite dimensional problem then boils down to finding the saddle point of the following Lagrangian

{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathtt{d}}({\bf X},{\mathbf{t}},\boldsymbol{\phi})=J_{\mathtt{d}}({\mathbf{t}})-\langle\mathrm{K}{\bf X}-(L_{\small\Omega}{\bf f}+L_{C}{\mathbf{t}}),\boldsymbol{\phi}\rangle.

Computing the partial derivative of
{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathtt{d}}
and then cancelling them, leads to the discrete adjoint problem (
14
):

\mathrm{K}\boldsymbol{\phi}=\mathcal{O}_{R}^{\top}\mathbf{P}^{\top}\mathbf{C}^{-\frac{1}{2}}M_{G}\mathbf{C}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbf{P}\mathcal{O}_{R}{\bf X}-\mathbf{R}_{d}),

(26)

and the gradient of
J_{\mathtt{d}}
is:

\nabla J_{\mathtt{d}}=\alpha_{0}M_{F_{0}}{\mathbf{t}}+\alpha_{1}M_{F_{1}}{\mathbf{t}}+L_{C}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\phi}.

(27)

The minimization Algorithm
1
can then be adapted to the new adjoint state and gradient to obtain the numerical approximation of the traction
{\mathbf{t}}
on the crack by using the surface measurements provided by radar interferometry
\mathbf{r}_{d}
.

The optimal step size

This section is dedicated to some mathematical details for interested readers. First part is concerned to compute the optimal step size. To simplify the presentation, let us introduce
c_{N}
defined on
\Gamma_{\text{C}}

c_{N}(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{w})=\int_{\Gamma_{\text{N}}}\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{C}^{-1}\mathbf{w}\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma_{\text{N}},

so that the cost functional in (
13
) becomes

J({\mathbf{t}}):=\frac{1}{2}c_{N}({\bf u}-{\bf u}_{d},{\bf u}-{\bf u}_{d})+\frac{\alpha_{0}}{2}\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma_{\text{C}}}\lvert{\mathbf{t}}\rvert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma_{\text{C}}+\frac{\alpha_{1}}{2}\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma_{\text{C}}}\lvert\nabla{\mathbf{t}}\rvert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma_{\text{C}}.

Then the directional derivative of
J
in the direction of a given
{\mathbf{d}}
reads

\langle\frac{\partial J}{\partial{\mathbf{t}}}({\mathbf{t}}),{\mathbf{d}}\rangle_{\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1}}=c_{N}({\bf u}-{\bf u}_{d},\mathbf{w})+\alpha_{0}\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma_{\text{C}}}({\mathbf{t}}\cdot{\mathbf{d}})\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma_{\text{C}}+\alpha_{1}\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma_{\text{C}}}(\nabla{\mathbf{t}}\cdot\nabla{\mathbf{d}})\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma_{\text{C}},

where
\mathbf{w}
is the solution to (
5
) with
{\mathbf{t}}={\mathbf{d}}
.
Therefore, we may compute the optimal step size
\rho
,
with a search direction
d
by solving

\langle\frac{\partial J}{\partial{\mathbf{t}}}({\mathbf{t}}+\rho{\mathbf{d}}),{\mathbf{d}}\rangle_{\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1}}=0,

that is

c_{N}(\mathbf{u}+\rho\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{u}_{d},\mathbf{w})+\alpha_{0}\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma_{\text{C}}}\big(({\mathbf{t}}+\rho{\mathbf{d}})\cdot{\mathbf{d}}\big)\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma_{\text{C}}+\alpha_{1}\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma_{\text{C}}}\big((\nabla{\mathbf{t}}+\rho\nabla{\mathbf{d}})\cdot\nabla{\mathbf{d}}\big)\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma_{\text{C}}=0,

which gives

\displaystyle\rho\left[c_{N}(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{w})+\alpha_{0}\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma_{\text{C}}}({\mathbf{d}}\cdot{\mathbf{d}})\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma_{\text{C}}+\alpha_{1}\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma_{\text{C}}}(\nabla{\mathbf{d}}\cdot\nabla{\mathbf{d}})\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma_{\text{C}}\right]+c_{N}({\bf u}-{\bf u}_{d},\mathbf{w})
