Double parton distributions in the nucleon from lattice QCD Preprint: DESY-21-066
Gunnar S. Bali, Markus Diehl, Benjamin Gläßle, Andreas Schäfer, and Christian Zimmermann

Abstract
We evaluate nucleon four-point functions in the framework of lattice QCD in order to extract the first Mellin moment of double parton distributions (DPDs) in the unpolarized proton. In this first study, we employ an n f = 2 + 1 n_{f}=2+1 ensemble with pseudoscalar masses of m π = 355 ​ MeV m_{\pi}=355\penalty\ \mathrm{MeV} and m K = 441 ​ MeV m_{K}=441\penalty\ \mathrm{MeV} . The results are converted to the scale μ = 2 ​ GeV \mu=2\penalty\ \mathrm{GeV} . Our calculation includes all Wick contractions, and for almost all of them a good statistical signal is obtained. We analyze the dependence of the DPD Mellin moments on the quark flavor and the quark polarization. Furthermore, the validity of frequently used factorization assumptions is investigated.

Introduction

The high-luminosity upgrade of the LHC will substantially improve its potential for discovering physics beyond the Standard Model. In parallel to the expected decrease of statistical errors, theoretical uncertainties for standard model processes must be reduced as much as possible to optimize the physics output. A particular challenge is the description of multiple hard scattering, which means that several hard parton-level interactions occur within the same proton-proton collision. Contributions from multiple scattering generically increase with the collision energy. They can be substantial for final states with high multiplicity. Many discovery channels for new physics are of this type. In this work, we focus on double parton scattering (DPS), which is the least complex and often most important representative of multiple hard scattering.

2
Theory

In this section we review certain basics on double parton distributions (DPDs) and their relevance in the context of double parton scattering.

2.1
Double parton distributions

The DPD of a given hadron parameterizes the joint probability of finding two partons with certain polarization and momentum fractions
x_{i}
at a given relative transverse distance
\boldsymbol{y}
. For the case of the unpolarized proton, DPDs of quarks and antiquarks are defined by a proton matrix element of two operators:

\displaystyle F_{a_{1}a_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2},\boldsymbol{y})=2p^{+}\int\mathrm{d}y^{-}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}z^{-}_{1}}{2\pi}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}z^{-}_{2}}{2\pi}\,

\displaystyle e^{i\mskip 1.5mu(x_{1}z_{1}^{-}+x_{2}z_{2}^{-})\mskip 1.5mup^{+}}

\displaystyle\times\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\sum}_{\lambda}\bra{p,\lambda}\mathcal{O}_{a_{1}}(y,z_{1})\,\mathcal{O}_{a_{2}}(0,z_{2})\ket{p,\lambda}\,,

(1)

where
\sum^{\prime}
indicates the average over the proton helicity states. In (
2.1
) we use light-cone coordinates
v^{\pm}=(v^{0}\pm v^{3})/\sqrt{2}
,
\boldsymbol{v}=(v^{1},v^{2})
for a given four-vector
v^{\mu}
. Moreover, we work in a frame where the transverse proton momentum vanishes, i.e.
\boldsymbol{p}=\boldsymbol{0}
. The light-cone operators appearing in (
2.1
) are defined as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{a}(y,z)

\displaystyle=\bar{q}\bigl(y-{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}z\bigr)\,\Gamma_{a}\,q\bigl(y+{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}z\bigr)\Big|_{z^{+}=y^{+}=0,\,\boldsymbol{z}=\boldsymbol{0}}\,,

(2)

where
a
specifies the quark flavor and polarization, which is determined by the spin projections

\displaystyle\Gamma_{q}

\displaystyle={\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\gamma^{+}\,,

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\Delta q}

\displaystyle={\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\gamma^{+}\gamma_{5}\,,

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\delta q}^{j}={\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}i\sigma^{j+}\gamma_{5}\quad(j=1,2)\,.

(3)

In this notation,
q
refers to the sum over all quark polarizations.
\Delta q
denotes the difference between positive and negative helicity contributions and, therefore, corresponds to the longitudinal quark polarization, whereas
\delta q
is the analogue for the case of transverse polarization. The expression of the light cone operators given in (
2
) is only valid in light cone gauge, otherwise a Wilson line has to be inserted. Notice that the light cone operators have to be renormalized. This leads to a scale dependence of the operators and, consequently, of the DPDs. For brevity, we do not indicate the scale.

Because of momentum conservation, the momentum fractions can take only values satisfying
|x_{1}|+|x_{2}|\leq 1
. Negative momentum fractions are associated with antiquarks, i.e.:

\displaystyle F_{a_{1}a_{2}}(-x_{1},x_{2},\boldsymbol{y})=\eta_{\textit{\tiny C}}^{a_{1}}F_{\bar{a}_{1}a_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2},\boldsymbol{y})\ ,\qquad F_{a_{1}a_{2}}(x_{1},-x_{2},\boldsymbol{y})=\eta_{\textit{\tiny C}}^{a_{2}}F_{a_{1}\bar{a}_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2},\boldsymbol{y})\ ,

(4)

where

\displaystyle\eta_{\textit{\tiny C}}^{a}=-1\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \text{for}\penalty\ a=q,\delta q\ ,\qquad\eta_{\textit{\tiny C}}^{a}=+1\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \text{for}\penalty\ a=\Delta q\,.

(5)

DPDs fulfill certain sum rules, which have been proposed in
Gaunt:2009re
and proven in
Gaunt:2012tfk
;
Diehl:2018kgr
. In this paper, we consider the number sum rule. In position space this can be formulated as:

\displaystyle\int_{-1+|x_{1}|}^{1-|x_{1}|}\mathrm{d}x_{2}\int_{|\boldsymbol{y}|>y_{\text{cut}}}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}^{2}\boldsymbol{y}\,F_{qq^{\prime}}(x_{1},x_{2},\boldsymbol{y})

\displaystyle\qquad=\left(N_{q^{\prime}}+\delta_{q\bar{q}^{\prime}}-\delta_{qq^{\prime}}\right)f_{q}(x_{1})+\mathcal{O}(\Lambda^{2}y_{\text{cut}}^{2})+\mathcal{O}\bigl(\alpha_{s}\bigr)\,,

(6)

where
f_{q}(x)
is an ordinary PDF for an unpolarized quark with flavor
q
and satisfies
f_{\bar{q}}(x)=f_{q}(-x)
.
N_{q^{\prime}}
is the number of valence quarks with flavor
q^{\prime}
. The lower cutoff in the integral over
\boldsymbol{y}
is necessary, because DPDs have a singular
1/\boldsymbol{y}^{2}
behavior for
\boldsymbol{y}^{2}\rightarrow 0
. This is caused by perturbative splitting processes, which are of
\mathcal{O}(\alpha_{s}(\mu))
. For more details see
Diehl:2017wew
. A common choice for the lower cutoff is
y_{\mathrm{cut}}=b_{0}/\mu
, where
\mu
is the renormalization scale and
b_{0}=2e^{-\gamma}\approx 1.12
with the Euler-Mascheroni constant
\gamma
.

The double parton distributions defined in (
2.1
) are needed to compute double parton scattering processes. The corresponding cross section can be written in terms of two DPDs, integrated over the transverse parton distance:

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\boldsymbol{y}\;F_{a_{1}a_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2},\boldsymbol{y})\,F_{b_{1}b_{2}}(x_{1}^{\prime},x_{2}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{y})\,.

(7)

Hence, the dependence of DPDs on the transverse distance is not directly accessible in experiments.

DPDs are often simplified and expressed in terms of single parton distributions within certain factorization approaches. The first procedure in this context is based on the insertion of a complete set of states between the operators in the matrix element in (
2.1
). Then it is assumed that nucleon states dominate, such that all other states can be neglected. This leads to an expression of DPDs in terms of impact parameter distributions
f_{a}(x,\boldsymbol{b})
:

\displaystyle F_{a_{1}a_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2},\boldsymbol{y})

\displaystyle\overset{?}{=}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\boldsymbol{b}\;f_{a_{1}}(x_{1},\boldsymbol{b}+\boldsymbol{y})\,f_{a_{2}}(x_{2},\boldsymbol{b})\,.

(8)

This kind of factorization has been investigated on the lattice for the case of the pion. Significant differences between the r.h.s. and l.h.s. of (
8
) have been found, while the orders of magnitude are consistent with each other
Bali:2020mij
. Similar observations have been made in quark model studies
Rinaldi:2020ybv
. We shall perform analogous investigations for the case of the nucleon in section
5
.

The other factorization approach frequently used assumes a complete factorization w.r.t. all arguments:

\displaystyle F_{a_{1}a_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2},\boldsymbol{y})

\displaystyle\overset{?}{=}f_{a_{1}}(x_{1})\,f_{a_{2}}(x_{2})\,G(\boldsymbol{y})\,.

(9)

This leads to the so-called ”pocket formula”, where the DPS cross section is written as a product of two SPS cross sections
Bartalini:2011jp
:

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{DPS},ij}=\frac{1}{C}\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{SPS},i}\ \sigma_{\mathrm{SPS},j}}{\sigma_{\mathrm{eff}}}\,,

(10)

where
i
and
j
indicate the final states of the two hard scattering processes.
C
is a combinatoric factor, which is
2
if
i=j
and
1
, otherwise. The effective cross section
\sigma_{\mathrm{eff}}
is defined by
\sigma_{\mathrm{eff}}^{-1}=\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\boldsymbol{y}[G(\boldsymbol{y})]^{2}
. The function
G(\boldsymbol{y})
must be independent of the quark flavor, which leads to the prediction that
\sigma_{\mathrm{eff}}
should be a universal constant. Since we are not able to resolve the
x_{i}
dependence of DPDs in lattice studies, we cannot investigate to what extent factorization approaches w.r.t. the momentum fractions are valid. However, we shall perform the evaluation of DPD Mellin moments for different quark flavor combinations, such that we are able to check the universality of the function
G(\boldsymbol{y})
.

2.2
Skewed double parton distributions

The DPDs defined in (
2.1
) can be extended by introducing an additional phase in the definition. This causes a difference between the momenta of the emitted and absorbed partons, respectively. We call the resulting functions skewed DPDs, which additionally depend on the skewness parameter
\zeta
:

\displaystyle F_{a_{1}a_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2},\zeta,\boldsymbol{y})=2p^{+}\int\mathrm{d}y^{-}e^{-i\zeta y^{-}p^{+}}

\displaystyle\int\frac{\mathrm{d}z^{-}_{1}}{2\pi}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}z^{-}_{2}}{2\pi}\,e^{i\mskip 1.5mu(x_{1}z_{1}^{-}+x_{2}z_{2}^{-})\mskip 1.5mup^{+}}

\displaystyle\quad\times\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\sum}_{\lambda}\bra{p,\lambda}\mathcal{O}_{a_{1}}(y,z_{1})\,\mathcal{O}_{a_{2}}(0,z_{2})\ket{p,\lambda}\,.

(11)

The partons have momentum fractions
x_{i}\pm{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\zeta
. The sign of the fractions determines whether there is a quark (antiquark) in the proton wave function or an antiquark (quark) in its complex conjugate. An overview of the corresponding regions is given in figure
2
. If all fractions are positive, we have two quarks with momentum fractions
x_{1}-{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\zeta
and
x_{2}+{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\zeta
in the proton wave function and two quarks with
x_{1}+{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\zeta
,
x_{2}-{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\zeta
in its complex conjugate. This is sketched in figure
1
for the case of a
u
and a
d
quark.

Figure 1:
Illustration of a skewed DPD of a
u
and a
d
quark for the case where all fractions
x_{i}\pm\zeta/2
are positive.

Because of momentum conservation, the region in the
(x_{1},x_{2},\zeta)
-parameter space where the skewed DPDs are non-zero is restricted by

\displaystyle|x_{i}\pm{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\zeta|\leq 1\,,\qquad|x_{1}|+|x_{2}|\leq 1\,,\qquad|\zeta|\leq 1\,.

(12)

The corresponding support region is also indicated in Figure
2
.

Figure 2:
Support regions of
F_{ud}(x_{i},\zeta,\boldsymbol{y})
w.r.t. the arguments
x_{i}
and
\zeta
. This is shown for positive (left) and negative (right) skewness parameter
\zeta
. For each sub-region we indicate the (anti-)quark content of the wave function and its complex conjugate. The notation
u|\bar{d}du
means that we have a
u
-quark in the proton wave function and
\bar{d}du
in its complex conjugate.

From
PT
invariance it follows that:

\displaystyle F_{a_{1}a_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2},\zeta,\boldsymbol{y})

\displaystyle=\eta_{\textit{\tiny PT}}^{a_{1}}\,\eta_{\textit{\tiny PT}}^{a_{2}}\,F_{a_{1}a_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2},-\zeta,-\boldsymbol{y})\,,

(13)

with

\displaystyle\eta_{\textit{\tiny PT}}^{a}=-1\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \text{for}\penalty\ a=\Delta q,\delta q\ ,\qquad\eta_{\textit{\tiny PT}}^{a}=+1\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \text{for}\penalty\ a=q\,.

(14)

Moreover, one can give a decomposition of the skewed DPDs in terms of functions that are rotationally invariant in the transverse plane.
F_{q_{1}q_{2}}
and
F_{\Delta q_{1}\Delta q_{2}}
have even parity and are scalar quantities, therefore they are already rotationally invariant. By contrast
F_{\Delta q_{1}q_{2}}
and
F_{q_{1}\Delta q_{2}}
are parity-odd, which implies that they have to vanish. From invariance under time reflection, it also follows that the
T
-odd quantities
F^{j_{1}}_{\delta q_{1}\Delta q_{2}}
and
F^{j_{2}}_{\Delta q_{1}\delta q_{2}}
are zero. The remaining DPDs can be decomposed in terms of transverse vectors as follows:

\displaystyle F_{q_{1}q_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2},\zeta,\boldsymbol{y})

\displaystyle=f_{q_{1}q_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2},\zeta,y^{2})\,,

\displaystyle F_{\Delta q_{1}\Delta q_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2},\zeta,\boldsymbol{y})

\displaystyle=f_{\Delta q_{1}\Delta q_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2},\zeta,y^{2})\,,

\displaystyle F_{\delta q_{1}q_{2}}^{j_{1}}(x_{1},x_{2},\zeta,\boldsymbol{y})

\displaystyle=\epsilon^{j_{1}k}\boldsymbol{y}^{k}\,mf_{\delta q_{1}q_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2},\zeta,y^{2})\,,

\displaystyle F_{q_{1}\delta q_{2}}^{j_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2},\zeta,\boldsymbol{y})

\displaystyle=\epsilon^{j_{2}k}\boldsymbol{y}^{k}\,mf_{q_{1}\delta q_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2},\zeta,y^{2})\,,

\displaystyle F_{\delta q_{1}\delta q_{2}}^{j_{1}j_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2},\zeta,\boldsymbol{y})

\displaystyle=\delta^{j_{1}j_{2}}f_{\delta q_{1}\delta q_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2},\zeta,y^{2})

\displaystyle\quad+\bigl(2\boldsymbol{y}^{j_{1}}\boldsymbol{y}^{j_{2}}-\delta^{j_{1}j_{2}}\boldsymbol{y}^{2}\bigr)\mskip 1.5mum^{2}f^{\mskip 1.5mut}_{\delta q_{1}\delta q_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2},\zeta,y^{2})\,,

(15)

where
m
is the proton mass and
\epsilon^{ij}
is the antisymmetric tensor in two dimensions, with
\epsilon^{12}=1
. Notice that
y^{2}=y^{\mu}y_{\mu}
denotes the Lorentz invariant scalar product. In our case we have
y^{+}=0
, i.e.
y^{2}=-\boldsymbol{y}^{2}
.
For
\zeta=0
the functions on the r.h.s. of (
2.2
) have the following physical interpretation:

•

f_{q_{1}q_{2}}
describes the probability of finding two quarks with momentum fractions
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
at a transverse distance
\boldsymbol{y}
. It contains a sum over all quark polarization states, i.e. the quarks are unpolarized.

•

f_{\Delta q_{1}\Delta q_{2}}
describes the difference between the probabilities of finding the two quarks with aligned or anti-aligned spins in the longitudinal direction. This gives a measure for the longitudinal quark polarization.

•

f_{\delta q_{1}\delta q_{2}}
is the analogue of
f_{\Delta q_{1}\Delta q_{2}}
for polarization in the transverse direction.

•

f_{\delta q_{1}q_{2}}
describes the correlation between the transverse polarization of the first quark and the transverse distance
\boldsymbol{y}
between the two quarks.
f_{q_{1}\delta q_{2}}
can be interpreted in analogy, where the second quark is polarized and the first unpolarized.

•

f_{\delta q_{1}\delta q_{2}}^{t}
gives the correlation between the transverse distance
\boldsymbol{y}
of the quarks and their transverse polarizations.

The functions
f_{\delta q_{1}q_{2}}
,
f_{q_{1}\delta q_{2}}
, and
f_{\delta q_{1}\delta q_{2}}^{t}
describe spin-orbit correlations, whereas
f_{\delta q_{1}\delta q_{2}}
and
f_{\Delta q_{1}\Delta q_{2}}
quantify spin-spin correlations. Combining (
2.2
) and (
13
) we find:

\displaystyle f_{a_{1}a_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2},\zeta,y^{2})

\displaystyle=f_{a_{1}a_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2},-\zeta,y^{2})\,.

(16)

The matrix elements in the definitions (
2.1
) and (
2.2
) are not directly accessible on a Euclidean lattice, since they involve light-like distances. A way to circumvent this obstacle is to consider Mellin moments of skewed DPDs. The lowest Mellin moment is defined as:

\displaystyle I_{a_{1}a_{2}}(\zeta,y^{2})

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}x_{1}\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}x_{2}\;f_{a_{1}a_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2},\zeta,y^{2})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}x_{1}\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}x_{2}\left[f_{a_{1}a_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2},\zeta,y^{2})+\eta_{\textit{\tiny C}}^{a_{1}}f_{\bar{a}_{1}a_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2},\zeta,y^{2})\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\left.+\eta_{\textit{\tiny C}}^{a_{2}}f_{a_{1}\bar{a}_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2},\zeta,y^{2})+\eta_{\textit{\tiny C}}^{a_{1}}\eta_{\textit{\tiny C}}^{a_{2}}f_{\bar{a}_{1}\bar{a}_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2},\zeta,y^{2})\right]\,.

(17)

The integrals over
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
in (
2.2
) together with the exponentials
e^{ix_{i}p^{+}z_{i}^{-}}
in (
2.1
) and (
2.2
) set
z_{i}^{-}
to zero, which is what we intended. The resulting matrix elements involve only local quark bilinears, which can be evaluated in lattice simulations.

2.3
Two-current matrix elements

We define two-current matrix elements of the proton with momentum
p
as:

\displaystyle M^{\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{2}\cdots}_{q_{1}q_{2},i_{1}i_{2}}(p,y)

\displaystyle:=\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\sum}_{\lambda}\bra{p,\lambda}J^{\mu_{1}\cdots}_{q_{1},i_{1}}(y)\,J^{\mu_{2}\cdots}_{q_{2},i_{2}}(0)\ket{p,\lambda}\,,

(18)

where we take the average of the proton spin. The currents
J^{\mu\dots}_{q,i}
are local quark bilinear operators. In this work we focus on three types of currents, which are defined as:

\displaystyle J_{q,V}^{\mu}(y)

\displaystyle=\bar{q}(y)\mskip 1.5mu\gamma^{\mu}\mskip 1.5muq(y)\,,

\displaystyle J_{q,A}^{\mu}(y)

\displaystyle=\bar{q}(y)\mskip 1.5mu\gamma^{\mu}\gamma_{5}\,q(y)\,,

\displaystyle J_{q,T}^{\mu\nu}(y)

\displaystyle=\bar{q}(y)\mskip 1.5mu\sigma^{\mu\nu}\mskip 1.5muq(y)\,.

(19)

These operators commute if the distance vector
y
is space-like, in particular for
y^{0}=0
. A consequence of this property is the relation:

\displaystyle M^{\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{2}\cdots}_{q_{1}q_{2},i_{1}i_{2}}(p,y)

\displaystyle=M^{\mu_{2}\cdots\mu_{1}\cdots}_{q_{2}q_{1},i_{2}i_{1}}(p,-y)\,.

(20)

Moreover, the currents have definite transformation behavior under charge conjugation and the combination of parity and time reflection:

\displaystyle J_{q,i}^{\mu\cdots}(y)

\displaystyle\underset{C}{\to}\eta_{\textit{\tiny C}}^{i}\,J_{q,i}^{\mu\cdots}(y)\,,

\displaystyle J_{q,i}^{\mu\cdots}(y)

\displaystyle\underset{PT}{\to}\eta_{\textit{\tiny PT}}^{i}\,J_{q,i}^{\mu\cdots}(-y)\,,

(21)

where in analogy to (
5
) and (
14
) the sign factors
\eta_{\textit{\tiny C}}
and
\eta_{\textit{\tiny PT}}
are defined as:

\displaystyle\eta_{\textit{\tiny C}}^{i}

\displaystyle=-1\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \text{for}\penalty\ i=V,T\,,

\displaystyle\eta_{\textit{\tiny C}}^{i}

\displaystyle=+1\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \text{for}\penalty\ i=A

(22)

and

\displaystyle\eta_{\textit{\tiny PT}}^{i}

\displaystyle=-1\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \text{for}\penalty\ i=A,T\,,

\displaystyle\eta_{\textit{\tiny PT}}^{i}

\displaystyle=+1\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \text{for}\penalty\ i=V\,.

(23)

The combined
PT
symmetry implies for the two-current matrix elements:

\displaystyle M^{\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{2}\cdots}_{q_{1}q_{2},i_{1}i_{2}}(p,y)

\displaystyle=\eta_{\textit{\tiny PT}}^{i_{1}}\,\eta_{\textit{\tiny PT}}^{i_{2}}\,M^{\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{2}\cdots}_{q_{1}q_{2},i_{1}i_{2}}(p,-y)\,.

(24)

In the context of DPDs we have to consider the current combinations
VV
,
AA
,
VT
,
TV
, and
TT
. The corresponding two-current matrix elements are by definition Lorentz tensors of a certain rank, which is determined by the involved currents. Therefore, the matrix elements can be decomposed in terms of Lorentz invariant functions and Lorentz tensors constructed from the four-vectors
p
and
y
. In order to reduce the number of independent quantities, we subtract trace contributions and consider symmetric combinations. For brevity we skip the arguments
y
and
p
of the matrix elements

Here we write

\displaystyle u_{VV,A}^{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=2p^{\mu}p^{\nu}-{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\mskip 1.5mug^{\mu\nu}p^{2}\,,

\displaystyle u_{VV,B}^{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=2p^{\{\mu}y^{\nu\}}-{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\mskip 1.5mug^{\mu\nu}py\,,

\displaystyle u_{VV,C}^{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=2y^{\mu}y^{\nu}-{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\mskip 1.5mug^{\mu\nu}y^{2}\,,

\displaystyle u_{TV,A}^{\mu\nu\rho}

\displaystyle=4y^{[\mu}p^{\nu]}p^{\rho}+\tfrac{4}{3}\mskip 1.5mug^{\rho[\mu}y^{\nu]}p^{2}-\tfrac{4}{3}\mskip 1.5mug^{\rho[\mu}p^{\nu]}py\,,

\displaystyle u_{TV,B}^{\mu\nu\rho}

\displaystyle=4y^{[\mu}p^{\nu]}y^{\rho}+\tfrac{4}{3}\mskip 1.5mug^{\rho[\mu}y^{\nu]}py-\tfrac{4}{3}\mskip 1.5mug^{\rho[\mu}p^{\nu]}y^{2}\,,

\displaystyle\tilde{u}_{TT,A}^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}

\displaystyle\tilde{u}_{TT,B}^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}

\displaystyle=-y^{2}\,u_{TT,A}^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}-16\mskip 1.5muy^{[\mu}p^{\nu]}y^{[\rho}p^{\sigma]}\,,

\displaystyle\tilde{u}_{TT,C}^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}

\displaystyle=-4p^{[\nu}g^{\mu][\rho}y^{\sigma]}-4y^{[\nu}g^{\mu][\rho}p^{\sigma]}\,,

\displaystyle\tilde{u}_{TT,D}^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}

\displaystyle=-8y^{[\nu}g^{\mu][\rho}y^{\sigma]}\,,

\displaystyle u_{TT,E}^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}

\displaystyle=2g^{\mu[\rho}g^{\sigma]\nu}\,.

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}y^{-}\,e^{-i\zeta y^{-}p^{+}}M_{q_{1}q_{2},VV}^{++}(p,y)\,\Big|_{y^{+}=0,\,\boldsymbol{p}=\boldsymbol{0}}

\displaystyle=2p^{+}\mskip 1.5muI_{q_{1}q_{2}}(\zeta,y^{2})\,,

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}y^{-}\mskip 1.5mue^{-i\zeta y^{-}p^{+}}M_{q_{1}q_{2},AA}^{++}(p,y)\,\Big|_{y^{+}=0,\,\boldsymbol{p}=\boldsymbol{0}}

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}y^{-}\mskip 1.5mue^{-i\zeta y^{-}p^{+}}M_{q_{1}q_{2},TV}^{k_{1}++}(p,y)\,\Big|_{y^{+}=0,\,\boldsymbol{p}=\boldsymbol{0}}

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!=2p^{+}\mskip 1.5mu\bigl[\mskip 1.5mu\delta^{k_{1}k_{2}}\,I_{\delta q_{1}\delta q_{2}}(\zeta,y^{2})-\bigl(2\boldsymbol{y}^{k_{1}}\boldsymbol{y}^{k_{2}}-\delta^{k_{1}k_{2}}\boldsymbol{y}^{2}\bigr)m^{2}\mskip 1.5muI^{t}_{\delta q_{1}\delta q_{2}}(\zeta,y^{2})\bigr]\,.

\Gamma_{\delta q}^{j}
by an extra

Comparing (

\displaystyle I_{a_{1}a_{2}}(\zeta,y^{2})

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}(py)\,e^{-i\zeta py}\,A_{a_{1}a_{2}}(py,y^{2})\,,

\displaystyle I^{t}_{\delta q\delta q^{\prime}}(\zeta,y^{2})

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}(py)\,e^{-i\zeta py}\,B_{\delta q\delta q^{\prime}}(py,y^{2})\,,

(28)

i.e. the Mellin moments are Fourier transforms of the invariant functions

\displaystyle A_{a_{1}a_{2}}(py=0,y^{2})=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}\zeta\,I_{a_{1}a_{2}}(\zeta,y^{2})\,.

(29)

We define even

\displaystyle\langle\zeta^{2m}\rangle_{a_{1}a_{2}}(y^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}\zeta\;\zeta^{2m}\,I_{a_{1}a_{2}}(\zeta,y^{2})}{\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}\zeta\,I_{a_{1}a_{2}}(\zeta,y^{2})}=\Biggl[\mskip 1.5mu\frac{(-1)^{m}}{A_{a_{1}a_{2}}(py,y^{2})}\frac{\partial^{2m}A_{a_{1}a_{2}}(py,y^{2})}{(\partial\mskip 1.5mupy)^{2m}}\mskip 1.5mu\Biggr]_{py=0}\,,

3
Two-current matrix elements on the lattice

In order to perform lattice simulations, we switch to Euclidean spacetime in this section. The corresponding time component of a four vector
x^{\mu}
is denoted by
x^{4}
instead of
x^{0}
. In Euclidean spacetime, the matrix elements given in (
18
) can be directly calculated in lattice QCD if the distance between the two insertion operators is purely spatial, i.e.
y^{4}=y^{0}=0
. In this section we describe the relation between the matrix elements and the lattice four-point functions defined below for the case of the nucleon and explain the techniques we use for the evaluation of the latter.

3.1
Four-point functions and matrix elements

Definition:

We define the proton four-point function
C^{ij,\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}_{\mathrm{4pt}}
as the correlator of a proton creation operator
\overline{\mathcal{P}}
(source), the corresponding annihilation operator
\mathcal{P}
(sink), and the two local currents
J_{i}
defined in (
19
):

\displaystyle C^{ij,\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}_{\mathrm{4pt}}(\vec{\mskip 0.5muy}\mskip 1.5mu,t,\tau)

\displaystyle:=a^{6}\sum_{\vec{\mskip 0.5muz}\mskip 1.5mu^{\prime},\vec{\mskip 0.5muz}\mskip 1.5mu}e^{-i\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu(\vec{\mskip 0.5muz}\mskip 1.5mu^{\prime}-\vec{\mskip 0.5muz}\mskip 1.5mu)}\ \left\langle\operatorname{tr}\left\{P_{+}\mathcal{P}(\vec{\mskip 0.5muz}\mskip 1.5mu^{\prime},t)\ J_{i}(\vec{\mskip 0.5muy}\mskip 1.5mu,\tau)\ J_{j}(\vec{\mskip 0.5mu0}\mskip 1.5mu,\tau)\ \overline{\mathcal{P}}(\vec{\mskip 0.5muz}\mskip 1.5mu,0)\right\}\right\rangle\,,

(31)

where the sum over
\vec{\mskip 0.5muz}\mskip 1.5mu
,
\vec{\mskip 0.5muz}\mskip 1.5mu^{\prime}
combined with the exponential injects a total proton momentum, and the operator

\displaystyle P_{+}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbb{1}+\gamma_{4}\right)\,

(32)

projects onto positive parity. The proton creation and annihilation operators, which we also refer to as interpolators, are given by tri-quark operators matching the proton’s spin
J=1/2
and isospin
I=1/2
:

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{P}}(\vec{\mskip 0.5mux}\mskip 1.5mu,t)

\displaystyle:=\left.\epsilon_{abc}\ \left[\bar{u}_{a}(x)\ C\gamma_{5}\ \bar{d}_{b}^{\,T}(x)\right]\bar{u}_{c}(x)\right|_{x^{4}=t}\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\vec{\mskip 0.5mux}\mskip 1.5mu,t)

\displaystyle:=\left.\epsilon_{abc}\ u_{a}(x)\left[u_{b}^{T}(x)\ C\gamma_{5}\ d_{c}(x)\right]\right|_{x^{4}=t}\,,

(33)

where
C
is the charge conjugation matrix in spinor space, and
[.]
indicates a scalar quantity w.r.t. spinor indices. The traces in (
31
) are taken w.r.t. the open spinor indices introduced by the quark fields
u_{a}
and
\bar{u}_{c}
, respectively. Furthermore, we define the two-point function:

\displaystyle C^{\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}_{\mathrm{2pt}}(t)

\displaystyle:=a^{6}\sum_{\vec{\mskip 0.5muz}\mskip 1.5mu^{\prime},\vec{\mskip 0.5muz}\mskip 1.5mu}e^{-i\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu(\vec{\mskip 0.5muz}\mskip 1.5mu^{\prime}-\vec{\mskip 0.5muz}\mskip 1.5mu)}\ \left\langle\operatorname{tr}\left\{P_{+}\mathcal{P}(\vec{\mskip 0.5muz}\mskip 1.5mu^{\prime},t)\ \overline{\mathcal{P}}(\vec{\mskip 0.5muz}\mskip 1.5mu,0)\right\}\right\rangle\,.

(34)

We denote the separation in Euclidean time direction between the source and the current insertions by
\tau
, and the separation between the source and the sink by
t
.

Wick contractions:

The evaluation of the correlation functions (
31
) and (
34
) leads to a definite set of Wick contractions. While there are only two contractions arising from permutations of
u\bar{u}
-pairs in the two-point function, there is a multitude of possible contractions in the case of the four-point functions, which can be grouped into five types. Following the notation of
Bali:2018nde
we call them
C_{1}
,
C_{2}
,
S_{1}
,
S_{2}
and
D
. They can be represented by the graphs illustrated in figure
3
.
S_{1}
,
S_{2}
and
D
are disconnected contractions involving the sub-graphs
G_{3\mathrm{pt}}
and
G_{2\mathrm{pt}}
, as well as the loops
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
. Explicit expressions are given in appendix
A.2
. The explicit contribution of a given type depends on the flavor content of the inserted operators
J_{qq^{\prime},i}
, which in general can be flavor changing. In the case of the graph
C_{1}
this is indicated by the four flavors
q_{1}\dots q_{4}
of the quark lines connected to the current insertions, where the first two indices correspond to the flavor of the first operator
J_{q_{1}q_{2},i}
and the last two flavor indices are those of the second operator
J_{q_{3}q_{4},j}
. For the proton there are three independent contributions called
C_{1,uuuu}
,
C_{1,uudd}
and
C_{1,uddu}
, where the latter is not considered in this work, since we restrict ourselves to flavor conserving currents
J_{qq,i}=J_{q,i}
, see definition (
19
). If all considered quarks have the same mass, the graphs
C_{2,q}
and
S_{1,q}
depend only on the flavor
q
of the two propagators connecting the source or sink with one of the current insertions. Therefore, in the case of proton-proton matrix elements there are two possibilities for each contraction:
C_{2,u}
,
C_{2,d}
,
S_{1,u}
,
S_{1,d}
. For each of the contractions
S_{2}
and
D
there is only one contribution, which is flavor independent. Notice that we define the quantities
C_{1,uuuu}
,
C_{1,uudd}
,
C_{1,uddu}
,
C_{2,u}
,
C_{2,d}
,
S_{1,u}
,
S_{1,d}
,
S_{2}
, and

as a sum of all quark permutations that share the same quark line topology. In particular, this includes permutations of the two
u

128

130

131

3.2
Technical details on Wick contractions

In the following, we discuss the technical details regarding the evaluation of each Wick contraction we have previously defined. A technical sketch of all graphs is shown in figure
4
. Each contraction is calculated on a smeared quark point source
S_{z}^{\Phi,\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}=\Phi^{\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}S_{z}
. It is a diagonal spinor-color matrix located at position
z
, i.e.
(S_{z})_{\alpha\beta}^{ab}(y)=\delta_{zy}\delta_{\alpha\beta}\delta_{ab}
, where
z^{4}
is the nucleon source timeslice. Notice that here and in the following spinor indices are denoted by Greek letters
\alpha,\beta,\dots
, whereas Latin letters
a,b,\dots
denote color indices of the fundamental representation. More details and explanations on the notation can be found in appendix
A.1

Sketch of all Wick contractions including the techniques used for the evaluation of each piece on the lattice. We use one color for each involved quark source, i.e. if two or more pieces within a graph share the same color, they involve the same quark source. Smearing:

As already mentioned above, we apply a smearing function
\Phi^{\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}
to the corresponding sources and propagators, in order to increase the overlap of the proton interpolators with the proton ground state.
\Phi^{\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}
includes a phase injecting a momentum
b\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu
to each of the quarks, where
\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu
denotes the proton momentum. The method is known as momentum smearing
Bali:2016lva
, which is based on the Wuppertal smearing technique
Gusken:1989ad
. Explicitly, the smearing function
\Phi^{\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}
reads:

\displaystyle(\Phi_{0}^{\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu})(x|y)=\frac{1}{1+6\epsilon}\left[\delta_{x,y}+\epsilon\sum_{j=1}^{3}\left(\delta_{x+\hat{\jmath},y}U^{\mathrm{sm}}_{j}(x)\ e^{ib\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu\hat{\jmath}}+\delta_{y+\hat{\jmath},x}U^{\mathrm{sm},\dagger}_{j}(y)\ e^{-ib\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu\hat{\jmath}}\right)\right]\ ,

(49)

where we set
\epsilon=0.25
and
b=0.45
, in order to obtain a maximal overlap with the ground state. The value of the latter parameter is specific to our setup. The smearing function is applied
n
times, which is denoted by
\Phi=\Phi_{0}^{n}
. The gauge links
U^{\mathrm{sm}}
appearing in (
49
) are obtained from the original gauge links by applying spatial APE-smearing
Falcioni:1984ei
, which reduces unphysical short-distance fluctuations.
M^{\Phi,\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}_{z}(y)
denotes the source-smeared point-to-all quark propagator, which is obtained by solving:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}M^{\Phi,\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}_{z}=S_{z}^{\Phi,\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}:=\Phi^{\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}S_{z}\,,

(50)

where
\mathcal{D}
is the Dirac operator. This propagator is used for the construction of each of the contractions relevant for the four-point function
C_{4\mathrm{pt}}
.

Stochastic propagators and improvements:

The all-to-all propagators required for the evaluation of most of the four-point graphs are estimated by use of time-local stochastic sources
\eta_{t}^{(\ell)}
. In this context the spatial unit matrix is approximated in the following way:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{st}}}\sum_{\ell}^{N_{\mathrm{st}}}\eta_{t}^{(\ell)}\otimes\eta_{t}^{\dagger(\ell)}\xrightarrow{N_{\mathrm{st}}\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{1}_{t}\,.

(51)

In the present study we employ
Z_{2}\otimes Z_{2}
wall sources defined for a specific timeslice
t
, i.e. the entries can take the values

\displaystyle\left(\eta_{t}^{(\ell)}\right)_{\alpha a}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\pm 1\pm i)\ \delta_{x^{4}t}\,.

(52)

The propagated stochastic source
\psi_{t}^{(\ell)}
, which we call ”stochastic propagator” in the remainder of this work, is obtained by solving:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\psi_{t}^{(\ell)}

\displaystyle=\eta_{t}^{(\ell)}\,.

(53)

It describes the propagation from any spatial position on timeslice
t
to any other site on the lattice.

The off-diagonal components in (
51
) are pure noise. This noise is particularly large for near-diagonal terms, where the propagator takes large values. Quantities involving these terms can be improved by a method that has also been used in
Bali:2009hu
, where one exploits ultra-locality of the action. The method consists of applying the hopping parameter expansion (HPE), where the Dirac operator is rewritten as
\mathcal{D}=(\mathbb{1}-H)/(2a\kappa)
. Subsequently, the corresponding propagator can be expanded in terms of powers
H^{n}
using the geometric series. Depending on the situation there exists a maximal order
N
in the series, up to which the corresponding terms vanish exactly in the stochastic limit or, equivalently, for the exact solution of the propagator. This enables us to rewrite the propagator as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{-1}

\displaystyle=2a\kappa\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}H^{n}=2a\kappa\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}H^{n}+2a\kappa\sum_{n=N}^{\infty}H^{n}

\displaystyle\to 0+2a\kappa\sum_{n=N}^{\infty}H^{n}=H^{N}2a\kappa\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}H^{n}=H^{N}\mathcal{D}^{-1}\,.

(54)

The replacement
\mathcal{D}^{-1}\rightarrow H^{N}\mathcal{D}^{-1}
removes the first
N
terms in the expansion.

In the context of our calculations this method is used in two different places. The first one is a propagator connecting two sites on the same timeslice, which is needed for the calculation of the
C_{2}
graph. Since the hopping term
H
connects only nearest neighbors, we set
aN(\vec{\mskip 0.5muy}\mskip 1.5mu)=|y_{1}|+|y_{2}|+|y_{3}|
. Taking into account the periodicity of the lattice, the exact definition of
N(\vec{\mskip 0.5muy}\mskip 1.5mu)
is:

\displaystyle N(\vec{\mskip 0.5muy}\mskip 1.5mu)=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\min\left(\frac{|y_{i}|}{a},L-\frac{|y_{i}|}{a}\right)\,.

(55)

In the expression of the graph to be evaluated, we then have to replace
\psi_{\tau}^{(\ell)}
by

\displaystyle\xi_{\tau}^{(\ell),N}=H^{N}\psi_{\tau}^{(\ell)}\,.

(56)

If the propagator is contracted with a Dirac matrix, e.g. in loops containing only one current, there is also a certain number of terms in the hopping parameter expansion that cancel. The number of terms that vanish depends on the Dirac matrix, see table

.

Interpolator kernels:

Before we continue to define the expressions to be evaluated, we introduce the compact notation:

\displaystyle\left(E^{a}\right)^{bc}_{\beta\gamma}

\displaystyle:=\epsilon^{abc}\left(C\gamma_{5}\right)_{\beta\gamma}\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}(x)

\displaystyle:=e^{-i\vec{\mskip 0.5mux}\mskip 1.5mu\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}\,.

(57)

We then define the annihilation operator kernel:

\displaystyle{}_{\sigma}\mathcal{O}^{abc}_{\alpha\beta\gamma}=\left(P_{+}\right)_{\sigma\alpha}\left(E^{a}\right)^{bc}_{\beta\gamma}\,.

(58)

Contracting with the quark field operators, this yields the baryon annihilation operator (
33
) itself. In analogy, the baryon creation operator kernel is defined as:

\displaystyle{}_{\sigma}\overline{\mathcal{O}}^{abc}_{\alpha\beta\gamma}=\left(E^{c}\right)^{ab}_{\alpha\beta}\left(P_{+}\right)_{\gamma\sigma}\,.

(59)

In both cases, the index
\sigma
corresponds to the open fermion index, which is consistent with the fermionic nature of baryons.
P_{+}
again denotes the parity projection operator (
32
).

The two point function

The proton two-point function involves two Wick contractions arising from permutations of the two

\displaystyle G^{\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}_{2\mathrm{pt}}(z,z^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{E}^{\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}(z^{\prime}-z)\left[\vphantom{\left[\left(M_{z}^{\Phi,\vec{\mskip 0.5muz}\mskip 1.5mu}\right)^{T}\right]_{\gamma\beta}^{cc}}\left(P_{+}\Phi^{\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}M_{z}^{\Phi,\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}(z^{\prime})\right)_{\alpha\alpha}^{ab}\ \operatorname{tr}\left\{\left(\Phi^{\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}M_{z}^{\Phi,\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}(z^{\prime})E^{b}\right)^{T}E^{a}\Phi^{\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}M_{z}^{\Phi,\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}(z^{\prime})\right\}\right.

\displaystyle\quad+\left.\left(P_{+}\Phi^{\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}M_{z}^{\Phi,\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}(z^{\prime})\right)_{\beta\gamma}^{ab}\left[\left(\Phi^{\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}M_{z}^{\Phi,\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}(z^{\prime})E^{b}\right)^{T}E^{a}\Phi^{\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}M_{z}^{\Phi,\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}(z^{\prime})\right]^{cc}_{\gamma\beta}\right]\,,

where

\displaystyle C^{\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}_{2\mathrm{pt}}(t)=\left\langle\tilde{G}^{\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}_{2\mathrm{pt}}(t)\right\rangle\,,\qquad\widetilde{G}^{\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}_{2\mathrm{pt}}(t)=a^{3}V\sum_{\vec{\mskip 0.5muz}\mskip 1.5mu^{\prime}}\left.G^{\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}_{2\mathrm{pt}}(z,z^{\prime})\right|_{(z^{\prime})^{4}=z^{4}+t}\,,

(61)

where

We implement two methods to calculate the loop

graphs are both constructed from a sequential source. For this we use the same source as for usual three-point functions, see (
A.3
). The corresponding sequential propagator
X_{t}
is obtained by inverting:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}X^{\Phi,\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}_{t,3\mathrm{pt}}=\Phi^{\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}\gamma_{5}S^{\dagger,\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}_{t,3\mathrm{pt}}\,,

(71)

where again

\displaystyle S^{\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}_{t,3\mathrm{pt}}(z^{\prime})=\mathcal{E}^{\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}(z^{\prime})\ S^{\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}_{3\mathrm{pt}}(z^{\prime})\ \delta_{z^{\prime 4}t}\,.

(72)

In the case of the
C_{2}
contraction, the sequential propagator is connected to one current insertion. The other current insertion is contracted with the forward propagator. Both current insertions are connected by a stochastic propagator, which is improved by the HPE trick we have discussed earlier. Explicitly, we find for the
C_{2}
graph:

\displaystyle C_{2}^{ij,\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}(\vec{\mskip 0.5muy}\mskip 1.5mu,t,\tau)

\displaystyle=\frac{a^{3}}{N_{\mathrm{st}}}\mathcal{E}^{-\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}(z)\sum_{\ell}^{N_{\mathrm{st}}}\sum_{\vec{\mskip 0.5mux}\mskip 1.5mu}\left\langle\left[X^{\dagger,\Phi,\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}_{t,3\mathrm{pt}}(\vec{\mskip 0.5mux}\mskip 1.5mu+\vec{\mskip 0.5muy}\mskip 1.5mu,\tau)\ \gamma_{5}\Gamma_{i}\ \xi_{\tau}^{(\ell),n(\vec{\mskip 0.5muy}\mskip 1.5mu)}(\vec{\mskip 0.5mux}\mskip 1.5mu+\vec{\mskip 0.5muy}\mskip 1.5mu,\tau)\right]\right.

\displaystyle\times\left.\left.\left[\eta^{\dagger(\ell)}(\vec{\mskip 0.5mux}\mskip 1.5mu,\tau)\ \Gamma_{j}\ M_{z}^{\Phi,\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}(\vec{\mskip 0.5mux}\mskip 1.5mu,\tau)\right]\right\rangle\right|_{z^{4}=0}\,.

(73)

The
S_{1}
graph consists of two disconnected pieces. The first one has the same structure as a usual three-point function calculated from a sequential source:

\displaystyle G^{i,\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}_{3\mathrm{pt}}(\vec{\mskip 0.5mux}\mskip 1.5mu,\tau,t)=\left.\mathcal{E}^{-\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}(z)\left[X^{\dagger,\Phi,\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}_{t,3\mathrm{pt}}(\vec{\mskip 0.5mux}\mskip 1.5mu,\tau)\ \gamma_{5}\Gamma_{i}\ M_{z}^{\Phi,\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}(\vec{\mskip 0.5mux}\mskip 1.5mu,\tau)\right]\right|_{z^{4}=0}\,.

(74)

The second part is given by the previously defined loop
L_{1}
. If the quantum numbers permit, there are disconnected contributions from the vacuum expectation values of
G_{3\mathrm{pt}}
and
L_{1}
. These must be subtracted, in order to obtain the
S_{1}
contribution we wish to calculate:

\displaystyle S_{1}^{ij,\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}(\vec{\mskip 0.5muy}\mskip 1.5mu,t,\tau)

\displaystyle=-a^{3}\sum_{\vec{\mskip 0.5mux}\mskip 1.5mu}\left\langle G^{i,\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}_{3\mathrm{pt}}(\vec{\mskip 0.5mux}\mskip 1.5mu+\vec{\mskip 0.5muy}\mskip 1.5mu,\tau,t)\ L_{1}^{j}(\vec{\mskip 0.5mux}\mskip 1.5mu,\tau)\right\rangle

\displaystyle+a^{3}\sum_{\vec{\mskip 0.5mux}\mskip 1.5mu}\left\langle G^{i,\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}_{3\mathrm{pt}}(\vec{\mskip 0.5mux}\mskip 1.5mu,\tau,t)\right\rangle\left\langle\left\langle L_{1}^{j}(\tau)\right\rangle\right\rangle\,.

(75)

Notice that the global sign corresponds to the permutation sign of the Wick contraction.

Graphs
S_{2}
and
D
:

The graph
S_{2}
consists of a two-point contraction and the loop
L_{2}
, whereas
D
consists of a two-point contraction and two
L_{1}
loops. As for the
S_{1}
graph, we have to consider vacuum contributions of the disconnected parts, which have to be subtracted. Notice that we defined the loop
L_{2}
at a fixed spatial position. Hence, we are not able to perform a volume average like in the previous cases:

\displaystyle S_{2}^{ij,\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}(\vec{\mskip 0.5muy}\mskip 1.5mu,t,\tau)=-\left\langle\widetilde{G}_{2\mathrm{pt}}^{\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}(t)\ L_{2}^{ij}(\vec{\mskip 0.5muy}\mskip 1.5mu,\tau)\right\rangle+\left\langle\widetilde{G}_{2\mathrm{pt}}^{\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}(t)\right\rangle\left\langle L_{2}^{ij}(\vec{\mskip 0.5muy}\mskip 1.5mu,\tau)\right\rangle\,.

(76)

We use two methods to evaluate the
D
graph: The first employs two stochastic loops
L_{1,\mathrm{st}}
, which allows us to perform a volume average. In the second method, we replace one stochastic loop by a loop attached to a point source
L_{1,\mathrm{pt}}
. This might reduce the stochastic noise but precludes the possibility to perform a volume average. For the doubly stochastic case, the
D
graph reads:

\displaystyle D^{ij,\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}(\vec{\mskip 0.5muy}\mskip 1.5mu,t,\tau)

\displaystyle=a^{3}\sum_{\vec{\mskip 0.5mux}\mskip 1.5mu}\left\{\left\langle\widetilde{G}_{2\mathrm{pt}}^{\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}(t)\ L_{1,\mathrm{st}}^{i}(\vec{\mskip 0.5mux}\mskip 1.5mu+\vec{\mskip 0.5muy}\mskip 1.5mu,\tau)\ L_{1,\mathrm{st}}^{j}(\vec{\mskip 0.5mux}\mskip 1.5mu,\tau)\right\rangle\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\left\langle\widetilde{G}_{2\mathrm{pt}}^{\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}(t)\right\rangle\left\langle L_{1,\mathrm{st}}^{i}(\vec{\mskip 0.5mux}\mskip 1.5mu+\vec{\mskip 0.5muy}\mskip 1.5mu,\tau)\ L_{1,\mathrm{st}}^{j}(\vec{\mskip 0.5mux}\mskip 1.5mu,\tau)\right\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\left\langle\widetilde{G}_{2\mathrm{pt}}^{\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}(t)\ L_{1,\mathrm{st}}^{i}(\vec{\mskip 0.5mux}\mskip 1.5mu,\tau)\right\rangle\left\langle\left\langle L_{1,\mathrm{st}}^{j}(\tau)\right\rangle\right\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\left.\left\langle\widetilde{G}_{2\mathrm{pt}}^{\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}(t)\ L_{1,\mathrm{st}}^{j}(\vec{\mskip 0.5mux}\mskip 1.5mu,\tau)\right\rangle\left\langle\left\langle\vphantom{L_{1,\mathrm{st}}^{j}}L_{1,\mathrm{st}}^{i}(\tau)\right\rangle\right\rangle\right\}

\displaystyle\quad+2\left\langle\widetilde{G}_{2\mathrm{pt}}^{\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu}(t)\right\rangle\left\langle\left\langle\vphantom{L_{1,\mathrm{st}}^{j}}L_{1,\mathrm{st}}^{i}(\tau)\right\rangle\right\rangle\left\langle\left\langle L_{1,\mathrm{st}}^{j}(\tau)\right\rangle\right\rangle\,.

(77)

Note that we use two different sets of stochastic sources for the two disconnected loops. Equation (
77
) is valid for the first method. For the second method
L_{1,\mathrm{st}}
has to be replaced by
L_{1,\mathrm{pt}}
. Furthermore, one has to replace the sum
a^{3}\sum_{\vec{\mskip 0.5mux}\mskip 1.5mu}
by a volume factor
V

4.4
Parameterization of the
py
dependence

A parameterization of the twist-two functions is in particular mandatory for the evaluation of the
py
-integral in (
2.3
). The reason is that one has to extrapolate in
py
, since the accessible range is restricted by the largest proton momentum:

\displaystyle|py|\underset{y^{0}=0}{\leq}|\vec{\mskip 0.5mup}\mskip 1.5mu||\vec{\mskip 0.5muy}\mskip 1.5mu|\leq\frac{2\pi\ \sqrt{12}\ y}{La}\leq 6\pi\approx 18.85\,.

(82)

In order to make an ansatz for the
py
-dependence, we consider the constraints on the
\zeta
-dependence of the skewed DPDs. These are the symmetry relation (
16
) and the constraints (
12
) restricting the support region in
\zeta
. Furthermore, we assume that the Mellin moment
I(\zeta,y^{2})
can be Taylor expanded around
\zeta=0
. Combining everything, we make the ansatz that the Mellin moment
I(\zeta,y^{2})
can be approximated by an even polynomial in
\zeta
within the region
|\zeta|\leq 1
:

\displaystyle I(\zeta,y^{2})=\pi\sum_{n=0}^{N}a_{n}(y^{2})\ \zeta^{2n}\ \Theta(1-\zeta^{2})\,.

(83)

This implies for the twist-two functions, which are related to the Mellin moments by a Fourier transform:

\displaystyle A(py,y^{2})=\sum_{n=0}^{N}a_{n}(y^{2})\ h_{n}(py)\,,

(84)

where the functions
h_{n}
are defined as:

\displaystyle h_{n}(x):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}\zeta\ e^{ix\zeta}\ \zeta^{2n}=\sin(x)\ s_{n}(x)+\cos(x)\ c_{n}(x)\,

(85)

with

\displaystyle s_{n}(x)=\sum_{m=0}^{n}\frac{(2n)!\,(-1)^{m}}{(2n-2m)!\,x^{1+2m}}\,,\qquad c_{n}(x)=\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}\frac{(2n)!\,(-1)^{m}}{(2n-2m-1)!\,x^{2+2m}}\,.

(86)

It is easy to check that the functions
h_{n}(x)
fulfill the following relations:

\displaystyle h_{n}(0)=\frac{1}{1+2n}\,,\qquad\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}h_{n}(x)}{\mathrm{d}x^{2}}=-h_{n+1}(x)\,.

(87)

We recall that
A(py=0,y^{2})
is already completely described by the double exponential ansatz in (
81
). Therefore,
in the analysis of the
py
dependence, we consider the normalized twist-two function

\displaystyle\widehat{A}(py,y^{2}):=\frac{A(py,y^{2})}{A(0,y^{2})}=\sum_{n=0}^{N}\hat{a}_{n}(y^{2})\ h_{n}(py)\,,

(88)

with the normalized coefficients

\displaystyle\hat{a}_{n}(y^{2})=\frac{a_{n}(y^{2})}{A(0,y^{2})}\,.

(89)

A useful quantity to investigate in the context of the
py
-analysis is the
2m
-th moment in
\zeta
of the DPD Mellin moment, which can be written as:

\displaystyle\langle\zeta^{2m}\rangle(y^{2}):=\frac{\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}\zeta\ \zeta^{2m}I(\zeta,y^{2})}{\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}\zeta\ I(\zeta,y^{2})}=\left.(-1)^{m}\frac{\partial^{2m}\widehat{A}(py,y^{2})}{\partial(py)^{2m}}\right|_{py=0}\,.

(90)

If we insert our ansatz (
84
) combined with (
88
) and replace the
2m
-th derivative of
\widehat{A}
according to (
87
), we find that
\langle\zeta^{2m}\rangle
can be expressed as:

\displaystyle\langle\zeta^{2m}\rangle(y^{2})=\sum_{n=0}^{N}T_{mn}\hat{a}_{n}(y^{2})\,,

(91)

where we defined the
(N+1)\times(N+1)
-matrix
T

\displaystyle T_{mn}=(1+2n+2m)^{-1}\,.

(92)

Equation (
91
) can be inverted, so that we are able to express the coefficients
\hat{a}_{n}
in terms of the
\zeta
-moments:

\displaystyle\hat{a}_{n}(y^{2})=\sum_{m=0}^{N}\left(T^{-1}\right)_{nm}\langle\zeta^{2m}\rangle(y^{2})\,,

(93)

and hence

\displaystyle\widehat{A}(py,y^{2})=\sum_{n,m=0}^{N}\left(T^{-1}\right)_{nm}\langle\zeta^{2m}\rangle(y^{2})\ h_{n}(py)\,.

(94)

One has
\langle\zeta^{0}\rangle(y^{2})\equiv 1
by definition. Thus, the first non-trivial term in (
94
) is the one with
m=1
. For each value of
y^{2}
we can perform a fit with the functional form (
94
) with
N
fit parameters. These kind of fits are referred to as ”local” fits in the following. Furthermore, we parameterize the moments of
\zeta
in terms of powers of the distance
y=\sqrt{-y^{2}}
, i.e. we write

\displaystyle\langle\zeta^{2m}\rangle(y^{2})=\sum_{k=0}^{K}c_{mk}\sqrt{-y^{2}}^{k}\,,

(95)

such that we obtain a global parameterization describing both the
y^{2}
and
py
-dependence:

\displaystyle\widehat{A}(py,y^{2})=\sum_{n,m=0}^{N}\sum_{k=0}^{K}\left(T^{-1}\right)_{nm}c_{mk}\sqrt{-y^{2}}^{k}h_{n}(py)\,.

(96)

Since
c_{0k}=\delta_{0k}
by definition, there are
N(K+1)
parameters to be determined in a ”global” fit to the parameterization (
96
).

py
-fits:

The results obtained for the
y^{2}
-fit are used to calculate the normalized function
\widehat{A}(py,y^{2})
, which is then fitted to the functional form (
94
) for certain values of
y^{2}
. We perform two sets of fits using
N=2
or
N=3
, i.e. there are two or three free fit parameters, respectively. The free fit parameters are the moments in
\zeta
, i.e.
\langle\zeta^{2m}\rangle
with
m=1,\dots,N
. For each accessible value of
y^{2}
, there is a number of available data points that can be used to fit the
py
-dependence. This number strongly varies with
y^{2}
. In order to avoid fluctuations caused by this circumstance we do not only consider the data points with
y=y_{\mathrm{fit}}
, but take into account all data points in a band
y_{\mathrm{fit}}-0.5a\leq y\leq y_{\mathrm{fit}}+0.5a
. The fit is carried out for

, where

is an integer.

4.6
The number sum rule

We consider the DPD number sum rule, which we have already stated in (
2.1
) in position space. We look at the flavor combination
ud
. The remaining two flavor combinations
uu
and
dd
cannot be investigated, since the corresponding expressions include sea quark contributions that would lead to diverging integrals over
x_{1}
. In the considered case of one
u
and one
d
quark, splitting contributions are at least of second order in
\alpha_{s}
. Inserting the sum rule for ordinary PDFs in (
2.1
) we can write:

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}x_{1}\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}x_{2}\int_{b_{0}/\mu}\mathrm{d}^{2}\boldsymbol{y}\ F_{ud}(x_{1},x_{2},\boldsymbol{y};\mu)=2+\mathcal{O}(\alpha_{s}^{2}(\mu))+\mathcal{O}((\Lambda/\mu)^{2})\,.

(98)

By executing the integrals over
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
, we can identify the DPD Mellin moments for
\zeta=0
. The Fourier transform in
py
(
2.3
) then yields up to corrections of order
\Lambda^{2}/\mu^{2}
and
\alpha_{s}^{2}
:

\displaystyle 2\pi\int^{\infty}_{b_{0}/\mu}\mathrm{d}y\ y\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}(py)\ A_{ud}(py,y^{2})=2\,.

(99)

The verification that this equations holds for the results we presented in the previous sections can be seen as a consistency check of our lattice calculations and our fitting ansatz. We evaluate the expression on the l.h.s. of (
99
) by inserting the parameters obtained from the

5
Factorization Tests

A crucial aspect to be studied in the context of DPDs is the strength of parton-parton correlations. These are neglected in factorization assumptions like (
8
). In the following we want to check to what extent this factorization ansatz is valid.

5.1
Derivation

Equation (
8
) can be derived by inserting a complete set of states in the two-current matrix element appearing in (
2.1
) or (
2.2
) and then assuming that the intermediate nucleon states dominate, i.e. omitting all remaining contributions:

\displaystyle\sum_{\lambda}\bra{p,\lambda}\mathcal{O}_{a_{1}}(y,z_{1})\ \mathcal{O}_{a_{2}}(0,z_{2})\ket{p,\lambda}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle?}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle?}}{{=}}\sum_{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}p^{\prime+}\mathrm{d}^{2}\boldsymbol{p}^{\prime}}{2p^{\prime+}(2\pi)^{3}}e^{-iy(p^{\prime}-p)}\bra{p,\lambda}\mathcal{O}_{a_{1}}(0,z_{1})\ket{p^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime}}\bra{p^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime}}\mathcal{O}_{a_{2}}(0,z_{2})\ket{p,\lambda}\ .

(100)

By writing
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle?}}{{=}}
, we emphasize that (
100
) is an assumption; its validity is investigated in this section. For the remaining derivation steps, we substitute the intermediate momentum
p^{\prime}
by:

\displaystyle p^{\prime+}=(1-\zeta)p^{+}\,,\qquad\boldsymbol{p}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{p}-\boldsymbol{r}\,.

(101)

Furthermore, we set
\boldsymbol{p}=\boldsymbol{0}
and identify:

\displaystyle\bar{x}_{i}=\frac{x_{i}}{1-\frac{\zeta}{2}}\,,\qquad\xi=\frac{\zeta}{2-\zeta}\,,\qquad t(\zeta,\boldsymbol{r}^{2})=-\frac{\zeta^{2}m^{2}+\boldsymbol{r}^{2}}{1-\zeta}\,.

(102)

This enables us to write a factorized expression of the skewed DPD defined in (
2.2
) in terms of GPD matrix elements
f^{\lambda^{\prime}\lambda}(\bar{x},\xi,\boldsymbol{p}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{p})

Notice that regions where the integral gives a higher value than the two-current data, or vice versa, are consistently the same for the twist-two functions and the Mellin moments. For
ud
we observe the integral to be larger for
y<8a
, while it is smaller if
y>8a
. This means that in a joint observation of an
u
and a
d
quark, we find the two quarks farther apart than we would if they were uncorrelated. This is similar to
u\bar{d}
in the
\pi^{+}
described in
Bali:2020mij
. For two quarks of the same flavor, the integration results are generally larger than the two-current data. An exception is given by the region
y<5a
, where at least the twist-two function results indicate a sign change in the absolute difference.

6
Conclusions

This paper presents the first lattice calculation that provides information about double parton distributions in the proton. The distributions in the neutron are readily obtained from isospin symmetry. Our simulations are done on a
32^{3}\times 96
lattice with spacing
a\approx 0.086\operatorname{fm}
and a pion mass of
m_{\pi}=355\operatorname{MeV}
. We compute the correlation functions (
18
) of two spatially separated currents in the proton and project out their twist-two parts. Our primary observables are the invariant functions
A
and
B
associated with that projection, see (
2.3
). They depend on the distance
y^{\mu}
between the two currents and on proton four-momentum
p^{\mu}
via the scalar products
y^{2}
and
py
. We consider the vector, axial, and tensor current, whose twist-two components respectively correspond to unpolarized, longitudinally polarized, and transversely polarized quarks.

Lattice aspects.

We evaluate all Wick contractions that contribute to the two-current correlation functions, making heavy use of stochastic sources, sequential sources, and the hopping parameter expansion. The statistical signal we obtain is in general very good for the connected graphs
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
and the disconnected graph
S_{2}
, and fair for the disconnected graph
S_{1}
(see figure
3
). Only for the doubly disconnected graph
D
are the errors so large that we must exclude it from our analysis. Lattice artifacts manifest themselves in the invariant functions as a breaking of rotation invariance (i.e. a dependence on direction of
\vec{\mskip 0.5muy}\mskip 1.5mu
) and a breaking of boost invariance (at given
y=|\vec{\mskip 0.5muy}\mskip 1.5mu|
and
py
the functions must be independent of
p^{\mu}
). We find a significant amount of anisotropy in the
C_{1}
data at large
y
and in the
C_{2}
and
S_{2}
data at small
y
. These can be interpreted as a finite size effect in the first case and as due to the anisotropy of the lattice propagator in the second case. We can largely remove these effects by selecting points
\vec{\mskip 0.5muy}\mskip 1.5mu
close to the lattice diagonals and by imposing a lower cutoff on
y
, which depending on the polarization channel is taken of order
4a\approx 0.34\operatorname{fm}
. After this selection, the violation of boost invariance is at an acceptable level, except for graph
S_{2}
, where a momentum dependence is seen up to about
y\sim 7a
. For larger
y
, the contribution of
S_{2}
to physical matrix elements is small compared with the one from
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
. The contribution of
S_{1}
is found to be small at the scale of
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
, except for larger
y
, where the errors on
S_{1}
prevent us from drawing strong conclusions. For our final physics analysis, we restrict ourselves to the contributions of the connected graphs
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
, where
C_{2}
is absent for the parton combination
ud
and
C_{1}
is absent for
dd
.

Results.

In a first stage, we analyze the invariant twist-two functions
A
and
B
at
py=0
, where the statistical signal is best and the data can be plotted as a function of the single variable
y
. To connect these functions with DPDs, we slightly deform them by a skewness in the parton momentum fractions that is parameterized by
\zeta
(see figure
1
). Twist-two functions at
py=0
are then equal to the Mellin moments of skewed DPDs integrated over
\zeta
. The size of these functions is seen to be largest for
A_{qq^{\prime}}
and
A_{\delta q\mskip 1.5muq^{\prime}}
, with the former corresponding to unpolarized partons and the latter to the correlation between the transverse polarization of one parton and the parton separation. Our results exhibit a clear flavor dependence, with
A_{ud}
and
A_{\delta u\mskip 1.5mud}\approx A_{\delta d\mskip 1.5muu}
decreasing more slowly with
y
than their counterparts for two
u
or two
d
quarks (see figure
12
). For unpolarized quarks, this finding is of particular importance, because one of the assumptions made for deriving the pocket formula (
10
) for DPS cross sections is a universal
y
dependence of DPDs for all flavor combinations. Interestingly,
A_{uu}
and
A_{dd}
have a rather similar
y
dependence, although the former receives a contribution from
C_{1}
but the latter does not.

The signal for spin dependent functions other than
A_{\delta q\mskip 1.5muq^{\prime}}
is best for the
ud
combination, whereas for
qq^{\prime}=uu
and
dd
it is mostly consistent with zero (see figure
13
). In the
ud
channel, the invariant functions for two polarized quarks are significantly smaller than
A_{\delta u\mskip 1.5mud}
. We see a clear difference between the spin-spin correlations
A_{\Delta u\Delta d}
and
A_{\delta u\delta d}
for longitudinal and transverse polarization, which shows the inadequacy of simple non-relativistic pictures that predict them to be equal. Moreover, we find that the longitudinal polarization ratios
A_{\Delta u\Delta d}/A_{ud}
and
A_{\Delta u\Delta u}/A_{uu}
are significantly smaller in size than the values
-2/3
and
+1/3
obtained with a static SU(6) invariant wave function for the three valence quarks in the proton
Diehl:2011yj
. Interestingly, the pattern of polarization dependence for
ud
in the proton is quite similar to the one we found for
u\bar{d}
in a
\pi^{+}
in our previous work
Bali:2020mij
.

In the second stage of our analysis, we assume a parametric form for the
y
and
py
dependence of the twist-two functions (see (
81
) and (
96
)). We use this to fit our data and to extrapolate it to the full range of
py
, which is needed to compute the Mellin moments
I_{qq^{\prime}}
,
I_{\delta qq^{\prime}}
, …of DPDs at given skewness
\zeta
. For flavor and polarization combinations with sufficiently small statistical errors, the results of fits with different numbers of parameters are consistent with each other for small to moderate
\zeta
(see figures
19
and
21
). This gives us confidence in analyzing the corresponding Mellin moments at
\zeta=0
and thus to make closer contact with the physics of double parton scattering.

The flavor and polarization dependence of Mellin moments at
\zeta=0
is very similar to the one of the associated twist-two functions at
py=0
, which corroborates the physics conclusions discussed above (see figures
21
and
22
). From the moment
I_{ud}
, we can also evaluate the
x
integral of the number sum rule for DPDs
Gaunt:2009re
;
Diehl:2018kgr
. We find excellent agreement with the predicted value of the sum rule (see table
12
) and regard this as a strong check of our fitting ansatz and analysis procedure.

Correlation effects.

Many models for DPDs rest on the assumption that the two partons are independent of each other. This assumption can be formalized and leads to factorization formulae for the twist-two functions
A_{qq^{\prime}}
and
A_{\Delta q\Delta q^{\prime}}
((
5.1
) and (
5.1
)), and for the associated Mellin moments ((
109
) and (
110
)). These functions are then expressed in terms of the nucleon Dirac and Pauli form factors
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
for unpolarized quarks, and of the axial and pseudoscalar form factors
g_{A}
and
g_{P}
for longitudinal quark polarization. We fit these form factors to lattice data from the same ensemble used for computing the two-current correlators, and then extrapolate the form factors in the momentum transfer. We find that the factorization formula for unpolarized quarks is to a good approximation saturated by the contribution from
F_{1}
, whilst for longitudinal polarization it is important to include the contributions from both
g_{A}
and
g_{P}
(see figure
26
).

We find that the factorization assumption for
A_{ud}
and
A_{uu}
at
py=0
works remarkably well, with deviations not larger than
20\%
in the
y
range considered (see figure
27
). It works rather well also for
A_{\Delta u\Delta d}
, whereas for
A_{dd}
and
A_{\Delta u\Delta u}
larger deviations from factorization are observed (see figure
28
). The factorization for the Mellin moments
I_{ud}
and
I_{uu}
at
\zeta=0
works rather well, albeit with deviations up to almost
60\%
, whereas for
I_{dd}
the discrepancies are again larger (see figure
29
). In other channels, the errors in our data or fits are too large for drawing solid conclusions.

Summary and outlook.

In summary, we find that the calculation of two-current correlators on the lattice can provide valuable physics insight into two-quark correlations inside the proton, which are essential for understanding double parton scattering. Our main results are as follows. (i) The dependence of two-parton distributions on the distance
y
is
not
the same for different flavors. (ii) Spin-spin correlations between two quarks are remarkably small, in contrast to spin-orbit correlations. (iii) The functions we studied approximately factorize into separate functions for the individual partons.

Important challenges for future work are to perform simulations at smaller lattice spacings, so as to extend the
y
range where lattice artifacts can be controlled, and to move closer to the physical pion mass. Improvements that will allow the inclusion of disconnected graphs in the physics analysis are also highly desired. The results obtained in the present study strongly motivate us to make efforts in these directions.
