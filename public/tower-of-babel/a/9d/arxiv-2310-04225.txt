Estimation of the incubation time distribution in the singly and doubly interval censored model
Piet Groeneboom

Abstract
We analyze nonparametric estimators for the distribution function of the incubation time in the singly and doubly interval censoring model. The classical approach is to use parametric families like Weibull, log-normal or gamma distributions in the estimation procedure. We propose nonparametric estimates for functions of the observations, which stay closer to the data than the classical parametric methods. We also give explicit limit distributions for discrete versions of the models and apply this to compute confidence intervals. The methods complement the analysis of the continuous model in [ 4 ] and [ 5 ] . R scripts for computation of the estimates are provided in [ 3 ] .

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\epsilon^{-1}\left\{\ell\left((1-\epsilon)\hat{F}_{n}+\epsilon F_{0}\right)-\ell\left(\hat{F}_{n}\right)\right\}\leq 0,

(2.11)

for

, where

Let the covariance matrix

\displaystyle n^{1/2}\left\{\bigl(\hat{F}_{n}(1),\dots,\hat{F}_{n}(m-1)\bigr)-\bigl(\bar{F}_{0}(1),\dots,\bar{F}_{0}(m-1)\bigr)\right\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\mathcal{D}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}N\left(\bm{0},{\bm{A}}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}{\bm{A}}^{T}\right),

3
Computation of the nonparametric maximum likelihood estimators

In
[
4
]
two methods were discussed to compute the nonparametric MLE in the singly interval censored model: the EM algorithm and the iterative convex minorant algorithm. The EM algorithm is excruciatingly slow for this model and for this reason the iterative convex minorant algorithm was used in the simulations. But in the case of doubly interval censored data it is less clear how the iterative convex minorant algorithm should be used, although we could think of ways to apply it in this situation too. However, we will turn to a third method of computing the nonparametric MLE, the
support reduction algorithm
, see
[
6
]
. This method can be applied with equal ease to the two models.

We first discuss the support reduction algorithm for the singly interval censored model. The support reduction algorithm starts by specifying a grid of points
\mathcal{S}=\{1,\dots,M_{1}+M_{2}\}
which could be points of mass of the MLE. As an example, for the data set analyzed in
[
4
]
, one could take set of points
\mathcal{S}=\{1,2,\dots,30\}
. We can also take the points
S_{i}
and
(S_{i}-E_{i})1_{\{S_{i}-E_{i}>0\}}
because these are the points appearing in the log likelihood.

The log likelihood, divided by
n
, for this set of points can be written

\displaystyle\ell(p_{1},\dots,p_{M})=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{M}p_{j}1_{\{j\in(S_{i}-E_{i},S_{i}]\}}\right\},

where
M=M_{1}+M_{2}+1
,
p_{j}\geq 0
, and
\sum_{j=1}^{M}p_{j}=1
, and where the subset of locations
j
of
strictly
positive mass
p_{j}
have to be estimated. Introducing the notation

\displaystyle w_{i}(j)=1_{\{j\in(S_{i}-E_{i},S_{i}]\}},

(3.1)

we can write the log likelihood, divided by
n
, for
\{p_{1},\dots,p_{M}\}
at
\{1,\dots,M\}

\displaystyle\ell(p_{1},\dots,p_{M})=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{M}p_{j}w_{i}(j)\right\}

(3.2)

Turning the maximization problem into a minimization problem on the cone
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{M}
, with a Lagrange term to ensure that the solution satisfies
\sum_{i=1}^{M}p_{i}=1
, we get as our criterion function

\displaystyle\phi(p_{1},\dots,p_{M})=-n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{M}p_{j}w_{i}(j)\right\}+\sum_{i=1}^{M}p_{i}-1

(3.3)

For this function have the following lemma.

Lemma 2
(Fenchel duality conditions for minimization on a cone).

The function
\phi
in (
3.3
) is minimized on
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{M}
if and only if

(i)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{j}}\phi(p_{1},\dots,p_{M})\geq 0,\qquad j=0,\dots,M,

(3.4)

and

(ii)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{M}p_{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{j}}\phi(p_{1},\dots,p_{m})=0.

(3.5)

The algorithms mentioned (EM, convex minorant algorithm and support reduction algorithm) run until the conditions of Lemma 1 are satisfied up to a certain tolerance, for which we take
10^{-10}
. The EM algorithm tries to do this by simple iteration:

\displaystyle p_{j}^{\prime}=p_{j}\left\{1-\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{j}}\phi(p_{1},\dots,p_{M})\right\},\qquad j=1,\dots,M,

(see (7) in
[
4
]
), the iterative convex minorant algorithm by introducing a quadratic approximation, parametrizing with the values of the distribution function instead of the point masses (see
[
4
]
).

As in the iterative convex minorant algorithm, the support reduction algorithm employs quadratic approximation, but the parametrization uses the point masses. Expanding the first two terms of the log likelihood at a fixed vector
\bm{p}^{(0)}=(p_{1}^{(0)},\dots,p_{M}^{(0)})
, we get:

\displaystyle n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{M}p_{j}w_{i}(j)\right\}-n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{M}p_{j}^{(0)}w_{i}(j)\right\}

\displaystyle\approx n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{M}\left(p_{j}-p_{j}^{(0)}\right)w_{i}(j)}{\sum_{j=1}^{M}p_{j}^{(0)}w_{i}(j)}-\tfrac{1}{2}n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{M}\left(p_{j}-p_{j}^{(0)}\right)w_{i}(j)\right\}^{2}}{\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{M}p_{j}^{(0)}w_{i}(j)\right\}^{2}}\,.

We now use iterative minimization.
For fixed
m_{0}<M
and a subset
\{j_{1},\dots,j_{m_{0}}\}\subset\{1,\dots,M\}
, we minimize

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{m_{0}}p_{j_{k}}^{2}w_{i}(j_{k})^{2}+2\sum_{k<\ell\leq m_{0}}p_{j_{k}}p_{j_{\ell}}w_{i}(j_{k})w_{i}(j_{\ell})}{\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{M}p_{j}^{(0)}w_{i}(j)\right\}^{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad-\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{m_{0}}p_{j_{k}}w_{i}(j_{k})}{\sum_{j=1}^{M}p_{j}^{(0)}w_{i}(j)}+\sum_{k=1}^{m_{0}}p_{j_{k}}.

(3.6)

as a function of
p_{1},\dots,p_{m_{0}}
, where, at the start of the iterations

\displaystyle m_{0}=1\qquad\text{ and }\qquad p_{j}^{(0)}=\frac{1}{M},\qquad j=1,\dots,M,

and where the double sum in the first numerator vanishes if
m_{0}=1
.

Then we investigate if adding a point
j_{m_{0}+1}
not in the set
\{j_{1},\dots,j_{m_{0}}\}
and minimizing (
3
) over
\{p_{j_{1}},\dots,p_{j_{m_{0}+1}}\}
, with
m_{0}
replaced by
m_{0}+1
, leads to a smaller value of (2.1) with the extra point. This may lead to a solution with negative
p_{i}
’s. In that case we remove the point
k
with the smallest value of
p_{k}<0
and solve the least squares minimization problem for (
3
) again. It can be proved that this procedure does not remove the point just added again (see, e.g.,
[
10
]
and
[
6
]
). If this solution gives again values
p_{i}<0
, we reduce the set further to a subset of
m_{0}-1
points and solve the least squares minimization problem for (
3
) again with
m_{0}
replaced by
m_{0}-1
, continuing until we find a solution with only positive
p_{i}
’s.

Then we repeat the whole procedure, starting by investigating whether adding a point
j_{m_{0}^{\prime}+1}
not in the set
j_{1},\dots,j_{m_{0}^{\prime}}\}
leads to a smaller value of the criterion for the new subset
\{j_{1},\dots,j_{m_{0}^{\prime}}\}\subset\{1,\dots,M\}
. Continuing in this way we find a subset
\{j_{1},\dots,j_{m_{0}}\}
and corresponding
p_{j_{1}},\dots,p_{j_{m_{0}}}
which solves the least squares problem for all possible subsets
\{j_{1},\dots,j_{m_{0}}\}\subset\{1,\dots,M\}
.

Next we change the values
p_{j}^{(0)}
in the denominators of (
3
). Let

\displaystyle\bm{p}=(p_{1},\dots,p_{M}),\qquad\bm{p}^{(0)}=\left(p_{1}^{(0)},\dots,p_{M}^{(0)}\right),

where
\bm{p}
consists of the values
p_{j_{k}}
found in the iterative least squares minimization procedure and zeroes for indices
j_{k}
not corresponding to indices of the subset
\{j_{1},\dots,j_{m_{0}}\}
.
Using a line search procedure, for which we use Armijo’s rule, we look for a convex combination

\displaystyle\bm{p}^{\prime}=\alpha\bm{p}+(1-\alpha)\bm{p}^{(0)},\qquad\alpha\in(0,1),

such that
\phi(\bm{p}^{\prime})<\phi(\bm{p}^{(0)})
, where
\phi
is defined by (
3.3

We repeat these inner and outer iterations until conditions (i) and (ii) of Lemma

5
Conclusion

We studied the nonparametric maximum likelihood estimator of the incubation time, which is such an important parameter in the Covid-19 pandemic. The incubation time in our model is part of the sum of the infection time
I
and the incubation time
U
. Usually the only really (partly) observable quantity is the time of becoming symptomatic, which is given by
S=I+U
. So our variable of interest
U
has to be pulled out of this sum by deconvolution. If
S
is observable, one speaks of the
singly interval censored model
(the beginning of the incubation time lies in an interval which represents the exposure time and this is the singly interval censored part). It seems most reasonable to assume that the time till infection and the incubation time have continuous distributions, and one can analyze this model under the assumption that
S
is exactly observable. In that case the MLE of the distribution function of the incubation time converges, under some conditions, at cube root
n
rate to Chernoff’s distribution, see
[
5
]
.

However, most of the time
S
is not directly observable, but only an interval
[S_{L},S_{R}]
is available, which we know to contain
S
. Taking into account that the observations are usually rounded to days, one can restrict oneself to estimating the means over one day, represented by

\displaystyle\bar{F}_{0}(i)=\int_{i-1}^{i}F_{0}(t)\,dt,

(5.1)

if
F_{0}
is the distribution function of the incubation time. Since this gives us a fixed bounded number of parameters, we can use classical theory, connecting maximum likelihood with the Fisher information, to derive asymptotic distribution theory for the maximum likelihood estimators of these parameters.

We applied the theory, developed in Section
2
to construct confidence intervals, either by using Theorems
1
and
2
directly, or by using a bootstrap method. In a sense, this purely nonparametric method lies at the other extreme of the parametric methods. If one wants to estimate the density, one will have to use some kind of smoothing, as was done in
[
4
]
, which is an intermediate method that is still nonparametric and avoids the need to choose between several parametric models.

The support reduction algorithm seems at present to be the most stable method to estimate the parameters. The computing of the MLE and the confidence intervals is implemented in
[
3
]
and discussed in Section
3
.
R
scripts for running the algorithms discussed in this paper are available at
[
3
]
.

Appendix. Proofs

In contrast with the difficulties of the continuous model in
[
5
]
, the estimation of the parameters
\int_{i-1}^{i}F_{0}(t)\,dt
seems rather standard, once we have figured out the relation between the continuous model and the discrete observations of the times of becoming symptomatic. We still have to deal with a deconvolution problem, which we do by using nonparametric maximum likelihood.

The key step is to reduce the maximization on the simplex
\{\bm{p}=(p_{1},\dots,p_{m})\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{m}:\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}=1\}
to maximization on the cone
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{m}
. One can check that minimizing minus the log likelihood (
3.2
) under the restriction
\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}=1
is equivalent to minimizing (
3.3
), which is the criterion function
+
a Lagrange term with Lagrange multiplier
\lambda=1
, on
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}_{+}
. Then the necessary and sufficient conditions for the minimum follow from Fenchel’s duality theorem, see
[
12
]
, Theorem 31.4.
∎

The log likelihood is of the form

\displaystyle\ell(p_{1},\dots,p_{m})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\sum_{j=1}^{m}p_{j}1_{((S_{i}-E_{i})_{+},S_{i}]}(j),

so we count the number of times the point of mass
j
belongs to an interval
(S_{i}-E_{i})_{+},S_{i}]
, where
S_{i}
and
E_{i}
are integers, and multiply this with the probability
p_{j}
. We have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{j}}\ell\biggl(p_{1},\dots,1-\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}p_{k}\biggr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1_{((S_{i}-E_{i})_{+},S_{i}]}(j)-1_{((S_{i}-E_{i})_{+},S_{i}]}(m)}{\sum_{k=1}^{m}p_{j}1_{((S_{i}-E_{i})_{+},S_{i}]}(k)}\,,\qquad j=1,\dots,m-1,

(6.1)

and

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial p_{j}\partial p_{l}}\ell\biggl(p_{1},\dots,1-\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}p_{k}\biggr)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\left\{1_{((S_{i}-E_{i})_{+},S_{i}]}(j)-1_{((S_{i}-E_{i})_{+},S_{i}]}(m)\right\}\left\{1_{((S_{i}-E_{i})_{+},S_{i}]}(l)-1_{((S_{i}-E_{i})_{+},S_{i}]}(m)\right\}}{\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{m}p_{j}1_{((S_{i}-E_{i})_{+},S_{i}]}(k)\right\}^{2}},

for
j,l=1,\dots,m-1
, using the convention
0/0=0
.

By the assumptions on
F_{0}
and
F_{E}
, the variables
S_{i}
and
(S_{i}-E_{i})_{+}
will be such that for large
n
, the score functions (
6
) will be zero for
p_{j}=\hat{p}_{j}
, where
\hat{\bm{p}}=(\hat{p}_{1},\dots,\hat{p}_{m})
is the MLE of
\bm{p}_{0}=(p_{0}(1),\dots,p_{0}(m))
(that is: no isotonization is needed), and the result now follows from standard theory.
∎
