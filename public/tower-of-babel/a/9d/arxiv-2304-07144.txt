Pitman’s discrete 2 ​ M − X 2M-X theorem for arbitrary initial laws and continuous time limits
Włodzimierz Bryc, Jacek Wesołowski

Abstract
. We discuss Pitman’s representation of a Markov process, which serves as a discrete analog to the Bessel 3D process starting at time 0 from an arbitrary initial law. This representation involves maxima of lazy simple random walks and an auxiliary independent random variable. The law of the auxiliary random variable is explicitly related to the initial law of the Markov process. The proof is kept at an elementary level and relies on a reconstruction formula for the generalized Pitman transform. We then use continuous-time limits to shed additional light on the relation between two representations of the Bessel 3D process that appeared in the description of the stationary measure of the KPZ fixed point on the half-line, as proposed in Barraquand and Le Doussal, 2022 .

Introduction

Let
\left(S_{t}\right)_{t=0,1,\dots}
be a symmetric random walk with increments
\pm 1
and
M_{t}=\max_{j\in\{0,1,\dots,t\}}\,S_{j}
.
Pitman
(
Pitman, 1975
, Lemma 3.1)
proved that
\left(Y_{t}\right)_{t=0,1,\dots}:=\left(2M_{t}-S_{t}\right)_{t=0,1,\dots}
is a discrete-time Markov process started at
X_{0}=0
with transition probabilities

(1.1)

\mathds{P}(Y_{t+1}-Y_{t}=\delta|Y_{t}=k)=\tfrac{1}{2}\begin{cases}\frac{k+2}{k+1}&\delta=1,\\
\frac{k}{k+1}&\delta=-1,\end{cases}\qquad t=0,1,\dots.

As part of the proof, he also established that the conditional law of
S_{n}
given
(Y_{0},Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n})
is uniform on the set
\{-Y_{n},-Y_{n}+2,\dots,Y_{n}-2,Y_{n}\}
, see
(
Chapon and Chhaibi, 2021
, Theorem 1.1)
for an explicit statement.

From (
1.1
) it follows that the Pitman transform
\left(S_{t}\right)_{t=0,1,\dots}\mapsto\left(2M_{t}-S_{t}\right)_{t=0,1,\dots}
maps a random walk to a Markov process with values in
\mathds{Z}_{\geq 0}=\{0,1,\ldots\}
, which can be interpreted as a random walk conditioned on remaining nonnegative. Random walks conditioned on positivity have been studied in numerous references, including
Keener, 1992
,
Bertoin and Doney, 1994
, resulting in processes with transition probabilities (
1.1
) and (
1.4
) (with
\sigma=0
).
Ref.
O’Connell and Yor, 2002
pointed out that
their results yield the following discrete analogue of Pitman’s lemma: if
\left(S_{t}\right)_{t=0,1,\ldots}
is a simple random walk with
non-negative drift (in continuous or discrete time) and
M_{t}=\max_{j\in\{0,1,\ldots,t\}}S_{j}
, then

\left(2M_{t}-S_{t}\right)_{t=0,1,\ldots}
has
the same law as that of
\left(S_{t}\right)_{t=0,1,\ldots}
conditioned to stay positive (in the case of a symmetric random walk, this conditioning is in the sense of Doob).

Hambly et al., 2001
relax independence assumption, proving that the process
\left(2M_{t}-S_{t}\right)_{t=0,1,\ldots}
has the same law as that of
\left(S_{t}\right)_{t=0,1,\ldots}

conditioned to stay non-negative when
\left(S_{t}-S_{t-1}\right)_{t=0,1,\ldots}
is a
\pm 1
-valued Markov process.
The Pitman transform
appears also in the description of the dynamics of the box-ball system in

Croydon et al., 2023
.

This transform inspired numerous modifications and generalizations, see
(
Corwin et al., 2014
, Section 3.4)
for a concise summary, including relations to the Robinson–Schensted–Knuth correspondence studied in
O’Connell, 2003a
;
O’Connell, 2003b
.

Miyazaki and Tanaka, 1989
and
Tanaka, 1989
generalized Pitman’s result, describing Markov processes that arise by the same representation from nonsymmetric simple random walks. In particular,
(
Miyazaki and Tanaka, 1989
, Proposition 2)
derived transition probabilities (
1.4
) when
\sigma=0
.
Mishchenko, 2006
established additional properties of
\left(Y_{t}\right)_{t=0,1,\dots}
, including a discrete version of a theorem from
Imhof, 1992
.
Biane,
Bougerol and O’Connell in
Biane et al., 2005
and
Biane et al., 2009
have extended Pitman’s theorem in the context of semisimple
Lie algebras and finite Coxeter groups. More recently,

Bougerol and Defosseux, 2022
and
Defosseux and Herent, 2023
have established Pitman-type
theorems in the context of affine Lie algebra.
Corwin et al., 2024
used periodic Pitman transforms to study invariant measures for KPZ. There is also a
geometric version of the Pitman transform that was introduced in
Matsumoto and Yor, 1999
and
was studied in
Donati-Martin et al., 2001
and
Hariya and Yor, 2004
. Its relation to the geometric RSK correspondence is discussed in
(
Corwin et al., 2014
, Section 3.4)
.

Pitman’s lemma yields the following folklore result, which we were not able to locate explicitly in the literature.

Theorem 1.1
.

If
(S_{t})_{t=0,1,\dots}
is a symmetric random walk on
\mathds{Z}
starting from
0
and
G
is an independent random variable, which is uniformly distributed on the set
\{0,1,\dots,g\}
for some non-negative integer
g
, then

(1.2)

X_{t}:=g+2(M_{t}-G)_{+}-S_{t},\quad t=0,1,\dots,

is a discrete Bessel process (that is, its transition probabilities are given by (
1.1
)) starting from
X_{0}=g
.

Theorem
1.1
can be derived from Pitman’s lemma by stopping
2M-S
process at the first time it reaches integer
g>0
, compare

(
Mishchenko, 2006
, Theorem 2)
.
(Theorem
1.1
follows also from more general Theorem
1.2
, which we prove independently of Pitman’s lemma.)

Proof.

Fix
g\in\mathds{Z}_{\geq 0}
. Take
t_{*}
large enough (and of the right parity) so that
\mathds{P}(S_{t_{*}}=g)>0
. Therefore, with the Pitman transform
(Y_{t}=2M_{t}-S_{t})_{t=0,1,\ldots}
as above,
\mathds{P}(Y_{t_{*}}=g)>0
and the conditional law of
S_{t_{*}}
given
A_{g}:=\{Y_{0}=0,Y_{1}=y_{1},\dots,Y_{t_{*}}=g\}
of positive probability, is uniform on the set
\{-g,-g+2,\dots g-2,g\}
.

Let
X_{t}=Y_{t+t_{*}}
and
G=\max_{k\leq t^{*}}S_{k}-S_{t_{*}}
. Then
G=\tfrac{1}{2}(Y_{t_{*}}-S_{t_{*}})
is uniform on
\{0,1,\dots,g\}
conditionally on
A_{g}
.
We will verify that (
1.2
) holds conditionally on
A_{g}
.

Since
\max\{a,b\}=a+(b-a)_{+}
we have

(1.1.1)

Y_{t+t_{*}}=2\max_{k\leq t+t_{*}}S_{k}-S_{t+t_{*}}=2\max_{k\leq t_{*}}S_{k}+2(\max_{j\leq t}S_{j+t_{*}}-\max_{k\leq t_{*}}S_{k})_{+}-S_{t+t_{*}}\\
=Y_{t_{*}}+2(\max_{j\leq t}(S_{j+t_{*}}-S_{t_{*}})-G)_{+}-(S_{t+t_{*}}-S_{t_{*}})=Y_{t_{*}}+2(\max_{j\leq t}(S_{j}^{*})-G)_{+}-S_{t}^{*}

where
S_{t}^{*}=S_{t+t_{*}}-S_{t_{*}}
. Note that
(S_{t}^{*})_{t=0,1,\ldots}
and
(G,A_{g})
are independent, and
(S_{t}^{*})_{t=0,1,\ldots}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}(S_{t})_{t=0,1,\ldots}
. Therefore,
(
1.1.1
) says that

(1.1.2)

X_{t}=X_{0}+2(\max_{j\leq t}S_{j}^{*}-G)_{+}-S_{t}^{*}

conditionally on
A_{g}
has the same law as the right-hand side of (
1.2
), and in particular
\mathds{P}(X_{0}=g|A_{g})=1
.

On the other hand, transition probabilities for
(X_{t})_{t=0,1,\ldots}
as defined by the right-hand side of (
1.1.2
) do not depend on conditioning on
A_{g}
and are the same as transition probabilities for
(Y_{t_{*}+t})_{t=0,1,\ldots}
, which are given by (
1.1
).
∎

Related results in continuous-time appeared recently in the description of the stationary measures for the hypothetical KPZ fixed point on the half-line in Refs.
Barraquand and Le Doussal, 2022
;
Bryc and Kuznetsov, 2022
. In discrete-time, Markov processes with transition probabilities given by (
1.4
) and special initial laws arise also as limits of random Motzkin paths in
Bryc and Wang, 2024
.

Our goal is to clarify these relations by providing explicit formulas that extend these examples to more general initial laws. For the proofs in continuous-time, we rely on discrete approximations. For the discrete-time results, we use elementary and explicit formulas for the finite-dimensional distributions.

For
\rho>0
and
\sigma\geq 0
we consider the following three processes: a random walk
(S_{t})_{t=0,1,\dots}
, starting at
S_{0}=0
, and with
S_{t}=\sum_{j\leq t}\xi_{j}
, where
\xi_{j}
are i.i.d. random variables with the law

(1.3)

\mathds{P}(\xi_{1}=\delta)=\tfrac{1}{\rho+\sigma+1/\rho}\begin{cases}1/\rho&\delta=1,\\
\sigma&\delta=0,\\
\rho&\delta=-1,\end{cases}

a random walk
(\widetilde{S}_{t})_{t=0,1,\dots}
, starting at
\widetilde{S}_{0}=0
, and with

\widetilde{S}_{t}=\sum_{j=1}^{t}\widetilde{\xi}_{j}
where
\widetilde{\xi}_{j}
are i.i.d. random variables with the law of
-\xi_{1}

and a Markov process
(X_{t})_{t=0,1,\dots}
with transition probabilities

(1.4)

\mathds{P}(X_{t+1}=k+\delta|X_{t}=k)=\mathds{P}(\xi_{1}=\delta)\,\frac{[k+\delta+1]_{\rho^{2}}}{[k+1]_{\rho^{2}}},

and with arbitrary initial law supported in
\mathds{Z}_{\geq 0}
. Here
[n]_{q}=1+\dots+q^{n-1}
.
We will occasionally extend this notation to
a\in\mathds{R}
,

[a]_{q}=\begin{cases}\frac{1-q^{a}}{1-q}&q\neq 1\\
a&q=1.\end{cases}.

We note that
(S_{t})_{t=0,1,\dots}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{=}}(-\widetilde{S}_{t})_{t=0,1,\dots}
, and that if
\rho=1

(S_{t})_{t=0,1,\dots}
is a symmetric (lazy) random walk,
and transition probabilities (
1.4
) become

\mathds{P}\left(X_{t+1}=k+\delta\middle|X_{t}=k\right)=\tfrac{1}{2+\sigma}\begin{cases}\frac{k+2}{k+1}&\delta=1,\\
\sigma&\delta=0,\\
\frac{k}{k+1}&\delta=-1,\end{cases}\qquad t=0,1,\ldots,

in agreement with (

We also note that formula (
1.3

that assign a positive mass to

and
-1

Assume

\sigma\geq 0

(1.5)

(X_{t}-X_{0})_{t=0,1,\dots}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{=}}(2(M_{t}-G)_{+}-S_{t})_{t=0,1,\dots},

4.2.
A comment on damage models

Let
(N,R)
be a pair of nonnegative integer-valued random variables. Assume that the conditional distribution
\mathds{P}_{R|N}
with support
\{0,1,\ldots,N\}
is specified by a function
(s(r|n))_{r\in\{0,\ldots,n\}}
,
n=0,1,\ldots
. Then
(N,R)
is called the damage model generated by
s
, where
R
represents the number of surviving observations when the original observation
N
is subject to a destructive process, while
D=N-R
is the damaged part of
N
.
The popular damage model is when the survival distribution is binomial, that is, with
s(r|n)=\tbinom{n}{r}\,p^{r}(1-p)^{n-r}
, with
p\in(0,1)
. It is well known that in the binomial damage model if
N
is a Poisson random variable then
R
and
D
are independent and both have Poisson distributions. It is also known that if in the binomial damage model random variables
R
and
D
are independent, then
N
is necessarily a Poisson random variable. Actually, it was shown in
Radhakrishna Rao and Rubin, 1964
that in the binomial damage model a weaker assumption of partial independence, known as the Rao-Rubin condition,

(4.1)

\mathds{P}_{R|D=0}=\mathds{P}_{R},

implies that
N
is a Poisson random variable.

In this context, formula (
1.9
) for
q=\rho^{2}
defines a damage model with function

(4.2)

s(r,n)=\tfrac{q^{r}}{[n+1]_{q}},\quad r=0,1,\ldots,n\geq 0.

Corollary
1.6
can be interpreted as follows.
If
N
is a
q
-negative binomial as given in (
1.12
), then the damaged
D
and undamaged
R
parts of
N
are independent and follow respectively
\mathrm{geo}(q\theta)
and
\mathrm{geo}(\theta)
distributions. It appears that under (
4.2
) the Rao-Rubin condition implies that
N
is of the form given in (
1.12
). More precisely, we have the following characterization result.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
R
and
N
be non-negative integer valued random variables such that
\mathds{P}(R=r|N=n)=s(r,n)
as in (
4.2
),
q>0
. Assume that
\mathds{P}(D=0)=1-\theta\in(0,1)
and
q\theta\in(0,1)
.

If the Rao-Rubin condition (
4.1
) holds, then
R\sim\mathrm{geo}(q\theta)
and
D=N-R\sim\mathrm{geo}(\theta)

are independent, that is,
N
is
q
-negative binomial as given in (
1.12
).

Proof.

Condition (
4.1
) says

\mathds{P}(R=r,D=0)=\mathds{P}(D=0)\mathds{P}(R=r),\quad r=0,1,\ldots.

Applying (
4.2
) to both sides above and canceling the common term
q^{r}
we get

\tfrac{\mathds{P}(N=r)}{[r+1]_{q}}=(1-\theta)\sum_{n\geq r}\tfrac{\mathds{P}(N=n)}{[n+1]_{q}}.

Subtracting sidewise two such identities for
r
and
r-1
,
r\geq 1
, we obtain recursion

\tfrac{\mathds{P}(N=r)}{[r+1]_{q}}=\theta\tfrac{\mathds{P}(N=r-1)}{[r]_{q}},\quad r=1,2,\ldots,

which gives

\mathds{P}(N=r)=[r+1]_{q}\,\theta^{r}\,\beta,\quad r=1,2,\ldots,

where
\beta=\mathds{P}(N=0)\neq 0
.

∎

Similar results for damage models that include
a result for
q=1
, i.e., for uniform damage and under some technical integrability conditions, can be found in
(
Patil and Ratnaparkhi, 1977
, Theorem 3.1)
.

4.3.
A Poisson Example

We give another explicit example of a pair satisfying (
1.9
). If
X_{0}\sim 1+\mathrm{Poisson}(1)
is a shift by one of a Poisson random variable with parameter
\lambda=1
,
then

from (

) with
\rho=1

is
\mathrm{Poisson}(1)
.
Indeed,

\mathds{P}(X_{0}=n)=e^{-1}/(n-1)!

we get

\mathds{P}(G=m)=e^{-1}\sum_{n=m}^{\infty}\tfrac{1}{(n-1)!(n+1)}=e^{-1}\sum_{n=m}^{\infty}\tfrac{n}{(n+1)!}\\
=e^{-1}\sum_{n=m}^{\infty}\left(\tfrac{n+1}{(n+1)!}-\tfrac{1}{(n+1)!}\right)=e^{-1}\sum_{n=m}^{\infty}\tfrac{1}{n!}-e^{-1}\sum_{n=m+1}^{\infty}\tfrac{1}{n!}=\tfrac{e^{-1}}{m!}.

Thus (

) holds with symmetric random walk (

),

and

Interestingly,

defines bivariate distribution

). In this proof,
r
is fixed, so we drop superscript
r
in the notation

{\boldsymbol{s}}^{(r)}=(s_{0},\dots,s_{t})
. Since
by (
2.8
) we have

s_{j}=2(r_{j}-K_{0})-x_{j}
, we see that

s_{j+1}-s_{j}=2(r_{j+1}-r_{j})-(x_{j+1}-x_{j})=2(r_{j+1}-r_{j+1}\wedge x_{j})-(x_{j+1}-x_{j}).

We now consider two cases:
x_{j}\geq r_{j+1}
and
x_{j}<r_{j+1}
. In the first case, we
get
r_{j+1}\wedge x_{j}=r_{j+1}
, so

s_{j+1}-s_{j}=x_{j}-x_{j+1}
. In the second case,
x_{j+1}-x_{j}=1
as
x_{j}<x_{j+1}
. Since

x_{j+1}-1=x_{j}<r_{j+1}\leq x_{j+1}
, we have
r_{j+1}=x_{j+1}
. We get

(A.7)

s_{j+1}-s_{j}=\begin{cases}x_{j}-x_{j+1}&x_{j}\geq r_{j+1},\\
x_{j+1}-x_{j}=1&x_{j}<r_{j+1}.\end{cases}

We now note that
x_{j}=x_{j+1}
if and only if
s_{j+1}=s_{j}
, so
H({\boldsymbol{s}}^{(r)})=H({\boldsymbol{x}})
.

Next, define
j_{v}
as the largest index
j
such that
x_{j}=K_{0}+v-1
,
v=1,\dots,r-K_{0}
.
Note that for
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in C\llbracket 0,t\rrbracket
such
j_{v}
are well defined, as the values of
{\boldsymbol{x}}
must cover
the entire range
\{K_{0},K_{0}+1,\ldots,x_{t}\}
and
r\in\llbracket K_{0},x_{t}\rrbracket
. From
x_{t}>r-1
, we see that
j_{v}<t
.
For each such
j_{v}
we have
x_{j_{v}}<r_{j_{v}+1}
, so by (
A.7
),
x_{j_{v}+1}-x_{j_{v}}=1
.
(To explain why
x_{j_{v}}<r_{j_{v}+1}
we observe that
x_{j_{v}}
is below
x_{j_{v}+1},\dots,x_{t}
and
x_{j_{v}}=K_{0}+v-1\leq r-1<r.
)

We note that
\{j\ \in\llbracket 0,t\rrbracket:x_{j}<r_{j+1}\}=\{j_{1},\dots,j_{r-K_{0}}\}
.
Indeed, let
A=\{j\ \in\llbracket 0,t\rrbracket:x_{j}<r_{j+1}\}
and
B=\{j_{1},\dots,j_{r-K_{0}}\}
. We already noted that
j_{v}\in A
, that is
B\subset A
.
To prove the converse inclusion, take
j\in A
. Since
x_{j}<r_{j+1}
thus
x_{j}\leq r-1
so
j<t
. Thus there exists
v\in\{1,\dots,r-K_{0}\}
such that
x_{j}=K_{0}+v-1
.
Since
x_{j}<r_{j+1}\leq\min\{x_{j+1},\dots,x_{t}\}
, we see that
j=j_{v}
since
x_{j}
is the last element on the level
K_{0}+v-1
.

Thus we identified
(r-K_{0})
up-steps of
{\boldsymbol{s}}^{r}
which coincide with the up-steps of
{\boldsymbol{x}}
. In view of (
A.7
), the remaining up-steps of
{\boldsymbol{s}}^{r}
are all the down-steps of
{\boldsymbol{x}}
, i.e.
U({\boldsymbol{s}}^{(r)})=r-K_{0}+D({\boldsymbol{x}})
. The last formula in (
2.11
) comes from
U+D+H=t
.
∎
