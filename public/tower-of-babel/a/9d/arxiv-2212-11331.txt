Uniqueness for the anisotropic fractional conductivity equation
Giovanni Covi

Abstract
. In this paper we study an inverse problem for fractional anisotropic conductivity. Our nonlocal operator is based on the well-developed theory of nonlocal vector calculus, and differs substantially from other generalizations of the classical anisotropic conductivity operator obtained spectrally. We show that the anisotropic conductivity matrix can be recovered uniquely from fractional Dirichlet-to-Neumann data up to a natural gauge. Our analysis makes use of techniques recently developed for the study of the isotropic fractional elasticity equation, and generalizes them to the case of non-separable, anisotropic conductivities. The motivation for our study stems from its relation to the classical anisotropic Calderón problem, which at the time of writing is one of the main open problems in the field.

1.
Introduction

Let
s\in(0,1)
. We study the anisotropic fractional conductivity operator

\mathbf{C}^{s}_{A}u:=(\nabla\cdot)^{s}(A(x,y)\cdot\nabla^{s}u),

where
A\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n},{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n})
is an
n\times n
matrix function of the variables
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. The operators
(\nabla\cdot)^{s},\nabla^{s}
, which we will define in detail in the upcoming Section
2
, are the fractional divergence and gradient. They were firstly introduced in the nonlocal vector calculus presented in
[
DGLZ12
]
, and their properties were further studied in
[
Cov20a
,
Cov20b
,
CRZ22
]
, among others. They represent fractional counterparts to the classical divergence and gradient operators. As a matter of fact, they enjoy the expected properties
(\nabla^{s})^{*}=(\nabla\cdot)^{s}
and
(\nabla\cdot)^{s}\nabla^{s}=(-\Delta)^{s}
. The operator
\mathbf{C}^{s}_{A}
will be rigorously defined in Section
3
.

Our inverse problem consists in recovering the values of
A
in
\Omega
from measurements performed in the exterior
\Omega_{e}:={\mathbb{R}}^{n}\setminus\overline{\Omega}
. These measurement will be given in the form of a Dirichlet-to-Neumann (DN) map
\Lambda_{A}
, associating each exterior datum
f
of the direct problem

\begin{array}[]{rll}\mathbf{C}^{s}_{A}u&=0&\quad\mbox{ in }\Omega\\
u&=f&\quad\mbox{ in }\Omega_{e}\end{array}

to the corresponding nonlocal Neumann datum
\mathbf{C}^{s}_{A}u_{f}|_{\Omega_{e}}
. Here
u_{f}\in H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
is the unique weak solution of the above problem associated to
f
. We will show that there exists in fact only one such
u_{f}
in Section
4
, and thus
\Lambda_{A}
is well-defined. In order to represent the inaccessibility to the measurements of certain regions of space, which is a very common physical situation, we assume that
\Lambda_{A}f
is known only on
W_{2}
and only for
f\in C^{\infty}_{c}(W_{1})
, where
W_{1},W_{2}\subset\Omega_{e}
are non-empty, open and disjoint subsets of the exterior.

The inverse problem we are interested in can be formulated as follows:

Q1:

Does
\Lambda_{A_{1}}f|_{W_{2}}=\Lambda_{A_{2}}f|_{W_{2}}
for all
f\in C^{\infty}_{c}(W_{1})
imply
A_{1}=A_{2}
?

However, by Remark
3.1
it becomes soon evident that the answer to question
Q1
can not be positive, since the anisotropic fractional conductivity operator
\mathbf{C}^{s}_{A}
possesses a natural gauge related to the properties of symmetry of the matrix
A
. Therefore, we shall rather ask:

Q2:

Does
\Lambda_{A_{1}}f|_{W_{2}}=\Lambda_{A_{2}}f|_{W_{2}}
for all
f\in C^{\infty}_{c}(W_{1})
imply
A_{1,s}=A_{2,s}
?

Here
A_{s}
is a symmetrized version of the matrix
A
, in a sense that will be made clear in the same Remark
3.1
.

The study of the fractional Calderón problem dates back to the seminal article
[
GSU20
]
, which considered the recovery of a bounded potential for the fractional Schrödinger equation in a bounded domain from measurements performed in the exterior in the form of a nonlocal DN map. This result was then generalized in many different directions by the works
[
BGU21
,
GLX17
,
GRSU20
,
Cov22
,
CGFR22
,
CMRU22
,
Li20b
,
Li20a
,
Li21
,
KRS21
,
RS19
]
, to cite a few. These contributions address the problem of uniqueness in the presence of both local and nonlocal perturbations, the problem of stability, and that of reconstruction. The inverse problem we study represents a generalization of the inverse problem for the
isotropic
fractional conductivity equation, which was first introduced in
[
Cov20b
]
and later studied quite extensively in the works
[
Cov20a
,
CRZ22
,
CRTZ22
,
LRZ22
,
RZ22
]
, among others.

Our study is also related to the inverse problem for the anisotropic
classical
conductivity equation. This is one of the main open problems in the field; it is only well understood at the time of writing in dimension
n=2
(see
[
LU01
]
), and only partial results are available in dimension
n\geq 3
(see
[
LTU03
,
LU89
,
DSFKSU07
,
DSFKLS16
]
).

The very recent article
[
FGKU21
]
also bears a relation to ours, in that the authors of this work also consider the problem of uniqueness for a fractional anisotropic conductivity operator. This is however obtained spectrally, and thus generalizes the classical anisotropic conductivity operator in a different way than done here. Therefore, the used techniques and obtained results differ substantially.

In order to answer question
Q2
, we attempt to adopt the technique from
[
GSU20
]
. We first prove the Alessandrini identity, that is, an integral identity which relates the difference of the DN maps
\Lambda_{A_{1}},\Lambda_{A_{2}}
to the differences of the matrices
A_{1},A_{2}
using special solutions to the direct problem. The left-hand side of the Alessandrini identity is then equated to
0
by the assumption on the DN maps. By testing the resulting identity with particular solutions, we are able to deduce the desired result. In order to produce such solutions we prove a Runge approximation property, using a technique involving the Hahn-Banach theorem and the unique continuation property (UCP) for the fractional Laplacian.

However, as observed in
[
CdHS22
, Remark 7.3]
, this technique will not lead to the wanted result by itself. This is due to the fact that the operator appearing in the right-hand side of the Alessandrini identity is nonlocal, which causes problems with the supports of both solutions and data. This issue may be avoided by means of the so called
fractional Liouville reduction
, which transforms a conductivity-type problem in a Schrödinger-type one. This technique, whose name and idea come from the method used for the classical conductivity equation, was introduced in
[
Cov20a
]
for the
isotropic
fractional conductivity equation and later generalized in
[
CdHS22
]
for studying the inverse problem for fractional linear elasticity. Once the reduction is performed (see Section
4
), the direct problem takes the form

(1)

\begin{split}\mathbf{(}-\Delta)^{s-1}Dw+w\cdot Q=0&\quad\mbox{ in }\Omega\\
w=g&\quad\mbox{ in }\Omega_{e}\end{split},

where the new solution
w
and the new exterior datum
g
are respectively computed from
u
and
f
. Here the transformed potential
Q
contains all the information about the matrix
A
, and
D
is a fixed second order differential operator. As it will be shown in Section
6
, the technique based on the Runge approximation property explained above works for the transformed problem. This is due to the fact that the relative Alessandrini identity has a local operator on the right-hand side, namely the matrix multiplication of
Q_{1}-Q_{2}
against two special solutions of the transformed problem.

There is however one more obstacle which needs to be addressed. In both
[
Cov20a
]
and
[
CdHS22
]
the coefficient part of the considered unreduced operators, respectively the matrix
\gamma^{1/2}(x)\gamma^{1/2}(y)
Id and the tensor
C^{1/2}(x):C^{1/2}(y)
, were by definition separable functions of the variables
x
and
y
. This detail is of great importance at the time of proving the fractional Liouville reduction. Since our matrix
A(x,y)
is not in general a separable function of the variables
x
and
y
, we are prevented from applying the fractional Liouville reduction directly. However, in Section
3
we shall prove the existence of a decomposition of the kind

(2)

A_{s}(x,y)=\sum_{k}\Phi_{k}(x)\odot\Phi_{k}(y),

where
\{\Phi_{k}\}_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is a suited sequence of matrices depending on one variable, and
\odot
indicates the Hadamard product (see Section
2
for the definition of this and other matrix operations). Once this formula is achieved, it will be possible to show that a fractional Liouville reduction holds for each element of the above sum. As a consequence, the functions
w,Q
and
g
in the reduced problem will rather be sequences.

In order to obtain a suitable Alessandrini identity for the transformed problem, we need to additionally assume that the anisotropic coefficients are in gauge in the following sense:

Definition 1.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be open and bounded. We say that
A_{1},A_{2}\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n},{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n})
are in gauge if there exists
\rho\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
such that
A_{2,s}(x,y)=(1+\rho(x))(1+\rho(y))A_{1,s}(x,y)
for all
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. In this case we write
A_{1}\sim A_{2}
.

Using the techniques described above, we have obtained the following uniqueness result:

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\Omega,W_{1},W_{2}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be bounded open sets such that
W_{1},W_{2}\subset\Omega_{e}
, and assume
s\in(0,1)
. Let
A_{1},A_{2}\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n},{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n})
be two anisotropic coefficients satisfying assumptions
(A1)
–
(A3)
and
(A4)
. Then
A_{1}\sim A_{2}
and

\Lambda_{A_{1}}f|_{W_{2}}=\Lambda_{A_{2}}f|_{W_{2}}\qquad\mbox{ for all }f\in C^{\infty}_{c}(W_{1})

hold if and only if
A_{1,s}=A_{2,s}
.

Assumptions
(A1)
–
(A3)
describe the Hölder regularity of
A
, its behaviour in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}\setminus\Omega^{2}
, and a uniform positivity condition which is required for the well-posedness of the direct problem (see Section
3.1
). Assumption
(A4)
is only needed for the proof of the Runge approximation property, and it is an isotropicity requirement for the exterior values of the coefficients (see Lemma
6.1
).

1.1.
Organization of the rest of the article

The article is organized as follows. The first section is the Introduction, which presents the main problem, its connection to the literature, the statements of the main results and the techniques involved in their proofs. The second section is dedicated to the necessary Preliminaries and definitions, in particular about tensors, fractional operators, Hölder and Sobolev spaces. Section 3 discusses our assumptions for the anisotropy matrix, introduces the anisotropic fractional conductivity operator, and proves our fundamental reduction result. The fourth section treats the problem of well-posedness both for our original problem and for the transformed one, presenting the fractional Liouville reduction related to our discussion. The DN map and Alessandrini identity are both presented in Section 5, while Section 6 contains the Runge approximation property and the proof of the main theorem. Finally, the last section 7 gives the relation between the classical and fractional problems of anisotropic conductivity in the limit case
s\rightarrow 1
.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Operations on tensors

In this section we introduce a number of operations between tensors and sequences of tensors. Define
{\mathbb{N}}
as the set of positive integers,
{\mathbb{N}}_{0}:={\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\}
as the set of non-negative integers, and
{\mathbb{N}}_{\infty}:={\mathbb{N}}\cup\{\infty\}
as the set of
extended
positive integers. In all the definitions below, we assume that
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
, that
a\in\mathbb{N}_{\infty}^{m},b\in\mathbb{N}_{\infty}^{n}
are two (extended) multi-indices, and that
A,B
are two (extended) tensors respectively given by
A_{\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{m}}
and
B_{\beta_{1},...,\beta_{n}}
, with
\alpha_{i}\in\{1,...,a_{i}\}
for
i=1,...,m
and
\beta_{j}\in\{1,...,b_{j}\}
for
j=1,...,n
.

Definition 2.1

(Tensor product)
.

The tensor
A\otimes B
of elements

(A\otimes B)_{\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{m},\beta_{1},...,\beta_{n}}:=A_{\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{m}}B_{\beta_{1},...,\beta_{n}}

is the
tensor product
of
A
and
B
. If
B
is a scalar, in which case
n=b_{1}=1
, we simply write
AB
for
A\otimes B
.

Definition 2.2

(Hadamard product)
.

Assume
m=n
and
a=b
. The tensor
A\odot B
of elements

(A\odot B)_{\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{m}}:=A_{\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{m}}B_{\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{m}}

is the
Hadamard product
of
A
and
B
.

Definition 2.3

(Tensor contraction of order
k
)
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
verify
k\leq\min\{m,n\}
, and let
a_{m+\ell-k}=b_{\ell}
for all
\ell\in\{1,...,k\}
. The tensor
A\cdot_{k}B
of elements

(A\cdot_{k}B)_{\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{m-k},\beta_{k+1},...,\beta_{n}}:=\sum_{\beta_{1}=1}^{b_{1}}...\sum_{\beta_{k}=1}^{b_{k}}A_{\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{m-k},\beta_{1},...,\beta_{k}}B_{\beta_{1},...,\beta_{n}}

is the
k
-th contraction
of
A
and
B
. For simplicity we use
\cdot
for
\cdot_{1}
,
:
for
\cdot_{2}
, and
\mathinner{\vbox{`:-\hbox{.}\hbox{.}\hbox{.}
}}
for
\cdot_{3}
.

A sequence
\{A_{k}\}_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of (possibly extended) tensors can itself be seen as an extended tensor
A_{k,\alpha_{1},...\alpha_{n}}
with one more index
k
with range in
{\mathbb{N}}
. With this identification in mind, all the above definitions can be extended to sequences of tensors. They can also be naturally extended to functions and function spaces (see
[
CdHS22
]
for more properties relative to these tensor operations).

Remark 2.4
.

It is customary to interpret the symbols
\nabla u,\nabla\cdot u
for the gradient and divergence of a function respectively as a tensor and a scalar product (i.e. a contraction), where
\nabla
formally represents a vector of partial derivatives. Building on this, we use the symbol
\nabla^{2}u:=(\nabla\otimes\nabla)u
for the Hessian matrix of
u
. Similarly, with clear meaning of the symbols,
\nabla^{2}\odot
is the differential operator mapping an
n\times n
matrix
M
to
\nabla^{2}\odot M
, with
(\nabla^{2}\odot M)_{ij}:=\partial_{i}\partial_{j}M_{ij}
.

Observe that here and everywhere else we are
not
assuming the Einstein summation convention on repeated indices.

2.2.
Fractional Sobolev spaces

Let
\hat{u}(\xi)=\mathcal{F}u(\xi):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-ix\cdot\xi}u(x)\,dx
indicate the Fourier transform. If
r
is any real number, we indicate the usual
L^{2}
-based Bessel potential spaces by
H^{r}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, with norm

\|u\|_{H^{r}}:=\|(1+|\xi|^{2})^{r/2}\hat{u}(\xi)\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}.

Let
\Omega,F\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
respectively be an open and a closed set. Following
[
McL00
]
and
[
GSU20
]
, we define the fractional Sobolev spaces

\displaystyle H^{r}(\Omega)

\displaystyle:=\{u|_{\Omega},u\in H^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\},

\displaystyle\widetilde{H}^{r}(\Omega)

\displaystyle:=\,\mbox{closure of }C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)\mbox{ in }H^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),

\displaystyle H^{r}_{F}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

\displaystyle:=\{u\in H^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{n}):\mbox{ supp}(u)\subset F\}.

As usual, the quotient norm
\|u\|_{H^{r}(\Omega)}:=\inf\{\|w\|_{H^{r}}:w\in H^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),w|_{\Omega}=u\}
is used for
H^{r}(\Omega)
. The spaces defined above are related to each other in the following ways:

\widetilde{H}^{r}(\Omega)\subseteq H^{r}(\Omega)\,,\quad\widetilde{H}^{r}(\Omega)\subseteq H^{r}_{\overline{\Omega}}\,,\quad H^{r}_{F}\subseteq H^{r}\,,

(\widetilde{H}^{r}(\Omega))^{*}=H^{-r}(\Omega)\,,\quad(H^{r}(\Omega))^{*}=\widetilde{H}^{-r}(\Omega).

Moreover, if
\partial\Omega
is Lipschitz, then
\widetilde{H}^{r}(\Omega)=H^{r}_{\overline{\Omega}}
for all
r\in\mathbb{R}
(see
[
CMRU22
,
McL00
]
).

2.3.
Fractional operators

Let
\mathcal{S}
be the set of Schwartz functions, and assume
s\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\setminus\mathbb{Z}
. The
fractional Laplacian
is the continuous map from
\mathcal{S}
to
L^{\infty}
defined by

(-\Delta)^{s}u:=\mathcal{F}^{-1}(|\xi|^{2s}\hat{u}(\xi))

for
u\in\mathcal{S}
. One can uniquely extend this definition to have
(-\Delta)^{s}:H^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\rightarrow H^{r-2s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
continuously for all
r\in\mathbb{R}
. It is also possible to have extensions to Sobolev spaces of negative exponent and to
L^{p}
-based Sobolev spaces (see e.g.
[
CMR21
,
GSU20
]
). Alternatively, one can define the fractional Laplacian as the singular integral

(-\Delta)^{s}u(x):=C_{n,s}\,PV\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{u(x)-u(y)}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}dy,

where
C_{n,s}:=\frac{4^{s}\Gamma(n/2+s)}{\pi^{n/2}|\Gamma(-s)|}
. Many other equivalent definitions are also possible (see
[
Kwa17
]
). The fractional Laplacian verifies the UCP (unique continuation property): If
u\in H^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is such that
u=(-\Delta)^{s}u=0
in a non-empty open set
V
, then
u\equiv 0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The proof of this fact can be found in
[
GSU20
]
for
s\in(0,1)
and in
[
CMR21
]
for higher exponents. The latter paper also proves the following fractional Poincaré inequality: If
s\geq t\geq 0
and
u\in H_{K}^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
for a compact set
K\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, then there exists a constant
\widetilde{c}=\widetilde{c}(n,K,s)>0
such that

\left\lVert(-\Delta)^{t/2}u\right\rVert_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}\leq\widetilde{c}\left\lVert(-\Delta)^{s/2}u\right\rVert_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}.

We also need the fractional gradient and divergence operators
\nabla^{s}
and
(\nabla\cdot)^{s}
. These were first introduced in the nonlocal vector calculus developed in
[
DGLZ12
,
DGLZ13
]
, and were later studied in their present form in
[
Cov20a
,
Cov20b
,
CMR21
,
CRZ22
,
CdHS22
]
and more. Let
u\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
s\in(0,1)
. The fractional gradient of
u
is the two-points function given by

\nabla^{s}u(x,y):=(u(y)-u(x))\otimes\zeta(x,y),\quad\mbox{ where }\zeta(x,y):=\frac{C_{n,s}^{1/2}}{\sqrt{2}}\frac{x-y}{|x-y|^{n/2+s+1}}.

This definition can be uniquely extended to act as
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\rightarrow L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
. The adjoint of
\nabla^{s}
, which is indicated by
(\nabla\cdot)^{s}
and acts as
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})\rightarrow H^{-s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, is by definition the fractional divergence. One can observe that the property

(\nabla\cdot)^{s}\nabla^{s}u=(-\Delta)^{s}u

holds in
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Because of their mapping properties and of the particular relation they have among themselves and with the Laplacian, the fractional gradient and divergence can be though of as nonlocal counterparts of the classical gradient and divergence operators.

2.4.
Hölder spaces

In this Section we introduce the Hölder spaces and their main properties, following
[
Tay96
]
(see also
[
CdHS22
]
). If
r\in[0,1)
and
k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, we define

(3)

\displaystyle C^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{n}):=\{u:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\rightarrow{\mathbb{C}}\;:\;u\mbox{ bounded and }|u(x)-u(y)|\leq C|x-y|^{r}\mbox{ for all }x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\},

(4)

\displaystyle C^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n}):=\{u:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\rightarrow{\mathbb{C}}\;:\;D^{\beta}u\mbox{ bounded continuous for all }|\beta|\leq k\},

(5)

\displaystyle C^{r+k}(\mathbb{R}^{n}):=\{u\in C^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\;:\;D^{\beta}u\in C^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\mbox{ for all }|\beta|=k\}.

The Hölder norm of
u\in C^{r+k}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
is defined as

\|u\|_{C^{r+k}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}:=\max_{|\beta|\leq k}\|D^{\beta}u\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}+\max_{|\beta|=k}\sup_{x\neq y}\frac{|D^{\beta}u(x)-D^{\beta}u(y)|}{|x-y|^{r}}.

Moreover, the following properties are known to hold:

(H1)

Continuous embedding property:

If
r,r^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
are such that
r\leq r^{\prime}
, then
C^{r^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\hookrightarrow C^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

(H2)

Closure under composition with smooth functions:

If
r\in{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
,
u\in C^{r}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
and
F\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}})
, then
F(u)\in C^{r}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
as well.

(H3)

Algebra property:

If
r\in{\mathbb{R}}^{+}\setminus\mathbb{Z}^{+}
and
u,v\in C^{r}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, then
uv\in C^{r}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
as well.

(H4)

Mapping property for pseudodifferential operators:

If
r\in{\mathbb{R}}^{+}\setminus\mathbb{Z}^{+}
and
m\in{\mathbb{R}}
verify
r-m\in{\mathbb{R}}^{+}\setminus\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, and
\Psi\in OPS^{m}_{1,0}
is a
\Psi
DO with symbol in the Hörmander class
S^{m}_{1,0}
, then
\Psi
maps
C^{r}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
continuously to
C^{r-m}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
.

(H5)

Mapping property for
(-\Delta)^{s-1}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}
:

If
r\in{\mathbb{R}}^{+}\setminus\mathbb{Z}^{+}
and
s\in(0,1)
verify
r-2s\in{\mathbb{R}}^{+}\setminus\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, then
(-\Delta)^{s-1}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}
maps
C^{r}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
continuously to
C^{r-2s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
.

We will also make use of the following Lemma:

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
s\in(0,1)
and
\varepsilon>0
. If
h\in C^{s+\varepsilon}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
and
u\in H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, then
hu\in H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
as well, and there exists a constant
C=C(n,s,\varepsilon)>0
such that

\|hu\|_{H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}\leq C\|h\|_{C^{s+\varepsilon}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}\|u\|_{H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}.

Proof.

Let
u,h
be as in the statement. Then we know that

\|hu\|^{2}_{H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}\lesssim\|hu\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}+[hu]^{2}_{H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})},

(see e.g.
[
DNPV12
]
) where the Gagliardo seminorm is given by

[hu]^{2}_{H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}:=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\frac{|h(x)u(x)-h(y)u(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}dxdy.

Since
h\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, the first term on the right hand side is immediately estimated as

\|hu\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}\leq\|h\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}\|u\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}\leq\|h\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}\|u\|_{H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}.

For the seminorm we compute

\displaystyle[hu]^{2}_{H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\frac{|h(x)|^{2}|u(x)-u(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}dxdy+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\frac{|h(x)-h(y)|^{2}|u(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}dxdy=:I_{1}+I_{2},

and then observe that

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\|h\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}^{2}[u]^{2}_{H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}\leq\|h\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}^{2}\|u\|^{2}_{H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})},

so that we only need to estimate
I_{2}
. With a change of variables we get

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}|u(y)|^{2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\frac{|h(y+z)-h(y)|^{2}}{|z|^{n+2s}}dzdy,

and then we split the second integral as follows, where
B
is the unitary ball centered at the origin:

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\frac{|h(y+z)-h(y)|^{2}}{|z|^{n+2s}}dz

\displaystyle=\int_{B}\frac{|h(y+z)-h(y)|^{2}}{|z|^{n+2s}}dz+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\setminus B}\frac{|h(y+z)-h(y)|^{2}}{|z|^{n+2s}}dz

\displaystyle\lesssim\|h\|_{C^{s+\varepsilon}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}^{2}\int_{B}\frac{|z|^{2s+2\varepsilon}}{|z|^{n+2s}}dz+\|h\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\setminus B}\frac{1}{|z|^{n+2s}}dz.

Observe that the remaining integrals converge to constant values depending on
n,s,\varepsilon
. Thus

I_{2}\lesssim\|h\|_{C^{s+\varepsilon}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}^{2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}|u(y)|^{2}dy=\|h\|_{C^{s+\varepsilon}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}^{2}\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}\leq\|h\|_{C^{s+\varepsilon}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}^{2}\|u\|^{2}_{H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}.

∎

2.5.
Spaces of tensors

If
A=A_{\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{m}}
with
\alpha_{i}\in\{1,...,a_{i}\}
for
i=1,...,m
and
a\in\mathbb{N}_{\infty}^{m}
for
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
is any (extended) tensor whose elements belong to
C^{r}
,
r\in{\mathbb{R}}
, we define its
C^{r}
norm as

\|A\|_{C^{r}}:=\sum_{\alpha_{1}=1}^{a_{1}}...\sum_{\alpha_{m}=1}^{a_{m}}\|A_{\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{m}}\|_{C^{r}}.

Similarly, if
B=B_{\beta_{1},...,\beta_{n}}
with
\beta_{j}\in\{1,...,b_{j}\}
for
j=1,...,n
and
b\in\mathbb{N}_{\infty}^{n}
for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
is any (extended) tensor whose elements belong to
H^{r}
, we define its
H^{r}
norm as

\|B\|_{H^{r}}:=\sum_{\beta_{1}=1}^{b_{1}}...\sum_{\beta_{n}=1}^{b_{n}}\|B_{\beta_{1},...,\beta_{n}}\|_{H^{r}}.

The result of Lemma
2.5
extends to products of tensors: if
A\in C^{r+\varepsilon}
and
B\in H^{r}
, then
A\otimes B,A\odot B
and
A\cdot_{k}B
all belong to
H^{r}
whenever the operations make sense, since

(6)

\begin{split}\|A\otimes B\|_{H^{r}}&=\sum_{\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{m},\beta_{1},...,\beta_{n}}\|A_{\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{m}}B_{\beta_{1},...,\beta_{n}}\|_{H^{r}}\\
&\lesssim\sum_{\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{m},\beta_{1},...,\beta_{n}}\|A_{\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{m}}\|_{C^{r+\varepsilon}}\|B_{\beta_{1},...,\beta_{n}}\|_{H^{r}}\\
&=\sum_{\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{m}}\|A_{\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{m}}\|_{C^{r+\varepsilon}}\sum_{\beta_{1},...,\beta_{n}}\|B_{\beta_{1},...,\beta_{n}}\|_{H^{r}}=\|A\|_{C^{r+\varepsilon}}\|B\|_{H^{r}},\end{split}

(7)

\begin{split}\|A\odot B\|_{H^{r}}&=\sum_{\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{m}}\|A_{\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{m}}B_{\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{m}}\|_{H^{r}}\\
&\lesssim\sum_{\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{m}}\|A_{\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{m}}\|_{C^{r+\varepsilon}}\|B_{\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{m}}\|_{H^{r}}\\
&\leq\sum_{\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{m}}\|A_{\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{m}}\|_{C^{r+\varepsilon}}\sum_{\beta_{1},...,\beta_{n}}\|B_{\beta_{1},...,\beta_{n}}\|_{H^{r}}=\|A\|_{C^{r+\varepsilon}}\|B\|_{H^{r}},\end{split}

and

(8)

\begin{split}\|A\cdot_{k}B\|_{H^{r}}&=\sum_{\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{m-k},\beta_{k+1},...,\beta_{n}}\|\sum_{\beta_{1},...,\beta_{k}}A_{\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{m-k},\beta_{1},...,\beta_{k}}B_{\beta_{1},...,\beta_{n}}\|_{H^{r}}\\
&\leq\sum_{\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{m-k},\beta_{1},...,\beta_{n}}\left\|A_{\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{m-k},\beta_{1},...,\beta_{k}}B_{\beta_{1},...,\beta_{n}}\right\|_{H^{r}}\\
&\lesssim\sum_{\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{m-k},\beta_{1},...,\beta_{n}}\left\|A_{\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{m-k},\beta_{1},...,\beta_{k}}\|_{C^{r+\varepsilon}}\|B_{\beta_{1},...,\beta_{n}}\right\|_{H^{r}}\\
&\leq\sum_{\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{m}}\|A_{\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{m}}\|_{C^{r+\varepsilon}}\sum_{\beta_{1},...,\beta_{n}}\|B_{\beta_{1},...,\beta_{n}}\|_{H^{r}}=\|A\|_{C^{r+\varepsilon}}\|B\|_{H^{r}}.\end{split}

Moreover, if
A\neq 0
we have
A/|A|^{2}\in C^{r+\varepsilon}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
by
(H2)
, since the function
F(x)=x/|x|^{2}
is smooth away from the origin. Therefore by the above computation

(9)

\begin{split}\|B\|_{H^{r}}=\left\|\frac{A}{|A|^{2}}\cdot_{m}(A\otimes B)\right\|_{H^{r}}&\lesssim\left\|\frac{A}{|A|^{2}}\right\|_{C^{r+\varepsilon}}\left\|A\otimes B\right\|_{H^{r}}.\end{split}

It now easily follows that if
A\in C^{r+\varepsilon}
, then
A\otimes H^{r}
is a Hilbert subspace of
H^{r}
.

Remark 2.6
.

We might as well have chosen to define

(10)

\|A\|^{2}_{C^{r}}:=\sum_{\alpha_{1}=1}^{a_{1}}...\sum_{\alpha_{m}=1}^{a_{m}}\|A_{\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{m}}\|^{2}_{C^{r}},

and similarly for the
H^{r}
norm of a tensor. If the tensors are not extended, i.e. if
a\in\mathbb{N}^{m}
rather than
\mathbb{N}_{\infty}^{m}
, the two definitions of course coincide in light of the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality for series, since

\sum_{\alpha_{1}=1}^{a_{1}}...\sum_{\alpha_{m}=1}^{a_{m}}\|A_{\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{m}}\|_{C^{r}}\leq\left(\sum_{\alpha_{1}=1}^{a_{1}}...\sum_{\alpha_{m}=1}^{a_{m}}\|A_{\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{m}}\|^{2}_{C^{r}}\right)^{1/2}\prod_{j=1}^{m}a_{j}^{1/2}

and

\sum_{\alpha_{1}=1}^{a_{1}}...\sum_{\alpha_{m}=1}^{a_{m}}\|A_{\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{m}}\|^{2}_{C^{r}}\leq\left(\sum_{\alpha_{1}=1}^{a_{1}}...\sum_{\alpha_{m}=1}^{a_{m}}\|A_{\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{m}}\|_{C^{r}}\right)^{2}.

However, since the first inequality does not hold for extended tensors, the two definitions are not equivalent for
a\in\mathbb{N}_{\infty}^{m}
. In particular, definition (
10
) would not allow us to get the desired estimate for
\|A\cdot_{k}B\|_{H^{r}}
.

3.
The anisotropic fractional conductivity equation

3.1.
Assumptions

Any square matrix
A
depending on two variables
x,y
is linked to two different concepts of symmetry. One can define the
matrix-wise symmetric part

A_{ms}
of
A
as

A_{ms}(x,y):=\frac{A(x,y)+A^{T}(x,y)}{2},

where
T
indicates transposition, and the
variable-wise symmetric part

A_{vs}
of
A
as

A_{vs}(x,y):=\frac{A(x,y)+A(y,x)}{2}.

One sees that
A_{ms}
is a symmetric matrix in the sense that
A_{ms}=A_{ms}^{T}
, while
A_{vs}
is a symmetric function of the variables
x,y
in the sense that
A_{vs}(x,y)=A_{vs}(y,x)
. We also define the
(matrix-wise and variable-wise) antisymmetric parts
of
A
as

A_{ma}:=A-A_{ms},\qquad A_{va}:=A-A_{vs}.

Thus we can write

A=A_{vs}+A_{va}=(A_{vs})_{ms}+(A_{vs})_{ma}+A_{va}=A_{s}+A_{a},

where
A_{s}:=(A_{vs})_{ms}
is symmetric both matrix-wise and variable-wise, and
A_{a}:=(A_{vs})_{ma}+A_{va}
.

Let now
s\in(0,1)
and
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be bounded and open. Moreover, let
A\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n},{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n})
verify the following assumptions:

(A1)

There exist two families of matrix-wise symmetric functions
\{\beta_{k}\}_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\subset L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n})
, each constant in
\Omega_{e}
, and
\{\phi_{k}\}_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\subset L^{2}(\Omega,{\mathbb{C}}^{n\times n})
, each compactly supported in
\Omega
, such that for almost every
(x,y)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}

(11)

A_{s}(x,y)=\tilde{a}(x,y)+\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\phi_{k}(x)\odot\phi_{k}(y),

where

\tilde{a}(x,y):=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\beta_{k}(x)\odot\beta_{k}(y)

coincides with
A_{s}(x,y)
whenever
(x,y)\not\in\Omega^{2}
. We also define a third family of matrix-wise symmetric functions
\{\Phi_{k}\}_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
given by

\Phi_{2k}:=\beta_{k},\qquad\Phi_{2k+1}:=\phi_{k}\qquad\qquad\mbox{for all }k\in{\mathbb{N}},

so that we can write

(12)

A_{s}(x,y)=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\Phi_{k}(x)\odot\Phi_{k}(y).

(A2)

A_{s}
is uniformly positive definite, i.e. there exists a constant
\nu>0
such that

A_{s}(x,y):(\xi\otimes\xi)\geq\nu|\xi|^{2},\qquad\mbox{ for all }\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\mbox{ and }x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}.

(A3)

There exists
\varepsilon>0
such that
\Phi\in C^{2s+\varepsilon}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
.

In the next section we will use these assumptions in order to construct the anisotropic fractional conductivity operator having
A
as coefficient matrix. The rest of this section is dedicated to comments about the assumptions.

•

Assumption
(A3)
implies that the functions
\beta_{k}
and
\phi_{k}
belong to
C^{2s+\varepsilon}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
for all
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
. It also gives
A_{s}\in C^{2s+\varepsilon}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
, since

\displaystyle\|A_{s}\|_{C^{2s+\varepsilon}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\|\Phi_{k}(x)\odot\Phi_{k}(y)\|_{C^{2s+\varepsilon}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\|\Phi_{k,ij}(x)\Phi_{k,ij}(y)\|_{C^{2s+\varepsilon}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\|\Phi_{k,ij}\|_{C^{2s+\varepsilon}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\|\Phi_{k,ij}\|_{C^{2s+\varepsilon}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}^{2}\leq\|\Phi\|_{C^{2s+\varepsilon}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}^{2},

where at the third step we used property
(H3)
, and at the following one just the definition of Hölder norm. Similarly,
\tilde{a}\in C^{2s+\varepsilon}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
.

•

The particular structure of the exterior values
\tilde{a}
of
A_{s}
emerges naturally as a generalization of the exterior condition for the isotropic fractional conductivity equation studied in
[
Cov20a
]
. In that case it holds

A(x,y)=A_{s}(x,y)=\gamma^{1/2}(x)\gamma^{1/2}(y)Id=(\gamma^{1/2}(x)Id)\odot(\gamma^{1/2}(y)Id),

and thus it is sufficient to take
\tilde{a}=A
.

•

The existence of a sequence
\{\phi_{k}\}_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
as in
(A1)
such that (
11
) holds can be proved in the assumption that the scalar function
(A_{s}-\tilde{a})_{ij}
is compactly supported in
\Omega^{2}
. Fix
i,j\in\{1,...,n\}
, and let
\psi:=(A_{s}-\tilde{a})_{ij}
. In this case we define the Hilbert-Schmidt integral operator
\Psi:L^{2}(\Omega)\rightarrow L^{2}(\Omega)
by

\Psi u(x):=\int_{\Omega}\psi(x,y)u(y)dy.

Since
\psi
is real-valued, symmetric in
x,y
, and compactly supported, the operator
\Psi
is compact and self-adjoint. By the spectral theorem there exists an orthonormal basis
\{\phi_{i}\}_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of
L^{2}(\Omega)
composed of eigenfunctions of
\Psi
with real eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
such that
|\lambda_{1}|\geq|\lambda_{2}|\geq...\geq 0
. One immediately sees that the new double sequence
\{\phi_{i}(x)\phi_{j}(y)\}_{i,j\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\Omega^{2})
. By writing
\psi
in this basis, we see that for all
x,y\in\Omega

\displaystyle\psi(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\left(\int_{\Omega\times\Omega}\psi(w,z)\phi_{i}(w)\phi_{j}(z)dwdz\right)\phi_{i}(x)\phi_{j}(y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\left(\int_{\Omega}\phi_{i}(w)\Psi\phi_{j}(w)dw\right)\phi_{i}(x)\phi_{j}(y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\lambda_{j}\left(\int_{\Omega}\phi_{i}(w)\phi_{j}(w)dw\right)\phi_{i}(x)\phi_{j}(y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\phi_{i}(x)\phi_{i}(y),

since we have the equalities

\Psi\phi_{j}=\lambda_{j}\phi_{j},\quad\mbox{for all }j\in{\mathbb{N}},\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\int_{\Omega}\phi_{i}(w)\phi_{j}(w)dw=\delta_{ij}.

If
\phi^{\prime}_{i}
is the extension by
0
of
\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}\phi_{i}
from
\Omega
to
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, then the decomposition

\psi(x,y)=\sum_{i}\phi^{\prime}_{i}(x)\phi^{\prime}_{i}(y)

holds for all
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, and formula (
11
) follows. Thus we see that the decomposition given in equation (
11
) is actually an assumption just on the exterior values of
A_{s}
.

If in particular
\psi
is a positive semidefinite kernel in
\Omega^{2}
, i.e.

\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\psi(x,y)g(x)g(y)dxdy\geq 0,\qquad\mbox{for all }g\in L^{2}(\Omega),

then by Mercer’s theorem
[
Jö82
, Section 8.7]
all the eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}
are non-negative,

\mbox{tr}\Psi:=\int_{\Omega}\psi(x,x)dx=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}<\infty,

and the functions
\phi^{\prime}_{i}
are real valued.

•

If
A_{1}\sim A_{2}
as in Definition
1.1
, then the decomposition (
12
) of
A_{2,s}
can be taken such that
\Phi_{2,k}(x)=(1+\rho(x))\Phi_{1,k}(x)
for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
. In particular,
\tilde{a}_{1}=\tilde{a}_{2}
on
(\Omega_{e})^{2}
.

3.2.
The operator

For this subsection we do not need any of the assumptions
(A1)
-
(A3)
. The fact that
A\in L^{\infty}
, the mapping properties of the fractional gradient
\nabla^{s}
and the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality are enough to imply that
A\cdot\nabla^{s}u\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
for all
u\in H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
. With this in mind, we can define the
anisotropic fractional conductivity operator

\mathbf{C}^{s}_{A}:H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})\rightarrow H^{-s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
as

\mathbf{C}^{s}_{A}u:=(\nabla\cdot)^{s}(A(x,y)\cdot\nabla^{s}u)

for
u\in H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
. If there exists
\sigma:{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
such that
A(x,y)=\sigma(x,y)Id
, the fractional conductivity operator is said to be
isotropic
, and

\mathbf{C}^{s}_{\sigma Id}u=(\nabla\cdot)^{s}(\sigma(x,y)\nabla^{s}u).

If the two-points function
\sigma
admits the decomposition
\sigma(x,y)=\gamma^{1/2}(x)\gamma^{1/2}(y)
for some function
\gamma:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
, then we recover the usual fractional conductivity operator
\mathbf{C}^{s}_{\gamma}
studied in
[
Cov20a
]
. Finally, in the case
\gamma(x)\equiv 1
our operator reduces to the fractional Laplacian
(-\Delta)^{s}
.

Remark 3.1
.

For any
u,v\in H^{s}
we see that

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{C}^{s}_{A}u,v\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle A,\nabla^{s}v\otimes\nabla^{s}u\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle A_{s},\nabla^{s}v\otimes\nabla^{s}u\rangle+\langle(A_{vs})_{ma},\nabla^{s}v\otimes\nabla^{s}u\rangle+\langle A_{va},\nabla^{s}v\otimes\nabla^{s}u\rangle.

Observe that the matrix
\nabla^{s}v\otimes\nabla^{s}u
is symmetric both matrix- and variable-wise. This makes the last two terms on the right hand side vanish, and we are left with

(13)

\langle\mathbf{C}^{s}_{A}u,v\rangle=\langle A_{s},\nabla^{s}v\otimes\nabla^{s}u\rangle=\langle\mathbf{C}^{s}_{A_{s}}u,v\rangle.

This means that the operator
\mathbf{C}^{s}_{A}
actually contains no information about
A_{a}
, which is therefore unrecoverable. Thus the anisotropic fractional conductivity equation possesses a natural gauge. This is reminiscent of the situation emerging for the fractional magnetic Schrödinger equation studied in
[
Cov20b
]
.

Lemma 3.2
.

The operator
\mathbf{C}^{s}_{A}
is self-adjoint.

Proof.

By Remark
3.1
we have

\langle\mathbf{C}^{s}_{A}u,v\rangle=\langle\mathbf{C}^{s}_{A_{s}}u,v\rangle=\langle u,\mathbf{C}^{s}_{A_{s}}v\rangle=\langle u,\mathbf{C}^{s}_{A}v\rangle,

since the symmetries of
A_{s}
ensure that
C^{s}_{A_{s}}
is itself self-adjoint.
∎

3.3.
The reduction lemma

The next result is a reduction lemma, by means of which we can write
\mathbf{C}^{s}_{A}
as a combination of fractional Laplacian operators acting on sequences. This result is related to the fractional Liouville reduction studied in
[
Cov20a
]
for the isotropic, separable case, and it also resembles the reduction lemma presented in
[
CdHS22
]
for the fractional elasticity equation.

Lemma 3.3
.

In weak sense it holds that

\mathbf{C}^{s}_{A}u=\Phi\mathinner{\vbox{`:-\hbox{.}\hbox{.}\hbox{.}
}}\left\{(-\Delta)^{s-1}D(\Phi u)+(\Phi u)\mathinner{\vbox{`:-\hbox{.}\hbox{.}\hbox{.}
}}Q\right\},

where
D:=-\frac{\left(\Delta Id+2s\nabla^{2}\right)\odot}{n+2s}
is a second order operator acting between
n\times n
matrices and
Q:=-\frac{\Phi\otimes(-\Delta)^{s-1}D\Phi}{|\Phi|^{2}}
is the transformed potential.

Proof.

Let
u,v\in H^{s}
, and compute

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{C}^{s}_{A}u,v\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle A_{s}\cdot\nabla^{s}u,\nabla^{s}v\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}(\Phi_{k}(x)\odot\Phi_{k}(y))\cdot\nabla^{s}u,\nabla^{s}v\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\langle(\Phi_{k}(x)\odot\Phi_{k}(y))\cdot[(u(y)-u(x))\otimes\zeta],(v(y)-v(x))\otimes\zeta\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\langle(u(y)-u(x))(\Phi_{k}(x)\odot\Phi_{k}(y)):(\zeta\otimes\zeta),v(y)-v(x)\rangle.

Observe that the order of summation and integration can be exchanged, given that for all
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
and almost all
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}

\displaystyle|(\Phi_{k}(x)\odot\Phi_{k}(y)):(\nabla^{s}v\otimes\nabla^{s}u)|

\displaystyle\leq|\Phi_{k}(x)\odot\Phi_{k}(y)|\,|\nabla^{s}v\otimes\nabla^{s}u|

\displaystyle\leq|\Phi_{k}(x)|\,|\Phi_{k}(y)|\,|\nabla^{s}u|\,|\nabla^{s}v|

\displaystyle\leq\|\Phi_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}^{2}|\nabla^{s}u|\,|\nabla^{s}v|

\displaystyle\leq\|\Phi_{k}\|_{C^{2s+\varepsilon}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}^{2}|\nabla^{s}u|\,|\nabla^{s}v|

\displaystyle\leq\|\Phi\|_{C^{2s+\varepsilon}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}^{2}|\nabla^{s}u|\,|\nabla^{s}v|=:g(x,y),

and
g\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
. For constants
\alpha=(n+2s)^{-1}
and
\beta=(n+2s)^{-1}(n+2s-2)^{-1}
we have (see e.g.
[
CdHS22
]
)

\frac{(x-y)\otimes(x-y)}{|x-y|^{n+2s+2}}=\alpha Id|x-y|^{-(n+2s)}-\beta\nabla_{y}\nabla_{x}(|x-y|^{-(n+2s-2)}),

and thus

\zeta\otimes\zeta=\frac{C_{n,s}}{2}\left(\alpha Id|x-y|^{-(n+2s)}-\beta\nabla_{y}\nabla_{x}(|x-y|^{-(n+2s-2)})\right).

It follows that we can write
\langle\mathbf{C}^{s}_{A}u,v\rangle=\sum_{k}\left(\alpha I_{1,k}-\frac{C_{n,s}}{2}\beta I_{2,k}\right)
, with

\displaystyle I_{1,k}

\displaystyle:=\frac{C_{n,s}}{2}\langle Id:(\Phi_{k}(x)\odot\Phi_{k}(y))(u(y)-u(x))|x-y|^{-(n+2s)},v(y)-v(x)\rangle,

and

I_{2,k}:=\langle(u(y)-u(x))(\Phi_{k}(x)\odot\Phi_{k}(y)):\nabla_{y}\nabla_{x}(|x-y|^{-(n+2s-2)}),v(y)-v(x)\rangle.

For
I_{1,k}
we compute

\displaystyle I_{1,k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\langle\Phi_{k,i,i}(x)\Phi_{k,i,i}(y)\nabla^{s}u,\nabla^{s}v\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\langle\Phi_{k,i,i}(-\Delta)^{s}(\Phi_{k,i,i}u)-\Phi_{k,i,i}u(-\Delta)^{s}\Phi_{k,i,i},v\rangle

\displaystyle=Id:\langle\Phi_{k}\odot(-\Delta)^{s}(\Phi_{k}u)-(\Phi_{k}u)\odot(-\Delta)^{s}\Phi_{k},v\rangle,

where at the second line we recognized the scalar fractional conductivity operator studied in
[
Cov20a
]
with conductivity
\Phi_{k,i,i}^{2}
, and thus applied the usual fractional Liouville reduction. We want to compute the second term
I_{2,k}
integrating by parts. We let
w_{k}:=\Phi_{k}u
and compute

\displaystyle I_{2,k}

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}}(\Phi_{k}(x)\odot w_{k}(y)-w_{k}(x)\odot\Phi_{k}(y))(v(y)-v(x)):\nabla_{y}\nabla_{x}(|x-y|^{-(n+2s-2)})dydx

\displaystyle=-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}}(\nabla_{y}\cdot)\left\{(\Phi_{k}(x)\odot w_{k}(y)-w_{k}(x)\odot\Phi_{k}(y))(v(y)-v(x))\right\}\cdot\nabla_{x}(|x-y|^{-(n+2s-2)})dydx

\displaystyle=-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}}\left\{(v(y)-v(x))\nabla_{y}\cdot[(\Phi_{k}(x)\odot w_{k}(y)-w_{k}(x)\odot\Phi_{k}(y))]+\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\left.\nabla v(y)\cdot[(\Phi_{k}(x)\odot w_{k}(y)-w_{k}(x)\odot\Phi_{k}(y))]\right\}\cdot\nabla_{x}(|x-y|^{-(n+2s-2)})dydx

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}}(\nabla_{x}\cdot)\left\{(v(y)-v(x))(\nabla_{y}\cdot)[(\Phi_{k}(x)\odot w_{k}(y)-w_{k}(x)\odot\Phi_{k}(y))]+\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\left.\nabla v(y)\cdot[(\Phi_{k}(x)\odot w_{k}(y)-w_{k}(x)\odot\Phi_{k}(y))]\right\}|x-y|^{-(n+2s-2)}dydx

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}}\left\{(v(y)-v(x))(\nabla_{x}\cdot)(\nabla_{y}\cdot)[(\Phi_{k}(x)\odot w_{k}(y)-w_{k}(x)\odot\Phi_{k}(y))]+\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad-\nabla v(x)\cdot(\nabla_{y}\cdot)[(\Phi_{k}(x)\odot w_{k}(y)-w_{k}(x)\odot\Phi_{k}(y))]+

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\left.\nabla v(y)\cdot(\nabla_{x}\cdot)[(\Phi_{k}(x)\odot w_{k}(y)-w_{k}(x)\odot\Phi_{k}(y))]\right\}|x-y|^{-(n+2s-2)}dydx.

Now observe that

\{(\nabla_{y}\cdot)(A(x)\odot B(y))\}_{j}=\sum_{i}\partial_{y_{i}}(A_{ij}(x)B_{ij}(y))=\sum_{i}A_{ij}(x)\partial_{y_{i}}B_{ij}(y)

and similarly

(\nabla_{x}\cdot)(\nabla_{y}\cdot)(A(x)\odot B(y))=\sum_{j}\partial_{x_{j}}\{(\nabla_{y}\cdot)(A(x)\odot B(y))\}_{j}=\sum_{i,j}\partial_{x_{j}}A_{ij}(x)\partial_{y_{i}}B_{ij}(y).

This implies

\displaystyle I_{2,k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}}v(y)\{\partial_{j}\Phi_{k,ij}(x)\partial_{i}w_{k,ij}(y)-\partial_{j}w_{k,ij}(x)\partial_{i}\Phi_{k,ij}(y)\}|x-y|^{-(n+2s-2)}dydx

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{i,j}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}}\partial_{j}v(y)\{\partial_{i}\Phi_{k,ij}(x)w_{k,ij}(y)-\partial_{i}w_{k,ij}(x)\Phi_{k,ij}(y)\}|x-y|^{-(n+2s-2)}dydx

\displaystyle\quad-\sum_{i,j}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}}v(x)\{\partial_{j}\Phi_{k,ij}(x)\partial_{i}w_{k,ij}(y)-\partial_{j}w_{k,ij}(x)\partial_{i}\Phi_{k,ij}(y)\}|x-y|^{-(n+2s-2)}dydx

\displaystyle\quad-\sum_{i,j}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}}\partial_{j}v(x)\{\Phi_{k,ij}(x)\partial_{i}w_{k,ij}(y)-w_{k,ij}(x)\partial_{i}\Phi_{k,ij}(y)\}|x-y|^{-(n+2s-2)}dydx.

Now we exchange
x
and
y
in the last two integrals, observing that the
n+2s-2<n
exponent ensures the well-definiteness of all the integrals involved. At this step we also use that
\Phi_{k}
and
w_{k}
are symmetric matrices for all values of
k
. We get

\displaystyle\frac{I_{2,k}}{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}}v(y)\{\partial_{j}\Phi_{k,ij}(x)\partial_{i}w_{k,ij}(y)-\partial_{j}w_{k,ij}(x)\partial_{i}\Phi_{k,ij}(y)\}|x-y|^{-(n+2s-2)}dydx

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{i,j}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}}\partial_{j}v(y)\{\partial_{i}\Phi_{k,ij}(x)w_{k,ij}(y)-\partial_{i}w_{k,ij}(x)\Phi_{k,ij}(y)\}|x-y|^{-(n+2s-2)}dydx.

Let us rewrite the last formula equivalently as

\displaystyle\frac{C_{n,s-1}I_{2,k}}{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}v\{R\partial_{j}\Phi_{k,ij}\,\partial_{i}w_{k,ij}-R\partial_{j}w_{k,ij}\,\partial_{i}\Phi_{k,ij}\}dy

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{i,j}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\partial_{i}v\{R\partial_{j}\Phi_{k,ij}\,w_{k,ij}-R\partial_{j}w_{k,ij}\,\Phi_{k,ij}\}dy,

where for the sake of readability we let
R:=(-\Delta)^{s-1}
. Since
R
commutes with the derivatives, one last integration by parts in the last integral gives

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\partial_{i}v\{R\partial_{j}\Phi_{k,ij}\,w_{k,ij}-R\partial_{j}w_{k,ij}\,\Phi_{k,ij}\}dy

\displaystyle=-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}v\partial_{i}\{R\partial_{j}\Phi_{k,ij}\,w_{k,ij}-R\partial_{j}w_{k,ij}\,\Phi_{k,ij}\}dy

\displaystyle=-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}v\{w_{k,ij}\,R\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\Phi_{k,ij}-\Phi_{k,ij}\,R\partial_{i}\partial_{j}w_{k,ij}\}dy

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}v\{\partial_{i}w_{k,ij}\,R\partial_{j}\Phi_{k,ij}-\partial_{i}\Phi_{k,ij}\,R\partial_{j}w_{k,ij}\}dy,

and so eventually

\displaystyle\frac{C_{n,s-1}I_{2,k}}{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}v\{\Phi_{k,ij}\,R\partial_{i}\partial_{j}w_{k,ij}-w_{k,ij}\,R\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\Phi_{k,ij}\}dy

\displaystyle=\langle\Phi_{k}:(-\Delta)^{s-1}(\nabla^{2}\odot w_{k})-w_{k}:(-\Delta)^{s-1}(\nabla^{2}\odot\Phi_{k}),v\rangle.

Putting together the computations for
I_{1,k}
and
I_{2,k}
we get the following equality

\displaystyle\mathbf{\langle}\mathbf{C}^{s}_{A}u,v\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\left(\alpha I_{1,k}-\frac{C_{n,s}}{2}\beta I_{2,k}\right)

\displaystyle=\alpha\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}Id:\langle\Phi_{k}\odot(-\Delta)^{s}w_{k}-w_{k}\odot(-\Delta)^{s}\Phi_{k},v\rangle

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{C_{n,s}\beta}{C_{n,s-1}}\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\langle w_{k}:(-\Delta)^{s-1}(\nabla^{2}\odot\Phi_{k})-\Phi_{k}:(-\Delta)^{s-1}(\nabla^{2}\odot w_{k}),v\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\langle\Phi_{k}:(-\Delta)^{s-1}Dw_{k}-w_{k}:(-\Delta)^{s-1}D\Phi_{k},v\rangle

\displaystyle=\lim_{K\rightarrow\infty}\langle\sum_{k=1}^{K}(\Phi_{k}:(-\Delta)^{s-1}Dw_{k}-w_{k}:(-\Delta)^{s-1}D\Phi_{k}),v\rangle=:\lim_{K\rightarrow\infty}F_{K}(v),

where the differential operator with constant coefficients
D
is

D:=-\left(\alpha\Delta Id+\beta\frac{C_{n,s}}{C_{n,s-1}}\nabla^{2}\right)\odot=-\frac{\left(\Delta Id+2s\nabla^{2}\right)\odot}{n+2s}.

By Lemma
2.5
and property
(H1)
,
\Phi_{k}
is a multiplier on
H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
(and thus also in
H^{-s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, see
[
MS09
,
CMRU22
]
) for every
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Moreover,
(-\Delta)^{s-1}D\Phi_{k}\in C^{\varepsilon}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
by property
(H5)
. This ensures that the operators
F_{K}
belong to
H^{-s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
for all
K\in{\mathbb{N}}
. They converge to
F:=\Phi\mathinner{\vbox{`:-\hbox{.}\hbox{.}\hbox{.}
}}(-\Delta)^{s-1}D(\Phi u)-\Phi u\mathinner{\vbox{`:-\hbox{.}\hbox{.}\hbox{.}
}}(-\Delta)^{s-1}D\Phi
in the norm of
H^{-s}
as
K\rightarrow\infty
, since

\displaystyle\|F_{K}-F\|_{H^{-s}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\sum_{k=1}^{K}(\Phi_{k}:(-\Delta)^{s-1}D(\Phi_{k}u))-\Phi\mathinner{\vbox{`:-\hbox{.}\hbox{.}\hbox{.}
}}(-\Delta)^{s-1}D(\Phi u)\|_{H^{-s}}

\displaystyle\quad+\|\sum_{k=1}^{K}(\Phi_{k}u:(-\Delta)^{s-1}D\Phi_{k})-\Phi u\mathinner{\vbox{`:-\hbox{.}\hbox{.}\hbox{.}
}}(-\Delta)^{s-1}D\Phi\|_{H^{-s}}

\displaystyle=\|\sum_{k>K}\Phi_{k}:(-\Delta)^{s-1}D(\Phi_{k}u)\|_{H^{-s}}+\|\sum_{k>K}\Phi_{k}u:(-\Delta)^{s-1}D\Phi_{k}\|_{H^{-s}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k>K}\left(\|\Phi_{k}:(-\Delta)^{s-1}D(\Phi_{k}u)\|_{H^{-s}}+\|\Phi_{k}u:(-\Delta)^{s-1}D\Phi_{k}\|_{H^{-s}}\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{k>K}\left(\|\Phi_{k}\|_{C^{2s+\varepsilon}}\|(-\Delta)^{s-1}D(\Phi_{k}u)\|_{H^{-s}}+\|\Phi_{k}u\|_{H^{s}}\|(-\Delta)^{s-1}D\Phi_{k}\|_{C^{\varepsilon}}\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{k>K}\|\Phi_{k}\|_{C^{2s+\varepsilon}}\|\Phi_{k}u\|_{H^{s}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|u\|_{H^{s}}\sum_{k>K}\|\Phi_{k}\|^{2}_{C^{2s+\varepsilon}}\leq\|u\|_{H^{s}}\left(\sum_{k>K}\|\Phi_{k}\|_{C^{2s+\varepsilon}}\right)^{2},

which converges to
0
as
K\rightarrow\infty
by assumption
(A3)
. Given that

|F_{K}(v)-F(v)|\leq\|v\|_{H^{s}}\sup_{\|\xi\|_{H^{s}}=1}|F_{K}(\xi)-F(\xi)|=\|v\|_{H^{s}}\|F_{K}-F\|_{H^{-s}}\rightarrow 0,

we deduce that
\mathbf{C}^{s}_{A}u=\Phi\mathinner{\vbox{`:-\hbox{.}\hbox{.}\hbox{.}
}}(-\Delta)^{s-1}D(\Phi u)-\Phi u\mathinner{\vbox{`:-\hbox{.}\hbox{.}\hbox{.}
}}(-\Delta)^{s-1}D\Phi
holds in weak sense. Equivalently, if the transformed potential
Q
is defined as
Q:=-\frac{\Phi\otimes(-\Delta)^{s-1}D\Phi}{|\Phi|^{2}}
, one can write

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}^{s}_{A}u

\displaystyle=\Phi\mathinner{\vbox{`:-\hbox{.}\hbox{.}\hbox{.}
}}\left\{(-\Delta)^{s-1}D(\Phi u)-u(-\Delta)^{s-1}D\Phi\right\}

\displaystyle=\Phi\mathinner{\vbox{`:-\hbox{.}\hbox{.}\hbox{.}
}}\left\{(-\Delta)^{s-1}D(\Phi u)-\frac{(\Phi u)\mathinner{\vbox{`:-\hbox{.}\hbox{.}\hbox{.}
}}\Phi}{|\Phi|^{2}}(-\Delta)^{s-1}D\Phi\right\}

\displaystyle=\Phi\mathinner{\vbox{`:-\hbox{.}\hbox{.}\hbox{.}
}}\left\{(-\Delta)^{s-1}D(\Phi u)+(\Phi u)\mathinner{\vbox{`:-\hbox{.}\hbox{.}\hbox{.}
}}Q\right\}.

Observe that all the terms on the right hand side make sense in
H^{-s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
. In fact, since
\Phi\in C^{2s+\varepsilon}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
and
|\Phi|>0
by assumptions
(A3)
and
(A2)
, following the reasoning of Section
2.5
we have that
\Phi/|\Phi|^{2}\in C^{2s+\varepsilon}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
. As observed,
(-\Delta)^{s-1}D\Phi\in C^{\varepsilon}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
by property
(H5)
. Since by
(H1)
we have
C^{2s+\varepsilon}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})\subset C^{\varepsilon}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, a new application of
(H3)
ensures
Q\in C^{\varepsilon}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})\subset L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
. Given that
\Phi
is a multiplier on
H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
and
H^{-s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
by Lemma
2.5
and property
(H1)
, we get
(-\Delta)^{s-1}D(\Phi u)+(\Phi u)\mathinner{\vbox{`:-\hbox{.}\hbox{.}\hbox{.}
}}Q\in H^{-s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, and eventually
\Phi\mathinner{\vbox{`:-\hbox{.}\hbox{.}\hbox{.}
}}\left\{(-\Delta)^{s-1}D(\Phi u)+(\Phi u)\mathinner{\vbox{`:-\hbox{.}\hbox{.}\hbox{.}
}}Q\right\}\in H^{-s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
. ∎

3.4.
Weakly anisotropic and isotropic matrices

In this section we look at
\mathbf{C}^{s}_{A}
for matrices
A
of special forms. In some of these cases, the structure of the operator simplifies noticeably, and we recover familiar operators related to the fractional Laplacian
(-\Delta)^{s}
.

Since
A_{s}(x,y)
is positive definite and symmetric for all
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, there exist a diagonal matrix
L(x,y)
with positive entries and an orthonormal matrix
U(x,y)
such that

A_{s}(x,y)=U^{T}(x,y)\,L(x,y)\,U(x,y)

for all
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. The columns of
U
(that is, the eigenvectors of
A_{s}
of unit norm) represent the principal directions of
A_{s}
at
(x,y)
, while the entries of
L
(i.e. the eigenvalues of
A_{s}
) are the corresponding scalar conductivities in the given directions. The anisotropy of
A_{s}
is due to two factors: the principal directions of
A_{s}
change according to position, and the
n
directional conductivities differ among themselves even at any given point. As a result, in general the electric field and the current density are not parallel to each other.

However, there exist materials showing a weaker form of anisotropy, in the sense that the principal directions do not change with respect to position. This is the case of many crystalline materials (such as graphite, high temperature superconductors, and some metals), which are characterized by atomic structures repeating periodically in fixed directions. In this case, the matrices of the family
\{A_{s}(x,y):x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\}
are
simultaneously diagonalizable
, which means that there exists a unique orthonormal matrix
U
such that the formula

A_{s}(x,y)=U^{T}\,L(x,y)\,U

holds for all
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. By letting
x^{\prime}:=U\cdot x
,
u^{\prime}:=u\circ U^{T}
and
L^{\prime}:=L\circ\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}U^{T}&0\\
0&U^{T}\end{array}\right)
for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, we see that

U\cdot\nabla^{s}v(x,y)=\frac{C_{n,s}^{1/2}}{\sqrt{2}}\frac{v(y)-v(x)}{|x-y|^{n/2+s+1}}U\cdot(x-y)=\frac{C_{n,s}^{1/2}}{\sqrt{2}}\frac{v^{\prime}(y^{\prime})-v^{\prime}(x^{\prime})}{|x^{\prime}-y^{\prime}|^{n/2+s+1}}(x^{\prime}-y^{\prime})=\nabla^{s}v^{\prime}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),

and thus the fractional conductivity operator becomes

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{C}^{s}_{A}u,v\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle A_{s}(x,y)\cdot\nabla^{s}u,\nabla^{s}v\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle L(x,y)\cdot(U\cdot\nabla^{s}u),U\cdot\nabla^{s}v\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle L^{\prime}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\cdot\nabla^{s}u^{\prime},\nabla^{s}v^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\mathbf{C}^{s}_{L^{\prime}}u^{\prime},v^{\prime}\rangle

Thus we see that it is interesting to consider
\mathbf{C}^{s}_{A}
for a diagonal matrix
A
. We shall also assume that the exterior value matrices
\beta_{k}
are diagonal for all
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, so that the matrices
\phi_{k}
can themselves be taken to be diagonal for all
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and
\Phi
is a sequence of diagonal matrices. Given that the usual matrix product and the Hadamard product coincide for diagonal matrices, in this case we can write

A_{s}(x,y)=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\Phi_{k}(x)\cdot\Phi_{k}(y).

Assume now that the operator is isotropic, that is
A_{s}(x,y)=\sigma(x,y)
Id for a symmetric scalar function
\sigma
, and
\beta_{k}=b_{k}
Id for all
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
. In this case, the functions
\phi_{k}^{(i,j)}
can be taken to vanish whenever
i\neq j
, and there exists a function
\phi_{k}
such that
\phi_{k}^{(i,i)}=\phi_{k}
for all
i\in\{1,...,n\}
. In other words,
\Phi_{k}=\phi_{k}
Id, and so we have

\displaystyle Id:D(u\Phi_{k})

\displaystyle=-Id:\frac{\left(\Delta Id+2s\nabla^{2}\right)}{n+2s}\odot(u\phi_{k}Id)=-\Delta(u\phi_{k}).

This implies that

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}^{s}_{A}u

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\phi_{k}Id:(-\Delta)^{s-1}D(u\Phi_{k})-u\phi_{k}Id:(-\Delta)^{s-1}D\Phi_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\phi_{k}\left((-\Delta)^{s}(u\phi_{k})-u(-\Delta)^{s}\phi_{k}\right)

\displaystyle=\phi\cdot((-\Delta)^{s}(u\phi)+(u\phi)\cdot Q),

where
Q:=-\frac{\phi\otimes(-\Delta)^{s}\phi}{|\phi|^{2}}
. We see that in this case the equation retains its form, but it is greatly simplified. If moreover
\sigma
is a separable function of
x,y
, then
\mathbf{C}^{s}_{A}
is the usual fractional conductivity operator from
[
Cov20a
]
, and Lemma
3.3
gives the relative fractional Liouville reduction.

4.
Well-posedness of the direct problems and reduction

We consider the direct problem

(14)

\begin{split}\mathbf{C}^{s}_{A}u=F&\quad\mbox{ in }\Omega,\\
u=f&\quad\mbox{ in }\Omega_{e},\end{split}

for
F\in H^{-s}(\Omega)
and
f\in H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
. Define the bilinear form

B^{s}_{A}(u,v):=\langle A(x,y)\cdot\nabla^{s}u,\nabla^{s}v\rangle=\langle A_{s}(x,y)\cdot\nabla^{s}u,\nabla^{s}v\rangle

for
u,v\in C^{\infty}_{c}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
. The boundedness estimate

(15)

|B^{s}_{A}(u,v)|\leq\|A\cdot\nabla^{s}u\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})}\|\nabla^{s}v\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})}\lesssim\|u\|_{H^{s}}\|v\|_{H^{s}}

holds since
A\in L^{\infty}
. Thus
B^{s}_{A}
can be extended by density to act as a bounded linear operator on
H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})\times H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
.
We also define the potential energy
U^{s}_{A}
for all
u\in H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
as

U^{s}_{A}(u):=B^{s}_{A}(u,u).

Since by assumption
(A2)

A_{s}
is uniformly positive definite, it holds that

U^{s}_{A}(u)=\langle A_{s}\cdot\nabla^{s}u,\nabla^{s}u\rangle\geq\nu\|\nabla^{s}u\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})}^{2}\approx\|(-\Delta)^{s/2}u\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}^{2},

so that if
u\in H^{s}_{K}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
for some compact set
K
, by the fractional Poincaré inequality (see e.g.
[
GSU20
]
and
[
CdHS22
]
) we obtain the coercivity estimate

(16)

B^{s}_{A}(u,u)=U^{s}_{A}(u)\gtrsim\|u\|_{H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}^{2}.

We say that
u\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is a weak solution to the inhomogeneous problem (
14
) if and only if
B^{s}_{A}(u,v)=F(v)
holds for all
v\in\widetilde{H}^{s}(\Omega)
, and
u-f\in\widetilde{H}^{s}(\Omega)
. The next Proposition gives the well-posedness for the direct problem:

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
s\in(0,1)
and assume
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a bounded open set. For any
f\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
F\in H^{-s}(\Omega)
there exists a unique
u\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that
u-f\in\widetilde{H}^{s}(\Omega)
and

B^{s}_{A}(u,v)=F(v)\quad\mbox{for all}\quad v\in\widetilde{H}^{s}(\Omega).

Moreover, the following estimate holds:

\|u\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq C\left(\|f\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+\|F\|_{H^{-s}(\Omega)}\right).

Proof.

The proof of this statement follows by the Lax-Milgram theorem, given the boundedness and coercivity estimates (
15
), (
16
). It goes along the same lines as the corresponding proposition in
[
CdHS22
]
, see also
[
GSU20
]
, and it is by now well understood. We thus omit it here.
∎

Next, we study the well-posedness of the transformed direct problem

(17)

\begin{split}\mathbf{(}-\Delta)^{s-1}Dw+w\mathinner{\vbox{`:-\hbox{.}\hbox{.}\hbox{.}
}}Q=G&\quad\mbox{ in }\Omega,\\
w=\Phi f&\quad\mbox{ in }\Omega_{e},\end{split}

for
G\in(\Phi\widetilde{H}^{s}(\Omega))^{*}
and
f\in H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, where
Q:=-\frac{\Phi\otimes(-\Delta)^{s-1}D\Phi}{|\Phi|^{2}}
. As for the original direct problem, we define the bilinear form

B^{s}_{Q}(w,v):=\langle(-\Delta)^{s-1}Dw,v\rangle+\langle w\mathinner{\vbox{`:-\hbox{.}\hbox{.}\hbox{.}
}}Q,v\rangle

for
w,v\in\Phi H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
. We say that
w\in\Phi H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is a weak solution to the inhomogeneous problem (
17
) if and only if
B^{s}_{Q}(w,v)=G(v)
holds for all
v\in\Phi\widetilde{H}^{s}(\Omega)
, and
w-\Phi f\in\Phi\widetilde{H}^{s}(\Omega)
. By virtue of the reduction Lemma
3.3
, we can compute

(18)

\begin{split}B^{s}_{Q}(\Phi u,\Phi v)&=\langle(-\Delta)^{s-1}D(\Phi u),\Phi v\rangle+\langle(\Phi u)\mathinner{\vbox{`:-\hbox{.}\hbox{.}\hbox{.}
}}Q,\Phi v\rangle\\
&=\langle\Phi\mathinner{\vbox{`:-\hbox{.}\hbox{.}\hbox{.}
}}\{(-\Delta)^{s-1}D(\Phi u)+(\Phi u)\mathinner{\vbox{`:-\hbox{.}\hbox{.}\hbox{.}
}}Q\},v\rangle\\
&=\langle\mathbf{C}^{s}_{A}u,v\rangle=B^{s}_{A}(u,v)\end{split}

for all
u,v\in H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, and thus
B^{s}_{Q}
is immediately bounded and coercive in the space
\Phi H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})\times\Phi H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
by formulas (
15
), (
16
), and Lemma
2.5
. Therefore, we obtain the following well-posedness result:

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
s\in(0,1)
and assume
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a bounded open set. For any
f\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
G\in(\Phi\widetilde{H}^{s}(\Omega))^{*}
there exists a unique
w\in\Phi H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that
w-\Phi f\in\Phi\widetilde{H}^{s}(\Omega)
and

B^{s}_{Q}(w,v)=G(v)\quad\mbox{for all}\quad v\in\Phi\widetilde{H}^{s}(\Omega).

The next proposition follows immediately from Lemma
3.3
.

Proposition 4.3

(Fractional Liouville reduction)
.

Let
s\in(0,1)
and assume
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a bounded open set. If
u\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
solves the original problem

(19)

\begin{array}[]{rll}\mathbf{C}^{s}_{A}u&=F&\quad\mbox{ in }\Omega\\
u&=f&\quad\mbox{ in }\Omega_{e}\end{array}

in weak sense in
\widetilde{H}^{s}(\Omega)
for some
f\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
F\in H^{-s}(\Omega)
, then
w:=\Phi u
solves the transformed problem

(20)

\begin{array}[]{rll}\mathbf{(}-\Delta)^{s-1}Dw+w\mathinner{\vbox{`:-\hbox{.}\hbox{.}\hbox{.}
}}Q&=G&\quad\mbox{ in }\Omega\\
w&=\Phi f&\quad\mbox{ in }\Omega_{e}\end{array}

in weak sense in
\Phi\widetilde{H}^{s}(\Omega)
, where
G:=\frac{\Phi F}{|\Phi|^{2}}
. Conversely, if
w\in\Phi H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
solves (
20
) in weak sense in
\Phi\widetilde{H}^{s}(\Omega)
for some
G\in(\Phi\widetilde{H}^{s}(\Omega))^{*}
, then
u:=\frac{\Phi\mathinner{\vbox{`:-\hbox{.}\hbox{.}\hbox{.}
}}w}{|\Phi|^{2}}
solves (
19
) in weak sense in
\widetilde{H}^{s}(\Omega)
, where
F=\Phi\mathinner{\vbox{`:-\hbox{.}\hbox{.}\hbox{.}
}}G
.

Proof.

Observe that
u\in H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
solves (
19
) in weak sense if and only if
u-f\in\widetilde{H}^{s}(\Omega)
, and also for all
v\in\widetilde{H}^{s}(\Omega)
we have
B^{s}_{A}(u,v)=F(v)
. Then of course
w:=\Phi u
belongs to
\Phi H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, it verifies
w-\Phi f\in\Phi\widetilde{H}^{s}(\Omega)
, and also by (
18
)

B^{s}_{Q}(w,\Phi v)=B^{s}_{A}(u,v)=\langle F,v\rangle=\langle G,\Phi v\rangle

for
G:=\frac{\Phi F}{|\Phi|^{2}}\in(\Phi\widetilde{H}^{s}(\Omega))^{*}
. Conversely, if
w\in\Phi H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
solves (
20
) in weak sense in
\Phi\widetilde{H}^{s}(\Omega)
for some
G\in(\Phi\widetilde{H}^{s}(\Omega))^{*}
, then
w-\Phi f\in\Phi\widetilde{H}^{s}(\Omega)
, and also
B^{s}_{Q}(w,\Phi v)=\langle G,\Phi v\rangle
for all
v\in\widetilde{H}^{s}(\Omega)
. Then
u:=\frac{\Phi\mathinner{\vbox{`:-\hbox{.}\hbox{.}\hbox{.}
}}w}{|\Phi|^{2}}\in H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
,
u-f=\frac{\Phi\mathinner{\vbox{`:-\hbox{.}\hbox{.}\hbox{.}
}}(w-\Phi f)}{|\Phi|^{2}}\in\widetilde{H}^{s}(\Omega)
, and again by (
18
)

B^{s}_{A}(u,v)=B^{s}_{Q}(w,\Phi v)=\langle G,\Phi v\rangle=\langle F,v\rangle

for
F:=\Phi\mathinner{\vbox{`:-\hbox{.}\hbox{.}\hbox{.}
}}G\in H^{-s}(\Omega)
.
∎

5.
The DN map and the Alessandrini identity

Following
[
GSU20
]
, define the abstract trace space
X:=H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})/\widetilde{H}^{s}(\Omega)
. Observe that
X=H^{s}(\Omega_{e})
holds for all Lipschitz
\Omega
. Since problem (
14
) is well-posed, we can define the Poisson operator
P_{A}:X\rightarrow H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
associating to each exterior value
f\in X
the unique solution
u_{f}\in H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
to (
14
) with
F=0
. It is then possible to define a continuous, self-adjoint, linear map
\Lambda_{A}:X\rightarrow X^{*}
by

\langle\Lambda_{A}[f],[g]\rangle:=B^{s}_{A}(P_{A}f,g),

where
f,g\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. This can be proved making use of the well-posedness of the direct problem and the properties of the bilinear form (see e.g.
[
GSU20
]
,
[
CMRU22
]
,
[
CdHS22
]
for the standard proof). In particular, the self-adjointness follows from the symmetry of the bilinear form
B^{s}_{A}
, which is due to formula (
13
). Equation (
18
) now ensures the symmetry of the bilinear form
B^{s}_{Q}
in
\Phi H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
(which may be non-trivial at first sight).

The DN map
\Lambda_{A}
is related to the transformed potential
Q
by the following integral identity:

Proposition 5.1

(Alessandrini identity)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded open set and
s\in(0,1)
. Let
A_{1},A_{2}
be two anisotropic conductivity matrices satisfying assumptions
(A1)
–
(A3)
and
A_{1}\sim A_{2}
. For
j=1,2
, let
\Phi_{j},Q_{j}
be the sequence and transformed potential corresponding to
A_{j}
. Then the following integral identity holds for all
f_{1},f_{2}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega_{e})

\langle(\Lambda_{A_{1}}-\Lambda_{A_{2}})[f_{1}],[f_{2}]\rangle=\langle w_{1}\mathinner{\vbox{`:-\hbox{.}\hbox{.}\hbox{.}
}}(Q_{2}-Q_{1}),w_{2}\rangle,

where
w_{1}:=\Phi_{1}P_{A_{1}}f_{1}
and
w_{2}:=\Phi_{2}P_{A_{2}}f_{2}
.

Proof.

By definition of the DN maps and formula (
18
) we have

\displaystyle\langle(\Lambda_{A_{1}}-\Lambda_{A_{2}})[f_{1}],[f_{2}]\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\Lambda_{A_{1}}[f_{1}],[f_{2}]\rangle-\langle\Lambda_{A_{2}}[f_{1}],[f_{2}]\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\Lambda_{A_{1}}[f_{1}],[f_{2}]\rangle-\langle\Lambda_{A_{2}}[f_{2}],[f_{1}]\rangle

\displaystyle=B^{s}_{A_{1}}(P_{A_{1}}f_{1},f_{2})-B^{s}_{A_{2}}(P_{A_{2}}f_{2},f_{1})

\displaystyle=B^{s}_{Q_{1}}(\Phi_{1}P_{A_{1}}f_{1},\Phi_{1}f_{2})-B^{s}_{Q_{2}}(\Phi_{2}P_{A_{2}}f_{2},\Phi_{2}f_{1})

Observe that
P_{A_{2}}f_{2}-f_{2}\in\widetilde{H}^{s}(\Omega)
by the definition of the Poisson operator. Thus
\Phi_{2}(P_{A_{2}}f_{2}-f_{2})\in\Phi_{2}\widetilde{H}^{s}(\Omega)
, and by the assumption
A_{1}\sim A_{2}
we can also deduce
\Phi_{2}(P_{A_{2}}f_{2}-f_{2})\in\Phi_{1}\widetilde{H}^{s}(\Omega)
. This implies
B^{s}_{Q_{1}}(\Phi_{1}P_{A_{1}}f_{1},\Phi_{2}(P_{A_{2}}f_{2}-f_{2}))=0
. Since moreover
A_{1}\sim A_{2}
implies
\Phi_{1}=\Phi_{2}
in
\Omega_{e}
, we eventually deduce

B^{s}_{Q_{1}}(\Phi_{1}P_{A_{1}}f_{1},\Phi_{1}f_{2})=B^{s}_{Q_{1}}(\Phi_{1}P_{A_{1}}f_{1},\Phi_{2}f_{2})=B^{s}_{Q_{1}}(\Phi_{1}P_{A_{1}}f_{1},\Phi_{2}P_{A_{2}}f_{2}),

and similarly for
B^{s}_{Q_{2}}
. Therefore

\displaystyle\langle(\Lambda_{A_{1}}-\Lambda_{A_{2}})[f_{1}],[f_{2}]\rangle

\displaystyle=B^{s}_{Q_{1}}(\Phi_{1}P_{A_{1}}f_{1},\Phi_{2}P_{A_{2}}f_{2})-B^{s}_{Q_{2}}(\Phi_{2}P_{A_{2}}f_{2},\Phi_{1}P_{A_{1}}f_{1})

\displaystyle=\langle w_{1}\mathinner{\vbox{`:-\hbox{.}\hbox{.}\hbox{.}
}}(Q_{1}-Q_{2}),w_{2}\rangle,

where we used the symmetry of
B^{s}_{Q_{2}}
and set
w_{1}:=\Phi_{1}P_{A_{1}}f_{1}
and
w_{2}:=\Phi_{2}P_{A_{2}}f_{2}
.
∎

6.
Proof of the main theorem

We now move forward to proving a Runge-type approximation result. The proof is based on the UCP for the fractional Laplacian and a standard technique (see
[
GSU20
,
CMRU22
]
).

Lemma 6.1

(Runge approximation property)
.

Let
\Omega,W\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be bounded open sets such that
W\subset\Omega_{e}
. Assume that the exterior coefficients are isotropic, i.e.

(A4)

there exist scalar functions
b_{k}
such that
\beta_{k}=b_{k}Id
for all
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

The set

\mathcal{R}:=\left\{P_{A}f-f:f\in C^{\infty}_{c}(W)\right\}

is dense in
L^{2}(\Omega)
.

Proof.

Assume that
F\in L^{2}(\Omega)
is such that
\langle F,v\rangle=0
for all
v\in\mathcal{R}
, and let
u\in\widetilde{H}^{s}(\Omega)
be the unique solution of (
19
) with vanishing exterior value. Moreover, let
w\in\Phi\widetilde{H}^{s}(\Omega)
correspond to
u
by the fractional Liouville reduction. Then for all
f\in C^{\infty}_{c}(W)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\langle F,P_{A}f-f\rangle=\langle\mathbf{C}^{s}_{A}u,P_{A}f-f\rangle=B^{s}_{A}(u,P_{A}f-f)=-B^{s}_{A}(u,f)=-B^{s}_{Q}(\Phi u,\Phi f).

Since the supports of
u
and
f
have empty intersection, we are left with

0=\langle(-\Delta)^{s-1}Dw,\Phi f\rangle=\langle\Phi\mathinner{\vbox{`:-\hbox{.}\hbox{.}\hbox{.}
}}(-\Delta)^{s-1}Dw,f\rangle,

which by the arbitrariety of
f\in C^{\infty}_{c}(W)
implies the vanishing of
\Phi\mathinner{\vbox{`:-\hbox{.}\hbox{.}\hbox{.}
}}(-\Delta)^{s-1}Dw
in
\Omega_{e}
.
Recall that by assumption
(A1)
in
\Omega_{e}
we have
\Phi_{2k+1}(x)=0
and
\Phi_{2k}(x)=\beta_{k}(x)
for all
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Thus in
\Omega_{e}
it holds

\displaystyle 0=\Phi\mathinner{\vbox{`:-\hbox{.}\hbox{.}\hbox{.}
}}(-\Delta)^{s-1}Dw=\sum_{k}\Phi_{k}:(-\Delta)^{s-1}D(\Phi_{k}u)=\sum_{k}\beta_{k}:(-\Delta)^{s-1}D(\beta_{k}u).

Let
\tilde{\beta}_{k}
be the constant exterior value of
\beta_{k}
, and define

\gamma(x):=\sum_{k}\tilde{\beta}_{k}\odot\beta_{k}(x)=\sum_{k}\beta_{k}(y)\odot\beta_{k}(x)=\tilde{a}(x,y)

for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and
y\in\Omega_{e}
. We have that in
\Omega_{e}

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=(-\Delta)^{s-1}\left(\sum_{k}\tilde{\beta}_{k}:D(\beta_{k}u)\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{n+2s}(-\Delta)^{s-1}\left(\sum_{k}\tilde{\beta}_{k}:(\Delta Id+2s\nabla^{2})\odot(\beta_{k}u)\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{n+2s}(-\Delta)^{s-1}(\Delta Id+2s\nabla^{2}):\left(\sum_{k}\tilde{\beta}_{k}\odot\beta_{k}u\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{(-\Delta)^{s-1}(\Delta Id+2s\nabla^{2}):\left(\gamma u\right)}{n+2s},

and thus
(-\Delta)^{s-1}(\Delta Id+2s\nabla^{2}):(\gamma u)=0
in
\Omega_{e}
. Since
(-\Delta)
and
(\Delta Id+2s\nabla^{2}):
are local operators, and also
u=0
in
\Omega_{e}
, we deduce

(-\Delta)^{s}[(\Delta Id+2s\nabla^{2}):(\gamma u)]=0,\qquad(\Delta Id+2s\nabla^{2}):(\gamma u)=0

in
\Omega_{e}
. Therefore
(\Delta Id+2s\nabla^{2}):(\gamma u)=0
in all
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
by the UCP for the fractional Laplacian.

Since
\gamma(x)
is a symmetric matrix for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, we can compute

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}:(\gamma u)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}(\gamma_{ij}u)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\gamma_{ij}u+\partial_{i}\gamma_{ij}\partial_{j}u+\partial_{j}\gamma_{ji}\partial_{i}u+\gamma_{ij}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}u

\displaystyle=\gamma:\nabla^{2}u+2(\nabla\cdot\gamma)\cdot\nabla u+(\nabla^{2}:\gamma)u

and

\displaystyle\Delta Id:(\gamma u)

\displaystyle=\Delta(u\mbox{ tr}\gamma)

\displaystyle=\mbox{ tr}\gamma\Delta u+2(\nabla\mbox{tr}\gamma)\cdot\nabla u+(\Delta\mbox{tr}\gamma)u

\displaystyle=(\mbox{tr}\gamma\,Id):\nabla^{2}u+2(\nabla\mbox{tr}\gamma)\cdot\nabla u+(\Delta\mbox{tr}\gamma)u,

which gives

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=(\mbox{tr}\gamma\,Id+2s\gamma):\nabla^{2}u+(2\nabla\mbox{tr}\gamma+4s\nabla\cdot\gamma)\cdot\nabla u+(\Delta\mbox{tr}\gamma+2s\nabla^{2}:\gamma)u

\displaystyle=\Gamma:\nabla^{2}u+(2\nabla\cdot\Gamma)\cdot\nabla u+(\nabla^{2}:\Gamma)u

\displaystyle=\nabla^{2}:(\Gamma u):=Lu

in all
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, with the definition
\Gamma:=\mbox{tr}\gamma\,Id+2s\gamma
.

Only at this point we use the assumption that the exterior coefficients are isotropic, i.e. that there exist scalar functions
b_{k}
such that
\beta_{k}=b_{k}Id
for all
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
. This gives

\gamma(x)=Id\sum_{k}\tilde{b}_{k}b_{k}(x)\qquad\mbox{ and }\qquad\Gamma(x):=(n+2s)\,Id\sum_{k}\tilde{b}_{k}b_{k}(x),

and therefore

0=Lu(x)=\nabla^{2}:(\Gamma(x)u(x))=(n+2s)\Delta(u(x)\sum_{k}\tilde{b}_{k}b_{k}(x)),

for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. The function
u\sum_{k}\tilde{b}_{k}b_{k}
is thus seen to be harmonic, and since it vanishes on
\Omega_{e}
it must be equal to
0
in all of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. However, given that by assumption
(A2)
we have
\sum_{k}\tilde{b}_{k}b_{k}(x)>0
for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, the function
u
must itself vanish everywhere.

We have proved that any
F\in L^{2}(\Omega)
such that
\langle F,v\rangle=0
for all
v\in\mathcal{R}
vanishes identically. This gives the wanted result by the Hahn-Banach theorem.

∎

Remark 6.2
.

The above result can be generalized to a larger class of exterior coefficients. In particular, any exterior coefficients
\beta_{k}
such that the weak maximum principle holds for the operator
L
would suffice. Let us make for the sake of this discussion the slightly stronger regularity assumption that
\Phi\in C^{2+\varepsilon}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
. Then
\Gamma\in C^{2+\varepsilon}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, and all the coefficients of the operator
L
belong to
C^{\varepsilon}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
. Moreover,
L
is uniformly elliptic, since for all
\xi,x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and
y\in\Omega_{e}

\displaystyle\Gamma(x):(\xi\otimes\xi)

\displaystyle=(\mbox{tr}\gamma(x)\,Id+2s\gamma(x)):(\xi\otimes\xi)

\displaystyle=\mbox{tr }\tilde{a}(x,y)|\xi|^{2}+2s\tilde{a}(x,y):(\xi\otimes\xi)

\displaystyle\geq(n\lambda_{min}(x,y)+2s\nu)|\xi|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq(n+2s)\nu|\xi|^{2}.

Here we used assumptions
(A1)
,
(A2)
and observed that
\nu\in(0,\lambda_{min}(x,y)]
for all
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, where
\lambda_{min}(x,y)
is the minimal eigenvalue of
\tilde{a}(x,y)
. Due to the Hölder continuity of the coefficients of
L
,
u\in C^{2+\varepsilon}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
is actually a classical solution. If now the weak maximum principle holds for the operator
L
, we may consider equation
Lu=0
in a large enough ball
B
containing
\Omega
, and the result would follow from the fact that
u
vanishes on
\partial B
.

We are now ready to give the proof of our main result.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Assume without loss of generality that
W_{1}
and
W_{2}
are disjoint, as this condition can always be achieved by restriction of the data. Let
f_{j}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(W_{j})
and
u_{j}:=P_{A_{j}}f_{j}
for
j=1,2
. Using the Alessandrini identity and the assumption on the DN maps, we can write

(21)

\begin{split}0&=\langle(\Lambda_{A_{1}}-\Lambda_{A_{2}})[f_{1}],[f_{2}]\rangle\\
&=\langle\Phi_{1}u_{1}\mathinner{\vbox{`:-\hbox{.}\hbox{.}\hbox{.}
}}(Q_{1}-Q_{2}),\Phi_{2}u_{2}\rangle\\
&=\langle\Phi_{1}\mathinner{\vbox{`:-\hbox{.}\hbox{.}\hbox{.}
}}(Q_{1}-Q_{2})\mathinner{\vbox{`:-\hbox{.}\hbox{.}\hbox{.}
}}\Phi_{2},u_{1}u_{2}\rangle.\end{split}

We now apply the Runge approximation property. For all
g_{1},g_{2}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
we can find two sequences of exterior values
\{f_{j,i}\}_{i}\subset C^{\infty}_{c}(W_{j})
,
j=1,2
, such that

u_{j,i}:=f_{j,i}+g_{j}+r_{j,i},\qquad\mbox{with}\quad\|r_{j,i}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq 1/i.

Substituting these solutions into the previous equation gives

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\langle\Phi_{1}\mathinner{\vbox{`:-\hbox{.}\hbox{.}\hbox{.}
}}(Q_{1}-Q_{2})\mathinner{\vbox{`:-\hbox{.}\hbox{.}\hbox{.}
}}\Phi_{2},u_{1,i}u_{2,i}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\Phi_{1}\mathinner{\vbox{`:-\hbox{.}\hbox{.}\hbox{.}
}}(Q_{1}-Q_{2})\mathinner{\vbox{`:-\hbox{.}\hbox{.}\hbox{.}
}}\Phi_{2},(f_{1,i}+g_{1}+r_{1,i})(f_{2,i}+g_{2}+r_{2,i})\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\Phi_{1}\mathinner{\vbox{`:-\hbox{.}\hbox{.}\hbox{.}
}}(Q_{1}-Q_{2})\mathinner{\vbox{`:-\hbox{.}\hbox{.}\hbox{.}
}}\Phi_{2},(g_{1}+r_{1,i})(g_{2}+r_{2,i})\rangle

where we used the support assumptions and the locality of the operators involved. Since
\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2}\in C^{2s+\varepsilon}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
and
Q_{1},Q_{2}\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
(as showed in the proof of Lemma
3.3
), we have

\displaystyle|\Phi_{1}\mathinner{\vbox{`:-\hbox{.}\hbox{.}\hbox{.}
}}(Q_{1}-Q_{2})\mathinner{\vbox{`:-\hbox{.}\hbox{.}\hbox{.}
}}\Phi_{2}|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{k,i,j,k^{\prime},i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}\Phi_{1,k,ij}(Q_{1}-Q_{2})_{k,ij,k^{\prime},i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}\Phi_{2,k^{\prime},i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{k,i,j,k^{\prime},i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}|(Q_{1}-Q_{2})_{k,ij,k^{\prime},i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left(\sum_{k,i,j,k^{\prime},i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}|\Phi_{1,k,ij}|^{2}|\Phi_{2,k^{\prime},i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}|^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\|Q_{1}-Q_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\Phi_{1}\|_{C^{2s+\varepsilon}}\|\Phi_{2}\|_{C^{2s+\varepsilon}}<\infty.

Thus
\Phi_{1}\mathinner{\vbox{`:-\hbox{.}\hbox{.}\hbox{.}
}}(Q_{1}-Q_{2})\mathinner{\vbox{`:-\hbox{.}\hbox{.}\hbox{.}
}}\Phi_{2}\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, and the error terms vanish in the limit by the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality. Therefore,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\langle\Phi_{1}\mathinner{\vbox{`:-\hbox{.}\hbox{.}\hbox{.}
}}(Q_{1}-Q_{2})\mathinner{\vbox{`:-\hbox{.}\hbox{.}\hbox{.}
}}\Phi_{2},g_{1}g_{2}\rangle,

and the arbitrariety of
g_{1},g_{2}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
ensures
\Phi_{1}\mathinner{\vbox{`:-\hbox{.}\hbox{.}\hbox{.}
}}(Q_{1}-Q_{2})\mathinner{\vbox{`:-\hbox{.}\hbox{.}\hbox{.}
}}\Phi_{2}=0
in
\Omega
.

Since
A_{1}\sim A_{2}
, there exists a function
\rho\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
such that
\Phi_{2}=(\rho+1)\Phi_{1}
. Thus

\displaystyle Q_{1}-Q_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Phi_{2}\otimes(-\Delta)^{s-1}D\Phi_{2}}{|\Phi_{2}|^{2}}-\frac{\Phi_{1}\otimes(-\Delta)^{s-1}D\Phi_{1}}{|\Phi_{1}|^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Phi_{1}\otimes(-\Delta)^{s-1}D((\rho+1)\Phi_{1})}{(\rho+1)|\Phi_{1}|^{2}}-\frac{\Phi_{1}\otimes(-\Delta)^{s-1}D\Phi_{1}}{|\Phi_{1}|^{2}}

\displaystyle=\Phi_{1}\otimes\frac{(-\Delta)^{s-1}D(\rho\Phi_{1})-\rho(-\Delta)^{s-1}D\Phi_{1}}{(\rho+1)|\Phi_{1}|^{2}},

and so in
\Omega
it holds that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\Phi_{1}\mathinner{\vbox{`:-\hbox{.}\hbox{.}\hbox{.}
}}(Q_{1}-Q_{2})\mathinner{\vbox{`:-\hbox{.}\hbox{.}\hbox{.}
}}\Phi_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\{(-\Delta)^{s-1}D(\rho\Phi_{1})-\rho(-\Delta)^{s-1}D\Phi_{1}\right\}\mathinner{\vbox{`:-\hbox{.}\hbox{.}\hbox{.}
}}\Phi_{1}

\displaystyle=((-\Delta)^{s-1}D(\rho\Phi_{1})+(\rho\Phi_{1})\mathinner{\vbox{`:-\hbox{.}\hbox{.}\hbox{.}
}}Q_{1})\mathinner{\vbox{`:-\hbox{.}\hbox{.}\hbox{.}
}}\Phi_{1}.

This means that
\rho\Phi_{1}
weakly solves the transformed problem (
17
) in
\Phi_{1}\widetilde{H}^{s}(\Omega)
with vanishing inhomogeneity and exterior value. Since this problem is well-posed, it must be
\rho\Phi_{1}\equiv 0
, which entails
\Phi_{1}\equiv\Phi_{2}
. By formula (
12
), we eventually obtain

A_{1,s}(x,y)=\sum_{k}\Phi_{1,k}(x)\odot\Phi_{1,k}(y)=\sum_{k}\Phi_{2,k}(x)\odot\Phi_{2,k}(y)=A_{2,s}(x,y).

∎

7.
The limit case
s\rightarrow 1

In this section we consider the limit case
s\rightarrow 1
for the fractional anisotropic conductivity operator. The following Proposition shows that, as expected, under slightly stronger conditions one recovers the classical anisotropic conductivity operator:

Proposition 7.1
.

Let
K\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
compact, and assume that
A\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n},{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n})
verifies assumptions
(A1)
,
(A2)
and

(A3)enumi

there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that
\Phi\in C^{2+\varepsilon}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
.

Then
A^{\prime}:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
defined for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
by

A^{\prime}(x):=\frac{Id\emph{tr}A_{s}(x,x)+2A_{s}(x,x)}{n+2}

belongs to
C^{2+\varepsilon}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n})
, is constant in
\Omega_{e}
, symmetric, and uniformly positive definite with the same constant
\nu
as
A_{s}
. Moreover, the following limit holds for all
u\in H^{2}_{K}
:

\displaystyle\lim_{s\rightarrow 1}\mathbf{C}^{s}_{A}u=-\nabla\cdot(A^{\prime}\cdot\nabla u).

Proof.

Since assumption
(A3)
is substituted by
(A3)enumi
,
\Phi
is a multiplier of
H^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
into itself. The proof of this fact follows the one of Lemma
2.5
. Therefore,
\Phi u\in H^{2}_{K}
and
D(\Phi u)\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
with support in
K
. Moreover, the facts that
\Phi_{2k+1}
has compact support in
\Omega
and that
\beta_{k}
is constant in
\Omega_{e}
for all
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
imply that
D\Phi\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
with compact support.

If
v\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
with compact support, then
\lim_{s\rightarrow 1^{-}}(-\Delta)^{s-1}v=v
almost everywhere. This can be proved directly by Fourier methods first for
v\in\mathcal{S}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, the set of Schwartz functions, then for general
v
by density.
In particular,
(-\Delta)^{s-1}D(\Phi u)\rightarrow D(\Phi u)
and
(-\Delta)^{s-1}D\Phi\rightarrow D\Phi
as
s\rightarrow 1^{-}
almost everywhere in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. Therefore, by the reduction Lemma
3.3
we compute

\displaystyle\lim_{s\rightarrow 1}\mathbf{C}^{s}_{A}u

\displaystyle=\lim_{s\rightarrow 1}\Phi\mathinner{\vbox{`:-\hbox{.}\hbox{.}\hbox{.}
}}\left\{(-\Delta)^{s-1}D(\Phi u)-u(-\Delta)^{s-1}D\Phi\right\}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\Phi}{n+2}\mathinner{\vbox{`:-\hbox{.}\hbox{.}\hbox{.}
}}\left\{\left(\Delta Id+2\nabla^{2}\right)\odot(\Phi u)-u\left(\Delta Id+2\nabla^{2}\right)\odot\Phi\right\}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{k}\frac{\Phi_{k}}{n+2}:\left\{\left(\Delta Id+2\nabla^{2}\right)\odot(\Phi_{k}u)-u\left(\Delta Id+2\nabla^{2}\right)\odot\Phi_{k}\right\}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{k}\frac{\Phi_{k}}{n+2}:\left\{Id\odot\left(\Delta(\Phi_{k}u)-u\Delta\Phi_{k}\right)\right\}-\sum_{k}\frac{2\Phi_{k}}{n+2}:\left\{\nabla^{2}\odot(\Phi_{k}u)-u\nabla^{2}\odot\Phi_{k}\right\}

\displaystyle=:S_{1}+S_{2}.

For the first term
S_{1}
we write

\displaystyle-(n+2)S_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\Phi_{k}:\left\{Id\odot\left(\Phi_{k}\Delta u+2\nabla u\cdot\nabla\Phi_{k}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=Id:\sum_{k}\left\{\left(\Phi_{k}\Delta u+2\nabla u\cdot\nabla\Phi_{k}\right)\odot\Phi_{k}\right\}

\displaystyle=Id:\sum_{k}\left\{\left(\Phi_{k}\odot\Phi_{k}\right)\Delta u+\nabla u\cdot\nabla\left(\Phi_{k}\odot\Phi_{k}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=Id:\left\{A_{s}(x,x)\Delta u+\nabla u\cdot\nabla A_{s}(x,x)\right\}

\displaystyle=\mbox{tr}A_{s}(x,x)\Delta u+\nabla u\cdot\nabla\mbox{tr}A_{s}(x,x)

\displaystyle=\nabla\cdot(\mbox{tr}A_{s}(x,x)\nabla u).

Similarly, for the second term
S_{2}
we see that

\displaystyle-(n+2)S_{2}

\displaystyle=2\sum_{k}\sum_{i,j}\Phi_{k,ij}\left\{\nabla^{2}\odot(\Phi_{k}u)-u\nabla^{2}\odot\Phi_{k}\right\}_{ij}

\displaystyle=2\sum_{k}\sum_{i,j}\Phi_{k,ij}\left\{\partial_{i}\partial_{j}(\Phi_{k,ij}u)-u\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\Phi_{k,ij}\right\}

\displaystyle=2\sum_{k}\sum_{i,j}\Phi_{k,ij}^{2}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}u+\Phi_{k,ij}\partial_{i}\Phi_{k,ij}\partial_{j}u+\Phi_{k,ij}\partial_{j}\Phi_{k,ij}\partial_{i}u

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}2(\sum_{k}\Phi_{k,ij}^{2})\partial_{i}\partial_{j}u+\partial_{i}(\sum_{k}\Phi_{k,ij}^{2})\partial_{j}u+\partial_{j}(\sum_{k}\Phi_{k,ij}^{2})\partial_{i}u.

However,

\sum_{k}\Phi_{k,ij}^{2}=\{\sum_{k}\Phi_{k}\odot\Phi_{k}\}_{ij}=A_{s}(x,x)_{ij}=A_{s}(x,x)_{ji},

and thus

\displaystyle-(n+2)S_{2}

\displaystyle=2\sum_{i,j}A_{s}(x,x)_{ij}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}u+\partial_{j}u\,\partial_{i}A_{s}(x,x)_{ij}

\displaystyle=2\sum_{i,j}\partial_{i}(A_{s}(x,x)_{ij}\partial_{j}u)

\displaystyle=2\nabla\cdot(A_{s}(x,x)\cdot\nabla u).

This gives the expected formula for
A^{\prime}
. Since
A_{s}\in C^{2+\varepsilon}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
, the function
x\mapsto A_{s}(x,x)
is itself in
C^{2+\varepsilon}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
,
and we have
A^{\prime}\in C^{2+\varepsilon}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
. The facts that
A^{\prime}
is symmetric and that it is constant in
\Omega_{e}
follow from the related properties of
A_{s}
. The new matrix
A^{\prime}
is also uniformly positive definite, since for all
x,\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
it holds

\displaystyle A^{\prime}(x):(\xi\otimes\xi)

\displaystyle=\frac{Id\mbox{ tr}A_{s}(x,x)+2A_{s}(x,x)}{n+2}:(\xi\otimes\xi)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mbox{ tr}A_{s}(x,x)|\xi|^{2}+2A_{s}(x,x):(\xi\otimes\xi)}{n+2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{n\nu|\xi|^{2}+2\nu|\xi|^{2}}{n+2}=\nu|\xi|^{2}.

The inequality in the above computation is due to
(A2)
and the consideration that
\nu\in(0,\lambda_{min}(x,y)]
for all
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, where
\lambda_{min}(x,y)
is the minimal eigenvalue of
A_{s}(x,y)
.

∎

Remark 7.2
.

When the matrix
A_{s}
is isotropic, i.e. of the form
\sigma(x,y)
Id for a scalar function
\sigma
, then the limit matrix
A^{\prime}
is

A^{\prime}(x):=\frac{Id\emph{tr}A_{s}(x,x)+2A_{s}(x,x)}{n+2}=\frac{Id(n\sigma(x,x))+2\sigma(x,x)Id}{n+2}=\sigma(x,x)Id=A_{s}(x,x),

and thus it is itself isotropic. In this case the above result can be inverted, since every isotropic, positive definite matrix
A^{\prime}(x)=\gamma(x)
Id can be realized as the limit of a separable, isotropic matrix
A_{s}(x,y)=\gamma^{1/2}(x)\gamma^{1/2}(y)
Id. There exist however many different, non-separable matrices
A_{s}
giving rise to the same limit matrix
A^{\prime}
.
