Abstract
. This paper continues the studies of symbolic integration by focusing on the stability problems on D-finite functions. We introduce the notion of stability index in order to investigate the order growth of the differential operators satisfied by iterated integrals of D-finite functions and determine bounds and exact formula for stability indices of several special classes of differential operators. With the basic properties of stability index, we completely solve the stability problem on general hyperexponential functions.



1.
Introduction

New functions were introduced when certain integrals could not be evaluated in term of a specific
class of functions. We need to introduce the logarithmic function
\log(x)
since
1/x
has no
primitive in the field of rational functions. The special functions, such as
\int\exp(x^{2})\,dx,\int 1/\log(x)\,dx
etc., are introduced likewise since they are not elementary. We can continue to study the integrals of the newly introduced functions,
e.g., the indefinite integral of
\log(x)
is
x\log(x)-x
. A natural question is whether we need keep introducing new functions in order to integrate a given function iteratively.

Motivated by irrationality proofs in number theory and the above question, Chen initialized the dynamical aspect of symbolic integration by studying
some stability problems in differential fields in
(
chen2022ISSAC
)
. For a given special function
f
in certain differential ring
(R,D)
, the stability problem is
deciding whether there exists a sequence
\{g_{i}\}_{i\geq 0}
in
R
such that
f=D^{i}(g_{i})
for all
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
Such stable special functions appear in any integro-differential algebra, which is an algebra equipped with derivation and integration operators. The theory of integro-differential algebras has been developed in
(
Guo2014
)
. Two basic examples of integro-differential algebras are the ring of polynomials
{\mathbb{C}}[x]
and the ring
C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}})
of smooth functions on
{\mathbb{R}}
. From the algorithmic point of view, the algebra
C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}})
is too big and we are more interested in some subalgebra whose elements are computable, such as the subalgebras
{\mathbb{C}}[x,\log(x)]
and
{\mathbb{C}}[x,\exp(x)]
. To find more rich examples, it is necessary to classify certain class of functions that are iteratively integrable in this class.

Focusing on elementary functions, Chen in
(
chen2022ISSAC
)
had solved the stability problems on three families of special functions including rational functions, logarithmic functions, and exponential functions. The main goal of this paper is to continue the work
(
chen2022ISSAC
)
by focusing on D-finite functions using the language of differential operators.

The notion of D-finite power series was first introduced by Stanley
(
Stanley1980
)
in 1980 in the univariate case and later studied by Lipshitz in the multivariate case
(
Lipshitz1989
)
. D-finite functions are
also called holonomic functions that had played a significant role in Zeilberger’s method of creative telescoping
(
Zeilberger1990
;
Wilf1992
)
.
These series, like algebraic numbers, can be algorithmically manipulated via its defining linear differential equations
(
AbramovLeLi2005
;
Salvy2019
)
. For a comprehensive
introduction to theory and algorithms for D-finite functions, one can see the coming monograph by Kauers
(
DfiniteBook
)
.
The indefinite integration problem on D-finite functions
was studied by Abramov and van Hoeij in
(
AbramovHoeij1997
)
.
The main goal of this paper is to investigate Abramov-van Hoeij’s algorithm iteratively.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. We recall some basic terminologies in differential algebra and then define
the notion of principal integrals related to iterated integration in Section
2
. We will focus on studying the order of the differential operators satisfied
by principal integrals in Section
3
, which inspires us to introduce the notion of stability index. In Section
4
, we will
determine the stability indices of two special classes of differential operators including Katz’s operators and first-order operators.
With the help of stability index, we will completely solve the stability problem for general hyperexponential functions in Section
5

which generalizes the results in
(
chen2022ISSAC
)
. As an application, we present an exact formula for the stability index of a special class of rational functions in Section
6
.

2.
Principal integrals

Throughout this paper, let
C
be any algebraically closed field of characteristic zero and
k=C(x)
be
the differential field with the usual derivation
′
such that
x^{\prime}=1
and
c^{\prime}=0
for all
c\in C
.
Let
\mathcal{K}
be a differential closure of
C(x)
(see Kolchin’s book
(
kolchin
)
for the existence of such closures).
Any system of algebraic differential equations with coefficients from
k
has solutions in
\mathcal{K}
if it has any solution in some differential extension of
k
.
Let
k\langle D\rangle
be the ring of linear differential operators with coefficients in
k
in which we have

D\cdot f=f\cdot D+f^{\prime},\quad\text{for any $f\in k$}.

The ring
k\langle D\rangle
is a left Euclidean domain in which any left ideal is principal
(
Ore1933
)
.
For an
m\in\mathbb{Z}
,
\mathbb{Z}_{\leq m}
(resp., we let
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq m}
) stand for the set of all integers not greater than
m
(resp., not less than
m
).

Let
L
be a nonzero operator in
k\langle D\rangle
. Write

L=\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{i}D^{i},\quad\text{where $a_{i}\in k$ and $a_{n}\neq 0$}.

The integer
n
is called the order of
L
, denoted by
\operatorname{ord}(L)
, and
a_{n}
is called the leading coefficient
of
L
, denoted by
\operatorname{lc}(L)
. If
a_{n}=1
, then
L
is called a monic operator.
The adjoint operator of
L
, denoted by
L^{*}
, is defined to be

L^{*}:=\sum_{i=0}^{n}(-D)^{i}a_{i}.

The field
\mathcal{K}
can be equipped with a left
{k\langle D\rangle}
-module structure with the action
L(f)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{i}f^{(i)}
for any
L=\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{i}D^{i}\in{k\langle D\rangle}
and
f\in\mathcal{K}
.
The annihilating ideal

\text{Ann}_{k\langle D\rangle}(f):=\{P\in{k\langle D\rangle}\mid P(f)=0\}

is a left ideal of
{k\langle D\rangle}
. Since
{k\langle D\rangle}
is a left Euclidean domain,
we have
\text{Ann}_{k\langle D\rangle}(f)=\langle L\rangle
for some monic operator
L\in{k\langle D\rangle}
.

Definition 2.1.

An element
f\in\mathcal{K}
is said to be
D-finite
over
k
if
\text{Ann}_{k\langle D\rangle}(f)=\langle L\rangle
for some nonzero and monic operator
L\in{k\langle D\rangle}
.
We call
L
the defining operator for
f
, denoted by
L_{f}
, whose order is called the order of
f
, denoted by
\operatorname{ord}(f)
.
For convenience, we set
\operatorname{ord}(f)=\infty
if
f
is not D-finite.

Lipshitz
(
Lipshitz1989
)
showed that the set of all D-finite elements over
k
forms a subalgebra of
\mathcal{K}
, which is also closed under taking
derivatives and integrals. We will investigate how the order changes when integrating a D-finite function.

Definition 2.2.

For
f\in\mathcal{K}
and
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
,
g\in\mathcal{K}
is called an
i
-th primitive or an
i
-th integral of
f
if
f=D^{i}(g)
. When
i=1
, we simply call
g
an integral of
f
.

Suppose
f
is a D-finite function and
g_{i}
is an
i
-th integral of
f
. Then
g_{i}
is also D-finite and
\operatorname{ord}(g_{i})\leq\operatorname{ord}(f)+i
. Set

\mathcal{N}_{i}(f)=\min\{\operatorname{ord}(g)\mid\mbox{$g$ is an $i$-th integral of $f$}\}.

The following example shows that different i-th integrals of a given function may have different orders.

Example 2.3.

Let
f=\exp(x)
. Then
f
itself is a second integral of
f
and
\operatorname{ord}(f)=1
. Since every second integral of
f
must be of order not less than 1,
\mathcal{N}_{2}(f)=1
. On the other hand, there exist second integrals of
f
that are of order greater than 1. In fact, each second integral of
f
is of the form
\exp(x)+\alpha x+\beta
with
\alpha,\beta\in C
. Assume that not all
\alpha,\beta
are zero. We claim that
\operatorname{ord}(\exp(x)+\alpha x+\beta)=2
. First, as
\exp(x)+\alpha x+\beta
is not hyperexponential,
\exp(x)+\alpha x+\beta
can not be annihilated by any first-oder operator. Second, if
\alpha\neq 0
then
\exp(x)+\alpha x+\beta
is annihilated by

\left(x+\frac{\beta}{\alpha}+1\right)D^{2}-\left(x+\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\right)D-1,

and if
\alpha=0
and
\beta\neq 0
then
\exp(x)+\beta
is annihilated by
D^{2}-D
. So
\operatorname{ord}(\exp(x)+\alpha x+\beta)=2
and the claim is shown.

The example above motivates the following notion of principal integrals.

Definition 2.4.

The
i
-th integrals of order
\mathcal{N}_{i}(f)
are called the principal
i
-th integrals of
f
.

It can happen that all integrals are principal as shown in the following example.

Example 2.5.

Let
f=\frac{1}{x}
. Then each principal second integral of
f
is of the form
x\ln(x)+\alpha x+\beta
with
\alpha,\beta\in C
. It is easy to check that
x\ln(x)+\alpha x+\beta
is annihilated by

(x-\beta)D^{2}-D+\frac{1}{x}.

A similar argument as in Example
2.3
implies that
\operatorname{ord}(x\ln(x)+\alpha x+\beta)=2
for all
\alpha,\beta\in C
.

It was proved in
(
AbramovHoeij1997
;
AbramovHoeij1999
)
that
\mathcal{N}_{1}(f)=\operatorname{ord}(f)
if and only if
L_{f}^{*}(y)=1
has a solution in
k
. If
L_{f}^{*}(y)=1
has no solution in
k
then
\mathcal{N}_{1}(f)=\operatorname{ord}(f)+1
and moreover
L_{g}=L_{f}D
for any integrals
g
of
f
. Suppose that
L_{f}^{*}(y)=1
has a solution
l
in
k
. Then
(1-lL_{f})^{*}(1)=0
and so there is a unique
H_{l}\in{k\langle D\rangle}
of order
n-1
such that

1-lL_{f}=DH_{l}

where
n=\operatorname{ord}(f)
.
Note that
-l
is the leading coefficient of
H_{l}
.
Set
g=H_{l}(f)
. Then
g
is an integral of
f
and

L_{g}=\frac{1}{l}(1-H_{l}D).

For a nonzero operator
P\in{k\langle D\rangle}
and
q\in C[x]
, define

\delta(P,q)=\begin{cases}1&\mbox{$P^{*}(y)=q$ has a solution in $C(x)$}\\
0&\mbox{otherwise}\end{cases}.

When
q=1
, we abbreviate
\delta(P,q)
to
\delta(P)
. The above discussions motivate the definition below.

Definition 2.6.

Suppose that
L
is a nonzero monic operator in
{k\langle D\rangle}
. A principal integral of
L
is defined to be

\begin{cases}LD&\delta(L)=0\\
\frac{1}{l}(1-H_{l}D)&\delta(L)=1\end{cases}

where
l\in k
is a solution of
L^{*}(y)=1
and
H_{l}
is the unique operator in
{k\langle D\rangle}
of order
\operatorname{ord}(L)-1
such that
lL+DH_{l}=1
. We also call
\frac{1}{l}(1-H_{l}D)
the principal integral of
L
with respect to
l
. An
i
-th principal integral of
L
is defined iteratively.

Remark 2.7.

Notice that in the case
\delta(L)=1
principal integrals of
L
depend on the rational solutions of
L^{*}(y)=1
and thus they are generally not unique. For example, let
L=D
. The adjoint
L^{*}
of
L
is
-D
. For every
c\in C
,
-x+c
is a rational solution of
L^{*}(y)=1
. One sees that
x-c
satisfies that
(-x+c)D+D(x-c)=1
.
Since

\frac{1}{-x+c}(1-(x-c)D)=D-\frac{1}{x-c},

by definition,
D-\frac{1}{x-c}
is a principal integral of
L
for any
c\in C
. Therefore the principal integrals of
L
are not unique. However they have the same order.

Lemma 2.8.

Suppose that
f\in\mathcal{K}
and
L_{i}
is an
i
-th principal integral of
L_{f}
. Then there is an
i
-th integral of
f
, say
g_{i}
, such that
L_{i}=L_{g_{i}}
.

Proof.

We shall show the lemma by induction on
i
. The case
i=1
follows from Definition
2.6
and the discussions before Definition
2.6
. Suppose that
i>1
and the assertion holds for the case
i-1
. Let
L_{i-1}
be an
(i-1)
-th principal integral of
L_{f}
and
L_{i}
is a principal integral of
L_{i-1}
. By induction hypothesis, there is an
(i-1)
-th integral
g_{i-1}
of
f
such that
L_{i-1}=L_{g_{i-1}}
. Since
L_{i}
is a principal integral of
L_{i-1}=L_{g_{i-1}}
, by induction hypothesis again, there is an integral
g_{i}
of
g_{i-1}
such that
L_{i}=L_{g_{i}}
. The lemma then follows from the fact that
g_{i}
is an
i
-th integral of
f
.

In next section, we shall show that the
g_{i}
in Lemma
2.8
is actually an
i
-th principal integral of
f
. Furthermore, we shall show that
\operatorname{ord}(L_{i})=\mathcal{N}_{i}(f)
, and Definition
2.4
and Definition
2.6
are consistent in some sense.

3.
Orders of principal integrals

Throughout this section,
L
is always a monic operator in
{k\langle D\rangle}
.
Suppose that
L_{i}
is a principal
i
-th integral of
L
for any positive integer
i
.
We are going to prove that
\operatorname{ord}(L_{i+1})=\operatorname{ord}(L_{i})
if and only if there is a polynomial
p\in C[x]
of degree
i
such that
L_{i}^{*}(y)=p
has a solution in
k
. This generalizes the results in
(
AbramovHoeij1997
;
AbramovHoeij1999
)
for the case when
i=1
. For a rational function
l\in k
,
l
is said to be rationally integrable if
l=D(h)
for some
h\in k
.

Lemma 3.1.

Suppose that
P=1+MD
and
l
is a rational solution of
P^{*}(y)=p
, where
M\in{k\langle D\rangle}
and
p\in C[x]
. Then
l
is rationally integrable.

Proof.

Note that
P^{*}=1-DM^{*}
. Since
l
is a solution of
P^{*}(y)=p
in
k
, we have
l-D(M^{*}(l))=p
.
Let
q\in C[x]
be such that
p=q^{\prime}
. Then
l=D(q+M^{*}(l))
. The lemma then follows from the fact that
q+M^{*}(l)\in k
.

Proposition 3.2.

Suppose that
L_{0}=L
and
L_{i+1}
is a principal integral of
L_{i}
for any
i\geq 0
. Set
I_{-1}=1
and for each
i\geq 0
set

I_{i}=\begin{cases}1&\delta(L_{i})=0\\
D+\frac{D(l_{i})}{l_{i}}&\delta(L_{i})=1\end{cases}

where
l_{i}\in k
is a solution of
L_{i}^{*}(y)=1
such that
L_{i+1}
is the principal integral of
L_{i}
with respect to
l_{i}
.
Then for each
n\geq 0
,

(1)

LD^{n}=\left(\prod_{i=-1}^{n-1}I_{i}\right)L_{n}.

Proof.

We shall prove the proposition by induction on
n
. The case
n=0
is clear. Assume that
n>0
and the assertion holds for
n-1
. By induction hypothesis,

LD^{n-1}=\left(\prod_{i=-1}^{n-2}I_{i}\right)L_{n-1}.

If
\delta(L_{n-1})=0
then
L_{n}=L_{n-1}D
and
I_{n-1}=1
. Thus

LD^{n}=LD^{n-1}D=\left(\prod_{i=-1}^{n-2}I_{i}\right)L_{n-1}D=\left(\prod_{i=-1}^{n-1}I_{i}\right)L_{n}.

If
\delta(L_{n-1})=1
then
l_{n-1}L_{n-1}+DH_{l_{n-1}}=1
and
L_{n}=\frac{1}{l_{n-1}}(1-H_{l_{n-1}}D)
. These imply that

\displaystyle L_{n-1}D

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{l_{n-1}}(1-DH_{l_{n-1}})D=\frac{1}{l_{n-1}}D(1-H_{l_{n-1}}D)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{l_{n-1}}Dl_{n-1}L_{n}=\left(D+\frac{D(l_{n-1})}{l_{n-1}}\right)L_{n}=I_{n-1}L_{n}.

Therefore

LD^{n}=LD^{n-1}D=\left(\prod_{i=-1}^{n-2}I_{i}\right)L_{n-1}D=\left(\prod_{i=-1}^{n-1}I_{i}\right)L_{n}.

The assertion holds for all nonnegative integers
n
.

Lemma 3.3.

Suppose that
L_{n}
is a principal integral of
L_{n-1}
. Then for every
p\in C[x]
,
\delta(L_{n},p)=\delta(L_{n-1},q)
for some
q\in C[x]
with
p=q^{\prime}
. In other words,
L_{n}^{*}(y)=p
has a solution in
k
if and only if
L_{n-1}^{*}(y)=q
has a solution in
k
.

Proof.

Assume that
\delta(L_{n-1})=0
. Then
L_{n}=L_{n-1}D
and thus
L_{n}^{*}=-DL_{n-1}^{*}
. One can verify that for each
h\in k
,
L_{n}^{*}(h)=p
if and only if
L_{n-1}^{*}(-h)=q
for some
q\in C[x]
with
p=q^{\prime}
. Now suppose that
\delta(L_{n-1})=1
and
L_{n}
is the principal integral of
L_{n-1}
with respect to
l_{n-1}
. Then
L_{n}=1/l_{n-1}(1-H_{l_{n-1}}D)
, where
H_{l_{n-1}}\in{k\langle D\rangle}
satisfies that
l_{n-1}L_{n-1}+DH_{l_{n-1}}=1
. Suppose that
h\in k
is a solution of
L_{n}^{*}(y)=p
. Then
h/l_{n-1}
is a solution of
(1-H_{l_{n-1}}D)^{*}(y)=p
in
k
. Due to Lemma
3.1
, there is a
g\in k
such that
D(g)=h/l_{n-1}
. Since
l_{n-1}L_{n-1}D=D-DH_{l_{n-1}}D=D(1-H_{l_{n-1}}D)
, one has that

\displaystyle DL_{n-1}^{*}(l_{n-1}g)

\displaystyle=DL_{n-1}^{*}l_{n-1}(g)=(1-H_{l_{n-1}}D)^{*}D(g)

\displaystyle=(1-H_{l_{n-1}}D)^{*}(h/l_{n-1})=p.

Let
\tilde{q}\in C[x]
be such that
D(\tilde{q})=p
. Then
D(L_{n-1}^{*}(l_{n-1}g)-\tilde{q})=0
and thus
L_{n-1}^{*}(l_{n-1}g)-\tilde{q}=c\in C
. Set
q=\tilde{q}+c
. Then
L_{n-1}^{*}(l_{n-1}g)=q
. Conversely, assume that
h
is a solution of
L_{n-1}^{*}(y)=q
for some
q\in C[x]
with
p=q^{\prime}
. Then
DL_{n-1}^{*}(h)=p
. Set
\tilde{h}=l_{n-1}D(h/l_{n-1})
. One then has that

\displaystyle L_{n}^{*}(\tilde{h})

\displaystyle=(1-H_{l_{n-1}}D)^{*}(\tilde{h}/l_{n-1})=(1-H_{l_{n-1}}D)^{*}D(h/l_{n-1})

\displaystyle=(1+DH_{l_{n-1}}^{*})D\frac{1}{l_{n-1}}(h)=D(1+H_{l_{n-1}}^{*}D)\frac{1}{l_{n-1}}(h)

\displaystyle=D\left(\frac{1}{l_{n-1}}(1-DH_{l_{n-1}})\right)^{*}(h)=DL_{n-1}^{*}(h)=p.

In other words,
L_{n}^{*}(y)=p
has a solution in
k
.

Proposition 3.4.

Let
L_{i}
be as in Proposition
3.2
.
Then

\delta(L_{n},p)=\delta(LD^{n},p)

for any
p\in C[x]\setminus\{0\}
and nonnegative integer
n
.

Proof.

We shall prove the assertion by induction on
n
. The case
n=0
is clear, because
L_{0}=L=LD^{0}
in this case. Suppose that
n>0
and the assertion holds for the case
n-1
.
Consider the case
n
. It suffices to show that
\delta(L_{n},p)=1
if and only if
\delta(LD^{n},p)=1
.
Assume that
\delta(L_{n},p)=1
. Lemma
3.3
implies that
\delta(L_{n-1},q)=1
for some
q\in C[x]
with
p=q^{\prime}
. By induction hypothesis,
\delta(LD^{n-1},q)=1
, i.e. there is an
r\in k
such that
(LD^{n-1})^{*}(r)=q
. Since
(LD^{n})^{*}=-D(LD^{n-1})^{*}
, one sees that

(LD^{n})^{*}(-r)=D((LD^{n-1})^{*}(r))=q^{\prime}=p
, i.e.
\delta(LD^{n},p)=1
. Conversely, assume that
\delta(LD^{n},p)=1
. Then
\delta(LD^{n-1},\tilde{q})=1
for some
\tilde{q}\in C[x]
with
D(\tilde{q})=p
. By induction hypothesis again,
\delta(L_{n-1},\tilde{q})=1
. By Lemma
3.3
again,
\delta(L_{n},p)=1
. Hence the assertion holds for the case
n
.

Corollary 3.5.

Let
L_{i}
be as in Proposition
3.2
. Then
\operatorname{ord}(L_{i+1})=\operatorname{ord}(L_{i})
if and only if
\delta(L,q)=1
for some
q\in C[x]
of degree
i
.

Proof.

From Definition
2.6
,
\operatorname{ord}(L_{i+1})=\operatorname{ord}(L_{i})
if and only if
\delta(L_{i})=\delta(L_{i},1)=1
. Note that
\delta(L_{i})=\delta(L_{i},c)
for any nonzero
c\in C
. Using Lemma
3.3
repeatedly, there is a
q\in C[x]
of degree
i
such that

\delta(L,q)=\delta(L_{1},q^{\prime})=\dots=\delta(L_{i},i!\operatorname{lc}(q))=\delta(L_{i}).

In other words, for such
q
,
\delta(L_{i})=1
if and only if
\delta(L,q)=1
. Hence
\operatorname{ord}(L_{i+1})=\operatorname{ord}(L_{i})
if and only if
\delta(L,q)=1
for some
q\in C[x]
of degree
i
.

Lemma 3.6.

Let
f\in\mathcal{K}
and
L_{i}
as in Proposition
3.2
with
L=L_{f}
. Then
\operatorname{ord}(L_{i})=\mathcal{N}_{i}(f)
.

Proof.

Set

S_{i}(f)=\{0\leq j\leq i-1\mid\delta(L_{f}D^{j})=1\}.

Then by Proposition
3.4
,
\operatorname{ord}(L_{i})=i+\operatorname{ord}(f)-|S_{i}(f)|
. Therefore,
it suffices to show that
|S_{i}(f)|=i-\mathcal{N}_{i}(f)+\operatorname{ord}(f)
.
We shall prove this by induction on
i
. The case
i=1
follows from the fact that
\mathcal{N}_{1}(f)=\operatorname{ord}(f)
if and only if
\delta(L_{f})=1

Remark 3.13.

L

Below are several examples of stable operators.

Example 3.14.

4.
Stability Indices of special operators

For a general operator , even for a first order operator, it is difficult to compute its stability index. In this section, we shall study the stability indices of some special operators.

Proposition 4.1.

The set

Suppose that

is a

-vector space.

Suppose that

is stable. Then for each

, there is a nonzero

of degree

such that
\delta(L,h_{i})=1
. Let

be a solution of

. Note that

. Assume that

. Write

4.1.
Stability indices of Katz’s operators

In

(3)

He proved that differential Galois groups of the operators of the above form are large. In this subsection, let us compute the stability indices of Katz’s operators.

Lemma 4.2.

Suppose that

For each

Remark 4.3.

The bound given in

is a nonzero constant and

Proposition 4.4.

Suppose that

, where

and

. Then
{\rm Sind}(L)=\max\{\deg(q),0\}

Proof.

If

Assume that

.
By Lemma

for any polynomial

of degree

\sigma^{L^{*}}=-t
. Hence
\mathcal{B}(L)=-t+\deg(f_{2})=\deg(f_{1})=\max\{\deg(f_{1}),\deg(f_{2})\}
. Now suppose that
t=1
. Then
\alpha=-\operatorname{res}_{\infty}(f)=-\nu
. Furthermore,
{\rm ind}^{L^{*}}(s)=-(s-\alpha)
and
\sigma^{L^{*}}=-1
. If
\nu
is not a nonnegative integer then
\max V_{\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}({\rm ind}^{L^{*}})=-1
and so
\mathcal{B}(L)=\deg(f_{2})-1
. Otherwise if
\nu
is a nonnegative integer then
\max V_{\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}({\rm ind}^{L^{*}})=-\nu
and thus
\mathcal{B}(L)=-\nu+\deg(f_{2})
.

In what follows, we shall present a lower bound.

Lemma 4.11.

Suppose that
L=D+f
with
\mathcal{S}(f)=\emptyset
. Set
N=\deg({\rm den}(f))
and
\nu=\operatorname{res}_{\infty}(f)
.
Then

{\rm Sind}(L)=\begin{cases}N-\min\{1,\operatorname{ord}_{\infty}(f)\}&\operatorname{ord}_{\infty}(f)\neq 1\,\,\mbox{or}\,\,\nu\notin\mathbb{Z}_{\leq-N}\\
-\nu&\mbox{otherwise}\end{cases}.

Proof.

Suppose that
r\in k
satisfies that
L^{*}(r)=d
for some nonzero polynomial
d
. By Corollary
3.5
,
r
must be a polynomial. Furthermore, from

-r^{\prime}+rf=d,

one sees that
{\rm den}(f)
divides
r
. In particular,
\deg(r)\geq\deg({\rm den}(f))=N
.
Let

\displaystyle r=cx^{s}+\dots,\,\,f=\alpha x^{-t}+\dots,

be the power series expansions of
r
and
f
respectively,
where
s=\deg(r)
and
t=\operatorname{ord}_{\infty}(f)
. From
-r^{\prime}+rf=d
again, one has that

(4)

-scx^{s-1}+\dots+c\alpha x^{s-t}+\dots=d.

Suppose that
t=\operatorname{ord}_{\infty}(f)<1
. Then

\deg(d)

must be equal to
s-t
that is not less than
N-t
.
In other words, if
\delta(L,d)=1
for some nonzero polynomial
d
then
\deg(d)\geq N-t
. So
{\rm Sind}(L)\geq N-t
. On the other hand, for each
i\geq 0
,

L^{*}({\rm den}(f)x^{i})=-\alpha x^{N+i-t}+\mbox{lower terms}.

This implies that for each
s\geq N-t
,
{\rm den}(f)x^{s-N+t}
is a solution of
L^{*}(y)=-\alpha x^{s}+\mbox{lower terms}
, i.e.
{\rm Sind}(L)\leq N-t
. Consequently,
{\rm Sind}(L)=N-t
.
Similarly, one can prove the case
t>1
.

Now assume that
t=1
.
In this case,
\alpha=-\operatorname{res}_{\infty}(f)=-\nu
and
s=\deg(r)\geq N>0
. Moreover, the equality (
4
) becomes

(5)

-c(s+\nu)x^{s-1}+\mbox{lower terms}=d.

Suppose that
\nu\notin\mathbb{Z}_{\leq-N}
. Then
s+\nu\neq 0
for all
s\geq N
. Hence
\deg(d)
must be equal to
s-1
. Using a similar argument as in the case
t<1
, one has that
{\rm Sind}(L)=N-1
.
It remains to show the case
\nu\in\mathbb{Z}_{\leq-N}
. We first show that
\delta(L,d)=0
for any polynomial
d\in C[x]
of degree
-\nu-1
. Suppose on the contrary that there is a polynomial
d\in C[x]
of degree
-\nu-1
such that
L^{*}(y)=d
has a solution
r\in C[x]
. If
s=\deg(r)>-\nu
then
s+\nu=0
by (
5
). This implies that
s=-\nu
, a contradiction. So
s\leq-\nu
. From (
5
), it is obvious that
s
can not be less than
-\nu
. Thus
s=-\nu
. While, this means that the degree of the left-hand side of (
5
) is less than
-\nu-1
. This contradicts the assumption that
\deg(d)=-\nu-1
. Consequently,
\delta(L,d)=0
for any polynomial
d
of degree
-\nu-1
and so
{\rm Sind}(L)\geq-\nu
. Finally, for each
i\geq 0
,

L^{*}\left({\rm den}(f)x^{i+1-\nu-N}\right)=-(i+1)x^{i-\nu}+\mbox{lower terms}.

This implies that
{\rm Sind}(L)\leq-\nu
. Therefore
{\rm Sind}(L)=-\nu
.

Proposition 4.12.

Suppose that
L=D+f
. Set

N=\deg({\rm den}(f))-|\mathcal{S}(f)|-\deg(\Delta(f))

and
\nu=\operatorname{res}_{\infty}(f).
Then

{\rm Sind}(L)\geq\begin{cases}N-\min\{1,\operatorname{ord}_{\infty}(f)\}&\operatorname{ord}_{\infty}(f)\neq 1\,\,\mbox{or}\,\,\nu\notin\mathbb{Z}_{\leq-N}\\
-\nu&\mbox{otherwise}\end{cases}.

Proof.

Set
M_{1}=\prod_{c\in\mathcal{S}(f)}(x-c)
and write
{\rm den}(f)=M_{1}M_{2}
. Then
\deg(M_{2})=\deg({\rm den}(f))-|\mathcal{S}(f)|
. Since
\gcd(M_{1},M_{2})=1
, there exist
q_{1},q_{2}\in C[x]
such that

f=q_{1}/M_{1}+q_{2}/M_{2}.

Since
M_{1}
is square-free and
\operatorname{res}_{c}(q_{1}/M_{1})=\operatorname{res}_{c}(f)
for each
c\in\mathcal{S}(f)
, one has that

\frac{q_{1}}{M_{1}}=\sum_{c\in\mathcal{S}(f)}\frac{\operatorname{res}_{c}(f)}{x-c}=-\frac{\Delta(f)^{\prime}}{\Delta(f)}.

So
f=-\Delta(f)^{\prime}/\Delta(f)+q_{2}/M_{2}
. Set
\tilde{L}=L+\Delta(f)^{\prime}/\Delta(f)=D+q_{2}/M_{2}
. Using Proposition
4.9
repeatedly yields that
{\rm Sind}(\tilde{L})\leq{\rm Sind}(L)+\deg(\Delta(f))
. Since
\mathcal{S}(q_{2}/M_{2})=\emptyset
, by Lemma
4.11
, one has that
if
\operatorname{ord}_{\infty}(q_{2}/M_{2})\neq 1

or

\operatorname{res}_{\infty}(q_{2}/M_{2})\notin\mathbb{Z}_{\leq-\deg(M_{2})}
then
{\rm Sind}(\tilde{L})=\deg(M_{2})-\min\{1,\operatorname{ord}_{\infty}(q_{2}/M_{2})\}
, otherwise
{\rm Sind}(\tilde{L})=-\operatorname{res}_{\infty}(q_{2}/M_{2})
.

Note that
\operatorname{ord}_{\infty}(-\Delta(f)^{\prime}/\Delta(f))=1
and
\operatorname{res}_{\infty}(-\Delta(f)^{\prime}/\Delta(f))=\deg(\Delta(f))
. Furthermore, one has that

\displaystyle\operatorname{res}_{\infty}(f)

\displaystyle\geq\min\left\{\operatorname{ord}_{\infty}\left(-\frac{\Delta(f)^{\prime}}{\Delta(f)}\right),\operatorname{ord}_{\infty}\left(\frac{q_{2}}{M_{2}}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=\min\left\{1,\operatorname{ord}_{\infty}\left(\frac{q_{2}}{M_{2}}\right)\right\}

and the equality holds if
\operatorname{ord}_{\infty}(q_{2}/M_{2})\neq 1
.
If
\operatorname{ord}_{\infty}(f)<1
then
\operatorname{ord}_{\infty}(q_{2}/M_{2})=\operatorname{ord}_{\infty}(f)<1
and so

\displaystyle{\rm Sind}(L)

\displaystyle\geq{\rm Sind}(\tilde{L})-\deg(\Delta(f))

\displaystyle=\deg(M_{2})-\min\{1,\operatorname{ord}_{\infty}(q_{2}/M_{2})\}-\deg(\Delta(f))

\displaystyle=N-\min\{1,\operatorname{ord}_{\infty}(f)\}.

Suppose that
\operatorname{ord}_{\infty}(f)>1
. Then
\operatorname{ord}_{\infty}(q_{2}/M_{2})=1
and moreover
\operatorname{res}_{\infty}(q_{2}/M_{2})=-\operatorname{res}_{\infty}(-\Delta(f)^{\prime}/\Delta(f))=-\deg(\Delta(f))
. Assume that
\deg(\Delta(f))\geq\deg(M_{2})
. Then
\operatorname{res}_{\infty}(q_{2}/M_{2})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\leq-\deg(M_{2})}
and so

{\rm Sind}(\tilde{L})=-\operatorname{res}_{\infty}(q_{2}/M_{2})=\deg(\Delta(f)).

Thus
{\rm Sind}(L)\geq 0
. On the other hand, in this case,

N-\min\{1,\operatorname{ord}_{\infty}(f)\}=\deg(M_{2})-\deg(\Delta(f))-1<0\leq{\rm Sind}(L).

Assume that
\deg(\Delta(f))<\deg(M_{2})
. Then we have
\operatorname{res}_{\infty}(q_{2}/M_{2})\notin\mathbb{Z}_{\leq-\deg(M_{2})}
. So

{\rm Sind}(\tilde{L})=\deg(M_{2})-1
and
{\rm Sind}(L)\geq{\rm Sind}(\tilde{L})-\deg(\Delta(f))=N-1
. Since
\operatorname{ord}_{\infty}(f)>1
, we have
N-1=N-\min\{1,\operatorname{ord}_{\infty}(f)\}
and then the assertion holds.

Note that
\nu=\operatorname{res}_{\infty}(f)=\operatorname{res}_{\infty}(-\Delta(f)^{\prime}/\Delta(f))+\operatorname{res}_{\infty}(q_{2}/M_{2})
and
\operatorname{res}_{\infty}(-\Delta(f)^{\prime}/\Delta(f))=\deg(\Delta(f))
. This implies that
\nu\notin\mathbb{Z}_{\leq-N}
if and only if
\operatorname{res}_{\infty}(q_{2}/M_{2})\notin\mathbb{Z}_{\leq-\deg(M_{2})}
. Suppose that
\operatorname{ord}_{\infty}(f)=1
and
\nu\notin\mathbb{Z}_{\leq-N}
. Then
\operatorname{ord}_{\infty}(q_{2}/M_{2})\geq 1
and
{\rm Sind}(\tilde{L})=\deg(M_{2})-1
. Thus
{\rm Sind}(L)\geq{\rm Sind}(\tilde{L})-\deg(\Delta(f))\geq N-1
. The assertion then follows from the fact that
\min\{1,\operatorname{ord}_{\infty}(f)\}=1
. Finally, assume that
\operatorname{ord}_{\infty}(f)=1
and
\nu\in\mathbb{Z}_{\leq-N}
. Then
\operatorname{res}_{\infty}(q_{2}/M_{2})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\leq-\deg(M_{2})}
. Hence
{\rm Sind}(\tilde{L})=-\operatorname{res}_{\infty}(q_{2}/M_{2})
and then
{\rm Sind}(L)\geq-\operatorname{res}_{\infty}(q_{2}/M_{2})-\deg(\Delta(f))=-\nu
.

5.
Stable first order operators

Any solution of a first order operator over
k
is called a hyperexponential function over
k
.
A first order operator is stable if and only if its corresponding hyperexponential solution is stable.
The stability problem for hyperexponential functions of the form
f\exp(g)
with
f,g\in k
has been
solved in
(
chen2022ISSAC
)
.
In this section, we shall solve the stability problem for general hyperexponential functions by presenting
a necessary and sufficient condition on the stability of first order operators.

Theorem 5.1.

Suppose that
L=D+f
where
f\in C(x)
. Then
L
is stable if and only if
f
admits one of the following forms:

-\frac{h^{\prime}}{h}+\alpha\,\,\mbox{or}\,\,-\frac{h^{\prime}}{h}+\frac{\beta}{x-c},\,\,h\in C[x]\setminus\{0\},\alpha,\beta,c\in C

and
\beta
is not a positive integer.

Proof.

Suppose that
f
admits one of the above forms.
By Proposition
4.7
, it suffices to show that
D+\alpha
and
D+\beta/(x-c)
are stable. The case
D+\alpha
follows from Proposition
4.4
and the case
D+\beta/(x-c)
follows from Example
3.14
.

Now suppose that
L
is stable.
Set
h=\Delta(f)
and
\tilde{f}=f+h^{\prime}/h
. If
\tilde{f}=0
then there is nothing to prove. Suppose that
\tilde{f}\neq 0
. Then there is no simple pole of
\tilde{f}
at which the residue of
\tilde{f}
is a negative integer, i.e.
\mathcal{S}(\tilde{f})=\emptyset
and
\Delta(\tilde{f})=1
. Using Proposition
4.9
repeatedly, one has that
\tilde{L}=L+h^{\prime}/h
is stable. Note that
\tilde{L}=D+\tilde{f}
.

Set
\tilde{f}_{1}={\rm num}(\tilde{f}_{1})
and
\tilde{f}_{2}={\rm den}(\tilde{f})
. Note that
\mathcal{S}(\tilde{f})=\emptyset
. If
\operatorname{ord}_{\infty}(\tilde{f})<0
then
\deg(\tilde{f}_{1})>0
, and by Lemma
4.11
one has that

{\rm Sind}(\tilde{L})\geq\deg(\tilde{f}_{2})-\operatorname{ord}_{\infty}(\tilde{f})=\deg(\tilde{f}_{1})>0

a contradiction.
If
\operatorname{ord}_{\infty}(\tilde{f})>1
then
\deg(\tilde{f}_{2})>1
and
{\rm Sind}(\tilde{L})\geq\deg(\tilde{f}_{2})-1>0
by Lemma
4.11
. One obtains a contradiction again.
Therefore
\operatorname{ord}_{\infty}(\tilde{f})=0
or
\operatorname{ord}_{\infty}(\tilde{f})=1
.

Suppose that
\operatorname{ord}_{\infty}(\tilde{f})=0
. Then by Lemma
4.11
again,
0={\rm Sind}(\tilde{L})=\deg(\tilde{f}_{1})
. This implies that
\deg(\tilde{f}_{2})=0
for
\operatorname{ord}_{\infty}(\tilde{f})=0
.
Hence one has that
\alpha=\tilde{f}\in C\setminus\{0\}
and then
f=-h^{\prime}/h+\alpha
. Now we consider the case
\operatorname{ord}_{\infty}(\tilde{f})=1
. Then
\deg(\tilde{f}_{2})\geq 1
and
\operatorname{res}_{\infty}(\tilde{f})\neq 0
. If
\operatorname{res}_{\infty}(\tilde{f})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\leq-\deg(\tilde{f}_{2})}
then
by Lemma
4.11
,
{\rm Sind}(\tilde{L})=-\operatorname{res}_{\infty}(\tilde{f})\geq\deg(\tilde{f}_{2})\geq 1
, a contradiction. So
\operatorname{res}_{\infty}(\tilde{f})\notin\mathbb{Z}_{\leq-\deg(\tilde{f}_{2})}.

In this case, by Lemma
4.11
again, one sees that

0={\rm Sind}(\tilde{L})=\deg(\tilde{f}_{2})-1.

Therefore
\deg(\tilde{f}_{2})=1
and
\deg(\tilde{f}_{1})=0
.
Set
\beta=\tilde{f}_{1}
and write
\tilde{f}_{2}=x-c
for some
c\in C
. Then
f=-h^{\prime}/h+\beta/(x-c)
.

It remains to show that
\beta
is not a positive integer. Assume on the contrary that
\beta
is a positive integer. Since
\tilde{L}
is stable, by Proposition
4.1
, there is an
r\in k
such that
\tilde{L}^{*}(r)=(x-c)^{\beta-1}
. As
\Delta(\tilde{f})=1
, Lemma
4.6
implies that
r
is a polynomial. From
\tilde{L}^{*}(r)=(x-c)^{\beta-1}
, one has that

-r^{\prime}(x-c)+r\beta=(x-c)^{\beta}.

Write
r=\alpha x^{s}+\dots
, where
s=\deg(r)
. Then

-\alpha(s-\beta)x^{s}+\mbox{lower terms}=(x-c)^{\beta}.

Comparing the degrees of both sides of the above equality, one sees that there does not exist any integer
s
such that the above equality holds, a contradiction. Therefore
\beta
is not a positive integer.

Example 5.2.

Let
L=D-\frac{1}{2x}-\frac{1}{2(x+1)}
. Then
L
is the defining operator of
\sqrt{x(x+1)}
. By Theorem
5.1
,
L
is not stable. Notice that the method presented in
(
chen2022ISSAC
)
is not valid for this example.

6.
Iterated integration of special rational functions

In the last section, we shall consider the iterated integrals of the rational function
1/q
, where
q
is a nonzero polynomial in
C[x]
.
Write
q=\operatorname{lc}(q)\prod_{i=1}^{n}(x-c_{i})^{m_{i}}
. Then the first order operator with
1/q
as a fundamental solution is of the form

(6)

L=D+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{m_{i}}{x-c_{i}}.

Lemma 6.1.

Suppose that
L=D-f^{\prime}/f
where
f\in k\setminus\{0\}
. Then for a nonzero
d\in C[x]
,
\delta(L,d)=1
if and only if
df
is rationally integrable.

Proof.

For
r\in k\setminus\{0\}
, one has that

L^{*}(r)=-r^{\prime}-\frac{f^{\prime}}{f}r=-\frac{f^{\prime}r+fr^{\prime}}{f}=-\frac{1}{f}\left(rf\right)^{\prime}.

So
L^{*}(r)=d
if and only if
(-fr)^{\prime}=df
. In other words,
\delta(L,d)=1
if and only if
df
is rationally integrable.

The following lemma may seem folklore, but we did not find any precise reference. So we include its full proof
for the sake of completeness.

Lemma 6.2.

Suppose that
q=c_{0}\prod_{i=1}^{n}(x-c_{i})^{m_{i}}
with
c_{i}\in C
,
m_{i},n>1
and
p\in C[x]
is a nonzero polynomial. If either
\deg(p)<n-1
or
\deg(p)=\deg(q)-1
, then
p/q
is not rationally integrable.

Proof.

Assume that
\deg(p)<n-1
and assume on the contrary that
p/q
is rationally integrable, i.e. there is an
r\in C(x)
such that
r^{\prime}=p/q
. Set
r_{1}={\rm num}(r)
and
r_{2}={\rm den}(r)
. We claim that
q=\tilde{q}r_{2}\prod_{i=1}^{n}(x-c_{i})
for some nonzero
\tilde{q}\in C[x]
. Note that
c\in C
is a pole of
r_{1}/r_{2}
if and only if
c
is a pole of
r
. Hence we may write
r_{2}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(x-c_{i})^{l_{i}}
where
l_{i}>0
. For each
1\leq i\leq n
,
\operatorname{ord}_{c_{i}}(r^{\prime})=\operatorname{ord}_{c_{i}}(r)-1=-l_{i}-1
and
\operatorname{ord}_{c_{i}}(p/q)\geq-m_{i}
. These imply that
m_{i}\geq l_{i}+1
for all
1\leq i\leq n
. So
q=\tilde{q}r_{2}\prod_{i=1}^{n}(x-c_{i})
and our claim holds. Now from
r^{\prime}=p/q
, one has that
q(r_{1}^{\prime}r_{2}-r_{1}r_{2}^{\prime})=pr_{2}^{2}
. Cancelling
r_{2}
from both sides yields that

(7)

\tilde{q}(r_{1}^{\prime}r_{2}-r_{1}r_{2}^{\prime})\prod_{i=1}^{n}(x-c_{i})=pr_{2}.

If
\deg(r_{1})\neq\deg(r_{2})
then
\deg(r_{1}^{\prime}r_{2}-r_{1}r_{2}^{\prime})=\deg(r_{1})+\deg(r_{2})-1
. Suppose that
\deg(r_{1})=\deg(r_{2})
. Write
r_{1}=cr_{2}+h
where
c\in C
and
h\in C[x]
with
\deg(h)<\deg(r_{2})
. Since
\gcd(r_{1},r_{2})=1
,
h\neq 0
. One sees that
r_{1}^{\prime}r_{2}-r_{1}r_{2}^{\prime}=h^{\prime}r_{2}-hr_{2}^{\prime}
and then
\deg(r_{1}^{\prime}r_{2}-r_{1}r_{2}^{\prime})=\deg(h^{\prime}r_{2}-hr_{2}^{\prime})=\deg(h)+\deg(r_{2})-1
. In either case, one sees that
\deg(r_{1}^{\prime}r_{2}-r_{1}r_{2}^{\prime})\geq\deg(r_{2})-1
. Then the degree of the left-hand side of (
7
) is not less than
\deg(r_{2})-1+n
. However the degree of the right-hand side of (
7
) equals
\deg(p)+\deg(r_{2})
that is less than
\deg(r_{2})+n-1
. We obtain a contradiction. Thus
p/q
is not rationally integrable.

Finally suppose that
\deg(p)=\deg(q)-1
. Then the residue of
p/q
at infinity is not zero because
\operatorname{ord}(x^{2}p/q)=-1
. It is well-known that a rational function is rationally integrable if and only if all of its residues vanish. So
p/q
is not rationally integrable.

Proposition 6.3.

Let
L
be of the form (
6
) and let
L_{j}
be a
j
-th principal integral of
L
. Then

\operatorname{ord}(L_{j})=\begin{cases}\operatorname{ord}(L)+j,&0\leq j\leq n-1\\
\operatorname{ord}(L)+n-1,&n-1<j\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}-1\\
\operatorname{ord}(L)+n,&j\geq\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}\end{cases}.

Proof.

Note that
\frac{q^{\prime}}{q}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{m_{i}}{x-c_{i}}
. Due to Lemmas
6.1
and
6.2
,

\delta(L,d)=0
for any nonzero polynomial
d
of degree less than
n-1
. By Corollary
3.5
,
\operatorname{ord}(L_{j+1})=\operatorname{ord}(L_{j})+1
for all
0\leq j<n-1
. Thus
\operatorname{ord}(L_{j})=\operatorname{ord}(L)+j
for all
0\leq j\leq n-1
.

Set
N=\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}
. Assume that
N>n
. Then there is at least one
m_{i}
that is greater than 1. Without loss of generality, we assume that all of
m_{1},\dots,m_{s}
are greater than 1 and
m_{s+1}=\dots=m_{n}=1
. Suppose that
j
is an integer satisfying that
n-1\leq j<N-1
.
Then
\sum_{i=1}^{s}(m_{i}-1)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(m_{i}-1)\geq N-1-j
. Hence there exist integers
t,l_{1},\dots,l_{t}
satisfying that

1\leq t\leq s,0<l_{i}<m_{i}
and
\sum_{i=1}^{t}l_{i}=N-1-j
. Set
r=1/\prod_{i=1}^{t}(x-c_{i})^{l_{i}}
. Then

\left(\frac{1}{r}\right)^{\prime}=\frac{h}{\prod_{i=1}^{t}(x-c_{i})^{l_{i}+1}}

where
h
is a nonzero polynomial of degree
t-1
. Let
\tilde{d}=q(1/r)^{\prime}
. Then
\tilde{d}
is a nonzero polynomial because
l_{i}+1\leq m_{i}
, and

\deg(\tilde{d})=\deg(h)+\deg(q)-\sum_{i=1}^{t}(l_{i}+1)=j.

As
(1/r)^{\prime}=\tilde{d}/q
, Lemma
6.1
implies that
\delta(L,\tilde{d})=1
. Due to Corollary
3.5
again,
\operatorname{ord}(L_{j+1})=\operatorname{ord}(L_{j})
for all
n-1\leq j<N-1
. Thus
\operatorname{ord}(L_{j})=\operatorname{ord}(L_{n-1})=\operatorname{ord}(L)+n-1
for all
n-1<j\leq N-1
.

By Lemma
6.2
, we have that
p/q
is not rationally integrable if
\deg(p)=\deg(q)-1
. Therefore
\delta(L,d)=0
for any
d\in C[x]
of degree
N-1
. Corollary
3.5
then implies that
\operatorname{ord}(L_{N})=\operatorname{ord}(L_{N-1})+1=\operatorname{ord}(L)+n
. For each
j\geq N
, one has that

L^{*}(qx^{j-N+1})=(j-N+1)qx^{j-N}.

In other words,
L^{*}(qx^{j-N+1})
is a polynomial of degree
j
. By Corollary
3.5
,
\operatorname{ord}(L_{j+1})=\operatorname{ord}(L_{j})
for all
j\geq N
. Hence
\operatorname{ord}(L_{j})=\operatorname{ord}(L)+n
for all
j\geq N
.

Corollary 6.4.

Let
L
be of the form (
6
). Then
{\rm Sind}(L)=\deg(q)
.
