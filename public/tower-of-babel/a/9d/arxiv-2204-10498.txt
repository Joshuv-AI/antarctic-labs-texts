Detecting quantumness in uniform precessions
Lin Htoo Zaw, Clive Cenxin Aw, Zakarya Lasmar, Valerio Scarani

Abstract
Building on work by Tsirelson, we present a family of protocols that detect the nonclassicality of suitable states of a single quantum system, under the sole assumption that the measured dynamical observable undergoes a uniform precession. The case of the harmonic oscillator was anticipated in the work by Tsirelson, which we extend. We then apply the protocols to finite-dimensional spins that undergo uniform precession in real space and find a gap between the classical and the quantum expectations for every j ≥ 3 2 j\geq\frac{3}{2} (excluding j = 2 j=2 ).



I
Introduction

Uniform precession is one of the most basic forms of motion. If a Hamiltonian
H
generates uniform precession of some dynamical variables in classical theory, the quantum theory obtained by canonical quantization also predicts uniform precession of the corresponding observables in the Heisenberg representation.

The one-dimensional harmonic oscillator is paradigmatic. Imported into quantum theory by Heisenberg, as a side remark while tackling the nonharmonic oscillator
Heisenberg 1925
, it is now textbook knowledge, in the even simpler algebraic approach of Dirac
Dirac 1930
. Since Planck’s blackbody radiation and the Einstein-Debye specific heat, countless observations have been made of nonclassical features of harmonic systems. Today’s level of control over individual quantum harmonic systems extends from optical modes as the most explored platform
Haroche and Raimond 2006
to mechanical modes as the frontier
Ockeloen-Korppi et al. 2018
;
Kotler et al. 2021
;
Gonzalez-Ballestero et al. 2021
. All the reported quantum features of harmonic oscillators have to do with the kinematics (preparing nonclassical states, which are actually independent of the dynamics), or originate from the discrete nature of the energy spectrum (e.g. the presence of a zero-point energy). By contrast, the dynamics of the independent variables
(x,p)
is a uniform precession in phase space both in classical and in quantum theory. It is then thought that proper quantum features of harmonic oscillators have to be found elsewhere.

Building on a virtually unnoticed work by Tsirelson
Tsirelson 2006
, we show that this common belief is unwarranted. For suitable initial states, evidence of nonclassicality can be obtained from the statistics of one measurement, under the sole assumption that the observed dynamical variable undergoes a uniform precession. We extend Tsirelson’s observation to systems with finitely many levels (spins). A necessary condition to have a gap between the classical and the quantum prediction is that the precession couples noncommuting variables. The protocols introduced here constitute a different type of certification of nonclassicality.

In Sec.
II
we introduce the protocol for general uniformly precessing observables and derive the classical bounds. In Sec.
III
we apply the protocol to the same physical system as Tsirelson: precession in the phase space of the one-dimensional harmonic oscillator. In Sec.
IV
we consider precessions in real space due to spin angular momentum. Sec.
V
sketches some of the analytical results, whose detailed description and derivation are given in the Appendixes.

II
Classical bounds of uniformly-precessing observables

Consider two physical quantities
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
that define a precession, that is, their evolution in time is given by

\displaystyle A_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=A_{1}(0)\cos(\omega t)+A_{2}(0)\sin(\omega t)

(1)

\displaystyle A_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=A_{2}(0)\cos(\omega t)-A_{1}(0)\sin(\omega t),

for a fixed pulsation
\omega=2\pi/T
. For classical systems,
A_{1}(t)
and
A_{2}(t)
are the values of the physical quantities measured at times
t
; for quantum systems,
A_{1}(t)
and
A_{2}(t)
are their corresponding Hermitian operators given in the Heisenberg representation.

Divide one period
T
of the precession into
K
equal times
t_{k}=(k/K)T
, with
K>0
an integer and
k=0,1,...,K-1
. In every round of the protocol, one of the times
t_{k}
is chosen with uniform probability
1/K
, and the observable
A_{1}
is measured at that time. After several rounds, one estimates the average probability of having found
A_{1}>0
:

\displaystyle{P}_{K}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\Big[\textrm{Prob}(A_{1}>0\textrm{ at }t=t_{k})

(2)

\displaystyle+\tfrac{1}{2}\textrm{Prob}(A_{1}=0\textrm{ at }t=t_{k})\Big]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\pos(A_{1}(t_{k}))\,,

where
\pos(x)=\frac{1}{2}(1+\sgn(x))
is the Heaviside step function. The term
\frac{1}{2}\textrm{Prob}(A_{1}=0)
is needed to control the behavior at points
(A_{1}=0,A_{2})
, as we will see shortly.

We will denote by
\mathbf{P}_{K}
the maximum of
P_{K}
over all states. The results obtained with classical mechanics are denoted by
{P}_{K}^{c}
and
\mathbf{P}^{c}_{K}
.

In classical physics, a pure state, i.e., a state of maximal knowledge, is represented by a point
(A_{1}(t),A_{2}(t))
in the
A_{1}
-
A_{2}
plane, which precesses therefore with period
T
. Let us introduce the angle
\theta\equiv\operatorname{arctan2}(A_{2},A_{1})
and the open sectors

\displaystyle\Theta_{+k}

\displaystyle=\Big\{(A_{1},A_{2}):\theta\in\left(\tfrac{\pi}{2},\tfrac{\pi}{2}-\tfrac{\pi}{K}\right)-\tfrac{2\pi k}{K}\Big\}

(3)

\displaystyle\Theta_{-k}

\displaystyle=\Big\{(A_{1},A_{2}):\theta\in\left(\tfrac{\pi}{2}-\tfrac{\pi}{K},\tfrac{\pi}{2}-\tfrac{2\pi}{K}\right)-\tfrac{2\pi k}{K}\Big\}\,.

Due to the uniform precession, points initially in the region
\Theta_{\pm k_{0}}
will be in the region
\Theta_{\pm\bqty{(k_0 + k) \bmod K}}
at

.

A simple graphical argument [see Figs.

positive
K/2
out of
K

. Meanwhile, for odd

will have

positive
(K\pm 1)/2

K

\frac{1}{2}\textrm{Prob}(A_{1}=0)
in (

over phase space, the classical score satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{K}\right)\leq{P}_{K}^{c}\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{1}{K}\right)\equiv\mathbf{P}_{K}^{c}

for

III
Harmonic oscillator: precession in phase space

A one-dimensional material point is a system, whose only independent dynamical variables are position
x
and momentum
p
. This system is called a harmonic oscillator if its dynamics is generated by the Hamiltonian
H=\frac{1}{2m}(p^{2}+m\omega^{2}x^{2})
. Under this Hamiltonian,
x
and
p
evolve in time according to
x(t)=x(0)\cos\omega t+\tilde{p}(0)\sin\omega t
and
\tilde{p}(t)=\tilde{p}(0)\cos\omega t-x(0)\sin\omega t
, with
\tilde{p}=p/m\omega
. In other words, the evolution describes a precession in phase space.

Therefore, we can identify
(A_{1},A_{2})=(x,\tilde{p})
and perform the protocol as introduced in the preceding section. The scores obtained with the quantum harmonic oscillator are denoted by
{P}_{K}^{\infty}
and
\mathbf{P}^{\infty}_{K}
because we are dealing with an infinite-dimensional vector space.

This protocol with
K=3
was originally considered by Tsirelson
Tsirelson 2006
. He
found that
\mathbf{P}_{3}^{\infty}\gtrsim 0.709>\mathbf{P}_{3}^{c}=\frac{2}{3}
. The origin of such a gap between the quantum and the classical prediction must be traced to the fact that the precession couples incompatible variables. Every probability density satisfies the classical bound: to violate the bound, the joint distribution of
(x,p)
must be a quasi-probability density with negative values. A very compelling image of Tsirelson’s discovery is indeed obtained by plotting the Wigner function
W(x,p)
of the state that is numerically found to yield
\mathbf{P}_{3}^{\infty}
[Figs.
2
(a)–
2
(c)]. Anticipating the use of our techniques, to be described below, we also plot the state for
K=7
, for which
\mathbf{P}_{7}^{\infty}\gtrsim 0.61>\mathbf{P}_{7}^{c}=\frac{4}{7}
[Figs.
2
(d)–
2
(f)]. The fact that these Wigner functions have the same symmetry as the classical probabilities will be proved rigorously below, where it will also be used to derive an upper bound on
\mathbf{P}_{K}^{\infty}
.

Spin
j
: precession in real space

Under the Hamiltonian
H=-\omega J_{z}
, where
J_{z}
is the component of the angular momentum
\vec{J}
of the system in the
z
direction, any vector
\vec{V}
precesses around the
z
axis at pulsation
\omega
:
V_{x}(t)=V_{x}(0)\cos\omega t+V_{y}(0)\sin\omega t
, and
V_{y}(t)=V_{y}(0)\cos\omega t-V_{x}(0)\sin\omega t
,
V_{z}(t)=V_{z}(0)
. Hence, the protocol can be performed by identifying
(A_{1},A_{2})=(V_{x},V_{y})
. To have a quantum gap, we need a vector whose quantum description is such that
[V_{x},V_{y}]\neq 0
(for instance, the position vector
\vec{x}
would not do).

For this study, we choose
\vec{J}
itself, since famously
[J_{x},J_{y}]=i\hbar J_{z}
. For simplicity of notation, we assume in what follows that
\vec{J}
is a spin
j
(the results are of course unchanged for a subspace of fixed
j
of a more complex angular momentum), with Hilbert space dimension
d=2j+1
. Analogously to the preceding section, the scores obtained with the
d
-dimensional Hilbert space are denoted by
{P}_{K}^{d}
and
\mathbf{P}^{d}_{K}

We label
\ket{m_x}
the eigenvector of
J_{x}
for the eigenvalue
\hbar m

is then represented by the operator

\displaystyle\pos(J_{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\bqty{\mathbbm{1} + \sgn(J_x)},

(5)

where

\displaystyle\sgn(J_{x})=\sum_{m=-j}^{j}\sgn(m)\outerproduct{m_x}{m_x}\,.

(6)

Then, given a state,
{P}_{K}^{d}
will be the expectation value of the operator

\displaystyle Q_{K}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}e^{-i\theta_{k}J_{z}}\,\pos(J_{x})\,e^{i\theta_{k}J_{z}}\,\equiv\mathbb{E}_{K}[\pos(J_{x})],

(7)

with
\theta_{k}=2\pi k/K
. Thus, the maximum

is the largest eigenvalue of

For

for all

. Indeed, we see that

K

For
K

is also the largest; for larger

Analytical results

The starting point to obtain analytical results is the observation that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{K}[\outerproduct{m_z}{m'_z}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\outerproduct{m_z}{m'_z}\delta_{0,(m-m^{\prime})\bmod K}\,.

In words, for any observable

VI
Conclusion

In summary, we have presented a criterion to certify the nonclassical nature of a single quantum system based on its time evolution, when the latter is a simple precession at frequency
\nu
. Any detection of quantumness must involve incompatible variables and ours is not an exception. A gap with the classical bound can only happen when the precession couples noncommuting variables and is well understood in terms of quasiprobability distributions with negative values
Ferrie 2011
. Our criterion can be tested on many platforms. For discrete systems, the most obvious that come to mind are a proper spin, for instance, a nuclear spin, and Zeeman subspaces in atoms and ions. For continuous variables, one could consider optical, acoustic, or mechanical modes. The dynamics being usually embedded in the system, the challenge lies mainly in the creation of a suitable state and in the detection depending on the platform.

Detection of nonclassical features has been a prominent topic in recent decades, in the context of emerging quantum technologies. As any violation of the classical bound requires negativity in the Wigner function, our protocol is a negativity witness
Chabaud et al. 2021
. In terms of usefulness, at a very general level one can say that any of the states detected by our protocol, together with the set of Gaussian operations, is sufficient for universal quantum computation
Lloyd and Braunstein 1999
. More specifically, with the states
\ket{\Psi_K}
that are optimal for our protocol one can construct an order-
K
bosonic error-correcting code
Grimsmo et al. 2020
with the logical encoding
\ket{\pm_K}=(\pm 1)^{a^{\dagger}a}\ket{\Psi_K}
and
Z=e^{i(\pi/K)a^{\dagger}a}
.

Since it does not involve entanglement, our criterion cannot be a “black box,” that is, a device-independent certification
Brunner et al. 2014
. Indeed, two elements of characterization are needed: The dynamics must be a precession at a known pulsation
\omega
and one must be measuring the same variable (
x
or
J_{x}
in the cases we studied) at any of the probing times
t_{k}
.

The fact that only one measurement is performed in each round, that of a single dynamical variable, distinguishes our criterion from tests of contextuality
Budroni et al. 2021
and Leggett-Garg-type criteria
Leggett and Garg 1985
, which require the simultaneous or sequential measurement of two or more observables in each round. In particular, our criterion does not utilize the noninvasive measurements required in similar Leggett-Garg tests of harmonic oscillators
Bose et al. 2018
;
Mawby and Halliwell 2022
, which avoids the “clumsiness” loophole entirely
Wilde and Mizel 2012
.

We also highlight that, under the very plausible conjecture that
\max_{d}\mathbf{P}_{K}^{d}=\mathbf{P}_{K}^{K+1}
, the observation of this maximal quantum violation identifies uniquely the dimension and the state, under the assumptions that the dynamics is a precession and that one is measuring a component of angular momentum. This would be a different type of single-particle self-test: Previously reported instances of such tests assumed either a bound on the dimension of the system (see Sec. 9.2 of
Šupić and Bowles 2020
for a review) or the properties of a set of observables
Hu et al. 2022
.

Some directions of future research should be noted. First, there is a possible conceptual connection between our protocol and the concept of dynamic nonlocality, so called because the time evolution of some observables depends on the value of the potential at different locations
Popescu 2010
. An example of such observables are modular variables, like
x(t)\bmod x_{0}
and
p(t)\bmod p_{0}
, which have already been considered together with Leggett-Garg inequalities for tests of contextuality
Asadian et al. 2014
. Modular variables split phase space into periodic cells, analogous to how our protocol splits phase space into sectors (see Fig.
1
). Further work would be required to investigate if a formal relationship exists between our protocol and that of dynamic nonlocality.

Second, while we have focused on the violation of the classical bound by a
quantum
state, casting this protocol on a general probabilistic theory (GPT) framework is another avenue for future research. It is clear that epistemically restricted theories cannot reproduce the quantum gap, as they recover only a subtheory of quantum mechanics with Gaussian states, which cannot violate the classical bound
Spekkens 2016
. While there have been recent works on continuous-variable GPT that permit negative quasiprobabilities
Plávala and Kleinmann 2022
;
Lin and Dahlsten 2020
, the full set of states allowed under such theories is yet to be characterized.

Beyond these timely concerns, we repeat the core of our surprising result by paraphrasing Tsirelon’s title
Tsirelson 2006
: Quantumness can be certified simply by asking how often the coordinate of a uniformly precessing system is positive.

Appendix A
Properties of
Q_{K}

A.1
Block diagonal structure enforced by
\EE_{K}
.

From the main text,
\mathbf{P}^{d}_{K}
is the largest eigenvalue of the observable
Q_{K}=\EE_{K}[\pos(J_{x})]
. In this section, we will show that
Q_{K}
, or indeed
\EE_{K}[A]
for an arbitrary operator
A=\sum_{m,m^{\prime}}\matrixelement{m_z}{A}{m_z'}\;\outerproduct{m_z}{m_z'}
, is block diagonal. We begin by considering the effect of
\EE_{K}
on each basis operator
\outerproduct{m_z}{m_z'}
,

\displaystyle\EE_{K}[\outerproduct{m_z}{m_z'}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}e^{-i(2\pi k/K)J_{z}}\outerproduct{m_z}{m_z'}e^{i(2\pi k/K)J_{z}}

(16)

\displaystyle=\outerproduct{m_z}{m_z'}\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}e^{-i2\pi k\bqty{(m-m')/K}}.

The summation is a sum over the roots of unity, which evaluates to 1 when
K
is a factor of
m-m^{\prime}
, and 0 otherwise:
\EE_{K}[\outerproduct{m_z}{m_z'}]=\outerproduct{m_z}{m_z'}\delta_{0,(m-m^{\prime})\bmod K}
.

This means that
\matrixelement{m_z}{\mathbb{E}_K[A]}{m_z'}
is nonzero only when
m\bmod K=m^{\prime}\bmod K
, which in turn implies that
\mathbb{E}_{K}[A]
has a block diagonal structure, where each unique value of
(m-m^{\prime})\bmod K\in\{0,1,\cdots,\min(K-1,2j)\}
defines a separate subspace. Here,
(m-m^{\prime})\bmod K
is maximally
\min(K-1,2j)
as it cannot be greater than or equal to the dimension of the system
d=2j+1
. It is convenient to label the subspaces with
\bar{m}\in\{-j,-j+1,\cdots,\min(-j+K-1,j)\}
, where the
\bar{m}
th block refers to the block containing the state
\ket{m_z = \bar{m}}
.

In summary, we have

\displaystyle\EE_{K}\bqty{A}

\displaystyle=\bigoplus_{\bar{m}=-j}^{\min(j,-j+K-1)}\Pi_{K}^{(\bar{m})}A\Pi_{K}^{(\bar{m})},

(17)

where
\Pi_{K}^{(\bar{m})}
is a projection into the
\bar{m}
th subspace,

\Pi_{K}^{(\bar{m})}\equiv\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor({j-\bar{m}})/{K}\rfloor}\outerproduct{m_z=\bar{m}+kK}{m_z=\bar{m}+kK}.

(18)

The upper limit comes from the requirement that
m_{z}=\bar{m}+kK\leq j
and it follows that the dimension of the
\bar{m}
th subspace is
\lfloor\frac{j-\bar{m}}{K}\rfloor+1
.

Notice that when
d\leq K
,
\lfloor\frac{j-\bar{m}}{K}\rfloor\leq\lfloor\frac{d-1}{K}\rfloor=0
, so the dimension of every subspace is 1. This means that

\EE_{K}\bqty{A}=\operatorname{diag}_{z}(A)\qquad\text{when}\;d\leq K.

(19)

For later convenience, we define further splits of each subspace
\Pi_{K}^{(\bar{m})}
into even and odd subspaces

\displaystyle\Pi_{K}^{(\bar{m})}=\Pi_{K}^{(+\bar{m})}+\Pi_{K}^{(-\bar{m})}\,,

(20)

where

\displaystyle\Pi_{K}^{(+\bar{m})}

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor\pqty{j-\bar{m}}/{2K}\rfloor}\outerproduct{m_z = \bar{m}+2kK}{m_z = \bar{m}+2kK},

(21)

\displaystyle\Pi_{K}^{(-\bar{m})}

\displaystyle\equiv\hskip-7.5pt\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor\pqty{j-\bar{m}-K}/{2K}\rfloor}\hskip-7.5pt\outerproduct{m_z = \bar{m}+(2k+1)K}{\bar{m}+(2k+1)K}.

The observable

Property 1

\displaystyle\matrixelement{m_z}{\sgn(J_x)}{m_z'}

\displaystyle}{\displaystyle={}-\bra{m_z}e^{-i\pi J_{z}}\sgn(J_{x})e^{i\pi J_{z}}\ket{m_z'}

\displaystyle}{\displaystyle={}-(-1)^{m^{\prime}-m}\bra{m_z}\sgn(J_{x})\ket{m_z'},

\displaystyle\EE_{K}[\sgn(J_{x})]\pqty{e^{i\pi J_z}\ket{\lambda}}

\displaystyle}{\displaystyle={}-\lambda\pqty{e^{i\pi J_z}\ket{\lambda}},

where

. Therefore,

A.3
Even and odd subspace of

Section

projects onto the

\displaystyle=\Pi_{K}^{(+\bar{m})}\sgn(J_{x})\Pi_{K}^{(-\bar{m})}\sgn(J_{x})\Pi_{K}^{(+\bar{m})}

\displaystyle\lambda_{2}=\hskip-7.5pt\max_{\begin{array}[]{c}\bar{m},\ket{\psi},\\[-2.15277pt]

C.7.1
Proof of Inseparability

Let us assume that the maximally violating state is separable, that is,
\ket{\Psi_K}=\ket{\psi_1}_{j_{1}}\otimes\ket{\psi_2}_{j_{2}}
. Then

\displaystyle\pqty{\bra{j_1}_{j_1}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{j_2}}\ket{\Psi_K}

\displaystyle=\innerproduct{j_1}{\psi_1}_{j_{1}}\;\ket{\psi_2}_{j_{2}}

(67)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}C_{j_{1},j-j_{1}}^{j}\ket{j-j_1}_{j_{2}}+(-1)^{j_{1}+j_{2}-\frac{1}{2}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}C_{-j_{1},j+j_{1}}^{j}\ket{-j_1-j}_{j_{2}},

\displaystyle\pqty{\bra{j-j_2}_{j_1}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{j_2}}\ket{\Psi_K}

\displaystyle=\innerproduct{j-j_2}{\psi_1}_{j_{1}}\;\ket{\psi_2}_{j_{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}C_{j-j_{2},j_{2}}^{j}\ket{j_2}_{j_{2}}+(-1)^{j_{1}+j_{2}-\frac{1}{2}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}C_{j_{2}-j,2j-j_{2}}^{j}\ket{j_2-2j}_{j_{2}}.

Note that
C_{j_{1},j-j_{1}}^{j}
and
C_{j-j_{2},j_{2}}^{j}
are always nonzero for
j_{2}-j_{1}\leq j\leq j_{2}+j_{1}
, while
C_{-j_{1},j+j_{1}}^{j}
is nonzero only when
j=j_{2}-j_{1}
and
C_{j_{2}-j,2j-j_{2}}^{j}
is nonzero only when
j_{2}-j_{1}\leq j\leq j_{2}
. Hence, there are always terms on the right-hand side that are nonzero, which implies that both
\innerproduct{j_1}{\psi_1}_{j_{1}}
and
\innerproduct{j-j_2}{\psi_1}_{j_{1}}
must be nonzero. Therefore, we can divide the equation by these terms to obtain an expression for
\ket{\psi_2}_{j_{2}}
and compare these expressions for the different cases of
j_{1}
and
j_{2}
.

For the case
j_{2}<j\leq j_{1}+j_{2}
,

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_2}_{j_{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\innerproduct{j_1}{\psi_1}_{j_{1}}}C_{j_{1},j-j_{1}}^{j}\ket{j-j_1}_{j_{2}},

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_2}_{j_{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\innerproduct{j-j_2}{\psi_1}_{j_{1}}}C_{j-j_{2},j_{2}}^{j}\ket{j_2}_{j_{2}}.

These two expressions for
\ket{\psi_2}_{j_{2}}
can be consistent only when
\ket{j-j_1}_{j_{2}}=\ket{j_2}_{j_{2}}
. This cannot be satisfied when
j_{2}<j<j_{1}+j_{2}
, and in those cases
\ket{\Psi_K}\neq\ket{\psi_1}_{j_{1}}\otimes\ket{\psi_2}_{j_{2}}
.

Meanwhile, for the case
j=j_{1}+j_{2}
, we can write out
\ket{\Psi_K}
explicitly:

\ket{\Psi_K}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\bqty{
\ket{j_1}_{j_1}\otimes\ket{j_2}_{j_2} + (-1)^{\pqty{K-1}/{2}}\ket{-j_1}_{j_1}\otimes\ket{-j_2}_{j_2}
}.

This state is clearly entangled. Therefore,
\ket{\Psi_K}
is entangled when
j_{2}<j\leq j_{1}+j_{2}
.

For the case
j_{2}-j_{1}<j\leq j_{2}
,

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_2}_{j_{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\innerproduct{j_1}{\psi_1}_{j_{1}}}C_{j_{1},j-j_{1}}^{j}\ket{j-j_1}_{j_{2}},

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_2}_{j_{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\innerproduct{j-j_2}{\psi_1}_{j_{1}}}\Big[C_{j-j_{2},j_{2}}^{j}\ket{j_2}_{j_{2}}

\displaystyle}{\displaystyle+{}(-1)^{j_{1}+j_{2}-\frac{1}{2}}C_{j_{2}-j,2j-j_{2}}^{j}\ket{j_2-2j}_{j_{2}}\Big].

The two expressions for
\ket{\psi_2}_{j_{2}}
are clearly contradictory, so
\ket{\Psi_K}\neq\ket{\psi_1}_{j_{1}}\otimes\ket{\psi_2}_{j_{2}}
for
j_{2}-j_{1}<j\leq j_{2}
.

For the case
j=j_{2}-j_{1}
,

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_2}_{j_{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\innerproduct{j_1}{\psi_1}_{j_{1}}}\Big[C_{j_{1},j-j_{1}}^{j}\ket{j-j_1}_{j_{2}}

\displaystyle}{\displaystyle+{}(-1)^{j_{1}+j_{2}-\frac{1}{2}}C_{-j_{1},j+j_{1}}^{j}\ket{-j_1-j}_{j_{2}}\Big],

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_2}_{j_{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\innerproduct{j-j_2}{\psi_1}_{j_{1}}}\Big[C_{j-j_{2},j_{2}}^{j}\ket{j_2}_{j_{2}}

\displaystyle}{\displaystyle+{}(-1)^{j_{1}+j_{2}-\frac{1}{2}}C_{j_{2}-j,2j-j_{2}}^{j}\ket{j_2-2j}_{j_{2}}\Big].

In this case, the expressions can be equal only if
\ket{j-j_1}_{j_{2}}=\ket{j_2}_{j_{2}}
and
\ket{-j_1}_{j_{2}}=\ket{j_2-2j}_{j_{2}}
or if
\ket{j-j_1}_{j_{2}}=\ket{j_2-2j}_{j_{2}}
and
\ket{-j_1-j}_{j_{2}}=\ket{j_2}_{j_{2}}
. Every one of these conditions contradicts
j=j_{2}-j_{1}
. Therefore,
\ket{\Psi_K}
is entangled in all four cases.

As a particular case, if the smallest spin is
j_{1}=\frac{1}{2}
, then
\ket{\Psi_K}
is maximally entangled. Indeed, from (
66
) we have

\displaystyle\ket{\Psi_K}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\ket{-\tfrac{1}{2}}_{j_{1}}\otimes\Big[C_{-\frac{1}{2},j+\frac{1}{2}}^{j}\ket{j+\tfrac{1}{2}}_{j_{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad{}+{}(-1)^{j_{2}}C_{\frac{1}{2},j-\frac{1}{2}}^{j}\ket{-j+\tfrac{1}{2}}_{j_{2}}\Big]

\displaystyle\quad{}+{}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\ket{\tfrac{1}{2}}_{j_{1}}\otimes\Big[C_{\frac{1}{2},j-\frac{1}{2}}^{j}\ket{j-\tfrac{1}{2}}_{j_{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad{}+{}(-1)^{j_{2}}C_{-\frac{1}{2},j+\frac{1}{2}}^{j}\ket{-j-\tfrac{1}{2}}_{j_{2}}\Big],

where indeed
\absolutevalue{C_{-\frac{1}{2},j+\frac{1}{2}}^j}^{2}+\absolutevalue{C_{\frac{1}{2},j-\frac{1}{2}}^j}^{2}=1
for all
j
. Therefore,
\rho_{j_{1}}=\frac{1}{2}\mathbbm{1}_{j_{1}}
.

D.1
Block-diagonal structure enforced by
\EE_{K}

For an arbitrary operator
A^{\infty}
in Hilbert space,

\EE_{K}[A^{\infty}]=\bigoplus_{\bar{n}=0}^{K-1}\Pi_{K}^{(\bar{n})}A^{\infty}\Pi_{K}^{(\bar{n})},

(69)

where
\Pi_{K}^{(\bar{n})}\equiv\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\outerproduct{\bar{n}+kK}{\bar{n}+kK}
. We further split
\Pi_{K}^{(\bar{n})}
into even- and odd-subspace projectors:
\Pi_{K}^{(\bar{n})}=\Pi_{K}^{(+\bar{n})}+\Pi_{K}^{(-\bar{n})}
, where

\displaystyle\Pi_{K}^{(+\bar{n})}

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\outerproduct{\bar{n}+2kK}{\bar{n}+2kK},

(70)

\displaystyle\Pi_{K}^{(-\bar{n})}

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\outerproduct{\bar{n}+(2k+1)K}{\bar{n}+(2k+1)K}.

D.2
Properties and matrix elements of
\sgn(X)

The operator
\sgn(X)
transforms under the parity transformation
e^{i\pi a^{\dagger}a}
as

e^{i\pi a^{\dagger}a}\sgn(X)e^{-i\pi a^{\dagger}a}=-\sgn(X)\,.

(71)

This entails the following properties: (i)
\matrixelement{n}{\sgn(X)}{n'}=0
when
n
and
n^{\prime}
share the same parity and (ii) if
\lambda
is an eigenvalue of
\EE_{K}[\sgn(X)]
, so is
-\lambda
. Based on these, we can prove that
\mathbf{P}_{K}^{\infty}=\frac{1}{2}(1+\sqrt{\lambda_{2}})
, where
\lambda_{2}
is the maximum eigenvalue of
(\EE_{K}[\sgn(X)])^{2}
over just the even blocks (or equivalently odd, but here the analytical form happens to be nicer in the even case):

\lambda_{2}=\hskip-7.5pt\max_{\begin{array}[]{c}\bar{n},\ket{\psi},\\
\scriptstyle\innerproduct{\psi}{\psi}=1\end{array}}\hskip-7.5pt\bra{\psi}\Pi_{K}^{(+\bar{n})}\sgn(X)\Pi_{K}^{(-\bar{n})}\sgn(X)\Pi_{K}^{(+\bar{n})}\ket{\psi}\,.

(72)

As the matrix elements of
\bra{n}\sgn(X)\ket{n'}
are zero when
n^{\prime}
and
n
are both even or both odd, throughout the rest of the section we will consider only
n^{\prime}
odd and
n
even. By first expanding
\sgn(X)
in the position basis,

\displaystyle\matrixelement{n}{\sgn(X)}{n'}

(73)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential{x}\pqty{
\braket{n}{x}\!\braket{x}{n'}
- \braket{n}{-x}\!\braket{-x}{n'}
}

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential{x}\innerproduct{n}{x}\!\innerproduct{x}{n'},

where we have used
\innerproduct{-x}{n}=\innerproduct{x}{n}
for
n
even and
\innerproduct{-x}{n'}=-\innerproduct{x}{n'}
for
n^{\prime}
odd. These wave functions are well known and are given by

\displaystyle\innerproduct{x}{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{-\frac{n}{2}}e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2}}}{\sqrt{n!\sqrt{\pi}}}H_{n}(x),

(74)

\displaystyle\innerproduct{x}{n'}

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{-\frac{n^{\prime}}{2}}e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2}}}{\sqrt{n^{\prime}!\sqrt{\pi}}}H_{n^{\prime}}(x),

(75)

where
H_{n}(x)
and
H_{n^{\prime}}(x)
are the Hermite polynomials

\displaystyle H_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=n!(-1)^{-\frac{n}{2}}\sum_{k=0}^{\frac{n}{2}}\frac{(-1)^{k}\pqty{2x}^{2k}}{(2k)!\pqty{\frac{n}{2}-k}!},

(76)

\displaystyle H_{n^{\prime}}(x)

\displaystyle=n^{\prime}!(-1)^{\frac{n^{\prime}-1}{2}}\sum_{k=0}^{\frac{n^{\prime}-1}{2}}\frac{(-1)^{k}\pqty{2x}^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!\pqty{\frac{n'-1}{2}-k}!}.

(77)

Then
\matrixelement{n}{\sgn(X)}{n'}=\matrixelement{n'}{\sgn(X)}{n}
can be evaluated by substituting Eqs. (
74
)–(
77
) into Eq. (
73
),

\displaystyle\matrixelement{n}{\sgn(X)}{n'}

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential{x}\frac{2^{-\frac{n}{2}}e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2}}}{\sqrt{n!\sqrt{\pi}}}\pqty{
n! (-1)^{-\frac{n}{2}}
\sum_{k=0}^{\frac{n}{2}} \frac{(-1)^k}{(2k)!\pqty{\frac{n}{2}-k}!}\pqty{2x}^{2k}
}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\times\frac{2^{-\frac{n^{\prime}}{2}}e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2}}}{\sqrt{n^{\prime}!\sqrt{\pi}}}\pqty{
n'! (-1)^{\frac{n'-1}{2}}
\sum_{k'=0}^{\frac{n'-1}{2}} \frac{(-1)^{k'}}{(2k'+1)!\pqty{\frac{n'-1}{2}-k'}!}\pqty{2x}^{2k'+1}
}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\frac{n^{\prime}-n-1}{2}}2^{-\pqty{\frac{n'+n}{2}-1}}\sqrt{\frac{n!n^{\prime}!}{\pi}}\sum_{k=0}^{\frac{n}{2}}\sum_{k=0}^{\frac{n^{\prime}-1}{2}}\frac{(-1)^{k+k^{\prime}}2^{2(k+k^{\prime})}}{(2k)!(2k^{\prime}+1)!\pqty{\frac{n}{2}-k}!\pqty{\frac{n'-1}{2}-k'}!}\underbrace{\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential{x^2}e^{-x^{2}}(x^{2})^{k+k^{\prime}}}_{(k+k^{\prime})!}

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{\frac{n^{\prime}-n-1}{2}}2^{-\pqty{\frac{n'+n}{2}-1}}}{n^{\prime}-n}\sqrt{\frac{n^{\prime}}{\pi}\pmqty{\lx@physics@matrix\endlx@physics@matrix}\pmqty{\lx@physics@matrix\endlx@physics@matrix}}\qquad\text{for $n$ even, $n^{\prime}$ odd.}

(78)

D.3
Equivalence of
\sgn(J_{x})
and
\sgn(X)
as
j\to\infty

While the operator
J_{x}(t)=J_{x}\cos(\omega t)+J_{y}\sin(\omega t)
that we considered in the main text evolves similarly to the position operator
X(t)=X\cos(\omega t)+P\sin(\omega t)
considered by Tsirelson, these operators are not exactly equivalent as
\commutator{X}{P}=i\mathbbm{1}
while
\commutator{J_x}{J_y}=iJ_{z}
. Nonetheless, we will show in this section that
\sgn(J_{x})\to\sgn(X)
as
j\to\infty
, which means that
\mathbf{P}_{K}^{d}\to\mathbf{P}_{K}^{\infty}
in that limit.

We relabel the angular momentum states
\ket{\tilde{n}}\equiv\ket{m_z = \tilde{n}-j}
such that
\ket{\tilde{n}=0}
refers to the lowest-energy state of
H^{d}=\omega J_{z}
and
\ket{\tilde{n}}
refers to the
\tilde{n}
th excited state. This is analogous to
\ket{n}
being the
\tilde{n}
th excited state of
H^{\infty}=\hbar\omega a^{\dagger}a
.

Consider
\matrixelement{\tilde{n}}{\sgn(J_x)}{\tilde{n}'}
, where
\tilde{n}^{\prime}=j+m^{\prime}
is odd and
\tilde{n}=j+m
is even. Then

\displaystyle\matrixelement{\tilde{n}}{\sgn(J_x)}{\tilde{n}'}

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{\frac{\tilde{n}^{\prime}-\tilde{n}-1}{2}}2^{-(2j-1)}}{\tilde{n}^{\prime}-\tilde{n}}\sqrt{\tilde{n}^{\prime}\pmqty{\lx@physics@matrix\endlx@physics@matrix}\pmqty{\lx@physics@matrix\endlx@physics@matrix}}\times\begin{cases}\sqrt{(2j-\tilde{n}^{\prime})\pmqty{\lx@physics@matrix\endlx@physics@matrix}\pmqty{\lx@physics@matrix\endlx@physics@matrix}}&\text{$d$ odd},\\
\sqrt{(2j-\tilde{n})\pmqty{\lx@physics@matrix\endlx@physics@matrix}\pmqty{\lx@physics@matrix\endlx@physics@matrix}}&\text{$d$ even},\end{cases}

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{\frac{\tilde{n}^{\prime}-\tilde{n}-1}{2}}2^{-\pqty{\frac{\tilde{n}'+\tilde{n}}{2}-1}}}{\tilde{n}^{\prime}-\tilde{n}}\sqrt{\tilde{n}^{\prime}\pmqty{\lx@physics@matrix\endlx@physics@matrix}\pmqty{\lx@physics@matrix\endlx@physics@matrix}}

(79)

\displaystyle\hskip 135.0002pt{}\times{}\begin{cases}\sqrt{\pqty{1-\frac{\tilde{n}'}{j}}\times\sqrt{j}2^{-(2j-\tilde{n})}\pmqty{\lx@physics@matrix\endlx@physics@matrix}\times\sqrt{j}2^{-(2j-\tilde{n}^{\prime}-1)}\pmqty{\lx@physics@matrix\endlx@physics@matrix}}&\text{$d$ odd},\\
\sqrt{\pqty{1-\frac{\tilde{n}}{j}}\times\sqrt{j}2^{-(2j-\tilde{n}-1)}\pmqty{\lx@physics@matrix\endlx@physics@matrix}\times\sqrt{j}2^{-(2j-\tilde{n}^{\prime})}\pmqty{\lx@physics@matrix\endlx@physics@matrix}}&\text{$d$ even}.\end{cases}

(80)

The dependence of these matrix elements on
j
is mostly of the form

\sqrt{j}2^{-(2j-y)}\pmqty{\lx@physics@matrix\endlx@physics@matrix}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi(1-\frac{y}{2j})}}e^{\gamma}

(81)

for some
\gamma
, with
y
replaced by
\tilde{n}
,
\tilde{n}-1
,
\tilde{n}^{\prime}
or
\tilde{n}^{\prime}-1
for the corresponding expression in Eq. (
79
). The right-hand side of Eq. (
81
) is due to Stirling’s formula, where
\gamma
has an upper and lower bound
Robbins 1955

\begin{gathered}\frac{1}{12(2j-y)+1}-\frac{1}{3(2j-y)}\leq\gamma,\\
\gamma\leq\frac{1}{12(2j-y)}-\frac{2}{6(2j-y)+1}.\end{gathered}

(82)

As
j\to\infty
, both the lower and upper bounds of
\gamma
approaches 0, so Eq. (
81
) approaches
1/\sqrt{\pi}
. Hence,

\displaystyle\lim_{j\to\infty}\matrixelement{\tilde{n}}{\sgn(J_x)}{\tilde{n}'}

(83)

\displaystyle}{\displaystyle={}\frac{(-1)^{\frac{\tilde{n}^{\prime}-\tilde{n}-1}{2}}2^{-\pqty{\frac{\tilde{n}'+\tilde{n}}{2}-1}}}{\tilde{n}^{\prime}-\tilde{n}}\sqrt{\frac{\tilde{n}^{\prime}}{\pi}\pmqty{\lx@physics@matrix\endlx@physics@matrix}\pmqty{\lx@physics@matrix\endlx@physics@matrix}}

\displaystyle}{\displaystyle={}\matrixelement{n = \tilde{n}}{\sgn(X)}{n' = \tilde{n}'}.

The converse case for
\tilde{n}
odd and
\tilde{n}^{\prime}
even can be found from
\sgn(J_{x})=\sgn(J_{x})^{\dagger}
, while all other matrix elements are zero, so
\lim_{j\to\infty}\sgn(J_{x})=\sgn(X)
. Therefore,
\mathbf{P}^{\infty}_{K}
, in both its original formulation and the angular momentum system as
j\to\infty
, are the same.

D.4
Closed-form lower bound for
\mathbf{P}^{\infty}_{K}

Let
\lambda_{2}
be the maximum eigenvalue of
\{\EE_{K}\bqty{\sgn(X)}\}^{2}
. Since
\lambda_{2}\geq(\matrixelement{\psi}{(\EE_{K}\bqty{\sgn(X)})^2}{\psi}_{K}\bqty{\sgn(X)})^{2}\psi\psi
for
\innerproduct{\psi}{\psi}=1
, any normalized state
\ket{\psi}
provides us with a lower bound for
\lambda_{2}
, hence for
\mathbf{P}^{\infty}_{K}=\frac{1}{2}(1+\sqrt{\lambda_{2}})
.

From the block-diagonal structure of
\{\EE_{K}\bqty{\sgn(X)}\}^{2}
, we can consider just the even states. In addition, from the first few finite-dimensional cases given in appendix
C
and from solving for the eigenvectors of
Q_{K}^{\infty}
in a truncated Hilbert space, we deduce that the eigenvector of
\{\EE_{K}\bqty{\sgn(X)}\}^{2}
will have a large component of
\ket{0}
. Hence, we study the lower bound for
\ket{\psi}=\ket{0}
:

\displaystyle\lambda_{2}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\matrixelement{0}{\sgn(X)}{(2k+1)K}\!\!\matrixelement{(2k+1)K}{\sgn(X)}{0}

(84)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\pi}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{2^{-(2k+1)K}}{(2k+1)K}\pmqty{\lx@physics@matrix\endlx@physics@matrix}.

Equation (
84
) is reminiscent of the series expansion

\frac{2}{\pi\sqrt{x}}\arcsin(\sqrt{x})=\frac{2}{\pi}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{2^{-(2k+1)}}{2k+1}\pmqty{\lx@physics@matrix\endlx@physics@matrix}x^{k}.

(85)

In fact, we recognize the series in Eq. (
84
) to be a subseries of the right-hand side of Eq. (
85
) with
x=1
and values of
k
such that
k\bmod K=\frac{K-1}{2}
. Given that the full series converges to some function of
x
, the subseries can be expressed in terms of this function as
Chen 2010

\displaystyle\lambda_{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}e^{-i\frac{2\pi k}{K}\pqty{\frac{K-1}{2}}}\frac{2}{\pi\sqrt{e^{i\frac{2\pi k}{K}}}}\arcsin(\sqrt{e^{i\frac{2\pi k}{K}}})

(86)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{K}+\frac{2}{\pi K}\sum_{k=1}^{\pqty{K-1}/{2}}(-1)^{k}\arccos\bqty{2\sin(\frac{\pi k}{K})-1}.

The simplification was performed by expressing
\arcsin
in terms of the complex . In particular, for the case
K=3
, we have

\lambda_{2}\geq\frac{1}{3}\pqty{1 - \frac{2}{\pi} \arccos(\sqrt{3}-1)}\approx 0.174,

(87)

and therefore
\mathbf{P}_{K}^{\infty}\geq 0.7087
.
