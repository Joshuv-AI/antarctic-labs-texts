The Cost of Sequential Adaptation and the Lower Bound for Mean Squared Error
Sergey Tarima

Abstract
Informative interim adaptations lead to random sample sizes. The random sample size becomes a component of the sufficient statistic and estimation based solely on observed samples or on the likelihood function does not use all available statistical evidence. The total Fisher Information (FI) is decomposed into the design FI and a conditional-on-design FI. The FI unspent by the interim adaptation is used to determine the lower mean squared error in post-adaptation estimation. Theoretical results are illustrated with simple normal samples collected according to a two-stage design with a possibility of early stopping.



Keywords:
Adaptive designs, adapted support, Cramer-Rao lower bound, group sequential designs, Fisher information, interim hypothesis testing

1
Introduction

Study designs with data-dependent sample sizes provide an attractive alternative to fixed sample size designs because interim decisions may reduce cost and decrease the duration of the study. Two groups of such sequential procedures, namely, groups sequential designs (GSD) and sample size re-estimation (SSR), are popular examples of studies with random sample sizes [see, for example,
Jennison and
Turnbull 1999
;
Proschan
et al. 2006
;
Chuang-Stein et al. 2006
;
Friede and
Kieser 2006
]. Our manuscript mostly deals with GSDs and SSR, but other informative adaptive designs may also benefit from the theoretical findings reported here.

The benefits of interim adaptations come with certain costs as the distributions of sample-based statistics change [
Armitage
et al. 1969
;
Efron et al. 1975
;
Siegmund 1985
;
Liu and Hall 1999
;
Liu
et al. 2006
;
Tarima and
Flournoy 2022
]. Some recognized consequences are that Bayesian procedures (that typically are not adjusted for multiple testing) do not control Type 1 error [see Table 18.1 in
Jennison and
Turnbull 1999
], maximum likelihood estimators become biased [
Whitehead 1986
], and Wald confidence intervals do not provide the desired coverage after GSDs or SSRs.
In this paper we use FI to quantify the information loss associated with interim decisions using interim data that depends on the parameter of interest.

For simplicity of exposition, we focus on two-stage adaptive designs with a single interim analysis. Extension to multiple interim analyses follows from recursive application of the presented methods. For illustrative purposes we use a normal example with arbitrary sample sizes
n_{1}
and
n_{2}
for stage 1 and 2, respectively.

Marschner 2021

. Section

New results reported in Sections

2.1
Cramer-Rao Lower Bound (CRLB) if no early stopping is possible

Let
\tilde{\theta}_{d}=\tilde{\theta}_{d}\left(\mathbf{X}_{d}\right)
be a stage-specific estimator for which
\textrm{E}[\tilde{\theta}_{d}]=\theta+b_{d}(\theta)
and
\left(\partial/\partial\theta\right)f_{\mathbf{X}_{d}}(t|\theta)
exist and
\partial/\partial\theta
can be passed under the integral sign in
\int f_{\mathbf{X}_{d}}(t|\theta)dt=1
and
\int t\ f_{\mathbf{X}_{d}}(t|\theta)dt
. Then

\textrm{Var}[\tilde{\theta}_{d}]\geq\frac{\left[1+\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}b_{d}(\theta)\right]^{2}}{{\cal{I}}_{\mathbf{X}_{d}}(\theta)}

(1)

and

\textrm{E}\left[(\tilde{\theta}_{d}-\theta)^{2}\right]\geq\frac{\left[1+\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}b_{d}(\theta)\right]^{2}}{{\cal{I}}_{\mathbf{X}_{d}}(\theta)}+b_{d}^{2}(\theta).

(2)

In a similar manner, for an estimator
\tilde{\theta}_{(2)}
defined
\mathbf{X}_{(2)}=\left(\mathbf{X}_{1},\mathbf{X}_{2}\right)
:

\textrm{Var}[\tilde{\theta}_{(2)}]\geq\frac{\left[1+\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}b_{(2)}(\theta)\right]^{2}}{{\cal{I}}_{\mathbf{X}_{(2)}}(\theta)}

(3)

and

\textrm{E}\left[(\tilde{\theta}_{(2)}-\theta)^{2}\right]\geq\frac{\left[1+\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}b_{(2)}(\theta)\right]^{2}}{{\cal{I}}_{\mathbf{X}_{(d)}}(\theta)}+b^{2}_{(2)}(\theta),

(4)

where
\textrm{E}[\tilde{\theta}_{(2)}]=\theta+b_{(2)}(\theta)
.
If
\mathbf{X}_{1}
and
\mathbf{X}_{2}
are independent
{\cal{I}}_{\mathbf{X}_{(2)}}={\cal{I}}_{\mathbf{X}_{1}}+{\cal{I}}_{\mathbf{X}_{2}}
.

The Cramer-Rao lower bound (CRLB) was suggested independently by
Rao 1945
and
Cramér 1946
under the assumption of fixed sample sizes.

2.2
Normal Example

Let
t_{d}=\sum_{i=n_{(d-1)}+1}^{n_{(d)}}x_{i}
. Now if
X_{i}\sim N(\theta,1)
, the stage-specific MLE is
\hat{\theta}_{d}=n_{d}^{-1}t_{d}\sim N(\theta,n_{d}^{-1})

with FI

{\cal{I}}_{\mathbf{X}_{d}}(\theta)={\cal{I}}^{obs}_{\mathbf{X}_{d}=\mathbf{x}_{d}}(\theta)=-\left(\partial^{2}/\partial\theta^{2}\right)l(\theta|\mathbf{X}_{d}=\mathbf{x}_{d})=n_{d}.

The random variable
(\hat{\theta}_{1},\hat{\theta}_{2})
is defined on the probability space
\left(R^{2},{\cal{B}},P\right)
, where
R^{2}
is the sample space [
R=\left(-\infty,\infty\right)
];

{\cal{B}}
is the Borel
\sigma
-algebra on
R^{2}
; and
if both
\mathbf{X}_{1}=\mathbf{x}_{1}
and
\mathbf{X}_{2}=\mathbf{x}_{2}
are
observed independently, the probability measure
P
is a bivariate normal distribution with mean
(\theta,\theta)
, variances
n_{1}^{-1}
and
n_{2}^{-1}
, and zero correlation. Then, the observed and expected Fisher informations are equal and

{\cal{I}}^{obs}_{\mathbf{X}_{1}=\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{X}_{2}=\mathbf{x}_{2}}(\theta)=-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\theta^{2}}l(\theta|\mathbf{X}_{1}=\mathbf{x}_{1})=-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\theta^{2}}l(\theta|\mathbf{X}_{2}=\mathbf{x}_{2})=n_{1}+n_{2}.

3
Two-Stage Experiments with the Possibility of Early Stopping

Let
Z_{1}=Z_{1}\left(\mathbf{X}_{1}\right)
be an interim test statistic based on stage one data alone. What is the distribution of two stage-specific statistics
\tilde{\theta}_{1}=\tilde{\theta}_{1}\left(\mathbf{X}_{1}\right)
and
\tilde{\theta}_{2}=\tilde{\theta}_{2}\left(\mathbf{X}_{2}\right)
in
sequential settings
when
\tilde{\theta}_{2}
is only observed if
z_{1}<c_{1}
, where
c_{1}
is some pre-determined critical value?

Let
D
denote an interim rule for choosing a decision from a decision space
{\cal{D}}
. In GSDs with one interim analysis, the interim decision rule is

{\cal{D}}=\begin{cases}1=\text{`stop'}&\text{if }z_{1}\geq c_{1};\\
2=\text{`collect }n_{2}\text{ extra observations'}&\text{if }z_{1}<c_{1}.\end{cases}

(5)

3.1
The Probability Distribution of the Observable Random Variables

In such sequential settings, the joint support of
(\tilde{\theta}_{1},\tilde{\theta}_{2}
) changes from that of independent statistics as
\tilde{\theta}_{2}
is not just missing but impossible when
Z_{1}\geq c_{1}
.
Tarima and
Flournoy 2022
observe that because some sampling combinations of
\tilde{\theta}_{1}
and
\tilde{\theta}_{2}
are not observable,
the support of
\tilde{\theta}_{1}
and
\tilde{\theta}_{2}
becomes

{\cal{T}}=\left\{\tilde{\theta}_{1}|z_{1}\geq c_{1}\right\}\cup\left\{\{\tilde{\theta}_{1}|z_{1}<c_{1}\}\cap\{\tilde{\theta}_{2}\in R\}\right\}.

Note that
{\cal{T}}\subset R^{2}
. If
A\in{\cal{T}}
, then the probability measure on the
\sigma
-algebra,
\sigma({\cal{T}})
is

\displaystyle\mu_{{\cal{T}}}=\text{Pr}[(\tilde{\theta}_{1},\tilde{\theta}_{2})\in A]

\displaystyle=\text{Pr}(Z_{1}\geq c_{1})\text{Pr}(\tilde{\theta}_{1}\in A|Z_{1}\geq c_{1})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\text{Pr}(Z_{1}<c_{1})\text{Pr}[(\tilde{\theta}_{1},\tilde{\theta}_{2})\in A|Z_{1}<c_{1}].

On the new probability space
\{{\cal{T}},\sigma({\cal{T}}),\mu_{{\cal{T}}}\}
, we have an observable random variable, namely,

\displaystyle\tilde{\theta}=\begin{cases}\tilde{\theta}_{1}&\textrm{ if }$D=1$\\
(\tilde{\theta}_{1},\tilde{\theta}_{2})&\textrm{ if }$D=2$.\end{cases}

Factor the density of
{\tilde{\theta}_{1}}
as

f_{\tilde{\theta}_{1}}(y_{1})=\sum_{d=1}^{2}f_{D}(d)[f_{\tilde{\theta}_{1}|D}(y_{1}|d)]^{I(D=d)}=f_{D}(d)[f_{\tilde{\theta}_{1}|D}(y_{1}|d)]^{I(D=d)},

(6)

where

f_{D}(d)=\prod_{d=1}^{2}[\text{Pr}(D=d)]^{I(D=d)}
is the probability distribution of the Bernoulli random variable
D
and

f_{\tilde{\theta}_{1}|D=d}
is a
random
probability distribution for different realizations of
D
:

\displaystyle f_{\tilde{\theta}_{1}|D=d}(y_{1}|d)=f_{\tilde{\theta}_{1}}(y_{1})\frac{I(D=d)}{\text{Pr}(D=d)}=\frac{\left[f_{\tilde{\theta}_{1}}(y_{1})\right]^{I(D=d)}}{\text{Pr}(D=d)}.

(7)

Alternatively,
\tilde{\theta}_{1}
can be viewed as a mixture of
\tilde{\theta}_{1}|D=1
and
\tilde{\theta}_{1}|D=2
with

f_{\tilde{\theta}_{1}}(y_{1})=\text{Pr}(D=1)f_{\tilde{\theta}_{1}|D=1}(y_{1}|D=1)+\text{Pr}(D=2)f_{\tilde{\theta}_{1}|D=2}(y_{1}|D=2).

(8)

The same decomposition applied to
f_{\tilde{\theta}}(\mathbf{y})
with
\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},y_{2})
provides

f_{\tilde{\theta}}(\mathbf{y})=\text{Pr}(D=1)f_{\tilde{\theta}_{1}|D=1}(y_{1}|D=1)+\text{Pr}(D=2)f_{\left(\tilde{\theta}_{1},\tilde{\theta}_{2}\right)|D=2}(\mathbf{y}|D=2).

(9)

Thus,
f_{\tilde{\theta}_{1}}(y_{1})
and
f_{\tilde{\theta}}(\mathbf{y})
both can be described as mixtures of distributions.

Using
f_{\tilde{\theta}_{1}|D=1}(y_{1}|D=1)
from Equation (
7
) and the analogous expression

\displaystyle f_{\tilde{\theta}|D=2}(\mathbf{y}|D=2)=f_{\left(\tilde{\theta}_{1},\tilde{\theta}_{2}\right)}(\mathbf{y})\frac{I(D=2)}{\text{Pr}(D=2)}=\frac{\left[f_{\left(\tilde{\theta}_{1},\tilde{\theta}_{2}\right)}(\mathbf{y})\right]^{I(D=2)}}{\text{Pr}(D=2)}

(10)

in Equation (
9
), another useful representation for the density
f_{\tilde{\theta}}(\mathbf{y})
is

\displaystyle f_{\tilde{\theta}}(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\left[f_{\tilde{\theta}_{1}}(y_{1})\right]^{I(D=1)}+\left[f_{\left(\tilde{\theta}_{1},\tilde{\theta}_{2}\right)}(\mathbf{y})\right]^{I(D=2)}

\displaystyle=\left[f_{\tilde{\theta}_{1}}(y_{1})\right]^{I(D=1)}\left[f_{\left(\tilde{\theta}_{1},\tilde{\theta}_{2}\right)}(\mathbf{y})\right]^{I(D=2)},

(11)

where the right-hand side of the mixture density can be represented as a sum or as a product of two
sub
densities.

3.2
The Likelihood Function’s Insensitivity to Early Stopping Possibilities

The log-likelihood function is conditional on
\mathbf{X}_{1}=\mathbf{x}_{1}
, and on
\mathbf{X}_{2}=\mathbf{x}_{2}
if stage-two observations are made, and consequently on the stopping stage
d
:

\displaystyle l(\theta|\mathbf{X}_{1}=\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{X}_{2}=\mathbf{x}_{2},D=d)=\begin{cases}l(\theta|\mathbf{X}_{1}=\mathbf{x}_{1})&\textrm{ if }D=1,\\
l(\theta|\mathbf{X}_{1}=\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{X}_{2}=\mathbf{x}_{2})&\textrm{ if }D=2.\end{cases}

Importantly, the log-likelihood function is the same as in the case of independent observations without the possibility of early stopping. More generally, the likelihood is insensitive to the effect of early stopping mechanisms. This insensitivity is inherited by the score function, the MLE, and the observed Fisher information.

The expected Fisher information, however, changes with early stopping options [see Section
4
]. Consider three experiments with
X_{i}\sim N(\theta,1)
:

E_{1}:

An experiment with no early stopping having a fixed sample size
n_{1}+n_{2}
and a joint sampling density
\prod_{i=1}^{n_{1}+n_{2}}f_{X_{i}}(x_{i}|\theta)
.

E_{2}:

A sequential experiment with early stopping if
\sqrt{n_{1}}\bar{X}_{1}>1.96
[motivated by a one-stage experiment with
\alpha=0.025
and no planned interim test] has joint sampling density
\prod_{i=1}^{n_{1}}f_{X_{i}}(x_{i}|\theta)\left[\prod_{i=n_{1}+1}^{n_{1}+n_{2}}f_{X_{i}}(x_{i}|\theta)\right]^{I\left(\sqrt{n_{1}}\bar{X}_{1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}<}1.96\right)}
.

E_{3}:

A sequential experiment with early stopping if
\sqrt{n_{1}}\bar{X}_{1}>2.78
[motivated by the
O’Brien and
Fleming 1979
stopping boundary] has joint sampling density

\prod_{i=1}^{n_{1}}f_{X_{i}}(x_{i}|\theta)\left[\prod_{i=n_{1}+1}^{n_{1}+n_{2}}f_{X_{i}}(x_{i}|\theta)\right]^{I\left(\sqrt{n_{1}}\bar{X}_{1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}<}2.78\right)}
.

The sampling density in
E_{1}
is defined on
{R}^{2}
, whereas the densities under
E_{2}
and
E_{3}
have support
\mathbf{\cal{T}}
as described in Section
3.1
.

Suppose each of the three experiments has been run, and
E_{2}
and
E_{3}
did not stop early because
\sqrt{n_{1}}\bar{X}_{1}>1.96
and
\sqrt{n_{1}}\bar{X}_{1}>2.78
are observed, respectively. Suppose further that same data (outcomes) are observed in all three experiments:
\mathbf{X}_{1}=\mathbf{x}_{1}

and
\mathbf{X}_{2}=\mathbf{x}_{2}
. Then despite different underlying designs and densities, all experiments (
E_{1}
,
E_{2}
and
E_{3}
) share the same likelihood function:

L(\theta|X_{1}=x_{1},\ldots,X_{n_{1}+n_{2}}=x_{n_{1}+n_{2}})=\prod_{i=1}^{n_{1}+n_{2}}f_{X_{i}}(x_{i}|\theta).

(12)

The likelihood
L(\theta|X_{1},\ldots,X_{n_{1}+n_{2}})
is a random variable, but its generating distributions differ across the three experiments, which illustrates that
L(\theta|X_{1},\ldots,X_{n_{1}+n_{2}})
is insensitive to the study design.
This is the same phenomenon as the well-known proportionality of the binomial and negative binomial likelihoods [see Example 9 in
Berger and
Wolpert 1988
and Example 6.3.7 on page 295 in
Casella and
Berger 2002
].

3.3
Normal Example (Two-Stage Design, Arbitrary
n_{1}
and
n_{2}
)

The log-likelihood function is

\displaystyle l\left(\theta\Big|\hat{\theta}_{(d)}=\frac{t_{(d)}}{n_{(d)}},D=d\right)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}n_{(d)}\left(t_{(d)}-\theta\right)^{2}\right),

where
t_{(d)}=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{(d)}}x_{i}
, and the value of
\hat{\theta}_{(2)}
is not observed when
D=2
. The interim test statistic is
Z_{1}=t_{1}/\sqrt{n_{1}}
and the MLE

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}

\displaystyle=I(z_{1}\geq c_{1})\frac{t_{1}}{n_{1}}+I(z_{1}<c_{1})\left(\frac{t_{(2)}}{n_{(2)}}\right)

\displaystyle=I(z_{1}\geq c_{1})\hat{\theta}_{1}+I(z_{1}<c_{1})\left(\frac{n_{1}}{n_{(2)}}\hat{\theta}_{1}+\frac{n_{2}}{n_{(2)}}\hat{\theta}_{2}\right)

\displaystyle=I(z_{1}\geq c_{1})\hat{\theta}_{1}+I(z_{1}<c_{1})\hat{\theta}_{(2)},

(13)

is a random variable defined on
\left({\cal{T}},\sigma({\cal{T}}),\mu_{{\cal{T}}}\right)
. Significantly,
\widehat{\theta}
is a mixture of a left truncated normal random variable
\{\hat{\theta}_{1}|D=1\}
and the weighted average of a right truncated normal
\{\hat{\theta}_{1}|D=2\}
and a normal
\hat{\theta}_{2}
. Thus, in this sequential setting,
\widehat{\theta}
is no longer a normal random variable.

4
Fisher Information with Informative Interim Decisions

To this point we have focused on simple binary interim decisions (Section
4.1
), but to accommodate SSR procedures, Section
4.2
introduces a wider decision space and more complex decision functions; FI is decomposed to expose the proportion consumed by the interim decision options for both GSD and SSR procedures.

4.1
Fisher Information with an Interim Test

Assuming standard regularity conditions [e.g.
Ferguson 1996
], FI in
\mathbf{X}_{1}
conditional on the interim decision variable
D
is the variance of the score function for

f_{\mathbf{X}_{1}|D}(y_{1}|D)
:

\displaystyle{\cal{I}}_{\mathbf{X}_{1}|D}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\textrm{Var}\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\log f_{\mathbf{X}_{1}|D}(y_{1}|D)\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\theta^{2}}[\log f_{\mathbf{X}_{1}|D}(y_{1}|D)]f_{\mathbf{X}_{1}}(\mathbf{x}_{1})d\mathbf{x}_{1}.

(14)

In contrast, FI in
\mathbf{X}_{1}
conditional on a specific
realization

d
of an interim decision is

\displaystyle{\cal{I}}_{\mathbf{X}_{1}|D=d}(\theta)=\int_{\infty}^{\infty}I(D=d)\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\theta^{2}}\left[-\log f_{\mathbf{X}_{1}|D}(\mathbf{x}_{1}|d)\right]f_{\mathbf{X}_{1}|D}(\mathbf{x}_{1}|d)d\mathbf{x}_{1}

\displaystyle\hskip-6.0pt=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\theta^{2}}\left[-\log\frac{f_{\mathbf{X}_{1}}(\mathbf{x}_{1})}{\text{Pr}(z_{1}\geq c_{1})}\right]\frac{f_{\mathbf{X}_{1}}(\mathbf{x}_{1})I(z_{1}\geq c_{1})}{\text{Pr}(z_{1}\geq c_{1})}d\mathbf{x}_{1}&\hskip-8.0pt\text{ if }z_{1}\geq c_{1};\\[10.0pt]
\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\theta^{2}}\left[-\log\frac{f_{\mathbf{X}_{1}}(\mathbf{x}_{1})}{\text{Pr}(z_{1}<c_{1})}\right]\frac{f_{\mathbf{X}_{1}}(\mathbf{x}_{1})I(z_{1}<c_{1})}{\text{Pr}(z_{1}<c_{1})}d\mathbf{x}_{1}&\hskip-8.0pt\text{ if }z_{1}<c_{1}.\end{cases}

(15)

We note that the integration is multiple to highlight the fact that
\mathbf{x}_{1}
has
n_{1}
components and each is integrated out.

The information measures defined by Equations (
14
) and (
4.1
) differ by the type of conditioning:
{\cal{I}}_{\mathbf{X}_{1}|D}(\theta)
averages over distributions of
\mathbf{X}_{1}
(and
D
is fully determined by
\mathbf{X}_{1}
), whereas
{\cal{I}}_{\mathbf{X}_{1}|D=d}(\theta)
averages over the distribution of the
conditional
random variable
\mathbf{X}_{1}|D=d
. Conditioning on a random variable, as in (
14
), is used in
Zegers 2015
for example, while conditioning on an observed constant, as in (
4.1
), is used in
Mihoc and
Ioana Fatu 2003
.

While in this manuscript, we condition is on
D=d
unless stated otherwise. However,
{\cal{I}}_{\mathbf{X}_{1}|D}(\theta)
provides an important measure of the information left in
\mathbf{X}_{1}
after informative adaptation.
Taking the
\log
of the joint density
f_{\mathbf{X}_{1}}(\mathbf{x}_{1})=f_{D}(d)f_{\mathbf{X}_{1}|D}(\mathbf{x}_{1}|d)^{I(D=d)}

and using the law of total variance on the score function, FI in
\mathbf{X}_{1}
and

\mathbf{X}_{\cal{T}}
, respectively, can be written as

\displaystyle{\cal{I}}_{\mathbf{X}_{1}}(\theta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\cal{I}}_{D}(\theta)+{\cal{I}}_{\mathbf{X}_{1}|D}(\theta)

(16)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\cal{I}}_{D}(\theta)+\text{Pr}(D=1|\theta){\cal{I}}_{\mathbf{X}_{1}|D=1}(\theta)+\text{Pr}(D=2|\theta){\cal{I}}_{\mathbf{X}_{1}|D=2}(\theta)

and

\displaystyle{\cal{I}}_{\mathbf{X}_{\cal{T}}}(\theta)=

\displaystyle{\cal{I}}_{D}(\theta)+\text{Pr}\left(D=1|\theta\right){\cal{I}}_{\mathbf{X}_{1}|D=1}(\theta)+\text{Pr}\left(D=2|\theta\right)\left[{\cal{I}}_{\mathbf{X}_{1}|D=2}(\theta)+{\cal{I}}_{\mathbf{X}_{2}}(\theta)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\cal{I}}_{D}(\theta)+{\cal{I}}_{\mathbf{X}|D}(\theta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\cal{I}}_{D}(\theta)+{\cal{I}}_{\mathbf{X}_{1}}(\theta)+\text{Pr}\left(D=2|\theta\right){\cal{I}}_{\mathbf{X}_{2}}(\theta),

(17)

where
{\cal{I}}_{D}(\theta)
is
the portion of the total FI that is consumed by permitting one interim test in a two-stage design (the design information):

{\cal{I}}_{D}(\theta)=\textrm{Var}\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\log f_{D}(d)\right]=\sum_{d=1}^{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\theta^{2}}\left[-\log\text{Pr}(D=d|\theta)\right]\text{Pr}(D=d|\theta).

(18)

4.2
Fisher Information with Sample Size Re-estimation

Given a sample size re-estimation formula, there are still many ways to construct the procedure to be used in an application. For example, if the calculated estimate is below the current sample size
n_{1}
, the procedure may call for the study to be stopped; if the estimate exceeds a given limit it will be truncated. To accommodate such practical considerations, we define the decision rule as a function of the available data
\mathbf{x}_{1}
rather than the summary
Z
-statistic.

Let
\left\{{\cal{C}}_{1},\ldots,{\cal{C}}_{K}\right\}
be a partition of all possible realizations of stage 1 data. Then the SSR can be formalized by the following decision function

{\cal{D}}=\begin{cases}1=\text{`stop'}&\text{if }\mathbf{x}_{1}\in{\cal{C}}_{1}\\
2=\text{`collect }n_{2}^{(2)}\text{ extra observations'}&\text{if }\mathbf{x}_{1}\in{\cal{C}}_{2}\\
\vdots\ =\,\vdots&\vdots\\
K=\text{`collect }n_{2}^{(K)}\text{ extra observations'}&\text{if }\mathbf{x}_{1}\in{\cal{C}}_{K}\end{cases}

(19)

The decision space specified by (
19
) accommodates all possible sample size recalculation procedures (e.g., procedures based on the observed variability of the treatment effect, the observed treatment effect itself, etc.). The resulting support
\left({\cal{T}}\right)
and the probability measure of the observable random variables are defined as generalizations of those detailed in Section
3.1
.

Note that different “labels” may be assigned to the
\{1,\ldots,K\}
decisions in Equation (
19
) to describe other types of interim decisions (e.g., joint futility and efficacy stopping, enrichment procedures).

The sequential decomposition of FI with an interim test shown in Equation (
4.1
) is extended to
designs that have multiple decision options at the time of the interim analysis in Theorem
1
:

Theorem 1
.

If a decision
d\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
can be made at an interim analysis, the following decomposition of Fisher Information applies:

\displaystyle{\cal{I}}_{\mathbf{X}_{\cal{T}}}(\theta)=

\displaystyle{\cal{I}}_{D}(\theta)+\sum_{d=1}^{K}\text{Pr}\left(D=d|\theta\right)\left[{\cal{I}}_{\mathbf{X}_{1}|D=d}(\theta)+{\cal{I}}_{\mathbf{X}_{2}|D=d}(\theta)\right].

(20)

When the interim decision
D=d
is made, the amount of FI available for further inferential (e.g., further hypothesis testing and estimation) consists of the previous information conditional on
D=d
[
{\cal{I}}_{\mathbf{X}_{1}|D=d}(\theta)
] and the new information to be collected before next analysis [
{\cal{I}}_{\mathbf{X}_{2}|D=d}(\theta)
].

If there is more than one interim analysis, Theorem
1
can be applied sequentially with
\mathbf{X}_{1}
referring to the data collected before an interim analysis and
\mathbf{X}_{2}
referring to the data that might be collected after the interim analysis. Thus, Theorem
1
describes how Fisher Information is allocated across the whole decision tree branching overtime with interim analyses.

5
Lower Bound for Mean Squared Error in Sequential Experiments

Section
2.1
reviews how Fisher information appears in the CRLB inequality that determines the minimum variance, and more generally the minimum MSE among all regular estimators. Moreover, this bound is reached in one-parameter exponential family models where the MLE absorbs all statistical evidence about the canonical parameter
\theta
. Section
4
shows that the situation changes with informative interim adaptations: not all Fisher information is available for post-adaptation inference. Thus, CRLB inequality needs to be extended to account for interim adaptations.

Theorem 2
.

(Lower Bounds for Mean Squared Error in Sequential Experiments)

If, for any interim decision
d\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
,
{\cal{I}}_{\mathbf{X}_{\cal{T}}|D=d}(\theta)
exists for any
\theta
and
\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}E(\tilde{\theta}\mid D=d)=E(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\tilde{\theta}\mid D=d)
, then

1.

conditionally on
D=d
, the lower boundary for the MSE of
\tilde{\theta}
is

\textrm{E}\left([\tilde{\theta}_{(d)}-\theta]^{2}\right)\geq\frac{\left[1+\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}b(\theta)\right]^{2}}{{\cal{I}}_{\mathbf{X}_{\cal{T}}|D=d}(\theta)}+b_{(d)}^{2}(\theta)

(21)

2.

and, unconditionally, the lower bound is

\textrm{E}\left([\tilde{\theta}-\theta]^{2}\right)\geq\sum_{d=1}^{K}P_{d}(\theta)\left[\frac{\left[1+\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}b_{(d)}(\theta)\right]^{2}}{{\cal{I}}_{\mathbf{X}_{\cal{T}}|D=d}(\theta)}+b_{(d)}^{2}(\theta)\right],

(22)

where
E(\tilde{\theta}_{(d)})=\theta+b_{(d)}(\theta)
.

Proof of Theorem
2
:
Existence of Fisher information and interchangeability of integration and differentiation ensures existence of the Cramer-Rao lower boundary at each
D=d
, which proves inequality (
21
). From
\textrm{E}([\hat{\theta}-\theta]^{2})=\sum_{d=1}^{K}P_{d}(\theta)\textrm{E}([\hat{\theta}_{(d)}-\theta]^{2})
, inequality (
22
) immediately follows.

Q.E.D.

Corollary 3
.

The MLE for a canonical parameter in the one-parameter exponential family attain the CRLB
.

The MLE
\hat{\theta}
in the one-parameter exponential family reaches the CRLB with minimum MSE:

\textrm{E}\left([\hat{\theta}-\theta]^{2}\right)=\sum_{d=1}^{K}P_{d}(\theta)\left(\frac{\left[1+\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}b(\theta)\right]^{2}}{{\cal{I}}_{\mathbf{X}_{\cal{T}}|D=d}(\theta)}+b_{(d)}^{2}(\theta)\right).

(23)

Proof of Corollary
3
:

Tarima and
Flournoy 2022
showed that the possibility of early stopping after an MLE-based interim hypothesis
test of the form
\hat{\theta}_{1}>c_{1}

changes the distribution of the test statistic, but conditionally on
D=d
,
\hat{\theta}_{(d)}
continues being sufficient and to belong to the exponential family. Same argument applies for interim decision specific
\hat{\theta}_{(d)}
, where interim decision are defined by (
19
). Thus, under Theorem
2
assumptions, the Cramer-Rao lower boundary is reached for any
D=d
:

\textrm{E}\left([\hat{\theta}_{(d)}-\theta]^{2}\right)=\frac{\left[1+\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}b(\theta)\right]^{2}}{{\cal{I}}_{\mathbf{X}_{\cal{T}}|D=d}(\theta)}+b_{(d)}^{2}(\theta)

(24)

and
{\cal{I}}_{\hat{\theta}_{(d)}}(\theta)={\cal{I}}_{\mathbf{X}_{\cal{T}}|D=d}(\theta)
. Further, from

\textrm{E}\left([\hat{\theta}-\theta]^{2}\right)=\sum_{d=1}^{K}P_{d}(\theta)\textrm{E}\left([\hat{\theta}_{(d)}-\theta]^{2}\right)

the result follows.

Q.E.D.

7
Summary

The cost of adaptation is quantified. We show how the cost of an interim test for early stopping varies with the parameter of interest and how it changes with the specific stopping rule even though the likelihood is insensitive to the study design and the data collection procedure.

Theorem
1
decomposes Fisher Information into a component resulting from the inclusion of informative decisions in the design, and separate components for before and after interim decision points. Formula (
4.1
) details this decomposition for an experiment having a single interim test. This decomposition of the total Fisher information allows us to make the following useful observations:

•

With non-informative adaptations,
{\cal{I}}_{D}(\theta)=0
; no information is lost for likelihood-based inference. For example, because the variance estimate of a normal random variable is independent of the estimated effect size, there is no loss of information when sample size re-estimation is based only on an estimate of the standard deviation at the time of the interim analysis.

•

It is possible that all the information in stage 1 is used for sample size re-estimation, in which case none is left for final estimation.

•

The higher
{\cal{I}}_{D}(\theta)
is the less Fisher information is left for estimation after stopping.

•

The Fisher Information available in design,
{\cal{I}}_{D}(\theta)
, is a measure of the cost of informative stopping. This information is not absorbed by the likelihood function and is lost for likelihood-based inference. More generally, any estimators based solely on the observed data do not use the design information.

•

With one interim test for early stopping, the greatest information loss occurs when the critical value
c_{1}
is equal to the parameter of interest
\theta
. At this value of
\theta
post-testing estimation is affected the most.

•

Fisher information available for estimation of
\theta
or further testing after stopping at stages 1 or proceeding to stage 2 is
{\cal{I}}_{\mathbf{X}_{1}|D=1}(\theta)
and
{\cal{I}}_{\mathbf{X}_{1}|D=2}(\theta)+{\cal{I}}_{\mathbf{X}_{2}}(\theta)
, respectively.

Since some Fisher Information is spent for interim adaptation, the amount available for post-adaptation inference is smaller than the total. This is one reason why Cramer-Rao lower bound needs to be adjusted for informative interim adaptations. Another reason is the change in support of observable random variables. A sequential version of the Cramer-Rao lower bound suggested in
Wolfowitz 1947
does not apply to designs with informative interim adaptations: see
Simons 1980
for examples illustrating when Wolfowitz’s bound does not work.

Theorem
2
gives a new bound for the smallest mean squared error, and as shown in Corollary
3
this lower bound is reached by the MLE in one-parameter exponential family with canonical parameterization.

We anticipate that our results will be useful for deriving and justifying optimal sequential designs with a dual goal of sequential testing and post-test estimation.
