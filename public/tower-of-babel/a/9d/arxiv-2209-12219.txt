Abstract
The linear switching system is a system of ODE with the time-dependent matrix taking values from a given control matrix set. The system is (asymptotically) stable if all its trajectories tend to zero for every control function. We consider possible mode-dependent restrictions on the lengths of switching intervals which keeps the stability of the system. When the stability of trajectories with short switching intervals implies the stability of all trajectories? To answer this question we introduce the concept of “cut tail points” of linear operators and study them by the convex analysis tools. We reduce the problem to the construction of Chebyshev-type exponential polynomials, for which we derive an algorithm and present the corresponding numerical results. Key words: linear switching system, dynamical system, stability, switching time intervals, quasipolynomials, extremal polynomial, Chebyshev system, convex extremal problem AMS 2010 subject classification 37N35, 93D20, 41A50, 15-04



1. Introduction

Linear switching system is a linear ODE
\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}(t)\,=\,A(t){\boldsymbol{x}}(t)

on the vector-function
{\boldsymbol{x}}\,:\,{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\,\to\,{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with the initial
condition
{\boldsymbol{x}}(0)={\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}
such that the matrix function
A(t)

takes values from a given compact set
{\mathcal{A}}
called

control set
. The
control function
,
or the

switching law
is an arbitrary measurable function

A:{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\to{\mathcal{A}}
. Each matrix
A\in{\mathcal{A}}
is called a
regime
of the system.
We shall identify the linear switching system with the corresponding
control set
{\mathcal{A}}
.
The
switching interval
is the segment between two
consecutive switching points. We shall use the same word also for the length of this
interval.

Linear switching systems naturally appear
in problems of robotics, electronic engineering, mechanics, planning, etc.
[
12
]
.
One of the main issues is the stability of the system.
A system is called
asymptotically stable
(in short, “stable”), if all its trajectories tend to zero.
For one-element control sets
{\mathcal{A}}=\{A\}
, the stability is equivalent to that
the matrix
A
is Hurwitz (or stable), i.e., all its
eigenvalues have negative real parts.

If
{\mathcal{A}}
contains more than one matrix, then the stability problem becomes
much harder. In general, we do not know the switching law
that provides the maximal rate of growth of trajectories. Moreover,
even some basic properties of those laws such that the frequency of switches or the
lengths of switching intervals are unknown. In this paper we make one step towards the solution
and prove that the “worst” trajectories do not have long switching intervals.
More precisely, we show that for each matrix
A\in{\mathcal{A}}
, one can associate
a positive number
T_{cut}(A)
, which is the moment of time
when the trajectory of ODE generated by
A
enters the interior of its symmetrized
convex hull. Our fist fundamental theorem states that
if all the trajectories whose switching intervals of the regime
A
do not exceed
T_{A}
, then
the system is stable. Therefore, if
the trajectories for which the length of the
switching intervals do not exceed the values
T_{cut}(A)

for the corresponding regimes
A\in{\mathcal{A}}
, then
the system is stable. Thus, the stability can be verified only for
switching laws with the restricted switching intervals.
Thus, long switching intervals do not influence the stability. Moreover, we present a
method of finding of the corresponding maximal lengths
T_{cut}(A)
. We
impose two assumptions:

Assumption 1.
All the matrices
A\in{\mathcal{A}}
are Hurwitz.

Assumption 2.
There is a mode-dependent dwell time restriction: all switching intervals
for every
A\in{\mathcal{A}}
is at least
m(A)
, where
m(A)>0
is a given number.

Both those assumptions are natural. If one of the matrices form
{\mathcal{A}}

is not Hurwitz, then the system cannot be stable. Hence, it makes sense
to decide stability only under Assumption 1. On the other hand, without
the dwell time assumption one can split all long switching intervals
to short parts by momentary interfering with other regimes.
In this case the length restrictions does not play a role, which
the problem becomes irrelevant.

Before formulating the main results we need to introduce one more concept,
which is probably of some independent interest.

2. Cut tail points

We are going to introduce the formal definition of the
maximal switching interval
T_{A}
and observe some of its properties, after which
we will be able to formulate the theorem
on the restriction of switching intervals for stable systems.

We consider a system
\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}\,=\,A{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{x}}(0)={\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}

with a constant Hurwitz matrix
A
, without switches. We assume that
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}
is a
generic point
, i.e., it does not belong to any proper invariant subspace of
A
. The trajectory starting at
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}
is also called generic.
This does not reduce the generality because any trajectory can be made generic after
reducing to the corresponding invariant subspace.

The following fact is elementary, we include the proof for convenience of the reader.

Lemma 1

A generic trajectory is not contained in a proper
subspace of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

Proof.
Let
L
be the linear span of the trajectory. Since

\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}(t)\in L
, we have
A{\boldsymbol{x}}(t)\in L
for all
t\geq 0
, and
therefore
AL\subset L
. Thus,
L
is an invariant subspace of
A
that contains

{\boldsymbol{x}}(0)={\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}
. Since
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}
is a generic point, it follows that

L={\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

\Box

For a subset
M\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, we denote by
{\rm co}_{s}M\,=\,{\rm co}\,\{M,-M\}

its
symmetrized convex hull
.
For a generic trajectory
{\boldsymbol{x}}(\cdot)
and for a segment
[t_{1},t_{2}]\subset{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, let
\Gamma(t_{1},t_{2})\,=\,\bigl\{{\boldsymbol{x}}(t)\ |\ t\in[t_{1},t_{2}]\bigr\}
and
G(t_{1},t_{2})\,=\,{\rm co}_{s}\,\Gamma(t_{1},t_{2})
.
It is seen easily that
G(t_{1}+h,t_{2}+h)\,=\,e^{hA}\,G(t_{1},t_{2})
.
We also denote by
\Gamma=\Gamma(0,+\infty)
the entire trajectory and, respectively,

G=G(0,+\infty)
. Thus,
G
is the symmetrized convex hull of
\Gamma
.
The origin is an interior point of
G
. Therefore, if the matrix
A
is
Hurwitz, then
G
is a convex body, i.e., a compact set with a nonempty interior.

Definition 1

A point
T>0
is called a cut tail point for
the stable system
\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}\,=\,A{\boldsymbol{x}},\ {\boldsymbol{x}}(0)={\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}
, if
for every
t>T
, the point
{\boldsymbol{x}}(t)
belongs to the interior of the set
G(0,T)
.

Proposition 1

For every stable system
\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}\,=\,A{\boldsymbol{x}},\ {\boldsymbol{x}}(0)={\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}
, the set of cut tail points is nonempty.

Proof.
Since the point
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}
is generic, it follows that every arc of the trajectory is not contained in a hyperplane, therefore, for each segment
[t_{1},t_{2}]
,
the set
G(t_{1},t_{2})

is full-dimensional and hence,
it contains a ball centered at the origin.
On the other hand, the trajectory converges to zero as
t\to\infty
, hence,
say,
G(0,1)
contains
{\boldsymbol{x}}(t)
for all
t
bigger than some
T>1
.
Since
G(0,1)\subset G(0,T)
, we see that
T
is a cut tail point.

\Box

The set of cut tail points is obviously closed, hence, there exists
the minimal point, which will be denoted by
T_{cut}=T_{cut}(A)
. Clearly,

{\boldsymbol{x}}(T_{cut})
is on the boundary of
G
. Thus,
T_{cut}
is the moment of time when the trajectory enters the interior of its
symmetrized convex hull. To proceed further we need the following well-known auxiliary fact:
if two trajectories
start at generic points
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0},\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}
, then
they are affinely similar. To show this we apply the following

Lemma 2

For an arbitrary
d\times d
matrix
A
and for arbitrary points
{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

such that
{\boldsymbol{a}}
does not belong to an eigenspace of
A
, there exists a
d\times d
matrix
C
that
commutes with
A
and takes
{\boldsymbol{a}}
to
{\boldsymbol{b}}
.

Proof.

We pass to the Jordan basis of the matrix

A
and consider one Jordan block
\Lambda
of size
s
corresponding to an
eigenvalue
\lambda
. Denote by
{\boldsymbol{a}}^{\prime}
and
{\boldsymbol{b}}^{\prime}
the
s
-dimensional components of the
vectors
{\boldsymbol{a}}
and
{\boldsymbol{b}}
respectively corresponding to this block. Denote by
{\mathcal{H}}
the set of Hankel upper-triangular matrices of size
s
,
i.e., matrices of the form

\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccc}\alpha_{1}&\alpha_{2}&\cdots&\alpha_{s-1}&\alpha_{s}\\
0&\alpha_{1}&\alpha_{2}&\cdots&\alpha_{s-1}\\
0&0&\alpha_{1}&\alpha_{2}&\cdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&0&\alpha_{1}\end{array}\right)\,.

Note that the set
{\mathcal{H}}
is a linear space and is a multiplicative group.
All elements of
{\mathcal{H}}
commute with
\Lambda
. The set
U\,=\,\{X{\boldsymbol{a}}^{\prime}\ |\ X\in{\mathcal{H}}\}
is a linear subspace of

{\mathbb{R}}^{s}
invariant with respect to every matrix from
{\mathcal{H}}
.
Let us prove that
U={\mathbb{R}}^{s}
. If this is not the case, then
U

is an invariant subspace for each matrix from
{\mathcal{H}}
, in particular, for the matrix
\Lambda
.
Therefore,
U
coincides with one of spaces

U_{j}=\bigl\{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{j},0,\ldots,0)^{T}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{s}\bigr\}

(they form the complete set of invariant subspaces of
\Lambda
).
Hence,
{\boldsymbol{b}}^{\prime}\in U_{j}
for some
j\leq s-1
. Consequently, the vector
{\boldsymbol{a}}

belongs to an eigenspace of the matrix
A
generated by all vectors of its Jordan basis
except for the last
s-j
vectors, which correspond to the block
\Lambda
.
This contradicts to the condition on
{\boldsymbol{a}}
. Thus,
U={\mathbb{R}}^{s}
.
Hence, there exists a matrix
X\in{\mathcal{H}}
for which
X{\boldsymbol{a}}={\boldsymbol{b}}
.
Let us remember that
X
commutes with
\Lambda
.
Having found such a matrix
X
for each Jordan block of the matrix
A

we compose a block matrix
C
for which
CA=AC
and
C{\boldsymbol{a}}={\boldsymbol{b}}
.

\Box

Now we are ready to establish the affine similarity of all
generic trajectories.

Proposition 2

All generic trajectories of a matrix
A
are affinely similar.

Proof.
Let
{\boldsymbol{x}}(\cdot),{\boldsymbol{y}}(\cdot)
be two trajectories and
their starting points
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0},{\boldsymbol{y}}_{0}
do not belong to an invariant subspace of
A
.
Lemma
2

gives a matrix
C
such that
CA=AC
and
C{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}={\boldsymbol{y}}_{0}
.
Then

C\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}\ =\ CA{\boldsymbol{x}}\ =\ AC{\boldsymbol{x}},\,\quad C{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}\,=\,{\boldsymbol{y}}_{0}\,.

Thus, the function
C{\boldsymbol{x}}(\cdot)
satisfied the same equation

\dot{\boldsymbol{y}}\,=\,A{\boldsymbol{y}}
with the same starting point
{\boldsymbol{y}}_{0}
, then by the uniqueness it coincides with
{\boldsymbol{y}}(\cdot)
. We see that
{\boldsymbol{y}}(\cdot)\,=\,C{\boldsymbol{y}}(\cdot)
.
It remains to note that the similarity transform is nondegenerate.
Otherwise the trajectory
{\boldsymbol{y}}(\cdot)
is not full-dimensional
which contradicts to Lemma
2
.

\Box

A direct consequence of Proposition
2
is that

the cut tail points do not depend on
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}
, provided
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}

is generic
. Thus
T_{cut}
is
a function of the matrix
A
. In particular, speaking about
the cut tail points we may not specify
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}
.

Proposition 3

For every generic trajectory, the arc
\Gamma(0,T_{cut})

lies on the boundary of
G
.

Proof.
If, on the contrary, there exists
t<T_{cut}
such
that
{\boldsymbol{x}}(t)\in{\rm int}\,G
, then, since the “tail”
\{{\boldsymbol{x}}(s)\ |\ s>T_{cut}\}

of the trajectory
\Gamma

lies in the interior of
G
,
it follows that
{\boldsymbol{x}}(t)\in{\rm int}\,G(0,T_{cut})
.
Hence,

{\boldsymbol{x}}(T_{cut})\ =\,e^{(T_{cut}-t)A}\,{\boldsymbol{x}}(t)\ \in\ {\rm int}\,e^{(T_{cut}-t)A}\,G(0,T_{cut})\ =\ {\rm int}\,G\,(T_{cut}-t\,,\,2T_{cut}-t)\ \subset\ {\rm int}\,G.

Thus,
{\boldsymbol{x}}(T_{cut})\in{\rm int}\,G
, which is a contradiction.

\Box

We see that the point
{\boldsymbol{x}}(T_{cut})
separates the part of trajectory that lies on the
boundary form that in the interior:
{\boldsymbol{x}}(t)\in\partial G
, for all
t\leq T_{cut}
, and

{\boldsymbol{x}}(t)\in{\rm int}\,G
, for all
t>T_{cut}
.

Corollary 1

The set of cut tail points is precisely the half-line

[T_{cut},+\infty)
.

Corollary 2

All extreme points of
G
are located on the union of the curves
\Gamma(0,T_{cut})

and
-\Gamma(0,T_{cut})
.

3. The first fundamental theorem

Now we are going to establish the relation
between the cut tail points and the switching intervals for
stable linear systems. Let us recall that we consider
linear switching systems under Assumptions 1 and 2:
all matrices
A\in{\mathcal{A}}
are Hurwitz and the dwell time is bounded below.

Definition 2

Let
{\mathcal{A}}
be a linear system satisfying
Assumptions 1 and 2 and let
{\mathcal{J}}
be an arbitrary subset of
{\mathcal{A}}
.
The system is called
{\mathcal{J}}
-stable if
all its trajectories tend to zero provided for every
A\in{\mathcal{J}}
all switching
intervals corresponding to
A
do not exceed
m(A)+T_{cut}(A)
.

Thus, a
{\mathcal{J}}
-stable system is stable not for all trajectories but only for those
with restricted lengths of intervals for the regimes from
{\mathcal{J}}
.
In case
{\mathcal{J}}=\emptyset
the
{\mathcal{J}}
stability becomes the usual stability.
In the proof we use the following result
from
[
18
]
. To every
A\in{\mathcal{A}}
we associate
numbers
m(A)>0
and
M(A)\in(m(A),+\infty]
.
Denote by
{\mathcal{M}}
the set of pairs
\bigl(m(A),M(A)\bigr)\,,\ A\in{\mathcal{A}}
.
We call a trajectory
admissible
if for every
A\in{\mathcal{A}}
, the lengths of all switching
intervals corresponding to
A
lie on the segment
\bigl[m(A),M(A)\bigr]
.
The system is called
{\mathcal{M}}
-stable if all its admissible trajectories
tend to zero. To every

A\in{\mathcal{A}}
, we associate a norm
f_{A}
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
The collection
f=\{f_{A}\ |\ A\in{\mathcal{A}}\}
is called a
multinorm
.
We assume that those norms are uniformly bounded.
The following theorem was proved in
[
18
]
.

Theorem A
.
A system is
{\mathcal{M}}
-stable if and only if
there exists a multinorm
f
that possesses the
following property: for every admissible trajectory
{\boldsymbol{x}}(\cdot)
and for an arbitrary
its switching point
\tau
between some regimes
A
and
A^{\prime}
,
we have
f_{A}({\boldsymbol{x}}(\tau))\,>\,\sup_{t\in[\tau+m(A^{\prime}),\tau+T]}f_{A^{\prime}}({\boldsymbol{x}}(t))
,
where
m=m(A)
and
\tau+T
is the next switching point (or
T=+\infty
if
\tau
is the largest switching point)
.

Now we formulate our first fundamental theorem on switching interval restrictions.

Theorem 1

Let a linear switching system
{\mathcal{A}}
satisfy Assumptions 1 and 2.
Then for every subset
{\mathcal{J}}\subset{\mathcal{A}}
, the
{\mathcal{J}}
-stability
of
{\mathcal{A}}
implies
the stability.

Proof
. For
A\in{\mathcal{J}}
, we set
M(A)=m(A)+T_{cut}(A)
, and

M(A)=+\infty
for all
A\notin{\mathcal{J}}
. If the system is
{\mathcal{J}}
-stable, then
it is
{\mathcal{M}}
-stable, and by Theorem A there exists a multinorm
f

such that
f_{A}({\boldsymbol{x}}(\tau))\,>\,\sup_{t\in[\tau+m(A^{\prime}),\tau+T]}f_{A^{\prime}}({\boldsymbol{x}}(t))

for every switching point
\tau
from a regime
A
to
A^{\prime}
,
where
T\leq M(A^{\prime})
is the length of the switching interval.
Note that since the function
f_{A^{\prime}}
is convex, it follows that the
its supremum over the arc
\gamma(\tau+m(A^{\prime}),\tau+T)\,=\,\{{\boldsymbol{x}}(t)\ |\ \tau+m(A^{\prime})\,\leq\,\tau+T\}
is equal to the supremum over the symmetrized convex
hull
G(\tau+m(A^{\prime}),\tau+T)
of that arc.
On the other hand, by the definition of
T_{cut}
, for all
T>T_{cut}(A)
,
we have
G(\tau+m(A^{\prime}),\tau+T)\,=\,G(\tau+m(A^{\prime}),\tau+T_{cut})
.
Hence, if the system is
{\mathcal{M}}
-stable for
M(A)=m(A)+T_{cut}(A)
,
then the assumptions of Theorem A are satisfied for
all larger
M(A)
and therefore, for
M(A)=+\infty
. Now by the same Theorem A
applied for
M(A)=+\infty,A\in{\mathcal{A}}
, we conclude that
the system is stable.

\Box

The multinorm from Theorem A is called the
Lyapunov multinorm

for
{\mathcal{M}}
-stability.
Thus, if the system with the lengths of all switching intervals
bounded by
T_{cut}(A)
is stable, then it is stable
without any restrictions. In the proof of Theorem
1
we established that
not only the stability but also the Lyapunov multifunction
stays the same after removing all the restrictions.

To restrict the lengths switching intervals in applied problems we need to
find or estimate the cut tail point for given matrices.
The definition of cut tail points involves the convex hull of the trajectory.
Computation of a convex hull in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is notoriously hard, therefore,
T_{cut}

can unlikely be found in a straightforward way.

4. Computing the cut tail points and the switching intervals

To find
T_{cut}
for a given Hurwitz matrix
A
one needs to decide
if a given
T
is a cut tail point? We reduce this problem to finding the
quasipolynomial of be, st approximation and present an algorithm for that.
The numerical results are reported in the next section.

Let us denote by

{\mathcal{P}}_{A}
the linear span of the functions
f_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}(t)=e^{tA}{\boldsymbol{x}},\ {\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
This is the space of quasipolynomials
which are linear combinations of functions
t^{k}e^{\,\alpha t}\cos\beta t
and
t^{k}e^{\,\alpha t}\,\sin\beta t\,
,
where
\alpha+i\beta
is an eigenvalue of
A
and
k=1,\ldots,r-1
, where
r
is the size of the
largest Jordan bock of
A
corresponding to that eigenvalue. The dimension of
{\mathcal{P}}_{A}

is equal to the degree of the minimal annihilating polynomial of the matrix
A
.

Theorem 2

Let
A
be a Hurwitz matrix and
T>0
be a number; then
T>T_{cut}
if and only if
the value of the
convex extremal problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}p(T)\to\max\\
\|{\boldsymbol{p}}\|_{C({\mathbb{R}}_{+})}\ \leq\ 1\\
p\in{\mathcal{P}}_{A}\end{array}\right.

(1)

is smaller than
1
.

Proof
. By Proposition
3
, the inequality
T>T_{cut}
is equivalent to that

{\boldsymbol{x}}(T)\in{\rm int}\,G
. This means that the point
{\boldsymbol{x}}(T)

cannot be separated from
G
by a linear functional, i.e.,
for every nonzero vector
{\boldsymbol{p}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, we have

\bigl({\boldsymbol{p}},{\boldsymbol{x}}(T)\bigr)\,<\,\sup\limits_{{\boldsymbol{y}}\in G}\,({\boldsymbol{p}},{\boldsymbol{y}})
.
Since
G
is a convex hull of points
\,\pm\,{\boldsymbol{x}}(t)\,,\,t\geq 0
,
it follows that
\sup\limits_{{\boldsymbol{y}}\in G}\,({\boldsymbol{p}},{\boldsymbol{y}})\,=\,\sup\limits_{t\geq 0}\,\bigl|({\boldsymbol{p}},{\boldsymbol{x}}(t))\bigr|
. Thus,

\bigl({\boldsymbol{p}},{\boldsymbol{x}}(T)\bigr)\ <\ \sup\limits_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\bigl|({\boldsymbol{p}},{\boldsymbol{x}}(t))\bigr|.

On the other hand,
({\boldsymbol{p}},{\boldsymbol{x}}(t))=p(t)
, where
p\in{\mathcal{P}}_{A}

is the quasipolynomial with the vector of coefficients
{\boldsymbol{p}}
.
We obtain
p(T)<\|p\|_{C({\mathbb{R}}_{+})}
.
Consequently,
p(T)<1

for every quasipolynomial from the unit ball
\|p\|_{C({\mathbb{R}}^{+})}\leq 1
.
Now by the compactness argument we conclude that the value of the
problem (
1
) is smaller than one.

\Box

Theorem 3

Suppose
A
is a stable matrix; then a number
T
is a cut tail point
for
A
if and only if there exists a quasipolynomal
p\in{\mathcal{P}}_{A}

for which
T
is a point of absolute maximum on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

Proof
. Proposition
3
implies that the point
{\boldsymbol{x}}(T)
lies on the
boundary of
G
precisely when
T\leq T_{cut}
.
On the other hand, this is equivalent to say that
{\boldsymbol{x}}(T)
can be separated from
G

by some linear functional. Arguing as in the proof of Theorem
2

we obtain a quasyipolynomial
p
such that
p(T)\geq\|p\|_{C({\mathbb{R}}_{+})}
, i.e.,

T
is a point of maximum of
p
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
. Thus,
T\leq T_{cut}

if and only if there exists a quasipolynomal
p
that has maximum at the point
T
.

\Box

The problem (
1
) is convex and can be solved by the
convex optimisation methods. However, its main disadvantage that it contains
the norm of a quasipolynomial on the whole real line, which can cause numerical problems.
Theorem
4
below is the second main result of this work.
It reduces problem (
1
) to the following problem on a compact interval:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\|{\boldsymbol{p}}\|_{[0,T]}\ \to\ \min\\
p(T)=1\\
p\in{\mathcal{P}}_{A}\end{array}\right.

(2)

Clearly, the answer to this problem cannot be smaller than one.
Theorem
4
claims that it is equal to one precisely
when
T\geq T_{cut}
.

Theorem 4

A number
T>0
is a cut tail point
if and only the value of the problem (
2
) is equal to one.

Proof
. If for some
p\in{\mathcal{P}}_{{\mathcal{A}}}
, we have
\|{\boldsymbol{p}}\|_{[0,T]}=1
, then
the largest point of maximum of
p
is bigger than or equal to
T
, which in view of Theorem
3
means that
T_{cut}\geq T
. Conversely, if the answer
to the problem (
2
) is larger than one,
then none of
p\in{\mathcal{P}}_{{\mathcal{A}}}
can have maximum in
T
and hence
T>T_{cut}
.

\Box

If we are able to solve the problem (
2
) for every
T
, then
T_{cut}

can be found merely by double division.

Thus, for given
T>0
, we need to solve problem (
2
) of minimizing
the norm of the quasipolynomial
p\in{\mathcal{P}}_{A}
on the segment
[0,T]

under the constraint
p(T)=1
. Since such quasipolynomials form
an affine space of dimension
d-1
and the function
f(p)=\|p\|_{C[0,T]}

is convex, it follows that we can invoke the refinement theorem (see, for instance,
[
11
]
),
and conclude that there exist at most
d
points
t_{1}<\ldots<t_{n}
, where
\ n\leq d
on the segment
[0,T]

such that

\min_{p\in{\mathcal{P}}_{A},\,p(T)=1}\|p\|_{C[0,T]}\ =\ \min_{p\in{\mathcal{P}}_{A},\,p(T)=1}\,\max_{i=1,\ldots,n}|p(t_{i})|\,.

For the optimal quasipolynomial
\bar{p}
, which exists by the coercivity of the function
f
, we must have
|\bar{p}(t_{i})|=\|\bar{p}\|,\,i=1,\ldots,n
. For all other feasible quasipolynomials
p
,
the absolute value of
p(t_{i})
is larger than
\|\bar{p}\|
for at least one point
t_{i},\,i=1,\ldots,n
.
To solve the problem (
2
) and to find the best quasipolynomial
\bar{p}

we suggest the following algorithm.

Algorithm 1
.
Initialisation.
We have the space of quasipolynomials

{\mathcal{P}}_{A}
of degree
d
on the segment
[0,T]
.
Take arbitrary
d
points on the segment
[0,T]
, denote this set by
{\mathcal{T}}_{0}

and choose arbitrary admissible
p_{0}\in{\mathcal{P}}_{A}
(i.e.,
p_{0}(T)=1
).
Set
b_{0}=1,B_{0}=\|p_{0}\|
. Choose arbitrary
\varepsilon>0
.

Main loop.
The
k
th iteration. We have a set of
n\leq d
points
{\mathcal{T}}_{k-1}

and the numbers
b_{k-1},B_{k-1}
which are
the lower and upper bounds respectively for the value of the problem (
2
).
Solve the linear programming problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}r\ \to\ \min\\
|p(t_{j})|\leq r,\ t_{j}\in{\mathcal{T}}_{d-1}\\
p\in{\mathcal{P}}_{A},\ p(T)=1.\end{array}\right.

(3)

The value
r
of this problem is a lower bound for the value of the
problem (
2
). Hence,
b_{k}=\max\{b_{k-1},r\}

is the new lower bound. If
\bar{p}
is an optimal quasipolynomial for the
problem (
3
), then we find the point
s

of absolute maximum of
|\bar{p}(t)|
on the segment
[0,T]
.
Then
|\bar{p}(s)|
is an upper bound for the value of the
problem (
2
). Hence,
B_{k}=\min\{B_{k-1},|\bar{p}(s)|\}

is the new upper bound.

We add the point
s
to the set
{\mathcal{T}}_{d-1}
and remove those points
t_{i}

for which
|\bar{p}(t_{i})|<r
.
Thus, we obtain a new set
{\mathcal{T}}_{k}
.
Then pass to the next iteration.

Termination.
If
B_{k}-b_{k}<\varepsilon
, then stop.
The value of the problem (
2
) belongs to the segment
[b_{k},B_{k}]

Remark 1

If all exponents of the quasipolynomials are real, i.e.,
if the spectrum of the matrix
A
is real,
then these exponents form a Chebyshev system
[
6
,
10
]
. Hence, the optimal polynomial
in the problem (
2
) has an alternans at the points
{\mathcal{T}}_{k}
,
which can be efficiently found by the Remez algorithm
[
19
]
. In the general case, however,
the system is not Chebyshev and the Remez algorithm is not applicable.
Algorithm 1 can be considered as a kind of replacement
of the Remez algorithm for non-Chebyshev systems.

Numerical results presented in Section 5 show that
Algorithm 1 can effectively find decide whether
T<T_{cut}
and hence, by the double division, find
T_{cut}
.

For
2\times 2
matrices, the parameter
T_{cut}
can be evaluated geometrically and in an explicit form.

The two-dimensional systems
.

We have a real
2\times 2
-matrix
A
.
For the sake of simplicity we exclude the case of multiple eigenvalues.
So, we assume that
A
either has two different real eigenvalue or
two complex conjugate eigenvalues.

Case 1. The eigenvalues of
A
are real and different
.
Denote them by
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}
.
The trajectory
\Gamma=\{{\boldsymbol{x}}(t)\,|\,t\geq 0\}
in the basis of eigenvectors of
A

has the equation
(x_{1}(t),x_{2}(t))\,=\,\bigl(e^{\alpha_{1}t}\,,\,e^{\alpha_{2}t}\bigr)\,,\ t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
.
Since
A
is stable, both
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}
are negative
and
{\boldsymbol{x}}(0)=(1,1),\,{\boldsymbol{x}}(+\infty)=(0,0)
.
Let
{\boldsymbol{a}}\in\Gamma
be the point where the tangent line
drawn from the point
(-1,-1)
touches
\Gamma
.
Then
G\,=\,{\rm co}_{s}\,\Gamma
is bounded by the line segment
from
(-1,-1)
to
{\boldsymbol{a}}
and by the arc of
\Gamma
from
{\boldsymbol{a}}
to

(1,1)
, then reflected about the origin.
Hence, if
{\boldsymbol{a}}={\boldsymbol{x}}(T)
, then
{\boldsymbol{x}}(t)\in{\rm int}\,G
for all
t>T
, and
so
T=T_{cut}
. We have
\,{\boldsymbol{a}}\,+\,s\,\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}(T_{cut})\,=\,(-1,-1)
, where
s>0
is some number.
Writing this equality coordinatewise, we obtain the equation for
t=T_{cut}
:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcl}e^{\alpha_{1}t}\ +\ s\,\alpha_{1}\,e^{\alpha_{1}t}&=&-1\\
e^{\alpha_{2}t}\ +\ s\,\alpha_{2}\,e^{\alpha_{2}t}&=&-1\end{array}\right.

(4)

which becomes after simplification

\frac{1+e^{-\alpha_{1}t}}{\alpha_{1}}\,=\,\frac{1+e^{-\alpha_{2}t}}{\alpha_{2}}
.
The unique solution is
T_{cut}
.

Case 2. The eigenvalues of
A
are complex conjugate
. They can be written as

\alpha\pm i\beta
with
\alpha<0
.
The trajectory
\Gamma
in a suitable basis
has the equation
(x_{1}(t),x_{2}(t))\,=\,e^{\alpha t}\,(\cos\beta t\,,\,\sin\beta t)\,,\ t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
. The trajectory
\Gamma

goes from the point
{\boldsymbol{x}}(0)=(1,0)
to zero making infinitely many rotations.
Taking the point of tangency
{\boldsymbol{a}}
of
\Gamma
with the line
going from the point
(-1,0)
and arguing as above we obtain

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcr}e^{\alpha t}\cos\beta t\ +\ s\,e^{\alpha t}\bigl(\alpha\cos\beta t\,-\,\beta\sin\beta t\bigr)&=&-1\\
e^{\alpha t}\sin\beta t\ +\ s\,e^{\alpha t}\bigl(\alpha\sin\beta t\,+\,\beta\cos\beta t\bigr)&=&0\end{array}\right.

(5)

from which it follows
\alpha\sin\beta t\,+\,\beta\cos\beta t\,+\,\beta\,e^{\alpha t}\,=\,0
. The unique solution of this equation is
t=T_{cut}
.
