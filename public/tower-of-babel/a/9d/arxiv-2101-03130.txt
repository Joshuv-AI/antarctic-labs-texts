Rotational Symmetries in Polynomial Rings
Keith Conrad, Ambar N. Sengupta

Abstract
. We obtain results describing the behavior of the action of rotation generators on polynomials over a commutative ring. We also explore harmonic polynomials in a purely algebraic setting.



1.
Introduction and summary of results

The purpose of this paper is to study the action of rotation generators on a polynomial ring. We establish numerous algebraic results, many of which have classical analytic counterparts for functions on Euclidean space.

Let
R
be a ring (always assumed commutative with identity) and consider the polynomial ring
{\mathcal{P}}_{N}=R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
with
N\geq 2
. Classically
R
is
{\mathbb{R}}
or
{\mathbb{C}}
. For us,
R
may be a ring of polynomials over further indeterminates with coefficients in some commutative ring. Thus, for our purposes, it is necessary to build the framework of results with
R
being a ring rather than a field. Other examples for
R
of interest include the ring of
p
-adic integers and the ring of formal power series over some integral domain.

By a
rotation generator
we mean an operator on
{\mathcal{P}}_{N}
of the form

M_{jk}=X_{j}\partial_{k}-X_{k}\partial_{j},

(1.1)

where
j,k\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
with
j\not=k
and
\partial_{j}=\partial/\partial X_{j}
.
The
R
-linear span of these operators is a Lie algebra over
R
.
The skew-symmetry

M_{jk}=-M_{kj},

implies
M_{jk}^{2}
is independent of the ordering of
j
and
k
.

We also work with the Laplacian operator

\Delta_{N}=\partial_{1}^{2}+\cdots+\partial_{N}^{2}

(1.2)

and sometimes we will drop the subscript
N
from
\Delta_{N}
.
The “quadratic Casimir” operator
{{M\cdot M}}
is given by

{M\cdot M}=\sum_{\{j,k\}\in P_{2}(N)}M_{jk}^{2},

(1.3)

where
P_{2}(N)
is the set of all
2
-element subsets of
\{1,\ldots,N\}
.
Lengthy but straightforward computations produce the following commutator relations: on
{\mathcal{P}}_{N}
,

\begin{split}[M_{jk},M_{lm}]&=0\qquad\hbox{if $\{j,k\},\{l,m\}\in P_{2}(N)$ are disjoint,}\\
[M_{jk},M_{kl}]&=M_{jl}\qquad\hbox{\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\! if $\{j,k\},\{k,l\}\in P_{2}(N)$ with $j\not=l$ },\\
[M_{jk},{M\cdot M}]&=0\qquad\hbox{if $\{j,k\}\in P_{2}(N)$}.\end{split}

(1.4)

1.1.
Summary of results

We present a sample of our results, indicating their overall narrative role in the development.
The version of the results stated below
may have a weaker hypothesis on
R
when it is proved later.

The following result, which is established later (with slightly weaker hypotheses on
R
)
as Propositions
2.3
and
2.5
,
is the algebraic counterpart of the geometric result that if a function on
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
is invariant under all rotations around the origin then it is a function of the Euclidean norm.

Proposition 1.1
.

Suppose the ring
R
contains
{\mathbb{Q}}
.
A polynomial
p\in R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
is annihilated by all the rotation generators

M_{jk}
if and only if
p
is a polynomial in
{{X\cdot X}}=X_{1}^{2}+\cdots+X_{N}^{2}
with
R
-coefficients.
An
R
-linear derivation on
R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
annihilates
{{X\cdot X}}
if and only if it is an
R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
-linear combination
of the operators
M_{jk}
.

For a more general picture, let
D_{A}
be the set of all derivations on an
R
-algebra
A
; then
D_{A}
is a Lie algebra over
A
. If
D
is any Lie subalgebra of
D_{A}
then the set

N(D)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\bigcap_{L\in D}\ker L

of common zeros of all
L\in D

is a subalgebra of
A
. Conversely for any subalgebra
B\subset A
the annihilator

{\mathcal{A}}(B)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\{L\in D_{A}:B\subset\ker L\}

is a Lie subalgebra of
D_{A}
. In this framework, Proposition
1.1
is the case where
A=R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
, and

B=R[{X\cdot X}]
is the subalgebra generated over
R
by the quadratic form
{{X\cdot X}}
: it says

The derivations on
R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
that annihilate
{{X\cdot X}}
form the Lie algebra spanned by all
the operators
M_{jk}
with coefficients in
R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
,
and every polynomial in
R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
annihilated by that Lie algebra is in the subalgebra
B
.

1.2.
Background and literature

A motivation for this work was to develop some of basic results concerning spherical harmonics in a manner that demonstrates that their validity is purely algebraic and to explain why some of these special functions have
rational coefficients.

Algebraic studies of spherical harmonics seem to be largely in the physics literature, with a focus on atomic physics; these works include Ogawa
[
Ogawa2017
]
. Mathematical studies include Axler and Ramey
[
Axler1995
]
and Reznick
[
Rez1996
]
. Spherical harmonics, originating in work of Legendre and later Laplace’s series expansion of the gravitational potential, are a classical subject; work on them from the 19th and early 20th century include Heine
[
Heine1861
]
, Kellogg
[
Kellogg1929
]
, Mehler
[
Mehler1866
]
, Thomson and Tait
[
Thom1863
,
TT1867
]
, and Whittaker and Watson
[
WW1902
, section 18.31]
. The relationship between special functions and representations of Lie groups is well-known (see, for example Vilenkin and Klimyk
[
VK1995
]
and, closer in spirit to our context, Macdonald
[
McD1995
]
).

Our study focuses on polynomials over general rings, typically containing the rationals, rather than complex-valued special functions that arise as matrix elements for representations of classical Lie groups. Instead of behavior under rotations and using integration over the sphere we use the Lie algebra of the rotation group and we devise purely algebraic counterparts of traditionally analytic notions such as an algebraic counterpart of integration over spheres (Section
4
).

2.
Polynomials and the action of rotation generators

For a ring
R
, each
p
in
R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
is written as

p=\sum_{{\vec{\jmath}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{N}}}p_{\vec{\jmath}}X^{\vec{\jmath}}=\sum_{{\vec{\jmath}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{N}}}p_{\vec{\jmath}}X_{1}^{j_{1}}\ldots X_{N}^{j_{N}},

indexed by vectors
\vec{\jmath}=(j_{1},\ldots,j_{N})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{N}
,
with
p_{\vec{\jmath}}=0
if any component of
\vec{\jmath}
is negative.
So
p
is homogeneous of degree
d
when the monomials
X_{1}^{j_{1}}\ldots X_{N}^{j_{N}}

appearing in
p
with nonzero coefficients have
j_{1}+\cdots+j_{N}=d
.

Proposition 2.1
.

With
\Delta_{N}
being the Laplacian on
R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
,

({{X\cdot X}})\Delta_{N}=(r\partial_{r})^{2}+(N-2)r\partial_{r}+{{M\cdot M}},

(2.1)

where

{{X\cdot X}}=X_{1}^{2}+\cdots+X_{N}^{2}\ \ \ \text{ and }\ \ \ r\partial_{r}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}X_{j}\partial_{j}.

The identity (
2.1
) is verified by a straightforward but tedious computation left to the reader.
It will play a central role for us.

The Euler operator
r\partial_{r}
on a degree-
n
homogeneous polynomial
p
in
R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]

multiplies it by
n
:
(r\partial_{r})(p)=np
. Just as the operator
M_{jk}
arises in the traditional setting as a generator of rotations in the
X_{j}
-
X_{k}
‘plane’, the Euler operator arises as a generator of scaling each
X_{j}
by a common constant.

A simple computation shows that

M_{jk}({X\cdot X})=0,

(2.2)

and hence, using the derivation property of
M_{jk}
, we also have

M_{jk}(({X\cdot X})^{a})=0,

(2.3)

for every integer
a\geq 0
. Consequently,

({M\cdot M})(({X\cdot X})^{a})=0.

(2.4)

We will make repeated use of the fact that
{X\cdot X}
, being a monic polynomial in
X_{1}
, is not a zero divisor. A more general form
of this observation is in Lemma
2.15
.

Proposition 2.2
.

With notation as before, for each positive integer
j

\Delta_{N}(({X\cdot X})^{j})=2j(2j+N-2)({{X\cdot X}})^{j-1}.

(2.5)

Proof.

Applying (
2.1
) to
({X\cdot X})^{j}
, and using the annihilation property (
2.4
), we have

({{X\cdot X}})\Delta_{N}(({X\cdot X})^{j})=(4j^{2}+(N-2)2j)({X\cdot X})^{j},

and then (
2.5
) follows because
{X\cdot X}
is not a zero divisor.
∎

2.1.
Rotation-invariant polynomials

Geometrically, it is clear that a function on
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
that is invariant under all rotations around the origin
is a function of the radial distance from the origin. The following result is an algebraic counterpart of this observation.

Proposition 2.3
.

Suppose that
R
is a ring in which
positive integer multiples of the identity
1_{R}
are not zero divisors, and
N
is an integer
\geq 2
.
A polynomial
p\in R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
satisfies
M_{jk}p=0
for all distinct
j,k\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
if and only if

p=q(X_{1}^{2}+\cdots+X_{N}^{2}),

for some
q(T)\in R[T]
. If
p
is homogeneous of degree
d
, then
d
is even and
q(T)=cT^{d/2}
for some
c\in R
.

The zero divisor condition on
R
implies
R
has characteristic 0 and
the condition is satisfied if
R
contains
{\mathbb{Q}}
. A ring of characteristic 0
not fitting the zero divisor condition is
{\mathbb{Z}}[x]/(2x)
.

Proof.

As noted in (
2.3
),
M_{jk}((X_{1}^{2}+\cdots+X_{N}^{2})^{a})=0
for all integers
a\geq 0
, so
each polynomial in
R[X_{1}^{2}+\cdots+X_{N}^{2}]
is annihilated by all
M_{jk}
.

For the converse, suppose
M_{jk}p=0
for all distinct
j
and
k
. To prove
p
is a polynomial in

{X\cdot X}=X_{1}^{2}+\cdots+X_{N}^{2}
, we may assume that
p
is homogeneous since
each
M_{jk}
maps homogeneous polynomials to
homogeneous polynomials of the same degree.

Let
d=\deg p
. The result is obvious if
d=0
.
If
d=1
then
p
is of the form
a_{1}X_{1}+\cdots+a_{N}X_{N}
, so

M_{jk}p=a_{k}X_{j}-a_{j}X_{k}.

This being
0
for all distinct
j,k\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
implies
p
is
0
, contradicting
p
having degree 1, so
we can’t have
d=1
.

Let
d\geq 2
. From all
M_{jk}p=0
we get
({M\cdot M})p=0
.
Applying the identity (
2.1
) to
p
,

({X\cdot X}){\Delta}p=(d^{2}+(N-2)d)p+({M\cdot M})p=d(d+N-2)p,

(2.6)

so

d(d+N-2)p=({X\cdot X})F,

(2.7)

where

F={\Delta}p\in R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}].

Equating the coefficients of
X_{1}^{j_{1}}\ldots X_{N}^{j_{N}}
on both sides of (
2.7
),

d(d+N-2)p_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{N}}=F_{j_{1}-2,j_{2},\ldots,j_{N}}+F_{j_{1},j_{2}-2,\ldots,j_{N}}+\cdots+F_{j_{1},j_{2},\ldots,j_{N}-2}

(2.8)

for all
j_{1},\ldots,j_{N}\geq 0
, where a coefficient on the right side of (
2.8
) is 0 if one of its indices is negative.
We will use induction on
|\vec{\jmath}|^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}j_{2}+\cdots+j_{N}
(
j_{1}
is not included in this sum)
to show each coefficient of
F_{\vec{j}}=F_{(j_{1},\ldots,j_{N})}
is
d(d+N-2)
times a linear combination of coefficients of
p
.

If
|\vec{\jmath}|^{\prime}=0
then
\vec{\jmath}=(j_{1},0,\ldots,0)
, so by
(
2.8
),

F_{\vec{\jmath}}=F_{j_{1},0,\ldots,0}=d(d+N-2)p_{j_{1}+2,0,\ldots,0}.

If
|\vec{\jmath}|^{\prime}=1
then
\vec{\jmath}=(j_{1},0,\ldots,1,\ldots,0)
, so by
(
2.8
),

F_{\vec{\jmath}}=F_{j_{1},0,\ldots,1,\ldots,0}=d(d+N-2)p_{j_{1}+2,0,\ldots,1,\ldots,0}.

Suppose, inductively, that for some integer
n\geq 1
,

F_{\vec{\jmath}}
is
d(d+N-2)
times a linear combination of
coefficients of
p
whenever
|\vec{\jmath}|^{\prime}<n
; we have just seen that this is true if
n=1
or 2.
For an
N
-tuple
\vec{\jmath}
where
|\vec{\jmath}|^{\prime}=n
, replacing
j_{1}
with
j_{1}+2
in (
2.8
) gives us

F_{\vec{\jmath}}=d(d+N-2)p_{j_{1}+2,j_{2},\ldots,j_{N}}-\left(F_{j_{1}+2,j_{2}-2,\ldots,j_{N}}+\cdots+F_{j_{1}+2,j_{2},\ldots,j_{N}-2}\right),

and the coefficients
F_{\vec{\imath}}
on the right have
|\vec{\imath}|^{\prime}=|\vec{\jmath}|^{\prime}-2<n
, so
by induction
F_{\vec{\jmath}}
is
d(d+N-2)
times a linear combination of coefficients of
p
when
|\vec{\jmath}|^{\prime}=n
.

Thus the polynomial
F
itself is
d(d+N-2)
times a polynomial
F_{*}\in R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
whose coefficients are linear combinations of the coefficients of
p
:

F=d(d+N-2)F_{*}.

(2.9)

Substituting this into (
2.7
), we have

d(d+N-2)\bigl(p-({X\cdot X})F_{*}\bigr)=0.

(2.10)

Finally we use the zero divisor condition on
R
.
If any coefficient in
p-({X\cdot X})F_{*}
were nonzero then
d(d+N-2)1_{R}
would be a zero divisor in
R
,
which contradicts the zero divisor condition on
R
since
d\geq 2
.
Therefore

p=({X\cdot X})F_{*}.

(2.11)

Since
p
is homogeneous of degree
d
, the polynomial
F_{*}
is homogeneous of degree
d-2
, because
{X\cdot X}
is not a zero divisor.
Moreover, since
M_{jk}({X\cdot X})=M_{jk}(X_{j}^{2}+X_{k}^{2})=0
, by the product rule for the first-order differential operator
M_{jk}
,
for each
g\in R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
we have

M_{jk}(({X\cdot X})g)=M_{jk}({{X\cdot X}})g+({X\cdot X})M_{jk}g=({X\cdot X})M_{jk}g.

(2.12)

Thus

M_{jk}p=M_{jk}(({X\cdot X})F_{*})=({X\cdot X})M_{jk}F_{*}.

(2.13)

The left side is
0
by hypothesis, so

({X\cdot X})M_{jk}F_{*}=0
for all
M_{jk}
.
Since
{X\cdot X}
is not a zero divisor in
R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
,

M_{jk}F_{*}=0

(2.14)

for all
M_{jk}
.

To summarize, if
p
is homogeneous of degree
d\geq 2
with all
M_{jk}p
being 0, then

p=({X\cdot X})F_{*}
where

F_{*}
is homogeneous of degree
d-2
and all
M_{jk}F_{*}
are 0.
By induction on the degree,
d-2
is even and
F_{*}=c({X\cdot X})^{(d-2)/2}
for some
c\in R
.
Thus
d
is even and

p=c({X\cdot X})({X\cdot X})^{(d-2)/2}=c(X_{1}^{2}+\cdots+X_{N}^{2})^{d/2},

which completes the proof.
∎

Remark 2.4
.

If we replace
R
by
R[Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{m}]
using new indeterminates
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{m}
, we get a result for polynomials with ‘partial’ rotational symmetry: if

S
is a subset of
\{1,\ldots,N\}
containing at least
2
elements and
M_{jk}p=0
for all
distinct
j,k\in S
then

p=q\left(\sum_{j\in S}X_{j}^{2},\{X_{l}\}_{l\notin S}\right)
, where
q
is a polynomial with coefficients in
R
.
Proposition
2.3
is the case
S=\{1,\ldots,N\}
.

Proposition 2.5
.

Suppose
2
is not a zero divisor in
R
, and
N
any positive integer. An
R
-linear derivation on
R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
annihilates
{X\cdot X}
if and only if it is an
R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
-linear combination of the operators
M_{jk}
.

Proof.

We saw in (
2.2
) that
{X\cdot X}
is annihilated by every

M_{jk}
, so
{X\cdot X}
is also annihilated by each
R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
-linear combination of the
M_{jk}
’s.
We turn now to proving the converse.

Each
R
-linear derivation on
R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
is determined by its effect on
X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}
, so
if
L
is an
R
-linear derivation on
R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
then

L=\sum_{j=1}^{N}a_{j}\partial_{j}

(2.15)

where
a_{j}=L(X_{j})\in R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
: both sides of (
2.15
)
are
R
-linear derivations that agree on each
X_{k}
.

The condition
L({X\cdot X})=0
is equivalent to

2\sum_{j=1}^{N}a_{j}X_{j}=0.

(2.16)

Since
2
is assumed not to be a zero divisor in
R
, (
2.16
) is equivalent to

a_{1}X_{1}+\cdots+a_{N}X_{N}=0.

(2.17)

To deduce from (
2.17
) that
L
in (
2.15
) is an
R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
-linear combination of all
M_{jk}
,
we induct on
N
. We will verify the cases
N=1
and
N=2
separately.

For
N=1
, the condition (
2.17
) implies that
a_{1}=0
, and so
L=0
, which is indeed a linear combination of
M_{11}=0
, trivially.

If
N=2
then (
2.17
) says

a_{1}X_{1}+a_{2}X_{2}=0

in
R[X_{1},X_{2}]
. Thus
a_{1}
is a multiple of
X_{2}
:
a_{1}=bX_{2}
where
b\in R[X_{1},X_{2}]
.
Since
X_{2}
is not a zero divisor in
R[X_{1},X_{2}]
, we get
a_{2}=-bX_{1}
. Therefore

L=a_{1}\partial_{1}+a_{2}\partial_{2}=bX_{2}\partial_{1}-bX_{1}\partial_{2}=bM_{21},

which proves the case
N=2
.

Now assume
N>2
and the proposition holds for all polynomial rings over
R
in fewer than
N
indeterminates. Write
a_{j}
as
a_{j}^{o}+b_{j}X_{N}
where the polynomial
a_{j}^{o}
does
not
involve
X_{N}
. Extracting the terms on the left side of (
2.17
) that do not involve
X_{N}
, we have

a_{1}^{o}X_{1}+\cdots+a_{N-1}^{o}X_{N-1}=0.

(2.18)

Then, inductively,
a_{1}^{o}\partial_{1}+\cdots+a_{N-1}^{o}\partial_{N-1}
is
an
R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N-1}]
-linear
combination of the operators
M_{jk}
for distinct
j,k\in\{1,\ldots,N-1\}
.
Since
M_{kj}=-M_{jk}
, we may take
j<k
:

a_{1}^{o}\partial_{1}+\cdots+a^{o}_{N-1}\partial_{N-1}=\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq N-1}c_{jk}M_{jk}

(2.19)

for some polynomials
c_{j,k}\in R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N-1}]
.

Subtracting (
2.18
) from (
2.17
),

(b_{1}X_{N})X_{1}+(b_{2}X_{N})X_{2}+\cdots+(b_{N-1}X_{N})X_{N-1}+a_{N}X_{N}=0,

and hence

b_{1}X_{1}+b_{2}X_{2}+\cdots+b_{N-1}X_{N-1}+a_{N}=0.

Thus the derivation

b_{1}X_{N}\partial_{1}+b_{2}X_{N}\partial_{2}+\cdots+b_{N-1}X_{N}\partial_{N-1}+a_{N}\partial_{N}
is

b_{1}X_{N}\partial_{1}+b_{2}X_{N}\partial_{2}+\cdots+b_{N-1}X_{N}\partial_{N-1}-(b_{1}X_{1}+b_{2}X_{2}+\cdots+b_{N-1}X_{N-1})\partial_{N},

which equals

b_{1}(X_{N}\partial_{1}-X_{1}\partial_{N})+\cdots+b_{N-1}(X_{N}\partial_{N-1}-X_{N-1}\partial_{N})=\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}b_{i}M_{Ni}.

Adding this to (
2.19
), we get

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}a_{j}\partial_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}(a_{j}^{o}+b_{j}X_{N})\partial_{j}+a_{N}\partial_{N}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}a_{j}^{o}\partial_{j}+(b_{1}X_{N}\partial_{1}+\cdots+b_{N-1}X_{N}\partial_{N-1}+a_{N}\partial_{N})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq N-1}c_{jk}M_{jk}+\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}b_{i}M_{Ni}.

∎

2.2.
Harmonic polynomials

A polynomial
p
is
harmonic
if
\Delta p=0
; thus the harmonic polynomials in
R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
form the kernel of the Laplacian
\Delta_{N}
.
Using the algebraic relationship between
\Delta_{N}
and the operator of multiplication by
{X\cdot X}
, principally coming from
(
2.1
),
we will see how
R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
decomposes in terms of these two operators when
R
contains
{\mathbb{Q}}
.

In this section, as before, we use the notation
{\mathcal{P}}_{N,d}
to denote the
R
-module of all polynomials in
R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
that are homogeneous of degree
d
.
The restriction of
\Delta_{N}
to
{\mathcal{P}}_{N,d}
is denoted
\Delta_{N,d}
, and the
R
-module of
all harmonic polynomials that are homogeneous of degree
d
will be denoted

{\mathcal{H}}_{N,d}=\ker(\Delta_{N,d})=\ker(\Delta_{N}|{\mathcal{P}}_{N,d}).

(2.20)

Let us note that

\Delta_{N}({\mathcal{P}}_{N,d})\subset{\mathcal{P}}_{N,d-2}.

(2.21)

Thus
\Delta_{N}
carries homogeneous polynomials into homogeneous polynomials, and so if
p
is harmonic then each homogeneous component of
p
is harmonic.

The following simple property of the second order differential operator
\Delta_{N}
will be used repeatedly:

\Delta_{N}(pq)=(\Delta_{N}p)q+2\sum_{j=1}^{N}(\partial_{j}p)(\partial_{j}q)+p\Delta_{N}q,

(2.22)

for all
p,q\in{\mathcal{P}}_{N}
.
For Proposition
2.7
we will use the following observation.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
m
be a non-negative integer and
q\in{\mathcal{P}}_{N,d}
. Then

\Delta_{N}p=(2m(2m+N-2)+4md)({X\cdot X})^{m-1}q+({X\cdot X})^{m}\Delta_{N}q,

(2.23)

where
p=({X\cdot X})^{m}q
, and the first term on the right side of (
2.23
) is taken to be
0
when
m=0
.

Proof.

Using the product formula (
2.22
), we have

\begin{split}\Delta_{N}p&=\Delta_{N}\bigl(({X\cdot X})^{m}q\bigr)\\
&=\Delta_{N}(({X\cdot X})^{m})q+2\sum_{j=1}^{N}m({X\cdot X})^{m-1}2X_{j}\partial_{j}q+({X\cdot X})^{m}\Delta_{N}q.\end{split}

(2.24)

The first term is

2m(2m+N-2)({X\cdot X})^{m-1}q
by Proposition
2.2
.
We have
\sum_{j=1}^{N}X_{j}\partial_{j}q=dq
since
q
is homogeneous, so
the second term is
4md({X\cdot X})^{m-1}q
. Hence we have (
2.23
). ∎

Now we can prove that nonzero multiples of
{X\cdot X}
cannot be harmonic.

Proposition 2.7
.

Suppose that in the ring
R
positive integer multiples of
1_{R}
are not zero divisors.
The only harmonic multiple of
{X\cdot X}
in
R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
is
0
.

Proof.

Even though
R
might not be an integral domain, for nonzero
h
in
R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
we have

\deg(({X\cdot X})h)=2+\deg h.

(2.25)

Indeed, this is reduced to the case of homogeneous
h
by writing
h
as a sum of homogeneous parts,
and when
h
is homogeneous every product of monomials in
({X\cdot X})h
has the same degree. So
the only way (
2.25
) fails for homogeneous
h
is if
({X\cdot X})h=0
, which is
impossible by thinking of
{X\cdot X}
and
h
as polynomials in
X_{N}
since

{X\cdot X}
is monic in
X_{N}
.

From (
2.25
) we have
\deg(({X\cdot X})^{m}h)=2m+\deg h
for all
m\geq 0
,
so a nonzero polynomial in
R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
isn’t arbitrarily highly divisible by
{X\cdot X}
.

Let
p
be a harmonic multiple of
{X\cdot X}
in
R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
. To prove
p=0
we will show

p
is arbitrarily highly divisible by
{X\cdot X}
: if
({X\cdot X})^{m}\mid p
for some
m\geq 1

then
({X\cdot X})^{m+1}\mid p
.

Let us recall that
\Delta_{N}
maps homogeneous polynomials to homogeneous polynomials, and we note also
that any homogeneous component of a polynomial multiple of
{X\cdot X}
is also a multiple of
{X\cdot X}
since multiplication by
{X\cdot X}
maps
any homogeneous polynomial to a homogeneous polynomial with degree raised by
2
.
We may then assume
p
is homogeneous.
Set
p=({X\cdot X})^{m}q
with
m\geq 1
and
q\in R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
. We may assume
q\not=0
.
The polynomial
q
has to be homogeneous, because if it has nonzero homogeneous
terms of different degrees then so does
p
by (
2.25
), which contradicts the homogeneity of
p
.

As seen in (
2.23
),

\Delta_{N}p=(2m(2m+N-2)+4md)({X\cdot X})^{m-1}q+({X\cdot X})^{m}\Delta_{N}q,

(2.26)

where
q\in{\mathcal{P}}_{N,d}
.

Since
\Delta_{N}p=0
we have

2m(2m+N-2+2d)q=-({{X\cdot X}})\Delta_{N}q.

(2.27)

We want to deduce from this that
({X\cdot X})\mid q
in
R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
, so

({X\cdot X})^{m+1}\mid p
in
R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
and we’d be done.

Since
{X\cdot X}
is monic in
X_{N}
, we can use the division algorithm by monics in
R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N-1}][X_{N}]

to write
q=({X\cdot X})g+r
where
r=0
or
\deg_{X_{N}}(r)\leq 1
. Multiplying both sides by

2m(2m+N-2+2d)
and using (
2.27
) we get
{X\cdot X}\mid 2m(2m+N-2+2d)r
, which
for degree reasons implies
2m(2m+N-2+2d)r=0
, so
r=0
from the zero divisor hypothesis about
R
.
∎

Proposition 2.8
.

Suppose that in the ring
R
positive integer multiples of
1_{R}
are not zero divisors.
For
c\in R
, the only harmonic multiple of
{X\cdot X}-c
in
R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
is
0
.

This result with
R={\mathbb{R}}
is standard in analysis but the statement here is purely algebraic, and the proof will be as well.

Proof.

Suppose
p\neq 0
is harmonic and a multiple of
({{X\cdot X}}-c)
. Then we can write
p
as

p=({{X\cdot X}}-c)q,

with
q\not=0
. Write
q
as a sum of homogeneous terms:

q=q_{0}+q_{1}+\cdots+q_{m},

with each
q_{k}\in R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
homogeneous of degree
k
, and
q_{m}\neq 0
.
Therefore the highest-degree homogeneous term in
p
is
({X\cdot X})q_{m}
, and

({X\cdot X})q_{m}
is nonzero since
q_{m}\not=0
and
{X\cdot X}
is not a zero divisor
(for example, it is monic in
X_{1}
).
From looking at the effect of
\Delta_{N}
on homogeneous parts of
({X\cdot X})q
, we get
\Delta_{N}(({X\cdot X})q_{m})=0
.
By Proposition
2.7
it follows that
({X\cdot X})q_{m}=0
, but we already explained why

({X\cdot X})q_{m}\not=0
, so we have a contradiction.
∎

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
A\colon V\to W
and
B\colon W\to V
be linear mappings between finite-dimensional vector spaces over a field. Suppose also that

\ker(A)\cap{\rm Im}(B)=\{0\}\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad\ker B=0.

Then
A
is surjective,
AB
is bijective, and

V=\ker(A)\oplus{\rm Im}(B).

(2.28)

Moreover,

v=(I-B(AB)^{-1}A)v+B(AB)^{-1}Av,

(2.29)

for all
v\in V
, where the first term on the right is in
\ker A
.

Proof.

The kernel of
AB\colon W\to W
is the set of all
w\in W
for which
Bw\in\ker A
, which means
Bw\in\ker(A)\cap{\rm Im}(B)=\{0\}
; since
\ker B=0
it follows then that
\ker(AB)
is also
0
. Since
W
is finite-dimensional over a field, we conclude that
AB
is bijective, so
A
is surjective. In the identity (
2.29
), the first term on the right side clearly lies in
\ker(A)
and the second term is in
{\rm Im}(B)
. Hence
\ker(A)+{\rm Im}(B)=V
. This sum is direct because
\ker(A)\cap{\rm Im}(B)=\{0\}
.
∎

Proposition 2.10
.

Suppose that the ring
R
contains
{\mathbb{Q}}
. Then every polynomial
p\in R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
can be expressed uniquely in the form

p=p_{0}+({X\cdot X})p_{1}+\cdots+({X\cdot X})^{s}p_{s},

(2.30)

where
p_{0},\ldots,p_{s}
are harmonic polynomials. Moreover, if
p
is homogeneous of degree
d
then
p_{j}
is
0
or
homogeneous of degree
d-2j
for each non-negative integer
j\leq d/2
.

This result is widely known in the analytic context; for example, Axler and Ramey
[
Axler1995
]
(Theorem 1.7 and Corollary 1.8) give an explicit formula for
p_{m}
. Reznick
[
Rez1996
]
(Theorem 4.7) proves a more general expansion and also traces some earlier works on the expansion (
2.30
). See also Peterson and Sengupta
[
PolyHigh2018
]
(equation (3.12)). The earliest reference we could find to this result is to Gauss
[
gauss
]
(page 630). Further work was done by Prasad
[
prasad1930
]
.

Proof.

First we treat the case
R={\mathbb{Q}}
. We use
Lemma
2.9
, with
V={\mathcal{P}}_{N,j}
,
W={\mathcal{P}}_{N,j-2}
, the operator
A
being
\Delta_{N,j}=\Delta_{N}|{\mathcal{P}}_{N,j}
and
B
being multiplication by
{{X\cdot X}}
. We apply repeatedly, to obtain

\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}_{N,d}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ker\Delta_{N,d}\oplus({X\cdot X}){\mathcal{P}}_{N,d-2}

(2.31)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ker\Delta_{N,d}\oplus({{X\cdot X}})\ker\Delta_{N,d-2}\oplus({X\cdot X})^{2}{\mathcal{P}}_{N,d-4}.

Continuing in this way we get the direct sum decomposition

{\mathcal{P}}_{N,d}={\mathcal{H}}_{N,d}\oplus({{X\cdot X}}){\mathcal{H}}_{N,d-2}\oplus\ldots,

(2.32)

and hence (
2.30
) for polynomials with rational coefficients.

In particular, each monomial in
{\mathbb{Q}}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
is the sum of a harmonic
polynomial and a multiple of
{X\cdot X}
with
{\mathbb{Q}}
-coefficients (preserving homogeneity as the proposition describes). Taking

R
-linear combinations of monomials, when
R
contains
{\mathbb{Q}}
, shows every element of
R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]

is the sum of a harmonic
polynomial and a multiple of
{X\cdot X}
(preserving homogeneity). This decomposition is unique by
Proposition
2.7
, and the degree part follows from (
2.25
).
∎

The decomposition (
2.31
), or more simply the surjectivity of
\Delta_{N,d}:{\mathcal{P}}_{N,d}\to{\mathcal{P}}_{N,d-2}
given by Lemma
2.9
, provides the following dimension formula for harmonic polynomials in case
R
is a field:

\displaystyle\dim_{R}{\mathcal{H}}_{N,d}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dim_{R}{\mathcal{P}}_{N,d}-\dim_{R}{\mathcal{P}}_{N,d-2}

(2.33)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\binom{N+d-1}{N-1}-\binom{N+d-3}{N-1},

where the second formula is based on

\dim_{R}{\mathcal{P}}_{N,d}=\binom{N+d-1}{N-1},

(2.34)

which is the number of monomials of degree
d
in
N
indeterminates. Formula (
2.33
) has appeared in the literature before; for example,
[
harmfunctionbook
]
(Theorem 5.8). We will obtain another expression for the dimension of
{\mathcal{H}}_{N,d}
below in (
2.41
).

We will see after Proposition
2.12

that
{\mathcal{H}}_{N,d}
is a free
R
-module when
R
is a ring
containing
{\mathbb{Q}}
, not necessarily a field, so (
2.33
) is true as a rank formula for
{\mathcal{H}}_{N,d}
as an
R
-module.
However, we can’t take
R={\mathbb{Z}}
: some denominators need to be allowed.
For example, taking
N=d=2
in (
2.31
) we have
x^{2}=\frac{1}{2}(x^{2}-y^{2})+(x,y)\cdot(x,y)\frac{1}{2}
,
where
\frac{1}{2}(x^{2}-y^{2})
is harmonic. Even though
x^{2}
on the left is in
{\mathbb{Z}}[x,y]
,
on the right
\frac{1}{2}(x^{2}-y^{2})
is not in
{\mathbb{Z}}[x,y]
.

2.3.
Eigenspaces of
\Delta_{N}

The Laplacian
\Delta_{N,d}
sends
{\mathcal{P}}_{N,d}
to
{\mathcal{P}}_{N,d-2}
, so the product

({X\cdot X})\Delta_{N,d}
sends
{\mathcal{P}}_{N,d}
to itself.
From (
2.32
) we get an eigenspace decomposition for this operator on
{\mathcal{P}}_{N,d}
, as follows.

Proposition 2.11
.

Suppose that the ring
R
contains
{\mathbb{Q}}
. The eigenvalues of
{({X\cdot X})}\Delta_{N,d}
acting on
{\mathcal{P}}_{N,d}

are
\lambda_{m}=2m(N-2+2d-2m)

for integers
m
where
0\leq m\leq d/2
, and
the
\lambda_{m}
-eigenspace of
{({X\cdot X})}\Delta_{N,d}
is
({X\cdot X})^{m}{\mathcal{H}}_{N,d-2m}
.
In particular, when
R
is a field of characteristic
0
, the characteristic polynomial of
{({X\cdot X})}\Delta_{N,d}
acting on
{\mathcal{P}}_{N,d}
is

\det\bigl(tI-({{X\cdot X}})\Delta_{N,d}\bigr)=\prod_{m=0}^{[d/2]}\left(t-\lambda_{m}\right)^{\dim_{R}{\mathcal{H}}_{N,d-2m}}.

(2.35)

Proof.

The case
d=0
is clear, and so we assume
d\geq 1
.
For
h\in\ker\Delta_{N,d-2m}
, where
0\leq m\leq d/2
, we have
\deg h=d-2m
.
Therefore by (
2.23
) with
d
there replaced by
d-2m
,

\Delta_{N,d}\bigl(({X\cdot X})^{m}h\bigr)=2m\left(2m+N-2+2(d-2m)\right)({X\cdot X})^{m-1}h.

(2.36)

Therefore
({X\cdot X})\Delta_{N,d}
acts on
({X\cdot X})^{m}{\mathcal{H}}_{N,d-2m}
as multiplication by
\lambda_{m}
.
Each
({X\cdot X})^{m}{\mathcal{H}}_{N,d-2m}
is an eigenspace for
({X\cdot X})\Delta_{N,d}
and these
are all of its eigenspaces in
{\mathcal{P}}_{N,d}
by the direct sum decomposition in
Proposition
2.10
.
∎

2.4.
Construction of harmonic polynomials

We show that a harmonic polynomial
p(X_{1},\ldots,X_{N})
, when viewed as a polynomial in
X_{N}
, is completely determined by the ‘constant term’ that depends only on the indeterminates
X_{1},\ldots,X_{N-1}
.

Proposition 2.12
.

Suppose
R
is a ring containing
{\mathbb{Q}}
, and
N
and
d
are non-negative integers with
N\geq 2
. Pick
p\in R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]

that is homogeneous of degree
d\geq 0
and write it as a polynomial in
X_{N}
:

p=p_{0}+p_{1}X_{N}+\cdots+p_{d}X_{N}^{d},

(2.37)

where
p_{0},\ldots,p_{d}\in R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N-1}]
. Then
p

is harmonic if and only if the
X_{N}
-coefficients
p_{2},p_{3},\ldots,p_{d}
are determined by
p_{0}
and
p_{1}
through the relations

\begin{split}p_{2k}&=(-1)^{k}\frac{1}{(2k)!}\Delta^{k}p_{0}\ {\rm for}\ 1\leq k\leq d/2,\\
p_{2k+1}&=(-1)^{k}\frac{1}{(2k+1)!}\Delta^{k}p_{1}\ {\rm for}\ 1\leq k\leq(d-1)/2.\end{split}

(2.38)

Proof.

Let us note first that for any polynomial
q\in R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
that is independent of
X_{N}
, we have
\Delta_{N}q=\Delta_{N-1}q
.

Since
p
is homogeneous of degree
d
, in (
2.37
) each nonzero
p_{k}
is homogeneous
of degree
d-k
.

Since
p_{j}
and
X_{N}^{j}
are polynomials in disjoint sets of indeterminates, using the product formula (
2.22
), we have:

\Delta(p_{j}X_{N}^{j})=p_{j}\Delta(X_{N}^{j})+(\Delta p_{j})X_{N}^{j}=j(j-1)p_{j}X_{N}^{j-2}+(\Delta p_{j})X_{N}^{j}

(the middle term
\sum_{i=0}^{d}2(\partial_{i}p_{j})(\partial_{i}X_{N}^{j})
is 0 since each summand is 0).
Therefore

\displaystyle\Delta p

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{d}\Delta(p_{j}X_{N}^{j})

(2.39)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{d}\left(j(j-1)p_{j}X_{N}^{j-2}+(\Delta p_{j})X_{N}^{j}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{d}\left((j+2)(j+1)p_{j+2}+(\Delta p_{j})\right)X_{N}^{j}.

We have
\Delta p=0
if and only if
every coefficient of
X_{N}
in (
2.39
) is 0, which is equivalent to

p_{j}=-(\Delta p_{j-2})/(j(j-1))
for
j\geq 2
.
This is the same as (
2.38
).
∎

Since
p_{0}
and
p_{1}
determine
p_{j}
for
j\geq 2
in
(
2.37
) when
p
is harmonic,
and there is no constraint on
p_{0}
and
p_{1}
other than their degrees (and being homogeneous if they are not 0) the mapping

{\mathcal{H}}_{N,d}\to{\mathcal{P}}_{N-1,d}\oplus{\mathcal{P}}_{N-1,d-1}\ \text{ given by }\ p\mapsto(p_{0},p_{1})

(2.40)

is an
R
-module isomorphism.
Both
{\mathcal{P}}_{N-1,d}
and
{\mathcal{P}}_{N-1,d-1}
are free
R
-modules for all
R
, so
if
R
is a ring containing
{\mathbb{Q}}

the
R
-module
{\mathcal{H}}_{N,d}
is free
with a basis of cardinality

\operatorname{rank}_{R}{\mathcal{H}}_{N,d}=\binom{N+d-2}{N-2}+\binom{N+d-3}{N-2},

(2.41)

where for the right side we use the rank formula (
2.34
) (that formula does not need
R
to be a field).
The formula in (
2.41
) agrees with the one in (
2.33
)
by using a standard binomial coefficient identity twice.

Corollary 2.13
.

When
R
contains
{\mathbb{Q}}
, there is a basis of
{\mathcal{H}}_{N,d}
with coefficients in
{\mathbb{Q}}
.

Proof.

Both
{\mathcal{P}}_{N-1,d}
and
{\mathcal{P}}_{N-1,d-1}
have monomial bases with coefficient 1. By
(
2.38
), if
p_{0}
and
p_{1}
have
{\mathbb{Q}}
-coefficients then the associated homogeneous
harmonic polynomial (
2.37
) has
{\mathbb{Q}}
-coefficients.
∎

Example 2.14
.

Let us look at the case
N=2
: from (
2.41
),
when the coefficient ring
R
contains
{\mathbb{Q}}
the
R
-module

{\mathcal{H}}_{2,d}
of homogeneous harmonic polynomials in
x
and
y
of degree
d
has a basis of size 2.
General polynomials in
{\mathcal{P}}_{N-1,d}
and
{\mathcal{P}}_{N-1,d-1}
are
p_{0}=ax^{d}
and
p_{1}=bx^{d-1}
, respectively,
where
a
and
b
are in
R
.
Using (
2.38
),
the homogeneous harmonic polynomial in
x
and
y
of degree
d
with such
p_{0}
and
p_{1}
is

a\sum_{2k\leq d}(-1)^{k}\binom{d}{2k}x^{d-2k}y^{2k}+b\sum_{2k+1\leq d}(-1)^{k}\frac{1}{d}\binom{d}{2k+1}x^{d-2k-1}y^{2k+1}.

The first sum is
((x+iy)^{d}+(x-iy)^{d})/2=\operatorname{Re}((x+iy)^{d})
and the second sum is

((x+iy)^{d}-(x-iy)^{d})/(2id)=\operatorname{Im}((x+iy)^{d})/d
, which recovers the classical fact that

\operatorname{Re}((x+iy)^{d})
and
\operatorname{Im}((x+iy)^{d})
are a basis of
{\mathcal{H}}_{2,d}
when
R={\mathbb{R}}
.

2.5.
Polynomials constant on spheres

If a polynomial
p
is in the ideal of
R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
generated by
{{X\cdot X}}-c
, for some
c\in R
,
then we think of
p
as being ‘equal to zero’ on the ‘sphere’ given by the equation
{{X\cdot X}}=c
. Similarly, we say that
p
is ‘constant’, equal to
c^{\prime}\in R
, on the sphere given by
{{X\cdot X}}-c
if
p-c^{\prime}
is zero on this sphere.

We will apply the harmonic polynomial decomposition in Proposition
2.10

to determine polynomials that are ‘constant on a sphere’ using the following two lemmas.

Lemma 2.15
.

For a commutative ring
R
,
p\in R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
and
a\in R
that is not a zero divisor, suppose

ap
is divisible by
{X\cdot X}-c
, where
c\in R
. Then
p
is divisible by
{X\cdot X}-c
.

Proof.

Since
{X\cdot X}-c
is monic as a polynomial in
X_{N}
we can write

p=({X\cdot X}-c)q+r

where
q,r\in R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N-1}][X_{N}]
with

r=0
or
\deg_{X_{N}}(r)\leq 1
since
\deg_{X_{N}}({X\cdot X})=2
.
Multiplying both sides by
a
,

ap=({X\cdot X}-c)aq+ar,

so from
ap
being divisible by
{X\cdot X}-c
we get
({X\cdot X}-c)\mid(ar)
in
R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
.
Write
ar=({X\cdot X}-c)g
.
Since
{X\cdot X}-c
is monic in
X_{N}
,
if
r\not=0
then

\deg_{X_{N}}(ar)=2+\deg_{X_{N}}(g)\geq 2
, but

\deg_{X_{N}}(ar)=\deg_{X_{N}}(r)\leq 1
since
a
is not a zero divisor. This is a contradiction,
so
r=0
and thus
({X\cdot X}-c)\mid p
.
∎

Lemma 2.16
.

Let
R
be a commutative ring, and
c_{1},\ldots,c_{m}
for
m\geq 2

be distinct elements of
R
such that no difference
c_{i}-c_{j}
for
i\not=j

is a zero-divisor. If
p\in R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
is divisible by
{X\cdot X}-c_{j}
for

j=1,\ldots,m
then
p
is divisible by
\prod_{j=1}^{m}({X\cdot X}-c_{j})
.

Proof.

First we will show that if
{X\cdot X}-a
divides
({X\cdot X}-b)g
for
a,b\in R

and
a-b
is not a zero divisor in
R
then

{X\cdot X}-a
divides
g
.

For some polynomial
h
we have
({X\cdot X}-a)h=({X\cdot X}-b)g
.
Then

bg=({X\cdot X})(g-h)+ah,

(2.42)

so

(b-a)g=({X\cdot X}-a)(g-h).

(2.43)

By Lemma
2.15
,
{X\cdot X}-a
divides
g
.

Now assume
p
is divisible by
{X\cdot X}-c_{j}
for
j=1,\ldots,m
with
c_{i}-c_{j}
not being a zero divisor
for all
i\not=j
.
Inductively we can suppose
p
is divisible by
({X\cdot X}-c_{1})\cdots({X\cdot X}-c_{m-1})
, say
p=({X\cdot X}-c_{1})\cdots({X\cdot X}-c_{m-1})q
in
R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
. We will show
{X\cdot X}-c_{m}
divides
q
and then we’ll be done.
Since
{X\cdot X}-c_{m}\mid({X\cdot X}-c_{1})({X\cdot X}-c_{2})\cdots({X\cdot X}-c_{m-1})q
, we get

{X\cdot X}-c_{m}\mid({X\cdot X}-c_{2})\cdots({X\cdot X}-c_{m-1})q
since
c_{m}-c_{1}
is not a zero divisor. Then

{X\cdot X}-c_{m}\mid({X\cdot X}-c_{3})\cdots({X\cdot X}-c_{m-1})q
since
c_{m}-c_{2}
is not a zero divisor.
Eventually we are left with
({X\cdot X}-c_{m})\mid q
and we are done.
∎

Proposition 2.17
.

Suppose that
R
is a ring containing
{\mathbb{Q}}
, and
p\in R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
, where
N\geq 2
.

(i)

If, for some
c\in R
, all
M_{jk}p
are divisible by
{X\cdot X}-c

then
p
is equal to a constant modulo
{X\cdot X}-c
.

(ii)

If
p
is nonzero of degree
d
and for an
m\geq d/2

there are
c_{1},\ldots,c_{m}
in
R

whose pairwise differences are not zero divisors with each
M_{jk}p
divisible by all
{X\cdot X}-c_{i}

then
p
is a polynomial in
{X\cdot X}
.

(iii)

If
p
is nonzero and homogeneous of degree
d
and there is
c\in R
that is not a zero divisor such that all

M_{jk}p
are divisible by
{X\cdot X}-c
then
d
is even and
p=a({X\cdot X})^{d/2}
where
a\in R
.

If
p
is equal to a ‘constant’ mod
{{X\cdot X}}-c
(that is, for some
c^{\prime}\in R
,
p-c^{\prime}
belongs to the ideal generated by
{X\cdot X}-c
)
then every
M_{jk}p
is
0
mod
{{X\cdot X}}-c
. The proposition provides statements in the converse direction to this observation. Note that in (iii) the hypothesis is that, for
p
homogeneous,
M_{jk}p
are divisible by
{X\cdot X}-c
and the conclusion implies that
M_{jk}p
are in fact all
0
.

Proof.

By Proposition
2.10
we can write

p=p_{0}+({X\cdot X})p_{1}+\cdots+({{X\cdot X}})^{s}p_{s},

(2.44)

with
p_{0},\ldots,p_{s}
all harmonic polynomials. By (
2.12
),

M_{jk}(({X\cdot X})^{m}p_{m})=({X\cdot X})^{m}M_{jk}(p_{m})

for all
m\geq 0
, so

M_{jk}p=M_{jk}p_{0}+({X\cdot X})M_{jk}p_{1}+\cdots+({{X\cdot X}})^{s}M_{jk}p_{s}.

Now let

p_{*}=p_{0}+cp_{1}+\cdots+c^{s}p_{s}.

(2.45)

This is harmonic (since each
p_{k}
is) and equal to
p
mod
({{X\cdot X}}-c)
.

(i) Since
M_{jk}({X\cdot X}-c)=0
, from

p\equiv p_{*}\bmod{X\cdot X}-c
we get

M_{jk}p_{*}\equiv M_{jk}p\bmod({{X\cdot X}}-c)
, and so, by the hypothesis in (i),
M_{jk}p_{*}\equiv 0\bmod({{X\cdot X}}-c)
.
Since
p_{*}
is harmonic and
M_{jk}
commutes with
\Delta
,

M_{jk}p_{*}
is harmonic. Thus by Proposition
2.8
,
M_{jk}p_{*}=0
. This being true for all
distinct
j,k\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
, it follows by Proposition
2.3
that
p_{*}
is a polynomial in
{X\cdot X}
with coefficients in
R
.
Therefore
p_{*}-p_{*}(0)
is a multiple of
{X\cdot X}
.
Since
p_{*}
is harmonic so is
p_{*}-p_{*}(0)
, so
p_{*}-p_{*}(0)=0
by Proposition
2.7
:
p_{*}
is a constant. Thus
p
is equal to a constant modulo
{X\cdot X}-c
.

(ii)
First we give a proof when
m>d/2
. By Lemma
2.16
, each
M_{jk}p
is divisible by
\prod_{i=1}^{m}({X\cdot X}-c_{i})
, so if
M_{jk}(p)\not=0
then
\deg(M_{jk}p)\geq 2m
by (
2.25
). Since
p
has degree
d
,
M_{jk}(p)
has degree at most
d
, so
2m\leq d
. Thus if
m>d/2
it follows that all
M_{jk}(p)=0
, so
p
is a polynomial in

{X\cdot X}
by Proposition
2.3
.

The argument above does not apply if
m=d/2
, but the following argument does (and also applies to the case
m>d/2
). By (i) we can write

p=c_{0}+({X\cdot X}-c_{1})q,

(2.46)

where
c_{0}\in R
and
q\in R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
.
Then
\deg q=d-2
by (
2.25
), unless
q=0
.
If
d=2
then
q
is constant and (
2.46
) shows

p
is a polynomial in
{X\cdot X}
.
Suppose that
d>2
and
m=d/2
. Since
M_{jk}({X\cdot X})=0
, applying
M_{jk}
to both sides of (
2.46
) gives us

M_{jk}p=({X\cdot X}-c_{1})M_{jk}q.

As seen in the proof of Lemma
2.16
(the first paragraph in the proof),
M_{jk}q
is a multiple of
{{X\cdot X}}-c_{i}
for
i=2,\ldots,m
; note that
m-1=(d-2)/2
. Inductively, we conclude that
q
is a polynomial in
{X\cdot X}
, so
p
is as well.

(iii) When
p
is homogeneous of degree
d
, each
p_{j}
in (
2.44
) is 0 or is homogeneous of degree
d-2j

by Proposition
2.10
. It follows then that for each
j
, the term
c^{j}p_{j}
is the homogeneous part of
p_{*}
of degree
d-2j
. By the proof of (i),
p_{*}
in (
2.45
)
is constant; hence each
c^{j}p_{j}
is
0
unless

d-2j=0
. Since
c
is not a zero divisor, if
c^{j}p_{j}=0
then
p_{j}=0
. Since
p
is not 0,
we get
p=p_{j_{0}}({X\cdot X})^{j_{0}}
, with
d-2j_{0}=0
. Therefore
d=2j_{0}
is even and

p=a({X\cdot X})^{d/2}
where
a=p_{j_{0}}
is in
R
.
∎

Next we note a consequence of Proposition
2.17
.

Proposition 2.18
.

Let
R
be a ring containing
{\mathbb{Q}}
and
N\geq 2
.
Suppose that
p\in R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
is such that
M_{12}p
is in the ideal generated by
X_{1}^{2}+X_{2}^{2}-c
for some
c\in R
. Then

p=a+(X_{1}^{2}+X_{2}^{2}-c)q

where
a\in R
and
q\in R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
.

Proof.

Since
M_{12}p
and
M_{21}p=-M_{12}p
are divisible by
X_{1}^{2}+X_{2}^{2}-c
,
by Proposition
2.17
(i)
with
R
replaced by
R[X_{3},\ldots,X_{N}]
we have
p\equiv a\bmod(X_{1}^{2}+X_{2}^{2}-c)
for some
a\in R
.
Therefore
p=a+(X_{1}^{2}+X_{2}^{2}-c)q
where
q
is in
R[X_{3},\ldots,X_{N}][X_{1},X_{2}]=R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
.
∎

2.6.
Polynomials that vanish on a sphere

We turn now to showing (Proposition
2.20
) that, under some conditions on the ring
R
, the ideal of polynomials in
R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
that vanish on the ‘sphere of radius’
a
in
R^{N}
is the ideal generated by
{{X\cdot X}}-a^{2}
.

For a ring
R
, any positive integer
k
and any
a\in R
we define the
k
-dimensional open ball
B_{k}(a)
to be the set of all
(c_{1},\ldots,c_{k})\in R^{k}
such that

c_{1}^{2}+\cdots+c_{k}^{2}+y^{2}=a^{2},

for some nonzero
y\in R
. (This is motivated by the geometry of the case
R={\mathbb{R}}
.)

For the following results we will impose a certain property on
R
that ensures that open balls
B_{N}(a)
contain infinitely many points on each ‘slice’ specified by fixing the first few coordinates. This includes assuming that
B_{1}(a)
itself contains infinitely many points. The condition on
R
, with any nonzero
a\in R
, holds if
R
is the field of algebraic numbers, the reals
{\mathbb{R}}
, or the complexes
{\mathbb{C}}
.

Lemma 2.19
.

Let
R
be an integral domain and suppose that
a\in R
is such that the open ball
B_{1}(a)
is infinite and, for every integer
k\geq 1
and every
(c_{1},\ldots,c_{k})\in B_{k}(a)
, there are infinitely many
c_{k+1}\in R
for which
(c_{1},\ldots,c_{k+1})\in B_{k+1}(a)
. If
p\in R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
is zero on
B_{N}(a)
then
p=0
.

Proof.

The result holds for
N=1
because a nonzero polynomial in
R[X]
cannot have infinitely many zeros in
R
:
if
p
is
0
at distinct points
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m}
then, using the fact that
R
is an integral domain and repeatedly applying the division algorithm, we have

p=(X-\alpha_{1})\ldots(X-\alpha_{m})q

for some polynomial
q\in R[X]
and so
m
can be at most the degree of
p
.

Now suppose
N>1
.
Write
p
as a polynomial in
X_{N}
with coefficients in
R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N-1}]
:

p=\sum_{k=0}^{m}p_{k}X_{N}^{k}.

For each
(c_{1},\ldots,c_{N-1})\in B_{N-1}(a)
the polynomial

p(c_{1},\ldots,c_{N-1},X_{N})
has infinitely many zeros, one for every point
c_{N}
for which
(c_{1},\ldots,c_{N})\in B_{N}(a)
. Hence each coefficient
p_{k}
is zero at each point of
B_{N-1}(a)
. Then by induction each polynomial
p_{k}
is
0
, so
p
is
0
.∎

Proposition 2.20
.

Let
R
be an integral domain and suppose that
a\in R
is nonzero and such that the open ball
B_{1}(a)
is infinite and, for every integer
k\geq 1
and every
(c_{1},\ldots,c_{k})\in B_{k}(a)
, there are infinitely many
c_{k+1}\in R
for which
(c_{1},\ldots,c_{k+1})\in B_{k+1}(a)
. Suppose also that
R
does not have
characteristic
2
. Let
p(X_{1},\ldots,X_{N})\in R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
be such that
p(c_{1},\ldots,c_{N})=0
whenever
c_{1}^{2}+\cdots+c_{N}^{2}=a^{2}
. Then

p(X_{1},\ldots,X_{N})=({{X\cdot X}}-a^{2})q,

(2.47)

for some
q\in R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
.

Proof.

For
N=1
, the hypotheses imply that
p(X_{1})
is divisible by
X_{1}-a
and
X_{1}+a
, which are distinct because
2a\neq 0
. Hence (
2.47
) holds.

Suppose
N>1
. By the division algorithm by monic polynomials in the ring of polynomials in
X_{N}
with coefficients in
R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N-1}]
we have

\begin{split}p(X_{1},\ldots,X_{N})&=\left(X_{N}^{2}\,+X_{1}^{2}+\cdots+X_{N-1}^{2}-a^{2}\right)q\\
&\qquad+r_{1}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{N-1})X_{N}+r_{0}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{N-1})\end{split}

(2.48)

for some polynomials
q\in R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
, and
r_{0},r_{1}\in R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N-1}]
. We will show that
r_{0}
and
r_{1}
are zero by using Lemma
2.19
.

Let
(c_{1},\ldots,c_{N-1})\in B_{N-1}(a)
. Then there is a non-zero
t\in R
such that

c_{1}^{2}+\cdots+c_{N-1}^{2}-a^{2}=-t^{2}.

Evaluating (
2.48
) at
(c_{1},\ldots,c_{N-1},t)
, and noting that the left side is
0
by the assumption on
p
, we have

0=r_{1}(c_{1},\ldots,c_{N-1})t+r_{0}(c_{1},\ldots,c_{N-1}).

This holds for a nonzero value of
t
and also for
-t\neq t
, since
R
does not have characteristic
2
. It follows that both
r_{1}
and
r_{0}
are
0
when evaluated at
(c_{1},\ldots,c_{N-1})
. Then by Lemma
2.19
, the polynomials
r_{0}
and
r_{1}
are both
0
.
∎

3.
Simultaneous eigenvectors for commuting generators

In this section we study the common eigenvectors in
R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]

of the commuting operators
M_{12}
,
M_{34}
,
\ldots
,
M_{2n-1,2n}
, where

n
is the largest integer for which
2n\leq N
.

Most, but not all, results in this section will use the hypothesis that in
R
, no
positive integer multiples of
1_{R}
are zero divisors. Some results will also use the assumption that
i=\sqrt{-1}\in R
. We will repeat these assumptions on
R
in the statements of the results.

The following result gives the eigenvectors of each operator
M_{jk}
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
R
be a ring containing

Proposition 3.4

4.
Spherical means

In this section we prove an algebraic counterpart (Corollary
4.8
below)
of the classical analytic result that the mean of a harmonic function over a sphere equals
the value of the function at the center of the sphere.

We would like to identify the integral of a polynomial over a sphere centered at
0
by using purely algebraic notions. Let
\lambda
be such an integral, viewed as a linear functional on
the space of polynomials. Applying
M_{jk}
to a polynomial
q
is the effect of taking the derivative of a one-parameter group of rotations of
q
in the
X_{j}
-
X_{k}
-plane. Thus
\lambda(M_{jk}q)
should be
0
, since the spherical integral would remain invariant under rotations. Thus, for any integer
N\geq 2
, we define a
spherical mean
to be an
R
-linear map

\lambda\colon R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]\to R

for which

\lambda\circ M_{jk}=0\qquad\hbox{for all $j,k\in\{1,\ldots,N\}$ with $j\not=k$.}

For any polynomials
p
and
q
,
M_{jk}(pq)=pM_{jk}(q)+qM_{jk}(p)
, so applying
\lambda
to
M_{jk}(pq)
shows that

\lambda(pM_{jk}q)=-\lambda\left(qM_{jk}p\right).

For
N=1
, when there are no nonzero operators
M_{jk}
, we require instead that
\lambda
vanish on
X_{1}^{n}
for all odd integers
n\geq 1

since a sphere centered at 0 in the real line is a pair of points
\{x,-x\}
.

4.1.
The spherical mean
\lambda_{0}

Our next goal is to show that there exists a unique spherical mean
\lambda_{0}
on
R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]

for which
\lambda_{0}\bigl(({X\cdot X})^{n}\bigr)=1
for all integers
n\geq 0
, and, moreover, that all spherical means on
R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
are
R
-multiples of
\lambda_{0}
when restricted to homogeneous polynomials of any fixed degree.

We will use the double factorial on nonnegative integers:

b!!=\begin{cases}b(b-2)\cdots\cdot 1\qquad\hbox{if $b$ is odd and $\geq 1$;}\\
b(b-2)\cdots\cdot 2\qquad\hbox{if $b$ is even and $\geq 2$;}\\
1\qquad\hbox{if $b=0$.}\end{cases}

(4.1)

It will also be convenient to set
(-1)!!=1
, which is a special case of the standard extension of double factorials to negative integers using the recursion
n!!=(n+2)!!/(n+2)
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
M
be an
R
-module, where the ring
R
contains
{\mathbb{Q}}
, and let
N
be an integer
\geq 2
.
For an
R
-linear map

\lambda\colon R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]\to M

(4.2)

the following two properties are equivalent:

•

for all
j,k\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
with
j\not=k
,

\lambda\circ M_{jk}=0,

(4.3)

•

for each even
d\geq 0
there is
v_{d}\in M
such that for all monomials
X_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots X_{N}^{a_{N}}
,

\lambda(X_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots X_{N}^{a_{N}})=\begin{cases}(a_{1}\!-\!1)!!\cdots(a_{N}\!-\!1)!!v_{a_{1}+\cdots+a_{N}},&\text{ if all }a_{j}\text{ are even},\\
0,&\text{ if some }a_{j}\text{ is odd}.\end{cases}

Moreover, there exists a unique spherical mean
\lambda_{0}
on
R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
whose value on
({X\cdot X})^{n}
is
1
for all integers
n\geq 0
.

Let us note that the last paragraph above holds even for
N=1
: we set
\lambda_{0}(X_{1}^{k})
equal to
0
for odd
k
and equal to
1
for even
k
.
We also note that the value
\lambda(X_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots X_{N}^{a_{N}})
is invariant under permutations of the indeterminates
X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}
, and so, using
\lambda_{0}({X\cdot X})=1
, it follows that

\lambda_{0}(X_{j}^{2})=\frac{1}{N},

(4.4)

for all
j\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
.

Proof of Proposition
4.1
.

First we assume (
4.3
) holds and derive the formula for
\lambda
on monomials.
Let
X
and
Y
be two distinct indeterminates among
X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}
, and let
q
be a polynomial in the other indeterminates. Then for any integers
a\geq 1
and
b\geq 0
, we have

(X\partial_{Y}-Y\partial_{X})\left(X^{a-1}Y^{b+1}q\right)=(b+1)X^{a}Y^{b}q-(a-1)X^{a-2}Y^{b+2}q,

(4.5)

where the second term on the right is defined to be
0
if
a=1
.
Applying
\lambda
to both sides, we get by (
4.3
)

0=(b+1)\lambda\left(X^{a}Y^{b}q\right)-(a-1)\lambda\left(X^{a-2}Y^{b+2}q\right).

(4.6)

Taking
a=1
we see that
\lambda(Xr)=0
for all polynomials
r
in the indeterminates other than
X
. Moreover, if
a
is an odd positive integer, repeated application of (
4.6
) reduces
\lambda(X^{a}Y^{b}q)
to a rational multiple of
\lambda(XY^{b+a-1}q)
, which is
0
. Conversely, if
\lambda(X^{a}Y^{b}q)=0
whenever
a
is odd and
q
does not involve
X
or

then (
4.6
) holds for odd

since both terms on the right are then
0
.
Thus
\lambda(X_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots X_{N}^{a_{N}})=0
if
some
a_{j}
is odd.

Now suppose
a
and
b
are both even positive integers. Dividing both sides of (

) by
the product of double factorials

(a-1)!!(b+1)!!
, we obtain

\frac{1}{(a-1)!!(b-1)!!}\lambda\left(X^{a}Y^{b}q\right)=\frac{1}{(a-3)!!(b+1)!!}\lambda\left(X^{a-2}Y^{b+2}q\right).

(4.7)

It follows
that
\lambda
has a common value on all monomials of the form

\frac{1}{(a-1)!!(b-1)!!}X^{a}Y^{b}q,

where
a
and

are even non-negative integers with
a fixed value for the sum

. Of course, this applies to
X
and
Y
being any two of the indeterminates

. It follows that
\lambda
has a common value on all monomials of the form

\frac{1}{(a_{1}-1)!!\cdots(a_{N}-1)!!}X_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots X_{N}^{a_{N}}

(4.8)

for all even integers
a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}\geq 0
having a fixed values for the sum
|a|
.
Conversely, if
\lambda
has a common value on all monomials of the form
(
4.8
), where the

are all even, then (
4.7
) and hence (
4.6
) hold for

and
Y
running over distinct indeterminates among
X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}
. Then by (

),
\lambda(M_{XY}p)
is

for all polynomials

and hence
\lambda\circ M_{jk}=0
for all

.

Thus an

-valued spherical mean
\lambda
is obtained by setting the value of
\lambda
to be 0 on monomials
having an odd exponent, and to have a common value

on the monomials in (

) having all non-negative even exponents and being of total degree

.

Focusing now on the case

, let
\lambda
be an

-valued spherical mean on

and set

\lambda(X_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots X_{N}^{a_{N}})=(a_{1}-1)!!\cdots(a_{N}-1)!!s_{2n,N},

(4.9)

where the exponents

are all even with

. Then expanding

with the multinomial theorem and applying

\displaystyle\lambda\bigl(({X\cdot X})^{n}\bigr)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b_{1},\ldots,b_{N}\geq 0\\
b_{1}+\cdots+b_{N}=n\end{subarray}}\frac{n!}{b_{1}!\ldots b_{N}!}(2b_{1}-1)!!\ldots(2b_{N}-1)!!s_{2n,N}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle s_{2n,N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b_{1},\ldots,b_{N}\geq 0\\
b_{1}+\cdots+b_{N}=n\end{subarray}}\frac{n!}{b_{1}!\ldots b_{N}!}(2b_{1}-1)!!\ldots(2b_{N}-1)!!.

The summations are really over ordered
N
-tuples
(b_{1},\ldots,b_{N})
.
Since the summation is positive, for each
n\geq 0
there is a unique positive rational number
s_{2n,N}
that makes
\lambda(({X\cdot X})^{n})=1
. This determines a unique

R

\lambda
on

, by

.
∎

Let us note, using (

\lambda_{0}(X_{1}^{2b_{1}}\cdots X_{N}^{2b_{N}})=(2b_{1}-1)!!\ldots(2b_{N}-1)!!s_{2n,N},

(4.10)

where

Remark 4.2
.

We observe, as a consequence of Proposition
4.1
, that the value of a spherical mean on
X_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots X_{N}^{a_{N}}
, with each
a_{i}
even, is a multiple of
(a_{1}-1)!!\cdots(a_{N}-1)!!
, the multiple being determined by the degree
a_{1}+\cdots+a_{N}
. Thus, a mapping
\lambda:R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]\to R
is a spherical mean if and only if it is an
R
-multiple of
\lambda_{0}
when restricted to homogeneous polynomials of fixed degree. For this reason we can, without loss of much generality, restrict our attention to
\lambda_{0}
rather than on all spherical means.

For a polynomial
p
with real coefficients, viewed as a function on
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
,
the value of
\lambda_{0}(p)
is the normalized integral of
p

over the unit sphere in
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
with center at the origin. The following result reflects the fact that multiplying by a factor of
{X\cdot X}
, which would correspond to just multiplying by
1
over the geometric sphere in
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
, does not affect the value of
\lambda_{0}
on any polynomial.

Proposition 4.3
.

For a commutative ring
R
containing
{\mathbb{Q}}
,

\lambda_{0}\bigl(({X\cdot X})p\bigr)=\lambda_{0}(p)

(4.11)

for all
p\in R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
.

Proof.

The case
N=1
follows by direct verification. We assume then that
N\geq 2
.
We will show the

R
-linear map
\mu\colon R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]\rightarrow R
given by

\mu(p)=\lambda_{0}(({X\cdot X})p)
has the properties that uniquely characterize
p\mapsto\lambda_{0}(p)
.

For each
M_{jk}
,

\mu(M_{jk}p)=\lambda_{0}(({X\cdot X})M_{jk}(p))=\lambda_{0}(M_{jk}(({X\cdot X})p))
by (
2.12

\mu(M_{jk}p)=(\lambda_{0}\circ M_{jk})(({X\cdot X})p)=0
, so
\mu
is a spherical mean.

We have
\mu(({X\cdot X})^{n})=1
for all

since

\mu(({X\cdot X})^{n})=\lambda_{0}(({X\cdot X})^{n+1})=1.

Hence
\mu=\lambda_{0}
.
∎

As an application of

Proposition 4.4

For each

s_{2n,N}=\frac{(N-2)!!}{(N+2n-2)!!}=\frac{1}{(N+2n-2)(N+2n-4)\cdots N},

(4.12)

where successive terms in the denominator drop by

Proof.

Use Proposition
4.3

where

are non-negative even integers with sum

\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}^{2}

\begin{split}\lambda_{0}(({X\cdot X})p)&=((a_{1}+1)!!(a_{2}-1)!!\cdots(a_{N}-1)!!+\cdots\\
&\qquad\qquad+(a_{1}-1)!!\cdots(a_{N-1}-1)!!(a_{N}+1)!!)s_{2n+2,N}\\

Division by

is okay since it is nonzero by the formula for it at the end of the proof of Proposition

Multiplying both sides by
\frac{n!}{(a_{1}/2)!\cdots(a_{N}/2)!}

Recall from the end of the proof of Proposition

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b_{1},\ldots,b_{N}\geq 0\\

For example, when

Proposition 4.5

Suppose

Proof.

We may assume first that

\lambda_{0}(X_{1}p)=(2b_{1}-1)!!\cdots(2b_{N}-1)!!s_{2n,N},

4.2.
Spherical mean and the Laplacian

Using Proposition

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
R
be a commutative ring containing
{\mathbb{Q}}
.
The normalized spherical mean
\lambda_{0}
on
R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
can be described on
homogeneous polynomials
p
as follows. If
p
has odd degree then
\lambda_{0}(p)=0
.
For a constant
r
,
\lambda_{0}(r)=r
.
If
p
has even degree
2n\geq 2
then

\lambda_{0}(p)=\frac{1}{n!2^{n}(2n+N-2)(2n+N-4)\cdots(2n+N-2n)}\Delta^{n}p.

(4.23)

On the right side of (
4.23
),
\Delta^{n}p
is in
R
since it has degree
0
.

By Remark
4.2
, formula (
4.23
) holds for a general spherical mean
\lambda
if a scaling term, depending only on the degree of
p
, is inserted on the right hand side.

Proof.

If
p
has odd degree then every monomial in
p
has an odd exponent,
so
\lambda_{0}(p)=0
. If
p=r
is constant in
R
, then
\lambda_{0}(r)=r\lambda_{0}(1)=r
.

If
p
has even degree

, then apply
\lambda_{0}
to the identity (
2.1
) on

. We get

\lambda_{0}\bigl(({X\cdot X})\Delta p\bigr)=d(d+N-2)\lambda_{0}(p)

(4.24)

since
\lambda_{0}\circ M_{jk}=0
for all

.
Therefore by Proposition
4.3
,

\lambda_{0}(p)=\frac{1}{d(d+N-2)}\lambda_{0}\left(\Delta p\right).

(4.25)

Writing
d=2n\geq 2
, apply (
4.25
)
n
times to obtain (
4.23
).
∎

4.3.
The mean-value property and harmonic polynomials

In classical analysis it is known that the average value of a harmonic function over a sphere is the value of the function at the center of the sphere. In this subsection we establish purely algebraic results concerning this mean value property and harmonic polynomials. The first result is a direct corollary of Proposition
4.6
.

Corollary 4.8
.

If
p
is a harmonic polynomial in
R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]

then the normalized spherical mean of
p
is the value of
p
at
0
:

\lambda_{0}(p)=p(0).

(4.27)

Proof.

Both sides of (
4.27
) are linear in
p
, so we can assume
p
is homogeneous.
If
p
is constant then (
4.27
) is true by linearity of
\lambda_{0}
and the condition
\lambda_{0}(1)=1
.
If
p
is not constant, then by (
4.25
) we get
\lambda_{0}(p)=0
(the derivation of (
4.25
)
works for all nonconstant homogeneous
p
).
∎

For
t=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{N})
, any
N
-tuple of indeterminates such that

(t,X)=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{N},X_{1},\ldots,X_{N})

is a
(2N)
-tuple of algebraically independent indeterminates over
R
, we have the normalized spherical mean

\lambda_{0}:R[t,X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]\to R[t],

(4.28)

simply by using the ring
R[t]=R[t_{1},\ldots,t_{N}]
in place of
R
. Now consider a harmonic polynomial
p(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{N})\in R[Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{N}]
; then the polynomial

p(X_{1}+t_{1},\ldots,X_{N}+t_{N})\in R[t][X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]

is harmonic as a polynomial in
X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}
with coefficients in
R[t]
. Hence

\lambda_{0}\bigl(p(X+t)\bigr)=p(t),

(4.29)

by Corollary
4.8
.

The following result is the converse to the observation in (
4.29
).

Proposition 4.9
.

Let
R
be a commutative ring containing
{\mathbb{Q}}
, and suppose
p(Y)\in R[Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{N}]
, where
Y=(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{N})
, satisfies the mean-value property:

\lambda_{0}\bigl(p(X+t)\bigr)=p(t)

(4.30)

where
X=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{N})
and
t=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{N})
are
N
-tuples of indeterminates. Then
p(X)
is harmonic.

Proof.

Let us first note that the result holds for
p
of degree
\leq 1
because all such polynomials are harmonic. Next, suppose
p
is of degree
2
. Then, writing

p(Y)=\sum_{m,n=1}^{N}a_{mn}Y_{m}Y_{n}+\sum_{m=1}^{N}a_{m}Y_{m}+a_{0},

we have

\begin{split}\lambda_{0}\bigl(p(X+t)\bigr)&=\lambda_{0}\left(\sum_{m,n=1}^{N}a_{mn}X_{m}X_{n}+\sum_{m=1}^{N}a_{m}X_{m}\right)\\
&\qquad+\sum_{m,n=1}^{N}a_{mn}\bigl(t_{n}\lambda_{0}(X_{m})+t_{m}\lambda_{0}(X_{n})\bigr)+p(t)\\
&=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{m=1}^{N}a_{mm}+0+0+p(t),\end{split}

(4.31)

and so

\sum_{n=1}^{N}a_{nn}=0,

which implies that
p(Y)
is harmonic, because
\Delta p(Y)=2\sum_{n=1}^{N}a_{nn}
.

We assume then that
p
has degree
>2
. Let
s=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{N})
be another
N
-tuple of indeterminates. Then from (
4.30
) we have

\lambda_{0}\bigl(p(X+s+t)\bigr)=p(s+t).

(4.32)

Expanding both sides in powers of the indeterminates
t_{j}
, and comparing coefficients of
t^{\underline{j}}=t_{1}^{j_{1}}\cdots t_{N}^{j_{N}}
, where
\underline{j}=(j_{1},\ldots,j_{N})\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{N}
, we have

\lambda_{0}\bigl(\partial^{{\underline{j}}}p(X+s)\bigr)=\partial^{{\underline{j}}}p(s).

(4.33)

We can focus just on those
\underline{j}
that have
|{\underline{j}}|=\sum_{k=1}^{N}j_{k}
equal to
1
(that is, we focus on the first partial derivatives of
p
). Then
\partial^{{\underline{j}}}p(Y)
is a polynomial of degree
1
less than that of
p
, and so the condition (
4.33
) implies, by the induction hypothesis, that
\partial^{{\underline{j}}}p(Y)
is harmonic. Hence

\partial^{{\underline{j}}}\bigl(\Delta p(Y)\bigr)=\Delta\partial^{{\underline{j}}}p(Y)=0.

(4.34)

Thus
\Delta p(Y)
is constant, an element of
R
. Writing
p(Y)
as

p(Y)=p_{0}+p_{1}(Y)+\cdots+p_{d}(Y),

where each
p_{k}(Y)
is homogeneous of degree
k
, we observe that

\Delta p(Y)=\Delta p_{2}(Y)+\sum_{k=3}^{d}\Delta p_{k}(Y),

where
\Delta p_{k}(Y)
is homogeneous of degree
k-2
. Since
\Delta p(Y)
is constant, an element of
R
, it then follows that
p_{k}(Y)
is harmonic for
k\geq 3
.

The polynomial

q(Y)=p_{0}+p_{1}(Y)+p_{2}(Y)=p(Y)-\Sigma_{k=3}^{d}p_{k}(Y),

(4.35)

satisfies the mean-value property (
4.30
) because it is
the difference of two polynomials that satisfy (
4.30
).
Since it is of degree
\leq 2
,
q(Y)
must then be harmonic. Hence
p(Y)
, being the sum of
q(Y)
and
\Sigma_{k=3}^{d}p_{k}(Y)
is also harmonic.
∎

4.4.
Matrices acting on polynomials

Let
{\rm Matr}_{N}(R)
be the
R
-algebra of all
N\times N
matrices with entries in the commutative ring
R
. For an
N
-tuple of indeterminates
X=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{N})
and any
A\in{\rm Matr}_{N}(R)
we denote by
XA
the
N
-tuple whose
k
-th component is

(XA)_{k}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}X_{m}A_{mk}.

(4.36)

This determines a natural action of
{\rm Matr}_{N}(R)
on
R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
:

A[p(X)]=p(XA),

(4.37)

for all
A\in{\rm Matr}_{N}(R)
and
p(X)\in R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
.
We note that this is indeed a left action:

(AB)[p(X)]=p(XAB)=A[Bp(X)],

(4.38)

for any
A,B\in{\rm Matr}_{N}(R)
. Of interest to us are the orthogonal matrices:

\operatorname{O}_{N}(R)=\{A\in{\rm Matr}_{N}(R)\,:\,{A^{\top}}A=I\}.

(4.39)

It is a fact that in any commutative ring
R
, the condition
{A^{\top}}A=I
implies that
A
is invertible and hence
A^{\top}=A^{-1}
is both a left and a right inverse for
A
.

4.5.
Rotation-invariance of spherical means

We work, as before, with a commutative ring
R
that contains
{\mathbb{Q}}
. A spherical mean
\lambda
on
R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
is an algebraic counterpart to integration over the unit
(N-1)
-sphere centered at the origin in
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
with respect to the uniform measure. Since that measure on the
(N-1)
-sphere is invariant under rotations of
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
around the origin, integration over that sphere is invariant under an orthogonal change of variables. We want to demonstrate an algebraic analogue:
\lambda_{0}(p(XA))=\lambda_{0}(p(X))
for all
p
in
R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
and

A
in
\operatorname{O}_{N}(R)=\{A\in{\rm Matr}_{N}(R):A^{\top}A=I_{N}\}
.
This is Proposition
4.12
below and
will be proved in two ways.
The first proof is based on the commutativity of
orthogonal matrices and the Laplacian, and the second proof
is based on a formula for the spherical mean of a product of homogeneous linear polynomials in terms of spherical means of product of two homogeneous linear polynomials at a time.

Lemma 4.10
.

For
A\in\operatorname{O}_{N}(R)
,

\Delta^{m}(p(XA))=(\Delta^{m}p)(XA)
for all

p
in
R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
and
m\geq 0
.

Proof.

The case
m=0
is obvious, and after we establish
m=1

the cases
m\geq 2
follow by induction.

The case
m=1
when
R={\mathbb{R}}
is a special case of
the classical
fact
[
harmfunctionbook
]
*p. 3 that the Laplacian
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
commutes with an orthogonal change of variables.
The proof carries over to
R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
. By the chain rule,

\partial_{k}(p(XA))=\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\partial_{i}p)(XA)a_{ki}

(4.40)

for all
p
, where
A=(a_{ij})
. Therefore

\displaystyle\Delta(p(XA))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}\partial_{k}(\partial_{k}(p(XA)))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}\partial_{k}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{ki}(\partial_{i}p)(XA)\right)\text{ by }(\ref{chain})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,k}a_{ki}\sum_{j=1}^{N}a_{kj}(\partial_{j}(\partial_{i}p))(XA)\text{ by }(\ref{chain})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}\left(\sum_{k}a_{ki}a_{kj}\right)(\partial_{j}(\partial_{i}p))(XA)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i}(\partial_{i}^{2}p)(XA)\text{ since }A\in\operatorname{O}_{N}(R)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\Delta p)(XA).

∎

Lemma 4.11
.

Let
p_{1},\ldots,p_{2n}
be polynomials in
X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}
that are homogeneous and linear. Then

\lambda_{0}(p_{1}\cdots p_{2n})=N^{n}s_{2n,N}\sum_{\pi\in P_{2n}}\prod_{\{a,b\}\in\pi}\lambda_{0}(p_{a}p_{b}),

(4.41)

where
P_{2n}
is the set of partitions of
\{1,\ldots,2n\}
into two-element subsets.

This result is the spherical counterpart of a feature of the standard Gaussian measure on
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
(for the relationship with Gaussian measure see
[
PolyHigh2018
]
*Theorem 2.1).

By Remark
4.2
, formula (
4.41
) holds for a general spherical mean
\lambda
if the right hand side is multiplied by a scaling term in
R
that depends only on
n
.

Proof.

Since both sides of (
4.41
) are multilinear in
(p_{1},\ldots,p_{2n})
, checking the identity is reduced to checking it when each
p_{j}
is one of
X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}
.

If some

Now suppose

Using each of the previous two lemmas we will prove the rotation-invariance of

Proposition 4.12
.

For a commutative ring
R
containing
{\mathbb{Q}}
and every
N\times N
matrix

with entries in the ring

that is orthogonal, in the sense that

,
\lambda_{0}\bigl(p(XA)\bigr)=\lambda_{0}\bigl(p(X)\bigr)
for all

.

In view of Remark

, this result holds for any spherical mean

.

Proof.

Both sides of the desired formula

are linear in

is a monomial.

If
p
is a monomial of odd degree then
\lambda_{0}(p(X))=0
by Proposition
•
‣
4.1
. Since

is a linear combination of odd-degree monomials, also
\lambda_{0}(p(XA))=0
, so the proposition is established in this case.

Now suppose

is a monomial of even degree.

Method 1
. By Proposition
4.6
, when

p
is homogeneous of even degree
2n
,

\lambda_{0}(p(X))
is a multiple of

\Delta^{n}p\in R
, where the the multiplier
is determined by

and
n
. Since
p(XA)

is homogeneous of the same degree as
p(X)
,
\lambda_{0}(p(XA))
is the same scalar multiple of

\Delta^{n}(p(XA))
, so it suffices
to show
\Delta^{n}(p(X))=\Delta^{n}(p(XA))
. By
Lemma

\Delta^{n}(p(XA))
equals

, which is

since
\Delta^{n}p\in R
.

Method 2
. In view of the pair-product formula (

) in Lemma

, it suffices to assume that the polynomial

is a product of two homogeneous linear factors. Thus, without loss of generality, we may assume that

, for some

that are not necessarily distinct. In this case, we have

\begin{split}\lambda_{0}\bigl((XA)_{j}(XA)_{k}\bigr)&=\sum_{\ell,m=1}^{N}A_{\ell j}A_{mk}\lambda_{0}(X_{\ell}X_{m})\\
&=\sum_{\ell,m=1}^{N}A_{\ell j}A_{mk}\frac{\delta_{\ell m}}{N}\\
&=\sum_{m=1}^{N}A_{mj}A_{mk}\frac{1}{N}\\
&=\frac{1}{N}\delta_{jk}\\
&=\lambda_{0}(X_{j}X_{k}).\end{split}

(4.42)

Thus

∎

5.
Spherical harmonics

In this section
R
is a ring such that positive integer multiples of
1_{R}
are not zero divisors (
e.g.
,
R\supset{\mathbb{Q}}
).
We follow some of the ideas in
[
PolyHigh2018
]
.
By a
spherical harmonic
we mean a homogeneous harmonic polynomial.

Denote by
{\mathcal{Z}}_{N}(c)
the ideal generated by
{{X\cdot X}}-c
:

{\mathcal{Z}}_{N}(c)=\hbox{ideal generated by ${{X\cdot X}}-c$ in $R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]$.}

(5.1)

Recall

{\mathcal{P}}_{N}=R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
R
be a commutative ring,
c\in R
not a zero-divisor, and
{\mathcal{Z}}_{N}(c)
the ideal in
{\mathcal{P}}_{N}=R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
generated by
{X\cdot X}-c
. Then the quotient map
{\mathcal{P}}_{N}\to{\mathcal{P}}_{N}/{\mathcal{Z}}_{N}(c)
is injective when restricted to homogeneous polynomials
of the same degree.

The analytic counterpart of this result is that a homogeneous function on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
of a specified degree is completely determined by its restriction to a sphere of nonzero radius centered at the origin.

Proof.

The difference of homogeneous polynomials
of the same degree is still homogeneous, so
it suffices to prove that
0
is the only homogeneous polynomial in
{\mathcal{Z}}_{N}(c)
.

Suppose
p\in{\mathcal{Z}}_{N}(c)
is homogeneous of degree
m
. Then

p=({X\cdot X}-c)q=({X\cdot X})q-cq,

(5.2)

for some
q\in R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
. If
q\neq 0
then, since
c
is not a zero-divisor, the two terms on the right side are of different degrees, contradicting the homogeneity of
p
. Thus
q=0
and hence
p=0
.
∎

5.1.
Commutation relations and
L_{c}
operators

The following result and its proof are from
[
PolyHigh2018
]
.

Proposition 5.2
.

The following commutation relations hold among operators on

{\mathcal{M}}_{{X\cdot X}}M_{jk}=M_{jk}{\mathcal{M}}_{{X\cdot X}},

(5.3)

where
{\mathcal{M}}_{{X\cdot X}}
is the operator that multiplies polynomials by

, and

\Delta_{N}M_{jk}=M_{jk}\Delta_{N},

(5.4)

so each

maps
{\mathcal{H}}_{N,d}
into itself, and

M_{jk}({{M\cdot M}})=({{M\cdot M}})M_{jk}.

(5.5)

Proof.

By (
2.12

M_{jk}(({X\cdot X})p)=({X\cdot X})M_{jk}p
.
Thus (
5.3
) holds.

The identity (
5.5

To prove (

), it suffices to check both sides are equal on homogeneous polynomials of a fixed degree.
Consider the expression for

{\mathcal{M}}_{{X\cdot X}}\Delta_{N}
in (

):

acts as a scalar on homogeneous polynomials of a fixed degree and therefore it commutes with the action of each

on those polynomials. Similarly,

commutes with each

.
Thus (
2.1
) tells us that

{\mathcal{M}}_{{X\cdot X}}\Delta_{N}
commutes with each

. Since

commutes with

by (

),
it follows that for each homogeneous polynomial

Dividing by

From (

),

is harmonic whenever

Proposition 5.3
.

Suppose
R
is a ring containing
{\mathbb{Q}}
, and
c\in R
. Let
{\mathcal{H}}_{N}=\ker\Delta_{N}
denote the submodule of harmonic polynomials in

. Then the inclusion map

induces an isomorphism of

-modules:

(5.6)

Moreover, there is a unique

-linear mapping

(5.7)

which restricts to the identity map on

.

Item (ii), combined with

(5.8)

Proof.

By Proposition

is a polynomial multiple of

. Next, if

and so

Then

Applying

Here each of the terms

Rewriting the right side of (

Since each

∎

5.2.
Zonal harmonics

By a
zonal harmonic
we mean a spherical harmonic that is congruent modulo
{\mathcal{Z}}_{N}(c)
to a polynomial of the form

q(t_{1}X_{1}+\cdots+t_{N}X_{N})
, with
q\in R[T]
and
t=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{N})\in R^{N}
. When
N=3
this agrees with the terminology in classical analysis. In higher dimensions there are other ways of defining this notion, but for our purposes we choose this more restrictive meaning.

In the following we use the notation

t\cdot X=t_{1}X_{1}+\cdots+t_{N}X_{N}\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad t^{2}=t_{1}^{2}+\cdots+t_{N}^{2},

(5.16)

so
t^{2}=t\cdot t
.

A case of interest is a spherical harmonic that coincides, modulo
{\mathcal{Z}}_{N}(c)
, with a polynomial in the variable
X_{1}
. These are spherical harmonics that have rotational symmetry around the axis
(1,0,\ldots,0)
. Let us verify this. We need to assume that
N\geq 3
. By Remark
2.4
, if
R
is a ring in which
positive integer multiples of
1_{R}
are not zero divisors,

N\geq 3
, and a polynomial
p\in R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
is annihilated by the operators
M_{jk}
for distinct
j,k\in\{2,\ldots,N\}
then

p=q_{*}(X_{2}^{2}+\cdots+X_{N}^{2}),

(5.17)

where
q_{*}
is a polynomial in one variable with coefficients in
R[X_{1}]
. Thus,

p\equiv q(X_{1},c-X_{1}^{2})\bmod{\mathcal{Z}}_{N}(c),

(5.18)

for some polynomial
q
in two indeterminates, with coefficients in
R
. Thus, for
N>2
, if
p
is a spherical harmonic that is annihilated by the operators
M_{jk}
for all
j,k\in\{2,\ldots,N\}
with

then
p
is a zonal harmonic, corresponding to

being
(1,0,\ldots,0)
.

Proposition 5.4
.

Suppose

is a ring containing
{\mathbb{Q}}
. Let

denote an arbitrary

Proof.

By focusing on the homogeneous components of

We write

where

is a homogeneous polynomial of degree

with coefficients in

. Thus the given condition is:

if

then

for all choices of homogeneous polynomials

For any

This holds for all

where

.
Now if

for

∎

Remark 5.5

If

n
terms on both sides, we have

q_{0}(t\cdot X)^{n}={{X\cdot X}}r_{n-2},

(5.30)

where
r_{n-2}
is the (necessarily nonzero) term of degree
n-2
within
r
and the right side of (
5.30
) is nonzero.
Then we have a contradiction from Lemma
5.8
(proved below).
∎

Lemma 5.8
.

Let
R
be an integral domain of characteristic
0
, and
t=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{N})\in R^{N}
, with at least one nonzero component.
Let
p(Y)\in R[Y]
be such that
p(t_{1}X_{1}+\cdots+t_{N}X_{N})
is divisible by
X_{1}^{2}+\cdots+X_{N}^{2}
in the polynomial ring
R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
and
N\geq 2
. Then
p=0
.

Proof.

Any nonzero polynomial multiple of
Q=X_{1}^{2}+\cdots+X_{N}^{2}
is of degree at least
2
in each
X_{j}
. Suppose that

p(t_{1}X_{1}+\cdots+t_{N}X_{N})=Qf,

(5.31)

where
f\in R[X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]
. Evaluating at
X_{j}=0
for all
j>2
, we can focus on the case of just two indeterminates

p(t_{1}X_{1}+t_{2}X_{2})=(X_{1}^{2}+X_{2}^{2})f(X_{1},X_{2}).

(5.32)

Let
n=\deg p(Y)
and
p_{0}Y^{n}
be the degree-
n
term in
p(Y)
;
so
p_{0}\in R
is non-zero and
n\geq 0
.

We look at the degree-
n
terms on both sides of (
5.32
).
On the left side of (
5.32
) we get
p_{0}(t_{1}X_{1}+t_{2}X_{2})^{n}
.

\begin{split}&(t_{1}X_{1}+t_{2}X_{2})^{n}\\
&=(t_{2}X_{2})^{n}+n(t_{2}X_{2})^{n-1}t_{1}X_{1}+\sum_{j=1}^{[n/2]}\binom{n}{2j}t_{1}^{2j}(X_{1}^{2})^{j}(t_{2}X_{2})^{n-2j}\\
&\qquad+X_{1}\sum_{j=1}^{[(n-1)/2]}\binom{n}{2j+1}t_{1}^{2j+1}(X_{1}^{2})^{j}(t_{2}X_{2})^{n-(2j+1)}.\end{split}

(5.33)

Upon writing
X_{1}^{2}
as
Q-X_{2}^{2}
, we have

\begin{split}&(t_{1}X_{1}+t_{2}X_{2})^{n}\\
&=(t_{2}X_{2})^{n}+n(t_{2}X_{2})^{n-1}t_{1}X_{1}+\sum_{j=1}^{[n/2]}\binom{n}{2j}t_{1}^{2j}(Q-X_{2}^{2})^{j}(t_{2}X_{2})^{n-2j}\\
&\qquad+X_{1}\sum_{j=1}^{[(n-1)/2]}\binom{n}{2j+1}t_{1}^{2j+1}(Q-X_{2}^{2})^{j}(t_{2}X_{2})^{n-(2j+1)}\\
&=aX_{1}+b+cQ,\end{split}

(5.34)

where
a,b\in R[X_{2}]
and
c\in R[X_{1},X_{2}]
; specifically,

\begin{split}a&=\sum_{j=0}^{[(n-1)/2]}\binom{n}{2j+1}(-1)^{j}t_{1}^{2j+1}t_{2}^{n-(2j+1)}X_{2}^{n-1}\\
b&=\sum_{j=0}^{[n/2]}\binom{n}{2j}(-1)^{j}t_{1}^{2j}t_{2}^{n-2j}X_{2}^{n}.\end{split}

(5.35)

Hence,

(t_{1}X_{1}+t_{2}X_{2})^{n}p_{0}=p_{0}aX_{1}+p_{0}b+cp_{0}Q.

(5.36)

Since the left side is a multiple of
Q
as in (
5.32
), we have

(f-p_{0}c)Q=p_{0}aX_{1}+p_{0}b.

(5.37)

The left side is either
0
or of degree
\geq 2
in
X_{1}
, whereas the right side is either
0
or of degree
1
in
X_{1}
. Thus both sides must be
0
. But neither
a
nor
b
is
0
, and so
p_{0}=0
, a contradiction.
∎

The solutions to equation (
5.24
) are traditionally understood in terms of Gegenbauer polynomials. We conclude with the following observation.

Proposition 5.9
.

Suppose
R
is an integral domain containing
{\mathbb{Q}}
,
\alpha\in R
, and
q(Y)\in R[Y]
a nonzero polynomial that satisfies the differential equation

[\alpha-Y^{2}]q^{\prime\prime}(Y)-(N-1)Yq^{\prime}(Y)+n(n+N-2)q(Y)=0,

(5.38)

where
n\geq 0
and
N\geq 3
are integers.
Then the degree of
q
is
n
. If
\alpha=0
then
q(Y)=Y^{n}
is, up to multiplication by any element of
R
, the unique solution to (
5.38
). If
\alpha\neq 0
then a solution
q(Y)
, unique up to scaling by any element of
R
, to
(\ref{E:dtdifc2})
exists, with coefficients in
R[\alpha^{-1}]
, and is an even polynomial if
n
is even and an odd polynomial of
n
is odd.

Proof.

Substituting

q(Y)=\sum_{k=0}^{m}q_{k}Y^{k}

into (
5.38
), and examining the coefficient of
Y^{k}
, (
5.38
) is equivalent to

\begin{split}{\alpha}q_{k+2}&=\frac{k(k-1)+k(N-1)-n(n+N-2)}{(k+2)(k+1)}q_{k}\\
&=\frac{(k-n)(k+n+N-2)}{(k+2)(k+1)}q_{k},\end{split}

(5.39)

holding for all
k\in\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
. The condition
N\geq 3
implies that
k+n+N-2\neq 0
since
k
and
n
are non-negative integers.

If
\alpha=0
then
q_{k}=0
unless
k=n
. Now suppose
\alpha\neq 0
. If
n
is even and
q_{1}
were nonzero then (
5.39
) would imply that
q_{k}
is nonzero for all odd
k
; this would contradict the fact that
q(Y)
is a polynomial. Thus
q(Y)
is an even polynomial. Moreover, (
5.39
) also implies that
q_{m}=0
for all even
m>n
. A similar argument works for
n
odd.
∎
