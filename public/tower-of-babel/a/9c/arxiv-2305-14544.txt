Hausdorff dimension of unions of k k -planes
Shengwen Gan

Abstract
. We prove a conjecture of Héra on the dimension of unions of k k -planes. Let 0 < k ≤ d < n 0<k\leq d<n be integers, and β ∈ [ 0 , k + 1 ) \beta\in[0,k+1) . If 𝒱 ⊂ A ⁡ ( k , n ) \mathcal{V}\subset A(k,n) , with dim ​ ( 𝒱 ) = ( k + 1 ) ​ ( d − k ) + β \textup{dim}(\mathcal{V})=(k+1)(d-k)+\beta , then dim ​ ( ⋃ V ∈ 𝒱 V ) ≥ d + min ⁡ { 1 , β } \textup{dim}(\bigcup_{V\in\mathcal{V}}V)\geq d+\min\{1,\beta\} . The proof combines a recent idea of Zahl and the Brascamp-Lieb inequality.

1.
Introduction

In
[
3
]
, Héra made the following conjecture (see Conjecture 1.16 in
[
3
]
). We slightly change the notation and state it in the following equivalent way.

Conjecture 1
.

Let
0<k\leq d<n
be integers and
\beta\in[0,k+1]
. Let
\mathcal{V}\subset A(k,n)
be a set of
k
-planes in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, with
\dim(\mathcal{V})=(k+1)(d-k)+\beta
. Here,
A(k,n)
denotes the affine Grassmannian in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\dim(\mathcal{V})
means the Hausdorff dimension of
\mathcal{V}
as a subset of the Riemannian manifold
A(k,n)
. Then,

(1)

\dim(\bigcup_{V\in\mathcal{V}}V)\geq d+\min\{1,\beta\}.

There are many works related to Conjecture
1
. See for example
[
3
]
,
[
4
]
,
[
6
]
,
[
7
]
and
[
8
]
. We refer to
[
8
]
for a detailed introduction on this problem.

1.1.
Sharp examples

We first give some examples to show (
1
) is sharp, i.e., we will find
\mathcal{V}\subset A(k,n)
with
\dim(\mathcal{V})=(k+1)(d-k)+\beta
, so that

\dim(\bigcup_{V\in\mathcal{V}}V)\leq d+\min\{1,\beta\}.

Our examples
V\in\mathcal{V}
will lie in
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}(\subset\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

•

When
\beta\in[1,k+1]
, let
\mathcal{V}
be a
((k+1)(d-k)+\beta)
-dimensional subset of
A(k,d+1)
. This is allowable since
\dim(A(k,d+1))=(k+1)(d+1-k)\geq(k+1)(d-k)+\beta
. Since every
V\in\mathcal{V}
is contained in
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
, we have
\dim(\bigcup_{V\in\mathcal{V}}V)\leq\dim(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})=d+1
.

•

When
\beta\in[0,1]
, we choose
I\subset\mathbb{R}
to be a
\beta
-dimensional set. Let

\mathcal{V}=\cup_{a\in I}A(k,\mathbb{R}^{d}+(0,\dots,0,a)).

Here,
A(k,\mathbb{R}^{d}+(0,\dots,0,a))
is the set of
k
-planes that are contained in the space
\mathbb{R}^{d}+(0,\dots,0,a)
. We see that
\dim(\mathcal{V})=\dim I+\dim A(k,d)=(k+1)(d-k)+\beta
, and
\dim(\bigcup_{V\in\mathcal{V}}V)=\dim(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times I)=d+\beta
.

1.2.
Notation

We introduce some notation that is needed for the later discussions.

Let
G(k,n)
be the set of
k
-dimensional subspaces in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For every
k
-plane
V
, we can uniquely write it as

V=\textup{dir}(V)+x_{V},

where
\textup{dir}(V)\in G(k,n)
and
x_{V}\in V^{\perp}
.
\textup{dir}(V)
refers to the direction of
V
, as can be seen that
\textup{dir}(V)=\textup{dir}(V^{\prime})\Leftrightarrow V\parallel V^{\prime}
.

In this paper, we use
A(k,n)
to denote the set of
k
-planes
V
such that
x_{V}\in B^{n}(0,1/2)
. (
B^{n}(0,1/2)
is the ball of radius
1/2
centered at the origin in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.)

A(k,n)=\{V:V\textup{~is~a~}k\textup{~dimensional~plane~},x_{V}\in B^{n}(0,1/2)\}.

Usually
A(k,n)
denotes all the
k
-planes in other references, but for our purpose we only care about those
V
that lie near the origin.

Next, we discuss the metrics on
G(k,n)
and
A(k,n)
.
For
V_{1},V_{2}\in G(k,n)
, we define

d(V_{1},V_{2})=\|\pi_{V_{1}}-\pi_{V_{2}}\|.

Here,
\pi_{V_{1}}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow V_{1}
is the orthogonal projection. We have another characterization for this metric. Define
\rho(V_{1},V_{2})
to be the smallest number
\rho
such that
B^{n}(0,1)\cap V_{1}\subset N_{\rho}(V_{2})
. We have the comparability of
d(\cdot,\cdot)
and
\rho(\cdot,\cdot)
.

Lemma 1
.

There exists a constant
C>0
(depending on
k,n
) such that

\rho(V_{1},V_{2})\leq d(V_{1},V_{2})\leq C\rho(V_{1},V_{2}).

Proof.

Suppose
B^{n}(0,1)\cap V_{1}\subset N_{\rho}(V_{2})
, then for any
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have

|\pi_{V_{1}}(v)-\pi_{V_{2}}(v)|\lesssim\rho|v|,

which implies
d(V_{1},V_{2})\lesssim\rho
. On the other hand, if for any
|v|\leq 1
we have

|\pi_{V_{1}}(v)-\pi_{V_{2}}(v)|\leq d|v|,

then we obtain that
\pi_{V_{1}}(v)\subset N_{d}(V_{2})
. Letting
v
ranging over
B^{n}(0,1)\cap V_{1}
, we get
B^{n}(0,1)\cap V_{1}\subset N_{d}(V_{2})
, which means
\rho(V_{1},V_{2})\leq d
.
∎

We can also define the metric on
A(k,n)
given by

(2)

d(V,V^{\prime})=d(\textup{dir}(V),\textup{dir}(V^{\prime}))+|x_{V}-x_{V^{\prime}}|.

Here, we still use
d
to denote the metric on
A(k,n)
and it will not make any confusion.

Similarly, for
V,V^{\prime}\in A(k,n)
we can define
\rho(V,V^{\prime})
to be the smallest number
\rho
such that
B^{n}(0,1)\cap V\subset N_{\rho}(V^{\prime})
. We also have the following lemma. We left the proof to the interested readers.

Lemma 2
.

There exists a constant
C>0
(depending on
k,n
) such that for
V,V^{\prime}\in A(k,n)
,

C^{-1}d(V,V^{\prime})\leq\rho(V,V^{\prime})\leq Cd(V,V^{\prime}).

Definition 3
.

For
V\in A(k,n)
and
0<r<1
, we define

V_{r}:=N_{r}(V)\cap B^{n}(0,1).

We see that
V_{r}
is morally a slab of dimensions
\underbrace{r\times\dots\times r}_{k\textup{~times}}\times\underbrace{1\times\dots\times 1}_{n-k\textup{~times}}
. We usually call
V_{r}
a
k
-dimensional
r
-slab. When
k
is already clear, we simply call
V_{r}
an
r
-slab.
If
W
is a convex set such that
C^{-1}W\subset V_{r}\subset CW
, then we also call
W
an
r
-slab. Here, the constant
C
will be a fixed large constant.

Definition 4
.

For two
r
-slab
V_{r}
and
V^{\prime}_{r}
. We say they are comparable if
C^{-1}V_{r}\subset V^{\prime}_{r}\subset CV_{r}
. We say they are essentially distinct if they are not comparable.

In this paper, we will also consider the balls and
\delta
-neighborhood on
A(k,n)
. Recall that we use
B_{r}(x)
to denote the ball in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
of radius
r
centered at
x
. To distinguish the ambient space, we will use letter
Q
to denote the balls in
A(k,n)
. For
V\in A(k,n)
, we use
Q_{r}(V)
to denote the ball in
A(k,n)
of radius
r
centered at
V
. More precisely,

Q_{r}(V_{0}):=\{V\in A(k,n):d(V,V_{0})\leq r\}.

For a subset
X\subset A(k,n)
, we use the fancy letter
\mathcal{N}
to denote the neighborhood in
A(k,n)
:

\mathcal{N}_{r}(X):=\{V\in A(k,n):d(V,X)\leq r\}.

Here,
d(V,X)=\inf_{V^{\prime}\in X}d(V,V^{\prime})
.

Next, we briefly recall how to define the Hausdorff dimension for subsets of a metric space. Let
(M,d)
be a metric space. For
X\subset M
, we denote the
s
-dimensional Hausdorff measure of
X
under the metric
d
to be
\mathcal{H}^{s}(X;d)
.
We see that if
d^{\prime}
is another metric on
M
such that
d(\cdot,\cdot)\sim d^{\prime}(\cdot,\cdot)
, then
\mathcal{H}^{s}(X;d)\sim\mathcal{H}^{s}(X;d^{\prime})
. It make sense to define the Hausdorff dimension of
X
which is independent of the choice of comparable metrics:

\dim X:=\sup\{s:\mathcal{H}^{s}(X;d)>0\}.

Throughout the paper we will work with the metric space
M=A(k,n)
and the metric
d
defined as in (
2
). For any
X\subset A(k,n)
, we will simply denote the
s
-dimensional Hausdorff measure of
X
under the metric
d
to be
\mathcal{H}^{s}(X)
. Similarly, we use
\mathcal{H}^{s}_{\infty}(X)
to denote the Hausdorff content.

1.3.
Strategy of proof

Our proof will follow the idea of Zahl
[
8
]
. The main goal is to prove a Kakeya maximal estimate for
k
-planes, and then as a corollary we can prove Conjecture
1
. To handle with the
k
-planes rather than lines, we need to use the Brascamp-Lieb inequality, which can be viewed as a generalized version of the
k
-linear Kakeya estimate. We state our Kakeya maximal estimate for
k
-planes. This can be viewed as a counterpart of Theorem 1.2 in
[
8
]
.

Theorem 5
.

Let
0<k\leq d<n
be integers and
\beta\in[0,1]
. Let
\mathcal{V}=\{V\}
be a subset of
A(k,n)
. Suppose that for all balls
Q_{r}\subset A(k,n)
of radius
r\in[\delta,1]
, we have

(3)

\#\{V\in\mathcal{V}:V\in Q_{r}\}\leq(r/\delta)^{(k+1)(d-k)+\beta}.

Then

(4)

\|\sum_{V\in\mathcal{V}}1_{V_{\delta}}\|_{L^{p}(B^{n}(0,1))}\leq C_{\varepsilon}\delta^{-\frac{k(d-k)}{p^{\prime}}-\varepsilon}\big(\sum_{V\in\mathcal{V}}|V_{\delta}|\big)^{1/p}.

Here,

p=\min\left\{\frac{d-k+\beta+1}{d-k+\beta},\frac{d+\beta}{d+\beta-(d-k+2)^{-1}},d-k+2\right\}

is a number strictly bigger than
1
.

Remark 6
.

For our range of
p
, the factor
\delta^{-\frac{k(d-k)}{p^{\prime}}}
(up to the
\varepsilon
loss) on the right hand side of (
4
) is sharp. Otherwise, it would give a better lower bound in (
1
) which is impossible. The author is able to prove a sharp estimate for
p
close to
1
. An interesting question would be to find the sharp factor
\delta^{-C_{p}}
for all
p\geq 1
.

The main idea in the proof of Theorem
5
is the Broad-Narrow method, which dates back to
[
1
]
. Let us look at the left hand side of (
4
). We first partition the integration domain
B^{n}(0,1)
into
\delta
-balls:
B^{n}(0,1)=\bigcup B_{\delta}
. For each such
\delta
-ball
B_{\delta}
, consider all the
V_{\delta}
that intersect
B_{\delta}
. We can morally view these
V_{\delta}
as a subset of
G(k,n)
. Here is the dichotomy: If there are not too “many”
V_{\delta}
intersecting
B_{\delta}
, then we say we are in the narrow case and it can be dealt with by the induction. If there are “many”
V_{\delta}
intersecting
B_{\delta}
, then most of these
V_{\delta}
are transverse to each other and we say we are in the broad case. The broad case is dealt with by the Brascamp-Lieb inequality. We will quantify the “many” in the next section.

In Section
2
, we prove theorem
5
. In Section
3
, we prove that Theorem
5
implies Conjecture
1
.

2.
Proof of Theorem
5

In this section we will use the Broad-Narrow method developed by Bourgain and Guth
[
1
]
. To estimate the left hand side of (
4
), we will decompose the integral into broad part and narrow part. The broad part will be handled by the Brascamp-Lied inequality, and the narrow part will be handled by the induction.

2.1.
Brascamp-Lieb inequality

The version of Brascamp-Lieb inequality we are going to use is due to Maldague. See Theorem 2 in
[
5
]
.

Theorem 7

(Maldague)
.

Consider in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Let
W_{j}\subset G(n-k,n)
for
j=1,\dots,J
. Fix
p\in[1,J]
. Define

(5)

\textup{BL}(\{W_{j}\}_{j=1}^{J},p):=\sup_{L\leq\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\dim L-\frac{p}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\dim\pi_{W_{j}}(L)).

Here,
L\leq\mathbb{R}^{n}
means
L
is a subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
\pi_{W_{j}}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow W_{j}
is the orthogonal projection.

There exists
\nu>0
depending on
\{W_{j}\}_{j=1}^{J}
, so that the following is true. For any
\mathcal{V}_{j}=\{V_{j}\}\subset A(k,n)\ (j=1,\dots,J)
being sets of
k
-planes, such that each
V_{j}\in\mathcal{V}_{j}
has its
k
longest directions orthogonal to some
W\in Q_{\nu}(W_{j})
, we have

(6)

\int_{[-1,1]^{n}}\prod_{j=1}^{J}\bigg(\sum_{V_{j}\in\mathcal{V}_{j}}1_{V_{j,\delta}}\bigg)^{\frac{p}{J}}\lesssim\delta^{n-\varepsilon}\delta^{-\textup{BL}(\{W_{j}\}_{j=1}^{J},p)}\prod_{j=1}^{J}\bigg(\#\mathcal{V}_{j}\bigg)^{\frac{p}{J}}.

By a compactness argument, we deduce the following result.

Theorem 8
.

Consider in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Let
\mathcal{W}_{j}\subset G(n-k,n)
be a compact set for
j=1,\dots,J
. Fix
p\in[1,J]
.
Define

(7)

\textup{BL}(\{\mathcal{W}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{J},p):=\sup_{W_{1}\in\mathcal{W}_{1},\dots,W_{J}\in\mathcal{W}_{J}}\textup{BL}(\{W_{j}\}_{j=1}^{J},p).

Then for any
\mathcal{V}_{j}=\{V_{j}\}\subset A(k,n)\ (j=1,\dots,J)
being sets of
k
-planes, such that each
V_{j}\in\mathcal{V}_{j}
has its
k
longest directions orthogonal to some
W\in\mathcal{W}_{j}
, we have

(8)

\int_{[-1,1]^{n}}\prod_{j=1}^{J}\bigg(\sum_{V_{j}\in\mathcal{V}_{j}}1_{V_{j,\delta}}\bigg)^{\frac{p}{J}}\leq C(\{\mathcal{W}_{j}\},p,\varepsilon)\cdot\delta^{n-\varepsilon}\delta^{-\textup{BL}((\mathcal{W}_{j})_{j=1}^{J},p)}\prod_{j=1}^{J}\bigg(\#\mathcal{V}_{j}\bigg)^{\frac{p}{J}}.

2.2.
Broad-narrow reduction

We discuss the broad-narrow reduction in this subsection. There are some important submanifolds of
G(k,n)
which resemble the great
(d-1)
-sphere
S^{d-1}
in
S^{n-1}\simeq G(1,n)
. For any
d
-dimensional subspace
\Pi\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let
G(k,\Pi)
be the set of
k
-dimensional subspaces that are contained in
\Pi
. We see that
\dim G(k,\Pi)=k(d-k)
.

Choose
\mathcal{D}\subset G(k,n)
to be a maximal
\delta
-separated subset.
We first modify the directions of the
k
-planes in
\mathcal{V}
in Theorem
5
so that they have directions in
\mathcal{D}
. For each
V\in\mathcal{V}
, we can find another
V^{\prime}
so that
\textup{dir}(V^{\prime})\in\mathcal{D}
and
V_{\delta}\subset V^{\prime}_{10\delta}
.
We denote the new set of planes
\{V^{\prime}\}
obtained in this way by
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
, then

(9)

\|\sum_{V\in\mathcal{V}}1_{V_{\delta}}\|_{L^{p}(B^{n}(0,1))}^{p}\leq\|\sum_{V^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}^{\prime}}1_{V^{\prime}_{10\delta}}\|_{L^{p}(B^{n}(0,1))}^{p}.

We cover
B(0,1)
by finitely overlapping
\delta
-balls
\{B_{\delta}\}
.
We bound the right hand side of (
9
) as

\|\sum_{V\in\mathcal{V}^{\prime}}1_{V_{10\delta}}\|_{L^{p}(B^{n}(0,1))}^{p}\leq\sum_{B_{\delta}}\|\sum_{V\in\mathcal{V}^{\prime}}1_{V_{10\delta}}\|_{L^{p}(B_{\delta})}^{p}\lesssim\sum_{B_{\delta}}\#\{V\in\mathcal{V}^{\prime}:V_{10\delta}\cap B_{\delta}\neq\emptyset\}^{p}\delta^{n}.

Fix a
B_{\delta}
, and let

\mathcal{V}^{\prime}(B_{\delta}):=\{V\in\mathcal{V}^{\prime}:V_{10\delta}\cap B_{\delta}\neq\emptyset\}.

Our goal is to find an upper bound of
\#\mathcal{V}^{\prime}(B_{\delta})
.
Since the planes in
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}(B_{\delta})
are
\delta
-separated and intersect
B_{11\delta}
, for any direction in
\mathcal{D}
there are
\lesssim 1
planes in
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}(B_{\delta})
that are in that direction. We can choose a subset
\mathcal{V}(B_{\delta})\subset\mathcal{V}^{\prime}(B_{\delta})
, so that any two planes in
\mathcal{V}(B_{\delta})
have different directions and

\#\mathcal{V}(B_{\delta})\gtrsim\#\mathcal{V}^{\prime}(B_{\delta}).

Therefore, we bound the left hand side of (
9
) by

\lesssim\sum_{B_{\delta}}\#\mathcal{V}(B_{\delta})^{p}\delta^{n}.

Since the planes in
\mathcal{V}(B_{\delta})
morally pass through
B_{\delta}
and have
\delta
-separated directions, we can view
\mathcal{V}(B_{\delta})
as a
\delta
-separated subset of
G(k,n)
.

Fix a
B_{\delta}
, there is a dichotomy to estimate
\#\mathcal{V}(B_{\delta})
. We talk about the heuristic. The first scenario is when there exists a
d
-dimensional subspace
\Pi\in G(d,n)
so that most of the elements
V\in\mathcal{V}(B_{\delta})
lie close to
G(k,\Pi)
(if we view
V
as a point in
G(k,n)
), or equivalently most of the elements
V\in\mathcal{V}(B_{\delta})
lie in a small neighborhood of
\Pi(\subset\mathbb{R}^{n})
(if we view
V
as a
k
-plane in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
). The second scenario is that we can find several “quite transverse” patches
\mathcal{U}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{U}_{J}\subset G(k,n)
so that
\#(\mathcal{U}_{j}\cap\mathcal{V}(B_{\delta}))\gtrsim\#\mathcal{V}(B_{\delta})
for
j=1,\dots,J
. The transverse condition on the patches allows us to use Theorem
8
.
We call the first scenario narrow case, and the second scenario broad case.
We make our heuristic rigorous as follows.

Fix a large number
K\gg 1
which is to be determined later. We cover
G(k,n)
by
\mathcal{Q}_{K^{-n}}=\{Q_{K^{-n}}\}
which are balls of radius
K^{-n}
with bounded overlaps:

G(k,n)=\bigcup Q_{K^{-n}}.

Note that
\#\mathcal{Q}_{K^{-n}}\lesssim(K^{n})^{\dim G(k,n)}\leq K^{n^{3}}
. We define the significant balls

\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}=\{Q_{K^{-n}}:\#(Q_{K^{-n}}\cap\mathcal{V}(B_{\delta}))\geq\frac{1}{K^{n^{4}}}\#\mathcal{V}(B_{\delta})\}.

We have

\#\mathcal{V}(B_{\delta})\leq 2\sum_{Q_{K^{-n}}\in\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}}\#(Q_{K^{-n}}\cap\mathcal{V}(B_{\delta})).

Next by pigeonhole principle, there is a subset of
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
denoted by
\mathcal{Q}
, such that the balls in
\mathcal{Q}
are
100K^{-n}
-separated,
\#(Q_{K^{-n}}\cap\mathcal{V}(B_{\delta}))
are all comparable for
Q_{K^{-n}}\in\mathcal{Q}
, and

\#\mathcal{V}(B_{\delta})\lesssim\log K\sum_{Q_{K^{-n}}\in\mathcal{Q}}\#(Q_{K^{-n}}\cap\mathcal{V}(B_{\delta})).

There is the dichotomy:

The first scenario
: We say it is in the narrow case when there exists
\Pi\in G(d,n)
so that half of balls in
\mathcal{Q}
is contained in
\mathcal{N}_{CK^{-1}}(G(k,\Pi))
. Here, we view
G(k,\Pi)
as a subset of
G(k,n)
and
\mathcal{N}_{C_{1}K^{-1}}(G(k,\Pi))
is the
C_{1}K^{-1}
-neighborhood of
G(k,\Pi)
in
G(k,n)
.
C_{1}
is a large constant to be determined later.

The second scenario
: We say it is in the broad case when there exists

\mathcal{U}_{k},\dots,\mathcal{U}_{d+1}\in\mathcal{Q},

so that there does not exists a
\Pi\in G(d,n)
such that all of these
\mathcal{U}_{j}
intersect
\mathcal{N}_{100K^{-n}}(G(k,\Pi))
. In other words, for any
\Pi\in G(d,n)
, there exists a
\mathcal{U}_{j}
that does not intersect
\mathcal{N}_{100K^{-n}}(G(k,\Pi))
. We remark that the subscript of
\mathcal{U}_{j}
ranges from
k
to
d+1
but not from
1
to
d-k+2
, just to make the discussion below more convenient.

We want to show that if we are not in the first scenario, then we are in the second scenario.
We let
\mathcal{G}
be the set of centers of the balls in
\mathcal{Q}
, i.e.,

\mathcal{Q}=\{Q_{K^{-n}}(U):U\in\mathcal{G}\}.

We just need to show there exists
U_{k},\dots,U_{d+1}\in\mathcal{G}
so that there does not exists a
\Pi\in G(d,n)
such that all these
U_{j}
are contained in
\mathcal{N}_{200K^{-n}}(G(k,\Pi))
.

If
U_{k},\dots,U_{d+1}\in\mathcal{N}_{200K^{-n}}(G(k,\Pi))
, then by Lemma
2
,

(10)

U_{k},\dots,U_{d+1}\subset N_{CK^{-n}}(\Pi)

for some large constant
C
depending on
k,n
.
To show the existence of
U_{k},\dots,U_{d+1}
that do not satisfy (
10
), we will inductively find
U_{k},\dots,U_{d+1}
and vectors
v_{1},\dots,v_{d+1}
in
\bigcup_{j=k}^{d+1}V_{j}
such that
v_{1}\dots,v_{d+1}
cannot all lie in
N_{CK^{-n}}(\Pi)
.

We observe the following fact: If the unit vectors
v_{1},\dots,v_{d+1}
lie in
CK^{-n}
-neighborhood of a
d
-dimensional plane
\Pi
, then the volume of the parallelepiped spanned by
\{v_{1},\dots,v_{d+1}\}
has the upper bound:

\textup{Vol}_{d+1}(v_{1},\dots,v_{d+1})\lesssim K^{-n}.

The proof is straightforward. Let
V^{d+1}=\textup{span}\{v_{1},\dots,v_{d+1}\}
, then parallelepiped spanned by
\{v_{1},\dots,v_{d+1}\}
is contained in
N_{K^{-n}}(\Pi)\cap V^{d+1}\cap B^{n}(0,1)
. We get

(11)

\textup{Vol}_{d+1}(v_{1},\dots,v_{d+1})\leq\textup{Vol}_{d+1}\bigg(N_{K^{-n}}(\Pi)\cap B^{n}(0,1)\cap V^{d+1}\bigg).

Noting that

Lemma 10

Let

\displaystyle\|\sum_{V\in\mathcal{V}}1_{V_{\delta}}\|_{p}^{p}\lesssim C(\log K)^{p}K^{-\varepsilon p}C_{\varepsilon}\delta^{-k(d-k)(p-1)-\varepsilon p}\sum_{V\in\mathcal{V}}|V_{\delta}|

\displaystyle+C_{K,\varepsilon}\delta^{-\varepsilon^{2}-k(d-k)(p-1)}\sum_{V\in\mathcal{V}}|V_{\delta}|.

To close the induction, it suffices to make

(30)

C(\log K)^{p}K^{-\varepsilon p}\leq 1/2

and

(31)

C_{K,\varepsilon}\delta^{-\varepsilon^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{2}\delta^{-\varepsilon p}C_{\varepsilon}.

We first choose
K
sufficiently large so that (
30
) holds. Then we choose
C_{\varepsilon}
large enough so that (
31
) holds.

3.
Proof of Conjecture
1

It is shown in
[
8
, Section 3]
that Theorem
5
implies Conjecture
1
in the case
k=1
. Actually, for all the
k
, the implication is the same. Here, we give a sketch of proof on why Theorem
5
implies Conjecture
1
.
We skip the technical details on the measurability and focus on how the numerology works for general
k
.

A sketch of proof: Theorem
5
implies Conjecture
1
.

We just need to consider the case when
\beta\in(0,1]
. If
\beta>1
, we just replace
\beta
by
1
and throw away some
k
-planes in
\mathcal{V}
to make
\dim(\mathcal{V})=(k+1)(d-k)+1
.
If
\beta=0
, we just replace
\beta
by
1
and
d
by
d-1
. Next, note that it suffices to prove a variant of (
1
), where the right hand side is replaced by
d+\beta-\varepsilon
for arbitrary
\varepsilon>0
.

Fix such an
\varepsilon
. Let
X=\bigcup_{V\in\mathcal{V}}V
. We will first localize
X
inside
B^{n}(0,1)
.
Define

\mathcal{V}^{N}:=\{V\in\mathcal{V}:\textup{dist}(0,V)<N/2\}.

Note that
\mathcal{V}^{N}
(
N=1,2,\cdots
) is an ascending sequence that converges to
\mathcal{V}
. Therefore, there exists
N
such that

\dim(\mathcal{V}^{N})\geq\dim(\mathcal{V})-\varepsilon/2.

Note that after the rescaling
x\mapsto x/N
, the Hausdorff dimensions of
\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}^{N}
and
X
do not change.
We use
\mathcal{V}_{1}
to denote the
\mathcal{V}^{N}
after the rescaling. By abuse of notation, we still keep the notation for
\mathcal{V},X
after the rescaling. We have

\mathcal{V}_{1}=\{V\in\mathcal{V}:\textup{dist}(0,V)<1/2\}\subset A(k,n),

and
\dim(\mathcal{V}_{1})\geq\dim(\mathcal{V})-\varepsilon/2
. By Frostman’s lemma, there is a probability measure
\mu
supported on
\mathcal{V}_{1}
, such that for any
Q_{r}\subset A(k,n)
being a ball of radius
r
we have

(32)

\mu(Q_{r})\leq C_{0}r^{(k+1)(d-k)+\beta-\varepsilon}.

We also define the localized version of
X
:

X_{1}:=\bigcup_{V\in\mathcal{V}_{1}}V\cap B^{n}(0,1).

We remark that for any
V\in\mathcal{V}_{1}
,

|V\cap B^{n}(0,1)|\gtrsim 1.

Our goal is to show

(33)

\dim(X_{1})\geq d+\beta-O(\varepsilon).

Let
k_{0}
be a large integer that will be chosen later.
Cover
X_{1}
by a union
\bigcup_{k=k_{0}}^{\infty}\bigcup_{B\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}B
, where
\mathcal{B}_{k}
is a collection of balls of radius
2^{-k}
, and

(34)

\sum_{k}2^{-k(\dim X_{1}+\varepsilon)}\#\mathcal{B}_{k}=:C_{1}<\infty.

For each
V\in\mathcal{V}_{1}
, there is an index
k=k(V)\geq k_{0}
, so that

(35)

|V\cap\bigcup_{B\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}B|\gtrsim\frac{1}{k^{2}}.

For each index
k
, define

\mathcal{V}^{(k)}=\{V\in\mathcal{V}_{1}:k(V)=k\}.

Then

\sum_{k\geq k_{0}}\mu(\mathcal{V}^{(k)})=1,

so there exists an index
k_{1}\geq k_{0}
with
\mu(\mathcal{V}^{(k_{1})})\geq\frac{1}{k_{1}^{2}}
. From now on, we just define
\delta=2^{-k_{1}}
and
E_{\delta}=\bigcup_{B\in\mathcal{B}_{k_{1}}}B.

From (
34
), we see that

(36)

|E_{\delta}|=\#\mathcal{B}_{k_{1}}2^{-k_{1}}\leq C_{1}\delta^{n-\varepsilon-\dim X_{1}}.

Noting that
\mu
is a
\big((k+1)(d-k)-\varepsilon\big)
-dimensional Frostman measure (see (
32
)), by
[
2
, Lemma 8]
, there exists a subset
\mathcal{V}_{2}\subset\mathcal{V}^{(k_{1})}
that satisfies
\#\mathcal{V}_{2}\gtrsim\frac{1}{k_{1}^{2}}\delta^{\varepsilon-(k+1)(d-k)-\beta}
, and

\#\{V\in\mathcal{V}_{2}:V\in Q_{r}\}\lesssim\delta^{-\varepsilon}(r/\delta)^{(k+1)(d-k)+\beta}

for any
Q_{r}\subset A(k,n)
being a ball of radius
r
(
\delta\leq r\leq 1
).

In what follows, we will write
A\lessapprox B
to mean
A\lesssim|\log\delta|^{O(1)}B
.

By Lemma
11
, we can find
\mathcal{V}_{3}\subset\mathcal{V}_{2}
so that
\#\mathcal{V}_{3}\gtrsim\delta^{O(\varepsilon)-\big((k+1)(d-k)+\beta\big)}
, and
\mathcal{V}_{3}
satisfies

\#\{V\in\mathcal{V}_{3}:V\in Q_{r}\}\leq(r/\delta)^{(k+1)(d-k)+\beta}.

From (
35
), we see that for any
V\in\mathcal{V}_{3}
,

(37)

|V_{\delta}\cap E_{\delta}|\gtrapprox 1\sim|V_{\delta}|.

Now, we can apply Theorem
5
to
\mathcal{V}_{3}
.
By (
4
) and Hölder’s inequality, we have

\big(\int_{E_{\delta}}\sum_{V\in\mathcal{V}_{3}}1_{V_{\delta}}\big)\big|E_{\delta}\big|^{-1/p^{\prime}}\leq\|\sum_{V\in\mathcal{V}_{3}}1_{V_{\delta}}\|_{p}\leq C_{\varepsilon}\delta^{-\frac{k(d-k)}{p^{\prime}}-\varepsilon}\big(\sum_{V\in\mathcal{V}_{3}}|V_{\delta}|\big)^{1/p}.

Noting (
37
), this implies

|E_{\delta}|\gtrsim\delta^{k(d-k)+p^{\prime}\varepsilon}\big(\sum_{V\in\mathcal{V}_{3}}|V_{\delta}|\big).

Combined with
\sum_{V\in\mathcal{V}_{3}}|V_{\delta}|=\#\mathcal{V}_{3}\delta^{n-k}\gtrsim\delta^{O(\varepsilon)-(k+1)(d-k)-\beta}\delta^{n-k}
and (
36
), we obtain that

\dim X_{1}\geq d+\beta-O(\varepsilon)

as
\delta
goes to
0
.
∎
