On Density Functional Theory models for one-dimensional homogeneous materials
Bouchra Bensiali, Salma Lahbabi, Abdallah Maichine, Othmane Mirinioui

Abstract
. This paper studies DFT models for homogeneous 1D materials in the 3D space. It follows our previous work about DFT models for homogeneous 2D materials in 3D. We show how to reduce the problem from a 3D energy functional to a 2D energy functional. The kinetic energy is treated as in [ 7 , 8 ] by diagonilizing admissible states, and writing the kinetic energy as the infimum of a modified kinetic energy functional on reduced states. Besides, we treat here the Hartree interaction term in 2D, and show how to properly define the mean-field potential, through Riesz potential. We then show the well posdness of the reduced model and present some numerical illustrations.



1.
Introduction

In recent years, low-dimensional materials, such as 2D sheets, 1D nanowires and quantum dots
have become of great importance in condensed matter physics due to their distinctive features, which are relevant in many applications
[
6
,
19
,
22
,
3
,
9
,
1
,
26
]
. In order to investigate their electronic properties, such as electron’s mobility and band gap, Density Functional Theory (DFT) calculations are often used

[
4
,
24
,
11
,
18
,
20
,
25
]
. Our objective is to prove and study DFT models for low-dimensional systems in order to allow low computational cost. In
[
2
]
, the Thoma-Fermi-von Weisäcker model for 1D and 2D materials is derived by means of thermodynamic limit procedure. The works
[
7
,
8
]
have initiated the study of DFT models for low dimensional materials that are homogeneous, and we were particularly interested in 2D materials embedded in the 3D space. In this paper, we focus on one–dimensional materials that have an atomic length in two dimensions of the space, and a crystalline structure in the remaining dimension.

In
[
7
]
, 2D materials are considered with a nuclear charge distribution of the form

\mu_{2D}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=\mu_{2D}(x_{3})

that depends only on the orthogonal direction
x_{3}
. The electrons are described by one body density matrices
\gamma
that satisfy Pauli principle

0\leq\gamma\leq 1
.
As the problem is convex,
\gamma
share the same symmetry as
\mu
,

(1.1)

U_{R}\gamma=\gamma U_{R},\quad\forall R\in\mathbb{R}^{2},

where
U_{R}f(x)=f(x_{1}-R_{1},x_{2}-R_{2},x_{3})
is the translation operator along the first two variables.
Note that the density associated to a
\gamma
satisfying (
1.1
) depends only on the third variable
\rho_{\gamma}(x)=\rho_{\gamma}(x_{3})
and the trace per unit surface of
\gamma
can be defined by
\underline{{\rm Tr}}_{2D}(\gamma)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho_{\gamma}
. In the reduced Hartree-Fock (rHF) model, the energy of the system is of the form

\underline{{\mathcal{E}}}_{2D}(\gamma)=\frac{1}{2}\underline{{\rm Tr}}_{2D}\left(-\Delta_{3}\gamma\right)+{\mathcal{D}}_{1}(\rho_{\gamma}-\mu),

where
\Delta_{d}
denotes the the Laplacian operator on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and
{\mathcal{D}}_{1}
is the 1D Hartree interaction.
One main result of
[
7
]
is a thorough discussion of this term and the definition of the mean-field potential (see
[
7
, Proposition 3.3]
). We prove that this problem is equivalent to a one-dimensional problem set on operators
G
acting on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, which are positive
G\geq 0
, but which no longer need to satisfy
Pauli principle. Instead, an additional term appears in the energy:
the three-dimensional kinetic energy
\frac{1}{2}\underline{{\rm Tr}}_{2D}(-\Delta_{3}\gamma)

is replaced by a one-dimensional kinetic energy
of the form

\frac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}(-\Delta_{1}G)+\pi{\rm Tr}(G^{2}).

Although we restrict ourselves to the reduced Hartree-Fock model for simplicity, similar derivations
can be performed for general Kohn–Sham models and one can consider models of the form

\inf\left\{\frac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}(-\Delta_{1}G)+\pi{\rm Tr}(G^{2})+{\mathcal{D}}_{1}(\rho_{G}-\mu)+E^{\rm xc}(\rho_{G})\right\}.

In
[
8
]
, we have considered a uniform magnetic field which is perpendicular to the 2D material axis. The reduction here is obtained considering operators commuting with
magnetic translations
. We first provide a decomposition of such operators and then state the equivalent one-dimensional problem in which the kinetic energy per unit surface
\frac{1}{2}\underline{{\rm Tr}}_{2D}(\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{A}}\gamma)
has been repalaced by

\frac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}(-\Delta_{1}G)+{\rm Tr}(F(b,G)),

where
\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{A}}
denotes the three-dimensional Landau operator,
b
the magnetic field strenghten, and
F(b,\cdot)
a suitable piecewise linear function, see for instance
[
8
, Theorem 3.1]
.

In the present paper, we consider homogeneous 1D materials, i.e. nuclear charge distributions of the form

\mu(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=\mu(x_{1},x_{2})

that do not depend on the third variable
x_{3}
, the axis of the material. As we consider convex models, we assume that the electronic density matrices follow the same symmetries

(1.2)

\tau_{R}\gamma=\gamma\tau_{R},\quad\forall R\in\mathbb{R},

where
\tau_{R}f(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=f(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}-R)
denotes the translation operator by the vector
Re_{3}
. Again, states satisfying the symmetry (
1.2
) have densities that depend only on
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
and the trace per unit length of
\gamma
can be defined by
\underline{{\rm Tr}}(\gamma)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\rho_{\gamma}
. In the reduced Hartree-Fock model, the energy of the system is

(1.3)

\underline{{\mathcal{E}}}(\gamma)=\frac{1}{2}\underline{{\rm Tr}}\left(-\Delta_{3}\gamma\right)+\tilde{{\mathcal{D}}}_{2}(\rho_{\gamma}-\mu),

where
\tilde{{\mathcal{D}}}_{2}
is the 2D Hartree interaction. Formally, the interaction term
{\mathcal{D}}_{2}
is defined by

(1.4)

\tilde{{\mathcal{D}}}_{2}(f,g)=-2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}}{f(x)}\log(\left|x-y\right|)g(y).

This definition is only valid for functions that decay fast enough at infinity. Besides, it is unclear whether this bilinear form
\tilde{{\mathcal{D}}}_{2}
is bounded from below or not.
One key result of this paper is a regularization of this expression adapted to neutral systems; and a definition of the corresponding mean-field potential (see Section
2
).

For the kinetic part, following the same arguments as in
[
7
]
,
we prove that this problem is equivalent to a two-dimensional problem,
where the three-dimensional kinetic energy
\frac{1}{2}\underline{{\rm Tr}}(-\Delta_{3}\gamma)

is replaced by a two-dimensional kinetic energy
of the form

\frac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}(-\Delta_{2}G)+\frac{\pi}{6}{\rm Tr}(G^{3}),

defined on
{\mathcal{G}}:=\{G\in{\mathbb{S}}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})):G\geq 0,\;{\rm Tr}(G)<{\infty}\}
. The reduced 2D rHF energy
then reads

(1.5)

{\mathcal{E}}(G)=\frac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}(-\Delta_{1}G)+\frac{\pi}{6}{\rm Tr}(G^{3})+{\mathcal{D}}_{2}(\rho_{G}-\mu).

We show that this problem of minimizing the energy (
1.5
) over a suitable set of admissible states admits a unique minimizer, called ground state. We also derive the self-consistent equation that this ground state needs to satisfy.

Finally, we perform numerical simulations on the simpler Thomas–Fermi model, where the kinetic energy is expressed as an explicit functional of the density
\int\rho^{5/3}
. This allows us to exhibit the properties of the regularized 2D Hartree interaction with respect to the classical expression (
1.4
).

This article is organized as follow. Section
2
is devoted to the regularization of the 2D Hartree interaction and the definition of the corresponding mean-field potential. In Section
3
, we show how to reduce the 3D problem (
1.3
) into the 2D model (
1.5
), that we study in Section
4
. Finally, in Section
5
, we consider the orbital free Thomas-Fermi model as a semi–classical limit of the rHF model in order to perform elementary numerical illustrations.

2.
2D Hartree interaction

For 1D crystals, the Hartree interaction kernel has been derived in
[
2
]
by means of thermodynamic limit procedure. It is given by

G(x)=-2\pi\log(\left|(x_{1},x_{2})\right|)+\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(\frac{1}{\left|x-ke_{3}\right|}-\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}\frac{dt}{\left|x-(y,k)e_{3}\right|}\right),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{3},

and the Hartree interaction energy is formally given by

{\mathcal{D}}_{\rm per}(f,g)=\int_{\Gamma\times\Gamma}f(x)G(x-y)g(y),

where
\Gamma=\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\left[-1/2,1/2\right]
.
For functions
f,g
which do not depend on the third variable
x_{3}
, this interaction reduces to

(2.1)

{\mathcal{D}}_{2}(f,g)=-2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}}{f(x)}\log(\left|x-y\right|)g(y).

The integral in (
2.1
) is only valid for functions decaying fast enough and the map
f\mapsto{\mathcal{D}}_{2}(f)
is not convex in general.
The aim of this section is to give a regularization of this expression suitable for neutral charge distributions, which, in our context, will be
f=\rho-\mu
. We therefore adopt the following definition

(2.2)

{\mathcal{D}}(f,g):=4\pi\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\dfrac{\overline{{\mathcal{F}}_{2}(f)(k)}{\mathcal{F}}_{2}(g)(k)}{|k|^{2}}{\mathrm{d}}k,

where
f
and
g
belong to the following Coulomb space

{\mathcal{C}}:=\left\{f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2}),\;k\mapsto\frac{{\mathcal{F}}_{2}(f)(k)}{|k|}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\right\}.

We have denoted by
{\mathcal{F}}_{2}
the Fourier transform in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
defined by
{\mathcal{F}}_{2}f(k)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f(x)e^{-ikx}dx
. We note that the integrability of
k\mapsto\frac{|{\mathcal{F}}_{2}(f)(k)|^{2}}{|k|^{2}}
over
\mathbb{R}^{2}
implies that

{\mathcal{F}}_{2}(f)(0)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f(x){\mathrm{d}}x=0,

which means that elements of
{\mathcal{C}}
are neutral distributions. Conversely, for neutral distributions that decay fast enough,
{\mathcal{D}}_{2}
and
{\mathcal{D}}
coincide.

Lemma 2.1
.

If
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
is such that
\int f=0
and
\log(1+\left|\cdot\right|)f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, then

{\mathcal{D}}(f)={\mathcal{D}}_{2}(f).

The proof of this lemma can be found in
[
5
]
.

For
f\in{\mathcal{C}}
, let us introduce
{\mathrm{W}}_{f}
as

{\mathrm{W}}_{f}:={\mathcal{F}}_{2}^{-1}\left(k\mapsto\frac{{\mathcal{F}}_{2}(f)(k)}{|k|}\right)=(-\Delta_{2})^{-\frac{1}{2}}f\quad\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}).

Thanks to Parseval identity, one can rewrite the interaction
{\mathcal{D}}
as

{\mathcal{D}}(f,g)=4\pi\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\overline{{\mathrm{W}}_{f}(x)}{W_{g}(x)}{\mathrm{d}}x,\qquad\forall f,g\in{\mathcal{C}}.

An explicit expression of
{\mathrm{W}}_{f}
, for
f\in{\mathcal{C}}
, is given through the Riesz potential as follows (see for instance
[
23
, Chapter V]
).

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
f\in{\mathcal{C}}
. Then,

(2.3)

{\mathrm{W}}_{f}(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{f(y)}{|x-y|}{\mathrm{d}}y.

The integral in (
2.3
) is absolutely convergent whenever
f\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, for some
1\leq p<2
(see for instance
[
23
, Theorem 1 of Chapter V]
). Furthermore, if
p\in(1,2)
and
q=\frac{2p}{2-p}
. Then,

(2.4)

\|{\mathrm{W}}_{f}\|_{q}\leq C\|f\|_{p},\qquad\forall f\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2}),

for a suitable constant
C
which is independent of
f
.

We turn now to the definition of a
mean-field potential

{\mathrm{V}}_{f}
associated to a charge distribution
f\in{\mathcal{C}}
. It needs to satisfy

{\mathcal{D}}(f,g)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}{\mathrm{V}}_{f}(x)g(x){\mathrm{d}}x,\qquad\forall g\in{\mathcal{C}}

and

-\Delta{\mathrm{V}}_{f}=4\pi f

in a weak sense. One would consider
{\mathrm{V}}_{f}=4\pi(-\Delta)^{-1}f=4\pi W_{W_{f}}
. However, the integral in (
2.3
) is not necessarily convergent in the critical case of
p=2
. We suggest an alternative way to define the potential
{\mathrm{V}}_{f}
, for
f\in{\mathcal{C}}
through a modification of the Riesz potential suggested in
[
13
]
. Let us define the following two functions

q_{1}(x)=|x|\left(1+\log^{+}\left(\frac{1}{|x|}\right)\right)\quad\text{and}\quad q_{2}(x)=|x|\left(1+\log^{+}\left(|x|\right)\right),

where

\log^{+}(|x|):=\log(|x|){\mathds{1}}_{\{|x|\geq 1\}}=\begin{cases}\log(|x|)\quad\text{if}\quad|x|\geq 1\\
0\quad\text{else}\end{cases}.

In the following proposition, we define and state some properties of the modified Riesz potential. For the proof and further details, we refer to
[
13
, Corollary 3.23]
.

Proposition 2.3
.

For
g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, define

(2.5)

\mathcal{I}_{1}^{g}(x):=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{|y|<1}\frac{g(y)}{|x-y|}\;{\mathrm{d}}y\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{I}_{2}^{g}(x):=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{|y|\geq 1}\left(\frac{1}{|x-y|}-\frac{1}{|y|}\right)g(y)\;{\mathrm{d}}y.

One has the following

a)

{\mathcal{I}}_{1}^{g}
and
{\mathcal{I}}_{2}^{g}
are well-defined, i.e., the integrals in (
2.5
) are convergent.

b)

There exist two real numbers
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
such that

(2.6)

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\mathcal{I}_{1}^{g}}{q_{1}}\right\|_{L^{2}}\;\;\leq\;C_{1}\left\|g\right\|_{L^{2}}\quad and\quad\left\|\frac{\mathcal{I}_{2}^{g}}{q_{2}}\right\|_{L^{2}}\;\;\leq\;C_{2}\left\|g\right\|_{L^{2}}.

As a consequence of Proposition
2.3
, we have
\mathcal{I}_{i}^{f}\in L^{1}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, for
i\in\left\{1,2\right\}
. Now, for
f\in{\mathcal{C}}
, we define the mean field potential as

(2.7)

{\mathrm{V}}_{f}:=4\pi\left(\mathcal{I}_{1}^{{\mathrm{W}}_{f}}+\mathcal{I}_{2}^{{\mathrm{W}}_{f}}\right).

The following theorem is one of the main results of the paper and it allows to rewrite the potential energy
{\mathcal{D}}
in terms of the mean–field potential
V_{f}
.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
f\in{\mathcal{C}}
. Then

(2.8)

\mathcal{D}(f,h)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}{\mathrm{V}}_{f}(x)\,h(x){\mathrm{d}}x\qquad\forall h\in\mathcal{C},

and
{\mathrm{V}}_{f}
satisfies

(2.9)

-\Delta{\mathrm{V}}_{f}=4\pi f

in a distributional sense.

Proof.

Let us first prove (
2.8
) for
h
in the Schwartz space of fast decaying functions
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.
Since

{\mathcal{I}}_{j}^{{\mathrm{W}}_{f}}h=(q_{j}^{-1}{\mathcal{I}}_{j}^{{\mathrm{W}}_{f}})\,q_{j}h\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, for
j\in\{1,2\}
. Then, one can write

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}{\mathrm{V}}_{f}(x)\,h(x)\,{\mathrm{d}}x:=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\mathcal{I}_{1}^{{\mathrm{W}}_{f}}(x)h(x)\,{\mathrm{d}}x+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\mathcal{I}_{2}^{{\mathrm{W}}_{f}}(x)h(x)\,{\mathrm{d}}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{\{|y|<1\}}\frac{{\mathrm{W}}_{f}(y)}{|x-y|}h(x)\,{\mathrm{d}}y{\mathrm{d}}x

(2.10)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{\{|y|\geq 1\}}\left(\frac{1}{|x-y|}-\frac{1}{|y|}\right){\mathrm{W}}_{f}(y)h(x)\,{\mathrm{d}}y{\mathrm{d}}x.

In order to apply Fubini theorem and change the order of the integrals in the above double intgrals, we have to check the absolute integrability.
Regarding the first term, one can note that
(|q_{1}|)^{-1}\mathcal{I}_{1}^{|{\mathrm{W}}_{f}|}
is in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, thanks to (
2.6
), and so is
q_{1}h
. Therefore, one has

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{\{|y|<1\}}\frac{{\mathrm{W}}_{f}(y)}{|x-y|}h(x)\,{\mathrm{d}}y{\mathrm{d}}x

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{|y|<1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{{\mathrm{W}}_{f}(y)}{|x-y|}h(x)\,{\mathrm{d}}x{\mathrm{d}}y

(2.11)

\displaystyle=\int_{\{|y|<1\}}{\mathrm{W}}_{f}(y){\mathrm{W}}_{h}(y)\;{\mathrm{d}}y.

Taking into the account that the estimate in (
2.6
) holds true if one substitutes the kernel defining
\mathcal{I}_{2}
by its absolute value (see for instance
[
13
, Theorem 3.19]
), then the last double intergal of (
2
) is absolutely convergent as well. Thus,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{|y|\geq 1}\left(\frac{1}{|x-y|}-\frac{1}{|y|}\right){\mathrm{W}}_{f}(y)h(x)\,{\mathrm{d}}y{\mathrm{d}}x

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{|y|\geq 1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{|x-y|}-\frac{1}{|y|}\right){\mathrm{W}}_{f}(y)h(x)\,{\mathrm{d}}x{\mathrm{d}}y

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\int_{|y|\geq 1}{\mathrm{W}}_{f}(y)\left({\mathrm{W}}_{h}(y)-\frac{1}{2\pi|y|}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}h\right)\,{\mathrm{d}}y

(2.12)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\int_{|y|\geq 1}{\mathrm{W}}_{f}(y){\mathrm{W}}_{h}(y)\,{\mathrm{d}}y.

By adding (
2.11
) et (
2.12
), we obtain

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}{\mathrm{V}}_{f}(x)\,h(x)\,dx\>=4\pi\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}{\mathrm{W}}_{f}(x){\mathrm{W}}_{h}(x)\,dx.

Now, let
h\in{\mathcal{C}}
. Then,
h\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and
{\mathrm{W}}_{h}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. By density of the Schwartz space
{\mathcal{S}}
, one can find
(h_{n})_{n}\subset{\mathcal{S}}
with
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}h_{n}\,{\mathrm{d}}x=0
such that
h_{n}\to h
in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and
\frac{{\mathcal{F}}_{2}(h_{n})}{|\cdot|}\to\frac{{\mathcal{F}}_{2}(h)}{|\cdot|}
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. Hence, one obtains (
2.8
), for
h\in{\mathcal{C}}
, by letting
n\to{\infty}
.

We move now to the proof of (
2.9
). If one denotes by
(\cdot,\cdot)
the distributional brakets, then,
for
\phi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
,

\displaystyle(-\Delta_{2}V_{f},\phi)=(V_{f},-\Delta_{2}\phi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}V_{f}\Delta_{2}\phi\,{\mathrm{d}}x.

One knows that
h:=-\Delta\phi
satisfies
\int h=0
. Thus, by the previous result

\displaystyle(-\Delta_{2}V_{f},\phi)={\mathcal{D}}(f,h)

\displaystyle=4\pi\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{\overline{{\mathcal{F}}_{2}(f)(k)}{\mathcal{F}}_{2}(h)(k)}{\left|k\right|^{2}}{\mathrm{d}}k

\displaystyle=4\pi\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\overline{{\mathcal{F}}_{2}(f)(k)}{\mathcal{F}}_{2}(\phi)(k){\mathrm{d}}k=\langle 4\pi f,\phi\rangle.

∎

3.
Model reduction

In this section, we show that the 3D reduced Hartree Fock model for homogeneous 1D crystal is equivalent to a 2D model. Other DFT models can be handled similarly. We recall that the nuclear charge distribution
\mu\in L^{1}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
satisfies

(3.1)

\mu\geq 0,\quad\text{and}\quad\mu(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=\mu(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}-R),\quad\forall R\in\mathbb{R}.

We assume that the charge per unit length is finite, that is,

Z:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\mu(x_{1},x_{2},0)\;{\mathrm{d}}x_{1}\,{\mathrm{d}}x_{2}<{\infty}.

In Hartree–Fock type models, the state of electrons is described by a bounded self–adjoint operator
\gamma
satisfying Pauli exclusion principle
0\leq\gamma\leq 1
. In our particular case, the system has the following features.

•

It is equidistributed in one direction of the three–dimensional space,

•

it is confined in the remaining two directions with a finite number of particles.

The electronic states
\gamma
share the aforementioned invariance as follows

(3.2)

\gamma\tau_{R}=\tau_{R}\gamma,\qquad\forall R\in\mathbb{R},

where
\tau_{R}
denotes the translation operator by the vector
Re_{3}
(
\tau_{R}f(x)=f(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}-R)
).
The 3D rHF energy of a state
\gamma
in

{\mathcal{K}}:=\{\gamma\in\underline{{\mathfrak{S}}}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})),\;0\leq\gamma\leq 1,\;\tau_{R}\gamma=\gamma\tau_{R},\;\forall R\in\mathbb{R}\}.

is

\underline{{\mathcal{E}}}(\gamma)=\frac{1}{2}\underline{{\rm Tr}}\left(-\Delta_{3}\gamma\right)+{\mathcal{D}}(\rho_{\gamma}-\mu),

where we have denoted by
\underline{{\mathfrak{S}}}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))
the space of self–adjoint, locally trace class operators acting on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
, and
{\mathcal{D}}
is the regularized 2D Hartree interaction introduced in the previous section. We now define the 2D energy functional for a state in

(3.3)

{\mathcal{G}}:=\{G\in{\mathfrak{S}}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})),\;G\geq 0\},

where we have denoted by
{{\mathfrak{S}}}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))
the space of self–adjoint, trace class operators acting on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, by

(3.4)

{\mathcal{E}}(G)=\frac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}(-\Delta_{1}G)+\frac{\pi}{6}{\rm Tr}(G^{3})+{\mathcal{D}}(\rho_{G}-\mu).

The main result of the section is the following theorem.

Theorem 3.1
.

The ground state energies

\underline{I}=\inf\left\{\underline{{\mathcal{E}}}(\gamma),\;\gamma\in{\mathcal{K}}\right\}

and

(3.5)

I=\inf\left\{{\mathcal{E}}(G),\;G\in{\mathcal{G}}\right\}

are equal and the minimizers of both energies share the same densities, which does not depend on
x_{3}
.

The proof of theorem (
3.1
) follows the same line as the one of
[
7
, Theorem 2.8]
. We highlight here some arguments in the 2D case.

Diagonalization of admissible states.
We start by showing that states in
{\mathcal{K}}
can be diagonalized. Let us introduce the partial Fourier transform

\displaystyle{\mathcal{U}}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})

\displaystyle\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R},L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle\mapsto{\mathcal{F}}_{1}(f(x_{1},x_{2},\cdot))(k),

where
{\mathcal{F}}_{1}
refers to the Fourier transform in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R})

{\mathcal{F}}_{1}(f(x_{1},x_{2},\cdot))(k)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\mathrm{e}}^{-{\mathrm{i}}kt}f(x_{1},x_{2},t){\mathrm{d}}t
. According to the Floquet–Bloch decomposition (see
[
21
, Section XIII–16]
), for any
\gamma\in{\mathcal{K}}
there exists a unique family of self–adjoint operators
(\gamma_{k})_{k}
on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, called the fibers of
\gamma
, such that

(3.6)

{\mathcal{U}}\gamma{\mathcal{U}}^{-1}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}^{\oplus}\gamma_{k}{\mathrm{d}}k,

that is, for any
f=(f_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{R}}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R},L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))
,

({\mathcal{U}}\gamma{\mathcal{U}}^{-1}f)_{k}=\gamma_{k}f_{k}.

We point out that, if
\gamma\in{\mathcal{K}}
, then its fibers
\gamma_{k}
satisfy Pauli principle
0\leq\gamma_{k}\leq 1
. The following proposition links other properties of
\gamma\in{\mathcal{K}}
to those of its fibers.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
\gamma=\int_{\mathbb{R}}^{\oplus}\gamma_{k}\in{\mathcal{K}}
and suppose that
\gamma
is locally trace class. One has

•

\gamma
admits an integral kernel
\gamma(\cdot;\cdot):(\mathbb{R}^{3})^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
and

(3.7)

\gamma(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3};y_{1},y_{2},y_{3})=\frac{1}{{2\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\mathrm{e}}^{{\mathrm{i}}k(x_{3}-y_{3})}\gamma_{k}(x_{1},x_{2};y_{1},y_{2}){\mathrm{d}}k,

where, for every
k\in\mathbb{R}
,
\gamma_{k}(\cdot;\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
is the integral kernel of
\gamma_{k}
.

•

Let
\rho_{\gamma}
denote the density of
\gamma
, given by

\rho_{\gamma}(x)=\gamma(x,x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Then,

\rho(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=\frac{1}{{2\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho_{k}(x_{1},x_{2};x_{1},x_{2}){\mathrm{d}}k,

where
\rho_{k}
refers to the density of
\gamma_{k}
.

In particular,
\rho_{\gamma}
does not depend on the third variable
x_{3}
.

•

The
trace per unit length
of
\gamma
is given by

\underline{\rm Tr}(\gamma):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\rho_{\gamma}=\frac{1}{{2\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\rm Tr}(\gamma_{k}){\mathrm{d}}k.

Proof.

Since
\gamma
is locally trace class, then
\gamma_{k}
is trace class, for all
k\in\mathbb{R}
.
By straightforward computation, one gets for a smooth enough
f
and
(x_{1},x_{2},k)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}

\displaystyle({\mathcal{U}}\gamma f)_{k}(x_{1},x_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\mathrm{e}}^{-{\mathrm{i}}kt}\,(\gamma f)(x_{1},x_{2},t){\mathrm{d}}t

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}{\mathrm{e}}^{-{\mathrm{i}}kt}\gamma(x_{1},x_{2},t;y_{1},y_{2},\tilde{t})f(y_{1},y_{2},\tilde{t})\;{\mathrm{d}}y_{1}{\mathrm{d}}y_{2}{\mathrm{d}}\tilde{t}{\mathrm{d}}t,

On the other hand,

\displaystyle(\gamma_{k}({\mathcal{U}}f)_{k})(x_{1},x_{2})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\gamma_{k}(x_{1},x_{2};y_{1},y_{2})({\mathcal{U}}f)_{k}(y_{1},y_{2}){\mathrm{d}}y_{1}{\mathrm{d}}y_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\mathrm{e}}^{-{\mathrm{i}}k\tilde{t}}\gamma_{k}(x_{1},x_{2};y_{1},y_{2})f(y_{1},y_{2},\tilde{t}){\mathrm{d}}y_{1}{\mathrm{d}}y_{2}{\mathrm{d}}\tilde{t}

By identification,

\gamma_{k}(x_{1},x_{2};y_{1},y_{2})=\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\mathrm{e}}^{-{\mathrm{i}}k(t-\tilde{t})}\gamma(x_{1},x_{2},t;y_{1},y_{2},\tilde{t}){\mathrm{d}}t.

Therefore, applying an inverse Fourier transform, one obtains (
3.7
). The claim on the density follows immediately by (
3.7
).
∎

Diagonalization of the kinetic energy

Let
\gamma=\int_{\mathbb{R}}^{\oplus}\gamma_{k}\in{\mathcal{K}}
.
Let
f=(f_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{R}}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R},L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))
such that
f_{k}
is smooth enough for any
k
. Straightforward computation yields

({\mathcal{U}}(-\Delta_{3})\gamma{\mathcal{U}}^{-1}f)_{k}=(-\Delta_{2}+k^{2})\gamma_{k}f_{k}.

Hence, the kinetic energy per unit length of
\gamma
can be written

\frac{1}{2}\underline{\rm Tr}(-\Delta_{3}\gamma)=\frac{1}{{4\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\rm Tr}((-\Delta_{2}+k^{2})\gamma_{k})\,{\mathrm{d}}k.

Reduced states

Now, we map any admissible state
\gamma
to a reduced 2D state
G
as follows

\displaystyle\Lambda:{\mathcal{K}}

\displaystyle\to{\mathcal{G}}

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle\mapsto\Lambda(\gamma):=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\gamma_{k}{\mathrm{d}}k.

We notice that elements of
{\mathcal{G}}
, unlike those of
{\mathcal{K}}
, are compact operators.
We have the following result.

Proposition 3.3
.

The map
\Lambda
is onto. Furthermore, for any
\gamma\in{\mathcal{K}}
,

\rho_{\gamma}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=\rho_{\Lambda(\gamma)}(x_{1},x_{2}),\qquad\forall(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

Proof.

Let
G\in{\mathcal{G}}
. One can write
G=\sum_{j}g_{j}|\varphi_{j}\rangle\langle\varphi_{j}|
, with
(\varphi_{j})_{j}
an orthonormal family in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and
g_{j}\geq 0
such that
\sum_{j}g_{j}={\rm Tr}(G)<{\infty}
. We construct
\gamma
satisfying
\Lambda(\gamma)=G
through its fibers
(\gamma_{k})_{k}
. Let us define, for any
k\in\mathbb{R}
,
\gamma_{k}
as

\gamma_{k}=\sum_{j}\alpha_{j}^{(k)}|\varphi_{j}\rangle\langle\varphi_{j}|\quad\text{with}\quad\alpha_{j}^{(k)}=1_{\{|k|\leq s_{j}\}},

where, for any
j\in\mathbb{N}
,
s_{j}\geq 0
will be determined later. One has
0\leq\gamma_{k}\leq 1
and
\gamma_{k}\in{\mathfrak{S}}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))
. Thus,
\gamma\in{\mathcal{K}}
. Moreover,
\rho_{\gamma_{k}}=\sum_{j}\alpha_{j}^{(k)}|\varphi_{j}|^{2}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\rho_{\gamma}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho_{\gamma_{k}}{\mathrm{d}}k

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{j}\int_{\mathbb{R}}1_{\{|k|\leq s_{j}\}}|\varphi_{j}(x_{1},x_{2})|^{2}{\mathrm{d}}k=\frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{j}s_{j}|\varphi_{j}(x_{1},x_{2})|^{2}.

Finally, with the choice
s_{j}=\pi{g_{j}}
, one obtains
\rho_{\gamma}=\rho_{G}
, which proves the claim.
∎

Reduction of the kinetic energy

We now rewrite the kinetic energy as a functional of elements of
{\mathcal{G}}
.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
G\in{\mathcal{G}}
. Then,

(3.8)

\inf_{\Lambda(\gamma)=G}\underline{{\mathcal{E}}}^{\rm kin}(\gamma)=\frac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}(-\Delta_{2}G)+\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}{\rm Tr}(G^{3}).

Proof.

Let
G\in{\mathcal{G}}
and
\gamma=\int^{\oplus}_{\mathbb{R}}\gamma_{k}\in{\mathcal{K}}
with
\Lambda(\gamma)=G
. Write
G=\sum_{j}g_{j}|\varphi_{j}\rangle\langle\varphi_{j}|
. We have

\displaystyle\underline{{\mathcal{E}}}^{\rm kin}(\gamma)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\underline{\rm Tr}(-\Delta_{3}\gamma)=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\rm Tr}((-\Delta_{2}+k^{2})\gamma_{k})\,{\mathrm{d}}k

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}(-\Delta_{2}G)+\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\sum_{j}k^{2}m_{j,k}{\mathrm{d}}k,

where
m_{j,k}:=\langle\gamma_{k}\varphi_{j},\varphi_{j}\rangle
. Notice that

(3.9)

0\leq m_{j,k}\leq 1\quad\text{and}\quad\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}m_{j,k}\,{\mathrm{d}}k=2\pi g_{j}.

Hence,

\displaystyle\underline{{\mathcal{E}}}^{\rm kin}(\gamma)\geq\frac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}(-\Delta_{2}G)+\frac{1}{4\pi}\sum_{j}\inf\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}}k^{2}m(k){\mathrm{d}}k,\;m(k)\;\text{satisifying}\;\eqref{eq bathtube cond}\right\}.

According to the Bath-tub principle,
[
14
, Theorem 1.14]
, the infinimum in the RHS of the above inequality is achieved by
m_{j}^{*}(k):=1_{\{|k|<\pi g_{j}\}}
, the same state exhibited in the proof of Proposition
3.3
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\underline{{\mathcal{E}}}^{\rm kin}(\gamma)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}(-\Delta_{2}G)+\frac{1}{6\pi}\sum_{j}(\pi g_{j})^{3}=\frac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}(-\Delta_{2}G)+\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}{\rm Tr}(G^{3}),

with equality for
\gamma^{*}\in{\mathcal{K}}
with
\gamma_{k}^{*}=\sum_{j}m_{j}^{*}(k)|\varphi_{j}\rangle\langle\varphi_{j}|
. ∎

Remark 3.5
.

A consequence of Theorem
3.4
is that for a representable charge density
\rho\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, one could associate the kinetic energy functional as follows

(3.10)

{\mathcal{E}}^{\rm kin}(\rho)=\inf\left\{{\rm Tr}(-\Delta_{2}G)+\frac{2\pi^{2}}{3}{\rm Tr}(G^{3}),\;G\in{\mathcal{G}}_{0},\;\rho_{G}=\rho\right\}.

Finding an explicit expression for the above functional
\rho\mapsto{\mathcal{E}}^{\rm kin}(\rho)
is highly challenging. It is only possible to associate a lower bound to this energy as follows

(3.11)

C\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\rho_{G}^{5/3}\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}(-\Delta_{2}G)+\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}{\rm Tr}(G^{3})={\mathcal{E}}^{\rm kin}(\rho).

for an appropriate constant
C
. This is a consequence of the Lieb–Thirring inequality, see for instance
[
15
,
16
]
and
[
7
, Appendix A]
(see Section
5.1
). In particular, this yields that
\rho_{G}\in L^{5/3}
, once the kinetic enegry of
G
is finite. Actually, the two dimensional Lieb–Thiring inequality applied to
G/Z
implies that
\rho_{G}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and the Hoffman-Ostenhof inequality claims that
\sqrt{\rho_{G}}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, see
[
10
]
.

The proof of Theorem
3.1
can be easily deduced from Theorem
3.4
and Proposition
3.3
.

4.
Well-posdness of the reduced model

This section is devoted to the analysis of the reduced two-dimensional problem (
3.5
). In particular, we prove well-posdness and show that the ground state satisfies a self-consistent equation.

Theorem 4.1
.

Assume that
\mu\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. Then the energy
{\mathcal{E}}
in (
3.4
) admits a unique minimizer
G_{*}
in
{\mathcal{G}}
. Furthermore, one has

(4.1)

\begin{cases}\quad G_{*}=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\,\pi}\left(\lambda_{*}-H_{*}\right)_{+}^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
\quad H_{*}=-\Delta_{2}+{\mathrm{V}}_{\rho_{*}-\mu}\end{cases},

where
\rho_{*}:=\rho_{G_{*}}
,
x_{+}:=\max(0,x)
for
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
\left(\lambda_{*}-H_{*}\right)_{+}^{\frac{1}{2}}
should be unterstood in terms of functional calculus for the self–adjoint operator
H_{*}
.

Proof.

The proof of this theorem follows the same lines as the one of
[
7
, Theorem 2.8]
. We detail here the main arguments in our 2D case.

Existence and uniqueness of the minimizer
. The set
{\mathcal{C}}
is convex and the functional
{\mathcal{E}}
is convex and positive. Then, the existence of a minimizer is reduced to the lower semi-continuity of
{\mathcal{E}}
. Let
let
(G_{n})_{n}\subset{\mathcal{G}}
be a minimizing sequence of
{\mathcal{E}}
and denote by
\rho_{n}:=\rho_{G_{n}}
. One has

{\mathcal{E}}(G_{n})=\frac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}(-\Delta_{2}G_{n})+\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}{\rm Tr}(G_{n}^{3})+{\mathcal{D}}(\rho_{n}-\mu)\leq C,

where
C
is a positive constant. In particular,
(G_{n})_{n}
and
(-\Delta G_{n})_{n}
are bounded sequences in the Schatten space of trace class operators
{\mathfrak{S}}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))
and
(G_{n})_{n}
is bounded in the Schatten space of cubic trace class operators
{\mathfrak{S}}_{3}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))
. Hence, taking into the account that
-\Delta_{2}
is closed, there exists
G_{*}\in{\mathfrak{S}}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))\cap{\mathfrak{S}}_{3}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))
such that

(G_{n},-\Delta_{2}G_{n})\rightharpoonup(G_{*},-\Delta_{2}G_{*})\quad\text{in}\quad\left({\mathfrak{S}}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))\right)^{2},

G_{n}\rightharpoonup G_{*}\quad\text{in}\quad{\mathfrak{S}}_{3}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})),

and

\frac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}(-\Delta_{2}G_{*})+\frac{2\pi^{2}}{3}{\rm Tr}(G_{*}^{3})\leq\liminf_{n}\left(\frac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}(-\Delta_{2}G_{n})+\frac{2\pi^{2}}{3}{\rm Tr}(G_{n}^{3})\right).

By the Lieb-Thirring inequality
[
15
]
,
(\rho_{n})_{n}
is bounded in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. Thus,
(\rho_{n})_{n}
converges weakly in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. Moreover, its weak limit is
\rho_{*}:=\rho_{G_{*}}
. Indeed, for any function
h\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
one has

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}h\rho_{*}{\mathrm{d}}x

\displaystyle={\rm Tr}\left((1-\Delta)^{-1}{\mathcal{M}}_{h}G_{*}(1-\Delta)\right)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n}{\rm Tr}\left((1-\Delta)^{-1}{\mathcal{M}}_{h}G_{n}(1-\Delta)\right)=\lim_{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}h\rho_{n},

where
{\mathcal{M}}_{h}
stands for the multiplication operator by
h
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, and the convergence is obtained by the fact that
(1-\Delta)^{-1}{\mathcal{M}}_{h}
is a compact operator.
Besides,
\|{\mathrm{W}}_{\rho_{n}-\mu}\|_{2}={\mathcal{D}}(\rho_{n}-\mu)\leq C
, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Hence,

W_{\rho_{n}-\mu}\rightharpoonup W_{*}\text{ in }L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\quad\text{and}\quad\|W_{*}\|_{2}\leq\liminf_{n}\|{\mathrm{W}}_{\rho_{n}-\mu}\|_{2}.

Let us prove that
W_{*}=W_{\rho_{*}-\mu}
. Let
\varphi
be a test function in the Schwartz space
{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})

such that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\varphi\,{\mathrm{d}}x=0
. By Parseval’s identity, one gets

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}{\mathrm{W}}_{\rho_{n}-\mu}(x)\varphi(x){\mathrm{d}}x=\left\langle\frac{{\mathcal{F}}_{2}(\rho_{n}-\mu)}{|k|},{\mathcal{F}}_{2}(\varphi)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle{\mathcal{F}}_{2}(\rho_{n}-\mu),\frac{{\mathcal{F}}_{2}(\varphi)}{|k|}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\rho_{n}-\mu,{\mathrm{W}}_{\varphi}\rangle.

Letting
n
go to
\infty
, and noting that
\rho_{n}-\mu\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, we obtain

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}{\mathrm{W}}_{*}(x)\varphi(x){\mathrm{d}}x=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left(\rho_{*}-\mu\right)(x)\,{\mathrm{W}}_{\varphi}(x)\,{\mathrm{d}}x=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}{\mathrm{W}}_{\rho_{*}-\mu}(x)\varphi(x){\mathrm{d}}x.

This actually identifies
{\mathrm{W}}_{*}
with
{\mathrm{W}}_{\rho_{*}-\mu}
. Summarizing, one has
G_{*}\in{\mathcal{G}}
and

I\leq{\mathcal{E}}(G_{*})\leq\liminf_{n}{\mathcal{E}}(G_{n})=I,

which proves that
G_{*}
is actually a minimizer of
{\mathcal{E}}
. The uniqueness comes from the strict convexity of
{\rm Tr}\left(G^{3}\right)
.

Proof of the Euler Lagrange equation.
Let
G_{*}
denote the minimizer of
{\mathcal{E}}
,
\rho_{*}:=\rho_{G_{*}}
its density and
V_{*}:=V_{\rho_{*}-\mu}
the mean-field potential. Let
G\in{\mathcal{G}}
and
t\in[0,1]
. One has
G_{t}:=G_{*}+t(G-G_{*})\in{\mathcal{G}}
and
{\mathcal{E}}(G_{t})\geq{\mathcal{E}}(G_{*})
. Besides,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{D}}(\rho_{G_{t}}-\mu)

\displaystyle={\mathcal{D}}(\rho_{*}-\mu)+2t{\mathcal{D}}(\rho_{*}-\mu,\rho_{G}-\rho_{*})+o(t)

\displaystyle={\mathcal{D}}(\rho_{*}-\mu)+2\,t\,{\rm Tr}(V_{*}(G-G_{*}))+o(t).

Thus,

t\left({\rm Tr}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{2}(G-G_{*})\right)+\frac{\pi^{2}}{2}{\rm Tr}\left(G_{*}^{2}(G-G_{*})\right)+{\rm Tr}(V_{*}(G-G_{*}))\right)+o(t)\geq 0.

Hence, dividing by
t
and letting
t\to 0
, one obtains

{\rm Tr}\left(\left(-\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{2}+\frac{\pi^{2}}{2}G_{*}^{2}+V_{*}\right)(G-G_{*})\right)\geq 0.

Proceeding as in
[
7
, Sect. 4.3.2]
and
[
8
, Proposition 3.8]
, set
H_{*}:=-\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{2}+V_{*}
and
L_{*}=H_{*}+\frac{\pi^{2}}{2}G_{*}^{2}
. Therefore,

{\rm Tr}(L_{*}G)\geq{\rm Tr}(L_{*}G_{*})
, for all
G\in{\mathcal{G}}
. This implies that
L_{*}
is bounded from below and

{\rm Tr}(L_{*}G_{*})=\inf\{{\rm Tr}(L_{*}G),\;G\in{\mathcal{G}}\}.

Set
\lambda_{*}=\inf\sigma(L_{*})
the bottom of the spectrum of
L_{*}
. Then,
(L_{*}-\lambda_{*})G_{*}=0
. Next, let us expand
G_{*}
as follows
G_{*}=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}g_{j}^{*}|\phi_{j}^{*}\rangle\langle\varphi_{j}^{*}|
, where
g_{j}\geq 0
for any
j\in\mathbb{N}
with
\sum_{j}g_{j}=Z
, and
\overline{\{\varphi_{j},\;j\in\mathbb{N}\}}=L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. One has
(L_{*}-\lambda_{*})\varphi_{j}^{*}=0
, for any
j\in\mathbb{N}
such that
g_{j}>0
. That is

H_{*}\varphi_{j}^{*}=\left(-\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{2}+V_{*}\right)\varphi_{j}^{*}=\left(\lambda_{*}-\frac{1}{2}(\pi g_{j}^{*})^{2}\right)\varphi_{j}^{*},\quad\text{once}\quad g_{j}>0.

Hence,
s_{j}:=\lambda_{*}-\frac{1}{2}(\pi g_{j}^{*})^{2}
belongs to the point spectrum of
H_{*}
. Conversely, if
s\in\sigma(H_{*})
such that
s<\lambda_{*}
, then
g=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\pi}\sqrt{\lambda_{*}-s}
is an eigenvalue of
G_{*}
. This yields
G_{*}=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\pi}\left(\lambda_{*}-H_{*}\right)_{+}^{1/2}
.
Note that
H_{*}
is a compact perturbation of
L_{*}
; thus they share the same essential spectrum. It follows that
H_{*}
cannot have essential spectrum below
\lambda_{*}
.
∎

5.
Numerical simulation

5.1.
Thomas-Fermi model

We perform numerical illustrations on the simple Thomas-Fermi model, which can be seen as a semi–classical limit of the rHF model. The kinetic energy in this model reads

{\mathcal{E}}^{\rm TF}_{\rm kin}(\rho)=c_{\rm TF}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\rho^{5/3}(x){\mathrm{d}}x,\quad\text{with}\quad c_{{\rm TF}}=\frac{3}{10}(3\pi^{2})^{2/3}.

Therefore, for a given
0\leq\mu\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, we define the Thomas-Fermi energy per unit length as

{\mathcal{E}}^{\rm TF}(\rho):=c_{\rm TF}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\rho^{\frac{5}{3}}(x){\mathrm{d}}x+\frac{1}{2}{\mathcal{D}}(\rho-\mu),

for every
\rho\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\rm TF}
, where

{\mathcal{C}}^{\rm TF}:=\{\rho\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap L^{5/3}(\mathbb{R}^{2}),\;\rho-\mu\in{\mathcal{C}}\}.

We point out that Thomas–Fermi model and its derivatives are widely considered in the literature, see
[
17
,
12
,
2
]
. The functional
{\mathcal{E}}^{\rm TF}
is stricltly convex and has a unique minimizer
\rho_{\rm TF}
that satisifes the following self–consistent equation

(5.1)

\begin{cases}\rho_{TF}=\left(\frac{3}{5c_{TF}}(\lambda-{\mathrm{V}}_{{\rm TF}})_{+}\right)^{3/2}\\
{\mathrm{V}}_{\rm TF}={\mathrm{V}}_{\rho_{\rm TF}-\mu}\end{cases},

with
\lambda
the Fermi level of the system is such that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\rho_{\rm TF}=Z:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\mu
.

We emphasize that we still use our expression for the potential energy
{\mathcal{D}}
and the mean–field potential in (
2.7
) which is involved in the self–consistent equation (
5.1
). We next show a numerical solution to (
5.1
). We also contrast the outcomes from this equation with the non-regularized reduced energy in which the Coulomb energy and potential are

(5.2)

{{\mathcal{D}}}_{2}(f,g)=-2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\log(|x-y|)f(x)g(y)\,{\mathrm{d}}x{\mathrm{d}}y

and

(5.3)

\widetilde{{\mathrm{V}}}_{f}(x)=-2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\log(|x-y|)f(y)\,{\mathrm{d}}y.

5.2.
Numerical schemes

Python is used to carry out the numerical simulation. The two-dimensional functions are evaluated on a two-dimensional grid representing
[-a,a]\times[-b,b]
, with
N_{a}\times N_{b}
discretization points (we took
a=b=8
and
N_{a}=N_{b}=41
). The various integrals inolved in the total energy are then approximated using quadrature techniques; we used the two-dimensional trapezoid rule.

We use two different methods in the regularized and non-regularized case. In the latter,
the total energy is approximated using a numerical integration method. The constraints of the model are simply the positivity of the density
\rho
along with the neutrality condition
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\rho=Z
. The resulting discretized problem is thus a finite-dimensional optimization problem on
\rho_{ij}
(the approximated values of the density on the grid points) subject to linear constraints. This problem is solved numerically in Python using the predefined function
minimize
from
Scipy
library.
In the regularized case, we apply an iterative process to solve equation (
5.1
) as follow:
at each iteration
n
, we evaluate the potential
{\mathrm{V}}_{n}:={\mathrm{V}}_{\rho_{n}-\mu}
using (
2.7
) and we compute
\lambda_{n}
solution to

(5.4)

Z_{n}(\lambda_{n})=0,\quad\text{where}\quad Z_{n}(\lambda)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left(\frac{3}{5c_{TF}}(\lambda-\Phi_{n})_{+}\right)^{3/2}-Z.

Note that, since
Z_{n}
is a non-decreasing function of
\lambda
, a simple dichotomy can be used to compute efficiently
\lambda_{n}
. We then update the density by setting

(5.5)

\widetilde{\rho_{n+1}}=\left(\frac{3}{5c_{TF}}(\lambda_{n}-\Phi_{n})_{+}\right)^{3/2}\quad\text{and}\quad\rho_{n+1}=t\widetilde{\rho_{n+1}}+(1-t)\rho_{n},

where
t\in[0,1]
is optimized to minimize the Thomas-Fermi energy (we use again the Python predefined function
minimize
). The algorithm is terminated when a tolerance
\varepsilon
is reached between two successively evaluated energies. We used
\varepsilon=10^{-5}
.

For the approximation of the regularized energy
\mathcal{D}(\rho-\mu)
by trapezoid rule, we use the formula (
2.8
) involving the potential, which is valid since
\rho-\mu
is neutral, instead of the one involving the Fourier transform.
