Principal minors of Gaussian orthogonal ensemble
Renjie Feng, Gang Tian, Dongyi Wei, Dong Yao

Abstract
. In this paper, we study the extremal process of the maxima of all the largest eigenvalues of principal minors of the classical Gaussian orthogonal ensemble (GOE). We prove that the fluctuation of the maxima is given by the Gumbel distribution in the limit. We also derive the limiting joint distribution of the maxima and the corresponding eigenvector, which implies that these two random variables are asymptotically independent.



1.
Introduction

Motivated by high-dimensional statistics and signal processing, the authors in
[
4
]
derived
the growth order of the extremal process of the maxima of all the largest eigenvalues of the principal minors of the classical random matrices of GOE and Wishart matrices, where the results have applications for the construction of the compressed sensing matrices as in
[
8
]
. In this paper, we further study the fluctuation of such maxima for the GOE case. Our main result is that the fluctuation is given by the Gumbel distribution with some Poisson structure involved in the limit, and we also derive the limiting joint distribution of such maxima and its corresponding eigenvector which indicates that these two random variables are asymptotically independent.

The Gaussian orthogonal ensemble (GOE) is the Gaussian measure defined on the space of real symmetric matrices, i.e.,
G=({g}_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq n}
is a
symmetric matrix whose upper triangular entries are independent real Gaussian variables with
the following distribution

\displaystyle{g}_{ij}\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\hbox{\footnotesize d}}}\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}N_{\mathbb{R}}(0,2)\ \text{if}\ i=j;\\
N_{\mathbb{R}}(0,1)\ \text{if}\ i<j.\end{array}\right.

Let
\lambda_{1}(G)>\lambda_{2}(G)>\cdots>\lambda_{n}(G)
be eigenvalues of GOE, then the distribution of these eigenvalues is invariant under the orthogonal group action and the joint density is

(1)

\frac{1}{Z_{n}}\prod_{k=1}^{n}e^{-\frac{1}{4}\lambda_{k}^{2}}\prod_{i<j}\big(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}\big),

where

(2)

Z_{n}=2^{n(n+1)/4}(2\pi)^{n/2}(n!)^{-1}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\Gamma(1+j/2)}{\Gamma(3/2)}

is the partition function. And the limit of the empirical measure of these eigenvalues is given by the classical semicircle law
[
1
]
.

Let’s first introduce some notations in order to present our main results. Given symmetric matrices
G=({g_{ij}})_{1\leq i\leq j\leq n}
sampled from GOE, for
\alpha\subset\{1,\cdots,n\}
with cardinality
|\alpha|=m\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, we denote
{G}_{\alpha}=({g}_{ij})_{i,j\in\alpha}
as the principal minors of
G
of size
m\times m
, then
G_{\alpha}
is also symmetric. Let
\lambda_{1}(G_{\alpha})>\lambda_{2}(G_{\alpha})>\cdots>\lambda_{m}(G_{\alpha})
be the ordered eigenvalues of
G_{\alpha}
. Now we define the extremal process of the maxima of all the largest eigenvalues of the principal minors as

\displaystyle{T}_{m,n}=\max_{\alpha\subset\{1,\cdots,n\},|\alpha|=m}\lambda_{1}({G}_{\alpha}).

In
[
4
]
, the authors studied the asymptotic properties of
{T}_{m,n}
and proved that under the assumption that
m
fixed or
m\to+\infty
with
m=o\Big(\frac{(\ln n)^{1/3}}{\ln\ln n}\Big)
, it holds

T_{m,n}-2\sqrt{m\ln n}\to 0

in probability as
n\to+\infty
.

In this paper, we further derive the fluctuation of
{T}_{m,n}
when
m
is fixed as
n
tends to infinity. Our first result is the following

Theorem 1
.

For
GOE
, we have the following convergence in distribution

(3)

{T}_{m,n}^{2}-4m\ln n-2(m-2)\ln\ln n\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{\hbox{\footnotesize d}}}Y

as
n\to+\infty
for fixed
m
, where the random
variable
Y
has the Gumbel distribution function

\displaystyle F_{Y}(y)=\exp(-c_{m}e^{-y/4}),\quad y\in\mathbb{R}.

Here, the constant

c_{m}=\frac{(2m)^{(m-2)/2}K_{m}}{(m-1)!2^{3/2}\Gamma(1+m/2)},

where
K_{m}=\mu(\mathcal{S}_{m})
is the probability of the event

(4)

\mathcal{S}_{m}:=\Big\{x\in S^{m-1}:\sum_{j\in\beta}x_{j}^{2}\leq\sqrt{k/m},\,\,\forall\,\beta\subset\{1,\cdots,m\}\,\,\text{with }\,\,\forall\,1\leq|\beta|=k<m\Big\}

under the uniform distribution
\mu
on the unit sphere
S^{m-1}
.
In particular,

c_{1}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}},\quad c_{2}=-\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}+\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\pi}\arcsin\left(\big(\frac{1}{2}\big)^{1/4}\right).

The following classical result in the theory of extremal processes is regarded as a special case of our main result. Let
a_{1},...,a_{n}
be i.i.d. Gaussian random variables
N_{\mathbb{R}}(0,2)
, for the extremal process

M_{n}:=\max\{a_{1},...,a_{n}\},

let

a_{n}=2\sqrt{\ln n}

and

b_{n}=2\sqrt{\ln n}-\frac{\ln\ln n+\ln(4\pi)}{2\sqrt{\ln n}}.

Then for any
y\in\mathbb{R}
, the following classical result holds (Theorem 1.5.3 in
[
6
]
)

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\mathbb{P}\left[a_{n}\left(M_{n}-b_{n}\right)\leq y\right]=e^{-e^{-y/2}}.

One can check that Theorem
1
for
T^{2}_{1,n}
when
m=1
is equivalent to this classical result for
M_{n}
. In this sense, our result is fundamental which is a natural generalization of such classical result for the extremal process of the scalar-valued random variables to the matrix-valued random variables with correlations.

The constant
K_{m}
appearing in the extremal index
c_{m}
represents the strength of the correlation between different principal minors. For a comprehensive overview of the extremal theory of stochastic processes, we refer to
[
6
,
7
]
.

Our proof for Theorem
1
also implies the joint distribution of the maxima of the largest eigenvalues of principal minors and its corresponding eigenvector. To be more precise,
given
n
, let
v^{*}\in S^{m-1}
be the unit eigenvector corresponding to the largest eigenvalue of the principal minor that attains the maxima
T_{m,n}
. By symmetry,
-v^{*}
is also the corresponding eigenvector. Now we have the following limit for the joint distribution of
(T_{m,n},v^{*})
.

Theorem 2
.

Given any
y\in\mathbb{R}
and symmetric Borel set
Q\subset S^{m-1}
such that
-Q=Q
, let
y_{m}^{2}=4m\ln n+2(m-2)\ln\ln n+y
,
then the joint distribution satisfies

(5)

\mathbb{P}(T_{m,n}>y_{m},v^{*}\in Q)\to(1-F_{Y}(y))\nu(Q)

as
n\to+\infty
, which implies that
T_{m,n}
and
v^{*}
are asymptotically independent.
Here,
\nu
is the uniform distribution on
\mathcal{S}_{m}
, i.e.,

\nu(Q)=\mu(Q\cap\mathcal{S}_{m})/\mu(\mathcal{S}_{m}).

The study of principal minors of random matrices holds significant importance and finds applications in various fields such as statistics, machine learning, deep learning, information theory, and more. In the following, we only list two of them.

In compressed sensing, one has to recover an input vector
f
from the corrupted measurements
y=Af+e
, where
A
is a coding matrix and
e
is an arbitrary and unknown vector of errors. The famous result by Candès-Tao
[
8
]
is that if the coding matrix
A
satisfies
the restricted isometry property (Definition 1.1 in
[
8
]
), then the input
f
is the unique solution to some
\ell_{1}
-minimization problem
provided that the support
S
(the number of nonzero entries) of errors
e
is not too large. Therefore, one of the major goals in compressed sensing is to construct the coding
matrix
A
that satisfies the restricted isometry property. In Section 3 of
[
8
]
, Candès-Tao proved that the Gaussian random matrices
A
can satisfy such property with overwhelming probability, and they can derive the estimate about the support
S
via the probabilistic estimate on the maxima of the largest eigenvalues of principal minors of Wishart matrices
A^{T}A
. A simple proof based on the concentration measure theory is present in
[
3
]
.

In statistics, the problem of finding the largest eigenvalue over principal minors (of some fixed order) of the sample covariance matrices is also called
sparse principal component analysis
.
Gamarnik and Li
[
5
, Section 6]
asked about the distribution of the largest eigenvalue as well as the algorithmic complexity of this problem, i.e., whether there exists efficient algorithms to find the principal minor which attains the (near-)optimal value for the largest eigenvalue. Consider the Wishart version of this problem, i.e.,
let
X
be an
n\times p
matrices with independent
N_{\mathbb{R}}(0,1)
distributed entries and take
R_{m,n,p}
to be the maximal eigenvalue of all
m
-th order principal minors of
X^{T}X
. It is proved in
[
4
]
that

\frac{R_{m,n,p}-n}{\sqrt{n}}-\sqrt{2m\log p}\to 0

in probability as
n\to\infty
, under conditions requiring
\log p=o(n^{1/2})
.
It seems that our method in the current paper can also be applied to study the Wishart case and it’s expected that some Gumbel fluctuation will be observed as well. We will postpone this to further investigations.

Notation.
In this paper,
c
,
C
and
C^{\prime}
stand for positive constants, but their values may change from line to line. For simplicity, the notation
a_{n}\sim b_{n}
means
\lim_{n\to+\infty}a_{n}/b_{n}=1
.

2.
Proof of Theorem
1

In this section, we will prove Theorem
1
by assuming some technical lemmas where the proofs of these lemmas are postponed to §
3
.

The proof of Theorem
1
is based on Lemma
1
with the proof given in
[
2
]
by the Stein-Chen method. It provides a criteria to prove the convergence of the total number of occurrences of the point process to the Poisson distribution, and thus it provides a method to derive the distribution for some extremal processes.

Lemma 1
.

Let
I
be an index set, and for
\alpha\in I
, let
X_{\alpha}
be a Bernoulli random variable with
p_{\alpha}=\mathbb{P}(X_{\alpha}=1)=1-\mathbb{P}(X_{\alpha}=0)
. For each
\alpha\in I
, let
N_{\alpha}
be a subset of
I
with
\alpha\in N_{\alpha}
, that is,
\alpha\in N_{\alpha}\subset I
. Let

S=\sum_{\alpha\in I}X_{\alpha},\,\,\lambda=\mathbb{E}S=\sum_{\alpha\in I}p_{\alpha}\in(0,+\infty),

let
Z
be the Poisson random variable with intensity
\mathbb{E}Z=\lambda
,
then it holds that

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{L}(S)-\mathcal{L}(Z)\|\leq 2(b_{1}+b_{2}+b_{3}),

and the probability of no occurrence has the estimate

\displaystyle|\mathbb{P}(S=0)-e^{-\lambda}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\mathbb{P}(X_{\alpha}=0,\ \forall\ \alpha\in I)-e^{-\lambda}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\min(1,\lambda^{-1})(b_{1}+b_{2}+b_{3}).

Here,
\|\mathcal{L}(S)-\mathcal{L}(Z)\|
is the total variation distance between the distributions
S
and
Z
,
and

\displaystyle b_{1}=\sum_{\alpha\in I}\sum_{\beta\in N_{\alpha}}p_{\alpha}p_{\beta},

\displaystyle b_{2}=\sum_{\alpha\in I}\sum_{\alpha\neq\beta\in N_{\alpha}}\mathbb{E}[X_{\alpha}X_{\beta}],

\displaystyle b_{3}=\sum_{\alpha\in I}\mathbb{E}|\mathbb{E}[X_{\alpha}|\sigma(X_{\beta},\beta\not\in N_{\alpha})]-p_{\alpha}|,

and
\sigma(X_{\beta},\beta\not\in N_{\alpha})
is the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
\{X_{\beta},\beta\not\in N_{\alpha}\}.
In particular, if
X_{\alpha}
is
independent of
\{X_{\beta},\beta\not\in N_{\alpha}\}
for each
\alpha
, then
b_{3}=0
.

One may think of
N_{\alpha}
as a ‘neighborhood of dependence’ for
\alpha
such that
X_{\alpha}

is independent or nearly independent of all of
X_{\beta}
for
\beta\notin N_{\alpha}
. And Lemma
1
indicates that when
b_{1}
,
b_{2}
and
b_{3}
are all small enough, then
S
which is the total number of occurrences tends to a Poisson distribution.

2.1.
Proof of Theorem
1

Given any
\ell\times\ell
symmetric matrix
S
, we rearrange the eigenvalues of
S
in descending order
\lambda_{1}(S)\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{\ell}(S)
, and we denote

(6)

|S|^{2}:=\Tr S^{2}

and

(7)

\lambda_{1}^{*}(S):=[(\lambda_{1}(S)^{2}+|S|^{2})/2]^{\frac{1}{2}}.

For fixed
m\geq 2
, we define the index set

I_{m}=\{\alpha\subset\{1,\cdots,n\},|\alpha|=m\}

and the neighborhood set

N_{\alpha}=\{\beta\in I_{m}:\alpha\cap\beta\neq\emptyset\}\,\,\,\mbox{for}\,\,\,\alpha\in I_{m}.

Throughout the article, for any fixed real number
y
,
we define

y_{k}^{2}:=4k\ln n+2k\ln\ln n,\ y_{k}>0,\ 1\leq k<m

and

y_{m}^{2}:=4m\ln n+2(m-2)\ln\ln n+y,\ y_{m}>0.

For symmetric matrices
G=({g_{ij}})_{1\leq i\leq j\leq n}
sampled from GOE, for
\alpha\in I_{m}
where
|\alpha|=m
, we denote
{G}_{\alpha}=({g}_{ij})_{i,j\in\alpha}
as the principal minor of size
m\times m
and we define the event

(8)

\displaystyle A_{\alpha}=\Big\{\lambda_{1}({G}_{\alpha})>y_{m};\ \ \lambda_{1}^{*}({G}_{\beta})\leq y_{k},\ \forall\ 1\leq k<m,\ \beta\subset\alpha,\ |\beta|=k\Big\}.

Recall the definition of
{T}_{m,n}
in §
1
, we first have

(9)

\begin{split}0&\leq\mathbb{P}(\cap_{\alpha\in I_{m}}A_{\alpha}^{c})-\mathbb{P}({T}_{m,n}\leq y_{m})\\
&\leq\mathbb{P}(\lambda_{1}^{*}({G}_{\beta})>y_{k}\ \text{for some}\ \beta\in I_{k},\ 1\leq k<m)\\
&\leq\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}\sum_{\beta\in I_{k}}\mathbb{P}(\lambda_{1}^{*}({G}_{\beta})>y_{k})\\
&=\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}{n\choose k}\mathbb{P}(\lambda_{1}^{*}({G}_{\{1,\cdots,k\}})>y_{k}).\end{split}

Now we need the following lemma and we postpone its proof to the next section.

Lemma 2
.

For fixed
k\geq
1, there exists a constant
C>0
(depending on
k
) so that for all
x>1
,

(10)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|{G}_{\{1,\cdots,k\}}|^{2}>x^{2})\leq Cx^{k(k+1)/2-2}e^{-x^{2}/4},

(11)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\lambda_{1}({G}_{\{1,\cdots,k\}})>x)\leq Cx^{k-2}e^{-x^{2}/4},

(12)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\lambda_{1}^{*}({G}_{\{1,\cdots,k\}})>x)\leq Cx^{k-2}e^{-x^{2}/4}.

Using (
12
) we have

(13)

\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}{n\choose k}\mathbb{P}(\lambda_{1}^{*}({G}_{\{1,\cdots,k\}})>y_{k})\leq C\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}n^{k}y_{k}^{k-2}e^{-y_{k}^{2}/4}\leq C/\ln n.

Combining this with (
9
) we get

(14)

0\leq\mathbb{P}(\cap_{\alpha\in I_{m}}A_{\alpha}^{c})-\mathbb{P}({T}_{m,n}\leq y_{m})\leq C/\ln n,

and thus we have

(15)

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\mathbb{P}({T}_{m,n}\leq y_{m})=\lim_{n\to+\infty}\mathbb{P}(\cap_{\alpha\in I_{m}}A_{\alpha}^{c}).

Therefore, it’s enough to derive the limit of
\mathbb{P}(\cap_{\alpha\in I_{m}}A_{\alpha}^{c})
to prove Theorem
1
.
By Lemma
1
, we have

(16)

|\mathbb{P}(\cap_{\alpha\in I_{m}}A_{\alpha}^{c})-e^{-t_{n}}|\leq b_{n,1}+b_{n,2},

where

\displaystyle t_{n}={n\choose m}\mathbb{P}(A_{\{1,\cdots,m\}}),

\displaystyle b_{n,1}\leq{n\choose m}\left({n\choose m}-{n-m\choose m}\right)\mathbb{P}(A_{\{1,\cdots,m\}})^{2},

\displaystyle b_{n,2}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}{n\choose k}{n-k\choose m-k}{n-m\choose m-k}\mathbb{P}(A_{\{1,\cdots,k,\cdots,m\}}\cap A_{\{1,\cdots,k,m+1,\cdots,2m-k\}}).

Using (
11
) we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(A_{\{1,\cdots,m\}})\leq\mathbb{P}(\lambda_{1}({G}_{\{1,\cdots,m\}})>y_{m})\leq Cy_{m}^{m-2}e^{-y_{m}^{2}/4}\leq Cn^{-m}.

And thus we have

\displaystyle b_{n,1}\leq Cn^{2m-1}\mathbb{P}(A_{\{1,\cdots,m\}})^{2}\leq C^{\prime}n^{-1},

which tends to
0
in the limit.

It remains to find the limit of
t_{n}
and show that
b_{n,2}
tends to
0
in order to complete the proof of Theorem
1
.

Let
\alpha=\{1,\cdots,m\},\gamma=\{m-k+1,\cdots,m\},\zeta=\{m-k+1,\cdots,2m-k\}
, then
\alpha\cap\zeta=\gamma
,
|\alpha|=|\zeta|=m
and
|\gamma|=k
. By rearranging the indices, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(A_{\{1,\cdots,k,\cdots,m\}}\cap A_{\{1,\cdots,k,m+1,\cdots,2m-k\}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(A_{\{1,\cdots,m\}}\cap A_{\{m-k+1,\cdots,2m-k\}})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\lambda_{1}({G}_{\alpha})>y_{m},\ \lambda_{1}({G}_{\zeta})>y_{m},

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}^{*}({G}_{\alpha\setminus\gamma})\leq y_{m-k},\ \lambda_{1}^{*}({G}_{\zeta\setminus\gamma})\leq y_{m-k},\ \lambda_{1}^{*}({G}_{\gamma})\leq y_{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{P}(\lambda_{1}({G}_{\alpha})>y_{m},\ \lambda_{1}({G}_{\zeta})>y_{m},\ \lambda_{1}^{*}({G}_{\alpha\setminus\gamma})\leq y_{m-k},\ \lambda_{1}^{*}({G}_{\zeta\setminus\gamma})\leq y_{m-k}|{G}_{\gamma})

\displaystyle\times\mathbf{1}_{\{\lambda_{1}^{*}({G}_{\gamma})\leq y_{k}\}}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{P}(\lambda_{1}({G}_{\alpha})>y_{m},\ \lambda_{1}^{*}({G}_{\alpha\setminus\gamma})\leq y_{m-k}|{G}_{\gamma})^{2}\mathbf{1}_{\{\lambda_{1}^{*}({G}_{\gamma})\leq y_{k}\}}].

The following lemma will imply that
b_{n,2}
tends to
0
as
n\to+\infty
.

Lemma 3
.

For
\alpha\in I_{m},\ \gamma\subset\alpha,\ |\gamma|=k,\ 1\leq k<m,\ \beta=\alpha\setminus\gamma,\ x>1,\ \delta,\delta^{\prime}\in(0,1)
,
then there are some constants
C
and
C^{\prime}
(depending on
m
,
\delta
and
\delta^{\prime}
) such that

(17)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\lambda_{1}({G}_{\alpha})>x|{G}_{\beta},{G}_{\gamma})\mathbf{1}_{\{\lambda_{1}^{*}(G_{\beta})\leq(1-\delta)x,\lambda_{1}^{*}(G_{\gamma})\leq(1-\delta^{\prime})x\}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle Cx^{-1}(x/(\lambda_{1}^{*}(G_{\beta})+1)+x/(\lambda_{1}^{*}(G_{\gamma})+1))^{k(m-k)-1}e^{-(x-\lambda_{1}^{*}(G_{\beta}))(x-\lambda_{1}^{*}(G_{\gamma}))/2}

and

(18)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\lambda_{1}({G}_{\alpha})>x,\ \lambda_{1}^{*}({G}_{\beta})\leq(1-\delta)x|{G}_{\gamma})\mathbf{1}_{\{\lambda_{1}^{*}(G_{\gamma})\leq(1-\delta^{\prime})x\}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C^{\prime}x^{m-k-2}(x/(\lambda_{1}^{*}(G_{\gamma})+1))^{k(m-k)-1}e^{((\lambda_{1}^{*}(G_{\gamma}))^{2}-x^{2})/4}.

By assuming Lemma
3
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{P}(\lambda_{1}({G}_{\alpha})>y_{m},\ \lambda_{1}^{*}({G}_{\alpha\setminus\gamma})\leq y_{m-k}|{G}_{\gamma})^{2}\mathbf{1}_{\{\lambda_{1}^{*}({G}_{\gamma})\leq y_{k}\}}]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\mathbb{E}[y_{m}^{2m-2k-4}(y_{m}/(\lambda_{1}^{*}(G_{\gamma})+1))^{2k(m-k)-2}e^{((\lambda_{1}^{*}(G_{\gamma}))^{2}-y_{m}^{2})/2}\mathbf{1}_{\{\lambda_{1}^{*}({G}_{\gamma})\leq y_{k}\}}]

(19)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle:C\mathbb{E}[f(\lambda_{1}^{*}(G_{\gamma})))\mathbf{1}_{\{\lambda_{1}^{*}({G}_{\gamma})\leq y_{k}\}}]

where we define

(20)

f(t)=y_{m}^{2m-2k-4}(y_{m}/(t+1))^{2k(m-k)-2}e^{(t^{2}-y_{m}^{2})/2},\,\,\,t\geq 0.

Integration by parts, we have

(21)

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}(f(\lambda_{1}^{*}(G_{\gamma}))\mathbf{1}_{\lambda_{1}^{*}(G_{\gamma})\leq y_{k}})=&\int_{0}^{y_{k}}f^{\prime}(t)\mathbb{P}(\lambda_{1}^{*}(G_{\gamma})>t)dt\\
&-f(y_{k})\mathbb{P}(\lambda_{1}^{*}(G_{\gamma})>y_{k})+f(0).\end{split}

Note that

(22)

\begin{split}f^{\prime}(t)&=-(2k(m-k)-2)(t+1)^{-1}f(t)+tf(t)\leq tf(t).\\
\end{split}

Hence, using (
12
) for
t>1
and (
22
), we have

(23)

\begin{split}&\int_{0}^{y_{k}}f^{\prime}(t)\mathbb{P}(\lambda_{1}^{*}(G_{\gamma})>t)dt\\
\leq&\int_{0}^{1}tf(t)dt+\int_{1}^{y_{k}}f^{\prime}(t)\mathbb{P}(\lambda_{1}^{*}(G_{\gamma})>t)dt\\
\leq&\max_{0\leq t\leq 1}f(t)+C\int_{1}^{y_{k}}ty_{m}^{2m-2k-4}(y_{m}/(t+1))^{2k(m-k)-2}e^{(t^{2}-y_{m}^{2})/2}t^{k-2}e^{-t^{2}/4}dt.\end{split}

Therefore, by (
21
) we further have

(24)

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}(f(\lambda_{1}^{*}(G_{\gamma}))\mathbf{1}_{\lambda_{1}^{*}(G_{\gamma})\leq y_{k}})\leq 2\max_{0\leq t\leq 1}f(t)\\
&+C\int_{1}^{y_{k}}ty_{m}^{2m-2k-4}(y_{m}/(t+1))^{2k(m-k)-2}e^{(t^{2}-y_{m}^{2})/2}t^{k-2}e^{-t^{2}/4}dt.\end{split}

We now separate this integration into
1<t<y_{k}/2
and
y_{k}/2<t<y_{k}
. For
1<t<y_{k}/2
the integrand is bounded by

Cy_{m}^{2m-k-6}y_{m}^{2k(m-k)-1}e^{y_{k}^{2}/16-y_{m}^{2}/2},

where we used the fact that
y_{k}\leq y_{m}
for
n
large enough.
For
y_{k}/2<t<y_{k}
, we can bound the integrand by

Cy_{m}^{2m-k-6}te^{t^{2}/4-y_{m}^{2}/2},

where we used the fact that
y_{m}/y_{k}\leq 2\sqrt{m/k}
as
n
large enough. Therefore, as
n
large enough, we have

(25)

\begin{split}&\int_{1}^{y_{k}}f^{\prime}(t)\mathbb{P}(\lambda_{1}^{*}(G_{\beta})>t)dt\\
\leq&Cy_{m}^{2m-k-6}e^{-y_{m}^{2}/2}\left(\int_{1}^{y_{k}/2}y_{m}^{2k(m-k)-1}e^{y_{k}^{2}/16}dt+\int_{y_{k}/2}^{y_{k}}te^{t^{2}/4}dt\right)\\
\leq&Cy_{m}^{2m-k-6}e^{-y_{m}^{2}/2}\left(y_{m}^{2k(m-k)}e^{y_{k}^{2}/16}+e^{y_{k}^{2}/4}\right)\\
\leq&Cy_{m}^{2m-k-6}e^{y_{k}^{2}/4-y_{m}^{2}/2},\end{split}

where in the last inequality we used the fact that
y_{m}^{2k(m-k)}e^{-3y_{k}^{2}/16}
can be bounded from above uniformly for all
n
.

The definition of
f(t)
in (
20
) and the fact that
y_{m}/y_{k}\sim\sqrt{m/k}
imply

(26)

\max_{0\leq t\leq 1}f(t)\leq y_{m}^{2m-2k-4}y_{m}^{2k(m-k)-2}e^{(1-y_{m}^{2})/2}\leq Cy_{m}^{2m-k-6}e^{y_{k}^{2}/4-y_{m}^{2}/2}

as
n
large enough.
It follows from (
19
), (
24
), (
25
) and (
26
) that

(27)

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{P}(\lambda_{1}({G}_{\alpha})>y_{m},\ \lambda_{1}^{*}({G}_{\alpha\setminus\gamma})\leq y_{m-k}|{G}_{\gamma})^{2}\mathbf{1}_{\{\lambda_{1}^{*}({G}_{\gamma})\leq y_{k}\}}]\\
\leq&Cy_{m}^{2m-k-6}e^{y_{k}^{2}/4-y_{m}^{2}/2}\end{split}

as
n
large enough.
Therefore, we have

(28)

\begin{split}b_{n,2}&\leq\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}{n\choose k}{n-k\choose m-k}{n-m\choose m-k}\mathbb{P}(A_{\{1,\cdots,k,\cdots,m\}}\cap A_{\{1,\cdots,k,m+1,\cdots,2m-k\}})\\
&\leq\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}n^{2m-k}\mathbb{P}(A_{\{1,\cdots,m\}}\cap A_{\{1,\cdots,k,m+1,\cdots,2m-k\}})\\
&\leq C\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}n^{2m-k}y_{m}^{2m-k-6}e^{y_{k}^{2}/4-y_{m}^{2}/2}\\
&\leq C\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}y_{m}^{2m-k-6}(\ln n)^{k/2-m+2}\leq C(\ln n)^{-1},\end{split}

which tends to
0
as
n\to+\infty
.

The following lemma gives the limit of
t_{n}
.

Lemma 4
.

We have the limit

(29)

\lim_{n\to+\infty}t_{n}=c_{m}e^{-y/4},

where

c_{m}=\frac{(2m)^{(m-2)/2}K_{m}}{(m-1)!2^{3/2}\Gamma(1+m/2)},

where

is the constant defined in Theorem
1

By assuming Lemma
4

and

By (

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\mathbb{P}(T_{m,n}\leq y_{m})=\exp(-c_{m}e^{-y/4}),

which proves Theorem

\lambda_{1}
large enough, (
69
) can be further bounded from above by (using (
31
))

C\lambda_{1}^{m-1}\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}^{m-2}\exp(-\lambda_{1}/4)\leq C^{\prime}\lambda_{1}^{m-2}.

Combining the estimates for
I_{5}
and
I_{6}
we get

g(\lambda_{1})\leq Z_{m-1}(\lambda_{1}+\sqrt{\lambda_{1}})^{m-1}+C\lambda_{1}^{m-2}

for
\lambda_{1}
large enough.
It follows that

\limsup_{\lambda_{1}\to+\infty}\frac{g(\lambda_{1})}{\lambda_{1}^{m-1}}\leq Z_{m-1}.

For the lower bound, for all
\lambda_{1}>1
we have

g(\lambda_{1})\geq(\lambda_{1}-\sqrt{\lambda_{1}})^{m-1}\int_{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}\geq\lambda_{2}>\cdots>\lambda_{m}}e^{-\sum\limits_{i=2}^{m}\lambda_{i}^{2}/4}\prod_{2\leq i<j\leq m}|\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}|d\lambda_{2}\cdots d\lambda_{m}.

It follows that

\liminf_{\lambda_{1}\to+\infty}\frac{g(\lambda_{1})}{\lambda_{1}^{m-1}}\geq\liminf_{\lambda_{1}\to+\infty}\int_{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}\geq\lambda_{2}>\cdots>\lambda_{m}}e^{-\sum\limits_{i=2}^{m}\lambda_{i}^{2}/4}\prod_{2\leq i<j\leq m}|\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}|d\lambda_{2}\cdots d\lambda_{m}=Z_{m-1}.

Then (
67
) follows from the upper and lower bounds.

Therefore, as
n\to+\infty
, i.e.,
y_{m}\to+\infty
, we have

(70)

\int_{\lambda_{1}>y_{m}}e^{-\lambda_{1}^{2}/4}g(\lambda_{1})d\lambda_{1}\sim Z_{m-1}\int_{\lambda_{1}>y_{m}}e^{-\lambda_{1}^{2}/4}\lambda_{1}^{m-1}d\lambda_{1}.

Standard results for the upper incomplete Gamma function imply

(71)

\begin{split}\int_{\lambda_{1}>y_{m}}e^{-\lambda_{1}^{2}/4}\lambda_{1}^{m-1}d\lambda_{1}&=\int_{s\geq y_{m}^{2}/4}e^{-s}2^{m-1}s^{(m-2)/2}ds\\
&\sim 2^{m-1}e^{-y_{m}^{2}/4}(y_{m}^{2}/4)^{(m-2)/2}\\
&=2e^{-y_{m}^{2}/4}y_{m}^{m-2}.\end{split}

Combining (
66
), (
70
) and (
71
) we get

(72)

\begin{split}&\mathbb{P}(\lambda_{1}(G_{\alpha})>y_{m})\\
\sim&\frac{Z_{m-1}}{Z_{m}}\int_{\lambda_{1}>y_{m}}e^{-\lambda_{1}^{2}/4}\lambda_{1}^{m-1}d\lambda_{1}\\
\sim&(Z_{m-1}/{Z_{m}})2y_{m}^{m-2}e^{-y_{m}^{2}/4}\\
=&m(2\pi)^{-1/2}\Gamma(3/2)/\Gamma(1+m/2)\cdot 2^{1-m/2}y_{m}^{m-2}e^{-y_{m}^{2}/4}\\
=&m2^{-(1+m)/2}/\Gamma(1+m/2)\cdot y_{m}^{m-2}e^{-y_{m}^{2}/4}.\end{split}

This completes the proof of (
64
).

Now we prove (
65
). We define the following two auxiliary events:

B_{\alpha}:=\Big\{y_{m}<\lambda_{1}({G}_{\alpha})<y_{m}+1,\lambda_{2}(G_{\alpha})<\sqrt{\log\log n},\lambda_{m}(G_{\alpha})>-\sqrt{\log\log n}\Big\}

and

D_{\alpha}:=\Big\{y_{m}<\lambda_{1}(G_{\alpha})<y_{m}+1\Big\}.

Then it holds that

B_{\alpha}\subset D_{\alpha}\subset\Big\{\lambda_{1}(G_{\alpha})>y_{m}\Big\}.

We first show that

(73)

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\mathbb{P}(B_{\alpha}|\lambda_{1}(G_{\alpha})>y_{m})=1.

Note that (
73
) follows if we can prove the following two limits,

(74)

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\mathbb{P}(D_{\alpha}|\lambda_{1}(G_{\alpha})>y_{m})=1

and

(75)

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\mathbb{P}(B_{\alpha}|D_{\alpha})=1.

By (
64
), we find that

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\frac{\mathbb{P}(\lambda_{1}(G_{\alpha})>y_{m}+1)}{\mathbb{P}(\lambda_{1}(G_{\alpha})>y_{m})}=0,

which implies (
74
).
We now prove (
75
) by finding
a sequence of numbers
\epsilon_{n}\to 0
such that for
n
large enough it holds that

(76)

\frac{\mathbb{P}(\lambda_{2}(G_{\alpha})>\sqrt{\log\log n}\mbox{ or }\lambda_{m}(G_{\alpha})<-\sqrt{\log\log n}|D_{\alpha})}{\mathbb{P}(\lambda_{2}(G_{\alpha})<1,\lambda_{m}(G_{\alpha})>0|D_{\alpha})}\leq\epsilon_{n}.

Indeed, (
76
) implies that

(77)

\mathbb{P}(\lambda_{2}(G_{\alpha})>\sqrt{\log\log n}\mbox{ or }\lambda_{m}(G_{\alpha})<-\sqrt{\log\log n}|D_{\alpha})\leq\frac{\epsilon_{n}}{\epsilon_{n}+1},

which converges to 0 as
n\to+\infty
, and thus this yields (
75
). To prove (
76
), we divide the set

\Big\{\lambda_{2}(G_{\alpha})>\sqrt{\log\log n}\mbox{ or }\lambda_{m}(G_{\alpha})<-\sqrt{\log\log n}\Big\}
as the union of two disjoint subsets
S_{1}\cup S_{2}
as follows. On the subset
S_{1}:=\Big\{\lambda_{m}(G_{\alpha})\leq-\sqrt{\log\log n}\Big\}
, if we condition on
D_{\alpha}
, we have

(78)

\begin{split}&\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq m}(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j})\\
\leq&C(\lambda_{1}+\left|\lambda_{m}\right|)^{m-1}(\left|\lambda_{2}\right|+\left|\lambda_{m}\right|)^{(m-1)(m-2)/2}\\
\leq&C\lambda_{1}^{m-1}\left|\lambda_{m}\right|^{m-1}(\left|\lambda_{2}\right|+1)^{(m-1)(m-2)/2}\left|\lambda_{m}\right|^{(m-1)(m-2)/2}\\
=&C\lambda_{1}^{m-1}\left|\lambda_{m}\right|^{(m-1)m/2}(\left|\lambda_{2}\right|+1)^{(m-1)(m-2)/2}.\end{split}

On the subset
S_{2}:=\Big\{\lambda_{2}(G_{\alpha})>\sqrt{\log\log n},\lambda_{m}(G_{\alpha})>-\sqrt{\log\log n}\Big\}
, if we condition on
D_{\alpha}
where
\lambda_{1}(G_{\alpha})\sim 2\sqrt{m\log n}
, then we easily have the upper bound

\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq m}(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j})\leq C\lambda_{1}^{m-1}\lambda_{2}^{(m-1)(m-2)/2}.

It follows that the
left hand side of (
76
) can be bounded from above by the summation of

\frac{C[\int_{y_{m}}^{y_{m}+1}\lambda_{1}^{m-1}e^{-\lambda_{1}^{2}/4}d\lambda_{1}]\int_{\lambda_{m}<-\sqrt{\log\log n}}\left|\lambda_{m}\right|^{(m-1)m/2}(\left|\lambda_{2}\right|+1)^{(m-1)(m-2)/2}e^{-\sum_{i=2}^{m}\lambda_{i}^{2}/4}d\lambda_{2}\cdots d\lambda_{m}}{[\int_{y_{m}}^{y_{m}+1}(\lambda_{1}-1)^{m-1}e^{-\lambda_{1}^{2}/4}d\lambda_{1}]\int_{1>\lambda_{2}>\cdots>\lambda_{m}>0}\prod_{2\leq i<j\leq m}(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j})e^{-\sum_{i=2}^{m}\lambda_{i}^{2}/4}d\lambda_{2}\cdots d\lambda_{m}}

and

\frac{C[\int_{y_{m}}^{y_{m}+1}\lambda_{1}^{m-1}e^{-\lambda_{1}^{2}/4}d\lambda_{1}]\int_{\lambda_{2}>\sqrt{\log\log n},\lambda_{m}>-\sqrt{\log\log n}}\lambda_{2}^{(m-2)(m-1)/2}e^{-\sum_{i=2}^{m}\lambda_{i}^{2}/4}d\lambda_{2}\cdots d\lambda_{m}}{[\int_{y_{m}}^{y_{m}+1}(\lambda_{1}-1)^{m-1}e^{-\lambda_{1}^{2}/4}d\lambda_{1}]\int_{1>\lambda_{2}>\cdots>\lambda_{m}>0}\prod_{2\leq i<j\leq m}(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j})e^{-\sum_{i=2}^{m}\lambda_{i}^{2}/4}d\lambda_{2}\cdots d\lambda_{m}}.

The above summation can be further bounded from above by

\begin{split}&C\Big(\int_{\lambda_{m}<-\sqrt{\log\log n}}\left|\lambda_{m}\right|^{(m-1)m/2}\exp(-\lambda_{m}^{2}/4)d\lambda_{m}\\
&+\int_{\lambda_{2}>\sqrt{\log\log n}}\lambda_{2}^{(m-1)(m-2)/2}\exp(-\lambda_{2}^{2}/4)d\lambda_{2}\Big)\\
&:=\epsilon_{n}\end{split}

as
n
large enough.
Clearly it holds that
\epsilon_{n}\to 0
since
\sqrt{\log\log n}\to+\infty
. This completes the proof of
(
76
) and thus the proof of (
73
).

Now we are ready to prove (
65
).
We define the event

H_{\alpha}=\Big\{\lambda_{1}^{*}({G}_{\beta})\leq y_{k},\ \forall\ 1\leq k<m,\ \beta\subset\alpha,\ |\beta|=k\Big\}.

Using (
73
), we see that (
65
) is equivalent to

(79)

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\mathbb{P}(H_{\alpha}|B_{\alpha})=K_{m}.

To prove (
79
) we need to use the fact that

\displaystyle{G}_{\alpha}\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\hbox{\footnotesize d}}}U^{T}\diag(\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{m})U\,\,

where
|\alpha|=m
and

U\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\hbox{\footnotesize d}}}\mathcal{U}(O(m))

is sampled from the uniform measure on the orthogonal group
O(m)
which is independent of
(\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{m})
and

(\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{m})\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\hbox{\footnotesize d}}}(\lambda_{1}({G}_{\alpha}),\cdots,\lambda_{m}({G}_{\alpha})).

Given any

\displaystyle X:=U^{T}\diag(\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{m})U,\ U\in O(m),\ \lambda_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{m},

by definition it holds

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(X)=\lambda_{1},\ \lambda_{1}^{*}(X)^{2}=\lambda_{1}^{2}+\sum_{k=2}^{m}\lambda_{k}^{2}/2.

Let
\lambda_{1}^{-}(X):=\sqrt{\sum_{k=2}^{m}\lambda_{k}^{2}}=\sqrt{|X|^{2}-\lambda_{1}^{2}(X)},\ X_{1}:=U^{T}\diag(\lambda_{1},0,\cdots,0)U,\ X_{2}:=X-X_{1},
then we have
|X_{2}|(=\sqrt{\mathrm{Tr(X_{2}^{2})}})=\lambda_{1}^{-}(X)
.
For
\beta\subset\alpha=\{1,\cdots,m\}
we have

(80)

|\lambda_{1}^{*}(X_{\beta})-\lambda_{1}^{*}((X_{1})_{\beta})|\leq|(X_{2})_{\beta}|\leq|X_{2}|=\lambda_{1}^{-}(X).

Here, by the Lipschitz continuity of eigenvalues for symmetric matrices (see Corollary A.6 in
[
1
]
), we can further derive the fact that

|\lambda_{1}^{*}(A)-\lambda_{1}^{*}(B)|\leq|A-B|.

Since
(X_{1})_{\beta}
is a matrix of rank at most
1
, we have

(81)

\lambda_{1}^{*}((X_{1})_{\beta})=(\Tr((X_{1})_{\beta}^{2}))^{1/2}=\left|\lambda_{1}\right|\sum_{k\in\beta}u_{k}^{2},

here
\mathbf{u}=(u_{1},\cdots,u_{m})
is the first row of

. Note that
\mathbf{u}
has the uniform distribution on the unit sphere
S^{m-1}
.
By (
80
) and (
81
), we have

(82)

\Big|\lambda_{1}^{*}(X_{\beta})-\left|\lambda_{1}\right|\sum_{k\in\beta}u_{k}^{2}\Big|\leq\lambda_{1}^{-}(X).

Now we replace
X
by
G_{\alpha}
. On the event

we have

\lambda_{1}^{-}(G_{\alpha})\leq\sqrt{m\log\log n}\mbox{ and }\,\,\,y_{m}<\lambda_{1}<y_{m}+1,

which together with (

) imply

(83)

y_{m}\sum_{k\in\beta}u_{k}^{2}-\sqrt{m\log\log n}\leq\lambda_{1}^{*}(G_{\beta})\leq(y_{m}+1)\sum_{k\in\beta}u_{k}^{2}+\sqrt{m\log\log n}

for all
\beta\subset\alpha
.

By (
83
) and the fact that the first row
\mathbf{u}
of the orthogonal group are independent of the eigenvalues

\lambda_{i}(G_{\alpha}),1\leq i\leq m
, we have

(84)

\begin{split}&\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{k\in\beta}u_{k}^{2}\leq\frac{y_{k}-\sqrt{m\log\log n}}{y_{m}+1},\forall\beta\subset\alpha,\left|\beta\right|=k\right)\\
\leq&\mathbb{P}(H_{\alpha}|B_{\alpha})\\
\leq&\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{k\in\beta}u_{k}^{2}\leq\frac{y_{k}+\sqrt{m\log\log n}}{y_{m}},\forall\beta\subset\alpha,\left|\beta\right|=k\right).\end{split}

This together with the facts that

and
y_{m}\sim 2\sqrt{m\log n}
as

imply

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\mathbb{P}(H_{\alpha}|B_{\alpha})=\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{k\in\beta}u_{k}^{2}\leq\sqrt{k/m},\forall\beta\in\alpha,\left|\beta\right|=k\right),

which is exactly the definition of
K_{m}
. This proves (
79
), and hence (
65
). Therefore, we complete the proof of Lemma
4
.

Proof of Theorem

Now we prove Theorem
2
. As before, for

sampled from GOE, we denote

as the principal minor of size

for

Let

be the eigenvector of the largest eigenvalue of

and

As in §

, we recall the definition of the event

We define the random variable

Otherwise, we set

.
We now set

\hat{v}:=v_{1}(G_{\hat{\alpha}}).

In other words,
\hat{v}
is the eigenvector of the largest eigenvalue of the principal sub-matrix
G_{\alpha}
that achieves the maximal eigenvalue under the constraint that

is true. By (

) and (

), we have

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\mathbb{P}(\cap_{\alpha\in I_{m}}H_{\alpha})=1.

On the event
\cap_{\alpha\in I_{m}}H_{\alpha}
we clearly have
\hat{\alpha}=\alpha^{*}
and
\hat{v}=v^{*}
since the constraint for
\hat{\alpha}
doesn’t have any effect.
Recall the definition of

in (

, on

coincide.
In other words, for symmetric

, we have

\begin{split}\left(\cap_{\alpha\in I_{m}}H_{\alpha}\right)\cap\{T_{G,m,n}\geq y_{m},v^{*}\in Q\}=\left(\cap_{\alpha\in I_{m}}H_{\alpha}\right)\cap\left\{\cup_{\alpha\in I_{m}}A_{\alpha},\hat{v}\in Q\right\}.\end{split}

It follows that

(89)

\begin{split}&\left|\mathbb{P}(T_{m,n}\geq y_{m},v^{*}\in Q)-\mathbb{P}(\cup_{\alpha\in I_{m}}A_{\alpha},\hat{v}\in Q)\right|\\
=&\left|\mathbb{P}\left(\left(\cap_{\alpha\in I_{m}}H_{\alpha}\right)^{c}\cap\{T_{m,n}\geq y_{m},v^{*}\in Q\}\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(\left(\cap_{\alpha\in I_{m}}H_{\alpha}\right)^{c}\cap\left(\cup_{\alpha\in I_{m}}A_{\alpha},\hat{v}\in Q\right)\right)\right|\\
\leq&2\mathbb{P}\left(\left(\cap_{\alpha\in I_{m}}H_{\alpha}\right)^{c}\right),\end{split}

which converges to 0 as
n\to+\infty
. Hence, (
5
) is equivalent to the limit

(90)

\mathbb{P}(\cup_{\alpha\in I_{m}}A_{\alpha},\hat{v}\in Q)\to(1-F_{Y}(y))\nu(Q).

All of the rest is to prove this convergence. Now we define four quantities

(91)

\begin{split}&k_{1}(\alpha)=\mathbb{P}(A_{\alpha},v_{1}(G_{\alpha})\in Q;\forall\alpha^{\prime}\neq\alpha,A_{\alpha^{\prime}}\mbox{ fails or }\lambda_{1}(G_{\alpha^{\prime}})<\lambda_{1}(G_{\alpha})),\\
&k_{2}(\alpha)=\mathbb{P}(A_{\alpha},v_{1}(G_{\alpha})\in Q;\forall\alpha^{\prime}\cap\alpha=\emptyset,A_{\alpha^{\prime}}\mbox{ fails or }\lambda_{1}(G_{\alpha^{\prime}})<\lambda_{1}(G_{\alpha})),\\

We note that

(92)

\begin{split}&\mathbb{P}\left(\cup_{\alpha\in I_{m}}A_{\alpha},\hat{v}\in Q\right)\\
=&\sum_{\alpha\in I_{m}}\mathbb{P}(A_{\alpha},v_{1}(G_{\alpha})\in Q;\forall\alpha^{\prime}\neq\alpha,A_{\alpha^{\prime}}\mbox{ fails or }\lambda_{1}(G_{\alpha^{\prime}})<\lambda_{1}(G_{\alpha}))\\
=&\sum_{\alpha\in I_{m}}k_{1}(\alpha).\end{split}

In fact
k_{1}(\alpha),\ldots,k_{4}(\alpha)
don’t depend on the specific choice of
\alpha
.
Recall the definition of

and (

), we have

(93)

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\sum_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\in I_{m};\alpha\cap\alpha^{\prime}\neq\emptyset}\mathbb{P}(A_{\alpha}\cap A_{\alpha^{\prime}})=\lim_{n\to+\infty}b_{n,2}=0.

By the definition of

and

Combining (

), (
93
) and (

) we see that

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\left|\mathbb{P}(\cup_{\alpha\in I_{m}}A_{\alpha},\hat{v}\in Q)-\sum_{\alpha\in I_{m}}k_{2}(\alpha)\right|=0.

We can similarly show that

(96)

\begin{split}\lim_{n\to+\infty}\left|\mathbb{P}(\cup_{\alpha\in I_{m}}A_{\alpha})-\sum_{\alpha\in I_{m}}k_{4}(\alpha)\right|\leq\lim_{n\to+\infty}\sum_{\alpha\in I_{m}}\left|k_{3}(\alpha)-k_{4}(\alpha)\right|=0.\end{split}

\mathbb{P}(\cup_{\alpha\in I_{m}}A_{\alpha})\to 1-F_{Y}(y)

as

. Hence, combining (

) and (

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\sum_{\alpha\in I_{m}}k_{4}(\alpha)=1-F_{Y}(y).

\begin{split}&\mathbb{P}(v_{1}(G_{\alpha})\in Q|A_{\alpha};\forall\alpha^{\prime}\cap\alpha=\emptyset,A_{\alpha^{\prime}}\mbox{ fails or }\lambda_{1}(G_{\alpha^{\prime}})<\lambda_{1}(G_{\alpha}))\\

Clearly the term
