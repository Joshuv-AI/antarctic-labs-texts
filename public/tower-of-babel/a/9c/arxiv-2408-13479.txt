Quantum-machine-assisted Drug Discovery: Survey and Perspective
Yidong Zhou, Jintai Chen ∗, Jinglei Cheng, Gopal Karemore, Marinka Zitnik, Frederic T. Chong, Junyu Liu, Tianfan Fu †, Zhiding Liang †

Abstract
Drug discovery and development is a highly complex and costly endeavor, typically requiring over a decade and substantial financial investment to bring a new drug to market. Traditional computer-aided drug design (CADD) has made significant progress in accelerating this process, but the development of quantum computing offers potential due to its unique capabilities. This paper discusses the integration of quantum computing into drug discovery and development, focusing on how quantum technologies might accelerate and enhance various stages of the drug development cycle. Specifically, we explore the application of quantum computing in addressing challenges related to drug discovery, such as molecular simulation and the prediction of drug-target interactions, as well as the optimization of clinical trial outcomes. By leveraging the inherent capabilities of quantum computing, we might be able to reduce the time and cost associated with bringing new drugs to market, ultimately benefiting public health.

BOX 1

Key quantum methods in drug discovery

Quantum computing offers novel approaches for accelerating drug discovery through enhanced molecular simulation, optimization, and secure data handling. Here we highlight several fundamental quantum methods:

Quantum Phase Estimation (QPE):
A fundamental quantum algorithm that determines the eigenvalues of a quantum operator. The pipeline begins by preparing an initial state that has some overlap with the desired eigenstate. A series of controlled unitary operations is then applied with increasing evolution times, encoding the phase information into a quantum register. The final step involves performing an inverse quantum Fourier transform on this register to extract the eigenvalue. The accuracy improves exponentially with the number of qubits in the phase register.

Variational Quantum Eigensolver (VQE):
A hybrid quantum-classical approach where a quantum circuit prepares trial states while a classical optimizer adjusts circuit parameters. The pipeline begins with an initial guess for the wavefunction, encoded into a parametrized quantum circuit (ansatz). The quantum hardware evaluates the energy expectation value, which feeds back to the classical optimizer. This cycle iterates until convergence to the ground state energy. The method incorporates error mitigation through techniques like zero-noise extrapolation.

Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA):
A hybrid algorithm specifically designed for combinatorial optimization problems. The pipeline starts by encoding the optimization problem into a cost Hamiltonian. The quantum circuit consists of alternating layers of problem-specific (cost) and mixing operators, with the number of layers determining the circuit depth. A classical optimizer adjusts the parameters controlling these layers to minimize the expected value of the cost function. The process iterates until reaching a satisfactory approximation of the optimal solution.

Quantum Generative Adversarial Networks (QGANs):
A quantum version of the GAN framework where both generator and discriminator utilize quantum circuits. The generator pipeline creates quantum states representing the target distribution, while the discriminator quantum circuit learns to distinguish real from generated samples. Training involves alternating optimization of generator and discriminator parameters using quantum measurements and classical optimization. The quantum advantage comes from the ability to represent and sample from complex probability distributions.

Quantum Kernel Methods:
Leverages quantum circuits to compute kernel functions in high-dimensional Hilbert spaces. The pipeline begins by mapping classical molecular data to quantum states. A quantum circuit implements feature space transformation, followed by measurements that estimate the kernel matrix elements. These quantum kernel values are then used in classical machine learning algorithms like SVM.

Quantum Imaginary Time Evolution (QITE):
Specialized algorithm for direct molecular dynamics simulation without deep circuits. Excels at studying proton transfer reactions and hydrogen-bonding networks where quantum nuclear effects play crucial roles. Enables accurate prediction of molecular behavior with reduced computational requirements.

Quantum Teleportation:
A protocol for transmitting quantum information using entanglement and classical communication. The pipeline begins with creating an entangled pair of qubits shared between sender and receiver. The sender performs a Bell measurement between their half of the entangled pair and the state to be teleported. The measurement results are sent classically to the receiver, who applies corresponding correction operations to their qubit to recover the original state.

Quantum Federated Learning (QFL):
A framework combining quantum computing with secure distributed learning. The pipeline involves local quantum models at each node processing quantum data privately. Model updates are encrypted using quantum-safe protocols before transmission. A central server performs secure aggregation of the encrypted updates, with quantum noise addition for differential privacy. The process iterates until model convergence while maintaining data privacy.

BOX 2

Key concepts in drug discovery

Quantum computing offers novel approaches for accelerating drug discovery through enhanced molecular simulation, optimization, and secure data handling. Here we highlight several fundamental quantum methods enabling these advances:

ADMET:
ADMET refers to basic small-molecule drug pharmacokinetics properties, including absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity.

Amino acids:
Amino acids are building blocks for proteins, which come in 20 different kinds. They can combine to form proteins.

Antibody:
Antibody refers to a kind of protein that could recognize and bind to the antigen and treat the disease. Antibodies are symmetric and have two identical heavy chains and two identical light chains.

Clinical trial:
A clinical trial is a research study designed to evaluate the safety and efficacy of new medical treatments, drugs, or devices in human participants. These trials follow strict protocols and are conducted in phases, from initial small-scale studies to assess safety to larger-scale studies that confirm effectiveness and monitor side effects.

Clinical trial phases:
A drug candidate needs to pass three phases of clinical trials before being approved, where phase I focuses on patient safety, phase II and III investigate safety and efficacy on a larger patient population.

De novo
drug design:

De novo
drug design refers to a wide category of drug design methods that identify novel and diverse drug molecule structures with desirable pharmaceutical properties from scratch.

Drug repurposing (a.k.a. drug repositioning or drug reuse):
Drug repurposing reuses the approved drugs (or the drugs that pass Phase I) to treat new diseases.

Epitope:
An epitope, or antigenic determinant, is part of a pathogen that antibodies recognize, triggering an adaptive immune response.

Genome-wide association studies (GWAS):
Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) is an observational study that identifies the association between genotypes (genome info) and phenotypes (e.g., trait, disease).

Interventional trial:
Interventional trial is a type of clinical trial where participants will receive intervention/treatment, and the effectiveness of the intervention is evaluated.

Lead compound:
Lead compound refers to the promising molecules that are selected from virtual screening or
de novo
drug design, which is usually not ideal in all the pharmaceutical properties and would be further optimized in lead optimization.

Macromolecule drugs:
Therapeutic molecules with large molecular weight (
>
1000 daltons), e.g., antibodies, enzymes, a carbon atom is 12 daltons, also known as biologics.

Protein structure prediction or protein folding:
Protein structure prediction, also known as protein folding, predicts the protein 3D structure given the protein amino acid sequence.
