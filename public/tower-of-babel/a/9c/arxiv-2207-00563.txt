The Power-Set Construction for Tree Algebras
Achim Blumensath

Abstract
. We study power-set operations on classes of trees and tree algebras. Our main result consists of a distributive law between the tree monad and the upwards-closed power-set monad, in the case where all trees are assumed to be linear. For non-linear ones, we prove that such a distributive law does not exist.

1.
Introduction

The main approaches to formal language theory are based on automata, logic, and algebra.
Each comes with their own strengths and weaknesses
and thereby complements the other two.
In the present article we focus on the algebraic approach,
which is well-known for producing proofs that are often simpler than automaton-based ones,
if not as elementary and at the cost of yielding worse complexity bounds.
Algebraic methods are especially successful at deriving structural results about
classes of languages. In particular, they are the method of choice when
deriving characterisations of subclasses of regular languages.
A prominent example of such a result is the Theorem of Schützenberger
[
Sch65
]

stating that a language is first-order definable if, and only if, its syntactic monoid
is aperiodic.
By now algebraic language theory is well-developed for a wide variety of settings
and types of languages, including finite words, infinite words, and finite trees.

In recent years several groups have started to work on a category-theoretic unification
of algebraic language theory

[
Boj
,
UACM17
,
Boj20
,
Blu20
,
Blu21
]
.
The motivations include both the wish to simplify the existing theories
and the need to generalise them to new settings, like infinite trees or data words.
Here, we are interested in the case of languages of infinite trees,
where an algebraic language theory has so far been missing.
We continue the technical development of the framework presented
in
[
Blu20
,
Blu21
]
by integrating a power-set operation.
(To be precise, we use the upwards-closed power set since our framework
is based on ordered sets.)
Such an operation has numerous uses in language theory:
for instance, when introducing regular expressions,
for determinisation, or when proving closure under projections.
We will present two such applications in Sections

5
and
6
below.

There are several ways to formalise languages of infinite trees.
Most of the choices involved do not make much of a difference,
but we isolate one design choice that does:
a framework built on linear trees is much better behaved
than one using possibly non-linear ones.
This continues a trend already established in
[
Blu21
]

indicating that non-linear trees are more complicated than linear ones.

The main technical result needed for an integration of the power-set operation is a theorem
stating that this operation can be lifted to the category of algebras under consideration.
In category-theoretical lingo this means we have to establish a distributive law
between the power-set monad and the monad our algebras are based on.
Note that there has been recent renewed interest in distributive laws also
in other parts of category theory (see, e.g.,
[
GPA21
,
ZM22
]
),
but the focus there is on different settings and, in particular, different functors.

We start in Section
2
by presenting our category-theoretical
framework for infinite trees. Furthermore, we define the power-set operation we will be
investigating, and we recall the notion of a distributive law, which will be central to our work.
Section
3
contains a general derivation of such laws
for a certain kind of polynomial monad, including the monad for linear trees,
and a proof that the same is not possible for non-linear trees.
The heart of the article is Section
4

where we will derive a partial result for non-linear trees that sometimes can be used as a
substitute for a full distributive law.
Finally, Sections
5
and
6
contain two applications:
the first one is a simplified proof of a recently published result on substitutions for tree
languages; while the second one describes how regular expressions can be defined using
power sets of non-linear trees.

Monads for trees

In algebraic language theory one uses tools from algebra to study sets

{defi}

The following lemma is frequently useful to prove that a functor forms a monad.

Lemma 2

Let
\bbM

\bbN

\mu:\bbM\bbM\Rightarrow\bbM

\langle\bbM,\mu,\varepsilon\rangle

\varrho:\bbM\Rightarrow\bbN

(b)

Suppose that
\varrho

\langle\bbM,\mu,\varepsilon\rangle

\langle\bbN,\nu,\eta\rangle

\langle\bbT,\mathrm{flat},\mathrm{sing}\rangle

Since our algebras are ordered it is natural to add meets (and joins) as operations.
We start by defining a monad just for meets and then add it to our algebras
via a standard construction based on so-called distributive laws.
In this and the next section we only consider the monads
\bbR
and
\bbT
.
The more complicated case of
\bbT^{\times}

{defi}

Let
A\in\mathsf{Pos}^{\Xi}
.

(a) For
X\subseteq A
, we write

\displaystyle\mathord{\Uparrow}X

\displaystyle:=\{\,a\in Aa\geq x\text{ for some }x\in X\,\}\,,

\displaystyle\text{and\penalty\ \penalty\ }\mathord{\Downarrow}X

\displaystyle:=\{\,a\in Aa\leq x\text{ for some }x\in X\,\}\,.

For single elements

\mathord{\Uparrow}x

and
\mathord{\Downarrow}x

(b)
The
(upward) power set

\displaystyle\bbU_{\xi}A:=\{\,I\subseteq A_{\xi}I\text{ is upwards closed}\,\}\,,\quad\text{for }\xi\in\Xi\,,

\displaystyle I\leq J\quad\@defiff\quad I\supseteq J\,,\quad\text{for }I,J\in\bbU_{\xi}A\,.

For a function

\displaystyle\bbU f(I):=\mathord{\Uparrow}f[I]\,,\quad\text{for }I\in\bbU A\,.

(c)

\displaystyle\bbD_{\xi}A:=\{\,I\subseteq A_{\xi}I\text{ is downwards closed}\,\}\,,\quad\text{for }\xi\in\Xi\,,

\displaystyle I\leq J\quad\@defiff\quad I\subseteq J\,,\quad\text{for }I,J\in\bbD_{\xi}A\,.

\displaystyle\bbD f(I):=\mathord{\Downarrow}f[I]\,,\quad\text{for }I\in\bbD A\,.

3.
Polynomial functors

It is not hard to manually find a distributive law between
\bbU
and the monads

\bbR
and
\bbT
, but it is not that much more difficult to prove a much more general result.
The monads used in language theory, including
\bbR
,
\bbT
, and
\bbT^{\times}
,
construct sets of labelled objects. The following definition captures the general form
of such a monad.

{defi}

A functor
\bbF:\mathsf{Pos}^{\Xi}\to\mathsf{Pos}^{\Xi}
is
polynomial
if it is of the following
form. For objects
A\in\mathsf{Pos}^{\Xi}
,

\displaystyle\bbF A=\sum_{i\in I}A^{D_{i}}\,,

for some fixed sequence
(D_{i})_{i\in I}
of sets with
I,D_{i}\in\mathsf{Set}^{\Xi}
.
Hence, an element of
\bbF A
is of the form
\langle i,s\rangle
with

i\in I
and
s:D_{i}\to A
sort-preserving. The sort of
\langle i,s\rangle
is
the sort of
i
. We usually omit the first component from the notation and simply write
s
.
The set
\mathop{dom}\nolimits(s):=D_{i}
is the called
domain
of
s
.

The ordering on
\bbF A
is defined componentwise:

\displaystyle\langle i,s\rangle\leq\langle j,t\rangle\quad\@iff\quad i=j\quad\text{and}\quad s(v)\leq t(v)\,,\quad\text{for all }v\in\mathop{dom}\nolimits(s)\,.

Finally,
\bbF
acts on morphisms by relabelling, that is,

\displaystyle\bbF f(s):=f\circ s:\mathop{dom}\nolimits(s)\to B\,,\quad\text{for }f:A\to B\,.

\lrcorner

Remark 5
.

(a) Note that the functors
\bbR
,
\bbT
, and
\bbT^{\times}
are polynomial since

\displaystyle\bbR A=\sum_{g\text{ graph}}A^{\mathop{dom}\nolimits_{0}(g)}\,,

where the sum ranges over all countable unlabelled graphs, i.e., the set
\bbR\textsf{1}
.
The same holds for the other two functors.

(b) As one can see from the above expression,
our notation for domains is not entirely consistent.
What we call
\mathop{dom}\nolimits(s)
for elements of a polynomial functor,
is called
\mathop{dom}\nolimits_{0}(g)
for graphs
g\in\bbR A
.

\lrcorner

As observed in
[
SN
]
we can describe natural transformations
between polynomial functors in the following way.

Lemma 6
.

Let
\bbF X=\sum_{i\in I}X^{D_{i}}
and
\bbG X=\sum_{j\in J}X^{E_{j}}

be polynomial functors.
There exists a one-to-one correspondence between natural transformations

\displaystyle\alpha:\bbF\Rightarrow\bbG

and families of functions (in
\mathsf{Set}^{\Xi}
)

\displaystyle\alpha^{\prime}:I\to J\quad\text{and}\quad\alpha^{\prime\prime}_{i}:E_{\alpha^{\prime}(i)}\to D_{i}\,,\quad\text{for }i\in I\,.

This correspondence is given by the equation

\displaystyle\alpha(\langle i,s\rangle)=\langle\alpha^{\prime}(i),t\rangle\quad\text{with}\quad t(v)=s(\alpha^{\prime\prime}_{i}(v))\,,\quad\text{for }v\in E_{\alpha^{\prime}(i)}\,.

Proof 3.1
.

The above equations induce a function mapping
\alpha^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime\prime}_{i}
to
\alpha
.
This function is clearly injective. Hence, it remains to show surjectivity.
Let
\alpha:\bbF\Rightarrow\bbG
be a natural transformation.
We start by recovering the function
\alpha^{\prime}:I\to J
.
Let
1
be a set with exactly
1
element
*_{\xi}
of each sort
\xi
.
Then
\textsf{1}^{D_{i}}=\textsf{1}^{E_{j}}
is a
1
-element set.
Hence, there are bijections between
\bbF\textsf{1}
and
I
and between
\bbG\textsf{1}
and
J
.
In particular, the component
\alpha_{\textsf{1}}:\bbF\textsf{1}\to\bbG\textsf{1}
of
\alpha

induces a function
\alpha^{\prime}:I\to J
.
Given some set
A
, let
u:A\to\textsf{1}
be the unique function.
For
\langle i,s\rangle\in\bbF A
it follows that

\displaystyle\bbG u(\alpha_{A}(\langle i,s\rangle))=\alpha_{\textsf{1}}(\bbF u(\langle i,s\rangle))=\alpha_{\textsf{1}}(\langle i,{*_{\xi}}\rangle)=\langle\alpha^{\prime}(i),{*_{\xi}}\rangle\,,

where
\xi
is the sort of
\langle i,s\rangle
.
This implies that

\displaystyle\alpha_{A}(\langle i,s\rangle)=\langle\alpha^{\prime}(i),t\rangle\,,\quad\text{for some }t:E_{\alpha^{\prime}(i)}\to A\,.

It thus remains to construct the functions
\alpha^{\prime\prime}_{i}:E_{\alpha^{\prime}(i)}\to D_{i}
.
We have just shown that
\alpha:\bbF\Rightarrow\bbG
induces a natural transformation

X^{D_{i}}\Rightarrow X^{E_{\alpha^{\prime}(i)}}
.
It is therefore sufficient to show that every natural transformation

\beta:X^{D}\Rightarrow X^{E}
(in
\mathsf{Pos}
) corresponds to a function
\beta^{\prime\prime}:E\to D

(in
\mathsf{Set}^{\Xi}
) such that

\displaystyle\beta(s)=t\quad\text{where}\quad t(v)=s(\beta^{\prime\prime}(v))\,.

We set

\displaystyle\beta^{\prime\prime}:=\beta_{D}(\mathrm{id}_{D})\in D^{E}.

Given
s\in A^{D}
and
v\in E
, it then follows that

\displaystyle\beta_{A}(s)(v)=\beta_{A}(s^{D}(\mathrm{id}))(v)=s^{E}(\beta_{D}(\mathrm{id}))(v)=s^{E}(\beta^{\prime\prime})(v)=s(\beta^{\prime\prime}(v))\,,

as desired.

We will need the following notation for relations between elements of
polynomial functors.

{defi}

Let
\bbF:\mathsf{Pos}^{\Xi}\to\mathsf{Pos}^{\Xi}
be a functor,
A,B
sets,
and
p:A\times B\to A
and
q:A\times B\to B
the two projections.

(a) The
lift
of a relation

is the relation

\theta^{\bbF}\subseteq\bbF A\times\bbF B
defined by

\displaystyle s\mathrel{\theta}^{\bbF}t\quad\@defiff\quad\bbF p(u)=s\text{ and }\bbF q(u)=t\,,\quad\text{for some }u\in\bbF\theta\,.

(b) We set
{\simeq_{\mathrm{sh}}}:=\theta^{\bbF}
for
\theta:=A\times B
.
If
s\simeq_{\mathrm{sh}}t
, we say that
s
and
t
have
the same shape.

\lrcorner

Remark 7
.

(a)
For a polynomial functor
\bbF X=\sum_{i\in I}X^{D_{i}}

and

,

, we have

\displaystyle s\simeq_{\mathrm{sh}}t\quad\@iff\quad s\in A^{D_{i}}\quad\text{and}\quad t\in B^{D_{i}},\quad\text{for the same index }i\in I\,.

(This implies that

Then

\displaystyle s\mathrel{\theta}^{\bbF}t\quad\@iff\quad s\simeq_{\mathrm{sh}}t\quad\text{and}\quad s(v)\mathrel{\theta}t(v)\,,\quad\text{for all }v\in\mathop{dom}\nolimits(s)=\mathop{dom}\nolimits(t)\,.

(b) In particular, two graphs

The goal of this section is to derive a distributive law between certain polynomial functors
and the monad
\bbU
. Our proof closely follows similar work
from
[
Jac04
,
GPA21
,
BKS
]
.
The differences are mainly technical and immaterial.
The only part of the following that can be considered original seems to be

•

the notion of linearity in Definition
3
,

•

Theorem
21
, which states that the distributive law we present is
unique, and

•

Theorem
22
, which states that there is no distributive law
for non-linear monads.

Our existence proof is based on the characterisation in terms of extensions
to the Kleisli category.
We start by developing a few tools to construct such extensions.
The first observation is that we can reduce the number of conditions we have to check.

Lemma 8
.

Let
\langle\bbM,\mu,\varepsilon\rangle
and
\langle\bbN,\nu,\eta\rangle
be monads on
\calC

and let
\widehat{\bbM}:\mathsf{Free}(\bbN)\to\mathsf{Free}(\bbN)
be a functor satisfying

•

\widehat{\bbM}\circ\bbF_{\bbN}=\bbF_{\bbN}\circ\bbM\,,

•

\bbF_{\bbN}\mu\circ\widehat{\bbM}\widehat{\bbM}\varphi=\widehat{\bbM}\varphi\circ\bbF_{\bbN}\mu\,,\quad\text{for every morphism }\varphi:A\to B\text{ of\/ }\mathsf{Free}(\bbN)\,,

•

\bbF_{\bbN}\varepsilon\circ\varphi=\widehat{\bbM}\varphi\circ\bbF_{\bbN}\varepsilon\,,\quad\text{for every morphism }\varphi:A\to B\text{ of\/ }\mathsf{Free}(\bbN)\,,

then
\langle\widehat{\bbM},\bbF_{\bbN}\mu,\bbF_{\bbN}\varepsilon\rangle
is an extension
of
\langle\bbM,\mu,\varepsilon\rangle
to
\mathsf{Free}(\bbN)
.

Proof 3.2
.

Our assumptions immediately imply that

\displaystyle\bbF_{\bbN}\mu:\widehat{\bbM}\widehat{\bbM}\Rightarrow\widehat{\bbM}\quad\text{and}\quad\bbF_{\bbN}\varepsilon:\mathrm{Id}\Rightarrow\widehat{\bbM}

are natural transformations. Hence, we only have to check the monad laws for

\langle\widehat{\bbM},\bbF_{\bbN}\mu,\bbF_{\bbN}\varepsilon\rangle
.

\displaystyle\bbF_{\bbN}\mu\circ\widehat{\bbM}\bbF_{\bbN}\mu

\displaystyle=\bbF_{\bbN}\mu\circ\bbF_{\bbN}\bbM\mu=\bbF_{\bbN}(\mu\circ\bbM\mu)=\bbF_{\bbN}(\mu\circ\mu)=\bbF_{\bbN}\mu\circ\bbF_{\bbN}\mu\,,

\displaystyle\bbF_{\bbN}\mu\circ\bbF_{\bbN}\varepsilon

\displaystyle=\bbF_{\bbN}(\mu\circ\varepsilon)=\mathrm{id}\,,

\displaystyle\bbF_{\bbN}\mu\circ\widehat{\bbM}\bbF_{\bbN}\varepsilon

\displaystyle=\bbF_{\bbN}\mu\circ\bbF_{\bbN}\bbM\varepsilon=\bbF_{\bbN}(\mu\circ\bbM\varepsilon)=\mathrm{id}\,.

Note that the action of
\widehat{\bbM}
on objects is already completely determined
by the requirement that
\widehat{\bbM}\circ\bbF_{\bbN}=\bbF_{\bbN}\circ\bbM
.
Hence, we only have to find a suitable definition of
\widehat{\bbM}
on morphisms

\varphi:\bbN A\to\bbN B
.
For the functor
\bbN=\bbU
, we adapt a construction
from
[
Jac04
,
Gar20
,
GPA21
]
based on the category of relations.
Note that every morphism
\varphi:\bbU A\to\bbU B
of
\bbU
-algebras
is uniquely determined by its restriction
f:A\to\bbU B
to
A
.
The key idea is to use the following encoding of such functions.

{defi}

(a) We denote the (sort-wise) power set of
A\in\mathsf{Pos}^{\Xi}
by

\mathscr{P}(A)\in\mathsf{Pos}^{\Xi}
.

(b) A
span
is a pair of morphisms
A\leftarrow^{p}R\to^{q}B

with the same domain.
We call a span
A\leftarrow^{p}R\to^{q}B

injective
if

\displaystyle p(c)=p(c^{\prime})\quad\text{and}\quad q(c)=q(c^{\prime})\quad\text{implies}\quad c=c^{\prime}\,,

and we call it
closed
if, for all
a\in A
,
b\in B
, and
c\in R
,

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle\leq p(c)

implies

\displaystyle p^{-1}(a)

\displaystyle\cap q^{-1}(q(c))

\displaystyle\neq\emptyset\,,

\displaystyle b

\displaystyle\geq q(c)

implies

\displaystyle q^{-1}(b)

\displaystyle\cap p^{-1}(p(c))

\displaystyle\neq\emptyset\,.

(c) The function
f:A\to\mathscr{P}(B)
(not necessarily monotone)
represented

by a span
A\leftarrow^{p}R\to^{q}B
is given by

\displaystyle f(a):=q[p^{-1}(a)]\,,\quad\text{for }a\in A\,.

(d)
The
graph
of a function
f:A\to\bbU B
is the relation

\displaystyle G(f):=\{\,\langle a,b\rangle\in A\times Bb\in f(a)\,\}\,,

and the
representation
of
f
is the span
A\leftarrow G(f)\to B

consisting of the two projections.

\lrcorner

Lemma 9
.

The correspondence between a function
A\to\bbU B
and its representation forms a bijection
between
(i)
the set of all functions
A\to\bbU B
in
\mathsf{Pos}^{\Xi}
and

(ii)
the set of all spans
A\leftarrow R\to B
that are injective and closed.

Proof 3.3
.

Let
f:A\to\bbU B
be a function with representation
A\leftarrow^{p}G(f)\to^{q}B
.
This span is injective as every pair is uniquely determined by the values of its two components.
To see that it is also closed, suppose that
b\geq q(c)
,
for some
b\in B
and
c\in G(f)
.
By definition of
G(f)
, we have
c=\langle a^{\prime},b^{\prime}\rangle
with
b^{\prime}\in f(a^{\prime})
.
As
f(a^{\prime})
is upwards closed,
b\geq q(c)=b^{\prime}
implies
b\in f(a^{\prime})
.
Hence,
\langle a^{\prime},b\rangle\in G(f)
and

\displaystyle\langle a^{\prime},b\rangle\in q^{-1}(b)\cap p^{-1}(a^{\prime})=q^{-1}(b)\cap p^{-1}[p(c)]\neq\emptyset\,.

Similarly, suppose that
a\leq p(c)
.
Then
c=\langle a^{\prime},b^{\prime}\rangle
with
b^{\prime}\in f(a^{\prime})
and
a\leq a^{\prime}
.
As
f
is monotone, it follows that
f(a)\supseteq f(a^{\prime})
. In particular,
b^{\prime}\in f(a)
.
Hence,
\langle a,b^{\prime}\rangle\in G(f)
and

\displaystyle\langle a,b^{\prime}\rangle\in p^{-1}(a)\cap q^{-1}(b^{\prime})=p^{-1}(a)\cap q^{-1}(q(c))\neq\emptyset\,.

Conversely, consider an injective, closed span
A\leftarrow^{p}R\to^{q}B

and let
f:A\to\mathscr{P}(B)
be the function it represents.
We have to show that
f
is monotone and that
f(a)
is upwards closed, for each
a\in A
.
For monotonicity, let
a\leq a^{\prime}
and
b^{\prime}\in f(a^{\prime})
.
We have to show that
b^{\prime}\in f(a)
. By definition of
f
, there is some
c\in R

with
p(c)=a^{\prime}
and
q(c)=b^{\prime}
.
Then
a\leq p(c)
implies that there is some
d\in p^{-1}(a)\cap q^{-1}(q(c))
.
Consequently,
b^{\prime}=q(c)=q(d)\in q[p^{-1}(a)]=f(a)
.

To show that
f(a)
is upwards closed, suppose that
b\geq b^{\prime}\in f(a)=q[p^{-1}(a)]
.
Then we can find some element
c\in R
with
p(c)=a
and
q(c)=b^{\prime}
.
Hence,
b\geq q(c)
and closedness implies that
we can find some element
c^{\prime}\in q^{-1}(b)\cap p^{-1}[p(c)]
.
It follows that
q(c^{\prime})=b
and
p(c^{\prime})=p(c)=a
.
Consequently,
b\in q[p^{-1}(a)]=f(a)
.

To conclude the proof, we have to show that these two operations
are inverse to each other.
Given a function
f:A\to\bbU B
, let
g
be the function
represented by
A\leftarrow^{p}G(f)\to^{q}B
.
Then

\displaystyle g(a)=q[p^{-1}(a)]=\{\,b\langle a,b\rangle\in G(f)\,\}=\{\,bb\in f(a)\,\}=f(a)\,.

Conversely, consider an injective, closed span
A\leftarrow^{p}R\to^{q}B
,
let
f:A\to\bbU B
be the function it represents,
and let
A\leftarrow^{u}G(f)\to^{v}B
be the representation of
f
.
Then

\displaystyle G(f)

\displaystyle=\{\,\langle a,b\rangle b\in f(a)\,\}

\displaystyle=\{\,\langle a,b\rangle b\in q[p^{-1}(a)]\,\}

\displaystyle=\{\,\langle a,b\rangle c\in R\,,\ b=q(c)\,,\ p(c)=a\,\}

\displaystyle=\{\,\langle p(c),q(c)\rangle c\in R\,\}\,.

Since the span
A\leftarrow^{p}R\to^{q}B
is injective, it follows that
the function
\langle p,q\rangle:R\to G(f)
is a bijection that commutes
with the two projections. Thus, the two spans
A\leftarrow^{p}R\to^{q}B
and

A\leftarrow^{u}G(f)\to^{v}B
are isomorphic.

We can compose spans by performing a pullback.

Lemma 10
.

Let
f:A\to\bbU B
and
g:B\to\bbU C
be represented by,
respectively,
A\leftarrow^{p}R\to^{q}B
and
B\leftarrow^{u}S\to^{v}C
.
Then the function

\displaystyle\mathrm{union}\circ\bbU g\circ f:A\to\bbU C

is represented by
A\leftarrow^{p\circ k}T\to^{v\circ l}C
, where

R\leftarrow^{k}T\to^{l}S
is the pullback of
R\to^{q}B\leftarrow^{u}S
.

Proof 3.4
.

Note that the pullback in
\mathsf{Pos}^{\Xi}
is given by

\displaystyle T=\{\,\langle r,s\rangle q(r)=u(s)\,\}

and
k
and
l
are the respective projections.
For
a\in A
, we therefore have

\displaystyle(\mathrm{union}\circ\bbU g\circ f)(a)

\displaystyle=\bigcup{\{\,g(b)b\in f(a)\,\}}

\displaystyle=\bigcup{\bigl\{\,v[u^{-1}(b)]\bigm b\in q[p^{-1}(a)]\,\bigr\}}

\displaystyle=\bigl\{\,c\in C\bigm c=v(s)\,,\ u(s)=q(r)\,,\ p(r)=a\,\bigr\}

\displaystyle=\bigl\{\,c\in C\bigm c=v(s)\,,\ \langle r,s\rangle\in T\,,\ p(r)=a\,\bigr\}

\displaystyle=\bigl\{\,v(l(t))\bigm t\in T\,,\ p(k(t))=a\,\bigr\}

\displaystyle=(v\circ l)[(p\circ k)^{-1}(a)]\,.

It remains to prove that polynomial functors satisfy the conditions in
Lemma
8
.
We start by taking a look at how such a functor operates on spans.

Lemma 11
.

Let
\bbM:\mathsf{Pos}^{\Xi}\to\mathsf{Pos}^{\Xi}
be a polynomial functor.

(a)

\bbM
preserves injective and closed spans.

(b)

\bbM
preserves pullbacks.

(c)

s\simeq_{\mathrm{sh}}\bbM f(s)\,,\quad\text{for all }s\in\bbM A\,,\ f:A\to B\,.

Proof 3.5
.

(a)
Let
A\leftarrow^{p}R\to^{q}B
be injective and closed
and let
\bbM A\leftarrow^{\bbM p}\bbM R\to^{\bbM q}\bbM B
be its image under
\bbM
.

For injectivity, consider elements
s,t\in\bbM R
.
Then

\displaystyle\bbM p(s)=\bbM p(t)\quad\text{and}\quad\bbM q(s)=\bbM q(t)

\displaystyle{}\Rightarrow\quad

\displaystyle p(s(v))=p(t(v))\quad\text{and}\quad q(s(v))=q(t(v))\,,\quad\text{for all }v\,,

\displaystyle{}\Rightarrow\quad

\displaystyle s(v)=t(v)\,,\quad\text{for all }v\,,

\displaystyle{}\Rightarrow\quad

\displaystyle s=t\,.

For closedness, suppose that
s\geq\bbM q(t)
. Then

\displaystyle s(v)\geq q(t(v))\,,\quad\text{for all }v\,.

Hence, we can fix elements
c_{v}\in q^{-1}[s(v)]\cap p^{-1}[p(t(v))]
.
Setting
t^{\prime}(v):=c_{v}
, it follows that
t^{\prime}\in\bbM R
and

\displaystyle q(t^{\prime}(v))=s(v)\quad\text{and}\quad p(t^{\prime}(v))=p(t(v))\,,\quad\text{for all }v\,,

\displaystyle{}\Rightarrow\quad

\displaystyle\bbM q(t^{\prime})=s\quad\text{and}\quad\bbM p(t^{\prime})=\bbM p(t)

\displaystyle{}\Rightarrow\quad

\displaystyle(\bbM q)^{-1}(s)\cap(\bbM p)^{-1}[\bbM p(t)]\neq\emptyset\,.

Similarly, suppose that
s\leq\bbM p(t)
. Then
s(v)\leq p(t(v))
, for all
v
.
Hence, we can fix elements
c_{v}\in p^{-1}[s(v)]\cap q^{-1}[q(t(v))]
.
Setting
t^{\prime}(v):=c_{v}
, it follows that
t^{\prime}\in\bbM R
and

\displaystyle p(t^{\prime}(v))=s(v)\quad\text{and}\quad q(t^{\prime}(v))=q(t(v))\,,\quad\text{for all }v\,,

\displaystyle{}\Rightarrow\quad

\displaystyle\bbM p(t^{\prime})=s\quad\text{and}\quad\bbM q(t^{\prime})=\bbM q(t)

\displaystyle{}\Rightarrow\quad

\displaystyle(\bbM p)^{-1}(s)\cap(\bbM q)^{-1}[\bbM q(t)]\neq\emptyset\,.

(b) Let
A\leftarrow^{p}P\to^{q}B
be the pullback of
A\to^{f}C\leftarrow^{g}B
.
Then

\displaystyle P=\{\,\langle a,b\rangle f(a)=g(b)\,\}

and
p
and
q
are the respective projections.
Similarly, the pullback of
\bbM A\to^{\bbM f}\bbM C\leftarrow^{\bbM g}\bbM B
is

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle:=\bigl\{\,\langle s,t\rangle\bigm\bbM f(s)=\bbM g(t)\,\bigr\}

\displaystyle=\bigl\{\,\langle s,t\rangle\bigm f(s(v))=g(t(v))\text{ for all }v\,\bigr\}

\displaystyle=\bigl\{\,\langle s,t\rangle\bigm\langle s(v),t(v)\rangle\in P\text{ for all }v\,\bigr\}\,.

Consequently, the map
\langle\bbM p,\bbM q\rangle:\bbM(A\times B)\to\bbM A\times\bbM B

induces a bijection between
\bbM P
and
Q
.

(c) Setting
r:=s
,
p:=\mathrm{id}
, and
q:=f
, we obtain

\bbM p(r)=s
and
\bbM q(r)=\bbM f(s)
.

Lemma 12
.

Let
\bbM
be a polynomial functor.
If
A\leftarrow^{p}R\to^{q}B
represents
f:A\to\bbU B
,
then its image
\bbM A\leftarrow^{\bbM p}\bbM R\to^{\bbM q}\bbM B
under
\bbM

represents
F:\bbM A\to\bbU\bbM B
where

\displaystyle F(s)=\{\,t\in\bbM Bt\in^{\bbM}\bbM f(s)\,\}\,.

Proof 3.6
.

We have shown in Lemma
11
that
polynomial functors preserve injective closed spans.
For
s\in\bbM A
, it therefore follows that

\displaystyle F(s)

\displaystyle=\bbM q\bigl[(\bbM p)^{-1}(s)\bigr]

\displaystyle=\{\,t\in\bbM Br\in\bbM R\,,\ t=\bbM q(r)\,,\ s=\bbM p(r)\,\}

\displaystyle=\{\,t\in\bbM Br\in\bbM R\,,\ t(v)=q(r(v))\,,\ s(v)=p(r(v))\,,\text{ for all }v\,\}

\displaystyle=\{\,t\in\bbM Bt(v)\in q[p^{-1}(s(v))]\,,\text{ for all }v\,\}

\displaystyle=\{\,t\in\bbM Bt(v)\in f(s(v))\,,\text{ for all }v\,\}

\displaystyle=\{\,t\in\bbM Bt\in^{\bbM}\bbM f(s)\,\}\,.

We obtain the following proof that every polynomial functor
\bbM
on
\mathsf{Pos}^{\Xi}

has an extension to
\mathsf{Free}(\bbU)
.

Proposition 13
.

Every polynomial functor
\bbM
on
\mathsf{Pos}^{\Xi}
induces a functor
\widehat{\bbM}

on
\mathsf{Free}(\bbU)
satisfying

\displaystyle\widehat{\bbM}\circ\bbF_{\bbN}=\bbF_{\bbN}\circ\bbM\,.

This functor maps a morphism
\varphi:\bbU A\to\bbU B
to

\displaystyle\widehat{\bbM}\varphi(x):=\bbM q[(\bbM p)^{-1}[x]]\,,

where
A\leftarrow^{p}R\rightarrow^{q}B
is the span representing the morphism

\varphi\circ\mathrm{pt}
.

Proof 3.7
.

As we have already explained above, for objects we are forced to set

\displaystyle\widehat{\bbM}\langle\bbU A,\mathrm{union}\rangle:=\langle\bbU\bbM A,\mathrm{union}\rangle\,.

For a morphism

\varphi:\langle\bbU A,\mathrm{union}\rangle\to\langle\bbU B,\mathrm{union}\rangle
of free
\bbU
-algebras
we define
\widehat{\bbM}\varphi
as follows.
Let
A\leftarrow^{p}G(\varphi)\to^{q}B
be the representation of

\varphi\circ\mathrm{pt}:A\to\bbU B
, and let

\hat{\varphi}:\bbM A\to\bbU\bbM B
be the function represented by
the span
\bbM A\leftarrow^{\bbM p}\bbM G(\varphi)\to^{\bbM q}\bbM B
.
Then we set

\displaystyle\widehat{\bbM}\varphi:=\mathrm{union}\circ\bbU\hat{\varphi}\,.

We claim that this defines the desired functor
\widehat{\bbM}
.

First, let us prove that
\widehat{\bbM}
is a functor
\mathsf{Free}(\bbU)\to\mathsf{Free}(\bbU)
.
Clearly,
\widehat{\bbM}
maps free
\bbU
-algebras to free
\bbU
-algebras.
Furthermore, by the above definition
\widehat{\bbM}\varphi
is the free extension of

\hat{\varphi}:\bbM A\to\bbU\bbM B
to a morphism
\bbU\bbM A\to\bbU\bbM B

of
\bbU
-algebras.
Hence, we only have to show that

\displaystyle\widehat{\bbM}(\varphi\circ\psi)=\widehat{\bbM}\varphi\circ\widehat{\bbM}\psi\,.

Let
\bbM B\leftarrow^{\bbM p}\bbM G(\varphi)\to^{\bbM q}\bbM C
and

\bbM A\leftarrow^{\bbM u}\bbM G(\psi)\to^{\bbM v}\bbM B
be
the representations of
\hat{\varphi}
and
\hat{\psi}
.
By Lemma
10
, the morphism

\displaystyle\mathrm{union}\circ\bbU\hat{\varphi}\circ\hat{\psi}:\bbM A\to\bbU\bbM C

is then represented by

\bbM A\leftarrow^{\bbM u\circ k}P\to^{\bbM q\circ l}\bbM C
where

\bbM G(\psi)\leftarrow^{k}P\to^{l}\bbM G(\varphi)
is the pullback of

\bbM G(\psi)\to^{\bbM v}\bbM B\leftarrow^{\bbM p}\bbM G(\varphi)
.
Since
\bbM
preserves pullbacks, we have
P=\bbM P^{\prime}
,
k=\bbM k^{\prime}
,
and
k=\bbM k^{\prime}
where
G(\psi)\leftarrow^{k^{\prime}}P^{\prime}\to^{l^{\prime}}G(\varphi)

is the pullback of
G(\psi)\to^{v}B\leftarrow^{p}G(\varphi)
.
Furthermore, it follows by Lemma
10
that

A\leftarrow^{u\circ k^{\prime}}P^{\prime}\to^{q\circ l^{\prime}}C
represents
\varphi\circ\psi
.
Consequently,
\widehat{\bbM}(\varphi\circ\psi)\circ\mathrm{pt}
is also represented
by
\bbM A\leftarrow^{\bbM u\circ k}P\to^{\bbM q\circ l}\bbM C
and we have

\displaystyle\widehat{\bbM}(\varphi\circ\psi)\circ\mathrm{pt}=\mathrm{union}\circ\bbU\hat{\varphi}\circ\hat{\psi}=\widehat{\bbM}\varphi\circ\widehat{\bbM}\psi\circ\mathrm{pt}\,.

As
\widehat{\bbM}(\varphi\circ\psi)
and
\widehat{\bbM}\varphi\circ\widehat{\bbM}\psi

are morphisms of
\bbU
-algebras, which are determined by their restriction to the
range of
\mathrm{pt}
, it follows that

\displaystyle\widehat{\bbM}(\varphi\circ\psi)=\widehat{\bbM}\varphi\circ\widehat{\bbM}\psi\,.

To conclude the proof, it remains to show that

\widehat{\bbM}\circ\bbF_{\bbU}=\bbF_{\bbU}\circ\bbM
.
For objects
A\in\mathsf{Pos}^{\Xi}
, this is obvious from the definition.
Hence, consider a function
f:A\to B
and set
\varphi:=\bbU f
.
Let
A\leftarrow^{p}G(\bbU f)\to^{q}B
be the span representing
\bbU f
.
Then
\hat{\varphi}:\bbM A\to\bbU\bbM B
is represented by

\bbM A\leftarrow^{\bbM p}\bbM G(\bbU f)\to^{\bbM q}\bbM B
.
By Lemma
12
, it follows that

\displaystyle\widehat{\bbM}\bbU f(I)

\displaystyle=\bigcup\bbU\hat{\varphi}(I)

\displaystyle=\bigcup\mathord{\Uparrow}\{\,\hat{\varphi}(s)s\in I\,\}

\displaystyle=\bigcup\mathord{\Uparrow}\bigl\{\,\{\,tt\in^{\bbM}\bbM(\bbU f\circ\mathrm{pt})(s)\,\}\bigm s\in I\,\bigr\}

\displaystyle=\mathord{\Uparrow}\bigl\{\,t\bigm s\in I\,,\ t\in^{\bbM}\bbM(\mathrm{pt}\circ f)(s)\,\bigr\}

\displaystyle=\mathord{\Uparrow}\bigl\{\,t\bigm s\in I\,,\ t(v)\in(\mathrm{pt}\circ f)(s(v))\text{ for all }v\,\bigr\}

\displaystyle=\mathord{\Uparrow}\bigl\{\,t\bigm s\in I\,,\ t(v)\geq f(s(v))\text{ for all }v\,\bigr\}

\displaystyle=\mathord{\Uparrow}\bigl\{\,t\bigm s\in I\,,\ t\geq\bbM f(s)\,\bigr\}

\displaystyle=\mathord{\Uparrow}\bigl\{\,\bbM f(s)\bigm s\in I\,\bigr\}

\displaystyle=\bbU\bbM f(I)\,.

To find the desired distributive law for polynomial monads,
it remains to prove the two remaining conditions of Lemma
8
.
To do so, we have to make additional assumptions on our monad:
we require that the multiplication
\bbM\bbM\Rightarrow\bbM
does not duplicate labels.
We will call such monads
linear.

Before we can give the formal definition, we need to take a look at the special form
the multiplication morphism for a polynomial functor takes.

Remark 14
.

Let
\langle\bbM,\mu,\varepsilon\rangle
be a monad with a polynomial functor

\bbM X=\sum_{i\in I}X^{D_{i}}
.
Note that the composition
\bbM\circ\bbM
is also a polynomial functor.
A straightforward computation yields

\displaystyle\bbM\bbM X=\sum_{i\in I}\sum_{g:D_{i}\to I}X^{\sum_{v\in D_{i}}\mathop{dom}\nolimits(g(v))}.

Thus
\bbM\bbM X=\sum_{j\in J}X^{E_{j}}
where

\displaystyle J:=\sum_{i\in I}I^{D_{i}}\quad\text{and}\quad E_{\langle i,g\rangle}:=\sum_{v\in D_{i}}D_{g(v)}\,.

Note that the identity functor
\mathrm{Id}
is polynomial, since

\displaystyle\mathrm{Id}(A)=\sum_{\xi\in\Xi}A^{\textsf{1}_{\xi}},

where
\textsf{1}_{\xi}
is a set with a single element, which has sort
\xi
.
Therefore, we can apply Lemma
6
to the natural
transformations
\mu:\bbM\bbM\Rightarrow\bbM
and
\varepsilon:\mathrm{Id}\Rightarrow\bbM

and we obtain induced maps

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\prime}

\displaystyle:\Xi\to I\,,

\displaystyle\qquad\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}_{\xi}

\displaystyle:D_{\varepsilon^{\prime}(\xi)}\to\textsf{1}_{\xi}\,,

\displaystyle\quad\text{for }\xi\in\Xi\,,

\displaystyle\mu^{\prime}

\displaystyle:J\to I\,,

\displaystyle\qquad\mu^{\prime\prime}_{j}

\displaystyle:D_{\mu^{\prime}(j)}\to E_{j}\,,

\displaystyle\quad\text{for }j\in J\,.

With our conventions regarding polynomial functors, we can write the latter as

\displaystyle\mu^{\prime\prime}_{s}:\mathop{dom}\nolimits(\mu(s))\to\sum_{v\in\mathop{dom}\nolimits(s)}\mathop{dom}\nolimits(s(v))\,,\quad\text{for }s\in\bbM\bbM A\,.

\lrcorner

{defi}

Let
\langle\bbM,\mu,\varepsilon\rangle
be a monad where
\bbM
is polynomial
and let
\mu^{\prime}
,
\mu^{\prime\prime}_{j}
,
\varepsilon^{\prime}
, and
\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}_{j}
be the functions
corresponding to the natural transformations

\mu:\bbM\bbM\Rightarrow\bbM
and
\varepsilon:\mathrm{Id}\Rightarrow\bbM

We call
\langle\bbM,\mu,\varepsilon\rangle

\lrcorner

{exa}

The monads
\bbR
and
\bbT

\mathrm{flat}(g)
corresponds
to exactly one vertex of exactly one component

The monad
\bbT^{\times}

for

.

Lemma 16

Let
\langle\bbM,\mu,\varepsilon\rangle

.

Let

(a) Let
p^{*}:\bbM A\to\textsf{1}

For

\displaystyle p\bigl(\mu(s)(w)\bigr)=p\bigl(s(v)(u)\bigr)=q\bigl(t(v)(u)\bigr)=q\bigl(\mu(t)(w)\bigr)\,,

(b) Choose

s^{\prime}\simeq_{\mathrm{sh}}t

s^{\prime}(v)\simeq_{\mathrm{sh}}t(v)
, for all

\displaystyle s^{\prime}(v)(u):=\begin{cases}s\bigl((\mu^{\prime\prime}_{j})^{-1}(v,u)\bigr)&\text{if }\langle v,u\rangle\in\mathop{rng}\nolimits\mu^{\prime\prime}_{j}\,,\\

Theorem 18
.

Let
\bbM
be a linear monad on
\mathsf{Pos}^{\Xi}
.
The functions
\mathrm{dist}_{A}:\bbM\bbU A\to\bbU\bbM A
defined by

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}_{A}(t):=\{\,s\in\bbM As\in^{\bbM}t\,\}

form a distributive law
\bbM\bbU\Rightarrow\bbU\bbM
.

Proof 3.10
.

By (the proof of) Theorem
2
,
we can obtain the desired distributive law from an extension
\widehat{\bbM}

of
\bbM
to
\mathsf{Free}(\bbU)
by setting

\displaystyle\delta:=\bbV\widehat{\bbM}\mathrm{id}\circ\mathrm{pt}\,,

where
\bbV:\mathsf{Free}(\bbU)\to\mathsf{Pos}^{\Xi}
is the forgetful functor.
Note that the span representing the identity
\mathrm{id}:\bbU A\to\bbU A

is
A\leftarrow^{\mathrm{id}}A\rightarrow^{\mathrm{pt}}\bbU A
.
For
t\in\bbM\bbU A
, it therefore follows that

\displaystyle\delta(t)

\displaystyle=\widehat{\bbM}\mathrm{id}(\mathrm{pt}(t))

\displaystyle=\bbM\mathrm{id}[(\bbM\mathrm{pt})^{-1}[\mathord{\Uparrow}\{t\}]]

\displaystyle=\{\,\bbM\mathrm{id}(s)\bbM\mathrm{pt}(s)\geq t\,\}

\displaystyle=\{\,s\mathrm{pt}(s(v))\subseteq t(v)\text{ for all }v\,\}

\displaystyle=\{\,ss(v)\in t(v)\text{ for all }v\,\}

\displaystyle=\{\,ss\in^{\bbM}t\,\}\,.

Corollary 19
.

The functions
\mathrm{dist}
from above form distributive laws

\bbT\bbU\Rightarrow\bbU\bbT
and
\bbR\bbU\Rightarrow\bbU\bbR
.

.

The distributive law

above was first stated in

We can strengthen this theorem in two ways:

(i)
the distributive law
\mathrm{dist}
is unique and

(ii)
there is no distributive law for non-linear monads.
We start with the former.

Theorem 21
.

Let
\bbM
be a polynomial monad on
\mathsf{Pos}^{\Xi}
and

\delta:\bbM\bbU\Rightarrow\bbU\bbM
a distributive law.
Then
\delta=\mathrm{dist}
.

Proof 3.11
.

(\supseteq)

Since
\delta
is monotone, we have

\displaystyle\delta(t)

\displaystyle\leq\inf{\{\,\delta(s)s\geq t\,\}}

\displaystyle\leq\inf{\bigl\{\,\delta(\bbM\mathrm{pt}(r))\bigm\bbM\mathrm{pt}(r)\geq t\,\bigr\}}

\displaystyle=\inf{\bigl\{\,\mathrm{pt}(r)\bigm\bbM\mathrm{pt}(r)(v)\geq t(v)\text{ for all }v\,\bigr\}}

\displaystyle=\inf{\bigl\{\,\mathrm{pt}(r)\bigm\mathrm{}{pt}(r(v))\geq t(v)\text{ for all }v\,\bigr\}}

\displaystyle=\bigcup{\bigl\{\,\mathrm{pt}(r)\bigm\mathrm{}{pt}(r(v))\subseteq t(v)\text{ for all }v\,\bigr\}}

\displaystyle=\bigcup{\bigl\{\,\mathrm{pt}(r)\bigm r(v)\in t(v)\text{ for all }v\,\bigr\}}

\displaystyle=\mathord{\Uparrow}{\{\,rr\in^{\bbM}t\,\}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{dist}(t)\,.

(\subseteq)

Suppose that
s\in\delta(t)
for
t\in\bbM\bbU A
.
To prove that
s\in\mathrm{dist}(t)
it is sufficient to show that
s(v)\in t(v)
, for all
v
.
Hence, fix
v\in\mathop{dom}\nolimits(t)
and
let
\theta:A\to[2]
be the map with

\displaystyle\theta(a):=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }a\in t(v)\,,\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Then
\bbM\bbU\theta(t)(v)=\bbU\theta(t(v))=\{1\}
.
Since
[2]
is well-ordered, we can find some
r\in\bbM[2]
such that

\bbM\bbU\theta(t)=\bbM\mathrm{pt}(r)
.
It follows that

\displaystyle\bbU\bbM\theta(\delta(t))=\delta(\bbM\bbU\theta(t))=\delta(\bbM\mathrm{pt}(t))=\mathrm{pt}(r)\,.

Consequently,

\displaystyle\theta(s(v))=\bbM\theta(s)(v)\geq r(v)=1\quad\text{implies}\quad s(v)\in t(v)\,.

As a consequence, we obtain the following strengthening of
Theorem
18
.

Theorem 22
.

Let
\langle\bbM,\mu,\varepsilon\rangle
be a polynomial monad on
\mathsf{Pos}^{\Xi}
.
There exists a distributive law
\delta:\bbM\bbU\Rightarrow\bbU\bbM
if, and only if,

\bbM
is linear.

Proof 3.12
.

(\Leftarrow)
has already been proved in
Theorem
18

(\Rightarrow)

Suppose that
\bbM
is not linear and let

\mu^{\prime}
,
\mu^{\prime\prime}_{j}
,
\varepsilon^{\prime}
, and
\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}_{j}
be the functions
corresponding to the natural transformations

\mu:\bbM\bbM\Rightarrow\bbM
and
\varepsilon:\mathrm{Id}\Rightarrow\bbM

as in the definition of linearity.
By Theorem
21
, it is sufficient to show that
\mathrm{dist}
is not
a distributive law. For a contradiction, suppose otherwise.

By assumption, there is some index
j
such that
\mu^{\prime\prime}_{j}
is not injective or

\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}_{j}
not bijective.
First, assume that
\mu^{\prime\prime}_{j}:D_{\mu^{\prime}(j)}\to E_{j}
is not injective, for some index
j
.
Then there are two positions
u,v\in D_{\mu^{\prime}(j)}
with
\mu^{\prime\prime}_{j}(u)=\mu^{\prime\prime}_{j}(v)
.
Set

be a set with at least two elements

\langle\bbU\bbM A,\bbU\mu\circ\mathrm{dist}\rangle

with product

\displaystyle\bigl\{\,\langle t(u),t(v)\rangle\bigm t\in\mathrm{dist}(\mu(s))\,\bigr\}

\displaystyle{}={}

\displaystyle\bigl\{\,\langle t(u),t(v)\rangle\bigm t\in^{\bbM}\mu(s)\,\bigr\}

\displaystyle{}={}

\displaystyle\bigl\{\,\langle p,q\rangle\bigm p\in\mu(s)(u),\,q\in\mu(s)(v)\,\bigr\}

\displaystyle{}={}

\displaystyle{}={}

Remark 23
.

(a) We did not make essential use of the fact that we are working with ordered sets.
All results of this section also hold in the category
\mathsf{Set}^{\Xi}
.

(b)
In the literature one can find many cases where there is no distributive law
between some variant of the power-set monad and some other monad. In particular,
there is no such law between the power-set monad and itself.
As a workaround there has been a lot of recent work
(see, e.g.,
[
Gar20
,
GPA21
]
)
on so-called
weak distributive laws

which satisfy the axioms for a distributive law, except possibly for

\delta\circ\varepsilon=\bbN\varepsilon
.
A closer look at the proofs above reveals that our results also hold for
weak distributive laws if we replace linearity with the weaker condition
that only the functions
\mu^{\prime\prime}_{j}
are injective.
If we call such a monad
weakly linear
it follows in particular that
there is a weak distributive law
\delta:\bbM\bbU\Rightarrow\bbU\bbM

if, and only if,
\bbM
is weakly linear.

(c)
In light of the above theorem, it is unsurprising that all known distributive
laws for variants of the power-set monad require some form of linearity,
although it is frequently expressed in terms of which equations the free
algebra satisfies, instead of using properties of the monad multiplication.

For instance, there is a distributive law
[
MM07
]
in
\mathsf{Set}
between so-call
‘commutative monads’ (like the power-set monad) and finitary term monads (which are linear
in our sense).
Similarly, there is a distributive law
[
MM08
]
between certain monads
and quotients of finitary term monads by linear equations (i.e., term equations where every
variable appears exactly once on each side).

In
[
ZM22
]
a variety of non-existence results for distributive laws
between quotients of finitary term monads is proved. In many of the cases,
one of the assumptions is that there is some term
s
satisfying the equation
s(x,\dots,x)=x

(which is non-linear).

It seems that much of the existing theory could be unified if the results of this section
(which also apply to monads that are non-finitary) could be generalised from
linear polynomial monads to suitable ‘linear’ quotients of such monads.

\lrcorner

4.
Non-linear trees

It is time to properly define our third monad, that of non-linear trees,
and to prove its limited compatibility with the power-set monad.
Unfortunately, this turns out to be much more complex than the case of linear trees.
In fact, as we have seen in Theorem
22
,
there does not exist a distributive law between
\bbT^{\times}
and
\bbU
.
We will therefore forego distributive laws and directly prove the existence of
a lift of
\bbU
to the class of free
\bbT^{\times}
-algebras,
a partial result that is sufficient for many applications.
We start by defining the monad structure of
\bbT^{\times}
.

{defi}

(a) We denote the
unravelling
(in the usual graph-theoretic sense)
of a graph
g\in\bbR_{\xi}A
by
\mathrm{un}_{0}(g)\in\bbR_{\xi}A
.
That is,
\mathrm{un}_{0}(g)
is the graph whose vertices consist of all finite paths of
g

that start at the root and there is an edge between two such paths if the second
one is the corresponding prolongation of the first one.

(b) We define
\mathrm{flat}^{\times}:\bbT^{\times}\bbT^{\times}A\to\bbT^{\times}A
and

\mathrm{sing}^{\times}:A\to\bbT^{\times}A
by

\displaystyle\mathrm{flat}^{\times}:=\mathrm{un}_{0}\circ\mathrm{flat}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{sing}^{\times}:=\mathrm{sing}\,.

\lrcorner

This gives us the desired monad structure for
\bbT^{\times}
.
The proof is straightforward.

Lemma 24
.

\langle\bbT^{\times},\mathrm{flat}^{\times},\mathrm{sing}^{\times}\rangle
is a monad.

In contrast to
\bbT
, the monad
\bbT^{\times}
is
not
a submonad of
\bbR
.
Instead it is a
quotient.

Lemma 25
.

\mathrm{un}_{0}:\bbR\Rightarrow\bbT^{\times}
is a morphism of monads.

Proof 4.1
.

We have to check that

\displaystyle\mathrm{sing}^{\times}=\mathrm{un}_{0}\circ\mathrm{sing}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{flat}^{\times}\circ\mathrm{un}_{0}\circ\bbR\mathrm{un}_{0}=\mathrm{un}_{0}\circ\mathrm{flat}\,.

The first equation immediately follows form the fact that
\mathrm{un}_{0}(\mathrm{sing}(a))=\mathrm{sing}(a)
.
For the second one, note that the vertices of
\mathrm{un}_{0}(\mathrm{flat}(g))
correspond to
the finite paths of
\mathrm{flat}(g)
, while those of
\mathrm{un}_{0}(\mathrm{flat}(\mathrm{un}_{0}(\bbR\mathrm{un}_{0}(g))))

correspond to those of
\mathrm{flat}(\mathrm{un}_{0}(\bbR\mathrm{un}_{0}(g)))
.
Furthermore, every path
\alpha
in a graph of the form
\mathrm{flat}(h)

corresponds to a path
(v_{n})_{n}
of
h
and a family of paths
\beta_{n}
of
h(v_{n})

such that
\alpha
can be identified with the concatenation
\beta_{0}\beta_{1}\dots
.
Finally, a path in
\mathrm{un}_{0}(h)
is the same as a path in
h
.
Consequently, each path of
\mathrm{flat}(\mathrm{un}_{0}(\bbR\mathrm{un}_{0}(g)))

corresponds to (i) a path of
g
together with (ii) a family of paths
in some components
g(v)
as above.
This correspondence induces a bijection between

\displaystyle\mathop{dom}\nolimits(\mathrm{un}_{0}(\mathrm{flat}(g)))\quad\text{and}\quad\mathop{dom}\nolimits(\mathrm{un}_{0}(\mathrm{flat}(\mathrm{un}_{0}(\bbR\mathrm{un}_{0}))))\,.

As this bijection preserves the labelling it follows that

\displaystyle\mathrm{un}_{0}(\mathrm{flat}(g))=\mathrm{un}_{0}(\mathrm{flat}(\mathrm{un}_{0}(\bbR\mathrm{un}_{0})))\,.

The fact that there is no distributive law for
\bbT^{\times}
follows directly from
Theorem
22
since
\bbT^{\times}
is not linear.
This means that our main goal is unreachable.
But having a distributive law between
\bbT^{\times}
and
\bbU
would be very useful.
For instance, it is needed when introducing regular expressions for infinite trees.
Therefore we will try to find a useable workaround, something weaker than an actual
distributive law that nevertheless covers the applications we have in mind.
The rest of this section is meant to get an overview over our options in this regard,
and to probe the dividing line between the possible and the impossible.

Remark 26
.

We have already mentioned above that, for cases where there is no distributive law,
there is the notion of a weak distributive law which often can be used instead.
Unfortunately, this does not work in our case since the problem above is
the monad multiplication, not the unit. (
\bbT^{\times}
is not even weakly linear.)

\lrcorner

4.1.
Infinite sorts

We start with some technical remarks considering sorts.
Below we will need to deal with trees with infinitely many different variables,
that is, we have to work in the category
\mathsf{Pos}^{\mathscr{P}(X)}
instead of
\mathsf{Pos}^{\Xi}
.
It is straightforward to extend the monads
\bbR
,
\bbT
, and
\bbT^{\times}
to this
more general setting.
Hence, let us consider the following situation: we are given two sets
\Delta\subseteq\Gamma

of sorts and a monad
\bbM
on
\mathsf{Pos}^{\Gamma}
. The following technical tools allow us to
translate between the associated categories
\mathsf{Pos}^{\Delta}
and
\mathsf{Pos}^{\Gamma}
.

{defi}

Let
\Delta\subseteq\Gamma
be sets of sorts.

(a) The
extension
of
A=(A_{\xi})_{\xi\in\Delta}\in\mathsf{Pos}^{\Delta}
to
\mathsf{Pos}^{\Gamma}

is the set
A^{\uparrow}\in\mathsf{Pos}^{\Gamma}
defined by

\displaystyle A^{\uparrow}_{\xi}:=\begin{cases}A_{\xi}&\text{if }\xi\in\Delta\,,\\
\emptyset&\text{otherwise}\,.\end{cases}

(b) The
restriction
of
A=(A_{\xi})_{\xi\in\Gamma}\in\mathsf{Pos}^{\Gamma}
to
\mathsf{Pos}^{\Delta}

is the set
A|_{\Delta}:=(A_{\xi})_{\xi\in\Delta}
.
Similarly, for a function
f:A\to B
in
\mathsf{Pos}^{\Gamma}
, we denote by

f|_{\Delta}:A|_{\Delta}\to B|_{\Delta}
the restriction to
\Delta
.
Finally, for an
\bbM
-algebra
\frakA=\langle A,\pi\rangle
,
we set

\displaystyle\frakA|_{\Delta}:=\langle A|_{\Delta},\pi|_{\Delta}\circ(\bbM i)|_{\Delta}\rangle\,,

where
i:(A|_{\Delta})^{\uparrow}\to A
is the inclusion map.

(c) The
restriction
of a functor
\bbM:\mathsf{Pos}^{\Gamma}\to\mathsf{Pos}^{\Gamma}
to
\mathsf{Pos}^{\Delta}

is the functor
\bbM|_{\Delta}:\mathsf{Pos}^{\Delta}\to\mathsf{Pos}^{\Delta}
defined by

\displaystyle\bbM|_{\Delta}A:=(\bbM(A^{\uparrow}))|_{\Delta}\,.

\lrcorner

{exa}

Let
\Delta:=\{\emptyset,\{x\}\}\subseteq\Xi
, for some fixed
x\in X
.
The monad
\bbT|_{\Delta}
is isomorphic to the functor

\displaystyle\bbM\langle X_{0},X_{1}\rangle=\langle X_{1}^{*}X_{0}+X_{1}^{\omega},\,X_{1}^{+}\rangle

(up to renaming of the sorts for readability) whose algebras are (ordered)
\omega
-semigroups

\langle S_{0},S_{1},\pi\rangle
.
The restriction
\bbM|_{\{1\}}X_{1}=X_{1}^{+}
is the monad for (ordered) semigroups,
while
\bbM|_{\{0\}}X_{0}=X_{0}
is just the identity monad.
Given an
\omega
-semigroup
\frakS=\langle S_{0},S_{1},\pi\rangle
, the corresponding
restrictions are the associated semigroup
\frakS|_{\{1\}}=\langle S_{1},\pi_{1}\rangle

and the set
\frakS|_{\{0\}}=\langle S_{0},\mathrm{id}\rangle
.

\lrcorner

Let us quickly check that these definitions make sense.

Lemma 27
.

Let
\langle\bbM,\mu,\varepsilon\rangle
be a monad on
\mathsf{Pos}^{\Gamma}
.

(a)

\bbM|_{\Delta}
forms a monad with multiplication

(\mu\circ\bbM i)|_{\Delta}
and unit map
\varepsilon|_{\Delta}
.

(b)

If
\frakA
is an
\bbM
-algebra, then
\frakA|_{\Delta}
is an

\bbM|_{\Delta}
-algebra.

Proof 4.2
.

To improve readability, let us denote the functor
({-})|_{\Delta}
by
R
and the
functor
({-})^{\uparrow}
by
E
. Then
\bbM|_{\Delta}=R\circ\bbM\circ E
.
We denote the inclusion
ER\Rightarrow\mathrm{Id}
by
i

and the identity function
\mathrm{Id}\Rightarrow RE
by
e
.
One can show that
E\dashv R
is an adjunction with unit
e
and counit
i
,
but for our purposes it is sufficient to note that we have the following equalities

\displaystyle i\circ Ee=\mathrm{id}\quad\text{and}\quad Ri\circ e=\mathrm{id}\,,

whose proofs are trivial.

(a) We have to check three axioms.

\displaystyle R(\mu\circ\bbM i)\circ R\varepsilon

\displaystyle=R(\mu\circ\bbM i)\circ R\varepsilon\circ e

\displaystyle=R(\mu\circ\varepsilon\circ i)\circ e

\displaystyle=Ri\circ e

\displaystyle=\mathrm{id}\,,

\displaystyle R(\mu\circ\bbM i)\circ\bbM|_{\Delta}R\varepsilon

\displaystyle=R(\mu\circ\bbM i)\circ\bbM|_{\Delta}(R\varepsilon\circ e)

\displaystyle=R\bigl(\mu\circ\bbM i\circ\bbM ER\varepsilon\circ\bbM Ee\bigr)

\displaystyle=R\bigl(\mu\circ\bbM(i\circ ER\varepsilon\circ Ee)\bigr)

\displaystyle=R\bigl(\mu\circ\bbM(\varepsilon\circ i\circ Ee)\bigr)

\displaystyle=R\bbM(\mathrm{id}\circ\bbM\mathrm{id})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{id}\,,

\displaystyle R(\mu\circ\bbM i)\circ R(\mu\circ\bbM i)

\displaystyle=R\bigl(\mu\circ\bbM i\circ\mu\circ\bbM i\bigr)

\displaystyle=R\bigl(\mu\circ\mu\circ\bbM\bbM i\circ\bbM i\bigr)

\displaystyle=R\bigl(\mu\circ\bbM\mu\circ\bbM(\bbM i\circ i)\bigr)

\displaystyle=R\bigl(\mu\circ\bbM(\mu\circ\bbM i\circ i\bigr)

\displaystyle=R\bigl(\mu\circ\bbM(i\circ ER(\mu\circ\bbM i))\bigr)

\displaystyle=R\bigl(\mu\circ\bbM i\circ\bbM ER(\mu\circ\bbM i)\bigr)

\displaystyle=R(\mu\circ\bbM i)\circ\bbM|_{\Delta}R(\mu\circ\bbM i)\,.

(b)
Note that the product has the correct type since

\displaystyle R(\pi\circ\bbM i):R\bbM ERA\to RA\quad\text{and}\quad\bbM|_{\Delta}(A|_{\Delta})=R\bbM ERA\,.

For the axioms of an
\bbM|_{\Delta}
-algebra, we have

\displaystyle R(\pi\circ\bbM i)\circ R\varepsilon

\displaystyle=R(\pi\circ\bbM i\circ\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=R(\pi\circ\varepsilon\circ i)

\displaystyle=Ri

\displaystyle=\mathrm{id}\,,

\displaystyle R(\pi\circ\bbM i)\circ\bbM|_{\Delta}R(\pi\circ\bbM i)

\displaystyle=R\bigl(\pi\circ\bbM(i\circ ER(\pi\circ\bbM i))\bigr)

\displaystyle=R\bigl(\pi\circ\bbM(\pi\circ\bbM i\circ i)\bigr)

\displaystyle=R\bigl(\pi\circ\mu\circ\bbM(\bbM i\circ i)\bigr)

\displaystyle=R\bigl(\pi\circ\bbM i\circ\mu\circ\bbM i\bigr)

\displaystyle=R(\pi\circ\bbM i)\circ R(\mu\circ\bbM i)\,.

In the remainder of this section, we work in the category
\mathsf{Pos}^{\Xi_{+}}

where
\Xi_{+}:=\mathscr{P}(\omega)
.
The functors
\bbR
,
\bbT
, and
\bbT^{\times}
have canonical extensions to this
category, which we will denote by the same letters to keep notation readable.

4.2.
The action on the variables

The problem with finding a distributive law for
\bbT^{\times}
is that this monad is not linear.
Its multiplication contains an unravelling operation
\mathrm{un}_{0}
which is used to duplicate
arguments for variables appearing multiple times.
To continue we need a variant of this operation that also modifies the variables
of the given graph.

{defi}

Let
g\in\bbR_{\zeta}A
be a graph.

(a)
For a surjective function
\sigma:\zeta\to\xi
, we denote by
{}^{\sigma}g\in\bbR_{\xi}A

the graph obtained from
g
by replacing each variable
x
by
\sigma(x)
.

(b)
We set

\displaystyle\mathrm{un}(g):=\langle\sigma,t\rangle\,,

where
t
is the tree obtained from the unravelling
\mathrm{un}_{0}(g)
by renaming the variables
so that each of them appears exactly once (note that this changes the sort)
and
\sigma
is the function such that
{}^{\sigma}t=\mathrm{un}_{0}(g)
.
(To make this well-defined, we can fix a standard well-ordering on the domain,
say, the length-lexicographic one, and we number the variables in increasing order
with respect to this ordering, i.e., if
v_{0}<_{\mathrm{llex}}v_{1}<_{\mathrm{llex}}\dots

is an enumeration of all vertices labelled by a variable, we set

t(v_{i}):=x_{i}
, where
x_{0},x_{1},\dots
is some fixed sequence of variables.)

(c) We denote by
\bbT^{\circ}A
the set of trees
t\in\bbT^{\times}A
such that

\mathrm{un}(t)=\langle\mathrm{id},t\rangle
.
Let
\iota:\bbT^{\circ}\Rightarrow\bbT^{\times}
be the inclusion.
(In actual calculations we will frequently omit
\iota
to keep the notation simple.)

\lrcorner

Remark 28
.

Note that the operation
\mathrm{un}
can introduce infinitely many different variables.
This is the reason why we have to work in
\mathsf{Pos}^{\Xi_{+}}
.

\lrcorner

{exa}

\mathrm{un}(a(x,y,x))=\langle\sigma,a(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2})\rangle
where
the function
\sigma
maps
x_{0},x_{1},x_{2}
to
x,y,x
.
Then
{}^{\sigma}a(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2})=a(x,y,z)
.

\lrcorner

To make sense of the type of the above operations, we introduce the following monad
where every element is annotated by some function renaming the variables.

{defi}

(a)
We define a functor
\bbX:\mathsf{Pos}^{\Xi_{+}}\to\mathsf{Pos}^{\Xi_{+}}
as follows.
For
A\in\mathsf{Pos}^{\Xi_{+}}
, we set

\displaystyle\bbX_{\xi}A:=\{\,\langle\sigma,a\rangle a\in A_{\zeta}\,,\ \sigma:\zeta\to\xi\text{ surjective}\,\}\,.

We define the order on
\bbX_{\xi}A
by

\displaystyle\langle\sigma,a\rangle\leq\langle\tau,b\rangle\quad\@defiff\quad\sigma=\tau\quad\text{and}\quad a\leq b\,.

For a morphism
f:A\to B
, we define
\bbX f:\bbX A\to\bbX B
by

\displaystyle\bbX f(\langle\sigma,a\rangle):=\langle\sigma,f(a)\rangle\,.

(b) We define functions
\mathrm{comp}:\bbX\bbX A\to\bbX A
and
\mathrm{in}:A\to\bbX A
by

\displaystyle\mathrm{comp}(\langle\tau,\langle\sigma,a\rangle\rangle):=\langle\tau\circ\sigma,a\rangle\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{in}(a):=\langle\mathrm{id},a\rangle\,.

\lrcorner

Lemma 29
.

\langle\bbX,\mathrm{comp},\mathrm{in}\rangle
and
\langle\bbT^{\circ},\mathrm{flat},\mathrm{sing}\rangle
are monads.

The set
\bbT^{\times}A
carries a canonical structure of a
\bbX
-algebra.

{defi}

For
\langle\sigma,t\rangle\in\bbX\bbT^{\times}A
, we define the
reconstitution operation

\displaystyle\mathrm{re}(\langle\sigma,t\rangle):={}^{\sigma}t\in\bbT^{\times}A\,.

We denote its restriction to
\bbX\bbT^{\circ}
by

\mathrm{re}_{0}:=\mathrm{re}\circ\bbX\iota:\bbX\bbT^{\circ}\Rightarrow\bbT^{\times}
.

\lrcorner

The unravelling operation on trees can now be formalised using the following
two natural transformations.

Lemma 30
.

The inclusion morphism
\iota:\bbT^{\circ}\Rightarrow\bbT^{\times}
is a morphism of monads.
The functions

\displaystyle\mathrm{un}:\bbT^{\times}\Rightarrow\bbX\bbT^{\circ},\quad\mathrm{re}_{0}:\bbX\bbT^{\circ}\Rightarrow\bbT^{\times},\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{re}:\bbX\bbT^{\times}\Rightarrow\bbT^{\times}

form natural transformations satisfying the following equations.

(a)

\mathrm{re}_{0}\circ\mathrm{un}=\mathrm{id}

(b)

\mathrm{un}\circ\mathrm{re}=\mathrm{comp}\circ\bbX\mathrm{un}

(c)

\mathrm{un}\circ\iota=\mathrm{in}

(d)

\mathrm{re}_{0}\circ\mathrm{comp}=\mathrm{re}\circ\bbX\mathrm{re}_{0}

(e)

\mathrm{flat}^{\times}\circ\mathrm{re}_{0}=\mathrm{re}\circ\bbX(\mathrm{flat}^{\times}\circ\iota)

(f)

\mathrm{re}\circ\mathrm{in}=\mathrm{id}

(g)

\mathrm{un}\circ\mathrm{re}_{0}=\mathrm{id}

Proof 4.3
.

The fact that
\iota
is a morphism of monads is straightforward.
To see that
\mathrm{un}
is natural, it is sufficient to note that

\displaystyle\mathrm{un}(t)=\langle\sigma,s\rangle\quad\@iff\quad\mathrm{un}(\bbT^{\times}f(t))=\langle\sigma,\bbT^{\circ}f(s)\rangle\,,

for every function
f:A\to B
.
For
\mathrm{re}
, we have

\displaystyle\bbT^{\times}f(\mathrm{re}(\langle\sigma,t\rangle))

\displaystyle=\bbT^{\times}f({}^{\sigma}t)

\displaystyle={}^{\sigma}\bigl(\bbT^{\times}f(t)\bigr)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{re}(\langle\sigma,\bbT^{\times}f(t)\rangle)=\mathrm{re}(\bbX\bbT^{\times}f(\langle\sigma,t\rangle))\,.

Since
\mathrm{re}_{0}=\mathrm{re}\circ\bbX\iota
, this implies that
\mathrm{re}_{0}
is natural as well.

(a) Note that
\mathrm{re}_{0}\circ\mathrm{un}=\mathrm{id}
holds since

\displaystyle\mathrm{un}(t)=\langle\sigma,s\rangle\quad\text{implies}\quad{}^{\sigma}s=t\,,\quad\text{for trees }t\in\bbT^{\times}A\,.

(b) Suppose that
\mathrm{un}(t)=\langle\sigma,s\rangle
and
\mathrm{un}({}^{\tau}t)=\langle\rho,r\rangle
.
Then

\displaystyle{}^{\tau\circ\sigma}s={}^{\tau}t={}^{\rho}r\,.

In particular,
s
and
r
only differ in the labelling of the variables.
But
s,r\in\bbT^{\circ}A
implies that the variables appear in the same order
in both trees. Hence,
s=r
and it follows that
\tau\circ\sigma=\rho
.
Consequently,

\displaystyle\mathrm{un}(\mathrm{re}(\langle\tau,t\rangle))

\displaystyle=\langle\rho,r\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\tau\circ\sigma,s\rangle=\mathrm{comp}(\langle\tau,\langle\sigma,s\rangle\rangle)=\mathrm{comp}(\bbX\mathrm{un}(\langle\tau,t\rangle))\,.

(c)–(f) We have

\displaystyle\mathrm{un}(\iota(t))

\displaystyle=\langle\mathrm{id},t\rangle=\mathrm{in}(t)\,,

\displaystyle\mathrm{re}_{0}\bigl(\mathrm{comp}\bigl(\langle\sigma,\langle\tau,t\rangle\rangle\bigr)\bigr)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{re}_{0}(\langle\sigma\circ\tau,t\rangle)

\displaystyle={}^{\sigma\circ\tau}\iota(t)

\displaystyle={}^{\sigma}({}^{\tau}\iota(t))

\displaystyle={}^{\sigma}\mathrm{re}_{0}(\langle\tau,t\rangle)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{re}\bigl(\bigl\langle\sigma,\mathrm{re}_{0}(\langle\tau,t\rangle)\bigr\rangle\bigr)=\mathrm{re}\bigl(\bbX\mathrm{re}_{0}\bigl(\langle\sigma,\langle\tau,t\rangle\rangle\bigr)\bigr)\,,

\displaystyle\mathrm{flat}^{\times}(\mathrm{re}_{0}(\langle\sigma,t\rangle))

\displaystyle=\mathrm{flat}^{\times}({}^{\sigma}\iota(t))

\displaystyle={}^{\sigma}(\mathrm{flat}^{\times}\circ\iota)(t)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{re}\bigl(\bigl\langle\sigma,(\mathrm{flat}^{\times}\circ\iota)(t)\bigr\rangle\bigr)=(\mathrm{re}\circ\bbX(\mathrm{flat}^{\times}\circ\iota))(\langle\sigma,t\rangle)\,,

\displaystyle\mathrm{re}(\mathrm{in}(t))

\displaystyle=\mathrm{re}(\langle\mathrm{id},t\rangle)={}^{\mathrm{id}}t=t\,.

(g) By (c), we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{un}\circ\mathrm{re}_{0}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{un}\circ\mathrm{re}\circ\bbX\iota=\mathrm{comp}\circ\bbX\mathrm{un}\circ\bbX\iota=\mathrm{comp}\circ\bbX\mathrm{in}=\mathrm{id}\,.

We can understand point (a) of this lemma as saying that
\bbT^{\times}

is a retract of
\bbX\bbT^{\circ}
, but only as functors, not necessarily as monads.
For the latter we first have to establish that
\bbX\bbT^{\circ}
forms a monad
and that the operations
\mathrm{un}
and
\mathrm{re}_{0}
are morphisms of monads.

Proposition 31
.

(a)

\bbX\bbT^{\circ}
forms a monad with multiplication

\displaystyle\mathrm{un}\circ\mathrm{re}\circ\bbX(\mathrm{flat}^{\times}\circ\iota\circ\bbT^{\circ}\mathrm{re}_{0}):\bbX\bbT^{\circ}\bbX\bbT^{\circ}\Rightarrow\bbX\bbT^{\circ}

and unit

\displaystyle\mathrm{in}\circ\mathrm{sing}:\mathrm{Id}\Rightarrow\bbX\bbT^{\circ}\,.

(b)

\mathrm{re}_{0}:\bbX\bbT^{\circ}\Rightarrow\bbT^{\times}

and
\mathrm{un}:\bbT^{\times}\Rightarrow\bbX\bbT^{\circ}
are isomorphisms of monads.

(c)

\mathrm{in}:\bbT^{\circ}\Rightarrow\bbX\bbT^{\circ}
is an injective morphism of monads.

Proof 4.4
.

(a), (b)
By Lemma
30
(c), (e), and (a), we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{re}_{0}\circ\mathrm{in}\circ\mathrm{sing}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{re}_{0}\circ\mathrm{un}\circ\iota\circ\mathrm{sing}=\iota\circ\mathrm{sing}=\mathrm{sing}^{\times},

\displaystyle\mathrm{flat}^{\times}\circ\mathrm{re}_{0}\circ\bbX\bbT^{\circ}\mathrm{re}_{0}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{re}\circ\bbX(\mathrm{flat}^{\times}\circ\iota\circ\bbT^{\circ}\mathrm{re}_{0})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{re}_{0}\circ\mathrm{un}\circ\mathrm{re}\circ\bbX(\mathrm{flat}^{\times}\circ\iota\circ\bbT^{\circ}\mathrm{re}_{0})\,.

As
\mathrm{re}_{0}
is a surjective natural transformation, most of the claim therefore follows
by Lemma
2
.
It only remains to check that
\mathrm{un}
is also a morphism of monads.
For this, note that by Lemma
30
(c), (a), and (e) we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{in}\circ\mathrm{sing}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{un}\circ\iota\circ\mathrm{sing}=\mathrm{un}\circ\mathrm{sing}^{\times}\,,

\displaystyle\mathrm{un}\circ\mathrm{flat}^{\times}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{un}\circ\mathrm{flat}^{\times}\circ\mathrm{re}_{0}\circ\mathrm{un}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{un}\circ\mathrm{re}\circ\bbX(\mathrm{flat}^{\times}\circ\iota)\circ\mathrm{un}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{un}\circ\mathrm{re}\circ\bbX(\mathrm{flat}^{\times}\circ\iota)\circ\mathrm{un}\circ\bbT^{\times}(\mathrm{re}_{0}\circ\mathrm{un})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{un}\circ\mathrm{re}\circ\bbX(\mathrm{flat}^{\times}\circ\iota\circ\bbT^{\circ}\mathrm{re}_{0})\circ\mathrm{un}\circ\bbT^{\times}\mathrm{un}\,.

(c) As
\mathrm{un}
and
\iota
are morphisms of monads, so is
\mathrm{un}\circ\iota=\mathrm{in}
.

Corollary 32
.

\bbT^{\times}\cong\bbX\bbT^{\circ}
(as monads)

One could hope to construct a distributive law
\bbT^{\circ}\bbX\Rightarrow\bbX\bbT^{\circ}

by applying the Theorem of Beck (Theorem
2
) to the monad
structure on
\bbX\bbT^{\circ}
. This does not work for the following reason.

Lemma 33
.

The natural transformation
\bbX\mathrm{sing}:\bbX\Rightarrow\bbX\bbT^{\circ}
is
not

a morphism of monads.

Proof 4.5
.

The following of the two axioms fails:

\displaystyle\bbX(\mathrm{flat}^{\times}\circ\iota\circ\bbT^{\circ}\mathrm{re}_{0})\circ\bbX\mathrm{sing}\circ\bbX\bbX\mathrm{sing}\neq\bbX\mathrm{sing}\circ\mathrm{comp}.

To see this, fix
\langle\sigma,\langle\tau,a\rangle\rangle\in\bbX\bbX A
.
Then

\displaystyle(\bbX(\mathrm{flat}^{\times}\circ\iota\circ\bbT^{\circ}\mathrm{re}_{0})\circ\bbX\mathrm{sing}\circ\bbX\bbX\mathrm{sing})(\langle\sigma,\langle\tau,a\rangle\rangle)

\displaystyle{}={}

\displaystyle\bbX(\mathrm{flat}^{\times}\circ\iota\circ\bbT^{\circ}\mathrm{re}_{0}\circ\mathrm{sing}\circ\bbX\mathrm{sing})(\langle\sigma,\langle\tau,a\rangle\rangle)

\displaystyle{}={}

\displaystyle\bbX(\mathrm{flat}^{\times}\circ\iota\circ\mathrm{sing}\circ\mathrm{re}_{0}\circ\bbX\mathrm{sing})(\langle\sigma,\langle\tau,a\rangle\rangle)

\displaystyle{}={}

\displaystyle\bbX(\mathrm{re}_{0}\circ\bbX\mathrm{sing})(\langle\sigma,\langle\tau,a\rangle\rangle)

\displaystyle{}={}

\displaystyle\langle\sigma,{}^{\tau}\mathrm{sing}(a)\rangle\,,

whereas

\displaystyle(\bbX\mathrm{sing}\circ\mathrm{comp})(\langle\sigma,\langle\tau,a\rangle\rangle)

\displaystyle{}={}

\displaystyle\bbX\mathrm{sing}(\langle\sigma\circ\tau,a\rangle)

\displaystyle{}={}

\displaystyle\langle\sigma\circ\tau,\mathrm{sing}(a)\rangle\,.

For
\tau\neq\mathrm{id}
, these two values are different.

4.3.
Graphs and unravellings

The next step is to transfer the unravelling operation from
\bbT^{\times}A

to arbitrary sets.

{defi}

(a)
An
unravelling structure

\langle A,\mathrm{re},\mathrm{un}\rangle

consists of a set
A\in\mathsf{Pos}^{\Xi_{+}}
equipped with two functions

\displaystyle\mathrm{re}:\bbX A\to A\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{un}:A\to\bbX A

such that
\langle A,\mathrm{re}\rangle
forms a
\bbX
-algebra while
\mathrm{un}
satisfies

\displaystyle\bbX\mathrm{un}\circ\mathrm{un}=\bbX\mathrm{in}\circ\mathrm{un}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{re}\circ\mathrm{un}=\mathrm{id}\,.

We call
\mathrm{un}(a)
the
unravelling
of
a
.
To keep notation simple, we write

\displaystyle{}^{\sigma}a:=\mathrm{re}(\langle\sigma,a\rangle)\,.

(b) A
morphism of unravelling structures
is a function
\varphi:A\to B

satisfying

\displaystyle\mathrm{un}\circ\varphi=\bbX\varphi\circ\mathrm{un}\quad\text{and}\quad\varphi\circ\mathrm{re}=\mathrm{re}\circ\bbX\varphi\,.

\lrcorner

Clearly, the operations
\mathrm{re}
and
\mathrm{un}
defined above for trees
t\in\bbT^{\times}A

induce an unravelling structure on
\bbT^{\times}A
.
But note that this is not the case for
\bbR A
since we have

\mathrm{re}(\mathrm{un}(g))\neq g
, for every
g\in\bbR A
that is not a tree.

{exa}

For each
\bbT^{\times}
-algebra
\frakA=\langle A,\pi\rangle
,
we can equip the universe
A
with the
trivial
unravelling structure where

\displaystyle\mathrm{un}:=\mathrm{in}\quad\text{and}\quad{}^{\sigma}a:=\pi({}^{\sigma}\mathrm{sing}(a))\,.

\lrcorner

Remark 34
.

Note that the monad multiplication
\mathrm{flat}^{\times}
is

not
a morphism of unravelling structures since

\mathrm{un}\circ\mathrm{flat}^{\times}\neq\bbX\mathrm{flat}^{\times}\circ\mathrm{un}
.
In what follows we will therefore
not
work in the category of unravelling structures
and their morphisms. Instead we will work in the weaker category of unravelling structures
with arbitrary monotone maps as morphisms.

\lrcorner

As a technical tool, we use the following generalisation of the unravelling relation for graphs
where we do not only unravel the graph itself but also each label.
The intuition is as follows. Suppose we are given a relation
\theta\subseteq A\times B

and a graph
h\in\bbR B
.
We construct an (unravelled) graph
g\in\bbR A
as follows.
Starting at the root
v
, we pick some element
c\mathrel{\theta}h(v)
,
and label
g(v)
by the unravelling of
c
.
Then we recursively choose labellings for the successors.
Note that the shapes of
g
and
h
are different since we are unravelling
g
,
so the labels in
h
might have a higher arity than the corresponding ones in
g
.
Consequently, we simultaneously construct a graph homomorphism
\varphi:g\to h

to keep track of which vertices of
g
correspond to which ones of
h
.

To simplify the definition, we will split the construction into two stages.
In the first step we apply the unravelling operation to every label of
h
,
resulting in a graph
\bbR\mathrm{un}(h)\in\bbR\bbX B
.
What is then left for the second step is the following relation,
which does the choosing of the label and the unravelling of the tree.
What makes this operation complicated is the fact that the unravelling depends on the chosen
label, while the label may depend on which copy (produced by previous unravelling steps)
of a vertex we are at. So we cannot separate the second stage into two independent phases.

{defi}

(a)
Let
g\in\bbR_{\xi}A
and
h\in\bbR_{\zeta}B
.
A
graph homomorphism
is a function
\varphi:\mathop{dom}\nolimits(g)\to\mathop{dom}\nolimits(h)
such that

•

\varphi
maps the root of
g
to the root of
h
;

•

\varphi(u)
is a successor of
\varphi(v)
if, and only if,
u
is a successor of
v

(not necessarily with the same edge labelling); and

•

\varphi(v)
is labelled by a variable if, and only if,
v
is labelled by one.

(b) Suppose that
\varphi:g\to h
is a surjective graph homomorphism and
let
v\in\mathop{dom}\nolimits(g)
be a vertex of sort
\xi
with successors
(u_{x})_{x\in\xi}

and suppose that
\varphi(v)
has sort
\zeta
.
We denote by
\varphi_{/v}:\xi\to\zeta
the function such that

\displaystyle\varphi(u_{x})\text{ is the $\varphi_{/v}(x)$-successor of }\varphi(v)\,.

(c)
Let
s\in\bbR A
,
t\in\bbR B
, and
\theta\subseteq\bbX A\times B
.
We write

\displaystyle\varphi,\sigma:s\mathrel{\theta}^{\mathrm{sel}}t

if the following conditions are satisfied.

•

s\in\bbT^{\circ}A

•

\varphi:s\to t
is a surjective graph homomorphism.

•

\sigma:\xi\to\zeta
is surjective.

•

\langle\varphi_{/v},s(v)\rangle\mathrel{\theta}t(\varphi(v))\,,\quad\text{for every }v\in\mathop{dom}\nolimits_{0}(g)\,.

•

\textstyle\sigma(s(v))=t(\varphi(v))\,,

if
s(v)=x
is a variable.

\lrcorner

We are mostly interested in the cases where
\theta
is either the identity
=

or set membership
\in
. The resulting relations are

\displaystyle\varphi,\sigma

\displaystyle:s=^{\mathrm{sel}}t\,,

\displaystyle\quad\text{for }s\in\bbT^{\times}A\text{ and }t\in\bbT^{\times}\bbX A\,,

\displaystyle\varphi,\sigma

\displaystyle:s\in^{\mathrm{sel}}t\,,

\displaystyle\quad\text{for }s\in\bbT^{\times}A\text{ and }t\in\bbT^{\times}\bbU\bbX A\,.

Combining them with the unravelling operation as explained above, we obtain the relations

\displaystyle\varphi,\sigma

\displaystyle:s=^{\mathrm{un}}t

\displaystyle\quad\@defiff\quad

\displaystyle\varphi,\sigma

\displaystyle:s=^{\mathrm{sel}}\bbR\mathrm{un}(t)\,,

\displaystyle\varphi,\sigma

\displaystyle:s\in^{\mathrm{un}}t\,,

\displaystyle\quad\@defiff\quad

\displaystyle\varphi,\sigma

\displaystyle:s\in^{\mathrm{sel}}\bbR\bbU\mathrm{un}(t)\,.

{exa}

We have
\varphi,\sigma:g\in^{\mathrm{un}}h
where
g
is the tree on the left,

h
the one on the right,
\varphi:g\to h

\sigma:\{x,y,z\}\to\{x\}

\lrcorner

Remark 35
.

(a)
For every graph
g

(b)
Note that

\displaystyle\varphi,\sigma:g=^{\mathrm{sel}}k\quad\text{and}\quad k\mathrel{\theta}^{\bbR}h\quad\text{implies}\quad\varphi,\sigma:g\mathrel{\theta}^{\mathrm{sel}}h\,,

but the converse is generally not true since the function

\lrcorner

Let us derive an algebraic description of the relation

We introduce a function

\displaystyle\langle\sigma,s\rangle=\mathrm{un}^{+}(t)\quad\@iff\quad\varphi,\sigma:s=^{\mathrm{sel}}t\,,\quad\text{for some }\varphi\,,

{defi}

(a) For a set

\displaystyle\mathrm{un}^{+}:=\mathrm{un}\circ\mathrm{flat}^{\times}\circ\bbT^{\times}(\mathrm{re}_{0}\circ\bbX\mathrm{sing})\,.

\mathrm{dun}:\bbT^{\times}A\to\bbX\bbT^{\circ}A

\displaystyle\mathrm{dun}:=\mathrm{un}^{+}\circ\bbT^{\times}\mathrm{un}\,.

\lrcorner

{exa}

To understand the definition of
\mathrm{un}^{+}

\displaystyle\langle\sigma,s\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathrm{un}^{+}(t)

\displaystyle\quad\@iff\quad\varphi,\sigma

\displaystyle:s=^{\mathrm{sel}}t\,,\quad\text{for some }\varphi\,,

\displaystyle\langle\sigma,s\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathrm{dun}(t)

\displaystyle:s=^{\mathrm{un}}t\,,\quad\text{for some }\varphi\,.

.

\displaystyle s(v)=\mathrm{flat}^{\times}(r)(v)=r(\varphi(v))({\mathchoice{\mbox{$\displaystyle\langle\rangle$}}{\mbox{$\textstyle\langle\rangle$}}{\mbox{$\scriptstyle\langle\rangle$}}{\mbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\langle\rangle$}}})=r(\varphi^{\prime}(v))({\mathchoice{\mbox{$\displaystyle\langle\rangle$}}{\mbox{$\textstyle\langle\rangle$}}{\mbox{$\scriptstyle\langle\rangle$}}{\mbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\langle\rangle$}}})=s^{\prime}(v)\,.

Lemma 37
.

(a)

\bbX(\mathrm{un}\circ\mathrm{flat}^{\times}\circ\iota)\circ\mathrm{dun}=\bbX(\mathrm{in}\circ\mathrm{flat}^{\times}\circ\iota)\circ\mathrm{dun}

(b)

(c)

Proof 4.7
.

(a)

\langle\sigma,s\rangle=\mathrm{dun}(t)
.

\varphi,\sigma:s=^{\mathrm{un}}t
.

This implies that
\mathrm{flat}(s)\in\bbT^{\circ}A

\mathrm{un}(\mathrm{flat}(s))=\langle\mathrm{id},\mathrm{flat}(s)\rangle

\displaystyle\bbX(\mathrm{un}\circ\mathrm{flat})(\mathrm{dun}(t))

\displaystyle=\langle\sigma,\mathrm{un}(\mathrm{flat}(s))\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\sigma,\langle\mathrm{id},\mathrm{flat}(s)\rangle\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\sigma,\mathrm{in}(\mathrm{flat}(s))\rangle=\bbX(\mathrm{in}\circ\mathrm{flat})(\mathrm{dun}(t))\,.

(b)
From Lemma

\displaystyle\mathrm{flat}^{\times}\circ\mathrm{re}_{0}\circ\bbX\mathrm{sing}\circ\mathrm{un}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{re}_{0}\circ\bbX(\mathrm{flat}^{\times}\circ\iota)\circ\bbX\mathrm{sing}\circ\mathrm{un}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{re}_{0}\circ\bbX(\mathrm{flat}^{\times}\circ\mathrm{sing}^{\times})\circ\mathrm{un}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{id}\,.

\displaystyle\mathrm{flat}^{\times}\circ\mathrm{re}\circ\mathrm{dun}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{flat}^{\times}\circ\mathrm{re}\circ\mathrm{un}\circ\mathrm{flat}^{\times}\circ\bbT^{\times}(\mathrm{re}_{0}\circ\bbX\mathrm{sing}\circ\mathrm{un})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{flat}^{\times}\circ\mathrm{flat}^{\times}\circ\bbT^{\times}(\mathrm{re}_{0}\circ\bbX\mathrm{sing}\circ\mathrm{un})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{flat}^{\times}\circ\bbT^{\times}\mathrm{flat}^{\times}\circ\bbT^{\times}(\mathrm{re}_{0}\circ\bbX\mathrm{sing}\circ\mathrm{un})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{flat}^{\times}\circ\bbT^{\times}\mathrm{id}

(c)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{comp}\circ\bbX(\mathrm{in}\circ\mathrm{flat}^{\times}\circ\iota)\circ\mathrm{dun}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{comp}\circ\bbX\mathrm{un}\circ\bbX(\mathrm{flat}^{\times}\circ\iota)\circ\mathrm{dun}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{un}\circ\mathrm{flat}^{\times}\circ\bbT^{\times}\mathrm{sing}

4.4.
A partial distributive law

The idea to find our partial distributive law is to work in the category
of unravelling structures, although this does not solve our problems entirely.
First of all, there is no obvious way to lift the functor
\bbU
to unravelling structures.
Given an unravelling structure
A
, we can define an ‘unravelling map’

\bbU\mathrm{un}:\bbU A\to\bbU\bbX A
, but we would need one of the form
\bbU A\to\bbX\bbU A
,
and there is no natural transformation
\bbU\bbX\Rightarrow\bbX\bbU
.
The functor
\bbT^{\times}
on the other hand
can
be lifted to the category
of unravelling structures, but only in a trivial way:
given
A
we can forget its unravelling structure, construct
\bbT^{\times}A
,
and equip it with the canonical unravelling structure
defined above (which does not depend on that of
A
).
In particular, with this definition the monad multiplication
\mathrm{flat}^{\times}
would
not be a morphism of the resulting unravelling structure.
What would be more useful would be a lift that uses deep unravelling
\mathrm{dun}
as the unravelling
operation on
\bbT^{\times}A
. But there is no corresponding reconstitution operation
\mathrm{re}

satisfying
\mathrm{re}\circ\mathrm{dun}=\mathrm{id}
.

What we will do instead is to use an ad-hoc argument showing how to define a lift of
\bbU

to sufficiently well-behaved
\bbT^{\times}
-algebras.
We are mainly interested in free
\bbT^{\times}
-algebras, but a slightly more abstract
definition helps to make the proof more modular.
We extract the needed properties of the algebras in question in the following technical
definition.

{defi}

We say that a
\bbT^{\times}
-algebra
\frakA=\langle A,\pi\rangle

supports unravelling

if its universe
A
can be equipped with an unravelling structure that
satisfies the following conditions.

\displaystyle\pi\circ\mathrm{re}\circ\bbX\mathrm{sing}^{\times}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{re}\,,

\displaystyle\mathrm{un}\circ\mathrm{re}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{comp}\circ\bbX\mathrm{un}\,,

\displaystyle\bbX(\mathrm{un}\circ\pi\circ\iota)\circ\mathrm{dun}

\displaystyle=\bbX(\mathrm{in}\circ\pi\circ\iota)\circ\mathrm{dun}\,.

\lrcorner

The intended target for this definition are the free algebras.
We start by noting that these satisfy the above conditions.

Proposition 41

\bbT^{\times}

\langle\bbT^{\times}A,\mathrm{flat}^{\times}\rangle

\displaystyle=\bbU(\pi\circ\mathrm{re}_{0})\circ\mathrm{sel}\circ\bbT^{\times}(\bbU\bbX\pi\circ\mathrm{sel}\circ\bbT^{\times}\bbU\mathrm{un})

\displaystyle=\bbU(\pi\circ\bbT^{\circ}\pi\circ\mathrm{re}_{0})\circ\mathrm{sel}\circ\bbT^{\times}(\mathrm{sel}\circ\bbT^{\times}\bbU\mathrm{un})

\displaystyle=\bbU(\pi\circ\mathrm{flat}^{\times}\circ\mathrm{re}_{0})\circ\mathrm{sel}\circ\bbT^{\times}(\mathrm{sel}\circ\bbT^{\times}\bbU\mathrm{un})

\displaystyle=\bbU(\pi\circ\mathrm{re}_{0}\circ\bbX\mathrm{flat}^{\times})\circ\mathrm{sel}\circ\bbT^{\times}(\mathrm{sel}\circ\bbT^{\times}\bbU\mathrm{un})

\displaystyle=\bbU(\pi\circ\mathrm{re}_{0})\circ\mathrm{sel}\circ\mathrm{flat}^{\times}\circ\bbT^{\times}\bbT^{\times}\bbU\mathrm{un}

\displaystyle=\bbU(\pi\circ\mathrm{re}_{0})\circ\mathrm{sel}\circ\bbT^{\times}\bbU\mathrm{un}\circ\mathrm{flat}^{\times}

\displaystyle=\hat{\pi}\circ\mathrm{flat}^{\times}\,.

where the third step follows by Lemma

We use term notation

5.
Substitutions

As a first application of the tools we have developed above, let us take a look at substitutions
for tree languages.
We present a simplified account of a recent result by Camino et al.
[
CDD+22
]

about finding solutions to inequalities of the form
\sigma[L]\subseteq R

for regular tree languages
L
and
R
.
This simplification stems mainly from the terminology and notation introduced above.
It does not rely on the results we have proved, except for Lemma
47
,
which depends on Theorem
44
.
In the next section we will give a second, more involved application that makes use of
Theorem
44
in a more substantial way.

{defi}

Let
\Sigma
be an alphabet.

(a)
A
substitution
is a function
\sigma:X\to\bbU\bbT^{\times}\Sigma
.
We call
\sigma

regular
if every
\sigma(x)\subseteq\bbT^{\times}\Sigma
is
a regular tree language.

(b)
A substitution
\sigma:X\to\bbU\bbT^{\times}\Sigma

induces a function
\bbT^{\times}X\to\bbU\bbT^{\times}\Sigma
in two different ways.
The
inside-out
morphism
\sigma_{\mathrm{io}}
is defined by

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{io}}(t):=\{\,\mathrm{flat}^{\times}(s)s\in^{\bbR}\bbR\sigma(t)\,\}\,,

while the
outside-in
morphism

is defined by

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{oi}}(t):=\{\,\mathrm{flat}^{\times}({}^{\sigma}s)\varphi,\sigma:s\in^{\mathrm{un}}\bbR\sigma(t)\,\}\,.

\lrcorner

Remark 46
.

(a) The reader should compare the simple definition above with the much more involved one
given in
[
CDD+22
]
.
As it turns out such simplifications are not uncommon when using the monadic framework.

(b)
Intuitively, the difference between these two variants is that, with the inside-out

for every vertex

The former has the advantage of simplicity, but the latter turns out to be

of
\bbT^{\times}
-algebras.

(c)
In the notation of Section
4
, we can rewrite the above definitions as

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{io}}

\displaystyle=\bbU\mathrm{flat}^{\times}\circ\mathrm{dist}\circ\bbT^{\times}\sigma\,,

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{oi}}

\displaystyle=\bbU(\mathrm{flat}^{\times}\circ\mathrm{re}_{0})\circ\mathrm{sel}\circ\bbT^{\times}(\bbU\mathrm{un}\circ\sigma)\,.

Hence,

.

\lrcorner

For the next lemma, let us recall from Theorem

that

\bbU\bbT^{\times}\Sigma

Lemma 47
.

\sigma_{\mathrm{oi}}:\bbT^{\times}X\to\bbU\bbT^{\times}\Sigma

\bbT^{\times}
-algebras.

Proof 5.1
.

According to Theorem

,
the product of the algebra

\displaystyle\hat{\pi}:=\bbU(\mathrm{flat}^{\times}\circ\mathrm{re}_{0})\circ\mathrm{sel}\circ\bbT^{\times}\bbU\mathrm{un}\,.

Note that the function

\lrcorner

For the simpler inside-out substitutions, we can solve inequalities

{thmC}
[
[

]
]

(c)

Proof 5.2
.

Since

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}(x):=\bigl\{\,s\in\bbT^{\times}\Sigma\bigm\eta(s)\in\bbU\eta(\rho(x))\,\bigr\}\,.

Then we have
\bbU\eta\circ\hat{\rho}=\bbU\eta\circ\rho

Note that we can rewrite the definition of

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathrm{io}}=\bbU\mathrm{flat}^{\times}\circ\mathrm{dist}\circ\bbT^{\times}\rho\,.

It follows that

\displaystyle\bbU\eta\circ\rho_{\mathrm{io}}

\displaystyle=\bbU(\eta\circ\mathrm{flat}^{\times})\circ\mathrm{dist}\circ\bbT^{\times}\rho

\displaystyle=\bbU(\pi\circ\bbT^{\times}\eta)\circ\mathrm{dist}\circ\bbT^{\times}\rho=\bbU\pi\circ\mathrm{dist}\circ\bbT^{\times}\bbU\eta\circ\bbT^{\times}\rho\,.

\displaystyle\bbU\eta\circ\rho_{\mathrm{io}}

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathrm{io}}(t)\subseteq R\quad\text{implies}\quad\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{io}}(t)\subseteq R\,.

For (a), note that the number of substitutions of the form

The more complicated case of outside-in substitutions is still open.

Remark 49
.

There is one technical detail worth mentioning:
the way we have defined substitutions, every tree in
\sigma(x)

,
contains

\xi

But usually one uses a more general notion of a substitution where the trees in
\sigma(x)

can omit some or all of these variables.
We can formalise this generalisation in our setting as follows.

We consider a substitution as a function
\sigma:X\to\bbU\bbT^{<}\Sigma
,
where
\bbT^{<}
is the functor with

\displaystyle\bbT^{<}_{\xi}X:=\sum_{\zeta\subseteq\xi}\bbT^{\times}_{\zeta}X\,.

We can extend the monad operation to
\bbT^{<}
in the obvious way.
As above we define two induced operations

\sigma_{\mathrm{io}},\sigma_{\mathrm{oi}}:\bbT^{<}X\to\bbU\bbT^{<}\Sigma
.
The definition of the outside-in version is the same as above

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{oi}}(t):=\{\,\mathrm{flat}^{<}({}^{\sigma}s)\varphi,\sigma:s\in^{\mathrm{un}}\bbR^{<}\sigma(t)\,\}

(where
\bbR^{<}
is the corresponding variant of

The problem is that some sets
\sigma(x)
might be empty, but a tree
t

might still have a non-empty image

for every vertex
v
with
\sigma(t(v))=\emptyset
,
there might be some vertex
u
higher up in the tree
where we have chosen an element
s\in t(u)
which omits the variable
corresponding to the subtree containing

.
The easiest way to formalise this process is to make the problem disappear
by adding dummy elements to all sets
\sigma(x)
.
Hence, fix some element
\bot\notin\Sigma
and let

\mu:\bbU\Sigma\to\bbU(\Sigma+\{\bot\})

be the function with

\displaystyle\mu(I):=I\cup\{\bot\}\,.

Then we set

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{io}}(t):=\bigl\{\,\mathrm{flat}^{<}(s)\bigm s\in^{\bbR^{<}}\bbR^{<}(\mu\circ\sigma)(t)\,,\ \mathrm{flat}^{<}(s)\in\bbT^{<}\Sigma\,\bigr\}\,.

The proof of Theorem
5

can now straightforwardly be adapted to these new definitions.

6.
Regular expressions for infinite trees

As a second, more involved application of our results let us define regular expressions
for languages of infinite trees.
Such expressions seem to be folklore, but we have not found them anywhere in the literature
(except for a few remarks in
[
Tho90
]
).

We consider tree languages of the form
L\subseteq\bbT^{\times}_{\xi}\Sigma
,
for some alphabet
\Sigma
and some fixed sort
\xi\in\Xi
.
Alphabets will always be assumed to be finite and unordered.
Note that, if
\Sigma
is unordered, so is
\bbT^{\times}_{\xi}\Sigma

and
\bbU\bbT^{\times}_{\xi}\Sigma
is just the power set.
Hence, we can regard every language
L\subseteq\bbT^{\times}_{\xi}\Sigma

as an element of
\bbU\bbT^{\times}_{\xi}\Sigma
.

We aim for a characterisation of which elements of this set are regular languages.
Towards this goal we introduce a few operations on
\bbU\bbT^{\times}\Sigma
.
They are based on the well-known version for finite trees (see, e.g,. Section 2.4
of
[
LT21
]
), suitably modified to work in the sorted setting
and to generate infinite trees.

Before presenting the definition we need to deal with the problem that

\bbU\circ\bbT^{\times}
does not form a monad and that

\bbU\bbT^{\times}\Sigma
not a
\bbU\bbT^{\times}
-algebra.
For this reason we will work with what we call
bialgebras:

a set
A
equipped both with a
\bbT^{\times}
-algebra product

\pi:\bbT^{\times}A\to A
and a
\bbU
-algebra product
\rho:\bbU A\to A

(without any compatibility condition between them).
(Note that this is not the usual use of the word ‘bialgebra’.)
By Theorem
44
,
\bbU\bbT^{\times}\Sigma
forms a bialgebra
with respect to the monads
\bbT^{\times}
and
\bbU
.

We use the following operations for our version of regular expressions:

•

variables
x\in X
,

•

letters of the alphabet
a\in\Sigma
,

•

substitution
{}\cdot_{x}{}
, iteration
{-}^{+x}
, and
\omega
-power
{-}^{\omega x}

with respect to a single variable
x
,

•

relabelling
{}^{\sigma}{-}
of the variables,

•

union
+
and the empty language
\emptyset
.

The formal definition is as follows.

{defi}

Given a bialgebra
\frakA=\langle A,\pi,\rho\rangle
we define the following operations.

(a) Each
a\in A_{\xi}
, induces an operation
a:A^{\xi}\to A
by

\displaystyle a(\bar{b}):=\pi(s)\,,

where
s\in\bbT^{\times}_{\xi}A
is the tree obtained form
\mathrm{sing}(a)
by replacing
each leaf with label
x\in\xi
by the tree
\mathrm{sing}(b_{x})
.

(b) For sorts
\xi,\zeta\in\Xi
and a variable
x\in\xi
, we define a binary

substitution operation

\displaystyle{}\cdot_{x}{}:A_{\xi}\times A_{\zeta}\to A_{(\xi\setminus\{x\})\cup\zeta}\quad\text{by}\quad a\cdot_{x}b:=\pi(s)\,,

where
s
is the tree obtained from
\mathrm{sing}(a)
by replacing the leaf labelled
x

by the tree
\mathrm{sing}(b)
.

(c) For
a\in A_{\xi}
and a surjective map
\sigma:\xi\to\zeta
, we set

\displaystyle{}^{\sigma}a:=\pi(s)\,,

where
s
is the tree obtained from
\mathrm{sing}(a)
by replacing each label
x\in\xi

by
\sigma(x)
.

(d) We define
{+}:A_{\xi}\times A_{\xi}\to A_{\xi}
and
\emptyset\in A_{\xi}
by

\displaystyle a+b:=\rho(\mathord{\Uparrow}\{a,b\})\quad\text{and}\quad\emptyset:=\rho(\emptyset)\,.

(e)
Let
\zeta\in\Xi
. We call a tree
s

\zeta
-trivial
if,
for all

and

, we have

\displaystyle s(v)=z\quad\@iff\quad v\text{ is an $z$-successor.}

(I.e., all
z

and there are no other occurrences of

For a finite sequence of elements

,

, and
a variable

, we
define the
\omega
-power
and the

by

\displaystyle(a_{0}+\dots+a_{n-1})^{\omega x}:={}

\displaystyle\qquad\rho\bigl(\bigl\{\,\pi(s)\bigm s\in\bbT^{\times}_{\zeta\setminus\{x\}}\{a_{0},\dots,a_{n-1}\}\text{ is $(\zeta\setminus\{x\})$-trivial }\,\bigr\}\bigr)\,,

\displaystyle(a_{0}+\dots+a_{n-1})^{+x}:={}

\displaystyle\qquad\begin{aligned} \rho\bigl(\bigl\{\,\pi(s)\bigm s\in\bbT^{\times}_{\zeta}\{a_{0},\dots,a_{n-1}\}\text{ has finite height and it is}\quad&\\
\text{$(\zeta\setminus\{x\})$-trivial }&\,\bigr\}\bigr)\,.\end{aligned}

(f) For a sort
\xi\in\Xi
and a set
\Sigma
,
the set
\bbE_{\xi}\Sigma
of

over
\Sigma

consists of all finite terms

that can be built up from variables and
the operations (a)–(e) (for the bialgebra
\bbU\bbT^{\times}\Sigma
), where

•

we restrict the operations from (a) to those where
a=\mathord{\Uparrow}\mathrm{sing}(c)
,
for some

, and

•

the free variables are exactly those in
\xi

We write
[\![R]\!]\subseteq\bbU\bbT^{\times}\Sigma
for the value of

in
\bbU\bbT^{\times}\Sigma
.

Remark 50
.

The iteration and the

\lrcorner

{exa}

We consider the alphabet
\Sigma=\{a,b,c\}
where
a
and

have sort
\{x,y\}

and

has sort
\emptyset

(a) A regular expression for the language

\displaystyle E:=\bigl(\bigl(a(x,y)+b(x,y)+c\bigr)^{\omega x}\bigr)^{\omega y}.

(b) An expression for the language of all trees with an infinite branch

\displaystyle R:=\bigl(a(x,z)+a(z,y)\bigr)^{\omega z}\cdot_{x}E\cdot_{y}E\,.

\displaystyle S:=a(x,y)\cdot_{x}E\cdot_{y}E+\bigl(b(x,z)+b(z,y)\bigr)^{+z}\cdot_{z}a(x,y)\cdot_{x}E\cdot_{y}E\,.

\lrcorner

We still have to show that regular expressions capture the class of regular languages.
For the proof, let us quickly recall the notion of a tree automaton
(see, e.g.,
[
Tho97
,
GTW02
,
Löd21
]
for details).
A
parity automaton

\calA=\langle Q,\Sigma,\zeta,\Delta,q_{\rmI},\Omega\rangle

consists of a finite set
Q
of states, an input alphabet
\Sigma
,
an input sort
\zeta\in\Xi
, an initial state
q_{\rmI}\in Q
, a priority function
\Omega
,
and a transition relation

\displaystyle\Delta\subseteq\sum_{\xi\in\Xi}(Q\times\Sigma_{\xi}\times Q^{\lvert\xi\rvert})+(Q\times\zeta)\,.

A
run

\rho
of such an automaton on an input tree
t\in\bbT^{\times}_{\zeta}\Sigma

is a labelling of
t
by states such that

•

the root is labelled by
q_{\rmI}
,

•

\bigl\langle\rho(v),t(v),\rho(u_{0}),\dots,\rho(u_{n-1})\bigr\rangle\in\Delta
,
for every vertex
v
with successors
u_{0},\dots,u_{n-1}
,

•

every infinite branch
v_{0},v_{1},\dots
of
t
satisfies the parity condition:

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\Omega(\rho(v_{n}))\quad\text{is even.}

A
partial run
is defined exactly like a run, except that the state at the root
can be arbitrary and that we do not require the transition relation to hold at vertices
v

labelled by a variable.
Let
\rho
be a partial run on the tree
t\in\bbT^{\times}_{\zeta}\Sigma
.
The
profile
of
\rho
is the pair
\langle p,(U_{z})_{z\in\zeta}\rangle

where
p
is the state at the root and, for each variable

,

is the set of all pairs
\langle k,q\rangle

such that there is a vertex
v
labelled
z
with state
q

and such that
k
is the least priority seen along the path from the root to
v
.
We define an ordering on profiles by

\displaystyle\langle p,\bar{U}\rangle\leq\langle p^{\prime},\bar{U}^{\prime}\rangle\quad\@defiff\quad p=p^{\prime}\text{ and }U_{z}\subseteq U^{\prime}_{z}\text{ for all }z\in\zeta\,.

If
\sigma\leq\tau
, we say that the profile
\sigma
is
bounded
by
\tau
.

Theorem 51
.

Let
\Sigma
be an alphabet.
A language
L\subseteq\bbT^{\times}_{\zeta}\Sigma
is regular if, and only if,

L=[\![R]\!]
, for some regular expression
R\in\bbE_{\zeta}\Sigma
.

Proof 6.1
.

(\Leftarrow)
The class of all regular tree languages
is closed under all operations that can appear in a regular expression.

(\Rightarrow)

Let
\calA=\langle Q,\Sigma,\zeta,\Delta,q_{\rmI},\Omega\rangle
be an automaton recognising
L

and fix an enumeration
q_{0},\dots,q_{n-1}
of
Q

such that
\Omega(q_{0})\geq\dots\geq\Omega(q_{n-1})
.
For every profile
\tau
of
\calA
and every number
k\leq n
,
we will construct a regular expressions
R^{k}_{\tau}
defining the language

\displaystyle[\![R^{k}_{\tau}]\!]=\bigl\{\,t\in\bbT^{\times}\Sigma\bigm

\displaystyle\text{there is a partial run on }t\text{ whose profile is bounded by}

\displaystyle\tau\text{ and whose internal states are among }q_{0},\dots,q_{k-1}\,\bigr\}\,.

Then we obtain the desired expression for
L
by setting

\displaystyle R:=\sum_{\tau\in H}R^{n}_{\tau}\,,

where
H
is the set of all profiles
\tau=\langle q_{\rmI},\bar{U}\rangle
such that,
for all
z\in\zeta
,

\displaystyle\langle k,p\rangle\in U_{z}\quad\text{implies}\quad\langle p,z\rangle\in\Delta\,.

We define the expressions
R^{k}_{\tau}
by induction on
k
.
For
k=0
, we only need to consider runs without internal states. Hence, we can set

\displaystyle R^{0}_{\tau}:=\sum{\{\,a(\bar{x}){}}

\displaystyle a\in\Sigma\,,\text{ there is a partial run on }\mathrm{sing}(a)\text{ whose profile}

\displaystyle\text{is bounded by }\tau\,\}\,.

For the inductive step, suppose that
\tau=\langle p,\bar{U}\rangle
,
let
\xi
be the sort of
\tau
, and
let
D:=\mathop{rng}\nolimits\Omega
be the set of priorities used by
\calA
.
We start with an expression describing runs starting with the state
q_{k}

and with only finitely many occurrences of
q_{k}
on each branch.
For a set
\eta\subseteq\xi
of variables,
we write
\bar{U}|_{\eta}
for the subtuple
(U_{x})_{x\in\eta}
.
Let
V:=D\times\{q_{k}\}
, let
y_{0},y_{1},\dots
be new variables not in
\xi
, and set

\displaystyle T^{k}_{\bar{U}}:=R^{k}_{q_{k},\bar{U}}+\sum_{\zeta\cup\eta_{0}\cup\dots\cup\eta_{n-1}=\xi}\begin{aligned} &(S^{\zeta,n}_{0}+\dots+S^{\zeta,n}_{m-1})^{+y_{0}}\\
&\quad{}\cdot_{y_{0}}R^{k}_{q_{k},\bar{U}|_{\eta_{0}}}\cdot_{y_{1}}\cdots\cdot_{y_{n-1}}R^{k}_{q_{k},\bar{U}|_{\eta_{n-1}}}\,,\end{aligned}

where

•

the sum ranges over all sequences
\zeta,\eta_{0},\dots,\eta_{n-1}
of subsets of
\xi

whose union is equal to
\xi
and such that
\eta_{i}\neq\eta_{j}
, for
i\neq j
, and

•

S^{\zeta,n}_{0},\dots,S^{\zeta,n}_{m-1}
is an enumeration of all expressions of the form

R^{k}_{q_{k},\bar{U}|_{\eta}V\cdots V}
where
\eta\subseteq\zeta
,

\upsilon\subseteq\{y_{0},\dots,y_{n-1}\}
, and with
\lvert\upsilon\rvert
copies of
V

that correspond to the variables
y\in\upsilon
.

Then
T^{k}_{\bar{U}}
describes all trees that have a run with profile bounded by

\langle q_{k},\bar{U}\rangle
and such that every branch contains only finitely many
occurrences of the state
q_{k}
.

Similarly, we obtain an expression for all such trees with possibly infinitely many occurrences
of
q_{k}
by setting

\displaystyle\hat{T}^{k}_{\bar{U}}:=T^{k}_{\bar{U}}+\sum_{\zeta\cup\eta_{0}\cup\dots\cup\eta_{n-1}=\xi}\begin{aligned} &(S^{\zeta,n}_{0}+\dots+S^{\zeta,n}_{m-1})^{\omega z}\\
&\quad{}\cdot_{y_{0}}T^{k}_{\bar{U}|_{\eta_{0}}}\cdot_{y_{1}}\cdots\cdot_{y_{n-1}}T^{k}_{\bar{U}|_{\eta_{n-1}}}\,,\end{aligned}

where the
S^{\zeta,n}_{i}
are defined as above, except that there is an additional copy of
V

corresponding to the variable
z
.

If
\Omega(q_{k})
is odd, we can now set

\displaystyle R^{k+1}_{\tau}:=R^{k}_{\tau}+\sum_{\zeta\cup\eta_{0}\cup\dots\cup\eta_{n-1}=\xi}R^{k}_{p,\bar{U}|_{\zeta}V\dots V}\cdot_{y_{0}}T^{k}_{\bar{U}|_{\eta_{0}}}\cdot_{y_{1}}\cdots\cdot_{y_{n-1}}T^{k}_{\bar{U}|_{\eta_{n-1}}}\,.

where the variables
y_{0},\dots,y_{n-1}
are the ones corresponding to the
n
copies of the
set
V
.
If
\Omega(q_{k})
is even, we instead use

\displaystyle R^{k+1}_{\tau}:=R^{k}_{\tau}+\sum_{\zeta\cup\eta_{0}\cup\dots\cup\eta_{n-1}=\xi}R^{k}_{p,\bar{U}|_{\zeta}V\dots V}\cdot_{y_{0}}\hat{T}^{k}_{\bar{U}|_{\eta_{0}}}\cdot_{y_{1}}\cdots\cdot_{y_{n-1}}\hat{T}^{k}_{\bar{U}|_{\eta_{n-1}}}\,.

7.
Conclusion

We have introduced the upwards-closed power-set monad
\bbU
on
\mathsf{Pos}^{\Xi}

and studied possible distributive laws between it and two monads of infinite trees:
linear trees
\bbT
and non-linear ones
\bbT^{\times}
.
For the monad
\bbT
, we have shown in Theorems

18
and
21

that there exists a unique distributive law
\mathrm{dist}:\bbT\bbU\Rightarrow\bbU\bbT
.
For the monad
\bbT^{\times}
on the other hand, we have proved in
Theorem
22
that there is no distributive law

\bbT^{\times}\bbU\Rightarrow\bbU\bbT^{\times}
.
Our main result (Theorem
44
) states that, nevertheless,
every set of the form
\bbU\bbT^{\times}A
forms a
\bbT^{\times}
-algebra when
equipped with a suitable product.
The two examples in Section
5
and
6
show
that this partial result is frequently sufficient for applications.

There are several possible directions where one can go from here.
Of interest to language theorists would be to consider other functors
similar to the power-set one, for instance the functor producing linear combinations
over a given semiring, or similar analogues of the power-set functor for weighted languages.

More category-theoretically inspired considerations would include a more systematic study
of when a distributive law with the power-set monad exists.
In particular, it would be interesting to transfer the results in Section
3

from polynomial monads to quotients of such monads.
Another avenue to pursue would be to generalise Theorem
22

to other monads than the power-set one
by extracting the abstract properties of the power-set monad needed for the proof.
