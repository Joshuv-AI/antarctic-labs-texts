A differential approach to Maxwell-Cremona liftings
Oleg Karpenkov, Fatemeh Mohammadi, Christian Müller, and Bernd Schulze

Abstract
. In 1864, J. C. Maxwell introduced a link between self-stressed frameworks in the plane and piecewise linear liftings to 3-space. This connection has found numerous applications in areas such as discrete geometry, control theory and structural engineering. While there are some generalisations of this theory to liftings of d d -complexes in d d -space, extensions for liftings of frameworks in d d -space for d ≥ 3 d\geq 3 have been missing. In this paper, we introduce and study differential liftings on general graphs using differential forms associated with the elements of the homotopy groups of the complements to the frameworks. Such liftings play the role of integrands for the classical notion of liftings for planar frameworks. We show that these differential liftings have a natural extension to self-stressed frameworks in higher dimensions. As a result we generalise the notion of classical liftings to both graphs and multidimensional k k -complexes in d d -space ( k = 2 , … , d k=2,\ldots,d ). Finally we discuss a natural representation of generalised liftings as real-valued functions on Grassmannians.



Introduction

In 1864, J. C. Maxwell and later also L. Cremona showed that a planar framework in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with a self-stress has a dual reciprocal diagram and can be lifted vertically to a polyhedral
surface in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
The present paper is dedicated to answer the following question:

What are the analogues of liftings for self-stressed frameworks in

\mathbb{R}^{n}
?
For
n\geq 3
, the answer to this question was not known. In
order to approach this question, we construct a new natural extension of
liftings for general frameworks in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and for Rybnikov’s polyhedral self-stressed complexes. This will provide the answer to the above problem.

The story begins with two papers by J. C. Maxwell
[
21
,
22
]
and one by L. Cremona
[
11
]
in which the authors founded the field of Graphic Statics. They discovered that self-stressability of planar frameworks can be visualised by piecewise linear functions whose singular set is the framework itself. This theory is closely related to the duality between Voronoi diagrams and Delaunay tessellations. The result of Maxwell and Cremona has important connections and applications in rigidity theory, polyhedral combinatorics and discrete and computational geometry; see e.g.
[
40
,
2
,
38
,
9
,
10
,
15
,
36
,
37
,
30
]
. In particular, the technique of liftings has contributed to the solution of the Carpenter’s Rule problem
[
6
]
. Liftings are also used in a well known proof of Steinitz’s Theorem from polyhedral combinatorics
[
31
]
. (See also
[
12
]
and
[
28
]
.)
Recently, F. Mohammadi and X. Wu in
[
25
]
introduced a link between self-stresses and Chow rings on toric varieties related to convex polytopes.
There has also been a resurgence of interest in techniques from Graphic Statics in architecture and structural engineering
[
24
]
. (In engineering, the 3-dimensional polyhedra projecting down to self-stressed frameworks in the plane are commonly known as
discrete Airy stress function polyhedra
.)
Moreover, liftings have found modern applications in control theory
[
26
,
27
]
. For general references about liftings in discrete and computational geometry, we refer the reader to

[
39
, Chapter 61]
, as well as to
[
41
]
and
[
35
]
.

Let us mention what is currently known about generalisations of the Maxwell-Cremona correspondence.
In 1993, H. Crapo and W. Whiteley investigated an extension of this correspondence to
3
-space in an unpublished manuscript
[
8
]
. 3D Graphic Statics has recently been further investigated in the engineering community (see e.g.
[
20
]
).
In 1999, K. Rybnikov introduced self-stresses on polyhedral
n
-complexes in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and studied liftings of these objects
[
32
,
33
]
. More recently,
A. Ribó Mor discussed liftings for planar self-touching frameworks
[
29
]
. In 2014, C. Borcea and I. Streinu proved an analogue of the theorem of Maxwell and Cremona for planar periodic frameworks
[
4
,
5
]
. Finally, J. Erickson and P. Lin studied reciprocal diagrams of toroidal frameworks in
[
13
]
, and Z. Cooperband, R. Ghrist and J. Hansen very recently gave a description of Graphic Statics for planar and non-planar frameworks using cosheaf homology in
[
7
]
.

Extensions of liftings for frameworks in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
for
n\geq 3
have not yet been obtained. In this paper we give a construction for this case and for more general cases. As was already done by K. Rybnikov in the literature
[
32
,
33
]
,
the liftings of
n
-complexes in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
are defined on the complement to
n
-complexes.
Our idea is to consider these complements as the set of path-connected
components denoted as
\pi_{0}
.
This consideration gives rise to a natural generalisation of liftings to the cases of complexes of arbitrary dimensions (in particular for frameworks in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
) and to the case of non-planar frameworks.
Here the elements of the higher fundamental groups
\pi_{m}
of the complement to the frameworks/complexes will serve as the domain sets in the definition of ‘differential liftings’.

In this paper, we consider piecewise constant
m
-forms on the elements of
\pi_{m}
of the complement to the frameworks/complexes in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
which we call
differential liftings

(see Definitions
2.3
,
3.6
, and
4.10
).
Differential liftings satisfy a certain boundary condition at every vertex induced by equilibrium conditions on the stress coefficients on edges incident with this vertex.
In the classical case the liftings of planar frameworks in
\mathbb{R}^{2}

are the integral graphs of the corresponding differential 1-forms.
We show that for every framework in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with a self-stress there exists a
unique differential lifting (Theorems
2.4
and
3.7
).
This provides the answer to the original question regarding generalisations of liftings for self-stressed frameworks in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Further we extend this result to the case of general polyhedral complexes (Theorem
4.11
).
We briefly discuss generalisations of liftings in the last section of this
paper.
It is interesting to note that
such liftings can be represented
as functions on affine Grassmannians
(see Subsection
5.2
).

We work mostly with differential liftings rather than with usual liftings for the following reasons.
On the one hand the differential liftings are simpler than usual liftings in the sense that they are piecewise constant over
\pi_{0}
rather than piecewise linear.
So they are invariants of the elements of
\pi_{0}
. Therefore, we prefer to use them when extending differential liftings to
\pi_{m}
as invariants of its elements.
On the other hand, the differential liftings provide the same information on self-stressability as the classical liftings for planar frameworks.
Indeed, the liftings are obtained from differential liftings by integration of the latter.

This paper is organised as follows.

We begin by providing the necessary classical notions from Geometric Rigidity Theory and Graphic Statics in Section
1
. In Section
2
, we approach the notion of liftings of self-stressed frameworks from a new perspective using differential forms and introduce the natural notion of differential liftings.
In Section
3
, we define this notion for frameworks in
n
-dimensional space. Finally, in Section
4
we extend the concept of differential liftings to the case of polyhedral complexes introduced by K. Rybnikov in 1999.
We conclude the paper in Section
5

with discussions on how to extend standard liftings from differential
liftings in general settings.

1.
Preliminaries

Our paper is based on two fundamental notions which are self-stressed
frameworks (Definition
1.1
) and liftings
(Definition
1.2
). Self-stressed frameworks are realisations
of graphs in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
which are in static equilibrium. Liftings are,
broadly speaking, polyhedra in a higher dimensional space whose vertical
projections are given self-stressed frameworks.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be an arbitrary finite graph without loops and without
multiple edges where
V=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{d}\}
denotes the set of

vertices
and
E
the set of
edges
.
We denote by
v_{i}v_{j}
the edge joining
v_{i}
and
v_{j}
.

•

A
framework

G(p)
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
consists of a graph

G=(V,E)
and an injective map
p:V\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We often denote

p(v_{i})
by
p_{i}
.
We say that there is an
edge
between
p_{i}
and
p_{j}
if

v_{i}v_{j}
is an edge of
G
and denote it by
p_{i}p_{j}
.
Note that we require that all points are pairwise distinct.

•

A
stress

\omega
on a framework is an assignment of real
scalars
\omega_{ij}
to its edges
v_{i}v_{j}
which are called

stress coefficients
(also known as
tensions
and

compressions
depending on the sign) with the property

\omega_{ij}=\omega_{ji}
.
We also set
\omega_{ij}=0
if there is no edge between the
corresponding vertices.

•

A stress
\omega
is called a
self-stress
if the
equilibrium condition

\sum\limits_{\{j\mid j\neq i\}}\omega_{ij}(p_{i}-p_{j})=0

is fulfilled at every vertex
i
.
By
p_{i}-p_{j}
we denote the vector from the point
p_{j}
to the
point
p_{i}
.

•

A pair
(G(p),w)
is called a
tensegrity
if
\omega
is
a self-stress for the framework
G(p)
.

•

A tensegrity
(G(p),w)
(or stress
\omega
) is said to be

non-zero
if there exists an edge
v_{i}v_{j}
of the framework
that has a non-vanishing stress coefficient
\omega_{ij}\neq 0
.

We recall in the following definition the classical notion of a polyhedral
lifting over a planar graph that has been put into relation with
self-stressed frameworks by Maxwell
[
22
]
.

Definition 1.2
.

Let
G
be a planar graph and
G(p)
a corresponding framework embedded
as planar straight-line graph in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
Denote by
F
the set of connected components of
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus G(P)
;
we call the elements of
F
the
faces
. The only unbounded face is
called the
infinite face
.

A continuous and piecewise affine function
L:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
is called

(
polyhedral
)
lifting
of
G(p)
if
L
restricted to any face of

F
is an affine map. See Figure
1
for an illustration
of the graph of such a function
L
.

Note that all faces are open sets with a polygonal boundary.

The graph
(u,v,L(u,v))
of a polyhedral lifting is therefore a
polyhedral surface whose edges and vertices vertically project to the
given graph.

Theorem 1.3

(
[
22
]
)
.

Let
G
be a planar graph and
G(p)
a framework embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{2}

without crossing edges. Then
G(p)
has a non-zero self-stress if and
only if
G(p)
has a non-trivial polyhedral lifting
L
.

Remark 1.4
.

Let us mention the classical notion of the Maxwell reciprocal
diagram. The
Maxwell reciprocal diagram
of a framework
G(p)

in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a framework
G^{*}(p^{*})
defined on the combinatorial dual
graph
G^{*}
such that corresponding edges of the frameworks
G
and

G^{*}
are orthogonal to each other with lengths given by the
corresponding stress coefficients. See the figure aside for an
illustration. It is important to note that the existence of the
Maxwell reciprocal diagram
G^{*}(p^{*})
is equivalent to non-zero
self-stressability of
G(p)
.

\end{overpic}

For
G(p)
with self-stress
\omega
, let us denote the normal vector to the face of the
polyhedral lifting
L
corresponding to a face
f\in F
by
\nu_{f}
and let these vectors be “normalized”
to
\nu_{f}=(*,*,-1)
. Then from
[
5
, Eqn. (4)]
we know that

(1)

\nu_{f_{2}}-\nu_{f_{1}}=\omega_{ij}(p_{j}-p_{i})^{\perp}

for neighbouring faces
f_{1},f_{2}\in F
with common edge
p_{i}p_{j}
.

2.
Differential liftings in
\mathbb{R}^{2}

In this section, we generalise the concept of the classical polyhedral
lifting (Definition
1.2
). By Maxwell’s theorem
(Theorem
1.3
) the lifting exists exactly for self-stressed
frameworks over their embedding as planar straight-line graph.
Instead of considering the lifting itself we now switch to a
discrete differential of that lifting. It turns out that such a discrete
differential (which we call
differential lifting
, cf. Definition
2.3
) exists also for frameworks which are not
embedded as planar graphs as well as for frameworks over non-planar
graphs.

While an embedding of a planar graph constitutes a cell decomposition of

\mathbb{R}^{2}
into faces, a general realisation of a graph in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
might have
crossing edges and divides the plane into cells which we call

chambers
.

Definition 2.1
.

For a framework
G(p)
, a
chamber
is a connected component of the
complement of
G(p)
to
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Two different chambers are neighbours
if they share an edge (see Figure
2
left) or if they
share a non-empty open sub-segment of an edge (see
Figure
2
right).

Note that as the framework
G(p)
is finite, there is always an unbounded
chamber
C_{\infty}
, the one “around” the framework.

A key idea in our paper is to relate differential
1
-forms to the chambers
of the framework. For that we identify vectors
v=(v_{1},v_{2})
with
differential
1
-forms
dv=v_{1}\,dx+v_{2}\,dy
via linear combinations
of coordinate differentials
dx
and
dy
. The space of differential

1
-forms is denoted by
\Omega^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.
For a given framework
G(p)
, we will consider maps from the set of
chambers to the space of constant
1
-forms on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. The space of
chambers is canonically identified with the elements of

\pi_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus G(p))
, the set of path components. Once the map
from the set of chambers to
\pi_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus G(p))
is fixed, we can
say that the corresponding
1
-forms are
associated
with the
chambers.

Definition 2.2
.

Consider a framework
G(p)
with a self-stress
\omega
. Let
C_{1}
and

C_{2}
be two neighbouring chambers of
G(p)
along
p_{i}p_{j}
(i.e. they are
either sharing the same edge
p_{i}p_{j}
or a non-empty open sub-segment of it).
The stress coefficient of
p_{i}p_{j}
is
\omega_{ij}
. Let
\alpha_{1}
and

\alpha_{2}
be two constant
1
-forms associated with
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
,
respectively. We say that
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
satisfy the

neighbouring condition
if

(2)

\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{1}={\rm sign}(\det(\mathbf{n}_{12},p_{i}-p_{j}))\cdot\omega_{ij}\cdot d(p_{i}{-}p_{j}),

where
\mathbf{n}_{12}
is the unit normal vector to
p_{i}-p_{j}
pointing in
the direction from
C_{1}
to
C_{2}
(cf. Figure
2
).

Note that the neighbouring condition is independent of the order of

\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
.

The following definition of differential liftings is central for the
present paper. It generalises the concept of the classical polyhedral
lifting (Definition
1.2
), in a sense, to a discrete differential of
a lifting which may or may not exist, as explained below.

Definition 2.3
.

For the framework
G(p)
and a self-stress
\omega
of
G(p)
, we call
a mapping

l_{\omega,p}:\pi_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus G(p))\longrightarrow\Omega^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})

a
differential lifting
if it satisfies the
neighbouring
condition
for each pair of neighbouring chambers and if it takes zero
on the unbounded chamber of
C_{\infty}\in\pi_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus G(p))
.

In Theorem
2.4
, we show that a differential lifting as defined
in Definition
2.3
exists and that it is unique.
Note that the set of differential liftings over a fixed framework
G(p)

is a vector space with

l_{\omega^{1},p}+l_{\omega^{2},p}=l_{\omega^{1}+\omega^{2},p}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and}\hskip 20.00003pt\lambda l_{\omega,p}=l_{\lambda\omega,p}.

Theorem 2.4
.

For every framework
G(p)
with a self-stress
\omega
, there exists a
unique differential lifting
l_{\omega,p}
.

Proof.

Let us first assume that our framework
G(p)
has no
crossing
edges
.

By Definition
2.3
, the value of the unbounded chamber

C_{\infty}
is set to be zero.
Any other value of
l_{\omega,p}
can be obtained by adding
differential forms via the neighbouring condition,
Equation (
2
), along a path of chambers starting at

C_{\infty}
, where a
path
between two chambers is a sequence of
chambers that share a common edge.
Our aim is to show that different paths with the same starting and
ending chambers do not give different values at the last chamber.

Consider a path
\mathcal{C}
of chambers
C_{1},\ldots,C_{k}
with
associated differential forms denoted by
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k}
.
Adding differential forms along the path via Equation (
2
)
yields

\alpha_{k}=\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{\mathcal{C}},\hskip 20.00003pt\hbox{where}\hskip 20.00003pt\alpha_{\mathcal{C}}=\sum\limits_{r=2}^{k}(\alpha_{r}-\alpha_{r-1}),

where
(\alpha_{r}-\alpha_{r-1})
stands for the right hand side of
Equation (
2
).

For the reverse path
C_{k},\ldots,C_{1}
, the differential forms then
satisfy
\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{k}-\alpha_{\mathcal{C}}
.

Let us consider two paths
\mathcal{C}_{1}
and
\mathcal{C}_{2}

with the same starting chamber
C_{s}
and the same end chamber
C_{e}
.
We will show that these two (potentially different) paths give the same
value at the last chamber. Adding differential forms along the two paths
yields

\alpha^{1}_{e}=\alpha_{s}+\alpha_{\mathcal{C}_{1}}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and}\hskip 20.00003pt\alpha^{2}_{e}=\alpha_{s}+\alpha_{\mathcal{C}_{2}}.

Consequently, adding differential forms along the path

\mathcal{C}_{1}
followed by the reversed path
\mathcal{C}_{2}

(which combined is a
closed
path as the starting chamber is the
same as the end chamber) adds

\alpha_{\mathcal{C}_{1}}-\alpha_{\mathcal{C}_{2}}
to
\alpha_{s}
.
Therefore, the two paths
\mathcal{C}_{1}
and
\mathcal{C}_{2}
imply equal
values
\alpha^{1}_{e}=\alpha^{2}_{e}
at
C_{e}
if and only if the closed path
above just adds zero to
\alpha_{s}
.

The uniqueness of the definition of
l_{\omega,p}
is therefore
equivalent to showing that adding differential forms via Equation (
2
),
along closed paths, adds zero to the starting chamber. We start with two
elementary cycles before we cover a general closed path.

Case 1
: Let us consider the closed path of chambers
C_{1},C_{2},C_{3}=C_{1}
. This path corresponds to passing through an edge and going
back. Let us assume
l_{\omega,p}(C_{1})=\alpha_{1}
. Adding the values
along this path yields first (cf. Figure
2
)

\alpha_{2}=\alpha_{1}+\omega_{ij}\cdot{\rm sign}(\det(\mathbf{n}_{12},p_{i}-p_{j}))\cdot d(p_{i}{-}p_{j}),

and further

\alpha_{3}=\alpha_{2}+\omega_{ij}\cdot{\rm sign}(\det(\mathbf{n}_{23},p_{i}-p_{j}))\cdot d(p_{i}{-}p_{j}).

since
\mathbf{n}_{23}=\mathbf{n}_{21}=-\mathbf{n}_{12}
, inserting these values into the
above formulas implies
\alpha_{3}=\alpha_{1}
. Consequently nothing is
added to the starting chamber.

Case 2
: Let us consider a closed path around a point
p_{i}
in
counterclockwise direction,
C_{1},\ldots,C_{k}=C_{1}
. Recursively
inserting Equation (
2
) yields

\alpha_{k}=\alpha_{1}+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\omega_{ij}\cdot{\rm sign}(\det(\mathbf{n}_{j,j+1},p_{i}-p_{j}))\cdot d(p_{i}{-}p_{j})=\alpha_{1}+d\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\omega_{ij}(p_{i}-p_{j})\Big)=\alpha_{1},

where we take indices modulo
k
, i.e.
\mathbf{n}_{k,k+1}=\mathbf{n}_{k,1}
.
The second equality holds true due to the linearity of the differential
operator, and because all the signs in the sum are positive (because of
counterclockwise orientation). The third equality holds by the equilibrium
condition for self-stressed frameworks at
p_{i}
.

General case
: Adding differential forms via (
2
) along
any closed path adds zero to the starting chamber since it can be
decomposed into elementary closed paths considered in Cases 1 and 2.

Let us now consider the case where the framework
G(p)
with
self-stress
\omega
has
crossing edges
which we excluded so
far. We can easily turn it into a self-stressed framework without
crossing edges. First, we add all crossing points to the set of
vertices and obtain a framework
G(\tilde{p})
. Second, we modify the
stress function
\omega
to
\tilde{\omega}
by the following rule:
Consider two neighbouring vertices
\tilde{p}_{k}\tilde{p}_{l}
forming an
edge of
G(\tilde{p})
which lies on an “old” edge
p_{i}p_{j}
. Then
the new stress coefficient
\tilde{\omega}_{kl}
is defined by

\tilde{\omega}_{kl}:=\frac{\omega_{ij}}{\lambda_{kl}},

where
\lambda_{kl}
is the scaling factor between these two edges:

\|p_{k}-p_{l}\|=\lambda_{kl}\|p_{i}-p_{j}\|
.

This is a self-stress since at every “new” vertex
\tilde{p}_{l}

opposite forces cancel:

\tilde{\omega}_{kl}(\tilde{p}_{k}-\tilde{p}_{l})+\tilde{\omega}_{ml}(\tilde{p}_{m}-\tilde{p}_{l})=\frac{\omega_{ij}}{\lambda_{kl}}\lambda_{kl}(p_{i}-p_{j})+\frac{\omega_{ij}}{\lambda_{ml}}\lambda_{ml}(p_{j}-p_{i})=0.

Analogously at “old” vertices
p_{i}
the equilibrium condition still
holds as any “new” edge
\tilde{p}_{k}\tilde{p}_{i}
contributes the same
force:

\tilde{\omega}_{ki}(\tilde{p}_{k}-\tilde{p}_{i})=\frac{\omega_{ij}}{\lambda_{ki}}\lambda_{ki}(p_{j}-p_{i})=\omega_{ij}(p_{j}-p_{i}).

Now note that the neighbouring condition (
2
) along
a “new” edge is the same as before when considered as two
neighbouring chambers along a non-empty open sub-segment of an edge
since

\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{1}={\rm sign}(\det(\mathbf{n}_{12},\tilde{p}_{k}-\tilde{p}_{l}))\cdot\tilde{\omega}_{kl}\cdot d(\tilde{p}_{k}{-}\tilde{p}_{l})={\rm sign}(\det(\mathbf{n}_{12},p_{i}-p_{j}))\cdot\omega_{ij}\cdot d(p_{i}{-}p_{j}),

which concludes the proof.

3.
Differential liftings in
\mathbb{R}^{n}

Consider a framework
G(p)
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For
n\geq 3
the complement

\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus G(p)
does not decompose into disconnected chambers but
rather consists of one connected component. Consequently we replace the notion
of chambers by the notion of equivalence classes of oriented loops in the
complement of
G(p)
to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Here the base point of the loops can be
arbitrarily chosen to be any point of the complement.

Similarly to the differential liftings of Section
2
(which
are maps from
\pi_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus G(p))
to
\Omega^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
) we set
differential liftings in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
to be special maps of the fundamental
group
\pi_{n-2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus G(p))
to
\Omega^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

(Definition
3.6
). In our setting homotopic loops will
map to the same associated
1
-form.

Given a vector
v=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n})
, we identify it with the
differential
1
-form
dv=v_{1}\,dx_{1}+\cdots+v_{n}\,dx_{n}
via linear
combinations of coordinate differentials.

For a given framework, we consider maps from the fundamental group

\pi_{n-2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus G(p))
to the space of constant
1
-forms in

\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Once the map is fixed, we can say that the corresponding

1
-forms are
associated
with the homotopy classes of loops.

The number of how many times two disjoint manifolds are intertwined is
called
linking number
. Let us recall its definition from the
original paper by Gauss
[
14
, p. 605]
.
In the literature, such as
[
23
, p. 53]
, one often
also finds another convention for the sign of this definition.

Definition 3.1
.

Consider two disjoint oriented manifolds

M_{1},M_{2}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}+m_{2}+1}

of respective dimensions
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
. Then the
linking map
is
the map

\lambda:M_{1}\times M_{2}\longrightarrow S^{m_{1}+m_{2}}

defined by
\lambda(x,y)=-(x-y)/\|x-y\|
.
If
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
are compact, oriented and without boundary, then the
degree of
\lambda
is called the
linking number

{\rm lk}(M_{1},M_{2})
.

Remark 3.2
.

The orientation of the sphere
S^{m_{1}+m_{2}}
is important for the definition of the linking number. We fixed the orientation in such a way that
at point

(1,0,\ldots,0)\in S^{m_{1}+m_{2}}
it coincides with the orientation of the basis
(e_{2},\ldots,e_{m_{1}+m_{2}+1})
.
In such settings the orientations of Hopf links in
\mathbb{R}^{3}

are as in Figure
5
.

Remark 3.3
.

Below, one of the manifolds will be a piecewise linear finite polyhedral

m

approximations by differentiable spheres in
\varepsilon

neighbourhoods of the piecewise linear sphere in the continuous topology

\varepsilon
tends to zero.

The linking number is an integer which is homotopy
invariant and can be computed with the so called
multi-dimensional Gauss linking integral (see,
e.g.,
[

).
Coming back to our frameworks

, for a simple cycle
\delta
in

and
\Gamma\in\pi_{n-2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus G(p))
we set

{\rm lk}(\Gamma,\delta)={\rm lk}(\gamma,\delta)

with
\gamma
being any element of
\Gamma
. The linking number of two
manifolds is well defined since it is invariant under homotopies and hence
it does not depend on the choice of
\gamma\in\Gamma
.

\Theta
is given
by an elementary loop
\theta\in\Theta
. The homotopy class
\theta

is a neighbour to the neutral element at edge
e
.

Centre and Right:
The sums
\Gamma\pm\Theta
are both neighbours
to
\Gamma
at edge
e
.

Definition 3.4
.

Consider a framework
G(p)
with a self-stress

\omega
. Let
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
be two different homotopy classes
of loops that are neighbours at an edge
p_{i}p_{j}
with stress
coefficient
\omega_{ij}
. Fix a simple cycle
\gamma
in
G(p)
which
includes the edge
p_{i}p_{j}
with orientation from
p_{i}
to
p_{j}
.
Let
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in\Omega^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be two constant
1
-forms
associated with
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
. We say that
\alpha_{1}
and

\alpha_{2}
satisfy the
neighbouring condition
if

(3)

\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{1}=\big({\rm lk}(\Gamma_{1},\gamma)-{\rm lk}(\Gamma_{2},\gamma)\big)\cdot\omega_{ij}\cdot d(p_{i}{-}p_{j}).

This definition is neither dependent on the choice of cycle
\gamma
nor
on the orientation of the edge
p_{i}p_{j}
: the choice of the simple cycle
in Definition
3.4
does not affect Equation (
3
)
since
\big({{\rm lk}}(\gamma,\Gamma_{1})-{\rm lk}(\gamma,\Gamma_{2})\big)
stays the
same due to the orientation choice. Furthermore, the choice of the
orientation of the edge
p_{i}p_{j}
does not affect the right hand side of
Equation (
3
) since the linking number changes sign and

p_{j}-p_{i}=-(p_{i}-p_{j})
.

Remark 3.5
.

Note that since
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
are neighbours, we have

{\rm lk}(\Gamma_{1},\gamma)-{\rm lk}(\Gamma_{2},\gamma)=\pm 1.

Definition 3.6
.

For the framework
G(p)
and a self-stress
\omega
of
G(p)
, we call a
mapping

l_{\omega,p}:\pi_{n-2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus G(p))\longrightarrow\Omega^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

a
differential lifting
, if
l_{\omega,p}(\Gamma)
is a constant

1
-form for all
\Gamma\in\pi_{n-2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus G(p))
, if it
satisfies the
neighbouring condition
for each neighbouring homotopy
classes, and if it takes zero on the neutral element
\Gamma_{\infty}\in\pi_{n-2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus G(p))
.

The following theorem is the higher dimensional analogue to
Theorem
2.4
of the
2
-dimensional case.

Theorem 3.7
.

For every framework
G(p)
with a self-stress
\omega
, there exists a
unique differential lifting
l_{\omega,p}
.

Proof.

For the simplicity of the exposition, we call the elements of
\pi_{n-2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus G(p))
chambers, similarly to the case
n=2
considered in the previous section.

Existence:
Let us say that a
path
between two chambers is a sequence of chambers that are neighbouring. The path is
closed
if the starting chamber is the same as the end chamber.

The
monodromy
of
\Omega^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
associated to a closed path
is the linear mapping defined by the summation of all forms on the right hand side of Equation (
3
).

Let us show that all monodromies are zero.

Case 1
: Let us consider monodromies for the closed path
C_{1},C_{2},C_{1}
. They correspond to passing through an edge and going back, which will result in

\alpha\mapsto\alpha+\big({\rm lk}(\Gamma_{1},\gamma)-{\rm lk}(\Gamma_{2},\gamma)\big)\omega_{ij}\cdot d(p_{i}{-}p_{j})+\big({\rm lk}(\Gamma_{2},\gamma)-{\rm lk}(\Gamma_{1},\gamma)\big)\omega_{ij}\cdot d(p_{i}{-}p_{j})=\alpha.

Case 2
: Here we need to extend the definition of a closed path around a point
p_{i}
. Consider a small sphere
S^{n-1}
centered at
p_{i}
and let us fix the marked point
O_{n-1}
for the homotopy group so that it lies in
S^{n-1}\setminus G(p)
. Let us consider a differential homotopy
H:[0,1]\times S^{n-2}\to S^{n-1}
such that

•

H(\{0\}\times S^{n-2})=\{O_{n-1}\}
and
H(\{1\}\times S^{n-2})=\{O_{n-1}\}
;

•

H([0,1]\times\{O_{n-2}\})=\{O_{n-1}\}
;

•

H:(0,1)\times S^{n-2}\setminus\{O_{n-2}\}\to S^{n-1}\setminus\{O_{n-1}\}
is a differentiable, non-singular bijection with positive Jacobian;

•

The pre-images of points
G(p)\cap S^{n-1}
have pairwise different first coordinates.

This homotopy induces the closed path between the chambers starting and ending in the identity chamber. We say that this path has a
counterclockwise
direction. If we reverse the first coordinate, then we have
clockwise
direction.

Let us consider a closed path around a point
p_{i}
in clockwise direction,
C_{1},\ldots,C_{s},C_{1}
. Then the monodromy is

(4)

\alpha\mapsto\alpha+\sum_{i=1}^{s}\big({\rm lk}(\Gamma_{i},\gamma)-{\rm lk}(\Gamma_{i+1},\gamma)\big)\omega_{ij}\cdot d(p_{i}{-}p_{j})=\alpha+d\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{s}\omega_{i,j}(p_{i}{-}p_{j})\Big)=\alpha.

The first equality is true by the linearity of the differentials and since the differences between all linking numbers are
+1
. The second equality holds by the equilibrium condition at
p_{i}
.

General case
: The monodromy for any closed path is zero since it can be decomposed into the elementary closed paths considered in Cases 1 and 2.

Therefore, if we have two paths
\mathcal{C}_{1}
and
\mathcal{C}_{2}
with the same starting chamber
C_{s}
and the same end chamber
C_{e}
, then the corresponding paths through neighbours will lead to the same differential form, since the closed path
\mathcal{C}_{2}
followed by the reversed path
\mathcal{C}_{1}
has zero monodromy.

Uniqueness:
By definition, the form corresponding to the unbounded element of
\pi_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus G(p))
(denoted by
C_{\infty}
) is zero. Then each path from
C_{\infty}
to
C
uniquely determines the form for
C
by Equation (
2
).
∎

Remark 3.8
.

Let
\Gamma=\Gamma_{1}+\Gamma_{2}
. Then

l_{\omega,p}(\Gamma)=l_{\omega,p}(\Gamma_{1})+l_{\omega,p}(\Gamma_{2}).

Therefore, once we know the values of the differential liftings for a
set of generators of
\pi_{n-2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus G(p))
, we get all the
values for
l_{\omega,p}
by the above additivity.

Theorem 3.9
.

Let
G
be any graph and
G(p)
be a framework in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The mapping

l_{\omega,p}
is a bijection between the space of self-stresses and
the space of differential liftings.

Proof.

The proof is the same as the proof of Theorem
2.8
.
∎

Remark 3.12
.

Definition
3.4
coincides with Definition
2.2
in

4.
Differential liftings of polytopal embeddings of complexes

In this section we will generalise differential liftings to higher
dimensional frameworks. We will investigate structures which are
polyhedral CW complexes where all faces in which forces appear are of the
same dimension. We introduce
m
-frameworks and their equilibrium
stresses.

4.1.
The
m
-frameworks

Let us recall a general definition of
m
-frameworks
from
[
18
]
. Our model for self-stressable
m
-frameworks
in this section will be based on the following structure. The term

plane
will refer to an affine subspace in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. In the
following let
n>m\geq 1
.

Definition 4.1
.

An
m
-framework

\mathcal{F}=(E,F,I,\mathbf{n})
consists of

E
, a collection of
(m{-}1)
-dimensional planes in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
;
F
, a
collection of
m
-dimensional planes in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
; a subset
I\subset\{(e,f)\in E\times F\mid e\subset f\}
; a function
\mathbf{n}

assigning to each pair
(e,f)\in I
, a unit vector
\mathbf{n}(e,f)

which is contained in
f
and which is orthogonal to
e
. We call planes
from
F

faces
and planes from
E

edges
.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
\mathcal{F}=(E,F,I,\mathbf{n})
be an
m
-framework. A

force-load

\omega
on
\mathcal{F}
is any function

\omega:F\to\mathbb{R}
. A framework
\mathcal{F}
together with a force-load

\omega
is said to be in
equilibrium
if for every
e\in E
we have

\sum_{(e,f)\in I}\omega(f)\mathbf{n}(e,f)=0.

Such a force-load is called an
equilibrium force-load
for

\mathcal{F}
.

Note that there is a difference between force-loads and stresses for
graphs. The latter is related the length of the edges whereas force-loads
are not. For now stresses are not defined as faces of
m
-frameworks are
planes and do not have specified shapes or magnitudes. After introducing
polytopal complexes below we could consider stresses as well.

Definition 4.3
.

An
m
-framework is said to be
self-stressable
or a

tensegrity
if there exists a non-zero
equilibrium force-load on it.

4.2.
Polytopal embeddings of complexes

An
m
-dimensional polytope
is the convex hull of a finite set of
points spanning
\mathbb{R}^{m}
. The boundary is piecewise linear so we have a
natural structure of faces. The faces of dimension
m{-}1
are called

facets
.

Definition 4.4
.

Consider a finite collection
F
of
m
-dimensional polytopes
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}

whose intersection is either a facet or a
q
-dimensional
polytope with
q\leq m-2
.
Let
E
be the collection of all facets of the polytopes in
F
(here we identify the same facets of distinct polytopes).
We also require that the planes of any two facets in
E
are distinct.
Then the pair
\mathcal{C}=(F,E)
is called an
m
-dimensional polytopal complex
in

\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Definition 4.5
.

The
m
-framework
\mathcal{F}_{C}=(E_{C},F_{C},I_{C},\mathbf{n})

associated
with an
m
-dimensional polytopal complex
C
is
defined by

•

E_{C}
, the collection of
(m{-}1)
-dimensional planes spanned
by facets of
E
;

•

F_{C}
, the collection of
m
-dimensional planes spanned by
polytopes of
F
;

•

I_{C}=\{(e,f)\in E_{C}\times F_{C}\mid\text{the facet}\ e\ \text{is a facet of polytope}\ f\}
;

•

a function
\mathbf{n}
assigning to each pair
(e,f)\in I_{C}

a unit vector
\mathbf{n}(e,f)
which is contained in
f
, is
orthogonal to
e
, and points to the interior of the polytope.

4.3.
Differential liftings of
m
-frameworks

Consider an
m
-dimensional polytopal complex
C
. While for graphs we
worked with
1
-forms, we now work with
m
-forms. As before, we will
study fundamental groups
\pi_{n-m-1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus C)
.
Again we can choose the base point to be any point of the complement. Note
that homotopic
(n{-}m{-}1)
-spheres will be associated with the same
m
-form.

For an oriented
m
-dimensional polytope
f
we set the differential form

\alpha_{f}=de_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge de_{m},

where
e_{1},\ldots,e_{m}
is an orthonormal basis of the span of
f
with
positive orientation.

For a given
m
-dimensional polytopal complex
C
, we consider maps from
the fundamental group
\pi_{n-m-1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus C)
to the space of
constant
m
-forms in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Once the map is fixed, we say that the
corresponding
m
-forms are
associated
with the homotopy classes of
spheres.

We say that two homotopy classes of spheres in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus C
are

neighbours at a facet

e
if there is a homotopy of spheres in
\mathbb{R}^{n}

crossing the framework along
e
only once (transversally).

An
m
-cycle
in
C
is an oriented sub-complex of
C
homeomorphic to the unit

m
-sphere.

Definition 4.7
.

Consider an
m
-dimensional polytopal complex
C
with a non-zero equilibrium force-load

\omega
on the associated
m
-framework
\mathcal{F}_{C}
. Let
\Gamma_{1}

and
\Gamma_{2}
be two different homotopy classes of spheres that are
neighbours at an oriented facet
f
with stress coefficient

\omega_{f}
. Fix a simple
m
-cycle
\gamma
in
C
which includes the
facet
f
with orientation of
f
.
Let
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
be two constant
m
-forms associated with

\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
. We say that
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
satisfy
the
neighbouring condition
if

(5)

\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{1}=({\rm lk}(\Gamma_{1},\gamma)-{\rm lk}(\Gamma_{2},\gamma))\cdot\omega_{f}\cdot\alpha_{f}.

Remark 4.8
.

Note that since
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
are neighbours, we have

{\rm lk}(\Gamma_{1},\gamma)-{\rm lk}(\Gamma_{2},\gamma)=\pm 1.

Remark 4.9
.

The choice of the simple
m
-cycle in Definition
4.7
does
not affect Equation (
5
) since

({\rm lk}(\gamma,\Gamma_{1})-{\rm lk}(\gamma,\Gamma_{2}))
does not change due to the orientation choice.

Note also that the choice of the orientation of the facet
f
does not
affect the right hand side of Equation (
5
) since the
linking number and the form
\alpha_{f}
simultaneously change their signs.

Definition 4.10
.

Consider an
m
-dimensional polytopal complex
C
with a non-zero force-load

\omega
on the associated
m
-framework
\mathcal{F}_{C}
. The

differential lifting

l_{\omega,C}
is the
m
-form which

•

is
constant on the connected components of the complement of
C
to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
;

•

satisfies the neighbouring condition for all neighbouring homotopy classes of spheres;

•

is zero on the homotopy class of spheres that includes the constant map.

Denote by
l_{\omega,C}(\Gamma)
the form corresponding to
\Gamma\in\pi_{n-m-1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus C)
.

Theorem 4.11
.

For every
m
-framework
\mathcal{F}_{C}
with a force-load
\omega
,
there exists a unique differential lifting of
C
.

Proof.

For the simplicity of the exposition, we call the elements of
\pi_{n-m-1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus G(p))
the
chambers
, similarly to the case
m=1
considered in the previous section.

Existence:
Let us say that a
path
between two
chambers is a sequence of chambers that are neighbouring. The path is
closed
if the starting chamber is the same as the end chamber.

The
monodromy
of
\Omega^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
associated to a closed path
is the linear mapping defined by the summation of all forms that are second summands on the right hand side of Equation (
5
).

Let us show that all monodromies are zero.

Case 1
: Let us consider monodromies for the closed path
\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2},\Gamma_{1}
. They correspond to passing through a polytope
f
and going back, which will result in

\alpha\mapsto\alpha+\big({\rm lk}(\Gamma_{1},\gamma)-{\rm lk}(\Gamma_{2},\gamma)\big)\omega_{ij}\cdot\alpha_{f}+\big({\rm lk}(\Gamma_{2},\gamma)-{\rm lk}(\Gamma_{1},\gamma)\big)\omega_{ij}\cdot\alpha_{f}=\alpha.

Case 2
: Here we need to extend the definition of a closed path
around a facet
e
in clockwise direction. It is done similarly to the
case
m=1
by restricting our
(n{-}m{-}1)
-spheres to the orthogonal
complement to
e
, which is
(n{-}m{+}1)
-dimensional.

Let us consider a closed path around a facet
e
in clockwise direction,
\Gamma_{1},\ldots,\Gamma_{s},\Gamma_{1}
. Then the monodromy is

\alpha\mapsto\alpha+\sum_{i=1}^{s}\big({\rm lk}(\Gamma_{i},\gamma)-{\rm lk}(\Gamma_{i+1},\gamma)\big)\omega_{ij}\cdot\alpha_{e}\wedge\mathbf{n}(e,f)=\alpha+\alpha_{e}\wedge d\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{s}\omega_{i,j}\mathbf{n}(e,f)\Big)=\alpha.

The first equality is true by the linearity of the differentials and since the differences between all linking numbers are
+1
. The second equality holds by the equilibrium condition at
e
.

General case
: The monodromy for any closed path is zero since it is decomposable into the elementary closed paths considered in Cases 1 and 2.

Therefore, if we have two paths
\mathcal{C}_{1}
and
\mathcal{C}_{2}
with
the same starting chamber
\Gamma_{s}
and the same end chamber
\Gamma_{e}
, then the
corresponding paths through neighbours will lead to the same differential form, since the closed path
\mathcal{C}_{2}
followed by the reversed path
\mathcal{C}_{1}
has zero monodromy.

Uniqueness:
This follows from exactly the same argument as in the proof of Theorem
4.11
.
∎

Remark 4.12
.

Consider
\Gamma,\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2}\in\pi_{n-m-1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus C)
satisfying the identity
\Gamma=\Gamma_{1}+\Gamma_{2}
. Then

l_{\omega,C}(\Gamma)=l_{\omega,C}(\Gamma_{1})+l_{\omega,C}(\Gamma_{2}).

Therefore, once we know the values of the differential liftings for a
set of generators of
\pi_{n-m-1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus C)
, we get all
the values for
l_{\omega,C}
by the above additivity.

Theorem 4.13
.

Let
C
be an
m
-dimensional polytopal complex.
The mapping
(C,\omega)\to l_{\omega,C}
is a bijection between the
space of force-loads and the space of differential liftings.

4.4.
Stresses and force-loads, and relation to the case
m=1

The general situation we considered in this section has one minor
difference from the settings of the previous two sections. Here, we
considered force-loads rather than stresses. As we know, the equilibrium
conditions for stresses are

\sum\limits_{\{j\mid j\neq i\}}\omega_{ij}(p_{i}-p_{j})=0,

while the equilibrium conditions for force-loads are normalised as

\sum\limits_{\{j\mid j\neq i\}}\omega_{ij}\frac{p_{i}-p_{j}}{\|p_{i}-p_{j}\|}=0.

For polytopes, we could also consider the corresponding stresses defined
by the equilibrium condition

\sum_{(e,f)\in I}\textrm{Vol}(f_{*})\omega(f)\mathbf{n}(e,f)=0,

where
f_{*}
is the facet corresponding to the
m
-plane
f
.

Now let us recall the Equation (
3
):

\alpha_{2}=\alpha_{1}+({\rm lk}(\Gamma_{1},\gamma)-{\rm lk}(\Gamma_{2},\gamma))\cdot\omega_{ij}\cdot d(p_{i}{-}p_{j}).

This equation coincides with the non-normalised version of
Equation (
5
):

\alpha_{2}=\alpha_{1}+\textrm{Vol}(f_{*})\big(({\rm lk}(\Gamma_{1},\gamma)-{\rm lk}(\Gamma_{2},\gamma))\cdot\omega_{f}\cdot\alpha_{f}\big).

Here, we have added the extra factor
\textrm{Vol}(f_{*})
to the second
summand of the right hand side of Equation (
5
).

5.
On liftings in general settings

In this section, we introduce general liftings.
In Subsection
5.1
we study liftings on regular spheres defined by integration of the differential liftings.
Further in Subsection
5.2
,
we discuss a slightly different notion of liftings on the Grassmannians of planes.

For simplicity we deal with polytopal embeddings of complexes that also cover all 1-frameworks without self-intersections. The theory for self-intersecting frameworks is very similar, so we omit it here. The problem for general complexes remains open.

5.1.
Liftings on regular spheres

In this subsection, we define liftings in the class of regular
k
-spheres in
\mathbb{R}^{n}

(i.e. differential embeddings of the unit

k
-sphere with maximal ranks of Jacobi matrices at every point; note that we allow self-intersections).
In this section we do not consider the case
k=0
.

5.1.1.
Definition of liftings

Denote the space of regular
k
-spheres passing through
p

by
\Omega_{k}(p)
.

Definition 5.1
.

Consider a regular sphere
\gamma
in
\Omega_{k}(p)
.
The homotopy corresponding to the linear homothety of
\gamma
(at time
1
) to
p
(at time
0
)
is called a
cone homotopy
. We denote it by

\mathcal{H}_{p}(\gamma)
.

Definition 5.2
.

Let
\alpha
be a decomposable
m
-form defined on
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a point
r\in S
.
Consider some oriented orthonormal basis

(e_{1},\ldots,e_{n})
in which we have

\alpha|_{r}=f\cdot e_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge e_{m}.

We say that the form

\beta|_{r}=f\cdot e_{m+1}\wedge\ldots\wedge e_{n}

is the
Hodge star
of
\alpha
at
p
.

An arbitrary
m
-form
\alpha
can be represented as a sum of decomposable forms. Here we define the
Hodge star
for
\alpha
by additivity.

We say that the form
\beta
is the
Hodge star

of
\alpha
if it is the Hodge star at every point of
S
.
Denote the Hodge star by
\star\alpha
.

Definition 5.3
.

Consider an
m
-dimensional polytopal complex
C
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with a force-load
\omega
on the associated framework
\mathcal{F}_{C}
. Let
n>m+1
and let
p
be in the complement of
\mathcal{F}_{C}
.
Consider the function
L_{\mathcal{F}_{C},\omega,p}
defined on the set of all
regular spheres of dimension
n-m-1
as follows:

L_{\mathcal{F}_{C},\omega,p}(\gamma)=\int\limits_{\mathcal{H}_{p}(\gamma)}\star\alpha[C,\omega].

We say that the function
L_{\mathcal{F}_{C},\omega,p}
is the
lifting
of
(\mathcal{F}_{C},\omega)
.

Remark 5.4
.

Liftings are continuous in the continuous topology on
\Omega_{n-m-1}(p)
.
There is no significant dependence on the choice of the point
p
(due to Proposition
5.5
below).

Let us collect several simple statements on integration of differential forms over
regular embeddings without boundary.

5.1.2.
Co-dimension 0 obstacles cycle integration

Let us start with the following proposition.

Proposition 5.5
.

Let
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
be two regular homotopies from
\gamma_{1}
to
\gamma_{2}
for the regular

(n-m-1)
-spheres, and let
\alpha
be any constant
(n-m)
-form
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Then

\int_{H_{1}}\alpha=\int_{H_{2}}\alpha.

Proof.

Let
H\times I
be a regular homotopy between two regular homotopies
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
.
Then by Stokes’ formula (see, e.g. in
[
1
]
) we have

\int_{H_{1}}\alpha-\int_{H_{2}}\alpha=\int_{H\times I}d\alpha=\int_{H\times I}0=0.

This implies the statement of the proposition.
∎

5.1.3.
Co-dimension 1 obstacles cycle integration

Let us continue with the following definition.

Definition 5.6
.

Let
\ell
be an
m
-dimensional plane in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
we say that a
Heaviside form
on the space of regular
m
-spheres with respect to

is defined by the following formula

\chi_{\ell}(\gamma)={\rm lk}(\ell,\gamma)\cdot\star d{\ell},

where
d\ell
is the volume
m
-form on
\ell
.

Note that
\chi_{\ell}
is an
(n{-}m)
-form.

Proposition 5.7
.

Let
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
be two regular homotopies from
\gamma_{1}
to
\gamma_{2}
for the regular

(n{-}m{-}1)
-spheres in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
and let
\ell
be an
m
-dimensional plane in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Then

\int_{H_{1}}\chi_{\ell}=\int_{H_{2}}\chi_{\ell}.

Proof.

Case 1
. Assume that the homotopies
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
from
\gamma_{1}
to
\gamma_{2}
cross the plane
\ell
at single points
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
(at parameter values
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
) respectively.

Denote the closed loop formed by
H_{1}
and
H_{2}

(where
H_{2}
is passed in the opposite direction).
The parameter values
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
(for
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
) divide this loop into two parts

S_{1}
and
S_{2}
containing
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
, respectively.

Consider the homotopy which is a parallel transform
of
H_{1}(*,t_{1})
by the vector
p_{2}{-}p_{1}

(here we add an infinitesimal tubular neighbourhood
from the base point for the
(n{-}m{-}1)
-spheres along the vector

, which will not affect the value of the integrals).
Denote the corresponding path by

and its inverse by

.

Finally, let us pick some regular homotopy
that joins
H_{1}(*,t_{1})
transported to

and
H_{2}(*,t_{2})
. Denote this path by
S_{5}
and its inverse by

(we assume that this homotopy keeps the point

fixed and the other points of
the homotopy do not intersect

), see Figure

Then we have:

General Case

5.1.4.
Co-dimension 2 obstacles cycle integration

Next we show the following monodromy statement for

(m-1)
-dimensional facets in

m
-dimensional polytopal complexes.

Proposition 5.8
.

Let us consider a homotopy from a point to itself by
m
-spheres along the
(m+1)
-sphere.
We assume that the convex interior of the

(m+1)
-sphere contains the only point of facets at the center of the sphere.
Then the integral of
\star\alpha[C,\omega]
over this sphere is zero.

Proof.

Let us consider the cone homotopy that shrinks the
(m+1)
-sphere to its center along the cone.
On the one hand by Propositions
5.5

and
5.7
,
this will not change the value of the integral.
On the other hand we are shrinking to a single point, and hence the limiting value of the integral tends to 0.

Therefore, the integral along the
(m+1)
-sphere around a facet
e
is zero.
∎

5.1.5.
Continuity of liftings

Let us finally formulate the following general property of liftings.

Theorem 5.9
.

The lifting is a continuous function in the continuous topology on

\Omega_{n-m-1}(p)
.

Proof.

Let us outline the proof.
Consider a small regular perturbation
(within any small distance in the space of continuous homotopies)
changing the original regular
homotopy to the one avoiding all
(m-1)
-dimensional facets of the complex and
transversally intersecting the complex in finitely many points.
The resulting homotopy can be decomposed into the homotopies studied in Propositions
5.5
,

5.7
, and
5.8
.
∎

5.2.
Grassmannian liftings

We conclude this section with the following remarkable representation of liftings as functions on affine Grassmannians.

The informal idea behind the below definitions is as follows.
First of all, let us restrict the sets of regular
m
-spheres to geometric
m
-spheres passing through a given point
p
.
Secondly, let us “move”
p
to infinity.
That will bring us to the affine Grassmannian of oriented
m
-planes
{\rm Gr}(m,n)
.

First of all we give a general definition of distances between planes.

Definition 5.10
.

The
distance between the planes

\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}

spanned by
(p_{1},\ldots,p_{n})
and
(q_{1},\ldots,q_{m})
respectively is the following number

{\rm dist}(\pi_{1},\pi_{2}):=\frac{\det(q_{2}-q_{1},q_{3}-q_{1},\ldots,q_{m}-q_{1},p_{1}-q_{1},\ldots,p_{n}-q_{1})}{\det(q_{2}-q_{1},q_{3}-q_{1},\ldots,q_{m}-q_{1})\cdot\det(p_{2}-p_{1},p_{3}-p_{2},\ldots,p_{n}-p_{1})}.

Informally, distance functions serve as linear functions over Grassmannians.

In order to define the values of the liftings at all lines of the Grassmannian we use the following notion.
We consider the Grassmannian as a subset of the projective plane
\mathbb{R}P^{N}
defined by Plücker coordinates of planes.
So we have a natural structure of the differential manifold on Grassmannians.

Definition 5.11
.

Let
\mathcal{F}_{C}
be a framework of an
m
-dimensional polytopal complex
C
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
A differentiable path in
{\rm Gr}(n{-}m{-}1,n)
is
\mathcal{F}_{C}
-simple
if it intersects

\mathcal{F}_{C}
only finitely many times;
each of the intersections, except the very last point of the path, is in the interior of some
m
-dimensional face and it is transversal to this face (see Figure
11
). The last line in the path can be taken arbitrarily.

Figure 11.
Transversal intersections of a differentiable path with one of the elements of
\mathcal{F}_{C}

Let us define a lifting along
\mathcal{F}_{C}
-simple paths.

Definition 5.12
.

Consider an
\mathcal{F}_{C}
-simple path
H
crossing an
m
-dimensional face
f
of
\mathcal{F}_{C}
.
We say that the
sign of the crossing
is the number

\mu(H,f):={\rm sign}\Big(\frac{\partial({\rm dist}(H(t),f))}{\partial t}\Big).

We say that a simple path
H
intersects an
m
-dimensional face
f
of
\mathcal{F}_{C}
with
sign multiplicity

k
if the total sum of its sign of crossings with
f
is
k
.

Definition 5.13
.

Consider a framework
\mathcal{F}_{C}
of an
m
-dimensional polytopal complex
C
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with a force-load
\omega
.
Let us fix some
\ell_{0}\in{\rm Gr}(n{-}m{-}1,n)
that is far away (namely it does not intersect the convex hull of
\mathcal{F}_{C}
).
Let
\ell
be an arbitrary plane in
{\rm Gr}(n{-}m{-}1,n)
.
Let now
H
be an
\mathcal{F}_{C}
-simple path
from
\ell_{0}
to
\ell
.
We say that the lifting at
\ell
with respect to
the path
H
is the following number

L_{\mathcal{F}_{C},\omega,H}(\ell):=\sum\limits_{f\in F_{C}}\mu(H,f)\cdot\omega_{f}\cdot{\rm dist}(\ell,f).

We conclude this paper with the following general theorem.

Theorem 5.14
.

The lifting
L_{\mathcal{F}_{C},\omega,H}(\ell)

does not depend on the choice of an

\mathcal{F}_{C}
-simple path
H
.

Proof.

The case of graph frameworks in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

Here we consider the Grassmannian
{\rm Gr}(1,3)
.

Direct calculations show
that the monodromy around a trivalent vertex is trivial.
Without loss of generality we go around the three edges
p_{1}p_{2}
,
p_{1}p_{3}
and
p_{1}p_{4}
, where

p_{1}=(0,0,0),\hskip 10.00002ptp_{2}=(1,0,0),\hskip 10.00002ptp_{3}=(\cos\alpha,\sin\alpha,0),\hskip 10.00002ptp_{4}=(\cos\beta,\sin\beta,0).

(Here
\alpha\neq\beta
and

\alpha,\beta\in(0,\pi)\cup(\pi,2\pi)
).
The starting and the ending line is
p_{5}p_{6}
where

p_{5}=(a,b,0),\hskip 10.00002ptp_{6}=(c,d,1).

In this case, the force-loads on edges
p_{1}p_{2}
,
p_{1}p_{3}
and
p_{1}p_{4}

are

\lambda\sin(\alpha-\beta)
,
\lambda\sin\beta
,
and
-\lambda\sin\alpha
, respectively (for some real
\lambda
).
Then the contribution of the monodromy around the origin (passed clockwise) is as follows:

\begin{array}[]{l}\lambda\sin(\alpha-\beta)\cdot{\rm dist}(p_{5}p_{6},p_{1}p_{2})+\lambda\sin\beta\cdot{\rm dist}(p_{5}p_{6},p_{1}p_{3})-\lambda\sin\alpha\cdot{\rm dist}(p_{5}p_{6},p_{1}p_{4}))\\
\displaystyle\qquad=\Bigg(-{{\frac{b\sin\left(\alpha-\beta\right)}{\sqrt{\left(c-a\right)^{2}+\left(d-b\right)^{2}+1}}}}+{{\frac{\left(a\sin\alpha-b\cos\alpha\right)\sin\beta}{\sqrt{\left(c-a\right)^{2}+\left(d-b\right)^{2}+1}}}}-{{\frac{\sin\alpha\left(a\sin\beta-b\cos\beta\right)}{\sqrt{\left(c-a\right)^{2}+\left(d-b\right)^{2}+1}}}}\Bigg)\cdot\lambda\\
\qquad=0.\end{array}

Let us now consider a vertex of general valency
d
.
Such vertex can be decomposed locally at the vertex into the sum of trivalent vertices of the original graph
(using the techniques of quantisations introduced in
[
16
]
).
Let us illustrate it with the following example:

Any
\mathcal{F}_{C}
-simple closed path is decomposable into several monodromies around single vertices.
Hence, by additivity the value of the corresponding lifting is zero.

Therefore, two different
\mathcal{F}_{C}
-simple paths
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
to the same line
\ell
define the same lifting (as the closed path obtained by
H_{1}
and then
H_{2}
inverse provides the zero lifting).
This concludes the proof for the graph frameworks in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

The case of
m
-polytopal frameworks in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Let us sketch the proof in this case; in fact it repeats the proof of the previous theorem.

Firstly, we consider a monodromy of parallel

(n{-}m{-}1)
-planes around some trivalent facet
e\in E_{C}
. Here we assume that any line of
e

is not parallel to a line in the parallel planes.
This monodromy can be projected to the
monodromy of a line around a trivalent vertex of a graph
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Hence it is trivial by the above case.

Secondly, the monodromy about an arbitrary facet
e
, can be decomposed into the sum of monodromies about trivalent copies of
e
.

Thirdly, by additivity the lifting along
any
\mathcal{F}_{C}
-simple closed path
is zero.

Therefore, the liftings along different paths
provide the same value.
∎

Remark 5.15
.

One can restrict liftings
to a pencil of parallel planes in Grassmannians.
Then (by identifying the points of the parallel planes) we can project the
liftings to the liftings over
\pi_{0}

of the corresponding projected polytopal

m
-dimensional complex in
\mathbb{R}^{m+1}
.
(Here we assume that the projection of
\mathcal{F}_{C}
does not reduce the dimensions of its faces.)
