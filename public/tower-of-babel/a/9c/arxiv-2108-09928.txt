Loss of regularity for the 2D Euler equations
In-Jee Jeong

Abstract
In this note, we construct solutions to the 2D Euler equations which belong to the Yudovich class but lose W 1 , p W^{1,p} regularity continuously with time.



1
Introduction

The dynamics of inviscid and incompressible fluid is described by the Euler equations: given some
n
-dimensional domain
\Omega
, the system is given by

\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}u+u\cdot\nabla u+\nabla p&=0,\\
\nabla\cdot u&=0,\end{aligned}\right.

(1.1)

where
u(t,\cdot):\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
p(t,\cdot):\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
denote the velocity and pressure of fluid at time
t
, respectively. When
\Omega
has boundary, (
1.1
) should be supplemented with the slip boundary condition
u(t,x)\cdot n(x)=0
for
x\in\partial\Omega
, where
n(x)
is the unit normal vector.

We shall be concerned with the two-dimensional Euler equations on
\mathbb{T}^{2}=(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})^{2}
; introducing the vorticity
\omega=\nabla\times u
and taking the curl of (
1.1
), we have the vorticity form of the 2D Euler equations:

\begin{split}\partial_{t}\omega+u\cdot\nabla\omega=0,\quad u=\nabla^{\perp}\Delta^{-1}\omega.\end{split}

(1.2)

Here,
\nabla^{\perp}=(-\partial_{x_{2}},\partial_{x_{1}})^{\top}
. The goal of this note is to construct solutions to (
1.2
) which belongs to a well-posedness class yet lose Sobolev regularity with time.

To motivate our results, we briefly review the well-posedness theory for the Euler equations (
1.1
). For sufficiently nice
n
-dimensional domains, (
1.1
) is locally well-posed in
C^{k,\alpha}\cap L^{2}
with
k\geq 1
and
0<\alpha<1
and
W^{s,p}
with
s>\frac{n}{p}+1
. That is, for
u_{0}
belonging to such a space, there exist
T>0
and a unique local-in time solution (
1.1
) such that
u(t)
belongs to the same space for all
0\leq t<T
and
u(0)=u_{0}
. It is known that these regularity requirements are sharp; for
u_{0}
less regular, the regularity may not propagate in time for a solution of (
1.1
). To be more precise, for
n=3
,
s=1
, and any
1\leq p
, there exist examples of solutions
u(t)
such that

\begin{split}u(0)\in W^{s,p}(\mathbb{T}^{n})\quad\mbox{and}\quad u(t)\notin W^{s,p}(\mathbb{T}^{n})\quad\mbox{for any}\quad t>0.\end{split}

(1.3)

Similar examples exist which do not propagate initial
C^{\alpha}
regularity of
u(0)
for any
\alpha<1
. We shall recall the constructions below; for now, let us just note that the examples are based the so-called
2+\frac{1}{2}
flow, which gives the restriction that
n\geq 3
(Bardos-Titi
[
1
,
2
]
). It seems that in the two dimensional case, solutions satisfying (
1.3
) have not been constructed before.

Recently, Bourgain-Li
[
3
,
4
,
5
]
established ill-posedness of (
1.1
) at critical regularity; roughly speaking, they were able to prove that (
1.3
) occurs with
s=\frac{n}{p}+1
and
p>1
, in dimensions two and three. (See also
[
11
,
13
,
17
,
22
,
18
,
16
]
for simpler proofs and further developments.) It does not imply that (
1.3
) occurs also in less regular Sobolev spaces; in principle, this question could be harder since one expects less control over weaker solutions.

Our main result shows that an
L^{\infty}\cap W^{1,p}
-vorticity with
p<2
may continuously lose integrability with time. For bounded initial vorticity, the uniqueness and existence of the solution
\omega(t,x)\in L^{\infty}([0,\infty);L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2}))
is provided by the celebrated Yudovich theory
[
25
]
. Moreover, we shall take the initial data
\omega_{0}
to be Lipschitz continuous away from a single point, which is a property preserved by Yudovich solutions (see
[
11
]
for a proof). In particular, for any
t>0
,
\nabla\omega
is well-defined almost everywhere in space and bounded away from a single point.

Theorem 1.1
.

There exist
1\leq p^{*}<2
and
c_{0}>0
such that for any
p^{*}<p_{0}<2
, we can find an initial data
\omega_{0}\in L^{\infty}\cap\left(\cap_{p<p_{0}}W^{1,p}\right)(\mathbb{T}^{2})
such that the unique solution
\omega(t)
satisfies, with some
T^{*}=T^{*}(p_{0})>0
,

\begin{split}\|\omega(t,\cdot)\|_{W^{1,{q(t)}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}=+\infty~,\end{split}

for

\begin{split}{q(t):=}1+\frac{1}{\frac{1}{p_{0}-1}+c_{0}t},\quad 0\leq t\leq T^{*}.\end{split}

Remark 1.2
.

Let us give a few remarks on the statement.

•

For any
t>0
, we may define the index
p(t)=\sup\{0<q:\omega(t)\in W^{1,q}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\}
. While the statement of Theorem
1.1
does not exclude the possibility that
p(t)
jumps downwards in time, this behavior is impossible due to the bound given in Proposition
2.2
. Hence, for the data
\omega_{0}
given in Theorem
1.1
, the index
p(t)
is continuous in time and satisfies
p(t)<p_{0}
for any
t\in(0,T^{*}]
.

•

In terms of the velocity, Theorem
1.1
says that (
1.3
) occurs at least for a small interval of time with
u_{0}\in W^{2,p}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
with
p^{*}<p<2
.

•

The restriction
p^{*}<p_{0}
should be technical but it could take significant work to remove it; we shall see in the proof that
p^{*}
depends only on the constant
C
in Lemma
3.2
.

•

Our data can be localized to any small ball and hence the specific choice of domain
\mathbb{T}^{2}
is not important.

Organization of the paper

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we present several examples and discussions which illustrate delicate nature of Euler equations at low regularity. The proofs are then presented in Section
3
.

2
Examples

We present several examples in order to give a sense of the behavior described in the main results. In
2.1
, we recall the well-known construction of Bardos-Titi in three dimensions and make a comparison with our result. Then in
2.2
, we present a time-independent vector field which is able to cause the phenomenon of continuous loss of Sobolev regularity for the advected scalar. In the same section we comment on some difficulties in actually using the vector field in the context of the Euler equations.

2.1
Loss of regularity in shear flows

Any solution of the 2D Euler equations can be lifted to solutions in 3D (and higher); given
(u^{2D},p^{2D})
solving (
1.1
) in
\mathbb{T}^{2}
, define

\begin{split}u(t,x)=(u^{2D}(t,x_{1},x_{2}),u_{3}(t,x_{1},x_{2}))\end{split}

where
u_{3}
is any solution to

\begin{split}\partial_{t}u_{3}+u^{2D}\cdot\nabla u_{3}=0.\end{split}

Then one can see that
u
defines a solution to the 3D Euler equations with
p=p^{2D}
. This is sometime referred to as the
2+\frac{1}{2}
-dimensional flow (see
[
20
]
). A special class of
2+\frac{1}{2}
-dimensional flows are given by the following
shear flows
:

\begin{split}u(t,x)=(u_{1}(x_{2}),0,u_{3}(x_{1}-tu_{1}(x_{2}),x_{2})).\end{split}

(2.1)

Note that this defines a solution to the 3D Euler equations in
\mathbb{T}^{3}
with zero pressure for any reasonably smooth functions
u_{1}
and
u_{3}
of one variable.

For
u_{1}
and
u_{3}
belonging to

[
1

The form (
2.1
) was introduced in a work of DiPerna-Majda

Proposition 2.1

(see

.

There exists initial data
u_{0}\in W^{1,p}(\mathbb{T}^{3})
with any
1\leq p
(
u_{0}\in C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T}^{3})
with any
0<\alpha<1
, resp.) of the form

\begin{split}u_{0}(x)=(u_{1}(x_{2}),0,u_{3}(x_{1},x_{2}))\end{split}

such that the corresponding shear flow solution
u(t)
given in (
2.1
) does not belong to
W^{1,p}(\mathbb{T}^{3})
(
C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T}^{3})
, resp.) for any
t>0
.

Proof.

We only consider the
W^{1,p}
-case with
1\leq p<\infty
. Note that

\begin{split}\|u_{0}\|_{W^{1,p}}^{p}\lesssim\int_{0}^{1}|\partial_{x_{2}}u_{1}(x_{2})|^{p}dx_{1}+\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}|\partial_{x_{1}}u_{3}(x_{1},x_{2})|^{p}+|\partial_{x_{2}}u_{3}(x_{1},x_{2})|^{p}dx_{1}dx_{2}\end{split}

and computing
\partial_{x_{2}}u(t)
using (
2.1
), we obtain that

\begin{split}\|u(t)\|_{W^{1,p}}^{p}\gtrsim-\|\partial_{x_{2}}u_{3}\|_{L^{p}}^{p}+t^{p}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}|\partial_{x_{2}}u_{1}(x_{2})|^{p}|\partial_{x_{1}}u_{3}(x_{1},x_{2})|^{p}dx_{1}dx_{2}.\end{split}

(2.2)

Given the above formula, let us define
u_{1}(x_{2})=|x_{2}|^{1-\frac{1}{p}+\epsilon}
for small
\epsilon>0
near
x_{2}=0
and smooth otherwise. Next, define
u_{3}(x_{1},x_{2})=|x|^{1-\frac{2}{p}+\epsilon}
near
|x|=0
, where
|x|=\sqrt{x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}}
. Since
|\partial_{x_{i}}u_{3}|\lesssim|x|^{-\frac{2}{p}+\epsilon}
for
i=1,2
, it is clear that
u_{0}\in W^{1,p}
. In particular, the first term on the right hand side of (
2.2
) is bounded. Furthermore, employing polar coordinates
(r,\theta)
,

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}|\partial_{x_{2}}u_{1}(x_{2})|^{p}|\partial_{x_{1}}u_{3}(x_{1},x_{2})|^{p}dx_{1}dx_{2}&\gtrsim\int_{0}^{r_{0}}r^{-1+\epsilon p}r^{-2+\epsilon p}rdr=+\infty\end{split}

for
\epsilon<p^{-1}
. This finishes the proof.
∎

Note that for solutions of the form (
2.1
), the integrability index may drop instantaneously at
t=0
but
u(t_{1})
and
u(t_{2})
have the same Sobolev regularity for any
t_{1},t_{2}>0
. (One can see that
u(t)
belongs to at least

for any

.) Similar phenomenon occurs in the scale of
C^{\alpha}
spaces, see
[
1
]
. This behavior is very different from continuous loss of integrability that our solutions exhibit. Actually, in two dimensions, conservation of
\omega\in L^{\infty}

prohibits
jump of the integrability index, as the following remarkable result due to Elgindi shows:

Proposition 2.2

(see
[
11
, Lemma 3]
)
.

Let
\omega_{0}\in(L^{\infty}\cap W^{1,p})(\mathbb{T}^{2})
with
0<p\leq 2
. Then, the Yudovich solution corresponding to
\omega_{0}
satisfies

\begin{split}\|\omega(t)\|_{W^{1,q(t)}}\leq\|\omega_{0}\|_{W^{1,p}}\end{split}

where
q(t)
is the solution to the ODE

\begin{split}\dot{q}(t)=-C\|\omega_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}}q(t)^{2},\quad q(0)=p\end{split}

with some universal constant
C>0
not depending on
p
.

2.2
Loss of regularity with the Bahouri-Chemin background

We shall consider the following vector field
v
on the positive quadrant
(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{2}
:

\begin{split}v_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})=-x_{1}\ln\frac{1}{x_{2}}~,\qquad v_{2}(x_{1},x_{2})=x_{2}\ln\frac{1}{x_{2}}~.\end{split}

(2.3)

This is a toy model for the Bahouri-Chemin velocity field which will be introduced below. Although
v
is not exactly divergence free (the divergence satisfies
\mathrm{div}(v)=-1
), one can add
x_{2}
to
v_{2}(x_{1},x_{2})
to make it divergence-free. (The resulting flow can be shown to demonstrate the same behavior but computations are more tedious.) We have the following

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
f_{0}
be the function defined in
(\mathbb{R}_{+})^{2}
by
f_{0}(r,\theta)=\sin(2\theta)
for
0\leq r<1
in polar coordinates. Then, the solution
f(t)
to the transport equation

\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}f+v\cdot\nabla f=0,&\\
f(t=0)=f_{0}&\end{aligned}\right.

(2.4)

satisfies

\begin{split}\|f(t)\|_{W^{1,q(t)}}=+\infty\end{split}

(2.5)

and

\begin{split}\|f(t)\|_{W^{1,q(t)-\epsilon}}<\infty,\mbox{ for any }\epsilon>0\end{split}

(2.6)

for all
t>0
, where
q(t):=\frac{2}{2-\exp(-t)}
.

Before we start the proof, let us observe a convenient lemma which gives a lower bound on the
W^{1,p}
norm based on the distance between two level sets.

Lemma 2.4
.

Assume that
f\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap Lip(\mathbb{R}^{2}\backslash\{0\})
written in polar coordinates satisfies
f(r,0)=0
and
f(r,\theta^{*}(r))=1
for all
0<r<r_{0}
with some
r_{0}>0
where
\theta^{*}:[0,r_{0}]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}
is a measurable function. Then,

\begin{split}\|\nabla f\|_{L^{p}}^{p}\geq c\int_{0}^{r_{0}}r^{1-p}(\theta^{*}(r))^{1-p}dr.\end{split}

Proof.

The
L^{p}
-norm of
\nabla f
is equivalent, up to absolute constants, with

\begin{split}\|\partial_{r}f\|_{L^{p}}^{p}+\|r^{-1}\partial_{\theta}f\|_{L^{p}}^{p}.\end{split}

(2.7)

Note that for any fixed
0<r<r_{0}
,

\begin{split}1=f(r,\theta^{*}(r))-f(r,0)=\int_{0}^{\theta^{*}(r)}\partial_{\theta}f(r,\theta^{\prime})d\theta^{\prime}\leq|\theta^{*}(r)|^{1-\frac{1}{p}}\left(\int_{0}^{2\pi}|\partial_{\theta}f(r,\theta)|^{p}d\theta\right)^{\frac{1}{p}},\end{split}

which gives

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{2\pi}|\partial_{\theta}f(r,\theta)|^{p}d\theta\geq|\theta^{*}(r)|^{1-p}.\end{split}

Hence

\begin{split}\|\nabla f\|_{L^{p}}^{p}\geq c\int_{0}^{r_{0}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}r^{1-p}|\partial_{\theta}f(r,\theta)|^{p}d\theta dr\geq c\int_{0}^{r_{0}}r^{1-p}(\theta^{*}(r))^{1-p}dr.\qed\end{split}

Proof of Proposition
2.3
.

We fix some
0<a<1
and let
\phi(t)=(\phi_{1}(t),\phi_{2}(t))
be the trajectory of the point
(a,a)
by the flow generated by
v
. Then, from

\begin{split}\dot{\phi}_{2}=\phi_{2}\ln\frac{1}{\phi_{2}},\end{split}

we have

\begin{split}\ln\frac{1}{\phi_{2}}=e^{-t}\ln\frac{1}{a}.\end{split}

Next, from

\begin{split}\dot{\phi}_{1}=-\phi_{1}\ln\frac{1}{\phi_{2}}=-\phi_{1}e^{-t}\ln\frac{1}{a},\end{split}

we obtain that

\begin{split}\ln\frac{1}{\phi_{1}}=(2-e^{-t})\ln\frac{1}{a}.\end{split}

This shows that the image by the flow map
\Phi(t,\cdot)
of the diagonal segment
\{(a,a):0<a<1\}
can be parameterized by

\Gamma(t):=\{(x_{1},x_{1}^{\frac{\exp(-t)}{2-\exp(-t)}}):0<x_{1}<1\}

for any
t>0
. (See Figure 1.) That is, the solution
f
with initial data
f_{0}(r,\theta)=\sin(2\theta)
for
r\leq 1
satisfies
f(t,\Gamma(t))\equiv 1
. Since
f(t,(0,x_{2}))\equiv 0
for all
t
, (
2.5
) follows from a computation similar to the one given in the proof of Lemma
2.4
: for any fixed
t>0
and
0<x_{2}<1

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{1}|\partial_{x_{1}}f(x_{1},x_{2})|^{p}dx_{1}\geq x_{2}^{-\frac{1}{\gamma}(p-1)},\quad\gamma=\frac{\exp(-t)}{2-\exp(-t)},\end{split}

and hence

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}|\partial_{x_{1}}f(x_{1},x_{2})|^{p}dx_{1}dx_{2}\geq\int_{0}^{1}x_{2}^{-\frac{1}{\gamma}(p-1)}dx_{2},\end{split}

which is integrable if and only if
-\frac{1}{\gamma}(p-1)>-1
. We omit the proof of (
2.6
).

In the explicitly solvable example above, continuous loss of Sobolev regularity occurs from the cusping of a level set for the advected scalar. While it is correct that the velocity corresponding to
\omega_{0}=\sin(2\theta)
has approximately the form (
2.3
) (cf. Lemma
3.2
), the velocity field immediately changes for
t>0
in the 2D Euler case, which is a nonlinear problem. Indeed, cusping of the level sets weakens the velocity gradient as some chunk of vorticity moves away from the origin (the integral
I(x)
in (
3.2
) is reduced), which at the same time slows down the cusping phenomenon. To illustrate this point, one can simply compute the 2D Euler velocity gradient corresponding to
f(t)
where
f
is the solution to (
2.4
). At the origin (which is the point
\nabla\nabla^{\perp}\Delta^{-1}f
is supposedly most singular),

\begin{split}\partial_{x_{1}}\partial_{x_{2}}\Delta^{-1}f(t,0)\simeq c\int\frac{y_{1}y_{2}}{|y|^{4}}f_{0}\circ\phi(t,y)dy\simeq c^{\prime}\int\frac{y_{1}y_{2}}{|y|^{4}}\frac{\phi_{1}(y)}{\phi_{2}(y)}dy\simeq\frac{c^{\prime\prime}}{t}\end{split}

for
0<t\ll 1
, using that
\phi_{1}(t,y)\simeq y_{1}y_{2}^{t}
and
\phi_{2}(t,y)\simeq y_{2}^{1-t}
. Hence, the velocity corresponding to
f
becomes Lipschitz continuous instantaneously for
t>0
.

It is an interesting problem by itself to determine the behavior of the Yudovich solution with initial data
\omega_{0}\sim\sin(2\theta)
near the origin. In
[
10
, Section 6.2]
, a formal nonlinear system which models this behavior was introduced. Roughly speaking, the model equation is obtained by replacing the 2D Euler velocity gradient with the main term
I(x)
in (
3.2
). The formal model is still not explicitly solvable, but it can be reduced to a second order ODE system with time-dependent coefficients. The numerical solution suggests that cusping of level sets occur but the cusps are not algebraic (that is, of the form
(x_{1},x_{1}^{\gamma})
) but just logarithmic of the form
(x_{1},x_{1}(\ln\frac{1}{x_{1}})^{\gamma})
for some time-dependent
\gamma
. The corresponding velocity indeed
regularizes
with time; it can be argued that
\|\nabla u(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}\simeq ct^{-1}
for
0<t\ll 1
. This does not allow for continuous in time loss of Sobolev regularity for
\omega
.

In the proof, we shall take the initial vorticity

3.1
) in time, one can obtain the following flow estimate which is valid for
0<t<t_{0}
and
|x-x^{\prime}|<\frac{1}{4}
with some absolute constants
t_{0},c>0
:

\begin{split}|x-x^{\prime}|^{1+ct}\leq|\Phi(t,x)-\Phi(t,x^{\prime})|\leq|x-x^{\prime}|^{1-ct}\end{split}

and in particular, by taking
x^{\prime}=0
,

\begin{split}|x|^{1+ct}\leq|\Phi(t,x)|\leq|x|^{1-ct}.\end{split}

(3.5)

We now prove that for
0<t<t_{0}
and
0<r<\delta_{0}
,
\omega(t)\equiv 1
on the region

\mathbb{A}:=\{(r,\theta):0<r<\delta_{0},\frac{\pi}{20}<\theta<\frac{\pi}{4}\}

for
t_{0}
and
\delta_{0}
taken sufficiently small. In the following we shall take them (in a way depending only on other absolute constants) smaller whenever it becomes necessary. Towards a contradiction, assume that there is a point
x_{0}\in[0,1]^{2}
with
\omega_{0}(x_{0})<1
and
0<t<t_{0}
that
\Phi(t,x_{0})\in\mathbb{A}
. For
\omega_{0}(x_{0})<1
,
x_{0}=(r_{0},\theta_{0})
(in polar coordinates) should satisfy at least one of the following: (i)
r_{0}>\frac{1}{2}
, (ii)
\theta_{0}<r_{0}^{\beta}
, (iii)
\frac{\pi}{2}-\theta_{0}<r_{0}^{\beta}
. By arranging
\delta_{0},t_{0}
small, we can exclude the possibility of (i) using (
3.5
). Now assume (ii) holds. We may further assume that
r_{0}<\sqrt{\delta_{0}}
by taking
t_{0}
smaller if necessary. Writing
\Phi(t,x_{0})=(r(t),\theta(t))
in polar coordinates and applying (
3.1
) with
x=\Phi(t,x_{0})
and
x^{\prime}=(r(t),0)
,

\begin{split}|u^{\theta}(t,\Phi(t,x_{0}))|\leq Cr(t)\theta(t)\ln\left(\frac{1}{r(t)\theta(t)}\right).\end{split}

Here
u^{\theta}=u\cdot e^{\theta}
is the angular component of the velocity and we have used that it vanishes on the axes, due to the odd symmetry of
\omega
. Hence we can bound

\begin{split}\left|\frac{d}{dt}\theta(t)\right|=\frac{|u^{\theta}(t,\Phi(t,x_{0}))|}{|\Phi(t,x_{0})|}\leq C\theta(t)\ln\left(\frac{1}{r(t)\theta(t)}\right)\leq C\theta(t)\ln\left(\frac{1}{r_{0}^{1+ct}\theta(t)}\right).\end{split}

Now we may take
t_{0}
small that
1+ct<2
for all
t\leq t_{0}
and then

\begin{split}\frac{d}{dt}\ln\left(\frac{1}{\theta(t)}\right)\geq-C\left(\ln\left(\frac{1}{r_{0}}\right)+\ln\left(\frac{1}{\theta(t)}\right)\right),\end{split}

which gives

\begin{split}\ln\left(\frac{1}{\theta(t)}\right)&\geq e^{-Ct}\ln\left(\frac{1}{\theta_{0}}\right)+(e^{-Ct}-1)\ln\left(\frac{1}{r_{0}}\right)\geq(1-Ct)\ln\left(\frac{1}{\theta_{0}}\right)-Ct\ln\left(\frac{1}{r_{0}}\right)\\
&\geq(1-Ct-\frac{Ct}{\beta})\ln\left(\frac{1}{\theta_{0}}\right),\end{split}

where in the last step we have used that
\theta_{0}<r_{0}^{\beta}
. Therefore we can guarantee that for all
t\leq t_{0}
and
r_{0}<\delta_{0}
,

\begin{split}\theta(t)\leq\theta_{0}^{1-Ct(1+\frac{1}{\beta})}<r_{0}^{\beta(1-Ct(1+\frac{1}{\beta}))}<\delta_{0}^{\frac{\beta}{2}(1-Ct(1+\frac{1}{\beta}))}<\frac{\pi}{20}\end{split}

by taking
t_{0},\delta_{0}
smaller if necessary. This gives the desired contradiction. The proof that (iii) is impossible can be done similarly.

Let us follow the trajectory of the curve
\{(r_{0},\frac{\pi}{2}-r_{0}^{\beta})_{*}:0<r_{0}<\delta_{0}^{4}\}
, on which
\omega_{0}\equiv 1
. Here and in the following, let us use the polar coordinates with notation
(r,\theta)_{*}=(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta)
. For convenience, let us define

(r(t),{\theta}(t))_{*}:=\Phi(t,(r_{0},\frac{\pi}{2}-r_{0}^{\beta})_{*}).

We can assume that on
[0,t_{0}]
,
r(t)<\delta_{0}^{2}
for any
r_{0}<\delta_{0}^{4}
. Moreover, on the diagonal
\{(a,a):a<{\delta_{0}^{2}}\}
, we may compute that

\begin{split}I(t,(a,a))&=\frac{4}{\pi}\int_{[2a,1]\times[2a,1]}\frac{y_{1}y_{2}}{|y|^{4}}\omega(t,y)dy\geq\frac{4}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{A}\cap([{2\delta_{0}^{2}},1]\times[{2\delta_{0}^{2}},1])}\frac{y_{1}y_{2}}{|y|^{4}}dy\\
&\geq c\int_{2\delta_{0}^{2}}^{\delta_{0}}\int_{2\delta_{0}^{2}}^{y_{1}}\frac{y_{2}}{|y_{1}|^{3}}dy_{2}dy_{1}\geq c\ln\frac{1}{\delta_{0}}-C,\end{split}

with some constants
c,C>0
, using that
\mathbb{A}\cap([{2\delta_{0}^{2}},1]\times[{2\delta_{0}^{2}},1])
contains the region
\{(y_{1},y_{2}):2\delta_{0}^{2}<y_{1}<\delta_{0},2\delta_{0}^{2}<y_{2}<y_{1}\}
. Hence, for
\delta_{0}>0
sufficiently small, the term
I(t,(a,a))
can “dominate”
B_{j}(t,(a,a))
for
j=1,2
in (
3.2
). To be precise, taking
\delta_{0}>0
smaller if necessary, it follows from the error estimate (
3.4
) with
x=(a,a)
that

\begin{split}|B_{j}(t,(a,a))|\leq\frac{1}{10}I(t,(a,a)),\qquad j=1,2.\end{split}

This implies that the velocity is pointing northwest on the diagonal, which guarantees in particular that
{\theta}(t)>\frac{\pi}{4}
. Now, along the trajectory
(r(t),\theta(t))_{*}
, we compute using (
3.2
) that

\begin{split}\dot{\theta}(t)=\frac{u^{\theta}(t,(r(t),\theta(t))_{*})}{|r(t)|}&=\frac{1}{2}\sin(2\theta(t))\left(2I+B_{1}+B_{2}\right)\\
&\geq\sin(2\theta(t))\left(I(t,(r(t),\theta(t))_{*})-C(1+\ln\frac{1}{\frac{\pi}{2}-\theta(t)})\right).\end{split}

Introducing
\bar{\theta}(t)=\frac{\pi}{2}-\theta(t)
, the above inequality becomes

\begin{split}\dot{\bar{\theta}}(t)\leq-2\bar{\theta}(t)\left({I(t,(r(t),\theta(t))_{*})}-C(1+\ln\frac{1}{\bar{\theta}(t)})\right).\end{split}

(3.6)

We now estimate
I(t,(r(t),\theta(t))_{*})
from below, using
\omega(t)\equiv 1
on
\mathbb{A}
. Note that

\begin{split}\mathbb{A}\cap([{2r(t)\cos(\theta(t))},1]\times[{2r(t)\sin(\theta(t))},1])\supset\{(y_{1},y_{2}):3r(t)<y_{1}<\delta_{0},3r(t)<y_{2}<y_{1}\}\end{split}

and using
r(t)\leq r_{0}^{1-ct}
,

\begin{split}I(t,(r(t),\theta(t))_{*})\geq c(1-ct)\ln\left(\frac{1}{r_{0}}\right)=\frac{c}{\beta}(1-ct)\ln\left(\frac{1}{\bar{\theta}_{0}}\right)\end{split}

where the constant
c>0
depends on
\delta_{0}
. Hence, as long as we have

\begin{split}\frac{c}{\beta}(1-ct)\ln\left(\frac{1}{\bar{\theta}_{0}}\right)>2C(1+\ln\frac{1}{\bar{\theta}(t)})\end{split}

(3.7)

where
C>0
is the constant from (
3.6
), we have

\begin{split}\dot{\bar{\theta}}(t)\leq-\frac{c}{\beta}{(1-ct)}\bar{\theta}(t)\ln\frac{1}{\bar{\theta}_{0}}\end{split}

for
t\leq t_{0}
by taking
t_{0}
smaller and therefore

\begin{split}\bar{\theta}(t)\leq\bar{\theta}_{0}^{1+\frac{ct}{\beta}}=r_{0}^{\beta+ct}\leq(r(t))^{(1-ct)(\beta+ct)}.\end{split}

Note that for
\beta>0
sufficiently small, (
3.7
) is satisfied for
t\in[0,t_{0}]
, again by taking
t_{0}
smaller to satisfy
ct\leq ct_{0}<\frac{1}{8}
, if necessary. Moreover,
(1-ct)(\beta+ct)\geq\beta+c_{0}t
with
c_{0}=\frac{c}{8}
if
0<\beta<\frac{1}{2}
and
ct_{0}<\frac{1}{8}
. Recall that

\begin{split}p_{0}=1+\frac{1}{1+\beta}>p^{*}\end{split}

and by taking
2>p^{*}
closer to 2, we can guarantee (
3.7
). Recalling that
\omega(t,\cdot)\equiv 1
on
(r(t),\frac{\pi}{2}-\bar{\theta}(t))
and applying Lemma
2.4
gives that

\begin{split}\omega(t)\notin W^{1,q(t)}(\mathbb{T}^{2}),\quad q(t)=1+\frac{1}{1+\beta+c_{0}t}.\end{split}

This finishes the proof.

3.3
Discussions

Let us close the paper with commenting on related issues that we have not touched upon so far.

Loss of regularity with
H^{1}
initial vorticity.

It is not clear to us whether it is possible for
\omega_{0}\in H^{1}\cap L^{\infty}
to lose
W^{1,p}
regularity with time. Let us briefly discuss the main difficulty. Note that as a consequence of Lemma
2.4
,
\omega_{0}\notin H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
if
f
takes on different constant values along two half-lines emanating from the origin. That is, insisting on the odd-odd scenario forces the vorticity to vanish near the origin. A natural choice, which was used in
[
11
]
is to take roughly
\omega_{0}(r,\theta)\sim(\ln\frac{1}{r})^{-\gamma}\sin(2\theta)
. Then one can check that the corresponding velocity gradient satisfies
|\nabla u_{0}(r,\theta)|\sim(\ln\frac{1}{r})^{1-\gamma}
, and even the passive transport with
u_{0}
is not strong enough to remove the vorticity from
W^{1,p}
with any
p<2
.
After the completion of this work, we have learned about an interesting recent paper
[
7
]
which proves that (see Theorem 3.1 therein) for
\omega_{0}\in H^{1}\cap C^{0}
, we have
\omega(t)\in W^{1,p}\cap W^{\alpha,2}
for any
p<2
and
\alpha<1
. That is, continuity (opposed to mere boundedness) of the vorticity makes the situation completely different.

Loss of regularity in negative Hölder spaces.

Another natural question to ask is whether there exists initial vorticity
\omega_{0}\in C^{-\alpha}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
with a solution
\omega(t)\notin C^{-\alpha}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
for
t>0
. There are serious essential difficulties in proving such a statement; now the initial vorticity is necessarily unbounded and the uniqueness of solution is not guaranteed (see very recent progress in
[
23
,
24
,
6
,
8
,
14
]
). On the other hand, any
L^{p,q}
norms of the vorticity is preserved for any solution, which makes it hard to lose Hölder regularity.

Loss of regularity for active scalars.

It will be an interesting problem to extend loss of regularity to active scalar equations, most notably to the case of SQG (surface quasi-geostrophic) equations. In this case, an analogue of Yudovich theorem is not available and therefore one should develop a new strategy.
