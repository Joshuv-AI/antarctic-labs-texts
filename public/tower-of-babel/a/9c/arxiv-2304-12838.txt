1.
Introduction

Let
{\mathbb{D}}
be the open unit disc in the complex plane
{\mathbb{C}}
, let
{\mathbb{T}}=\partial{\mathbb{D}}
be the unit circle, and denote by

\partial_{z}=\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{x}-i\partial_{y})\quad\quad\text{and}\quad\quad\partial_{\overline{z}}=\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{x}+i\partial_{y}).

1.1.
({\alpha},{\beta})
-harmonic functions

Consider the family of second order partial differential operators on
{\mathbb{D}}
of the form

\Delta_{{\alpha},{\beta}}:=(1-|z|^{2})\left((1-|z|^{2})\partial_{z}\partial_{\overline{z}}+{\alpha}z\partial_{z}+{\beta}\,\overline{z}\partial_{\overline{z}}-{\alpha}{\beta}\right),

where
{\alpha},{\beta}
are real parameters. These operators have been introduced and studied in higher dimensions by Geller
[
10
]
and Ahern et al.
[
1
,
2
]
and recently investigated in the planar case in
[
15
]
.

Of particular interest are the solutions of the associated homogeneous equation in
{\mathbb{D}}
:

\Delta_{{\alpha},{\beta}}\,u=0.

(1.1)

We say that a function
u
is
({\alpha},{\beta})
-harmonic if
u
is twice continuously differentiable in
{\mathbb{D}}
and
\Delta_{{\alpha},{\beta}}u=0
. We remark if
u
is
({\alpha},{\beta})
-harmonic, then
\overline{u}
is
({\beta},{\alpha})
-harmonic.

(0,{\alpha})
-harmonic functions are called
\alpha
-harmonic functions.

An interesting example of an
({\alpha},{\beta})
-harmonic function is
K_{{\alpha},{\beta}}
defined by

K_{{\alpha},{\beta}}(z)=\frac{(1-|z|^{2})^{{\alpha}+{\beta}+1}}{(1-z)^{{\alpha}+1}(1-\overline{z})^{{\beta}+1}}.

For
{\alpha},{\beta}\in{\mathbb{R}}\backslash{\mathbb{Z}}^{-}
such that
{\alpha}+{\beta}>-1
, the
({\alpha},{\beta})-
harmonic Poisson kernel is defined by

P_{{\alpha},{\beta}}(z)=c_{{\alpha},{\beta}}\,\frac{(1-|z|^{2})^{{\alpha}+{\beta}+1}}{(1-z)^{{\alpha}+1}(1-\overline{z})^{{\beta}+1}},\quad c_{{\alpha},{\beta}}=\frac{\Gamma({\alpha}+1)\Gamma({\beta}+1)}{\Gamma({\alpha}+{\beta}+1)},

(1.2)

where
\Gamma
is the Gamma function.

The
({\alpha},{\beta})-
harmonic Poisson integral of
f\in L^{1}({\mathbb{T}})
is defined by

P_{{\alpha},{\beta}}[f](z):=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}P_{{\alpha},{\beta}}(ze^{-i\theta})f(e^{i\theta})d\theta,\quad z\in{\mathbb{D}}.

Let

M_{{\alpha},{\beta}}(z):=P_{{\alpha},{\beta}}[1](z)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}P_{{\alpha},{\beta}}(ze^{-i\theta})\,d\theta.

Then
M_{{\alpha},{\beta}}
is a radial function and

M_{{\alpha},{\beta}}(z)=c_{{\alpha},{\beta}}F(-{\alpha},-{\beta};1;|z|^{2}),

where
F
is the Gaussian hypergeometric function. The constant
c_{{\alpha},{\beta}}
is chosen such that
\lim_{|z|\to 1}M_{{\alpha},{\beta}}(z)=1
. We should point out that in the case
{\alpha}=0
or
{\beta}=0
, the function
M_{{\alpha},{\beta}}
is constant and
M_{{\alpha},{\beta}}(z)=1
. For more properties and Hölder continuity of
({\alpha},{\beta})
-harmonic functions, see
[
1
,
5
,
14
,
15
,
16
]
.

Let us recall the Gaussian hypergeometric function defined by

F(a,b;c;z)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{(a)_{n}(b)_{n}}{(c)_{n}}\frac{z^{n}}{n!},\ \ \ \ z\in{\mathbb{D}},

for
a,b,c\in{\mathbb{C}}
such that
c\neq-1,-2,...
where

(a)_{n}=\begin{cases}1&\mbox{for}\ \ n=0,\\
a(a+1)\ldots(a+n-1)&\mbox{for}\ \ n=1,2,3,...\par\end{cases}

which are called Pochhammer symbols.

We list few properties, see for instance (
[
3
]
, Chapter 2)

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{x\rightarrow 1}F(a,b;c;x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma(c)\Gamma(c-a-b)}{\Gamma(c-a)\Gamma(c-b)},\ \ \ \ \ \text{if}\ \ \ c-a-b>0,

(1.3)

\displaystyle F(a,b;c;x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-x)^{c-a-b}F(c-a,c-b;c;x),

(1.4)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}F(a,b;c;x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{ab}{c}F(1+a,1+b;1+c;x),

(1.5)

\displaystyle F(a,b;a+b;x)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle-\frac{\Gamma(a+b)}{\Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)}\log(1-x)\quad(x\to 1^{-}).

(1.6)

The asymptotic relation (
1.6
) is due to Gauss, and its refined form is due to Ramanujan
[
4
, p. 71]
.

Also, we consider the associated Dirichlet boundary value problem of functions

, satisfying the equation (

\begin{cases}\Delta_{{\alpha},{\beta}}\,u&=0\quad\text{in}\quad{\mathbb{D}},\\

The function

Let

1.2.
Hardy type spaces

For
p\in(0,\infty]
,
the generalized Hardy space

H_{G}^{p}({\mathbb{D}})
consists of all measurable functions
u
from
{\mathbb{D}}
to
{\mathbb{C}}
such that
M_{p}(r,u)
exists for all
r\in(0,1)
, and
\|u\|_{p}<\infty
, where

M_{p}(r,u)=\bigg(\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}|u(re^{i\theta})|^{p}d\theta\bigg)^{\frac{1}{p}}

and

\|u\|_{p}=\begin{cases}\sup\{M_{p}(r,u):0<r<1\}&\mbox{if}\ \ p\in(0,\infty)\\
\sup\{|u(z)|:z\in{\mathbb{D}}\}&\mbox{if}\ \ p=\infty.\end{cases}

Obviously,
H^{p}_{G}({\mathbb{D}})\subset L^{p}({\mathbb{D}})
for
each
p\in(0,\infty]
. We denote by
h_{{\alpha},{\beta}}^{p}({\mathbb{D}})
the corresponding Hardy space for
({\alpha},{\beta})
-harmonic functions.
The classical
Hardy space
H^{p}(\mathbb{D})
(resp.
h^{p}(\mathbb{D})
) is the set of all elements of
H_{G}^{p}(\mathbb{D})
which are analytic (resp. harmonic) on
{\mathbb{D}}
. (cf.
[
8
,
9
]
).

The Hardy theory of
({\alpha},{\beta})
-harmonic functions is studied in detail in
[
1
]
. The authors proved the following

Theorem C.

[
1
]

Let
u
be an
({\alpha},{\beta})
-harmonic function, and assume
{\alpha}+{\beta}>-1
. Then :

(i)

u=P_{{\alpha},{\beta}}[f]
for some
f\in L^{p}({\mathbb{T}})
,
1<p\leq\infty
if and only if
\|u\|_{p}<+\infty
.

(ii)

u=P_{{\alpha},{\beta}}[\mu]
for some measure
\mu
if and only if
\|u\|_{1}<+\infty
.

1.3.
Lebesgue spaces

Denote by
L^{p}({\mathbb{T}})
where
p\in[1,\infty]
the space of all measurable functions
f
of
{\mathbb{T}}
into
{\mathbb{C}}
with

\|f\|_{L^{p}}=\begin{cases}\bigg(\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}|f(e^{i\theta})|^{p}d\theta\bigg)^{\frac{1}{p}}&\mbox{if}\ \ p\in[1,\infty)\\
\text{ess}\sup\{|f(e^{i\theta})|:\theta\in[0,2\pi)\}&\mbox{if}\ \ p=\infty.\end{cases}

It is well-known that if
f
is absolutely continuous, then it is of bounded variation. This implies that for almost all
e^{i\theta}\in{\mathbb{T}}
, the derivative
\dot{f}(e^{i\theta})
exists and integrable on
(0,2\pi)
, where

\dot{f}(e^{i\theta}):=\frac{d}{d\theta}f(e^{i\theta}).

1.4.
Harmonic conjugates and M. Riesz theorem

If
u
is a harmonic function on
{\mathbb{D}}
given by

u(re^{i\theta})=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}c_{n}r^{|n|}e^{in\theta},

then its harmonic conjugate is defined by

\tilde{u}(re^{i\theta}):=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}m_{n}c_{n}r^{|n|}e^{in\theta},

where
m_{n}=-i\,{\rm sign}\,n
; in particular
\tilde{u}(0)=0
. In addition, we have

u(z)+i\tilde{u}(z)=-u(0)+2\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}z^{n}.

(1.12)

The expression
\sum_{n\geq 0}c_{n}z^{n}
is called the
Riesz projection
of
u
, and denoted by
P_{+}u
i.e.,

P_{+}:h({\mathbb{D}})\to H({\mathbb{D}})

is defined by

P_{+}(u)(z):=\sum_{n\geq 0}c_{n}z^{n}.

Thus Eq. (
1.12
) can be rewritten as

u+i\tilde{u}=-u(0)+2P_{+}(u).

It is natural to consider the following question: if
u
is harmonic in
{\mathbb{D}}
and
u\in h^{p}({\mathbb{D}})
,
1\leq p\leq\infty
, does
P_{+}(u)\in H^{p}({\mathbb{D}})
?

The question has an affirmative answer for all
1<p<\infty
. This is the content of the famous theorem proved by M. Riesz
[
18
]
, see also Rudin
[
19
, Theorem 17.26]
.

Theorem D.

(M. Riesz) For
1<p<\infty
, the Riesz projection
P_{+}

P_{+}:h^{p}({\mathbb{D}})\to H^{p}({\mathbb{D}})

is a bounded operator, i.e.,
there is a constant
A_{p}
such that

\|P_{+}(u)\|_{p}\leq A_{p}\|u\|_{p}

for any
\ f\in h^{p}({\mathbb{D}})
.

It turns out that the Riesz theorem is not true for
p=1
and
p=\infty
.

In this paper, we consider the following :

Question:
Under what conditions on the boundary function
f
ensure that the partial derivatives of its
({\alpha},{\beta})
-harmonic extension

Theorem 2.1
.

Assume that

Proof.
By assumptions, we have

Let

\partial_{\theta}K_{{\alpha},{\beta}}(ze^{-it})=-\partial_{t}K_{{\alpha},{\beta}}(ze^{-it}).

An integration by parts leads to

\partial_{\theta}u(z)=P_{{\alpha},{\beta}}[\dot{f}](z),

By Jensen’s inequality, we get

For

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}(1-|z|^{2})^{{\alpha}+{\beta}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{{\beta}\overline{z}f(e^{it})}{(1-ze^{-it})^{{\alpha}+1}(1-\overline{z}e^{it})^{{\beta}+1}}\,dt.

For the converse, we prove the following

Theorem 2.5
.

{\alpha}

\partial_{z}u_{-n}(z)=c_{-n}\frac{{\alpha}(n-{\alpha})}{n+1}F(n-{\alpha}+1,{\alpha}+1;n+2;|z|^{2})\overline{z}^{n+1},

and by (
1.6
), we have

F(n-{\alpha}+1,{\alpha}+1;n+2;x)\approx\frac{\Gamma(n+2)}{\Gamma(n-{\alpha}+1)\Gamma({\alpha}+1)}\log(1-x),\quad(x\to 1^{-}).

(2.20)

As
{\alpha}
is not an integer, we deduce that the limit of the hypergeometric function in (
2.20
) is
\infty
as
x\to 1
, therefore
\partial_{z}u_{-n}
cannot be bounded unless
c_{-n}=0
, for
n\geq 1
.

Moreover, according to (
2.18
), the partial derivative with respect to
z
of
u_{n}
is given by

\displaystyle\partial_{z}u_{n}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{n}\frac{(-{\alpha})(n+{\alpha})}{n+1}F(n+{\alpha}+1,1-{\alpha};n+2;|z|^{2})\overline{z}z^{n}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle nc_{n}F(-{\alpha},n+{\alpha};n+1,|z|^{2})z^{n-1}.

Remark that the function
F(-{\alpha},n+{\alpha};n+1;|z|^{2})
has a finite limit as
|z|
goes to
1
, therefore it is bounded on
{\mathbb{D}}
. In addition notice that, as in (
2.20
),
\lim_{|z|\to 1}F(n+{\alpha}+1,1-{\alpha};n+2;|z|^{2})=\infty
.
Therefore
\partial_{z}u_{n}
is bounded unless
c_{n}=0
, for
n\geq 0
. Finally, we conclude that
u=0
.
∎

Finally, given
u=P_{{\alpha},{\beta}}[f]
a quasi-regular
({\alpha},{\beta})
-harmonic function with
\dot{f}\in L^{p}({\mathbb{T}})
, we prove that both
\partial_{z}u
and
\partial_{\overline{z}}u
are in
H_{G}^{p}({\mathbb{D}})
for

.

Recall that a mapping
u:{\mathbb{D}}\to{\mathbb{C}}
is said to be
K-
quasi-regular,
if

|\partial_{z}u(z)|+|\partial_{\overline{z}}u(z)|\leq K(|\partial_{z}u(z)|-|\partial_{\overline{z}}u(z)|),

on
{\mathbb{D}}
, where
K\in[1,\infty)
is a constant.

Theorem 2.7
.

Suppose
1\leq p\leq\infty
,
{\alpha},{\beta}\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus{\mathbb{Z}}^{-}
such that
{\alpha}+{\beta}>-1
and
u=P_{{\alpha},{\beta}}[f]
is an
({\alpha},{\beta})
-harmonic quasi-regular mapping of

{\mathbb{D}}
with the boundary function
f
is absolutely continuous and satisfies
\dot{f}\in L^{p}({\mathbb{T}})

Then
\partial_{z}u
and
\partial_{\overline{z}}u
are in
H_{G}^{p}({\mathbb{D}})
.

Proof.
Let
z=re^{i\theta}
, by using the identity (
2.1
) and the inequality (
2.2
), we get

\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\big(|z\partial_{z}u(re^{i\theta})|-|\overline{z}\partial_{\overline{z}}u(re^{i\theta})|\big)^{p}d\theta\leq\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}|\partial_{\theta}u(re^{i\theta})|^{p}d\theta\leq(|c_{{\alpha},{\beta}}|\,c_{{\alpha}+{\beta}+1})^{p}\|\dot{f}\|^{p}_{L^{p}},

where
1\leq p<\infty.
As
u
is quasi-regular, it follows that there exists a constant
K\geq 1
, such that

|\partial_{z}u(z)|+|\partial_{\overline{z}}u(z)|\leq K\big(|\partial_{z}u(z)|-|\partial_{\overline{z}}u(z)|\big).

Hence

\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\big(|z\partial_{z}u(re^{i\theta})|+|\overline{z}\partial_{\overline{z}}u(re^{i\theta})|\big)^{p}d\theta\leq(K|c_{{\alpha},{\beta}}|c_{{\alpha}+{\beta}+1})^{p}\|\dot{f}\|^{p}_{L^{p}},

it yields that

\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}|z\partial_{z}u(re^{i\theta})|^{p}d\theta\leq(K|c_{{\alpha},{\beta}}|c_{{\alpha}+{\beta}+1})^{p}\|\dot{f}\|^{p}_{L^{p}}

and

\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}|\overline{z}\partial_{\overline{z}}u(re^{i\theta})|^{p}d\theta\leq\bigg(\frac{K-1}{2}|c_{{\alpha},{\beta}}|c_{{\alpha}+{\beta}+1}\bigg)^{p}\|\dot{f}\|^{p}_{L^{p}}.

Thus

M_{p}(r,z\partial_{z}u)\leq K|c_{{\alpha},{\beta}}|c_{{\alpha}+{\beta}+1}\|\dot{f}\|_{L^{p}}\quad\quad\text{and}\quad\quad M_{p}(r,\overline{z}\partial_{\overline{z}}u)\leq\frac{K-1}{2}c_{{\alpha}+{\beta}+1}|c_{{\alpha},{\beta}}|\|\dot{f}\|_{L^{p}}.

Hence
\partial_{z}u
and
\partial_{\overline{z}}u
are in
H_{G}^{p}({\mathbb{D}})
.
∎

3.

-harmonic functions

Let
{\alpha}\in{\mathbb{R}}
and denote by

\omega_{\alpha}(z)=(1-|z|^{2})^{\alpha};\quad z\in{\mathbb{D}},

L_{\alpha}=\partial_{z}\omega_{{\alpha}}^{-1}\partial_{\overline{z}}\quad\mbox{ and }L_{\alpha}^{*}=\partial_{\overline{z}}\omega_{{\alpha}}^{-1}\partial_{z}\mbox{ in }{\mathbb{D}}.

One can check that

Thus

T_{\alpha}=(1-|z|^{2})^{-{\alpha}-2}\Delta_{{\alpha}/2,{\alpha}/2}.

For
{\alpha}>-1
, we denote by

Recall that a function

. Remark that

({\alpha}/2,{\alpha}/2)

2.2
and Theorem
2.3
, we obtain
