Abstract
We advocate a numerically reliable and accurate approach for practical parameter identifiability analysis: Applying column subset selection (CSS) to the sensitivity matrix, instead of computing an eigenvalue decomposition of the Fischer information matrix. Identifiability analysis via CSS has three advantages: (i) It quantifies reliability of the subsets of parameters selected as identifiable and unidentifiable. (ii) It establishes criteria for comparing the accuracy of different algorithms. (iii) The implementations are numerically more accurate and reliable than eigenvalue methods applied to the Fischer matrix, yet without an increase in computational cost. The effectiveness of the CSS methods is illustrated with extensive numerical experiments on sensitivity matrices from six physical models, as well as on adversarial synthetic matrices. Among the CSS methods, we recommend an implementation based on the strong rank-revealing QR algorithm because of its rigorous accuracy guarantees for both identifiable and non-identifiable parameters.

Introduction

In data-driven mathematical modeling, the ability to reliably estimate model parameters depends on the set of available observations, the scope of system responses for which such observations are available, the inherent mathematical structure of the model, and the parameter estimation method.
Identifiability analysis evaluates the ability to accurately estimate each parameter in a model and, in some cases, quantifies the extent to which this estimate is reliable.
It has wide-ranging implications for a variety of applications, including analysis of disease and epidemiology models to guide treatment regimes, physiologically-based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) and quantitative system pharmacology (QSP) models for drug development, and coupled multi-physics models for next-generation nuclear power plant design. In particular, identifiability analysis can be more challenging, yet also have greater impact, in applications where the number of model variables and parameters is significantly greater than the number of responses with available data.

Practical identifiability analysis refers to the partitioning of parameters in a mathematical model into two groups:

identifiable
parameters that can be reliably estimated from data and those that cannot, termed
unidentifiable
.
At the heart of many practical identifiability methods is the sensitivity matrix
\boldsymbol{S}
, whose columns
represent model parameters and whose rows
represent observations (data) for a quantity of interest.
A common approach
extracts identifiable and unidentifiable
parameters from eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the Fischer information matrix
\boldsymbol{S}^{T}\boldsymbol{S}
.
However, the sensitivity matrix
\boldsymbol{S}
is often ill-conditioned,
that is, sensitive to small perturbations, so that the explicit
formation of the cross product
\boldsymbol{S}^{T}\boldsymbol{S}
can
inflict a serious loss of accuracy.

We apply instead column subset selection (CSS)
to the sensitivity matrix
\boldsymbol{S}
, which has the same
computational complexity as eigenvalue methods on the Fischer
matrix
\boldsymbol{S}^{T}\boldsymbol{S}
.
We derive bounds that show the
superior accuracy of CSS, and corroborate
this with extensive numerical experiments on a
variety of model-based and adversarial synthetic matrices.
The higher accuracy of the CSS methods produces a
more reliable distinction between identifiable and unidentifiable parameters, as illustrated by their highly consistent performance across across this suite of test matrices.
This is especially critical when the identifiable parameters
inform subsequent investigations

3.2
Eigenvalue decomposition of the Fischer matrix

Existing methods
[

,

]

Since it is real symmetric positive semi-definite, its eigenvalue decomposition has the form

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{F}=\boldsymbol{V}\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{1}&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&\lambda_{p}\end{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{V}^{T},\qquad\lambda_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{p}\geq 0,

(1)

where

The eigenvector matrix

Its columns and elements are

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{V}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{v}_{1}&\cdots&\mathbf{v}_{p}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}v_{11}&\cdots&v_{1p}\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
v_{p1}&\cdots&v_{pp}\end{bmatrix}

In particular, the trailing column

is nonsingular.

The parameter with index

\boldsymbol{S}

The corresponding column of the Fischer matrix is

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{S}^{T}\boldsymbol{S}\boldsymbol{e}_{j}=\boldsymbol{F}\boldsymbol{e}_{j}=\boldsymbol{V}\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{1}&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&\lambda_{p}\end{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{V}^{T}\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\quad\text{where}\quad\boldsymbol{V}^{T}\boldsymbol{e}_{j}=\begin{bmatrix}v_{j1}\\
\vdots\\
v_{jp}\end{bmatrix},\qquad 1\leq j\leq p.

Thus, column

Caution

Explicit formation of the Fischer matrix

For instance

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{F}=\boldsymbol{S}^{T}\boldsymbol{S}=\boldsymbol{P}\,\boldsymbol{R}^{T}\boldsymbol{R}\,\boldsymbol{P}^{T}.

Since permutation matrices are orthogonal matrices,
the eigenvalues of
\boldsymbol{F}
are still equal to the squared singular values of
\boldsymbol{R}
, while each eigenvector of
\boldsymbol{R}^{T}\boldsymbol{R}

is a permutation of the corresponding eigenvector of
\boldsymbol{F}
.

Algorithms
1
–
4
start with
a preliminary QR decomposition
to reduce the dimension of the matrix. The
following remark shows that such a
preliminary decomposition is also effective prior
to an SVD computation, and the proofs in
Appendix
A
exploit this.

Remark 3.1
.

A preliminary QR decomposition
\boldsymbol{S}\boldsymbol{P}=\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{R}
is an efficient way
to compute the SVD of a
dense matrix
\boldsymbol{S}\in\real^{n\times p}
with
n\geq p

[
6
]
, since it
reduces the dimension for the SVD from that of a tall and skinny

n\times p
matrix down to that of a small square
p\times p
matrix
with the same dimension as the Fischer matrix
\boldsymbol{F}=\boldsymbol{S}^{T}\boldsymbol{S}
.

To see this, compute the pivoted QR factorization

\boldsymbol{S}\boldsymbol{P}=\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{R}
, and
let the upper triangular
\boldsymbol{R}
have an SVD

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{R}=\boldsymbol{U}_{r}\begin{bmatrix}\sigma_{1}&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&\sigma_{p}\end{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{V}^{T},

where
\boldsymbol{U}_{r}
and
\boldsymbol{V}\in\real^{p\times p}
are orthogonal matrices.
Then the SVD of the permuted sensitivity matrix
\boldsymbol{S}\boldsymbol{P}
is

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{S}\boldsymbol{P}=(\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{U}_{r})\begin{bmatrix}\sigma_{1}&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&\sigma_{p}\end{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{V}^{T},

where the left singular vector matrix
\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{U}_{r}\in\real^{n\times p}

has orthonormal columns.

This approach retains the asymptotic complexity of an SVD of
\boldsymbol{S}
,
but has the advantage of reducing the actual operation count,
and, in particular, reducing the problem dimension to
that of the Fischer matrix
\boldsymbol{F}=\boldsymbol{S}^{T}\boldsymbol{S}
.

3.6.3
Rank revealing QR decompositions

These pivoted QR decompositions are designed to ‘reveal’ the numerical rank of
a matrix
\boldsymbol{S}
that is rank deficient, or ill-conditioned with regard to left inversion

[
7
, section 2]
,
[
16
, 5.4.2]
.
[
18
, section 1.1]
.
Although there are numerous ways to compute such decompositions

[
7
,
18
]
, most share the same overall strategy.

Assume the sensitivity matrix has numerical
\rank(\boldsymbol{S})\approx k
,
where
1\leq k<p
. Partition
the pivoted QR decomposition (
7
) commensurately with the column partitioning (
3
),

\displaystyle\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{S}_{1}&\boldsymbol{S}_{2}\end{bmatrix}}_{\boldsymbol{S}\boldsymbol{P}}=\boldsymbol{S}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{P}_{1}&\boldsymbol{P}_{2}\end{bmatrix}}_{\boldsymbol{P}}=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}&\boldsymbol{Q}_{2}\end{bmatrix}}_{\boldsymbol{Q}}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{R}_{11}&\boldsymbol{R}_{12}\\
\boldsymbol{0}&\boldsymbol{R}_{22}\end{bmatrix}}_{\boldsymbol{R}},

(8)

with submatrices

\boldsymbol{P}_{1}\in\real^{p\times k}
,
\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}\in\real^{n\times k}
,

\boldsymbol{R}_{11}\in\real^{k\times k}
, and
\boldsymbol{R}_{22}\in\real^{(p-k)\times(p-k)}
.

Since
\boldsymbol{S}_{1}=\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}\boldsymbol{R}_{11}
,
the leading diagonal block
\boldsymbol{R}_{11}
has the same singular values as the matrix
\boldsymbol{S}_{1}
of
identifiable parameters

\displaystyle\sigma_{j}(\boldsymbol{S}_{1})=\sigma_{j}(\boldsymbol{R}_{11}),\qquad 1\leq j\leq k.

(9)

Similarly, since

\displaystyle\sigma_{j}((\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{S}_{1}\boldsymbol{S}_{1}^{\dagger})\boldsymbol{S})=\sigma_{j}((\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{S}_{1}\boldsymbol{S}_{1}^{\dagger})\boldsymbol{S}_{2})=\sigma_{j}(\boldsymbol{R}_{22}),\qquad 1\leq j\leq p-k,

(10)

the trailing diagonal block
\boldsymbol{R}_{22}
has the same non-zero
singular values as the residuals of the low-rank approximation
of
\range(\boldsymbol{S})
by
\range(\boldsymbol{S}_{1})
.

We call a QR decomposition (
8
) qualitatively ‘rank-revealing’
if it tries to optimize subselection criteria (
4
) or (
5
),
that is,

\displaystyle\sigma_{k}(\boldsymbol{R}_{11})\approx\sigma_{k}\qquad\text{or}\qquad\sigma_{1}(\boldsymbol{R}_{22})=\|\boldsymbol{R}_{22}\|_{2}\approx\sigma_{k+1}.

The first criterion tries to produce a well conditioned basis
\boldsymbol{S}_{1}=\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}\boldsymbol{R}_{11}
, and its
approximation
\boldsymbol{S}_{1}\boldsymbol{P}_{1}^{T}\approx\boldsymbol{S}
.
The second criterion aligns with the popular and robust
requirement

\|(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{S}_{1}\boldsymbol{S}_{1}^{\dagger})\boldsymbol{S}\|_{2}\approx\sigma_{k+1}

for low-rank approximations
[
12
]
.

Rank-revealing QR decompositions try to select
as identifiable
parameters

those columns of
\boldsymbol{S}
that
are the most linearly independent or

that approximate well the unidentifiable parameters.

Rigorous, stringent versions of the subselection criteria (
4
) and (
5
) are presented in

[
18
, Section 1.2]
and Theorem
4
.

6
Conclusion

We have presented a numerically accurate and reliable approach for practical parameter identifiability analysis in the context of physical models.

Our recommendation is to perform column subset selection (CSS)
directly on the sensitivity matrix
\boldsymbol{S}
, rather than detouring through the error-prone formation of the Fischer matrix
\boldsymbol{F}=\boldsymbol{S}^{T}\boldsymbol{S}
followed by an eigenvalue decomposition.

We applied the four CSS Algorithms
1
–
4
,
to a large variety of
practical and adversarial sensitivity matrices, and
they produced almost identical sets of identifiable
parameters
\boldsymbol{S}_{1}
with vastly improved condition numbers compared to
the condition number of the original matrix
\boldsymbol{S}
.

The superior accuracy of CSS is important
when identifiability analysis is part of a larger application.
In the context of inverse problems,
for instance, parameters designated as unidentifiable
may be fixed at a nominal value, for the purpose
of dimension reduction.
If this is an iterative process, reliable designation
of unidentifiable parameters is important.

Future research

We discuss several avenues for future research, many of which will necessitate
challenging modifications to Algorithms
1
–
4
.

1.

Efficient implementation of Algorithms
1
–
4
.

This includes the
choice of QR decompositions and data structure; as well as
fast updates, searches for magnitude-largest elements, and computation of
k
.

2.

Application of CSS methods to pharmacology.

Physiologically-based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) and quantitative systems pharmacology (QSP) models
exhibit moderate- to high-dimensional parameter spaces with highly nonlinear dependencies in their ODEs. For example, the minimal brain PBPK model in
[
2
]
has as many as 37 parameters in 16 coupled ODEs. This requires that unidentifiable parameters be determined and fixed
at nominal values at the very start –prior to optimization, sensitivity analysis, Bayesian inference for computing parameter distributions, and uncertainty propagation for constructing prediction intervals for QoIs.

Another difficulty is the optimization of criteria (
12
)–(
14
)
for larger QSP and PBPK models, as they
may depend strongly on the number
n
of observations,
the number
p
of parameters, and the number
k
of identifiable parameters.

3.

Global CSS algorithms.

Algorithms
1
–
4
are local in the sense that they operate on a single set of nominal parameter values. However, there is significant motivation in the PBPK and QSP communities to identify parameter dependencies for a
range
of admissible parameter values.
Although it
might be tempting to simply average the sensitivity values, in the manner of active subspace analysis
[
10
]
,
the highly nonlinear nature of parameter dependencies tends to rule out this approach.

4.

Mixed effects.

Another challenge in PBPK and QSP models are the regimes that combine
both, population and individual attributes. This necessitates mixed-effects models, which try to quantify the fixed-effects due to population parameters on the one hand; and the distributions for random effects associated with individuals on the other. A first step would be to incorporate
CSS methods into the initial parameter subset selection algorithm for mixed-effects models in
[
45
]
.

5.

Virtual populations.

A broad area of research in QSP models concerns the
generation of virtual populations for the purpose of safe and efficient drug development
[
1
]
. This requires the perturbation of QSP models about nominal values and characterization of sensitivities and uncertainties associated with model parameters. We anticipate that the CSS algorithms will play an increasing role in this growing field of virtual population generation and selection.

(Revealing a smallest singular value).

Let
\mathbf{v}
with
\|\mathbf{v}\|_{2}=1
be a right singular vector of

\boldsymbol{B}\in\real^{m\times m}
associated with a smallest
singular value
\sigma_{m}(\boldsymbol{B})
, so that
\|\boldsymbol{B}\mathbf{v}\|_{2}=\sigma_{m}(\boldsymbol{B})
.
Let
\boldsymbol{P}\in\real^{m\times m}
be a permutation that moves a magnitude-largest
element of
\mathbf{v}
to the bottom,
|(\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\mathbf{v})_{m}|=\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\infty}
.
If
\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{P}=\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{R}
is an unpivoted QR decomposition (
6
) of
\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{P}
,
then
the trailing diagonal element of the upper triangular matrix
\boldsymbol{R}
satisfies

\displaystyle\sigma_{m}(\boldsymbol{B})\leq|r_{mm}|\leq\sqrt{m}\,\sigma_{m}(\boldsymbol{B}).

Proof.

The lower bound follows from singular value interlacing (
15
).
As for the upper bound, the relation between the right singular
vector
\mathbf{v}
and a corresponding left singular vector
\mathbf{u}

with
\boldsymbol{B}\mathbf{v}=\sigma_{m}(\boldsymbol{B})\mathbf{u}
and
\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2}=1
implies

\displaystyle\sigma_{m}(\boldsymbol{B})\mathbf{u}=\boldsymbol{B}\mathbf{v}=(\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{P})\,(\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\mathbf{v})=\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{R}\,(\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\mathbf{v})=\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{R}\begin{bmatrix}*\\
(\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\mathbf{v})_{m}\end{bmatrix}

From this,
\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2}=1
, the unitary invariance
of the two-norm, and the upper triangular nature
of
\boldsymbol{R}
follows

\displaystyle\sigma_{m}(\boldsymbol{B})=\|\sigma_{m}(\boldsymbol{B})\mathbf{u}\|_{2}=\|\boldsymbol{R}(\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\mathbf{v})\|_{2}\geq|r_{mm}(\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\mathbf{v})_{m}|=|r_{mm}|\,\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\infty}\geq|r_{mm}|/\sqrt{m}.

The last inequality follows from the fact that
\mathbf{v}\in\real^{m}
has
unit two-norm
\|\mathbf{v}\|_{2}=1
, so at least one of its
m
elements must be sufficiently
large with
\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\infty}\geq 1/\sqrt{m}
.

Lemma 7
(Proof of Theorem
1
).

\boldsymbol{S}\in\real^{n\times p}

\boldsymbol{0}&\boldsymbol{R}_{22}\end{bmatrix},

,

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{W}^{-1}\|_{2}\leq 2^{p-k-1}.

Let

be computed by Algorithm
1
with
input
k
. The proof is an extension of Lemma

From the right singular vectors in Algorithm

\boldsymbol{Z}

The indexing of the partition is different than the one in (

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{R}^{(\ell)}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{R}_{11}^{(\ell)}&\boldsymbol{R}^{(\ell)}_{12}\\
\boldsymbol{0}&\boldsymbol{R}^{(\ell)}_{22}\end{bmatrix}\qquad\boldsymbol{R}^{(\ell)}_{11}\in\real^{\ell\times\ell},\quad\boldsymbol{R}^{(\ell)}_{22}\in\real^{(p-\ell)\times(p-\ell)},\qquad k+1\leq\ell\leq p.

In the statement of this lemma, the partitioning is

.

Let
\mathbf{v}^{(\ell)},\mathbf{u}^{(\ell)}\in\real^{\ell}
be right and left singular vectors associated
with a smallest singular value of
\boldsymbol{R}_{11}^{(\ell)}
,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{R}_{11}^{(\ell)}\mathbf{v}^{(\ell)}=\sigma_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{R}_{11}^{(\ell)})\,\mathbf{u}^{(\ell)},\qquad\|\mathbf{v}^{(\ell)}\|_{2}=\|\mathbf{u}^{(\ell)}\|_{2}=1,\qquad k+1\leq\ell\leq p.

Algorithm
1
has permuted the right singular vectors so that
a magnitude-largest element is at the bottom,

\displaystyle|\mathbf{v}^{(\ell)}_{\ell}|\geq 1/\sqrt{\ell}\quad\text{and}\quad|\mathbf{v}^{(\ell)}_{j}|\leq|\mathbf{v}^{(\ell)}_{\ell}|,\qquad 1\leq j<\ell,\quad k+1\leq\ell\leq p.

(20)

The trailing elements in singular vectors associated with larger-dimensional

), where permutations in the
(1,1)

(2,2)

Construct an upper trapezoidal matrix

\boldsymbol{Z}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{z}_{1}&\cdots&\boldsymbol{z}_{p-k}\end{bmatrix}\in\real^{p\times(p-k)}
,
whose columns are the right singular vectors

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{z}_{\ell-k}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{v}^{(\ell)}\\
\boldsymbol{0}_{p-\ell}\end{bmatrix},\qquad k+1\leq\ell\leq p.

Factor out the diagonal elements and focus on the trailing

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{Z}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{Z}_{1}\\
\boldsymbol{W}\end{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{D},\qquad\text{where}\qquad\boldsymbol{D}=\begin{bmatrix}v_{k+1}^{(k+1)}&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&v_{p}^{(p)}\end{bmatrix}\in\real^{(p-k)\times(p-k)}

From (

\displaystyle|w_{\ell\ell}|=1,\qquad|w_{\ell j}|\leq 1,\qquad 1\leq\ell\leq p-k,\quad j>\ell.

\|\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{Z}\|_{2}

\boldsymbol{S}\boldsymbol{P}=\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{R}

\boldsymbol{Q}^{T}
on the left and

\boldsymbol{Z}
on the right gives

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{Q}^{T}\boldsymbol{S}\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{Z}=\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{Z}\in\real^{p-k}.

The columns of
\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{Z}
are

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{z}_{\ell-k}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{R}_{11}^{(\ell)}\mathbf{v}^{(\ell)}\\
\boldsymbol{0}_{p-\ell}\end{bmatrix}=\sigma_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{R}_{11}^{(\ell)})\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{u}^{(\ell)}\\

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{z}_{\ell-k}\|_{2}=\sigma_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{R}_{11}^{(\ell)})\leq\sigma_{\ell},\qquad k+1\leq\ell\leq p.

16

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{Z}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{p-k}\,\max_{k+1\leq\ell\leq p}{\|\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{z}_{\ell-k}\|_{2}}\leq\sqrt{p-k}\,\max_{k+1\leq\ell\leq p}{\sigma_{\ell}}\leq\sqrt{p}\,\sigma_{k+1}.

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{Z}\|_{2}\geq\|\boldsymbol{R}_{22}\boldsymbol{W}\boldsymbol{D}\|_{2}\geq\frac{\|\boldsymbol{R}_{22}\|_{2}}{\|\boldsymbol{W}^{-1}\|_{2}\|\boldsymbol{D}^{-1}\|_{2}}\geq\frac{\|\boldsymbol{R}_{22}\|_{2}}{\sqrt{p}\,\|\boldsymbol{W}^{-1}\|_{2}}.

At last combine the above upper bound with the lower bound (

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{R}_{22}\|\leq p\,\|\boldsymbol{W}^{-1}\|_{2}\,\sigma_{k+1}.

The bound for

are classes of matrices for which it can essentially be tight

Appendix C
Dynamical systems for adversarial CSS matrices

Given an adversarial CSS matrix
\boldsymbol{S}\in\real^{n\times p}
with
n\geq p
, we construct a dynamical system whose sensitivity matrix is identical to
\boldsymbol{S}
.

Let
\boldsymbol{S}=\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{V}^{\top}
have
have a thin SVD as in (
2
) and distinguish
the columns of the singular vector matrices,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{U}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{u}_{1}&\ldots&\mathbf{u}_{p}\end{bmatrix}\in\real^{n\times p},\qquad\boldsymbol{V}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{v}_{1}&\ldots&\mathbf{v}_{p}\end{bmatrix}\in\real^{p\times p}.

Pick some vector
\mathbf{q}\in\real^{p}
,
we are going to construct a system of ODEs parameterized by
\mathbf{q}
.

To this end, let

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Lambda}\equiv\diag\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1}&\cdots&\lambda_{p}\end{pmatrix}\in\real^{p\times p}

be a diagonal matrix yet to be specified.
Denote by
\boldsymbol{x}(t)\in\real^{p}
the state vector
and by
\boldsymbol{y}(t)
the observation vector,
and combine everything
into the initial value problem

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{d\boldsymbol{x}}{dt}&=\>\mathbf{\Lambda}\boldsymbol{x},\qquad\boldsymbol{x}(0)=\boldsymbol{V}^{T}\mathbf{q},\\
\boldsymbol{y}(t)&=\>\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{x}(t)\end{split}

(36)

with solution
\boldsymbol{x}(t)=\exp(t\mathbf{\Lambda})\boldsymbol{V}^{T}\mathbf{q}
.
Since
\mathbf{\Lambda}
is diagonal, the observation equals

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{y}(t)=\boldsymbol{U}\exp(t\mathbf{\Lambda})\boldsymbol{V}^{T}\mathbf{q}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}{\mathbf{u}_{j}e^{t\lambda_{j}}\mathbf{v}_{j}^{T}\mathbf{q}}.

As in section
2.2.1
, differentiate
\boldsymbol{y}
with respect to
\mathbf{q}
, and then evaluate at time
t=\tau>0
. The
rows of the resulting sensitivity matrix equal

\frac{\partial y_{i}}{\partial\mathbf{q}}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}{u_{ij}\,e^{\tau\lambda_{j}}\,\mathbf{v}_{j}^{T}},\qquad 1\leq i\leq n.

Set
\sigma_{j}=e^{\tau\lambda_{j}}
so that
\lambda_{j}=\frac{1}{\tau}\ln{\sigma_{j}}
,
1\leq j\leq p
. Then the sensitivity matrix at time
t=\tau
is

\boldsymbol{S}(t;\mathbf{q})=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\mathbf{u}_{j}\sigma_{j}\,\mathbf{v}_{j}^{T}=\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{V}^{T}.

Therefore, the dynamical system (
36
)
has the desired sensitivity matrix
\boldsymbol{S}
at time
\tau
.
