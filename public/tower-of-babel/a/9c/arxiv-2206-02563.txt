Learning "best" kernels from data in Gaussian process regression. With application to aerodynamics Thanks: Corresponding author: É. Savin, ONERA–The French Aerospace Lab, 6 chemin de la Vauve aux Granges, FR-91123 Palaiseau cedex, France (Eric.Savin@onera.fr).
Jean-Luc Akian, Luc Bonnet, Houman Owhadi, Éric Savin

Abstract
. This paper introduces algorithms to select/design kernels in Gaussian process regression/kriging surrogate modeling techniques. We adopt the setting of kernel method solutions in ad hoc functional spaces, namely Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Spaces (RKHS), to solve the problem of approximating a regular target function given observations of it, i.e. supervised learning. A first class of algorithms is kernel flow, which was introduced in the context of classification in machine learning. It can be seen as a cross-validation procedure whereby a "best" kernel is selected such that the loss of accuracy incurred by removing some part of the dataset (typically half of it) is minimized. A second class of algorithms is called spectral kernel ridge regression, and aims at selecting a "best" kernel such that the norm of the function to be approximated is minimal in the associated RKHS. Within Mercer’s theorem framework, we obtain an explicit construction of that "best" kernel in terms of the main features of the target function. Both approaches of learning kernels from data are illustrated by numerical examples on synthetic test functions, and on a classical test case in turbulence modeling validation for transonic flows about a two-dimensional airfoil.



1.
Introduction

Cruise flight conditions of commercial aircraft are mostly transonic, such that the flow is locally supersonic due to the geometry of actual wing profiles. Acceleration of the flow on the profile upper surface induces a depression yielding the lift force. A discontinuity, or shock wave, arises if this depression is too sharp in order to balance the pressure gradient at the trailing edge between the upper and lower surfaces. Both the location and strength of the shock wave are responsible for a significant part of the drag force. These features are thus critical in view of optimizing a wing profile, for instance minimizing the drag force considering constant lift force. Mild alterations of profiles can alleviate these issues by smoothing out the discontinuity, hence increasing the lift force while decreasing the drag force.

Such complex aerodynamic design and analysis typically use high-fidelity computational fluid dynamics (CFD) tools for optimization or uncertainty quantification, considering in addition some uncertain operational, environmental, or manufacturing parameters. High-fidelity simulations are needed to detail the flow structures, while non-intrusive methods are further required when variable parameters have to be taken into account for sensitivity and robustness analyses. Due to their complexity, flow solvers are indeed preferably treated as black boxes computing output quantities of interest as functions of input parameters. In aerodynamic applications with CFD software, one single function evaluation can take up to several hours. It is then not conceivable to use this type of complex models for all function evaluations that may be needed to estimate an optimum or an average output, say. A middle ground has thus to be found. One way to solve this issue is to accept to evaluate the complex model at some sample points while using an approximation at the remaining points to mimic the behavior of that complex model. This approximation is called a metamodel, or a surrogate model. Obviously, its quality will strongly influence the trustworthiness of the obtained optimum or average. In that respect, one may wish the surrogate model to fulfill the following two properties:

•

It has to be cheap to evaluate in order to be able to possibly obtain tens of thousands of function evaluations in a reasonable time; typically, an evaluation should be less than 1 second;

•

It has to be as accurate as possible (given some metric) in order to be confident in these multiple evaluations.

3.
Kernel Flow algorithm

A challenging aspect of kernel methods is to determine which kernel

[

Early attempts to learn kernels from data can be found in geostatistics, for example, where the spatial correlation structures of data are described in terms of so-called variograms introduced by Matheron

4.
Spectral Kernel Ridge Regression algorithms

In this section, we adopt a different perspective to determine what is a “best” kernel. New algorithms that we call Spectral Kernel Ridge Regression (SKRR) are introduced in this respect. Recalling
Section

2.3
and the deterministic error bounds of
Equation

11

12

\left\|F\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}
and
\smash{\left\|F\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{K+\lambda}}}
apart from the local variance
\sigma^{2}(\mathbf{x})
which is, again, independent of the ground truth
F
. Therefore, we suggest to study the effect of the kernel
K
on the norm
\left\|F\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}
and in this way to find a “best” kernel by minimizing the latter with respect to
K
. That is, we aim to find the kernel

such that:

(18)

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:best_{m}in_{K}ers}K^{\star}=\operatornamewithlimits{arg\,min}\limits_{K}\left\|F\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}\,.

For that purpose, we resort to Mercer’s theorem and the spectral expansion of the integral operator associated to a Mercer kernel. These notions are summarized in the appendix
8.3

\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is compact.

4.1.
Minimizing the norm
\left\|F\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}
: the SKRR core

We assume that a family

of continuous functions on
\mathcal{X}
is given (these are the “features” in machine learning techniques, for example), which forms an orthonormal basis of

\mathcal{X}

\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle_{L^{2}}

where the eigenvalues
\{\sigma_{i}>0\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
are to be chosen such that, if

, its norm
\left\|F\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}
is minimal. By

53

(19)

\left\|F\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}^{2}=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{+\infty}\frac{{\left|F_{i}\right|{}^{2}}}{\sigma_{i}}\,,

where

,

, such that
\sum_{i}\left|F_{i}\right|{}^{2}<+\infty

(20)

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:min_{s}igma}\min\limits_{\{\sigma_{i}\}_{i}}\sum\limits_{i}\frac{\left|F_{i}\right|{}^{2}}{\sigma_{i}}\;\text{subjected to }\sum\limits_{i}\sigma_{i}=\kappa\,,

where

, one has:

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:lagrange}\begin{split}\frac{\partial}{\partial\sigma_{k}}\left[\sum\limits_{i}\frac{\left|F_{i}\right|{}^{2}}{\sigma^{\star}_{i}}+\nu\left(\sum\limits_{i}\sigma^{\star}_{i}-\kappa\right)\right]&=0\quad\text{ for }k=0,1,2,\dots\,,\\
\frac{\partial}{\partial\nu}\left[\sum\limits_{i}\frac{\left|F_{i}\right|{}^{2}}{\sigma^{\star}_{i}}+\nu\left(\sum\limits_{i}\sigma^{\star}_{i}-\kappa\right)\right]&=0\,.\end{split}

Then one finds that:

(21)

\nu=\frac{1}{\kappa^{2}}\left(\sum\limits_{i}\left|F_{i}\right|{}\right)^{2},\quad\sigma^{\star}_{i}=\frac{|{F_{i}}|}{\sqrt{\nu}},\quad i=0,1,2,\dots\,,

or:

(22)

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:finish_{l}agrange}\sigma^{\star}_{i}=\frac{\kappa|{F_{i}}|}{\sum\limits_{j}\left|F_{j}\right|{}},\quad i=0,1,2,\dots

In this way we built a Mercer kernel
K^{\star}=\sum_{i}\sigma^{\star}_{i}e_{i}\otimes e_{i}
which minimizes the norm
\left\|F\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}
. Practically, the sum extends up to a finite rank
R
. It remains to compute the expansion coefficients
\{F_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
(the "feature extraction" step, say): two approaches based on regression are outlined in the next section.
We note at this stage that although the family
\{e_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
may depend on the measure
\mu
, the RKHS
\mathcal{H}_{K}
associated to
K
does not; see for example
[
62
, Prop. 11.17]
and the comment after the proof of this proposition, and
Remark

2
in the appendix
8.3
.

4.2.
Computing the expansion coefficients

Given the orthonormal basis
\{e_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
of
L^{2}(\mathcal{X},\mu)
and
F\in L^{2}(\mathcal{X},\mu)
, one has thus the following expansions:

F(\mathbf{X}_{j})=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{+\infty}\left\langle F,e_{i}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}e_{i}(\mathbf{X}_{j})=Y_{j}\,,\quad j=1,\dots I\,,

because the functions
\{e_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
are continuous. Let
\mathcal{K}
be a set of indices such that
\#\mathcal{K}=R
and let the corresponding orthonormal family
\{e_{k}\}_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\subset\{e_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
in
L^{2}(\mathcal{X},\mu)
be the basis
\mathcal{B}^{R}
. Let
\bm{\Theta}
be the
I\times R
measurement matrix and
\mathbf{c}
be the expansion coefficients vector in
\mathbb{R}^{R}
such that:

(23)

\bm{\Theta}=\begin{bmatrix}e_{k_{1}}(\mathbf{X}_{1})&\cdots&e_{k_{R}}(\mathbf{X}_{1})\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
e_{k_{1}}(\mathbf{X}_{I})&\cdots&e_{k_{R}}(\mathbf{X}_{I})\end{bmatrix},\quad\mathbf{c}=\begin{pmatrix}c_{k_{1}}\\
\vdots\\
c_{k_{R}}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\left\langle F,e_{k_{1}}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}\\
\vdots\\
\left\langle F,e_{k_{R}}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}\end{pmatrix}\,;

then one arrives at the following
I\times R
system:

(24)

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:illposed}\mathbf{Y}=\bm{\Theta}\mathbf{c}+\bm{\eta}\,,

where
\bm{\eta}=(\eta_{1},\ldots\eta_{I})^{\textsf{T}}
is an error vector with
\left\|\bm{\eta}\right\|_{2}\leq\eta
accounting for the truncation of the ground truth function
F
on the orthonormal set of
R
vectors
\{e_{k}\}_{k\in\mathcal{K}}
, and possible noise.

4.2.1.
Least-squares regression

We first assume that
I\geq R
. Finding the expansion coefficients
\mathbf{c}
can be done through a regression approach formulated as a least-squares minimization problem, that is, solving the following problem:

(25)

\mathbf{c}^{\star}=\operatornamewithlimits{arg\,min}_{\mathbf{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{R}}\left(\bm{\Theta}\mathbf{h}-\mathbf{Y}\right)^{\textsf{T}}\left(\bm{\Theta}\mathbf{h}-\mathbf{Y}\right)\,.

This approach is detailed in
e.g.

[
33
]
and references therein. Here, we are more interested in the case where
I<R
or even
I\ll R
, namely when the number of possible “features”
R
is way more larger than the number of observations
I
of
F
. This is the topic of the following section.

4.2.2.
Sparse regression

We now assume that
I\ll R
, and that the “features” are actually chosen such that the ground truth function
F
is expected to be sparse or nearly sparse on this basis: many components of the vector
\mathbf{c}
of its expansion coefficients are negligible. Such expansion is known as compressible in the terminology of compressed sensing, or compressive sampling (CS)
[
10
,
12
,
23
]
. Thus one introduces the sparsity
S
defined by:

(26)

S=\#\left\{i;\left|c_{i}\right|>\delta\right\}\,,

where
\delta>0
is some tolerance, and assume that
S\ll R
. In other words, only a small number of vectors within the basis
\mathcal{B}^{R}
is relevant to reconstructing the ground truth function
F
without much loss, and this number is that sparsity
S
. Then
Equation

24
in this context may be solved by adaptative methods such as least angle regression as in
e.g.

[
4
]
, or by non-adaptive methods such as the following convex
\ell_{1}
-minimization known as Basis Pursuit Denoising (BPDN)
[
15
]
:

(27)

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:BPDN}\min\limits_{\mathbf{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{R}}\left\|\mathbf{h}\right\|_{1}\;\text{ subjected to}\;\left\|\bm{\Theta}\mathbf{h}-\mathbf{Y}\right\|_{2}\leq\eta\,,

where
\left\|\mathbf{h}\right\|_{p}=(\sum_{j=1}^{R}\left|h_{j}\right|^{p})^{\frac{1}{p}}
,
p>0
. BPDN is non-adapted because it identifies both the sparsity pattern, that is the order of the negligible components in the sought vector
\mathbf{c}
, and the leading components at the same time. This is clearly a desirable feature for practical industrial applications. Therefore this approach is favored in the subsequent numerical examples. The conditions on the measurement matrix
\bm{\Theta}
for which
Equation

27
yields a unique solution, and associated recovery bounds are analyzed in
e.g.

[
9
,
11
,
12
,
13
]
for the noiseless (
\eta=0
) and noisy (
\eta>0
) cases.

It should be noted that in practical applications, the sparsity of
F
is typically seen
a posteriori
and not
a priori
. If the orthonormal set of vectors
\left\{e_{k}\right\}_{k\in\mathcal{K}}
and
\eta
are well chosen,
Equation

27
will yield a sparse solution which approximates well the ground truth function
F
,
i.e.
a solution where only a few terms are non vanishing. In this paper, the selection of the value
\eta
is done arbitrarily but it can actually be chosen through cross-validation
[
5
,
24
,
90
]
, for example.

4.3.
Sparse SKRR algorithm

We propose the following algorithm that we coin Sparse Spectral Kernel Ridge Regression (SSKRR) which couples the sparse reconstruction by, say,
\ell_{1}
-minimization presented in the foregoing section, and the KRR approximation detailed in
Section

2.2
. The main idea of this algorithm is to minimize the RKHS norm of the ground truth function
F
with respect to the eigenvalues of a Mercer kernel, which are obtained by
Equation

22
where the expansion coefficients
\{F_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{K}}
in a finite basis
\mathcal{B}^{R}
are computed by
\ell_{1}
-minimization. The procedure is sketched in
Algorithm

1
. It is organized as follows. First, starting from an orthonormal set of
R
vectors
\mathcal{B}^{R}\equiv\{e_{k}\}_{k\in\mathcal{K}}
in
L^{2}(\mathcal{X},\mu)
,
I
observations of
F
, and the parameter
\eta
, the BPDN minimization of
Equation

27
is solved, yielding a solution
\mathbf{c}^{\star}
. If the set
\mathcal{B}^{R}
of “features” is well chosen, only a limited number of terms in the vector
\mathbf{c}^{\star}
is not close to zero. Then one builds the KRR approximation (
10
), which allows us to get a prediction of the ground truth function
F
at an unobserved location
\mathbf{x}
with a nugget
\lambda
and using the kernel obtained at the previous step. One of the main advantages of the proposed algorithm is that it provides the prediction variance
\sigma^{2}(\mathbf{x})
at the unobserved point
\mathbf{x}
, as in the Gaussian Process Regression framework.

Obviously, the algorithm will strongly depend on the performance of
\ell_{1}
-minimization to approximate the expansion coefficients
\{F_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{K}}
. Also, several remarks can be made about the parameter
\kappa
in
Equation

22
. The SKRR approximation at an unobserved point
\mathbf{x}
is:

G^{\star}_{{\lambda}}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{K}^{\star}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{X})\left(\mathbf{K}^{\star}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X})+\lambda\mathbf{I}_{I}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{Y}\,,

and using
Equation

22
one arrives at:

\displaystyle G^{\star}_{{\lambda}}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\frac{|{F_{k}}|}{\sum\limits_{j\in\mathcal{K}}|{F_{j}}|}e_{k}(\mathbf{x})\otimes e_{k}(\mathbf{X})\left(\sum\limits_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\frac{|{F_{k}}|}{\sum\limits_{j\in\mathcal{K}}|{F_{j}}|}e_{k}(\mathbf{X})\otimes e_{k}(\mathbf{X})+\frac{\lambda}{\kappa}\mathbf{I}_{I}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{Y}\,.

So, one can see that the SKRR approximation only depends on the ratio between the nugget
\lambda
and the parameter
\kappa
. Likewise for the prediction variance of
Equation

13
, one has:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sigma^{2}(\mathbf{x})&=K^{\star}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{K}^{\star}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{X})\left(\mathbf{K}^{\star}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X})+\lambda\mathbf{I}_{I}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{K}^{\star}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{x})\\
&=\kappa\Biggl[\sum\limits_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\frac{|{F_{k}}|}{\sum\limits_{j\in\mathcal{K}}|{F_{j}}|}e_{k}(\mathbf{x})\otimes e_{k}(\mathbf{x})-\sum\limits_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\frac{|{F_{k}}|}{\sum\limits_{j\in\mathcal{K}}|{F_{j}}|}e_{k}(\mathbf{x})\otimes e_{k}(\mathbf{X})\\
&\mathrel{\phantom{=}}\times\left(\sum\limits_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\frac{|{F_{k}}|}{\sum\limits_{j\in\mathcal{K}}|{F_{j}}|}e_{k}(\mathbf{X})\otimes e_{k}(\mathbf{X})+\frac{\lambda}{\kappa}\mathbf{I}_{I}\right)^{-1}\sum\limits_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\frac{|{F_{k}}|}{\sum\limits_{j\in\mathcal{K}}|{F_{j}}|}e_{k}(\mathbf{X})\otimes e_{k}(\mathbf{x})\Biggr]\,.\end{split}

The parameter
\kappa
fixing the trace of the integral operator with kernel
K^{\star}
can be understood as a scaling factor on the prediction variance. At last, when the nugget
\lambda
vanishes one arrives at:

(28)

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:SORS}G^{\star}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{K}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\frac{\left|F_{k}\right|}{\left|F_{j}\right|}\left\langle e_{j}(\mathbf{X}),e_{k}(\mathbf{X})\right\rangle_{I}\left\langle e_{j}(\mathbf{X}),\mathbf{Y}\right\rangle_{I}e_{k}(\mathbf{x})\,,

where
\left\langle e_{j}(\mathbf{X}),e_{k}(\mathbf{X})\right\rangle_{I}=\sum_{i=1}^{I}e_{j}(\mathbf{X}_{i})e_{k}(\mathbf{X}_{i})
and
\left\langle e_{j}(\mathbf{X}),\mathbf{Y}\right\rangle_{I}=\sum_{i=1}^{I}e_{j}(\mathbf{X}_{i})Y_{i}
.

Input:

Basis
\left\{e_{k}\right\}_{k\in\mathcal{K}}
,
I
observations of
F
, parameters
\eta
,
\lambda
and
\kappa
.

Output:

SSKRR approximation
G^{\star}_{{\lambda}}

.

Build the observation matrix

4.5.
Polynomial Chaos Expansion

The remaining question is: how to choose a basis
\{e_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
of
L^{2}(\mathcal{X},\mu)
? In this section, we focus on orthonormal polynomial bases. Such kind of representations are referred to as Polynomial Chaos (PC) expansions in the case where
\mu
is a Gaussian probability measure
[
30
,
91
]
. For more general probability measures, they are called generalized Polynomial Chaos (gPC) expansions
[
26
,
46
,
78
,
94
]
.
gPC expansions with non-Gaussian probability measures are considered in the synthetic numerical examples of
Section

5
and the aerodynamic example of
Section

6
below for comparisons with our proposed algorithms.
Besides, the use of polynomial bases with Gaussian process regression is illustrated in
e.g.

[
95
]
.
We also note that mixtures of models may be worth considering to improve the accuracy of surrogates, as in Polynomial Chaos-Kriging for example
[
73
]
. We leave that possibility to future works.

In the context of gPC expansions, the input variables
\mathbf{X}
with values in
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
are assumed to be mutually independent random variables with probability distribution
\mu
. One can then build a gPC surrogate model
G_{R}
of
F
by a standard
L^{2}
projection on a finite dimensional subspace of
L^{2}(\mathcal{X},\mu)
spanned by a truncated orthonormal family of
d
-variate polynomials up to total order
p
denoted by
\smash{\mathcal{B}^{R}}=\smash{\{\phi_{\mathbf{i}}\}_{\left\|\mathbf{i}\right\|_{1}\leq p}}
, where
R=\smash{\binom{p+d}{p}}
.
Here
\mathbf{i}=\smash{(i_{1},i_{2},\dots i_{d})}
is a multi-index in
\smash{\mathbb{N}^{d}}
and
\smash{\left\|\mathbf{i}\right\|_{1}}=\smash{\sum_{j=1}^{d}i_{j}}
. These
d
-variate polynomials read:

(31)

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:phi_{m}ulti}\phi_{\mathbf{i}}(\mathbf{x})=\prod\limits_{j=1}^{d}\phi_{i_{j}}^{(j)}(x_{j})\,,

where
\mathbf{x}=\smash{(x_{1},x_{2},\dots x_{d})}
, and
\smash{\{\phi^{(j)}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}}
are the univariate orthonormal polynomials with respect to the law of the
j
-th input variable. Renumbering the
R
polynomials in the truncated family
\mathcal{B}^{R}
with a single index
k\in\mathcal{K}=\{0\leq k\leq R-1\}
, the orthonormality condition reads:

(32)

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:ortho_{p}roperty}\left\langle\phi_{j},\phi_{k}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\phi_{j}(\mathbf{x})\phi_{k}(\mathbf{x})\mu(d\mathbf{x})=\delta_{jk}\,,\quad j,k\in\mathcal{K}\,,

where
\delta_{jk}
is the Kronecker symbol such that
\delta_{jk}=1
if
j=k
, and
\delta_{jk}=0
otherwise. Consequently, the gPC surrogate model
G_{R}
using the truncated basis
\mathcal{B}^{R}
reads:

(33)

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:G_{P}CE}G_{R}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}F_{k}\phi_{k}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\left\langle F,\phi_{k}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}\phi_{k}(\mathbf{x})\,.

Such an expansion is amenable to a direct computation of moments,
e.g.
expectation and variance:
\mathbb{E}(G_{R})=\left\langle G_{R},1\right\rangle_{L^{2}}=F_{0}
, and

(34)

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:gPCmoments}\mathrm{Var}(G_{R})=\left\langle G_{R}-\mathbb{E}(G_{R}),G_{R}-\mathbb{E}(G_{R})\right\rangle_{L^{2}}=\sum_{k=1}^{R-1}F_{k}^{2}\,,

resorting to the orthonormality condition (
32
). Higher-order moments and Sobol’ sensitivity indices can be computed along the same lines
[
72
,
82
]
.

This gPC surrogate can be compared to the surrogate (
28
) with
e_{k}\equiv\phi_{k}
:

G^{\star}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{K}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\frac{\left|F_{k}\right|}{\left|F_{j}\right|}\left\langle\phi_{j}(\mathbf{X}),\phi_{k}(\mathbf{X})\right\rangle_{I}\left\langle\phi_{j}(\mathbf{X}),\mathbf{Y}\right\rangle_{I}\phi_{k}(\mathbf{x})\,.

Both approaches are considered in the numerical examples below, using however a nugget
\lambda>0
. In gPC expansion, the expansion coefficients
F_{k}=\smash{\left\langle F,\phi_{k}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}}
are usually computed by a numerical quadrature rule:

(35)

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:quad_{n}odes}F_{k}\simeq F_{k,q}=\sum_{l=1}^{q}\omega_{l}F(\bm{\vartheta}_{l})\phi_{k}(\bm{\vartheta}_{l})\,,

where
\{\omega_{l}\}_{l=1}^{q}
are positive weights and
\{\bm{\vartheta}_{l}\}_{l=1}^{q}
are nodes in
\mathcal{X}
. The number of nodes
q
that is needed depends on the selected rule. A classical Gauss quadrature rule requires
q
nodes to exactly integrate univariate polynomials up to order
2q-1
. If one quadrature node is fixed, a Gauss-Radau (GR) rule is obtained, which exactly integrates univariate polynomials up to order
2q-2
. If two quadrature nodes are fixed, a Gauss-Lobatto (GL) rule is obtained, which exactly integrates univariate polynomials up to order
2q-3
. Thus using quadrature rules one needs about
I\approx\left\lfloor\smash{\frac{p}{2}}\right\rfloor^{d}
sampling points to exactly integrate
d
-variate polynomials of total order
p
. For complex models with high dimensional input spaces, the expansion (
33
) can be unaffordable; this is the so-called curse of dimensionality. Sparse quadrature rules based on Smolyak’s algorithm can be used to circumvent this limitation
[
77
]
. In practical examples though, the ground truth function
F
is often sparse or nearly sparse owing to a “sparsity-of-effects” principle
[
54
]
whereby the vector
\mathbf{c}=\smash{(c_{k})_{k\in\mathcal{K}}}\equiv\smash{(F_{k})_{k\in\mathcal{K}}}
of the expansion coefficients of the polynomial surrogate
G_{R}
has many negligible components
[
18
,
65
]
. In these situations, they can be evaluated within the framework of compressed sensing outlined in
Section

4.2.2
. This is the approach retained in
e.g.

[
24
,
49
]
; see also
[
34
]
and references therein, or
[
71
]
for an application to aerodynamics.

;

•

The test set which consists of
I_{\mathrm{T}}
observations:
\left(\mathbf{X}_{I_{\mathrm{T}}},\mathbf{Y}_{I_{\mathrm{T}}}=F(\mathbf{X}_{I_{\mathrm{T}}})\right)
.

The test set can be understood as an unseen set and it is used to validate the surrogate models. For both test functions, we replicate the four surrogate models through ten independent runs. As we are considering synthetic functions, we are not limited in the choice of the size of the test set. Therefore, we choose
I_{\mathrm{T}}=1\times 10^{5}
observations taken at random. One can notice that we do not use a validation set, because we know the ground truth functions
F
and we assume that the test set is large enough to ensure generalization.

The performance of each surrogate model
G
is quantified by computing the empirical Normalized Root Mean Square Error
e_{\mathrm{NRMSE}}
defined by:

(36)

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:error_{N}RMSE}e_{\mathrm{NRMSE}}=\sqrt{\frac{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{I_{\mathrm{T}}}\left(Y_{i}-G(\mathbf{X}_{i})\right)^{2}}{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{I_{\mathrm{T}}}Y_{i}^{2}}}\,,

and the empirical Root Mean Square Error
e_{\mathrm{RMSE}}
defined by:

(37)

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:error_{R}MSE}e_{\mathrm{RMSE}}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{I_{\mathrm{T}}}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{I_{\mathrm{T}}}\left(Y_{i}-G(\mathbf{X}_{i})\right)^{2}}\,.

The only difference between
e_{\mathrm{NRMSE}}
and
e_{\mathrm{RMSE}}
is that
e_{\mathrm{RMSE}}
is divided by
\sum_{i=1}^{I_{\mathrm{T}}}Y_{i}^{2}
in order to remove any scaling factor of the ground truth function
F
. In addition, the prediction coefficient
\mathrm{Q}^{2}
, or coefficient of determination
[
4
]
, defined by:

(38)

\mathrm{Q}^{2}=1-\frac{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{I_{\mathrm{T}}}\left(Y_{i}-G(\mathbf{X}_{i})\right)^{2}}{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{I_{\mathrm{T}}}\left(Y_{i}-\displaystyle\frac{1}{I_{\mathrm{T}}}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{I_{\mathrm{T}}}Y_{i}\right)^{2}}

is computed. A prediction coefficient
\mathrm{Q}^{2}
close to one indicates that the surrogate model is accurate over the
I_{\mathrm{T}}
test samples. In other words, the closer
\mathrm{Q}^{2}
is to one, the more accurate the surrogate model is. All the three metrics above are computed on the test set. The results concerning the errors
e_{\mathrm{RMSE}}
and
e_{\mathrm{NRMSE}}
are presented using box plots. In more details, the central horizontal line is the median value over the ten independent runs, and the edges of the boxes correspond to the 25th
q_{25}
and 75th
q_{75}
percentiles. The circles are the outliers defined as being either smaller than
q_{25}-1.5(q_{75}-q_{25})
, or larger than
q_{75}+1.5(q_{75}-q_{25})
.

Here in this paper, the Spectral Projected Gradient Algorithm (SPGL1) developed by van den Berg and Friedlander in
python

[
86
,
87
]
is considered in order to compute the solution of (
27
). This algorithm is based on primal-dual interior point methods. In order to tune the nugget
\lambda
of the kernel of the SSKRR surrogate (
29
) in
Algorithm

1
, we use a grid search algorithm on the
I_{\mathrm{T}}
data points with the error
e_{\mathrm{RMSE}}
as the metric. That is, we compute
e_{\mathrm{RMSE}}
with respect to
\lambda
and then select the parameter
\lambda=\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}
corresponding to the minimum of
e_{\mathrm{RMSE}}
. This can be done because the ground truth function
F
is known and inexpensive to evaluate. In addition, the parameter
\kappa
of the kernel of the SSKRR surrogate is set to
\kappa=\mathrm{Var}(\mathbf{Y})
. Finally, the parameters
\bm{\theta}=(\lambda_{\mathrm{KRR}},\{\gamma_{i}\}_{i=1}^{d})
of the Gaussian kernel of the KRR surrogate are determined using the parametric KF algorithm presented in
Section

3.2
.

5.1.
Ishigami function

The Ishigami function
[
38
]
is commonly used for benchmarking global sensitivity analyses and uncertainty quantification. The analytic expression of this three-dimensional (
d=3
) function is:

(39)

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:Ishigami_{f}unc}F(\mathbf{X})=\sin(X_{1})+a\sin^{2}(X_{2})+bX_{3}^{4}\sin(X_{1}),

with
a=7
,
b=0.1

[
47
]

\mathbf{X}=(X_{1},X_{2},X_{3})\in[-\pi,\pi]^{3}

\mathbf{X}

(40)

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:Ishigami_{l}aw}X_{i}\sim\mathcal{U}(-\pi,\pi)\,,\quad i=1,2,3\,.

The expectation and variance of
F
are given by:

(41)

\mathbb{E}(F)=\frac{a}{2}\,,\quad\mathrm{Var}(F)=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{a^{2}}{8}+\frac{b^{2}\pi^{8}}{18}+\frac{b\pi^{4}}{2}\,.

We select polynomials of total order up to
p=10
to form the polynomial basis
\mathcal{B}^{R}

R={p+d\choose d}={10+3\choose 3}=286
multi-dimensional Legendre polynomials. The latter are indeed orthonormal with respect to the uniform probability distribution.

q=12^{3}=1728
GL quadrature nodes are needed to exactly recover the orthonormality condition of
Equation

32
since we have chosen a total order
p=10
. For the sparse gPC and the SSKRR surrogates, two learning sets with
I=50
and
I=100
observations of the ground truth function
F
are considered to test the influence of

on the recovery of the expansion coefficients by the BPDN minimization (

and

is shown to yield stable solutions of (

Section

5.2.

The Rosenbrock function is an analytical function widely used in benchmarks for optimization
[
68
]

(42)

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:Rosenbrock_{f}unc}F(\mathbf{X})=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d-1}\left[100\left(X_{i+1}-X_{i}^{2}\right)^{2}+\left(1-X_{i}\right)^{2}\right]

with
d=10
and
\mathbf{X}\in[-2,2]^{10}
. The input variables
\mathbf{X}
are assumed to be mutually independent and uniformly distributed:

(43)

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:Rosenbrock_{l}aw}X_{i}\sim\mathcal{U}(-2,2)\,,\quad i=1,\dots 10\,.

We proceed as in
Section

5.1
. We select polynomials of total order up to
p=4
to form the polynomial basis
\mathcal{B}^{R}
, which corresponds to
R={p+d\choose d}={4+10\choose 10}=1001
multi-dimensional Legendre polynomials. The latter are indeed orthonormal with respect to the uniform probability distribution.
\mathcal{B}^{R}
is considered for the construction of the fully tensorized gPC, the sparse gPC, and the SSKRR surrogates. For the fully tensorized gPC surrogate model,
q=6^{10}=60,466,176
GL quadrature nodes are needed to exactly recover the orthonormality property given by
Equation

since we have chosen a total order

. The fully tensorized gPC surrogate model is not doable due to the numbers of points needed: this is the curse of dimensionality invoked in

. Therefore this model will not be considered in this example. For the sparse gPC and the SSKRR surrogates, one learning set with

observations of the ground truth function

\mathcal{B}^{R}

7.
Conclusions

In this paper we have devised two classes of algorithms to design a kernel from data in kernel methods aimed to approximate regular functions.
The first class is kernel flow which iteratively learns the parameters of a base kernel in a variant of cross-validation, albeit a non parametric version exists where observations in the dataset are moved along directions that minimize the metric used for learning.
The second class is coined spectral kernel ridge regression. It builds on a suitable representation of the data in a carefully chosen basis to design a kernel which is expanded on that basis. Both approaches can be implemented concurrently. For example, we have used kernel flow to tune the nugget in a regressor derived by the spectral kernel ridge approach. Our numerical experiments suggest that the second class of algorithms increases the accuracy of the obtained approximations.
However it has been shown in some numerical experiments that the accuracy of kernel flow can be improved if it is trained with alternative metrics such as maximum mean discrepancy. Here we have only considered a relative error, and parameterized base kernels instead of mixtures of parameterized base kernels.
The algorithm proposed to address non sparse functions by spectral kernel ridge regression has not been tested so far, so we also aim to explore its performances in future works. Time series are ubiquitous in engineering science and we shall consider the application of kernel methods in transient aerodynamic forecasting as well, with kernels learned from data. In this respect, greedy approaches whereby the accuracy of the surrogates is improved by incrementally adding observations of the ground truth function may be worth considering, with the use of
e.g.
Newton bases.

8.1.
Reproducing kernel Hilbert space

This appendix follows
[
3
, Chapter 1]
and
[
62
, Chapters 1 and 2]
. It is a short reminder on reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces (RKHS). The set of functions from
\mathcal{X}
to
\mathbb{R}
is denoted by
\mathfrak{F}(\mathcal{X},\mathbb{R})
, which is a vector space over
\mathbb{R}
with the operations of addition and scalar multiplication.

Definition 1

(RKHS)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a non-empty set. A subset
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\mathfrak{F}(\mathcal{X},\mathbb{R})
is called a RKHS on
\mathcal{X}
if

•

\mathcal{H}
is a vector subspace of
\mathfrak{F}(\mathcal{X},\mathbb{R})
;

•

\mathcal{H}
is endowed with an inner product
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
, with respect to which
\mathcal{H}
is a Hilbert space;

•

for every
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
, the linear evaluation functional
E_{\mathbf{x}}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by
E_{\mathbf{x}}(f)=f(\mathbf{x})
is bounded:
\exists C_{\mathbf{x}}>0,\;\forall f\in\mathcal{H},\;\left|f(\mathbf{x})\right|=\left|E_{\mathbf{x}}(f)\right|\leq C_{\mathbf{x}}\left\|f\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}
, where
\left\|f\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}=\smash{\sqrt{\left\langle f,f\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}}}
.

If
\mathcal{H}
is a RKHS, then the Riesz representation theorem shows that the linear evaluation functional
E_{\mathbf{x}}
is given by the inner product with a unique vector in
\mathcal{H}
; that is,
\forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
,
\exists!K(\mathbf{x},\cdot)\in\mathcal{H}
such that
\forall f\in\mathcal{H}
,
E_{\mathbf{x}}(f)=f(\mathbf{x})=\left\langle f,K(\mathbf{x},\cdot)\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
.

Definition 2

(Reproducing kernel
[
62
]
)
.

The function
K(\mathbf{x},\cdot)
is called the reproducing kernel for
\mathbf{x}
. The function
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\mapsto K(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}):\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by
K(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\left\langle K(\mathbf{y},\cdot),K(\mathbf{x},\cdot)\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
is called the reproducing kernel for
\mathcal{H}
.

In other words,
K(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
is for
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}
in
\mathcal{X}
the evaluation of the function
K(\mathbf{y},\cdot)
of
\mathcal{H}
at
\mathbf{x}
. Following the previous definition, we introduce a kernel function as follows:

Definition 3

(Kernel function
[
62
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a non-empty set and let
K:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
be a function.
K
is called a kernel function if it is symmetric and positive semi-definite that is, for any
m\geq 1
, for any
(a_{1},\dots a_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, for any
(\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots\mathbf{x}_{m})\in\mathcal{X}^{m}
,

(46)

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:kernel_{p}os_{s}emi_{d}ef}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m}a_{i}a_{j}K(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j})\geq 0\,.

Proposition 1
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a non-empty set and let
\mathcal{H}
be a RKHS on
\mathcal{X}
with reproducing kernel
K
. Then
K
is a kernel function.

Indeed let
m\geq 1
and
(\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots\mathbf{x}_{m})\in\mathcal{X}^{m}
,
(a_{1},\dots a_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
; then one has:

\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m}a_{i}a_{j}K(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j})=\left\langle\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m}a_{j}K(\mathbf{x}_{j},\cdot),\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}K(\mathbf{x}_{i},\cdot)\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\left\|\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}K(\mathbf{x}_{i},\cdot)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\geq 0\,.

In addition, let
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{X}
; one has:

K(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\left\langle K(\mathbf{y},\cdot),K(\mathbf{x},\cdot)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\langle K(\mathbf{x},\cdot),K(\mathbf{y},\cdot)\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=K(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x})\,,

which shows that
K
is symmetric. From
Proposition

1
, a RKHS
\mathcal{H}
defines a reproducing kernel
K
which is a symmetric kernel function. Although
Proposition

1
is quite elementary, it has a deep converse known as the Moore-Aronszajn theorem.

Theorem 1
.

[
3
, Theorem 3]
,
[
62
, Theorem 2.14]

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a non-empty set and let
K:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
be a function. If
K
is a kernel function, then there exists a unique RKHS
\mathcal{H}
of functions on
\mathcal{X}
such that
K
is the reproducing kernel of
\mathcal{H}
.

Therefore,
Proposition

1
and
Theorem

1
show there is a one-to-one correspondence between RKHS on a set and kernel functions on this set. In this paper, the unique RKHS associated with the kernel function
K
is denoted by
\mathcal{H}_{K}
, and
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}
is its inner product with the associated norm
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}
:

Definition 4
.

Given a kernel function
K:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
,
\mathcal{H}_{K}
denotes the unique RKHS with reproducing kernel
K
.

One more useful assumption about the kernel function
K
is made in this work, namely:

Assumption 1

(Positive definite)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a non-empty set and let
K:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
be a kernel function.
K
, which is thus symmetric, is assumed to be positive definite, or non-degenerate, that is, for any
m\geq 1
, for any
{\bf a}=(a_{1},\dots a_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
{\bf a}\neq{\bf 0}
, for any
(\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots\mathbf{x}_{m})\in\mathcal{X}^{m}
,

\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m}a_{i}a_{j}K(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j})>0\,.

Definition 5

This definition allows us to state Mercer’s theorem

, Chapter 11]

Theorem 2

(Mercer’s theorem)
.

Let
\mu

\mathcal{X}
and let
L^{2}(\mathcal{X},\mu)
be the set of square integrable functions on
\mathcal{X}
with respect to
\mu
. Let
K
be a Mercer kernel on
\mathcal{X}
and let
T_{K}:L^{2}(\mathcal{X},\mu)\to L^{2}(\mathcal{X},\mu)
be the associated integral operator defined by
\forall f\in L^{2}(\mathcal{X},\mu),\forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X},T_{K}f(\mathbf{x})=\int_{\mathcal{X}}K(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})f(\mathbf{y})\mu(d\mathbf{y})
. Then there exists a countable, orthonormal collection of functions
\{e_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
of
L^{2}(\mathcal{X},\mu)
which are eigenvectors of
T_{K}
with associated non-negative eigenvalues
\{\sigma_{i}\geq 0\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
. Moreover, taking the eigenvectors corresponding to the non-zero eigenvalues, they are continuous functions on
\mathcal{X}
and
K(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
has the following representation:

K(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{+\infty}\sigma_{i}e_{i}(\mathbf{x})\otimes e_{i}(\mathbf{y})\,,

where the series converges absolutely and uniformly:

\lim\limits_{n\to+\infty}\sup\limits_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{X}}\left|K(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})-\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n}\sigma_{i}e_{i}(\mathbf{x})\otimes e_{i}(\mathbf{y})\right|{}=0\,.

From Mercer’s theorem,
T_{K}
is a trace class operator with:

(52)

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:TK_{t}race_{c}lass}\text{Tr}(T_{K})=\int\limits_{\mathcal{X}}K(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x})\mu(d\mathbf{x})=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{+\infty}\sigma_{i}<+\infty\,.

Remark 1
.

Since
T_{K}
is self-adjoint and compact,
\{e_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is a basis of
L^{2}(\mathcal{X},\mu)
.
We remind that a family of functions
\{e_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is a basis of a Banach space
H
if
\forall f\in H,\;\exists!\{\alpha_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
\forall\varepsilon>0
,
\exists N
such that
\forall n\geq N

\left\|f-\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n}\alpha_{i}e_{i}\right\|_{H}\leq\varepsilon\,.

Mercer’s theorem also allows us to define explicitly the RKHS
\mathcal{H}_{K}
associated with the kernel
K
, on the condition that
K
is a Mercer kernel. Indeed, one has
[
62
, Theorem 11.18]
:

\mathcal{H}_{K}=\left\{f\in L^{2}(\mathcal{X},\mu),f=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{+\infty}\left\langle f,e_{i}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}e_{i}\text{ with }\sum\limits_{i=0}^{+\infty}\frac{\left\langle f,e_{i}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}^{2}}{\sigma_{i}}<+\infty\right\}\,,

where
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle_{L^{2}}
is the inner product of
L^{2}(\mathcal{X},\mu)
. The inner product of
\mathcal{H}_{K}
is given as
\forall(f,g)\in\mathcal{H}_{K}\times\mathcal{H}_{K}
,

\left\langle f,g\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{+\infty}\frac{\left\langle f,e_{i}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}\left\langle g,e_{i}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}}{\sigma_{i}}\,.

Consequently, the norm
\left\|f\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}
reads:

(53)

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:norm_{R}KHS}\left\|f\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}^{2}=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{+\infty}\frac{\left\langle f,e_{i}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}^{2}}{\sigma_{i}}\,,

which gives its expression in terms of the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of
K
. One can notice that
\left\langle f,e_{i}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}=\sigma_{i}\left\langle f,e_{i}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}
, so one also has:

\left\langle f,g\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{+\infty}\sigma_{i}\left\langle f,e_{i}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}\left\langle g,e_{i}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}\,.

Remark 2
.

The functional space
\mathcal{H}_{K}
does not depend on the measure
\mu
, actually. Only the eigenvectors
\{e_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
and the eigenvalues
\{\sigma_{i}\geq 0\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
do.

Remark 3
.

It can be shown that
\{\sqrt{\sigma_{i}}e_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{H}_{K}
; see
[
62
, Theorem 11.18]
.
