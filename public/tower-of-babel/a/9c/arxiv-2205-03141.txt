Freezing in space-time: A functional equation linked with a PDE system Thanks: KB’s research was supported in part by Simons Foundation Grant 506732.
Krzysztof Burdzy and Adam J. Ostaszewski

Abstract
. We analyze the functional equation F ⁡ ( x + F ⁡ ( x ) ) = − F ⁡ ( x ) F(x+F(x))=-F(x) and reveal its relationship with a system of partial differential equations arising as the hydrodynamic limit of a system of pinned billiard balls on the line. The system of balls must freeze at some time, i.e., no velocity may change after the freezing time. The terminal velocity and the freezing time profiles play the role of boundary conditions for the PDEs (qua terminal conditions, despite being “initial conditions” from the wave equation perspective pursued here). Solutions to the functional equation provide the link between the freezing time and terminal velocity profiles on the one hand, and the solution to the PDE in the entirety of the space-time domain on the other.

1.
Introduction

This article is concerned with the functional equation

(1.1)

\displaystyle F(x+F(x))=-F(x),

where
F
represents an unknown real-valued function defined on
\mathbb{R}
or a subset of
\mathbb{R}
,
and its key role in linking the solutions of a PDE system to its boundary conditions.
In view of its structure (
1.1
) is termed a single-variable, composite, functional equation (cf.
[
AD89
]
Chap. 19). Nearest in structure to (
1.1
), but in two variables,
is the Goła̧b-Schinzel equation

(1.2)

\displaystyle F(x+yF(x))=F(x)F(y).

Its continuous solutions take one of the three forms:
F(x)\equiv 0
,
F_{\rho}(x):=1+\rho x
, or
F(x)=\max\{F_{\rho}(x),0\}
, for some constant
\rho\in\mathbb{R}
(see
[
Brz05
, p. 17]
).
For a further discussion of (
1.2
) see
[
AD89
, Ch. 19]
and for a survey of the literature
[
Brz05
]
. Since
F_{-2}(1)=-1
, substituting
y=1
in (
1.2
) shows that
F_{-2}
is a solution to (
1.1
); cf. Example
3.4
. However, the solutions to (
1.1
) are more plentiful.

Equation (
1.1
) arises in the analysis of a system of “pinned balls” and the corresponding partial differential equations. We will discuss this application of (
1.1
) in Section
4
. See Remark
4.2
for a brief outline of the relationship between pinned balls and partial differential equations.
The differential equations discussed in this article are amenable to analysis using well-known methods. A more complete and more general analysis of our system of PDEs will be presented in a separate paper.

There are numerous books devoted to functional equations, see for example,
[
Acz66
,
AD89
,
Sma07
]
. We did not find equation (
1.1
) in any of these books or in other publications.

Equation (
1.1
) is closely related to involutions. We will use results on involutions presented in
[
Zam14
]
. We refer the reader to that article for a review of the literature, noting here only the early contribution
[
Acz48
]
.

Equation (
1.1
) and associated involutions will be discussed in Section
2
. This will be followed by Section
3
with examples. Finally, Section
4
will present an application of (
1.1
) to partial differential equations, including examples.

2.
The functional equation (
1.1
) and involutions

We will discuss solutions
F
to (
1.1
) defined on a set
A\subset\mathbb{R}
.
Note that
F(x)\equiv 0
is a solution to (
1.1
) on
\mathbb{R}
so the set of solutions is non-empty.

Let

\displaystyle\varphi(x)=x+F(x),\qquad x\in A.

The dependence of the function
\varphi
on
F
is not reflected in the notation but this should not cause confusion.

Proposition 2.1
.

Suppose that
F
is continuous and solves (
1.1
) on an interval
I
, and
I
is a maximal interval in this respect, i.e., if
F
is continuous and solves (
1.1
) on an interval
I^{\prime}
such that
I\subset I^{\prime}
then
I^{\prime}=I
. Note that
F
must necessarily be defined on
J:=\varphi(I)
for (
1.1
) to be meaningful.

(i) The function
\varphi
is an involution on
I
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\varphi(\varphi(x))=x,\qquad x\in I.

(ii)
\varphi(J)=I
.

(iii) The function
\varphi
is continuous and strictly monotone on
I
.

(iv) The function
\varphi
is continuous and strictly monotone on
J
.

(v) If
I\cup J
is an interval then
\varphi
is continuous and strictly decreasing on
J
.

Proof.

Consider any
x\in I
and let
z=\varphi(x)=x+F(x)
. Then, using (
1.1
),

\varphi(\varphi(x))=\varphi(z)=z+F(z)=x+F(x)+F(x+F(x))=x+F(x)-F(x)=x.

This proves (i). Claim (ii) follows from (i).

(iii)
If
\varphi(x)=\varphi(y)
then
x=\varphi(\varphi(x))=\varphi(\varphi(y))=y
. In other words,
\varphi
is injective on
I
. Since
F
is continuous on
I
, so is
\varphi
. This implies that
\varphi
is continuous and strictly monotone on
I
.

(iv) The graphs
\{(x,\varphi(x)),x\in I\}
and
\{(x,\varphi(x)),x\in J\}
are symmetric with respect to the diagonal
\{(x,x),x\in\mathbb{R}\}
. This and (iii) imply (iv).

(v) We copy an argument from
[
Zam14
, Prop. 1.1]
.
If
I\cup J
is an interval then
\varphi
is continuous and strictly monotone on
I\cup J
, by (iii) and (iv).
Suppose that
\varphi
is strictly increasing.
If
x\in I\cup J
and
x<\varphi(x)
then
\varphi(x)<\varphi(\varphi(x))=x
, a contradiction;
similarly,
x>\varphi(x)
implies
\varphi(x)>x
.
∎

Remark 2.2
.

(i)
Proposition
2.1
applies to open, half-open, closed, finite, semi-infinite and infinite intervals
I
, including intervals consisting of a single point. In the case of a semi-infinite interval
I=(a,\infty)
, note that
F(x)
is asymptotic on
that interval to
-x
, i.e.,
\lim_{x\to\infty}F(x)+x=0
, if and only if
\lim_{x\to\infty}\varphi(x)=0
, since
F(x)=-x+\varphi(x)
. A similar remark applies to
I=(-\infty,a)
.

(ii) Suppose that
I\cup J
is an interval. Then
\varphi
is continuous and strictly decreasing on
I\cup J
. Since
\{(x,\varphi(x)),x\in I\}
and
\{(x,\varphi(x)),x\in J\}
are symmetric with respect to the diagonal
\{(x,x),x\in\mathbb{R}\}
, there exists a unique
x_{0}
such that
\varphi(x_{0})=x_{0}
and, therefore,
F(x_{0})=0
. The function
F
is strictly negative on the part of
I\cup J
to the right of
x_{0}
and it is strictly positive to the left of
x_{0}
.

Corollary 2.3
.

The domain
A
of a solution
F
to (
1.1
) can be decomposed into a family
\mathcal{A}
of disjoint sets of the form
I_{\alpha}\cup J_{\alpha}
, where the pair
(I_{\alpha},J_{\alpha})
satisfies the conditions in Proposition
2.1
.

Remark 2.4
.

The set
\mathcal{A}
in Corollary
2.3
can be finite, countably infinite or uncountable. The sets
I_{\alpha}\cup J_{\alpha}
may be intervals, pairs of disjoint intervals, single points or pairs of points.

Proposition 2.5
.

Consider intervals
I,J\subset\mathbb{R}
and a function
\varphi
such that
\varphi:I\to J
,
\varphi:J\to I
,
\varphi
is continuous and strictly monotone on
I
, and is an involution on
I\cup J
. Then
F(x):=\varphi(x)-x
is continuous on
I\cup J
and solves (
1.1
) on
I\cup J
.

Proof.

The graphs
\{(x,\varphi(x)),x\in I\}
and
\{(x,\varphi(x)),x\in J\}
are symmetric with respect to the diagonal
\{(x,x),x\in\mathbb{R}\}
because
\varphi
is an involution. Since
\varphi
is continuous and strictly monotone on
I
, it follows that
\varphi
is continuous and strictly monotone on
J
.

Since
\varphi
is continuous on
I\cup J
, so is
F
.

Consider any
x\in I\cup J
and let
z=\varphi(x)=x+F(x)
. Then

\displaystyle x+F(x)-F(x)=x=\varphi(\varphi(x))=\varphi(z)=z+F(z)=x+F(x)+F(x+F(x)).

Subtracting
x+F(x)
from both sides, we obtain
-F(x)=F(x+F(x))
, i.e.,
F
solves (
1.1
) for
x\in I\cup J
.
∎

Lemma 2.6
.

(Conjugacy, reflection and shift invariance)
Suppose that
F
is a solution to (
1.1
) on
A
.
For a fixed
a\neq 0
, let
F_{a}^{\times}(x):=a^{-1}F(ax)
for
x
such that
x\in A/a:=\{y:ya\in A\}
.
Let
F_{a}^{+}(x):=F(a+x)
for
x
such that
x\in A-a
. Then
F_{a}^{\times}
and
F_{a}^{+}
solve (
1.1
) on
A/a
and
A-a
, resp. In particular, the “reflection”
F^{-}(x):=-F(-x)
is a solution on
-A
.

Proof.

Putting
ax
for
x
in (
1.1
) yields the following

\displaystyle F(a[x+a^{-1}F(ax)]))+F(ax)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

\displaystyle a^{-1}F(a[x+a^{-1}F(ax)]))+a^{-1}F(ax)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

\displaystyle F_{a}^{\times}(x+F_{a}^{\times}(x))+F_{a}^{\times}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.

Likewise putting
a+x
for
x
yields

\displaystyle F(a+x+F(a+x))+F(a+x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

\displaystyle F_{a}^{+}(x+F_{a}^{+}(x))+F_{a}^{+}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.

∎

The following lemma is a slightly modified
[
Zam14
, Thm. 2.1]
.

Lemma 2.7
.

Suppose that
a>0
and
\psi:[-a,a]\to\mathbb{R}
is even, differentiable and
\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\psi(x)\right|<1
for all
x
. Let
u\to(u+\psi(u))^{-1}
denote the inverse of the function
x\to x+\psi(x)
.
Then

(2.1)

\displaystyle\varphi(u)

\displaystyle=u-2(u+\psi(u))^{-1}

is an involution on the interval
[-a+\psi(a),a+\psi(a)]
.

Proof.

The condition
\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\psi(x)\right|<1
implies that if we rotate the graph
\Gamma_{1}
of
\psi
by
\pi/4
clockwise and stretch it by the factor of
\sqrt{2}
(in all directions) then it will be the graph
\Gamma_{2}
of a function
\varphi
on the interval
[-a+\psi(-a),a+\psi(a)]=[-a+\psi(a),a+\psi(a)]
. The graph
\Gamma_{1}
is symmetric with respect to the vertical axis, so
\Gamma_{2}
is symmetric with respect to the diagonal.
Hence, the function
\varphi
is an involution.
We will find a formula for
\varphi
in terms of
\psi
.

If
y=\psi(x)
then
(x,y)\in\Gamma_{1}
and, therefore,

(x+y,-x+y)\in\Gamma_{2}
. We will denote this point
(u,\varphi(u))
. We have

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=x+y=x+\psi(x),

\displaystyle\psi(x)

\displaystyle=u-x.

Recall that
u\to(u+\psi(u))^{-1}
denotes the inverse of the function
x\to x+\psi(x)
. Then
x=(u+\psi(u))^{-1}
and

\displaystyle\varphi(u)

\displaystyle=-x+y=-x+\psi(x)=-x+u-x=-2x+u=-2(u+\psi(u))^{-1}+u.

∎

3.
Examples

Example 3.1
.

Our first example is just a special case of Proposition
2.5
.

Consider a finite interval
(x_{1},x_{2})
and a strictly increasing function
\varphi:(x_{1},x_{2})\to\mathbb{R}
. Let
y_{1}=\varphi(x_{1})
and
y_{2}=\varphi(x_{2})
. Suppose that
(x_{1},x_{2})\cap(y_{1},y_{2})=\emptyset
and let
\varphi(y)=\varphi^{-1}(y)
for

. Then
F(x):=\varphi(x)-x
solves (
1.1
) on
(x_{1},x_{2})\cup(y_{1},y_{2})
.

Alternatively, consider a finite interval
(x_{1},x_{2})
and a strictly decreasing function
\varphi:(x_{1},x_{2})\to\mathbb{R}
. Let
y_{1}=\varphi(x_{1})
and
y_{2}=\varphi(x_{2})
. If

\varphi(y)=\varphi^{-1}(y)
for

\varphi(y)=\varphi^{-1}(y)

) on

.

Example 3.3

show that one can decompose the real line into an arbitrary family of disjoint (open or closed) intervals or pairs of intervals (these could be points or pairs of points), and then construct a solution to (

4.
PDEs with freezing

The main result of this section is the following.

Theorem 4.1
.

Suppose that
a>0
,
h:[-a,a]\to\mathbb{R}
is strictly increasing, differentiable, and odd.
Suppose that
T:[-a,a]\to\mathbb{R}
is even, differentiable, strictly decreasing on
[0,a]
, and such that
\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial x}T(x)\right|<1
for all
x\in(-a,a)
. Then
there exist continuous functions
\sigma:[-a,a]\times\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
and
\mu:[-a,a]\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying

(4.1)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\mu(x,t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\sigma(x,t),

(4.2)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\sigma(x,t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\mu(x,t),

on the set
\{(x,t)\in(-a,a)\times\mathbb{R}:\sigma(x,t)>0\}
, and such that
for
x\in[-a,a]
,

(4.3)

\displaystyle\inf\{t\geq 0:\sigma(x,t)=0\}

\displaystyle=T(x),

(4.4)

\displaystyle\mu(x,T(x))

\displaystyle=h(x).

Remark 4.2
.

(i)
The equations (
4.1
)-(
4.2
) arose in the analysis of the hydrodynamic limit of a system of pinned billiard balls on a line. See
[
ABD21
]
for the definition of the pinned billiard balls model.
Heuristically speaking, after rescaling the position and time for the set of
n
pinned balls and taking the limit as
n\to\infty
, we obtain a continuum family of infinitesimal pinned balls with velocities

v(x,t)=\mu(x,t)+\sigma(x,t)W(x,t),

where
W
is space-time white noise.
The function
\mu(x,t)
represents the mean velocity of the infinitesimal ball at position
x
at time
t
, and
\sigma(x,t)
represents its standard deviation.
The equations (
4.1
)-(
4.2
) are the result of informal calculations at the discrete level and passage to the limit of the rescaled system.

(ii)
Theorem
[
ABD21
, Thm. 3.1]
shows that the evolution of a finite system of pinned billiard balls must terminate after a finite number of collisions.
In other words, the system
must completely
freeze
at each location
x
at some time (depending on
x
).
In terms of the hydrodynamic limit, this means that
\sigma(x,t)
must be identically equal to
0
for some
t_{\infty}<\infty
and all
t\geq t_{\infty}
.
According to (
4.1
),
\mu(x,t)
will not change after time
t_{\infty}
. These two phenomena are formally represented by (
4.3
)-(
4.4
).

(iii)
It follows easily from
(
4.1
)-(
4.2
)
that
\sigma
satisfies the wave equation

(4.5)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}\sigma(x,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}\sigma(x,t),

on the set
\{(x,t):\sigma(x,t)>0\}
.

Let
X
be the inverse of
T
on
[0,a]
, i.e.,
X(t)=x
if
T(x)=t
. Since
\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial x}T(x)\right|<1
, we have
\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial t}X(t)\right|>1
. This seemingly contradicts the bound on the wave speed which is normalized to be 1 in (
4.5
). However, there is no contradiction because the boundary of the frozen region does not represent a physical signal that could be used to send information, so it is not limited by the wave speed.

(iv)
Key to Theorem
4.1
are solutions to the functional equation

(4.6)

\displaystyle G(x-2G(x))=-G(x)

(see (
4.15
) below with
G=g^{-1}
), which is equivalent, after substituting
2x
for
x
and taking
F(x)=-G(2x)
, to

F(x+F(x))=-F(x),

i.e., (
1.1
). For any solution
G
to (
4.6
) and any strictly increasing
odd function
h
(the desired terminal velocity profile), the composition

f(x)=\frac{1}{2}h(G(x))

yields a familiar solution to the wave equation satisfied by
\sigma
,
with forward and backward traveling waves with standardized velocity
c=1
:

\sigma(x,t)=f(x+ct)+f(-x-ct).

In this sense, the functional equation (
4.6
) generates
the fundamental solution to the PDE system. In the proof below,
G
is the
inverse function
g^{-1}
to
g(x)=x-T(x),
where
T(x)
is the desired
freezing time, satisfying the conditions of Theorem 4.1. Thus the freezing
time
T(x)
determines and is determined by the “generator”
G.
Indeed

(4.7)

\displaystyle T(x)=x-g(x)=x-G^{-1}(x).

(v)
Note that the shape
h
of the frozen function
\mu

can be chosen independently of the “speed” of freezing represented by
T
.

(vi) It is easy to see that the proof applies also to the case when the finite interval
[-a,a]
is replaced with
(-\infty,\infty)
.

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

In view of (
4.5
), we will look for

\sigma
having the form
\sigma(x,t)=f(x-t)+f(-x-t)
.
It is easy to check that the following functions solve (
4.1
)-(
4.2
),

(4.8)

\displaystyle\sigma(x,t)

\displaystyle=f(x-t)+f(-x-t),

(4.9)

\displaystyle\mu(x,t)

\displaystyle=f(x-t)-f(-x-t),

for any differentiable function
f
.

Let
z=g(x)=x-T(x)
and note that
g
is strictly increasing, by the assumption that
\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial x}T(x)\right|<1
. Hence the inverse function
g^{-1}
is well defined and accordingly
x=g^{-1}(z)
.
Let

(4.10)

\displaystyle f(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}h(g^{-1}(z)).

This defines the function
f(z)
for

(4.11)

\displaystyle z\in g([-a,a])=[-a-T(-a),a-T(a)]=[-a-T(a),a-T(a)].

We extend
f
to the whole real line so that it remains differentiable but is otherwise arbitrary.

We will show that if we substitute this function
f

We now illustrate Theorem
4.1
with three explicit examples. From the purely mathematical point of view, the examples are routine applications of Theorem
4.1
. However, in the context of the pinned balls model discussed in Remark
4.2
(i), it is far from obvious how the freezing might proceed. Does it have to be progressive? Or can it happen simultaneously
along a substantial stretch of the space? Our examples show that three possible scenarios can occur: continuous progression of the front of the frozen region, simultaneous freezing over the whole spacial interval at the terminal time, and a mixture of the two phenomena—progressive freezing followed by the simultaneous freezing of a part of the space at the terminal time.

Below, the notation is as in the statement and proof of Theorem
4.1
.

Example 4.4
.

Let

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle=\sqrt{4-1/e^{4}},

\displaystyle T(x)

\displaystyle=2-\sqrt{x^{2}+1/e^{4}},

\displaystyle h(x)

\displaystyle=2\log\left(\sqrt{x^{2}+1/e^{4}}+x\right)+4.

Then for

and

\displaystyle=\log(x+2)+2,

\displaystyle\sigma(x,t)

\displaystyle=\log(x-t+2)+\log(-x-t+2)+4,

\displaystyle\mu(x,t)

\displaystyle=\log(x-t+2)-\log(-x-t+2),

\displaystyle\sigma(x,0)

\displaystyle=\log(2-x)+\log(x+2)+4,
