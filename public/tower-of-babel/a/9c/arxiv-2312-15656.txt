Unconditionally stable exponential integrator schemes for the 2D Cahn-Hilliard equation
Xinyu Cheng

Abstract
. Phase field models are gradient flows with their energy naturally dissipating in time. In order to preserve this property, many numerical schemes have been well-studied. In this paper we consider a well-known method, namely the exponential integrator method (EI). In the literature a few works studied several EI schemes for various phase field models and proved the energy dissipation by either requiring a strong Lipschitz condition on the nonlinear source term or certain L ∞ L^{\infty} bounds on the numerical solutions (maximum principle). However for phase field models such as the (non-local) Cahn-Hilliard equation, the maximum principle no longer exists. As a result, solving such models via EI schemes remains open for a long time. In this paper we aim to give a systematic approach on applying EI-type schemes to such models by solving the Cahn-Hilliard equation with a first order EI scheme and showing the energy dissipation. In fact second order EI schemes can be handled similarly and we leave the discussion in a subsequent paper. To our best knowledge, this is the first work to handle phase field models without assuming any strong Lipschitz condition or L ∞ L^{\infty} boundedness. Furthermore, we will analyze the L 2 L^{2} error and present some numerical simulations to demonstrate the dynamics.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Introduction to the models and historical review

Many mathematical models
of physical, biological phenomena can be decribed via partial differential equations (PDEs). Among PDEs, phase field equations are models of essential importance in the area of material sciences.
In this work we consider two classic phase field models: Allen-Cahn (
AC
) and Cahn-Hilliard (
CH
) equations. The (
AC
) model was developed in
[
2
]
by Allen and Cahn to study the competition of crystal grain orientations in an annealing process separation of different metals in a binary alloy; while the (
CH
) was introduced in
[
7
]
by Cahn and Hilliard to describe the process of phase separation of different metals in a binary alloy. These equations are presented as:

(AC)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}u=\nu\Delta u-f(u),\ \ \ (x,t)\in\Omega\times(0,\infty),\\
&u(x,0)=u_{0},\end{aligned}\right.\

and

(CH)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}u=\Delta(-\nu\Delta u+f(u)),\ \ \ (x,t)\in\Omega\times(0,\infty),\\
&u(x,0)=u_{0},\end{aligned}\right.\

where
u(x,t)
is a real valued function and values of
u
in
(-1,1)
represent a mixture of the two phases, with
-1
representing the pure state of one phase and
+1
representing the pure state of the other phase. Vector position
x
is in the spatial domain
\Omega
, which is oftentimes taken to be two or three dimensional periodic domain and
t
is the time variable. Here
\nu
is a small parameter, occasionally we denote
\varepsilon=\sqrt{\nu}
to represent an average distance over which phases mix. The energy term
f(u)
is often chosen to be

f(u)=F^{\prime}(u)=u^{3}-u\ ,\ F(u)=\frac{1}{4}(u^{2}-1)^{2}.

It is well known that, as
\varepsilon\to 0
, the limiting problem of (
AC
) is driven under a (mean) curvature flow while the limiting problem of (
CH
) follows from the Mullins-Sekerka flow; we refer to
[
16
]
for AC and
[
22
]
,
[
1
]
for CH and a recent work for matrix-valued AC
[
13
]
. Both asymptotic and rigorous analysis are well-studided. This current work will present an idea about how to approach these limiting behaviors in an appropriate way numerically.

To be more specific, throughout this paper we choose the spatial domain
\Omega
to be the two dimensional
2\pi
-periodic torus
\mathbb{T}^{2}=(\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z})^{2}
. In fact, our analysis can be applied to more general settings such as Dirichlet and Neumann boundary conditions in a bounded domain. However, considering the periodic domain allows us to use the efficient and accurate Fourier-spectral numerical algorithms. Beyond this, periodic domain is often appropriate for practical questions, which involve the formation of micro-structure away from physical boundaries. As is well-known that both the AC and CH behave as gradient flows, therefore there energy dissipates in time. Here the associated energy functional is given by

(1.1)

\displaystyle E(u)=\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{1}{2}\nu|\nabla u|^{2}+F(u)\right)\ dx.

Assume that
u(x,t)
is a smooth solution, it is clear that the energy decays in time:
\frac{d}{dt}E(u(t))\leq 0
. Such property thus provides an
a\ priori

H^{1}
-norm bound and since the scaling-critical space for (
CH
) is
L^{2}
in 2D (and
H^{\frac{1}{2}}
in 3D), the global well-posedness follow from standard energy estimates. Therefore from the analysis point of view, the energy dissipation property is an important index for whether a numerical scheme is “stable” or not.

There are various approaches existing in the literature that are developed to study numerical behaviors of the Cahn-Hilliard and other related phase field models, cf.
[
10
,
15
,
11
,
14
,
8
,
4
,
12
,
3
,
17
]
. Among which different time stepping approaches are applied including the fully explicit (forward Euler) scheme, fully implicit (backward Euler) scheme, finite element scheme and convex splitting scheme; moreover various schemes are adopted for the spatial discretization including the Fourier-spectral method and finite difference schemes. To guarantee the accuracy and stability, numerical approximations usually need to obey certain qualitative behaviors and features. In this paper we focus on one of the key features, namely the energy dissipation or conservation as mentioned earlier.

Based on the discussion above, we briefly go through the related results on the energy stability in the literature. To start with, Feng and Prohl
[
14
]
introduced a semi-discrete in time and fully spatially discrete finite element method for CH where they obtained an error bound of size of powers of
1/\nu
. However, explicit time-stepping schemes usually require strict time-step restrictions and do not obey energy decay in general (one can compare with Remark
1.4
). To guarantee the energy dissipation with bigger time steps, a good alternative is to use semi-implicit schemes in which the linear term is implicit (such as backward time differentiation) and the nonlinear term is treated explicitly. Having only a linear implicit at every time step has computational advantages, as suggested in
[
8
]
, where a semi-implicit Fourier-spectral scheme for CH was considered. On the other hand, semi-implicit schemes can lose stability for large time steps and thus smaller time steps are needed in practice. To resolve this problem, semi-implicit methods with better stability have been introduced, cf.
[
19
,
20
,
9
]
. Motivated by the work on semi-implicit schemes, the so-called “exponential integrator schemes” (EI schemes) start to draw attention in the study of PDEs, cf.
[
18
,
23
]
. In a short word, the EI schemes discretize the semi-group presentation of the solutions to nonlinear PDEs. In more details, take the Allen-Cahn equation as an example. It is well-known that the smooth solution to (
AC
) is given by the semi-group mild form (or Duhamel’s Formula):

(1.2)

u(t)=e^{\nu t\Delta}u(t_{0})+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}e^{\nu(t-s)\Delta}f(u(s))\ ds,

where
e^{t\Delta}
is the standard heat semi-group (heat kernel). Then the idea of the classic EI is to discretize the semi-group presentation in each time interval
(t_{n},t_{n+1})
by approximating the integrand function by a constant (cf.
[
18
]
):

\displaystyle u(t_{n+1})=

\displaystyle e^{\nu\tau\Delta}u(t_{n})+\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}e^{\nu(t_{n+1}-s)\Delta}f(u(s))\ ds

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle e^{\nu\tau\Delta}u(t_{n})+\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}e^{\nu(t_{n+1}-s)\Delta}f(u(t_{n}))\ ds

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle e^{\nu\tau\Delta}u(t_{n})+\frac{1-e^{\nu(t_{n+1}-t_{n})\Delta}}{\nu\Delta}f(u(t_{n})),

or equivalently writing
u(t_{n})=u^{n},u(t_{n+1})=u^{n+1}
and
\tau=t_{n+1}-t_{n}
we have

\displaystyle u^{n+1}=e^{\nu\tau\Delta}u^{n}+\frac{1-e^{\nu\tau\Delta}}{\nu\Delta}f(u^{n}).

However, those works either require a strong Lipschitz condition on the nonlinear source term, or require certain
L^{\infty}
bounds on the numerical solutions. Such requirement is satisfied by the Allen-Cahn equation by the standard maximum principle (we refer the readers to
[
19
,
9
]
for example). While for other phase field models such as the (non-local) Cahn-Hilliard equation, the maximum principle no longer exists. As a result, solving phase field models via EI schemes where strong Lipschitz conditions or maximum principle are no longer applicable remains open for a long time. One of our modest goals of this paper is to give a systematic approach on applying EI-type schemes to such models. In particular, we consider one of the classic phase models, the Cahn-Hilliard equation with a first order EI scheme. In fact second order EI schemes can be handled similarly and we leave the discussion in a forthcoming paper.

In this work we consider the following stabilized EI scheme for (
CH
):

(1.3)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &u^{n+1}=e^{-\nu\Delta^{2}\tau}u^{n}+\frac{1-e^{-\nu\Delta^{2}\tau}}{\nu\Delta}\Pi_{N}[f(u^{n})]-S\tau\Delta^{2}(u^{n+1}-u^{n}),\\
&u^{0}=\Pi_{N}u_{0},\end{aligned}\right.

where
\tau
is the time step and
S>0
is the coefficient for the
O(\tau)
regularization term. In fact (
1.3
) above can be reorganized and rewritten into the following form:

(1.4)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{u^{n+1}-u^{n}}{\tau}+S\Delta^{2}(u^{n+1}-u^{n})=\frac{e^{-\nu\Delta^{2}\tau}-1}{\tau}u^{n}+\frac{1-e^{-\nu\Delta^{2}\tau}}{\tau\nu\Delta}\Pi_{N}[f(u^{n})],\\
&u^{0}=\Pi_{N}u_{0}.\end{aligned}\right.

Remark 1.1
.

It is worth mentioning that from (
1.4
) one can observe that our exponential integrator scheme behaves very similarly to an explicit forward Euler method since

\displaystyle\frac{e^{-\nu\Delta^{2}\tau}-1}{\tau}=-\nu\Delta^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\nu^{2}\Delta^{4}\tau+O(\tau^{2}),

\displaystyle\frac{1-e^{-\nu\Delta^{2}\tau}}{\tau\nu\Delta}=\Delta-\frac{1}{2}\nu\Delta^{3}\tau+O(\tau^{2}).

Therefore informally speaking, (
1.4
) can be understood as the usual explicit forward Euler scheme for (
CH
) with a stabilization term:

(1.5)

\displaystyle\frac{u^{n+1}-u^{n}}{\tau}+S\Delta^{2}(u^{n+1}-u^{n})=-\nu\Delta^{2}u^{n}+\Delta\Pi_{N}[f(u^{n})]+O(\nu\tau,\tau^{2}).

Remark 1.2
.

The stabilizer
S\Delta^{2}(u^{n+1}-u^{n})
is not unique. In fact our analysis works for many other stabilizers such as
Se^{-\Delta}(u^{n+1}-u^{n})
and so on. Another particular choice is
\frac{\nu}{2}\Delta^{2}(u^{n+1}-u^{n})-S\Delta(u^{n+1}-u^{n})
. Such particular choice results in the analysis of the energy dissipation. More precisely speaking, we need
\frac{\nu}{2}\Delta^{2}(u^{n+1}-u^{n})
to balance the drawback caused by the “explicit forward” diffusion; the real stabilizer is indeed
-S\Delta(u^{n+1}-u^{n})
. We choose
S\Delta^{2}(u^{n+1}-u^{n})
just for the sake of simplicity. Even though such choices of stabilizers are not unique, they are necessary. As will be presented in Section 5 with numerical evidence supporting, we will see examples without stabilizers have growing energy.

Remark 1.3
.

The exact explicit forward Euler method seems challenging to obtain the energy stability because it seems unlikely to obtain a uniform Sobolev
H^{s}
-bound estimate. To our best knowledge, we believe such problem arises from the essence of the forward Euler method since the regularity at step
n
is higher than the regularity at step
n+1
due to the “explicit forward” diffusion
-\Delta^{2}u^{n}
. This seems to be the crucial technical difference between our exponential integrator scheme (
1.4
) and the
exact explicit forward Euler scheme (
1.5
) without the stabilizer
S\Delta^{2}(u^{n+1}-u^{n})
and higher order
O(\nu\tau,\tau^{2})
perturbation.

Remark 1.4
.

As mentioned earlier in Remark
1.1
, our scheme (
1.4
) can be understood as an explicit forward Euler scheme. It is well known that after spatial discretization on a grid of size
h
, any explicit scheme will have a time step
\tau
restriction of size
\tau<O(h^{4})
for the fourth order CH equation. However as will be explained later (see Theorem
1.7
), in order to show the convergence of our numerical scheme, we only require
\tau\ll(\log(N))^{-1}
, which is a very mild restriction. Moreover the energy stability holds for any size of
\tau
, see Theorem
1.5
.

1.2.
Main results

Our main results state below.

Theorem 1.5

(Unconditional energy stability for
CH
)
.

Consider (
1.3
) or (
1.4
) with
\nu>0
and assume
u_{0}\in H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
. Then there exists a constant
\beta_{0}
depending only on the initial energy
E_{0}=E(u_{0})
such that if

\begin{aligned} S\geq\beta\cdot\left(\|u_{0}\|^{2}_{H^{2}}+\nu^{-1}|\log\nu|+\nu^{-1}\log N+\nu\right)\end{aligned},\quad\beta\geq\beta_{0}.

Then
E(u^{n+1})\leq E(u^{n})
,
\forall n\geq 0
and for any choice of the time step
\tau
, where the energy
E
is defined in (
1.1
).

Remark 1.6
.

The choice of
S
is not optimal here; in fact we refer the readers to Section 5 for numerical evidence with much smaller
S
. Determining the optimal bound of the stabilizers can be a very interesting question, however it is not the focus of the presenting paper; we leave this question to the readers.

Theorem 1.7

(
L^{2}
error estimate)
.

Fix
\nu>0
and let
u_{0}\in H^{s}
,
s\geq 10
. Let
0<\tau\leq M
for some
M>0
. Let
u(t)
be the continuous solution to the 2D Cahn-Hilliard equation with initial data
u_{0}
. Let
u^{m}
,
m\geq 1
be defined in (
1.4
) with initial data
u^{0}
. Assume
S
satisfies the same condition in Theorem
1.5
. Define
t_{0}=0
and
t_{m}=m\tau
for
m\geq 1
. Then for any
m\geq 1
,

\|u^{m}-u(t_{m})\|_{2}\leq(1+S)\cdot C_{2}\cdot e^{C_{1}t_{m}}\left(N^{-s}+\tau+\tau\cdot N^{-s+4}\right),

where
C_{1}>0
is a constant depending on
\nu\ ,u_{0}
;
C_{2}>0
is a constant depending on
s\ ,\nu
and
u_{0}
.

Remark 1.8
.

It is worth mentioning that the requirement
S\geq\beta\nu^{-1}\log N
in Theorem
1.5
is troublesome in the convergence of the numerical scheme. However as stated in Theorem
1.7
above,
S\cdot N^{-s}\to 0
as
N\to\infty
therefore such choice of
S
will not ruin the convergence. On the other hand, we do need
\tau\cdot S\ll 1
, which means
\tau\ll(\log N)^{-1}
. This is still a very mild restriction as discussed in Remark
1.4
because if one uses spatial discretization, the restriction on the size of the time step
\tau
is usually much smaller (of polynomial order of
\frac{1}{N}
) to be numerically stable.

Remark 1.9
.

Here we need to require that
\tau
is not arbitrarily large. However, in practice it is not a big issue as we always use small (or at least not arbitrarily large) time steps.

Notation and preliminaries

Throughout this paper, for any two (non-negative in particular) quantities
X
and
Y
, we denote
X\lesssim Y
if

X\leq CY
for some constant
C>0
. Similarly
X\gtrsim Y
if
X\geq CY
for some
C>0
. We denote
X\sim Y
if
X\lesssim Y
and
Y\lesssim X
. The dependence of the constant
C
on
other parameters or constants are usually clear from the context and
we will often suppress this dependence. We shall denote

X\lesssim_{Z_{1},Z_{2},\cdots,Z_{k}}Y

if
X\leq CY
and the constant
C
depends on the quantities
Z_{1},\cdots,Z_{k}
.

For any two quantities
X
and
Y
, we shall denote
X\ll Y
if

X\leq cY
for some sufficiently small constant
c
. The smallness of the constant
c
is
usually clear from the context. The notation
X\gg Y
is similarly defined. Note that
our use of
\ll
and
\gg
here is
different
from the usual Vinogradov notation
in number theory or asymptotic analysis.

For a real-valued function
u:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
we denote its usual Lebesgue
L^{p}
-norm by

(2.1)

\displaystyle\|u\|_{p}=\|u\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}=\begin{cases}&\left(\int_{\Omega}|u|^{p}\ dx\right)^{\frac{1}{p}},\quad 1\leq p<\infty;\\
&\operatorname{esssup}_{x\in\Omega}|u(x)|,\quad p=\infty.\end{cases}

Similarly, we use the weak derivative in the following sense: For
u
,
v\in L^{1}_{loc}(\Omega)
, (i.e they are locally integrable);
\forall\phi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega)
, i.e
\phi
is infinitely differentiable (smooth) and compactly supported; and

\int_{\Omega}u(x)\ \partial^{\alpha}\phi(x)\ dx=(-1)^{\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{n}}\int_{\Omega}v(x)\ \phi(x)\ dx,

then
v
is defined to be the weak partial derivative of
u
, denoted by
\partial^{\alpha}u
.
Suppose
u\in L^{p}(\Omega)
and all weak derivatives
\partial^{\alpha}u
exist for
|\alpha|=\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{n}\leq k
, such that
\partial^{\alpha}u\in L^{p}(\Omega)
for
|\alpha|\leq k
, then we denote
u\in W^{k,p}(\Omega)
to be the standard Sobolev space. The corresponding norm of
W^{k,p}(\Omega)
is :

\|u\|_{W^{k,p}(\Omega)}=\left(\sum_{|\alpha|\leq k}\int_{\Omega}|\partial^{\alpha}u|^{p}\ dx\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\ .

For
p=2
case, we use the convention
H^{k}(\Omega)
to denote the space
W^{k,2}(\Omega)
. We often use
D^{m}u
to denote any differential operator
D^{\alpha}u
for any
|\alpha|=m
:
D^{2}
denotes
\partial^{2}_{x_{i}x_{j}}u
for
1\leq i,j\leq d
, as an example.

In this paper we use the following convention for Fourier expansion on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
:

f(x)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\widehat{f}(k)e^{ik\cdot x}\ ,\ \widehat{f}(k)=\int_{\Omega}f(x)e^{-ik\cdot x}\ dx\ .

Taking advantage of the Fourier expansion, we use the well-known equivalent
H^{s}
-norm and
\dot{H}^{s}
-semi-norm of function
f
by

\|f\|_{H^{s}}=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}}\left(\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}(1+|k|^{2s})|\widehat{f}(k)|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\ ,\ \|f\|_{\dot{H}^{s}}=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}}\left(\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}|k|^{2s}|\widehat{f}(k)|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

In addition, for
N\geq 2
, we define

X_{N}=\text{span}\left\{\cos(k\cdot x)\ ,\sin(k\cdot x):\ \ k=(k_{1},k_{2})\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\ ,\ |k|_{\infty}=\max\{|k_{1}|,|k_{2}|\}\leq N\right\}\ .

Lemma 2.1

(Sobolev inequality on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
)
.

Let
0<s<d
and
f\in L^{q}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
for any
\frac{d}{d-s}<p<\infty
, then

\displaystyle\|\left<\nabla\right>^{-s}f\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\lesssim_{s,p,d}\|f\|_{L^{q}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\ ,\ \mbox{where}\ \frac{1}{q}=\frac{1}{p}+\frac{s}{d}\ ,

where
\left<\nabla\right>^{-s}
denotes
(1-\Delta)^{-\frac{s}{2}}
and
A\lesssim_{s,p,d}B
is defined as
A\leq C_{s,p,d}\ B
where
C_{s,p,d}
is a constant dependent on
s,p
and
d
.

Remark 2.2
.

Note that the this Sobolev inequality is a variety of the standard version. Note that on the Fourier side the symbol of
\left<\nabla\right>^{-s}
is given by
(1+|k|^{2})^{-\frac{s}{2}}
.
In particular,
\|f\|_{\infty(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\lesssim\|f\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}
, known as Morrey’s inequality.

Remark 2.3
.

If one further requires that
f\in H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
has zero mean, i.e.
\widehat{f}(0)=0
we have

(2.2)

\displaystyle\|f\|_{4}\leq\|f\|_{2}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\nabla f\|_{2}^{\frac{1}{2}},

and

(2.3)

\displaystyle\|f\|_{6}\leq\|f\|_{2}^{\frac{1}{3}}\|\nabla f\|_{2}^{\frac{2}{3}}.

The proof of (
2.2
) and (
2.3
) follows from Lemma
2.1
and standard interpolation.

Lemma 2.4
.

Suppose
f\in H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
and
f
has zero mean, i.e.
\widehat{f}(0)=0
. Then

(2.4)

\displaystyle\|f\|_{2}\leq\||\nabla|^{-1}f\|_{2}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\nabla f\|_{2}^{\frac{1}{2}},

(2.5)

\displaystyle\|f\|_{2}\leq\|\nabla f\|_{2}.

Proof.

The proof follows from Parseval’s identity directly:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}f\ dx=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}|\nabla|^{-1}f\cdot|\nabla|f\ dx.

Then (
2.4
) follows from the classic Cauchy-Schwarz inequality by noting
\||\nabla|f\|_{2}=\|\nabla f\|_{2}
. (
2.5
) follows from the standard Poincaré inequality (or directly from the Fourier side).
∎

Lemma 2.5

(Log-type interpolation)
.

For all
f\in H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\ ,\ s>1
and suppose
f
has zero mean, i.e.
\widehat{f}(0)=0
, then

\|f\|_{\infty}\leq C_{s}\cdot\left(\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}\sqrt{\log(\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{s}}+3)}+1\right)\ .

Here
C_{s}
is a constant which only depends on s.

Proof.

This lemma is a special case of Lemma 3.1 in
[
9
]
and we only sketch the proof here. Note that
f
is mean-zero, we then consider the Fourier series
f(x)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\sum_{|k|\geq 1}\widehat{f}(k)\ e^{ik\cdot x}
. It then follows that

\displaystyle\|f\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\sum_{|k|\geq 1}|\widehat{f}(k)|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\left(\sum_{1\leq|k|\leq N}|\widehat{f}(k)|+\sum_{|k|>N}|\widehat{f}(k)|\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{1\leq|k|\leq N}(|\widehat{f}(k)\|k|\cdot|k|^{-1})+\sum_{|k|>N}(|\widehat{f}(k)\|k|^{s}\cdot|k|^{-s})

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(\sum_{1\leq|k|\leq N}|\widehat{f}(k)|^{2}|k|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\cdot\left(\sum_{1\leq|k|\leq N}|k|^{-2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\left(\sum_{|k|>N}|\widehat{f}(k)|^{2}|k|^{2s}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\cdot(\sum_{|k|>N}|k|^{-2s})^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{N^{s-1}}\left(\sum_{|k|>N}|\widehat{f}(k)|^{2}|k|^{2s}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\left(\sum_{1\leq|k|\leq N}|\widehat{f}(k)|^{2}|k|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\cdot\sqrt{\log(N+3)}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{N^{s-1}}\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{s}}+\sqrt{\log(N+3)}\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}\ .

If
\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{s}}\leq 3
, we can simply take
N=1
; otherwise take
N^{s-1}
close to
\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{s}}
.
∎

Lemma 2.6

(Discrete Grönwall’s inequality)
.

Let
\tau>0
and
y_{n}\geq 0
,
\alpha_{n}\geq 0
,
\beta_{n}\geq 0
for
n=1,2,3\cdots
. Suppose

\frac{y_{n+1}-y_{n}}{\tau}\leq\alpha_{n}y_{n}+\beta_{n}\ ,\ \forall\ n\geq 0\ .

Then for any
m\geq 1
, we have

\displaystyle y_{m}\leq\exp\left(\tau\sum_{n=0}^{m-1}\alpha_{n}\right)\left(y_{0}+\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\beta_{k}\right)\ .

Proof.

We only sketch the proof here for the sake of completeness. By the assumption, it follows that for
n\geq 0
,

y_{n+1}\leq(1+\alpha_{n}\tau)y_{n}+\tau\beta_{n}\leq e^{\tau\alpha_{n}}y_{n}+\tau\beta_{n};

therefore one can derive that

\exp\left(-\tau\sum_{j=0}^{n}\alpha_{j}\right)y_{n+1}\leq\exp\left(-\tau\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\alpha_{j}\right)y_{n}+\exp\left(-\tau\sum_{j=0}^{n}\alpha_{j}\right)\beta_{n}.

We thus obtain the desired result by performing a telescoping summation.
∎

Lemma 2.7

(
H^{k}
boundedness of the exact CH solution)
.

Assume
u(x,t)
is a smooth solution to the Cahn-Hilliard equation in
\mathbb{T}^{2}
and the initial data
u_{0}\in H^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
for
k\geq 2
. Then,

(2.6)

\sup_{t\geq 0}\|u(t)\|_{H^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}\lesssim_{k}1

where we omit the dependence on
\nu
and
u_{0}
.

Proof.

The proof is standard and we postpone the proof to the Appendix.
∎

3.
Energy stability of the scheme (
1.4
) on the 2D Cahn-Hilliard equation

Recall that the Cahn-Hilliard equation (
CH
) takes the following form:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u=-\nu\Delta^{2}u+\Delta f(u),\\
u(x,0)=u_{0}.\end{cases}

Here
f(u)=u^{3}-u
, and the spatial domain
\Omega
is taken to be the two dimensional
2\pi-
periodic torus
\mathbb{T}^{2}
. The corresponding energy is defined by
E(u)=\int_{\Omega}(\frac{\nu}{2}|\nabla u|^{2}+F(u))\ dx
, where
F(u)=\frac{1}{4}(u^{2}-1)^{2}
, the anti-derivative of
f(u)
. Recall that we consider the stabilized exponential integrator scheme (
1.4
):

(3.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{u^{n+1}-u^{n}}{\tau}+S\Delta^{2}(u^{n+1}-u^{n})=\frac{e^{-\nu\Delta^{2}\tau}-1}{\tau}u^{n}+\frac{1-e^{-\nu\Delta^{2}\tau}}{\tau\nu\Delta}\Pi_{N}f(u^{n}),\\
&u^{0}=\Pi_{N}u_{0}.\end{aligned}\right.

Here
\Pi_{N}
is truncation of Fourier modes of
L^{2}
functions to
|k|_{\infty}\leq N
. Note that here
E^{0}=E(\Pi_{N}u_{0})
while
E_{0}=E(u_{0})
and in general
E_{0}\neq E^{0}
. In particular the following statement holds.

Lemma 3.1
.

Suppose
E^{0}=E(\Pi_{N}u_{0})
and
E_{0}=E(u_{0})
as defined above, the following inequality holds:

\sup_{N}\ E(\Pi_{N}u_{0})\lesssim 1+E_{0},\mbox{where}\ u_{0}\in H^{1}({\mathbb{T}^{2}})\ .

Proof.

We rewrite
\Pi_{N}u_{0}
as
\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\sum_{|k|\leq N}\widehat{u_{0}}(k)e^{ik\cdot x}
, namely the Dirichlet partial sum of
u_{0}
.

\begin{aligned} \|\nabla\left(\Pi_{N}u_{0}\right)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}^{2}=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\sum_{|k|\leq N}|k|^{2}|\widehat{u_{0}}(k)|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\sum_{|k|\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}|k|^{2}|\widehat{u_{0}}(k)|^{2}=\|\nabla\left(u_{0}\right)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}^{2}\end{aligned}\ .

On the potential energy part, by the Sobolev inequality Lemma
2.1
,
\|u_{0}\|_{L^{4}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}\lesssim\|u_{0}\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}
, this shows
u_{0}\in L^{4}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
and hence the Dirichlet partial sum
\Pi_{N}u_{0}
converges to
u_{0}
in
L^{4}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
. Then

\lim_{N}\|\Pi_{N}u_{0}\|_{L^{4}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}=\|u_{0}\|_{L^{4}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}
, which leads to
\sup_{N}\|\Pi_{N}u_{0}\|_{L^{4}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}<\infty
. By the Uniform Boundedness Principle, we derive
\sup_{N}\|\Pi_{N}\|<\infty
, i.e.
\sup_{N}\|\Pi_{N}u_{0}\|_{L^{4}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}\leq c\|u_{0}\|_{L^{4}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}
for an absolute constant
c
. Combining the two estimates above we finish the proof.
∎

We will prove Theorem
1.5
by induction. To start with, let us multiply the equation by
\frac{\nu\Delta\tau}{e^{-\nu\tau\Delta^{2}}-1}(u^{n+1}-u^{n})
and integrate over
\mathbb{T}^{2}
. Then the LHS of (
3.1
) is

(3.2)

\displaystyle\mbox{LHS}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau}\left(u^{n+1}-u^{n},\frac{\nu\Delta\tau}{e^{-\nu\tau\Delta^{2}}-1}(u^{n+1}-u^{n})\right)+S\left(\Delta^{2}(u^{n+1}-u^{n}),\frac{\nu\Delta\tau}{e^{-\nu\tau\Delta^{2}}-1}(u^{n+1}-u^{n})\right)

(3.3)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(u^{n+1}-u^{n},\frac{-\nu\Delta}{1-e^{-\nu\tau\Delta^{2}}}(u^{n+1}-u^{n})\right)+S\left(u^{n+1}-u^{n},\frac{-\nu\Delta^{3}\tau}{1-e^{-\nu\tau\Delta^{2}}}(u^{n+1}-u^{n})\right),

where
(\cdot,\cdot)
is the usual
L^{2}
inner product. We observe that
both the two operators
A=\frac{-\nu\Delta}{1-e^{-\nu\tau\Delta^{2}}}
and
B=\frac{-\nu\Delta^{3}\tau}{1-e^{-\nu\tau\Delta^{2}}}
are positive semi-definite and self-adjoint, therefore we have

(3.4)

\displaystyle\mbox{LHS}=\|\sqrt{A}(u^{n+1}-u^{n})\|_{2}^{2}+S\|\sqrt{B}(u^{n+1}-u^{n})\|_{2}^{2},

where the operator
\sqrt{A}
and
\sqrt{B}
correspond to the following Fourier symbols:

(3.5)

\displaystyle\widehat{\sqrt{A}u}(k)=\sqrt{\frac{\nu|k|^{2}}{1-e^{-\nu|k|^{4}\tau}}}\widehat{u}(k);\quad\widehat{\sqrt{B}u}(k)=\sqrt{\frac{\nu|k|^{6}\tau}{1-e^{-\nu|k|^{4}\tau}}}\widehat{u}(k).

To proceed from here, it is important to examine the behaviors of these two operators. Indeed we state the following lemma.

Lemma 3.2
.

Suppose the operators
\sqrt{A}
and
\sqrt{B}
are defined in (
3.5
) above. Then the following inequalities hold for any function
u\in H^{3}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
and any
v\in H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
with zero-mean:

(3.6)

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{\tau}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\||\nabla|^{-1}v\|_{2}\leq\|\sqrt{A}v\|_{2},\quad\|\nabla u\|_{2}\leq\|\sqrt{B}u\|_{2}.

Proof.

The (
3.6
) can be proved from the Fourier side. In particular, by the Parseval’s identity we have

(3.7)

\displaystyle\|\sqrt{A}v\|_{2}^{2}=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\sum_{|k|\geq 1}\frac{\nu|k|^{2}}{1-e^{-\nu|k|^{4}\tau}}|\widehat{v}(k)|^{2},

therefore it suffices to show

(3.8)

\displaystyle\frac{\nu|k|^{2}}{1-e^{-\nu|k|^{4}\tau}}\geq\frac{1}{\tau|k|^{2}},\quad\mbox{or}\quad\frac{\nu\tau|k|^{4}}{1-e^{-\nu\tau|k|^{4}}}\geq 1.

To show (
3.8
), we can define an 1D function for
x\geq 1
,
h(x)=\frac{\nu\tau x^{4}}{1-e^{-\nu\tau x^{4}}}-1
. By direct computation we can show
h^{\prime}(x)>0
for
x\geq 1
and
h(1)>0
noting
\nu\tau>0
. Therefore (
3.8
) is proved. Then (
3.7
) implies

(3.9)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau}\||\nabla|^{-1}v\|_{2}^{2}\leq\|\sqrt{A}v\|_{2}^{2}.

By the same reason we can show
\|\nabla u\|_{2}\leq\|\sqrt{B}u\|_{2}
. We hereby conclude (
3.6
).
∎

We then continue the proof of Theorem
1.5
. We now focus on the RHS of (
3.1
):

(3.10)

\displaystyle\mbox{RHS}=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{e^{-\nu\Delta^{2}\tau}-1}{\tau}u^{n},\frac{-\nu\Delta\tau}{1-e^{-\nu\tau\Delta^{2}}}(u^{n+1}-u^{n})\right)+\left(\frac{1-e^{-\nu\Delta^{2}\tau}}{\nu\tau\Delta}\Pi_{N}f(u^{n}),\frac{-\nu\Delta\tau}{1-e^{-\nu\tau\Delta^{2}}}(u^{n+1}-u^{n})\right)

(3.11)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nu\left(u^{n},\Delta(u^{n+1}-u^{n})\right)-\left(\Pi_{N}f(u^{n}),u^{n+1}-u^{n}\right).

Note that
u^{n+1},u^{n}\in X_{N}
, we have

(3.12)

\displaystyle\mbox{RHS}=\nu\left(\nabla u^{n},\nabla(u^{n}-u^{n+1})\right)-\left(f(u^{n}),u^{n+1}-u^{n}\right).

Using the identity
2a(a-b)=a^{2}-b^{2}+(a-b)^{2}
, we derive from (
3.12
) that

(3.13)

\displaystyle\mbox{RHS}=\frac{\nu}{2}(\|\nabla u^{n}\|_{2}^{2}-\|\nabla u^{n+1}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\nabla u^{n}-\nabla u^{n+1}\|_{2}^{2})-(f(u^{n}),u^{n+1}-u^{n}).

To proceed further, by the fundamental theorem of calculus we arrive at

(3.14)

\displaystyle F(u^{n+1})-F(u^{n})

\displaystyle=f(u^{n})(u^{n+1}-u^{n})+\int_{u^{n}}^{u^{n+1}}f^{\prime}(s)(u^{n+1}-s)\ ds

\displaystyle=f(u^{n})(u^{n+1}-u^{n})+\int_{u^{n}}^{u^{n+1}}(3s^{2}-1)(u^{n+1}-s)\ ds

\displaystyle=f(u^{n})(u^{n+1}-u^{n})+\frac{1}{4}(u^{n+1}-u^{n})^{2}\left(3(u^{n})^{2}+(u^{n+1})^{2}+2u^{n}u^{n+1}-2\right)\ .

Combining the estimates (
3.4
), (
3.13
) and (
3.14
) we arrive at

(3.15)

\displaystyle\|\sqrt{A}(u^{n+1}-u^{n}\|_{2}^{2}+S\|\sqrt{B}(u^{n+1}-u^{n})\|_{2}^{2}+E(u^{n+1})-E(u^{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\nu}{2}\|\nabla(u^{n+1}-u^{n})\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\left((u^{n+1}-u^{n})^{2},3(u^{n})^{2}+(u^{n+1})^{2}+2u^{n}u^{n+1}-2\right).

By further considering (
3.6
) and (
3.15
) we indeed have

(3.16)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau}\||\nabla|^{-1}(u^{n+1}-u^{n})\|_{2}^{2}+(S-\frac{\nu}{2})\|\nabla(u^{n+1}-u^{n})\|_{2}^{2}+E(u^{n+1})-E(u^{n})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4}\left((u^{n+1}-u^{n})^{2},3(u^{n})^{2}+(u^{n+1})^{2}+2u^{n}u^{n+1}-2\right).

Moreover
by Lemma
2.4
, we derive that

(3.17)

\displaystyle(\sqrt{\frac{2S}{\tau}}+\frac{S}{2}-\frac{\nu}{2})\|u^{n+1}-u^{n}\|_{2}^{2}+E(u^{n+1})-E(u^{n})\leq\frac{1}{4}\left((u^{n+1}-u^{n})^{2},3(u^{n})^{2}+(u^{n+1})^{2}+2u^{n}u^{n+1}-2\right).

Therefore to show the energy dissipation, it suffices to take

(3.18)

\begin{aligned} \sqrt{\frac{2S}{\tau}}+\frac{S}{2}-\frac{\nu}{2}\geq\frac{3}{2}\max\left\{\|u^{n}\|_{\infty}^{2}\ ,\ \|u^{n+1}\|_{\infty}^{2}\right\}\end{aligned}\ .

We rewrite the numerical scheme (
1.4
) as follows:

(3.19)

\displaystyle u^{n+1}=\frac{S\tau\Delta^{2}+e^{-\nu\Delta^{2}\tau}}{1+S\tau\Delta^{2}}u^{n}+\frac{1-e^{-\nu\Delta^{2}\tau}}{(1+S\tau\Delta^{2})\nu\Delta}\Pi_{N}f(u^{n})

By the interpolation lemma (Lemma
2.5
), to control
\|u^{n+1}\|_{\infty}
and
\|u^{n}\|_{\infty}
, we can only focus on the
\dot{H}^{1}
-norm and
\dot{H}^{\frac{3}{2}}
-norm.

Lemma 3.3
.

There is an absolute constant
c_{1}>0
such that for any
n\geq 0

(3.20)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\|u^{n+1}\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{3}{2}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}&\leq c_{1}\cdot\left(\sqrt{\frac{N}{\nu}}+\frac{1}{S}\right)\cdot(E^{n}+1)\\
\|u^{n+1}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}&\leq\left(\frac{1}{S}+\frac{1}{S}\|u^{n}\|^{2}_{\infty}\right)\cdot\|u^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}+\|u^{n}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}\ .\end{cases}

Proof.

It suffices to consider Fourier modes
1\leq|k|\leq N
from the Fourier side. We then obtain that

(3.21)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{(S\tau|k|^{4}+e^{-\nu\tau|k|^{4}})|k|^{\frac{3}{2}}}{1+S\tau|k|^{4}}\leq|k|\cdot N^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
&\frac{(1-e^{-\nu\tau|k|^{4}})|k|^{\frac{3}{2}}}{(1+S\tau|k|^{4})\nu|k|^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{S|k|^{\frac{1}{2}}},\end{aligned}\right.

where we have observed that
1-e^{-\nu\tau|k|^{4}}\leq\nu\tau|k|^{4}
. It then follows that

(3.22)

\|u^{n+1}\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{3}{2}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}\leq N^{\frac{1}{2}}\|u^{n}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}+\frac{1}{S}\|\big\langle\nabla\big\rangle^{-\frac{1}{2}}f(u^{n})\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}.

Here the notaion
\big\langle\nabla\big\rangle^{s}=(1-\Delta)^{\frac{s}{2}}
, corresponds to the Fourier side
(1+|k|^{2})^{s/2}
.
Note that by the Sobolev inequality Lemma
2.1
we have

\displaystyle\|\big\langle\nabla\big\rangle^{-\frac{1}{2}}f(u^{n})\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|f(u^{n})\|_{L^{\frac{4}{3}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}=\|(u^{n})^{3}-u^{n}\|_{L^{\frac{4}{3}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}((u^{n})^{3}-u^{n})^{\frac{4}{3}}\ dx\right)^{\frac{3}{4}}

\displaystyle\lesssim E^{n}+1\ .

Therefore (
3.22
) can be estimated by the following:

\|u^{n+1}\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{3}{2}}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}\lesssim\left(\sqrt{\frac{N}{\nu}}+\frac{1}{S}\right)(E^{n}+1).

Similarly, we get

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{(S\tau|k|^{4}+e^{-\nu\tau|k|^{4}})|k|}{1+S\tau|k|^{4}}\leq|k|\\
&\frac{(1-e^{-\nu\tau|k|^{4}})|k|}{(1+S\tau|k|^{4})\nu|k|^{2}}\leq\frac{\nu\tau|k|^{4}}{S\tau\nu|k|^{6}}|k|=\frac{1}{S|k|}\ .\end{aligned}\right.

This implies for
|k|\geq 1
that

\displaystyle\|u^{n+1}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}

\displaystyle\leq\|u^{n}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}+\frac{1}{S}\|f(u^{n})\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}

\displaystyle\leq\|u^{n}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}+\frac{1}{S}\|(1-(u^{n})^{2})\cdot u^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}

\displaystyle\leq\|u^{n}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}+(\frac{1}{S}+\frac{\|u^{n}\|^{2}_{\infty}}{S})\|u^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}.

∎

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

Now we will complete the proof for
Theorem

1.5
by induction:

Step 1:
The induction
n\to n+1
step.
Assuming
E^{n}\leq E^{n-1}\leq\cdots\leq E^{0}
and
E^{n}\leq\sup_{N}\ E(\Pi_{N}u_{0})
, we will show

. This implies
\|u^{n}\|^{2}_{\dot{H}^{1}}=\|\nabla u^{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\leq\frac{2E^{n}}{\nu}\leq\frac{2E^{0}}{\nu}
. By Lemma
2.5
and adopting the notation
f\lesssim_{E^{0}}g
to denote that
f\leq C(E^{0})\cdot g
for some constant
C(E^{0})
depending only on
E^{0}
, we have

(3.23)

\displaystyle\|u^{n}\|^{2}_{\infty}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|u^{n}\|^{2}_{\dot{H}^{1}}\left(\sqrt{\log(3+c_{1}\left(\sqrt{\frac{N}{\nu}}+\frac{1}{S}\right)(E^{n-1}+1))}\right)^{2}+1

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{2E^{0}}{\nu}\left(1+\log(S)+\log(\frac{1}{\nu})+(\log(N))\right)+1

\displaystyle\lesssim_{E^{0}}\nu^{-1}\left(1+\log(S)+\log(\frac{1}{\nu})+\log(N)\right)+1\ .

Define
m_{0}\coloneqq\nu^{-1}\left(1+\log(S)+|\log(\nu)|+\log(N)\right)
, and note that
E^{0}\leq\sup_{N}\ E(\Pi_{N}u_{0})\lesssim E_{0}+1
, the inequality above (
3.23
) is then rewritten as follows:

\|u^{n}\|^{2}_{\infty}\lesssim_{E_{0}}m_{0}+1.

On the other hand by Lemma
3.3
,

(3.24)

\displaystyle\|u^{n+1}\|_{\infty}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim 1+\|u^{n+1}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}^{2}\log(3+\|u^{n+1}\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{3}{2}}})

\displaystyle\lesssim 1+(1+\frac{1+\|u^{n}\|^{2}_{\infty}}{S})^{2}\|u^{n}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}^{2}\log(3+\|u^{n+1}\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{3}{2}}})

\displaystyle\lesssim_{E_{0}}1+(1+\frac{m_{0}+1}{S})^{2}\frac{1}{\nu}\log(3+\|u^{n+1}\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{3}{2}}})

\displaystyle\lesssim_{E_{0}}1+(1+\frac{m_{0}}{S})^{2}m_{0}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{E_{0}}1+\frac{m_{0}^{3}}{S^{2}}+m_{0}.

The sufficient condition (
3.18
) thus becomes

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\sqrt{\frac{2S}{\tau}}+\frac{S}{2}-\frac{\nu}{2}\geq C(E_{0})\left(1+m_{0}+\frac{m_{0}^{3}}{S^{2}}\right),\\
&m_{0}=\nu^{-1}\left(1+\log(S)+|\log(\nu)|+\log(N)\right)\ .\end{aligned}\right.

It then suffices for us to choose
S
such that

S\gg_{E_{0}}m_{0}+\nu=\nu^{-1}\left(1+\log(S)+|\log(\nu)|+\log(N)\right)+\nu\ ,

where
B\gg_{E_{0}}D
means there exists a large constant depending only on
E_{0}
. In fact, for
\nu\gtrsim 1
, we can take
S\gg_{E_{0}}\nu+\log(N)
; if
0<\nu\ll 1
, we will choose
S=C_{E_{0}}\cdot\nu^{-1}(|\log\nu|+\log N)
, where
C_{E_{0}}
is a large constant depending only on
E_{0}
. Therefore it suffices to choose

(3.25)

S=C_{E_{0}}\nu^{-1}(\log N+|\log\nu|)+\nu.

Step 2:
We now check the induction base step
n=1
. Clearly we only need to check

\sqrt{\frac{2S}{\tau}}+\frac{S}{2}-\frac{\nu}{2}\geq\|\Pi_{N}u_{0}\|^{2}_{\infty}+\frac{1}{2}\|u^{1}\|^{2}_{\infty}.

By Lemma
3.3
,

\displaystyle\|u^{1}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+\frac{1}{S}+\frac{1}{S}\|\Pi_{N}u_{0}\|^{2}_{\infty}\right)\cdot\|u_{0}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+\frac{1}{S}+\frac{1}{S}\|\Pi_{N}u_{0}\|^{2}_{\infty}\right)\cdot\sqrt{\frac{2E^{0}}{\nu}}\ .

As a result,

\displaystyle\|u^{1}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\lesssim 1+\|u^{1}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}\sqrt{\log(3+\|u^{1}\|_{\dot{H}^{\frac{3}{2}}})}

\displaystyle\lesssim 1+\left(1+\frac{1}{S}+\frac{1}{S}\|\Pi_{N}u_{0}\|^{2}_{\infty}\right)\sqrt{\frac{2E^{0}}{\nu}}\sqrt{\log\left(3+c_{1}\left(\sqrt{\frac{S}{\nu}}+\frac{1}{S}\right)(E_{0}+1)\right)}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{E_{0}}1+\left(1+\frac{1}{S}+\frac{1}{S}\|\Pi_{N}u_{0}\|^{2}_{\infty}\right)\cdot\nu^{-\frac{1}{2}}\cdot\sqrt{1+\log(S)+|\log(\nu)|+\log N}.

Thus we need to choose
S
such that

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{2S}{\tau}}+\frac{S}{2}-\frac{\nu}{2}

\displaystyle\geq\|\Pi_{N}u_{0}\|^{2}_{\infty}+C_{E_{0}}\cdot\left(1+\frac{1}{S}+\frac{1}{S}\|\Pi_{N}u_{0}\|^{2}_{\infty}\right)^{2}\cdot\nu^{-1}

\displaystyle\cdot\left(1+\log(S)+|\log(\nu)|+\log N\right)\ ,

where
C_{E_{0}}
is a large constant depending only on
E_{0}
. Note that by Morrey’s inequality,

\|\Pi_{N}u_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}\lesssim\|\Pi_{N}u_{0}\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}\lesssim\|u_{0}\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}.

Then it suffices to take
S
such that

(3.26)

S\gg_{E_{0}}\|u_{0}\|^{2}_{H^{2}}+\nu^{-1}(|\log(\nu)|+\log N)+\nu\ .

This completes the induction and hence proves the theorem.

∎

4.
L^{2}
error estimate for the scheme (
1.4
) on the 2D Cahn-Hilliard equation

In this section, we will like to study the
L^{2}
error between the semi-implicit numerical solution and the exact PDE solution in the domain
\mathbb{T}^{2}
and eventually prove Theorem
1.7
. To start with, we consider the auxiliary
L^{2}
error estimate for near solutions.

4.1.
Auxiliary
L^{2}
error estimate for near solutions

Consider the following auxiliary system
u^{n}
and
v^{n}
in
X_{N}
for the first order scheme:

(4.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{u^{n+1}-u^{n}}{\tau}+S\Delta^{2}(u^{n+1}-u^{n})=\frac{e^{-\nu\Delta^{2}\tau}-1}{\tau}u^{n}+\frac{1-e^{-\nu\Delta^{2}\tau}}{\tau\nu\Delta}\Pi_{N}f(u^{n})+\Delta G^{1}_{n}\\
&\frac{v^{n+1}-v^{n}}{\tau}+S\Delta^{2}(v^{n+1}-v^{n})=\frac{e^{-\nu\Delta^{2}\tau}-1}{\tau}v^{n}+\frac{1-e^{-\nu\Delta^{2}\tau}}{\tau\nu\Delta}\Pi_{N}f(v^{n})+\Delta G^{2}_{n}\\
&u^{0}=u_{0}\ ,\ v^{0}=v_{0}\ .\end{aligned}\right.

We define that
G_{n}=G^{1}_{n}-G^{2}_{n}
.

Proposition 4.1
.

For solutions of (
4.1
), assume for some
N_{1}>0
,

(4.2)

\sup_{n\geq 0}\|\nabla u^{n}\|_{2}+\sup_{n\geq 0}\|v^{n}\|_{\infty}+\sup_{n\geq 0}\|\nabla v^{n}\|_{2}\leq N_{1}\ .

Then for any
m\geq 1
,

(4.3)

\displaystyle\|u^{m}-v^{m}\|^{2}_{2}=\|\varepsilon^{m}\|^{2}_{2}\leq y_{m}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\exp\left(m\tau\cdot\frac{C(1+N_{1}^{4})}{\nu}\right)\cdot\left(\|Lu_{0}-Lv_{0}\|_{2}^{2}+S\tau\|Mu_{0}-Mv_{0}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{4}{\nu}\tau\sum_{n=0}^{m-1}\|L^{2}G_{n}\|_{2}^{2}\right).

where
C>0
is an absolute constant and

are defined from the Fourier side as below:

(4.4)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}&\widehat{Lu}(k)=\sqrt{\frac{\nu|k|^{4}\tau}{1-e^{-\nu|k|^{4}\tau}}}\widehat{u}(k)\\
&\widehat{Mu}(k)=\sqrt{\frac{\nu|k|^{8}\tau}{1-e^{-\nu|k|^{4}\tau}}}\widehat{u}(k).\end{cases}

The operator
L^{2}=L\circ L
, i.e.
L
composite with
L
.

Lemma 4.2
.

We remark here by similar arguments in Lemma
3.2
we have for
g,h\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
that the following holds:

(4.5)

\|\Delta g\|_{2}\leq\|Mg\|_{2}\lesssim\|\Delta g\|_{2}+\sqrt{\tau}\|\Delta^{2}g\|_{2},\quad\|h\|_{2}\leq\|Lh\|_{2}\lesssim\|h\|_{2}+\sqrt{\tau}\|\Delta h\|_{2}.

Proof of Proposition
4.1
.

We assume Lemma
4.2
for the time being. Write
\varepsilon^{n}=u^{n}-v^{n}
. Then

(4.6)

\frac{\varepsilon^{n+1}-\varepsilon^{n}}{\tau}+S\Delta^{2}(\varepsilon^{n+1}-\varepsilon^{n})=\frac{e^{-\nu\Delta^{2}\tau}-1}{\tau}\varepsilon^{n}+\frac{1-e^{-\nu\Delta^{2}\tau}}{\tau\nu\Delta}\Pi_{N}(f(u^{n})-f(v^{n}))+\Delta G_{n}\ .

Taking
L^{2}
-inner product with
\frac{\nu\Delta^{2}\tau}{1-e^{-\nu\Delta^{2}\tau}}\varepsilon^{n+1}
on both sides of (
4.6
) and recalling similar computations in previous section, we have the LHS of (
4.6
) is

(4.7)

\displaystyle\mbox{LHS}=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\varepsilon^{n+1}-\varepsilon^{n}}{\tau}+S\Delta^{2}(\varepsilon^{n+1}-\varepsilon^{n}),\ \frac{\nu\Delta^{2}\tau}{1-e^{-\nu\Delta^{2}\tau}}\varepsilon^{n+1}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\tau}(\|L\varepsilon^{n+1}\|_{2}^{2}-\|L\varepsilon^{n}\|_{2}^{2}+\|L(\varepsilon^{n+1}-\varepsilon^{n})\|_{2}^{2})+\frac{S}{2}(\|M\varepsilon^{n+1}\|_{2}^{2}-\|M\varepsilon^{n}\|_{2}^{2}+\|M(\varepsilon^{n+1}-\varepsilon^{n})\|_{2}^{2}).

Similarly, the RHS of (
4.6
) can be given as below

(4.8)

\displaystyle\mbox{RHS}=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{e^{-\nu\Delta^{2}\tau}-1}{\tau}\varepsilon^{n}+\Delta G_{n}+\frac{1-e^{-\nu\Delta^{2}\tau}}{\tau\nu\Delta}\Pi_{N}(f(u^{n})-f(v^{n})),\ \frac{\nu\Delta^{2}\tau}{1-e^{-\nu\Delta^{2}\tau}}\varepsilon^{n+1}\right)

\displaystyle\coloneqq

\displaystyle I_{1}+I_{2}+I_{3},

where
(.\ ,\ .)
denotes the
L^{2}
inner product.
We then estimate different parts
I_{1},I_{2}
and
I_{3}
as follows.

(4.9)

\displaystyle I_{1}=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{e^{-\nu\Delta^{2}\tau}-1}{\tau}\varepsilon^{n},\frac{\nu\Delta^{2}\tau}{1-e^{-\nu\Delta^{2}\tau}}\varepsilon^{n+1}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\nu(\Delta\varepsilon^{n},\Delta\varepsilon^{n+1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\nu(\Delta\varepsilon^{n+1},\Delta\varepsilon^{n+1})+\nu(\Delta\varepsilon^{n+1},\Delta\varepsilon^{n+1}-\Delta\varepsilon^{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\nu}{2}(\|\Delta\varepsilon^{n+1}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\Delta\varepsilon^{n}\|_{2}^{2})+\frac{\nu}{2}\|\Delta\varepsilon^{n+1}-\Delta\varepsilon^{n}\|_{2}^{2}.

Similarly, by Hölder’s inequality we obtain that

(4.10)

I_{2}=\left(\Delta G_{n},\frac{\nu\Delta^{2}\tau}{1-e^{-\nu\Delta^{2}\tau}}\varepsilon^{n+1}\right)\leq\|\Delta\varepsilon^{n+1}\|_{2}\|L^{2}G_{n}\|_{2}\leq\frac{2}{\nu}\|L^{2}G_{n}\|^{2}_{2}+\frac{\nu}{8}\|\Delta\varepsilon^{n+1}\|_{2}^{2},

where
L^{2}(G_{n})=L(LG_{n})
. On the other hand, note that
\Pi_{N}
is a self-adjoint operator
\left(\Pi_{N}f,\ g\right)=\left(f,\ \Pi_{N}g\right)
, since it is just an
N
-th Fourier mode truncation. Therefore we have

(4.11)

\displaystyle I_{3}=\left(\frac{1-e^{-\nu\Delta^{2}\tau}}{\tau\nu\Delta}\Pi_{N}(f(u^{n})-f(v^{n})),\frac{\nu\Delta^{2}\tau}{1-e^{-\nu\Delta^{2}\tau}}\varepsilon^{n+1}\right)=\left(f(u^{n})-f(v^{n}),\Delta\Pi_{N}\varepsilon^{n+1}\right).

Then by the fundamental theorem of calculus, we have

(4.12)

\displaystyle f(u^{n})-f(v^{n})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}f^{\prime}(v^{n}+s\varepsilon^{n})ds\cdot\varepsilon^{n}

\displaystyle=(a_{1}+a_{2}(v^{n})^{2})\varepsilon^{n}+a_{3}v^{n}(\varepsilon^{n})^{2}+a_{4}(\varepsilon^{n})^{3}\ ,

where
a_{i}
are exact constants can be computed. Note that we will denote
C
to be an absolute constant whose value may vary in different lines. To proceed further we indeed have by (
4.2
) that

(4.13)

\displaystyle|\left((a_{1}+a_{2}(v^{n})^{2})\varepsilon^{n}\ ,\ \Delta\Pi_{N}\varepsilon^{n+1}\right)|

\displaystyle\leq C(1+\|v^{n}\|_{\infty}^{2})\|\Delta\varepsilon^{n+1}\|_{2}\|\varepsilon^{n}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C(1+N_{1}^{4})}{\nu}\|\varepsilon^{n}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\nu}{8}\|\Delta\varepsilon^{n+1}\|_{2}^{2}.

Moreover, the other two terms can be estimated similarly. In particular, recalling (
2.2
) that for
f
defined in
\mathbb{T}^{2}
with zero mean one has
\|f\|_{4}^{2}\lesssim\|f\|_{2}\|\nabla f\|_{2}
. Therefore we have

(4.14)

\displaystyle|\left(a_{3}v^{n}(\varepsilon^{n})^{2}\ ,\Delta\Pi_{N}\varepsilon^{n+1}\right)|

\displaystyle\leq C\|v^{n}\|_{\infty}\|\varepsilon^{n}\|_{4}^{2}\|\Delta\varepsilon^{n+1}\|_{2}\leq\frac{CN_{1}^{2}}{\nu}\|\varepsilon^{n}\|_{4}^{4}+\frac{\nu}{8}\|\Delta\varepsilon^{n+1}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\frac{N_{1}^{2}}{\nu}\|\varepsilon^{n}\|_{2}^{2}\|\nabla\varepsilon^{n}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\nu}{8}\|\Delta\varepsilon^{n+1}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{CN_{1}^{4}}{\nu}\|\varepsilon^{n}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\nu}{8}\|\Delta\varepsilon^{n+1}\|_{2}^{2}.

Similarly, recalling (
2.3
) that we also have

(4.15)

\displaystyle|\left(a_{4}(\varepsilon^{n})^{3}\ ,\Delta\Pi_{N}\varepsilon^{n+1}\right)|

\displaystyle\leq C\|\varepsilon^{n}\|_{6}^{3}\|\Delta\varepsilon^{n+1}\|_{2}\leq\frac{C}{\nu}\|\varepsilon^{n}\|_{6}^{6}+\frac{\nu}{8}\|\Delta\varepsilon^{n+1}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{\nu}\|\varepsilon^{n}\|_{2}^{2}\|\nabla\varepsilon^{n}\|_{2}^{4}+\frac{\nu}{8}\|\Delta\varepsilon^{n+1}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{CN_{1}^{4}}{\nu}\|\varepsilon^{n}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\nu}{8}\|\Delta\varepsilon^{n+1}\|_{2}^{2}.

One can then conclude from (
4.12
), (
4.13
), (
4.14
) and (
4.15
) that

(4.16)

|I_{3}|=|\left(f(u^{n})-f(v^{n}),\Delta\Pi_{N}\varepsilon^{n+1}\right)|\leq\frac{C(1+N_{1}^{4})}{\nu}\|\varepsilon^{n}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{3\nu}{8}\|\Delta\varepsilon^{n+1}\|_{2}^{2}.

Collecting the estimates (
4.8
), (
4.9
), (
4.10
) and (
4.16
), we get

(4.17)

\displaystyle\mbox{RHS}\leq\frac{\nu}{2}\|\Delta\varepsilon^{n+1}-\Delta\varepsilon^{n}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{2}{\nu}\|L^{2}G_{n}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{C(1+N_{1}^{4})}{\nu}\|\varepsilon^{n}\|_{2}^{2}.

Taking the same choice of
S
as in Theorem
1.5
, it then follows by (
4.7
) and (
4.17
) that

(4.18)

\displaystyle\frac{\|L\varepsilon^{n+1}\|^{2}_{2}-\|L\varepsilon^{n}\|_{2}^{2}}{\tau}+S(\|M\varepsilon^{n+1}\|^{2}_{2}-\|M\varepsilon^{n}\|_{2}^{2})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{4}{\nu}\|L^{2}G_{n}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{C(1+N_{1}^{4})}{\nu}\|\varepsilon^{n}\|_{2}^{2},

where
C
is an absolute positive constant that can be computed exactly. Then by defining

(4.19)

\displaystyle y_{n}=\|L\varepsilon^{n}\|_{2}^{2}+\tau S\|M\varepsilon^{n}\|_{2}^{2},

\displaystyle\alpha=\frac{C(1+N_{1}^{4})}{\nu},

\displaystyle\beta_{n}=\frac{4}{\nu}\|L^{2}G_{n}\|_{2}^{2},

(
4.18
) thus can be formulated into

\frac{y_{n+1}-y_{n}}{\tau}\leq\alpha y_{n}+\beta_{n}\ .

Applying discrete Grönwall’s inequality Lemma
2.6
, we arrive at

(4.20)

\displaystyle\|u^{m}-v^{m}\|^{2}_{2}=\|\varepsilon^{m}\|^{2}_{2}\leq y_{m}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\exp\left(m\tau\cdot\frac{C(1+N_{1}^{4})}{\nu}\right)\cdot\left(\|Lu_{0}-Lv_{0}\|_{2}^{2}+S\tau\|Mu_{0}-Mv_{0}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{4}{\nu}\tau\sum_{n=0}^{m-1}\|L^{2}G_{n}\|_{2}^{2}\right).

∎

Remark 4.3
.

The proof of Proposition
4.1
only requires the uniform boundedness of
\|\nabla u^{n}\|_{2}+\|\nabla v^{n}\|_{2}
rather than
\|\Delta u^{n}\|_{2}+\|\Delta v^{n}\|_{2}
. It somehow makes sense by considering the equation (
1.4
) where the nonlinear part requires lower regularity assumption and the linear part can be controlled by its sign.

Proof of Lemma
4.2
.

First of all the same arguments from Lemma
3.2
give

\displaystyle\|\Delta g\|_{2}\leq\|Mg\|_{2},\quad\|h\|_{2}\leq\|Lh\|_{2}.

To see the rest, we consider the Fourier series of
Lh
again:

(4.21)

\displaystyle\|Lh\|_{2}^{2}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\sum_{|k|\geq 1}\frac{\nu|k|^{4}\tau}{1-e^{-\nu|k|^{4}\tau}}|\widehat{h}(k)|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}(\sum_{\nu|k|^{4}\tau\leq\log 2}+\sum_{\nu|k|^{4}\tau\geq\log 2}\frac{\nu|k|^{4}\tau}{1-e^{-\nu|k|^{4}\tau}}|\widehat{h}(k)|^{2})

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\sum_{\nu|k|^{4}\tau\leq\log 2}\frac{\nu|k|^{4}\tau}{1-e^{-\nu|k|^{4}\tau}}|\widehat{h}(k)|^{2}+\tau\sum_{\nu|k|^{4}\tau\geq\log 2}|k|^{4}|\widehat{h}(k)|^{2}.

The high frequency part can be controlled by
\|\Delta h\|_{2}
by using the standard Parseval’s
identity. For the lower frequency part, we then consider the function
\phi(x)=\frac{x}{1-e^{-x}}
. By direct computation, we see that
\phi^{\prime}(x)>0
for
x>0
which shows that
\phi(x)
is monotone increasing. Therefore we have

(4.22)

\displaystyle\sum_{\nu|k|^{4}\tau\leq\log 2}\frac{\nu|k|^{4}\tau}{1-e^{-\nu|k|^{4}\tau}}|\widehat{h}(k)|^{2}\lesssim\sum_{|k|\geq 1}|\widehat{h}(k)|^{2}.

Collecting the estimates (
4.21
) and (
4.22
) we then get the desired result. The same reason gives

\displaystyle\|Mg\|_{2}\lesssim\|\Delta g\|_{2}+\sqrt{\tau}\|\Delta^{2}g\|_{2}.

∎

4.2.
L^{2}
error estimate of the 2D Cahn-Hilliard equation

In this section, to simplify the notation, we will write
x\lesssim y
if
x\leq C(\nu,u_{0})\ y
for a constant
C
depending on
\nu
and
u_{0}
.
We consider the system

(4.23)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{u^{n+1}-u^{n}}{\tau}+S\Delta^{2}(u^{n+1}-u^{n})=\frac{e^{-\nu\Delta^{2}\tau}-1}{\tau}u^{n}+\frac{1-e^{-\nu\Delta^{2}\tau}}{\tau\nu\Delta}\Pi_{N}f(u^{n})\\
&\partial_{t}u=-\nu\Delta^{2}u+\Delta(f(u))\\
&u^{0}=\Pi_{N}u_{0}\ ,\ u(0)=u_{0}\ .\end{aligned}\right.

In order to prove Theorem
1.7
, it is clear that we shall estimate
G_{n}
introduced in (
4.1
) from the previous proposition. Note that for a one-variable function
h(t)
, one has the formula:

(4.24)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}h(t)=h(t_{n})+\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}h^{\prime}(t)\cdot(t_{n+1}-t)\ dt\\
&\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}h(t)=h(t_{n+1})+\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}h^{\prime}(t)\cdot(t_{n}-t)\ dt\ .\end{aligned}\right.

Using the formula (
4.24
) above and integrating the Cahn-Hilliard equation (
CH
) on the time interval
[t_{n},t_{n+1}]
, we get

(4.25)

\displaystyle\frac{u(t_{n+1})-u(t_{n})}{\tau}=

\displaystyle-\nu\Delta^{2}u(t_{n})-S\Delta^{2}\left(u(t_{n+1})-u(t_{n})\right)

\displaystyle+\Delta\Pi_{N}f(u)(t_{n})+\Delta\Pi_{>N}f(u)(t_{n})+\Delta Q_{n}\ ,

where
\Pi_{>N}=id-\Pi_{N}
, the large mode truncation operator, and

(4.26)

\displaystyle Q_{n}=-\frac{\nu}{\tau}\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}\partial_{t}\Delta u\cdot(t_{n+1}-t)\ dt+\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}\partial_{t}(f(u))(t_{n+1}-t)\ dt+S\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}\partial_{t}\Delta u\ dt\ .

We then rewrite (
4.25
) into the following form:

(4.27)

\displaystyle\frac{u(t_{n+1})-u(t_{n})}{\tau}+

\displaystyle S\Delta^{2}\left(u(t_{n+1})-u(t_{n})\right)=\frac{e^{-\nu\Delta^{2}\tau}-1}{\tau}u(t_{n})+\frac{1-e^{-\nu\Delta^{2}\tau}}{\tau\nu\Delta}\Pi_{N}f(u(t_{n}))

\displaystyle+\left(\frac{1-e^{-\nu\Delta^{2}\tau}}{\tau}-\nu\Delta^{2}\right)u(t_{n})+\left(\Delta+\frac{e^{-\nu\Delta^{2}\tau}-1}{\nu\tau\Delta}\right)\Pi_{N}f(u(t_{n}))

\displaystyle+\Delta\Pi_{>N}f(u)(t_{n})+\Delta Q_{n}\ ,

For the sake of simplicity, we denote
H_{1},H_{2},H_{3}
to be the following quantities:

(4.28)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}&H_{1}\coloneqq\left(\frac{1-e^{-\nu\Delta^{2}\tau}}{\tau\Delta}-\nu\Delta\right)u(t_{n})=\frac{1-\nu\Delta^{2}\tau-e^{-\nu\Delta^{2}\tau}}{\tau\Delta}u(t_{n}),\\
&H_{2}\coloneqq\left(1+\frac{e^{-\nu\Delta^{2}\tau}-1}{\nu\tau\Delta^{2}}\right)\Pi_{N}f(u)(t_{n}),\\
&H_{3}\coloneqq\Pi_{>N}f(u)(t_{n}).\end{cases}

In order to prove Theorem
1.7
, we now estimate
\|L^{2}G_{n}\|_{2}
by estimating
\|L^{2}Q_{n}\|_{2}
and
\|L^{2}H_{i}\|_{2}
,
i=1,2,3
. Recall that
L^{2}u=\frac{\nu\Delta^{2}\tau}{1-e^{-\nu\Delta^{2}\tau}}u
.

Proof of Theorem
1.7
.

By Theorem
1.5
and Lemma
2.7
,
\sup_{n\geq 0}\|u^{n}\|_{H^{1}}\lesssim 1
and
\sup_{t>0}\|u(t)\|_{H^{s}}\lesssim 1
for
s\geq 10
given in the statement. Thus the assumptions of Proposition
4.1
(auxiliary
L^{2}
error estimate proposition) are satisfied for (
4.23
) and (
4.27
). Recall that

\displaystyle Q_{n}=-\frac{\nu}{\tau}\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}\partial_{t}\Delta u\cdot(t_{n+1}-t)\ dt+\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}\partial_{t}(f(u))(t_{n+1}-t)\ dt+S\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}\partial_{t}\Delta u\ dt\ .

Recalling (
4.5
) that we can control
\|L^{2}Q_{n}\|_{2}\lesssim\|Q_{n}\|_{2}+\sqrt{\tau}\|\Delta Q_{n}\|_{2}+\tau\|\Delta^{2}Q_{n}\|_{2}
as follows:

(4.29)

\displaystyle\|Q_{n}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}\|\partial_{t}\Delta u\|_{2}\ dt+\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}\|\partial_{t}(f(u))\|_{2}\ dt+S\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}\|\partial_{t}\Delta u\|_{2}\ dt

\displaystyle\lesssim(1+S)\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}\|\partial_{t}\Delta u\|_{2}\ dt+\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}\|\partial_{t}u\|_{2}\ dt\cdot\|f^{\prime}(u)\|_{L^{\infty}_{t}L^{\infty}_{x}}.

Note that by Lemma
2.7
,

\|\partial_{t}u\|_{2}+\|\partial_{t}\Delta u\|_{2}+\|f^{\prime}(u)\|_{\infty}\lesssim 1\ .

Therefore we can estimate (
4.29
) as following

\displaystyle\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}\|\partial_{t}\Delta u\|_{2}\ dt\lesssim\left(\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}\|\partial_{t}\Delta u\|_{2}^{2}\ dt\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\cdot\sqrt{\tau}\ .

Therefore when
t_{m}\geq 1
, it is not hard to see that

(4.30)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{m-1}\|Q_{n}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{n=0}^{m-1}\left(\tau(1+S)^{2}\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}\|\partial_{t}\Delta u\|_{2}^{2}\ dt+\tau\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}\|\partial_{t}u\|_{2}^{2}\ dt\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim\tau(1+S)^{2}\int_{0}^{t_{m}}\|\partial_{t}\Delta u\|_{2}^{2}\ dt+\tau\int_{0}^{t_{m}}\|\partial_{t}u\|_{2}^{2}\ dt

\displaystyle\lesssim\tau(1+S)^{2}(1+t_{m})+\tau

\displaystyle\lesssim(1+S)^{2}\tau\cdot(1+t_{m})\ .

For the rest terms
\sqrt{\tau}\|\Delta Q_{n}\|_{2}
and
\tau\|\Delta^{2}Q_{n}\|_{2}
we will obtain very similar results as long as
s\geq 10
and thereby we can conclude that:

(4.31)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{m-1}\|L^{2}Q_{n}\|_{2}^{2}\lesssim(1+S)^{2}\tau\cdot(1+t_{m}).

We now focus on the
\|L^{2}H_{i}\|_{2}
terms. Firstly it is easy to see from the Fourier side that

(4.32)

\displaystyle\|L^{2}H_{1}\|_{2}^{2}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\sum_{|k|\geq 1}\frac{\nu^{2}|k|^{4}(1-\nu|k|^{4}\tau-e^{-\nu|k|^{4}\tau})^{2}}{(1-e^{-\nu|k|^{4}\tau})^{2}}|\widehat{u}(k)|^{2}(t_{n})

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\sum_{\nu|k|^{4}\tau\leq c}+\sum_{\nu|k|^{4}\tau\geq c}\frac{|k|^{4}(1-\nu|k|^{4}\tau-e^{-\nu|k|^{4}\tau})^{2}}{(1-e^{-\nu|k|^{4}\tau})^{2}}|\widehat{u}(k)|^{2}(t_{n})

\displaystyle\coloneqq

\displaystyle J_{1}+J_{2}.

For the part where
\nu|k|^{4}\tau\geq c
, we indeed have

(4.33)

\displaystyle J_{2}\coloneqq

\displaystyle\sum_{\nu|k|^{4}\tau\geq c}\frac{|k|^{4}(1-\nu|k|^{4}\tau-e^{-\nu|k|^{4}\tau})^{2}}{(1-e^{-\nu|k|^{4}\tau})^{2}}|\widehat{u}(k)|^{2}(t_{n})

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\sum_{\nu|k|^{4}\tau\geq c}|k|^{12}\tau^{2}|\widehat{u}(k)|^{2}(t_{n})

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\tau^{2}\|\Delta^{3}u\|^{2}_{2}(t_{n}).

For the lower frequency part, we get

\displaystyle J_{1}\coloneqq

\displaystyle\sum_{\nu|k|^{4}\tau\leq c}\frac{|k|^{4}(1-\nu|k|^{4}\tau-e^{-\nu|k|^{4}\tau})^{2}}{(1-e^{-\nu|k|^{4}\tau})^{2}}|\widehat{u}(k)|^{2}(t_{n})

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq|k|\lesssim(\frac{1}{\tau})^{\frac{1}{4}}}\frac{|k|^{8}(1-\nu|k|^{4}\tau-e^{-\nu|k|^{4}\tau})^{2}}{(1-e^{-\nu|k|^{4}\tau})^{2}}|\widehat{u}(k)|^{2}(t_{n}).

Recall that
\phi(x)=\frac{x}{1-e^{-x}}
is monotone increasing for
x>0
as in Lemma
4.2
. Moreover, we have (by a simple observation) that

(4.34)

\displaystyle 0<\nu|k|^{4}\tau+e^{-\nu|k|^{4}\tau}-1<\nu^{2}|k|^{8}\tau^{2}.

Therefore we have

(4.35)

\displaystyle J_{1}\lesssim

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}\sum_{1\leq|k|\lesssim(\frac{1}{\tau})^{\frac{1}{4}}}(\nu^{2}|k|^{8}\tau^{2})^{2}|\widehat{u}(k)|^{2}(t_{n})\lesssim\tau^{2}\sum_{|k|\geq 1}|k|^{16}|\widehat{u}(k)|^{2}(t_{n})\lesssim\tau^{2}\|\Delta^{4}u\|_{2}^{2}(t_{n}).

Combing (
4.33
)-(
4.35
), we get

(4.36)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{m-1}\|L^{2}H_{1}\|_{2}^{2}\lesssim\sum_{n=0}^{m-1}\tau^{2}(\|\Delta^{3}u\|_{2}^{2}(t_{n})+\|\Delta^{4}u\|_{2}^{2}(t_{n}))\lesssim m\tau^{2}\lesssim(t_{m}+1)\cdot\tau,

where the uniform Sobolev bound lemma (Lemma
2.7
) is applied.

Secondly we estimate
\|L^{2}H_{2}\|_{2}^{2}
. Note that again by considering the Fourier expansion we arrive at:

(4.37)

\displaystyle\|L^{2}H_{2}\|_{2}^{2}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\sum_{1\leq|k|\leq N}\frac{(\nu\tau|k|^{4}+e^{-\nu|k|^{4}\tau}-1)^{2}}{(1-e^{-\nu\tau|k|^{4}})^{2}}|\widehat{f(u)}(k)|^{2}(t_{n})

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\sum_{\nu|k|^{4}\tau\leq c}+\sum_{\nu|k|^{4}\tau\geq c}\frac{(\nu\tau|k|^{4}+e^{-\nu|k|^{4}\tau}-1)^{2}}{(1-e^{-\nu\tau|k|^{4}})^{2}}|\widehat{f(u)}(k)|^{2}(t_{n})

\displaystyle\coloneqq

\displaystyle J_{3}+J_{4}.

Similar to (
4.32
) above, we have

(4.38)

\displaystyle J_{4}\coloneqq

\displaystyle\sum_{\nu|k|^{4}\tau\geq c}\frac{(\nu\tau|k|^{4}+e^{-\nu|k|^{4}\tau}-1)^{2}}{(1-e^{-\nu\tau|k|^{4}})^{2}}|\widehat{f(u)}(k)|^{2}(t_{n})

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\sum_{|k|\geq 1}|k|^{8}\tau^{2}|\widehat{f(u)}(k)|^{2}(t_{n})

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\tau^{2}\|\Delta^{2}f(u)\|_{2}^{2}(t_{n})\lesssim\tau^{2}.

Note that here by Lemma
2.7
we have

\sup_{t\geq 0}\|u\|_{H^{s}}(t)\lesssim_{s}1
, which leads to
\sup_{n\geq 0}\|f(u)\|_{H^{s}}(t_{n})\lesssim_{s}1
. For
J_{3}
, we have

(4.39)

\displaystyle J_{3}\coloneqq

\displaystyle\sum_{\nu|k|^{4}\tau\leq c}\frac{(\nu\tau|k|^{4}+e^{-\nu|k|^{4}\tau}-1)^{2}}{(1-e^{-\nu\tau|k|^{4}})^{2}}|\widehat{f(u)}(k)|^{2}(t_{n})

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq|k|\lesssim(\frac{1}{\tau})^{\frac{1}{4}}}\frac{|k|^{8}(\nu|k|^{4}\tau+e^{-\nu|k|^{4}\tau}-1)^{2}}{(1-e^{-\nu|k|^{4}\tau})^{2}}|\widehat{f(u)}(k)|^{2}(t_{n})

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}\sum_{1\leq|k|\lesssim(\frac{1}{\tau})^{\frac{1}{4}}}\tau^{4}|k|^{16}|\widehat{f(u)}(k)|^{2}(t_{n})\lesssim\tau^{2}\|\Delta^{4}f(u)\|_{2}^{2}(t_{n}),

where again we use the monotonicity of
\phi(x)=\frac{x}{1-e^{-x}}
for
x>0
and (
4.34
). We can then derive that

(4.40)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{m-1}\|L^{2}H_{2}\|_{2}^{2}\lesssim m\tau^{2}\lesssim(t_{m}+1)\cdot\tau.

Lastly let us focus on
\|L^{2}H_{3}\|_{2}^{2}
. Note that
\|L^{2}H_{3}\|_{2}^{2}\lesssim\|H_{3}\|_{2}^{2}+\tau\|\Delta H_{3}\|_{2}^{2}+\tau^{2}\|\Delta^{2}H_{3}\|_{2}^{2}
, we then estimate
\|H_{3}\|_{2}^{2}
below.

(4.41)

\displaystyle\|H_{3}\|_{2}^{2}=\|\Pi_{>N}f(u)\|_{2}^{2}(t_{n})

\displaystyle=\sum_{|k|>N}\left|\widehat{f(u)}(k)\right|^{2}(t_{n})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{|k|>N}|k|^{2s}\left|\widehat{f(u)}(k)\right|^{2}(t_{n})\cdot{|k|^{-2s}}

\displaystyle\lesssim N^{-2s}\cdot\|f(u)\|_{H^{s}}^{2}(t_{n})

\displaystyle\lesssim N^{-2s}\ .

Similarly, we get
\tau\|\Delta H_{3}\|_{2}^{2}\lesssim\tau N^{-2s}
and
\tau^{2}\|\Delta^{2}H_{3}\|_{2}^{2}\lesssim\tau^{2}N^{-2s}
. As a result, assuming
\tau\lesssim 1
or the time step is bounded (which is very natural) we get

(4.42)

\sum_{n=0}^{m-1}\|\Pi_{>N}f(u(t_{n}))\|_{2}^{2}\lesssim_{s}m\cdot(1+\tau^{2})N^{-2s}\lesssim\frac{t_{m}N^{-2s}}{\tau}\ .

Therefore, combining (
4.31
), (
4.36
), (
4.40
) and (
4.42
) we can conclude that

(4.43)

\tau\sum_{n=0}^{m-1}\left(\|L^{2}Q_{n}\|_{2}^{2}+\sum_{i=1,2,3}\|L^{2}H_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\right)\lesssim_{s}(1+t_{m})(\tau^{2}+N^{-2s})(1+S)^{2}\ .

In order to apply Proposition
4.1
, it remains to control
\|Lu^{0}-Lu(0))\|_{2}
and
\|Mu^{0}-Mu(0)\|_{2}
.
Note that by (
4.5
) in Lemma
4.2
and similar arguments in (
4.41
) we have

(4.44)

\displaystyle\|Lu^{0}-Lu(0)\|_{2}^{2}=\|L\Pi_{N}u_{0}-Lu_{0}\|_{2}^{2}\lesssim\|\Pi_{N}u_{0}-u_{0}\|_{2}^{2}+\tau\|\Delta(\Pi_{N}u_{0}-u_{0})\|_{2}^{2}\lesssim N^{-2s}+\tau N^{-2(s-2)}.

Similarly, we have

(4.45)

\|Mu^{0}-Mu(0)\|_{2}^{2}=\|M\Pi_{N}u_{0}-Mu_{0}\|_{2}^{2}\lesssim N^{-2(s-2)}+\tau N^{-2(s-4)}\ .

We now apply the auxiliary solutions estimate in Proposition
4.1
. Noting the estimates (
4.43
), (
4.44
) and (
4.45
) we can get

\|u^{m}-u(t_{m})\|_{2}^{2}\lesssim_{s}(1+S)^{2}e^{Ct_{m}}\left(N^{-2s}+\tau^{2}\cdot N^{-2(s-4)}+\tau^{2}\right)\ .

Thus

(4.46)

\|u^{m}-u(t_{m})\|_{2}\leq(1+S)\cdot C_{2}\cdot e^{C_{1}t_{m}}\left(N^{-s}+\tau+\tau\cdot N^{-s+4}\right)\ ,

where
C_{1}>0
is a constant depending on
\nu
and
u_{0}
;
C_{2}>0
is a constant depending on
s\ ,\nu
and
u_{0}
. This completes the proof of
L^{2}
error estimate.
∎

6.
Concluding remark

To conclude, we give a systematic approach on applying EI-type schemes to models where maximum principle no longer exists by solving the Cahn-Hilliard equation with a first order EI scheme and showing the energy dissipation. In fact second order EI schemes will be handled similarly in a subsequent paper. In particular, we prove the energy dissipation of EI schemes for the Cahn-Hilliard equation without assuming any strong Lipschitz condition or
L^{\infty}
boundedness. Furthermore, we also analyze the
L^{2}
error and present some numerical simulations to demonstrate the dynamics. Indeed, the analysis framework can be applied to more general models and higher order schemes. We leave the discussion in a subsequent work.

Appendix A
Proof of Lemma
2.7

Lemma A.1

(
H^{k}
boundedness of the exact CH solution)
.

Assume
u(x,t)
is a smooth solution to the Cahn-Hilliard equation in
\mathbb{T}^{2}
and the initial data
u_{0}\in H^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
for
k\geq 2
. Then,

(A.1)

\sup_{t\geq 0}\|u(t)\|_{H^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}\lesssim_{k}1

where we omit the dependence on
\nu
and
u_{0}
.

Proof.

The proof is standard and we only sketch the details. To start with we can write the solution
u
in the mild form

u(t)=e^{-\nu t\Delta^{2}}u_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\nu(t-s)\Delta^{2}}\Delta(u^{3}-u)\ ds\ .

Note that by the energy dissipation we indeed have
\|u\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2})}\lesssim 1
for any
t>0
. Therefore it suffices to this argument inductively, namely we show
\|u\|_{H^{2}}\lesssim 1
for any
t\geq 0
.
Then by taking any second order spatial derivative and
L^{2}
norm in the formula above, we derive

\displaystyle\|D^{2}u\|_{2}\leq\|D^{2}e^{-\nu t\Delta^{2}}u_{0}\|_{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\|D^{2}e^{-\nu(t-s)\Delta^{2}}\Delta(u^{3}-u)\|_{2}\ ds,

where
D^{m}u
denotes any differential operator
D^{\alpha}u
for any
|\alpha|=m
.

Firstly, we consider the nonlinear part. We indeed observe that

\displaystyle\|D^{2}e^{-\nu(t-s)\Delta^{2}}\Delta(u^{3}-u)\|_{2}\lesssim\|K_{1}*(u^{3}-u)\|_{2},

where
K_{1}
is the kernel corresponding to
\Delta D^{2}e^{-\nu(t-s)\Delta^{2}}
. (It is easy to see that here
D
can interchange with
\Delta
.) Let
\gamma=t-s
, it then suffices to estimate the following quantity:

(A.2)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\|K_{1}*(u^{3}-u)\|_{2}\ d\gamma=\int_{0}^{1}\|K_{1}*(u^{3}-u)\|_{2}\ d\gamma+\int_{1}^{t}\|K_{1}*(u^{3}-u)\|_{2}\ d\gamma.

Here we assume
t>1
with no loss since the case
t<1
follows easily as a special case. For the region when
\gamma\geq 1
we get

\displaystyle\|K_{1}*(u^{3}-u)\|_{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|K_{1}*(u^{3}-u)\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|K_{1}\|_{2}\cdot\|u^{3}-u\|_{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|K_{1}\|_{2}\cdot\|u\|_{6}^{3}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|K_{1}\|_{2},

by the standard Sobolev embedding
\|u\|_{6}\lesssim\|u\|_{H^{1}}\lesssim 1
.
Note that for
\gamma>1
we have

(A.3)

\displaystyle\|K_{1}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(\sum_{|k|\geq 1}|k|^{8}e^{-2\nu\gamma|k|^{4}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(\int_{1}^{\infty}e^{-2\nu\gamma r^{4}}r^{9}\ dr\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\gamma^{-\frac{1}{2}}e^{-\nu\gamma}.

As a result, we have

(A.4)

\displaystyle\int_{1}^{t}\|K_{1}*(u^{3}-u)\|_{2}\ d\gamma\lesssim\int_{1}^{t}\gamma^{-\frac{1}{2}}e^{-\nu\gamma}\ d\gamma\lesssim 1.

The arguments above indeed indicate a smoothing effect. The region
0<\gamma<1
follows from the standard local theory.
This shows
\int_{0}^{t}\|De^{\nu(t-s)\Delta}u\|_{2}\ ds\lesssim 1
; the linear part follows from similar arguments. As a remark our proof works at least for dimension
d=1,2,3
.

∎
