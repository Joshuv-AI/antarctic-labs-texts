Unified Neural Backdoor Removal with Only Few Clean Samples through Unlearning and Relearning
Nay Myat Min, Long H. Pham, Jun Sun

Abstract
. The application of deep neural network models in various security-critical applications has raised significant security concerns, particularly the risk of backdoor attacks. Neural backdoors pose a serious security threat as they allow attackers to maliciously alter model behavior. While many defenses have been explored, existing approaches are often bounded by model-specific constraints, or necessitate complex alterations to the training process, or fall short against diverse backdoor attacks. In this work, we introduce a novel method for comprehensive and effective elimination of backdoors, called ULRL (short for U n L earn and R e L earn for backdoor removal). ULRL requires only a small set of clean samples and works effectively against all kinds of backdoors. It first applies unlearning for identifying suspicious neurons and then targeted neural weight tuning for backdoor mitigation (i.e., by promoting significant weight deviation on the suspicious neurons). Evaluated against 12 different types of backdoors, ULRL is shown to significantly outperform state-of-the-art methods in eliminating backdoors whilst preserving the model utility.



1.
Introduction

Deep Neural Networks (DNNs) have revolutionized fields such as computer vision and natural language processing, solving complex problems with remarkable accuracy and efficiency. However, their widespread adoption and integration into security-critical systems have raised significant security concerns, including backdoor attacks
(
Gu et al. 2017
;
Chen et al. 2017
)
. Introduced through methods such as data poisoning
(
Gu et al. 2017
;
Liu et al. 2020
)
, neuron hijacking
(
Liu et al. 2018b
)
, or biased training
(
Liu et al. 2021
)
, these backdoors manipulate predictions, whenever certain specific triggers are detected. The shift towards AI-as-a-Service (AIaaS)
(
Hugging Face 2024
)
, the reliance on pre-trained models, and outsourced training amplify these security threats. Despite considerable efforts to fortify DNNs against such threats, existing defenses exhibit significant drawbacks, underscoring the need for more effective solutions.

Current backdoor defense mechanisms fall predominantly into two categories: detection and mitigation strategies. Detection aims to identify the presence of backdoors, while mitigation seeks to neutralize their effects. Examples include inversion based detection
(
Wang et al. 2019
)
, input purification
(
Doan et al. 2019
)
, knowledge distillation
(
Li et al. 2021c
)
, and adversarial unlearning
(
Zeng et al. 2021a
)
. However, many existing methods
(
Wang et al. 2019
;
Liu et al. 2018a
;
Wu and Wang 2021
)
rely heavily on access to large sets of clean, unpoisoned data to effectively detect or mitigate backdoors. In practical scenarios, obtaining a substantial volume of clean samples is often impractical or cost-prohibitive, limiting the applicability of these defenses in real-world settings. Furthermore, the most effective defenses, particularly those that involve pruning or retraining of the model
(
Liu et al. 2018a
;
Li et al. 2023b
)
, lead to a notable decrease in the clean accuracy of the model. This trade-off between security and performance is a major concern, as it compromises the model’s utility.

In addition, implementing existing defenses often requires substantial computational resources or complex modifications to the training process, rendering them impractical in complex enclosing systems or resource-constrained environments. Neuron pruning
(
Li et al. 2021d
;
Tran et al. 2018
;
Chen et al. 2018
;
Liu et al. 2022
)
, for instance, involves a computationally intensive process to remove neurons associated with backdoor mechanisms, necessitating iterative performance evaluations to preserve model accuracy. However, the success of these methods heavily relies on model-specific factors, such as architecture and pruning thresholds, introducing outcome variability. Similarly, reverse engineering for anomaly detection
(
Wang et al. 2019
;
Harikumar et al. 2020
;
Gao et al. 2019
;
Tao et al. 2022
)
demands a significant computational investment, primarily due to the per-class optimization required for trigger inversion. This process is characterized by complex optimization in high-dimensional spaces, iterative refinement for trigger accuracy, and the need for substantial computational power to assess trigger effectiveness across various classes. Additionally, robust training methods
(
Huang et al. 2022
;
Li et al. 2021b
;
Zeng et al. 2021a
)
offer a defensive stance, but at the cost of complex architectural modifications, disproportionately burdening larger models with increased costs.

Furthermore, existing approaches often falter against the evolving spectrum of backdoor attacks, especially those leveraging sophisticated or semantic backdoor triggers
(
Bagdasaryan et al. 2019
;
Li et al. 2020
;
Liu et al. 2020
;
Nguyen and Tran 2021
)
. Traditional defenses, tailored for specific, easily identifiable triggers, struggle to adapt to attacks that embed backdoors within semantic features or across hidden layers
(
Wang et al. 2019
;
Liu et al. 2019
;
Guo et al. 2019
)
. This issue is further compounded by the rapid evolution of adversarial tactics, leaving such defenses outdated against novel or intricately designed backdoors
(
Shen et al. 2021
;
Barni et al. 2019
)
. In particular, methods focusing on anomalies in the input space or integrate fine-tuning strategies face challenges in addressing these complex backdoor scenarios
(
Karim et al. 2023
;
Liu et al. 2018a
)
, underscoring the urgent need for more versatile and unified defense mechanisms.

In response to these challenges, we introduce ULRL (short for
U
n
L
earn and
R
e
L
earn for backdoor removal), a novel defense mechanism that emphasizes targeted and precise interventions, thus providing a flexible and effective mechanism against the evolving threat of neural backdoors, without the drawbacks of existing methods. ULRL efficiently identifies and mitigates backdoor threats with just a small set of clean samples, addressing the practical challenges of data scarcity. ULRL employs a two-phase process, i.e., unlearning and relearning. In the first phase, unlearning identifies suspicious neurons in the classifier layer, highlighting those exhibiting abnormal weight changes during an unlearning process. Subsequently, relearning involves strategic reinitialization and recalibration of these neurons’ weights by regularizing them through weight shifting. By minimizing the cosine similarity between weights, ULRL ensures that these neurons no longer respond to backdoor triggers while maintaining their functionality for legitimate inputs. Crucially, ULRL aims to orient the adjusted and original weight vectors in diametric opposition, a move beyond mere orthogonality. This approach allows for precise correction of backdoor influences without resorting to the broad-stroke interventions typical of existing methods such as feature shift tuning (FST)
(
Min et al. 2023b
)
.

Through unlearning and relearning, ULRL not only effectively mitigates backdoor influences, but also maintains model performance. In addition, ULRL integrates seamlessly into existing training pipelines, requiring minimal resources or modifications.

In summary, our contributions are:

•

Refined Definition:
We propose a novel definition for neural backdoors in DNNs, laying the groundwork for an effective unified neural backdoor removal.

•

Unified Backdoor Removal:
We introduce ULRL, a novel defense mechanism that integrates unlearning and relearning phases to combat neural backdoors. ULRL represents a new approach to neutralizing a broad spectrum of backdoor attacks without extensive data or computational resources.

•

Comprehensive Benchmarking:
Our extensive benchmarking demonstrates ULRL’s superiority over 5 state-of-the-art defenses across 12 distinct backdoor types. This rigorous evaluation not only contributes new insights into defense robustness and effectiveness, but also establishes ULRL as a new standard for the field.

2.
Problem Definition

Dataset.

In this work, we consider a K-class classification model trained by a dataset
\mathcal{D}
. This dataset bifurcates into two critical subsets: the clean subset
\mathcal{D}_{c}=\left\{\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{c}^{(i)},y_{c}^{(i)}\right)\right\}_{i=1}^{N}
, encapsulating
N
instances of genuine feature vectors
\boldsymbol{x}_{c}^{(i)}
and their corresponding class labels
y_{c}^{(i)}
, and the poisoned subset
\mathcal{D}_{b}=\left\{\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{b}^{(j)},y_{b}^{(j)}\right)\right\}_{j=1}^{M}
, composed of
M
instances each characterized by a modified feature vector
\boldsymbol{x}_{b}^{(j)}
designed to embed malicious intent through an associated class label
y_{b}^{(j)}
. For defense, we assume the availability of a tiny subset of
\mathcal{D}_{c}
, i.e. a defense dataset
\mathcal{D}_{d}
.

Convolutional Neural Network.

We define a convolutional neural network (CNN) as a specialized form of neural network designed for processing structured data, where it maps an input vector space
\mathbb{R}^{p}
to an output vector space
\mathbb{R}^{q}
, denoted as
\mathcal{N}:\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{q}
. Its architecture is made up of: an input layer, convolutional layers that serve as the primary feature extraction function
\phi
, pooling layers for dimensionality reduction, and an output layer for classification, represented by the function
f
. Convolutional layers perform feature extraction by applying filters to capture hierarchical patterns within the data. Each convolutional layer is followed by a non-linear activation function
\sigma
(e.g., ReLU) to introduce nonlinearity, allowing the network to learn complex features.

Feature Extraction and Classification.

For clarity, we conceptualize the feature extraction function as
\phi(\theta;x):\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightarrow\phi(x)
, which maps clean
\boldsymbol{x}_{c}^{(i)}
and poisoned
\boldsymbol{x}_{b}^{(j)}
input features into a nuanced feature space
\phi(\boldsymbol{x})
. The linear classification function further refines this process through
\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{w};\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{w}^{T}\phi(\boldsymbol{x})
, which projects the features extracted by
\phi(x)
with
x\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
into the output space
\mathbb{R}^{q}
.

Backdoor Learning.

A backdoor attack, which involves training a model with embedded backdoors, can be conceptualized as a problem of dual-task learning
(
Li et al. 2023b
)
. This entails optimizing the model’s performance on a clean dataset
\mathcal{D}_{c}
(the clean task) alongside its behavior when exposed to a dataset containing backdoor instances
\mathcal{D}_{b}
(the backdoor task). Formally, this learning challenge seeks to optimize a set of parameters
\theta
, subdivided into subsets
\theta_{c}
and
\theta_{b}
, and a corresponding set of parameters
\boldsymbol{w}
, subdivided into subsets
\boldsymbol{w}_{c}
and
\boldsymbol{w}_{b}
, for clean and backdoor tasks, respectively. The optimization goal can be formally expressed as

(1)

\displaystyle\underset{\theta=\theta_{c}\cup\theta_{b},\boldsymbol{w}=\boldsymbol{w}_{c}\cup\boldsymbol{w}_{b}}{\arg\min}\Bigg[

\displaystyle\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{(\boldsymbol{x}_{c},y_{c})\in\mathcal{D}_{c}}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{w}_{c};\phi(\theta_{c},\boldsymbol{x}_{c})),y_{c})}_{\text{clean task}}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{(\boldsymbol{x}_{b},y_{b})\in\mathcal{D}_{b}}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{w}_{b};\phi(\theta_{b},\boldsymbol{x}_{b})),y_{b})}_{\text{backdoor task}}\Bigg],

where
\mathcal{L}
signifies the classification loss function, such as cross-entropy, used to measure the discrepancy between the model’s predictions and the actual labels. This decomposition of the parameters into
\theta=\theta_{c}\cup\theta_{b}
and
\boldsymbol{w}=\boldsymbol{w}_{c}\cup\boldsymbol{w}_{b}
is based on the significant independence between processing clean inputs and backdoor inputs
(
Cheng et al. 2021
)
. It underscores the stealthiness of backdoor attacks, designed to not compromise the model’s performance on clean data
(
Gu et al. 2017
)
.

However, it is crucial to recognize that the delineation between
\theta_{c}
and
\theta_{b}
, as well as between
\boldsymbol{w}_{c}
and
\boldsymbol{w}_{b}
, does not necessarily imply complete segregation. In fact, there may be overlap between these subsets (i.e.,
\theta_{c}\cap\theta_{b}\neq\emptyset
and
\boldsymbol{w}_{c}\cap\boldsymbol{w}_{b}\neq\emptyset
), indicating that certain parameters and weights contribute to both tasks
(
Li et al. 2023b
)
. This overlap underlines the intricate interplay between the model’s legitimate functionalities and its susceptibility to backdoor manipulations, highlighting the complexity and challenge of securing neural networks against such attacks.

Neural Backdoor.
While the traditional backdoor definition focuses on input manipulation, it falls short of accounting for the more intricate backdoors that leverage the model’s internal architecture or the semantic features. To fill this gap, we introduce a refined definition centered on the attack’s neural mechanisms.

Definition 2.1 (Backdoored Network).

A neural network
\mathcal{N}
harbors a backdoor iff there exists a subset of neurons
S\subset\mathcal{N}
satisfying:

•

Effectiveness:
The model predicts the target label
t
whenever neurons in
S
are activated;

•

Feasibility:
There exists an input trigger
\delta
and a backdoor function
G(\boldsymbol{x}_{c},\delta)
activates
S
, leading to the prediction of
t
;

•

Shortcut-ness:

|S|<\epsilon_{S}
where
\epsilon_{S}
is a small constant, indicating the compactness of the backdoor mechanism, and
|\delta|<\epsilon_{\delta}
where
\epsilon_{\delta}
is a stealthiness threshold for the trigger.

This definition highlights how backdoor attacks exploit a neural network’s internal configurations, particularly certain neuron subsets, beyond mere input manipulations. It reveals that these attacks can leverage the model’s vulnerabilities or its learned features, broadening the scope of attack mechanisms from direct input alterations to more nuanced semantic activations.

Threat Model.
We consider a threat model in which attackers aim to covertly manipulate a neural network model
\mathcal{N}
, ensuring that it correctly classifies clean inputs
\boldsymbol{x}_{c}
as
\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{x}_{c})=y_{c}
, while for poisoned inputs
\boldsymbol{x}_{b}
, crafted by embedding a sophisticated trigger, it misclassifies them as
\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{x}_{b})=y_{t}
, where
y_{t}
is the predefined target. The attacker has complete control over the poisoning process, without any constraints on the choice of the triggers or the complexity of the attack strategies employed. This adversary operates under the assumption of having no prior knowledge of the deployed defenses, generally aim to make the attack stealthy to evade detection by both automated systems and human inspection. Different forms of trigger can be used, allowing the attacker to orchestrate a multifaceted attacks on models by integrating various strategies and trigger modalities.

In this work, we assume that, by operating with white-box access to
\mathcal{N}
, the defender has comprehensive insight into the model architecture but does not have control over its initial training phase. The defense strategy hinges on the use of a small set of clean dataset
\mathcal{D}_{d}
, devoid of any poisoned instances, to assess and correct the model’s behavior.

Defense Objective.
We define four main objectives for our defense.

(1)

Backdoor Mitigation:
To neutralize backdoors in the neural network
\mathcal{N}
, ensuring it mitigates diverse backdoor attacks, including those with semantic triggers.

(2)

Preserve Performance:
To balance security with performance, ensuring that defense mitigates backdoor without sacrificing the model’s accuracy on legitimate tasks.

(3)

Minimize Data Dependency:
To effectively mitigate with minimal clean data, ensuring practicality in data-limited or cost-sensitive scenarios, an existing key challenge.

(4)

Practicality and Scalablity:
To ensure easy integration and minimal computational demands, countering the complexity and resource intensiveness of existing defenses.

3.1.
Overview

Our ULRL approach combats sophisticated backdoor attacks on DNNs through two key phases. Illustrated in Figure
1
and detailed in Algorithm
1
, these two phases are as follows.

(1)

Unlearning:
This phase challenges the conventional model training paradigms by intentionally deteriorating the performance with the defense dataset,
\mathcal{D}_{d}
. Instead of minimizing loss to improve accuracy, we strategically maximize loss, leveraging the insight that neurons influenced by backdoor mechanisms respond differently to this process. This phase identifies suspicious neurons by identifying abnormal weight changes during the unlearning process.

(2)

Relearning:
Following the identification of suspicious neurons, the relearning phase focuses on recalibrating the weights of these neurons. This recalibration employs targeted reinitialization methods to preserve the clean performance of the model while neutralizing the backdoors. It is designed to enhance the model’s resistance to backdoor attacks, promoting divergences in neuron weight orientations to dismantle embedded backdoor mechanisms.

The subsequent sections detail these two phases of our ULRL.

Example 3.1.

As a running example, we use a PreAct-ResNet18 DNN model trained on the CIFAR-10 dataset, which has been compromised by a Blended backdoor
(
Chen et al. 2017
)
that subtly incorporates a nearly invisible trigger into the training data by blending it with a controlled intensity. This causes the neural network to misclassify inputs with the trigger while maintaining normal clean accuracy, targeting class 0 with 10% poisoned training data. This architecture features preactivation techniques, incorporating an initial convolution layer, four PreActBlock layers, adaptive average pooling, and a final linear layer with 10 neurons. Each of these neurons is connected through 512 incoming weights and a bias. This backdoor model shows a 93.47% clean accuracy (C-ACC) and a 99.92% attack success rate (ASR).

3.2.
Unlearning

Our method is designed based on the hypothesis that neurons associated with backdoor mechanisms will exhibit the most significant weight changes when subjected to unlearning with a set of clean samples. This hypothesis is based on the understanding that backdoor-related neurons, due to their unique adaptation to recognize backdoor triggers, respond differently from normal neurons when subjected to conditions that reverse their learning. Specifically, we propose that these neurons possess a unique composition of weights: those related to clean features, backdoor features, and irrelevant features. During an unlearning process, we anticipate that the weights related to the backdoor feature will demonstrate pronounced malleability and undergo significant alterations. This behavior is expected to starkly contrast with the stability of clean feature-related weights, which are aligned with the model’s primary classification tasks, and the minimal changes in irrelevant weights. In the following, we first present how unlearning works and then discuss how unlearning allows us to identify these neurons associated with backdoor-related features.

Intuitively, we use unlearning as a diagnostic tool. Unlike traditional training methods that improve model accuracy, unlearning seeks to deliberately exacerbate the model’s loss for a specified task, effectively diminishing its performance on that task. In our setting, unlearning leverages a small set of clean data
\mathcal{D}_{d}
and is achieved through the following objective:

(2)

\arg\max_{\theta,\boldsymbol{w}}\mathbb{E}_{(\boldsymbol{x}_{d},y_{d})\in\mathcal{D}_{d}}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{w};\phi(\theta,\boldsymbol{x}_{d})),y_{d}),

where
\mathcal{L}
denotes the cross-entropy loss function, and the pairs
(\boldsymbol{x}_{d},y_{d})
correspond to the clean samples and their associated labels within the dataset,
\mathcal{D}_{d}
. The parameters of the model after the unlearning process are indicated by
\hat{\theta}
and
\hat{\boldsymbol{w}}
, respectively. Unlearning aims to degrade the model’s performance systematically by maximizing loss on a defense dataset, targeting individual parameters for a detailed analysis to backdoor-vulnerable neurons.

Discussion.
In the following, we analyze why backdoor neurons are likely to show high sensitivity to unlearning.

Unlearning significantly impacts DNNs, particularly at the crucial linear classifier layer, where high-level features are mapped to class labels. This process induces weight adjustments
\Delta\boldsymbol{w}
in DNN, revealing two trends: neurons for clean data
\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{w}_{c};\phi(\boldsymbol{x}_{c}))
experience changes
\Delta\boldsymbol{w}_{c}
that impair their classification of clean inputs, whereas backdoor neurons
\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{w}_{b};\phi(\boldsymbol{x}_{b}))
show resilience, even improving decision making. This leads to a dynamic shift, reducing efficacy on clean data but increasing sensitivity to backdoor triggers, a counterintuitive outcome as backdoor neurons become more active and responsive post-unlearning. We analyse how unlearning affects neurons differently based on their feature associations—Clean, Backdoor, and Irrelevant features—following unlearning. First, neurons correlated with irrelevant features, which neither aid the primary task nor trigger backdoors, show negligible changes, underscoring their peripheral role. The primary goal of the unlearning is pursued by deliberately tweaking the model’s parameters to amplify the loss it incurs when evaluating clean samples. This manipulation entails the modification of the weights corresponding to both clean and backdoor features. Specifically, the weights tied to clean features undergo steady reductions, diminishing their influence on the network’s ability to classify correctly. Consequently, this leads to a pronounced decline in the network’s capacity to discern and learn from legitimate patterns. Although the main focus lies on compromising the model’s performance, alterations to the weights related to backdoor features emerge as a collateral consequence of the push to maximize classification loss.

Let us consider two cases. First, if the backdoor target is different from
y_{d}
, the unlearning objective can be achieved by amplifying the (negative) contribution of backdoor neurons (i.e., so that the model is more wrong). Second, if the target of the backdoor is the same as
y_{d}
, the objective of unlearning can be achieved by amplifying the contribution of the backdoor neurons in a negative way (i.e., the absence of the backdoor trigger increases the loss). In both cases, there is incentive to modify the backdoor neurons for achieving the unlearning objective.

3.3.
Relearning

After pinpointing the backdoor-related neurons, the crucial next step is to selectively reinitialize their weights to dismantle the embedded backdoor’s influence. We choose the He initialization method, renowned for its ability to maintain the variance of neuron activations across layers
(
He et al. 2015
)
, ensuring the model’s clean performance remains unaffected. Specifically, for each backdoor neuron
i
identified in the last step
\mathcal{S}
, weights are recalibrated using a zero-centered uniform distribution with a range determined by the standard deviation (lines 1-5). This reinitialization maintains the model’s learned behaviors on clean data and requires minimal retraining, offering a resource-efficient alternative to a full reset
(
Min et al. 2023b
)
.

After reinitialization, we advance our defense strategy against backdoors with relearning. Relearning, described in Algorithm
3
applies a targeted regularization approach aimed at ensuring the backdoor neurons to substantially diverge from potentially compromised configurations that may retain backdoor influences. This process is inspired by foundational works
(
Kumar et al. 2022
;
Liu et al. 2018a
;
Min et al. 2023b
)
, adopting an end-to-end parameter update strategy,
\{\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{w}\}
, with a particular focus on the backdoor neurons of the classifier layer,
\mathcal{S}
. The objective of relearning is formalized as an optimization problem to minimize the cosine similarity between the updated and original weights of suspicious neurons,
\boldsymbol{w}_{i}
and
\boldsymbol{w}_{i}^{\text{ori}}
, promoting substantial divergence:

(3)

\displaystyle\underset{\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{w}}{\arg\min}\Bigg[

\displaystyle\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\sim\mathcal{D}_{T}}[\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{w};\boldsymbol{\phi}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\boldsymbol{x})),\boldsymbol{y})]}_{\text{clean task performance}}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\alpha\cdot\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\frac{\boldsymbol{w}_{i}\cdot\boldsymbol{w}_{i}^{\text{ori}}}{\|\boldsymbol{w}_{i}\|_{2}\|\boldsymbol{w}_{i}^{\text{ori}}\|_{2}}}_{\text{targeted weight divergence}}\Bigg],

This formulation ensures clean data performance remains intact via the cross-entropy loss,
\mathcal{L}
, while the regularization term, influenced by
\alpha
, specifically attenuates the backdoor capabilities of the identified neurons by minimizing their cosine similarity. The parameter
\alpha
balances the components of the loss function, managing the trade-off between preserving accuracy and enhancing robustness against backdoor attacks through feature adjustments. A higher value of
\alpha
promotes more significant shifts in feature representations.

We remark that relearning effectively neutralizes backdoor influences through subtle but impactful shifts in feature representations, reflecting a balance between security and operational efficiency.

Example 3.3.

Figure
4
illustrates the relearning process. Initially, we reinitialize the weights of suspicious neurons (0 and 6) using the He initialization method and reset their biases, impacting 2 * (512 + 1) parameters. This is highlighted for neuron 0 with red dashed lines indicating the initialized parameters. To diverge these neurons’ weights from their original, potentially compromised states, we minimize their cosine similarity, denoted by the angle
\theta
. This regularization aims to drive the weight vectors towards an opposite orientation, making them more than orthogonal. We apply this regularization exclusively to suspicious neurons while performing standard end-to-end fine-tuning for the remaining neurons. Retraining over 20 epochs effectively decreases ASR, with significant reductions in the first three epochs, resulting in a test accuracy of 89.29% and an ASR of 28.09%. This early decline demonstrates relearning’s ability to mitigate backdoor threats promptly. Meanwhile, the clean accuracy also improves, enhancing the generalization of the model. By the eighth epoch, relearning further reduces the ASR to a negligible 0.49%, while maintaining a high test accuracy of 90.43%, achieving a balance between backdoor mitigation and clean performance. After this point, no substantial changes in ASR or test accuracy occur, suggesting that the model has reached its mitigation capacity, with further training providing minimal additional benefits. This empirical example shows that targeting just a few neurons (in our case, only two neurons) can effectively neutralize backdoors while preserving overall model performance.

Discussion.
In the following, we discuss our objective function in detail, especially the choice of cosine similarity as a regularization.

The relearning objective function can be decomposed into two main components: clean task performance and targeted weight divergence. In contrast to traditional fine-tuning, which prioritizes clean task performance, our approach includes a complexity layer by adding divergence, which not only optimizes the linear classifier weight
\boldsymbol{w}
for clean performance but also ensures it diverges from the potentially compromised initial state (
\boldsymbol{w}^{\text{ori}}
). This divergence promotes a substantial shift in learned features by disrupting the alignment between the model’s feature representation and any backdoor features, particularly in neurons identified as suspicious.

Figure 5.
Example images of 12 backdoor samples from CIFAR-10. The caption below each figure is the backdoor trigger.
Different Types of Backdoor Images.

Cosine similarity, chosen for its ability to measure vector orientation independently of magnitude, supports this by offering a refined measure of divergence. This metric, focusing on the cosine of the angle between vectors, is crucial for recalibrating neuron weights where backdoor influences typically manifest in orientation rather than magnitude. It effectively addresses the challenges posed by the high-dimensional spaces in neural networks, enabling the disentanglement and correct orientation of neuron weight vectors that may have been manipulated to encode backdoor triggers. The inclusion of cosine similarity regularization presents several key advantages:

•

Targeted Adjustment:
Cosine similarity facilitates precise neuron weight adjustments by modifying orientation while preserving magnitude. This ensures that defense efforts are focused on effectively disrupting backdoor mechanisms.

•

Preservation of Model Utility:
Adjusting orientation instead of magnitude preserves the overall model’s parameter distribution. This is crucial for maintaining the model’s effectiveness on clean tasks, ensuring that the defense mechanism does not compromise the original functionality.

•

Robustness to Varied Attack Strategies:
Focusing on the vector orientation rather than their size enables the model to effectively counter diverse and subtle backdoors. This approach enhances the model’s resilience against manipulations that might not significantly alter the weight’s magnitude but could still trigger malicious functionalities.

Experiments

We evaluate the efficacy of our method through a set of experiments.

4.1.
Experimental Setup

To evaluate our approach, we set up a variety of backdoored models trained on four benchmark datasets. 1) CIFAR-10
(
Krizhevsky and Hinton 2009
)
, with 50,000 training samples and 10,000 test samples, each being a 32
\times

(
Stallkamp et al. 2011
)

(
Krizhevsky and Hinton 2009
)

(
Chrabaszcz et al. 2017
)
, with 100,000 training and 10,000 validation images of 64
\times
64 pixels across 200 classes. Our experiments were conducted using two architectures: PreAct-ResNet18
(
He et al. 2016
)
and VGG19-BN
(
Simonyan and Zisserman 2015
)
.

We consider a comprehensive set of 12 kinds of backdoor attacks: BadNets

)
, Blind backdoor (Blind)
(
Bagdasaryan and Shmatikov 2021
)
, BPPAttack (BPP)
(
Wang et al. 2022
)
, Label Consistent backdoor (LC)
(
Turner et al. 2019
)
, Sinusoidal signal backdoor attack (SIG)
(
Barni et al. 2019
)

(
Zeng et al. 2021b
)

5

(

0^{th}

Defense Setup.
We consider a total of 5 sate-of-the-art baseline backdoor removal methods: Fine-pruning (FP)
(

)

(
Wu et al. 2022
)
for all defense methods, except FST and RNP
(
Li et al. 2023a
;
Min et al. 2023a
)
. For these exceptions, we strictly follow their original open-sourced implementations to ensure a fair comparison. For ULRL, we employed SGD for 20 epochs with a momentum of 0.9, setting a uniform unlearning rate of 0.01 and a clean threshold of 0.2 across all datasets. For relearning, we made dataset-specific adjustments: for PreAct-ResNet18, the learning rate was 0.005 for CIFAR-10 and 0.001 for GTSRB, CIFAR-100, and Tiny-ImageNet, with
\alpha
set to 0.7 for CIFAR-10 and 0.001 for the others. For VGG19-BN, the learning rate was adjusted to 0.1 and
\alpha

\alpha

A.2
.

Experimental Results

We answer four research questions based on experimental results.

RQ1 (Backdoor Mitigation and Performance Maintenance): Can ULRL mitigate different types of backdoor attacks?
To address this question, we evaluate the effectiveness of ULRL against 12 backdoors. Tables
1
and
2
present a comprehensive analysis of the impact of ULRL on C-ACC and ASR across various attacks targeting the PreAct-ResNet18 and VGG19-BN architectures, respectively. Our findings indicate that ULRL significantly reduces the ASR for all examined backdoor attacks, effectively neutralizing them.

The average reduction in ASR across all attacks are approximately 94.94% for PreAct-ResNet18 and 88.25% for VGG19-BN. Regarding performance, the impact of ULRL on overall C-ACC is nuanced: it improves by an average of 2.99% for PreAct-ResNet18 and slightly decreases by an average of 1.24% for VGG19-BN, with varying individual results. In particular, the Blind attack shows a significant improvement in C-ACC across both architectures, highlighting scenarios where ULRL not only mitigates the attack effectively but also improves model accuracy. In contrast, attacks such as Blended and BadNets show a slight decrease in C-ACC, suggesting areas where further tuning of ULRL may be required to balance defense effectiveness with performance maintenance. Additionally, LC and Blind attacks on PreAct-ResNet18 demonstrate higher residual ASRs, highlighting their greater resistance. RQ3 results indicate that increasing defense data to 2% neutralizes ASR for LC in PreAct-ResNet18 while preserving C-ACC. For the Blind attack on GTSRB, a higher learning rate is essential for complete mitigation. In VGG19-BN, attacks such as LC, Blended, and InputAware exhibit higher residual ASRs; however, raising the learning rate eliminates them with minimal impact on accuracy.

Furthermore, our analysis investigates the neural dynamics of the models, specifically identifying suspicious neurons via the unlearning process. This reveals significant weight changes in neurons associated with the target and victim classes, particularly in those most affected. This precise identification of compromised neurons underlines ULRL’s ability not only to counteract backdoor effects but also to pinpoint the specific neural targets manipulated by the attacks. This capability is consistent across various scenarios, including different attacks, architectures, and datasets, offering valuable insights into the mechanisms of neural manipulation.

These results confirm that ULRL is capable of mitigating a wide range of backdoor attacks while maintaining a high C-ACC.

Our comparative analysis, summarized in Tables

The results for GTSRB are similarly impressive, with high C-ACC preservation and ASR reduced to almost zero, even under complex attacks with very few exceptions. Each defense exhibits unique strengths and weaknesses, often dependent on the attack complexity. ANP is beneficial in situations where malicious neurons are clearly identifiable, though it also faces trade-offs between C-ACC and ASR, especially in complex scenarios. RNP attempts a balanced approach, moderately impacting C-ACC while trying to reduce ASR, yet it does not always effectively mitigate sophisticated attacks. FST excels in scenarios involving conspicuous feature anomalies, but encounters challenges with more nuanced attacks, where backdoor triggers are subtle and well-integrated into benign features. Overall, while these methods have their applicable scenarios and benefits, ULRL demonstrates superior adaptability and robustness, maintaining high C-ACC and drastically reducing ASR across various datasets and attacks on both architectures.

RQ3: How does the size of clean defense data affect the results?
In practice, users often rely on a limited set of clean data to conduct an in-house evaluation of third-party trained models due to the high costs associated with building larger datasets. Therefore, it is crucial that the backdoor mitigation methods remain effective even with minimal clean data. To this end, we analyzed the performance of ULRL across various proportions of the CIFAR-10 defense dataset, precisely 0.1% (50), 0.2% (100), 0.5% (250), 1% (500), 2% (1000), 5% (2500) and 10% (5000) of the clean training set, utilizing PreAct-ResNet18 and VGG19-BN. The results are presented in Figure
7
.

Initially, ULRL displayed variability in defense efficacy with minimal clean data (0.1% and 0.2%) on both architectures. A moderate decline in average C-ACC signified an adaptation phase, where ULRL began addressing backdoor influences. Notably, a marked decrease in ASR illustrated ULRL’s aptitude for leveraging even scarce amounts of defense data. However, with extremely limited datasets, ASR reduction was not adequate for more sophisticated attacks such as LF, WaNet, SSBA, and InputAware. We conjecture that this is because effective unlearning requires a critical amount of clean data; with insufficient data, pinpointing the backdoor neuron, crucial for successful relearning becomes less effective, thus only marginally reducing ASR. Enhancing the defense data to 0.5% saw a discernible improvement in ULRL’s defensive capabilities, characterized by a substantial decrease in ASR for both architectures, confirming the method’s effectiveness against various backdoor attacks with minimal data. As the defense data size increased to the optimal range of 1% to 2%, we observed the most striking balance: a significant diminishment in ASR, minimized variability in defense efficacy, and, in certain cases, enhancements in C-ACC. For instance, within this range, ULRL improved the PreAct-ResNet18 model’s C-ACC by 8.93% for Blind and 3.60% for SIG attacks, with ASR effectively neutralized. Concurrently, the VGG19-BN model’s C-ACC increased by 1.36% for Blind and 2.13% for WaNet attacks.

The nuanced nature of this balance became more evident when expanding the defense data size to 5% and beyond. Here, both architectures began to show signs of fluctuations in effectiveness, suggesting a suboptimal utilization of clean data, and at 10%, certain attacks like TrojanNN and BPP demonstrated a plateau in defense gains, indicating a limit to the benefits of additional clean data.

Through comparative analysis, we identified a consistently effective range between 1% and 2% for ULRL across both architectures. This finding highlights ULRL’s proficiency in substantially reducing ASR while preserving accuracy for a spectrum of backdoor attacks. However, the variability in response to complex attacks like TrojanNN and LC highlights the challenges in establishing a universally efficient defense against diverse backdoors. This analysis not only reinforces ULRL’s efficacy as a formidable defense mechanism but also accentuates the strategic significance of optimizing clean defense data, thereby effectively addressing RQ3.

Related Work

We review the literature on backdoor attacks and defenses.

Backdoor Attacks.
Backdoor attacks aim to mislead the backdoor model into showing abnormal behavior in samples stamped with the backdoor trigger, but behave normally on all benign samples. Backdoor attacks on DNNs generally fall into two categories: data poisoning and model poisoning
(
Wu et al. 2022
)
. Data poisoning involves training data manipulation with triggers that vary in visibility
(
Li et al. 2020
;
Liu et al. 2020
)
, locality
(
Shafahi et al. 2018
;
Barni et al. 2019
)
, additivity
(
Chen et al. 2017
;
Zeng et al. 2021b
)
and specificity
(
Gu et al. 2017
;
Li et al. 2021a
)
. Model poisoning gives attackers control over training, allowing joint optimization of trigger and model weights
(
Doan et al. 2021
;
Chen et al. 2017
;
Nguyen and Tran 2021
)
.

To illustrate the diversity and sophistication of these attacks, we highlight several notable methodologies: Traditional backdoor methods, such as Badnets
(
Gu et al. 2017
)
and Blended
(
Chen et al. 2017
)
attacks, incorporate explicit triggers into inputs, where the blending factor controls the trigger’s visibility. In contrast, methods like LF
(
Zeng et al. 2021b
)
employ subtler strategies by filtering out high-frequency components, making the trigger less noticeable. Similarly, the SIG attack
(
Barni et al. 2019
)
employs a sinusoidal signal as its trigger, subtly altering clean target images while retaining their original labels for a label-consistent backdoor attack. This method exemplifies the increasing sophistication in crafting triggers that blend seamlessly with natural image properties. Blind attacks
(
Bagdasaryan and Shmatikov 2021
)
manipulate the loss computation during training to create poisoned inputs dynamically, without requiring access to the training data. Similarly, BPP
(
Wang et al. 2022
)
leverages image quantization and dithering to embed triggers imperceptibly, illustrating the evolving complexity of attack mechanisms. TrojanNN
(
Liu et al. 2018b
)
offers a distinct model poisoning tactic by inverting the neural network to create a universal trojan trigger, then retraining with specially crafted data to embed malicious behaviors. This sophisticated strategy complicates detection, highlighting the importance of effective defenses. LC
(
Turner et al. 2019
)
and SSBA
(
Li et al. 2021a
)
represent advanced strategies in which poisoned images closely mimic clean images or are uniquely tailored to each sample, respectively. These methods challenge conventional detection and mitigation strategies by deeply embedding the backdoor within the model’s learning process. InputAware
(
Nguyen and Tran 2020
)
attacks and WaNet
(
Nguyen and Tran 2021
)
utilize adaptive learning and fixed warping functions to create highly customized and effective poisoned inputs. These methods show how attackers can improve their techniques to avoid detection and increase the effectiveness of the backdoor.

Backdoor Defenses.
Backdoor defenses are crucial to protecting DNNs from attacks aimed at undermining their integrity. These defenses fall broadly into two categories: detection and removal strategies. Detection focuses on identifying the presence of backdoors, employing techniques that analyze prediction biases or detect statistical deviations in feature space
(
Li et al. 2021d
;
Tran et al. 2018
;
Chen et al. 2018
;
Liu et al. 2022
)
, and advanced methods using reverse engineering to find triggers through anomaly detection
(
Wang et al. 2019
;
Harikumar et al. 2020
;
Gao et al. 2019
;
Hu et al. 2022
)
. Removal strategies, conversely, directly eliminate backdoors using various approaches, including robust training and post-processing purification.

Robust training
(
Huang et al. 2022
;
Li et al. 2021b
;
Zeng et al. 2021a
;
Min et al. 2023b
)
aims to prevent the learning of backdoor triggers, but may lead to reduced accuracy and increased costs, especially for large models. Alternatively, post-processing purification addresses already trained models, to mitigate compromised neurons through techniques such as pruning or unlearning strategies
(
Liu et al. 2018a
;
Wang et al. 2019
;
Wu and Wang 2021
;
Zheng et al. 2022
;
Foret et al. 2021
;
Li et al. 2023b
)
. However, these methods may affect clean accuracy and struggle to generalize across different architectures. Furthermore, innovative defenses include attention, channel analysis, and value estimation for more effective removal
(
Li et al. 2021c
;
Zheng et al. 2022
;
Guan et al. 2022
)
. Adversarial Unlearning (I-BAU)
(
Zeng et al. 2021a
)
and Adversarial Neuron Pruning (ANP)
(
Wu and Wang 2021
)
target adversarially sensitive components, although their efficacy varies with the attack complexities.

Backdoored DNNs contain a mix of clean and backdoor neurons, with the latter activated exclusively by trigger patterns. It’s been found that these neurons are unusually sensitive to adversarial perturbations
(
Wu and Wang 2021
)
, suggesting that identifying and pruning them could significantly purify the model. Methods like Reconstructive Neuron Pruning (RNP)
(
Li et al. 2023b
)
have advanced in this direction, using asymmetric neuron-level unlearning and filter-level recovery. However, achieving a balance between pruning effectiveness and maintaining clean accuracy requires a dynamic thresholding approach and remains challenging, often leading to a trade-off between defense efficacy and model performance.

Feature Shift Tuning (FST)
(
Min et al. 2023b
)
represents a significant step forward in mitigating data poisoning backdoor attacks by incorporating a classification loss penalty based on the divergence between the classifier’s adjusted and original weights. This method requires a comprehensive reinitialization of classifier neuron parameters, potentially leading to the loss of valuable feature representations. FST’s focus on inner product regularization, primarily concerned with weight magnitude, overlooks the importance of directional weight properties, possibly compromising its effectiveness. Additionally, the necessary projection constraint to prevent parameter explosion limits the model’s exploration capabilities, adding complexity to the fine-tuning process.

Our ULRL method targets and mitigates vulnerable neurons in the classifier layer, overcoming the drawbacks of current defenses with precise and focused interventions. Unlearning, which focuses on the classifier layer to pinpoint and mitigate backdoor neurons. This approach simplifies the defense process, eliminating the complexities and performance compromises often seen with extensive neuron pruning or the implementation of stringent threshold settings. Diverging from methods like FST, which depend on complete reinitialization and feature shift regularization, ULRL innovates by introducing relearning to preserve the integrity of learned features. Prioritizing the minimization of cosine similarity between weights, ULRL focuses on their directional alignment rather than their magnitude. This strategic shift not only obviates the need for norm constraints—thereby streamlining the optimization process—but also boosts model generalization and fortifies defenses against backdoor attacks. Most crucially, ULRL aims to orient the adjusted and original weight vectors in diametric opposition. ULRL guarantees a robust defense against more complex backdoor strategies, adeptly circumventing the limitations associated with projection constraints in existing methods such as FST.

Implementation Details

We provide the details of our experimental settings in this section.

A.1.
Attack Details

This section details the configurations for implementing 12 attacks on the CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, GTSRB, and Tiny-ImageNet datasets.

For our experiments, a consistent set of hyperparameters was employed to maintain uniformity across all models. The attack objective was consistently to mislabel poisoned samples as class 0, using an all-to-one attack. We utilized SGD with a momentum of 0.9 and a weight decay of
5e^{-4}
for model optimization, training with a batch size of 128. The CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 models were trained for 100 epochs, while GTSRB models were trained for 50 epochs to ensure effective backdoor integration without overfitting.

We employed a Cosine Annealing Learning Rate schedule for the CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100 and GTSRB datasets, except for attacks where training was controllable. In contrast, the Tiny-ImageNet models were trained for 200 epochs, using a ReduceLROnPlateau schedule to finely adjust the learning rate, thereby avoiding premature convergence on local minima and ensuring that the backdoor patterns were robustly learned. For CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100 and GTSRB, we initiated the training with a learning rate of 0.1, whereas for Tiny-ImageNet, the learning rate was adjusted to suit the larger and more complex dataset. However, for WaNet, we reduced the initial rate to 0.01 to achieve a stable and successful backdoor implantation.

All backdoor attacks were executed with standard configurations from BackdoorBench
(
Wu et al. 2022
)
, apart from the Semantic backdoor.
BadNets
employed a 3x3 white checkerboard pattern placed at the image’s bottom right. The
Blended
attack’s trigger was the ‘Hello Kitty’ pattern, set with a 0.2 alpha.
Label Consistent
used Projected Gradient Descent (PGD) with a 1.5 step size over 100 steps, epsilon at 8, and delta at 40. The
Blind
backdoor balanced normal and backdoor losses with weights of 1.0 and 0.9, refined through 1000 batches.
BPP
attack applied a negative ratio of 0.1 and a squeeze number of 8.
SIG
’s sinusoidal signal had an amplitude of 40 and a frequency of 6.
Low Frequency
attack used a fooling rate of 0.2, with 50 termination iterations and a 0.02 overshoot.
SSBA
encoded a single bit, with the autoencoder’s training step at
14e^{4}
.
Input-Aware
applied a 0.01 learning rate with a MultiStepLR schedule.
TrojanNN
targeted two linear neurons with activations of 100 and optimized an ‘apple’ logo mask over 1000 iterations.
WaNet
used a grid rescale and cross-ratio of 2. Pre-generated Invisible triggers from BackdoorBench ensured consistency in SIG, SSBA, LC, and WaNet.
Semantic
backdoors, implemented as per
(
Bagdasaryan et al. 2020
)
, misclassified green car images from the Automobile (class 2) to Frogs (class 6) at a learning rate of 0.01 over 100 epochs.

This detailed account of attack implementations highlights the depth and precision of our experimental approach.

A.2.
Defense Details

To rigorously evaluate our ULRL defense method, we conducted comparative analyses against five leading defense strategies, ensuring a comprehensive benchmarking by following established protocols and parameter settings from BackdoorBench and other authoritative sources.

•

Fine-Pruning (FP):
FP was implemented with an initial learning rate of 0.01 over 100 epochs. Pruning targeted the model’s last convolutional layer, proceeding until the C-ACC dipped below the 80% threshold.

•

Adversarial Neural Pruning (ANP):
With ANP, the perturbation budget was set at 0.4 and the trade-off coefficient at 0.2. A range of thresholds (0.4 to 0.9) was tested to balance performance and backdoor resistance, with the purification results being gauged on low ASR and high C-ACC.

•

Reconstructive Neuron Pruning (RNP):
Utilizing the open-source PyTorch implementation, the neuron unlearning phase in RNP maximized the compromised model for 20 epochs, reducing C-ACC to 10%, with a learning rate of 0.01 and weight decay of
5e^{-2}
. The subsequent recovery phase lasted 20 epochs with a learning rate of 0.2, employing dynamic threshold pruning within the [0.4, 0.7] interval.

•

Feature Shift Tuning (FST):
FST was deployed using its open-sourced codebase. The SGD optimizer was set with an initial learning rate of 0.01 and momentum of 0.9 for CIFAR-10 and GTSRB, and a learning rate of
1e^{-3}
for CIFAR-100 and Tiny-ImageNet. FST’s duration spanned 10 epochs for CIFAR-10, 15 epochs for GTSRB, CIFAR-100 and Tiny-ImageNet. The regularization parameter,
\alpha
, was tuned to 0.2 for CIFAR-10, 0.1 for GTSRB, and 0.001 for CIFAR-100 and Tiny-ImageNet, optimizing the balance between the fidelity of the model and the robustness against backdoor exploits. Given that VGG19-BN has multiple layers in its classifier, FST was extended to apply the original last-layer regularization across all the final linear layers of the model.

Across all defenses, the batch size was standardized to 256. This uniform approach in defense configuration enabled a fair and rigorous evaluation of each method’s efficacy in mitigating backdoor threats, setting a benchmark for our ULRL’s performance assessment.
