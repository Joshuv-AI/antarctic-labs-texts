Information divergences of Markov chains and their applications
Youjia Wang, Michael C.H. Choi

Abstract
In this paper, we first introduce and define several new information divergences in the space of transition matrices of finite Markov chains which measure the discrepancy between two Markov chains. These divergences offer natural generalizations of classical information-theoretic divergences, such as the f f -divergences and the Rényi divergence between probability measures, to the context of finite Markov chains. We begin by detailing and deriving fundamental properties of these divergences and notably gives a Markov chain version of the Pinsker’s inequality and Chernoff information. We then utilize these notions in a few applications. First, we investigate the binary hypothesis testing problem of Markov chains, where the newly defined Rényi divergence between Markov chains and its geometric interpretation play an important role in the analysis. Second, we propose and analyze information-theoretic (Cesàro) mixing times and ergodicity coefficients, along with spectral bounds of these notions in the reversible setting. Examples of the random walk on the hypercube, as well as the connections between the critical height of the low-temperature Metropolis-Hastings chain and these proposed ergodicity coefficients, are highlighted. Keywords : Markov chains; f f -divergences; Rényi divergence; ergodicity coefficients; mixing times; Metropolis-Hastings AMS 2020 subject classification : 60J10, 60J20, 94A15, 94A17

Introduction

Given two Markov chains with transition matrices
M,L
on a common finite state space
\mathcal{X}
, how to measure their information-theoretic discrepancies? The present manuscript is largely motivated by this question and one of the main aims is to systematically analyze various natural notions of information divergences of Markov chains and to present some interesting and important applications, particularly in the context of mixing times, convergence towards equilibrium and hypothesis testing of Markov chains.

Let us begin by briefly recalling several established notions of information divergences of Markov chains in the literature. In
(
Billera & Diaconis 2001a
)
, the total variation distance between
M,L
with respect to a given probability distribution
\pi
is introduced and investigated for Metropolis-Hastings chains, namely

\mathrm{TV}(M,L):=\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}\pi(x)\left|M(x,y)-L(x,y)\right|,

while the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence from
L
to
M
with respect to
\pi
, as studied in
Wolfer & Watanabe 2021a
, is given by

D_{KL}(M\|L):=\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}\pi(x)M(x,y)\ln\left(\dfrac{M(x,y)}{L(x,y)}\right).

These two divergences are in fact special cases of
f
-divergences. Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}
be a convex function with
f(1)=0
that we call it as the generator of
f
-divergence. The
f
-divergence from
L
to
M
with respect to
\pi
is defined to be

D_{f}(M\|L):=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\pi(x)\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}}L(x,y)f\left(\dfrac{M(x,y)}{L(x,y)}\right).

Note that by taking
f(t)=\frac{1}{2}|t-1|
and
f(t)=t\ln t
, we recover respectively the total variation distance and KL divergence introduced earlier.

An important family of
f
-divergences is known as the
\alpha
-divergence, that we denote by
D_{\alpha}
, for
\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,+\infty)
by taking
f(t)=\frac{t^{\alpha}-1}{\alpha-1}
. The Rényi divergence from
L
to
M
of the order
\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,+\infty)
can be defined to be

\displaystyle R_{\alpha}(M\|L)

\displaystyle:=\dfrac{1}{\alpha-1}\ln\left(1+(\alpha-1)D_{\alpha}(M\|L)\right).

All these divergences shall be introduced in a more precise manner in Section
2
.

With the above notations in mind, we proceed to highlight several important and interesting achievements of this manuscript. The first major achievements lie in
introducing and analyzing fundamental properties of
D_{f},D_{\alpha}
and
R_{\alpha}
in the context of discrete-time Markov chains. While these divergences are frequently utilized to quantify the information divergences of probability measures, to the best of our knowledge these divergences have not been systematically investigated in finite Markov chains. Notable highlights include

•

Markov chain Pinsker’s inequality (Proposition
3.5
): for
0<\alpha\leq 1
,

\dfrac{\alpha}{2}\mathrm{TV}^{2}(M,L)\leq R_{\alpha}(M\|L).

•

Chernoff information of Markov chain (Proposition
3.8
): for
\alpha>0
,

(1-\alpha)R_{\alpha}(M\|L)=\inf_{P\in\mathcal{L}}\left\{\alpha D_{KL}(P\|M)+(1-\alpha)D_{KL}(P\|L)\right\},

where
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X})
is the set of all transition matrices on
\mathcal{X}
.

•

Ergodicity coefficients in the space of finite transition matrices, and their properties (Proposition
3.9
):
given
P\in\mathcal{L}
and
\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,+\infty)
, we define

\displaystyle\eta^{f}(P)=\eta^{f}(P,\pi):=\sup_{M,L\in\mathcal{L};~M\neq L}\dfrac{D_{f}(MP\|LP)}{D_{f}(M\|L)},

\displaystyle\eta^{(R_{\alpha})}(P)=\eta^{(R_{\alpha})}(P,\pi):=\sup_{M,L\in\mathcal{L};~M\neq L}\dfrac{R_{\alpha}(MP\|LP)}{R_{\alpha}(M\|L)}.

In the remaining of this section, let us assume that
P
is ergodic and admits
\pi
as its stationary distribution. Let
\Pi
be a matrix with each row being
\pi
for all rows. To quantify and assess the convergence rate of
P^{t}
towards
\Pi
, we define the
\pi
-weighted
\alpha
-divergence mixing time to be

\displaystyle\bar{t}_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(D_{\alpha})}(\varepsilon,P)

\displaystyle:=\inf\left\{t>0:D_{\alpha}(P^{t}\|\Pi)<\varepsilon\right\}.

Analogously we shall define Rényi divergence mixing times. In this vein, one of our major achievements in this direction lie in
proposing and analyzing new information-theoretic mixing times
, in which We offer spectral bounds on these mixing times:

Theorem 1.1

(Informal presentation of Theorem
4.3
)
.

Let
P
be an irreducible, aperiodic and
\pi
-reversible transition matrix, and we denote
\pi_{\mathrm{min}}:=\min_{x}\pi(x)>0
. For
\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,+\infty)
and sufficiently small

\displaystyle\bar{t}_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(D_{\alpha})}(\varepsilon,P)

\displaystyle\leq\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{\gamma_{*}}\left(\ln\dfrac{2\alpha}{(\alpha-1)\pi_{\mathrm{min}}}+\ln\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\right),\quad\textrm{if }\,\alpha>1,\\
\dfrac{1}{\gamma_{*}}\left(\ln\dfrac{8\alpha}{(1-\alpha)\pi_{\mathrm{min}}}+\ln\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\right),\quad\textrm{if }\,0<\alpha<1,\end{cases}

and

\displaystyle\bar{t}_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(D_{\alpha})}(\varepsilon,P)

\displaystyle\geq\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{2\ln\frac{1}{1-\gamma_{*}}}\left(\ln\dfrac{\pi_{\mathrm{min}}^{2}}{8}+\ln\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\right),\quad\textrm{if }\,\alpha>1,\\
\dfrac{1}{2\ln\frac{1}{1-\gamma_{*}}}\left(\ln\dfrac{\pi_{\mathrm{min}}^{2}\alpha}{16}+\ln\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\right),\quad\textrm{if }\,0<\alpha<1,\end{cases}

where
\gamma_{*}
is the absolute spectral gap of

On the other hand, the worst-case mixing time based upon

\displaystyle:=\inf\left\{t>0:\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\widetilde{D}_{\alpha}(P^{t}(x,\cdot)\|\pi)=\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\dfrac{1}{\alpha-1}\left(\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}}\pi(y)\left(\dfrac{P^{t}(x,y)}{\pi(y)}\right)^{\alpha}-1\right)<\varepsilon\right\}.

To compare the average mixing time

\dfrac{1}{5}t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(D_{\alpha})}(\varepsilon,P)\leq\bar{t}_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(D_{\alpha})}(\varepsilon,P)\leq t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(D_{\alpha})}(\varepsilon,P).

Analogous results comparing the average and the worst-case mixing time based upon Rényi divergence and total variation distance are also provided in Theorem

. We also remark on the series of work that compare the order of various mixing times and hitting times of Markov chains or Markov processes, see for example
(

2
Basic definitions

On a finite state space
\mathcal{X}
, we define
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X})
as the set of transition matrices of discrete-time Markov chains. We denote by
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
to be the set of probability masses on
\mathcal{X}
. Let
\pi\in\mathcal{P}
is any given positive probability distribution on
\mathcal{X}
, and denote
\mathcal{L}(\pi)\subset\mathcal{L}
as the set of
\pi
-reversible transition matrices, where a transition matrix
P\in\mathcal{L}
is said to be
\pi
-reversible if
\pi(x)P(x,y)=\pi(y)P(y,x)
for all
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
. We also say that
P\in\mathcal{L}
is
\pi
-stationary if it satisfies
\pi P=\pi
.

First, we give the definition of
f
-divergence of Markov chains and recall that of probability measures.

Definition 2.1

(
f
-divergence of Markov chains and of probability measures)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{+}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
be a convex function and
f(1)=0
.
For given
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
and transition matrices
M,L\in\mathcal{L}
, we define the
f
-divergence from
L
to
M
as

D_{f}(M\|L):=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\pi(x)\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}}L(x,y)f\left(\dfrac{M(x,y)}{L(x,y)}\right).

(1)

For two probability measures
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
with
\Omega
finite, the
f
-divergence from
\nu
to
\mu
is defined to be

\widetilde{D}_{f}(\mu\|\nu):=\sum_{x\in\Omega}\nu(x)f\left(\frac{\mu(x)}{\nu(x)}\right),

(2)

where we apply the usual convention that
0f(\frac{0}{0}):=0
and
0f(\frac{a}{0}):=af^{\prime}(+\infty)
with
f^{\prime}(+\infty):=\lim_{x\to 0^{+}}xf(\frac{1}{x})
for
a>0
in the two definitions above.

Note that
D_{f}(M\|L)
can be understood as a
\pi
-weighted average of
\widetilde{D}_{f}(M(x,\cdot)\|L(x,\cdot))
, since we have

\displaystyle D_{f}(M\|L)=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\pi(x)\widetilde{D}_{f}(M(x,\cdot)\|L(x,\cdot)).

(3)

In the sequel, a majority of our focus is devoted to the special case of
\alpha
-divergences, where we take
f(t)=\frac{t^{\alpha}-1}{\alpha-1}
with
\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,+\infty)
in Definition
2.1
. As a result, this yields

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}(M\|L)

\displaystyle:=\dfrac{1}{\alpha-1}\left(\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\pi(x)\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}}L(x,y)\left(\dfrac{M(x,y)}{L(x,y)}\right)^{\alpha}-1\right),

(4)

\displaystyle\widetilde{D}_{\alpha}(\mu\|\nu)

\displaystyle:=\dfrac{1}{\alpha-1}\left(\sum_{x\in\Omega}\nu(x)\left(\dfrac{\mu(x)}{\nu(x)}\right)^{\alpha}-1\right),

(5)

where the same convention as in Definition
2.1
still applies in the equations above. Using
\widetilde{D}_{\alpha}(\mu\|\nu)
, we now introduce the Rényi divergence between probability measures as systematically investigated in
(
Van & Harremos 2014a
)
.

Definition 2.2

(Rényi divergence of probability measures)
.

For probability measures
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
and
\Omega
is finite, the Rényi divergence from
\nu
to
\mu
of the order
\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,+\infty)
is given by

\displaystyle\widetilde{R}_{\alpha}(\mu\|\nu)

\displaystyle:=\dfrac{1}{\alpha-1}\ln\sum_{x\in\Omega}\nu(x)\left(\dfrac{\mu(x)}{\nu(x)}\right)^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\alpha-1}\ln\left(1+(\alpha-1)\widetilde{D}_{\alpha}(\mu\|\nu)\right).

(6)

Using simple convexity arguments, it can readily be shown that
\widetilde{R}_{\alpha}(\mu\|\nu)\geq 0
(see for example
(
Van & Harremos 2014a
)
), from which we see that
1+(\alpha-1)\widetilde{D}_{\alpha}(\mu\|\nu)\geq 0
. In the case for Markov chains however, the definition for Rényi divergence from
L
to
M
is more involved, as we cannot directly define it in the form (
6
) via simply replacing
\widetilde{D}_{\alpha}(\mu\|\nu)
with
D_{\alpha}(M\|L)
, since such definition can be ill-defined as it is a priori unclear whether
1+(\alpha-1)D_{\alpha}(M\|L)\geq 0
when
\alpha<1
. To give a meaningful definition in this case, one possible remedy is to instead consider the divergence of the edge measure of the two chains.

Definition 2.3

(Edge measure of a Markov chain with respect to
\pi
)
.

For
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
,
M\in\mathcal{L}
and
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
, if we write

\displaystyle\widetilde{M}(x,y)

\displaystyle:=\pi(x)M(x,y),

then
\widetilde{M}=\left(\widetilde{M}(x,y)\right)_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}}
is a probability measure on
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}
.
\widetilde{M}
is known as the edge measure of
M
with respect to
\pi
, see
(
Wolfer & Watanabe 2021a
)
. Analogously we can define the edge measure of
L
with respect to
\pi
, denoted by
\widetilde{L}
.

Using Definition
2.3
and (
5
), this immediately yields an important equality which will play a key role in the following sections:

\widetilde{D}_{\alpha}(\widetilde{M}\|\widetilde{L})=\dfrac{1}{\alpha-1}\left(\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}\pi(x)L(x,y)\left(\dfrac{M(x,y)}{L(x,y)}\right)^{\alpha}-1\right)=D_{\alpha}(M\|L).

(7)

Intuitively, it means that the
\alpha
-divergence from the edge measure
\widetilde{L}
to
\widetilde{M}
equals to the
\alpha
-divergence from the chain
L
to
M
. Thus, to analyze the property of
D_{\alpha}(M\|L)
, it suffices to investigate the corresponding property of
\widetilde{D}_{\alpha}(\widetilde{M}\|\widetilde{L})
. With equality (
7
), we can proceed to give a well-defined Rényi divergence
R_{\alpha}(M\|L)
.

Definition 2.4

(Rényi divergence of Markov chains)
.

Given
M,L\in\mathcal{L}
, the Rényi divergence from
L
to
M
of the order
\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,+\infty)
is given by

\displaystyle R_{\alpha}(M\|L)

\displaystyle:=\widetilde{R}_{\alpha}(\widetilde{M}\|\widetilde{L})

(8)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\alpha-1}\ln\left(1+(\alpha-1)\widetilde{D}_{\alpha}(\widetilde{M}\|\widetilde{L})\right)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\alpha-1}\ln\left(1+(\alpha-1)D_{\alpha}(M\|L)\right).

(9)

Note that for
t\geq 0
(resp.
0\leq t\leq 1/(1-\alpha)
) and
\alpha>1
(resp.
\alpha\in(0,1)
), the mapping
t\mapsto\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\ln(1+(\alpha-1)t)
is concave (resp. convex), and hence by Jensen’s inequality and (
3
), this yields

\displaystyle R_{\alpha}(M\|L)\begin{cases}\geq\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\pi(x)\widetilde{R}_{\alpha}(M(x,\cdot)\|L(x,\cdot)),\quad\textrm{if }\,\alpha>1,\\
\leq\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\pi(x)\widetilde{R}_{\alpha}(M(x,\cdot)\|L(x,\cdot)),\quad\textrm{if }\,0<\alpha<1,\end{cases}

(10)

where the right hand side above can be understood as the
\pi
-weighted average of the Rényi divergence
\widetilde{R}_{\alpha}(M(x,\cdot)\|L(x,\cdot))
. These two inequalities also contrast with the equality in (
3
).

In addition to
D_{\alpha}
and
R_{\alpha}
as discussed earlier, we next introduce some
f
-divergences of Markov chains. Their counterparts in probability measures are well-studied in the literature, see for example
(
Van & Harremos 2014a
, Eq.5-7)
.

Definition 2.5

(Some common
f
-divergences of Markov chains)
.

For
M,L\in\mathcal{L}
and
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
, by taking
f(t)
to be respectively
(\sqrt{t}-1)^{2},(t-1)^{2},|t-1|/2
in Definition
2.1
, we define

\displaystyle(\mathbf{Hellinger\kern 5.74997ptdistance})\quad\mathrm{Hel}^{2}(M,L)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}\pi(x)\left(\sqrt{M(x,y)}-\sqrt{L(x,y)}\right)^{2},

(11)

\displaystyle(\mathbf{\chi^{2}-divergence})\quad\chi^{2}(M\|L)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{L>0}\pi(x)\dfrac{\left(M(x,y)-L(x,y)\right)^{2}}{L(x,y)},

(12)

\displaystyle(\mathbf{Total\kern 5.74997ptvariation\kern 5.74997ptdistance})\quad\mathrm{TV}(M,L)

\displaystyle:=\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}\pi(x)\left|M(x,y)-L(x,y)\right|.

(13)

The next definition concerns the closest
\pi
-reversible transition matrix to any given transition matrix
L
as measured by the Rényi divergence of the order
\alpha
:

Definition 2.6

(
\alpha
-projection)
.

For
L\in\mathcal{L}
and
\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,+\infty)
, we say that
L^{(R_{\alpha})}
is an
\alpha
-projection of
L
to
\mathcal{L}(\pi)
, if

L^{(R_{\alpha})}=L^{(R_{\alpha})}(L,\pi)\in\mathop{\arg\min}\limits_{M\in\mathcal{L}(\pi)}R_{\alpha}(M\|L),

where we recall that
\mathcal{L}(\pi)
denotes the set of
\pi
-reversible transition matrices.

Note that the set
\mathcal{L}(\pi)
is a compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{X}|\times|\mathcal{X}|}
and the mapping
M\mapsto R_{\alpha}(M\|L)
is clearly continuous, and hence
L^{(R_{\alpha})}
exists. We shall investigate the uniqueness of
\alpha
-projection later using the Pythagorean inequality (
14
) in Proposition
3.7
.

The final definition of the section introduces new ergodicity coefficients of
P
on the space of transition matrices
\mathcal{L}
based upon information divergences. These can be used to assess the contraction rate of
P
.

Definition 2.7

(Ergodicity coefficients of Markov chains based on
D_{f}
and
R_{\alpha}
)
.

Given
P\in\mathcal{L}
,
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
and
\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,+\infty)
, we define

\displaystyle\eta^{f}(P)=\eta^{f}(P,\pi):=\sup_{M,L\in\mathcal{L};~M\neq L}\dfrac{D_{f}(MP\|LP)}{D_{f}(M\|L)},

\displaystyle\eta^{(R_{\alpha})}(P)=\eta^{(R_{\alpha})}(P,\pi):=\sup_{M,L\in\mathcal{L};~M\neq L}\dfrac{R_{\alpha}(MP\|LP)}{R_{\alpha}(M\|L)}.

Their classical counterparts on the space of probability measures
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
are defined analogously to be

\displaystyle\widetilde{\eta}^{f}(P):=\sup_{\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X});~\mu\neq\nu}\dfrac{\widetilde{D}_{f}(\mu P\|\nu P)}{\widetilde{D}_{f}(\mu\|\nu)},

\displaystyle\widetilde{\eta}^{(R_{\alpha})}(P):=\sup_{\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X});~\mu\neq\nu}\dfrac{\widetilde{R}_{\alpha}(\mu P\|\nu P)}{\widetilde{R}_{\alpha}(\mu\|\nu)}.

In the special cases of total variation distance and
\alpha
-divergence, we shall write the corresponding ergodicity coefficients of
P
to be
\eta^{(\mathrm{TV})}(P),\widetilde{\eta}^{(\mathrm{TV})}(P)
for
f(t)=|t-1|/2
and
\eta^{(D_{\alpha})}(P),\widetilde{\eta}^{(D_{\alpha})}(P)
for
f(t)=\frac{t^{\alpha}-1}{\alpha-1}
respectively. In the literature,
\eta^{(\mathrm{TV})}(P)
is also called the Dobrushin contraction coefficient, see
(
Cohen et al. 1993a
)
.

Properties of

and

and

•

and

: Proposition

•

Monotonicity in
\alpha
of

•

•

•

The first result that we present demonstrates the non-negativity of
D_{\alpha}(M\|L)
and
R_{\alpha}(M\|L)
.

Proposition 3.1

(Non-negativity of
D_{\alpha}(M\|L)
and
R_{\alpha}(M\|L)
)
.

Given
M,L\in\mathcal{L}
and
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
is strictly positive on
\mathcal{X}
, we have
D_{\alpha}(M\|L)\geq 0
and
R_{\alpha}(M\|L)\geq 0
, and the equality holds if and only if
M=L
.

Proof.

In view of (
3
), the non-negativity of
D_{\alpha}
is clear and inherits from that of
\widetilde{D}_{\alpha}
, see e.g.
Polyanskiy & Wu 2022a
, Theorem
7.5
. Since
\pi
is strictly positive, the equality holds if and only if
\widetilde{D}_{\alpha}(M(x,\cdot)\|L(x,\cdot))=0
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
if and only if
M=L
.
As to
R_{\alpha}(M\|L)
, since
R_{\alpha}(M\|L)=\widetilde{R}_{\alpha}\left(\widetilde{M}\|\widetilde{L}\right)
, it suffices to show
\widetilde{R}_{\alpha}\left(\widetilde{M}\|\widetilde{L}\right)\geq 0
, which is a classic result as in
(
Van & Harremos 2014a
, Theorem 8)
.
∎

The second result proves the convexity of the
\alpha
-divergence and Rényi divergence for suitable
\alpha

Proposition 3.2

(Convexity of
D_{\alpha}(M\|L)
and
R_{\alpha}(M\|L)
)
.

For
M,L\in\mathcal{L}
and
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
, the following statements hold:

(i).

For
\alpha\in(0,1)
, both of
D_{\alpha}(M\|L)
and
R_{\alpha}(M\|L)
are jointly convex in
(M,L)
.

(ii).

For
\alpha\in(1,+\infty)
,
D_{\alpha}(M\|L)
is jointly convex in
(M,L)
, and
R_{\alpha}(M\|L)
is convex in
L
.

(iii).

For
\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,+\infty)
,
R_{\alpha}(M\|L)
is jointly quasi-convex in (M,L), i.e. for
\lambda\in(0,1)
and
(M_{1},L_{1}),(M_{2},L_{2})
, we have

R_{\alpha}(\lambda M_{1}+(1-\lambda)M_{2}\|\lambda L_{1}+(1-\lambda)L_{2})\leq\max\left\{R_{\alpha}(M_{1}\|L_{1}),R_{\alpha}(M_{2}\|L_{2})\right\}.

Proof.

We first prove that for
\alpha>0
and
\alpha\neq 1
,
D_{\alpha}(M\|L)
is jointly convex in
(M,L)
, i.e. for
\lambda\in(0,1)
and
(M_{1},L_{1}),(M_{2},L_{2})\in\mathcal{L}\times\mathcal{L}
, we need to prove

D_{\alpha}\left(\lambda M_{1}+(1-\lambda)M_{2}\|\lambda L_{1}+(1-\lambda)L_{2}\right)\leq\lambda D_{\alpha}(M_{1}\|L_{1})+(1-\lambda)D_{\alpha}(M_{2}\|L_{2}).

This can be readily verified: as
f(t)=\frac{t^{\alpha}-1}{\alpha-1}
is convex, which implies that
L(x,y)f\left(\frac{M(x,y)}{L(x,y)}\right)
is convex in
(M(x,y),L(x,y))
, since

\displaystyle L_{\lambda}(x,y)f\left(\dfrac{M_{\lambda}(x,y)}{L_{\lambda}(x,y)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\lambda L_{1}(x,y)f\left(\dfrac{M_{1}(x,y)}{L_{1}(x,y)}\right)+(1-\lambda)L_{2}(x,y)f\left(\dfrac{M_{2}(x,y)}{L_{2}(x,y)}\right)

\displaystyle\iff f\left(\dfrac{M_{\lambda}(x,y)}{L_{\lambda}(x,y)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\lambda\dfrac{L_{1}(x,y)}{L_{\lambda}(x,y)}f\left(\dfrac{M_{1}(x,y)}{L_{1}(x,y)}\right)+(1-\lambda)\dfrac{L_{2}(x,y)}{L_{\lambda}(x,y)}f\left(\dfrac{M_{2}(x,y)}{L_{2}(x,y)}\right),

where
M_{\lambda}(x,y)=\lambda M_{1}(x,y)+(1-\lambda)M_{2}(x,y)
and
L_{\lambda}(x,y)=\lambda L_{1}(x,y)+(1-\lambda)L_{2}(x,y)
are the shorthand notations, and the last inequality indeed holds from the convexity of
f
.

When
\alpha\in(0,1)
, the mapping
t\mapsto\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\ln(1+(\alpha-1)t)
is convex, hence
R_{\alpha}(M\|L)=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\ln\left(1+(\alpha-1)D_{\alpha}(M\|L)\right)
is also convex.

When
\alpha\in(1,+\infty)
, for all
\lambda\in(0,1)
and
(M,L_{1}),(M,L_{2})\in\mathcal{L}\times\mathcal{L}
, we need to prove

R_{\alpha}(M\|\lambda L_{1}+(1-\lambda)L_{2})\leq\lambda R_{\alpha}(M\|L_{1})+(1-\lambda)R_{\alpha}(M\|L_{2}).

Note that for
L_{\lambda}=\lambda L_{1}+(1-\lambda)L_{2}
, we have

\displaystyle R_{\alpha}(M\|L_{\lambda})

\displaystyle=\widetilde{R}_{\alpha}\left(\widetilde{M}\|\widetilde{L}_{\lambda}\right)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{R}_{\alpha}\left(\widetilde{M}\|\lambda\widetilde{L}_{1}+(1-\lambda)\widetilde{L}_{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\lambda\widetilde{R}_{\alpha}\left(\widetilde{M}\|\widetilde{L}_{1}\right)+(1-\lambda)\widetilde{R}_{\alpha}\left(\widetilde{M}\|\widetilde{L}_{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\lambda R_{\alpha}(M\|L_{1})+(1-\lambda)R_{\alpha}(M\|L_{2}),

where the inequality follows from the convexity of
\widetilde{R}_{\alpha}(M\|L)
in
L
from
(
Van & Harremos 2014a
, Theorem 12)
, and hence
R_{\alpha}(M\|L)
is convex in
L
. Similarly, the last item can also be proved via using
R_{\alpha}(M\|L)=\widetilde{R}_{\alpha}(\widetilde{M}\|\widetilde{L})
.
∎

The next result proves the monotonicity of the Rényi divergence in
\alpha
:

Proposition 3.3

(Monotonicity in
\alpha
)
.

For
M,L\in\mathcal{L}
and
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
,
R_{\alpha}(M\|L)
is non-decreasing with respect to
\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,+\infty)
.

Proof.

For
\alpha,\beta\in(0,1)\cup(1,+\infty)
and
\alpha<\beta
, the mapping
t\mapsto t^{\frac{\alpha-1}{\beta-1}}
is strictly convex when
\alpha<1
and strictly concave when
\alpha>1
, hence we have

\displaystyle R_{\alpha}(M\|L)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\alpha-1}\ln\left(\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}\pi(x)L(x,y)\left(\dfrac{M(x,y)}{L(x,y)}\right)^{\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\alpha-1}\ln\left(\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}\pi(x)M(x,y)\left(\dfrac{M(x,y)}{L(x,y)}\right)^{\alpha-1}\right)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\alpha-1}\ln\left(\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}\pi(x)M(x,y)\left(\dfrac{M(x,y)}{L(x,y)}\right)^{(\beta-1)\frac{\alpha-1}{\beta-1}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{1}{\alpha-1}\ln\left(\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}\pi(x)M(x,y)\left(\dfrac{M(x,y)}{L(x,y)}\right)^{(\beta-1)}\right)^{\frac{\alpha-1}{\beta-1}}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\beta-1}\ln\left(\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}\pi(x)M(x,y)\left(\dfrac{M(x,y)}{L(x,y)}\right)^{(\beta-1)}\right)

\displaystyle=R_{\beta}(M\|L).

∎

We naturally extend the notion of Rényi divergence of Markov chains to order
0,1,+\infty
in the next result:

Proposition 3.4

(Extension of
R_{\alpha}
to
\alpha=0,1
and
+\infty
)
.

For
M,L\in\mathcal{L}
and
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
, we define

\displaystyle R_{0}(M\|L):=\lim_{\alpha\searrow 0}R_{\alpha}(M\|L),\quad R_{1}(M\|L):=\lim_{\alpha\nearrow 1}R_{\alpha}(M\|L),\quad R_{\infty}(M\|L):=\lim_{\alpha\nearrow\infty}R_{\alpha}(M\|L),

then we have

\displaystyle R_{0}(M\|L)

\displaystyle=-\ln\left(\sum_{x,y;~M(x,y)>0}\pi(x)L(x,y)\right),

\displaystyle R_{1}(M\|L)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y;~L(x,y)>0}\pi(x)M(x,y)\ln\dfrac{M(x,y)}{L(x,y)},

\displaystyle R_{\infty}(M\|L)

\displaystyle=\ln\left(\max\limits_{x,y;~L(x,y)>0}\dfrac{M(x,y)}{L(x,y)}\right),

where
R_{1}(M\|L)
is the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence from
L
to
M
, and we write
D_{KL}(M\|L):=R_{1}(M\|L)
.

Proof.

When
0<\alpha<1
, it is easy to see that
M(x,y)^{\alpha}L(x,y)^{1-\alpha}\leq\alpha M(x,y)+(1-\alpha)L(x,y)
, then by the dominated convergence theorem, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\searrow 0}R_{\alpha}(M\|L)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\alpha\searrow 0}\dfrac{1}{\alpha-1}\ln\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}\pi(x)L(x,y)\left(\dfrac{M(x,y)}{L(x,y)}\right)^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=-\ln\left(\lim_{\alpha\searrow 0}\sum_{M,L>0}\pi(x)L(x,y)\left(\dfrac{M(x,y)}{L(x,y)}\right)^{\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle=-\ln\left(\sum_{M,L>0}\pi(x)L(x,y)\right)

\displaystyle=-\ln\left(\sum_{M>0}\pi(x)L(x,y)\right).

Denote
x_{\alpha}=\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}\pi(x)L(x,y)\left(\frac{M(x,y)}{L(x,y)}\right)^{\alpha}
. Similar to the argument above, by the dominated convergence theorem we have
\lim_{\alpha\nearrow 1}x_{\alpha}=1
. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\nearrow 1}R_{\alpha}(M\|L)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\alpha\nearrow 1}\dfrac{\ln x_{\alpha}}{\alpha-1}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\alpha\nearrow 1}\dfrac{x_{\alpha}-1}{\alpha-1}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\alpha\nearrow 1}\sum_{L>0}\pi(x)M(x,y)\left(\dfrac{\left(\frac{M(x,y)}{L(x,y)}\right)^{\alpha-1}-1}{\alpha-1}\right).

Given
t>0
, the mapping
g:\beta\mapsto\frac{t^{\beta}-1}{\beta}
is non-decreasing for
\beta\in(-1,0)
, since
g^{\prime}(t)=\frac{t^{\beta}}{\beta^{2}}\left(\beta\ln t-(1-t^{-\beta})\right)=\frac{t^{\beta}}{\beta^{2}}\left((t^{-\beta}-1)-\ln t^{-\beta}\right)\geq 0
. Hence by monotone convergence theorem, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\nearrow 1}R_{\alpha}(M\|L)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\alpha\nearrow 1}\sum_{L>0}\pi(x)M(x,y)\left(\dfrac{\left(\frac{M(x,y)}{L(x,y)}\right)^{\alpha-1}-1}{\alpha-1}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{M,L>0}\pi(x)M(x,y)\lim_{\alpha\nearrow 1}\left(\dfrac{\left(\frac{M(x,y)}{L(x,y)}\right)^{\alpha-1}-1}{\alpha-1}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{M,L>0}\pi(x)M(x,y)\ln\dfrac{M(x,y)}{L(x,y)}.

As to
R_{\infty}(M\|L)
, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\nearrow\infty}R_{\alpha}(M\|L)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\alpha\nearrow\infty}\ln\left(\sum_{L>0}\pi(x)M(x,y)\left(\dfrac{M(x,y)}{L(x,y)}\right)^{\alpha-1}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha-1}}

\displaystyle=\ln\left(\max\limits_{L>0}\dfrac{M(x,y)}{L(x,y)}\right).

∎

Building upon Proposition
3.4
, in the next result we prove various inequalities between information divergences of Markov chains, and in particular we give an analogue of Pinsker’s inequality in this context.

Proposition 3.5

(Inequalities with other divergences)
.

For
M,L\in\mathcal{L}
,
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
and recall the
f
-divergences as in Definition
2.5
, we have

(i).

\mathrm{Hel}^{2}(M,L)\leq R_{\frac{1}{2}}(M\|L)\leq R_{1}(M\|L)\leq R_{2}(M\|L)\leq\chi^{2}(M\|L)
.

(ii).

(Pinsker’s inequality)

For
0<\alpha\leq 1
, we have

\dfrac{\alpha}{2}\mathrm{TV}^{2}(M,L)\leq R_{\alpha}(M\|L).

Proof.

First we observe that

\displaystyle R_{\frac{1}{2}}(M\|L)

\displaystyle=-2\ln\left(1-\dfrac{\mathrm{Hel}^{2}(M,L)}{2}\right),

\displaystyle R_{2}(M\|L)

\displaystyle=\ln\left(1+\chi^{2}(M\|L)\right),

then by
\ln t\leq t-1
for
t>0
, we have
\mathrm{Hel}^{2}(M,L)\leq R_{\frac{1}{2}}(M\|L)
and
R_{2}(M\|L)\leq\chi^{2}(M\|L)
.

For probability measures
\mu,\nu
on finite state space
\Omega
, we denote
\widetilde{\mathrm{TV}}(\mu,\nu):=\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in\Omega}|\mu(x)-\nu(x)|
, then we have

\displaystyle R_{\alpha}(M\|L)=\widetilde{R}_{\alpha}\left(\widetilde{M}\|\widetilde{L}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\dfrac{\alpha}{2}\widetilde{\mathrm{TV}}^{2}\left(\widetilde{M}\|\widetilde{L}\right)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\alpha}{8}\left(\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}\left|\pi(x)M(x,y)-\pi(x)L(x,y)\right|\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\alpha}{2}\mathrm{TV}^{2}(M\|L),

where the inequality follows from
(
Van & Harremos 2014a
, Theorem 31)
.
∎

We proceed to prove the skew symmetry of equivalence of Rényi divergence of Markov chains:

Proposition 3.6

(Skew symmetry and equivalence)
.

Given
M,L\in\mathcal{L}
,
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
and
0<\alpha<\beta<1
, we have

(i).

R_{\alpha}(M\|L)=\dfrac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}R_{1-\alpha}(L\|M)
.

(ii).

\dfrac{\alpha}{\beta}\dfrac{1-\beta}{1-\alpha}R_{\beta}(M\|L)\leq R_{\alpha}(M\|L)\leq R_{\beta}(M\|L)
.

Proof.

Since

\displaystyle(1-\alpha)R_{\alpha}(M\|L)

\displaystyle=-\ln\left(\sum_{M,L>0}\pi(x)M^{\alpha}(x,y)L^{1-\alpha}(x,y)\right),

\displaystyle\alpha R_{1-\alpha}(L\|M)

\displaystyle=-\ln\left(\sum_{M,L>0}\pi(x)L^{1-\alpha}(x,y)M^{\alpha}(x,y)\right),

the first item is verified. The second item follows from
\frac{1-\beta}{\beta}R_{\beta}(M\|L)=R_{1-\beta}(L\|M)
and
\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}R_{\alpha}(M\|L)=R_{1-\alpha}(L\|M)
.
∎

We prove the Pythagorean inequality and weak triangle inequality of the Rényi divergence of Markov chains in the following Proposition:

Proposition 3.7

(Pythagorean inequality and weak triangle inequality)
.

Suppose we are given
L\in\mathcal{L}
and recall that an
\alpha
-projection
L^{(R_{\alpha})}\in\mathop{\arg\min}_{M\in\mathcal{L}(\pi)}R_{\alpha}(M\|L)
is introduced in Definition
2.6
and exists. For any
M\in\mathcal{L}(\pi)
and
\alpha>0
, we have the following Pythagorean inequality:

R_{\alpha}(M\|L)\geq R_{\alpha}(M\|L^{(R_{\alpha})})+R_{\alpha}(L^{(R_{\alpha})}\|L).

(14)

In addition, for
\alpha>1
and any
U,V,W\in\mathcal{L}
, we have the weak triangle inequality

R_{\alpha}(U\|V)\leq\dfrac{2\alpha-1}{2\alpha-2}R_{2\alpha}(U\|W)+R_{2\alpha-1}(W\|V).

(15)

Proof.

For
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
, we denote the symmetric edge measure family on
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}
as

\mathcal{E}(\pi):=\left\{\pi(x)g(x,y):g(x,y)\geq 0,\sum_{y}g(x,y)=1,\pi(x)g(x,y)=\pi(y)g(y,x)\right\}.

Then we will prove
\mathcal{E}(\pi)
is
\alpha
-convex, i.e.
for
P_{0},P_{1}\in\mathcal{E}(\pi)
,
\alpha>0
and
\lambda\in(0,1)
, there exists

P_{\lambda}(x,y):=\dfrac{\left((1-\lambda)P_{0}^{\alpha}(x,y)+\lambda P_{1}^{\alpha}(x,y)\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}{\sum_{x,y}\left((1-\lambda)P_{0}^{\alpha}(x,y)+\lambda P_{1}^{\alpha}(x,y)\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}\in\mathcal{E}(\pi).

Actually, we only need to verify that
P_{\lambda}(x,y)
is symmetric, after which
G_{\lambda}(x,y):=P_{\lambda}(x,y)/\pi(x)
is reversible and hence satisfies the condition for
\mathcal{E}(\pi)
. Actually, we have

\displaystyle P_{\lambda}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\left((1-\lambda)P_{0}^{\alpha}(x,y)+\lambda P_{1}^{\alpha}(x,y)\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}{\sum_{x,y}\left((1-\lambda)P_{0}^{\alpha}(x,y)+\lambda P_{1}^{\alpha}(x,y)\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\left((1-\lambda)P_{0}^{\alpha}(y,x)+\lambda P_{1}^{\alpha}(y,x)\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}{\sum_{x,y}\left((1-\lambda)P_{0}^{\alpha}(y,x)+\lambda P_{1}^{\alpha}(y,x)\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}

\displaystyle=P_{\lambda}(y,x),

where the second equality follows from the symmetry of
P_{0},P_{1}
. Moreover, since
\pi(x)>0
, we can define a bijection
\phi\in\left(\mathcal{L}(\pi),\mathcal{E}(\pi)\right):M(x,y)\mapsto\pi(x)M(x,y)
, and therefore we have

\displaystyle R_{\alpha}(M\|L)\geq R_{\alpha}\left(L^{(R_{\alpha})}\|L\right),\quad\forall M\in\mathcal{L}(\pi)

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\widetilde{R}_{\alpha}\left(P\|\widetilde{L}\right)\geq\widetilde{R}_{\alpha}\left(\widetilde{L^{(R_{\alpha})}}\|L\right),\quad\forall P\in\mathcal{E}(\pi),

which implies
\widetilde{L^{(R_{\alpha})}}\in\mathop{\arg\min}_{P\in\mathcal{E}(\pi)}\widetilde{R}_{\alpha}\left(P\|\widetilde{L}\right)
. Thus by
(
Van & Harremos 2014a
, Theorem 14)
, for
M\in\mathcal{L}(\pi)
and
\widetilde{M}(x,y)=\pi(x)M(x,y)\in\mathcal{E}(\pi)
, we have

\displaystyle R_{\alpha}(M\|L)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{R}_{\alpha}\left(\widetilde{M}\|\widetilde{L}\right)\geq R_{\alpha}\left(\widetilde{M}\|\widetilde{L^{(R_{\alpha})}}\right)+R_{\alpha}\left(\widetilde{L^{(R_{\alpha})}}\|\widetilde{L}\right)

\displaystyle=R_{\alpha}(M\|L^{(R_{\alpha})})+R_{\alpha}(L^{(R_{\alpha})}\|L),

which is (
14
).

To prove the weak triangle inequality, by Cauchy-Schwarz inequality we have

\displaystyle\sum_{x,y}\pi(x)U^{\alpha}(x,y)V^{1-\alpha}(x,y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{x,y}\pi(x)W(x,y)\left(\dfrac{U(x,y)}{W(x,y)}\right)^{\alpha}\left(\dfrac{W(x,y)}{V(x,y)}\right)^{\alpha-1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{x,y}\pi(x)W(x,y)\left(\dfrac{U(x,y)}{W(x,y)}\right)^{2\alpha}\right)^{1/2}\left(\sum_{x,y}\pi(x)W(x,y)\left(\dfrac{W(x,y)}{V(x,y)}\right)^{2\alpha-2}\right)^{1/2},

taking logarithm we have

(\alpha-1)R_{\alpha}(U\|V)\leq\dfrac{2\alpha-1}{2}R_{2\alpha}(U\|W)+\dfrac{2\alpha-2}{2}R_{2\alpha-1}(W\|V),

which is (
15
).
∎

Remark 3.1
.

Using Proposition
3.7
, the uniqueness of
\alpha
-projection can be easily verified. Suppose
L_{1}^{(R_{\alpha})}
and
L_{2}^{(R_{\alpha})}
are two
\alpha
-projections, then by (
14
) we have

R_{\alpha}\left(L_{2}^{(R_{\alpha})}\|L\right)\geq R_{\alpha}\left(L_{2}^{(R\alpha)}\|L_{1}^{(R_{\alpha})}\right)+R_{\alpha}\left(L_{1}^{(R_{\alpha})}\|L\right),

which implies
R_{\alpha}\left(L_{2}^{(R_{\alpha})}\|L_{1}^{(R_{\alpha})}\right)=0
and therefore
L_{1}^{(R_{\alpha})}=L_{2}^{(R_{\alpha})}
by Proposition
3.1
.

The next result introduces the notion of Chernoff information of Markov chains:

Proposition 3.8

(Chernoff information)
.

For
\alpha>0
,
M,L\in\mathcal{L}
and
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
, we have

(1-\alpha)R_{\alpha}(M\|L)=\inf_{P\in\mathcal{L}}\left\{\alpha D_{KL}(P\|M)+(1-\alpha)D_{KL}(P\|L)\right\}.

(16)

Moreover, if
D_{KL}(M\|L)<\infty
, we have the saddle property

\displaystyle\sup_{\alpha>0}\inf_{P\in\mathcal{L}}\left\{\alpha D_{KL}(P\|M)+(1-\alpha)D_{KL}(P\|L)\right\}

(17)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf_{P\in\mathcal{L}}\sup_{\alpha>0}\left\{\alpha D_{KL}(P\|M)+(1-\alpha)D_{KL}(P\|L)\right\},

where we call (
17
) as the Chernoff information with respect to
M,L
.

Proof.

Following the proof in Proposition
3.7
, we denote the edge measure family

\mathcal{P}_{e}:=\left\{\pi(x)g(x,y):g(x,y)\geq 0,\sum_{y}g(x,y)=1\right\},

then we have
\mathcal{E}(\pi)\subset\mathcal{P}_{e}
, and there exists a bijection from
\mathcal{L}
to
\mathcal{P}_{e}
. Therefore, by
(
Van & Harremos 2014a
, Theorem 30)
we have

\displaystyle\inf_{P\in\mathcal{L}}\left\{\alpha D_{KL}(P\|M)+(1-\alpha)D_{KL}(P\|L)\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf_{P\in\mathcal{L}}\left\{\alpha\widetilde{R}_{1}\left(\widetilde{P}\|\widetilde{M}\right)+(1-\alpha)\widetilde{R}_{1}\left(\widetilde{P}\|\widetilde{L}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{P}_{e}}\left\{\alpha\widetilde{R}_{1}\left(Q\|\widetilde{M}\right)+(1-\alpha)\widetilde{R}_{1}\left(Q\|\widetilde{L}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-\alpha)\widetilde{R}_{\alpha}\left(\widetilde{M}\|\widetilde{L}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-\alpha)R_{\alpha}(M\|L).

For the saddle property, the proof is similar by using
(
Van & Harremos 2014a
, Theorem 32)
.
∎

4.1
, we will illustrate the role of Chernoff information in estimating the Bayesian error of binary hypothesis testing between two transition matrices.

The final result of this section discusses some properties of the ergodicity or contraction coefficients as introduced in Definition
2.7
. In particular, these results offer a bridge and connect
\eta^{f},\eta^{(R_{\alpha})}
with their counterparts such as
\widetilde{\eta}^{f},\widetilde{\eta}^{(R_{\alpha})}
, thus transferring properties from one to another. We shall also see in the proof that many of the properties of these coefficients are inherited from analogous properties of their respective information divergences such as
D_{f}
or
R_{\alpha}
.

Proposition 3.9

(Ergodicity coefficients)
.

Given
P\in\mathcal{L}
,
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
,
\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,+\infty)
and
f
being a convex function on
\mathbb{R}
with
f(1)=0
, we have

(i).

(Non-negativity and normalization)

0\leq\eta^{f}(P)\leq\eta^{(\mathrm{TV})}(P)\leq 1
,
0\leq\eta^{(R_{\alpha})}(P)\leq 1
and
0\leq\widetilde{\eta}^{(R_{\alpha})}(P)\leq 1
.

(ii).

(Ergodicity coefficients on the space
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
are the same as that on the space
\mathcal{L}
)

\eta^{f}(P)=\widetilde{\eta}^{f}(P)
and
\eta^{(R_{\alpha})}(P)\geq\widetilde{\eta}^{(R_{\alpha})}(P)
.

(iii).

(Bounds)
Suppose that the sequence
(U^{*}_{n},V^{*}_{n})
approaches
\eta^{(R_{\alpha})}(P)
, and the sequence
(\mu^{*}_{n},\nu^{*}_{n})
approaches
\widetilde{\eta}^{R_{\alpha}}(P)
, that is,

\displaystyle\eta^{(R_{\alpha})}(P)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\dfrac{R_{\alpha}(U^{*}_{n}P\|V^{*}_{n}P)}{R_{\alpha}(U^{*}_{n}\|V^{*}_{n})},\quad\widetilde{\eta}^{(R_{\alpha})}(P)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\dfrac{\widetilde{R}_{\alpha}(\mu^{*}_{n}P\|\nu^{*}_{n}P)}{\widetilde{R}_{\alpha}(\mu^{*}_{n}\|\nu^{*}_{n})},

and by passing to a subsequence if necessary, we assume that
(U^{*}_{n},V^{*}_{n})\rightarrow(U^{*},V^{*})
and
(\mu^{*}_{n},\nu^{*}_{n})\rightarrow(\mu^{*},\nu^{*})
pointwise. We then have, for
\alpha>1
,

\displaystyle\eta^{(D_{\alpha})}(P)

\displaystyle\leq\eta^{(R_{\alpha})}(P)\leq\begin{cases}\dfrac{\ln(1+(\alpha-1)\eta^{(D_{\alpha})}(P)D_{\alpha}(U^{*}\|V^{*}))}{\ln(1+(\alpha-1)D_{\alpha}(U^{*}\|V^{*}))}\leq 1,\quad&\textrm{if }\,U^{*}\neq V^{*},\\
\eta^{(D_{\alpha})}(P)\leq 1,\quad&\textrm{if }\,U^{*}=V^{*},\end{cases}

\displaystyle\widetilde{\eta}^{(D_{\alpha})}(P)

\displaystyle\leq\widetilde{\eta}^{(R_{\alpha})}(P)\leq\begin{cases}\dfrac{\ln(1+(\alpha-1)\widetilde{\eta}^{(D_{\alpha})}(P)\widetilde{D}_{\alpha}(\mu^{*}\|\nu^{*}))}{\ln(1+(\alpha-1)\widetilde{D}_{\alpha}(\mu^{*}\|\nu^{*}))}\leq 1,\quad&\textrm{if }\,\mu^{*}\neq\nu^{*},\\
\widetilde{\eta}^{(D_{\alpha})}(P)\leq 1,\quad&\textrm{if }\,\mu^{*}=\nu^{*},\end{cases}

while for
\beta\in(0,1)
, we have

\displaystyle\eta^{(R_{\beta})}(P)

\displaystyle\leq\eta^{(D_{\beta})}(P),\quad\widetilde{\eta}^{(R_{\beta})}(P)\leq\widetilde{\eta}^{(D_{\beta})}(P).

(iv).

(Convexity)

The mappings
\mathcal{L}\ni P\mapsto\eta^{f}(P)
and
\mathcal{L}\ni P\mapsto\eta^{(R_{\alpha})}(P)
are convex if
\alpha\in(0,1)
.

(v).

(Submultiplicativity)

For
P,Q\in\mathcal{L}
, we have

\eta^{f}(PQ)\leq\eta^{f}(P)\eta^{f}(Q).

(vi).

(Symmetry)

If
\alpha\in(0,1)
, then
\eta^{(R_{\alpha})}(P)=\eta^{(R_{1-\alpha})}(P)
and
\eta^{(D_{\alpha})}(P)=\eta^{(D_{1-\alpha})}(P)
.

(vii).

(Equivalence)

For
0<\alpha<\beta<1
, we have

\dfrac{\alpha}{\beta}\dfrac{1-\beta}{1-\alpha}\eta^{(R_{\beta})}(P)\leq\eta^{(R_{\alpha})}(P)\leq\dfrac{\beta}{\alpha}\dfrac{1-\alpha}{1-\beta}\eta^{(R_{\beta})}(P).

(viii).

(Scrambling)

P
is scrambling if and only if any one (and hence all) of the following ergodicity coefficients is less than
1
:

\eta^{f}(P),\eta^{(D_{\alpha})}(P),\eta^{(R_{\beta})}(P),\widetilde{\eta}^{(D_{\alpha})}(P),\widetilde{\eta}^{(R_{\beta})}(P)<1,

where
\beta>1
.

(ix).

(Unit rank and independence)

Let
f
be strictly convex at
1
. Then
P
has unit rank if and only if any one (and hence all) of the following ergodicity coefficients equals to
0
:

\eta^{f}(P),\eta^{(D_{\alpha})}(P),\eta^{(R_{\beta})}(P),\widetilde{\eta}^{(D_{\alpha})}(P),\widetilde{\eta}^{(R_{\beta})}(P)=0,

where
\beta>1
.

Proof.

Item
(i).
: Using item
(ii).
and the property that
0\leq\widetilde{\eta}^{f}(P)\leq\widetilde{\eta}^{(\mathrm{TV})}(P)\leq 1
from
(
Cohen et al. 1993a
, Theorem 4.1)
, we see that
\eta^{f}(P)\in[0,1]
. By item
(iii).
,
\eta^{(R_{\alpha})}(P),\widetilde{\eta}^{(R_{\alpha})}(P)\in[0,1]
.

Item
(ii).
: We first prove that
\widetilde{\eta}^{f}(P)\geq\eta^{f}(P)
. Fix
M,L,P\in\mathcal{L}
. Using (
3
) twice, we see that

\displaystyle D_{f}(MP\|LP)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\pi(x)\widetilde{D}_{f}(MP(x,\cdot)\|LP(x,\cdot))

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\pi(x)\widetilde{\eta}_{f}(P)\widetilde{D}_{f}(M(x,\cdot)\|L(x,\cdot))

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\eta}^{f}(P)D_{f}(M\|L),

where the inequality follows from the definition of
\widetilde{\eta}^{f}(P)
. Taking supremum over
M,L
with
M\neq L
yields the desired result.

Next, we prove the opposite inequality that
\widetilde{\eta}^{f}(P)\leq\eta^{f}(P)
and
\widetilde{\eta}^{(R_{\alpha})}(P)\leq\eta^{(R_{\alpha})}(P)
. Fix
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
, and let
U(x,\cdot)=\mu,V(x,\cdot)=\nu
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
. Using the definitions of
D_{f}
and
\widetilde{D}_{f}
, we thus see that

D_{f}(UP\|VP)=\widetilde{D}_{f}(\mu P\|\nu P),

which yields

R_{\alpha}(UP\|VP)=\widetilde{R}_{\alpha}(\mu P\|\nu P),

and hence

\displaystyle\eta^{f}(P)

\displaystyle\geq\sup_{\mu\neq\nu;U(x,\cdot)=\mu,V(x,\cdot)=\nu}\dfrac{D_{f}(UP\|VP)}{D_{f}(U\|V)}=\sup_{\mu\neq\nu}\dfrac{\widetilde{D}_{f}(\mu P\|\nu P)}{\widetilde{D}_{f}(\mu\|\nu)}=\widetilde{\eta}^{f}(P),

\displaystyle\eta^{(R_{\alpha})}(P)

\displaystyle\geq\sup_{\mu\neq\nu;U(x,\cdot)=\mu,V(x,\cdot)=\nu}\dfrac{R_{\alpha}(UP\|VP)}{R_{\alpha}(U\|V)}=\sup_{\mu\neq\nu}\dfrac{\widetilde{R}_{\alpha}(\mu P\|\nu P)}{\widetilde{R}_{\alpha}(\mu\|\nu)}=\widetilde{\eta}^{(R_{\alpha})}(P).

Therefore we have
\widetilde{\eta}^{f}(P)=\eta^{f}(P)
and
\eta^{(R_{\alpha})}(P)\geq\widetilde{\eta}^{(R_{\alpha})}(P)
.

Item
(iii).
: We first prove the second case. For any
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
, we see that

\displaystyle\widetilde{R}_{\alpha}(\mu P\|\nu P)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\alpha-1}\ln(1+(\alpha-1)\widetilde{D}_{\alpha}(\mu P\|\nu P))

\displaystyle\leq\widetilde{\eta}^{(R_{\alpha})}(P)\dfrac{1}{\alpha-1}\ln(1+(\alpha-1)\widetilde{D}_{\alpha}(\mu\|\nu))

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{1}{\alpha-1}\ln(1+\widetilde{\eta}^{(R_{\alpha})}(P)(\alpha-1)\widetilde{D}_{\alpha}(\mu\|\nu)),

where the first inequality follows from the definition of
\widetilde{\eta}^{(R_{\alpha})}(P)
and the second inequality stems from
\alpha>1
and the inequality

\displaystyle(1+x)^{a}

\displaystyle\leq 1+ax

(18)

for
x>-1
and
a\in[0,1]
. Rearranging the above inequalities give

\displaystyle\widetilde{D}_{\alpha}(\mu P\|\nu P)

\displaystyle\leq\widetilde{\eta}^{(R_{\alpha})}(P)\widetilde{D}_{\alpha}(\mu\|\nu),

and hence

\widetilde{\eta}^{(D_{\alpha})}(P)\leq\widetilde{\eta}^{(R_{\alpha})}(P).

On the other hand, using the definition of
\widetilde{\eta}^{(D_{\alpha})}(P)
, we see that

\displaystyle\widetilde{D}_{\alpha}(\mu^{*}_{n}P\|\nu^{*}_{n}P))\leq\widetilde{\eta}^{(D_{\alpha})}(P)\widetilde{D}_{\alpha}(\mu^{*}_{n}\|\nu^{*}_{n}),

and applying the non-decreasing transformation
t\mapsto\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\ln(1+(\alpha-1)t)
gives

\displaystyle\widetilde{R}_{\alpha}(\mu^{*}_{n}P\|\nu^{*}_{n}P))\leq\dfrac{1}{\alpha-1}\ln(1+(\alpha-1)\widetilde{\eta}^{(D_{\alpha})}(P)\widetilde{D}_{\alpha}(\mu^{*}_{n}\|\nu^{*}_{n})).

Now, we divide both sides by
\widetilde{R}_{\alpha}(\mu^{*}_{n}\|\nu^{*}_{n})>0
and pass to
n\to\infty
to yield

\widetilde{\eta}^{(R_{\alpha})}(P)\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}\dfrac{\ln(1+(\alpha-1)\widetilde{\eta}^{(D_{\alpha})}(P)\widetilde{D}_{\alpha}(\mu^{*}_{n}\|\nu^{*}_{n}))}{\ln(1+(\alpha-1)\widetilde{D}_{\alpha}(\mu^{*}_{n}\|\nu^{*}_{n}))}\leq 1.

The remaining inequalities can be obtained analogously by simply replacing
\mu^{*}_{n},\nu^{*}_{n}
by
U_{n},V_{n}
respectively and
\widetilde{D}_{\alpha},\widetilde{R}_{\alpha}
by their untilded counterparts.

For the case of
\beta\in(0,1)
, similar to the reasoning above we obtain that

\displaystyle\widetilde{\eta}^{(R_{\beta})}(P)\leq\begin{cases}\dfrac{\ln(1+(\beta-1)\widetilde{\eta}^{(D_{\beta})}(P)\widetilde{D}_{\beta}(\mu^{*}\|\nu^{*}))}{\ln(1+(\beta-1)\widetilde{D}_{\beta}(\mu^{*}\|\nu^{*}))}\leq\widetilde{\eta}^{(D_{\beta})}(P),\quad&\textrm{if }\,\mu^{*}\neq\nu^{*},\\
\widetilde{\eta}^{(D_{\beta})}(P),\quad&\textrm{if }\,\mu^{*}=\nu^{*},\end{cases}

where we use again (
18
) in the second inequality and
\beta\in(0,1)
.

Item
(iv).
: First, we note that he mapping
P\mapsto\eta^{f}(P)=\widetilde{\eta}^{f}(P)
is convex according to
(
Cohen et al. 1993a
, Section 4)
. Next, for
P,M\neq L\in\mathcal{L}
, the ratio

\displaystyle(M,L)\mapsto\dfrac{R_{\alpha}(MP\|LP)}{R_{\alpha}(M\|L)}

is jointly convex when
\alpha\in(0,1)
by Proposition
3.2
, and taking the supremum over
M\neq L
preserves its convexity.

Item
(v).
: It follows readily from item
(ii).
and the submultiplicativity of
\widetilde{\eta}^{f}
as in
(
Cohen et al. 1993a
, Section
4
)
.

Item
(vi).
: Thanks to the skew symmetry property of
R_{\alpha}
as in Proposition
3.6
, we see that for
P,M\neq L\in\mathcal{L}
,

\displaystyle\dfrac{R_{\alpha}(MP\|LP)}{R_{\alpha}(M\|L)}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{R_{1-\alpha}(LP\|MP)}{R_{1-\alpha}(L\|M)},

which yields the desired result by taking the supremum over
M\neq L\in\mathcal{L}
. The analogous result for
D_{\alpha}
holds due to the skew symmetry of
D_{\alpha}
, that is,
D_{\alpha}(M\|L)=\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}D_{\alpha}(L\|M)
.

Item
(vii).
: It follows directly from the equivalence property of
R_{\alpha}
in Proposition
3.6
.

Item
(viii).
: If
P
is scrambling, then according to
(
Cohen et al. 1993a
, Theorem 4.2)
,
\eta^{f}(P)<1
. In particular,
\eta^{(D_{\alpha})}(P)=\widetilde{\eta}^{(D_{\alpha})}(P)<1
by item
(ii).
. By item
(iv).
, this yields
\eta^{(R_{\beta})}(P),\widetilde{\eta}^{(R_{\beta})}(P)<1
. On the other hand, if
P
is not scrambling, then by
(
Cohen et al. 1993a
, Theorem 4.2)
again,
\eta^{f}(P)=1
. In particular,
\eta^{(D_{\alpha})}(P)=\widetilde{\eta}^{(D_{\alpha})}(P)=1
by item
(ii).
. Using again item
(iv).
, this yields
\eta^{(R_{\beta})}(P),\widetilde{\eta}^{(R_{\beta})}(P)=1
.

Item
(ix).
: Using
(
Cohen et al. 1993a
, Section 4)
, unit rank of
P
is equivalent to
\eta^{f}(P)=0
. The rest of the proof is similar to that of item
(viii).
and is omitted.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

In Proposition
3.9
item
(viii).
, a transition matrix
P\in\mathcal{L}
is called scrambling if for each pair of rows
(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}
, there exists a column
z\in\mathcal{X}
such that
P(x,z),P(y,z)>0
, i.e.

\min_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{z\in\mathcal{X}}P(x,z)\wedge P(y,z)>0,

and this means that every two rows of
P
are not orthogonal, see
(
Seneta 2006a
, Theorem 3.1)
.

In Section
4.3
, we will use the ergodicity coefficients to give the relationship between Cesàro mixing time and classical mixing time.

4
Applications

The main aim of this section is to provide applications of these notions of information divergences and ergodicity coefficients in the context of Markov chain mixing and binary hypothesis testing.

4.1
Binary hypothesis testing between Markov chains

Given
n
pairs of i.i.d. data
\left\{(X_{1},Y_{1}),(X_{2},Y_{2}),...,(X_{n},Y_{n})\right\}
which are observed from an arbitrary inital distribution
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
and transition matrix
P
, i.e.
X_{i}\sim\pi
and
Y_{i}|X_{i}\sim P(X_{i},\cdot)
. Note that in this section
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
is given and needs not be the stationary distribution of
P
. Then, we consider the following hypothesis testing problem between transition matrices:

H_{0}:P=P_{0}\quad\longleftrightarrow\quad H_{1}:P=P_{1},\quad\pi\kern 5.0pt\text{is known}.

(19)

Denote
Z_{i}:=(X_{i},Y_{i})\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}
, therefore
Z_{i}
follows the edge measure
Z_{i}\sim Q(x,y):=\pi(x)P(x,y)
, and the hypothesis testing (
19
) is equivalent to the following hypothesis testing between edge measures:

H_{0}^{\prime}:Q=Q_{0}\quad\longleftrightarrow\quad H_{1}^{\prime}:Q=Q_{1},

(20)

where
Q_{i}(x,y)=\pi(x)P_{i}(x,y)
for
i=0,1
.

Now, let
A_{n}\subseteq(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X})^{n}
be the region of rejecting
H_{0}
or
H_{0}^{\prime}
, and
A_{n}^{c}
be the region of accepting
H_{0}
or
H_{0}^{\prime}
, then the first and second type error are

\alpha_{n}:=Q_{0}^{n}(A_{n}),\quad\beta_{n}:=Q_{1}^{n}(A_{n}^{c}),

(21)

where
Q_{0}^{n}
and
Q_{1}^{n}
are extended measures from
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}
to
(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X})^{n}
. Using
(
Cover & Thomas 2012a
, Theorem 11.7.1)
to apply Neyman-Pearson lemma on (
20
), we can find that the optimal test for both (
19
) and (
20
) has the form of likelihood ratio test, i.e.

A_{n}=A_{n}(T)=\left\{\mathbb{z}=(z_{1},z_{2},...,z_{n})\in(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X})^{n}:\dfrac{Q_{0}(z_{1})Q_{0}(z_{2})...Q_{0}(z_{n})}{Q_{1}(z_{1})Q_{1}(z_{2})...Q_{1}(z_{n})}<T\right\},

(22)

where there is a slight abuse of notation regarding
Q_{0}
and
Q_{1}
.
Then, we have the following asymptotic equipartition property (AEP):

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\{Z_{i}=(X_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
be i.i.d. random variables drawn from
Q_{0}(x,y)=\pi(x)P_{0}(x,y)
. For another edge measure
Q_{1}(x,y)=\pi(x)P_{1}(x,y)
, we have

\dfrac{1}{n}\ln\dfrac{Q_{0}(Z_{1})Q_{0}(Z_{2})...Q_{0}(Z_{n})}{Q_{1}(Z_{1})Q_{1}(Z_{2})...Q_{1}(Z_{n})}\xrightarrow{a.s.}R_{1}(P_{0}\|P_{1})\quad as\kern 5.0ptn\rightarrow\infty,

(23)

where we recall that
R_{1}(P_{0}\|P_{1})
is the
\mathrm{KL}
divergence from
P_{1}
to
P_{0}
as defined in Proposition
3.4
.

Proof.

By strong law of large numbers, recalling the definition of
R_{1}(P_{0}\|P_{1})
defined in Proposition
3.4
, we have

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{n}\ln\dfrac{Q_{0}(Z_{1})Q_{0}(Z_{2})...Q_{0}(Z_{n})}{Q_{1}(Z_{1})Q_{1}(Z_{2})...Q_{1}(Z_{n})}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ln\dfrac{Q_{0}(Z_{i})}{Q_{1}(Z_{i})}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{a.s.}\mathbb{E}_{Z_{1}\sim Q_{0}}\left[\ln\dfrac{Q_{0}(Z_{1})}{Q_{1}(Z_{1})}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y}\pi(x)P_{0}(x,y)\ln\dfrac{P_{0}(x,y)}{P_{1}(x,y)}=R_{1}(P_{0}\|P_{1}).

∎

Next, we will consider the Bayesian case. Suppose
P=P_{0}
has a prior probability
\pi_{0}
and
P=P_{1}
has a prior probability
\pi_{1}
, where
\pi_{0}+\pi_{1}=1
and
0<\pi_{0}<1
. Then, the overall Bayesian error for both (
19
) and (
20
) is

P_{e}(A_{n})=\pi_{0}\alpha_{n}+\pi_{1}\beta_{n},

(24)

and we need to estimate the best achievable Bayesian error

\displaystyle P_{e}^{*}(n)

\displaystyle:=\inf_{A_{n}\subseteq(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X})^{n}}P_{e}(A_{n})

(25)

\displaystyle=\inf_{A_{n}\subseteq(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X})^{n}}\left\{\pi_{0}Q_{0}^{n}(A_{n})+\pi_{1}Q_{1}^{n}(A_{n}^{c})\right\}.

Before presenting the estimation of
P_{e}^{*}(n)
, we will first give a rigorous definition of Chernoff information between transition matrices
P_{0}
and
P_{1}
:

\displaystyle C(P_{0},P_{1})

\displaystyle:=\max_{0\leq\alpha\leq 1}(1-\alpha)R_{\alpha}(P_{0}\|P_{1})

(26)

\displaystyle=\max_{0\leq\alpha\leq 1}-\ln\sum_{x,y}\pi(x)P_{0}^{\alpha}(x,y)P_{1}^{1-\alpha}(x,y).

(27)

When
\alpha>1
,
(1-\alpha)R_{\alpha}(P_{0}\|P_{1})\leq 0
, hence (
26
) is in accordance with
17
in Proposition
3.8
using (
16
), that is,

\displaystyle\sup_{\alpha>0}\inf_{P\in\mathcal{L}}\left\{\alpha D_{KL}(P\|M)+(1-\alpha)D_{KL}(P\|L)\right\}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\alpha>0}(1-\alpha)R_{\alpha}(P_{0}\|P_{1})

\displaystyle=\max_{0\leq\alpha\leq 1}(1-\alpha)R_{\alpha}(P_{0}\|P_{1}).

Then, we have the following result using Chernoff information.

Theorem 4.2
.

Recalling the best achievable Bayesian error defined in (
25
), we have

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}-\dfrac{1}{n}\ln P_{e}^{*}(n)=C(P_{0},P_{1}),

(28)

Proof.

According to
(
Cover & Thomas 2012a
, Theorem 11.9.1)
, denote

Q_{\lambda}(x,y):=\dfrac{Q_{0}^{\lambda}(x,y)Q_{1}^{1-\lambda}(x,y)}{\sum_{x,y}Q_{0}^{\lambda}(x,y)Q_{1}^{1-\lambda}(x,y)},

then we have

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}-\dfrac{1}{n}\ln P_{e}^{*}(n)

\displaystyle=\max_{0\leq\lambda\leq 1}\min\left\{\widetilde{R}_{1}(Q_{\lambda}\|Q_{0}),\widetilde{R}_{1}(Q_{\lambda}\|Q_{1})\right\}

\displaystyle=\max_{0\leq\alpha\leq 1}-\ln\sum_{x,y}Q_{0}^{\alpha}(x,y)Q_{1}^{1-\alpha}(x,y).

Since

\sum_{x,y}Q_{0}^{\alpha}(x,y)Q_{1}^{1-\alpha}(x,y)=\sum_{x,y}\pi(x)P_{0}^{\alpha}(x,y)P_{1}^{1-\alpha}(x,y),

and by the definition of
C(P_{0},P_{1})
in (
26
), the statement is verified.
∎

4.2
Information-theoretic mixing times and their spectral bounds

For given
\varepsilon>0
and transition matrix
P\in\mathcal{L}
which admits
\pi
as its stationary distribution, we first introduce three notions of average mixing times based upon suitable information divergences. We define the
\pi
-weighted
\alpha
-divergence mixing time,
\pi
-weighted Rényi divergence mixing time of the order
\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,+\infty)
and the
\pi
-weighted total variation distance mixing time to be respectively

\displaystyle\bar{t}_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(D_{\alpha})}(\varepsilon,P)

\displaystyle:=\inf\left\{t>0:D_{\alpha}(P^{t}\|\Pi)<\varepsilon\right\},

(29)

\displaystyle\bar{t}_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(R_{\alpha})}(\varepsilon,P)

\displaystyle:=\inf\left\{t>0:R_{\alpha}(P^{t}\|\Pi)<\varepsilon\right\},

(30)

\displaystyle\bar{t}_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(\mathrm{TV})}(\varepsilon,P)

\displaystyle:=\inf\left\{t>0:\mathrm{TV}(P^{t},\Pi)<\varepsilon\right\},

(31)

where
\Pi
is a
|\mathcal{X}|\times|\mathcal{X}|
matrix with each row being
\pi
, i.e.
\Pi(x,y)=\pi(y)
, for all
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
. These average mixing times can be used to assess the quantitative convergence rate of the transition matrix
P^{t}
towards
\Pi
. In the first main result below, we obtain spectral bounds of these mixing times in terms of
\gamma_{*}
, the absolute spectral gap of
P
, which is defined as

\lambda_{*}:=\max\left\{|\lambda|:\lambda\kern 5.0pt\text{is an eigenvalue of}\kern 5.0ptP,\kern 5.0pt\lambda\neq 1\right\},

and we also call
\gamma_{*}:=1-\lambda_{*}
as the absolute spectral gap of
P
. By
(
Levin & Peres 2017a
, Lemma 12.1)
, if
P
is aperiodic and irreducible, then
\gamma_{*}>0
.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
P\in\mathcal{L}(\pi)
be an irreducible, aperiodic and
\pi
-reversible transition matrix, and we denote
\pi_{\mathrm{min}}:=\min_{x}\pi(x)>0
. For
\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,+\infty)
, if
\varepsilon<\min\left\{\frac{1}{2|\alpha-1|},\frac{4\alpha}{|\alpha-1|}\right\}
, we have

\displaystyle\bar{t}_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(D_{\alpha})}(\varepsilon,P)

\displaystyle\leq\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{\gamma_{*}}\left(\ln\dfrac{2\alpha}{(\alpha-1)\pi_{\mathrm{min}}}+\ln\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\right),\quad\textrm{if }\,\alpha>1,\\
\dfrac{1}{\gamma_{*}}\left(\ln\dfrac{8\alpha}{(1-\alpha)\pi_{\mathrm{min}}}+\ln\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\right),\quad\textrm{if }\,0<\alpha<1,\end{cases}

and

\displaystyle\bar{t}_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(D_{\alpha})}(\varepsilon,P)

\displaystyle\geq\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{2\ln\frac{1}{1-\gamma_{*}}}\left(\ln\dfrac{\pi_{\mathrm{min}}^{2}}{8}+\ln\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\right),\quad\textrm{if }\,\alpha>1,\\
\dfrac{1}{2\ln\frac{1}{1-\gamma_{*}}}\left(\ln\dfrac{\pi_{\mathrm{min}}^{2}\alpha}{16}+\ln\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\right),\quad\textrm{if }\,0<\alpha<1,\end{cases}

where
\gamma_{*}
is the absolute spectral gap of
P
.

Proof.

P
is irreducible and aperiodic implies
\gamma_{*}>0
.
According to
(
Levin & Peres 2017a
, Eq.12.13)
, we have

\left|\dfrac{P^{t}(x,y)}{\pi(y)}-1\right|\leq\dfrac{(1-\gamma_{*})^{t}}{\sqrt{\pi(x)\pi(y)}}\leq\dfrac{e^{-\gamma_{*}t}}{\pi_{\text{min}}}.

(32)

Then, for
\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,+\infty)
, we rewrite
D_{\alpha}(P^{t}\|\Pi)
as

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}(P^{t}\|\Pi)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\alpha-1}\sum_{x}\pi(x)\sum_{y}\Pi(x,y)\left(\left(\dfrac{P^{t}(x,y)}{\Pi(x,y)}\right)^{\alpha}-1\right)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\alpha-1}\sum_{x}\pi(x)\sum_{y}\pi(y)\left(\left(\dfrac{P^{t}(x,y)}{\pi(y)}\right)^{\alpha}-1\right).

Note that for
x\geq-1
, when
\alpha>1
,
\left|(1+x)^{\alpha}-1\right|\leq(1+|x|)^{\alpha}-1
. Moreover, for
x,y>0
, it can be easily verified that
\left(1+\frac{x}{y}\right)^{y}<e^{x}
. Therefore, using (
32
), for
\alpha>1
, we have

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}(P^{t}\|\Pi)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\alpha-1}\sum_{x}\pi(x)\sum_{y}\pi(y)\left(\left(1+\dfrac{P^{t}(x,y)}{\pi(y)}-1\right)^{\alpha}-1\right)

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{1}{\alpha-1}\sum_{x}\pi(x)\sum_{y}\pi(y)\left(\left(1+\left|\dfrac{P^{t}(x,y)}{\pi(y)}-1\right|\right)^{\alpha}-1\right)

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{1}{\alpha-1}\sum_{x}\pi(x)\sum_{y}\pi(y)\left(\exp\left(\alpha\left|\dfrac{P^{t}(x,y)}{\pi(y)}-1\right|\right)-1\right)

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{1}{\alpha-1}\left(\exp\left(\dfrac{\alpha}{\pi_{\mathrm{min}}}e^{-\gamma_{*}t}\right)-1\right).

Therefore, in order to have
D_{\alpha}(P^{t}\|\Pi)<\varepsilon
, we have

\displaystyle\bar{t}_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(D_{\alpha})}(\varepsilon,P)

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{1}{\gamma_{*}}\left(\ln\dfrac{\alpha}{\pi_{\mathrm{min}}}+\ln\dfrac{1}{\ln\left(1+(\alpha-1)\varepsilon\right)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{1}{\gamma_{*}}\left(\ln\dfrac{2\alpha}{(\alpha-1)\pi_{\mathrm{min}}}+\ln\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\right),\quad\alpha>1,

where the last inequality comes from
\varepsilon<\frac{1}{\alpha-1}
and
\ln(1+x)\geq x-\frac{x^{2}}{2}\geq\frac{x}{2}
for
x<1
.

When
0<\alpha<1
, denote
h_{t}=h_{t}(x,y):=P^{t}(x,y)/\pi(y)\geq 0
and
g_{t}=g_{t}(x,y):=1-h_{t}(x,y)\leq 1
as shorthand notation. From (
32
), we directly have for all
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
,

|g_{t}(x,y)|\leq\dfrac{e^{-\gamma_{*}t}}{\pi_{\mathrm{min}}},\quad 1-\dfrac{e^{-\gamma_{*}t}}{\pi_{\mathrm{min}}}\leq h_{t}(x,y)\leq 1+\dfrac{e^{-\gamma_{*}t}}{\pi_{\mathrm{min}}}.

(33)

Therefore, for
t>\frac{1}{\gamma_{*}}\ln\frac{2}{\pi_{\mathrm{min}}}
, we have
\frac{1}{2}\leq h_{t}(x,y)\leq\frac{3}{2}
, in which case

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}(P^{t}\|\Pi)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\alpha-1}\sum_{x}\pi(x)\sum_{y}\pi(y)\left(\left(\dfrac{P^{t}(x,y)}{\pi(y)}\right)^{\alpha}-1\right)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{1-\alpha}\sum_{x}\pi(x)\sum_{y}\pi(y)\left(1-h_{t}^{\alpha}(x,y)\right)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{1-\alpha}\sum_{x}\pi(x)\sum_{y}\pi(y)\left(\left(g_{t}(x,y)+h_{t}(x,y)\right)^{\alpha}-h_{t}^{\alpha}(x,y)\right)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{1-\alpha}\sum_{x}\pi(x)\sum_{y}\pi(y)h_{t}^{\alpha}\left(\left(1+\dfrac{g_{t}}{h_{t}}\right)^{\alpha}-1\right)

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{1}{1-\alpha}\sum_{x}\pi(x)\sum_{y}\pi(y)h_{t}^{\alpha}\left(\left(1+\left|\dfrac{g_{t}}{h_{t}}\right|\right)^{\alpha}-1\right)

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{1}{1-\alpha}\sum_{x}\pi(x)\sum_{y}\pi(y)h_{t}^{\alpha}\left(\exp\left(\alpha\left|\dfrac{g_{t}}{h_{t}}\right|\right)-1\right)

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{1}{1-\alpha}\left(\dfrac{3}{2}\right)^{\alpha}\left(\exp\left(\dfrac{2\alpha}{\pi_{\mathrm{min}}}e^{-\gamma_{*}t}\right)-1\right)

\displaystyle<\dfrac{2}{1-\alpha}\left(\exp\left(\dfrac{2\alpha}{\pi_{\mathrm{min}}}e^{-\gamma_{*}t}\right)-1\right),

where the second inequality comes from
\left(1+\frac{x}{y}\right)^{y}<e^{x}
for
x,y>0
, the third inequality comes from (
33
), and the last inequality comes from
\alpha<1
. Hence, in order to have the last term above smaller than
\varepsilon
, apart from
t>\frac{1}{\gamma_{*}}\ln\frac{2}{\pi_{\mathrm{min}}}
, we still need

t>\dfrac{1}{\gamma_{*}}\left(\ln\dfrac{2\alpha}{\pi_{\mathrm{min}}}+\ln\dfrac{1}{\ln\left(1+\frac{1-\alpha}{2}\varepsilon\right)}\right),

which implies

\displaystyle\bar{t}_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(D_{\alpha})}(\varepsilon,P)

\displaystyle\leq\max\left\{\dfrac{1}{\gamma_{*}}\ln\dfrac{2}{\pi_{\mathrm{min}}},\dfrac{1}{\gamma_{*}}\left(\ln\dfrac{2\alpha}{\pi_{\mathrm{min}}}+\ln\dfrac{1}{\ln\left(1+\frac{1-\alpha}{2}\varepsilon\right)}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{1}{\gamma_{*}}\max\left\{\ln\dfrac{2}{\pi_{\mathrm{min}}},\left(\ln\dfrac{8\alpha}{(1-\alpha)\pi_{\mathrm{min}}}+\ln\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\gamma_{*}}\left(\ln\dfrac{8\alpha}{(1-\alpha)\pi_{\mathrm{min}}}+\ln\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\right),\quad 0<\alpha<1,

where the second inequality comes from
\varepsilon<\frac{1}{1-\alpha}
and
\ln(1+x)\geq\frac{x}{2}
for
x<1
, and the equality comes from
\varepsilon<\frac{4\alpha}{1-\alpha}
.

On the other hand, we first recall the Pinsker’s inequality Proposition
3.5
which implies for
\alpha>1
,

\dfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{TV}^{2}(P^{t},\Pi)\leq R_{\alpha}(P^{t}\|\Pi)=\dfrac{1}{\alpha-1}\ln\left(1+(\alpha-1)D_{\alpha}(P^{t}\|\Pi)\right),

(34)

and for
0<\alpha<1
,

\dfrac{\alpha}{2}\mathrm{TV}^{2}(P^{t},\Pi)\leq R_{\alpha}(P^{t}\|\Pi)=\dfrac{1}{\alpha-1}\ln\left(1+(\alpha-1)D_{\alpha}(P^{t}\|\Pi)\right).

(35)

Then we use the statement in
(
Levin & Peres 2017a
, Remark 12.6)
that if
\lambda
is an eigenvalue of
P
and
\lambda\neq 1
, there exists

d^{(\mathrm{TV})}(t):=\max_{x}\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{y}\left|P^{t}(x,y)-\pi(y)\right|\geq\dfrac{1}{2}|\lambda|^{t},

(36)

which yields

\mathrm{TV}(P^{t}\|\Pi)=\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{x,y}\pi(x)\left|P^{t}(x,y)-\pi(y)\right|\geq\pi_{\mathrm{min}}d^{(\mathrm{TV})}(t)\geq\dfrac{\pi_{\mathrm{min}}}{2}|\lambda|^{t},

since
\lambda
is arbitrary, we have
\mathrm{TV}(P^{t}\|\Pi)\geq\frac{\pi_{\mathrm{min}}}{2}(1-\gamma_{*})^{t}
. Combining with the inequalities (
34
) and (
35
) above, we obtain that when
\alpha>1
,

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}(P^{t}\|\Pi)

\displaystyle\geq\dfrac{1}{\alpha-1}\left(\exp\left(\dfrac{\alpha-1}{2}\mathrm{TV}^{2}(P^{t},\Pi)\right)-1\right)

\displaystyle\geq\dfrac{1}{\alpha-1}\left(\exp\left(\dfrac{(\alpha-1)\pi_{\mathrm{min}}^{2}}{8}(1-\gamma_{*})^{2t}\right)-1\right),

and when
0<\alpha<1
, similarly we have

D_{\alpha}(P^{t}\|\Pi)\geq\dfrac{1}{\alpha-1}\left(\exp\left(\dfrac{\alpha(\alpha-1)\pi_{\mathrm{min}}^{2}}{8}(1-\gamma_{*})^{2t}\right)-1\right).

Therefore, we can obtain

\displaystyle\bar{t}_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(D_{\alpha})}(\varepsilon,P)

\displaystyle\geq\dfrac{1}{2\ln\frac{1}{1-\gamma_{*}}}\left(\ln\dfrac{\pi_{\mathrm{min}}^{2}(\alpha-1)}{8}+\ln\dfrac{1}{\ln(1+(\alpha-1)\varepsilon)}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\dfrac{1}{2\ln\frac{1}{1-\gamma_{*}}}\left(\ln\dfrac{\pi_{\mathrm{min}}^{2}}{8}+\ln\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\right),\quad\alpha>1,

where the second inequality comes from
\ln(1+x)\leq x
. Similary for
0<\alpha<1
, we have

\displaystyle\bar{t}_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(D_{\alpha})}(\varepsilon,P)

\displaystyle\geq\dfrac{1}{2\ln\frac{1}{1-\gamma_{*}}}\left(\ln\dfrac{\pi_{\mathrm{min}}^{2}\alpha(1-\alpha)}{8}+\ln\left|\dfrac{1}{\ln(1+(\alpha-1)\varepsilon)}\right|\right)

\displaystyle\geq\dfrac{1}{2\ln\frac{1}{1-\gamma_{*}}}\left(\ln\dfrac{\pi_{\mathrm{min}}^{2}\alpha}{16}+\ln\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\right),\quad 0<\alpha<1,

where the second inequality comes from
\ln(1+x)\geq\frac{x}{1+x}
for
x>-1
and
\varepsilon<\frac{1}{2(1-\alpha)}
.
∎

From the definition of
\alpha
-divergence and Rényi divergence of Markov chains, we see that

R_{\alpha}(P^{t}\|\Pi)<\varepsilon\iff D_{\alpha}(P^{t}\|\Pi)<\dfrac{1}{\alpha-1}\left(e^{(\alpha-1)\varepsilon}-1\right),

and hence the
\pi
-weighted
\alpha
-divergence mixing time and the Rényi divergence mixing time are related via

\bar{t}_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(R_{\alpha})}(\varepsilon,P)=\bar{t}_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(D_{\alpha})}\left(\dfrac{1}{\alpha-1}\left(e^{(\alpha-1)\varepsilon}-1\right),P\right).

(37)

Thus, to give spectral bounds on
\bar{t}_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(R_{\alpha})}(\varepsilon,P)
, using (
37
) yields the following corollary of Theorem
4.3
:

Corollary 4.1
.

Let
P\in\mathcal{L}(\pi)
be an irreducible, aperiodic, reversible transition matrix and we denote by
\pi_{\mathrm{min}}:=\min_{x}\pi(x)>0
. For
\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,+\infty)
, if
\varepsilon<\min\left\{\frac{1}{2|\alpha-1|},\frac{4\alpha}{|\alpha-1|}\right\}
, we have

\displaystyle\bar{t}_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(R_{\alpha})}(\varepsilon,P)

\displaystyle\leq\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{\gamma_{*}}\left(\ln\dfrac{2\alpha}{\pi_{\mathrm{min}}}+\ln\dfrac{1}{e^{(\alpha-1)\varepsilon}-1}\right),&\quad\textrm{if }\,\alpha>1,\\
\dfrac{1}{\gamma_{*}}\left(\ln\dfrac{8\alpha}{\pi_{\mathrm{min}}}+\ln\dfrac{1}{1-e^{(\alpha-1)\varepsilon}}\right),&\quad\textrm{if }\,0<\alpha<1,\end{cases}

and

\displaystyle\bar{t}_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(R_{\alpha})}(\varepsilon,P)

\displaystyle\geq\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{2\ln\frac{1}{1-\gamma_{*}}}\left(\ln\dfrac{(\alpha-1)\pi_{\mathrm{min}}^{2}}{8}+\ln\dfrac{1}{e^{(\alpha-1)\varepsilon}-1}\right),&\quad\textrm{if }\,\alpha>1,\\
\dfrac{1}{2\ln\frac{1}{1-\gamma_{*}}}\left(\ln\dfrac{\pi_{\mathrm{min}}^{2}\alpha(1-\alpha)}{16}+\ln\dfrac{1}{1-e^{(\alpha-1)\varepsilon}}\right),&\quad\textrm{if }\,0<\alpha<1,\end{cases}

where
\gamma_{*}
is the absolute spectral gap of
P
.

Remark 4.1
.

To study the order of
\gamma_{*}
in the upper and lower bound of
\bar{t}_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(D_{\alpha})}(\varepsilon,P)
in Theorem
4.3
and
\bar{t}_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(R_{\alpha})}(\varepsilon,P)
in Corollary
4.1
, we can see that if
\gamma_{*}
is close to
0
, we have

\lim_{\gamma_{*}\rightarrow 0^{+}}\dfrac{1}{\gamma_{*}}\bigg/\dfrac{1}{\ln\frac{1}{1-\gamma_{*}}}=1,

which implies the upper bound and lower bound are approximately the same order with respect to
\gamma_{*}
in this situation. Indeed, in many statistical physics models or Markov chains of interest,
\gamma_{*}=\mathcal{O}(1/N^{a})
for some
a>0
and
N
being a problem-dependent parameter which is large. One example is random walk on
N
-dimensional hypercube which will be explained in Example
4.1
.

Next, we recall the classical worst-case mixing times based upon
\alpha
-divergence, Rényi divergence and total variation distance to be respectively, for
\varepsilon>0
,

\displaystyle t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(\mathrm{D_{\alpha}})}(\varepsilon,P)

\displaystyle:=\inf\left\{t>0:d^{(D_{\alpha})}(t)<\varepsilon\right\},

\displaystyle t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(\mathrm{R_{\alpha}})}(\varepsilon,P)

\displaystyle:=\inf\left\{t>0:d^{(R_{\alpha})}(t)<\varepsilon\right\},

\displaystyle t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(\mathrm{TV})}(\varepsilon,P)

\displaystyle:=\inf\left\{t>0:d^{(\mathrm{TV})}(t)<\varepsilon\right\},

(38)

where the worst-case divergences are defined as, for
t\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle d^{(D_{\alpha})}(t)

\displaystyle:=\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\widetilde{D}_{\alpha}(P^{t}(x,\cdot)\|\pi)=\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\dfrac{1}{\alpha-1}\left(\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}}\pi(y)\left(\dfrac{P^{t}(x,y)}{\pi(y)}\right)^{\alpha}-1\right),

\displaystyle d^{(R_{\alpha})}(t)

\displaystyle:=\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\widetilde{R}_{\alpha}(P^{t}(x,\cdot)\|\pi)=\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\dfrac{1}{\alpha-1}\ln\left(\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}}\pi(y)\left(\dfrac{P^{t}(x,y)}{\pi(y)}\right)^{\alpha}\right),

\displaystyle d^{(\mathrm{TV})}(t)

\displaystyle:=\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\left\|P^{t}(x,\cdot)-\pi\right\|_{\mathrm{TV}}=\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}}\left|P^{t}(x,y)-\pi(y)\right|.

We prove the following relationships between these
\pi
-weighted mixing times and and their worst-case mixing times counterpart, and in particular implies that the
\pi
-weighted mixing times and worst-case mixing times are of the same order under some reasonable assumptions on the absolute spectral gap
\gamma_{*}
:

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
P\in\mathcal{L}(\pi)
be an irreducible, aperiodic,
\pi
-reversible transition matrix, and we denote
\pi_{\mathrm{min}}:=\min_{x}\pi(x)>0
, then we have the following relationships:

(i).

t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(\mathrm{TV})}\left(\varepsilon,P\right)
and
\bar{t}_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(\mathrm{TV})}(\varepsilon,P)
: if
\varepsilon<\frac{\pi_{\mathrm{min}}^{3}}{4}
, then

\dfrac{1-\gamma_{*}}{2}t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(\mathrm{TV})}\left(\varepsilon,P\right)\leq\bar{t}_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(\mathrm{TV})}(\varepsilon,P)\leq t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(\mathrm{TV})}(\varepsilon,P).

(ii).

t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(D_{\alpha})}\left(\varepsilon,P\right)
and
\bar{t}_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(D_{\alpha})}(\varepsilon,P)
: if
\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}:=\min\left\{\frac{1}{2|\alpha-1|},\frac{4\alpha}{|\alpha-1|},\frac{|\alpha-1|\pi_{\mathrm{min}}^{6}}{128\alpha},\frac{\alpha|\alpha-1|\pi_{\mathrm{min}}^{6}}{2048}\right\}
, then

\dfrac{1-\gamma_{*}}{4}t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(D_{\alpha})}(\varepsilon,P)\leq\bar{t}_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(D_{\alpha})}(\varepsilon,P)\leq t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(D_{\alpha})}(\varepsilon,P),\quad\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,\infty).

(iii).

t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(R_{\alpha})}\left(\varepsilon,P\right)
and
\bar{t}_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(R_{\alpha})}(\varepsilon,P)
: if
\varepsilon<\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\ln(1+(\alpha-1)\varepsilon_{0})
with
\varepsilon_{0}
defined in item (
(ii).
), then

\dfrac{1-\gamma_{*}}{4}t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(R_{\alpha})}(\varepsilon,P)\leq\bar{t}_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(R_{\alpha})}(\varepsilon,P)\leq t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(R_{\alpha})}(\varepsilon,P),\quad\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,\infty).

Proof.

Item
(i).
: Recalling the definition of
\mathrm{TV}(P^{t},\Pi)
in Definition
2.5
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{TV}(P^{t},\Pi)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{x}\pi(x)\sum_{y}\left|P^{t}(x,y)-\pi(y)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{x}\pi(x)\left(\max_{x}\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{y}\left|P^{t}(x,y)-\pi(y)\right|\right)

\displaystyle=d^{(\mathrm{TV})}(t),

and

\displaystyle\mathrm{TV}(P^{t},\Pi)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{x}\pi(x)\sum_{y}\left|P^{t}(x,y)-\pi(y)\right|

\displaystyle\geq\pi_{\mathrm{min}}d^{(\mathrm{TV})}(t),

therefore we can obtain

t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(\mathrm{TV})}\left(\dfrac{\varepsilon}{\pi_{\mathrm{min}}},P\right)\leq\bar{t}_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(\mathrm{TV})}(\varepsilon,P)\leq t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(\mathrm{TV})}(\varepsilon,P).

(39)

According to
(
Levin & Peres 2017a
, Theorem 12.4, 12.5)
, there exists

\left(\dfrac{1}{\gamma_{*}}-1\right)\ln\dfrac{1}{2\varepsilon}\leq t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(\mathrm{TV})}(\varepsilon,P)\leq\dfrac{1}{\gamma_{*}}\ln\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon\pi_{\mathrm{min}}},

(40)

which implies

\displaystyle t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(\mathrm{TV})}\left(\dfrac{\varepsilon}{\pi_{\mathrm{min}}},P\right)\geq\left(\dfrac{1}{\gamma_{*}}-1\right)\ln\dfrac{\pi_{\mathrm{min}}}{2\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\geq(1-\gamma_{*})\left(1+\dfrac{\ln\frac{\pi_{\mathrm{min}}^{2}}{2}}{\ln\frac{1}{\varepsilon}-\ln\pi_{\mathrm{min}}}\right)t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(\mathrm{TV})}(\varepsilon,P)

\displaystyle\geq\dfrac{1-\gamma_{*}}{2}t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(\mathrm{TV})}(\varepsilon,P),

where the last inequality is from
\varepsilon<\dfrac{\pi_{\mathrm{min}}^{3}}{4}
. Plugging into (
39
), we have

\dfrac{1-\gamma_{*}}{2}t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(\mathrm{TV})}\left(\varepsilon,P\right)\leq\bar{t}_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(\mathrm{TV})}(\varepsilon,P)\leq t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(\mathrm{TV})}(\varepsilon,P).

Item
(ii).
: Similar to analysis in item
(i).
, we have

t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(D_{\alpha})}\left(\dfrac{\varepsilon}{\pi_{\mathrm{min}}},P\right)\leq\bar{t}_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(D_{\alpha})}(\varepsilon,P)\leq t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(D_{\alpha})}(\varepsilon,P).

Therefore, recalling Theorem
4.3
, for
\alpha>1
we have

\displaystyle\bar{t}_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(D_{\alpha})}(\varepsilon,P)\leq t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(D_{\alpha})}(\varepsilon,P)

\displaystyle\leq\bar{t}_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(D_{\alpha})}(\pi_{\mathrm{min}}\varepsilon,P)\leq\dfrac{1}{\gamma_{*}}\left(\ln\dfrac{2\alpha}{(\alpha-1)\pi_{\mathrm{min}}^{2}}+\ln\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\bar{t}_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(D_{\alpha})}(\varepsilon,P)\dfrac{2\ln\frac{1}{1-\gamma_{*}}}{\gamma_{*}}\dfrac{\ln\frac{1}{\varepsilon}+\ln\frac{2\alpha}{(\alpha-1)\pi_{\mathrm{min}}^{2}}}{\ln\frac{1}{\varepsilon}+\ln\frac{\pi_{\mathrm{min}}^{2}}{8}}

\displaystyle\leq\bar{t}_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(D_{\alpha})}(\varepsilon,P)\cdot\dfrac{4}{1-\gamma_{*}},\quad\alpha>1,

where the last inequality comes from
\ln\frac{1}{1-\gamma_{*}}\leq\frac{\gamma_{*}}{1-\gamma_{*}}
and
\varepsilon<\frac{(\alpha-1)\pi_{\mathrm{min}}^{6}}{128\alpha}
. For
0<\alpha<1
, we also have

\displaystyle\bar{t}_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(D_{\alpha})}(\varepsilon,P)\leq t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(D_{\alpha})}(\varepsilon,P)

\displaystyle\leq\bar{t}_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(D_{\alpha})}(\pi_{\mathrm{min}}\varepsilon,P)\leq\dfrac{1}{\gamma_{*}}\left(\ln\dfrac{8\alpha}{(1-\alpha)\pi_{\mathrm{min}}^{2}}+\ln\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\bar{t}_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(D_{\alpha})}(\varepsilon,P)\dfrac{2\ln\frac{1}{1-\gamma_{*}}}{\gamma_{*}}\dfrac{\ln\frac{1}{\varepsilon}+\ln\frac{8\alpha}{(1-\alpha)\pi_{\mathrm{min}}^{2}}}{\ln\frac{1}{\varepsilon}+\ln\frac{\alpha\pi_{\mathrm{min}}^{2}}{16}}

\displaystyle\leq\bar{t}_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(D_{\alpha})}(\varepsilon,P)\cdot\dfrac{4}{1-\gamma_{*}},\quad 0<\alpha<1,

where in the last inequality we have used
\varepsilon<\frac{\alpha(1-\alpha)\pi_{\mathrm{min}}^{6}}{2048}
.

Item
(iii).
: Similar to (
37
), since the mapping
t\mapsto\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\ln(1+(\alpha-1)t)
is increasing, we have

d^{(D_{\alpha})}(t)<\varepsilon\iff d^{(R_{\alpha})}(t)<\dfrac{1}{\alpha-1}\left(e^{(\alpha-1)\varepsilon}-1\right),

which implies

t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(R_{\alpha})}(\varepsilon,P)=t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(D_{\alpha})}\left(\dfrac{1}{\alpha-1}\left(e^{(\alpha-1)\varepsilon}-1\right),P\right).

Therefore, plugging into item
(ii).
we get the result.
∎

Theorem
4.4
illustrates that the mixing time under total variation distance and worst case distance are of the same order. As an illustration for Theorem
4.3
and
4.4
, we consider the following lazy random walk on high-dimensional hypercube.

Example 4.1

(Random walk on the
N
-dimensional hypercube)
.

Consider
\mathcal{X}=\{-1,1\}^{N}
where
N>1
. In this state space, at each step the chain uniformly select a coordinate from
N
coordinates and update the chosen coordinate to
-1
and
1
with probability
\frac{1}{2}
respectively. For
\mathbb{x},\mathbb{y}\in\mathcal{X}
, we denote
\mathbb{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{N})
and
\mathbb{y}=(y_{1},y_{2},...,y_{N})
, and the transition matrix
P
of the random walk on
\mathcal{X}
can be written as

P(\mathbb{x},\mathbb{y})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\dfrac{1}{N}Q(x_{i},y_{i})\mathbf{1}_{\{x_{j}=y_{j},j\neq i\}}(\mathbb{x},\mathbb{y}),

(41)

where
Q
is the transition matrix defined on
\mathcal{X}_{0}=\{-1,1\}
such that
Q(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}
for
x,y\in\mathcal{X}_{0}
, and therefore the chain under
P
is lazy. According to
(
Levin & Peres 2017a
, Example 12.16)
, there exists
\gamma_{*}=\frac{1}{N}
and
\pi_{\mathrm{min}}=2^{-N}
. Plugging into Theorem
4.3
, if
\varepsilon<\min\left\{\frac{1}{2|\alpha-1|},\frac{4\alpha}{|\alpha-1|}\right\}
, we have for
\alpha>1
,

\displaystyle\bar{t}_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(D_{\alpha})}(\varepsilon,P)

\displaystyle\leq N\left(N\ln 2+\ln\dfrac{2\alpha}{(\alpha-1)}+\ln\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\right),\quad\alpha>1,

\displaystyle\bar{t}_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(D_{\alpha})}(\varepsilon,P)

\displaystyle\geq\dfrac{N-1}{2}\left(\ln\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon}-(2N+3)\ln 2\right),\quad\alpha>1,

where we use
\ln\frac{N}{N-1}\leq\frac{1}{N-1}
in the second inequality. For
\alpha<1
, we similarly have

\displaystyle\bar{t}_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(D_{\alpha})}(\varepsilon,P)

\displaystyle\leq N\left(N\ln 2+\ln\dfrac{8\alpha}{(1-\alpha)}+\ln\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\right),\quad 0<\alpha<1,

\displaystyle\bar{t}_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(D_{\alpha})}(\varepsilon,P)

\displaystyle\geq\dfrac{N-1}{2}\left(\ln\alpha+\ln\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon}-(2N+4)\ln 2\right),\quad 0<\alpha<1,

Moreover, plugging
\gamma_{*}=\frac{1}{N}
and
\pi_{\mathrm{min}}=2^{-N}
into Theorem
4.4
and (
40
), when
\varepsilon<2^{-(3N+2)}
, we have

\dfrac{(N-1)^{2}}{2N}\ln\dfrac{1}{2\varepsilon}\leq\bar{t}_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(\mathrm{TV})}(\varepsilon,P)\leq N\left(N\ln 2+\ln\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\right).

4.3
Cesàro mixing time and ergodicity coefficients

In Section
4.2
, we have investigated various average or worst-case mixing times of Markov chains with irreducible, aperiodic and
\pi
-reversible stochastic matrices. However, the convergence results similar to Theorem
4.3
and
4.4
may not hold for a periodic transition matrix
P
, as
P
can have an eigenvalue of
-1
, which implies
\gamma_{*}=0
. This motivates us to study other notions of mixing times that are suitable for periodic chains.

One such alternative is to consider the so-called Cesáro mixing. Following similar notations in Section
4.2
, for irreducible transition matrix
P\in\mathcal{L}
that is
\pi
-stationary and
\varepsilon>0
, we define several Cesàro mixing times that are based upon information divergences as follows:

\displaystyle\bar{t}_{\mathrm{Ces}}^{(\mathrm{TV})}(\varepsilon,P)

\displaystyle:=\inf\left\{t>0:\mathrm{TV}\left(\dfrac{1}{t}\sum_{s=1}^{t}P^{s},\Pi\right)<\varepsilon\right\},

(42)

\displaystyle\bar{t}_{\mathrm{Ces}}^{(\mathrm{D_{\alpha}})}(\varepsilon,P)

\displaystyle:=\inf\left\{t>0:D_{\alpha}\left(\dfrac{1}{t}\sum_{s=1}^{t}P^{s}\bigg\|\Pi\right)<\varepsilon\right\},

\displaystyle\bar{t}_{\mathrm{Ces}}^{(\mathrm{R_{\alpha}})}(\varepsilon,P)

\displaystyle:=\inf\left\{t>0:R_{\alpha}\left(\dfrac{1}{t}\sum_{s=1}^{t}P^{s}\bigg\|\Pi\right)<\varepsilon\right\}.

Next, we offer inequalities between Cesàro mixing time and related mixing times as introduced earlier. These for instance can be used for upper bounding the Cesáro mixing time. Note that the following results in Theorem
4.5
hold without assuming
\pi
-reversibility or aperiodicity of
P
.

Theorem 4.5
.

For irreducible
\pi
-stationary
P\in\mathcal{L}
on
\mathcal{X}
and
\varepsilon>0
, we have

\bar{t}_{\mathrm{Ces}}^{(\mathrm{TV})}(\varepsilon,P)\leq\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon(1-2\varepsilon)}t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(\mathrm{TV})}(\varepsilon,P),

(43)

where
\bar{t}_{\mathrm{Ces}}^{(\mathrm{TV})}(\varepsilon,P)
and
t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(\mathrm{TV})}(\varepsilon,P)
are introduced respectively in (
42
) and (
38
).

Furthermore, we assume that there exists
0<\theta<1
such that the ergodicity coefficient
\eta^{(\mathrm{TV})}(P)\leq\theta
(i.e.
P
is scrambling). If
\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,+\infty)
and
D_{\alpha}(P\|\Pi)<\infty
, we have

\displaystyle\bar{t}_{\mathrm{Ces}}^{(\mathrm{TV})}(\varepsilon,P)

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\cdot\bar{t}_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(\mathrm{TV})}(\varepsilon,P)+\dfrac{1}{1-\theta},

(44)

\displaystyle\bar{t}_{\mathrm{Ces}}^{(D_{\alpha})}(\varepsilon,P)

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{D_{\alpha}(P\|\Pi)}{\varepsilon}\cdot\bar{t}_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(D_{\alpha})}(\varepsilon,P)+\dfrac{1}{1-\theta},

(45)

\displaystyle\bar{t}_{\mathrm{Ces}}^{(R_{\alpha})}(\varepsilon,P)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1+\varepsilon}{\varepsilon}D_{\alpha}(P\|\Pi)\cdot\bar{t}_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(R_{\alpha})}\left(\varepsilon,P\right)+\dfrac{1}{1-\theta},

(46)

where we recall that
\bar{t}_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(\mathrm{TV})}(\varepsilon,P),\bar{t}_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(D_{\alpha})}(\varepsilon,P),\bar{t}_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(R_{\alpha})}(\varepsilon,P)
are respectively defined in (
31
), (
29
) and (
30
).

Proof.

Denote
t=t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(\mathrm{TV})}(\varepsilon,P)
, and hence
d^{(\mathrm{TV})}(t)=\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\frac{1}{2}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}}\left|P^{t}(x,y)-\pi(y)\right|<\varepsilon
.
For any positive integer
l>0
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{TV}\left(\dfrac{1}{lt}\sum_{s=1}^{lt}P^{s},\Pi\right)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{x,y}\pi(x)\left|\dfrac{1}{lt}\sum_{s=1}^{lt}P^{s}(x,y)-\pi(y)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{1}{lt}\sum_{x}\pi(x)\sum_{s=1}^{lt}\left\|P^{s}(x,\cdot)-\pi(\cdot)\right\|_{\mathrm{TV}}

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{1}{lt}\sum_{s=1}^{lt}\max_{x}\left\|P^{s}(x,\cdot)-\pi(\cdot)\right\|_{\mathrm{TV}}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{lt}\sum_{s=1}^{lt}d^{(\mathrm{TV})}(s).

According to
(
Levin & Peres 2017a
, Lemma 4.10,4.11,Ex.4.2)
,
d(t)
is non-increasing, and there exists

d^{(\mathrm{TV})}(cs)\leq(2d^{(\mathrm{TV})}(s))^{c},\quad\text{c is any positive integer},

hence we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{TV}\left(\dfrac{1}{lt}\sum_{s=1}^{lt}P^{s},\Pi\right)

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{1}{lt}\left(t+t\varepsilon+t(2\varepsilon)^{2}+...+t(2\varepsilon)^{l-1}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{1}{l}\left(\dfrac{1}{1-2\varepsilon}-\varepsilon\right),

and in order for the right hand side above smaller than
\varepsilon
, it suffices to have

l\geq\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon(1-2\varepsilon)}\geq\left\lceil\dfrac{1-\varepsilon+2\varepsilon^{2}}{\varepsilon-2\varepsilon^{2}}\right\rceil.

Therefore,

\bar{t}_{\mathrm{Ces}}^{(\mathrm{TV})}(\varepsilon,P)\leq\dfrac{t}{\varepsilon(1-2\varepsilon)}=\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon(1-2\varepsilon)}t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(\mathrm{TV})}(\varepsilon,P).

To prove (
44
) and (
45
), by convexity of
D_{\alpha}(\cdot\|\cdot)
from Proposition
3.2
, for any integer
T>0
, it is easy to verify

\displaystyle\mathrm{TV}\left(\dfrac{1}{T}\sum_{s=1}^{T}P^{s},\Pi\right)

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{1}{T}\sum_{s=1}^{T}\mathrm{TV}(P^{s},\Pi),

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}\left(\dfrac{1}{T}\sum_{s=1}^{T}P^{s}\bigg\|\Pi\right)

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{1}{T}\sum_{s=1}^{T}D_{\alpha}(P^{s}\|\Pi).

Denote
t=\bar{t}_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(\mathrm{TV})}(\varepsilon,P)
, which gives
\mathrm{TV}(P^{t}\|\Pi)\leq\varepsilon
. According to Proposition
3.9
item
(i).
, we thus have, for
s>t
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{TV}(P^{s},\Pi)\leq\theta^{s-t}\varepsilon,

which yields

\displaystyle\mathrm{TV}\left(\dfrac{1}{T}\sum_{s=1}^{T}P^{s},\Pi\right)

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{1}{T}\left(\sum_{s=1}^{t}\mathrm{TV}(P^{s},\Pi)+\sum_{s=t+1}^{T}\mathrm{TV}(P^{s},\Pi)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{T}\left(t+\frac{\varepsilon\theta}{1-\theta}\right).

We set the right hand side above to be smaller than
\varepsilon
. As such we require that

T\geq\frac{t}{\varepsilon}+\frac{1}{1-\theta}\geq\left\lceil\frac{t}{\varepsilon}+\frac{\theta}{1-\theta}\right\rceil,

and therefore (
44
) is obtained. The proof for (
45
) is similar by recalling that
\eta^{(D_{\alpha})}(P)\leq\eta^{(\mathrm{TV})}(P)\leq\theta
as stated in Proposition
3.9
item
(i).
. For (
46
), analogous to the proof of (
37
) we see that

\bar{t}_{\mathrm{Ces}}^{(R_{\alpha})}(\varepsilon,P)=\bar{t}_{\mathrm{Ces}}^{(D_{\alpha})}\left(\dfrac{1}{\alpha-1}\left(e^{(\alpha-1)\varepsilon}-1\right),P\right),

then by (
45
) and (
37
) we have

\displaystyle\bar{t}_{\mathrm{Ces}}^{(R_{\alpha})}(\varepsilon,P)

\displaystyle=\bar{t}_{\mathrm{Ces}}^{(D_{\alpha})}\left(\dfrac{1}{\alpha-1}\left(e^{(\alpha-1)\varepsilon}-1\right),P\right)

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{\alpha-1}{e^{(\alpha-1)\varepsilon}-1}D_{\alpha}(P\|\Pi)\bar{t}_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(D_{\alpha})}\left(\dfrac{1}{\alpha-1}\left(e^{(\alpha-1)\varepsilon}-1\right),P\right)+\dfrac{1}{1-\theta}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\alpha-1}{e^{(\alpha-1)\varepsilon}-1}D_{\alpha}(P\|\Pi)\bar{t}_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(R_{\alpha})}\left(\varepsilon,P\right)+\dfrac{1}{1-\theta}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1+\varepsilon}{\varepsilon}D_{\alpha}(P\|\Pi)\bar{t}_{\mathrm{mix}}^{(R_{\alpha})}\left(\varepsilon,P\right)+\dfrac{1}{1-\theta},

where the last inequality comes from
e^{(\alpha-1)\varepsilon}\geq(\alpha-1)\varepsilon+1
for
\alpha>1
and
e^{(1-\alpha)\varepsilon}\geq(1-\alpha)\varepsilon+1
for
0<\alpha<1
.
∎

In (

), (
45
) and (
46
), we require that
P
is a scrambling matrix so that
\eta^{(\mathrm{TV})}(P)<1
(see Proposition
3.9
item
(viii).
and Remark
3.2
), while in many cases this condition is hard to guarantee. An alternative consideration is, if we can show that
\eta^{(\mathrm{TV})}(P^{t})<1
for some
t>0
, then by submultiplicativity of
\eta^{(\mathrm{TV})}(\cdot)
from Proposition
3.9
item
(v).
, it is a direct corollary that for all
s\geq t
,
\eta^{(\mathrm{TV})}(P^{s})\leq\eta^{(\mathrm{TV})}(P^{t})<1
, and we can still obtain similar bound for Cesàro mixing time. In the following example, we will show that in general Metropolis-Hastings algorithms under low temperature, the first time that the ergodicty/Dobrushin coefficient
\eta^{(\mathrm{TV})}(P^{t})<\varepsilon
for any
\varepsilon>0
has an exponential dependence on the exit height.

Example 4.2

(Ergodicity/Dobrushin coefficient of Metropolis-Hastings chain)
.

Let
Q
be a reversible transition matrix with respect to probability measure
\mu
on finite state space
\mathcal{X}
. Let
U:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be the target function,
\beta
be the inverse temperature, and we use the Metropolis-Hastings algorithm to sample from the Gibbs distribution
\pi_{\beta}(x)\propto\mu(x)e^{-\beta U(x)}
. The transition matrix of the chain can be written as

P_{\beta}(x,y)=\begin{cases}Q(x,y)\exp\left(-\beta(U(y)-U(x))_{+}\right),\quad&x\neq y,\\
1-\sum_{z\neq x}P_{\beta}(x,z),\quad&x=y.\end{cases}

Suppose the chain generated by
P_{\beta}
is
\{X_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
. Following
(
Catoni 2006a
)
and
(
Miclo 2002a
)
, for
A\subseteq\mathcal{X}
, we define the first hitting time of the set
A
to be
\tau_{A}:=\inf\{n:X_{n}\in A\}
, and the exit height
H(A)
as

\displaystyle h(x,A)

\displaystyle:=\lim_{\beta\rightarrow\infty}\dfrac{1}{\beta}\ln\left(\mathbb{E}_{x}[\tau_{A^{c}}]\right),

\displaystyle H(A)

\displaystyle:=\max_{x\in A}h(x,A).

Note that there is a dependency on the inverse temperature
\beta
of
\mathbb{E}_{x}[\tau_{A^{c}}]
via the underlying Metropolis-Hastings chain. Next, we define the product chain

P_{\beta}^{\otimes 2}\left((x_{1},x_{2}),(y_{1},y_{2})\right):=P_{\beta}(x_{1},y_{1})P_{\beta}(x_{2},y_{2}),\quad(x_{1},x_{2}),(y_{1},y_{2})\in\mathcal{X}^{2},

and the constant

H_{3}:=H(\mathcal{X}^{2}\setminus\Delta),\quad\Delta:=\{(x,x)\in\mathcal{X}^{2}:x\in\mathcal{X}\},

where
H_{3}
is an exit height with respect to the product chain
P_{\beta}^{\otimes 2}
.

For any
0<\varepsilon<1
, we define the first time that
\eta^{(\mathrm{TV})}(P_{\beta}^{t})<\varepsilon
as

\displaystyle t_{\mathrm{Dob}}^{(\mathrm{TV})}(\varepsilon,P_{\beta})

\displaystyle:=\inf\{t\geq 0:\eta^{(\mathrm{TV})}(P_{\beta}^{t})<\varepsilon\},

\displaystyle\widetilde{t}_{\mathrm{Dob}}^{(\mathrm{TV})}(\varepsilon,P_{\beta})

\displaystyle:=\inf\{t\geq 0:\widetilde{\eta}^{(\mathrm{TV})}(P_{\beta}^{t})<\varepsilon\},

then according to
(
Miclo 2002a
, Proposition 2.1)
, there exists

\lim_{\beta\rightarrow\infty}\dfrac{1}{\beta}\ln\left(\widetilde{t}_{\mathrm{Dob}}^{(\mathrm{TV})}(\varepsilon,P_{\beta})\right)=H_{3}.

Since
\eta^{(\mathrm{TV})}(P_{\beta}^{t})=\widetilde{\eta}^{(\mathrm{TV})}(P_{\beta}^{t})
by Proposition
3.9
item
(ii).
, we then have

\lim_{\beta\rightarrow\infty}\dfrac{1}{\beta}\ln\left(t_{\mathrm{Dob}}^{(\mathrm{TV})}(\varepsilon,P_{\beta})\right)=H_{3}.
