Abstract
. Sequences of bivariate orthogonal polynomials written as vector polynomials of increasing size satisfy a couple of three term relations with matrix coefficients. In this work, introducing a time-dependent parameter, we analyse a Lax-type pair system for the coefficients of the three term relations. We also deduce several characterizations relating the Lax-type pair, the shape of the weight, Stieltjes function, moments, a differential equation for the weight, and the bidimensional Toda-type systems.

Primary: 42C05; 33C50; 35Q53

1.
Introduction

Several areas of Classical Analysis have profound connections with the theory of orthogonal polynomials, such as moment problems, spectral theory of Jacobi matrices, and random matrices.

One of the more recent connection is with the integrable systems that starts in the 1970’s with the work
by Gardner, Greene, Kruskal and Miura in
[
11
,
12
]
,
with a method for the exact solution of the initial-value problem for the KdV equation, which is now referred to as the inverse scattering transform
[
1
]
.
In
[
15
]
, Lax put the inverse scattering method for solving the KdV equation into a more general framework, which subsequently paved the way to generalizations of the technique, as a method for solving other partial differential equations.

In fact, he considered, associated with the KdV equation, two time dependent operators
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{M}
, where
\mathcal{L}
is the operator of the spectral problem and
\mathcal{M}
is the operator governing the associated time evolution of the eigenfunctions

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}v=\lambda v,

\displaystyle\dfrac{\dd}{\dd t}v=\mathcal{M}v,

and hence

\displaystyle\dfrac{\dd}{\dd t}\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{M}\,\mathcal{L}-\mathcal{L}\,\mathcal{M},

if, and only if,

\displaystyle\dfrac{\dd}{\dd t}\lambda=0.

The relation between Toda lattices and univariate orthogonality has been analysed by several authors, for an introduction and first properties,
[
4
,
14
,
17
,
18
]
, among many others, can be consulted. In the multivariate case, some papers studied the case when several continuous time dependent variables are considered, see
[
6
,
7
]
, and in
[
5
]
multidimensional analogues of continuous and discrete-time Toda lattices with two or more space coordinates relating them with multiple orthogonal polynomials are studied.

The
2
D Toda lattices with only a time dependent variable can be seen in
[
8
]
.
There the authors depart from the knowledge of the weight representation and derive some Toda like lattices and at the end a Lax type representation for them.
In this paper we apply the method of moments, presented in
[
3
]
for the univariate Toda lattice, to a theory so far general of
2
D Toda lattice.

Usually, orthogonal polynomials in two variables are written as vector polynomials of increasing size, and they satisfy three term relations with matrix coefficients (see
[
10
]
).
We consider orthogonality with respect to a bivariate weight function and introduce a time-dependent parameter such that we work with an evolution weight function. Here we depart from the two tridiagonal block Jacobi operators and present the correspondence between dynamics of the
2
D Toda equations for the block-coefficients of the operators and the Stieltjes function associated with the block Jacobi operators.
This gives a method to solve an inverse problem,
i.e., we can get an integral representation for the coefficients of the block Jacobi matrices that satisfies a
2
D Toda equations in terms of a weight function completely determined by the data.

The work is organized in four sections. After this introduction, in Section
2
we briefly present the theory and basic facts of bivariate orthogonal polynomials that we will need in the sequel.
In Section
3
we give, in Theorem
3.1
, an interpretation of the
2
D Toda lattice in terms of the theory of bivariate orthogonal polynomials. This is in fact the main results of the paper, where we characterize the
2
D Toda lattice in terms of the moments and the Stieltjes function associated with the
2
D Toda lattice.
There we also give a representation for the weight that governs the
2
D Toda lattice, and deduce several characterizations relating the Lax-type pair, the moments, a differential equation for the weight, and the bidimensional Toda-type system.
Finally, in Section
4
we end the work by establishing a Lax type theorem and proving the isospectrality of the associated block Jacobi matrices.

2.
Preliminary results

For each
n\geqslant 0
, let
\Pi_{n}
denote the linear space of bivariate polynomials of total degree not greater than
n
(cf.
[
10
]
).
We consider
\Pi=\bigcup_{n\geqslant 0}\Pi_{n}
the linear space of all bivariate polynomials with real coefficients.

We say that
p(x,y)\in\Pi_{n}
is
monomial
if there is only one term of higher degree, i.e., there exists

0\leqslant k\leqslant n
such that

\displaystyle p(x,y)=a_{n-k,k}x^{n-k}y^{k}+\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}\sum_{i=0}^{m}a_{m-i,i}x^{m-i}y^{i},

with
a_{n-k,k}\neq 0
. Moreover, if
a_{n-k,k}=1
, we
say that
p(x,y)
is
monic
.

Let us denote by
\mathcal{M}_{h\times k}(\mathbb{R})
the linear space of matrices of size
h\times k
with
real entries and by
\mathcal{M}_{h\times k}(\Pi)
the linear space of
h\times k
matrices with polynomials in
two variables entries. The
degree of a matrix polynomial
is defined as the maximum of the degrees of its
polynomial entries.

Given a matrix
\oM\in\mathcal{M}_{h\times k}
, we denote by
\oM^{\top}
its transpose. If
h=k
, we will denote
\mathcal{M}_{h\times h}\equiv\mathcal{M}_{h}
. In particular,
\oI_{h}
denotes the identity matrix of size
h
.
We say that
\oM\in\mathcal{M}_{h}(\mathbb{R})
is
non-singular
if
\det\oM\neq 0
.

2.1.
Vector notation

For each
n\geqslant 0
, let
\mathbb{X}_{n}
denote the column vector

\displaystyle\mathbb{X}_{n}=\begin{bmatrix}x^{n}&x^{n-1}y&\cdots&xy^{n-1}&y^{n}\end{bmatrix}^{\top},

of size
(n+1)\times 1
. Then
\big\{\mathbb{X}_{n}\big\}_{n\geqslant 0}
is called the
canonical basis
of
\Pi
and
every polynomial
\oP\in\Pi
of degree
n
can be represented as

\displaystyle\oP(x,y)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\oC_{k}^{\top}\,\mathbb{X}_{k},

where

\oC_{k}
is a
(k+1)\times 1
vector of constants.
We continue, following the notation in
[
10
]
, for
n\geqslant 0
, denoting
\oL_{n,1}
and
\oL_{n,2}
, as the matrices of size
(n+1)\times(n+2)
, such that

\displaystyle\oL_{n,1}\,\mathbb{X}_{n+1}=x\,\mathbb{X}_{n}

and

\displaystyle\oL_{n,2}\,\mathbb{X}_{n+1}=y\,\mathbb{X}_{n},

where,
\oL_{n,1}
and
\oL_{n,2}
represent the so-called shift operators associated with the multiplication of

\mathbb{X}_{n}
by the variables
x
and
y
, respectively. Therefore,

\displaystyle\oL_{n,1}=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc|c}1&&&0\\[-3.55658pt]
&\ddots&&\vdots\\
&&1&0\end{array}\right],

\displaystyle\oL_{n,2}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c|ccc}0&1&&\\[-3.55658pt]
\vdots&&\ddots&\\
0&&&1\end{array}\right].

Observe that,
\rank\oL_{n,i}=n+1
,

\oL_{n,i}\oL_{n,i}^{\top}=\oI_{n+1}
,
for
i=1,2
, and
\oL_{n,1}\oL_{n+1,2}

=\oL_{n,2}\oL_{n+1,1}
.

Let
\big\{\oP_{n,m}(x,y):0\leqslant m\leqslant n,n\geqslant 0\big\}
denote a basis of
\Pi
such that, for a
fixed
n\geqslant 0
,

\deg\oP_{n,m}(x,y)=n
, and the set
\big\{\oP_{n,m}(x,y):0\leqslant m\leqslant n\big\}
contains
n+1

linearly independent polynomials of total degree exactly
n
.
We can write the vector of polynomials

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{n}=\begin{bmatrix}\oP_{n,0}(x,y)&\oP_{n,1}(x,y)&\cdots&\oP_{n,n}(x,y)\end{bmatrix}^{\top}.

The sequence of polynomial vectors of increasing size

\big\{\mathbb{P}_{n}\big\}_{n\geqslant 0}
is called a
polynomial system
(PS), and it is a basis of
\Pi
.
Moreover, for each
n\geqslant 0
, the vector
\mathbb{P}_{n}
can be written as

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{n}=\oG_{n}^{n}\,\mathbb{X}_{n}+\oG^{n}_{n-1}\,\mathbb{X}_{n-1}+\cdots+\oG^{n}_{0}\,\mathbb{X}_{0},

where
\oG_{n}^{n}
is a
n+1
non-singular matrix of constants, called
the matrix leading coefficient
of
\mathbb{P}_{n}
, and
\oG^{n}_{m}
are
(n+1)\times(m+1)
constant matrices.
When
\oG_{n}^{n}
is the identity matrix, i.e.,
\oG_{n}^{n}=\oI_{n+1}
, for every
n\geqslant 0
, then we say that

\big\{\mathbb{P}_{n}\big\}_{n\geqslant 0}
is a
monic PS
. Observe that the system of polynomials given by

\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}=(\oG_{n}^{n})^{-1}\,\mathbb{P}_{n}
, for
n\geqslant 0
, is a monic polynomial system, in the sense that

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}=\mathbb{X}_{n}+\widehat{\oG}^{n}_{n-1}\,\mathbb{X}_{n-1}+\cdots+\widehat{\oG}^{n}_{0}\,\mathbb{X}_{0},

where
\widehat{\oG}^{n}_{m}=(\oG_{n}^{n})^{-1}\,\oG^{n}_{m}
, for
0\leqslant m\leqslant n-1
.

2.2.
Orthogonal polynomial systems (OPS)

Let
\dd\mu(x,y)
be a normalized measure defined on a region
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
and we assume that it is positive definite, in the way that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}p^{2}(x,y)\,\dd\mu(x,y)>0,

\displaystyle p\in\Pi,

\displaystyle p\not\equiv 0.

Moreover, we suppose that the moments

\displaystyle\omega_{h,k}=\int_{\Omega}x^{h}\,y^{k}\,\dd\mu(x,y)<+\infty,

\displaystyle h,k\geqslant 0,

(2.7)

exist, and

\displaystyle\omega_{0,0}=\int_{\Omega}\dd\mu(x,y)=1.

As usual, we define the inner product

\displaystyle\langle p,q\rangle=\int_{\Omega}p(x,y)\,q(x,y)\,\dd\mu(x,y),

\displaystyle p,q\in\Pi,

with

\displaystyle\langle 1,1\rangle=1.

We will see how the inner product acts over polynomial matrices. Let

\oA=\begin{bmatrix}a_{i,j}(x,y)\end{bmatrix}_{i,j=1}^{h,k}

and
\oB=\begin{bmatrix}b_{i,j}(x,y)\end{bmatrix}_{i,j=1}^{k,l}

be two polynomial matrices, i.e.,
a_{i,j}(x,y),b_{i,j}(x,y)\in\Pi
.
The action of the above inner product over polynomial matrices is
defined as the
h\times l
matrix (cf.
[
10
]
),

\displaystyle\langle\oA,\oB\rangle=\int_{\Omega}\oA(x,y)\,\oB(x,y)\,\dd\mu(x,y)=\begin{bmatrix}\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}c_{i,j}(x,y)\dd\mu(x,y)\end{bmatrix}_{i,j=1}^{h,l},

where

\displaystyle\oC=\oA\cdot\oB=\begin{bmatrix}c_{i,j}(x,y)\end{bmatrix}_{i,j=1}^{h,l}
.
We say that
\big\{\mathbb{P}_{n}\big\}_{n\geqslant 0}
is an

orthogonal polynomial system (OPS) with respect to
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
if

\displaystyle\langle\mathbb{P}_{n},\mathbb{P}^{\top}_{m}\rangle=\begin{cases}\mathtt{0}_{(n+1)\times(m+1)},&n\neq m,\\
\oH_{n},&n=m,\end{cases}

(2.8)

where
\oH_{n}
is a symmetric and positive-definite matrix of size
n+1
, and
\mathtt{0}_{(n+1)\times(m+1)}
, or
\mathtt{0}
for short, is the zero matrix of adequate size. When

\oH_{n}
is diagonal,

n\in\mathbb{N}
, we say
that
\big\{\mathbb{P}_{n}\big\}_{n\geqslant 0}
is a
mutually orthogonal polynomial system
.
Moreover, there exists an
orthonormal PS
satisfying (
2.8
) with
\oH_{n}=\oI_{n+1}
. In addition, there exists a unique monic orthogonal polynomial system associated to
\dd\mu(x,y)
.

2.3.
Three term relations

Orthogonal polynomials in two variables satisfy, in each variable, a three term relation. These three
term relations are written in a vector form and have matrix coefficients.

Proposition 2.1

(cf.
[
10
]
)
.

Let
\big\{\mathbb{P}_{n}\big\}_{n\geqslant 0}
be a monic OPS associated to an inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
.
For
n\geqslant 0
, there exist constant matrices
\oD_{n,i}
and
\oC_{n,i}
of sizes respectively given by
(n+1)\times(n+1)
and
(n+1)\times n
,
i=1,2
, such that

\displaystyle\begin{cases}x\,\mathbb{P}_{n}=\oL_{n,1}\,\mathbb{P}_{n+1}+\oD_{n,1}\,\mathbb{P}_{n}+\oC_{n,1}\,\mathbb{P}_{n-1},\\
y\,\mathbb{P}_{n}=\oL_{n,2}\,\mathbb{P}_{n+1}+\oD_{n,2}\,\mathbb{P}_{n}+\oC_{n,2}\,\mathbb{P}_{n-1},\end{cases}

(2.9)

where
\mathbb{P}_{-1}=0
,
\oC_{-1,i}=0
, and

\displaystyle\oD_{n,1}\,\oH_{n}=\langle x\,\mathbb{P}_{n},\,\mathbb{P}_{n}^{\top}\rangle,

\displaystyle\oD_{n,2}\,\oH_{n}=\langle y\,\mathbb{P}_{n},\,\mathbb{P}_{n}^{\top}\rangle,

\displaystyle\oC_{n,1}\,\oH_{n-1}=\oH_{n}\,\oL_{n-1,1}^{\top},

\displaystyle\oC_{n,2}\,\oH_{n-1}=\oH_{n}\,\oL_{n-1,2}^{\top}.

Moreover, the rank conditions

\displaystyle\rank\oC_{n,i}=n,\quad i=1,2,

\displaystyle\rank\begin{bmatrix}\oC_{n,1}&\oC_{n,2}\end{bmatrix}=n+1,

hold.

On the other hand, we can add the relations in (
2.9
), to get

\displaystyle(x+y)\,\mathbb{P}_{n}=\oL_{n}\,\mathbb{P}_{n+1}+\oD_{n}\,\mathbb{P}_{n}+\oC_{n}\,\mathbb{P}_{n-1},

(2.10)

where

\displaystyle\oL_{n}

\displaystyle=\oL_{n,1}+\oL_{n,2}\,\in\,\mathcal{M}_{(n+1)\times(n+2)}(\mathbb{R}),

\displaystyle\oD_{n}

\displaystyle=\oD_{n,1}+\oD_{n,2}\,\in\,\mathcal{M}_{(n+1)\times(n+1)}(\mathbb{R}),

\displaystyle\oC_{n}

\displaystyle=\oC_{n,1}+\oC_{n,2}\,\in\,\mathcal{M}_{(n+1)\times n}(\mathbb{R}).

Here we pointed out that, taking
n=0
in (
2.9
), and multiplying by
\mathbb{P}_{0}=1
, we get

\displaystyle\langle x\mathbb{P}_{0},\mathbb{P}_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=\oL_{0,1}\langle\mathbb{P}_{1},\mathbb{P}_{0}\rangle+\oD_{0,1}\langle\mathbb{P}_{0},\mathbb{P}_{0}\rangle,

\displaystyle\langle y\mathbb{P}_{0},\mathbb{P}_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=\oL_{0,2}\langle\mathbb{P}_{1},\mathbb{P}_{0}\rangle+\oD_{0,2}\langle\mathbb{P}_{0},\mathbb{P}_{0}\rangle,

and then

\displaystyle\omega_{1,0}=\oD_{0,1},

\displaystyle\omega_{0,1}=\oD_{0,2}.

since
\langle\mathbb{P}_{0},\mathbb{P}_{0}\rangle=\langle 1,1\rangle=\omega_{0,0}=1
.

For the orthonormal polynomials
\big\{\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{n}\big\}_{n\geqslant 0}
, such that
\oH_{n}=\oI_{n+1}
, the three term relations (
2.9
) take a simpler form, as we can see in the next result.

Proposition 2.2

(
[
10
]
)
.

For
n\geqslant 0
, there exist real matrices
\oA_{n,i}
,
\oB_{n,i}
of respective sizes
(n+1)\times(n+2)
and

(n+1)\times(n+1)
such that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &x\,\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{n}=\oA_{n,1}\,\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{n+1}+\oB_{n,1}\,\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{n}+\oA^{\top}_{n-1,1}\,\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{n-1},\\
&y\,\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{n}=\oA_{n,2}\,\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{n+1}+\oB_{n,2}\,\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{n}+\oA^{\top}_{n-1,2}\,\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{n-1},\end{aligned}

(2.11)

where we define
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{-1}=0
and
\oA_{-1,i}=0
. Moreover, each
\oB_{n,i}
is symmetric.

Following
[
10
, p. 64]
, given an OPS
\big\{\mathbb{P}_{n}\big\}_{n\geqslant 0}
we can construct an orthonormal PS in the form

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{n}=\oH_{n}^{-1/2}\,\mathbb{P}_{n},

where
\oH_{n}=\langle\mathbb{P}_{n},\mathbb{P}^{\top}_{n}\rangle
, and
\oH_{n}^{-1/2}=(\oH_{n}^{1/2})^{-1}
is the inverse of the
positive
square root
of
\oH_{n}
, the unique positive-definite matrix satisfying
\oH_{n}^{1/2}\,\oH_{n}^{1/2}=\oH_{n}
(cf.
[
13
]
).
In such a case, we get

\displaystyle\oA_{n,i}=\oH_{n}^{-1/2}\,\oL_{n,i}\,\oH_{n+1}^{1/2},

\displaystyle\oB_{n,i}=\oH_{n}^{-1/2}\,\oD_{n,i}\,\oH_{n}^{1/2}.

2.4.
Block Jacobi matrices

As in the one variable case, we can consider in the two variables
one, two infinite tridiagonal block matrices
\boldsymbol{\oJ}_{i}
,
i=1,2
, defined as follows

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\oJ}_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}\oD_{0,i}&\oL_{0,i}&&&\\
\oC_{1,i}&\oD_{1,i}&\oL_{1,i}&&\\
&\oC_{2,i}&\oD_{2,i}&\oL_{2,i}&\\
&&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\end{bmatrix},

(2.12)

where the matrices in the main diagonal
\oD_{n,i}
are square matrices of increasing size
n+1
, the matrices in the subdiagonal,
\oC_{n,i}
, are
(n+1)\times n
matrices, and in the superdiagonal, the matrices
\oL_{n,i}
are
n\times(n+1)
matrices, and the other elements are zero matrices of appropriate size.

Defining the infinite column vector

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\oP}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbb{P}^{\top}_{0}&\mathbb{P}^{\top}_{1}&\cdots&\mathbb{P}^{\top}_{n}&\cdots\end{bmatrix}^{\top},

the three term relations (
2.9
) can be written as

\displaystyle x\,\boldsymbol{\oP}=\boldsymbol{\oJ}_{1}\,\boldsymbol{\oP},

\displaystyle y\,\boldsymbol{\oP}=\boldsymbol{\oJ}_{2}\,\boldsymbol{\oP}.

Directly from the three term relation we have a formal result above the commutativity of the matrix entries of
both block matrices.

Lemma 2.3
.

For
n\geqslant 0
, we get

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \phantom{ola}&\oC_{n,1}\oC_{n-1,2}=\oC_{n,2}\oC_{n-1,1},\\
\phantom{ola}&\oD_{n,1}\,\oC_{n,2}+\oC_{n,1}\,\oD_{n-1,2}=\oD_{n,2}\,\oC_{n,1}+\oC_{n,2}\,\oD_{n-1,1},\\
\phantom{ola}&\oL_{n,1}\oC_{n+1,2}+\oD_{n,1}\oD_{n,2}+\oC_{n,1}\oL_{n-1,2}\\
&\phantom{olaolaolaola}=\oL_{n,2}\oC_{n+1,1}+\oD_{n,2}\oD_{n,1}+\oC_{n,2}\oL_{n-1,1},\\
\phantom{ola}&\oL_{n,1}\oD_{n+1,2}+\oD_{n,1}\oL_{n,2}=\oL_{n,2}\oD_{n+1,1}+\oD_{n,2}\oL_{n,1},\end{aligned}

(2.13)

where we assume
\oC_{0,1}=\oC_{0,2}=0
.

Proof.

Since
y\,x\,\mathbb{P}_{n}=x\,y\,\mathbb{P}_{n}
, from (
2.9
) we get

\oL_{n,1}\big(\oL_{n+1,2}\,\mathbb{P}_{n+2}+\oD_{n+1,2}\,\mathbb{P}_{n+1}+\oC_{n+1,2}\,\mathbb{P}_{n})\\
+\oD_{n,1}\big(\oL_{n,2}\,\mathbb{P}_{n+1}+\oD_{n,2}\,\mathbb{P}_{n}+\oC_{n,2}\,\mathbb{P}_{n-1}\big)\\
+\oC_{n,1}\big(\oL_{n-1,2}\,\mathbb{P}_{n}+\oD_{n-1,2}\,\mathbb{P}_{n-1}+\oC_{n-1,2}\,\mathbb{P}_{n-2}\big)\\
=\oL_{n,2}\big(\oL_{n+1,1}\,\mathbb{P}_{n+2}+\oD_{n+1,1}\,\mathbb{P}_{n+1}+\oC_{n+1,1}\,\mathbb{P}_{n}\big)\\
+\oD_{n,2}\big(\oL_{n,1}\,\mathbb{P}_{n+1}+\oD_{n,1}\,\mathbb{P}_{n}+\oC_{n,1}\,\mathbb{P}_{n-1}\big)\\
+\oC_{n,2}\big(\oL_{n-1,1}\,\mathbb{P}_{n}+\oD_{n-1,1}\,\mathbb{P}_{n-1}+\oC_{n-1,1}\,\mathbb{P}_{n-2}\big).

Taking
\langle y\,x\,\mathbb{P}_{n},\mathbb{P}_{j}^{\top}\rangle=\langle x\,y\,\mathbb{P}_{n},\mathbb{P}_{j}^{\top}\rangle
, successively for
j=n-2
,
n-1
,
n
,

n+1
,
n+2
we get the desired result.
∎

As it is shown in
[
10
, pp. 83 and seq.]
, above conditions are not enough to assure the commutativity of
\boldsymbol{\oJ}_{1}

and
\boldsymbol{\oJ}_{2}
since they are operators. The results about commutativity are showed for the Jacobi matrix for orthonormal
polynomial systems. We consider the Jacobi block matrix based on the three term relations for the orthonormal polynomials (
2.11
):

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\oL}_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}\oB_{0,i}&\oA_{0,i}&&\\
\oA^{\top}_{0,i}&\oB_{1,i}&\oA_{1,i}&\\
&\oA^{\top}_{1,i}&\oB_{2,i}&\oA_{2,i}\\
&&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle i=1,2,

(2.14)

where the matrices
\oA_{n,i}
and
\oB_{n,i}
are given in the three term relations, and the other elements are zero matrices of appropriate size. Then the three term relations (
2.11
) can be written as

\displaystyle x\,\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\oP}}=\boldsymbol{\oL}_{1}\,\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\oP}},

\displaystyle y\,\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\oP}}=\boldsymbol{\oL}_{2}\,\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\oP}},

where
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\oP}}=\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}^{\top}_{0}&\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}^{\top}_{1}&\cdots&\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}^{\top}_{n}&\cdots\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
is the vector of the orthonormal polynomials.

The matrices
\boldsymbol{\oL}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{\oL}_{2}
can be considered as linear operators which act
via matrix multiplication on
\ell^{2}
, where the domain of the operator consists of all sequences
in
\ell^{2}
for which matrix multiplication yields sequences in
\ell^{2}
. As linear operators,
the matrices
\boldsymbol{\oL}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{\oL}_{2}
, does not need to be bounded, however, according to
[
10
, pp. 83 and seq.]
, a sufficient condition for the boundedness of
\boldsymbol{\oL}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{\oL}_{2}
is the compactness of the support
\Omega
of de measure
\mu
having the sequence
\left\{\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{n}\right\}_{n\geqslant 0}
as orthonormal polynomials.

Now, defining

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\oH}=\begin{bmatrix}\oH_{0}&&&\\
&\oH_{1}&&\\
&&\ddots\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\oH}^{1/2}=\begin{bmatrix}\oH_{0}^{1/2}&&&\\
&\oH_{1}^{1/2}&&\\
&&\ddots\end{bmatrix},

and
\boldsymbol{\oH}^{-1}
,
\boldsymbol{\oH}^{-1/2}
, their respective inverse matrices, we have,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\oJ}_{i}=\boldsymbol{\oH}^{1/2}\,\boldsymbol{\oL}_{i}\,\boldsymbol{\oH}^{-1/2},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\oL}_{i}=\boldsymbol{\oH}^{-1/2}\,\boldsymbol{\oJ}_{i}\,\boldsymbol{\oH}^{1/2},

\displaystyle i=1,2.

Following Lemma 3.4.4 in
[
10
, p. 85]
,
and supposing that
\boldsymbol{\oL}_{i}
are bounded, then they
are self-adjoint and
commute. In this way,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\oJ}_{1}\,\boldsymbol{\oJ}_{2}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\oH}^{1/2}\,\boldsymbol{\oL}_{1}\,\boldsymbol{\oH}^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{\oH}^{1/2}\,\boldsymbol{\oL}_{2}\,\boldsymbol{\oH}^{-1/2}=\boldsymbol{\oH}^{1/2}\,\boldsymbol{\oL}_{1}\,\boldsymbol{\oL}_{2}\,\boldsymbol{\oH}^{-1/2}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\oH}^{1/2}\,\boldsymbol{\oL}_{2}\,\boldsymbol{\oL}_{1}\,\boldsymbol{\oH}^{-1/2}=\boldsymbol{\oH}^{1/2}\,\boldsymbol{\oL}_{2}\,\boldsymbol{\oH}^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{\oH}^{1/2}\,\boldsymbol{\oL}_{1}\,\boldsymbol{\oH}^{-1/2}=\boldsymbol{\oJ}_{2}\,\boldsymbol{\oJ}_{1}.

We can summarize this in the following result.

Lemma 2.4
.

If

\boldsymbol{\oL}_{i}=\boldsymbol{\oH}^{-1/2}\,\boldsymbol{\oJ}_{i}\,\boldsymbol{\oH}^{1/2}
,
i=1,2
, are bounded matrices, then

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\oJ}_{1}\,\boldsymbol{\oJ}_{2}=\boldsymbol{\oJ}_{2}\,\boldsymbol{\oJ}_{1}.

In general, for the bounded case we have,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\oJ}_{1}^{h}\,\boldsymbol{\oJ}_{2}^{k}=\boldsymbol{\oJ}_{2}^{k}\,\boldsymbol{\oJ}_{1}^{h},

\displaystyle h,k\geqslant 0,

and the product
\boldsymbol{\oJ}_{1}^{h}\,\boldsymbol{\oJ}_{2}^{k}
preserves the block structure described in (
2.12
), but not the
zero block matrices. In particular, we write

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\oJ}_{1}^{h}\,\boldsymbol{\oJ}_{2}^{k}=\begin{bmatrix}\oD^{(h,k)}_{0,0}&\oL^{(h,k)}_{0,1}&(*)&\cdots\\
\oC^{(h,k)}_{1,0}&\oD^{(h,k)}_{1,1}&\oL^{(h,k)}_{1,2}&\ddots\\
(*)&\oC^{(h,k)}_{2,1}&\oD^{(h,k)}_{2,2}&\ddots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\end{bmatrix},

where the
(*)
denotes block matrices of adequate size not necessarily zero. For
h,k,n\geqslant 0
, the respective
size of the involved matrices are
(n+1)\times(n+1)
for
\oD^{(h,k)}_{n,n}
,
(n+2)\times(n+1)
for

\oC^{(h,k)}_{n+1,n}
, and

(n+1)\times(n+2)
for
\oL^{(h,k)}_{n,n+1}
.

Observe that

\displaystyle\oD^{(1,0)}_{n,n}=\oD_{n,1},

\displaystyle\oC^{(1,0)}_{n,n-1}=\oC_{n,1},

\displaystyle\oL^{(1,0)}_{n-1,n}=\oL_{n-1,1},

\displaystyle\oD^{(0,1)}_{n,n}=\oD_{n,2},

\displaystyle\oC^{(0,1)}_{n,n-1}=\oC_{n,2},

\displaystyle\oL^{(0,1)}_{n-1,n}=\oL_{n-1,2}.

Let

\ell_{0}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0&\cdots\end{bmatrix}^{\top}

be the zero column vector except for the first element. Then, it is clear that

\displaystyle\oD^{(h,k)}_{0,0}=\ell_{0}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\oJ}_{1}^{h}\,\boldsymbol{\oJ}_{2}^{k}\,\ell_{0},

i.e., we can access to the
(0,0)
element of the matrix
\boldsymbol{\oJ}_{1}^{h}\,\boldsymbol{\oJ}_{2}^{k}
.

Next result shows that all the moments are determined by the powers of the Jacobi matrices.

Proposition 2.5
.

For
h,k\geqslant 0
,

\displaystyle\omega_{h,k}=\ell_{0}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\oJ}_{1}^{h}\,\boldsymbol{\oJ}_{2}^{k}\,\ell_{0}.

(2.15)

Proof.

Observe that, for all
h,k\geqslant 0
, we have

\displaystyle x^{h}y^{k}\,\boldsymbol{\oP}=\boldsymbol{\oJ}_{1}^{h}\,\boldsymbol{\oJ}_{2}^{k}\,\boldsymbol{\oP},

and then, the first element in both sides are equal,

\displaystyle x^{h}y^{k}\,\mathbb{P}_{0}=\oD_{0,0}^{(h,k)}\,\mathbb{P}_{0}+\oL^{(h,k)}_{0,1}\,\mathbb{P}_{1}+(*)\,\mathbb{P}_{2}+\cdots

Integrating by means of the measure
\dd\mu(x,y)
, using the orthogonality, and the fact that
\mathbb{P}_{0}=1
, we arrive to

\displaystyle\displaystyle\omega_{h,k}=\oD_{0,0}^{(h,k)}\omega_{0,0}=\oD_{0,0}^{(h,k)}
.
∎

2.5.
Stieltjes function in two variables

From now on, we suppose that the positive definite measure is defined on a compact domain

\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
is given in terms of a weight function in the form:

\displaystyle\dd\mu(x,y)=\omega(x,y)\dd x\dd y.

Suppose that all the moments in (
2.7

Following
[
2
]
, we define the Stieltjes function in two variables as a double formal power series.

Definition 2.6
.

The
Stieltjes function
associated with a weight function
\omega(x,y)
(or, equivalently, the operators
\boldsymbol{\oJ}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{\oJ}_{2}

is defined as

\displaystyle\oS(z_{1},z_{2})=\int_{\Omega}\frac{\omega(x,y)}{(z_{1}-x)\,(z_{2}-y)}\dd x\dd y.

Observe that, substituting the formal series (cf.
[

)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(z_{1}-x)\,(z_{2}-y)}=\sum_{h,k\geqslant 0}\,\frac{x^{h}\,y^{k}}{z_{1}^{h+1}z_{2}^{k+1}},

defined in

\big\{(z_{1},z_{2})\in\mathbb{C}^{2}:|z_{1}|>\|{\hskip-1.1097pt|}\boldsymbol{\oJ}_{1}\|{\hskip-1.1097pt|}\mbox{ and }\ |z_{2}|>\|{\hskip-1.1097pt|}\boldsymbol{\oJ}_{2}\|{\hskip-1.1097pt|}\big\}
, where by
\|{\hskip-1.1097pt|}\boldsymbol{\oJ}_{i}\|{\hskip-1.1097pt|}
we mean the operator norm of
\boldsymbol{\oJ}_{i}
,
i=1,2
,
we get

\displaystyle\oS(z_{1},z_{2})=\sum_{h,k\geqslant 0}\frac{1}{z_{1}^{h+1}z_{2}^{k+1}}\int_{\Omega}x^{h}\,y^{k}\,\omega(x,y)\dd x\dd y=\sum_{h,k\geqslant 0}\,\frac{\omega_{h,k}}{z_{1}^{h+1}z_{2}^{k+1}}.

We introduce the
first
k
-marginal series
for the Stieltjes function (
2.16
),

\displaystyle\oS_{1,k}(z)=\sum_{h=0}^{+\infty}\,\frac{\omega_{h,k}}{z^{h+1}},

\displaystyle k\geqslant 0,

(2.17)

and the
second
h
-marginal series
,

\displaystyle\oS_{2,h}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\,\frac{\omega_{h,k}}{z^{k+1}},

\displaystyle h\geqslant 0.

(2.18)

We observe that the functions

\oS_{1,k}(z)
,
\oS_{2,h}(z)
,
are Stieltjes function in one variable associated with the moments

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}x^{h}\,y^{k}\,\omega(x,y)\dd x\dd y=\omega_{h,k},

\displaystyle h,k\geqslant 0.

3.
The 2D Toda lattices

Now, we will show that the bivariate orthogonal polynomials modelize the
2

\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is given in terms of a weight function in the form:

\displaystyle\dd\mu(x,y)=\omega(x,y)\dd x\dd y,

and suppose that all the moments in (
2.7
) exist. Let
\big\{\mathbb{P}_{n}\big\}_{n\geqslant 0}
be the monic OPS associated to the measure

\dd\mu(x,y)
.

From now on we consider a weight function,
\omega
, with an

evolution term

t\geqslant 0
, i.e.

\omega(x,y,t)

such that
\omega(x,y,0)\equiv\omega(x,y)
. Observe that the explicit shape of the weight function depending on the evolution term
t
is unknown.

We also suppose that the time dependent weight function
\omega(x,y,t)
has finite moments, i.e.

\displaystyle\omega_{h,k}(t)=\int_{\Omega}x^{h}\,y^{k}\,\omega(x,y,t)\dd x\dd y<+\infty,

\displaystyle h,k\geqslant 0,

\displaystyle t\geqslant 0,

exist,
and is normalized such that
\omega_{0,0}(t)=1
.

Observe that the moments depend on the time variable
t
and verify

\omega_{h,k}(0)=\omega_{h,k}
. For
t\geqslant 0
, we define the inner product,

\displaystyle\langle p,q\rangle_{t}=\int_{\Omega}p(x,y)\,q(x,y)\,\omega(x,y,t)\dd x\dd y,

\displaystyle p,q\in\Pi.

Obviously,
\langle\boldsymbol{\cdot},\boldsymbol{\cdot}\rangle_{0}=\langle\boldsymbol{\cdot},\boldsymbol{\cdot}\rangle
.

Let
\big\{\mathbb{P}_{n}(t)\big\}_{n\geqslant 0}

\equiv

\big\{\mathbb{P}_{n}(x,y,t)\big\}_{n\geqslant 0}
be the monic orthogonal
polynomial system associated to
\omega(x,y,t)
. Clearly,
these are two variables polynomials (on
x
and
y
) whose coefficients depend on
t
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{n}(t)=\mathbb{X}_{n}+\oG^{n}_{n-1}(t)\,\mathbb{X}_{n-1}+\cdots+\oG^{n}_{0}(t)\,\mathbb{X}_{0},

\displaystyle n\geqslant 0,

with
\mathbb{P}_{n}(0)=\mathbb{P}_{n}
.

As usual, we use the
dot
-notation for the derivative with respect to the time variable
t
. Since
\mathbb{P}_{n}(t)
is a monic polynomial we get

\displaystyle\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{\mathbb{P}}_{n}(t)=\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\mathbb{P}_{n}(t)=\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{\oG}^{n}_{n-1}(t)\,\mathbb{X}_{n-1}+\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{\oG}^{n}_{n-2}(t)\,\mathbb{X}_{n-2}+\cdots+\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{\oG}^{n}_{0}(t)\,\mathbb{X}_{0},

i.e.,
\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{\mathbb{P}}_{n}(t)
is a
(n+1)
vector of polynomials of degree less than or equal to
n-1
.

In addition, the symmetric positive-definite matrix

\displaystyle\oH_{n}(t)=\langle\mathbb{P}_{n}(t),\mathbb{P}^{\top}_{n}(t)\rangle_{t},

\displaystyle n\geqslant 0,

also depends on
t
, and
\oH_{n}(0)=\oH_{n}
.

The sequence
\{\mathbb{P}_{n}\}_{n\geqslant 0}
satisfy the three term relations (
2.9
), but now, the matrix coefficients
depend on
t
. In this way, for
n\geqslant 0
, there exist matrices

\oD_{n,i}(t),\oC_{n,i}(t)
, of respective sizes
(n+1)\times(n+1)
and
(n+1)\times n

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} x\,\mathbb{P}_{n}(t)=\oL_{n,1}\,\mathbb{P}_{n+1}(t)+\oD_{n,1}(t)\,\mathbb{P}_{n}(t)+\oC_{n,1}(t)\,\mathbb{P}_{n-1}(t),\\

where
\mathbb{P}_{-1}(t)=0
and
\oC_{-1}(t)=0

\displaystyle\oD_{n,1}(t)\,\oH_{n}(t)

\displaystyle=\langle x\,\mathbb{P}_{n}(t),\mathbb{P}^{\top}_{n}(t)\rangle_{t},

\displaystyle\oD_{n,2}(t)\,\oH_{n}(t)

\displaystyle=\langle y\,\mathbb{P}_{n}(t),\mathbb{P}^{\top}_{n}(t)\rangle_{t},

\displaystyle\oC_{n,1}(t)\,\oH_{n-1}(t)

\displaystyle=\oH_{n}(t)\,\oL^{\top}_{n-1,1},

\displaystyle\oC_{n,2}(t)\,\oH_{n-1}(t)

\displaystyle=\oH_{n}(t)\,\oL^{\top}_{n-1,2},

\oD_{n,i}(0)=\oD_{n,i}
,
\oC_{n,i}(0)=\oC_{n,i}
,
i=1,2
. We must remark that the matrices
\oL_{n,i}
defined
in (
2.1

independent of
t
.

We also define

\displaystyle\oD_{n}(t)

\displaystyle=\oD_{n,1}(t)+\oD_{n,2}(t),

\displaystyle\oC_{n}(t)=\oC_{n,1}(t)+\oC_{n,2}(t).

In this case, the tridiagonal Jacobi matrices also depend on the
t

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\oJ}_{i}(t)=\begin{bmatrix}\oD_{0,i}(t)&\oL_{0,i}&&\\
\oC_{1,i}(t)&\oD_{1,i}(t)&\oL_{1,i}&\\
&\oC_{2,i}(t)&\oD_{2,i}(t)&\ddots\\
&&\ddots&\ddots\end{bmatrix},

where the main diagonal matrices,
\oD_{n,i}(t)

, the matrices in the subdiagonal,
\oC_{n,i}
, are
(n+1)\times n

\oL_{n,i}
are

matrices defined in (

We define the infinite block matrix

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\oA}(t)=\begin{bmatrix}\mathtt{0}&&&\\
\oC_{1}(t)&\mathtt{0}&&\\
&\oC_{2}(t)&\mathtt{0}&\\
&&\ddots&\ddots\end{bmatrix},

(3.3)

where
\mathtt{0}

For

4.

\boldsymbol{\oJ}_{i}(t)

In this Section, we study the eigenvalues of the matrix

Proposition 4.1
.

The eigenvalues of the matrix
\boldsymbol{\oJ}_{i}(t)
are real, for
i=1,2.

Proof.

By (
2.14
), we get that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\oH}^{1/2}\,\boldsymbol{\oL}_{i}\,\boldsymbol{\oH}^{-1/2}=\boldsymbol{\oJ}_{i},

\displaystyle i=1,2,

i.e.,
\boldsymbol{\oL}_{i}
and
\boldsymbol{\oJ}_{i}
are similar and have the same eigenvalues. Since
\boldsymbol{\oL}_{i}
is
self-adjoint
and real, then
all of its eigenvalues are real, and then, the eigenvalues of
\boldsymbol{\oJ}_{i}

∎

Next, we end the paper by proving that the matrices
