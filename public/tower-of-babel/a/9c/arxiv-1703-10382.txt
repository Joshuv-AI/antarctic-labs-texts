Relational Graph Models at Work
Flavien Breuvart, Giulio Manzonetto, Domenico Ruoppolo

Abstract
. We study the relational graph models that constitute a natural subclass of relational models of λ \lambda -calculus. We prove that among the λ \lambda -theories induced by such models there exists a minimal one, and that the corresponding relational graph model is very natural and easy to construct. We then study relational graph models that are fully abstract, in the sense that they capture some observational equivalence between λ \lambda -terms. We focus on the two main observational equivalences in the λ \lambda -calculus, the λ \lambda -theory ℋ + \mathcal{H^{+}} generated by taking as observables the β \beta -normal forms, and ℋ ∗ \mathcal{H^{*}} generated by considering as observables the head normal forms. On the one hand we introduce a notion of λ \lambda -König model and prove that a relational graph model is fully abstract for ℋ + \mathcal{H^{+}} if and only if it is extensional and λ \lambda -König. On the other hand we show that the dual notion of hyperimmune model, together with extensionality, captures the full abstraction for ℋ ∗ \mathcal{H^{*}} .

Introduction

The untyped
\lambda
-calculus is a paradigmatic programming language introduced by Church in
[
25
]
. It has a prominent role in theoretical computer science
[
7
]
and, despite its very simple syntax, it is Turing-complete
[
53
,
87
]
.
Its denotational models have been fruitfully used for proving the consistency of extensions of
\beta
-convertibility, called
\lambda
-theories
, and for exposing operational features of
\lambda
-terms.
The first model of
\lambda
-calculus,
\mathscr{D}_{\infty}
, was defined by Scott in the pioneering article
[
81
]
.
Subsequently, a wealth of models have been introduced in various categories of domains and classified into
semantics
according to the nature of their representable functions.
Scott’s
continuous semantics

[
82
]
corresponds to the category whose objects are complete partial orders and morphisms are continuous functions.
The
stable semantics

[
12
]
and the
strongly stable semantics

[
19
]
are refinements of the continuous semantics, introduced to capture the notion of “sequential” continuous function.
In each of these semantics all the models come equipped with a partial order, and some of them, called
webbed models
, are built from lower level structures called “webs”
[
10
]
.
The simplest class of webbed models is the class of
graph models

[
62
]
, which was isolated in the seventies by Plotkin, Scott and Engeler within the continuous semantics
[
39
,
73
,
82
]
.

In each of the aforementioned semantics there exists a continuum of models inducing pairwise distinct
\lambda
-theories. Nevertheless, certain models are particularly important because they allow to capture operational properties of the
\lambda
-terms.
For instance, two
\lambda
-terms have the same interpretation in Engeler’s graph model
\mathscr{E}
exactly when they are equal in the
\lambda
-theory
\mathcal{B}
, which equates all
\lambda
-terms having the same Böhm tree.
The main technical tool for proving such a result is the Approximation Theorem
[
45
]
, stating that the interpretation of a
\lambda
-term is given by the supremum of the interpretations of the finite approximants of its Böhm tree.
Other models are significant because they are
fully abstract
, which means that the induced
\lambda
-theory captures some observational equivalence between
\lambda
-terms.
A celebrated result by Hyland
[
47
]
and Wadsworth
[
89
]
shows that Scott’s
\mathscr{D}_{\infty}
is fully abstract for the
\lambda
-theory
\mathcal{H^{*}}
, that corresponds to the observational equivalence where the observables are the head normal forms.
In
[
26
]
, Coppo, Dezani-Ciancaglini and Zacchi constructed a
filter model

\mathscr{D}_{\mathrm{cdz}}
and proved that it is fully abstract for
\mathcal{H^{+}}
, the observational equivalence where the observables are the
\beta
-normal forms.
The
\lambda
-theory
\mathcal{H^{+}}
is the original extensional observational theory defined by Morris in his thesis
[
69
]
.
It is maybe less ubiquitously studied in the literature than
\mathcal{H^{*}}
but we believe is equally important.
For instance, its notion of observables is central in the Böhm Theorem
[
14
]
and in other separability results
[
28
]
.

Graph Models in the Relational Semantics.

In the present paper we focus on the
relational semantics
of
\lambda
-calculus, that has been introduced by Girard as a quantitative model of linear logic in
[
43
]
.
The first concrete examples of relational models of
\lambda
-calculus were built in
[
20
,
49
]
.
Recently, Manzonetto and Ruoppolo individuated the subclass of
relational graph models
encompassing all previous examples
[
67
]
.
The definition of a relational graph model (Definition
3.2
) really is the relational analogue of the definition of a graph model living in the continuous semantics.
In particular, relational graph models can be built by free completion and by forcing like the continuous ones.
However, from the point of view of the induced
\lambda
-theories, they share more similarities with filter models.
For instance, the relational graph model
\mathcal{D}_{\omega}
built in
[
20
]
has the same theory as Scott’s
\mathscr{D}_{\infty}
, namely it is fully abstract for
\mathcal{H^{*}}

[
65
]
.
Similarly, the model
\mathcal{D}_{\star}
from
[
67
]
is fully abstract for
\mathcal{H^{+}}
, like the filter model
\mathscr{D}_{\mathrm{cdz}}
.
On the other hand, no graph model living in the continuous semantics can represent such
\lambda
-theories because no graph model is extensional.
When comparing relational graph models with filter models inducing the same
\lambda
-theory, one can see that the former are in general simpler because their elements are not partially ordered.
Moreover, an element
\sigma
in the relational interpretation of a
\lambda
-term
M
carries information concerning intensional properties of
M
.
In particular, from
\sigma
it is possible to compute a bound to the number of head-reduction steps towards its normal form and infer the amount of resources consumed by
M
during such a reduction sequence
[
30
,
31
]
.

Relational Graph Models as Type Systems

The Stone duality between filter models and intersection type systems has been widely studied in the literature, e.g.,
[
2
,
27
,
45
,
9
,
80
]
.
(We refer to Ronchi Della Rocca and Paolini’s book for a thoughtful discussion
[
77
, Ch. 13]
.)
Such a correspondence shows that some interesting classes of domain-based models can be described in logical form.
The intuition is that a functional intersection type
\alpha_{1}\land\cdots\land\alpha_{n}\to\beta
can be seen as a continuous step function sending the set
\{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}\}
to the element
\beta
.
Types come equipped with inference rules reflecting the structure of the underlying domain.
In
[
72
]
, Paolini et al. introduce the
strongly linear
relational models (a class encompassing relational graph models, but included in the
linear
relational models of
[
66
]
) and show that they can be represented as relevant (i.e., without weakening) intersection type systems where the intersection is a non-idempotent operation (it is actually a linear logic tensor
\otimes
).
The idea, already present in
[
30
]
, is that in the absence of idempotency and partial orders the type
\alpha_{1}\land\cdots\land\alpha_{n}\to\beta
can be seen as a relation associating the multiset
[\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}]
with the element
\beta
.
As a consequence of the work in
[
72
]
, all relational graph models can be presented in logical form, that is, as non-idempotent intersection type systems.
We use this kind of representation to expose and exploit their quantitative features.

The Approximation Theorem.

Besides soundness (Theorem
3.5
), one of the main properties enjoyed by relational graph models is the Approximation Theorem (Theorem
3
).
Typically such a theorem is proved by exploiting Wadsworth’s stratified refinements of the
\beta
-reduction
[
89
]
, that also work in the relational framework as shown in
[
65
]
.
Other techniques are based on Tait and Girard’s
reducibility candidates

[
85
,
42
]
, that are widespread in logic and the theory of programming languages
[
75
,
56
,
57
,
9
]
, but notoriously give rise to proofs based on impredicative principles.
Thanks to its quantitative nature, in the context of the relational semantics it is possible to get rid of the traditional methods and provide a combinatorial proof.
This is the case of the proof given in
[
67
]
by relying on these facts:

•

Relational graph models are also models of Ehrhard’s
differential
\lambda
-calculus

[
37
]
and Tranquilli’s resource calculus
[
86
]
.
This follows from the fact that they all are
linear
reflexive objects in the Cartesian closed differential category
{\bf M\!Rel}

[
66
]
.

•

An easy induction shows that the interpretation of a
\lambda
-term
M
in a relational graph model is equal to the interpretation of its
Taylor expansion

[
37
]
, which is a representation of
M
as a power series of resource approximants (replacing in a way the finite approximants of its Böhm tree).

•

The usual Approximation Theorem follows from the above result by applying a theorem due to Ehrhard and Regnier
[
38
]
stating that the normal form of the Taylor expansion of
M
coincides with the Taylor expansion of its Böhm tree.

In Section
4.5
we provide a new combinatorial proof of the Approximation Theorem by exploiting the logical presentation discussed above.
We are going to associate a measure with the derivation tree
\pi
of
\Gamma\vdash M:\alpha
and show that when
M

\beta
-reduces to
N
by contracting a redex
R
two cases are possible: either there exists a derivation of
\Gamma\vdash N:\alpha
having a strictly smaller measure, or
\pi
is a derivation of
\,\Gamma\vdash M\{\bot/R\}:\alpha
, where
M\{\bot/R\}
denotes the approximant obtained by substituting a constant
\bot
for the redex
R
in
M
.
In both cases, either the measure of the derivation or the number of redexes in
M
has decreased.
Therefore the Approximation Theorem follows by a simple induction over the ordinal
\omega^{2}
.

The Minimal Relational Graph Theory.

Every relational graph model induces a
\lambda
-theory through the kernel of its interpretation function.
We call
relational graph theories
those
\lambda
-theories induced by some relational graph models.
A natural question that arises is what
\lambda
-theories are in addition relational graph theories.
We do not provide a characterization, but we show that the
\lambda
-theories
\mathcal{B}
,
\mathcal{H^{+}}
and
\mathcal{H^{*}}
are.
Another question is whether there exists a minimal relational graph theory: for instance, in
[
22
]
Bucciarelli and Salibra proved that the minimal
\lambda
-theory among the ones represented by usual graph models exists, but their construction of the minimal model is complicated and what
\lambda
-terms are actually equated in the minimal theory remains a mystery.
In Section
5
we show not only that a minimal relational graph theory exists, but also that such a
\lambda
-theory is actually
\mathcal{B}
. Also, the corresponding model
\mathcal{E}
is very simple to define (its construction is actually analogous to the one of Engeler’s graph model).
Moreover, we prove that even the preorder induced by
\mathcal{E}
on
\lambda
-terms is minimal among representable inequational theories.
Our model
\mathcal{E}
shares many properties with Ronchi Della Rocca’s filter model defined in
[
76
]
.

Preliminaries

1.1.
Coinduction.

In this article we will often use coinductive structures and coinductive reasoning.
We recall here some basic facts about coinduction and discuss some terminology, but we mainly take them for granted.
Nice tutorials on the subject are
[
52
,
55
]
; see also
[
1
]
.

A coinductive structure, corresponding to a coinductive datatype, is just the greatest fixed point over a grammar, or equivalently the terminal coalgebra over the corresponding functor.
We also consider coinductive propositions (and relations), that are the greatest propositions over such coinductive structures respecting the structural constraints. Coinductive propositions are proven by infinite derivation trees that are coinductive structures.

As opposed to the inductive principle, the coinductive one is unable to destruct a coinductive object, it is only able to construct one. More precisely, we can prove by coinduction a statement of the form
\forall x{\in}X\,\exists y{\in}Y\,\varphi(x,y)
where
Y
and
\varphi
are coinductively defined.
Consider for now the simpler case where we want to prove
\forall x\!\in\!X\,\varphi(x)
for some coinductive proposition
\varphi
. In order to apply the coinduction principle, it is sufficient to show

\textstyle\forall x\in X\;\,\exists\,n\in{\rm Nature}\;\,\exists\,\vec{x}\in X^{n}\;\Big(\bigwedge_{i\leq n}\varphi(x_{i})\;\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\Rightarrow}}\;\,\varphi(x)\Big)

where the “
p
” in
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\Rightarrow}}
refers to the productivity of the implication. In this case “productive” means that in the proof of the implication some patterns of the grammar coinductively defining
\varphi
are actually applied to the various
\varphi(x_{i})
.
Usually
x
is a certain (inductive or coinductive) structure and
\vec{x}
can be seen as an “unfolding” of
x
.

In the general case, namely
\forall x{\in}X\,\exists y{\in}Y\,\varphi(x,y)
, it is sufficient to show that

\textstyle\forall x\in X\;\,\exists\,n\in{\rm Nature}\;\,\exists\,\vec{x}\in X^{n}\;\,\forall\vec{y}\in Y^{n}\;\,\exists\,^{p}y\in Y\;\,\Big(\bigwedge_{i\leq n}\varphi(x_{i},y_{i})\;\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\Rightarrow}}\;\,\varphi(x,y)\Big).

The “productivity” of the existential
\exists\,^{p}
simply requires that
y=\mathsf{p}(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})
for some pattern
\mathsf{p}
of the grammar coinductively generating
Y
.
Notice that the negation of a coinductive statement is inductive and
vice versa
, a fact that we use in the proof of Lemma
5.7
.

Since structural coinduction has been around for decades and many efforts have been made in the community to explain why it should be used as innocently as structural induction, in our proofs we will not reassert the coinduction principle every time it is used.
Borrowing the terminology from
[
55
]
, we say that we apply the “coinductive hypothesis” whenever the coinduction principle is applied.
We believe that this mathematical writing greatly improves the readability of our proofs without compromising their correctness: the suspicious reader can study
[
55
]
where it is explained how this informal terminology actually corresponds to a formal application of the coinduction principle.

.
Similarly, an object

.

We say that

is a

)
if

is an object of
{\bf C}
and

.

.

1.5.
The Lambda Calculus

We generally use the notation of Barendregt’s book
[
7
]
for
\lambda
-calculus.
The set
\Lambda
of
\lambda
-terms
over a denumerable set
\mathrm{Var}
of variables is
defined by:

\Lambda:\quad M,N,P,Q\ \mathrel{::=}\ x\ \mid\ \lambda x.M\ \mid\ MN\qquad\textrm{for all }x\in\mathrm{Var}.

We assume that the application associates to the left and has a higher precedence than
\lambda
-abstraction.
For instance, we write
\lambda xyz.xyz
for the
\lambda
-term
\lambda x.(\lambda y.(\lambda z.((xy)z)))
.
Moreover, we often write
\vec{x}
for the sequence
\langle{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}}\rangle
and
\lambda\vec{x}.M
for
\lambda x_{1}\dots\lambda x_{n}.M
.

The set
\mathsf{FV}(M)
of
free variables
of
M
and the
\alpha
-conversion are defined as in
[
7
, Ch. 1§2]
.
Hereafter, we consider
\lambda
-terms up to
\alpha
-conversion.

Definition
\thethm
.

A
\lambda
-term
M
is
closed
whenever
\mathsf{FV}(M)=\emptyset
and
in this case it is also called a
combinator
.
The set of all combinators is denoted by
\Lambda^{o}
.

We often consider relations on
\lambda
-terms that have the property of being “context closed”.
Intuitively a context
C[-]
is a
\lambda
-term with a
hole
denoted by
[-]
.
Formally, the hole is an algebraic variable and contexts are defined as follows.

Definition
\thethm
.

•

A
context

C[-]
is generated by the grammar (for
x\in\mathrm{Var}
):

C[-]\ ::=\ [-]\ \mid\ x\ \mid\ \lambda x.C[-]\ \mid\ (C_{1}[-])(C_{2}[-])

•

A context
C^{\prime}[-]
is called
single hole
if it has a unique occurrence of the algebraic variable
[-]
.
Single hole contexts are generated by (for
M\in\Lambda
):

C^{\prime}[-]\ ::=\ [-]\ \mid\ \lambda x.C^{\prime}[-]\ \mid\ M(C^{\prime}[-])\ \mid\ (C^{\prime}[-])M

•

A context
H[-]
is a
head context
if it has the shape
(\lambda x_{1}\dots x_{n}.[-])M_{1}\cdots M_{k}
for some
n,k\geq 0
and
M_{1},\dots,M_{k}\in\Lambda
.

Given a context
C[-]
, we write
C[M]
for the
\lambda
-term obtained from
C[-]
by substituting
M
for the hole
[-]
possibly with capture of free variables in
M
.

A relation
\mathtt{R}\subseteq\Lambda\times\Lambda
is
context closed
whenever
M\ \mathtt{R}\ N
entails
C[M]\ \mathtt{R}\ C[N]
for all single hole contexts
C[-]
.
The
context closure
of a relation
\mathtt{R}\subseteq\Lambda\times\Lambda
is the smallest context closed relation
\mathtt{R}^{\prime}
containing
\mathtt{R}
.

Reductions
. The
\lambda
-calculus is a higher-order term rewriting system and several notions of reduction can be considered.
As a matter of notation, given a reduction
\mathtt{R}
, we write
\rightarrow_{\mathtt{R}}
for its context closure,
\twoheadrightarrow_{\mathtt{R}}
for the transitive and reflexive closure of
\rightarrow_{\mathtt{R}}
, finally
=_{\mathtt{R}}
for the corresponding
\mathtt{R}
-conversion
, that is the transitive, reflexive and symmetric closure of
\rightarrow_{\mathtt{R}}
.
We denote by
\mathsf{nf}_{\mathtt{R}}(M)
the
\mathtt{R}
-normal form (
\mathtt{R}
-nf
, for short) of
M
(if it exists) and by
\mathrm{NF}_{\mathtt{R}}
the set of all
\mathtt{R}
-normal forms.
Given two reductions
\mathtt{R}_{1},\mathtt{R}_{2}
we denote their union by simple juxtaposition, i.e.
\rightarrow_{\mathtt{\mathtt{R}_{1}\mathtt{R}_{2}}}
represents the relation
\rightarrow_{\mathtt{\mathtt{R}_{1}}}\cup\rightarrow_{\mathtt{\mathtt{R}_{2}}}
.

The main notion of reduction is the
\beta
-reduction
, which is the context closure of:

(\lambda x.M)N\rightarrow_{\mathtt{}}M\{N/x\}

where
M\{N/x\}
denotes the capture-free simultaneous substitution of
N
for all free occurrences of
x
in
M
.
The term on the left hand-side of the arrow is called
\beta
-redex
, while the term on the right hand-side is its
\beta
-contractum
.

A
\lambda
-term
M
is in
\beta
-normal form if and only if
M=\lambda\vec{x}.x_{i}M_{1}\cdots M_{k}
and each
M_{i}
is in
\beta
-normal form. We say that
M

has a
\beta
-normal form
whenever
\mathsf{nf}_{\mathtt{\beta}}(M)
exists.

It is well known that the
\lambda
-calculus is an intensional language — there are
\beta
-different
\lambda
-terms that are extensionally equal.
This justifies the definition of the
\eta
-reduction:

\lambda x.Mx\rightarrow_{\mathtt{}}M\textrm{ provided }x\notin\mathsf{FV}(M).

Notice however that, when
M
is a
\lambda
-abstraction, the
\eta
-reduction is actually a
\beta
-step.

Solvability.

Lambda terms are classified into solvable and unsolvable, depending on their capability of interaction with the environment, represented here by a context.

Definition
\thethm
.

•

A
\lambda
-term

such that
{H[M]=_{\beta}{\bf I}}
. Otherwise

.

•

Two

such that

.

•

Two

is solvable while

is unsolvable or

Solvability has been characterized by Wadsworth in terms of

[
88
]
.
We recall that a
\lambda
-term

is in

(

, for short) if it is of the form

for

.
Remark that in our notation the head variable

can be either bound or free.
A
\lambda
-term

\beta
-convertible to a hnf.
The

of a

-term

by

in

.

Theorem
\thethm

(Wadsworth

]
)
.

A
\lambda
-term

We say that

A Böhm tree can be also seen as the least upper bound of its finite approximants, and finite approximants can be seen as the normal forms of a

A

-term

\Lambda_{\bot}:\quad M,N\mathrel{::=}\ x\ \mid\ \lambda x.M\ \mid\ MN\ \mid\ \bot

\bot

The
\bot

\thethm
.

{\#\bot}=0,\qquad{\#(}\lambda x.t)=1+{\#t},\qquad{\#(}xt_{1}\cdots t_{k})=1+\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}{\#t}_{i}.

Inequational theories and

\thethm

63

\thethm

, and the unique element of

is the empty relation.

Given two sets
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
, their categorical product in
{\bf M\!Rel}
is
their disjoint union
{A_{1}}\hskip-2.0pt\mathrel{\&}\hskip-3.0pt{A_{2}}
and the projections
\pi_{1},\pi_{2}
are given by:

\pi_{i}=\big\{([{(i,a)}],a)\ |\ a\in A_{i}\big\}\in{\bf M\!Rel}({A_{1}}\hskip-2.0pt\mathrel{\&}\hskip-3.0pt{A_{2}},A_{i})\textrm{, for }i=1,2.

It is easy to check that this is actually the categorical product of
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
in
{\bf M\!Rel}
;
given
f\in{\bf M\!Rel}(B,A_{1})
and
g\in{\bf M\!Rel}(B,A_{2})
, the corresponding morphism
\langle{f},{g}\rangle\in{\bf M\!Rel}(B,{A_{1}}\hskip-2.0pt\mathrel{\&}\hskip-3.0pt{A_{2}})
is given by:

\langle{f},{g}\rangle=\big\{(b,(1,\alpha))\ |\ (b,\alpha)\in f\}\cup\{(b,(2,\alpha))\ |\ (b,\alpha)\in g\big\}\,.

Given two objects
A
and
B
, the exponential object
{A}\hskip-2.0pt\Rightarrow\hskip-2.0pt{B}
is
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{f}}(A)\times B
and the evaluation morphism is given by:

\mathrm{ev}_{AB}=\big\{\big(([{(a,\beta)}],a),\beta\big)\ |\ a\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{f}}(A)\ \text{and}\ \beta\in B\big\}\in{\bf M\!Rel}({(A\Rightarrow B)}\hskip-2.0pt\mathrel{\&}\hskip-3.0pt{A},B)\,.

Again, it is easy to check that in this way we defined an exponentiation.
Indeed, given any set
C
and any morphism
f\in{\bf M\!Rel}({C}\hskip-2.0pt\mathrel{\&}\hskip-3.0pt{A},B)
, there is exactly one morphism
\Uplambda(f)\in{\bf M\!Rel}(C,A\Rightarrow B)
such that:

\mathrm{ev}_{AB}\hskip-1.0pt\circ\hskip-1.0pt(\Uplambda(f)\times\mathrm{Id}_{S})=f.

which is
\Uplambda(f)=\big\{(c,(a,\beta))\ |\ ((c,a),\beta)\in f\big\}
.
∎

The category
{\bf M\!Rel}
provides a simple example of a non-well-pointed category.

Theorem
\thethm
.

No object
A\neq\top
is well-pointed, so neither is
{\bf M\!Rel}
.

Proof.

For every
A\neq\emptyset
, we can always find
f,g:A\to A
such that
f\neq g
and, for all
h:\top\to A,f\hskip-1.0pt\circ\hskip-1.0pth=g\hskip-1.0pt\circ\hskip-1.0pth
. Indeed, by definition of composition,
f\hskip-1.0pt\circ\hskip-1.0pth=\{(\omega,\alpha)\ |\ \exists\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{k}\in A,(\omega,\beta_{i})\in h,([\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{k}],\alpha)\in f\}
, and similarly for
g\hskip-1.0pt\circ\hskip-1.0pth
. Hence it is sufficient to choose
f=\{(a_{1},\alpha)\}
and
g=\{(a_{2},\alpha)\}
for
a_{1},a_{2}
different multisets with the same support.
∎

3.2.
The Class of Relational Graph Models

The class of graph models constitutes a subclass of the continuous semantics
[
81
]
and is the simplest generalization of the Engeler and Plotkin’s construction
[
39
,
74
]
.
This class has been widely studied in the literature and has been used to prove several interesting results
[
10
]
.
We recall that a graph models is given by a set
A
and a total injection
i:\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{f}}(A)\times A\to A
, and induces
\mathcal{P}(A)
as a reflexive object in the category of Scott’s domains and continuous functions.
Therefore, a bijective
i
does not induce automatically an isomorphism between
\mathcal{P}(A)
and
{\mathcal{P}(A)}\hskip-2.0pt\Rightarrow\hskip-2.0pt{\mathcal{P}(A)}
.
As shown in
[
10
, §5.5]
, no graph model
\mathscr{G}
can be extensional because
\mathscr{G}\models{\bf 1}\sqsubseteq{\bf I}
is never satisfied.

The definition of a relational graph model mimics the one of a graph model while replacing finite sets with finite multisets.
As we will see, relational graph models capture a particular subclass of reflexive objects living in
M​Rel
.

Definition
\thethm
.

A
relational graph model

\mathcal{D}=(D,i)
is given by an infinite set
D
and a total injection
i:\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{f}}(D)\times D\to D
.
We say that
\mathcal{D}
is
extensional
when
i
is bijective.

The equality
i(a,\alpha)=\beta
indicates that the “arrow type”
a\to\alpha
is equivalent to the type
\beta
. In particular, in an extensional relational graph model, every element of the model can be seen as an arrow. Keeping this intuition in mind, we adopt the notation below.

Notation
\thethm
.

Given an rgm
\mathcal{D}=(D,i)
,
a\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{f}}(D)
and
\alpha\in D
, we write
a\to_{i}\alpha
(or simply
a\to\alpha
, when
i
is clear) as an alternative notation for
i(a,\alpha)
.

As shown in the next proposition, the reflexive object induced by a relational graph model
(D,i)
is not some powerset of
D
as in the case of regular graph models, but rather
D
itself.
This opens the way to define extensional reflexive objects.

Proposition
\thethm
.

Given an rgm
\mathcal{D}=(D,i)
we have that:

(1)

\mathcal{D}
induces a reflexive object
(D,\textrm{App},\uplambda)
where

\uplambda=\big\{([{(a,\alpha)}],a\to_{i}\alpha)\ |\ a\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{f}}(D),\alpha\in D\big\}\in{\bf M\!Rel}({D}\hskip-2.0pt\Rightarrow\hskip-2.0pt{D},D),

\textrm{App}=\big\{([{a\to_{i}\alpha}],(a,\alpha))\ |\ a\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{f}}(D),\alpha\in D\big\}\in{\bf M\!Rel}(D,{D}\hskip-2.0pt\Rightarrow\hskip-2.0pt{D}),

(2)

If moreover
\mathcal{D}
is extensional, then also the induced reflexive object is.

Proof.

(
1
)
\textrm{App}\circ\uplambda=\big\{\big([(a,\alpha)],(a,\alpha)\big)\ |\ ([(a,\alpha)],a\to\alpha)\in\uplambda,([a\to\alpha],(a,\alpha))\in\textrm{App}\big\}=\big\{\big([(a,\alpha)],(a,\alpha)\big)\ |\ a\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{f}}(D),\alpha\in D\big\}=\mathrm{Id}_{\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{f}}(D)\times D}=\mathrm{Id}_{D\Rightarrow D}
.

(
2
) If
i
is bijective for every
\beta\in D
we have
\beta=a\to_{i}\alpha
for some
a\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{f}}(D)
and
\alpha\in D
. So
\uplambda\circ\textrm{App}=\big\{\big([a\to\alpha],a\to\alpha\big)\ |\ ([a\to\alpha],(a,\alpha))\in\textrm{App},([(a,\alpha)],a\to\alpha)\in\uplambda\big\}=\big\{\big([a\to\alpha],a\to\alpha\big)\ |\ a\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{f}}(D),\alpha\in D\big\}=\big\{\big([\beta],\beta\big)\ |\ \beta\in D\big\}=\mathrm{Id}_{D}
.
∎

Note that, when
i
is just injective, there are in principle different morphisms that could be chosen as inverses of
\uplambda
.
Therefore, there exist reflexive objects in
M​Rel
that are not relational graph models.
However, all inverses of
\uplambda
induce relational models that are strongly linear in the sense of
[
72
]
. Finally, since every isomorphism
f\in{\bf M\!Rel}(A,A)
is of the form
f=\{([\alpha],i(\alpha))\ |\ \alpha\in A\}
for some bijective map
i
, the class of extensional relational graph models coincides with the one of extensional reflexive objects living in this category.

3.3.
Building Relational Graph Models by Completion

Relational graph models – just like the regular ones – can be built by performing the free completion of a partial pair.

Definition
\thethm
.

(1)

A
partial pair

\mathcal{A}
is a pair
(A,j)
where
A
is a non-empty set of elements (called
atoms
) and
j:\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{f}}(A)\times A\to A
is a partial injection.

(2)

A partial pair
\mathcal{A}
is called
extensional
when
j
is a bijection between
\mathrm{dom}(j)
and
A
.

(3)

A partial pair
\mathcal{A}
is called
total
when
j
is a total function, and in this case
\mathcal{A}
is a relational graph model.

Hereafter, we consider without loss of generality partial pairs
\mathcal{A}
whose underlying set
A
does not contain any pair of elements. In other words, we assume
(\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{f}}(A)\times A)\cap A=\emptyset
.
This is not restrictive because partial pairs can be considered up to isomorphism.

Definition
\thethm
.

The
free completion

\overline{\mathcal{A}}
of a partial pair
\mathcal{A}
is the pair
(\overline{A},\overline{j})
defined as:

\overline{A}=\bigcup_{n\in{\rm Nature}}A_{n}
, where
A_{0}=A
and
A_{n+1}=((\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{f}}(A_{n})\times A_{n})-\mathrm{dom}(j))\cup A
; moreover

\overline{j}(a,\alpha)=\begin{cases}j(a,\alpha)&\textrm{if }(a,\alpha)\in\mathrm{dom}(j),\\
(a,\alpha)&\textrm{otherwise.}\\
\end{cases}

It is well known for graph models, and easy to check for relational graph models, that every
\mathcal{D}
is isomorphic to its own free completion
\overline{\mathcal{D}}\cong\mathcal{D}
.
In particular, given a partial pair
\mathcal{A}
, we have that
\overline{\overline{\mathcal{A}}}\cong\overline{\mathcal{A}}
.

Proposition
\thethm
.

If
\mathcal{A}
is an (extensional) partial pair, then
\overline{\mathcal{A}}
is an (extensional) rgm.

Proof.

The proof of the fact that
\overline{\mathcal{A}}
is an rgm is analogous to the one for regular graph models
[
10
]
. It is easy to check that when
j
is bijective, also its completion
\overline{j}
is.
∎

The following relational graph models are built by free completion and will be running examples in the next sections.

Example
\thethm
.

Some examples of relational graph model:

•

\mathcal{E}=\overline{({\rm Nature},\emptyset)}
was introduced in
[
49
]
,

•

\mathcal{D}_{\omega}=\overline{(\{\star\},\{(\omega,\star)\mapsto\star\})}
was first defined
(up to isomorphism) in
[
20
]
,

•

\mathcal{D}_{\star}=\overline{(\{\star\},\{([\star],\star)\mapsto\star\})}
was introduced in
[
67
]
.

Notice that
\mathcal{D}_{\omega}
and
\mathcal{D}_{\star}
are extensional, while
\mathcal{E}
is not.

3.4.
Categorical Interpretation

We now show how
\lambda
-terms and Böhm trees can be interpreted in a relational graph model,
and we review their main properties.

Recall that notions and notations concerning multisets have been introduced in Section
1.2
.
Given two
n
-uples
\vec{a},\vec{b}\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{f}}(A)^{n}
we write
\vec{a}+\vec{b}
for
(a_{1}+b_{1},\dots,a_{n}+b_{n})\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{f}}(A)^{n}
.

Definition
\thethm
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be an rgm,
M\in\Lambda
and
\mathsf{FV}(M)\subseteq\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
.
The
categorical interpretation of
M
in
\mathcal{D}
w.r.t.
\vec{x}
is the relation
|M|^{\mathcal{D}}_{\vec{x}}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{f}}(D)^{n}\times D
defined by:

(1)

|x_{i}|^{\mathcal{D}}_{\vec{x}}\,=\,\big\{((\omega,\dots,\omega,[\alpha],\omega,\dots,\omega),\alpha\big)\ |\ \alpha\in D\big\}
,
where
[\alpha]
stands in
i
-th position.

(2)

|\lambda y.N|^{\mathcal{D}}_{\vec{x}}\,=\,\big\{(\vec{a},a\to_{i}\alpha)\ |\ ((\vec{a},a),\alpha)\in|N|^{\mathcal{D}}_{\vec{x},y}\big\}
where we take

by
\alpha
-conversion.

(3)

|PQ|^{\mathcal{D}}_{\vec{x}}\,=\,\big\{((\vec{a}_{0}+\dots+\vec{a}_{k}),\alpha)\ |\ \exists\,\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k}\in\mathcal{D}\textrm{ such that }\\
{}\hskip 57.0pt(\vec{a}_{0},[\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k}]\to_{i}\alpha)\in|P|^{\mathcal{D}}_{\vec{x}}\textrm{ and }(\vec{a}_{j},\alpha_{j})\in|Q|^{\mathcal{D}}_{\vec{x}}\textrm{ for all }1\leq j\leq k

}
.

This definition extends to
\lambda\bot
-terms

by setting

and to Böhm trees of
\lambda
-terms by interpreting all their finite approximants, namely by setting
|\mathrm{BT}(M)|^{\mathcal{D}}_{\vec{x}}=\bigcup_{t\in\mathrm{BT}^{*}(M)}|t|^{\mathcal{D}}_{\vec{x}}
.

It is easy to check that the definition above is an inductive characterization of the usual categorical interpretation of
\lambda
-terms as morphisms of a Cartesian closed category.

Convention
.

From now on, whenever we write
|M|^{\mathcal{D}}_{\vec{x}}
we always assume that
\mathsf{FV}(M)\subseteq\vec{x}
.
When
M
is a closed
\lambda
-term we consider

simply as a subset of

.
In all our notations we omit the model
\mathcal{D}
when it is clear from the context.

Example
\thethm
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be any rgm. Then we have:

(1)

|{\bf I}|^{\mathcal{D}}=\{[\alpha]\to\alpha\ |\ \alpha\in D\}
and

(2)

|{\bf 1}|^{\mathcal{D}}=\{[a\to\alpha]\to a\to\alpha\ |\ \alpha\in D,a\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{f}}(D)\}
, thus:

(3)

|{\bf J}|^{\mathcal{D}}=\{[\alpha]\to\alpha\ |\ \alpha\in D^{\prime}\}\subseteq|{\bf 1}|^{\mathcal{D}}\subseteq|{\bf I}|^{\mathcal{D}}
, where
D^{\prime}
is the smallest subset of

then
\omega\to\alpha\in D^{\prime}
; if
\alpha\in D
and

then
a\to\alpha\in D^{\prime}
,

(4)

|\Delta|^{\mathcal{D}}=\{(a+[a\to\alpha])\to\alpha\ |\ \alpha\in D,a\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{f}}(D)\}
therefore:

(5)

|\Omega|^{\mathcal{D}}=|\bot|^{\mathcal{D}}=\emptyset
,

(6)

|\lambda x.x\boldsymbol{\Omega}|^{\mathcal{D}}=\{[\omega\to\alpha]\to\alpha\ |\ \alpha\in D\}
.

Consider the relational graph models
\mathcal{D}_{\omega}
and

from Example

.
From the calculations above it follows that

in both

.

3.5.
Soundness

Relational graph models satisfy the following substitution property and are sound
models of
\lambda
-calculus in the sense that they equate all
\beta
-convertible
\lambda
-terms.

Lemma
\thethm

(Substitution)
.

Let
M,N\in\Lambda
and
\mathcal{D}
be an rgm.
For
\vec{a}\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{f}}(D)^{n}
and
\alpha\in D
we have that

(\vec{a},\alpha)\in|M\{N/y\}|^{\mathcal{D}}_{\vec{x}}
if and only if there exist

b=[\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{k}]\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{f}}(D)
and
\vec{a}_{0},\dots,\vec{a}_{k}\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{f}}(D)^{n}
such
that
(\vec{a}_{\ell},\beta_{\ell})\in|N|^{\mathcal{D}}_{\vec{x}}
, for
1\leq\ell\leq k
,

((\vec{a}_{0},b),\alpha)\in|M|^{\mathcal{D}}_{\vec{x},y}
and
{\vec{a}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}\vec{a}_{\ell}}
.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
M
, the only interesting case being
M=PQ
.

(\Rightarrow)

We know that
(\vec{a},\alpha)\in|M\{N/y\}|_{\vec{x}}
if and only if there are
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{k}
and a decomposition
\vec{a}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}\vec{a}^{\ell}
such that
(\vec{a}^{\,0},[\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{k}]\to\alpha)\in|P\{N/y\}|_{\vec{x}}
and
(\vec{a}^{\ell},\gamma_{\ell})\in|Q\{N/y\}|_{\vec{x}}
for
1\leq\ell\leq k
.
By applying the induction hypothesis to the former assumption, we get
b^{0}=[\beta^{0}_{1},\dots,\beta^{0}_{m}]
and a decomposition
\vec{a}^{\,0}=\sum_{j=0}^{m}\vec{a}^{\,0}_{j}
such that
((\vec{a}^{\,0}_{0},b^{0}),{[\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{k}]\to\alpha})\in|P|_{\vec{x},y}
and
(\vec{a}^{\,0}_{i},\beta^{0}_{i})\in|N|_{\vec{x}}
for
1\leq i\leq m
.
From the latter, for each
\ell=1,\dots,k
we get
b^{\ell}=[\beta^{\ell}_{1},\dots,\beta^{\ell}_{k_{\ell}}]
and a decomposition
\vec{a}^{\ell}=\sum_{j=0}^{k_{\ell}}\vec{a}_{j}^{\ell}
such that
((\vec{a}^{\ell}_{0},b^{\ell}),\gamma_{\ell})\in|Q|_{\vec{x},y}
and
(\vec{a}^{\ell}_{j},\beta^{\ell}_{j})\in|N|_{\vec{x}}
for
1\leq j\leq k_{\ell}
.
We conclude that
((\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}\vec{a}_{0}^{\ell},\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}b^{\ell}),\alpha)\in|PQ|_{\vec{x},y}
.

(\Leftarrow)
By analogue calculations.
∎

Lemma
\thethm

(Monotonicity)
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be an rgm, and
M,N\in\Lambda
.
If
|M|^{\mathcal{D}}_{\vec{x}}\subseteq|N|^{\mathcal{D}}_{\vec{x}}
then for all contexts
C[-]
we have
|C[M]|^{\mathcal{D}}_{\vec{x}}\subseteq|C[N]|^{\mathcal{D}}_{\vec{x}}
.

Proof.

Notice that, by the Convention above, we assume
\mathsf{FV}(M)\cup\mathsf{FV}(N)\cup\mathsf{FV}(C[-])\subseteq\vec{x}
.
The result follows by a straightforward induction on
C[-]
.
∎

Theorem
\thethm

(Soundness)
.

Let
M,N\in\Lambda
and
\mathcal{D}
be a relational graph model.
If
M=_{\beta}N
then
|M|^{\mathcal{D}}_{\vec{x}}=|N|^{\mathcal{D}}_{\vec{x}}
.

Proof.

From the substitution lemma and Lemma
3.5
we have that
M\rightarrow_{\mathtt{\beta}}M^{\prime}
entails
|M|_{\vec{x}}=|M^{\prime}|_{\vec{x}}
.
By Church Rosser
M=_{\beta}N
if and only if they have a common reduct
P
such that
M\twoheadrightarrow_{\mathtt{\beta}}P
and
N\twoheadrightarrow_{\mathtt{\beta}}P
.
Summing up, we have
|M|_{\vec{x}}=|P|_{\vec{x}}=|N|_{\vec{x}}
.
∎

Definition
\thethm
.

•

The
\lambda
-theory
induced by a relational graph model
\mathcal{D}
is defined by

\mathrm{Th}(\mathcal{D})=\{(M,N)\in\Lambda\times\Lambda\ |\ |M|_{\vec{x}}=|N|_{\vec{x}}\}\,.

We write
\mathcal{D}\models M=N
for
(M,N)\in\mathrm{Th}(\mathcal{D})
.

•

Similarly, the
inequational theory
induced by
\mathcal{D}
is given by

\mathrm{Th_{\sqsubseteq}}(\mathcal{D})=\{(M,N)\in\Lambda\times\Lambda\ |\ |M|_{\vec{x}}\subseteq|N|_{\vec{x}}\}\,.

We write
\mathcal{D}\models M\sqsubseteq N
for
(M,N)\in\mathrm{Th_{\sqsubseteq}}(\mathcal{D})
.

•

An rgm
\mathcal{D}
is called
\mathcal{O}
-fully abstract
when
\mathcal{D}\models M=N
if and only if
M\equiv^{\mathcal{O}}N
.

•

\mathcal{D}
is
inequationally
\mathcal{O}
-fully abstract
when
\mathcal{D}\models M\sqsubseteq N
if and only if
M\sqsubseteq^{\mathcal{O}}N
.

•

We say that a
\lambda
-theory (resp. inequational theory)
\mathcal{T}
is
representable by a relational graph model
if there exists an rgm
\mathcal{D}
such that
\mathrm{Th}(\mathcal{D})=\mathcal{T}
(resp.
\mathrm{Th_{\sqsubseteq}}(\mathcal{D})=\mathcal{T}
).

•

We say that a
\lambda
-theory (resp. inequational theory) is a
(resp. inequational) relational graph theory
if it is represented by some relational graph model.

Lemma
\thethm
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be an rgm.

(i)

If
M\to_{\eta}N
then
|N|^{\mathcal{D}}_{\vec{x}}\subseteq|M|^{\mathcal{D}}_{\vec{x}}
,

(ii)

\mathcal{D}
is extensional if and only if
\boldsymbol{\lambda}\eta\subseteq\mathrm{Th}(\mathcal{D})
.

Proof.

(
i
) By inspecting their interpretations (Example
3.4
(1-2)) it is clear that
|{\bf 1}|\subseteq|{\bf I}|
.
For all
N\in\Lambda
,
\mathcal{D}\models{\bf 1}N\sqsubseteq{\bf I}N
(monotonicity) which entails
\mathcal{D}\models\lambda x.Nx\sqsubseteq N
(soundness).

(
ii
)
By monotonicity
\boldsymbol{\lambda}\eta\subseteq\mathrm{Th}(\mathcal{D})
if and only if
\mathcal{D}\models{\bf 1}={\bf I}
.
As above, we know that
\mathcal{D}\models{\bf 1}\sqsubseteq{\bf I}
holds.
The other inclusion holds if and only if for all
\alpha\in D
there exist
a,\beta
such that
i(a,\beta)=\alpha
if and only if
i
is bijective.
∎

As a consequence, the
\lambda
-theories induced by relational graph models and by
ordinary graph models are different, since no graph model is extensional.

, then it is extensional and
\lambda
-König.

Theorem 6.9
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be an rgm. If
\mathrm{Th_{\sqsubseteq}}(\mathcal{D})=\ \sqsubseteq_{\mathcal{H^{+}}}
or
\mathrm{Th}(\mathcal{D})=\mathcal{H^{+}}
then
\mathcal{D}
is extensional and
\lambda
-König.

Proof 6.10
.

Obviously
\mathcal{D}
must be extensional since
\mathcal{H^{+}}
is an extensional
\lambda
-theory.
By contradiction, we suppose that it is not
\lambda
-König. Then there is

such that
\mathsf{W}_{\mathcal{D}}(T)=\emptyset
and, by Proposition

, we get
|{\bf I}|=|{\bf J}_{T}|
.
This is impossible since
{\bf I}\not\sqsubseteq_{\mathcal{H^{+}}}{\bf J}_{T}
.

From Theorem

and Theorem
8
we get the main result of this section.

Theorem 6.10
.

For an rgm
\mathcal{D}
, the following are equivalent:

(i)

\mathcal{D}
is extensional and
\lambda
-König,

(ii)

\mathcal{D}
is inequationally fully abstract for
\sqsubseteq_{\mathcal{H^{+}}}
,

(iii)

\mathcal{D}
is fully abstract for
\mathcal{H^{+}}
.

In particular, the model
\mathcal{D}_{\star}
induces the same inequational and
\lambda
-theories as Coppo, Dezani-Ciancaglini and Zacchi’s filter model
[

]
, a result first appeared in
[
67
]
.

Corollary 6.11
.

The model
\mathcal{D}_{\star}
of Example
3.3
is inequationally fully abstract for Morris’s preorder

. In particular
\mathrm{Th}(\mathcal{D}_{\star})=\mathcal{H^{+}}

In this section we provide a characterization of those relational graph models that are (inequationally) fully abstract for

At this purpose, we introduce the notion of

The technique in this section is Böhm-like tree oriented, therefore it is convenient to fix some notations concerning Böhm-like trees and their interpretation.

(Decomposition of

7.2.
Hyperimmune Relational Graph Models

In Section
6
we exploited the Morris Separation (Theorem
2.3
) to reduce the problem of being fully abstract for
\mathcal{H^{+}}
to the property
\llbracket x\rrbracket\neq\llbracket{\bf J}_{T}x\rrbracket
, namely
\mathsf{W}_{\mathcal{D}}(T)=\llbracket x\rrbracket-\llbracket{\bf J}_{T}x\rrbracket\not=\emptyset
, for every tree
T\in\mathbb{T}^{\infty}_{\textrm{\sf rec}}
. The notion of
\lambda
-König relational graph model provided that.
Here there is a similar phenomenon: we exploit the decomposition seen above (Lemma
7.5
)
to reduce the full abstraction for
\mathcal{H^{*}}
to the property
\llbracket x\rrbracket=\llbracket{\bf J}_{T}x\rrbracket
, namely
\mathsf{W}_{\mathcal{D}}(T)=\llbracket x\rrbracket-\llbracket{\bf J}_{T}x\rrbracket=\emptyset
, for every
T\in\mathbb{T}^{\infty}_{\textrm{\sf rec}}
.
This is the intuition behind the following definition, which is some kind of dual of
\lambda
-König.

Definition 7.12

(Hyperimmune models)
.

An rgm
\mathcal{D}
is
hyperimmune
if for every
T\in\mathbb{T}^{\infty}_{\textrm{\sf rec}}

\mathsf{W}_{\mathcal{D}}(T)=\emptyset
.

The name refers to a standard concept in computability theory: a function
f:{\rm Nature}\to{\rm Nature}
is called
hyperimmune
if it is not bounded (upwardly) by any recursive function. One can prove that a relational graph model
\mathcal{D}
is hyperimmune exactly when it only admits witnesses that follow hyperimmune functions.
This observation justifies the choice of the terminology.
The notion of hyperimmunity first appeared in these terms in
[
16
]
for Krivine’s models.

Example 7.13
.

The model
\mathcal{D}_{\omega}
of Example
3.3
is hyperimmune.
As a matter of fact, since the model is freely generated by the equation
\star=\omega\to\star
, it is easy to verify that no element of
\mathcal{D}_{\omega}
can be a witness for any
T\in\mathbb{T}^{\infty}_{\textrm{\sf rec}}
following any infinite path
f
.

In the hypothesis of extensionality, this notion admits some additional characterizations.

Proposition 7.14
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be an extensional rgm. The following statements are equivalent:

(i)

\mathcal{D}
is hyperimmune,

(ii)

\llbracket x\rrbracket\subseteq\llbracket{\bf J}_{T}x\rrbracket
for all
T\in\mathbb{T}^{\infty}_{\textrm{\sf rec}},

(iii)

U\,_{\eta!}\!\!\twoheadleftarrow V
implies

for all Böhm-like trees

(iv)

U\leq^{\eta}_{!}V

(

.

Let
\mathcal{D}

\mathcal{D}

\mathcal{D}

(

By Theorem

(

\Rightarrow

iii
) Trivial.

(

\Rightarrow

satisfies

8.
Conclusions

We have studied the class of the relational graph models living inside the relational semantics of
\lambda
-calculus, and proved that they all enjoy the Approximation Theorem.
We exhibited a model inducing the minimum relational (in)equational graph theory, and provided sufficient and necessary conditions for a relational graph model to be fully abstract for
\mathcal{H^{+}}
(resp.
\mathcal{H^{*}}
).
Actually such characterizations of full abstraction hold more generally for all relational models, since these theories are extensional and the class of extensional relational graph models coincide with the class of extensional reflexive objects in
M​Rel
.

We conclude presenting some open problems that we consider interesting.

Problem 8.1
.

It is well known that the
\lambda
-theory
\mathcal{H^{*}}
satisfies the
\omega
-rule
[
7
, Def. 4.1.10]
, and the analogous result was recently proved for
\mathcal{H^{+}}
in
[
18
]
.
Does every extensional graph model satisfy the
\omega
-rule?

Problem 8.2
.

Are all
\lambda
-theories in the interval
[\mathcal{H^{+}},\mathcal{H^{*}}]
relational graph theories?
