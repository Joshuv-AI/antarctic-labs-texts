Hyperuniformity of the determinantal point processes associated with the Heisenberg group This manuscript was prepared for the Proceedings of the 2021 RIMS (Research Institute for Mathematical Sciences) Workshop ‘Mathematical Aspects of Quantum Fields and Related Topics’, which was held online on December 6–8, 2021. The Proceedings will be issued in RIMS Kôkyûroku edited by Fumio Hiroshima and published at Kyoto University Research Information Repository and RIMS Homepage.
Makoto Katori

Abstract
The Ginibre point process is given by the eigenvalue distribution of a non-hermitian complex Gaussian matrix in the infinite matrix-size limit. This is a determinantal point process (DPP) on the complex plane ℂ {\mathbb{C}} in the sense that all correlation functions are given by determinants specified by an integral kernel called the correlation kernel. Shirai introduced the one-parameter ( m ∈ ℕ 0 m\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0} ) extensions of the Ginibre DPP and called them the Ginibre-type point processes. In the present paper we consider a generalization of the Ginibre and the Ginibre-type point processes on ℂ {\mathbb{C}} to the DPPs in the higher-dimensional spaces, ℂ D , D = 2 , 3 , … {\mathbb{C}}^{D},D=2,3,\dots , in which they are parameterized by a multivariate level m ∈ ℕ 0 D m\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{D} . We call the obtained point processes the extended Heisenberg family of DPPs, since the correlation kernels are generally identified with the correlations of two points in the space of Heisenberg group expressed by the Schrödinger representations. We prove that all DPPs in this large family are in Class I of hyperuniformity. Keywords Hyperuniformity; Ginibre and Ginibre-type point processes; Determinantal point processes; Extended Heisenberg family of DPPs; Schrödinger representations of Heisenberg group

Recently in condensed matter physics and related material sciences,

8

23

Torquato

23

In order to clearly assert this classification,

Consider the classical and quantum kinetics of a single particle

The symplectic form of the
Lie bracket

[(p,q),(p^{\prime},q^{\prime})]=p\cdot q^{\prime}-q\cdot p^{\prime}

comes from
the Poisson bracket in the classical mechanics
and the commutator
[A,B]:=AB-BA
in quantum mechanics.
The
Heisenberg group
\mathsf{H}_{D}
is the Lie group
on
\mathbb{R}^{2D+1}
satisfying the group law

ZZ^{\prime}=Z+Z^{\prime}+\frac{1}{2}[Z,Z^{\prime}],\quad Z,Z^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{2D+1};

that is,

(p,q,\tau)(p^{\prime},q^{\prime},\tau^{\prime})=\left(p+p^{\prime},q+q^{\prime},\tau+\tau^{\prime}+\frac{1}{2}(p\cdot q^{\prime}-q\cdot p^{\prime})\right).

Let
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{D})
be the set of square integrable functions on

\mathbb{R}^{D}
, where the inner product is given by

\langle f,g\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{D})}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{D}}f(\zeta)\overline{g(\zeta)}d\zeta,\quad f,g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{D})

with the norm

\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{D})}:=\sqrt{\langle f,f\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{D})}}
,

f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{D})
,
where

\zeta=(\zeta^{(1)},\dots,\zeta^{(D)})\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
and

d\zeta
denotes the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{D}
.
For a smooth function
f
,
we introduce operators
X^{(\ell)}
and
\mathcal{D}^{(\ell)}
defined by

(X^{(\ell)}f)(\zeta)=\zeta^{(\ell)}f(\zeta),\quad(\mathcal{D}^{(\ell)}f)(\zeta)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{-1}}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\zeta^{(\ell)}}(\zeta),\quad\ell=1,\dots,D.

They satisfy the commutation relations

[X^{(\ell)},\mathcal{D}^{(\ell^{\prime})}]=\frac{\sqrt{-1}}{2}\delta_{\ell\ell^{\prime}},\quad\ell,\ell^{\prime}=1,\dots,D.

Note that the above will represent the

canonical commutation relations
in quantum mechanics,

[Q^{(\ell)},P^{(\ell^{\prime})}]=\sqrt{-1}\hbar\delta_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}
.
Here we should claim that the value of the Planck constant

\hbar
is specially chosen to be 1/2.
This corresponds to the choice of
the reference measure on
\mathbb{C}^{D}
as (
1.3
).
We consider a map from
\mathsf{H}_{D}
to the group of
unitary operators acting on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{D})
defined by

\rho(p,q,\tau)=e^{2\sqrt{-1}(p\cdot\mathcal{D}+q\cdot X+\tau I)},

where
\mathcal{D}:=(\mathcal{D}^{(1)},\dots,\mathcal{D}^{(D)})
,

X:=(X^{(1)},\dots,X^{(D)})
and
I
denotes the identity operator.
By the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula, we can show that

\rho(p,q,\tau)f(\zeta)=e^{2\sqrt{-1}(\tau+q\cdot\zeta+p\cdot q/2)}f(\zeta+p),\quad f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{D}).

The map
\rho
is called the
Schrödinger representation

of
\mathsf{H}_{D}
.
The kernel of
\rho
is
\{(0,0,k\pi):k\in\mathbb{Z}\}
,
since
e^{2\pi k\sqrt{-1}}=1,k\in\mathbb{Z}
.
The
reduced Heisenberg group

\mathsf{H}_{D}^{\rm red}
is defined by

\mathsf{H}_{D}^{\rm red}:=\mathsf{H}_{D}/\{(0,0,k\pi):k\in\mathbb{Z}\}
.
We consider the
matrix-element functions

of
\rho(p,q,\tau)

at
(f,g)\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{D})^{2}
,

\displaystyle M_{f,g}(p,q,\tau)

\displaystyle:=\langle\rho(p,q,\tau)f,g\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{D})}=\langle\rho(-p,q,\tau)\overline{g},\overline{f}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{D})}

\displaystyle=e^{2\sqrt{-1}\tau}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{D}}e^{2\sqrt{-1}q\cdot\zeta}f\left(\zeta+\frac{p}{2}\right)\overline{g\left(\zeta-\frac{p}{2}\right)}d\zeta,\quad f,g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}),

(2.1)

which is also called the
Fourier–Wigner transform

(see, for instance,
[
6
, Section 1.4]
).

2.2
Ground-state representation

We put

g(\zeta)=\left(\frac{2}{\pi}\right)^{D/4}\frac{e^{-\zeta^{2}}}{\pi^{D/2}}=:G_{0}(\zeta),\quad\zeta\in\mathbb{R}^{D},

(2.2)

and define the complex variables

x=(x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(D)}):=p+\sqrt{-1}q=(p^{(1)}+\sqrt{-1}q^{(1)},\dots,p^{(D)}+\sqrt{-1}q^{(D)})\in\mathbb{C}^{D}.

Then (
2.1
) becomes

M_{f,G_{0}}(p,q,\tau)=e^{2\sqrt{-1}\tau}\mathsf{B}[f](x)\frac{e^{-|x|^{2}/2}}{\pi^{D/2}}

with

\mathsf{B}[f](x):=\left(\frac{2}{\pi}\right)^{D/4}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{D}}f(\zeta)e^{2\zeta\cdot x-\zeta^{2}-x^{2}/2}d\zeta,\quad f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{D}).

(2.3)

This integral
is called the
Bargmann transform
.
For
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{D})
, this integral
converges uniformly for
x
in any compact subset of
\mathbb{C}^{D}
,
and hence
\mathsf{B}[f]
is an entire function on
\mathbb{C}^{D}
.
We can prove that for
f_{1},f_{2}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{D})

\langle M_{f_{1},G_{0}},M_{f_{2},G_{0}}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2D})}=\langle f_{1},f_{2}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{D})}=\langle\mathsf{B}[f_{1}],\mathsf{B}[f_{2}]\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{D},\lambda_{\mathrm{N}(0,1;\mathbb{C}^{D})})},

(2.4)

where
\lambda_{\mathrm{N}(0,1;\mathbb{C}^{D})}
is given by (
1.3
)
and

\langle F_{1},F_{2}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{D},\lambda_{\mathrm{N}(0,1;\mathbb{C}^{D})})}:=\int_{\mathbb{C}^{D}}F_{1}(x)\overline{F_{2}(x)}\lambda_{\mathrm{N}(0,1;\mathbb{C}^{D})}(dx)

with the norm

\|F\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{D},\lambda_{\mathrm{N}(0,1;\mathbb{C}^{D})})}:=\sqrt{\langle F,F\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{D},\lambda_{\mathrm{N}(0,1;\mathbb{C}^{D})})}}
.
The Bargmann–Fock space
\mathcal{F}_{D}
is
defined by

\mathcal{F}_{D}:=\left\{F:\mbox{$F$ is entire on $\mathbb{C}^{D}$ and
$\|F\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{D},\lambda_{\mathrm{N}(0,1;\mathbb{C}^{D})})}<\infty$}\right\}.

The equalities (
2.4
) imply that
the Bargmann transform is an

isometry
from
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{D})
into
\mathcal{F}_{D}
.
Hence,
if
\{f_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is a complete orthonormal system (CONS)
of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{D})
,
then
\{\mathsf{B}[f_{n}]\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{D}}
makes a CONS for
\mathcal{F}_{D}
.

For
(p,q,\tau),(p^{\prime},q^{\prime},\tau^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{2D+1}
,
we consider the correlation of these two points
expressed by the Schrödinger operators
acting on
G_{0}
such that

{\rm Cor}_{G_{0}}((p,q,\tau),(p^{\prime},q^{\prime},\tau^{\prime})):=\langle\rho(-p,q,\tau)G_{0},\rho(-p^{\prime},q^{\prime},\tau^{\prime})G_{0}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{D})}.

(2.5)

We take a CONS
\{f_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{D})
,
where we assume
f_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
,

n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Then
the two-point correlation (
2.5
)
is expanded and expressed using the matrix-element functions
(
2.1
)
as

\displaystyle{\rm Cor}_{G_{0}}((p,q,\tau),(p^{\prime},q^{\prime},\tau^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\langle\rho(-p,q,\tau)G_{0},f_{n}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{D})}\langle f_{n},\rho(-p^{\prime},q^{\prime},\tau^{\prime})G_{0}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{D})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\langle\rho(p,q,\tau)f_{n},G_{0}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{D})}\langle G_{0},\rho(p^{\prime},q^{\prime},\tau^{\prime})f_{n}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{D})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}M_{f_{n},G_{0}}(p,q,\tau)\overline{M_{f_{n},G_{0}}(p^{\prime},q^{\prime},\tau^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-(|x|^{2}+|x^{\prime}|^{2})/2}}{\pi^{D}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\mathsf{B}[f_{n}](x)\overline{\mathsf{B}[f_{n}](x^{\prime})}.

(2.6)

The Hermite polynomials on
\mathbb{R}
are defined by

H_{n}(\zeta):=(-1)^{n}e^{\zeta^{2}}\frac{d^{n}}{d\zeta^{n}}e^{-\zeta^{2}},\quad n\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\quad\zeta\in\mathbb{R},

and then the
Hermite orthonormal functions
are given by

\psi_{n}(\zeta):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{n}n!\sqrt{\pi}}}e^{-\zeta^{2}/2}H_{n}(\zeta),\quad n\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\quad\zeta\in\mathbb{R};

(2.7)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\psi_{n}(\zeta)\psi_{m}(\zeta)d\zeta=\delta_{nm},\,n,m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
Here we make a slight modification as

\widetilde{\psi}_{n}(\zeta):=2^{1/4}\psi_{n}(\sqrt{2}\zeta),\quad n\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\quad\zeta\in\mathbb{R}.

\{\widetilde{\psi}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
makes
a
real
CONS of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\widetilde{\psi}_{n}(\xi)\widetilde{\psi}_{m}(\xi)d\xi=\delta_{nm},\,n,m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, as assumed above.

We extend the above results on
\mathbb{R}
to

\mathbb{R}^{D}
,
D\in\mathbb{N}
by simply considering
direct products;
for
n:=(n^{(1)},\dots,n^{(D)})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{D}
,

\zeta:=(\zeta^{(1)},\dots,\zeta^{(D)})\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
,

\Psi_{n}(\zeta):=\prod_{\ell=1}^{D}\widetilde{\psi}_{n^{(\ell)}}(\zeta^{(\ell)}).

(2.8)

We can show that

\displaystyle\mathsf{B}[\Psi_{n}](x)

\displaystyle:=\prod_{\ell=1}^{D}\mathsf{B}[\widetilde{\psi}_{n^{(\ell)}}](x^{(\ell)})

\displaystyle=\prod_{\ell=1}^{D}\phi_{n^{(\ell)}}(x^{(\ell)})=:\Phi_{n}(x),\quad n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{D},\quad x\in\mathbb{C}^{D},

where

\phi_{n}(x):=\frac{x^{n}}{\sqrt{n!}},\quad n\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\quad x\in\mathbb{C}.

(2.9)

Hence (
2.6
) is calculated as

\displaystyle{\rm Cor}_{G_{0}}((p,q,\tau),(p^{\prime},q^{\prime},\tau^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-(|x|^{2}+|x^{\prime}|^{2})/2}}{\pi^{D}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{D}}\Phi_{n}(x)\overline{\Phi_{n}(x^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-(|x|^{2}+|x^{\prime}|^{2})/2}}{\pi^{D}}\prod_{\ell=1}^{D}\sum_{n^{(\ell)}=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^{(\ell)}!}\Big(x^{(\ell)}\overline{{x^{\prime}}^{(\ell)}}\Big)^{n^{(\ell)}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{e^{-|x|^{2}}}{\pi^{D}}}K_{\mathsf{H}_{D}}(x,x^{\prime})\sqrt{\frac{e^{-|x^{\prime}|^{2}}}{\pi^{D}}},\quad x,x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{C}^{D}.

By the gauge invariance of DPP
(see Lemma
3.2
below),
this can be identified with the
correlation kernel
K_{\mathsf{H}_{D}}(x,x^{\prime})
of
the Heisenberg family of DPPs
given by (
1.4
).
Note that this result is obtained due to
the special choice of
g
given by (
2.2
), which is
also written as

g(\zeta)=G_{0}(\zeta):=\frac{1}{\pi^{D/2}}\Psi_{0}(\zeta),\quad\zeta\in\mathbb{R}^{D}.

(2.10)

We think that (
2.8
) represents
the eigenstate in a multivariate level

n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{D}
of the
D
-dimensional
direct-product system of harmonic oscillators.
Since
\Psi_{0}(\zeta)
gives the ground state,
the above results on the choice (
2.10
)
can be regarded as the

ground-state representation
.

2.3
Higher-level representations

Now we consider the following general choice
of
g
,

\displaystyle g(\zeta)

\displaystyle=G_{m}(\zeta):=\frac{1}{\pi^{D/2}}\Psi_{m}(\zeta),

\displaystyle\quad m=(m^{(1)},\dots,m^{(D)})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{D},\quad\zeta=(\zeta^{(1)},\dots,\zeta^{(D)})\in\mathbb{R}^{D},

which corresponds to the higher-level state of
the
D
-dimensional system of harmonic oscillators.
The matrix-element function is then given by

M_{f,G_{m}}(p,q,\tau):=\langle\rho(p,q,\tau)f,G_{m}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{D})}=e^{2\sqrt{-1}\tau}\mathsf{B}_{m}[f](x,\overline{x})\frac{e^{-|x|^{2}/2}}{\pi^{D/2}},

where

\displaystyle\mathsf{B}_{m}[f](x,\overline{x})

\displaystyle:=\left(\frac{2}{\pi}\right)^{D/4}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{m}m!}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{D}}f(\zeta)e^{2\zeta\cdot x-\zeta^{2}-x^{2}/2}\mathbf{H}_{m}\left(\sqrt{2}\left(\zeta-\frac{x+\overline{x}}{2}\right)\right)d\zeta

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m!}}e^{|x|^{2}}\frac{\partial^{m}}{\partial x^{m}}\Big[e^{-|x|^{2}}\mathsf{B}[f](x)\Big].

Here we have used the multivariate notations,

\displaystyle 2^{m}:=\prod_{\ell=1}^{D}2^{m^{(\ell)}},\quad m!:=\prod_{\ell=1}^{D}m^{(\ell)}!,\quad\frac{\partial^{m}}{\partial x^{m}}:=\prod_{\ell=1}^{D}\frac{\partial^{m^{(\ell)}}}{\partial{x^{(\ell)}}^{m^{(\ell)}}},\quad\mathbf{H}_{m}(\xi):=\prod_{\ell=1}^{D}H_{m^{(\ell)}}(\xi^{(\ell)}).

By the definition (
2.3
),

\mathsf{B}_{0}[f](x)=\mathsf{B}[f](x)
.
The integral transformation

\mathsf{B}_{m}[f](x,\overline{x})
,
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{D}
is called the

true-
m
-Bargmann transform

by Vasilevski
[
25
]
,
the
polyanalytic Bargmann transform

by Abreu and Feichtinger
[
1
]
, and
has been studied as the
coherent state transform

by Mouayn
[
15
]
.

We can read from
Shirai’s paper
[
18
]
that,
for (
2.9
),

\frac{1}{\sqrt{m!}}e^{|x|^{2}}\frac{\partial^{m}}{\partial x^{m}}\Big[e^{-|x|^{2}}\phi_{n}(x)\Big]=\sqrt{\frac{m!}{n!}}L_{m}^{(n-m)}(|x|^{2})x^{n-m},\quad n,m\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\quad x\in\mathbb{C},

where
L_{n}^{(\alpha)}
is defined by (
1.6
).
Hence, we have

\mathsf{B}_{m}[\Phi_{n}](x,\overline{x})=\prod_{\ell=1}^{D}\sqrt{\frac{m^{(\ell)}!}{n^{(\ell)}!}}L_{m^{(\ell)}}^{(n^{(\ell)}-m^{(\ell)})}(|x^{(\ell)}|^{2})x^{n^{(\ell)}-m^{(\ell)}}

for

m=(m^{(1)},\dots,m^{(D)}),n=(n^{(1)},\dots,n^{(D)})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{D},\,x=(x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(D)})\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
.
It was also shown in
[
18
]
that

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}L_{m}^{(n-m)}(|x|^{2})L_{m}^{(n-m)}(|x^{\prime}|^{2})(x\overline{x^{\prime}})^{n-m}=\frac{1}{m!}e^{x\cdot\overline{x^{\prime}}}L_{m}(|x-x^{\prime}|^{2}),\quad m\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\quad x,x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{C}.

For each multivariate level
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{D}
,
as an extension of (
2.5
),
the correlation of two points

(p,q,\tau)
and
(p^{\prime},q^{\prime},\tau^{\prime})
in the space
of
\mathsf{H}_{D}
shall be given by

{\rm Cor}_{G_{m}}((p,q,\tau),(p^{\prime},q^{\prime},\tau^{\prime})):=\langle\rho(-p,q,\tau)G_{m},\rho(-p^{\prime},q^{\prime},\tau^{\prime})G_{m}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{D})}.

It is evaluated as

\displaystyle{\rm Cor}_{G_{m}}((p,q,\tau),(p^{\prime},q^{\prime},\tau^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{D}}M_{\Phi_{n},G_{m}}(p,q,\tau)\overline{M_{\Phi_{n},G_{m}}(p^{\prime},q^{\prime},\tau^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-(|x|^{2}+|x^{\prime}|^{2})/2}}{\pi^{D}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{D}}\mathsf{B}_{m}[\Phi_{n}](x,\overline{x})\overline{\mathsf{B}_{m}[\Phi_{n}](x^{\prime},\overline{x^{\prime}})}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-(|x|^{2}+|x^{\prime}|^{2})/2}}{\pi^{D}}e^{x\cdot\overline{x^{\prime}}}\prod_{\ell=1}^{D}L_{m^{(\ell)}}(|x^{(\ell)}-{x^{\prime}}^{(\ell)}|^{2})

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{e^{-|x|^{2}}}{\pi^{D}}}K_{\mathsf{H}_{D}}^{(m)}(x,x^{\prime})\sqrt{\frac{e^{-|x^{\prime}|^{2}}}{\pi^{D}}},\quad x,x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{C}^{D}.

By Lemma
3.2
,
this can be identified with the
correlation kernel
K_{\mathsf{H}_{D}}^{(m)}(x,x^{\prime})
of
the extended Heisenberg family of DPPs
given by (
1.9
).

3
Preliminaries and Proofs

3.1
Preliminaries for point processes

The configuration space of
point process

\Xi=\Xi(\cdot)

is given by

\mathrm{Conf}(S)=\Big\{\xi=\sum_{i}\delta_{x_{i}}:\mbox{$x_{i}\in S$,
$\xi(\Lambda)<\infty$ for all bounded set $\Lambda\subset S$}\Big\}.

Let

\mathcal{B}_{\rm c}(S)
be the set of
all bounded measurable
complex functions on
S
of compact support,
and for

\xi\in\mathrm{Conf}(S)
and
\phi\in\mathcal{B}_{\rm c}(S)

we set

\langle\xi,\phi\rangle:=\int_{S}\phi(x)\,\xi(dx)=\sum_{i}\phi(x_{i}).

Random variables written in this form
are generally called

linear statistics
of a point process
\Xi

[
7
]
.
For a point process
\Xi
,
if there exists a non-negative measurable function

\rho_{1}
such that

\mathbf{E}[\langle\Xi,\phi\rangle]=\int_{S}\phi(x)\rho_{1}(x)\lambda(dx)\quad\forall\phi\in\mathcal{B}_{\rm c}(S).

\rho_{1}
is called the
first correlation function
of
\Xi

with respect to the reference measure
\lambda(dx)
.
By definition,
\rho_{1}(x)
gives the
density of point

at
x\in S
with respect to
\lambda(dx)
.
For
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
from
\xi\in\mathrm{Conf}(S)
we define

\xi_{n}:=\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{n}:i_{j}\not=i_{k},j\not=k}\delta_{x_{i_{1}}}\cdots\delta_{x_{i_{n}}},

and denote the
n
-product measure of
\lambda
as

\lambda^{\otimes n}(dx_{1}\cdots dx_{n}):=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\lambda(dx_{i})
.
For a point process
\Xi
,
if there exists a symmetric, non-negative measurable function

\rho_{n}
on
S^{n}
such that

\mathbf{E}[\langle\Xi_{n},\phi\rangle]=\int_{S^{n}}\phi(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\rho_{n}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\lambda^{\otimes n}(dx_{1}\cdots dx_{n})\quad\forall\phi\in\mathcal{B}_{\rm c}(S^{n}),

then we say that
\rho_{n}
is the

n
-th correlation function
of
\Xi

with respect to
\lambda(dx)
.

Determinantal point process (DPP)
is defined as follows.

Definition 3.1

A point process
\Xi

on
(S,\mathcal{B}_{\rm c}(S),\lambda(dx))
is said to be a DPP with
a measurable kernel
K:S\times S\to\mathbb{C}
, if
the correlation functions
with respect to
\lambda(dx)
are given by

\rho_{n}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=\det_{1\leq i,j\leq n}[K(x_{i},x_{j})]\quad\mbox{for every $n\in\mathbb{N}$
and any $x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in S$}.

(3.1)

The integral kernel
K
is called the
correlation kernel.
The DPP is specified by the
triplet
(\Xi,K,\lambda(dx))
.

The following fact is well known.

Lemma 3.2

Consider a non-vanishing function

f:S\to\mathbb{C}
.
Even if the correlation kernel
K(x,y)
is transformed as

K(x,y)\to K_{f}(x,y):=f(x)K(x,y)\frac{1}{f(y)},\quad x,y\in S,

(3.2)

all correlation functions (
3.1
) are
the same and hence

(\Xi,K,\lambda(dx))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm(law)}}{{=}}(\Xi,K_{f},\lambda(dx)).

The transformation (
3.2
) is
called the
gauge transformation

and the above property of DPP is
referred to
gauge invariance
.
See, for instance, Lemma 3.8 in Section 3.6 of
[
12
]
.

If the point process is a DPP,
(\Xi,K,\lambda(dx))
, then

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[\langle\Xi,\phi\rangle]

\displaystyle=\int_{S}\phi(x)K(x,x)\lambda(dx),

\displaystyle\mathrm{var}[\langle\Xi,\phi\rangle]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{S\times S}|\phi(x)-\phi(y)|^{2}K(x,y)K(y,x)\lambda^{\otimes 2}(dxdy),\quad\phi\in\mathcal{B}_{\rm c}(S).

In particular, when
\phi
is the indicator function
of a bounded domain
\Lambda\subset S
;

\phi(x)={\bf 1}_{\Lambda}(x):=1
, if

x\in\Lambda
, and
:=0
, otherwise,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[\langle\Xi(\Lambda)\rangle]

\displaystyle=\int_{\Lambda}K(x,x)\lambda(dx),

\displaystyle\mathrm{var}[\langle\Xi(\Lambda)\rangle]

\displaystyle=\int_{\Lambda}\int_{S\setminus\Lambda}K(x,y)K(y,x)\lambda(dx)\lambda(dy).

3.2
Comments on Theorem
1.2

The
Bessel function of the first kind

and the
modified Bessel function of the first kind

are defined as

\displaystyle J_{\nu}(z)

\displaystyle:=\left(\frac{z}{2}\right)^{\nu}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\frac{(z/2)^{2n}}{n!\Gamma(\nu+n+1)},

\displaystyle I_{\nu}(z)

\displaystyle:=\left(\frac{z}{2}\right)^{\nu}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(z/2)^{2n}}{n!\Gamma(\nu+n+1)},\quad\nu>-1,\quad z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus(-\infty,0],

respectively.
The following were proved in
[
14
]
.

Proposition 3.3

For the Heisenberg family of DPPs,

(\Xi_{\mathsf{H}_{D}},K_{\mathsf{H}_{D}},\lambda_{\mathrm{N}(0,1;\mathbb{C}^{D})}(dx))

on
\mathbb{C}^{D}
,

D\in\mathbb{N}
, the following hold,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[\Xi_{\mathsf{H}_{D}}(\mathbb{B}^{(2D)}_{R})]

\displaystyle=\frac{R^{2D}}{D!},

\displaystyle\mathrm{var}[\Xi_{\mathsf{H}_{D}}(\mathbb{B}^{(2D)}_{R})]

\displaystyle=\frac{2R^{2D}}{(D-1)!}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{J_{D}(\kappa R)^{2}}{\kappa}(1-e^{-\kappa^{2}/4})d\kappa

\displaystyle=\frac{R^{2D}e^{-2R^{2}}}{D!}\sum_{n=0}^{D-1}[I_{n}(2R^{2})+I_{n+1}(2R^{2})],\quad R>0.

Theorem
1.2
is concluded from the above proposition,
if we use the following
asymptotic formula of the modified Bessel functions
(see, for instance,
[
16
, Section 10.17]
),

I_{\nu}(x)\sim\frac{e^{x}}{\sqrt{2\pi x}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}\frac{\alpha_{k}(\nu)}{k!2^{3k}}x^{-k},\quad\mbox{as $x\to\infty$}.

3.3
Proof of Theorem
1.6

For
R>0,n,m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, let

p_{n}^{(R,m)}:=\frac{m!}{n!}\int_{0}^{R^{2}}u^{n-m}e^{-u}|L_{m}^{(n-m)}(u)|^{2}du.

We introduce a series of random variables

Y^{(R,m^{(\ell)})}_{n^{(\ell)}}\in\{0,1\}
,
m^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,

n^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}

\ell=1,\dots,D
