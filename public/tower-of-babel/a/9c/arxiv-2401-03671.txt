Receiver-Oriented Cheap Talk Design
Itai Arieli, Ivan Geffner, Moshe Tennenholtz

Abstract
This paper considers the dynamics of cheap talk interactions between a sender and receiver, departing from conventional models by focusing on the receiver’s perspective. We study two models, one with transparent motives and another one in which the receiver can filter the information that is accessible by the sender. We give a geometric characterization of the best receiver equilibrium under transparent motives and prove that the receiver does not benefit from filtering information in this case. However, in general, we show that the receiver can strictly benefit from filtering and provide efficient algorithms for computing optimal equilibria. This innovative analysis aligns with user-based platforms where receivers (users) control information accessible to senders (sellers). Our findings provide insights into communication dynamics, leveling the sender’s inherent advantage, and offering strategic interaction predictions.



1
Introduction

The conventional sender-receiver model focuses on the interaction between
an informed sender, who is fully aware of the state of the game, and an oblivious receiver. In these interactions, the sender must disclose some of the information to the receiver in order to influence its behavior, and the receiver must then play an action that affects the utility of both players.
Relevant information disclosure models include cheap talk
[
10
,
17
]
, Bayesian persuasion
[
16
]
, and their extensions to multiple senders and receivers
[
12
,
7
,
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
]
.
Traditionally, all these interactions are always viewed from the point of view of the sender. However, in order to get a complete picture of the communication model, it is also critical to consider the receiver’s perspective.
Moreover, since the sender, as the informed side, has an inherent advantage in cheap talk models,
analyzing the best outcomes for the receiver can
level this advantage and provide a more reliable prediction of
strategic interactions.

We focus on interactions where the sender and the receiver can communicate via cheap talk and analyze two settings. First, we consider a setting where the sender has transparent motives (i.e., where the sender has a state-independent utility) in a similar fashion as Lipnowski and Ravid
[
17
]
, and we follow with a setting where there is a pre-play stage before the communication phase in which the receiver can
filter
the information that the sender receives about the realized state.

Our first result is providing a geometric characterization of the best sequential equilibrium for the receiver when the sender has transparent motives. It follows from this characterization that (a) the maximum utility that the receiver can get in equilibrium can be computed as a function of the prior by taking the maximum of finitely many linear functions, and (b) that the receiver cannot improve her utility by applying filters when the sender has transparent motives. We continue by showing that, in general (i.e., when the sender does not necessarily have transparent motives), the receiver can strictly benefit from filtering information. Moreover, if the receiver can filter, we provide an algorithm that outputs the best sequential equilibrium for the receiver when her action set is binary. Our algorithm runs in
O(k\log k)
time, where
k
is the number of possible states. For our final result, we consider the same setting but with two senders instead, and reduce the problem of finding the best sequential equilibrium for the receiver to a linear programming instance with
O(k)
variables and
O(k)
constraints. If there are three or more senders, it is easy to check that the receiver does not benefit from filtering information (see Section
4
for more details.)

A novelty in the second part of our analysis is giving the receiver the advantage of determining the information that the sender receives.
Intuitively, we can see this setting as the
dual
of Bayesian persuasion
[
16
]
. More precisely, in Bayesian persuasion settings, the sender can freely choose the mechanism by which the information about the realized state is disclosed to the receiver. By contrast, in our setting, the receiver can freely choose the mechanism by which the information about the realized state is disclosed to the sender (note, however, that the sender has to disclose this information to the receiver via cheap talk, which means that the sender can still manipulate the information received if it is in her interests to do so.) This aspect is motivated by several scenarios, as for instance:

•

Interacting with search engines like Google, Yahoo! or Bing. Here, the search engine plays the role of a sender that can access the necessary information about a given query on a large database and the user plays the role of a receiver that can impact the outcome of her interaction with the search engine by using specific keywords that influence how the information in the database is retrieved by the search engine.

•

Scenarios in which the state of the game is a function of both the sender and the receiver’s information and the sender should propose an agreement to the receiver (as, for example, in big economic transactions or in diplomacy and politics). In these cases, the receiver should strategically decide how much information it should disclose to the sender in order to influence her proposal.

•

Online platforms, such as Amazon, store a significant amount of information about their users. They have the ability to determine which information is disclosed to sellers, who, in turn, can utilize this data to target users through advertisements. Our model essentially examines the scenario in which the platform operates for the average user, revealing seller information to maximize the revenue for the average buyer in equilibrium.

Our analysis attempts to model these scenarios more accurately.

1.1
Related Literature

Our work on the setting with transparent motives is closely related to that of Lipnowski and Ravid
[
17
]
, who studied a cheap talk model where the sender has transparent motives and showed that the best sender equilibrium is the quasi-concave envelope of the sender’s utility function. Our result is complementary since we give the geometric characterization of the best equilibrium for the receiver. In fact, we show that the best equilibrium for the receiver can be computed as the maximum of at most
k^{2}
affine functions, where
k
is the number of states in the game.

Restricting the information available to the sender has been increasingly gaining traction in the community. Most notably, Bergemann, Brooks and Morris
[
6
]
considered a buyer-seller setting and studied possible ways to limit the seller’s information. In their work, they characterized the set of all pairs of possible utilities achievable in equilibrium. In particular, they provide an algorithm that achieves the optimal way to limit the seller’s information to maximize the expected buyers’ utility. Ichihashi
[
14
]
considered a Bayesian persuasion setting and studied how the outcome of the interaction is affected when the sender’s information is restricted. One of their results is that, if the receiver restricts sender information in a pre-play stage, the best utility that the receiver can get in this setting coincides with the one that the receiver would get in the “flipped game”, where the receiver persuades the sender. Other papers have studied similar questions in the context of common-interest coordination games
[
9
,
11
]
and cheap talk games
[
15
,
13
]
. The main differences with our work are the fact that (a) we have no restrictions on the utility of the sender or that of the receiver, and (b) we focus on the best equilibrium for the receiver.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we introduce the cheap talk setting where the sender has transparent motives. We also provide a geometric characterization of the best equilibrium for the receiver. In Section
3
we introduce
filtered information aggregation games
, where the receiver can limit the information of the senders. In Section
4
we state our main results regarding the characterization of the best sequential equilibrium for the receiver in filtered information aggregation games, and these are proved in Sections
5
,
6
and
7
, where we also show how to extend the results of Section
2
to sequential equilibria. We end with a conclusion in Section
8
.

2
Cheap Talk with Transparent Motives

2.1
Model

An
information transmission game
involves a
sender

s
and a
receiver

r
, and is defined by a tuple
\Gamma=(A,\Omega,p,M,u)
, where
A=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{\ell}\}
is the set of actions,
\Omega=\{\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{n}\}
is the set of possible states,
p
is a commonly known prior distribution over
\Omega
that assigns a strictly positive probability to each possible state,
M
is a finite set that contains the messages that the sender can send to the receiver (
M
is usually assumed to be the set of binary strings of length at most
N
, for some positive integer
N
), and
u:\{s,r\}\times\Omega\times A\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a utility function such that
u(i,\omega,a)
gives the utility of player
i
(where
i
is either the sender or the receiver) when action
a
is played at state
\omega
. Each information transmission game instance is divided into three phases. In the first phases, a state
\omega
is sampled from
\Omega
following the prior distribution
p
, and this state is disclosed to the sender
s
. During the second phase, the sender sends a message
m\in M
to the receiver, and in the third phase the receiver plays an action
a\in A
and each player
i\in\{s,r\}
receives
u(i,\omega,a)
utility.

Given an information transmission game
\Gamma=(A,\Omega,p,M,u)
, a strategy profile
\vec{\sigma}
for
\Gamma
consists of a pair of strategies
(\sigma_{s},\sigma_{r})
for the sender and the receiver, where
\sigma_{s}
is a map from
\Omega
to distributions over
M
(which is denoted by
\Delta(M)
), and
\sigma_{r}
is a map from
M
to
\Delta(A)
.
We say that
\vec{\sigma}
is a Nash equilibrium if no player can increase its utility by defecting from
\vec{\sigma}
. More precisely, denote by
u_{i}(\vec{\sigma})
the expected utility that
i
gets when players play
\vec{\sigma}
, then
\vec{\sigma}
is a Nash equilibrium if

u_{i}(\vec{\sigma}_{-i},\tau_{i})\leq u_{i}(\vec{\sigma})

for all players
i\in\{s,r\}
and all strategies
\tau_{i}
for
i
.

For future reference, some of the results in this paper require a stronger notion of equilibrium known as
sequential equilibrium
. A sequential equilibrium consists of a strategy profile
\vec{\sigma}
and a belief system
\beta
that (in this case) assigns a posterior distribution over
\Omega
to each possible message in
M
. Intuitively,
\beta
represents the receiver’s beliefs about the posterior distribution over
\Omega
given the message that it receives from the sender. The pair
(\vec{\sigma},\beta)
form a sequential equilibrium if

•

\beta
is
consistent
with
\vec{\sigma}
, which means that there exists a sequence of completely mixed strategies
\vec{\sigma}^{1},\vec{\sigma}^{2},\ldots
that converge to
\vec{\sigma}
such that the sequence of maps from messages to posterior distributions over
\Omega
obtained by applying Bayes’ rule assuming that the sender plays
\sigma_{s}^{1},\sigma_{s}^{2},\ldots
converges to
\beta
.

•

\sigma_{r}
is, according to
\beta
, a best-response for the sender for all messages
m\in M
.

In this section, we are interested in information transmission games in which the sender has
transparent motives
. This means that the sender’s utility
u(s,\omega,a)
only depends only on the action
a
played by the receiver, and not on the state
\omega
. Thus, for simplicity, we will define functions
u_{s}:A\to\mathbb{R}
and
u_{r}:\Omega\times A\to\mathbb{R}
that denote the utilities of the sender and the receiver respectively. More precisely,
u_{s}(a):=u(s,\omega,a)
and
u_{r}(\omega,a):=u(r,\omega,a)
for all
\omega\in\Omega
and
a\in A
.

2.2
Characterizing the Best Equilibrium for the Receiver

In this section, we are interested in finding the best Nash equilibrium for the receiver given an information transmission game with transparent motives. We show later how to extend this equilibrium to a sequential equilibrium in Section
7
.

We start by considering the case where the state space
\Omega
is binary (i.e.,
\Omega=\{\omega_{0},\omega_{1}
). We can assume without loss of generality that there exist
\ell+1
real numbers
0=b_{1}<b_{2}<\ldots<b_{\ell+1}=1
such that action
a_{i}
is optimal for the receiver if and only if the posterior probability
q
of state
\omega_{1}
after her interaction with the sender is in the interval
[b_{i},b_{i+1}]
. To see this, note that the expected utility of the receiver given some action
a
is a linear function over
q
that goes from
(0,u_{r}(\omega_{0},a))
to
(1,u_{r}(\omega_{1},a))
. Therefore, the set of points in which an action
a
gives the maximal utility over all other actions is an interval in
[0,1]
. If this interval is empty for some action
a
, it means that
a
will never be played under any circumstances since there will always be better options for the receiver. This implies that, without loss of generality, we can simply remove
a
from
A
and keep only those actions that are maximal at some point.

Using a slight abuse of notation, let
u_{r}(a,q)
denote the expected utility of the receiver given action
a
and the posterior probability
q
of
\omega_{1}
. Note that
u_{r}(a,q)
can be computed by the following expression:

u_{r}(a,q):=q\cdot u_{r}(a,\omega_{1})+(1-q)\cdot u_{r}(a,\omega_{0}).

For each
j\in\{1,2,\ldots,\ell+1\}
, let
I(j):=[u_{s}(a_{j-1}),u_{s}(a_{j})]
(using the convention that
a_{-1}:=a_{0}
and
a_{\ell+1}:=a_{\ell}
), and let
C
be the subset of pairs
(j,k)\in[l+1]^{2}
such that
I(j)\cap I(k)\not=\emptyset
. At a high level,
(j,k)\in C
if the optimal actions with prior
b_{i}
and the optimal actions with prior
b_{j}
can be combined in such a way that the utility of the sender is equal in both combinations.

u_{r}(a,p)=pu_{r}(a,\omega_{1})+(1-p)u_{r}(a,\omega_{0}).

Let

\tilde{u}_{r}(q):=\max_{b_{j}\leq q\leq b_{k}\ s.t.\ (j,k)\in C}\left(\alpha_{j,k}u_{r}(a_{j},b_{j})+(1-\alpha_{j,k})u_{r}(a_{k},b_{k})\right),

where
q\in[0,1]
and
\alpha_{j,k}
is a value in
[0,1]
that satisfies that
\alpha_{j,k}b_{j}+(1-\alpha_{j,k})b_{k}=q
.

\tilde{u}_{r}

Proof of Theorem
1
.

First we show that the receiver can guarantee at least
\tilde{u}_{r}(q)
utility in a Nash equilibrium. Let
q
be the prior probability that the realized state is
\omega_{1}
and suppose that
\tilde{u}_{r}(q)=\alpha_{j,k}u_{r}(a_{j},b_{j})+(1-\alpha_{j,k})u_{r}(a_{k},b_{k})
, for some
(j,k)\in C
. Since
(j,k)\in C
, it follows that
I(b_{j})\cap I(b_{k})\neq\emptyset
. Therefore, by definition, we can find
\beta_{j},\beta_{k}\in[0,1]
such that

\displaystyle\beta_{j}u_{s}(a_{j-1})+(1-\beta_{j})u_{s}(a_{j})=\beta_{k}u_{s}(a_{k-1})+(1-\beta_{k})u_{s}(a_{k}).

(1)

We next describe a cheap talk equilibrium that gives the receiver a utility of
\alpha_{j,k}u_{r}(a_{j},b_{j})+(1-\alpha_{j,k})u_{r}(a_{k},b_{k})
. Using the fact that
\alpha_{j,k}b_{j}+(1-\alpha_{j,k})b_{k}=q
, the splitting lemma of Aumann and Maschler
[
5
]
implies that there exists a communication protocol such that

•

The sender sends a message
m_{1}
with probability
\alpha_{j,k}
and another message
m_{2}
with probability
1-\alpha_{j,k}
.

•

The posterior probability of
\omega_{1}
after the receiver receives message
m_{1}
is
b_{j}
and the posterior probability of
\omega_{1}
after the receiver receives message
m_{2}
is
b_{k}
.

Consider such a communication protocol in which, in the last phase, the receiver plays action
a_{j-1}
with probability
\beta_{j}
and action
a_{j}
with probability
1-\beta_{j}
whenever she receives message
m_{1}
. If she receives message
m_{2}
instead, she plays
a_{k-1}
with probability
\beta_{k}
and
a_{k}
with probability
1-\beta_{k}
.
If the receiver receives a message that is not
m_{1}
or
m_{2}
, she plays the action that gives the least possible utility for the sender.
By construction, since the posterior probability of
\omega_{1}
after receiving message
m_{1}
is
b_{j}
, both
a_{j-1}
and
a_{j}
are best responses for the receiver. Similarly, since the posterior probability of
\omega_{1}
after receiving message
m_{2}
is
b_{k}
, so are
a_{k-1}
and
a_{k}
in this scenario. Altogether, this implies that the receiver best-responds to the sender’s strategy. To see that this strategy profile is also incentive-compatible for the sender note that, by Equation
1
, the sender is indifferent between sending
m_{1}
or
m_{2}
. Moreover, sending any other message
m
would give her less utility than sending
m_{1}
or
m_{2}
. This shows that the receiver can indeed achieve
\tilde{u}_{r}(q)
utility in equilibrium.

We next show that the receiver’s utility is at most
\tilde{u}_{r}(q)
in any Nash equilibrium. It follows from standard extreme point considerations that if there exists a Nash equilibrium
\vec{\sigma}
in which the sender sends three or more messages with strictly positive probability, there exists another Nash equilibrium
\vec{\sigma}^{\prime}
in which the sender sends at most two different messages and both the sender and the receiver at least as much utility with
\vec{\sigma}^{\prime}
than with
\vec{\sigma}
. Thus, for the rest of the proof, we’ll restrict our analysis to communication protocols in which the sender sends at most two different messages with positive probability.

Suppose that the sender only sends one message with strictly positive probability. This means that the receiver plays her action with no additional information about the realized state, except for the prior
p
. Let
q
be the prior probability that the realized state is
\omega_{1}
and let
j\in[\ell]
be an index such that
q\in[b_{j},b_{j+1}]
. Since, in this case, the posterior distribution is the same as the prior distribution,
a_{j}
is the receiver’s best response, and her expected utility is given by
(1-q)\cdot u_{r}(\omega_{0},a_{j})+q\cdot u_{r}(\omega_{1},a_{j})
. We can rewrite this as an affine combination of
u_{r}(a_{j},b_{j})
and
u_{r}(a_{j},b_{j+1})
with the following expression:

u_{r}(a_{j},q)=\alpha u_{r}(a_{j},b_{j})+(1-\alpha)u_{r}(a_{j},b_{j+1}),

where
\alpha
is the value in
[0,1]
such that
\alpha b_{j}+(1-\alpha)b_{j+1}=q
. This shows that, by definition,
\tilde{u}_{r}(q)\geq u_{r}(a_{j},q)
, and therefore the receiver cannot get more utility than
\tilde{u}_{r}(q)
in Nash equilibria where the sender sends only one message.

Assume instead that the sender sends two messages
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
such that the posterior probability that the realized state is
\omega_{1}
after the receiver receives
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
are
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
, respectively. We first claim that we can assume without loss of generality that
x_{1}=b_{j}
and
x_{2}=b_{k}
for some
j,k\in[\ell+1]
. Suppose that
b_{j}<x_{1}<b_{j+1}
for some
j\in[\ell+1]
. Then, it means that the best response for the receiver when receiving message
m_{1}
is to play action
a_{j}
with probability
1
. Then, we can construct another equilibrium
\vec{\sigma}
in which the posterior probability after receiving message
m_{1}
is
b_{j}
while the posterior probability after receiving message
m_{2}
remains unchanged (note that the conditional probabilities of sending
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
are uniquely determined by Bayes’ plausibility constraints). By definition, playing
a_{j}
is still a receiver’s best response to
m_{1}
in
\vec{\sigma}
. Moreover, it is straightforward to check that the sender receives the same utility in the new equilibrium and the original equilibrium. It remains to check that the receiver also (weakly) increases her utility. To see this, note that the posterior distribution of
\vec{\sigma}
is a mean preserving spread of the original one. Therefore the new posterior distribution Blackwell dominates the original distribution and by Blackwell theorem
[
8
]
the receiver is (weakly) better. We can therefore assume from now on that
x_{1}=b_{j}
and
x_{2}=b_{k}
for some
j,k\in[\ell+1]
.

We next show that the posterior probabilities
b_{j}
and
b_{k}
described in the paragraph above satisfy that
(j,k)\in C
. To see this, note that the sender’s expected utility must be equal when sending
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
(otherwise, she can increase her utility by sending one of these messages by probability 1 and the other with probability
0
). By definition, the only best responses for the receiver given posterior probabilities
b_{j}
and
b_{k}
are
\{a_{j-1},a_{j}\}
, and
\{a_{k-1},a_{k}\}
, respectively. Suppose that the sender plays
a_{j-1}
with probability
\alpha_{j}
when receiving message
m_{1}
, and plays
a_{k-1}
with probability
\alpha_{k}
when receiving message
m_{2}
. Since the sender is indifferent between
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
,
\alpha_{j}
and
\alpha_{k}
must satisfy that

\alpha_{j}u_{s}(a_{j-1})+(1-\alpha_{j})u_{s}(a_{j})=\alpha_{k}u_{s}(a_{k-1})+(1-\alpha_{k})u_{s}(a_{k}),

which implies that
I(j)\cap I(k)\not=\emptyset
, and therefore that
(j,k)\in C
. This implies that we can represent the receiver’s utility as an affine combination of
u_{r}(a_{j},b_{j})
and
u_{r}(a_{k},b_{k})
using the following expression:

u_{r}(\vec{\sigma})=\alpha_{j,k}u_{r}(a_{j},b_{j})+(1-\alpha_{j,k})u_{r}(a_{k},b_{k}),

where
\alpha_{j,k}
is the unique value in
[0,1]
such that
\alpha_{j,k}b_{j}+(1-\alpha_{j,k})b_{k}=q
(note that this equality must hold because of Bayes’ plausibility constraints). This completes the proof of Theorem

.

2.3
General State space

In this section we generalize the proof of Theorem
1
to arbitrary information transmission games with transparent motives. At a high level, both the statement and the proof are quite similar to the case with binary states, although they require additional notation.

Let
\Gamma=(A,\Omega,p,M,u)
be an information transmission game with transparent motives where
\Omega=\{\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{n}\}
and
A=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{\ell}\}
. As in the proof of Theorem
1
, we identify
\Delta(\Omega)
with
\Delta^{n-1}
(the
n-1
-dimensional simplex) and assume without loss of generality that there exists no strictly dominated action. More generally, we assume without loss of generality that all actions are optimal in
some
posterior distribution, which is that, for all actions
a\in A
, the set

\left\{q\in\Delta^{n-1}\quad:\quad\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}u_{r}(a,\omega_{i})>\max_{a^{\prime}\in A,\ a^{\prime}\neq a}\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}u_{r}(a^{\prime},\omega_{i})\right\}

is non-empty.

Let
D_{1},D_{2},\ldots,D_{\ell}
be the polytopes contained in
\Delta^{n-1}
such that
D_{j}
contains the set of points
q\in\Delta^{n-1}
for which action
a_{j}
is a best response given that the posterior distribution is
q
. Given a point

be the set of indices
j\in[\ell]
such that

and let

. Intuitively,

is the interval ranging from the minimum to the maximum utility that the sender can get with a receiver’s best-response for

be the union of the set of vertices of

(i.e.,

is the set of points that are vertices of at least one of the polytopes), and let

of

and the intersection of all of the intervals

is non-empty. More precisely,

Moreover, given any vertex
\vec{v}=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{k})\in C

be the
convex hull
of
\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}\}
(i.e., the set of all the affine combinations of points in
\vec{v}
). Let

\tilde{u}_{r}(p):=\max\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}u_{r}(a(v_{i}),v_{i})\ \mid\ \vec{v}=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{k})\in C,p\in H(\vec{v})\right\},

where

is the action in

that has the lowest index and
(\alpha_{i})_{i=1}^{k}\in\Delta^{k-1}
are some affine parameters such that

. The generalization of Theorem
1
is as follows.

Theorem 2
.

Given an information transmission game
\Gamma=(A,\Omega,p,M,u)
with transparent motives, the maximum utility that the receiver can get in a Nash equilibrium is given by
\tilde{u}_{r}(p)
.

Proof.

Showing that
\tilde{u}_{r}(p)
is achievable by the receiver in a Nash equilibrium is analogous to the case with two states.
However, the converse direction requires a slightly different reasoning. As in the case with binary states, for every Nash equilibrium in which the sender sends
n+1
or more messages with positive probability (where
n
is the number of states), there exists a Nash equilibrium in which the sender sends
n
or fewer messages and gives the same utility for the sender and the receiver.

Suppose that
\vec{\sigma}
is a Nash equilibrium where the sender sends
k\leq n
different messages
m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}
with positive probability.
For each
i\in[k]
, denote by
v_{i}
the posterior distribution of possible states after the receiver receives message
m_{i}
when the sender plays
\sigma_{s}
.
As in the case of
n=2
, we claim that, without loss of generality, we can assume that
v_{i}\in V
for every
i\in[k]
. More precisely, we show next that, for every equilibrium
\vec{\sigma}
, there exists possibly a different equilibrium
\vec{\sigma}^{\prime}
in which each message
m_{i}
induces a posterior
v^{\prime}_{i}
such that (a)
v^{\prime}_{i}\in V
for all
i\in[k]
, (b) the sender gets the same utility in
\vec{\sigma}
and
\vec{\sigma}^{\prime}
, and (c) the receiver gets a (weakly) higher utility in
\vec{\sigma}^{\prime}
than in
\vec{\sigma}
.

Assume that
v_{i}\not\in V
for some
i\in[k]
. We claim that we can find a distribution
\lambda\in\Delta(V)
such that (i)
\sum_{v\in V}\lambda(v)v=v_{i}
and (ii) if
a_{j}\in A
is a best response for the receiver given a posterior distribution
v_{i}
, then
a_{j}
is also a best response for the receiver in every

v\in V
such that
\lambda(v)>0
. To see this, let
M_{i}\subseteq A
be the set of actions that are a best response for the receiver in
v_{i}
and let
T_{i}:=\cap_{a_{j}\in M_{i}}D_{j}
. Since
T_{i}
is a convex polytope that contains
v_{i}
, we can define
\lambda
as an affine combination of the vertices of
T_{i}
(where the coefficients represent the probabilities of each vertex). All such vertices satisfy the above (ii) property.

Assume that, in
\vec{\sigma}
, each message
m_{j}
is sent with total probability probability
q_{j}
. We define
\vec{\sigma}^{\prime}
as follows. The strategy
\sigma^{\prime}_{r}
of the receiver is equal to
\sigma_{r}
. The sender does the following. For all
j\neq i
, the
sender sends message
m_{j}
with total probability
q_{j}
(the same probability as in
\sigma_{s}
). However, instead of sending message

with probability
q_{i}
, in

the sender sends each vertex

with probability
\lambda(v)\cdot q_{i}
. By property (i), the strategy
\sigma^{\prime}_{s}
is well defined and, by property (ii), strategy
\sigma^{\prime}_{r}
is a best-response for the receiver to
\sigma^{\prime}_{s}

By construction, the sender has identical utility in
\vec{\sigma}
and in
\vec{\sigma}^{\prime}
. To see that the receiver (weakly) increases her utility, note that the posterior distribution in
\vec{\sigma}^{\prime}

is a mean preserving spread of

since we split

to the vertices of

gives the desired equilibrium.

To finish the proof, note that we can express the receiver’s expected utility as an affine combination of
\{u_{r}(a(m_{i}),m_{i})\}_{i=1}^{k}
. Moreover, by Bayes’ plausibility assumption, the affine coefficients

of the combination are uniquely determined by the identity

∎

2.4
Efficiently computing
\tilde{u}_{r}(q)
for a Binary State Sace

In this section we provide an algorithm that, given an information transmission game
\Gamma=(A,\Omega,p,M,u)
with
|A|=n
, computes the best utility of the receiver in a Nash equilibrium and runs in
O(n\log n)
time. By Theorem
1
, we know that the best utility of the receiver in a Nash equilibrium of
\Gamma
is a piece-wise linear function. More precisely, there exists a positive integer
k\leq n
, a sequence of
k+1
real numbers
0=x_{1}<x_{2}<\ldots<x_{k+1}=1
, and
k
linear functions
f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}
such that, if
q
is the prior probability that the realized state is
\omega_{1}
, then

\tilde{u}_{r}(q)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}f_{1}(q)&\mbox{if }q\in[x_{1},x_{2}]\\
f_{2}(q)&\mbox{if }q\in[x_{2},x_{3}]\\
\vdots&\vdots\\
f_{k}(q)&\mbox{if }q\in[x_{k},x_{k+1}].\\
\end{array}\right.

Before describing the algorithm, we need the following additional notation. Given two pairs of indices
(i,j)\in C
and
(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\in C
, we say that
(i,j)
is
dominated
by
(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})
if
i\geq i^{\prime}
and
j\leq j^{\prime}
. Intuitively,
(i,j)
is dominated by
(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})
if they both belong to
C
and the interval
[i^{\prime},j^{\prime}]
contains the interval
[i,j]
. It is easy to check that, if
(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})
dominates
(i,j)
, then, for each point
(x,y)
in the segment
s_{i,j}
that goes from
(b_{i},u_{r}(a_{i}))
to
(b_{j},u_{r}(a_{j}))
, there exists a point
(x,y^{\prime})\in s_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}
with
y^{\prime}\geq y
(this follows directly from the fact that the utility of the receiver is a convex function over the prior). This means that, in order to find the maximum of all the segments
s_{i,j}
with
(i,j)\in C
, we can restrict the search to segments that are not dominated by other segments. In particular, if we denote by
C_{\max}(i)
the maximum index
j
such that
(i,j)\in C
, we can restrict our search to all segments of the form
s_{i,C_{\max}(i)}
.

Suppose that we have an oracle that gives
C_{\max}(i)
for each
i
in constant time. We show next how to construct an
O(n\log n)
algorithm
\pi^{C_{\max}}
that outputs
k
and the sequences
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k+1}
and
f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}
using
C_{\max}
as a black box. At a high level, the algorithm computes all the segments of the form
s_{i,C_{\max}(i)}
and runs a variant of the Bentley-Ottmann algorithm in which, (a) we only output the segment intersections that involve the segment with the highest
y
-coordinate, and (b) whenever two segments
s
and
s^{\prime}
intersect, it eliminates the segment with the lowest slope. This guarantees that the number of segment intersections processed by the algorithm is at most
n
, and therefore that the total complexity of the algorithm is
O(n\log n)
. More precisely,
\pi^{C_{\max}}
consists of running a vertical sweep-line
L
that moves across the
x
-axis from left to right while keeping a list of all the segments that
L
intersects, sorted by the
y
-coordinate of their intersection with
L
. In order to do so, we need two data structures: a
binary search tree

T
whose purpose is to keep track of all the segments that
L
intersects, sorted by
y
-coordinate, and a
priority queue

Q
whose purpose is to keep a list of all the
events
that occur as
L
moves from left to right, sorted by
x
-coordinate. There are three types of events:

•

A segment
s
begins.
In this case, we insert
s
in
T
and query from
T
the segments
s^{\prime}
and
s^{\prime\prime}
that are immediately above and below
s
, respectively. If
s
intersects
s^{\prime}
or
s
intersects
s^{\prime\prime}
, we add these intersections to the event queue.

•

A segment
s
ends.
If
s
is still in
T
, we remove
s
from
T
. Let
s^{\prime}
and
s^{\prime\prime}
be the segments in
T
that are immediately above and below
s
. If
s^{\prime}
and
s^{\prime\prime}
intersect, we add the intersection to the event queue.

•

There is an intersection
P
between two segments
s
and
s^{\prime}
.
If either
s
or
s^{\prime}
has been previously deleted from
T
, this event is ignored. Otherwise, we erase from
T
the topmost segment between
s
and
s^{\prime}
and proceed as if the erased segment ended (i.e., we check the segments that are immediately above and below of the erased segment and, if they intersect, we add the intersection). If the segment removed was also the topmost segment of all
T
, we append
P
to the output.

The full algorithm is as follows. We initialize an empty binary search tree
T
and an empty priority queue
Q
and push
2n
events to
Q
indicating the beginning and end of segments
s_{1,C_{\max}(1)},s_{2,C_{\max}(2)},\allowbreak\ldots,s_{n,C_{\max}(n)}
. We then process the events in
Q
as described above until
Q
becomes empty and output the sequence of points obtained this way, in addition to
(0,u_{r}(a_{1}))
and
(1,u_{r}(a_{n}))
. The values
x_{i}
correspond to the
x
-coordinates of the points in the output, and the linear functions
f_{i}
are the ones that interpolate each pair of consecutive points.

The proof of correctness is equivalent to that of the Bentley-Ottmann algorithm. Note that the only difference between the algorithms is the way that we process the intersection events in
\pi^{C_{\max}}
. The Bentley-Ottmann algorithm outputs all segment intersections and, to do so, it swaps
s
and
s^{\prime}
inside
T
whenever
s
and
s^{\prime}
intersect (instead of processing them the way that we do). However, since a segment that is surpassed in
y
-coordinate by another segment can never be maximal again, we can optimize the algorithm by removing it right after processing its intersection. Since each intersection event erases one of the segments involved, we process at most
n
intersection events, which means that we process in total
3n
events (
n
starting points,
n
endpoints, and
n
intersections). Since each event is processed in
O(\log n)
time, the total time complexity is
O(n\log n)
. To see that the output of
\pi^{C_{\max}}
is correct, note that the vertices that define the piece-wise linear function
\tilde{u}_{r}
are either (a) intersections of different segments
s_{i,j}
or (b) points in which a segment
s_{i,j}
ends and another segment
s_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}
begins. If we use an implementation trick where we always process starting point events before endpoint events, the points defined in (b) would also be considered as intersections. This means that, using this implementation trick, the desired set of vertices is the subset of all segment intersections that occur at the top layer of the segments, which is precisely what
\pi^{C_{\max}}
outputs.

As a final note regarding this part of the algorithm, note that, as in Theorem
1
, we are assuming that
\Gamma
is given in a form such that
there exist
n+1
real numbers
0=b_{1}<b_{2}<\ldots<b_{n+1}=1
such that action
a_{i}
is the receiver’s optimal action in the interval
[b_{i},b_{i+1}]
. However, even if we can assume that
\Gamma
has this form without loss of generality, the input of the algorithm may not be reduced to this form yet. However, we can perform the same variant of the Bentley-Ottmann algorithm in order to reduce
\Gamma
to such a form, and to compute
b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{n+1}
. To see this, note that the linear function that outputs the utility that the receiver gets by playing
a_{i}\in A
as a function of the prior is a segment going from
(0,u_{r}(0,a_{i}))
to
(1,u_{r}(1,a_{i}))
. Reducing
\Gamma
to the desired form means precisely to compute the upper envelope of this set of segments.

Now, we have an
O(n\log n)
algorithm
\pi^{C_{\max}}
that, given
C_{\max}
as a black box, computes the best utility for the receiver. It remains to show how to compute
\{C_{\max}(1),\ldots,C_{\max}(n)\}
in
O(n\log n)
time as well.

By definition,

Filtered Information Aggregation Games

3.1
Model and Analysis

In this section we analyze the effects of filtering on
information aggregation games
. At a high level, information aggregation games are information transmission games with multiple senders. For a rigorous definition, an information aggregation game consists of a receiver
r
, and a tuple
\Gamma=(S,A,\Omega,p,M,u)

S=\{1,2,\ldots,k\}
is the set of senders and
A=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{\ell}\}
,
\Omega=\{\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{k}\}
,
p\in\Delta(\Omega)
,
M
and
u
are defined as in Information Transmission games (see Section
2.1
), with the only exception that
u
is a function from
S\cup\{r\}\times\Omega\times A
to
\mathbb{R}
instead of a function from
\{s,r\}\times\Omega\times A
to
\mathbb{R}
.

We are interested in a setting where the receiver can filter potential information from the senders. We model this as follows: at the beginning of the game, there is an additional phase (phase 0) where the receiver publicly chooses a function
X:\Omega\to\Delta(\{0,1\}^{*})
that maps each state
\omega
to a distribution over possible signals (we assume that signals are binary strings of arbitrary length). We call this function
filter
.
The rest of the game follows exactly in the same way as in a (standard) information aggregation game, except that, if the realized state is
\omega
, the senders receive a signal
x
sampled from
X(\omega)
instead of
\omega
itself. We call this a
filtered information aggregation game
(FIAG). More formally, a FIAG
\Gamma^{F}=(S,A,\Omega,p,M,u)
runs as follows:

•

Phase 0:
The receiver chooses a filter
X:\Omega\to\Delta(\{0,1\}^{*})
. The filter is disclosed to the senders.

•

Phase 1:
A state
\omega\in\Omega
is sampled according to the distribution
p
and is disclosed to the senders.

•

Phase 2:
Each sender
i\in S
sends a message
m_{i}\in M
to the receiver
r
.

•

Phase 3:
The receiver plays an action
a\in A
.

•

Phase 4:
Each player
i\in S\cup\{r\}
receives
u(i,\omega,a)
utility.

For an illustration of how the receiver may benefit from filtering, consider the following example.

Example 1
.

Let
\Gamma=(S,A,\Omega,p,M,u)
be an information aggregation game with
S=\{s\}
,
A=\{0,1\}
,
\Omega=\{\omega_{1},\omega_{2},\omega_{3},\omega_{4}\}
,
p
is the uniform distribution over
\Omega
and
u
is defined by the following table, where the first component of each cell is the utility of the sender and the second component is the utility of the receiver:

u(0,\omega)

u(1,\omega)

\omega_{1}

0,0

1,1

\omega_{2}

1,0

0,1

\omega_{3}

1,1

0,0

\omega_{4}

0,1

1,0

We show next that, in Example
1
, the receiver can double her maximal utility by filtering the sender’s information.

Proposition 1
.

If
\Gamma
is the game defined in Example
1
,

(a)

The utility of the receiver in all sequential equilibria of
\Gamma
is
\frac{1}{2}
.

(b)

There exists a sequential equilibrium in
\Gamma^{F}
where the receiver ends up playing her preferred action in every single state.

Proof.

We claim that in a standard cheap talk equilibrium, the receiver utility is always
\frac{1}{2}
and thus the receiver cannot get more than his utility at the bubbling equilibrium where information is not revealed. To see this consider without loss of generality an equilibrium with only two messages
m_{1},m_{2}
that are sent with positive probability. If, by way of contradiction, the equilibrium induces a utility that is strictly higher than
\frac{1}{2}
to the receiver, then both actions are played with positive probability and the receiver is strictly better off playing one of the actions, say action
1
, for at least one of the messages, say
m_{1}
. This means that the sender is strictly better off sending
m_{1}
at states
\Omega=\omega_{1},\omega_{4}
and message
m_{2}
at states
\omega_{2},\omega_{3}
. But in this equilibrium, the receiver’s utility is
\frac{1}{2}
. A contradiction.

In contrast, consider the following equilibrium in the filtered game. First, the receiver filters the sender’s information by partitioning
\Omega
as follows
\{\{\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\},\{\omega_{3},\omega_{4}\}\}
so that the sender only observes the chosen partition element. Then, at the second stage, the sender has two messages that are used to fully reveal his information to the receiver who plays action
a=1
if the chosen partition element is
\{\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\}
and action
a=0
if the chosen partition element is
\{\omega_{3},\omega_{4}\}
. It is easy to see that the sender maximizes his utility given
the realized partition element as he is indifferent among the two possible messages. The receiver in contrast receives his maximal utility under this equilibrium.
∎

However, even if filtering can greatly improve the receiver’s utility in in general, the following result complements Theorems
1
and
2
by stating that the receiver cannot improve her utility by filtering information if the sender has transparent motives.

Proposition 2
.

Let
\Gamma^{F}=(S,A,\Omega,p,M,u)
be a FIAG where the sender has transparent motives. Then, the receiver cannot increase her utility by filtering information.

Proof.

Given a sequential equilibrium
\vec{\sigma}
for
\Gamma^{F}
, consider the following strategy profile
\vec{\sigma}^{\prime}
where the receiver does not filter. In Phase 0, the receiver selects the filter that simply forwards the realized state to the senders (i.e., the information that the senders receive is not filtered). In Phase 2, the sender first simulates what signal would she get with
\vec{\sigma}
and what message
m
would she send to the receiver in
\vec{\sigma}
, and then she sends
m
to the receiver. In Phase 3, the receiver plays whatever it would have played in
\vec{\sigma}
after receiving message
m
.

It is straightforward to check that
\vec{\sigma}^{\prime}
is also sequential equilibrium that gives each player the same utility as in
\vec{\sigma}
.
∎

In the next section we give an efficient protocols that computes the optimal filters for the receiver in arbitrary games with binary actions and one or two senders.

also includes the missing details in the proof of Theorem
5
.

5
Proof of Theorem
3

In this section we provide an algorithm that outputs the best Nash equilibrium for the receiver in any filtered information game
\Gamma^{F}=(S,A,\Omega,p,u)
with
S=\{s\}
and
A=\{0,1\}
.
In Section
7
we show how to extend the resulting Nash equilibrium to a sequential equilibrium.

Consider a game
\Gamma^{F}_{X}
that is identical to
\Gamma^{F}
except for the fact that the filter
X
is fixed beforehand (which means that the receiver doesn’t get to choose the filter in Phase 0).
The following proposition shows that there exists a Pareto-optimal Nash equilibrium with a relatively simple form.

Proposition 3
.

There exists a Pareto-optimal Nash equilibrium
\vec{\sigma}
in
\Gamma^{F}_{X}
such that, in
\vec{\sigma}
, either:

•

The receiver always plays 0.

•

The receiver always plays 1.

•

The receiver always plays the best action for the sender.

Before proving Proposition
3
we need additional notation.
First, given a filtered information game
\Gamma^{F}_{X}=(S,A,\Omega,p,u)
, let
u_{i}(x,a)
be the expected utility of player
i
on signal
x
and action
a
. This expected utility can be computed by the following equation:

u_{i}(x,a)=\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}\Pr[\omega\mid x]\cdot u_{i}(\omega,a),

where
\Pr[\omega\mid x]
is the probability that the realized state is
\omega
conditional on the fact that the sender received signal
x
. Similarly,
\Pr[\omega\mid x]
has the following expression:

\Pr[\omega\mid x]=\frac{\Pr[\omega\longleftarrow p,\ x\longleftarrow X(\omega)]}{\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}\Pr[\omega\longleftarrow p,\ x\longleftarrow X(\omega)]}.

Let
X_{0}
be the set of signals
x
in
\{X(\omega)\}_{\omega\in\Omega}
such that

u_{s}(x,0)>u_{s}(x,1)
(i.e., the set of signals in which the sender prefers
0
), let
X_{1}
be the set of signals such that

u_{s}(x,0)<u_{s}(x,1)
, and
X_{=}
be the set signals such that

u_{s}(x,0)=u_{s}(x,1)
.
Given a strategy profile
\vec{\sigma}
for
\Gamma^{F}_{X}
, denote by
\sigma_{s}(x,m)
the probability that the sender sends message
m
given signal
x
and denote by
\sigma_{r}(a,m)
the probability that the receiver plays action
a
given message
m
. Moreover, let
M_{0}^{\vec{\sigma}}
denote the set of messages that have a strictly positive probability to be sent by the sender on at least one signal
x
in which the sender prefers 0 (i.e.,
M_{0}^{\vec{\sigma}}
is the set of messages
m
such that there exists
x\in X_{0}
such that
u_{s}(x,m)>0
). We define
M_{1}^{\vec{\sigma}}
and
M_{=}^{\vec{\sigma}}
analogously.

With this notation, the following lemma describes all strategy profiles in
\Gamma^{F}_{X}
that are incentive-compatible for the sender.

Lemma 1
.

A strategy profile
\vec{\sigma}
for
\Gamma^{F}_{X}
is incentive-compatible for the sender if and only if the following is satisfied:

(a)

If
m\in M^{\vec{\sigma}}_{0}
, then
\sigma_{r}(0,m)\geq\sigma_{r}(0,m^{\prime})
for all messages
m^{\prime}
.

(b)

If
m\in M^{\vec{\sigma}}_{1}
, then
\sigma_{r}(1,m)\geq\sigma_{r}(1,m^{\prime})
for all messages
m^{\prime}
.

Lemma
1
states that, for a strategy profile to be incentive-compatible for the sender, the receiver should play
0
with maximal probability on all messages that could be sent on signals in which the sender prefers 0, and the receiver should play
0
with minimal probability on all messages that could be sent on signals in which the sender prefers 1. In particular, we have the following Corollary.

Corollary 2
.

A strategy profile
\vec{\sigma}
for
\Gamma^{F}_{X}
is incentive-compatible for the sender if and only if there exist two real numbers
a,b\in[0,1]
with
a\geq b
such that:

(a)

m\in M^{\vec{\sigma}}_{0}\ \Longrightarrow\ \sigma_{r}(0,m)=a
.

(b)

m\in M^{\vec{\sigma}}_{1}\ \Longrightarrow\ \sigma_{r}(0,m)=b
.

(c)

m\in M^{\vec{\sigma}}_{=}\ \Longrightarrow\ b\leq\sigma_{r}(0,m)\leq a
.

Proof of Lemma
1
.

Clearly, if (a) and (b) are satisfied, then
\vec{\sigma}
is incentive-compatible for the sender. Conversely, suppose that
\vec{\sigma}
is incentive-compatible for the sender but it doesn’t satisfy (a). This means that there exists a signal
x\in X_{0}
and a message
m
that satisfies
u_{s}(x,m)>0
and such that
u_{r}(0,m)<u_{r}(0,m^{\prime})
for some other message
m^{\prime}
. Therefore, if the sender sends
m^{\prime}
instead of
m
whenever it receives signal
x
, it could increase its expected utility. This contradicts the fact that
\vec{\sigma}
is incentive-compatible for the sender. The proof of the case in which
\vec{\sigma}
doesn’t satisfy (b) is analogous.
∎

Corollary
2
characterizes the necessary and sufficient conditions for a strategy profile
\vec{\sigma}
in
\Gamma^{F}_{X}
to be incentive-compatible for the sender. We show next that Proposition
3
follows from adding the receiver incentive-compatibility constraints into the mix. Denote by
u_{r}^{\vec{\sigma}}(m,0)
the receiver’s expected utility when playing action
0
conditioned on the fact that it received message
m
and that the sender plays
\sigma_{s}
. Then, we have the following cases:

Case 1:
There exists
m\in M_{0}^{\vec{\sigma}}
such that
u_{r}^{\vec{\sigma}}(m,0)<u_{r}^{\vec{\sigma}}(m,1)
: Since
\vec{\sigma}
is incentive-compatible for the receiver, it must be the case that
a=0
and therefore that
\sigma_{r}(0,m)=0
for all messages
m
.

Case 2:
There exists
m\in M_{1}^{\vec{\sigma}}
such that
u_{r}^{\vec{\sigma}}(m,0)>u_{r}^{\vec{\sigma}}(m,1)
: This time it must be the case that
b=1
and therefore that
\sigma_{r}(0,m)=1
for all messages
m
.

Case 3:

u_{r}^{\vec{\sigma}}(m,0)\geq u_{r}^{\vec{\sigma}}(m,1)
for all
m\in M_{0}^{\vec{\sigma}}
and
u_{r}^{\vec{\sigma}}(m,0)\leq u_{r}^{\vec{\sigma}}(m,1)
for all
m\in M_{1}^{\vec{\sigma}}
: In this case, consider a strategy profile
\vec{\sigma}^{\prime}
that is identical to
\vec{\sigma}
except that, whenever the receiver receives a message
m\in M_{0}^{\vec{\sigma}}
, it plays action
0
with probability 1 (as opposed to probability
a
), and when the receiver receives a message
m\in M_{1}^{\vec{\sigma}}
, it plays action
1
with probability 1 (as opposed to
1-b
). It is easy to check that, by construction,
\vec{\sigma}^{\prime}
Pareto-dominates
\vec{\sigma}
. Even so,
\vec{\sigma}^{\prime}
can be further improved: consider a strategy profile
\vec{\sigma}^{s}
such that the sender sends message
0
in all subsets
C\in X_{0}
and in all subsets
C\in X_{=}
such that
u_{r}(C,0)>u_{r}(C,1)
, and the sender sends message
1
in all other subsets. Additionally, the receiver plays action
0
when receiving message
0
and plays action
1
when receiving message
1
. It is easy to check that
\vec{\sigma}^{s}
satisfies that
u_{r}^{\vec{\sigma}^{s}}(0,0)\geq u_{r}^{\vec{\sigma}^{s}}(0,1)
and
u_{r}^{\vec{\sigma}^{s}}(1,0)\leq u_{r}^{\vec{\sigma}^{s}}(1,1)
. Therefore,
\vec{\sigma}^{s}
is a Nash equilibrium of
\Gamma^{E}_{X}
. Moreover,
\vec{\sigma}^{s}
Pareto-dominates
\vec{\sigma}^{\prime}
. To check this, note that both
\vec{\sigma}^{r}
and
\vec{\sigma}^{\prime}
play the best action for the sender at every possible signal
x
. The only difference is that, in signals in which the sender is indifferent, the receiver plays the best action for herself in
\vec{\sigma}^{s}
while it doesn’t necessarily do so in
\vec{\sigma}^{\prime}
.

This analysis provides a refinement of Proposition
3
. In fact, consider the following two strategy profiles:

•

Strategy
\vec{\sigma}^{r}
: Regardless of the signal received, the sender sends an empty
\bot
message. The receiver plays the action that gives her the most utility with no information.

•

Strategy
\vec{\sigma}^{s}
: After receiving the signal, the sender sends its preferred action. The receiver plays the action sent by the sender.

We have the following characterization of Pareto-optimal Nash equilibria:

Proposition 4

(Refinement of Proposition
3
)
.

If
\vec{\sigma}^{s}
is incentive-compatible for the receiver, then
\vec{\sigma}^{s}
is a Pareto-optimal Nash equilibrium of
\Gamma^{F}_{X}
. Otherwise,
\vec{\sigma}^{r}
is a Pareto-optimal Nash equilibrium of
\Gamma_{F}^{X}
.

Proposition
4
shows that there is a simple algorithm that outputs a Pareto-optimal Nash equilibrium of
\Gamma^{F}_{X}
once the filter
X
has been fixed beforehand. This reduces the problem of finding the best Nash equilibrium of
\Gamma^{F}
for the receiver to that of finding the filter
X^{OPT}
such that the Pareto-optimal Nash equilibrium of
\Gamma^{F}_{X}
gives the best possible utility for the receiver. In fact, the best Nash equilibrium
\vec{\sigma}^{OPT}
goes as follows:

1.

The receiver chooses the filter
X^{OPT}
such that
\vec{\sigma}^{s}
is incentive-compatible for the receiver and gives the receiver the maximum utility in
\Gamma^{F}_{X}
. If there is no filter such filter
X^{OPT}
, the receiver chooses an arbitrary filter.

2.

Given the chosen filter
X
and the signal
x
, the sender computes the Pareto-optimal Nash equilibrium
\vec{\sigma}
of
\Gamma^{F}_{X}
and plays
\sigma_{s}
with signal
x
.

3.

If the receiver sent an arbitrary filter in step 1, it plays the best action assuming no information. Otherwise, if the receiver receives
0
, it plays action
0
. If it receives
1
, it plays action
1
. If it receives something else it plays the best action assuming no information.

The following proposition shows that
\vec{\sigma}^{OPT}
satisfies the desired properties.

Proposition 5
.

\vec{\sigma}^{OPT}
is the Nash equilibrium of
\Gamma^{F}
that gives the most utility to the receiver.

Proof.

Once the sender chooses a filter
X
, the receiver and the sender both maximize their utilities if they play the Pareto-optimal strategy profile described in Proposition
4
. Therefore, it is optimal for the receiver to choose
X^{OPT}
.
∎

5.1
Computation of
X^{OPT}

In this section we show how to compute the optimal filter
X^{OPT}
for the receiver. Our aim is to find a filter
X^{OPT}
such that
\vec{\sigma}^{s}
is incentive-compatible for the receiver in
\Gamma^{F}_{X^{OPT}}
and such that the expected utility for the receiver with
\vec{\sigma}^{s}
is as large as possible. We begin by showing an algorithm that runs in
O(k\log k)
time, where
k
is the number of states (i.e., the size of
\Omega
), and then we show the proof of correctness for the algorithm provided.

5.1.1
An
O(k\log k)
Algorithm

For our algorithm, we restrict our search to
binary
filters (i.e., filters that only send signals in
\{0,1\}
) and later, in Lemma
2
, we show that this is done without loss of generality. With this restriction, we can describe possible candidates
X
by a function
X_{*}
that maps each state
\omega
to the probability that
X(\omega)
outputs
0
. For simplicity, we’ll also say that
X
is incentive-compatible for the sender (resp., for the receiver) if the strategy profile in which the sender forwards the signal to the receiver and the receiver plays whatever it gets from the sender is incentive-compatible for the sender (resp., for the receiver). We will also say that the sender (resp., the receiver) gets
a
expected utility in
X
, if
a
is the expected utility it gets when running the previous strategy in
\Gamma^{F}_{X}
. Note that
\vec{\sigma}^{s}
is always incentive-compatible for the sender (for all filters
X
), but a binary filter
X
might not always be since we are forcing the sender to send the signal received instead of its true preference. However, if
\vec{\sigma}^{s}
is incentive-compatible for the sender (resp., for the receiver) in some game
\Gamma^{F}_{X}
for some binary filter
X
, and the sender does not always have the same preference in
\Gamma^{F}_{X}
, then either
X
or its
complementary
(i.e., the filter
X^{\prime}
that sends
0
whenever
X
sends
1
, and sends
1
whenever
X
sends
0
) is incentive-compatible for the sender (resp., the receiver) and gives the same utility as
\vec{\sigma}^{s}
in
\Gamma^{F}_{X}
. This shows that, without loss of generality, we can simply search for the binary filter that (a) is incentive-compatible for both the sender and the receiver, and (b) that gives the most utility for the receiver.

Before we start, let
\Omega^{0}
(resp.,
\Omega^{1}
) denote the set of states in which both the sender and the receiver prefer
0
and let
\Omega_{0}^{1}
(resp.,
\Omega_{1}^{0}
) denote the set of states in which the sender strictly prefers
0
and the receiver strictly prefers
1
(resp., the sender strictly prefers
1
and the receiver strictly prefers
0
). The following algorithm outputs the optimal filter
X^{OPT}
for the receiver.

Step 1

Set
X^{OPT}_{*}(\omega)=1
for all
\omega\in\Omega^{0}
.

Step 2

Set
X^{OPT}_{*}(\omega)=0
for all
\omega\in\Omega^{1}
.

Step 3

Sort all states
\omega\in\Omega_{0}^{1}\cup\Omega_{1}^{0}
according to the value
\frac{u_{r}(\omega,0)-u_{r}(\omega,1)}{u_{s}(\omega,1)-u_{s}(\omega,0)}
. Let
\omega^{1},\omega^{2},\ldots,\omega^{k^{\prime}}
be the resulting list.

Step 4

Set
X^{OPT}_{*}(\omega)=1
for all
\omega\in\Omega_{1}^{0}
and
X^{OPT}_{*}(\omega)=0
for all
\omega\in\Omega_{0}^{1}
. If the resulting filter is incentive-compatible for the sender, output
X^{OPT}
.

Step 5

For
i=1,2,\ldots,k^{\prime}
do the following. If
X^{OPT}_{*}(\omega^{i})
is set to
1
(resp., to
0
), set it to
0
(resp., to 1). If the resulting filter is incentive-compatible for the sender, find the maximum (resp., the minimum)
q
such that setting
X^{OPT}_{*}(\omega^{i})
to
q
is incentive-compatible for the sender (we show below that it can be computed with a linear equation with amortized cost). If the resulting filter is incentive-compatible for the receiver, output
X^{OPT}
, otherwise output the constant filter
X^{OPT}_{*}\equiv 0
.

It is important to note that the algorithm always terminates, since if we get to
k^{\prime}
in step 5, the resulting filter will always output the signal that the sender prefers. It remains to show how to compute
q
in amortized linear time. For this purpose, let
d_{i}^{s}
(resp.,
d_{i}^{r}
) denote the difference in utility for the sender (resp., for the receiver) between playing
0
and playing
1
. More precisely,

\begin{array}[]{cc}d_{i}^{s}:=u_{s}(\omega_{i},0)-u_{s}(\omega_{i},1)\\
d_{i}^{r}:=u_{r}(\omega_{i},0)-u_{r}(\omega_{i},1).\\
\end{array}

Then, given a binary filter
X
,
\vec{\sigma}^{s}
is incentive-compatible for the sender in
\Gamma^{F}_{X}
if and only if

\begin{array}[]{ll}\sum_{i}p(\omega_{i})\cdot d_{i}^{s}\cdot X_{*}(\omega_{i})\geq 0\\
\sum_{i}p(\omega_{i})\cdot d_{i}^{s}\cdot(1-X_{*}(\omega_{i}))\leq 0.\end{array}

(2)

The first and second equation state that the sender prefers
0
on signal
0
and prefers
1
on signal
1
, respectively. Similarly,
\vec{\sigma}^{s}
is incentive-compatible for the receiver in
\Gamma^{F}_{X}
if and only if

\begin{array}[]{ll}\sum_{i}p(\omega_{i})\cdot d_{i}^{r}\cdot X_{*}(\omega_{i})\geq 0\\
\sum_{i}p(\omega_{i})\cdot d_{i}^{r}\cdot(1-X_{*}(\omega_{i}))\leq 0.\end{array}

(3)

It is straightforward to check that the incentive-compatibility equations for the sender monotonically increase and decrease, respectively, whenever we iterate in the fifth step of the algorithm. Therefore, we can simply pre-compute the sums

\begin{array}[]{l}Y_{0}^{s}:=\sum_{\omega\in\Omega_{0}^{0}}p(\omega)\cdot(u_{s}(\omega,0)-u_{s}(\omega,1))\\
Y_{1}^{s}:=\sum_{\omega\in\Omega_{1}^{1}}p(\omega)\cdot(u_{s}(\omega,0)-u_{s}(\omega,1))\\
W_{i,0}^{(s,s)}:=\sum_{j<i,\omega^{j}\in\Omega_{0}^{1}}p(\omega^{j})\cdot(u_{s}(\omega^{j},0)-u_{s}(\omega^{j},1))\\
W_{i,1}^{(s,s)}:=\sum_{j<i,\omega^{j}\in\Omega_{1}^{0}}p(\omega^{j})\cdot(u_{s}(\omega^{j},0)-u_{s}(\omega^{j},1))\\
W_{i,0}^{(s,r)}:=\sum_{j>i,\omega^{j}\in\Omega_{0}^{1}}p(\omega^{j})\cdot(u_{s}(\omega^{j},0)-u_{s}(\omega^{j},1))\\
W_{i,1}^{(s,r)}:=\sum_{j>i,\omega^{j}\in\Omega_{1}^{0}}p(\omega^{j})\cdot(u_{s}(\omega^{j},0)-u_{s}(\omega^{j},1))\end{array}

and analogues for the receiver:

\begin{array}[]{l}Y_{0}^{r}:=\sum_{\omega\in\Omega_{0}^{0}}p(\omega)\cdot(u_{r}(\omega,0)-u_{r}(\omega,1))\\
Y_{1}^{r}:=\sum_{\omega\in\Omega_{1}^{1}}p(\omega)\cdot(u_{r}(\omega,0)-u_{r}(\omega,1))\\
W_{i,0}^{(r,s)}:=\sum_{j<i,\omega^{j}\in\Omega_{0}^{1}}p(\omega^{j})\cdot(u_{r}(\omega^{j},0)-u_{r}(\omega^{j},1))\\
W_{i,1}^{(r,s)}:=\sum_{j<i,\omega^{j}\in\Omega_{1}^{0}}p(\omega^{j})\cdot(u_{r}(\omega^{j},0)-u_{r}(\omega^{j},1))\\
W_{i,0}^{(r,r)}:=\sum_{j>i,\omega^{j}\in\Omega_{0}^{1}}p(\omega^{j})\cdot(u_{r}(\omega^{j},0)-u_{r}(\omega^{j},1))\\
W_{i,1}^{(r,r)}:=\sum_{j>i,\omega^{j}\in\Omega_{1}^{0}}p(\omega^{j})\cdot(u_{r}(\omega^{j},0)-u_{r}(\omega^{j},1)).\end{array}

Note that these sums can be computed in amortized linear time over the total number of states. Once this sums are computed, if we are in the
i
th iteration of Step 5, to find
q
we can check in constant time if the solution of any of the following linear equations is in
[0,1]
:

\begin{array}[]{l}Y_{0}^{s}+W_{i,0}^{(s,s)}+p(\omega^{i})\cdot q\cdot{u_{s}(\omega,0)-u_{s}(\omega,1)}+W_{i,0}^{(s,r)}=0\\
Y_{1}^{s}+W_{i,1}^{(s,s)}+p(\omega^{i})\cdot(1-q)\cdot{u_{s}(\omega,0)-u_{s}(\omega,1)}+W_{i,1}^{(s,r)}=0.\\
\end{array}

If there exists such solution
q
, to check that it is incentive-compatible for both players, we should simply check if the following inequalities are satisfied:

\begin{array}[]{l}Y_{0}^{s}+W_{i,0}^{(s,s)}+p(\omega^{i})\cdot q\cdot{u_{s}(\omega,0)-u_{s}(\omega,1)}+W_{i,0}^{(s,r)}\geq 0\\
Y_{1}^{s}+W_{i,1}^{(s,s)}+p(\omega^{i})\cdot(1-q)\cdot{u_{s}(\omega,0)-u_{s}(\omega,1)}+W_{i,1}^{(s,r)}\leq 0.\\
Y_{0}^{r}+W_{i,0}^{(r,s)}+p(\omega^{i})\cdot q\cdot{u_{r}(\omega,0)-u_{r}(\omega,1)}+W_{i,0}^{(r,r)}\geq 0\\
Y_{1}^{r}+W_{i,1}^{(r,s)}+p(\omega^{i})\cdot(1-q)\cdot{u_{r}(\omega,0)-u_{r}(\omega,1)}+W_{i,1}^{(r,r)}\leq 0.\\
\end{array}

All of these computations can be performed in constant time if the necessary sums are pre-computed. This completed the description of the algorithm, note that it runs in
O(k\log k)
time since it takes
O(k\log k)
operations to sort the elements of
\Omega_{0}^{1}\cup\Omega_{1}^{0}
,
O(k)
operations to compute the sums, and
O(1)
operations in each iteration of Step 5. In the next section, we show that the algorithm’s output is correct.

5.1.2
Proof of Correctness

In this section, we show that the Algorithm presented in Section
5.1
is correct. We begin by showing that we can indeed restrict our search to binary filters filters.

Lemma 2
.

Let
X
be a filter such that
\vec{\sigma}^{s}
is incentive-compatible for the receiver in
\Gamma^{F}_{X}
. Then, there exists a binary filter
X^{\prime}
such that

(a)

\vec{\sigma}^{s}
is incentive-compatible for the receiver in
\Gamma^{F}_{X^{\prime}}

(b)

With strategy profile
\vec{\sigma}^{s}
, the expected utility of the receiver in
\Gamma^{F}_{X}
and
\Gamma^{F}_{X^{\prime}}
is identical.

Proof.

Recall that
\vec{\sigma}^{s}
is a strategy profile in which, for each possible signal
x
, the sender sends its preferred action and then the receiver plays whatever is sent by the sender. This means that, if
\vec{\sigma}
is incentive-compatible for the receiver in
\Gamma^{F}_{X}
, then we can merge all signals in which the sender prefers
0
, and also merge all signals in which the sender prefers
1
. More precisely, given filter
X
, let
X_{0}
and
X_{1}
be the sets of signals in which the sender prefers
0
and
1
respectively. Consider a filter
X^{\prime}
that sends signal
0
whenever
X
would send a signal in
X_{0}
, and sends signal
1
whenever
X
would send a signal in
X_{1}
. In
\Gamma^{F}_{X^{\prime}}
, by construction, both the sender and the receiver prefer action
0
on signal
0
and action
1
on signal
1
. This means that
\vec{\sigma}^{s}
is a Nash equilibrium of
\Gamma^{F}_{X^{\prime}}
. Moreover, again by construction, the expected utility of the receiver (and the sender) when playing
\vec{\sigma}^{s}
in
\Gamma^{F}_{X^{\prime}}
is identical to the one they’d get in
\Gamma^{F}_{X}
.
∎

Recall that binary filters
X
can be described by a function
X_{*}:\Omega\longrightarrow[0,1]
that maps each state
\omega\in\Omega
to the probability that
X(\omega)
assigns to
0
.
Because of Lemma
1
, our aim is to find which values in
[0,1]
we should assign to each element in
\Omega
. The following lemmas characterizes these values.

Lemma 3
.

There exists a filter
X^{OPT}
that maximizes the utility for the receiver such that

(a)

X^{OPT}_{*}(\omega)=1
for all
\omega\in\Omega_{0}^{0}
.

(b)

X^{OPT}_{*}(\omega)=0
for all
\omega\in\Omega_{1}^{1}
.

(c)

At most one state
\omega\in\Omega_{0}^{1}\cup\Omega_{1}^{0}
satisfies that
X^{OPT}_{*}(\omega)\not\in\{0,1\}
.

Proof.

Given a binary filter
X
, if we increase
X_{*}(\omega_{i})
by
\epsilon
, the expected utility of the receiver increases by
\epsilon p(\omega_{i})d_{i}^{r}
. This means that, if
d_{i}^{s}\geq 0
and
d_{i}^{r}\geq 0
, setting
X_{*}(\omega_{i})
to
1
both increases the receiver’s utility and preserves the incentive-compatibility constraints (see Equations
2
and
3
). Analogously, the same happens by setting
X_{*}(\omega_{i})
to
0
when
d_{i}^{s}\leq 0
and
d_{i}^{r}\leq 0
. This proves (a) and (b).

To prove (c) suppose that there exist two states
\omega_{i}
and
\omega_{j}
such that
X_{*}^{OPT}(\omega_{i}),X_{*}^{OPT}(\omega_{j})\not\in\{0,1\}
. Then, because of (a) and (b) we can assume without loss of generality that
\omega_{i},\omega_{j}\in\Omega_{0}^{1}\cup\Omega_{1}^{0}
. Therefore, if we increase
X_{*}(\omega_{i})
by
\epsilon d_{j}^{s}
and decrease
X_{*}(\omega_{j})
by
\epsilon d_{j}^{s}
, we’d have that the sender’s utility and incentive-compatibility constraints remain unchanged, but the receiver’s utility increases by
\epsilon(d_{j}^{s}d_{i}^{r}-d_{i}^{s}d_{j}^{r})
(note that this value can be negative). This means that if we choose an
\epsilon
that is small enough and is of the same sign as
d_{j}^{s}d_{i}^{r}-d_{i}^{s}d_{j}^{r}
, not only the expected utility of the receiver increases, but also the values
\sum_{i}p(\omega_{i})\cdot d_{i}^{r}\cdot X_{*}(\omega_{i})
and
\sum_{i}p(\omega_{i})\cdot d_{i}^{r}\cdot(1-X_{*}(\omega_{i}))
increase and decrease respectively. This contradicts the fact that the filter
X^{OPT}
is optimal for the receiver.
∎

Lemma
3
shows that we can assign
X_{*}^{OPT}(\omega)=1
for all
\omega\in\Omega_{0}^{0}

Intuitively, Theorem

For instance, suppose that we want to implement the outcome described in (a). Consider the strategy profile

Extending Nash to sequential equilibria

For simplicity, we showed that the strategies provided in the proofs of Theorems
1
,
2
,
3
and
4
are Nash equilibria. In this section we show how to extend them to sequential equilibria. This means that, for each of these strategy profiles, we have to provide a compatible belief assessment
b
and argue that the behavior of the players defined off the equilibrium path is a best-response according to their beliefs.

Note that, by construction, the only time that a player can be off the equilibrium path is when the receiver receives a message that the sender was not supposed to send under any circumstances (i.e., a message that had probability
0
of being sent). In the proofs of the Theorems stated above, the strategy profiles that we constructed either had the receiver play the best action for her in the bubbling equilibrium (i.e., the best action for the receiver without any information), or to play the action that gives the least utility to the sender between all the
relevant
actions (i.e., the actions that are not dominated by combinations of other actions). We show next that there exist belief assessments that are compatible with the given strategy profiles in which playing these actions is justified. More generally, we show that, for all relevant actions
a
, there exists a belief assessment that justifies playing
a
whenever the receiver receives an impossible message.

We prove the claim above for the case of one sender, the extension to Theorem
4
is straightforward. Denote by
\Omega=\{\omega_{1},\omega_{2},\ldots,\omega_{n}\}
the set of states and suppose that, with the proposed strategy
\sigma_{s}
, the sender sends messages in
M
following a certain function
msg:\Omega\to\Delta(M)
. Let
a
be an action that is optimal for the receiver at some posterior distribution
\lambda\in\Delta(\Omega)
and let
m
be a message such that
msg(\omega)(m)=0
for all
\omega\in\Omega
. Consider a sequence of strategies
\sigma_{s}^{1},\sigma_{s}^{2},\ldots
for the sender in which, in each strategy
\sigma_{s}^{k}
, the sender computes the message that she would send to the receiver using function
msg
. However, before sending the message, the sender tosses a coin that lands tails with probability
\frac{\lambda(\omega)}{k}
. If the coin lands heads, she sends the computed message. Otherwise, she sends message
m
. Clearly, the sequence
\sigma_{s}^{1},\sigma_{s}^{2},\ldots
converges to
\sigma_{s}
. Moreover, the posterior distribution over states induced by each of these strategies conditioned to the fact that the receiver received message
m
is precisely
\lambda
. This means that playing action

is always a best-response for the receiver when receiving message
m
. Therefore, in the belief assessment induced by
\sigma_{s}^{1},\sigma_{s}^{2},\ldots
,

is also a best response to message

.

8
Conclusion

This paper diverges from traditional sender-receiver models, emphasizing the receiver’s perspective in cheap talk interactions. We explore two models: one, with transparent motives and state-independent utility, provides a geometric profile of the optimal receiver equilibrium; the second introduces a pre-play stage, empowering the receiver to influence information available to the sender. Our approach aligns with user-centric platforms, where receivers control information, similar to platforms like Amazon. Our findings yield nuanced insights into communication dynamics, enhancing our comprehension of strategic interactions. An intriguing avenue not explored in this paper is the integration of a third intermediary controlling senders’ information to maximize the intermediary’s utility.
