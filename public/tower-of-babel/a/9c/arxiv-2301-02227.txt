Optimal lower bounds for Quantum Learning via Information Theory Thanks: A preliminary version of the results in Section 3 was included in the S.B.H.’s PhD thesis at University of Waterloo, December 2020 [ 5 ] . An extended abstract of the results in Section 4 was included in the P.S.’ bachelor’s project report at Indian Institute of Science, April 2022.
Shima Bab Hadiashar, Ashwin Nayak, Pulkit Sinha

Abstract
Although a concept class may be learnt more efficiently using quantum samples as compared with classical samples in certain scenarios, Arunachalam and de Wolf [ 3 ] proved that quantum learners are asymptotically no more efficient than classical ones in the quantum PAC and Agnostic learning models. They established lower bounds on sample complexity via quantum state identification and Fourier analysis. In this paper, we derive optimal lower bounds for quantum sample complexity in both the PAC and agnostic models via an information-theoretic approach. The proofs are arguably simpler, and the same ideas can potentially be used to derive optimal bounds for other problems in quantum learning theory. We then turn to a quantum analogue of the Coupon Collector problem, a classic problem from probability theory also of importance in the study of PAC learning. Arunachalam, Belovs, Childs, Kothari, Rosmanis, and de Wolf [ 1 ] characterized the quantum sample complexity of this problem up to constant factors. First, we show that the information-theoretic approach mentioned above provably does not yield the optimal lower bound, at least in its current form. As a by-product, we get a natural ensemble of pure states in arbitrarily high dimensions which are not easily (simultaneously) distinguishable, whereas the ensemble has close to maximal Holevo information. Second, we discover that the information-theoretic approach yields an asymptotically optimal bound for an approximation variant of the problem. Finally, we derive a sharper lower bound for the Quantum Coupon Collector problem, via the generalised Holevo-Curlander bounds on the distinguishability of an ensemble. All the aspects of the Quantum Coupon Collector problem we study rest on properties of the spectrum of the associated Gram matrix, which may be of independent interest.

Introduction

Suppose we wish to predict a feature, given an individual from a
certain population, drawn from an unknown distribution. For example, we
may wish to recognize whether or not the individual has “medium
build”.
We are given access to “labelled” random samples from the
population, i.e., we are told whether a given sample has the feature.
How many samples are needed for us to be able to predict the feature
with high probability? Valiant
[
29
]
developed the

probably approximately correct
(PAC) model to formalize such
learning tasks. Later, Bshouty and Jackson
[
9
]
extended the
notion of PAC learning to the quantum setting.
In reality, the labeled examples may be noisy, or they may not
necessarily be consistent with an underlying feature.
A more realistic model, the
agnostic
learning model, was introduced by
Haussler
[
15
]
, and Kearns, Schapire and
Sellie
[
17
]
. In the agnostic model, the goal is to
make predictions that approximate the feature that is most consistent
with the examples. Like the PAC model, the agnostic model can
be extended to the quantum setting. Other models for quantum learning and their advantages, or lack thereof, have also been studied (see, e.g., Refs.
[
6
,
10
]
). A comprehensive discussion of these models and the broader area is beyond the scope of this work.

The learning models mentioned above are all defined formally in Section
2
. Features
correspond to Boolean functions called
concepts
, and a
collection of features is called a
concept class
. The goal in
these models is to find
efficient
learners. A major figure of merit is
the
sample complexity
, the minimum number of examples required
to learn a concept. The sample complexity of learning a concept class
{\mathscr{C}}

in the above models depends heavily on a combinatorial quantity called the
VC
dimension
of
{\mathscr{C}}
, in addition to an approximation
parameter
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, and the probability of failure
\delta\in(0,1]
of the learner.

In a series of works
[
8
,
14
]
, it has been
shown that the
(\epsilon,\delta)
-PAC classical sample
complexity of a concept class with VC dimension
d
is

\Theta\left(\frac{d}{\epsilon}+\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\epsilon}\right)\kern 5.0pt.

(1.1)

For the agnostic model, the
(\epsilon,\delta)
-agnostic classical
sample complexity is

\Theta\left(\frac{d}{\epsilon^{2}}+\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\epsilon^{2}}\right)\kern 5.0pt.

(1.2)

The lower bound was shown by Vapnik and Chervonenkis
[
31
]
and
this bound was shown to be achievable by Talagrand
[
25
]
.

Although a concept class may be learnt more efficiently using quantum samples as compared with classical samples in certain scenarios (see, e.g.,
Refs.
[
9
,
23
]
), the quantum sample
complexity in the PAC and agnostic models can be at most a
constant factor lower than their classical counterparts. In particular,
Eq. (
1.1
) and
Eq. (
1.2
) also hold
for
(\epsilon,\delta)
-PAC
quantum
sample complexity
and
(\epsilon,\delta)
-agnostic
quantum
sample complexity,
respectively. The upper bounds carry over directly from the classical
learning algorithms, since a classical labelled example can be derived by
measuring a quantum example in the standard basis. The quantum lower
bounds are due to Arunachalam and de Wolf
[
3
]
.

Arunachalam and de Wolf begin with a simple information-theoretic argument to
re-derive the
classical
lower bounds in Eq.(
1.1
) and Eq.(
1.2
). They extend the argument to derive bounds for quantum
sample complexity, but the bounds are smaller by a factor of
\log(d/\epsilon)
.
They state that “the logarithmic loss
\dotsc
is actually inherent in
this information-theoretic argument”, and argue why this
is the case
[
3
, Section 1.2.1]
.
They present more sophisticated proofs via the “pretty good measurement” for quantum state identification and Fourier analysis for the optimal lower bounds.

From the arguments made by Arunachalam and de Wolf, one might be led to
believe that a logarithmic loss is inherent in the information-theoretic
approach. In this paper, we derive
optimal
lower bounds for quantum sample complexity
in both the PAC and agnostic models via an information-theoretic approach.
The proofs are arguably simpler, and the same ideas can potentially be used to derive optimal
bounds for other problems in quantum learning theory.

The proofs we develop refine the information-theoretic argument given in
Ref.
[
3
]
. The approach consists of
three steps. For concreteness, consider the PAC model. In the first
step, using the properties of a PAC learner, they show that the information contained in the quantum samples about the target concept is
\Omega(d)
. In the second step, they use

subadditivity
to bound this information by
t
times the
entropy of a single quantum sample (averaged over concepts), where
t

is the number of samples used by the learner. By bounding the entropy of
a single quantum sample, they conclude an
\Omega\big(\tfrac{d}{\epsilon\log(d/\epsilon)}\big)
bound. It turns out that the second step, i.e.,
the use of subadditivity, is the origin of the sub-optimality. Although their bound on information is tight for small values of
t
, we point out that the information does not exceed
d
, even as
t
goes to infinity. This hints at the possibility of a bound of order
\epsilon t
for sufficiently large
t
. This is essentially what we establish.

We estimate the (mutual) information directly. Since the quantum examples are pure states, mutual information simplifies to the von Neumann entropy of a uniformly random example. We determine the spectral decomposition of the corresponding quantum state. The eigenvalues of the state involve combinatorial quantities that do not seem easy to analyse. Nonetheless, we show that the aggregate expression for entropy may be analysed using standard tail inequalities from probability theory. The resulting bound on entropy is
\order(d)
, when
t\in\order(d/\epsilon)
. This gives us the optimal lower bound on sample complexity. The arguments for agnostic learning go along the same lines. (On a closer look, Arunachalam and de Wolf encounter similar quantities in their approach, and it is likely that the ideas we use also lead to a simplification of the proofs they give.)

We then turn to the
Coupon Collector
problem, a classic problem from probability theory which turns out to be important in the study of PAC learning. In this problem, given integers
k,n
(with
1<k<n
) the task is to independently draw uniformly distributed elements from an unknown size-
k
subset
S
of
[n]
, until all
k
elements of
S
have been observed. It is well-known that
k\ln k+\Theta(k)
samples are necessary and sufficient for the task to be accomplished with constant probability of success
[
20
, Theorem 5.13]
. Viewed as a learning problem, the task is to learn the unknown set
S
. It turns out that with
k\coloneqq n-1
the problem gives us an example of a concept class that is
(1/n,1/4)
-PAC learnable with
\Theta(n)
samples, albeit
improperly
, whereas
proper
PAC learning with the same parameters (i.e., producing a size-
(n-1)
subset as the hypothesis satisfying the PAC learning condition) requires
\Theta(n\ln n)
samples. We refer the reader to, e.g., Ref.
[
1
, Section 5]
for the details.

Motivated by the question of proper versus improper
quantum
learning, Arunachalam, Belovs, Childs, Kothari, Rosmanis, and de Wolf
[
1
]
studied a quantum analogue of the Coupon Collector problem. In the Quantum Coupon Collector problem, the learner has access to quantum samples, i.e., uniform superpositions over the elements of an unknown size-
k
subset
S
of
[n]
. The task is to learn
S
with high probability.
Arunachalam
et al.
showed that the quantum sample complexity of the problem matches that of classical coupon collection until
k
gets “too close” to
n
. More precisely, with
m\coloneqq n-k
, they proved that the sample complexity of the Quantum Coupon Collector problem with constant probability of error
<1
is

\Theta(k\log\min\left\{k,m+1\right\})\kern 5.0pt.

(1.3)

In particular, when
k=n-1
, the sample complexity is
\Theta(n)
. Thus, unlike in the classical case, the quantum version of the problem
does not
separate proper from improper quantum PAC learning.

Arunachalam
et al.
proved the lower bound for the Quantum Coupon Collector problem using the
adversary bound
(see, e.g., Ref.
[
7
]
) and properties of the Johnson association scheme. We study potentially simpler information-theoretic techniques for analysing the problem. First, we show that the natural approach we follow in Section
3

provably
does not yield the optimal lower bound, at least in its current form (
Section

4.3
). As a by-product, we get a natural ensemble of pure states in arbitrarily high dimensions which are not easily (simultaneously) distinguishable, whereas the ensemble has close to maximal Holevo information. (Since the states in the ensemble are pure, the Holevo information equals the von Neumann entropy of the ensemble average.) This complements early work in quantum information theory due to Jozsa and Schlienz
[
16
]
, which presents ensembles of states in three dimensions which can be “deformed” so that the von Neumann entropy increases whereas the states become
pairwise
less distinguishable.

Although it fails for the Quantum Coupon Collector problem, we discover that the standard information-theoretic approach yields an asymptotically optimal bound for a possibly harder variant of the problem, namely that of
approximating
the set to within a constant number of incorrect elements in expectation (
Theorem

4.15
). Finally, we take a different route—via the generalised Holevo-Curlander bounds
[
18
,
12
,
22
,
27
]
on the distinguishability of an ensemble of states—to derive
a sharper lower bound for the Quantum Coupon Collector problem (
Corollary

4.18
). In fact, we establish a bound of
(1-\order_{k}(1))k\ln\min\left\{k,m+1\right\}
, which has the optimal leading order term for
k\leq n/2
, including the constant factor
1
. We conjecture that this optimality holds for
k>n/2
as well.

All the aspects of the Quantum Coupon Collector problem we study rest on properties of the spectrum of the associated Gram matrix. In particular, we make essential use of a recurrence relation involving the spectrum of the Gram matrix. Similar to the analysis in Ref.
[
1
]
, the recurrence allows us to interpret certain spectral quantities as probabilities arising from random walks that resemble the classical Coupon Collector process. In contrast with Ref.
[
1
]
, though, the spectral quantities of relevance turn out to be the product of the eigenvalues with the corresponding multiplicity (informally speaking). The corresponding probabilities pertain to different events, and call for fresh analysis. The relationship with the aforesaid random walks lies at the heart of the delicate bounds on the spectral quantities, the corresponding entropy, etc., that are required for the results. These properties of the spectrum, as also the ideas underlying the bounds for PAC and Agnostic learning may be of wider applicability and interest.

.

Classical information theory.

We briefly describe the basics from information theory that we use in this work, and refer the reader to the text
[
11
]
for a thorough introduction to the subject.

We only consider random variables with finite sample spaces, and denote them with capital letters, like
X
,
Y
and
Z
. For a random
variable
X
over sample space
{\mathcal{X}}
, the
Shannon entropy
of
X
is defined as

\entropy(X)\quad\coloneqq\quad\sum_{x\in{\mathcal{X}}}p(x)\log\frac{1}{p(x)}\kern 5.0pt,

where
p
is the distribution of
X
. For jointly distributed random variables
X
and
Y
, the
conditional entropy
of
X
given
Y
is defined as

\entropy(X|\,Y)\quad\coloneqq\quad\sum_{y\in{\mathcal{Y}}}\Pr[Y=y]\entropy(X|Y=y)\kern 5.0pt,

where
{\mathcal{Y}}
is the sample space of
Y
.
By definition, conditional (Shannon) entropy is a non-negative quantity and
\entropy(X|\,Y)\leq\entropy(X)
. Moreover, Shannon entropy satisfies the following
chain rule
:

\entropy(XY)\quad=\quad\entropy(X)+\entropy(Y|\,X)\kern 5.0pt.

Suppose
X
and
Y
are two random variables with the same sample space such that
\Pr[X\neq Y]\leq\epsilon
for some
\epsilon\in[0,1]
. The
Fano inequality
bounds the conditional entropy of
X
given
Y
as

\entropy(X|\,Y)\quad\leq\quad\bentropy(\epsilon)+\epsilon\log(|{\mathcal{X}}|-1)\kern 5.0pt,

where
{\mathcal{X}}
is the sample space of
X
and has cardinality
|{\mathcal{X}}|
, and
\bentropy(\epsilon)\coloneqq-\epsilon\log\epsilon-(1-\epsilon)\log(1-\epsilon)
denotes the binary entropy function.

Tail bounds.

Let
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{n}
be
n
independent random variables over
\left\{0,1\right\}
and
X
denote their sum, i.e.,
X\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
. Let
\mu
be the expected value of
X
. The
Chernoff bound

[
21
]
states that
X
is concentrated around
\mu
. In particular, for
\delta\geq 0
, we have

\Pr\left[X\geq(1+\delta)\mu\right]\quad\leq\quad\exp\left(-\,\frac{\delta^{2}\mu}{2+\delta}\right)\kern 5.0pt.

(2.1)

The above bound is also known as the
multiplicative
Chernoff bound in the literature.
In the case that
X_{i}
are all Bernoulli random variables with
\expct X_{i}=1/2
, the random variable
X
has the binomial distribution
{\mathsf{B}}(n,1/2)
with
\mu=n/2
, and we have a tighter bound. For
k\leq n/2
,

\Pr\left[X\leq k\right]\quad=\quad\Pr\left[X\geq n-k\right]\quad=\quad\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{r=0}^{k}\binom{n}{r}\quad\leq\quad 2^{-\left(1-\bentropy(k/n)\right)n}\kern 5.0pt.

(2.2)

See, e.g., Ref.
[
13
, Lemma 16.19, page 427]
for this bound.

Quantum Information Theory.

For a thorough introduction to basics of quantum information, we refer the reader to the book by Watrous
[
32
]
. We briefly review the notation and some results that we use in this article.

We denote (finite dimensional) Hilbert spaces either by capital script letters like
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
, or directly as
{\mathbb{C}}^{m}
for a positive integer
m
.
A
register
is a physical quantum system, and we denote it by a capital letter, like
A
or
B
. A quantum register
A
is associated with a Hilbert space
{\mathcal{A}}
, and the state of the register is specified by a unit-trace positive semi-definite operator on
{\mathcal{A}}
. The state is called a
quantum state
, or also as a
density operator
. We denote
quantum states by lower case Greek letters, like
\rho
and
\sigma
. We use notation such as
\rho^{A}
to indicate that register
A
is in state
\rho
, and
may omit the superscript when the register is clear from the context. We use
ket
and
bra
notation to denote unit vectors
and their adjoints in a Hilbert space, respectively. A quantum state is
pure
if it has rank one, i.e.,
\rho=|\phi\rangle\!\langle\phi|
where
|\phi\rangle
is a unit vector.
Any register
A
with Hilbert space
{\mathbb{C}}^{S}
, for some non-empty finite set
S
, is called
classical
if any joint state with any other register
B
is invariant under the operator

\sigma^{AB}\quad\mapsto\quad\sum_{x\in S}\big(|x\rangle\!\langle x|^{A}\otimes{\mathbb{I}}^{B}\big)\,\sigma\,\big(|x\rangle\!\langle x|^{A}\otimes{\mathbb{I}}^{B}\big)\kern 5.0pt,

where
\left\{|x\rangle:x\in S\right\}
is the canonical basis for
{\mathbb{C}}^{S}
. In other words, the joint state of
A
and any other register
B
is always of the form
\sum_{x}p_{x}|x\rangle\!\langle x|^{A}\otimes\rho_{x}^{B}
, where
(p_{x})
is some distribution over
S
. A classical register corresponds to a random variable whose probability distribution is determined by the diagonal entries of the state of the register.

We denote the set of all linear operators on Hilbert space
{\mathcal{A}}

by
{\mathsf{L}}({\mathcal{A}})
, and we denote quantum channels, i.e., completely positive and trace-preserving linear maps from the space of linear operators
on a Hilbert space to another such space, by capital Greek letters
like
\Psi
.

For a register
A
with quantum state
\rho
, the
von Neumann entropy

\vnentropy(A)_{\rho}
of
A
is defined as

\vnentropy(A)_{\rho}\quad\coloneqq\quad-\trace\left(\rho\log\rho\right)\kern 5.0pt.

This definition coincides with the Shannon entropy of the spectrum of
\rho^{A}
. Hence, the entropy of a quantum state is invariant under the action of unitary transformations. For registers
A
and
B
with joint quantum state
\rho^{AB}
, the
mutual Information

\mi(A:B)_{\rho}
of
A
and
B
is defined as

\mi(A:B)_{\rho}\quad\coloneqq\quad\vnentropy(A)_{\rho}+\vnentropy(B)_{\rho}-\vnentropy(AB)_{\rho}\kern 5.0pt.

In the above notation, the subscript
\rho
may be omitted when the state is clear from the context. The mutual information of
A
and
B
is monotonic under the application of local quantum channels.
In particular, for a quantum channel
\Psi:{\mathsf{L}}({\mathcal{B}})\rightarrow{\mathsf{L}}({\mathcal{B}}^{\prime})
mapping states of register
B
to those of register
B^{\prime}
, we have

\mi(A:B)_{\rho}\quad\geq\quad\mi(A:B^{\prime})_{({\mathbb{I}}\otimes\Psi)(\rho)}\kern 5.0pt.

This is also known as the
Data Processing Inequality
.

Quantum learning theory.

We refer the readers to the book
[
24
]
for an introduction to machine learning theory and the survey
[
2
]
for an introduction to quantum learning theory. Here, we briefly review the
notation related to the PAC model and agnostic model that we study in this article.

We refer to a Boolean function
c:\left\{0,1\right\}^{n}\rightarrow\left\{0,1\right\}
as a
concept
.
We may also think of a concept as a bit-string in
\left\{0,1\right\}^{N}
for
N\coloneqq 2^{n}
which contains
the value of
c
at all possible
n
-bit strings. A
concept class
is a
subset
{\mathscr{C}}\subseteq\left\{0,1\right\}^{N}
of Boolean functions. For a concept
c
, we refer to
c(x)

as the
label
of
x\in\left\{0,1\right\}^{n}
, and the tuple
(x,c(x))
as
a
labeled example
. We say a concept class
{\mathscr{C}}
is
non-trivial
if it contains two distinct concepts
c_{1},c_{2}
such that for some
x_{1},x_{2}\in\left\{0,1\right\}^{n}
, we have
c_{1}(x_{1})=c_{2}(x_{1})
and
c_{1}(x_{2})\neq c_{2}(x_{2})
.

A crucial combinatorial quantity in learning Boolean functions is the
VC dimension
of a concept class, introduced by Vapnik and Chervonenkis
[
30
]
. We say a set
S\coloneqq\left\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{d}\right\}\subseteq\left\{0,1\right\}^{n}
is
shattered
by a concept class
{\mathscr{C}}
if for every
a\in\left\{0,1\right\}^{d}
, there exists a concept
c\in{\mathscr{C}}
such that
(c(s_{1}),\ldots,c(s_{d}))=a
. The
VC dimension
of
{\mathscr{C}}
, denoted as
\VC({\mathscr{C}})
, is the size of the largest set shattered by
{\mathscr{C}}
.

PAC model.

Consider a concept class
{\mathscr{C}}\subseteq\left\{0,1\right\}^{N}
. The PAC (
probably approximately correct
) model for learning concepts was introduced—in the classical setting—by Valiant
[
29
]
, and was extended to the quantum setting by Bshouty and Jackson
[
9
]
. In the quantum PAC model, a learning algorithm is given a
quantum PAC example oracle

\QPEX(c,D)
for an unknown concept
c\in{\mathscr{C}}
and an
unknown distribution
D
over
\left\{0,1\right\}^{n}
. The oracle
\QPEX(c,D)
does not have any inputs. When invoked, it outputs a superposition of labeled examples of
c
with amplitudes given by the distribution
D
,
namely, the pure state

\sum_{x\in\left\{0,1\right\}^{n}}\sqrt{D(x)}\,|x,c(x)\rangle\kern 5.0pt,

which we call a
quantum sample
, or simply a
sample
. Note that measuring a quantum sample in the computational basis gives us a labeled example distributed according to
D
, i.e., a classical sample.
We say a Boolean function
h
, commonly called a
hypothesis
, is an
\epsilon
-
approximation
of
c
(or has error
\epsilon
) with respect to distribution
D
, if

\Pr_{x\sim D}\left[h(x)\neq c(x)\right]\quad\leq\quad\epsilon\kern 5.0pt.

(2.3)

Given access to the oracle
\QPEX(c,D)
, the goal of a quantum PAC learner is to find a hypothesis
h
that is an
\epsilon
-approximation of
c
with sufficiently high success probability.

Definition 2.1
.

For
\epsilon,\delta\in[0,1]
, we say that an algorithm
{\mathscr{A}}
is an
(\epsilon,\delta)
-PAC quantum learner
for concept class
{\mathscr{C}}
if for every
c\in{\mathscr{C}}
and distribution
D
, given access to
\QPEX(c,D)
,
with probability at least
1-\delta
,
{\mathscr{A}}
outputs a hypothesis
h\in\left\{0,1\right\}^{N}
which is an
\epsilon
-approximation of
c

The
sample complexity
of a quantum learner
{\mathscr{A}}
is the maximum number of times
{\mathscr{A}}
invokes the oracle
\QPEX(c,D)
for any concept
c\in{\mathscr{C}}
and any distribution
D
over
\left\{0,1\right\}^{n}
. The
(\epsilon,\delta)
-
PAC quantum sample complexity
of a concept class
{\mathscr{C}}
is the minimum sample complexity of a
(\epsilon,\delta)
-PAC quantum learner for
{\mathscr{C}}
. Since we can readily derive classical samples from quantum ones, quantum learning algorithms are at least as efficient as classical ones in terms of sample complexity, as well as other measures such as time and space complexity.

Agnostic model.

In the PAC model it is assumed that examples are generated perfectly according to some unknown concept
c\in{\mathscr{C}}
. The
agnostic
model is a more realistic model where examples correspond to random labeled pairs
(x,l)
distributed according to an unknown distribution
D
over
\left\{0,1\right\}^{n+1}
, not necessarily derived from a specific concept
c\in{\mathscr{C}}
. This model was introduced in the classical setting by Haussler
[
15
]
, and Kearn, Schapire, and Sellie
[
17
]
. It was first studied in the quantum setting by Arunachalam and de Wolf
[
3
]
.
In the agnostic model, the learning algorithm has access to an agnostic quantum oracle
\QAEX(D)
for an unknown distribution
D
over
\left\{0,1\right\}^{n+1}
. When invoked, the oracle
\QAEX(D)
outputs the superposition

\sum_{(x,l)\in\left\{0,1\right\}^{n+1}}\sqrt{D(x,l)}\,|x,l\rangle\kern 5.0pt.

The
error
of a hypothesis
h\in\left\{0,1\right\}^{N}
under distribution
D
is defined as

\err_{D}(h)\quad\coloneqq\quad\Pr_{(x,l)\sim D}\left[h(x)\neq l\right]\kern 5.0pt.

(2.4)

For a concept class
{\mathscr{C}}
, the minimal error achievable is defined as

\opt_{D}({\mathscr{C}})\quad\coloneqq\quad\min_{c\,\in\,{\mathscr{C}}}\quad\err_{D}(c)\kern 5.0pt.

Given access to a
\QAEX(D)
oracle, the goal of quantum agnostic learner is to find a hypothesis
h\in{\mathscr{C}}
with error not “much larger” than
\opt_{D}({\mathscr{C}})
.

Definition 2.2
.

For
\epsilon,\delta\in[0,1]
, we say a learning algorithm
{\mathscr{A}}
is an
(\epsilon,\delta)
-agnostic quantum learner
for
{\mathscr{C}}
if for every distribution
D
, given access to
\QAEX(D)
,
with probability at least
1-\delta
,
{\mathscr{A}}
outputs a hypothesis
h\in{\mathscr{C}}
such that
\err_{D}(h)\leq\opt_{D}({\mathscr{C}})+\epsilon
.

As in the PAC model, we define the
sample complexity
of a quantum learner
{\mathscr{A}}
as the maximum number of times
{\mathscr{A}}
invokes the oracle
\QAEX(D)
for any distribution
D
over
\left\{0,1\right\}^{n+1}
. The
(\epsilon,\delta)
-
agnostic quantum sample complexity
of a concept class
{\mathscr{C}}
is the minimum sample complexity of an
(\epsilon,\delta)
-agnostic quantum learner for
{\mathscr{C}}
. Again, since a classical labeled example may be generated from a quantum sample by measurement in the computational basis, quantum learning algorithms in this model are also at least as efficient as their classical counterparts (with respect to the standard complexity measures).

Quantum Coupon Collection.

Let
n
be an integer
\geq 3
. For a positive integer
k\in(1,n)
, let
S
be a
k
-element subset of
[n]
, and let
|\psi_{S}\rangle
denote the uniform superposition over the elements of
S
:

|\psi_{S}\rangle\quad\coloneqq\quad\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\sum_{i\in S}|i\rangle\kern 5.0pt.

(2.5)

This is a quantum analogue of a uniformly random sample from
S
, and we call this a
quantum sample
of
S
. For ease of notation, we define
m\coloneqq n-k
.

In the Quantum Coupon Collector problem, we are given
n,k
and quantum samples of an arbitrary but fixed, unknown
k
-element subset
S
, and we would like to learn the subset using as few samples as possible. By “learning the subset”, we mean that we would like to determine, with probability at least
1-\delta
for some parameter
\delta\in[0,1)
, all the
k
elements of the set
S
. We are interested in the quantum sample complexity of the problem, i.e., the least number of samples required by a quantum algorithm to learn the set with probability of error at most
\delta
. Observe that by permuting the elements of
[n]
by a uniformly random permutation, we can show that the optimal worst-case error equals the optimal average-case error under the uniform distribution over the sets.

We also study a variant of this problem, in which given a parameter
\Delta\geq 0
the goal of the learning algorithm is to
approximate
the set
S
to within
\Delta
in expectation, i.e., output a size-
k
subset
S^{\prime}
such that the expected number of
mismatches

\left|S\setminus S^{\prime}\right|
is bounded:
\expct\left|S\setminus S^{\prime}\right|\leq\Delta
. Note that when
\Delta\leq 1
, with probability at least
1-\Delta
, such an algorithm outputs the set
S
. So the condition that the expected number of mismatches is bounded by
1
is a stronger condition than the learnability of the set
S
.

3
Learning Boolean functions

In this section, we give simple, information-theoretic lower bounds for the sample complexity of learning Boolean functions in the PAC and agnostic learning models.

3.1
Sample complexity of PAC learning

The lower bound for PAC learning in Eq. (
1.1
) contains two parts, one depends on VC dimension and the other one is independent of it. The VC-independent part of the lower bound was shown by Atıcı and Servedio
[
4
]
. For completeness, we provide a proof here.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
{\mathscr{C}}
be a non-trivial concept class. For every
\delta\in(0,1/2)
and
\epsilon\in(0,1/4)
, an
(\epsilon,\delta)
-PAC quantum learner for
{\mathscr{C}}
has quantum sample complexity at least
\Omega\left(\tfrac{1}{\epsilon}\log\tfrac{1}{\delta}\right)
.

Proof:

Since
{\mathscr{C}}
is non-trivial, there exist concepts
c_{1},c_{2}\in{\mathscr{C}}
and inputs
x_{1},x_{2}\in\left\{0,1\right\}^{n}
such that
c_{1}(x_{1})=c_{2}(x_{1})
and
c_{1}(x_{2})\neq c_{2}(x_{2})
. Consider the distribution
D
defined as follows:
D(x_{1})\coloneqq 1-2\epsilon
and
D(x_{2})\coloneqq 2\epsilon
. Let
|\psi_{i}\rangle\coloneqq\sqrt{1-2\epsilon}\,|x_{1},c_{i}(x_{1})\rangle+\sqrt{2\epsilon}\,|x_{2},c_{i}(x_{2})\rangle
for
i\in\left\{1,2\right\}
.

Under the distribution
D
, no hypothesis can simultaneously
\epsilon
-approximate
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
. So, the hypotheses output with probability at least
1-\delta
by an
(\epsilon,\delta)
-PAC quantum learner for
{\mathscr{C}}
, given samples for
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
are different. The learner thus distinguishes the states
|\psi_{1}\rangle^{\otimes t}
and
|\psi_{2}\rangle^{\otimes t}
with success probability at least
1-\delta
, where
t
is its sample complexity.

On the other hand, by Holevo-Helstrom theorem (see, e.g., Ref.
[
32
, Theorem 3.4]
),
|\psi_{1}\rangle^{\otimes t}
and
|\psi_{2}\rangle^{\otimes t}
are distinguishable with probability at most

\tfrac{1}{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{1-\left|\langle\psi_{1}|\psi_{2}\rangle\right|^{2t}}\kern 5.0pt.

So, we have
\langle\psi_{1}|\psi_{2}\rangle^{t}\leq 2\sqrt{\delta(1-\delta)}
. Since
\langle\psi_{1}|\psi_{2}\rangle=1-2\epsilon
, it follows that
t\in\Omega\left(\tfrac{1}{\epsilon}\log\tfrac{1}{\delta}\right)
.

Next, we prove the VC-dependent part of the lower bound.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
n\geq 2
,
d\geq 1
,
N\coloneqq 2^{n}
, and let
{\mathscr{C}}\subseteq\left\{0,1\right\}^{N}
be a concept class with
\VC({\mathscr{C}})
being
d+1
. There is a universal constant
d_{0}
such that if
d\geq d_{0}
,
\epsilon\in(0,1/4)

and
\delta\in(0,1/8)
, every
(\epsilon,\delta)
-PAC quantum learner for
{\mathscr{C}}
has sample
complexity
\Omega(d/\epsilon)
.

Proof:

Suppose there is an
(\epsilon,\delta)
-PAC quantum learner
{\mathscr{A}}
for
{\mathscr{C}}

with quantum sample complexity
t
.
Let
S\coloneqq\{s_{0},\ldots,s_{d}\}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}
be
a maximal set shattered by the concept class
{\mathscr{C}}
. For each
a\in\{0,1\}^{d}
, let
c_{a}\in{\mathscr{C}}
be a concept such that
c_{a}(s_{0})=0
and
c_{a}(s_{i})=a_{i}
for
all
i\in[d]
. Let
D
be a probability distribution over
\{0,1\}^{n}
defined as
D(s_{0})\coloneqq 1-4\epsilon
,
D(s_{i})\coloneqq 4\epsilon/d
for
i\in[d]
, and
D(x)\coloneqq 0
for
x\in\left\{0,1\right\}^{n}\setminus S
. Since
D
is only supported over the set
S
, in the rest of the proof, we write
i
instead of
s_{i}
for the sake of brevity. Hence, we consider
D
as a distribution over
{\mathbb{N}}_{d+1}
and write the PAC quantum example for
c_{a}
according to
D
as

|\psi_{a}\rangle\quad\coloneqq\quad\sum_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}_{d+1}}\sqrt{D(i)}\,|i,a_{i}\rangle\quad=\quad\sqrt{1-4\epsilon}\;|0,0\rangle+\sqrt{\frac{4\epsilon}{d}}\;\sum_{i=1}^{d}|i,a_{i}\rangle\kern 5.0pt.

Let
\rho_{a}
denote
t
copies of the example
|\psi_{a}\rangle\!\langle\psi_{a}|
and let
\rho^{AB_{1}\dotsb B_{t}}
be the following classical-quantum state

\rho^{AB_{1}\dotsb B_{t}}\quad\coloneqq\quad\frac{1}{2^{d}}\sum_{a\in\{0,1\}^{d}}|a\rangle\!\langle a|^{A}\otimes\rho^{B_{1}\dotsb B_{t}}_{a}\quad=\quad\frac{1}{2^{d}}\sum_{a\in\{0,1\}^{d}}|a\rangle\!\langle a|\otimes|\psi_{a}\rangle\!\langle\psi_{a}|^{\otimes t}\kern 5.0pt.

(3.1)

Let
B\coloneqq B_{1}\dotsb B_{t}
. First, we show that
\mi(A:B)_{\rho}\in\Omega(d)
using the hypothesis that
{\mathscr{C}}
, and therefore, its subset
\{c_{a}:a\in\{0,1\}^{d}\}
, is
(\epsilon,\delta)
-PAC quantum learnable using
t
quantum examples. Then, we show that if
t\in\order(d/\epsilon)
, we have
\mi(A:B)_{\rho}\in\order(d)
. This implies that
t\in\Omega(d/\epsilon)
.

We begin with the lower bound on the mutual information.

Lemma 3.3
.

\mi(A:B)\quad\geq\quad(1-\bentropy(3/8))d\,
.

Proof:

There are several ways of showing that
\mi(A:B)_{\rho}\in\Omega(d)
; see the proof of Theorem 16 in Ref.
[
3
]
for one. We give a different argument.

Suppose the learner
{\mathscr{A}}
is given the
t
quantum examples in the
register
B
and produces the (classical) output in register
F
. Let
E
be the a binary random variable indicating whether or not
{\mathscr{A}}
outputs a desirable hypothesis, i.e., a hypothesis
f\in\left\{0,1\right\}^{N}

satisfying
\Pr_{i\sim D}\left[f(i)\neq c_{a}(i)\right]\leq\epsilon
. The event
E=1
occurs with probability at least
1-\delta
. For any
i\in[d]
,

\displaystyle\Pr[F_{i}\neq A_{i}]\quad

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\quad\Pr[E=0]\;\Pr[F_{i}\neq A_{i}\,|\,E=0]+\Pr[E=1]\;\Pr[F_{i}\neq A_{i}\,|\,E=1]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\quad\delta+\Pr[F_{i}\neq A_{i}\,|\,E=1]\kern 5.0pt.

From the PAC learning condition, we have

\frac{4\epsilon}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\Pr[F_{i}\neq A_{i}\,|\,E=1]\quad\leq\quad\epsilon\kern 5.0pt,

whereby

\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\Pr[F_{i}\neq A_{i}]\quad\leq\quad\delta+\frac{1}{4}\quad\leq\quad\frac{3}{8}\kern 5.0pt.

By the Data Processing Inequality and the hardness of the Index function under the uniform distribution over inputs
[
19
, Lemma V.3]
, we get

\mi(A:B)\quad\geq\quad\mi(A:F)\quad\geq\quad(1-\bentropy(3/8))d\kern 5.0pt,

as claimed.

Note that
t=0
is not possible. Assume that
t=\nu d/\epsilon
for some
\nu\in(0,1)
. Otherwise, the bound stated in the theorem holds. We show that there are positive universal constants
\beta,d_{0}
such that if
\nu<\beta
and
d\geq d_{0}
, the mutual information
\mi(A:B)
violates
Lemma

3.3
.

Notice that
\rho^{AB}
and
\rho^{A}
have the same eigenvalues, as
B
is in a pure state for a fixed value
a
of
A
. Hence,
\mi(A:B)_{\rho}=\vnentropy(A)_{\rho}+\vnentropy(B)_{\rho}-\vnentropy(AB)_{\rho}=\vnentropy(B)_{\rho}\,
. Arunachalam and de Wolf
[
3
, proof of Theorem 16]
bound the entropy
\vnentropy(B)_{\rho}
by
t\vnentropy(B_{1})_{\rho}\,
, using subadditivity. Since
\vnentropy(B_{1})_{\rho}
is of the order of
\epsilon\log d
, they get a lower bound that is a factor
\log d
smaller than optimal. Although this bound is tight for small values of
t
, note that the state
\rho^{B}
has rank at most
2^{d}
. Hence, its entropy does not exceed
d
, even as
t
goes to infinity. This hints at the possibility of a bound on entropy of order
\epsilon t
for sufficiently large
t
. This is essentially what we establish, by a direct analysis of the spectrum of
\rho^{B}
.

We extend the distribution
D
to
{\mathbb{N}}_{d+1}^{k}
for
k>1
by defining
D(w)\coloneqq\prod_{i\in[k]}D(w_{i})
, for any
w\in{\mathbb{N}}_{d+1}^{k}\,
. We have

\rho^{B}\quad=\quad\frac{1}{2^{d}}\sum_{a\in\{0,1\}^{d}}|\psi_{a}\rangle\!\langle\psi_{a}|^{\otimes t}\quad=\quad\frac{1}{2^{d}}\sum_{a\in\{0,1\}^{d}}\ \sum_{u,v\in{\mathbb{N}}_{d+1}^{t}}\sqrt{D(u)D(v)}\;|u\rangle\!\langle v|^{I}\otimes|a_{u}\rangle\!\langle a_{v}|^{L}\kern 5.0pt,

where we partition register
B
into registers
I
and
L
which contain the sequences of indices and labels from the
t
samples, respectively.

By considering small
t
(e.g.,
t=1,2
), we see that
\rho^{B}
is diagonalized when we express register
L
in the Hadamard basis. This generalizes to all
t
, and allows us to obtain an explicit spectral decomposition for the state. Recall from
Section

2
that
n_{l,h}
is the number of strings in
[d]^{l}
that have a specific parity signature, say
b
, with Hamming weight
|b|=h
.

Lemma 3.4
.

The state
\rho^{B}
has
\min\left\{d+1,t+1\right\}
non-zero eigenvalues
\lambda_{h}
given by

\displaystyle\lambda_{h}\quad\coloneqq\quad\sum_{l=0}^{t}\binom{t}{l}\left(\frac{2\epsilon}{d}\right)^{l}(1-2\epsilon)^{t-l}\,n_{l,h}

(3.2)

for
h\in{\mathbb{N}}_{d+1}\cap{\mathbb{N}}_{t+1}
. The eigenvalue
\lambda_{h}
has multiplicity
\binom{d}{h}
.

Proof:

Let
\sigma^{B}
be the quantum state after applying Hadamard operator
H^{\otimes t}
on register
L
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\sigma^{B}\quad

\displaystyle\coloneqq

\displaystyle\quad\frac{1}{2^{d}}\sum_{a\in\{0,1\}^{d}}\ \sum_{u,v\in{\mathbb{N}}_{d+1}^{t}}\sqrt{D(u)D(v)}\;|u\rangle\!\langle v|^{I}\otimes H^{\otimes t}|a_{u}\rangle\!\langle a_{v}|^{L}H^{\otimes t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\quad\frac{1}{2^{t}2^{d}}\sum_{u,v\in{\mathbb{N}}_{d+1}^{t}}\sqrt{D(u)D(v)}\;|u\rangle\!\langle v|^{I}\otimes\sum_{a\in\{0,1\}^{d}}\ \sum_{x,y\in\{0,1\}^{t}}(-1)^{x\cdot a_{u}+y\cdot a_{v}}|x\rangle\!\langle y|^{L}\kern 5.0pt.

We have

x\cdot a_{u}\quad=\quad\sum_{i\in[t]\,:\,x_{i}=1}a_{u_{i}}\quad=\quad\sum_{j=1}^{d}\parity(u_{x})_{j}\cdot a_{j}\quad=\quad\parity(u_{x})\cdot a\kern 5.0pt,

where
\parity:{\mathbb{N}}_{d+1}^{t}\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{d}
is the parity signature function defined in
Section

2
. So, for fixed
x,y\in\{0,1\}^{d}
and
u,v\in{\mathbb{N}}_{d+1}^{t}
, we have

\sum_{a\in\{0,1\}^{d}}(-1)^{x\cdot a_{u}+y\cdot a_{v}}\quad=\quad\begin{cases}2^{d}&\text{if}\ \parity(u_{x})=\parity(v_{y})\kern 5.0pt,\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

From this we get

\sigma^{B}\quad=\quad\frac{1}{2^{t}}\sum_{x,y\in\{0,1\}^{t}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u,v\in{\mathbb{N}}_{d+1}^{t}\\
\parity(u_{x})=\parity(v_{y})\end{subarray}}\sqrt{D(u)D(v)}\;|u\rangle\!\langle v|^{I}\otimes|x\rangle\!\langle y|^{L}\kern 5.0pt.

We can identify the eigenvectors of the state
\sigma^{B}
from this expression. We may verify that for each
b\in\{0,1\}^{d}
, the vector

|\phi_{b}\rangle\quad\coloneqq\quad\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{t}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in{\mathbb{N}}_{d+1}^{t}\\
\parity(u_{x})=b\end{subarray}}\sqrt{D(u)}\;|u\rangle|x\rangle

is an eigenvector of
\sigma^{B}
with eigenvalue

\displaystyle\lambda_{b}\quad

\displaystyle\coloneqq

\displaystyle\quad\frac{1}{2^{t}}\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{t}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in{\mathbb{N}}_{d+1}^{t}\\
\parity(u_{x})=b\end{subarray}}D(u)

whenever
\left|b\right|\leq t
, where
\left|b\right|
is the Hamming weight of
b
. The states
|\phi_{b}\rangle
are readily seen to be orthogonal for distinct
b
. Since

\frac{1}{2^{t}}\sum_{b\in\left\{0,1\right\}^{d},\;\left|b\right|\leq t}|\phi_{b}\rangle\!\langle\phi_{b}|\quad=\quad\sigma^{B}\kern 5.0pt,

we have all the non-zero eigenvalues of
\sigma^{B}
.

The eigenvalues do not seem to be easy to analyse, as they involve the combinatorial quantity
n_{l,b}\,
, the number of strings of length
l
over
[d]
with parity signature
b
. Nonetheless, we show that the aggregate expression we get by considering the entropy of
\sigma^{B}
may be analysed using standard tail inequalities from probability theory. We express the eigenvalues in a form that enables such analysis.

Let
\overline{x}
denote the bit-wise complement of
x\in\left\{0,1\right\}^{t}
. Then
D(u)=D(u_{x})\cdot D(u_{\overline{x}})
, and for a fixed
x

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in{\mathbb{N}}_{d+1}^{t}\\
\parity(u_{x})=b\end{subarray}}D(u)\quad=\quad\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in{\mathbb{N}}_{d+1}^{\left|x\right|}\\
\parity(v)=b\end{subarray}}\sum_{w\in{\mathbb{N}}_{d+1}^{t-\left|x\right|}}D(v)\cdot D(w)\quad=\quad\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in{\mathbb{N}}_{d+1}^{\left|x\right|}\\
\parity(v)=b\end{subarray}}D(v)\kern 5.0pt.

Using this, setting
r\coloneqq\left|x\right|
, noting that the parity signature of a string
v\in{\mathbb{N}}_{d+1}^{r}
only depends on its non-zero coordinates, and denoting Hamming weight
\left|v\right|
by
l
, we rewrite
\lambda_{b}
as follows.

\displaystyle\lambda_{b}\quad

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\quad\frac{1}{2^{t}}\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{t}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in{\mathbb{N}}_{d+1}^{|x|}\\
\parity(v)=b\end{subarray}}D(v)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\quad\frac{1}{2^{t}}\sum_{r=0}^{t}\binom{t}{r}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in{\mathbb{N}}_{d+1}^{r}\\
\parity(v)=b\end{subarray}}(1-4\epsilon)^{r-|v|}\left(\frac{4\epsilon}{d}\right)^{|v|}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\quad\frac{1}{2^{t}}\sum_{r=0}^{t}\binom{t}{r}\sum_{l=0}^{r}\binom{r}{l}(1-4\epsilon)^{r-l}\left(\frac{4\epsilon}{d}\right)^{l}n_{l,|b|}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{l=0}^{t}\sum_{r=l}^{t-l}\binom{t}{l}\binom{t-l}{r-l}\frac{1}{2^{t-r}}\left(\frac{1-4\epsilon}{2}\right)^{r-l}\left(\frac{4\epsilon}{2d}\right)^{l}n_{l,|b|}\qquad\left(\text{as }\binom{t}{r}\binom{r}{l}=\binom{t}{l}\binom{t-l}{r-l}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{l=0}^{t}\binom{t}{l}\left(\frac{2\epsilon}{d}\right)^{l}n_{l,|b|}\left(\sum_{r=l}^{t-l}\binom{t-l}{r-l}\frac{1}{2^{t-r}}\left(\frac{1-4\epsilon}{2}\right)^{r-l}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{l=0}^{t}\binom{t}{l}\left(\frac{2\epsilon}{d}\right)^{l}(1-2\epsilon)^{t-l}\,n_{l,|b|}\kern 5.0pt.

Note that the eigenvalue
\lambda_{b}
only depends on the Hamming weight
|b|
. Thus its multiplicity is
\binom{d}{|b|}
and we denote it by
\lambda_{h}
when the string
b
has Hamming weight
h
.

For convenience, we define
\lambda_{h}
as in
Lemma

3.4
even when
h>t
. (Observe that
\lambda_{h}=0
in this case.) Define
\mu_{h}\coloneqq\binom{d}{h}\lambda_{h}\,
, for
h\in{\mathbb{N}}_{d+1}\,
. This is a distribution over
{\mathbb{N}}_{d+1}\,
.

Since the Hadamard operator is unitary, we have
\vnentropy(B)_{\rho}=\vnentropy(B)_{\sigma}
, and therefore

\displaystyle\vnentropy(B)_{\rho}\quad

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{h=0}^{d}\binom{d}{h}\lambda_{h}\log\frac{1}{\lambda_{h}}

(3.3)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{h=0}^{d}\mu_{h}\log\frac{1}{\mu_{h}}+\sum_{h=0}^{d}\binom{d}{h}\lambda_{h}\log\binom{d}{h}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\quad\log(d+1)+\sum_{h=0}^{d}\binom{d}{h}\lambda_{h}\log\binom{d}{h}

(since
\entropy(\mu)\leq\log(d+1)
)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\quad\log(d+1)+\sum_{h=0}^{d}\mu_{h}\log\binom{d}{h}\kern 5.0pt.

Denote the second term in Eq. (
3.3
) by
S_{t,d}
. In the rest of the proof, we bound
S_{t,d}
by interpreting it as an expectation of
\log\binom{d}{h}
with respect to a distribution that is tightly concentrated around its mean.

Lemma 3.5
.

For
\nu\leq\frac{1}{12}\,
, we have
S_{t,d}\;\leq\;d\bentropy(6\nu)+d\exp(-2\nu d)\,
.

Proof:

We define a distribution
{\widetilde{D}}
on
{\mathbb{N}}_{d+1}
motivated by the form of
\lambda_{h}
:
{\widetilde{D}}(0)\coloneqq 1-2\epsilon
, and
{\widetilde{D}}(i)\coloneqq\frac{2\epsilon}{d}
for
i\in[d]
. Let
Y_{t}
be a random string in
{\mathbb{N}}_{d+1}^{t}
each symbol of which is chosen independently according to the distribution
{\widetilde{D}}
. Let
Z_{tj}
be the binary random variable corresponding to the parity of the number of occurrences of the symbol
j\in[d]
in
Y_{t}
. Then
Z_{t}\coloneqq\sum_{j}Z_{tj}
is the random variable corresponding to the Hamming weight of the parity signature of
Y_{t}\,
.

Observe that
\mu_{h}
is the probability that the parity signature of
Y_{t}
has Hamming weight
h
, i.e.,
\mu_{h}=\Pr[Z_{t}=h]
. Also,
|Y_{t}|\geq Z_{t}\,
, as the Hamming weight of the parity signature of
Y_{t}
is at most the number of non-zero symbols in
Y_{t}\,
. Since
|Y_{t}|\sim\mathrm{Binomial}(t,2\epsilon)
, we have
\expct|Y_{t}|=2\epsilon t=2\nu d
. By the multiplicative Chernoff bound in Eq. (
2.1
), we have

\Pr[\,|Y_{t}|\;\geq\;6\nu d\,]\quad\leq\quad\exp\left(-2\nu d\right)\kern 5.0pt.

We use this to bound
S_{t,d}\,
. Since
6\nu d\leq d/2
, we have

\displaystyle S_{t,d}\quad

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{h=0}^{d}\mu_{h}\log\binom{d}{h}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{h\,<\,6\nu d}\mu_{h}\log\binom{d}{h}+\sum_{h\,\geq\,6\nu d}\mu_{h}\log\binom{d}{h}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\quad d\bentropy(6\nu)+d\Pr[Z_{t}\;\geq\;6\nu d\,]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\quad d\bentropy(6\nu)+d\Pr[\,|Y_{t}|\;\geq\;6\nu d\,]\qquad(\text{since }Z_{t}\leq\left|Y_{t}\right|)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\quad d\bentropy(6\nu)+d\exp(-2\nu d)\kern 5.0pt,

as claimed.

Combining Eq. (
3.3
) and
Lemma

3.5
, we get

\displaystyle\mi(A:B)_{\rho}\quad=\quad\vnentropy(B)_{\rho}\quad

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\quad\log(d+1)+d\bentropy(6\nu)+d\exp(-2\nu d)

for
\nu\leq\frac{1}{12}\,
. This contradicts
Lemma

3.3
for small enough
\nu
and large enough
d
.

3.2
Sample complexity of agnostic learning

As in the case of the PAC model, the lower bound for the sample complexity of agnostic quantum learning in Eq. (
1.2
) has two parts. For completeness, we provide the proof of the VC-independent part due to Arunachalam and de Wolf
[
3
]
.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
{\mathscr{C}}
be a non-trivial concept class. For every
\delta\in(0,1/2)
,
\epsilon\in(0,1/4)
, an
(\epsilon,\delta)
-agnostic quantum learner for
{\mathscr{C}}
has quantum sample complexity at least
\Omega(\tfrac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}\log\tfrac{1}{\delta})
.

Proof:

Since
{\mathscr{C}}
is non-trivial, there exists two distinct concepts
c_{1},c_{2}\in{\mathscr{C}}
and an input

such that
c_{1}(x)\neq c_{2}(x)
. Define distributions
D_{+}
and
D_{-}
as follows.

D_{\pm}(x,c_{1}(x))\quad\coloneqq\quad\frac{1\pm 2\epsilon}{2}\qquad\text{and}\qquad D_{\pm}(x,c_{2}(x))\quad\coloneqq\quad\frac{1\mp 2\epsilon}{2}\kern 5.0pt.

Let
|\psi_{\pm}\rangle\coloneqq\sqrt{\left(1\pm 2\epsilon\right)/2}\;|x,c_{1}(x)\rangle+\sqrt{\left(1\mp 2\epsilon\right)/2}\;|x,c_{2}(x)\rangle
be the outputs of agnostic quantum oracles
\QAEX(D_{\pm})
.

Under the above distributions,
\opt_{D_{\pm}}({\mathscr{C}})=\left(1-2\epsilon\right)/2
. Any hypothesis that has error at most
1/2
with respect to
D_{+}
, i.e., error at most
\epsilon
larger than
\opt_{D_{+}}({\mathscr{C}})
agrees with
c_{1}
at
x
, and any hypothesis that has error at most
1/2
with respect to
D_{-}
agrees with
c_{2}
at
x
.
So, an
(\epsilon,\delta)
-agnostic quantum learner for
{\mathscr{C}}
with sample complexity

can be used to distinguish
|\psi_{+}\rangle^{\otimes t}
from

with probability at least

. As in Lemma
3.1

\langle\psi_{+}|\psi_{-}\rangle^{t}\leq 2\sqrt{\delta(1-\delta)}
whereas

.

Next, we prove the VC-dependent part of the lower bound, using the same ideas as in the proof of Theorem
3.2
— via information theory, spectral analysis, and concentration of measure. We refer the reader to that proof for the intuition behind the proof below, as also for some details.

Theorem 3.7

Let

for

using

t

by
\rho_{a}

\rho^{AB_{1}\dotsb B_{t}}\quad\coloneqq\quad\frac{1}{2^{d}}\sum_{a\in\{0,1\}^{d}}|a\rangle\!\langle a|^{A}\otimes\rho^{B_{1}\dotsb B_{t}}_{a}\quad=\quad\frac{1}{2^{d}}\sum_{a\in\{0,1\}^{d}}|a\rangle\!\langle a|\otimes|\psi_{a}\rangle\!\langle\psi_{a}|^{\otimes t}\kern 5.0pt.

(3.4)

Suppose the learner
{\mathscr{A}}
is given the

\rho_{a}
in register

By construction,

with respect to the distribution

and
\err_{D_{a}}\!(c_{a})=(1-4\epsilon)/2

\err_{D_{a}}\!(H_{a})\quad=\quad\err_{D_{a}}\!(c_{a})+\frac{4\epsilon}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\Pr[H_{a}(i)\neq c_{a}(i)]\kern 5.0pt.

By the agnostic learning criterion the additional error over
\err_{D_{a}}\!(c_{a})
is at most
\epsilon
. Therefore, we have

\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\Pr[H_{a}(i)\neq c_{a}(i)]\quad\leq\quad\frac{1}{4}\kern 5.0pt.

Through the same reasoning as in
Lemma

3.3
, we get

\displaystyle\mi(A:B)_{\rho}\quad\geq\quad\left(1-\bentropy(3/8)\right)d\kern 5.0pt.

(3.5)

Let
\alpha\coloneqq\frac{1-\sqrt{1-16\epsilon^{2}}}{2}
.
We show that when
t=\nu d/2\alpha
with
\nu
chosen to be a sufficiently small positive constant, the mutual information

violates the inequality (
3.5
). Therefore, we get

.

For
u\in[d]^{t}
and
l\in\left\{0,1\right\}^{t}
, denote
\prod_{j=1}^{t}D_{a}(u_{j},l_{j})
by
D_{a}(u,l)
. We separate the indices and the corresponding bits in register
B
into subregisters
I
and
L
, respectively. We then have

\displaystyle\rho^{B}\quad

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\quad\frac{1}{2^{d}}\sum_{a\in\{0,1\}^{d}}\ \sum_{u,v\in[d]^{t}}\sum_{l,k\in\{0,1\}^{t}}\sqrt{D_{a}(u,l)D_{a}(v,k)}\,|u\rangle\!\langle v|^{I}\otimes|l\rangle\!\langle k|^{L}\kern 5.0pt.

(3.6)

We first derive the spectrum of
\rho^{B}
.

Lemma 3.8
.

The state
\rho^{B}
has
\min\left\{d+1,t+1\right\}

\lambda_{h}
given by

\displaystyle\lambda_{h}\quad

\displaystyle\coloneqq\quad\sum_{r=0}^{t}\binom{t}{r}\frac{n_{r,h}}{d^{r}}\,\alpha^{r}(1-\alpha)^{t-r}

for

. The eigenvalue

has multiplicity

.

Proof:

Define
\sigma^{B}
to be the state obtained by applying the
t

in
\rho^{B}
. We have

\displaystyle\sigma^{B}\quad

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\quad\frac{1}{2^{d}2^{t}}\sum_{u,v\in[d]^{t}}|u\rangle\!\langle v|^{I}\otimes\sum_{a\in\{0,1\}^{d}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l,k\in\{0,1\}^{t}\\
x,y\in\{0,1\}^{t}\end{subarray}}(-1)^{x\cdot l+y\cdot k}\sqrt{D_{a}(u,l)D_{a}(v,k)}\,|x\rangle\!\langle y|^{L}\kern 5.0pt.

We simplify this expression as follows. Consider fixed
a,u,v,x,y
as in the expression. Define

\beta_{x}\quad\coloneqq\quad\frac{1}{(2d)^{t/2}}\left(\sqrt{1+4\epsilon}+\sqrt{1-4\epsilon}\,\right)^{t-\left|x\right|}\left(\sqrt{1+4\epsilon}-\sqrt{1-4\epsilon}\,\right)^{\left|x\right|}\kern 5.0pt,

and note that

\beta_{x}^{2}\quad=\quad\left(\frac{2}{d}\right)^{t}\alpha^{\left|x\right|}(1-\alpha)^{t-\left|x\right|}\kern 5.0pt.

We have

\displaystyle\sum_{l\in\left\{0,1\right\}^{t}}(-1)^{x\cdot l}\sqrt{D_{a}(u,l)}\quad

\displaystyle=\quad\prod_{j=1}^{t}\sum_{l_{j}\in\left\{0,1\right\}}(-1)^{x_{j}l_{j}}\sqrt{D_{a}(u_{j},l_{j})}

\displaystyle=\quad\prod_{j=1}^{t}\left(\sqrt{D_{a}(u_{j},0)}+(-1)^{x_{j}}\sqrt{D_{a}(u_{j},1)}\right)

\displaystyle=\quad\frac{1}{(2d)^{t/2}}\prod_{j=1}^{t}(-1)^{a_{u_{j}}x_{j}}\left(\sqrt{1+4\epsilon}+(-1)^{x_{j}}\sqrt{1-4\epsilon}\,\right)

\displaystyle=\quad(-1)^{a_{u}\cdot x}\beta_{x}\kern 5.0pt.

\displaystyle\sum_{a\in\{0,1\}^{d}}\sum_{l,k\in\{0,1\}^{t}}(-1)^{x\cdot l+y\cdot k}\sqrt{D_{a}(u,l)D_{a}(v,k)}\quad

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{a\in\{0,1\}^{d}}(-1)^{a_{u}\cdot x+a_{v}\cdot y}\beta_{x}\beta_{y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\quad\begin{cases}2^{d}\beta_{x}\beta_{y}&\text{if }\parity(u_{x})=\parity(v_{y})\\
0&\text{otherwise, }\end{cases}

and

\displaystyle\sigma^{B}\quad

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\quad\frac{1}{2^{t}}\sum_{x,y\in\{0,1\}^{t}}\beta_{x}\beta_{y}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u,v\in[d]^{t}\\
\parity(u_{x})=\parity(v_{y})\end{subarray}}|u\rangle\!\langle v|^{I}\otimes|x\rangle\!\langle y|^{L}\kern 5.0pt.

We may verify that for each

with

, the vector

|\phi_{b}\rangle\quad\coloneqq\quad\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{t}}\beta_{x}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in[d]^{t}\\

is an eigenvector of

with corresponding eigenvalue

\displaystyle\lambda_{b}\quad

\displaystyle\coloneqq

\displaystyle\quad\frac{1}{2^{t}}\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{t}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in[d]^{t}\\

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\quad\frac{1}{2^{t}}\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{t}}d^{t-|x|}\;\beta_{x}^{2}\;n_{|x|,|b|}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{t}}\frac{n_{|x|,|b|}}{d^{|x|}}\;\alpha^{|x|}(1-\alpha)^{t-|x|}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{r=0}^{t}\binom{t}{r}\frac{n_{r,|b|}}{d^{r}}\;\alpha^{r}(1-\alpha)^{t-r}\kern 5.0pt.

\lambda_{b}
only depends on the Hamming weight

. Thus its multiplicity is
\binom{d}{h}
, and we write
\lambda_{h}
instead of

for any string

with Hamming weight

. Since

\frac{1}{2^{t}}\sum_{b\in\left\{0,1\right\}^{d},\;\left|b\right|\leq t}|\phi_{b}\rangle\!\langle\phi_{b}|\quad=\quad\sigma^{B}\kern 5.0pt,

we have all the non-zero eigenvalues of

, and hence of
\rho^{B}
.

For convenience, we define
\lambda_{h}
as in

3.8
even when

. (Observe that
\lambda_{h}=0
in this case.)
We may now bound the entropy of

as

\displaystyle\vnentropy(B)_{\rho}\quad

\displaystyle\leq\quad\log(d+1)+\sum_{h=0}^{d}\binom{d}{h}\lambda_{h}\log\binom{d}{h}

\displaystyle=\quad\log(d+1)+\sum_{r=0}^{t}\binom{t}{r}\alpha^{r}(1-\alpha)^{t-r}\sum_{h=0}^{d}\binom{d}{h}\frac{n_{r,h}}{d^{r}}\log\binom{d}{h}\kern 5.0pt.

, we have

\sum_{h=0}^{d}\binom{d}{h}\frac{n_{r,h}}{d^{r}}\log\binom{d}{h}\quad\leq\quad\log\binom{d}{r}\kern 5.0pt.

Since the binomial distribution

is concentrated around its mean

, by the multiplicative Chernoff bound (Eq. (

)) we have

\displaystyle\sum_{r=\frac{3\alpha t}{2}}^{t}\binom{t}{r}\alpha^{r}(1-\alpha)^{t-r}\quad

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\quad\exp\left(-\,\frac{\alpha t}{10}\right)\kern 5.0pt.

We take
\nu<1/2
so that

. To bound the second term in Eq. (

), we partition the sum over

and

. Using the bounds derived above, we have

\displaystyle\vnentropy(B)_{\rho}\quad

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\quad\log(d+1)+\log\binom{d}{\frac{3\alpha t}{2}}+d\;\exp\left(-\,\frac{\alpha t}{10}\right)\kern 5.0pt.

Let

.
If the last term on the right hand side above is at least

. For any fixed positive constant

, this does not hold for sufficiently large

. So for sufficiently large

, the last term is at most

\displaystyle\vnentropy(B)_{\rho}\quad

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\quad\log(d+1)+\log\binom{d}{\frac{3\alpha t}{2}}+1

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\quad\log(d+1)+d\bentropy\!\left(\frac{3\nu}{4}\right)+1\kern 5.0pt.

This violates the bound on mutual information in Eq. (
3.5
) for sufficiently small positive

, and large enough

.

4
Quantum Coupon Collection

In this section, we study the Quantum Coupon Collector problem, in particular the sample complexity of both its standard and approximation versions.

4.1
Technical preparations

We begin by introducing the notation and technical background on which we build. The spectrum of a quantum state
\rho^{B}
defined formally below plays an important role in the sequel. Most of the section is devoted to presenting properties of the spectrum that may be inferred directly from prior work.

We use the following notation in the remaining subsections. Let

be positive integers such that

\rho_{S}\coloneqq(|\psi_{S}\rangle\!\langle\psi_{S}|)^{\otimes t}

(cf. Eq. (
2.5

\rho^{AB_{1}\dotsb B_{t}}
be the following classical-quantum state

\rho^{AB_{1}\dotsb B_{t}}\quad\coloneqq\quad\frac{1}{\binom{n}{k}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq[n]\\
\left|S\right|=k\end{subarray}}|S\rangle\!\langle S|^{A}\otimes\rho_{S}^{B_{1}\dotsb B_{t}}\quad=\quad\frac{1}{\binom{n}{k}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq[n]\\
\left|S\right|=k\end{subarray}}|S\rangle\!\langle S|\otimes(|\psi_{S}\rangle\!\langle\psi_{S}|)^{\otimes t}\kern 5.0pt.

(4.1)

Let
B\coloneqq B_{1}\dotsb B_{t}\,
, with each subregister

holding one quantum sample. The learning algorithms we consider are given the
t

|\psi_{S}\rangle

The following observation allows us to focus on a smaller range of parameters when analysing the sample complexity of the learning algorithms.

Lemma 4.1
.

Suppose that any learning algorithm with probability of error at most
\delta\in[0,1)
for the Quantum Coupon Collector problem with parameters

(with
1<k<n_{0}

Since any algorithm for the problem (or its approximation version) with parameters
k,n,\delta
also solves the problem with parameters

We write the state
\rho^{B}
as
\rho_{t}^{B}
(with subscript
t
) when we wish to make its dependence on
t

m\coloneqq n-k

\rho_{t}^{B}

\rho_{t}^{B}\quad=\quad\frac{1}{\binom{n}{m}}\sum_{S\in[n],|S|=k}\left(|\psi_{S}\rangle\!\langle\psi_{S}|\right)^{\otimes t}\kern 5.0pt.

C_{t}\quad\coloneqq\quad\frac{1}{{\binom{n}{m}}^{1/2}}\sum_{S\in[n],|S|=k}|S\rangle\left(\langle\psi_{S}|\right)^{\otimes t}\kern 5.0pt.

\displaystyle C_{t}^{*}C_{t}\quad

\displaystyle C_{t}C_{t}^{*}\quad

\displaystyle=\quad\frac{1}{\binom{n}{m}}\sum_{S,S^{\prime}\in[n],|S|=|S^{\prime}|=k}\langle\psi_{S^{\prime}}|\psi_{S}\rangle^{t}\;|S\rangle\!\langle S^{\prime}|\kern 5.0pt.

Let

The following may be inferred directly from the properties of the Johnson association scheme.

Lemma 4.2

Corollary 4.3

For any

For
j=0,1,\dotsc,m
, define

\displaystyle p_{j,-1}\quad

\displaystyle\coloneqq\quad\frac{j(k-j+1)(m-j+1)}{(n-2j+1)(n-2j+2)k}

\displaystyle p_{j,0}\quad

\displaystyle\coloneqq\quad\frac{k}{n}+\frac{j(n-j+1)(k-m)^{2}}{nk(n-2j)(n-2j+2)}

\displaystyle p_{j,+1}\quad

\displaystyle\coloneqq\quad\frac{(k-j)(n-j+1)(m-j)}{(n-2j)(n-2j+1)k}\kern 5.0pt.

Further, define
p_{-1,i}\coloneqq 0
and
p_{m+1,i}\coloneqq 0
for
i\in\left\{-1,0,+1\right\}
, and similarly
E_{-1}\coloneqq 0
and
E_{m+1}\coloneqq 0
. We use the following properties proven in Ref.
[
1
]
.

Lemma 4.4

(Lemma 7 and Corollary 8 in Ref.
[
1
]
)
.

For each
j\in{\mathbb{N}}_{m+1}
, we have

E_{j}\circledcirc D\quad=\quad p_{j+1,-1}E_{j+1}+p_{j,0}E_{j}+p_{j-1,+1}E_{j-1}\kern 5.0pt,

and the numbers
p_{j,0},p_{j,-1},p_{j,+1}
are non-negative and satisfy
p_{j,0}+p_{j,-1}+p_{j,+1}=1
.

This property helps us derive a recurrence for the eigenvalues
(\lambda_{s,t})
. For convenience, define
\lambda_{-1,t}
and
\lambda_{m+1,t}
to be
0
for all
t\geq 0
.

Lemma 4.5
.

We have
\lambda_{0,0}=1
, and
\lambda_{s,0}=0
for
s\in[m]
. For
t\geq 1
, for all
s\in{\mathbb{N}}_{m+1}
,

\lambda_{s,t}\quad=\quad p_{s,0}\lambda_{s,t-1}+p_{s,-1}\lambda_{s-1,t-1}+p_{s,+1}\lambda_{s+1,t-1}\kern 5.0pt.

Proof:

We prove this by induction. Recall that
M_{t}=\sum_{s=0}^{m}\lambda_{s,t}E_{s}
for all
t\geq 0
, and
M_{0}={\binom{n}{m}}^{-1}{\mathbb{J}}=E_{0}
. So we get the claimed values of
\lambda_{s,0}
for all
s
.

For
t\geq 0
we have

\displaystyle M_{t+1}\quad=\quad M_{t}\circledcirc D\quad

\displaystyle=\quad\sum_{s=0}^{m}\lambda_{s,t}E_{s}\circledcirc D

\displaystyle=\quad\sum_{s=0}^{m}\lambda_{s,t}\left(p_{s+1,-1}E_{s+1}+p_{s,0}E_{s}+p_{s-1,+1}E_{s-1}\right)

\displaystyle=\quad\sum_{s=0}^{m}\left(p_{s,0}\lambda_{s,t}+p_{s,-1}\lambda_{s-1,t}+p_{s,+1}\lambda_{s+1,t}\right)E_{s}

\displaystyle=\quad\sum_{s=0}^{m}\lambda_{s,t+1}E_{s}\kern 5.0pt.

The recurrence follows. (Since
p_{0,-1}=p_{m,+1}=0
, we do not have to consider the boundary points
0
and
m
separately.)

4.2
Properties of the spectrum

In this section, we develop new properties of the spectrum of the quantum state
\rho_{t}^{B}
defined in Eq. (
4.1
) in
Section

4.1
.

In our analysis, the eigenvalues
\lambda_{s,t}
always occur in multiples of
l_{s}\,
. We derive a recurrence for
l_{s}\lambda_{s,t}
to analyse the quantities we encounter. For convenience, define
l_{-1}\coloneqq 1
and
l_{m+1}\coloneqq\binom{n}{m+1}-\binom{n}{m}
.

Lemma 4.6
.

We have
l_{0}\lambda_{0,0}=1
, and
l_{s}\lambda_{s,0}=0
for
s\in[m]
. For
t\geq 1
, for all
s\in{\mathbb{N}}_{m+1}
,

l_{s}\lambda_{s,t}\quad=\quad p_{s,0}\big(l_{s}\lambda_{s,t-1}\big)+p_{s-1,+1}\big(l_{s-1}\lambda_{s-1,t-1}\big)+p_{s+1,-1}\big(l_{s+1}\lambda_{s+1,t-1}\big)\kern 5.0pt.

Proof:

The values for
t=0
are straightforward to verify. Multiplying the recurrence relation in
Lemma

4.5
by
l_{s}
on both sides, we get

l_{s}\lambda_{s,t}\quad=\quad p_{s,0}\big(l_{s}\lambda_{s,t-1}\big)+p_{s,-1}\frac{l_{s}}{l_{s-1}}\big(l_{s-1}\lambda_{s-1,t-1}\big)+p_{s,+1}\frac{l_{s}}{l_{s+1}}\big(l_{s+1}\lambda_{s+1,t-1}\big)\kern 5.0pt.

We may verify by direct calculation that

\displaystyle p_{s-1,+1}

\displaystyle\quad=\quad p_{s,-1}\frac{l_{s}}{l_{s-1}}\kern 5.0pt,\qquad\text{and}

\displaystyle p_{s+1,-1}

\displaystyle\quad=\quad p_{s,+1}\frac{l_{s}}{l_{s+1}}\kern 5.0pt,

which gives us the recurrence relation.

Due to the recurrence for
l_{s}\lambda_{s,t}
and the property that
p_{s,0}+p_{s,-1}+p_{s+1}=1
, we may interpret
l_{s}\lambda_{s,t}
as a probability arising from a suitably defined random walk. Define random variables
(W_{t}:t\geq 0)
inductively as follows. Let
W_{0}\coloneqq 0
, and
W_{t+1}
be given via the transition probabilities below. For
t\geq 0
and
s\in{\mathbb{N}}_{m+1}
,

\displaystyle\Pr[W_{t+1}=a\,|\,W_{t}=s]\quad=\quad\begin{cases}p_{s,0}&\text{if }a=s\\
p_{s,-1}&\text{if }a=s-1\\
p_{s,+1}&\text{if }a=s+1\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

This defines a random walk
W
on the points
{\mathbb{N}}_{m+1}
. (Again, since
p_{0,-1}=p_{m,+1}=0
, we do not have to consider the transitions at the boundary points
0
and
m
separately.) Furthermore, using the recurrence in
Lemma

4.6
we may verify that
l_{s}\lambda_{s,t}=\Pr[W_{t}=s]
.

A similar random walk was also used in Ref.
[
1
]
, but we do not know of a direct relation between the two. In particular, we are not sure if there is an intrinsic reason why the recurrences for the two quantities
l_{s}\lambda_{s,t}
and
\lambda_{s,t}
involve the same set of coefficients. Due to this connection we may also relate
\lambda_{s,t}
to the random walk
(W_{t})
by changing the initial condition
W_{0}=0
. We may verify that
\lambda_{s,t}=\Pr[W_{t}=0\,|\,W_{0}=s]
. So,
\lambda_{s,t}
is the probability that the walk arrives at
0
in
t
steps when we start at
s
, whereas
l_{s}\lambda_{s,t}
is the probability for the reverse traversal (the probability that it arrives at
s
in
t
steps when we start at
0
).

The relationship between the quantity
l_{s}\lambda_{s,t}
and the random walk
W
helps us derive several useful bounds that we present next. We say that a random walk
U\coloneqq(U_{t}:t\geq 0)
on
{\mathbb{Z}}

dominates
another random walk
U^{\prime}\coloneqq(U^{\prime}_{t}:t\geq 0)
on
{\mathbb{Z}}
if for all
t\geq 0
and
i\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, we have
\Pr[U_{t}\geq i]\geq\Pr[U^{\prime}_{t}\geq i]
. I.e., for any integer
i
, the first walk has at least as high a probability of being to right of
i
as the second walk. We start with some general conditions under which one random walk on the line dominates another. In their simplest form, the conditions state that a nearest-neighbour walk on the integers that has a higher probability of moving to the right and a lower probability of moving to the left than another similar walk dominates the other walk.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
U\coloneqq(U_{t}:t\geq 0)
and
U^{\prime}\coloneqq(U^{\prime}_{t}:t\geq 0)
be two possibly time-dependent random walks on the integers that start at
0
, and move by at most
1
in either direction in one time step. For
i,j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, let
q_{t,i,j}\coloneqq\Pr[U_{t}=j\,|\,U_{t-1}=i]
, and
q^{\prime}_{t,i,j}
be the analogous transition probability for
(U^{\prime}_{t})
.
Suppose that for all
t\geq 0
, for all
i\in{\mathbb{Z}}
reachable by both walks at time
t
, we have

q_{t,i,i+1}\quad\geq\quad q^{\prime}_{t,i,i+1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad q_{t,i,i-1}\quad\leq\quad q^{\prime}_{t,i,i-1}\kern 5.0pt;

and for all
i
such that
i
is reachable by
U
and
i-1
by
U^{\prime}
at time
t
, we have

q_{t,i,i+1}+q_{t,i,i}\quad\geq\quad q^{\prime}_{t,i-1,i}\kern 5.0pt.

Then
U
dominates
U^{\prime}
, i.e., for all
t\geq 0
and
i\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, we have
\Pr[U_{t}\geq i]\geq\Pr[U^{\prime}_{t}\geq i]
. Moreover,
\expct[U_{t}]\geq\expct[U^{\prime}_{t}]
.

We defer the proof of this lemma to
Appendix

B
.

The transition probabilities of the walk
W
are quite complicated. However, they may be approximated by simpler quantities, and this leads us to walks resembling the classical coupon collection process. For a positive integer
n^{\prime}\geq m
consider a set of
n^{\prime}
coupons out of which
m
coupons are “marked”. As in the classical coupon collector problem, suppose we start with no coupons, and at each step, pick a coupon uniformly at random out of the
n^{\prime}
coupons. Let
{\widetilde{W}}(n^{\prime})\coloneqq\Big({\widetilde{W}}_{t}(n^{\prime}):t\geq 0\Big)
be the random walk on
{\mathbb{N}}_{m+1}
where
{\widetilde{W}}_{t}(n^{\prime})
is the number of distinct marked coupons coupons collected within
t
steps. In more detail, the walk is defined by the following initial condition and transition probabilities, where
t\geq 0
, and
s\in{\mathbb{N}}_{m+1}
.

\begin{split}{\widetilde{W}}_{0}(n^{\prime})\quad&\coloneqq\quad 0\kern 5.0pt,\qquad\text{and}\\
\Pr\Big[{\widetilde{W}}_{t+1}(n^{\prime})=a\;\Big|\;{\widetilde{W}}_{t}(n^{\prime})=s\Big]\quad&\coloneqq\quad\begin{cases}1-\frac{m-s}{n^{\prime}}&\text{if }a=s\\
\frac{m-s}{n^{\prime}}&\text{if }a=s+1\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}\end{split}

(4.2)

We use the coupon-collector-like random walk
W(n^{\prime})
for deriving the bounds in the next three lemmas. We would like to obtain the tightest possible results for the Quantum Coupon Collector problem, i.e., those matching the performance of the known algorithms. For this, we require bounds on the spectral quantities in the lemmas for values of the parameter
m
ranging from
1
up to
\Theta(n)
. For technical reasons that become apparent in the proofs, we are only able to obtain bounds for
m
up to
\Theta(n/\ln n)
in some of the lemmas. Nevertheless, this suffices to characterise the leading term of the sample complexity tightly.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
t\coloneqq k\ln m+cn
for some
c\in{\mathbb{R}}
, and suppose
m\ln m\leq\left|c\right|n/10
, and
1\leq m\leq n/40
. Then
\expct[W_{t}]\leq m-\min\left\{\e^{-2c},\e^{-c/3}\right\}
.

Proof:

Let
W^{\prime}
be the random walk given by
W^{\prime}\coloneqq{\widetilde{W}}(n-5m)
. Then, by
Lemma

B.1
, the walk
W^{\prime}
is an “optimistic” approximation of
W
, i.e., it dominates
W
, and
\expct[W^{\prime}_{t}]\geq\expct[W_{t}]
. Further, by
Lemma

A.1
,

\expct[W^{\prime}_{t}]\quad\leq\quad m-m\exp\!\left(-\,\frac{t}{n-5m-1}\right)\kern 5.0pt.

We show in
Lemma

C.2
that

\displaystyle\frac{t}{n-5m-1}\quad

\displaystyle\leq\quad\ln m+\max\left\{2c,c/3\right\}\kern 5.0pt.

Using this, we get

\displaystyle\expct[W_{t}]\quad

\displaystyle\leq\quad m-m\exp\!\left(-\,\frac{t}{n-5m-1}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\quad m-m\exp\left(-\ln m-\max\left\{2c,c/3\right\}\right)

\displaystyle=\quad m-\min\left\{\e^{-2c},\e^{-c/3}\right\}\kern 5.0pt,

as claimed.

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
t\coloneqq cn
for some
c\geq 0
, and
2\leq m\leq n/10
. Then

\expct[W_{t}]\quad\geq\quad m\left(1-\e^{-c}-\frac{cm}{n}\right)\kern 5.0pt.

Proof:

Consider the random walk
W^{\prime\prime}\coloneqq{\widetilde{W}}(n)
on
{\mathbb{N}}_{m+1}
, and an independent random walk
V^{\prime\prime}
on the non-negative integers defined as follows. Let
V^{\prime\prime}_{t}\coloneqq 0
, and for
t\geq 0
and
r\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\Pr[\,V^{\prime\prime}_{t+1}=a\,|\,V^{\prime\prime}_{t}=r]\quad

\displaystyle\coloneqq\quad\begin{cases}\frac{m^{2}}{n^{2}}&\text{if }a=r+1\\
1-\frac{m^{2}}{n^{2}}&\text{if }a=r\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

The random process
(W^{\prime\prime}_{t}-V^{\prime\prime}_{t})
may be viewed as a “pessimistic” version of
(W_{t})
; indeed, as we show in
Lemma

B.2
, the walk
(W_{t})
dominates
(W^{\prime\prime}_{t}-V^{\prime\prime}_{t})
. So,

\expct[W_{t}]\quad\geq\quad\expct[W^{\prime\prime}_{t}-V^{\prime\prime}_{t}]\quad=\quad\expct[W^{\prime\prime}_{t}]-\expct[V^{\prime\prime}_{t}]\quad=\quad\expct[W^{\prime\prime}_{t}]-\frac{m^{2}}{n^{2}}t\kern 5.0pt.

From
Lemma

A.1
,

\expct[W^{\prime\prime}_{t}]\quad\geq\quad m\left(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{t}{n}}\right)\quad=\quad m(1-\mathrm{e}^{-c})\kern 5.0pt.

Thus,

\expct[W_{t}]\quad\geq\quad m\left(1-\e^{-c}\right)-\frac{cm^{2}}{n}\kern 5.0pt,

as claimed.

Lemma 4.10
.

Let
t\coloneqq k\ln m+cn
for some
c\geq 0
,
m\ln m\leq cn/10
, and
1\leq m\leq n/40
. Then we have

\left(1-\frac{tm^{2}}{n^{2}}\right)\left(1-\e^{-9c/10}\right)\quad\leq\quad l_{m}\lambda_{m,t}\quad\leq\quad 1-\frac{\e^{-2c}}{2}\kern 5.0pt,

where the lower bound additionally requires
m\geq 2
.

Proof:

We use the random walks
W^{\prime}
,
W^{\prime\prime}
, and
V^{\prime\prime}
as defined in the proofs of
Lemmas

4.8
and
4.9
.

Recall that
l_{m}\lambda_{m,t}=\Pr[W_{t}=m]
; the random variables
W_{t},W^{\prime}_{t}
, and
W^{\prime\prime}_{t}-V^{\prime\prime}_{t}
are all bounded by
m
; by
Lemma

B.1
,
(W^{\prime}_{t})
dominates
(W_{t})
; and by
Lemma

B.2
, when in addition,
m\geq 2
, the walk
(W_{t})
dominates
(W^{\prime\prime}_{t}-V^{\prime\prime}_{t})
. So we have

\Pr[W^{\prime\prime}_{t}-V^{\prime\prime}_{t}=m]\quad\leq\quad l_{m}\lambda_{m,t}\quad\leq\quad\Pr[W^{\prime}_{t}=m]\kern 5.0pt,

where the lower bound additionally requires
m\geq 2
.

For the upper bound, we have by
Lemma

A.1

\displaystyle\Pr[W^{\prime}_{t}=m]\quad

\displaystyle\leq\quad 1-m\exp\!\left(-\,\frac{t}{n-5m-1}\right)+\frac{m^{2}}{2}\exp\!\left(-\,\frac{2t}{n-5m}\right)\kern 5.0pt.

We bound the second term on the right hand side using
Lemma

C.2
, and the third term by substituting the value of
t
to get

\Pr[W^{\prime}_{t}=m]\quad\leq\quad 1-\e^{-2c}+\frac{m^{2}}{2}\e^{-2\ln m-2c}\quad\leq\quad 1-\frac{\e^{-2c}}{2}\kern 5.0pt.

For the lower bound in the lemma, we have by
Lemma

A.1

\displaystyle\Pr[W^{\prime\prime}_{t}-V^{\prime\prime}_{t}=m]\quad

\displaystyle=\quad\Pr[V^{\prime\prime}_{t}=0]\cdot\Pr[W^{\prime\prime}_{t}=m]

\displaystyle\geq\quad\left(1-\frac{tm^{2}}{n^{2}}\right)\left(1-m\e^{-t/n}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\quad\left(1-\frac{tm^{2}}{n^{2}}\right)\left(1-\e^{-9c/10}\right)\kern 5.0pt,

since
t=(n-m)\ln m+cn\geq n\ln m-cn/10+cn
.

4.3
Failure of the standard argument

In this section, we show that it is not possible to derive optimal lower bound for Quantum Coupon Collection through the standard information-theoretic argument.

Suppose that with probability
1-\delta
, for some
\delta\in(0,1/4]
, a learning algorithm
{\mathscr{A}}
identifies the set
S
in register
A
, given the
t
copies of the corresponding quantum sample
|\psi_{S}\rangle
in register
B
. Note that this is equivalent to the assumption that
{\mathscr{A}}
solves the Quantum Coupon Collector problem with average error
\delta
with respect to the uniform distribution over subsets of size
k
. The algorithm outputs its guess for the set
S
in register
F
. By the Data Processing Inequality,
\mi(A:F)\leq\mi(A:B)_{\rho}
. The standard argument gives a lower bound (say,
\beta
) for
\mi(A:F)
based on the algorithm
{\mathscr{A}}
, and aims to show that if the number of samples
t
is
\order(k\log\min\left\{m+1,k\right\})
, then
\mi(A:B)_{\rho}
is strictly smaller than
\beta
. This would imply lower bound in Eq. (
1.3
).

We exhibit adversarial algorithms such that
\mi(A:F)
is a constant factor smaller than
\mi(A:B)
even when
t\in\Theta(n)
. So it is not possible to show that
t
is asymptotically larger than
\Omega(n)
using such an argument. Note that
\mi(A:B)_{\rho}=\vnentropy(B)_{\rho}
. Since the optimal sample complexity is
\Theta(k\log\min\left\{m+1,k\right\})
, we see that whenever
\min\left\{m,k\right\}\in\upomega(1)
, and
t\in\Theta(n)
, the uniform ensemble over

\left(|\psi_{S}\rangle^{\otimes t}:S\subseteq[n],~\left|S\right|=k\right)

has high von Neumann entropy, but there is no measurement which correctly identifies the states with constant probability of success.

We start by describing the aforementioned algorithms.

Lemma 4.11
.

Let
\delta\in(0,1/4]
, and
let
{\mathscr{A}}^{\prime}
be any learner that identifies the set in register
A
with probability
1-\delta
, given
t^{\prime}
copies of the corresponding quantum sample in the subregister
B^{\prime}
of
B
. Then there is a learner
{\mathscr{A}}
which uses
t
copies of the sample in register
B
, with
t=t^{\prime}+ck
for some positive constant
c
, such that
\mi(A:F)\leq(1-c_{0}\delta)\entropy(A)
, where
c_{0}
is a positive constant that depends only on
c
.

Proof:

Essentially, the learner
{\mathscr{A}}
uses the
ck
additional copies of the quantum sample to check whether the output of
{\mathscr{A}}^{\prime}
is correct or not. If
{\mathscr{A}}
believes the answer is
incorrect
, then it ignores the answer, and simply outputs a uniformly random subset of size
k
.

The learner
{\mathscr{A}}
first runs
{\mathscr{A}}^{\prime}
using
t^{\prime}
quantum samples
|\psi_{S}\rangle
. Suppose
{\mathscr{A}}^{\prime}
outputs
S^{\prime}
. Then
{\mathscr{A}}
performs the projective measurement
\left\{M,{\mathbb{I}}-M\right\}
, where
M\coloneqq|\psi_{S^{\prime}}\rangle\!\langle\psi_{S^{\prime}}|
, on the remaining quantum samples. If the outcome is
M
for all
ck
copies,
{\mathscr{A}}
outputs
S^{\prime}
. Otherwise, it outputs a uniformly random size-
k
subset of
[n]
.

If
S^{\prime}=S
(the set in register
A
), then with probability
1
the outcome is
M
for all
ck
measurements. Otherwise, suppose that
d\coloneqq\left|S\setminus S^{\prime}\right|
. Then, the probability that the outcome is
M
in all
ck
trials is precisely
(1-d/k)^{2ck}
, which is at most
\e^{-2cd}
. Thus, with probability at least
1-\e^{-2c}
, the learner
{\mathscr{A}}
confirms that
S^{\prime}\neq S
, and outputs a uniformly random size-
k
subset.

There are three cases for the output
S^{\prime\prime}
of
{\mathscr{A}}
:

1.

S^{\prime}=S
, learner
{\mathscr{A}}
confirms this via the measurement of the additional
ck
samples, and
S^{\prime\prime}=S
.

2.

S^{\prime}\neq S
, learner
{\mathscr{A}}
detects this, and
S^{\prime\prime}
is a uniformly random size-
k
subset.

3.

S^{\prime}\neq S
, learner
{\mathscr{A}}
fails to detect this, and
S^{\prime\prime}=S^{\prime}
.

Let the random variable
E
denote which of the three cases occurs, and let
\epsilon_{i}\coloneqq\Pr[E=i]
. Since the probability of correctness of
{\mathscr{A}}^{\prime}
is exactly
1-\delta
, we have
\epsilon_{2}+\epsilon_{3}=\delta
. By the above analysis,
\epsilon_{3}\leq\delta\e^{-2c}
.

We have

\displaystyle\mi(A:F)\quad

\displaystyle=\quad\entropy(F)-\entropy(F|A)

\displaystyle\leq\quad\entropy(F)-\entropy(F|AE)

\displaystyle\leq\quad\log\binom{n}{k}-\epsilon_{2}\log\binom{n}{k}

\displaystyle\leq\quad(1-\delta(1-\e^{-2c}))\log\binom{n}{k}\kern 5.0pt,

where the second inequality follows by noting that

\entropy(F|AE)\quad=\quad\sum_{i\in[3]}\epsilon_{i}\entropy(F|A,E=i)\quad\geq\quad\epsilon_{2}\entropy(F|A,E=2)\quad=\quad\epsilon_{2}\log\binom{n}{k}\kern 5.0pt.

So the lemma holds with
c_{0}\coloneqq 1-\e^{-2c}
.

We show that
\mi(A:B)
is larger than this expression even when
t\in\Theta(n)
, for which we give a lower bound on the mutual information
\mi(A:B)_{\rho}\,
.

Lemma 4.12
.

Let
2\leq m\leq n/10
,
\kappa\in(0,n/m)
, and
t\coloneqq\kappa n
. Then

\mi(A:B)_{\rho}\quad=\quad\vnentropy(B)_{\rho}\quad\geq\quad\left(1-\e^{-\kappa}-\frac{\kappa m}{n}\right)\log\binom{n}{m}-1\kern 5.0pt.

Proof:

The spectrum of
\rho^{B}
is given by
((l_{s},\lambda_{s,t}):s=0,1,\dotsc,m)
, where
l_{s}
and
\lambda_{s,t}
are as described in
Section

4.1
. We have

\displaystyle\vnentropy(B)\quad

\displaystyle=\quad\sum_{s=0}^{m}l_{s}\lambda_{s,t}\log\frac{1}{\lambda_{s,t}}

\displaystyle=\quad\sum_{s=0}^{m}l_{s}\lambda_{s,t}\log\frac{1}{l_{s}\lambda_{s,t}}+\sum_{s=0}^{m}l_{s}\lambda_{s,t}\log l_{s}

\displaystyle\geq\quad\sum_{s=0}^{m}l_{s}\lambda_{s,t}\log l_{s}\kern 5.0pt,

as
(l_{s}\lambda_{s,t}:0\leq s\leq m)
is a distribution. Using the value of
l_{s}
,

\displaystyle\vnentropy(B)\quad

\displaystyle\geq\quad\sum_{s=0}^{m}l_{s}\lambda_{s,t}\log\binom{n}{s}+\sum_{s=0}^{m}l_{s}\lambda_{s,t}\log\frac{\binom{n}{s}-\binom{n}{s-1}}{\binom{n}{s}}

\displaystyle=\quad\left[\sum_{s=0}^{m}l_{s}\lambda_{s,t}\frac{\log\binom{n}{s}}{\log\binom{n}{m}}\right]\log\binom{n}{m}+\sum_{s=0}^{m}l_{s}\lambda_{s,t}\log\frac{n-2s+1}{n-s+1}\kern 5.0pt.

Now,

\frac{n-2s+1}{n-s+1}\quad\geq\quad\frac{n-2m+1}{n-m+1}\quad\geq\quad\frac{1}{2}\kern 5.0pt,

as
(n-2s+1)/(n-s+1)
is a decreasing function of
s
. Moreover, by Lemma
C.1
,

\frac{\log\binom{n}{s}}{\log\binom{n}{m}}\quad=\quad\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{s-1}\log\frac{n-i}{i+1}}{\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\log\frac{n-i}{i+1}}\quad\geq\quad\frac{s}{m}\kern 5.0pt,

as
\log\tfrac{n-i}{i+1}
is decreasing in
i
.
Thus, we have that

\displaystyle\vnentropy(B)\quad

\displaystyle\geq\quad\left[\frac{1}{m}\sum_{s=0}^{m}sl_{s}\lambda_{s,t}\right]\log\binom{n}{m}-1\kern 5.0pt.

The claim now follows from
Lemma

4.9
, as
\sum_{s=0}^{m}sl_{s}\lambda_{s,t}=\expct[W_{t}]
.

Note that by taking
\kappa
to be an arbitrarily large constant, we get
\vnentropy(B)_{\rho}
arbitrarily close to
\entropy(A)
— the maximum it can be.

Let
\delta\coloneqq 1/4
.
Taking
t^{\prime}\coloneqq n/2
and
c_{0}
to be a suitable constant greater than
1/2
in Lemma
4.11
, we see that for the adversarial algorithms described therein,
\mi(A:F)\leq\tfrac{7}{8}\log\binom{n}{m}
. On the other hand, taking
m\coloneqq{\lfloor\sqrt{n}\,\rfloor}
,
\kappa
a sufficiently large constant, and
n
sufficiently large, we see that
\mi(A:B)_{\rho}\geq\gamma\log\binom{n}{m}=\gamma\log\binom{n}{k}
, for some constant
\gamma>7/8
.

4.4
Sample complexity for approximation

In this section, we show that the standard argument
does
yield a tight lower bound on sample complexity when we are interested in approximating the unknown set. The bound rests on the following estimate.

Lemma 4.13
.

Let
t\coloneqq k\ln m+cn
for some
c\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
m\ln m\leq\left|c\right|n/10
, and
1\leq m\leq n/40
. Then

\displaystyle\mi(A:B)_{\rho}\quad\leq\quad\log\binom{n}{m}-\left(\log n-\log m-\frac{2m}{n}\log\e\right)\min\left\{\e^{-2c},\e^{-c/3}\right\}+\log(m+1)\kern 5.0pt.

Proof:

Note that
\mi(A:B)_{\rho}=\vnentropy(B)_{\rho}\,
. The spectrum of
\rho^{B}
is given by
((l_{s},\lambda_{s,t}):s=0,1,\dotsc,m)
, where
l_{s}
and
\lambda_{s,t}
are as given by
Corollary

4.3
. Since
\sum_{s=0}^{m}l_{s}\lambda_{s,t}=1
, we have

\displaystyle\vnentropy(B)_{\rho}\quad

\displaystyle=\quad\sum_{s=0}^{m}l_{s}\lambda_{s,t}\log\frac{1}{\lambda_{s,t}}

\displaystyle\leq\quad\sum_{s=0}^{m}l_{s}\lambda_{s,t}\log l_{s}+\log(m+1)

\displaystyle=\quad\log\binom{n}{m}-\sum_{s=0}^{m}l_{s}\lambda_{s,t}\log\frac{\binom{n}{m}}{l_{s}}+\log(m+1)\kern 5.0pt.

Since
l_{s}\leq\binom{n}{s}
, and the ratio
\tfrac{n-s}{s+1}
is decreasing in
s
, and
1-z\geq\e^{-2z}
when
0\leq z\leq\tfrac{\ln 2}{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\binom{n}{m}}{l_{s}}\quad

\displaystyle\geq\quad\left(\frac{n-s}{s+1}\right)\left(\frac{n-s+1}{s+2}\right)\dotsb\left(\frac{n-m+1}{m}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\quad\left(\frac{n-m+1}{m}\right)^{m-s}

\displaystyle=\quad\left(\frac{n}{m}\right)^{m-s}\left(1-\frac{m-1}{n}\right)^{m-s}

\displaystyle\geq\quad\left(\frac{n}{m}\right)^{m-s}\exp\!\left(-\,\frac{2m(m-s)}{n}\right)\kern 5.0pt.

Combining these bounds, we get

\displaystyle\vnentropy(B)_{\rho}\quad

\displaystyle\leq\quad\log\binom{n}{m}-\left(\log n-\log m-\frac{2m}{n}\log\e\right)\sum_{s=0}^{m}(m-s)l_{s}\lambda_{s,t}+\log(m+1)\kern 5.0pt.

By Lemma
4.8
, the expectation
\sum_{s=0}^{m}sl_{s}\lambda_{s,t}\leq m-\min\left\{\e^{-2c},\e^{-c/3}\right\}
, and the claim follows.

Suppose given
t
copies of the sample
|\psi_{S}\rangle
in register
B
, an algorithm
{\mathscr{A}}
outputs a size-
k
subset such that the expected number of mismatches is bounded by
\Delta
. In other words, if the output is denoted by the random variable
F(S)
, then
\expct\left|S-F(S)\right|\leq\Delta
. We first argue that without loss of generality, we may assume that the algorithm is invariant under permutations of
[n]
in the sense that
\Pr[F(S)=S^{\prime}]=\Pr[F(\pi(S))=\pi(S^{\prime})]
for all pairs of size-
k
subsets
S,S^{\prime}\subseteq[n]
, and permutations
\pi
of
[n]
.

Lemma 4.14
.

Suppose an algorithm
{\mathscr{A}}
takes some number of quantum samples corresponding to a size-
k
subset
S\subseteq[n]
, and outputs a possibly random size-
k
subset
F(S)
of
[n]
. Then, there is an algorithm
{\mathscr{A}}^{\prime}
with output
F^{\prime}(S)
given quantum samples of
S
that satisfies the following properties. The algorithm
{\mathscr{A}}^{\prime}
is invariant under permutations of
[n]
, and has the same sample complexity as
{\mathscr{A}}
. Moreover, for every size-
k
subset
S
and uniformly random size-
k
subset
A
, the algorithm
{\mathscr{A}}^{\prime}
satisfies

1.

\Pr[F^{\prime}(S)=S]=\Pr[F(A)=A]\,
, and

2.

\expct\left|S\setminus F^{\prime}(S)\right|=\expct\left|A\setminus F(A)\right|\,
.

Proof:

The algorithm
{\mathscr{A}}^{\prime}
samples a uniformly random permutation
\pi
. Then it maps each copy of
|\psi_{S}\rangle
to
|\psi_{\pi(S)}\rangle
and runs the algorithm
{\mathscr{A}}
on these samples. Suppose the output of
{\mathscr{A}}
is
S^{\prime}
. The algorithm
{\mathscr{A}}^{\prime}
outputs
\pi^{-1}(S^{\prime})
. Permutation invariance follows from the uniform choice of
\pi
, and we may verify that the other properties claimed in the lemma are also satisfied.

Permutation invariance helps us prove the lower bound for approximation.

Theorem 4.15
.

Let
\Delta\geq 0
be a constant. Given
n,k
and quantum samples for an unknown size-
k
subset
S\subseteq[n]
, any algorithm that approximates
S
to within
\Delta
in expectation uses
\Omega(k\log\min\left\{m+1,k\right\})
samples.

Proof:

Let
\alpha\in(0,1)
be constant to be specified later. By Lemma
4.1
, it suffices to prove an
\Omega(k\log(m+1))
bound on the sample complexity when
m\leq n^{\alpha}
and
n^{(1-\alpha)}\geq 2
. (For the details, we refer the reader to
Corollary

4.18
, where we make a similar argument.)

Suppose an algorithm uses
t
quantum samples and produces a
\Delta
-approximation to the unknown set. We follow the notation for the registers used by the learning algorithm introduced in
Section

4.1
. By the Data Processing Inequality we have

\mi(A:B)_{\rho}\quad\geq\quad\mi(A:F)\quad=\quad\entropy(F)-\entropy(F|A)\kern 5.0pt.

We show that this inequality is violated if
t=k\ln m+cn
for a suitable constant
c\in{\mathbb{R}}
. By the Data Processing Inequality,
\mi(A:B)_{\rho}
is non-decreasing in
t
, so the inequality is also violated for
t<k\ln m+cn
. We thus conclude a
k\ln m+cn
lower bound.

We start with a tight lower bound on
\mi(A:F)
. By permutation invariance,
\entropy(F)=\entropy(A)=\log\binom{n}{m}
and
\entropy(F|A)=\sum_{i=0}^{m}q_{i}u_{i}\log\frac{1}{u_{i}}
, where
q_{i}
is the number of subsets
S^{\prime}
such that for a fixed size-
k
set
S
, the pair
S,S^{\prime}
have exactly
i
mismatches, and
u_{i}
is the probability of getting a particular such
S^{\prime}
as output given samples for
S
. Note that
q_{i}
and
u_{i}
are independent of
S
and
S^{\prime}
, and
q_{i}=\binom{k}{i}\binom{m}{i}
.
Now

\log q_{i}\quad=\quad\log\binom{k}{i}+\log\binom{m}{i}\quad\leq\quad i(\log k+\log m)\kern 5.0pt.

Thus

\displaystyle\mi(A:F)\quad

\displaystyle=\quad\log\binom{n}{m}-\sum_{i=0}^{m}q_{i}u_{i}\log\frac{1}{u_{i}}

\displaystyle=\quad\log\binom{n}{m}-\sum_{i=0}^{m}q_{i}u_{i}\log q_{i}-\sum_{i=0}^{m}q_{i}u_{i}\log\frac{1}{q_{i}u_{i}}

\displaystyle\geq\quad\log\binom{n}{m}-\sum_{i=0}^{m}iq_{i}u_{i}(\log k+\log m)-\log(m+1)

\displaystyle=\quad\log\binom{n}{m}-\Delta(\log k+\log m)-\log(m+1)\kern 5.0pt.

Combining this with the upper bound on
\mi(A:B)
given by Lemma
4.13
when
t=k\ln m+cn
, we get

\displaystyle\log\binom{n}{m}-\left(\log n-\log m-\frac{2m}{n}\log\e\right)

\displaystyle\min\left\{\e^{-2c},\e^{-c/3}\right\}+\log(m+1)

\displaystyle\geq\quad\log\binom{n}{m}-\Delta(\log k+\log m)-\log(m+1)\kern 5.0pt.

This is equivalent to

\displaystyle-\left(\log n-\log m-\frac{2m}{n}\log\e\right)\min\left\{\e^{-2c},\e^{-c/3}\right\}+2\log(m+1)+\Delta\log m\quad\geq\quad-\Delta\log k\kern 5.0pt.

Using
1\leq m\leq n^{\alpha}
and
k<n
, we get

\displaystyle-\left((1-\alpha)\log n-\frac{2}{n^{1-\alpha}}\log\e\right)\min\left\{\e^{-2c},\e^{-c/3}\right\}+2\alpha\log n+2+\alpha\Delta\log n\quad\geq\quad-\Delta\log n\kern 5.0pt.

(4.3)

Dividing throughout by
\log n
and taking
n\rightarrow\infty
, we get

\min\left\{\e^{-2c},\e^{-c/3}\right\}\quad\leq\quad\Delta+\frac{2\alpha}{1-\alpha}\quad\leq\quad\frac{\Delta+2\alpha}{1-\alpha}\kern 5.0pt.

Suppose
\Delta\in[0,1)
. Taking
\alpha<(1-\Delta)/3
and taking
c\geq 0
such that

c\quad<\quad\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{1-\alpha}{\Delta+2\alpha}\kern 5.0pt,

and
n
large enough, we see that the inequality (
4.3
) is violated. (The choice of
\alpha
ensures that there is such a
c\geq 0
.) If
\Delta\geq 1
, we take
c<0
such that

-c\quad>\quad 3\log\frac{\Delta+2\alpha}{1-\alpha}

for any choice of
\alpha\in(0,1)
. The right hand side is positive as
\Delta\geq 1
. Taking
n
large enough, we again see that Eq. (
4.3
) is violated. This proves the claimed bound. Note that the second order term in the resulting lower bound is
\Theta(n)
when
\Delta<1
, and
-\Theta(n)
when
\Delta\geq 1
.

4.5
Sample complexity of Quantum Coupon Collection

In this section, we prove
a sharper lower bound on the sample complexity of the Quantum Coupon Collector problem.

To characterize sample complexity, we bound the decoding error of any learning algorithm by studying the
distinguishability
of the ensemble given by a fixed number of samples. The
distinguishability
of an ensemble is the probability that an optimal measurement correctly identifies a state selected at random from the ensemble.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
{\mathcal{F}}\coloneqq\big((p_{i},\sigma_{i}):\sigma_{i}\in{\mathsf{D}}({\mathbb{C}}^{d}),i\in[l]\big)
be an ensemble of states in which state
\sigma_{i}
occurs with probability
p_{i}
.
We define the
distinguishability

\dist
of
{\mathcal{F}}
as

\dist({\mathcal{F}})\quad\coloneqq\quad\max_{\textrm{measurement }M}\sum_{i=1}^{l}p_{i}\trace(M_{i}\sigma_{i})\kern 5.0pt,

where the maximization is over all measurements
M\coloneqq(M_{1},M_{2},\dotsc,M_{l})
with
l
outcomes.

We can estimate the distinguishability of an ensemble of states via the generalised Holevo-Curlander bounds stated below
[
18
,
12
,
22
,
27
]
. The upper bound on distinguishability was shown by Curlander
[
12
]
in the case of ensembles of equiprobable
linearly independent
pure states. In the ensemble at hand, however, the states are not necessarily linearly independent for arbitrary
t\geq 1
. The upper bound in the case of ensembles of equiprobable, possibly mixed states was derived by Ogawa and Nagaoka
[
22
, Lemma 1]
. The proof they gave also extends with minor modifications to non-uniform ensembles. The two bounds were proven — re-proven independently in the case of the upper bound
[
26
]
— by Tyson
[
27
, Theorem 10]
. Tyson later gave another proof of the bounds which also generalizes to error-recovery
[
28
, Section III]
.

Theorem 4.16

(generalised Holevo-Curlander bounds;
[
22
,
27
]
)
.

Let
{\mathcal{F}}\coloneqq\big((p_{i},\sigma_{i}):\sigma_{i}\in{\mathsf{D}}({\mathbb{C}}^{d}),i\in[l]\big)

be an ensemble of
l
quantum states. Then the distinguishability of
{\mathcal{F}}
satisfies

\left(\hc({\mathcal{F}})\right)^{2}\quad\leq\quad\dist({\mathcal{F}})\quad\leq\quad\hc({\mathcal{F}})\kern 5.0pt,

where

\hc({\mathcal{F}})\quad\coloneqq\quad\trace\left(\sum_{i=1}^{l}p_{i}^{2}\sigma_{i}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\kern 5.0pt.

Using the upper bound on distinguishability above, we first derive a bound for a small range of parameters for which the Quantum Coupon Collector problem appears to be the hardest. (With some additional arguments, we can also prove the theorem below using the original bound due to Curlander.)

Theorem 4.17
.

Let
\delta\in(0,1/40]
be a constant.
Any algorithm for the Quantum Coupon Collector problem with parameters
n,k
, and error probability at most
\delta
has sample complexity at least

k\ln m+c_{0}n

when
1\leq m\leq\delta n
and
m\ln m\leq c_{0}n/20
, where
c_{0}\coloneqq\tfrac{1}{2}\ln\big(\tfrac{1-\delta}{32\delta}\big)
.

Proof:

We follow the notation introduced in Section
4.1
. Suppose that for a constant
\delta
as in the statement, with probability
1-\delta
, a learner
{\mathscr{A}}
identifies the unknown
k
-element subset
S
of
[n]
in register
A
, given
t
copies of the corresponding quantum sample
|\psi_{S}\rangle
in register
B
. The algorithm outputs its guess for the set
S
in register
F
.

Let
{\mathcal{E}}
be the ensemble consisting of states
(|\psi_{S}\rangle\!\langle\psi_{S}|)^{\otimes t}
for a uniformly random size-
k
subset
S\subset[n]
. By
Theorem

4.16
, we have

\displaystyle 1-\delta\quad

\displaystyle\leq\quad\Pr[F=A]

\displaystyle\leq\quad\hc({\mathcal{E}})

\displaystyle=\quad\frac{1}{{\binom{n}{m}}^{1/2}}\trace\sqrt{\rho^{B}}\kern 5.0pt.

Suppose that
t=k\ln m+cn
, for some parameter
c
. Since the probability of error may only decrease if the algorithm has access to more quantum samples, we assume that
c\geq 0
. We show that if
c
is “too small” a constant, then
\hc({\mathcal{E}})<1-\delta
, which is a contradiction.

By
Corollary

4.3
, the state
\rho^{B}
has an eigenvalue
\lambda_{s,t}
with multiplicity
l_{s}
for each
s=0,1,\dotsc,m
. Using the value of
l_{s}
, the Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality, and
\trace(\rho^{B})=1
, we have

\displaystyle\hc({\mathcal{E}})\quad

\displaystyle=\quad{\binom{n}{m}}^{-1/2}\sum_{s=0}^{m}l_{s}\sqrt{\lambda_{s,t}}

\displaystyle=\quad{\binom{n}{m}}^{-1/2}\left(\sqrt{l_{m}}\sqrt{l_{m}\lambda_{m,t}}+\sum_{s=0}^{m-1}\sqrt{l_{s}}\sqrt{l_{s}\lambda_{s,t}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\quad{\binom{n}{m}}^{-1/2}\left(\sqrt{l_{m}}\sqrt{l_{m}\lambda_{m,t}}+\left(\sum_{s=0}^{m-1}l_{s}\right)^{1/2}\left(\sum_{s=0}^{m-1}l_{s}\lambda_{s,t}\right)^{1/2}\right)

\displaystyle=\quad{\binom{n}{m}}^{-1/2}\left(\left(\binom{n}{m}-\binom{n}{m-1}\right)^{1/2}\sqrt{l_{m}\lambda_{m,t}}+{\binom{n}{m-1}}^{1/2}(1-l_{m}\lambda_{m,t})^{1/2}\right)\kern 5.0pt.

Defining
v\coloneqq l_{m}\lambda_{m,t}
and
w\coloneqq\tfrac{\binom{n}{m-1}}{\binom{n}{m}}
, the above inequality simplifies to

\hc({\mathcal{F}})\quad\leq\quad\sqrt{v(1-w)}+\sqrt{w(1-v)}\kern 5.0pt.

Intuitively, we have
1-v=1-l_{m}\lambda_{m,t}\ll 1
and
w=\frac{m}{n-m+1}\ll 1
. So if the upper bound on
l_{m}\lambda_{m,t}
in
Lemma

4.10
were tight, we would have

\hc({\mathcal{F}})\quad\lesssim\quad\sqrt{l_{m}\lambda_{m,t}}\left(1-\frac{m}{n-m+1}\right)^{1/2}\quad\approx\quad 1-\frac{e^{-2c}}{4}-\frac{m}{2(n-m+1)}\kern 5.0pt.

When combined with the lower bound of
1-\delta
on
\hc({\mathcal{E}})
, this would give us
c\in\Omega\big(\ln\tfrac{1}{4\delta}\big)
. However, the lower bound we have on
l_{m}\lambda_{m,t}
does not suffice for such an argument, and we analyse the bound on distinguishability differently.

Observe that
w\in(0,1)
and that as a function in
x
, the expression
\sqrt{x(1-w)}+\sqrt{w(1-x)}
is increasing in the range
[0,1-w]
. Also, from the bound
m\leq\delta n
, we have that
w\leq\tfrac{\delta}{1-\delta}
. Combining these with the upper bound from
Lemma

4.10
, we see that for any
c\geq c_{0}/2
,

\displaystyle\hc({\mathcal{E}})\quad

\displaystyle\leq\quad\left(1-\frac{{\e}^{-2c}}{2}\right)^{1/2}\sqrt{1-w}+\left(\frac{w\e^{-2c}}{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\quad 1-\frac{\e^{-2c}}{4}+\left(\frac{\delta\e^{-2c}}{2(1-\delta)}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\quad 1-\frac{\e^{-2c}}{8}\kern 5.0pt,

if
\tfrac{\delta}{1-\delta}\leq\tfrac{\e^{-2c}}{32}
, or equivalently,
c\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\ln\left(\tfrac{1-\delta}{32\delta}\right)=c_{0}
. This is a contradiction as

\hc({\mathcal{E}})\quad\geq\quad 1-\delta\quad\geq\quad\left(1+\frac{\e^{-2c}}{32}\right)^{-1}\quad\geq\quad 1-\frac{\e^{-2c}}{16}\kern 5.0pt.

This completes the proof.

Finally, we explain how this implies a more general bound, when
m
may be
\Omega(n/\log n)
. (The
\order_{k}(1)
term in the statement below stands for
\order(1)
with respect to the parameter
k
.)

Corollary 4.18
.

Let
\delta\in(0,1/40]
be a constant.
Any algorithm for the Quantum Coupon Collector problem with parameters
n,k
, and error probability at most
\delta
has sample complexity at least

(1-\order_{k}(1))\;k\ln\left(\min\left\{k,m+1\right\}\right)

(4.4)

whenever
(\ln k)/k\leq\min\left\{c_{0}/20,\;\delta\ln 2\right\}
, where
c_{0}
is as defined in
Theorem

4.17
.

Proof:

Consider a fixed
k>1
. Let
n_{0}\coloneqq{\lfloor k(1+\beta/\ln k)\rfloor}
for a positive constant
\beta\leq 1
to be specified later, and let
m_{0}\coloneqq n_{0}-k
. We have
n_{0}>k
if
\beta\geq(\ln k)/k
. Secondly,
1\leq m_{0}\leq\beta k/\ln k\leq\delta k\leq\delta n_{0}
if
\beta\leq\delta\ln 2
. Finally,

\displaystyle m_{0}\ln m_{0}\quad

\displaystyle\leq\quad\frac{\beta k}{\ln k}\ln\left(\frac{\beta k}{\ln k}\right)

\displaystyle=\quad\beta k+\frac{\beta k}{\ln k}\ln\left(\frac{\beta}{\ln k}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\quad\frac{c_{0}n_{0}}{20}\kern 5.0pt,

if, in addition,
\beta\leq c_{0}/20
. A parameter
\beta
(depending on the constant
\delta
) satisfying all three conditions exists as long as
(\ln k)/k
is at most
c_{0}/20
and
\delta\ln 2
.

With
\beta
as above, the bound given by Theorem
4.17
applies, and the sample complexity of the Quantum Coupon Collector problem with parameters
n_{0},k,\delta
is at least

\displaystyle k\ln m_{0}+c_{0}n_{0}\kern 5.0pt.

Up to lower order terms, this is at least
k\ln k-k\ln\ln k
. By Lemma
4.1
, this lower bound continues to hold for all
n\geq n_{0}
.

For a fixed
k
, and
n
such that
k<n<n_{0}
, both
m/n
and
(m\ln m)/n
decrease as
n
decreases. So for such
n
we have
m\leq\delta n
and
m\ln m\leq c_{0}n/20
, and the lower bound given by Theorem
4.17
holds. Thus we get the bound claimed in Eq. (
4.4
) for all
n>k
.

Note that
Theorem

4.17
gives us a stronger lower bound when the parameter
m
is “small” in comparison with
n
. We lose some of this tightness in extending the lower bound to all parameter regimes.

Appendix A
Coupon-Collector-like random walks

In
Section

4.2
, we analyse properties of the random walk
(W_{t})
by comparing it with the random walks
(W^{\prime}_{t})
and
(W^{\prime\prime}_{t}-V^{\prime\prime}_{t})
defined in the proofs of
Lemma

4.8
and
Lemma

4.9
. Recall that
W^{\prime}\coloneqq{\widetilde{W}}(n-5m)
and
W^{\prime\prime}\coloneqq{\widetilde{W}}(n)
, with
n,m
and
{\widetilde{W}}(n^{\prime})
as in that section. Here we derive a few properties of the random walk
{\widetilde{W}}(n^{\prime})
that are used in the analysis. The walk is very similar to the standard coupon collector process.

Lemma A.1
.

The expectation of
{\widetilde{W}}_{t}(n^{\prime})
is given by the exact relation

\expct\big[\,{\widetilde{W}}_{t}(n^{\prime})\,\big]\quad=\quad m\left(1-\left(1-\frac{1}{n^{\prime}}\right)^{t}\right)\kern 5.0pt,

and is bounded as

m\left(1-\exp\!\left(-\,\frac{t}{n^{\prime}-1}\right)\right)\quad\geq\quad\expct\big[\,{\widetilde{W}}_{t}(n^{\prime})\,\big]\quad\geq\quad m\left(1-\exp\!\left(-\,\frac{t}{n^{\prime}}\right)\right)\kern 5.0pt.

The probability that
{\widetilde{W}}(n^{\prime})
reaches
m
at time
t
is bounded as

1-m\exp\!\left(-\frac{t}{n^{\prime}}\right)\quad\leq\quad\Pr\big[\,{\widetilde{W}}_{t}(n^{\prime})=m\,\big]\quad\leq\quad 1-m\exp\!\left(-\,\frac{t}{n^{\prime}-1}\right)+\frac{m^{2}}{2}\exp\left(-\,\frac{2t}{n^{\prime}}\right)\kern 5.0pt.

Proof:

Consider the binary random variables
X_{i,t}
for
i\in[m]
and
t\in{\mathbb{N}}
which is
1
if the
i
-th coupon is sampled at least once within the first
t
steps in the random process underlying
{\widetilde{W}}(n^{\prime})
. Then,

{\widetilde{W}}_{t}(n^{\prime})\quad=\quad\sum_{i=1}^{m}X_{i,t}\kern 5.0pt.

For a fixed
t
, the random variables
X_{i,t}
are identically distributed for all
i
, and

\expct[X_{i,t}]\quad=\quad\Pr[X_{i,t}=1]\quad=\quad 1-\Pr[X_{i,t}=0]\quad=\quad 1-\left(1-\frac{1}{n^{\prime}}\right)^{t}\kern 5.0pt.

Thus,
\expct\big[\,{\widetilde{W}}_{t}(n^{\prime})\,]=m\left(1-\left(1-\frac{1}{n^{\prime}}\right)^{t}\right)
.
Furthermore,

\exp\!\left(-\,\frac{t}{n^{\prime}-1}\right)\quad\leq\quad\left(1-\frac{1}{n^{\prime}}\right)^{t}\quad\leq\quad\exp\!\left(-\,\frac{t}{n^{\prime}}\right)\kern 5.0pt,

so we get the inequalities

m\left(1-\exp\left(-\,\frac{t}{n^{\prime}-1}\right)\right)\quad\geq\quad\expct\big[\,{\widetilde{W}}_{t}(n^{\prime})\,\big]\quad\geq\quad m\left(1-\exp\left(-\,\frac{t}{n^{\prime}}\right)\right)\kern 5.0pt.

For the final part of the lemma, we would like to bound the probability of collecting all
m
marked coupons, i.e., of the event that
{\widetilde{W}}_{t}(n^{\prime})=m
. By the Union Bound, this probability is bounded from below as

\displaystyle\Pr\big[\,{\widetilde{W}}_{t}(n^{\prime})=m\,\big]\quad

\displaystyle=\quad\Pr\big[\,\sum_{i=1}^{m}X_{i,t}=m\,\big]

\displaystyle\geq\quad 1-\sum_{i=0}^{m}\Pr[X_{i,t}=0]

\displaystyle=\quad 1-m\left(1-\frac{1}{n^{\prime}}\right)^{t}

\displaystyle\geq\quad 1-m\exp\left(-\,\frac{t}{n^{\prime}}\right)\kern 5.0pt.

By considering the first two terms in the Principle of Inclusion-Exclusion, we may also upper bound the probability as

\displaystyle\Pr\big[\,{\widetilde{W}}_{t}(n^{\prime})=m\,\big]\quad

\displaystyle\leq\quad 1-\sum_{i=0}^{m}\Pr[X_{i,t}=0]+\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq m}\Pr[X_{i,t}=X_{j,t}=0]

\displaystyle=\quad 1-m\left(1-\frac{1}{n^{\prime}}\right)^{t}+\binom{m}{2}\left(1-\frac{2}{n^{\prime}}\right)^{t}

\displaystyle\leq\quad 1-m\exp\left(-\,\frac{t}{n^{\prime}-1}\right)+\frac{m^{2}}{2}\exp\left(-\,\frac{2t}{n^{\prime}}\right)\kern 5.0pt,

as claimed.

Appendix B
Random walk domination

Here, we relate the random walk
W\coloneqq(W_{t})
with the random walks
W^{\prime}\coloneqq(W^{\prime}_{t})
and
Z\coloneqq(W^{\prime\prime}_{t}-V^{\prime\prime}_{t})
defined in the proofs of
Lemma

4.8
and
Lemma

4.9
. In particular, we show that
W^{\prime}
dominates
W
, and
W
dominates
Z
.

We begin with some general conditions under which one random walk dominates another, as first stated in
Section

4.2
.
See
4.7

Proof:

The initial condition and the relationship between the transition probabilities allow us to define a coupling between
(U_{t})
and
(U^{\prime}_{t})
from which the stated properties follow. I.e., we define a pair of correlated random walks
(Q_{t},R_{t})
on the integers such that
(Q_{t})
has the same distribution as
(U_{t})
, and
(R_{t})
has the same distribution as
(U^{\prime}_{t})
. Moreover, we ensure that
Q_{t}\geq R_{t}
for all
t\geq 0
. As
Q_{t}\geq R_{t}
, we get

\displaystyle\Pr[U_{t}\geq i]\quad

\displaystyle=\quad\Pr[Q_{t}\geq i]

\displaystyle\geq\quad\Pr[Q_{t}\geq i\text{ and }R_{t}\geq i]

\displaystyle=\quad\Pr[R_{t}\geq i]

\displaystyle=\quad\Pr[U^{\prime}_{t}\geq i]\kern 5.0pt,

and

\expct[U_{t}]\quad=\quad\expct[Q_{t}]\quad\geq\quad\expct[R_{t}]\quad=\quad\expct[U^{\prime}_{t}]\kern 5.0pt.

It remains to define the coupling. Let
(H_{t}:t\geq 0)
be a sequence of i.i.d. random variables distributed uniformly over the unit interval
[0,1]
. We define
(Q_{t},R_{t})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
inductively using
(H_{t})
while ensuring that
\left|Q_{t}-Q_{t-1}\right|\leq 1
,
\left|R_{t}-R_{t-1}\right|\leq 1
, and
Q_{t-1}\geq R_{t-1}
for all
t\geq 1
.

We set
Q_{0}=R_{0}\coloneqq 0
. For
t\geq 1
, suppose we have defined
Q_{t-1}
and
R_{t-1}
satisfying the properties stated above. For
i,j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, conditioned on the event that
Q_{t-1}=i
we define

\displaystyle Q_{t}\quad\coloneqq\quad\begin{cases}i-1&\text{if }H_{t}\leq q_{t,i,i-1}\\
i+1&\text{if }H_{t}>1-q_{t,i,i+1}\\
i&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

and similarly, conditioned on the event that
R_{t-1}=j
, we define

\displaystyle R_{t}\quad\coloneqq\quad\begin{cases}j-1&\text{if }H_{t}\leq q^{\prime}_{t,j,j-1}\\
j+1&\text{if }H_{t}>1-q^{\prime}_{t,j,j+1}\\
j&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

We show
Q_{t}\geq R_{t}
by induction on
t
. It holds for
t=0
. Assume
Q_{t-1}\geq R_{t-1}
for some

. Since the two walks move by at most
1
at every time step, we need only consider the case when
Q_{t-1}-R_{t-1}\leq 1
. When
Q_{t-1}=R_{t-1}=i
, the two conditions
q_{t,i,i+1}\geq q^{\prime}_{t,i,i+1}
and
q_{t,i,i-1}\leq q^{\prime}_{t,i,i-1}
on the transition probabilities ensure that

•

whenever
R_{t}
moves right,
Q_{t}
also moves right (i.e., if
R_{t}=i+1
, then
Q_{t}=i+1
), and

•

whenever
Q_{t}
moves left,
R_{t}
also moves left (i.e., if
Q_{t}=i-1
, then
R_{t}=i-1
).

When
Q_{t-1}=i=R_{t-1}+1
, the condition
q_{t,i,i+1}+q_{t,i,i}\geq q^{\prime}_{t,i-1,i}
on the transition probabilities ensures that whenever
R_{t}
moves right,
Q_{t}
either moves right or stays at the same point (i.e., if
R_{t}=i
, then
Q_{t}\in\left\{i,i+1\right\}
). In all the cases, we obtain
Q_{t}\geq R_{t}
.

Lemma B.1

Proof:

We refer the reader to

for the transition probabilities of the walks

W^{\prime}

Since
(k-s)/k\leq 1
,
n-s+1\leq n+1
, and
n-2s+1\geq n-2s\geq n-2m
, we see that

p_{s,+1}\quad=\quad\frac{(k-s)(n-s+1)(m-s)}{(n-2s)(n-2s+1)k}\quad\leq\quad\frac{(n+1)(m-s)}{(n-2m)^{2}}\kern 5.0pt.

W

Recall that
W^{\prime\prime}\coloneqq{\widetilde{W}}(n)

is the random walk on
{\mathbb{Z}}
defined in the proof of

Lemma B.2
.

The random process
(W^{\prime\prime}_{t}-V^{\prime\prime}_{t})
is a time-dependent random walk, and if

, the random walk
(W_{t})

Proof:

For any
s\in{\mathbb{N}}_{m+1}
and non-negative integer

define

\displaystyle p^{\prime\prime}_{s,r,-1}\quad

\displaystyle\coloneqq\quad\left(1-\frac{m-s-r}{n}\right)\frac{m^{2}}{n^{2}}\kern 5.0pt,

\displaystyle p^{\prime\prime}_{s,r,+1}\quad

\displaystyle\coloneqq\quad\left(1-\frac{m^{2}}{n^{2}}\right)\frac{m-s-r}{n}\kern 5.0pt.

Note that

\displaystyle\Pr[W^{\prime\prime}_{t+1}-V^{\prime\prime}_{t+1}=a\,|\,W^{\prime\prime}_{t}=s+r,\,V^{\prime\prime}_{t}=r]\quad

\displaystyle=\quad\begin{cases}p^{\prime\prime}_{s,r,-1}&\text{if }a=s-1\\
p^{\prime\prime}_{s,r,+1}&\text{if }a=s+1\\
1-p^{\prime\prime}_{s,r,-1}-p^{\prime\prime}_{s,r,+1}&\text{if }a=s\\

Consequently, the transition probabilities of the random walk
(W^{\prime\prime}_{t}-V^{\prime\prime}_{t})

\displaystyle\Pr[W^{\prime\prime}_{t+1}-

\displaystyle V^{\prime\prime}_{t+1}=a\,|\,W^{\prime\prime}_{t}-V^{\prime\prime}_{t}=s]

\displaystyle=\quad\sum_{r\geq 0}\Pr[V^{\prime\prime}_{t}=r]\cdot\Pr[W^{\prime\prime}_{t}=s+r]\cdot\Pr[W^{\prime\prime}_{t+1}-V^{\prime\prime}_{t+1}=a\,|\,W^{\prime\prime}_{t}=s+r,\,V^{\prime\prime}_{t}=r]\kern 5.0pt.

We show below that

We start with

Lemma C.1
.

For any non-increasing sequence of positive numbers
a_{1},a_{2},\dotsc
, and positive integers
j_{1},j_{2}
such that
j_{1}\leq j_{2}
we have

\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{j_{1}}a_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{j_{2}}a_{i}}\quad\geq\quad\frac{j_{1}}{j_{2}}\kern 5.0pt.

Proof:

It suffices to show that

j_{2}\sum_{i=1}^{j_{1}}a_{i}\quad\geq\quad j_{1}\sum_{i=1}^{j_{2}}a_{i}\kern 5.0pt.

View each side as a sum of
j_{1}j_{2}
elements, given by an appropriate number of repetitions of
a_{i}
for each
i\in[j_{2}]
. Note that
j_{1}^{2}
elements given by
j_{1}
repetitions of each
a_{i}
for
i\in[j_{1}]
occur on both sides. Each remaining element on the left hand side is at least as large as every remaining element on the right.

Let
k,n
be positive integers such that
1<k<n
, and let
m\coloneqq n-k
.

Lemma C.2
.

If
t\coloneqq k\ln m+cn
for some
c\in\mathbb{R}
,
m\ln m\leq|c|n/10
and
1\leq m\leq n/40
, then

\frac{t}{n-5m-1}\quad\leq\quad\ln m+\max\{2c,c/3\}\kern 5.0pt.

Proof:

The claimed inequality is equivalent to

\displaystyle\frac{k\ln m+cn}{n-5m-1}-\ln m\quad

\displaystyle\leq\quad\max\left\{2c,c/3\right\}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\qquad\frac{(4m+1)\ln m+cn}{n-5m-1}\quad

\displaystyle\leq\quad\max\left\{2c,c/3\right\}\kern 5.0pt.

Using
1\leq m\leq n/40
,
m\ln m\leq\left|c\right|n/10
, we have

\frac{(4m+1)\ln m}{n-5m-1}\quad\leq\quad\frac{5m\ln m}{n-6m}\quad\leq\quad\frac{2\left|c\right|}{3}\kern 5.0pt.

Further, when
c\geq 0
,
cn/(n-5m-1)\leq cn/(n-6m)\leq 4c/3
, and when
c<0
,
cn/(n-5m-1)\leq c
. Combining these, we see that the claimed inequality holds.
