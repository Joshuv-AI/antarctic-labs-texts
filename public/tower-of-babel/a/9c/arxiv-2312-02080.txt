Abstract
In this study we derive novel optimal algorithms for joint power control and beamforming design in modern large-scale MIMO systems, such as those based on the cell-free massive MIMO and XL-MIMO concepts. In particular, motivated by the need for scalable system architectures, we formulate and solve nontrivial two-timescale extensions of the classical uplink power minimization and max-min fair resource allocation problems. In our formulations, we let the beamformers be functions mapping partial instantaneous channel state information (CSI) to beamforming weights, and we jointly optimize these functions and the power control coefficients based on long-term statistical CSI. This long-term approach mitigates the severe scalability issues of competing short-term iterative algorithms in the literature, where a central controller endowed with global instantaneous CSI must solve a complex optimization problem for every channel realization, hence imposing very demanding requirements in terms of computational complexity and signaling overhead. Moreover, our approach outperforms the available long-term approaches, which do not jointly optimize powers and beamformers. The obtained optimal long-term algorithms are then illustrated and compared against existing short-term and long-term algorithms via numerical simulations in a cell-free massive MIMO setup with different levels of cooperation.

Introduction

There is a growing consensus in both industry and academia that the physical layer of next-generation mobile communication networks will primarily rely on sophisticated multi-user multiple-input-multiple-output (MU-MIMO) systems for spectrally efficient multiple access in the lower (sub-6 GHz) and upper mid-band (7-24 GHz). The vast majority of these envisioned systems are rooted in the now established massive MIMO paradigm

Specifically, by deploying a large number of infrastructure antennas across the service area, these technologies are able to effectively multiplex many users in the same time-frequency resource using standard single-user channel codes, linear array processing (i.e., beamforming), and appropriate power control mechanisms

I-A

Conventional short-term approaches

To jointly optimize the beamformers and the power control coefficients, two popular criteria are the sum power minimization subject to signal-to-interference-to-noise (SINR) constraints, and the maximization of the minimum weighted SINR subject to power constraints (sometimes also referred to as the SINR balancing problem)
[
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
]
. By focusing on the uplink of a system with
K
single-antenna users and a base station equipped with
M
antennas, these two optimization problems are typically stated as

\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{p},\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{K}\end{subarray}}{\text{minimize}}\;\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}p_{k}\text{ s.t. }(\forall k\in\mathcal{K})~\mathsf{SINR}_{k}^{\mathsf{inst}}(\mathbf{H},\mathbf{v}_{k},\mathbf{p})\geq\gamma_{k},

(1)

and

\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{p},\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{K}\end{subarray}}{\text{maximize}}\;\min_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\frac{\mathsf{SINR}_{k}^{\mathsf{inst}}(\mathbf{H},\mathbf{v}_{k},\mathbf{p})}{\gamma_{k}}\text{ s.t. }(\forall k\in\mathcal{K})~p_{k}\leq P,

(2)

where
\mathcal{K}=\{1,\ldots,K\}
is the set of user indexes,
(\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{K})\in\mathbbmss{R}_{++}^{K}
and
P\in\mathbbmss{R}_{+}
are problem parameters, and
\mathsf{SINR}_{k}^{\mathsf{inst}}
denotes the instantaneous SINR of user
k
for a given MU-MIMO channel matrix
\mathbf{H}\in\mathbbmss{C}^{K\times M}
, power control coefficients
\mathbf{p}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{K})\in\mathbbmss{R}_{+}^{K}
, and receive beamformers
\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{K}\in\mathbbmss{C}^{M}
. The classical MU-MIMO literature provides many efficient and provably convergent algorithms for solving not only (
1
) and (
2
), but also many variants covering, for example, downlink transmission. Notably, these problems can be solved via fixed-point methods based on an axiomatic uplink power control framework introduced in
[
16
]
for sum power minimization problems, and extended in
[
17
]
to weighted max-min problems. Furthermore, although developed for the uplink, this framework can also be applied to related downlink problems by means of uplink-downlink duality arguments
[
12
,
14
,
15
]
. Embracing this abstract framework offers many advantages. In particular, it not only gives valuable insights into the network behavior at a high-level
[
18
,
19
,
20
]
, but it also gives access to powerful numerical techniques for addressing more involved variants of (
1
) and (
2
) without the need for ad-hoc proofs

15
,
21
,
22
,
23
]
.

In addition to sum power minimization and weighted max-min problems, another popular optimization criterion is the maximization of the weighted sum-rate subject to power constraints. However, solving weighted sum-rate maximization problems is notoriously challenging. In particular, in canonical MU-MIMO settings where interference is treated as noise, the sum-rate maximization problem is NP-hard in general
[
24
]
(taking aside restricted scenarios where specific symmetries or other channel characteristics simplify the problem). Therefore, either highly complex global optimization methods
[
25
,
26
]
or efficient suboptimal algorithms
[
27
,
28
]
are typically considered in the literature. Since in this study we focus on efficient optimal designs, we consider only sum power minimization and weighted max-min problems, and we leave weighted sum-rate maximization problems for future work.

I-B

Long-term joint power control and beamforming design

The above conventional approaches, which we refer to as the
short-term
approaches, suffer from the following issues:

•

First, (
1
) and (
2
) require running an iterative algorithm
for every realization
of
\mathbf{H}
, which imposes demanding requirements in terms of computational complexity.

•

Second, (
1
) and (
2
) require collecting the problem input
\mathbf{H}
and distributing the solution
(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{K})
across all the appropriate network entities
for every channel realization
, which results in high signaling overhead.

Importantly, note that all the above operations must be completed under strict latency constraints, since the optimized powers
\mathbf{p}
must be conveyed to the users within each channel coherence block (in time and frequency). The main working assumption of this study, which is also ubiquitous in the massive MIMO literature (see, e.g.,
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
,
4
,
10
]
among others), is that the above challenges make short-term approaches impractical for large-scale MIMO systems, which calls for alternative approaches.

Leaving heuristics aside, we can circumvent the above challenges by considering optimization problems involving ergodic rate expressions of the type

\mathsf{E}\left[\log_{2}\left(1+\mathsf{SINR}_{k}^{\mathsf{inst}}(\mathbbm{H},\mathbbm{v}_{k},\mathbf{p}\right)\right],

(3)

where
\mathbbm{H}
is a random channel matrix,
\mathbf{p}
is a deterministic power vector, and where the beamformers
\mathbbm{v}_{k}
are
functions
of the available instantaneous channel state information (CSI). This two-timescale formulation, which we refer to as the
long-term
approach, has the advantage that the power vector
\mathbf{p}
can be optimized only sporadically
for many channel realizations
based on relatively slowly time-varying channel statistics. The same advantage applies to beamforming design, i.e., the optimization of the
functions
mapping CSI to instantaneous beamforming coefficients. In addition, the long-term approach facilitates the introduction of non-trivial beamforming constraints modeling limited CSI sharing in distributed architectures, using the notion of information constraints proposed in
[
29
]
. Introducing these information constraints is particularly useful for optimizing cell-free networks in scenarios where beamformers must be computed locally at each access point (AP) based on local or partially shared CSI
[
3
,
4
]
. In particular, note that common AP clustering techniques
[
30
,
4
]
alone are not sufficient to model these scenarios. However, the main drawback of the long-term approach based on (
3
) is that it typically leads to intractable functional optimization problems. In fact, as of today, no optimal method for long-term joint power control and beamforming design is available in the literature. In this work, we address this limitation and present the first optimal yet tractable long-term method that overcomes the above challenges of short-term approaches.

Main contribution and related long-term approaches

Building on the related literature on long-term power control in (cell-free) massive MIMO systems

To relate the main contribution of the present study with existing long-term approaches, we recall that iterative algorithms for long-term power control under fixed beamforming design based on the UatF bound (or its related downlink version) have been extensively studied in both the cellular and cell-free massive MIMO literature (see, e.g.,

Problem statement

We consider the uplink of a large-scale MIMO system composed by
K
single-antenna users indexed by
\mathcal{K}:=\{1,\ldots,K\}
, and a radio access infrastructure equipped with
M
antennas indexed by
\mathcal{M}:=\{1,\ldots,M\}
. The
M
infrastructure antennas may be split among many geographically distributed access points equipped with a relatively low number of antennas as in the cell-free massive MIMO concept
[
3

4
]
, or colocated in a single array as in the XL-MIMO concept
[
5
]
. By assuming for each time-frequency resource a standard synchronous

(UatF) inner bound on the ergodic capacity region

(4)

\mathsf{SINR}_{k}(\mathbbm{v}_{k},\mathbf{p}):=\resizebox{15600900}{}{$\dfrac{p_{k}|\mathsf{E}[\mathbbm{h}_{k}^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbbm{v}_{k}]|^{2}}{p_{k}\mathsf{V}(\mathbbm{h}_{k}^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbbm{v}_{k})+\underset{j\neq k}{\sum}p_{j}\mathsf{E}[|\mathbbm{h}_{j}^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbbm{v}_{k}|^{2}]+\mathsf{E}[\|\mathbbm{v}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}]}$},

is a deterministic vector of transmit powers,

\mathcal{V}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{V}_{K}\subseteq\mathcal{H}^{M}
are given
information constraints
(formally defined in Section
II-B
) that are imposed to limit the joint processing and information sharing burden, thus enabling scalable designs for large-scale MIMO systems. For example, as we will see, the set
\mathcal{V}_{k}
can be used to limit the dependency of the entries of
\mathbbm{v}_{k}
to portions of the CSI available at the infrastructure side, and also the number of antennas that are serving user
k
. Note that letting
\mathcal{V}_{k}=\mathcal{H}^{M}
(i.e., omitting the information constraint) allows
\mathbbm{v}_{k}
to be essentially any function of the global channel
\mathbbm{H}
, which is not practical. However, we remark that a solution to (
5
) or (
6
) is generally not known even by omitting the information constraints.

Remark 2
.

The long-term power control algorithm in
[
4
, Algorithm 7.1]
solves a particular case of (
6
) recovered by letting
\mathcal{V}_{k}=\{\mathbbm{v}_{k}^{\mathsf{fix}}\}
for some fixed
\mathbbm{v}^{\mathsf{fix}}_{k}\in\mathcal{H}^{M}
, i.e., by restricting the set of feasible beamformers to a singleton (e.g.,
\mathcal{V}_{k}=\{\mathbbm{h}_{k}\}
for the case of maximum-ratio combining with perfect CSI). Similarly, the long-term joint power control and LSFD design algorithm in
[
7
]
solves a particular case of (
6
) recovered by letting
\mathcal{V}_{k}=\{\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{a}_{k})\mathbbm{v}_{k}^{\mathsf{fix}}~|~\mathbf{a}_{k}\in\mathbbmss{C}^{M}\}
for some
\mathbbm{v}_{k}^{\mathsf{fix}}\in\mathcal{H}^{M}
. Additional comparisons are given in Section
IV
.

The proposed problems share the following features:

•

The power control vector
\mathbf{p}
is kept fixed for the whole duration of the transmission, i.e., it does not depend on the small-scale fading realizations, and it is optimized based on channel statistics.

•

The beamformers
(\mathbbm{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbbm{v}_{K})
adapt to small-scale fading based on the CSI specified by the information constraints. However, their design, i.e., the function mapping CSI realizations to the corresponding beamforming coefficients, is optimized based on channel statistics as for the power control vector. Note that this leads to functional optimization problems in infinite-dimensional vector spaces, for which standard solvers in finite-dimensional Euclidean spaces (e.g.,
cvx
) are not applicable.

Compared to traditional short-term approaches that solve an optimization problem such as (
1
) and (
2
) for each channel (or global CSI) realization, the long-term approach in this study is particularly attractive for large-scale MIMO systems due to the following reasons:

•

First, the power control vector and beamforming design can be updated following large-scale fading variations, which are typically several order of magnitude slower than small-scale fading variations.

•

Second, the beamforming design space covers distributed implementations where the computation of the beamformers can be split among multiple distributed processing units with limited CSI sharing capabilities, each controlling a subset of the
M
infrastructure antennas.

Remark 3
.

A known challenge in long-term approaches is the need to identify and track some form of channel statistics that can be used to produce effective solutions. In contrast, short-term approaches can be often implemented based on minimal or even no statistical information. However, the main working assumption of this study is that short-term approaches are not feasible in large-scale MIMO systems, thus making long-term approaches not only an option but rather a necessity.

II-B

Information constraints

In distributed beamforming implementations, each processing unit must often form its portion of the beamformers on the basis of different CSI. In particular, the distributed processing units typically acquire CSI from locally received pilot signals, and then further share these measurements across the network. However, due to limited interconnections, these measurements may not be perfectly shared. As an extreme example, in distributed cell-free massive MIMO networks the access points are typically assumed to compute the beamformers on the basis of only
local
CSI
[
3
]
, i.e., without any CSI sharing. However, to date, most related studies do not formally model this type of constraint, so, strictly speaking, they do not truly consider distributed beamforming optimization. To address this limitation, we model constraints related to imperfect CSI sharing following the approach introduced in
[
29
]
. We first partition the
M=NL
infrastructure antennas into
L
groups of
N
antennas, and assume that the corresponding portions of the beamformers are computed by
L
separate processing units. Given an arbitrarily distributed tuple
(\mathbbm{H},S_{1},\ldots,S_{L})
, where
S_{l}
is the available CSI at the
l
th processing unit, we then let

\mathcal{V}_{k}:=\mathcal{H}_{1}^{N}\times\ldots\times\mathcal{H}_{L}^{N},

(7)

where
\mathcal{H}_{l}^{N}\subseteq\mathcal{H}^{N}
denotes the set of
N
-tuples of
\Sigma_{l}
-measurable functions
\Omega\to\mathbbmss{C}^{N}
satisfying
(\forall\mathbbm{x}\in\mathcal{H}_{l}^{N})

\mathsf{E}[\|\mathbbm{x}\|_{2}^{2}]<\infty
, and where
\Sigma_{l}\subseteq\Sigma
is the sub-
\sigma
-algebra induced by the CSI
S_{l}
, which is also called the
information subfield
of the
l
th processing unit. The interested reader is referred to
[
39
]
for an introduction to the measure theoretical notions used in the above definitions. However, we stress that these notions are by no means required for understanding the key results of this study. The crucial point is that, informally, the constraint set
\mathcal{V}_{k}
enforces the portion of
\mathbbm{v}_{k}
computed by the
l
th processing unit to be a function of only
S_{l}
.

Furthermore, another typical constraint for enabling scalable large-scale MIMO designs is that only a subset of all infrastructure antennas should be used for decoding the message of a given user. For example, cell-free massive MIMO networks are expected to implement some type of user-centric rule
[
30
]
, which allocates to each user a cluster of serving access points. As shown in
[
40
,
41
]
, this constraint can be modeled by forcing the corresponding portions of
\mathbbm{v}_{k}
to zero, i.e., by replacing
\mathcal{H}_{l}^{N}
in (
7
) with the set
\{\mathbf{0}_{N}~(\text{a.s.})\}
if the
l
th processing unit is not serving the
k
th user. Considering this replacement, we remark that
\mathcal{V}_{k}
is a subspace of the real vector space
\mathcal{H}^{M}

[
41
]
. Concrete examples are provided in Section
IV
.

III-A

Power control with implicit beamforming optimization

We now map Problem (
5
) and Problem (
6
) to equivalent power control problems where the optimization of the beamformers is implicit. More precisely, by exploiting the fact that uplink SINRs are only coupled through the power vector
\mathbf{p}
, we consider the following optimization problems:

\displaystyle\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{p}\in\mathbbmss{R}_{+}^{K}\end{subarray}}{\text{minimize}}

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{p}\|

(8)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle(\forall k\in\mathcal{K})~u_{k}(\mathbf{p})\geq\gamma_{k};

and

\displaystyle\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{p}\in\mathbbmss{R}_{+}^{K}\end{subarray}}{\text{maximize}}

\displaystyle\min_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\gamma_{k}^{-1}u_{k}(\mathbf{p})

(9)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{p}\|\leq P,

where
\|\cdot\|
denotes a given monotone norm, and where, for each user
k\in\mathcal{K}
, we define the utility

(\forall\mathbf{p}\in\mathbbmss{R}_{+}^{K})~u_{k}(\mathbf{p}):=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbbm{v}_{k}\in\mathcal{V}_{k}\\
\mathsf{E}[\|\mathbbm{v}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}]\neq 0\end{subarray}}\mathsf{SINR}_{k}(\mathbbm{v}_{k},\mathbf{p}).

(10)

The above problems can be formally connected to the original long-term joint power control and beamforming design problems as follows.

Proposition 1
.

Let
\mathbf{p}^{\star}
be a solution to Problem (
8
) (resp. Problem (
9
)) with utilities in (
10
) and norm
\|\cdot\|=\|\cdot\|_{1}
(resp.,
\|\cdot\|=\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
). If
(\forall k~\in\mathcal{K})

\exists\mathbbm{v}^{\star}_{k}\in\mathcal{V}_{k}
such that
u_{k}(\mathbf{p}^{\star})=\mathsf{SINR}_{k}(\mathbbm{v}^{\star}_{k},\mathbf{p}^{\star})
(i.e., the supremum in (
10
) is attained), then
(\mathbf{p}^{\star},\mathbbm{v}_{1}^{\star},\ldots,\mathbbm{v}_{K}^{\star})
solves Problem (
5
) (resp. Problem (
6
)).

Proof.

Let us focus on the connection between (
5
) and (
8
). By construction,
(\mathbf{p}^{\star},\mathbbm{v}_{1}^{\star},\ldots,\mathbbm{v}_{K}^{\star})
satisfies all constraints of Problem (5). Hence,
\|\mathbf{p}^{\star}\|_{1}\geq\tilde{p}
, where
\tilde{p}
denotes the optimum of Problem (5). Furthermore, let
(\mathbf{p},\mathbbm{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbbm{v}_{K})
satisfy all constraints of Problem (5). Then, by definition of supremum, we have that
(\forall k\in\mathcal{K})~u_{k}(\mathbf{p})\geq\mathsf{SINR}_{k}(\mathbbm{v}_{k},\mathbf{p})\geq\gamma_{k}
, i.e.,
\mathbf{p}
also satisfies all constraints of Problem (8). This in turns implies that
\tilde{p}\geq\|\mathbf{p}^{\star}\|_{1}
. By combining the above arguments, we conclude that
\tilde{p}=\|\mathbf{p}^{\star}\|_{1}
and that
(\mathbf{p}^{\star},\mathbbm{v}_{1}^{\star},\ldots,\mathbbm{v}_{K}^{\star})
solves Problem (5). The connection between (
6
) and (
9
) follows from similar arguments.

By definition,
(\forall k\in\mathcal{K})~(\forall\mathbf{p}\in\mathbbmss{R}_{+}^{K})

u_{k}(\mathbf{p})\geq\mathsf{SINR}_{k}(\mathbbm{v}_{k},\mathbf{p})
for all
\mathbbm{v}_{k}\in\mathcal{V}_{k}
satisfying
\mathsf{E}[\|\mathbbm{v}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}]\neq 0
. Furthermore,
\mathsf{SINR}_{k}(\mathbbm{v}_{k},\mathbf{p})
does not depend on the beamformer
\mathbbm{v}_{j}
of any other user

. These two properties imply that the optimum of Problem (
8
) (resp. Problem (
9
)) with utilities in (
10
) and norm
\|\cdot\|=\|\cdot\|_{1}
(resp.,
\|\cdot\|=\|\cdot\|_{\infty}

) (resp. Problem (

∎

We now demonstrate a key structural property of the utility in (
10
), which allows us to connect Problem (
8
) and Problem (
9
) to the axiomatic power control framework in
[
16
,
17
]

Proposition 2
.

Assume the technical condition
(\forall k~\in\mathcal{K})

\mathcal{V}^{\prime}_{k}:=\{\mathbbm{v}_{k}\in\mathcal{V}_{k}~|~\mathsf{E}[\mathbbm{h}_{k}^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbbm{v}_{k}]\neq 0\}\neq\emptyset
. Then, for all
k\in\mathcal{K}
and
\mathbf{p}\in\mathbbmss{R}_{+}^{K}
, the utility (
10
) takes the form
u_{k}(\mathbf{p})=\frac{p_{k}}{f_{k}(\mathbf{p})}
, where

~f_{k}(\mathbf{p}):=\inf_{\mathbbm{v}_{k}\in\mathcal{V}^{\prime}_{k}}\dfrac{p_{k}\mathsf{V}(\mathbbm{h}_{k}^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbbm{v}_{k})+\underset{j\neq k}{\sum}p_{j}\mathsf{E}[|\mathbbm{h}_{j}^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbbm{v}_{k}|^{2}]+\mathsf{E}[\|\mathbbm{v}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}]}{|\mathsf{E}[\mathbbm{h}_{k}^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbbm{v}_{k}]|^{2}}

is a positive concave function.

Proof.

The following steps hold for all
k\in\mathcal{K}
and
\mathbf{p}\in\mathbbmss{R}_{+}^{K}
. By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we first observe that the bound
\mathsf{SINR}_{k}(\mathbbm{v}_{k},\mathbf{p})\leq\frac{p_{k}|\mathsf{E}[\mathbbm{h}_{k}^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbbm{v}_{k}]|^{2}}{\mathsf{E}[\|\mathbbm{v}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}]}\leq\frac{p_{k}\mathsf{E}[\|\mathbbm{h}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}]\mathsf{E}[\|\mathbbm{v}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}]}{\mathsf{E}[\|\mathbbm{v}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}]}=p_{k}\mathsf{E}[\|\mathbbm{h}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}]
holds for all
\mathbbm{v}_{k}\in\mathcal{V}_{k}
such that
\mathsf{E}[\|\mathbbm{v}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}]\neq 0
. Then, by noticing that
\mathbbm{v}_{k}\in\mathcal{V}^{\prime}_{k}\implies\mathsf{E}[\|\mathbbm{v}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}]\neq 0
, and hence that
\emptyset\neq\mathcal{V}^{\prime}_{k}\subset\{\mathbbm{v}_{k}\in\mathcal{V}_{k}~|~\mathsf{E}[\|\mathbbm{v}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}]\neq 0\}
, we obtain

\displaystyle u_{k}(\mathbf{p})=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbbm{v}_{k}\in\mathcal{V}_{k}\\
\mathsf{E}[\|\mathbbm{v}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}]\neq 0\end{subarray}}\mathsf{SINR}_{k}(\mathbbm{v}_{k},\mathbf{p})

\displaystyle=\max\Big\{\sup_{\mathbbm{v}_{k}\in\mathcal{V}^{\prime}_{k}}\mathsf{SINR}_{k}(\mathbbm{v}_{k},\mathbf{p}),\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbbm{v}_{k}\in\mathcal{V}_{k}\backslash\mathcal{V}_{k}^{\prime}\\
\mathsf{E}[\|\mathbbm{v}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}]\neq 0\end{subarray}}\mathsf{SINR}_{k}(\mathbbm{v}_{k},\mathbf{p})\Big\}

\displaystyle=\max\Big\{\sup_{\mathbbm{v}_{k}\in\mathcal{V}^{\prime}_{k}}\mathsf{SINR}_{k}(\mathbbm{v}_{k},\mathbf{p}),0\Big\}=\sup_{\mathbbm{v}_{k}\in\mathcal{V}^{\prime}_{k}}\mathsf{SINR}_{k}(\mathbbm{v}_{k},\mathbf{p})

\displaystyle=p_{k}\sup_{\mathbbm{v}_{k}\in\mathcal{V}^{\prime}_{k}}\dfrac{|\mathsf{E}[\mathbbm{h}_{k}^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbbm{v}_{k}]|^{2}}{p_{k}\mathsf{V}(\mathbbm{h}_{k}^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbbm{v}_{k})+\underset{j\neq k}{\sum}p_{j}\mathsf{E}[|\mathbbm{h}_{j}^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbbm{v}_{k}|^{2}]+\mathsf{E}[\|\mathbbm{v}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}]}

\displaystyle=p_{k}/f_{k}(\mathbf{p}),

where the second equality follows from elementary properties on the supremum of the union of bounded sets, and where the third equality follows by definition of
\mathcal{V}_{k}^{\prime}
. Furthermore, we observe that
f_{k}
is concave because it is constructed by taking the point-wise infimum of affine (and hence concave) functions. Finally, again by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we observe that
f_{k}(\mathbf{p})\geq\inf_{\mathbbm{v}_{k}\in\mathcal{V}^{\prime}_{k}}\frac{\mathsf{E}[\|\mathbbm{v}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}]}{|\mathsf{E}[\mathbbm{h}_{k}^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbbm{v}_{k}]|^{2}}\geq\frac{1}{\mathsf{E}[\|\mathbbm{h}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}]}>0
, where the last inequality follows since
\mathcal{V}_{k}^{\prime}\neq\emptyset\implies\mathsf{E}[\|\mathbbm{h}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}]\neq 0
.
∎

The main implication of Proposition

Remark 4
.

The condition
\mathcal{V}_{k}^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
holds for most fading and CSI distributions of practical interest. However, note that

does not hold if the infrastructure has no CSI (equivalently, if

and

are independent) and, in addition, if the channels have zero mean. In this case,

holds. This is related to one of the main drawbacks of the UatF bounding technique, which requires either sufficient CSI or a strong channel mean to produce nonzero rate bounds.

III-B

SINR maximization via MSE minimization

Solving the SINR maximization problems to compute the functions
u_{k}
in (
10
) appears quite challenging for the following reasons: (i) the non-convex fractional objective involving expectations in both the numerator and denominator, and (ii) the information constraints. However, the next proposition shows that (i) is not the main challenge, because we can consider an alternative and simpler convex utility, in the same spirit of the known relation between SINR and MSE for fixed channel matrices. Let us consider for all
k\in\mathcal{K}
the MSE between the signal
x_{k}\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,1)
of user
k
and its soft estimate
\hat{x}_{k}:=\mathbbm{v}_{k}^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbbm{y}
obtained by processing the output of the uplink channel
\mathbbm{y}:=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\sqrt{p_{k}}\mathbbm{h}_{k}x_{k}+\mathbbm{n}
with noise
\mathbbm{n}\sim\mathcal{CN}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})
, where the noise and all user signals are mutually independent and independent of
(\mathbbm{H},\mathbbm{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbbm{v}_{K})
. Specifically, let
(\forall k\in\mathcal{K})(\forall\mathbbm{v}_{k}\in\mathcal{V}_{k})(\forall\mathbf{p}\in\mathbbmss{R}_{+}^{K})

\begin{split}\mathsf{MSE}_{k}(\mathbbm{v}_{k},\mathbf{p}):=&\;\mathsf{E}[|x_{k}-\hat{x}_{k}|^{2}]\\
=&\;\mathsf{E}\left[\|\mathbf{P}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbbm{H}^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbbm{v}_{k}-\mathbf{e}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}\right]+\mathsf{E}\left[\|\mathbbm{v}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}\right],\end{split}

where
\mathbf{P}:=\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{p})
, and where the second equality can be verified via simple manipulations. We then have:

Proposition 5
.

For given
k\in\mathcal{K}
and
\mathbf{p}\in\mathbbmss{R}_{+}^{K}
, consider the optimization problem

\underset{\mathbbm{v}_{k}\in\mathcal{V}_{k}}{\emph{minimize}}~\mathsf{MSE}_{k}(\mathbbm{v}_{k},\mathbf{p}).

(11)

Problem (
11
) has a unique solution
\mathbbm{v}_{k}^{\star}\in\mathcal{V}_{k}
. Furthermore,

\mathsf{MSE}_{k}(\mathbbm{v}_{k}^{\star},\mathbf{p})=\frac{1}{1+u_{k}(\mathbf{p})}.

Moreover, if
\mathcal{V}_{k}^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
and
p_{k}>0
, then
u_{k}(\mathbf{p})=\mathsf{SINR}_{k}\left(\mathbbm{v}_{k}^{\star},\mathbf{p}\right)
.

Proof.

The proof follows similar steps as in the proof of
[
41
, Proposition 8]
, and they are reported here for better clarity. Existence and uniqueness of the solution follows from
[
29
, Lemma 4]
. For the second statement, we observe that

\displaystyle\mathsf{MSE}_{k}(\mathbbm{v}^{\star}_{k},\mathbf{p})=\inf_{\mathbbm{v}_{k}\in\mathcal{V}_{k}}\mathsf{MSE}_{k}(\mathbbm{v}_{k},\mathbf{p})

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{=}\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbbm{v}_{k}\in\mathcal{V}_{k}\\
\mathsf{E}[\|\mathbbm{v}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}]\neq 0\end{subarray}}\inf_{\beta\in\mathbbmss{C}}\mathsf{MSE}_{k}(\beta\mathbbm{v}_{k},\mathbf{p})

\displaystyle\overset{(b)}{=}\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbbm{v}_{k}\in\mathcal{V}_{k}\\
\mathsf{E}[\|\mathbbm{v}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}]\neq 0\end{subarray}}1-\dfrac{p_{k}|\mathsf{E}[\mathbbm{h}_{k}^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbbm{v}_{k}]|^{2}}{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{K}}p_{j}\mathsf{E}[|\mathbbm{h}_{j}^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbbm{v}_{k}|^{2}]+\mathsf{E}\left[\|\mathbbm{v}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}\right]}

\displaystyle=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbbm{v}_{k}\in\mathcal{V}_{k}\\
\mathsf{E}[\|\mathbbm{v}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}]\neq 0\end{subarray}}\dfrac{1}{1+\mathsf{SINR}_{k}(\mathbbm{v}_{k},\mathbf{p})}=\dfrac{1}{1+u_{k}(\mathbf{p})},

where
(a)
follows from
(\forall\beta\in\mathbbmss{C})(\forall\mathbbm{v}_{k}\in\mathcal{V}_{k})

\beta\mathbbm{v}_{k}\in\mathcal{V}_{k}
, and where
(b)
follows from standard minimization of scalar quadratic forms (see, e.g.,
[
41
, Appendix D]
).
It remains to verify that if
\mathcal{V}_{k}^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
and
p_{k}>0
, then
\mathbbm{v}_{k}^{\star}
also attains the supremum in (
10
). This can be verified by first observing that, by Proposition
2
,
u_{k}(\mathbf{p})>0\implies\mathsf{MSE}_{k}(\mathbbm{v}^{\star}_{k},\mathbf{p})<1\implies\mathsf{E}[\|\mathbbm{v}_{k}^{\star}\|_{2}^{2}]\neq 0
, where the last step follows since
\mathsf{E}[\|\mathbbm{v}_{k}^{\star}\|_{2}^{2}]=0\iff\mathbbm{v}_{k}^{\star}=\mathbf{0}\text{ a.s.}\implies\mathsf{MSE}_{k}(\mathbbm{v}^{\star}_{k},\mathbf{p})=1
. Then, the proof is concluded by following similar steps as in the above chain of equalities:
\mathsf{MSE}_{k}(\mathbbm{v}_{k}^{\star},\mathbf{p})=\inf_{\beta\in\mathbbmss{C}}\mathsf{MSE}_{k}(\beta\mathbbm{v}_{k}^{\star},\mathbf{p})=(1+\mathsf{SINR}_{k}(\mathbbm{v}_{k}^{\star},\mathbf{p}))^{-1}
.
∎

In the particular case where all users are served by all antennas based on perfect knowledge of the channel
\mathbbm{H}
(trivial information constraints), the solution to Problem (
11
) is simply given by the well-known MMSE beamforming solution

(\forall k\in\mathcal{K})~\mathbbm{v}_{k}=\left(\mathbbm{H}\mathbf{P}\mathbbm{H}^{\mathsf{H}}+\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}\mathbbm{h}_{k}\sqrt{p_{k}}.

(12)

Variations of (
12
) are readily obtained also for the case of imperfect yet perfectly shared CSI among all processing units serving each user, under mild assumptions on the channel estimation error model (see, e.g.,
[
2
, Corollary 4.3]
or the example in Section
IV
).

[Axiomatic framework for power control]

In this appendix we provide an up-to-date introduction to the axiomatic framework for power control in
[
16
,
17
]
. In addition to offering a concise self-contained background for the main results of this study, this appendix may be of independent interest, since it uncovers overlooked connections within the existing literature. In more detail, we investigate networks consisting of
K
users denoted by the set
\mathcal{K}:=\{1,\ldots,K\}
. To each user
k\in\mathcal{K}
we associate an utility function
u_{k}:\mathbbmss{R}^{K}_{+}\to\mathbbmss{R}_{+}
, and we assume that all functions
(u_{k})_{k\in\mathcal{K}}
take a common argument
\mathbf{p}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{K})\in\mathbbmss{R}_{+}^{K}
, which we interpret as the vector of powers of all users. We consider power control problems involving the above utilities and a given monotone norm
\|\cdot\|
, e.g., to impose power constraint of the type
(\forall\mathbf{p}\in\mathbbmss{R}_{+}^{K})~\|\mathbf{p}\|\leq P
, where
P
is a given constant. To keep the utility optimization problems tractable while maintaining the framework reasonably general, we need to restrict the class of utility functions. To this end, we leverage the concept of standard interference functions, defined as follows:

Definition 1
.

[
16
]
A function
f:\mathbbmss{R}^{K}_{+}\to\mathbbmss{R}_{++}
is said to be a
standard interference function
if the following properties hold:

(i)

(\forall\mathbf{p}\in\mathbbmss{R}^{K}_{+})(\forall\mathbf{q}\in\mathbbmss{R}^{K}_{+})~\mathbf{p}\geq\mathbf{q}\Rightarrow f(\mathbf{p})\geq f(\mathbf{q})
; and

(ii)

(\forall\mathbf{p}\in\mathbbmss{R}^{K}_{+})

(\forall\alpha>1)

\alpha{f}(\mathbf{p})>f(\alpha\mathbf{p})
.

Likewise, a mapping
T:\mathbbmss{R}_{+}^{K}\to\mathbbmss{R}_{++}^{K}:\mathbf{p}\mapsto(f_{1}(\mathbf{p}),\ldots,f_{N}(\mathbf{p}))
is said to be a standard interference (SI) mapping if each coordinate function
f_{k}:\mathbbmss{R}^{K}_{+}\to\mathbbmss{R}_{++}
(
k=1,\ldots,K
) is a standard interference function.

Remark 6
.

Standard interference functions
f:\mathbbmss{R}^{K}_{+}\to\mathbbmss{R}_{++}
are continuous on
\mathbbmss{R}_{++}^{K}
, but they are not necessarily continuous on the boundary
\mathbbmss{R}^{K}_{+}\backslash\mathbbmss{R}_{++}^{K}
. Nevertheless, for all practical purposes, hereafter we can tacitly assume continuity. The reason is that the restriction
f|_{\mathbbmss{R}_{++}^{K}}:\mathbbmss{R}^{K}_{++}\to\mathbbmss{R}_{++}:\mathbf{p}\mapsto f(\mathbf{p})
of
f
to
{\mathbbmss{R}_{++}^{K}}
can be continuously extended to
\mathbbmss{R}_{+}^{K}

[
47
, Theorem 5.1.5]
.

Identifying standard interference functions directly from the definition is not necessarily easy. However, the next proposition provides a simple technique that can assist us in this task.

Proposition 6
.

Let
f:\mathbbmss{R}^{K}_{+}\to\mathbbmss{R}_{++}
be a (positive) concave function (not necessarily everywhere continuous). Then
f
is a standard interference function (see
[
19
, Proposition 1]
for a simple proof of this standard result).

For later reference, we say that a mapping
T:\mathbbmss{R}_{+}^{K}\to\mathbbmss{R}_{++}^{K}
is a positive concave mapping if it can be written as
(\forall\mathbf{p}\in\mathbbmss{R}_{+}^{K})~T(\mathbf{p})=(f_{1}(\mathbf{p}),\ldots,f_{K}(\mathbf{p}))
, where, for every
k\in\mathcal{K}
,
f_{k}:\mathbbmss{R}^{K}_{+}\to\mathbbmss{R}_{++}
is a (positive) concave function. We now have in place all the required definitions to describe the utility functions covered by the framework.

Assumption 1
.

The utility function
u_{k}
of each user
k\in\mathcal{K}
takes the form

u_{k}:\mathbbmss{R}_{+}^{K}\to\mathbbmss{R}_{+}:\mathbf{p}\mapsto\dfrac{p_{k}}{f_{k}(\mathbf{p})},

where
f_{k}:\mathbbmss{R}_{+}^{K}\to\mathbbmss{R}_{++}
is a
continuous
