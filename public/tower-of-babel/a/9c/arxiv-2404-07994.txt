The Choquet-like operator with respect to an admissible order as a tool for aggregating multivalued data Journal: Fuzzy Sets and Systems
Michał Boczek, Tomasz Józefiak, Marek Kaluszka, Andrzej Okolewski

Abstract
In this paper, we propose a new generalization of the classical discrete Choquet integral to the multivalued framework in terms of an admissible order that refines the natural partial order on the considered value set. The new Choquet-like operator takes as input a finite number of values of a given type, in particular real numbers, intervals, and vectors, and returns a single output value of the same type as the input values. We give necessary and sufficient conditions for the operator to be monotone with respect to the admissible order. We then provide a complete characterization of the Choquet-like operator as an aggregation function with respect to the admissible order and study its selected special cases.

Introduction

Information fusion is central to both theoretical and applied research. In recent years, there has been a growing interest in the application of fuzzy integrals to data fusion, with a particular focus on the discrete Choquet integral. The integral has proven to be extremely useful in various machine learning problems
[
1
,
17
,
22
]
. This has led to several generalizations of the Choquet integral in the real number setting being proposed in the literature, resulting in concepts such as
the CQO-integrals
[
2
]
,
\displaystyle\mathrm{C}_{\mathrm{T}}
-integrals
[
19
]
,
CC-integrals
[
28
]
,
d-Choquet integrals
[
14
]
,
and
\displaystyle\mathrm{C}_{\mathrm{F_{1}F_{2}}}
- integrals
[
27
]
.
A detailed survey of many of these integrals can be found in
[
19
]
.
A unified approach to integration in the Choquet sense in the real number setting was presented in
[
5
,
9
]
, introducing the concept of an operator based on conditional aggregation and relation operators.
This operator
includes many Choquet-like integrals known in the literature, in particular those in
[
2
,
19
,
28
,
14
,
27
]
.

Recent research has increasingly focused on the generalization of classical fuzzy set theory
[
40
]
to include interval-valued fuzzy sets
[
36
]

and
\displaystyle n
-dimensional fuzzy sets
[
3
]
. The degrees of membership for such extensions are certain sets that consist of two or more real numbers.
Several generalizations of the Choquet integral in multivalued settings have been proposed,
in particular for the interval setting
[
13
]
, the
\displaystyle n
-dimensional vector setting
[
20
,
24
]
, and the
\displaystyle n
-dimensional interval setting
[
33
]
.
The construction of such extensions requires, among other things, overcoming problems arising from the fact that the natural product order on a set of vectors with real coordinates is not a linear order.
Bustince et al.
[
12
]
tackled this problem in the interval-valued setting by introducing the concept of
admissible orders, i.e., total orders that refine the product order.

The main reason for using the standard Choquet integral
[
16
]
for data aggregation is its native properties.
It is desirable that the functionals that are extensions of the Choquet integral inherit its original
properties.
Monotonicity, the fundamental axiom of the aggregation function, is one of these properties.
Its verification is a major challenge in determining whether an operator qualifies as an aggregation function.
Among the various generalized Choquet integrals proposed in the literature, only some satisfy the monotonicity condition, while some others fulfill a weakened monotonicity condition, such as the directional monotonicity or ordered directional monotonicity
[
19
]
.
A full characterization of the monotonicity condition for two important variants of the extended Choquet-like operator in the
real number framework, which as special cases include the operators studied in
[
11
,
23
,
32
]
,
along with the necessary and sufficient conditions that determine when the analyzed operators qualify as aggregation functions, are presented in
[
8
]
.

In this paper we focus on the aggregation of a finite number of data of a given type, e.g., real-valued, interval-valued, or
\displaystyle n
-dimensional-valued, from the perspective of Choquet integral theory.
We introduce the notion of a Choquet-like operator with respect to an admissible order, which refines the natural order on the considered data set
[
12
,
33
]
.
This operator extends the discrete Choquet integral to the multivalued framework, replacing subtraction and multiplication by a general function, and addition by an operation compatible with the admissible order.
We characterize the classes of all functions for which the Choquet-like operator is: (a) well defined, i.e., invariant with respect to permutations of the input data, (b) monotone
with respect to the admissible order.
Finally, we give a complete characterization of the Choquet-like operator as an aggregation function with respect to the admissible order, and study some special cases in detail.

The structure of the paper is as follows. In Section
2
we present some preliminary definitions and results which are necessary for the rest of the paper.
In Section
3
we introduce
a Choquet-like operator with respect to an admissible
order and study its
well-definedness and monotonicity. In Section
4
we provide necessary and sufficient conditions for the operator to be an
aggregation function, and analyze these conditions in detail for two important particular variants of the operator.
To improve readability, we move the well-definedness proof and all related monotonicity proofs to Appendix.

2
Preliminaries

Let
\displaystyle{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
and
\displaystyle{\mathbb{N}}
be the sets of non-negative real numbers and positive integers, respectively.
By
\displaystyle[n]
we denote the set
\displaystyle\{1,\ldots,n\}
for
\displaystyle n\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
Let
\displaystyle\mathsf{U}
be a non-empty universal set,
sometimes called a reference set or universe of discourse.

2.1
Ordered sets

A
partial order

\displaystyle\preceq_{\mathrm{p}}
on
\displaystyle\mathsf{U}
is a binary relation on
\displaystyle\mathsf{U}
, which is:

•

reflexive:
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}\preceq_{\mathrm{p}}\mathbf{x}
for any
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}\in\mathsf{U}
,

•

antisymmetric: for all
\displaystyle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}\in\mathsf{U}
,
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}\preceq_{\mathrm{p}}\mathbf{z}
and
\displaystyle\mathbf{z}\preceq_{\mathrm{p}}\mathbf{x}
implies
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{z}
,

•

transitive: for all
\displaystyle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z}\in\mathsf{U}
, if
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}\preceq_{\mathrm{p}}\mathbf{y}
and
\displaystyle\mathbf{y}\preceq_{\mathrm{p}}\mathbf{z}
, then
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}\preceq_{\mathrm{p}}\mathbf{z}
.

In the rest of the paper, we will write “partial order” instead of “partial order on
\displaystyle\mathsf{U}
” when the form of the universal set
\displaystyle\mathsf{U}
is clear from the context.

Using the partial order
\displaystyle\preceq_{\mathrm{p}}
, we establish the relation
\displaystyle\prec_{\mathrm{p}}
as follows: for any elements
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}
and
\displaystyle\mathbf{z}
of the set
\displaystyle\mathsf{U}
, the statement
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}\prec_{\mathrm{p}}\mathbf{z}
holds true if and only if
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}\preceq_{\mathrm{p}}\mathbf{z}
and
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}\neq\mathbf{z}
.
By the reflexivity and antisymmetry of the partial order
\displaystyle\preceq_{\mathrm{p}}
, it follows that for any
\displaystyle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}\in\mathsf{U}
,
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{z}
if and only if
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}\preceq_{\mathrm{p}}\mathbf{z}
and
\displaystyle\mathbf{z}\preceq_{\mathrm{p}}\mathbf{x}
.
Hence, for all
\displaystyle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}\in\mathsf{U}
,
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}\preceq_{\mathrm{p}}\mathbf{z}
if and only if
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}\prec_{\mathrm{p}}\mathbf{z}
or
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{z}
.

A non-empty set
\displaystyle\mathsf{K}\subseteq\mathsf{U}
is said to be
bounded w.r.t. a partial order

\displaystyle\preceq_{\mathrm{p}}
if it has both the least element
\displaystyle\mathbf{0}
and the greatest
element
\displaystyle\mathbf{1}
w.r.t.
\displaystyle\preceq_{\mathrm{p}}
, that is,

\displaystyle\mathbf{0},\mathbf{1}\in\mathsf{K}
and
\displaystyle\mathbf{0}\preceq_{\mathrm{p}}\mathbf{x}\preceq_{\mathrm{p}}\mathbf{1}
for all
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}\in\mathsf{K}
.
A partial order
\displaystyle\preceq
for which every two elements are comparable, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}\preceq\mathbf{z}
or
\displaystyle\mathbf{z}\preceq\mathbf{x}
for any
\displaystyle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}\in\mathsf{U}
, is called a
total order
.

We will now present some examples of restricted subsets for certain universal sets with usual partial orders, which will be used later in the paper.

Example 2.1
.

(a)

For
\displaystyle\mathsf{U}={\mathbb{R}}_{+}
,
the set
\displaystyle\mathsf{K}=[0,1]
is bounded w.r.t. the usual order
\displaystyle\leqslant
with the least element
\displaystyle 0
and the greatest element
\displaystyle 1.

Hereafter, the symbol
\displaystyle[a,b]
with
\displaystyle a\leqslant b
represents an interval in
\displaystyle{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
.

(b)

Let
\displaystyle\mathsf{U}=\mathsf{Int}_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}
be the family of all closed subintervals of
\displaystyle{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
.
The natural partial order is the
interval order

\displaystyle\preceq_{\small{\mathrm{Int}}}
defined as follows

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbf{x}\preceq_{\small{\mathrm{Int}}}\mathbf{z}\qquad\Leftrightarrow\qquad x^{l}\leqslant z^{l}\text{ and }x^{u}\leqslant z^{u},

for any
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}=[x^{l},x^{u}]\in\mathsf{Int}_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}
and
\displaystyle\mathbf{z}=[z^{l},z^{u}]\in\mathsf{Int}_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}

Then
\displaystyle\mathsf{K}=\mathsf{Int}_{[0,1]}
, where
\displaystyle\mathsf{Int}_{[0,1]}=\{\mathbf{x}=[x^{l},x^{u}]\mid 0\leqslant x^{l}\leqslant x^{u}\leqslant 1\}
, is a bounded set w.r.t.
\displaystyle\preceq_{\small{\mathrm{Int}}}
with the least element
\displaystyle[0,0]
and the greatest element
\displaystyle[1,1]
.

(c)

Let
\displaystyle\mathsf{U}={\mathbb{R}}^{k}_{+}
with
\displaystyle k\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
The usual partial order
\displaystyle\preceq_{\mathrm{V}}
is defined as follows

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbf{x}\preceq_{\mathrm{V}}\mathbf{z}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad x_{i}\leqslant z_{i}\text{ for all }i\in[k],

for any
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})^{T}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k}_{+}
and
\displaystyle\mathbf{z}=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k})^{T}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k}_{+}
, where
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}^{T}
denotes the transpose of a vector
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}
.
Then
\displaystyle\mathsf{K}=[0,1]^{k}
is a bounded set w.r.t.
\displaystyle\preceq_{\mathrm{V}}
with the least element
\displaystyle(0,\ldots,0)^{T}
and the greatest element
\displaystyle(1,\ldots,1)^{T}
.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
\displaystyle\preceq_{\mathrm{p}}
be a partial order.
A total order
\displaystyle\preceq
is called an
admissible order w.r.t.
\displaystyle\preceq_{\mathrm{p}}
if for any
\displaystyle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}\in\mathsf{U}
, it holds
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}\preceq\mathbf{z}
whenever
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}\preceq_{\mathrm{p}}\mathbf{z}
.

The notion of the admissible order was introduced in
[
12
]
in the context of comparing two intervals.
One of the most significant admissible orders w.r.t.
\displaystyle\preceq_{\small{\mathrm{Int}}}
is the
\displaystyle(\alpha,\beta)
-order defined as follows:
for any
\displaystyle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}\in\mathsf{Int}_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbf{x}\preceq_{(\alpha,\beta)}\mathbf{z}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\begin{cases}K_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x})<K_{\alpha}(\mathbf{z})\text{ or }\\
K_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x})=K_{\alpha}(\mathbf{z})\text{ and }K_{\beta}(\mathbf{x})\leqslant K_{\beta}(\mathbf{z}),\end{cases}

where
\displaystyle\alpha,\beta\in[0,1]
,
\displaystyle\alpha\neq\beta
, and
\displaystyle K_{\alpha}(\mathbf{y})=(1-\alpha)y^{l}+\alpha y^{u}
for any
\displaystyle\alpha\in[0,1]
and
\displaystyle\mathbf{y}\in\mathsf{Int}_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}
.
The
\displaystyle(\alpha,\beta)
-order generalizes three well-known admissible orders:
(i) the lexicographical order for
\displaystyle(\alpha,\beta)=(0,1)
,
(ii) the antilexicographical order for
\displaystyle(\alpha,\beta)=(1,0)
,
(iii) the Xu-Yager order for
\displaystyle(\alpha,\beta)=(0.5,1)
.
In
[
6
]
it is proved that in a certain class of orders, the only admissible order w.r.t.
\displaystyle\preceq_{\small{\mathrm{Int}}}
is the
\displaystyle(\alpha,\beta)
-order.

Definition 2.3
.

A partial order
\displaystyle\preceq_{\mathrm{p}}

is said to be
compatible with
\displaystyle\oplus
if for any
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\mathbf{v}\in\mathsf{U}
we have

\mathbf{x}_{1}\preceq_{\mathrm{p}}\mathbf{x}_{2}\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad\mathbf{x}_{1}\oplus\mathbf{v}\preceq_{\mathrm{p}}\mathbf{x}_{2}\oplus\mathbf{v}.

Replacing
\displaystyle\preceq_{\mathrm{p}}
with
\displaystyle\prec_{\mathrm{p}}
, we get the notion of
compatibility of
\displaystyle\prec_{\mathrm{p}}
with
\displaystyle\oplus
.

All partial orders from Example
2.1
are compatible with the corresponding operations
\displaystyle\oplus
from Table
1
.

Definition 2.4
.

An addition operation
\displaystyle\oplus
has the
cancellation law
, whenever
for any
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\mathbf{v}\in\mathsf{U}
the following implication holds

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{1}\oplus\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{x}_{2}\oplus\mathbf{v}\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad\mathbf{x}_{1}=\mathbf{x}_{2}.

All operations from Table
1
have the cancellation law.
We now present some auxiliary results.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
\displaystyle\preceq_{\mathrm{p}}
be a partial order.
If
\displaystyle\prec_{\mathrm{p}}
is compatible with
\displaystyle\oplus
, then
\displaystyle\preceq_{\mathrm{p}}
is compatible with
\displaystyle\oplus
.

Proof 1
.

By assumption, we have
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{1}\oplus\mathbf{v}\preceq_{\mathrm{p}}\mathbf{x}_{2}\oplus\mathbf{v}
for any
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{1}\prec_{\mathrm{p}}\mathbf{x}_{2}
and
\displaystyle\mathbf{v}\in\mathsf{U}
. Clearly, if
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{1}=\mathbf{x}_{2}
, then
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{1}\oplus\mathbf{v}\preceq_{\mathrm{p}}\mathbf{x}_{2}\oplus\mathbf{v}
as
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{1}\oplus\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{x}_{2}\oplus\mathbf{v}
. ∎

The minimum operation on
\displaystyle{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
is commutative and associative, and the usual order
\displaystyle\leqslant
is compatible with this operation.
However, the relation
\displaystyle<
is not compatible with the minimum operation as
\displaystyle\min\{x_{1},c\}=\min\{x_{2},c\}
for any
\displaystyle 0\leqslant c<x_{1}<x_{2}
.

To reverse the implication of Lemma
2.5
, an additional assumption is necessary.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
\displaystyle\oplus
satisfy the cancellation law and
\displaystyle\preceq_{\mathrm{p}}
be a partial order.
If
\displaystyle\preceq_{\mathrm{p}}
is compatible with
\displaystyle\oplus
, then
\displaystyle\prec_{\mathrm{p}}
is compatible with
\displaystyle\oplus
.

Proof 2
.

By assumption,
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{1}\oplus\mathbf{v}\preceq_{\mathrm{p}}\mathbf{x}_{2}\oplus\mathbf{v}
for any
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{1}\prec_{\mathrm{p}}\mathbf{x}_{2}
and
\displaystyle\mathbf{v}
.
Suppose there exists
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{1}\prec_{\mathrm{p}}\mathbf{x}_{2}
and
\displaystyle\mathbf{v}
such that
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{1}\oplus\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{x}_{2}\oplus\mathbf{v}
. Then by the cancellation law, we have
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{1}=\mathbf{x}_{2}
, a contradiction.
The proof is complete.
∎

Lemma 2.7
.

Assume that
\displaystyle\preceq
is an admissible order w.r.t.
\displaystyle\preceq_{\mathrm{p}}
such that
\displaystyle\prec
is compatible with
\displaystyle\oplus
.
Then:

(a)

\displaystyle\oplus
has the cancellation law,

(b)

for any
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\mathbf{v}\in\mathsf{U}
, if
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{1}\oplus\mathbf{v}\preceq\mathbf{x}_{2}\oplus\mathbf{v}
, then
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{1}\preceq\mathbf{x}_{2}
,

(c)

for any
\displaystyle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\mathsf{K}
, if

\displaystyle\mathbf{0}
is the least element of
\displaystyle\mathsf{K}
w.r.t.
\displaystyle\preceq_{\mathrm{p}}
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{0}\oplus\mathbf{0}\in\mathsf{K}
and
\displaystyle\mathbf{u}\oplus\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}
, then
\displaystyle\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{0}
and
\displaystyle\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}
.

Proof 3
.

(a) Suppose that
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{1}\oplus\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{x}_{2}\oplus\mathbf{v}
for some
\displaystyle\mathbf{v}
and
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{1}\neq\mathbf{x}_{2}
.
Since
\displaystyle\preceq
is a total order, any two elements are comparable, i.e., for any
\displaystyle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathsf{U}
we have either
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}\prec\mathbf{y}
or
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{y}
or
\displaystyle\mathbf{y}\prec\mathbf{x}
.
In our case, we get either
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{1}\prec\mathbf{x}_{2}
or
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{2}\prec\mathbf{x}_{1}
, as
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{1}\neq\mathbf{x}_{2}
.
If
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{1}\prec\mathbf{x}_{2}
, then by the compatibility of
\displaystyle\prec
we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{1}\oplus\mathbf{v}\prec\mathbf{x}_{2}\oplus\mathbf{v}
, so it is not true that
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{1}\oplus\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{x}_{2}\oplus\mathbf{v}
, a contradiction.
The case
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{2}\prec\mathbf{x}_{1}
can be examined in a similar way.

(b)
Suppose that
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{1}\oplus\mathbf{v}\preceq\mathbf{x}_{2}\oplus\mathbf{v}
and
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{2}\prec\mathbf{x}_{1}
for some
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\mathbf{v}
. By the compatibility of
\displaystyle\prec
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{2}\oplus\mathbf{v}\prec\mathbf{x}_{1}\oplus\mathbf{v}
, a contradiction.

(c)
Since
\displaystyle\mathbf{u}\oplus\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}
,
\displaystyle\mathbf{0}\oplus\mathbf{0}\in\mathsf{K}
, and
\displaystyle\mathbf{0}
is the least element of
\displaystyle\mathsf{K}
, we have
\displaystyle\mathbf{u}\oplus\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}\preceq\mathbf{0}\oplus\mathbf{0}.

From Lemma
2.5
, we get
\displaystyle\mathbf{0}\oplus\mathbf{0}\preceq\mathbf{u}\oplus\mathbf{0}.

Thus, by the transitivity of
\displaystyle\preceq
,
\displaystyle\mathbf{u}\oplus\mathbf{v}\preceq\mathbf{u}\oplus\mathbf{0}.

Point (b) now gives
\displaystyle\mathbf{v}\preceq\mathbf{0}.
Hence,
\displaystyle\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}
.
Similarly, it can be shown that
\displaystyle\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{0}.

∎

2.3
\displaystyle\mathsf{K}
-valued aggregation function

Let
\displaystyle\preceq
be an admissible order w.r.t.
\displaystyle\preceq_{\mathrm{p}}
.
Fix
\displaystyle\mathbf{X}=(\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n})\in\mathsf{U}^{n}
and
\displaystyle\mathbf{Z}=(\mathbf{z}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{z}_{n})\in\mathsf{U}^{n}
.
We define a binary relation on
\displaystyle\mathsf{U}^{n}
, say
\displaystyle\preceq_{(n)}
, as

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbf{X}\preceq_{(n)}\mathbf{Z}\qquad\Leftrightarrow\qquad\mathbf{x}_{i}\preceq\mathbf{z}_{i}\text{ for any }i\in[n].

Definition 2.8
.

Let
\displaystyle n\geqslant 2
,
\displaystyle\preceq
be an admissible order w.r.t.
\displaystyle\preceq_{\mathrm{p}}
, and
\displaystyle\mathsf{K}
be a bounded set w.r.t.
\displaystyle\preceq_{\mathrm{p}}
with the least element
\displaystyle\mathbf{0}
and the greatest element
\displaystyle\mathbf{1}
.
A mapping
\displaystyle\mathrm{Ag}\colon\mathsf{K}^{n}\to\mathsf{K}
is called an (
\displaystyle n
-ary
)
\displaystyle\mathsf{K}
-valued aggregation function w.r.t.
\displaystyle\preceq
if it satisfies the following monotonicity condition:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathrm{Ag}(\mathbf{X})\preceq\mathrm{Ag}(\mathbf{Z})\qquad\text{if}\qquad\mathbf{X}\preceq_{(n)}\mathbf{Z},

and meets the boundary conditions:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathrm{Ag}(\mathbf{0},\ldots,\mathbf{0})=\mathbf{0}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{Ag}(\mathbf{1},\ldots,\mathbf{1})=\mathbf{1}.

(1)

Putting
\displaystyle\mathsf{K}=[0,1]
and
\displaystyle\leqslant
in place of
\displaystyle\preceq

in Definition
2.8
, we get the well-known definition of the real-valued
aggregation function
(see
[
15
]
).
If we consider
the set
\displaystyle\mathsf{K}=\mathsf{Int}_{[0,1]}
with the partial order
\displaystyle\preceq_{\small{\mathrm{Int}}}
we recover the concept of an interval-valued (IV, for short) aggregation function (see
[
4
]
).
In the case of
\displaystyle\mathsf{K}=[0,1]^{k}
, to
the best of our knowledge, the definition of the vector-valued (VV, for short) aggregation function as a special form of Definition
2.8
has not yet been considered in the literature (although a definition of the monotonicity w.r.t. the admissible order
\displaystyle\preceq

has been introduced in
[
38
, Def. 4.5]
).
On the other hand, in the case of the
\displaystyle k
-dimensional upper simplex
\displaystyle\mathsf{K}=\{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\mid 0\leqslant x_{1}\leqslant x_{2}\leqslant\ldots\leqslant x_{k}\leqslant 1\}
such a definition has been implemented in the context of
\displaystyle k
-dimensional fuzzy sets (see
[
33
, Def. 7]
).

2.4
\displaystyle\mathsf{K}
-valued dissimilarity function

Definition 2.9
.

Let
\displaystyle\preceq
be an admissible order w.r.t.
\displaystyle\preceq_{\mathrm{p}}
and
\displaystyle\mathsf{K}
be a bounded set w.r.t.
\displaystyle\preceq_{\mathrm{p}}
with the least element
\displaystyle\mathbf{0}
and the greatest element
\displaystyle\mathbf{1}
.
A function
\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\colon\mathsf{K}^{2}\to\mathsf{K}
is said to be a
\displaystyle\mathsf{K}
-valued dissimilarity function w.r.t.
\displaystyle\preceq
if for all
\displaystyle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z}\in\mathsf{K}
, it satisfies the following conditions:

(a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})=\mathrm{d}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{x})
,

(b)

By

Definition 3.1

Hereafter,
\displaystyle{\textstyle{}\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{z}_{i}=\mathbf{z}_{1}\oplus\ldots\oplus\mathbf{z}_{n}.}

\displaystyle\mathsf{K}=[0,1]

5
Conclusion

In this article, we have introduced the Choquet-like operator, which is a generalization of the classical discrete Choquet integral to a multivalued setting in terms of a general function that replaces subtraction and multiplication, and an admissible order that refines the natural partial order on the data set under consideration. The new operator takes an input consisting of a finite number of values from the data set and returns a single output value belonging to that set. We have characterized the class of all functions for which the Choquet-like operator is invariant with respect to permutations of the input data, and then provided necessary and sufficient conditions for the operator to exhibit monotonicity with respect to the admissible order, as well as a complete characterization of the operator as an aggregation function with respect to the admissible order.
We have also studied two important special types of the Choquet-like operator and a number of their particular forms, both considered and not considered in the literature, for data of general type as well as for scalar, interval, and vector data.
In particular, we have shown that the only order
in a certain class of admissible orders for which the
\displaystyle\preceq_{A,B}
- Choquet integral defined in
[
13
, Def. 4]
is an interval-valued aggregation function with respect to
\displaystyle\preceq_{A,B}
, is the
\displaystyle(\alpha,\beta)
-order.

(25)

\displaystyle N
