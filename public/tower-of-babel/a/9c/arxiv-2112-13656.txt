1.
Introduction

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a complex Hilbert space
and
{{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})}
the algebra of all bounded linear operators
on
\mathcal{H}
.
If
{\mathcal{H}}
has finite dimension

n
, we can identify

{\mathcal{H}}
and
{{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})}
respectively as
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
and

M_{n}
, the set of all
n\times n
complex matrices.
Let
\|\cdot\|_{\rm sp}
be the operator norm on
{{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})}
. For any
A\in{{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})}
, let

s_{k}(A)=\inf\{\|A-X\|_{\rm sp}:X\in{{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})}\hbox{ has rank less than }k\}

be the
k
th singular value of
A
. They are also called the
s
-numbers or approximation numbers
of
A
. See

[
19
]
for the background.
If
A
is compact, then
s_{k}(A)
is the
k
th largest eigenvalue of
(A^{*}A)^{1/2}
.
We can use the singular values of matrices to define
those for a general operator
A\in{{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})}
as

s_{k}(A)=\sup\{s_{k}(X^{*}AY):X,Y:\mathbb{C}^{n}\to{\mathcal{H}},X^{*}X=Y^{*}Y=I_{k}\}.

A norm
f
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is called symmetric if

f(Px)=f(x)
for any
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and any permutation or
diagonal orthogonal matrix
P
.
Suppose
f
is a symmetric norm on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and

n\leq\dim{\mathcal{H}}
.
One can define
a norm on
{{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})}
by

\|A\|_{f}=f(s_{1}(A),\dots,s_{n}(A))\quad\mbox{for all $A$}.

For example,
if
f(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}(|c_{j}x_{j}|^{p})^{1/p}
for

x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})^{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, where

(c_{1},\dots,c_{n})
is a nonzero vector, then
\|\cdot\|_{f}
is
the
(c,p)
-norm studied in
[
3
]
.

It is clear that
\|\cdot\|_{f}
is unitarily invariant, i.e.,

\|UAV\|=\|A\|\quad\hbox{ for all }A\hbox{ and unitary }U,V\hbox{ in }{{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})}.

Unitarily invariant norms on
M_{n}
are well studied.
There has also been considerable interest in unitarily invariant
norms on
{{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})}
, but most of the study were
restricted to symmetrically norm ideals in
{{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})}

to ensure that the norm
is bounded; for example, see
[
1
,
6
,
7
,
21
,
22
]
.

In this paper, we study norm properties and distance preserving maps related to

\|\cdot\|_{f}
defined above.
In Section 2, we study basic properties for

\|\cdot\|_{f}
, and obtain results analogous to those
on matrices and compact operators; e.g., see

[
5
,
10
,
14
,
17
]
.
In Section 3, we obtain some geometrical properties of the unit
norm ball of
\|\cdot\|_{f}
and use the result to determine
the structure of maps
L:{{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})}\rightarrow{{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})}
such that

\|A-B\|_{f}=\|L(A)-L(B)\|_{f}
for all
A,B\in{{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})}
.
These extend the results on isometries and distance preserving maps
of unitarily invariant norms in the literature;
e.g., see
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
8
,
11
,
13
,
15
,
22
]
.

2.
Basic norm properties and inequalities

The construction of
\|\cdot\|_{f}
on
{{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})}
also arises naturally from other
considerations.
For any norm
\|\cdot\|
on
M_{n}
with

n\leq\dim{\mathcal{H}}
, one can define a
norm on
{{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})}
by

\|A\|_{\rm ui1}=\sup\{\|X^{*}AY\|:X,Y:\mathbb{C}^{n}\to{\mathcal{H}},X^{*}X=Y^{*}Y=I_{n}\}.

Then
\|\cdot\|_{\rm ui1}
is unitarily invariant.
Clearly, if
n=1

and
\|[a]\|=|a|
, then
\|\cdot\|_{\rm ui1}
is the operator norm.
In fact, one may assume that the norm
\|\cdot\|
on
M_{n}
is
unitarily invariant. Else, we may modify the norm
as

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|A\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}=\max\{\|UAV\|:U,V\in M_{n}\hbox{ are unitary}\}.

Then
{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\ \cdot\ \right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}
is unitarily invariant;
\|\cdot\|
and

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\ \cdot\ \right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}
induce the same

\|\cdot\|_{\rm ui1}
on
{{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})}
.
By a result of von Neumann in

[
18
]
(see also
[
15
,
17
]
),

(U1) there is a symmetric norm
f
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}

such that
\|A\|_{\rm ui1}=f(s_{1}(A),\dots,s_{n}(A))
for all
A\in M_{n}
.

As a result,
\|A\|_{\rm ui1}=\|A\|_{f}
for all
A\in{{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})}
.
So, instead of using a symmetric norm
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
to define

\|\cdot\|_{f}
on
{{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})}
, one can start with an arbitrary norm
\|\cdot\|
on

M_{n}
and the corresponding
\|\cdot\|_{\rm ui1}
defined above will coincide with

\|\cdot\|_{f}
for some symmetric norm
f
.

Also, let
\|\cdot\|
be any norm on
{{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})}
.
If
n\leq\dim{\mathcal{H}}
,
one can define

\|A\|_{\rm ui2}=\sup\{\|X^{*}AY\|:X,Y\hbox{ are rank $n$ partial isometries}\}.

Then
\|\cdot\|_{\rm ui2}
is a unitarily invariant norm
on
{{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})}
such that

\|A\|\leq\|A\|_{\rm ui2}\qquad\hbox{ for all }A\in{{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})}\hbox{ with }{\rm rank}\,(A)\leq n.

Take two orthonormal families
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
and
\{y_{1},\dots,y_{n}\}

in
{\mathcal{H}}
and define
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
by

f(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})=\left\|\mbox{$\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}x_{j}y_{j}^{*}$}\right\|_{\rm ui2}\quad\mbox{for all $(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}$}.

Then one can check that
f
is a symmetric norm on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and

\|A\|_{\rm ui2}=\|A\|_{f}\qquad\hbox{ for all }A\in{{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})}.

So,
\|\cdot\|_{f}
can be generated from any norm on
{{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})}
.

There are other interesting connections between unitarily invariant norms and symmetric
norms in the finite dimensional case,
see
[
13
]
and
[
14
]
.
In particular,
if
\|\cdot\|
is a unitarily
invariant norm on
M_{n}
, then (U1) holds, and

(U2)

there is a compact set
{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n}

consisting of
vectors

c=(c_{1},\dots,c_{n})
with
c_{1}\geq\cdots\geq c_{n}\geq 0

such that

\|A\|=\max\{\|A\|_{c}:c\in{\mathcal{S}}\},

where
\|A\|_{c}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{j}s_{j}(A)
is the
c
-norm of
A
.

From the above discussion we have the following proposition, which
will be useful in our study.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
be a symmetric norm.
Suppose
{\mathcal{H}}
is a Hilbert space with
\dim{\mathcal{H}}\geq n
,
and
\|\cdot\|
is a norm on
{{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})}
defined by

(2.1)

\|A\|=f(s_{1}(A),\dots,s_{n}(A))\quad\hbox{ for all }A\in{{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})}.

Then there is a compact set
{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n}

consisting of
vectors

c=(c_{1},\dots,c_{n})
with
c_{1}\geq\cdots\geq c_{n}\geq 0

such that

(2.2)

\|A\|=\max\{\|A\|_{c}:c\in{\mathcal{S}}\}.

By the above result,
we can use the properties of symmetric norms and

c
-norms to study unitarily invariant norm
\|\cdot\|
defined
on
{{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})}
as in (
2.1
).
For example, it is easy to extend
[
10
, Theorem 1]

concerning the comparison of a unitarily invariant norm
and the numerical radius defined by

r(A)=\sup\{|{\langle}Ax,x{\rangle}|:x\in{\mathcal{H}},{\langle}x,x{\rangle}=1\}.

To present our result and proof, we need the following definition.
For two nonnegative vectors
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}

we say that
x
is weakly majorized by

y
, denoted by
x\prec_{w}y
,
if the sum of the
k
largest entries of
x
is not
larger than that of
y
for every
1\leq k\leq n
. See
[
16
]
.

Proposition 2.2
.

Suppose
\|\cdot\|
is a norm
on
{{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})}
defined as in
(
2.1
)
.
Let

\alpha=f(e_{1})\quad\hbox{ and }\quad\beta=\begin{cases}2f(e_{1}+\cdots+e_{k})&\hbox{ if }n=2k,\cr 2f(e_{1}+\cdots+e_{k}+e_{k+1}/2)&\hbox{ if }n=2k+1.\cr\end{cases}

Then for any
A\in{{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})}
,

\alpha r(A)\leq\|A\|\leq\beta r(A).

The constants
\alpha
and
\beta
are the best possible in view of the following:

•

\alpha r(A)=\alpha s_{1}(A)=\|A\|
if
A=xx^{*}
for a unit vector
x
;

•

\|A\|=\beta r(A)
if

A=\begin{cases}\sum_{j=1}^{k}2x_{j}y_{j}^{*}&\hbox{ if }n=2k,\cr\sum_{j=1}^{k}2x_{j}y_{j}^{*}+x_{k+1}x_{k+1}^{*}&\hbox{ if }n=2k+1,\cr\end{cases}

for an orthonormal set

{\mathcal{F}}=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k},y_{1},\dots,y_{k}\}\subseteq{\mathcal{H}}
and
x_{k+1}\in{\mathcal{F}}^{\perp}
if
n=2k+1
.

Proof.
Denote by
\{E_{jk}\}_{j,k=1}^{n}

the standard basis for
M_{n}
.
Let
s(Y)
be the vector of singular values of

Y\in M_{n}
.
If
A\in{{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})}
has
r(A)=1
, then for any

X,Y:{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\to{\mathcal{H}}
such that
X^{*}X=Y^{*}Y=I_{n}
, we have
from the proof of Theorem 1 in
[
10
]
,

s(E_{11})\prec_{w}s(X^{*}AY)\prec_{w}s(F),

where
F=2(E_{12}+E_{34}+\cdots+E_{2k-1,2k})
if
n=2k

and
F=2(E_{12}+E_{34}+\cdots+E_{2k-1,2k})+E_{nn}

if
n=2k+1
.
It is known that if
R,T\in M_{n}
with
s(R)\prec_{w}s(T)

then

|R|\leq|T|
for any unitarily invariant norm
|\cdot|
;
see
[
14
,
17
]
. We get the inequalities.
The equality cases can be checked directly. ∎

We extend the definitions in (
[
13
, pp. 54-55]
)
for
\|\cdot\|
defined as in (
2.1
):

(i)
\|\cdot\|
is a uniform norm if

\|AB\|\leq s_{1}(A)\|B\|
and
\|AB\|\leq s_{1}(B)\|A\|
,

(ii)
\|\cdot\|
is a cross norm if

\|A\|=s_{1}(A)
for any rank one operator

A\in{{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})}
.

(iii)
\|\cdot\|
on
{{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})}
is submultiplicative if

\|AB\|\leq\|A\|\|B\|

for all
A,B\in{{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})}
;

if, in addition,

\|I\|=1
, it is an algebra (or a ring) norm.

We have the following results.

Proposition 2.3
.

Suppose
\|\cdot\|
is a norm
on
{{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})}
defined as in
(
2.1
)
.

(a)

Then
\|\cdot\|
is a uniform norm.

(b)

The norm
\|\cdot\|
is a cross norm
if and only if
f(e_{1})=1
.

Proof.
(a) As observed above, we may assume that
\|\cdot\|
is induced by
a unitarily invariant norm
{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\ \cdot\ \right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}
on
M_{n}
. For any
A,B\in{{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})}
and
X,Y:{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\to{\mathcal{H}}
such that

X^{*}X=Y^{*}Y=I_{n}
, let
P
be any rank
n
partial isometry whose image contains that of
BY
.
Then by
[
5
, Lemma 2.2]
,

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|X^{*}ABY\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}={\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(X^{*}AP)(P^{*}BY)\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}\leq s_{1}(X^{*}AP){\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|P^{*}BY\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}\leq s_{1}(A)\|B\|.

The result follows.

(b) Evidently,
f(e_{1})=1

if and only if
1=\|xy^{*}\|=f(e_{1})

for any unit vectors
x,y
. ∎

Proposition 2.4
.

Suppose
\|\cdot\|
is a norm
on
{{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})}
defined as in
(
2.1
)
.
Then
\|\cdot\|
is submultiplicative if and only if
any one of the following holds.

(1)

\|A\|\geq s_{1}(A)
for all
A\in{{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})}
.

(2)

There are unit vectors
x,y\in{\mathcal{H}}
such that

\|xy^{*}\|\geq 1
.

(3)

f(e_{1})\geq 1
, equivalently,
there is
(c_{1},\dots,c_{n})\in{\mathcal{S}}
with
c_{1}\geq 1
.

Moreover,
\|\cdot\|
is an algebra norm if and only
if it is the operator norm.

Proof.
The equivalence of (1) – (3) can be proved as in the finite
dimensional case, e.g., see
[
14
]
.
Suppose
\|\cdot\|
is multiplicative. Then
for any unit vector
x\in{\mathcal{H}}
,

\|xx^{*}\|^{2}\geq\|(xx^{*})^{2}\|=\|xx^{*}\|

so that
1\leq\|xx^{*}\|=f(e_{1})
. Thus, (3) holds.
Suppose (1) holds. Then by Proposition 2.3 (a),
\|AB\|\leq s_{1}(A)\|B\|\leq\|A\|\|B\|
for any

A,B\in{{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})}
.

To prove the last assertion,
suppose
\|\cdot\|
is an algebra norm.
Then for any
c=(c_{1},\dots,c_{n})\in{\mathcal{S}}
,

1=\|I\|\geq\|I\|_{c}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{j}
. For any
A\in{{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})}
,
there is a
c\in{\mathcal{S}}
such that

\|A\|=\|A\|_{c}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{j}s_{j}(A)\leq s_{1}(A).

But
\|\cdot\|
is submultiplicative, so that
\|A\|\geq s_{1}(A)
.
Thus,
\|\cdot\|
is the operator norm. The converse is clear.
∎

One may obtain similar inequalities for the triple product on
{{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})}

defined by

A\circ B\circ C=\frac{1}{2}(AB^{*}C+CB^{*}A).

Proposition 2.5
.

Suppose
\|\cdot\|
is a norm
on
{{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})}
defined as in
(
2.1
)
.
The following condition are equivalent.

(1)

\|A\circ B\circ C\|\leq\|A\|\|B\|\|C\|
for all
A,B,C\in{{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})}
.

(2)

\|A\circ A\circ A\|\leq\|A\|^{3}
for all
A,\in{{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})}
.

(3)

f(e_{1})\geq 1
, equivalently,
there is
(c_{1},\dots,c_{n})\in{\mathcal{S}}
with

c_{1}\geq 1
.

Proof.
If (1) holds, then (2) clearly holds.
If (2) holds, consider
A=xy^{*}
for unit vectors
x,y
.
Clearly,
\|A\|=f(e_{1})
and
A\circ A\circ A=A
.
Thus, the inequality in (2) becomes
f(e_{1})\leq f(e_{1})^{3}
, and hence

f(e_{1})\geq 1
.

Let
(s_{1},\dots,s_{n}),(a_{1},\dots,a_{n}),(b_{1},\dots,b_{n}),(c_{1},\dots,c_{n})
be the vectors of the

n
largest singular values of

A\circ B\circ C,A,B,C,
respectively.
Then

\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}s_{j}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}a_{j}b_{j}c_{j}
for
\ell=1,\dots,n
. This ensures that

f(s_{1},\dots,s_{n})\leq f(a_{1}b_{1}c_{1},\dots,a_{n}b_{n}c_{n}).

If (3) holds, then for vectors
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}),(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})
with nonnegative entries
arranging in descending order, we have

f(x_{1}y_{1},\dots,x_{n}y_{n})\leq f(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})f(y_{1},\dots,y_{n}).

Consequently,

\|A\circ B\circ C\|_{f}=f(s_{1},\dots,s_{n})\leq f(a_{1}b_{1}c_{1},\dots,a_{n}b_{n}c_{n})

\leq f(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})f(b_{1},\dots,b_{n})f(c_{1},\dots,c_{n})=\|A\|_{f}\|B\|_{f}\|C\|_{f}.

Thus (1) holds. ∎

The next theorem extends the result for the finite dimensional
case in
[
5
]
. In fact, there was a gap in line 4 of
the proof of
[
5
, Theorem 2.4]
, which
asserted that
1\leq s_{1}(AB)/\|A\|\|B\|

without justification.
Our proof fills the gap.

Theorem 2.6
.

Suppose
\|\cdot\|

on
{{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})}
defined as in
(
2.1
)

is a submultiplicative norm.
Then there exist nonzero
A,B\in{{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})}

such that
\|AB\|=\|A\|\|B\|
if and only if

\|\cdot\|
is a cross norm.

In case
\|\cdot\|
is a cross norm, the following conditions
hold.

(1)

Two nonzero operators
A,B\in{{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})}
satisfy

\|AB\|=\|A\|\|B\|
if and only if

s_{1}(AB)=\|A\|\|B\|
.

(2)

Assume that

\{T\in{{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})}:\|T\|=s_{1}(T)\}

is a subset of rank one matrices.
Two nonzero operators
A,B\in{{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})}
satisfy

\|AB\|=\|A\|\|B\|
if and only if

A=xy^{*}
and
B=y^{*}z
for some nonzero

x,y,z\in{\mathcal{H}}
with
{\langle}y,y{\rangle}=1
.

Proof.
Suppose
\|\cdot\|
is submultiplicative.
If
\|\cdot\|
is a cross norm, then for any unit vector

x\in{\mathcal{H}}
and
A=B=xx^{*}
, we have

\|AB\|=\|A\|\|B\|=1.

Suppose
\|\cdot\|
is not a cross norm. Then

\|xy^{*}\|=d>1
for any unit vectors
x,y\in{\mathcal{H}}
.
We will show that
\|A\|\|B\|>\|AB\|

for any nonzero
A,B\in{{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})}
. For any
\varepsilon\in(0,s_{1}(A))
,
take a unit vector
x\in{\mathcal{H}}
such that
\|Ax\|>s_{1}(A)-\varepsilon

and let
y=Ax/\|Ax\|
. Then
s_{1}(Axy^{*})\leq s_{1}(A)

and
s_{j}(Axy^{*})=0
for all
j>1
. Consequently,

\sum_{j=1}^{k}s_{j}(A)\geq\sum_{j=1}^{k}s_{j}(Axy^{*})
for all
k=1,\ldots,n
.
It follows that
\|A\|\geq\|Axy^{*}\|=\|Ax\|\|yy^{*}\|>(s_{1}(A)-\varepsilon)d
.
As this is true for all such
\varepsilon
,
\|A\|\geq s_{1}(A)d>s_{1}(A)
.
Thus
\|A\|\|B\|>s_{1}(A)\|B\|\geq\|AB\|
.

Now, assume that
\|\cdot\|
is a cross norm.

(1) Note that

s_{1}(AB)\leq\|AB\|\leq\|A\|\|B\|
.
If
s_{1}(AB)=\|A\|\|B\|
, then
\|AB\|=\|A\|\|B\|
.

Conversely, if
\|AB\|=\|A\|\|B\|
, then
by Proposition 2.3 (a) and Proposition 2.4 (1),
s_{1}(A)=\|A\|
and
s_{1}(B)=\|B\|
.
Suppose
s_{1}(AB)<\|A\|\|B\|=s_{1}(A)s_{1}(B)
.
Also, recall that
s_{j}(AB)\leq s_{j}(A)s_{1}(B)

for
j=2,\dots,n
.
Hence, for
k=1,\dots,n
,

\sum_{j=1}^{k}s_{j}(AB)<\sum_{j=1}^{k}s_{j}(A)s_{1}(B)\quad\hbox{ so that }\quad\sum_{j=1}^{k}s_{j}(AB)\leq t\sum_{j=1}^{k}s_{j}(A)s_{1}(B)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}s_{j}(ts_{1}(B)A)

for some
t\in(0,1)
.
Consequently,

\|AB\|\leq\|ts_{1}(B)A\|=t\|A\|\|B\|<\|A\|\|B\|,

which
is a contradiction.

(2) Direct verification. ∎

We remark that in Theorem

By

(2) is not true if the above set equality fails.
For instance, if
\|\cdot\|

Lemma 3.2

Let

be an extreme point
of

. Then

for every maximal partial isometry

Proof.
Let

and

is an eigenvalue of

. Take a unit eigenvector

corresponding to

and let

.
Next, we claim that

An argument as above shows that it is true if every non-zero element of

is an eigenvalue.
Otherwise we can use the construction in
[

So,

Lemma 3.3

It is known that
R\in M_{p}

\ \hskip 21.68121pt\leq\sum_{j=1}^{u-1}(\xi_{j}-\xi_{j+1})[s_{1}(R)+\cdots+s_{p_{1}+\cdots+p_{j}}(R)]+\xi_{u}\sum_{j=1}^{p}s_{j}(R)=\|R\|_{c}.

\dim{\mathcal{K}}<n
.
Now,
A=B+D
for
B=A_{j}
and
D=(A-A_{j})
, and (
3.4
) holds.
We must have either (a) or (b) of Lemma
3.4
.

Case 1.

Suppose for each
j
, there is a
\varphi_{j}\in{{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{K}})}^{*}
such that

\varphi(B_{j})=1
and that
\varphi(Y_{1})=0
whenever

Y\in{{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})}
satisfies

\|\mu A_{j}+\nu Y\|=\max\{|\mu|,|\nu|\}

for any
\mu,\nu\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
Y_{1}
is the compression of
Y
onto
{\mathcal{K}}
.
The condition is clearly satisfied by
Y=A_{k}
for
k\neq j
.
So, the bi-orthogonal condition
\varphi_{j}(B_{k})=\delta_{jk}
ensures

B_{1},\dots,B_{N}
are linearly independent. However,
\dim{\mathcal{K}}\leq n-1
.
There cannot be so many linearly independent matrices.
This case is impossible.

Case 2.
Suppose for some
A_{j}
, say
A_{1}
,
there is a
c\in{\mathcal{S}}
with nonzero entries
c_{1},\ldots,c_{p}

for
p\geq r_{1}
such that
\|A+A_{1}\|_{c}=\|A+A_{1}\|=2
.
By Lemma
3.4
, the compression of
A_{1}
to
{\mathcal{K}}
is
(a_{1}/2)I_{r_{1}}
.

For
j>1
, we construct the
p\times p
matrices
\hat{A}
,
\hat{A}_{1}
and

\hat{A}_{j}
as in the proof of Lemma
3.4
. Then for
C={\rm diag}(c_{1},\ldots,c_{p})
,

|1\pm{\rm tr}\,C\hat{A}_{j}|=|{\rm tr}\,C(\hat{A}_{1}\pm\hat{A}_{j})|\leq\|\hat{A}_{1}\pm\hat{A}_{j}\|_{c}\leq\|A_{1}\pm A_{j}\|=1.

Thus
{\rm tr}\,(C\hat{A}_{j})=0
. It follows that

2={\rm tr}\,C[A+(\hat{A}_{1}+\hat{A}_{j})]\leq\|\hat{A}+(\hat{A}_{1}+\hat{A}_{j})\|_{c}\leq\|A+(A_{1}+A_{j})\|_{c}\leq\|A+(A_{1}+A_{j})\|=2.

We have
\|A+(A_{1}+A_{j})\|_{c}=\|A+(A_{1}+A_{j})\|=2
for
c
with
p\geq r_{1}

nonzero entries.
So, Lemma
3.4
applied to

B=A_{1}+A_{j}
and
D=A-B
gives us

.
(Here, note that
\dim{\mathcal{K}}<n
ensures
n>1
so that
n^{2}+1\geq 5

and
A-B
is nontrivial.)
But we already have

B_{1}=(a_{1}/2)I_{r_{1}}
.
So,
B_{j}=0_{r_{1}}
for all
j>1
.
Then the compression of
A
onto
{\mathcal{K}}
is

B_{1}+\cdots+B_{N}=(a_{1}/2)I_{r_{1}}
,
which contradicts the form of
A
. This case is also impossible.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

The sufficiency is clear. To prove the necessity, suppose
L:{{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})}\to{{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})}
satisfies the
hypothesis of the theorem.
Observe that by the Mazur-Ulam Theorem, the map
\hat{L}:A\mapsto L(A)-L(0)
is
real linear and satisfies

\|\hat{L}(A)\|=\|A\|
for all
A\in{{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})}
.
So,
\hat{L}
preserves the extreme points of
{\mathcal{B}}
.
By Theorem
3.6
,

\hat{L}
sends maximal isometries to maximal isometries.
It follows from
[
9
, Solution to Problem 107]
(see also
[
20
, Lemma 3]
) that
\hat{L}
is indeed
an isometry of the spectral norm.
Using the characterization
[
4
, Corollary 3.3]
, we conclude that
L
is of the asserted form.
∎
