Set-Based Training for Neural Network Verification
Lukas Koller, Tobias Ladner, Matthias Althoff

Abstract
Neural networks are vulnerable to adversarial attacks, i.e., small input perturbations can significantly affect the outputs of a neural network. Therefore, to ensure safety of safety-critical environments, the robustness of a neural network must be formally verified against input perturbations, e.g., from noisy sensors. To improve the robustness of neural networks and thus simplify the formal verification, we present a novel set-based training procedure in which we compute the set of possible outputs given the set of possible inputs and compute for the first time a gradient set, i.e., each possible output has a different gradient. Therefore, we can directly reduce the size of the output enclosure by choosing gradients toward its center. Small output enclosures increase the robustness of a neural network and, at the same time, simplify its formal verification. The latter benefit is due to the fact that a larger size of propagated sets increases the conservatism of most verification methods. Our extensive evaluation demonstrates that set-based training produces robust neural networks with competitive performance, which can be verified using fast (polynomial-time) verification algorithms due to the reduced output set.



I
Introduction

Neural networks demonstrate impressive performance for many complex tasks, such as speech recognition
[
1
]
or object detection
[
2
]
. However, many neural networks are sensitive to input perturbations
[
3
]
: Small, carefully chosen input perturbations can lead to vastly different outputs. This behavior is problematic for the adoption of neural networks in safety-critical environments, where the input often contains noisy sensor data or is subject to external disturbances, e.g., autonomous vehicle control
[
4
]
or airborne collision avoidance
[
5
]
.
Thus, the formal verification of neural networks gained interest in recent years
[
6
]
. Given a set of inputs, the formal verification of neural networks attempts to find a proof that the neural network returns the correct output for every input from the set. Typically, when verifying the robustness of a neural network, the perturbations are modeled as an
\ell_{\infty}
-ball of radius
\epsilon\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
around an input.

In this work, we address the challenge of training and verifying the robustness of a neural network with a novel set-based training procedure. During training, we enclose the output set of the neural network and compute a gradient set, i.e., each possible output has a different gradient.
We can directly enforce smaller output enclosures by choosing gradients that point toward the center of the enclosure.
To compute the gradient set, we use a set-based loss function, which considers the position (for accuracy) and the size of the output enclosure (for robustness).
Small output enclosures improve the robustness of the neural network and simplify subsequent formal verification.
Previous works are limited to a single gradient for training, thereby discarding much set-based information.

I-A

Formal Verification of Neural Networks

The formal verification of neural networks is computationally challenging, i.e., the problem is
NP
-hard with only rectified linear unit (ReLU) activation functions
[
9
]
; thus, even verifying small neural networks often takes a long time.
Most formal verification approaches either formulate an optimization problem, which is solved with an off-the-shelf solver, e.g., (mixed-integer) linear programming
[
10
,
11
]
or satisfiability modulo theories (SMT)
[
9
]
, or use reachability analysis
[
6
]
. Often, branch-and-bound algorithms
[
12
,
13
]
are utilized for the verification of neural networks: The input space is recursively split until each subspace of the input space is either verified or falsified; due to the recursive process, branch-and-bound algorithms have exponential runtime in the worst case.
Hence, many verification methods sacrifice accuracy for a polynomial runtime: Reachability analysis uses efficient set representations, e.g., zonotopes
[
14
]
, combined with set-based computations to efficiently enclose the output set of a neural network (in polynomial time)
[
15
,
16
,
17
,
18
]
. If the enclosure of the output set is sufficiently tight, it can be used to formally verify a neural network.

On the contrary, neural networks can be falsified by adversarial attacks, i.e., small input perturbations that lead to an incorrect output.
Often, adversarial attacks are fast to compute and effective at provoking incorrect outputs
[
19
]
. The most prominent approaches are the fast gradient sign method (FGSM) and projected gradient descent (PGD). The FGSM is a single-step gradient-based adversarial attack that efficiently generates adversarial attacks
[
19
]
. PGD uses multiple iterations of FGSM to compute stronger adversarial attacks
[
20
]
. However, neural networks can be robust for one type of attack but can remain vulnerable to another type of attack
[
21
]
; thus, it is necessary to formally verify the robustness of neural networks.

I-B

Training Robust Neural Networks

The training objective of a robust neural network is typically formulated as a min-max optimization problem
[
21
]
: minimize the worst-case loss within a set of input possible inputs.
Computing the worst-case loss within a set is computationally difficult
[
22
]
. Nonetheless, robust neural networks can be effectively trained by approximating the worst-case loss with adversarial attacks, e.g., computed using PGD
[
21
]
.

The well-established trade-off loss
[
23
]
combines a regular loss for accuracy with a boundary loss for robustness, which is approximated with adversarial attacks. The boundary loss pushes the decision boundary of a classifier away from the training samples and thereby improves the robustness of the trained neural network. However, neural networks trained with adversarial attacks remain hard to formally verify.

Therefore, some approaches combine the training and formal verification of neural networks. In these works, the approximation of a worst-case loss is replaced by an upper bound, guaranteeing that no perturbation will lead to an incorrect output. Different methods for computing an upper bound of the worst-case loss have been proposed: Interval bound propagation (IBP)
[
7
]
, linear relaxation
[
24
]
, (mixed-integer) linear programming
[
25
]
, or abstract interpretation
[
26
]
.
IBP computes conservative output bounds and uses the worst case within the bounds for training and verification
[
7
]
(
Figure

1
a). Large approximation errors can create an over-regularization and lead to poor performance
[
8
]
.

Thus, by propagating smaller input sets, state-of-the-art robustness results are achieved
[
8
]
(
Figure

1
b). However, a branch-and-bound algorithm with worst-case exponential-time complexity is used for their formal verification. Instead, we aim to train neural networks that can be quickly verified using polynomial-time verification algorithms.
Moreover, IBP can be combined with adversarial attacks computed in a latent space
[
27
]
. Similarly, zonotopes can be partially propagated through a neural network to compute adversarial attacks in latent space
[
28
]
; however, the partial propagation results in layer-wise training of the neural network, which has a significant computational overhead.
Furthermore, the training of robust neural networks can be improved by using a specialized initialization for the parameters of a neural network
[
29
]
.

More closely related to our work is an approach using zonotopic enclosures during training
[
26
]
; however, much set-based information is discarded by only using the enclosure to bound the worst-case loss. All related approaches for training robust neural networks consider a single gradient. Conversely, our approach considers a gradient set (
Figure

1
c), thereby simultaneously increasing the robustness of the neural network and simplifying the formal verification.

I-C

Contributions

Our main contributions are:

•

A novel set-based training procedure for robust neural networks that, for the first time, uses a gradient set for training that generalizes the well-established trade-off loss
[
23
]
. The gradient set enables direct control of the size of the output enclosure to improve the robustness of the neural network and simplify formal verification.

•

A fast, batch-wise, and differentiable set-based forward propagation and backpropagation that is efficiently computed on a GPU. The set propagation uses analytical solutions for the image enclosure of typical nonlinear activation functions.

•

An extensive empirical evaluation in which we demonstrate the competitive performance of our set-based training and compare it with state-of-the-art robust training approaches. Moreover, we include large-scale ablation studies to justify our design choices.

II-B

Feed-Forward Neural Networks

A feed-forward neural network
N_{\theta}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n_{0}}\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{\kappa}}
consists of a sequence of
\kappa\in\mathbb{N}
layers. For the
k
-th layer,
n_{k-1}\in\mathbb{N}
denotes the number of input neurons and
n_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
denotes the number of output neurons. A layer can either be a linear layer, which applies an affine map, or a nonlinear (activation) layer, which applies a nonlinear activation function element-wise.

Definition 1

(Neural Network,
[
30
, Sec. 5.1]
)
.

For an input
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{0}}
, the output
y=N_{\theta}(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\kappa}}
of a neural network
N_{\theta}
is computed by

\displaystyle h_{0}

\displaystyle=x\text{,}

\displaystyle h_{k}

\displaystyle=L_{k}(h_{k-1})\quad\text{for $k\in[\kappa]$,}

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle=h_{\kappa}\text{,}

where

h_{k}=L_{k}(h_{k-1})=\begin{cases}W_{k}\,h_{k-1}+b_{k}&\text{if $k$-th layer is linear,}\\
\phi_{k}(h_{k-1})&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

with weights
W_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{k}\times n_{k-1}}
, bias
b_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{k}}
, and nonlinear activation function
\phi_{k}
which is applied element-wise.

We denote the parameters of the neural network with
\theta
, which include all weight matrices and bias vectors from its linear layers.

Training of Neural Networks

We consider supervised training settings of a classification task, where a neural network is trained on a dataset
\mathcal{D}=\{(x_{1},t_{1}),\dots,(x_{n},t_{n})\}
, containing inputs
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{0}}
with associated targets
t_{i}\in\{0,1\}^{n_{\kappa}}
. A target
t_{i}\in\{0,1\}^{n_{\kappa}}
is a one-hot encoding of the target label
l_{i}\in[n_{\kappa}]
, i.e.,
t_{i}=e_{l_{i}}
is the
l_{i}
-th standard basis vector
e_{l_{i}}
.
A loss function
L:\mathbb{R}^{n_{\kappa}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{\kappa}}\to\mathbb{R}
measures how well a neural network predicts the targets.
A typical loss function for classification tasks is the cross-entropy error:

\displaystyle L_{\text{CE}}(t,y)\coloneqq-\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\kappa}}t_{(i)}\,\ln(p_{(i)})\text{,}

(1)

where
\ln
denotes the natural logarithm and
p_{(i)}=\exp(y_{(i)})/(\exp(y)\,\mathbf{1})
are the predicted class probabilities.

The training goal of a neural network is to find parameters
\theta
that minimize the total loss of the dataset
\mathcal{D}

[
30
, Sec. 5.2]
:

\min_{\theta}\sum_{(x_{i},t_{i})\in\mathcal{D}}L(t_{i},N_{\theta}(x_{i}))\text{.}

(2)

We revisit the training of a neural network to later augment it with sets.
A popular algorithm to train a neural network is gradient descent
[
30
, Sec. 5.2.4]
: the parameters are randomly initialized
[
31
]
and iteratively optimized using the gradient of the loss function.
We denote the gradient of the loss function
L
w.r.t. the output of the
k
-th layer
h_{k}
as:

g_{k}\coloneqq\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!h_{k}}L(t,y)\text{,}

(3)

where
y=N_{\theta}(x)
for input
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{0}}
. The weight matrix
W_{k}
and bias vector
b_{k}
of the
k
-th layer are updated as
[
30
, Sec. 5.3]

\displaystyle\begin{split}W_{k}&\leftarrow W_{k}-\eta\,\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!W_{k}}L(t,y)=W_{k}-\eta\,g_{k}\,h^{\top}_{k-1}\text{,}\\
b_{k}&\leftarrow b_{k}-\eta\,\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!b_{k}}L(t,y)=b_{k}-\eta\,g_{k}\text{,}\end{split}

(4)

where
\eta\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
is the learning rate.
The gradients
g_{k}
are efficiently computed with backpropagation
[
30
, Sec. 5.3]
: by utilizing the chain rule, the gradient
g_{\kappa}
of the last layer is propagated backward through all neural network layers.

Proposition 1

(Backpropagation,
[
30
, Sec. 5.3]
)
.

Let
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\kappa}}
be an output of a neural network with target
t\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\kappa}}
.
The gradients
g_{k}
are computed in reverse order as

\displaystyle g_{\kappa}

\displaystyle=\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!y}L(t,y)\text{,}

\displaystyle g_{k-1}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}W_{k}^{\top}\,g_{k}&\text{if $k$-th layer is linear,}\\
\operatorname{Diag}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\phi^{\prime}_{k}(h_{k-1}^{\phantom{\top}})}}\right)\,g_{k}&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

for all
k\in\{\kappa,\dots,1\}
.

From now on, we refer to this (standard) neural network training as point-based training.

II-C

Set-Based Computation

Our approach extends point-based training to sets, which we represent by zonotopes. A zonotope is a convex set representation describing the Minkowski sum of a finite number of line segments.

Definition 2

(Zonotope,
[
14
, Def. 1]
)
.

Given a center
c\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a generator matrix
G\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times q}
, a zonotope
\mathcal{Z}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is defined as

\mathcal{Z}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.c+G\,\beta\;}}\right|\;\beta\in[-1,1]^{q}}}\right\}\eqqcolon\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<c,G}}\right>_{Z}\text{.}

Subsequently, we define several operations for zonotopes used in our training approach.
Please note, that for the complexity analysis, we only consider the number of binary operations and we neglect the computational effort of unary operations. Moreover, we consider the textbook method and do not assume any special numerical tricks that have been developed for large matrices.

Proposition 2

(Interval Enclosure,
[
32
, Prop. 2.2]
)
.

A zonotope
\mathcal{Z}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<c,G}}\right>_{Z}
with
c\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
G\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times q}
is enclosed by the interval
[l,u]\supseteq\mathcal{Z}
, where

\displaystyle l

\displaystyle=c-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|G}}\right|\,\mathbf{1}\text{,}

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=c+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|G}}\right|\,\mathbf{1}\text{,}

and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\cdot}}\right|
computes the element-wise absolute value. The time complexity of computing an interval enclosure is
\mathcal{O}(n\,q)
.

Proposition 3

(Minkowski Sum,
[
32
, Prop. 2.1 and Sec. 2.4]
)
.

The Minkowski sum of a zonotope
\mathcal{Z}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<c,G}}\right>_{Z}
and an interval
\mathcal{I}=[l,u]\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
c,l,u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
G\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times q}
is computed as

\mathcal{Z}\oplus\mathcal{I}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<c+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}\,(u+l),\begin{bmatrix}G&\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}\,\operatorname{Diag}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(u-l}}\right)\end{bmatrix}}}\right>_{Z}\text{,}

and has time complexity
\mathcal{O}(n)
.

Proposition 4

(Affine Map,
[
32
, Sec. 2.4]
)
.

The result of an affine map
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m},\,x\mapsto W\,x+b
with
W\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and
b\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
applied to a zonotope
\mathcal{Z}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<c,G}}\right>_{Z}
with
c\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
G\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times q}
is

f(\mathcal{Z})=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{f(z)\mid z\in\mathcal{Z}}}\right\}=W\,\mathcal{Z}+b=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<W\,c+b,W\,G}}\right>_{Z}\text{,}

and has time complexity
\mathcal{O}(m\,n\,q)
.

Determining the volume of a zonotope is computationally demanding
[
33
]
. However, we can effectively approximate the size of a zonotope with its F-radius
[
34
]
: The F-radius of a zonotope is the Frobenius norm of its generator matrix.

Proposition 5

(F-Radius,
[
34
, Def. 3]
)
.

For a zonotope
\mathcal{Z}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<c,G}}\right>_{Z}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
G\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times q}
, the F-radius is

\lVert\mathcal{Z}\rVert_{F}\coloneqq\nicefrac{{1}}{{n}}\,\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{q}G_{(i,j)}^{2}}\text{.}

Please note that in contrast to
[
34
, Def. 3]
, we include a normalization factor of
\nicefrac{{1}}{{n}}
.
For set-based training, we require the gradient of a function w.r.t. a zonotope, which is also a zonotope, where the center is the derivative w.r.t. the center and the generator matrix is the derivative w.r.t. the generator matrix.

Definition 3

(Zonotope Gradient)
.

The gradient of a function
f(\cdot)
w.r.t. a zonotope
\mathcal{Z}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<c,G}}\right>_{Z}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is defined as

\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!\mathcal{Z}}f(\mathcal{Z})\coloneqq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!c}f(\mathcal{Z}),\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!G}f(\mathcal{Z})}}\right>_{Z}\text{.}

II-D

Formal Verification of Neural Networks

In this work, we consider the robustness of neural networks for classification tasks: Each dimension of an output
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\kappa}}
corresponds to a class, and the dimension with the maximum value determines the predicted class.
An input
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{0}}
is correctly classified by a neural network if the predicted class matches the target label:

\argmax_{k\in[n_{\kappa}]}y_{(k)}=l\text{.}

(5)

We call a neural network (locally) robust for a given set of inputs if the neural network correctly classifies every input within the set. As an input set, we use the
\ell_{\infty}
-ball of radius
\epsilon\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
around an input
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{0}}
:

\pi_{\epsilon}(x)\coloneqq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<x,\epsilon\,I_{n_{0}}}}\right>_{Z}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\tilde{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{0}}\;}}\right|\;\lVert\tilde{x}-x\rVert_{\infty}\leq\epsilon}}\right\}\text{.}

(6)

For an input set
\mathcal{X}=\pi_{\epsilon}(x)\subset\mathbb{R}^{n_{0}}
, we formally verify the robustness of a neural network
N_{\theta}
by using set-based computations to efficiently compute (in polynomial time) an enclosure
\mathcal{Y}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n_{\kappa}}
of its output set
\mathcal{Y}^{*}\coloneqq N_{\theta}(\mathcal{X})\subseteq\mathcal{Y}
.
If
\mathcal{Y}
does not contain unsafe outputs, we have formally verified the neural network for the input set
\mathcal{X}
.

\mathcal{U}_{t}\coloneqq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\kappa}}\;\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\;\exists k\in[n_{\kappa}]\colon y_{(k)}>y_{(l)}}}\right.}}\right\}\text{.}

Proposition 6

For an input set

II-E

Problem Statement

We want to leverage set-based training to create robust neural networks.

Definition 4

(Set-Based Training)
.

Given a set-based loss function
\mathcal{L}:\mathbb{R}^{n_{\kappa}}\times 2^{\mathbb{R}^{n_{\kappa}}}\to\mathbb{R}
, set-based training trains a neural network using a gradient set
\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!\mathcal{Y}}\mathcal{L}(t,\mathcal{Y})\subset\mathbb{R}^{n_{\kappa}}
(
Definition

3
), which defines a different gradient
g\in\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!\mathcal{Y}}\mathcal{L}(t,\mathcal{Y})
for each point
y\in\mathcal{Y}
.

Set-based training can directly enforce smaller output sets by choosing gradients that point toward the center of an output set (
Figure

4
). Thereby, we can simultaneously improve the robustness of the neural network and simplify the formal verification.
Therefore, we want to use set-based training to train the parameters
\theta
of a robust neural network
N_{\theta}
:

\min_{\theta}\sum_{(x_{i},t_{i})\in\mathcal{D}}\mathcal{L}\bigl(t_{i},\mathtt{enclose}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N_{\theta},\pi_{\epsilon}(x_{i})}}\right)\bigr)\text{.}

By reducing the size of the output set using set-based training, we can verify neural networks with fast (polynomial time) algorithms.

III
Set-Based Training of Neural Networks

We present a novel set-based algorithm to train robust neural networks with a gradient set (
Definition

4
).
In each training iteration, we

(i)

enclose the output set of the neural network by zonotopes (
Section

IV-A
),

(ii)

compute a gradient set derived from a set-based loss function using features of the output enclosure (
Section

III-A
),

(iii)

backpropagate the gradient set (
Section

IV-B
), and

(iv)

aggregate over the gradient set to update the parameters of the neural network (
Section

IV-C
).

III-A

Set-Based Loss Function and Gradient Set

We derive the gradient set used for training by computing the gradient of a set-based loss function which maps an output set to a loss value (
Definition

4
).
In this work, we define the set-based loss function
\mathcal{L}:\mathbb{R}^{n_{\kappa}}\times 2^{\mathbb{R}^{n_{\kappa}}}\to\mathbb{R}
so that it combines

(i)

a standard loss of the center of the enclosure (for accuracy) with

(ii)

the F-radius (
Proposition

5
) of the enclosure to approximate its size (for robustness) (
Figure

1
c).

Definition 5

(Set-Based Loss)
.

Given a loss function
L\colon\mathbb{R}^{n_{\kappa}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{\kappa}}\to\mathbb{R}
, we define a set-based loss function as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(t,\mathcal{Y})\coloneqq\underbrace{(1-\tau)\,L(t,c_{\kappa})}_{\text{Accuracy}}+\underbrace{\nicefrac{{\tau}}{{\epsilon}}\,\lVert\mathcal{Y}\rVert_{F}}_{\text{Robustness}}\text{,}

where
\epsilon\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
is the perturbation radius and
\mathcal{Y}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<c_{\kappa},G_{\kappa}}}\right>_{Z}
is an output enclosure.

The set-based loss function balances the center loss and the F-radius using a hyperparameter
\tau\in[0,1]
, controlling the trade-off between accuracy and robustness. To make tuning the hyperparameter
\tau
easier, the F-radius in
Definition

5
is normalized using the input perturbation radius
\epsilon\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
.
The normalization is derived by taking the ratio of the F-radii of output enclosure
\mathcal{Y}
and input set
\mathcal{X}=\pi_{\epsilon}(x)
:
\lVert\mathcal{Y}\rVert_{F}/\lVert\pi_{\epsilon}(x)\rVert_{F}\overset{\text{\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{def:eps_perturbed_inputs}}}{=}\nicefrac{{1}}{{\epsilon}}\,\lVert\mathcal{Y}\rVert_{F}
.

Our set-based loss generalizes the well-established tradeoff-loss
[
23
, Eq. 5]
, which combines a standard training loss (first summand) with a boundary loss (second summand):

L_{\text{TRADES}}(t,y)=L(t,y)+\max_{\tilde{x}\in\pi_{\epsilon}(x)}\nicefrac{{1}}{{\lambda}}\,L(y,N_{\theta}(\tilde{x}))\text{,}

where
y=N_{\theta}(x)
and
\lambda
is a weighting factor.

The F-radius in our set-based loss captures the size of an output set in all dimensions and corresponds to the boundary loss, which only considers the size of the output set along a single direction.
Moreover, through the sound set-based computations (
Proposition

6
), the set-based loss accurately over-approximates the size of the output set. In contrast, the boundary loss is only approximated in
[
23
]
.

Given the set-based loss function (
Definition

5
), we compute its gradient to derive the gradient set.
The second term of the set-based loss function is the F-radius of the output set, the gradient of which is computed as follows.

Proposition 7

(Gradient of F-Radius)
.

The gradient of the F-radius is

\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!\mathcal{Y}}\lVert\mathcal{Y}\rVert_{F}=\frac{1}{n_{\kappa}\,\lVert\mathcal{Y}\rVert_{F}}\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\mathbf{0},G_{\kappa}}}\right>_{Z}\text{,}

where
\mathcal{Y}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<c_{\kappa},G_{\kappa}}}\right>_{Z}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n_{\kappa}}
.

Proof.

See
Appendix

B
.
∎

Figure

); hence, minimizing the F-radius of a zonotope reduces the size of the zonotope.
With
Proposition

7

Proposition 8

(Set-Based Loss Gradient)
.

The gradient of the set-based loss function
\mathcal{L}
is

\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!\mathcal{Y}}\mathcal{L}(t,\mathcal{Y})=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<(1-\tau)\,\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!c_{\kappa}}L(t,c_{\kappa}),\frac{\tau}{\epsilon\,n_{\kappa}\,\lVert\mathcal{Y}\rVert_{F}}\,G_{\kappa}}}\right>_{Z}\kern-6.13329pt,\phantom{\scriptstyle Z}

where
\mathcal{Y}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<c_{\kappa},G_{\kappa}}}\right>_{Z}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n_{\kappa}}

We require efficient, batch-wise, and differentiable set propagation for the implementation of set-based training. In this section, we first define a forward propagation with the required properties (

IV-A

Forward Propagation

The set propagation through linear layers is computed with an affine map, which can be efficiently implemented batch-wise using matrix multiplications on a GPU. Further, we want to efficiently compute batch-wise image enclosures of nonlinear layers.
Sampling-based methods
[
18
,
17
]
, are impractical for this task, because they use polynomial regression and thus are not efficient enough and cannot be used for backpropagation.
In contrast,
[
16
]
derives fast analytical solutions for the approximation errors of a specific linear approximation of s-shaped activation functions; however, this approach causes large approximation errors. During training, large approximation errors induce an over-regularization and therefore lead to poor performance
[
8
]
.
To address this issue, we derive analytical solutions for the approximation errors of an arbitrary monotonically increasing linear approximation for three typical activation functions: rectified linear unit (
\relu
), hyperbolic tangent, and logistic sigmoid.
Secondly, we provide a linear approximation whose approximation errors are smaller or equal to Singh’s enclosure
[
16
, Thm. 3.2]
while being equally fast to compute.

For the remainder of this section, let
\phi\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a monotonically increasing function, which is approximated by a linear function
p\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R},\,x\mapsto a\,x+b
within an interval
[l,u]\subset\mathbb{R}
.
The approximation errors of
p
are given by the largest lower distance and upper distance between
\mu
and
p
(
Figure

5
).

Definition 6

(Approximation Error of Linear Approximation)
.

The approximation error of a linear approximation
p
for
\phi
within the interval

are defined as

\displaystyle\underline{d}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\min_{x\in[l,u]}\phi(x)-p(x)\text{,}

\displaystyle\overline{d}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\max_{x\in[l,u]}\phi(x)-p(x)\text{.}

We enclose the output of the activation function
\phi
as,

\forall x\in[l,u]\colon\phi(x)\in p(x)+d_{c}\oplus[-d,d]\text{,}

where
d_{c}=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}\,(\overline{d}+\underline{d})
and
d=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}\,(\overline{d}-\underline{d})
.

We efficiently find the approximation errors

Definition 7

Within the interval

\displaystyle a

IV-B

Set-Based Backpropagation

We now derive the corresponding set-based backpropagation.
Analogous to the point-based backpropagation (
Proposition

1
), the set-based backpropagation computes for every layer of the neural network the gradient set w.r.t. the output set
\mathcal{H}_{k}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<c_{k},G_{k}}}\right>_{Z}
:

\mathcal{G}_{k}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<c^{\prime}_{k},G^{\prime}_{k}}}\right>_{Z}\coloneqq\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!\mathcal{H}_{k}}\mathcal{L}(t,\mathcal{Y})\text{.}

(9)

The set-based backpropagation of linear layers is straightforward as it just applies an affine map (
Proposition

1
). However, the backpropagation of nonlinear layers is more involved: while the image enclosure only uses linear approximations, these depend on the input set. Thus, intuitively, we have to apply the product rule.

Proposition 10

(Backpropagation through Image Enclosure)
.

Assume the
k
-th layer is a nonlinear layer with activation function
\phi_{k}
. Given an input set
\mathcal{H}_{k-1}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<c_{k-1},G_{k-1}}}\right>_{Z}
with
G_{k-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{k-1}\times p}
and a gradient set
\mathcal{G}_{k}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<c^{\prime}_{k},G^{\prime}_{k}}}\right>_{Z}
, the gradient set
\mathcal{G}_{k-1}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<c^{\prime}_{k-1},G^{\prime}_{k-1}}}\right>_{Z}
is computed for each dimension
i\in[n_{k}]
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{k-1(i)}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(c^{\prime}_{k(i)}\,c_{k-1(i)}+G^{\prime}_{k(i,[p])}\,G_{k-1(i,\cdot)}^{\top}}}\right)\,\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!\mathcal{H}_{k-1(i)}}a_{k(i)}

\displaystyle\quad+\mathcal{G}_{k(i)}\,a_{k(i)}+c^{\prime}_{k(i)}\,\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!\mathcal{H}_{k-1(i)}}d_{c,k(i)}

\displaystyle\quad+G^{\prime}_{k(i,p+i)}\,\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!\mathcal{H}_{k-1(i)}}d_{k(i)}\text{.}

VI
Conclusion

This paper introduces the first set-based training procedure for neural networks that uses gradient sets: During training, we enclose the output set of the neural network using set propagation and derive a gradient set, which contains a different gradient for each possible output. By choosing gradients that point to the center of the output set, we can directly reduce the size of the output set. Thereby, we can simultaneously improve robustness and simplify the formal verification.
The set-based training is made possible by a fast, batch-wise, and differentiable propagation of zonotopes which utilizes analytical solutions for approximation errors.
Our experimental results demonstrate that our set-based approach effectively trains robust neural networks, which have competitive performance and admit fast verification (in polynomial time). Thereby, we demonstrate that gradient sets can be effectively used to train robust neural networks. Hence, set-based training represents a promising new direction for robust neural network training and a significant step toward fast verification of neural networks and, thus, the widespread adoption of formal verification of neural networks.

Improvements for Scalability

The complexity of our set-based training depends on the number of generators of the output set used during training (
Proposition

14
). We use two methods to reduce the number of generators:

(i)

We propagate the approximation errors as intervals through the neural network and compute the Minkowski sum at the end.

(ii)

We use input sets with fewer number of generators.

Our set-based training can use any zonotopic input set and is not limited to
\ell_{\infty}
-input sets. Building on previous work
[
8
]
, we use adversarial attacks to construct smaller input sets that focus on critical regions of the input; thereby, the regularization through large approximation errors is reduced. Previous works only use a single adversarial attack to construct a smaller
\ell_{\infty}
-input set
[
8
]
. We extend this idea and use several adversarial attacks to construct zonotopic input sets: We shift the center of the input set to the average attack and use scaled directions of the attacks as generators for the input set. Given an input
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{0}}
, we compute
\lambda

\tilde{x}_{i}=x+\delta_{i}
for

(e.g., using FGSM). The input set is constructed as

\mathcal{X}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<x+\nicefrac{{1}}{{\lambda}}\,\sum_{\lambda}^{i=1}\delta_{i},\nicefrac{{1}}{{\lambda}}\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\begin{matrix}\delta_{1}&\delta_{2}&\cdots&\delta_{\lambda}\end{matrix}}}\right]}}\right>_{Z}\kern-6.11111pt.\phantom{\scriptstyle Z}

\displaystyle{=}(1-\tau)\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!c_{\kappa}}L(t,c_{\kappa}),\mathbf{0}}}\right>_{Z}

\displaystyle{=}(1-\tau)\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!c_{\kappa}}L(t,c_{\kappa}),\mathbf{0}}}\right>_{Z}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\tau}{\epsilon\,n_{\kappa}\,\lVert\mathcal{Y}\rVert_{F}}\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\mathbf{0},G_{\kappa}}}\right>_{Z}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<(1-\tau)\,\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!c_{\kappa}}L(t,c_{\kappa}),\frac{\tau}{\epsilon\,n_{\kappa}\,\lVert\mathcal{Y}\rVert_{F}}\,G_{\kappa}}}\right>_{Z}\kern-6.11111pt.\phantom{\scriptstyle Z}\qed

Moreover, in this section, we give three proofs for the set-based backpropagation:

(i)

set-based backpropagation through an image enclosure (
Proposition

10
),

(ii)

set-based backpropagation through all layers of a neural network (
Proposition

11
), and

(iii)

set-based weight and bias update (
Proposition

12
).

Before we prove the propositions required for the set-based backpropagation, we unfold
Equation

9
and rewrite the gradient set
\mathcal{G}_{k-1}
using the chain rule for partial derivatives.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{k}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<c_{k},G_{k}}}\right>_{Z}
with
G_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{k}\times q}
be the output set of the
k
-th layer and let
\mathcal{G}_{k}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<c^{\prime}_{k},G^{\prime}_{k}}}\right>_{Z}
be the gradient set w.r.t.
\mathcal{H}_{k}
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{G}_{k-1}&\overset{\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{eq:set-backprop-grads}}{=}\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!\mathcal{H}_{k-1}}\mathcal{L}(t,\mathcal{Y})\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{k}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(t,\mathcal{Y})}{\partial c_{k(i)}}\,\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!\mathcal{H}_{k-1}}c_{k(i)}\\
&\quad+\sum_{i=1}^{n_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{q}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(t,\mathcal{Y})}{\partial G_{k(i,j)}}\,\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!\mathcal{H}_{k-1}}G_{k(i,j)}\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{k}}c^{\prime}_{k(i)}\,\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!\mathcal{H}_{k-1}}c_{k(i)}\\
&\quad+\sum_{i=1}^{n_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{q}G^{\prime}_{k(i,j)}\,\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!\mathcal{H}_{k-1}}G_{k(i,j)}\text{.}\end{split}

(19)

Note: the plus symbol (
+
) between zonotopes denotes their element-wise addition:
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<c_{1},G_{1}}}\right>_{Z}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<c_{2},G_{2}}}\right>_{Z}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<c_{1}+c_{2},G_{1}+G_{2}}}\right>_{Z}
, whereas the Minkowski sum is denoted as
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<c_{1},G_{1}}}\right>_{Z}\oplus\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<c_{2},G_{2}}}\right>_{Z}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<c_{1}+c_{2},\begin{bmatrix}{\displaystyle G_{1}}&{\displaystyle G_{2}}\end{bmatrix}}}\right>_{Z}

Proposition
10

(Backpropagation through Image Enclosure)
.

Assume the
k
-th layer is a nonlinear layer with activation function
\phi_{k}
. Given an input set
\mathcal{H}_{k-1}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<c_{k-1},G_{k-1}}}\right>_{Z}
with
G_{k-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{k-1}\times p}
and a gradient set
\mathcal{G}_{k}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<c^{\prime}_{k},G^{\prime}_{k}}}\right>_{Z}
, the gradient set
\mathcal{G}_{k-1}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<c^{\prime}_{k-1},G^{\prime}_{k-1}}}\right>_{Z}
is computed for each dimension
i\in[n_{k}]
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{k-1(i)}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(c^{\prime}_{k(i)}\,c_{k-1(i)}+G^{\prime}_{k(i,[p])}\,G_{k-1(i,\cdot)}^{\top}}}\right)\,\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!\mathcal{H}_{k-1(i)}}a_{k(i)}

\displaystyle\quad+\mathcal{G}_{k(i)}\,a_{k(i)}+c^{\prime}_{k(i)}\,\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!\mathcal{H}_{k-1(i)}}d_{c,k(i)}

\displaystyle\quad+G^{\prime}_{k(i,p+i)}\,\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!\mathcal{H}_{k-1(i)}}d_{k(i)}\text{.}

The operation
\mathtt{backpropEnclose}
computes the gradient set of our image enclosure (
Proposition

10
).

Proof.

The image enclosure adds
n_{k}
generators, hence the input set
\mathcal{H}_{k-1}
has
n_{k}
generators less than gradient set
\mathcal{G}_{k}
, i.e.
G_{k-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{k}\times p}
and
G^{\prime}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{k}\times q}
with
q=p+n_{k}
.
We split
Equation

19
into three summands:

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{k-1,c}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{k}}c^{\prime}_{k(i)}\,\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!\mathcal{H}_{k-1}}c_{k(i)}\text{,}

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{k-1,p}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{p}G^{\prime}_{k(i,j)}\,\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!\mathcal{H}_{k-1}}G_{k(i,j)}\text{,}

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{k-1,q}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{k}}\sum_{j=p+1}^{q}G^{\prime}_{k(i,j)}\,\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!\mathcal{H}_{k-1}}G_{k(i,j)}\text{.}

Hence,

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{k-1}=\mathcal{G}_{k-1,c}+\mathcal{G}_{k-1,p}+\mathcal{G}_{k-1,q}\text{.}

(20)

Furthermore, the input set
\mathcal{H}_{k-1}
is enclosed by the interval
[l_{k-1},u_{k-1}]
, where
l_{k-1}=c_{k-1}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|G_{k-1}}}\right|\,\mathbf{1}
and
u_{k-1}=c_{k-1}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|G_{k-1}}}\right|\,\mathbf{1}
(
Proposition

2
). Moreover, let
e_{i}\in\{0,1\}^{n_{k}}
be the
i
-th standard basis vector.

Firstly, we derive the gradient
\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!\mathcal{H}_{k-1}}c_{k(i)}
needed for
\mathcal{G}_{k-1,c}
, for which we need the gradients of center
c_{k(i)}=a_{k(i)}\,c_{k-1(i)}+d_{c,k(i)}
w.r.t. the input set
\mathcal{H}_{k-1}
for each dimension
i\in[n_{k}]
. The image enclosure is applied for each dimension individually; therefore, we can consider each dimension separately because for any dimensions
i,j\in[n_{k-1}]
, where
i\neq j
:

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!\mathcal{H}_{k-1(j)}}c_{k(i)}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<0,\mathbf{0}}}\right>_{Z}\text{,}

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!\mathcal{H}_{k-1(j)}}G_{k(i,\cdot)}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<0,\mathbf{0}}}\right>_{Z}\text{.}

(21)

Let
i\in[n_{k}]
be a fixed dimension and
j\in[p]
a fixed index of a generator.
We require the gradient of the slope
a_{k(i)}
and the offset
d_{c,k(i)}
. The gradient of the slope
a_{k(i)}
is:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial a_{k(i)}}{\partial c_{k-1(i)}}

\displaystyle{=}\frac{\phi^{\prime}(u_{k-1(i)})-\phi^{\prime}(l_{k-1(i)})}{u_{k-1(i)}-l_{k-1(i)}}\text{,}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial a_{k(i)}}{\partial G_{k-1(i,j)}}

\displaystyle{=}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\phi^{\prime}(u_{k-1(i)})+\phi^{\prime}(l_{k-1(i)})-2\,a_{k(i)}}{u_{k-1(i)}-l_{k-1(i)}}}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\sign(G_{k-1(i,j)})\text{.}

Let
\overline{x}_{k}
and
\underline{x}_{k}
be the points of the approximation errors
\overline{d}_{k}
and
\underline{d}_{k}
:

\displaystyle\overline{x}_{k}

\displaystyle=\argmax_{x\in\mathcal{P}}\phi_{k}(x)-p_{k}(x)\text{,}

\displaystyle\underline{x}_{k}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{x\in\mathcal{P}}\phi_{k}(x)-p_{k}(x)\text{.}

To prevent repetitions in this proof, let
g
denote the center, or an arbitrary generator of the input set
\mathcal{H}_{k-1}
:

g\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{c_{k-1},G_{k-1(\cdot,1)},G_{k-1(\cdot,2)},\dots,G_{k-1(\cdot,p)}}}\right\}\text{.}

For
(x,d)\in\{(\overline{x}_{k},\overline{d}_{k}),(\underline{x}_{k},\underline{d}_{k})\}
, we apply the chain rule and the product-rule to derive the gradients of the approximation error
d_{(i)}
:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial d_{(i)}}{\partial g_{(i)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\phi_{k}(x_{(i)})-a_{k(i)}\,x_{(i)}}}\right)}{\partial g_{(i)}}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\phi^{\prime}_{k}(x_{(i)})-a_{k(i)}}}\right)\,\frac{\partial x_{(i)}}{\partial g_{(i)}}-\frac{\partial a_{k(i)}}{\partial g_{(i)}}\,x_{(i)}\text{.}

Please recall the offset
d_{c,k}=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\overline{d}_{k}+\underline{d}_{k}}}\right)
and approximation error
d_{k}=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\overline{d}_{k}-\underline{d}_{k}}}\right)
(
Proposition

9
); hence,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial d_{c,k(i)}}{\partial g_{(i)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\partial\overline{d}_{k(i)}}{\partial g_{(i)}}+\frac{\partial\underline{d}_{k(i)}}{\partial g_{(i)}}}}\right)\text{,}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial d_{k(i)}}{\partial g_{(i)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\partial\overline{d}_{k(i)}}{\partial g_{(i)}}-\frac{\partial\underline{d}_{k(i)}}{\partial g_{(i)}}}}\right)\text{.}

Using the gradient of the slope
a_{k(i)}
and the gradient of the offset
d_{c,k(i)}
, we derive the gradient of the center
c_{k(i)}
:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial c_{k(i)}}{\partial c_{k-1(i)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a_{k(i)}\,c_{k-1(i)}+d_{c,k(i)}}}\right)}{\partial c_{k-1(i)}}

\displaystyle=a_{k(i)}+\frac{\partial a_{k(i)}}{\partial c_{k-1(i)}}\,c_{k-1(i)}+\frac{\partial d_{c,k(i)}}{\partial c_{k-1(i)}}\text{,}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial c_{k(i)}}{\partial G_{k-1(i,j)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a_{k(i)}\,c_{k-1(i)}+d_{c,k(i)}}}\right)}{\partial G_{k-1(i,j)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial a_{k(i)}}{\partial G_{k-1(i,j)}}\,c_{k-1(i)}+\frac{\partial d_{c,k(i)}}{\partial G_{k-1(i,j)}}\text{.}

Hence, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!\mathcal{H}_{k-1(i)}}c_{k(i)}&=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\frac{\partial c_{k(i)}}{\partial c_{k-1(i)}},\frac{\partial c_{k(i)}}{\partial G_{k-1(i,\cdot)}}}}\right>_{Z}\\
&=a_{k(i)}+\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!\mathcal{H}_{k-1(i)}}a_{k(i)}\,c_{k-1(i)}\\
&\quad+\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!\mathcal{H}_{k-1(i)}}d_{c,k(i)}\text{.}\end{split}

(22)

Secondly, we derive the gradient
\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!\mathcal{H}_{k-1(i)}}G_{k(i,j)}
needed for
\mathcal{G}_{k-1,p}
. Let
j^{\prime}\in[n_{k}]
be a different generator index:
j^{\prime}\neq j
; the gradient of
G_{k(i,j)}
is

\displaystyle\frac{\partial G_{k(i,j)}}{\partial c_{k-1(i)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a_{k(i)}\,G_{k-1(i,j)}}}\right)}{\partial c_{k-1(i)}}=\frac{\partial a_{k(i)}}{\partial c_{k-1(i)}}\,G_{k-1(i,j)}\text{,}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial G_{k(i,j)}}{\partial G_{k-1(i,j)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a_{k(i)}\,G_{k-1(i,j)}}}\right)}{\partial G_{k-1(i,j)}}

\displaystyle=a_{k(i)}+\frac{\partial a_{k(i)}}{\partial G_{k-1(i,j)}}\,G_{k-1(i,j)}\text{,}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial G_{k(i,j)}}{\partial G_{k-1(i,j^{\prime})}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a_{k(i)}\,G_{k-1(i,j)}}}\right)}{\partial G_{k-1(i,j^{\prime})}}=\frac{\partial a_{k(i)}}{\partial G_{k-1(i,j^{\prime})}}\,G_{k-1(i,j)}\text{.}

Hence, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!\mathcal{H}_{k-1(i)}}G_{k(i,j)}&=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\frac{\partial G_{k(i,j)}}{\partial c_{k-1(i)}},\frac{\partial G_{k(i,j)}}{\partial G_{k-1(i,\cdot)}}}}\right>_{Z}\\
&=\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!\mathcal{H}_{k-1(i)}}a_{k(i)}\,G_{k-1(i,j)}\\
&\quad+a_{k(i)}\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\mathbf{0},e_{i}\,e_{j}^{\top}}}\right>_{Z}\text{.}\end{split}

(23)

Thirdly, we derive
\mathcal{G}_{k-1,q}
. Please recall, the diagonal entries of
G_{k(\cdot,p+[n_{k}])}
contain the approximation errors, while the non-diagonal entries are
0
; hence, the gradient of any non-diagonal entry
j^{\prime}\in[q]\colon j^{\prime}>p\wedge j^{\prime}\neq p+i
is
0
:
\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!\mathcal{H}_{k-1(i)}}G_{k(i,j^{\prime})}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<0,\mathbf{0}}}\right>_{Z}
.
Hence, we can simplify
\mathcal{G}_{k-1,q}
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{G}_{k-1,q(i)}&=\sum_{j=p+1}^{q}G^{\prime}_{k(i,j)}\,\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!\mathcal{H}_{k-1(i)}}G_{k(i,j)}\\
&=G^{\prime}_{k(i,p+i)}\,\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!\mathcal{H}_{k-1(i)}}d_{k(i)}\text{.}\end{split}

(24)

We add
\mathcal{G}_{k-1,c}
and
\mathcal{G}_{k-1,p}
together and reorder the terms:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathcal{G}_{k-1,c(i)}+\mathcal{G}_{k-1,p(i)}\\
&=c^{\prime}_{k(i)}\,\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!\mathcal{H}_{k-1(i)}}c_{k(i)}+\sum_{j=1}^{p}G^{\prime}_{k(i,j)}\,\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!\mathcal{H}_{k-1(i)}}G_{k(i,j)}\\
&\overset{\text{\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{eq:prop:bpfastenc:2}}}{=}c^{\prime}_{k(i)}\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a_{k(i)}+\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!\mathcal{H}_{k-1(i)}}a_{k(i)}\,c_{k-1(i)}+\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!\mathcal{H}_{k-1(i)}}d_{c,k(i)}}}\right)\\
&\quad+\sum_{j=1}^{p}G^{\prime}_{k(i,j)}\,\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!\mathcal{H}_{k-1(i)}}G_{k(i,j)}\\
&\overset{\text{\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{eq:prop:bpfastenc:3}}}{=}c^{\prime}_{k(i)}\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a_{k(i)}+\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!\mathcal{H}_{k-1(i)}}a_{k(i)}\,c_{k-1(i)}+\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!\mathcal{H}_{k-1(i)}}d_{c,k(i)}}}\right)\\
&\quad+\sum_{j=1}^{p}G^{\prime}_{k(i,j)}\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!\mathcal{H}_{k-1(i)}}a_{k(i)}\,G_{k-1(i,j)}+a_{k(i)}\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\mathbf{0},e_{i}\,e_{j}^{\top}}}\right>_{Z}}}\right)\\
&=\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!\mathcal{H}_{k-1(i)}}a_{k(i)}\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(c_{k-1(i)}\,c^{\prime}_{k(i)}+G^{\top}_{k-1(i,\cdot)}\,G^{\prime}_{k(i,[p])}}}\right)\\
&\quad+\mathcal{G}_{k(i)}\,a_{k(i)}+c^{\prime}_{k(i)}\,\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!\mathcal{H}_{k-1(i)}}d_{c,k(i)}\text{.}\end{split}

(25)

Finally, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{k-1(i)}

\displaystyle{=}\mathcal{G}_{k-1,c(i)}+\mathcal{G}_{k-1,p(i)}+\mathcal{G}_{k-1,q(I)}

\displaystyle{=}\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!\mathcal{H}_{k-1(i)}}a_{k(i)}\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(c_{k-1(i)}\,c^{\prime}_{k(i)}+G^{\prime}_{k(i,[p])}\,G_{k-1(i,\cdot)}^{\top}}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\mathcal{G}_{k(i)}\,a_{k(i)}+c^{\prime}_{k(i)}\,\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!\mathcal{H}_{k-1(i)}}d_{c,k(i)}+\mathcal{G}_{k-1,q(i)}

\displaystyle{=}\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!\mathcal{H}_{k-1(i)}}a_{k(i)}\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(c_{k-1(i)}\,c^{\prime}_{k(i)}+G^{\prime}_{k(i,[p])}\,G_{k-1(i,\cdot)}^{\top}}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\mathcal{G}_{k(i)}\,a_{k(i)}+c^{\prime}_{k(i)}\,\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!\mathcal{H}_{k-1(i)}}d_{c,k(i)}

\displaystyle\quad+G^{\prime}_{k(i,p+i)}\,\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!\mathcal{H}_{k-1(i)}}d_{k(i)}\text{.}\qed

Proposition
11

(Set-Based Backpropagation)
.

The gradient sets
\mathcal{G}_{k}
are computed in reverse order as

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\kappa}

\displaystyle=\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!\mathcal{Y}}\mathcal{L}(t,\mathcal{Y})\text{,}

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{k-1}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}W_{k}^{\top}\,\mathcal{G}_{k}&\text{if $k$-th layer is linear,}\\
\mathtt{backpropEnclose}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\phi_{k},\mathcal{G}_{k}}}\right)&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

for all
k\in\{\kappa,\dots,1\}
.

Proof.

If
k=\kappa
, we compute the gradient of the set-based loss according to
Proposition

8
. We assume
k<\kappa
.
Let
\mathcal{G}_{k}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<c^{\prime}_{k},G^{\prime}_{k}}}\right>_{Z}
and
\mathcal{H}_{k}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<c_{k},G_{k}}}\right>_{Z}
.

We split cases on the type of the
k
-th layer and simplify the terms.

Case (i).

The
k
-th layer is linear. For dimension
i\in[n_{k}]
, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!\mathcal{H}_{k-1}}c_{k(i)}&=\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!\mathcal{H}_{k-1}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(W_{k(i,\cdot)}\,c_{k-1}+b_{k(i)}}}\right)\\
&=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<W_{k(i,\cdot)}^{\top},\mathbf{0}}}\right>_{Z}\text{,}\\
\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!\mathcal{H}_{k-1}}G_{k(i,j)}&=\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!\mathcal{H}_{k-1}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(W_{k(i,\cdot)}\,G_{k-1(\cdot,j)}}}\right)\\
&=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\mathbf{0},W_{k(i,\cdot)}^{\top}\,e_{j}^{\top}}}\right>_{Z}\text{.}\end{split}

(26)

Thus,

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{k-1}

\displaystyle{=}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{k}}c^{\prime}_{k(i)}\,\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!\mathcal{H}_{k-1}}c_{k(i)}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{i=1}^{n_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{q}G^{\prime}_{k(i,j)}\,\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!\mathcal{H}_{k-1}}G_{k(i,j)}

\displaystyle{=}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{k}}c^{\prime}_{k(i)}\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<W_{k(i,\cdot)}^{\top},\mathbf{0}}}\right>_{Z}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{i=1}^{n_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{q}G^{\prime}_{k(i,j)}\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\mathbf{0},W_{k(i,\cdot)}^{\top}\,\,e_{j}^{\top}}}\right>_{Z}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\sum_{i=1}^{n_{k}}W_{k(i,\cdot)}^{\top}\,c^{\prime}_{k(i)},\sum_{i=1}^{n_{k}}W_{k(i,\cdot)}^{\top}\,G^{\prime}_{k(i,\cdot)}}}\right>_{Z}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<W_{k}^{\top}\,c^{\prime}_{k},W_{k}^{\top}\,G^{\prime}_{k}}}\right>_{Z}

\displaystyle=W_{k}^{\top}\,\mathcal{G}_{k}\text{.}

Case (ii).

The
k
-th layer is nonlinear.
Proposition

10
proves the correctness.

∎

Proposition
12

(Gradient Set w.r.t. Weights and Bias)
.

The gradients of the set-based loss w.r.t. a weight matrix and a bias vector are

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!W_{k}}\mathcal{L}(t,\mathcal{Y})

\displaystyle=\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!\mathcal{H}_{k}}\mathcal{L}(t,\mathcal{Y})\odot\mathcal{H}_{k-1}^{\top}

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!b_{k}}\mathcal{L}(t,\mathcal{Y})

\displaystyle=\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!\mathcal{H}_{k}}\mathcal{L}(t,\mathcal{Y})\odot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<1,\mathbf{0}}}\right>_{Z}^{\top}\text{.}

Proof.

We rewrite the gradient by applying the chain rule for partial derivatives:

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!W_{k}}\mathcal{L}(t,\mathcal{Y})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{k}}c^{\prime}_{k(i)}\,\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!W_{k}}c_{k(i)}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{i=1}^{n_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{q}G^{\prime}_{k(i,j)}\,\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!W_{k}}G_{k(i,j)}\text{,}

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!b_{k}}\mathcal{L}(t,\mathcal{Y})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{k}}c^{\prime}_{k(i)}\,\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!b_{k}}c_{k(i)}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{i=1}^{n_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{q}G^{\prime}_{k(i,j)}\,\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!b_{k}}G_{k(i,j)}\text{,}

where
G_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{k}\times q}
. Moreover, we have for dimension
i\in[n_{k}]
and generator index
j\in[q]
:

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!W_{k}}c_{k(i)}

\displaystyle=\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!W_{k}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(W_{k(i,\cdot)}\,c_{k-1}+b_{k(i)}}}\right)=e_{i}\,c_{k-1}^{\top}\text{,}

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!W_{k}}G_{k(i,j)}

\displaystyle=\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!W_{k}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(W_{k(i,\cdot)}\,G_{k-1(\cdot,j)}}}\right)=e_{i}\,G_{k-1(\cdot,j)}^{\top}\text{,}

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!b_{k}}c_{k(i)}

\displaystyle=\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!b_{k}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(W_{k(i,\cdot)}\,c_{k-1}+b_{k(i)}}}\right)=e_{i}\text{,}

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!b_{k}}G_{k(i,j)}

\displaystyle=\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!b_{k}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(W_{k(i,\cdot)}\,G_{k-1(\cdot,j)}}}\right)=\mathbf{0}\text{,}

where
e_{i}\in\{0,1\}^{n_{k}}
is the
i
-th standard basis vector. Thus,

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!W_{k}}\mathcal{L}(t,\mathcal{Y})

\displaystyle=c^{\prime}_{k}\,c_{k-1}^{\top}+G^{\prime}_{k}\,G_{k-1}^{\top}=\mathcal{G}_{k}\odot\mathcal{H}_{k-1}^{\top}\text{,}

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\nabla_{\!b_{k}}\mathcal{L}(t,\mathcal{Y})

\displaystyle=c^{\prime}_{k}=\mathcal{G}_{k}\odot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<1,\mathbf{0}}}\right>_{Z}^{\top}\text{.}\qed

Proposition
14
.

The zonotopes used in
Algorithm

2
have at most
q\leq n_{0}+\sum_{k\in[\kappa]}n_{k}
number of generators.
Let
n_{\text{max}}\coloneqq\max_{k\in[\kappa]}n_{k-1}\,n_{k}
be the maximum size of a weight matrix in the neural network.
Moreover,
Algorithm

2
has time complexity
\mathcal{O}(n_{\text{max}}\,q\,\kappa)
w.r.t.
n_{\text{max}}
,
q
and the number of layers
\kappa
.

Proof.

The initial
\epsilon
-perturbance set has
n_{0}
generators (
Algorithm

2
) and every nonlinear layer adds
n_{k}
new generators for the approximation errors (
Algorithm

1
). Moreover, there are at most
