Deterministic and Stochastic Accelerated Gradient Method for Convex Semi-Infinite Optimization Thanks: This work is jointly supported by the University of Iowa Jumpstarting Tomorrow Program and NSF award 2147253.
Yao Yao, Qihang Lin, Tianbao Yang

Abstract
This paper explores numerical methods for solving a convex differentiable semi-infinite program. We introduce a primal-dual gradient method which performs three updates iteratively: a momentum gradient ascend step to update the constraint parameters, a momentum gradient ascend step to update the dual variables, and a gradient descend step to update the primal variables. Our approach also extends to scenarios where gradients and function values are accessible solely through stochastic oracles. This method extends the recent primal-dual methods, for example, [ 25 , 7 ] , for optimization with a finite number of constraints. We show the iteration complexity of the proposed method for finding an ϵ \epsilon -optimal solution under different convexity and concavity assumptions on the functions.

Assumption 1.1
(Convexity and compactness).

The following statements hold.

a.

{\mathcal{X}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}^{i}\subset\mathbb{R}^{q}
,
i=1,\dots,m
are convex and closed.

b.

There exists

such that

for any
{\mathbf{y}}^{i},{\mathbf{y}}^{i\prime}\in{\mathcal{Y}}^{i}

c.

d.

.

e.

\mu_{y}
-strongly concave in
{\mathbf{y}}^{i}
for

.

Note that
\mu_{f}
and

can be zero. As shown in this paper, the theoretical complexity of the studied method depends on whether
\mu_{f}

-strongly convex in
{\mathbf{x}}
for

Let
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{m})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{m}
be the Lagrangian multipliers of the constraints in (
1.1
). We assume that there exist Lagrangian multipliers that satisfy the optimality conditions together with the optimal solution of (
1.1
).

Assumption 1.2
(Existence of optimal multipliers).

For any optimal solution
{\mathbf{x}}^{*}
of (
1.1
), there exists
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{m}
such that

\displaystyle{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\in\argmin_{{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathcal{X}}}\left[f({\mathbf{x}})+({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{*})^{\top}{\mathbf{g}}^{*}({\mathbf{x}})\right]\text{ and }\lambda_{i}^{*}g_{i}^{*}({\mathbf{x}}^{*})=0,\quad i=1,\dots,m,

where

\displaystyle{\mathbf{g}}^{*}({\mathbf{x}})=(g_{1}^{*}({\mathbf{x}}),\dots,g_{m}^{*}({\mathbf{x}}))^{\top}.

(1.4)

In addition, we assume
f
and
g_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,m
, are real-valued and continuous. We consider both smooth and non-smooth cases. In the smooth case, let
\nabla f
be the gradient of

{\mathbf{x}}

be the subdifferential of

and let
\partial_{x}g_{i}
and
\partial_{y}g_{i}
be the subdifferential of

with respect to
{\mathbf{x}}
and
{\mathbf{y}}^{i}
, respectively. With a little abuse of notation, in the non-smooth case, we still use

and

to denote subgradients in
\partial f
,
\partial_{x}g_{i}
and
\partial_{y}g_{i}
, respectively. Let
\|\cdot\|

Assumption 1.3
(Smoothness and Lipschitz continuity).

There exist non-negative constants
L_{f}
,
L_{g}^{xx}
,
L_{g}^{yx}
,

,

,

a.

f({\mathbf{x}})\leq f({\mathbf{x}}^{\prime})+\nabla f({\mathbf{x}}^{\prime})^{\top}({\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime})+\frac{L_{f}}{2}\|{\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}\|^{2}+H_{f}\|{\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}\|
for any
{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{X}}
.

b.

g_{i}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}^{i})-g_{i}({\mathbf{x}}^{\prime},{\mathbf{y}}^{i})-\nabla_{x}g_{i}({\mathbf{x}}^{\prime},{\mathbf{y}}^{i})^{\top}({\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime})\leq\frac{L_{g}^{xx}}{2}\|{\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}\|^{2}+H_{g}^{x}\|{\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}\|
for any
{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{X}}
and any
{\mathbf{y}}^{i}\in{\mathcal{Y}}^{i}
.

c.

\|\nabla_{y}g_{i}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}^{i})-\nabla_{y}g_{i}({\mathbf{x}}^{\prime},{\mathbf{y}}^{i\prime})\|\leq L_{g}^{yx}\|{\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}\|+L_{g}^{yy}\|{\mathbf{y}}^{i}-{\mathbf{y}}^{i\prime}\|+H_{g}^{y}
for any
{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{X}}
and any
{\mathbf{y}}^{i},{\mathbf{y}}^{i\prime}\in{\mathcal{Y}}^{i}
.

d.

|g_{i}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}^{i})-g_{i}({\mathbf{x}}^{\prime},{\mathbf{y}}^{i\prime})|\leq M_{g}^{x}\|{\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}\|+M_{g}^{y}\|{\mathbf{y}}^{i}-{\mathbf{y}}^{i\prime}\|
for any
{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{X}}
and any
{\mathbf{y}}^{i},{\mathbf{y}}^{i\prime}\in{\mathcal{Y}}^{i}
.

This assumption encompasses both smooth and non-smooth cases. In fact, the smooth case occurs when
H_{f}=H_{g}^{x}=H_{g}^{y}=0
. In this case, Assumptions
1.3
a, b and c require
\nabla f
,
\nabla_{x}g_{i}
and
\nabla_{y}g_{i}
are unique at each point and satisfy some Lipschitz properties. In fact, the non-smooth case occurs when
H_{f}
,
H_{g}^{x}
and
H_{g}^{y}
are non-zero while
L_{f}=L_{g}^{xx}=L_{g}^{yx}=L_{g}^{yy}=0
. In this case, Assumptions
1.3
a, b and c imply that
\nabla f
,
\nabla_{x}g_{i}
and
\nabla_{y}g_{i}
are bounded. Assumption
1.3
d means
g_{i}
is Lipschitz continuous, which implies

\|\nabla_{x}g_{i}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}^{i})\|\leq M_{g}^{x}\text{ and }\|\nabla_{y}g_{i}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}^{i})\|\leq M_{g}^{y}

(1.5)

for any
{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathcal{X}}
and any
{\mathbf{y}}^{i},{\mathbf{y}}^{i\prime}\in{\mathcal{Y}}^{i}
.

We consider both the case where the gradients and function values above can be computed exactly and the case where they can only be approximated by stochastic oracles. In the latter case, we call (
1.1
) a
stochastic semi-infinite program
(SSIP), for which the following standard assumptions are made.

Assumption 1.4
(Stochastic oracles).

There exist a random vector
\xi
and measurable mappings
\nabla F({\mathbf{x}},\xi)\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
,
G_{i}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}^{i},\xi)\in\mathbb{R}
,
\nabla_{x}G_{i}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}^{i},\xi)\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, and
\nabla_{y}G_{i}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}^{i},\xi)\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
that provide stochastic oracles for
\nabla f({\mathbf{x}})
,
g_{i}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}^{i})
,
\nabla_{x}g_{i}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}^{i})
, and
\nabla_{y}g_{i}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}^{i})
, respectively. Moreover, the following conditions hold.

Unbiasedness:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\nabla F({\mathbf{x}},\xi)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nabla f({\mathbf{x}}),

(1.6)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[G_{i}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}^{i},\xi)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g_{i}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}^{i}),\quad\quad i=1,\cdots,m

(1.7)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{x}G_{i}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}^{i},\xi)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nabla_{x}g_{i}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}^{i}),\quad\quad i=1,\cdots,m

(1.8)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{y}G_{i}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}^{i},\xi)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nabla_{y}g_{i}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}^{i}),\quad\quad i=1,\cdots,m.

(1.9)

Bounded variance:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla F({\mathbf{x}},\xi)-\nabla f({\mathbf{x}})\|^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sigma_{f^{\prime}}^{2},

(1.10)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|G_{i}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}^{i},\xi)-g_{i}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}^{i})\|^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sigma_{g}^{2},\quad\quad i=1,\cdots,m

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla_{x}G_{i}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}^{i},\xi)-\nabla_{x}g_{i}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}^{i})\|^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq

Let
{\mathcal{Y}}:={\mathcal{Y}}^{1}\times{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}\times\cdots\times{\mathcal{Y}}^{m}\subset\mathbb{R}^{mq}

{\mathbf{y}}=({\mathbf{y}}^{1\top},\dots,{\mathbf{y}}^{m\top})^{\top}\in{\mathcal{Y}}

Consider the Lagrangian function of (
1.1
)

\phi({\mathbf{x}},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}},{\mathbf{y}})=f({\mathbf{x}})+{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{\top}{\mathbf{g}}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}).

Given an optimal solution
{\mathbf{x}}^{*}

{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{*}

({\mathbf{x}}^{*},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{*},{\mathbf{y}}^{*})
is a saddle-point of (
1.14

\phi({\mathbf{x}}^{*},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}},{\mathbf{y}})\leq\phi({\mathbf{x}}^{*},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{*},{\mathbf{y}}^{*})\leq\phi({\mathbf{x}},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{*},{\mathbf{y}}^{*})

for any
({\mathbf{x}},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}},{\mathbf{y}})\in{\mathcal{X}}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{m}\times{\mathcal{Y}}

That being said, (
1.1
) is indeed tractable under the convexity and concavity assumptions from Assumptions
1.1
. In fact, thanks to the concavity of
g_{i}
in
{\mathbf{y}}^{i}
, the lower-level problem
\max_{{\mathbf{y}}^{i}\in{\mathcal{Y}}^{i}}g_{i}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}^{i})
for
i=1,\dots,m
are relatively easy to solve. Suppose the optimal solution to each lower problem, denoted by
{\mathbf{y}}^{i}_{*}
for
i=1,\dots,m
, is obtained. By Danskin’s theorem, one can obtain
\nabla g_{i}^{*}({\mathbf{x}})=\nabla_{x}g_{i}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}^{i}_{*})
and then solve (
1.1
) as a problem with finitely many constraints,
g_{i}^{*}({\mathbf{x}})\leq 0
for
i=1,\dots,m
, for example, using the methods in
[
25
,
7
]
. When computing
{\mathbf{y}}^{i}_{*}
exactly is not practical, one can first find an approximate solution to the lower-level problem, denoted by
\widehat{\mathbf{y}}^{i}
for
i=1,\dots,m
, and then approximate
\nabla g_{i}^{*}({\mathbf{x}})
by
\nabla_{x}g_{i}({\mathbf{x}},\widehat{\mathbf{y}}^{i})
. This way, one can still solve (
1.1
) as a finitely constrained problem using the inexact gradients of
g_{i}^{*}({\mathbf{x}})
. However, this approach usually requires a small approximation error in terms of
\|\nabla g_{i}^{*}({\mathbf{x}})-\nabla_{x}g_{i}({\mathbf{x}},\widehat{\mathbf{y}}^{i})\|
, which not only needs strong assumptions, for example, strong concavity and smoothness of
g_{i}
in
{\mathbf{y}}
, but also leads to high computational complexity for solving each lower-level problem. Moreover, this approach involves double loops, which is not easy to implement.

To address these issues, we propose a first-order algorithm that only performs one gradient ascend step on
g_{i}
to update
{\mathbf{y}}^{i}
in each iteration, followed by primal-dual gradient steps to update
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
and
{\mathbf{x}}
using the function values and gradients on the historical iterates. This approach involves only a single loop, making it easy to implement. {\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
and
{\mathbf{y}}

\phi
in
({\boldsymbol{\lambda}},{\mathbf{y}})

SIP has been systematically discussed in several monographs
[
51
,
50
,
20
,
34
,
27
,
21
]
and has many applications as listed in the previous section. Most numerical algorithms for optimization with finitely many constraints can be somehow extended for SIP. The examples include the penalty method
[

,
32
,

]
, the barrier method
[
28
,
36
]
, the interior-point method
[
19
,

]
, the Lagrangian method
[
53
,

]
, the sequential quadratic programming method
[

,
22
]
, and the trust-region method
[
57
]
. However, most of these works only show an asymptotic convergence property or a local convergence rate. On the contrary, we establish the global non-asymptotic convergence rate for our algorithm and characterize its iteration complexity for finding an
\epsilon
-optimal solution. Luo et al.
[
36
]
also analyze the complexity of a logarithmic barrier method, but their study only focuses on a linear SIP instead of the nonlinear problem (
1.1
).

Besides the aforementioned approaches stemmed from nonlinear programming, there exist techniques specifically developed for SIP based on different strategies for handling infinitely many constraints. The cutting-plane method
[
29
,
65
,
6
,
47
,
17
,
43
,
9
]
solves the lower-level problem,
\max_{{\mathbf{y}}^{i}\in{\mathcal{Y}}^{i}}g_{i}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}^{i})
,
i=1,\dots,m
up to certain optimality gap in order to construct a cutting plane to update solutions. The computational complexity for solving the subproblems is non-negligible but not explicitly analyzed in those works. Our method does not directly solve
\max_{{\mathbf{y}}^{i}\in{\mathcal{Y}}^{i}}g_{i}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}^{i})
for any targeted precision but only take a momentum gradient ascend step on
{\mathbf{y}}^{i}
for
i=1,\dots,m
in each iteration.

The discretization method
[
59
,
55
,
67
]
chooses or samples a finite set
\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}^{i}\subset{\mathcal{Y}}^{i}
and solve a relaxation of (
1.1
) where the constraints are
g_{i}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}^{i})\leq 0
for any
{\mathbf{y}}^{i}\in\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}^{i}
and
i=1,\dots,m
. It is easy to implement but, to ensure a tight relaxation, the size of
\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}^{i}
must typically increase exponentially with the dimension

, known as the curse of dimensionality.
A related method is the exchange method
[
17
,
70
,
29
,
65
]
which updates
\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}^{i}
iteratively by including the (nearly) optimal solution of the lower-level problem and thus has the same computational issue as the cutting-plane method mentioned earlier.

The reduction method
[
49
,
27
]
assumes the optimal solution to the lower problem, namely,
{\mathbf{y}}^{i}({\mathbf{x}})\in\argmax_{{\mathbf{y}}^{i}\in{\mathcal{Y}}^{i}}g_{i}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}^{i})
, is an implicit function of
{\mathbf{x}}
so the infinitely many constraints in (
1.1
) can be replaced by finitely many constraints
g_{i}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}^{i}({\mathbf{x}}))\leq 0
,
i=1,\dots,m
only on
{\mathbf{x}}
. However, this approach needs strong assumptions on the regularity of
g_{i}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}^{i}
to ensure uniqueness and differentiability of
{\mathbf{y}}^{i}({\mathbf{x}})
.

[

,

,
11
]
for a bilinear min-max problem. In their method, a momentum gradient direction is introduced to update the solutions, which improves the convergence rate. Their method is extended by
[
25
]
for a non-bilinear min-max problem, which covers convex optimization with finitely many constraints as a special case. The method by
[
25
]
is further modified by
[
7
]
by combining an extrapolation technique with a momentum gradient step and allowing stochastic gradient oracles. The same convergence rates are obtained by
[
25
]
and
[
7
]
for deterministic convex constrained optimization. However, to achieve the claimed convergence rates,
[
25
]
does not require a known upper bound of the optimal Lagrangian while
[
7
]
does when the problem is not strongly convex. Besides, Hamedani and Aybat (2021) also introduce a back tracking technique to estimate the local Lipschitz constants which effectively improves the numerical performance.
Similar momentum gradient steps are also used for convex nested composite optimization
[
71
]
.

Our method also uses a momentum gradient step similar to the ones in
[
10
,
11
,
25
,
7
,
71
]
to update the constraint parameters
{\mathbf{y}}
and uses the momentum gradient step with extrapolation in
[
7
]
to update the dual variable
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
. Different from
[
25
,
7
]
, our method can be applied to (
1.1
) with infinitely many constraints. In
[
68
]
, the authors studied a class of fairness constraints in machine learning that can be formulated as a stochastic composite constraint

d^{*}(\mathbb{E}_{\xi}{\mathbf{G}}_{i}({\mathbf{x}},\xi))=\max_{{\mathbf{y}}^{i}\in{\mathcal{Y}}^{i}}\left[({\mathbf{y}}^{i})^{\top}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}{\mathbf{G}}_{i}({\mathbf{x}},\xi)-d({\mathbf{y}}^{i})\right]\leq 0,

where
{\mathcal{Y}}^{i}\subset\mathbb{R}_{+}^{q}
is a convex closed set,
\xi
is a random variable,
d(\cdot)
is a convex closed function whose Fenchel conjugate is
d^{*}(\cdot)
, and
{\mathbf{G}}_{i}({\mathbf{x}},\xi)
is a vector-valued mapping that is convex in
{\mathbf{x}}
in each component. Problem (
1.1
) covers their problem as a special case when
g_{i}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}^{i})=({\mathbf{y}}^{i})^{\top}\mathbb{E}{\mathbf{G}}({\mathbf{x}},\xi)-d({\mathbf{y}}^{i})
. First-order methods for SIP and SSIP have also be studied in
[
63
,
64
]
where the algorithms need strong oracles per iteration. The differences between
[
63
,
64
]
and this work is discussed in the end of previous section. We also refer readers to Table
1.1
for a clear comparison.

\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{0}&\cdots&\nabla_{y}g_{m}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}^{m})^{\top}\\
\end{array}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times mq}.

Similarly, using the notations from Assumption
1.4
, the stochastic oracles of
{\mathbf{g}}
,
\nabla_{x}{\mathbf{g}}
and
\nabla_{y}{\mathbf{g}}
can be defined as follows

\displaystyle{\mathbf{G}}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}},\xi)=(G_{1}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}^{1},\xi),\dots,G_{m}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}^{m},\xi))^{\top},

(3.11)

\displaystyle\nabla_{x}{\mathbf{G}}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}},\xi)=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\nabla_{x}G_{1}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}^{1},\xi)^{\top}\\
\nabla_{x}G_{2}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}^{2},\xi)^{\top}\\
\vdots\\
\nabla_{x}G_{m}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}^{m},\xi)^{\top}\\
\end{array}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times p}

and

\displaystyle\nabla_{y}{\mathbf{G}}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}},\xi)=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}\nabla_{y}G_{1}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}^{1},\xi)^{\top}&\mathbf{0}&\cdots&\mathbf{0}\\

Given any

as

(3.20)

By the convexity of
g_{i}
in
{\mathbf{x}}
, the following inequality holds in a component-wise sense

{\mathbf{g}}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}})\geq\ell_{{\mathbf{g}}}({\mathbf{x}};{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime},{\mathbf{y}}),~\forall{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X},~{\mathbf{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}.

(3.21)

Using the oracles from Assumption

, a stochastic estimation of

can be constructed, which is a stochastic mapping
\ell_{{\mathbf{G}}}(\cdot,\xi;{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime},{\mathbf{y}}):\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
defined as

\ell_{{\mathbf{G}}}({\mathbf{x}},\xi;{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime},{\mathbf{y}}):={\mathbf{G}}({\mathbf{x}}^{\prime},{\mathbf{y}},\xi)+\nabla_{x}{\mathbf{G}}({\mathbf{x}}^{\prime},{\mathbf{y}},\xi)({\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}).

(3.22)

Given
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{m})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{m}
and
{\mathbf{y}},\tilde{\mathbf{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}}
, we define
\|{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\|_{1}
as the
\ell_{1}
-norm and define

\|{\mathbf{y}}-\tilde{\mathbf{y}}\|_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}^{2}:=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}\|{\mathbf{y}}^{i}-\tilde{\mathbf{y}}^{i}\|^{2}.

4
Algorithm

Recall the Lagrangian function
\phi({\mathbf{x}},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}},{\mathbf{y}})
in (
1.14
). Suppose
({\mathbf{x}}_{k},{\mathbf{y}}_{k},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{k})
are the primal and dual solutions of (
1.14
) generated at the
k
th iteration of an algorithm. To generate the next dual solutions, the existing approach (e.g.
[
40
,
41
,
25
]
) for a saddle-point problem typically will update
({\mathbf{y}}_{k},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{k})
together using the gradient or the momentum of the gradient of
\phi
with respect to
{\mathbf{y}}
and
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
. However, the convergence of such a method to the global optimal solution becomes unclear because of the non-concavity of
\phi
in
({\mathbf{y}},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})
. Note that
\phi
is still concave in
{\mathbf{y}}
while
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
is fixed, and vice versa, so a potential approach is to update
{\mathbf{y}}
and
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
sequentially. This leads to the algorithm in Algorithm
1
, which we will explain in details next.

In particular, one can update
{\mathbf{y}}_{k}^{i}
for each
i
first by taking a gradient step with a momentum term towards maximizing
g_{i}({\mathbf{x}}_{k},{\mathbf{y}}^{i})
over
{\mathbf{y}}^{i}\in{\mathcal{Y}}^{i}
, namely,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{y}}_{k+1}^{i}=\argmin_{{\mathbf{y}}^{i}\in{\mathcal{Y}}^{i}}-\left\langle\nabla_{y}g_{i}({\mathbf{x}}_{k},{\mathbf{y}}_{k}^{i})+\theta_{k}\left[\nabla_{y}g_{i}({\mathbf{x}}_{k},{\mathbf{y}}_{k}^{i})-\nabla_{y}g_{i}({\mathbf{x}}_{k-1},{\mathbf{y}}_{k-1}^{i})\right],{\mathbf{y}}\right\rangle+\frac{\sigma_{k}}{2}\|{\mathbf{y}}^{i}-{\mathbf{y}}_{k}^{i}\|^{2}.

Here,
\theta_{k}\geq 0
is a momentum parameter and
\sigma_{k}\geq 0
is a step size. Note that
\nabla_{y}g_{i}({\mathbf{x}}_{k},{\mathbf{y}}_{k}^{i})-\nabla_{y}g_{i}({\mathbf{x}}_{k-1},{\mathbf{y}}_{k-1}^{i})
can be viewed as the momentum of the
\nabla_{y}g_{i}
between two consecutive iterations. When
\theta_{k}=0
, the updating equation above is just the gradient ascend step to maximize
g_{i}({\mathbf{x}}_{k},{\mathbf{y}}^{i})
over
{\mathbf{y}}^{i}\in{\mathcal{Y}}^{i}
. When
\theta_{k}>0
, the updating direction
\nabla_{y}g_{i}({\mathbf{x}}_{k},{\mathbf{y}}_{k}^{i})+\theta_{k}\left[\nabla_{y}g_{i}({\mathbf{x}}_{k},{\mathbf{y}}_{k}^{i})-\nabla_{y}g_{i}({\mathbf{x}}_{k-1},{\mathbf{y}}_{k-1}^{i})\right]
can be used to further accelerate the gradient methods as known in literature
[
25
,
39
,
11
,
10
,
71
]
. This update can be performed for
i=1,\dots,m
independently and is what Line 4 and 5 do in Algorithm
1
.

Similarly, one can update
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{k}
by taking a gradient step with a momentum term towards maximizing
\phi({\mathbf{x}}_{k},{\mathbf{y}}_{k+1},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})
over
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}
, namely,

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{k+1}=\argmin_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\geq\textbf{0}}-\left\langle{\mathbf{g}}({\mathbf{x}}_{k},{\mathbf{y}}_{k+1})+\theta_{k}\left[{\mathbf{g}}({\mathbf{x}}_{k},{\mathbf{y}}_{k})-{\mathbf{g}}({\mathbf{x}}_{k-1},{\mathbf{y}}_{k})\right],{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\right\rangle+\frac{\gamma_{k}}{2}\|{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}-{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{k}\|^{2}.

(4.1)

Similar to the updating step for
{\mathbf{y}}^{i}
,
\theta_{k}\geq 0
is a momentum parameter and
\gamma_{k}\geq 0
is a step size. In our algorithm, we implement an variant of (
4.1
) for updating
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{k}
, where
{\mathbf{g}}
is replaced by its linear approximation
\ell_{{\mathbf{g}}}
in (
3.20
). In particular, we update
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
using

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{k+1}=\argmin_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\geq\textbf{0}}-\left\langle\ell_{{\mathbf{g}}}({\mathbf{x}}_{k};{\mathbf{x}}_{k-1},{\mathbf{y}}_{k+1})+\theta_{k}\left[\ell_{{\mathbf{g}}}({\mathbf{x}}_{k};{\mathbf{x}}_{k-1},{\mathbf{y}}_{k})-\ell_{{\mathbf{g}}}({\mathbf{x}}_{k-1};{\mathbf{x}}_{k-2},{\mathbf{y}}_{k})\right],{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\right\rangle+\frac{\gamma_{k}}{2}\|{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}-{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{k}\|^{2}.

(4.2)

This method is originally proposed by
[
7
]
for optimization with finitely many constraints and, here, we generalize it for (
1.1
). This is the updates performed in Lines 6 and 7 do in Algorithm
1
.

Once we have updated
({\mathbf{y}}_{k+1},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{k+1})
, we can generate
{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1}
by a gradient step from
{\mathbf{x}}_{k}
using the gradient of
\phi
with respect to
{\mathbf{x}}
at
({\mathbf{x}}_{k},{\mathbf{y}}_{k+1},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{k+1})
. This is performed in Line 8 of Algorithm
1
where
\tau_{k}
is a step size. After
K
iterations, the algorithm is terminated and returns the weighted average of
{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1}
for
k=0,\dots,K-1
using
t_{k}\geq 0
as the weight.

As shown in
[
25
]
and
[
7
]
for finitely constrained problems, there are subtle differences between (
4.1
) and (
4.2
) in their impacts to the algorithm’s convergence analysis. When (
4.1
) is applied, the convergence analysis in
[
25
]
either assumes an upper bound
\Lambda
of
\|{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{*}\|
is known and
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
is projected to a bounded domain
\{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\geq\textbf{0}|\|{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\|\leq\Lambda\}
in each iteration, or uses induction to show that
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{k}
is bounded for all iterations and uses a backtracking step to select
\tau_{k}
. On the contrary, when (
4.2
) is applied, the algorithm in
[
7
]
does not require knowing
\Lambda
but only achieve the optimal convergence rate when
\Lambda
is known.

5
Bounding Primal-Dual Gap

In this section, we present the main results on the convergence properties of Algorithms
1
and
2
.
Let
{\mathbf{z}}=({\mathbf{x}},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}},{\mathbf{y}})
and
{\mathbf{z}}_{k}=({\mathbf{x}}_{k},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{k},{\mathbf{y}}_{k})
for
k\geq 0
. Following
[
71
]
, we write

\phi({\mathbf{x}}_{k+1},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}},{\mathbf{y}})-\phi({\mathbf{x}},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{k+1},{\mathbf{y}}_{k+1})
as the summation of three gaps and derive an upper bound on gap. In particular, according to (
1.14
), we have

\displaystyle~~~\phi({\mathbf{x}}_{k+1},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}},{\mathbf{y}})-\phi({\mathbf{x}},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{k+1},{\mathbf{y}}_{k+1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Q_{2}({\mathbf{z}}_{k+1},{\mathbf{z}})+Q_{1}({\mathbf{z}}_{k+1},{\mathbf{z}})+Q_{0}({\mathbf{z}}_{k+1},{\mathbf{z}}),

(5.1)

where

\displaystyle Q_{2}({\mathbf{z}}_{k+1},{\mathbf{z}})

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle~\phi({\mathbf{x}}_{k+1},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}},{\mathbf{y}})-\phi({\mathbf{x}}_{k+1},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}},{\mathbf{y}}_{k+1})

(5.2)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{\top}({\mathbf{g}}({\mathbf{x}}_{k+1},{\mathbf{y}})-{\mathbf{g}}({\mathbf{x}}_{k+1},{\mathbf{y}}_{k+1}))

\displaystyle Q_{1}({\mathbf{z}}_{k+1},{\mathbf{z}})

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle~\phi({\mathbf{x}}_{k+1},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}},{\mathbf{y}}_{k+1})-\phi({\mathbf{x}}_{k+1},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{k+1},{\mathbf{y}}_{k+1})

(5.3)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}-{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{k+1})^{\top}{\mathbf{g}}({\mathbf{x}}_{k+1},{\mathbf{y}}_{k+1})

\displaystyle Q_{0}({\mathbf{z}}_{k+1},{\mathbf{z}})

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle~\phi({\mathbf{x}}_{k+1},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{k+1},{\mathbf{y}}_{k+1})-\phi({\mathbf{x}},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{k+1},{\mathbf{y}}_{k+1})

(5.4)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~f({\mathbf{x}}_{k+1})-f({\mathbf{x}})+{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{k+1}^{\top}({\mathbf{g}}({\mathbf{x}}_{k+1},{\mathbf{y}}_{k+1})-{\mathbf{g}}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}_{k+1})).

The convergence properties of Algorithm

are then derived by bounding

from above by summing up some upper bounds of

,

and
Q_{2}
, and choosing parameters
\sigma_{k}
,
\gamma_{k}
,
\tau_{k}
,
\theta_{k}
and

for

appropriately according to the smoothness of the problem, strong convexity/concavity of the problem, and the types (deterministic or stochastic) of oracle used.

The following lemma is a conclusion from Lemmas 2.1 and 6.1 in
[
40
]
and is needed to obtain the upper bounds of

,
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
. Its proof is provided for the sack of completeness.

Lemma 5.1
.

Given a closed convex set
\mathcal{W}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a point
{\mathbf{w}}_{0}\in\mathcal{W}
, a sequence
\{{\mathbf{w}}_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
is generated by

{\mathbf{w}}_{k+1}=\argmin_{{\mathbf{w}}\in\mathcal{W}}\left\langle{\mathbf{s}}_{k}+{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{k},{\mathbf{w}}\right\rangle+\frac{\eta_{k}}{2}\|{\mathbf{w}}-{\mathbf{w}}_{k}\|^{2},~k=0,\dots,

where
\eta_{k}>0
,
{\mathbf{s}}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. It holds, for any
{\mathbf{w}}\in\mathcal{W}
, that

\displaystyle\left\langle{\mathbf{s}}_{k}+{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{k},{\mathbf{w}}_{k+1}-{\mathbf{w}}\right\rangle+\frac{\eta_{k}}{2}\|{\mathbf{w}}-{\mathbf{w}}_{k+1}\|^{2}\leq-\frac{\eta_{k}}{2}\|{\mathbf{w}}_{k+1}-{\mathbf{w}}_{k}\|^{2}+\frac{\eta_{k}}{2}\|{\mathbf{w}}-{\mathbf{w}}_{k}\|^{2}.

(5.5)

Additionally, given
\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{0}={\mathbf{w}}_{0}
, a sequence
\{\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
is generated by

\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{k+1}=\argmin_{{\mathbf{w}}\in\mathcal{W}}-\left\langle{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{k},{\mathbf{w}}\right\rangle+\frac{\eta_{k}}{2}\|{\mathbf{w}}-\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{k}\|^{2},~k=0,1,\dots.

It holds, for any
{\mathbf{w}}\in\mathcal{W}
, that

\displaystyle\left\langle{\mathbf{s}}_{k},{\mathbf{w}}_{k+1}-{\mathbf{w}}\right\rangle+\frac{\eta_{k}}{2}\|{\mathbf{w}}-{\mathbf{w}}_{k+1}\|^{2}+\frac{\eta_{k}}{2}\|{\mathbf{w}}-\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{k+1}\|^{2}

(5.6)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-\frac{\eta_{k}}{4}\|{\mathbf{w}}_{k+1}-{\mathbf{w}}_{k}\|^{2}+\frac{\eta_{k}}{2}\|{\mathbf{w}}-{\mathbf{w}}_{k}\|^{2}+\frac{\eta_{k}}{2}\|{\mathbf{w}}-\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle+\left\langle{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{k},\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{k}-{\mathbf{w}}_{k}\right\rangle+\frac{3}{2\eta_{k}}\|{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{k}\|^{2}.

Proof:

Inequality (
5.5
) is a direct outcome of the

8
Conclusions

Semi-infinite programming has applications in transportation, robotics, statistics and machine learning but it is challenging to solved due to the infinite number of constraints. Under the assumption of convexity, an effective primal-dual gradient method is proposed where, in each iteration, momentum-driven gradient ascend and descend steps are performed. This approach is also extended using stochastic gradient and function oracles, allowing it to solve large-scale data-driven problems through data sampling. The iteration complexity of the proposed method for finding an
\epsilon
-optimal solution is establish under different strong convexity assumptions and smoothness assumptions, encompassing both deterministic and stochastic cases.
