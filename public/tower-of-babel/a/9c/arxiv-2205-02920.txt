Convergence of a scheme for elastic flow with tangential mesh movement
Paola Pozzi, and Björn Stinner

Abstract
Elastic flow for closed curves can involve significant deformations. Mesh-based approximation schemes require tangentially redistributing vertices for long-time computations. We present and analyze a method that uses the Dirichlet energy for this purpose. The approach effectively also penalizes the length of the curve, and equilibrium shapes are equivalent to stationary points of the elastic energy augmented with the length functional. Our numerical method is based on linear parametric finite elements. Following the lines of [ 8 ] we prove convergence and establish error estimates, noting that the addition of the Dirichlet energy simplifies the analysis in comparison with the length functional. We also present a simple semi-implicit time discretization and discuss some numerical result that support the theory.



Keywords:

elastic flow, gradient flow, finite element method, semi-discrete scheme

Introduction

Elastic flow is the
L^{2}
-gradient flow of the bending energy

\mathcal{E}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi}|\vec{\kappa}|^{2}ds

for the elasticity of a rod that is modelled by a closed (i.e. periodic) regular smooth curve
[
23
]
. Here,
x:[0,2\pi]\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
x=x(u)
, stands for its parametrisation,
ds=|x_{u}|du
its length element, and
\vec{\kappa}=x_{ss}=\tau_{s}
its curvature vector where
\tau=\partial_{s}x=\frac{x_{u}}{|x_{u}|}
is a unit tangent vector. Because of the scaling properties of
\mathcal{E}
(a circle of radius
R
has energy
\pi/R\to 0
as
R\to\infty
), one instead can consider
[
10
]

\mathcal{E}_{\tilde{\lambda}}(x):=\mathcal{E}(x)+\tilde{\lambda}\mathcal{L}(x)

(1.1)

for some positive
\tilde{\lambda}
where

\mathcal{L}(x)=\int_{0}^{2\pi}ds=\int_{0}^{2\pi}|x_{u}|du

is the length functional. The corresponding
L^{2}
-gradient flow of
\mathcal{E}_{\tilde{\lambda}}
is given by

\displaystyle x_{t}=-\nabla_{s}^{2}\vec{\kappa}-\frac{1}{2}|\vec{\kappa}|^{2}\vec{\kappa}+\tilde{\lambda}\vec{\kappa}.

(1.2)

Here
\nabla_{s}\phi=\partial_{s}\phi-(\partial_{s}\phi\cdot\tau)\tau
denotes the normal component of the derivative with respect to arc length for a given vector field
\phi:[0,2\pi]\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
along the curve.
Note that the first variation of the energy functional
\mathcal{E}_{\tilde{\lambda}}
yields a gradient in normal direction and thus does not “suggest” any tangential components. A more geometrical formulation (specifying the motion in the normal direction only) is thus given by

\displaystyle Px_{t}=x_{t}-(x_{t}\cdot\tau)\tau=-\nabla_{s}^{2}\vec{\kappa}-\frac{1}{2}|\vec{\kappa}|^{2}\vec{\kappa}+\tilde{\lambda}\vec{\kappa}

(1.3)

where
P=I-\tau\otimes\tau
with a unit tangent
\tau
is the projection to the normal space. With other words, in (
1.2
) the velocity vector is entirely normal to the curve, whereas in the formulation (
1.3
) different tangential components are admissible.

This geometric flow (and its variations) has been thoroughly studied in recent years both numerically and analytically (see for instance
[
10
]
,
[
19
]
,
[
13
]
,
[
14
]
,
[
25
]
,
[
24
]
,
[
15
]
,
[
17
]
,
[
4
]
,
[
5
]
,
[
1
]
,
[
20
]
,
[
6
]
,
[
8
]
, and references given in there).

From a numerical point of view, quantitative error analysis of a FEM scheme has been provided only in the case of (
1.2
) where the flow has no tangential component (see
[
8
]
). However, the lack of tangential components has major disadvantages from a computational viewpoint, as the grid might degenerate quickly.
In
[
4
]
the authors work with (
1.3
) and add a tangential movement that is beneficial for the mesh quality.
They provide a FEM scheme for which a stability bound for a continuous in time semi-discrete scheme holds together with an equi-distribution mesh property.
In
[
5
]
the author considers the case of inextensible curves, therefore the problem of a possible degeneration of the mesh is ruled out by the formulation of the problem itself. A convergence analysis is presented, while a quantitative control of the error is missing.

Here we are interested in the problem where the curves are permitted to change their length in time.
We would like to achieve two goals: good grid properties and a quantitative numerical error analysis.
Ruling out (
1.2
) because of the aforementioned grid degeneration problems, one way to tackle the problem would be to
introduce a tangential component for (
1.3
) that is suitable both for analytical and numerical purposes.
This step is sort of “artificial” but the task far from straightforward
because of the following two “clashing” points:

•

From an analytical point of view (for instance when showing short-time existence of the flow)
one is interested in tangential components that remove the degeneracy of the differential operator.

•

From a numerical perspective it is usually advantageous to stay as close as possible to the gradient structure of the flow (for instance, for stability) and to derive a variational problem such that only operations are performed that are also admissible in a finite element setting.

Our approach to the problem is to revisit the ideas behind the definition of elastic flow and find a suitable compromise between analysis and numerics.
Thus we propose a new model of elastic flow for which we are able to provide an error analysis of the semi-discrete scheme (similarly to
[
8
]
) and such that the mesh presents good numerical properties. Analytical questions, such as long-time existence of the flow, are treated in
a separate work
[
21
]
.

Let us thus consider again the classical definition of elastic flow.
As noted above the penalization of the bending energy through the length functional is motivated by the fact that we do not wish the curve to flatten out at infinity.
A penalization of the length as in (
1.1
) is effective in this respect.
There is however another way to penalize growth of the curve that also induces a nice control of the parametrization of the curve.
Inspired by classical theory on minimal surfaces (
[
9
]
), our idea is to replace the length functional
\mathcal{L}(x)
with the Dirichlet energy

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(x):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi}|x_{u}|^{2}du

whose beneficial effects for the mesh quality in moving mesh methods have been already experienced in different ways, see for instance
[
7
,
11
]
and related work. Thus, we want to minimize

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}(x):=\mathcal{E}(x)+\lambda\mathcal{D}(x)

(1.4)

for some positive given
\lambda
.
Note that
\mathcal{L}(x)\leq\sqrt{2\pi}\sqrt{2\mathcal{D}(x)}
, whence
\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}(x)
is dominated by
\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}(x)
.
Noting that
\frac{x_{uu}}{|x_{u}|}\cdot\tau=(|x_{u}|)_{s}
,
the
L^{2}
-gradient flow for
\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}(x)
is given by

\displaystyle x_{t}

\displaystyle=-\nabla_{s}^{2}\vec{\kappa}-\frac{1}{2}|\vec{\kappa}|^{2}\vec{\kappa}+\lambda\frac{x_{uu}}{|x_{u}|}

(1.5)

\displaystyle=-\nabla_{s}^{2}\vec{\kappa}-\frac{1}{2}|\vec{\kappa}|^{2}\vec{\kappa}+\lambda\vec{\kappa}|x_{u}|+\lambda(|x_{u}|)_{s}\tau.

(1.6)

The flow is obviously different from (
1.2
) and (
1.3
) but the set of extremal points for the energies
\mathcal{E}_{\tilde{\lambda}}
and
\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}
are the same in the following sense:
since
\mathcal{D}
is not invariant under re-parametrization of the domain, the first variation in a stationary point
x
of
\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}
with respect to tangential deformations yields that
|x_{u}|=const
,
so
x
is parametrized by constant speed
|x_{u}|=:S_{x}
.
Comparing (
1.6
) with (
1.2
) we see that
x
then also is a stationary point of
\mathcal{E}_{\tilde{\lambda}}
for
\tilde{\lambda}=\lambda S_{x}
.

Vice versa, if
x
is stationary point for
\mathcal{E}_{\tilde{\lambda}}
, then its parametrization by constant speed, denoted by
S_{x}
again for convenience, is a stationary point of
\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}
with
\lambda=\tilde{\lambda}/S_{x}
.
We also note that (
1.5
) is not a geometric flow:
indeed, the Dirichtlet functional is not invariant under reparametrizations as the length functional. Its purpose here is to single out a tangential contribution to the parametrization that yields good mesh properties during the evolution.

In Section
2
we present a FEM discretization very much in the style and spirit of
[
8
]
. As a consequence, the main error estimates, see Theorem
2.5
below, are analogous to those presented in
[
8
, Thm. 2.3]
: more precisely, after considering a mixed formulation of the flow, we prove a linear control with respect to the space grid parameter
h
for suitable norms of the error of the parametrization
x
and its curvature vector
\vec{\kappa}
. Notice, however, that the substitution of the length functional with the Dirichlet energy simplifies the analysis in a significant manner (see comments below Theorem
2.5
).
Numerical experiments in Section
4
support the theoretical result. They also highlight the good mesh properties of our scheme in 2D and 3D. We also provide some comparisons with other schemes. Finally, although the Dirichlet energy is a great ally in the numerical analysis for the semi-discrete formulation, it is less clear how it could help in showing stability for the fully discrete scheme that we use in our computations (cp. also
[
6
]
and
[
20
, Remark 6.4]
). This aspect will be subject of future research.

Main results

2.1
Variational problem formulation

We now consider the
L^{2}
-gradient flow for (
1.4
) and are interested in numerically
approximating the evolution given in (
1.5
) for some positive fixed
\lambda>0
.
Before formulating the problem in a variational form that is amenable to a finite element approximation we first make a remark on how self-similar solutions to (
1.5
) and (
1.2
) compare.

Example 2.1

(Self-similar evolution of a circle parametrized by constant speed)
.

Let
\lambda,\tilde{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
and consider functions of the form
x(t,u)=R(t)(\cos u,\sin u)
. A direct computation gives that
x
solves

\displaystyle x_{t}=-\nabla_{s}^{2}\vec{\kappa}-\frac{1}{2}|\vec{\kappa}|^{2}\vec{\kappa}+\tilde{\lambda}\vec{\kappa}

if and only if
\dot{R}=\frac{1}{2R^{3}}-\frac{\tilde{\lambda}}{R}=\frac{1}{R^{3}}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\tilde{\lambda}R^{2}\right)
,
which has the stationary solution
\tilde{R}=1/\sqrt{2\tilde{\lambda}}
.

On the other hand,
x
solves

\displaystyle x_{t}=-\nabla_{s}^{2}\vec{\kappa}-\frac{1}{2}|\vec{\kappa}|^{2}\vec{\kappa}+\lambda\frac{x_{uu}}{|x_{u}|}

if and only if
\dot{R}=\frac{1}{2R^{3}}-\lambda=\frac{1}{R^{3}}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\lambda R^{3}\right)

with the stationary solution
\bar{R}=1/\sqrt[3]{2\lambda}
.

Since our approach follows very closely the one presented in
[
8
]
, we use for simplicity the same notation. In particular the curvature vector of
x
is now denoted by
y
,

\vec{\kappa}=y\quad\mbox{in the following.}

The strong formulation (
1.5
) then becomes

x_{t}=-\nabla_{s}^{2}y-\frac{1}{2}|y|^{2}y+\lambda\frac{x_{uu}}{|x_{u}|},\quad y=\frac{1}{|x_{u}|}\Big(\frac{x_{u}}{|x_{u}|}\Big)_{u}.

(2.1)

The problem is closed by imposing an initial condition of the form

x(0,\cdot)=x_{0}(\cdot)\quad\mbox{on }[0,2\pi)

(2.2)

for given periodic initial data denoted by
x_{0}
.
For the error analysis, we make the following assumptions:

Assumption 2.2
.

We assume that
x_{0}
is smooth and that (
2.1
) with (
2.2
) has a smooth, periodic (in space) solution
x:[0,T]\times[0,2\pi]\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We furthermore assume that there exist constants
0<c_{0}\leq C_{0}
such that

\displaystyle c_{0}\leq|x_{u}|\leq C_{0},\qquad\qquad|y|\leq C_{0}\qquad\text{ in }[0,T]\times[0,2\pi].

(2.3)

Weak formulation.

Following the same steps presented in
[
8
, § 2]
in the computation of the first variation we consider the following weak form for the gradient flow of
\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}
:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}(x_{t}\cdot\phi)|x_{u}|-\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{Py_{u}\cdot\phi_{u}}{|x_{u}|}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi}|y|^{2}(\tau\cdot\phi_{u})+\lambda\int_{0}^{2\pi}x_{u}\cdot\phi_{u}

\displaystyle=0

(2.4)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}(y\cdot\psi)|x_{u}|+\int_{0}^{2\pi}(\tau\cdot\psi_{u})

\displaystyle=0

(2.5)

for all
\phi,\psi\in H^{1}_{per}(0,2\pi)
and
t\in[0,T]
, together with the initial condition (
2.2
). Here,
P
denotes the normal projection matrix
P=I-\tau\otimes\tau
.

A key advantage of the above formulation is that
no integration by parts
has been carried out in the derivation of the first variation. In particular, all steps performed hold also in the finite dimensional spaces that we will choose below.
This allows to recover very easily the equivalent of the energy estimate
\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}(x)\leq 0
(see Remark
2.3
below).
Therefore, as mentioned in the introduction, we have stayed true to our principles, choosing a formulation that is close to the variational structure of the problem and that uses operations that are admissible in the discrete space.

2.2
Spatial discretization

We use the same notation adopted in
[
8
]
with the exception that we denote the Euclidean scalar product with
\cdot
instead of
(\cdot,\cdot)
. Let
0=u_{0}<u_{1}<\ldots<u_{N}=2\pi

\displaystyle\|(I_{h}f)_{u}\|_{L^{2}}

Moreover recall the following useful estimates (

\displaystyle\int_{I_{j}}|\phi_{h}|^{2}

\displaystyle\int_{I_{j}}\phi_{h}\cdot\psi_{h}

\displaystyle=\int_{I_{j}}I_{h}(\phi_{h}\cdot\psi_{h})-\frac{1}{6}h_{j}^{2}\int_{I_{j}}\phi_{hu}\cdot\psi_{hu}

for all

Z_{h}:=\{z_{h}:[0,2\pi]\to\mathbb{R}^{n}\,:\,z_{h}|_{I_{j}}\text{ is constant },j=1,\ldots,N\}

(Q_{h}f)|_{I_{j}}:=\frac{1}{|I_{j}|}\int_{I_{j}}f

Notice that by

(2.8)

we have

\displaystyle\|f-Q_{h}f\|_{L^{2}}\leq Ch\|f\|_{H^{1}}\qquad\forall f\in H^{1}((0,2\pi),\mathbb{R}^{n}).

(2.12)

The semi-discrete problem corresponding to (
2.4
), (
2.5
) reads as follows:

find
x_{h},y_{h}:[0,T]\times[0,2\pi]\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

x_{h}(t,\cdot),y_{h}(t,\cdot)\in X_{h}^{n}
,

and

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}I_{h}(x_{ht}\cdot\phi_{h})|x_{hu}|-\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{P_{h}y_{hu}\cdot\phi_{hu}}{|x_{hu}|}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi}I_{h}(|y_{h}|^{2})(\tau_{h}\cdot\phi_{hu})

(2.13)

\displaystyle+\lambda\int_{0}^{2\pi}x_{hu}\cdot\phi_{hu}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}I_{h}(y_{h}\cdot\psi_{h})|x_{hu}|+\int_{0}^{2\pi}(\tau_{h}\cdot\psi_{hu})

\displaystyle=0

(2.14)

\displaystyle x_{h}(0,\cdot)

\displaystyle=I_{h}x_{0}

(2.15)

for all
\phi_{h},\psi_{h}\in X_{h}^{n}
and
t\in[0,T]
. Here
P_{h}
denotes the discrete normal projection matrix
P_{h}=I-\tau_{h}\otimes\tau_{h}
with
\tau_{h}=\frac{x_{hu}}{|x_{hu}|}
.

Remark 2.3
.

The energy estimates in the discrete setting are derived in exactly the same way as in the continuous setting.
More precisely:

•

(Step 1)
take
\phi_{h}=x_{ht}
in (

•

(Step 2)
take
\psi_{h}=y_{h}
in

•

(Step 3)
combine the two equations to obtain

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}I_{h}(|x_{ht}|^{2})|x_{hu}|+\frac{d}{dt}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi}I_{h}(|y_{h}|^{2})|x_{hu}|+\frac{\lambda}{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi}|x_{hu}|^{2}\right\}=0.

(2.16)

The reason for highlighting the structure is that the error analysis follows essentially the same steps.

2.3
Statement of the main results

As pointed out in
[
8
]
the initial datum (
2.15
) determines the curvature vector
y_{h}(0,\cdot)\in X_{h}^{n}
through (

), that is through the relation

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}I_{h}(y_{h}(0,\cdot)\cdot\psi_{h})|(I_{h}x_{0})_{u}|+\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{(I_{h}x_{0})_{u}\cdot\psi_{hu}}{|(I_{h}x_{0})_{u}|}=0

for all

Lemma 2.4
.

For
h\leq h_{0}
, with

sufficiently small, we have that

\|y(0,\cdot)-y_{h}(0,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}}\leq Ch.

Proof.

See
[
8

. The claim follows from (
2.17
) with

and using (

and appropriate interpolation estimates.

Theorem 2.5
.

Let Assumption

holds.
There exists
h_{0}>0
such that (
2.13
)-(
2.15
) has a unique solution and

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|x(t,\cdot)-x_{h}(t,\cdot)\|^{2}_{H^{1}}+\int_{0}^{T}\|x_{t}(t,\cdot)-x_{ht}(t,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}dt\leq Ch^{2},

(2.18)

for all

,

,

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left(\frac{1}{|x_{u}|}Py_{u}-\frac{1}{|x_{hu}|}P_{h}y_{hu}\right)\cdot(I_{h}x_{t}-x_{ht})_{u}

\displaystyle=\|(x-x_{h})(0)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+2\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{2\pi}(x-x_{h})(t^{\prime})\cdot(x_{t}-x_{ht})(t^{\prime})dxdt^{\prime}

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{2}+\epsilon\int_{0}^{t}\|x_{t}-x_{ht}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}dt^{\prime}+C_{\epsilon}\int_{0}^{t}\|x-x_{h}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}dt^{\prime}

and using Lemma
3.5
we obtain for appropriate choice of
\epsilon
that

\displaystyle\frac{c_{0}}{32}\int_{0}^{t}

\displaystyle\|x_{t}-x_{ht}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}dt^{\prime}+\frac{c_{0}}{16}\|(y-y_{h})(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{8}\|(x_{u}-x_{hu})(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|(x-x_{h})(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{2}+C\int_{0}^{t}(\|y-y_{h}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|x_{u}-x_{hu}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|x-x_{h}\|_{L^{2}}^{2})dt^{\prime}.

The Gronwall Lemma implies that

\displaystyle\sup_{[0,\hat{T}_{h}]}\big(\|(y-y_{h})(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|(x-x_{h})(t)\|_{H^{1}}^{2}\big)+\int_{0}^{\hat{T}_{h}}

\displaystyle\|x_{t}-x_{ht}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}dt^{\prime}\leq Ch^{2}.

(3.7)

Together with Lemma
3.5
we have therefore verified the bounds (
2.18
), (
2.19
) on
[0,\hat{T}_{h}]
.
Note that the constants depend on
c_{0}
,
C_{0}
,
T
,
\lambda
, and norms of the continuous solution
x
only.

We can now prove that
\hat{T}_{h}=T
. If we had
\hat{T}_{h}<T
, then (
3.7
) and an inverse estimate would imply

\displaystyle\|(x_{u}-x_{hu})(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C\sqrt{h},\qquad\|(y-y_{h})(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C\sqrt{h}.

Combined with (
2.3
) this would yield that

\displaystyle\frac{3c_{0}}{4}\leq|x_{hu}|\leq\frac{3}{2}C_{0},\qquad|y_{h}|\leq\frac{3}{2}C_{0}\text{ in }[0,\hat{T}_{h}]\times[0,2\pi]

provided
h\leq h_{0}
, with
h_{0}
small enough. This would contradict the maximality of
\hat{T}_{h}
. Therefore
\hat{T}_{h}=T
, and the theorem is proved.

4.5
Extensions

We briefly discuss two extensions of our novel method
and showcase their impact but without analyzing them in detail.
Our focus here is on modifications of the
L^{2}
-gradient flow for the geometric functional (
1.1
).

1.

Picking up an idea from
[
22
]

(which is on planar curves)
we may split up the velocity in its normal and tangential part,
x_{t}=Px_{t}+(x_{t}\cdot\tau)\tau
and then scale all terms with tangential contributions with a small parameters
0<\varepsilon\ll 1
. Equivalently, we consider a weighted
L^{2}
gradient flow
for

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(x)+\tilde{\lambda}\mathcal{L}(x)+\epsilon\mathcal{D}(x),

(4.5)

where the tangential part has an
\varepsilon
weighting.
If we do this then we obtain (see also (
4.6
), (
4.7
) below with
M=1
)

Px_{t}+\varepsilon(x_{t}\cdot\tau)\tau=-\nabla_{s}^{2}\vec{\kappa}-\frac{1}{2}|\vec{\kappa}|^{2}\vec{\kappa}+\tilde{\lambda}\vec{\kappa}+\varepsilon\frac{x_{uu}}{|x_{u}|}.

In effect, the tangential movement of mesh points that is beneficial for the mesh quality and due to the Dirichlet energy contribution in (
1.4
) is kept.
We have not analyzed this idea but refer to
[
22
]
, where a network of curves moving by curve shortening flow is investigated. Let us note that, there, constants in error estimates depend in an unfavorable way on
\varepsilon
, as does the conditioning of the linear systems in the fully discrete scheme.
We expect this to be the case for the scheme (
4.10
), (
4.11
) (with
M=1
) for the above approach, too.

2.

Whilst equi-distribution of mesh points along the curve might generally be desirable, some applications might benefit from more mesh points in certain areas of interest. These could simply be parts of the curve where the curvature is high. Or, when combining the geometric equation with a reaction-diffusion system on the curve to model cell migration (for instance, see
[
12
]
), then the fields on the curve might require a better resolution in some parts.

Drawing on ideas presented in
[
16
]

we introduce a monitor function that acts as a weighting in the term arising from the Dirichlet energy. Intuitively, it may be interpreted as kind of a tension parameter. The higher
M
the higher the attraction between close points. In the discrete setting one will therefore expect that vertices move the closer the higher
M
is.

For simplicity, we here only consider a (constant in time) function defined on the ambient space
M:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to(0,\infty)
.

Combinding these ideas,
the weak formulation reads

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}\big(Px_{t}\cdot\phi+\varepsilon(x_{t}\cdot\tau)\tau\cdot\phi\big)|x_{u}|-\int_{0}^{2\pi}\Big(\frac{Py_{u}\cdot\phi_{u}}{|x_{u}|}+\frac{|y|^{2}}{2}(\tau\cdot\phi_{u})\Big)

\displaystyle+\tilde{\lambda}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\tau\cdot\phi_{u}+\varepsilon\int_{0}^{2\pi}M(x)x_{u}\cdot\phi_{u}

\displaystyle=0,

(4.6)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}(y\cdot\psi)|x_{u}|+\int_{0}^{2\pi}(\tau\cdot\psi_{u})

\displaystyle=0,

(4.7)

for all
\phi,\psi\in H^{1}_{per}(0,2\pi)
and
t\in[0,T]
. The corresponding strong PDE can be split up in normal and tangential part in the form

\displaystyle Px_{t}

\displaystyle=-\nabla_{s}^{2}\vec{\kappa}-\frac{1}{2}|\vec{\kappa}|^{2}\vec{\kappa}+\tilde{\lambda}\vec{\kappa}+\varepsilon P\big(Mx_{u}\big)_{u}\frac{1}{|x_{u}|},

(4.8)

\displaystyle x_{t}\cdot\tau

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|x_{u}|}\big(Mx_{u}\big)_{u}\cdot\tau.

(4.9)

Thus formally as
\epsilon\to 0
we recover (
1.3
).
Moreover note that, in general, the above scheme is not a gradient flow due to the term with the monitor function
M
.

5
Conclusion

The elastic flow can be amended in various ways to prevent solutions from continuous growing, adding the Dirichlet energy as proposed in (
1.4
) is one of them. However, this approach has a significant advantage for finite element computations. First, it involves a tangential movement of mesh points of great benefit to the mesh quality during evolution. Second, it provides additional control so that the error due to the finite element discretization can be estimated. We provided such estimates in Theorem
2.5
in norms that are natural for the problem. These results were supported by numerical simulations, which also displayed good mesh qualities throughout the evolution.

The resulting flow is no longer geometric, however, in the sense that the right-hand side of (
1.5
) depends on the parametrization of the curves (as opposed to (
1.2
) or (
1.3
)). As an alternative, we briefly looked into extensions of (
1.3
) for which the addition of a Dirichlet functional plays the role of an
\epsilon
perturbation term.
Here some remaining open questions are related
to the time discretization, the impact of the small parameter
\epsilon
and a monitor function involved in the extensions.
