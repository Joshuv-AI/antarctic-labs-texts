A granular model for crowd motion and pedestrian flow
Noureddine Igbida, José Miguel Urbano

Abstract
. We study a granular model for congested crowd motion and pedestrian flow. Our approach is based on an approximation through a Hele-Shaw type equation involving a degenerate operator of p − \displaystyle p- Laplacian type and a linear drift, for which we prove existence and uniqueness using nonlinear semigroup methods and the doubling variables technique. Our main result shows that, as p → ∞ \displaystyle p\to\infty , the weak solutions of the p − \displaystyle p- problem converge to a solution of the congested crowd motion problem interpreted in a variational sense.

1.
Introduction

Macroscopic models for pedestrian flow, in which the crowd is modeled as a moving fluid, were first introduced in
[
8
]
and later explored in
[
21
,
22
]
. The space-time dynamics of the crowd is governed by a flow velocity vector field
\displaystyle V
according to the transport equation

(1.1)

\partial_{t}u+\nabla\cdot(u\>V)=f.

Here,
\displaystyle u=u(t,x)
is the density of individuals at time
\displaystyle t\geq 0
and position
\displaystyle x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, which needs to accurately describe an admissible global distribution of the population, and
\displaystyle f
is a given source.

The vector field
\displaystyle V
takes into account the overall behaviour of the crowd (for example, the goal of reaching an exit or the avoidance of some danger) but neglects the local behaviour of pedestrians (who may, for example, be in a hurry, adapt their speed or try to avoid the crowd). To deal with local effects and following the predicting-correcting algorithms introduced in
[
27
]
, we consider a new vector field
\displaystyle W
that will, in particular, consider congestion effects, thus obtaining the master equation

(1.2)

\partial_{t}u+\nabla\cdot\left(W+u\>V\right)=f.

The vector field
\displaystyle W=W(\nabla v)
will be driven by the gradient of a potential
\displaystyle v\geq 0
such that

(1.3)

v(u-1)=0,

which is then a kind of Lagrange multiplier associated with the two-sided constraint
\displaystyle 0\leq u\leq 1
. We can express this by requiring

u\in{\hbox{Sign}^{+}}(v)\hbox{ and }v\geq 0,

where
\displaystyle{\hbox{Sign}^{+}}
denotes the maximal monotone graph given by

{\hbox{Sign}^{+}}(r):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cll}1&\mathrm{if}&r>0\\
\displaystyle[0,1]&\mathrm{if}&r=0\\
0&\mathrm{if}&r<0.\end{array}\right.

The linear case, corresponding to the choice

(1.4)

W(\nabla v)=-\nabla v,

leads to the equation

\partial_{t}u-\Delta v+\nabla\cdot(u\>V)=f,

which is relatively well-understood (see
[
26
]
). Here, we explore the nonlinear case

(1.5)

W(\nabla v)=-\left|\nabla v\right|^{p-2}\nabla v,\qquad 2<p<\infty,

leading to the degenerate PDE

(1.6)

\partial_{t}u-\Delta_{p}v+\nabla\cdot(u\>V)=f,

and study the asymptotic limit problem obtained by taking
\displaystyle p\to\infty
.

In the linear case
\displaystyle p=2
, congestion is modelled through linear diffusion and Brownian motion, and the crowd behaves like a Newtonian fluid. For
\displaystyle p>2
, the crowd behaves like a non-Newtonian shear-thickening or dilatant fluid, with the viscosity depending on the shear stress.

As we let
\displaystyle p
approach infinity, we aim to capture a granular type of behaviour exhibited by the crowd, mirroring the well-established behaviour of sandpiles. Formally, the limiting problem aims to patch the transport equation with

(1.7)

W=-m\>\nabla v,\quad\left|\nabla v\right|\leq 1,\quad m(\left|\nabla v\right|-1)=0,

where
\displaystyle v
is an unknown potential connected to the distance to the exit, supported in the congested region
\displaystyle[u=1]
,
i.e.
, satisfying (
1.3
). The parameter
\displaystyle m
is a Lagrange multiplier associated with the constraint
\displaystyle\left|\nabla v\right|\leq 1,
which could be connected to the random movements of the individuals in the congested region (see
[
20
]
and
[
24
]
). For a geometrical interpretation of
\displaystyle m
in terms of the boundary curvature and the normal distance to the cut locus of the domain
\displaystyle\Omega
, see also
[
11
,
12
,
13
]
. Moreover, rather than addressing the intricacies of the tangential gradient required due to the Radon measure
\displaystyle m
, as seen in
[
9
]
for certain closely related particular cases, we adopt an equivalent formulation based on the variational characterization of the solution. To keep the presentation focused, we do not pursue a weak formulation (in the distributional sense) based on (
1.7
) in this work; this will likely be the subject of future investigations.

Connecting the dynamics of a pedestrian moving towards a fixed target to that of sandpile particles moving towards the exit of a table is a plausible scenario introduced and studied numerically in
[
18
]
. In this model, the pile’s height is linked to a
potential
value so that higher potential areas have more particles (think of crowded zones).
The self-organization of particles in a sandpile is a captivating natural phenomenon that has directly or indirectly inspired numerous physical models (see, for instance,
[
5
,
6
,
16
,
17
,
30
,
31
]
). Unlike the growth of a sandpile, where a source and gravity govern the dynamics, the movement in crowd motion is determined by the instantaneous movements of particles driven by the spontaneous velocity field
\displaystyle V
. Additionally, the approach could be formally grounded at the microscopic level by employing the stochastic sandpile model introduced by Evans and Rezakhanlou (see
[
20
]
and
[
24
]
).

Imagine a grid of cubes (see Figure
1
) representing pedestrians trying to reach an exit. Like a person, each cube can only move downhill (to a lower cube) randomly until it gets stuck. This creates a flow of
pedestrian-cubes
similar to sand in a sandpile. Using an appropriate scaling of time and space, one would guess the resulting continuous dynamics follows a sandpile macroscopic flow to remedy the congestion. People (cubes) move downhill (following the gradient) but only when it is
favourable
(think of a passage leading to the exit around a congested zone with a staircase offering sequential available positions). This movement is described by a
flow
equation, where the flow is controlled by the potential’s gradient. Indeed, in their pioneering work
[
20
]
(see also
[
24
]
), Evans and Rezakhanlou study the case of a sandpile when the congestion constraint and the transport term are absent,
i.e.
, for
\displaystyle v=u
and
\displaystyle V=0
. They prove that the rescaled pile’s height converges to the solution of a nonlinear sandpile dynamics governed by a flux
\displaystyle\Phi
derived from a potential
\displaystyle z
, as expressed by

\Phi=-m\>\nabla z,

where
\displaystyle z
, linked to the sandpile’s height, satisfies the gradient constraint

\left|\nabla z\right|\leq 1,

closely mirroring the discrete constraint on the cubes at the microscopic level. Additionally,
\displaystyle m\geq 0
is an unknown parameter subject to the condition
\displaystyle m(\left|\nabla z\right|-1)=0
, which reflects the fact that particle movement towards the exit occurs only under favourable circumstances delineated by the gradient of
\displaystyle z.
Hence, one can formally map the random cube movement to the behaviour of pedestrians in the congested regime, as depicted in the formal illustration of Figure
1
.

Figure 1
.
Toy pedestrian-cubes model

The well-established micro-macro limit for sandpiles — rigorously derived by transitioning from a discrete spatio-temporal stochastic model at the cube scale to a continuous one — strongly motivates the use of the
\displaystyle p-
Laplacian to describe sand-grain-like granular dynamics. However, extending this approach to population dynamics remains challenging. The presence of a two-sided constraint on the density and the first-order transport term
\displaystyle V
obstructs the adaptation of the techniques from
[
20
]
and
[
24
]
, primarily due to difficulties with spatial and temporal scaling. The problem remains open and deserves further investigation, particularly within this context, which is highly relevant for applications in pedestrian dynamics.

We conclude that (
1.5
) and (
1.7
) provide two variants of macroscopic crowd motion models with hard congestion, aligning with the class of models introduced by Maury and collaborators (
cf.

[
27
,
28
,
29
]
). Unlike the linear scenario (
1.4
), which represents the homogeneous random movement of pedestrians around the congested zone, these variants enable the natural handling (at the macroscopic level) of pedestrian movement, allowing them to occupy empty adjacent sites in the congested area towards the exit when possible, or to come to a halt if necessary.

The plan of the paper is the following: in Section
2
, we gather some notation and the assumptions and state the main results; in Section
3
, following
[
26
]
, we establish the uniqueness of nonnegative weak solutions for the
\displaystyle p-
problem using the ideas of DiPerna-Lions on renormalization, and Kruzhkov’s doubling and de-doubling techniques; in Section
4
, we prove the existence of a weak solution by employing nonlinear semigroup methods, building upon the
\displaystyle L^{1}-
contraction results from the previous section; finally, Section
5
provides the proof of the convergence of solutions of the
\displaystyle p-
problem to the congested crowd motion problem, as the parameter
\displaystyle p
approaches infinity; the appendix contains the proofs of some technical results used in Section
3
.

2.
Assumptions and main results

We assume that
\displaystyle\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
is a bounded open set, with a regular boundary, split into
\displaystyle\partial\Omega=\Gamma_{D}\cup\Gamma_{N},
such that
\displaystyle\Gamma_{D}\cap\Gamma_{N}=\emptyset
and

\mathcal{L}^{N-1}(\Gamma_{D})>0.

For
\displaystyle T>0
, we denote

Q:=(0,T)\times\Omega;\qquad\Sigma_{D}:=(0,T)\times\Gamma_{D};\qquad\Sigma_{N}:=(0,T)\times\Gamma_{N}.

Given a source
\displaystyle f
, a velocity vector field
\displaystyle V
and an initial datum
\displaystyle u_{0}
, we consider the problem of finding a pair of nonnegative functions
\displaystyle(u,v)
such that

(2.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcl}\left.\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\partial_{t}u-\nabla\cdot\left(\left|\nabla v\right|^{p-2}\nabla v-u\>V\right)=f\\
\\
u\in\hbox{Sign}^{+}(v)\end{array}\right\}&\mathrm{in}&Q\\
\\
\ \ v=0&\mathrm{on}&\Sigma_{D}\\
\\
\ \ \left(\left|\nabla v\right|^{p-2}\nabla v-u\>V\right)\cdot\nu=0&\mathrm{on}&\Sigma_{N}\\
\\
\ \ u(0)=u_{0}&\mathrm{in}&\Omega,\end{array}\right.

where
\displaystyle p>2
and
\displaystyle\nu
is the outward unitary normal to
\displaystyle\partial\Omega
.

Throughout the paper, we assume

(2.2)

V\in\left[W^{1,p^{\prime}}(\Omega)\right]^{N},\qquad\nabla\cdot V\in L^{\infty}(\Omega),

and

(2.3)

V\cdot\nu\geq 0\quad\mathrm{on}\ \Gamma_{D}\qquad\mathrm{and}\qquad V\cdot\nu=0\quad\mathrm{on}\ \Gamma_{N},

in the sense that

(2.4)

\liminf_{h\to 0}\frac{1}{h}\int_{\{x\in\Omega\>:\>d(x,\partial\Omega)<h\}}\xi\,V(x)\cdot\nu(\pi(x))\,dx\geq 0,

for all
\displaystyle 0\leq\xi\in L^{p}(\Omega)
. Here,
\displaystyle d(.,\partial\Omega)
is the Euclidean distance to the boundary of
\displaystyle\Omega
, and
\displaystyle\pi(x)
denotes the projection of
\displaystyle x
onto the boundary
\displaystyle\partial\Omega
.

Our first result concerns the existence and uniqueness of a weak solution to (
2.1
) in the sense we next precise. We denote

W^{1,p}_{D}(\Omega):=\left\{w\in W^{1,p}(\Omega)\,:\,w=0\ \mathrm{on}\ \Gamma_{D}\right\}.

Definition 2.1
.

A couple
\displaystyle(u,v)
is a weak solution of (
2.1
) if

(u,v)\in C\left([0,T),L^{1}(\Omega)\right)\cap L^{\infty}(Q)\times L^{p}\left(0,T;W^{1,p}_{D}(\Omega)\right),

v\geq 0,\qquad u\in{\hbox{Sign}^{+}}(v),\mbox{ a.e. in }Q,

and

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\iint_{Q}u\>\xi\>\psi^{\prime}+\iint_{Q}\left(\left|\nabla v\right|^{p-2}\nabla v-u\>V\right)\cdot\nabla\xi\>\psi

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\iint_{Q}f\>\xi\>\psi+\int_{\Omega}u_{0}\>\xi\>\psi(0),

for any
\displaystyle\xi\in W^{1,p}_{D}(\Omega)
and
\displaystyle\psi\in\mathcal{D}\left([0,T)\right)
.

Theorem 2.1
.

For any
\displaystyle 0\leq f\in L^{p^{\prime}}(Q)
and
\displaystyle u_{0}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
such that

0\leq u_{0}\leq 1,\quad\mbox{a.e. in }\Omega,

the problem (
2.1
) has a unique weak solution in the sense of Definition
2.1
.

We next analyze the behaviour of the evolution problem (
2.1
) as the exponent
\displaystyle p
approaches infinity. Since the pioneering work
[
3
]
(see also
[
31
]
), this limit represents a fundamental shift in the dynamics, revealing a critical connection between the long-term behaviour of the original
\displaystyle p-
Laplacian equation and the dynamics of grains in sandpile models (see also
[
16
,
17
,
19
,
30
]
). Indeed, letting
\displaystyle p\to\infty
in the original equation

\frac{\partial z}{\partial t}-\nabla\cdot\left(\left|\nabla z\right|^{p-2}\nabla z\right)=f\quad\text{in }Q,

we obtain the limiting problem given by

(2.5)

\frac{\partial z}{\partial t}-\nabla\cdot\left(m\>\nabla z\right)=f,\qquad|\nabla z|\leq 1\quad\text{in }Q,

where
\displaystyle m\geq 0
is an unknown parameter that depends on the solution itself through the condition

m(|\nabla z|-1)=0\quad\text{in }Q.

So, formally, the limiting problem of (
2.1
), as
\displaystyle p\to\infty
, may be given by

(2.6)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcl}\left.\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\partial_{t}u-\nabla\cdot\left(m\>\nabla v-u\>V\right)=f\\
\\
u\in\hbox{Sign}^{+}(v),\quad|\nabla v|\leq 1\\
\\
m\geq 0,\quad m(|\nabla v|-1)=0\end{array}\right\}&\mathrm{in}&Q\\
\\
\ \ v=0&\mathrm{on}&\Sigma_{D}\\
\\
\ \ \left(m\>\nabla v-u\>V\right)\cdot\nu=0&\mathrm{on}&\Sigma_{N}\\
\\
\ \ u(0)=u_{0}&\mathrm{in}&\Omega.\end{array}\right.

However, while
\displaystyle m
is typically a Radon measure in similar settings (see, for instance,
[
9
]
for the case of (
2.5
)), the gradient of
\displaystyle v
requires a specialized approach called the
tangential gradient
(see
[
9
]
for details). To avoid this complexity, which we plan to explore further in future works, we will instead leverage an equivalent formulation based on the variational description of the solution (see also
[
23
,
25
,
14
]
for details regarding this equivalence in the context of (
2.5
)). Our focus will be on the characterization of the limit of the solutions to (
2.1
) using this variational formulation. We formally justify our choice as follows: considering the subgradient constraint in the diffusion operator governing the PDE in (
2.6
), we propose an integral formulation incorporating
\displaystyle v-\xi
as a test function, for
\displaystyle 0\leq\xi\in W_{D}^{1,\infty}(\Omega)
and
\displaystyle|\nabla\xi|\leq 1
. Following this setup, it is straightforward to observe that

\int_{\Omega}m\>\nabla v\cdot\nabla(v-\xi)=\int_{\Omega}m\>(1-\nabla v\cdot\nabla\xi)\geq 0.

Furthermore, since
\displaystyle u\in{\hbox{Sign}^{+}}(v)
, we formally derive that

\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}u\>(v-\xi)=\int_{[u=1]}\partial_{t}u\>v-\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}u\>\xi=-\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}u\>\xi.

This suggests the viability of reformulating the problem into the variational form

-\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}u\>\xi\leq\int_{\Omega}u\>V\cdot\nabla\left(v-\xi\right)+\int_{\Omega}f\left(v-\xi\right),\quad\hbox{ in }\mathcal{D}^{\prime}([0,T)),

according to the following definition.

Definition 2.2
.

A couple
\displaystyle(u,v)
is a variational solution of (
2.6
) if

(u,v)\in L^{\infty}(Q)\times L^{q}\left(0,T;W^{1,q}_{D}(\Omega)\right),\mbox{ for any }1\leq q<\infty,

v\geq 0,\qquad u\in{\hbox{Sign}^{+}}(v),\quad\left|\nabla v\right|\leq 1,\quad\mbox{a.e. in }Q,

and

\displaystyle\displaystyle\iint_{Q}u\>\xi\>\psi^{\prime}(t)+\int_{\Omega}u_{0}\>\xi\>\psi(0)\leq\iint_{Q}u\>V\cdot\nabla\left(v-\xi\right)\psi+\iint_{Q}f\left(v-\xi\right)\psi,

for any
\displaystyle 0\leq\xi\in W_{D}^{1,\infty}(\Omega)
such that
\displaystyle|\nabla\xi|\leq 1
, a.e. in
\displaystyle\Omega
, and
\displaystyle 0\leq\psi\in\mathcal{D}\left([0,T)\right)
.

Theorem 2.2
.

For any
\displaystyle 0\leq f\in L^{p^{\prime}}(Q)
and
\displaystyle u_{0}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
such that

0\leq u_{0}\leq 1,\quad\mbox{a.e. in }\Omega,

let
\displaystyle(u_{p},v_{p})
be the weak solution of (
2.1
). For subsequences that we relabel for convenience, we have

u_{p}\rightharpoonup u\quad\mathrm{in}\quad L^{\infty}(Q)-\mbox{weak-}\ast;

, where
\displaystyle(u,v)

) in the sense of Definition

Remark 2.1
.

As previously noted, establishing the equivalence between the notion of solution involving the tangential gradient and the variational formulation we consider remains an open problem, one that we plan to explore in future work. Here, we remind the reader that this equivalence hinges on the subgradient operator governing the evolution being defined by

v\to-\nabla\cdot(m\>\nabla v),

where
\displaystyle m\geq 0
and
\displaystyle|\nabla v|\leq 1
. It coincides with the subdifferential operator
\displaystyle v\to\partial I\!\!I_{K}
, where
\displaystyle K
is given by

K:=\Big\{z\in W^{1,p}_{D}(\Omega)\>:\>|\nabla z|\leq 1\hbox{ a.e. in }\Omega\Big\}.

A function g belongs to
\displaystyle\partial I\!\!I_{K}(v)
if and only if

\int_{\Omega}g\>(v-z)\geq 0,\quad\hbox{ for any }z\in K.

However, working with the subgradient operator within (
2.6
) requires additional tools and developments, which, while important, extend beyond the primary focus of this paper.

To close this section, we introduce some further notations to be used in the paper. Define, for each
\displaystyle h>0
,

\xi_{h}(x):=\frac{1}{h}\min\left\{h,d(x,\partial\Omega)\right\}\qquad\mathrm{and}\qquad\nu_{h}(x)=-\nabla\xi_{h}(x),

for
\displaystyle x\in\Omega
. The function
\displaystyle\xi_{h}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
is regular (as smooth as the boundary) and concave,
\displaystyle 0\leq\xi_{h}\leq 1
and, for any
\displaystyle x\in\Omega
such that
\displaystyle d(x,\partial\Omega)<h
,

\nu_{h}(x)=-\frac{1}{h}\nabla d(x,\partial\Omega).

In particular, for such
\displaystyle x
, we have
\displaystyle h\nu_{h}(x)=\nu(\pi(x))
.
We denote

\nabla^{p-1}w:=\left|\nabla w\right|^{p-2}\nabla w

and let
\displaystyle\hbox{Sign}_{0}
be the real discontinuous function defined in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}
by

\hbox{Sign}_{0}(r)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccl}1&\mathrm{if}&r>0\\

3.
\displaystyle L^{1}-
contraction

In this section, we focus first on the uniqueness and
\displaystyle L^{1}-
comparison principle for weak solutions. Following the approach developed in
[
26
]
, we need a Kato’s inequality, whose proof uses, in an essential way, the fact that weak solutions are also renormalized solutions
à la
DiPerna-Lions. This is the object of the following result.

Proposition 3.1

(Renormalised formulation)
.

If
\displaystyle(u,v)
is a weak solution of (
2.1
), then

\partial_{t}\beta(u)-\Delta_{p}v+V\cdot\nabla\beta(u)+u\>\nabla\cdot V\beta^{\prime}(u)\leq f\>\beta^{\prime}(u)\quad\textit{in}\ \mathcal{D}^{\prime}(Q),

for any
\displaystyle\beta\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(I\!\!R)
such that
\displaystyle\beta^{\prime}\leq 1
and
\displaystyle\beta^{\prime}(1)=1
.

We postpone the proof of this proposition to the appendix.

Proposition 3.2

(Kato’s inequality)
.

If
\displaystyle(u_{1},v_{1})
and
\displaystyle(u_{2},v_{2})
are two weak solutions of (
2.1
) associated with
\displaystyle f_{1},f_{2}\in L^{1}(Q)
, then there exists
\displaystyle\kappa\in L^{\infty}(Q)
such that
\displaystyle\kappa\in{\hbox{Sign}^{+}}(u_{1}-u_{2})
, a.e. in
\displaystyle Q
, and

\partial_{t}\left|u_{1}-u_{2}\right|-\Delta_{p}(v_{1}+v_{2})+\nabla\cdot\left(\left|u_{1}-u_{2}\right|\>V\right)

(3.1)

\hskip 170.71652pt\leq\kappa(f_{1}-f_{2})\quad\mathit{in}\ \mathcal{D}^{\prime}(Q).

Proof.

First, we see that if
\displaystyle(u,v)
is a weak solution of (
2.1
), then

\partial_{t}\left|u-k\right|-\Delta_{p}v+\nabla\cdot(\left|u-k\right|\>V)+k\>\nabla\cdot V\>\hbox{Sign}_{1}(u-k)

(3.2)

\hskip 170.71652pt\leq f\>\hbox{Sign}_{1}(u-k)\quad\mathrm{in}\ \mathcal{D}^{\prime}(Q),

for any
\displaystyle k\leq 1
, where

\hbox{Sign}_{1}(r)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccl}1&\mathrm{if}&r\geq 0\\
-1&\mathrm{if}&r<0.\end{array}\right.

Indeed, it is enough to take in Proposition
3.1

\beta_{\epsilon}(r)=\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(r+\varepsilon-k),\quad r\in\mathbb{R},

where

\widetilde{{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}}(r)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccl}r-{\varepsilon}/{2}&\mathrm{if}&r>\varepsilon\\
r^{2}/2\varepsilon&\mathrm{if}&\left|r\right|\leq\varepsilon\\
-r-{\varepsilon}/{2}&\mathrm{if}&r<-\varepsilon,\end{array}\right.

and let
\displaystyle\varepsilon\to 0
. Notice that, since
\displaystyle k\leq 1
, we have
\displaystyle\beta_{\epsilon}^{\prime}(1)=\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(1+\varepsilon-k)=1

and

\beta_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(u)=\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(u+\varepsilon-k)\to\hbox{Sign}_{1}(u-k),\quad\hbox{ as }\varepsilon\to 0.

The proof is now based on the doubling and de-doubling variables technique. Let us briefly revisit the arguments for the reader’s convenience. Since
\displaystyle u_{2}(s,y)\leq 1
, we use the fact that
\displaystyle(u_{1},v_{1})
satisfies (
3.2
) with
\displaystyle k=u_{2}(s,y),
to get

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}\left|u_{1}(t,x)-u_{2}(s,y)\right|\zeta(x,y)\,dx\hskip 170.71652pt

\displaystyle\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega}\left(\nabla_{x}^{p-1}v_{1}(t,x)-\left|u_{1}(t,x)-u_{2}(s,y)\right|V(x)\right)\cdot\nabla_{x}\zeta(x,y)\,dx

\displaystyle\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega}u_{2}(s,y)\left(\nabla_{x}\cdot V\right)\hbox{Sign}_{1}\left(u_{1}(t,x)-u_{2}(s,y)\right)\zeta(x,y)\,dx

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}f_{1}(t,x)\,\hbox{Sign}_{1}\left(u_{1}(t,x)-u_{2}(s,y)\right)\zeta(x,y)\,dx,\hskip 85.35826pt

for any
\displaystyle 0\leq\zeta\in\mathcal{D}(\Omega\times\Omega)
, where
\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}
is taken in
\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(0,T).

Note that

\int_{\Omega}\nabla_{y}^{p-1}v_{2}(s,y)\cdot\nabla_{x}\zeta\,dx=0,

so that

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}\left|u_{1}(t,x)-u_{2}(s,y)\right|\zeta\,dx\hskip 170.71652pt

\displaystyle\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega}\left(\nabla_{x}^{p-1}v_{1}(t,x)+\nabla_{y}^{p-1}v_{2}(s,y)\right)\cdot\nabla_{x}\zeta\,dx

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\left|u_{1}(t,x)-u_{2}(s,y)\right|V(x)\cdot\nabla_{x}\zeta\,dx

\displaystyle\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega}u_{2}(s,y)\left(\nabla_{x}\cdot V\right)\hbox{Sign}_{1}\left(u_{1}(t,x)-u_{2}(s,y)\right)\zeta\,dx

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}f_{1}(t,x)\,\hbox{Sign}_{1}\left(u_{1}(t,x)-u_{2}(s,y)\right)\zeta\,dx.\hskip 85.35826pt

Denoting

u(t,s,x,y):=u_{1}(t,x)-u_{2}(s,y),

and integrating with respect to
\displaystyle y
, we obtain

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}\!\int_{\Omega}\left|u(t,s,x,y)\right|\zeta\,dxdy\hskip 170.71652pt

\displaystyle\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega}\!\int_{\Omega}\left(\nabla_{x}^{p-1}v_{1}(t,x)+\nabla_{y}^{p-1}v_{2}(s,y)\right)\cdot\nabla_{x}\zeta\,dxdy

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\!\int_{\Omega}\left|u(t,s,x,y)\right|V(x)\cdot\nabla_{x}\zeta\,dxdy

\displaystyle\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega}\!\int_{\Omega}u_{2}(s,y)\left(\nabla_{x}\cdot V\right)\hbox{Sign}_{1}\left(u(t,s,x,y)\right)\zeta\,dxdy

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}\!\int_{\Omega}f_{1}(t,x)\,\hbox{Sign}_{1}\left(u(t,s,x,y)\right)\zeta\,dxdy.\hskip 85.35826pt

On the other hand, using the fact that
\displaystyle(u_{2},v_{2})
satisfies (
3.2
) with
\displaystyle k=u_{1}(t,x)
, we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{d}{ds}\int_{\Omega}\left|u(t,s,x,y)\right|\zeta(x,y)\,dy\hskip 170.71652pt

\displaystyle\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega}\left(\nabla_{y}^{p-1}v_{2}(s,y)-\left|u(t,s,x,y)\right|V(y)\right)\cdot\nabla_{y}\zeta(x,y)\,dy

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}u_{1}(t,x)\left(\nabla_{y}\cdot V\right)\hbox{Sign}_{1}(u(t,s,x,y))\>\zeta(x,y)\,dy

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq-\int_{\Omega}f_{2}(s,y)\,\hbox{Sign}_{1}(u(t,s,x,y))\>\zeta(x,y)\,dy,\hskip 85.35826pt

where, again,
\displaystyle\frac{d}{ds}
is taken in
\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(0,T)
. Working in the same way, we get

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{d}{ds}\int_{\Omega}\!\int_{\Omega}\left|u(t,s,x,y)\right|\zeta\,dxdy\hskip 170.71652pt

\displaystyle\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega}\!\int_{\Omega}\left(\nabla_{x}^{p-1}v_{1}(t,x)+\nabla_{y}^{p-1}v_{2}(s,y)\right)\cdot\nabla_{y}\zeta\,dxdy

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\!\int_{\Omega}\left|u(t,s,x,y)\right|V(y)\cdot\nabla_{y}\zeta\,dxdy

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\!\int_{\Omega}u_{1}(t,x)\left(\nabla_{y}\cdot V\right)\hbox{Sign}_{1}\left(u(t,s,x,y)\right)\zeta\,dxdy

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq-\int_{\Omega}\!\int_{\Omega}f_{2}(s,y)\,\hbox{Sign}_{1}\left(u(t,s,x,y)\right)\zeta\,dxdy.\hskip 85.35826pt

Adding both inequalities, we obtain

(3.3)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left(\frac{d}{dt}+\frac{d}{ds}\right)\int_{\Omega}\!\int_{\Omega}\left|u(t,s,x,y)\right|\zeta\,dxdy\hskip 142.26378pt

\displaystyle\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega}\!\int_{\Omega}\left(\nabla_{x}^{p-1}v_{1}(t,x)+\nabla_{y}^{p-1}v_{2}(s,y)\right)\cdot\left(\nabla_{x}+\nabla_{y}\right)\zeta\,dxdy

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\!\int_{\Omega}\left|u(t,s,x,y)\right|\left(V(x)\cdot\nabla_{x}\zeta+V(y)\cdot\nabla_{y}\zeta\right)dxdy

\displaystyle\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega}\!\int_{\Omega}\left(u_{2}(s,y)\left(\nabla_{x}\cdot V\right)-u_{1}(t,x)\left(\nabla_{y}\cdot V\right)\right)\hbox{Sign}_{1}\left(u(t,s,x,y)\right)\zeta\,dxdy

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}\!\int_{\Omega}\left(f_{1}(t,x)-f_{2}(s,y)\right)\hbox{Sign}_{1}\left(u(t,s,x,y)\right)\zeta\,dxdy,\hskip 56.9055pt

where
\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}+\frac{d}{ds}
is taken in
\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\left((0,T)\times(0,T)\right)
.

We can now de-double the variables
\displaystyle t
and
\displaystyle s
, as well as
\displaystyle x
and
\displaystyle y
, by taking as usual the sequences of test functions

\psi_{\varepsilon}(t,s)=\psi\left(\frac{t+s}{2}\right)\rho_{\varepsilon}\left(\frac{t-s}{2}\right)

and

\zeta_{\lambda}(x,y)=\xi\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)\delta_{\lambda}\left(\frac{x-y}{2}\right),

for any
\displaystyle t,s\in(0,T)
and
\displaystyle x,y\in\Omega
. Here,
\displaystyle\psi,\xi\in\mathcal{D}(\Omega)
, and
\displaystyle\rho_{\varepsilon},\delta_{\lambda}
are sequences of standard mollifiers in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}
and
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{N}
, respectively.
Observe that

\left(\frac{d}{dt}+\frac{d}{ds}\right)\psi_{\varepsilon}(t,s)=\rho_{\varepsilon}\left(\frac{t-s}{2}\right)\psi^{\prime}\left(\frac{t+s}{2}\right)

and

\left(\nabla_{x}+\nabla_{y}\right)\zeta_{\lambda}(x,y)=\delta_{\lambda}\left(\frac{x-y}{2}\right)\nabla\xi\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right).

Moreover, for any
\displaystyle h\in L^{1}\left((0,T)^{2}\times\Omega^{2}\right)
and
\displaystyle\Phi\in L^{1}\left((0,T)^{2}\times\Omega^{2}\right)^{N}
, we have

\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\!\int_{\Omega}h(t,s,x,y)\,\zeta_{\lambda}(x,y)\,\psi_{\varepsilon}(t,s)\,dsdtdxdy

=\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}h(t,t,x,x)\,\xi(x)\,\psi(t)\,dtdx,

\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\!\int_{\Omega}h(t,s,x,y)\,\zeta_{\lambda}(x,y)\,\left(\frac{d}{dt}+\frac{d}{ds}\right)\psi_{\varepsilon}(t,s)\,dsdtdxdy

=\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}h(t,t,x,x)\,\xi(x)\,\psi^{\prime}(t)\,dtdx

and

\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\!\int_{\Omega}\Phi(t,s,x,y)\cdot(\nabla_{x}+\nabla_{y})\,\zeta_{\lambda}(x,y)\,\psi_{\varepsilon}(t,s)\,dsdtdxdy

=\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\Phi(t,t,x,x)\cdot\nabla\xi(x)\,\psi(t)\,dtdx.

Thus, replacing
\displaystyle\zeta
in (
3.3
) by
\displaystyle\zeta_{\lambda}
, testing with
\displaystyle\psi_{\varepsilon}
and letting
\displaystyle\varepsilon\to 0
and
\displaystyle\lambda\to 0
, we obtain (see, for instance,
[
26
]
)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}\left|u_{1}-u_{2}\right|\,\xi\,dx+\int_{\Omega}\left(\nabla^{p-1}v_{1}+\nabla^{p-1}v_{2}\right)\cdot\nabla\xi\,dx

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\left|u_{1}-u_{2}\right|\left(V\cdot\nabla\xi-\left(\nabla\cdot V\right)\xi\right)dx

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}\kappa\,(f_{1}-f_{2})\,\xi\,dx+\int_{\Omega}\left|u_{1}-u_{2}\right|\left(\nabla\cdot V\right)\xi\,dx,

where
\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}
is taken in
\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(0,T)
. We conclude, as desired, that

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}\left|u_{1}-u_{2}\right|\,\xi\,dx+\int_{\Omega}\nabla^{p-1}\left(v_{1}+v_{2}\right)\cdot\nabla\xi\,dx

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\left|u_{1}-u_{2}\right|\,V\cdot\nabla\xi\,dx

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}\kappa\,(f_{1}-f_{2})\,\xi\,dx,\quad\hbox{ in }\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(0,T).

∎

The idea behind the proof of the next theorem is to consider the sequence of test functions
\displaystyle\xi_{h}
given by (
2.7
) in Kato’s inequality and let
\displaystyle h\to 0,
to get the contraction inequality (
3.4
).

Theorem 3.1
.

If
\displaystyle(u_{1},v_{1})
and
\displaystyle(u_{2},v_{2})
are two weak solutions of (
2.1
) associated with
\displaystyle f_{1},f_{2}\in L^{1}(Q)
, respectively, then there exists
\displaystyle\kappa\in L^{\infty}(Q)
such that
\displaystyle\kappa\in{\hbox{Sign}^{+}}(u_{1}-u_{2})
, a.e. in
\displaystyle Q
, and

(3.4)

\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}\left|u_{1}-u_{2}\right|dx\leq\int_{\Omega}\kappa\left(f_{1}-f_{2}\right)dx,\quad\mathit{in}\ \mathcal{D}^{\prime}(0,T).

Proof.

Observe that, for
\displaystyle\xi_{h}
given by (
2.7
), we have

\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}\left|u_{1}-u_{2}\right|dx-\int_{\Omega}\kappa\left(f_{1}-f_{2}\right)dx

=\lim_{h\to 0}\ \underbrace{\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}\left|u_{1}-u_{2}\right|\,\xi_{h}\>dx-\int_{\Omega}\kappa\left(f_{1}-f_{2}\right)\,\xi_{h}\,dx}_{I(h)}.

Taking
\displaystyle\xi_{h}
as a test function in (
3.1
), we obtain

\displaystyle\displaystyle I(h)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\left(\nabla^{p-1}v_{1}+\nabla^{p-1}v_{2}-\left|u_{1}-u_{2}\right|V\right)\cdot\nabla\xi_{h}\,dx

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\left(\nabla^{p-1}v_{1}+\nabla^{p-1}v_{2}\right)\cdot\nabla\xi_{h}\,dx-\int_{\Omega}\left|u_{1}-u_{2}\right|V\cdot\nu_{h}(x)\,dx.

On the other hand, thanks to (
A.1
), we see that, for each
\displaystyle i=1,2
, for any
\displaystyle 0\leq\psi\in\mathcal{D}(0,T),
we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\nabla^{p-1}v_{i}\cdot\nabla\xi_{h}\,\psi\,dtdx

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\nabla^{p-1}v_{i}\cdot\nabla\left(1-\xi_{h}\right)\psi\,dtdx

\displaystyle\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\left(\nabla\cdot V-f_{i}\right)\left(1-\xi_{h}\right)\hbox{Sign}_{0}(v_{i})\,\psi\,dtdx.

Letting
\displaystyle h\to 0
and using the fact that
\displaystyle\xi_{h}\to 1
in
\displaystyle L^{\infty}(\Omega)-\hbox{weak}^{*}
, we deduce that

\liminf_{h\to 0}\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\nabla^{p-1}v_{i}\cdot\nabla\xi_{h}\,\psi\,dtdx\geq 0.

Coming back to
\displaystyle I(h),
we get

\lim_{h\to 0}I(h)\leq-\lim_{h\to 0}\int_{\Omega}\left|u_{1}-u_{2}\right|V\cdot\nu_{h}(x)\,dx\leq 0,

using assumption (
2.4
). Thus, we obtain (
3.4
).

∎

An immediate consequence of Theorem
3.1
is the uniqueness of a solution for (
2.1
).

Corollary 3.1
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
2.1
, the problem (
2.1
) has at most one solution.

4.
Existence for the evolution problem

The proof of the existence of a solution to (
2.1
) will be carried out in the framework of nonlinear semigroup theory in
\displaystyle L^{1}(\Omega)
. We consider the stationary problem, related to the Euler implicit discretization scheme of the evolution problem (
2.1
)

(4.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcl}\left.\begin{array}[]{l}u-\lambda\>\Delta_{p}v+\lambda\>\nabla\cdot(u\>V)=f\\
\\
u\in\hbox{Sign}^{+}(v)\end{array}\right\}&\mathrm{in}&\Omega\\
\\
\ \ v=0&\mathrm{on}&\Gamma_{D}\\
\\
\ \ \left(\nabla^{p-1}v-u\>V\right)\cdot\nu=0&\mathrm{on}&\Gamma_{N},\end{array}\right.

where
\displaystyle f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
and
\displaystyle\lambda>0
are given.

Definition 4.1
.

A couple
\displaystyle(u,v)\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)\times W^{1,p}_{D}(\Omega)
is a weak solution of (
4.1
) if
\displaystyle u\in{\hbox{Sign}^{+}}(v)
, a.e. in
\displaystyle\Omega
, and

\int_{\Omega}u\>\xi\,dx+\lambda\>\int_{\Omega}\nabla^{p-1}v\cdot\nabla\xi\,dx-\lambda\>\int_{\Omega}u\>V\cdot\nabla\xi\,dx=\int_{\Omega}f\>\xi\,dx,\quad\forall\,\xi\in W^{1,p}_{D}(\Omega).

As a consequence of Theorem
3.1
, we can deduce the following result.

Corollary 4.1
.

If
\displaystyle(u_{1},v_{1})
and
\displaystyle(u_{2},v_{2})
are two solutions of (
4.1
) associated with
\displaystyle f_{1},f_{2}\in L^{1}(\Omega)
, respectively, then

\left|u_{1}-u_{2}\right|_{1}\leq\left|f_{1}-f_{2}\right|_{1}.

Proof.

This is a simple consequence of the fact that if the (independent of
\displaystyle t
) couple
\displaystyle(u,v)
is a weak solution of (
4.1
), then it can be thought out as a time-independent solution of the evolution problem (
2.1
) with
\displaystyle f
replaced by
\displaystyle f-u
(which is also independent of
\displaystyle t
).

∎

We will consider in the sequel
\displaystyle\lambda=1
, the changes being obvious in the general case
\displaystyle\lambda>0
. For
\displaystyle\varepsilon>0
, let

{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(r)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccl}1&\mathrm{if}&r>\varepsilon\\
r/\varepsilon&\mathrm{if}&|r|\leq\varepsilon\\
-1&\mathrm{if}&r<-\varepsilon\end{array}\right.

and consider the regularized problem

(4.2)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcl}\left.\begin{array}[]{l}u_{\varepsilon}-\Delta_{p}v_{\varepsilon}+\nabla\cdot(u_{\varepsilon}\>V)=f\\
\\
u_{\varepsilon}={\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(v_{\varepsilon})\end{array}\right\}&\mathrm{in}&\Omega\\
\\
\ \ v_{\varepsilon}=0&\mathrm{on}&\Gamma_{D}\\
\\
\ \ \left(\nabla^{p-1}v_{\varepsilon}-u_{\varepsilon}\>V\right)\cdot\nu=0&\mathrm{on}&\Gamma_{N}.\end{array}\right.

Observe that, for any
\displaystyle\varepsilon>0
,
\displaystyle\left|{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}\right|\leq 1
,
\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}
is Lipschitz continuous and it satisfies

(I+{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon})^{-1}(r)\longrightarrow(I+{\hbox{Sign}^{+}})^{-1}(r),\quad\mathrm{as}\ \varepsilon\to 0,\quad\hbox{for any}\ r\in\mathbb{R},

i.e.
,
\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}
converges to
\displaystyle{\hbox{Sign}^{+}}
in the sense of the resolvent, which is equivalent to the convergence in the sense of the graph (cf.
[
10
]
).

The following result establishes the existence for the regularized problem and, through a passage to the limit, the existence for (
4.1
).

Proposition 4.1
.

For any
\displaystyle f\in L^{p^{\prime}}(\Omega)
and
\displaystyle\varepsilon>0
, problem (
4.2
) has a weak solution
\displaystyle(u_{\varepsilon},v_{\varepsilon})
, in the sense that
\displaystyle v_{\varepsilon}\in W^{1,p}_{D}(\Omega)
,
\displaystyle u_{\varepsilon}={\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(v_{\varepsilon})
, a.e. in
\displaystyle\Omega
, and

(4.3)

\begin{array}[]{l}\int_{\Omega}u_{\varepsilon}\>\xi\,dx+\int_{\Omega}\nabla^{p-1}v_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla\xi\,dx-\int_{\Omega}u_{\varepsilon}\>V\cdot\nabla\xi\,dx\\
\\
\quad\quad\quad=\int_{\Omega}f\>\xi\,dx,\quad\forall\,\xi\in W^{1,p}_{D}(\Omega).\end{array}

Moreover, as
\displaystyle\varepsilon\to 0,
we have

(4.4)

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(v_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle\displaystyle\longrightarrow

\displaystyle\displaystyle u\quad\mathit{in}\ L^{\infty}(\Omega)-\mathit{weak}^{\star},

(4.5)

\displaystyle\displaystyle v_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\longrightarrow

\displaystyle\displaystyle v\quad\mathit{in}\ W^{1,p}_{D}(\Omega)-\mathit{weak}

and
\displaystyle(u,v)
is the weak solution of (
4.1
). Finally, if
\displaystyle f\geq 0
then
\displaystyle u,v\geq 0
, a.e. in
\displaystyle\Omega
.

Proof.

The existence of a solution for (
4.2
) is standard, but for completeness and the reader’s convenience, we reproduce the main arguments.

Let us denote the topological dual space of
\displaystyle W^{1,p}_{D}(\Omega)
by
\displaystyle\left[W^{1,p}_{D}(\Omega)\right]^{\star}
and the associated duality bracket by
\displaystyle\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
. Observe that the operator

A_{\varepsilon}\,:\,W^{1,p}_{D}(\Omega)\longrightarrow\left[W^{1,p}_{D}(\Omega)\right]^{\star},

defined, for
\displaystyle\xi\in W^{1,p}_{D}(\Omega)
, by

\langle A_{\varepsilon}v,\xi\rangle=\int_{\Omega}{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(v)\,\xi\,dx+\int_{\Omega}\nabla^{p-1}v\cdot\nabla\xi\,dx-\int_{\Omega}{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(v)\,V\cdot\nabla\xi\,dx,

is bounded and weakly continuous. Moreover,
\displaystyle A_{\varepsilon}
is coercive since, for any
\displaystyle u\in W^{1,p}_{D}(\Omega),
we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\langle A_{\varepsilon}v,v\rangle

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(v)\,v\,dx+\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla v\right|^{p}dx-\int_{\Omega}{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(v)\,V\cdot\nabla v\,dx,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla v\right|^{p}dx-\int_{\Omega}\left|V\right|\left|\nabla v\right|dx

\displaystyle\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla v\right|^{p}dx-\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}\int_{\Omega}\left|V\right|^{p^{\prime}}\>dx,

using Young’s inequality. Thus, for any
\displaystyle f\in\left[W^{1,p}_{D}(\Omega)\right]^{\star}\supset L^{p^{\prime}}(\Omega)
, the problem
\displaystyle A_{\varepsilon}v=f
has a solution
\displaystyle v_{\varepsilon}\in W^{1,p}_{D}(\Omega).

To pass to the limit as
\displaystyle\varepsilon\to 0
, we first note that

(4.6)

\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla v_{\varepsilon}\right|^{p}dx\leq C(N,p,\Omega)\left(\int_{\Omega}\left|V\right|^{p^{\prime}}dx+\int_{\Omega}\left|f\right|^{p^{\prime}}dx\right).

Indeed, taking
\displaystyle v_{\varepsilon}
as a test function, we have

\int_{\Omega}u_{\varepsilon}v_{\varepsilon}\,dx+\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla v_{\varepsilon}\right|^{p}dx=\int_{\Omega}u_{\varepsilon}\,V\cdot\nabla v_{\varepsilon}\,dx+\int_{\Omega}fv_{\varepsilon}\,dx.

Using Young’s inequality and the fact that
\displaystyle\left|u_{\varepsilon}\right|=\left|{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(v_{\varepsilon})\right|\leq 1
, we obtain

\int_{\Omega}u_{\varepsilon}\,V\cdot\nabla v_{\varepsilon}\,dx\leq\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}\int_{\Omega}\left|V\right|^{p^{\prime}}+\frac{1}{p}\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla v_{\varepsilon}\right|^{p}dx

and, by combining Poincaré’s with Young’s inequalities, also

\int_{\Omega}fv_{\varepsilon}\,dx\leq\frac{C}{p^{\prime}}\int_{\Omega}\left|f\right|^{p^{\prime}}+\frac{1}{p}\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla v_{\varepsilon}\right|^{p}dx.

Using the fact that
\displaystyle u_{\varepsilon}v_{\varepsilon}\geq 0
, we deduce (
4.6
).

Now, it is clear that the sequences
\displaystyle v_{\varepsilon}
and
\displaystyle u_{\varepsilon}={\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(v_{\varepsilon})
are bounded, respectively, in
\displaystyle W^{1,p}_{D}(\Omega)
and in
\displaystyle L^{\infty}(\Omega)
. Thus, there exists a subsequence (that we denote again by
\displaystyle v_{\varepsilon}
) such that (
4.4
) and (
4.5
) are fulfilled. In particular, using a monotonicity argument (see, for instance,
[
10
]
), this implies that
\displaystyle u\in{\hbox{Sign}^{+}}(v)
, a.e. in
\displaystyle\Omega
, and, letting
\displaystyle\varepsilon\to 0
in (
4.3
), we obtain that
\displaystyle(u,v)
is a weak solution of (
4.1
).

For the last part of the proposition, it is enough to prove that, for each
\displaystyle\varepsilon>0,

\displaystyle u_{\varepsilon}\geq 0
and
\displaystyle v_{\varepsilon}\geq 0
, a.e. in
\displaystyle\Omega
. With this in mind, we consider
\displaystyle\xi=\mathcal{H}_{\sigma}(v_{\varepsilon}^{-})
, for an arbitrary
\displaystyle\sigma>0
, as a test function in (
4.3
), obtaining

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u_{\varepsilon}\>\mathcal{H}_{\sigma}(v_{\varepsilon}^{-})+\int_{\Omega}\nabla^{p-1}v_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla\mathcal{H}_{\sigma}(v_{\varepsilon}^{-})-\int_{\Omega}u_{\varepsilon}\>V\cdot\nabla\mathcal{H}_{\sigma}(v_{\varepsilon}^{-})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}f\>\mathcal{H}_{\sigma}(v_{\varepsilon}^{-}).

This implies

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}u_{\varepsilon}^{-}\>\mathcal{H}_{\sigma}(v_{\varepsilon}^{-})-\int_{\Omega}|\nabla v_{\varepsilon}^{-}|^{p}\>\mathcal{H}_{\sigma}^{\prime}(v_{\varepsilon}^{-})+\int_{\Omega}u_{\varepsilon}^{-}\>V\cdot\nabla\mathcal{H}_{\sigma}(v_{\varepsilon}^{-})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}f\>\mathcal{H}_{\sigma}(v_{\varepsilon}^{-}),

and so

(4.7)

\int_{\Omega}u_{\varepsilon}^{-}\>\mathcal{H}_{\sigma}(v_{\varepsilon}^{-})-\int_{\Omega}u_{\varepsilon}^{-}\>V\cdot\nabla\mathcal{H}_{\sigma}(v_{\varepsilon}^{-})\leq-\int_{\Omega}f\>\mathcal{H}_{\sigma}(v_{\varepsilon}^{-})\leq 0,

where we used the fact that
\displaystyle f\geq 0.
Observe that

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u_{\varepsilon}^{-}\>V\cdot\nabla\mathcal{H}_{\sigma}(v_{\varepsilon}^{-})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(v_{\varepsilon}^{-})\>V\cdot\nabla\mathcal{H}_{\sigma}(v_{\varepsilon}^{-})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{H}_{\sigma}^{\prime}(v_{\varepsilon}^{-})\>{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(v_{\varepsilon}^{-})\>V\cdot\nabla v_{\varepsilon}^{-}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma}\int_{[v_{\varepsilon}^{-}<\sigma]}{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(v_{\varepsilon}^{-})\>V\cdot\nabla v_{\varepsilon}^{-}\ \longrightarrow 0,\hbox{ as }\sigma\to 0,

where we used the fact that
\displaystyle r\to{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(r)
is Lipchitz continuous. So, letting
\displaystyle\sigma\to 0
in (
4.7
), we obtain

\int_{\Omega}u_{\varepsilon}^{-}\leq 0,

which implies that
\displaystyle u_{\varepsilon}^{-}=0
, a.e. in
\displaystyle\Omega
, and then also
\displaystyle v_{\varepsilon}^{-}=0
, a.e. in
\displaystyle\Omega
. Thus,
\displaystyle u_{\varepsilon}
and
\displaystyle v_{\varepsilon}
are nonnegative a.e. in
\displaystyle\Omega.

∎

To prove the existence of a weak solution to (
2.1
), we fix
\displaystyle f\in L^{p^{\prime}}(Q)
and, for an arbitrary
\displaystyle 0<\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
and
\displaystyle n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\displaystyle n\varepsilon=T
, we consider the sequence
\displaystyle(u_{i},v_{i})
given by the
\displaystyle\varepsilon-
Euler implicit scheme associated with (
2.1
), namely

(4.8)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcl}\left.\begin{array}[]{l}u_{i+1}-\varepsilon\Delta_{p}v_{i+1}+\varepsilon\,\nabla\cdot(u_{i+1}V)=u_{i}+\varepsilon f_{i}\\
\\
u_{i+1}\in\hbox{Sign}^{+}(v_{i+1})\end{array}\right\}&\mathrm{in}&\Omega\\
\\
\ \ v_{i+1}=0&\mathrm{on}&\Gamma_{D}\\
\\
\ \ \left(\nabla^{p-1}v_{i+1}-u_{i+1}\>V\right)\cdot\nu=0&\mathrm{on}&\Gamma_{N}.\end{array}\right.

where, for each
\displaystyle i=0,\ldots,n-1
,
\displaystyle f_{i}
is given by

f_{i}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{i\varepsilon}^{(i+1)\varepsilon}f(s)\,ds,\quad\hbox{a.e. in }\Omega.

Now, for a given
\displaystyle\varepsilon-
time discretization
\displaystyle 0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\ldots<t_{n}=T
, satisfying
\displaystyle t_{i+1}-t_{i}=\varepsilon
, we define the
\displaystyle\varepsilon-
approximate solution by

u_{\varepsilon}:=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}u_{i}\,\chi_{[t_{i},t_{i+1})}\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad v_{\varepsilon}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}v_{i}\,\chi_{[t_{i},t_{i+1})}.

Due to Proposition
4.1
and the general theory of evolution problems governed by accretive operators (see, for instance,
[
7
,
4
]
), we define the operator
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
in
\displaystyle L^{1}(\Omega)
by
\displaystyle\mu\in\mathcal{A}(z)
if, and only if,
\displaystyle\mu,z\in L^{1}(\Omega)
and
\displaystyle z
is a solution of the problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcl}\left.\begin{array}[]{l}-\Delta_{p}v+\nabla\cdot(z\>V)=\mu\\
\\
z\in\hbox{Sign}^{+}(v)\end{array}\right\}&\mathrm{in}&\Omega\\
\\
\ \ v=0&\mathrm{on}&\Gamma_{D}\\
\\
\ \ \left(\nabla^{p-1}v-z\>V\right)\cdot\nu=0&\mathrm{on}&\Gamma_{N}.\end{array}\right.

in the sense that
\displaystyle z\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
and there exists
\displaystyle v\in W^{1,p}_{D}(\Omega)
satisfying
\displaystyle z\in{\hbox{Sign}^{+}}(v)
, a.e. in
\displaystyle\Omega
, and

\int_{\Omega}\nabla^{p-1}v\cdot\nabla\xi\,dx-\int_{\Omega}z\>V\cdot\nabla\xi\,dx=\int_{\Omega}\mu\>\xi\,dx,\quad\forall\>\xi\in W^{1,p}_{D}(\Omega).

As a consequence of Corollary
4.1
, we know that the operator
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
is accretive in
\displaystyle L^{1}(\Omega)
. Moreover, we have

\overline{\mathcal{D}(A)}=\left\{u\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)\>:\>\left|u\right|\leq 1,\hbox{ a.e. in }\Omega\right\}.

It then follows from the general theory of nonlinear semigroups governed by accretive operators (see, for instance,
[

) that, as
\displaystyle\varepsilon\to 0
,

(4.9)

u_{\varepsilon}\longrightarrow u,\quad\hbox{ in }\mathcal{C}\left([0,T),L^{1}(\Omega)\right),

and
\displaystyle u
is the so-called
mild solution
of the evolution problem

(4.10)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}u_{t}+\mathcal{A}u\ni f&\hbox{in }(0,T)\\
\\
u(0)=u_{0}.\end{array}\right.

To complete the proof of the existence for problem (
2.1
), we show that the mild solution
\displaystyle u
is, in fact, the solution of (
2.1
). More precisely, we prove the following result.

Theorem 4.1
.

For any non-negative
\displaystyle f\in L^{p^{\prime}}(Q)
and
\displaystyle u_{0}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, the mild solution of (

\displaystyle(u,v)

. Moreover, we have

(4.11)

\iint_{Q}\left|\nabla v\right|^{p}\leq\iint_{Q}f\>v+\iint_{Q}V\cdot\nabla v.

To this aim, we use the limit of the sequence
\displaystyle v_{\varepsilon}
, given by the
\displaystyle\varepsilon-
approximate solution.

Lemma 4.1
.

We have, as
\displaystyle\varepsilon\to 0
,

v_{\varepsilon}\longrightarrow v,\quad\hbox{in }L^{p}\left(0,T;W^{1,p}_{D}(\Omega)\right),

\displaystyle v
satisfies (
4.11
) and
\displaystyle(u,v)
is a weak solution of (
2.1
).

Proof.

Due to Proposition
4.1
, the sequence
\displaystyle(u_{i},v_{i})
given by (

) is well defined in
\displaystyle L^{\infty}(\Omega)\times W^{1,p}_{D}(\Omega)
,
\displaystyle v_{i}\geq 0
,
\displaystyle u_{i}\in{\hbox{Sign}^{+}}(v_{i})

(4.12)

\begin{array}[]{l}\int_{\Omega}u_{i+1}\>\xi\,dx+\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}\nabla^{p-1}v_{i+1}\cdot\nabla\xi\,dx-\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}u_{i+1}\>V\cdot\nabla\xi\,dx\\

\quad\quad\quad=\int_{\Omega}u_{i}\>\xi\,dx+\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}f_{i}\>\xi\,dx,\quad\forall\,\xi\in W^{1,p}_{D}(\Omega).\end{array}

Taking

\iint_{Q}\left|\nabla v_{\varepsilon}\right|^{p}\leq\iint_{Q}\left(f_{\varepsilon}\>v_{\varepsilon}+V\cdot\nabla v_{\varepsilon}\right),

\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla v_{\varepsilon}\right|^{p}dx\leq C(N,p,\Omega)\left(\int_{\Omega}\left|V\right|^{p^{\prime}}dx+\int_{\Omega}\left|f_{\varepsilon}\right|^{p^{\prime}}dx\right).

\displaystyle 0\leq v_{\varepsilon}
is bounded in

\displaystyle 0\leq v\in L^{p}\left(0,T;W^{1,p}_{D}(\Omega)\right)

v_{\varepsilon}\longrightarrow v,\quad\hbox{in }L^{p}\left(0,T;W^{1,p}_{D}(\Omega)\right)-\hbox{weak}.

\displaystyle u\in\hbox{Sign}^{+}(v)
, a.e. in
\displaystyle Q

\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon}=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac{(t-t_{i})u_{i+1}-(t-t_{i+1})u_{i}}{\varepsilon}\>\chi_{[t_{i},t_{i+1})},

So, letting
\displaystyle\varepsilon\to 0
and using the convergence of
\displaystyle(\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon},u_{\varepsilon},v_{\varepsilon},f_{\varepsilon})
to
\displaystyle(u,u,v,f)
, we deduce that
\displaystyle(u,v)
is a weak solution of (
2.1
), and
\displaystyle v
satisfies (
4.11

5.
Asymptotic behaviour as
\displaystyle p\to\infty

This section contains the proof of Theorem
2.2
, namely the study of the limit as
\displaystyle p\to\infty
of the solution of (
2.1
).
We start with appropriate
a priori
estimates independent of
\displaystyle p
. We obviously have

(5.1)

\left\|u_{p}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}\leq 1.

Thanks to (
4.11
) and (
2.3
), we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\iint_{Q}\left|\nabla v_{p}\right|^{p}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\iint_{Q}\left(f-\nabla\cdot V\right)v_{p}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{1}{p^{\prime}\epsilon^{p^{\prime}}}\iint_{Q}\left|f-\nabla\cdot V\right|^{p^{\prime}}+\frac{\epsilon^{p}}{p}\iint_{Q}\left|v_{p}\right|^{p}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{1}{p^{\prime}\epsilon^{p^{\prime}}}\iint_{Q}\left|f-\nabla\cdot V\right|^{p^{\prime}}+\frac{C_{p}^{p}\epsilon^{p}}{p}\iint_{Q}\left|\nabla v_{p}\right|^{p},

using Young’s and Poincaré’s inequalities.
We now take

\epsilon=\left(\frac{p}{2}\right)^{1/p}\frac{1}{C_{p}}

to get

\iint_{Q}\left|\nabla v_{p}\right|^{p}\leq(p-1)\left(\frac{2C_{p}}{p}\right)^{p^{\prime}}\iint_{Q}\left|f-\nabla\cdot V\right|^{p^{\prime}}.

Now, for any
\displaystyle q\geq 1
and
\displaystyle p\geq q
, we have, by Hölder’s inequality,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\iint_{Q}\left|\nabla v_{p}\right|^{q}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left|Q\right|^{1-\frac{q}{p}}\left(\iint_{Q}\left|\nabla v_{p}\right|^{p}\right)^{\frac{q}{p}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left|Q\right|^{1-\frac{q}{p}}(p-1)^{\frac{q}{p}}\left(\frac{2C_{p}}{p}\right)^{{}^{\frac{q}{p-1}}}\left(\iint_{Q}\left|f-\nabla\cdot V\right|^{p^{\prime}}\right)^{\frac{q}{p}}.

Taking the limit as
\displaystyle p\to\infty
, we obtain

(5.2)

\lim_{p\to\infty}\left\|\nabla v_{p}\right\|_{q}^{q}\leq\left|Q\right|,

since
\displaystyle C_{p}\to C
(see
[
32
, page 110]
) and

\left\|f-\nabla\cdot V\right\|_{p^{\prime}}^{\frac{q}{p-1}}\longrightarrow\left\|f-\nabla\cdot V\right\|_{1}^{0}=1.

Using again Poincaré’s inequality, we conclude that
\displaystyle(v_{p})_{p}
is bounded in
\displaystyle L^{q}\left(0,T;W^{1,q}(\Omega)\right)
.

Proof of Theorem
2.2

From (
5.1
) and (
5.2
), we find a pair

\left(u,v\right)\in L^{\infty}(Q)\times L^{q}\left(0,T;W^{1,q}(\Omega)\right)

such that, for subsequences (that we relabel for convenience),

\begin{array}[]{cccrl}u_{p}&\rightharpoonup&u&\mathrm{in}&L^{\infty}(Q)-\mbox{weak-}\ast;\\
\\
v_{p}&\rightharpoonup&v&\mathrm{in}&L^{q}\left(0,T;W^{1,q}(\Omega)\right)-\mathrm{weak}.\end{array}

Moreover, we have
\displaystyle 0\leq v,

\displaystyle 0\leq u\leq 1

and

\left\|\nabla v\right\|_{q}\leq\left|Q\right|^{1/q},\qquad\forall q>1.

So, taking
\displaystyle q\to\infty
, we get

\left\|\nabla v\right\|_{\infty}\leq 1.

Next, we show that
\displaystyle\partial_{t}u_{p}
is uniformly weakly bounded. Indeed, for
\displaystyle\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(Q)
,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\iint_{Q}u_{p}\partial_{t}\varphi

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\iint_{Q}\left|\nabla v_{p}\right|^{p-2}\nabla v_{p}\cdot\nabla\varphi+\iint_{Q}u_{p}\>V\cdot\nabla\varphi+\iint_{Q}f\varphi

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\|\nabla\varphi\|_{\infty}\left\{\iint_{Q}\left|\nabla v_{p}\right|^{p-1}+\iint_{Q}|V|+C\|f\|_{1}\right\}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle C\>\|\nabla\varphi\|_{\infty}.

We can then apply
[
2
, Proposition 1.4]
, to conclude that

u\in\hbox{Sign}^{+}(v).

Thanks to (
4.11
), one sees that

(5.3)

\begin{array}[]{c}-\delta\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}u\xi+\iint_{Q}\left|\nabla v_{p}\right|^{p-2}\nabla v_{p}\cdot\nabla(v_{p}-\delta\xi)\\
\leq\iint_{Q}u_{p}\>V\cdot\nabla\left(v_{p}-\delta\xi\right)+\iint_{Q}f\left(v_{p}-\delta\xi\right),\quad\hbox{ in }\mathcal{D}^{\prime}([0,T)),\end{array}

for any
\displaystyle\xi\in W_{D}^{1,\infty}(\Omega)
and
\displaystyle 0<\delta<1
, where we use again that

\int_{\Omega}u_{p}\>V\cdot\nabla v_{p}=\int_{\Omega}V\cdot\nabla v_{p},\quad\hbox{ a.e }t\in[0,T).

Our aim now is to let
\displaystyle p\to\infty
:

(1)

for the first term, we have

(5.4)

-\delta\>\lim_{p\to\infty}\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}u_{p}\xi\quad\rightharpoonup\quad-\delta\>\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}u\xi,\quad\hbox{ in }\mathcal{D}^{\prime}([0,T));

(2)

concerning the second term, by monotonicity and (
5.2
), we have

\hskip-28.45274pt\liminf_{p\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla v_{p}\right|^{p-2}\nabla v_{p}\cdot\nabla(v_{p}-\delta\>\xi)

\hskip 42.67912pt\geq\lim_{p\to\infty}\delta^{p-1}\>\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla\xi\right|^{p-2}\nabla\xi\cdot\nabla(v_{p}-\delta\>\xi)=0;\quad\hbox{ in }\mathcal{D}^{\prime}([0,T))

(3)

as for the first term on the right-hand side, we get

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lim_{p\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}u_{p}\>V\cdot\nabla\left(v_{p}-\delta\>\xi\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lim_{p\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}u_{p}\>V\cdot\nabla v_{p}

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\delta\>\lim_{p\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}u_{p}\>V\cdot\nabla\xi

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lim_{p\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}V\cdot\nabla v_{p}

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\delta\>\lim_{p\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}u_{p}\>V\cdot\nabla\xi

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u\>V\cdot\nabla(v-\delta\>\xi),\quad\hbox{ in }\mathcal{D}^{\prime}([0,T))

since
\displaystyle u_{p}\in\hbox{Sign}^{+}(v_{p})
and
\displaystyle u\in\hbox{Sign}^{+}(v)
, a.e. in
\displaystyle Q;

(4)

for the second term on the right-hand side, the passage to the limit is straightforward.

So, for any
\displaystyle 0<\delta<1,
we get

-\delta\>\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}u\xi-\int_{\Omega}u\>V\cdot\nabla\left(v-\delta\xi\right)\leq\int_{\Omega}f\left(v-\delta\xi\right)\quad\hbox{ in }\mathcal{D}^{\prime}([0,T)).

Letting at last
\displaystyle\delta\to 1
, we obtain the result.

\displaystyle\square

Appendix A

We need two lemmas to prepare for the proof of Proposition

Lemma A1
.

If
\displaystyle(u,v)
is a weak solution of (
2.1
), then

(A.1)

-\Delta_{p}v+\left(\nabla\cdot V-f\right)\hbox{Sign}_{0}(v)\leq 0\quad\hbox{in }\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\left((0,T)\times\overline{\Omega}\right).

Proof.

We extend
\displaystyle v
to
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}\times\Omega
by
\displaystyle 0
, for any
\displaystyle t\not\in(0,T)
, and, for any
\displaystyle h>0
, we consider, with
\displaystyle\xi\in\mathcal{D}(\overline{\Omega})
and
\displaystyle\psi\in\mathcal{D}\left((0,T)\right)
,

\Phi^{h}(t,x)=\xi(x)\,\psi(t)\,\frac{1}{h}\int_{t}^{t+h}{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(v(s,x))\,ds,\quad\hbox{for a.e. }(t,x)\in Q,

where
\displaystyle\psi
is extended, in turn, to
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}
by
\displaystyle 0
, and
\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}
is given in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}
by

{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(r)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\hbox{ if }r>\varepsilon\\
r/\varepsilon&\hbox{ if }\left|r\right|\leq\varepsilon\\
-1&\hbox{ if }r<-\varepsilon,\end{array}\right.

for
\displaystyle\varepsilon>0.
It is clear that
\displaystyle\Phi^{h}\in W^{1,p}\left(0,T;W^{1,p}_{D}(\Omega)\right)\cap L^{\infty}(Q)
is an admissible test function for the weak formulation, so that

(A.2)

-\iint_{Q}u\,\partial_{t}\Phi^{h}+\iint_{Q}\left(\nabla^{p-1}v-V\,u\right)\cdot\nabla\Phi^{h}=\iint_{Q}f\,\Phi^{h}.

Observe that

(A.3)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\iint_{Q}u\,\partial_{t}\Phi^{h}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\iint_{Q}u\,\xi\,\partial_{t}\psi\,\frac{1}{h}\int_{t}^{t+h}{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(v((s))\,ds

\displaystyle\displaystyle+\iint_{Q}u(t)\,\frac{{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(v(t+h))-{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(v(t))}{h}\,\psi(t)\,\xi.

Moreover, using the fact that, for a.e.
\displaystyle t\in(0,T)
,
\displaystyle 0\leq u(t)\leq 1
,
\displaystyle{{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}}\geq 0
and
\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(0)=0
, we have

u(t,x)\,{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(v(t,x))={\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(v(t,x))

and

u(t,x)\>{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(v(t+h,x))\leq{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(v(t+h,x)),\quad\mbox{a.e. }(t,x)\in Q.

So, for
\displaystyle h>0
small enough, we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\iint_{Q}u(t)\,\frac{{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(v(t+h))-{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(v(t))}{h}\,\psi(t)\,\xi

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\iint_{Q}\frac{{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(v(t+h))-{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(v(t))}{h}\,\psi(t)\,\xi

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\iint_{Q}\frac{\psi(t-h)-\psi(t)}{h}\,{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(v(t))\,\xi.

This implies that

\limsup_{h\to 0}\iint_{Q}u(t)\,\frac{{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(v(t+h))-{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(v(t))}{h}\,\psi(t)\,\xi\leq-\iint_{Q}\partial_{t}\psi\,{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(v(t))\,\xi,

so that, by letting
\displaystyle h\to 0
in (
A.3
), we get

\lim_{h\to 0}\iint_{Q}u\,\partial_{t}\Phi^{h}\leq 0.

Then, by letting
\displaystyle h\to 0
in (
A.2
), we obtain

(A.4)

\iint_{Q}\left(\nabla^{p-1}v-V\>u\right)\cdot\nabla\left({\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(v(t))\,\xi\right)\psi\leq\iint_{Q}f\,{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(v(t))\,\xi\,\psi.

On the other hand, using again the fact that
\displaystyle u\,{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(v)={\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(v)
, a.e. in
\displaystyle Q
, we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\iint_{Q}\left(\nabla^{p-1}v-V\>u\right)\cdot\nabla\left({\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(v(t))\,\xi\right)\psi\hskip 113.81102pt

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\iint_{Q}{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(v)\,\nabla^{p-1}v\cdot\nabla\xi\,\psi+\iint_{Q}\left|\nabla v\right|^{p}({{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}})^{\prime}(v)\,\xi\,\psi

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\iint_{Q}V\cdot\nabla\left(\xi{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(v)\right)\psi

\displaystyle\displaystyle\geq\iint_{Q}{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(v)\,\nabla^{p-1}v\cdot\nabla\xi\,\psi+\iint_{Q}\nabla\cdot V\left(\xi{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}(v)\right)\psi,

using (
2.3

\displaystyle\left|\nabla v\right|^{p}({{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}})^{\prime}(v)\geq 0

Thanks to (

Lemma A2
.

Let
\displaystyle u\in L^{1}_{loc}(Q)
,
\displaystyle F\in L^{1}_{loc}(Q)^{N}
and
\displaystyle J_{1}\in L^{1}_{loc}(Q)
be such that

(A.5)

\partial_{t}u+V\cdot\nabla u-\nabla\cdot F=J_{1}\quad\hbox{in }\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(Q),

where
\displaystyle V\cdot\nabla u
is taken in the sense
\displaystyle V\cdot\nabla u=\nabla\cdot(u\,V)-u\,\nabla\cdot V
in
\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(Q)
. If

(A.6)

-\nabla\cdot F\leq J_{2}\quad\hbox{in }\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(Q),

for some
\displaystyle J_{2}\in L^{1}_{loc}(Q)
, then

(A.7)

\partial_{t}\beta(u)+V\cdot\nabla\beta(u)-\nabla\cdot F\leq{J_{1}}\beta^{\prime}(u)+{J_{2}}(1-\beta^{\prime}(u))\quad\hbox{in }\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(Q),

for any
\displaystyle\beta\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(\mathbb{R})
such that
\displaystyle\beta^{\prime}\leq 1
.

Proof.

We set

Q_{\varepsilon}:=\left\{(t,x)\in Q\>:\>d((t,x),\partial Q)>\varepsilon\right\}.

Moreover, for any
\displaystyle z\in L^{1}_{loc}(Q)
, we denote by
\displaystyle z_{\varepsilon}
the usual regularization of
\displaystyle z
by convolution given by

z_{\varepsilon}:=z\star\rho_{\varepsilon},\quad\hbox{in }Q_{\varepsilon},

where
\displaystyle\rho_{\varepsilon}
is the standard mollifying sequence in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}
.
We can show that (
A.5
) and (
A.6
) imply, respectively,

(A.8)

\partial_{t}u_{\varepsilon}+V\cdot\nabla u_{\varepsilon}-\nabla\cdot F_{\varepsilon}={J_{1}}_{\varepsilon}+\mathcal{C}_{\varepsilon}\quad\hbox{in }Q_{\varepsilon}

and

(A.9)

-\nabla\cdot F_{\varepsilon}\leq{J_{2}}_{\varepsilon}\quad\hbox{in }Q_{\varepsilon},

where
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\varepsilon}
is the usual commutator given by

\mathcal{C}_{\varepsilon}:=V\cdot\nabla u_{\varepsilon}-(V\cdot\nabla u)_{\varepsilon}.

Here
\displaystyle(V\cdot\nabla u)_{\varepsilon}
needs to be understood in the sense

(V\cdot\nabla u)_{\varepsilon}=(u\,V)\star\nabla\rho_{\varepsilon}-(u\,\nabla\cdot V)\star\rho_{\varepsilon},\quad\hbox{in }Q_{\varepsilon}.

Multiplying (
A.8
) by
\displaystyle\beta^{\prime}(u_{\varepsilon})
and (
A.9
) by
\displaystyle 1-\beta^{\prime}(u_{\varepsilon})
, and adding the resulting equations, we obtain

\beta^{\prime}(u_{\varepsilon})\,\partial_{t}u_{\varepsilon}+\beta^{\prime}(u_{\varepsilon})\,V\cdot\nabla u_{\varepsilon}-\nabla\cdot F_{\varepsilon}\leq\mathcal{C}_{\varepsilon}\,\beta^{\prime}(u_{\varepsilon})+{J_{1}}_{\varepsilon}\beta^{\prime}(u_{\varepsilon})+{J_{2}}_{\varepsilon}(1-\beta^{\prime}(u_{\varepsilon}))

and

(A.10)

\partial_{t}\beta(u_{\varepsilon})+V\cdot\nabla\beta(u_{\varepsilon})-\nabla\cdot F_{\varepsilon}\leq\mathcal{C}_{\varepsilon}\,\beta^{\prime}(u_{\varepsilon})+{J_{1}}_{\varepsilon}\beta^{\prime}(u_{\varepsilon})+{J_{2}}_{\varepsilon}(1-\beta^{\prime}(u_{\varepsilon})),

in
\displaystyle Q_{\varepsilon}
. Since
\displaystyle V\in W^{1,1}_{loc}(\Omega)
and
\displaystyle\nabla\cdot V\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, it is well-known that taking a subsequence if necessary, the commutator converges to
\displaystyle 0
in
\displaystyle L^{1}_{loc}(Q)
, as
\displaystyle\varepsilon\to 0
(see, for instance,
[
1
]
). Thus, letting
\displaystyle\varepsilon\to 0
in (
A.10
), we obtain (
A.7
).
