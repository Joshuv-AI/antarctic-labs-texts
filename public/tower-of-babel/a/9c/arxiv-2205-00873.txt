A generalization of Newton-Maclaurin’s inequalities Thanks: Research of author is supported by an NSFC Grant No. 11871243
Changyu Ren

Abstract
. In this paper, we prove Newton-Maclaurin type inequalities for functions obtained by linear combination of two neighboring primary symmetry functions, which is a generalization of the classical Newton-Maclaurin inequality.

26D05, 26D15

1.
Introduction

The
k
-th elementary symmetric function of the variables

x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{n}
is defined by

\sigma_{k}(x)=\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{k}\leq n}x_{i_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{k}},\quad 1\leq k\leq n,

where
x=(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{n})
. For example, when
n=3
,

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}(x)=x_{1}+x_{2}+x_{3},\quad\sigma_{2}(x)=x_{1}x_{2}+x_{1}x_{3}+x_{2}x_{3},\quad\sigma_{n}(x)=x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}.

It will be convenient to define
\sigma_{0}(x)=1
, and define

\sigma_{k}(x)=0
if
k<0
or
k>n
. Furthermore, define a
k
-th
elementary symmetric mean as

E_{k}(x)=\dfrac{\sigma_{k}(x)}{C_{n}^{k}},\quad k=0,1,\cdots,n,

where
C_{n}^{k}=\dfrac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}
.

Theorem 1.1
.

(Newton
[
19
]
and Maclaurin
[
17
]
) Let

x=(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})
be an
n
-tuple non-negative real numbers. Then

(1.1)

E_{k}^{2}(x)\geq E_{k-1}(x)E_{k+1}(x),\quad k=1,2\cdots,n-1,

(1.2)

E_{1}(x)\geq E_{2}^{1/2}(x)\geq\cdots\geq E_{n}^{1/n}(x),

and the inequality is strict unless all entries of
x
coincide.

The inequalities (
1.1
) in Theorem
1.1
is a consequence of a rule stated by Newton
[
19
]

which gives a lower bound on the number of nonreal roots of a real
polynomial. Since Newton did not give a proof of his rule, the
proof of Theorem
1.1
is due to MacLaurin
[
17
]
.
Actually, the Newton’s inequalities (
1.1
) work for
n
-tuples
of real, not necessarily positive elements. For an inductive proof in the case where

x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{n}
are nonnegative, see
[
9
]

\lx@sectionsign
2.22. For a proof by differential calculus in the case
where
x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{n}
are real, see
[
9
]

\lx@sectionsign
4.3, or
[
21
]
.
Several reformulations/generalisations of Newton’s inequalities have been given over the years,
see for instance
[
18
,
21
,
20
,
2
]
.

The Newton-Maclaurin inequalities play important roles in deriving
theoretical result for fully nonlinear partial differential
equations and geometric analysis. There are many important results
that need to use the Newton-Maclaurin inequalities, such as

[
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
11
]
etc. This is because the following

k
-Hessian equations and curvature equations

\displaystyle\sigma_{k}(\lambda(u_{ij}))

\displaystyle=f(x,u,\nabla u),

\displaystyle\sigma_{k}(\kappa(X))

\displaystyle=f(X,\nu)

are central studies in the field of fully nonlinear partial
differential equations and geometric analysis. Their left-hand side

k
-Hessian operator
\sigma_{k}
is the primary symmetric function
about the eigenvalues
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{n})
of the Hessian
matrix
(u_{ij})
or
the principal curvature

\kappa=(\kappa_{1},\cdots,\kappa_{n})
of the surface. In recent years,
the fully non-linear equations derived from linear combinations of
primary symmetric functions have received increasing attention. For
example, the following special Lagrangian equation

{\rm Imdet}(\delta_{ij}+{\rm i}u_{ij})=\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{[(n-1)/2]}(-1)^{k}\sigma_{2k+1}(\lambda(u_{ij}))=0,

which derived by Harvey and Lawson in their study of the minimal
submanifold problem
[
10
]
. The fully non-linear partial
differential equations

P_{m}(u_{ij})=\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}(l_{k}^{+})^{m-k}(x)\sigma_{k}(u_{ij})=g^{m-1}(x)

studied by Krylov
[
12
]
and Dong
[
1
]
. In
[
13
]
,
Li-Ren-Wang studied the concavity of operators
\displaystyle\sum_{s=0}^{k}\alpha_{s}\sigma_{s}
and
\sigma_{k}+\alpha\sigma_{k-1}
, and
discussed the curvature estimates for the convex surface of the
corresponding equations

\displaystyle\sum_{s=0}^{k}\alpha_{s}\sigma_{s}(\kappa(X))=f(X,\nu(X)),\quad X\in M.

Guan and Zhang
[
8
]
investigated the curvature estimates of the
following curvature equation

\sigma_{k}(W_{u}(x))+\alpha(x)\sigma_{k-1}(W_{u}(x))=\displaystyle\sum_{l=0}^{k-2}\alpha_{l}(x)\sigma_{l}(W_{u}(x)),\quad x\in\mathbb{S}^{n}.

Recently, Liu and Ren
[
16
]
discussed the Pogorelov-type
C^{2}

estimates for
(k-1)
-convex and
k
-convex solutions of the
following Sum Hessian equations

\sigma_{k}(\lambda(u_{ij}))+\alpha\sigma_{k-1}(\lambda(u_{ij}))=f(x,u,\nabla u),

and established a rigidity theorem when the right-hand side of the
equation is constant.

In the further study of the above problem, the Newton-Maclaurin type
inequalities for the operators derived from the left-hand side of
the equations are always needed. A natural question is that whether
the Newton-Maclaurin type inequalities for the operators of linear
combinations of these primary symmetric functions still hold?

Now the main result of this paper is stated as follows.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
n\geq 3,1\leq k\leq n-2
. For any real number
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and any
n
-tuple real numbers

x=(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have

(1.3)

[\alpha E_{k}(x)+E_{k+1}(x)]^{2}\geq[\alpha E_{k-1}(x)+E_{k}(x)][\alpha E_{k+1}(x)+E_{k+2}(x)].

The inequality is strict unless
x_{1}=\cdots=x_{n}
, or

\dfrac{\alpha E_{k}(x)+E_{k+1}(x)}{\alpha E_{k-1}(x)+E_{k}(x)}=\dfrac{\alpha E_{k+1}(x)+E_{k+2}(x)}{\alpha E_{k}(x)+E_{k+1}(x)}=-\alpha.

A special case in which the above equality holds is that there are

n-1
elements of
x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{n}
taking the value

-\alpha
.

Furthermore, if
\alpha\geq 0
and

\alpha E_{m}(x)+E_{m+1}(x)\geq 0,\quad{\rm for~all~~}m=0,1,\cdots,k,

then

(1.4)

[\alpha+E_{1}(x)]\geq[\alpha E_{1}(x)+E_{2}(x)]^{1/2}\geq\cdots\geq[\alpha E_{k}(x)+E_{k+1}(x)]^{1/(k+1)}.

Theorem
1.2
is the Newton-Maclaurin type inequality for the
function obtained by linear combination of two neighboring terms

E_{k}
and
E_{k+1}
. Obviously, it is the classical Newton-Maclaurin
inequality when
\alpha=0
. Furthermore, one may ask if the
Newton-Maclaurin type inequality still holds for the functions which
obtained by linear combination of general primary symmetric
functions. That is, for
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

x=(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n+1})\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, whether the following
inequalities hold?

(1.5)

\left(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\alpha_{k}E_{k}(x)\right)^{2}\geq\left(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\alpha_{k}E_{k-1}(x)\right)\left(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\alpha_{k}E_{k+1}(x)\right).

The answer is no in general. For example, let
n=3
,
\alpha=(1,0,1)
,

x=(4,4,\dfrac{1}{4},\dfrac{1}{4})
, we have

\displaystyle E_{1}(x)=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{4}(4+4+\dfrac{1}{4}+\dfrac{1}{4})=\dfrac{17}{8},

\displaystyle E_{2}(x)=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{6}(4\times 4+4\times 4\times\dfrac{1}{4}+\dfrac{1}{4}\times\dfrac{1}{4})=\dfrac{107}{32},

\displaystyle E_{3}(x)=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{4}(2\times 4\times 4\times\dfrac{1}{4}+2\times 4\times\dfrac{1}{4}\times\dfrac{1}{4})=\dfrac{17}{8},

\displaystyle E_{4}(x)=

\displaystyle 4\times 4\times\dfrac{1}{4}\times\dfrac{1}{4}=1,

therefore,

\displaystyle[E_{1}(x)+E_{3}(x)]^{2}-[1+E_{2}(x)][E_{2}(x)+E_{4}(x)]=-\dfrac{825}{1024}<0.

Maybe inequalities (
1.5
) hold when
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{n})

satisfy some structural conditions.

A straightforward conclusion of Newton’s inequality (
1.1
) is

(1.6)

\sigma_{k}^{2}(x)-\sigma_{k-1}(x)\sigma_{k+1}(x)\geq\theta\sigma_{k}^{2}(x),

where
0<\theta<1
is a constant depending on
n
and
k
. The
inequality (
1.6
) is also a common sense which is widely used
in the study of
k
-Hessian equations and curvature equations, see

[
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
11
,
13
,
14
,
15
]
, etc. For the Sum Hessian
operators
\sigma_{k}+\alpha\sigma_{k-1}
, if
\alpha>0
and

x=(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})\in\Gamma_{k}
, Liu and Ren
[
16
]
proved

[\sigma_{k}(x)+\alpha\sigma_{k-1}(x)]^{2}\geq[\sigma_{k-1}(x)+\alpha\sigma_{k-2}(x)][\sigma_{k+1}(x)+\alpha\sigma_{k}(x)]

by Newton’s inequality (
1.6
). Here
\Gamma_{k}
is the
Garding’s cone

\Gamma_{k}=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\ |\quad\sigma_{m}(\lambda)>0,\quad m=1,\cdots,k\}.

The requirement
x\in\Gamma_{k}
of above inequalities restrict their
applications in the study of Sum Hessian equations. In this paper,
we remove the restrictions from
\alpha
and
x
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
n\geq 3,0\leq k\leq n-1
. For any real number
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and any
n
-tuple real numbers

x=(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have

(1.7)

(1-\theta)[\alpha\sigma_{k}(x)+\sigma_{k+1}(x)]^{2}-[\alpha\sigma_{k-1}(x)+\sigma_{k}(x)][\alpha\sigma_{k+1}(x)+\sigma_{k+2}(x)]\geq 0,

where
0<\theta<1
is a constant depending only on
n
and
k
.

Remark 1.4
.

When
k=0
or
k=n-1
, (
1.3
) does not hold, one can take

x_{1}=x_{2}=\cdots=x_{n}
for verification. It is a difference between
(
1.7
) and (
1.3
).

2.
The proof of Theorem
1.2

First, we consider the following inequality in Euclidean space

\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

For any real number
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and
z=(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, we have

(2.1)

[\alpha E_{1}(z)+E_{2}(z)]^{2}\geq[\alpha E_{0}(z)+E_{1}(z)][\alpha E_{2}(z)+E_{3}(z)].

The inequality is strict unless
z_{1}=z_{2}=z_{3}
or

\dfrac{\alpha E_{1}(z)+E_{2}(z)}{\alpha E_{0}(z)+E_{1}(z)}=\dfrac{\alpha E_{2}(z)+E_{3}(z)}{\alpha E_{1}(z)+E_{2}(z)}=-\alpha.

Proof.

A straightforward calculation shows

\displaystyle 18[\alpha E_{1}(z)+E_{2}(z)]^{2}-18[\alpha E_{0}(z)+E_{1}(z)][\alpha E_{2}(z)+E_{3}(z)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[(z_{1}+\alpha)(z_{2}+\alpha)-(z_{1}+\alpha)(z_{3}+\alpha)]^{2}+[(z_{1}+\alpha)(z_{2}+\alpha)-(z_{2}+\alpha)(z_{3}+\alpha)]^{2}

\displaystyle+[(z_{1}+\alpha)(z_{3}+\alpha)-(z_{2}+\alpha)(z_{3}+\alpha)]^{2}\geq 0.

Obviously, the inequality is strict unless
z_{1}=z_{2}=z_{3}
or any two
elements of
z_{1},z_{2},z_{3}
valued
-\alpha
. Without loss of generality,
we assume
z_{1}=z_{2}=-\alpha
. Then we obtain

\displaystyle\alpha E_{0}(z)+E_{1}(z)=\dfrac{z_{3}+\alpha}{3},\alpha E_{1}(z)+E_{2}(z)=\dfrac{-\alpha(z_{3}+\alpha)}{3},\alpha E_{2}(z)+E_{3}(z)=\dfrac{\alpha^{2}(z_{3}+\alpha)}{3}.

Thus,

\dfrac{\alpha E_{1}(z)+E_{2}(z)}{\alpha E_{0}(z)+E_{1}(z)}=\dfrac{\alpha E_{2}(z)+E_{3}(z)}{\alpha E_{1}(z)+E_{2}(z)}=-\alpha.

∎

The following Lemma is a useful tool to prove Newton’s inequalities
(
1.1
) from Sylvester
[
22
,
20
]
.

Lemma 2.2
.

If

F(x,y)=c_{0}x^{m}+c_{1}x^{m-1}y+\cdots+c_{m}y^{m}

is a homogeneous function of the
n
-th degree in
x
and
y
which
has all its roots
x/y
real, then the same is true for all
non-identical 0 equations
\dfrac{\partial^{i+j}F}{\partial x^{j}\partial y_{j}}=0
, obtained from it by partial differentiation
with respect to
x
and
y
. Further, if
E
is one of these
equations, and it has a multiple root
\alpha
, then
\alpha
is also a
root, of multiplicity one higher, of the equation from which
E
is
derived by differentiation.

Let us now present the proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Proof.

We assume
P(t)
is a polynomial of
n
-degree, with real roots

x_{1},x_{2},\cdots x_{n}
. Then
P(t)
is represented as

P(t)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(t-x_{i})=E_{0}(x)t^{n}-C_{n}^{1}E_{1}(x)t^{n-1}+C_{n}^{2}E_{2}(x)t^{n-2}-\cdots+(-1)^{n}E_{n}(x),

and we shall apply Lemma
2.2
to the associated homogeneous
polynomial

F(t,s)=E_{0}(x)t^{n}-C_{n}^{1}E_{1}(x)t^{n-1}s+C_{n}^{2}E_{2}(x)t^{n-2}s^{2}-\cdots+(-1)^{n}E_{n}(x)s^{n}.

Considering the case of the
derivatives
\dfrac{\partial^{n-3}F}{\partial t^{n-2-k}\partial s^{k-1}}
(for
k=1,\cdots,n-2
), we arrive to the fact that all the
cubic polynomials

(2.2)

E_{k-1}(x)t^{3}-3E_{k}(x)t^{2}s+3E_{k+1}(x)ts^{2}-E_{k+2}(x)s^{3}

for

k=1,\cdots,n-2
also have real roots.

If
E_{k-1}(x)=E_{k+2}(x)=0
, it is easy to get

\displaystyle[\alpha E_{k}(x)+E_{k+1}(x)]^{2}-[\alpha E_{k-1}(x)+E_{k}(x)][\alpha E_{k+1}(x)+E_{k+2}(x)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2}[\alpha E_{k}(x)+E_{k+1}(x)]^{2}+\dfrac{\alpha^{2}}{2}E_{k}^{2}(x)+\dfrac{1}{2}E_{k+1}^{2}(x)\geq 0.

So we divide it in two cases to deal with (
1.3
):

E_{k-1}(x)\neq 0
or
E_{k+2}(x)\neq 0
.

Case A: When
E_{k-1}(x)\neq 0
, by (
2.2
), the polynomial

t^{3}-\dfrac{3E_{k}(x)}{E_{k-1}(x)}t^{2}+\dfrac{3E_{k+1}(x)}{E_{k-1}(x)}t-\dfrac{E_{k+2}(x)}{E_{k-1}(x)}

has three real roots. We denote the roots by
z_{1},z_{2},z_{3}
and
denote
z=(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})
, then

E_{1}(z)=\dfrac{E_{k}(x)}{E_{k-1}(x)},\quad E_{2}(z)=\dfrac{E_{k+1}(x)}{E_{k-1}(x)},\quad E_{3}(z)=\dfrac{E_{k+2}(x)}{E_{k-1}(x)}.

Using Lemma
2.1
we
obtain (
1.3
).

Case B: When
E_{k+2}(x)\neq 0
, by (
2.2
), the polynomial

s^{3}-\dfrac{3E_{k+1}(x)}{E_{k+2}(x)}s^{2}+\dfrac{3E_{k}(x)}{E_{k+2}(x)}s-\dfrac{E_{k-1}(x)}{E_{k+2}(x)}

has three real roots. Then the proof of (
1.3
) is similar to
Case A.

By Lemma
2.1
, the inequalities of (
1.3
) are strict
unless
x_{1}=x_{2}=\cdots=x_{n}
or

\dfrac{\alpha E_{k}(x)+E_{k+1}(x)}{\alpha E_{k-1}(x)+E_{k}(x)}=\dfrac{\alpha E_{k+1}(x)+E_{k+2}(x)}{\alpha E_{k}(x)+E_{k+1}(x)}=-\alpha.

A special case in which the above equality holds is that there are

n-1
elements of
x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{n}
valued
-\alpha
.

Now, we prove (
1.4
). When
k=0
, we get from (
1.1
),

\displaystyle[\alpha+E_{1}(x)]^{2}-[\alpha E_{1}(x)+E_{2}(x)]=

\displaystyle\alpha^{2}+\alpha E_{1}(x)+E_{1}^{2}(x)-E_{2}(x)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\alpha(\alpha+E_{1}(x))\geq 0.

Therefore,

[\alpha+E_{1}(x)]\geq[\alpha E_{1}(x)+E_{2}(x)]^{1/2}.

For
1\leq k\leq n-2
, we assume

[\alpha E_{k-2}(x)+E_{k-1}(x)]^{1/(k-1)}\geq[\alpha E_{k-1}(x)+E_{k}(x)]^{1/k}.

Combining (
1.3
) and the
inequalities above, we get

\displaystyle[\alpha E_{k-1}(x)+E_{k}(x)]^{2}\geq

\displaystyle[\alpha E_{k-2}(x)+E_{k-1}(x)][\alpha E_{k}(x)+E_{k+1}(x)]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle[\alpha E_{k-1}(x)+E_{k}(x)]^{(k-1)/k}[\alpha E_{k}(x)+E_{k+1}(x)].

Thus,

[\alpha E_{k-1}(x)+E_{k}(x)]^{1/k}\geq[\alpha E_{k}(x)+E_{k+1}(x)]^{1/(k+1)}

by canceling the common factor.

∎

3.
The proof of Theorem
1.3

We divide it in two cases to prove Theorem
1.3
:

(i) Special cases:
k=0
,
k=n-1
or
n=3,k=1
;

(ii) General cases:
n\geq 4,1\leq k\leq n-2
.

3.1.
Special cases

\bullet
When
k=0
, choose
\theta=\dfrac{1}{2}
in (
1.7
).
Using the identity
\sigma_{1}^{2}(x)=\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}+2\sigma_{2}
, we
have

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2}[\alpha+\sigma_{1}(x)]^{2}-[\alpha\sigma_{1}(x)+\sigma_{2}(x)]=

\displaystyle\dfrac{\alpha^{2}}{2}+\dfrac{1}{2}\sigma_{1}^{2}(x)-\sigma_{2}(x)\geq 0.

\bullet
When
k=n-1
, choose
\theta=\dfrac{1}{2}
. By using the
identity

\sigma_{n-1}^{2}(x)=\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\dfrac{\sigma_{n}^{2}(x)}{x_{i}^{2}}+2\sigma_{n-2}(x)\sigma_{n}(x),

we get

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2}[\alpha\sigma_{n-1}(x)+\sigma_{n}(x)]^{2}-[\alpha\sigma_{n-2}(x)+\sigma_{n-1}(x)]\alpha\sigma_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\sigma_{n-1}^{2}(x)+\dfrac{1}{2}\sigma_{n}^{2}(x)-\alpha^{2}\sigma_{n-2}(x)\sigma_{n}(x)\geq 0.

\bullet
When
n=3,k=1
, choose
\theta=\dfrac{1}{2}
. Using the
identities

\sigma_{1}(x)^{2}=x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2}+2\sigma_{2}(x),\quad\sigma_{2}^{2}(x)=x_{1}^{2}x_{2}^{2}+x_{1}^{2}x_{3}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}x_{3}^{2}+2\sigma_{1}(x)\sigma_{3}(x),

we obtain

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2}[\alpha\sigma_{1}(x)+\sigma_{2}(x)]^{2}-[\alpha+\sigma_{1}(x)][\alpha\sigma_{2}(x)+\sigma_{3}(x)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\sigma_{1}^{2}(x)+\dfrac{1}{2}\sigma_{2}^{2}(x)-\alpha^{2}\sigma_{2}(x)-\alpha\sigma_{3}(x)-\sigma_{1}(x)\sigma_{3}(x)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\dfrac{\alpha^{2}}{2}x_{1}^{2}+\dfrac{1}{2}x_{2}^{2}x_{3}^{2}-\alpha x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}\geq 0.

3.2.
General cases

For
n\geq 4,1\leq k\leq n-2
, we denote

(3.1)

a=C_{n}^{k-1},\quad b=C_{n}^{k},\quad c=C_{n}^{k+1},\quad d=C_{n}^{k+2}.

Then the inequalities (
1.7
) can be rewritten as

(1-\theta)[\alpha bE_{k}(x)+cE_{k+1}(x)]^{2}-[\alpha aE_{k-1}(x)+bE_{k}(x)][\alpha cE_{k+1}(x)+dE_{k+2}(x)]\geq 0.

From Lemma
2.2
, and by a similar proof to (
1.3
), we
shall complete the proof Theorem 1.3 via establishing the following
inequalities.

(1-\theta)[\alpha bE_{1}(z)+cE_{2}(z)]^{2}-[\alpha a+bE_{1}(z)][\alpha cE_{2}(z)+dE_{3}(z)]\geq 0.

Here
z=(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Furthermore, it is
equivalent to prove

(3.2)

L(z):=(\alpha b\sigma_{1}+c\sigma_{2})^{2}-(3\alpha a+b\sigma_{1})(\alpha c\sigma_{2}+3d\sigma_{3})\geq\theta(\alpha b\sigma_{1}+c\sigma_{2})^{2},

where

\sigma_{1}=z_{1}+z_{2}+z_{3},\quad\sigma_{2}=z_{1}z_{2}+z_{1}z_{3}+z_{2}z_{3},\quad\sigma_{3}=z_{1}z_{2}z_{3}.

Now we prove (
3.2
). Let us expand the left-hand and
right-hand sides of (
3.2
) separately. By using identities

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}^{2}=z_{1}^{2}+z_{2}^{2}+z_{3}^{2}+2\sigma_{2},\quad\sigma_{2}^{2}=z_{1}^{2}z_{2}^{2}+z_{1}^{2}z_{3}^{2}+z_{2}^{2}z_{3}^{2}+2\sigma_{1}\sigma_{3},

we have

\displaystyle L(z)=

\displaystyle\alpha^{2}b^{2}\sigma_{1}^{2}+\alpha bc\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}+c^{2}\sigma_{2}^{2}-3\alpha^{2}ac\sigma_{2}-9\alpha ad\sigma_{3}-3bd\sigma_{1}\sigma_{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha^{2}b^{2}\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{3}z_{i}^{2}+\alpha^{2}(2b^{2}-3ac)\sigma_{2}+\alpha bc\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}

(3.3)

\displaystyle+c^{2}\displaystyle\sum_{p<q}^{3}z_{p}^{2}z_{q}^{2}+(2c^{2}-3bd)\sigma_{1}\sigma_{3}-9\alpha ad\sigma_{3},

(3.4)

\displaystyle(\alpha b\sigma_{1}+c\sigma_{2})^{2}=

\displaystyle\alpha^{2}b^{2}\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{3}z_{i}^{2}+2\alpha^{2}b^{2}\sigma_{2}+2\alpha bc\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}+c^{2}\displaystyle\sum_{p<q}^{3}z_{p}^{2}z_{q}^{2}+2c^{2}\sigma_{1}\sigma_{3}.

For any
z=(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, we define a nonnegative
function

\displaystyle W(z,t)=

\displaystyle(z_{1}-z_{2})^{2}(\alpha+tz_{3})^{2}+(z_{1}-z_{3})^{2}(\alpha+tz_{2})^{2}+(z_{2}-z_{3})^{2}(\alpha+tz_{1})^{2}.

Expanding
W(z,t)
we get

(3.5)

\displaystyle W(z,t)=

\displaystyle 2\alpha^{2}\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{3}z_{i}^{2}-2\alpha^{2}\sigma_{2}+2\alpha t\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}+2t^{2}\displaystyle\sum_{p<q}^{3}z_{p}^{2}z_{q}^{2}-2t^{2}\sigma_{1}\sigma_{3}-18\alpha t\sigma_{3}.

We want to absorb the terms
\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}
,
\sigma_{1}\sigma_{3}

and
