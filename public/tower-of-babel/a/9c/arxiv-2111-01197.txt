Special Solutions to the Space Fractional Diffusion Problem
Tokinaga Namba, Piotr Rybka, Shoichi Sato

Abstract
We derive a fundamental solution ℰ {\mathscr{E}} to a space-fractional diffusion problem on the half-line. The equation involves the Caputo derivative. We establish properties of ℰ {\mathscr{E}} as well as formulas for solutions to the Dirichlet and Neumann problems in terms of convolution of ℰ {\mathscr{E}} with data. We also study integrability of derivative of solutions given in this way. We present conditions sufficient for uniqueness. Finally, we show the infinite speed of signal propagation.



Key words:
Caputo derivative, space-fractional diffusion operator, fundamental solution, regularity of solutions, decay of solutions, speed of propagation

2020 Mathematics Subject Classification.
Primary: 35R11, Secondary: 35A08

Introduction

In
[
9
]
and
[
14
]
the authors studied the following equation

\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}-\frac{\partial}{\partial x}D^{\alpha}_{x}u=0\qquad(x,t)\in\Omega\times(0,\infty),

(1.1)

when
\Omega=(0,L)
augmented with the boundary data and initial conditions, here
D^{\alpha}_{x}
is the fractional Caputo derivative with respect to the spacial variable
x
.
The interest in such problems stems from the fact that eq. (
1.1
) appears in the Green–Ampt infiltration models of subsurface flows, see
[
16
]
. In fact, (
1.1
) is a simplification of free boundary problem, which was studied in
[
15
]
.

In this note we derive in Theorem
3.1
a formula for
{\mathscr{E}}
, a self-similar solution to (
1.1
) considered on
(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
(here we write
\mathbb{R}_{+}
for
(0,\infty)
). Studying self-similar solutions or travelling fronts is important, when we wish to gain insight into the structure of solution and in particular their long time behavior.

Function
{\mathscr{E}}
, which we find, is sufficiently smooth to be a classical solution to (
1.1
). We show that

{\mathscr{E}}(x,t)=\frac{a_{0}}{t^{\frac{1}{1+\alpha}}}E_{\alpha,1+1/\alpha,1/\alpha}\left(-\frac{x^{1+\alpha}}{(1+\alpha)t}\right),

where
E_{\alpha,1+1/\alpha,1/\alpha}

is the 3-parameter generalized Mittag-Leffler function, see (
2.8
). We show that
{\mathscr{E}}
is positive and the integral of
{\mathscr{E}}(\cdot,t)
over
\mathbb{R}_{+}
does not depend on
t>0
.
In fact,
{\mathscr{E}}
is a fundamental solution of (
1.1
), namely solutions to the Dirichlet and Neumann problems for
\Omega=\mathbb{R}_{+}

may be expressed by means of convolution of the initial condition with
{\mathscr{E}}
, this is the content of Theorem
3.12
.

The problem of existence of a fundamental solution to various versions of time-fractional problems has already been addressed in the literature. We name just a few papers dealing with this issues, see
[
1
]
,
[
8
]
,
[
11
]
,
[
5
]
,
[
12
]
. The tools used there are different from ours. However, it is not surprising that generalized Mittag-Leffler functions play a role. We could justify positivity of
{\mathscr{E}}
on the grounds of the theory of Mittag-Leffler functions, (actually we do this in the appendix). However,
we would like to stress that our proof of positivity of
{\mathscr{E}}
is entirely based on a PDE tool, which the maximum principle, we use this idea after
[
13
]
.

Let us stress that the justification of formulas for solution based on the convolution requires establishing a number of properties of
{\mathscr{E}}(\cdot,1)
. In particular, we show that
{\mathscr{E}}(\cdot,1)
is monotone. For this purpose we use a PDE tool, which is the maximum principle. We also show some sort of decay, namely
x{\mathscr{E}}(x,1)
is uniformly bounded, see Lemma
3.14
. These properties of
{\mathscr{E}}
seem to be of independent interest.

Once we have a convolution formula for solutions to (
1.1
), we may study properties of solutions to (
1.1
) when
\Omega=\mathbb{R}_{+}
. An urging question is about uniqueness of solutions given in this way. We show that if the initial conditions are sufficiently regular, i.e. they are absolutely continuous with compact support, then solutions enjoy sufficient regularity for employing the method of testing the equation with the solution itself, see Proposition
3.16
. This technique immediately yields uniqueness and decay of solutions.

Eq. (
1.1
) contains a parameter
\alpha
, so does
{\mathscr{E}}
. Due to analyticity of
E_{\alpha,1+1/\alpha,1/\alpha}
we deduce that if
u^{\alpha}
is a solution to (
1.1
) on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, then
u^{\alpha}\to u^{\alpha_{0}}
in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
when
\alpha
goes to
\alpha_{0}\in(0,1]
. We explicitly exclude
\alpha_{0}=0
, which is due to the fact that
E_{\alpha,1+1/\alpha,1/\alpha}

has no limit on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
when
\alpha\to 0
. However, this case is covered by

[
9
, Theorem 6.1]
.

With this observation we may
address here the
issue of the speed of the signal propagation. This is a bit puzzling because for
\alpha=1
, eq. (
1.1
) becomes the heat equation with the infinite speed of propagation, while for
\alpha=0
problem (
1.1
) is the transport eq. for which the speed is finite.

Our numerical experiments presented in
[
10
]
show that an initial pulse moves to the left with a finite speed. The same conclusions are drawn on the basis of numerical simulations by the authors of
[
7
]
who dealt with the time-fractional diffusion-wave equation. However, our Proposition
3.21
stated for (
1.1
) with
\Omega=\mathbb{R}_{+}
and the Neumann boundary condition shows that actually the speed of the signal is infinite – the support of the solution instantly becomes equal to
[0,\infty)
. This is shown
with the help of the explicit formulas employing the fundamental solution,
{\mathscr{E}}
constructed here. This fact does not contradict numerical observation.

After presenting the content we describe the organization of the note. In Section 2 we recall the fundamentals of the fractional calculus. Section 3 is devoted to the derivation of a formula for a self-similar solution
{\mathscr{E}}
. Here we also study its properties collected in Theorem
3.1
and we derive the formulas for the integral representation of unique solutions. In the Appendix we present the derivation of
{\mathscr{E}}
which based on the properties of the generalized Mittag-Leffler function
E_{\beta kl}
and not the series manipulation. We also show a short proof of positivity of
{\mathscr{E}}
, which follows from the theory of the generalized Mittag-Leffler function.

2
Preliminaries

We will simultaneously recall the definitions of the Caputo and Riemann-Liouville fractional derivatives. For a function
f\in L^{1}(0,L)
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
we introduce the fractional integration operator by setting,

(I^{\alpha}f)(x)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{0}^{x}(x-z)^{\alpha-1}f(z)\,dz.

(2.1)

For an absolutely continuous function
u\in AC[0,L]
we define the Caputo fractional derivative of order
\alpha\in(0,1)
by the following formula,

D^{\alpha}_{C}u(x)=(I^{1-\alpha}u^{\prime})(x)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\int_{0}^{x}\frac{u^{\prime}(s)}{(x-s)^{\alpha}}\,ds,

(2.2)

while the Riemann-Liouville fractional derivative has the form,

D^{\alpha}_{RL}u=\frac{d}{dx}(I^{1-\alpha}u).

(2.3)

Later, for the sake of simplicity of notation we will write
D^{\alpha}
for
D^{\alpha}_{C}
, occasionally with a lower index
x
indicating the space variable.

We notice that if
u
is absolutely continuous and
u(0)=0,
then we have

\frac{d}{dx}\left(I^{1-\alpha}u\right)=I^{1-\alpha}\frac{d}{dx}u\qquad\hbox{i.e.}\qquad D^{\alpha}_{C}u=D^{\alpha}_{{RL}}u.

(2.4)

The following formula explains the relationship between the two types of derivatives for a general function
u\in AC[0,L]
,

D^{\alpha}_{x_{RL}}u=D^{\alpha}_{x_{C}}u+\frac{x^{-\alpha}}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}u(0).

(2.5)

The fractional integration is the inverse of the Caputo derivative up to a constant,

I^{\alpha}D^{\alpha}_{C}u(x)=u(x)-u(0).

(2.6)

In our analysis we will need a more convenient representation of the operator
(D^{\alpha}_{x}u)_{x}
.
For this purpose we need to recall:

Lemma 2.1
.

(see
[
9
, Proposition 2.1]
)
Let
u:[0,l)\to\mathbb{R}
be such that
u\in C^{2}(0,l)\cap C[0,l)
and
u^{\prime}\in L^{1}(0,l)
.
Then,
(D^{\alpha}_{x}u)_{x}
exists everywhere in
(0,l)
and

\begin{split}(D^{\alpha}u)_{x}(x)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}&\left(\frac{\alpha(u(0)-u(x))+(\alpha+1)u^{\prime}(x)x}{x^{\alpha+1}}\right.\\
&\quad\left.+\alpha(\alpha+1)\int_{0}^{x}[u(x-z)-u(x)+u^{\prime}(x)z]\frac{dz}{z^{\alpha+2}}\right)\end{split}

(2.7)

for
x\in(0,l)
.

With the help of this Lemma we will compute action of
(D^{\alpha}u)_{x}
on scaled functions:

Corollary 2.2
.

Let
u:[0,l)\to\mathbb{R}
be such that
u\in C^{2}(0,l)\cap C[0,l)
and
u^{\prime}\in L^{1}(0,l)
. If
\lambda>0
and we set
v_{\lambda}(x)=u(\lambda^{\frac{1}{1+\alpha}}x)
, then
(D^{\alpha}_{x}v_{\lambda})_{x}(x)=\lambda(D^{\alpha}_{x}u)_{y}(\lambda^{\frac{1}{1+\alpha}}x)
.

Proof.

We use Lemma
2.1
to calculate
(D_{x}^{\alpha}v_{\lambda})_{x}
,

\displaystyle(D_{x}^{\alpha}v_{\lambda})_{x}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\left(\frac{\alpha(v_{\lambda}(0)-v_{\lambda}(x))+(\alpha+1)(v_{\lambda})_{x}(x)x}{x^{\alpha+1}}+\alpha(\alpha+1)\int_{0}^{x}[v_{\lambda}(x-z)-v_{\lambda}(x)+(v_{\lambda})_{x}(x)z]\frac{dz}{z^{\alpha+2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\left(\frac{\alpha(u(0)-u(\lambda^{\frac{1}{1+\alpha}}x))+(\alpha+1)\lambda^{\frac{1}{1+\alpha}}u_{y}(\lambda^{\frac{1}{1+\alpha}}x)x}{x^{\alpha+1}}\right.

\displaystyle\quad\left.+\alpha(\alpha+1)\int_{0}^{x}[u(\lambda^{\frac{1}{1+\alpha}}(x-z))-u(\lambda^{\frac{1}{1+\alpha}}x)+\lambda^{\frac{1}{1+\alpha}}u_{y}(\lambda^{\frac{1}{1+\alpha}}x)z]\frac{dz}{z^{\alpha+2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\left(\frac{\alpha(u(0)-u(\lambda^{\frac{1}{1+\alpha}}x))+(\alpha+1)u_{y}(\lambda^{\frac{1}{1+\alpha}}x)(\lambda^{\frac{1}{1+\alpha}}x)}{\lambda^{-1}(\lambda^{\frac{1}{1+\alpha}}x)^{\alpha+1}}\right.

\displaystyle\quad\left.+\alpha(\alpha+1)\int_{0}^{x}[u(\lambda^{\frac{1}{1+\alpha}}(x-z))-u(\lambda^{\frac{1}{1+\alpha}}x)+u_{y}(\lambda^{\frac{1}{1+\alpha}}x)(\lambda^{\frac{1}{1+\alpha}}z)]\frac{dz}{z^{\alpha+2}}\right).

Changing the variable of integration by
\lambda^{\frac{1}{1+\alpha}}z=\xi
we see
dz/z^{\alpha+2}=\lambda d\xi/\xi^{2+\alpha}
. Hence that the last term of the right-hand-side (RHS) is equal to

\frac{\alpha(\alpha+1)\lambda}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\int_{0}^{\lambda^{\frac{1}{1+\alpha}}x}[u(\lambda^{\frac{1}{1+\alpha}}x-\xi)-u(\lambda^{\frac{1}{1+\alpha}}x)+u_{y}(\lambda^{\frac{1}{1+\alpha}}x)\xi]\frac{d\xi}{\xi^{\alpha+2}}.

Therefore applying Lemma
2.1
again yields

(D_{x}^{\alpha}v_{\lambda})_{x}(x)=\lambda^{1}(D_{y}^{\alpha}u)_{y}(\lambda^{\frac{1}{1+\alpha}}x).

∎

In our construction of the self-similar solution we will use a three-parameter generalized Mittag-Leffler function
E_{\beta,m,l}
.
It is defined by the following series for
z\in\mathbb{C}
, (see
[
4
, formula (1.9.19)]
)

E_{\beta,m,l}(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}^{\beta ml}z^{n},\qquad\Re\beta>0,\ m>0,\ -\beta(jm+l)\not\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\},

(2.8)

where

c_{n}^{\beta ml}=\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac{\Gamma(\beta(im+l)+1)}{\Gamma(\beta(im+l+1)+1)}.

It is worth recalling that due to

[
2
, Theorem 1]
, see also page 48 in
[
4
]
, we know that
E_{\beta,m,l}
is an entire function of order
(\Re\beta)^{-1}
and type
m^{-1}
, i.e.

|E_{\beta,m,l}(z)|<\exp((m^{-1}+\epsilon)|z|^{1/\beta})

holds for
|z|\geq r_{0}(\epsilon),
where
r_{0}(\epsilon)
is sufficiently large.

In several places we will use the following observation concerning the Mittag-Leffler functions. Let us set,

\Phi(x)=E_{\alpha,1+1/\alpha,1/\alpha}\left(-\frac{x^{1+\alpha}}{1+\alpha}\right).

(2.9)

We recall:

Proposition 2.3
.

(see
[
4
, Example 4.11]
,
[
3
, Theorem 4]
)
Function
\Phi
defined in (
2.9
) satisfies the following fractional ordinary differential equation,

D_{y_{C}}^{\alpha}v(y)+\frac{1}{1+\alpha}yv(y)=0,\quad y>0,\qquad v(0)=1.

(2.10)

3
The fundamental solution and its properties

We will derive a
self-similar solution,
{\mathscr{E}},
to the following equations,

\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}-\frac{\partial}{\partial x}D^{\alpha}u=0\qquad(x,t)\in(0,\infty)^{2}.

(3.1)

This is done in the theorem below,
where we also present the basic properties of
{\mathscr{E}}
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Function
{\mathscr{E}}:(0,\infty)^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
given by formula

{\mathscr{E}}(x,t)=a_{0}t^{-\frac{1}{1+\alpha}}E_{\alpha,1+1/\alpha,1/\alpha}\left(-\frac{x^{1+\alpha}}{(1+\alpha)t}\right).

(3.2)

where

\frac{1}{a_{0}}=2\int_{0}^{\infty}E_{\alpha,1+1/\alpha,1/\alpha}(-x^{1+\alpha}/(1+\alpha))\,dx

(3.3)

is well-defined:

(1)
{\mathscr{E}}\in C^{2}((0,\infty)^{2})
and
{\mathscr{E}}
is a self-similar solution to (
3.1
), i.e. it is invariant under the transformation
(x,t)\mapsto(\lambda^{\frac{1}{1+\alpha}}x,\lambda t)
for
\lambda>0
.

(2)
{\mathscr{E}}
is positive for all
x,t>0
.

(3) For all
t>0
function
{\mathscr{E}}(\cdot,t)
is decreasing.

(4) For all
t>0
function
{\mathscr{E}}(\cdot,t)
is in
L^{1}(0,\infty)
and
the integral
\int_{0}^{\infty}{\mathscr{E}}(x,t)\,dx=\frac{1}{2}
.

Remark 3.2
.

Using
\Phi
defined in (
2.9
) we can write
{\mathscr{E}}
shortly as follows,

{\mathscr{E}}(x,t)=a_{0}t^{-\frac{1}{1+\alpha}}\Phi\left(\frac{x}{t^{\frac{1}{1+\alpha}}}\right).

Our proof of Theorem
3.1
will be conducted in a number of steps. Here is the first one:

Lemma 3.3
.

Let us suppose that
u\in C^{2}((0,\infty)^{2})
. Then
u
is a solution to (
3.1
) if and only if
u_{\lambda}
is a solution, where

u_{\lambda}(x,t)=u(\lambda^{\frac{1}{1+\alpha}}x,\lambda t).

Proof.

Now, it is straightforward to see that

(u_{\lambda})_{t}(x,t)=\lambda^{1}u_{s}(\lambda^{\frac{1}{1+\alpha}}x,\lambda t).

We use Corollary
2.2
to compute
(D^{\alpha}u_{\lambda})_{x}
. We apply this result to
v_{\lambda}
defined as
v_{\lambda}(x)=u(\lambda^{\frac{1}{1+\alpha}},\lambda t)
. This leads us to the identity,

(D^{\alpha}u_{\lambda})_{x}(x,t)=(D^{\alpha}v_{\lambda})_{x}(x)=\lambda^{1}(D^{\alpha}u)_{y}(\lambda^{\frac{1}{1+\alpha}},\lambda t).

Thus, we see that

u
satisfies (
3.1
) if and only if
u_{\lambda}
fulfills

(u_{\lambda})_{t}(x,t)-(D_{x}^{\alpha}u_{\lambda})_{x}(x,t)=0

for all
x>0,\ t>0
.
∎

The above Lemma tells us that self-similar solutions depend only on
\xi=xt^{-\frac{1}{1+\alpha}}
. However, if we want to obtain a solution, whose average over
\mathbb{R}_{+}
is independent of time, then we must consider
{\mathscr{E}}
of the form

{\mathscr{E}}(x,t)=a_{0}t^{\gamma}v(x^{1+\alpha}/t),

(3.4)

where
v
is integrable. It is easy to see that for

\gamma=-\frac{1}{1+\alpha}

and
t_{2}>t_{1}>0
we have

\int_{0}^{\infty}{\mathscr{E}}(x,t_{2})\,dx=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\mathscr{E}}(x,t_{2})\,dx.

We are now ready to derive the form of
{\mathscr{E}}
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let us assume that
v
appearing in (
3.4
) is analytic and
v(0)=1
. Then,

v(z)=E_{\alpha,1+1/\alpha,1/\alpha}\left(-\frac{z}{1+\alpha}\right),

where
E_{\beta,m,l}
is the generalized Mittag-Leffler function defined in (
2.8
).

Proof.

Let us suppose that

v(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}z^{n}.

Then, inserting
{\mathscr{E}}
defined by (
3.4
) into (
3.1
) yields

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}(\gamma-n)\frac{x^{(1+\alpha)n}}{t^{n-\gamma+1}}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}c_{n}\frac{\Gamma((1+\alpha)n+1)[(1+\alpha)(n-1)+1]}{\Gamma((1+\alpha)(n-1)+2)}\frac{x^{(1+\alpha)(n-1)}}{t^{n-\gamma}},

where we took into account that
D^{\alpha}x^{\beta}=\frac{\Gamma(\beta+1)}{\Gamma(\beta+1-\alpha)}x^{\beta-\alpha}.
Hence, we find the formula for
c_{n}
,

c_{n}=\frac{(-1)^{n}}{(1+\alpha)^{n}}b_{n},

where we set
c_{0}=1
and

b_{n}=\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac{\Gamma(\alpha i+i+2)}{\Gamma(\alpha(i+1)+i+2)}.

(3.5)

Hence,

v(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}b_{n}\left(\frac{z}{1+\alpha}\right)^{n}.

If we take into account the form of
b_{n}
’s, then we realize that

v(z)=E_{\alpha,1+1/\alpha,1/\alpha}\left(-\frac{z}{1+\alpha}\right),

where
E_{\beta,m,l}
is a generalized Mittag-Leffler function defined in (
2.8
).
Thus, we reache
{\mathscr{E}}
of the form (
3.2
), where the multiplicative constant
a_{0}
has to be determined by other means.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

The argument above is based on the series manipulation. In the Appendix, we present the proof of this lemma, which is based only on the theory of the generalized Mittag-Leffler function
E_{\beta,m,l}
.

An important step in our analysis is checking that
{\mathscr{E}}
is indeed positive. The argument we use is based on the use of the maximum principle for (
1.1
). This idea was used first in the proof of a similar result in
[
13
]
. We provide our own and extended version of the argument. In the Appendix we present a short proof of the same result, which is of independent interest. It is based on the theory of the three-parameter Mittag-Leffler function.

Lemma 3.6
.

Function

\Phi
defined in (
2.9
) is positive for all
x>0
.

Proof.

Let us set

u(x,t):=\int_{0}^{xt^{-\frac{1}{1+\alpha}}}\Phi(z)\,dx.

We will see that

\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}-\frac{\partial}{\partial x}D^{\alpha}_{x_{C}}\right)u<0.

(3.6)

Indeed,

\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}(x,t)=-\frac{1}{t(1+\alpha)}y\Phi(y)
,
where
y=x{t^{-\frac{1}{1+\alpha}}}

and by Corollary
2.2
we see that

\frac{\partial}{\partial x}D^{\alpha}_{x_{C}}u(x,t)=\frac{1}{t}\frac{\partial}{\partial y}D^{\alpha}_{y_{C}}u(y,t)
. Thus, due to

\frac{\partial}{\partial x}D^{\alpha}_{x_{C}}u=D^{\alpha}_{x_{RL}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}u

and (
2.5
) we obtain

\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}-\frac{\partial}{\partial x}D^{\alpha}_{x_{C}}\right)u(x,t)=-\frac{1}{t}\left(\frac{y\Phi(y)}{1+\alpha}+D^{\alpha}_{y_{RL}}\Phi(y)\right)=-\frac{1}{t}\left(\frac{y\Phi(y)}{1+\alpha}+D^{\alpha}_{y_{C}}\Phi(y)\right)-\frac{y^{-\alpha}}{t\Gamma(1-\alpha)}.

Now, we invoke Proposition
2.3
to conclude that the expression in parenthesis vanishes. Thus, (
3.6
) follows.

Let us suppose our claim is not valid and the set

A=\{x>0:\Phi(x)<0\}

is not empty. Since
\Phi(0)=1
, we see that
\inf A=:x_{0}>0
. Due to the continuity of
\Phi

there is
x_{1}>x_{0}
such that

u(x,1)>0\qquad\hbox{for }x\in(0,x_{1}).

Let us set

\Omega=\{(x,t)\in(0,\infty)^{2}:\ x\in(0,x_{1}t^{-1/(1+\alpha)}),t\in(1,2)\}.

(3.7)

By the weak maximum principle for (
3.6
) in non-cylindrical regions, see
[
15
, Lemma 8]
,
we have

\sup\{u(x,t):(x,t)\in\Omega\}=\max\{u(x,t):(x,t)\in\partial\Omega\setminus(\{2\}\times\mathbb{R}_{+})\}=:M.

However, since
u(0,t)=0
and
u(x_{1}t^{-1/(1+\alpha)},t)=u(x_{1},1)<u(x_{0},1)
for
t\in[1,2]
, then we see that

M=u(x_{0},1).

If we had the strong maximum principle, then we would have reached a contradiction. But we do not have it, thus, we have to continue our argument. For a positive
\epsilon
and
(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times(1,\infty)
we set,

v_{\epsilon}(x,t)=\epsilon c_{\alpha}x^{1+\alpha}+\epsilon(t-1),

where
c_{\alpha}=\frac{1}{(1+\alpha)\Gamma(1+\alpha)}.
Due to the choice of
c_{\alpha}
we see that
\frac{\partial v_{\epsilon}}{\partial t}-\frac{\partial}{\partial x}D^{\alpha}v_{\epsilon}=0
. Thus, the sum
u+v_{\epsilon}
satisfies (
3.6
) and we may apply the weak maximum principle, see
[
15
, Lemma 8]
, which yields,

\sup\{(u+v_{\epsilon})(x,t):(x,t)\in\Omega\}=\max\{(u+v_{\epsilon})(x,t):(x,t)\in\partial\Omega\setminus(\{2\}\times\mathbb{R}_{+})\}=:M_{\epsilon}.

(3.8)

We want to select such
\epsilon
that
M_{\epsilon}=\max(u+v_{\epsilon})(x,1)
.
We notice that
(u+v_{\epsilon})(0,t)=\epsilon(t-1)\leq\epsilon
and we may restrict
\epsilon
so that
\epsilon<u(x_{1},1)
. We take even smaller
\epsilon
so that

\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(u+v_{\epsilon})(x_{1},t)<0.

Hence, there is
x_{2}\in(x_{0},x_{1})
such that

\max\{(u+v_{\epsilon})(x,1):x\in(0,x_{1})\}=(u+v_{\epsilon})(x_{2},1)

We want to guarantee that
M_{\epsilon}
equals
(u+v_{\epsilon})(x_{2},1)=u(x_{2},1)+\epsilon c_{\alpha}x_{2}^{1+\alpha}.

We look at
u+v_{\epsilon}
on the last part of the parabolic boundary of
\Omega
,

\gamma=\{(x_{1}t^{1/(1+\alpha)},t):\ t\in(1,2)\}.

For
(x,t)\in\gamma
we have

(u+v_{\epsilon})(x_{1}t^{1/(1+\alpha)},t)=u(x_{1},1)+\epsilon(c_{\alpha}x_{1}^{1+\alpha}t+t-1)=u(x_{1},1)+\epsilon c_{\alpha}x_{1}^{1+\alpha}+(t-1)\epsilon(c_{\alpha}x_{1}^{1+\alpha}+1).

We see that right-hand-side above is greater than
(u+v_{\epsilon})(x_{1},1)
for
t\in(1,2)
. At the same time
we see that the left-hand-side attains its maximum for
t=2
and we may take
\epsilon
so small that

\epsilon(c_{\alpha}x_{1}^{1+\alpha}+1)+(u+v_{\epsilon})(x_{1},1)<(u+v_{\epsilon})(x_{2},1)=M_{\epsilon}.

Now, we shall see that
\sup_{\Omega}(u+v_{\epsilon})>M_{\epsilon}
. Let us consider points
(x,t)
of the following form,
(x_{2}t^{1/(1+\alpha)},t)
,
t\in(1,2).
Now, we compute
values of
(u+v_{\epsilon})
there for
t>1
. We obtain,

\displaystyle(u+v_{\epsilon})(x_{2}t^{1/(1+\alpha)},t)

\displaystyle=

Lemma 3.10

Appendix

Here we present a different and more elegant approach to the derivation of
{\mathscr{E}}
and its positivity. It depends on the properties of the three-parameter generalized Mittag-Leffler function and the arguments are shorter.

We first derive the form of
{\mathscr{E}}
assuming that
u
defined as

u(x,t)=t^{-\frac{1}{1+\alpha}}v(xt^{-\frac{1}{1+\alpha}})

is a solution to (
3.1
). Then, Corollary (
2.2
) yields,

(D^{\alpha}_{x}u)_{x}(x,t)=t^{-1-\frac{1}{1+\alpha}}(D^{\alpha}_{y}v)_{y}(y),

where
y=xt^{-\frac{1}{1+\alpha}}.
Moreover, it is easy to check directly that

u_{t}(x,t)=-\frac{1}{1+\alpha}t^{-1-\frac{1}{1+\alpha}}(v(y)+yv^{\prime}(y)).

Combining these observations with (
3.1
) yields,

-\frac{1}{1+\alpha}t^{-1-\frac{1}{1+\alpha}}(v(y)+yv^{\prime}(y))=(D^{\alpha}_{y}v)_{y}(y).

This can be rewritten as

\frac{d}{dy}\left(D_{y}^{\alpha}v(y)+\frac{1}{1+\alpha}yv(y)\right)=0.

Since we are interested in smooth solutions then, due to
[
10
, Proposition 3.1]
we see that

D_{y}^{\alpha}v(y)+\frac{1}{1+\alpha}yv(y)=0,\qquad y>0.

[formula]

According to Proposition
2.3
a solution to this equation is given by the formula

v(y)=v(0)E_{\alpha,1+1/\alpha,1/\alpha}\left(-\frac{y^{1+\alpha}}{1+\alpha}\right).

Here
E_{\alpha,1+1/\alpha,1/\alpha}
is a generalized Mittag-Leffler function defined in (
2.8
).

We can also offer much shorter and easier proof of the positivity of
{\mathscr{E}}
. It is based again on the theory of the Mittag-Leffler function and ODEs.

Lemma A.

Function
(0,\infty)\ni x\mapsto v(x)\equiv E_{\alpha,1+\frac{1}{\alpha},\frac{1}{\alpha}}(-\frac{x^{1+\alpha}}{1+\alpha})
is positive for all
x>0
.

Proof.

We consider eq. (*) for
v
with the initial condition
v(0)=1

Then,
v(x)\equiv E_{\alpha,1+\frac{1}{\alpha},\frac{1}{\alpha}}(-\frac{x^{1+\alpha}}{1+\alpha})
is a solution to (*).
Let us suppose our claim is not valid and the set

A:=\{x>0:v(x)\leq 0\}

is not empty. Due to the continuity of
v
, there is
x_{0}
such that

v(x_{0})=0\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad v(x)>0\quad\mbox{for}\,\,x\in(0,x_{0}).

However, we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle v(x_{0})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1+\alpha}{x_{0}}D_{x}^{\alpha}v(x_{0})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1+\alpha}{x_{0}\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\left(\frac{v(x_{0})-v(0)}{x_{0}^{\alpha}}+\alpha\int_{0}^{x_{0}}\frac{v(x_{0})-v(x_{0}-z)}{z^{\alpha+1}}dz\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1+\alpha}{x_{0}\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\left(\frac{1}{x_{0}^{\alpha}}+\alpha\int_{0}^{x_{0}}\frac{v(x_{0}-z)}{z^{\alpha+1}}dz\right)>0.

This is a contradiction.
∎
