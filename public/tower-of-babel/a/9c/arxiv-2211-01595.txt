Reinforcement Learning in Non-Markovian Environments
Siddharth Chandak, Pratik Shah, Vivek S Borkar, Parth Dodhia

Abstract
Motivated by the novel paradigm developed by Van Roy and coauthors for reinforcement learning in arbitrary non-Markovian environments, we propose a related formulation and explicitly pin down the error caused by non-Markovianity of observations when the Q-learning algorithm is applied to this formulation. Based on this observation, we propose that the criterion for agent design should be to seek good approximations for certain conditional laws. Inspired by classical stochastic control, we show that our problem reduces to that of recursive computation of approximate sufficient statistics. This leads to an autoencoder-based scheme for agent design which is then numerically tested on partially observed reinforcement learning environments.

This is explicitly so e.g., when the model used is a discrete or finite dimensional caricature of a more complex problem, or because the convenient approximations imposed on the dynamics for analytic ease are only approximate (such as exponentiality of interarrival times in controlled queues). In this work, we take a somewhat different view of this problem. We take the observation process

Our main contributions are the following.

1.

By explicitly pinning down the error caused by potential non-Markovianity of the observation process in the application of the Q-learning algorithm
[
4
]
, we propose an alternative performance criterion for agent design.

2.

We argue that this reduces the problem to seeking good approximations for certain conditional laws.

3.

We justify achieving such approximation by ‘recursively computable approximate sufficient statistics’ (RCASS), drawing upon the classical notion of Partially Observed Markov Decision Processes (POMDPs) in stochastic control.

The notion of RCASS is neither very novel nor surprising. In fact, it is essentially the same as the notion of an ‘approximate information state’ proposed and analyzed in the excellent recent article
[
5
]
, which also includes a perspective on how such notions have evolved in related literature, both classical and contemporary (see section 2.5 and the appendices of
ibid.
). The difference is in the way we motivate it. We take Q-learning for discounted cost as a test case, a similar treatment should be possible in principle for other learning schemes as well. We show that if we use a surrogate state model as in (
1.1
) and treat it as Markovian (though it is not) for purposes of the Q-learning algorithm, the latter still converges a.s. to a certain limit that can be interpreted. Furthermore, we give an analysis of transient errors where the error due to non-Markovianity can be isolated clearly and should in fact be the basis for choosing the agent state dynamics (
1.1
), viz., with the objective of approximating certain conditional laws. This naturally leads to RCASS. Of course, we could target approximation of the relevant conditional
expectations
alone, in this case of the value function estimate and the reward, which would be closer to the viewpoint taken in
[
5
]
. We opt for approximation of conditional laws because of its kinship with the classical ‘separated control problem’ for POMDPs. This is the problem of controlling the completely observed Markov decision process of conditional laws of the state given past observations and controls (aka the ‘belief states’), given by the recursive nonlinear filter. This is dubbed ‘separated control’ because the estimation and control aspects get separately delineated. Thus we view the dynamics (
1.1
) as a finite state approximation to the nonlinear filter. This point will be discussed later in this work along with proposals for schemes to learn the agent dynamics. At a meta level, our attempt is to make a connection between reinforcement learning in general environments and classical stochastic control
[
6
]
. In the process, we also highlight a parallel with the classical ‘internal model principle’ of control theory that deserves to be better appreciated as a guiding principle.

In Section 2, we begin by recalling the Q-learning algorithm applied to the agent state described above. We show that this algorithm converges to a fixed point which can be used to approximate the true
Q
-values corresponding to the policy based on the entire history and make control decisions accordingly. We also present a finite-time analysis, where using a suitable ‘decomposition’ of the
Q
-learning iterate, we isolate different error terms along the lines of
[
7
]
, where all but the new term that captures the error due to non-Markovianity are inherited from
[
7
]
and,
(i)
are applicable to the controlled Markov case as well, and,
(ii)
are asymptotically negligible. The error due to non-Markovianity also vanishes asymptotically, but the concentration bounds, when available, are weaker, suggesting non-negligible finite time errors. We dub this difficulty in approximating the true
Q
-values and this additional error term as the ‘
the curse of non-Markovianity
’. Hence a reasonable target is to minimize them ‘to the extent possible’. This is precisely what gives us the criterion of well approximating conditional laws by a judicious choice of ‘approximate sufficient statistics’ or, to get closer to machine learning parlance, ‘dynamic features’.

Section 3 uses the foregoing to make a case for approximation by a simpler model using recursively computable approximate sufficient statistics, with some rigorous justification. This amounts to developing an approximate dynamic model for the controller. This idea is not new, see, e.g.,
[
8
]
, which seeks finite state machines as candidate controllers for POMDPs and establishes their near-optimality. In the latter context, these finite state controllers can be thought of as approximate controlled nonlinear filters, or approximate POMDPs. We point out these parallels.

In Section 4, we present a computation scheme inspired by the autoencoder model from
[
9
]
. Unlike autoencoders, where one tries to match the input with the output, our architecture tries to match the output with the next input (i.e., tries to predict the next observation). The agent state in this architecture is given by the output of the encoder. These agents states are passed to a Deep Q-Network (DQN)

10

which then learns the control policy. We call the agent trained in this manner as the
Non-Markovian Q-Agent

\displaystyle\Bigg|\sum_{s^{\prime}}q(s^{\prime}\mid S_{n},U_{n})\max_{a}Q^{*}(s^{\prime},a)

\displaystyle=\Bigg|\int p(d\tilde{o}|S_{n},U_{n})\max_{a}Q^{*}\left(f(S_{n},U_{n},\tilde{o}),a\right)

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;-\int p(d\tilde{o}|O^{n},U^{n})\max_{a}\widehat{Q}((X_{n},(\tilde{o},U_{n})),a)\Bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\Bigg|\int p(d\tilde{o}|S_{n},U_{n})\max_{a}Q^{*}\left(f(S_{n},U_{n},\tilde{o}),a\right)

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;-\int p(d\tilde{o}|O^{n},U^{n})\max_{a}Q^{*}\left(f(S_{n},U_{n},\tilde{o}),a\right)\Bigg|

\displaystyle\;\;+\Bigg|\int p(d\tilde{o}|O^{n},U^{n})\max_{a}Q^{*}\left(f(S_{n},U_{n},\tilde{o}),a\right)

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;-\int p(d\tilde{o}|O^{n},U^{n})\max_{a}\widehat{Q}((X_{n},(\tilde{o},U_{n})),a)\Bigg|

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leq}}\frac{1}{1-\gamma}d_{TV}(P(\cdot|S_{n},U_{n}),P(\cdot|O^{n},U^{n}))

\displaystyle\;\;+\sup_{m}\sup_{O^{m},U^{m}}\left|Q^{*}(S_{m},U_{m})-\widehat{Q}(X_{m},U_{m})\right|.

The first term in inequality
(a)
follows from the fact that
Q^{*}
and
\hat{Q}
always lies in
[0,1/(1-\gamma)]
.

This implies that for all
n
and sequences
O^{n},U^{n}
,

\displaystyle\left|Q^{*}(S_{n},U_{n})-\widehat{Q}(X_{n},U_{n})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon+\gamma\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{1-\gamma}+\sup_{m}\sup_{O^{m},U^{m}}\left|Q^{*}(S_{m},U_{m})-\widehat{Q}(X_{m},U_{m})\right|\right)

Finally,

\displaystyle\sup_{m}\sup_{O^{m},U^{m}}\left|Q^{*}(S_{m},U_{m})-\widehat{Q}(X_{m},U_{m})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{1-\gamma}+\gamma\sup_{m}\sup_{O^{m},U^{m}}\left|Q^{*}(S_{m},U_{m})-\widehat{Q}(X_{m},U_{m})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{(1-\gamma)^{2}},

which completes the proof for Theorem
3
.
∎

It is clear from this analysis that the criterion for agent design should be to well approximate certain conditional expectations given the entire history till
n
, by the corresponding conditional expectations given
S_{n},U_{n},
alone. Since we do not know the objects under the conditional expectation operator a priori, it makes sense to approximate the conditional laws themselves, viz., the conditional laws given the entire past by those given
S_{n},U_{n},
alone. This issue is further highlighted by the concentration phenomena we study next.

2.3
Finite-time Analysis

We now present a concentration inequality for the iterates in (
2.3
) and isolate the error due to non-Markovianity. Let
Z_{n}\coloneqq(S_{n},U_{n})
, where
Z_{n}\in\mathcal{Z}\coloneqq\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{U}
. Then

\displaystyle P(Z_{n+1}=(s^{\prime},u^{\prime})\mid Z_{n}=(s,u))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\psi((s^{\prime},u^{\prime})\mid(s,u))

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle q(s^{\prime}\mid s,u)\phi(u^{\prime}\mid s^{\prime}).

As defined before,
\tilde{\pi}(z)
is the stationary distribution for

\tilde{\pi}
is necessarily the stationary distribution for the transition probability kernel
\psi(\cdot\mid\cdot)

to be the solution of the Poisson equation:

V(q,z)=F(q,z)-\sum_{z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}}\tilde{\pi}(z^{\prime})F(q,z^{\prime})+\sum_{z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}}\psi(z^{\prime}\mid z)V(q,z^{\prime}),

for each
z\in\mathcal{Z}
. The Poisson equation specifies

uniquely only up to an additive constant. Fixing
V(q,z_{0})=0
for a fixed

and for all

gives us a unique solution to the set of Poisson equations given above. Henceforth
V
refers to this unique solution of the Poisson equation. Then define

\displaystyle\omega(Q_{n},O^{n},U^{n})

\displaystyle\coloneqq E[V(Q_{n},Z_{n+1})\mid O^{n},U^{n}]

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;-E[V(Q_{n},Z_{n+1})\mid S_{n},U_{n}].

Similar to
[
7
]

a(n)
required for the concentration bound. We assume that there exists

such that for all

a(n+1)\leq a(n),a(n)<1
and

, where

and

Define
\pi_{min}=\min_{s,u}\tilde{\pi}(s,u)
. For

\displaystyle b_{k}(n)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m=k}^{n}a(m),\ 0\leq k\leq n<\infty,

\displaystyle\beta_{k}(n)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\frac{1}{k^{d_{2}-d_{1}}n^{d_{1}}},&\text{if}\ d_{1}\leq d_{2}\\
\frac{1}{n^{d_{2}}},&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

\displaystyle\chi(n,m)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\prod_{k=m}^{n}(1-a(k)),&\text{if}\ n\geq m\\
1,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

\displaystyle\Delta_{k}(n)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m=k}^{n-1}\chi(n-1,m+1)a(m)\Big(\zeta(Q_{m},O^{m},U^{m})

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;+\ \omega(Q_{m},O^{m},U^{m})\Big),\ n>k.

We then have:

Theorem 4
.

Let
n_{0}\geq N
. Then there exist finite positive constants
c_{1}
,
c_{2}
, and
D
, depending on
\|Q_{N}\|
, such that for
\delta_{1}>0
and

\|Q_{n}-Q^{*}\|_{\infty}\leq e^{-(1-\alpha)b_{n_{0}}(n)}\|Q_{n_{0}}-Q^{*}\|_{\infty}+\ \frac{\delta_{1}+a(n_{0})c_{1}}{1-\alpha}+\|\Delta_{n_{0}}(n)\|_{\infty},

(2.7)

holds with probability exceeding

\displaystyle 1-2\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{u}\sum_{m=n_{0}+1}^{n}e^{-D\delta_{1}^{2}/\beta_{n_{0}}(m)},\ 0<\delta_{1}\leq C,

(2.8)

\displaystyle 1-2\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{u}\sum_{m=n_{0}+1}^{n}e^{-D\delta_{1}/\beta_{n_{0}}(m)},\ \delta_{1}>C,

(2.9)

where
C=e^{\left(2\left(1+\|Q_{N}\|_{\infty}+\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\right)+c_{2}\right)}
and

.

Proof Sketch.

The proof for Theorem
4
follows similarly to the proof of Corollary 2 from
[
7
]
. For this, we first rewrite (
2.4
) in the form of

Q_{n+1}=Q_{n}+a(n)(G(Q_{n},S_{n},U_{n})-Q_{n}+M_{n+1}+\zeta(Q_{n},O^{n},U^{n})),

where

and
\zeta(Q_{n},O^{n},U^{n})
is an additional term due to non-Markovianity. Note that

\|\sum_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\tilde{\pi}(z)(G(q,z)-G(q^{\prime},z))\|_{\infty}\leq\alpha\|z-z^{\prime}\|_{\infty},

where

(see

.

Now define

for

R_{n+1}=R_{n}+a(n)\left(\sum_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\tilde{\pi}(z)G(R_{n},z)-R_{n}\right),

where

The term

\displaystyle Q_{n+1}-R_{n+1}

\displaystyle=(1-a(n))(Q_{n}-R_{n})+a(n)(\zeta(Q_{n},Y_{n})+M_{n+1})

\displaystyle\;\;\;+a(n)\left(G(Q_{n},Z_{n})-\sum_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\tilde{\pi}(z)G(R_{n},z)\right)

\displaystyle=(1-a(n))(Q_{n}-R_{n})+a(n)(\zeta(Q_{n},Y_{n})+M_{n+1})

\displaystyle\;\;\;+a(n)\left(\sum_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\tilde{\pi}(z)(G(Q_{n},z)-G(R_{n},z))\right)

\displaystyle\;\;\;+a(n)\left(G(Q_{n},Z_{n})-\sum_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\tilde{\pi}(z)G(Q_{n},z)\right).

(2.10)

To simplify the last term in the above equation, we use

\displaystyle G(Q_{n},Z_{n})-\sum_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\tilde{\pi}(z)G(Q_{n},z)

\displaystyle=F(Q_{n},Z_{n})-\sum_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\tilde{\pi}(z)F(Q_{n},z)

\displaystyle Q_{n+1}-R_{n+1}=\sum_{k=n_{0}}^{n}\chi(n,k+1)a(k)M_{k+1}

Our objective is to use these observations to motivate a particular philosophy for agent design. 3
Recursively Computable Approximate Sufficient Statistics

In view of Theorem
3
, Theorem
4
and the discussion that follows, a legitimate criterion for agent design is that we need the conditional distribution of
(S_{n+1},O_{n+1})
given
(O^{n},U^{n})
to be well approximated by its conditional distribution given
(S_{n},U_{n})
alone. In other words, we need
S_{n}
to be an ‘approximate sufficient statistics’. We shall call a sequence
\{Y_{n}\}
taking values in a finite set
\mathcal{S}_{1}
to be a
Recursively Computable Approximate Sufficient Statistics
(RCASS) if it is of the form
Y_{n}=g_{1}(\Gamma_{n})
for some function
g_{1}:\mathcal{S}_{2}\mapsto\mathcal{S}_{1}
for some finite set
\mathcal{S}_{2}
, where
\{\Gamma_{n}\}\subset\mathcal{S}_{2}
is given by a recursion of the form

\Gamma_{n+1}=h_{1}(\Gamma_{n},U_{n},O_{n+1}),\ n\geq 0,

(3.1)

for some
h_{1}:\mathcal{S}_{2}\times\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{O}\mapsto\mathcal{S}_{2}
. Clearly,
\{S_{n}\}
above is a RCASS where
\mathcal{S}_{1}=\mathcal{S}_{2}=\mathcal{S}
and
g_{1}
is the identity map. That
\mathcal{S}_{1},\mathcal{S}_{2}
be finite is our choice. In any case, note that
Y_{n}
is a function of
(O^{n},U^{n})
and therefore any conditional probability
\tilde{p}(O_{n+1}=o\mid Y_{n},U_{n})
can also be written alternatively as
\breve{p}(O_{n+1}=o\mid O^{n},U^{n})
for a suitable
\breve{p}(\cdot\mid\cdot)
. With this backdrop, we have the following result.

Lemma 5
.

The transition probability function
p(\cdot\mid\cdot):\mathcal{O}^{\infty}\times\mathcal{U}^{\infty}\mapsto\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{O})
can be uniformly approximated by a suitable choice of
\mathcal{S}_{1}
and
\tilde{p}(\cdot\mid\cdot):\mathcal{S}_{1}\times\mathcal{U}\mapsto\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{O})
in the sense that the error
\|p(\cdot\mid\cdot)-\breve{p}(\cdot\mid\cdot)\|
can be made smaller than any prescribed
\epsilon>0
by a suitable choice of
\tilde{p}
, equivalently, of
\breve{p}
, which is related to
\tilde{p}
as described above.

Proof.

If we drop the requirement that
\breve{p}(i\mid\cdot)
be a probability and require only that it be a continuous function, then the result is immediate from the Stone-Weierstrass theorem for
S_{1}=S_{2}:=\mathcal{O}^{N}
and
Y_{n}=\Gamma_{n}:=[(O_{n},U_{n-1}),\cdots,(O_{n-N},U_{n-N-1})]
with
N
large enough. The resulting vector can then be projected to
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{S})\times(\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{U})^{N}
to get the desired claim.
∎

This justifies restricting our attention to RCASS. Since
\sigma(Y_{m},m\leq n)\subset\sigma(Z_{m},m\leq n)\ \forall n
, we might as well take
g_{1}
to be the identity map as above. This justifies our model for agent dynamics given by (
1.1
).
It is instructive here to draw a parallel with the theory of POMDPs in classical stochastic control
[
6
]
. To make the parallels apparent, we shall repeat the same notation. In a POMDP, one controls a (controlled) Markov chain
\{X_{n}\}
on a finite state space
\mathcal{S}_{3}
given a process of observations
\{O_{n}\}
taking values in another finite space, with a controlled transition kernel (say)
p^{\prime}(s^{\prime},o\mid s,u),s,s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S},
that satisfies
\forall n
,

P(X_{n+1}=s^{\prime},O_{n+1}=o\mid X^{n},U^{n},O^{n})=p^{\prime}(s^{\prime},o\mid S_{n},U_{n}).

(3.2)

The control
U_{n}
is allowed to depend only upon
O^{n},U^{n-1}
, and independent extraneous randomization. Letting
\pi_{n}(\cdot)
denote the conditional law of
S_{n}
given
(O^{n},U^{n})
, the process
\{\pi_{n}\}
of belief states is given recursively by the nonlinear filter

\pi_{n+1}(i)=\frac{\sum_{j}\pi_{n}(j)p^{\prime}(i,O_{n+1}\mid j,U_{n})}{\sum_{j,k}\pi_{n}(j)p^{\prime}(k,O_{n+1}\mid j,U_{n})},\ \forall n.

(3.3)

This is an easy consequence of the Bayes rule. Furthermore, the process
\{\pi_{n}\}
is a
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{S})
-valued controlled Markov chain. The equation (
3.3
) has the same form as (
1.1
), except that
\pi_{n}
resides in the probability simplex
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{S})
, which is not a finite set. Thus replacing it with (
1.1
) amounts to a finite state approximation to (
3.3
). A further and critical difference in our case is that (
3.2
) does not hold in our case. Therefore
\{\pi_{n}\}
is
not
a controlled Markov chain. This is the crux of the
curse of non-Markovianity
.

Note that
\{O_{n}\}
is trivially a POMDP if we view
X_{n}:=(O^{n},U^{n-1})
as a controlled Markov chain in state space
\mathcal{S}_{2}:=\mathcal{O}^{\infty}\times\mathcal{U}^{\infty}
, with control process
\{U_{n}\}
. The transition at time
n
given the control choice
U_{n}=u
causes the chain to move to the next state
(O^{n+1}=(O^{n},O_{n+1}=o),U^{n}=(U^{n-1},U_{n}=u))
with

\displaystyle P\left(O^{n+1}=(O^{n},u),U^{n}=(U^{n-1},u)|(O^{n},U^{n-1}),U_{n}=u\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p\left(\ o\ |O^{n},(U^{n-1},u)\right)

for
p(\cdot\ |\cdot,\cdot)
as in (
2.2
). The associated observation process
O_{n}=g^{\prime}(X_{n})
where

g^{\prime}:((o_{n},u_{n-1}),(o_{n-1},u_{n-2}),\cdots)\in(\mathcal{O}\times\mathcal{U})^{\infty}\mapsto o_{n}\in\mathcal{O}.

The agent design task is to develop an approximate
finite state
model of the original system as above, which in this case is the controlled nonlinear filter in view of the ‘separated control’ formalism. This is reminiscent of the
internal model principle
in adaptive control
[
15
]
(see
[
16
]
for a recent survey). An informal statement of this principle says: ‘a good controller incorporates a model of the dynamics that generate the signals which the control system is intended to track’. It should also be mentioned that a natural choice for this purpose that comes to mind is approximating
(O^{n},U^{n-1})
by a finite window
[O_{n-K},U_{n-K},\cdots,O_{n-1},U_{n-1},O_{n}]
. (See the proof of Lemma
5
above.) This can be viewed as a special case of the framework described above. This situation has been extensively analyzed in
[
17
]
and a Q-learning algorithm is proposed and analyzed in this framework therein. Another important work in this spirit is
[
5
]
, which postulates an
approximate information state
that corresponds to our RCASS, by stating a priori desirable properties thereof that facilitate approximate dynamic programming. In contrast, we are motivated by the error decomposition in the concentration bound and take a more statistical viewpoint of seeking approximate sufficient statistics to mimic certain conditional laws.
Whereas we justified the need to well approximate the conditional laws using agent state in terms of the transients of the Q-learning algorithm, we can also motivate it by looking at the asymptotic behavior as characterized in (
2
).

Our input to the learning scheme is a stationary process controlled through a randomized control policy that chooses at time
n
a control
U_{n}
in
\mathcal{U}
according to a probability distribution on
\mathcal{U}
that depends only on the current agent state
S_{n}
. As argued earlier, the agent state
S_{n}
will be a fixed function of the state
(O^{n},U^{n-1})
of the underlying
\mathcal{O}^{\infty}\times\mathcal{U}^{\infty}
-valued controlled Markov chain, therefore so will be the conditional law of
U_{n}
given
(O^{n},U^{n-1})
. Hence this is a stationary randomized policy for the underlying controlled Markov chain
(O^{n},U^{n-1}),n>-\infty
. This, however, decides only the sampling distribution of state-action pairs for the Q-learning algorithm. (Note that the learning scheme is ‘off-line’.) As shown in Theorem 1- Proposition 2, the ‘optimal choice’ of the algorithm obtained from the Q-values learnt, will be a fixed function of
S_{n}
at time
n
and again by the above argument, it corresponds to a stationary policy. Since the state and action spaces of this
\mathcal{O}^{\infty}\times\mathcal{U}^{\infty}
-valued chain are compact and the transition mechanism is continuous in state and action variables, standard dynamic programming arguments guarantee the sufficiency of stationary policies. But since we are working with an approximate sufficient statistics, the best one can hope for is a near-optimal stationary or stationary randomized policy. Since the agent state and its dynamics are only approximations of the nonlinear filter, one cannot in general expect this class to contain an optimal policy for the original problem, but in view of the interpretation given in Proposition 2, it will be near-optimal if the state aggregation indicated in Proposition 2 is fine enough. Thus there will be a trade-off between the complexity of the agent dynamics which we design, and the optimality gap.

4
Autoencoder-based Computation Scheme

We now propose a specific architecture inspired by the autoencoder model from
[
9
]
. However, unlike their model, where one tries to match the output with the input, we match the output with the
next
input, i.e., our error is not to be viewed as a decoding error, but as a prediction error. Our architecture has a ‘state’ neural network that takes
S_{n-1},U_{n-1},O_{n}
as input and gives the next agent state
S_{n}
as output. This feeds into the ‘observation’ neural network that takes this
S_{n}
along with
U_{n}
as input and outputs a prediction
\widehat{O}_{n+1}
of the next observation
O_{n+1}
as the output (see Figure
1
). The neural networks are trained by comparing
O_{n+1},\widehat{O}_{n+1}
. The criterion for this comparison can be chosen depending on the problem specifics. For example, it could be one of the classical loss functions penalizing the difference between
O_{n+1}
and
\widehat{O}_{n+1}
, or it may compare their distributions or conditional distributions (see, e.g.,
[
18
]
). See section 3.1 of
[
5
]
for some popular metrics for comparing probability measures. There are also other architectures that have been proposed with alternative underlying philosophies, e.g.,
[
19
]
.

dimensions: horizontal displacement and velocity, and angular displacement and velocity. The agent is just able to observe the displacements (horizontal and angular). The agent’s action space consists of two actions: applying force on left or right. The episode runs for a maximum of 200 timesteps or stops when either of the horizontal or angular displacement constraints are violated and the agent receives reward
1
at each timestep until the episode ends. The environment is said to be solved if the agent achieves a reward of
200
(the maximum reward).

2.

Mountain Car:
This environment simulates an under-powered car which must drive up a steep hill. The car is situated in a valley and must learn to drive up the opposite hill before it can make it to the top of the desired hill. The state space consists of velocity and position, and in our partially observed version, the agent can just observe the position. The action space consists of 3 actions: force towards left or right or no force. We use a common variation for the reward function where the reward depends on the distance of the car from the hill, increasing as the car reaches close to the top of the hill, and also on the absolute velocity of the car.

3.

Non-Markovian Random Walk:
This environment simulates a controlled random walk where the next position depends not only on the current position and action but also on the past positions. Here the position at each time
n
is the observation
O_{n}
and the actions
U_{n}
can be in the set
\{-5,-4,\ldots,4,5\}
. Let
sum_{n}
denote the sum of past positions, i.e.,
sum_{n}=\sum_{m=0}^{n}O_{m}
. Then the next observation is given by

O_{n+1}=O_{n}+(sum_{n}\%10)-5+U_{n},

where
\%
denotes the modulus operator. The agent is initialized at a random position between
-100
and
100
and receives high positive reward of 100 on reaching
0
, and slight negative reward of
-|O_{n}|/100
otherwise. The episode ends either when the agent reaches
0
or after 150 timesteps, if the agent fails to reach
0
before then.

Figure

compares the performance of our NMQ agent with a DQN agent where the observations are directly given as inputs to the Deep Q-Network. For all experiments, the plots are for a single run of the environment. We plot the moving average of the episodic rewards and the light dotted line represents the reward in each episode. Results show that the NMQ agent is able to ‘learn’ and solve the environments, while it seems that the DQN fails to learn anything significant. These are plots for a single run and the fluctuations in episodic rewards are due to the partially-observed setting
[
20
]
.

5
Future Directions

5.1
The assumption of stationarity

One of the limitations of the above analysis has been the assumption of stationarity. The key consequence of it for us was to justify the time-homogeneous transition probabilities
q(\cdot\mid\cdot,\cdot)
. As already observed, asymptotic stationarity suffices. In the absence of this assumption, the aforementioned time-homogeneity itself will be an
assumption
. The justification for using the Poisson equation as stated in our analysis is also no longer valid without this additional assumption. In general, analyzing reinforcement learning in non-stationary environments is a major open problem, except perhaps when the environment is slowly varying and therefore quasi-stationary, whence it becomes a tracking issue. See
[
22
]
for some recent work in this direction and
[
23
]
for analysis of nonstationary stochastic control problems.

5.2
RKHS-based Computation scheme

Another method for approximating agent dynamics given by
P(S_{n}\mid O^{n})
, is to use Hilbert space embeddings of conditional distributions
[
24
,
25
,
26
]
. This involves representing probability distributions by elements in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS). First, we give a brief overview of how distributions can be embedded into an RKHS. An RKHS
\mathcal{F}
with kernel
k
on
\mathcal{X}
is a Hilbert space of functions
f:\mathcal{X}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
with inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{F}}
that satisfies the property
\langle f(\cdot),k(x,\cdot)\rangle_{\mathcal{F}}=f(x)
. Examples of such kernel functions include polynomial kernels and Gaussian kernels. These kernels are associated with feature maps
\phi:\mathcal{X}\mapsto\mathcal{F}
such that
k(x,x^{\prime})=\langle\phi(x),\phi(x^{\prime})\rangle_{\mathcal{F}}
. Once we have a fixed feature map, for distribution
P(X)
over
\mathcal{X}
, we can define the embedding “mean map"
\mu_{X}\coloneqq E_{X}[\phi(X)]
. Similarly, we can define the empirical mean map
\hat{\mu}_{X}
which converges with high probability to
\mu_{X}
in the RKHS norm at a rate of
\mathcal{O}_{p}(1/\sqrt{m})
, where
m
denotes the number of samples. This mean map
\mu_{X}
can be used to represent a distribution and under certain assumptions on the kernel
k
, we can guarantee that distinct distributions map to distinct mean maps in RKHS.

Now, to represent conditional distributions as embeddings, we use the cross-covariance operator
C_{XY}\coloneqq E_{XY}[\phi(X)\otimes\varphi(Y)]-\mu_{X}\otimes\mu_{Y},

where
\otimes
denotes the tensor product and
\phi(\cdot)
and
\varphi(\cdot)
are feature maps on
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
, respectively. Then the conditional map operator
\mathcal{U}_{Y\mid X}
is defined as
\mathcal{U}_{Y\mid X}=C_{YX}C_{XX}^{-1}
and the conditional mapping given
X=x
is given by
\mu_{Y\mid x}=\mathcal{U}_{Y\mid X}\phi(x)
. The cross covariance operators can be empirically estimated recursively.

We can model our problem of approximating
P(S_{n}\mid H_{n})
as a hidden Markov model, where
\{S_{n}\}
are modeled as states from a Markov chain (not observed) and the observations
O_{n}
satisfy
P(O_{n}\in\mathcal{A}\mid O^{n-1},S^{n})=P(O_{n}\in\mathcal{A}\mid S_{n})
. Under this model, the conditional distribution of
(S_{n+1},O_{n+1})
given
(O^{n},U^{n})
would be well approximated by its conditional distribution given
(S_{n},U_{n})
.
[
24
]
gives an algorithm for approximating
\mu_{S_{n+1}\mid o^{n+1}}
in a recursive manner, and then inferring
s_{n+1}
from this conditional distribution. They first show that

\mu_{S_{n+1}\mid s_{n},o_{n+1}}=\mathcal{T}_{1}\varphi(s_{n})+\mathcal{T}_{2}\phi(o_{n+1}),

where
\mathcal{T}_{1}
and
\mathcal{T}_{2}
are conditional map operators. Then our required distribution can be approximated as follows

\mu_{S_{n+1}\mid o^{n+1}}\approx\mathcal{T}_{1}\mu_{S_{n}\mid o^{n}}+\mathcal{T}_{2}\phi(o_{n+1}).

This decomposes the update into two simple operations: the first term here propagates from time
n
to
n+1
and the second term accounts for the current observation. Estimates of these operators (
\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{1}
and
\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{2}
) can be learned empirically
[
24
, Alg. 1]
and can then be used for inference
[
24
, Alg. 2]
.

Appendix A
A Bound on
\Delta(n)

Motivated by
[
27
]
, we give a bound on
\Delta(n)
. We need certain assumptions on the observation process and the state mapping for the bound to hold. We first define a matrix that measures the strength of dependence between the random variables. Define
W_{1}=(O^{1},U^{1})
and
W_{m}=(O_{m},U_{m})
for all
m>1
. As defined before,
Z_{m}=(S_{m},U_{m})
and
Z_{m}=g(W_{1},\ldots,W_{m})
. Fix
n\geq 1
. Let
p^{w_{1},\ldots,w_{i}}(\cdot)
denote the conditional probability
P(\cdot\mid W_{1}=w_{1},\ldots,W_{i}=w_{i})
. Then we have the following four assumptions:

1.

Define the upper triangular matrix
\Phi
where
\Phi_{i,i}=1
for
i\leq n
,
\Phi_{j,i}=0
for
1\leq i<j\leq n
and, for
1\leq i<j\leq n,

\displaystyle\Phi_{i,j}=\sup_{w_{1},\ldots,w_{i},w_{i}^{\prime}}d_{TV}\Big(p^{w_{1},\ldots,w_{i-1},w_{i}}

\displaystyle(Z_{j},O_{j+1}),

\displaystyle p^{w_{1},\ldots,w_{i-1},w^{\prime}_{i}}(Z_{j},O_{j+1})\Big),

where
d_{TV}(\cdot,\cdot)
denotes the total variation distance between probability distributions. We assume that
\|\Phi\|_{2}\leq d_{4}
where
d_{4}>0
. Here
\|\Phi\|_{2}
denotes the matrix norm induced by the euclidean norm.

2.

Similarly, define the upper triangular matrix
\Psi
where
\Psi_{j,i}=0
for
1\leq i<j\leq n
and

\displaystyle\Psi_{i,j}=\sup_{w_{1},\ldots,w_{i},w_{i}^{\prime}}d_{TV}\Big(p^{w_{1},\ldots,w_{i-1},w_{i}}

\displaystyle(Z_{j},Z_{j+1}),

\displaystyle p^{w_{1},\ldots,w_{i-1},w^{\prime}_{i}}(Z_{j},Z_{j+1})\Big),

for
1\leq i\leq j\leq n
. We again assume that
\|\Psi\|_{2}\leq d_{4}
.

While these exact assumptions have been chosen to suit the proof of Lemma
6
, the intuition behind them is that these assumptions restrict the dependence in the observation process and between observations and mapped states. Before stating the lemma, we give a hidden Markov model-based example adapted from
[
27
, Example 2.15]
, which justifies our assumptions. Let
\{\tilde{O}_{j}\}
be a Markov chain and let
\{O_{j}\}
be random variables such that

P(O_{n}\in\mathcal{A}\mid O_{1},\ldots,O_{n-1},\tilde{O}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{O}_{n})=P(O_{n}\in\mathcal{A}\mid\tilde{O}_{n}).

Then the value
\Phi_{i,j}
can be bounded by the sum of

\Phi^{\prime}_{i,j}\coloneqq\sup_{w_{1},\ldots,w_{i},w_{i}^{\prime}}d_{TV}\left(p^{w_{1},\ldots,w_{i-1},w_{i}}(O_{j+1}),p^{w_{1},\ldots,w_{i-1},w^{\prime}_{i}}(O_{j+1})\right),

and

\Phi^{\prime\prime}_{i,j}\coloneqq\sup_{w_{1},\ldots,w_{i},w_{i}^{\prime},o_{j+1}}d_{TV}\left(p^{w_{1},\ldots,w_{i-1},w_{i},o_{j+1}}(Z_{j}),p^{w_{1},\ldots,w_{i-1},w^{\prime}_{i},o_{j+1}}(Z_{j})\right).

Then the norm of the matrix
\Phi^{\prime}
formed by the first term above is bounded by a constant proportional to the mixing time of the underlying Markov chain
[
27
, Corollary 2.16]
. The norm of the matrix
\Phi^{\prime\prime}
can be bounded by making suitable assumptions on the state mapping.
\|\Psi\|
can be bounded similarly.

Remark 4
.

We have made the assumption that
\|\Phi\|_{2}\leq d_{4}
and
\|\Psi\|_{2}\leq d_{4}
to improve readability. They can be generalized to
\|\Phi\|_{2}\leq d_{4}n^{d_{5}}
(and similarly for
\Psi
), where
2d_{5}<2d_{2}-1
. This would give
n^{1-2d_{2}+2d_{5}}
(instead of
n^{1-2d_{2}}
) in the denominator of the bound in Lemma
6
.

Lemma 6
.

There exists constant
c_{7}>0
such that for
n\geq 1
,

P(\|\Delta(n)\|_{\infty}\geq\delta)\leq 2\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{u}\exp\left(\frac{-c_{7}\delta^{2}}{n^{1-2d_{2}}}\right).

Proof.

For a fixed
s,u
, we know that

\displaystyle\Delta^{s,u}(n)=\sum_{m=1}^{n-1}\chi(n-1,m+1)a(m)\times

\displaystyle\Bigg(I\{S_{m}=s,U_{m}=u\}\bigg(E[r(S_{m},U_{m},O_{m+1})\mid O^{m},U^{m}]

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;-E[r(S_{m},U_{m},O_{m+1})\mid S_{m},U_{m}]\bigg)

\displaystyle+\gamma I\{S_{m}=s,U_{m}=u\}\bigg(E[\max_{a}Q_{m}(S_{m+1},a)\mid O^{m},U^{m}]

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;-E[\max_{a}Q_{m}(S_{m+1},a)\mid S_{m},U_{m}]\bigg)

\displaystyle+\bigg(E[V^{s,u}(Q_{m},Z_{m+1})\mid O^{m},U^{m}]-E[V^{s,u}(Q_{m},Z_{m+1})\mid S_{m},U_{m}]\bigg)\Bigg).

For ease of notation, we omit
s,u
from the left-hand side and define

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}(W_{1},\ldots,W_{n})=\sum_{m=1}^{n-1}\chi(n-1,m+1)a(m)B_{m}(W_{1},\ldots,W_{m}),

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}(W_{1},\ldots,W_{n})=\sum_{m=1}^{n-1}\chi(n-1,m+1)a(m)C_{m}(S_{m},U_{m}),

where

\displaystyle B_{m}(W_{1},\ldots,W_{m})

\displaystyle=I\{S_{m}=s,U_{m}=u\}E[r(S_{m},U_{m},O_{m+1})\mid O^{m},U^{m}]

\displaystyle\;\;\;+\gamma I\{S_{m}=s,U_{m}=u\}E[\max_{a}Q_{m}(S_{m+1},a)\mid O^{m},U^{m}]

\displaystyle\;\;\;+E[V^{s,u}(Q_{m},Z_{m+1})\mid O^{m},U^{m}]

and

\displaystyle C_{m}(S_{m},U_{m})

\displaystyle=I\{S_{m}=s,U_{m}=u\}E[r(S_{m},U_{m},O_{m+1})\mid S_{m},U_{m}]

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\gamma I\{S_{m}=s,U_{m}=u\}E[\max_{a}Q_{m}(S_{m+1},a)\mid S_{m},U_{m}]

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle E[V^{s,u}(Q_{m},Z_{m+1})\mid S_{m},U_{m}].

We first give a bound on
P(|\mathbb{C}(W)-E[\mathbb{C}(W)]|\geq\delta)
. Note that
C_{m}(S_{m},U_{m})
is bounded by
C_{max}\coloneqq(\frac{1}{1-\gamma}+V_{max})
for all
m
, where
V_{max}
is an upper bound on the term
V^{s,u}(\cdot,\cdot)
for all
s,u
. One such bound is derived in
[
7
]
. Define
\mathcal{F}_{i}=\sigma(W_{1},\ldots,W_{i})
for
i\leq n
, and write
\mathbb{C}(W)-E[\mathbb{C}(W)]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathfrak{C}_{i}(W)
with
\mathfrak{C}_{i}(W)=E[\mathbb{C}(W)\mid\mathcal{F}_{i}]-E[\mathbb{C}(W)\mid\mathcal{F}_{i-1}]
. Then

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}_{i}(W)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{a}E[\mathbb{C}(W)\mid W_{1},\ldots,W_{i-1},W_{i}=a]

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\;\;-\inf_{b}E[\mathbb{C}(W)\mid W_{1},\ldots,W_{i-1},W_{i}=b].

Let
\mathfrak{C}_{i,max}(W)
and
\mathfrak{C}_{i,min}(W)
denote the supremum and the infimum terms on the right, respectively. Also, assume that these values are achieved at
a
and
b
respectively. Then

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}_{i,max}(W)-\mathfrak{C}_{i,min}(W)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=i}^{n-1}\chi(n-1,j+1)a(j)\Big(E[C_{j}(S_{j},U_{j})\mid W_{1},\ldots,W_{i-1},W_{i}=a]

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;-E[C_{j}(S_{j},U_{j})\mid W_{1},\ldots,W_{i-1},W_{i}=b]\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=i}^{n-1}\chi(n-1,j+1)a(j)C_{max}2\Psi_{i,j}

\displaystyle\leq 2C_{max}\sum_{j=i}^{n-1}\Psi_{i,j}\kappa_{j},

where
\kappa_{j}=\chi(n-1,j+1)a(j)
.
Using the Azuma-Hoeffding inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle P(|\mathbb{C}(W)-E[\mathbb{C}(W)]|\geq\delta)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(\frac{-2\delta^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\sum_{j=i}^{n}2C_{max}\Psi_{i,j}\kappa_{j}\right)^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\frac{-2\delta^{2}}{4C_{max}^{2}\|\Psi\kappa\|^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(\frac{-2\delta^{2}}{4C_{max}^{2}\|\Psi\|^{2}\|\kappa\|^{2}}\right).

Using the assumption
\frac{d_{1}}{n}\leq a(n)\leq d_{3}\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)^{d_{2}},

\displaystyle\chi(n-1,j+1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{\ell=j+1}^{n-1}(1-a(\ell))\leq\exp\left(-\sum_{\ell=j+1}^{n-1}a(\ell)\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\sum_{\ell=j+1}^{n-1}\frac{d_{1}}{\ell}\right)\leq\left(\frac{j+1}{n-1}\right)^{d_{1}}.

Then

\chi(n-1,j+1)a(j)\leq d_{3}\left(\frac{1}{j}\right)^{d_{2}}\left(\frac{j+1}{n-1}\right)^{d_{1}}\leq\frac{d_{3}2^{d_{1}}j^{d_{1}-d_{2}}}{(n-1)^{d_{1}}},

and finally

\|\kappa\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{d_{3}^{2}4^{d_{1}}}{(n-1)^{2d_{1}}}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}j^{2d_{1}-2d_{2}}\leq c_{4}\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)^{2d_{2}-1},

for some constant
c_{4}
(assuming
n>1
). This implies that

P(|\mathbb{C}(W)-E[\mathbb{C}(W)]|\geq\delta)\leq\exp\left(\frac{-c_{5}\delta^{2}}{n^{1-2d_{2}}}\right),

(A.1)

for some constant
c_{5}
.

We now give a bound on
P(|\mathbb{B}(W)-E[\mathbb{B}(W)]|\geq\delta)
.
As before,
\mathbb{B}(W)-E[\mathbb{B}(W)]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathfrak{B}_{i}(W)
where

\mathfrak{B}_{i}(W)=E[\mathbb{B}(W)\mid\mathcal{F}_{i}]-E[\mathbb{B}(W)\mid\mathcal{F}_{i-1}].

Then

\displaystyle\mathfrak{B}_{i}(W)

\displaystyle=E[\mathbb{B}(W)\mid\mathcal{F}_{i}]-E[\mathbb{B}(W)\mid\mathcal{F}_{i-1}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\chi(n-1,j+1)a(j)\bigg(E[B_{j}(W_{1},\ldots,W_{j})\mid\mathcal{F}_{i}]

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;-E[B_{j}(W_{1},\ldots,W_{j})\mid\mathcal{F}_{i-1}]\bigg)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=i}^{n-1}\chi(n-1,j+1)a(j)\times

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\Bigg(E\bigg[V^{s,u}(Q_{j},Z_{j+1})+I\{S_{j}=s,U_{j}=u\}r(S_{j},U_{j},O_{j+1})

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;+\gamma I\{S_{j}=s,U_{j}=u\}\max_{a}Q_{j}(S_{j+1},a)\mathrel{\bigg|}\mathcal{F}_{i}\bigg]

\displaystyle\;\;-E\bigg[V^{s,u}(Q_{j},Z_{j+1})+I\{S_{j}=s,U_{j}=u\}r(S_{j},U_{j},O_{j+1})

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;+\gamma I\{S_{j}=s,U_{j}=u\}\max_{a}Q_{j}(S_{j+1},a)\mathrel{\bigg|}\mathcal{F}_{i-1}\bigg]\Bigg).

Here the last equality follows from the law of total expectation. Define

\displaystyle\mathfrak{B}_{i,max}(W)=\sup_{a}\sum_{j=i}^{n-1}\chi(n-1,j+1)a(j)\times

\displaystyle E\Bigg[V^{s,u}(Q_{j},Z_{j+1})+I\{S_{j}=s,U_{j}=u\}r(S_{j},U_{j},O_{j+1})

\displaystyle+I\{S_{j}=s,U_{j}=u\}\gamma\max_{a}Q_{j}(S_{j+1},a)\mid W_{1},\ldots,W_{i-1},W_{i}=a\Bigg],

and

\displaystyle\mathfrak{B}_{i,min}(W)=\inf_{b}\sum_{j=i}^{n-1}\chi(n-1,j+1)a(j)\times

\displaystyle E\Bigg[V^{s,u}(Q_{j},Z_{j+1})+I\{S_{j}=s,U_{j}=u\}r(S_{j},U_{j},O_{j+1})

\displaystyle+I\{S_{j}=s,U_{j}=u\}\gamma\max_{a}Q_{j}(S_{j+1},a)\mid W_{1},\ldots,W_{i-1},W_{i}=b\Bigg].

Note that
r(S_{j},U_{j},O_{j+1})\leq 1
and
\gamma Q_{j}(S_{j+1},a)\leq\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}
for all
1\leq j\leq n-1
and
a\in\mathcal{U}
. Then using the definition of
\Phi
and
\Psi
, we have

\displaystyle\mathfrak{B}_{i}(W)\leq\mathfrak{B}_{i,max}(W)-\mathfrak{B}_{i,min}(W)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=i}^{n-1}\chi(n-1,j+1)a(j)\left(2\Phi_{i,j}\kappa_{j}+\left(\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}+V_{max}\right)2\Psi_{i,j}\kappa_{j}\right).

Applying Azuma-Hoeffding inequality again, we have as before

P(|\mathbb{B}(W)-E[\mathbb{B}(W)]|\geq\delta)\leq\exp\left(\frac{-c_{6}\delta^{2}}{n^{1-2d_{2}}}\right),

(A.2)

where
c_{6}
is some constant. Note that
E[\mathbb{B}(W)]=E[\mathbb{C}(W)]
. Then defining
c_{7}\coloneqq 1/4\min\{c_{5},c_{6}\}
, applying union bound on (
A.1
) and (
A.2
), and summing over all
s\in\mathcal{S},u\in\mathcal{U}
, we get

P(\|\Delta(n)\|_{\infty}\geq\delta)\leq 2\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{u}\exp\left(\frac{-c_{7}\delta^{2}}{n^{1-2d_{2}}}\right).

∎
