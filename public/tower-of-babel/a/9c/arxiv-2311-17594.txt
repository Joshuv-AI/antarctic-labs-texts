Scale Invariant Correspondence Analysis
Choulakian V, Université de Moncton, Canada, vartan.choulakian@umoncton.ca

Abstract
Résumé Correspondence analysis is a dimension reduction method for visualization of nonnegative data sets, in particular contingency tables ; but it depends on the marginals of the data set. Two transformations of the data have been proposed to render correspondence analysis row and column scales invariant : These two kinds of transformations change the initial form of the data set into a bistochastic form. The power transorfmation applied by Greenacre (2010) has one positive parameter. While the transormation applied by Mosteller (1968) and Goodman (1996) has (I+J) positive parameters, where the raw data is row and column scaled by the Sinkhorn (RAS or ipf) algorithm to render it bistochastic. Goodman (1996) named correspondence analsis of a bistochastic matrix marginal-free correspondence analysis. We discuss these two transformations, and further generalize Mosteller-Goodman approach. Key words : Greenacre’s Theorem ; Goodman’s marginal-free correspondence analysis ; Sinkhorn (RAS-ipf) algorithm ; bistochastic blocks diagonal matrix ; log-linear association. AMS 2010 subject classifications : 62H25, 62H30

Introduction

Correspondence analysis (CA) and logratio analysis (LRA) are two popular
methods for the analysis-visualization of a two-way contingency table or a
compositional data set
\mathbf{N}=(n_{ij})

of size
I\times J
for

i=1,...,I
and
j=1,...,J.
LRA is considered an ideal desirable method for
analysis-visualization of
N
, because it is row and column scales
invariant, see Lemma 1 ; but it can be applied only to positive data

n_{ij}>0.
While CA can be applied to nonnegative data
n_{ij}\geq 0
, but it
is NOT row and column scale invariant because it is dependent on its row and
column marginals. This paper presents two transformations of data that render
CA row and column scales invariant, similar to LRA. These two transformations
are proposed by Mosteller (1968) and Goodman (1996, equation (40)), and, by
Greenacre (2010, result 1).

Goodman (1996, equation (46)) in his seminal paper outlined marginal-free
correspondence analysis (mfCA) without details ; where his principal aim was to
reconcile Pearson correlation measure on which CA is based with Yule’s
association measure on which LRA is based in the analysis of contingency
tables. Choulakian (2022) showed that Goodman’s mfCA is a particular case of
CA with prespecified marginals studied by Benzécri and his students in the
early 1980’s. Goodman’s aim of reconciliation is perfectly attained by
Greenacre’s Theorem, see section 3 below, which concerns the convergence of CA
of
\mathbf{N}^{(\alpha)}=(n_{ij}^{\alpha})
for
\alpha\in(0,1]
as

\alpha\rightarrow 0
to uniformly weighted LRA of
\mathbf{N}
when
n_{ij}>0
; for further details see Greenacre (2009, 2010 Result 1, 2022) and
Choulakian (2023).

The aim of this paper is to delve deeper from applied point of view into
Goodman (1996, equation (46))’s statement that mfCA ” can be
used as a replacement for the usual CA model (or canonical correlation model)
when marginal-free methods are desired” ; here, marginal-free
means row and column scales invariant. This will be done within the framework
of bistochastic (row and column stochastic) matrices that we introduce next.

1.1
Bistochastic matrix

In this paper, the notion of bistochastic (or doubly stochastic) matrix plays
an essential role. A nonnegative matrix
\mathbf{D=}(d_{ij})
for
i=1,...,I

and
j=1,...,J,
is named doubly stochastic (or bistochastic) if the following
two conditions are satisfied :
\frac{1}{I}\sum_{i}d_{ij}=\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j}d_{ij}=1
for
i=1,...,I
and
j=1,...,J;
see among others Loukaki
(2022). It follows that
\sum_{i,j}d_{ij}=IJ.
So
\mathbf{Q=D/(}IJ\mathbf{)}
is a probability measure with uniform marginals ; that is,

\frac{1}{J}=q_{+j}=\sum_{i}q_{ij}
and
\frac{1}{I}=q_{i+}=\sum_{j}q_{ij};

Mosteller (1968) and Goodman (1996, section 6, equations (39) and (40)) named

\mathbf{Q}
”the standardized
distribution”. So the concepts of doubly stochastic matrix

\mathbf{D}
and and its associated probability measure
\mathbf{Q=D/(}IJ\mathbf{)}
with uniform marginal weights are equivalent for nonnegative
data sets.

According to Goodman (1996, section 6), the first step for mfCA is to
transform the contingency table
\mathbf{N=}(n_{ij})
into a bistochastic
matrix
D
by row and column scalings, where
\mathbf{D}=(d_{ij}=a_{i}n_{ij}b_{j})
,
a_{i}>0
and
b_{j}>0
for
i=1,...,I
and
j=1,...,J.

Scaling a matrix consists of pre- and post-multiplying the original matrix

\mathbf{N=}(n_{ij})
by two diagonal matrices
Diag(a_{i})
and
Diag(b_{j})
. The computational tool to achieve this will be the Sinkhorn algorithm,
also known as the RAS method or the iterative proportional fitting (ipf)
procedure ; for a comprehensive survey see Idel (2016)
.
We note that
often bistochasticity of a matrix is used for square matrices, see for
instance, Le Gorrec et al. (2022).

2.1
Association indices

The CA association index of a probability table
P
is

\Delta_{ij}(\mathbf{P})=\frac{p_{ij}}{p_{i+}p_{+j}}-1,

(1)

where
\frac{p_{ij}}{p_{i+}p_{+j}}
is named the density function of
p_{ij}

with respect to the product measure
p_{i+}p_{+j}.

Assuming
p_{ij}>0,
we define the LRA association index (named log-linear
association index by Goodman (1996)) with uniform weights to be

\lambda(p_{ij},w_{i}^{R}=1/I,w_{j}^{C}=1/J)=\log(p_{ij})+\frac{1}{IJ}\sum_{i,j}\log(p_{ij})-(\frac{1}{I}\sum_{i}\log(p_{ij})+\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j}\log(p_{ij})).

(2)

Equation (2) appears also in compositional data analysis, see Aitchison (1986).

What’s the difference between the CA and LRA association indices (1) and (2) ?
The CA index is marginal-scale dependent, while the LRA index is scale
invariant, that we describe. Let
\tau_{ij}(p_{ij})
represent an interaction
(association) index, such as
\Delta_{ij}(\mathbf{P})
or
\lambda(p_{ij},w_{i}^{R}=1/I,w_{j}^{C}=1/J)
described in equations (1) and (2) respectively.

Following Yule (1912) and Goodman (1996), we state the following

Definition 1
: An interaction index
\tau_{ij}(p_{ij}=\frac{n_{ij}}{\sum_{i,j}n_{ij}})
is scale invariant if
\tau_{ij}(p_{ij})=\tau_{ij}(q_{ij}=\frac{a_{i}n_{ij}b_{j}}{\sum_{i,j}a_{i}n_{ij}b_{j}})
for
arbitrary positive scales
a_{i}>0
and
b_{j}>0
.

It is evident that the CA association index
\Delta_{ij}(\mathbf{P})
in (1)
is not scale invariant, because it depends on the data dependent marginal
weights
(p_{i+},p_{+j}).

Lemma 1
: The LRA association index (3) with prespecified weights
(
w_{i}^{R},w_{j}^{C})

\lambda(p_{ij},w_{i}^{R},w_{j}^{C})=\log(p_{ij})+\sum_{i,j}w_{i}^{R}w_{j}^{C}\log(p_{ij})-(\sum_{i}w_{i}^{R}\log(p_{ij})+\sum_{j}w_{j}^{C}\log(p_{ij}))

(3)

is scale invariant. In particular the LRA association index (2) with uniform
weights is scale invariant.

For a proof see Choulakian et al. (2023).

Greenacre’s Theorem

Greenacre’s Theorem relates both CA and LRA association indices in a very
simple way (that is equations
(1)=(2)
indirectly) : Let
\mathbf{P}^{(\alpha)}=(p_{ij}^{(\alpha)}=\frac{n_{ij}^{\alpha}}{\sum_{i,j}n_{ij}^{\alpha}})
for
i=1,...,I
and
j=1,...,J\
be the correspondence table of
the simple power transformed data,
p_{i+}^{(\alpha)}=\sum_{j}p_{ij}^{(\alpha)}
and
p_{+j}^{(\alpha)}=\sum_{i}p_{ij}^{(\alpha)}
for
\alpha>0.

We consider the CA association index

\Delta_{ij}(\mathbf{P}^{(\alpha)})=\frac{p_{ij}^{(\alpha)}-p_{i+}^{(\alpha)}p_{+j}^{(\alpha)}}{p_{i+}^{(\alpha)}p_{+j}^{(\alpha)}}.

(7)

Then we have, see Choulakian (2023) and by Lemma 1,

Greenacre’s Theorem
(Greenacre (2010, Result 1)

Under the assumption
n_{ij}>0,
for
\alpha\rightarrow 0
and
\alpha>0

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\rightarrow 0}\frac{\Delta_{ij}(\mathbf{P}^{(\alpha)})}{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\alpha\rightarrow 0}(\frac{p_{ij}^{(\alpha)}}{p_{i+}^{(\alpha)}p_{+j}^{(\alpha)}}-1)/\alpha

\displaystyle=\lambda(p_{ij},w_{i}^{R}=1/I,w_{j}^{C}=1/J)

\displaystyle=\lambda(q_{ij}=a_{i}p_{ij}b_{j},w_{i}^{R}=1/I,w_{j}^{C}=1/J)\text{ for
}a_{i},b_{j}>0

(8)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\alpha\rightarrow 0}\frac{\Delta_{ij}(\mathbf{Q}^{(\alpha)})}{\alpha}.

Furthermore,

\lim_{\alpha\rightarrow 0}p_{i+}^{(\alpha)}=\lim_{\alpha\rightarrow 0}q_{i+}^{(\alpha)}=1/I\text{\ \ for }i=1,...,I\text{\ \ and\ \ }\lim_{\alpha\rightarrow 0}p_{+j}^{(\alpha)}=\lim_{\alpha\rightarrow 0}q_{+j}^{(\alpha)}=1/J\ \text{for}\ j=1,...,J.

(9)

Remark 1
: There are two important aspects in Greenacree’s Theorem
that we want to emphasize.

a) First, it shows the conditions under which CA becomes row and column scales
invariant, equation (8).

b) Second, the row and column marginals of
\mathbf{P}^{(\alpha)}
for

\alpha>0
and
\alpha\simeq 0
are almost uniform, equation (9) ; so

\mathbf{P}^{(\alpha)}
is bistochastic, see subsection 1.1.

Remark 2
: The CA decomposition of (8) is

\displaystyle\lambda(p_{ij},w_{i}^{R}

\displaystyle=1/I,w_{j}^{C}=1/J)=\lim_{\alpha\rightarrow 0}\frac{\Delta_{ij}(\mathbf{P}^{(\alpha)})}{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mu_{mi}\nu_{mj}\rho_{m},

where
M=rank(\mathbf{P})-1
and the parameters (
\mu_{mi},\nu_{mj},\rho_{m})

satisfy, for

1=\sum_{i=1}^{I}\mu_{mi}^{2}/I=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\upsilon_{mj}^{2}/J

0=\sum_{i=1}^{I}\mu_{mi}\mu_{si}/I=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\upsilon_{mj}\upsilon_{sj}/J\text{\ \ for }m\neq s.

The parameter
\rho_{m},
which is positive, has two interpretations : First,

\rho_{m}=corr(\mu_{mi},\nu_{mj})
is the Pearson’s correlation coefficient
within the framework of CA of
\mathbf{P}^{(\alpha)}
; second, it represents
the uniformly weighted intrinsic association in the framework of Goodman’s RC
association models. In the latter case, Goodman (1991, eqn. .3.7) interpreted
it as a function of log-odds ratio

\displaystyle\log(\frac{p_{ij}p_{i_{1}j_{1}}}{p_{ij_{1}}p_{i_{1}j}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{M}(\mu_{mi}-\mu_{mi_{1}})(\upsilon_{mj}-\upsilon_{mj_{1}})\rho_{m}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\alpha\rightarrow 0}(\frac{p_{ij}^{(\alpha)}}{p_{i+}^{(\alpha)}p_{+j}^{(\alpha)}}+\frac{p_{i_{1}j_{1}}^{(\alpha)}}{p_{i_{1}+}^{(\alpha)}p_{+j_{1}}^{(\alpha)}}-\frac{p_{ij_{1}}^{(\alpha)}}{p_{i+}^{(\alpha)}p_{+j_{1}}^{(\alpha)}}-\frac{p_{i_{1}j}^{(\alpha)}}{p_{i_{1}+}^{(\alpha)}p_{+j}^{(\alpha)}})/\alpha.

The last relation is called tetra differences by Rao (1973, p.12) ; and it
perfectly reconciles Pearson correlation measure
\rho_{m}
with Yule’s
log-odds ratio measure, an effort attempted by Mosteller (1968) and by Goodman
(1996, section 10) whom we quote :

”Pearson’s approach to the analysis of
cross-classified data was based primarily on the bivariate normal. He assumed
that the row and column classifications arise from underlying continuous
random variables having a bivariate normal distribution, so that the sample
contingency table comes from a discretized bivariate normal ; and he then was
concerned with the estimation of the correlation coefficient for the
underlying bivariate normal. On the other hand, Yule felt that, for many kinds
of contingency tables, it was not desirable in scientific work to introduce
assumptions about an underlying bivariate normal in the analysis of these
tables ; and for such tables, he used, to a great extent, coefficients based on
the odds-ratios (for example, Yule’s Q and Y), coefficients that did not
require any assumptions about underlying distributions. The Pearson approach
and the Yule approach appear to be wholly different, but a kind of
reconciliation of the two perspectives was obtained in Goodman
(1981a)”
.

4.1
Case 1 : sparse fully indecomposable

The transformed matrix

The Sinkhorn (RAS-ipf) algorithm

The Sinkhorn (RAS-ipf) algorithm is a very simple method to adjust a
contingency table to prespecified marginals. Given that the literature on this
topic is vast, we refer the reader to Idel (2016) for a comprehensive survey.

The Sinkhorn (RAS-ipf) algorithm has three variants, clearly described in
Madre (1980) : two of them apply alternating adjustments of the marginals,
which is the standard and the most discussed method ; for an example see Landa
et al. (2021, page 6, algorithm 2) ; and the other uses simultaneous adjustment
of the row and column marginals, which we apply in this paper and in the R
code given below.

We use two criteria of convergence of the algorithm that a bistochastic matrix

\mathbf{D}=(d_{ij})
satisfies as described in subsection 1.1 and Corollary
1b. The first criterion is
C2_{dist}
and the second
ratio
defined by

C2_{dist}=\sum_{i,j}|(\frac{1}{I}\sum_{i}d_{ij}+\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j}d_{ij})-2|,

ratio=\sum_{i,j}d_{ij}/IJ.

If
\mathbf{D}
is bistochastic, then
C2_{dist}=0
and
ratio=1.\vskip 12.0pt plus 4.0pt minus 4.0pt

Data transformation

The main instigation of this paper is the bistochastic similarity of equations
(9) and (10). In (9) there is one parameter
\alpha\approx 0
, while in (10)
there are
(I+J)
positive scaling parameters
(a_{i},b_{j}).
To understand
this similarity, or equivalently the difference between (9) and (10), we
introduce the concept of a transformation that does not change the internal
order structure in a contingency table, and show that Cases 1 and 2 are
transformations that may change dramatically the internal order structure.

Definition 2
: A transformation
T
of a probability measure
(
p_{ij})
keeps the
internal order structure
unchanged if it
satisfies the following inequality :

p_{ij}\leq p_{i_{1}j_{1}}\ \text{then}\ \ T(p_{ij})\leq T(p_{i_{1}j_{1}}).

(14)

It is evident that the simple power transformation by
T(p_{ij})=p_{ij}^{(\alpha)}
for
\alpha>0
in Greenacre’s Theorem satisfies (14) ; while the
transformations in Cases 1 and 2 represented in equations (10) and (12) by

T(p_{ij})=a_{i}p_{ij}b_{j}
and
T(p_{ij})=a_{i\beta}^{\ast}p_{ij}b_{j\beta}^{\ast}
in general do not satisfy (14), as shown by the following simple
contrived example.

Example 1 :
The application of the Sinkhorn (RAS-ipf) algorithm to the
following table, a variant first studied by Sinkhorn(1964),

\mathbf{N}=\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{0\ \ \ \ \ 1}{1\ \ \ \ \ 2}

produces the following transformed matrix after
nit=\ 50
iterations

T(\mathbf{N)=D}=\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{0\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ 1.98076923}{1.98076923\ \ \ \ \ \epsilon_{nit=50}=0.03921569},

which does not satisfy (14). Note that
\epsilon_{nit\rightarrow\infty}=0.

7.4
Map interpretation of the Milazzese data set

CA is a dimension reduction method for exploratory visualization of
non-negative data, such as counts or abundances. CA has body and soul : the
body resides in the production of
maps
, mostly two-dimensional ; the
soul resides in the
interpretation
of the maps. We consider the maps
and their interpretation as the essential aspects of CA.

Given that interpretability of a map is an essential aspect in CA, we classify
it into two kinds : objective and subjective. An objective interpretation of a
map is that it reflects meta-information ; that is, an external covariate (or
covariates) which is (or are) not included in the computations.

The Punta Milazzese of Panarea data set represents the abundances of 31
artifacts found in 19 huts, excavated at the Middle Bronze age settlement at
Punta Milazzese on the island of Panarea (Aeolian Archipelago, Italy).
Furthermore, two complementary pieces of meta-information (2 external
covariates) on the huts are also available from Alberti (2013a). First, their
geographic locations displayed in Figure 1. Second, their surface areas in

m^{2}
, which we include in the labelling of the huts ; for example, 10M1
means hut number 1 has
10m^{2}
of surface area, and M14 ? means the surface
area of hut number 14 is unknown and  ? means it has very low marginal count (a
rare observation less than 5 counts). Furthermore, Alberti (2013a) suggested
two functional uses of the huts : residential (surface area larger than
15
m^{2}
is sufficient for a couple) or utilitarian.

The aim of using CA and TCA is to help discover meaningful patterns and
clusters of the huts and the artifacts, from which some useful information may
be inferred on the past activities of the settlement. Given that 58% of the
cells are zero counts, TCA produced more interpretable results than CA (not
shown) of the original counts data
N
as shown in Figure 2 copied from
Gauthier and Choulakian (2015). TCA map of the huts admits an objective
interpretation : Globally it reflects the two complementary meta-information
provided by Alberti (2013a) stated above : the geographical locations east-west
and the surface areas ; all huts on the right side of Figure 2 have surface
areas less than
15m^{2}.

Figure 3 displays mfCA : The Guttman effect (parabola) is evident, and which
reflects the meta-information : on the right-side the huts have surface areas
less than 15m
{}^{2}.
However, the second dimension is dominated by the row 28
which has only 1 count and which intersects the column 17M3. So, mfCA is also
sensitive to rare points.

Note that TCA plot (not shown) of the bistochastic matrix
Q
is not
objectively interpretable.

8
Conclusion

Two kinds of transformations render CA scale-marginal free, the power
transformation for positive data as in Greenacre’s Theorem and by
double-scaling for positive and non-negative data first studied quite in
detail by Mosteller (1968) in his presidential address, and later on outlined
by Goodman (1996). We highlighted the important notion of bistochasticity of
the two transformations.

We think that generalized or multiple mfCA of extremely sparse and large data
sets, such as textual or networks data, can be useful : This will be pursued
on.
