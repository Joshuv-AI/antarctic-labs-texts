Jaime E. Pineda, Jorma Harju, Paola Caselli, Olli Sipilä, Mika Juvela, Charlotte Vastel, Erik Rosolowsky, Andreas Burkert, Rachel K. Friesen, Yancy Shirley, María José Maureira, Spandan Choudhury, Dominique M. Segura-Cox, Rolf Güsten, Anna Punanova, Luca Bizzocchi, Alyssa A. Goodman

Abstract
Spectral lines of ammonia, NH 3 \text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{3}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{3}}\text{}} , are useful probes of the physical conditions in dense molecular cloud cores. In addition to advantages in spectroscopy, ammonia has also been suggested to be resistant to freezing onto grain surfaces, which should make it a superior tool for studying the interior parts of cold, dense cores. Here we present high-resolution NH 3 \text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{3}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{3}}\text{}} observations with the Very Large Array (VLA) and Green Bank Telescope (GBT) towards a prestellar core. These observations show an outer region with a fractional NH 3 \text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{3}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{3}}\text{}} abundance of X ⁡ ( NH 3 ) = ( 1.975 ± 0.005 ) × 10 − 8 X({}\mathrm{NH}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\vphantom{\mathrm{2}}\smash[t]{\mathrm{3}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\hphantom{\mathrm{3}}\mathrm{}})=(1.975\pm 0.005)\times 10^{-8} ( ± \pm 10% systematic), but it also reveals that after all, the X ⁡ ( NH 3 ) X({}\mathrm{NH}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\vphantom{\mathrm{2}}\smash[t]{\mathrm{3}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\hphantom{\mathrm{3}}\mathrm{}}) starts to decrease above a H 2 {}\mathrm{H}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\vphantom{\mathrm{2}}\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\hphantom{\mathrm{2}}\mathrm{}} column density of ≈ 2.6 × 10 22 ​ cm − 2 \approx 2.6\times 10^{22}\,\rm cm^{-2} . We derive a density model for the core and find that the break-point in the fractional abundance occurs at the density n ⁡ ( H 2 ) ∼ 2 × 10 5 ​ cm − 3 n({}\mathrm{H}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\vphantom{\mathrm{2}}\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\hphantom{\mathrm{2}}\mathrm{}})\sim 2\times 10^{5}\,\rm cm^{-3} , and beyond this point the fractional abundance decreases with increasing density, following the power law n − 1.1 n^{-1.1} . This power-law behavior is well reproduced by chemical models where adsorption onto grains dominates the removal of ammonia and related species from the gas at high densities. We suggest that the break-point density changes from core to core depending on the temperature and the grain properties, but that the depletion power law is anyway likely to be close to n − 1 n^{-1} owing to the dominance of accretion in the central parts of starless cores.



I
Introduction

Dense cores are the places where stars are formed. Their physical
conditions determine when the gravitational collapse starts and how
it proceeds
(
di Francesco et al. 2007
)
.
Observations of molecular lines allow us to extract information not only
about the physical structure of dense cores but also about their
chemical composition
(
Bergin & Tafalla 2007
)
.

When high densities and low temperatures are present, gas-phase
molecules can be depleted because of accretion onto the dust grains.
The effect of depletion is commonly observed in
CO
(and isotopologues),
and it is identified as a strong decrease in the
CO
abundance towards
the central part of a dense core

(
Caselli et al. 1999
;
Tafalla et al. 2004
, e.g.,)
.
Surprisingly, some nitrogen-bearing species, such as
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{3}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{3}}\text{}}
,
\text{N}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{2}}\text{}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{}}\text{+}}
and

CN
, do not present strong evidence for depletion

(
Tafalla et al. 2004
;
Crapsi et al. 2007
;
Hily-Blant et al. 2008
;
Hily-Blant et al. 2010
)
.
However, it is theoretically expected that all species,
other than pure hydrogen species and helium, should be depleted by at
least two orders of magnitude in the central
region of a dense, starless core; this region can be called the zone of “complete depletion”
(
Walmsley et al. 2004
)
.
In the past, modeling of single dish observations have provided indirect
evidence for depletion of
\text{N}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{2}}\text{}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{}}\text{+}}
in the L183 and L1544 dense cores

(
Pagani et al. 2005
;
Pagani et al. 2007
;
Redaelli et al. 2019
)
.
Only recent observations with ALMA, resolving the densest
parts of the prestellar core L1544, show evidence for depletion of
deuterated ammonia,
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{2}}\text{}}\text{D}

(
Caselli et al. 2022
)
.

To validate the concept of complete depletion, it is important to
investigate if also the common isotopolog of ammonia,
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{3}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{3}}\text{}}
, is also depleted
at high densities. The starless core H-MM1
(
Johnstone et al. 2004
;
Parise et al. 2011
)
is an excellent target for this purpose.
H-MM1 lies in relative isolation on the outskirts of the L1688 region in the nearby Ophiuchus molecular cloud.
The distance to L1688 is
\approx
138.4
\pm
2.6 pc
(
Ortiz-León et al. 2018
)
.
Ammonia line emission in the L1688
region has been previously studied with single-dish observations

(
Friesen et al. 2017
;
Harju et al. 2017
;
Chen et al. 2019
;
Auddy et al. 2019
;
Choudhury et al. 2020
;
Choudhury et al. 2021
)
.
These studies show a clear transition from the supersonic turbulence to a
subsonic regime at the boundary of the H-MM1 core, and that the interior
parts of the core are characterized by a high degree of deuteration in

\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{3}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{3}}\text{}}

(
Harju et al. 2017
)
. This suggests that the densities and
temperatures in the central region are appropriate for
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{3}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{3}}\text{}}
depletion.

High-resolution
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{3}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{3}}\text{}}
observations are also useful for the
interpretation of recent ALMA observations that revealed the presence
of a methanol (
\rm CH_{3}OH
) sheath around the dense core
(
Harju et al. 2020
)
. Of the methanol
desorption mechanisms discussed in
Harju et al. 2020
, shocks
and grain-grain collisions induced by vigorous turbulence or shear
instability should be associated with increased kinetic temperatures or
velocity dispersion in the methanol emission region. Ammonia provides
excellent means to determine these quantities and thereby test
these desorption scenarios.

Here we present
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{3}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{3}}\text{}}
(1,1) and (2,2) observations of the starless core
H-MM1 with the Karl G. Jansky Very Large Array (VLA), which we combine
with previous Robert C. Byrd Green Bank Telescope (GBT) observations

(
Friesen et al. 2017
)
.
The acquired data allow us to study the
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{3}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{3}}\text{}}
emission at high angular
resolution to resolve the possible
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{3}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{3}}\text{}}
depletion zone and to improve our knowledge of the origin of methanol.

II
Data

II.1
VLA

We conducted VLA observations of H-MM1 on 2020 January 9, 12, 14, and 18
in the D-array configuration (project 19B-178, PI: Pineda).
We used the high-frequency K-band receiver and configured the WIDAR
correlator to observe the
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{3}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{3}}\text{}}
(1,1) and (2,2) lines with 8 and 4 MHz
bandwidth windows, respectively.
Each window has a channel separation of 5.208 kHz (
\approx
0.065
\rm km\,s^{-1}
).
The quasar 3C 286 is used as the flux calibrator, J1256-0547 as the
bandpass calibrator, and J1625-2527 as the phase calibrator.

The calibration was performed using the VLA pipeline with the Common
Astronomy Software Applications package (CASA) version 5.6.2-2

(
McMullin et al. 2007
)
.
This included Hanning-smoothing, and therefore the effective spectral resolution is 0.13
\rm km\,s^{-1}
.
The imaging used the
tclean
command with natural weighting and
uvtaper

to increase the signal to noise ratio, a restoring beam of 6″,
and with multi-scale clean (with scales 0, 6, 18, and 54″and a
smallscalebias
parameter of 0).
We also included the
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{3}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{3}}\text{}}
single dish data obtained with the Robert C.
Byrd Green Bank Telescope
(
Friesen et al. 2017
)
as a model image to recover
the extended emission
using the
startmodel
option in
tclean
.
This method initializes the clean model image as the
single dish data.
The final noise level achieved in the data cubes is
3.5 m
\rm Jy\,beam^{-1}
per channel, which corresponds to
\approx
0.21 K per channel.
We estimate our absolute calibration uncertainty to be 10%.

II.2
Total
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{2}}\text{}}
Column Density

The H-MM1 core appears as an absorption feature in the 8
\rm\mu m
map
measured by the InfraRed Array Camera (IRAC) of the
Spitzer Space
Telescope
.
We use this to derive a high-resolution
{}\mathrm{H}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\vphantom{\mathrm{2}}\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\hphantom{\mathrm{2}}\mathrm{}}
column
density map of the core. The method is described in Appendix
A
of

Harju et al. 2020
, and briefly summarized here.
The method also uses
the 850
\rm\mu m
emission map from SCUBA-2
(
Pattle et al. 2015
)
, and the
dust color temperature map,
T_{\rm C}
, derived from
Herschel
/SPIRE maps

(
Harju et al. 2017
)
.
We assume the dust opacity model for unprocessed dust grains with thin ice mantles by

Ossenkopf & Henning 1994
. This model has a dust emissivity index of

\beta=2.0
over
Herschel
/SPIRE wavelengths (250–500
\rm\mu m
).
The absorption cross-sections per unit mass of gas at 8
\rm\mu m
and
850
\rm\mu m
are
\kappa_{8\,\rm\mu m}=8.86\,\rm cm^{2}\,\rm g^{-1}
and

\kappa_{850\,\rm\mu m}=1.13\times 10^{-2}\,\rm cm^{2}\,\rm g^{-1}
,
respectively. The
Spitzer
map is smoothed to the resolution of the
VLA ammonia map,
6\,\arcsec
, before the derivation of the column
density map. The resulting
N({}\mathrm{H}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\vphantom{\mathrm{2}}\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\hphantom{\mathrm{2}}\mathrm{}})
distribution is shown in
Fig.
2
.

Lefèvre et al. 2016
pointed out that scattering by dust particles may bias
column density derived from
8\,\rm\mu m
absorption.
This issue is discussed in Appendix
A
.
Simulations using different dust models
show that the effect of scattering is likely to be small compared to the
uncertainties related to the intensities of the foreground and background
emission components, and thermal dust emission at 8
\rm\mu m
.
The 8
\rm\mu m
background intensity in the region of H-MM1 is
\sim
10
\rm MJy\,sr^{-1}
, whereas the scattered intensity according to our
simulations should stay below
0.1\,\rm MJy\,sr^{-1}
(see Appendix
A
).

IV
Discussion

IV.1
Gas Kinematics

The velocity dispersion,
\sigma_{\rm v}
, derived from
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{3}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{3}}\text{}}
observations changes only little within the core and indicates subsonic
non-thermal motions. The H-MM1 core is thus a “coherent core” as
termed by
Barranco & Goodman 1998
and
Goodman et al. 1998

(
Pineda et al. 2010
, see also)
. The kinematics of H-MM1 and other coherent
cores in the Ophiuchus region have been previously studied using observations
with the Green Bank Telescope (GBT) by
Auddy et al. 2019
,

Chen et al. 2019
, and
Choudhury et al. 2020
.
The thermal r.m.s.
velocity of
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{3}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{3}}\text{}}
molecules at 11 K is
73\,\rm m\,s^{-1}
in one dimension. The
broadening owing to the channel width of the Hanning smoothed spectra,

130\,\rm m\,s^{-1}
, corresponds to an instrumental
\sigma
of
52\,\rm m\,s^{-1}

(see Appendix A of
Choudhury et al. 2020
). So, the median
velocity dispersion,

\sigma_{\rm v}\sim 110\,\rm m\,s^{-1}
, from the present VLA observations indicates
a median non-thermal dispersion of
\sigma_{\rm NT}\sim 63\,\rm m\,s^{-1}
,
or a median sonic Mach number of

\sim 0.3
. This value is approximately half of that derived by
Choudhury et al. 2020

for the narrow
{}\mathrm{NH}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\vphantom{\mathrm{2}}\smash[t]{\mathrm{3}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\hphantom{\mathrm{3}}\mathrm{}}
line component from GBT observations with an angular
resolution of
31\arcsec
.

Velocity dispersion of
{}\mathrm{NH}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\vphantom{\mathrm{2}}\smash[t]{\mathrm{3}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\hphantom{\mathrm{3}}\mathrm{}}
lines (color map) and the distribution of bright 96.7 GHz methanol emission (contours) in H-MM1. The contour levels go from 0.8 to 4.0 K
\rm km\,s^{-1}
by steps of 0.8 K

The velocity dispersion attains values clearly higher than the median
in a narrow region in the southeastern part of the core. This region
overlaps partially with the southern “tail” of the bright
\rm CH_{3}OH

emission mapped by
Harju et al. 2020
. The integrated intensity contours of four methanol lines at 96.7 GHz are overlaid onto the
{}\mathrm{NH}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\vphantom{\mathrm{2}}\smash[t]{\mathrm{3}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\hphantom{\mathrm{3}}\mathrm{}}

\sigma_{\rm v}
map in Fig.
8
.
However, as can be seen in Fig.
8
, methanol emission
arises mostly from regions where the dispersion is close to the average.
Therefore, the present data do not support the idea that efficient
\rm CH_{3}OH
desorption is only associated with enhanced turbulence or large velocity fluctuations, lending credence to the chemical desorption scenario of methanol formation at the edge of the catastrophic CO freeze-out zone
(
Vasyunin et al. 2017
)
.
Perhaps it is worth to notice, though, that the most quiescent region in the northern part of the core with

\sigma_{\rm v}\sim 90\,\rm m\,s^{-1}
(implying
\sigma_{\rm NT}<10\,\rm m\,s^{-1}
) is
clearly devoid of
\rm CH_{3}OH
.

The elongated redshifted feature seen in the velocity centroid map at
V_{\rm LSR}>4.3\,\rm km\,s^{-1}
seems to connect the compact dense core with the outer regions.
This feature bears resemblance to the streamer detected using gas tracers around binary protostar Per-emb-2
(
Pineda et al. 2020
)
.
We will discuss the core kinematics and the nature of this possible streamer in detail in a separate paper.

IV.2
Gas Temperature

The gas temperature shows only a small drop toward the core
center. The temperature 11 K in the inner parts is consistent with
previous gas and dust temperature determinations using GBT and

Herschel
data

(
Harju et al. 2017
;
Auddy et al. 2019
;
Choudhury et al. 2020
)
.
The temperature profile in
H-MM1 is very different from that in the well-studied prestellar core
L1544, where the central temperature reaches down to
\approx 6
K

(
Crapsi et al. 2007
)
,
or the starless core CB17, where the central temperature
reaches down to
\approx 9.5
K
(
Spear et al. 2021
)
.
The main difference is that H-MM1 is located in
an environment with strong radiation field
(
Rawlings et al. 2013
, e.g.,)
.
Far-infrared radiation which penetrates the core is
absorbed by dust particles, and this heating is conveyed to gas by
gas-dust collisions. The density in the core, in excess of

10^{5}\,\rm cm^{-3}
, is high enough to make gas-dust thermal coupling
efficient
(
Goldsmith 2001
;
Galli et al. 2002
;
Ivlev et al. 2019
)
.

Arzoumanian et al. 2011

model is shown in Fig.

only include cells that have densities higher than

IV.4

The formation of ammonia in the interstellar gas is
recapitulated in, for example,

reactions with
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{2}}\text{}}
lead quickly to the ammonium ion,
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{4}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{4}}\text{+}}
,
which is the principal precursor of
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{3}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{3}}\text{}}
. Electron
recombination of
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{4}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{4}}\text{+}}
and smaller cations give the
nitrogen hydrides
NH
,
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{2}}\text{}}
and
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{3}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{3}}\text{}}
. Ammonia is
expected to be the most abundant of these in dense cores.
The pivotal ion,
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{}}\text{+}}
, is formed by the charge transfer reaction

NH
{}+{}
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{}}\text{+}}
{}\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\rightarrow\displaystyle}}\limits}{}
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{}}\text{+}}
{}+{}
H

or by the reaction

\text{N}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{}}\text{+}}
{}+{}
\text{ortho}{-}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{2}}\text{}}
{}\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\rightarrow\displaystyle}}\limits}{}
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{}}\text{+}}
{}+{}
H
.
In dark cloud
conditions, the
\text{N}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{}}\text{+}}
ion forms by the dissociative charge
transfer reaction

\text{N}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{2}}\text{}}
{}+{}
\text{He}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{}}\text{+}}
{}\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\rightarrow\displaystyle}}\limits}{}
\text{N}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{}}\text{+}}
{}+{}
N
{}+{}
He

(
Hily-Blant et al. 2010
;
Hily-Blant et al. 2020
)
.

In the gas, nitrogen hydrides are constantly converted to
cations through charge or proton exchange reactions, and returned to
neutral species in recombination reactions with electrons.
The dominant ions reacting with ammonia are
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{}}\text{+}}
and
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{3}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{3}}\text{+}}

with approximately equal destruction rates, and the ionic species
formed in these reactions,
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{3}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{3}}\text{+}}
and
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{4}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{4}}\text{+}}
, are quickly
returned to ammonia;
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{3}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{3}}\text{+}}
reacts with
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{2}}\text{}}
to form

\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{4}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{4}}\text{+}}
, which gives mainly
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{3}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{3}}\text{}}
in recombination with electrons
(see Fig. 2 in
Sipilä et al. 2015
).

Neutral nitrogen hydrides, as well as nitrogen atoms and
molecular nitrogen, are also likely to be adsorbed onto dust
grains, and this should eventually diminish the abundances of
ammonia and related species, unless they are returned into the
gas to the same degree. The accreted
N
atoms, and the
NH
and

\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{2}}\text{}}
radicals, are efficiently hydrogenated to
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{3}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{3}}\text{}}
,
and most nitrogen in interstellar ices is probably bound to
ammonia. As a consequence, ammonia is much more abundant in the
icy mantles of dust grains than in the gas phase, and the ice
mantles constitute the biggest ammonia reservoir in dense
molecular clouds. In quiescent, shielded regions, the most
important mechanisms releasing part of this ammonia probably
are the cosmic-ray-induced desorption

(
Leger et al. 1985
, e.g.,)
and reactive desorption

(
Garrod et al. 2006
, e.g.,)
. At very high densities, where the accretion timescale is short, the gas-phase abundance of ammonia probably depends on the balance between accretion and desorption.

IV.5
Chemical Modeling

To aid the interpretation of the present observational results which
indicate strong depletion of ammonia, we simulated the chemistry of

\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{3}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{3}}\text{}}
in H-MM1 using our rate-equation gas-grain astrochemical model

(
Sipilä et al. 2015
;
Sipilä et al. 2019a
, e.g.,)
. In brief, the model solves a set of
rate equations connecting chemical reactions in the gas phase and on
grain surfaces. Several desorption mechanisms are considered: thermal
desorption, cosmic-ray induced desorption, photodesorption, and chemical
desorption. We employ a one-dimensional physical model that was
constructed by extracting density and dust temperature cuts through the
density peak of the three-dimensional model described in
Sect.
IV.3
and making these cuts symmetric with respect to
the density peak by averaging. The dust temperature distribution of the
three-dimensional model was calculated by exposing the density model to
an external radiation field composed of a) diffuse isotropic
interstellar radiation, and b) radiation from B-type stars located

\sim
1 pc west of the core. The intensities of these components were
adjusted until the 850
\rm\mu m
surface brightness map from the model
agreed with the SCUBA-2 map and the average dust temperature map agreed
with the color temperature
T_{\rm C}
map derived from
Herschel
. We
discretized the physical model into a series of points, and ran the
chemical simulations separately in each point to obtain time- and
radially-dependent
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{3}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{3}}\text{}}
abundance profiles. We employ here the same
initial chemical abundances and other chemical model parameters as in

Harju et al. 2017
. The physical model is shown in
Fig.
11
, and the initial abundances
are listed in Table

.

IV.6
Density dependence of the ammonia abundance

As discussed in Section
IV.4
, the dominant
gas-phase reactions involving ammonia produce closely related molecular
ions that are quickly returned back to
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{3}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{3}}\text{}}
. Therefore, the diminishing
ammonia abundance at high densities must ultimately be caused by
adsorption of
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{3}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{3}}\text{}}
and related species, mainly
NH
,
N
, and
\text{N}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{2}}\text{}}
, onto grains. The rate at which an atom or molecule accretes onto grains
is proportional to its average thermal speed and the total surface
area of dust grains. The latter can be expressed as the
product of the grain surface area per H atom,
\sigma_{\rm H}
, and
the total hydrogen density,
n_{\rm H}
(
\sim 2\times n({}\mathrm{H}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\vphantom{\mathrm{2}}\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\hphantom{\mathrm{2}}\mathrm{}})
).
In other words, the accretion rate is proportional to the density.

We examined the dependence of
X({}\mathrm{NH}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\vphantom{\mathrm{2}}\smash[t]{\mathrm{3}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\hphantom{\mathrm{3}}\mathrm{}})
and related species on

n({}\mathrm{H}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\vphantom{\mathrm{2}}\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\hphantom{\mathrm{2}}\mathrm{}})
by employing our full chemical network. This was done by
running a series of one-point models at different densities with the
gas and dust temperatures fixed to 11 K. Ammonia abundances were
extracted at the simulation time
3\times 10^{5}
yr. Two different
set-ups were used: one with chemical desorption (CD) and another with
CD turned off. The results are shown in Fig.
13
.
One can see that at densities above
\sim 10^{5}\,\rm cm^{-3}
,
X({}\mathrm{NH}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\vphantom{\mathrm{2}}\smash[t]{\mathrm{3}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\hphantom{\mathrm{3}}\mathrm{}})

follows the decreasing abundances of
N
and
\text{N}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{2}}\text{}}
. The slope of the

\log X({}\mathrm{NH}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\vphantom{\mathrm{2}}\smash[t]{\mathrm{3}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\hphantom{\mathrm{3}}\mathrm{}})
vs.
\log n({}\mathrm{H}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\vphantom{\mathrm{2}}\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\hphantom{\mathrm{2}}\mathrm{}})
curve is
-1.04\pm 0.01
in the model
with CD, and a little steeper,
\sim-1.15\pm 0.04
, when CD is turned off.
The dominance of accretion leads to a density
dependence close to
n^{-1}
for neutral species, whereas
electrons and light ions (
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{}}\text{+}}
and
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{3}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{3}}\text{+}}
) show a shallower
power law,
\sim n^{-0.44}
in our models. The latter is determined by
the competition between the cosmic ray ionization and neutralization in
recombination reactions.

According to our model, most of ammonia is to be found on dust grains, where it reaches a

\sim 10^{-4}

, and stays

Replenishment of the
gas with ammonia from grains is significant when CD is
effective. Consequently,
X({}\mathrm{NH}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\vphantom{\mathrm{2}}\smash[t]{\mathrm{3}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\hphantom{\mathrm{3}}\mathrm{}})
decreases more slowly and at higher
densities in the model with CD than in that without CD.

Inspection of reaction rates during our chemical simulation shows that
the density
5\times 10^{4}\,\rm cm^{-3}
, where the
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{3}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{3}}\text{}}
abundance turns
down, is where the adsorption rate of ammonia exceeds the production
of
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{}}\text{+}}
ions in the gas. In the model with CD, desorption from
grains always gives more ammonia than ion-molecule reactions, but the
adsorption rate surpasses the desorption rate at a density of

\sim 1\times 10^{5}\,\rm cm^{-3}
. At still higher densities, above

n({}\mathrm{H}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\vphantom{\mathrm{2}}\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\hphantom{\mathrm{2}}\mathrm{}})=2\times 10^{5}\,\rm cm^{-3}
, a balance between the primary
production of gaseous ammonia and its adsorption is established:
the adsorption rate is equal to the combined rates of desorption and

\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{}}\text{+}}
production up to the highest density modeled,
10^{6}\,\rm cm^{-3}
.
In our CD model, desorption accounts for
\sim 80\%
of
ammonia production in this density range.

The dominant surface reaction releasing ammonia into the gas in this
model is

\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{2}}\text{\smash{\raisebox{-3.18884pt}{\scalebox{1.3}{*}}}}}
{}+{}
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{}}\text{\smash{\raisebox{-3.18884pt}{\scalebox{1.3}{*}}}}}
{}\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\rightarrow\displaystyle}}\limits}{}
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{3}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{3}}\text{}}
.
Here we have indicated
species residing on the surface with asterisks. The mentioned reaction is the
end point of hydrogenation of N atoms trapped on grains. On the other
hand, a majority of
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{2}}\text{\smash{\raisebox{-3.18884pt}{\scalebox{1.3}{*}}}}}
molecules on the surface is formed by
dissociation of
{}\mathrm{NH}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\vphantom{\mathrm{2}}\smash[t]{\mathrm{3}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\hphantom{\mathrm{3}}\mathrm{\ast}}
, either by cosmic rays or by secondary photons
induced by these. We can therefore present a simplified picture, where
desorption is proportional to the density of ammonia residing on grains
and a desorption coefficient. This picture is also valid for direct
ammonia desorption caused by external agents, such as grain heating
through cosmic-ray hits.

The equilibrium condition between adsorption and desorption
can be written as
k_{\rm ads}\,n({}\mathrm{NH}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\vphantom{\mathrm{2}}\smash[t]{\mathrm{3}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\hphantom{\mathrm{3}}\mathrm{}})=k_{\rm des}\,n({}\mathrm{NH}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\vphantom{\mathrm{2}}\smash[t]{\mathrm{3}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\hphantom{\mathrm{3}}\mathrm{\ast}})
,
where
k_{\rm ads}=n_{\rm H}\sigma_{\rm H}{\bar{v}}_{{}\mathrm{NH}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\vphantom{\mathrm{2}}\smash[t]{\mathrm{3}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\hphantom{\mathrm{3}}\mathrm{}}}
is the
adsorption rate per molecule (
\rm s^{-1}
),
{\bar{v}}_{{}\mathrm{NH}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\vphantom{\mathrm{2}}\smash[t]{\mathrm{3}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\hphantom{\mathrm{3}}\mathrm{}}}
is the
mean thermal speed of ammonia molecules, and
k_{\rm des}
is the
desorption rate per molecule (
\rm s^{-1}
). The ammonia densities in the
gas and on grains (per cubic centimeter) have been denoted by
n({}\mathrm{NH}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\vphantom{\mathrm{2}}\smash[t]{\mathrm{3}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\hphantom{\mathrm{3}}\mathrm{}})
and

n({}\mathrm{NH}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\vphantom{\mathrm{2}}\smash[t]{\mathrm{3}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\hphantom{\mathrm{3}}\mathrm{\ast}})
. Dividing the equilibrium equation by
n({}\mathrm{H}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\vphantom{\mathrm{2}}\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\hphantom{\mathrm{2}}\mathrm{}})
and
re-arranging the terms one gets for the fractional ammonia abundance

X({}\mathrm{NH}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\vphantom{\mathrm{2}}\smash[t]{\mathrm{3}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\hphantom{\mathrm{3}}\mathrm{}})=k_{\textrm{des}}\,X({}\mathrm{NH}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\vphantom{\mathrm{2}}\smash[t]{\mathrm{3}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\hphantom{\mathrm{3}}\mathrm{\ast}})/(\sigma_{\rm H}\,{\bar{v}}_{{}\mathrm{NH}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\vphantom{\mathrm{2}}\smash[t]{\mathrm{3}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\hphantom{\mathrm{3}}\mathrm{}}}\,n_{\rm H})
. According to our chemistry model, the ammonia abundance in ice
exceeds the gas-phase abundance by several orders of magnitude in starless dense cores,
and accretion of ammonia from the gas does not change
X({}\mathrm{NH}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\vphantom{\mathrm{2}}\smash[t]{\mathrm{3}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\hphantom{\mathrm{3}}\mathrm{\ast}})
significantly
at this stage. Assuming that also the desorption rate is approximately constant,
balance between adsorption and desorption leads to an
n^{-1}
dependence of

X({}\mathrm{NH}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\vphantom{\mathrm{2}}\smash[t]{\mathrm{3}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\hphantom{\mathrm{3}}\mathrm{}})
in the gas.

As can be seen from Fig.
10
-right, both
model set-ups, with or without CD, fail to reproduce
the observed abundances at low densities. This probably depends on
the fact that the simulation starts from atomic gas composition in
physical conditions appropriate for a dense core. In reality, the
dense core material has already been processed in ambient molecular
cloud conditions with
n({}\mathrm{H}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\vphantom{\mathrm{2}}\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\hphantom{\mathrm{2}}\mathrm{}})\sim 10^{3}-10^{4}\,\rm cm^{-3}
, where ammonia
production on grains and in the gas has already started, and a
substantial fraction of nitrogen is already incorporated in ammonia ice.

V
Conclusions

We present high spatial resolution ammonia observations with the Very Large Array and
Green Bank Telescope
toward the starless core H-MM1 in Ophiuchus.
They indicate that ammonia has
a constant fractional abundance,
X({}\mathrm{NH}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\vphantom{\mathrm{2}}\smash[t]{\mathrm{3}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\hphantom{\mathrm{3}}\mathrm{}})={(}1.975{\pm 0.005)}\times 10^{-8}

(
\pm
10% systematic),
up to a column density of
N({}\mathrm{H}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\vphantom{\mathrm{2}}\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\hphantom{\mathrm{2}}\mathrm{}})=2.575\times 10^{22}\,\rm cm^{-2}
,
beyond which the abundance starts to decrease.
This is direct evidence of the
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{3}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{3}}\text{}}
depletion from the gas phase,
in a region of size comparable to the single dish beam size, which
explains why the depletion region is only detected
in deep high-angular resolution observations.
By
deriving a volume density model of the core we established that the
critical density where
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{3}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{3}}\text{}}
starts to disappear from the gas is
approximately
2\times 10^{5}\,\rm cm^{-3}
in this core, and at higher
densities
X({}\mathrm{NH}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\vphantom{\mathrm{2}}\smash[t]{\mathrm{3}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\hphantom{\mathrm{3}}\mathrm{}})
follows the power-law
X({}\mathrm{NH}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\vphantom{\mathrm{2}}\smash[t]{\mathrm{3}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\hphantom{\mathrm{3}}\mathrm{}})\propto n^{-1.1}
. This
tendency at high densities is reproduced by chemical simulations, and
is consistent with the idea that adsorption onto grains is the main
mechanism reducing the gas-phase abundances of neutral molecules
in the core center. Our
chemistry simulations suggest that the break-point in
X({}\mathrm{NH}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\vphantom{\mathrm{2}}\smash[t]{\mathrm{3}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\hphantom{\mathrm{3}}\mathrm{}})

versus
n({}\mathrm{H}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\vphantom{\mathrm{2}}\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\hphantom{\mathrm{2}}\mathrm{}})
relationship marks the density where the adsorption
rate surpasses the production rate of gaseous ammonia through
ion-molecule reactions and desorption. The density threshold for
depletion is likely to vary from core to core because it depends on
the temperature and the total grain surface area per hydrogen atom.

N({}\mathrm{H}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\vphantom{\mathrm{2}}\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\hphantom{\mathrm{2}}\mathrm{}})
map

Lefèvre et al. 2016
discussed the possible effect of dust scattering
for the 8
\mu
m absorption. With models of the cloud LDN 183, they
concluded that scattering could correspond to several times

0.1\,\rm MJy\,sr^{-1}
, helping to conciliate column density estimates
from mid-infrared (MIR) absorption and molecular line
emission. Scattering at this level required aggregate grains with
sizes much larger than in the general interstellar medium.

We examined H-MM1 similarly with 3D radiative transfer
models. The models were optimised to reproduce the
Herschel

250–500
\mu
m observations under the assumption that the
line-of-sight density profile is similar to that derived from the
surface brightness, along a constant-declination cross section through
the core centre. The external radiation field was set according to the

Mathis et al. 1983
model, adopting the angular distribution on the sky
from COBE/DIRBE observations
(
Hauser et al. 1998
)
. We further added an
energetically equal component for radiation from the equatorial west,
to qualitatively account for the effect of the nearby B-type star.
The optimisation of the model resulted in a scaling of the radiation
field and the column-density for each map pixel. The models reproduce
the
Herschel
surface brightness observations within 1 % at
350
\mu
m and within
\sim 10
% at 250
\mu
m and
500
\mu
m. The model optimisation was repeated for several dust
models, including the
Compiègne et al. 2011
dust model, the DustEM
core-mantle-mantle (CMM) and ice coated aggregates (AMMI) grains

(
Ysard et al. 2016
)
, and the
Weingartner & Draine 2001

R_{\rm V}=5.5
“B” model. The estimated radiation fields were consistently
slightly more than ten times the
Mathis et al. 1983
values but,
because of different far-infrared emissivities, the column densities
varied by a factor of
\sim
3 between the dust models.
In each case,
we also computed 8
\mu
m maps for the scattered light and for the
emission due to stochastically heated grains. All calculations were
performed with the radiative transfer program SOC
(
Juvela 2019
)
.

For all of the tested dust models, the 8
\mu
m scattered light
remained below
0.1\,\rm MJy\,sr^{-1}
. The largest value was obtained for
the
Weingartner & Draine 2001
grains, some
0.055\,\rm MJy\,sr^{-1}
,
the AMMI grains produced less than half of this value. The CMM and
AMMI models do not contain small grains and therefore result in no
significant MIR emission. The level of scattered light depends not
only on the scattering properties of the grains but equally on their
far-infrared emissivity that, via the fits to
Herschel
data, determines
the model column densities. The results suggest that (in the absence
of very large aggregates) the scattering is not a significant
factor. The 8
\mu
m extended sky brightness in H-MM1 is of the
order of
10\,\rm MJy\,sr^{-1}
, much higher than for LDN 183. Therefore,
more than to the scattered light, the analysis of MIR extinction is
sensitive to the assumed fraction of the extended sky brightness that
originates behind the core.

For the
Compiègne et al. 2011
and
Weingartner & Draine 2001
dust
models,
the emission from stochastically heated grains at 8
\mu
m
reaches a level of a few
\rm MJy\,sr^{-1}
. However, the
surface-brightness difference between the inner core and its
surroundings was observed to be either positive or negative, depending
on how the line-of-sight extent of the cloud was assumed to be
correlated with the column density. If the abundance of small grains
decreases towards the core centre, their emission is more likely to
bias the MIR extinction estimates upwards than downwards.

Appendix D
Density model of H-MM1

We estimate the density structure of the core by fitting a
Plummer-type function to the cross-sectional
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\vphantom{\text{2}}\smash[t]{\text{2}}}^{\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\hphantom{\text{2}}\text{}}
column density
profiles in different positions along the north-south oriented ridge
of the core. The method is adopted from
Arzoumanian et al. 2011
.
We assume that the density distribution has circular symmetry in the
plane perpendicular to the spine of the core. The latter is defined
by a spline fit through local
N(\rm H_{2})
peaks. The spline fit is
shown in Figure
16
a. A horizontal
(constant-declination) cut through the
N(\rm H_{2})
maximum of the core is
shown in Figure
16
b together with the fitted
function. The function is of the form

f(\Delta y)=\frac{a}{\left[1+(\Delta y/b)^{2}\right]^{c}}
,
where
\Delta y
is the projected distance from the peak.
The radial density profile,

n(r)=\frac{n_{0}}{\left[1+(r/r_{0})^{2}\right]^{\eta}}
,
is obtained from
this by the substitutions
\eta=c+0.5
,
r_{0}=b
,

n_{0}=a/(A_{p}r_{0})
, where
n_{0}
is the
central density and
A_{p}=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}{(1+u^{2})^{-\eta}}{du}
. For the cut shown in the figure, the
solution is
\eta=1.6\pm 0.3
,
r_{0}=(1600\pm 300)
au,

n_{0}=(1.30\pm 0.08)\times 10^{6}\,{\rm cm}^{-3}
. The three-dimensional
density model is illustrated in Figure
16
c. Here,
the line of sight coincides with the negative
x
-axis, and the
y
-axis
points to the equatorial west. This density model is used for the mean-density vs. column density correlation shown in Fig.
10
-left.
