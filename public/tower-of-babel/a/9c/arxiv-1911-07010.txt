Large N N behaviour of the two-dimensional Yang–Mills partition function
Thibaut Lemoine

Abstract
We compute the large N N limit of the partition function of the Euclidean Yang–Mills measure on orientable compact surfaces with genus g ⩾ 1 g\geqslant 1 and non-orientable compact surfaces with genus g ⩾ 2 g\geqslant 2 , with structure group the unitary group U ⁡ ( N ) {\mathrm{U}}(N) or special unitary group SU ⁡ ( N ) {\mathrm{SU}}(N) . Our proofs are based on asymptotic representation theory: more specifically, we control the dimension and Casimir number of irreducible representations of U ⁡ ( N ) {\mathrm{U}}(N) and SU ⁡ ( N ) {\mathrm{SU}}(N) when N N tends to infinity. Our main technical tool, involving ‘almost flat’ Young diagram, makes rigorous the arguments used by Gross and Taylor [ 12 ] in the setting of QCD, and in some cases we recover formulae given by Douglas [ 6 ] and Rusakov [ 19 ] .

Two-dimensional Yang–Mills theory, large
N
limit, asymptotic representation theory, Witten zeta function, almost flat highest weights

1.1
The Yang–Mills partition function on a compact surface

Let
\lambda=(\lambda_{1}\geqslant\ldots\geqslant\lambda_{N})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{N}
be a non-increasing sequence of relative integers. We associate two real numbers to
\lambda

dimension

(1)

d_{\lambda}=\prod_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant N}\frac{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}+j-i}{j-i}=\prod_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant N}\left(1+\frac{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}}{j-i}\right),

which is indeed a positive integer, and the
quadratic Casimir number

(2)

c_{2}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{N}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}^{2}+\sum_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant N}(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j})\right).

These definitions are dictated by the representation theory of the unitary group
{\mathrm{U}}(N)
: the
N

\lambda
, which we will also call a

in this paper, labels (up to isomorphism) an irreducible representation of
{\mathrm{U}}(N)
with dimension
d_{\lambda}
, and on which the Casimir operator of
{\mathrm{U}}(N)
, that is, the Laplacian, acts by the scalar
-c_{2}(\lambda)
. We will use the notation

\widehat{\mathrm{U}}(N)=\{(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{N})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{N}:\lambda_{1}\geqslant\ldots\geqslant\lambda_{N}\}.

Throughout this article, a
surface
will denote a
compact connected closed surface
. A standard classification theorem (see in

(i)

The connected sum of
g

g

,

(ii)

The connected sum of
g

In the first case, the surface is said to be
orientable
, otherwise it will be

, and in either case we will call

of the surface. Given an orientable surface
\Sigma_{g,T}

\Sigma_{g,T}

If
\Sigma_{g,T}^{-}
is a non-orientable compact surface of area

Z_{N}^{-}(g,T)=\sum_{\lambda\in\widehat{{\mathrm{U}}}(N)}e^{-\frac{T}{2}c_{2}(\lambda)}d_{\lambda}^{2-g}(\iota_{\lambda})^{g},

where
\iota_{\lambda}
is the
Frobenius–Schur indicator
of an irreducible representation of

These partition functions admit a special unitary variant, which differs from them in two aspects: the summation is restricted to the

c^{\prime}_{2}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{N}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}^{2}-\frac{1}{N}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}\right)^{2}+\sum_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant N}(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j})\right).

It is worth emphasizing that

\displaystyle Z_{N}^{{}^{\prime}-}(g,T)=\sum_{\lambda\in\widehat{{\mathrm{SU}}}(N)}e^{-\frac{T}{2}c^{\prime}_{2}(\lambda)}d_{\lambda}^{2-g}(\iota^{\prime}_{\lambda})^{g},

1.2
Statement of the results

The purpose of this paper is to establish the large
N
limit of the partition functions, depending on the orientability and the genus of the underlying surface. The two following theorems state the limit of orientable surfaces of genus 1 and higher, and non-orientable surfaces of genus 2 and higher. The next sections will then be devoted to prove these theorems. Before stating the theorems, let us recall two special functions.

•

The
Jacobi theta function

\vartheta
is defined, for
(z,\tau)\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
such that
\mathrm{Im}(\tau)>0
:

\vartheta(z;\tau)=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}e^{i\pi n^{2}\tau+2i\pi nz}.

We will also denote a particular version of this function by
\theta(q)=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}q^{n^{2}}
for
q\in\mathbb{C}
such that
|q|<1
.

•

The
Euler function

\phi
is defined, for
q\in\mathbb{C}
such that
|q|<1
, as the infinite product

\phi(q)=(q;q)_{\infty}=\prod_{m=1}^{\infty}(1-q^{m}).

Theorem 1.1

(Orientable limits)
.

Let
\Sigma
be an orientable surface of genus
g
and area
T\geqslant 0
. Set
q=e^{-\tfrac{T}{2}}
.

(i)

If
g\geqslant 2
and
T>0
, then the following convergences hold:

\lim_{N\to\infty}Z_{N}(g,T)=\theta(q)\ \text{ and }\ \lim_{N\to\infty}Z^{\prime}_{N}(g,T)=1.

Moreover, if
g\geqslant 2
and
T=0
, we have

\lim_{N\to\infty}Z^{\prime}_{N}(g,0)=1.

(ii)

If
g=1
and
T>0
, then the following convergences hold:

\lim_{N\to\infty}Z_{N}(1,T)=\frac{\theta(q)}{\phi(q)^{2}}\ \text{ and }\ \lim_{N\to\infty}Z^{\prime}_{N}(1,T)=\frac{1}{\phi(q)^{2}}.

Theorem 1.2

(Non-orientable limits)
.

Let
\Sigma
be a non-orientable surface of genus
g\geqslant 2
and area
T\geqslant 0
. Set
q=e^{-\tfrac{T}{2}}
.

(i)

If
g\geqslant 3
and
T\geqslant 0
, then the following convergences hold:

\lim_{N\to\infty}Z_{N}^{-}(g,T)=Z_{N}^{{}^{\prime}-}(g,T)=1.

(ii)

If
g=2
and
T>0
, then the following convergences hold:

\lim_{N\to\infty}Z_{N}^{-}(2,T)=\lim_{N\to\infty}Z_{N}^{{}^{\prime}-}(2,T)=\frac{1}{\phi(q^{2})}.

1.3
Comparison with other results

In our setting, we only consider the case of compact connected closed surfaces with a fixed area
T
. In the orientable cases, this was already studied by Gross alone
[
10
]
as well as with Taylor
[
12
]
: they found some results that we generalize here (see Section
2.2
for more details). The limit given in Theorem
1.1
for
g=1
is also mentioned without proof in
[
6
, eq.(3.2)]
, and the limit for
g>1
is in adequation with a result by Rusakov
[
19
]
. Let us remark that Rusakov affirmed that there is a nonzero free energy in the case of the torus, which is contradicted by the point
(ii)
of Theorem
1.1
. The asymptotic behaviour of the partition function on the sphere is very different from the higher genus surfaces, and needs more analytical tools. Its free energy was computed rigorously by Boutet de Monvel and Shcherbina
[
2
]
and later by Lévy and Maïda
[
17
]
, as well as Dahlqvist and Norris
[
4
]
; in particular, Lévy and Maïda proved a phase transition conjectured by Douglas and Kazakov
[
5
]
. We do not consider this case because it needs different tools than the ones we use. We also leave aside the case of a non-orientable surface of genus
1
, which is homeomorphic to the projective plane, because our tools do not provide any concluding result; we expect it to be more closely related to the case of the sphere, because the dimensions of the irreducible representations are raised to a positive power.

Although we do not consider surfaces with boundaries, there are plenty of works, in particular in physics, devoted to the partition function on a cylinder. For instance, Gross and Matytsin
[
11
]
conjectured that there might be the same kind of phase transition for the cylinder with fixed boundary conditions as the one happening for the sphere. However, they used non-rigorous techniques leading to the asymptotic estimation of irreducible characters of
{\mathrm{SU}}(N)
for which Tate and Zelditch
[
26
]
exhibited a counterexample. Zelditch then obtained in
[
30
]
a different result, using the so-called MacDonald identities, and computed the free energy on a cylinder with area
\tfrac{T}{N}
. In the mathematical literature, Guionnet and Maïda
[
13
]
developed some character expansion techniques that applied in this setting. Note here that the scaling regime is different from ours, and it might be enlightening to see how the limits of partition functions change when the area depends on
N
as in the works of Zelditch or Guionnet–Maïda.

1.4
General remarks

Before diving into the proofs of Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
, let us state a few facts that we find interesting around these Theorems.

•

The limit of the partition function in the unitary case for
g\geqslant 2
is the common value of
Z_{1}(g,T)
for all
g\geqslant 0
. Indeed, the irreducible representations of
{\mathrm{U}}(1)
are indexed by integers
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, and as
{\mathrm{U}}(1)
is abelian, they all have dimension 1. Moreover, the Casimir number
c_{2}(n)
is simply equal to
n^{2}
, therefore the partition function
Z_{1}(g,T)
can be written

\displaystyle Z_{1}(g,T)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{-\frac{T}{2}n^{2}}=\theta(q)

as expected. It could also be said that the limiting value of the partition functions
Z^{\prime}_{N}(g,T)
is also the value
Z^{\prime}_{1}(g,T)
, understood as the partition function associated with the trivial group
{\mathrm{SU}}(1)
, with a unique irreducible representation of dimension
1
and Casimir number
0
.

•

In the case of orientable surfaces, it appears that the limits of
Z_{N}
and
Z^{\prime}_{N}
always differ from one factor, which is actually
Z_{1}(g,T)
. We can summarize this asymptotic factorization as follows:

(8)

\lim_{N\to\infty}Z_{N}(g,T)=\lim_{N\to\infty}Z_{1}(g,T)Z^{\prime}_{N}(g,T).

•

Numerical simulations suggest that for all
g\geqslant 2
and all
T\geqslant 0
, the sequences
(Z_{N}(g,T))_{N\geqslant 2}
and
(Z^{\prime}_{N}(g,T))_{N\geqslant 2}
might be non-increasing. This would be an interesting fact, that we are not yet able to establish.

•

Using the Jacobi triple product formula

\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}q^{n^{2}}=\prod_{m=1}^{\infty}(1-q^{2m})(1+q^{2m-1})^{2},

the limit of
Z_{N}(1,T)
can be rewritten as an infinite product:

\lim_{N\to\infty}Z_{N}(1,T)=\prod_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{(1+q^{m})(1+q^{2m-1})^{2}}{1-q^{m}}.

It does not particularly enlightens the nature of the limit but it makes it at least easier to approximate numerically.

2.1
Orientable surfaces of genus
g\geqslant 2

The special unitary case

We will start by proving Theorem
1.1
.(i) in the special unitary case. Let us first reduce the problem to the case where
T=0
and
g=2
.

Lemma 2.1
.

For all
g\geqslant 0
, all
T\geqslant 0
, and all
N\geqslant 1
, we have

1\leqslant Z^{\prime}_{N}(g,T)\leqslant Z^{\prime}_{N}(2,0).

It follows from this lemma that the special unitary case of Theorem
1.1
.(i) is implied by the assertion

(9)

\lim_{N\to\infty}Z^{\prime}_{N}(2,0)=1,

which we will prove in this section.

Proof of Lemma
2.1
.

The
N
-tuple
(0,\ldots,0)
has dimension
1
and Casimir number
0
. Thus, it contributes
1
to the partition function
Z^{\prime}_{N}(g,T)
, which explains the first inequality. The second inequality is an immediate consequence of the fact that all Casimir numbers are non-negative, and that all dimensions are positive integers.
∎

Our goal is now to prove (
9
). We will deduce it from the following fact about Witten zeta functions.

Proposition 2.2
.

For all real
s>1
, one has

\sup_{N\geqslant 1}\zeta_{{\mathfrak{su}}(N)}(s)=\sup_{N\geqslant 1}\sum_{\lambda\in\widehat{\mathrm{SU}}(N)}d_{\lambda}^{-s}<\infty.

More precisely,

\lim_{N\to\infty}\zeta_{{\mathfrak{su}}(N)}(s)=1\ \text{ and }\ \lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\in\widehat{\mathrm{SU}}(N)\\
\lambda\neq(0,\ldots,0)\end{subarray}}d_{\lambda}^{-s}=0.

The proof of this proposition relies on three lemmas.

Lemma 2.3
.

For all
s>1
and all
N\geqslant 1
, one has

(10)

\sum_{\lambda\in\widehat{\mathrm{SU}}(N)}d_{\lambda}^{-s}\leqslant\prod_{k=1}^{N-1}\sum_{n\geqslant k}\binom{n}{k}^{-s}.

Proof.

Let us choose
s>1
and
N\geqslant 1
. In the left-hand side of (
10
), which is a sum over
\lambda_{1}\geqslant\ldots\lambda_{N}\geqslant 0
, let us make the change of variables

m_{1}=\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}+1,\ldots,m_{N-1}=\lambda_{N-1}-\lambda_{N}+1.

The new variables
m_{1},\ldots,m_{N-1}
are now independent, and positive. Using (
1
), we find

(11)

d_{\lambda}=\prod_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant N}\frac{m_{i}+\ldots+m_{j-1}}{j-i},

so that

\displaystyle\sum_{\lambda_{1}\geqslant\ldots\geqslant\lambda_{N}=0}d_{\lambda}^{-s}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m_{1},\ldots,m_{N-1}\geqslant 1}\ \prod_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant N}\frac{(j-i)^{s}}{(m_{i}+\ldots+m_{j-1})^{s}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m_{1},\ldots,m_{N-1}\geqslant 1}\ \prod_{k=1}^{N-1}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{(k-i+1)^{s}}{(m_{i}+\ldots+m_{k})^{s}}

\displaystyle(k=j-1)

Since
m_{i}+\ldots+m_{k-1}\geqslant k-i
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{\lambda_{1}\geqslant\ldots\geqslant\lambda_{N}=0}d_{\lambda}^{-s}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{m_{1},\ldots,m_{N-1}\geqslant 1}\prod_{k=1}^{N-1}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{(k-i+1)^{s}}{(m_{k}+k-i)^{s}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m_{1},\ldots,m_{N-1}\geqslant 1}\prod_{k=1}^{N-1}\binom{k+m_{k}-1}{k}^{-s}

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=1}^{N-1}\sum_{n\geqslant k}\binom{n}{k}^{-s},

which is the announced upper bound.
∎

Lemma 2.4
.

For all real
s>1
,

\sum_{k\geqslant 1}\sum_{n>k}\binom{n}{k}^{-s}<\infty.

Proof.

We use the fact that for
k
between
2
and
n-2
, the inequality
\binom{n}{k}\geqslant\binom{n}{2}
holds. Thus,

\sum_{k\geqslant 1}\sum_{n>k}\binom{n}{k}^{-s}\leqslant 2^{-s}+\sum_{n=3}^{\infty}\bigg(\frac{2}{n^{s}}+(n-3)\frac{2^{s}}{n^{s}(n-1)^{s}}\bigg),

which is indeed finite for
s>1
.
∎

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
\lambda
be an element of
\widehat{\mathrm{SU}}(N)
. If
\lambda=(0,\ldots,0)
, then
d_{\lambda}=1
. Otherwise,
d_{\lambda}\geqslant N
.

Proof.

Let us use again the variables
m_{1},\ldots,m_{N-1}
introduced in the proof of Lemma
2.3
. It is manifest on the expression (
11
) of
d_{\lambda}
that this dimension is increasing in each of the variables
m_{1},\ldots,m_{r}
. The case where each of these variables is equal to
1
is the case where
\lambda=(0,\ldots,0)
and
d_{\lambda}=1
. Any other irreducible representation has a dimension that is at least equal to the dimension of one of the representations

\lambda_{1}=(1,0,\ldots,0),\lambda_{2}=(1,1,0,\ldots,0),\ldots,\lambda_{N-1}=(1,\ldots,1,0).

These representations, which are the exterior powers of the standard representation of
{\mathrm{SU}}(N)
, have dimensions

d_{\lambda_{k}}=\binom{N}{k}\geqslant N,\ k\in\{1,\ldots,N-1\}.

Thus,
d_{\lambda}\geqslant N
, as expected.
∎

We can now prove Proposition
2.2
.

Proof of Proposition
2.2
.

The bound obtained in Lemma
2.3
can be rewritten as

\sum_{\lambda_{1}\geqslant\ldots\geqslant\lambda_{N}=0}d_{\lambda}^{-s}\leqslant\prod_{k=1}^{N-1}\left[1+\sum_{n>k}\binom{n}{k}^{-s}\right]\leqslant\exp\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{n>k}\binom{n}{k}^{-s}

and this last bound, independent of
N
, is finite by Lemma
2.4
. This proves the first assertion.

For the second, let us introduce a real
s^{\prime}\in(1,s)
and use Lemma
2.5
. We find

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\in\widehat{\mathrm{SU}}(N)\\
\lambda\neq(0,\ldots,0)\end{subarray}}d_{\lambda}^{-s}\leqslant N^{-(s-s^{\prime})}\sum_{\lambda\in\widehat{\mathrm{SU}}(N)}d_{\lambda}^{-s^{\prime}},

which tends to
0
as
N
tends to infinity.
∎

In order to prove (
9
), we need a last piece of information about the dimensions of the irreducible representations of
{\mathrm{SU}}(N)
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.(i) in the special unitary case.

On one hand, Lemma
2.1
implies that
Z^{\prime}_{N}(2,0)\geqslant 1
. On the other hand,

Z^{\prime}_{N}(2,0)=\sum_{\lambda\in\widehat{\mathrm{SU}}(N)}d_{\lambda}^{-2}=1+\sum_{\lambda\neq(0,\ldots,0)}d_{\lambda}^{-2}.

Using Lemma
2.5
, we find

Z^{\prime}_{N}(2,0)\leqslant 1+N^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{\lambda\neq(0,\ldots,0)}d_{\lambda}^{-\frac{3}{2}}\leqslant 1+N^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sup_{N\geqslant 1}\sum_{\lambda\in\widehat{\mathrm{SU}}(N)}d_{\lambda}^{-\frac{3}{2}}.

Thanks to Proposition
2.2
, this implies

\limsup_{N\to\infty}Z^{\prime}_{N}(2,0)\leqslant 1

and this concludes the proof of (
9
), hence of Theorem
1.1
.(i) in the special unitary case.
∎

The unitary case

We treat the unitary case of Theorem
1.1
.(i) using our understanding of the special unitary case, and the bijection

\displaystyle\Phi:\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}\widehat{{\mathrm{SU}}}(N)\times{\mathbb{Z}}&\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{\sim}}&\widehat{{\mathrm{U}}}(N)\\
(\lambda,n)&\longmapsto&\lambda+n=(\lambda_{1}+n,\ldots,\lambda_{N}+n).\end{array}\right.

We will keep throughout this section the notation
\lambda
for an element of
\widehat{\mathrm{SU}}(N)
,
n
for an element of
{\mathbb{Z}}
,
\lambda+n
for the corresponding element of
\widehat{\mathrm{U}}(N)
, and
|\lambda|=\lambda_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{N}
. The first observation is the following.

Lemma 2.6
.

We have the equality

c_{2}(\lambda+n)=c_{2}^{\prime}(\lambda)+\bigg(n+\frac{|\lambda|}{N}\bigg)^{2}.

Proof.

The proof is a simple verification using the definitions (
2
) and (
5
) of
c_{2}
and
c_{2}^{\prime}
.
∎

It is the contribution of the highest weights of the form
0+n=(n,\ldots,n)
which produces the Jacobi theta function in the unitary part of Theorem
1.1
.(i). We will prove that the contribution of all other elements of
\widehat{\mathrm{U}}(N)
vanishes in the large
N
limit.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.(i) in the unitary case.

Let us consider
g\geqslant 2
and
T>0
, and set
q=e^{-\tfrac{T}{2}}
. We split the partition function
Z_{N}(g,T)
into two parts

Z_{N}(g,T)=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}q^{n^{2}}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\in\widehat{\mathrm{SU}}(N)\\
\lambda\neq(0,\ldots,0)\end{subarray}}\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}q^{c_{2}(\lambda+n)}d_{\lambda+n}.

The first part corresponds to highest weights of the form
(n,\ldots,n)
, which have Casimir numbers
n^{2}
and dimension
1
, and is equal to
\theta(q)
. The second part is the contribution of all the other highest weights. To compute it, we observe that
d_{\lambda+n}=d_{\lambda}
and we use Lemma
2.6
.
We find

0\leqslant Z_{N}(g,T)-\theta(q)\leqslant\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\in\widehat{\mathrm{SU}}(N)\\
\lambda\neq(0,\ldots,0)\end{subarray}}\bigg(\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}q^{(n+|\lambda|/N)^{2}}\bigg)q^{c_{2}^{\prime}(\lambda)}d_{\lambda}^{2-2g}.

The sum between the brackets is bounded independently of
N
, for example, in a very elementary way, by
C=1+\theta(q)
. Hence, the right-hand side is bounded by

C\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\in\widehat{\mathrm{SU}}(N)\\
\lambda\neq(0,\ldots,0)\end{subarray}}d_{\lambda}^{2-2g}=C\big(\zeta_{{\mathfrak{su}}(N)}(2g-2)-1\big)

which, thanks to Proposition
2.2
, converges to
0
.
∎

2.2
The torus

Our proof of the convergence of the partition function when
g\geqslant 2
was based on our study of the dimensions of the irreducible representations of
{\mathrm{SU}}(N)
, expressed in Proposition
2.2
. A glance at (
3
) shows that when
g=1
, these dimensions do not appear anymore in the partition function, and to treat this case we need to use completely different estimates. In this section, we will prove that
Z_{N}(1,T)
still admits a finite limit for

Before entering the technical details, let us explain the idea of the proof of Theorem

The main result of this section is the following.

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
\alpha
and

be two partitions of respective lengths

c_{2}(\lambda(\alpha,\beta,n))=|\alpha|+|\beta|+n^{2}+\frac{2}{N}\big(K(\alpha)+K(\beta)+n(|\alpha|-|\beta|)\big)

in the unitary case, and

(14)

in the special unitary case.

Proof.

Let us start with the unitary case. Using the definition of Casimir number and the definition of
\lambda(\alpha,\beta,n)
, we obtain

\displaystyle Nc_{2}(\lambda(\alpha,\beta,n))=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{r}\alpha_{i}^{2}+\sum_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant r}(\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{j})+2n|\alpha|+\sum_{i=1}^{s}\beta_{i}^{2}+\sum_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant s}(\beta_{i}-\beta_{j})-2n|\beta|

\displaystyle+|\alpha|(N-r-s)+|\beta|(N-r-s)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant i\leqslant r\\
1\leqslant j\leqslant s\end{subarray}}(\alpha_{i}+\beta_{j})+Nc^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle N(|\alpha|+|\beta|+n^{2})+2n(|\alpha|-|\beta|)+\sum_{i=1}^{r}\alpha_{i}^{2}+\sum_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant r}(\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{j})-r|\alpha|

\displaystyle\hskip 155.92097pt+\sum_{i=1}^{s}\beta_{i}^{2}+\sum_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant s}(\beta_{i}-\beta_{j})-s|\beta|.

On the other hand,

K(\alpha)=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\frac{\alpha_{i}(\alpha_{i}+1)}{2}-i\alpha_{i}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{r}\alpha_{i}^{2}+\sum_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant r}(\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{j})-r|\alpha|\right)

and we find (
13
) as announced.

Concerning the special unitary case, we simply need to subtract from

, which leads to

\displaystyle c^{\prime}_{2}(\lambda(\alpha,\beta))=

\displaystyle c_{2}(\lambda(\alpha,\beta,\beta_{1}))-\frac{1}{N^{2}}\left(|\alpha|-|\beta|+N\beta_{1}\right)^{2}

from which (

The special unitary case

In our treatment of the special unitary case, we want to adopt a systematic way of writing a highest weight of
{\mathrm{SU}}(N)
under the form
\lambda_{N}(\alpha,\beta)
. We do this in a way that depends on the parity of
N
, but that in any case rests on the observation that for all
M_{1},M_{2}\geqslant 0
, the map

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathrm{SU}}(M_{1}+1)\times\widehat{\mathrm{SU}}(M_{2}+1)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\longrightarrow}}\widehat{\mathrm{SU}}(M_{1}+M_{2}+1)

\displaystyle(\alpha,\beta)

\displaystyle\longmapsto\lambda_{M_{1}+M_{2}+1}(\alpha,\beta)

is a bijection.

In the case where
N
is odd, equal to
2M+1
, we take
M_{1}=M_{2}=M
. When
N=2M
is even, and positive, we choose
M_{1}=M-1
and
M_{2}=M
. In this section, we will always write highest weights of
{\mathrm{SU}}(N)
as
\lambda(\alpha,\beta)
, and this will always refer to the decomposition just described.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
.(ii) will rely on two estimates of the Casimir number: one that helps proving the convergence of the sum of
q^{c^{\prime}_{2}(\lambda)}
over almost flat highest weights
\lambda
to the expected limit, and one that helps controlling the sum over remaining highest weights.

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
\lambda=\lambda(\alpha,\beta)\in\widehat{{\mathrm{SU}}}(N)
. Set
k=|\alpha|+|\beta|
. Then the following inequalities hold:

(15)

k-\frac{k^{2}}{N}\leqslant c^{\prime}_{2}(\lambda(\alpha,\beta))\leqslant k+\frac{k^{2}}{N}+\frac{k^{2}}{N^{2}},

(16)

\frac{k}{2}\leqslant c^{\prime}_{2}(\lambda(\alpha,\beta)).

Proof.

We start from the expression of
c^{\prime}_{2}(\lambda(\alpha,\beta))
given by (
14
). The point is to bound
K(\alpha)
and
K(\beta)
.

The list of the contents of the boxes of
\alpha
taken row after row and from left to right in each row (as on the left of Fig.
4
) is a sequence
x_{1},\ldots,x_{|\alpha|}
such that
|x_{i}|\leqslant i-1
for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,|\alpha|\}
. It follows that

-|\alpha|(|\alpha|-1)\leqslant 2K(\alpha)\leqslant|\alpha|(|\alpha|-1).

This implies immediately

2|K(\alpha)+K(\beta)|\leqslant k^{2},

and (
15
), after observing that
0\leqslant(|\alpha|-|\beta|)^{2}\leqslant(|\alpha|+|\beta|)^{2}=k^{2}
.

Figure 4:
Two ways of listing the contents of the boxes of the diagram
(3,3,2,1)
.

Let us turn to the proof of (
16
). We will establish a different lower bound on
K(\alpha)
and
K(\beta)
. For this, let us list the contents of the boxes of
\alpha
, now taken column after column and from top to bottom in each column (as on the right of Fig.
4
). It is now a sequence
x_{1},\ldots,x_{|\alpha|}
of integers that along each column of
\alpha
decreases by
1
at each step, and at each change of column jumps to a positive integer. The crucial point is that the height of the columns of
\alpha
is bounded by the integer that we called
M_{1}
at the beginning of this section, and that is in any case not greater than
\frac{N}{2}
. The contribution of each column is thus bounded below by
-\frac{N}{4}
times the number of boxes in this column. It follows that

K(\alpha)\geqslant-\frac{N}{4}|\alpha|,

and a similar argument holds for
\beta
. The result follows again from (
14
).
∎

Proof of theorem
1.1
.(ii) in the special unitary case.

Let us fix a real
\gamma\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
. Let us split the set of highest weights of
{\mathrm{SU}}(N)
in four disjoint subsets:

\displaystyle\Lambda_{N,1}

\displaystyle=\{\lambda(\alpha,\beta):|\alpha|\leqslant N^{\gamma},|\beta|\leqslant N^{\gamma}\},

\displaystyle\Lambda_{N,2}

\displaystyle=\{\lambda(\alpha,\beta):|\alpha|>N^{\gamma},|\beta|\leqslant N^{\gamma}\},

\displaystyle\Lambda_{N,3}

\displaystyle=\{\lambda(\alpha,\beta):|\alpha|\leqslant N^{\gamma},|\beta|>N^{\gamma}\},

\displaystyle\Lambda_{N,4}

\displaystyle=\{\lambda(\alpha,\beta):|\alpha|>N^{\gamma},|\beta|>N^{\gamma}\}.

For each
i\in\{1,2,3,4\}
, we define

S^{\prime}_{N,i}=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{N,i}}q^{c_{2}^{\prime}(\lambda)},

so that

Z^{\prime}_{N}(1,T)=S^{\prime}_{N,1}+S^{\prime}_{N,2}+S^{\prime}_{N,3}+S^{\prime}_{N,4}.

The set
\Lambda_{N,1}
is the set of highest weights that we think of as being almost flat, and we will now prove, in a first step, that they bring the only non-zero contribution in the limit where
N
tends to infinity.

Let
\lambda(\alpha,\beta)
be an element of
\Lambda_{N,1}
. Then thanks to (
15
), we have

(17)

|\alpha|+|\beta|-4N^{2\gamma-1}\leqslant c^{\prime}_{2}(\lambda(\alpha,\beta))\leqslant|\alpha|+|\beta|+4N^{2\gamma-1}+4N^{2\gamma-2}.

For
N
large enough, any partition of an integer not greater than
N^{\gamma}
has less than
\frac{N}{2}
positive parts. Thus, if
\alpha
and
\beta
are any two such partitions, the highest weight
\lambda_{N}(\alpha,\beta)
is well defined, and belongs to
\Lambda_{N,1}
. Thus, for
N
large enough,

S^{\prime}_{N,1}=\sum_{|\alpha|,|\beta|\leqslant N^{\gamma}}q^{c^{\prime}_{2}(\lambda(\alpha,\beta))}.

From (
17
), we deduce that

q^{4N^{2\gamma-1}+4N^{2\gamma-2}}\sum_{|\alpha|,|\beta|\leqslant N^{\gamma}}q^{|\alpha|+|\beta|}\leqslant S^{\prime}_{N,1}\leqslant q^{-4N^{2\gamma-1}}\sum_{|\alpha|,|\beta|\leqslant N^{\gamma}}q^{|\alpha|+|\beta|}.

Since
2\gamma-1
is negative, the powers of
q
in front of the sums on either side tend to
1
as
N
tends to infinity. On the other hand, the sum over
\alpha
and
\beta
tends, as
N
tends to infinity, to the square of the generating function of partitions. Hence,

\lim_{N\to\infty}S^{\prime}_{N,1}=\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{|\alpha|,|\beta|\leqslant N^{\gamma}}q^{|\alpha|+|\beta|}=\bigg(\sum_{\alpha}q^{|\alpha|}\bigg)^{2}=\prod_{m=1}^{\infty}(1-q^{m})^{-2}.

In a second step, we prove that the three other contributions to
Z^{\prime}_{N}(1,T)
vanish as
N
tends to infinity. For this, we use (
16
). Let us treat the case of
S^{\prime}_{N,2}
, the case of
S^{\prime}_{N,3}
being perfectly similar, and the case of
S^{\prime}_{N,4}
even simpler. We have

\displaystyle 0\leqslant S^{\prime}_{N,2}\leqslant\sum_{|\alpha|\leqslant N^{\gamma},|\beta|>N^{\gamma}}q^{\frac{1}{2}(|\alpha|+|\beta|)}\leqslant\sum_{\alpha}q^{\frac{1}{2}|\alpha|}\sum_{|\beta|>N^{\gamma}}q^{\frac{1}{2}|\beta|}=\sum_{\alpha}q^{\frac{1}{2}|\alpha|}\sum_{k>N^{\gamma}}p(k)q^{\frac{k}{2}}.

The first sum is finite, and the second, as a remainder of a convergent series, tends to
0
as
N
tends to infinity. This concludes the proof.
∎

The unitary case

The proof of Theorem
1.1
.(ii) in the unitary case will rely on the same tools as the special unitary case, that is, the use of almost flat highest weights, combined with the bijection
\Phi:(\lambda,n)\mapsto\lambda+n

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.(ii) in the unitary case.

Let
\lambda(\alpha,\beta)
be an element of
\widehat{{\mathrm{SU}}}(N)
. Using Lemma
2.6
and Proposition
2.8

so that we can write, up to a change of index

(18)

Z_{N}(1,T)=\sum_{\lambda(\alpha,\beta)\in\widehat{{\mathrm{SU}}}(N)}\left(\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}q^{\left(n+\frac{|\alpha|-|\beta|}{N}\right)^{2}}\right)q^{c^{\prime}_{2}(\lambda(\alpha,\beta))}.

The main difference with the case of
{\mathrm{SU}}(N)
is the sum over

between the brackets, and we will need to control it in order to get the convergence.

Let
\gamma\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
, and the subsets
(\Lambda_{N,i})_{1\leqslant i\leqslant 4}
of
\widehat{{\mathrm{SU}}}(N)
as in the special unitary case. We define, for

and we obtain the following decomposition:

Z_{N}(1,T)=S_{N,1}+S_{N,2}+S_{N,3}+S_{N,4}.

\lambda(\alpha,\beta)
be an element of
\Lambda_{N,1}
. From the fact that
\big||\alpha|-|\beta|\big|\leqslant|\alpha|+|\beta|\leqslant 2N^{\gamma}

(19)

S_{N,1}=\sum_{|\alpha|,|\beta|\leqslant N^{\gamma}}\left(\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}q^{\left(n+\frac{|\alpha|-|\beta|}{N}\right)^{2}}\right)q^{c^{\prime}_{2}(\lambda(\alpha,\beta))};

3
Non-orientable surfaces

We now turn to the study of non-orientable surfaces. Let us recall that any such surface can be constructed as the connected sum of
g
projective planes. In order to estimate the large
N
asymptotics of its associated partition function, we need to compute the Frobenius–Schur indicator associated to any highest weight.

3.1
Frobenius–Schur indicator of a highest weight of
{\mathrm{U}}(N)
or
{\mathrm{SU}}(N)

Let
(\rho,V)
a complex finite-dimensional representation of a compact group
G
of character
\chi_{V}
. The Frobenius–Schur indicator

\iota_{\chi_{V}}=\int_{G}\chi_{V}(g^{2})\mathrm{d}g

appears in particular in the study of symmetric and alternating parts of the tensor product representation

V\otimes V=\mathrm{Sym}^{2}V\oplus{\bigwedge}^{2}V.

Indeed, straightforward computations involving the canonical bases of
V\otimes V
,
\text{Sym}^{2}V
and
{\bigwedge}^{2}V
yield

(22)

\chi_{V}(g^{2})=\chi_{V}(g)^{2}-2\chi_{{\bigwedge}^{2}V}(g)

and

(23)

\chi_{V}(g^{2})=2\chi_{\text{Sym}^{2}V}(g)-\chi_{V}(g)^{2}.

Furthermore,
\rho
is said to be:

(i)

Real
if it exists a symmetric
G
-invariant nondegenerate bilinear form;

(ii)

Quaternionic
if it exists a skew-symmetric
G
-invariant nondegenerate bilinear form;

(iii)

Complex
if there is no
G
-invariant nondegenerate bilinear form.

The value of
\iota_{\chi_{V}}
is actually based on this classification, as stated by the following Proposition.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
(\rho,V)
be a complex finite-dimensional representation of a compact group
G
, with character
\chi
. Its Frobenius–Schur indicator satisfies the following equation:

(24)

\iota_{\chi_{V}}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\text{ if }\langle\chi_{\text{triv}},\chi_{\text{Sym}^{2}V}\rangle=1,\ i.e.\ \rho\text{ is real;}\\
-1&\text{ if }\langle\chi_{\text{triv}},\chi_{\bigwedge^{2}V}\rangle=1,\ i.e.\ \rho\text{ is quaternionic;}\\
0&\text{ otherwise},\ i.e.\ \rho\text{ is complex.}\end{array}\right.

Proof.

If we sum up Equations (
22
) and (
23
), we have

2\iota_{\chi_{V}}=\int_{G}2\chi_{\text{Sym}^{2}V}(g)\mathrm{d}g-2\int_{G}\chi_{\bigwedge^{2}V}(g)\mathrm{d}g=2\left(\langle\chi_{\text{triv}},\chi_{\text{Sym}^{2}V}\rangle-\langle\chi_{\text{triv}},\chi_{\bigwedge^{2}V}\rangle\right).

Then, as
\text{Sym}^{2}V
and
\bigwedge^{2}V
are in direct sum, it appears that there are 3 cases:

•

\langle\chi_{\text{triv}},\chi_{\text{Sym}^{2}V}\rangle=1
and
\langle\chi_{\text{triv}},\chi_{\bigwedge^{2}V}\rangle=0
, which gives
\iota_{\chi}=1
;

•

\langle\chi_{\text{triv}},\chi_{\text{Sym}^{2}V}\rangle=0
and
\langle\chi_{\text{triv}},\chi_{\bigwedge^{2}V}\rangle=1
, which gives
\iota_{\chi}=-1
;

•

\langle\chi_{\text{triv}},\chi_{\text{Sym}^{2}V}\rangle=0
and
\langle\chi_{\text{triv}},\chi_{\bigwedge^{2}V}\rangle=0
, which gives
\iota_{\chi}=0
.

∎

As a consequence of this result, computing the Frobenius–Schur indicator of an irreducible representation of
{\mathrm{U}}(N)
or
{\mathrm{SU}}(N)
can be done by determining whether the representation is real, complex or quaternionic. The following theorem gives a classification depending on the highest weight.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
\lambda\in\widehat{{\mathrm{SU}}}(N)
be a highest weight and
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
be an integer.

(i)

If
N=2M+1
is odd and
(\alpha,\beta)\in\widehat{{\mathrm{SU}}}(M+1)^{2}
such that
\lambda=\lambda(\alpha,\beta)
, then an irreducible representation of
{\mathrm{SU}}(N)
with highest weight
\lambda
is complex iff
\alpha\neq\beta
. Moreover, an irreducible representation of
{\mathrm{U}}(N)
with highest weight
\lambda+n
is real if
\alpha=\beta
and

, otherwise it is complex.

(ii)

If
N=2M
is even,
(\alpha,\beta)\in\widehat{{\mathrm{SU}}}(M)\times\widehat{{\mathrm{SU}}}(M+1)
such that
\lambda=\lambda(\alpha,\beta)
, then set
\tilde{\beta}=(\beta_{1}-\beta_{M},\ldots,\beta_{M-1}-\beta_{M},0)\in\widehat{{\mathrm{SU}}}(M)
, and an irreducible representation of
{\mathrm{SU}}(N)
with highest weight
\lambda
is complex iff
\alpha\neq\tilde{\beta}
. Moreover, an irreducible representation of
{\mathrm{U}}(N)
with highest weight
\lambda+n
is real if
\alpha=\tilde{\beta}
and
n=-\alpha_{1}
, otherwise it is complex.

(iii)

If
N
is large enough and
\lambda\in\Lambda_{N,1}
is an almost flat highest weight, then there is no quaternionic irreducible representation of
{\mathrm{SU}}(N)
with highest weight
\lambda
.

Note that when
\lambda=\lambda(\alpha,\beta)
and
\alpha=\beta
or
\alpha=\tilde{\beta}
(depending on the parity of
N
), the integer
\lambda_{1}=\alpha_{1}+\beta_{1}=2\alpha_{1}
is always even, so that the condition
n=\tfrac{\lambda_{1}}{2}
makes sense. The main point of this theorem is that highest weights that are not symmetric are complex and therefore do not contribute to the non-orientable partition function because their Frobenius–Schur indicator vanishes. We can also notice that quaternionic representations of
{\mathrm{SU}}(N)
with almost flat highest weight do not appear in the large
N
scale, and that the partition function becomes a sum of nonnegative terms.

The proof of Theorem
3.2
will rely on two propositions.

Proposition 3.3

(
[
9
]
, Prop.26.24)
.

Let
\lambda=(\lambda_{1}\geqslant\cdots\geqslant\lambda_{N}=0)
be a highest weight of
{\mathrm{SU}}(N)
. Let
m_{i}=\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{i+1}\in{\mathbb{N}}
for every
i\in\{1,\ldots,N-1\}
. An irreducible representation of
{\mathrm{SU}}(N)
with highest weight
\lambda
is:

•

Complex if there exists
i
such that
m_{i}\neq m_{N-i}
;

•

Real if for all
i

m_{i}=m_{N-i}
and one of the following cases is satisfied:

–

N
is odd;

–

N=4k
for a given
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
;

–

N=4k+2
for a given
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
m_{2k+1}
is even;

•

Quaternionic if for all
i

m_{i}=m_{N-i}
,
N=4k+2
for a given
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
m_{2k+1}
is odd.

Proposition 3.4

(
[
3
]
,§5.2)
.

Let
(\pi,V)
be an irreducible representation of
{\mathrm{U}}(N)
of highest weight
\lambda
. If it is self-conjugate, that is,
\lambda_{i}=-\lambda_{N+1-i}
for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant N
, then it is real, otherwise it is complex.

Proof of Theorem
3.2
.

(i)
and
(ii)
are direct consequences of Prop.
3.3
and
3.4
.
(iii)
follows from the fact that for
N=4k+2
with
k
large enough, if
\lambda=\lambda(\alpha,\beta)
is almost flat, then there is no ‘jump’ between
\lambda_{2k+1}
and
\lambda_{2k+2}
, thus
m_{2k+1}=0
is always even.
∎

3.2
Non-orientable surfaces of genus
g\geqslant 3

The special unitary case

The proof of Theorem
1.2
.(i) will be based on the same reasoning as for orientable surfaces of genus
g\geqslant 2
, that is, using Proposition
2.2
to show that the contribution of all other highest weights than
(0,\ldots,0)
vanish in the large
N
limit. However, the case of non-orientable surfaces with
g=3
will need a finer control, as we will see later. In particular, for even values of
N
and
g=3
the following inequality is needed.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
N=2M
be an integer,
\alpha\in\widehat{{\mathrm{SU}}}(M)
and
\beta\in\widehat{{\mathrm{SU}}}(M+1)
be two highest weights. We define
\lambda(\alpha,\beta)
as in Section
2.2
, and
\tilde{\beta}=(\beta_{1}-\beta_{M},\ldots,\beta_{M-1}-\beta_{M},0)\in\widehat{{\mathrm{SU}}}(N)
. Then,

d_{\lambda(\alpha,\beta)}\geqslant\left(1+\frac{\beta_{M}}{M}\right)^{M}d_{\alpha}d_{\tilde{\beta}}.

Proof.

Using Equation (
1
) and the fact that

\lambda(\alpha,\beta)=(\alpha_{1}+\beta_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{M-1}+\beta_{1},\beta_{1},\beta_{M}-\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{2}-\beta_{1},0),

it is clear that
d_{\lambda(\alpha,\beta)}\geqslant d_{\alpha}d_{\beta}
. Moreover,

\displaystyle d_{\beta}=

\displaystyle\prod_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant M+1}\left(1+\frac{\beta_{i}-\beta_{j}}{j-i}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{M}\left(\frac{\beta_{i}}{M+1-i}\right)d_{\tilde{\beta}}

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\beta_{M}}{M}\right)^{M}d_{\tilde{\beta}}.

Combining both inequalities gives the expected result.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.(i).

The highest weight
(0,\ldots,0)
is associated to the trivial representation, which is real by Proposition
3.1
and has dimension
1
and Casimir number
0
. We can then rewrite

Z_{N}^{{}^{\prime}-}(g,T)=1+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\in\widehat{{\mathrm{SU}}}(N)\\
\lambda\neq(0,\ldots,0)\end{subarray}}q^{c^{\prime}_{2}(\lambda)}d_{\lambda}^{2-g}(\iota_{\lambda})^{g},

and the remaining sum can be bounded as follows:

\bigg|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\in\widehat{{\mathrm{SU}}}(N)\\
\lambda\neq(0,\ldots,0)\end{subarray}}q^{c^{\prime}_{2}(\lambda)}d_{\lambda}^{2-g}\iota_{\lambda}\bigg|\leqslant\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\in\widehat{{\mathrm{SU}}}(N)\\
\lambda\neq(0,\ldots,0)\end{subarray}}q^{c^{\prime}_{2}(\lambda)}d_{\lambda}^{2-g}.

If
g\geqslant 4
, then the right-hand side has been proved to converge to
0
as
N\to\infty
in the proof of Theorem
1.1
, hence the result follows.

Now, if
g=3
, we need to refine the analysis in order to get the convergence. From Theorem
3.2
, it appears that
\lambda\in{\mathrm{SU}}(N)
contributes to the partition function iff it is symmetric. The case
N=2M+1
is easier to prove, so we start with it. As
\iota_{\lambda}=0
if
\lambda
is associated with a complex representation, we have

\displaystyle Z_{N}^{{}^{\prime}-}(3,T)=

\displaystyle 1+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\in\widehat{{\mathrm{SU}}}(N)\\
\lambda\neq(0,\ldots,0)\\
\lambda\text{ is symmetric}\end{subarray}}q^{c^{\prime}_{2}(\lambda)}d_{\lambda}^{-1}(\iota_{\lambda})^{3},

which means that

\displaystyle|Z_{N}^{{}^{\prime}-}(3,T)-1|=

\displaystyle\bigg|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\in\widehat{{\mathrm{SU}}}(M+1)\\
\alpha\neq(0,\ldots,0)\end{subarray}}q^{c^{\prime}_{2}(\lambda(\alpha,\alpha))}d_{\lambda(\alpha,\alpha)}^{-1}(\iota_{\lambda(\alpha,\alpha)})^{3}\bigg|

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\in\widehat{{\mathrm{SU}}}(M+1)\\
\alpha\neq(0,\ldots,0)\end{subarray}}q^{c^{\prime}_{2}(\lambda(\alpha,\alpha))}d_{\alpha}^{-2}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\zeta_{\mathfrak{su}(M)}(2).

Then, letting
M
tend to infinity and using Proposition
2.2
, we have indeed

\lim_{M\to\infty}Z_{2M+1}^{{}^{\prime}-}(3,T)=1.

Now consider
N=2M
. Let
\tilde{\beta}=(\beta_{1}-\beta_{M},\ldots,\beta_{M-1}-\beta_{M},0)
. Theorem
3.2
states that
\lambda(\alpha,\beta)
contributes to the partition function iff
\alpha=\tilde{\beta}
. It implies:

\displaystyle|Z_{N}^{{}^{\prime}-}(3,T)-1|=

\displaystyle\bigg|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(\alpha,\beta)\in\widehat{{\mathrm{SU}}}(M)\times\widehat{{\mathrm{SU}}}(M+1)\\
\alpha=\tilde{\beta}\end{subarray}}q^{c^{\prime}_{2}(\lambda(\alpha,\beta))}d_{\lambda(\alpha,\beta)}^{-1}(\iota_{\lambda(\alpha,\alpha)})^{3}\bigg|.

We can then apply Proposition
3.5
to get

\displaystyle|Z_{N}^{{}^{\prime}-}(3,T)-1|\leqslant

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\in\widehat{{\mathrm{SU}}}(M+1)\\
\alpha\neq(0,\ldots,0)\end{subarray}}\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\left(1+\frac{n}{M}\right)^{-M}d_{\alpha}^{-2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\left(1+\frac{n}{M}\right)^{-M}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\in\widehat{{\mathrm{SU}}}(M+1)\\
\alpha\neq(0,\ldots,0)\end{subarray}}d_{\alpha}^{-2}.

The first sum is bounded because
\left(1+\frac{n}{M}\right)^{-M}\leqslant e^{-n}
for any
n,M
, and the second one converges, following the same argument as in the case
N=2M+1
. We finally get

\lim_{M\to\infty}Z_{2M}^{{}^{\prime}-}(3,T)=1.

∎

The unitary case

As for the special unitary case, the proof of the unitary case for non-orientable surfaces of genus
g\geqslant 3
is similar to the one of orientable surfaces of genus
g\geqslant 2
. Indeed, the point is to show that only constant highest weights contribute to the large
N
limit.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.(i) in the unitary case.

Let us consider
g\geqslant 3
and
T>0
. The only constant highest weight of
{\mathrm{U}}(N)
corresponding to a non-complex irreducible representation is
(0,\ldots,0)
, and has Frobenius–Schur indicator equal to 1. We can then split the partition function
Z_{N}^{-}(g,T)
into two parts:

Z_{N}^{-}(g,T)=1+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\in\widehat{\mathrm{SU}}(N)\\
\lambda\neq(0,\ldots,0)\end{subarray}}\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}e^{-\frac{T}{2}c_{2}(\lambda+n)}d_{\lambda+n}^{2-g}\iota_{\lambda+n}^{g}.

Now, given
\lambda\in\widehat{{\mathrm{SU}}}(N)
and
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, we know that a necessary and sufficient condition for
\iota_{\lambda+n}
to be nonzero is that
\lambda_{1}=-2n
, therefore we have

\left|Z_{N}^{-}(g,T)-1\right|=\bigg|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\in\widehat{\mathrm{SU}}(N)\\
\lambda\neq(0,\ldots,0)\\
\lambda_{1}\text{ is even}\end{subarray}}q^{c_{2}(\lambda-\tfrac{\lambda_{1}}{2})}d_{\lambda}^{2-g}\bigg|\leqslant\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\in\widehat{\mathrm{SU}}(N)\\
\lambda\neq(0,\ldots,0)\end{subarray}}d_{\lambda}^{2-g}.

We are now in the same setting as in the special unitary case, and the convergence follows from the same arguments.
∎

3.3
The Klein bottle

The Klein bottle is the non-orientable equivalent to the torus, as we will see, in the sense that the dimension of the irreducible representations do not appear in the formula of the partition function. Hence, the proof of Theorem
1.2
.(ii) is using almost flat highest weights as well.

The special unitary case

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.(ii) in the special unitary case.

Let
\gamma\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
, and the subsets
(\Lambda_{N,i})_{1\leqslant i\leqslant 4}
of
\widehat{{\mathrm{SU}}}(N)
as in the case of the torus. We define, for
1\leqslant i\leqslant 4
,

S^{\prime}_{N,i}=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{N,i}}\iota_{\lambda(\alpha,\beta)}^{2}q^{c^{\prime}_{2}(\lambda)}=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{N,i}}q^{c^{\prime}_{2}(\lambda)},

and we obtain the following decomposition:

Z_{N}^{{}^{\prime}-}(1,T)=S^{\prime}_{N,1}+S^{\prime}_{N,2}+S^{\prime}_{N,3}+S^{\prime}_{N,4}.

Let
\lambda(\alpha,\beta)
be an element of
\Lambda_{N,1}
. We will discuss the case when
N
is even and the case when it is odd, and show that the subsequences
(Z_{2M}^{{}^{\prime}-})
and
(Z_{2M+1}^{{}^{\prime}-})
both converge to the same limit.

•

If
N=2M+1
, from Theorem
3.2
we know that
\iota_{\lambda(\alpha,\beta)}^{2}=1
if
\alpha=\beta
, and 0 otherwise. If this is the case, we can simplify Equation (
14
) into

(25)

c^{\prime}_{2}(\lambda(\alpha,\alpha))=2|\alpha|+\frac{4K(\alpha)}{N},

for any
\alpha
of length
r
and
N\geqslant 2r
. Let us recall the estimation

|2K(\alpha)|\leqslant|\alpha|(|\alpha|-1),

which leads for
\lambda(\alpha,\alpha)\in\Lambda_{N,1}
to

|c^{\prime}_{2}(\lambda(\alpha,\alpha))-2|\alpha||\leqslant 4N^{2\gamma-1}.

We then get the estimate

(26)

q^{4N^{2\gamma-1}}\sum_{|\alpha|\leqslant N^{\gamma}}q^{2|\alpha|}\leqslant S^{\prime}_{N,1}\leqslant q^{-4N^{2\gamma-1}}\sum_{|\alpha|\leqslant N^{\gamma}}q^{2|\alpha|},

and both bounds converge to the expected quantity
\prod_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{1-q^{2m}}
.

•

If
N=2M
, let
\tilde{\beta}=(\beta_{1}-\beta_{M},\ldots,\beta_{M-1}-\beta_{M},0)
as in the
g\geqslant 3
case. We know from Theorem
3.2
that
\iota_{\lambda(\alpha,\beta)}=1
if
\alpha=\tilde{\beta}
and 0 otherwise, so we have

\beta_{M}=\frac{2(|\beta|-|\alpha|)}{N}.

The condition
|\alpha|,|\beta|\leqslant N^{\gamma}
is then equivalent to

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}|\alpha|&\leqslant&N^{\gamma}\\
\beta_{M}&\leqslant&\tfrac{1}{2}N^{\gamma-1}-\frac{|\alpha|}{2N}\end{array}\right..

Furthermore, Equation (
17
) becomes

2|\alpha|+M\beta_{M}-4N^{2\gamma-1}\leqslant c^{\prime}_{2}(\lambda(\alpha,\beta))\leqslant 2|\alpha|+M\beta_{M}+4N^{2\gamma-1}+4N^{2\gamma-2}.

We obtain that

(27)

S^{\prime}_{N,1}\leqslant q^{-4N^{2\gamma-1}}\sum_{|\alpha|\leqslant N^{\gamma}}\Bigg(\sum_{0\leqslant n\leqslant\tfrac{1}{2}N^{\gamma-1}-\frac{|\alpha|}{2N}}q^{Mn}\Bigg)q^{2|\alpha|},

(28)

S^{\prime}_{N,1}\geqslant q^{4N^{2\gamma-1}+4N^{2\gamma-2}}\sum_{|\alpha|\leqslant N^{\gamma}}\Bigg(\sum_{0\leqslant n\leqslant\tfrac{1}{2}N^{\gamma-1}-\frac{|\alpha|}{2N}}q^{Mn}\Bigg)q^{2|\alpha|}.

The sums over
n
are bounded between 1 and
\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}q^{Mn}
which is bounded because
|q^{M}|<1
and converges to 1 as
N
tends to infinity (by dominated convergence). It finally appears that both bounds of (
27
) and (
28
) converge to
\prod_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{1-q^{2m}}
.

By similar arguments as the ones used in the case of the torus, we can prove that
S^{\prime}_{N,2}
,
S^{\prime}_{N,3}
and
S^{\prime}_{N,4}
all converge to 0 as the remainders of convergent series. This concludes the proof.
∎

The unitary case

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.(ii) in the unitary case.

Let us start from the definition of
Z_{N}^{-}(2,T)
. We have

Z_{N}^{-}(2,T)=\sum_{\lambda(\alpha,\beta)\in\widehat{{\mathrm{SU}}}(N)}\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}q^{c_{2}(\lambda(\alpha,\beta)+n)}(\iota_{\lambda(\alpha,\beta)+n})^{2}.

We know from Corollary
3.2
that
\iota_{\lambda(\alpha,\beta)+n}=1
if
\lambda(\alpha,\beta)
is symmetric and
n=-\tfrac{\lambda}{2}=-\alpha_{1}
, and 0 otherwise. We can then simplify the formula into

Z_{N}^{-}(2,T)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda(\alpha,\beta)\in\widehat{{\mathrm{SU}}}(N)\\
\lambda\text{ is symmetric}\end{subarray}}q^{c_{2}(\lambda(\alpha,\beta)-\alpha_{1})}.

As in the special unitary case, we will distinguish between the odd and even values of
N
, and prove that

\lim_{M\to\infty}Z_{2M}^{{}^{\prime}-}=\lim_{M\to\infty}Z_{2M+1}^{{}^{\prime}-}=\frac{1}{\phi(q)^{2}},

which implies the convergence of
(Z_{N}^{-})
.

•

If
N=2M+1
, the symmetry condition is equivalent to
\alpha=\beta
, and in particular
{\lambda(\alpha,\beta)-\beta_{1}}=\lambda(\alpha,\beta,0)
. Its Casimir number is given in Equation (
13
):

c_{2}(\lambda(\alpha,\beta,0))=|\alpha|+|\beta|+\frac{2}{N}(K(\alpha)+K(\beta)).

Comparing with (
14
) we remark that
c_{2}(\lambda(\alpha,\beta,0))=c^{\prime}_{2}(\lambda(\alpha,\beta))
. Then,

Z_{2M}^{-}(2,T)=Z_{2M}^{{}^{\prime}-}(2,T)

and we can conclude from the special unitary case.

•

If
N=2M
, let
\tilde{\beta}=(\beta_{1}-\beta_{M},\ldots,\beta_{M-1}-\beta_{M},0)
as in the
g\geqslant 3
case, then the symmetry condition is equivalent to the fact that
\alpha=\tilde{\beta}
and we have

\beta_{M}=\frac{2(|\beta|-|\alpha|)}{N}.

Let
\gamma\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
, and the subsets
(\Lambda_{N,i})_{1\leqslant i\leqslant 4}
of
\widehat{{\mathrm{SU}}}(N)
as usual. We define, for
1\leqslant i\leqslant 4
,

S_{N,i}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda(\alpha,\beta)\in\Lambda_{N,i}\\
\alpha=\tilde{\beta}\end{subarray}}q^{c_{2}(\lambda(\alpha,\beta,0))},

and we obtain the following decomposition:

Z_{N}^{-}(1,T)=S_{N,1}+S_{N,2}+S_{N,3}+S_{N,4}.

The condition
|\alpha|,|\beta|\leqslant N^{\gamma}
is then equivalent to

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}|\alpha|&\leqslant&N^{\gamma}\\
\beta_{M}&\leqslant&\tfrac{1}{2}N^{\gamma-1}-\frac{|\alpha|}{2N}\end{array}\right..

From (
13
) and (
14
) we have

c_{2}(\lambda(\alpha,\beta,0))=c^{\prime}_{2}(\lambda(\alpha,\beta))-\frac{(|\alpha|-|\beta|)^{2}}{N^{2}}=c^{\prime}_{2}(\lambda(\alpha,\beta))-4\beta_{M}^{2}.

We can combine all these estimations with (
17
) to obtain

2|\alpha|+M\beta_{M}-4N^{2\gamma-1}-4\beta_{M}^{2}\leqslant c_{2}(\lambda(\alpha,\beta,0))\leqslant 2|\alpha|+M\beta_{M}+4N^{2\gamma-1}+4N^{2\gamma-2}-4\beta_{M}^{2}.

Recall that for
\lambda(\alpha,\beta)\in\Lambda_{N,1}
we have
\beta_{M}\leqslant 2\frac{N^{\gamma}-|\alpha|}{N}\leqslant 2N^{\gamma-1}
, which yields

\beta_{M}^{2}\leqslant 4N^{2\gamma-2}.

We obtain therefore new bounds for
c_{2}(\lambda(\alpha,\beta,0))
:

2|\alpha|+M\beta_{M}-4N^{2\gamma-1}-4N^{2\gamma-2}\leqslant c_{2}(\lambda(\alpha,\beta,0))\leqslant 2|\alpha|+M\beta_{M}+4N^{2\gamma-1}+4N^{2\gamma-2}.

We obtain similar bounds for
S_{N,1}
as we did in (
27
) and (
28
), and we recover the same limit using the same arguments. We can also use the same routine as we did for the torus, to prove the convergence of
S_{N,2}
,
S_{N,3}
and
S_{N,4}
to 0 as the remainders of convergent series.

∎
