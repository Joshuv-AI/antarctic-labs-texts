High-dimensional Nonlinear Bayesian Inference of Poroelastic Fields from Pressure Data
Mina Karimi, Mehrdad Massoudi, Kaushik Dayal, Matteo Pozzi

Abstract
We investigate solution methods for large-scale inverse problems governed by partial differential equations (PDEs) via Bayesian inference. The Bayesian framework provides a statistical setting to infer uncertain parameters from noisy measurements. To quantify posterior uncertainty, we adopt Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) approaches for generating samples. To increase the efficiency of these approaches in high-dimension, we make use of local information about gradient and Hessian of the target potential, also via Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC). Our target application is inferring the field of soil permeability processing observations of pore pressure, using a nonlinear PDE poromechanics model for predicting pressure from permeability. We compare the performance of different sampling approaches in this and other settings. We also investigate the effect of dimensionality and non-gaussianity of distributions on the performance of different sampling methods.

Many problems in science and engineering problems can be modeled by partial differential equations (PDEs). Inverse problems constrained by PDEs are a challenging class of these problems, which play an essential role in investigating many physical systems, including geomechanical engineering, medical engineering, astrophysics, e.g.

Inverse problems aim at inferring the unknown model parameters of a physical system from observations, which may be limited, indirectly related to the parameters, and affected by noise.
When the parameter domain is high-dimensional, and the relation between parameters and observations is defined by a complex mechanics model,
solving inverse problems is computationally expensive.
One approach for addressing these problems is to identify a point-based estimator via the minimizing of a function quantifying the discrepancies between model predictions and observations, e.g. the negative log-likelihood function.
However, in this formulation the solution may not be unique, and the point-based estimate may be not informative on the posterior knowledge.
One classical method to overcome the ill-posedness and to decrease the sensitivity of the solution to measurements’ noise is to add a regularization term, enforcing continuity and smoothness of the solution

Prior and likelihood are integrated in the posterior distribution.
A point-based estimator for the posterior evaluation is the Maximum a Posteriori (MAP), but the posterior uncertainty can also be represented, describing the posterior distribution via samples or approximations, when it cannot be derived in exact form.

In this paper, we adopt a Bayesian inference framework to infer the uncertain parameters of a PDE-based forward poromechanics model.
For solving the Bayesian inverse problem numerically, the infinite-dimensional parameter space has to be discretized to a finite-dimensional domain.
Different discretization methods have been studied in the context of infinite-dimensional inverse problems.

2
Bayesian Framework for High-Dimensional Inverse Problems

We infer field
\displaystyle\theta(x)
, where
\displaystyle x
is a spatial coordinate on reference domain
\displaystyle\Omega
, following the Bayesian framework.
The prior knowledge about the field is modeled by a function
\displaystyle\pi(\theta)=\text{log}\big(p(\theta)\big)
, and
\displaystyle p(\theta)
is assumed to be Gaussian, so that:

\pi(\theta)=-\frac{1}{2}||\mathcal{A}(\theta-m_{\pi})||^{2}+\mathrm{const.}

(1)

where
\displaystyle m_{\pi}(x)
is the prior mean function and
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
is a Laplacian-like operator, that will be defined in Section
2.1
.
For any function
\displaystyle f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
,
\displaystyle L^{2}(\Omega)
being the space of square integrable functions, the norm is defined as:

||f(x)||^{2}=\int_{\Omega}f^{2}(x)\ \mathrm{d}x

(2)

A dataset of
\displaystyle n
noisy observations is available, listed in a vector
\displaystyle{\mathbfit y}=[y_{1},y_{2},\cdots,y_{n}]^{\top}
.
The observation
\displaystyle i
is related to the field via equation
\displaystyle y_{i}=Q_{\theta}(x_{i},t_{i})+\epsilon_{i}
,
where the function
\displaystyle Q_{\theta}
predicts the value to be measured, as a function of the parameter field
\displaystyle\theta
, time
\displaystyle t

and location
\displaystyle x
, and it is computed by solving the forward predictive model. Noise terms are listed in a vector
\displaystyle\mathbfit{\epsilon}=[\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2},\cdots,\epsilon_{n}]^{\top}
, modeled as a Gaussian random variable, independent of
\displaystyle\theta
, with a zero mean and covariance matrix
\displaystyle\mathbfit{\Gamma}_{\epsilon}
, i.e.,
\displaystyle\mathbfit{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbfit{\Gamma}_{\epsilon})
.

Probabilistically, the agreement between predictions and measurements is modeled by the log-likelihood function
\displaystyle l(\theta)=\text{log}\big(p({\mathbfit y}|\theta)\big)
:

l(\theta)=-\frac{1}{2}||\mathbfit{\Gamma}^{-1/2}_{\epsilon}({\mathbfit Q}_{\theta}-{\mathbfit y})||^{2}+\mathrm{const.}

(3)

where
\displaystyle{\mathbfit Q}_{\theta}=[Q_{\theta}(x_{1},t_{1}),\cdots,Q_{\theta}(x_{n},t_{n})]^{\top}
is the vector of predictions. In this paper, for each positive definite and symmetric matrix
\displaystyle{\mathbfit G}
, the matrix
\displaystyle{\mathbfit G}^{-1/2}
is a decomposition of inverse of that matrix, so that
\displaystyle{\mathbfit G}^{-1}={\mathbfit G}^{-1/2}{\mathbfit G}^{-\top/2}
(e.g. the decomposition can be based on eigenvalue analysis or on Cholesky’s method).
For column vectors, the norm is defined as
\displaystyle||{\mathbfit v}||^{2}={\mathbfit v}^{\top}{\mathbfit v}
.
If
\displaystyle\mathbfit{\Gamma}_{\epsilon}=\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}{\mathbfit I}
(where
\displaystyle{\mathbfit I}
is the identity matrix), then
\displaystyle\mathbfit{\Gamma}^{-1/2}_{\epsilon}
can be replaced by
\displaystyle\sigma^{-1}_{\epsilon}
in Eq. (
3
).

Following the Bayes’ rule, the log-posterior density
\displaystyle\omega(\theta)=\text{log}\big(p(\theta|{\mathbfit y})\big)
is:

\omega(\theta)=l(\theta)+\pi(\theta)+\text{const.}

(4)

Motivated by the equation above, we define the objective function as
\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(\theta)=-\omega(\theta)+\text{const.}
:

\mathcal{J}(\theta)=\frac{1}{2}||\mathbfit{\Gamma}^{-1/2}_{\epsilon}({\mathbfit Q}_{\theta}-{\mathbfit y})||^{2}+\frac{1}{2}||\mathcal{A}(\theta-m_{\pi})||^{2}

(5)

The MAP point is
\displaystyle\theta_{\text{MAP}}=\argmin\big(\mathcal{J}(\theta)\big)
, and it is a parameter field that maximizes the posterior density.
To identify
\displaystyle\theta_{\text{MAP}}
, we minimize
\displaystyle\mathcal{J}
: while least squares approaches minimize the first term (based on the likelihood), the second one (based on prior knowledge) usually acts as a regularization.

2.1
Choice of Prior Model

The choice of
\displaystyle p(\theta)
is crucial as it allows to include the prior knowledge on the parameter field
\displaystyle\theta
in a Bayesian inference setting.
In this paper, we use a continuous Gaussian measure
\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(m_{\pi},\mathcal{C}_{\pi})

instead of adopting a discretized Gaussian distribution and covariance matrix
[
6
,
7
]
.
Here,
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\pi}
is the covariance operator which is defined as below:

(\mathcal{C}_{\pi}\phi)({\mathbfit x})=\int_{\Omega}{\mathbfit c}({\mathbfit x},{\mathbfit y})\mathbfit{\phi}({\mathbfit y})\ \mathrm{d}{\mathbfit y}

(6)

where
\displaystyle{\mathbfit c}({\mathbfit x},{\mathbfit z})
is covariance function:

{\mathbfit c}({\mathbfit x},{\mathbfit z})=\E\big[\big(\theta({\mathbfit x})-m_{\pi}({\mathbfit x})\big)\big(\theta({\mathbfit z})-m_{\pi}({\mathbfit z})\big)\big]

(7)

Given measure function
\displaystyle\mu_{\pi}
, the operator is defined as:

\mathcal{C}_{\pi}=\int_{\Omega}(\theta-m_{\pi})\otimes(\theta-m_{\pi})\mu_{\pi}(\ \mathrm{d}\theta)=\E[(\theta-m_{\pi})\otimes(\theta-m_{\pi})]

(8)

The operator
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\pi}
is fast-to-apply and allows a simple discretization in high dimension
[
6
]
. As it is described in Appendix
A
the prior function
\displaystyle\pi
(Eq. (
1
)) is written as follows:

\pi(\theta)=-\frac{1}{2}||\mathcal{C}_{\pi}^{-1/2}(\theta-m_{\pi})||^{2}+\text{const.}

(9)

Commonly, the covariance operator is considered as the fractional power of Laplacian-like operator
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
, i.e.
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\pi}=\mathcal{A}^{-\alpha}~,~\alpha>0
, where
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
is positive definite, self adjoint and invertible
[
11
,
31
,
9
]
.
Here we choose the covariance operator as
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\pi}=\mathcal{A}^{-2}
, so that
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\pi}^{-1/2}=\mathcal{A}
, and the action of operator
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
on
\displaystyle\theta
, i.e.,
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}\theta
is defined by the following elliptic Boundary Value Problem (
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}=-\gamma{\mathbfit{\Psi}}\Delta+\delta{\mathbfit I}
):

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\gamma\divergence(\mathbfit{\Psi}\nabla\theta)+\delta\theta=s~~\text{in}~\Omega

(10)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\gamma(\nabla\theta)\cdot{\mathbfit n}=0~~\text{on}~\partial\Omega

where
\displaystyle\Delta
is the Laplacian operator,
\displaystyle\gamma
and
\displaystyle\delta
are positive (hyper)parameters,
\displaystyle s
is a white noise field and the random
\displaystyle\theta
field can be expressed as linear function of the random noise, as
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{-1}s
.
Here,
\displaystyle{\mathbfit n}
is the unit normal vector on
\displaystyle\partial\Omega
. The symmetric and positive-definite second order tensor
\displaystyle\mathbfit{\Psi}
defines the anisotropy, and indicates the direction
\displaystyle\hat{{\mathbfit m}}
in which the parameter space
\displaystyle\theta
has a larger variation.

\displaystyle\mathbfit{\Psi}
can then be defined in terms of the unit vector
\displaystyle\hat{{\mathbfit m}}
as follows:

\mathbfit{\Psi}=a\left({\mathbfit I}-(1-b/a)\hat{{\mathbfit m}}\otimes\hat{{\mathbfit m}}^{\top}\right)

(11)

In a two-dimensional setting,
\displaystyle\mathbfit{\Psi}
is can be represented as:

\mathbfit{\Psi}=\begin{bmatrix}a\sin{(\beta)}^{2}+b\cos{(\beta)}^{2}&(b-a)\sin{(\beta)}\cos{(\beta)}\\
(b-a)\sin{(\beta)}\cos{(\beta)}&b\sin{(\beta)}^{2}+a\cos{(\beta)}^{2}\end{bmatrix}

(12)

where
\displaystyle a
,
\displaystyle b
and
\displaystyle\beta
are constants.
The larger
\displaystyle\gamma
and
\displaystyle\delta
are, the smaller becomes the variance (Eq. (
10
)).
Based on the above formulation, the continuous form of prior term can be written as:

\mathcal{A}\theta=\begin{cases}-\gamma\divergence(\mathbfit{\Psi}\nabla\theta)+\delta\theta&\text{ in }\Omega\\
\gamma(\nabla\theta)\cdot{\mathbfit n}&\text{ on }\partial\Omega\end{cases}

(13)

2.2
Discretization of the field

We use a finite element discretization with continuous Lagrangian basis functions
\displaystyle\{\phi_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
, and the corresponding nodal values
\displaystyle\{{\mathbfit x}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
, to obtain the finite-dimensional approximation of the problem.
Thus, the discretized field can be described as a vector of coefficients
\displaystyle{\mathbfit{\psi}}=[\psi_{1},\dots,\psi_{m}]^{\top}
.
The inner product between nodal coefficient vectors can be weighted by a positive-definite and symmetric matrix
\displaystyle{\mathbfit N}
, with entries given by:

N_{ij}=\int_{\Omega}\phi_{i}({\mathbfit x})\phi_{j}({\mathbfit x})\ \mathrm{d}{\mathbfit x}

(14)

Then, for every pair of functions
\displaystyle\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, we can define the weighted inner product in the discretized form as
\displaystyle(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\approx(\mathbfit{\psi}_{1},\mathbfit{\psi}_{2})_{\mathbfit N}=\mathbfit{\psi}_{1}^{\top}{\mathbfit N}\mathbfit{\psi}_{2}
.
The matrix representation of the the Laplacian operator
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}
can be defined as
\displaystyle{\mathbfit A}={\mathbfit N}^{-1}{\mathbfit K}

[
33
]
, with entry
\displaystyle\{i,j\}
of
\displaystyle{\mathbfit K}
as:

K_{ij}=\int_{\Omega}\big[\gamma\mathbfit{\Psi}\nabla\phi_{i}({\mathbfit x})\cdot\nabla\phi_{j}({\mathbfit x})+\delta\phi_{i}({\mathbfit x})\phi_{j}({\mathbfit x})\big]\ \mathrm{d}{\mathbfit x}

(15)

Therefore, the log prior term can be approximated as:

\pi(\theta)\cong-\frac{1}{2}||{\mathbfit A}^{-1/2}~\mathbfit{\psi}||^{2}+\text{const.}

(16)

and the objective function can be expressed in terms of
\displaystyle\mathbfit{\psi}
as:

\mathcal{J}(\mathbfit{\psi})=\frac{1}{2}||\mathbfit{\Gamma}^{-1/2}_{\epsilon}({\mathbfit Q}{\psi}-{\mathbfit y})||^{2}+\frac{1}{2}||{\mathbfit A}^{-1/2}~\mathbfit{\psi}||^{2}

(17)

where
\displaystyle{\mathbfit Q}{\psi}
is the predictive vector, reconstructing field
\displaystyle\theta=\sum^{m}_{j=1}\psi_{j}\phi_{j}
.
To minimize this nonlinear function with respect to
\displaystyle\theta
, quasi-Newton methods can be generally used.

Sampling Methods for High-dimension Inverse Problems

In this section we review some key methods for drawing samples from probability density functions.
We discuss the Metropolis-Hastings MCMC and Hessian based MCMC methods, and compare their computational costs and efficiency for sampling high-dimensional parameter space.

3.1
Metropolis Hastings Method

The Metropolis Hastings MCMC (MH-MCMC) method (Algorithm

) relies on proposal density
\displaystyle q
, which samples next value of random variables
\displaystyle{\mathbfit z}
, along a chain, centered at current location
\displaystyle{\mathbfit c}
.
One of the most popular choices of proposal (log) density is the isotropic Gaussian distribution:

q({\mathbfit c},{\mathbfit z})=-\frac{1}{2}||\Delta t^{-1}({\mathbfit c}-{\mathbfit z})||^{2}+\text{const.}

(18)

where
\displaystyle\Delta t
is a fixed step-size.

In high-dimensional problems governed by PDEs, the posterior standard deviation of a linear combination of the field variables varies significantly depending of the direction of the combination, and this poses some challenges in choosing the step size.
Selecting a large step size will induce a low acceptance rate, due to the small standard deviation of some directions, and a step size as small as the smallest standard deviation will induce a slow mixing (and, since we need to solve the forward model in each iteration, the simulation campaign will be computationally expensive).

In the following sections, we discuss some accelerated sampling methods using the local gradient and Hessian information of the objective function
\displaystyle\mathcal{J}

3.2
Hessian-based MCMC Methods

In this section we review three Hessian informed MCMC methods proposed by Ghattas and coworkers
[
28
,
6
,
33
]
:

The local quadratic form of Eq. (
5
), based on the Newton method at the vicinity of a current sample
\displaystyle\mathbfit{\psi}_{k}
can be approximated using the Laplace approximation (based on a second order Taylor series) as follows
[
29
]
:

\mathcal{J}(\mathbfit{\psi})\approx\tilde{\mathcal{J}}(\mathbfit{\psi})=\frac{1}{2}||{\mathbfit H}^{1/2}_{k}(\mathbfit{\psi}-\mathbfit{\psi}_{k})||^{2}+{\mathbfit g}^{\top}_{k}(\mathbfit{\psi}-\mathbfit{\psi}_{k})+\mathcal{J}(\mathbfit{\psi}_{k})

(19)

where
\displaystyle{\mathbfit g}_{k}=\nabla\mathcal{J}(\mathbfit{\psi}_{k})
and
\displaystyle{\mathbfit H}_{k}=\nabla^{2}\mathcal{J}(\mathbfit{\psi}_{k})
are the gradient and the Hessian matrix of the objective function, respectively, and
\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{J}}
is the approximate function.
We note that the Hessian matrix is positive definite at the MAP point. However, we cannot guarantee a positive definite matrix at any arbitrary point.
Therefore, to guarantee a positive definite Hessian matrix, we can use a low-rank approximation
\displaystyle\tilde{{\mathbfit H}}_{k}
(Appendix
B
), which is calculated based on the eigenvalue decomposition, truncating negative and zero eigenvalues.
By expanding Eq. (
19
), and adding and subtracting the term
\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}{\mathbfit g}^{T}_{k}\tilde{{\mathbfit H}}^{-1}_{k}{\mathbfit g}_{k}
, we obtain:

\tilde{\mathcal{J}}(\mathbfit{\psi})=\frac{1}{2}(\mathbfit{\psi}-\mathbfit{\psi}_{k})^{\top}\tilde{{\mathbfit H}}_{k}(\mathbfit{\psi}-\mathbfit{\psi}_{k})+{\mathbfit g}^{\top}_{k}(\mathbfit{\psi}-\mathbfit{\psi}_{k})+\mathcal{J}(\mathbfit{\psi}_{k})+\frac{1}{2}{\mathbfit g}^{T}_{k}\tilde{{\mathbfit H}}^{-1}_{k}{\mathbfit g}_{k}-\frac{1}{2}{\mathbfit g}^{T}_{k}\tilde{{\mathbfit H}}^{-1}_{k}{\mathbfit g}_{k}

(20)

Therefore, Eq. (
20
) can be re-written as
[
28
]
:

\tilde{\mathcal{J}}(\mathbfit{\psi})=\frac{1}{2}||\tilde{{\mathbfit H}}^{1/2}_{k}~(\mathbfit{\psi}-\mathbfit{\psi}_{k}+\tilde{{\mathbfit H}}^{-1}_{k}{\mathbfit g}_{k})||^{2}+\mathrm{const.}

(21)

where term
\displaystyle\tilde{{\mathbfit H}}^{-1}_{k}{\mathbfit g}_{k}
can be interpreted as the Newton step.
Inspired by this approximation, the Stochastic Newton MCMC (SN-MCMC) method adopts the following proposal log density:

q(\mathbfit{\psi}_{k},{\mathbfit z})=-\frac{1}{2}||\tilde{{\mathbfit H}}^{1/2}_{k}~({\mathbfit z}-\mathbfit{\psi}_{k}+\tilde{{\mathbfit H}}_{k}^{-1}{\mathbfit g}_{k})||^{2}+\mathrm{const.}

(22)

When implementing the method, we need to solve the forward problem at each iteration of the sampling process, and calculate the local Hessian, local gradient, and approximated Hessian at each iteration.

Since recalculation of Hessian and approximated Hessian at each sample point is expensive, Bui et al.
[
6
,
33
]
proposed a modified method which is based on always using the Hessian at the MAP point.
Therefore, in the Stochastic Newton MCMC with MAP-based Hessian (SN-MAP) method we rewrite the proposal log density function as follows:

q(\mathbfit{\psi}_{k},{\mathbfit z})=-\frac{1}{2}||{{\mathbfit H}}^{1/2}_{\text{MAP}}~({\mathbfit z}-\mathbfit{\psi}_{k}+{\mathbfit H}_{\text{MAP}}^{-1}~{\mathbfit g}_{k})||^{2}+\mathrm{const.}

(23)

Thus, implementing this approach at each iteration we need to run the forward problem, and compute the local gradient at each sampled point, but we calculate the Hessian matrix just once, at the MAP point.

Using the locally approximated proposal density can increase the convergence speed, respect to MH-MCMC. However, when the target distribution is far from a Gaussian distribution, changing the mean value of the proposal density by adding term
\displaystyle({\mathbfit H}_{\text{MAP}}^{-1}{\mathbfit g}_{k})
, see Eq. (
19
), can decrease the speed of convergence or the sampling process can be stuck in a low probability region.
In order to overcome these problems, we can define a small random learning rate
\displaystyle\gamma\leq 1
, substituting term
\displaystyle({\mathbfit H}_{\text{MAP}}^{-1}{\mathbfit g}_{k})
with term
\displaystyle(\gamma{\mathbfit H}_{\text{MAP}}^{-1}{\mathbfit g}_{k})
, or use a trust region method
[
35
]
.

Another Hessian informed MCMC method proposed by Bui et al.
[
6
,
33
]
, called Independence Sampling with MAP point-based Gaussian proposal
(IS-MAP), neglects recalculating the local gradient at each sample by adopting a proposal density centered at the MAP point:

q(\mathbfit{\psi}_{\text{MAP}},{\mathbfit z})=-\frac{1}{2}||{\mathbfit H}^{1/2}_{\text{MAP}}~({\mathbfit z}-\mathbfit{\psi}_{\text{MAP}})||^{2}+\mathrm{const.}

(24)

where
\displaystyle\mathbfit{\psi}_{\text{MAP}}
is the parameter value at the MAP point.
Therefore, by avoiding recalculation of local Hessian and gradient at each sampling point, we need to calculate the Hessian at the MAP point only once and solve forward problem at each sampling point.
Note that, in this method, samples are generated independently at each step, so the sample generation is simpler than that of any other MCMC method. However, the acceptance criteria is still the traditional one, for MH-MCMC. This method can be related to the classical rejection sampling approach.

In the Metropolis-Adjusted Langevin Algorithm (MALA) method
[
18
,
36
,
37
]
, the proposal density is defined as:

q(\mathbfit{\psi}_{k},{\mathbfit z})=-\frac{1}{2}||(2\tau{\mathbfit B})^{-1/2}~({\mathbfit z}-\mathbfit{\psi}_{k}+\tau{\mathbfit B}{\mathbfit g}_{k})||^{2}+\mathrm{const.}

(25)

where
\displaystyle{\mathbfit B}
is a positive-definite preconditioning matrix, and
\displaystyle\tau
is a fixed time step, which has to be taken as
\displaystyle 0<\tau\leq 1
.
If we use the inverse of local Hessian information at the MAP as the preconditioning matrix, the proposal density becomes:

q(\mathbfit{\psi}_{k},{\mathbfit z})=-\frac{1}{2}||\big({\mathbfit H}_{\text{MAP}}/(2\tau)\big)^{1/2}~({\mathbfit z}-\mathbfit{\psi}_{k}+\tau{\mathbfit H}_{\text{MAP}}^{-1}{\mathbfit g}_{k})||^{2}+\mathrm{const.}

(26)

The scalar parameter
\displaystyle\tau
in Eqs. (
25
), and (
26
) can play a similar role as the small learning rate
\displaystyle\gamma
, by preventing the algorithm from being stuck in the low probability regions and yield better results for non-Gaussian distributions.

3.3
Hamiltonian Monte Carlo Method

HMC is one of the efficient sampling methods relying on local gradient information.
In the HMC algorithm (Algorithm
2
), we define an auxiliary momentum value, then we update the position and momentum according to the Hamiltonian’s system of differential equations.
For a general system, the position is the variable of interest (e.g. the parameters to be inferred). The potential energy is defined as the objective function of Section
2
, as the negative logarithm of the target distribution
[
17
,
30
]
.
The Hamiltonian function can be defined as:

\mathcal{H}(\mathbfit{\psi},{\mathbfit p})=\mathcal{K}(\mathbfit{\psi},{\mathbfit p})+\mathcal{J}(\mathbfit{\psi})

(27)

where
\displaystyle\mathcal{K}(\mathbfit{\psi},{\mathbfit p})
is the kinetic energy and
\displaystyle{\mathbfit p}
is the momentum vector. In order to update variables
\displaystyle{\mathbfit p}
and
\displaystyle\mathbfit{\psi}
, we need to calculate the derivatives of these variables with respect to the "pseudo time" variable
\displaystyle t
.
Therefore, Hamiltonian equations are:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{d\mathbfit{\psi}}{dt}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial{\mathbfit p}}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{K}}{\partial{\mathbfit p}}+\frac{\partial\mathcal{J}}{\partial{\mathbfit p}}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{K}}{\partial{\mathbfit p}}

(28)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{d{\mathbfit p}}{dt}=-\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial\mathbfit{\psi}}=-\frac{\partial\mathcal{K}}{\partial\mathbfit{\psi}}-\frac{\partial\mathcal{J}}{\partial\mathbfit{\psi}}=-\frac{\partial\mathcal{J}}{\partial\mathbfit{\psi}}

(29)

Different choices of
\displaystyle\mathcal{K}
function have been proposed in the literature. One of the most common choices is the
Euclidean-Gaussian
kinetic energy
[
4
]
:

\mathcal{K}(\mathbfit{\psi},{\mathbfit p})=\frac{1}{2}||{\mathbfit M}^{-1/2}{\mathbfit p}||^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\log|{\mathbfit M}|+\mathrm{const.}

(30)

where
\displaystyle{\mathbfit M}
is a positive-definite matrix known as the "mass matrix".
The choice of the mass matrix influences the convergence speed and the performance of the sampling process.

3.3.1
Hessian-based HMC Methods

It has been proved that we can reach a better convergence speed in linear problems by setting the mass matrix as the inverse of posterior covariance
[
13
]
.
In a more general setting, Riemannian manifold HMC method uses a
Riemannian-Gaussian
kinetic energy
[
3
]
to choose the mass matrix as
\displaystyle{\mathbfit M}={\mathbfit C}(\mathbfit{\psi})
,
where
\displaystyle{\mathbfit C}(\mathbfit{\psi})
is a function of the parameter of interest which has to be updated with the local information
[
4
]
.

When we consider
\displaystyle{\mathbfit C}(\mathbfit{\psi})=\nabla^{2}\mathcal{J}(\mathbfit{\psi})
, the Hamiltonian updating equations will induce an updating step similar to that of Newton method
[
26
]
, which can significantly improve the sampling performance.
Using the Riemannian manifold HMC for high-dimensional inverse problems is computationally expensive since we need to calculate the local Hessian of the posterior at each iteration.

To decrease the computational cost, we can use the Hessian of the posterior at the MAP point as a mass matrix
[
7
]
, which prevent calculating Hessian in each iteration.

3.4
Comparison of SN-MCMC, MALA, and H-HMC Sampling Methods

This section compares the formulation of MCMC sampling methods relying on Hessian information for high-dimension parameter spaces.
We discuss their similarities and differences.

3.4.1
SN-MAP

Following Eq. (
23
), the updating step of SN-MAP method is (here we directly indicate the next candidate point as
\displaystyle{\mathbfit{\psi}}_{k+1}
, instead of as
\displaystyle{\mathbfit z}
):

{\mathbfit{\psi}}_{k+1}={\mathbfit{\psi}}_{k}-{{\mathbfit H}}_{\text{MAP}}^{-1}{\mathbfit g}_{k}+{{\mathbfit H}}_{\text{MAP}}^{-1/2}{\mathbfit u}

(31)

where
\displaystyle{\mathbfit u}
is a random sample from a multivariate standard Gaussian distribution (i.e., with identity covariance matrix and zero mean vector).
For MCMC methods, as seen above, the acceptance criterion is based on log-ratio
\displaystyle\alpha
.
For MH-MCMC (described in Algorithm
1
), it is:

\alpha=\mathcal{J}(\mathbfit{\psi}_{k})-\mathcal{J}(\mathbfit{\psi}_{k+1})+\Delta q

(32)

The extended form of Eq. (
32
), using Eq. (
23
), becomes:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\alpha=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{J}({\mathbfit{\psi}}_{k})-\mathcal{J}({\mathbfit{\psi}}_{k+1})-\frac{1}{2}||{{\mathbfit H}}_{\text{MAP}}^{1/2}({\mathbfit{\psi}}_{k}-{\mathbfit{\psi}}_{k+1}+{{\mathbfit H}}_{\text{MAP}}^{-1}{\mathbfit g}_{k+1})||^{2}

\displaystyle\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}||{{\mathbfit H}}_{\text{MAP}}^{1/2}({\mathbfit{\psi}}_{k+1}-{\mathbfit{\psi}}_{k}+{{\mathbfit H}}_{\text{MAP}}^{-1}{\mathbfit g}_{k})||^{2}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{J}({\mathbfit{\psi}}_{k})-\mathcal{J}({\mathbfit{\psi}}_{k+1})+({\mathbfit{\psi}}_{k+1}-{\mathbfit{\psi}}_{k})^{\top}({\mathbfit g}_{k}+{\mathbfit g}_{k+1})+\frac{1}{2}||{\mathbfit H}^{-1/2}_{\text{MAP}}{\mathbfit g}_{k}||^{2}-\frac{1}{2}||{\mathbfit H}^{-1/2}_{\text{MAP}}{\mathbfit g}_{k+1}||^{2}

3.4.2
MALA

Following Eq. (
26
) the updating step of MALA can be derived as:

{\mathbfit{\psi}}_{k+1}={\mathbfit{\psi}}_{k}-\tau{{\mathbfit H}}_{\text{MAP}}^{-1}{\mathbfit g}_{k}+\sqrt{(2\tau)}{{\mathbfit H}}_{\text{MAP}}^{-1/2}{\mathbfit u}

(33)

where
\displaystyle{\mathbfit u}
is defined as above.
Considering
\displaystyle\sqrt{(2\tau)}=\Delta t
, the above equation can be re-written as:

{\mathbfit{\psi}}_{k+1}={\mathbfit{\psi}}_{k}-\frac{\Delta t^{2}}{2}{{\mathbfit H}}_{\text{MAP}}^{-1}{\mathbfit g}_{k}+\Delta t{{\mathbfit H}}_{\text{MAP}}^{-1/2}{\mathbfit u}

(34)

The extended form of the log ratio for the acceptance criterion for MALA method, considering Eq. (
26
), can be derived as:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\alpha=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{J}({\mathbfit{\psi}}_{k})-\mathcal{J}({\mathbfit{\psi}}_{k+1})-\frac{1}{4\tau}||{{\mathbfit H}}_{\text{MAP}}^{1/2}({\mathbfit{\psi}}_{k}-{\mathbfit{\psi}}_{k+1}+\tau{{\mathbfit H}}_{\text{MAP}}^{-1}{\mathbfit g}_{k+1})||^{2}

(35)

\displaystyle\displaystyle+\frac{1}{4\tau}||{{\mathbfit H}}_{\text{MAP}}^{1/2}({\mathbfit{\psi}}_{k+1}-{\mathbfit{\psi}}_{k}+\tau{{\mathbfit H}}_{\text{MAP}}^{-1}{\mathbfit g}_{k})||^{2}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{J}({\mathbfit{\psi}}_{k})-\mathcal{J}({\mathbfit{\psi}}_{k+1})+\frac{1}{2}({\mathbfit{\psi}}_{k+1}-{\mathbfit{\psi}}_{k})^{\top}({\mathbfit g}_{k}+{\mathbfit g}_{k+1})+\frac{\tau}{4}||{\mathbfit H}^{-1/2}_{\text{MAP}}{\mathbfit g}_{k}||^{2}-\frac{\tau}{4}||{\mathbfit H}^{-1/2}_{\text{MAP}}{\mathbfit g}_{k+1}||^{2}

3.4.3
H-HMC

Similarly, the updating step of H-HMC method using the Hessian information at the MAP point and leapfrog algorithm
2
can be written as:

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\mathbfit p}_{k+\frac{1}{2}}={\mathbfit p}_{k}-\frac{\Delta t}{2}{\mathbfit g}_{k}

(36)

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\mathbfit{\psi}}_{k+1}={\mathbfit{\psi}}_{k}+\Delta t{{\mathbfit H}}_{\text{MAP}}^{-1}{\mathbfit p}_{k+\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\mathbfit p}_{k+1}={\mathbfit p}_{k+\frac{1}{2}}-\frac{\Delta t}{2}{\mathbfit g}_{k+1}

where
\displaystyle{\mathbfit p}_{k}
is a random sample from a Gaussian distribution with zero mean and covariance
\displaystyle{{\mathbfit H}}_{\text{MAP}}
.
Therefore, the updating step is as Eq. (
34
).
The extended form of the log ratio, for H-HMC method, considering Eq. (
27
), is:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\alpha=\mathcal{J}({\mathbfit{\psi}}_{k})+\frac{1}{2}||{\mathbfit H}_{\text{MAP}}^{-1/2}{\mathbfit p}_{k}||^{2}-\mathcal{J}({\mathbfit{\psi}}_{k+1})-\frac{1}{2}||{\mathbfit H}_{\text{MAP}}^{-1/2}{\mathbfit p}_{k+1}||^{2}

(37)

where from the leapfrog algorithm (Eq. (
36
)) we can define:

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\mathbfit p}_{k}=\frac{(\mathbfit{\psi}_{k+1}-\mathbfit{\psi}_{k})^{\top}}{\Delta t}{\mathbfit H}_{\text{MAP}}+\frac{\Delta t}{2}{\mathbfit g}_{k}

(38)

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\mathbfit p}_{k+1}=\frac{(\mathbfit{\psi}_{k+1}-\mathbfit{\psi}_{k})^{\top}}{\Delta t}{\mathbfit H}_{\text{MAP}}-\frac{\Delta t}{2}{\mathbfit g}_{k+1}

and by substituting Eq. (
38
) in Eq. (
37
) we derive:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\alpha=\mathcal{J}({\mathbfit{\psi}}_{k})-\mathcal{J}({\mathbfit{\psi}}_{k+1})+\frac{1}{2}({\mathbfit{\psi}}_{k+1}-{\mathbfit{\psi}}_{k})^{\top}({\mathbfit g}_{k}+{\mathbfit g}_{k+1})+\frac{\Delta t^{2}}{8}||{\mathbfit H}^{-1/2}_{\text{MAP}}{\mathbfit g}_{k}||^{2}-\frac{\Delta t^{2}}{8}||{\mathbfit H}^{-1/2}_{\text{MAP}}{\mathbfit g}_{k+1}||^{2}

which is similar to the acceptance criterion of MALA method (Eq. (
35
)).

We summarize the important points from the comparison of Hessian-based sampling methods.
The above analysis proves that, with a proper choice of positive-definite matrix
\displaystyle{\mathbfit B}
in the MALA method and Mass matrix
\displaystyle{\mathbfit M}
in the HMC method, the updating step and acceptance coefficient of these two methods are the same.
Choice of Mass matrix
\displaystyle{\mathbfit M}
in HMC method and of positive-definite matrix
\displaystyle{\mathbfit B}
in MALA method plays a crucial role in tuning and performance of HMC and MALA methods, respectively.
In the above analysis, we propose using the Hessian information at the MAP point to tune HMC and MALA methods, which can significantly improve the performance of these methods compared to standard sampling methods.
Comparison of these three Hessian-based methods shows that, although the SN-MAP method performs perfectly for high-dimensional Gaussian distributions, H-HMC and MALA methods with a variable step size can perform better for exploring non-Gaussian parameter spaces.
These methods will be compared numerically in Section
5
.

4
Poroelastic Forward Model

In this section, we present the forward model, based on a continuum energetic formulation of poroelasticity
[
22
]
, to predict the water pore pressure
\displaystyle p
in the soil as a function of the permeability
\displaystyle\kappa
and accounting for fluid transport in a deformable medium.
Relating this to the notation of Section
2
, the observation
\displaystyle i
is a noisy pressure measure
\displaystyle y_{i}=p^{i}_{obs}
, and the uncertain field is
\displaystyle\theta(x)=-\log_{e}\kappa(x)
, where
\displaystyle\kappa
has the units
\displaystyle\text{\,}\mathrm{m}^{2}
.
Below, we describe the governing PDEs of this model.

The governing equations of the porous medium, wherein the solid matrix is incompressible, can be written as:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\ \mathrm{d}\trace(\mathbfit{\varepsilon})}{\ \mathrm{d}t}+\frac{\ \mathrm{d}{p}}{\ \mathrm{d}t}\frac{\phi_{f}}{K_{f}}=-\frac{1}{\rho_{f}}\divergence{\mathbfit Q}

(39)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\divergence\mathbfit{\sigma}+{\mathbfit b}=\bf 0

(40)

where
\displaystyle\epsilon
,
\displaystyle\phi_{f}
,
\displaystyle K_{f}
, and
\displaystyle\gamma
are the strain tensor, fluid volume fraction, fluid bulk modulus, and dynamic viscosity of fluid, respectively.

The flux vector
\displaystyle{\mathbfit Q}
is defined as:

{\mathbfit Q}=-\frac{\kappa}{\gamma}\rho_{f}\left(\nabla p+P_{f}{\mathbfit g}\right)

(41)

where
\displaystyle\rho_{f}
and
\displaystyle P_{f}
are the true fluid density and fluid density, respectively, with the relation
\displaystyle P_{f}=\phi_{f}\rho_{f}
. In Eq. (
40
)
\displaystyle\mathbfit{\sigma}
is the Cauchy stress tensor, which is defined as:

\mathbfit{\sigma}=(1-\phi_{f})\big(2\mu\mathbfit{\varepsilon}+\lambda\trace(\mathbfit{\varepsilon}){\mathbfit I}\big)-\phi_{f}p{\mathbfit I}

(42)

where
\displaystyle\mu
and
\displaystyle\lambda
are Lame constants, and the body force is defined as
\displaystyle{\mathbfit b}:=(P_{s}+P_{f}){\mathbfit g}
. where
\displaystyle P_{s}
is the density of solid phase.

6
Conclusion

We have presented a Bayesian inference framework for high-dimension inverse problems governed by PDE equations.
We used a continuous Gaussian prior distribution (based on a Laplacian-like operator) to ensure the well-posedness of the infinite-dimensional inverse problem.
The main advantage of this prior function is that it can be applied to continuous domains, and it allows simple discretization.
We implemented the inverse problem in the
FEniCS
library and used an quasi-Newton solver (BFGS) of
dolfin-adjoint
package to solve the minimization problem.

We have investigated several sampling methods to describe the posterior distribution.
We discussed the complexities of sampling methods in high-dimensional parameter spaces and compared the performance of MH-MCMC and HMC sampling methods with the accelerated methods using the Hessian and gradient information, such as SN-MCMC, MALA, and H-HMC.
By considering several one-dimensional and high-dimensional problems with Gaussian and non-Gaussian posterior distributions, we showed that using the modified Hessian-based sampling methods can significantly increase the speed of convergence and exploring in high-dimensional inverse problems. However, using the local Hessian information in nonlinear high-dimensional problems is computationally expensive. We considered Hessian sampling methods using the Hessian information calculated at the MAP point to overcome this problem. The results revealed that the MAP-based Hessian sampling methods are both computationally efficient and fast in exploring high-dimensional distributions.

We also applied the developed framework to a high-dimensional inverse problem governed by poroelastic PDE equations to infer the unknown permeability distribution from the point-wise pore pressure observations.
We calculated the MAP point and the posterior credible intervals by applying the HMC and H-HMC methods, using the Hessian information at the MAP point. Our results indicate that the H-HMC method using the Hessian information at the MAP point has a better performance in exploring this non-Gaussian high-dimensional distribution.

\displaystyle\{\lambda_{j}\}^{\infty}_{j=1}

Appendix B
Low-rank Hessian

The Hessian or second derivative of the objective function (Eq. (
17
)) w.r.t the controlling parameter can be written as:

{\mathbfit H}={\mathbfit H}_{\text{misfit}}+{\mathbfit A}^{-1}

(49)

where
\displaystyle{\mathbfit H}_{\text{misfit}}
is the Hessian of first term in the objective function, which shows the misfit between the observations and predicted values, and
\displaystyle{\mathbfit A}
is the Hessian of prior term. We can consider the Cholesky decomposition of the prior covariance as
\displaystyle{\mathbfit A}={\mathbfit L}{\mathbfit L}^{\top}
.
Using this decomposition, we can rewrite Eq. (
49
) as
\displaystyle{\mathbfit H}={\mathbfit L}^{-\top}\big({\mathbfit L}^{\top}{\mathbfit H}_{\text{misfit}}{\mathbfit L}+{\mathbfit I}\big){\mathbfit L}^{-1}
.

where the first term
\displaystyle{\mathbfit L}{\mathbfit H}_{\text{misfit}}{\mathbfit L}
is more informative about the controlling parameter, and its
\displaystyle r-
dimensional low rank approximation can be written as
\displaystyle{\mathbfit L}^{\top}{\mathbfit H}_{\text{misfit}}{\mathbfit L}\approx{\mathbfit V}_{r}{\mathbfit D}_{r}{\mathbfit V}_{r}^{\top}
.
where
\displaystyle{\mathbfit V}_{r}
is the
\displaystyle n\times r
matrix of dominant eigenvectors, and
\displaystyle{\mathbfit D}_{r}
is the
\displaystyle r\times r
diagonal matrix of dominant eigenvalues.
Therefore, the low rank approximation of
\displaystyle{\mathbfit H}
can be written as
\displaystyle\tilde{{\mathbfit H}}={\mathbfit L}^{-\top}\big({\mathbfit V}_{r}{\mathbfit D}_{r}{\mathbfit V}_{r}^{\top}+{\mathbfit I}\big){\mathbfit L}^{-1}
.

Lagrangian Formulation

We minimize the objective function (negative log-posterior) to find the MAP point. In this example, the objective function is defined as
\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(\theta)=\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}}\sum^{n}_{i=1}(p(\theta)-p^{i}_{obs})^{2}+\frac{1}{2}||\mathcal{A}(\theta-\theta_{pr})||^{2}
.

where
\displaystyle p(k)
,
\displaystyle\{p_{obs}\}^{N}_{i=1}
, and
\displaystyle\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}
are predicted values of the pore pressure, pressure measurements, and noise covariance, respectively.
\displaystyle\theta_{pr}

In addition, the Lagrangian form can be written as the summation of objective function and weak form of the governing equations:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\theta,p,\tilde{p},\mathbfit{\epsilon},\tilde{{\mathbfit u}})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\mathcal{J}(\theta)+\int^{\top}_{0}\int_{\Omega}(\trace(\mathbfit{\epsilon})_{t}+p_{t})\tilde{p}\ \mathrm{d}{\mathbfit x}\ \mathrm{d}t+\int^{\top}_{0}\int_{\Omega}\frac{e^{-\theta}}{\gamma}(\nabla p+{\mathbfit b})\cdot\nabla\tilde{p}\ \mathrm{d}{\mathbfit x}\ \mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\int^{\top}_{0}\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{e^{-\theta}}{\gamma}(\nabla p+{\mathbfit b})\cdot{\mathbfit n}\tilde{p}\ \mathrm{d}{\mathbfit x}\ \mathrm{d}t+\int^{\top}_{0}\int_{\Omega}({\mathbfit b}\cdot\tilde{{\mathbfit u}}-\mathbfit{\sigma}\cdot\tilde{{\mathbfit u}})\ \mathrm{d}{\mathbfit x}\ \mathrm{d}t

where
\displaystyle\tilde{p}
and
\displaystyle\tilde{{\mathbfit u}}

\displaystyle(\cdot)_{t}

\displaystyle\frac{\ \mathrm{d}(\cdot)}{\ \mathrm{d}t}

The optimality conditions (Karush-Kuhn-Tucker conditions) for solving the minimization problem, can be found by calculating the variation with respect to the Lagrangian function variables. We have used the

Appendix E
Sampling from High-dimensional Log-normal Distribution

In this section, we consider a high-dimensional log-normal distribution, the mean value and covariance of
\displaystyle\log(\mathbfit{\psi})
is assumed as the covariance and mean value of the first example (section
5.1
).
Since the range of
\displaystyle\mathbfit{\psi}
value is small, the negative and zero values for the log-normal distribution are not defined; taking samples from this distribution is complicated. To overcome this problem, we increase the mean value by adding a constant vector
\displaystyle{\mathbfit m}^{\prime}={\mathbfit m}+c\bf 1\Rightarrow\mathbfit{\psi}^{\prime}\sim\log\mathcal{N}({\mathbfit m}+c\bf 1,\mathbfit{\Sigma})
.

We apply MH-MCMC, HMC, and H-HMC (with the Hessian at the MAP point) to explore the log-normal distribution. The step size is assumed
\displaystyle\Delta t=1
for MH-MCMC and HMC methods, and
\displaystyle\Delta t=0.01
for the H-HMC method, and the acceptance rate for all of these methods is approximately
\displaystyle 98\%
.

We take
\displaystyle 5\times 10^{5}
samples to explore the log-normal distribution; since the
\displaystyle\mathbfit{\psi}^{\prime}
covers a wide range of values, the convergence speed of MH-MCMC and HMC methods is significantly lower than the convergence speed of the H-HMC method. Using the Hessian information as the mass matrix in the H-HMC method modifies the sampling process to explore the distribution in different directions (modes) faster.
Even after
\displaystyle 5\times 10^{5}
samples MH-MCMC, and HMC methods are far from convergence. However, H-HMC sampling chains converge after
\displaystyle 2\times 10^{5}
samples.
