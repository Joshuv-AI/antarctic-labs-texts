1
Introduction to Witness Encryption

Witness encryption for an NP language
L
(with witness relation
R
) involves allowing
decryption of a message, that has been encoded with respect to some problem instance
x
, if
x\in L
and one has a
witness

w
for which
R(x,w)=1
(
[
11
]
).
Along with applications in creating cryptographic primitives

[
11
]
, this allows us to encode monetary rewards
or messages for those who are able to solve a puzzle or problem.
To cite a famous example, consider the Riemann Hypothesis. Perhaps
the Clay Mathematics Institute would like us to encrypt a key to a
cryptocurrency wallet worth $1 million, retrievable only by those
who possess a correct proof to the Riemann Hypothesis.
Using witness encryption, the key to the wallet could be encrypted
using an encoding of the Riemann Hypothesis in some formal language,
with decryption possible if and only if a proof of the Riemann
Hypothesis is presented.

Note that the encrypting party themselves may not know whether or
not a solution exists
[
11
]
. This fact makes
witness encryption promising for problems of which the solution
to is unknown, but can be verified quickly.

Definition 1.1

(Witness Encryption by Garg et al.
[
9
]
)
.

We define witness encryption for an NP language
L
(specifically, solutions to the
Exact Cover
problem). Since the problem is in NP, we have a circuit
C
that, given a witness, can determine whether some
x
is in
L
or not. From here, our witness encryption scheme comprises of two PPT machines
(Enc,Dec)
. We will encrypt a single bit
m\in\{0,1\}
.

1.

\mathsf{Enc}(C,m)
outputs a cipher text
e\in\{0,1\}^{*}
.

2.

\mathsf{Dec}(C,e,w)
, where
w
is a witness. Our decryption box outputs

m
if
C(w)=1
and
\perp
otherwise.

We want these machines to satisfy two standard conditions.

•

Correctness:
Given that
\exists w
such that
C(w)=1
, we have
Dec(C,e,w)=m
.

•

Security:
If
\nexists w
such that
C(w)=1
, then

Enc(C,0)
is computationally indistinguishable from
Enc(C,1)

As Garg et. al notes, the security definition of witness encryption only requires indistinguishability of encryptions when the language
L
is empty (i.e. the problem is unsolvable). When
L\neq\varnothing
, this definition does not guarantee that an adversary with no knowledge of a witness would not be able to decrypt the ciphertext in polynomial time. To that end, there exist stronger definitions of witness encryptions, such as
extractable witness encryption
, which give security guarantees in the solvable case.

Witness Encryption Scheme

Encryption Scheme for
Exact Cover
from Garg et al.
[
9
]

Consider the
Exact Cover
problem, where
given some
S_{1},\ldots,S_{\ell}\in\mathcal{P}([n])
, we want to
find an index set
T\subset[\ell]
such that
\bigcup_{i\in T}S_{i}=[n]
and

S_{i}\cap S_{j}=\varnothing
for all
i\neq j
.

•

\mathsf{Enc}(x,m)
. Takes as input
x=(n,S_{1},\ldots,S_{\ell})
and bit
m\in\{0,1\}
. In order to encrypt this bit, perform the following

–

Sample
a_{0},\ldots,a_{n}
uniformly and independently from
\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{*}
.

–

Let
c_{i}=g_{|S_{i}|}^{\prod_{j\in S_{i}}a_{j}}
for all
i=1,\ldots,\ell
. We are encoding the value

\prod_{j\in S_{i}}a_{j}
at
level

|S_{i}|
. This notion of levels will be detailed in Section 3.

–

Sample uniformly an element
r\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{*}
.

–

If
m=1
, set
d=g^{\prod_{j\in[n]}a_{j}}
; otherwise set
d=g^{r}_{n}
.

–

Output ciphertext
c=(d,c_{1},\ldots,c_{\ell})
.

•

\mathsf{Dec}(x,w)
, where
w
is a witness that has some proposed
solution
T\subset[\ell]
. Compute
c^{*}=\prod_{i\in T}c_{i}
.
Output 1 if
c^{*}=d
and output 0 otherwise.

•

Correctness:
Clearly if
T
is indeed an exact
cover, we know that

\prod_{i\in T}c_{i}=\prod_{i\in T}g^{\prod_{j\in S_{i}}a_{j}}_{|S_{i}|}=g_{n}^{\prod a_{i}}

•

Security
This follows directly from Definition
2.1
below. In particular, consider the following reduction: given that no
witness
w
exists, if one could distinguish between
d=g^{\prod_{j\in[n]}a_{j}}
and the random
g_{n}^{r}
, then this
automatically breaks the Definition
2.1
assumption.

We will use this scheme, with a slight modification to a graded
encoding scheme that will be described below. In order to prevent

zeroizing attacks
, one of the primary pitfalls that graded
encoding schemes has had, we will use an asymmetric implementation of CLT13
[
7
]
, and set these levels to be vectors.

Existing Multilinear Map/Graded Encoding Scheme Constructions

While the existence of multilinear maps satisfying the above is still an open question, many candidates have been proposed, and subsequently broken. Multilinear maps are a special case of what are known as graded-encoding schemes.

Definition 3.1

(Symmetric
\kappa
-Graded Encoding Scheme,
[
10
,
7
]
)
.

A
\kappa
-Graded Encoding System for a ring
R
is a system of sets
\mathcal{S}=\{S_{v}^{(\alpha)}\subset\{0,1\}^{*}\ :\ v\in\mathbb{N},\alpha\in R\}
, where each set
S_{v}^{(\alpha)}
represents the collection of
v
-level encodings of
\alpha
, with the following properties:

1.

For every
v\in\mathbb{N}
, the sets
\{S^{(\alpha)}_{v}:\alpha\in R\}
are disjoint.

2.

There are binary operations
+
and
-
(on
\{0,1\}^{*}
) such that for every
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in R
, every
v\in\mathbb{N}
, and every
u_{1}\in S_{v}^{(\alpha_{1})}
and
u_{1}\in S_{v}^{(\alpha_{2})}
, it holds that
u_{1}+u_{2}\in S_{v}^{(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})}
and
u_{1}-u_{2}\in S_{v}^{(\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2})}
where
\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}
and
\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2}
are addition and subtraction in
R
.
There is an associative binary operation
\times
(on
\{0,1\}^{*}
) such that for every
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in R
, every
v_{1},v_{2}
with
0\leq v_{1}+v_{2}\leq\kappa
, and every
u_{1}\in S_{v_{1}}^{(\alpha_{1})}
and
u_{2}\in S_{v_{2}}^{(\alpha_{2})}
, it holds that
u_{1}\times u_{2}\in S^{(\alpha_{1}\cdot\alpha_{2})}_{v_{1}+v_{2}}
where
\alpha_{1}\cdot\alpha_{2}
is multiplication in
R
.

Graded encoding schemes, as outlined in GGH13
[
10
]
, have a set of procedures they provide:

•

Instance Generation.

\mathsf{InstGen}(1^{\lambda},1^{\kappa})
takes as inputs
\lambda
, the security parameter, and
\kappa
, the required multilinearity level, and outputs
(\mathsf{params},\boldsymbol{p}_{zt})
.

•

Ring Sampler.

\mathsf{samp}(\mathsf{params})
outputs a level-0 encoding
a\in S^{(a)}_{0}
, where
\alpha
is (nearly) uniform on
R
, though the encoding may not be uniform in
S_{0}^{(\alpha)}

•

Encoding.

\mathsf{enc}(\mathsf{params},a)
is a (possibly randomized, which in the case of CLT, it is) level-one encoding
u\in S^{(\alpha)}_{1}
, where
a\in S^{(\alpha)}_{0}
.

•

Re-Randomization.

\mathsf{reRand}(\mathsf{params},i,u)
re-randomizes encodings relative to the same level
i
, meaning given
u\in S^{(\alpha)}_{i}
, it outputs
u^{\prime}\in S^{(\alpha)}_{v}
such that, for any two
u_{1},u_{2}\in S^{(\alpha)}_{v}
, the output distributions of
\mathsf{reRand}(\mathsf{params},i,u_{1})
and
\mathsf{reRand}(\mathsf{params},i,u_{2})
are nearly the same.

•

Addition and negation.
For two encodings
u_{1}\in S^{(\alpha_{1})}_{i}
and
u_{2}\in S^{(\alpha_{2})}_{i}
at the same level
i
, we have
\mathsf{add}(\mathsf{params},u_{1},u_{2})=u_{1}+u_{2}\in S^{(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})}_{i}
and
\mathsf{neg}(\mathsf{params},u_{1})=-u_{1}=\in S^{(-\alpha_{1})}_{i}
.

•

Multiplication.
For two encodings
u_{1}\in S^{(\alpha_{1})}_{i}
and
u_{2}\in S^{(\alpha_{2})}_{j}
,
\mathsf{mul}(\mathsf{params},u_{1},u_{2})=u_{1}\times u_{2}\in S_{i+j}^{(\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2})}
.

•

Zero-test.
The procedure
isZero
, with outputs in
\{0,1\}
, satisfies
\mathsf{isZero}(\mathsf{params},\boldsymbol{p}_{zt},u)=1\iff u\in S_{\kappa}^{(0)}
, i.e. it reveals whether a given encoding is a top-level encoding of 0.

•

Extraction.

\mathsf{ext}(\mathsf{params},\boldsymbol{p}_{zt},u)
computes a random function of a ring element from their top-level encoding.

Multilinear maps can be viewed as a special case of graded encoding schemes, where the levels are given a group structure and where the encoding is deterministic.

For a multilinear map defined on the groups
G_{1}\times G_{2}\times\dots\times G_{n}
with respect to generators
(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})
, simply take
R=\mathbb{Z}/|G|\mathbb{Z}
and let
S^{(\alpha)}_{v}
just be
g_{v}^{\alpha}
. The operation
+
is then multiplication within a given group
G_{v}
, and
\times
is then application of the multilinear map, which takes
g_{v_{1}}^{\alpha_{1}},g_{v_{2}}^{\alpha_{2}}\mapsto g_{v_{1}+v_{2}}^{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}
.

GGH13 was the first proposal of a graded-encoding scheme
[
10
]
. We do not discuss the construction in detail, and instead focus on the construction used in our implementation, CLT13.

In the subsequent section, we outline portions of the originally proposed CLT13 construction relevant to our use and the break in CLHRS15
[
3
]
, as well as some other breaks.

3.1
CLT13 Graded Encoding Scheme Construction

3.1.1
Protocol

Overview:
We generate
n
secret random primes
p_{i}
, compute
x_{0}=\prod p_{i}
, and randomly sample some
z
that is invertible modulo
x_{0}
. We will encode a message
\boldsymbol{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
at level
k
as

\displaystyle c\equiv\frac{r_{i}\cdot g_{i}+m_{i}}{z^{k}}\pmod{p_{i}}

where
c
is well-defined modulo
x_{0}
via Chinese Remainder Theorem,
\{g_{i}\}
are random small primes generated at the start of the protocol and the
r_{i}
are small values generated for each encoding. Note that by construction,
m_{i}
is well defined modulo
g_{i}
, so in fact the scheme encodes vectors from the ring
R=\mathbb{Z}_{g_{1}}\times\cdots\times Z_{g_{n}}
.

Instance Generation.
For security parameter
\lambda
and multilinearity level
\kappa
, we generate the following:

•

n
secret random
\eta
-bit primes
p_{i}
. Let
x_{0}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}
. These
p_{i}
can be considered
ciphertext
moduli.

•

A random integer
z
that is invertible modulo
x_{0}
.

•

n
random
\alpha
-bit primes
g_{i}
(these are the
plaintext
moduli).

•

A secret matrix
\boldsymbol{A}=(a_{ij})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times\ell}
, where each
a_{ij}\leftarrow_{R}[0,g_{i})\cap\mathbb{Z}
.

•

An integer
y
, and three sets of integers
\{x_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\tau}
,
\{x^{\prime}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\ell}
,
\{\Pi_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}
, a zero-testing vector
\boldsymbol{p}_{zt}
, and a seed

for a strong randomness extractor. Of relevance for us are only the integer
y
, the sets
\{x^{\prime}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\ell}
and
\{x_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\ell}
, and the zero-testing vector
\boldsymbol{p}_{zt}

We publish the parameters
\mathsf{params}=(n,\eta,\alpha,\rho,\beta,\tau,\ell,y,\{x_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\tau},\{x^{\prime}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\ell},\{\Pi_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n},s)
.

Sampling level-zero encodings
:
\{x^{\prime}_{j}\}_{j=0}^{\ell}
is a set of level-0 encodings of the column vector
\boldsymbol{a}_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
of the secret matrix
\boldsymbol{A}
. More precisely, we set

\displaystyle x^{\prime}_{j}\equiv r^{\prime}_{ij}\cdot g_{i}+a_{ij}\pmod{p_{i}},\qquad r^{\prime}_{ij}\leftarrow_{R}(-2^{\rho},2^{\rho})\cap\mathbb{Z}

where
x^{\prime}_{j}
is defined
x_{0}
using Chinese Remainder Theorem. To generate a random level-0 encoding, we simply take a random subset sum of these
x^{\prime}_{j}
, i.e. we generate a random binary vector
\boldsymbol{b}\in\{0,1\}^{\ell}
and output, for
\mathsf{samp}(\mathsf{params})
,

\displaystyle c=\sum_{i:\boldsymbol{b}_{i}=1}x^{\prime}_{i}\mod x_{0}

which is then some encoding of
\boldsymbol{m}=\boldsymbol{A}\cdot\boldsymbol{b}
. The authors show that the distribution of this
\boldsymbol{m}
is then close to uniform over
R
.

Encoding at higher levels:

y
is a level-1 random encoding of
1
. Explicitly,

\displaystyle y\equiv\frac{r_{i}\cdot g_{i}+1}{z}\pmod{p_{i}}\qquad r_{i}\leftarrow_{R}(-2^{\rho},2^{\rho})\cap\mathbb{Z}

Now given a level-0 encoding
c
of some
\boldsymbol{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
(i.e.
c
satisfying
c\equiv r_{i}^{\prime}\cdot g_{i}+m_{i}\mod p_{i}
),
c_{1}=c\cdot y\mod x_{0}
is then a level-1 encoding:

\displaystyle c_{1}\equiv\frac{(r^{\prime}_{i}+m_{i}\cdot r_{i}+r_{i}\cdot r^{\prime}_{i}\cdot g_{i})\cdot g_{i}+m_{i}}{z}\pmod{p_{i}}

Thus, we have that
\mathsf{enc}(\mathsf{params},c)
computes
c^{\prime}=c\cdot y\mod x_{0}
. More generally, to compute a level
k
encoding, we compute
c\cdot y^{k}\mod x_{0}
.

However, in cases where it is necessary to prevent recovery of
c
given
c^{\prime}
, we must also rerandomize
c^{\prime}
at its encoding level using
\mathsf{reRand}
. This is done by publishing public encodings of
0
at level
k
, (for example, the
\{x_{j}\}
are encodings of
0
at level
1
), and adding them to
c^{\prime}
, as in the case of generating random level-0 encodings, but with slight adjustments. We omit the specific details of the construction.

Adding and Multiplying Encodings.
It is clear from the form of the encryptions that homomorphic addition and multiplication are possible, provided the numerators remain small enough.

Zero-testing.

\mathsf{isZero}(\mathsf{params},\boldsymbol{p}_{zt},u_{\kappa})
outputs a value in
\{0,1\}
, with
\mathsf{isZero}(\mathsf{params},\boldsymbol{p}_{zt},u_{\kappa})=1\iff u_{\kappa}\in S_{\kappa}^{(0)}
. This is done by constructing an appropriate integer matrix
\boldsymbol{H}=(h_{ij})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times n}
. We then publish the
zero-testing vector

\boldsymbol{p}_{zt}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
satisfying

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{p}_{zt})_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}h_{ij}\cdot(z^{\kappa}\cdot g_{i}^{-1}\mod p_{i})\cdot\frac{x_{0}}{p_{i}}

This enables us to test whether a top level encoding

is zero or not, by computing
\omega=c\cdot\boldsymbol{p}_{zt}\mod x_{0}
and testing whether
\|\omega\|_{\infty}=\max_{j}|\omega_{j}|
is less than
x_{0}\cdot 2^{-\nu}
, where

is another parameter used for the randomness extractor

, which we will not discuss. Intuitively, this works due to the fact that we can write

c=q_{i}\cdot p_{i}+(r_{i}\cdot g_{i}+m_{i})\cdot(z^{-\kappa}\mod p_{i})
, for some
q_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
, and then we have that

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{\omega})_{j}

\displaystyle=(c\cdot\boldsymbol{p}_{zt}\mod x_{0})_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}h_{ij}\cdot(r_{i}+m_{i}\cdot(g_{i}^{-1}\mod p_{i}))\cdot\frac{x_{0}}{p_{i}}\mod x_{0}

(1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}h_{ij}\cdot r_{i}\cdot\frac{x_{0}}{p_{i}}\mod x_{0}

Thus, when each

is small and each

is

will be small, provided

is chosen appropriately, the details of which we omit.

3.2
CLT13 Zeroizing attack

Attacks against the original CLT13 encryption scheme as well as other graded encoding schemes surfaced about a year later and were predominantly based on exploiting the multiple public encodings of zero that are published by the protocol. This class of attacks, termed as “zeroizing” attacks, were demonstrated to be able to successfully recover all secret parameters used in the encryption scheme, rendering it unusable.

3.2.1
CLHRS15
[
3
]
CHL
+
14 Zeroizing Attack

Keeping to the notation used by the authors, we define the following:
Let
\mathsf{CRT}_{(p_{1},\cdots,p_{n})}(r_{1},\cdots,r_{n})
be the unique integer
x
in the interval
(-\frac{1}{2}\prod_{i=1}^{n}p_{i},\frac{1}{2}\prod_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}]
that satisfies the congruence
x\equiv r_{i}\mod p_{i}
for all
i\in[n]
.
For succinctness we write
\mathsf{CRT}_{(p_{i})}(r_{i})
to represent the above.

The attack is predicated upon finding solutions to a modified version of the approximate greatest common divisor problem:

Definition 3.2

(Sampleable
\mathsf{CRT-ACD}
distribution
[
3
]
)
.

Let
n,\eta,\varepsilon\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\chi_{\varepsilon}
be some distribution over the integers in
(-2^{\varepsilon},2^{\varepsilon})
. Let
p_{1},\cdots,p_{n}
be
\eta
-bit primes and define
x_{0}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}
as in CLT. Then the
sampleable
\mathsf{CRT}
-
\mathsf{ACD}
distribution

\mathcal{D}_{\chi_{\varepsilon},\eta,n}(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\chi_{\varepsilon},\eta,n}(p_{1},\cdots,p_{n})=\{\mathsf{CRT}_{(p_{i})}(r_{i})\mid\forall i,\;r_{i}\leftarrow_{R}\chi_{\varepsilon}\}

Definition 3.3

(
\mathsf{CRT}
-
\mathsf{ACD}
with auxiliary input
[
3
]
)
.

Let
n,\eta,\varepsilon,\chi_{\varepsilon},p_{i},x_{0}
be defined as above. Additionally define
\widehat{p}_{i}=\frac{x_{0}}{p_{i}}
. Then the
\mathsf{CRT}
-
\mathsf{ACD}
with auxiliary input problem is the following:

Given the values
x_{0}
,
\widehat{P}=\mathsf{CRT}_{(p_{i})}(\widehat{p}_{i})
, and polynomially many samples from
\mathcal{D}_{\chi_{\varepsilon},\eta,n}(p_{1},\cdots,p_{n})
, determine the values of all
p_{i}
.

The solution-finding algorithm relies on the following lemma, proven in full in the paper:

Lemma 3.4
.

For a given auxiliary value
\widehat{P}=\mathsf{CRT}_{(p_{i})}(\widehat{p}_{i})
, any value
a=\mathsf{CRT}_{(p_{i})}(r_{i})
drawn from
\mathcal{D}_{\chi_{\varepsilon},\eta,n}(p_{1},\cdots,p_{n})
satisfies:

\displaystyle a\cdot\widehat{P}\mod x_{0}=\mathsf{CRT}_{(p_{i})}(r_{i}\cdot\widehat{p}_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}\cdot\widehat{p}_{i}

as long as
\varepsilon+\log n+1<\eta
.

Using this lemma, the paper demonstrates how to retrieve the secret parameters
p_{1},\cdots,p_{n}
of the CLT encryption given access to polynomially many samples from
\mathcal{D}_{\chi_{\varepsilon},\eta,n}(p_{1},\cdots,p_{n})
. Given two draws
a=\mathsf{CRT}_{(p_{i})}(a_{i})
and
b=\mathsf{CRT}_{(p_{i})}(b_{i})
, we have that
ab\widehat{P}\mod x_{0}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}b_{i}\widehat{p}_{i}\mod x_{0}
. Supposing that the
a_{i}
’s and
b_{i}
’s are small enough such that the above lemma is satisfied. Then the right-hand side is equal to
\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}b_{i}\widehat{p}_{i}
. This can be represented in matrix form as follows:

\displaystyle ab\widehat{P}\mod x_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&\cdots&a_{n}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\widehat{p}_{1}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&\widehat{p}_{n}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}b_{1}\\
\vdots\\
b_{n}\end{pmatrix}

For the attack, we assume a draw of
2n+1
samples from
\mathcal{D}_{\chi_{\varepsilon},\eta,n}(p_{1},\cdots,p_{n})
with the following labels:

\displaystyle a_{i}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{CRT}_{(p_{k})}(a_{k.i}),\quad i\in[n]

\displaystyle b

\displaystyle=\mathsf{CRT}_{(p_{k})}(b_{k})

\displaystyle c_{j}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{CRT}_{(p_{k})}(c_{k.j}),\quad j\in[n]

It is worth emphasizing here that we do not have access to the remainders
a_{k,i},b_{k},c_{k,j}
themselves.
To ensure that the conditions of Lemma
3.4
are satisfied, the parameters of the problem must additionally satisfy
3\varepsilon+\log n+1<\eta
.
Under those conditions, we define and compute the parameters
w_{i,j},w^{\prime}_{i,j}
as the following, where equality is given by Lemma
3.4
:

\displaystyle w_{i,j}=a_{i}bc_{j}\widehat{P}\mod x_{0}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k,i}b_{k}c_{k,j}\widehat{p}_{k}\qquad 1\leq i,j\leq n

\displaystyle w^{\prime}_{i,j}=a_{i}c_{j}\widehat{P}\mod x_{0}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k,i}c_{k,j}\widehat{p}_{k}\qquad\quad\;1\leq i,j\leq n

Using the matrix representation as presented earlier, the relations can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{A}^{T}\cdot\mathrm{diag}(b_{1}\widehat{p}_{1},\cdots,b_{n}\widehat{p}_{n})\cdot\mathbf{C}

\displaystyle\mathbf{W^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{A}^{T}\cdot\mathrm{diag}(\widehat{p}_{1},\cdots,\widehat{p}_{n})\cdot\mathbf{C}

where the matrices are defined as
\mathbf{A}^{T}=(a_{k,i})
,
\mathbf{C}=(c_{k,j})
,
\mathbf{W}=(w_{i,j})
, and
\mathbf{W^{\prime}}=(w^{\prime}_{i,j})
, all elements of
M_{n\times n}(\mathbb{Z})
.

Under the assumption that
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{C}
are invertible over
\mathbb{Q}
, the following matrix is well-defined:

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}:=\mathbf{W}\cdot(\mathbf{W^{\prime}})^{-1}=\mathbf{A}^{T}\cdot\mathrm{diag}(b_{1},\cdots,b_{n})\cdot(\mathbf{A}^{T})^{-1}

The eigenvalues of this matrix are then the set of remainders
\{b_{1},\cdots,b_{n}\}
, which can be computed in polynomial time.
It is shown in the paper that as long as the
b_{i}
’s are distinct, it suffices to compute a few

3.3
Modifications to CLT13/other attacks

Following the CHLRS15 attack, the CLT13 scheme was updated to the CLT15
[
5
]
scheme, where the zero-testing procedure was modified to be non-linear in the moduli, thus preventing the quadratic form representation in the previous section.
The scheme was also modified so that the prime
x_{0}
did not need to be published.
However, this modification was demonstrated in
[
4
]
to exhibit similar vulnerabilities. It turns out that using the public parameters, the value of
x_{0}
could be recovered, under which the original attack could then be mounted.

The attack in CHLRS15
[
3
]
was extended in
[
1
]
to not specifically require the level-0 encodings of
0
that are originally given publically in the parameters. As described in
[
6
]
, most rely on the ability to construct top-level encodings of
0
and successful zero tests.

As we will detail, our implementation of witness encryption will require no public encodings of zero (at level-
\kappa
or level-0). However, there still exist attacks for this, such as in
[
12
]
that do not explicitly require encodings of zeros beyond at the top level either.

3.4
Specifics of Our Implementation

The C implementation of CLT13 that we build upon has been modified to support asymmetric graded-encoding schemes, the definition of which we give below. Essentially, instead of having integer indices for the levels, we have vector indices; elements within the same level can still be added. Multiplying two elements produces an element that is indexed by the vector that is the sum of the original indices.

Definition 3.5

(
T
-Graded Encoding Scheme,
[
10
]
)
.

Let

be a finite set (for some integer
\tau>0
), and let

be a ring. A
T

is a system of sets
\mathcal{S}=\{S^{(\alpha)}_{\boldsymbol{v}}\subset\{0,1\}^{*}\mid v\in\Lambda(T),\alpha\in R\}
, with the following properties:

•

For every fixed index
\boldsymbol{v}\in\Lambda(T)
, the sets
\{S_{\boldsymbol{v}}^{(\alpha)}\mid\alpha\in R\}
are disjoint (hence they form a partition of
S_{\boldsymbol{v}}:=\bigcup_{\alpha}S_{\boldsymbol{v}}^{(\alpha)}
.

•

There are binary operations ‘
+
’ and ‘
-
’ (on
\{0,1\}^{*}
) such that for every
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}
in the same level
\boldsymbol{v}\in\Lambda(T)
, and every

and
u_{2}\in S_{\boldsymbol{v}}^{(\alpha_{2})}

\displaystyle u_{1}+u_{2}\in S_{\boldsymbol{v}}^{(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})}\text{ and }u_{1}-u_{2}\in S_{\boldsymbol{v}}^{(\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2})}

\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}
and

•

There is an associative binary operation ‘

\{0,1\}^{*}

\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in R

\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\boldsymbol{v}_{2}

Correctness.
If

4.2

4.2.1
class

We first give a theoretical outline of the reduction:

In Sudoku, the goal is to fill up a
9\times 9
grid, such that each row, column and
3\times 3
sub-grid contains the numbers 1-9 exactly once. The reduction from Sudoku to
Exact Cover
rests on the observation that the constraints of the problem can be succinctly stated as follows:

•

Each cell contains exactly one integer in the set
\{1,\cdots,9\}
.

•

Each integer appears exactly once in every row.

•

Each integer appears exactly once in every column.

•

Each integer appears exactly once in every sub-grid.

Each constraint in the list above is instanced
81
times in the puzzle. For example, the first applies to every pair
(r,c)
corresponding to the coordinates of a cell. Likewise, the second applies to every pair
(r,v)
of row index and value. A completed Sudoku grid is then a valid solution if those
4\times 81=324
constrains are all satisfied.

We begin by presenting how a blank Sudoku puzzle can be encoded, and the follow up with how to account for pre-filled cells.

For a blank Sudoku puzzle, we let the
324
elements of the set-cover problem represent the constraints. For concreteness, let these elements be
\{p_{1},\cdots,p_{81},q_{1},\cdots,q_{81},r_{1},\cdots r_{81},s_{1},\cdots,s_{81}\}
.
We let each set be associated with a triple
(r,c,v)
, which represents the assignment of the cell
(r,c)
to the value
v
. Each such assignment implies 4 statements.

•

Cell
(r,c)
contains one integer.

•

Row
r
contains one copy of
v
.

•

Column
c
contains one copy of
v
.

•

The sub-grid containing
(r,c)
contains one copy of
v
.

Therefore we can construct a map
F
that sends each triple
(r,c,v)
to some 4-element set
\{w,x,y,z\}
that represent each of the statements above. Then the
Exact Cover
problem over these elements and sets enforces that the collection of all such statements satisfy the constraints of the Sudoku problem.

For Sudoku puzzles with pre-filled cells, we transform the
Exact Cover
problem of a blank Sudoku to that of the particular puzzle by eliminating sets that correspond to conflicting assignments to the assignments already on the board (Say the top left corner is a ’1’. Then we get rid of the sets
(1,1,2),\cdots,(1,1,9)
since those correspond to assigning some other number to the top left cell, and so on).

We note that Sudoku can be extended to an

This reduction is slightly less involved since the constraints are easier to represent.

Suppose there are
5n
cells arranged in some manner on a grid and
n
Pentomino tiles. Then the goal of Pentomino tiling is to find an arrangement of the
n
tiles such that each cell on the board is covered by exactly one tile.

The elements in the corresponding exact set cover can be taken to be the set
\{c_{1},\cdots,c_{5n},p_{1},\cdots,p_{n}\}
, where the
c
’s correspond to some labelling of the cells and the
p
’s correspond to some labelling of the Pentominos. Each set then has 6 elements and takes the form
\{c_{i_{1}},c_{i_{2}},c_{i_{3}},c_{i_{4}},c_{i_{5}},p_{i}\}
, where
p_{i}
is a certain Pentomino and the
c_{i}
’s are a set of cells that can potentially be covered by that Pentomino. It is clear that the
Exact Cover
problem encodes the constraints in the original tiling problem.

In the classic Pentomino tiling problem, the board has exactly 60 cells and there is exactly one of each type of Pentomino. However, this can also be generalized to arbitrary board sizes and sets of available Pentominos (with multiplicity).

, along with instructions on how to load the puzzles and decrypt them.

Sudoku 2 was encrypted with security parameter
\lambda=20
, an intentionally low security parameter (and implicitly
\kappa=248
, which is determined by the number of clues). Sudokus 1 and 3 are encrypted with
\lambda=60
- a more reasonable security parameter (though still lower than what is regularly used in private key encryption).

For the first clever individual
to solve the puzzle, we have encrypted the 256 bits of randomness used to generate a private key for a wallet containing a large number of Satoshi. In order to decrypt the bits, one should clone a copy of our code (also hosted
here
, more detailed instructions in the README) onto their local repository in order to instantiate the graded encoding scheme and reduce their solution into an element of the ring
c^{*}\in\mathbb{Z}_{x_{0}}
.

The Sudoku in figures 2 and 3 are connected to Bitcoin wallets with 2,770 Satoshi (in reference to Harvard’s CS 227). This is worth about $1.10 at writing. The unsolvable Sudoku in figure 1 is our grand prize, as the funds can only be retrieved through breaking our scheme. This wallet contains 27,700 Satoshi, worth $11 at writing.
