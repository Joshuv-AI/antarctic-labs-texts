Clustering theorem in 1D long-range interacting systems at arbitrary temperatures
Yusuke Kimura 1, Tomotaka Kuwahara 1,2,3

Abstract
This paper delves into a fundamental aspect of quantum statistical mechanics—the absence of thermal phase transitions in one-dimensional (1D) systems. Originating from Ising’s analysis of the 1D spin chain, this concept has been pivotal in understanding 1D quantum phases, especially those with finite-range interactions, as extended by Araki. In this work, we focus on quantum long-range interactions and successfully derive a clustering theorem applicable to a wide range of interaction decays at arbitrary temperatures. This theorem applies to any interaction forms that decay faster than r − 2 r^{-2} and does not rely on translation invariance or infinite system size assumptions. Also, we rigorously established that the temperature dependence of the correlation length is given by e const . β e^{{\rm const.}\beta} , which is the same as the classical cases. Our findings indicate the absence of phase transitions in 1D systems with super-polynomially decaying interactions, thereby expanding upon previous theoretical research. To overcome significant technical challenges originating from the divergence of the imaginary-time Lieb–Robinson bound, we utilize the quantum belief propagation to refine the cluster expansion method. This approach allowed us to address divergence issues effectively and contributed to a deeper understanding of low-temperature behaviors in 1D quantum systems.



I
Introduction

One of the most fundamental aspects of quantum statistical mechanics is the absence of thermal phase transition in one-dimensional (1D) systems. This concept has historical roots tracing back to the seminal work of Ising
Ising et al. 2017
, who solved the one-dimensional spin chain problem, thereby illuminating the lack of phase transition in 1D. Building upon this, Araki
Araki 1969
extended these insights into the quantum domain by investigating general quantum spin systems with finite-range interactions, particularly in the thermodynamic limit.
In quantum equilibrium states, non-critical phases are typically characterized by a finite correlation length for bipartite correlations within quantum Gibbs states. Furthermore, the analyticity of the partition function
Dobrushin and Shlosman 1987
and the uniqueness of the Kubo–Martin–Schwinger (KMS) state
Kubo 1957
;
Martin and Schwinger 1959
;
Araki 1978
are also recognized as alternative hallmarks of non-criticality in these systems.

In contrast to one-dimensional (1D) systems, thermal phase transitions are more prevalent in higher-dimensional systems and have been a subject of extensive study in the history of statistical mechanics
Onsager 1944
;
Stanley 1971
. In these higher-dimensional settings, the absence of phase transitions is typically only guaranteed at higher temperatures. Interestingly, even within the confines of one dimension, it has been known that long-range interactions, characterized by a slow decay in interaction strength, can lead to thermal phase transitions.
This poses a significant question regarding the occurrence of thermal phase transitions under interactions of varying ranges, a topic that has been actively researched for many decades. The nature of these interactions is often modeled by the polynomial decay with distance
r
, expressed as
r^{-\alpha}
. Here, the critical value of
\alpha
plays a crucial role: In classical theory, if
\alpha\leq 2
, it indicates the presence of a thermal phase transition
Dyson 1969
;
Fisher et al. 1972
;
Kosterlitz 1976
;
Thouless 1969
, whereas
\alpha>2
suggests its absence
Dobrushin 1973
. Further insights can be found in the footnote
*1
*1

*1

Precisely, the condition is related to the finiteness of the boundary energy between two split subsystems. Generally, for polynomially decaying interactions, this finiteness is assured when
\alpha>2

Kuwahara and Saito 2020a
.
.

A pivotal question in the study of quantum long-range interacting chains is the determination of the regime of
\alpha
that ensures non-criticality at arbitrary temperatures. While initial studies, such as those by Araki
Araki 1975
and Kishimoto
Kishimoto 1976
, have established the uniqueness of the KMS state in 1D systems with long-range interactions for
\alpha>2
,
it has been still challenging to characterize the 1D quantum phases beyond these foundational works. Remarkably, it has been over half a century since the generalization of the methodologies used in Araki’s seminal work
Araki 1969
.
In
Araki 1969
, the analysis of the 1D Ising model was conducted using an intricate operator algebra technique. This advanced mathematical approach enabled the exploration of phase transitions within a specific scope, notably in systems where interactions were of finite range and exhibited translational invariance. However, the complexity of these methods presented substantial challenges in generalizing the analyses to broader system classes, particularly in contexts where these constraints were not met. As a result, the pursuit of understanding phase transitions in more generalized settings remained a formidable task, underscoring the need for novel approaches in this field of research.

In recent developments, the paper by Pérez-García et al.
Pérez-García and Pérez-Hernández 2023
has shed light on the challenges in generalizing the understanding of quantum long-range interacting systems. A significant hurdle identified is the divergence of the imaginary-time Lieb–Robinson bound, which describes the quasi-locality of systems after undergoing imaginary-time evolution. This divergence, as generally proven in
Bouch 2015
, implies that an imaginary-time-evolved local operator becomes entirely non-local across the whole system after a threshold time.
However, in one-dimensional systems with finite-range interactions, this quasi-locality is preserved for any imaginary time. In these cases, the length scale of the quasi-locality increases exponentially with imaginary time, as detailed in
(
Kuwahara and Saito 2018
, Lemma 1)
for example. Extending beyond finite-range interactions, Pérez-García et al.
Pérez-García and Pérez-Hernández 2023
demonstrated that the convergence of the imaginary Lieb–Robinson bound is attainable as long as the interaction decays super-exponentially with distance.
For interactions that decay exponentially or slower, the imaginary Lieb–Robinson bound remains convergent only below a specific threshold time.
This insight indicates that any attempt to generalize beyond exponentially decaying interactions requires the development of alternative mathematical techniques to effectively handle one-dimensional quantum Gibbs states.

In our current work, we address a portion of this open problem and derive a clustering theorem applicable to any form of interaction decay at arbitrary temperatures. The merits of our results are summarized as follows:

1.

The theorem is applicable to all forms of interaction, provided the decay rate is faster than
r^{-2}
[see the assumption (
5
)].

2.

Our result is established unconditionally, meaning we do not assume translation invariance or infinite system size.
Moreover, for slower decay of interactions than subexponential form, the obtained decay rate for the bi-correlation is qualitatively optimal in the sense that it shows qualitatively the same behavior as the interaction decay (see Theorem
1
).

3.

We determine the explicit form of the correlation length as a function of the inverse temperature. In detail, we identified that the correlation length is proportional to
e^{{\rm const.}\times\beta}
. This is qualitatively optimal since the correlation length in the classical Ising model has an exponential dependence on the inverse temperature.

These strengths enable the broad application of our findings, extending and generalizing the scope of previous works based on Araki’s foundational studies
Araki 1969
. Examples include clustering theorems for mutual information
Bluhm et al. 2022
and conditional mutual information
Kato and Brandão 2019
, refined characterizations of locality properties
Ángela Capel et al. 2024
;
Bluhm et al. 2024
,
studies on rapid thermalization
Bardet et al. 2023
, and the equivalence of statistical mechanical ensembles
Brandao and Cramer 2015
;
Kuwahara and Saito 2020b
;
Kuwahara and Saito 2020c
, efficient simulation of the quantum Gibbs state
Fawzi et al. 2023
;
Trivedi et al. 2024
, learning the thermal states
Rouzé et al. 2024
, among others.
However, our results are not without limitations:

1.

In the case of exponentially decaying interactions, the decay rate of the bipartite correlation function is subexponential, following
e^{-\Omega(\sqrt{R})}
(where
R
denotes distance), rather than an exponential form.

2.

Our findings do not extend to the analyticity of the partition function. For finite systems, analyzing the positions of complex zeroes in the partition function is essential
Harrow et al. 2020
. Currently, our techniques appear limited in analyzing quantum Gibbs states at complex temperatures.

Despite these limitations, our research significantly enhances the understanding of the structure of 1D quantum Gibbs states at low temperatures. Notably, our results suggest the absence of phase transitions in any one-dimensional system with super-polynomially decaying interactions, a significant extension from previous results
Pérez-García and Pérez-Hernández 2023
limited to super-exponentially decaying interactions

*2

There is still potential for a phase transition from purely exponential decay to subexponential decay of bipartite correlations.
.

Finally, we highlight our principal technical contributions in this work. To address the challenge of the divergence of the imaginary Lieb–Robinson bound, we have integrated the quantum belief propagation technique
Hastings 2007
;
Kim 2012
with the established Lieb–Robinson bound
Lieb and Robinson 1972
;
Bravyi et al. 2006

Brandão and Kastoryano 2019
;
Anshu et al. 2021
;
Kuwahara et al. 2021
;
Kuwahara and Saito 2022
.
We show the basic techniques in Sec.

.
Our approach utilizes the quantum belief propagation method to refine the cluster expansion technique (see Sec.
IV

Gross 1979
;
Park and Yoo 1995
;
Ueltschi 2004
;
Kliesch et al. 2014
;
Fröhlich and Ueltschi 2015
;
Kuwahara et al. 2020
. To circumvent this issue at arbitrary temperatures, we draw upon strategies from classical cases, where divergence can be more readily avoided through the use of a standard transfer-matrix method (see Sec.
IV.1
).
In our exploration, we identify several challenges in extending these classical methods to quantum scenarios.
Our paper proposes solutions to these challenges (see Sec.

II
Setup

Consider a quantum system consisting of
n
spins, or qudits, arranged in a one-dimensional (1D) chain. We denote the total set of spins by
\Lambda
.
For any arbitrary subset
X
within
\Lambda
, denoted as
X\subseteq\Lambda
, the cardinality of
X
—the number of sites in
X
—is represented by
|X|
. Given that the system contains
n
spins, it follows that
|\Lambda|=n
.
For a sequence of subsets
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{q}
, we use the notation
X_{1:q}
to represent the union of the
X_{i}
’s, i.e.,
X_{1:q}:=X_{1}\cup X_{2}\cup\cdots\cup X_{q}
.
For any two subsets
X
and
Y
in
\Lambda
, we define the distance between them,
d_{X,Y}
, as the shortest path length on the graph connecting
X
and
Y
. It is important to note that if
X
and
Y
have a non-empty intersection, the distance between them is considered to be zero. Additionally, when
X
consists of a single element, i.e.,
X=\{i\}
, we simplify the notation for distance from
d_{\{i\},Y}
to
d_{i,Y}
for convenience.
We denote the complement of
X
by
X^{\rm c}
, i.e.,
X^{\rm c}:=\Lambda\setminus X
, and the boundary of
X
by
\partial X
, defined as
\partial X:=\{i\in X\,|\,d_{i,X^{\rm c}}=1\}
.

We consider a general
k
-local Hamiltonian
H
, described by

\displaystyle H=\sum_{Z:|Z|\leq k}h_{Z},

(1)

where
h_{Z}
is an arbitrary operator supported on the subset
Z\subset\Lambda
with
|Z|\leq k
.
For an arbitrary subset
L
, we denote the subset Hamiltonian supported on
L
by
H_{L}
:

\displaystyle H_{L}=\sum_{Z:Z\subseteq L}h_{Z}.

(2)

We characterize the interaction decay as follows: Let
g>0
, and
\bar{J}:\mathbb{N}\to[0,\infty)
be a monotonically decreasing function with
\bar{J}(0)=1
such that

\displaystyle J_{i,i^{\prime}}:=\sum_{Z:Z\ni\{i,i^{\prime}\}}\left\|h_{Z}\right\|\leq g\bar{J}(d_{i,i^{\prime}}).

(3)

Since the total system
\Lambda
is finite, such
g
and
\bar{J}
always exist.
We note that the one-site energy is upper-bounded by
g
:

\displaystyle\sum_{Z:Z\ni i}\left\|h_{Z}\right\|\leq g\bar{J}(0)=g.

(4)

We assume that
\bar{J}(x)
decays faster than
1/x^{2}
in the following sense: We assume that there exists
\gamma>0
such that for
z\in\{0,1\}
and all
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, the following inequality holds:

\displaystyle\sum_{x=\ell}^{\infty}x^{z}\bar{J}(x)\leq\gamma\ell^{z+1}\bar{J}(\ell).

(5)

For Hamiltonians with finite-range interactions, we define the term interaction length,
d_{H}
, such that for any distance
d_{i,j}
larger than
d_{H}
(
d_{i,j}>d_{H}
),
\bar{J}(d_{i,j})
vanishes, i.e.,
\bar{J}(d_{i,j})=0
.

We define the quantum Gibbs state
\rho_{\beta}
at a fixed inverse temperature
\beta
as follows:

\displaystyle\rho_{\beta}:=\frac{e^{\beta H}}{Z_{\beta}},\quad Z_{\beta}={\rm tr}\left(e^{\beta H}\right).

(6)

In this definition, the minus sign appearing in the standard Gibbs state is included in the Hamiltonian itself. Throughout the analyses, we assume that the interaction terms satisfy the following condition:

\displaystyle h_{Z}\succeq 0\quad\textrm{for}\quad Z\subset\Lambda.

(7)

This assumption can always be satisfied by appropriately shifting the energy origin,specifically by adding a constant operator
\left\|h_{Z}\right\|\hat{1}_{Z}
to
-h_{Z}
.

In the following,
\Theta(x)
will always denote a function of the form

\displaystyle\theta_{0}+\theta_{1}\,x,

(8)

with positive coefficients
\theta_{0},\theta_{1}
that only depend on the fundamental parameters
g
,
k
and
\gamma
.

For an arbitrary operator
O
, we define the time evolution of
O
by another operator
O^{\prime}
(e.g., a subset Hamiltonian
H_{L}
) as

\displaystyle O(O^{\prime},t):=e^{iO^{\prime}t}Oe^{-iO^{\prime}t}.

(9)

For simplicity, we usually denote
O(H,t)
by
O(t)
.

We use
O_{X}
to represent an operator that acts on the Hilbert space supported on the subset
X\subset\Lambda
. Given operators
O_{X}
and
O_{Y}
supported on subsets
X,Y\subset\Lambda
, the correlation function
{\rm Cor}_{\rho_{\beta}}(O_{X},O_{Y})
is defined as follows:

\displaystyle{\rm Cor}_{\rho_{\beta}}(O_{X},O_{Y}):={\rm tr}(\rho_{\beta}O_{X}O_{Y})-{\rm tr}(\rho_{\beta}O_{X}){\rm tr}(\rho_{\beta}O_{Y}).

(10)

The correlation length
\xi_{\beta,\delta}
is such that for any sets
X
and
Y
with a distance
d_{X,Y}
larger than
\xi_{\beta,\delta}
, i.e.,
d_{X,Y}>\xi_{\beta,\delta}
, the correlation between them becomes smaller than
\delta
, where
\delta
is an arbitrary constant. For example, when the correlation function
{\rm Cor}_{\rho_{\beta}}(O_{X},O_{Y})
is proportional to
(d_{X,Y}/\eta_{\beta})^{-\alpha}
, i.e.,
{\rm Cor}_{\rho_{\beta}}(O_{X},O_{Y})=c(d_{X,Y}/\eta)^{-\alpha}
, the parameter
\eta_{\beta}
characterizes the correlation length, meaning
\xi_{\beta,\delta}=\eta_{\beta}(c/\delta)^{1/\alpha}
. Our purpose is to identify the
\beta
dependence of
\xi_{\beta,\delta}
when
\delta
is an arbitrary constant independent of
\beta
.

For
\Lambda_{0}
and
L,L^{\prime}\subset\Lambda
, we define the interaction operator

between

and
L^{\prime}
as follows:

\displaystyle V_{L,L^{\prime}}(\Lambda_{0}):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Z:Z\subset\Lambda_{0}\\
Z\cap L\neq\emptyset,Z\cap L^{\prime}\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}h_{Z}.

(11)

V_{L,L^{\prime}}(\Lambda_{0})
characterizes the interactions between blocks

and

acting on the region

. Following Ref.
(

The norm of

is bounded above by

\displaystyle\|V_{L,L^{\prime}}(\Lambda_{0})\|\leq g\gamma^{2}r^{2}\bar{J}(r),

(12)

where

represents the distance between

and

, and the function

is defined in Eq. (

Proof of Lemma
1
.

In our proof, we aim to calculate an upper bound for the term
\overline{V}_{L,L^{\prime}}
, defined as:

\displaystyle\overline{V}_{L,L^{\prime}}:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Z:Z\cap L\neq\emptyset,\\
Z\cap L^{\prime}\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\|h_{Z}\|,

(13)

.
Assuming that subsystem

is located to the right of

, we define

and

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Z:Z\cap L\neq\emptyset,\\

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=0}^{n_{L}-1}\sum_{j^{\prime}=0}^{n_{L^{\prime}}-1}\sum_{Z:Z\ni\{i_{0}-j,i_{0}+r+j^{\prime}\}}\|h_{Z}\|\leq g\sum_{j=0}^{n_{L}-1}\sum_{j^{\prime}=0}^{n_{L^{\prime}}-1}\bar{J}(r+j+j^{\prime}),

where we used inequality (

Furthermore, substituting

and

\displaystyle\sum_{j^{\prime}=0}^{n_{L^{\prime}}-1}\bar{J}(r+j+j^{\prime})\leq\sum_{x=r+j}^{\infty}\bar{J}(x)\leq\gamma(r+j)\bar{J}(r+j).

Similarly, substituting

II.1
Main results

For our purpose, we categorize the forms of interaction into two classes: i) faster than or equal to sub-exponential decay, i.e.,
\bar{J}(r)\propto e^{-r^{\kappa}}
,
(0<\kappa\leq 1)

*3
*3

*3

We can treat the super-exponentially decaying interactions by reducing them to the case of
\kappa=1
.
The decay rate of the Lieb–Robinson bound cannot be improved from the exponential form since the condition (
32
) breaks down for
\bar{J}(r)\propto e^{-r^{\kappa}}
with
\kappa>1
.
, and ii) slower than sub-exponential decay, i.e.,
\lim_{r\to\infty}e^{-r^{\kappa}}/\bar{J}(r)\to 0
for
\forall\kappa>0
.
For both types of interaction decay, we can prove the following theorem:

Theorem 1
.

Let
O_{X}
and
O_{Y}
be arbitrary unit-norm operators supported on subsets
X
and
Y
(
X,Y\subset\Lambda
),
\left\|O_{X}\right\|=\left\|O_{Y}\right\|=1
, and
R:=d_{X,Y}
. If there exist
\kappa>0
and
c>0
such that
\bar{J}(r)\leq e^{-cr^{\kappa}}
for all
r\geq 0
(i.e., when the interaction decays subexponentially or faster), then the correlation function
{\rm Cor}_{\rho_{\beta}}(O_{X},O_{Y})
satisfies the following inequality:

\displaystyle{\rm Cor}_{\rho_{\beta}}(O_{X},O_{Y})\leq c_{1}e^{-R_{\beta}^{\kappa/(\kappa+1)}}

(18)

for some positive real constant
c_{1}
that depends only on the system details, where
R_{\beta}=R/\xi_{\beta}
and
\xi_{\beta}:=e^{\Theta(\beta)}
.
\Theta(\beta)
is a function of the form (
8
).

In the case where the interaction decays more slowly than subexponential forms, there exists a positive real constant
c_{2}
, and the following inequality holds:

\displaystyle{\rm Cor}_{\rho_{\beta}}(O_{X},O_{Y})

\displaystyle\leq c_{2}R_{\beta}^{2}\bar{J}\left(R_{\beta}/\log^{2}\left(\bar{J}(R)^{-1}\right)\right).

(19)

For any
\varepsilon>0
there exists
r_{0,\beta}
such that for
R>r_{0,\beta}
,

\displaystyle\bar{J}\left(R_{\beta}/\log^{2}\left(\bar{J}(R)^{-1}\right)\right)\leq\bar{J}\left(R_{\beta}^{1-\epsilon}\right).

(20)

Remark.
We mention several points below:

1.

The bound in (
18
) is independent of
|X|
and
|Y|
. For practical applications, it is quite important to keep track of how the bound depends on the size of the supports. See
Ángela Capel et al. 2024
for relevant discussion.

2.

In deriving the results, we do not need to assume translation invariance or an infinite system size.
The key technique we use is quantum belief propagation (discussed in Sec.
III.3
), along with the Lieb–Robinson bound (to be discussed in Sec.
III.1
), as an alternative to the imaginary Lieb–Robinson bound, which played a crucial role in the original works
Pérez-García and Pérez-Hernández 2023
;
Araki 1969
.

3.

We identified the dependence of the correlation length on the inverse temperature,
\beta
.
The correlation length
\xi_{\beta,\delta}
defined below Eq. (
10
) is now given by

\displaystyle\xi_{\beta,\delta}=K(\delta)\xi_{\beta}

(21)

with
K:\mathbb{R}^{+}\to[0,\infty)
, where the explicit form of
K(\delta)
depends on the interaction decay
\bar{J}(r)
.
From the above expression, it can be seen that
\beta
dependence of the correlation length is at most exponential.
As far as we know, this is the first result to rigorously prove that the correlation length follows the form
\xi_{\beta}=e^{{\rm const}\times\beta}
.
This type of dependence is often assumed as empirical knowledge without proof, e.g., in Ref.
Kato and Brandão 2019
.

4.

When the interaction decay is polynomial, such as
\bar{J}(r)\propto r^{-\alpha}
, the clustering theorem states that there is a positive real constant
c_{3}
and the inequality

\displaystyle{\rm Cor}_{\rho_{\beta}}(O_{X},O_{Y})

\displaystyle\leq c_{3}R_{\beta}^{-\alpha+2}\log^{2\alpha}(R)=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(R^{-\alpha+2})

(22)

holds.

5.

The bipartite correlation is expected to be lower-bounded by the interaction strength between
X
and
Y
:

\displaystyle{\rm Cor}_{\rho_{\beta}}(O_{X},O_{Y})\gtrsim\left\|V_{X,Y}(\Lambda)\right\|\propto R^{2}\bar{J}(R).

(23)

The latter relation is obtained when we choose
X=(-\infty,x]
and
Y=[x+R+1,\infty)
in the thermodynamic limit.
Therefore, the clustering theorem (
19
) for slower-decaying interactions is expected to be qualitatively optimal in the limit at which
|X|\to\infty
and
|Y|\to\infty
.

6.

On the other hand, when there exist small positive constants
n_{1}
and
n_{2}
, such that
|X|\leq n_{1}
and
|Y|\leq n_{2}
hold, the upper bound may be improved to the following form:

\displaystyle{\rm Cor}_{\rho_{\beta}}(O_{X},O_{Y})\lesssim\left\|V_{X,Y}(\Lambda)\right\|\propto|X|\cdot|Y|\bar{J}(R).

(24)

At this stage, our current analyses cannot be straightforwardly extended to derive this upper bound.
For example, at high temperatures, one can prove an improved version of the clustering theorem in long-range interacting systems
Kim et al.
.
In this context, the optimal clustering theorem for such
|X|
and
|Y|
remains open, even for power-law interaction decays.

7.

For sub-exponentially decaying interactions, our results are far from optimal. In particular, for
\kappa=1
, our bound gives

\displaystyle{\rm Cor}_{\rho_{\beta}}(O_{X},O_{Y})=e^{-\Omega\left(\sqrt{R_{\beta}}\right)}\quad\textrm{for}\quad\bar{J}(r)\leq e^{-{\rm const.}r},

(25)

which is worse than the previous results in Refs.
Pérez-García and Pérez-Hernández 2023
;
Araki 1969
, which achieved exponential decay of correlation for arbitrary interaction decay with
\kappa>1
.
Still, our result has the advantage of not requiring threshold temperatures as in Ref.
Pérez-García and Pérez-Hernández 2023
, and our theorem holds without assuming an infinite system size or translation invariance.

II.2
Technical overview for the proof

The main techniques are summarized in the following three points:

1.

Construction of the interaction-truncated Hamiltonian (Sec.
III.2
),

2.

Cluster expansion technique (Sec.
IV
),

3.

Efficient block decomposition using quantum belief propagation operators (Sec.
V.2
).

The first technique serves as the foundation for previous work on proving the long-range entanglement area law
Kuwahara and Saito 2020a
.
Here, we do
not
simply truncate all the long-range interactions across the entire region, but only those around the boundary of interest (in this case, the region between
X
and
Y
. See also Fig.
2
below).
For this interaction truncated Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\left\|[O_{Z}(H_{\rm t},t),O_{Z^{\prime}}]\right\|\leq\left\|O_{Z}\right\|\cdot\left\|O_{Z^{\prime}}\right\|\cdot|Z|\cdot|Z^{\prime}|e^{v|t|}\tilde{\mathcal{F}}(r),\quad r=d_{Z,Z^{\prime}},

(26)

where
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}(r)
is defined as
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}(r)=C\min\left(e^{-r/(2l_{0})},\bar{J}(r)\right)
with
C
a constant defined in Eq. (
36
) and

is the cutoff interaction length.
Therefore, under interaction truncation, exponential quasi-locality through time evolution is restored.
Additionally, in inequality (
III.2
), we can show that the closeness between
e^{\beta H}
and
e^{\beta H_{\rm t}}
is approximately estimated as

\left\|e^{\beta H}-e^{\beta H_{\rm t}}\right\|_{1}\leq\beta Rl_{0}\bar{J}(l_{0})Z_{\beta}

with
\bar{J}(l_{0})
representing the decay rate of interaction as in (
3
).

As the second analytical tool, we employ the cluster expansion technique
Kliesch et al. 2014
;
Fröhlich and Ueltschi 2015
.
Following the notation in Ref.
Kliesch et al. 2014
(see also Sec.
IV
), we express the correlation function as
{\rm Cor}_{\rho_{\beta}}(O_{X},O_{Y})={\rm tr}(\rho_{\rm cl}O_{X}^{(0)}O_{Y}^{(1)})
, where
\rho_{\rm cl}
is given by the sum of the relevant cluster terms [which will be presented later in Eq. (
113
)]:

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm cl}:=\frac{1}{Z_{\beta}^{2}}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\sum_{Z_{1},Z_{2},\ldots,Z_{m}:{\rm connected}}\frac{\beta^{m}}{m!}h_{Z_{1}}^{(+)}h_{Z_{2}}^{(+)}\cdots h_{Z_{m}}^{(+)}.

(27)

Here, the operators
O^{(+)}
,
O^{(0)}
and
O^{(1)}
are defined by applying a copy of the original Hilbert space:

O^{(+)}=O\otimes\hat{1}+\hat{1}\otimes O
,
O^{(0)}=O\otimes\hat{1}
and
O^{(1)}=O\otimes\hat{1}-\hat{1}\otimes O
.
As is well-known
Kotecký and Preiss 1986
;
Mann and Minko 2024
, each term’s norm in the expansion (
27
) is bounded above by the form
\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}(\beta/\beta_{c})^{m}
, and this upper bound diverges for
\beta>\beta_{c}
.
Because of this, even in one-dimensional cases, the method of simply counting the connected clusters does not work for values of
\beta
above a threshold
\beta_{c}
.
In 1D cases, the cluster expansion (
27
) corresponds to the removal of zeroth-order interaction terms that connect subsets
X
and
Y
.
In classical (i.e., commuting) cases, this method allows us to efficiently count connected clusters, analogous to the transfer-matrix method (Sec.
IV.1
below).

When extending the analysis from commuting to non-commuting cases, we encounter a significant challenge: we cannot independently eliminate the zeroth-order interaction terms. This difficulty arises because operators do not commute in the non-commuting case.
To explain this issue in more detail, consider the exponential operator
e^{\lambda_{1}O_{1}+\lambda_{2}O_{2}}
and the task of removing the zeroth-order terms for
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
.
In the commuting case, the zeroth-order terms can be easily removed by factorizing as follows:

\left(e^{\lambda_{1}O_{1}}-1\right)\left(e^{\lambda_{2}O_{2}}-1\right).

However, in the non-commuting case, this factorization is not possible because
[O_{1},O_{2}]\neq 0
.
A physical interpretation of this is the following. In general, the effects of local interactions for quantum Gibbs states are not confined to a specific location but spread over distant sites.

To address this challenge, we partition the entire system into blocks of length
2\ell
, in such a way that, approximately, the central sites of the resulting blocks are independent of one another, as illustrated in Fig.
5
.
The approximation error,
\mathcal{G}_{\beta}(\ell)
, is evaluated based on the quasi-local nature of quantum belief propagation, as detailed in Proposition
10
.
This evaluation can also be done using imaginary time evolution, provided that the interaction decay is faster than exponential. However, quantum belief propagation offers better error estimates and applies to all forms of interaction.
For each central site of the blocks, it becomes possible to effectively eliminate the zeroth-order terms, allowing us to approximate the correlation decay rate as
e^{-m/e^{\Theta(\beta)}}
, where
m
represents the number of blocks, which scales with
R/\ell
(see also Subtheorem
1
).
Conversely, to reduce the approximation error
\mathcal{G}_{\beta}(\ell)
, a larger block size
\ell
is preferred. However, choosing a larger block size reduces the number of blocks,
m\propto R/\ell
, and thus weakens the decay rate to
e^{-m/e^{\Theta(\beta)}}
. Selecting optimal values for
m
and
\ell
to minimize both
\mathcal{G}_{\beta}(\ell)
and
e^{-m/e^{\Theta(\beta)}}
is crucial for our main theorem, with explicit choices shown in Sec.
V.3
.
We note that this trade-off between
\mathcal{G}_{\beta}(\ell)
and
e^{-m/e^{\Theta(\beta)}}
prevents us from achieving the optimal clustering theorem for interactions that decay faster than sub-exponentially (see also the concluding remarks in Section
VII
).

III
Basic locality techniques

III.1
Lieb–Robinson bound

The Lieb–Robinson bound captures the quasilocality of dynamics through time evolution
Lieb and Robinson 1972
;
Hastings and Koma 2006
;
Nachtergaele et al. 2006
;
Nachtergaele and Sims 2006
by providing a bound on the error when approximating the dynamics with a light cone. This bound plays a key role in deriving the main result of this work, and we briefly review its formulation here. We first discuss the case where the Hamiltonian has a finite interaction length. (Recall that we introduced the term "interaction length" below equation (
4
).)

Lemma 2

(Finite-range interaction)
.

Let
H
be a Hamiltonian with interaction length
d_{H}
. Then, for any operators
O_{Z}
and
O_{Z^{\prime}}
with
d_{Z,Z^{\prime}}=r
, the following relation holds for the commutator
[O_{Z}(t),O_{Z^{\prime}}]
:

\displaystyle\left\|[O_{Z}(t),O_{Z^{\prime}}]\right\|\leq\frac{2}{k}\left\|O_{Z}\right\|\cdot\left\|O_{Z^{\prime}}\right\|\cdot|Z|\frac{(2gk|t|)^{n_{0}}}{n_{0}!},

(28)

where the number
n_{0}
is defined by

\displaystyle n_{0}=\left\lfloor\frac{r}{d_{H}}+1\right\rfloor.

(29)

Here,
\left\lfloor\cdot\right\rfloor
represents rounding down. By definition,
n_{0}
is larger than
\frac{r}{d_{H}}
, i.e.,
n_{0}>\frac{r}{d_{H}}
. A proof of Lemma
2
can be found, for example, in
(
Kuwahara 2015
, Ineq. (24))
.

Remark.

Using the following inequality, which holds for
x,x_{0}>0
:

\displaystyle\left(\frac{x_{0}}{x}\right)^{x}\leq e^{-x+x_{0}},

(30)

inequality (
28
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\left\|[O_{Z}(t),O_{Z^{\prime}}]\right\|\leq\frac{2}{k}\left\|O_{Z}\right\|\cdot\left\|O_{Z^{\prime}}\right\|\cdot|Z|e^{-r/d_{H}+2egk|t|},

(31)

where we used the inequality
n!\geq(n/e)^{n}
.

Next, we consider the case where the Hamiltonian has an infinite interaction length (i.e., when
d_{H}=\infty
). The following lemma, whose application is central to the subsequent discussion, applies to this case:

Lemma 3

(Infinite interaction length)
.

Let
H
be a Hamiltonian with an infinite interaction length.
The parameter
\mathfrak{g}
is defined as the infimum over all constants
c
that satisfy the inequality

\displaystyle\max_{i,i^{\prime}\in\Lambda}\sum_{i_{0}\in\Lambda}\bar{J}(d_{i,i_{0}})\bar{J}(d_{i_{0},i^{\prime}})\leq c\bar{J}(d_{i,i^{\prime}}).

(32)

The set of such constants is nonempty (and therefore,
\mathfrak{g}
is well-defined). The Lieb–Robinson bound for the commutator
[O_{Z}(t),O_{Z^{\prime}}]
is then given by

\displaystyle\left\|[O_{Z}(t),O_{Z^{\prime}}]\right\|\leq\frac{2}{\mathfrak{g}}\left\|O_{Z}\right\|\cdot\left\|O_{Z^{\prime}}\right\|\cdot|Z|\cdot|Z^{\prime}|\left(e^{2\mathfrak{g}|t|}-1\right)\bar{J}(r),

(33)

where
r:=d_{Z,Z^{\prime}}
.

A proof of Lemma
3
can be found, e.g., in
(
Hastings and Koma 2006
, Theorem A.1)
.

Combining the two Lieb–Robinson bounds mentioned above provides an upper bound on the norm of the commutator
[O_{Z}(t),O_{Z^{\prime}}]
as follows:

\displaystyle\left\|[O_{Z}(t),O_{Z^{\prime}}]\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|O_{Z}\right\|\cdot\left\|O_{Z^{\prime}}\right\|\cdot|Z|\cdot|Z^{\prime}|\min\left[2,e^{v|t|}\mathcal{F}_{0}(r)\right]

\displaystyle=:\left\|O_{Z}\right\|\cdot\left\|O_{Z^{\prime}}\right\|\cdot|Z|\cdot|Z^{\prime}|\mathcal{F}(t,r),

(34)

with

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(t,r):=\min\left[2,e^{v|t|}\mathcal{F}_{0}(r)\right],

(35)

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{0}(r)=\begin{cases}Ce^{-r/d_{H}}&\quad\textrm{for}\quad\textrm{finite-range interactions},\\
C\bar{J}(r)&\quad\textrm{for}\quad\textrm{infinite-range interactions},\\
\end{cases}

(36)

C=2(1/k+1/\mathfrak{g})
, and
v=\max(2gk,2\mathfrak{g})
.
The norm of the commutator
\left\|[O_{Z}(t),O_{Z^{\prime}}]\right\|
also has the following trivial bound:
\left\|[O_{Z}(t),O_{Z^{\prime}}]\right\|\leq 2\left\|O_{Z}\right\|\cdot\left\|O_{Z^{\prime}}\right\|
. In subsequent discussions, we will utilize this trivial upper bound.

Furthermore, approximating
O_{L_{0}}(t)
by the operator
O_{L_{0}}(H_{L},t)
, using time evolution with respect to the subset Hamiltonian
H_{L}
(
L_{0}\subset L\subseteq\Lambda)
, can be useful in certain situations. As the distance between
L_{0}
and
L^{\rm c}
increases, the approximation error is expected to decrease. The following lemma confirms this (see Fig.
1
):

Lemma 4

(Supplementary Lemma 14 in Ref.
Kuwahara and Saito 2021
)
.

Let
H
be an arbitrary time-independent Hamiltonian of the form

\displaystyle H=\sum_{Z\subset\Lambda}h_{Z}.

(37)

Then, for any subset
L
with
L\supseteq L_{0}
, the following inequality holds using the notation (
9
), i.e.,
O(O^{\prime},t):=e^{iO^{\prime}t}Oe^{-iO^{\prime}t}
:

\displaystyle\|O_{L_{0}}(H,t)-O_{L_{0}}(H_{L,\tau},t)\|\leq\sum_{Z:Z\in\mathcal{S}_{L}}\int_{0}^{t}\left\|[h_{Z,x}(H_{L},x),O_{L_{0}}]\right\|dx,

(38)

where
H_{L}
represents the subset Hamiltonian on
L
, as introduced in (
2
), and
\mathcal{S}_{L}
is defined as the collection of subsets
\{Z\}_{Z\subseteq\Lambda}
that have a nonempty intersection with the boundary of
L
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{L}:=\{Z\subseteq\Lambda|Z\cap L\neq\emptyset,Z\cap L^{\rm c}\neq\emptyset\}.

(39)

Figure 1:
The image illustrates an approximation of the time-evolved operator
O_{L_{0}}(t)
by
O_{L_{0}}(H_{L},t)
, where
L_{0}
and
L^{\rm c}
are separated by a distance
\ell
(Corollary
5
).
The approximation error is obtained by applying the Lieb–Robinson bound and Lemma
4
, with the boundary interactions
h_{Z}
such that
Z
satisfies
Z\cap L\neq\emptyset
and
Z\cap L^{\rm c}\neq\emptyset
.

Using Lemma
4
and the Lieb–Robinson bound, we obtain the following corollary:

Corollary 5
.

Let
\mathcal{F}(t,r)
be the function introduced in (
III.1
). If the condition
d_{L^{\rm c},L_{0}}\geq\ell
is satisfied, the bound on
\|O_{L_{0}}(H,t)-O_{L_{0}}(H_{L},t)\|
in inequality (
38
) can be replaced with an explicit expression as follows:

\displaystyle\|O_{L_{0}}(H,t)-O_{L_{0}}(H_{L},t)\|\leq|t|\cdot|L_{0}|\cdot\left\|O_{L_{0}}\right\|\left[\frac{g\gamma^{2}\ell^{2}}{2}\bar{J}(\ell/2)+\tilde{g}k\mathcal{F}(t,\ell/2)\right],

(40)

where
\tilde{g}
is defined as:

\displaystyle\tilde{g}:=g\gamma^{2}\bar{J}(1).

(41)

Proof of Corollary
5
.

We estimate the quantity

\displaystyle\sum_{Z:Z\in\mathcal{S}_{L}}\int_{0}^{t}\left\|[h_{Z}(H_{L},x),O_{L_{0}}]\right\|dx.

(42)

from inequality (
38
). We consider the decomposition

\displaystyle\sum_{Z:Z\in\mathcal{S}_{L}}h_{Z}=\sum_{Z:Z\in\mathcal{S}_{L},d_{Z,L_{0}}\leq\ell/2}h_{Z}+\sum_{Z:Z\in\mathcal{S}_{L},d_{Z,L_{0}}>\ell/2}h_{Z}.

(43)

It follows from the definition of
\mathcal{S}_{L}
in Eq. (
39
) that

\displaystyle\sum_{Z:Z\in\mathcal{S}_{L},d_{Z,L_{0}}\leq\ell/2}\left\|h_{Z}\right\|\leq\sum_{Z:Z\cap L^{\rm c}\neq\emptyset,Z\cap L_{1}\neq\emptyset}\left\|h_{Z}\right\|\leq\bar{V}_{L^{\rm c},L_{1}},

(44)

where we define the subset
L_{1}
as
L_{1}:=\{i\in\Lambda|d_{i,L_{0}}\leq\ell/2\}
(see also Fig.
1
). The upper bound
\bar{V}_{L^{\rm c},L_{1}}
was defined as in Eq. (
13
), and this
\bar{V}_{L^{\rm c},L_{1}}
is also bounded above by (
12
). In the situation under discussion, upper bound (
12
) becomes

\displaystyle\bar{V}_{L^{\rm c},L_{1}}\leq g\gamma^{2}(\ell/2)^{2}\bar{J}(\ell/2).

(45)

Therefore, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{Z:Z\in\mathcal{S}_{L},d_{Z,L_{0}}\leq\ell/2}\int_{0}^{t}\left\|[h_{Z}(H_{L},x),O_{L_{0}}]\right\|dx\leq 2\cdot\frac{g\gamma^{2}\ell^{2}}{4}|t|\cdot\left\|O_{L_{0}}\right\|\bar{J}(\ell/2).

(46)

For the second term on the RHS of Eq. (
43
), using the Lieb–Robinson bound, we find that the following relations hold for
0\leq x\leq t
and
d_{Z,L_{0}}>\ell/2
:

\displaystyle\left\|[h_{Z}(H_{L},x),O_{L_{0}}]\right\|\leq|Z|\cdot|L_{0}|\cdot\left\|O_{L_{0}}\right\|\cdot\left\|h_{Z}\right\|\mathcal{F}(x,d_{Z,L_{0}})\leq k|L_{0}|\cdot\left\|O_{L_{0}}\right\|\cdot\left\|h_{Z}\right\|\mathcal{F}(t,\ell/2),

(47)

where we used
|Z|\leq k
.
Then, from the relations

\displaystyle\sum_{Z:Z\in\mathcal{S}_{L},d_{Z,L_{0}}>\ell/2}\left\|h_{Z}\right\|\leq\sum_{Z:Z\in\mathcal{S}_{L}}\left\|h_{Z}\right\|=\sum_{Z:Z\cap L\neq\emptyset,Z\cap L^{\rm c}\neq\emptyset}\left\|h_{Z}\right\|=\bar{V}_{L,L^{\rm c}}\leq g\gamma^{2}\bar{J}(1)=:\tilde{g},

(48)

we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Z:Z\in\mathcal{S}_{L},d_{Z,L_{0}}>\ell/2\end{subarray}}\int_{0}^{t}\left\|[h_{Z}(H_{L},x),O_{L_{0}}]\right\|dx\leq\tilde{g}k|t|\cdot|L_{0}|\cdot\left\|O_{L_{0}}\right\|\mathcal{F}(t,\ell/2).

(49)

Combining inequalities (
46
) and (
49
) proves the main inequality (
40
). This completes the proof.
\square

We use the definition of the interaction-truncated Hamiltonian as provided in Ref.

We consider partitioning the total system into blocks:

We then truncate all interactions between non-adjacent blocks.
After truncation, only interactions between adjacent blocks remain, leading to the truncated Hamiltonian:

\displaystyle H_{\rm t}=\sum_{s=0}^{q+1}v_{s}+\sum_{s=0}^{q}h_{s},\quad v_{s}:=H_{B_{s}},\quad h_{s}:=V_{B_{s},B_{s+1}}(B_{s}\sqcup B_{s+1}),

where we used the definition of

as in Eq. (

From Lemma

\displaystyle\left\|h_{s}\right\|=\left\|V_{B_{s},B_{s+1}}(B_{s}\sqcup B_{s+1})\right\|\leq g\gamma^{2}\bar{J}(1)=:\tilde{g}.

(52)

Applying Lemmas
6
and

\displaystyle\left\|e^{\beta H}-e^{\beta H_{\rm t}}\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq 3\beta\gamma^{2}gql_{0}^{2}\bar{J}(l_{0}){\rm tr}\left(e^{\beta H}\right)

\displaystyle=3\gamma^{2}g\beta Rl_{0}\bar{J}(l_{0}){\rm tr}\left(e^{\beta H}\right)\quad\textrm{for}\quad\beta\gamma^{2}gql_{0}^{2}\bar{J}(l_{0})\leq 1,

.

The norm of the difference between

Lemma 7
.

For arbitrary Hermitian operators

\displaystyle\left\|e^{A+B}-e^{A}\right\|_{1}\leq\|B\|e^{\left\|B\right\|}\cdot\left\|e^{A}\right\|_{1}.

(55)

In particular, if

\displaystyle\left\|e^{A+B}-e^{A}\right\|_{1}\leq 3\|B\|\cdot\left\|e^{A}\right\|_{1}.

(56)

Remark.
The inequality in Lemma

is stronger than the trivial inequality

\displaystyle\left\|e^{A+B}-e^{A}\right\|_{1}=\left\|e^{A+B}e^{-A}e^{A}-e^{A}\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|e^{A+B}e^{-A}-1\right\|\cdot\left\|e^{A}\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\left\|\mathcal{T}e^{\int_{0}^{1}e^{\tau A}Be^{-\tau A}d\tau}-1\right\|\cdot\left\|e^{A}\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq e^{\left\|B\right\|e^{2\left\|A\right\|}}\left\|B\right\|e^{2\left\|A\right\|}\cdot\left\|e^{A}\right\|_{1},

(57)

which is meaningful only when
\left\|B\right\|\ll e^{-2\left\|A\right\|}
.

Proof of Lemma
7
.

We consider the decomposition

\displaystyle e^{A+B}=\Phi_{B}e^{A}\Phi_{B}^{\dagger},

(58)

where
\Phi_{B}
is given by the belief propagation operator
Kato and Brandão 2019
;
Hastings 2007
;
Kim 2012
(see also Lemma
9
below):

\displaystyle\Phi_{B}=\mathcal{T}e^{\int_{0}^{1}\phi_{B,\tau}d\tau},

\displaystyle\phi_{B,\tau}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(t)B(A+\tau B,t)dt,

\displaystyle f(t):=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\tilde{f}(\omega)e^{-i\omega t}d\omega,\hskip 10.84006pt\tilde{f}(\omega)=\frac{{\rm tanh}(\omega/2)}{\omega/2},

(59)

and
\mathcal{T}
is the time ordering operator. The function
f(t)
is nonnegative, i.e.,
f(t)\geq 0
, which becomes evident when rewritten in the explicit form Eq. (

) below.
Hence, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\phi_{B,\tau}\right\|\leq\frac{\left\|B\right\|}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(t)dt=\frac{\left\|B\right\|}{2}\tilde{f}(0)=\frac{\left\|B\right\|}{2},

(60)

which yields

\displaystyle\left\|\Phi_{B}-\hat{1}\right\|\leq e^{\int_{0}^{1}\left\|\phi_{B,\tau}\right\|d\tau}\int_{0}^{1}\left\|\phi_{B,\tau}\right\|d\tau\leq\frac{\left\|B\right\|}{2}e^{\left\|B\right\|/2},

(61)

where we applied
(
Kuwahara et al. 2021
, Claim 25 therein)
to the first inequality, i.e.,

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{T}e^{\int_{0}^{1}A_{\tau}d\tau}-\mathcal{T}e^{\int_{0}^{1}B_{\tau}d\tau}\right\|\leq e^{\int_{0}^{1}\max\left(\left\|A_{\tau}\right\|,\left\|B_{\tau}\right\|\right)d\tau}\int_{0}^{1}\left\|A_{\tau}-B_{\tau}\right\|d\tau.

(62)

We then obtain

\displaystyle\left\|e^{A+B}-e^{A}\right\|_{1}=\left\|\Phi_{B}e^{A}\Phi_{B}^{\dagger}-e^{A}\Phi_{B}^{\dagger}+e^{A}\Phi_{B}^{\dagger}-e^{A}\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|(\Phi_{B}-\hat{1})e^{A}\Phi_{B}^{\dagger}\right\|_{1}+\left\|e^{A}(\Phi_{B}^{\dagger}-\hat{1})\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\left\|B\right\|}{2}e^{\left\|B\right\|/2}\left(1+e^{\left\|B\right\|/2}\right)\left\|e^{A}\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|B\right\|e^{\left\|B\right\|}\left\|e^{A}\right\|_{1}.

(63)

This completes the proof.
\square

III.3
Quantum belief propagation

We adopt the interaction-truncated Hamiltonian introduced in the previous section. For notational simplicity, we denote

as

The belief propagation operator is formulated as follows
Kato and Brandão 2019
;
Hastings 2007
;
Kim 2012
:

Lemma 9
.

Given the decomposition of the Hamiltonian as

\displaystyle H=H_{\leq s}+H_{>s}+h_{s},

(67)

\displaystyle e^{\beta H}=\Phi_{s}e^{\beta(H_{\leq s}+H_{>s})}\Phi_{s}^{\dagger},

(68)

where the explicit expression for the belief propagation operator

In proving the clustering theorem, we utilize the cluster expansion technique.
We first rewrite an arbitrary bipartite correlation function

by following

For this purpose, we adopt a copy of the original Hilbert space and define the notations

and

for an arbitrary operator

as follows:

\displaystyle O^{(+)}=O\otimes\hat{1}+\hat{1}\otimes O,\quad O^{(0)}=O\otimes\hat{1},\quad O^{(1)}=O\otimes\hat{1}-\hat{1}\otimes O.

(102)

Using the above notation, we obtain

\displaystyle{\rm Cor}_{\rho_{\beta}}(O_{X},O_{Y})={\rm tr}\left(\rho_{\beta}\otimes\rho_{\beta}O_{X}^{(0)}O_{Y}^{(1)}\right)=\frac{1}{Z_{\beta}^{(+)}}{\rm tr}\left(e^{\beta H^{(+)}}O_{X}^{(0)}O_{Y}^{(1)}\right),

(103)

where

.
One can easily verify that Eq. (

Then, the following lemma applies:

Lemma 11
.

\displaystyle\rho_{{\rm cl}}:=\frac{1}{Z_{\beta}^{(+)}}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\sum_{Z_{1},Z_{2},\ldots,Z_{m}:{\rm connected}}\frac{\beta^{m}}{m!}h_{Z_{1}}^{(+)}h_{Z_{2}}^{(+)}\cdots h_{Z_{m}}^{(+)}.

\displaystyle{\rm Cor}_{\rho_{\beta}}(O_{X},O_{Y})\leq\left\|O_{X}\right\|\cdot\left\|O_{Y}\right\|\cdot\left\|\rho_{{\rm cl}}\right\|_{1}.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Z_{\beta}^{(+)}}\sum_{Z_{1},Z_{2},\ldots,Z_{m}:{\rm connected}}\frac{1}{m!}\left\|h_{Z_{1}}^{(+)}h_{Z_{2}}^{(+)}\cdots h_{Z_{m}}^{(+)}\right\|\lesssim\left(\beta/\beta_{c}\right)^{m},

This limitation presents the main challenge in proving the clustering theorem in a 1D system at arbitrary temperatures.
Therefore, this argument does not apply to inverse temperatures above the threshold value

In the following, we focus on the cluster expansion in 1D finite-range interacting systems as in Eq. (

In the following discussions, for notational simplicity, we denote

Since we have

\displaystyle\left\|h_{s}\right\|\leq 2\tilde{g}

(115)

\displaystyle G_{s}:=e^{\beta H}-e^{\beta(H-h_{s})}=-\int_{0}^{1}\frac{d}{d\lambda_{s}}e^{\beta H-\beta\lambda_{s}h_{s}}d\lambda_{s}.

e^{\beta H}
.
This generalizes to situations involving multiple parameters. In the case of multiple parameters, we define

\displaystyle G_{s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{m}}

\displaystyle:=(-1)^{m}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\cdots\int_{0}^{1}d\lambda_{s_{1}}d\lambda_{s_{2}}\cdots d\lambda_{s_{m}}\frac{d}{d\lambda_{s_{1}}}\frac{d}{d\lambda_{s_{2}}}\cdots\frac{d}{d\lambda_{s_{m}}}e^{\beta H-\beta\sum_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{s}h_{s_{j}}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{D}_{h_{s_{1}},\ldots,h_{s_{m}}}e^{\beta H}.

(117)

The super-operator
\mathcal{D}_{h_{s_{1}},\ldots,h_{s_{m}}}
removes all zeroth-order terms of
h_{s_{j}}
(
j=1,\ldots,m
) from the Taylor expansion of
e^{\beta H}
.
Using Lemma
11
, we obtain

\displaystyle{\rm tr}\left[\Psi_{X,Y}\left(\rho_{\beta}-G_{s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{m}}\right)\right]=0

(118)

for an arbitrary set
\{s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{m}\}
. From this, we deduce the following inequalities:

\displaystyle{\rm Cor}_{\rho_{\beta}}(O_{X},O_{Y})\leq\frac{1}{Z_{\beta}}\left|{\rm tr}\left(\Psi_{X,Y}G_{s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{m}}\right)\right|\leq\frac{2}{Z_{\beta}}\left\|G_{s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{m}}\right\|_{1}.

(119)

Therefore, upper-bounding the norm
\left\|G_{s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{m}}\right\|_{1}
leads to the clustering theorem.

IV.1
1D cases: commuting Hamiltonian

We demonstrate the commutative situation here, i.e.,
[h_{s},h_{s^{\prime}}]=0

\forall s,s^{\prime}\in[0,q]
in Eq. (
51
). The aforementioned restriction on the values of
\beta
does not apply in this simple scenario. Comparing the commutative and noncommutative situations allows for a clear identification of the challenges that arise in proving the clustering theorem. The challenges for noncommutative situations will be identified afterwards.

In the commutative case, the operation
\mathcal{D}_{h_{s_{1}},\ldots,h_{s_{m}}}
in Eq. (
IV
)
simply replaces
e^{\beta h_{s_{1}}}
with
e^{\beta h_{s_{1}}}-\hat{1}
in the Gibbs state

\displaystyle e^{\beta H}=\prod_{s=0}^{q+1}e^{\beta v_{s}}\prod_{s=0}^{q}e^{\beta h_{s}},

(120)

where we used the notations defined in Eq. (
51
).
The trace norm
\left\|G_{0,1,\ldots,q}\right\|_{1}
is simply the trace of
G_{0,1,\ldots,q}
:

\displaystyle\left\|G_{0,1,\ldots,q}\right\|_{1}={\rm tr}\left(G_{0,1,\ldots,q}\right)={\rm tr}\left[\prod_{s=0}^{q+1}e^{\beta v_{s}}\prod_{s=0}^{q}\left(e^{\beta h_{s}}-\hat{1}\right)\right].

(121)

Note that the positivity of the operator
\left(e^{\beta h_{s}}-\hat{1}\right)
is ensured by
h_{s}\succeq 0
.

To obtain an upper bound on this quantity, as an initial step, we observe the following inequality:

\displaystyle{\rm tr}\left[e^{\beta H-\beta h_{0}}\left(e^{\beta h_{0}}-\hat{1}\right)\right]={\rm tr}\left(e^{\beta H}\right)\cdot\frac{{\rm tr}\left[e^{\beta H-\beta h_{0}}\left(e^{\beta h_{0}}-\hat{1}\right)\right]}{{\rm tr}\left(e^{\beta H-\beta h_{0}}e^{\beta h_{0}}\right)}\leq{\rm tr}\left(e^{\beta H}\right)\cdot\frac{e^{\beta||h_{0}||}-1}{e^{\beta||h_{0}||}},

(122)

where the last inequality holds owing to the fact that for any quantum state
\rho
, the inequality

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm tr}\left[\rho(e^{\beta h_{0}}-\hat{1})\right]}{{\rm tr}\left(\rho e^{\beta h_{0}}\right)}\leq\frac{e^{\beta||h_{0}||}-1}{e^{\beta||h_{0}||}}

(123)

holds.
In the second step, the same procedure yields the inequalities

\displaystyle{\rm tr}\left[e^{\beta(H-h_{0}-h_{1})}\left(e^{\beta h_{0}}-\hat{1}\right)\left(e^{\beta h_{1}}-\hat{1}\right)\right]

\displaystyle={\rm tr}\left[e^{\beta(H-h_{0})}\left(e^{\beta h_{0}}-\hat{1}\right)\right]\cdot\frac{{\rm tr}\left[e^{\beta(H-h_{0}-h_{1})}\left(e^{\beta h_{0}}-\hat{1}\right)\left(e^{\beta h_{1}}-\hat{1}\right)\right]}{{\rm tr}\left[e^{\beta(H-h_{0}-h_{1})}\left(e^{\beta h_{0}}-\hat{1}\right)e^{\beta h_{1}}\right]}

\displaystyle\leq{\rm tr}\left[e^{\beta(H-h_{0})}\left(e^{\beta h_{0}}-\hat{1}\right)\right]\cdot\frac{e^{\beta||h_{1}||}-1}{e^{\beta||h_{1}||}}

\displaystyle\leq{\rm tr}\left(e^{\beta H}\right)\cdot\frac{e^{\beta||h_{0}||}-1}{e^{\beta||h_{0}||}}\cdot\frac{e^{\beta||h_{1}||}-1}{e^{\beta||h_{1}||}},

(124)

where in the first inequality, we used the fact that
e^{\beta(H-h_{0}-h_{1})}\left(e^{\beta h_{0}}-\hat{1}\right)
is still positive semidefinite since
e^{\beta h_{0}}-\hat{1}\succeq 0
.
In an analogous manner, after iterating this method, one deduces the following relation:

\displaystyle{\rm tr}\left[\prod_{s=0}^{q+1}e^{\beta v_{s}}\prod_{s=0}^{q}\left(e^{\beta h_{s}}-\hat{1}\right)\right]\leq{\rm tr}\left(e^{\beta H}\right)\cdot\prod_{s=0}^{q}\frac{e^{\beta||h_{s}||}-1}{e^{\beta||h_{s}||}}.

(125)

Using inequality (
119
), we obtain the following upper bound on the correlation function:

\displaystyle{\rm Cor}_{\rho_{\beta}}(O_{X},O_{Y})\leq 2\prod_{s=0}^{q}\frac{e^{\beta||h_{s}||}-1}{e^{\beta||h_{s}||}}.

(126)

We obtained an upper bound on
||h_{s}||
in (
115
):

\displaystyle||h_{s}||\leq 2\tilde{g},

(127)

therefore, we have

\displaystyle{\rm Cor}_{\rho_{\beta}}(O_{X},O_{Y})\leq 2\prod_{s=0}^{q}\frac{e^{2\beta\tilde{g}}-1}{e^{2\beta\tilde{g}}}

\displaystyle=2\left(1-e^{-2\beta\tilde{g}}\right)^{q+1}\leq 2e^{-R/\left(l_{0}e^{2\beta\tilde{g}}\right)},

(128)

where we used
q=R/l_{0}
(this relation was introduced in Sec.
III.2
) and the fact that for
x>0
, the inequality

\displaystyle 1-\frac{1}{x}\leq e^{-\frac{1}{x}}

(129)

holds. We thus conclude that this proves the clustering theorem for the commutative case with the correlation length
l_{0}e^{2\beta\tilde{g}}
.
When the interaction length
l_{0}
is a constant independent of
\beta
, the correlation length is given by
e^{{\rm const.}\times\beta}
.

When extending this discussion to the non-commuting Hamiltonian, we need to address the following problems.
To clarify the discussion, we explicitly express the operator
G_{s_{1},s_{2}}
in Eq. (
IV
) as follows:

\displaystyle G_{s_{1},s_{2}}=e^{\beta H}-e^{\beta(H-h_{s_{1}})}-e^{\beta(H-h_{s_{2}})}+e^{\beta(H-h_{s_{1}}-h_{s_{2}})}.

(130)

The first difficulty arises because

\displaystyle e^{\beta H}-e^{\beta(H-h_{s_{1}})}-e^{\beta(H-h_{s_{2}})}+e^{\beta(H-h_{s_{1}}-h_{s_{2}})}\neq e^{\beta(H-h_{s_{1}}-h_{s_{2}})}\left(1-e^{\beta h_{s_{1}}}\right)\left(1-e^{\beta h_{s_{2}}}\right),

(131)

and LHS is not equal to RHS since the operators
h_{s_{1}}
and
h_{s_{2}}
do not commute.
This fact prevents us from using the prescription as in (
IV.1
).

The second difficulty arises from the fact that, in general,

\displaystyle|G_{s_{1},s_{2}}|\neq G_{s_{1},s_{2}}.

(132)

This inequality stems from the positivity of
h_{s_{1}}
and
h_{s_{2}}
.
To explain this point in more detail, the relation

\displaystyle e^{\beta H}-e^{\beta H-\beta(h_{s_{1}}+\zeta)}\succeq 0

(133)

is guaranteed only when
\zeta
is sufficiently larger than
\left\|H\right\|\propto|\Lambda|
, not
\left\|h_{s_{1}}\right\|
.
In general, we can only prove the following lemma:

Lemma 12
.

Let
A
and
B
be arbitrary operators.
If
A\succeq 0
and
B\succeq 0
,

\displaystyle e^{A+B+\zeta}-e^{A}\succeq 0

(134)

holds for
\zeta\geq\left\|A\right\|
*4
*4

*4

This condition is qualitatively optimal up to a constant factor: if we choose

A=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&a\\
\end{pmatrix}

and

B=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
1&1\\
\end{pmatrix}
,
the positivity of
e^{A+B+\zeta}-e^{A}
is guaranteed only for
c\geq\left\|A\right\|
in the limit at which
a\to\infty
.

.

Proof.

e^{A+B+\zeta}-e^{A}
can be rewritten as:

\displaystyle e^{A+B+\zeta}-e^{A}=e^{A/2}\left(e^{-A/2+\zeta/2}e^{A+B}e^{-A/2+\zeta/2}-1\right)e^{A/2}.

(135)

Our goal is to prove the positivity of the operator
e^{-A/2+\zeta/2}e^{A+B}e^{-A/2+\zeta/2}-1
.
First, from
A+B\succeq 0
, for an arbitrary normalized quantum state
\ket{\psi}
(i.e.,
\left\|\ket{\psi}\right\|=1
), we have
\bra{\psi}e^{A+B}\ket{\psi}\geq 1
.
Second, for an arbitrary normalized quantum state
\ket{\phi}
, the state
\ket{\tilde{\phi}}:=e^{-A/2+\zeta/2}\ket{\phi}
satisfies
\left\|\ket{\tilde{\phi}}\right\|\geq 1
due to
-A+\zeta\hat{1}\succeq 0
from
\zeta\geq\left\|A\right\|
.
Therefore, combining the two statements above, we have
\bra{\phi}e^{-A/2+\zeta/2}e^{A+B}e^{-A/2+\zeta/2}\ket{\phi}\geq 1
.
This completes the proof.
\square

This implies that we cannot guarantee the positivity of the operator
G_{s_{1},s_{2}}
by simply shifting the energy origins of
h_{s_{1}}
and
h_{s_{2}}
.
For that reason, we must handle the quantity
(1/Z_{\beta})\left|{\rm tr}\left(\Psi_{X,Y}G_{s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{m}}\right)\right|
in inequality (
119
) without relying on the upper bound
(2/Z_{\beta})\left\|G_{s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{m}}\right\|_{1}
.
This restricts the use of various mathematical tools for evaluating the upper bounds of the trace norm.

V
Proof of the main theorem

V.1
Cluster expansion for approximate quantum Gibbs state

We now consider an approximation of
e^{\beta H}
, denoted by
\tilde{\rho}_{\beta}
, such that

\displaystyle\left\|\tilde{\rho}_{\beta}-e^{\beta H}\right\|_{1}\leq\epsilon Z_{\beta},

(136)

where
\tilde{\rho}_{\beta}
is defined using an appropriate polynomial in the interaction terms
\{h_{Z}\}_{Z\subset\Lambda}
.
We then apply Lemma
11
to the expansions of
\tilde{\rho}_{\beta}
with respect to
\{h_{Z}\}_{Z\subset\Lambda}
.
Next, instead of considering
G_{s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{m}}
in Eq. (
IV
), we analyze

\displaystyle\tilde{G}_{s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{m}}:=\mathcal{D}_{h_{s_{1}},\ldots,h_{s_{m}}}\tilde{\rho}_{\beta},

(137)

(as introduced in (
151
) below), which leads to

\displaystyle\left|{\rm Cor}_{\rho_{\beta}}(O_{X},O_{Y})\right|\leq 2\epsilon+\left|\frac{1}{Z_{\beta}}{\rm Cor}_{\tilde{\rho}_{\beta}}(O_{X},O_{Y})\right|\leq 2\epsilon+\frac{1}{Z_{\beta}}{\rm tr}\left(\Psi_{X,Y}\tilde{G}_{s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{m}}\right),

(138)

where we used
\left\|\Psi_{X,Y}\right\|\leq 2
[see Eq. (
114
) for the definition of
\Psi_{X,Y}
] and

\displaystyle\left|{\rm Cor}_{\rho_{\beta}}(O_{X},O_{Y})-\frac{1}{Z_{\beta}}{\rm Cor}_{\tilde{\rho}_{\beta}}(O_{X},O_{Y})\right|=\left|{\rm tr}\left[\Psi_{X,Y}\left(\rho_{\beta}-\frac{1}{Z_{\beta}}\tilde{\rho}_{\beta}\right)\right]\right|\leq\frac{\left\|\Psi_{X,Y}\right\|}{Z_{\beta}}\left\|e^{\beta H}-\tilde{\rho}_{\beta}\right\|_{1}.

(139)

V.2
Decomposition of the system

In estimating the operator
G_{s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{m}}
in Eq. (
IV
), we encountered difficulties, as previously explained: owing to the inequality between the LHS and the RHS of equation (
131
), simultaneously removing the zeroth-order terms from the interaction terms is considerably challenging.

We decompose the central system
\Lambda\setminus(X\cup Y)
into
m
blocks
L_{1},L_{2},\ldots,L_{m}
(Fig.
5
):

\displaystyle\Lambda=L_{0}\cup L_{1}\cup L_{2}\cdots L_{m}\cup L_{m+1},\quad L_{0}=X,\quad L_{m+1}=Y,

(140)

each of length of
2\ell
, i.e.,
m\propto R/\ell
.
We choose
\ell
to satisfy the condition

\displaystyle\ell>6l_{0},

(141)

so that Proposition
10
, under condition (
77
), applies.
We use
\{i_{s_{j}}\}_{j=1}^{m}
to represent the central sites in
\{L_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
. Then, using the belief propagation operator, we have

\displaystyle e^{\beta H}=\Phi_{0}\Phi_{1}\cdots\Phi_{m}e^{\beta H_{L_{0}}}e^{\beta H_{L_{1}}}\cdots e^{\beta H_{L_{m}}}e^{\beta H_{L_{m+1}}}\left(\Phi_{0}\Phi_{1}\cdots\Phi_{m}\right)^{\dagger},

(142)

where
\Phi_{j}
connects the blocks
L_{j}
and
L_{j+1}
. We introduce the notations
\Phi_{0:m}
and
\tilde{\Phi}_{0:m}
to define the following products of operators:

\displaystyle\Phi_{0:m}:=\Phi_{0}\Phi_{1}\cdots\Phi_{m},\quad\tilde{\Phi}_{0:m}:=\tilde{\Phi}_{0}\tilde{\Phi}_{1}\cdots\tilde{\Phi}_{m}.

(143)

Figure 5:
Approximating
\{\Phi_{j}\}_{j=0}^{m+1}
by
\{\tilde{\Phi}_{j}\}_{j=0}^{m+1}
,
the dependence on
h_{s_{1}},\ldots,h_{s_{m}}
are removed from
\{\tilde{\Phi}_{j}\}_{j=0}^{m+1}
.
These approximations allow for a simple analysis to remove the zeroth-order terms for
h_{s_{1}},\ldots,h_{s_{m}}
.
The center site in the region
L_{j}
is denoted by
i_{s_{j}}
, and the operator
h_{s_{j}}
characterizes the interaction between the left half and the right half of the block
L_{j}
.

We approximate
\{\Phi_{j}\}_{j=0}^{m+1}
by
\{\tilde{\Phi}_{j}\}_{j=0}^{m+1}
so that they do not include interaction terms
\{h_{s_{j}}\}_{j=1}^{m}
(as illustrated in Fig.
5
).
To be more precise,
\tilde{\Phi}_{0}
,
\{\tilde{\Phi}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
and
\tilde{\Phi}_{m+1}
are
defined by using the subset Hamiltonians
H_{(-\infty,i_{s_{1}})}
,
H_{(i_{s_{j}},i_{s_{j+1}})}
and
H_{(i_{s_{m}},\infty)}
, respectively.
From Proposition
10
, the approximation error between
\Phi_{j}
and
\tilde{\Phi}_{j}
is given by

\displaystyle\left\|\Phi_{j}-\tilde{\Phi}_{j}\right\|\leq\mathcal{G}_{\beta}(\ell),

(144)

where
\mathcal{G}_{\beta}(\ell)
is given by Eq. (
79
).
Applying this bound leads to the following lemma:

Lemma 13
.

The norm of the difference between
\Phi_{0:m}
and
\tilde{\Phi}_{0:m}
is bounded above as follows:

\displaystyle\left\|\Phi_{0:m}-\tilde{\Phi}_{0:m}\right\|\leq(m+1)e^{2m\tilde{g}\beta}\mathcal{G}_{\beta}(\ell)\leq 2me^{2m\tilde{g}\beta}\mathcal{G}_{\beta}(\ell).

(145)

Proof of Lemma
13
.

We introduce the notation:

\displaystyle\Phi_{0:m}^{(s)}:=\tilde{\Phi}_{0}\cdots\tilde{\Phi}_{s-1}\Phi_{s}\cdots\Phi_{m}.

(146)

With this notation, we obtain the following expression:

\displaystyle\Phi_{0:m}-\tilde{\Phi}_{0:m}=\sum_{s=0}^{m}\left(\Phi_{0:m}^{(s)}-\Phi_{0:m}^{(s+1)}\right),

(147)

where we have used
\Phi_{0:m}^{(0)}=\Phi_{0:m}
and
\Phi_{0:m}^{(m+1)}=\tilde{\Phi}_{0:m}
.
By applying the upper bound from (
144
), we obtain

\displaystyle\left\|\Phi_{0:m}^{(s)}-\Phi_{0:m}^{(s+1)}\right\|

\displaystyle=\left\|\tilde{\Phi}_{0}\cdots\tilde{\Phi}_{s-1}\left(\Phi_{s}-\tilde{\Phi}_{s}\right)\Phi_{s+1}\cdots\Phi_{m}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\tilde{\Phi}_{0}\right\|\cdots\left\|\tilde{\Phi}_{s}\right\|\cdot\mathcal{G}_{\beta}(\ell)\cdot\left\|\Phi_{s+1}\right\|\cdots\left\|\Phi_{m}\right\|\leq e^{2m\tilde{g}\beta}\mathcal{G}_{\beta}(\ell),

(148)

where we used the following upper bounds:

\displaystyle\left\|\Phi_{j}\right\|\leq e^{\beta\left\|h_{s_{j}}\right\|}\leq e^{2\beta\tilde{g}}.

(149)

(The same inequalities also apply to
\left\|\tilde{\Phi}_{j}\right\|
.) Applying inequality (
V.2
) to Eq. (
147
) results in the main inequality (
145
). This completes the proof.
\square

We denote the sum
H_{L_{0}}+H_{L_{1}}+\cdots+H_{L_{m+1}}
by
H_{0}
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\left\|e^{\beta H}-\tilde{\Phi}_{0:m}e^{\beta H_{0}}\tilde{\Phi}_{0:m}^{\dagger}\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\left\|e^{\beta H}-\tilde{\Phi}_{0:m}\Phi_{0:m}^{-1}e^{\beta H}\left(\Phi_{0:m}^{\dagger}\right)^{-1}\tilde{\Phi}_{0:m}^{\dagger}\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\left\|\left(1-\tilde{\Phi}_{0:m}\Phi_{0:m}^{-1}\right)e^{\beta H}+\tilde{\Phi}_{0:m}\Phi_{0:m}^{-1}e^{\beta H}\left[1-\left(\Phi_{0:m}^{\dagger}\right)^{-1}\tilde{\Phi}_{0:m}^{\dagger}\right]\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+\left\|\tilde{\Phi}_{0:m}\Phi_{0:m}^{-1}\right\|\right)\left\|1-\tilde{\Phi}_{0:m}\Phi_{0:m}^{-1}\right\|\cdot\left\|e^{\beta H}\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq(1+\left\|\tilde{\Phi}_{0:m}\right\|\cdot\left\|\Phi_{0:m}^{-1}\right\|)\left\|\Phi_{0:m}^{-1}\right\|\cdot\left\|\Phi_{0:m}-\tilde{\Phi}_{0:m}\right\|{\rm tr}\left(e^{\beta H}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 4me^{8(m+1)\beta\tilde{g}}\mathcal{G}_{\beta}(\ell){\rm tr}\left(e^{\beta H}\right),

(150)

where we used
\left\|\tilde{\Phi}_{0:m}\right\|\leq e^{2(m+1)\beta\tilde{g}}
and
\left\|\Phi_{0:m}^{-1}\right\|\leq e^{2(m+1)\beta\tilde{g}}
.
We also introduce the following notation:

\displaystyle\tilde{G}_{s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{m}}:=\mathcal{D}_{h_{s_{1}},\ldots,h_{s_{m}}}\tilde{\Phi}_{0:m}e^{\beta H_{L_{0}}}e^{\beta H_{L_{1}}}\cdots e^{\beta H_{L_{m+1}}}\tilde{\Phi}_{0:m}^{\dagger}.

(151)

Using inequality (
138
) and taking the limit as
\epsilon\to e^{m\Theta(\beta)}\mathcal{G}_{\beta}(\ell)
with error bound (
V.2
), we obtain

\displaystyle{\rm Cor}_{\rho_{\beta}}(O_{X},O_{Y})\leq e^{m\Theta(\beta)}\mathcal{G}_{\beta}(\ell)+\frac{1}{Z_{\beta}}{\rm tr}\left(\Psi_{X,Y}\tilde{G}_{s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{m}}\right).

(152)

Because
\tilde{\Phi}_{0:m}
does not include the interaction terms
h_{s_{1}},\ldots,h_{s_{m}}
, and Hamiltonians
\{H_{L_{j}}\}_{j=0}^{m+1}
commute with one another, Eq. (
151
) simplifies to

\displaystyle\tilde{G}_{s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{m}}=\tilde{\Phi}_{0:m}e^{\beta H_{L_{0}}}\prod_{j=1}^{m}\left[e^{\beta H_{L_{j}}}-e^{\beta\left(H_{L_{j}}-h_{s_{j}}\right)}\right]e^{\beta H_{L_{m+1}}}\tilde{\Phi}_{0:m}^{\dagger}.

(153)

Now, the remaining and central task is to deduce an upper bound on the second term on the RHS of (
152
):

\displaystyle{\rm tr}\left(\Psi_{X,Y}\tilde{G}_{s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{m}}\right)

\displaystyle={\rm tr}\left(\Psi_{X,Y}\tilde{\Phi}_{0:m}e^{\beta H_{L_{0}}}\prod_{j=1}^{m}\left[e^{\beta H_{L_{j}}}-e^{\beta\left(H_{L_{j}}-h_{s_{j}}\right)}\right]e^{\beta H_{L_{m+1}}}\tilde{\Phi}_{0:m}\right).

(154)

We state the result here as a subtheorem. The proof of this key subtheorem is postponed to section
VI
.

Subtheorem 1
.

The quantity
\left|{\rm tr}\left(\Psi_{X,Y}\tilde{G}_{s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{m}}\right)\right|
has the following upper bound:

\displaystyle\left|{\rm tr}\left(\Psi_{X,Y}\tilde{G}_{s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{m}}\right)\right|\leq

\displaystyle\left(6e^{-me^{-\Theta(\beta)}}+e^{m\Theta(\beta)}\mathcal{G}_{\beta}(\ell)\right){\rm tr}\left(e^{\beta H}\right).

(155)

V.3
Completing the proof

We are now ready to prove the main theorem.
Since we adopted the interaction-truncated Hamiltonian with interaction length
l_{0}
, we have

\displaystyle{\rm Cor}_{\rho_{\beta}}(O_{X},O_{Y})\leq{\rm Cor}_{\rho_{{\rm t},\beta}}(O_{X},O_{Y})+\tilde{\gamma}\beta Rl_{0}\bar{J}(l_{0}),

(156)

where we used inequality (
III.2
) and introduced the notation
\tilde{\gamma}:=3\gamma^{2}g
.
Then, inequality (
152
) with Subtheorem
1
gives

\displaystyle{\rm Cor}_{\rho_{{\rm t},\beta}}(O_{X},O_{Y})\leq e^{m\Theta(\beta)}\mathcal{G}_{\beta}(\ell)+\left(6e^{-me^{-\Theta(\beta)}}+e^{m\Theta(\beta)}\mathcal{G}_{\beta}(\ell)\right).

(157)

Combining the above two inequalities yields the following result:

\displaystyle{\rm Cor}_{\rho_{\beta}}(O_{X},O_{Y})\leq e^{m\Theta(\beta)}\min\left(e^{-\ell/[l_{0}\Theta(\beta)]},\left(\bar{J}(\ell/3)\right)^{1/\Theta(\beta)}\right)+6e^{-me^{-\Theta(\beta)}}+\tilde{\gamma}\beta Rl_{0}\bar{J}(l_{0}),

(158)

where we used the expression (
79
) for
\mathcal{G}_{\beta}(\ell)
.

Now, we introduce the free parameters
\ell
and
l_{0}
:

\displaystyle\ell=\frac{R}{c(R)},\quad l_{0}=\frac{R}{c_{0}(R)},

(159)

where
c(R)
and
c_{0}(R)
are functions of
R
to be determined below. Owing to condition (
141
), they satisfy the relation
c(R)<c_{0}(R)
. We also note that
m\ell
is proportional to
R
, i.e.,
m\ell=c_{4}R
for some positive constant
c_{4}
. Then, inequality (
158
) simplifies to

\displaystyle{\rm Cor}_{\rho_{\beta}}(O_{X},O_{Y})

\displaystyle\leq e^{c_{4}\Theta(\beta)c(R)}\min\left(e^{-\frac{R}{\Theta(\beta\ell_{0})c(R)}},\bar{J}\left(\frac{R}{3c(R)}\right)^{1/\Theta(\beta)}\right)+6e^{-c_{4}c(R)e^{-\Theta(\beta)}}+\frac{\tilde{\gamma}\beta R^{2}}{c_{0}(R)}\bar{J}\left(\frac{R}{c_{0}(R)}\right),

(160)

Note that, by the definition of
\Theta(\beta)
, the leading contribution to
e^{-\frac{R}{\Theta(\beta)\ell_{0}c(R)}}
, which is in the first term on the RHS of (
V.3
), is
e^{-\frac{c_{0}(R)}{\Theta(\beta)c(R)}}
. It only remains to choose appropriate
c_{0}(R)
and
c(R)
to satisfy inequality (
18
) for interactions with sub-exponential decay, and inequality (
19
) for other types of interaction, respectively. This completes the proof of the main theorem.

First, we discuss interactions with sub-exponential decay, i.e.,

\displaystyle\bar{J}(r)\leq e^{-\Theta(r^{\kappa})}.

(161)

In this case, we choose

\displaystyle c(R)=c_{5}c_{0}(R),

(162)

where
c_{5}
represents a positive constant, and

\displaystyle\min\left(e^{-\frac{R}{\Theta(\beta\ell_{0})c(R)}},\bar{J}\left(\frac{R}{3c(R)}\right)^{1/\Theta(\beta)}\right)\leq\bar{J}\left(\frac{R}{3c(R)}\right)^{1/\Theta(\beta)}\leq\exp\left(-\frac{1}{\Theta(\beta)}\left(\frac{R}{c(R)}\right)^{\kappa}\right).

(163)

We then impose the following condition on the first term on the RHS of (
158
):

\displaystyle e^{c_{4}\Theta(\beta)c(R)}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{\Theta(\beta)}\left(\frac{R}{c(R)}\right)^{\kappa}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\Theta(\beta)}\left(\frac{R}{c(R)}\right)^{\kappa}\right),

(164)

which is equivalent to

\displaystyle c_{4}\Theta(\beta)c(R)\leq\frac{1}{2\Theta(\beta)}\left(\frac{R}{c(R)}\right)^{\kappa}.

(165)

This holds when we choose

\displaystyle c(R)=c_{6}\frac{R^{\kappa/(\kappa+1)}}{\Theta(\beta)^{2/(\kappa+1)}},

(166)

under the condition
0<c_{6}\leq\left(\frac{1}{2c_{4}}\right)^{1/\kappa+1}
.
With the aforementioned choices for
c_{0}(R)
and
c(R)
, we obtain

\displaystyle{\rm Cor}_{\rho_{\beta}}(O_{X},O_{Y})

\displaystyle\leq e^{c_{4}\Theta(\beta)c(R)}\min\left(e^{-\frac{R}{\Theta(\beta\ell_{0})c(R)}},\bar{J}\left(\frac{R}{3c(R)}\right)^{1/\Theta(\beta)}\right)+6e^{-c_{4}c(R)e^{-\Theta(\beta)}}+\frac{\tilde{\gamma}\beta R^{2}}{c_{0}(R)}\bar{J}\left(\frac{R}{c_{0}(R)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\left(\frac{1}{c_{6}}\right)^{\kappa}R^{\frac{\kappa}{\kappa+1}}\Theta(\beta)^{\frac{\kappa-1}{\kappa+1}}\right)+6e^{-c_{4}c_{6}\frac{R^{\kappa/\kappa+1}}{\Theta(\beta)^{2/\kappa+1}}e^{-\Theta(\beta)}}+\frac{\tilde{\gamma}\beta R^{2}}{c_{0}(R)}\,e^{-\Theta\left(\left(\frac{c_{5}}{c_{6}}\right)^{\kappa}R^{\frac{\kappa}{\kappa+1}}\Theta(\beta)^{\frac{2\kappa}{\kappa+1}}\right)}.

(167)

Therefore, we find that there exists a positive constant

such that

\displaystyle{\rm Cor}_{\rho_{\beta}}(O_{X},O_{Y})\leq c_{1}e^{-R^{\kappa/(\kappa+1)}/e^{\Theta(\beta)}},

(168)

yielding inequality (

Next, we discuss decay that is slower than sub-exponential, i.e.,

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to\infty}e^{-r^{\kappa}}/\bar{J}(r)=0\hskip 7.22743pt\forall\kappa>0.

(169)

In this case, we choose

\displaystyle\min\left(e^{-\frac{c_{0}(R)}{\Theta(\beta)c(R)}},\bar{J}\left(\frac{R}{3c(R)}\right)^{1/\Theta(\beta)}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{c_{0}(R)}{\Theta(\beta)c(R)}\right).

(170)

Analogous to (
164
), we impose the following condition:

\displaystyle e^{c_{4}\Theta(\beta)c(R)}\exp\left(-\frac{c_{0}(R)}{\Theta(\beta)c(R)}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{c_{0}(R)}{2\Theta(\beta)c(R)}\right).

(171)

This condition is equivalent to

\displaystyle c_{4}\Theta(\beta)c(R)\leq\frac{c_{0}(R)}{2\Theta(\beta)c(R)},

(172)

which is satisfied when
c(R)
and
c_{0}(R)
satisfy the relation

\displaystyle c(R)=c_{7}\,\frac{\sqrt{c_{0}(R)}}{\Theta(\beta)}

(173)

with
0<c_{7}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2c_{4}}}
.

Finally, we choose the function
c(R)
such that it satisfies the inequality

\displaystyle e^{-c(R)e^{-\Theta(\beta)}}\leq\bar{J}(R).

(174)

This inequality is satisfied when
c(R)
satisfies the relation

\displaystyle c(R)=e^{\Theta(\beta)}\log\left(\bar{J}(R)^{-1}\right).

(175)

Applying relation (
173
), in the form
c_{0}(R)=\frac{\Theta(\beta)^{2}c(R)^{2}}{c_{7}^{2}}
, to this expression for
c(R)
, we deduce that
c_{0}(R)
takes the following value:

\displaystyle c_{0}(R)=\frac{e^{2\Theta(\beta)}\Theta(\beta)^{2}\log^{2}\left(\bar{J}(R)^{-1}\right)}{c_{7}^{2}}.

(176)

For decay that is slower than sub-exponential, the third term on the RHS of inequality (
158
) yields the dominant contribution. For these values of

and

, this term can be written explicitly as:

(177)

Therefore, we deduce that there is a positive constant

such that the following inequality holds:

(178)

Selecting a positive constant

that satisfies

yields inequality (
19

1
.
\square

Lemma 14
.

Let
\{W_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
be mutually commuting semi-definite operators (i.e.,
W_{j}\succeq 0
, and
[W_{i},W_{j}]=0
) supported on
X\subset\Lambda
. Then, the following inequality holds for an arbitrary quantum state
\ket{\psi_{X^{\rm c}}}
supported on the subset
X^{\rm c}
:

\displaystyle\bra{\psi_{X^{\rm c}}}{\rm tr}_{X}\left(\rho\prod_{j=1}^{m}W_{j}\right)\ket{\psi_{X^{\rm c}}}\leq\bra{\psi_{X^{\rm c}}}{\rm tr}_{X}\left[\rho\prod_{j=1}^{m}(W_{j}+1)\right]\ket{\psi_{X^{\rm c}}}\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{m}\frac{\left\|W_{j}\right\|}{\left\|W_{j}\right\|+1},

(201)

where
\rho
represents an arbitrary positive semi-definite operator.

Proof.

We first introduce notations:

\displaystyle\rho_{X}:=\bra{\psi_{X^{\rm c}}}\rho\ket{\psi_{X^{\rm c}}},\quad W_{j}:=\sum_{x}w_{j,x}\ket{x}\bra{x},

(202)

where the latter one is the spectral decomposition of
W_{j}
using the simultaneous eigenstates
\{\ket{x}\}_{x}
of
\{W_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}

since we have assumed
[W_{j},W_{j^{\prime}}]=0
for any pair
j,j^{\prime}
.
Because
\{W_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
is positive semi-definite, the relation
w_{j,x}\geq 0
holds
\forall j,x
.
Therefore, we have the following equalities:

\displaystyle\bra{\psi_{X^{\rm c}}}{\rm tr}_{X}\left(\rho\prod_{j=1}^{m}W_{j}\right)\ket{\psi_{X^{\rm c}}}={\rm tr}_{X}\left(\rho_{X}\prod_{j=1}^{m}W_{j}\right)=\sum_{x}\bra{x}\rho_{X}\ket{x}\prod_{j=1}^{m}w_{j,x}.

(203)

In the same manner, we have

\displaystyle\bra{\psi_{X^{\rm c}}}{\rm tr}_{X}\left[\rho\prod_{j=1}^{m}(W_{j}+1)\right]\ket{\psi_{X^{\rm c}}}=\sum_{x}\bra{x}\rho_{X}\ket{x}\prod_{j=1}^{m}\left(w_{j,x}+1\right).

(204)

Denoting
\bra{x}\rho_{X}\ket{x}
by
\rho_{x}
, we obtain the equality:

\displaystyle\bra{\psi_{X^{\rm c}}}{\rm tr}_{X}\left(\rho\prod_{j=1}^{m}W_{j}\right)\ket{\psi_{X^{\rm c}}}=\bra{\psi_{X^{\rm c}}}{\rm tr}_{X}\left[\rho\prod_{j=1}^{m}(W_{j}+1)\right]\ket{\psi_{X^{\rm c}}}\frac{\sum_{x}\rho_{x}\prod_{j=1}^{m}w_{j,x}}{\sum_{x}\rho_{x}\prod_{j=1}^{m}\left(w_{j,x}+1\right)}.

(205)

Next, we define

\displaystyle\tilde{W}_{j_{0}}:=\sum_{x}\rho_{x}\prod_{j=1}^{j_{0}}\left(w_{j,x}+1\right)\prod_{j=j_{0}+1}^{m}w_{j,x},

(206)

where
\tilde{W}_{0}=\sum_{x}\rho_{x}\prod_{j=1}^{m}w_{j,x}
and
\tilde{W}_{m}=\sum_{x}\rho_{x}\prod_{j=1}^{m}(w_{j,x}+1)
.
We then consider

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{x}\rho_{x}\prod_{j=1}^{m}w_{j,x}}{\sum_{x}\rho_{x}\prod_{j=1}^{m}\left(w_{j,x}+1\right)}=\frac{\tilde{W}_{0}}{\tilde{W}_{m}}=\frac{\tilde{W}_{0}}{\tilde{W}_{1}}\cdot\frac{\tilde{W}_{1}}{\tilde{W}_{2}}\cdot\frac{\tilde{W}_{2}}{\tilde{W}_{3}}\cdots\frac{\tilde{W}_{m-1}}{\tilde{W}_{m}}.

(207)

For
j_{0}\in[1,m]
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\tilde{W}_{j_{0}-1}}{\tilde{W}_{j_{0}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{x}\rho_{x}\prod_{j=1}^{j_{0}-1}\left(w_{j,x}+1\right)w_{j_{0},x}\prod_{j=j_{0}+1}^{m}w_{j,x}}{\sum_{x}\rho_{x}\prod_{j=1}^{j_{0}-1}\left(w_{j,x}+1\right)(w_{j_{0},x}+1)\prod_{j=j_{0}+1}^{m}w_{j,x}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{x}\rho_{x}w_{j_{0},x}Q_{j_{0},x}}{\sum_{x}\rho_{x}(w_{j_{0},x}+1)Q_{j_{0},x}},

(208)

where we introduced the notation
Q_{j_{0},x}
as:

\displaystyle Q_{j_{0},x}:=\prod_{j=1}^{j_{0}-1}\left(w_{j,x}+1\right)\prod_{j=j_{0}+1}^{m}w_{j,x}.

(209)

We then deduce the following inequality:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d_{w_{j_{0},x}}}\frac{\sum_{x}\rho_{x}w_{j_{0},x}Q_{j_{0},x}}{\sum_{x}\rho_{x}(w_{j_{0},x}+1)Q_{j_{0},x}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho_{x}Q_{j_{0},x}}{\sum_{x}\rho_{x}(w_{j_{0},x}+1)Q_{j_{0},x}}-\frac{\sum_{x}\rho_{x}w_{j_{0},x}Q_{j_{0},x}}{\sum_{x}\rho_{x}(w_{j_{0},x}+1)Q_{j_{0},x}}\cdot\frac{\rho_{x}Q_{j_{0},x}}{\sum_{x}\rho_{x}(w_{j_{0},x}+1)Q_{j_{0},x}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho_{x}Q_{j_{0},x}}{\sum_{x}\rho_{x}(w_{j_{0},x}+1)Q_{j_{0},x}}\left(1-\frac{\sum_{x}\rho_{x}w_{j_{0},x}Q_{j_{0},x}}{\sum_{x}\rho_{x}(w_{j_{0},x}+1)Q_{j_{0},x}}\right)\geq 0,

(210)

where we used the positivity of
\{w_{j,x}\}_{j,x}
, implying
Q_{j_{0},x}\geq 0
.
Because
w_{j_{0},x}\leq\left\|W_{j_{0}}\right\|
, we can derive the following inequality

\displaystyle\frac{\tilde{W}_{j_{0}-1}}{\tilde{W}_{j_{0}}}\leq\frac{\sum_{x}\rho_{x}\left\|W_{j_{0}}\right\|Q_{j_{0},x}}{\sum_{x}\rho_{x}(\left\|W_{j_{0}}\right\|+1)Q_{j_{0},x}}=\frac{\left\|W_{j_{0}}\right\|}{\left\|W_{j_{0}}\right\|+1}.

(211)

Application of the above upper bound to Eq. (
207
) provides the desired inequality (
201
), completing the proof.

\square

VII
Concluding remarks

In this paper, we have demonstrated a clustering theorem that applies to systems beyond those with finite-range or exponentially decaying interactions. The core findings and significant observations are encapsulated in Theorem
1
. Our methodology integrates the quantum belief propagation technique, combined with a specific decomposition approach outlined in Section
V.2
.
This approach enables us to replicate analyses typical of classical one-dimensional systems, effectively circumventing the cluster expansion divergence (see Section
IV.1
).
Our findings conclusively address several aspects of the clustering theorem within one-dimensional quantum systems:
i) the dependence of correlation length on temperature,
ii) the behavior of correlation decay rates for interactions decaying slower than subexponential forms.

Despite these advancements, our study unveils several unresolved issues.
Among the most critical is improving the correlation decay rate in one-dimensional systems with subexponential (or faster) interaction decay.
This challenge primarily stems from the approximation used in the inequality (
V.2
).
To minimize the error in the approximate belief propagation, i.e.,
\mathcal{G}_{\beta}(\ell)
, a larger block size
\ell
is necessary.
Yet, this results in fewer blocks (
m\propto R/\ell
), leading to a less strict decay rate of
e^{-m/e^{\Theta(\beta)}}
as per equation (
155
).
At this stage, the improvement is not a straightforward task, and addressing this issue may require fundamentally novel analytical methods.

One potential strategy for overcoming this challenge involves analyzing the zero-free region of the partition function, as discussed in
Harrow et al. 2020
. This entails to find a complex number
z
such that the partition function satisfies

Z_{\beta+iz}={\rm tr}\left(e^{(\beta+iz)H}\right)\neq 0

for any
|z|\leq\Delta
, with
\Delta>0
. By proving a zero-free region for the partition function for
\Delta=\Omega(1)
, we can prove the exponential clustering theorem from
(
Harrow et al. 2020
, Theorem 10 therein)
. Achieving this would leverage a bootstrapping method: initially applying the current ‘loose’ clustering theorem to establish the zero-free region, then applying this result to refine the clustering of correlation based on Ref.
Harrow et al. 2020
.
This approach is anticipated to be viable if the quasi-locality
\mathcal{G}_{\beta}(r)
decays exponentially with distance
r
, applicable in scenarios of interactions decaying (faster than) exponentially.

Furthermore, analyzing the complex zeros of the partition function reveals additional implications. The current clustering theorem does not exclude the possibility of a phase transition in systems with power-law decaying interaction since the correlation decay is polynomial in both critical and non-critical phases. A zero-free region for
|z|\leq\Delta=\Omega(1)
leads to the partition function’s analyticity in the thermodynamic limit, suggesting the absence of phase transition in long-range interacting systems.
So far, there are no quantum analogs to the classical no-go theorem
Dobrushin 1973
for the phase transition in long-range interacting systems.
We speculate that the critical condition for the power-law decay rate
\alpha
might also be
2
in quantum scenarios, yet this remains a significant open question. We hope that our new analytical techniques, including the quantum belief propagation, will pave the way for resolving further open questions regarding the 1D long-range interacting systems.
