Realizable Standard Young Tableaux
Igor Araujo *, Alexander E. Black †, Amanda Burcroff ‡, Yibo Gao §, Robert A. Krueger *, Alex McDonough †

Abstract
. Given two vectors u u and v v , their outer sum is given by the matrix A A with entries A i ​ j = u i + v j A_{ij}=u_{i}+v_{j} . If the entries of u u and v v are increasing and sufficiently generic, the total ordering of the entries of the matrix is a standard Young tableau of rectangular shape. We call standard Young tableaux arising in this way realizable . The set of realizable tableaux was defined by Mallows and Vanderbei for studying a deconvolution algorithm, but we show they have appeared in many other contexts including sorting algorithms, quantum computing, random sorting networks, reflection arrangements, fiber polytopes, and Goodman and Pollack’s theory of allowable sequences. In our work, we prove tight bounds on the asymptotic number of realizable rectangular tableaux. We also derive tight asymptotics for the number of realizable allowable sequences, which are in bijection with realizable staircase-shaped standard Young tableaux with the notion of realizability coming from the theory of sorting networks. As a consequence, we resolve an open question of Angel, Gorin, and Holroyd from 2012 and improve upon a 1986 result of Goodman and Pollack.

Introduction

Motivated by the study of a deconvolution algorithm in
[
16

x\in\mathbb{R}^{k}

x_{1}<x_{2}<\dots<x_{k}

x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}

as the matrix

(x\circ y)_{ij}=x_{i}+y_{j}
. Since

and
y
are increasing, the entries of
x\circ y
must be increasing from left to right and top to bottom. For a generic choice of
x
and

x\circ y
are distinct. In this case, the total order on
[m]\times[n]
given by the entries of
x\circ y
must correspond to a standard Young tableau of rectangular shape
m\times n
, which we denote
\mathcal{T}(x\circ y)
. We call a rectangular standard Young tableau, or rectangular tableau for short,
realizable
if it is equal to
\mathcal{T}(x\circ y)
for some
x
and
y
. See Figure
1

m\times n

\SYT(m,n)
, and the set of realizable rectangular tableaux by
\rSYT(m,n)
.

As a first step for studying realizable rectangular tableaux, Mallows and Vanderbei showed that all
2\times n
tableaux are realizable and gave examples of
m\times n
tableaux for small choices of
m
and
n
that are not realizable. They concluded that the problem of studying realizable tableaux is difficult and required some new perspective. The goal of our work is to asymptotically enumerate these tableaux and describe many other contexts in which they arise including sorting algorithms, quantum computing, random sorting networks, reflection arrangements, fiber polytopes, and Goodman and Pollack’s theory of allowable sequences. The key starting point for our analysis comes from the following observation by the second author and Sanyal in
[
5
]
.

Proposition 1.1

(
[
5
]
)
.

Define the
\rSYT(m,n)
-arrangement
H
to be the union of
\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}:x_{i}+y_{k}=x_{j}+y_{\ell}\}
over all
i,j\in[m]
and
k,\ell\in[n]
. Then, the set of regions of
H
contained in the cone
\{(x,y):x_{i}<x_{i+1},y_{j}<y_{j+1}\text{ for all }i\in[m],j\in[n]\}
is in bijection with
\rSYT(m,n)
. Thus,
|\rSYT(m,n)|=\frac{|r(H)|}{m!n!}
, where
r(H)
denotes the set of regions of
H
.

This hyperplane arrangement and the corresponding observation also appeared independently due to Klyachko in the context of quantum computing for the
N
-representability problem for qubits (see Remark
3.4.2
of
[
15
]
). In that context, Klyachko called realizable rectangular tableaux
cubicles
. The case of cubicles is a special case of a larger story of arrangements associated to fundamental particles in physics
[
6
,
7
]
. This arrangement is also of independent interest in geometric combinatorics for studying generalizations of generalized permutahedra to allow for edge directions of the form
e_{i}-e_{j}+e_{k}-e_{\ell}
, which was one of the primary motivations for the introduction of nested braid fans in
[
8
]
. This geometric perspective is key for proving our first two main theorems, which provide asymptotic bounds on the number of realizable rectangular tableaux.

Theorem 1.2
.

For every
m\leq n
, we have

|\rSYT(m,n)|\leq\frac{1}{m!n!}\sum_{i=0}^{m+n}\binom{2\binom{m}{2}\binom{n}{2}+\binom{m}{2}+\binom{n}{2}}{i}.

In particular, this implies
|\rSYT(n,n)|\leq n^{4n+o(n)}
as
n\to\infty
.

We provide lower bounds via a recurrence.

Theorem 1.3
.

For every
m,n
, we have

|\rSYT(m,n)|\geq|\rSYT(m-1,n)|\left(\binom{n}{2}+1\right).

In particular, this implies
|\rSYT(n,n)|\geq n^{4n-o(n)}
.

Theorems

1.2
and
1.3
imply for
m\leq n
that, as both
m
and
n
tend to infinity, we have
|rSTY(m,n)|=m^{(2+o(1))(n+m)}
. When
m
is constant,
Theorems

1.2
and
1.3
show that
|rSTY(m,n)|
is exponential in
n
but are not precise enough to determine the base of the exponential.

Our upper bound arguments generalize an argument of Fredman in
[
12
]
from complexity theory. In the algorithmic theory of sorting, a longstanding open problem is to find the time complexity of sorting a list of the form
X+Y=\{x+y:x\in X,~y\in Y\}
for two lists
X,Y\subseteq\mathbb{R}
with
|X|=|Y|=n
. Fredman
[
12
]
studied this problem nearly fifty years ago, and in doing so proved an upper bound of
n^{8n+o(n)}
on the number of orderings of a list of the form
X+Y
. If the two lists
X
and
Y
are ordered such that they are increasing, then the set of possible total orderings is the set of realizable rectangular tableaux. Through a more careful analysis of the asymptotics,
Theorems

1.2
and
1.3
improve Fredman’s estimate, providing the correct number of orderings of
X+Y
up to the
o(n)
term in the exponent.

By the hook length formula, we may establish the following corollary in contrast to the case
m=2
.

Corollary 1.4
.

For every
m=m(n)\geq 3
, the probability that a uniformly randomly chosen
m\times n
tableau is realizable tends to
0
as
n\to\infty
.

Next, we move to staircase-shaped standard Young tableaux, or staircase tableaux for short, where there is an analogous notion of realizability coming from the theory of sorting networks. A sorting network is any way of sorting the identity permutation to its reverse permutation using adjacent transpositions, where at each step, we increase the number of inversions by applying a single adjacent transposition. Sorting networks are in bijection with staircase tableaux, monotone paths on the permutahedron, and maximal chains in the weak Bruhat order
[
2
]
. Sorting networks also have a notion of realizability called being
geometrically realizable
or
stretchable
; see
[
1
]
. A
realizable staircase tableau
is one whose corresponding sorting network is geometrically realizable, and we denote the set of all realizable staircase tableaux by
\rSYT(n)
. In
[
1
]
, Angel, Gorin, and Holroyd showed that the probability that a random sorting network is geometrically realizable tends to
0
, which informs the story of limit shapes of random sorting networks (see
[
2
,
9
]
for details).

The analogy between the notions of realizability for staircase tableaux and rectangular tableaux may be understood in terms of paths on polytopes. Given a polytope
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
with vertices
V(P)
and
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\mathbf{0}
, a
monotone path
is a path on the graph of a polytope (i.e., its set of vertices and edges) from a minimizer of
w^{T}x
to a maximizer of
w^{T}x
such that each step of the path increases
w^{T}x
. A monotone path
\ell
is called
coherent
with respect to
w
if there exists some
c\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\mathbf{0}
such that the set of vertices
V(\ell)
in
\ell
is exactly

\{v\in V(P):v=\argmax_{x\in P}(c+\lambda w)^{T}x\text{ for some }\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\}.

See
[
3
]
for more details. Let
S_{n}
denote the symmetric group on
n
letters. For rectangular tableaux, the second author and Sanyal showed the following.

Theorem 1.5

(
[
5
]
)
.

Monotone paths on the permutahedron
\Pi_{m+n}
with respect to the vector
e_{[m]}=\sum_{i\in[m]}e_{i}
are in bijection with
S_{m}\times S_{n}\times\SYT(m,n)
. Furthermore, coherent monotone paths with respect to
e_{[m]}
are in bijection with
S_{m}\times S_{n}\times\rSYT(m,n)
.

For staircase tableaux, there is a similar relationship.

Proposition 1.6

(
[
11
,
18
]
)
.

Monotone paths on the permutahedron
\Pi_{n}
with respect to any generic
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
w_{1}<w_{2}<\dots<w_{n}
are in bijection with standard Young tableaux of staircase shape. Furthermore, realizable staircase tableaux are in bijection with monotone paths such that there exists some
w
for which they are coherent.

The first bijection is the Edelman-Greene bijection, and the connection to coherence comes from
[
18
]
. Hence, there is a fundamental geometric relationship between these two notions of realizability. In
[
1
]
, Angel, Gorin, and Holroyd showed that the probability of a random sorting network being realizable tends to
0
as
n\to\infty
at a rate of at most
e^{-Cn}
and asked if this can be improved to
e^{-Cn^{2}}
, for some constant
C
. We resolve their question by showing the probability tends to
0
even faster.

Theorem 1.7
.

The number of realizable standard Young tableaux of staircase shape
(1,2,\dots,n)
is
n^{5n+o(n)}
. In particular, by the hook length formula, the probability that a standard Young tableau of staircase shape is realizable tends to
0
at a rate of
n^{-\Theta(n^{2})}
.

Sorting networks are also equivalent to allowable sequences in the language of Goodman and Pollack
[
14
]
. They called an allowable sequence realizable if its corresponding sorting network is geometrically realizable. Hence, our result is an asymptotic enumeration of realizable allowable sequences. Goodman and Pollack showed in
[
13
]
that the number of allowable sequences, or equivalently combinatorial types of point configurations of
n
points in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, is at most
n^{8n}
meaning that the open question of Angel, Gorin, and Holroyd had already been settled 20 years prior up to the translation of terminology that we provide here. Our result is an improvement upon theirs in that we provide tight asymptotics. For the upper bound, we use precisely the same tools as theirs with a more careful analysis as with the bound we derive from Fredman’s work for rectangular tableaux. For the lower bound, we directly generalize their argument for estimating the number of order types of labeled point configurations.

Our final results are in the vein of geometric combinatorics. Coherent monotone paths on a polytope
P
for a fixed vector
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
correspond to the vertices of the
monotone path polytope
of
P
with respect to
w
denoted by
\Sigma_{w}(P)
. Monotone path polytopes are a special case of the fiber polytope construction introduced by Billera and Sturmfels in
[
4
]
. Up to permutation factors, realizable rectangular tableaux are in bijection with coherent
e_{[m]}
-monotone paths on the permutahedron
\Pi_{m+n}=\text{conv}(\{S_{m+n}(1,2,\dots,m+n)\})
, where
S_{m+n}
is the symmetric group on
m+n
letters. An alternative approach to understanding realizable rectangular tableaux is to instead try to compute the monotone path polytope directly. In particular, the second author and Sanyal showed the following in
[
5
]
.

Proposition 1.8

(
[
5
]
)
.

The zonotope dual to the
\rSYT(m,n)
-arrangement is normally equivalent to the Minkowski sum

\sum_{k=\binom{m+1}{2}}^{\binom{m+n+1}{2}-\binom{n+1}{2}}\left\{x\in\Pi_{m+n}:\sum_{i=1}^{m}x_{i}=k\right\}.

To better understand the combinatorics of the arrangement, we describe the normal fans of these summands giving insight into the cones that appear in the arrangement. In particular, each summand is given by intersecting the permutahedron with a hyperplane, and this observation is fundamental for our analysis of the slices.

The paper is organized as follows. In
Sections

2
and
3

Section

4

1.8
and some observations about the combinatorics of the regions of the arrangement. Finally, in
Section

5
, we supply many open problems and further directions for where to go next with realizable tableaux.

2.
Rectangular Tableaux

In this section, we study the number of realizable rectangular standard Young tableaux. We place upper bounds by bounding the number of regions in a related hyperplane arrangement, and our lower bounds are obtained by considering iterated row extensions of realizable tableaux. In the case of fixed height and increasing width, as well as the case of simultaneously increasing height and width, we show that our bounds are asymptotically tight.

We begin with a result of Mallows and Vanderbei.

Proposition 2.1
.

[
16
]

For any positive integer
n
, all
2\times n
Young tableaux are realizable. In particular,

|\SYT(2,n)|=|\rSYT(2,n)|=C_{n},

where
C_{n}
is the
n^{th}
Catalan number.

2.1.
Structure of realizable tableaux

In this subsection, we discuss some useful preliminaries to aid in understanding the structure of realizable rectangular tableaux.

Realizability of rectangular tableaux is a rather delicate property, and the presence of certain substructures can prevent a tableau from being realized. Mallows and Vanderbei identified a few such substructures, which they called
taboo configurations

[
16
]
. We identify a class of these obstructions to realizability in the following proposition.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
M=\begin{bmatrix}M_{x}\end{bmatrix}_{x\in[m]\times[n]}
be an
m\times n
tableau. If there exist two disjoint sets of equal size
A=\{a_{1},\dots,a_{\ell}\},B=\{b_{1},\dots,b_{\ell}\}\subseteq[m]\times[n]
such that

(i)

M_{a_{k}}<M_{b_{k}}
for each
k\in\{1,\dots,\ell\}
,

(ii)

\left|A\cap\left(\{i\}\times[n]\right)\right|=\left|B\cap\left(\{i\}\times[n]\right)\right|
for each
i\in[m]
, and

(iii)

\left|A\cap\left([m]\times\{j\}\right)\right|=\left|B\cap\left([m]\times\{j\}\right)\right|
for each
j\in[n]
,

then
M
is not realizable.

Proof.

Consider the sums
S_{A}
and
S_{B}
of the entries indexed by each of the sets
A
and
B
, respectively. Condition (
i
) implies that
S_{A}<S_{B}
, while, if the tableau is indeed realizable, Conditions (
ii
) and (
iii
) imply that
S_{A}=S_{B}
.
∎

We conjecture that the presence of the substructures described in Proposition
2.2
is the only obstruction to realizability; see Section
5
for further discussion.

2.2.
An upper bound via hyperplane arrangements

We consider the following set of hyperplanes in
\mathbb{R}^{m+n}
:

\mathcal{C}=\left\{x_{i}+y_{j}=x_{k}+y_{\ell}:i,k\in[n],~j,\ell\in[m],\text{ and $i\neq k$ or $j\neq\ell$}\right\},

where the coordinates of a point in
\mathbb{R}^{m+n}
are denoted by
x_{1},\dots,x_{m},y_{1},\dots,y_{n}
. Let

\mathcal{P}=\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{m},y_{1},\dots,y_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{m+n}:x_{1}<x_{2}<\cdots<x_{m},~y_{1}<y_{2}<\cdots<y_{n}\},

and observe that the realizable
m\times n
standard Young tableaux are in bijective correspondence with the regions inside
\mathcal{P}
after partitioning with the hyperplanes in
\mathcal{C}
.

Note that
\mathcal{C}
is symmetric with respect to the action of
S_{m}
on the first
m
coordinates and
S_{n}
on the last
n
coordinates. Hence, the number of regions
\mathcal{C}
splits
\mathbb{R}^{m+n}
into is
m!n!
times the number of regions
\mathcal{C}
splits
P
into. The following well-known result (see, for example,
[
19
, Proposition 2.4]
) gives a general upper bound for the number of regions in any hyperplane arrangement.

Lemma 2.6
.

The number of regions formed by partitioning
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by
r
hyperplanes is at most
\sum_{i=0}^{d}\binom{r}{i}
.

We will also use the following known result regarding the total number of
m\times n
standard Young tableaux, which follows from the hook length formula.

Lemma 2.7
.

The total number of
m\times n
standard Young tableaux is given by

|\SYT(m,n)|=(mn)!\prod_{j=0}^{m-1}\frac{j!}{(n+j)!}.

Proof of
Theorem

1.2
.

By the previous discussion, the number of regions of the arrangement
\mathcal{C}
contained in the region
\mathcal{P}
is an upper bound on the number of
m\times n
realizable standard Young tableaux. The number of hyperplanes in the arrangement
\mathcal{C}
is precisely
2\binom{m}{2}\binom{n}{2}+\binom{m}{2}+\binom{n}{2}
. The desired upper bound then follows directly by applying
2.6
.
∎

Proof of
1.4
.

For sufficiently large
n
, we have by
Theorem

1.2
that

\displaystyle|\rSYT(m,n)|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n!m!}(n+m)\binom{2\binom{m}{2}\binom{n}{2}+\binom{m}{2}+\binom{n}{2}}{n+m}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\left(\left(\frac{m(m-1)+1}{2}\right)n^{2}\right)^{n+m}}{n!m!(n+m-1)!}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{m(m-1)+1}{2}\right)^{n+m}n^{2(n+m)}\left(\frac{e}{n}\right)^{n}\left(\frac{e}{m}\right)^{m}\left(\frac{e}{n+m-1}\right)^{n+m-1}

\displaystyle\leq n\left(\frac{n}{m}\right)^{m}\left(\frac{e^{2}}{2}\left(m(m-1)+1\right)\right)^{n+m}.

Note that for
m=n
, this upper bound becomes
n^{4n+o(n)}
as in Theorem
1.2
.

Let
\beta
denote the number of
m\times n
standard Young tableaux. We have by Lemma
2.7
that

\beta=(mn)!\prod_{j=0}^{m-1}\frac{j!}{(n+j)!}\,.

For sufficiently large
n
, we have that

\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle\geq\frac{(mn)!}{(n+m)^{\binom{m}{2}}(n!)^{m}}\geq\frac{(mn/e)^{mn}}{(n+m)^{\binom{m}{2}}n^{m}(n/e)^{mn}}\geq\frac{m^{mn}}{(n+m)^{\binom{m}{2}}n^{m}}.

Combining our bounds, we have for
3\leq m\leq n
,

\displaystyle\frac{|\rSYT(m,n)|}{\beta}

\displaystyle\leq n\left(\frac{n}{m}\right)^{m}\left(\frac{e^{2}}{2}\left(m(m-1)+1\right)\right)^{n+m}\cdot\frac{(n+m)^{\binom{m}{2}}n^{m}}{m^{mn}}

\displaystyle\leq n\left(\frac{n^{2}}{m}\right)^{m}(2nm^{2})^{m^{2}/2}\left(\frac{e^{2}}{2}\cdot\frac{m(m-1)+1}{m^{m}}\right)^{n+m}.

Since
\frac{e^{2}(m(m-1)+1)}{2(m^{m})}<1
for
m\geq 3
, the exponential term in
n
dominates the polynomial term in
n
, so for fixed
m
, this quantity goes to
0
as
n
goes to infinity. For
m
sufficiently large, we can further simplify the above upper bound as

\frac{|\rSYT(m,n)|}{\beta}\leq n\left(\frac{n^{2}}{m}\right)^{m}(2nm^{2})^{m^{2}/2}m^{-(m-3)(n+m)}=n\left(\frac{n^{2}m^{2}(2n)^{m/2}}{m^{n(1-3/m)}}\right)^{m},

which goes to
0
as
n
goes to infinity.
∎

2.3.
Single-row extensions and a lower bound

To prove the lower bound from
Theorem

1.3
, we introduce
single-row extensions
of realizable rectangular tableaux and apply a recursive argument.

Definition 2.8
.

Given a rectangular tableau
T\in\rSYT(m-1,n)
, a
single-row extension
of
T
is a rectangular tableau
T^{\prime}\in\rSYT(m,n)
such that when the bottom row of
T^{\prime}
is removed, the relative order of the remaining entries corresponds to
T
.

Lemma 2.9
.

Any tableau
T\in\rSYT(m-1,n)
has at least
\binom{n}{2}+1
single-row extensions.

Proof.

Fix increasing
x=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{m-1})
and
y=(y_{1},y_{2},\dots,y_{n})
such that
\mathcal{T}(x\circ y)=T
.
By shifting the points slightly if necessary, we can assume that differences of the form
y_{i}-y_{j}
for
i>j
are all distinct.

Let
x_{m}
be a real number greater than
x_{m-1}
, and let

Proof of

By Lemma

Proposition 2.10

Let

n^{2}(m-1)+1-(\text{sum of entries in the bottom row of $T$}).

Proof.

The general method of the proof is similar to the proof of Lemma

3.
Staircase Tableaux

To bound the number of realizable staircase tableaux, we leverage their equivalence to geometrically realizable sorting networks. A
sorting network
is a sequence
\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{\binom{n}{2}}
of permutations such that
\sigma_{0}
is the identity,
\sigma_{\binom{n}{2}}
is the reverse permutation,
\sigma_{i+1}=s_{k}\sigma_{i}
for some adjacent transposition
s_{k}
, and
\sigma_{i+1}
has more inversions than
\sigma_{i}
for all
0\leq i\leq\binom{n}{2}-1
. Sorting networks correspond to staircase tableaux of length
n-1
via the Edelman-Greene bijection
[
11
]
. We are interested in a certain subclass of sorting networks for comparison.

Definition 3.1
.

Consider a subset
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
|X|=n
such that the slope between any pair of points is distinct and no two have the same first coordinate. Then the direction
(1,0)
imposes a total order on
X
. Furthermore, the set of total orderings of the point configuration induced by taking the dot product with a vector
z_{\theta}=(\cos(\theta),\sin(\theta))
as
\theta
ranges from
0
to
\pi
yields a sorting network. A sorting network that arises from some subset
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
in this way is called
geometrically realizable
. See Figure
4
for an example of a realizable sorting network together with a set of points in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
that realize it.

z_{\theta}^{T}v^{i}>z_{\theta}^{T}v^{j}
for each
i<j\in[n]
. In particular, consider the hyperplane arrangement
H
with normals given by
\{v^{i}-v^{j}:1\leq i<j\leq n\}
. Thus, the regions of this arrangement correspond to total orderings of
v^{1},v^{2},\dots,v^{n}
. In particular, the ordering induced by
z_{\theta}
is determined by which region of the hyperplane arrangement
z_{\theta}
is contained in.

Let
Z=\sum_{i=1}^{n}[v^{i}-v^{j},v^{j}-v^{i}]
be a zonogon dual to this hyperplane arrangement. The upper path on
Z
is the path traced by the set of vertices

\{v\in V(Z):\text{ there exists }(a,b)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\text{ such that }v=\text{argmax}_{x\in Z}(a,b)\cdot x\}.

Since
Z
is dual to
H
, the upper path corresponds to the set of regions of
H
containing a vector with a non-negative second coordinate. These are precisely the set of regions containing
z_{\theta}
for some choice of
\theta\in[0,\pi]
. Hence, the upper path in the zonogon
Z
is determined by a corresponding geometrically realizable sorting network.

We will use this observation to define a geometric space that we may use to upper bound the number of geometrically realizable sorting networks. Define a linear map
\pi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
by
\pi(e_{i})=v^{i}
. Then
Z=\pi\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}[e_{i}-e_{j},e_{j}-e_{i}]\right)=\pi(\Pi_{n})
, where
\Pi_{n}
is normally equivalent to the permutahedron. Since
\pi
is linear, there exist
c,w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\pi(x)=(w^{T}x,c^{T}x)
. Furthermore, since
(1,0)
imposes a total order on
X
, we may up to reordering of indices assume that
w_{1}<w_{2}<\dots<w_{n}
. The upper path then corresponds to a path from a vertex with minimal first coordinate to one with maximal first coordinate. Since
Z
is a zonogon, this path uses all edge directions
v^{i}-v^{j}
exactly once. The ordering of the edges that are used then determines the path. At each step, the slope of an edge in the path must decrease. Thus, the total ordering on edges in the path is determined by the total ordering on the slopes
\left\{\frac{c_{i}-c_{j}}{{w_{i}-w_{j}}}:1\leq j<i\leq n\right\}
.

Hence, the set of geometrically realizable sorting networks is always given by a total ordering on slopes by some choice of
c
and
w
. We have the following lemma as an immediate consequence.

Lemma 3.2
.

The number of realizable staircase tableaux is at most the number of different possible total orderings of
\{(i,j):i<j\}
across all
c,w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
induced by the relation that

(i,j)<(k,\ell)\quad\text{ if }\quad\frac{c_{i}-c_{j}}{w_{i}-w_{j}}<\frac{c_{k}-c_{\ell}}{w_{k}-w_{\ell}}.

In particular,
|\rSYT(n)|
is precisely the number of regions of this arrangement containing a point such that
w_{1}<w_{2}<\dots<w_{n}
.

Note that
\frac{c_{i}-c_{j}}{w_{i}-w_{j}}<\frac{c_{k}-c_{\ell}}{w_{k}-w_{\ell}}
if and only if
(c_{i}-c_{j})(w_{k}-w_{\ell})-(c_{k}-c_{\ell})(w_{i}-w_{j})<0
, assuming the coordinates of
w
are increasing. Hence, the set of possible orderings from
3.2
is the number of regions of the hypersurface arrangement with hypersurfaces

H_{i,j,k,\ell}=\{c,w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:(c_{i}-c_{j})(w_{k}-w_{\ell})-(c_{k}-c_{\ell})(w_{i}-w_{j})=0\},

for every
1\leq i<j\leq n
,
1\leq k<\ell\leq n
and
(i,j)\neq(k,\ell)
. Furthermore, for a geometrically realizable sorting network, we may always assume that the coordinates of
w_{i}
are increasing. Hence, to bound the number of geometrically realizable sorting networks or equivalently, the number of realizable staircase tableaux, it suffices to bound the number of regions of this hypersurface arrangement when intersected with the cone
\{(c,w):w_{i}<w_{i+1}\text{ for all }1\leq i\leq n\}
. To do this, we rely on the following result.

Theorem 3.3

(Milnor
[
17
]
, Thom
[
20
]
, Warren
[
21
]
)
.

Given a set of
N
polynomials in
\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{k}]
of degree at most
D
, the number of different regions of the corresponding hypersurface arrangement is at most

\left(\frac{4eDN}{k}\right)^{k}.

This general upper bound for arrangements implies our asymptotic upper bound.

Lemma 3.4
.

The number of realizable staircase tableaux is at most
n^{5n+o(n)}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.2
and the discussion thereafter, it suffices to bound the number of regions of the hypersurface arrangement with hypersurfaces

H_{i,j,k,\ell}=\{c,w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:(c_{i}-c_{j})(w_{k}-w_{\ell})-(c_{k}-c_{\ell})(w_{i}-w_{j})=0\},

where
i,j,k,\ell
satisfy
1\leq i<j\leq n
,
1\leq k<\ell\leq n
, and
(i,j)\neq(k,\ell)
, intersected with the cone
\{(c,w)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:{w_{i}<w_{i+1}\text{ for all }1\leq i\leq n}\}
. The degree
D
of these polynomials is
2
, the number of variables is
2n
, and the number of polynomials is
\binom{\binom{n}{2}}{2}=O(n^{4})
. Then the total number of regions of the hypersurface arrangement
\mathcal{H}
of all
H_{i,j,k,\ell}
is at most

\left(\frac{4e\cdot 2\cdot\binom{\binom{n}{2}}{2}}{2n}\right)^{2n}=n^{6n+o(n)}.

Note that this arrangement is symmetric with respect to reordering the coordinates
w_{1},\dots,w_{n}
. Hence, the set of regions containing a point such that
w_{1}<w_{2}<\dots<w_{n}
, is
\frac{1}{n!}
times the number of regions of
\mathcal{H}
. Therefore, the total number of realizable staircase tableaux is at most
\frac{n^{6n+o(n)}}{n!}=n^{5n+o(n)}
.
∎

Lemma 3.5
.

For every
n\geq 2
, we have that
|\rSYT(n)|\geq\left(\frac{1}{n+1}\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}2(i-1)\binom{\binom{i}{2}}{2}\right)|\rSYT(n-1)|

Proof.

For each realizable staircase tableau
T
of shape
(1,2,\dots,n-1)

n

(x_{1},y_{1}),\dots,(x_{n},y_{n})
. In particular, we may choose this realization to be generic, since a small perturbation the points will not affect the total ordering of the slopes.

To create a new tableau, we add a new point
(x_{n+1},y_{n+1})
to this configuration. Consider the line arrangement
\mathcal{A}_{n}
given by taking all lines through each of the points
(x_{i},y_{i})
of slope
\frac{y_{k}-y_{j}}{x_{k}-x_{j}}
for each
1\leq j<k\leq n
. Let
I_{n}
be the set of points in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
where any of the lines in
\mathcal{A}_{n}
intersect. Let
L(x,y)
be the set of lines through
(x,y)
of slope
\frac{y_{k}-y_{j}}{x_{k}-x_{j}}
for each
1\leq j<k\leq n
. The points
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that
L(x,y)
intersects nontrivially with
I_{n}
form a union of lines in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Thus, inside any region in the arrangement
\mathcal{A}_{n}
, we can choose a point
(x_{n+1},y_{n+1})
such that no line in
L(x_{n+1},y_{n+1})
passes through any point of
I_{n}
.

We now want to lower bound the number of new regions formed by such a choice of
(x_{n+1},y_{n+1})
. Given such a choice, consider the line arrangement
\mathcal{A}_{n+1}
and the set of intersection points
I_{n+1}
. It is a standard results in the theory of line arrangements that the number of new regions is at least the number of new intersection points, i.e.,

|r(A_{n+1})|-|r(A_{n})|\geq|I_{n+1}\setminus I_{n}|\,.

By our restriction on the choice of
(x_{n+1},y_{n+1})
, we know that the intersection point between a line in
L(x_{n+1},y_{n+1})
and each non-parallel line in
\mathcal{A}_{n}

\left(\binom{n}{2}-1\right)(n-1)
, does not lie in
I_{n}
. Moreover, all these intersection points are distinct since the lines in
L(x_{n+1},y_{n+1})
all pass through
(x_{n+1},y_{n+1})
, and hence cannot intersect elsewhere. Thus, we have

|I_{n+1}\setminus I_{n}|\geq|L(x_{n+1},y_{n+1})|\cdot\left(\binom{n}{2}-1\right)(n-1)=\binom{n}{2}\left(\binom{n}{2}-1\right)(n-1)=2(n-1)\binom{\binom{n}{2}}{2}\,.

Applying this bound inductively, we may lower bound the number of regions of this line arrangement to be at least
\sum_{i=1}^{n}2(i-1)\binom{\binom{i}{2}}{2}
. Thus, the number of possible extensions of any fixed
T
by adding a new point is at least
\sum_{i=1}^{n}2(i-1)\binom{\binom{i}{2}}{2}
. Note that this map is at most
(n+1)
-to-
1
, since the original tableau may be recovered by deleting one of the points from the new configuration. Therefore, the total number of new tableaux generated across all realizable staircase tableaux of shape
(1,2,\dots,n-1)
is at least
\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}2(i-1)\binom{\binom{i}{2}}{2}|\rSYT(n-1)|
.
∎

Proof.

4.2.
Normal cones

A simple description of the edges of
S_{m,n,k}
allows us to describe the normal fan at a vertex
x
. In the remainder of the section, we describe the normal cone
C_{x}
at vertex
x
independent of
4.2
, and in a root-theoretic manner.

Let
\Phi=\{e_{i}-e_{j}\>|\>1\leq i\neq j\leq m{+}n\}
be the root system of type
A_{m+n-1}
, with a standard choice of positive roots
\Phi^{+}=\{e_{i}-e_{j}\>|\>1\leq i<j\leq m{+}n\}
and simple roots
\Delta=\{e_{i}-e_{i+1}\>|\>1\leq i\leq m{+}n{-}1\}
. For a vertex
x
of
S_{m,n,k}
, define

\Phi_{x}^{+}=\{\alpha\in\Phi\>|\>\langle\alpha,x\rangle>0\}

and let its corresponding simple roots be
\Delta_{x}\subset\Phi_{x}^{+}
, i.e.,
\Delta_{x}
is a basis of the ambient vector space and every
\alpha\in\Phi_{x}^{+}
can be written as a nonnegative linear combination of
\Delta_{x}
. Define the set
\Delta_{x}^{m}=\{\alpha\in\Delta_{x}\>|\>\langle\alpha,\omega_{m}\rangle\neq 0\}
where the fundamental weight
\omega_{m}=(1,1,\ldots,1,0,\ldots,0)
with
m

1
’s.

Lemma 4.3
.

With the above notation, the normal cone at a vertex
x
of
S_{m,n,k}
can be written as

C_{x}=\{u\>|\>\langle u,\beta\rangle>0\ \forall\beta\in\Delta_{x}\setminus\Delta_{x}^{m},\ \langle u,\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}\rangle>0\ \forall\beta_{1}\neq\beta_{2}\in\Delta_{x}^{m}\}.

Proof.

Given a vertex
x
of
S_{m,n,\ell}
, the set
\Delta_{x}
consists of roots of the form
e_{i}-e_{j}
where
x_{i}-x_{j}=1
. The condition
\langle e_{i}-e_{j},\omega_{m}\rangle=0
means that either
i,j\leq m
or
i,j>m
. Therefore, we have

\begin{cases}\Delta_{x}\setminus\Delta_{x}^{m}&=\{e_{i}-e_{j}\>|\>u_{i}-u_{j}=1\text{ and }i,j\leq m\text{ or }i,j>,\},\\
\Delta_{x}^{m}&=\{e_{i}-e_{j}\>|\>u_{i}-u_{j}=1\text{ and }i\leq m<j\text{ or }j\leq m<i\}\end{cases}.

By definition, the normal cone is described by

C_{x}=\{u\>|\>\langle u,x-v\rangle>0\text{ for all vertex }v\text{ incident to }x\}.

We have described the three types of edges in
S_{m,n,k}
in
4.2
. The edges of type (1) correspond to
x-v=\beta\in\Delta_{x}\setminus\Delta_{x}^{m}
; the edges of type (2) correspond to
x-v=\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}
where
\beta_{1}=e_{i}-e_{j}
,
\beta_{2}=e_{p}-e_{q}\in\Delta_{x}^{m}
and
\{i,j\}\cap\{p,q\}=\varnothing
; the edges of type (3) correspond to
x-v=\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}
where
\beta_{1}=e_{i}-e_{j}
,
\beta_{2}=e_{j}-e_{\ell}\in\Delta_{x}^{m}
. Together,
4.2
translates to the desired result.
∎

\begin{cases}\Delta_{x}\setminus\Delta_{x}^{m}&=\{e_{4}-e_{2},e_{5}-e_{3},e_{8}-e_{6}\},\\
\Delta_{x}^{m}&=\{e_{7}-e_{4},e_{1}-e_{7},e_{9}-e_{1},e_{3}-e_{9},e_{6}-e_{5}\}.\end{cases}

A point

needs to satisfy the conditions

4.3.
Dimension of faces

Faces of
S_{m,n,k}
can be obtained from faces of
\Pi_{m+n}
by intersecting the hyperplane
H=\{x_{1}+\cdots+x_{m}=k\}
. Recall from
4.1
that for a flag
\mathcal{F}
of
d-1
proper subsets of
[N]
, we denote its corresponding face in
\Pi_{N}
by
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{F})
, which has dimension
N-d
. The goal of this section is to explicitly provide the dimension of the face
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{F})\cap H
in
S_{m,n,k}
.

For a flag
\mathcal{F}
of
[m+n]
, define

A_{2i-1}=[m]\cap(F_{i}\setminus F_{i-1})\quad\text{and}\quad A_{2i}=\{m{+}1,\ldots,m{+}n\}\cap(F_{i}\setminus F_{i-1})

for
i=1,\ldots,d
so that
[m+n]
has a partition into disjoint union
A_{1}\sqcup A_{2}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup A_{2d}
. Define
\min_{[m]}(\mathcal{F})
to be the sum of the first
m
coordinates of a vector
v
obtained by putting values
1,\ldots,|A_{1}|
onto coordinates in
A_{1}
, values
|A_{1}|+1,\ldots,|A_{1}|+|A_{2}|
onto coordinates in
A_{2}
, the next smallest values onto coordinates in
A_{3}
and so on. Similarly, define
\max_{[m]}(\mathcal{F})
to be the sum of the first
m
coordinates of a vector obtained by putting the largest values among
1,\ldots,m+n
onto coordinates in
A_{1}
, the next largest values onto coordinates in
A_{2}
and so on.

Lemma 4.4
.

If
\min_{[m]}(\mathcal{F})<k<\max_{[m]}(\mathcal{F})
, then
\dim(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{F})\cap H)=m+n-d-1
. If
k=\min_{[m]}(\mathcal{F})
or
k=\max_{[m]}(\mathcal{F})
, then
\dim(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{F})\cap H)=m+n-|\{i\>|\>A_{i}\neq\varnothing\}|.

Proof.

By construction,
\min_{[m]}(\mathcal{F})=\min(x_{1}+\cdots+x_{m}\>|\>x\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{F}))
and same with
\max_{[m]}
. The condition
\min_{[m]}(\mathcal{F})<k<\max_{[m]}(\mathcal{F})
precisely says that the hyperplane
H=\{x_{1}+\cdots+x_{m}=k\}
goes through the interior of the face
L_{\mathcal{F}}
, thus decreasing the dimension by
1
after intersecting. Since
\dim\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{F})=m+n-d
, we have
\dim(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{F})\cap H)=m+n-d-1
.

If
\min_{[m]}(\mathcal{F})=\min(x_{1}+\cdots+x_{m}\>|\>x\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{F}))
, then vertices
v\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{F})\cap H
have
\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}\}
fixed (to be as minimal as possible), and
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{F})\cap H
equals a product of permutahedron on the index sets
A_{i}
’s, each with dimension
|A_{i}|-1
for
A_{i}\neq\varnothing
. Thus,

\dim(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{F})\cap H)=\sum_{A_{i}\neq\varnothing}|A_{i}|-1=\sum|A_{i}|-|\{i\>|\>A_{i}\neq\varnothing\}|=m+n-|\{i\>|\>A_{i}\neq\varnothing\}|.\qed

5.
Further Directions

In the work of Mallows and Vanderbei, they characterize the non-realizable
3\times n
tableaux for
3\leq n\leq 6
as well as the non-realizable
4\times 4
tableaux in terms of
taboo configurations

[
16
]
. These are minimal sets of inequalities between entries of a tableau that guarantee that the tableau is not realizable. Farkas’ lemma guarantees that for every non-realizable tableau, there is some minimal set of inequalities between linear combinations of the entries that demonstrates non-realizability. However, in the taboo configurations provided by Vanderbei and Mallows, it is enough to consider inequalities between two entries, with each entry appearing in at most one such inequality. We conjecture that this is always the case.

Conjecture 5.1
.

An
m\times n
tableau
M=\begin{bmatrix}M_{x}\end{bmatrix}_{x\in[m]\times[n]}
is not realizable if and only if there exist two disjoint sets of equal size
A=\{a_{1},\dots,a_{\ell}\},B=\{b_{1},\dots,b_{\ell}\}\subseteq[m]\times[n]
such that

(i)

M_{a_{k}}<M_{b_{k}}
for each
k\in\{1,\dots,\ell\}
,

(ii)

\left|A\cap\left(\{i\}\times[n]\right)\right|=\left|B\cap\left(\{i\}\times[n]\right)\right|
for each
i\in[m]
, and

(iii)

\left|A\cap\left([m]\times\{j\}\right)\right|=\left|B\cap\left([m]\times\{j\}\right)\right|
for each
j\in[n]
.

It is straightforward to prove that the above conditions are sufficient to show that a tableau is not realizable (see Proposition
2.2
). Thus the difficulty lies in showing whether these conditions are necessary for non-realizability.

In Subsection
2.3
, we provide an asymptotically tight lower bound on the number of realizable rectangular tableaux by lower bounding the number of single-row extensions of a fixed realizable rectangular tableau. In Proposition
2.10
, we give an exact formula for the number of single-row extensions of a realizable rectangular tableaux
\mathcal{T}(x\circ y)
, where
x
and
y
are fixed, that can be obtained appending an entry to
x
. This formula only depends on the size of the tableau and the sum of the entries in the last row. It would be interesting to perform a similar enumeration without fixing the vectors realizing the tableau.

Open Problem 5.2
.

Provide a formula for the number of a single-row extensions of a given realizable rectangular tableau.

We can generalize the notion of realizability to higher-dimensional Young tableaux and to functions other than outer sums. Consider an
d
-dimensional array having length
n
in each dimension with entries
a_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d}}
, along with a degree
D
polynomial
f\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}[z_{1},\dots,z_{d}]
. Given
x_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
for
j=1,\dots,d
, we define the entries of the array by
a_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d}}=f(x_{1,i_{1}},\dots,x_{d,i_{d}})
. When all the entries are unique, we can then obtain a
d
-dimensional standard Young tableau by replacing each entry with its relative position among the entries of the array, using the numbers
1
through
n^{d}
. We call such a tableau an
f
-realizable
n^{\times d}
(standard Young) tableau
. Taking
d=2
and
f=z_{1}+z_{2}
recovers the realizable
n\times n
tableaux. As a natural analogue to Corollary
1.4
, we can quickly show that almost all
n^{\times d}
standard Young tableaux are not realizable.

Proposition 5.3
.

For
d\geq 2
and a polynomial
f\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}[z_{1},\dots,z_{d}]
, the probability that an
n^{\times d}
tableau is
f
-realizable goes to
0
as
n
tends to infinity.

Proof.

By a straightforward adaptation of the methods in Subsection
2.2
, we can biject the
f
-realizable
n^{\times d}
tableaux with the regions of an arrangement formed by
\binom{\binom{n}{d}}{2}\leq n^{2d}
hyperplanes. By the Milnor-Thom theorem
[
17
,
20
]
(see Theorem
3.3
), the number of regions in this arrangement is at most

\left(\frac{4eDn^{2d}}{dn}\right)^{dn}=n^{(2d^{2}-d)n+o(n)}\,.

However, even just enumerating all possible
n\times n
subtableaux, we can see that there are at least
n^{\Omega(n^{2})}
total
n^{\times d}
standard Young tableaux.
∎

As the above proposition demonstrates, some of our methods extend to the generalized notion of
f
-realizable
n^{[d]}
tableaux. It would be interesting to obtain similar results about the asymptotic enumeration of realizable tableaux in this more general setting, possibly by using an adaptation of the existing techniques.

Open Problem 5.4
.

Prove tight asymptotic bounds on the number of
f
-realizable
n^{[d]}
tableaux.

Our notion of realizability for rectangular tableaux also has further generalizations. Namely, the set of matrices that arise from an outer sum are precisely the set of matrices of tropical rank
1
(see
[
10
]
). Our question of which tableaux are realizable is equivalent to asking which regions of the intersection of the tropical variety of rank
1
matrices with the braid arrangement are nonempty. In fact, one can see this as the dual expression of the zonotope corresponding to our hyperplane as a projection of the permutahedron. Such a projection always exists for monotone path polytopes of zonotopes
[
18
]
. A natural extension of our question is then to ask what occurs when we allow the rank to grow. Of course for matrices of full rank, all possible total orders of coordinates are attained, but what is the smallest rank for which that phenomenon occurs?

Open Problem 5.5
.

We call a rectangular tableau
r
-realizable if there exists a rank
r
matrix such that the total ordering of the entries of that matrix corresponds to the total ordering of the entries of the tableau. In terms of
m
and
n
, what is the minimal (tropical) rank
r(m,n)
such that all rectangular tableaux of shape
(m,n)
are
r(m,n)
-realizable?

Note that this question may depend on the chosen notion of tropical rank, since there are many that do not coincide in general tropical geometry but do coincide in the case of rank
1
. Furthermore, considering classical rank for matrices with positive entries would still be a proper generalization of what we do here, since applying log to each entry is an order preserving bijection taking rank
1
matrices with positive entries to tropical rank
1
matrices. That is we have the following corollary to our results:

Corollary 5.6
.

The number of possible orderings of coordinates of a rank
1

m\times n
matrix with positive entries is asymptotically
m^{(2+o(1))(n+m)}m!n!
, for
m\leq n
and both tending to infinity.

Another direction forward comes from the sorting networks perspective on our results. One advantage of the results of Angel, Gorin, and Holroyd in
[
1
]
for finding upper bounds on the number of realizable staircase tableaux is that they apply for other purely combinatorial notions of realizability. Namely, they show that if a single constant-size subconfiguration is forbidden for some subset of all staircase tableaux, then the probability a random staircase tableau of size
n
is in that subset tends to
0
exponentially fast as
n
approaches
\infty
. It would be interesting to have an analogous result for rectangular or square tableaux. Our results suggest that such a result could exist even for
3\times n
tableaux.

Similarly, realizable sorting networks play a vital role in the analysis of random staircase tableaux. In particular, Angel, Holroyd, Romik, and Virag observe in Theorem
5
of
[
2
]
that a random sorting network is approximated arbitrarily well by a realizable sorting network, and Dauvergne sharpened this result in Theorem
4
of
[
9
]
. We conjecture that there should exist an analogous statement saying that a random rectangular tableau may be approximated by a realizable rectangular tableau for the notion of realizability we study here.
