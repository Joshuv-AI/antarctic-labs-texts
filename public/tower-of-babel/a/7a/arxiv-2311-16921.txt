Comparing intrusive and non-intrusive polynomial chaos for a class of exponential time differencing schemes
Julian Clausnitzer, Andreas Kleefeld

Abstract
We consider the numerical approximation of different ordinary differential equations (ODEs) and partial differential equations (PDEs) with periodic boundary conditions involving a one-dimensional random parameter, comparing the intrusive and non-intrusive polynomial chaos expansion (PCE) method. We demonstrate how to modify two schemes for intrusive PCE (iPCE) which are highly efficient in solving nonlinear reaction-diffusion equations: A second-order exponential time differencing scheme (ETD-RDP-IF) as well as a spectral exponential time differencing fourth-order Runge-Kutta scheme (ETDRK4). In numerical experiments, we show that these schemes show superior accuracy to simpler schemes such as the EE scheme for a range of model equations and we investigate whether they are competitive with non-intrusive PCE (niPCE) methods. We observe that the iPCE schemes are competitive with niPCE for some model equations, but that iPCE breaks down for complex pattern formation models such as the Gray-Scott system.

Introduction.

In this work, we aim to solve time-dependent random differential equations of the form

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\frac{\partial u(x,t,\omega)}{\partial t}&=D\Delta u(x,t,\omega)+F(\omega,u(x,t,\omega)),\\
u(x,0,\omega)&=u_{\mathrm{init}}(x),\end{cases}

Using PCE representations to approximate random processes has been investigated in stochastic mechanics
[

and computational fluid dynamics (CFD)
[

21
,
23
]
for problems involving random parameters. With the usage of Wiener-Askey polynomial chaos
[

, it became possible to not only efficiently treat Gaussian inputs, but other input distributions as well. However, especially intrusive PCE (iPCE), which relies on inserting the PCE into the equation such as (
1

[
8
, p. 1]
and
[

). This has also been noted for unsteady flow problems in CFD
[

,
11
,
22
]

Since numerical solvers for deterministic problems have to be modified considerably in order to be used for iPCE, a relevant question is to what extent the benefits of these solvers carry over to the iPCE case. We consider a few model equations with a diffusion term and a nonlinear term, and we also look at a reaction-diffusion equation, the Gray-Scott model. While there are many numerical methods to solve reaction-diffusion equations (see
[
3
,
30
,
32
,
35
]
), in this paper we consider the following two numerical schemes: Firstly, a second-order finite difference exponential time differencing scheme which approximates matrix exponentials using rational functions having real distinct poles combined with an integrating factor approach (ETD-RDP-IF)
[
1
]
. Secondly, we look at a spectral exponential time differencing Runge-Kutta fourth order scheme (ETDRK4)
[
18

. The performances of these schemes are compared along with a simple explicit Euler (EE) scheme. We show that the iPCE schemes based on ETD-RDP-IF and ETDRK4 are competitive with their niPCE counterparts, but that more complex models such as the Gray-Scott model cannot be treated with the iPCE implementation presented here.

The contribution of this paper is a development and implementation of the iPCE method using the ETD-RDP-IF and ETDRK4 algorithms and a thorough quantitative investigation of the errors. The iPCE errors are compared to errors produced by Monte Carlo simulations and Gaussian Quadrature which represent niPCE simulations.

3.1.3
ETDRK4

The ETDRK4 scheme has been shown in

18

For its spatial discretization, it uses a spectral approach, making use of the discrete Fourier transform (DFT) which can be implemented using the well-known fast Fourier transform (FFT). Using the shorthand

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{j}=\sum_{n=0}^{p-1}\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}jx_{n}}\boldsymbol{u}_{n},\quad j=-\frac{p}{2}+1,\dots,\frac{p}{2},\quad\boldsymbol{u}_{n}=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{j=-p/2+1}^{p/2}\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}jx_{n}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{j},\quad n=0,\dots,p-1.

3.2
Non-intrusive PCE

The idea of non-intrusive PCE is to compute the coefficient functions
u_{i}
in (
2
) as numerical integrals

\displaystyle u_{i}(x,t)=\frac{1}{\langle P_{i}^{2}\rangle}\int_{a}^{b}u(x,t,\xi)P_{i}(\xi)\rho(\xi)\;\mathrm{d}\xi\approx\sum_{j=1}^{q}w_{j}u(x,t,\xi_{j})P_{i}(\xi_{j})\rho(\xi_{j}),

(16)

(with
\langle P_{i}^{2}\rangle=1
, see p. 2) where the integral in (
16
) is a Banach space-valued integral (for example a Bochner integral, see
[
9
, Appendix C]
) and the points
\xi_{j}
and weights
w_{j}
,
j=1,\dots,q
are chosen according to the used method. We will compare three different methods:

•

For the classical Monte Carlo (MC) method,
w_{j}=q^{-1}
and
\xi_{j}
are sampled randomly from the distribution of the random variable
\xi
.

•

For a quasi-Monte Carlo (QMC) method,
w_{j}=q^{-1}
and
\xi_{j}
are drawn from low-discrepancy sequences such as a Sobol sequence (see
[
31
]
) or Halton sequences (see
[
14
]
). These sequences cover the parameter space
[a,b]
more evenly and generally achieve a convergence order of almost one instead of
1/2
for classical MC (see
[
15
]
and
[
25
, p. 980]
).

•

For Gaussian quadrature (GQ), the
\xi_{j}
are quadrature nodes given by the roots of
P_{q}
and the quadrature weights are given by

\displaystyle w_{j}=\frac{a_{q}}{a_{q-1}}\frac{\langle P_{q-1},P_{q-1}\rangle}{P_{q}^{\prime}(x_{j})P_{q-1}(x_{j})},\quad j=1,\dots,q,

where
a_{k}
is the coefficient of
x^{k}
in
P_{q}
. An efficient and numerically stable way to compute the quadrature nodes and weights is given by the Golub-Welsch algorithm (see
[
13
]
and
[
28
, p. 188]
).

We have already given an introduction to intrusive PCE in Section

For both schemes, it is a necessary prerequisite to discuss the gPCE for the term
u^{3}
given the PCE of

. Given the gPCE (

\displaystyle u^{3}(x,t,\omega)

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}u_{i}(x,t)P_{i}(\xi)\right)\cdot\left(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}u_{j}(x,t)P_{j}(\xi)\right)\cdot\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}u_{k}(x,t)P_{k}(\xi)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}u_{i}(x,t)u_{j}(x,t)u_{k}(x,t)P_{i}(\xi)P_{j}(\xi)P_{k}(\xi).

After a Galerkin projection (multiplying with
P_{\eta}
for
\eta\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and taking

N+1

(see also
[

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}u_{\eta}(x,t)=D\Delta u_{\eta}(x,t)+\mathbb{E}\left[K\sum_{i,j,k=0}^{N}u_{i}(x,t)u_{j}(x,t)u_{k}(x,t)P_{i}(\xi)P_{j}(\xi)P_{k}(\xi)P_{\eta}(\xi)\right]\\
u_{0}(x,0)=u_{\mathrm{init}}(x),\quad u_{\eta}(x,0)=0,\ \eta>0.\end{cases}

Extracting the deterministic coefficient functions, we have

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}u_{\eta}(x,t)=D\Delta u_{\eta}(x,t)+\sum_{i,j,k=0}^{N}u_{i}(x,t)u_{j}(x,t)u_{k}(x,t)\mathbb{E}\left[KP_{i}(\xi)P_{j}(\xi)P_{k}(\xi)P_{\eta}(\xi)\right]\\
u_{0}(x,0)=u_{\mathrm{init}}(x),\quad u_{\eta}(x,0)=0,\ \eta>0.\end{cases}

and, using from now on the notation

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}u_{\eta}(x,t)=D\Delta u_{\eta}(x,t)+\sum_{i,j,k=0}^{N}\boldsymbol{K}_{ijk\eta}u_{i}(x,t)u_{j}(x,t)u_{k}(x,t)\\
u_{0}(x,0)=u_{\mathrm{init}}(x),\quad u_{\eta}(x,0)=0,\ \eta>0.\end{cases}

(17)

The tensor
\boldsymbol{K}
will be computed as a pre-processing step and used throughout a simulation.

Remark 3.7
.

If the random input
\boldsymbol{\xi}
is multivariate with dimension

, the underlying base of orthogonal polynomial has, for up to degree

\hat{N}+1:=\frac{(N+P)!}{N!P!}

(where

denotes the number of expansion terms corresponding to non-constant polynomials). This means that the system (

that especially equations involving a

On the other hand, suppose that a one-dimensional quadrature formula (such as Gauss-Legendre) has an error bound for
f\in\mathcal{C}^{r}([-1,1])
for
q\in\mathbb{N}
quadrature nodes

\displaystyle|E_{q}^{1}f|=\mathcal{O}(q^{-r}).

Then, it is known that in

dimensions, the full grid quadrature error
|\tilde{E}_{q}^{d}f|
and the error for Smolyak sparse quadrature
|E_{q}^{d}f|

for

is given by

\displaystyle|\tilde{E}_{q}^{d}f|=\mathcal{O}(q^{-r/d}),\quad|E_{q}^{d}f|=\mathcal{O}(\log(q)^{(d-1)(r+1)}q^{-r})

(see e.g.
[
17
, p. 39]
).
This means that in higher dimensions, provided that the integrand is sufficiently smooth, the number of necessary function evaluations can be drastically reduced by using a sparse grid, and the curse of dimensionality is much less severe than in the iPCE case as outlined above.

We now discuss the application of ETD-RDP-IF in the case where the spatial dimension is greater than one. For simplicity of the presentation, we restrict ourselves here to the case of two spatial dimensions. In this case, the finite difference discretization of the Laplacian with periodic boundary conditions is given by
A=A_{1}+A_{2}:=(D\cdot I_{p})\otimes A_{p}+A_{p}\otimes(D\cdot I_{p})
. We use the notation
\boldsymbol{U}^{n}=(\boldsymbol{u}_{0},\dots,\boldsymbol{u}_{N})^{\top}
from above and denote

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}:=I_{N+1}\otimes A,\quad\boldsymbol{A}_{1}:=I_{N+1}\otimes A_{1},\quad\boldsymbol{A}_{2}:=I_{N+1}\otimes A_{2},\quad\boldsymbol{I}:=I_{N+1}\otimes I_{p^{2}}=I_{p^{2}\cdot(N+1)},

so in particular it is
\boldsymbol{A}=\boldsymbol{A}_{1}+\boldsymbol{A}_{2}
. In order to solve the discretized PCE system

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{U}}{\partial t}+\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{U}=\boldsymbol{F}(\boldsymbol{U}),\quad\boldsymbol{u}_{0}^{0}=\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{init}},

(26)

with
\boldsymbol{F}
as above, we apply a dimensional splitting technique. We introduce the new time-dependent function
\boldsymbol{V}=\mathrm{e}^{\boldsymbol{A}_{1}t}\boldsymbol{U}
. The term
I_{N+1}\otimes\mathrm{e}^{\boldsymbol{A}_{1}t}
is called the integrating factor for PCE. We will also use that
\boldsymbol{A}
and
\boldsymbol{A}_{1}
commute (see
[
1

), and therefore also
\boldsymbol{A}
and
\mathrm{e}^{\boldsymbol{A}_{1}t}
commute. For the dimensional splitting, we define
\boldsymbol{V}(t):=\mathrm{e}^{\boldsymbol{A}_{1}t}\boldsymbol{U}(t)
. Carrying out the steps as in
[
1
, pp. 3]
for the PCE case, the time derivative of
\boldsymbol{V}
is

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{V}}{\partial t}=\mathrm{e}^{\boldsymbol{A}_{1}t}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{U}}{\partial t}+\boldsymbol{A}_{1}\mathrm{e}^{\boldsymbol{A}_{1}t}\boldsymbol{U}

and inserting (
26
), we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{V}}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{\boldsymbol{A}_{1}t}(\boldsymbol{F}(\boldsymbol{U})-\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{U})+\boldsymbol{A}_{1}\mathrm{e}^{\boldsymbol{A}_{1}t}\boldsymbol{U}=\mathrm{e}^{\boldsymbol{A}_{1}t}\boldsymbol{F}(\boldsymbol{U})-\mathrm{e}^{\boldsymbol{A}_{1}t}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{U}+\boldsymbol{A}_{1}\mathrm{e}^{\boldsymbol{A}_{1}t}\boldsymbol{U}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{\boldsymbol{A}_{1}t}\boldsymbol{F}(\boldsymbol{U})-\boldsymbol{A}\mathrm{e}^{\boldsymbol{A}_{1}t}\boldsymbol{U}+\boldsymbol{A}_{1}\mathrm{e}^{\boldsymbol{A}_{1}t}\boldsymbol{U}=\mathrm{e}^{\boldsymbol{A}_{1}t}\boldsymbol{F}(\boldsymbol{U})-\boldsymbol{A}_{2}\mathrm{e}^{\boldsymbol{A}_{1}t}\boldsymbol{U}=\mathrm{e}^{\boldsymbol{A}_{1}t}\boldsymbol{F}(\mathrm{e}^{-\boldsymbol{A}_{1}t}\boldsymbol{V})-\boldsymbol{A}_{2}\boldsymbol{V},

so the system

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{V}}{\partial t}+\boldsymbol{A}_{2}\boldsymbol{V}=\boldsymbol{G}(\boldsymbol{V}),\quad\boldsymbol{v}_{0}^{0}=\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{init}}

with
\boldsymbol{G}(\boldsymbol{V}):=\mathrm{e}^{\boldsymbol{A}_{1}t}\boldsymbol{F}(\mathrm{e}^{-\boldsymbol{A}_{1}t}\boldsymbol{V})
must be solved. The next step is to apply the ETD-RDP scheme given in (
25
) to
\boldsymbol{V}
. The derivation in the PCE case works analogously as described in
[
1
, p. 5–7]
. In addition to the ETD-RDP scheme (
25
), the unwinding of the integrating factor is needed. With the substitutions
\boldsymbol{V}^{n}=\mathrm{e}^{\boldsymbol{A}_{2}nk}\boldsymbol{U}^{n}
,
\boldsymbol{V}^{n+1}=\mathrm{e}^{\boldsymbol{A}_{2}nk}\mathrm{e}^{\boldsymbol{A}_{2}k}\boldsymbol{U}^{n+1}
,
\boldsymbol{G}(\boldsymbol{V}^{n})=\mathrm{e}^{\boldsymbol{A}_{2}nk}\boldsymbol{F}(\boldsymbol{U}^{n})
and
\boldsymbol{G}(\boldsymbol{V}^{n+1})=\mathrm{e}^{\boldsymbol{A}_{2}nk}\mathrm{e}^{\boldsymbol{A}_{2}k}\boldsymbol{F}(\boldsymbol{U}^{n+1})

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{U}^{n+1}

\displaystyle=\left(\boldsymbol{I}+\frac{k}{3}D\boldsymbol{A}_{2}\right)^{-1}\left[\left\{9\left(\boldsymbol{I}+\frac{k}{3}D\boldsymbol{A}_{1}\right)^{-1}-8\left(\boldsymbol{I}+\frac{k}{4}D\boldsymbol{A}_{1}\right)^{-1}\right\}\right.

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{b-a}\int_{a}^{b}\exp(-(\xi+D\pi^{2})t)\cos(\pi x)\;\mathrm{d}\xi

(29)

and in our tests we choose again

and

.
The variance is

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}[u(x,t,\cdot)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[u(x,t,\cdot)^{2}]-\mathbb{E}[u(x,t,\cdot)]^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{b-a}\int_{a}^{b}\exp(-2(\xi+D\pi^{2})t)\cos^{2}(\pi x)\;\mathrm{d}\xi-\mathbb{E}[u(x,t,\cdot)]^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{b-a}\frac{\cos^{2}(\pi x)}{2t}\left[\exp(-2(D\pi^{2}+a)t)-\exp(-2(D\pi^{2}+b)t)\right]

\displaystyle-\left[\frac{1}{b-a}\frac{\cos(\pi x)}{t}\exp(-(D\pi^{2}+a)t)-\exp(-(D\pi^{2}+b)t)\right]^{2}.

(30)

4.6
Random Gray-Scott model

We consider the random Gray-Scott model

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\frac{\partial u(x,t,\omega)}{\partial t}=D_{u}\Delta u(x,t,\omega)-u(x,t,\omega)v(x,t,\omega)^{2}+F(1-u(x,t,\omega)),\\
\frac{\partial v(x,t,\omega)}{\partial t}=D_{v}\Delta v(x,t,\omega)+u(x,t,\omega)v(x,t,\omega)^{2}-(F+k(\omega))v(x,t,\omega).\end{cases}

(36)

for
x\in(-1,1)
,
t\in(0,T]
for some
T>0
,
D_{u}=2\cdot 10^{-5}
,
D_{v}=10^{-5}
, a constant
F
and a uniformly distributed random variable
k
. We take the initial condition from
[
10
, Figure 9]
which is given by

\displaystyle u_{\mathrm{init}}(x)=1-\frac{5}{3\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\left(-6(x-\mu)^{2}\right),\quad v_{\mathrm{init}}(x)=0.37\cdot\frac{7.5}{2\sqrt{2}\Gamma(\frac{1}{3})}\exp\left(\frac{-7(x-\mu)^{3}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)

where
\mu=0
is the midpoint of the interval. For the two-dimensional plots shown in Figures
12
and
13
, we use as an initial condition a function with four off-center local extrema:

\displaystyle v(x,y,0)=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i=1}^{4}\exp\left(-150((x-x_{i})^{2}+(y-y_{i})^{2})\right),\quad u(x,y,0)=1-v(x,y,0),

(37)

where
(x_{1},y_{1})=(2/7,2/7)
,
(x_{2},y_{2})=(-2/7,2/7)
,
(x_{3},y_{3})=(2/7,-2/7)
,
(x_{4},y_{4})=(-2/7,-2/7)
.

Remark 4.1
.

The Gray-Scott model has been extensively studied and is known to show a wide variety of pattern formation behavior. The patterns are called Turing patterns, for an overview we refer to
[
26
,
27
]
. Depending on the parameter values of
k
and
F
,
u
and
v
may enter trivial or non-trivial homogeneous steady states, or non-homogeneous states called Turing patterns. A necessary condition in the
k
-
F
parameter space in order for Turing pattern formation to occur is
[
24
, Eq. (19)]

\displaystyle[2(F+k)-(v_{0}^{2}+F)]^{2}>8(F+k)(v_{0}^{2}-F),

(38)

where
v_{0}
is either the trivial steady state (the ‘red state’)
v_{R}=0
or, if
d:=1-4(F+k)^{2}/F>0
holds, one of the two non-trivial steady states
[
24
, Eqs. (7), (8)]
(with
v_{B}
also called the ‘blue state’)

\displaystyle v_{B}=\frac{1}{2}\alpha(1+\sqrt{d}),\quad v_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\alpha(1-\sqrt{d}).

Since now there are two coupled functions instead of one, the schemes need to take into account these two functions, but for the sake of brevity we will not spell out all the schemes again, but refer to
[
1
,
18
]
(the intrusive PCE schemes given in Section
3.3
can then easily be extended to two functions).

We pick for all simulations (1D and 2D)
F=0.04
and
k(\omega)\sim\mathcal{U}[0.058,0.062]
, which falls into the region of complex pattern formation described by (
38
). It is seen in Figure
11
that the iPCE schemes fail to compute correct solutions for the Gray-Scott model. Furthermore, the needed time step numbers in order for the iPCE schemes are even higher than for Equation
33
with a single cubic term. This leaves niPCE as the only viable option to treat the random Gray-Scott system. We show an example of such a simulation in two spatial dimensions for a mean
\mathbb{E}[u(x,t,\cdot)]
in Figure
13
with the correct result produced by niPCE juxtaposed by iPCE simulations which fail to reproduce the pattern. This is likely due to the challenge of sharp dependency in the random variable, as in the seemingly small interval
k\in[0.058,0.062]
, a wide range of patterns emerge (see also
[
27
]
). Furthermore, long-term integration of the Gray-Scott system is a challenge even in the deterministic case, as show in Figure
12
for
T=5000
, where it is seen that a high level of accuracy in both space and time is needed in order to produce the correct pattern shown on the bottom right. Especially in the cases of EE and ETD-RDP-IF, for
p=256
the error of the second-order spatial finite difference approximation appears to dominate, causing the pattern to look rather different.

Given the difficulties of long-term integration for the Gray-Scott model and the numerical challenges and substantially increased computation time for iPCE, it appears to be advisable to use niPCE for systems with strong nonlinearities such as Gray-Scott.

5
Conclusion and outlook.

In this work, we investigated how the ETD-RDP-IF and ETDRK4 schemes can be implemented in an iPCE scheme and compared the performance of these two schemes and an EE scheme to a niPCE approach. While niPCE using Monte Carlo methods or Gaussian quadrature is in most cases superior to iPCE, we also found that in some cases such as the model equation with a quadratic term, iPCE results in lower errors for comparable runtimes. For complex pattern formation dynamics such as the Gray-Scott model, iPCE breaks down for all three schemes which leaves only the non-intrusive variance as a viable option. While not implemented in full detail, it is also apparent that the curse of dimensionality poses a bigger problem to iPCE than it does to niPCE since solving bigger iPCE systems scales much worse than using sparse grids for niPCE.

Future work could include methods to remedy the shortcomings of iPCE for complex dynamical systems. In this work, it was seen that a direct iPCE implementation of algorithms which are powerful in the deterministic case is not sufficient. Works such as
[
2
]
have introduced an asynchronous time integration method for iPCE to deal with sharp dependencies in the random variable. Recent work
[
8
]
also shows that B-splines can be used instead of classical orthogonal polynomial bases to further reduce the error. One possibility for achieving speedups for the ETD-RDP-IF scheme is the use of parallelization as has been demonstrated in
[
1
, pp. 8]
. It could be carried out in an analogous fashion for the iPCE scheme.

Another topic which could be investigated are non-polynomial nonlinear functions, which can be handled using truncations of Taylor series
[
6
]
