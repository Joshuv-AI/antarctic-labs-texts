A perturbation theory for the Coulomb phase infrared-divergence
Luke Lippstreu

Abstract
We construct a perturbation theory which we conjecture to be free of the Coulomb-phase infrared divergence. This perturbation theory is developed for one of the simplest yet prototypical scattering amplitudes which would otherwise exhibit this divergence: the semiclassical scattering of a spinless boson on a background Coulomb field. The perturbation theory is based upon replacing plane waves with Coulomb wavefunctions, and the free-field propagator with the Coulomb propagator, in order to appropriately match the asymptotics of the exact in/out states. We compute the leading-order (LO) and next-to-leading-order (NLO) infrared-finite scattering amplitudes in this framework, which include all-order-in-the-coupling effects, and demonstrate that these amplitudes are in agreement with the known exact amplitude at these orders. We comment on the Runge-Lenz symmetry of the LO amplitude which occurs as a principal series representation of the Euclidean conformal group on the 2 2 -sphere.

Introduction

Infrared divergences occur in interacting theories that contain massless particles in four or fewer spacetime dimensions, and are thus of considerable importance for the Standard Model of particle physics. These divergences can be alleviated on the level of inclusive cross-sections for sufficiently simple theories like QED
Bloch:1937pw
. However, in order to study questions regarding causality and unitarity it is preferable to alleviate these divergences on the level of amplitudes, ideally without introducing an IR-cutoff. The Faddeev-Kulish (FK) framework

;
Dollard1964AsymptoticCA
;
Kibble:1968sfb
;
Chung:1965zza
represents one solution to the problem of constructing an IR-finite S-matrix, and while it has been useful for uncovering many features of the infrared structure of gauge theories and gravity, it has rarely been used for the explicit calculation of IR-finite scattering amplitudes, with
Hannesdottir:2019opa
;
Hannesdottir:2019umk
;
Forde:2003jt
being the only instances of explicitly calculated amplitudes we were able to find.

One of the simplest examples of an infrared divergence is encountered when using standard techniques to compute the semiclassical scattering of a Klein-Gordon (KG) particle off a Coulomb background
kang1962higher
. In this paper, we construct a perturbation theory, conjectured to be IR-finite, for this semiclassical amplitude and calculate it up to the NLO. The LO (
69
) and NLO (
87

91
) is known by other non-perturbative means, and we find agreement with this result up to the orders we compute, thus providing confidence for extending the perturbation theory to less trivial settings where the exact answer is not known.

The underlying cause of infrared divergences, both in the semiclassical and the quantum cases, is that states in such theories do not appropriately converge towards non-interacting free particle states at asymptotic times, which is counter to an assumption of standard perturbation theory. This does not imply that scattering amplitudes are ambiguous, zero, infinite, or cut-off dependent, it only implies that one should not use a perturbation theory that assumes asymptotically non-interacting states. In the semiclassical case, it is this assumption regarding asymptotically non-interacting particles which leads to the use of plane-waves as a useful basis of wavefunctions for perturbatively constructing the amplitude. In the presence of long-range forces it would be preferable to use a basis of wavefunctions whose asymptotics appropriately match those of the exact in/out states. In this paper we develop a perturbation theory which uses a basis of wavefunctions, Coulomb wavefunctions, whose asymptotic behaviour appropriately matches those of the exact in/out states, namely the perturbation theory implements the replacement

2.2
On the definition of the Coulomb scattering amplitude

Different textbooks report different expressions for the Coulomb scattering amplitude, which may give the impression that the probability amplitude is ambiguous. For example, Landau and Lifshitz
Landau:1991wop
give the amplitude as (
13
), while Weinberg

instead gives

\displaystyle-\frac{i\pi}{2}\frac{e_{1}e_{2}}{|\vec{p}|^{2}}\delta(E_{1}-E_{2})\frac{\Gamma(1+i\gamma)}{\Gamma(1-i\gamma)}\frac{1}{\sin^{2}(\theta/2)},

(15)

which omits the phase factor
e^{-i\gamma\log\sin^{2}\theta/2}

Taylor:1972pty
)

\displaystyle f(E,\hat{p}\cdot\hat{p}^{\prime})=-(2\pi)^{2}m\mathcal{T}(\vec{p}\rightarrow\vec{p}\,^{\prime}).

(16)

The terms in (
16
) are defined as follows. For a short-range potential, the in-state wavefunction has a large-distance expansion of the form

\displaystyle\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\psi^{\text{in}}_{\vec{p}}(\vec{x})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{\frac{3}{2}}}\Big(e^{i\vec{p}\cdot\vec{x}}+f(E,\hat{p}\cdot\hat{x})\frac{e^{ipr}}{r}+\mathcal{O}(1/r^{2})\Big)

(17)

which defines the quantity
f(E,\hat{p}\cdot\hat{x})
. Next, we define
\mathcal{T}
. For short-range potentials, the inner product of the in-state with the out-state takes the form

\displaystyle\braket{p^{\prime}_{\text{out}}|p_{\text{in}}}=\delta^{(3)}(\vec{p}-\vec{p}\,^{\prime})-2\pi i\delta(E_{p}-E_{p^{\prime}})\mathcal{T}(\vec{p}\to\vec{p}\,^{\prime}).

(18)

On the left-hand side, we have the inner product of the in-state with the out-state, which in non-relativistic quantum mechanics is computed using
\int d^{3}\vec{x}
. On the right-hand side, the first term represents a disconnected contribution, while the second term, after factoring out energy conservation, defines
\mathcal{T}(\vec{p}\rightarrow\vec{p}\,^{\prime})
.

We now have all the necessary definitions to understand theorem (
16
): the connected part of the inner product of the in-state with the out-state can be obtained directly from the large
|\vec{x}|
expansion of the in-state wavefunction, as specified in the theorem.

We can now explain why different textbooks report different results for the Coulomb amplitude. Unlike short-range potentials, the in/out states of the Coulomb potential do not allow for a large
|\vec{x}|
expansion of the form (
17
), meaning that theorem (
16
) does not apply—compare this to (
10
) for the true large
|\vec{x}|
expansion. To our knowledge, no analog of this theorem has been established for long-range potentials. Nevertheless, textbooks take inspiration from theorem (
16
), which, as we have noted, does not hold in this case, to
define
the Coulomb scattering amplitude as part of the coefficient of
\frac{e^{ipr}}{r}
in the large
|\vec{x}|
expansion of the in-state (
10
). Different choices for what factor to extract lead to the different definitions for the Coulomb amplitude found in Weinberg and Landau & Lifshitz.

However, there is no ambiguity in the probability amplitude. It is a fundamental axiom of quantum mechanics that probability amplitudes are given by inner products (Chapter 2
Weinberg:1995mt
). Since the exact in- and out-state wavefunctions are known, there is no need to rely on any theorem to compute the probability amplitude indirectly. The probability amplitude is unambiguously given by the
\int d^{3}\vec{x}
inner product of the in-state with the out-state, as in (
13
).

Conclusions

We have demonstrated that by using a basis of wavefunctions, namely in/out Coulomb wavefunctions, whose asymptotics appropriately match, in the precise sense of (
19

the

We note that any choice of basis wavefunctions whose asymptotics appropriately match those of the exact in/out states should also yield an IR-finite perturbative framework for this semiclassical amplitude. In this paper we have only explored one such choice. A natural choice, and one worth exploring, is the relativistic analog of the first term on the RHS of (

barrachina1989scattering
;
Kadyrov_2005

19
), whereas the latter modifies the states.

Let us briefly outline which aspects of the perturbation theory we expect to be relevant when going to more realistic settings like QED where the fields are quantized and the gauge field is no longer a fixed background, but dynamical. Incorporating the spin of the electron is straightforward as the solution to “semi-free” Dirac equation is well-known
Berestetskii1982118
. We expect the early time dynamics of the exact in-states in QED to differ from free particle states in two respects: firstly, charged particles move in the fixed Coulomb background of all the other charged particles, and secondly there is a cloud of soft photons surrounding pairs of charged particles. If only the former difference occurred, then the Coulomb wavefunction perturbation theory would carry over straightforwardly to QED. However, the extent to which these two processes interfere with one another makes it not immediately apparent whether Coulomb wavefunctions
per se
are the appropriate wavefunctions to use in QED and is a topic for future research.

In summary, standard perturbation theory views a scattering event as free-particles meeting at vertices transitioning to other free-particles, while Coulomb wavefunction perturbation theory views a scattering process as Coulomb states meeting at vertices transitioning to other Coulomb states.

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2|\vec{p}|}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}i^{l}(2l+1)e^{\pm i\delta_{l}}R_{l}(|\vec{p}|,r)P_{l}(\hat{x}\cdot\hat{p})

(134)

\displaystyle R_{l}(|\vec{p}|,r)

\displaystyle=2|\vec{p}|e^{-\frac{\pi\gamma}{2}}\frac{|\Gamma(1+l+i\gamma)|}{(2l+1)!}(2|\vec{p}|r)^{l}e^{ipr}{}_{1}F_{1}(1+l+i\gamma,2l+2,-2ipr)

(135)

\displaystyle e^{2i\delta_{l}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(1+l+i\gamma)}{\Gamma(1+l-i\gamma)},

(136)

where the phase factors
e^{+i\delta_{l}}
and
e^{-i\delta_{l}}
correspond to the in-state and out-state, respectively, in (
134

134

messiah1999quantum
. The
\mathrm{d}^{3}x
inner product between the in-state and out-state can then be computed directly using

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\Omega P_{l}(\hat{p}_{1}\cdot\hat{x})P_{l^{\prime}}(\hat{p}_{2}\cdot\hat{x})=\delta_{ll^{\prime}}\frac{4\pi}{2l+1}P_{l}(\hat{p}_{1}\cdot\hat{p}_{2}),

(137)

to evaluate the angular integral. We can then use the orthonormality of the radial wavefunctions (

Landau:1991wop

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}r\,r^{2}R_{l}(|\vec{p}_{1}|,r)R_{l}(|\vec{p}_{2}|,r)=2\pi\delta(|\vec{p}_{1}|-|\vec{p}_{2}|).

(138)

Altogether one obtains

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}^{3}x\,\phi^{\star}_{\text{out}}(\vec{x},\vec{p}_{2})\phi_{\text{in}}(\vec{x},\vec{p}_{1})

\displaystyle=\delta(|\vec{p}_{1}|-|\vec{p}_{2}|)\frac{2\pi^{2}}{|\vec{p}_{1}|^{2}}\sum_{l}(2l+1)\frac{\Gamma(1+l+i\gamma)}{\Gamma(1+l-i\gamma)}P_{l}(\hat{p}_{1}\cdot\hat{p}_{2})

(139)

The sum over partial waves can be performed
mott1932polarisation
;
Lin_2000

\displaystyle\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}(2l+1)\frac{\Gamma(1+l+i\gamma)}{\Gamma(1+l-i\gamma)}P_{l}(\hat{p}_{1}\cdot\hat{p}_{2})=-i\gamma\lim_{\epsilon_{+}\rightarrow 0}\frac{\Gamma(1+i\gamma)}{\Gamma(1-i\gamma)}\Bigg(\frac{2}{(1-\hat{p}_{1}\cdot\hat{p}_{2})}\Bigg)^{1+i\gamma-\epsilon_{+}},

(140)

where the equality (
140

Taylor
;
herbst1974connectedness

\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\Omega_{\hat{p}_{1}\cdot\hat{p}_{2}}P_{l^{\prime}}(\hat{p}_{1}\cdot\hat{p}_{2})
and check that they produce the same result (see (
156
) for the relevant integral evaluation). The positive infinitesimal exponent in
\epsilon_{+}>0
was shown by Herbst
herbst1974connectedness
to be the unique distributional interpretation of the Coulomb amplitude consistent with unitarity when including the forward direction. A more detailed discussion of these points is provided in the next subsection.

Using (
140
) in (
139
), and converting the Dirac delta function’s argument to
E=\frac{|\vec{p}|^{2}}{2m}
, we conclude that

\displaystyle A^{\text{NRQM}}(\vec{p}_{1},\vec{p}_{2})

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}^{3}x\,\phi^{\star}_{\text{out}}(\vec{x},\vec{p}_{2})\phi_{\text{in}}(\vec{x},\vec{p}_{1})

(141)

\displaystyle=-\frac{i\pi}{2}\frac{e_{1}e_{2}}{|\vec{p}|^{2}}\delta(E_{1}-E_{2})\frac{\Gamma(1+i\gamma)}{\Gamma(1-i\gamma)}\Bigg(\frac{2}{(1-\hat{p}_{1}\cdot\hat{p}_{2})}\Bigg)^{1+i\gamma},\quad\hat{p}_{1}\cdot\hat{p}_{2}\neq 1

(142)

which can be seen to be equal to (
133

(1-\hat{p}_{1}\cdot\hat{p}_{2})=1-\cos\theta=2\sin^{2}\theta/2
.

The distributional nature of the amplitude

The distributional equality (
140
) is a subtle point, so we summarize the key arguments from
Taylor
;
herbst1974connectedness
. First, the large-
l
behavior of the left-hand side of (
140
),

\displaystyle\lim_{l\rightarrow\infty}e^{2i\delta_{l}}=l^{2i\gamma}\big(1+\mathcal{O}(1/l)\big),

(143)

indicates that the partial-wave sum diverges. However, as demonstrated in
Taylor
, this sum defines a perfectly well-behaved distribution that acts unambiguously and unitarily on any sufficiently smooth wavepacket. We now explain this in more detail.

Scattering amplitudes are distributions, so the fundamental requirement for an amplitude
A(p_{\text{out}},p_{\text{in}})
is that it provides a well-defined and unitary mapping from in-wavepackets
f_{\text{in}}(\vec{p}_{\text{in}})
to out-wavepackets
f_{\text{out}}(\vec{p}_{\text{out}})
:

\displaystyle f_{\text{out}}(\vec{p}_{\text{out}})=\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{3}p_{\text{in}}}{(2\pi)^{3}}A(p_{\text{out}},p_{\text{in}})f_{\text{in}}(\vec{p}_{\text{in}})

(144)

Let us consider an in-wavepacket that can be decomposed in the partial-wave basis,

\displaystyle f_{\text{in}}(\vec{p}_{\text{in}})=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}f^{\text{in}}_{l}(|\vec{p}_{\text{in}}|)P_{l}(\hat{p}_{\text{in}}\cdot\hat{z}),

(145)

where
f^{\text{in}}_{l}
are the partial-wave coefficients. For simplicity, we have assumed a wavepacket that is symmetric about the
\hat{z}
-axis, though this assumption is not essential.

If we take the amplitude to be (
139
)

\displaystyle A(\hat{p}_{\text{2}},\hat{p}_{\text{1}})=\delta(|\vec{p}_{1}|-|\vec{p}_{2}|)\frac{2\pi^{2}}{|\vec{p}_{1}|^{2}}\sum_{l}(2l+1)\frac{\Gamma(1+l+i\gamma)}{\Gamma(1+l-i\gamma)}P_{l}(\hat{p}_{1}\cdot\hat{p}_{2})

(146)

then we can examine the action of the amplitude on the in-wavepacket

\displaystyle\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{3}p_{\text{in}}}{(2\pi)^{3}}A(p_{\text{out}},p_{\text{in}})f_{\text{in}}(\vec{p}_{\text{in}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}f^{\text{in}}_{l}(|\vec{p}_{\text{in}}|)\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{3}p_{\text{in}}}{(2\pi)^{3}}A(p_{\text{out}},p_{\text{in}})P_{l}(\hat{p}_{\text{in}}\cdot\hat{z})

(147)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}f^{\text{in}}_{l}(|\vec{p}_{\text{in}}|)e^{2i\delta_{l}}P_{l}(\hat{p}_{\text{out}}\cdot\hat{z})

(148)

where in going to (
148
) we used (
137
). Thus, (
146
) defines a well-defined distribution acting on wavepackets, provided that the sum (
148
) converges. Furthermore, the action is unitary since each partial-wave coefficient
f_{l}^{\text{in}}
is only modified by a phase, ensuring that the norm of the wavepacket is preserved under the action of the amplitude.

Next, we need to show that the amplitude

\displaystyle\tilde{A}(\hat{p}_{2},\hat{p}_{1})=-i\gamma\lim_{\epsilon_{+}\rightarrow 0}\delta(|\vec{p}_{1}|-|\vec{p}_{2}|)\frac{2\pi^{2}}{|\vec{p}_{1}|^{2}}\frac{\Gamma(1+i\gamma)}{\Gamma(1-i\gamma)}\Bigg(\frac{2}{(1-\hat{p}_{1}\cdot\hat{p}_{2})}\Bigg)^{1+i\gamma-\epsilon_{+}}

(149)

has the exact same distributional action as (
148
). Acting on the in-wavepacket with (
149
),

\displaystyle\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{3}p_{\text{in}}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\tilde{A}(p_{\text{out}},p_{\text{in}})f_{\text{in}}(\vec{p}_{\text{in}})

\displaystyle=-\frac{i\gamma}{4\pi}\frac{\Gamma(1+i\gamma)}{\Gamma(1-i\gamma)}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\Omega_{\text{in}}\Bigg(\frac{2}{(1-\hat{p}_{\text{in}}\cdot\hat{p}_{\text{out}})}\Bigg)^{1+i\gamma-\epsilon_{+}}f_{\text{in}}(\vec{p}_{\text{in}})

(150)

\displaystyle=-\frac{i\gamma}{4\pi}\frac{\Gamma(1+i\gamma)}{\Gamma(1-i\gamma)}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\Omega_{\text{in}}\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}x\delta(x-\hat{p}_{\text{in}}\cdot\hat{p}_{\text{out}})\Bigg(\frac{2}{1-x}\Bigg)^{1+i\gamma-\epsilon_{+}}f_{\text{in}}(\vec{p}_{\text{in}})

(151)

\displaystyle=-\frac{i\gamma}{4\pi}\frac{\Gamma(1+i\gamma)}{\Gamma(1-i\gamma)}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\Omega_{\text{in}}\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}x\sum_{l^{\prime}}\frac{2l^{\prime}+1}{2}P_{l^{\prime}}(x)P_{l^{\prime}}(\hat{p}_{\text{in}}\cdot\hat{p}_{\text{out}})\Bigg(\frac{2}{1-x}\Bigg)^{1+i\gamma-\epsilon_{+}}f_{\text{in}}(\vec{p}_{\text{in}})

(152)

\displaystyle=-\frac{i\gamma}{2}\frac{\Gamma(1+i\gamma)}{\Gamma(1-i\gamma)}\sum_{l}f^{\text{in}}_{l}(|\vec{p}_{\text{in}}|)P_{l}(\hat{p}_{\text{out}}\cdot\hat{z})\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}x\Bigg(\frac{2}{1-x}\Bigg)^{1+i\gamma-\epsilon_{+}}P_{l}(x).

(153)

In going to (
152
), we used the identity

\displaystyle\delta(x-y)=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\frac{2l+1}{2}P_{l}(x)P_{l}(y),

(154)

which expresses the Dirac delta function as a sum over Legendre polynomials. In going to the equality (
153
), we used the partial-wave expansion of the in-wavepacket
f_{\text{in}}(\vec{p}_{\text{in}})
given in (
145
), and then applied (
137
) to perform the angular integral. Next we perform the remaining
x
-integral,

\displaystyle\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{3}p_{\text{in}}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\tilde{A}(p_{\text{out}},p_{\text{in}})f_{\text{in}}(\vec{p}_{\text{in}})

\displaystyle=-\frac{i\gamma}{2}\frac{\Gamma(1+i\gamma)}{\Gamma(1-i\gamma)}\sum_{l}f^{\text{in}}_{l}(|\vec{p}_{\text{in}}|)\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}x\Bigg(\frac{2}{1-x}\Bigg)^{1+i\gamma-\epsilon_{+}}P_{l}(x)

(155)

\displaystyle=-i\gamma\frac{\Gamma(1+i\gamma)}{\Gamma(1-i\gamma)}\sum_{l}f^{\text{in}}_{l}(|\vec{p}_{\text{in}}|)\frac{(-1)^{l}[\Gamma(-i\gamma+\epsilon)]^{2}}{\Gamma(1-i\gamma+l+\epsilon)\Gamma(-l-i\gamma+\epsilon)}

(156)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}f^{\text{in}}_{l}(|\vec{p}_{\text{in}}|)e^{2i\delta_{l}}P_{l}(\hat{p}_{\text{out}}\cdot\hat{z}).

(157)

In going to (
156
), we applied equation 7.127 from
gradshteyn2014table
. Next, in transitioning to (
157

\epsilon\to 0
and then used Euler’s reflection formula to simplify the expression. This confirms that (

157

The
\epsilon_{+}
exponent is an especially natural regulator in light of the fact that when one allows for soft-photon production the exponent of the amplitude would naturally acquire a finite dampening factor, namely the

C.4

Following Hostler

\displaystyle\delta(x^{0}-y^{0})\Bigg\{

Hostler considered the Green’s function in the partial wave basis, and furthermore considered the solutions of the full-SQED equations of motion. For ease of reference, and to adapt the proof to our conventions for the normalization of the states, we reproduce their proof with minor modifications here.

Assuming, without proof, that the continuum and bound state solutions form a complete basis for the solution space to the semi-free EQM, we have that any SQED normalizable solution to the semi-free EQM can be expressed in this basis
