Dynamic Spatiotemporal ARCH Models: Small and Large Sample Results
Philipp Otto, Osman Doğan, Süleyman Taşpınar

Abstract
This paper explores the estimation of a dynamic spatiotemporal autoregressive conditional heteroscedasticity (ARCH) model. The log-volatility term in this model can depend on (i) the spatial lag of the log-squared outcome variable, (ii) the time-lag of the log-squared outcome variable, (iii) the spatiotemporal lag of the log-squared outcome variable, (iv) exogenous variables, and (v) the unobserved heterogeneity across regions and time, i.e., the regional and time fixed effects. We examine the small and large sample properties of two quasi-maximum likelihood estimators and a generalized method of moments estimator for this model. We first summarize the theoretical properties of these estimators and then compare their finite sample properties through Monte Carlo simulations.



JEL-Classification: C11, C23, C58.

Keywords: Spatial ARCH, GMM, QMLE, volatility clustering, volatility, spatial dependence.

1
Introduction

This paper investigates the small and large sample properties of three estimators for dynamic spatiotemporal ARCH models suggested by
Otto et al., 2023
. This model allows the log-volatility term to depend on (i) the spatial lag of the log-squared outcome variable, (ii) the time-lag of the log-squared outcome variable, (iii) the spatiotemporal lag of the log-squared outcome variable, (iv) exogenous variables, and (v) the unobserved heterogeneity across regions and time, i.e., the regional and time fixed effects. The estimation equation of the model is obtained through a log-squared transformation
(
Robinson, 2009
;
Taşpınar et al., 2021
)
. Notably, while the estimation equation obtained is in the form of a standard spatial dynamic panel data model considered by
Yu et al., 2008
and
Lee and Yu, 2010
, it incorporates two important new features.

Firstly, the outcome variable, the spatial, temporal, and spatiotemporal lags are formulated using the log-squared original outcome variable. Secondly, the disturbance term in the model is the log-square of the original disturbance term due to the log-squared transformation. Following
Lee, 2007
and
Lee and Yu, 2014
,
Otto et al., 2023
consider a generalized method of moment (GMM) estimator based on a set of linear and quadratic moment functions. In this paper, we also consider two quasi-maximum likelihood (QML) estimators considered in
Yu et al., 2008
and
Lee and Yu, 2010
for the estimation of the model. The first estimator is based on a transformation approach requiring the estimation of regional fixed effects along with the other model parameters. The second estimator is based on a direct approach and necessitates the estimation of both regional and time fixed effects. We first compare the theoretical properties of these estimators and subsequently investigate their small-sample properties through Monte Carlo simulations.

The rest of the paper proceeds in the following way. In Section
3
, we describe the dynamic spatiotemporal ARCH model and show how it differs from a standard spatial dynamic panel data model. In Section
3
, we summarize the QML and the GMM estimation approaches and provide their large sample results. In Section
4
, we investigate the finite sample properties of both estimation approaches through Monte Carlo simulations. In Section
5
, we conclude and provide some directions for future studies.

3
Estimation Approaches

Although the elements of
\boldsymbol{\e}^{*}_{t}
in (
2.6
) are i.i.d across
\mathbf{s}_{i}
and
t
, we may have
\mathbb{E}\left(\boldsymbol{\e}^{*}_{t}\right)\neq\mathbf{0}
because of the log-squared transformation. Let
\mathbb{E}\left(\boldsymbol{\e}^{*}_{t}\right)=\mu_{\e}\mathbf{1}_{n}
, where
\mu_{\e}
is a scalar unknown parameter, and define
\mathbf{U}_{t}=(u_{t}(\mathbf{s}_{1}),\ldots,u_{t}(\mathbf{s}_{n}))^{{}^{\prime}}=\boldsymbol{\e}^{*}_{t}-\mathbb{E}\left(\boldsymbol{\e}^{*}_{t}\right)=\boldsymbol{\e}^{*}_{t}-\mu_{\e}\mathbf{1}_{n}
. Then, we can express the estimation equation as

\displaystyle\mathbf{Y}^{*}_{t}

\displaystyle=\rho_{0}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{Y}^{*}_{t}+\gamma_{0}\mathbf{Y}^{*}_{t-1}+\delta_{0}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{Y}^{*}_{t-1}+\mathbf{X}_{t}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}+\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0}+\alpha_{t0}\mathbf{1}_{n}+\mu_{\e}\mathbf{1}_{n}+\mathbf{U}_{t}.

(3.1)

Following the spatial econometric literature, we consider two estimation approaches for (
3.1
): (i) the QML method, and (ii) the GMM method. In both approaches, we assume that
\{u_{t}(\mathbf{s}_{i})\}
are i.i.d across
t
and
\mathbf{s}_{i}
with mean zero and variance
\sigma^{2}_{0}
.

3.1
The QML Methods

Following
Lee and Yu, 2010
, we consider two QML methods: (i) the transformation approach and (ii) the direct approach. In the case of the transformation approach, we will eliminate the time fixed effects from the model through a suitable transformation and then estimate the regional fixed effects along with the other parameters. In the direct approach, we will estimate both
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{0}
and
\mathbf{c}_{n0}=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0}+\mu_{\e}\mathbf{1}_{n}
simultaneously. The transformation approach is only applicable if
\mathbf{M}
is row-normalized, i.e., if
\mathbf{M}\mathbf{1}_{n}=\mathbf{1}_{n}
, while the direct approach does not require the row-normalization.

We start with the transformation approach and assume that

be the orthonormal matrix of eigenvectors of
\mathbf{J}_{n}
, where
\mathbf{F}_{n,n-1}
is the
n\times(n-1)
matrix of eigenvectors corresponding to the eigenvalues of ones.

holds, we have
\sqrt{nT)}(\widehat{\text{$\boldsymbol{\theta}$}}^{d}_{nT}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0})+\sqrt{c}\mathbf{b}_{1}+\sqrt{1/c}\mathbf{b}_{2}\xrightarrow{d}N\left(\mathbf{0},\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}+\boldsymbol{\Omega})\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\right)
, which indicates that we need a bias correction for
\widehat{\text{$\boldsymbol{\theta}$}}^{d}_{nT}
. Let
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{d1}_{nT}=\widehat{\text{$\boldsymbol{\theta}$}}^{d}_{nT}-\widehat{\text{$\mathbf{b}$}}_{1}/T-\widehat{\text{$\mathbf{b}$}}_{2}/T
be the bias corrected estimator, where
\widehat{\text{$\mathbf{b}$}}_{1}
and
\widehat{\text{$\mathbf{b}$}}_{2}
are the plug-in estimators of
\mathbf{b}_{1}
and
\mathbf{b}_{2}
, respectively. Then, it can be shown that if
\frac{n}{T^{3}}\to 0
and
\frac{T}{n^{3}}\to\infty
, then we have
\sqrt{nT)}(\widehat{\text{$\boldsymbol{\theta}$}}^{d1}_{nT}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0})\xrightarrow{d}N\left(\mathbf{0},\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}+\boldsymbol{\Omega})\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\right)
.

3.2
The GMM Method

In the GMM approach, we will use two different transformations to eliminate
\boldsymbol{\mu}
,
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
and
\mu_{\e}
from the model. The first transformation is based on the decomposition of
\mathbf{J}_{T}=\left(\mathbf{I}_{T}-\frac{1}{T}\mathbf{1}_{T}\mathbf{1}^{{}^{\prime}}_{T}\right)
, where
\mathbf{I}_{T}
is the
T\times T
identity matrix. Let
\left(\mathbf{F}_{T,T-1},\mathbf{1}_{T}/\sqrt{T}\right)
be the orthonormal eigenvector matrix of
\mathbf{J}_{T}
, where
\mathbf{F}_{T,T-1}
is the
T\times(T-1)
sub-matrix containing eigenvectors corresponding to the eigenvalues of one. Let
\mathbf{D}=\left(\mathbf{d}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{d}_{T}\right)
be an
n\times T
matrix, where
\mathbf{d}_{t}
is an
n\times 1
column vector for
t=1,\ldots,T
. Using
\mathbf{F}_{T,T-1}
, we can transform
\mathbf{D}
into an
n\times(T-1)
matrix in the following way:
\mathbf{D}^{*}=\left(\mathbf{d}^{*}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{d}^{*}_{T-1}\right)=\left(\mathbf{d}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{d}_{T}\right)\mathbf{F}_{T,T-1}
. Since
\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0}\right)\mathbf{F}_{T,T-1}=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0}\mathbf{1}^{{}^{\prime}}_{T}\mathbf{F}_{T,T-1}=\mathbf{0}_{n\times(T-1)}
and
\left(\mu_{\e}\mathbf{1}_{n},\ldots,\mu_{\e}\mathbf{1}_{n}\right)\mathbf{F}_{T,T-1}=\mu_{\e}\mathbf{1}_{n}\mathbf{1}^{{}^{\prime}}_{T}\mathbf{F}_{T,T-1}=\mathbf{0}_{n\times(T-1)}
, this transformation can remove both
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0}
and
\mu_{\e}
from the model. If we apply
\mathbf{F}_{T,T-1}
to (
3.1
) in a similar manner, we will obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{Y}^{**}_{t}

\displaystyle=\rho_{0}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{Y}^{**}_{t}+\_0\mathbf{Y}^{**,-1}_{t-1}+\delta_{0}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{Y}^{**,-1}_{t-1}+\mathbf{X}^{*}_{t}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}+\alpha^{*}_{t0}\mathbf{1}_{n}+\mathbf{U}^{*}_{t}.

(3.10)

Note that (
3.10
) still includes the transformed time fixed effects denoted by
\alpha^{*}_{t0}
for
t=1,\ldots,T
. To remove these effects, we apply a second transformation by pre-multiplying (
3.10
) with
\mathbf{J}_{n}
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}_{n}\mathbf{Y}^{**}_{t}

\displaystyle=\rho_{0}\mathbf{J}_{n}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{Y}^{**}_{t}+\_0\mathbf{J}_{n}\mathbf{Y}^{**,-1}_{t-1}+\delta_{0}\mathbf{J}_{n}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{Y}^{**,-1}_{t-1}+\mathbf{J}_{n}\mathbf{X}^{*}_{t}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}+\mathbf{J}_{n}\mathbf{U}^{*}_{t}.

(3.11)

Following
Lee, 2007
and
Lee and Yu, 2014
,
Otto et al., 2023
consider both linear and quadratic moment functions for the estimation of (
3.11
). Let
N=n(T-1)
,
\boldsymbol{\theta}=(\rho,\boldsymbol{\eta}^{{}^{\prime}})^{{}^{\prime}}
and
\mathbf{J}_{N}=\mathbf{I}_{T-1}\otimes\mathbf{J}_{n}
. Then, the vector of moment functions takes the following form:

\displaystyle\mathbf{g}_{N}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{U}^{{}^{\prime}}_{N}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\mathbf{J}_{N}\mathbf{P}_{1N}\mathbf{J}_{N}\mathbf{U}_{N}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{U}^{{}^{\prime}}_{N}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\mathbf{J}_{N}\mathbf{P}_{mN}\mathbf{J}_{N}\mathbf{U}_{N}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\\
\mathbf{Q}^{{}^{\prime}}_{N}\mathbf{J}_{N}\mathbf{U}_{N}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\end{pmatrix},

(3.12)

where
\mathbf{U}_{N}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=(\mathbf{U}^{*^{\prime}}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),\ldots,\mathbf{U}^{*^{\prime}}_{T-1}(\boldsymbol{\theta}))^{{}^{\prime}}
with
\mathbf{U}^{*}_{t}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=(\mathbf{I}_{n}-\rho\mathbf{M})\mathbf{Y}^{**}_{t}-\mathbf{Z}^{**}_{t}\boldsymbol{\eta}-\alpha^{*}_{t}\mathbf{1}_{n}
. In (
3.12
), the linear moment function takes the form of
\mathbf{Q}^{{}^{\prime}}_{N}\mathbf{J}_{N}\mathbf{U}_{N}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, where
\mathbf{Q}_{N}
is the
n(T-1)\times k_{q}
matrix of IVs, and the quadratic moment function takes the form of
\mathbf{U}^{{}^{\prime}}_{N}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\mathbf{J}_{N}\mathbf{P}_{jN}\mathbf{J}_{N}\mathbf{U}_{N}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, where
\mathbf{P}_{jN}=\mathbf{I}_{T-1}\otimes\mathbf{P}_{j}
and
\mathbf{P}_{j}
is an
n\times n
matrix satisfying
\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{J}_{n}\mathbf{P}_{j}\mathbf{J}_{n})=0
for
j=1,2,\ldots,m
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{N}=\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}\left(\mathbf{g}_{N}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0})\mathbf{g}^{{}^{\prime}}_{N}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0})\right)
. Then, the optimal GMME is defined by

\displaystyle\widehat{\text{$\boldsymbol{\theta}$}}_{N}=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\boldsymbol{\Theta}}\mathbf{g}^{{}^{\prime}}_{N}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\widehat{\text{$\boldsymbol{\Omega}$}}^{-1}_{N}\mathbf{g}_{N}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),

(3.13)

where
\widehat{\text{$\boldsymbol{\Omega}$}}_{N}
be a consistent estimator of
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{N}
, i.e.,
\widehat{\text{$\boldsymbol{\Omega}$}}_{N}-\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{N}=o_{p}(1)
. Under some assumptions,
Otto et al., 2023
show that
\frac{1}{N}\frac{\partial\mathbf{g}_{N}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0})}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}^{{}^{\prime}}}=\mathbf{D}_{1N}+\mathbf{D}_{2N}+O_{p}(N^{-1/2})
, where
\mathbf{D}_{1N}=O(1)
and
\mathbf{D}_{2N}=O(T^{-1})
.
3
3

3

See
Otto et al., 2023
for the explicit forms of
\mathbf{D}_{1N}
and
\mathbf{D}_{2N}
.
Then, when
T
is finite and
n\to\infty
, it can be shown that

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\left(\widehat{\text{$\boldsymbol{\theta}$}}_{N}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}\right)\xrightarrow{d}N\left(\mathbf{0},\,\plim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{T-1}\left(\left(\mathbf{D}_{1N}+\mathbf{D}_{2N}\right)^{{}^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{-1}_{N}\left(\mathbf{D}_{1N}+\mathbf{D}_{2N}\right)\right)^{-1}\right).

(3.14)

On the other hand, when
T\to\infty
, we have
\mathbf{D}_{2N}=O(T^{-1})=o(1)
. Thus, when
T\to\infty
and
n\to\infty
,
Otto et al., 2023
show that

\displaystyle\sqrt{N}\left(\widehat{\text{$\boldsymbol{\theta}$}}_{N}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}\right)\xrightarrow{d}N\left(\mathbf{0},\,\plim_{n,T\to\infty}\left(\mathbf{D}^{{}^{\prime}}_{1N}\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{-1}_{N}\mathbf{D}_{1N}\right)^{-1}\right).

(3.15)

Note that the asymptotic results in (
3.14
) and (
3.15
) hold for the arbitrary IV matrix
\mathbf{Q}_{N}
and the quadratic moment matrices
\mathbf{P}_{jN}
for
j=1,2,\ldots,m
. The asymptotic efficiency of
\widehat{\text{$\boldsymbol{\theta}$}}_{N}
should be considered in choosing the IV and quadratic moment matrices. Following
Lee and Yu, 2014
,
Otto et al., 2023
provide a vector of moment functions that can lead to the most efficient GMME when both
n
and
T
are large.

4
Monte Carlo Simulations

In this section, we investigate the finite sample performance of all three considered estimators when the sample size is small. In particular, we are looking at the case of short time series (i.e.,
T
is small), which is often encountered in practice for geo-referenced data. More precisely, we simulate
Y_{t}(\boldsymbol{s}_{i})=h_{t}(\boldsymbol{s}_{i})^{1/2}\varepsilon_{t}(\boldsymbol{s}_{i})
with

\log h_{t}(\boldsymbol{s}_{i})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\rho_{0}m_{ij}\log Y^{2}_{t}(\boldsymbol{s}_{j})+\gamma_{0}\log Y^{2}_{t-1}(\boldsymbol{s}_{i})+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\delta_{0}m_{ij}\log Y^{2}_{t-1}(\boldsymbol{s}_{j})+\mathbf{x}^{{}^{\prime}}_{it}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}+\mu_{i0}+\alpha_{t0}.

(4.1)

We consider three different data-generating processes with the following parameter settings:

\displaystyle M_{1}:\,\rho_{0}=0.2,\,\gamma_{0}=0.5,\,\delta_{0}=-0.2,\,\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}=(0.5,1)^{\prime},\alpha_{t0}=0\,\forall t,

\displaystyle M_{2}:\,\rho_{0}=0.3,\,\gamma_{0}=0.2,\,\delta_{0}=0.2,\,\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}=(0.5,1)^{\prime},\alpha_{t0}=0\,\forall t,

\displaystyle M_{3}:\,\rho_{0}=0.8,\gamma_{0}=0.1,\,\delta_{0}=-0.2,\,\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}=(0.5,1)^{\prime}.

In
M_{1}
, the temporal effect is relatively dominant; in
M_{2}
, all effects are relatively weak; and finally, in
M_{3}
, the spatial effect is relatively strong. Both
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
include only the spatial fixed effects, while
M_{3}
includes both the spatial and time fixed effects. The spatial/time fixed effects and the exogenous regressors are independently simulated from
N(0,1)
. We generate the error terms
\varepsilon_{t}(\boldsymbol{s}_{i})
’s independently from
N(0,1)
, and consider a queen contiguity row-normalized weights matrix. The sample size gradually increases for both
n
and
T
with
n\in\{25,49,81\}
on a regular two-dimensional lattice (with side length 5, 7, and 9) and
T\in\{5,10,20\}
. The number of repetitions is set to
1000
in all cases. Thus, we considered 27 different model specifications in total and three different estimators, which were always applied to the same simulated values.

For each estimator, we report bias (BIAS), the root mean square error (RMSE) and the mean absolute error (MAE). The results are reported in Tables

and

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we considered the small and large sample properties of (i) the QMLE based on the transformation approach, (ii) the QMLE based on the direct approach, and (iii) the GMME for the estimation of a dynamic spatiotemporal ARCH model. Theoretically, the estimators based on the QML method require a large
T
and a bias correction approach. The bias-corrected QMLE based on the transformation approach requires that
\frac{n}{T^{3}}\to 0
, while the bias-corrected QMLE based on the direct approach requires both
\frac{n}{T^{3}}\to 0
and
\frac{T}{n^{3}}\to\infty
. The QMLE based on the transformation approach is only applicable for models with row-normalized weights matrices. The GMME does not require bias correction and is valid under both finite and large
T
cases. In a Monte Carlo simulation study, we compare the finite sample properties of these estimators. Our results indicate that the QMLE based on the direct approach performs relatively better for the estimation of the spatial effect (
\rho
), while the GMME performs relatively better for the estimation of the temporal effect (
\gamma
). Overall, when both
T
and
n
are large enough, all estimators perform similarly and satisfactorily. In future studies, the performance of these estimators can be investigated for a dynamic spatiotemporal ARCH model that has a multiplicative structure for the regional and time-fixed effects.
