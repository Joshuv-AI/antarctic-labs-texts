ParamReL: Learning Parameter Space Representation via Progressively Encoding Bayesian Flow Networks
Zhangkai Wu, Xuhui Fan, Jin Li, Zhilin Zhao, Hui Chen, Longbing Cao

Abstract
The recently proposed Bayesian Flow Networks (BFNs) show great potential in modeling parameter spaces, offering a unified strategy for handling continuous, discretized, and discrete data. However, BFNs cannot learn high-level semantic representation from the parameter space since common encoders, which encode data into one static representation, cannot capture semantic changes in parameters. This motivates a new direction: learning semantic representations hidden in the parameter spaces to characterize mixed-typed noisy data. Accordingly, we propose a representation learning framework named ParamReL, which operates in the parameter space to obtain parameter-wise latent semantics that exhibit progressive structures. Specifically, ParamReL proposes a self- encoder to learn latent semantics directly from parameters, rather than from observations. The encoder is then integrated into BFNs, enabling representation learning with various formats of observations. Mutual information terms further promote the disentanglement of latent semantics and capture meaningful semantics simultaneously. We illustrate conditional generation and reconstruction in ParamReL via expanding BFNs, and extensive quantitative experimental results demonstrate the superior effectiveness of ParamReL in learning parameter representation. We release the code at: https://github.com/amasawa/ParamReL/tree/main .

By operating in the parameter space, BFNs design a multi-step mechanism to approximate the ground-truth parameters of observation sequentially. Pilot studies of BFNs have shown promising results in modelling different data formats.

Leveraging BFNs, this paper introduces ParamReL, a novel parameter space representation learning framework that employs a unified strategy to extract meaningful high-level semantics from continuous, discretized, and discrete data. Specifically, a
self-

ParamReL is applied on benchmark datasets and verifies its effectiveness in obtaining meaningful high-level semantics for discrete and continuous-valued observations. Sampling and reverse-sampling procedures are developed here to complete conditional image reconstruction and generation tasks. In particular, our developed self-encoder discovers interesting progressive semantics along with the flow steps. That is, our ParamReL obtains meaningful and clearer disentangled representations while maintaining high sample quality.

The main contributions of this work can be summarized as follows: (1) A parameter space representation learning framework introduces a uniform strategy for modelling continuous, discretized and discrete observations; (2) A
self-

Bayesian Flow Networks

Bayesian Flow Networks (BFNs)
[
16
,
38
,
50
]
are a new class of latent variable models. Similar to the reverse process of diffusion models
[
18
,
37
]
, BFNs target optimizing the neural network architecture on posterior parameters to simulate observations as they are gradually observed. In particular, BFNs assume two types of distributions: a simple
input distribution

P_{\mathrm{I}}(\cdot)
representing the initial belief about observations and an
output distribution

P_{\mathrm{O}}(\cdot)
representing the simulating distribution for observations. The parameters of input distribution are first updated through a Bayesian inference scheme and then passed into a neural network to form the parameters of output distributions. The main objective of BFNs is to minimize the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence between the ground-truth data distribution and the output distribution, ensuring that the output distribution closely approximates the ground-truth data distribution.

ParamReL: Parameter Space Representation Learning Framework

This section introduces the ParamReL framework, which facilitates parameter space representation learning through low-dimensional latent semantics. Instead of approximating the data distribution
p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0})
, ParamReL aims to learn the joint distribution over data
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0}
and latent semantics
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}
, with
|{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}|\ll|{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0}|
. This ensures that low-dimensional latent semantics
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}
are learned from high-dimensional data
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0}
. In other words, ParamReL seeks to reconstruct observations
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0}
using a high-performing likelihood function while obtaining meaningful low-dimensional latent semantics
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}
.

T

t=\{T,T-1,\ldots,1\}
, the observations first go through a Bayesian update function and a sender distribution to form the posterior

3.1
Self-encoder
q_{\mathbf{\phi}}(\mathbf{z}_{t}|\mathbf{\theta}_{t},t)

Common autoencoders, such as
q_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}}}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}|{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0})
, aim to encode the observation
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0}
into a
\emph{static}
latent semantic
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}
. However, this construction does not align well with the ParamReL framework, which employs multiple steps to generate observations. In ParamReL, a single static semantic
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}
may not be sufficient to encode all intermediate variables
\{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t}\}_{t}
. Specifically,
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}
works together with the updated parameter
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}_{t}
in
\psi({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}_{t},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}})
. A static semantic
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}
derived from the initial observation
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0}
may fail to capture the complex dynamics in
\{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}_{t}\}_{t}
.

In ParamReL, we propose a
self-encoder

q_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}}}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}_{t}|{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}_{t},t)
that differs from the standard encoder
q_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}}}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}|{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0})
in two key ways: (1) We adopt a step-wise representation
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}_{t}
tailored to the variable behaviours at each step
t
; (2) We utilize
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}_{t}
, which summarizes information from all previous steps, instead of
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t}
, which only captures the current step
t
, to generate the step-wise
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}_{t}
. Through
q_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}}}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}_{t}|{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}_{t},t)
,
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}_{t}
effectively encodes
itself
into
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}_{t}
, and they then jointly form
\psi({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}_{t},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}_{t})
. Therefore, we refer to it as a
self-encoder
.

The proposed self-encoder works consistently with the decoder network here as both work on
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}_{t}
, see Figure
1
(b). By obtaining
a series of
latent semantics
\{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}_{t}\}_{t}
, it is expected these encoded latents would show progressive semantic behaviour (representing, for example, the gradual changes of age
and
smiling
and
skin colour of a person) along with the generation procedures. This is followed by a reconstruction phase, where lower-level generative latent
{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{t}
(such as hair texture) are progressively incorporated. In our experiments, we parameterize
q_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}}}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}_{t}|{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}_{t},t)
as a UNet encoder (see Appendix
C
for more details).

Prior for
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}_{t}
:
Ideally, the latent semantics
\{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}_{t}\}_{t}
should provide low-dimensional representations that are different from the intermediates
\{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t}\}_{t}
of BFNs, yet they should not compromise the data reconstruction process. To generate high-quality semantic representations, a learnable and smooth latent space is essential
[
4
]
. This necessitates the integration of the prior distribution
p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}_{t})
into a robust probabilistic framework, thereby enabling the unconditional sampling of
{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{0}
. For simplicity and sampling efficiency,
p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}_{t})
could be assumed to follow a straightforward distribution, such as a Gaussian.

3.2
Training and Testing with ParamReL

By augmenting the latents
\{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}_{t}\}_{t}
, the joint distribution of
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0},\{{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{t}\}_{t},\{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}_{t}\}_{t}
can be written as:

\displaystyle p\left({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{0},\{{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{t}\}_{t},\{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}_{t}\}_{t}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}_{0},\alpha\right)=p_{\mathrm{O}}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0};\psi({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}_{0},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}_{0}))\prod_{t=1}^{T}p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}_{t})\mathbb{E}_{p_{\mathrm{O}}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t};\psi({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}_{t},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}_{t}))}[p_{\mathrm{S}}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t-1}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t};\alpha_{t})],

(2)

where the model implements an output distribution
p_{\mathrm{O}}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t};\psi({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}_{t},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}_{t}))
conditioned on auxiliary latent
{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}}_{t}
, which is distributed according to prior
p({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}}_{t})
. The latent variable
{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}}_{t}
of prior
p({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}}_{t})
is independent of the BFNs process as a latent representation of input. Through a neural network architecture
\psi(\cdot,\cdot)
,
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}_{t}
works together with the posterior parameter
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}_{t}
to form the parameters of the output distribution as
\psi({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}_{t},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}_{t})
. We use
p_{\mathrm{O}}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0};\psi({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}_{0},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}))\prod_{t=1}^{T}p_{\mathrm{R}}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t-1};\psi({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}_{t},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}_{t}),\alpha_{t})
to decode both the auxiliary latent representation and the generative latent.

Training with ParamRL:
Variational inference methods approximate the intractable joint distribution. With
q_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}}}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}_{t}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}_{t},t)
defined as the encoder for
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}_{t}
and
p_{\mathrm{S}}(\{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t}\}_{t}|-)
defined as the variational distribution for
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{T:1}
, the evidence lower bound on the marginal log-likelihood of data
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0}
is (see the full derivation in Appendix
B
):

\log p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0})\geq-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{p_{\mathrm{F}}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}_{t}|-)}\mathbb{E}_{q_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}}}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}_{t})}\left\{{\textsc{kl}\left[p_{\mathrm{S}}\left({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t-1}\,|\,{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0}};\alpha_{T:t}\right)\;\|\;p_{\mathrm{R}}\left({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t-1};\psi({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}_{t},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}_{t}),\alpha_{t}\right)\right]}\right.\\
\left.-\textsc{kl}\left[q_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}}}\left({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}}_{t}\,|\,{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}_{t}},t\right)\;\|\;p({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}}_{t})\right]\right\}+\mathbb{E}_{q_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}}}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}_{0},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}_{0})}\left[{\ln p_{\mathrm{O}}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0};\psi({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}_{0},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}_{0}))}\right]:=\mathcal{L}_{\texttt{ParamReL}}.

(3)

Maximizing the objective function
\mathcal{L}_{\text{ParamReL}}
is equivalent to performing amortized inference
[
22
]
through encoders
q_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}}}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}_{t}|{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}_{t},t)
and learning likelihood function through decoders
[
55
]
. The encodable posterior
q_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}}}\left({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}_{t}|{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}_{t},t\right)
is used to infer high-level semantics
\{{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{t}\}_{t}
. During the
decoding
stage,
{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{0}
is used into generative latent variables
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{1:T}
, which contains low-level semantic features in generating the observations. In ParamReL, the parameters of the output distribution are learned through iteratively proceeding the Bayesian updating process and a learned noise model
{\psi}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}})
parameterized by neural networks
\psi
.

Sampling and Reverse-sampling with ParamReL in the Test Phase
Once ParamReL is trained, we can use the generative procedure to do pseudo-data sampling, i.e., use
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{T}
to sequentially generate
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{T-1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0}
from
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{T}
. In particular, at the
t
-th step, we have

\displaystyle\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{t-1}\sim p_{\mathrm{S}}\left(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{t-1}|{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t},\alpha_{t}\right),\ {\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}_{t}=h({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}_{t+1},\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{t-1},\alpha_{t}),\ {\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}_{t}\sim p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}_{t}),\ \widehat{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{t}\sim p_{O}(\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{t};\psi({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}_{t},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}_{t})),\ {\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t-1}=\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{t}

(4)

where
\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{t-1}
is a pseudo value for the intermediate
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t-1}
. Since the self-encoder is already trained, it may be possible to use
q_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}}}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}_{t}|{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}_{t},t)
to replace
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}_{t}
’s prior
p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}_{t})
to improve the sampling quality.

However, the reverse-sampling procedure, which transits observation
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0}
through
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{2},\ldots
until its noisy state
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{T}
, is not as straightforward as the sampling procedure. In fact, we notice the output distribution
p_{O}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t};\psi({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}_{t},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}_{t}))
may help us to derive the reverse-sampling procedure. At the
t
-th step, the reverse-sampling procedure may be implemented as

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}_{t+1}=h^{-1}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}_{t},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t-1},\alpha_{t}),\quad{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}_{t+1}=q_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}}}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}_{t+1},t+1),\quad{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t+1}\sim p_{\mathrm{O}}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t+1};\psi({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}_{t+1},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}_{t+1})).

(5)

where
h^{-1}(\cdot)
refers to the inverse Bayesian update function. Given the sampling and reverse-sampling procedure, we can do various tasks such as image reconstruction, interpolation, etc. The details of such results are shown in the experiments.

3.3
Regularizing Semantics By Maximizing Mutual Information

Ideally, during the training phase, we want to acquire the semantic representation
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}_{t}
by the self-encoder
q_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}}}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}_{t}|{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}_{t},t)
and achieve high-quality reconstruction
\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0}}
by the decoder (i.e., the output distribution
p_{O}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0};\psi({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}_{0},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}_{0}))
). However, there exists a trade-off between inference and learning
[
34
,
46
]
coherent in optimizing the objective function
\mathcal{L}_{\text{ParamReL}}
in Eq. (
3
). In most cases, ParamReL favours fitting likelihood rather than inference
[
56
]
. Based on the rate-distortion theory
[
1
,
3
]
, the rate (KL divergence term) constrained by the encoders compresses enough information for the distortion (reconstruction error) but less informative to induce a smooth latent space.

To remedy the insufficient representation learning during the inference stage, we want to increase the dependence between input parameters
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}_{t}
and encoded latent
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}_{t}
by maximizing the mutual information
I({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}_{t},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}_{t})
.

We can rewrite the tractable learning object in ParamReL by adding the mutual information maximization term as:
\mathcal{L}_{\text{ParamReL+}}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{ParamReL}}+\frac{\gamma}{T}\sum_{t}I_{q}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}_{t};{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}_{t})
, where
I_{q}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}_{t};{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}_{t})
is the mutual information between
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}_{t},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}_{t}
under the distribution
q_{\phi}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}_{t}|{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}_{t})
. Considering that we cannot optimize this object directly, we can rewrite this object by factorizing the rate term into MI and Total Correlation (TC):

\mathcal{L}_{\texttt{ParamReL+}}=-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{p_{\mathrm{F}}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}_{t}|-)}\mathbb{E}_{q_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}}}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}_{t})}\left\{{\textsc{kl}\left[p_{\mathrm{S}}\left({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t-1}\,|\,{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0}};\alpha_{T:t}\right)\;\|\;p_{\mathrm{R}}\left({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{t-1};\psi({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}_{t},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}_{t}),\alpha_{t}\right)\right]}\right.\\
\left.-\frac{1-\gamma}{T}\textsc{kl}\left[q_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}}}\left({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}}_{t}\,|\,{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}_{t}}\right)\;\|\;p({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}})\right]-\frac{\gamma+\lambda-1}{T}\textsc{kl}\left[q_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}}}\left({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}}_{t}\right)\;\|\;p({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}})\right]\right\}+\mathbb{E}_{q_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}}}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}_{0},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}_{0})}\left[{\ln p_{\mathrm{O}}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0};\psi({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}_{0},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}_{0}))}\right].

(6)

Mutual Information Learning

Unlike the rest of the terms that can be optimized directly using reparameterization tricks, the TC term cannot be directly optimized due to intractable marginal distribution
q_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}}}({{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}_{t}})
. Here, we follow the guidance in
[
56
]
to replace the TC term with any strict divergence
D
, where
D\left(q_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}}}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}})\|p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}})\right)=0
iff
q_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}}}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}})=p({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}})
. We implement the Maximum-Mean Discrepancy (MMD)
[
56
]
from the divergence family. MMD is a statistical measure that quantifies the difference between two probability distributions by comparing their mean embeddings in a high-dimensional feature space. By defining the kernel function
\kappa(\cdot,\cdot)
,
D_{\mathrm{MMD}}
is denoted as:

Related Work

Recent advances have demonstrated that diffusion-based models
[
18
,
37
]
are capable of generating high-quality data. Nonetheless, compared to the autoencoder framework, the intermediate outputs in diffusion stages are high-dimensional and lack smoothness, making them unsuitable for representation learning. Contemporary research focuses on encoding a conditional latent space to acquire semantic low-dimensional representations. However, sample-based models
[
31
,
45
]
, such as VAEs and Diffusions, exhibit limitations when applied to discrete data.

Deep hierarchical VAEs have seen progress in capturing latent dependence structures for encoding an expressive posterior, statistically or semantically. VQVAE-based

[
44
,
32
]
models have local-to-global feature-based explanatory hierarchies at the image level, forming a codebook-based discrete posterior.
[
36
,
40
]
build recursive latent structures in multi-layer networks to form an aggregated posterior. NVAE
[
43
]
demonstrates that depth-wise hierarchies encoded by residual networks can approximate the posterior precisely despite using shallow networks. Unlike the sample-based encoder, where the information flow between input and latent is maximized in encoding-decoding pipelines on the sample space, we develop progressive encoders in the parameter space to capture the dynamic semantics.

6
Conclusion and Limitations

A novel parameter space representation learning framework ParamReL is introduced in this work, which provides a uniform strategy for dealing with continuous, discretized, and discrete data. Unlike previous encoder methods that encode observations into static latent semantics, a self-encoder is capable of obtaining a series of progressively structured latent semantics from the step-wise parameters. Experimental results on tasks of latent interpolation, disentanglement, time-varying conditional reconstruction, and conditional generation validate the merits of ParamReL, demonstrating its superior performance in gaining unified representations and a clearer visual understanding.

ParamReL discloses interesting follow-ups, including: (1) The precision variables
\alpha_{t}
could potentially be optimized to accelerate the sampling procedure, which will be further explored.
(2) Utilizing a pre-trained model for the U-Net architecture might enhance the performance of the proposed ParamReL, which will be investigated in future work.

Latent Variable Models (LVMs)

{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}

represents prior knowledge for inference
[
42
]
, thus facilitating learning the conditional distribution

Among LVMs, Variational AutoEncoders (VAEs)
[
22
]
and diffusion models
[
18

37
]
are two representative approaches
[
25
]
.

In VAEs, latent variables
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}
is obtained through an
encoder network

q_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}}}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}})
, whereas observations are reconstructed through a
decoder network

p_{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}}({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}})
, with
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\phi}}}
and
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}
being the encoder and decoder parameters.
The dimensions of
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}
are usually much smaller than those of
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}
, denoted as
|{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}}|\ll|{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}|
, such that redundant information is effectively removed and the most semantically meaningful factors are abstracted
[
28
]
. VAEs are popular for downstream tasks like disentanglement
[
17
,
51
,
19
,
12
]
, classification
[
39
,
41
]
, and clustering
[
20
,
49
]
.

On the other hand, diffusion models
[
18

37
]
first use
T
diffusion steps to transform observation
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}
into a white noise
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{T}
and then use
T

{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}
and
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{T}
are the same as
|{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}|=|{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{T}|
.

[
31
,
45
]
have attempted to integrate a decodable auxiliary variable

Appendix E
Latent Interpolation

The latent space interpolation can be described as follows. Firstly, we noise source images to generate latent pairs by sender distribution,
<{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}^{1}_{1},{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}^{2}_{1}>
, where
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}^{1}_{1}\sim q(\cdot\mid{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}^{1}_{N})
and
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}^{2}_{1}\sim q(\cdot\mid{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}^{2}_{N})
. Then, we implement two methods from
[
35
]
to generate four interpolated latent pairs
\bar{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{1:4}
, i.e., linear interpolation, and spherical interpolation:

\displaystyle\bar{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{i}

\displaystyle=(1-\lambda){\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0}^{1}+\lambda{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0}^{2},

(13)

\displaystyle\bar{{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sin((1-\alpha)\theta)}{\sin(\theta)}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0}^{1}+\frac{\sin(\alpha\theta)}{\sin(\theta)}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0}^{1},

where
\lambda
is the scale coefficient,
\alpha\in[0,1]
denotes the interpolation steps, and
\theta=\arccos\left(\frac{\left({\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0}^{1}\right)^{\top}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0}^{2}}{\left\|{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0}^{1}\right\|\left\|{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0}^{2}\right\|}\right)
is the angle between
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0}^{1}
and
{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0}^{2}
.
