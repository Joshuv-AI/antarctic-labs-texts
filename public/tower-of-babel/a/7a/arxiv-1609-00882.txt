Abstract
The perspective of Kac–Schwarz operators is introduced to the authors’ previous work on the quantum mirror curves of topological string theory in strip geometry and closed topological vertex. Open string amplitudes on each leg of the web diagram of such geometry can be packed into a multi-variate generating function. Introduction

It is argued
[
1
,
11
,
10
,
31
]
that a “quantum mirror curve” emerges on the B-model (Kodaira–Spencer theory) side of A-model topological string theory when a brane is inserted as a probe. This quantum curve is represented by a quantum mechanical equation of the form

\displaystyle\hat{H}(\hat{x},\hat{y})\Psi=0.

(1.1)

\hat{H}(\hat{x},\hat{y})
is quantization of a function
H(x,y)
on a 2D phase space,
and the curve

\displaystyle H(x,y)=0

is called the mirror curve. In the case of local toric Calabi–Yau threefolds,
H(x,y)
is the function with which the mirror manifold is defined by the equation

\displaystyle zw=H(x,y).

The phase space therein has the logarithmic symplectic form
d\log x\wedge d\log y
, and one can choose
\hat{x}
and
\hat{y}
as

\displaystyle\hat{x}=x,\qquad\hat{y}=q^{D},

where

\displaystyle q=e^{-\hbar},\qquad D=x\frac{d}{dx}.

(
1.1
) thereby becomes a
q
-difference equation for the wave function
\Psi=\Psi(x)
of the probe.

It is also pointed out
[
26
]
that quantum curves are closely related to the Eynard–Orantin topological recursion
[
18
]
. The notion of topological recursion was introduced to topological string theory through a matrix model description
[
12
,
41
]
. This new computational tool led to the BKMS remodeling conjecture
[
7
]
as well as a new conjectural recursion formula for simple Hurwitz numbers
[
8
]
(both of which were solved by mathematicians
[
17
,
21
,
45
]
). The mirror curve and a 2-point kernel
B(p,q)
on this curve are the inputs of topological recursion. The outputs are a set of multi-point kernels
W_{g,n}
,
g\geq 0
,
n\geq 1
, that amount to multi-point resolvents of a matrix model. Amplitudes (or partition functions)
of strings are constructed from these multi-point kernels. Gukov and Sułkowski’s observation
[
26
]
(based on Eynard and Orantin’s interpretation
of the notion of Baker–Akhiezer functions
[
18
]
) is that a WKB expansion of the wave function
\Psi(x)
, too, can be constructed from
W_{g,n}
’s.
Gukov and Sułkowski show, along with many other examples, a partial result on computation of the WKB expansion in the case of
\mathbb{C}^{3}
and the resolved conifold. A complete derivation of the quantum mirror curve for these two cases is achieved by Zhou
[
66
]
.

We are interested in deriving the quantum mirror curve from the A-model side, namely, in the topological vertex formalism
[
2
]
. The wave function
\Psi(x)
is defined as a generating function of open string amplitudes. Topological vertex offers a combinatorial description of these amplitudes.
If one can compute this generating function explicitly, one should be able to verify the equation (
1.1
) of the quantum mirror curve by any means.
This is indeed done for
\mathbb{C}^{3}

[
1
,
2
]
and the resolved conifold
[
32
,
35
,
36
]
. The wave functions for these cases are quantum dilogarithmic functions
[
19
,
20
]
and
q
-hypergeometric (or basic hypergeometric) functions
[
23
]
, hence satisfy equations of the form (
1.1
). Some other examples of computations, all falling into “strip geometry”
[
33
]
, can be found in the literature on different subjects
[
6
,
14
,
39
,
62
]
. A unified expression of the quantum mirror curves for general strip geometry is presented in our previous work
[
60
]
. Moreover, we extended these results to the simplest example of “off-strip geometry” called closed topological vertex
[
61
]
.

The aim of this paper is to re-examine our previous results
[
60
,
61
]
in the perspective of “Kac–Schwarz operators”. This enables us to highlight a role of the Sato Grassmannian and the KP hierarchy
[
53
,
56
]
that was hidden in the previous derivation of quantum mirror curves of topological string theory. Moreover, Kac–Schwarz operators for topological string theory exhibit some new features, which are significant in the context of integrable hierarchies.

The notion of Kac–Schwarz operators
[
34
,
54
]
originates in the string equations of 2D quantum gravity
[
13
,
15
,
22
,
43
]

(see also the recent paper
[
55
]
focused on quantum curves). The Kac–Schwarz operators for the
(a,b)
minimal model of 2D quantum gravity are a pair of ordinary differential operators
A
,
B
of the form

\displaystyle A=\frac{1}{bz^{b-1}}\frac{d}{dz}+z^{a}+\text{terms of lower degrees},\qquad B=z^{b}

that satisfy the canonical commutation relation

\displaystyle[A,B]=1.

They characterize a point
W
of the Sato Grassmannian as a linear subspace of the space
V=\mathbb{C}((z^{-1}))
of formal Laurent series by the condition

\displaystyle AW\subset W,\qquad BW\subset W.

The Witten–Kontsevich theory of intersection numbers on a moduli space of curves
[
38
,
63
]
, which amounts to the special case where
(a,b)=(1,2)
, can be treated in the same way.

A variant of this conventional formulation of Kac–Schwarz operators is used to derive quantum spectral curves of various Hurwitz numbers
[
3
,
4
]
and quantum mirror curves of a few cases of topological string theory
[
67
]
. The Kac–Schwarz operators therein are differential operators of infinite order (in the Hurwitz case) or
q
-difference operators (in the topological string case), and satisfy the modified commutation relation

\displaystyle[A,B]=A

in the Hurwitz case and

\displaystyle AB=qBA

(1.2)

in the topological string case. Moreover, it is pointed out that a particular element
\Psi
of
W
(more precisely, the first element of an admissible basis of
W
) satisfies the equation

\displaystyle A\Psi=0

in the Hurwitz case and

\displaystyle A\Psi=\Psi

in the topological string case. These equations are interpreted as quantum spectral/mirror curves.

To capture the quantum mirror curves of general strip geometry and closed topological vertex along these lines, we borrow the idea of
generating operator
developed by Alexandrov et al.
[
3
,
4
]
. This is an invertible operator
G
on
V=\mathbb{C}((x))
,
x=z^{-1}
, that generates an admissible basis
\{\Phi_{j}(x)\}_{j=0}^{\infty}
of the relevant point
W
of the Sato Grassmannian. The Kac–Schwarz operators in this case are
q
-difference operators,
and defined as

\displaystyle A=G\cdot q^{-D}\cdot G^{-1},\qquad B=G\cdot x^{-1}\cdot G^{-1}.

(1.3)

The basis functions
\Phi_{j}(x)
satisfy the linear equations

\displaystyle A\Phi_{j}(x)=q^{j}\Phi_{j}(x),\qquad B\Phi_{j}(x)=\Phi_{j+1}(x).

(1.4)

Since
\Phi_{0}(x)
turns out to be the generating function of open string amplitudes, the lowest equation
A\Phi_{0}(x)=\Phi_{0}(x)
of (
1.4
) can be identified with the quantum mirror curve of topological string theory. As the algebraic relation (
1.2
) implies, the other equations
A\Phi_{j}(x)=q^{j}\Phi_{j}(x)
can be derived from the lowest equation by applying
B
iteratively. Thus
B
plays the role of a “ladder operator” connecting these equations, though we do not know what physical meaning this tower of extra linear equations have.

We derive the generating operator
G
from a fermionic expression of the KP tau function that is defined as a multi-variate generating function of open string amplitudes. Our computational technique developed in the previous work
[
60
,
61
]
yield a fermionic expression of the tau function
almost automatically. This expression contains the vector
|W\rangle
of the fermionic Fock space representing
W
.
|W\rangle
is generated from
the vacuum vector
|0\rangle
by an operator
g
on the Fock space. In the case of topological string theory of strip geometry and closed topological vertex,

g
is made from building blocks that are familiar in the operator formalism of topological vertex
[
50
,
64
]
. We can thereby find an explicit expression of
G
. This enables us to find the admissible basis and the
q
-difference Kac–Schwarz operators as well.

Viewing this machinery on the whole, one may say that the KP hierarchy plays the role of a “mirror map”. The inputs of this map are open string amplitudes
in the topological vertex formalism. They are manufactured into a KP tau function. This tau function determines a point
W
of the Sato Grassmannian, hence a vector
|W\rangle
of the fermionic Fock space. From an admissible basis of
W
and associated Kac–Schwarz operators, one obtains a quantum curve as the output. Such a mapping from the A-model side to the B-model side is commonly called a mirror map.

This paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, the notion of generating operators and Kac–Schwarz operators are introduced and illustrated
for topological string theory on
\mathbb{C}^{3}
and the resolved conifold. These two cases are presented as a prototype of the subsequent case studies. Relation to “hypergeometric tau functions” is briefly mentioned in the end of this section. Section
3
deals the case of general strip geometry. A multi-variate generating function of open string amplitudes is constructed for each external line, or “leg”, of the web diagram, and shown to be a KP tau function with a fermionic expression. Actually, the tau functions on the vertical legs and those on the non-vertical legs turn out to have distinct structures. This difference is reflected in the types of functions forming the admissible bases. The generating operator
G
and the
q
-difference operators
A
,
B
are computed explicitly. Results of the previous work
[
60
]
are reproduced successfully. Section
4
presents similar results in the case of closed topological vertex. The relevant tau functions are shown to have a slightly more complicated structure. This characteristic is inherited by the functions forming the associated admissible basis. Nevertheless, computations are mostly parallel to the case of strip geometry. Actually, the previous work
[
61
]
can be reproduced in a more systematic way in the language of the operators
G
,
A
,
B
.

\boldsymbol{q}
-difference Kac–Schwarz operators

2.1
Generating operator of admissible basis

A point
W
of the big cell of the Sato Grassmannian is realized by a linear subspace

\displaystyle W=\operatorname{Span}\{\Phi_{j}(x)\}_{j=0}^{\infty}

of the space
V=\mathbb{C}((x))
of formal Laurent series with an admissible basis

\displaystyle\Phi_{j}(x)=\sum_{i=-j}^{\infty}\phi_{ij}x^{i},\qquad\phi_{-j,j}\not=0,\qquad j=0,1,\ldots.

For convenience, we allow the leading coefficients
\phi_{-j,j}
to be unnormalized, though they can be normalized to
1
. The variable
x
amounts to the inverse of the usual spectral parameter
z
of the KP hierarchy:

\displaystyle x=z^{-1}.

If the admissible basis is generated by a single invertible
q
-difference operator
G=G(x,q^{D})
as

\displaystyle\Phi_{j}(x)=Gx^{-j},\qquad j=0,1,\dots,

(2.1)

the basis elements satisfy the linear
q
-difference equations (
1.4
) with respect to the operators
A
,
B
defined by (
1.3
). In particular, the first basis element
\Phi_{0}(x)
satisfies the equation

\displaystyle A\Phi_{0}(x)=\Phi_{0}(x),

(2.2)

which we identify with the quantum mirror curve of topological string theory.

(
1.3
) are
q
-difference analogues of the Kac–Schwarz operators for Hurwitz numbers of various types
[
3
,
4
,
67
]
. In those cases,
G
is a differential operator of infinite order,
G=G(x,D)
. The Kac–Schwarz operators
A
,
B
are defined as

\displaystyle A=-G\cdot D\cdot G^{-1},\qquad B=G\cdot x^{-1}\cdot G^{-1}

and satisfy the twisted canonical commutation relation (
1.2
). The admissible basis generated by
G
, in turn, satisfies the linear differential equations

\displaystyle A\Phi_{j}(x)=j\Phi_{j}(x),\qquad B\Phi_{j}(x)=\Phi_{j+1}(x).

The lowest equation

\displaystyle A\Phi_{0}(x)=0

gives the quantum spectral curve for Hurwitz numbers.

q
-difference Kac–Schwarz operators are presented by Zhou
[
67
]
for open string amplitudes of
\mathbb{C}^{3}
and the resolved conifold with a general framing parameter
f
. We review these results (in the case where
f=0
) as prototypes of our subsequent consideration. Unlike Zhou’s formulation, we make full use of the theory of Schur functions
[
40
]
and the fermionic formalism of integrable hierarchies
[
42
]
.

2.2
Example:
\boldsymbol{\mathbb{C}^{3}}

The open string amplitudes
Z_{\beta}
,
\beta=(\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\ldots)\in\mathcal{P}
(the set of all partitions), of topological string theory on
\mathbb{C}^{3}
in the presence of a stack of branes (Fig.
1
, left) are given by special values of the infinite-variate Schur functions
s_{\lambda}
as

\displaystyle Z_{\beta}=s_{\,{\vphantom{\beta}}{}^{\mathrm{t}}\!\,\beta}\big(q^{-\rho}\big),\qquad q^{-\rho}=\big(q^{1/2},q^{3/2},\ldots,q^{i-1/2},\ldots\big),

where
\,{\vphantom{\beta}}{}^{\mathrm{t}}\!\,\beta
denotes the conjugate partition of
\beta
.
Z_{\beta}
is nothing but the special case
C_{\varnothing\varnothing\beta}
of the topological vertex.

We use the Schur functions
s_{\,{\vphantom{\beta}}{}^{\mathrm{t}}\!\,\beta}(\boldsymbol{x})
of auxiliary variables
\boldsymbol{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots)
to define a generating function
\tau(\boldsymbol{x})
of
Z_{\beta}
’s:

\displaystyle\tau(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{\beta\in\mathcal{P}}s_{\,{\vphantom{\beta}}{}^{\mathrm{t}}\!\,\beta}(\boldsymbol{x})Z_{\beta}.

This sum can be readily computed by the Cauchy identity of Schur functions:

\displaystyle\tau(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{\beta\in\mathcal{P}}s_{\,{\vphantom{\beta}}{}^{\mathrm{t}}\!\,\beta}(\boldsymbol{x})s_{\,{\vphantom{\beta}}{}^{\mathrm{t}}\!\,\beta}\big(q^{-\rho}\big)=\prod_{i,j=1}^{\infty}\big(1-q^{i-1/2}x_{j}\big)^{-1}.

By the so called Miwa transformation

\displaystyle t_{k}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}x_{i}^{k}

(2.3)

to the time variables
\boldsymbol{t}=(t_{1},t_{2},\ldots)
of the KP hierarchy, this infinite product turns into an exponential function:

\displaystyle\tau(\boldsymbol{x})=\exp\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{q^{k/2}}{1-q^{k}}t_{k}\right).

This is an exponential tau function of the KP hierarchy.

To find an associated generating operator
G
, it is convenient to start from the fermionic expression

\displaystyle\tau(\boldsymbol{x})=\big\langle 0|\Gamma_{+}(\boldsymbol{x})\Gamma_{-}\big(q^{-\rho}\big)|0\big\rangle

(2.4)

of
\tau(\boldsymbol{x})
.
\langle 0|
and
|0\rangle
are the vacuum vectors of the fermionic Fock spaces on which the creation-annihilation operators
\psi_{n}
,
\psi^{*}_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, of a 2D charged free fermions act.
\Gamma_{\pm}(\boldsymbol{x})
are multi-variate extensions

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\pm}(\boldsymbol{x})=\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}\Gamma_{\pm}(x_{i})

of the vertex operators
[
50
,
64
]

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\pm}(z)=\exp\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{z^{k}}{k}J_{\pm k}\right),

where
J_{k}
’s are basis elements of the
U(1)
current algebra:

\displaystyle J_{k}=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}{:}\psi_{k-n}\psi^{*}_{n}{:}.

Note that the Schur functions can be expressed as

\displaystyle s_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{x})=\langle 0|\Gamma_{+}(\boldsymbol{x})|\lambda\rangle=\langle\lambda|\Gamma_{-}(\boldsymbol{x})|0\rangle.

Assembling these building blocks, one obtains the fermionic expression (
2.4
) of
\tau(\boldsymbol{x})
. Moreover, by the Miwa transformation (
2.3
),
\Gamma_{+}(\boldsymbol{x})
turns into the generator

\displaystyle\gamma_{+}(\boldsymbol{t})=\exp\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}t_{k}J_{k}\right)

of time evolutions of the KP hierarchy. (
2.4
) thereby becomes the standard expression

\displaystyle\tau(\boldsymbol{x})=\big\langle 0|\gamma_{+}(\boldsymbol{t})\Gamma_{-}\big(q^{-\rho}\big)|0\big\rangle

of a KP tau function. The vector

\displaystyle|W\rangle=\Gamma_{-}\big(q^{-\rho}\big)|0\rangle

in the Fock space represents the associated point
W
of the Sato Grassmannian. This vector is generated from
|0\rangle
by the operator
g=\Gamma_{-}(q^{-\rho})
.

The generating operator
G
can be obtained by translating the operator
g
on the fermionic Fock space to an operator on the space
V
of Laurent series.
The rule of translation is based on the following well known correspondences
[
42
,
53
,
56
]
(see also Alexandrov’s detailed account
[
3
]
):

1.

The correspondence

\displaystyle\hat{A}=\sum_{i,j\in\mathbb{Z}}a_{ij}{:}\psi_{-i}\psi^{*}_{j}{:}\longleftrightarrow A=(a_{i,j})_{i,j\in\mathbb{Z}}

between fermion bilinears and
\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}
matrices
based on the commutation relations

\displaystyle[\hat{A},\hat{B}]=\widehat{[A,B]}+\text{$c$-number term}.

2.

The correspondence

\displaystyle D\longleftrightarrow\Delta=(i\delta_{ij})_{i,j\in\mathbb{Z}},

\displaystyle q^{D}\longleftrightarrow q^{\Delta}=(q^{i}\delta_{ij})_{i,j\in\mathbb{Z}},

\displaystyle x^{-k}\longleftrightarrow\Lambda^{k}=(\delta_{i+k,j})_{i,j\in\mathbb{Z}}

between differential/difference operators and
\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}
matrices based on the relations

\displaystyle D\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\phi_{i}x^{i}=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}i\phi_{i}x^{i},

\displaystyle q^{D}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\phi_{i}x^{i}=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}q^{i}\phi_{i}x^{i},

\displaystyle x^{-k}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\phi_{i}x^{i}=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\phi_{i+k}x^{i}.

By this rule, the operator

\displaystyle g=\Gamma_{-}(q^{-\rho})=\exp\left(\sum_{i,k=1}^{\infty}\frac{q^{k(i-1/2)}}{k}J_{-k}\right)

on the fermionic Fock space turns into the multiplication operator

\displaystyle G=\exp\left(\sum_{i,k=1}^{\infty}\frac{q^{k(i-1/2)}}{k}x^{k}\right)=\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}\big(1-q^{i-1/2}x\big)^{-1}

(2.5)

on
V
. The multiplier is a quantum dilogarithmic function. The admissible basis (
2.1
) consists of the functions

\displaystyle\Phi_{j}(x)=x^{-j}\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}\big(1-q^{i-1/2}x\big)^{-1}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{q^{k/2}}{(q;q)_{k}}x^{k-j},

(2.6)

where we have used the
q
-Pochhammer symbol

\displaystyle(a;q)_{k}=\begin{cases}(1-a)(1-aq)\cdots\big(1-aq^{k-1}\big)&\text{if $k>0$},\\
1&\text{if $k=0$}.\end{cases}

It is straightforward to compute the
q
-difference Kac–Schwarz operators (
1.3
) from (
2.5
). The operator
A=G\cdot q^{-D}\cdot G^{-1}
can be computed with the aid of the operator identity

\displaystyle q^{D}f(x)q^{-D}=f(qx)

(2.7)

as

\displaystyle A=q^{-D}\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}\big(1-q^{i+1/2}x\big)^{-1}\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}\big(1-q^{i-1/2}x\big)=q^{-D}\big(1-q^{1/2}x\big).

(2.8)

The operator
B=G\cdot x^{-1}\cdot G^{-1}
becomes a trivial one:

\displaystyle B=x^{-1}.

The
q
-difference equation (
2.2
) for
\Phi_{0}(x)
reads

\displaystyle q^{-D}\big(1-q^{1/2}x\big)\Phi_{0}(x)=\Phi_{0}(x)

or, equivalently,

\displaystyle\big(1-q^{1/2}x-q^{D}\big)\Phi_{0}(x)=0,

which agrees with the well known equation of the quantum mirror curve of
\mathbb{C}^{3}

[
1
,
2
]
.

2.3
Example: Resolved conifold

The foregoing construction of the operators
G
,
A
,
B
can be extended to the resolved conifold. The unnormalized open string amplitudes
Z_{\beta}
with a stack of branes on one of the external lines of the web diagram (Fig.
1
, right) can be expressed as

\displaystyle Z_{\beta}=s_{\,{\vphantom{\beta}}{}^{\mathrm{t}}\!\,\beta}\big(q^{-\rho}\big)\prod_{i,j=1}^{\infty}\big(1-Qq^{-\beta_{i}+i+j-1}\big),

where
Q
is the Kähler parameter of the internal line of the web diagram. We consider the generating function

\displaystyle\tau(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{\beta\in\mathcal{P}}s_{\,{\vphantom{\beta}}{}^{\mathrm{t}}\!\,\beta}(\boldsymbol{x})Z_{\beta}/Z_{\varnothing}=\sum_{\beta\in\mathcal{P}}s_{\,{\vphantom{\beta}}{}^{\mathrm{t}}\!\,\beta}(\boldsymbol{x})s_{\,{\vphantom{\beta}}{}^{\mathrm{t}}\!\,\beta}\big(q^{-\rho}\big)\prod_{i,j=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{1-Qq^{-\beta_{i}+i+j-1}}{1-Qq^{i+j-1}}

of the normalized amplitudes
Z_{\beta}/Z_{\varnothing}
.

To derive a fermionic expression of
\tau(\boldsymbol{x})
, we recall the following formula,
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots)\in\mathcal{P}
, from the previous work
[
61
]
:

\displaystyle\prod_{i,j=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{1-Qq^{-\lambda_{i}+i+j-1}}{1-Qq^{i+j-1}}=\left\langle\,{\vphantom{\lambda}}{}^{\mathrm{t}}\!\,\lambda\,|\,\exp\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{Q^{k}}{k(1-q^{k})}V^{(k)}_{0}\right)|\,\,{\vphantom{\lambda}}^{\mathrm{t}}\!\,\lambda\right\rangle.

(2.9)

\langle\lambda|
and
|\lambda\rangle
denote the excited states

\displaystyle\langle\lambda|=\langle-\infty\,|\,\cdots\psi^{*}_{\lambda_{i}-i+1}\cdots\psi^{*}_{\lambda_{2}-1}\psi^{*}_{\lambda_{1}},\qquad|\lambda\rangle=\psi_{-\lambda_{1}}\psi_{-\lambda_{2}+1}\cdots\psi_{-\lambda_{i}+i-1}\cdots\,|\,-\infty\rangle

in the charge-
0
sector of the Fock spaces. The ground states
\langle\varnothing|,|\varnothing\rangle
labeled by the zero partition are nothing but
the vacuum vectors
\langle 0|,|0\rangle
.
V^{(k)}_{0}
’s are the
m=0
part of the basis

\displaystyle V^{(k)}_{m}=q^{-km/2}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}q^{kn}{:}\psi_{m-n}\psi^{*}_{n}{:},\qquad k,m\in\mathbb{Z},

of the quantum torus algebra
[
47
,
49
]
. Since
V^{(k)}_{0}
’s do not mix the excited states, the fermionic expression (
2.9
) of the
Q
-dependent factor in the definition of
\tau(\boldsymbol{x})
can be merged to the fermionic expression

\displaystyle s_{\,{\vphantom{\beta}}{}^{\mathrm{t}}\!\,\beta}(q^{-\rho})=\big\langle\,{\vphantom{\beta}}^{\mathrm{t}}\!\,\beta\,|\,\Gamma_{-}(q^{-\rho})\,|\,0\big\rangle

of
s_{\,{\vphantom{\beta}}{}^{\mathrm{t}}\!\,\beta}(q^{-\rho})
as

\displaystyle\prod_{i,j=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{1-Qq^{-\beta_{i}+i+j-1}}{1-Qq^{i+j-1}}s_{\,{\vphantom{\beta}}{}^{\mathrm{t}}\!\,\beta}\big(q^{-\rho}\big)=\left\langle\,{\vphantom{\beta}}{}^{\mathrm{t}}\!\,\beta\,|\,\exp\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{Q^{k}}{k(1-q^{k})}V^{(k)}_{0}\right)\Gamma_{-}\big(q^{-\rho}\big)\,|\,0\right\rangle.

Thus
\tau(\boldsymbol{x})
can be expressed in the fermionic form

\displaystyle\tau(\boldsymbol{x})=\left\langle 0\,|\,\Gamma_{+}(\boldsymbol{x})\exp\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{Q^{k}}{k(1-q^{k})}V^{(k)}_{0}\right)\Gamma_{-}\big(q^{-\rho}\big)\,|\,0\right\rangle.

(2.10)

The associated point
W
of the Sato Grassmannian is determined by the vector

\displaystyle|W\rangle=\exp\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{Q^{k}}{k(1-q^{k})}V^{(k)}_{0}\right)\Gamma_{-}\big(q^{-\rho}\big)|0\rangle

in the fermionic Fock space.

The generating operator
G
of an admissible basis of
W
can be obtained by translating the generating operator

\displaystyle g=\exp\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{Q^{k}}{k(1-q^{k})}V^{(k)}_{0}\right)\Gamma_{-}\big(q^{-\rho}\big)

to an operator on
V
. We have seen that
\Gamma_{-}(q^{-\rho})
amounts to the multiplicative operator (
2.5
). As regard the first factor, since

\displaystyle V^{(k)}_{0}=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}q^{kn}{:}\psi_{-n}\psi^{*}_{n}{:}

corresponds to the
q
-shift operator
q^{kD}
, one can compute its avatar on
V
as

\displaystyle\exp\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{Q^{k}}{k(1-q^{k})}q^{kD}\right)=\exp\left(\sum_{i,k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(Qq^{i-1+D})^{k}}{k}\right)=\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}\big(1-Qq^{i-1+D}\big)^{-1}.

G
is a product of these two operators:

\displaystyle G=\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}\big(1-Qq^{i-1+D}\big)^{-1}\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}\big(1-q^{i-1/2}x\big)^{-1}.

(2.11)

To compute the admissible basis (
2.1
), one can partly make use of the results for
\mathbb{C}^{3}
. Namely, applying
G
to
x^{-j}
’s amount to applying

\prod\limits_{i=1}^{\infty}(1-Qq^{i-1+D})^{-1}
to the admissible basis (

) of
\mathbb{C}^{3}

\displaystyle\Phi_{j}(x)=\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}\big(1-Qq^{i-1+D}\big)^{-1}\left(x^{-j}\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}\big(1-q^{i-1/2}x\big)^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle\hphantom{\Phi_j(x)}{}=\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}\big(1-Qq^{i-1+D}\big)^{-1}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{q^{k/2}}{(q;q)_{k}}x^{k-j}\right)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{q^{k/2}}{(q;q)_{k}}x^{k-j}\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}\big(1-Qq^{i-1+k-j}\big)^{-1}.

Rewriting this expression slightly, one finds that

\displaystyle\Phi_{j}(x)=\frac{1}{\prod\limits_{i=1}^{\infty}(1-q^{i-j-1})}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(Qq^{-j};q)_{k}q^{k/2}}{(q;q)_{k}}x^{k-j}.

(2.12)

Thus the essential part of
\Phi_{j}(x)
’s turn out to be
q
-hypergeometric functions.

One can similarly cut unnecessary computation for the
q
-difference Kac–Schwarz operators (
1.3
). Namely, one can use (

) to rewrite

as

\displaystyle A=\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}\big(1-Qq^{i-1+D}\big)^{-1}\cdot q^{-D}\big(1-q^{1/2}x\big)\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}\big(1-Qq^{i-1+D}\big)

\displaystyle\hphantom{A=}{}=q^{-D}-q^{-D}q^{1/2}\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}\big(1-Qq^{i-1+D}\big)^{-1}\cdot x\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}\big(1-Qq^{i-1+D}\big).

By the operator identity

\displaystyle x^{-1}f(D)x=f(D+1),

(2.13)

the main part of the second term on the right side can be computed as

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}\big(1-Qq^{i-1+D}\big)^{-1}\cdot x\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}\big(1-Qq^{i-1+D}\big)=x\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}\big(1-Qq^{i+D}\big)^{-1}\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}\big(1-Qq^{i-1+D}\big)

Thus

can be expressed as

\displaystyle A=q^{-D}\big(1-q^{1/2}x\big(1-Qq^{D}\big)\big).

(2.14)

Moreover, this computation shows that
B
can be obtained as an inverse of

\displaystyle B=\big(1-Qq^{D}\big)^{-1}x^{-1}.

(2.15)

The

\displaystyle q^{-D}\big(1-q^{1/2}x\big(1-Qq^{D}\big)\big)\Phi_{0}(x)=\Phi_{0}(x).

This equation can be converted to the equation

\displaystyle\big(1-q^{1/2}x\big)\Phi_{0}(x)=\big(1-Qq^{1/2}x\big)\Phi_{0}(qx),

(2.16)

known in the literature

2.4
Relation to hypergeometric tau functions

(
2.4
) and (
2.10
) belong to a class of KP tau functions that can be expressed in the
\boldsymbol{x}
-variables as

\displaystyle\tau(\boldsymbol{x})=\big\langle 0\,|\,\Gamma_{+}(\boldsymbol{x})h\Gamma_{-}\big(q^{-\rho}\big)\,|\,0\big\rangle,

(2.17)

where
h
is an operator of the form

\displaystyle h=\exp\left(\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\log h_{n}{:}\psi_{-n}\psi^{*}_{n}{:}\right).

(2.18)

In the correspondence with
\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}
matrices,
h
amounts to a diagonal matrix, and the parameters
h_{n}
are nothing but the diagonal matrix elements.
The generating operator
G
on
V
corresponds to the generating operator
g=h\Gamma_{-}(q^{-\rho})
on the fermionic Fock space.

These KP tau functions can be derived from tau functions of the 2D Toda hierarchy of the form

\displaystyle\tau(s,\boldsymbol{t},\tilde{\boldsymbol{t}})=\big\langle s\,|\,\gamma_{+}(\boldsymbol{t})h\gamma_{-}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{t}})\,|\,s\big\rangle,

(2.19)

where

\displaystyle\gamma_{-}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{t}})=\exp\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\tilde{t}_{k}J_{-k}\right),

by specialization to

\displaystyle s=0,\qquad t_{k}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}x_{i}^{k},\qquad\tilde{t}_{k}=\frac{q^{k/2}}{k(1-q^{k})}.

Toda tau functions of the form (
2.19
), called “hypergeometric tau functions”, were introduced by Orlov and Scherbin in their study on multi-variate hypergeometric functions
[
51
,
52
]
. As pointed out therein (see also the work of Kharchev, Marshakov, Mironov and Morozov
[
37
]
in a different context),
h
is diagonal with respect to the basis
\{|\lambda\rangle\}_{\lambda\in\mathcal{P}}
of the charge-
0
sector, and the diagonal matrix elements have the contents-product form

\displaystyle\langle\lambda\,|\,h\,|\,\lambda\rangle=\prod_{(i,j)\in\lambda}r_{j-i+1},

where

\displaystyle r_{n}=\frac{h_{n}}{h_{n-1}}.

Since the work Okounkov
[
48
]
, hypergeometric tau functions have played a significant role as generating functions of Hurwitz numbers
and many other combinatorial notions
[
5
,
24
,
25
,
27
,
28
,
30
,
44
,
59
,
65
]
(see also Harnad’s comprehensive review
[
29
]
).

In the case of the resolved conifold, see (
2.10
), the operator
h
can be expressed as

\displaystyle h=\exp\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{Q^{k}}{k(1-q^{k})}V^{(k)}_{0}\right).

Since

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{Q^{k}}{k(1-q^{k})}V^{(k)}_{0}=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{Q^{k}q^{kn}}{k(1-q^{k})}{:}\psi_{-n}\psi^{*}_{n}{:},

the parameters
h_{n}
,
r_{n}
can be computed as

\displaystyle h_{n}=\exp\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{Q^{k}q^{kn}}{k(1-q^{k})}\right)=\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}\big(1-Qq^{i-1+n}\big)^{-1}

and

\displaystyle r_{n}=1-Qq^{n-1}.

This belongs to a case considered by Orlov and Scherbin in the context of
q
-hypergeometric functions. As we show in the next section, tau functions in more general strip geometry, too, have a similar interpretation.

3
Strip geometry

3.1
Generalities

The toric diagram of strip geometry (Fig.

The legs other than the leftmost and rightmost ones are vertical. Let
Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{N-1}
denote the Kähler parameters assigned to the internal lines.

Web diagram (solid) and toric diagram (gray) of strip geometry.

Let

\alpha_{0}

\alpha_{N}
are partitions on the leftmost and rightmost legs, and

In the case where

Z_{2,\beta}/Z
turns out to be expressed as

\displaystyle Z_{2,\beta}/Z=\big\langle 0\,|\,g_{2}\,|\,\,{\vphantom{\beta}}^{\mathrm{t}}\!\,\beta\big\rangle,

where

\displaystyle g_{2}=\Gamma_{+}\big({-}Q_{1}Q_{2}Q_{3}q^{-\rho}\big)\Gamma^{\prime}_{+}\big(Q_{2}Q_{3}q^{-\rho}\big)\Gamma_{+}\big({-}Q_{2}q^{-\rho}\big)\Gamma^{\prime}_{+}\big(q^{-\rho}\big)

\displaystyle\hphantom{g_2 =}{}\times\exp\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(Q_{1}Q_{2}q)^{k}}{k(1-q^{k})}V^{(-k)}_{0}\right)q^{-K/2}.

(4.4)

These expressions of the normalized amplitudes lead us to the following conclusion.

Theorem 4.1
.

\tau_{1}(\boldsymbol{x})
and
\tau_{2}(\boldsymbol{x})
are tau functions of the KP hierarchy with the fermionic expression

\displaystyle\tau_{1}(\boldsymbol{x})=\big\langle 0\,|\,\Gamma_{+}(\boldsymbol{x})g_{1}\,|\,0\big\rangle,\qquad\tau_{2}(\boldsymbol{x})=\big\langle 0\,|\,g_{2}\Gamma_{-}(\boldsymbol{x})\,|\,0\big\rangle,

where
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
are the operators defined in (
4.3
) and (
4.4
).

Remark 4.2
.

The fermionic expression of
\tau_{2}(\boldsymbol{x})
can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\tau_{2}(\boldsymbol{x})=\big\langle 0\,|\,\Gamma_{+}(\boldsymbol{x})\,{\vphantom{g_{2}}}^{\mathrm{t}}\!\,g_{2}\,|\,0\big\rangle,

where

\,{\vphantom{g_{2}}}{}^{\mathrm{t}}\!\,g_{2}=q^{-K/2}\exp\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(Q_{1}Q_{2}q)^{k}}{k(1-q^{k})}V^{(-k)}_{0}\right)

\displaystyle\hphantom{\,{\vphantom{g_2}}^\mathrm{t}\!\,g_2 =}{}\times\Gamma^{\prime}_{-}\big(q^{-\rho}\big)\Gamma_{-}\big({-}Q_{2}q^{-\rho}\big)\Gamma^{\prime}_{-}\big(Q_{2}Q_{3}q^{-\rho}\big)\Gamma_{-}\big({-}Q_{1}Q_{2}Q_{3}q^{-\rho}\big).

4.2
Admissible basis and Kac–Schwarz operators

Since all building blocks of
\tau_{1}(\boldsymbol{x})
and
\tau_{2}(\boldsymbol{x})
are now familiar, one can derive the associated admissible bases and
q
-difference Kac–Schwarz operators in almost the same way as we have done in the preceding sections. Actually, the computations are partly more complicated, and exhibit a new aspect that is characteristic of this case. Let us consider
\tau_{1}(\boldsymbol{x})
to illustrate the computations.

The generating operator
G
of an admissible basis
\{\Phi_{j}(x)\}_{j=0}^{\infty}
for
\tau_{1}(\boldsymbol{x})

takes such a form as

\displaystyle G=\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}\big(1-Q_{1}Q_{2}q^{i-1+D}\big)\cdot\Phi(x),

where
\Phi(x)
is the composite dilogarithmic function

\displaystyle\Phi(x)=\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{(1-Q_{1}q^{i-1/2}x)(1-Q_{1}Q_{2}Q_{3}q^{i-1/2}x)}{(1-q^{i-1/2}x)(1-Q_{1}q_{3}q^{i-1/2}x)}.

The admissible basis is generated from
\Phi(x)
as

\displaystyle\Phi_{j}(x)=\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}\big(1-Q_{1}Q_{2}q^{i-1+D}\big)\big(x^{-j}\Phi(x)\big).

\Phi_{j}(x)
’s are neither quantum dilogarithmic nor
q
-hypergeometric. Power series expansion of these functions take such a form as

\displaystyle\Phi_{j}(x)=\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}\big(1-Q_{1}Q_{2}q^{i-1-j}\big)\cdot\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{b_{k}}{(Q_{1}Q_{2}q^{-j};q)_{k}}x^{k-j},

(4.5)

where
b_{k}
’s are coefficients of the power series expansion

\displaystyle\Phi(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}b_{k}x^{k}

of
\Phi(x)
. Comparing the expansion of
\Phi_{0}(x)
with the expansion of the generating function
\Psi_{1}(x)
presented in our previous work
[
61
]
,
one can confirm that
\Phi_{0}(x)
agrees with
\Psi_{1}(x)
except for the constant multiplier
\prod\limits_{i=1}^{\infty}(1-Q_{1}Q_{2}q^{i-1})
.

In the previous work, we derived a
q
-difference equation for
\Psi_{1}(x)
from the
q
-difference equation

\displaystyle\Phi(qx)=\frac{(1-q^{1/2}x)(1-Q_{1}Q_{3}q^{1/2}x)}{(1-Q_{1}q^{1/2}x)(1-Q_{1}Q_{2}Q_{3}q^{1/2}x)}\Phi(x)

(4.6)

for
\Phi(x)
by a quite primitive method. The notion of Kac–Schwarz operators leads us to a more systematic derivation of the
q
-difference equation.

Let us proceed to the
q
-difference Kac–Schwarz operators. It is easy to compute
B=G\cdot x^{-1}\cdot G^{-1}
:

\displaystyle B=\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}\big(1-Q_{1}Q_{2}q^{i-1+D}\big)\cdot x^{-1}\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}\big(1-Q_{1}Q_{2}q^{i-1+D}\big)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}\big(1-Q_{1}Q_{2}q^{i-1+D}\big)\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}\big(1-Q_{1}Q_{2}q^{i+D}\big)^{-1}\cdot x^{-1}=\big(1-Q_{1}Q_{2}q^{D}\big)\cdot x^{-1}.

(4.7)

To compute
A=G\cdot q^{-D}\cdot G^{-1}
, one can start from the algebraic relation

\displaystyle\Phi(x)\cdot q^{-D}\cdot\Phi(x)^{-1}=q^{-D}\frac{(1-q^{1/2}x)(1-Q_{1}Q_{3}q^{1/2}x)}{(1-Q_{1}q^{1/2}x)(1-Q_{1}Q_{2}Q_{3}q^{1/2}x)},

which is the
q
-difference equation (
4.6
) in disguise. This relation yields the following intermediate expression of
A
:

\displaystyle A=q^{-D}\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}\big(1-Q_{1}Q_{2}q^{i-1+D}\big)\cdot\frac{(1-q^{1/2}x)(1-Q_{1}Q_{3}q^{1/2}x)}{(1-Q_{1}q^{1/2}x)(1-Q_{1}Q_{2}Q_{3}q^{1/2}x)}

\displaystyle\hphantom{A=}{}\times\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}\big(1-Q_{1}Q_{2}q^{i-1+D}\big)^{-1}.

Since (
4.7
) implies that

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}\big(1-Q_{1}Q_{2}q^{i-1+D}\big)\cdot x\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}\big(1-Q_{1}Q_{2}q^{i-1+D}\big)^{-1}=x\big(1-Q_{1}Q_{2}q^{D}\big)^{-1},

one can rewrite the foregoing intermediate expression of
A
as

\displaystyle A=q^{-D}\frac{\left(1-q^{1/2}x(1-Q_{1}Q_{2}q^{D})^{-1}\right)\left(1-Q_{1}Q_{3}q^{1/2}x(1-Q_{1}Q_{2}q^{D})^{-1}\right)}{\left(1-Q_{1}q^{1/2}x(1-Q_{1}Q_{2}q^{D})^{-1}\right)\left(1-Q_{1}Q_{2}Q_{3}q^{1/2}x(1-Q_{1}Q_{2}q^{D})^{-1}\right)}.

(4.8)

Let us summarize these computations. Although we omit details, similar results hold for
\tau_{2}(\boldsymbol{x})
as well.

Theorem 4.3
.

(
4.7
) and (
4.8
) are
q
-difference Kac–Schwarz operators for
\tau_{1}(\boldsymbol{x})
. (
4.5
) give an admissible basis
of the associated point of the Sato Grassmannian.

4.3
Remarks on
\boldsymbol{q}
-difference equations

Let us rewrite the equation
A\Phi_{0}(x)=\Phi_{0}(x)
for
\Phi_{0}(x)
as

\displaystyle\big(1-q^{1/2}x\big(1-Q_{1}Q_{2}q^{D}\big)^{-1}\big)\big(1-Q_{1}Q_{3}q^{1/2}x\big(1-Q_{1}Q_{2}q^{D}\big)^{-1}\big)\Phi_{0}(x)

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\big(1-Q_{1}q^{1/2}x\big(1-Q_{1}Q_{2}q^{D}\big)^{-1}\big)\big(1-Q_{1}Q_{2}Q_{3}q^{1/2}x\big(1-Q_{1}Q_{2}q^{D}\big)^{-1}\big)q^{D}\Phi_{0}(x).\!\!\!

(4.9)

Although this equation looks considerably different from the
q
-difference equation presented in our previous work
[
61
]
, they are equivalent
as we show below.

Lemma 4.4
.

For any constants
P_{1}
,
P_{2}
and
Q
,

\displaystyle\big(1-P_{1}x\big(1-Qq^{D}\big)^{-1}\big)\big(1-P_{2}x\big(1-Qq^{D}\big)^{-1}\big)

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\big(1-Qq^{D-1}\big)^{-1}\big(1-Qq^{D-2}\big)^{-1}\big(1-Qq^{D-2}-P_{1}x\big)\big(1-Qq^{D-1}-P_{2}x\big).

Proof.

Do straightforward computations using (
2.13
) as follows:

\displaystyle\big(1-P_{1}x\big(1-Qq^{D}\big)^{-1}\big)\big(1-P_{2}x\big(1-Qq^{D}\big)^{-1}\big)

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\big(1-P_{1}\big(1-Qq^{D-1}\big)^{-1}x\big)\big(1-P_{2}\big(1-Qq^{D-1}\big)^{-1}x\big)

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\big(1-Qq^{D-1}\big)^{-1}\big(1-Qq^{D-1}-P_{1}x\big)\big(1-Qq^{D-1}\big)^{-1}\big(1-Qq^{D-1}-P_{2}x\big)

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\big(1-Qq^{D-1}\big)^{-1}\big(1-P_{1}x\big(1-Qq^{D-1}\big)^{-1}\big)\big(1-Qq^{D-1}-P_{2}x\big)

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\big(1-Qq^{D-1}\big)^{-1}\big(1-P_{1}\big(1-Qq^{D-2}x\big)^{-1}x\big)\big(1-Qq^{D-1}-P_{2}x\big)

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\big(1-Qq^{D-1}\big)^{-1}\big(1-Qq^{D-2}x\big)^{-1}\big(1-Qq^{D-2}-P_{1}x\big)\big(1-Qq^{D-1}-P_{2}x\big).

∎

One can use this lemma to rewrite both sides of (
4.9
) and collect terms to the left side. The outcome reads

\displaystyle\big(1-Q_{1}Q_{2}q^{D-1}\big)^{-1}\big(1-Q_{1}Q_{2}q^{D-2}\big)^{-1}\hat{H}\Phi_{0}(x)=0,

(4.10)

where

\displaystyle\hat{H}=\big(1-Q_{1}Q_{2}q^{D-2}-q^{1/2}x\big)\big(1-Q_{1}Q_{2}q^{D-1}-Q_{1}Q_{3}q^{1/2}x\big)

\displaystyle\hphantom{\hat{H}=}{}-\big(1-Q_{1}Q_{2}q^{D-2}-Q_{1}q^{1/2}x\big)\big(1-Q_{1}Q_{2}q^{D-1}-Q_{1}Q_{2}Q_{3}q^{1/2}x\big)q^{D}.

We are thus substantially in the same situation as encountered in the previous work. Namely, as far as
Q_{1}Q_{2}
is not equal to integral powers of
q
(and this condition is assumed implicitly in the foregoing consideration), the two operators in front of

\displaystyle\hat{H}\Phi_{0}(x)=0,

(4.11)

which is one of the equivalent forms of the
q
-difference equations derived in the previous work. Moreover, as pointed out therein,
\hat{H}
itself can be factorized as

\displaystyle\hat{H}=\big(1-Q_{1}Q_{2}q^{D-2}\big)\hat{K},

where
\hat{K}

4.11

\displaystyle\hat{K}\Phi_{0}(x)=0.

(4.12)

It is this equation (

\displaystyle\big(1-q^{1/2}x\big(1-Q_{1}Q_{2}q^{D}\big)^{-1}\big)\big(1-Q_{1}Q_{3}q^{1/2}x\big(1-Q_{1}Q_{2}q^{D}\big)^{-1}\big)

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\big(1-Q_{1}Q_{2}q^{D-1}\big)^{-1}\hat{K}.
