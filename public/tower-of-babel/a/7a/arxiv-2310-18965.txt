Power of Counting by Nonuniform Families of


Abstract
Lately, there have been intensive studies on strengths and limitations of nonuniform families of promise decision problems solvable by various types of polynomial-size finite automata families, where “polynomial-size” refers to the polynomially-bounded state complexity of a finite automata family. In this line of study, we further expand the scope of these studies to families of partial counting and gap functions, defined in terms of nonuniform families of polynomial-size nondeterministic finite automata, and their relevant families of promise decision problems. Counting functions have an ability of counting the number of accepting computation paths produced by nondeterministic finite automata. With no unproven hardness assumption, we show numerous separations and collapses of complexity classes of those partial counting and gap function families and their induced promise decision problem families. We also investigate their relationships to pushdown automata families of polynomial stack-state complexity. Key words. nonuniform polynomial state complexity, counting functions, gap functions, counting complexity classes, closure properties, stack-state complexity

Background and New Approach

We quickly review basic background knowledge on counting and gap functions and discuss a new approach taken in this work toward the better understandings of counting.

1.1
Counting by Resource-Bounded Machines

Computing the permanent of a given positive-integer matrix and the number of perfect matchings of a given graph are well-known tasks of “counting” the total number of specified elements in a given set of objects.

Counting
is undoubtedly one of the most intensively studied subjects in computational complexity theory.
The centerpiece of the subsequent research has pivoted around two crucial notions of
counting function
that computes the total number of accepting computation paths of each run of an underlying nondeterministic machine, and
gap function
that computes the difference between the number of accepting computation paths and the number of rejecting computation paths.
Counting functions were formally introduced in the late 1990s by Valiant
[
21
,
22
]
and gap functions were studied in the mid 1990s by Fenner, Fortnow, and Kurtz
[
9
]
, founded on the mathematical model of polynomial-time nondeterministic Turing machines.

The nature of counting has been intensively studied for various resource-bounded computations. Here, we wish to look into “counting” specifically in low-complexity settings.
In the logarithmic-space (or log-space, for short) setting, for instance, Àlvarez and Jenner
[
2
]
and Allender and Ogihara
[
1
]
studied the behaviors and structural properties of logarithmic-space counting and gap functions, whose collections are succinctly denoted
\mathrm{\#\mathrm{L}}
and
\mathrm{GapL}
. Computing the determinant of a given integer matrix is a typical gap function in
\mathrm{GapL}
.
Counting and gap functions can be viewed as useful tools to formally introduce so-called
counting complexity classes
, such as
\mathrm{UL}
,
\mathrm{C}_{=}\mathrm{L}
,
\oplus\mathrm{L}
, and
\mathrm{PL}
, which were intensively studied in the 1980s and 1990s by Allender and Ogihara
[

[

[

A study of the classes of counting and gap functions and their corresponding counting complexity classes has been expanded into more restrictive models of Turing machines in the past literature. One of such restrictive models is
one-tape linear-time Turing machines
, which have been studied since Hennie’s fundamental result on their recognition power
[
8
]
.
A significant feature of this model is exemplified by the fact
that, for one-tape time-bounded Turing machines,
O(n\log{n})
execution time provides significantly more power to the linear-time computation; however,
o(n\log{n})
execution time does not outpower the linear-time computation
[

19

In general, the one-tape linear-time restriction makes a significant effect on the overall behaviors of underlying Turing machines. This effect also contributes to the computational power of counting and gap functions introduced in terms of these restrictive machines.
In 2004, using this restrictive model of computation, Tadaki, Yamakami, and Li
[
19

initiated a study on counting and gap functions whose collections are respectively known as

\mathrm{1\mbox{-}C_{=}LIN}

\mathrm{1\mbox{-}SPLIN}

\mathrm{1\mbox{-}PLIN}

Since Valiant’s notion of counting functions tells the number of accepting computation paths of NTMs, the study of counting is deeply rooted at the nature of nondeterminism, and thus
those counting complexity classes seem to tailor the characteristics of nondeterminism. In this regard, the counting complexity classes
1\mbox{-}\!\oplus\!\mathrm{LIN}
,
\mathrm{1\mbox{-}C_{=}LIN}

\mathrm{1\mbox{-}SPLIN}

In computational complexity theory, “nonuniformity” has played a distinguishing role. Nonuniform families of polynomial-size circuits,
for instance, have been treated as a nonuniform version of
\mathrm{P}

Nonuniformity can be also implemented in terms of “advice”, which is an external source of information to enhance the computational power of underlying machines.

A further expansion of the scope of this theory has been expected to promote our basic understandings of the theory.
Moreover, the study of such nonuniform families may benefit the promotion of the understandings of a restrictive model of machines, such as one-tape linear-time machines.
In the case of two-way finite automata, for instance, it was frequently argued in

Along the line of the aforementioned literature, we wish to expand the scope of the study on counting and gap functions and their induced decision problems by taking a new, distinctive approach with nonuniform families of nondeterministic finite automata of polynomially-bounded state complexity. 3
Introduction of Counting Functions and Counting Complexity Classes

Let us introduce two main complexity classes,

3.1
Definitions of Counting and Gap Functions

We define two counting and gap function classes
1\#
and
1\mathrm{Gap}
using families of 1nfa’s as follows.
The counting function class
1\#
(pronounced “one sharp” or “one pound”) is the collection of all families
\{(f_{n},D_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of partial functions such that there exists a nonuniform family
\{M_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of polynomial-size 1nfa’s for which

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and all
x\in D_{n}
.
In contrast,
1\mathrm{Gap}
is defined to be the collection of all families
\{(f_{n},D_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of partial functions characterized as

for all
x\in D_{n}
for an appropriately chosen nonuniform family
\{M_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of polynomial-size 1nfa’s.
Additionally,
1\mathrm{Gap}_{\geq 0}
denotes the class of all partial functions in
1\mathrm{Gap}
that take only nonnegative integer values unless their outputs are undefined.

1\mathrm{D}
.

To show that
1\#\subseteq 1\mathrm{Gap}_{\geq 0}
, let
{\cal F}=\{(f_{n},D_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be any family in
1\#
. Take a family
{\cal M}=\{M_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of 1nfa’s that witnesses “
{\cal F}\in 1\#
”. Consider the following 1nfa’s
N_{n}
. On input
x
, simulate
M_{n}
. If
M_{n}
enters an accepting state on each path, then
N_{n}
does the same. Otherwise,
N_{n}
branches into two computation paths and accepts on one path and rejects on the other. This implies that
{\cal F}\in 1\mathrm{Gap}_{\geq 0}
.

(2) Since
1\mathrm{F}_{\mathbb{Z}}
contains families of partial functions handling negative values,
1\mathrm{F}_{\mathbb{Z}}
is not included in
1\mathrm{Gap}_{\geq 0}
.
For the inclusion
1\mathrm{F}_{\mathbb{Z}}\subseteq 1\mathrm{Gap}
, its proof is in essence similar to that of (1) but we produce exactly
f_{n}(x)
more accepting computation paths than rejecting ones if
f_{n}(x)\geq 0
, and exactly
f_{n}(x)
more rejecting computation paths than accepting ones otherwise.

\Box

Given two function classes
{\cal U}
and
{\cal V}
, the special notation
{\cal U}-{\cal V}
denotes the collection of families
\{(f_{n},D_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that there are two families
{\cal G}=\{(g_{n},E_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in{\cal U}
and
{\cal G}^{\prime}=\{(g^{\prime}_{n},E^{\prime}_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in{\cal V}
for which
D_{n}=E_{n}\cap E^{\prime}_{n}
and
f_{n}=g_{n}-g^{\prime}_{n}
(i.e.,
f_{n}(x)=g_{n}(x)-g^{\prime}_{n}(x)
for all
x\in D_{n}
) for any index
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
With this notation,
1\mathrm{Gap}
is succinctly expressed in terms of
1\#
as follows.

Lemma 3.3

(1)
1\mathrm{Gap}=1\#-1\#=1\#-1\mathrm{F}_{\mathbb{N}}=1\mathrm{F}_{\mathbb{N}}-1\#
. (2)
1\mathrm{Gap}=1\mathrm{Gap}-1\mathrm{Gap}
.

Proof.

(1) We want to show that
1\mathrm{Gap}\subseteq 1\#-1\#
.
We begin with any family
{\cal F}=\{(f_{n},D_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
1\mathrm{Gap}
. Take a family
{\cal M}=\{M_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of polynomial-size 1nfa’s witnessing the fact of “
{\cal F}\in 1\mathrm{Gap}
”. We define
N_{n}
as
\overline{M}_{n}
(which is obtained from
M_{n}
by flipping accepting and rejecting states). Define
g_{n}(x)=\#M_{n}(x)
and
h_{n}(x)=\#N_{n}(x)
for all strings
x\in D_{n}
. Clearly,
{\cal G}=\{(g_{n},D_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
{\cal H}=\{(h_{n},D_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are both in
1\#
. Since
f_{n}=g_{n}-h_{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we conclude that
{\cal F}\in 1\#-1\#
.

We next want to show that
1\#-1\#\subseteq 1\#-1\mathrm{F}_{\mathbb{N}}
. Take two families
{\cal G}=\{(g_{n},D_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
{\cal G}^{\prime}=\{(g^{\prime}_{n},D_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
1\#
.
There are two families
{\cal M}=\{M_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
{\cal N}=\{N_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfying both
g_{n}(x)=\#M_{n}(x)
and
g^{\prime}_{n}(x)=\#N(x)
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and all
x\in D_{n}
.
Let us take the partial function
h_{n}
defined by
h_{n}(x)=g_{n}(x)-g^{\prime}_{n}(x)
for all
x\in D_{n}
. We need to verify that
{\cal H}=\{(h_{n},D_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
belongs to
1\#-1\mathrm{F}_{\mathbb{Z}}
.
By Lemma
2.1
, we can assume that
N_{n}
makes exactly
c
nondeterministic choices at every step. This instantly implies that
\#N_{n}(x)+\#\overline{N}_{n}(x)=c^{|x|+2}
, where
c
is an appropriate positive constant.
We then define a 1nfa
H_{n}
, which works as follows. On input
x
,
H_{n}
first branches out into two computation paths and then simulates
M_{n}
on one branch and
\overline{N}_{n}
on the other.
It follows that
h_{n}(x)=\#M_{n}(x)-\#N_{n}(x)=\#M_{n}(x)-(c^{|x|+2}-\#\overline{N}_{n}(x))=(\#M_{n}(x)+\#\overline{N}_{n}(x))-c^{|x|+2}
. By setting
f_{n}(x)=\#M_{n}(x)+\#\overline{N}_{n}(x)
, it is not difficult to verify that
\{(f_{n},D_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is in
1\#
.
Since
h_{n}(x)=f_{n}(x)-c^{|x|+2}
,
{\cal H}
belongs to
1\#-1\mathrm{F}_{\mathbb{N}}
.

With a similar idea, we can show that
1\#-1\#\subseteq 1\mathrm{F}_{\mathbb{N}}-1\#
.
Since
1\mathrm{F}_{\mathbb{N}}\subseteq 1\#
by Lemma
3.2
(1), it follows that
1\#-1\mathrm{F}_{\mathbb{N}}\subseteq 1\#-1\#
and
1\mathrm{F}_{\mathbb{N}}-1\#\subseteq 1\#-1\#
.

Finally, we want to verify that
1\#-1\#\subseteq 1\mathrm{Gap}
.
Take two families
{\cal M}=\{M_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
{\cal N}=\{N_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfying
f_{n}(x)=\#M_{n}(x)-\#N_{n}(x)
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and all valid
x
. Define a new 1nfa
H_{n}
in the following way. On input
x
, branch out into two computation paths. On one path, simulate
M_{n}
; on the other path, simulate
N_{n}
. If
M_{n}
accepts, then accept the input. If
M_{n}
rejects
x
, then branch out into two distinct configurations, one of which leads to an accepting state and the other leads to a rejecting state. A similar treatment is applied to
N_{n}
but “accept” and “reject” should be exchanged.

(2) It is obvious that
1\mathrm{Gap}\subseteq 1\mathrm{Gap}-1\mathrm{Gap}
. To show the converse inclusion, let us consider a family
{\cal H}=\{(h_{n},D_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
1\mathrm{Gap}-1\mathrm{Gap}
. There are two families
\{(f_{n},D_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\{(g_{n},D_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
1\mathrm{Gap}
satisfying
h_{n}=f_{n}-g_{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. From (1), these two families are further characterized by four families
\{(f_{n}^{(i)},D_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\{(g_{n}^{(i)},D_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
for
i\in\{1,2\}
in
1\#
defined by
f_{n}=f_{n}^{(1)}-f_{n}^{(2)}
and
g_{n}=g_{n}^{(1)}-g_{n}^{(2)}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. We then obtain
f_{n}-g_{n}=(f_{n}^{(1)}-f_{n}^{(2)})-(g_{n}^{(1)}-g_{n}^{(2)})=(f_{n}^{(1)}+g_{n}^{(2)})-(f_{n}^{(2)}+g_{n}^{(1)})
. It thus suffices to define
h_{n}^{(1)}=f_{n}^{(1)}+g_{n}^{(2)}
and
h_{n}^{(2)}=f_{n}^{(2)}+g_{n}^{(1)}
.
By combining two
1\#
-function families, we can show that
\{(h_{n}^{(1)},D_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\{(h_{n}^{(2)},D_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
belong to
1\#
. Since
h_{n}=h_{n}^{(1)}-h_{n}^{(2)}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we conclude by (1) that
{\cal H}
is in
1\mathrm{Gap}
.
∎

We next examine closure properties of
1\#
associated with
homomorphisms
. Given two alphabets
\Sigma
and
\Gamma
, a homomorphism
h:\Sigma\to\Gamma^{*}
is said to be
non-erasing
if
h(\sigma)\neq\lambda
for all
\sigma\in\Sigma
, and
h
is called
prefix-free
if there is no pair
\sigma,\tau\in\Sigma
such that
h(\sigma)
is a proper prefix of
h(\tau)
. As usual,
h
is naturally expanded to a map from
\Sigma^{*}
to
\Gamma^{*}
. The
inverse
of
h
, denoted
h^{-1}
, is defined as
h^{-1}(y)=\{x\in\Sigma^{*}\mid h(x)=y\}
for any
y\in\Gamma^{*}
.
It is important to note that the inverse
h^{-1}
of a non-erasing prefix-free homomorphism
h
becomes a function. Here, we wish to apply homomorphisms and inverse homomorphisms to partial functions.
We write
K
for either the set of all homomorphisms or the set of all inverses of non-erasing prefix-free homomorphisms. We say that a class of families of partial functions is
closed under

K
if, for any element
k
in
K
and for any
{\cal F}=\{(f_{n},D_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in this class, the family
\{(l_{n},C_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
also belongs to the class, where
C_{n}=\{x\in\Sigma^{*}\mid k(x)\in D_{n}\}
and
l_{n}(x)=f_{n}(k(x))
for all
x\in C_{n}
.

Lemma 3.4

1\#
is closed under both homomorphism and inverse of non-erasing prefix-free homomorphism.

Proof.

We first consider the case of homomorphisms.
Take any homomorphism
k:\Gamma\to\Sigma^{*}
and expand it to a map from
\Gamma^{*}
to
\Sigma^{*}
in a standard way. We then define
C_{n}=\{x\in\Sigma^{*}\mid k(x)\in D_{n}\}
and
l_{n}(x)=f_{n}(k(x))
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and all strings
x\in C_{n}
. To show that
\{(l_{n},C_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in 1\#
, it suffices to simulate an underlying 1dft computing
f_{n}
after executing
k
. Since
k
does not depend on
n
, we can embed the behavior of
k
into the desired simulator 1dft.

We next consider the case of inverses of non-erasing prefix-free homomorphisms.
Let us consider a family
{\cal F}=\{(f_{n},D_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of partial functions over alphabet, say,
\Sigma
in
1\#
and a non-erasing prefix-free homomorphism
h:\Sigma\to\Gamma^{*}
. As usual, we naturally expand
h
to a map from
\Sigma^{*}
to
\Gamma^{*}
. Since
h^{-1}
becomes a function, we can define
E_{n}=\{y\in\Gamma^{*}\mid h^{-1}(y)\in D_{n}\}
, which equals
\{h(x)\mid x\in D_{n}\}
. We further set
g_{n}(y)=f_{n}(h^{-1}(y))
for all
y\in E_{n}
. For the obtained family
{\cal G}=\{(g_{n},E_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, we wish to verify that
{\cal G}
also belongs to
1\#
.

For this purpose, let
\{M_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
denote a family of polynomial-size 1dft’s “witnessing” the membership of
{\cal F}
to
1\#
. By modifying each 1dft
M_{n}
, we introduce another 1dft
N_{n}
as follows. Let
H_{\Sigma}=\{h(\sigma)\mid\sigma\in\Sigma\}
. On input
y\in\Gamma^{*}
,
N_{n}
moves its tape head over
y
from left to right and identifies a prefix, say,
z
of
y
that appears in
H_{\Sigma}
. Since
|z|
is constantly bounded, it is possible to remember
z
using
N_{n}
’s inner states.
Since
h
is non-erasing and prefix-free,
z
is not empty and there exists a unique symbol
\sigma\in\Sigma
satisfying
h(\sigma)=z
.
The machine
N_{n}
then runs
M_{n}
on
\sigma
to obtain its outcome.
We repeat this procedure on the rest of
y
until
N_{n}
completes the simulation of
M_{n}
’s computation on the entire string
h^{-1}(y)
. In the end,
N_{n}
produces
g_{n}(y)
. This demonstrates that
{\cal G}
is indeed in
1\#
.
∎

We further seek out closure properties under various functional operations. Such properties were discussed by Ogiwara and Hemachandra
[
16
]
in the polynomial-time setting for
\#\mathrm{P}
and
\mathrm{GapP}
.

Let
\circ
denote any binary functional operation. Given two partial functions
f
and
g
from
\Sigma^{*}
to
\mathbb{N}
(or
\mathbb{Z}
) for a fixed alphabet
\Sigma
, we define new partial function
f\circ g
by setting
(f\circ g)(x)=f(x)\circ g(x)
for all valid strings
x
in
\Sigma^{*}
.
Let
{\cal U}
denote a collection of families of partial functions. We say that
{\cal U}
is
closed under

\circ
if, for any two families
{\cal F}=\{(f_{n},D_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
{\cal G}=\{(g_{n},E_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
{\cal U}
, another family
\{(f_{n}\circ g_{n},D_{n}\cap E_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
also belongs to
{\cal U}
.
As long as there is no confusion, we express
\{(f_{n}\circ g_{n},D_{n}\cap E_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
as
{\cal F}\circ{\cal G}
for brevity.

Lemma 3.5

The function classes
1\#
and
1\mathrm{Gap}
are closed under addition and multiplication.

Proof.

(1) We first focus on the case of
1\#
. Given any two function families
{\cal F}=\{(f_{n},D_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
{\cal G}=\{(g_{n},E_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
1\#
, we take two families
\{M_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\{N_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of polynomial-size 1nfa’s that “witness”
{\cal F}
and
{\cal G}
, respectively. We intend to prove that
{\cal F}\circ{\cal G}
is also in
1\#
for any operator
\circ\in\{+,*\}
. For simplicity, we assume that
M_{n}
and
N_{n}
halts at reading
\lhd
.

For “addition”, we define a new 1nfa
H_{n}
from
M_{n}
and
N_{n}
as follows. On input
x
,
H_{n}
first branches out into two computation paths and then simulates
M_{n}
on one path and
N_{n}
on the other path. Clearly,
H_{n}
produces
\#M_{n}(x)+\#N_{n}(x)
accepting computation paths. Hence, if we set
h_{n}(x)=\#H_{n}(x)
for all valid
x
and set
{\cal F}+{\cal G}=\{(h_{n},D_{n}\cap E_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, then
{\cal F}+{\cal G}
belongs to
1\#
. We remark that, by the definition of partial functions, we do not need to determine whether the input
x
belongs to
D_{n}\cap E_{n}
or not.

For “multiplication”, we define another 1nfa
K_{n}
in the following fashion. On input
x
,
K_{n}
simulates
M_{n}
and
N_{n}
simultaneously by keeping heir own inner states together. This is possible because there is no
\lambda
-move conducted by
M_{n}
and
N_{n}
. After both
M_{n}
and
N_{n}
halt simultaneously, if
M_{n}
enters an accepting state, then we set
K_{n}
’s outcome (i.e., accepting or rejecting) to be
N_{n}
’s outcome. Otherwise,
K_{n}
ignores
N_{n}
’s outcome and simply enters a rejecting state. This implies that the value
\#K_{n}(x)
matches
\#M_{n}(x)\cdot\#N_{n}(x)
, which equals
f_{n}(x)\cdot g_{n}(x)
. We therefore define
k_{n}(x)=\#K_{n}(x)
for all valid strings
x
. It then follows that
k_{n}=f_{n}\cdot g_{n}
, and thus
{\cal F}\cdot{\cal G}=\{(k_{n},D_{n}\cap E_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
belongs to
1\#
.

(2) Next, we turn to the case of
1\mathrm{Gap}
. Let
{\cal F}=\{(f_{n},D_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
{\cal G}=\{(g_{n},E_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
denote any two families of partial functions in
1\mathrm{Gap}
. Without loss of generality, we assume that
D_{n}=E_{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
since, otherwise, we can take
D_{n}\cap E_{n}
as a new domain. By Lemma
3.3
(1), there are four families
\{(f_{n}^{(i)},D_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\{(g_{n}^{(i)},D_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
for any
i\in\{1,2\}
in
1\#
satisfying that
f_{n}=f_{n}^{(1)}-f_{n}^{(2)}
and
g_{n}=g_{n}^{(1)}-g_{n}^{(2)}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. We then define
k_{n}=f_{n}^{(1)}\cdot g_{n}^{(1)}+f_{n}^{(2)}\cdot g_{n}^{(2)}
and
h_{n}=f_{n}^{(1)}\cdot g_{n}^{(2)}+f_{n}^{(2)}\cdot g_{n}^{(1)}
. It follows that
f_{n}\cdot g_{n}=(f_{n}^{(1)}-f_{n}^{(2)})(g_{n}^{(1)}-g_{n}^{(2)})=(f_{n}^{(1)}\cdot g_{n}^{(1)}+f_{n}^{(2)}\cdot g_{n}^{(2)})-(f_{n}^{(1)}\cdot g_{n}^{(2)}+f_{n}^{(2)}\cdot g_{n}^{(1)})=k_{n}-h_{n}
. By (1),
\{(k_{n},D_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\{(h_{n},D_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
both must be in
1\#
. Since
f_{n}\cdot g_{n}=k_{n}-h_{n}
, the family
{\cal F}\cdot{\cal G}=\{(f_{n}\cdot g_{n},D_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
falls in
1\#-1\#
. Lemma
3.3
(1) thus concludes that
{\cal F}\cdot{\cal G}
belongs to
1\mathrm{Gap}
.
The case of “addition” is similar.
Since
f_{n}+g_{n}=(f_{n}^{(1)}+g_{n}^{(1)})-(f_{n}^{(2)}+g_{n}^{(2)})
, it suffices to set
k^{\prime}_{n}=f_{n}^{(1)}+g_{n}^{(1)}
and
h^{\prime}_{n}=f_{n}^{(2)}+g_{n}^{(2)}
. The family
{\cal F}+{\cal G}=\{(f_{n}+g_{n},D_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
thus falls in
1\#-1\#
, which equals
1\mathrm{Gap}
by Lemma
3.3
(1).
∎

3.3
Definitions of Counting Complexity Classes

Let us formally introduce five additional nonuniform polynomial state complexity classes associated with “counting” used in the rest of this work except for
1\mathrm{D}
and
1\mathrm{N}
.
In what follows, we define five counting complexity classes
1\mathrm{U}
,
1\oplus
,
1\mathrm{C_{=}}
,
1\mathrm{SP}
, and
1\mathrm{P}
using function families in
1\#
and
1\mathrm{Gap}
. We remark that
1\mathrm{P}
is originally defined in, e.g.,
[
31
]
in terms of unbounded-error probabilistic finite automata; nonetheless, it is easy to rephrase its definition in terms of nonuniform families of polynomial-size 1nfa’s.

We first define the parity class
1\oplus
(pronounced “one parity”) as the collection of all families
\{(L_{n}^{(+)},L_{n}^{(-)})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of promise problems such that there exists a function family
\{(f_{n},D_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
1\#
satisfying that,
for any index
n\in\mathbb{N}
, (1)
L_{n}^{(+)}\cup L_{n}^{(-)}\subseteq D_{n}
and (2)
f_{n}(x)
is odd for all
x\in L_{n}^{(+)}
, and
f_{n}(x)
is even for all
x\in L_{n}^{(-)}
.
In a similar way, the unambiguous class
1\mathrm{U}
is obtained by replacing Condition (2) with the following condition :
f_{n}(x)=1
for all
x\in L_{n}^{(+)}
and
f_{n}(x)=0
for all
x\in L_{n}^{(-)}
.
The aforementioned definition of
1\mathrm{N}
given in Section
1.1
is also rephrased in terms of function families in
1\#
by simply demanding the following condition:
f_{n}(x)>0
for all
x\in L_{n}^{(+)}

and
f_{n}(x)=0
for all
x\in L_{n}^{(-)}
.

The exact counting class
1\mathrm{C_{=}}
(pronounced “one C equal”) is defined as the collection of all families
\{(L_{n}^{(+)},L_{n}^{(-)})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of promise
problems such that there exists a function family

\{(f_{n},D_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
1\mathrm{Gap}
satisfying the following: for any index
n\in\mathbb{N}
, (1
′
)
L_{n}^{(+)}\cup L_{n}^{(-)}\subseteq D_{n}
and (2
′
)
f_{n}(x)=0
for all
x\in L_{n}^{(+)}
, and
f_{n}(x)\neq 0
for all
x\in L_{n}^{(-)}
.
In contrast, the stoic probabilistic class
1\mathrm{SP}
refers to the collection of all families
\{(L_{n}^{(+)},L_{n}^{(-)})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
defined by replacing Condition (2
′
) with the following condition:
f_{n}(x)=1
for all
x\in L_{n}^{(+)}
, and
f_{n}(x)=0
for all
x\in L_{n}^{(-)}
.
Finally, the bounded-error probabilistic class
1\mathrm{P}
is defined in a similar way with the following condition:
f_{n}(x)>0
for all
x\in L_{n}^{(+)}
and
f_{n}(x)\leq 0
for all
x\in L_{n}^{(-)}
.

The complexity classes
1\mathrm{C_{=}}
and
1\mathrm{SP}
are closely related to
\mathrm{co}\mbox{-}1\mathrm{N}
and
1\mathrm{U}
because the latter two classes are obtained directly by replacing
1\mathrm{Gap}
in the above definitions of
1\mathrm{C_{=}}
and
1\mathrm{SP}
with
1\#
, respectively.

4
Relationships among Counting Complexity Classes

Let us discuss relationships among various counting complexity classes introduced in Section
3.3
. In particular, we focus our attention on the containment/separation relationships of these classes.
Our results are illustrated in Figure
1
.
In the log-space setting, however, such relationships among
\mathrm{UL}
,
\mathrm{NL}
,
\mathrm{SPL}
,
\oplus\mathrm{L}
,
\mathrm{C}_{=}\mathrm{L}
, and
\mathrm{PL}
are not yet known except for trivial ones. On the contrary, it is possible in our restricted setting to completely determine the relationships among
1\mathrm{U}
,
1\mathrm{N}
,
1\mathrm{SP}
,
1\oplus
,
1\mathrm{C_{=}}
, and

4.1
Basic Closure Properties

We begin with a brief discussion on basic closure properties of
1\oplus
,
1\mathrm{SP}
, and
1\mathrm{C_{=}}
. We first take a close look at the closure property under complementation. The complexity class
1\mathrm{D}
is obviously closed under complementation. It is proven in
[
31
]
that
1\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{co}\mbox{-}1\mathrm{P}
. This closure property also holds for
1\oplus
and
1\mathrm{SP}
.
This situation sharply contrasts the situation that
1\mathrm{U}
and
1\mathrm{N}
are not closed under complementation
[
12
,
32
]
.

Lemma 4.1

1\oplus=\mathrm{co}\mbox{-}1\oplus
and
1\mathrm{SP}=\mathrm{co}\mbox{-}1\mathrm{SP}
.

Proof.

We first show that
1\mathrm{SP}=\mathrm{co}\mbox{-}1\mathrm{SP}
. Take an arbitrary family
{\cal L}=\{(L_{n}^{(+)},L_{n}^{(-)})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
1\mathrm{SP}
and take a family
{\cal F}=\{(f_{n},D_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
1\mathrm{Gap}
that “witnesses” the membership of
{\cal L}
to
1\mathrm{SP}
. Moreover, we take a family
\{M_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of polynomial-size 1nfa’s that “witnesses” the membership
{\cal F}\in 1\mathrm{Gap}
.
We modify each
M_{n}
by swapping between the accepting states and the rejecting states of
M_{n}
and by adding an extra accepting computation path on every input. The resulting machine is denoted
N_{n}
. It then follows that
\#N_{n}(x)=\#\overline{M}_{n}(x)+1
and
\#\overline{N}_{n}(x)=\#M_{n}(x)
.
If we set
g_{n}(x)=\#N_{n}(x)-\#\overline{N}_{n}(x)
for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
x\in D_{n}
, then the family
{\cal G}=\{(g_{n},D_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
belongs to
1\mathrm{Gap}
and also makes
\mathrm{co}\mbox{-}{\cal L}
fall in
1\mathrm{SP}
. This implies
\mathrm{co}\mbox{-}1\mathrm{SP}\subseteq 1\mathrm{SP}
. This is logically equivalent to
1\mathrm{SP}=\mathrm{co}\mbox{-}1\mathrm{SP}
.

In a similar way, we obtain
1\oplus=\mathrm{co}\mbox{-}1\oplus
by generating one extra accepting computation path on each input given to an underlying 1nfa.
∎

It is known that
1\mathrm{N}
is closed under intersection and union
[
31
]
but not under complementation
[
12
]
. In comparison, we show the following closure properties of
1\mathrm{C_{=}}
.

Lemma 4.2

1\mathrm{C_{=}}
is closed under intersection and
\mathrm{co}\mbox{-}1\mathrm{C_{=}}
is closed under union.

Proof.

Let
{\cal L},{\cal K}\in 1\mathrm{C_{=}}
have the form
{\cal L}=\{(L_{n}^{(+)},L_{n}^{(-)})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
{\cal K}=\{(K_{n}^{(+)},K_{n}^{(-)})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. Take two families
\{M_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\{N_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of polynomial-size 1nfa’s satisfying the following condition. For any instance
x
,
x\in L_{n}^{(+)}
implies
\#M_{n}(x)=\#\overline{M}_{n}(x)
,
x\in L_{n}^{(-)}
implies
\#M_{n}(x)\neq\#\overline{M}_{n}(x)
,
x\in K_{n}^{(+)}
implies
\#N_{n}(x)=\#\overline{N}_{n}(x)
, and
x\in K_{n}^{(-)}
implies
\#N_{n}(x)\neq\#\overline{N}_{n}(x)
. We denote by
(Q_{M,acc,n},Q_{M,rej,n})
(resp.,
(Q_{N,acc,n},Q_{N,rej,n})
) the pair of the set of accepting states and that of rejecting states of
M_{n}
(resp.,
N_{n}
).

As remarked in Section
3.3
, Lemma
2.2
makes it possible to replace
\#M_{n}(x)\neq\#\overline{M}_{n}(x)
and
\#N_{n}(x)\neq\#\overline{N}_{n}(x)
given in the above conditions by
\#M_{n}(x)>\#\overline{M}_{n}(x)
and
\#N_{n}(x)>\#\overline{N}_{n}(x)
, respectively.
For the intersection
{\cal L}\cap{\cal K}
, we construct another family
{\cal P}=\{P_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of 1nfa’s as follows. On input
x
,
P_{n}
runs both
M_{n}
and
N_{n}
simultaneously by keeping inner states
q_{1}
of
M_{n}
and
q_{2}
of
N_{n}
as pairs
(q_{1},q_{2})
. If both
M_{n}
and
N_{n}
accept (resp., reject), then
P_{n}
accepts (resp., rejects). Otherwise,
P_{n}
further branches out into one accepting state and one rejecting state.
Let
m(x)
denote
\#M_{n}(x)\cdot\#\overline{N}_{n}(x)+\#\overline{M}_{n}(x)\cdot\#N_{n}(x)
. It then follows that
\#P_{n}(x)
equals
\#M_{n}(x)\cdot\#N_{n}(x)+m(x)
and that
\#\overline{P}_{n}(x)
equals
\#\overline{M}_{n}(x)\cdot\#\overline{N}_{n}(x)+m(x)
.
If
x\in L_{n}^{(+)}\cap K_{n}^{(+)}
, then we obtain
\#P_{n}(x)=\#\overline{P}_{n}(x)
. In contrast, if
x\in L_{n}^{(-)}\cup K_{n}^{(-)}
, then
\#P_{n}(x)>\#\overline{P}_{n}
follows. These facts together conclude that
{\cal P}
solves
{\cal L}\cap{\cal K}
.

Clearly, the union closure of
\mathrm{co}\mbox{-}1\mathrm{C_{=}}
comes directly from the intersection closure of
1\mathrm{C_{=}}
.
∎

As for the closure property under complementation, similarly to those of
1\mathrm{N}
and
1\mathrm{U}
, we will see in Corollary
4.7
that
1\mathrm{C_{=}}
does not satisfy this property.

4.2
Complexity Class 1U

We next discuss containments and separations of
1\mathrm{U}
in comparison with other counting complexity classes.
Between
1\mathrm{U}
and
1\mathrm{SP}
, we claim that
1\mathrm{U}\subseteq 1\mathrm{SP}
and
\mathrm{co}\mbox{-}1\mathrm{U}\subseteq 1\mathrm{SP}
. It is further possible to prove that these inclusions are, in fact, proper.
For later convenience, we write
T_{n}
to denote the set
\{x\#y\mid x,y\in\{0,1\}^{2^{n}}\}
, representing pairs of exponentially long strings for each index
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proposition 4.3

1\mathrm{U}\subsetneqq 1\mathrm{SP}
.

Proof.

We first focus on the proof of
1\mathrm{U}\subseteq 1\mathrm{SP}
.
Let
{\cal L}
denote an arbitrary family
\{(L_{n}^{(+)},L_{n}^{(-)})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of promise problems in
1\mathrm{U}
and take a family
{\cal M}=\{M_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of polynomial-size 1nfa’s solving
{\cal L}
, where each
M_{n}
is unambiguous at least on
L^{(n)}
(
=L_{n}^{(+)}\cup L_{n}^{(-)}
). From each
M_{n}
, we construct another 1nfa
N_{n}
that behaves as follows.
Start to simulate
M_{n}
on input
x
. If
M_{n}
enters an accepting state, then
N_{n}
does the same. If
M_{n}
enters a rejecting state, then
N_{n}
branches into two computation paths and accepts on one path and rejects on the other. It then follows that
x\in L_{n}^{(+)}
implies
\#N_{n}(x)-\#\overline{N}_{n}(x)=1
and
x\in L_{n}^{(-)}
implies
\#N_{n}(x)=\#\overline{N}_{n}(x)
. Hence,
{\cal L}
belongs to
1\mathrm{SP}
.

Next, we intend to show that
1\mathrm{U}\neq 1\mathrm{SP}
. We write
A
for the set
\{x\#y\mid x,y\in\{0,1\}^{*}\}
of all pairs. The strings
x
and
y
in
x\#y
in
A
are respectively called the
first block
and the
second block
of
x\#y
.
For each index
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we further define
L_{n}^{(+)}=\{x\#y\in A\mid\#_{0}(x)=\#_{0}(y)+1\}
and
L_{n}^{(-)}=\{x\#y\in A\mid\#_{0}(x)=\#_{0}(y)\}
.
We then write
{\cal L}_{sp}
for the family
\{(L_{n}^{(+)},L_{n}^{(-)})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and claim that
{\cal L}_{sp}
belongs to
1\mathrm{SP}
. For this purpose, let us consider the following 1nfa
M_{n}
. On input
w
of the form
x\#y
in
A
,
M_{n}
repeats the following procedure whenever reading a fresh input symbol until the right endmarker is reached. While reading the first block
x
of
w
, if
\sigma=0
, then
M_{n}
branches out into three computation paths. On one path,
M_{n}
rejects, and on the two other paths,
M_{n}
accepts. When
\sigma=1
, on the contrary, we just skip to the next input symbol.
The machine
M_{n}
also skips
\#
and continues the above procedure by swapping accepting and rejecting states. At reading
\lhd
,
M_{n}
branches out into two computation paths leading to one accepting state and one rejecting state.
It then follows that
\#_{0}(x)=\#\overline{M}_{n}(x)-\#M_{n}(x)
and
\#_{0}(y)=\#M_{n}(y)-\#\overline{M}_{n}(y)
. Thus, we obtain
\#_{0}(x)-\#_{0}(y)=\#M_{n}(x)+\#M_{n}(y)-(\#\overline{M}_{n}(x)+\#\overline{M}_{n}(y))=\#M_{n}(x\#y)-\#\overline{M}_{n}(x\#y)
.
The last equality clearly implies that
{\cal L}_{sp}\in 1\mathrm{SP}
.

Finally, we claim that
{\cal L}_{sp}\notin 1\mathrm{U}
. Assuming otherwise, we take a family
{\cal N}=\{N_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of polynomial-size 1nfa’s solving
{\cal L}_{sp}
such that, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
st(N_{n})\leq n^{k}
holds for a certain absolute constant
k>0
and
N_{n}
is unambiguous (at least) on all inputs in
L^{(n)}
. Let
Q_{n}
denote the set of all inner states of
N_{n}
.
For every string
x\#y
in
L_{n}^{(+)}
, the notation
\mu(x,y)
denotes a unique inner state
q
such that there exists a unique accepting computation path
\gamma
along which
N_{n}
enters
q
just after reading off
x\#
.

Fix an arbitrary
n\in\mathbb{N}
satisfying
n^{k}<2^{n/2}
. We set
I_{n}
to be
\{x\#y\in L_{n}^{(+)}\mid\#_{0}(x)\geq 2^{n/2}\}
. We choose two strings
x_{1}\#y_{1}
and
x_{2}\#y_{2}
in
I_{n}
for which
\#_{0}(x_{1})\neq\#_{0}(x_{2})
and
\mu(x_{1},y_{1})=\mu(x_{2},y_{2})
. This is possible because the total number of different values of
\mu(x,y)
over all
x\#y
in
I_{n}
is upper-bounded by
|Q_{n}|
.

Let us consider the following computation on the input
x_{1}\#y_{2}
. For simplicity, we write
q
for the inner state
\mu(x_{1},y_{1})
. We first simulate
N_{n}
on
x_{1}\#
and enter
q
. Starting with
q
, we simulate
N_{n}
on
y_{2}
and enter its own accepting state whenever entering an accepting state of
M_{n}
. Note that this computation forms a legitimate computation path of
N_{n}
on
x_{1}\#y_{2}
. Thus,
x_{1}\#y_{2}\in L_{n}^{(+)}
follows. This is a clear contradiction against the definition of
L_{n}^{(+)}
.
∎

It is proven in
[
33
]
that
\mathrm{co}\mbox{-}1\mathrm{ReachU}\nsubseteq 1\mathrm{N}
, where
1\mathrm{ReachU}
is a proper subclass of
1\mathrm{U}
. This directly implies the desired separation of
\mathrm{co}\mbox{-}1\mathrm{U}\nsubseteq 1\mathrm{N}
. For the sake of completeness, we include the proof of
\mathrm{co}\mbox{-}1\mathrm{U}\nsubseteq 1\mathrm{N}
without mentioning
1\mathrm{ReachU}
.
In the proof, we use the notion of
Kolmogorov complexity
.
Given a binary string
x
, the notation
C(x)
denotes the (unconditional) Kolmogorov complexity of
x
; namely, the minimum length of a binary-encoded program
p
such that a
universal Turing machine

U
takes
p
as an input and produces
x
on its output tape in a finite number of steps. See, e.g.,
[
15
]
for more detailed information.

Theorem 4.4

\mathrm{co}\mbox{-}1\mathrm{U}\nsubseteq 1\mathrm{N}

Proof.

Recall the notation
B_{n}(m,k)
from Section
2.1
.
For every index
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we define
L_{n}^{(+)}=\{u\#v\mid u,v\in B_{n}(n,n),\exists!e\in[n]((u)_{(e)}\neq(v)_{(e)})\}
and
L_{n}^{(-)}=\{u\#v\mid u,v\in B_{n}(n,n),\forall e\in[n]((u)_{(e)}=(v)_{(e)})\}
. Notice that
|u\#v|=2n^{2}+1
if
u\#v\in L_{n}^{(+)}\cup L_{n}^{(-)}
, since
u\in B_{n}(n,n)
implies
|u|=n^{2}
.
Let us consider the family
{\cal L}_{U}=\{(L_{n}^{(+)},L_{n}^{(-)})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.
It then follows that
{\cal L}_{U}
belongs to
1\mathrm{U}
. We focus on the complement of
{\cal L}_{U}
, that is,
\mathrm{co}\mbox{-}{\cal L}_{U}=\{(L_{n}^{(-)},L_{n}^{(+)})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, which belongs to
\mathrm{co}\mbox{-}1\mathrm{U}
.

In what follows, we intend to verify that
\mathrm{co}\mbox{-}{\cal L}_{U}\notin 1\mathrm{N}
. To lead to a contradiction, we assume the existence of a family
{\cal M}=\{M_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of polynomial-size 1nfa’s that solves
\mathrm{co}\mbox{-}{\cal L}_{U}
. For each index
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
Q_{n}
denotes the set of all inner states of
M_{n}
. There exists a polynomial
p
satisfying

|Q_{n}|\leq p(n)
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. It is possible to assume that all elements of
Q_{n}
are expressed as numbers in
[0,|Q_{n}|-1]_{\mathbb{Z}}
.
Given any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and any string
u\in B_{n}(n,n)
, we define
S_{n}(u)
to be the set of all inner states
q
in
Q_{n}
such that, in a certain accepting computation path of
M_{n}
on the input
u\#u
,
M_{n}
enters
q
just after reading off
u\#
.

Take a sufficiently large integer
n
satisfying
p(n)<2^{n}
. Let us consider the Kolmogorov complexity
C(w)
of any string
w
. We choose a string
u
in
B_{n}(n,n)
for which
C(u)\geq n\log{n}
.
Such a string exists because, otherwise,
|B_{n}(n,n)|\leq|\{u\in\{0,1\}^{n^{2}}\mid C(u)\leq n\log{n}\}|\leq 2^{n\log{n}}
, a contradiction against
|B_{n}(n,n)|=n^{n}
.
We then choose an inner state
q\in S_{n}(u)
, which is the smallest value in
[0,|Q_{n}|-1]_{\mathbb{Z}}
.
Note that the length of the binary expression of
q
is
O(\log{n})
since
|Q_{n}|\leq p(n)
.

Let us design a deterministic algorithm, say,
{\cal B}
. By choosing all strings
w
in
B_{n}(n,n)
inductively one by one, we run
M_{n}
on the input
w\#w
starting with the inner state
q
. If
M_{n}
enters no accepting states on all computation paths, then output
w
and halt. Otherwise, choose another
w
and continue the algorithm. Let
w^{\prime}
denote the outcome of this algorithm
{\cal B}
. The string
w^{\prime}
must be
u
because, otherwise,
M_{n}
also accepts
u\#w^{\prime}
, a contradiction. Let
r_{0}
denote the binary encoding of the algorithm
{\cal B}
. It is important to note that
|r_{0}|
is a constant independent of
u,w^{\prime}
. It thus follows that
C(u)\leq|bin(n)|+|bin(q)|+|r_{0}|+O(1)\leq O(\log{n})
, which is in contradiction to
C(u)\geq n\log{n}-1
.
∎

Since
1\mathrm{U}\subseteq 1\mathrm{N}
, Theorem
4.4
yields the known separation of
1\mathrm{U}\neq\mathrm{co}\mbox{-}1\mathrm{U}

[
32
,
33
]
. Theorem
4.4
also leads to the following immediate consequence on the complexity of
1\mathrm{SP}
.

Corollary 4.5

1\mathrm{SP}\nsubseteq 1\mathrm{N}
.

Proof.

Assume that
1\mathrm{SP}\subseteq 1\mathrm{N}
. Since
1\mathrm{U}\subseteq 1\mathrm{SP}
by Proposition
4.3
, it follows that
\mathrm{co}\mbox{-}1\mathrm{U}\subseteq\mathrm{co}\mbox{-}1\mathrm{SP}=1\mathrm{SP}
. We thus obtain
\mathrm{co}\mbox{-}1\mathrm{U}\subseteq 1\mathrm{N}
. This obviously contradicts Theorem
4.4
. Therefore, we can conclude that
1\mathrm{SP}\nsubseteq 1\mathrm{N}
.
∎

4.3

=

Let us discuss containment/separation relationships concerning
1\mathrm{C_{=}}
.
A core of this subsection is an exploitation of a close connection between nonuniform (polynomial) state complexity classes of this work and one-tape linear-time Turing machines with linear-size advice discussed in
[

24
]
.
This close connection helps us adopt a key lemma of
[

Theorem 4.6

(1)
1\mathrm{N}\subsetneqq\mathrm{co}\mbox{-}1\mathrm{C_{=}}
and

Proposition 4.8

1\mathrm{SP}\subsetneqq\mathrm{co}\mbox{-}1\mathrm{C_{=}}

The inclusion

As another consequence of Theorem
4.6
, we obtain the following.

Proposition 4.9

1\mathrm{C_{=}}\subsetneqq 1\mathrm{P}
.

Let
{\cal L}
denote any family in

and take a family

“witnesses” the membership of

\mathrm{co}\mbox{-}1\mathrm{P}
.
Since
1\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{co}\mbox{-}1\mathrm{P}

[

]
, this implies that
1\mathrm{C_{=}}\subseteq 1\mathrm{P}
.

Next, we intend to prove that

. Note that this inequality implies

as well because of

. Since

[

]
, we obtain

(2).
∎

In the rest of this subsection, we intend to prove Theorem
4.6
. The desired proof requires an idea from
[
24
]
, more specifically,
a useful, characteristic property of

, presented in
[

, to demonstrate that
1\mbox{-}\mathrm{C}_{=}\mathrm{LIN/lin}

As noted in Section

, there is a close connection

This connection seems to be adopted to one-tape linear-time advised computation and nonuniform finite automata families. In particular, we focus on a connection

Lemma 4.10

Let
{\cal L}=\{(L_{n}^{(+)},L_{n}^{(-)})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
denote any family in

over alphabet

that satisfies the following statement. Let

,

, and

be any numbers in

with

, let

, if

, then
\{xy\mid x\in A_{n,m,l,z}\}\subseteq L_{n}^{(+)}
.

Meanwhile, we postpone the proof of Lemma
4.10
and we wish to prove Theorem
4.6
with the use of the lemma.

Proof of Theorem
4.6
.

(1) We intend to prove that
1\mathrm{N}\subseteq\mathrm{co}\mbox{-}1\mathrm{C_{=}}
.
We first claim that
1\mathrm{N}\subseteq\mathrm{co}\mbox{-}1\mathrm{C_{=}}
. For any family
{\cal L}=\{(L_{n}^{(+)},L_{n}^{(-)})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of promise problems in
1\mathrm{N}
, let us consider a family

of partial functions in

such that (*)
f_{n}(x)>0

and
f_{n}(x)=0
for all
x\in L_{n}^{(-)}
. Since

(1),
{\cal F}
belongs to
1\mathrm{Gap}_{\geq 0}
. Condition (*) then implies that
{\cal L}
belongs to
\mathrm{co}\mbox{-}1\mathrm{C_{=}}
by the definition of

. Therefore, we conclude that
1\mathrm{N}\subseteq\mathrm{co}\mbox{-}1\mathrm{C_{=}}
.

The remaining task is to prove

, we obtain
|S^{\prime}|\leq k\leq p(n)
.
Let us assume that

is expressed as

.
For each string

, we write

for the binary string

such that, for any index

, and

. In case that no such

exists,
the lemma is vacuously true.

Fix an arbitrary string

and take a series

of real numbers satisfying

.
We next claim that

. Given a (row) vector
\xi

|Q_{n}|
-dimension, we succinctly write

to denote the sum of all entries of
\xi

\|\nu_{ini}^{(n)}M_{\triangleright w_{i}}^{(n)}\|=1

is a stochastic matrix and
\|\nu_{ini}^{(n)}\|=1
.
It then follows that

. From this fact, we instantly obtain

because of
\|\nu_{ini}^{(n)}M_{\triangleright x}^{(n)}\|=1
.

Given any
y\in\Sigma^{l}
and any

, there exists a series

of real numbers for which

.
It then follows that

denotes the

th bit of the string

.

The desired strings

and

are defined as follows. We take a
k
-bit-long string

such that
b_{i}=1
if
\alpha_{i}>0
, and
b_{i}=0
if
\alpha_{i}\leq 0
for any index
i\in[k]
.
From this

is chosen to satisfy

.
Such a
y
exists because
|\{a(y^{\prime})\mid y^{\prime}\in R\}|\geq 2^{k}
implies

. For this

, we obtain
\sum_{i\in[k]}(-1)^{1-a(y)[i]}|\beta_{iy}|\alpha_{i}=\sum_{i\in[k]}|\beta_{iy}||\alpha_{i}|>0
. This implies
p_{acc,n}(xy)>1/2
. For the other string
y^{\prime}
, we first define

to be the bitwise negation of

and then choose

to satisfy

. It then follows that
\sum_{i\in[k]}(-1)^{1-a(y^{\prime})[i]}|\beta_{iy^{\prime}}|\alpha_{i}=-\sum_{i\in[k]}|\beta_{iy^{\prime}}||\alpha_{i}|<0
. From this inequality,

follows. We thus conclude that

and
xy^{\prime}\notin L_{n}^{(+)}
.

\Box

Non-Closure Properties of 1# under Functional Operations

Under the typical functional operations of addition and multiplication, we have shown in Lemma
3.5
the closure properties of
1\#
and
1\mathrm{Gap}
. We wish to expand the scope of such functional operations. In this section, we examine six additional functional operations.
we first recall four operations on
\mathbb{Z}
from Section
2.1
and expand them to functional operations in a natural way.
Given two partial functions
(f,D)
and
(g,E)
with
D,E\subseteq\Sigma^{*}
for a fixed alphabet
\Sigma
, we define

f\oslash g
,
f\oslash 2
,
f\ominus g
, and
f\ominus 1
by setting
(f\oslash g)(x)=f(x)\oslash g(x)
,
(f\oslash 2)(x)=f(x)\oslash 2
,
(f\ominus g)(x)=f(x)\ominus g(x)
, and
(f\ominus 1)(x)=f(x)\ominus 1
for all valid strings
x
in
D\cap E
.
Furthermore, we introduce two more functional operations,
\max(f,g)
and
\min(f,g)
, by setting
\max(f,g)(x)=\max\{f(x),g(x)\}
and
\min(f,g)(x)=\min\{f(x),g(x)\}
for any
x\in D\cap E
.

We wish to show the non-closure properties of
1\#
under the above-mentioned six functional operations.

Theorem 6.1

The counting function class
1\#
is not closed under the following six functional operations: minimum, maximum, proper subtraction, integer division, proper decrement, and integer division by two.

Proof.

The non-closure properties under proper subtraction and integer division follow immediately from those under proper decrement and integer division by two. Therefore, it suffices to target the following four operations: (1) proper decrement, (2) integer division by two, (3) maximum, and (4) minimum. Following the arguments given in
[
16
]
, we relate those non-closure properties to the separations of nonuniform state complexity classes shown in Section
4
.

(1) We begin with the case of proper decrement. Toward a contradiction, assuming that
1\#
is closed under proper decrement, we wish to prove that
1\mathrm{N}\subseteq 1\mathrm{SP}
. If this statement is true, then we obtain
1\mathrm{N}\subseteq 1\mathrm{C_{=}}
since
1\mathrm{SP}\subseteq 1\mathrm{C_{=}}\cap\mathrm{co}\mbox{-}1\mathrm{C_{=}}
(Proposition
4.8
).
However, this contradicts Theorem
4.6
(2) and we thus conclude that
1\#
is not closed under proper decrement.

In what follows, we concentrate on proving that
1\mathrm{N}\subseteq 1\mathrm{SP}
.
Let
{\cal L}=\{(L_{n}^{(+)},L_{n}^{(-)})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be any family of promise problems in
1\mathrm{N}
and take a family
\{M_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of polynomial-size 1nfa’s that “witnesses” the fact of “
{\cal L}\in 1\mathrm{N}
”.
For each index
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we set
D_{n}
to be
L_{n}^{(+)}\cup L_{n}^{(-)}
and define
f_{n}(x)=\#M_{n}(x)
for all strings
x\in D_{n}
.
Let us consider the function family
{\cal F}=\{(f_{n},D_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. We further define
h_{n}(x)=f_{n}(x)\ominus 1
and set
{\cal H}=\{(h_{n},D_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. Since
{\cal F}\in 1\#
, our assumption implies that
{\cal H}\in 1\#
. There is a family
{\cal N}=\{N_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of polynomial-size 1nfa’s that “witnesses” the membership of
{\cal H}
to
1\#
.
By Lemma
2.1
, we can assume that
N_{n}
is in a branching normal form and it thus produces exactly
c^{|\rhd x\lhd|}
(
=c^{|x|+2}
) computation paths for all valid inputs
x
, where
c>0
is a fixed constant.
For convenience, we write
g_{n}(x)
for
-h_{n}(x)
for all strings
x\in D_{n}
. Since
\{(g_{n},D_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
belongs to
1\mathrm{Gap}
by Lemma
3.3
(1), we conclude from Lemma
3.5
that the family
{\cal F}+{\cal G}=\{(f_{n}+g_{n},D_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
also belongs to
1\mathrm{Gap}
.
We remark that, if
x\in L_{n}^{(+)}
then
f_{n}(x)+g_{n}(x)=\#M_{n}(x)-(\#M_{n}(x)-1)=1
, and if
x\in L_{n}^{(-)}
then
f_{n}(x)+g_{n}(x)=0-0=0
. This implies that
{\cal L}
is in
1\mathrm{SP}
, as requested.

(2) We next focus on the integer division by two. Assume that
1\#
is closed under integer division by two. From this assumption, we intend to derive the equality
1\mathrm{SP}=1\oplus
. This clearly contradicts Proposition
4.12
. Let
{\cal L}=\{(L_{n}^{(+)},L_{n}^{(-)})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
denote any family of promise problems in
1\oplus
.
There exists a family
{\cal F}=\{(f_{n},D_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of partial functions in
1\#
that “witnesses” the membership of
{\cal L}
to
1\oplus
. Let us further take a family
\{M_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of polynomial-size 1nfa’s that “witnesses” the fact of “
{\cal F}\in 1\#
”. It is possible to assume by Lemma
2.1
that
M_{n}
is in a branching normal form.

We define
{\cal H}=\{(h_{n},D_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
by setting
h_{n}(x)=2(f_{n}(x)\oslash 2)
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
x\in D_{n}
. Since
{\cal H}
is in
1\#
by our assumption, there is another family
\{N_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of polynomial-size 1nfa’s that “witnesses” the membership of
{\cal H}
to
1\#
.
We then define
g_{n}(x)=c^{|x|+2}-f_{n}(x)
.
Note that, by Lemma
3.3
(1), the family
{\cal G}=\{(g_{n},D_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
belongs to
1\mathrm{Gap}
. Lemma
3.5
then implies that
{\cal F}+{\cal G}=\{(g_{n}+h_{n},D_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
also falls in
1\mathrm{Gap}
. Given any index
n
and any string

x\in L_{n}^{(+)}
implies
g_{n}(x)+h_{n}(x)=c^{|x|+2}
and that
x\in L_{n}^{(-)}
implies
g_{n}(x)+h_{n}(x)=c^{|x|+2}-1
. Hence,
{\cal L}
belongs to
\mathrm{co}\mbox{-}1\mathrm{SP}

.

(3) We then target the maximum operation. In this case, we want to show that, if

is closed under maximum, then

collapses to
1\mathrm{SP}
. Since

We begin with taking

By Lemmas

(4) Finally, we consider the minimum operation. Assuming that

A Short Discussion and Open Problems

Counting has remained as an important research subject in computational complexity theory. To promote the better understandings of the nature of counting in various low complexity classes, we have initiated in this work a study on “counting” within the framework of nonuniform models of polynomial-size finite automata families.
This work has made a significant contribution to expanding and exploring the existing world of nonuniform polynomial state complexity classes

1.

Throughout this work, we have demonstrated numerous containments and separations among counting complexity classes. These properties are summarized in Figure
