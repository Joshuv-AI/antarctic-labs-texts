Optimal performance of a three stroke heat engine in the microscopic regime
Tanmoy Biswas, Chandan Datta

Abstract
We consider a three-stroke engine in the microscopic regime, where the working body of the engine is composed of a two-level system. The working body of the engine aims to withdraw heat from the hot heat bath, generate work, and discharge the surplus heat into the cold heat bath through the successive execution of three strokes. In this process, the interaction of the working body with the heat baths is assumed to be energy-conserving and thus can be described by thermal operations. While earlier studies analyzed the optimal performance of this engine when the working body could be transformed by any arbitrary thermal operation, we present closed expressions for the maximum work produced by the engine and the maximum efficiency of the engine when only a restricted class of thermal operations can be implemented on the working body. Furthermore, we explore the engine’s optimal performance under two well-studied classes of restrictions: thermal operations realized via Jaynes-Cummings interaction and thermal operations realizable with finite-sized heat baths. Therefore, on one hand, our results are general, as they reproduce the optimal performance achieved when any arbitrary thermal operation can be implemented on the working body once the restriction is relaxed. On the other hand, our results allow us to determine the engine’s maximum work production and efficiency in a more realistic scenario, where only a restricted class of thermal operations are possible, thereby bringing our findings closer to experimental feasibility.

II
Description of a stroke-based discrete engine

II.1
Construction of the engine

Any stroke-based heat engine operates in a sequence of strokes that allows it to consume heat from a hot heat bath and convert it into work in subsequent strokes. The second law of thermodynamics asserts that it is impossible to convert all the heat that has been drawn from the hot heat bath to work. Thus, in order to work in a cyclic manner, the engine needs to release a fraction of the consumed heat into the cold heat bath after work extraction in the final stroke. Here, we consider a model of a stroke-based engine in the microscopic regime that requires three subsequent strokes to complete the thermodynamic cycle. The heat engine consists of the following key components:

1.

A hot heat bath is thermalized at an inverse temperature
\beta_{H}
, which serves as a source of heat that requires to produce work. We label this component by
H
.

2.

A cold heat bath is thermalized at inverse temperature
\beta_{C}
and works as a heat sink for dumping the remainder of the consumed heat from the hot heat bath after work has been extracted. We denote this component by
C
.

3.

A working substance or working body represented by a
d
-level quantum system that controls the flow of energy among other components of the engine. We label this component by
S
.

Therefore, the total free Hamiltonian of the whole system is

H_{\text{free}}=H_{S}+H_{H}+H_{C},

(1)

where
H_{S}
,
H_{H}
, and
H_{C}
denote the Hamiltonian of the working body, hot heat bath, and cold heat bath, respectively. In the start, we assume all the components of the engine are uncorrelated, which allows us to write the full initial state
\rho_{\text{in}}
of the engine as

\rho_{\text{in}}=\rho_{S}\otimes\gamma_{H}\otimes\gamma_{C},

(2)

where
\gamma_{H}
and
\gamma_{C}
denote the thermal states of the hot and cold heat baths, i.e.,

\gamma_{H}=\frac{e^{-\beta_{H}H_{H}}}{\text{Tr}(e^{-\beta_{H}H_{H}})};\quad\gamma_{C}=\frac{e^{-\beta_{C}H_{C}}}{\text{Tr}(e^{-\beta_{C}H_{C}})}.

(3)

Whereas,
\rho_{S}
represent the initial state of the working body. In the following section, we shall discuss the functioning of the engine.

II.2
Characterization of the strokes of the engine

This section focuses on the various strokes that allow the engine to operate. The engine operates on three major strokes: i) heat stroke, ii) work stroke, and iii) cold stroke. While the engine involves energy-conserving stroke operations, this does not result in a fully autonomous engine since it needs external control to carry out the various steps. Nevertheless, the engine is an advancement towards creating an autonomous machine as it preserves total energy. To put it simply, the process of enabling or disabling interactions within the system does not impact the flow of energy into or out of the system. Following this, we describe the workings of the engine in more detail.

II.2.1
Heat stroke

This is the initial stroke of the engine, in which the working body interacts with the hot heat bath at thermal equilibrium using energy-conserving unitary
U_{S,H}
, i.e,
[U_{S,H},H_{S}+H_{H}]=0
. Thus, the transformation of the state
\rho_{S}
is governed by a thermal operation
{\cal E}_{H}

[
27
]
, i.e.,

\rho_{S}\rightarrow{\cal E}_{H}(\rho_{S}):=\text{Tr}_{H}\big(U_{S,H}(\rho_{S}\otimes\gamma_{H})U_{S,H}^{\dagger}\big).

(4)

The aim of this stroke is to consume heat from the hot heat bath, which increases average energy and induces non-passivity in the working body. In accordance with classical thermodynamics, where heat is defined as the amount of energy transferred to the system from the hot heat bath, we define the amount of heat transferred, denoted as
Q_{H}
, as the change in the system’s average energy caused by its interaction with the hot heat bath, which can be expressed as:

Q_{H}=\text{Tr}\Big(H_{S}\big(\mathcal{E}_{H}(\rho_{S})-\rho_{S}\big)\Big).

(5)

Here,
\mathcal{E}_{H}(\rho_{S})
represents the final state of the working body after the interaction with the hot heat bath.

II.2.2
Work stroke

In the second stroke of the engine, the working body, which resulted from the first stroke, aims to produce work using the heat it drew from the first stroke. The engine extracts work using a
cyclic
unitary operation that acts on the working body’s state from the first stroke, i.e.,
{\cal E}_{H}(\rho_{S})
, and transforms it to the one with lower average energy
[
8
]
. A unitary operation is called cyclic if it is generated by a time-dependent Hamiltonian
G(t)
such that
G(0)=G(\tau)=H_{S}
, where
\tau
denotes the duration of the work stroke and
H_{S}
is the Hamiltonian of the working body. We denote the set of such cyclic unitary operations by
\mathcal{V}_{\text{cyc}}
.

In the following, we emphasize an important distinction between our engine and the familiar four-stroke engine operating in Otto cycle (See Fig. 1 (b) of Ref.
[
55
]
). In a four-stroke engine operating in an Otto cycle, the thermodynamic cycle consists of two work strokes: the expansion stroke and the compression stroke. After the first work stroke, the free Hamiltonian of the two-level working body changes, and after the second work stroke, it returns to its initial value. In contrast, the proposed three-stroke engine operates with a thermodynamic cycle that includes only one work stroke that extracts work by switching on an interaction that alters the external Hamiltonian of the engine’s working body. The interaction is switched off upon completion of the work stroke. Thus, the Hamiltonian of the working body is reduced to the free Hamiltonian at the end of the work stroke, which is the same as the Hamiltonian at the beginning of the work stroke. In other words, the external driving parameter undergoes cyclic changes, meaning it reverts back to its original value at the end of the work stroke
[
8
,
24
]
.

Therefore, this stroke transforms the working body’s state as follows:

\mathcal{E}_{H}(\rho_{S})\rightarrow V(\mathcal{E}_{H}(\rho_{S}))V^{\dagger},

(6)

where
V
is a cyclic unitary operation. The amount of work produced by the engine is defined as
[
20
,
52
]

W=\text{Tr}\Big(H_{S}\big(\mathcal{E}_{H}(\rho_{S})-V(\mathcal{E}_{H}(\rho_{S}))V^{\dagger}\big)\Big).

(7)

To maximize the work
W
over all cyclic unitary operations, we describe the concept of
ergotropy
introduced in the seminal Ref.
[
8
]
by Allahverdyan et al. Consider any arbitrary
d
-dimensional state
\sigma

having eigendecomposition

\sigma=\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}\lambda_{i}|\lambda_{i}\rangle\!\langle\lambda_{i}|\quad\text{such that}\quad\forall{i},\;\;\lambda_{i}\geq\lambda_{i+1},

(8)

associated with Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
with eigendecomposition

\mathcal{H}=\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}\varepsilon_{j}|\varepsilon_{j}\rangle\!\langle\varepsilon_{j}|\quad\text{such that}\quad\forall{j},\;\;\varepsilon_{j}\leq\varepsilon_{j+1}.

(9)

Then the ergotropy of the state
\sigma
defined as follows:

\displaystyle R(\sigma)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\max_{\mathcal{U}\in\mathcal{V}_{\text{cyc}}}\text{Tr}\left(\mathcal{H}\left(\sigma-\mathcal{U}\sigma\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}\right)\right)

(10)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{Tr}\left(\mathcal{H}\left(\sigma-\mathcal{U}_{\text{max}}\sigma\mathcal{U}_{\text{max}}^{\dagger}\right)\right),

where
\mathcal{U}_{\text{max}}
has the following form:

\mathcal{U}_{\text{max}}=\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}|\varepsilon_{j}\rangle\!\langle\lambda_{j}|.

(11)

By inspection, we observe that
\mathcal{U}_{\text{max}}
decreasingly orders the eigenvalues of
\sigma
such that they correspond to the eigenlevels of Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
arranged in increasing order of energy.

Employing the concept of ergotropy, we can now conclude that when the state of the working body in state
{\cal E}_{H}(\rho_{S})
transforms via a cyclic unitary operation
V
, the work production by the engine satisfies the following condition:

\displaystyle W

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{Tr}\Big(H_{S}\big(\mathcal{E}_{H}(\rho_{S})-V(\mathcal{E}_{H}(\rho_{S}))V^{\dagger}\big)\Big)

(12)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\max_{V\in\mathcal{V}_{\text{cyc}}}\text{Tr}\Big(H_{S}\big(\mathcal{E}_{H}(\rho_{S})-V(\mathcal{E}_{H}(\rho_{S}))V^{\dagger}\big)\Big)

(13)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle R(\mathcal{E}_{H}(\rho_{S})).

(14)

In other words, the maximum amount of the work produced by the engine is simply equals to the ergotropy of the state of the working body after the first stroke.

II.2.3
Closing the thermodynamic cycle

In the final stroke of the engine, the state of the working body that results from the work stroke
V{\cal E}_{H}(\rho_{S})V^{\dagger}
interacts with the cold heat bath in thermal equilibrium via an energy conserving unitary operation
U_{S,C}
, i.e.,
[U_{S,C},H_{S}+H_{C}]=0
. This transformation corresponds to a thermal operation with respect to the cold heat bath denoted by
{\cal E}_{C}
, i.e.,

\mathcal{E}_{C}\big(V{\cal E}_{H}(\rho_{S})V^{\dagger}\big):=\text{Tr}_{C}\Big(U_{S,C}\big((V{\cal E}_{H}(\rho_{S})V^{\dagger})\otimes\gamma_{C}\big)U_{S,C}^{\dagger}\Big).

(15)

The goal of this stroke is to return the working body to its initial state, i.e.,

\mathcal{E}_{C}\big(V{\cal E}_{H}(\rho_{S})V^{\dagger}\big)=\rho_{S},

(16)

that results in the closing of the thermodynamic cycle. Our aim is to figure out the
cyclic state

\rho_{S}
that satisfies the relation given in Eq. (
16
).

We define
cold heat
as the amount of heat that the working body releases into the cold heat bath, expressed as follows:

Q_{C}=\text{Tr}\Big(H_{S}\big(\rho_{S}-V{\cal E}_{H}(\rho_{S})V^{\dagger}\big)\Big).

(17)

This step is critical for the entire setup to function as an engine. More generally, this stroke encodes the manifestation of the second law for this heat engine.

II.3
The open cycle engine

The open cycle engine introduced in Ref.
[
20
]
represents a subclass of the discussed three-stroke engine. This kind of engine starts with a working body that is thermalized at the inverse temperature of the cold heat bath. Therefore, the final stroke of the engine simply reduces to thermalization at the inverse temperature of the cold bath, which results in the completion of the cycle. Our research aims to bridge the understanding between an open-cycle engine and a generic three-stroke engine that we have introduced previously, particularly when the working body comprises two-level systems. This connection significantly simplifies the optimization of efficiency and work for the three-stroke engines with a two-level working body. The comparison between these two engines has been illustrated in Fig.

.

. The first and third strokes are characterised by thermal operations at the hot and cold inverse temperatures
\beta_{H}

\beta^{v}_{C}
, as shown in the right panel. This equivalence captures the underlying idea behind the proof of theorem

II.4
Mathematical tools

This section provides a brief overview to the mathematical tools associated with thermal operations that are important to evaluate the performance of the engine. A thermal operation can be defined as a complete positive trace preserving (CPTP) map
{\cal E}
, which transforms a system’s state
\rho
as

\mathcal{E}(\rho)=\text{Tr}_{E}\Big(U(\rho\otimes\gamma_{E})U^{\dagger}\Big),

(18)

where
U
satisfies the relation
[U,H_{S}+H_{E}]=0
with
H_{S}
,
H_{E}
are the Hamiltonians of the system and bath, respectively, and
\gamma_{E}=\frac{e^{-\beta H_{E}}}{\text{Tr}(e^{-\beta H_{E}})}
is the thermal state of the bath that is thermalized at an inverse temperature
\beta

[
27
,
54
]
.

The central task is then described by the following interconversion problem: what is the set of final states that can be achieved from a given state
\rho
via thermal operations? This set is convex and referred to as the
thermal cone
of the state
\rho
, denoted by
{\cal T}(\rho)
, which follows straightforwardly from the convexity of thermal operation
[
56
]
. Characterizing the necessary and sufficient conditions for the interconversion problem in general is challenging and unknown beyond dimension two
[
57
,
58
]
. Nonetheless, the seminal work by Horodecki and Oppenheim in Ref.
[
27
]
characterizes the necessary and sufficient conditions for the state interconversion problem when
[\rho,H_{S}]=0
. These states are referred to as
energy-incoherent states
as they do not possess coherence in the energy eigenbasis. For a given energy incoherent state
\rho
, the thermal cone
{\cal T}(\rho)
is reduced to a polytope, i.e., the set will have a finite number of extremal points, as coherence in the energy eigenbasis can not be generated under thermal operations (See theorem 14 of Ref.
[
54
]
,
[
56
]
). In this case, we refer the set
{\cal T}(\rho)
as the
thermal polytope
. The thermal operation that transforms the initial energy incoherent state
\rho
to an extremal point of the thermal polytope
{\cal T}(\rho)
is called an
extremal thermal operation
. Later, we shall see that these extremal points of the set
{\cal T}(\rho)
and the extremal thermal operations play a crucial role in optimizing the performance of the three stroke engine.

Let us briefly describe the necessary and sufficient conditions for the interconversion problem for energy-incoherent states. In order to do so, we associate a probability vector
\bm{p}
, called the
population vector
, with an energy incoherent state
\rho
such that
p_{i}=\langle E_{i}|\rho|E_{i}\rangle
, where
|E_{i}\rangle
is an eigenstate of Hamiltonian
H_{S}
with eigenvalue
E_{i}
. Then, an energy incoherent state
\rho
can be converted to another energy incoherent state
\sigma
via a thermal operation
{\cal E}
iff there exists a stochastic matrix
T^{{\cal E}}
such that

T^{{\cal E}}\bm{p}=\bm{q}\quad\quad T^{{\cal E}}\bm{\gamma}=\bm{\gamma},

(19)

where
\bm{p}
,
\bm{q}
and
\bm{\gamma}
are the population vectors associated with
\rho
,
\sigma
, and
\gamma
, respectively. Here,
\gamma
is the Gibbs state of the system. We call such a stochastic matrix
T^{{\cal E}}
a
Gibbs-stochastic matrix
or
thermal process

[
54
,
59
]
. Employing the concept of relative majorization, one can prove that the existence of such a Gibbs-stochastic matrix is equivalent to the thermomajorization condition, which we have described in Appendix
B

[
54
,
60
,
61
]
. The set of all thermal processes for a given dimension is a polytope. Among the extremal thermal processes, we shall be interested in those thermal processes that correspond to the extremal thermal operation.

Let us illustrate the above-mentioned concepts for a two-level energy incoherent state, which will be crucial for optimizing the performance of the heat engine. For an energy incoherent initial state

\rho=\begin{pmatrix}p&0\\
0&1-p\end{pmatrix},

(20)

with Hamiltonian

H_{S}=\omega|1\rangle\!\langle 1|,

(21)

we associate the population vector

\bm{p}=\begin{pmatrix}p&1-p\end{pmatrix}^{T}.

(22)

The extremal thermal process in dimension
2
is given by
[
27
]
:

\quad\Bigg\{\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}1-e^{-\beta\omega}&1\\
e^{-\beta\omega}&0\\
\end{pmatrix}\Bigg\}.

(23)

Therefore, the thermal polytope for the state
\rho
in Eq. (
20
) is given by

{\cal T}(\rho)=\text{Conv}\Bigg\{\rho,\begin{pmatrix}1-pe^{-\beta\omega}&1\\
pe^{-\beta\omega}&0\end{pmatrix}\Bigg\},

(24)

where
\text{Conv}\{A\}
denotes the convex hull of the set
A
. Thus, any arbitrary element of the set
{\cal T}(\rho)
can be expressed as:

\lambda\begin{pmatrix}1-pe^{-\beta\omega}&1\\
pe^{-\beta\omega}&0\end{pmatrix}+(1-\lambda)\rho

(25)

with
\lambda\in[0,1]
.

Here, we would like to make an important remark. When we define thermal operations, we suppose that our system of interest is in contact with a very large bath that has an effectively infinite volume. In such an ideal scenario, all possible states within the set
{\cal T}(\rho)
can be realized. In a more practical context, various restricted subsets of states emerge within the thermal polytope
{\cal T}(\rho)
as a result of constraints involving the feasibility of thermal operations. In such cases, the range of
\lambda
in Eq. (
25
) is more generally, confined between

\displaystyle[0,\lambda^{\text{max}}_{\beta}],\;\text{where}\;0\leq\lambda^{\text{max}}_{\beta}\leq 1.

(26)

In this paper, two particular subsets that will be of interest to us are as follows:

1.

The set of states that can be achieved from a given state of two-level systems through thermal operations in the presence of a finite heat bath, which can be realized with a truncated harmonic oscillator.
[
62
]
.

2.

The set of states which are achievable from a given state of two-level systems via thermal operations that can be realized via rotating wave approximated Jaynes-Cummings interaction
[
63
]
.

After explaining all of the necessary concepts related to the functioning of the three-stroke engine, we will now proceed by describing the main results of this work.

III
Main Results

III.1
Thermodynamic framework

In this section, we shall develop the thermodynamic framework of the proposed heat engine. From the description of the three stroke heat engine that we have discussed in the previous section (see Ref.
II
), we can summarize the thermodynamic cycles of the three stroke engine as

\rho_{S}\rightarrow{\cal E}_{H}(\rho_{S})\rightarrow V{\cal E}_{H}(\rho_{S})V^{\dagger}\rightarrow{\cal E}_{C}\big(V{\cal E}_{H}(\rho_{S})V^{\dagger}\big)=\rho_{S}.

(27)

From the flowchart of Eq. (
27
), we can write the total change in average energy as

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E(\rho_{S})-E(\rho_{S})

(28)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E(\rho_{S})-E({\cal E}_{H}(\rho_{S}))+E({\cal E}_{H}(\rho_{S}))-E(V{\cal E}_{H}(\rho_{S})V^{\dagger})

\displaystyle+E(V{\cal E}_{H}(\rho_{S})V^{\dagger})-E(\rho_{S})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-Q_{H}+W-Q_{C},

where
E(\cdot)=\text{Tr}(H_{S}\;\cdot)
denotes average energy, and in the last line, we have used Eqs. (
5
), (
7
), and (
17
). Equation (
28
) establishes the
first law of thermodynamics
as the manifestation of the conservation of energy

W=Q_{H}+Q_{C},

(29)

which allows us to express the work done by the engine as the net average flow of heat. Then, the efficiency of the engine is defined as

\eta:=\frac{W}{Q_{H}}=\frac{Q_{C}+Q_{H}}{Q_{H}}=1+\frac{Q_{C}}{Q_{H}}.

(30)

Next, we demonstrate that the efficiency of the engine is upper bounded by the Carnot efficiency, therefore establishing the second law of thermodynamics. The result is stated in the following theorem.

Theorem 1
.

The efficiency of the three-stoke engine is bounded by the Carnot efficiency,

\eta\leq 1-\frac{\beta_{H}}{\beta_{C}}:=\eta_{\textnormal{Carnot}}.

(31)

The proof of the theorem can be found in appendix
A
. This establishes the thermodynamic framework of the three-stroke heat engine. We shall now optimize the work production and efficiency of the three-stroke engine.

III.2
Optimal performance of the three stroke engine

In this section, we characterize the optimal efficiency and work produced by the three-stroke engine.

Theorem 2
.

Consider a three-stroke engine with a two-level system as the working body with Hamiltonian
H_{S}=\omega|1\rangle\langle 1|
and the range of
\lambda
in Eq. (
26
) is restricted between
0
to
\lambda^{\textnormal{max}}_{H}
and
\lambda^{\textnormal{max}}_{C}
for the first and third strokes, respectively. Then, the optimal work produced by the engine and the optimal efficiency are given by

IV
Conclusion

In this paper, we studied a stroke-based engine in the microscopic regime that requires three strokes to complete the thermodynamic cycles. In particular, we considered the three-stroke engine that involves a two-level system as a working body that steers the energy flow from a hot heat bath to a cold heat bath. The transformation of the working body in the presence of both heat baths is described by thermal operations. We figured out the optimal protocol that leads to the optimal production of work as well as the optimal efficiency when one can perform a restricted class of thermal operations on the working body in the presence of hot and cold heat baths. We analyzed the optimal efficiency and work production by the engine for two such restricted classes: first, when one has access to finite-sized heat baths, and second, when the thermal operations involved in the first and third strokes are realized via the Jaynes-Cummings interaction. Furthermore, we showed that the obtained expressions of optimal work production and efficiency reduce to the expressions of optimal efficiency and work production given in Theorem 3 of Ref.
[
52
]
when we relax the restrictions, hence allowing any arbitrary thermal operation on the working body in the presence of hot and cold baths.

Our research paves the way for numerous exciting research directions. One could explore how the optimal efficiency and maximum work produced by the engine change with the dimension of the working body. Exploring how the engine’s optimal efficiency and maximum work production vary with the dimensions of the working body presents an intriguing challenge. The complexity of this problem lies in the intricate structure of the thermal cone, which becomes increasingly convoluted as the dimension of the working body grows. On top of that, the complexity inherent in the Hamiltonian structure of the working body further adds to the intricacy of the problem.

Investigating the significance of coherence in the operation of three-stroke engines presents a captivating avenue for research. In this paper, we have focused only on the working bodies that do not have any coherence in energy eigenbasis (i.e., in the eigenasis of Hamiltonian
H_{S}
). Ref.
[
52
]
demonstrated that coherence in the state of the working body, modeled as a two-level system, fails to improve the performance of a three-stroke engine. This raises the question: can this result be generalized for arbitrary dimensional working body? Specifically, it will be interesting to prove or disprove that the coherence present in the state of the working body is not useful to improve the performance of a three-stroke engine. However, characterizing the transformation of coherence in a state under thermal operations remains a formidable challenge, particularly beyond two dimensions. Nonetheless, related results suggest that the dephasing of the state in the local energy eigenbasis is compatible with thermal operations in all dimensions
[
56
,
54
]
. These results might be helpful in this endeavor.

This work centers on stroke-based heat engines, where work extraction and heat exchange happen in discrete, distinct time intervals. We anticipate a correspondence between the three-stroke discrete heat engine and the continuous heat engine. Notably, analyzing the continuous heat engine enables us to compute the engine’s power or rate of work extraction with time, while the proposed three-stroke heat engine yields the average work per cycle. Upon rigorously bridging the gap between these stroke-based discrete and continuous heat engines, an interesting question emerges: what is the minimum time required to complete a thermodynamic cycle? We envision that elucidating the connection between the three-stroke heat engine and the continuous heat engine could offer valuable insights into realizing the three-stroke heat engines experimentally. In a related study, as proposed in Ref.
[
64
]
, an experimental implementation of an autonomous refrigerator within the framework of cavity quantum electrodynamics was suggested. Moreover, recent work outlined in Ref.
[
34
]
explores the construction of an autonomous refrigerator using superconducting circuits. Therefore, we believe that designing the three-stroke heat engine is attainable within the frameworks of cavity QED and superconducting circuits.

of
\bm{p}
with respect to
\bm{\gamma}

(75)

is referred to as
\beta

Appendix D
Range of thermal operation with finite sized baths

In this section, we describe the range of thermal operations for a qubit system with a finite size heat bath. The thermal operations on a qubit system can be expressed as a convex mixture of two extremal thermal processes
\lambda T^{{\cal E}}+(1-\lambda)\mathbbm{1}
, where
0\leq\lambda\leq 1
and

\mathcal{E}=\begin{pmatrix}1-e^{-\beta\omega}&1\\
e^{-\beta\omega}&0\end{pmatrix}.

(95)

However, when the bath is of finite dimension the full range of
\lambda
is not allowed. Let’s consider the dimension of the heat bath is
d_{E}=d+1
. Tha Hamiltonians of the system and heat bath are

\displaystyle H_{S}=\omega|{1}\rangle\!\langle{1}|\,\,\mbox{and}\,\,H_{E}=\omega\sum_{n=0}^{d}n|{n}\rangle\!\langle{n}|

(96)

respectively. Note that we assume that the ground state energies of the system and heat bath are zero, and the energy levels are eqispaced. With this we can state the following theorem:

Theorem 5
.

The allowed range of thermal operation for a qubit system when the heat bath is finite, is

\lambda\in\{0,\lambda^{\max}\}
, where

\lambda^{\max}=\frac{1-e^{-\beta\omega d}}{1-e^{-\beta\omega(d+1)}}.

(97)

Proof.

As
[U,H_{S}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{E}+\mathbbm{1}_{S}\otimes H_{E}]=0
, then for a thermal operation with such a heat bath,
U
has the following
2\times 2
block-diagonal structure in the energy eigenbasis:

U=\mathbbm{1}_{\{|{0}\rangle_{S}|{0}\rangle_{E}\}}\bigoplus_{i=1}^{d}V_{i_{\{|{0}\rangle_{S}|{i}\rangle_{E},|{1}\rangle_{S}|{i-1}\rangle_{E}\}}}\oplus\mathbbm{1}_{\{|{1}\rangle_{S}|{d}\rangle_{E}\}},

(98)

where
V_{i}
’s are
2\times 2
unitary matrix in the sub-space
\{|{0}\rangle_{S}|{i}\rangle_{E},|{1}\rangle_{S}|{i-1}\rangle_{E}\}
and
\mathbbm{1}
’s are basically 1. Further, we take initial system state
\rho_{S}
to be diagonal in the energy eigenbasis, i.e.,

\rho_{S}=\begin{pmatrix}p&0\\
0&1-p\end{pmatrix}.

(99)

The state of the bath is

\rho_{E}=\frac{e^{-\beta H_{E}}}{Z_{H_{E}}}=\frac{1}{Z_{H_{E}}}\begin{pmatrix}1&0&\cdots&0\\
0&e^{-\beta\omega}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&e^{-d\beta\omega}\end{pmatrix},

(100)

where
Z_{H_{E}}=\mbox{Tr}\left(e^{-\beta H_{E}}\right)
. Hence, the initial state of the system and bath has the following form in the energy eigenbasis (see equation Eq. (
98
)):

\rho_{S}\otimes\rho_{E}=\frac{1}{Z_{H_{E}}}=p\bigoplus_{i=1}^{d}\begin{pmatrix}pe^{-i\beta\omega}&0\\
0&(1-p)e^{-(i-1)\beta\omega}\end{pmatrix}\oplus(1-p)e^{-d\beta\omega}.

(101)

Note that the energies of the blocks from left to right are
0,\omega,2\omega,\ldots,(d+1)\omega
. As the unitary operation
\left(U\right)
and the initial state of the system and bath have similar block diagonal structures,
U\left(\rho_{S}\otimes\rho_{E}\right)U^{\dagger}
also has a similar block diagonal structure. Therefore, it is enough to find out the elements of one block. Consider a block position
j
with energy
j\omega
, then we have

\displaystyle\rho_{SE}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle V_{j}(\rho_{S}\otimes\rho_{E})V_{j}^{\dagger}=\begin{pmatrix}e^{i\phi_{j}}\cos\theta_{j}&e^{i\alpha_{j}}\sin\theta_{j}\\
-e^{-i\alpha_{j}}\sin\theta_{j}&e^{-i\phi_{j}}\cos\theta_{j}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}pe^{-j\beta\omega}&0\\
0&(1-p)e^{-(j-1)\beta\omega}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}e^{i\phi_{j}}\cos\theta_{j}&-e^{i\alpha_{j}}\sin\theta_{j}\\
e^{-i\alpha_{j}}\sin\theta_{j}&e^{i\phi_{j}}\cos\theta_{j}\end{pmatrix}

(102)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}pe^{-j\beta\omega}\cos^{2}\theta_{j}+(1-p)e^{-(j-1)\beta\omega}\sin^{2}\theta_{j}&e^{i(\phi_{j}+\alpha_{j})}\left(e^{-(j-1)\beta\omega}(1-p)-pe^{-j\beta\omega}\right)\sin\theta_{j}\cos\theta_{j}\\
e^{-i(\phi_{j}+\alpha_{j})}\left(e^{-(j-1)\beta\omega}(1-p)-pe^{-j\beta\omega}\right)\sin\theta_{j}\cos\theta_{j}&pe^{-j\beta\omega}\sin^{2}\theta_{j}+(1-p)e^{-(j-1)\beta\omega}\cos^{2}\theta_{j}\end{pmatrix},

where
V_{j}
is an arbitrary
2\times 2
special unitary matrix. After tracing out the thermal bath from the final state
\rho_{SE}
, we get the final state of the system as follows

\rho^{\prime}_{S}=\frac{1}{Z_{H_{E}}}\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{A}&0\\
0&Z_{H_{E}}-\mathcal{A}\end{pmatrix},

(103)

where
\mathcal{A}=p+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left(pe^{-j\beta\omega}\cos^{2}\theta_{j}+(1-p)e^{-(j-1)\beta\omega}\sin^{2}\theta_{j}\right)
.

On the other hand, under thermal operations as described above theorem
5
, one of the diagonal elements of the state
\rho_{S}
changes to

\displaystyle p\rightarrow p^{\prime}=1-\left((1-p)(1-\lambda)+e^{-\beta\omega}p\lambda\right).

(104)

To find the solution of
\lambda
, we need to compare
p^{\prime}
with
\mathcal{A}/Z_{H_{E}}
, i.e.,

\lambda=\frac{1}{Z_{H}}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sin^{2}\theta_{j}e^{-\beta(j-1)\omega}.

(105)

The maximum value of
\lambda
is obtained when
\sin^{2}\theta_{j}=1

\forall\theta_{j}
and the value is

\lambda^{\max}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{e^{-\beta(j-1)\omega}}{Z_{H}}=\frac{1-e^{-\beta\omega d}}{1-e^{-\beta\omega(d+1)}}.

(106)

This completes the proof.
∎

Therefore, the range of
\lambda
is
\{0,\lambda^{\max}\}
. It is easy to check that for
d\rightarrow\infty
,
\lambda^{\max}=1
, and thus all thermal operations are possible. If the heat bath is a qubit, then
\lambda^{\max}=1/\left(1+e^{\beta\omega}\right)
, which implies that the thermal operation corresponds to
\lambda^{\max}
just thermalizes any input system.
