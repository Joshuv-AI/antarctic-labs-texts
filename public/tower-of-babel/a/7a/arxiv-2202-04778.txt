Quasi-triangle inequality for absolute correlation distance
Stanislav Dubrovskiy

Abstract
. We show that absolute correlation distance satisfies a K K -relaxed triangle inequality, with the best K = 2 K=2 .



High correlation is not a transitive relation between datasets (considered as vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
):
u
correlated with
v
and
w
, does not in general imply
v
and
w
are correlated . For distances determined by a metric, there is the triangle inequality to quantify this. Correlation too gives rise to a distance - the absolute correlation distance,
d_{|corr|}
, which does not satisfy the triangle inequality (cf.
[
DL
]
). See T. Tao’s blog
[
T
]
for discussion, examples and results. We show that it nevertheless satisfies a
K
-relaxed triangle inequality, with
K=2
as the best constant (that is any
K<2
fails).

Absolute correlation distance between two samples
X
and
Y
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is given as

(1)

d_{|corr|}(X,Y)=1-\left|\frac{\langle X-\bar{X},\,Y-\bar{Y}\rangle}{\lVert X-\bar{X}\rVert\cdot\lVert Y-\bar{Y}\rVert}\right|.

Here
\bar{X}
is the arithmetic mean of
X
, and
\lVert X\rVert
- its euclidean norm.
While useful as evidence of linear relation between
X
and
Y
,
d_{|corr|}
is not a metric, in particular it does not satisfy triangle inequality. Take for example three vectors (of zero mean) forming angles of
\pi/2
,
\pi/4
, and
\pi/4
(possible in dimensions
n\geq 3
):

(2)

1-|\cos\pi/2|\nleq 1-|\cos\pi/4|+1-|\cos\pi/4|\ .

In lieu of triangle inequality, an estimate of the type

(3)

d_{|corr|}(X,Z)\leq K(d_{|corr|}(X,Y)+d_{|corr|}(Y,Z)),

for some
K
(a
K
-relaxed triangle inequality), if true, would be useful as a measure of transitivity of correlation closeness.

The above example (
2
) implies
K\geq\frac{1}{2-\sqrt{2}}
, which is not far from the best possible
K=2
.

Theorem 1

(Quasi-triangle inequality)
.

Absolute correlation distance
d_{|corr|}
satisfies a
K
-relaxed triangle inequality, with the best
K=2
. Namely, for
X,Y,Z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
:

(4)

d_{|corr|}(X,Z)\leq K(d_{|corr|}(X,Y)+d_{|corr|}(Y,Z))

for
K\geq 2
. The inequality fails (at least for some
X,Y,Z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
) for
K<2
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we can assume
X,Y,Z\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
(a unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
).
In fact we can assume that
X,Y,Z\in\mathbb{S}^{n-2}=\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\cap\Pi_{0}
, where
\Pi_{0}
is a hyperplane of zero mean vectors,
defined by its normal:
{\bf n}=(1,1,\ldots,1)
.
Then
d_{|corr|}(X,Y)=1-|\cos\widehat{XY}|
, and (
4
) can be rewritten as:

1-|\cos\widehat{XZ}\,|\leq K(1-|\cos\widehat{XY}|+1-|\cos\widehat{YZ}|)\ .

To simplify notation let us use
\gamma=\widehat{XZ}
,
\alpha=\widehat{XY}
and
\beta=\widehat{YZ}
.
Considering angles (in radian measure) as their corresponding big circle arcs on the unit sphere, we can make use of the triangle inequality from Riemann geometry on
\mathbb{S}^{n-2}
:

(5)

|\alpha-\beta|\leq\gamma\leq\alpha+\beta.

Call the left side of (
4
)
f(\gamma)
, and its right hand side
g(\alpha,\beta)
:

f(\gamma)=1-|\cos\gamma|,\quad g(\alpha,\beta)=K(1-|\cos\alpha|+1-|\cos\beta|).

First assume
\gamma=\alpha+\beta
. Since
0\leq\alpha,\beta\leq\pi
it would suffice to establish the inequality on
I=[0,\pi]\times[0,\pi]
. Cover
I
with four regions:

R_{1}=[0,\pi/2]\times[0,\pi/2],R_{2}=[\pi/2,\pi]\times[0,\pi/2],R_{3}=[0,\pi/2]\times[\pi/2,\pi],

\text{ and }R_{4}=[\pi/2,\pi]\times[\pi/2,\pi].

It is enough to show

(6)

f(\alpha+\beta)\leq g(\alpha,\beta)

in each of these regions.

Begin with
R_{1}
. Since the left side of (
6
) has lines
\alpha+\beta=C
(constant) as level curves, all we need to show is that minimum of
g
, restricted to each
f
-level curve, is not less than the value of
f
on it:

(7)

f(C)\leq\min_{\alpha+\beta=C}g(\alpha,\beta)

If
\beta=C-\alpha
(on the level line), we see that

g^{\prime}_{\alpha}=K(\sin\alpha-\sin\beta),

and the minimum is achieved when
\alpha=\beta
. Thus we need to compare

G(\alpha)=\left.g\right|_{\alpha=\beta}=2K(1-\cos\alpha)

with

F(\alpha)=\left.f\right|_{\alpha=\beta}=\left[\begin{array}[]{ll}1-\cos{2\alpha}=2(1-\cos{\alpha})(1+\cos{\alpha})&\text{if }0\leq\alpha\leq\pi/4,\\
1+\cos{2\alpha}\leq 1&\text{if }\pi/4\leq\alpha\leq\pi/2.\end{array}\right.

While
\alpha\leq\pi/4,\ (1+\cos{\alpha})\leq K
, and the inequality holds. At
\pi/4
we check that

(8)

G(\pi/4)=K(2-\sqrt{2})>1,

and increasing from there, while
F(\alpha)\leq 1
, thus
F(\alpha)\leq G(\alpha)
on
R_{1}
when
K\geq 2
.

Let us now consider
R_{2}
and again, behavior of
g
on level lines of
f
. Since

\left.g^{\prime}_{\alpha}\right|_{\alpha+\beta=C}=-K(\sin\alpha+\sin\beta)<0

we see that
g
is strictly decreasing on any level line throughout
R_{2}
(the only 0 of derivative is achieved at
(\pi,0)
, on the boundary). However let us compare the values of the two functions on two edges of the region:
R_{2}^{bottom}=[\pi/2,\pi]\times 0
and
R_{2}^{right}=\pi\times[0,\pi/2]
. On the bottom
f
and
g
are identical up to a multiple of
K
, so inequality holds. On the right edge,
\left.g\right|_{R_{2}^{right}}=K(1-\cos\beta)
, while
\left.f\right|_{R_{2}^{right}}=1-|\cos(\pi+\beta)|=1-\cos\beta\leq K(1-\cos\beta)
. This amounts to (
6
) holding on right ends of all level line segments in
R_{2}
, while
g
stays above and
f
the same as their respective right end values throughout the level line segments.

While the cases of
R_{3}
and
R_{4}
are analogous to
R_{2}
and
R_{1}
respectively, they are in fact unnecessary to consider as we shall shortly discover.

We would like now to remove assumption
\gamma=\alpha+\beta
. This is easy when
\alpha,\beta\in R_{1}
. We cover
R_{1}
with lower left corner region,
R_{1}^{\llcorner}=\{\alpha+\beta\leq\pi/2\}
, and upper right corner one,
R_{1}^{\urcorner}=\overline{R_{1}\setminus R_{1}^{\llcorner}}
. Since
f(\gamma)
increases on
R_{1}^{\llcorner}
(while
\gamma\in[0,\pi/2]
), we can use (
5
) and (
6
) to obtain:
f(\gamma)\leq f(\alpha+\beta)\leq g(\alpha,\beta)
. When
\alpha,\beta\in\hskip 0.0ptR_{1}^{\hskip 0.0pt\urcorner}
we have

(9)

\left.g\right|_{R_{1}^{\urcorner}}>1

according to (
8
), while
f\leq 1
always.

Similar to
R_{1}
above,
R_{2}
splits into
R_{2}^{\ulcorner}=\{\alpha-\beta\leq\pi/2\}
and
R_{2}^{\lrcorner}=\overline{R_{2}\setminus R_{2}^{\ulcorner}}
. We use the fact that
g
is symmetric against the line
\alpha=\pi/2
. Thus
\left.g\right|_{R_{2}^{\ulcorner}}>1
due to (
9
), and the inequality holds on
R_{2}^{\ulcorner}
. For
\alpha,\beta\in R_{2}^{\lrcorner}
(the mirror image of
R_{1}^{\llcorner}
) we notice that

(10)

g(\alpha,\beta)=g(\pi-\alpha,\beta)\geq f(\pi-\alpha+\beta)=f(\alpha-\beta),

with the first equality due to
g
-symmetry against
(\alpha,\beta)\mapsto(\pi-\alpha,\beta)
, the last one due to

f
-symmetry against
\gamma\mapsto\pi-\gamma
, and the middle inequality due to (
6
).
However
\alpha-\beta\geq\pi/2
on
R_{2}^{\lrcorner}
, so together with (
5
) we obtain:

(11)

\pi/2\leq\alpha-\beta\leq\gamma\leq\pi,

as
\gamma
also represents an angle between two vectors. Couple (
11
) with the fact that
f
decreases on
[\pi/2,\pi]
, and we can extend (
10
) as follows:

(12)

f(\alpha-\beta)\geq f(\gamma).

Combining (
10
) with (
12
) we obtain the desired result on
R_{2}
.

R_{3}
and
R_{4}
can be treated via the following trick. Consider
-Y
instead of
Y
in Theorem
1
.
Angle
\gamma
will not change, while
(\alpha,\beta)\mapsto(A,B)=(\pi-\alpha,\pi-\beta)
.
Triangle inequality must hold with the new angles too:

(13)

|A-B|\leq\gamma\leq A+B.

Thus for
(\alpha,\beta)\in R_{3}\text{ or }R_{4}
the corresponding
(A,B)
, in
R_{2}
or
R_{1}
respectively, will satisfy

f(\gamma)\leq g(A,B)

with the same
\gamma
. Also
g
is symmetric with respect to maps
x\mapsto\pi-x
in either argument, hence
g(A,B)=g(\alpha,\beta)
, and we are done with
R_{3}
,
R_{4}
.

To prove sharpness, let us consider the case when
\gamma=\alpha+\beta
, and
\alpha=\beta
. Then dividing the left side of (
4
) by its right side, and taking a limit as
\alpha\to 0
we have:

\lim_{\alpha\to 0}\left(\frac{1-\cos{2\alpha}}{2-2\cos\alpha}\right)\leq K\iff K\geq\lim_{\alpha\to 0}\left(\frac{2-2\cos^{2}{\alpha}}{2-2\cos\alpha}\right)=2

Thus
K
cannot be less than 2 and our estimate is sharp.
∎
