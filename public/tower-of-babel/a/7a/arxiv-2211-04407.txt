Multiple Packing: Lower Bounds via Infinite Constellations
Yihan Zhang 1 Shashank Vatedka 2

Abstract
We study the problem of high-dimensional multiple packing in Euclidean space. Multiple packing is a natural generalization of sphere packing and is defined as follows. Let N > 0 N>0 and L ∈ ℤ ≥ 2 L\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 2} . A multiple packing is a set 𝒞 \mathcal{C} of points in ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} such that any point in ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} lies in the intersection of at most L − 1 L-1 balls of radius n ​ N \sqrt{nN} around points in 𝒞 \mathcal{C} . Given a well-known connection with coding theory, multiple packings can be viewed as the Euclidean analog of list-decodable codes, which are well-studied for finite fields. In this paper, we derive the best known lower bounds on the optimal density of list-decodable infinite constellations for constant L L under a stronger notion called average-radius multiple packing. To this end, we apply tools from high-dimensional geometry and large deviation theory.

V
Our techniques

We summarize our techniques below.

To obtain lower bounds on the largest multiple packing density, our basic strategy is random coding with expurgation, a standard tool from information theory.
To show the existence of a list-decodable code of rate
R
, we simply randomly sample
e^{nR}
points independently each according to a certain distribution.
There might be bad lists of size
L
that violates the multiple packing condition.
We then throw away (a.k.a. expurgate) one point from each of the bad lists.
By carefully analyzing the error event and choosing a proper rate, we can guarantee that the remaining code has essentially the same rate after the removal process.
We then get a list-decodable code of rate
R
by noting that the remaining code contains no bad lists.

In the above framework, the key ingredient is a good estimate on the probability of the error event, i.e., the probability that the
list-decoding radius
of a size-
L
list is smaller than
\sqrt{nN}
.
Under the standard notion of multiple packing, the list-decoding radius is the
Chebyshev radius
of the list, i.e., the radius of the smallest ball containing the list.
Under the average-radius notion of multiple packing, the (squared) list-decoding radius is the
average squared radius
of the list, i.e., the average squared distance from each point in the list to the centroid of the list.

Using the above idea, we first construct a
finite
codebook with minimum average squared radius
nN
and supported over the hypercube
[-K,K]^{n}
for a suitably chosen
K
. This is obtained by expurgating a random codebook obtained by choosing points independently and uniformly from
[-K,K]^{n}
. The finite codebook is then tiled across
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
to obtain an infinite constellation with the aforementioned density and minimum average squared radius
nN
. This construction is loosely inspired by the infinite constellations
[
Pol94
]
which was originally studied in the context of coding for the additive white Gaussian noise channel. A similar construction was used by
[
ZV22b
]
to derive lower bounds on
C_{\mathrm{LD}}(N)
for large
L
.

The exact exponents of the probability of the error event are obtained using Cramér’s large deviation principle and the Laplace’s method.

As a technical contribution, we discover several new representations of the average radius and the Chebyshev radius.
They play crucial roles in facilitating the analyses and the applications of some of these representations go beyond the scope of this paper.
To name a few, the average squared radius of a list can be written as a quadratic form associated with the list.
This representation is used to analyze
Gaussian codes and spherical codes in
[
ZV22c
]
and infinite constellations in this paper.
The average squared radius can also be written as the difference between the average (squared) norm of points in the list and the (squared) norm of the centroid of the list.
This representation is used to analyze spherical codes and ball codes in
[
ZV22c
]
.
The average squared radius can be further written as the average pairwise distance of the list.
This allows us to give a one-line proof of the Blachman–Few reduction and its strengthened version
[
ZV22c
]
.
Yet another way of writing the average squared radius using the average norm and the average pairwise correlation turns out to be useful for the proof of the Plotkin-type bound
[
ZV22c
]
.

is defined as, for any

\mathds{1}_{{\mathcal{A}}}(x)\coloneqq\begin{cases}1,&x\in{\mathcal{A}}\\
0,&x\notin{\mathcal{A}}\end{cases}.

At times, we will slightly abuse notation by saying that
\mathds{1}_{{\mathsf{A}}}
is
1
when event
{\mathsf{A}}
happens and 0 otherwise. Note that
\mathds{1}_{{\mathcal{A}}}(\cdot)=\mathds{1}{\left\{\cdot\in{\mathcal{A}}\right\}}
.

Sets.

For any two nonempty sets
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
with addition and multiplication by a real scalar, let
{\mathcal{A}}+{\mathcal{B}}

denote the Minkowski sum
of them which is defined as

{\mathcal{A}}+{\mathcal{B}}\coloneqq\left\{a+b\colon a\in{\mathcal{A}},b\in{\mathcal{B}}\right\}
.
If
{\mathcal{A}}=\{x\}

and
for
\{x\}+{\mathcal{B}}
.
For any
r\in{\mathbb{R}}
, the

r{\mathcal{A}}\coloneqq\left\{r{\underline{a}}:{\underline{a}}\in{\mathcal{A}}\right\}
.
In particular,
-{\mathcal{A}}\coloneqq(-1){\mathcal{A}}
.

For
M\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}
, we let
[M]

M
positive integers
\{1,2,\cdots,M\}

Geometry.

Let
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{2}
denote the Euclidean/

{\underline{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
,

\left\|{\underline{x}}\right\|_{2}\coloneqq\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\underline{x}}(i)^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

With slight abuse of notation, we let
|\cdot|
denote the “volume” of a set w.r.t. a measure that is obvious from the context.
If
{\mathcal{A}}
is a finite set, then

w.r.t. the counting measure.

\displaystyle\aff({\mathcal{A}})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}{\underline{a}}_{i}:k\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 1};\;\forall i\in[k],{\underline{a}}_{i}\in{\mathcal{A}},\lambda_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}},\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}=1\right\}

denote the

{\mathcal{A}}
.
If

with nonempty interior and

w.r.t. the

If

is a

, then

denotes the

{\mathcal{A}}
.

The closed

{\mathcal{B}}^{n}\coloneqq\left\{{\underline{y}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\colon\left\|{\underline{y}}\right\|_{2}\leq 1\right\}.

The

-dimensional Euclidean unit sphere is defined as

For any
{\underline{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}

VIII
Preliminaries

Lemma 3

(Change of variable)
.

Let
{\mathcal{U}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be an open set and
\varphi\colon{\mathcal{U}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be an injective differentiable function with continuous partial derivatives, the Jacobian of which is nonzero for every
{\underline{x}}\in{\mathcal{U}}
.
Then for any compactly supported, continuous function
f\colon\varphi({\mathcal{U}})\to{\mathbb{R}}
, the substitution
{\underline{v}}=\varphi({\underline{u}})
yields the following formula

\displaystyle\int_{\varphi({\mathcal{U}})}f({\underline{v}})\mathrm{d}{\underline{v}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{U}}f(\varphi({\underline{u}}))\left|\det(\nabla\varphi)({\underline{u}})\right|\mathrm{d}{\underline{u}},

where
\nabla\varphi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
denotes the Jacobian matrix of
\varphi
.

Theorem 4

(Laplace’s method)
.

Suppose
f\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is a twice continuously differentiable function on
{\mathcal{A}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, and there exists a unique point
\vec{t}^{*}\in\interior({\mathcal{A}})
(where
\interior(\cdot)
denotes the interior of a set) such that

\displaystyle f(\vec{t}^{*})=\min_{\vec{t}\in{\mathcal{A}}}f(\vec{t}),\quad(\hess f)(\vec{t}^{*})\succ 0,

where
\hess f\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
denotes the Hessian matrix of
f
.
Suppose
g(\vec{t})
is positive.
Then

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathcal{A}}}g(\vec{t})e^{-Mf(\vec{t})}\mathrm{d}\vec{t}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle M\to\infty}}{{\asymp}}e^{-Mf(\vec{t}^{*})}\left(\frac{2\pi}{M}\right)^{d/2}\frac{g(\vec{t}^{*})}{\sqrt{\det((\hess f)(\vec{t}^{*}))}}.

Theorem 5

(Cramér)
.

Let
\left\{{\mathbf{x}}_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n}
be a sequence of i.i.d. real-valued random variables.
Let
{\mathbf{s}}_{n}\coloneq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\mathbf{x}}_{i}
.
Then for any closed
{\mathcal{F}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}
,

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\ln\Pr\left[{\mathbf{s}}_{n}\in{\mathcal{F}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq-\inf_{x\in{\mathcal{F}}}\sup_{\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}}\left\{\lambda x-\ln\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\lambda{\mathbf{x}}_{1}}\right]\right\};

and for any open
{\mathcal{G}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}
,

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\ln\Pr\left[{\mathbf{s}}_{n}\in{\mathcal{G}}\right]

\displaystyle\geq-\inf_{x\in{\mathcal{G}}}\sup_{\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}}\left\{\lambda x-\ln\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\lambda{\mathbf{x}}_{1}}\right]\right\}.

Furthermore, when
{\mathcal{F}}
or
{\mathcal{G}}
corresponds to the upper (resp. lower) tail of
{\mathbf{s}}_{n}
, the maximizer
\lambda\geq 0
(resp.
\lambda\leq 0
).

IX
Basic definitions and facts

Given the intimate connection between packing and error-correcting codes, we will interchangeably use the terms “multiple packing” and “list-decodable code”.
The parameter
L\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 2}
is called the
multiplicity of overlap
or the
list-size
.
The parameter
N
is called the
noise power constraints
.
Elements of a packing are called either
points
or
codewords
.
We will call a size-
L
subset of a packing an
L
-list
.
This paper is only concerned with the fundamental limits of multiple packing for asymptotically large dimension
n\to\infty
.
When we say “a” code
{\mathcal{C}}
, we always mean an infinite sequence of codes
\left\{{\mathcal{C}}_{i}\right\}_{i\geq 1}
where
{\mathcal{C}}_{i}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{i}}
and
\left\{n_{i}\right\}_{i\geq 1}
is an increasing sequence of positive integers.

In the rest of this section, we list a sequence of formal definitions and some facts associated with these definitions.

Definition 1

(Multiple packing)
.

Let
N>0
and
L\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 2}
.
A subset
{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is called a
(N,L-1)
-list-decodable code
(a.k.a. an
(N,L-1)
-multiple packing
) if for every
{\underline{y}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
,

\displaystyle\left|{\mathcal{C}}\cap{\mathcal{B}}^{n}({\underline{y}},\sqrt{nN})\right|\leq L-1.

(6)

The
rate
(a.k.a.
density
) of
{\mathcal{C}}
is defined as

\displaystyle R({\mathcal{C}})\coloneqq

\displaystyle\limsup_{K\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\ln\frac{\left|{\mathcal{C}}\cap(K{\mathcal{B}})\right|}{\left|K{\mathcal{B}}\right|},

(7)

where
{\mathcal{B}}
is an arbitrary centrally symmetric connected compact set in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
with nonempty interior.

Remark 1
.

Common choices of
{\mathcal{B}}
include the unit ball
{\mathcal{B}}^{n}
, the unit cube
[-1,1]^{n}
, the fundamental Voronoi region
{\mathcal{V}}_{\Lambda}
of a (full-rank) lattice
\Lambda\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, etc.
Some choices of
{\mathcal{B}}
may be more convenient than the others for analyzing certain ensembles of packings.
Therefore, we do not fix the choice of
{\mathcal{B}}
in
Definition

1
.

Remark 2
.

The rate of a packing (as per
Equation

7
) is also called the
normalized logarithmic density
in the literature.
It measures the the normalized number of points per unit volume.

Note that the condition given by
Equation

6
is equivalent to that for any
({\underline{x}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{x}}_{L})\in\binom{{\mathcal{C}}}{L}
,

\displaystyle\bigcap_{i=1}^{L}{\mathcal{B}}^{n}({\underline{x}}_{i},\sqrt{nN})=\emptyset.

(8)

Definition 2

(Chebyshev radius and average squared radius of a list)
.

Let
{\underline{x}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{x}}_{L}
be
L
points in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.
Then the
squared Chebyshev radius

\rad^{2}({\underline{x}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{x}}_{L})
of
{\underline{x}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{x}}_{L}
is defined as the (squared) radius of the smallest ball containing
{\underline{x}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{x}}_{L}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\rad^{2}({\underline{x}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{x}}_{L})\coloneqq

\displaystyle\min_{{\underline{y}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\max_{i\in[L]}\left\|{\underline{x}}_{i}-{\underline{y}}\right\|_{2}^{2}.

(9)

The
average squared radius

\overline{\rad}^{2}({\underline{x}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{x}}_{L})
of
{\underline{x}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{x}}_{L}
is defined as the average squared distance to the centroid, i.e.,

\displaystyle\overline{\rad}^{2}({\underline{x}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{x}}_{L})\coloneqq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i=1}^{L}{\left\|{\underline{x}}_{i}-{\underline{x}}\right\|_{2}^{2}},

(10)

where
{\underline{x}}\coloneqq\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i=1}^{L}{\underline{x}}_{i}
is the centroid of
{\underline{x}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{x}}_{L}
. We refer to the square root of the average squared radius as the average radius of the list.

Remark 3
.

One should note that for an
L
-list
{\mathcal{L}}
of points, the smallest ball containing
{\mathcal{L}}
is not necessarily the same as the
circumscribed ball
, i.e., the ball such that all points in
{\mathcal{L}}
live on the boundary of the ball.
The circumscribed ball of the polytope
\conv\left\{{\mathcal{L}}\right\}
spanned by the points in
{\mathcal{L}}
may not exist.
If it does exist, it is not necessarily the smallest one containing
{\mathcal{L}}
.
However, whenever it exists, the smallest ball containing
{\mathcal{L}}
must be the circumscribed ball of a certain
subset
of
{\mathcal{L}}
.

Remark 4
.

We remark that the motivation behind the definition of average squared radius (
Equation

10
) is to replace the maximization in
Equation

9
with average.

\displaystyle\min_{{\underline{y}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{{\mathbf{i}}\sim[L]}\left[\left\|{\underline{x}}_{\mathbf{i}}-{\underline{y}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\min_{{\underline{y}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left({\underline{x}}_{i}(j)-{\underline{y}}(j)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\min_{(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\left({\underline{x}}_{i}(j)-y_{j}\right)^{2}

(11)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\min_{y_{j}\in{\mathbb{R}}}\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\left({\underline{x}}_{i}(j)-y_{j}\right)^{2}

(12)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left({\underline{x}}_{i}(j)-y_{j}^{*}\right)^{2}

(13)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\left\|{\underline{x}}_{i}-{\underline{x}}\right\|_{2}^{2}.

(14)

Equation

12
holds since the inner summation
\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\left({\underline{x}}_{i}(j)-{\underline{y}}(j)\right)^{2}
in
Equation

11
only depends on
y_{j}
among all
y_{1},\cdots,y_{n}
.

Equation

13
follows since for each
j
, the minimizer of the minimization in
Equation

12
is
y_{j}^{*}\coloneqq\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i=1}^{L}{\underline{x}}_{i}(j)
.
In
Equation

14
, the minimizer
{\underline{y}}^{*}
equals
{\underline{x}}\coloneqq\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i=1}^{L}{\underline{x}}_{i}
.

Definition 3

(Chebyshev radius and average squared radius of a code)
.

Given a code
{\mathcal{C}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
of rate
R
, the squared
(L-1)
-list-decoding radius
of
{\mathcal{C}}
is defined as

\displaystyle\rad^{2}_{L}({\mathcal{C}})\coloneqq

\displaystyle\min_{{\mathcal{L}}\in\binom{{\mathcal{C}}}{L}}\rad^{2}({\mathcal{L}}).

(15)

The
(L-1)
-average squared radius
of
{\mathcal{C}}
is defined as

\displaystyle\overline{\rad}^{2}_{L}({\mathcal{C}})\coloneqq

\displaystyle\min_{{\mathcal{L}}\in\binom{{\mathcal{C}}}{L}}\overline{\rad}^{2}({\mathcal{L}}).

(16)

Definition 4

(Average-radius multiple packing)
.

A subset
{\mathcal{C}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is called an
(N,L-1)
-average-radius list-decodable code
(a.k.a. an
(N,L-1)
-average-radius multiple packing
) if
\overline{\rad}^{2}_{L}({\mathcal{C}})>nN
.
The
rate
(a.k.a.
density
)
R({\mathcal{C}})
of
{\mathcal{C}}
is given by
Equation

7
.
The
(N,L-1)
-average-radius list-decoding capacity
(a.k.a.
(N,L-1)
average-radius multiple packing density
) is defined as

\overline{C}_{L-1}(N)\coloneqq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\limsup_{{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\colon\overline{\rad}^{2}_{L}({\mathcal{C}})>nN}R({\mathcal{C}}).

The
squared
(L-1)
-average-radius list-decoding radius
at rate
R
(without input constraint) is defined as

\displaystyle\overline{\rad}^{2}_{L}(R)\coloneqq

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\limsup_{{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\colon R({\mathcal{C}})\geq R}\overline{\rad}^{2}_{L}({\mathcal{C}}).

Note that
(L-1)
-list-decodability defined by
Equation

8
is equivalent to
\rad^{2}_{L}({\mathcal{C}})>nN
.
We also define the
(N,L-1)
-list-decoding capacity
(a.k.a.
(N,L-1)
-multiple packing density
)
C_{L-1}(N)
and the
(L-1)
-list-decoding radius

\rad^{2}_{L}(R)
at rate
R
:

\displaystyle C_{L-1}(N)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\limsup_{{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\colon\rad^{2}_{L}({\mathcal{C}})>nN}R({\mathcal{C}}),

\displaystyle\rad^{2}_{L}(R)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\limsup_{{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\colon R({\mathcal{C}})\geq R}\rad^{2}_{L}({\mathcal{C}}).

Since the average radius is at most the Chebyshev radius, average-radius list-decodability is stronger than regular list-decodability.
Any lower (resp. upper) bound on
\overline{C}_{L-1}(N)
(resp.
C_{L-1}(N)
) is automatically a lower (resp. upper) bound on
C_{L-1}(N)
(resp.
\overline{C}_{L-1}(N)
).
Proving upper/lower bounds on
C_{L-1}(N)
(resp.
\overline{C}_{L-1}(N)
) is equivalent to proving upper/lower bounds on
\rad^{2}_{L}(R)
(resp.
\overline{\rad}^{2}_{L}(R)
).

IX-A

Different notions of density of packings

We measure the density of
a packing using
Equation

7
.
In the literature, there exists another commonly used notion of density for multiple packings.
It counts the fraction of space occupied by the union of the balls of radius
\sqrt{nN}
centered around points in the packing.
Specifically, for an
(N,L-1)
-packing
{\mathcal{C}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
,

\displaystyle\Delta({\mathcal{C}})\coloneqq

\displaystyle\limsup_{P\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\ln\left(\left|\bigcup_{{\underline{x}}\in{\mathcal{C}}}{\mathcal{B}}^{n}({\underline{x}},\sqrt{nN})\cap{\mathcal{B}}^{n}(\sqrt{nP})\right|\big/\left|{\mathcal{B}}^{n}(\sqrt{nP})\right|\right).

(17)

We prove the following statement.

Theorem 6
.

Let
N>0
and
L\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 2}
.
Let
{\mathcal{C}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be an
(N,L-1)
-multiple packing.
Then
\Delta({\mathcal{C}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n\to\infty}}{{\asymp}}R({\mathcal{C}})+\frac{1}{2}\ln(2\pi eN)
.

Proof.

Note that for sufficiently large
P
,

\displaystyle\left|\bigcup_{{\underline{x}}\in{\mathcal{C}}}{\mathcal{B}}^{n}({\underline{x}},\sqrt{nN})\cap{\mathcal{B}}^{n}(\sqrt{nP})\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim\left|{\mathcal{C}}\cap{\mathcal{B}}^{n}(\sqrt{nP})\right|\cdot\left|{\mathcal{B}}^{n}(\sqrt{nN})\right|.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\Delta({\mathcal{C}})

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{P\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\ln\frac{\left|{\mathcal{C}}\cap{\mathcal{B}}^{n}(\sqrt{nP})\right|\cdot\left|{\mathcal{B}}^{n}(\sqrt{nN})\right|}{\left|{\mathcal{B}}^{n}(\sqrt{nP})\right|}

\displaystyle=R({\mathcal{C}})+\frac{1}{n}\ln\left|{\mathcal{B}}^{n}(\sqrt{nN})\right|

\displaystyle\asymp R({\mathcal{C}})+\frac{1}{n}\ln\left(\sqrt{nN}^{n}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi n}}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi e}{n}}^{n}\right)

\displaystyle\asymp R({\mathcal{C}})+\frac{1}{2}\ln(2\pi eN).

(18)

On the other hand, we claim that for sufficiently large
P
,

\displaystyle\left|\bigcup_{{\underline{x}}\in{\mathcal{C}}}{\mathcal{B}}^{n}({\underline{x}},\sqrt{nN})\cap{\mathcal{B}}^{n}(\sqrt{nP})\right|

\displaystyle\gtrsim\frac{1}{L-1}\left|{\mathcal{C}}\cap{\mathcal{B}}^{n}(\sqrt{nP})\right|\cdot\left|{\mathcal{B}}^{n}(\sqrt{nN})\right|.

(19)

The above claim is justified at the end of this subsection.
Then following the same lines of calculations above, we have

\displaystyle\Delta({\mathcal{C}})

\displaystyle\geq R({\mathcal{C}})+\frac{1}{2}\ln(2\pi eN)-o(1).

(20)

Combining
Equations

18
and
20
, we have that for any
(N,L-1)
-packing
{\mathcal{C}}
,

\displaystyle\Delta({\mathcal{C}})

\displaystyle\asymp R({\mathcal{C}})+\frac{1}{2}\ln(2\pi eN).

To see
Equation

19
, consider the set of convex sets

\displaystyle{\mathscr{D}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left\{{\mathcal{D}}({\underline{x}}):{\underline{x}}\in{\mathcal{C}},\;{\mathcal{B}}^{n}({\underline{x}},\sqrt{nN})\cap{\mathcal{B}}^{n}(\sqrt{nP})\neq\emptyset\right\},

where

\displaystyle{\mathcal{D}}({\underline{x}})

\displaystyle\coloneqq{\mathcal{B}}^{n}({\underline{x}},\sqrt{nN})\cap{\mathcal{B}}^{n}(\sqrt{nP}).

Note that some elements in
{\mathscr{D}}
are balls
{\mathcal{B}}^{n}({\underline{x}},\sqrt{nN})
, while other elements are the intersection of two balls
{\mathcal{B}}^{n}({\underline{x}},\sqrt{nN})\cap{\mathcal{B}}^{n}(\sqrt{nP})
for some
{\underline{x}}\in{\mathcal{C}}
.

We now rearrange all
{\mathcal{D}}({\underline{x}})
in
{\mathscr{D}}
so that they become disjoint.
Then the volume induced by the resulting packing is

\displaystyle\sum_{{\mathcal{D}}\in{\mathscr{D}}}\left|{\mathcal{D}}\right|

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\underline{x}}\in{\mathcal{C}}\\
{\mathcal{B}}^{n}({\underline{x}},\sqrt{nN})\cap{\mathcal{B}}^{n}(\sqrt{nP})\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}|{\mathcal{D}}({\underline{x}})|

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{{\underline{x}}\in{\mathcal{C}}\cap{\mathcal{B}}^{n}(\sqrt{nP})}\left|{\mathcal{D}}({\underline{x}})\right|

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\underline{x}}\in{\mathcal{C}}\cap{\mathcal{B}}^{n}(\sqrt{nP})}\left|{\mathcal{B}}^{n}({\underline{x}},\sqrt{nN})\cap{\mathcal{B}}^{n}(\sqrt{nP})\right|.

(21)

On the other hand, for any
{\underline{y}}\in\bigcup\limits_{{\mathcal{D}}\in{\mathscr{D}}}{\mathcal{D}}
,
it can split into at most
L-1
points after the above process since by
(L-1)
-list-decodability, each point in a ball is covered by at most
L-2
other balls.
Therefore, the volume induced by the rearranged packing is at most

\displaystyle(L-1)\left|\bigcup_{{\mathcal{D}}\in{\mathscr{D}}}{\mathcal{D}}\right|

\displaystyle=(L-1)\left|\bigcup_{{\underline{x}}\in{\mathcal{C}}}{\mathcal{B}}^{n}({\underline{x}},\sqrt{nN})\cap{\mathcal{B}}^{n}(\sqrt{nP})\right|.

(22)

Combining
Equations

21
and
22
, we have, for sufficiently large
P
,

\displaystyle\left|\bigcup_{{\underline{x}}\in{\mathcal{C}}}{\mathcal{B}}^{n}({\underline{x}},\sqrt{nN})\cap{\mathcal{B}}^{n}(\sqrt{nP})\right|

\displaystyle\gtrsim\frac{1}{L-1}\sum_{{\underline{x}}\in{\mathcal{C}}\cap{\mathcal{B}}^{n}(\sqrt{nP})}\left|{\mathcal{B}}^{n}({\underline{x}},\sqrt{nN})\cap{\mathcal{B}}^{n}(\sqrt{nP})\right|

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{L-1}\left|{\mathcal{C}}\cap{\mathcal{B}}^{n}(\sqrt{nP})\right|\left|{\mathcal{B}}^{n}(\sqrt{nN})\right|,

as claimed in
Equation

19
.
∎

IX-B

Chebyshev radius and average radius

In this section, we present several different representations of the Chebyshev radius and average squared radius.
Some of them will be crucially used in the subsequent sections of this paper.
These representations are summarized in the following theorem which will be proved in the subsequent subsections.

Theorem 7
.

Let
L\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 2}
and
{\underline{x}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{x}}_{L}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.
Then the squared Chebyshev radius of
{\underline{x}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{x}}_{L}
admits the following alternative representations:

1.

\begin{aligned} \rad^{2}({\underline{x}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{x}}_{L})&=\max_{{\vec{z}}\in\Delta_{L}}\sum_{i=1}^{L}{\vec{z}}(i)\left\|{\underline{x}}_{i}-{\underline{y}}_{\vec{z}}\right\|_{2}^{2},\end{aligned}

where
{\underline{y}}_{\vec{z}}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{L}{\vec{z}}(i){\underline{x}}_{i}
and
\Delta_{L}
denotes the probability simplex on
[L]
;

2.

\begin{aligned} \rad^{2}({\underline{x}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{x}}_{L})&=\lim_{p\to\infty}\rad^{(p)}({\underline{x}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{x}}_{L}),\end{aligned}

where

\displaystyle\rad^{(p)}({\underline{x}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{x}}_{L})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left(\min_{{\underline{y}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\left\|{\underline{x}}_{i}-{\underline{y}}\right\|_{2}^{2p}\right)^{1/p};

3.

there exists a unique
1\leq q\leq\infty
depending on
{\underline{x}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{x}}_{L}
such that

\displaystyle\rad^{2}({\underline{x}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{x}}_{L})

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\left\|{\underline{x}}_{i}-{\underline{x}}\right\|_{2}^{2q}\right)^{1/q},

and
{\underline{x}}\coloneqq\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i=1}^{L}{\underline{x}}_{i}
.

The average squared radius of
{\underline{x}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{x}}_{L}
admits the following alternative representations:

1.

\begin{aligned} \overline{\rad}^{2}({\underline{x}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{x}}_{L})&=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\left\|{\underline{x}}_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}-\left\|{\underline{x}}\right\|_{2}^{2};\end{aligned}

2.

\begin{aligned} \overline{\rad}^{2}({\underline{x}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{x}}_{L})&=\frac{L-1}{L^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\left\|{\underline{x}}_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{L^{2}}\sum_{(i,j)\in[L]^{2}:i\neq j}\left\langle{\underline{x}}_{i},{\underline{x}}_{j}\right\rangle;\end{aligned}

3.

\begin{aligned} \overline{\rad}^{2}({\underline{x}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{x}}_{L})&=\frac{1}{2L^{2}}\sum_{(i,j)\in[L]^{2}:i\neq j}\left\|{\underline{x}}_{i}-{\underline{x}}_{j}\right\|_{2}^{2}.\end{aligned}

IX-B
1
Another representation of the Chebyshev radius

The Chebyshev radius involves a minimax expression which is in general tricky to handle.
One can use minimax theorem to interchange the min and max and then compute the inner min explicitly.

\displaystyle\rad^{2}({\underline{x}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{x}}_{L})

\displaystyle=\min_{{\underline{y}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\max_{i\in[L]}\left\|{\underline{x}}_{i}-{\underline{y}}\right\|_{2}^{2}=\min_{{\underline{y}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\max_{{\vec{z}}\in\Delta_{L}}\sum_{i=1}^{L}{\vec{z}}(i)\left\|{\underline{x}}_{i}-{\underline{y}}\right\|_{2}^{2}.

The last equality follows since the maximum is always achieved by a singleton
{\vec{z}}\in\{0,1\}^{L}
.
Note that the objective function on the RHS is linear (hence concave) in
{\vec{z}}
and quadratic (hence convex) in
{\underline{y}}
.
Therefore the max and min can be interchanged and we get

\displaystyle\max_{{\vec{z}}\in\Delta_{L}}\min_{{\underline{y}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{L}{\vec{z}}(i)\left\|{\underline{x}}_{i}-{\underline{y}}\right\|_{2}^{2}=\max_{{\vec{z}}\in\Delta_{L}}\min_{{\underline{y}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{L}{\vec{z}}(i)\left({\underline{x}}_{i}(j)-{\underline{y}}(j)\right)^{2}=\max_{{\vec{z}}\in\Delta_{L}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\min_{y_{j}\in{\mathbb{R}}}\sum_{i=1}^{L}{\vec{z}}(i)\left({\underline{x}}_{i}(j)-y_{j}\right)^{2}.

The last equality follows since each inner summation
\sum_{i=1}^{L}{\vec{z}}(i)\left({\underline{x}}_{i}(j)-{\underline{y}}(j)\right)^{2}
only depends on
{\underline{y}}(j)
among all
{\underline{y}}(1),\cdots,{\underline{y}}(n)
.
For each
j
, the minimizing
y_{j}^{*}
equals

\displaystyle y_{j}^{*}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{L}{\vec{z}}(i){\underline{x}}_{i}(j)}{\sum_{i=1}^{L}{\vec{z}}(i)}=\sum_{i=1}^{L}{\vec{z}}(i){\underline{x}}_{i}(j),

where the last equality is because
{\vec{z}}\in\Delta_{L}
.
Therefore

\displaystyle\rad^{2}({\underline{x}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{x}}_{L})

\displaystyle=\max_{{\vec{z}}\in\Delta_{L}}\sum_{i=1}^{L}{\vec{z}}(i)\left\|{\underline{x}}_{i}-{\underline{y}}_{\vec{z}}\right\|_{2}^{2},

(23)

where
{\underline{y}}_{\vec{z}}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{L}{\vec{z}}(i){\underline{x}}_{i}
.

IX-B
2
Higher-order approximations to the Chebyshev radius

As explained in
Remark

4
, the average squared radius is a linear relaxation of the squared Chebyshev radius:

\displaystyle\overline{\rad}^{2}({\underline{x}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{x}}_{L})

\displaystyle=\min_{{\underline{y}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{{\mathbf{i}}\sim[L]}\left[\left\|{\underline{x}}_{\mathbf{i}}-{\underline{y}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right].

(24)

One may obtain better and better approximations to the squared Chebyshev radius by taking higher and higher order relaxations:

\displaystyle\rad^{(p)}({\underline{x}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{x}}_{L})

\displaystyle=\min_{{\underline{y}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\left(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{{\mathbf{i}}\sim[L]}\left[\left\|{\underline{x}}_{\mathbf{i}}-{\underline{y}}\right\|_{2}^{2p}\right]\right)^{1/p}=\left(\min_{{\underline{y}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{{\mathbf{i}}\sim[L]}\left[\left\|{\underline{x}}_{\mathbf{i}}-{\underline{y}}\right\|_{2}^{2p}\right]\right)^{1/p},

(25)

where
p\geq 1
.
The second equality in
Equation

25
follows since the
f(\cdot)=(\cdot)^{1/p}
is monotonically increasing.
Note that
\rad^{(1)}({\underline{x}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{x}}_{L})=\overline{\rad}^{2}({\underline{x}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{x}}_{L})
.
Moreover, since
\mathbb{E}\left[(\cdot)^{p}\right]^{1/p}
is increasing in
p
, we have

\displaystyle\rad^{(p)}({\underline{x}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{x}}_{L})\xrightarrow{p\to\infty}\rad^{2}({\underline{x}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{x}}_{L}).

However, we do not know how to analyze
\rad^{(p)}
.
It seems difficulty to get a closed-form solution of the minimization since the minimizer
{\underline{y}}^{*}
cannot be obtained by minimizing over
{\underline{y}}(j)
for different
j\in[n]
separately.

IX-B
3
More representations of the average squared radius

Recall that
Equation

24
, as a lower bound on
\rad^{2}({\underline{x}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{x}}_{L})
, admits an explicit formula given by

\displaystyle\rad^{2}({\underline{x}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{x}}_{L})\geq\overline{\rad}^{2}({\underline{x}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{x}}_{L})=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\left\|{\underline{x}}_{i}-{\underline{x}}\right\|_{2}^{2},

(26)

where
{\underline{x}}\coloneqq\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i=1}^{L}{\underline{x}}_{i}
denotes the centroid of
{\underline{x}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{x}}_{L}

On the other hand, we have

\displaystyle\rad^{2}({\underline{x}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{x}}_{L})=\min_{{\underline{y}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\max_{i\in[L]}\left\|{\underline{x}}_{i}-{\underline{y}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq\max_{i\in[L]}\left\|{\underline{x}}_{i}-{\underline{x}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\eqqcolon\rad^{2}_{\max}({\underline{x}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{x}}_{L}).

(27)

Contrasting

and
27

\left\|\cdot\right\|_{p}

\displaystyle\rad^{2}({\underline{x}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{x}}_{L})=\left(\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\left\|{\underline{x}}_{i}-{\underline{x}}\right\|_{2}^{2p}\right)^{1/p}.

However, we do not know how to use the above observation for the following two reasons.
Firstly, the above expression seems tricky to handle.
Secondly and more importantly, the number

depends on
{\underline{x}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{x}}_{L}

Finally, we provide several alternative expressions for

\displaystyle\overline{\rad}^{2}({\underline{x}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{x}}_{L})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\left\|{\underline{x}}_{i}-{\underline{x}}\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\left\langle{\underline{x}}_{i}-{\underline{x}},{\underline{x}}_{i}-{\underline{x}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\left(\left\|{\underline{x}}_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}-2\left\langle{\underline{x}}_{i},{\underline{x}}\right\rangle+\left\|{\underline{x}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\overline{\rad}^{2}({\underline{x}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{x}}_{L})

\displaystyle=\frac{L-1}{L^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\left\|{\underline{x}}_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{L^{2}}\sum_{(i,j)\in[L]^{2}:i\neq j}\left\langle{\underline{x}}_{i},{\underline{x}}_{j}\right\rangle.

(29)

At last,

can in turn be rewritten as

\displaystyle\overline{\rad}^{2}({\underline{x}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{x}}_{L})

\displaystyle=\frac{L-1}{L^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\left\|{\underline{x}}_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{L^{2}}\sum_{(i,j)\in[L]^{2}:i\neq j}\left\langle{\underline{x}}_{i},{\underline{x}}_{j}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{L-1}{L^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\left\|{\underline{x}}_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{2L^{2}}\sum_{(i,j)\in[L]^{2}:i\neq j}\left(\left\|{\underline{x}}_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\left\|{\underline{x}}_{j}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)+\frac{1}{2L^{2}}\sum_{(i,j)\in[L]^{2}:i\neq j}\left(\left\|{\underline{x}}_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\left\|{\underline{x}}_{j}\right\|_{2}^{2}-2\left\langle{\underline{x}}_{i},{\underline{x}}_{j}\right\rangle\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{L-1}{L^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\left\|{\underline{x}}_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{L^{2}}\sum_{(i,j)\in[L]^{2}:i\neq j}\left\|{\underline{x}}_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{2L^{2}}\sum_{(i,j)\in[L]^{2}:i\neq j}\left\|{\underline{x}}_{i}-{\underline{x}}_{j}\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2L^{2}}\sum_{(i,j)\in[L]^{2}:i\neq j}\left\|{\underline{x}}_{i}-{\underline{x}}_{j}\right\|_{2}^{2}.

(30)

If all

have the same

norm

, then

\displaystyle\overline{\rad}^{2}({\underline{x}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{x}}_{L})=nP-\left\|{\underline{x}}\right\|_{2}^{2}.

(31)

and

becomes

(32)

Lower bounds
for unbounded packings

In this section, we analyze average-radius list-decodability of a class of regular infinite constellations obtained by expurgating and tiling a random code supported over an
n
-dimensional hypercube. Using this, we prove the following lower bound on the
(N,L-1)
-average-radius list-decoding capacity of multiple packings.

Theorem 8
.

For any

and

, the
(N,L-1)
-average-radius list-decoding capacity is at least

\displaystyle\overline{C}_{L-1}(N)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{L-1}{2\pi eNL}-\frac{1}{2(L-1)}\ln L.

(33)

Remark 5
.

Note that the above bound (
Equation

33
) approaches
\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{1}{2\pi eN}
as
L\to\infty
.
The latter quantity is known to be the list-decoding capacity for asymptotically large
L
(see
Section

IV
).
On the extreme, when
L=2
, the above bound becomes
\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{1}{8\pi eN}
which recovers the best known bound due to Minkowski
[
Min10
]
.

To prove the above theorem, let
R<\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{L-1}{2\pi eNL}-\frac{1}{2(L-1)}\ln L
and
\lambda_{n}\doteq e^{nR}
.
The exact choice of
\lambda_{n}
is given by
Equation

54
.

To analyze average-radius list-decodability of
{\mathcal{C}}
, we first construct an average-radius list-decodable code

supported within
{\mathcal{A}}={\mathcal{I}}^{n}
where

is a sufficiently large interval for some

. We later tile this codebook over

to obtain an infinite constellation having the same average squared radius as the finite codebook.

The finite codebook

is obtained by drawing

points independently and uniformly at random from
{\mathcal{A}}
and expurgating the resulting codebook.
Let

denote the

independent points uniformly distributed over
{\mathcal{A}}
.

Lemma 9

There exists a finite codebook

supported over
{\mathcal{A}}

and density

The first step is to bound

(34)

for every subset of
L
codewords in
{\mathcal{C}}_{K}^{\prime}
.
In fact, we will prove the following lemma.

.

For any
{\underline{\mathbf{x}}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{\mathbf{x}}}_{L}

, we have

First, we note that

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\overline{\rad}^{2}({\underline{\mathbf{x}}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{\mathbf{x}}}_{L})\leq nN\right]

\displaystyle=\Pr\left[\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\left\|{\underline{\mathbf{x}}}_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}-\left\|{\underline{\mathbf{x}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq nN\right]

\displaystyle=\Pr\left[\sum_{i=1}^{L}\left\|{\underline{\mathbf{x}}}_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}-L\left\|{\underline{\mathbf{x}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq LnN\right]

\displaystyle=\Pr\left[\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{L}{\underline{\mathbf{x}}}_{i}(j)^{2}-L\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i=1}^{L}{\underline{\mathbf{x}}}_{i}(j)\right)^{2}\leq LnN\right]

(36)

where

{\underline{\mathbf{x}}}\coloneqq\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i=1}^{L}{\underline{\mathbf{x}}}_{i}

Define

The above probability (

Large deviation principle

Since
\vec{\mathbf{x}}_{j}^{\top}A\vec{\mathbf{x}}_{j}
is independent for each

Theorem

5

Specifically,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\ln\lx@cref{creftypecap\lx@tilde refnum}{eqn:ppp-to-bound-clean}

f(t_{1},\cdots,t_{L-1})\coloneqq\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{L-1}t_{i}^{2}
is nonnegative and attains its unique minimum (which is zero) at
[t_{1},\cdots,t_{L-1}]=[0,\cdots,0]
which is in the interior of
U^{\top}[-1,1]^{L}
.
Therefore, by Laplace’s method (
Theorem

4
),
Equation

46
converges to

\displaystyle\left(\frac{2\pi}{K^{2}}\right)^{\frac{L-1}{2}}\frac{g_{2}(0,\cdots,0)-g_{1}(0,\cdots,0)}{\sqrt{\det((\hess f)(0,\cdots,0))}}

as
K\to\infty
.
Since
\hess f=2\lambda I_{L-1}\succ 0
, we have

\displaystyle\left(\frac{2\pi}{K^{2}}\right)^{\frac{L-1}{2}}\frac{g_{2}(0,\cdots,0)-g_{1}(0,\cdots,0)}{\sqrt{(2\lambda)^{L-1}}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\pi}{K^{2}\lambda}\right)^{\frac{L-1}{2}}(g_{2}(0,\cdots,0)-g_{1}(0,\cdots,0)).

Note that
g_{2}(0,\cdots,0)-g_{1}(0,\cdots,0)
is nothing but the length of the range of the last coordinate
t_{L-1}
of vectors in
U^{\top}[-1,1]^{L}
.
Since any vector
\vec{t}\in U^{\top}[-1,1]^{L}
can be written as
U^{\top}\vec{u}
for some
\vec{u}\in[-1,1]^{L}
, the length of the range of the last coordinate of
\vec{t}
is twice the
\ell_{1}
-norm of the last row of
U^{\top}
, i.e., the last column of
U
.
From
Equation

42
, it is not hard to see that

\displaystyle g_{2}(0,\cdots,0)-g_{1}(0,\cdots,0)

\displaystyle=2\cdot L\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{L}}=2\sqrt{L}.

(47)

Finally, we get that
Equation

46
(asymptotically) equals

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\pi}{K^{2}\lambda}\right)^{\frac{L-1}{2}}\cdot 2\sqrt{L}.

(48)

Recall that
E(K)
is the error
exponent corresponding to
Equation

34
.
Plugging
Equation

48
back to
Equation

45
and then back to
Equation

40
, we have

\displaystyle E(K)

\displaystyle=\max_{\lambda\geq 0}\left\{-\lambda LN-\ln\left(\frac{1}{2^{L}}\cdot\left(\frac{\pi}{K^{2}\lambda}\right)^{\frac{L-1}{2}}\cdot 2\sqrt{L}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=\max_{\lambda\geq 0}\left\{-\lambda LN-\ln\left(\left(\frac{\pi}{(2K)^{2}\lambda}\right)^{\frac{L-1}{2}}\sqrt{L}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=\max_{\lambda\geq 0}\left\{-LN\lambda+\frac{L-1}{2}\ln\lambda+(L-1)\ln(2K)\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\left.-\frac{L-1}{2}\ln\pi-\frac{1}{2}\ln L\right\}

\displaystyle=\max_{\lambda\geq 0}\left\{-LN\lambda+\frac{L-1}{2}\ln\lambda\right\}+(L-1)\ln(2K)

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{L-1}{2}\ln\pi-\frac{1}{2}\ln L

(49)

\displaystyle=-\frac{L-1}{2}+\frac{L-1}{2}\ln\frac{L-1}{2LN}-\frac{L-1}{2}\ln\pi

\displaystyle\qquad+(L-1)\ln(2K)-\frac{1}{2}\ln L

(50)

\displaystyle=\frac{L-1}{2}\ln\frac{L-1}{2\pi eNL}-\frac{1}{2}\ln L+(L-1)\ln(2K).

(51)

Equation

50
follows since the function of
\lambda
in the maximization in
Equation

49
is convex and attains its maximum at
\lambda=\frac{L-1}{2LN}
. This completes the proof.
∎

X-C

Finite codebook with minimum average squared radius
nN
and proof of
Lemma

9

Since the number
M
of points in
{\mathcal{A}}
is
\lambda_{n}|{\mathcal{A}}|=\lambda_{n}(2K)^{n}
, the expected number of lists with average squared radius at most
nN
is

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\left\{{\mathcal{L}}\in\binom{{\mathcal{C}}_{K}^{\prime}}{L}:\overline{\rad}^{2}({\mathcal{L}})\leq nN\right\}\right|\right]

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{{\underline{\mathbf{x}}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{\mathbf{x}}}_{M}}\left[\left|\left\{{\mathcal{L}}\in\binom{[M]}{L}:\overline{\rad}^{2}(\left\{{\underline{\mathbf{x}}}_{i}\right\}_{i\in{\mathcal{L}}})\leq nN\right\}\right|\right]

(52)

\displaystyle=\binom{M}{L}e^{-nE(K)}

\displaystyle=\frac{M^{L}e^{o(n)}}{L!}e^{-nE(K)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda_{n}^{L}(2K)^{nL}e^{-nE(K)+o(n)}}{L!}.

(53)

We now set
\lambda_{n}
in such a way that
Equation

53
is at most
\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[M\right]=\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{n}|{\mathcal{A}}|=\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{n}(2K)^{n}
.
That is,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{n}(2K)^{n}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\lambda_{n}^{L}(2K)^{nL}e^{-nE(K)+o(n)}}{L!}

\displaystyle\impliedby

\displaystyle L!/2

\displaystyle\geq\lambda_{n}^{L-1}(2K)^{(L-1)n}e^{-nE(K)+o(n)}

\displaystyle\impliedby

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}

\displaystyle\leq(L!/2)^{\frac{1}{L-1}}\frac{e^{nE(K)/(L-1)+o(n)}}{(2K)^{n}}

\displaystyle=(L!/2)^{\frac{1}{L-1}}e^{n\left(\frac{E(K)}{L-1}-\ln(2K)\right)+o(n)}

\displaystyle=(L!/2)^{\frac{1}{L-1}}\exp\left(n\left(\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{L-1}{2\pi eNL}-\frac{1}{2(L-1)}\ln L\right)+o(n)\right).

(54)

After expurgating out one codeword from each bad list, we get an
(N,L-1)
-average-radius multiple packing
{\mathcal{C}}_{K}
of size at least
\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[M\right]
and the density
\frac{1}{n}\ln\frac{|{\mathcal{C}}_{K}|}{|{\mathcal{A}}|}
is therefore at least

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\ln\frac{\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[M\right]}{|{\mathcal{A}}|}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\ln\frac{\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{n}|{\mathcal{A}}|}{|{\mathcal{A}}|}=\frac{1}{n}\ln\frac{\lambda_{n}}{2}.

(55)

Substituting
Equation

54
here, we get the following lower bound on the density

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{L-1}{2\pi eNL}-\frac{1}{2(L-1)}\ln L+o(1),

(56)

as promised in
Lemma

9
.

Unbounded packing and proof of
Theorem

8

The above derivation shows the existence of a
finite
codebook
{\mathcal{C}}\cap[-K,K]^{n}
in which all
L
-tuple of points have radius at least
\sqrt{nN}
.
To obtain an
unbounded

(N,L-1)
-packing, let us take
K=n^{2}
and define
{\mathcal{C}}_{n}\coloneqq{\mathcal{C}}\cap[-n^{2},n^{2}]^{n}
and

\displaystyle{\mathcal{C}}_{\infty}

\displaystyle\coloneqq{\mathcal{C}}_{n}+(n^{2}+n^{0.6}){\mathbb{Z}}^{n}.

In words,
{\mathcal{C}}_{\infty}
is obtained by tiling
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
using translations of
{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
and leaving a gap of width
2\cdot n^{0.6}
between adjacent copies of
{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
.
The NLD of
{\mathcal{C}}_{\infty}
is essentially the same as that of
{\mathcal{C}}\cap[-n^{2},n^{2}]^{n}
which is given by
Equation

56
.
Indeed, since
{\mathcal{C}}_{\infty}
is periodic, we have

\displaystyle R({\mathcal{C}}_{\infty})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\ln\frac{|{\mathcal{C}}_{n}|}{\left|[-(n^{2}+n^{0.6}),(n^{2}+n^{0.6})]^{n}\right|}=\frac{1}{n}\ln\frac{|{\mathcal{C}}_{n}|}{\left|[-n^{2},n^{2}]^{n}\right|}+\frac{1}{n}\ln\frac{(2n^{2})^{n}}{(2(n^{2}+n^{0.6}))^{n}}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}R({\mathcal{C}}_{n}).

Moreover, we claim that
{\mathcal{C}}_{\infty}
is an
(N,L-1)
-packing.
To see this, take any
{\mathcal{L}}\subset\binom{{\mathcal{C}}_{\infty}}{L}
.
If
{\mathcal{L}}\subset{\mathcal{C}}_{n}+{\underline{z}}
for some
{\underline{z}}\in(n^{2}+n^{0.6}){\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
, then
\rad({\mathcal{L}})\geq\sqrt{nN}
by the guarantee of
{\mathcal{C}}_{n}
.
Otherwise, there exist two points
{\underline{x}}_{1},{\underline{x}}_{2}\in{\mathcal{L}}
such that
{\underline{x}}_{1}\in{\mathcal{C}}_{n}+{\underline{z}}_{1}
and
{\underline{x}}_{2}\in{\mathcal{C}}_{n}+{\underline{z}}_{2}
for two distinct
{\underline{z}}_{1}\neq{\underline{z}}_{2}\in(n^{2}+n^{0.6}){\mathbb{Z}}_{n}
.
Then

\displaystyle\rad({\mathcal{L}})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\underline{x}}_{1}-{\underline{x}}_{2}\right\|_{2}\geq n^{0.6}\geq\sqrt{nN}.

Therefore, we obtain an
(N,L-1)
-packing
{\mathcal{C}}_{\infty}
of NLD asymptotically equal to
Equation

56
.
The proof of
Theorem

8
is complete.

X-E

Alternate approaches for bounding
Equation

39

We managed to compute the exact asymptotics (up to lower order terms in the exponent) of the tail probability given by
Equation

39
.
The way we did so is by applying the large deviation principle and performing ad hoc calculations on the moment generating function of the random quadratic form of interest.
From the perspective of concentration of measure, the tail probability we computed can be cast from several different angles:

1.

Gaussian integral w.r.t. a general (non-identity) degenerate covariance matrix
A
;

2.

Concentration of the uniform measure on a solid cube (a useful trick for which is to push it forward to the Gaussian measure and apply Lipschitz concentration
[
Bob10
]
);

3.

The (standard) Gaussian measure of a parallelepiped defined by the linear transformation
U
;

4.

The probability that a Gaussian (with zero mean and general covariance matrix) lies in a cube;

5.

Hanson–Wright inequality for quadratic forms in subgaussian random vectors
[
Ver18
]
which, in our case, are uniform vectors in a solid cube.

We tried all the above techniques.
However, they do not seem to yield the correct exponent, at least in their vanilla forms, though they may give certain exponentially decaying bounds.
Therefore, we feel that
Equation

39
is a cute example for which standard concentration tools are not able to produce the optimal bound.

X-F

Connections to
[
Bli05b
]

The paper
[
Bli05b
]
analyzed the list-decodability of expurgated PPPs and arrived at the same bound (
Equation

33
) as ours, and in fact the current paper was inspired by
[
Bli05b
]
.

However, there were some gaps in the proof of
[
Bli05b
]
that we were not able to resolve.
In the paper, it was shown that for every sufficiently large
K>0
, there exists an (infinite) codebook
{\mathcal{C}}
obtained by expurgating a PPP such that every
L
-tuple of points in
{\mathcal{C}}\cap[-K,K]^{n}
has radius at least
\sqrt{nN}
.
However, we could not find a rigorous way to pass to the limit as
K\to\infty
and argue that
{\mathcal{C}}
itself as an
infinite
point set is an
(N,L-1)
-multiple packing.
Indeed, it is claimed in
[
ST01
, Note 5]
that
{\mathcal{C}}\cap[-K,K]^{n}
may not converge as
K\to\infty
.

Although our proof also involves analyzing the tail probability of the average squared radius (
Equation

34
), our techniques are different, as outlined below.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be a PPP (without expurgation yet) and
K\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
Let
{\underline{\mathbf{x}}}_{1},\cdots,{\underline{\mathbf{x}}}_{L}\in{\mathcal{C}}\cap[-K,K]^{n}
be an
L
-list.
Recall that they are independent and uniformly distributed in
[-K,K]^{n}
.
Let
{\underline{\mathbf{x}}}=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i=1}^{L}{\underline{\mathbf{x}}}_{i}
denote the centroid of the list.
To compute
Equation

34
,
[
Bli05b
]
claimed that we could use an orthogonal transformation
{\underline{\mathbf{x}}}_{i}\mapsto{\underline{\mathbf{u}}}_{i}
(
1\leq i\leq L
) to the list so that
\frac{1}{L}\left\|{\underline{\mathbf{u}}}_{1}\right\|_{2}=\left\|{\underline{\mathbf{u}}}\right\|_{2}
where
{\underline{\mathbf{u}}}\coloneqq\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\left\|{\underline{\mathbf{u}}}_{i}\right\|_{2}
. This is in contrast to our approach.
However, it should be noted that an orthogonal transformation only reflects and/or rotates the list, but does not translate it.

From our understanding of the paper
[
Bli05b
]
, the ideas can be interpreted as follows.
Since the average squared radius is invariant under rigid transformations (i.e., translations, rotations, reflections and their combination) and a homogeneous PPP is stationary and isotropic,
[
Bli05b
]
attempts to transform the list rigidly so that the resulting average squared radius admits a simpler expression and the list is still independent and uniformly distributed in the cube. However, it appears that such a rigid transformation does not exist. We instead use a different, much simpler approach by first constructing a finite codebook and then tiling this. The high-level construction is similar to
[
Pol94
]
which was originally studied for the problem of reliable communication over additive-white Gaussian noise channels.

XI
Open questions

We end the paper with several intriguing open questions.

1.

The problem of packing spheres in
\ell_{p}
space was also addressed in the literature
[
Ran55
,
Spe70
,
Bal87
,
Sam13
]
.
Recently, there was an exponential improvement on the optimal packing density in
\ell_{p}
space
[
SSSZ20
]
relying on the Kabatiansky–Levenshtein bound
[
KL78
]
.
It is worth exploring the
\ell_{p}
version of the multiple packing problem.
One obstacle here is that the
\ell_{p}
average radius does not admit a closed form expression unlike the
p=2
case.

2.

In this paper, we treat (regular) list-decoding and average-radius list-decoding as two different notions and obtain bounds for the latter (which automatically lower bounds the former).
It follows from our bounds that
the largest multiple packing density under these two notions coincide as
L\to\infty
.
However, as far as we know, it is unknown whether the largest multiple packing density under standard and average-radius list-decoding is the same for any
finite

L
.
