Existence of Bass martingales and the martingale Benamou–Brenier problem in ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d}
Julio Backhoff-Veraguas, Mathias Beiglböck, Walter Schachermayer, Bertram Tschiderer

Abstract
In classical optimal transport, the contributions of Benamou–Brenier and McCann regarding the time-dependent version of the problem are cornerstones of the field and form the basis for a variety of applications in other mathematical areas. In this article, we characterize solutions to the martingale Benamou–Brenier problem as Bass martingales , i.e. transformations of Brownian motion through the gradient of a convex function. Our result is based on a new (static) Brenier-type theorem for a particular weak martingale optimal transport problem. As in the classical case, the structure of the primal optimizer is derived from its dual counterpart, whose derivation forms the technical core of this article. A key challenge is that dual attainment is a subtle issue in martingale optimal transport, where dual optimizers may fail to exist, even in highly regular settings.

1.1
Benamou–Brenier transport problem and McCann interpolation in probabilistic terms

Given
\mu,\nu
in the space
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
of
d
-dimensional distributions with finite second moment, Brenier’s theorem asserts that if
\mu
is absolutely continuous, the following are equivalent for a coupling
\pi\in\mathsf{Cpl}(\mu,\nu)
, i.e., a probability on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
with marginals
\mu
,
\nu
:

(1)

\pi
minimizes the transport costs with respect to the squared distance between
\mu
and
\nu
.

(2)

\pi
is concentrated on the graph of a function
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
which is
monotone
in the sense that
f=\nabla v
for a convex function
v\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow(-\infty,+\infty]
.

Brenier’s theorem gives a structural description of the optimal transport plan for the most widely used cost function. Another essential consequence of the result is that it provides a particularly natural way to move probabilities: it implies that
\mu
can be transported to
\nu
via the gradient of a convex function. In fact, in many applications it is the mere existence of this monotone transport map that is required, irrespective of its optimality properties.

Ideally, a martingale counterpart of Brenier’s theorem should mimic these aspects. That is, starting from a natural optimization problem, our goal is to define a natural martingale that connects the probabilities
\mu
and
\nu
.

While
[
12
,
39
,
31
]
have proposed martingale versions of Brenier’s monotone transport map based on static transport problems, our starting point is a continuous formulation in the spirit of the Benamou–Brenier continuous-time transport problem given in
[
5
,
41
]
.

We first recapitulate the continuous-time formulation of Brenier’s theorem in probabilistic language. For
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, consider

T_{2}(\mu,\nu)\coloneqq\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}X_{0}\sim\mu,\,X_{1}\sim\nu,\\
X_{t}=X_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}b_{s}\,ds\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{0}^{1}|b_{t}|^{2}\,dt\Big].

(BB)

Under the above assumptions, (
BB
) has a unique optimizer (in law), and the following are equivalent for a process
X=(X_{t})_{0\leqslant t\leqslant 1}
with
X_{0}\sim\mu
,
X_{1}\sim\nu
:

(1)

X
solves (
BB
).

(2)

X_{1}=f(X_{0})
, where
f
is the gradient of a convex function
v\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow(-\infty,+\infty]
and all particles move with constant speed, i.e.
X_{t}=X_{0}+bt
,
t\in[0,1]
, for the random variable
b=X_{1}-X_{0}
.

In this case, setting
\mu_{t}\coloneqq\operatorname{Law}(X_{t})
,
t\in[0,1]
, defines McCann’s displacement interpolation between
\mu
and
\nu
. This interpolation is time-consistent in the sense that interpolation between
\mu_{s}
and
\mu_{t}
, for
0\leqslant s<t\leqslant 1
, recovers the family
(\mu_{r})_{s\leqslant r\leqslant t}
up to the obvious affine-time transformation.

1.2
Martingale optimization problem

Assume that
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
are in convex order, in signs
\mu\preceq_{\textnormal{c}}\nu
, that is,
\int\phi\,d\mu\leqslant\int\phi\,d\nu
for all convex functions
\phi\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with linear growth. We consider the optimization problem

MT(\mu,\nu)\coloneqq\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}M_{0}\sim\mu,\,M_{1}\sim\nu,\\
M_{t}=M_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{s}\,dB_{s}\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{0}^{1}|\sigma_{t}-I_{d}|^{2}_{\textnormal{HS}}\,dt\Big],

(MBB)

where
|\cdot|_{\textnormal{HS}}
denotes the Hilbert–Schmidt norm. Here the infimum is taken over all filtered probability spaces
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
, with
\sigma
an
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
-valued
\mathcal{F}
-progressive process and
B
a
d
-dimensional
\mathcal{F}
-Brownian motion, such that
M
is a martingale. The stochastic integral
\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{s}\,dB_{s}
is in the Itô sense.

The main result of
[
5
]
is that (
MBB
) admits a unique optimizer
M^{\ast}
. Moreover,
M^{\ast}
is a continuous strong Markov martingale. While (
BB
) implies that particles move along straight lines, the functional in (
MBB
) stipulates that
M^{\ast}
maximizes the correlation with Brownian motion subject to the given marginal constraints. It is also shown in
[
5
]
that
M^{\ast}
is the process whose evolution follows the movement of a Brownian particle as closely as possible with respect to an
adapted Wasserstein distance
(see, e.g.,
[
4
,
29
]
) subject to the marginal conditions
M^{\ast}_{0}\sim\mu
and
M^{\ast}_{1}\sim\nu
. As in the classical case, setting
\mu_{t}\coloneqq(\operatorname{Law}(M^{\ast}_{t}))_{0\leqslant t\leqslant 1}
defines a time-consistent interpolation between
\mu
and
\nu
.
These properties motivate to interpret (
MBB
) as a Martingale Benamou–Brenier problem and to call the martingale
M^{\ast}

stretched Brownian motion
between
\mu
and
\nu
as in
[
5
]
.

1.3
The Bass martingale

Our main result relates the optimality property in the definition of stretched Brownian motion to a structural description. As motivation we recall a classical construction of Bass
[
7
]
which provides a particularly appealing martingale
M=(M_{t})_{0\leqslant t\leqslant 1}
that terminates in a fixed measure
\nu
on the real line and starts at the barycenter of
\nu
. This amounts to a solution of the Skorokhod embedding problem (modulo time change). Let
(B_{t})_{0\leqslant t\leqslant 1}
be Brownian motion (started in
B_{0}=0
), let
\gamma\coloneqq\operatorname{Law}(B_{1})
denote the standard Gaussian and define
f\colon\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
as the
\gamma
-a.e. unique increasing mapping that pushes
\gamma
to
\nu
. Bass then defines the martingale

M_{t}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[f(B_{1})\,|\,\sigma(B_{s}\colon s\leqslant t)]=\mathbb{E}[f(B_{1})\,|\,B_{t}],\qquad 0\leqslant t\leqslant 1,

(1.1)

so that
M_{0}
starts at the barycenter of
\nu
and
\operatorname{Law}(M_{1})=\nu
.

As we are interested in martingales in multiple dimensions with possibly non-degenerate starting law
\mu
, we consider the construction of Bass in the following generality:

Definition 1.1
.

Let
B=(B_{t})_{0\leqslant t\leqslant 1}
be Brownian motion on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
B_{0}\sim\alpha\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and let
v\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be convex such that
\nabla v(B_{1})
is square-integrable. Then we call

M_{t}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[\nabla v(B_{1})\,|\,\sigma(B_{s}\colon s\leqslant t)]=\mathbb{E}[\nabla v(B_{1})\,|\,B_{t}],\qquad 0\leqslant t\leqslant 1

a
Bass martingale
from
\mu\coloneqq\operatorname{Law}(M_{0})
to
\nu\coloneqq\operatorname{Law}(M_{1})
.

The question arises under which conditions on
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
there is a Bass martingale
M
from
\mu
to
\nu
, i.e. satisfying
M_{0}\sim\mu
and
M_{1}\sim\nu
, for
\mu
and
\nu
prescribed in advance?

1.4
Structure of stretched Brownian motion

We need a connectivity assumption on the marginals, known under the name of
irreducibility
in the martingale transport literature.

Definition 1.2
.

For
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
we say that the pair
(\mu,\nu)
is
irreducible
if for all measurable sets
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\mu(A),\nu(B)>0
there is a martingale
X=(X_{t})_{0\leqslant t\leqslant 1}
with
X_{0}\sim\mu
,
X_{1}\sim\nu
such that
\mathbb{P}(X_{0}\in A,X_{1}\in B)>0
.

With this definition in hand, we can announce our first main result.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\mu\preceq_{\textnormal{c}}\nu
be probabilities on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with finite second moments and suppose that
(\mu,\nu)
is irreducible. Then the following are equivalent for a martingale
M=(M_{t})_{0\leqslant t\leqslant 1}
with
M_{0}\sim\mu
and
M_{1}\sim\nu
:

(1)

M
is stretched Brownian motion, i.e. the optimizer of (
MBB
).

(2)

M
is a Bass martingale.

We briefly provide some context for the irreducibility assumption in Theorem
1.3
. In classical optimal transport, the product coupling of the marginals guarantees that mass can be transported from an arbitrary starting position to an arbitrary target position. However, this is not necessarily true in the case of martingale transport where it may happen that
\mathbb{R}^{d}
decomposes into disjoint (convex) regions that do not communicate with each other. The irreducibility assumption excludes this, as it guarantees that for any sets
A
and
B
that are charged by
\mu
and
\nu
, respectively, there exists a martingale
X
connecting them (in the sense that
\mathbb{P}(X_{0}\in A,X_{1}\in B)>0
). For
d>1
, the appearance of more than one irreducible component leads to intricate phenomena, analyzed in the remarkable contributions
[
31
,
27
,
52
]
. This problem is revisited in the follow-up paper
[
56
]
in terms of Bass martingales. For equivalent characterizations of the irreducibility assumption we refer to Theorem
D.1
in Appendix
D
. In particular, it is equivalent to consider continuous- or discrete-time martingales in Definition
1.2
.

We emphasize that irreducibility is not only a sufficient assumption for Theorem
1.3
, but it is in fact necessary. Indeed, the Bass martingale connects any two sets which are charged by
\mu
and
\nu
, see Remark
D.3
in Appendix
D
. In particular, if there is a Bass martingale from
\mu
to
\nu
, then
(\mu,\nu)
is irreducible.

An important consequence of Theorem
1.3
is that for any irreducible pair
\mu\preceq_{\textnormal{c}}\nu
there exists a unique Bass martingale

M_{t}=\mathbb{E}[\nabla v(B_{1})\,|\,\sigma(B_{s}\colon s\leqslant t)]=\mathbb{E}[\nabla v(B_{1})\,|\,B_{t}],\qquad 0\leqslant t\leqslant 1,

(1.2)

with
M_{0}\sim\mu
,
M_{1}\sim\nu
and it is worthwhile to comment on the properties of
M
. We write
\gamma^{t}
for the
d
-dimensional centered Gaussian distribution with covariance matrix
tI_{d}
and
v_{t}\coloneqq v\ast\gamma^{1-t}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
for the convolution of the function
v
and the measure
\gamma^{1-t}
. In these terms, (
1.2
) amounts to

M_{t}=\nabla v_{t}(B_{t}),\qquad 0\leqslant t\leqslant 1,

(1.3)

which emphasizes that the Bass martingale is obtained as a monotone transformation of Brownian motion at each time point. Finally, as a Brownian martingale,
M
is a diffusion which connects
\mu
and
\nu
. Indeed, applying Itô’s formula to (
1.3
) we obtain

dM_{t}=\nabla^{2}v_{t}\circ\nabla v_{t}^{\ast}(M_{t})\,dB_{t},\qquad 0\leqslant t\leqslant 1,

where
v_{t}^{\ast}
denotes the convex conjugate of
v_{t}
.

McCann
[
48
]
managed to extend the validity of Brenier’s theorem: using Aleksandrov’s lemma he showed that the Brenier map is well defined without any moment assumptions. We leave the question of whether a similar extension is possible in the case of Bass martingales for future research.

Finally, we note that the implication “
(2)

\Rightarrow

(1)
” is comparably easy and follows from
[
5
, Theorem 1.10]
. We will establish the reverse implication that every optimizer of (
MBB
) is a Bass martingale, based on a duality result, which we describe in the next section.

1.5
Weak transport formulation and dual viewpoint

Our arguments rely on a novel dual viewpoint on stretched Brownian motion, which is in turn based on a reformulation of problem (
MBB
) as a weak transport problem.

In classical transport, minimization of the squared distance is equivalent to maximization of correlation and the latter formulation yields a dual problem which is simpler to interpret. A similar fact holds true in the present martingale setting: problem (
MBB
) is equivalent to maximizing the covariance with Brownian motion

P(\mu,\nu)\coloneqq\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}M_{0}\sim\mu,\,M_{1}\sim\nu,\\
M_{t}=M_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{s}\,dB_{s}\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{0}^{1}\textnormal{tr}(\sigma_{t})\,dt\Big],

(1.4)

in the sense that both problems have the same optimizer and the values are related via
MT(\mu,\nu)=d+\int|y|^{2}\,d\nu(y)-\int|x|^{2}\,d\mu(x)-2P(\mu,\nu)
.

We go on to reformulate (
1.4
) as a weak (martingale) transport problem in the sense of
[
35
]
. Indeed

P(\mu,\nu)=\sup_{\pi\in\mathsf{MT}(\mu,\nu)}\int\textnormal{MCov}(\pi_{x},\gamma)\,\mu(dx),

(1.5)

where the
maximal covariance
(
MCov
) between
p_{1},p_{2}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is defined as

\textnormal{MCov}(p_{1},p_{2})\coloneqq\sup_{q\in\mathsf{Cpl}(p_{1},p_{2})}\int\langle x_{1},x_{2}\rangle\,q(dx_{1},dx_{2}).

The optimization problem (
1.5
) is a
weak
martingale optimal transport problem since the function
\pi\mapsto\int\textnormal{MCov}(\pi_{x},\gamma)\,\mu(dx)
is non-linear, as opposed to classical optimal transport, where linear problems of the form
\pi\mapsto\int c(x,y)\,\pi(dx,dy)
are studied.

The equality (
1.5
) was established in
[
5
, Theorem 2.2]
. Furthermore, there exists a unique optimizer
\pi^{\textnormal{SBM}}\in\mathsf{MT}(\mu,\nu)
of (
1.5
) and if
(M_{t})_{0\leqslant t\leqslant 1}
is the stretched Brownian motion from
\mu
to
\nu
, then the law of
(M_{0},M_{1})
equals
\pi^{\textnormal{SBM}}
. Indeed, the discrete-time formulation (
1.5
) will play a central role in this paper.
In particular, it will be the basis for the following duality result, which implies the difficult implication “
(1)

\Rightarrow

(2)
” in Theorem
1.3
.

Theorem 1.4
.

Assume that
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
are in convex order. The value
P(\mu,\nu)
of the continuous-time optimization problem (
1.4
) is equal to

D(\mu,\nu)\coloneqq\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\psi\in L^{1}(\nu),\\
\textnormal{$\psi$ convex}\end{subarray}}\Big(\int\psi\,d\nu-\int(\psi^{\ast}\ast\gamma)^{\ast}\,d\mu\Big).

(1.6)

The infimum is attained by a lower semicontinuous convex function
\psi_{\textnormal{opt}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow(-\infty,+\infty]
satisfying
\mu(\operatorname{ri}(\operatorname{dom}\psi_{\textnormal{opt}}))=1
if and only if
(\mu,\nu)
is irreducible. In this case the (unique) optimizer to (
MBB
) is given by the Bass martingale

M_{t}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[\nabla v(B_{1})\,|\,\sigma(B_{s}\colon s\leqslant t)]=\mathbb{E}[\nabla v(B_{1})\,|\,B_{t}],\qquad 0\leqslant t\leqslant 1,

where
v=\psi_{\textnormal{opt}}^{\ast}
and
B_{0}\sim\nabla(\psi_{\textnormal{opt}}^{\ast}\ast\gamma)^{\ast}(\mu)
.

Here,
\operatorname{ri}(\operatorname{dom}\psi_{\textnormal{opt}})
denotes the relative interior of the domain of
\psi_{\textnormal{opt}}
, i.e. the set on which
\psi_{\textnormal{opt}}
is finite. The symbol
\ast
used as a superscript denotes the convex conjugate of a function, otherwise it is the standard convolution operator.

It turns out that the optimizer
\psi_{\textnormal{opt}}
is not necessarily
\nu
-integrable. Therefore, in order for the difference of the integrals in (
1.6
) to be well defined for
\psi=\psi_{\textnormal{opt}}
, attainment of
D(\mu,\nu)
has to be understood in a “relaxed” sense frequently encountered in martingale transport problems; see
[
12
,
14
,
15
]
and Propositions
4.1
,
4.2
below.

Note that, since the primal optimizer
\pi^{\textnormal{SBM}}
equals the law of
(M_{0},M_{1})
, Theorem
1.4
describes the optimizer of the static weak martingale transport problem (
1.5
) in terms of the gradient of a convex function, which originates from its dual problem (
1.6
). More precisely, we have

\pi^{\textnormal{SBM}}=\operatorname{Law}\big((\nabla v\ast\gamma)(B_{0}),\nabla v(B_{1})\big).

This is analogous to the classical Brenier theorem and hence one might view Theorem
1.4
as a Brenier-type theorem for weak martingale transport.

We emphasize that while Brenier’s theorem is a direct consequence of attainment for the dual transport problem (which is now well understood), the situation is more delicate in case of Theorem
1.4
. The main reason is that dual attainment for martingale transport can fail even in very regular settings, e.g. for Lipschitz costs and compactly supported measures on the real line, see
[
10
, Section 4.3]
. Positive results are only available for
d=1
and under strong assumptions
[
12
,
13
,
15
]
. In addition, the duality theory for weak optimal transport is known to be significantly more complicated than its classical counterpart. Specifically first results for dual attainment have appeared only recently
[
16
]
and are not applicable to weak martingale transport problems.
Accordingly, the technical core of our work is to establish dual attainment in the framework of Theorem
1.4
.

1.6
Bass martingales for specific marginals

Theorem
1.3
is only an existence result, which does not provide an explicit Bass martingale connecting the given marginals. This is parallel to the situation in optimal transport, where the question to numerically approximate optimal transport plans has generated enormous interest. Already in dimension one, the problem to explicitly determine Bass martingales is non-trivial (as well as highly important for financial applications). Here
[
25
]
gives an efficient iteration algorithm. See
[
1
]
for a rigorous proof of the algorithm’s linear convergence and
[
42
]
for a multi-dimensional variant of the algorithm.

On the other hand (as in the case of Brenier’s theorem), one can easily generate examples of Bass martingales by specifying a convex function
v
and the starting distribution of
B_{0}
. We give here one such example that also serves to illustrate some of the intricacies related to irreducible decompositions. Fix parameters
\rho,k>0
and consider the radially symmetric function
v(x,y)=h(|(x,y)|)
, where
h
is continuous and piecewise affine with slope
1/2
on
[0,k]
, slope
8/5
on
[k,\infty)
, and
h(0)=0
. We consider the Bass martingale
M_{t}=\mathbb{E}[\nabla v(B_{1})\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{t}]
, where
B_{0}
is uniformly distributed on the centered circle with radius
\rho
. Then
\nabla v(x)=\frac{x}{2|x|}
,
|x|\in(0,k)
and
\nabla v(x)=\frac{8x}{5|x|}
,
|x|\in(k,\infty)
, from which it follows easily that
M
terminates on either the circle with radius
1/2
or the circle with radius
8/5
and that
M
starts uniformly distributed on some centered circle. Specifying
\rho=3
,
k=3.17
, we find (numerically) that
M_{0}
is uniformly distributed on the unit circle, while
M_{1}
charges the circles with radius
1/2
and
8/5
equally, see Figures
2
and

.

Notably this is not the case for every martingale

1.7
Literature

The first article that provides structural results for the martingale transport problem in general dimensions is the work
[
31
]
of Ghoussoub–Kim–Lim. They obtain descriptions for the minimizers and maximizers for the cost function
c(x,y)=|x-y|
, when marginals are supported on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, as well as for marginals on higher-dimensional state spaces that are in subharmonic order. Given a specific martingale
X
, Ghoussoub–Kim–Lim also define a finest paving of the source space into cells that are invariant under the martingale
X
.

The contributions of De March–Touzi
[
27
]
and Obłój–Siorpaes
[
52
]
put the theme of irreducible decompositions center stage. In contrast to the work of Ghoussoub–Kim–Lim, their interest lies in pavings that are invariant under
all
martingales which start in
\mu
and terminate in
\nu
. Specifically it is shown in
[
27
]
that there exists a unique finest paving with this property. This De March–Touzi paving is revisited in the follow-up paper
[
56
]
, where it is characterized in terms of Bass martingales. While the Ghoussoub–Kim–Lim paving and the De March–Touzi paving agree for
d=1
, they can be different for
d\geqslant 2
, as discussed in the previous section. The interested reader is referred to Ciosmak’s works
[
24
,
23
]
, where a more general notion of irreducibility (going beyond martingale transports) is studied.

Huesmann–Trevisan
[
41
]
investigate Benamou–Brenier-type formulations for the martingale transport problem on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In particular, they provide equivalent PDE-formulations and establish existence and duality results. In the context of market impact in finance Loeper
[
46
]
arrives independently to problem (
MBB
).

For further contributions to the martingale transport in continuous time, we mention
[
28
,
11
,
26
,
33
,
37
,
32
,
22
,
36
]
among many others.

Finally,
[
5
]
is a predecessor of the present article in which it is established that the martingale transport problem (
MBB
) admits a unique solution, solves further related optimization problems and has properties of time consistency in the spirit of classical optimal transport for the squared distance cost function. Furthermore, the counterpart of our main result is established in dimension one (without reference to duality). As discussed in length above, the main difficulty in higher dimensions stems from the subtleties surrounding the concept of irreducible component.

3
Duality for stretched Brownian motion

We fix
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\mu\preceq_{\textnormal{c}}\nu
.
Recall from (
1.5
) that our main focus will lie on the (primal)
weak martingale transport problem

\displaystyle P(\mu,\nu)\coloneqq\sup_{\pi\in\mathsf{MT}(\mu,\nu)}\int\textnormal{MCov}(\pi_{x},\gamma)\,\mu(dx),

where the maximal covariance (
MCov
) between
p_{1},p_{2}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is given by

\textnormal{MCov}(p_{1},p_{2})\coloneqq\sup_{q\in\mathsf{Cpl}(p_{1},p_{2})}\int\langle x_{1},x_{2}\rangle\,q(dx_{1},dx_{2}).

Throughout this paper we shall make the following assumption.

Assumption 3.1
.

The support of
\nu
affinely spans
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

This assumption will be convenient, mainly for notational reasons. But it does not restrict the generality of the results of this paper. Indeed, let
A
denote the affine space spanned by
\operatorname{supp}(\nu)
. For
\pi(dx,dy)=\pi_{x}(dy)\,\mu(dx)\in\mathsf{MT}(\mu,\nu)
we have, for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, that
\operatorname{supp}(\pi_{x})\subseteq\operatorname{supp}(\nu)\subseteq A
. Choose an arbitrary point
x_{0}\in A
and denote by
\gamma_{x_{0}}^{A}
the standard Gaussian distribution on the affine space
A
centered at
x_{0}
. It is easy to see that there is a constant
c=c(x_{0},A)
such that, for
\pi\in\mathsf{MT}(\mu,\nu)
, we have

\int\textnormal{MCov}(\pi_{x},\gamma)\,\mu(dx)=\int\textnormal{MCov}(\pi_{x},\gamma_{x_{0}}^{A})\,\mu(dx)+c.

In other words, replacing
\gamma
by
\gamma_{x_{0}}^{A}
in (
1.5
) only changes the primal problem in a trivial way by adding a constant. Denoting by
m
the affine dimension of
A
, we can choose an isometry from
A
to
\mathbb{R}^{m}
which maps
x_{0}
to
0
and which maps
\mu,\nu
to measures
\overline{\mu},\overline{\nu}
on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
. In this way we have transformed the primal problem (
1.5
) for general
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\mu\preceq_{\textnormal{c}}\nu
to the problem (
1.5
) for
\overline{\mu},\overline{\nu}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
with
\overline{\mu}\preceq_{\textnormal{c}}\overline{\nu}
such that
\operatorname{supp}(\overline{\nu})
affinely spans
\mathbb{R}^{m}
. In conclusion, we shall assume without loss of generality Assumption
3.1
throughout this paper.

We define the dual problem

\tilde{D}(\mu,\nu)\coloneqq\inf_{\psi\in C_{\textnormal{q}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\Big(\int\psi\,d\nu-\int\varphi^{\psi}\,d\mu\Big),

(3.1)

where the function
\mathbb{R}^{d}\ni x\mapsto\varphi^{\psi}(x)
is given by

\varphi^{\psi}(x)\coloneqq\inf_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{2}^{x}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\Big(\int\psi\,dp-\textnormal{MCov}(p,\gamma)\Big).

(3.2)

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\psi\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow(-\infty,+\infty]
be a proper, lower semicontinuous convex function. The function
\varphi^{\psi}
is convex on
\operatorname{dom}\psi
and
\varphi^{\psi}\leqslant\psi
.

The convexity of
x\mapsto\varphi^{\psi}(x)
is repeated and proved in Lemma
5.1
below. By taking
p=\delta_{x}
in (
3.2
), we immediately see that
\varphi^{\psi}(x)\leqslant\psi(x)
.
∎

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\mu\preceq_{\textnormal{c}}\nu
. There is no duality gap between the primal problem (
1.5
) and the dual problem (
3.1
), i.e.,
P(\mu,\nu)=\tilde{D}(\mu,\nu)
. Moreover, the primal problem is uniquely attained and has a finite value, i.e., there exists a unique
\pi^{\textnormal{SBM}}\in\mathsf{MT}(\mu,\nu)
such that

P(\mu,\nu)=\int\textnormal{MCov}(\pi^{\textnormal{SBM}}_{x},\gamma)\,\mu(dx)<+\infty.

(3.3)

Unique attainment of the primal problem is a consequence of the results in
[
5
]
, while duality can be derived from the general duality results for weak optimal transport provided in
[
6
]
, see Appendix
A
for details. To motivate (
3.1
) as a plausible dual formulation of (
1.5
) we provide some heuristics:

Indeed, considering

\chi(\pi)\coloneqq\inf_{\psi\in C_{\textnormal{q}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\Big(\int\psi\,d\nu-\int\psi\,d\pi_{x}\,d\mu(x)\Big)=\begin{cases}0,&\mbox{if }\int\pi_{x}\,d\mu(x)=\nu,\\
-\infty,&\mbox{else},\end{cases}

we
formally
obtain the desired duality relation by interchanging
\inf
and
\sup
:

\displaystyle P(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\pi(dx,dy)=\pi_{x}(dy)\,\mu(dx),\\
\pi_{x}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}^{x}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\end{subarray}}\,\Big(\int\textnormal{MCov}(\pi_{x},\gamma)\,d\mu(x)+\chi(\pi)\Big)

\displaystyle=\inf_{\psi\in C_{\textnormal{q}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\bigg(\int\sup_{\pi_{x}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}^{x}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\Big(\textnormal{MCov}(\pi_{x},\gamma)+\int\psi\,d(\nu-\pi_{x})\Big)\,d\mu(x)\bigg)=\tilde{D}(\mu,\nu).

In the remainder of this section we make the important observation that in the dual problem (
3.1
) it suffices to optimize over the class of functions
\psi\in C_{\textnormal{q}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
which are
convex
. We refer to
[
34
, Theorem 1.1]
for an analogous result. Another similar restriction to convex functions in the setting of weak transport costs has been shown in
[
35
, Theorem 2.11, (3)]
.

We then show in Lemma
3.7
below that it is also equivalent to optimize over all convex functions
\psi\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow(-\infty,+\infty]
which are only
\mu
-a.s. finite, but not necessarily of quadratic growth.

Given some
\psi\in C_{\textnormal{q}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, it will be convenient to have an explicit representation for the convex hull of
\psi
, as in (
3.4
) below. This identity is usually stated in the more specific form

(\operatorname{conv}\psi)(y)=\inf\bigg\{\sum_{i=1}^{d+1}\lambda_{i}\psi(y_{i})\colon\sum_{i=1}^{d+1}\lambda_{i}y_{i}=y\bigg\},

where the infimum is taken over all expressions of
y
as a convex combination of
d+1
points, see
[
54
, Corollary 17.1.5]
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\psi\in C_{\textnormal{q}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Then the convex hull
\operatorname{conv}\psi
satisfies

(\operatorname{conv}\psi)(y)=\inf_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{2}^{y}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\int\psi\,dp,\qquad y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

(3.4)

and again
\operatorname{conv}\psi\in C_{\textnormal{q}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Recalling the dual problem (
3.1
), we define the dual function

\mathcal{D}(\psi)\coloneqq\int\psi\,d\nu-\int\varphi^{\psi}\,d\mu,

(3.5)

for
\psi\in C_{\textnormal{q}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Now we can prove our crucial observation, that it suffices to optimize the dual function over the class of functions
\psi\in C_{\textnormal{q}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
which are
convex
.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\mu\preceq_{\textnormal{c}}\nu
. Then
\mathcal{D}(\operatorname{conv}\psi)\leqslant\mathcal{D}(\psi)
for all
\psi\in C_{\textnormal{q}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and consequently

\tilde{D}(\mu,\nu)=\inf_{\psi\in C_{\textnormal{q}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\mathcal{D}(\psi)=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\psi\in C_{\textnormal{q}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\\
\textnormal{$\psi$ convex}\end{subarray}}\mathcal{D}(\psi).

(3.6)

Let
\varepsilon>0
,
\psi\in C_{\textnormal{q}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\{p_{x}\}_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be a measurable collection of probability measures with
\operatorname{bary}(p_{x})=x
. To show the claim, it is sufficient to construct a measurable family
\{\bar{p}_{x}\}_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\operatorname{bary}(\bar{p}_{x})=x
such that

\textnormal{MCov}(p_{x},\gamma)+\int\operatorname{conv}\psi\,d(\nu-p_{x})\leqslant\textnormal{MCov}(\bar{p}_{x},\gamma)+\int\psi\,d(\nu-\bar{p}_{x})+\varepsilon.

(3.7)

Let us construct appropriate probability measures
\{\bar{p}_{x}\}_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}
. By Lemma
3.4
and a measurable selection argument we can choose a measurable collection of probability measures
\{\tilde{p}_{y}\}_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\operatorname{bary}(\tilde{p}_{y})=y
such that

\int\psi\,d\tilde{p}_{y}\leqslant(\operatorname{conv}\psi)(y)+\varepsilon.

(3.8)

Then we define
\bar{p}_{x}(dz)\coloneqq\int_{y}\tilde{p}_{y}(dz)\,p_{x}(dy)
, so that
\operatorname{bary}(\bar{p}_{x})=x
. Integrating (
3.8
) with respect to
p_{x}(dy)
yields

\int\psi\,d\bar{p}_{x}\leqslant\int\operatorname{conv}\psi\,dp_{x}+\varepsilon.

(3.9)

Since
\psi,\operatorname{conv}\psi\in C_{\textnormal{q}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
p_{x}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}^{x}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
we conclude

\ell+\tfrac{1}{2}\int|y|^{2}\,\bar{p}_{x}(dy)\leqslant a+k\int|y|^{2}\,p_{x}(dy)+\varepsilon<+\infty,

so that
\bar{p}_{x}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}^{x}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

In order to show the inequality (
3.7
), we first observe that
p_{x}\preceq_{\textnormal{c}}\bar{p}_{x}
by Jensen’s inequality. Together with the Kantorovich duality (see, e.g.
[
59
, Theorem 5.10]
), we conclude that

In fact,

is equivalent to the inequality
\textnormal{MCov}(p_{x},q)\leqslant\textnormal{MCov}(\bar{p}_{x},q)

, see. e.g.
[

or
[

.

\displaystyle\textnormal{MCov}(p_{x},\gamma)

\displaystyle=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\\
\textnormal{convex}\end{subarray}}\Big(\int f\,dp_{x}+\int f^{\ast}\,d\gamma\Big)

(3.10)

\displaystyle\leqslant\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\\

On the other hand, from

Remark 3.6
.

On the right-hand side of (
3.6
) we can further require
\psi
to be smooth. Indeed, if
\psi\in C_{\textnormal{q}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is convex, then
\psi^{\varepsilon}\coloneqq\psi\ast\gamma^{\varepsilon}
is a smooth convex function in
C_{\textnormal{q}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and

\liminf_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\Big(\int\psi^{\varepsilon}\,d\nu-\int\varphi^{\psi^{\varepsilon}}\,d\mu\Big)\leqslant\int\psi\,d\nu-\int\varphi^{\psi}\,d\mu,

as follows by dominated convergence and the inequality
\psi^{\varepsilon}\geqslant\psi
.

For
\psi\in C_{\textnormal{q}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we recall the definition (
3.5
) of the dual function
\mathcal{D}(\,\cdot\,)
, which we rewrite as

\mathcal{D}(\psi)=\int\Big(\int\psi(y)\,\pi_{x}(dy)-\varphi^{\psi}(x)\Big)\,\mu(dx),

(3.12)

where
\pi(dx,dy)=\pi_{x}(dy)\,\mu(dx)
is an arbitrary fixed element of
\mathsf{MT}(\mu,\nu)
. The “relaxed” representation (
3.12
) of (
3.5
) allows us to extend the definition of the dual function
\mathcal{D}(\,\cdot\,)
to convex functions
\psi\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow(-\infty,+\infty]
which are not confined to be in
C_{\textnormal{q}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, but which are only required to be
\mu
-a.s. finite, i.e., satisfy
\mu(\operatorname{dom}\psi)=1
. This is summarized in the following lemma.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\mu\preceq_{\textnormal{c}}\nu
and fix
\pi\in\mathsf{MT}(\mu,\nu)
. Let
\psi\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow(-\infty,+\infty]
be a convex function which is
\mu
-a.s. finite. Formula (
3.12
) then defines
\mathcal{D}(\psi)\in[0,+\infty]
and, recalling (
3.1
), we have the inequality

\mathcal{D}(\psi)\geqslant\tilde{D}(\mu,\nu).

(3.13)

In particular, recalling (
3.6
), we have

\tilde{D}(\mu,\nu)=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\psi\in C_{\textnormal{q}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\\
\textnormal{$\psi$ convex}\end{subarray}}\mathcal{D}(\psi)=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mu(\operatorname{dom}\psi)=1,\\
\textnormal{$\psi$ convex}\end{subarray}}\mathcal{D}(\psi).

(3.14)

Let
\psi\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow(-\infty,+\infty]
be a convex function which is
\mu
-a.s. finite. First, note that by Jensen’s inequality we have

\int\psi(y)\,\pi_{x}(dy)\geqslant\psi\Big(\int y\,\pi_{x}(dy)\Big)=\psi(x).

Together with the inequality
\varphi^{\psi}(x)\leqslant\psi(x)
(recall Lemma
3.2
), we conclude that

\int\psi(y)\,\pi_{x}(dy)-\varphi^{\psi}(x)\geqslant 0,

(3.15)

for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, so that
\mathcal{D}(\psi)\in[0,+\infty]
. In order to prove the inequality (
3.13
), we distinguish two cases. In the case
\int\psi\,d\pi_{x}=+\infty
, for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
in a set of positive
\mu
-measure, we conclude from (
3.15
) that
\mathcal{D}(\psi)=+\infty
, so that (
3.13
) is trivially satisfied. Now suppose that
\int\psi\,d\pi_{x}<+\infty
, for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Recall from Theorem
3.3
that there exists an optimizer
\pi^{\textnormal{SBM}}\in\mathsf{MT}(\mu,\nu)
of (
1.5
). Taking
p=\pi^{\textnormal{SBM}}_{x}
in (
3.2
) we get

\varphi^{\psi}(x)\leqslant\int\psi\,d\pi^{\textnormal{SBM}}_{x}-\textnormal{MCov}(\pi^{\textnormal{SBM}}_{x},\gamma)

and therefore

\mathcal{D}(\psi)\geqslant\int\Big(\int\psi\,d\pi_{x}+\textnormal{MCov}(\pi^{\textnormal{SBM}}_{x},\gamma)-\int\psi\,d\pi^{\textnormal{SBM}}_{x}\Big)\,\mu(dx)=P(\mu,\nu),

as
\pi^{\textnormal{SBM}}
is a primal optimizer and both
\pi^{\textnormal{SBM}}
and

have
\nu
as second marginal. Since

by Theorem
3.3
, we again see that (
3.13
) is satisfied.

Finally, from (
3.13
) and recalling (

\tilde{D}(\mu,\nu)\leqslant\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mu(\operatorname{dom}\psi)=1,\\
\textnormal{$\psi$ convex}\end{subarray}}\mathcal{D}(\psi)\leqslant\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\psi\in C_{\textnormal{q}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\\
\textnormal{$\psi$ convex}\end{subarray}}\mathcal{D}(\psi)=\tilde{D}(\mu,\nu),

which shows (
3.14

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\mu\preceq_{\textnormal{c}}\nu
. Then
\tilde{D}(\mu,\nu)
is equal to

D(\mu,\nu)=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\psi\in L^{1}(\nu),\\
\textnormal{$\psi$ convex}\end{subarray}}\Big(\int\psi\,d\nu-\int(\psi^{\ast}\ast\gamma)^{\ast}\,d\mu\Big).

(4.1)

In Subsection

we will prove the second part of Theorem
1.4
and in particular discuss the existence of dual optimizers of (
4.1
). Since in general we cannot expect a dual optimizer to be integrable with respect to the probability measure

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\mu\preceq_{\textnormal{c}}\nu
. Then
\tilde{D}(\mu,\nu)
is equal to

D_{\textnormal{rel}}(\mu,\nu)\coloneqq\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mu(\operatorname{dom}\psi)=1,\\
\textnormal{$\psi$ convex}\end{subarray}}\mathcal{E}(\psi),

(4.2)

where

\mathcal{E}(\psi)\coloneqq\int\Big(\int\psi(y)\,\pi_{x}(dy)-(\psi^{\ast}\ast\gamma)^{\ast}(x)\Big)\,\mu(dx),

(4.3)

with
\pi
being an arbitrary fixed element of
\mathsf{MT}(\mu,\nu)
.

The main idea behind the proofs of Propositions
4.1
,
4.2
(which we will present at the end of this section) is to apply Proposition
3.5
and then to show that
\varphi^{\psi}=(\psi^{\ast}\ast\gamma)^{\ast}
, for every convex function
\psi\in C_{\textnormal{q}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. This motivates our next goal, namely to solve the minimization problem (
3.2
), which we rewrite as a maximization problem

-\varphi^{\psi}(x)=\sup_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{2}^{x}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\Big(\textnormal{MCov}(p,\gamma)-\int\psi\,dp\Big).

(4.4)

As a preliminary step, we consider the simpler problem

\varrho^{\psi}\coloneqq\sup_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\Big(\textnormal{MCov}(p,\gamma)-\int\psi\,dp\Big),

(4.5)

where we do not prescribe the barycenter
x
of
p\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. In Lemma
4.4
below we will show for an arbitrary proper convex function
\psi\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow(-\infty,+\infty]
, that the value
\varrho^{\psi}
equals
\int\psi^{\ast}\,d\gamma
. In fact, this information is essentially enough to prove Propositions
4.1
,
4.2
.

Solving the maximization problem (
4.5
) leads to an interesting connection with Brenier maps in Lemma
4.3
below. By
Brenier’s theorem
(see, e.g.,
[
58
, Theorem 2.12]
), the optimal transport for quadratic cost between
\gamma
and
p\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is induced by the
\gamma
-a.e. defined gradient
\nabla v
of some convex function
v\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
via
(\nabla v)(\gamma)=p
.

Lemma 4.3
(“reverse Brenier”).

Let
v\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a finite-valued convex function and
\psi\coloneqq v^{\ast}
its convex conjugate. Assume that the probability measure
\hat{p}\coloneqq(\nabla v)(\gamma)
has finite second moment. Then
\hat{p}
is the unique maximizer of the optimization problem (
4.5
) and

\varrho^{\psi}=\int v\,d\gamma=\int\psi^{\ast}\,d\gamma<+\infty.

We denote by
T_{\gamma}^{p}
the Brenier map from
\gamma
to
p\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and note that
T_{\gamma}^{\hat{p}}=\nabla v
. Since the convex function
v
is finite-valued, its gradient exists
\gamma
-a.e. and we have that

v(z)=\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big(\langle y,z\rangle-v^{\ast}(y)\big)=\langle\nabla v(z),z\rangle-v^{\ast}(\nabla v(z)),

for
\gamma
-a.e.
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Using these observations, for
p\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
we get

\displaystyle\textnormal{MCov}(p,\gamma)-\int\psi\,dp

\displaystyle=\int\Big(\big\langle T_{\gamma}^{p}(z),z\big\rangle-v^{\ast}\big(T_{\gamma}^{p}(z)\big)\Big)\,\gamma(dz)

\displaystyle\leqslant\int\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big(\langle y,z\rangle-v^{\ast}(y)\big)\,\gamma(dz)=\int v(z)\,\gamma(dz)

\displaystyle=\int\Big(\big\langle\nabla v(z),z\big\rangle-v^{\ast}\big(\nabla v(z)\big)\Big)\,\gamma(dz)

\displaystyle=\textnormal{MCov}(\hat{p},\gamma)-\int\psi\,d\hat{p},

with equality if and only if
T_{\gamma}^{p}(z)=T_{\gamma}^{\hat{p}}(z)
, for
\gamma
-a.e.
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. This in turn is the case if and only if
p=\hat{p}
. Finally, from the convexity of
v
and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality we obtain

\int|v|\,d\gamma\leqslant|v(0)|+\sqrt{\int|\nabla v|^{2}\,d\gamma}\cdot\sqrt{d}<+\infty,

(4.6)

which proves that
\varrho^{\psi}<+\infty
.
∎

Note that in Lemma
4.3
we started with a
finite-valued
convex function
v\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and then
defined

\psi\coloneqq v^{\ast}
. If we additionally
know
that the probability measure
\hat{p}=(\nabla v)(\gamma)
has finite second moment, then
\hat{p}
is the unique maximizer of the optimization problem (
4.5
). These are rather strong assumptions. However, if we are given just a proper convex function
\psi\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow(-\infty,+\infty]
, we can still compute the value of the supremum in (
4.5
), without explicitly constructing a maximizer of this optimization problem.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
\psi\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow(-\infty,+\infty]
be a proper convex function. Then

\varrho^{\psi}=\int\psi^{\ast}\,d\gamma.

(4.7)

We postpone the proof of Lemma
4.4
to Appendix
B
. As already announced, with the help of (
4.7
), we are now able to prove Propositions
4.1
,
4.2
. We first show a simpler variant, where we optimize over the class of convex functions
\psi
in
C_{\textnormal{q}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
(i.e., which have quadratic growth), as in (
4.8
) of Proposition
4.5
below. Proposition
4.2
, where we optimize over
all

\mu
-a.s. finite-valued convex functions
\psi\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow(-\infty,+\infty]
as in (
4.2
), is then a straightforward consequence. Proposition
4.1
, where we optimize over all
\nu
-integrable convex functions
\psi
as in (
4.1
), follows from a “sandwich argument”.

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\mu\preceq_{\textnormal{c}}\nu
. Then
\tilde{D}(\mu,\nu)
is equal to

D_{\textnormal{q}}(\mu,\nu)\coloneqq\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\psi\in C_{\textnormal{q}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\\
\textnormal{$\psi$ convex}\end{subarray}}\Big(\int\psi\,d\nu-\int(\psi^{\ast}\ast\gamma)^{\ast}\,d\mu\Big).

(4.8)

By Proposition
3.5
we have

\tilde{D}(\mu,\nu)=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\psi\in C_{\textnormal{q}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\\
\textnormal{$\psi$ convex}\end{subarray}}\Big(\int\psi\,d\nu-\int\varphi^{\psi}\,d\mu\Big).

Hence it remains to show that
\varphi^{\psi}=(\psi^{\ast}\ast\gamma)^{\ast}
, for every convex function
\psi\in C_{\textnormal{q}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. In order to do this, we will first prove that
(\varphi^{\psi})^{\ast}=\psi^{\ast}\ast\gamma
. By definition of the convex conjugate and (
4.4
), for
\zeta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

\displaystyle(\varphi^{\psi})^{\ast}(\zeta)

\displaystyle=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big(\langle x,\zeta\rangle-\varphi^{\psi}(x)\big)

(4.9)

\displaystyle=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sup_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{2}^{x}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\Big(\textnormal{MCov}(p,\gamma)-\int\psi_{\zeta}\,dp\Big)

(4.10)

\displaystyle=\sup_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\Big(\textnormal{MCov}(p,\gamma)-\int\psi_{\zeta}\,dp\Big)=\varrho^{\psi_{\zeta}},

(4.11)

where the function
\psi_{\zeta}
is defined by
\psi_{\zeta}(y)\coloneqq\psi(y)-\langle\zeta,y\rangle
, for
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Now applying Lemma
4.4
to the proper convex function
\psi_{\zeta}
yields

(\varphi^{\psi})^{\ast}(\zeta)=\varrho^{\psi_{\zeta}}=\int\psi_{\zeta}^{\ast}\,d\gamma=\int\psi^{\ast}(\zeta+z)\,d\gamma(z)=(\psi^{\ast}\ast\gamma)(\zeta).

(4.12)

To complete the proof, we must justify that
(\varphi^{\psi})^{\ast\ast}=\varphi^{\psi}
, i.e. that the Fenchel–Moreau theorem is applicable. To see this, we first note that the function
x\mapsto\varphi^{\psi}(x)
is convex (recall Lemma
3.2
) and we have the upper bound
\varphi^{\psi}\leqslant\psi<+\infty
. Furthermore, for any
p\in\mathcal{P}_{2}^{x}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
we have the inequalities

\textnormal{MCov}(p,\gamma)\leqslant\tfrac{1}{2}\int|y|^{2}\,dp(y)+\tfrac{d}{2}

as well as

\int\psi\,dp\geqslant\ell+\tfrac{1}{2}\int|y|^{2}\,dp(y),

the latter following from the fact that
\psi\in C_{\textnormal{q}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. As a consequence, we get the lower bound
\varphi^{\psi}\geqslant\ell-\frac{d}{2}>-\infty
. Altogether,
\varphi^{\psi}
is a convex function which is finite everywhere on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, thus it is continuous and we indeed have
(\varphi^{\psi})^{\ast\ast}=\varphi^{\psi}
.
∎

We first show that the function
\mathcal{E}(\,\cdot\,)
in (
4.3
) is well defined for every convex function
\psi\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow(-\infty,+\infty]
, which is
\mu
-a.s. finite. To this end, we will prove the inequality
(\psi^{\ast}\ast\gamma)^{\ast}\leqslant\psi
. By Jensen’s inequality, this implies that the integrand in (
4.3
) is
\mu
-a.s. non-negative, and hence
\mathcal{E}(\psi)
is well defined and
[0,+\infty]
-valued. Recalling the equation (
4.12
) above, we have
\psi^{\ast}\ast\gamma=(\varphi^{\psi})^{\ast}
. Taking the convex conjugate and using that
\varphi^{\psi}\leqslant\psi
, we obtain
(\psi^{\ast}\ast\gamma)^{\ast}=(\varphi^{\psi})^{\ast\ast}\leqslant\varphi^{\psi}\leqslant\psi
, as required.

Now let us turn to the proof of
\tilde{D}(\mu,\nu)=D_{\textnormal{rel}}(\mu,\nu)
. Recalling (
4.2
), (
4.8
), and using Proposition
4.5
, we have
D_{\textnormal{rel}}(\mu,\nu)\leqslant D_{\textnormal{q}}(\mu,\nu)=\tilde{D}(\mu,\nu)
, so that we need to show the inequality
\tilde{D}(\mu,\nu)\leqslant D_{\textnormal{rel}}(\mu,\nu)
. Let
\pi\in\mathsf{MT}(\mu,\nu)
and
\psi\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow(-\infty,+\infty]
be convex with
\mu(\operatorname{dom}\psi)=1
. Since
\tilde{D}(\mu,\nu)=P(\mu,\nu)
(recall Theorem
3.3
and (
3.3
)), we have to verify the inequality

\int\textnormal{MCov}(\pi_{x},\gamma)\,d\mu(x)\leqslant\int\Big(\int\psi(y)\,\pi_{x}(dy)-(\psi^{\ast}\ast\gamma)^{\ast}(x)\Big)\,\mu(dx).

It is sufficient to prove, for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, that

\textnormal{MCov}(\pi_{x},\gamma)\leqslant\int\psi\,d\pi_{x}-(\psi^{\ast}\ast\gamma)^{\ast}(x).

(4.13)

We express the convex conjugate on the right-hand side of (
4.13
) as

-(\psi^{\ast}\ast\gamma)^{\ast}(x)=\inf_{\zeta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big((\psi^{\ast}\ast\gamma)(\zeta)-\langle\zeta,x\rangle\big)=\inf_{\zeta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Big(\int\psi_{\zeta}^{\ast}\,d\gamma-\langle\zeta,x\rangle\Big),

where the function
\psi_{\zeta}
is defined by
\psi_{\zeta}(y)\coloneqq\psi(y)-\langle\zeta,y\rangle
, for
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Substituting back into (
4.13
) yields

\textnormal{MCov}(\pi_{x},\gamma)\leqslant\inf_{\zeta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Big(\int\psi_{\zeta}\,d\pi_{x}+\int\psi_{\zeta}^{\ast}\,d\gamma\Big).

(4.14)

Now observe that by the Fenchel–Young inequality we have

\textnormal{MCov}(\pi_{x},\gamma)\leqslant\int\phi\,d\pi_{x}+\int\phi^{\ast}\,d\gamma,

for every proper convex function
\phi\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow(-\infty,+\infty]
. In particular, for every
\zeta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, it holds that

\textnormal{MCov}(\pi_{x},\gamma)\leqslant\int\psi_{\zeta}\,d\pi_{x}+\int\psi_{\zeta}^{\ast}\,d\gamma,

which implies (
4.14
). This completes the proof of Proposition
4.2
.
∎

The assertion follows immediately from Proposition
4.2
and Proposition
4.5
by a “sandwich argument”. Indeed, we have

\tilde{D}(\mu,\nu)=D_{\textnormal{rel}}(\mu,\nu)\leqslant D(\mu,\nu)\leqslant D_{\textnormal{q}}(\mu,\nu)=\tilde{D}(\mu,\nu).

(4.15)

The inequalities in (
4.15
) are due to the inclusions

\{\psi\in C_{\textnormal{q}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\colon\textnormal{$\psi$ convex}\}\subseteq\{\psi\in L^{1}(\nu)\colon\textnormal{$\psi$ convex}\}\subseteq\{\textnormal{$\psi$ convex}\colon\mu(\operatorname{dom}\psi)=1\};

the equalities on the left-hand side and on the right-hand side of (
4.15
) are justified by Proposition
4.2
and Proposition
4.5
, respectively.
∎

5
Preparation for the proof of the second part of Theorem
1.4

The goal of this rather technical section is to outline and prepare the proof of the second part of Theorem
1.4
, for which we will need the results of Sections
6
and
7
. In Section
7
we will show that the value
D(\mu,\nu)
is attained by a convex function
\psi_{\textnormal{opt}}
with
\mu(\operatorname{ri}(\operatorname{dom}\psi_{\textnormal{opt}}))=1
if and only if
(\mu,\nu)
is irreducible. In Section
6
(see Theorem
6.6
) we will prove that there is such a dual optimizer
\psi_{\textnormal{opt}}
if and only if there exists a Bass martingale from
\mu
to
\nu
. At a first reading one might skip the present Section
5
.

We recall the primal problem (
1.5
), with optimizer
\pi^{\textnormal{SBM}}\in\mathsf{MT}(\mu,\nu)
, i.e.

P(\mu,\nu)=\int\textnormal{MCov}(\pi^{\textnormal{SBM}}_{x},\gamma)\,\mu(dx);

(5.1)

the dual problem (
3.1
), with optimizer
\psi_{\textnormal{opt}}
(supposing that this optimizer exists), i.e.

\tilde{D}(\mu,\nu)=\Big(\int\psi_{\textnormal{opt}}\,d\nu-\int\varphi^{\psi_{\textnormal{opt}}}\,d\mu\Big);

(5.2)

and the fact that there is no duality gap by Theorem
3.3
. Therefore, comparing the primal value (
5.1
) with the dual value (
5.2
), we see that the minimization problem

\varphi^{\psi_{\textnormal{opt}}}(x)=\inf_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{2}^{x}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\Big(\int\psi_{\textnormal{opt}}\,dp-\textnormal{MCov}(p,\gamma)\Big)

(recall (
3.2
)) is attained by
\pi^{\textnormal{SBM}}_{x}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}^{x}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. To draw the connection to the Bass martingale, we need to express the optimizer
\pi^{\textnormal{SBM}}_{x}
in terms of the (single) convex function
\psi_{\textnormal{opt}}
. More precisely, we will see as a consequence of Proposition
5.3
that
\pi^{\textnormal{SBM}}_{x}=(\nabla\psi_{\textnormal{opt}}^{\ast})(\gamma_{\zeta})
, for some appropriate
\zeta=\zeta(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For this purpose, we have to return to the optimization problem (
3.2
) and study its optimizers.

First, we need the following technical result, formulated in Lemma
5.1
below. Let
\psi\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow(-\infty,+\infty]
be a lower semicontinuous convex function. We recall from convex analysis that
\psi
is called co-finite if

\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\}\colon\quad\lim_{t\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{\psi(tx)}{t}=+\infty.

According to
[
54
, Corollary 13.3.1]
, the convex function
\psi
is co-finite if and only if its convex conjugate
\psi^{\ast}
is finite everywhere on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We derive now a sufficient condition for the co-finiteness of
\psi
, in terms of the function
\varphi^{\psi}
defined in (
3.2
). We defer the proof of Lemma
5.1
to Appendix
B
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\psi\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow(-\infty,+\infty]
be a proper, lower semicontinuous convex function. Then
\varphi^{\psi}
is convex on
\operatorname{dom}\psi
. If we additionally assume that
\varphi^{\psi}(x)>-\infty
, for some
x\in\operatorname{int}(\operatorname{dom}\psi)
, then
\varphi^{\psi}>-\infty
on
\operatorname{int}(\operatorname{dom}\psi)
and
\psi
is co-finite.

Our next result is the analogue of Lemma
4.4
. But now, instead of maximizing over all
p\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
as in (
4.5
), we have to maximize over all
p\in\mathcal{P}_{2}^{x}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, with a fixed barycenter
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, as in (
4.4
).

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
\psi\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow(-\infty,+\infty]
be a lower semicontinuous convex function and assume that
\varphi^{\psi}(x)>-\infty
for some
x\in\operatorname{int}(\operatorname{dom}\psi)
. Then we have the duality formula

\varphi^{\psi}(x)=\sup_{\zeta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Big(\langle\zeta,x\rangle-\int\psi^{\ast}(\zeta+z)\,d\gamma(z)\Big),

(5.3)

and the right-hand side admits a unique maximizer
\zeta(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

We deploy a similar strategy as in the proof of Proposition
4.5
. Recalling equations (
4.9
) – (
4.12
), for each
\zeta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

(\varphi^{\psi})^{\ast}(\zeta)=\sup_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\Big(\textnormal{MCov}(p,\gamma)-\int\psi_{\zeta}\,dp\Big)=\int\psi^{\ast}(\zeta+z)\,d\gamma(z),

(5.4)

where the function
\psi_{\zeta}
is defined by
\psi_{\zeta}(y)\coloneqq\psi(y)-\langle\zeta,y\rangle
, for
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Since
\varphi^{\psi}(x)>-\infty
for some
x\in\operatorname{int}(\operatorname{dom}\psi)
, the convex function
\xi\mapsto\varphi^{\psi}(\xi)
is finite and thus also continuous in a neighbourhood of
x
. Hence we can apply the variant
[
8
, Proposition 13.44]
of the Fenchel–Moreau theorem and obtain

\varphi^{\psi}(x)=(\varphi^{\psi})^{\ast\ast}(x)=\sup_{\zeta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Big(\langle\zeta,x\rangle-\int\psi^{\ast}(\zeta+z)\,d\gamma(z)\Big),

(5.5)

which proves the duality formula (
5.3
).

To prove the existence of a maximizer
\zeta(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we define the function

f_{x}(\zeta)\coloneqq\sup_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\Big(\textnormal{MCov}(p,\gamma)-\int\psi\,dp+\int\langle\zeta,y-x\rangle\,dp(y)\Big),

(5.6)

so that
-\varphi^{\psi}(x)=\inf_{\zeta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f_{x}(\zeta)
. Indeed, it follows from (
5.4
), (
5.5
) that

\displaystyle-\varphi^{\psi}(x)

\displaystyle=\inf_{\zeta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Big(\int\psi^{\ast}(\zeta+z)\,d\gamma(z)-\langle\zeta,x\rangle\Big)

\displaystyle=\inf_{\zeta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sup_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\Big(\textnormal{MCov}(p,\gamma)-\int\psi_{\zeta}\,dp-\langle\zeta,x\rangle\Big).

By assumption, the function
f_{x}
takes values in
(-\infty,+\infty]
and is not constant equal to
+\infty
. Equivalently, we can express
f_{x}(\zeta)
as

f_{x}(\zeta)=\int\big(\psi^{\ast}(\zeta+z)+\psi(x)-\langle\zeta+z,x\rangle\big)\,d\gamma(z)-\psi(x).

(5.7)

We will show that
\zeta\mapsto f_{x}(\zeta)
is lower semicontinuous and coercive, implying the existence of an optimizer
\zeta(x)
. It is easy to see from the representation (
5.7
) that
f_{x}
is lower semicontinuous. Indeed, this directly follows from the lower semicontinuity of the non-negative integrand in (
5.7
) and Fatou’s lemma. For the verification of the coercivity, we take a sequence
(\zeta^{(n)})_{n\geqslant 1}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
|\zeta^{(n)}|\rightarrow+\infty
. Then there is a coordinate
k\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
such that
|\zeta_{k}^{(n)}|\rightarrow+\infty
. Since
x\in\operatorname{int}(\operatorname{dom}\psi)
, we can choose
\varepsilon>0
small enough such that
x\pm\varepsilon\,e_{k}\in\operatorname{dom}\psi
, where
e_{k}
denotes the
k
-th standard basis vector of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Defining

y^{(n)}\coloneqq x+\operatorname{sign}(\zeta_{k}^{(n)})\,\varepsilon\,e_{k}\in\operatorname{dom}\psi

and taking
p=\delta_{y^{(n)}}
, we conclude from (
5.6
) that

f_{x}(\zeta^{(n)})\geqslant-\psi(y^{(n)})+\varepsilon\,|\zeta_{k}^{(n)}|\rightarrow+\infty,

which shows
\lim_{|\zeta|\rightarrow+\infty}f_{x}(\zeta)=+\infty
.

We now show that the maximizer
\zeta(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
of the right-hand side of (
5.3
) is unique. As the assumptions of Lemma
5.1
are satisfied, the convex function
\psi
is co-finite, and thus
\psi^{\ast}
is finite everywhere on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In particular,
\psi^{\ast}
is continuous everywhere and differentiable Lebesgue-a.e. on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In order to establish the uniqueness of
\zeta(x)
, we show that the function
\zeta\mapsto\int\psi^{\ast}(\zeta+z)\,d\gamma(z)\in(-\infty,+\infty]
is strictly convex on its domain. By contradiction, suppose that there are
\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\zeta_{1}\neq\zeta_{2}
such that both
\int\psi^{\ast}(\zeta_{1}+z)\,d\gamma(z)
and
\int\psi^{\ast}(\zeta_{2}+z)\,d\gamma(z)
are finite and

\int\psi^{\ast}\big(t\zeta_{1}+(1-t)\zeta_{2}+z\big)\,d\gamma(z)=t\int\psi^{\ast}(\zeta_{1}+z)\,d\gamma(z)+(1-t)\int\psi^{\ast}(\zeta_{2}+z)\,d\gamma(z),

for some
t\in(0,1)
. Then

\psi^{\ast}\big(t\zeta_{1}+(1-t)\zeta_{2}+z\big)=t\psi^{\ast}(\zeta_{1}+z)+(1-t)\psi^{\ast}(\zeta_{2}+z),

for
\gamma
-a.e.
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Consequently, the function
\psi^{\ast}
is affine on the line segment from
\zeta_{1}+z
to
\zeta_{2}+z
, for Lebesgue-a.e.
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. By convexity and finiteness of
\psi^{\ast}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we thus have that the function

\mathbb{R}\in\lambda\longmapsto\psi^{\ast}\big(\lambda(\zeta_{2}-\zeta_{1})+z\big)

is affine, for every
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. By
[
54
, Theorem 8.8]
, there exists some
c\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\psi^{\ast}\big(\lambda(\zeta_{2}-\zeta_{1})+z\big)=\psi^{\ast}(z)+\lambda c.

Differentiating at the point
\lambda=1
yields
\langle\nabla\psi^{\ast}(z),\zeta_{2}-\zeta_{1}\rangle=c
, for Lebesgue-a.e.
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. But this is a contradiction to
\varnothing\neq\operatorname{int}(\operatorname{dom}\psi)\subseteq(\partial\psi^{\ast})(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, where the symbol
\partial
, applied to a convex function, denotes its subdifferential.
∎

The final result of this section is the analogue of Lemma
4.3
. The duality formula (
5.3
) established in Proposition
5.2
enables us to identify the structure of the optimizer in the maximization problem (
4.4
).

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
\psi\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow(-\infty,+\infty]
be a lower semicontinuous convex function. For fixed
\zeta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
we define the function
\psi_{\zeta}
by
\psi_{\zeta}(y)\coloneqq\psi(y)-\langle\zeta,y\rangle
, for
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, so that
\psi_{\zeta}^{\ast}(y)=\psi^{\ast}(\zeta+y)
.

(i)

If there are
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\zeta=\zeta(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
(\nabla\psi_{\zeta}^{\ast})(\gamma)\in\mathcal{P}_{2}^{x}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, then this is actually the unique optimizer of the supremum in (
4.4
). If additionally
\varphi^{\psi}(x)>-\infty
and
x\in\operatorname{int}(\operatorname{dom}\psi)
, then
\zeta=\zeta(x)
is the optimizer of the supremum in (
5.3
).

(ii)

If
\varphi^{\psi}(x)>-\infty
for some
x\in\operatorname{int}(\operatorname{dom}\psi)
and the supremum in (
4.4
) is attained by some
\hat{p}_{x}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}^{x}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, then
\hat{p}_{x}=(\nabla\psi_{\zeta}^{\ast})(\gamma)
with
\zeta=\zeta(x)
being the optimizer of the supremum in (
5.3
).

(i)
We set
\hat{p}_{x}\coloneqq(\nabla\psi_{\zeta}^{\ast})(\gamma)
and let
p_{x}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}^{x}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be arbitrary. We denote by
T_{\gamma}^{p_{x}}
the Brenier map from
\gamma
to
p_{x}
and note that
T_{\gamma}^{\hat{p}_{x}}=\nabla\psi_{\zeta}^{\ast}
. Similar to the proof of Lemma
4.3
, we compute

\displaystyle\int\psi\,dp_{x}-\textnormal{MCov}(p_{x},\gamma)

\displaystyle=\int\Big(\psi_{\zeta}\big(T_{\gamma}^{p_{x}}(z)\big)+\big\langle\zeta,T_{\gamma}^{p_{x}}(z)\big\rangle-\big\langle T_{\gamma}^{p_{x}}(z),z\big\rangle\Big)\,d\gamma(z)

\displaystyle\geqslant\langle\zeta,x\rangle+\int\inf_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big(\psi_{\zeta}(y)-\langle y,z\rangle\big)\,d\gamma(z)

\displaystyle=\langle\zeta,x\rangle-\int\psi^{\ast}(\zeta+z)\,d\gamma(z)

\displaystyle=\int\psi\,d\hat{p}_{x}-\textnormal{MCov}(\hat{p}_{x},\gamma),

with equality if and only if
T_{\gamma}^{p_{x}}(z)=T_{\gamma}^{\hat{p}_{x}}(z)
, for
\gamma
-a.e.
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. This in turn is the case if and only if
p_{x}=\hat{p}_{x}
. We conclude that
\hat{p}_{x}
is the unique optimizer of the supremum in (
4.4
), i.e.

\varphi^{\psi}(x)=\int\psi\,d\hat{p}_{x}-\textnormal{MCov}(\hat{p}_{x},\gamma)=\langle\zeta,x\rangle-\int\psi^{\ast}(\zeta+z)\,d\gamma(z).

(5.8)

On the other hand, if additionally
\varphi^{\psi}(x)>-\infty
and
x\in\operatorname{int}(\operatorname{dom}\psi)
, by Proposition
5.2
we have that

\varphi^{\psi}(x)=\sup_{\zeta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Big(\langle\zeta,x\rangle-\int\psi^{\ast}(\zeta+z)\,d\gamma(z)\Big),

which in light of (
5.8
) implies that
\zeta=\zeta(x)
is the optimizer of the supremum in (
5.3
).

(ii)
By assumption and Proposition
5.2
, we have that

\displaystyle\varphi^{\psi}(x)

\displaystyle=\langle\zeta,x\rangle+\int\Big(\psi_{\zeta}\big(T_{\gamma}^{\hat{p}_{x}}(z)\big)-\big\langle T_{\gamma}^{\hat{p}_{x}}(z),z\big\rangle\Big)\,d\gamma(z)

\displaystyle=\langle\zeta,x\rangle-\int\psi_{\zeta}^{\ast}(z)\,d\gamma(z)

and therefore

\int\Big(\psi_{\zeta}\big(T_{\gamma}^{\hat{p}_{x}}(z)\big)+\psi^{\ast}_{\zeta}(z)-\big\langle T_{\gamma}^{\hat{p}_{x}}(z),z\big\rangle\Big)\,d\gamma(z)=0.

As the integrand is pointwise non-negative, we conclude that

\psi\big(T_{\gamma}^{\hat{p}_{x}}(z)\big)+\psi^{\ast}(\zeta+z)=\big\langle T_{\gamma}^{\hat{p}_{x}}(z),\zeta+z\big\rangle

and consequently
T_{\gamma}^{\hat{p}_{x}}(z)\in\partial\psi^{\ast}(\zeta+z)
, for
\gamma
-a.e.
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Hence
T_{\gamma}^{\hat{p}_{x}}(z)=\nabla\psi^{\ast}(\zeta+z)
, for
\gamma
-a.e.
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which implies that
\hat{p}_{x}=(\nabla\psi_{\zeta}^{\ast})(\gamma)
.
∎

6
Existence of dual optimizers and Bass martingales

Throughout this section we fix
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\mu\preceq_{\textnormal{c}}\nu
.

Definition 6.1
.

A lower semicontinuous convex function
\psi_{\textnormal{opt}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow(-\infty,+\infty]
satisfying
\mu(\operatorname{ri}(\operatorname{dom}\psi_{\textnormal{opt}}))=1
is called an optimizer of the dual problem (
3.14
) (in short, a dual optimizer) if
\tilde{D}(\mu,\nu)=\mathcal{D}(\psi_{\textnormal{opt}})
, for the dual function
\mathcal{D}(\,\cdot\,)
as defined in (
3.12
).

Let us recall that by Assumption
3.1
the support of
\nu
affinely spans
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. As the next result shows, we could have required in the definition of a dual optimizer
\psi_{\textnormal{opt}}
that also the domain of
\psi_{\textnormal{opt}}
affinely spans
\mathbb{R}^{d}
; we defer the proof to Appendix
B
.

Lemma 6.2
.

If
\psi_{\textnormal{opt}}
is an optimizer according to Definition
6.1
, then the domain of
\psi_{\textnormal{opt}}
has non-empty interior. In particular, by Assumption
3.1
, we have that

\dim(\operatorname{dom}\psi_{\textnormal{opt}})=\dim(\operatorname{supp}\nu)=d.

We recall Definition
1.1
and list some important properties of Bass martingales. Such martingales were introduced in
[
5
]
under the name of a “standard stretched Brownian motion”. In this paper, we use — with Richard Bass’ permission — the term “Bass martingale” instead.

Remark 6.3
.

Let
M=(M_{t})_{0\leqslant t\leqslant 1}
be a Bass martingale from
\mu
to
\nu
, with corresponding convex function
v\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and initial distribution
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\ni\alpha\sim B_{0}
of the underlying Brownian motion
B=(B_{t})_{0\leqslant t\leqslant 1}
.

(i)

As shown in
[
5
]
(and as a result of Theorem
6.6
below), the martingale transport

\operatorname{Law}(M_{0},M_{1})\in\mathsf{MT}(\mu,\nu)

is equal to the unique optimizer
\pi^{\textnormal{SBM}}
of (
1.5
). Furthermore, the knowledge of
\pi^{\textnormal{SBM}}
already determines the martingale
(M_{t})_{0\leqslant t\leqslant 1}
as well as the function
v
, which is
(\alpha\ast\gamma)
-a.e. (equivalently, Lebesgue-a.e.) unique up to an additive constant.

(ii)

The convex function
v
and the probability measure
\alpha
satisfy the identities

(\nabla v\ast\gamma)(\alpha)=\mu\qquad\textnormal{ and }\qquad\nabla v(\alpha\ast\gamma)=\nu,

(6.1)

which we summarize in the following graphic:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\nu}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\alpha}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mu}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\nabla v}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\ast}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\nabla v\ast\gamma}

(iii)

We also remark (see
[
5
]
) that we have

M_{t}=(\nabla v\ast\gamma^{1-t})(B_{t})\quad\textnormal{ and }\quad\pi^{\textnormal{SBM}}=\operatorname{Law}\big((\nabla v\ast\gamma)(B_{0}),\nabla v(B_{1})\big).

(6.2)

The next result, Theorem
6.6
below, explains how the existence of a dual optimizer
\psi_{\textnormal{opt}}
is related to the existence of a Bass martingale. Along with it we need the following technical lemmas, whose proofs we postpone to Appendix
B
.

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a finite convex function such that
\nabla f\in L^{2}(\gamma_{\zeta};\mathbb{R}^{d})
, for some
\zeta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then

(i)

\nabla(f\ast\gamma)=(\nabla f)\ast\gamma
.

If additionally
\operatorname{int}(\operatorname{dom}f^{\ast})\neq\varnothing
, then

(ii)

(f\ast\gamma)^{\ast}
is differentiable and strictly convex on
\operatorname{int}(\operatorname{dom}(f\ast\gamma)^{\ast})\supseteq\operatorname{int}(\operatorname{dom}f^{\ast})
,

(iii)

\nabla(f\ast\gamma)^{\ast}\colon\operatorname{int}(\operatorname{dom}(f\ast\gamma)^{\ast})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\nabla(f\ast\gamma)\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\operatorname{int}(\operatorname{dom}(f\ast\gamma)^{\ast})
are bijections, and we have

(\nabla f\ast\gamma)^{-1}=\nabla(f\ast\gamma)^{\ast}.

Lemma 6.5
.

Let
\psi\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow(-\infty,+\infty]
be a lower semicontinuous convex function and assume that
\varphi^{\psi}(x)>-\infty
, for some
x\in\operatorname{int}(\operatorname{dom}\psi)
. Furthermore, we suppose that
\nabla\psi^{\ast}\in L^{2}(\gamma_{\zeta};\mathbb{R}^{d})
, for some
\zeta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then, for all
\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and all
t>0
, we have

\operatorname{int}(\operatorname{dom}\psi)=\operatorname{int}\big((\partial\psi^{\ast})(\mathbb{R}^{d})\big)=(\nabla\psi^{\ast}\ast\gamma_{\eta}^{t})(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

(6.3)

Theorem 6.6
.

There exists a dual optimizer
\psi_{\textnormal{opt}}
in the sense of Definition
6.1
if and only if there exists a Bass martingale
(M_{t})_{0\leqslant t\leqslant 1}
from
\mu
to
\nu
. In this case,
\operatorname{Law}(M_{0},M_{1})
is equal to the optimizer
\pi^{\textnormal{SBM}}\in\mathsf{MT}(\mu,\nu)
of the primal problem (
1.5
), and
\psi_{\textnormal{opt}},v,\alpha,\pi^{\textnormal{SBM}}
are related via
v^{\ast}=\psi_{\textnormal{opt}}
,
\alpha=\zeta(\mu)
and

\pi^{\textnormal{SBM}}_{x}=\operatorname{Law}(M_{1}\,|\,M_{0}=x)=\nabla v\big(\zeta(x)+\,\cdot\,\big)(\gamma),

(6.4)

where the function
\zeta\colon X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
is given by

\zeta(x)\coloneqq\operatorname{argmax}_{\zeta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Big(\langle\zeta,x\rangle-\int v(\zeta+z)\,d\gamma(z)\Big)

(6.5)

as in (
5.3
), and we have

\zeta(x)=(\nabla v\ast\gamma)^{-1}(x)=\nabla(v\ast\gamma)^{\ast}(x),

(6.6)

for all
x\in X
. The domain
X
of the function
\zeta
satisfies
X\subseteq\operatorname{int}(\operatorname{dom}\psi_{\textnormal{opt}})
and
\mu(X)=1
.

“
\Rightarrow
”: Suppose that a dual optimizer
\psi_{\textnormal{opt}}
exists. Then
\tilde{D}(\mu,\nu)-\mathcal{D}(\psi_{\textnormal{opt}})=0
and from (
3.3
), (
3.12
) we obtain

\int\bigg(\varphi^{\psi_{\textnormal{opt}}}(x)-\Big(\int\psi_{\textnormal{opt}}(y)\,\pi^{\textnormal{SBM}}_{x}(dy)-\textnormal{MCov}(\pi^{\textnormal{SBM}}_{x},\gamma)\Big)\bigg)\,\mu(dx)=0.

Hence, for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the infimum in

\varphi^{\psi_{\textnormal{opt}}}(x)=\inf_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{2}^{x}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\Big(\int\psi_{\textnormal{opt}}\,dp-\textnormal{MCov}(p,\gamma)\Big)

must be attained by
\pi^{\textnormal{SBM}}_{x}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}^{x}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Furthermore, as
\mathcal{D}(\psi_{\textnormal{opt}})
is finite, we deduce as in the proof of Lemma
3.7
that
\int\psi_{\textnormal{opt}}\,d\pi^{\textnormal{SBM}}_{x}<+\infty
, for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Thus also
\varphi^{\psi_{\textnormal{opt}}}(x)
is finite, for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Recalling Lemma
6.2
, we have
\mu(\operatorname{int}(\operatorname{dom}\psi_{\textnormal{opt}}))=1
by Definition
6.1
. In particular,
\psi_{\textnormal{opt}}
is co-finite by Lemma
5.1
. All in all, we can apply part
(ii)
of Proposition
5.3
to
\psi_{\textnormal{opt}}
, for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. This yields that
\pi^{\textnormal{SBM}}_{x}=\nabla v(\zeta(x)+\,\cdot\,)(\gamma)
, with
v\coloneqq\psi_{\textnormal{opt}}^{\ast}
and
\zeta(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
as in (
6.5
). We now define
\alpha\coloneqq\zeta(\mu)
. Since
\pi^{\textnormal{SBM}}
has second marginal equal to
\nu
, we obtain
\nabla v(\alpha\ast\gamma)=\nu
. On the other hand, as
\pi^{\textnormal{SBM}}_{x}
has barycenter
x
, we have

\int\nabla v\big(\zeta(x)+z\big)\,d\gamma(z)=(\nabla v\ast\gamma)\big(\zeta(x)\big)=x.

(6.7)

We denote the set of points
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
for which the identity (
6.7
) holds by
X
and note that
X\subseteq\operatorname{int}(\operatorname{dom}\psi_{\textnormal{opt}})
as well as
\mu(X)=1
. Since
\alpha=\zeta(\mu)
, we conclude from (
6.7
) that
(\nabla v\ast\gamma)(\alpha)=\mu
. By (
6.1
), this establishes the existence of a Bass martingale
(M_{t})_{0\leqslant t\leqslant 1}
from
\mu
to
\nu
, which by construction satisfies
\operatorname{Law}(M_{0},M_{1})=\pi^{\textnormal{SBM}}
, and connects
\psi_{\textnormal{opt}},v,\alpha,\pi^{\textnormal{SBM}}
as claimed.

It remains to check the identity (
6.6
). Since
\pi^{\textnormal{SBM}}_{x}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}^{x}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and as we already established (
6.4
), we conclude that

\int\big|\nabla v\big(\zeta(x)+z\big)\big|^{2}\,d\gamma(z)<+\infty,

(6.8)

for every
x\in X
. In particular, we can apply part
(iii)
of Lemma
6.4
to the function
f=v
. Then from (
6.7
) we derive (
6.6
).

“
\Leftarrow
”: Conversely, suppose that a Bass martingale
M=(M_{t})_{0\leqslant t\leqslant 1}
from
\mu
to
\nu
exists. We denote by
v\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
the associated convex function and define the proper, lower semicontinuous convex function
\psi_{\textnormal{opt}}\coloneqq v^{\ast}
. Recall that
\dim(\operatorname{supp}\nu)=d
by Assumption
3.1
. Since
(\partial v)(\mathbb{R}^{d})\subseteq\operatorname{dom}v^{\ast}
and

\operatorname{int}\operatorname{conv}\big((\partial v)(\mathbb{R}^{d})\big)=\operatorname{int}\overline{\operatorname{conv}}\big((\partial v)(\mathbb{R}^{d})\big)=\operatorname{int}\widehat{\operatorname{supp}}(\nu)\neq\varnothing,

we also have that
\operatorname{int}(\operatorname{dom}v^{\ast})\neq\varnothing
. In the following we show that
\psi_{\textnormal{opt}}
is indeed a dual optimizer in the sense of Definition
6.1
.

First, we verify that
\varphi^{\psi_{\textnormal{opt}}}(x)>-\infty
, for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. As a consequence, we will see that the identity (
6.6
) holds for all
x\in\operatorname{int}(\operatorname{dom}\psi_{\textnormal{opt}})
. Note that from (
6.1
) we have the relation
\nabla v(\alpha\ast\gamma)=\nu
. Since
\nu
has finite second moment, we conclude that

\int|\nabla v(\zeta+z)|^{2}\,d\gamma(z)<+\infty,

(6.9)

for
\alpha
-a.e.
\zeta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In particular, by analogy with (
4.6
), we obtain that

(v\ast\gamma)(\zeta)=\int v(\zeta+z)\,d\gamma(z)\in(-\infty,+\infty),

(6.10)

for
\alpha
-a.e.
\zeta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Recalling the second equality in (
5.5
), we have

\varphi^{\psi_{\textnormal{opt}}}(x)\geqslant(\varphi^{\psi_{\textnormal{opt}}})^{\ast\ast}(x)=\sup_{\zeta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Big(\langle\zeta,x\rangle-\int\psi_{\textnormal{opt}}^{\ast}(\zeta+z)\,d\gamma(z)\Big),

(6.11)

and by (
6.10
), the right-hand side of (
6.11
) is greater than
-\infty
, for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In particular, we can apply Proposition
5.2
to the function
\psi_{\textnormal{opt}}
. As a result, for every
x\in\operatorname{int}(\operatorname{dom}\psi_{\textnormal{opt}})
, we obtain an equality in (
6.11
) and the right-hand side admits a unique maximizer
\zeta(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Differentiating under the integral sign, which is justified by part
(i)
of Lemma
6.4
applied to the function
f=v=\psi_{\textnormal{opt}}^{\ast}
, the first order condition for the optimality of
\zeta(x)
reads

(\nabla\psi_{\textnormal{opt}}^{\ast}\ast\gamma)\big(\zeta(x)\big)=x.

(6.12)

Now we use part
(iii)
of Lemma
6.4
and obtain the identity (
6.6
) from (
6.12
) above, for all
x\in\operatorname{int}(\operatorname{dom}\psi_{\textnormal{opt}})
.

Next, we show that
\alpha=\zeta(\mu)
and
\mu(\operatorname{int}(\operatorname{dom}\psi_{\textnormal{opt}}))=1
. Recalling from (
6.1
) that
(\nabla v\ast\gamma)(\alpha)=\mu
, and using (
6.6
), we get
\alpha=(\nabla v\ast\gamma)^{-1}(\mu)=\zeta(\mu)
. For the second claim, we again use (
6.6
) and obtain

1=\alpha(\mathbb{R}^{d})=\mu\big((\nabla v\ast\gamma)(\mathbb{R}^{d})\big)=\mu(\operatorname{int}(\operatorname{dom}\psi_{\textnormal{opt}})),

where the last equality follows from Lemma
6.5
.

It remains to check that
\tilde{D}(\mu,\nu)=\mathcal{D}(\psi_{\textnormal{opt}})
and
\operatorname{Law}(M_{0},M_{1})=\pi^{\textnormal{SBM}}
. For each
x\in\operatorname{int}(\operatorname{dom}\psi_{\textnormal{opt}})
, we define a probability measure
\pi_{x}^{v}\coloneqq\nabla v(\zeta(x)+\,\cdot\,)(\gamma)
. By (
6.9
) and (
6.12
),
\pi_{x}^{v}
is an element of
\mathcal{P}_{2}^{x}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Hence we can apply part
(i)
of Proposition
5.3
, showing that the infimum

\varphi^{\psi_{\textnormal{opt}}}(x)=\inf_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{2}^{x}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\Big(\int\psi_{\textnormal{opt}}\,dp-\textnormal{MCov}(p,\gamma)\Big)

is attained by
\pi_{x}^{v}
. Therefore, from the representation (
3.12
) of the dual function
\mathcal{D}(\,\cdot\,)
and the fact that
\pi_{x}^{v}(dy)\,\mu(dx)\in\mathsf{MT}(\mu,\nu)
, we obtain

\mathcal{D}(\psi_{\textnormal{opt}})=\int\Big(\int\psi_{\textnormal{opt}}(y)\,\pi^{v}_{x}(dy)-\varphi^{\psi_{\textnormal{opt}}}(x)\Big)\,\mu(dx)=\int\textnormal{MCov}(\pi_{x}^{v},\gamma)\,\mu(dx).

(6.13)

On the other hand, from (
1.5
), Theorem
3.3
and (
3.13
) we have

\int\textnormal{MCov}(\pi_{x}^{v},\gamma)\,\mu(dx)\leqslant P(\mu,\nu)=\tilde{D}(\mu,\nu)\leqslant\mathcal{D}(\psi_{\textnormal{opt}}),

which in light of (
6.13
) implies that
\tilde{D}(\mu,\nu)=\mathcal{D}(\psi_{\textnormal{opt}})
. This completes the proof that
\psi_{\textnormal{opt}}
is a dual optimizer. We still have to show that
\operatorname{Law}(M_{0},M_{1})
equals the optimizer
\pi^{\textnormal{SBM}}
of (
1.5
). Since
M
is a Bass martingale with associated convex function
v
, it follows from the identities (
6.1
) that
\pi_{x}^{v}=\operatorname{Law}(M_{1}\,|\,M_{0}=x)
. But then from (
6.13
) we conclude that
\pi_{x}^{v}=\pi^{\textnormal{SBM}}_{x}
. In particular, this shows (
6.4
).
∎

We now give a one-dimensional example in which a dual optimizer
\psi_{\textnormal{opt}}
in the sense of Definition
6.1
is not integrable with respect to
\nu
. Example
6.7
below illustrates that the form (
3.5
) of the dual function
\mathcal{D}(\,\cdot\,)
may fail to make sense when we allow
\psi
to range more generally than in
C_{\textnormal{q}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. On the other hand, the dual function written in the form (
3.12
) makes perfect sense for general convex functions
\psi\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow(-\infty,+\infty]
, which are
\mu
-a.s. finite (recall Lemma
3.7
). This leads to the satisfactory characterization of dual attainment as given by Theorem
6.6
.

Example 6.7
.

Let
\alpha\coloneqq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}2^{-n}\delta_{z_{n}}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
, with the sequence
(z_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
satisfying
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{|z_{n}|}{2^{n}}=+\infty
. Consider the convex function
v(z)\coloneqq z\arctan z-\frac{1}{2}\log(1+z^{2})
, with the derivative
v^{\prime}(z)=\arctan z
being a strictly increasing, continuous and bounded function. Define
\mu\coloneqq(v^{\prime}\ast\gamma)(\alpha)
, so that
\mu=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}2^{-n}\delta_{x_{n}}
for some bounded sequence
(x_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, and let
\nu\coloneqq v^{\prime}(\alpha\ast\gamma)
. Note that
\mu
and
\nu
are supported by
(-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2})
. Then, according to (
6.1
), the pair
(v,\alpha)
defines a Bass martingale from
\mu
to
\nu
. Theorem
6.6
shows that the derivative of the dual optimizer is given by

\tfrac{d}{dy}\psi_{\textnormal{opt}}(y)=(v^{\prime})^{-1}(y)=\tan y.

By definition of
\nu
and
\alpha
, this function is not
\nu
-integrable, and neither is its antiderivative
\psi_{\textnormal{opt}}
.
\Diamond

We finish this section by deducing a trajectorial property of Bass martingales from Lemma
6.5
and Theorem
6.6
, namely that a Bass martingale
M
with
M_{1}\sim\nu
can only reach the boundary of
\widehat{\operatorname{supp}}(\nu)
at time
1
. In this paper we only make use of this result as a technical step in the proof of Lemma
7.17
. However, this is a natural property to examine from the point of view of stochastic analysis (e.g. Feller’s explosion test is devoted to a related question), and it gives us some intuition about the behavior of Bass martingales.

Corollary 6.8
.

Let
M=(M_{t})_{0\leqslant t\leqslant 1}
be a Bass martingale with
M_{1}\sim\nu
. Define
\tau
as the stopping time

\tau\coloneqq\inf\big\{t\in[0,1]\colon M_{t}\notin\operatorname{ri}\widehat{\operatorname{supp}}(\nu)\big\}\wedge 1,

i.e., the minimum of
1
and the first time that
M
reaches the boundary of
\widehat{\operatorname{supp}}(\nu)
. Then
\tau=1
a.s.

We will prove that

\mathbb{P}\big(t\in[0,1)\colon M_{t}\in\operatorname{ri}\widehat{\operatorname{supp}}(\nu)\big)=1.

Thus, if
M
ever reaches the boundary of
\widehat{\operatorname{supp}}(\nu)
, this will happen at time
t=1
, so
\tau=1
. Otherwise, i.e., if
M
never reaches the boundary of
\widehat{\operatorname{supp}}(\nu)
, then by definition
\tau=1
, too.

Recall that
\dim(\operatorname{supp}\nu)=d
by Assumption
3.1
, so we can replace the relative interior operator by the interior operator throughout the argument. By (
6.2
), the Bass martingale is given by
M_{t}=(\nabla v\ast\gamma^{1-t})(B_{t})
, where
\nabla v(\gamma)=\nu
, for some finite-valued convex function
v
. We now define the proper, lower semicontinuous convex function
\psi\coloneqq v^{\ast}
. As in the proof of the second implication of Theorem
6.6
, namely the part of the proof corresponding to “
\Leftarrow
”, we see that
\operatorname{int}(\operatorname{dom}v^{\ast})\neq\varnothing
, that
\varphi^{\psi}(x)>-\infty
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and that
\nabla\psi^{\ast}\in L^{2}(\gamma_{B_{0}};\mathbb{R}^{d})
a.s. (for the latter, see (
6.9
) and recall that
B_{0}\sim\alpha
). Therefore we can apply Lemma
6.5
and from (
6.3
) we obtain for all
t\in[0,1)
the equality

\operatorname{int}(\operatorname{dom}\psi)=\operatorname{int}\big((\partial\psi^{\ast})(\mathbb{R}^{d})\big)=(\nabla\psi^{\ast}\ast\gamma^{1-t})(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

As we justify in (
B.11
) in Appendix
B
, we automatically have the equality

\operatorname{int}\big((\partial\psi^{\ast})(\mathbb{R}^{d})\big)=\operatorname{int}\operatorname{conv}\big((\partial\psi^{\ast})(\mathbb{R}^{d})\big),

so we conclude that

\operatorname{int}\operatorname{conv}\big((\partial\psi^{\ast})(\mathbb{R}^{d})\big)=(\nabla\psi^{\ast}\ast\gamma^{1-t})(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

On the other hand, we clearly have

\operatorname{int}\operatorname{conv}\big((\partial\psi^{\ast})(\mathbb{R}^{d})\big)=\operatorname{int}\overline{\operatorname{conv}}\big((\partial\psi^{\ast})(\mathbb{R}^{d})\big)=\operatorname{int}\widehat{\operatorname{supp}}(\nu),

so we obtain for all
t\in[0,1)
that

\operatorname{int}\widehat{\operatorname{supp}}(\nu)=(\nabla\psi^{\ast}\ast\gamma^{1-t})(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

Since
M_{t}=(\nabla\psi^{\ast}\ast\gamma^{1-t})(B_{t})
, we conclude as desired that
M_{t}
lies in
\operatorname{int}\widehat{\operatorname{supp}}(\nu)
as long as
t\in[0,1)
.
∎

7
Irreducibility and existence of dual optimizers

7.1
Outline and objectives

In this technically demanding section we connect our findings with the groundbreaking analysis
presented in
[
27
]
by H. De March and N. Touzi. Their work establishes the existence of a
unique maximal paving of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
into relatively open, convex, invariant sets. Each martingale transport from
\mu
to
\nu
maps these relatively open sets into their closures. Our focus here is on the irreducible case, where this paving is trivial, i.e., it consists of a single cell. The general case is treated in the follow-up paper
[
56
]
.

We show that the triviality of the De March–Touzi (DMT) paving (as formalized in Definition
7.2
below) is equivalent to the irreducibility condition in Definition
1.2
. This equivalence, along with additional equivalent characterizations of irreducibility, is established in Theorem
D.1
of Appendix
D
, which builds on the results of the present section.

Assuming the triviality of the DMT paving in Assumption
7.3
below, we fix a martingale transport
\pi^{\textnormal{DMT}}\in\mathsf{MT}(\mu,\nu)
such that every transition kernel
\pi^{\textnormal{DMT}}_{x}
has full convex support. The existence of such a martingale transport
\pi^{\textnormal{DMT}}
was established in
[
27
]
. In Lemma
7.5
, we prove a uniformity property of this full-support feature, valid for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

The main result of this section is Theorem
7.6
, which links the triviality of the DMT paving to the existence of an optimizer for the dual problem (
3.14
). As a direct consequence of this theorem, we obtain that triviality of the DMT paving implies the existence of a Bass martingale
M
from
\mu
to
\nu
with
\operatorname{Law}(M_{0},M_{1})=\pi^{\textnormal{SBM}}
. Notably, this Bass martingale serves as a concrete example of a De March–Touzi transport since, for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the measures
\pi^{\textnormal{DMT}}_{x}
are equivalent to
\nu
.

The first step in proving Theorem
7.6
is to identify a limit
\psi_{\textnormal{lim}}
of an arbitrary optimizing sequence of convex functions
(\psi_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
for the dual problem (
3.1
). A crucial observation is that the value of the dual function (
3.12
) remains invariant under the addition of affine functions to
\psi
. This invariance allows us to choose and add affine functions that facilitate our analysis. Concrete examples of such choices of affine functions are provided in
[
56
]
(Examples 6.1 and 6.2). After appropriately selecting representatives of
\psi_{n}
(by adding affine functions), we establish in Lemma
7.12
(by applying Komlós’ theorem) the existence of a sequence of Cesàro means of a subsequence that is bounded on compact subsets of
I=\operatorname{ri}\widehat{\operatorname{supp}}(\nu)
. Using arguments from convex analysis, we extract a further subsequence that converges uniformly on compact subsets
K\subseteq I
to a convex function
\psi_{\textnormal{lim}}
.

The second step in proving Theorem
7.6
is to verify that the limiting function
\psi_{\textnormal{lim}}
is indeed a dual optimizer. To this end, we introduce in Definition
7.14
the Brenier map
\nabla v_{\textnormal{SBM}}^{x}
from
\gamma
to
\pi^{\textnormal{SBM}}_{x}
. The next goal (see Lemma
7.18
) is to show that, for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in I
, the function
\psi_{\textnormal{lim}}
equals
(v_{\textnormal{SBM}}^{x})^{\ast}
, modulo the addition of an affine function. Specifically, we introduce (see (
7.6
)) the set

A=\big\{x\in I\colon\psi_{\textnormal{lim}}\not\equiv(v_{\textnormal{SBM}}^{x})^{\ast}\textnormal{ mod (aff)}\big\},

and prove that
\mu(A)=0
. This proof proceeds by contradiction. Assuming
\mu(A)>0
, we construct, for
x\in A
, measures
\check{\pi}_{x}\in\mathcal{P}^{2}_{x}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
in Lemma
7.16
, which are adjusted in Lemma
7.17
to have compact support. The measures
\check{\pi}_{x}
then contradict the optimality of
\pi^{\textnormal{SBM}}_{x}
, allowing us to conclude
\mu(A)=0
and thereby complete the proof of Lemma
7.18
. With the help of Lemma
7.19
we can then finish the second step and thus also the proof of Theorem
7.6
. Broadly speaking, Lemma
7.19
shows that
\psi_{\textnormal{lim}}
is a dual optimizer if and only if
\mu(A)=0
. The proof of this result is based on Proposition
5.3
and Theorem
6.6
.

At this point we encounter a remarkable and somewhat surprising feature. In Proposition
7.20
we use the wisdom of hindsight to show that in the arguments outlined above, there is no need of passing to a subsequence or of forming Cesàro means of the optimizing sequence
(\psi_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
. Rather already the original sequence
(\psi_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
— modulo adding affine functions — converges to the optimizer
\psi_{\textnormal{lim}}
. Note that in Proposition
7.20
the pair
(\mu,\nu)
is not necessarily irreducible, rather the boundedness assumption (
7.25
) is made (cf. Lemma
7.12
). This refinement plays a crucial role in the follow-up paper
[
56
]
.

Towards the end of Section
7
we are finally in the position to prove Theorem
1.3
as well as the second part of Theorem
1.4
, thereby completing the main body of the paper (apart from four appendices).

In the following subsections we now proceed with a formal and detailed treatment of the outlined program for Section
7
.

7.2
Irreducibility and dual attainment

We fix
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\mu\preceq_{\textnormal{c}}\nu
. The following notation from
[
27

will be used throughout this section.

Definition 7.1
.

We denote by

the closed convex hull of the support of

its relative interior.

Recall that

Definition 7.2
.

We say that the pair

(i)

(ii)

The main assumption of this section (with the exception of Lemma

and Corollary

Assumption 7.3
.

The pair
(\mu,\nu)
is De March–Touzi irreducible.

Assumption

is equivalent to the irreducibility of the pair

in the sense of Definition

D.1
in Appendix

In Lemma
7.5
below we give a more quantitative description of the defining property of a De March–Touzi transport
\pi^{\textnormal{DMT}}\in\mathsf{MT}(\mu,\nu)
between an irreducible pair
(\mu,\nu)
.

Definition 7.4
.

Let
K\subseteq I
be compact. For an element
y^{\ast}
of the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
we define the slice
S_{y^{\ast}}
of

beyond

S_{y^{\ast}}\coloneqq\big\{y\in C\colon\langle y,y^{\ast}\rangle>\sup\{\langle\tilde{y},y^{\ast}\rangle\colon\tilde{y}\in K\}\big\}.

(7.1)

Lemma 7.5
.

Under Assumption
7.3
, for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and for every compact set
K\subseteq I
, there exists a constant
\delta(K,x)>0
such that
\pi^{\textnormal{DMT}}_{x}(S_{y^{\ast}})\geqslant\delta(K,x)
for every

.

We fix
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
C=\widehat{\operatorname{supp}}(\pi^{\textnormal{DMT}}_{x})
and note that the map
r_{x}\colon\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\rightarrow[0,1]
given by
r_{x}(y^{\ast})\coloneqq\pi^{\textnormal{DMT}}_{x}(S_{y^{\ast}})
, for
y^{\ast}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, is lower semicontinuous. As the set
C
equals the closed convex hull of the support of
\pi^{\textnormal{DMT}}_{x}
, the map

is also strictly positive. Indeed, if there was some

with

, then the closed convex set

would support

, which is in contradiction to

. Since

is compact we conclude that

attains its minimum
\delta(K,x)\coloneqq\min_{y^{\ast}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}r_{x}(y^{\ast})>0
.
∎

The main result of this section is the following.

Together with Theorem

.

7.3
Proof of Theorem

The proof is split into two main steps.

7.3.1
Step 1 of the proof of Theorem

We first construct a

(\psi_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}\subseteq C_{\textnormal{q}}^{\textnormal{aff}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
(see Remark

Proposition 7.8
.

Under Assumption
7.3
, there is an optimizing sequence
(\psi_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
of non-negative convex functions in

for the dual problem (

Before we turn to the proof of Proposition

7.3
, but solely relies on the finiteness of the value
\tilde{D}(\mu,\nu)
of the dual problem (

Lemma 7.9
.

Let
(\psi_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}\subseteq C_{\textnormal{q}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be an optimizing sequence for the dual problem (
3.1
) and take any
\pi\in\mathsf{MT}(\mu,\nu)

\sup_{n\geqslant 1}\int\int\big(\psi_{n}(y)-\psi_{n}(x)\big)\,\pi_{x}(dy)\,\mu(dx)<+\infty.

(7.2)

Recalling (
3.2

n\geqslant 1
the functions

\mathbb{R}^{d}\ni x\longmapsto\varphi^{\psi_{n}}(x)=\inf_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{2}^{x}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\Big(\int\psi_{n}\,dp-\textnormal{MCov}(p,\gamma)\Big).

(7.3)

By taking

in (

and consequently

\int\int\big(\psi_{n}(y)-\psi_{n}(x)\big)\,\pi_{x}(dy)\,\mu(dx)\leqslant\int\int\big(\psi_{n}(y)-\varphi^{\psi_{n}}(x)\big)\,\pi_{x}(dy)\,\mu(dx).

By definition (
3.12
) of the dual function

\mathcal{D}(\psi_{n})=\int\Big(\int\psi_{n}(y)\,\pi_{x}(dy)-\varphi^{\psi_{n}}(x)\Big)\,\mu(dx),

and as the sequence of real numbers

converges to the finite number

∎

Definition 7.10
.

We fix an arbitrary optimizing sequence
(\psi_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}\subseteq C_{\textnormal{q}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
of

so that

and

takes values in

, for every

. Here — by abuse of notation —

at

at

Remark 7.11
.

We observe that passing from

Our goal is to show that — under the irreducibility Assumption

of (

, for
\mu
-a.e.

. This is the content of the following lemma.

Lemma 7.12
.

Under Assumption
7.3
, for any given optimizing sequence
(\psi_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}\subseteq C_{\textnormal{q}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
of convex functions, there is a sequence of Cesàro means of a subsequence, still denoted by

, such that, for
\mu
-a.e.

and for every compact set

, for every choice of
(\psi_{n}^{x})_{n\geqslant 1}\subseteq C_{\textnormal{q}}^{\textnormal{aff}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
as in (

We rely on
[
27
]
and fix a De March–Touzi transport
\pi^{\textnormal{DMT}}\in\mathsf{MT}(\mu,\nu)
satisfying point

7.2

7.3

(\psi_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
and

Proposition 7.13
.

Under Assumption

Definition 7.14
.

For
\mu
-a.e.

the Brenier potential from

to

Remark 7.15
.

As

has full support, the convex function

is finite-valued and continuous everywhere on

. In particular,
v_{\textnormal{SBM}}^{x}
is unique, up to an additive constant. Therefore also its convex conjugate

is finite-valued on

on

, with

and

.

Our goal is to show that
\psi_{\textnormal{lim}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow[0,+\infty]
is a dual optimizer. For this purpose, we will prove that, for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in I
, the function
\psi_{\textnormal{lim}}(\,\cdot\,)
equals
\psi_{\textnormal{SBM}}^{x}(\,\cdot\,)
, modulo adding an affine function; in short,
\psi_{\textnormal{lim}}\equiv\psi_{\textnormal{SBM}}^{x}
mod (aff), for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In Lemma

54
, Theorem 10.9]
we can select a further subsequence, still denoted by
(\psi_{n_{k}})_{k\geqslant 1}
, such that (
C.1
) holds for all
y\in I_{\textnormal{lim}}\coloneqq I
and the convergence is uniform on compact subsets of
I_{\textnormal{lim}}
. Furthermore, condition (
C.2
) is still satisfied for this further subsequence thanks to (
C.4
).

Step 4.
In light of Step 3, in order to prove Lemma
C.1
, it suffices to show the existence of a maximal pair
(I,\mathscr{N})
, which we will denote by
(I_{\textnormal{lim}},\mathscr{N}_{\textnormal{lim}})
. The construction of the maximal pair
(I_{\textnormal{lim}},\mathscr{N}_{\textnormal{lim}})
is done via transfinite recursion along the countable ordinal numbers
\alpha<\omega_{1}
, where
\omega_{1}
is the first uncountable ordinal.

We start the construction as follows. If
\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\psi_{n}(y)=+\infty
holds for all
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the pair
(I_{0},\mathscr{N}_{0})=(\varnothing,\mathbb{N})
is admissible. Otherwise, we can find some
y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\psi_{n}(y_{0})<+\infty
and then the pair
(I_{0},\mathscr{N}_{0})=(\{y_{0}\},\mathbb{N})
is admissible.

Now let
\alpha<\omega_{1}
be an ordinal number and suppose that the transfinite family
(I_{\beta},\mathscr{N}_{\beta})_{\beta<\alpha}
in
\mathcal{I}\times\mathcal{N}
is such that

(i)

the pair
(I_{\beta},\mathscr{N}_{\beta})
is admissible, for all
\beta<\alpha
,

(ii)

the pair
(I_{\beta_{2}},\mathscr{N}_{\beta_{2}})
is finer than
(I_{\beta_{1}},\mathscr{N}_{\beta_{1}})
, for all
\beta_{1}<\beta_{2}<\alpha
.

We first assume that
\alpha
is a successor ordinal, i.e., there is an ordinal
\beta
such that
\alpha=\beta+1
. Since
(I_{\beta},\mathscr{N}_{\beta})
is admissible, by Step 2 only two cases are possible. If
(I_{\beta},\mathscr{N}_{\beta})
is maximal, we set
(I_{\textnormal{lim}},\mathscr{N}_{\textnormal{lim}})\coloneqq(I_{\beta},\mathscr{N}_{\beta})
and finish the construction. If
(I_{\beta},\mathscr{N}_{\beta})
is refinable, we can find a pair
(I_{\alpha},\mathscr{N}_{\alpha})
which is finer than
(I_{\beta},\mathscr{N}_{\beta})
.

Suppose that
\alpha
is a limit ordinal, i.e., it can be written as
\alpha=\{\beta\colon\beta<\alpha\}
. Since
\alpha<\omega_{1}
, the ordinal
\alpha
is countable and can be enumerated as
\alpha=\{\alpha_{k}\colon k<\omega\}
, where
\omega
is the first infinite ordinal. Letting
\beta_{0}\coloneqq 0
and defining inductively
\beta_{m+1}\coloneqq\alpha_{\ell}
, where
\ell\coloneqq\min\{k\colon\beta_{m}<\alpha_{k}\}
, we obtain a strictly increasing cofinal sequence
(\beta_{m})_{m<\omega}
in
\alpha
. Now we define
I_{\alpha}\coloneqq\bigcup_{m<\omega}I_{\beta_{m}}
. By
(ii)
,
(I_{\beta_{m}})_{m<\omega}
is a strictly increasing sequence of relatively open convex sets, therefore also
I_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{I}
. Again by
(ii)
, the sequence
(\mathscr{N}_{\beta_{m}})_{m<\omega}
satisfies
\mathscr{N}_{\beta_{m_{1}}}\succcurlyeq\mathscr{N}_{\beta_{m_{2}}}
, for all
m_{1}<m_{2}<\omega
. Thus we can find a diagonal subsequence
\mathscr{N}_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{N}
of
(\mathscr{N}_{\beta_{m}})_{m<\omega}
, meaning that
\mathscr{N}_{\alpha}\succcurlyeq\mathscr{N}_{\beta_{m}}
, for all
m<\omega
. Since by
(i)
the pair
(I_{\beta_{m}},\mathscr{N}_{\beta_{m}})
is admissible, for all
m<\omega
, by construction also
(I_{\alpha},\mathscr{N}_{\alpha})
is admissible. Furthermore,
(I_{\alpha},\mathscr{N}_{\alpha})
satisfies
(I_{\alpha},\mathscr{N}_{\alpha})\succcurlyeq(I_{\beta_{m}},\mathscr{N}_{\beta_{m}})
, for all
m<\omega
. Since
(\beta_{m})_{m<\omega}
is cofinal, it follows that
(I_{\alpha},\mathscr{N}_{\alpha})\succcurlyeq(I_{\beta},\mathscr{N}_{\beta})
, for all
\beta<\alpha
.

After countably many steps, this transfinite recursion must stop, which happens when
\alpha<\omega_{1}
is such that
(I_{\alpha},\mathscr{N}_{\alpha})
is maximal and we can then set
(I_{\textnormal{lim}},\mathscr{N}_{\textnormal{lim}})\coloneqq(I_{\alpha},\mathscr{N}_{\alpha})
. Indeed, otherwise we would have a transfinite sequence
(I_{\beta})_{\beta<\omega_{1}}
of strictly increasing relatively open convex sets, indexed by the countable ordinals, which is impossible.
∎

Step 1.
By Lemma
C.1
, there is a closed convex set
C_{\textnormal{lim}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
with relative interior
I_{\textnormal{lim}}
and a subsequence
(\psi_{n_{k}})_{k\geqslant 1}
such that the limits

\displaystyle\psi_{\textnormal{lim}}(y)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\psi_{n_{k}}(y)<+\infty,\qquad

\displaystyle y\in I_{\textnormal{lim}}

(C.5)

\displaystyle\psi_{\textnormal{lim}}(y)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\psi_{n_{k}}(y)=+\infty,\qquad

\displaystyle y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus C_{\textnormal{lim}}

(C.6)

exist, where the convergence in (
C.5
) is uniform on compact subsets of
I_{\textnormal{lim}}
. Via (
C.5
) and (
C.6
) we define a lower semicontinuous convex function
\psi_{\textnormal{lim}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow[0,+\infty]
on all of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Step 2.
By the hypothesis (
7.25
) and the construction of
I_{\textnormal{lim}}
, we clearly have
I\subseteq I_{\textnormal{lim}}
. Our goal is to show that
I_{\textnormal{lim}}=I
. To this end, we define the set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
as in (
7.6
), now with
I
replaced by
I_{\textnormal{lim}}
, i.e.,

A=\big\{x\in I_{\textnormal{lim}}\colon\psi_{\textnormal{lim}}\not\equiv\psi_{\textnormal{SBM}}^{x}\textnormal{ mod (aff)}\big\}.

Again, we will show that
\mu(A)=0
. Repeating the reasoning of Lemma
7.16
we obtain, for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in A
, measures
\check{\pi}_{x}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}^{x}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfying (
7.7
), which now are supported by
C_{\textnormal{lim}}
. Then we choose an increasing sequence
(K_{j})_{j\geqslant 1}
of relatively compact subsets of
I_{\textnormal{lim}}
such that
\bigcup_{j\geqslant 1}K_{j}=I_{\textnormal{lim}}
. As in Lemma
7.17
, for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in A
, we find measures
\check{\pi}_{x}^{j(x)}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}^{x}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfying (
7.13
) and supported by
K_{j(x)}
, for some
j(x)\in\mathbb{N}
. Arguing as in Lemma
7.18
we conclude that
\mu(A)=0
, i.e.,
\psi_{\textnormal{lim}}\equiv\psi_{\textnormal{SBM}}^{x}
mod (aff), for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in I_{\textnormal{lim}}
. In particular, since
I_{\textnormal{lim}}=\operatorname{ri}(\operatorname{dom}\psi_{\textnormal{lim}})
and
\operatorname{ri}(\operatorname{dom}\psi_{\textnormal{SBM}}^{x})=\operatorname{ri}\widehat{\operatorname{supp}}(\nu)
, we conclude from the assumption
\widehat{\operatorname{supp}}(\nu)\subseteq C
that
I_{\textnormal{lim}}=I
and consequently
C=\widehat{\operatorname{supp}}(\nu)
. From Lemma
7.19
, we conclude that the limiting function
\psi_{\textnormal{lim}}
is a dual optimizer. Together with Step 1, this proves the statement of Proposition
7.20
for the subsequence
(\psi_{n_{k}})_{k\geqslant 1}
.

Step 3.
Applying Step 1 and Step 2 to an arbitrary subsequence
(\psi_{m_{\ell}})_{\ell\geqslant 1}
instead of the original sequence
(\psi_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
, we obtain a further subsequence
(\psi_{m_{\ell_{j}}})_{j\geqslant 1}
and a lower semicontinuous convex function
\tilde{\psi}_{\textnormal{lim}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow[0,+\infty]
such that

\displaystyle\forall y\in I\colon\ \tilde{\psi}_{\textnormal{lim}}(y)

\displaystyle=\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}\psi_{m_{\ell_{j}}}(y)<+\infty,

\displaystyle\forall y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus C\colon\ \tilde{\psi}_{\textnormal{lim}}(y)

\displaystyle=\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}\psi_{m_{\ell_{j}}}(y)=+\infty,

and
\tilde{\psi}_{\textnormal{lim}}\equiv\psi_{\textnormal{SBM}}^{x}
mod (aff), for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in I
. We conclude that there is an affine function
aff
depending on the subsequence
({m_{\ell_{j}}})_{j\geqslant 1}
such that
\psi_{\textnormal{lim}}=\tilde{\psi}_{\textnormal{lim}}+\textnormal{aff}
. In particular,

\forall y\in I\cup(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus C)\colon\ \lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}\big(\psi_{m_{\ell_{j}}}(y)+\textnormal{aff}(y)\big)=\psi_{\textnormal{lim}}(y).

(C.7)

Step 4.
Let
x_{0},\ldots,x_{d}
be affinely independent points in
I\cup(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus C)
. For each
n\geqslant 1
there is a unique affine function
\textnormal{aff}_{n}
such that

\forall i\in\{0,\ldots,d\}\colon\ \psi_{n}(x_{i})+\textnormal{aff}_{n}(x_{i})=\psi_{\textnormal{lim}}(x_{i}).

(C.8)

We claim that

\forall y\in I\cup(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus C)\colon\ \lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\big(\psi_{n}(y)+\textnormal{aff}_{n}(y)\big)=\psi_{\textnormal{lim}}(y).

(C.9)

Indeed, if this were not the case, there would be some
y_{0}\in I\cup(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus C)
and a subsequence
(m_{\ell})_{\ell\geqslant 1}
such that

\lim_{\ell\rightarrow\infty}\big(\psi_{m_{\ell}}(y_{0})+\textnormal{aff}_{m_{\ell}}(y_{0})\big)\neq\psi_{\textnormal{lim}}(y_{0}).

(C.10)

By Step 3 there is a further subsequence
(m_{\ell_{j}})_{j\geqslant 1}
and an affine function
aff
such that (
C.7
) holds. In particular,

\displaystyle\forall i\in\{0,\ldots,d\}\colon\ \lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}\big(\psi_{m_{\ell_{j}}}(x_{i})+\textnormal{aff}(x_{i})\big)

\displaystyle=\psi_{\textnormal{lim}}(x_{i}),

(C.11)

\displaystyle\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}\big(\psi_{m_{\ell_{j}}}(y_{0})+\textnormal{aff}(y_{0})\big)

\displaystyle=\psi_{\textnormal{lim}}(y_{0}).

(C.12)

From (
C.8
) and (
C.11
) we obtain that

\forall i\in\{0,\ldots,d\}\colon\ \lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}\textnormal{aff}_{m_{\ell_{j}}}(x_{i})=\textnormal{aff}(x_{i}).

(C.13)

Since the points
x_{0},\ldots,x_{d}
are affinely independent, the convergence in (
C.13
) holds for every
y\in I\cup(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus C)
. Then (
C.12
) leads to a contradiction to (
C.10
), which proves claim (
C.9
). Defining
\tilde{\psi}_{n}\coloneqq\psi_{n}+\textnormal{aff}_{n}
, for
n\geqslant 1
, we derive from (
C.9
) the limiting assertions (
7.26
) and (
7.27
). Finally, by
[
54
, Theorem 10.8]
the convergence in (
7.26
) is uniform on compact subsets of
I
and we have already seen in Step 2 that
\psi_{\textnormal{lim}}\equiv\psi_{\textnormal{SBM}}^{x}
mod (aff), for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in I
.
∎

D
Characterization of irreducibility

Theorem D.1
.

Let
\mu,\nu
in
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\mu\preceq_{\textnormal{c}}\nu
. Then the following are equivalent.

(1)

The pair
(\mu,\nu)
is De March–Touzi irreducible in the sense of Definition
7.2
.

(2)

There exists
\pi\in\mathsf{MT}(\mu,\nu)
such that
\pi_{x}\sim\nu
, for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

(3)

The pair
(\mu,\nu)
is irreducible in the sense of Definition
1.2
, i.e., for all Borel sets
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\mu(A),\nu(B)>0
there is a martingale
(X_{t})_{0\leqslant t\leqslant 1}
with
X_{0}\sim\mu
,
X_{1}\sim\nu
such that
\mathbb{P}(X_{0}\in A,X_{1}\in B)>0
.

(4)

For all Borel sets
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\mu(A),\nu(B)>0
there exists
\pi\in\mathsf{MT}(\mu,\nu)
such that
\pi(A\times B)>0
.

(5)

For all compact sets
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and open halfspaces
H
with
\mu(A),\nu(H)>0
there exists
\pi\in\mathsf{MT}(\mu,\nu)
such that
\pi(A\times H)>0
.

For the proof of “
(1)

\Rightarrow

(2)
” we can take
\pi=\pi^{\textnormal{SBM}}
in
(2)
by Corollary
7.7
. We turn to the proof of the implication “
(2)

\Rightarrow

(3)
”: Clearly condition
(2)
implies
(4)
. Thus, in order to show
(3)
, it suffices to construct a continuous-time martingale
(X_{t})_{0\leqslant t\leqslant 1}
with
\operatorname{Law}(X_{0},X_{1})=\pi
. This can be achieved as in the proof of
[
5
, Theorem 2.2]
. For the proof of “
(3)

\Rightarrow

(4)
” we can take
\pi=\operatorname{Law}(X_{0},X_{1})
in
(4)
. The implication “
(4)

\Rightarrow

(5)
” is trivial. It remains to show that “
(5)

\Rightarrow

(1)
”: We fix an open halfspace
H
satisfying
\nu(H)>0
and set

m\coloneqq\sup_{\pi\in\mathsf{MT}(\mu,\nu)}\Big\{\mu\big(\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\colon\pi_{x}(H)>0\}\big)\Big\}.

Considering countable convex combinations of elements of
\mathsf{MT}(\mu,\nu)
it follows that the supremum is attained by some
\bar{\pi}\in\mathsf{MT}(\mu,\nu)
. If the set

\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\colon\bar{\pi}_{x}(H)=0\}

had positive
\mu
-measure, it would contain a compact set
A
with positive
\mu
-measure in contradiction to condition
(5)
. Hence
m=1
.

Observe that there is a countable family of open halfspaces
\{H_{n}\}_{n\geqslant 1}
with
\nu(H_{n})>0
such that, for every open halfspace
H
with
\nu(H)>0
, there is some
n\geqslant 1
satisfying
H_{n}\subseteq H
.

Next, for each
n\geqslant 1
, we pick
\pi^{(n)}\in\mathsf{MT}(\mu,\nu)
such that
\pi^{(n)}_{x}(H_{n})>0
, for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Set
\hat{\pi}\coloneqq\sum_{n\geqslant 1}2^{-n}\pi^{(n)}\in\mathsf{MT}(\mu,\nu)
. Then by Lemma
D.2
below it follows that
\widehat{\operatorname{supp}}(\nu)\subseteq\widehat{\operatorname{supp}}(\hat{\pi}_{x})
, for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
∎

Lemma D.2
.

Let
\rho\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Then
y\in\widehat{\operatorname{supp}}(\rho)
if and only if
\rho(H)>0
for every open halfspace
H
such that
y\in H
.

This is a simple consequence of Hahn–Banach.
∎

Remark D.3
.

We note that irreducibility is not only a sufficient assumption for the existence of a Bass martingale from
\mu
to
\nu
, but in fact also necessary. Indeed, as in the proof of Corollary
7.7
one sees that the Bass martingale does the job of connecting any two sets which are charged by
\mu
and
\nu
, implying the irreducibility of
(\mu,\nu)
by Theorem
D.1
.
