Abstract
. We reveal a complexity chasm, separating the trinomial and tetranomial cases, for solving univariate sparse polynomial equations over certain local fields. First, for any fixed field K ∈ { ℚ 2 , ℚ 3 , ℚ 5 , … } K\!\in\!\{\mathbb{Q}_{2},\mathbb{Q}_{3},\mathbb{Q}_{5},\ldots\} , we prove that any polynomial f ∈ ℤ ⁡ [ x ] f\!\in\!\mathbb{Z}[x] with exactly 3 3 monomial terms, degree d d , and all coefficients having absolute value at most H H , can be solved over K K within deterministic time log O ⁡ ( 1 ) ⁡ ( d ​ H ) \log^{O(1)}(dH) in the classical Turing model. (The best previous algorithms were of complexity exponential in log ⁡ d \log d , even for just counting roots in ℚ p \mathbb{Q}_{p} .) In particular, our algorithm generates approximations in ℚ \mathbb{Q} with bit-length log O ⁡ ( 1 ) ⁡ ( d ​ H ) \log^{O(1)}(dH) to all the roots of f f in K K , and these approximations converge quadratically under Newton iteration. On the other hand, we give a unified family of tetra nomials requiring Ω ⁡ ( d ​ log ⁡ H ) \Omega(d\log H) digits to distinguish the base- p p expansions of their roots in K K .

1.1.
Why is the Field Fixed?

Much as real algebraic geometry fixes the underlying field to

K\!=\!\mathbb{R}
once and for all, our results focus on

K\!=\!\mathbb{Q}_{p}
with
p
fixed once and for all. In particular, while there are

(\log p)^{O(1)}
on an input prime

sparse polynomial equations in one variable
over
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
appears to have much larger complexity as a function of

There are naive reasons, and subtle reasons, for this:

R1.

Whereas a binomial has at most
3

\mathbb{R}
(e.g.,

x^{3}-x
), a binomial can have as many as
p
roots in
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
(e.g.,

x^{p}-x
). Furthermore, trinomials have at most
5
,
7
,
9
, or

3p-2

roots in
K
, according as

is
\mathbb{R}
,
\mathbb{Q}_{2}

[
19
]
,

\mathbb{Q}_{3}

[
36
]
, or
\mathbb{Q}_{p}

[
4
]
, and each

R2.

Approximating square-roots of
p
-adic integers not divisible
by

, is equivalent to finding square-roots in

\mathbb{F}_{p}

[

,

]

In particular, even if one only wants to approximate just one or two

Algorithm 2.17

(a_{3}-a_{2})
-th term of the sum exactly

\operatorname{ord}_{p}(T-\zeta^{a_{3}-a_{2}})\!=\!\operatorname{ord}_{p}(T-1)
,
i.e., the third summand from (1).

Suppose
\operatorname{ord}_{p}T\!<\!0
. Then for each
i\!\in\!\{1,\ldots,a_{3}-a_{2}\}
, the
Ultrametric Inequality gives us
\operatorname{ord}_{p}(T-\zeta^{j})=\operatorname{ord}_{p}T<0
, since roots
of unity always have
p
-adic valuation
0
. We must then have

\operatorname{ord}_{p}f(m)=\operatorname{ord}_{p}(c_{1})+\operatorname{ord}_{p}(T-\zeta^{a_{3}-a_{2}})<\frac{\log(dH)}{\log p}
and we obtain our lemma.

On the other hand, should
\operatorname{ord}_{p}T\geq 0
, we get

\operatorname{ord}_{p}(T-\zeta^{j})\geq j\operatorname{ord}_{p}(\zeta)=0
, for each
j
, by the Ultrametric
Inequality. So
M\!\geq\!\operatorname{ord}_{p}(T-1)
and we’ll be done if we find a
sufficiently good upper bound on
M
.

By luck,
M
is boundable directly from Theorem
3.1
, upon setting

n\!=\!2
,
\alpha_{1}\!=\!-c_{2}\frac{a_{3}-a_{2}}{c_{1}a_{3}}
,

\alpha_{2}\!=\!-\frac{a_{2}c_{2}}{a_{3}c_{3}}
,
b_{1}\!=\!a_{3}-a_{2}
, and
b_{2}\!=\!a_{2}
. In particular,
we can use
A_{i}=\max\{dH,p\}
for
i\!\in\!\{1,2\}
and
B=\max\{d,3\}
,
yielding
\log A_{1},\log A_{2},\log B=O(\max\{\log(dH),\log p\})
, so that

M\!\leq\!Cp\log^{2}\max\{dH,p\}\log(4\max\{d,3\})\left.\times\max\left\{\log\left(18\cdot 2^{12}\log\max\{dH,p\}\right),\frac{\log p}{2}\right\}\right/\log^{4}p

for
C\!=\!11145\cdot 216^{4}
. So then

M\!=\!O\!\left(\frac{p}{\log^{4}p}\log^{2}(dH+p)(\log d)(\log\log(dH+p)+\log p)\right)

=O\!\left(\frac{p}{\log^{4}p}\log(d)\log^{2}(dH+p)\log\log(dH+p)\log p\right)

=O\!\left(\frac{p}{\log^{3}p}\log(d)\log^{2}(dH+p)\log\log(dH+p)\right)
.

In other words, the third summand from (1) is bounded from above by
the last
O
-bound,

and thus
\operatorname{ord}_{p}f(m)\!=\!O(M)
since
\frac{\log H}{\log p}\!=\!O(M)
. Since
|f(m)|_{p}\!=\!e^{-\log(p)\operatorname{ord}_{p}f(m)}
,
we are done.
\blacksquare

Theorem 5.6

[

Suppose

.
\blacksquare

We point out that the vector of parameters

:
First note that replacing

in

Note that the lower hull of any Newton polygon can be identified with a

\operatorname{Newt}_{p}(f_{1,1})

. In particular, if

5.6
then
tells us that all lower edges of

, where

is the largest

(
\star
)

,

Lemma 5.8
.

For any trinomial

with
\tilde{f}(0)\!\neq\!0

,

.

Proof:
By Lemma
2.13
,

\mathcal{T}_{p,k}(f)
has depth
\leq\!\left\lfloor\frac{k-1}{2}\right\rfloor
.
By Lemma
5.2
, all non-root nodal polynomials have mod

reduction
of degree no greater than

. Thus, the root of
\mathcal{T}_{p,k}(f)
has at most

p-1
children (since
\tilde{f}(0)\!\neq\!0
), and any node at depth
\geq\!1
has
no more than
2
children (since a degree
4
polynomial has at most
2

degenerate roots). Lemma
2.13
also tells us that

is at most the multiplicity of

\zeta_{i-1}\!\in\!\mathbb{F}^{*}_{p}
as a root of
\tilde{f}_{i-1,\mu}
. So any node
v

that has an ancestor at level
\geq\!1
with
2

no more than
1
child. Thus, there can be no more than
2(p-1)

nodes at depth

. It is then clear that

has at most

in the monomial term expansion of

mod

mod

. Since

[

Proof of Theorem
1.1

First note that

The height bound for our approximate roots from Assertion (1)

Assertion (2) follows easily from Theorem

Assertion (3) on correctly counting the roots of

Correctness:
Thanks to Theorem
2.3

guarantees that
f

necessary condition for their to be roots in
\mathbb{Q}_{p}

Step 2 merely involves simple substitutions that only negligibly affect

Steps 3 and 4 correctly count the number of non-degenerate roots
of

\mathbb{Z}_{p}

0

For Step 5, since
0
is not a root, we can
rearrange the equations

that
\zeta\!\in\!\mathbb{Q}^{*}_{p}

[c_{1},c_{2}\zeta^{a_{2}},c_{3}\zeta^{a_{3}}]^{T}
is a right null-vector for

0&a_{2}&a_{3}\end{bmatrix}$}}

The latter equation can then be solved easily, within our overall time

2.17

while the coefficient heights look much larger, any root

\zeta

original

thus implying
\zeta

Step 6 merely takes care of the remaining roots, at
negligible affect to the coefficient heights.

Note that we do need to renormalize the roots at the end, due to
the various rescalings, but this adds a neglible summand of
O(\log H)

to the logarithmic heights of the roots. So we are done.
\blacksquare
