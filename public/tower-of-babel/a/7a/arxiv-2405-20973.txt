LCQ: Low-Rank Codebook based Quantization for Large Language Models
Wen-Pu Cai, Ming-Yang Li, Wu-Jun Li

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) have recently demonstrated promising performance in many tasks. However, the high storage and computational cost of LLMs has become a challenge for deploying LLMs. Weight quantization has been widely used for model compression, which can reduce both storage and computational cost. Most existing weight quantization methods for LLMs use a rank-one codebook for quantization, which results in substantial accuracy loss when the compression ratio is high. In this paper, we propose a novel weight quantization method, called l ow-rank c odebook based q uantization (LCQ), for LLMs. LCQ adopts a low-rank codebook, the rank of which can be larger than one, for quantization. Experiments show that LCQ can achieve better accuracy than existing methods with a negligibly extra storage cost.

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs), such as GPT
(
Brown et al. 2020
)
, GLM
(
Zeng et al. 2023
)
, LLaMA
(
Touvron et al. 2023
)
and LLaVA
(
Liu et al. 2022a
)
, have recently demonstrated promising performance in many tasks including both understanding and generation of natural language, image, video and speech. However, as these models grow in size, their parameter counts also increase dramatically. For instance, the number of parameters in GPT
(
Brown et al. 2020
)
can reach up to 1.8 trillion. A large number of parameters will result in high storage and computational cost, which has become a challenge for deploying LLMs in resource-constrained devices such as mobile phones or edge devices.

Weight quantization
(
Lin et al. 2024
;
Frantar et al. 2023
;
Dettmers et al. 2023
;
Xu et al. 2024
)
, which represents (quantizes) each high bit-width weight by a low bit-width value, has been widely used for model compression. Weight quantization can reduce both storage and computational cost for model inference
(
Frantar et al. 2023
)
, and hence can facilitate model deployment in real applications. For example, quantizing a model from 16-bit floating-point precision to 4-bit integers can reduce the model size by a factor of four, enabling deployment on devices with limited memory.

Despite the benefits, naive weight quantization will typically cause accuracy degradation, especially for cases when the compression ratio is high, i.e., the bit-width after quantization is too low. Existing weight quantization methods for LLMs have proposed different techniques to mitigate the accuracy degradation caused by quantization. For example,
GPTQ
(
Frantar et al. 2023
)
uses output reconstruction error rather than simple parameter reconstruction error for quantization. GPTQ
(
Frantar et al. 2023
)
primarily proposes a learning method to compensate for quantization residuals and does not perform additional optimizations for the quantization codebook.
AWQ
(
Lin et al. 2024
)
and OmniQuant
(
Shao et al. 2024
)
mainly focus on learning the quantization codebook, further reducing the performance loss of the quantized model.
Although existing weight quantization methods can achieve accuracy comparable to the original model with 4-bit or larger bit-width quantization,
there remains a significant accuracy degradation for quantization when the bit-width is lower than 4.

One common characteristic of most existing weight quantization methods for LLMs is that they use a rank-one codebook for quantization.
Specifically, the quantization codebook for weights can be seen as the outer product of a scaling vector
(
Lin et al. 2024
;
Frantar et al. 2023
;
Dettmers et al. 2023
)
and a quantization point set (QPS) vector
(
Oh et al. 2022
)
.
Each element in the scaling vector corresponds to a scaling factor for a group of weights and the QPS vector is constructed from quantiles of uniform distribution
(
Frantar et al. 2023
;
Lin et al. 2024
)
or Gaussian distribution
(
Dettmers et al. 2023
)
.
Although the rank-one codebook can reduce the storage cost of the model, it has limited representation ability. This might be one of the main reasons to explain the significant accuracy degradation for existing quantization methods when the bit-width is lower than 4.

In this paper, we propose a weight quantization method, called
l
ow-rank
c
odebook based
q
uantization (LCQ), for LLMs. The main contributions of LCQ are outlined as follows:

•

LCQ adopts a low-rank codebook, the rank of which can be larger than one, for quantization.

•

In LCQ, a gradient-based optimization algorithm is proposed to optimize the parameters of the codebook.

•

LCQ adopts a double quantization strategy for compressing the parameters of the codebook, which can reduce the storage cost of the codebook.

•

Experiments show that LCQ can achieve better accuracy than existing methods with a negligibly extra storage cost.

2
Related Work

Existing weight quantization methods for LLMs typically employ post-training quantization (PTQ) which is relatively fast and does not require access to the full original training data
(
Frantar et al. 2023
;
Lin et al. 2024
;
Dettmers et al. 2023
)
.
PTQ methods such as BRECQ
(
Li et al. 2021
)
, OBC
(
Frantar & Alistarh 2022
)
and QDrop
(
Wei et al. 2022
)
are designed for quantizing traditional non-LLMs, which cannot be directly used for LLMs due to their high optimization cost.
Recently, some PTQ methods have been specially designed for LLM quantization.
GPTQ
(
Frantar et al. 2023
)
primarily proposes a learning method to compensate for quantization residuals and uses output reconstruction error as the objective function.
However, GPTQ does not perform optimization for the quantization codebook, which results in significant accuracy loss with low bit-width.
AWQ
(
Lin et al. 2024
)
and OmniQuant
(
Shao et al. 2024
)
mainly focus on learning the quantization codebook, and
the quantization indices are directly computed based on the index of the quantization value that is nearest to the full-precision weight.
The codebooks of AWQ and OmniQuant are rank-one, limiting the representation ability. Very recently, there appear some PTQ methods like OWQ
(
Lee et al. 2024
)
, SpQR
(
Dettmers et al. 2024
)
and SquzzeLLM
(
Kim et al. 2024
)
, which mainly address the issue of outliers in activations by
assigning higher bit-widths to the channels/connections of activation outliers.
Our LCQ is orthogonal to methods like OWQ, SpQR and SqueezeLLM, and can be further integrated with these methods.

Moreover, some LLMs compression methods
(
Dettmers et al. 2022
;
Xiao et al. 2023
;
Yuan et al. 2023
;
Wei et al. 2023
)
integrate weight quantization and activation quantization for further accelerating LLMs inference. However, the accuracy drops significantly when quantizing both weights and activations.

PTQ methods have also been integrated with parameter-efficient fine tuning (PEFT). Representative methods include QLoRA
(
Dettmers et al. 2023
)
, PEQA
(
Kim et al. 2023
)
, QA-LoRA
(
Xu et al. 2024
)
, and LoftQ
(
Li et al. 2024
)
. But the main goal of these methods is for PEFT rather than directly quantizing the original LLMs.
Our LCQ can also be used for PEFT, but this is not the focus of this paper.

3
Methodology

As in existing weight quantization methods for LLMs
(
Frantar et al. 2023
;
Lin et al. 2024
;
Shao et al. 2024
)
, we also employ post-training quantization in our LCQ.
In post-training quantization, we have a calibration dataset, which is denoted as
\{\mathbf{X}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{X}}
.
Here, each calibration sample
\mathbf{X}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times D}
has a sequence length of
L
and feature dimension of
D
,
and
N_{X}
is the total number of calibration samples. As in AWQ
(
Lin et al. 2024
)
and OmniQuant
(
Shao et al. 2024
)
, we perform grouped quantization for the weights of linear layers.
More specifically, for a linear layer with
N_{W}
weights, we first divide all the weights into
N_{V}
subsets, with each subset having
N_{W}/N_{V}
weights. Then for each subset of weights, we group its weights into
N_{G}
groups.
We denote each subset of weights as
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{G}\times G}
, in which
G
denotes the weight group size and
N_{G}=\frac{N_{W}}{N_{V}\times G}
denotes the number of weight groups per subset.
A quantization codebook containing quantization values is maintained for each group of weights.
For weight quantization with
b
bits, the number of all quantization values of each group of weights is
N_{Q}=2^{b}
. We denote the quantization codebook of all groups as
\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{G}\times N_{Q}}
, the
i
th row of which is the codebook corresponding to the
i
th group of weights.
The quantization function operates on the weights in an element-wise way, which quantizes each weight to the nearest quantization value in the codebook. Let
\mathbf{Z}\in[0,1,\cdots,N_{Q}]^{N_{G}\times G}
denote the quantization index of
\mathbf{W}
, in which
Z_{i,j}
denotes the quantization index of
W_{i,j}
. We have

\displaystyle Z_{i,j}=\mathop{\arg\min}\limits_{1\leq k\leq N_{Q}}||W_{i,j}-C_{i,k}||.

(1)

The quantization function
Q(\cdot)
can be defined as follows:

\displaystyle Q(W_{i,j},\mathbf{C}_{i})=C_{i,Z_{i,j}}.

(2)

Here,
\mathbf{C}_{i}
denotes the
i
th row of
\mathbf{C}
.
\mathbf{W}^{Q}=Q(\mathbf{W},\mathbf{C})
is the quantized weight matrix with
W_{i,j}^{Q}=Q(W_{i,j},\mathbf{C}_{i})
.

Then we can use the quantized weight matrix
\mathbf{W}^{Q}
for inference. In real deployment, we only need to store the codebook matrix
\mathbf{C}
and the quantization index matrix
\mathbf{Z}

(
Han et al. 2016
)
. From (
1
), we can find that
\mathbf{Z}
is computed based on
\mathbf{C}
.
Hence,
\mathbf{C}
is the key for quantization, which will affect accuracy, storage cost and computational cost.

3.1
Low-Rank Codebook

In AWQ
(
Lin et al. 2024
)
and GPTQ
(
Frantar et al. 2023
)
, the intervals between quantization values are equal, and hence it is not necessary to explicitly store the quantization codebook
\mathbf{C}
.
More specifically,
\mathbf{C}
can be represented by two vectors, a scaling vector
\mathbf{S}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times N_{G}}
and a fixed quantization point set (QPS) vector
\mathbf{V}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times N_{Q}}
.
\mathbf{V}_{1}=[-1,-1+\frac{2}{2^{b}-1},\cdots,1-\frac{2}{2^{b}-1},1]
is a uniformly spaced vector between
[-1,1]
.

\mathbf{S}_{1}
can be adaptively obtained based on the weight matrix
\mathbf{W}
.
The codebook is then obtained by computing the outer product of
\mathbf{S}_{1}
and
\mathbf{V}_{1}
.
Furthermore, to adapt to asymmetric weight distribution, a quantization offset
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{G}\times 1}
is introduced, with each group of weights having its own quantization offset.
The final quantization codebook can be formulated as
\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{S}^{T}_{1}\mathbf{V}_{1}-\mathbf{B}
, which is a rank-one matrix. The storage cost for
\mathbf{S}_{1}
,
\mathbf{V}_{1}
and
\mathbf{B}
is
O(2*N_{G}+N_{Q})
, which is much lower than
O(N_{G}*N_{Q})
for storing
\mathbf{C}
. Although rank-one codebook can reduce the storage cost, it has limited representation ability.

To improve the representation ability of the codebook, we propose to use a low-rank codebook for quantization.
More specifically, we introduce two matrices:
\mathbf{S}=\left[\mathbf{S}_{1};\cdots;\mathbf{S}_{N_{D}}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{D}\times N_{G}}
,
\mathbf{V}=\left[\mathbf{V}_{1};\cdots;\mathbf{V}_{N_{D}}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{D}\times N_{Q}}
.
The quantization codebook
\mathbf{C}
is obtained as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}=\Phi(\mathbf{S},\mathbf{V},\mathbf{B})=\mathbf{S}^{T}\mathbf{V}-\mathbf{B}.

(3)

When
N_{D}>1
, the rank of the codebook
\mathbf{C}
can be larger than one.
When
N_{D}=1
, the codebook
\mathbf{C}
degenerates to rank-one codebook as in existing methods
(
Lin et al. 2024
;
Frantar et al. 2023
)
.
Hence, our LCQ has a stronger representation ability than existing methods, including existing rank-one codebook based methods as special cases.
We will show that LCQ with
N_{D}>1
will achieve better accuracy than LCQ with
N_{D}=1
in Section
4
.
This verifies the stronger representation ability of our low-rank codebook compared to the rank-one codebook.

shows the low-rank codebook with
N_{D}=2
.

3.2
Objective Function

As illustrated in Figure
1
, LCQ starts with a pre-trained model and tries to find the optimal
\mathbf{S}
,
\mathbf{V}
and
\mathbf{B}
for minimizing the loss caused by quantization.
We use the output reconstruction error of a Transformer block as the objective function like the methods in
(
Bai et al. 2022
;
Li et al. 2021
;
Yao et al. 2022
)
,
which can make the parameters of a Transformer block be optimized simultaneously.
Compared to the methods that use the output reconstruction error of a single linear layer as the objective function
(
Frantar et al. 2023
;
Lin et al. 2024
)
,
using the output reconstruction error of a Transformer block can avoid falling into local optima and prevent the accumulation of quantization error.
A Transformer block in the Transformer-based models
(
Vaswani et al. 2017
)
typically contains the following linear layers: the query/key/value linear projection layers
\mathbf{W}^{qproj},\mathbf{W}^{kproj},\mathbf{W}^{vproj}
, the output projection layer
\mathbf{W}^{oproj}
of the multi-head self-attention, and two linear layers
\mathbf{W}^{fc1}
and
\mathbf{W}^{fc2}
of the feed forward network.
Therefore, the parameters of a Transformer block can be represented as
\mathcal{W}=\left[\mathbf{W}^{qproj},\mathbf{W}^{kproj},\mathbf{W}^{vproj},\mathbf{W}^{oproj},\mathbf{W}^{fc1},\mathbf{W}^{fc2}\right]
.
Similarly,
parameters
\mathcal{S},\mathcal{V},\mathcal{B}
are used to represent all
\mathbf{S}
s,
\mathbf{V}
s,
\mathbf{B}
s within a Transformer block.
We define the objective function to minimize the norm of the difference between the output with quantized weights and the output with unquantized weights in a Transformer block as follows:

\begin{split}\min_{\mathcal{S},\mathcal{V},\mathcal{B}}&\sum_{i=1}^{N_{X}}||h(\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{i},\mathcal{W}^{Q})-h(\mathbf{X}^{fp}_{i},\mathcal{W})||_{F}^{2}\\
&+||h(\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{i},\mathcal{W}^{Q})-h(\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{i},\mathcal{W})||_{F}^{2}.\end{split}

(4)

where
h(\cdot)
denotes the function of a Transformer block, and
N_{X}
is the number of calibration samples.

\mathbf{X}^{fp}_{i}
represents the feature with all previous blocks having full precision and

\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{i}
represents the feature with all previous blocks quantized.

\mathcal{W}^{Q}=Q(\mathcal{W},\Phi(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{V},\mathcal{B}))
represents the quantized weights in a Transformer block.

h(\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{i},\mathcal{W}^{Q})
represents the output feature of the Transformer block with all blocks quantized including this block.
The objective function contains two terms, one uses
h(\mathbf{X}^{fp}_{i},\mathcal{W})
as the reconstruction target and
the other uses
h(\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{i},\mathcal{W})
.
Note that the quantization algorithm does not change the model weights
\mathbf{W}

(
Shao et al. 2024
)
, in order to avoid forgetting existing knowledge.
By fixing
\mathbf{W}
, we can also reduce the optimization overhead by reducing the number of parameters to be optimized.

3.3
Gradient-based Optimization

To solve the problem in (
4
),
we propose a gradient-based optimization algorithm with strategies of gradient approximation for the quantization function, reparameterization of quantization parameters, and initialization of quantization parameters.

3.3.1
Gradient Approximation for Quantization Function

The
argmin(\cdot)
operation in (
1
) will make the gradient unable to back-propagate through this function.
This makes it challenging to learn the quantization codebook.
We propose a gradient approximation strategy to address this issue.

As in N2UQ
(
Liu et al. 2022b
)
, we rewrite the quantization function into a form that adds up multiple segments,
each corresponding to the interval between two adjacent quantization values. The form is as follows:

\begin{split}Q(W_{i,j},\mathbf{C}_{i})&=C_{i,\pi_{i}(1)}\\
&+\sum_{k=1}^{N_{Q}-1}\left(C_{i,\pi_{i}(k+1)}-C_{i,\pi_{i}(k)}\right)\\
&*\chi_{0.5}^{(k)}\left(\text{clip}\left(\frac{W_{i,j}-C_{i,\pi_{i}(k)}}{C_{i,\pi_{i}(k+1)}-C_{i,\pi_{i}(k)}},0,1\right)\right)\end{split}

(5)

where
\pi_{i}(\cdot)
is a sorting function that is responsible for sorting each row of the quantization codebook
\mathbf{C}
in ascending order.
The function
\text{clip}(\cdot,0,1)
clips values outside
[0,1]
to be within the
[0,1]
range.
The step function
\chi_{0.5}^{(k)}(x)
is an element-wise function that discretizes weights, defined as follows:

\displaystyle\chi_{0.5}^{(k)}(x)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }x>0.5,\text{ or }\left(x=0.5\text{ and }k\%2=1\right),\\
0&\text{if }x<0.5,\text{ or }\left(x=0.5\text{ and }k\%2=0\right).\end{cases}

(6)

Here, we quantize critical values that are in the middle of two quantization values to the quantization value at the even position, corresponding to the condition
k\%2=0
in (
6
).
This avoids quantizing all critical values to the smaller/larger quantization value, preventing an overall bias in the quantization results. Furthermore, the elements which make
C_{i,\pi_{i}(k+1)}-C_{i,\pi_{i}(k)}

equal zero, i.e., two adjacent quantization values are equal, can cause division by zero. To prevent such cases, we truncate values of
C_{i,\pi_{i}(k+1)}-C_{i,\pi_{i}(k)}
that are less than
\epsilon
to
\epsilon
.
We can verify that the quantization function in (
5
) is equivalent to that in (
2
).

To address the issue that the gradient of the
\chi^{(k)}_{0.5}(x)
is almost zero everywhere, we adopt the straight-through estimator (STE) approximation for the backward pass of the step function
\chi^{(k)}_{0.5}(x)
. This is defined as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\chi^{(k)}_{0.5}(x)}{\partial x}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }0\leq x\leq 1,\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(7)

This means that the gradient for parameters within the
[0,1]
interval is 1, while the gradient for parameters outside this interval is 0.
The gradients for the rest of the operations in the quantization function
Q(\cdot)
remain unchanged. By applying the chain rule, we can obtain
\frac{\partial\mathbf{W}^{Q}}{\partial\mathbf{S}},\frac{\partial\mathbf{W}^{Q}}{\partial\mathbf{V}},\frac{\partial\mathbf{W}^{Q}}{\partial\mathbf{B}}
to optimize the quantization parameters
\mathbf{S},\mathbf{V},\mathbf{B}
with gradient descent.

3.3.2
Reparameterization of Quantization Parameters

During the gradient descent process,
we find it challenging to directly learn the scaling vectors
\mathcal{S}
for all layers in a block.
The main reason is that the values can vary significantly among different layers, and even within the same layer (e.g., in the fc1 layer), as shown in Figure
2
. Directly learning
\mathcal{S}
can result in excessive gradient updates for some weights while being too small for others, leading to difficulty in convergence. As in OmniQuant
(
Shao et al. 2024
)
, we propose a reparameterization strategy to address the divergence issue caused by the large differences in
\mathcal{S}
values. The reparameterization of
\mathbf{S}_{*,i}
for the
i
th group of weights can be written as follows:

\begin{split}\mathbf{S}_{*,i}&=g_{S}(\bar{\mathbf{S}}_{*,i},\mathbf{W}_{i})\\
&=\text{tanh}(\bar{\mathbf{S}}_{*,i})\left(\frac{\text{max}(\mathbf{W}_{i})-\text{min}(\mathbf{W}_{i})}{2}\right).\end{split}

(8)

Here, different groups of weights have different reparameterization coefficients
\frac{\text{max}(\mathbf{W}_{i})-\text{min}(\mathbf{W}_{i})}{2}
, decoupling the weight range from
\mathbf{S}
. After reparameterization, the parameter to be optimized changes from
\mathbf{S}
to
\bar{\mathbf{S}}
, which is relatively uniform across different layers and can improve the consistency among gradient magnitudes to facilitate convergence.
The use of
\text{tanh}(\cdot)
constrains the range of
\bar{\mathbf{S}}
, and subsequently prevents
\mathbf{S}
from exceeding the maximum range of a group of weights.

For the reparameterization of the quantization offsets
\mathbf{B}
, we employ a similar method as that for the reparameterization of
\mathbf{S}
.
But we need to solve an extra issue. More specifically, according to previous studies
(
Lin et al. 2024
;
Frantar et al. 2023
)
, it is generally necessary to include the value
0
in the set of quantized values.
The main reason is that many weights in the model are close to
0
, and hence excluding
0
from the quantized values would lead to significant quantization loss and severely degrade the accuracy of the final model.
Assume the current offsets are
\mathbf{B}^{\prime}
.
For the offsets
\mathbf{B}^{\prime}_{i}
of the
i
th group, the reparameterized form is as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{i}^{\prime}=g_{S}(\bar{\mathbf{B}}_{i},\mathbf{W}_{i}).

(9)

A new substitute offset
\mathbf{B}_{i}
must be recalculated in each optimization iteration based on
\mathbf{B}_{i}^{\prime}
,
ensuring that the value
0
is included in the codebook
\mathbf{C}_{i}
when using
\mathbf{B}_{i}
.
The offset calculation formula is as follows:
\mathbf{B}_{i}=Q(\mathbf{B}_{i}^{\prime},\mathbf{C}_{i}^{\prime})
, where
\mathbf{C}_{i}^{\prime}=\mathbf{S}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{V}
.
This ensures that
\mathbf{B}_{i}
is definitely an element of
\mathbf{C}_{i}^{\prime}
, and consequently,
0
is assuredly an element of the quantization codebook
\mathbf{C}_{i}=\mathbf{S}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{V}-\mathbf{B}_{i}
.

We also reparameterize
\mathbf{V}
. As the range of weights has already been represented by
\mathbf{S}
,
\mathbf{V}
does not need to represent the range of weights. We only need to use the
\text{tanh}(\cdot)
function to constrain the range of
\mathbf{V}
to be in
[-1,1]
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}=g_{V}(\bar{\mathbf{V}})=\text{tanh}(\bar{\mathbf{V}}).

(10)

Although the reparameterization strategy might result in a codebook in which the maximum range could exceed the maximum range of the group weights,
we can constrain the initial maximum range of the codebook to be within the maximum range of the group weights during initialization.

Subsequent optimization during gradient descent will then fine-tune the codebook within the vicinity of the initial values.

.

3.3.3
Initialization of Quantization Parameters

We first get the initialization of
\mathbf{S},\mathbf{V},\mathbf{B}^{\prime}
.
To get the initialization of reparameterization parameters, we calculate
\bar{\mathbf{S}}
,
\bar{\mathbf{V}}
,
\bar{\mathbf{B}}
based on the inverse functions corresponding to (
8
), (
9
), and (
10
).

We use the results of AWQ
(
Lin et al. 2024
)
to initialize rank-1 quantization parameters.
From the results of AWQ, we can obtain the parameters
\mathbf{S}_{1}
,
\mathbf{V}_{1}
,
\mathbf{B}^{\prime}
.
Specifically, AWQ
(
Lin et al. 2024
)
optimizes the equivalent transformation parameters and the clipping parameters of each linear layer.
For the equivalent transformation parameters, we apply the optimized equivalent transformation parameters of AWQ to scale the weights of each linear layer.
For the clipping parameters, AWQ searches for the clipping parameters for each group of weights and then clips the weights.
The range and mid-point of the clipped weights correspond to
\mathbf{S}_{1}
,
\mathbf{B}^{\prime}
.
In addition,
\mathbf{V}_{1}
is a vector with uniform interval, i.e.,
\mathbf{V}_{1}=[-1,-1+\frac{2}{2^{b}-1},\cdots,1-\frac{2}{2^{b}-1},1]
in AWQ.

For the remaining parameters,
\mathbf{S}_{r}(r\geq 2)
is initialized to zero.

\mathbf{V}_{2}
is initialized with equidistant points from a standard Gaussian distribution and

\mathbf{V}_{r}(r\geq 3)
is initialized with sorted random values from
\text{Uniform}(-0.1,0.1)
.
We get the initialization of
\bar{\mathbf{S}}_{r}(r\geq 2)
and
\bar{\mathbf{V}}_{2}(r\geq 2)
based on the inverse functions corresponding to (
8
) and (
10
).
This ensures that the initialization of LCQ corresponds exactly to the results of AWQ,
making the optimization process easier to converge to a good result.

3.4
Double Quantization

When
N_{D}>1
, LCQ will have more parameters than rank-one codebook based methods like AWQ, which will result in extra storage cost. To mitigate the extra storage cost associated with the enlarged
\mathbf{S}
and
\mathbf{V}
,
we adopt a double quantization strategy as in QLoRA
(
Dettmers et al. 2023
)
to further quantize
\mathbf{S}
and
\mathbf{V}
.
Taking
\mathbf{S}
as an example, with double quantization, the optimization goal is to minimize the quantization error of
\mathbf{S}
:

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{S}^{dq},\mathbf{V}^{dq},\mathbf{B}^{dq}}

\displaystyle||Q_{\Phi(\mathbf{S}^{dq},\mathbf{V}^{dq},\mathbf{B}^{dq})}(\mathbf{S})-\mathbf{S}||^{2}_{2}.

(11)

Here,
\mathbf{S}^{dq},\mathbf{V}^{dq},\mathbf{B}^{dq}
represent the parameters for double quantization. We adopt uniform quantization for double quantization.
Like AWQ, we employ grid search to find the optimal
\mathbf{S}^{dq},\mathbf{V}^{dq},\mathbf{B}^{dq}
.
More specifically, grid search is first used to identify the double quantization parameters for
\mathbf{S}
, which are then fixed.

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we propose a novel weight quantization method, called low-rank codebook based quantization (LCQ), for large language models (LLMs). LCQ leverages a low-rank codebook with a rank potentially greater than one, significantly enhancing the accuracy of quantized models while incurring only a negligible additional storage cost.
Experiments demonstrate that LCQ surpasses existing methods, especially in low bit-width scenarios. It effectively reduces storage and computational requirements while maintaining high accuracy, making LCQ a practical solution for deploying large models on resource-constrained devices.

Impact Statement

The LCQ method represents a significant advancement in the field of model compression, offering a practical solution to the challenges of deploying large language models in resource-constrained environments. By enabling high compression ratios with minimal accuracy loss, LCQ makes it feasible to deploy LLMs on mobile and edge devices, opening up new possibilities for real-time applications in natural language processing and multimodal tasks. Furthermore, the reduced energy consumption associated with LCQ contributes to the development of sustainable AI technologies, addressing the growing environmental concerns related to large-scale AI model deployment. The compatibility of LCQ with existing quantization techniques ensures its versatility and broad applicability, making it a valuable tool for both academia and industry.
