Tidal Disruption of Planetesimals from an Eccentric Debris Disk Following a White Dwarf Natal Kick
Tatsuya Akiba, Selah McIntyre, Ann-Marie Madigan

Abstract
The surfaces of many white dwarfs are polluted by metals, implying a recent accretion event. The tidal disruption of planetesimals is a viable source of white dwarf pollution and offers a unique window into the composition of exoplanet systems. The question of how planetary material enters the tidal disruption radius of the white dwarf is currently unresolved. Using a series of N N -body simulations, we explore the response of the surrounding planetesimal debris disk as the white dwarf receives a natal kick caused by anisotropic mass loss on the asymptotic giant branch. We find that the kick can form an apse-aligned, eccentric debris disk in the range 30 to 240 AU 30240\text{\,}\mathrm{AU} which corresponds to the orbits of Neptune, the Kuiper Belt, and the scattered disk in our solar system. In addition, many planetesimals beyond 240 AU 240\text{\,}\mathrm{AU} flip to counter-rotating orbits. Assuming an isotropic distribution of kicks, we predict that approximately 80 % 80\% of white dwarf debris disks should exhibit significant apsidal alignment and fraction of counter-rotating orbits. The eccentric disk is able to efficiently and continuously torque planetesimals onto radial, star-grazing orbits. We show that the kick causes both an initial burst in tidal disruption events as well as an extended period of 100 Myr 100\text{\,}\mathrm{Myr} where tidal disruption rates are consistent with observed mass accretion rates on polluted white dwarfs.

II
Eccentric Debris Disk Formation and Stability

When a gravitational recoil kick is imparted on a supermassive black hole, the surrounding stellar orbits in a nuclear star cluster can form an eccentric, apse-aligned disk
[
1
,
2
]
, and these eccentric disks exhibit stellar tidal disruption rates as high as
0.1\text{\,}{\mathrm{yr}}^{-1}\text{\,}{\mathrm{gal}}^{-1}
, three to four orders of magnitude higher than rates expected from isotropic distributions
[
40
]
. The dynamics work on all scales in a near-Keplerian system and thus are directly applicable to planetesimals surrounding white dwarfs following the impartment of a natal kick. To quantify apsidal alignment, we make use of the eccentricity vector

\vec{e}=\frac{\vec{v}\times\vec{j}}{GM_{*}}-\hat{r}\ ,

(1)

where
\vec{v}
is the velocity vector,
\vec{j}
is the (specific) angular momentum vector,
M_{*}
is the white dwarf mass, and
\hat{r}
is the unit position vector. The eccentricity vector points from the apoapsis to the periapsis of a given orbit, with a magnitude equal to the scalar eccentricity.

The
mean eccentricity vector
is a measure of apsidal alignment defined by

\langle\vec{e}\rangle\equiv\frac{\sum_{i}^{N}\vec{e}_{i}}{N}\ ,

(2)

where
\vec{e}_{i}
is the eccentricity vector of the
i
-th planetesimal and
N
is the number of planetesimals considered. When the white dwarf experiences an in-plane kick, planetesimals on initially circular orbits will align their eccentricity vectors such that

|\langle\vec{e}\rangle|=\frac{3}{2}\frac{v_{\rm kick}}{v_{\rm circ}}\ ,

(3)

where
v_{\rm kick}
is the natal kick speed and
v_{\rm circ}
is the initial circular speed of the planetesimals. Apsidal alignment is strongest when
|\langle\vec{e}\rangle|=1
. From Equation
3
, this occurs at a
characteristic radius

r_{c}=\frac{4GM_{*}}{9v_{\rm kick}^{2}}\ .

(4)

For
M_{*}=$0.6\text{\,}\mathrm{M_{\odot}}$
and
v_{\rm kick}=$1\text{\,}\mathrm{km}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}$
, this radius occurs at
r_{c}=$240\text{\,}\mathrm{AU}$
. For
v_{\rm kick}=$3\text{\,}\mathrm{km}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}$
,
r_{c}=$30\text{\,}\mathrm{AU}$
.

In the solar system, an orbital distance of
30\text{\,}\mathrm{AU}
corresponds to that of Neptune and the Kuiper Belt, a dynamically rich region of space that includes both kinematically cold and hot primordial planetesimal populations intermixed with those in orbital resonance with Neptune
[
34
,
42
, e.g.,]
. A distance of
240\text{\,}\mathrm{AU}
corresponds to that of the scattered disk, a population of planetesimals on eccentric orbits with periapses that bring them into contact with Neptune’s orbit
[
16
,
56
]
. We note that this comparison does not take into account orbital expansion due to the star’s mass loss
[
54
, e.g.,]
. The existence of Kuiper Belt or scattered disk-like structures in exoplanet systems has been inferred from observations
[
28
,
58
, e.g.,]
; disks of icy bodies in the outskirts of planetary systems should be common.

Dynamical stability in eccentric disks comes about via mutual gravitational torques between orbiters
[
40
]
. When a planetesimal precesses ahead of the eccentric disk, its orbit is negatively torqued by the disk, and its angular momentum decreases which in turn increases its scalar eccentricity. This change in eccentricity works to slow down the orbit’s precession and allows the rest of the disk to catch up to it. The opposite is true for an orbit that lags behind the disk: it feels a positive torque which circularizes the orbit and increases its precession speed. In this way, the eccentric disk maintains its apsidal alignment as individual orbits undergo oscillations in precession speeds and eccentricity. It is the latter oscillation in eccentricity that causes an enhancement in the rate of tidal disruption events as strong mutual torques throw planetesimals onto radial, star-grazing orbits.

III.3
Eccentric Debris Disk Evolution and the Tidal Disruption of Planetesimals

To investigate the burst of tidal disruption events following the kick, we redistribute
N=$5\text{\times}{10}^{4}$
massless planetesimals in the region
a=$2402400\text{\,}\mathrm{AU}$

planetesimals in the range
a=$240480\text{\,}\mathrm{AU}$
and switch them from massless test particles to massive, self-interacting particles to study the eccentric debris disk’s long-term evolution. We use
REBOUND

IAS15
integrator, a high-order, non-symplectic integrator with adaptive time-stepping
[
47
]

N
and narrow range of semi-major axes explored are in part due to the computational limitations of
IAS15
, but high-accuracy integration is critical in studying eccentric disk evolution
1
1

1

We also tested
WHFast

[
48
]
. Our results showed that
WHFast
runs significantly deviate from
IAS15
runs of the same disk mass with the former exhibiting total energy and angular momentum errors of order unity within a

.

III.4
Long-term Enhancement of Tidal Disruption Rates in Eccentric Disks

In the top panel of Figure
4
, we show the cumulative mass accreted by the white dwarf from planetesimal tidal disruption events during the
M_{\mathrm{disk}}=$20\text{\,}\mathrm{M_{\bigoplus}}$
simulation. We note that we do not include a treatment of the accretion process; we are inherently assuming that a planetesimal, once tidally disrupted, is entirely accreted by the white dwarf on orbital timescales
t_{\rm{orb}}\approx$\mathrm{kyr}$
. We see that the eccentric debris disk is able to produce tidal disruption events for an extended period of
100\text{\,}\mathrm{Myr}
following the impartment of the kick. This particular disk mass simulation implies a very high accretion rate of
{10}^{12}\text{\,}\mathrm{g}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}
. Except for a few periods or dormancy (e.g.,
t=$5869\text{\,}\mathrm{Myr}$
), at least one tidal disruption event is observed every few
\mathrm{Myr}
and hence the expected time-averaged accretion rate is at least
{10}^{9}\text{\,}\mathrm{g}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}
throughout the simulation. In addition, due to the significant retrograde fraction induced by the kick, we find that approximately 40% of tidally disrupted planetesimals are on retrograde orbits when they become tidally disrupted. If the initial disk angular momentum axis is the same as that of the star’s rotation axis, this mechanism would predict a significant fraction of white dwarf systems to have a circumstellar debris disk following a tidal disruption event that is retrograde with respect to the white dwarf spin axis.

In the center panel of Figure
4
, we plot the eccentricity evolution of a small sample of planetesimals that become tidally disrupted. While the initial jump to high eccentricities at
t=0
is due to the natal kick, the kick alone is unable to tidally disrupt these planetesimals. The subsequent evolution to higher eccentricities is caused by the strong mutual torques between planetesimals within the eccentric disk (see Section
II
). The bottom panel of Figure
4
shows the apsidal alignment evolution. Statistically-significant alignment is maintained throughout the simulation. We note that the oscillation in
|\langle\vec{e}\rangle|
is caused by the periodic alignment and misalignment between the eccentric disk and planetesimals near the outer edge of the disk that break off and precess in the retrograde direction
[
40
,
1

. This figure shows that the oscillation is correlated with bursts of tidal disruption events; as the retrograde-precessing planetesimals come back into alignment with the eccentric disk, mutual gravitational torques increase the amplitude of the eccentricity oscillations which consequently increase tidal disruption rates. The white dwarf natal kick causes an initial burst of tidal disruption events followed by an extended
100\text{\,}\mathrm{Myr}
period where tidal disruption rates from the eccentric debris disk are consistent with some of the highest mass accretion rates observed. We note that
t=$100\text{\,}\mathrm{Myr}$
corresponds to polluted white dwarfs on the warmer end with temperatures around
20\,000\text{\,}\mathrm{K}

[
19
, see]
; a higher
N

\mathrm{G}\mathrm{y}\mathrm{r}
or older.

III.5
Tidal Disruption Rate Dependence on Eccentric Debris Disk Mass

Figure 5:
The average mass accretion rate,
\dot{M}
from tidal disruption events as a function of the debris disk mass,
M_{\mathrm{disk}}
. The average is calculated from the first
10\text{\,}\mathrm{Myr}
of each simulation. The axis on the right shows the corresponding fraction of the debris disk that is tidally disrupted in each case. For simulations with at least one tidal disruption event detection, the results are consistent with a power-law dependence
\dot{M}\propto(M_{\mathrm{disk}})^{2}
.

The expected planetesimal tidal disruption rate has a steep dependence on the mass of the eccentric debris disk. The following analysis assumes that the accretion process happens on the orbital timescale
t_{\rm{orb}}\approx$\mathrm{kyr}$
which is much shorter than the
secular timescale
defined by
t_{\rm{sec}}\equiv(M_{*}/M_{\mathrm{disk}})~t_{\rm{orb}}
. The eccentricity oscillations that cause tidal disruption events happen on the secular timescale
[
40
]
, so the tidal disruption rate
\dot{N}_{\rm{TDE}}\propto t_{\rm{sec}}^{-1}\propto M_{\mathrm{disk}}
. The mass accretion rate is estimated as
\dot{M}=m\dot{N}_{\rm{TDE}}
where
m
is the planetesimal mass. In our simulations, we fix
N
and vary
M_{\mathrm{disk}}
, so
m\propto M_{\mathrm{disk}}
. Hence, we analytically predict
\dot{M}\propto(M_{\mathrm{disk}})^{2}
.

In Figure
5
, we show the estimated mass accretion rate from tidal disruption events for each of our massive disk simulations. For simulations with at least one tidal disruption event detected, the correlation is well-modeled by our analytic prescription
\dot{M}\propto(M_{\mathrm{disk}})^{2}
. The
M_{\mathrm{disk}}=$1$
and
2\text{\,}\mathrm{M_{\bigoplus}}
simulations detect no tidal disruption events in the first
10\text{\,}\mathrm{Myr}
, but this is likely due to the low
N
and short simulation time. For instance,
M_{\mathrm{disk}}=$1\text{\,}\mathrm{M_{\bigoplus}}$
implies a secular timescale of
t_{\rm{sec}}\approx$100\text{\,}\mathrm{Myr}$
. If instead, we extrapolate the
\dot{M}\propto(M_{\mathrm{disk}})^{2}
scaling to lower debris disk masses, we predict that typical instantaneous accretion rates of
{10}^{7}\text{\,}\mathrm{g}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}
on hydrogen white dwarfs are reproduced for any eccentric debris disk with mass above
0.03\text{\,}\mathrm{M_{\bigoplus}}
. The highest measured instantaneous accretion rates of
{10}^{9}\text{\,}\mathrm{g}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}
are predicted for
M_{\mathrm{disk}}>$0.3\text{\,}\mathrm{M_{\bigoplus}}$
. Finally, the highest time-averaged accretion rates inferred from helium white dwarfs of
{10}^{11}\text{\,}\mathrm{g}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}
require debris disk masses of at least a few
\mathrm{M_{\bigoplus}}
.

IV
Discussion

We perform numerical simulations to explore the formation of an eccentric debris disk following a white dwarf natal kick. We run 2500 massless simulations with isotropically-distributed kick angles to estimate the fraction of white dwarf debris disks with significant apsidal alignment, retrograde fraction, and planetesimal tidal disruption rates. For the most likely case of an in-plane kick, we follow the evolution of the eccentric disk and tidal disruption rates by performing a series of massive
N
-body simulations. Our main findings are:

1.

An eccentric debris disk and a retrograde planetesimal population are expected for approximately
80\%
of planetesimal debris disks. The retrograde population forms beyond
240\text{\,}\mathrm{AU}
for a white dwarf natal kick of
1\text{\,}\mathrm{km}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}
.

2.

For a
1\text{\,}\mathrm{km}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}
kick,
10\%
of white dwarfs are expected to immediately tidally disrupt a planetesimal. This fraction increases to
16\%
for a
3\text{\,}\mathrm{km}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}
kick. This is consistent with earlier results of
Stone et al. [51]
.

3.

The eccentric debris disk hosts planetesimal tidal disruption rates consistent with observed mass accretion rates for an extended
100\text{\,}\mathrm{Myr}
period. Around
40\%
of the tidal disruption events are retrograde with respect to the initial disk angular momentum axis.

4.

The predicted mass accretion rates have a steep dependence on the eccentric debris disk mass,
\dot{M}\propto(M_{\mathrm{disk}})^{2}
. A debris disk of mass above
0.03\text{\,}\mathrm{M_{\bigoplus}}
is required to explain typical instantaneous mass accretion rates of
{10}^{7}\text{\,}\mathrm{g}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}
.

The estimated fraction of white dwarf systems that exhibit eccentric debris disks, retrograde planetesimal populations, and tidal disruption events is dependent on the density profile of the pre-kick debris disk. Our results from Figure
5
show that our mechanism requires at least
0.03\text{\,}\mathrm{M_{\bigoplus}}
in the debris disk within the range
240480\text{\,}\mathrm{AU}
assuming a kick of
1\text{\,}\mathrm{km}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}
. Throughout the letter, we have assumed that the natal kick is impulsive. In reality, the investigated range of semi-major axes lies in the complex transition region between planetesimals with
a<$10\text{\,}\mathrm{AU}$
where mass loss seems adiabatic, and planetesimals with
a>$1000\text{\,}\mathrm{AU}$
where the mass loss seems impulsive
[
51
]
. Furthermore, stellar models predict that mass loss is pulsed
[
45
,
6
,
8
, e.g.,]
which adds more layers of complication. In the future, our work will consider the treatment of mass loss and the complex orbital evolution of planetesimals in response to a more realistic natal kick.

Additionally, our current work only considers a pre-kick circular disk with equal-mass particles. Moving forward, we plan to repeat this numerical experiment with a scattered disk-like distribution of planetesimals accompanied by one or more giant planets. Preliminary results have shown that the initial burst of tidal disruption events is more significant for the scattered disk but apsidal alignment is weaker. A follow-up study with long-term simulations is necessary to determine whether the eccentric debris disk and high tidal disruption rates are maintained in this case. We note that higher
N
simulations are necessary to thoroughly explore debris disks with masses around
1\text{\,}\mathrm{M_{\bigoplus}}
or below. In future studies, we aim to extend our mechanism to polluted white dwarfs with cooling ages
\mathrm{Gyr}
or older, and make our claims about the tidal disruption event bursts more robust through longer and higher
N
simulations.
