MATRIX HERMITE POLYNOMIALS, RANDOM DETERMINANTS AND THE GEOMETRY OF GAUSSIAN FIELDS
Notarnicola Massimo

Abstract
We study generalized Hermite polynomials with rectangular matrix arguments arising in multivariate statistical analysis and the theory of zonal polynomials. We show that these are well-suited for expressing the Wiener-Itô chaos expansion of functionals of the spectral measure associated with Gaussian matrices. In particular, we obtain the Wiener chaos expansion of Gaussian determinants of the form det ( X ​ X T ) 1 / 2 \det(XX^{T})^{1/2} and prove that, in the setting where the rows of X X are i.i.d. centred Gaussian vectors with a given covariance matrix, its projection coefficients admit a geometric interpretation in terms of intrinsic volumes of ellipsoids, thus extending the content of [ ZK12 ] to arbitrary chaotic projection coefficients. Our proofs are based on a crucial relation between generalized Hermite polynomials and generalized Laguerre polynomials. In a second part, we introduce the matrix analog of the classical Mehler’s formula for the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroup and prove that matrix-variate Hermite polynomials are eigenfunctions of these operators. As a byproduct, we derive an orthogonality relation for Hermite polynomials evaluated at correlated Gaussian matrices. We apply our results to vectors of independent arithmetic random waves on the three-torus, proving in particular a CLT in the high-energy regime for a generalized notion of total variation on the full torus.



Keywords:
Generalized Hermite polynomials, Gaussian random matrices, Zonal polynomials, Wiener chaos expansions, Intrinsic and mixed volumes, Arithmetic Random Waves, Limit Theorems

1
Introduction

In applications to stochastic geometry dealing with the asymptotic analysis of local geometric quantities associated with Gaussian random fields on manifolds, one is often confronted with expressions involving quantities of the type
F(X)
, where
X
is a rectangular centred Gaussian matrix and
F
is a certain
spectral
function, that is,
F
only depends on the spectral measure associated with the matrix
XX^{T}
. For instance, if
Z=\{Z(x):x\in\mathcal{M}\}
is a
\ell
-dimensional stationary Gaussian field on a manifold
\mathcal{M}
of dimension
n
(with
1\leq\ell\leq n
), the
nodal volume
of
Z
over a region
R\subset\mathcal{M}
has typically the form

\displaystyle\int_{R}\delta_{0}(Z(x))F(J_{Z}(x))\mathrm{vol}_{\mathcal{M}}(dx),

where
\delta_{0}
indicates the Dirac mass at zero,
J_{Z}(x)
stands for the Jacobian matrix of
Z
computed at
x
and
F(X)=\sqrt{\det{(XX^{T})}}
, see for instance
[
Wig10
,
MPRW16
,
MRW20
,
NPR19
,
Not21
]
for some distinguished examples.
While objects of this type are amenable to analysis by Wiener-Itô chaos expansions (which involves in particular the decomposition of
F(J_{Z}(x))
into Hermite polynomials having the entries of
J_{Z}(x)
as arguments, see the references above), it is to be expected that such a technique will generate combinatorially untractable expressions for large values of the dimensions
\ell
and
n
(see for instance
[
DEL21
]
and
[
Not21
]
). The aim of this paper is to tackle directly such a difficulty by initiating a systematic study of chaotic expansions for spectral random variables
F(X)
as above by using
matrix-variate Hermite polynomials
, that is, a collection of orthogonal polynomials with matrix entries which are indexed by partitions of integers, obtained by orthogonalizing matrix monomials of the type
\Tr([XX^{T}]^{s})
with respect to the law of a Gaussian matrix. We will see that matrix-variate Hermite polynomials inherit the rich combinatorial structure and actually can be defined in terms of
zonal polynomials
introduced in
[
Jam61
]
, thus allowing one to deduce explicit formulae in any dimension. We now describe the principal achievements of the present work.

(a)

In
[
Tha93
]
(see also the related work
[
Koc96
]
), the author studies Hermite expansions of functions of the form
F(x)=f_{0}(\left\lVert x\right\rVert)P(x)
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where
f_{0}
is a function depending only on the norm
\left\lVert x\right\rVert
and
P
is a harmonic polynomial. In particular, in such a work, the author provides explicit formulae for the projection coefficients associated with the Wiener-Itô chaos expansion of functionals
F
as above in terms of
Laguerre polynomials
on the real line. In Theorem
3.2
, we extend this framework by studying matrix-Hermite expansions of radial functionals of the type
F(X)=f_{0}(XX^{T})
on matrix spaces. Our results involve
generalized Laguerre polynomials with matrix argument
, thus yielding a natural counterpart to the work by Thangavelu
[
Tha93
]
in higher dimensions.

(b)

In
[
ZK12
]
(see Theorem 1.1 therein), Kabluchko and Zaporozhets establish a formula for the expected value of Gaussian determinants of the form

F(X)=\sqrt{\det(XX^{T})}
in terms of mixed volumes and intrinsic volumes of ellipsoids associated with the covariance matrices of the underlying Gaussian vectors, yielding in particular an expression for the projection of
F
onto the Gaussian Wiener chaos of order zero associated with
X
(which corresponds to the expectation). In Theorem
3.5
and Theorem
3.6
of the present paper, we substantially extend their framework by considering
arbitrary
projection coefficients of the form
\mathbb{E}\left[F(X)H_{\kappa}^{(\ell,n)}(X)\right]
(where
X
is a centred Gaussian matrix of dimension
\ell\times n
) associated with such random determinants. Our results can be formulated using integrations on the so-called
Stiefel manifold
(see Theorem
3.5
), which can subsequently be interpreted in terms of mixed and intrinsic volumes (see Theorem
3.6
).

(c)

In Section
3.3
, we introduce a
collection of operators on matrix spaces via a
Mehler-type
formula, whose definition is amenable to that of the classical Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroup on the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. In Theorem
3.10
we provide a characterization of matrix-Hermite polynomials as the eigenfunctions of these operators, thus yielding a direct analog of the action of the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroup on
classical Hermite polynomials on the real line.
We subsequently use Theorem
3.10
in order to deduce an intrinsic orthogonality relation between two matrix-Hermite polynomials evaluated in correlated Gaussian matrices. Such a result extends the classical orthogonality relation for matrix-Hermite polynomials as well as the
case of Hermite polynomials on the real line.
Conjecturally, the objects and techniques introduced in Section
3.3
generate a basis for a special Malliavin Calculus on matrix spaces via the introduction of further operators, such as Malliavin derivatives, adjoints and generators of the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroup (see e.g.
[
Nua95
,
NP12
]
). Such a program of study however largely falls outside the scope of the present paper and is left open for further research.

(d)

In Section
3.4
, we apply our results to the study of the
generalized total variation
of multi-dimensional Gaussian random fields, defined as the integral of the square root of the Gramian determinant of its normalized Jacobian matrix. More specifically, we study the high-energy behaviour of the generalized total variation of multiple independent
Arithmetic Random Waves
on the three-torus. In particular, in Theorem
3.17
we establish its expected mean, an asymptotic law for its variance and a Central Limit Theorem for the suitably normalized total variation. Our arguments rely on the expansion in matrix-Hermite polynomials of the total variation, allowing us to prove that its probabilistic fluctuations are entirely characterized by its projection on the second Wiener chaos. Throughout this application, we also make use of variance expansions of radial functionals by means of its projection coefficients (see Proposition
3.3
). Our findings are to be compared with Theorem 1 of
[
PR18
]
, where the authors prove a CLT for the
Leray measure
of Arithmetic Random Waves on the two-torus.

The organization of the paper is as follows: In Section
2
, we present preliminary notions that will be used in our proofs, notably on zonal polynomials and generalized Laguerre polynomials (Section
2.1
),
polar matrix factorizations (Section
2.2
) and
tools from integral geometry such as mixed volumes, intrinsic volumes of convex bodies and general facts about ellipsoids (Section
2.3
). Our main contributions are presented in Section
3
. Finally, the entire Section
4
is devoted to the proofs of our results.

Preliminaries

2.1
Zonal polynomials and generalized Laguerre polynomials

Zonal polynomials.

Zonal polynomials with matrix argument were introduced in
[
Jam61
]
, using group representation theory, as certain homogeneous symmetric functions of the eigenvalues (also called the
latent roots
) of the matrix.
We give a brief overview of zonal polynomials and their properties; the reader is referred for instance to the books
[
MPH95
]
and
[
Chi03
]
for a thorough introduction to zonal polynomials.
Let us now fix integers
\ell\geq 1
and
k\geq 0
. We write
\kappa\vdash k
to denote a
partition

\kappa
of
k
into no more than
\ell
integer parts (note that such a notation does not involve the integer

\ell
, whose role should be understood from the context), that is

\displaystyle\kappa=(k_{1},\ldots,k_{\ell}),\quad k_{1}\geq k_{2}\geq\ldots\geq k_{\ell}>0,\quad k_{1}+\ldots+k_{\ell}=k.

For instance, if
\ell=1
, then
\kappa=(k)
is the only partition of an integer
k
; if
\ell\geq 2
, then
\kappa=(2)
and
\kappa=(1,1)
are the only partitions of
k=2
. Sometimes it is useful to represent the partition
\kappa\vdash k
as
\kappa=(1^{\nu_{1}}2^{\nu_{2}}\ldots k^{\nu_{k}})
to indicate that the integer
j
occurs with multiplicity
\nu_{j}
; in particular
\nu_{1}+2\nu_{2}+\ldots+k\nu_{k}=k
. With this notation, we have for instance
(1,1)=(1^{2})\vdash 2
and
(1,2,3,3)=(1^{1}2^{1}3^{2})\vdash 9
.

Let
S\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times\ell}
be a symmetric matrix with eigenvalues
s_{1},\ldots,s_{\ell}
. For an integer
k\geq 1
, we denote by
\mathrm{Pol}_{k}(S)
the space of homogeneous polynomials of degree
k
in the
\ell(\ell+1)/2
variables of
S
. For an invertible matrix
L\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times\ell}
, the transformation
S\to LSL^{T}
induces a representation
\pi
of
\mathrm{GL}_{\ell}(\mathbb{R})
into the vector space
\mathrm{GL}(\mathrm{Pol}_{k}(S))
of isomorphisms from
\mathrm{Pol}_{k}(S)
to itself (
[
Chi03
, Eq.(A.2.1)]
):

\displaystyle\pi:\mathrm{GL}_{\ell}(\mathbb{R})\to\mathrm{GL}(\mathrm{Pol}_{k}(S))\ ;\quad L\to\pi(L)\ ,

given by

\displaystyle\pi(L)(P):=P(L^{-1}S(L^{-1})^{T}).

It can be shown that
\mathrm{Pol}_{k}(S)
can be decomposed as direct sum (
[
Chi03
, p.297]
)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pol}_{k}(S)=\bigoplus_{\kappa\vdash k}V_{\kappa}(S),

(2.1)

where
\{V_{\kappa}(S):\kappa\vdash k\}
are irreducible and
\pi
-invariant subspaces. Since
\Tr(S)^{k}
is a homogeneous symmetric polynomial of degree
k
in the eigenvalues of
S
, it can accordingly be decomposed in the spaces
V_{\kappa}(S)
as follows (
[
MPH95
, Eq.(4.3.38)]
),

\displaystyle\Tr(S)^{k}=(s_{1}+\ldots+s_{\ell})^{k}=\sum_{\kappa\vdash k}C_{\kappa}(S),

(2.2)

where
C_{\kappa}(S)
denotes the
zonal polynomial
associated with the partition
\kappa
of
k
, that is,
C_{\kappa}(S)
is the projection of
\Tr(S)^{k}
onto the space
V_{\kappa}(S)
.
Applying (
2.2
) with
\ell=1
gives
C_{(k)}(s)=s^{k}
, so that zonal polynomials can be interpreted as a generalization of classical monomials. In particular, evaluating at
s=1
yields
C_{(k)}(1)=1
.
Zonal polynomials satisfy a generalized binomial formula (
[
MPH95
, Eq.(4.5.1)]
),

\displaystyle\frac{C_{\kappa}(S+\Id_{\ell})}{C_{\kappa}(\Id_{\ell})}=\sum_{s=0}^{k}\sum_{\sigma\vdash s}{\kappa\choose\sigma}\frac{C_{\sigma}(S)}{C_{\sigma}(\Id_{\ell})},\quad\kappa\vdash k.

(2.3)

This relation in particular defines the generalized binomial coefficients
{\kappa\choose\sigma}
. Taking
S=a\Id_{\ell}
for
a\in\mathbb{R}
in (
2.3
) yields

\displaystyle\frac{C_{\kappa}((a+1)\Id_{\ell})}{C_{\kappa}(\Id_{\ell})}=\sum_{s=0}^{k}\sum_{\sigma\vdash s}{\kappa\choose\sigma}\frac{C_{\sigma}(a\Id_{\ell})}{C_{\sigma}(\Id_{\ell})},

so that, using the homogeneity property of zonal polynomials gives

\displaystyle(a+1)^{k}=\sum_{s=0}^{k}\sum_{\sigma\vdash s}a^{s}{\kappa\choose\sigma}.

In particular, using the usual binomial formula for real numbers on the left-hand side, one deduces a relation linking classical and generalized binomial coefficients (
[
MPH95
, Eq.(4.5.2)]
):

\displaystyle{k\choose s}=\sum_{\sigma\vdash s}{\kappa\choose\sigma}.

A table with generalized binomial coefficients up to order
5
can be found in Table 4.4.1 of
[
MPH95
]
.
For
X\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times n}
, zonal polynomials associated with partition
\kappa\vdash k
and matrix argument
XX^{T}
can be decomposed as (
[
MPH95
, Theorem 4.3.6]
)

\displaystyle C_{\kappa}(XX^{T})=\sum_{(1^{\nu_{1}}2^{\nu_{2}}\ldots k^{\nu_{k}})\vdash k}z_{\kappa\nu}^{(k)}t_{1}(X)^{\nu_{1}}\ldots t_{k}(X)^{\nu_{k}},

(2.4)

where

\displaystyle t_{s}(X):=\Tr{([XX^{T}]^{s})},\quad s\geq 1

(2.5)

and
z_{\kappa\nu}^{(k)}
are numerical constants.
Writing
t_{j}:=t_{j}(X)
, the zonal polynomials associated with partitions up to order
3
are given by (see e.g.
[
MPH95
, Table 4.3.1]
)

\displaystyle C_{(1)}(XX^{T})=t_{1}

\displaystyle C_{(2)}(XX^{T})=\frac{1}{3}(t_{1}^{2}+2t_{2}),\quad C_{(1,1)}(XX^{T})=\frac{2}{3}(t_{1}^{2}-t_{2})

\displaystyle C_{(3)}(XX^{T})=\frac{1}{15}(t_{1}^{3}+6t_{1}t_{2}+8t_{3}),\quad C_{(2,1)}(XX^{T})=\frac{3}{5}(t_{1}^{3}+t_{1}t_{2}-2t_{3})

\displaystyle C_{(1,1,1)}(XX^{T})=\frac{1}{3}(t_{1}^{3}-3t_{1}t_{2}+2t_{3}).

In particular, since for every
j\in[k]
,
t_{j}(X)^{\nu_{j}}
is a homogeneous polynomial of degree
2j\nu_{j}
in the entries of
X
, it follows from (
2.4
) that
C_{\kappa}(XX^{T})
is a homogeneous polynomial of degree
2k
in the entries of
X
,

\displaystyle C_{\kappa}(XX^{T})=\sum_{|\alpha|=2k}z_{\alpha}^{\kappa}\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}\prod_{j=1}^{n}X_{ij}^{\alpha_{ij}},

(2.6)

where
\alpha=(\alpha_{ij})\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell\times n}
is a multi-index such that
|\alpha|=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{ij}=2k
and
z_{\alpha}^{\kappa}
is a numerical constant depending on
\alpha
and
\kappa
.
Zonal polynomials evaluated at the identity matrix
\Id_{\ell}
can be computed to be (
[
Chi03
, Eq.(A.2.7)]
)

\displaystyle C_{\kappa}(\Id_{\ell})=2^{2k}k!\bigg(\frac{\ell}{2}\bigg)_{\kappa}\frac{\prod_{i<j}^{p}(2k_{i}-2k_{j}-i+j)}{\prod_{j=1}^{p}(2k_{j}+p-j)!},

where
p=p(\kappa)
is the number of non-zero parts in
\kappa
, and for
a\in\mathbb{C}
,
(a)_{\kappa}
stands for the generalized Pochammer symbol (
[
Chi03
, Eq.(A.2.4)]
)

\displaystyle(a)_{\kappa}:=\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}\bigg(a-\frac{j-1}{2}\bigg)_{k_{j}},\quad(a)_{n}=a(a+1)\cdots(a+n-1)

(2.7)

defined in terms of classical Pochammer symbols
(a)_{n}
.
The product of two zonal polynomials associated with partitions
\tau\vdash t
and
\sigma\vdash s
respectively, is given by (
[
MPH95
, Eq.(4.3.65)]
)

\displaystyle C_{\tau}(S)C_{\sigma}(S)=\sum_{\kappa\vdash t+s}a_{\tau,\sigma}^{\kappa}C_{\kappa}(S),

(2.8)

for some uniquely determined coefficients
a_{\tau,\sigma}^{\kappa}
. A table for these coefficients is found in Table 4.3.2(a) of
[
MPH95
]
.
Moreover, for positive-definite matrices
S
and
T
, zonal polynomials have the property (
[
MPH95
, Eq.(4.3.18)]
)

\displaystyle C_{\kappa}(S^{1/2}TS^{1/2})=C_{\kappa}(ST)=C_{\kappa}(TS)=C_{\kappa}(T^{1/2}ST^{1/2}).

(2.9)

Generalized Laguerre polynomials.

For a symmetric matrix
S\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times\ell}
, the generalized Laguerre polynomial of order
\gamma>-1
associated with a partition
\kappa
of
k
and matrix variable
S
is defined as (
[
MPH95
, Eq.(4.6.5)]
)

\displaystyle L_{\kappa}^{(\gamma)}(S)=\bigg(\gamma+\frac{\ell+1}{2}\bigg)_{\kappa}C_{\kappa}(\Id_{\ell})\sum_{s=0}^{k}\sum_{\sigma\vdash s}{\kappa\choose\sigma}\frac{(-1)^{s}}{(\gamma+\frac{\ell+1}{2})_{\sigma}}\frac{C_{\sigma}(S)}{C_{\sigma}(\Id_{\ell})}.

(2.10)

The first Laguerre polynomials associated with partitions up to order three are listed in (4.6.8) of
[
MPH95
]
.
The generalized Laguerre polynomials define a class of orthogonal polynomials on
\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(\mathbb{R})
with respect to the weight function
\mathrm{etr}\left(-R\right)\det(R)^{\gamma}
, that is, for every integers
k,l\geq 0
and every partitions
\kappa\vdash k,\sigma\vdash l
, one has (
[
MPH95
, Theorem 4.6.4]
)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(\mathbb{R})}L_{\kappa}^{(\gamma)}(R)L_{\sigma}^{(\gamma)}(R)\mathrm{etr}\left(-R\right)\det(R)^{\gamma}\nu(dR)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{I}\left\{\kappa=\sigma\right\}\times k!C_{\kappa}(\Id_{\ell})\Gamma_{\ell}\left(\gamma+\frac{\ell+1}{2}\right)\left(\gamma+\frac{\ell+1}{2}\right)_{\kappa},

(2.11)

where
\nu(dR)
denotes the Lebesgue measure on
\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(\mathbb{R})
. Here,
for
a\in\mathbb{R}
,
\Gamma_{\ell}(a)
denotes the multivariate Gamma function defined by

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\ell}(a):=\pi^{\ell(\ell-1)/4}\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}\Gamma(a-2^{-1}(i-1))\ ,\quad\ell\geq 1,

where
\Gamma(\cdot)
is the usual Gamma function.
A useful formula that we will use at several occasions is the following (see e.g.
[
MPH95
, Theorem 4.4.1]
)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(\mathbb{R})}\mathrm{etr}\left(-AR\right)\det(R)^{t-\frac{\ell+1}{2}}C_{\kappa}(RB)\nu(dR)=(t)_{\kappa}\Gamma_{\ell}(t)\det(A)^{-t}C_{\kappa}(BA^{-1}),

(2.12)

where
A\in\mathbb{C}^{\ell\times\ell}
is a complex symmetric matrix with positive real part,
B\in\mathbb{C}^{\ell\times\ell}
is a complex symmetric matrix and
t
is such that
\Re(t)>(\ell-1)/2
.

2.2
Polar decomposition for matrices

Let
1\leq\ell\leq n
be integers. For
X=(X_{ij})\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times n}
, we denote by
dX:=(dX_{ij})
its associated differential matrix. We endow the spaces
\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times n}
and
\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(\mathbb{R})
with the measures

\displaystyle(dX):=\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}\prod_{j=1}^{n}dX_{ij}\ ,\quad\nu(dX):=\prod_{1\leq i\leq j\leq\ell}dX_{ij}

respectively.
Assuming that the rows of
X
are linearly independent, the
polar decomposition
of
X
is uniquely given by (see for instance
[
Dow72
]
)

\displaystyle X=R^{1/2}\cdot U\ ,\quad R=XX^{T}\in\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(\mathbb{R})\ ,\quad\qquad U=(XX^{T})^{-1/2}X\in O(n,\ell),

(2.13)

where
R^{1/2}
denotes the positive square root of
R
, that is the unique matrix
B
such that
B^{2}=R
. We also define
R^{-1/2}:=(R^{1/2})^{-1}
.
The space
O(n,\ell)
in (
2.13
) denotes the so-called
Stiefel manifold
of matrices
Y\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times n}
such that
YY^{T}=\Id_{\ell}
, that is,
Y
has orthonormal rows. An element of
O(n,\ell)
is called an
\ell
-frame in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, see for instance
[
Chi03
, p.8]
.The matrices
R
and
U
in (
2.13
) are seen to be the radial part and orientation of
X
, respectively and hence the decomposition
X=R^{1/2}U

is a generalization of the standard polar factorization for vectors (obtained for
\ell=1
).

Haar measure on the Stiefel manifold.

The family of Stiefel manifolds
O(n,\ell)
contains as special cases the
n
-shpere
O(n,1)=\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
and the orthogonal group
O(n,n)=O(n)
. The space
O(n,\ell)
is the compact manifold of dimension
n\ell-\ell-\ell(\ell-1)/2
realized as the homogeneous space
O(n)/O(n-\ell)
. The Stiefel manifold is endowed with a left and right-invariant Haar measure
\mu
, that is, for every
P\in O(n)
and every
Q\in O(\ell)
,

\displaystyle\mu(UP)=\mu(U)=\mu(QU),

for every
U\in O(n,\ell)
. Remark that our notation of
\mu
is independent of
\ell
and
n
, and should be understood from the context. We refer the reader for instance to
[
Chi03
]
or
[
Mui82
]
for details on the construction of such a measure.
The total volume of
O(n,\ell)
is given by (
[
Chi03
, Eq.(1.4.8)]
)

\displaystyle v(n,\ell):=\mu(O(n,\ell))=\int_{O(n,\ell)}\mu(dU)=\frac{2^{\ell}\pi^{n\ell/2}}{\Gamma_{\ell}(n/2)}.

The normalised measure

\displaystyle\tilde{\mu}(dU):=\frac{1}{v(n,\ell)}\mu(dU)

(2.14)

hence defines a left and right invariant probability measure on
O(n,\ell)
. We call it the
Haar probability measure
on
O(n,\ell)
.

2.3
Intrinsic volumes, mixed volumes and ellipsoids

Intrinsic volumes and mixed volumes.

We present two important notions from integral geometry: intrinsic and mixed volumes. We mainly follow
[
SW08
]
for this part (see in particular Section 14.2 therein).
For an integer
n\geq 1
, we denote by
\mathbb{K}^{n}
the set of convex bodies in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We write
\mathbb{B}_{n}
for the unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\mathrm{vol}_{n}
for the
n
-dimensional volume measure in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For
K\in\mathbb{K}^{n}
and
\varepsilon>0
, we write

\displaystyle K^{\varepsilon}:=K+\varepsilon\mathbb{B}_{n}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\mathrm{dist}(x,K)\leq\varepsilon\}

for the parallel body of
K
at distance
\varepsilon
.
Steiner’s formula
(
[
SW08
, Eq.(14.5)]
) asserts that its volume is a polynomial of degree
n
in
\varepsilon
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{vol}_{n}(K^{\varepsilon})=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\varepsilon^{n-j}\kappa_{n-j}V_{j}(K),

(2.15)

where the coefficients
\{V_{j}(K),j=0,\ldots,n\}
denote the
intrinsic volumes
of
K
. We set
V_{j}(\emptyset):=0
. For instance, when
n=2
,
V_{2}(K)
is the area,
V_{1}(K)
is half the boundary length and
V_{0}(K)
is the Euler characteristic of
K
. Moreover, for every
n\geq 1
, we have
V_{n}(K)=\mathrm{vol}_{n}(K)
, that is, the
n
-th intrinsic volume coincides with the
n
-dimensional volume measure.
The intrinsic volumes of the unit ball
\mathbb{B}_{n}
can be computed to be (
[
SW08
, Eq.(14.8)]
)

\displaystyle V_{j}(\mathbb{B}_{n})={n\choose j}\frac{\kappa_{n}}{\kappa_{n-j}},\quad\kappa_{n}=\frac{\pi^{n/2}}{\Gamma(1+n/2)}.

For an integer
1\leq j\leq n
, we denote by
G(n,j)
the Grassmannian of
j
-dimensional linear subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. It carries a unique invariant Haar probability measure
\nu_{n,j}
. One possible way to realize Grassmannians is as the quotient space
G(n,j)=O(n,j)/O(j)
, where two elements
U_{1},U_{2}\in O(n,j)
are equivalent if and only if there exists an orthogonal matrix
Q\in O(j)
such that
U_{1}=QU_{2}
, see for instance
[
Chi03
, p.8-9]
.
Intrinsic volumes admit a useful integral representation, known as
Kubota’s formula
(
[
SW08
, Eq.(6.11)]
),

\displaystyle V_{j}(K)={n\choose j}\frac{\kappa_{n}}{\kappa_{j}\kappa_{n-j}}\int_{G(n,j)}\mathrm{vol}_{j}(K|\mathscr{L})\nu_{n,j}(d\mathscr{L}),

(2.16)

where
K|\mathscr{L}
stands for the image of the orthogonal projection of
K
onto
\mathscr{L}\in G(n,j)
, and integration is with respect to the Haar probability measure on
G(n,j)
.

Let
m\geq 1
and consider
m
convex bodies
K_{1},\ldots,K_{m}\in\mathbb{K}^{n}
. Then, for real numbers
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{m}\geq 0
, the
n
-dimensional volume of the Minkowski sum
\lambda_{1}K_{1}+\ldots\lambda_{m}K_{m}
is a homogeneous polynomial of degree
n
in the variables
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{m}
(
[
SW08
, Eq.(14.7)]
),

\displaystyle\mathrm{vol}_{n}(\lambda_{1}K_{1}+\ldots\lambda_{m}K_{m})=\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}=1}^{m}V(K_{i_{1}},\ldots,K_{i_{n}})\lambda_{i_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{n}},

for uniquely determined symmetric coefficients
V(K_{i_{1}},\ldots,K_{i_{n}})
. These coefficients are called the
mixed volumes
of the convex bodies
K_{i_{1}},\ldots,K_{i_{n}}
. This formula is a generalization of Steiner’s formula in (
2.15
).
Whenever we have mixed volumes involving only two distinct convex bodies
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
, we use the short-hand notation

\displaystyle V(\underbrace{K_{1},\ldots,K_{1}}_{\ell\textrm{ times}},\underbrace{K_{2},\ldots,K_{2}}_{n-\ell\textrm{ times}})=:V(K_{1}[\ell],K_{2}[n-\ell]),\quad\ell\geq 1.

(2.17)

Intrinsic volumes of a convex body
K\in\mathbb{K}^{n}
are related to mixed volume by the relation (
[
SW08
, Eq.(14.18)]
)

\displaystyle V_{j}(K)=\frac{{n\choose j}}{\kappa_{n-j}}V(K[j],\mathbb{B}_{n}[n-j]),\quad j=1,\ldots,n

(2.18)

General facts about ellipsoids.

We will need some preliminaries about a particular type of convex bodies, namely ellipsoids, see e.g.
[
ZK12
]
.
Let
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be a non-singular symmetric matrix.
We define the ellipsoid
\mathcal{E}_{\Sigma}
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
represented by the matrix
\Sigma
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\Sigma}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:x^{T}\Sigma^{-1}x\leq 1\},

obtained as an affinity of the unit
n
-dimensional ball
\mathbb{B}_{n}
, that is
\mathcal{E}_{\Sigma}=\{\Sigma^{1/2}y:y\in\mathbb{B}_{n}\}
. In particular, its
n
-dimensional volume is given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{vol}_{n}(\mathcal{E}_{\Sigma})=\kappa_{n}\det(\Sigma)^{1/2}.

(2.19)

For any non-degenerate linear transformation represented by a matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, the ellipsoid
A\mathcal{E}_{\Sigma}=\{Ax:x\in\mathcal{E}_{\Sigma}\}

is represented by the matrix
A\Sigma A^{T}
, that is

\displaystyle A\mathcal{E}_{\Sigma}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:x^{T}(A\Sigma A^{T})^{-1}x\leq 1\}=\mathcal{E}_{A\Sigma A^{T}}.

Let
\mathscr{L}\in G(n,\ell)
be a
\ell
-dimensional linear subspace in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and denote by
L\in O(n,\ell)
any matrix whose rows form an orthonormal basis of
\mathscr{L}
. Then, the image of the orthogonal projection of
\mathcal{E}_{\Sigma}
onto
\mathscr{L}
, written
\mathcal{E}_{\Sigma}|\mathscr{L}
, is an ellipsoid in
\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
that is represented by the matrix
L\Sigma L^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times\ell}
. In particlar, it follows from (
2.19
) that its
\ell
-dimensional volume is

\mathrm{vol}_{\ell}(\mathcal{E}_{\Sigma}|\mathscr{L})=\kappa_{\ell}\det(L\Sigma L^{T})^{1/2}
.

3
Main results

3.1
Wiener-chaos expansion of matrix-variate functions

Hermite polynomials on the real line.

Let
m\geq 1
be an integer and
\mathbf{X}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{m})
be a standard
m
-dimensional Gaussian vector. For
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m})\in\mathbb{N}^{m}
, we write
\alpha!:=\alpha_{1}!\cdots\alpha_{m}!
and
|\alpha|:=\alpha_{1}+\ldots+\alpha_{m}
and define the
multivariate Hermite polynomials
associated with the vector
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{m})
as the tensor product of univariate Hermite polynomials, that is

\displaystyle H^{\otimes m}_{\alpha}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}):=\prod_{l=1}^{m}H_{{\alpha}_{l}}(X_{l}),

where
H_{\alpha_{l}}
denotes the Hermite polynomial of order
\alpha_{l}
on the real line. It is well-known that the normalised Hermite polynomials

\{(k!)^{-1/2}H_{k}:k\geq 0\}
form a complete orthonormal system of
L^{2}(\phi):=L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\phi(z)dz)
(see e.g.
[
NP12
]
).
This implies that the collection of normalised multivariate Hermite polynomials

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}_{[m]}=\left\{(\alpha!)^{-1/2}H^{\otimes m}_{\alpha}:\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{m}\right\}

(3.1)

form a complete orthonormal system of
L^{2}(\phi^{\otimes m})
, where
\phi^{\otimes m}
stands for the standard
m
-dimensional Gaussian measure. In particular, every random variable

F\in L^{2}(\phi^{\otimes m})
admits a unique decomposition

\displaystyle F=\sum_{k\geq 0}\sum_{|\alpha|=k}\widehat{F}(\alpha)H^{\otimes m}_{\alpha},

(3.2)

where

\displaystyle\widehat{F}(\alpha):=(\alpha!)^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}F(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})H^{\otimes m}_{\alpha}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})\phi^{\otimes m}(dx_{1},\ldots,dx_{m})

(3.3)

denotes the Fourier-Hermite coefficients of
F
associated with the multi-index
\alpha
. For
k\geq 0
, we write

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}_{k}^{\mathbf{X}}=\overline{\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{R}}}\{H_{\alpha}^{\otimes m}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}):|\alpha|=k\}

(3.4)

for the closed linear subspace of
L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
generated by multivariate Hermite polynomials of cumulative degree
k
. The space
\mathbf{C}_{k}^{\mathbf{X}}
is the so-called
k
-th Wiener chaos
associated with the vector
\mathbf{X}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{m})
. We have that
\mathbf{C}_{0}^{\mathbf{X}}=\mathbb{R}
. For
F\in L^{2}(\phi^{\otimes m})
, we denote by
\proj(F|\mathbf{C}_{k}^{\mathbf{X}})
the projection of
F
onto
\mathbf{C}_{k}^{\mathbf{X}}
, that is, (
3.2
) can be rewritten as the
L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
-converging series

\displaystyle F=\sum_{k\geq 0}\proj(F|\mathbf{C}_{k}^{\mathbf{X}}).

This decomposition is known as the
Wiener-Itô chaos expansion of
F
.

Matrix-variate Hermite polynomials.

Matrix-variate Hermite polynomials on the matrix space

\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times n}
are introduced in
[
Chi92
]
and admit an expansion in
zonal polynomials
. More specifically, the matrix-variate Hermite polynomials associated with the partition
\kappa\vdash k
of an integer
k\geq 0
, written
H_{\kappa}^{(\ell,n)}
, is given by (
[
Chi92
, Eq. (4.11)]
):

\displaystyle H^{(\ell,n)}_{\kappa}(X)=k!C_{\kappa}(\Id_{\ell})\sum_{s=0}^{k}\sum_{\sigma\vdash s}\sum_{\tau\vdash k-s}\frac{a^{\kappa}_{\tau,\sigma}}{(-2)^{k+s}(k-s)!}\frac{C_{\sigma}(XX^{T})}{s!(\frac{n}{2})_{\sigma}C_{\sigma}(\Id_{\ell})},

(3.5)

where the coefficients
a^{\kappa}_{\tau,\sigma}
are defined by the relation (
2.8
) and
(\ell/2)_{\sigma}
denotes the generalized Pochammer symbol, formally defined in (
2.7
). Zonal polynomials being generalizations of monomials, the expansion in (
3.5
) is to be compared to the classical expansion of univariate Hermite polynomials in the basis of monomials (see e.g.
[
NP12
, p.19]
)

\displaystyle H_{k}(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}\frac{k!(-1)^{n}}{n!(k-2n)!2^{n}}x^{k-2n}.

Alternatively,
H^{(\ell,n)}_{\kappa}
are defined by Rodrigues formula (
[
Chi92
, Eq.(4.9)]
)

\displaystyle H^{(\ell,n)}_{\kappa}(X)\phi^{(\ell,n)}(X)=4^{-k}\bigg(\frac{n}{2}\bigg)_{\kappa}^{-1}C_{\kappa}(\partial X\partial X^{T})\phi^{(\ell,n)}(X),

(3.6)

where, for
X=(X_{ij})\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times n}
, the differential matrix
\partial X
is given by

\partial X=\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial{X_{ij}}}\right)
.
We note that (
3.6
) is a generalization of the classical well-known Rodrigues formula for univariate Hermite polynomials (see for instance
[
NP12
, Proposition 1.4.2]
)

\displaystyle H_{k}(x)\phi(x)=(-1)^{k}\frac{d^{k}}{dx^{k}}\phi(x),\quad k\geq 0.

(3.7)

Matrix-variate Hermite polynomials are linked to the generalized matrix-variate Laguerre polynomials by the relation (
[
Chi92
, Eq.(5.16)]
and
[
Hay69
, Eq.(10)]
)

\displaystyle\gamma_{\kappa}\cdot L_{\kappa}^{(\frac{n-\ell-1}{2})}(XX^{T})=H^{(\ell,n)}_{\kappa}(\sqrt{2}X),\quad\gamma_{\kappa}:=(-2)^{-k}\bigg(\frac{n}{2}\bigg)_{\kappa}^{-1},\quad\kappa\vdash k.

(3.8)

Moreover, matrix-variate Hermite polynomials are orthonormal on

\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times n}
with respect to the matrix-normal density function
\phi^{(\ell,n)}
, that is (see e.g.
[
Hay69
, Corollary 3]
) for every integers
k,l\geq 0
and every partitions
\kappa\vdash k,\sigma\vdash l
,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times n}}H_{\kappa}^{(\ell,n)}(X)H_{\sigma}^{(\ell,n)}(X)\phi^{(\ell,n)}(X)(dX)=\mathbb{I}\left\{\kappa=\sigma\right\}\times 4^{-k}\bigg(\frac{n}{2}\bigg)_{\kappa}^{-1}k!C_{\kappa}(\Id_{\ell}).

(3.9)

Let now
X\sim\mathscr{N}_{\ell\times n}(0,\Id_{\ell}\otimes\Id_{n})
and write

s_{1},\ldots,s_{\ell}
for the eigenvalues of
XX^{T}
. The
spectral measure
of
XX^{T}
associated with the matrix
X
is the measure

\displaystyle\mu_{X}(ds):=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\delta_{s_{i}}(ds),

supported on the spectrum of
XX^{T}
, where
\delta_{y}
is the Dirac mass at
y
. We write
L^{2}(\mu_{X}):=L^{2}(\Omega,\sigma(\mu_{X}),\mathbb{P})
to indicate the subspace of
L^{2}(\phi^{(\ell,n)}):=L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times n},\phi^{(\ell,n)}(X)(dX))
consisting of those random variables that are measurable with respect to the sigma algebra generated by
\mu_{X}
. By this, we mean the subspace of
L^{2}(\phi^{(\ell,n)})
of random variables that are generated by elements of the type

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(t)\mu_{X}(dt)=:\mu_{X}(f).

(3.10)

Since matrix-variate Hermite polynomials in (
3.5
) admit an expansion into zonal polynomials, they are themselves symmetric functionals of the eigenvalues
s_{1},\ldots,s_{\ell}
.
This fact together with the orthogonality relation (
3.9
), implies that the family of normalised matrix-variate Hermite polynomials

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}_{[\ell\times n]}:=\left\{c(\kappa)^{-1/2}H_{\kappa}^{(\ell,n)}:\kappa\vdash k,k\geq 0\right\},\quad c(\kappa):=4^{-k}\bigg(\frac{n}{2}\bigg)_{\kappa}^{-1}k!C_{\kappa}(\Id_{\ell})

(3.11)

forms an orthonormal system in

L^{2}(\mu_{X})
.
In Appendix
A
, we prove the following Proposition, stating that this system is also complete in
L^{2}(\mu_{X})
.

Proposition 3.1
.

The system
\mathbb{H}_{[\ell\times n]}
is complete in
L^{2}(\mu_{X})
.

Therefore, every
F\in L^{2}(\mu_{X})
admits a unique decomposition in the basis (
3.11
),

\displaystyle F=\sum_{k\geq 0}\sum_{\kappa\vdash k}\widehat{F}(\kappa)H_{\kappa}^{(\ell,n)},

(3.12)

where

\displaystyle\widehat{F}(\kappa):=c(\kappa)^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times n}}F(X)H_{\kappa}^{(\ell,n)}(X)\phi^{(\ell,n)}(X)(dX)

(3.13)

is the Fourier-Hermite coefficient of
F
associated with the partition
\kappa
and
c(\kappa)
is as in (
3.11
).
To state our result, we introduce some further notation.
For an integer
s\geq 0
and
X\sim\mathscr{N}_{\ell\times n}(0,\Id_{\ell}\otimes\Id_{n})
, we recall the notation

t_{s}(X):=\Tr{([XX^{T}]^{s})}
introduced in (
2.5
) and define the spaces

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{0}^{X}:=\mathbb{R},\quad\mathcal{U}_{k}^{X}:=\overline{\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{R}}}\left\{\prod_{j=1}^{m}t_{s_{j}}(X):s_{1}+\ldots+s_{m}\leq k,m\geq 1\right\},\ k\geq 1,

where the closure is with respect to
L^{2}(\mu_{X})
.
By construction, we have that
\mathcal{U}_{k}^{X}\subset\mathcal{U}_{k+1}^{X}
. We let

\displaystyle\mathbf{U}_{k}^{X}:=\mathcal{U}_{k}^{X}\ominus\mathcal{U}_{k-1}^{X}:=\mathcal{U}_{k}^{X}\cap(\mathcal{U}_{k-1}^{X})^{\perp},

that is,
\mathbf{U}_{k}^{X}
is the space of those random variables in
\mathcal{U}_{k}^{X}
that are orthogonal in
L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
to elements of
\mathcal{U}_{k-1}^{X}
.
Expanding matrix-Hermite polynomials into zonal polynomials by (
3.5
) and subsequently zonal polynomials into monomials of the type
t_{s}(X)
by (
2.4
) shows that Hermite polynomials admit an expansion into monomials
t_{s}(X)
. In particular, since Hermite polynomials are orthogonal in view of (
3.9
), it follows that

\displaystyle\mathbf{U}_{k}^{X}=\overline{\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{R}}}\{H_{\kappa}^{(\ell,n)}(X):\kappa\vdash k\}.

The following result links matrix-variate Hermite polynomials with the classical Wiener-Itô decomposition in
(
3.2
). In particular, we establish an explicit formula for projection coefficients associated with radial functionals of the form
F(X)=f_{0}(XX^{T})\in L^{2}(\mu_{X})
where
X\sim\mathscr{N}_{\ell\times n}(0,\Id_{\ell}\otimes\Id_{n})
in terms of generalized Laguerre polynomials (see Section
2.1
for definitions). Such a formula is to be compared to
[
Tha93
,
Koc96
]
, where the authors study Hermite expansions of functions of the form
F(x)=f_{0}(\left\lVert X\right\rVert)P(x)
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where
P
is a harmonic polynomial.

Theorem 3.2
.

For integers
1\leq\ell\leq n
, let
X\sim\mathscr{N}_{\ell\times n}(0,\Id_{\ell}\otimes\Id_{n})
and write
\mathbf{X}=\mathrm{Vec}(X)
.
Then, for every integer
k\geq 0
and every partition
\kappa\vdash k
, we have that
H_{\kappa}^{(\ell,n)}(X)
is an element of
\mathbf{C}_{2k}^{\mathbf{X}}
and for every
F\in L^{2}(\mu_{X})
,

\displaystyle\proj(F|\mathbf{C}_{2k}^{\mathbf{X}})=\proj(F|\mathbf{U}_{k}^{X})=\sum_{\kappa\vdash k}\widehat{F}(\kappa)H_{\kappa}^{(\ell,n)}(X),

(3.14)

where
\widehat{F}(\kappa)
is as in (
3.13
). In particular, we have that
\proj(F|\mathbf{C}_{2k+1}^{\mathbf{X}})=0
. Moreover, if
F(X)=f_{0}(XX^{T})
, then

\displaystyle\widehat{F}(\kappa)=\frac{1}{2^{n\ell/2}\Gamma_{\ell}(\frac{n}{2})}\frac{(-2)^{k}}{k!C_{\kappa}(\Id_{\ell})}\times\int_{\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(\mathbb{R})}f_{0}(R)L_{\kappa}^{(\frac{n-\ell-1}{2})}(2^{-1}R)\mathrm{etr}\left(-2^{-1}R\right)\det(R)^{\frac{n-\ell-1}{2}}\nu(dR),

(3.15)

where
L_{\kappa}^{(\gamma)}
denotes the generalized Laguerre polynomial of order
\gamma>-1
associated with the partition
\kappa
, defined in (
2.10
) and
\nu(dR)
is the Lebesgue measure on
\mathcal{P}_{\ell}(\mathbb{R})
.

Our proof of Theorem
3.2
suggests that, combining the generalized Rodrigues formula (
3.6
) with the univariate Rodrigues formula (
3.7
), matrix-variate Hermite polynomials can be expressed in terms of multivariate Hermite polynomials. For instance, combining (
3.6
) with (
3.7
) in the case
\ell=n=1
(so that for every integer
k\geq 0
,

\kappa=(k)
is the only partition of
k
) and writing
\phi=\phi^{(1,1)}
for the standard Gaussian density function
yields for every
k\geq 0

\displaystyle H_{(k)}^{(1,1)}(X)\phi(X)=4^{-k}\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)_{(k)}^{-1}C_{(k)}([\partial X]^{2})\phi(X)

\displaystyle=4^{-k}\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)_{k}^{-1}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial X}\right)^{2k}\phi(X)=4^{-k}\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)_{k}^{-1}H_{2k}(X)\phi(X),

where we used that
\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)_{(k)}=\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)_{k},C_{(k)}(a)=a^{k}
for
a\in\mathbb{R}
and the Rodrigues formula for classical Hermite polynomials in (
3.7
). This shows in particular that

\displaystyle H_{(k)}^{(1,1)}(X)=4^{-k}\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)_{k}^{-1}H_{2k}(X).

Proceeding similarly for arbitrary dimensions
\ell
and
n
, we compute the first matrix-variate Hermite polynomials associated with partitions of order up to 2 to be

\displaystyle H_{(1)}^{(\ell,n)}(X)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\sum_{j=1}^{n}H_{2}(X_{ij})\ ,

\displaystyle H_{(2)}^{(\ell,n)}(X)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{12n(n+2)}\bigg(3\sum_{i\in[\ell]}\sum_{j\in[n]}H_{4}(X_{ij})+3\sum_{i_{1}\neq i_{2}\in[\ell]}\sum_{j\in[n]}H_{2}(X_{i_{1}j})H_{2}(X_{i_{2}j})

\displaystyle+3\sum_{i\in[\ell]}\sum_{j_{1}\neq j_{2}\in[n]}H_{2}(X_{ij_{1}})H_{2}(X_{ij_{2}})+\sum_{i_{1}\neq i_{2}\in[\ell]}\sum_{j_{1}\neq j_{2}\in[n]}H_{2}(X_{i_{1}j_{1}})H_{2}(X_{i_{2}j_{2}})

\displaystyle+2\sum_{i_{1}\neq i_{2}\in[\ell]}\sum_{j_{1}\neq j_{2}\in[n]}H_{1}(X_{i_{1}j_{1}})H_{1}(X_{i_{2}j_{1}})H_{1}(X_{i_{1}j_{2}})H_{1}(X_{i_{2}j_{2}})\bigg)\ ,

\displaystyle H_{(1,1)}^{(\ell,n)}(X)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{6n(n-1)}\bigg(\sum_{i_{1}\neq i_{2}\in[\ell]}\sum_{j_{1}\neq j_{2}\in[n]}H_{2}(X_{i_{1}j_{1}})H_{2}(X_{i_{2}j_{2}})

(3.16)

\displaystyle-\sum_{i_{1}\neq i_{2}\in[\ell]}\sum_{j_{1}\neq j_{2}\in[n]}H_{1}(X_{i_{1}j_{1}})H_{1}(X_{i_{2}j_{1}})H_{1}(X_{i_{1}j_{2}})H_{1}(X_{i_{2}j_{2}})\bigg).

Combining the content of Theorem
3.2
with the orthogonality relation (
3.9
), allows one to derive variance expansions of spectral variables
F(X)\in L^{2}(\mu_{X})
where
X\sim\mathscr{N}_{\ell\times n}(0,\Id_{\ell}\otimes\Id_{n})
as a converging series in terms of its Fourier-Hermite coefficients.

Proposition 3.3
.

For integers
1\leq\ell\leq n
, let
X\sim\mathscr{N}_{\ell\times n}(0,\Id_{\ell}\otimes\Id_{n})
and
F(X)\in L^{2}(\mu_{X})
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}{\left[F(X)\right]}=\sum_{k\geq 1}\sum_{\kappa\vdash k}\frac{4^{k}(\frac{n}{2})_{\kappa}}{k!C_{\kappa}(\Id_{\ell})}\mathbb{E}\left[F(X)H_{\kappa}^{(\ell,n)}(X)\right]^{2},

where the convergence of the series is part of the conclusion.

3.2
Fourier-Hermite coefficients of Gaussian determinants as intrinsic volumes of ellipsoids

In this section, we consider rectangular Gaussian matrices
X
and provide the Wiener chaos expansion of determinants of the form
\det(XX^{T})^{1/2}
. In
[
ZK12
]
, Kabluchko and Zaporozhets consider the case where
X\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times n}
has centred independent rows with respective covariance matrices
\Sigma_{1},\ldots,\Sigma_{\ell}
,
and prove that (see in particular
[
ZK12
, Theorem 1.1]
)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\det(XX^{T})^{1/2}\right]=\frac{(n)_{\ell}}{(2\pi)^{\ell/2}\kappa_{n-\ell}}V(\mathcal{E}_{\Sigma_{1}},\ldots,\mathcal{E}_{\Sigma_{\ell}},\mathbb{B}_{n},\ldots,\mathbb{B}_{n}),

(3.17)

where
V(\mathcal{E}_{\Sigma_{1}},\ldots,\mathcal{E}_{\Sigma_{\ell}},\mathbb{B}_{n},\ldots,\mathbb{B}_{n})
denotes the mixed volume of the ellipsoids
\mathcal{E}_{\Sigma_{i}},i=1,\ldots,\ell
associated with matrices
\Sigma_{i}
and
\mathbb{B}_{n}
denotes the unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with volume
\kappa_{n}=\pi^{n/2}/\Gamma(1+n/2)
. We also refer the reader to
[
Vit91
, Theorem 3.2]
, where the author proves a similar formula linking the expected absolute determinant of a matrix with i.i.d. copies of a random vector to the volume of the zonoid associated with the random distribution.

In Theorem
3.6
below, we substantially extend the framework of Kabluchko and Zaporozhets to arbitrary projection coefficients associated with the Wiener chaos expansion of such Gaussian determinants in the case where the rows of
X
are i.i.d centred Gaussian vectors with the same covariance matrix
\Sigma
.

Let
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be a symmetric positive-definite matrix and
\{X^{(i)}=(X_{1}^{(i)},\ldots,X_{n}^{(i)}):i\in[\ell]\}
a collection of independent Gaussian vectors with covariance matrix
\Sigma
. We write
X\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times n}
for the matrix whose
i
-th row is
X^{(i)}
. It follows that
X
has distribution
\mathcal{N}_{\ell\times n}(0,\Id_{\ell}\otimes\Sigma)
with density function

\displaystyle\phi^{(\ell,n)}_{\Sigma}(X)=(2\pi)^{-n\ell/2}\det(\Sigma)^{-\ell/2}\mathrm{etr}\left(-2^{-1}X\Sigma^{-1}X^{T}\right).

(3.18)

As a consequence, the matrix

X\Sigma^{-1/2}
has the
\mathcal{N}_{\ell\times n}(0,\Id_{\ell}\otimes\Id_{n})
distribution (see e.g.
[
GN00
, Theorem 2.3.10]
).
Based on the matrix-variate Hermite polynomials
H_{\kappa}^{(\ell,n)}
and their orthogonality relation with respect to
\phi^{(\ell,n)}
in (
3.9
), we define

\displaystyle H^{(\ell,n)}_{\kappa}(X;\Sigma):=\det(\Sigma)^{\ell k}H_{\kappa}^{(\ell,n)}(X\Sigma^{-1/2}).

(3.19)

In particular, we note that
H_{\kappa}^{(\ell,n)}(\cdot,\Id_{n})=H_{\kappa}^{(\ell,n)}(\cdot)
. The following proposition shows that
H^{(\ell,n)}_{\kappa}(\cdot,\Sigma)
are orthogonal with respect to the density
\phi^{(\ell,n)}_{\Sigma}
in (
3.18
).

Proposition 3.4
.

For every integers
k,l\geq 0
and every partitions
\kappa\vdash k,\sigma\vdash l
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times n}}H^{(\ell,n)}_{\kappa}(X;\Sigma)H^{(\ell,n)}_{\sigma}(X;\Sigma)\phi^{(\ell,n)}_{\Sigma}(X)(dX)=\mathbb{I}\left\{\kappa=\sigma\right\}\times\det(\Sigma)^{2\ell k}4^{-k}\bigg(\frac{n}{2}\bigg)_{\kappa}^{-1}k!C_{\kappa}(\Id_{\ell}).

Therefore, the family of normalized polynomials

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}_{\Sigma}:=\left\{c(\kappa;\Sigma)^{-1/2}H_{\kappa}^{(\ell,n)}(\cdot;\Sigma):\kappa\vdash k\geq 0\right\},\quad c(\kappa;\Sigma):=\det(\Sigma)^{2\ell k}4^{-k}\bigg(\frac{n}{2}\bigg)_{\kappa}^{-1}k!C_{\kappa}(\Id_{\ell})

(3.20)

forms a complete orthonormal system of
L^{2}(\mu_{X})
, where
\mu_{X}
denotes the spectral measure of
XX^{T}
associated with
X
. Hence, for every

F\in L^{2}(\mu_{X})
, one has the expansion

\displaystyle F(X)=\sum_{k\geq 0}\sum_{\kappa\vdash k}\widehat{F}(\kappa;\Sigma)H_{\kappa}^{(\ell,n)}(X;\Sigma),

where the projection coefficients are given by

\displaystyle\widehat{F}(\kappa;\Sigma)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c(\kappa;\Sigma)^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times n}}F(X)H^{(\ell,n)}_{\kappa}(X;\Sigma)\phi_{\Sigma}^{(\ell,n)}(X)(dX)

(3.21)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c(\kappa;\Sigma)^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[F(X)H^{(\ell,n)}_{\kappa}(X;\Sigma)\right],\quad X\sim\mathscr{N}_{\ell\times n}(0,\Id_{\ell}\otimes\Sigma).

The next result provides an explicit formula for the projection coefficients
\widehat{F}(\kappa;\Sigma)
in the special case where
F(X)=\det(XX^{T})^{1/2}
.

Theorem 3.5
.

For integers
1\leq\ell\leq n
and
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
positive-definite symmetric, let
X\sim\mathscr{N}_{\ell\times n}(0,\Id_{\ell}\otimes\Sigma)
. Then,
F(X)=\det(XX^{T})^{1/2}
is an element of
L^{2}(\mu_{X})
, and one has the decomposition

\displaystyle F(X)=\sum_{k\geq 0}\sum_{\kappa\vdash k}\widehat{F}(\kappa;\Sigma)H_{\kappa}^{(\ell,n)}(X;\Sigma),

where the Fourier-Hermite coefficients of
F
are given by the formula

\displaystyle\widehat{F}(\kappa;\Sigma)=\frac{(-2)^{k}}{\det(\Sigma)^{\ell k}k!}\bigg(\frac{n}{2}\bigg)_{\kappa}\sum_{s=0}^{k}\sum_{\sigma\vdash s}{\kappa\choose\sigma}(-1)^{s}\frac{(\frac{n+1}{2})_{\sigma}}{(\frac{n}{2})_{\sigma}}

\displaystyle\times\det(\Sigma)^{-\ell/2}2^{\ell/2}\frac{\Gamma_{\ell}(\frac{n+1}{2})}{\Gamma_{\ell}(\frac{n}{2})}\int_{O(n,\ell)}\det(U\Sigma^{-1}U^{T})^{-(n+1)/2}\tilde{\mu}(dU).

(3.22)

Here,
{\kappa\choose\sigma}
denote the generalized binomial coefficients defined by (
2.3
), and
\tilde{\mu}
stands for the Haar probability measure on the Stiefel manifold
O(n,\ell)
of
\ell
-frames in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

As anticipated, our next result yields a geometric interpretation of the projection coefficients
\widehat{F}(\kappa;\Sigma)
appearing in (
3.22
) in terms of mixed volumes and intrinsic volumes of ellipsoids (see Section
2.3
for preliminaries on these notions and in particular notation (
2.17
)).

Theorem 3.6
.

For integers
1\leq\ell\leq n
and
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
positive-definite symmetric, let
X\sim\mathscr{N}_{\ell\times n}(0,\Id_{\ell}\otimes\Sigma)
. Then, for
F(X)=\det(XX^{T})^{1/2}
, we have

\displaystyle\widehat{F}(\kappa;\Sigma)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(\kappa;\Sigma,\ell,n)\cdot V(\mathcal{E}_{\Sigma}[\ell],\mathbb{B}_{n}[n-\ell])

(3.23)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(\kappa;\Sigma,\ell,n)\cdot\kappa_{n-\ell}{n\choose\ell}^{-1}V_{\ell}(\mathcal{E}_{\Sigma}),

(3.24)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(\kappa;\Sigma,\ell,n):=\frac{(-2)^{k}}{\det(\Sigma)^{\ell k}k!}\bigg(\frac{n}{2}\bigg)_{\kappa}\sum_{s=0}^{k}\sum_{\sigma\vdash s}{\kappa\choose\sigma}(-1)^{s}\frac{(\frac{n+1}{2})_{\sigma}}{(\frac{n}{2})_{\sigma}}\frac{(n)_{\ell}}{(2\pi)^{\ell/2}\kappa_{n-\ell}}

and
where
V(\cdot,\ldots,\cdot)
and
V_{\ell}(\cdot)
stand for the mixed and
\ell
-th intrinsic volumes, respectively (see also notation (
2.17
)),
\mathbb{B}_{n}
denotes the unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\kappa_{n}=\pi^{n/2}/\Gamma(1+n/2)
denotes its volume.
In particular, for
\kappa=(0)
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\det(XX^{T})^{1/2}\right]=\frac{(n)_{\ell}}{(2\pi)^{n\ell/2}\kappa_{n-\ell}}V(\mathcal{E}_{\Sigma}[\ell],\mathbb{B}_{n}[n-\ell])=\frac{(n)_{\ell}}{(2\pi)^{\ell/2}}{n\choose\ell}^{-1}V_{\ell}(\mathcal{E}_{\Sigma}).

(3.25)

Remark 3.7
.

(a)

We point out that (
3.25
) coincides with (
3.17
) in the case where

\Sigma_{i}=\Sigma
for
i=1,\ldots,\ell
. In this sense, relations (
3.23
) and (
3.24
) therefore considerably generalize the content of Theorem 1.1 in
[
ZK12
]
to arbitrary chaotic projection coefficients
\hat{F}(\kappa;\Sigma)
associated with partitions
\kappa
of order
k\geq 1
.

(b)

Our proof of Theorem
3.6
suggests the following new relation for intrinsic volumes of ellipsoids

\displaystyle V_{\ell}(\mathcal{E}_{\Sigma})={n\choose\ell}\frac{\kappa_{n}}{\kappa_{n-\ell}}\det(\Sigma)^{-\ell/2}\int_{O(n,\ell)}\det(U\Sigma^{-1}U^{T})^{-(n+1)/2}\tilde{\mu}(dU),\quad 1\leq\ell\leq n,

where
\tilde{\mu}
indicates the Haar probability measure on the Stiefel manifold
O(n,\ell)
.

(c)

In Section
4.2.1
, we sketch an attempt to further generalize the findings of Kabluchko and Zaporozhets to the more general setting where the rows of
X
are independent with respective covariance matrices
\Sigma_{1},\ldots,\Sigma_{\ell}
. As we will explain, we are not successful to adapt our techniques employed in the proof of Theorems
3.5
and
3.6
to this more general framework. Such a difficulty may be explained by the fact that the polynomials defined in (
4.17
) are not easily tractable for matrix calculus, as we have to deal with each row separately.

The following Corollary is obtained from Theorem
3.5
applied with
\Sigma=\Id_{n}
, that is, when
X
has independent rows with independent coordinates. In this case, we have
H_{\kappa}^{(\ell,n)}(X;\Id_{n})=H_{\kappa}^{(\ell,n)}(X)
and
\widehat{F}(\kappa;\Id_{n})=\widehat{F}(\kappa)
as in (
3.13
).

Corollary 3.8
.

For integers
1\leq\ell\leq n
, let
X\sim\mathscr{N}_{\ell\times n}(0,\Id_{\ell}\otimes\Id_{n})
. Then,
F(X)=\det(XX^{T})^{1/2}
is an element of
L^{2}(\mu_{X})
, and one has the decomposition

\displaystyle F(X)=\sum_{k\geq 0}\sum_{\kappa\vdash k}\widehat{F}(\kappa)H_{\kappa}^{(\ell,n)}(X),

where the Fourier-Hermite coefficients of
F
are given by the formula

\displaystyle\widehat{F}(\kappa)=2^{\ell/2}\frac{\Gamma_{\ell}(\frac{n+1}{2})}{\Gamma_{\ell}(\frac{n}{2})}\frac{(-2)^{k}}{k!}\bigg(\frac{n}{2}\bigg)_{\kappa}\sum_{s=0}^{k}\sum_{\sigma\vdash s}{\kappa\choose\sigma}(-1)^{s}\frac{(\frac{n+1}{2})_{\sigma}}{(\frac{n}{2})_{\sigma}}.

(3.26)

In particular,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\det(XX^{T})^{1/2}\right]=2^{\ell/2}\frac{\Gamma_{\ell}(\frac{n+1}{2})}{\Gamma_{\ell}(\frac{n}{2})}.

(3.27)

Remark 3.9
.

(a)

Combining the contents of Corollary
3.8
and Theorem
3.2
, we see that (
3.26
) provides the chaotic projection coefficients associated with the Wiener-chaos decomposition of
\det(XX^{T})^{1/2}
. In Section
3.4
, we consider functionals of multi-dimensional Gaussian fields arising in stochastic geometry, that admit a certain integral representation in terms of Jacobian determinants, and
effectively use formula (
3.26
) to obtain a compact expression of their Wiener-Itô chaos expansions.

(b)

Formula (
3.27
) is to be compared with the definition of
\alpha(\ell,n)
in
[
Not21
, Eq.(1.8)]
and in particular with Remark 1.2 (a) therein for a link to the so-called
flag coefficients

\displaystyle{n\brack\ell}:={n\choose\ell}\frac{\kappa_{n}}{\kappa_{n-\ell}\kappa_{\ell}},

also appearing in the Gaussian Kinematic formula (see for instance Chapter 13 in
[
AT07
]
). In particular, one has that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\det(XX^{T})^{1/2}\right]=\frac{\ell!\kappa_{\ell}}{(2\pi)^{\ell/2}}{n\brack\ell}.

3.3
Generalized Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroup

3.3.1
A Mehler-type representation

In this section, we provide the
equivalent counterpart on matrix spaces of the classical Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroup

\{P_{t}:t\geq 0\}
on
\mathbb{R}
defined via
Mehler’s formula
(see e.g.
[
NP12
, Theorem 2.8.2]
)

\displaystyle P_{t}f(x)=\mathbb{E}\left[f(e^{-t}x+\sqrt{1-e^{-2t}}X_{0})\right],\quad X_{0}\sim\mathscr{N}(0,1),\quad x\in\mathbb{R},\quad t\geq 0.

For an integer
d\geq 1
and
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, we write

\displaystyle P_{t}^{(d)}f(x)=\mathbb{E}\left[f(e^{-t}x+\sqrt{1-e^{-2t}}X_{0})\right],\quad X_{0}\sim\mathscr{N}_{d}(0,\Id_{d}),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\quad t\geq 0

(3.28)

for the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck operator in dimension
d
, in such a way that
P_{t}=P_{t}^{(1)}
.
We fix integers
1\leq\ell\leq n
, and define the space

\displaystyle\Pi(\ell,n)=\{f:\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times n}\to\mathbb{R}:f(XH)=f(X)\textrm{ for every }H\in O(n)\},

(3.29)

that is, an element of
\Pi(\ell,n)
is a matrix-variate function that is right-invariant under orthogonal transformations. For a diagonal matrix
A=\mathrm{diag}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
with
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\geq 0
and
f\in\Pi(\ell,n)
, we introduce the operator

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{t;A}^{(\ell,n)}f(X)=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{O(n)}f(XHe^{-tA}+X_{0}(\Id_{n}-e^{-2tA})^{1/2})\tilde{\mu}(dH)\bigg|X\right],\quad t\geq 0

(3.30)

where the expectation is taken with respect to
X_{0}\sim\mathscr{N}_{\ell\times n}(0,\Id_{\ell}\otimes\Id_{n})
, for a matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
,

\displaystyle e^{tM}=\sum_{p\geq 0}\frac{1}{p!}(tM)^{p}

denotes the matrix exponential of
M
, and
\tilde{\mu}
indicates the probability Haar measure on the orthogonal group
O(n)
.

The next result specifies the action of the operators

\mathcal{O}_{t;A}^{(\ell,n)}
for generic diagonal matrices
A
with non-negative entries on the class of matrix-variate Hermite polynomials and naturally complements the action of
P_{t}
on Hermite polynomials on the real line given by (see e.g.
[
NP12
, Proposition 1.4.2]
)

\displaystyle P_{t}H_{k}(x)=e^{-kt}H_{k}(x).

(3.31)

Theorem 3.10
.

For every diagonal matrix
A=\mathrm{diag}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
such that
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\geq 0
,
every integer
k\geq 0
and every partition

\kappa\vdash k
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{t;A}^{(\ell,n)}H_{\kappa}^{(\ell,n)}(X)=\frac{C_{\kappa}(e^{-2tA})}{C_{\kappa}(\Id_{n})}H_{\kappa}^{(\ell,n)}(X).

(3.32)

In particular, the family
\{\mathcal{O}_{t;A}^{(\ell,n)}:t\geq 0\}
is a semigroup on the class
\Pi(\ell,n)
if and only if
a_{1}=\ldots=a_{n}=a
. More precisely, in this case,
\mathcal{O}_{t;A}^{(\ell,n)}
coincides with
P_{at}^{(\ell n)}
on the class
\Pi(\ell,n)
.

From (
3.32
) it becomes clear that the polynomials
H_{\kappa}^{(\ell,n)}
are eigenfunctions of
\mathcal{O}_{t;A}^{(\ell,n)}
with respective eigenvalue
C_{\kappa}(e^{-2tA})C_{\kappa}(\Id_{n})^{-1}
. Moreover, if
F\in L^{2}(\mu_{X})
admits the expansion (
3.12
),
then
\mathcal{O}_{t;A}^{(\ell,n)}F\in L^{2}(\mu_{X})
and

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{t;A}^{(\ell,n)}F=\sum_{k\geq 0}\sum_{\kappa\vdash k}\widehat{F}(\kappa)\frac{C_{\kappa}(e^{-2tA})}{C_{\kappa}(\Id_{n})}H_{\kappa}^{(\ell,n)},

that is, the projection coefficients of
\mathcal{O}_{t;A}^{(\ell,n)}F
are obtained from those of
F
by multiplying by
C_{\kappa}(e^{-2tA})C_{\kappa}(\Id_{n})^{-1}
. Let us make some remarks about Theorem
3.10
.

Remark 3.11
.

(a)

Using the fact that
H_{\kappa}^{(\ell,n)}
is an element of the class
\Pi(\ell,n)
(as can be seen for instance from (
3.8
)),
we deduce from (
3.32
) applied with
A=\Id_{n}
that

\displaystyle P_{t}^{(\ell n)}H_{\kappa}^{(\ell,n)}(X)=\mathcal{O}_{t;\Id_{n}}^{(\ell,n)}H_{\kappa}^{(\ell,n)}(X)=\frac{C_{\kappa}(e^{-2t\Id_{n}})}{C_{\kappa}(\Id_{n})}H_{\kappa}^{(\ell,n)}(X)=e^{-2kt}H_{\kappa}^{(\ell,n)}(X),

(3.33)

where we used that
C_{\kappa}(e^{-2t\Id_{n}})=e^{-2kt}C_{\kappa}(\Id_{n})
by homogeneity.
Recalling that
H_{\kappa}^{(\ell,n)}(X)
is an element of the
2k
-th Wiener chaos associated with
\mathrm{Vec}(X)
, it is clear that the classical Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroup
\{P_{t}^{(\ell n)}:t\geq 0\}
acts on the entries
X_{ij}
of
X
via the relation

P_{t}^{(\ell n)}H_{\kappa}^{(\ell,n)}(X)=e^{-2kt}H_{\kappa}^{(\ell,n)}(X)
, which is consistent with (
3.33
).

(b)

Let us assume that
A=\mathrm{diag}(a,\ldots,a),a\geq 0
. Then the relation in (
3.32
) reduces to

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{t;A}^{(\ell,n)}H_{\kappa}^{(\ell,n)}(X)=e^{-2ta}H_{\kappa}^{(\ell,n)}(X),

in view of the relation
C_{\kappa}(e^{-2tA})=e^{-2ta}C_{\kappa}(\Id_{n})
. In particular, from this identity, one can directly verify the semigroup property verified by
\mathcal{O}_{t;A}^{(\ell,n)}
on matrix-Hermite polynomials, as for every
s,t\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{t+s;A}^{(\ell,n)}H_{\kappa}^{(\ell,n)}(X)=e^{-2(t+s)a}H_{\kappa}^{(\ell,n)}(X)=e^{-2ta}e^{-2sa}H_{\kappa}^{(\ell,n)}(X)=\mathcal{O}_{t;A}^{(\ell,n)}\mathcal{O}_{s;A}^{(\ell,n)}H_{\kappa}^{(\ell,n)}(X).

Combining this relation with
(
3.32
) in particular suggests the identity

\displaystyle\frac{C_{\kappa}(e^{-2(t+s)A})}{C_{\kappa}(\Id_{n})}=\frac{C_{\kappa}(e^{-2tA})C_{\kappa}(e^{-2sA})}{C_{\kappa}(\Id_{n})^{2}},

which fails to hold in the case where the diagonal entries of
A
are not all equal. Indeed, for simplicity a direct computation in the case
\ell=n=2,\kappa=(1),a_{1}=1,a_{2}=2
shows that the left and right-hand sides of the above relation are respectively given by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}[e^{-2(t+s)}+e^{-4(t+s)}],\quad\frac{1}{4}e^{-2t}e^{-4s},

which are different.

3.3.2
An extension of the orthogonality relation for matrix-variate Hermite polynomials

It is well-known that for jointly standardized Gaussian random variables
X,Y
such that
\mathbb{E}\left[XY\right]=\rho
, the
univariate Hermite polynomials on the real line satisfy the orthogonality relation (see e.g.
[
NP12
, Proposition 2.2.1]
)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[H_{k}(X)H_{l}(Y)\right]=\mathbb{I}\left\{k=l\right\}\times k!\rho^{k}.

(3.34)

Exploiting the action of the operator
\mathcal{O}_{t;A}^{(\ell,n)}
on matrix-variate Hermite polynomials derived in Theorem
3.10
allows us to establish the matrix-counterpart of the orthogonality relation (
3.34
)
in the setting where the correlation of the Gaussian matrix entries
X
and
Y
is reflected in a matrix
R
.
This is the content of the following Theorem.

Theorem 3.12
.

Let
X,X_{0}\sim\mathscr{N}_{\ell\times n}(0,\Id_{\ell}\otimes\Id_{n})
be independent and
R
be a deterministic matrix of dimension
n\times n
. Let
Y\overset{\mathcal{L}}{=}XR+X_{0}(\Id_{n}-R^{2})^{1/2}
in distribution. Then, for every integers
k,l\geq 0
and every partitions
\kappa\vdash k,\sigma\vdash l
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[H_{\kappa}^{(\ell,n)}(X)H_{\sigma}^{(\ell,n)}(Y)\right]=\mathbb{I}\left\{\kappa=\sigma\right\}\times 4^{-k}\bigg(\frac{n}{2}\bigg)_{\kappa}^{-1}k!C_{\kappa}(R^{2})\frac{C_{\kappa}(\Id_{\ell})}{C_{\kappa}(\Id_{n})}.

(3.35)

Some remarks concerning Theorem
3.12
are in order.

Remark 3.13
.

(a)

By independence of
X
and
X_{0}
and the distributional identity
Y\overset{\mathcal{L}}{=}XR+X_{0}(\Id_{n}-R^{2})^{1/2}
, we have that for every
i,i^{\prime}\in[\ell],j,j^{\prime}\in[n]
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[X_{ij}Y_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}\right]=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[X_{ij}X_{i^{\prime}k}\right]R_{kj^{\prime}}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{I}\left\{i=i^{\prime},j=k\right\}R_{kj^{\prime}}=\mathbb{I}\left\{i=i^{\prime}\right\}R_{jj^{\prime}},

where we used that
X\sim\mathscr{N}_{\ell\times n}(0,\Id_{\ell}\otimes\Id_{n})
, yielding that, for every
j,j^{\prime}=1,\ldots,n,|R_{jj^{\prime}}|\leq 1
by virtue of the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. The above observation implies that
R
is necessarily symmetric and positive-semidefinite as a covariance matrix, and therefore has non-negative eigenvalues
r_{1},\ldots,r_{n}
. Note that if
R=\Delta=\mathrm{diag}(r_{1},\ldots,r_{n})
is diagonal, we therefore necessarily have
|r_{j}|\leq 1
for every
j=1,\ldots,n,
so that
(\Id_{n}-R^{2})^{1/2}
is well-defined. Our arguments to prove Theorem
3.12
are based on the following general reduction argument: for
f,g\in\Pi(\ell,n)
, writing
R=O\Delta O^{T}
with
O\in O(n)
and
\Delta=\mathrm{diag}(r_{1},\ldots,r_{n})
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[f(X)g(XO\Delta O^{T}+X_{0}O(\Id_{n}-R^{2})^{1/2}O^{T})\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[f(XO)g(XO\Delta+X_{0}O(\Id_{n}-\Delta^{2})^{1/2})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[f(X)g(X\Delta+X_{0}(\Id_{n}-\Delta^{2})^{1/2})\right],

where we used the fact that
f(X)=f(XO)
and
g(XO^{T})=g(X)
since
f,g\in\Pi(\ell,n)
as well as the fact that
(XO,X_{0}O)\overset{\mathcal{L}}{=}(X,X_{0})
, showing in particular that

|r_{j}|\leq 1
for every
j=1,\ldots,n
.

(b)

We point out two particular cases of Theorem
3.12
: (1) if
R=\Id_{n}
, relation (
3.35
) reduces to the orthogonality of Hermite polynomials stated in (
3.9
) and
(2) when
R=\mathrm{diag}(\rho,\ldots,\rho)
, (
3.35
) gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[H_{\kappa}^{(\ell,n)}(X)H_{\sigma}^{(\ell,n)}(\rho X+\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}X_{0})\right]=\mathbb{I}\left\{\kappa=\sigma\right\}\times 4^{-k}\bigg(\frac{n}{2}\bigg)_{\kappa}^{-1}k!C_{\kappa}(\rho^{2}\Id_{n})\frac{C_{\kappa}(\Id_{\ell})}{C_{\kappa}(\Id_{n})}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{I}\left\{\kappa=\sigma\right\}\times 4^{-k}\bigg(\frac{n}{2}\bigg)_{\kappa}^{-1}k!\rho^{2k}C_{\kappa}(\Id_{\ell}),

(3.36)

where we used homogeneity of zonal polynomials. For completeness, in Example
3.14
we present three explicit examples of (
3.36
) for the Hermite polynomials in (
3.16
) by relying on product formulae for univariate Hermite polynomials.

(c)

Writing
\mathbf{X}=\mathrm{Vec}(X)
and
\mathbf{Y}=\mathrm{Vec}(Y)
, we know
by Theorem
3.2
that
H_{\kappa}^{(\ell,n)}(X)\in\mathbf{C}_{2k}^{\mathbf{X}}

and
H_{\kappa}^{(\ell,n)}(Y)\in\mathbf{C}_{2k}^{\mathbf{Y}}
. In particular by orthogonality of Wiener chaoses, it is clear that
H_{\kappa}^{(\ell,n)}(X)
and
H_{\sigma}^{(\ell,n)}(Y)
are orthogonal in
L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
when
k\neq l
. Remarkably relation (
3.35
) yields a stronger orthogonality in the sense that, even if
k=l
, the elements
H_{\kappa}^{(\ell,n)}(X)
and
H_{\sigma}^{(\ell,n)}(Y)
, belonging both to the Wiener chaos of order
2k
, are orthogonal as soon as
\kappa\neq\sigma
.

(d)

Combining relation (
3.8
) with (
3.35
), we deduce an extended orthogonality relation for generalized matrix-variate Laguerre polynomials

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[L_{\kappa}^{(\frac{n-\ell-1}{2})}(2^{-1}XX^{T})L_{\sigma}^{(\frac{n-\ell-1}{2})}(2^{-1}YY^{T})\right]=\gamma_{\kappa}^{-1}\gamma_{\sigma}^{-1}\mathbb{E}\left[H_{\kappa}^{(\ell,n)}(X)H_{\sigma}^{(\ell,n)}(Y)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{I}\left\{\kappa=\sigma\right\}\times\bigg(\frac{n}{2}\bigg)_{\kappa}^{-1}k!C_{\kappa}(R^{2})\frac{C_{\kappa}(\Id_{\ell})}{C_{\kappa}(\Id_{n})},

where we used that

\gamma_{\kappa}:=(-2)^{-k}(\frac{n}{2})_{\kappa}^{-1}
, thus extending the orthogonality relation in (
2.11
) obtained for
R=\Id_{n}
.

Example 3.14
.

In this example, we explicitly compute the covariance

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[H_{\kappa}^{(\ell,n)}(X)H_{\sigma}^{(\ell,n)}(Y)\right],\quad Y:=\rho X+\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}X_{0}

in the three examples (i)
\kappa=\sigma=(1)
, (ii)
\kappa=(2),\sigma=(1,1)
and (iii)

\kappa=\sigma=(1,1)

by relying on the explicit expansions of the corresponding matrix-Hermite polynomials in terms of univariate Hermite polynomials in (
3.16
) and moment formulae for products of the latter. Our computations developed below are consistent with (
3.36
).
In view of the covariance structure between
X
and
Y
, we have that
\mathbb{E}\left[X_{ij}Y_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}\right]=\mathbb{I}\left\{i=i^{\prime},j=j^{\prime}\right\}\rho
.
We start with (i). Using the covariance structure together with the expression for
H_{(1)}^{(\ell,n)}
in (
3.16
) yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[H_{(1)}^{(\ell,n)}(X)H_{(1)}^{(\ell,n)}(Y)\right]=\frac{1}{4n^{2}}\sum_{i_{1},i_{2}\in[\ell]}\sum_{j_{1},j_{2}\in[n]}\mathbb{E}\left[H_{2}(X_{i_{1}j_{1}})H_{2}(Y_{i_{2}j_{2}})\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho^{2}}{2n^{2}}\sum_{i_{1},i_{2}\in[\ell]}\sum_{j_{1},j_{2}\in[n]}\mathbb{I}\left\{i_{1}=i_{2},j_{1}=j_{2}\right\}=\frac{\rho^{2}}{2n^{2}}\ell n=\rho^{2}\frac{\ell}{2n},

where we used (
3.34
).
On the other hand, using that
(n/2)_{(1)}=n/2
and

which is a sum of ten terms. First we recall the following relations for jointly standard Gaussian random variables

,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[H_{4}(N_{1})H_{2}(Z_{1})H_{2}(Z_{2})\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 24\mathbb{E}\left[N_{1}Z_{1}\right]^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[N_{1}Z_{2}\right]^{2},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[H_{2}(N_{1})Z_{1}Z_{2}\right]

\displaystyle 2\mathbb{E}\left[N_{1}Z_{1}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[N_{1}Z_{2}\right],

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[N_{1}N_{2}Z_{1}Z_{2}\right]

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[N_{1}Z_{1}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[N_{2}Z_{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[N_{1}Z_{2}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[N_{2}Z_{1}\right].

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[A_{i}(X)B_{j}(Y)\right]=0,\quad\forall(i,j)\notin\{(5,2),(4,1)\}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[H_{(1,1)}^{(\ell,n)}(X)H_{(1,1)}^{(\ell,n)}(Y)\right]=\frac{1}{36n^{2}(n-1)^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[B_{i}(X)B_{j}(Y)\right]

On the other hand, computing

3.4
Applications to geometric functionals of Gaussian random fields

In this section, we apply our main results of Sections
3.1
,
3.2
and
3.3
to the study of geometric functionals of multidimensional Gaussian fields.

In Section
3.4.1
, we consider random variables admitting an integral representation in terms of Jacobian determinants associated with multi-dimensional Gaussian fields. We argue that such a definition can be interpreted as the
total variation
of vector-valued
functions, generalizing the classical definition of total variation of multi-variate functions. More specifically, in the setting of a certain matrix correlation structure between two Jacobian matrices, appearing notably in the study of Gaussian Laplace eigenfunctions, we exploit the findings of Theorem
3.12
to obtain a precise expression for the variance of the total variation in terms of integrals of zonal polynomials.

In Section
3.4.2
, we apply the general framework of Section
3.4.1
to vectors of independent arithmetic random waves with the same eigenvalue on the three-dimensional torus, and prove a CLT in the high-energy regime for their generalized total variation on the full torus.

In Section
3.4.3
, we consider the nodal volumes associated with vectors of independent arithmetic random waves on the three torus. In particular, we provide its Wiener-Itô chaos expansions in terms of both, multivariate and matrix-variate Hermite polynomials, and provide some insight for variance estimates of its chaotic components.

3.4.1
Generalized total variation of vector-valued functions

Let
n\geq 1
be an integer and consider a centred smooth Gaussian field
\mathfrak{f}=\{\mathfrak{f}(z):z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}

1\leq\ell\leq n

\ell

We define the following random variable.

(3.39)

\mathfrak{f}_{\ell}^{\prime}(z)
.

where

[

, Proposition 6.13]

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}_{\ell,n}(\mathfrak{f}_{\ell};U)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\ell}}\sigma_{y}(\mathfrak{f}_{\ell};U)dy,

denotes the
(n-\ell)

From now on,

In the following proposition, we compute the variance of the total variation of
\mathfrak{f}_{\ell}

Proposition 3.16

Let the above notation prevail. Assume furthermore that for every

In this section, we apply the general framework presented in Section

to the setting of vectors of independent arithmetic random waves on the three-torus,
\mathbb{T}^{3}

Arithmetic random waves on the
d
-torus.

Let
\mathbb{T}^{d}=\mathbb{R}^{d}/\mathbb{Z}^{d}=[0,1]/_{\sim},\ d\geq 2
denote the torus of dimension
d
. Arithmetic random waves on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
, first introduced in
[
RW08
,
ORW08
]
are Gaussian Laplace eigenfunctions satisfying

\displaystyle\Delta T_{n}(z)+E_{n}T_{n}(z)=0,\quad E_{n}=4\pi^{2}n,\ z\in\mathbb{T}^{d}

where

\displaystyle n\in S_{d}:=\left\{m\geq 1:\exists(m_{1},\ldots,m_{d})\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}:m_{1}^{2}+\ldots+m_{d}^{2}=m\right\},

that is,
n
is an integer expressible as a sum of
d
integer squares. The set of
frequencies
associated with
n\in S_{d}
is

\displaystyle\Lambda_{n}:=\left\{\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d})\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}:\lambda_{1}^{2}+\ldots+\lambda_{d}^{2}=n\right\},

and we write
|\Lambda_{n}|=:\mathcal{N}_{n}
for its cardinality, that is,
\mathcal{N}_{n}
is the number of ways in which
n
is represented as a sum of squares. An
L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
-basis of eigenfunctions is given by complex exponentials of the form
\{e_{\lambda}(\cdot):=\exp(2\pi i\langle\lambda,\cdot\rangle):\lambda\in\Lambda_{n}\}
, where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the standard Euclidean inner product on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For
n\in S_{d}
, the ARW with eigenvalue
E_{n}
is defined as random a linear combination of complex exponentials

\displaystyle T_{n}(z)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathcal{N}_{n}}}\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{n}}a_{\lambda}e_{\lambda}(z),

(3.43)

where the coefficients
\{a_{\lambda}:\lambda\in\Lambda_{n}\}
are independent standard complex Gaussian random variables save for the relation
a_{-\lambda}=\overline{a_{\lambda}}
, which makes
T_{n}
real-valued. Alternatively, ARWs are defined as the Gaussian process
\{T_{n}(z):z\in\mathbb{T}^{d}\}
on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
with covariance function

\displaystyle r^{(n)}(z,z^{\prime}):=\mathbb{E}\left[T_{n}(z)T_{n}(z^{\prime})\right]=\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}_{n}}\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{n}}e_{\lambda}(z-z^{\prime})=:r^{(n)}(z-z^{\prime}),\quad z,z^{\prime}\in\mathbb{T}^{d}.

(3.44)

Note that
r^{(n)}
only depends on the difference
z-z^{\prime}
, meaning that the random field
\{T_{n}(z):z\in\mathbb{T}^{d}\}
is stationary. Moreover, the fact that
r_{n}(0)=1
, implies that for every
z\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
,
T_{n}(z)
has variance one.

Total variation of vectors of ARW on
\mathbb{T}^{3}
.

For an integer
1\leq\ell\leq 3
and
n\in S_{3}
, we consider i.i.d copies
T_{n}^{(1)},\ldots,T_{n}^{(\ell)}
of
T_{n}
in (
3.43
) and consider the associated
\ell
-dimensional Gaussian field

\displaystyle\bT_{n}^{(\ell)}:=\left\{\bT_{n}^{(\ell)}(z)=(T_{n}^{(1)}(z),\ldots,T_{n}^{(\ell)}(z)):z\in\mathbb{T}^{3}\right\}.

(3.45)

Our specific goal is to study the high-energy behaviour of the total variation
\mathbf{V}(\bT_{n}^{(\ell)};\mathbb{T}^{3})
(as defined in (
3.39
)) of
\bT_{n}^{(\ell)}
on the full torus, that is when
\mathcal{N}_{n}\to\infty
.
Since for every
z\in\mathbb{T}^{3}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}{\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial{z_{j}}}T_{n}^{(i)}(z)\right]}=\frac{E_{n}}{3},\quad i\in[\ell],\ j\in[n],

we introduce the normalised partial derivatives

\displaystyle\tilde{\partial}_{j}T_{n}^{(i)}(z):=\left(\frac{E_{n}}{3}\right)^{-1/2}\frac{\partial}{\partial{z_{j}}}T_{n}^{(i)}(z),

(3.46)

with unit variance and write
\dot{\bT}_{n}^{(\ell)}(z)\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times n}
for the normalised Jacobian matrix of
\bT_{n}^{(\ell)}
.
According to (
3.39
), we use the homogeneity of the determinant in order to rewrite the total variation as

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}(\bT_{n}^{(\ell)};\mathbb{T}^{3})=\left(\frac{E_{n}}{3}\right)^{\ell/2}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\Phi(\dot{\bT}_{n}^{(\ell)}(z))dz,

(3.47)

where
\Phi(M)=\sqrt{\det(MM^{T})}
. Differentiating (
3.43
) and using the fact that
r^{(n)}(0)=1
implies that
\dot{\bT}_{n}^{(\ell)}(z)\sim\mathscr{N}_{\ell\times n}(0,\Id_{\ell}\otimes\Id_{n})
and is stochastically independent of
\bT_{n}^{(\ell)}(z)
for every
z\in\mathbb{T}^{3}
.
We furthermore adopt the notation

\displaystyle{r}^{(n)}_{j,j^{\prime}}(z):=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial z_{j}\partial z_{j}^{\prime}}r^{(n)}(z),\quad\tilde{r}^{(n)}_{j,j^{\prime}}(z):=\left(\frac{E_{n}}{3}\right)^{-1}r^{(n)}_{j,j^{\prime}}(z).

(3.48)

The statement of our result is divided into three parts: (i) gives the expected total variation of vector-valued ARWs on the full torus, (ii) is an exact variance asymptotic and (iii) is a Central Limit Theorem in the high-energy regime for the normalised total variation

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{V}}(\bT_{n}^{(\ell)};\mathbb{T}^{3}):=\frac{\mathbf{V}(\bT_{n}^{(\ell)};\mathbb{T}^{3})-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{V}(\bT_{n}^{(\ell)};\mathbb{T}^{3})\right]}{\mathrm{Var}{\left[\mathbf{V}(\bT_{n}^{(\ell)};\mathbb{T}^{3})\right]}^{1/2}}.

(3.49)

Theorem 3.17
.

Let the above notation prevail.

(i)

(Expected total variation)
For every
n\in S_{3}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{V}(\bT_{n}^{(\ell)};\mathbb{T}^{3})\right]=\left(\frac{E_{n}}{3}\right)^{\ell/2}2^{\ell/2}\frac{\Gamma_{\ell}(2)}{\Gamma_{\ell}(\frac{3}{2})}

(3.50)

(ii)

(Asymptotic variance)
As
n\to\infty,{n}\not\equiv{0,4,7}\pmod{8}
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}{\left[\mathbf{V}(\bT_{n}^{(\ell)};\mathbb{T}^{3})\right]}=\left(\frac{E_{n}}{3}\right)^{\ell}2^{\ell}\frac{\Gamma_{\ell}(2)^{2}}{\Gamma_{\ell}(\frac{3}{2})^{2}}\frac{\ell}{2\mathcal{N}_{n}}\left(1+O(n^{-1/28+o(1)})\right)

(3.51)

(iii)

(CLT)
As
n\to\infty,{n}\not\equiv{0,4,7}\pmod{8}
,

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{V}}(\bT_{n}^{(\ell)};\mathbb{T}^{3})\xrightarrow{\mathcal{L}}\mathscr{N}(0,1),

(3.52)

where

denotes convergence in distribution.

We remark that (

\displaystyle 4^{-k}\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)_{\kappa}^{-1}\sum_{|\alpha|=2k}z_{\alpha}^{\kappa}\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}\prod_{j=1}^{n}[\phi(X_{ij})]^{-1}\frac{\partial^{\alpha_{ij}}}{\partial X_{ij}^{\alpha_{ij}}}\phi(X_{ij}).

An attempt at generalizing to distinct covariance matrices

In this section, we try to generalize the results of Theorem

and Theorem

to the more general setting where the rows of

are independent Gaussian vectors with distinct covariance matrices.

Let
\{\Sigma_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}:i\in[\ell]\}
be positive-definite symmetric matrices and
\{X^{(i)}=(X^{(i)}_{1},\ldots,X^{(i)}_{n}):i\in[\ell]\}
a collection of
\ell
independent Gaussian vectors with respective covariance matrices
\Sigma_{1},\ldots,\Sigma_{\ell}
.
We write
X
for the
\ell\times n
matrix whose
i
-th row is
X^{(i)}
.
Then, the vector
\mathrm{Vec}(X^{T})
has the multivariate normal distribution
\mathcal{N}_{\ell n}(0,\Omega)
, where

\displaystyle\Omega=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\left(e_{i}e_{i}^{T}\otimes\Sigma_{i}\right)=\mathrm{diag}(\Sigma_{1},\ldots,\Sigma_{\ell})=\Sigma_{1}\oplus\ldots\oplus\Sigma_{\ell},

with
e_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
denoting the
i
-th canonical basis vector. The density function of
X
is given by

\displaystyle\phi_{\Omega}(\mathrm{Vec}(X^{T}))=(2\pi)^{-n\ell/2}\det(\Omega)^{-n\ell/2}\mathrm{etr}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\Omega^{-1}\mathrm{Vec}(X^{T})\mathrm{Vec}(X^{T})^{T}\right).

If
X
is distributed as above, a computation shows that the
\ell\times n
matrix

\displaystyle Y_{X}:=(\Id_{\ell}\otimes\mathrm{Vec}(X^{T})^{T}\Omega^{-1/2})(\mathrm{Vec}(\Id_{\ell})\otimes\Id_{n})

has the standard matrix normal distribution. Therefore, we consider the matrix-variate polynomials

\displaystyle H^{(\ell,n)}_{\kappa}(X;\Omega)=\det(\Omega)^{k}H_{\kappa}^{(\ell,n)}(Y_{X}),\quad\kappa\vdash k

(4.17)

satisfying the orthogonality relation (similar as in the proof of Proposition
3.4
)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times n}}H^{(\ell,n)}_{\kappa}(X;\Omega)H^{(\ell,n)}_{\sigma}(X;\Omega)\phi_{\Omega}(\mathrm{Vec}(X^{T}))(dX)=\mathbb{I}\left\{\kappa=\sigma\right\}\times\det(\Omega)^{2k}4^{-k}\bigg(\frac{n}{2}\bigg)_{\kappa}^{-1}k!C_{\kappa}(\Id_{\ell}),

and thus the family

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}_{\Omega}:=\left\{c(\kappa;\Omega)^{-1/2}H_{\kappa}^{(\ell,n)}(\cdot;\Omega):\kappa\vdash k\geq 0\right\},\quad c(\kappa;\Omega):=\det(\Omega)^{2k}4^{-k}\bigg(\frac{n}{2}\bigg)_{\kappa}^{-1}k!C_{\kappa}(\Id_{\ell})

forms an orthonormal system of
L^{2}(\mu_{X})
, where, as usual,
\mu_{X}
indicates the spectral measure associated with
XX^{T}
. Expanding the function
F(X)=\det(XX^{T})^{1/2}\in L^{2}(\mu_{X})
in the basis
\mathbb{H}_{\Omega}
, using the relation

\displaystyle\phi_{\Omega}(\mathrm{Vec}(X^{T}))=\det(\Omega)^{-1/2}\phi^{(\ell,n)}(Y_{X})

and the definition of
H_{\kappa}^{(\ell,n)}(\cdot;\Omega)
, we have that the associated projection coefficients are

\displaystyle\widehat{F}(\kappa;\Omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c(\kappa;\Omega)^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times n}}F(X)H^{(\ell,n)}_{\kappa}(X;\Omega)\phi_{\Omega}(\mathrm{Vec}(X^{T}))(dX)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\det(\Omega)^{k-1/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times n}}F(X)H_{\kappa}^{(\ell,n)}(Y_{X})\phi^{(\ell,n)}(Y_{X})(dX)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\det(\Omega)^{k-1/2}\gamma_{\kappa}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times n}}F(X)L_{\kappa}^{(\frac{n-\ell-1}{2})}(2^{-1}Y_{X}Y_{X}^{T})(2\pi)^{-n\ell/2}\mathrm{etr}\left(-2^{-1}Y_{X}Y_{X}^{T}\right)(dX).

The idea is now to perform the polar change of variables
X=R^{1/2}U
. In order to do so, we compute
\Tr(Y_{X}Y_{X}^{T})
:

\displaystyle\Tr(Y_{X}Y_{X}^{T})=\Tr(\Omega^{-1}\mathrm{Vec}(X^{T})\mathrm{Vec}(X^{T})^{T})=\mathrm{Vec}(X^{T})^{T}\Omega^{-1}\mathrm{Vec}(X^{T})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Vec}(X^{T})^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\left(e_{i}e_{i}^{T}\otimes\Sigma_{i}^{-1}\right)\mathrm{Vec}(X^{T})=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\mathrm{Vec}(X^{T})^{T}\left(e_{i}e_{i}^{T}\otimes\Sigma_{i}^{-1}\right)\mathrm{Vec}(X^{T}).

Then, using the relation

\mathrm{Vec}(S)^{T}(BD\otimes E)\mathrm{Vec}(S)=\Tr(DS^{T}ESB)
(see e.g.
[
GN00
, Theorem 1.2.22]
),
we obtain

\displaystyle\Tr(Y_{X}Y_{X}^{T})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\mathrm{Vec}(X^{T})^{T}\left(e_{i}e_{i}^{T}\otimes\Sigma_{i}^{-1}\right)\mathrm{Vec}(X^{T})=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\Tr\left(e_{i}^{T}R^{1/2}U\Sigma^{-1}_{i}U^{T}R^{1/2}e_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\Tr\left(e_{i}e_{i}^{T}R^{1/2}U\Sigma^{-1}_{i}U^{T}R^{1/2}\right)=\Tr\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}e_{i}e_{i}^{T}R^{1/2}U\Sigma^{-1}_{i}U^{T}R^{1/2}\right).

The difficulty to proceed now is the following: the above computation suggests that we cannot write
\Tr(Y_{X}Y_{X}^{T})
as
\Tr(AR)
for some matrix
A
, due to the fact that one cannot exploit the permutation invariance of the trace in view of
presence of the matrix
e_{i}e_{i}^{T}
. We remark that, when
\Sigma_{i}=\Sigma
for every

, the above formula gives
\Tr(Y_{X}Y_{X}^{T})=\Tr(\Id_{\ell}U\Sigma^{-1}U^{T})=\Tr(U\Sigma^{-1}U^{T})

3.5

2.12
), and thus hints to the fact that the polynomials

are not easily amenable to matrix calculus.

Proof of Theorem

We proceed in two steps. In view of Remark

is necessarily symmetric and has non-negative eigenvalues. We start by showing that (

Step 1:
R
is diagonal.
Let us first assume that

X\overset{\mathcal{L}}{=}XH,H\in O(n)
and using the fact that

for every

(as can be seen e.g. from (

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[H_{\kappa}^{(\ell,n)}(X)H_{\sigma}^{(\ell,n)}(XR+X_{0}(\Id_{n}-R^{2})^{1/2})\right]

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[H_{\kappa}^{(\ell,n)}(X)\mathbb{E}\left[H_{\sigma}^{(\ell,n)}(XR+X_{0}(\Id_{n}-R^{2})^{1/2})\big|X\right]\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{O(n)}H_{\kappa}^{(\ell,n)}(XH)\mathbb{E}\left[H_{\sigma}^{(\ell,n)}(XHR+X_{0}(\Id_{n}-R^{2})^{1/2})\big|X\right]\tilde{\mu}(dH)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[H_{\kappa}^{(\ell,n)}(X)\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{O(n)}H_{\sigma}^{(\ell,n)}(XHR+X_{0}(\Id_{n}-R^{2})^{1/2})\tilde{\mu}(dH)\bigg|X\right]\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[H_{\kappa}^{(\ell,n)}(X)\mathcal{O}_{1;R_{*}}^{(\ell,n)}H_{\sigma}^{(\ell,n)}(X)\right],

where
R_{*}:=\mathrm{diag}(\ln(1/r_{1}),\ldots,\ln(1/r_{n}))
.
Then, exploiting the action of
\mathcal{O}_{t;R_{*}}^{(\ell,n)}
on matrix-variate Hermite polynomials given in (

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[H_{\kappa}^{(\ell,n)}(X)H_{\sigma}^{(\ell,n)}(XR+X_{0}(\Id_{n}-R^{2})^{1/2})\right]=\frac{C_{\kappa}(e^{-2R_{*}})}{C_{\kappa}(\Id_{n})}\mathbb{E}\left[H_{\kappa}^{(\ell,n)}(X)H_{\sigma}^{(\ell,n)}(X)\right]

where we used that

and the orthogonality relation for Hermite polynomials (

Step 2:
R
is symmetric.

yielding

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[H_{\kappa}^{(\ell,n)}(X)H_{\sigma}^{(\ell,n)}(XO\Delta_{R}O^{T}+X_{0}O(\Id_{n}-\Delta_{R}^{2})^{1/2}O^{T})\right]

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[H_{\kappa}^{(\ell,n)}(X)H_{\sigma}^{(\ell,n)}((XO\Delta_{R}+X_{0}O(\Id_{n}-\Delta_{R}^{2})^{1/2})O^{T})\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[H_{\kappa}^{(\ell,n)}(XO)H_{\sigma}^{(\ell,n)}(XO\Delta_{R}+X_{0}O(\Id_{n}-\Delta_{R}^{2})^{1/2})\right]

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[H_{\kappa}^{(\ell,n)}(X)H_{\sigma}^{(\ell,n)}(X\Delta_{R}+X_{0}(\Id_{n}-\Delta_{R}^{2})^{1/2})\right],

where the last equality follows from the fact that the pair

where in the last line, we used the fact that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{V}(\bT_{n}^{(\ell)};Q_{0})^{2}\right]=O\left(E_{n}^{\ell}M^{-6}\right)=O(E_{n}^{\ell-3}).

Combining this with the estimate in (
4.37
) yields

\left|\Delta_{n,1}^{(\ell)}\right|\ll E_{n}^{3}\mathcal{R}_{n}(6)E_{n}^{\ell-3}\ll E_{n}^{\ell}\mathcal{R}_{n}(6)
. By
[
BM19
, Eq.(1.18)]
, we have that
\mathcal{R}_{n}(6)\ll\mathcal{N}_{n}^{-7/3+o(1)}
, as
n\to\infty,{n}\not\equiv{0,4,7}\pmod{8}
. Combining this with the estimate in Lemma
4.6
yields the desired conclusion.
∎

Lemma 4.10

(Non-singular part)
.

As
n\to\infty,{n}\not\equiv{0,4,7}\pmod{8}
, we have that

\displaystyle\left|\Delta_{n,2}^{(\ell)}\right|=o\left(\mathrm{Var}{\left[\mathbf{V}(\bT_{n}^{(\ell)};\mathbb{T}^{3})[2]\right]}\right).

Proof.

Using the expansion in matrix-Hermite polynomials and arguing as in the proof of Proposition
3.16
, we have that

\displaystyle\left|\Delta_{n,2}^{(\ell)}\right|\leq E_{n}^{\ell}\sum_{k\geq 2}\sum_{\kappa\vdash k}\widehat{\Phi}(\kappa)^{2}4^{-k}\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)^{-1}_{\kappa}k!\frac{C_{\kappa}(\Id_{\ell})}{C_{\kappa}(\Id_{3})}\sum_{(Q,Q^{\prime})\in\mathcal{S}^{c}}\int_{Q\times Q^{\prime}}\left|C_{\kappa}(R_{n}(x-y)^{2})\right|dxdy.

Now for a matrix
S\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
, we denote by
\rho(S):=\max(|\lambda_{1}|,\ldots,|\lambda_{m}|)
, where
\lambda_{i}
denote the eigenvalues of
S
.
We now use the following two facts: (i) For every partition
\kappa\vdash k
, every matrix
S\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
and every
x
such that
\rho(S)<x
, one has that
\left|C_{\kappa}(S)\right|\leq x^{k}C_{\kappa}(\Id_{m})
(see for instance
[
MPH95
]
, p.197) and (ii) by Gerschgorin’s Theorem (see for instance
[
GR14
]
, p.1084), writing
S=(s_{ij})
,

\displaystyle\rho(S)\leq\min\left(\max_{i=1,\ldots,m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}|s_{ij}|,\max_{j=1,\ldots,m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}|s_{ij}|\right)=:\tilde{\rho}(S).

Applying the facts above with the symmetric matrix
S=R_{n}(x-y)^{2}
and

x=2\tilde{\rho}(S)
yields

\displaystyle\left|\Delta_{n,2}^{(\ell)}\right|\leq E_{n}^{\ell}\sum_{k\geq 2}\sum_{\kappa\vdash k}\widehat{\Phi}(\kappa)^{2}4^{-k}\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)^{-1}_{\kappa}k!C_{\kappa}(\Id_{\ell})\sum_{(Q,Q^{\prime})\in\mathcal{S}^{c}}\int_{Q\times Q^{\prime}}\left(2\tilde{\rho}(R_{n}(x-y)^{2})\right)^{k}dxdy.

(4.38)

By definition of
\tilde{\rho}
and the triangular inequality, we have that

\displaystyle 2\tilde{\rho}(R_{n}(x-y)^{2})\leq 2\max_{i=1,2,3}\sum_{j,l=1}^{3}\left|\tilde{r}^{(n)}_{il}(x-y)\right|\left|\tilde{r}^{(n)}_{lj}(x-y)\right|,

(4.39)

which is bounded by
18\eta^{2}<1
on the non-singular regions.
Combining this with the fact that we are summing over integers

k\geq 2
yields

\displaystyle\sum_{(Q,Q^{\prime})\in\mathcal{S}^{c}}\int_{Q\times Q^{\prime}}\left(2\tilde{\rho}(R_{n}(x-y)^{2})\right)^{k}dxdy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{(Q,Q^{\prime})\in\mathcal{S}^{c}}\int_{Q\times Q^{\prime}}\left(2\tilde{\rho}(R_{n}(x-y)^{2})\right)^{k-2}\left(2\tilde{\rho}(R_{n}(x-y)^{2})\right)^{2}dxdy

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\left(\tilde{\rho}(R_{n}(z)^{2})\right)^{2}dz.

Combining (
4.39
) with the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and the estimate

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}[\tilde{r}^{(n)}_{j,j^{\prime}}(z)]^{2p}dz=O(\mathcal{R}_{n}(2p)),\quad p\geq 1,

where the constant involved in the ’big-O’ notation depends only on
p
(see for instance
[
Not21
, Lemma E.2]
), we deduce that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\left(\tilde{\rho}(R_{n}(z)^{2})\right)^{2}dz\ll\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\max_{i,l=1,2,3}\left|\tilde{r}^{(n)}_{il}(z)\right|^{2}\max_{j,l=1,2,3}\left|\tilde{r}^{(n)}_{jl}(z)\right|^{2}dz=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\max_{j,l=1,2,3}\left|\tilde{r}^{(n)}_{jl}(z)\right|^{4}dz\ll\mathcal{R}_{n}(4).

Therefore, in view of the estimate (
4.38
),
and the fact that
\Phi\in L^{2}(\mu_{X})
for
X\sim\mathscr{N}_{\ell\times n}(0,\Id_{\ell}\otimes\Id_{n})
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\left|\Delta_{n,2}^{(\ell)}\right|\ll E_{n}^{\ell}\mathcal{R}_{n}(4)\sum_{k\geq 2}\sum_{\kappa\vdash k}\widehat{\Phi}(\kappa)^{2}4^{-k}\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)^{-1}_{\kappa}k!C_{\kappa}(\Id_{\ell})\leq E_{n}^{\ell}\mathcal{R}_{n}(4)\mathbb{E}\left[\Phi(X)^{2}\right]\ll E_{n}^{\ell}\mathcal{R}_{n}(4).

Now, the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality implies that

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{n}(4)=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}[r^{(n)}(z)]^{4}dz\leq\left(\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}[r^{(n)}(z)]^{2}dz\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}[r^{(n)}(z)]^{6}dz\right)^{1/2}=\sqrt{\mathcal{R}_{n}(2)\mathcal{R}_{n}(6)}.

Using the estimates (see
[
BM19
, Eq.(1.16) and (1.18)]
)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{n}(2)=\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}_{n}},\quad\mathcal{R}_{n}(6)\ll\mathcal{N}_{n}^{-7/3+o(1)},\quad n\to\infty,{n}\not\equiv{0,4,7}\pmod{8}

implies that
\sqrt{\mathcal{R}_{n}(2)\mathcal{R}_{n}(6)}\ll\mathcal{N}_{n}^{-5/3+o(1)}
. The fact that
E_{n}^{\ell}\mathcal{N}_{n}^{-5/3+o(1)}=o\left(\mathrm{Var}{\left[\mathbf{V}(\bT_{n}^{(\ell)},\mathbb{T}^{3})\right]}\right)
follows from the order of the variance of the second Wiener chaos in Lemma
4.6
.
∎

Limiting distribution of the normalised total variation.

The next proposition establishes a CLT in the high-frequency regime for normalised version of the second chaotic component of the total variation

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{V}}(\bT_{n}^{(\ell)};\mathbb{T}^{3})[2]:=\frac{\mathbf{V}(\bT_{n}^{(\ell)};\mathbb{T}^{3})[2]}{\mathrm{Var}{\left[\mathbf{V}(\bT_{n}^{(\ell)};\mathbb{T}^{3})[2]\right]}^{1/2}}.

(4.40)

Proposition 4.11
.

As
n\to\infty,{n}\not\equiv{0,4,7}\pmod{8}
, we have

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{V}}(\bT_{n}^{(\ell)};\mathbb{T}^{3})[2]\xrightarrow{\mathcal{L}}\mathscr{N}(0,1).

Proof.

The expression of
\mathbf{V}(\bT_{n}^{(\ell)};\mathbb{T}^{3})[2]
is given in (
4.28
).
Normalising that expression by the square root of the order of the variance in (
3.51
) yields

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{V}}(\bT_{n}^{(\ell)};\mathbb{T}^{3})[2]=\frac{\sqrt{2}\sqrt{\mathcal{N}_{n}}}{\sqrt{\ell}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}H_{(1)}^{(\ell,3)}(\dot{\bT}_{n}^{(\ell)}(z))dz.

Using (
3.16
), we can rewrite

\displaystyle H_{(1)}^{(\ell,3)}(\dot{\bT}_{n}^{(\ell)}(z))=\frac{1}{6}\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\sum_{j=1}^{3}H_{2}(\tilde{\partial}_{j}T_{n}^{(k)}(z)).

Now, for every
k\in[\ell]
, we
write
H_{2}(u)=u^{2}-1
and
exploit once more the orthogonality relations for complex exponentials on the torus (
4.32
) in order to write

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{3}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}H_{2}(\tilde{\partial}_{j}T_{n}^{(k)})dz=\sum_{j=1}^{3}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\left[\left(\tilde{\partial}_{j}T_{n}^{(k)}(z)\right)^{2}-1\right]dz

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{3}\frac{3}{n\mathcal{N}_{n}}\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{n}}\lambda_{j}^{2}(|a_{k,\lambda}|^{2}-1)=\frac{3}{\mathcal{N}_{n}}\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{n}}(|a_{k,\lambda}|^{2}-1),

where we used that
\lambda_{1}^{2}+\lambda_{2}^{2}+\lambda_{3}^{2}=n
, so that

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{V}}(\bT_{n}^{(\ell)};\mathbb{T}^{3})[2]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{2}\sqrt{\mathcal{N}_{n}}}{\sqrt{\ell}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}H_{(1)}^{(\ell,3)}(\dot{\bT}_{n}^{(\ell)}(z))dz=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\ell}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathcal{N}_{n}}}\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{n}}(|a_{k,\lambda}|^{2}-1)

(4.41)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{\ell}}\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathcal{N}_{n}/2}}\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{n}/_{\sim}}(|a_{k,\lambda}|^{2}-1),

where
\Lambda_{n}/_{\sim}
stands the equivalence classes in
\Lambda_{n}
obtained by identifying
\lambda
with
-\lambda
, so that
|\Lambda_{n}/_{\sim}|=\mathcal{N}_{n}/2
. Note that the random variables
\{|a_{k,\lambda}|^{2}-1:\lambda\in\Lambda_{n}/_{\sim}\}
are i.i.d, centred and have unit variance. The classical CLT thus implies that, as
n\to\infty,{n}\not\equiv{0,4,7}\pmod{8}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathcal{N}_{n}/2}}\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{n}/_{\sim}}(|a_{k,\lambda}|^{2}-1)\xrightarrow{\mathcal{L}}\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}Z_{k},

where
(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{\ell})
is a standard Gaussian vector. The statement then follows from (
4.41
).
∎

End of the proof of Theorem
3.17
.

Relation (
4.34
) implies that the second chaotic component of the total variation dominates the Wiener chaos expansion in (
4.27
). In particular, (
4.34
) implies that, as
n\to\infty,{n}\not\equiv{0,4,7}\pmod{8}

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}{\left[\mathbf{V}(\bT_{n}^{(\ell)};\mathbb{T}^{3})\right]}=\mathrm{Var}{\left[\mathbf{V}(\bT_{n}^{(\ell)};\mathbb{T}^{3})[2]\right]}(1+o(1)),

and that the normalised sequences of random variables

\displaystyle\left\{\widehat{\mathbf{V}}(\bT_{n}^{(\ell)};\mathbb{T}^{3}):n\in S_{3}\right\}\ ,\quad\left\{\widehat{\mathbf{V}}(\bT_{n}^{(\ell)};\mathbb{T}^{3})[2]:n\in S_{3}\right\}

defined in (
3.49
) and (
4.40
) respectively, have the same limiting distribution.
Combining this with the asymptotic variance for the second chaos in Lemma
4.6
proves the variance estimate in (
3.51
).
Finally, the CLT in (
3.52
) for the total variation follows when combining (
4.34
) with the content of Proposition
4.11
. This concludes the proof of Theorem
3.17
.

Appendix A
Proof of Proposition
3.1

We recall that
L^{2}(\mu_{X})=L^{2}(\Omega,\sigma(\mu_{X}),\mathbb{P})
, where
\sigma(\mu_{X})
is the
\sigma
-field generated by random variables of the form

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)\mu_{X}(dx)

where
f
is a finite linear combination of trigonometric functions of the form
\cos(ax),\sin(bx)
with
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
. Since
f(x)
is equal to the limit of its Taylor expansion for every
x\in\mathbb{R}
, and since the support of
\mu_{X}
consists of at most
\ell
points, we deduce that
\sigma(\mu_{X})
is generated by random variables as above where
f=p
is a polynomial.
Our goal is now to prove that, if
F\in L^{2}(\mu_{X})
is such that
\mathbb{E}\left[FH_{\kappa}^{(\ell,n)}(X)\right]=0
for every
\kappa\vdash k
and every
k\geq 0
, then
F=0
,
\mathbb{P}
-almost everywhere. In order to obtain the desired conclusion, we will use the following three facts: (i) zonal polynomials can be expanded into a finite linear combination of matrix-Hermite polynomials (see e.g
[
Chi92
, Eq.(4.12)]
), (ii) the product of finitely many zonal polynomials is a finite linear combination of zonal polynomials (see (
2.8
)) and (iii) every monomial of the form
t_{k}(X):=\Tr([XX^{T}]^{k})=s_{1}^{k}+\ldots+s_{\ell}^{k}
(where
s_{1},\ldots,s_{\ell}
denote the eigenvalues of
XX^{T}
) can be represented as a linear combination of zonal polynomials (see (
2.1
)). Using these three facts shows that, whenever
F
satisfies the assumption above, then
\mathbb{E}\left[Ft_{j_{0}}(X)^{a_{0}}\ldots t_{j_{M}}(X)^{a_{M}}\right]=0
for every finite
M\geq 1
and every collection of integers
j_{0},\ldots,j_{M}\geq 0
and
a_{0},\ldots,a_{M}\geq 0
. In particular, writing
p(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{M}c_{j}x^{j}
for a polynomial of degree
M
, one has that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[F\exp\left(i\int_{\mathbb{R}}p(x)\mu_{X}(dx)\right)\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[F\exp\left(i\sum_{j=0}^{M}c_{j}(s_{1}^{j}+\ldots+s_{\ell}^{j})\right)\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[F\exp\left(i\sum_{j=0}^{M}c_{j}t_{j}(X)\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[F\prod_{j=0}^{M}\exp\left(ic_{j}t_{j}(X)\right)\right]=\sum_{a_{0},\ldots,a_{M}\geq 0}\frac{(ic_{0})^{a_{0}}\cdots(ic_{M})^{a_{M}}}{a_{0}!\cdots a_{M}!}\mathbb{E}\left[Ft_{0}(X)^{a_{0}}\ldots t_{M}(X)^{a_{M}}\right]=0

by assumption. By a standard approximation argument, we therefore deduce that
\mathbb{E}\left[F|\sigma(\mu_{X})\right]=0
, yielding the desired conclusion.
