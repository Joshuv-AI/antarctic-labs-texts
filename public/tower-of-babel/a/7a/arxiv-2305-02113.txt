On the Löwner-John Ellipsoids of the Metric Polytope
Raziel Gartsman, Nati Linial

Abstract
The collection of all n n -point metric spaces of diameter ≤ 1 \leq 1 constitutes a polytope ℳ n ⊂ ℝ ( n 2 ) \mathcal{M}_{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{\binom{n}{2}} , called the Metric Polytope . In this paper, we consider the best approximations of ℳ n \mathcal{M}_{n} by ellipsoids. We give an exact explicit description of the largest volume ellipsoid contained in ℳ n \mathcal{M}_{n} . When inflated by a factor of Θ ⁡ ( n ) \Theta(n) , this ellipsoid contains ℳ n \mathcal{M}_{n} . It also turns out that the least volume ellipsoid containing ℳ n \mathcal{M}_{n} is a ball. When shrunk by a factor of Θ ⁡ ( n ) \Theta(n) , the resulting ball is contained in ℳ n \mathcal{M}_{n} . We note that the general theorems on such ellipsoid posit only that the pertinent inflation/shrinkage factors can be made as small as O ⁡ ( n 2 ) O(n^{2}) .

Introduction

For an integer

\binom{n}{2}

This allows us to view an

-vertex metric space as a point

is the distance between the two vertices

-point metric spaces

{\mathcal{M}\mathcal{C}}_{n}=\left\{d\in\mathbb{R}^{\binom{[n]}{2}}\mid\text{ for all distinct $1\leq i,j,k\leq n$: }d_{ij}\geq 0\text{~and~}d_{ij}+d_{jk}-d_{ik}\geq 0\right\}.

We denote by

, comprised of all
n
-point metric spaces of diameter

Other versions of this polytope appear as well in the literature (see, e.g.

7

It is a basic fact in convex geometry that every convex body

These theorems say that

denotes the ellipsoid that is obtained from
\mathcal{E}

Fact 1.1

(Löwner-John
[
5

6
]
)

For every convex body

\mathcal{E}_{inner}\subseteq K\subset m\mathcal{E}_{inner}.

Also, there is a unique minimal volume ellipsoid

These inner and outer ellipsoids are sometimes referred to as Löwner-John ellipsoids
[
4
]
. Our main results are explicit expressions for the Löwner-John ellipsoids of

(Outer Löwner-John Ellipsoid of

)

Some preliminaries

Here we collect several standard facts for reference:

2
The Outer Löwner-John Ellipsoid of
\mathcal{M}_{n}

The following theorem of John characterizes the least-volume ellipsoid
\mathcal{E}
containing a given convex body.
In view of the comment in Section
1.1
, it suffices to consider the special case
\mathcal{E}=B_{n}
.

Theorem 2.1

(John
[
6
]
)
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be a convex body where
K\subseteq B_{N}
. Then the following statements are equivalent:

•

B_{N}
is the unique least volume ellipsoid containing
K
.

•

There exist contact points
u_{1},...,u_{m}\in\partial K\cap\partial B_{N}
, and positive numbers
\lambda_{1},...,\lambda_{m}
, with

, such that

\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\lambda_{i}u_{i}}=0
and
I_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\lambda_{i}\left(u_{i}u_{i}^{t}\right)}
,
where
I_{n}
is the identity matrix.

We prove Theorem
1.2
by finding the appropriate contact points and the coefficients
\lambda_{i}
. As it turns out, and as we show now these contact points coincide
with
cut metrics
over
[n]
.

Definition 2.1

(Cut Metric)
.

An
n
-point metric
d\in\mathbb{R}^{\binom{[n]}{2}}
is called a Cut Metric if there is a set
S\subseteq[n]
for which
\forall 1\leq i\neq j\leq n:d_{ij}=\left\{\begin{smallmatrix}1&|\{i,j\}\cap S|=1\\
0&else\end{smallmatrix}\right..

The cut metric corresponding to a set
S\subseteq[n]
is denoted by
\delta(S)\in\mathbb{R}^{\binom{[n]}{2}}
.

The convex hull of all cut metrics over
[n]
is called the Cut Polytope
\mathcal{C}_{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{\binom{[n]}{2}}
. We next recall its close relationship with
\mathcal{M}_{n}
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Every cut metric in
\mathbb{R}^{\binom{[n]}{2}}
is a vertex of
\mathcal{M}_{n}
.

Proof.

A point in
\mathcal{M}_{n}
is a vertex if and only if it is not the convex combinations of any two distinct points in
\mathcal{M}_{n}
. Since all cut metrics have only
0
- or
1
-coordinates, they clearly cannot be such combinations.
∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem
1.2
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

There are
m=2^{n-1}-1\geq\binom{n}{2}
cut metrics over
[n]
.
As Theorem
2.2
shows, every cut metric
\delta(S)
is a vertex of
\mathcal{M}_{n}
.
For a set
S\subseteq[n]
, denote

\delta^{{}^{\prime}}(S)=2\delta(S)-1=\left\{\begin{smallmatrix}1&|\{i,j\}\cap S|=1\\
-1&else\end{smallmatrix}\right.

and let
\mathcal{M}_{n}^{{}^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\binom{n}{2}}}\mathcal{M}_{n}-\frac{1}{2}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1\\
\vdots\\
1\end{smallmatrix}\right)
.
Note that
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\binom{n}{2}}}\delta^{{}^{\prime}}(S)
is a unit vector and a vertex of
\mathcal{M}_{n}^{{}^{\prime}}
.
Denote those vertices as
u_{1},...,u_{m}
, and set
\forall i:\>\lambda_{i}=\lambda=\frac{\binom{n}{2}}{m}
. For any
1\leq k\neq l\leq n
, the following holds:

\left[\sum^{m}_{i=1}{\lambda_{i}(u_{i}u_{i}^{T})}\right]_{kl,kl}=\lambda\sum_{\delta(S),S\subseteq[n]}{\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\binom{n}{2}}}\left[\delta^{{}^{\prime}}(S)\right]_{kl}\right)^{2}}=\lambda\frac{m}{\binom{n}{2}}=1.

Let us sample a subset
S\subseteq[n]
and consider the corresponding cut metric
\delta(S)
.
Note that for
\{k,l\}\neq\{p,q\}
, the random variables
\left[\delta^{{}^{\prime}}(S)\right]_{kl}
,
\left[\delta^{{}^{\prime}}(S)\right]_{pq}
are independent. Therefore:

\left[\sum^{m}_{i=1}{\lambda_{i}\left(u_{i}u_{i}^{T}\right)}\right]_{kl,pq}=\lambda\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}{\frac{1}{\binom{n}{2}}\left[\delta^{{}^{\prime}}(S)\right]_{kl}\left[\delta^{{}^{\prime}}(S)\right]_{pq}}=\frac{\lambda m}{\binom{n}{2}}\mathop{\mathbb{E}^{2}}_{S\subseteq[n]}{\left[\delta^{{}^{\prime}}(S)\right]_{kl}}=0.

Now,
\sum^{m}_{i=1}{\lambda_{i}u_{i}u_{i}^{T}}=I_{\binom{n}{2}}
and
\sum^{m}_{i=1}{\lambda_{i}u_{i}}=0
. By John’s Theorem
2.1
, the minimal volume ellipsoid containing
\mathcal{M}_{n}^{{}^{\prime}}
is
\textbf{B}_{{[n]\choose 2}}
. By applying the inverse of the above affine map, the first part of Theorem
1.2
is obtained.

Finally, denote
p=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}\\
\vdots\\
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
. Note that
\mathcal{M}_{n}
has 2 families of facets.
One expresses the upper bound on the diameter of metric spaces, and each
such facet is at distance
\frac{1}{2}
from
p
. The other ones are the triangle inequalities, and each one is at distance
\frac{1}{2\sqrt{3}}
from
p
.
Thus, the largest ball centered at
p
and contained in
\mathcal{M}_{n}
has radius
\frac{1}{2\sqrt{3}}
. The result now directly follows.
∎

3
The Inner Löwner-John Ellipsoid of
\mathcal{M}_{n}

In this section, we discuss the (unique) largest volume ellipsoid
\mathcal{E}_{n}
contained in
\mathcal{M}_{n}
. Our main result is a description of this ellipsoid, given in Theorem

. The proof is in four parts:

1.

The fact that

is invariant under the action of the symmetric group

2.

An inner Löwner-John Ellipsoid is the solution to a maximization problem. The symmetry of
\mathcal{M}_{n}

mentioned above
help us solve this optimization problem, see Lemma
3.3

3.

An asymptotic solution of the problem in Lemma

\mathcal{E}_{n}
.

4.

Finally, this description lets us bound the minimal
r
for which
\mathcal{M}_{n}\subseteq r\mathcal{E}_{n}
, proving Theorem
1.3
.

We then continue to discuss some implications of this result. Namely, corollary

describes the minimal distance possible in a metric space

, and Theorem

describes all contact points

4
Discussion and Open Problems

•

Let us mention one not-so-obvious reason to study the approximation of convex
bodies by ellipsoids: Optimizing a linear function on an ellipsoid is a rather
trivial matter, since an ellipsoid is the affine image of a ball. It is conceivable
that this could offer a method for finding approximate solutions to hard
optimization problems. If you are unable to determine the maximum of some
(linear or convex) function
f
on a convex body
K
, you may first approximate
K

by an ellipsoid
\mathcal{E}
and optimize
f
on it. We are presently unable
to offer any concrete examples of interest, but this leads us to our next point.

•

Can one find the Löwner-John ellipsoid of the Cut Polytope? How good is
the approximation that this provides to the MAXCUT problem?
Note that the symmetry arguments that we used in the study of
\mathcal{M}_{n}
’s
approximating ellipsoids apply here as well.

•

We managed to get good upper and lower bounds on the optimal inflating factor
in Theorem
1.3
, but the exact number still eludes us. It remains
open to determine the exact factor.
