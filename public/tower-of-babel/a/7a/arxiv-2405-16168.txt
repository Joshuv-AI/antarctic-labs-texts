Multi-Player Approaches for Dueling Bandits
Or Raveh, Junya Honda, Masashi Sugiyama

Abstract
Various approaches have emerged for multi-armed bandits in distributed systems. The multiplayer dueling bandit problem, common in scenarios with only preference-based information like human feedback, introduces challenges related to controlling collaborative exploration of non-informative arm pairs, but has received little attention. To fill this gap, we demonstrate that the direct use of a Follow Your Leader black-box approach matches the lower bound for this setting when utilizing known dueling bandit algorithms as a foundation. Additionally, we analyze a message-passing fully distributed approach with a novel Condorcet-winner recommendation protocol, resulting in expedited exploration in many cases. Our experimental comparisons reveal that our multiplayer algorithms surpass single-player benchmark algorithms, underscoring their efficacy in addressing the nuanced challenges of the multiplayer dueling bandit setting.

Introduction

In decision-making under uncertainty, multi-armed bandit (MAB)
[
4
]
problems are a key paradigm with applications in recommendation systems and online advertising. These problems entail balancing exploration-exploitation trade-offs, as an agent draws from a set of
K
arms with unknown reward distributions to maximize cumulative rewards or minimize regret over time. Two notable variations of MAB include the dueling-bandit problem and the cooperative multiplayer MAB problem. In the dueling-bandit scenario
[
36
]
, feedback comes from pairwise comparisons between
K
arms, useful in situations like human-feedback driven tasks, including ranker evaluation
[
25
]
and preference-based recommendation systems
[
10
]
.
Meanwhile, the cooperative multiplayer MAB focuses on a group of
M
players collaboratively solving challenges in a distributed decision-making environment, enhancing learning through shared information. This approach finds applications in fields like multi-robot systems
[
19
]
and distributed recommender systems
[
27
]
.

The
M
-player
K
-arm cooperative dueling bandit problem, combining aspects of the two previously studied variations, introduces a new dimension to cooperative decision-making with preference-based feedback, yet remains unexplored to the best of our knowledge. A compelling application arises in large-scale distributed recommendation or ranker evaluation systems
[
12
]
, where a network of backend servers directs users to local algorithms that elicit preference-based feedback. These systems, driven by high demand and the need for quick decisions, optimize performance through low-rate local communication with nearby servers, mirroring the dynamics of a multiplayer dueling bandit. Another example is a network of self-ordering machines in regional fast-food chains, where each machine collects customer preferences for food products
[
3
]
. Local communication is beneficial due to varied preferences across different areas.

The multiplayer dueling bandit setting, unlike its MAB counterpart, demands complex coordination for exploration-exploitation across pairs of arms. In multiplayer MAB, communication delays can lead to drawing suboptimal arms, but these still provide useful information that reduces future regret. In contrast, in multiplayer dueling bandits, communication delays can lead to pulling pairs of either identical or non-identical suboptimal arms. This not only results in immediate regret but also provides no new information in the former case, and limited information in the latter compared to pulling a pair of optimal and suboptimal arms. Thus, careful a communication strategy is crucial in this setting.

In this study, our focus centers on the widely explored Condorcet Winner (CW) assumption, in which there exists a unique preferable arm
[
32
]
. We establish an asymptotic regret lower bound of
O\left(K\log T\right)
that is independent of player counts. We introduce a Follow Your Leader (FYL) black-box algorithm, that aligns well with existing dueling-bandit algorithms like Relative Upper Confidence Bound (RUCB)
[
40
]
and Relative Minimal Empirical Divergence (RMED)
[
16
]
, and offers a more natural fit than multiplayer MAB variants
[
20
]
. Recognizing the limitations of relying solely on one leader for exploration
[
35
]
, we also propose a decentralized extension to RUCB. Unlike in a MAB counterpart
[
11
]
, we demonstrate that incorporating an additional CW recommendation protocol between players significantly accelerates the identification of the CW in many instances. We validate our approach with real-world data experiments. To summarize, our contributions encompass:

•

We propose an intuitive black-box algorithm adept at integrating with existing single-player dueling bandit algorithms, conduct comprehensive regret analysis, and illustrate its capability to match an established lower bound when initialized with the RMED algorithm.

•

We conduct an in-depth regret analysis of a fully decentralized multiplayer algorithm based on RUCB, showcasing asymptotic optimality of the incurred regret up to constants.

•

We demonstrate that a novel CW recommendation protocol results in a quick identification of the CW in many instances.

•

Through simulated experiments on real data, we substantiate our claim that our algorithms exhibit superior performance compared to a single-player dueling bandit system.

Related Work

The dueling bandit problem, introduced as a useful variant of the MAB problem in Yue & Yoachims
[
37
]
, initially underwent analysis with stringent assumptions about the preference matrix. These assumptions, including stochastic transitivity and stochastic triangle inequality, resulted in regret bounds of
O(K\log T)

[

,
36
]
.
Subsequent works relaxed these assumptions by introducing the more realistic CW assumption
[
40
,
16
,
9
,
1
]
, which remains widely adopted today.
Notably, the RUCB algorithm
[
40
]
, based on the UCB approach for MAB, achieves an
O(K\log T)
regret with high probability. Komiyama et al.
[
16
]
established a comprehensive lower bound for this setting of the same order and provided the RMED family of algorithms that match it. Recently, Saha & Gaillard
[
29
]
demonstrated a general reduction from dueling bandit algorithms to MAB and introduced a best-of-both-worlds algorithm effective for both adversarial and stochastic dueling bandits. Other recent advancements include a batched variant
[
2
]
and a differentially private extension
[
28
]
. Additionally, in cases where the CW does not exist, alternative definitions of winners, such as the Borda Winner
[
13
]
and the Copeland Winner
[
17
,
39
]
, have been considered in dueling bandit literature.

Considerable attention utilizing various approaches has been directed towards cooperative multiplayer MAB scenarios, revealing an
O(K\log T)
regret lower bound
[
11
]
. Among them, in an approach called the leader-follower paradigm, players designate one or multiple leaders and base their arm selections on the leaders’ instructions. In Kolla et al.
[
15
]
and Landgren et al.
[
20
]
, followers emulate leaders employing a UCB strategy with neighbor-only communication, but the resulting algorithm does not match the lower bound. In the context of a Bernoulli bandit, Wang et al.
[
33
]
introduced a distributed parsimonious exploration strategy with message passing akin to ours called DPE2, in which a leader explores using a UCB strategy, and followers exploit a designated arm sent by it. Their approach showcased a regret that aligns with the lower bound, but required a meticulous derivation that cannot be applied in general. In the fully distributed setting, which is more suitable in situations where players are non-synchronized or should have similar performance, both a message-passing approach
[
11
,
23
]
and a running-consensus approach
[
21
]
yield a regret that matches the lower bound up to constants. Alternatively, certain approaches adopt a gossiping strategy
[
8
,
30
]
, in which a player can randomly communicate with one other player at each round.

For some matrix

Players are positioned on a connected undirected communication graph

[

,
23
]
with a decay parameter

)
[
15
]

)
[

]

The number of wins of arm

N_{ij}^{m}(t)=\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}(w_{ij}^{m}(\tau)+w_{ji}^{m}(\tau))
represents the number of visits to
(i,j)
or
(j,i)
.
We denote

as the reward gap, and define the cumulative regret as
\mathcal{R}(T)=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{t=1}^{T}0.5(\Delta_{1i_{m}(t)}+\Delta_{1j_{m}(t)})

\Delta_{1\max}:=\max\{\Delta_{1i}\}
and
\Delta_{1\min}:=\min_{i>1}\{\Delta_{1i}\}
.

Asymptotic Lower Bound

We first establish a lower bound for the multiplayer dueling bandit setting in Theorem
2.1
Let
\mathcal{O}_{i}=\{j|j\in[K],q_{ij}<1/2\}
denote the superiors of arm
i
. An algorithm is considered consistent over the class
\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{CW}}
if
\mathbb{E}\mathcal{R}(T)=o(T^{p})
for all
p>0
and
Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{CW}}
. Furthermore, we define
N_{ij}(T)=\sum_{m=1}^{M}N_{ij}^{m}(T)
as the total number of visits to
(i,j)
or
(j,i)
in the system, and
\mathrm{KL}(p,q)=p\log\frac{p}{q}+(1-p)\frac{1-p}{1-q}
as the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence between Bernoulli distributions.

Theorem 2.1
.

For any consistent algorithm on
\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{CW}}
and
Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{CW}}
, the group regret obeys:

\liminf_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\mathbb{E}\mathcal{R}(T)}{\log T}\geq\sum_{i\in[K]\setminus\{1\}}\min_{j\in\mathcal{O}_{i}}\frac{\Delta_{1i}+\Delta_{1j}}{2\mathrm{KL}(q_{ij},1/2)}.

This bound scales as
O(K\log T)
similarly to the single-player case
[
16
]
, irrespective of the number of players
M
. This is straightforward from the fact that a multiplayer system cannot perform better than in a centralized setting consisting of a single player that draws
M
pairs at each round. Nevertheless, in Appendix
A
, we prove Theorem
2.1
in a novel way compared to the single-player setting. To do so, we first leverage a carefully constructed divergence decomposition in Lemma
A.8
. We then derive a challenging environment instance, which is more difficult compared to the MAB setting
[
22
]
due to the structured nature of the problem, and employ information-theoretic bounds in Lemma
A.9
. These bounds are more useful in the multiplayer case compared to existing single-player techniques
[
16
]
, which are more cumbersome and less applicable in this setting.

While our setup resembles the DPE2 algorithm for multiplayer MAB
[

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathcal{R}(T)\leq g(K,T,Q)+M\Delta_{1\max}f(K,T,Q)+M(T_{\mathrm{LE}}+2D)\Delta_{1\max}~.

\sum_{m\in C}N_{ij}^{m,T_{0}}(t)>\left[4\alpha\log t/\overline{\Delta}_{ij}^{2}+(\gamma^{\prime}+2)|C|\right]\mathds{1}\{i\neq j\}
do not exist.

The establishment of this bound is pivotal, as it allows us to both bound the leading term of the group regret with a multiplicative factor smaller than
M
, and to bound the non-leading term in
T
corresponding to the regret incurred before identifying the CW. A naive analysis akin to the single-player case would lead to only bounding the local counters
N_{ij}^{m,T_{0}}(t)
, resulting in a regret that scales like
O(MK\log T)
. To avoid that, we capitalize on the shared observations among players to limit the total number of visits within any clique, and rely on the fact that the first arm is drawn from the player-specific champion set regardless of recommendations. Unlike existing multiplayer MAB methods
[
23
]
, we demonstrate the visitation counter bound holds with high probability even for rounds in which the pair
(i,j)
is not drawn by any player, and for any clique in the graph to facilitate faster CW identification.

Before presenting the group regret bound for Algorithm
2
, we introduce the following term.

\tilde{C}=\frac{1.7\alpha-2.4}{1.7\alpha-1.4}\left(4MK^{2}\left(3+2\log\left(d_{\max}^{\gamma}+1\right)\right)\right)^{\frac{1}{1.7\alpha-1.4}}.

Theorem 4.2
.

For any
\delta>0
and
\gamma^{\prime}\leq\gamma
, define
\mathcal{D}:=\sum_{i<j}4\alpha/\overline{\Delta}_{ij}^{2}
,
\Gamma(m,\gamma^{\prime})
as the size of the largest clique player

belongs to in
\mathcal{G}_{\gamma^{\prime}}
and

\displaystyle\hat{C}:

\displaystyle=\Delta_{1\max}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\min_{\gamma^{\prime}\leq\gamma}\left(K^{2}(\gamma^{\prime}+2)+\frac{2\mathcal{D}}{\Gamma(m,\gamma^{\prime})}\log 2\mathcal{D}\right)+(2\tilde{C}+K(3\gamma+2))M\Delta_{1\max}~.

Let
\chi(\mathcal{G}_{\gamma})
denote the clique covering number of
\mathcal{G}_{\gamma}

\alpha>1.4
and all times

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathcal{R}(T)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j>1}\frac{2\alpha\chi(\mathcal{G}_{\gamma})}{\Delta_{1j}}\log T+\hat{C}~.

The regret bound presented in Theorem
4.2
demonstrates that a message-passing protocol is an effective distributed approach for the dueling bandit problem. It emphasizes the significance of leveraging candidate recommendations in reducing the regret incurred before identifying the CW, a characteristic unique to this scenario. In the asymptotic regime, the regret scales as
O\left(K\chi(\mathcal{G}_{\gamma})\log T\right)
, consistent with distributed approaches in the multiplayer MAB setting
[
11

23
]
. For the no communication case (

O\left(MK\log T\right)
bound, while for complete communication (
\gamma=D
), where every player can communicate with any other, the regret scales optimally as
O\left(K\log T\right)
.

Similarly, in the finite-time regime, the non-dominant term
\hat{C}
is non-increasing with
\gamma
, and is determined separately for each graph
\mathcal{G}
and parameter
\gamma
by minimization over two competing delay-dependent terms. While providing a direct expression for this term is difficult, in a complete communication setting it scales like
O\left(MKD+\frac{K^{2}\log K}{\Delta^{2}_{1\min}}+MK^{2}\min\Big\{\frac{\log K}{\Delta^{2}_{1\min}},D\Big\}\right)
as discussed in Appendix
C

and the instance complexity
1/\Delta^{2}_{1\min}
. For challenging instances where
\Delta^{2}_{1\min}
is sufficiently small, the non-leading term scales as
O(K^{2}\log K/\Delta^{2}_{1\min})
compared to the
O(MK^{2}\log K/\Delta^{2}_{1\min})
term for Follow Your Leader RUCB (FYLRUCB). This observation helps explain the better empirical performance observed in Section
5
.

Proving Theorem
4.2
is a challenging task, necessitating the establishment that each player actively identifies the CW quicker than a single-player counterpart and that visitation counters within cliques cannot be too large with high probability. To achieve this, we leverage Lemma
4.1
and demonstrate that, owing to CW candidate recommendations, each player identifies the CW within
O\left(\gamma+K^{2}\gamma^{\prime}+K^{2}\log(K)/\Gamma(m,\gamma^{\prime})\Delta^{2}_{1\min}\right)
rounds with high probability for any clique such that
\gamma^{\prime}\leq\gamma
. We then decompose the regret to accommodate both the finite-time and asymptotic regimes. In the latter, each player consistently selects the CW as the first arm, leading to a high probability regret bound that is later converted into an expected regret bound using basic probability properties.

6
Conclusion and Future Work

This paper introduced novel approaches to address the multiplayer dueling bandit problem, demonstrating the existence of efficient algorithms despite the increased complexity compared to a MAB setting. We established a regret lower bound for this problem and discussed a versatile black-box algorithm, leveraging either RUCB or RMED as base algorithms. However, as demonstrated in the experimental evaluation, finite-time performance can vary depending on the exploration efficiency of the base algorithm. To address this, we devised a novel message-passing protocol with CW recommendations for RUCB, showcasing more consistent performance and quick identification of the CW. All algorithms exhibited asymptotic regret comparable to that of a single player, a result validated theoretically and through simulations.

In future work, one promising avenue is the development of a black-box algorithm capable of compatibility with diverse base algorithms, while also enhancing collaborative exploration. A parallel approach could involve devising a multiplayer black-box algorithm using a base multiplayer bandit algorithm. Additionally, making our algorithms more practical for real-world applications could involve incorporating federated learning
[
34
]
and privacy-preserving methods
[
5
]
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{G},T}(\pi,\nu)

Next, we present a divergence decomposition lemma tailored to the multiplayer dueling bandit setting. This will be used to prove the lower bound.

Lemma A.8

\nu=(P_{ij})

\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q}

Assume that

It is evident that the policy terms

\log\frac{d\mathbb{P}_{\nu\pi}}{d\mathbb{P}_{\nu^{\prime}\pi}}(i_{1,1},\ldots,r_{M,T})=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\log\frac{p_{i_{m,t},j_{m,t}}(r_{m,t})}{p_{i_{m,t},j_{m,t}}^{{}^{\prime}}(r_{m,t})}~,

and

\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[\log\frac{d\mathbb{P}_{\nu\pi}}{d\mathbb{P}_{\nu^{\prime}\pi}}(i_{1,1},\ldots,r_{M,T})\right]=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[\log\frac{p_{i_{m,t},j_{m,t}}(r_{m,t})}{p_{i_{m,t},j_{m,t}}^{{}^{\prime}}(r_{m,t})}\right].

For every term in the sum we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[\log\frac{p_{i_{m,t},j_{m,t}}(r_{m,t})}{p_{i_{m,t},j_{m,t}}^{{}^{\prime}}(r_{m,t})}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[\log\frac{p_{i_{m,t},j_{m,t}}(r_{m,t})}{p_{i_{m,t},j_{m,t}}^{{}^{\prime}}(r_{m,t})}\Big|i_{m,t},j_{m,t}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[\mathrm{KL}(P_{i_{m,t},j_{m,t}},P_{i_{m,t},j_{m,t}}^{{}^{\prime}})\right].

Returning to the previous equation:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\nu}

\displaystyle\left[\log\frac{d\mathbb{P}_{\nu\pi}}{d\mathbb{P}_{\nu^{\prime}\pi}}(i_{1,1},\ldots,r_{M,T})\right]=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[\mathrm{KL}(P_{i_{m,t},j_{m,t}},P_{i_{m,t},j_{m,t}}^{{}^{\prime}})\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathds{1}\{(i_{m,t}=i,j_{m,t}=j)\}\mathrm{KL}(P_{ij},P_{ij}^{{}^{\prime}})\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in[K]}\sum_{j\in[K]\setminus\{i\}}\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[N_{ij}(T)]\mathrm{KL}(P_{ij},P_{ij}^{{}^{\prime}})+\sum_{i\in[K]}\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[N_{ii}(T)]\mathrm{KL}(P_{ij},P_{ij}^{{}^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in[K]}\sum_{j\in[K]\setminus\{i\}}\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[N_{ij}(T)]\mathrm{KL}(q_{ij},q_{ij}^{{}^{\prime}})+\sum_{i\in[K]}\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[N_{ii}(T)]\mathrm{KL}(q_{ii},q_{ii}^{{}^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in[K]}\sum_{j\in[K]\setminus\{i\}}\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[N_{ij}(T)]\mathrm{KL}(q_{ij},q_{ij}^{{}^{\prime}}),

where in the last transition we used the fact that
q_{ii}=q_{ii}^{{}^{\prime}}=1/2
. Given that
d\mathbb{P}_{\nu\pi}/d\mathbb{P}_{\nu^{\prime}\pi}<\infty
, it holds that

\mathrm{KL}\left(\mathbb{P}_{\nu\pi},\mathbb{P}_{\nu^{\prime}\pi}\right)=\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[\log\frac{d\mathbb{P}_{\nu\pi}}{d\mathbb{P}_{\nu^{\prime}\pi}}\right].

It is evident that in our case the expectation is finite, so

\mathrm{KL}\left(\mathbb{P}_{\nu\pi},\mathbb{P}_{\nu^{\prime}\pi}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in[K]}\sum_{j\in[K]\setminus\{i\}}\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[N_{ij}(T)]\mathrm{KL}(q_{ij},q_{ij}^{{}^{\prime}}).

In case
\mathrm{KL}(q_{ij},q_{ij}^{{}^{\prime}})=\infty
for some
i,j\in[K]
, this relation still holds since both sides of the equation become infinite.
∎

We also use the following lemma.

Lemma A.9
.

For any consistent algorithm on
\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{CW}}
and
Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{CW}}
, and for any arm
i\in[K]\setminus\{1\}
the following holds.

\liminf_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j\in\mathcal{O}_{i}}\mathrm{KL}(q_{ij},1/2)N_{ij}(T)\right]}{\log T}\geq 1.

Proof.

Fix some arm
i\in[K]\setminus\{1\}
and define
\mathcal{O}_{i}^{{}^{\prime}}=\{j~|~j\in[K],q_{ij}\leq 1/2\}
. We consider a challenging instance
Q^{{}^{\prime}}
, which is defined such that the means
q_{ij}^{{}^{\prime}}
for
j\in\mathcal{O}_{i}^{{}^{\prime}}
satisfy
\mathrm{KL}(q_{ij},q_{ij}^{{}^{\prime}})=\mathrm{KL}(q_{ij},1/2)+\epsilon
with
q_{ij}^{{}^{\prime}}>1/2
, for some
\epsilon>0
(we leave
q_{ii}^{{}^{\prime}}=q_{ii}=1/2
). The rest of the means remain the same as
q_{ij}
. For this new instance,
Q^{{}^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{CW}}
and arm
i
is the CW.

It is important to highlight that a proof scheme akin to ours that is used for MAB
[
22
]
is applicable only to an unstructured class of bandits. This is due to the necessity of the unstructured assumption to construct the challenging instance
\nu^{{}^{\prime}}
. The unstructured assumption becomes crucial when attempting to change the mean of a single arm, as the presence of interdependence among arms restricts such modifications. In the dueling bandit scenario, we can directly provide a challenging instance within the structured class, even though the arms’ means may be correlated. This distinction allows for a more flexible approach to constructing challenging instances within the dueling bandit framework.

Note that since only the means
q_{ij}
for
j\in\mathcal{O}_{i}^{{}^{\prime}}
are different in the two environments, Lemma
A.8
states that

\mathrm{KL}\left(\mathbb{P}_{\nu\pi},\mathbb{P}_{\nu^{\prime}\pi}\right)=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{O}_{i}^{{}^{\prime}}}\mathrm{KL}(q_{ij},q_{ij}^{{}^{\prime}})\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[N_{ij}(T)\right]\leq\sum_{j\in\mathcal{O}_{i}^{{}^{\prime}}}(\mathrm{KL}(q_{ij},1/2)+\epsilon)\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[N_{ij}(T)\right].

From the Bretagnolle-Huber inequality, for any event
A\in\mathcal{F}_{M,T}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\nu\pi}(A)+\mathbb{P}_{\nu^{\prime}\pi}(A^{c})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\exp\left(-\mathrm{KL}\left(\mathbb{P}_{\nu\pi},\mathbb{P}_{\nu^{\prime}\pi}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\exp\left(-\sum_{j\in\mathcal{O}_{i}^{{}^{\prime}}}(\mathrm{KL}(q_{ij},1/2)+\epsilon)\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[N_{ij}(T)\right]\right).

Choose
A=\{N_{ii}(T)>MT/2\}
, and let
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{G}}:=\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{G},T}(\pi,\nu),\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{G}}^{{}^{\prime}}:=\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{G},T}(\pi,\nu^{\prime})
. By the Markov inequality

\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{G}}\geq\Delta_{1i}\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[N_{ii}(T)\right]\geq\frac{MT\Delta_{1i}}{2}\mathbb{P}_{\nu\pi}\left(N_{ii}(T)\geq\frac{MT}{2}\right)=\frac{MT\Delta_{1i}}{2}\mathbb{P}_{\nu\pi}(A).

Additionally, the regret for the challenging instance is

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{G}}^{{}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{l\in[K]\setminus\{i\}}\sum_{j\in[K]\setminus\{i,l\}}\frac{\Delta_{il}^{{}^{\prime}}+\Delta_{ij}^{{}^{\prime}}}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\nu^{{}^{\prime}}}N_{lj}(T)

\displaystyle+\sum_{j\in[K]}\Delta_{ij}^{{}^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}_{\nu^{{}^{\prime}}}N_{jj}(T)+\sum_{j\in[K]\setminus\{i\}}\frac{\Delta_{ij}^{{}^{\prime}}}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\nu^{{}^{\prime}}}N_{ij}(T).

By definition, since arm
i
is the CW for the challenging instance, the minimal gap with respect to it
\Delta_{min}^{{}^{\prime}}=\min_{j\in[K]\setminus\{i\}}\Delta_{ij}^{{}^{\prime}}
is positive, so

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{G}}^{{}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{l\in[K]\setminus\{i\}}\sum_{j\in[K]\setminus\{i,l\}}\frac{\Delta_{min}^{{}^{\prime}}/2+\Delta_{min}^{{}^{\prime}}/2}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\nu^{{}^{\prime}}}N_{lj}(T)

\displaystyle+\sum_{j\in[K]\setminus\{i\}}(\Delta_{min}^{{}^{\prime}}/2)\mathbb{E}_{\nu^{{}^{\prime}}}N_{jj}(T)+\sum_{j\in[K]\setminus\{i\}}\frac{\Delta_{min}^{{}^{\prime}}}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\nu^{{}^{\prime}}}N_{ij}(T)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta_{min}^{{}^{\prime}}}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\nu^{{}^{\prime}}}\left[\frac{1}{2}\sum_{l\in[K]\setminus\{i\}}\sum_{j\in[K]\setminus\{i,l\}}N_{lj}(T)+\sum_{j\in[K]\setminus\{i\}}N_{jj}(T)+\sum_{j\in[K]\setminus\{i\}}N_{ij}(T)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta_{min}^{{}^{\prime}}}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\nu^{{}^{\prime}}}\left[MT-N_{ii}(T)\right]\geq\frac{\Delta_{min}^{{}^{\prime}}}{2}(MT-\frac{MT}{2})\mathbb{P}_{\nu^{{}^{\prime}}\pi}\left(MT-N_{ii}(T)\geq MT-\frac{MT}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{MT\Delta_{min}^{{}^{\prime}}}{4}\mathbb{P}_{\nu^{{}^{\prime}}\pi}(A^{c}).

Putting it all together:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{G}}+\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{G}}^{{}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{MT\Delta_{1i}}{2}\mathbb{P}_{\nu\pi}(A)+\frac{MT\Delta_{min}^{{}^{\prime}}}{4}\mathbb{P}_{\nu^{{}^{\prime}}\pi}(A^{c})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{MT}{4}\min\{2\Delta_{1i},\Delta_{min}^{{}^{\prime}}\}\left(\mathbb{P}_{\nu\pi}(A)+\mathbb{P}_{\nu^{\prime}\pi}(A^{c})\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{MT}{8}\min\{2\Delta_{1i},\Delta_{min}^{{}^{\prime}}\}\exp\left(-\sum_{j\in\mathcal{O}_{i}^{{}^{\prime}}}(\mathrm{KL}(q_{ij},1/2)+\epsilon)\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[N_{ij}(T)\right]\right).

Rearranging the previous inequality:

\sum_{j\in\mathcal{O}_{i}^{{}^{\prime}}}(\mathrm{KL}(q_{ij},1/2)+\epsilon)\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[N_{ij}(T)\right]\geq\log\left(\frac{MT\min\{2\Delta_{1i},\Delta_{min}^{{}^{\prime}}\}}{8\left(\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{G}}+\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{G}}^{{}^{\prime}}\right)}\right),

and:

\displaystyle\liminf_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{O}_{i}^{{}^{\prime}}}(\mathrm{KL}(q_{ij},1/2)+\epsilon)\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[N_{ij}(T)\right]}{\log T}

\displaystyle\geq\liminf_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\log\left(\frac{MT\min\{2\Delta_{1i},\Delta_{min}^{{}^{\prime}}\}}{8\left(\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{G}}+\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{G}}^{{}^{\prime}}\right)}\right)}{\log T}

\displaystyle=\left(1-\limsup_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\log\left(\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{G}}+\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{G}}^{{}^{\prime}}\right)}{\log T}\right)=1.

In the last transition we used that fact that
\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{cons}}\left(\mathcal{Q}\right)
, so for every
p>0
there exists some constant
C_{p}
such that
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{G}}+\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{G}}^{{}^{\prime}}\leq C_{p}T^{p}
for large enough
T
. This means that
\log\left(\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{G}}+\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{G}}^{{}^{\prime}}\right)\leq p\log T+\log C_{p}
, so

\limsup_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\log\left(\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{G}}+\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{G}}^{{}^{\prime}}\right)}{\log T}\leq\limsup_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{p\log T+\log C_{p}}{\log T}=p.

Since
p
is an arbitrary positive constant, by taking it to
0
, the transition above holds. Similarly,
\epsilon
is also an arbitrary positive constant, so by taking it to zero, all the arms for which
j\in\mathcal{O}_{i}^{{}^{\prime}}\setminus\mathcal{O}_{i}
disappear from the sum above, as
\mathrm{KL}(q_{ij},1/2)=0
. This concludes the proof.
∎

As the final part of this section, we provide a proof for Theorem
2.1
.

Proof.

We use the definition of the regret and Lemma
A.9
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{G},T}(\pi,\nu)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in[K]}\sum_{j\in[K]\setminus\{i\}}\frac{\Delta_{1i}+\Delta_{1j}}{2}N_{ij}(T)+\sum_{i\in[K]}\Delta_{1i}N_{ii}(T)\right]

B.2
Proof for the Claims in Section
3

We start with a proof for Theorem
3.2
.

Proof.

First, we discuss the random regret
\mathcal{R}(T)
. This can be decomposed into four parts:

•

Leader Election Phase:
For a deterministic
T_{\mathrm{LE}}
number of rounds, each player draws some arm pair determined by the base algorithm. Taking into account the time required for the leader’s CW candidate to reach all the players for the first time after the former’s election, we have a contribution of
M(T_{\mathrm{LE}}+D)\Delta_{1\max}
to the regret bound.

•

Followers Exploitation Rounds:
For rounds where the followers draw the same arm as the CW candidate held by the leader, regret is incurred only if this arm is not the CW. This results in a regret bound contribution of
(M-1)\sum_{t=T_{\mathrm{LE}}+1}^{T}\Delta_{1\max}\mathds{1}\{\mathrm{cw}(t)\neq 1\}.

•

Communication Rounds:
These are rounds in which the arm drawn by followers differs from the CW candidate held by the leader due to communication delay, i.e., rounds where the updated arm information has not yet reached these followers. Given the maximal delay of
D
rounds, this contributes an additional factor of
MD\Delta_{1\max}
to the regret bound, as in the worst case a given player draws at round
T
the leader’s candidate from round
T-D
.

•

Leader’s Regret:
The leader draws arms as indicated by the base algorithm, and by only utilizing its own samples, and the resulting contribution to the regret is denoted by
\mathcal{R}_{m_{l}}(T)
.

Using the decomposition above, we bound the random regret as:

\mathcal{R}(T)\leq M(T_{\mathrm{LE}}+2D)\Delta_{1\max}+M\Delta_{1\max}\sum_{t=T_{\mathrm{LE}}+1}^{T}\mathds{1}\{\mathrm{cw}(t)\neq 1\}+\mathcal{R}_{m_{l}}(T)~.

To complete the proof for the average regret, let us denote
\mathbb{P}
as the probability measure for the entire multiplayer system, and
\mathbb{P}^{\prime}
as the measure for a single-player system containing only the leader operating as indicated by the base algorithm. For any history
\mathcal{H}^{m_{l}}_{t}
containing only the leader’s draws and rewards, we show that it has the same probability under both measures. To this end, we use an induction principle. This holds trivially for
t=1
, and for
t>1
we get that since the leader uses the base algorithm without information outside of
\mathcal{H}^{m_{l}}_{t}
, and since it is elected deterministically by the leader election algorithm, the following holds:

\displaystyle P(\mathcal{H}^{m_{l}}_{t+1})=P((i_{m_{l}}(t+1),j_{m_{l}}(t+1)),r_{m_{l}}(t+1),\mathcal{H}^{m_{l}}_{t})

\displaystyle=P((i_{m_{l}}(t+1),j_{m_{l}}(t+1)),r_{m_{l}}(t+1)|\mathcal{H}^{m_{l}}_{t})P(\mathcal{H}^{m_{l}}_{t})

\displaystyle=P^{\prime}((i_{m_{l}}(t+1),j_{m_{l}}(t+1)),r_{m_{l}}(t+1)|\mathcal{H}^{m_{l}}_{t})P^{\prime}(\mathcal{H}^{m_{l}}_{t})=P^{\prime}(\mathcal{H}^{m_{l}}_{t+1})~,

where the third transition follows from the induction assumption and from the fact that the leader follows the base algorithm in both environments. Since neither
\mathcal{R}_{m_{l}}(T)
nor
\mathrm{cw}(t)
contain information outside
\mathcal{H}^{m_{l}}_{t}
, Assumption
3.1
leads to:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathcal{R}(T)

\displaystyle\leq M(T_{\mathrm{LE}}+2D)\Delta_{1\max}+M\Delta_{1\max}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\sum_{t=T_{\mathrm{LE}}+1}^{T}\mathds{1}\{\mathrm{cw}(t)\neq 1\}\right]+\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\mathcal{R}_{m_{l}}(T)

\displaystyle=M(T_{\mathrm{LE}}+2D)\Delta_{1\max}+M\Delta_{1\max}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P^{\prime}}}\left[\sum_{t=T_{\mathrm{LE}}+1}^{T}\mathds{1}\{\mathrm{cw}(t)\neq 1\}\right]+\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P^{\prime}}}\mathcal{R}_{m_{l}}(T)

\displaystyle\leq M(T_{\mathrm{LE}}+2D)\Delta_{1\max}+M\Delta_{1\max}f(K,T,Q)+g(K,T,Q)~.

The decomposition above guarantees that communication, once the leader election phase is completed, is only initiated by the leader in rounds where
\mathrm{cw}(t-1)\neq\mathrm{cw}(t)
, and the expected number of such rounds is bounded by
2f
. This completes the proof for the expected regret bound and the bound for the expected number of communication rounds.
∎

Appendix C
Message-Passing RUCB Proofs

In this section, we elaborate on the proof scheme for the theorem and lemmas presented in Section
4
and provide a slightly more general formulation that allows for more flexibility in the choice of constants.

For clarity, it is important to note that the UCB indices calculated by each player
m
for all
i\neq j
are expressed as follows:

u_{ij}^{m}(t):=\frac{\tilde{w}^{m}_{ij}(t-1)}{\tilde{N}^{m}_{ij}(t-1)}+\sqrt{\frac{\alpha\log t}{\tilde{N}^{m}_{ij}(t-1)}}~.

Using this notation, at the beginning of round
t

\tilde{w}^{m}_{ij}(t-1),\tilde{N}^{m}_{ij}(t-1)
to update the UCB terms. The values of
\tilde{w}^{m}_{ij}(t),\tilde{N}^{m}_{ij}(t)
are then updated at the end of the round after the communication phase is concluded.

In the proof scheme presented in this section, we utilize a different probability space compared to the one defined in Section
A
. We introduce a collection of independent Bernoulli random variables
(q_{ij}^{m}(t))
for
t\in[T],i,j\in[K],m\in[M]
, satisfying
\mathbb{E}q_{ij}^{m}(t)=q_{ij}~
. Caratheodory’s extension theorem allows us to define this collection of random variables on the probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})=\left(\mathbb{R}^{TMK^{2}},\mathcal{B}\left(\mathbb{R}^{TMK^{2}}\right)\right)
. Under this model, the reward
r_{m}(t)
collected by player
m
at round
t
is represented as
r_{m}(t)=q^{m}_{i_{m}(t)j_{m}(t)}(t)~
. By defining the filtration
\mathcal{F}_{t}=\sigma\left(q_{ij}^{m}(\tau)\right)_{\tau\leq t}
, we ensure that
i_{m}(t),j_{m}(t)\in\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
and
r_{m}(t)\in\mathcal{F}_{t}~
. Subsequently, we define
r_{m}^{ij}(t)
as the reward obtained by player
m
, given that the pair
(i,j)
was drawn at round
t
. Otherwise, it is defined to be
0
.

r_{ij}^{m}(t)=q_{ij}^{m}(t)\mathds{1}\{(i_{m}(t),j_{m}(t))=(i,j)\}+(1-q_{ji}^{m}(t))\mathds{1}\{(i_{m}(t),j_{m}(t))=(j,i)\}~,

In addition, define the LCB terms for all
i,j,m
as:

l_{ij}^{m}(t)=1-u_{ij}^{m}(t)~.

We begin by presenting a concentration bound in the following lemma.

Lemma C.1
.

The following concentration bounds hold for all
i,j,m,t>0,\eta>1
and
\delta>0
:

\displaystyle P\left(q_{ij}<l_{ij}^{m}(t)\right)

\displaystyle<\frac{\log\left(d_{m}(\mathcal{G}_{\gamma})+1\right)t}{t^{2\alpha\eta^{-1/2}}\log\eta}

\displaystyle P\left(q_{ij}>u_{ij}^{m}(t)\right)

\displaystyle<\frac{\log\left(d_{m}(\mathcal{G}_{\gamma})+1\right)t}{t^{2\alpha\eta^{-1/2}}\log\eta}~,

where
\mathcal{G}_{\gamma}
stands for the
\gamma
-power of the graph
\mathcal{G}
, and
d_{m}(\cdot)
is the degree of node
m
on the graph.

Proof.

For
i=j
, this holds by definition. Going forward, we concentrate on the case
i\neq j
. For simplicity, let us denote

\mathds{1}^{m,m^{\prime}}(t,\tau):=\mathds{1}\{d(m,m^{\prime})\leq\min(\gamma,t-\tau+1)\}~,

and

\chi_{ij}^{m,m^{\prime}}(t,\tau):=\mathds{1}\{(i_{m^{\prime}}(\tau),j_{m^{\prime}}(\tau))=(i,j)\}\mathds{1}^{m,m^{\prime}}(t,\tau)~.

This indicator determines whether the arm pair
(i,j)
was drawn by player
m^{\prime}
at round
\tau
and whether it is included in the statistics of player
m
at time
t
. The latter condition is satisfied only if the distance between the nodes on the graph is smaller than
\gamma
and if the time difference between
t
and the drawing time
\tau
is greater than the time it takes to traverse the graph (
d(m,m^{\prime})-1
). Next, for
\tau\leq t
define:

\tilde{r}_{ij}^{m,m^{\prime}}(t,\tau):=q_{ij}^{m^{\prime}}(\tau)\chi_{ij}^{m,m^{\prime}}(t,\tau)+(1-q_{ji}^{m^{\prime}}(\tau))\chi_{ji}^{m,m^{\prime}}(t,\tau)~,

and:

Y_{ij}^{m}(t,\tau):=\sum_{m^{\prime}=1}^{M}\left(\tilde{r}_{ij}^{m,m^{\prime}}(t,\tau)-\mathbb{E}\left[\tilde{r}_{ij}^{m,m^{\prime}}(t,\tau)|\mathcal{F}_{\tau-1}\right]\right)~.

For any player
m
and arm pair
(i,j)
,
Y_{ij}^{m}(t,\tau)
represents the contribution at round
t
of conditional centralized rewards drawn at round
\tau
from all players from which the message can be received at the current round. Since the first indicator in the definition of
\chi_{ij}^{m,m^{\prime}}(t,\tau)
only depends on draws at round
\tau
and the second indicator is deterministic, it holds that
\chi_{ij}^{m,m^{\prime}}(t,\tau)\in\mathcal{F}_{\tau-1}
. Therefore, we can decompose
Y_{ij}^{m}(t,\tau)
as follows:

Y_{ij}^{m}(t,\tau)=\sum_{m^{\prime}=1}^{M}\left((q_{ij}^{m^{\prime}}(\tau)-q_{ij})\chi_{ij}^{m,m^{\prime}}(t,\tau)+(1-q_{ji}^{m^{\prime}}(\tau)-q_{ij})\chi_{ji}^{m,m^{\prime}}(t,\tau)\right)~.

By taking an expected value, it holds that for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\lambda Y_{ij}^{m}(t,\tau)\right)|\mathcal{F}_{\tau-1}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\lambda\sum_{m^{\prime}=1}^{M}\left((q_{ij}^{m^{\prime}}(\tau)-q_{ij})\chi_{ij}^{m,m^{\prime}}(t,\tau)+(1-q_{ji}^{m^{\prime}}(\tau)-q_{ij})\chi_{ji}^{m,m^{\prime}}(t,\tau)\right)\right)\Bigg|\mathcal{F}_{\tau-1}\right]

\displaystyle=\prod_{m^{\prime}=1}^{M}\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\lambda\left((q_{ij}^{m^{\prime}}(\tau)-q_{ij})\chi_{ij}^{m,m^{\prime}}(t,\tau)+(1-q_{ji}^{m^{\prime}}(\tau)-q_{ij})\chi_{ji}^{m,m^{\prime}}(t,\tau)\right)\right)\Big|\mathcal{F}_{\tau-1}\right]

\displaystyle=\prod_{m^{\prime}=1}^{M}\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\lambda(q_{ij}^{m^{\prime}}(\tau)-q_{ij})\chi_{ij}^{m,m^{\prime}}(t,\tau)\right)\big|\mathcal{F}_{\tau-1}\right]

\displaystyle\times\prod_{m^{\prime}=1}^{M}\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\lambda(1-q_{ji}^{m^{\prime}}(\tau)-q_{ij})\chi_{ji}^{m,m^{\prime}}(t,\tau)\right)\big|\mathcal{F}_{\tau-1}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{m^{\prime}=1}^{M}\exp\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}}{8}\chi_{ij}^{m,m^{\prime}}(t,\tau)\right)\exp\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}}{8}\chi_{ji}^{m,m^{\prime}}(t,\tau)\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{m^{\prime}=1}^{M}\exp\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}}{8}\left(\chi_{ij}^{m,m^{\prime}}(t,\tau)+\chi_{ji}^{m,m^{\prime}}(t,\tau)\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}}{8}\sum_{m^{\prime}=1}^{M}\zeta_{ij}^{m,m^{\prime}}(t,\tau)\right)~,

where

\zeta_{ij}^{m,m^{\prime}}(t,\tau):=\zeta^{m^{\prime}}_{ij}(\tau)\mathds{1}^{m,m^{\prime}}(t,\tau)~,

and
\zeta^{m}_{ij}(t)
is an indicator specifying whether the pair
(i,j)
was drawn by player
m
at round
t
in any order:

\zeta_{ij}^{m}(t):=\mathds{1}\{(i_{m}(t),j_{m}(t))\in\{(i,j),(j,i)\}\}~.

In the second transition above, we leverage the independence of
q_{ij}^{m}(t)
across different players and arms, and the fact that all other terms are determined by conditioning on
\mathcal{F}_{\tau-1}
. The third transition follows a similar logic. For the fourth transition, we use the fact that both
q_{ij}^{m}(t)-q_{ij}
and
1-q_{ji}^{m}(t)-q_{ij}
are centered Bernoulli random variables, and as such, they are also
1/2-
sub-Gaussian. The inequality above implies that:

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\lambda Y_{ij}^{m}(t,\tau)-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{8}\sum_{m^{\prime}=1}^{M}\zeta_{ij}^{m,m^{\prime}}(t,\tau)\right)|\mathcal{F}_{\tau-1}\right]\leq 1~.

Note that the visitation counter utilized by players can be decomposed as the following sum of contributions from all players and prior rounds:

\displaystyle\tilde{N}_{ij}^{m}(t)=\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}\sum_{m^{\prime}=1}^{M}\zeta_{ij}^{m,m^{\prime}}(t,\tau)~.

Next, define:

Z_{ij}^{m}(t):=\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}Y_{ij}^{m}(t,\tau)~.

It holds that:

\displaystyle Y_{ij}^{m}(t,\tau)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m^{\prime}=1}^{M}\left(r_{ij}^{m^{\prime}}(\tau)\mathds{1}^{m,m^{\prime}}(t,\tau)-q_{ij}\zeta^{m^{\prime}}_{ij}(\tau)\mathds{1}^{m,m^{\prime}}(t,\tau)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m^{\prime}=1}^{M}r_{ij}^{m^{\prime}}(\tau)\mathds{1}^{m,m^{\prime}}(t,\tau)-q_{ij}\sum_{m^{\prime}=1}^{M}\zeta^{m,m^{\prime}}_{ij}(t,\tau)~,

meaning that
Z_{ij}^{m}(t)
is a random variable representing the centered number of wins:

Z_{ij}^{m}(t)=\tilde{w}^{m}_{ij}(t)-q_{ij}\tilde{N}^{m}_{ij}(t)~.

Employing the previous inequality, it holds that:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\lambda Z_{ij}^{m}(t)-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{8}\tilde{N}_{ij}^{m}(t)\right)\Bigg|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\lambda Y_{ij}^{m}(t,t)-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{8}\sum_{m^{\prime}=1}^{M}\zeta_{ij}^{m,m^{\prime}}(t,t)\right.\right.

\displaystyle\left.\left.+\lambda\sum_{\tau=1}^{t-1}Y_{ij}^{m}(t,\tau)-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{8}\sum_{\tau=1}^{t-1}\sum_{m^{\prime}=1}^{M}\zeta_{ij}^{m,m^{\prime}}(t,\tau)\right)\Bigg|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\lambda Y_{ij}^{m}(t,t)-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{8}\sum_{m^{\prime}=1}^{M}\zeta_{ij}^{m,m^{\prime}}(t,t)\right)\Bigg|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]

\displaystyle\times\exp\left(\lambda\sum_{\tau=1}^{t-1}Y_{ij}^{m}(t,\tau)-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{8}\sum_{\tau=1}^{t-1}\sum_{m^{\prime}=1}^{M}\zeta_{ij}^{m,m^{\prime}}(t,\tau)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(\lambda\sum_{\tau=1}^{t-1}Y_{ij}^{m}(t,\tau)-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{8}\sum_{\tau=1}^{t-1}\sum_{m^{\prime}=1}^{M}\zeta_{ij}^{m,m^{\prime}}(t,\tau)\right)~,

where the second transition follows the fact that samples collected before time
t
are determined by
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
. By extending this inequality further in time and applying the tower rule, we obtain the following:

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\lambda Z_{ij}^{m}(t)-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{8}\tilde{N}_{ij}^{m}(t)\right)\right]\leq 1~.

Next, from the Markov inequality it holds that for any
\theta,\kappa,\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
:

e^{-2\kappa\theta}\geq P\left(\exp\left(\lambda Z_{ij}^{m}(t)-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{8}\tilde{N}_{ij}^{m}(t)\right)\geq e^{2\kappa\theta}\right)=P\left(\lambda Z_{ij}^{m}(t)-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{8}\tilde{N}_{ij}^{m}(t)\geq 2\kappa\theta\right)~.

While this is valid for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, dividing the subsequent steps into segments for
\lambda>0
and
\lambda<0
will facilitate the proofs of the first and second concentration bounds, respectively. Given the similarity between the two proofs, we concentrate on the case
\lambda>0
in the following. It is evident that:

P\left(\frac{Z_{ij}^{m}(t)}{\sqrt{\tilde{N}_{ij}^{m}(t)}}\geq\frac{2\kappa\theta}{\lambda\sqrt{\tilde{N}_{ij}^{m}(t)}}+\frac{\lambda}{8}\sqrt{\tilde{N}_{ij}^{m}(t)}\right)\leq e^{-2\kappa\theta}~.

Next, we note that for any pair of arms
(i,j)
, player
m
can receive no more than
d_{m}(\mathcal{G}_{\gamma})
samples at each round from other players. Hence,
1\leq\tilde{N}_{ij}^{m}(t)\leq\left(1+d_{m}(\mathcal{G}_{\gamma})\right)t
. Utilizing this fact, for any
\eta>1
, it holds that
1\leq\tilde{N}_{ij}^{m}(t)\leq\eta^{D_{t}}
for:

D_{t}:=\frac{\log\left(1+d_{m}(\mathcal{G}_{\gamma})\right)t}{\log\eta}~.

This enables us to address the randomness in
Z_{ij}^{m}(t),\tilde{N}_{ij}^{m}(t)
by employing a peeling argument and partitioning the potential values of
\tilde{N}_{ij}^{m}(t)
into intervals
[\eta^{l-1},\eta^{l}]
for
1\leq l\leq D_{t}
. For this purpose, we introduce:

\lambda_{l}:=4\sqrt{\frac{\kappa\theta}{\eta^{l-0.5}}}~,\kappa=\eta^{-\frac{1}{2}}~.

For
\tilde{N}_{ij}^{m}(t)\in[\eta^{l-1},\eta^{l}]
it holds that:

\frac{2\kappa\theta}{\lambda_{l}\sqrt{\tilde{N}_{ij}^{m}(t)}}+\frac{\lambda_{l}}{8}\sqrt{\tilde{N}_{ij}^{m}(t)}=\frac{\sqrt{\kappa\theta}}{2}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\eta^{l-0.5}}{\tilde{N}_{ij}^{m}(t)}}+\sqrt{\frac{\tilde{N}_{ij}^{m}(t)}{\eta^{l-0.5}}}\right)\leq\sqrt{\theta}~,

and by summing over the different intervals:

\displaystyle P\left(\frac{Z_{ij}^{m}(t)}{\sqrt{\tilde{N}_{ij}^{m}(t)}}\geq\sqrt{\theta}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{l=1}^{D_{t}}P\left(\frac{Z_{ij}^{m}(t)}{\sqrt{\tilde{N}_{ij}^{m}(t)}}\geq\sqrt{\theta},\tilde{N}_{ij}^{m}(t)\in[\eta^{l-1},\eta^{l}]\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{l=1}^{D_{t}}P\left(\frac{Z_{ij}^{m}(t)}{\sqrt{\tilde{N}_{ij}^{m}(t)}}\geq\frac{2\kappa\theta}{\lambda_{l}\sqrt{\tilde{N}_{ij}^{m}(t)}}+\frac{\lambda_{l}}{8}\sqrt{\tilde{N}_{ij}^{m}(t)},~\tilde{N}_{ij}^{m}(t)\in[\eta^{l-1},\eta^{l}]\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{l=1}^{D_{t}}P\left(\frac{Z_{ij}^{m}(t)}{\sqrt{\tilde{N}_{ij}^{m}(t)}}\geq\frac{2\kappa\theta}{\lambda_{l}\sqrt{\tilde{N}_{ij}^{m}(t)}}+\frac{\lambda_{l}}{8}\sqrt{\tilde{N}_{ij}^{m}(t)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{l=1}^{D_{t}}e^{-2\kappa\theta}=\frac{\log\left(1+d_{m}(\mathcal{G}_{\gamma})\right)t}{\log\eta}e^{-2\kappa\theta}~.

To conclude, substitute
\theta=\alpha\log t
:

\displaystyle P\left(q_{ij}\leq l_{ij}^{m}(t+1)\right)

\displaystyle=P\left(\frac{\tilde{w}^{m}_{ij}(t)}{\tilde{N}^{m}_{ij}(t)}-q_{ij}\geq\sqrt{\frac{\alpha\log t}{\tilde{N}^{m}_{ij}(t)}}\right)

\displaystyle=P\left(\frac{Z^{m}_{ij}(t)}{\tilde{N}^{m}_{ij}(t)}\geq\sqrt{\frac{\alpha\log t}{\tilde{N}^{m}_{ij}(t)}}\right)\leq\frac{\left(1+d_{m}(\mathcal{G}_{\gamma})\right)(t+1)}{\log\eta}\frac{1}{t^{2\alpha\eta^{-1/2}}}~.

The concentration bound in the theorem results from replacing
t+1
with
t
.
∎

The next lemma demonstrates that with high probability,
q_{ij}\leq u_{ij}^{m}(t)
for all players, arm pairs, and rounds that are sufficiently large.

Lemma C.2
.

For any
\epsilon>0,\delta>0
, and
\alpha
such that
\alpha>0.5\sqrt{\eta}(1+\epsilon)
, define:

T_{\epsilon}=\begin{cases}\left(\frac{-W_{-1}(-\epsilon)}{\epsilon}\right)^{\frac{1}{\epsilon}}&if $\epsilon\in(0,e^{-1})$\\
0&if $\epsilon\geq e^{-1}$~,\end{cases}

where
W_{-1}(\cdot)
is the lower part of the Lambert function. Additionally, define:

C_{\epsilon}(\delta):=\max\left(e^{\frac{1}{\epsilon}},T_{\epsilon},\left(\frac{MK^{2}\left(3+2\log\left(d_{\max}^{\gamma}+1\right)\right)}{\min\{1,2\alpha\eta^{-1/2}-1\}\delta\log\eta}\right)^{\frac{1}{2\alpha\eta^{-1/2}-1-\epsilon}}\right)~,

where
d_{\max}^{\gamma}:=\max_{m}\left(d_{m}(\mathcal{G}_{\gamma})\right)~
. UCB terms
u_{ij}^{m}(t)
satisfy:

P\left(\forall t\geq C_{\epsilon}(\delta),i,j,m:~q_{ij}\leq u_{ij}^{m}(t)\right)>1-\delta~.

Proof.

Utilizing Lemma
C.1
, it holds that:

\displaystyle P\left(\exists t\geq C_{\epsilon}(\delta),i,j,m:~q_{ij}>u_{ij}^{m}(t)\right)

\displaystyle\leq MK^{2}\sum_{t=C_{\epsilon}(\delta)}^{\infty}\frac{\log\left(d_{\max}^{\gamma}+1\right)t}{t^{2\alpha\eta^{-1/2}}\log\eta}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{MK^{2}}{\log\eta}\sum_{t=C_{\epsilon}(\delta)}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\log t}{t^{2\alpha\eta^{-1/2}}}+\frac{\log\left(d_{\max}^{\gamma}+1\right)}{t^{2\alpha\eta^{-1/2}}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{MK^{2}}{\log\eta}\left[\frac{\log C_{\epsilon}(\delta)}{C_{\epsilon}(\delta)^{2\alpha\eta^{-1/2}}}+\frac{\log\left(d_{\max}^{\gamma}+1\right)}{C_{\epsilon}(\delta)^{2\alpha\eta^{-1/2}}}+\int_{C_{\epsilon}(\delta)}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\log t}{t^{2\alpha\eta^{-1/2}}}+\frac{\log\left(d_{\max}^{\gamma}+1\right)}{t^{2\alpha\eta^{-1/2}}}\right)dt\right]~,

where we took advantage of the fact that for
t>C_{\epsilon}(\delta)>e
, the functions
\log t/t^{2\alpha\eta^{-1/2}}
and
1/t^{2\alpha\eta^{-1/2}}
are decreasing. Calculating the integrals above:

\displaystyle P\left(\exists t\geq C_{\epsilon}(\delta),i,j,m:~q_{ij}>u_{ij}^{m}(t)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{MK^{2}}{\log\eta}\left[\frac{\log C_{\epsilon}(\delta)}{C_{\epsilon}(\delta)^{2\alpha\eta^{-1/2}}}+\frac{\log\left(d_{\max}^{\gamma}+1\right)}{C_{\epsilon}(\delta)^{2\alpha\eta^{-1/2}}}+\right.

\displaystyle+\left.\frac{(2\alpha\eta^{-1/2}-1)\log C_{\epsilon}(\delta)+1}{(2\alpha\eta^{-1/2}-1)^{2}C_{\epsilon}(\delta)^{2\alpha\eta^{-1/2}-1}}+\frac{\log\left(d_{\max}^{\gamma}+1\right)}{(2\alpha\eta^{-1/2}-1)C_{\epsilon}(\delta)^{2\alpha\eta^{-1/2}-1}}\right]~.

For clarity, we denote
\overline{T}:=C_{\epsilon}(\delta)
in the remainder of this proof. To evaluate the right-hand side above, we are interested in the approximation
\log\overline{T}\leq\overline{T}^{\epsilon}~
for some
\epsilon>0
. For
\epsilon\geq e^{-1}
, this holds for all
\overline{T}>0
, and for
\epsilon\in(0,e^{-1})
this is true for
\overline{T}\geq\left(-W_{-1}(-\epsilon)/\epsilon\right)^{1/\epsilon}~
, where
W_{-1}(\cdot)
stands for the lower part of the Lambert function. Therefore, when this inequality holds:

\displaystyle P\left(\exists t\geq C_{\epsilon}(\delta),i,j,m:~q_{ij}>u_{ij}^{m}(t)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{MK^{2}}{\log\eta}\left[\frac{\log\overline{T}}{\overline{T}^{2\alpha\eta^{-1/2}}}+\frac{\log\left(d_{\max}^{\gamma}+1\right)}{\overline{T}^{2\alpha\eta^{-1/2}}}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\frac{2\log\overline{T}}{(2\alpha\eta^{-1/2}-1)\overline{T}^{2\alpha\eta^{-1/2}-1}}+\frac{\log\left(d_{\max}^{\gamma}+1\right)}{(2\alpha\eta^{-1/2}-1)\overline{T}^{2\alpha\eta^{-1/2}-1}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{MK^{2}}{\log\eta}\left[\frac{1}{\overline{T}^{2\alpha\eta^{-1/2}-1-\epsilon}}+\frac{\log\left(d_{\max}^{\gamma}+1\right)}{\overline{T}^{2\alpha\eta^{-1/2}-1-\epsilon}}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\frac{2}{(2\alpha\eta^{-1/2}-1)\overline{T}^{2\alpha\eta^{-1/2}-1-\epsilon}}+\frac{\log\left(d_{\max}^{\gamma}+1\right)}{(2\alpha\eta^{-1/2}-1)\overline{T}^{2\alpha\eta^{-1/2}-1-\epsilon}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{MK^{2}\left(3+2\log\left(d_{\max}^{\gamma}+1\right)\right)}{\min\{1,2\alpha\eta^{-1/2}-1\}\log\eta}\frac{1}{\overline{T}^{2\alpha\eta^{-1/2}-1-\epsilon}}~,

where we also used the fact that
\overline{T}>e^{1/\epsilon}
. By requiring the right-hand side to be smaller than
\delta
, we have:

C_{\epsilon}(\delta)\geq\left(\frac{MK^{2}\left(3+2\log\left(d_{\max}^{\gamma}+1\right)\right)}{\min\{1,2\alpha\eta^{-1/2}-1\}\delta\log\eta}\right)^{\frac{1}{2\alpha\eta^{-1/2}-1-\epsilon}}~.

The proof concludes by combining the requirements on
C_{\epsilon}(\delta)
mentioned above.
∎

Next, we show that with high probability, players in a clique cannot visit arm pairs different from

for too many rounds. For brevity, we define:

D_{ij}:=\begin{cases}4\alpha/\overline{\Delta}_{ij}^{2}&if $i\neq j$\\

Lemma C.3
.

For any
\epsilon>0,\delta>0
,
\gamma^{\prime}\leq\gamma

\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{G}_{\gamma^{\prime}})

. The number of visitations satisfies:

\displaystyle P\left(\exists t>T_{0},(i,j)\neq(1,1),\gamma^{\prime}\leq\gamma,C\in\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{G}_{\gamma^{\prime}}):~\sum_{m\in C}N_{ij}^{m,T_{0}}(t)>D_{ij}\log t+(\gamma^{\prime}+2)|C|\right)

\displaystyle<\delta~.

For the remainder of this proof, we assume that the good event
\{\forall t\geq C_{\epsilon}(\delta),i,j,m:~q_{ij}\leq u_{ij}^{m}(t)\}
holds, which occurs with probability
1-\delta
according to Lemma
C.2
. First, for draws of identical arms
(i,i)\neq(1,1)
during rounds

, the RUCB decision rule stipulates that player

can only draw arm
i
as both the first and the second arm if

for all

, since it holds that

. However, given the good event,

, which is a contradiction. Hence, pairs of identical arms, apart from
(1,1)
, cannot be drawn under this event. Consequently, for any
C\in\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{G}_{\gamma^{\prime}})
it holds that
\sum_{m\in C}N_{ii}^{m,T_{0}}(t)=0<D_{ii}\log t+(\gamma^{\prime}+1)|C|
.

For pairs of non-identical arms
i\neq j
with some given
C\in\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{G}_{\gamma^{\prime}})
, let us assume that these arms have been compared for a sufficiently large number of rounds between
T_{0}
and
t
, such that
\sum_{m\in C}N_{ij}^{m,T_{0}}(t-1)>D_{ij}\log t+(\gamma^{\prime}+1)|C|
. Note that this also dictates
t\geq\gamma^{\prime}+1+T_{0}
. Let
s
denote the last round in which this pair was drawn by any player in
C
, such that
s\geq T_{0}+\gamma^{\prime}+1

1
1

1

During round
s
, several players may have drawn this pair.
. Next, Consider several cases for the draw at round
s
, and notice that Algorithm
2
dictates that the first arm for each player
m
is always chosen from the winners set
\mathcal{C}^{m}
, which necessarily contains the CW and is thus non-empty:

•

For
i,j\neq 1
, assume that
i_{m}(s)=i,j_{m}(s)=j
. It holds that
u^{m}_{ij}(s)\geq 0.5
, and
l_{ij}^{m}(s):=1-u_{ji}^{m}(s)\leq 1-u_{1i}^{m}(s)\leq 1-q_{1i}(s)
. Therefore,
u_{ij}^{m}(s)-l_{ij}^{m}(s)\geq\Delta_{1i}
.

•

For
i,j\neq 1

u_{ji}^{m}(s)-l_{ji}^{m}(s)\geq\Delta_{1j}

u_{1i}^{m}(s)\geq q_{1i}
and
l_{1i}^{m}(s)=1-u_{i1}^{m}(s)\leq 0.5
. Hence,

.

For

to be drawn, it must happen that

and

\displaystyle u_{ij}^{m}(s)-l_{ij}^{m}(s)=u_{ij}^{m}(s)+u_{ji}^{m}(s)-1=2\sqrt{\frac{\alpha\log s}{\tilde{N}^{m}_{ij}(s-1)}}~.

Utilizing the assumption about the number of visits, it holds that:

\displaystyle\tilde{N}^{m}_{ij}(s-1)\geq\tilde{N}^{m,T_{0}}_{ij}(s-1)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{m\in C}N_{ij}^{m,T_{0}}(s-\gamma^{\prime}-1)\geq\sum_{m\in C}N_{ij}^{m,T_{0}}(s-1)-\gamma^{\prime}|C|

\displaystyle>D_{ij}\log t+(\gamma^{\prime}+1)|C|-|C|-\gamma^{\prime}|C|

\displaystyle=D_{ij}\log t~,

By the definition of

, this is a contradiction. Therefore, under the good event

for all

,
(i,j)\neq(1,1),C\in\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{G}_{\gamma^{\prime}})
. Hence,

, with probability

Lemma C.4

Proof.

To prove the lemma, it is sufficient to find a value of

. We will demonstrate that

\displaystyle h(K,\gamma^{\prime},T_{0})+a\log\tau

\displaystyle=h(K,\gamma^{\prime},T_{0})+a\log\left(2h(K,\gamma^{\prime},T_{0})+2a\log(2a)\right)

\displaystyle=h(K,\gamma^{\prime},T_{0})+a\log\left(2a\log(2a)\right)+a\log\left(1+\frac{2h(K,\gamma^{\prime},T_{0})}{2a\log(2a)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq h(K,\gamma^{\prime},T_{0})+a\log\left((2a)^{2}\right)+h(K,\gamma^{\prime},T_{0})

Before presenting the regret bound in its most general form, we introduce the following term:

Theorem C.5
.

For any
\epsilon>0
,
\gamma^{\prime}\leq\gamma
and
\delta>0
, denote
\Gamma(m,\gamma^{\prime})
as the size of the largest clique player

belongs to in
\mathcal{G}_{\gamma^{\prime}}
, and define,

\displaystyle\hat{C}_{\epsilon}(\delta):

\displaystyle=\Delta_{1\max}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\min_{\gamma^{\prime}\leq\gamma}\left(K^{2}(\gamma^{\prime}+2)+\frac{2\mathcal{D}}{\Gamma(m,\gamma^{\prime})}\log(\frac{2\mathcal{D}}{\Gamma(m,\gamma^{\prime})})\right)

\displaystyle+(2C_{\epsilon}(\delta)+K(3\gamma+2))M\Delta_{1\max}~,

and

\displaystyle\hat{C}_{\epsilon}:

\displaystyle=\Delta_{1\max}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\min_{\gamma^{\prime}\leq\gamma}\left(K^{2}(\gamma^{\prime}+2)+\frac{2\mathcal{D}}{\Gamma(m,\gamma^{\prime})}\log(\frac{2\mathcal{D}}{\Gamma(m,\gamma^{\prime})})\right)

\displaystyle+(2\tilde{C}_{\epsilon}+K(3\gamma+2))M\Delta_{1\max}~.

Let
\chi(\mathcal{G}_{\gamma})
denote the clique covering number of
\mathcal{G}_{\gamma}
. Then the following holds for Algorithm
2
.

(a)

For any
\alpha
such that
\alpha>0.5\sqrt{\eta}(1+\epsilon)
and all times

, with probability larger than

\mathcal{R}(T)\leq\sum_{j>1}\frac{2\alpha\chi(\mathcal{G}_{\gamma})}{\Delta_{1j}}\log T+\hat{C}_{\epsilon}(\delta)

(b)

For any
\alpha
such that
\alpha>0.5\sqrt{\eta}(2+\epsilon)
and all times
T
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathcal{R}(T)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j>1}\frac{2\alpha\chi(\mathcal{G}_{\gamma})}{\Delta_{1j}}\log T+\hat{C}_{\epsilon}~.

Proof.

First, we observe the following fact. For
t>C_{\epsilon}(\delta)
with probability larger than
1-\delta
, no pair
(i,i)\neq(1,1)
can be drawn by any player. Since every player

recommends an arm
i

, messages received at rounds

contain
\mathcal{B}^{m}_{\mathrm{rec}}\in\{\emptyset,1\}
.

For any
\gamma^{\prime}(m)\leq\gamma
denote by

as a maximal clique in
\mathcal{G}_{\gamma^{\prime}(m)}
that player
m
belongs to with the maximal size, and denote its size as
\Gamma(m,\gamma^{\prime}(m))
.
The proof for the first part relies on the following observation. Lemma
C.3
implies that with probability
1-\delta
, for all such cliques and all players
m
, times
t>T_{0}=C_{\epsilon}(\delta)+\gamma
and arm pairs
(i,j)\neq(1,1)
:

\sum_{m\in C_{\gamma^{\prime}(m)}^{m}}N_{ij}^{m,T_{0}}(t)\leq D_{ij}\log t+(\gamma^{\prime}(m)+2)\Gamma(m,\gamma^{\prime}(m))~.

Let
\hat{T}^{m}_{\epsilon}(\delta)
be a round such that
\hat{T}^{m}_{\epsilon}(\delta)>C_{\epsilon}(\delta)+\gamma+\frac{K(K-1)}{2}(\gamma^{\prime}(m)+2)+\frac{\mathcal{D}}{\Gamma(m,\gamma^{\prime}(m))}\log\hat{T}^{m}_{\epsilon}(\delta)
. At round
\hat{T}^{m}_{\epsilon}(\delta)
, it holds that:

\displaystyle\sum_{m\in C_{\gamma^{\prime}(m)}^{m}}N_{11}^{m,T_{0}}\left(\hat{T}^{m}_{\epsilon}(\delta)\right)=\Gamma(m,\gamma^{\prime}(m))(\hat{T}^{m}_{\epsilon}(\delta)-C_{\epsilon}(\delta)-\gamma)-\sum_{i<j}\sum_{m\in C_{\gamma^{\prime}(m)}^{m}}N_{ij}^{m,T_{0}}\left(\hat{T}^{m}_{\epsilon}(\delta)\right)

\displaystyle\geq\Gamma(m,\gamma^{\prime}(m))(\hat{T}^{m}_{\epsilon}(\delta)-C_{\epsilon}(\delta)-\gamma)-\sum_{i<j}D_{ij}\log\hat{T}^{m}_{\epsilon}(\delta)-\frac{K(K-1)}{2}(\gamma^{\prime}(m)+2)\Gamma(m,\gamma^{\prime}(m))

\displaystyle>0~,

where we used the fact that at each round there are
\Gamma(m,\gamma^{\prime}(m))
draws in the clique
C_{\gamma^{\prime}(m)}^{m}
. Therefore, for at least one player
m^{\prime}\in C_{\gamma^{\prime}(m)}^{m}
the pair
(1,1)
is drawn at least once between rounds
C_{\epsilon}(\delta)+\gamma
and
\hat{T}^{m}_{\epsilon}(\delta)~
. We now consider two scenarios:

•

If
m=m^{\prime}
, at the round of this first draw, for all
j>1
, we have
u^{m}_{j1}<0.5
, so that arm
1
is the only arm in the set
\mathcal{C}^{m}
and
\mathcal{B}^{m}=\{1\}
for this round regardless of what recommendations arrive.

•

If
m^{\prime}\neq m
, a recommendation about arm
1
arrives to player
m
before round
\hat{T}^{m}_{\epsilon}(\delta)+\gamma^{\prime}(m)~
. At that round
\mathcal{B}^{m}_{\mathrm{rec}}\cap\mathcal{C}^{m}=\{1\}
since a recommendation about no other arm is possible, and so it updates
\mathcal{B}^{m}=1
.

In both scenarios, player
m
updates its
\mathcal{B}^{m}
set to
1
before round
\hat{T}^{m}_{\epsilon}(\delta)+\gamma^{\prime}(m)~
. Since arm
1
remains in
\mathcal{C}^{m}
for subsequent rounds and no recommendation other than
1
or
\emptyset
arrives, the set
\mathcal{B}^{m}
never changes going forward - so the first arm drawn after round
\hat{T}^{m}_{\epsilon}(\delta)+\gamma^{\prime}(m)
by player
m
is always arm
1
.

By Lemma
C.4
it holds that for any
\gamma^{\prime}(m)\leq\gamma
:

\hat{T}^{m}_{\epsilon}(\delta)\leq 2C_{\epsilon}(\delta)+2\gamma+K(K-1)(\gamma^{\prime}(m)+2)+\frac{2\mathcal{D}}{\Gamma(m,\gamma^{\prime}(m))}\log(\frac{2\mathcal{D}}{\Gamma(m,\gamma^{\prime}(m))})~,

so with probability
1-\delta
decompose the regret with respect to the different cliques in
\mathcal{C}_{\gamma}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(T)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\leq j}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\frac{\Delta_{1i}+\Delta_{1j}}{2}N_{ij}^{m}(T)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\leq j}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\frac{\Delta_{1i}+\Delta_{1j}}{2}N_{ij}^{m}(\hat{T}^{m}_{\epsilon}(\delta)+\gamma^{\prime}(m))

\displaystyle+\sum_{i\leq j}\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{\gamma}}\sum_{m\in C}\frac{\Delta_{1i}+\Delta_{1j}}{2}N_{ij}^{m,\hat{T}^{m}_{\epsilon}(\delta)+\gamma^{\prime}(m)}(T)

\displaystyle\leq\Delta_{1\max}\sum_{m=1}^{M}(\hat{T}^{m}_{\epsilon}(\delta)+\gamma^{\prime}(m))+\sum_{j>1}\frac{\Delta_{1j}}{2}\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{\gamma}}\sum_{m\in C}N_{ij}^{m,\hat{T}^{m}_{\epsilon}(\delta)+\gamma^{\prime}(m)}(T)

\displaystyle\leq\Delta_{1\max}\sum_{m=1}^{M}(\hat{T}^{m}_{\epsilon}(\delta)+\gamma^{\prime}(m))+\sum_{j>1}\frac{\Delta_{1j}}{2}\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{\gamma}}\sum_{m\in C}N_{ij}^{m,C_{\epsilon}(\delta)}(T)

\displaystyle\leq\Delta_{1\max}\sum_{m=1}^{M}(\hat{T}^{m}_{\epsilon}(\delta)+\gamma^{\prime}(m))+\sum_{j>1}\frac{\Delta_{1j}}{2}\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{\gamma}}\left(D_{1j}\log T+(\gamma+2)|C|\right)

\displaystyle\leq\Delta_{1\max}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\left(K^{2}(\gamma^{\prime}(m)+2)+\frac{2\mathcal{D}}{\Gamma(m,\gamma^{\prime}(m))}\log(\frac{2\mathcal{D}}{\Gamma(m,\gamma^{\prime}(m))})\right)

\displaystyle+(2C_{\epsilon}(\delta)+K(3\gamma+2))M\Delta_{1\max}+\sum_{j>1}\frac{\Delta_{1j}}{2}\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{\gamma}}D_{1j}\log T~.

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(T)

\displaystyle\leq\Delta_{1\max}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\min_{\gamma^{\prime}\leq\gamma}\left(K^{2}(\gamma^{\prime}+2)+\frac{2\mathcal{D}}{\Gamma(m,\gamma^{\prime})}\log(\frac{2\mathcal{D}}{\Gamma(m,\gamma^{\prime})})\right)

\displaystyle+(2C_{\epsilon}(\delta)+K(3\gamma+2))M\Delta_{1\max}+\sum_{j>1}\frac{\Delta_{1j}}{2}\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{\gamma}}D_{1j}\log T

\displaystyle\leq\hat{C}_{\epsilon}(\delta)+\sum_{j>1}\frac{\Delta_{1j}}{2}\chi(G_{\gamma})D_{1j}\log T~.

To prove the second part, note that the following holds for any random variable X:
\mathbb{E}X=\int_{0}^{1}F_{X}^{-1}(q)dq~
, where
F_{X}^{-1}(\cdot)
stands for the quantile function of
X
. For some invertible function
H(q)
, given that
F_{\mathcal{R}(T)}(H(q))=P\left(\mathcal{R}(T)\leq H(q)\right)>q
, it holds that

depends on

. For clarity, we denote:

\hat{C}_{\epsilon}(\delta)=\max\left(A,\left(\frac{B}{\delta}\right)^{\frac{1}{2\alpha\eta^{-1/2}-1-\epsilon}}\right)~,

where

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\left(\frac{1}{1-q}\right)^{\frac{1}{2\alpha\eta^{-1/2}-1-\epsilon}}dq=\int_{0}^{1}\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)^{\frac{1}{2\alpha\eta^{-1/2}-1-\epsilon}}d\delta=\frac{2\alpha\eta^{-1/2}-2-\epsilon}{2\alpha\eta^{-1/2}-1-\epsilon}~.

Putting it all together we have that:

\mathbb{E}\mathcal{R}(T)\leq\hat{C}_{\epsilon}+\sum_{j>1}\frac{\Delta_{1j}}{2}\chi(\mathcal{G}_{\gamma})D_{1j}\log T~.

∎

Since a minimization is involved in the calculation of this term, we can observe the two extreme cases where
