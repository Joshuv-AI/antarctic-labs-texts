1.
Introduction

This article is concerned with finding the unitary parts of contractive Toeplitz operators with operator-valued symbols on vector-valued Hardy spaces. For any Hilbert space
\mathcal{E}
, the
\mathcal{E}
-valued Hardy space on the unit disc
\mathbb{D}
in the complex plane is defined by

H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D}):=\{f=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{n}z^{n}\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{D},\mathcal{E}):a_{n}\in\mathcal{E},\|f\|:=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\|a_{n}\|_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}<\infty\}.

The space of operator-valued bounded analytic functions on
\mathbb{D}
is denoted by
H_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E})}^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
, where
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E})
denotes the class of all bounded operators on
\mathcal{E}
. Let us also recall that
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is said to be a
contraction
if
\|T\|\leq 1
.
Toeplitz operators are defined in the following manner.

Definition 1.1
.

A bounded linear operator
T
on
H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})
is said to be Toeplitz if there exists an operator-valued function
\Phi\in L_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E})}^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})
such that
T=P_{H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})}L_{\Phi}|_{H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})},
where
L_{\Phi}
is the Laurent operator (multiplication operator) associated to
\Phi.
In this case,
T
is denoted by
T_{\Phi}
, and the function
\Phi
is called the symbol of the operator
T_{\Phi}.

A starting point of several studies on operators involves finding an orthogonal decomposition of the Hilbert space into unitary and non-unitary parts. Following the celebrated monograph
[
16
]
, we define a completely non-unitary operator as the following:

Definition 1.2
.

T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is said to be completely non-unitary (c.n.u.) if there does not exist a
T
-reducing subspace
\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
such that
T|_{\mathcal{M}}
is a unitary.

Contractions on Hilbert spaces admit a canonical orthogonal decomposition into unitary and c.n.u. parts as can be seen in the following result
[
16
]
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
T
be a contraction on
\mathcal{H}
, then
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{U}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{\text{c.n.u.}}
, where
\mathcal{H}_{U},\mathcal{H}_{\text{c.n.u.}}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
are
T
-reducing closed subspaces, and therefore

T=T|_{\mathcal{H}_{U}}+T|_{\mathcal{H}_{\text{c.n.u.}}}.

Moreover,
T|_{\mathcal{H}_{U}}
is unitary and
T|_{\mathcal{H}_{\text{c.n.u.}}}
is completely non-unitary.

From its inception, c.n.u. contractions have played a major role in classifying contractions. The most striking result is the explicit functional model for c.n.u. contractions established by Nagy and Foias (
[
16
]
). We refer the reader to the articles
[
3
,
4
,
9
,
14
,
15
,
21
]
for several interesting aspects of c.n.u. contractions on Hilbert spaces. Thus, it is a central question in operator theory to decide:
when does a contraction become completely non-unitary?
It is evident from Theorem
1.3
, that the most natural approach to finding conditions for c.n.u. contractions depend on the understanding of the unitary subspace. Motivated by this, let us put the following definition.

Definition
.

T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is said to have a
unitary part
if there exists a non-zero
T
-reducing subspace
\mathcal{M}_{T}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
such that
T|_{\mathcal{M}_{T}}
is unitary.

From the advent of the theory of Toeplitz operators following the work of Brown and Halmos in
[
6
]
, it is an intriguing and challenging question to find the correspondence between operator theoretic properties of
T_{\Phi}
and function-theoretic properties of
\Phi
. The understanding of this interplay is far from complete for the case of Toeplitz operators with operator-valued symbols as many natural questions remain unanswered. This motivates us to raise the following question.

Which Toeplitz operators on Hardy spaces on the unit disc admit a unitary part?

Goor answered this question (
[
13
]
) for Toeplitz operators on
H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
by proving the following surprising result:
any non-constant contractive
T_{\phi}
is always completely non-unitary.
Goor’s result can be stated in the following equivalent manner.

Theorem

(Goor)
.

Any Toeplitz operator
T_{\phi}
on
H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
with a non-constant symbol
\phi
and
\|T_{\phi}\|\leq 1
, does not admit a unitary part.

A natural question is to seek analogous results for Toeplitz operators with operator-valued symbols on vector-valued Hardy spaces. A fundamental challenge that lies for operator-valued symbols is that, unlike the scalar-valued cases, we no longer have the commutativity of the symbols. This is a reason why we need new approaches to tackle the general problem that often brings out a better understanding of the connection between a Toeplitz operator and its symbol. In this article, we have achieved the following result which gives a complete characterization for Toeplitz operators admitting a unitary part in
H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})
.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
T_{\Phi}
be a contractive Toeplitz operator on
H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})
. Then the following are equivalent

(i)

T_{\Phi}
has a unitary part, say
\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi}}
,

(ii)

there exists a Hilbert space
\mathcal{F}
, an inner function
\Theta(z)\in H_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{E})}^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
and a unitary
U:\mathcal{F}\rightarrow\mathcal{F}
such that

\Phi(e^{it})\Theta(e^{it})=\Theta(e^{it})U\quad\text{and}\quad\Phi(e^{it})^{*}\Theta(e^{it})=\Theta(e^{it})U^{*}\quad(\text{ a.e. on }\mathbb{T}).

In this case
T_{\Phi}|_{\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi}}}
is unitarily equivalent to a constant unitary.

As expected, this result enables us to identify sufficient conditions for Toeplitz operators to become completely non-unitary (see Theorem
5.1
).

We point out that an extension of Goor’s result to the case of vectorial Toeplitz operators was claimed by Butz
[
7
]
. Unfortunately, there is a gap in the proof of the main theorem in
[
7
]
which we point out in Section
3.1
.
The author’s result when applied to
T_{\Phi}
on
H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})
implies that the unitary part of
T_{\Phi}
must be of the form
H_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})
for some closed subspace
\mathcal{E}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{E}
. We show in Theorem
3.6
that this happens only when
T_{\Phi}
restricted to its unitary part is equal to a constant unitary. More precisely, we establish the following result.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
T_{\Phi}
be a contractive Toeplitz operator on
H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})
with a unitary part
\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi}}
. Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi}}=H_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})
for some closed subspace
\mathcal{E}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{E}
,

(ii)

there exists a closed subspace
\mathcal{E}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{E}
such that
\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi}}\subseteq H_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})
, and
T_{\Phi}|_{\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi}}}=T_{W}|_{H_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})}
, for some unitary
W:\mathcal{E}_{0}\rightarrow\mathcal{E}_{0}
,

(iii)

there exists a closed subspace
\mathcal{E}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{E}
such that
\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi}}\subseteq H_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})
, and
\mathcal{E}_{0}
is
\Phi(e^{it})
-reducing and
\Phi(e^{it})|_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}
is a constant unitary a.e. on
\mathbb{T}
.

Note that
T_{W}
is the constant Toeplitz operator
W
acting on the full Hardy space. Section
3.1
is dedicated to proving the above result and also highlights the gap in the proof of the main theorem in
[
7
]
.

Analytic Toeplitz operators, that is, Toeplitz operators with operator-valued bounded analytic symbols play a central role in connecting several topics with Operator theory. There are two main reasons for this. The operator-theoretic advantage is that in this case, the Toeplitz operator becomes simply the multiplication operator. For this reason, we choose to use the following notational convention throughout this article:

\text{If }\Phi(z)\in H_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E})}^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})\text{ then the corrsponding Toeplitz operator is denoted by }M_{\Phi}.

The other advantage lies in the fact that bounded analytic functions admit a realization formula. More precisely, any
\Phi(z)\in H_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E})}^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
admits a transfer-function realization corresponding to a unitary

W:=\begin{bmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{bmatrix}:\mathcal{E}\oplus\mathcal{K}\rightarrow\mathcal{E}\oplus\mathcal{K},

that is,
\Phi(z):=\tau_{W}(z)=A+zB(I_{\mathcal{E}}-zD)^{-1}C
. This way of representing bounded analytic functions has a profound impact on several topics in operator theory and function theory. In particular, analytic Toeplitz operators with no unitary part play a vital role in the characterization of distinguished varieties in the bidisc by Agler and McCarthy in
[
1
]
. Their result shows that a distinguished variety in the bidisc always admits the following description

\mathcal{V}=\{(z,w)\in\mathbb{D}^{2}:\det(\Phi(z)-wI)=0\},

where
\Phi(z)
is a matrix-valued rational inner function in
H_{\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{C}^{n})}^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
. The boundary behaviour of the distinguished variety is closely related to the fact that
M_{\Phi}
on
H_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})
does not admit a unitary part. This connection has been further explored in some recent works
[
10
,
11
]
.

Thus, it is evident that characterizing Toeplitz operators on
H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})
with unitary parts will have far-reaching consequences. Through the realization formula of
\Phi(z)
it is easy to observe that the existence of the unitary part of
\Phi(0)
will always imply that
M_{\Phi}
will have a unitary part. Several examples in the literature show that the converse does not hold. For instance, see
[
11
, Remark 3.2]
. So, the main challenge lies in identifying the conditions which together with the existence of the unitary part of
M_{\Phi}
will imply that
\Phi(0)
will have a unitary part. This we have achieved in this article by showing the following result.

Theorem
.

Let
\Phi(z)\in H_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E})}^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
with
\|\Phi\|_{\infty}\leq 1
. If
M_{\Phi}
has an unitary part
\mathcal{M}_{M_{\Phi}}
, then the condition
\|T_{\Phi(0)}f\|=\|f\|
for all
f\in\mathcal{M}_{M_{\Phi}}
implies that
\Phi(0)
has a unitary part.

This result shows that for investigating the unitary parts of
M_{\Phi}
, we need to consider
\Phi(0)
as an operator on the full Hardy space. In particular, for the existence of the unitary part of
\Phi(0)
in
\mathcal{E}
, we need to check whether
\Phi(0)
is an isometry on the unitary part of
M_{\Phi}
.

Let us now discuss the plan of this paper. In section
2
, we briefly give some background details and establish a few results that will be used in later sections. Following this, in section
3
, we establish our main results involving the unitary parts of Toeplitz operators. In this section, we also discuss Butz’s characterization and related results. In section
4
, we deduce refined versions for analytic Toeplitz operators. In the last section
5
, we discuss the conditions that are sufficient for Toeplitz operators to become completely non-unitary. We end with some relevant questions which are interesting in their own right.

2.
Preliminaries

This section will establish the background details that will be used throughout this article. Let us begin by looking at vector-valued Hilbert spaces. More precisely, let
\mathcal{E}
be a Hilbert space, then the space of square-integrable
\mathcal{E}
-valued functions is denoted by
L_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{T})
, that is,

L_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{T}):=\{f\text{ is a }\mathcal{E}-\text{valued measurable function on }\mathbb{T}\text{ and }\|f\|_{2}:=\big(\int_{\mathbb{T}}{\|f(z)\|_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}}\hskip 5.69054pt\mathrm{d}\mu\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}<\infty\},

where
\mu
is the normalized Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{T}
. The space of all essentially bounded
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E})
-valued functions on
\mathbb{T}
is denoted by
L_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E})}^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})
. Given
\Phi\in L_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E})}^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})
the Laurent operator
L_{\Phi}
on
L_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{T})
is defined by

L_{\Phi}f(z)=\Phi(z)f(z)\quad(z\in\mathbb{T}).

Brown and Halmos observed in
[
6
]
that an operator
T
on
H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
is a Toeplitz operator if and only if
M_{z}^{*}TM_{z}=T
, where
M_{z}
is the shift operator on
H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
. The same characterization holds for Toeplitz operators on
H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})
, see for instance
[
17
]
. This algebraic characterization immediately gives us the following (probably well-known) results.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
T_{\Phi}
be a Toeplitz operator on
H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})
. Then
T_{\Phi}
is an isometry if and only if
\Phi(z)
is an inner function in
H_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E})}^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
.

Proof.

Since
T_{\Phi}
is an isometry, we get

\displaystyle I_{H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})}=M_{z}^{*}T_{\Phi}^{*}T_{\Phi}M_{z}=M_{z}^{*}T_{\Phi}^{*}M_{z}M_{z}^{*}T_{\Phi}M_{z}+M_{z}^{*}T_{\Phi}^{*}P_{\mathcal{E}}T_{\Phi}M_{z}

\displaystyle=T_{\Phi}^{*}T_{\Phi}+M_{z}^{*}T_{\Phi}^{*}P_{\mathcal{E}}T_{\Phi}M_{z}

\displaystyle=I_{H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})}+M_{z}^{*}T_{\Phi}^{*}P_{\mathcal{E}}T_{\Phi}M_{z},

where
P_{\mathcal{E}}
is the projection to
\mathcal{E}
viewed as constant functions in
H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})
.
Therefore,we get
M_{z}^{*}T_{\Phi}^{*}P_{\mathcal{E}}T_{\Phi}M_{z}=0
, which is equivalent to
P_{\mathcal{E}}T_{\Phi}M_{z}=0
. In other words,

0=P_{\mathcal{E}}T_{\Phi}M_{z}=\big(I_{H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})}-M_{z}M_{z}^{*}\big)T_{\Phi}M_{z}=T_{\Phi}M_{z}-M_{z}T_{\Phi}.

This implies that
\Phi(z)
must be in
H_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E})}^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
and furthermore,
T_{\Phi}
is an isometry forces
\Phi(z)
to be an inner function.
∎

Recall that
\Phi\in H_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E})}^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
is defined to be an inner function if
\Phi(e^{it})
is an isometry a.e. on
\mathbb{T}
. Applying the above result for unitary Toeplitz operators gives the following.

Corollary 2.2
.

Let
T_{\Phi}
be a Toeplitz operator on
H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})
. Then
T_{\Phi}
is a unitary if and only if
\Phi(z)
is a constant unitary on
\mathcal{E}
.

Before ending this section, let us note a result that will be used in the later sections.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
A
be a bounded operator on
\mathcal{H}
and
V\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
be an isometry. Then
V\mathcal{H}
is
A
-reducing if and only if

AVV^{*}=VV^{*}A.

Proof.

First, let us note that
AVV^{*}=VV^{*}A
implies that
V\mathcal{H}
is
A
-reducing closed subspace of
\mathcal{H}
. Now suppose that
V\mathcal{H}
is
A
-reducing then it immediately implies that

VV^{*}AV=AV,

which further implies that
VV^{*}AVV^{*}=AVV^{*}
and therefore,

VV^{*}A(I-P_{\ker V^{*}})=AVV^{*}.

Now,
V\mathcal{H}
is
A
-reducing implies that
\ker V^{*}
is
A
-reducing and therefore,
VV^{*}AP_{\ker V^{*}}=0
. Hence,

VV^{*}A(I-P_{\ker V^{*}})=VV^{*}A.

This completes the proof.
∎

3.
Unitary parts of Toeplitz operators

In this section, we aim to characterize the unitary parts of Toeplitz operators on vector-valued Hardy spaces. Let us begin by first analyzing the unitary parts of the Laurent operators
L_{\Phi}
on
L_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{T})
.

Theorem 3.1
.

A contractive Laurent operator
L_{\Phi}
on
L_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{T})
has a unitary part if and only if there exists a measurable subset
N\subseteq\mathbb{T}
of positive measure such that
\Phi(z)
is a unitary a.e. on
N
.

Proof.

Note that the unitary subspace of the contractive Laurent operator
L_{\Phi}
is

\mathcal{M}_{L_{\Phi}}=\{h\in L_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{T}):\|L_{\Phi}^{n}h\|=\|h\|=\|L_{\Phi}^{*n}h\|,\forall n\in\mathbb{N}\},

equivalently,

\mathcal{M}_{L_{\Phi}}=\{h\in L_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{T}):L_{\Phi}^{n}L_{\Phi}^{*n}h=h=L_{\Phi}^{*n}L_{\Phi}^{n}h,\forall n\in\mathbb{N}\},

Since
L_{\Phi}
commutes with the bilateral shift
B
on
L_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{T})
we get that
\mathcal{M}_{L_{\Phi}}
is a
B
-reducing closed subspace of
L_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{T})
. From
[
18
, Theorem 3.6, Page 39]
it follows that there exists a measurable set
N\subseteq\mathbb{T}
such that

\mathcal{M}_{L_{\Phi}}=\{f\in L_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{T}):f=0\text{ a.e. on N}\}=\ran L_{\chi_{N^{\complement}}},

where
\chi_{N^{c}}
is the characteristic function of
N^{c}:=\mathbb{T}\setminus N
. Thus, for any
f\in L_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{T})
we have

L_{\Phi}^{*n}L_{\Phi}^{n}L_{\chi_{N^{c}}}f=L_{\chi_{N^{c}}}f;\quad L_{\Phi}^{n}L_{\Phi}^{*n}L_{\chi_{N^{c}}}f=L_{\chi_{N^{c}}}f\quad(\forall n\in\mathbb{N}).

In other words, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\Phi(e^{it})^{n}\Phi(e^{it})^{*n}\chi_{N^{c}}(e^{it})=\chi_{N^{c}}(e^{it});\quad\Phi(e^{it})^{*n}\Phi(e^{it})^{n}\chi_{N^{c}}(e^{it})=\chi_{N^{c}}(e^{it})\quad(\text{ a.e. on }\mathbb{T}).

The converse direction is straightforward. This completes the proof.
∎

We will now prove the main result of this article.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
T_{\Phi}
be a contractive Toeplitz operator on
H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})
. Then the following are equivalent

(i)

T_{\Phi}
has a unitary part say
\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi}}
,

(ii)

there exists a Hilbert space
\mathcal{F}
and an inner function
\Theta(z)\in H_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{E})}^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
and a unitary
U:\mathcal{F}\rightarrow\mathcal{F}
such that

(3.1)

\Phi(e^{it})\Theta(e^{it})=\Theta(e^{it})U\quad\text{and}\quad\Phi(e^{it})^{*}\Theta(e^{it})=\Theta(e^{it})U^{*}\quad(\text{ a.e. on }\mathbb{T}).

In this case
T_{\Phi}|_{\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi}}}
is unitarily equivalent to a constant unitary.

Proof.

Since
T_{\Phi}=P_{H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})}L_{\Phi}|_{H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})}
, the assumption
\|T_{\Phi}\|\leq 1
, implies that
\|L_{\Phi}\|=\|T_{\Phi}\|\leq 1
. Let us now prove
(i)\implies(ii)
. From the assumption in
(i)
, the following unitary part of
T_{\Phi}
is non-zero

\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi}}=\{h\in H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D}):\|T_{\Phi}^{n}h\|=\|h\|=\|T_{\Phi}^{*n}h\|,\forall n\in\mathbb{N}\}

Now,
h\in\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi}}
implies that

\|h\|=\|T_{\Phi}h\|=\|P_{H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})}L_{\Phi}h\|\leq\|L_{\Phi}h\|\leq\|h\|,

and therefore,
L_{\Phi}h=T_{\Phi}h
. This implies that
L_{\Phi}h\in H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})
and
\|L_{\Phi}h\|=\|h\|
. In a similar manner, we can show that
L_{\Phi}^{*}h\in H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})
and
\|L_{\Phi}^{*}h\|=\|h\|
. Now, we will prove by induction that

\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi}}=\{h\in H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D}):L_{\Phi}^{n}h,L_{\Phi}^{*n}h\in H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})\text{ and }\|L_{\Phi}^{n}h\|=\|h\|=\|L_{\Phi}^{*n}h\|,\forall n\in\mathbb{N}\}

Suppose
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
1\leq n\leq k
, the following,

L_{\Phi}^{n}h,L_{\Phi}^{*n}h\in H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})\text{ and }\|L_{\Phi}^{n}h\|=\|h\|=\|L_{\Phi}^{*n}h\|,

holds for all
h\in\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi}}
. Then

\|h\|=\|T_{\Phi}^{k+1}h\|=\|(P_{H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})}L_{\Phi}|_{H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})})^{k+1}h\|\leq\|L_{\Phi}^{k+1}h\|\leq\|h\|,

shows that
\|L_{\Phi}^{k+1}h\|=\|T_{\Phi}^{k+1}h\|=\|h\|
and therefore,
L_{\Phi}^{k+1}h\in H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})
. Similarly, we can show that
\|L_{\Phi}^{*k+1}h\|=\|T_{\Phi}^{*k+1}h\|=\|h\|
and therefore,
L_{\Phi}^{*k+1}h\in H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})
. This proves our claim about
\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi}}
. Furthermore,
\|L_{\Phi}^{*}\|=\|L_{\Phi}\|=\|T_{\Phi}\|\leq 1
, implies that for any
h\in\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi}}

\|(I_{H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})}-L_{\Phi}^{n}L_{\Phi}^{*n})^{\frac{1}{2}}h\|^{2}=\langle I_{H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})}-L_{\Phi}^{n}L_{\Phi}^{*n}h,h\rangle=\|h\|^{2}-\|L_{\Phi}^{*n}h\|^{2}=0,

and similarly,

\|(I_{H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})}-L_{\Phi}^{*n}L_{\Phi}^{n})^{\frac{1}{2}}h\|^{2}=\langle I_{H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})}-L_{\Phi}^{*n}L_{\Phi}^{n}h,h\rangle=\|h\|^{2}-\|L_{\Phi}^{n}h\|^{2}=0.

Thus, we get that

(3.2)

\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi}}=\{h\in H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D}):L_{\Phi}^{n}h,L_{\Phi}^{*n}h\in H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})\text{ and }L_{\Phi}^{n}L_{\Phi}^{*n}h=h=L_{\Phi}^{*n}L_{\Phi}^{n}h,\forall n\in\mathbb{N}\}

From the above structure, we can immediately identify the following properties.

(3.3)

L_{\Phi}\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi}}=\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi}};\quad L_{\Phi}^{*}\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi}}=\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi}}.

Now, for any
h\in\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi}}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

L_{\Phi}^{n}M_{z}h=L_{\Phi}^{n}Bh=BL_{\Phi}^{n}h=M_{z}L_{\Phi}^{n}h,

holds, where
B
is the bilateral shift on
L_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{T})
, and thus
M_{z}=B|_{H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})}
. In a similar manner,
L_{\Phi}^{*n}M_{z}h=M_{z}L_{\Phi}^{*n}h
. Thus, for any
h\in\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi}}
we get,

\|L_{\Phi}^{n}M_{z}h\|=\|L_{\Phi}^{n}h\|=\|h\|=\|L_{\Phi}^{*n}h\|=\|L_{\Phi}^{*n}M_{z}h\|.

The above observations prove that
\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi}}
is a
M_{z}
-invariant closed subspace of
H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})
. Thus, we can use the Beurling-Lax-Halmos theorem to show that there exists a Hilbert space
\mathcal{F}
and an inner function
\Theta(z)\in H_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{E})}^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
such that

\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi}}=\Theta(z)H_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}(\mathbb{D}).

From this description of
\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi}}
and using condition (
3.2
) we get

(3.4)

L_{\Phi}^{*}L_{\Phi}M_{\Theta}=M_{\Theta}=L_{\Phi}L_{\Phi}^{*}M_{\Theta},~\text{on}~H_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}(\mathbb{D}).

Now,

L_{\Phi}M_{\Theta}M_{z}=L_{\Phi}M_{z}M_{\Theta}=L_{\Phi}BM_{\Theta}=BL_{\Phi}M_{\Theta}=M_{z}L_{\Phi}M_{\Theta},

where the last equality follows from the fact
L_{\Phi}M_{\Theta}H_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})\subseteq H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})
. This implies that

(3.5)

L_{\Phi}M_{\Theta}=M_{\Psi_{1}},

for some
\Psi_{1}\in H_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{E})}^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
and thus, from condition (
3.4
) we also get that,

(3.6)

M_{\Theta}=L_{\Phi}^{*}M_{\Psi_{1}},

Moreover, for any
f\in H_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})
,

\|M_{\Psi_{1}}f\|=\|L_{\Phi}M_{\Theta}f\|=\|f\|,

shows that
\Psi_{1}(z)
must be an inner function. Similarly, we can show that there exists an inner function
\Psi_{2}(z)\in H_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{E})}^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
such that

(3.7)

L_{\Phi}^{*}M_{\Theta}=M_{\Psi_{2}}.

and thus, using condition (
3.4
), we get

(3.8)

M_{\Theta}=L_{\Phi}M_{\Psi_{2}}.

Now using conditions (
3.3
) and (
3.7
), we get that

M_{\Psi_{2}}H_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})=L_{\Phi}^{*}M_{\Theta}H_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})=L_{\Phi}^{*}\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi}}=\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi}}=M_{\Theta}H_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}(\mathbb{D}).

By the uniqueness of the Beurling-Lax-Halmos theorem
[
12
, Theorem 2.1, Page 239]
, there must exist a unitary (say)
U:\mathcal{F}\rightarrow\mathcal{F}
such that

(3.9)

\Theta(z)=\Psi_{2}(z)U\quad(z\in\mathbb{D}).

Moreover, from conditions (
3.5
), (
3.8
), and (
3.9
) we have

M_{\Psi_{1}}=L_{\Phi}M_{\Theta}=L_{\Phi}M_{\Psi_{2}}U=M_{\Theta}U.

In other words,

(3.10)

\Theta(z)=\Psi_{1}(z)U^{*}\quad(z\in\mathbb{D}).

Now, from conditions (
3.8
) and (
3.9
) we get,

(3.11)

\Phi(e^{it})\Theta(e^{it})=\Phi(e^{it})\Psi_{2}(e^{it})U=\Theta(e^{it})U\quad(\text{ a.e. on }\mathbb{T}).

and furthermore, from conditions (
3.6
) and (
3.10
) we get

(3.12)

\Phi(e^{it})^{*}\Theta(e^{it})=\Phi(e^{it})^{*}\Psi_{1}(e^{it})U^{*}=\Theta(e^{it})U^{*}\quad(\text{ a.e. on }\mathbb{T}).

From the above conditions, we can observe that

M_{\Theta}^{*}T_{\Phi}M_{\Theta}=U;\quad M_{\Theta}^{*}T_{\Phi}^{*}M_{\Theta}=U^{*},

which shows that
T_{\Phi}|_{\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi}}}
is unitarily equivalent to a constant unitary.

Conversely, if there exists a Hilbert space
\mathcal{F}
, for which there exists an inner function
\Theta(z)\in H_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{E})}^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
and an unitary
U:\mathcal{F}\rightarrow\mathcal{F}
such that

\Phi(e^{it})\Theta(e^{it})=\Theta(e^{it})U\quad\text{and}\quad\Phi(e^{it})^{*}\Theta(e^{it})=\Theta(e^{it})U^{*}\quad(\text{ a.e. on }\mathbb{T}).

then it further implies that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

(3.13)

\Phi(e^{it})^{n}\Theta(e^{it})=\Theta(e^{it})U^{n};\quad\Phi(e^{it})^{*n}\Theta(e^{it})=\Theta(e^{it})U^{*n}\quad(\text{ a.e. on }\mathbb{T}).

and therefore,

(3.14)

\Phi(e^{it})^{*n}\Phi(e^{it})^{n}\Theta(e^{it})=\Theta(e^{it});\quad\Phi(e^{it})^{n}\Phi(e^{it})^{*n}\Theta(e^{it})=\Theta(e^{it})\quad(\text{ a.e. on }\mathbb{T}).

This shows that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
L_{\Phi}^{n}M_{\Theta}
and
L_{\Phi}^{*n}M_{\Theta}
both belongs to
H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})
and moreover,

L_{\Phi}^{*n}L_{\Phi}^{n}M_{\Theta}=M_{\Theta}\quad\text{and}\quad L_{\Phi}^{n}L_{\Phi}^{*n}M_{\Theta}=M_{\Theta}\quad(\forall n\in\mathbb{N}),

Thus clearly,
M_{\Theta}H_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi}}
and therefore,
\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi}}\neq\{0\}
, which implies that
T_{\Phi}
has a non-zero unitary part. This completes the proof.
∎

The above result can be used to prove the following equivalent statement of Goor’s characterization of completely non-unitary Toeplitz contractions in
[
13
]
.

Corollary 3.3
.

If
\phi\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})
,
\|\phi\|\leq 1
and
\phi
is not constant a.e. on
\mathbb{T}
, then
T_{\phi}
does not have a unitary part.

Proof.

From the assumption,
T_{\phi}
is a contractive Toeplitz operator on
H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
. Now, if there exists a unitary part of
T_{\phi}
, then by the above result there must exist an inner function
\theta(z)\in H^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
such that

\phi(e^{it})\theta(e^{it})=\mu\theta(e^{it});\quad\phi(e^{it})^{*}\theta(e^{it})=\bar{\mu}\theta(e^{it}),\quad(\text{a.e. on }\mathbb{T}),

for some uni-modular constant
\mu
. This immediately implies that
\phi(e^{it})=\bar{\mu}
a.e. on
\mathbb{T}
, which is a contradiction to our assumption that
\phi
is non-constant a.e. on
\mathbb{T}
. This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 3.1
.

Note that from Theorem
3.2
, if
T_{\Phi}
has a unitary part, then there exists a Hilbert space
\mathcal{F}
and an inner function say
\Theta(z)\in H_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{E})}^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
, such that

\Theta(e^{it})\mathcal{F}\subseteq\{\eta\in\mathcal{E}:\Phi(e^{it})^{n}\Phi^{*n}(e^{it})\eta=\eta=\Phi(e^{it})^{*n}\Phi^{n}(e^{it})\eta,\forall n\in\mathbb{N}\}\quad(\text{ a.e. on }\mathbb{T}),

However, it is unclear when the above two sets are equal a.e. on
\mathbb{T}
. For this reason, we cannot deduce that
T_{\Phi}
has a non-zero unitary part if and only if
\Phi(e^{it})
has a non-zero unitary part almost everywhere on
\mathbb{T}
.

Remark 3.2
.

The unitary part of a contraction
T
on
\mathcal{H}
is the intersection of the co-isometry and isometry parts, that is,

\mathcal{H}_{U}(T)=\mathcal{H}_{i}(T)\cap\mathcal{H}_{i}(T^{*}),

where

\mathcal{H}_{i}(T)=\{h\in\mathcal{H}:\|T^{n}h\|=\|h\|,\text{ for all $n$ }\in\mathbb{N}\}

The method of proving Theorem
3.2
shows that if
T_{\Phi}
has a isometry part, then it is also
M_{z}
-invariant and there exists a Hilbert space
\mathcal{F}
, an inner function
\Theta(z)\in H_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{E})}^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
, and a unitary
U:\mathcal{F}\rightarrow\mathcal{F}
such that

\Phi(e^{it})\Theta(e^{it})=\Theta(e^{it})U\quad(\text{ a.e. on }\mathbb{T}).

Similarly, if
T_{\Phi}
has a co-isometry part, then there exists a Hilbert space
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}
, an inner function
\Psi(z)\in H_{\mathcal{B}(\tilde{\mathcal{F}},\mathcal{E})}^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
, and a unitary
\tilde{U}^{*}:\tilde{\mathcal{F}}\rightarrow\tilde{\mathcal{F}}
such that

\Phi(e^{it})^{*}\Psi(e^{it})=\Psi(e^{it})\tilde{U}^{*}\quad(\text{ a.e. on }\mathbb{T}).

Theorem
3.2
shows that if
T_{\Phi}
has a unitary part then we can choose the same inner function and unitary in the above identities.

Let us now identify the restriction imposed on
T_{\Phi}
by the conditions (
3.1
) on the symbol
\Phi
.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
\Phi\in L_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E})}^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})
then the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

There exists a Hilbert space
\mathcal{F}
, an inner function
\Theta(z)\in H_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{E})}^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
, and a unitary
U:\mathcal{F}\rightarrow\mathcal{F}
such that

\Phi(e^{it})\Theta(e^{it})=\Theta(e^{it})U\quad\text{and}\quad\Phi(e^{it})^{*}\Theta(e^{it})=\Theta(e^{it})U^{*}\quad(\text{ a.e. on }\mathbb{T}).

(ii)

There exists a
M_{z}
-invariant closed subspace
\mathcal{S}\subseteq H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})
which is
T_{\Phi}
-reducing and
T_{\Phi}|_{\mathcal{S}}
is a unitary. In this case,
T_{\Phi}|_{\mathcal{S}}
is always unitarily equivalent to a constant unitary.

Proof.

First, let us look at the proof of
(i)\implies(ii)
. Note that,

(3.15)

\Phi(e^{it})\Theta(e^{it})=\Theta(e^{it})U\quad\text{and}\quad\Phi(e^{it})^{*}\Theta(e^{it})=\Theta(e^{it})U^{*},

a.e. on
\mathbb{T}
implies that

(3.16)

T_{\Phi}M_{\Theta}=M_{\Theta}U;\quad T_{\Phi}^{*}M_{\Theta}=M_{\Theta}U^{*}.

This implies that
M_{\Theta}H_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})
is
T_{\Phi}
-reducing. Furthermore,

T_{\Phi}^{n}T_{\Phi}^{*n}M_{\Theta}=M_{\Theta};\quad T_{\Phi}^{*n}T_{\Phi}^{n}M_{\Theta}=M_{\Theta},

shows that
T_{\Phi}|_{M_{\Theta}H_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})}
is a unitary. Now, from condition
3.16
, we get

M_{\Theta}^{*}T_{\Phi}M_{\Theta}=U;\quad M_{\Theta}^{*}T_{\Phi}^{*}M_{\Theta}=U^{*}.

This implies that

T_{\Phi}|_{M_{\Theta}H_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})}\cong U;\quad T_{\Phi^{*}}|_{M_{\Theta}H_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})}\cong U^{*}.

Thus,
T_{\Phi}|_{M_{\Theta}H_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})}
is unitarily equivalent to the constant unitary
U
.

For proving that
(ii)\implies(i)
, let us note that there exists a Hilbert space
\mathcal{F}
and an inner function
\Theta(z)\in H_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{E})}^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
such that
\mathcal{S}=M_{\Theta}H_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})
. Using Lemma
2.3
and the fact that
\mathcal{S}
is
T_{\Phi}
-reducing we get that

T_{\Phi}M_{\Theta}M_{\Theta}^{*}=M_{\Theta}M_{\Theta}^{*}T_{\Phi}.

Now note that,

T_{\Theta^{*}\Phi\Theta}=M_{\Theta}^{*}T_{\Phi}M_{\Theta},

and therefore,

\displaystyle T_{\Theta^{*}\Phi\Theta}^{*}T_{\Theta^{*}\Phi\Theta}=M_{\Theta}^{*}T_{\Phi}^{*}M_{\Theta}M_{\Theta}^{*}T_{\Phi}M_{\Theta}=M_{\Theta}^{*}T_{\Phi}^{*}T_{\Phi}M_{\Theta}=I_{H_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})}.

The last equality follows from the assumption that
T_{\Phi}|_{\mathcal{S}}
is unitary. Thus, we get that
T_{\Theta^{*}\Phi\Theta}
is an isometry, and hence, from Theorem
2.1
, we get

(3.17)

\Theta(z)^{*}\Phi(z)\Theta(z)\quad\text{ is an inner function in }H_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{E})}^{\infty}(\mathbb{D}).

Similarly, using the assumption of reducibility, we get

T_{\Phi}^{*}M_{\Theta}M_{\Theta}^{*}=M_{\Theta}M_{\Theta}^{*}T_{\Phi}^{*},

and therefore,

\displaystyle T_{\Theta^{*}\Phi^{*}\Theta}^{*}T_{\Theta^{*}\Phi^{*}\Theta}=M_{\Theta}^{*}T_{\Phi}M_{\Theta}M_{\Theta}^{*}T_{\Phi}^{*}M_{\Theta}=M_{\Theta}^{*}T_{\Phi}T_{\Phi}^{*}M_{\Theta}=I_{H_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})}.

This implies that
T_{\Theta^{*}\Phi^{*}\Theta}
is an isometry, and by using Theorem
2.1
, we get

(3.18)

\Theta(z)^{*}\Phi(z)^{*}\Theta(z)\quad\text{ is an inner function in }H_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{E})}^{\infty}(\mathbb{D}).

Thus, from conditions (
3.17
) and (
3.18
) we can deduce that

\Theta(z)^{*}\Phi(z)\Theta(z)\quad\text{ is a constant unitary (say) }\quad U:\mathcal{F}\rightarrow\mathcal{F},

which imply that

M_{\Theta}^{*}T_{\Phi}^{*}M_{\Theta}=U^{*};\quad M_{\Theta}^{*}T_{\Phi}M_{\Theta}=U.

Applying by
M_{\Theta}
on the left side of both identities we get

T_{\Phi}^{*}M_{\Theta}=M_{\Theta}U^{*};\quad T_{\Phi}M_{\Theta}=M_{\Theta}U,

and therefore,

\Phi(e^{it})\Theta(e^{it})=\Theta(e^{it})U\quad\text{and}\quad\Phi(e^{it})^{*}\Theta(e^{it})=\Theta(e^{it})U^{*},

almost everywhere on
\mathbb{T}
. This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

In a sense, conditions (
3.1
) are associated with an operator equation related to Toeplitz operators. More precisely, if
T_{\Phi}
is a contractive Toeplitz operator and
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{T}_{\Phi}}=M_{\Theta}H_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})
is the unitary part, then the following equation for

\Phi\Theta=\Theta X;\quad\Phi^{*}\Theta=\Theta Y,

always have a unique solution, i.e.
X=U
, and
Y=U^{*}
.

3.1.
A note on Butz’s characterization

In this section, we show that Butz’s result
[
7
]
, for Toeplitz operators on vector-valued Hardy spaces holds only for some special cases. Note that, the author’s result when applied to
T_{\Phi}
on
H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})
gives the following.

Theorem 3.5

(Butz)
.

Let
T_{\Phi}
be a Toeplitz operator on
H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})
. Then there exists an orthogonal decomposition
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}_{0}\oplus\mathcal{E}_{1}
such that

\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi}}=H_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}^{2}(\mathbb{D});\quad\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi}}^{\perp}=H_{\mathcal{E}_{1}}^{2}(\mathbb{D}),

and
x=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}M_{z}^{n}f_{n}\in\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi}}
if and only if
f_{n}\in\mathcal{E}_{0}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
. Furthermore, if
\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi}}\neq\{0\}
(i.e.
\mathcal{E}_{0}\neq\{0\}
), then there exists a unitary operator
R_{0}:\mathcal{E}_{0}\rightarrow\mathcal{E}_{0}
such that for
x=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}M_{z}^{n}f_{n}\in\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi}}
, we have
T_{\Phi}x=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}M_{z}^{n}R_{0}f_{n}
.

The following example shows that the above result is not always true.

Example
.

Consider
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}_{0}\oplus\mathcal{E}_{1}
a non-trivial orthogonal decomposition. Let
P=P_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E})
and thus,
P^{\perp}=P_{\mathcal{E}_{1}}
. Now let us consider the symbol
\Phi(z):=zP+P^{\perp}
. Note that

I_{\mathcal{E}}-\Phi(z)^{*}\Phi(z)=(1-|z|^{2})P=I_{\mathcal{E}}-\Phi(z)\Phi(z)^{*},

shows that
\Phi(z)
is unitary-valued on
\mathbb{T}
and therefore, the corresponding Toeplitz operator
M_{\Phi}
is an isometry. Since,
\Phi(0)=P^{\perp}
is not a pure contraction on
\mathcal{E}
, we know from
[
19
, Theorem 3.4]
that
M_{\Phi}
is not completely non-unitary. In other words, the subspace
\mathcal{M}_{M_{\Phi}}\neq\{0\}
. Now suppose, by Butz’s characterization we have
\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi}}=H_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})
for some
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{E}
. From the structure of
\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi}}
, for any
h\in\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi}}
, we have

h\in\cap_{n=1}^{\infty}\ker(I_{H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})}-M_{\Phi}^{n}M_{\Phi}^{*n}).

Now note that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

I-M_{\Phi}^{n}M_{\Phi}^{*n}=I-(M_{z}^{n}P+P^{\perp})(M_{z}^{*n}P+P^{\perp})=I-M_{z}^{n}M_{z}^{*n}P-P^{\perp}=(I-M_{z}^{n}M_{z}^{*n})P

Therefore,

h\in\cap_{n=1}^{\infty}\ker(I_{H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})}-M_{\Phi}^{n}M_{\Phi}^{*n})\Longleftrightarrow h\in\cap_{n=1}^{\infty}\ker(I_{H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})}-M_{z}^{n}M_{z}^{*n})P\subseteq\mathcal{E}.

This immediately contradicts
\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi}}=H_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})
for some closed subspace
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{E}
.

Now let us identify the situation when the decomposition considered by Butz holds.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
T_{\Phi}
be a contractive Toeplitz operator on
H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})
with a unitary part
\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi}}
. Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi}}=H_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})
for some closed subspace
\mathcal{E}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{E}
,

(ii)

there exists a closed subspace
\mathcal{E}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{E}
such that
\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi}}\subseteq H_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})
, and
T_{\Phi}|_{\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi}}}=T_{W}|_{H_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})}
, for some unitary
W:\mathcal{E}_{0}\rightarrow\mathcal{E}_{0}
,

(iii)

there exists a closed subspace
\mathcal{E}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{E}
such that
\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi}}\subseteq H_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})
, and
\mathcal{E}_{0}
is
\Phi(e^{it})
-reducing and
\Phi(e^{it})|_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}
is a constant unitary a.e. on
\mathbb{T}
.

Proof.

Let us look in the direction
(i)\implies(ii)
. In Theorem
3.2
, we have shown that
\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi}}
is a
M_{z}
-invariant closed subspace of
H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})
, and thus, by the Beurling-Lax-Halmos’s theorem there exists a Hilbert space
\mathcal{F}
and an inner function
\Theta(z)\in H_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{E})}^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
such that

\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi}}=M_{\Theta}H_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}(\mathbb{D}).

Now, if there exists a closed subspace
\mathcal{E}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{E}
such that
\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi}}=H_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})
, then

M_{\Theta}H_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})=H_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})=i_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}H_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}^{2}(\mathbb{D}),

where
i_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}:\mathcal{E}_{0}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{E}
is the inclusion map. Now, from the uniqueness of the Beurling-Lax-Halmos theorem there exists a unitary
A:\mathcal{F}\rightarrow\mathcal{E}_{0}
such that

\Theta(z)=i_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}A.

From condition (
3.1
) we know that
\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi}}\neq\{0\}
, if and only if there exists a unitary
U:\mathcal{F}\rightarrow\mathcal{F}
such that

\Phi(e^{it})\Theta(e^{it})=\Theta(e^{it})U,\quad(\text{ a.e. on }\mathbb{T}).

and therefore, we get that
\Phi(e^{it})|_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}=i_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}AUA^{*}i_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}^{*}:\mathcal{E}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{E}\rightarrow\mathcal{E}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{E}
, a.e. on
\mathbb{T}
. Similarly, the other identity

\Phi(e^{it})^{*}\Theta(e^{it})=\Theta(e^{it})U^{*},\quad(\text{ a.e. on }\mathbb{T}).

gives
\Phi(e^{it})^{*}|_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}=i_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}AU^{*}A^{*}i_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}^{*}
, a.e. on
\mathbb{T}
. This shows that
\Phi(e^{it})|_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}
is a constant unitary a.e. on
\mathbb{T}
and moreover, the above identities imply that for any
\eta\in\mathcal{E}_{0}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
we get

T_{\Phi}z^{k}\eta=L_{\Phi}z^{k}\eta=L_{\Phi}M_{z}^{k}\eta=M_{z}^{k}L_{\Phi}\eta=M_{z}^{k}L_{\Phi|_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}}\eta=z^{k}i_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}AUA^{*}i_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}^{*}\eta,

which further implies that

z^{k}\eta\in\ker(T_{\Phi}|_{H_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})}-T_{W}|_{H_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})}),

where
W=i_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}AUA^{*}i_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}^{*}
. Since
H_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})=\overline{\mbox{span }}\{z^{k}\eta:k\in\mathbb{N},\eta\in\mathcal{E}_{0}\}
, we get

T_{\Phi}|_{H_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})}=T_{W}|_{H_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})}.

Similarly, we will get

T_{\Phi}^{*}|_{H_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})}=T_{W}^{*}|_{H_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})}.

In other words, we prove that
T_{\Phi}|_{\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi}}}
is a constant unitary
T_{W}
on
\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi}}=H_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})
.
For proving the direction
(ii)\implies(iii)
, let us first note that
T_{\Phi}|_{\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi}}}=T_{W}|_{H_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})}
, implies that

H_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})=T_{W}|_{H_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})}H_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})=T_{\Phi}|_{\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi}}}H_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})=T_{\Phi}\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi}}=\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi}}.

The last but one equality follows since
\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi}}\subseteq H_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})
. Thus, we have

T_{\Phi}|_{H_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})}=T_{W}|_{H_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})},

and therefore,

T_{{(\Phi|_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}}-W)}|_{H_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})}=0.

Since,
H_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})
is both
T_{\Phi}
and
T_{W}
reducing subspace, we can think
T_{(\Phi|_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}-W)}
as a Toeplitz operator on
H_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})
. This implies that

\Phi(e^{it})|_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}=W\quad(\text{ a.e. on }\mathbb{T}).

Similarly, we will get

\Phi(e^{it})^{*}|_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}=W^{*}\quad(\text{ a.e. on }\mathbb{T}).

For the direction,
(iii)\implies(i)
, let
\Phi(e^{it})|_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}=W
(a constant unitary) a.e. on
\mathbb{T}
. Thus for any
\eta\in\mathcal{E}_{0}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

T_{\Phi}\eta=P_{H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})}L_{\Phi}\eta=P_{H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})}L_{\Phi|_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}}\eta=P_{H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})}W\eta=W\eta,

and

T_{\Phi}^{*}\eta=P_{H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})}L_{\Phi}^{*}\eta=P_{H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})}L_{\Phi|_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}}^{*}\eta=P_{H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})}W^{*}\eta=W^{*}\eta,

shows that

\mathcal{E}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi}}.

Since
\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi}}
is a
M_{z}
-invariant closed subspace of
H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})
, we have
H_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi}}
. From our assumption we already have
\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi}}\subseteq H_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})
, which implies that
\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi}}=H_{\mathcal{E}_{0}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})
. This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

We point out that the gap in the proof of the main theorem in
[
7
]
lies in Line 17 of page 97 in the following sentence

``\ldots\text{ Also for }S^{*}x=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}S^{n}c_{n+1}".

Here, the author had considered
x=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}S^{n}c_{n}
, where
c_{n}\in C_{0}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. The space
C_{0}:=\mathcal{M}\ominus S\mathcal{M}
, where
\mathcal{M}
is the unitary part of the generalized Toeplitz operator
T
on
\mathcal{H}
. For the above expression (within quotes) to hold one should have
c_{0}\in C\cap\mathcal{M}
. However, the author did not prove this fact. The above expression is then used to prove that
\mathcal{M}
is
M_{z}
-reducing, which is again used to prove that
C_{0}=C\cap\mathcal{M}
in Line 21. In a certain sense, the author’s argument is circular and incorrect, as can be seen in the example and main result of this section.

4.
Analytic Toeplitz operators

We shall now focus on analytic Toeplitz operators on
H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})
. Let us briefly recall that any
\Phi(z)\in H_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E})}^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
is a Schur function that is,
\|\Phi\|_{\infty}\leq 1
, if and only if there exists a transfer-function realization for
\Phi(z)
(for instance, see
[
2
, Theorem 1.1]
). More precisely, there exists an auxiliary Hilbert space
\mathcal{K}
and a unitary

W:=\begin{bmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{bmatrix}:\mathcal{E}\oplus\mathcal{K}\rightarrow\mathcal{E}\oplus\mathcal{K}

such that

(4.1)

\Phi(z):=\tau_{W}(z)=A+zB(I_{\mathcal{E}}-zD)^{-1}C.

Since
W
is unitary we get the following properties:

(a)

A^{*}A+C^{*}C=I_{\mathcal{E}}=B^{*}B+D^{*}D
,

(b)

A^{*}B+C^{*}D=0=AC^{*}+BD^{*}
.

(c)

DD^{*}+CC^{*}=I_{\mathcal{E}}=AA^{*}+BB^{*}
,

Using this we get the following identities for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}
,

(4.2)

I_{\mathcal{E}}-\Phi(\lambda)\Phi(\lambda)^{*}=(1-|\lambda|^{2})B(I-\lambda D)^{-1}(I-\bar{\lambda}D^{*})^{-1}B^{*},

and

(4.3)

I_{\mathcal{E}}-\Phi(\lambda)^{*}\Phi(\lambda)=(1-|\lambda|^{2})C^{*}(I-\bar{\lambda}D^{*})^{-1}(I-\lambda D)^{-1}C.

The above identities also show that if we begin with a transfer function
\tau_{W}
, then it is contractive for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}
. The transfer-function realization is a fundamental tool used for understanding the properties of analytic Toeplitz operators. Using the identities (
4.2
) and (
4.3
), the following observations equivalent to
[
11
, Theorem 3.1]
can be made.

Proposition 4.1

(Das and Sau)
.

Let
\Phi(z)\in H_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E})}^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
such that
\|\Phi\|_{\infty}\leq 1
and
\Phi(0)
is not a unitary, then the following statements

(i)

\Phi(0)\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E})
, has a unitary part,

(ii)

\Phi(\lambda)\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E})
, has a unitary part for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}
,

(iii)

M_{\Phi}\in\mathcal{B}(H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D}))
, has a unitary part.

have the following implications:
(i)\Longleftrightarrow(ii)
, and
(i)\implies(iii)
.

Let us note down a result that can deduced from the above characterization. We believe this observation is new and independently interesting.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
\Phi(z)\in H_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E})}^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
such that
\|\Phi\|_{\infty}\leq 1
. Furthermore, assume that
\Phi(0)
is an isometry on
\mathcal{E}
. Then either
M_{\Phi}
has a unitary part or
M_{\Phi}
is a
C_{\cdot 0}
-contraction, that is,
\{M_{\Phi}^{*n}\}_{n}\rightarrow 0
in the strong operator topology.

Proof.

Since
\Phi(0)
is a isometry on
\mathcal{E}
by the Wold-von Neumann decomposition
\Phi(0)
has a unitary part or
\Phi(0)
is a
C_{\cdot 0}
-isometry (also known as a
shift
operator). From the above Proposition
4.1
, if
\Phi(0)
has a unitary part then
M_{\Phi}
has a unitary part. The proof gets complete by using
[
19
, Theorem 3.4]
which shows that
M_{\Phi}
on
H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})
is a
C_{\cdot 0}
-contraction if and only
\Phi(0)
is a
C_{\cdot 0}
-contraction.
∎

From Proposition
4.1
, it is clear that the missing piece and the most important direction is whether
(iii)\implies(i)
. There is an example in
[
11
, Remark 3.2]
, where the authors have shown this does not hold in general. It is evident from this discussion that we need to first analyze when
\Phi(0)\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E})
has a unitary part. This is the content of the following result.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
\Phi(z)\in H_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E})}^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
such that
\|\Phi\|_{\infty}\leq 1
, then the following are equivalent

(i)

the operator
\Phi(0)
on
\mathcal{E}
has a unitary part,

(ii)

the constant Toeplitz operator
T_{\Phi(0)}
on
H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})
has a unitary part,

(iii)

there exists a Hilbert space
\mathcal{F}
, an inner function
\Theta(z)\in H_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{E})}^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
, and a unitary
U:\mathcal{F}\rightarrow\mathcal{F}
such that

(4.4)

\Phi(0)\Theta(\lambda)=\Theta(\lambda)U;\quad\Phi(0)^{*}\Theta(\lambda)=\Theta(\lambda)U^{*}\quad(\forall\lambda\in\mathbb{D}).

(iv)

there exists a Hilbert space
\mathcal{F}
, an inner function
\Theta(z)\in H_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{E})}^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
, and a unitary
U:\mathcal{F}\rightarrow\mathcal{F}
such that

(4.5)

\Phi(\lambda)\Theta(\lambda)=\Theta(\lambda)U;\quad\Phi(\lambda)^{*}\Theta(\lambda)=\Theta(\lambda)U^{*}\quad(\forall\lambda\in\mathbb{D}).

Proof.

Our approach is to show the following directions:
(i)\implies(ii)\implies(iii)\implies(i)
and
(iii)\Longleftrightarrow(iv)
. It is easy to see that when
\Phi(0)
has a unitary part in
\mathcal{E}
, then the operator
T_{\Phi(0)}
on
H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})
has a unitary part. In particular,
\mathcal{M}_{\Phi(0)}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi(0)}}
. This implies the direction
(i)\implies(ii)
. Now let us look at
(ii)\implies(iii)
. Let
\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi(0)}}
denote the unitary part of the operator
T_{\Phi(0)}
on
H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})
. Note that

\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi(0)}}=\{f\in H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D}):\|\Phi(0)^{n}f\|=\|f\|=\|\Phi(0)^{*n}f\|,\forall n\in\mathbb{N}\}

It is clear from the above description that
\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi(0)}}
is a
M_{z}
-invariant closed subspace of
H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})
. From assumption,
\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi(0)}}\neq\{0\}
, and therefore, by the Beurling-Lax-Halmos theorem, there exists a Hilbert space
\mathcal{F}
and an inner function
\Theta(z)\in H_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{E})}^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
such that

\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi(0)}}=M_{\Theta}H_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}(\mathbb{D}).

Since
\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi(0)}}
is
T_{\Phi(0)}
-reducing, we get, from Lemma
2.3
,

T_{\Phi(0)}M_{\Theta}M_{\Theta}^{*}=M_{\Theta}M_{\Theta}^{*}T_{\Phi(0)}.

which implies that

T_{\Phi(0)}M_{\Theta}=M_{\Theta}M_{\Theta}^{*}T_{\Phi(0)}M_{\Theta}.

Since
T_{\Phi(0)}
is unitary on
M_{\Theta}H_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})
, the operator
M_{\Theta}^{*}T_{\Phi(0)}M_{\Theta}
is an isometry. In the same manner,

T_{\Phi(0)}^{*}M_{\Theta}=M_{\Theta}M_{\Theta}^{*}T_{\Phi(0)}^{*}M_{\Theta}.

will imply that
M_{\Theta}^{*}T_{\Phi(0)}^{*}M_{\Theta}
is an isometry. From Corollary
2.2
, there must exist an unitary (say)
U:\mathcal{F}\rightarrow\mathcal{F}
such that

M_{\Theta}^{*}T_{\Phi(0)}M_{\Theta}=T_{U}.

and thus,

M_{\Theta}M_{\Theta}^{*}T_{\Phi(0)}M_{\Theta}=M_{\Theta}T_{U}.

Since
M_{\Theta}H_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})
is
T_{\Phi(0)}
-reducing, we get

T_{\Phi(0)}M_{\Theta}=M_{\Theta}T_{U},

which further implies that

\Phi(0)\Theta(\lambda)=\Theta(\lambda)U\quad(\lambda\in\mathbb{D}).

Similarly, from

M_{\Theta}^{*}T_{\Phi(0)}^{*}M_{\Theta}=T_{U}^{*}.

we have

\Phi(0)^{*}\Theta(\lambda)=\Theta(\lambda)U^{*}\quad(\lambda\in\mathbb{D}).

This completes the proof for the direction
(ii)\implies(iii)
. For the direction
(iii)\implies(i)
, this is straightforward, since
\Theta(z)\not\equiv 0
, there exists
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}
for which
\Theta(\lambda)\neq 0
. For this
\lambda
, the condition

\Phi(0)\Theta(\lambda)=\Theta(\lambda)U;\quad\Phi(0)^{*}\Theta(\lambda)=\Theta(\lambda)U^{*}.

implies that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\Phi(0)^{n}\Phi(0)^{*n}\Theta(\lambda)=\Theta(\lambda);\quad\Phi(0)^{*n}\Phi(0)^{n}\Theta(\lambda)=\Theta(\lambda).

This implies that
\Phi(0)
has a unitary part containing the range of
\Theta(\lambda)
. Now let us prove
(iii)\implies(iv)
. Note that conditions (
4.4
) imply that

M_{\Theta}^{*}T_{\Phi(0)}^{*}T_{\Phi(0)}M_{\Theta}=M_{\Theta}^{*}M_{\Theta}=I_{H_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})}.

Similarly, we get

M_{\Theta}^{*}T_{\Phi(0)}T_{\Phi(0)}^{*}M_{\Theta}=M_{\Theta}^{*}M_{\Theta}=I_{H_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})}.

Now, note that

M_{\Theta}^{*}T_{\Phi(0)}^{*}T_{\Phi(0)}M_{\Theta}=M_{\Theta}^{*}T_{A}^{*}T_{A}M_{\Theta}=M_{\Theta}^{*}M_{\Theta}-M_{\Theta}^{*}T_{C}^{*}T_{C}M_{\Theta},

The last equality follows from property
(a)
corresponding to the unitary
W
. Thus, we get

M_{\Theta}^{*}T_{C}^{*}T_{C}M_{\Theta}=0,

which further implies that
T_{C}M_{\Theta}=0
, and therefore,
C\Theta(\lambda)=0
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}
. Using the transfer-function formula (
4.1
) we get

\Phi(\lambda)\Theta(\lambda)=(A+\lambda B(I_{\mathcal{E}}-\lambda D)^{-1}C)\Theta(\lambda)=A\Theta(\lambda)=\Phi(0)\Theta(\lambda)=\Theta(\lambda)U.

Similarly, note from condition
(c)
that

M_{\Theta}^{*}T_{\Phi(0)}T_{\Phi(0)}^{*}M_{\Theta}=M_{\Theta}^{*}T_{A}T_{A}^{*}M_{\Theta}=M_{\Theta}^{*}M_{\Theta}-M_{\Theta}^{*}T_{B}T_{B}^{*}M_{\Theta},

Thus, we get

M_{\Theta}^{*}T_{B}T_{B}^{*}M_{\Theta}=0,

which further implies that
T_{B}^{*}M_{\Theta}=0
, and therefore,
B^{*}\Theta(\lambda)=0
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}
. This implies that

\Phi(\lambda)^{*}\Theta(\lambda)=(A^{*}+\bar{\lambda}C^{*}(I_{\mathcal{E}}-\bar{\lambda}D^{*})^{-1}B^{*})\Theta(\lambda)=A^{*}\Theta(\lambda)=\Phi(0)^{*}\Theta(\lambda)=\Theta(\lambda)U^{*}.

This completes the proof of
(iii)\implies(iv)
. Now, if

\Phi(\lambda)\Theta(\lambda)=\Theta(\lambda)U;\quad\Phi(\lambda)^{*}\Theta(\lambda)=\Theta(\lambda)U^{*}

is satisfied for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}
, then

(I_{\mathcal{E}}-\Phi(\lambda)\Phi(\lambda)^{*})\Theta(\lambda)=\Theta(\lambda)-\Phi(\lambda)\Phi(\lambda)^{*}\Theta(\lambda)=\Theta(\lambda)-\Theta(\lambda)UU^{*}=0.

and therefore,

0=\Theta(\lambda)^{*}(I_{\mathcal{E}}-\Phi(\lambda)\Phi(\lambda)^{*})\Theta(\lambda)=(1-|\lambda|^{2})\Theta(\lambda)^{*}B(I-\lambda D)^{-1}(I-\bar{\lambda}D^{*})^{-1}B^{*}\Theta(\lambda).

This implies that
B^{*}\Theta(\lambda)=0
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}
. Similarly, the above conditions imply that

(I_{\mathcal{E}}-\Phi(\lambda^{*})\Phi(\lambda))\Theta(\lambda)=\Theta(\lambda)-\Phi(\lambda)^{*}\Phi(\lambda)\Theta(\lambda)=\Theta(\lambda)-\Theta(\lambda)U^{*}U^{*}=0,

and therefore,

0=\Theta(\lambda)^{*}(I_{\mathcal{E}}-\Phi(\lambda)^{*}\Phi(\lambda)^{*})\Theta(\lambda)=(1-|\lambda|^{2})\Theta(\lambda)^{*}C^{*}(I-\bar{\lambda}D^{*})^{-1}(I-\lambda D)^{-1}C\Theta(\lambda).

This implies that
C\Theta(\lambda)=0
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}
. Thus, we get

\Phi(0)\Theta(\lambda)=\Phi(\lambda)\Theta(\lambda)=\Theta(\lambda)U

and

\Phi(0)^{*}\Theta(\lambda)=\Phi(\lambda)^{*}\Theta(\lambda)=\Theta(\lambda)U^{*}.

This completes the proof that
(iv)\implies(iii)
, and also the proof of this proposition.
∎

Now we are ready to establish the conditions that force
\Phi(0)
to have a unitary part.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
\Phi\in H_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E})}^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
with
\|\Phi\|_{\infty}\leq 1
. If
M_{\Phi}
has a unitary part
\mathcal{M}_{M_{\Phi}}
, then the condition
\|T_{\Phi(0)}f\|=\|f\|
for all
f\in\mathcal{M}_{M_{\Phi}}
implies that
\Phi(0)
has a unitary part.

Proof.

Since
M_{\Phi}
has a unitary part, from Theorem
3.2
, we know that there must exist a Hilbert space
\mathcal{F}
and a unitary
U:\mathcal{F}\rightarrow\mathcal{F}
such that

\Phi(e^{it})\Theta(e^{it})=\Theta(e^{it})U;\quad\Phi(e^{it})^{*}\Theta(e^{it})=\Theta(e^{it})U^{*}\quad(\text{ a.e. on }\mathbb{T}).

Now
\Phi(z)
is a bounded analytic function further implies that

(4.6)

\Phi(\lambda)\Theta(\lambda)=\Theta(\lambda)U\quad(\forall\lambda\in\mathbb{D}).

From Proposition
4.3
, it is clear that we have to find conditions that gives

(4.7)

\Phi(\lambda)^{*}\Theta(\lambda)=\Theta(\lambda)U^{*}\quad(\forall\lambda\in\mathbb{D}).

We claim that condition (
4.6
) will imply condition (
4.7
) if
\|T_{\Phi(0)}f\|=\|f\|
for all
f\in\mathcal{M}_{M_{\Phi}}
. To prove this claim, let us first fix a
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}
and note that from condition (
4.6
), we get

\Phi(\lambda)^{*}\Phi(\lambda)\Theta(\lambda)U^{*}=\Phi(\lambda)^{*}\Theta(\lambda)

and thus,

-\big(I-\Phi(\lambda)^{*}\Phi(\lambda)\big)\Theta(\lambda)U^{*}=\Phi(\lambda)^{*}\Theta(\lambda)-\Theta(\lambda)U^{*}.

This is equivalent to

-(1-|\lambda|^{2})C^{*}(I-\bar{\lambda}D^{*})^{-1}(I-\lambda D)^{-1}C\Theta(\lambda)U^{*}=\Phi(\lambda)^{*}\Theta(\lambda)-\Theta(\lambda)U^{*}.

Hence,
\Phi(\lambda)^{*}\Theta(\lambda)=\Theta(\lambda)U^{*}
if and only if

C^{*}(I-\bar{\lambda}D^{*})^{-1}(I-\lambda D)^{-1}C\Theta(\lambda)=0.

This is again equivalent to

\Theta(\lambda)^{*}C^{*}(I-\bar{\lambda}D^{*})^{-1}(I-\lambda D)^{-1}C\Theta(\lambda)=0,

and therefore, equivalent to the condition

(I-\lambda D)^{-1}C\Theta(\lambda)=0.

Now acting on the left by
(I-\lambda D)
, we get,
C\Theta(\lambda)=0
. Thus,

\Phi(\lambda)^{*}\Theta(\lambda)=\Theta(\lambda)U^{*}\text{ if and only if }C\Theta(\lambda)=0,

Using the fact that
W
is a unitary, we know
A^{*}A+C^{*}C=I_{\mathcal{E}}
. Thus,
C\Theta(\lambda)=0
is equivalent to

A^{*}A\Theta(\lambda)=\Theta(\lambda)\quad(\lambda\in\mathbb{D}),

Since
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}
was arbitrary, we observe that condition (
4.7
) holds if and only if
A^{*}A\Theta(\lambda)=\Theta(\lambda)
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}
. The later condition is equivalent to
T_{A}^{*}T_{A}M_{\Theta}=M_{\Theta}
, which is again equivalent to

M_{\Theta}^{*}T_{A}^{*}T_{A}M_{\Theta}=I_{H_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})}

or in other words,

\|T_{\Phi(0)}f\|=\|T_{A}f\|=\|f\|.

for all
f\in\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi}}
. This is because,
M_{\Theta}^{*}T_{A}^{*}T_{A}M_{\Theta}=I_{H_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})}
will imply that
M_{\Theta}^{*}T_{C}^{*}T_{C}M_{\Theta}=0
, that is,
T_{C}M_{\Theta}=0
, which is again equivalent to
T_{A}^{*}T_{A}M_{\Theta}=M_{\Theta}
. This completes the proof.
∎

It is evident from the above result that we need to consider constant operator
T_{\Phi(0)}
on the full Hardy space
H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})
for finding the correspondence between the unitary part of
\Phi(0)
and the unitary part of
T_{\Phi}
on
H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})
. We will now focus on a special class of Schur functions. In particular, we consider transfer functions corresponding to the following unitary,

W:=\begin{bmatrix}A&B\\
C&0\end{bmatrix}:\mathcal{E}\oplus\mathcal{K}\rightarrow\mathcal{E}\oplus\mathcal{K},

that is,
\Phi(z):=\tau_{W}(z)=A+zBC
. Here, we have the following properties.

(a)

A^{*}A+C^{*}C=I_{\mathcal{E}}=AA^{*}+BB^{*}
,

(b)

A^{*}B=0=AC^{*}
,

(c)

B
and
C^{*}
are isometries, and
A
is a partial isometry on
\mathcal{E}
.

Although the above class of functions seems restrictive, it serves an important role in finding analytic models for abstract operators on Hilbert spaces. For instance, Berger, Coburn, and Lebow in
[
5
]
proved that tuple of commuting isometries whose product is a shift is unitarily equivalent to tuple of Toeplitz operators on certain
H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})
, corresponding to symbols of the form
U(zP+P^{\perp})
, where
P
is an orthogonal projection and
U
is a unitary on
\mathcal{E}
. We have the following result for Toeplitz operators corresponding to these symbols.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
\Phi(z)\in H_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E})}^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
such that
\Phi(z):=\tau_{W}(z)=A+zBC
, such that
M_{\Phi}
has a unitary part, then any one of the following conditions

(i)

\|T_{\Phi(0)}f\|=\|f\|
for all
f\in\mathcal{M}_{M_{\Phi}}
,

(ii)

\|T_{\Phi(0)}^{*}f\|=\|f\|
for all
f\in\mathcal{M}_{M_{\Phi}}
, and
\ker\Phi(0)\perp\mathcal{M}_{M_{\Phi}}
,

will imply that
\Phi(0)
has a unitary part.

Proof.

If
M_{\Phi}
has a unitary part, then from Theorem
3.2
, there exist a Hilbert space
\mathcal{F}
and an inner function
\Theta(z)\in H_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{E})}^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
such that

(4.8)

(A+\lambda BC)\Theta(\lambda)=\Phi(\lambda)\Theta(\lambda)=\Theta(\lambda)U\quad(\forall\lambda\in\mathbb{D}),

where
\mathcal{M}_{T_{\Phi}}=M_{\Theta}H_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})
. Let us fix a
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}
. Now, acting on the left of both sides by
B^{*}
and using condition
(b)
, we get

\lambda B^{*}BC\Theta(\lambda)=B^{*}\Theta(\lambda)U

and thus using condition
(c)
it implies that

(4.9)

\lambda C\Theta(\lambda)=B^{*}\Theta(\lambda)U,

Since
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}
was arbitrary, the above identity for
\lambda\neq 0
gives

(4.10)

C\Theta(\lambda)=0\text{ if and only if }B^{*}\Theta(\lambda)=0.

For
\lambda=0
, we get
B^{*}\Theta(0)=0
, and therefore,
AA^{*}\Theta(0)=\Theta(0)
. Now, let us again go back to condition (
4.8
). Acting on the left by
A^{*}
and using condition
(b)
, we get

(4.11)

A^{*}A\Theta(\lambda)=A^{*}\Theta(\lambda)U.

Since
A
is a partial isometry, acting on the left of both sides by
A
, we get

(4.12)

AA^{*}\Theta(\lambda)=A\Theta(\lambda)U^{*}.

From Proposition
4.3
, we know that
A=\Phi(0)
has a unitary part if and only if

(4.13)

A\Theta(\lambda)=\Theta(\lambda)U;\quad A^{*}\Theta(\lambda)=\Theta(\lambda)U^{*}\quad(\forall\lambda\in\mathbb{D})

Since
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}
was arbitrary, it follows from the observations (
4.11
) and (
4.12
) that the above conditions are true if and only if

A^{*}A\Theta(\lambda)=\Theta(\lambda);\quad AA^{*}\Theta(\lambda)=\Theta(\lambda)\quad(\forall\lambda\in\mathbb{D}).

In turn, these conditions are equivalent to
T_{A}T_{A}^{*}M_{\Theta}=M_{\Theta}
and
T_{A}^{*}T_{A}M_{\Theta}=M_{\Theta}
. Using properties of the unitary
W
, we see that these conditions are again equivalent to
T_{B}^{*}M_{\Theta}=0
and
T_{C}M_{\Theta}=0
, respectively. From condition (
4.9
), it follows that
B^{*}\Theta(\lambda)=0
if and only if
C\Theta(\lambda)=0
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}\setminus\{0\}
. Since
B^{*}\Theta(0)=0
, we can deduce that
T_{C}M_{\Theta}=0
will imply
T_{B}^{*}M_{\Theta}=0
but not vice versa. This is because,
B^{*}\Theta(0)=0
may not imply
C\Theta(0)=0
. So we need to find an extra condition along with
T_{B}^{*}M_{\Theta}=0
that forces
C\Theta(0)=0
. We claim that this condition is equivalent to
\ker\Phi(0)\perp\mathcal{M}_{M_{\Phi}}
. Since
A=\Phi(0)
is a partial isometry this is again equivalent to

(I_{\mathcal{E}}-\Phi(0)^{*}\Phi(0))\mathcal{E}\perp\mathcal{M}_{M_{\Phi}}.

Now, note that for any
\eta\in\mathcal{E}
and
f\in H_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})
,

\langle(I_{\mathcal{E}}-\Phi(0)^{*}\Phi(0))\eta,M_{\Theta}f\rangle=\langle(I_{\mathcal{E}}-A^{*}A)\eta,M_{\Theta}f\rangle=\langle M_{\Theta}^{*}C^{*}C\eta,f\rangle=\langle\Theta(0)^{*}C^{*}C\eta,f(0)\rangle.

Thus,
\ker\Phi(0)\perp\mathcal{M}_{M_{\Phi}}
is equivalent to
\Theta(0)^{*}C^{*}C=0
. Now,
C^{*}
is an isometry further implies that
\Theta(0)^{*}C^{*}C=0
if and only if
\Theta(0)^{*}C^{*}=0
, that is,
C\Theta(0)=0
. This completes the proof of the claim. So, we have proved that any one of the following conditions

T_{C}M_{\Theta}=0,

and

T_{B}^{*}M_{\Theta}=0\text{ and }\ker\Phi(0)\perp\mathcal{M}_{M_{\Phi}},

will imply the existence of the unitary part of
\Phi(0)
. Note that using the properties of unitary
W
, the above conditions are again equivalent to

M_{\Theta}^{*}T_{A}^{*}T_{A}M_{\Theta}=I_{H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})},

and

M_{\Theta}^{*}T_{A}T_{A}^{*}M_{\Theta}=I_{H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})}\text{ and }\ker\Phi(0)\perp\mathcal{M}_{M_{\Phi}},

respectively. In turn, these are again equivalent to

\|T_{\Phi(0)}f\|=\|f\|,

and

\|T_{\Phi(0)}^{*}f\|=\|f\|\text{ and }\ker\Phi(0)\perp\mathcal{M}_{M_{\Phi}},

respectively. This completes the proof.
∎

5.
Concluding remarks and questions

An important aspect of any c.n.u. contraction is that it admits a
H^{\infty}
-calculus (see
[
16
, Chapter 3]
). We can deduce the following result from Theorem
3.2
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
T_{\Phi}
be a Toeplitz operator on
H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})
such that any one of the following conditions hold,

(I)

\Phi(z)
is completely non-unitary on some subset of
\mathbb{T}
with positive measure,

(II)

\Phi(z)
has a unitary part a.e. on
\mathbb{T}
, but for any Hilbert space
\mathcal{F}
, and unitary
U\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{F})
, there does not exist any inner function
\Theta(z)\in H_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{E})}^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
satisfying

\Phi(z)\Theta(z)=\Theta(z)U;\quad\Phi(z)^{*}\Theta(z)=\Theta(z)U^{*}\quad(\text{a.e. on }\mathbb{T}).

Then
T_{\Phi}
is completely non-unitary and therefore, for any non-zero function
u\in H^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
with
|u(z)|<1
for all
z\in\mathbb{D}
, the operator
u(T_{\Phi})
exists and is a completely non-unitary contraction.

Let us highlight several natural questions worthy of further investigation.

Question
.

Characterize analytic Toeplitz operators
M_{\Phi}
on
H_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbb{D})
for which,
M_{\Phi}
is completely non-unitary if and only if
\Phi(0)
is complete non-unitary on
\mathcal{E}
.

Question
.

Characterize Toeplitz operators
T_{\Phi}
belonging to the class of
C_{\cdot 0}
-contractions.

Question
.

Characterize Toeplitz operators
T_{\Phi}
belonging to the class of
C_{0}
-contractions.

The answer to the above questions is unknown even in the scalar-valued Hardy spaces. However, if we consider analytic Toeplitz operators on both scalar/vector-valued Hardy spaces, we have a definite answer on the characterization of
C_{\cdot 0}
contractions (see
[
8
,
20
]
).
