1.
Introduction

Let
H
be the Hilbert transform, corresponding to the Fourier multiplier
i\operatorname{sgn}\xi
. (For further elaboration on notational conventions, see §
2
.)
We consider the Benjamin-Ono equation (BO)

\text{(BO)}\qquad\partial_{t}u=\partial_{x}(-H\partial_{x}u-\frac{1}{2}u^{2})

with
u
real-valued, on
\mathbb{R}
. The equation (BO) is a model for 1D long internal waves in stratefied fluid, introduced by Benjamin
[
3
]
and Ono
[
35
]
. (BO) has much in common with the Korteweg-de Vries equation (KdV)

\text{(KdV)}\qquad\partial_{t}u=\partial_{x}(-\partial_{x}^{2}u-3u^{2})

such as physical origin (KdV is a model of waves on shallow water surfaces) and the mathematical structure of complete integrability. Notably for our purposes as we discuss below, (KdV) has the same symplectic structure as (BO), when regarded as a Hamiltonian system, and both (KdV) and (BO) possess single solitary waves that propagate to the right. A key difference is that (BO) involves the Hilbert transform, which is a
nonlocal
operator, and this leads to solitary waves that have only algebraic decay for (BO), as opposed to exponential decay for (KdV).

By working with the three transformations
u(x,-t)
,
u(-x,t)
, and
-u(x,t)
we are in fact covering all four sign choices in
\partial_{t}u=\partial_{x}(\pm H\partial_{x}u\pm\frac{1}{2}u^{2})
. Hence we do not have a distinction between “focusing” or “defocusing” problems. Moreover, (BO) also satisfies translational invariance in space and has the scaling invariance, for
\lambda>0
,

u\text{ solves (BO)}\implies u_{\lambda}(x,t)=\lambda u(\lambda x,\lambda^{2}t)\text{ solves (BO)}

(BO) is completely integrable, so it enjoys infinitely many conserved quantities
[
5
]
, the first three of which are

M_{0}(u)=\frac{1}{2}\int u^{2}\,,\quad E_{0}(u)=-\frac{1}{2}\int uHu_{x}-\frac{1}{6}\int u^{3},

E_{1}(u)=\frac{1}{2}\int u^{2}_{x}+\frac{3}{8}\int u^{2}Hu_{x}-\frac{1}{16}\int u^{4}

Tao
[
42
]
proved local well-posedness of (BO) in
H_{x}^{1}
, and global well-posedness follows using the aforementioned conserved quantities. This result followed several earlier results at higher regularity, including
[
40
,
23
,
15
,
38
,
27
,
24
]
. The innovation Tao introduced was a gauge transformation to reduce the effective regularity of the nonlinearity. Following
[
42
]
, there were a few improvements to even lower regularity, using the gauge transformation idea combined with bilinear Strichartz estimates, culminating in the
L^{2}
result by Ionescu & Kenig
[
22
]
and Molinet & Pilod
[
33
]
.

By
soliton
we mean a coherent traveling wave solution. Amick & Toland
[
1
]
, Frank & Lenzmann
[
11
]
showed that there is a unique (up to translations) nontrivial
L^{\infty}
solution to

Q-HQ^{\prime}-\frac{1}{2}Q^{2}=0

given by

Q(x)=\frac{4}{1+x^{2}}

For any
c>0
,
a\in\mathbb{R}
, taking
Q_{a,c}(x)=cQ(c(x-a))
we have

(1.1)

cQ_{a,c}-HQ_{a,c}^{\prime}-\frac{1}{2}Q^{2}_{a,c}=0

Then

u(x,t)=Q_{ct,c}(x)=cQ(c(x-ct))

solves (BO) and we call it the
single soliton
solution to distinguish it from the exact multi-soliton solutions
[
6
]
arising from the completely integrable structure. The (BO) soliton is only decaying at infinity at power rate unlike for (KdV) where the soliton enjoys exponential decay.

Having summarized the basic properties of (BO), we now consider the following Hamiltonian perturbation of (BO)

\text{(pBO)}\qquad\partial_{t}u=\partial_{x}(-H\partial_{x}u+Vu-\frac{1}{2}u^{2})

with
slowly varying
potential

(1.2)

V(x)=W(hx)\,,\qquad W\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})\text{ and }0<h\ll 1

The well-posedness of (pBO) in
H^{1}
can be proved by adapting the gauge-transform method of Tao
[
42
]
. The Hamiltonian has been perturbed to

E(u)=E_{0}(u)+\frac{1}{2}\int Vu^{2}

(pBO) is of the form
\partial_{t}u=JE^{\prime}(u)
, where
J=\partial_{x}
. The symplectic form is given by
\omega(v_{1},v_{2})=\langle v_{1},J^{-1}v_{2}\rangle
, which is only densely defined.

1

Any integrable function
v_{2}
for which
\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}v_{2}(x)\,dx\neq 0
has the property that
\partial_{x}^{-1}v_{2}\notin L_{x}^{2}
. Hence the symplectic form is only densely defined. Despite this fact, the symplectic projection onto the soliton manifold
\mathcal{M}
is well-defined due to the fact that the tangent space of
\mathcal{M}
is spanned by functions that are the derivatives of smooth functions in
L^{2}
.
The restriction (pull-back by the inclusion map
i
) of this symplectic form to the two-dimensional
soliton manifold

\mathcal{M}=\{\,Q_{a,c}\,|\,a\in\mathbb{R}\,,\;c>0\;\}

is the canonical form

i^{*}(\omega)=4\pi da\wedge dc

A heuristic states that
if we assume
that the solution remains close to
\mathcal{M}
, then the projected flow on
\mathcal{M}
follows the Hamilton equations corresponding to
i^{*}(\omega)

(1.3)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\dot{c}=\frac{1}{4\pi}\partial_{a}E(Q_{a,c})=chW^{\prime}(ha)+\frac{1}{2}c^{-1}h^{3}W^{\prime\prime\prime}(ha)+O(h^{5})\\
&\dot{a}=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\partial_{c}E(Q_{a,c})=c-W(ha)+\frac{1}{2}c^{-2}h^{2}W^{\prime\prime}(ha)+O(h^{4})\end{aligned}\right.

This ODE system in
(a,c)
, scales to an
h
-independent system at leading order. Let
s=ht
, and consider
A(s)
and
C(s)
defined by

(1.4)

A(ht)=ha(t)\,,\qquad C(ht)=c(t)

Then the above becomes

(1.5)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\dot{C}=CW^{\prime}(A)+\frac{1}{2}C^{-1}h^{2}W^{\prime\prime\prime}(A)+O(h^{4})\\
&\dot{A}=C-W(A)+\frac{1}{2}C^{-2}h^{2}W^{\prime\prime}(A)+O(h^{4})\end{aligned}\right.

We have

0\leq s\leq 1\quad\iff\quad 0\leq t\leq h^{-1}

The time scale
0\leq s\ll\ln h^{-1}
corresponds to
0\leq t\ll\delta h^{-1}\ln h^{-1}
, which we call the
Ehrenfest time
. Analytically, we will
prove
that a solution starting close to
\mathcal{M}
remains close to
\mathcal{M}
for the Ehrenfest time and recover ODEs describing the parameter motion with slightly less precision than (
1.3
) and (
1.5
).

For the statement of our main result, we will need the
reference trajectory
, which is the solution
(\bar{A}(s),\bar{C}(s))
to

(1.6)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\dot{\bar{C}}=\bar{C}W^{\prime}(\bar{A})\\
&\dot{\bar{A}}=\bar{C}-W(\bar{A})\end{aligned}\right.

with initial condition
(\bar{A}(0),\bar{C}(0))=(0,1)
. This is an
h
-independent system and captures the leading-order
h
-independent terms in the expected full parameter dynamics (
1.5
). We let
S_{0}>0
be the first time
s>0
such that
\bar{C}(s)=\frac{1}{2}
or
\bar{C}(s)=2
, and
S_{0}=+\infty
if
C(s)
never reaches either
\frac{1}{2}
or
2
. Thus, for all
0\leq s<S_{0}
, we have

\frac{1}{2}\leq\bar{C}(s)\leq 2

Now let

(1.7)

\bar{a}(t)=h^{-1}\bar{A}(ht)\,,\qquad\bar{c}(t)=\bar{C}(ht)

Throughout the paper, the notation
A\lesssim B
means there exists a constant
K>0
such that
A\leq KB
.

Theorem 1.1

(main theorem)
.

Given a potential
W\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
(as in (
1.2
)), there exists
\kappa\geq 1
,
\mu>0
, and
0<h_{0}\ll 1
such that the following holds. Let
0<h\leq h_{0}
and suppose the initial data
u_{0}\in H_{x}^{1}
satisfies

\|u_{0}(x)-Q_{0,1}(x)\|_{H_{x}^{1/2}}\leq h^{3/2}

Then there exists a trajectory
(a(t),c(t))
such that
u
solving (pBO) with initial condition
u_{0}
satisfies

(1.8)

\|u(x,t)-Q_{a(t),c(t)}(x)\|_{H_{x}^{1/2}}\leq\kappa h^{3/2}e^{\mu ht}

for
0\leq t\leq T_{0}=h^{-1}\min(\frac{1}{4}\mu^{-1}\ln h^{-1},S_{0})
.

We have the following information about the trajectory
(a(t),c(t))
. Let
(\bar{A}(s),\bar{C}(s))
solve (
1.6
)
on
0\leq s\leq\min(\frac{1}{4}\mu^{-1}\ln h^{-1},S_{0})
, with initial condition

(\bar{A}(0),\bar{C}(0))=(0,1)
, and then define
(\bar{a}(t),\bar{c}(t))
according to (
1.7
). Then we have

(1.9)

|a(t)-\bar{a}(t)|\lesssim he^{2\mu ht}\,,\qquad|c(t)-\bar{c}(t)|\lesssim h^{2}e^{2\mu ht}

Notice that (
1.9
) implies that we can replace
c(t)
by
\bar{c}(t)
in (
1.8
), but cannot replace
a(t)
by
\bar{a}(t)
unless we sacrifice
h^{3/2}
accuracy for
h
accuracy in (
1.8
).

Let us remark on the
O(h^{-1})
time scale in Theorem
1.1
. The symplectic restriction heuristic produces the expected ODEs (
1.3
). The rescaling of time given by
s=ht
converts this ODE system to (
1.5
), which has the feature that the leading order terms in both components are
independent of

h
and
nontrivial perturbations of the free soliton dynamics
(involve
W
). Then achieving time
s=O(1)
(that is,
t=O(h^{-1})
) lets us observe the nontrivial distortions of the position
a
and scale
c
parameters. This makes
O(h^{-1})
a dynamically relevant time frame as

.

The key technical device needed to control the evolution is the Lyapunov functional
L_{c}(u)
appearing in (
2.8
). We are not able to extend Theorem
1.1
beyond the time scale

due to error terms that appear in the time derivative of

, so that the best possible estimate is

|\partial_{t}L_{c}(u)|\lesssim hL_{c}(u)

which results in the bound

, and this bound is only useful slightly beyond the time scale

2.
Notation, soliton profile and linearization properties

We take the Fourier transform in 1D as

\hat{f}(\xi)=\int e^{-ix\xi}f(x)\,dx

and inverse Fourier transform

\check{g}(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int e^{ix\xi}g(\xi)\,d\xi

We define the Hilbert transform as

Hf(x)=-\frac{1}{\pi}\operatorname{pv}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{f(y)}{x-y}\,dy=-\frac{1}{\pi}\operatorname{pv}\frac{1}{x}*f

and hence

\widehat{Hf}(\xi)=i(\operatorname{sgn}\xi)\hat{f}(\xi)

The fractional derivative operator
D^{s}
is defined as

\widehat{D^{s}f}(\xi)=|\xi|^{s}\hat{f}(\xi)

and thus
-H\partial_{x}=D
. Define the soliton profile (in standard position and scale) as

(2.1)

Q(x)=\frac{4}{1+x^{2}}

We have the partial fraction decomposition

\frac{1}{1+y^{2}}\frac{1}{x-y}=-\frac{1}{1+x^{2}}\frac{1}{y-x}+\frac{x}{1+x^{2}}\frac{1}{1+y^{2}}+\frac{1}{1+x^{2}}\frac{y}{1+y^{2}}

and hence (since first and third term integrate to zero)

HQ(x)=-\frac{4}{\pi}\frac{x}{1+x^{2}}\int\frac{dy}{1+y^{2}}=\frac{-4x}{1+x^{2}}

Taking the derivative in
x
we have

HQ_{x}=-\frac{8}{(1+x^{2})^{2}}+\frac{4}{1+x^{2}}=-\frac{1}{2}Q^{2}+Q

which is the soliton profile equation, which we rewrite for convenient reference:

(2.2)

-HQ_{x}+Q-\frac{1}{2}Q^{2}=0

We will also need that

(2.3)

\int Q^{2}=8\pi\,,\qquad\int Q^{3}=24\pi\,,\qquad\int x^{2}Q^{2}=8\pi

Recall that

E_{0}(Q)=-\frac{1}{2}\int Q\,HQ_{x}\,dx-\frac{1}{6}\int Q^{3}

Substituting (
2.2
),

E_{0}(Q)=-\frac{1}{2}\int Q^{2}+\frac{1}{12}\int Q^{3}

Plugging in (
2.3
), we obtain

(2.4)

E_{0}(Q)=-2\pi

Taking

Q_{a,c}(x)=cQ(c(x-a))

we obtain

(2.5)

\int Q_{a,c}^{2}=c\int Q^{2}=8\pi c\,,\qquad\text{and}\qquad E_{0}(Q_{a,c})=c^{2}E_{0}(Q)=-2\pi c^{2}

Let

\mathcal{L}=I-H\partial_{x}-Q

Key properties of
\mathcal{L}
follow by differentiating (
1.1
) with respect to
a
and with respect to
c
. Specifically, we have

(2.6)

\mathcal{L}Q_{x}=0\,\qquad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{L}(Q+xQ^{\prime})=-Q

Key spectral properties of
\mathcal{L}
follow from the two identities

(2.7)

\mathcal{L}Q=-\frac{1}{2}Q^{2}\,,\qquad\mathcal{L}Q^{2}=-2Q-Q^{2}

Summary of derivation:

•

direct computation gives
xQ^{\prime}=\frac{1}{2}Q^{2}-2Q

•

substitute into
\mathcal{L}(Q+xQ^{\prime})=-Q
to obtain
\mathcal{L}(-Q+\frac{1}{2}Q^{2})=-Q

•

soliton equation gives
\mathcal{L}Q=(Q-H\partial_{x}Q-\frac{1}{2}Q^{2})-\frac{1}{2}Q^{2}=-\frac{1}{2}Q^{2}

•

add to get
\mathcal{L}(\frac{1}{2}Q^{2})=-Q-\frac{1}{2}Q^{2}

By (
2.7
)

\mathcal{L}(2\alpha Q+\beta Q^{2})=-2\beta Q-(\alpha+\beta)Q^{2}

By suitably selecting
\alpha
and
\beta
, we can achieve the two eigenfunctions corresponding to
\lambda_{+}
and
\lambda_{-}
in the following proposition.
To achieve eigenfunctions, we need
\frac{\beta}{\alpha}=\frac{\alpha+\beta}{\beta}
, which yields a quadratic equation with solutions
\frac{\beta}{\alpha}=\frac{1}{2}\pm\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{5}
. If we take
\alpha=1
and
\beta=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{5}
and

e_{-}=2Q+(\tfrac{1}{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{5})Q^{2}\,,\qquad\lambda_{-}=-(\tfrac{1}{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{5})

then
\mathcal{L}e_{-}=\lambda_{-}e_{-}
.

Proposition 2.1

(from Appendix of
[
4
]
)
.

The operator
\mathcal{L}
has exactly four eigenvalues

\lambda_{1}=1,\quad\lambda_{0}=0,\quad\lambda_{+}=\frac{-1+\sqrt{5}}{2},\quad\lambda_{-}=\frac{-1-\sqrt{5}}{2}

and a continuous spectrum
[1,+\infty)
.
Moreover, the corresponding eigenspaces are one-dimensional, the eigenfunction for
\lambda_{0}=0
is
Q^{\prime}
, and the (non normalized) eigenfunction for
\lambda_{-}
is

e_{-}=2Q+\tfrac{1}{2}(1+\sqrt{5})Q^{2}

The above properties of
\mathcal{L}
convert, via scaling, to corresponding properties of
\mathcal{L}_{a,c}
, where

\mathcal{L}_{a,c}=cI-H\partial_{x}-Q_{a,c}

For example, (
2.6
) implies

\mathcal{L}_{a,c}(\partial_{a}Q_{a,c})=0\,,\qquad\mathcal{L}_{a,c}(\partial_{c}Q_{a,c})=-Q_{a,c}

In the case
a=0
, we simply denote the operator
\mathcal{L}_{c}=\mathcal{L}_{0,c}
.

For any nonlinear functional
\mathcal{A}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\to\mathbb{R}
(perhaps densely defined), the Frechet derivative
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(u)\in L^{2}
can be identified with a function, and the second derivative
\mathcal{A}^{\prime\prime}(u)
can be regarded as an operator
L^{2}\to L^{2}
, by the identifications

\forall\;v\in L^{2}\,,\quad\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(u)(v)=\langle\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(u),v\rangle

\forall\;v_{1},v_{2}\in L^{2}\,,\quad\mathcal{A}^{\prime\prime}(u)(v_{1},v_{2})=\langle\mathcal{A}^{\prime\prime}(u)v_{1},v_{2}\rangle

For our analysis, there are a few important functionals. The free Hamiltonian is

E_{0}(u)=\frac{1}{2}\|D^{1/2}u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}-\frac{1}{6}\int u^{3}

For the first and second derivatives, we have

E_{0}^{\prime}(u)=Du-\frac{1}{2}u^{2}

E_{0}^{\prime\prime}(u)=D-u

We also consider the perturbed Hamiltonian

E(u)=E_{0}(u)+\frac{1}{2}\int Vu^{2}

For the first and second derivatives, we have

E^{\prime}(u)=E_{0}^{\prime}(u)+Vu

E^{\prime\prime}(u)=E_{0}^{\prime\prime}(u)+V

The mass functional is

M(u)=\frac{1}{2}\|u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

For the first and second derivatives

M^{\prime}(u)=u

M^{\prime\prime}(u)=I

Consider the combined functional

Z_{c}(u)=cM(u)+E_{0}(u)

For the first and second derivatives (holding
c
fixed), we have

Z_{c}^{\prime}(u)=cu-H\partial_{x}u-\frac{1}{2}u^{2}

Z_{c}^{\prime\prime}(u)=c-H\partial_{x}-u

Using the equation for
Q_{a,c}
, we obtain

Z_{c}^{\prime}(Q_{a,c})=0

We note that

\mathcal{L}_{a,c}=Z_{c}^{\prime\prime}(Q_{a,c})

With
\eta\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm{def}}}{{=}}u-Q_{a,c}
, let

(2.8)

\displaystyle L_{c}(u)

\displaystyle=Z_{c}(u)-Z_{c}(Q_{a,c})-\langle Z_{c}^{\prime}(Q_{a,c}),\eta\rangle

\displaystyle=Z_{c}(u)-Z_{c}(Q_{a,c})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\langle\mathcal{L}_{a,c}\eta,\eta\rangle-\frac{1}{6}\int\eta^{3}

i.e.
L_{c}(u)
is the quadratic and higher order part of
Z_{c}(u)
around the reference function
Q_{a,c}
. This will be used as the Lyapunov functional in §
8
.

3.
Symplectic restriction heuristics

The phase space is
N=L^{2}(\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R})
(real-valued
L^{2}
functions on
\mathbb{R}
), with the inner product

\langle v_{1},v_{2}\rangle=\int v_{1}v_{2}\,dx

The energy is

(3.1)

E(u)=\frac{1}{2}\|D^{1/2}u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}-\frac{1}{6}\int u^{3}+\frac{1}{2}\int Vu^{2}

which is a densely defined nonlinear map
N\to\mathbb{R}
.
With respect to the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot,\rangle
,
E^{\prime}(u)
is identified with an element of
N
and
E^{\prime\prime}(u)
is identified with a map
N\to N
, which are given explicitly by

E^{\prime}(u)=Du-\frac{1}{2}u^{2}+Vu=-Hu_{x}-\frac{1}{2}u^{2}+Vu

E^{\prime\prime}(u)=-H\partial_{x}-u+V

Introduce the operator
J:N\to N
given by
J=\partial_{x}
, which is skew with respect to
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
. This gives a symplectic form on
N

\omega(v_{1},v_{2})=\langle v_{1},J^{-1}v_{2}\rangle

which is only densely defined. The corresponding Hamiltonian flow is

\partial_{t}u=JE^{\prime}(u)

which is precisely (pBO).

Let us consider the
soliton manifold

M
given by

M=\{\;Q_{a,c}\;\}_{a\in\mathbb{R},c>0}

where

Q_{a,c}(x)=cQ(c(x-a))

From (
2.2
), we obtain that
Q_{a,c}
solves the equation

(3.2)

-HQ_{a,c}^{\prime}+cQ_{a,c}-\frac{1}{2}Q_{a,c}^{2}=0

It is simpler to phrase some calculations using the group action

g_{a,c}f(x)=cf(c(x-a))

so that, in particular,
Q_{a,c}=g_{a,c}Q
.
To compute the restricted symplectic form, we need

\partial_{a}Q_{a,c}=-\partial_{x}Q_{a,c}=-cg_{a,c}\partial_{x}Q

\partial_{c}Q_{a,c}=c^{-1}g_{a,c}\partial_{x}(xQ)=c^{-2}\partial_{x}g_{a,c}(xQ)

It is also convenient to use the following (which follows by the change of variable
y=c(x-a)
)

\langle g_{a,c}v_{1},g_{a,c}v_{2}\rangle=c\langle v_{1},v_{2}\rangle

We compute

\displaystyle\omega(\partial_{a}Q_{a,c},\partial_{c}Q_{a,c})

\displaystyle=\langle\partial_{a}Q_{a,c},J^{-1}\partial_{c}Q_{a,c}\rangle=-c^{-1}\langle g_{a,c}Q^{\prime},g_{a,c}(xQ)\rangle

\displaystyle=-\langle Q^{\prime},xQ\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\|Q\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=4\pi

Thus, the restricted symplectic form is

i^{*}(\omega)=4\pi da\wedge dc

where
i:M\to N
is the inclusion. This form is non degenerate, so
M
is a
symplectic submanifold
. Let us now restrict the Hamiltonian
E
to
M
.

E(Q_{a,c})=\frac{1}{2}\|D^{1/2}Q_{a,c}\|^{2}-\frac{1}{6}\|Q_{a,c}\|_{L^{3}}^{3}+\frac{1}{2}\int VQ_{a,c}^{2}

To compute the first term, we substitute the soliton equation

\frac{1}{2}\|D^{1/2}Q_{a,c}\|^{2}=-\frac{1}{2}\langle HQ_{a,c}^{\prime},Q_{a,c}\rangle=-\frac{1}{2}c\|Q_{a,c}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\|Q_{a,c}\|_{L^{3}}^{3}

Hence

E(Q_{a,c})=-\frac{1}{2}c\|Q_{a,c}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{1}{12}\|Q_{a,c}\|_{L^{3}}^{3}+\frac{1}{2}\int VQ_{a,c}^{2}

E(Q_{a,c})=c^{2}(-\frac{1}{2}\|Q\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{1}{12}\|Q\|_{L^{3}}^{3})+\frac{1}{2}\int VQ_{a,c}^{2}

Substituting the values of the integrals (
2.3
),

(3.3)

E(Q_{a,c})=-2\pi c^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\int VQ_{a,c}^{2}

Now we view the two-dimensional symplectic manifold
M
, with sympletic form
4\pi da\wedge dc
, and the Hamiltonian
E(Q_{a,c})
(which is just a function
M\to\mathbb{R}
), as a two-dimensional Hamiltonian system. The corresponding equations of motion are

(3.4)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\dot{c}=\frac{1}{4\pi}\partial_{a}E(Q_{a,c})\\
&\dot{a}=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\partial_{c}E(Q_{a,c})\end{aligned}\right.

For this, we will want an asymptotic (as
h\to 0
) computation of
\frac{1}{2}\int VQ_{a,c}^{2}
. Changing variable to
y=c(x-a)
, we obtain

\frac{1}{2}\int W(hx)c^{2}Q(c(x-a))^{2}\,dx=\frac{1}{2}c\int W(ha+hc^{-1}y)Q(y)^{2}\,dy

=\frac{1}{2}c\int(W(ha)+\frac{1}{2}h^{2}c^{-2}y^{2}W^{\prime\prime}(ha)+O(h^{3})y^{3})Q(y)^{2}\,dy

where the odd terms in the Taylor expansion are dropped since they have zero integral
(although actually the term
\int y^{3}Q(y)^{2}
is not absolutely convergent, but since
\operatorname{supp}W
is compact, the integral can be localized to
|x|\lesssim h^{-1}
, leaving only a
\log h^{-1}
loss).

=\frac{1}{2}cW(ha)\int Q^{2}+\frac{1}{4}h^{2}c^{-1}W^{\prime\prime}(ha)\int y^{2}Q(y)^{2}\,dy+O(h^{3})

Substituting the values of the integrals (
2.3
),

\frac{1}{2}\int W(hx)c^{2}Q(c(x-a))^{2}\,dx=4\pi cW(ha)+2\pi c^{-1}W^{\prime\prime}(ha)h^{2}+O(h^{3})

Plugging into (
3.3
), we obtain

E(Q_{a,c})=-2\pi c^{2}+4\pi cW(ha)+2\pi c^{-1}W^{\prime\prime}(ha)h^{2}+O(h^{3})

Plugging in to (
3.4
), we get

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\dot{c}=\frac{1}{4\pi}\partial_{a}E(Q_{a,c})=chW^{\prime}(ha)+\frac{1}{2}c^{-1}h^{3}W^{\prime\prime\prime}(ha)+O(h^{4})\\
&\dot{a}=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\partial_{c}E(Q_{a,c})=c-W(ha)+\frac{1}{2}c^{-2}h^{2}W^{\prime\prime}(ha)+O(h^{3})\end{aligned}\right.

These indeed match, to leading order, the ODEs (
1.6
) describing the parameter dynamics in Theorem
1.1
.

4.
Estimates for the Hilbert transform

Below we provide two estimates for quadratic forms involving the Hilbert transform, Lemma
4.1
,
4.2
, that will later be needed in the proof of Lemma
8.1
.

Lemma 4.1
.

For
\chi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
and
0<h\leq 1
, we have

(4.1)

\left|\int_{y}\chi(hy)\cdot w\cdot H\partial_{y}w\,dy\right|\lesssim\|w\|_{H_{y}^{1/2}}^{2}

where the implicit constant depends on
\chi
but is uniform in
h
.

Proof.

By
[
26
, Theorem A.8]
, the fractional Leibniz rule states: for
0<\alpha<1
,
0\leq\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\leq\alpha
with
\alpha=\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}
,
1<p,p_{1},p_{2}<\infty
with
\frac{1}{p}=\frac{1}{p_{1}}+\frac{1}{p_{2}}
, there holds

(4.2)

\|D_{x}^{\alpha}(fg)-fD_{x}^{\alpha}g-gD_{x}^{\alpha}f\|_{L_{x}^{p}}\lesssim\|D_{x}^{\alpha_{1}}f\|_{L_{x}^{p_{1}}}\|D_{x}^{\alpha_{2}}g\|_{L_{x}^{p_{2}}}

To prove (
4.1
), we write

\int_{y}\chi(hy)\cdot w\cdot H\partial_{y}w\,dy=-\int_{y}D_{y}^{1/2}[\chi(hy)\cdot w]\cdot D_{y}^{1/2}w\,dy

so by Cauchy-Schwarz,

(4.3)

\left|\int_{y}\chi(hy)\cdot w\cdot H\partial_{y}w\,dy\right|\leq\|D_{y}^{1/2}[\chi(hy)\cdot w]\|_{L_{y}^{2}}\|D_{y}^{1/2}w\|_{L_{y}^{2}}

We apply (
4.2
) with
\alpha=\frac{1}{2}
,
\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}=\frac{1}{4}
, and
p=2
,
p_{1}=p_{2}=4
, to obtain

\|D_{y}^{1/2}[\chi(hy)\cdot w]-D_{y}^{1/2}[\chi(hy)]\cdot w-\chi(hy)\cdot D_{y}^{1/2}w\|_{L_{y}^{2}}\lesssim\|D_{y}^{1/4}[\chi(hy)]\|_{L_{y}^{4}}\|D_{y}^{1/4}w\|_{L_{y}^{4}}

To the right side, we apply the Sobolev embedding
\|f\|_{L^{4}}\lesssim\|D^{1/4}f\|_{L^{2}}
, to obtain

(4.4)

\|D_{y}^{1/2}[\chi(hy)\cdot w]\|_{L_{y}^{2}}\lesssim\begin{aligned} &\|D_{y}^{1/2}[\chi(hy)]\|_{L_{y}^{\infty}}\|w\|_{L_{y}^{2}}+\|\chi(hy)\|_{L_{y}^{\infty}}\|D_{y}^{1/2}w\|_{L_{y}^{2}}\\
&+\|D_{y}^{1/2}[\chi(hy)]\|_{L_{y}^{2}}\|D_{y}^{1/2}w\|_{L_{y}^{2}}\end{aligned}

By the interpolation estimates,

\|D_{y}^{1/2}[\chi(hy)]\|_{L_{y}^{\infty}}\lesssim\|\chi(hy)\|_{L_{y}^{2}}^{1/2}\|[\chi(hy)]^{\prime\prime}\|_{L_{y}^{2}}^{1/2}\lesssim h^{1/2}\|\chi\|_{L^{2}}^{1/2}\|\chi^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{2}}^{1/2}

\|D_{y}^{1/2}[\chi(hy)]\|_{L_{y}^{2}}\lesssim\|\chi(hy)\|_{L^{2}}^{1/2}\|[\chi(hy)]^{\prime}\|_{L_{y}^{2}}^{1/2}\lesssim h^{1/2}\|\chi\|_{L^{2}}^{1/2}\|\chi^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}}^{1/2}

inserted into (
4.4
) we obtain

\|D_{y}^{1/2}[\chi(hy)\cdot w]\|_{L_{y}^{2}}\lesssim\|w\|_{H_{y}^{1/2}}

where now the implicit constant depends on
\chi
, but is independent of
h
for
0<h\leq 1
. Plugging into (
4.3
), we obtain the desired estimate.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

For
\chi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
and
0<h\leq 1
, we have

(4.5)

\left|\int_{y}\chi(hy)\cdot H\partial_{y}w\cdot\partial_{y}w\,dy\right|\lesssim h^{2}\|w\|_{L_{y}^{2}}^{2}

where the implicit constant depends on
\chi
but is uniform in
h
.

Proof.

By scaling, it suffices to take
h=1
.
We follow the beginning of the proof of Lemma 3 on p. 916 of Kenig & Martel
[
25
]
, where it is observed that

(4.6)

\int_{y}\chi(hy)\cdot H\partial_{y}w\cdot\partial_{y}w\,dy=\iint_{y,y^{\prime}}K(y,y^{\prime})w(y)w(y^{\prime})\,dy\,dy^{\prime}

where

\displaystyle K(y,y^{\prime})

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial y\partial y^{\prime}}\left(\frac{\chi(y)-\chi(y^{\prime})}{y-y^{\prime}}\right)

(4.7)

\displaystyle=\frac{2(\chi(y)-\chi(y^{\prime}))-(\chi^{\prime}(y)+\chi^{\prime}(y^{\prime}))(y-y^{\prime})}{(y-y^{\prime})^{3}}

Using Taylor formulas to third order for
\chi(y)
and
\chi(y^{\prime})
, we obtain that for each
y
,
y^{\prime}
, there exists
y_{1}
,
y_{2}
between
y
and
y^{\prime}
such that

K(y,y^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\chi^{\prime\prime}(y^{\prime})-\chi^{\prime\prime}(y)}{y-y^{\prime}}+\frac{1}{6}(\chi^{\prime\prime\prime}(y_{1})+\chi^{\prime\prime\prime}(y_{2}))

By the mean-value theorem, there exists
y_{3}
between
y
and
y^{\prime}
such that

K(y,y^{\prime})=\frac{1}{6}\chi^{\prime\prime\prime}(y_{1})+\frac{1}{6}\chi^{\prime\prime\prime}(y_{2})+\frac{1}{2}\chi^{\prime\prime\prime}(y_{3})

and hence

(4.8)

|K(y,y^{\prime})|\lesssim 1

for all
y,y^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
.

By decomposition into
|y-y^{\prime}|\leq 1
(in which case we appeal to (
4.8
)) and
|y-y^{\prime}|\geq 1
(in which case we appeal to (
4.7
)), we obtain

\|K\|_{L_{y}^{\infty}L_{y^{\prime}}^{1}}\lesssim 1\,,\qquad\|K\|_{L_{y^{\prime}}^{\infty}L_{y}^{1}}\lesssim 1

We complete the proof by estimating the right side of (
4.6
) using the Schur test to obtain the bound

\|K\|_{L_{y}^{\infty}L_{y^{\prime}}^{1}}^{1/2}\|K\|_{L_{y^{\prime}}^{\infty}L_{y}^{1}}^{1/2}\|w\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

∎

5.
Energy estimate

Below in Lemma
5.1
, we state and prove the spectral lower bound on
\mathcal{L}
, following ideas of Weinstein
[
43
]
and Fröhlich et. al.
[
12
]
, and using the explicit spectral resolution of
\mathcal{L}
provided in the Appendix of Bennett et. al
[
4
]
quoted above as Prop.
2.1
. Lemma
5.1
will be needed in the proof of Lemma
8.1
.

Lemma 5.1

(energy bound)
.

There exists
\kappa_{1}>1
such that the following holds. If, for some
\frac{1}{2}\leq c\leq 2
,
w
satisfies
\langle w(y),cQ(cy)\rangle=0
and
\langle w(y),cyQ(cy)\rangle=0
, then

\kappa_{1}^{-1}\|w\|_{H^{1/2}}^{2}\leq\langle\mathcal{L}_{c}w,w\rangle\leq\kappa_{1}\|w\|_{H^{1/2}}^{2}

Proof.

We treat the case
c=1
. By elliptic regularity, it suffices to prove that

(5.1)

\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\langle w,Q\rangle=0\\
\langle w,xQ\rangle=0\\
\|w\|_{L^{2}}=1\end{subarray}}\langle\mathcal{L}w,w\rangle>0

To prove (
5.1
), it suffices to prove

(5.2)

\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\langle w,Q\rangle=0\\
\|w\|_{L^{2}}=1\end{subarray}}\langle\mathcal{L}w,w\rangle=0

Indeed, suppose that (
5.2
) holds. Then we know that the inf in (
5.1
) is
\geq 0
. Thus, we can assume by contradiction that it is
=0
. A minimizer
f_{0}
satisfies the Euler-Lagrange equation

(5.3)

\mathcal{L}f_{0}=\mu f_{0}+\nu Q+\omega xQ

Pairing (
5.3
) with
f_{0}
yields
\mu=0
. Pairing (
5.3
) with
Q^{\prime}
yields
\omega=0
. Then
\mathcal{L}f_{0}=\nu Q
. But
Q
is orthogonal to the kernel of
\mathcal{L}
, and hence there is a unique solution given by
f_{0}=\nu\mathcal{L}^{-1}Q
. We know that
\mathcal{L}(Q+xQ^{\prime})=-Q
, so
f_{0}=-\nu(Q+xQ^{\prime})
. However, the property
\langle f_{0},Q\rangle=0
yields that
\nu=0
. Thus,
f_{0}=0
, which contradicts that
\|f_{0}\|_{L^{2}}=1
. This concludes the proof that (
5.2
) implies (
5.1
).

It remains to prove (
5.1
). Let
\alpha
be the stated infimum, so that we aim to show that
\alpha=0
. Since
w=\frac{Q^{\prime}}{\|Q^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}}}
satisfies
\langle w,Q\rangle=0
and
\|w\|_{L^{2}}=1
, we know that
\alpha\leq 0
. We assume by contradiction that
\alpha<0
. A minimizer
w_{0}
satisfies the Euler-Lagrange equation

(5.4)

\mathcal{L}w_{0}=\mu w_{0}+\nu Q

Pairing (
5.4
) with
w_{0}
yields
\alpha=\mu
, we can now rewrite (
5.4
) as

(5.5)

\mathcal{L}w_{0}=\alpha w_{0}+\nu Q

We know that
\mathcal{L}
has one negative eigenvalue
\lambda_{-}=-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{5}
with corresponding eigenfunction
e_{-}=2Q+\frac{1}{2}(1+\sqrt{5})Q^{2}
. Since

\lambda_{-}=\inf_{\|w\|_{L^{2}}=1}\langle\mathcal{L}w,w\rangle

it follows that
\alpha\geq\lambda_{-}
. If
\alpha=\lambda_{-}
, then pairing (
5.5
) with
e_{-}
yields
0=\nu\langle Q,e_{-}\rangle
. From the formula for
e_{-}
, we conclude that
\nu=0
, and so
w_{0}=\frac{e_{-}}{\|e_{-}\|_{L^{2}}}
. This contradicts that
\langle w_{0},Q\rangle=0
. Therefore

\lambda_{-}<\alpha<0

and we know that
\mathcal{L}-\alpha
is invertible. Returning to (
5.5
), we can write

(5.6)

w_{0}=\nu(\mathcal{L}-\alpha)^{-1}Q

Pairing with
Q
we obtain

(5.7)

0=\nu\langle(\mathcal{L}-\alpha)^{-1}Q,Q\rangle

Consider the function

q(\lambda)=\langle(\mathcal{L}-\alpha)^{-1}Q,Q\rangle

Since
q^{\prime}(\lambda)=\|(\mathcal{L}-\alpha)^{-1}Q\|^{2}>0
, the function
q(\lambda)
is increasing. Moreover
q(0)=\langle\mathcal{L}^{-1}Q,Q\rangle=-\langle Q+xQ^{\prime},Q\rangle=-\frac{1}{2}\|Q\|^{2}<0
. Consequently
q(\alpha)<0
. Returning to (
5.7
), we conclude that
\nu=0
. Then (
5.6
) becomes
w_{0}=0
, contradicting that
\|w_{0}\|_{L^{2}}=1
.

∎

9.
ODE perturbation theory

Lemma 9.1

(Gronwall)
.

Suppose
X,\bar{X}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
solve

\displaystyle\dot{X}(s)=f(X(s))+h^{2}g(X,s)

\displaystyle\dot{\bar{X}}(s)=f(\bar{X}(s))

with the same initial condition
X(0)=\bar{X}(0)
,
where
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
g:\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Suppose that the
d\times d
matrix
f^{\prime}(X)
is uniformly bounded: for all
X\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\|f^{\prime}(X)\|_{\ell^{2}}\leq\kappa

where
\ell^{2}
is the square sum norm on the
d^{2}
entries of the matrix. Then

|X(s)-\bar{X}(s)|^{2}\leq h^{4}\int_{0}^{s}e^{-(2\kappa+1)(s-s^{\prime})}|g(X(s^{\prime}),s^{\prime})|^{2}\,ds^{\prime}

Proof.

Let
V(s)=X(s)-\bar{X}(s)
. Then (
|\bullet|
is the usual square sum norm on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
)

(9.1)

\frac{d}{ds}|V|^{2}=2V\dot{V}=2V\cdot(f(X)-f(\bar{X}))+2h^{2}V\cdot g(X,s)

We have

f(X)-f(\bar{X})=\int_{\sigma=0}^{1}\frac{d}{d\sigma}[f(\bar{X}+\sigma V)]\,d\sigma=\left(\int_{\sigma=0}^{1}f^{\prime}(\bar{X}+\sigma V)\,d\sigma\right)V

Then by Cauchy-Schwarz,

|f(X)-f(\bar{X})|\leq\kappa|V|

Substituting this into (
9.1
), and using that
2h^{2}V\cdot g(X,s)\leq|V|^{2}+h^{4}|g(X,s)|^{2}
, we obtain

\frac{d}{ds}|V|^{2}\leq(2\kappa+1)|V|^{2}+h^{4}|g(X,s)|^{2}

The standard integrating factor method completes the proof.
∎

In our application,

X=\begin{bmatrix}A\\
C\end{bmatrix}\,,\qquad f(X)=\begin{bmatrix}C-W(A)\\
CW^{\prime}(A)\end{bmatrix}

Then

f^{\prime}(X)=\begin{bmatrix}-W^{\prime}(A)&1\\
CW^{\prime\prime}(A)&W^{\prime}(A)\end{bmatrix}

Since
\frac{1}{2}\leq C\leq 2
, this is uniformly bounded.
