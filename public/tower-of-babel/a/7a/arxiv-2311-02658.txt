Nonparametric Estimation and Comparison of Distance Distributions from Censored Data
Lucas H. McCabe

Abstract
Transportation distance information is a powerful resource, but location records are often censored due to privacy concerns or regulatory mandates. We outline methods to approximate, sample from, and compare distributions of distances between censored location pairs, a task with applications to public health informatics, logistics, and more. We validate empirically via simulation and demonstrate applicability to practical geospatial data analysis tasks.

Analysis of transportation data is often constrained by the censoring of location records. This can occur in the public health domain, where precise location data may be risky to share. It has been reported that rural Americans live, on average, over twice as far from their nearest hospital as their counterparts in urban areas
[
1
]
. What impact does this have on the practical experiences of patients in emergency situations? For instance, evidence suggests that incident location-to-destination ambulance journey distance is positively correlated with patient mortality
[
2
]
. Such questions are important to the study of equity in medicine, but they can be more difficult to answer when location information is censored.

The study of migration flows can also be hindered by location-censoring. As part of the American Community Survey (ACS), the United States Census collects detailed migration information, but the data is censored (e.g., to county-level) for public release
[
3
]
. Franklin and Plane underscore this geographic censoring (e.g., varying spatial scales over time) as a limitation for migration research and express interest in more informative records while ensuring confidentiality
[
4
]
.

In such situations, some researchers assign event origin and destination points to the centroid (or other representative point) of their corresponding censored regions
[
5
,
6
]
. The National Bureau of Economic Research hosts a County Distance Database, containing the Haversine distances between internal points of U.S. counties, which can support such analyses
[
7
]
. When the level of spatial aggregation is relatively small for the problem, the resulting approximation can be satisfactory
[
8
]
, but this is not always the case and can lead to errors and contradictory results
[
9
]
.

It is important to consider the informativeness of the censoring mechanism. For instance, in surveys where respondents offer intervals covering a quantity of interest, the intervals provide information about the true values
[
10
]
. In an estimation of length distributions from line segment processes, censoring arises from the sampling scheme, allowing authors Pawlas and Zikmundová to treat the mechanism as uninformative
[
11
]
. In our case, the censoring of transportation events to administrative boundaries is done to meet privacy standards, which we consider uninformative, as well.

In this work, our contributions are as follows:

•

We treat the estimation of transportation event distance distributions from location-censored records as a survival analysis problem (Section
II-B
).

•

We present a stochastic dominance test for collections of location-censored transportation events (Section
II-D
).

•

We validate our approach empirically via simulation (Section
III-A
,
III-B
) and demonstrate practical applicability by reanalyzing data from a study involving breast cancer screening (Section
III-C
).

Although we are, of course, not the first to employ survival analysis for transportation geography (e.g.,
[

13
]
) nor the first to compare survival curves (e.g.,
[

,

), we present a framework for comparing collections of censored transportation events and provide a sampling-based U-test for the task.

II
Transportation Event Distance Distribution Reconstruction

II-A

Overview

Briefly, we define:

•

a
location
as a geographical point characterized by coordinates,

•

a
locale
as a censored representation of a location, and

•

a
transportation event
as an origin-destination pair. When the origin and destination are locations, it is an
uncensored
transportation event. When the origin and destination are locales, it is a
censored
transportation event.

The distance associated with a transportation event is the distance between start and end locations, a lower-bound on the actual distance that may have been traveled. Provided a sample of
censored
transportation events, we aim to estimate the distribution of the
uncensored
transportation event distances.

II-B

Drawing Uncensored Samples

We are provided a list of censored transportation events, where each event is a tuple of origin and destination locales.

1.

Obtain Distance Intervals:
For each start and end locale, we extract coordinates along the boundary (
\{b_{\text{start}_{1}},b_{\text{start}_{2}},\ldots\}
and
\{b_{\text{end}_{1}},b_{\text{end}_{2}},\ldots\}
, respectively), which may be obtained from administrative shape files.

For each event, we construct a distance matrix
D
with elements
D_{ij}=f(b_{\text{start}_{i}},b_{\text{end}_{j}})
and distance function
f
. The event’s observation interval
[\min(D),\max(D)]
includes the true distance of the transportation event.

2.

CDF Estimation:
Our distance intervals are interval-censored event records. We estimate the complementary cumulative distribution (survival function) using Turnbull’s nonparametric estimator, provided by the iterative algorithm detailed in
[
16
]
. For computational efficiency, the survival function may be obtained by an expectation-maximization algorithm, as provided in
[
17
]
.

For any
d

100(1-\alpha)\%
confidence interval
\Big(\hat{S}_{\text{lower},\alpha}(d),\hat{S}_{\text{upper},\alpha}(d)\Big)
from our estimated survival function
\hat{S}
via the exponential Greenwood formula, as provided by
[
18
,
19
]
:

\widehat{Var}[\hat{S}(d)]=\frac{1}{{\left(\log(\hat{S}(d))\right)}^{2}}\sum_{d_{i}\leq d}\frac{y_{i}}{n_{i}(n_{i}-y_{i})}

(1)

g(d)=\log(-\log(\hat{S}(d)))

(2)

h(d)=Q\Big(\frac{\alpha}{2}\Big)\sqrt{\widehat{Var}[\hat{S}(d)]}

(3)

\hat{S}_{\text{lower},\alpha}(d),\hat{S}_{\text{upper},\alpha}(d)=\exp(-\exp(g(d)\pm h(d)))

(4)

where

is the quantile function of the standard normal distribution,
n_{i}
is the number of events with distance at least
d_{i}
, and
y_{i}
is the number of events with distance between

d_{i+1}

\hat{F}(d)=1-\hat{S}(d)

\hat{F}_{\text{lower},\alpha}(d)=1-\hat{S}_{\text{upper},\alpha}(d)

(5)

\hat{F}_{\text{upper},\alpha}(d)=1-\hat{S}_{\text{lower},\alpha}(d)

(6)

3.

Survival Function Sampling:
We sample in two stages to account for the uncertainty in estimating
F

d_{*}=\hat{F}^{-1}(u),\quad u\sim\text{Uniform}(0,1)

Assuming the uncertainty to be Gaussian, we draw:

\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}),\quad\sigma=\frac{\hat{F}_{\text{upper},\alpha}(d_{*})-\hat{F}(d_{*})}{Q(1-\frac{\alpha}{2})}

d_{\text{sample}}=d_{*}+\epsilon

Where
d_{\text{sample}}
is our sampled distance.

II-C

Stochastic Dominance

To illustrate the application of this approach to practical data analysis workflows, we consider the following situation: we are provided collections
E_{A}=\{e_{A,1},e_{A,2},\ldots,e_{A,i}\}
and
E_{B}=\{e_{B,1},e_{B,2},\ldots,e_{B,j}\}

A
and
B

A

B

Because the sampling procedure is non-deterministic, instability of downstream test statistics and p-values may arise. This can be addressed by repeating a hypothesis test, collecting the p-values, and applying Fisher’s combined probability test. Given p-values

T=-2\sum_{i=1}^{k}\log(p_{i}),

(7)

where
T\sim\chi^{2}_{2k}
under the null hypothesis of uniformly distributed and independent p-values
[
20

II-D

Statistical Distance

Given
E_{A}
and
E_{B}
, we may wish to estimate some notion of distance between their uncensored generating distributions. One such notion is the Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) statistic. We may obtain
\hat{F}_{A}
and
\hat{F}_{B}
for collections
E_{A}
and
E_{B}
, respectively, via Steps 1-2 in Section
II-B
before calculating
[
22
]
:

K=\max_{d}|\hat{F}_{A}(d)-\hat{F}_{B}(d)|.

(8)

This is equivalent to the approach described in
[
23
]
, which calculates the maximum vertical distance between patient survival curves.

Alternatively, we may consider Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD), a statistical distance between distributions expressed as the largest difference in expected values across functions of a class
\mathcal{G}
, described in detail in
[
24
,
25
]
. Absent direct access to their uncensored generating distributions, we can draw distance samples
s_{A}
(of size
m
) and
s_{B}
(of size
n
) from
\hat{F}_{A}
and
\hat{F}_{B}
, respectively, and apply the empirical MMD estimate provided in
[
24
]
:

\displaystyle\text{MMD}_{\mathcal{G}}(s_{A},s_{B})=\bigg(

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}k(s_{A,i},s_{A,j})

(9)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}k(s_{B,i},s_{B,j})

\displaystyle-\frac{2}{mn}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}k(s_{A,i},s_{B,j})\bigg)^{1/2}

with kernel
k
, which we take to be the radial basis function kernel with bandwidth parameter
\sigma
:

k(x,y)=\exp\left(-\frac{\|x-y\|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)

We conduct a brief simulation study to evaluate the performance of the proposed approach. We design a simple two-dimensional environment divided into equally-sized rectangular locales, with locations determined uniformly at random. Transportation event generation simulates trips between locations on the grid. Source locations are selected uniformly at random, and the destination of each trip is chosen based on an inverse distance policy, where the probability of choosing a location as the destination is inversely proportional to its distance

\displaystyle P(\text{dest.}

An analysis of
1998

2001
public health records from North Derbyshire, United Kingdom studied breast cancer screening uptake among women aged
50-64
and its relationship with socioeconomic status and distance from screening facility. The authors had access to precise locations of the screening facilities, but only postal codes for resident addresses. The authors consider the minimum and maximum distances the residents could have traveled to each location within their postal code. When treating the censored transportation events as categorical data (Table
II
), they conduct a chi-squared test to analyze the impact of distance on attendance
[
27
]
.

Although this is a sensible analysis, there are limitations. Because it treats distance as a categorical variable, the ordinality of distance is ignored. Additionally, the chi-squared test aims to assess whether the expected frequency of events occurring in the distance bins differs between the “Invited” and “Attended” groups; even if the result is deemed significant, it does not specify the nature of the relationship. The authors also interpret distance as a continuous variable by mapping postal codes to their corresponding centroid location, a technique not uncommon in this situation, but we believe this mapping makes results difficult to interpret.

We apply Algorithm
1
to the data in Table
II
, with the alternative hypothesis that the distribution of distances from screening center for women invited for screening stochastically dominates the distribution of distances for women actually attending screening. We assume a comfortable
100
km upper bound on distance traveled, run
100
trials with
100
samples drawn per trial, and do not find a statistically significant result (
p=0.8813
). We repeat the procedure for “Not Attended” (i.e., those invited, but did not attend) and “Attended,” also failing to find a statistically significant result (
p=0.7671
).

IV
Conclusion

In this work, we discuss the analysis of censored transportation event records. We leverage ideas from survival analysis to perform this estimation and suggest a sampling-based stochastic dominance test for comparing sets of censored transportation records. Future work should refine the approach and consider other tests for comparing survival curves, such as the Mantel–Cox test
[
14
]
.
