Strong posterior contraction rates via Wasserstein dynamics
Emanuele Dolera, Stefano Favaro, Edoardo Mainini

Abstract
In Bayesian statistics, posterior contraction rates (PCRs) quantify the speed at which the posterior distribution concentrates on arbitrarily small neighborhoods of a true model, in a suitable way, as the sample size goes to infinity. In this paper, we develop a new approach to PCRs, with respect to strong norm distances on parameter spaces of functions. Critical to our approach is the combination of a local Lipschitz-continuity for the posterior distribution with a dynamic formulation of the Wasserstein distance, which allows to set forth an interesting connection between PCRs and some classical problems arising in mathematical analysis, probability and statistics, e.g., Laplace methods for approximating integrals, Sanov’s large deviation principles in the Wasserstein distance, rates of convergence of mean Glivenko-Cantelli theorems, and estimates of weighted Poincaré-Wirtinger constants. We first present a theorem on PCRs for a model in the regular infinite-dimensional exponential family, which exploits sufficient statistics of the model, and then extend such a theorem to a general dominated model. These results rely on the development of novel techniques to evaluate Laplace integrals and weighted Poincaré-Wirtinger constants in infinite-dimension, which are of independent interest. The proposed approach is applied to the regular parametric model, the multinomial model, the finite-dimensional and the infinite-dimensional logistic-Gaussian model and the infinite-dimensional linear regression. In general, our approach leads to optimal PCRs in finite-dimensional models, whereas for infinite-dimensional models it is shown explicitly how the prior distribution affect PCRs.



Keywords:
Bayesian consistency; density estimation; dominated statistical model; Laplace method; Lipschitz-continuity; posterior contraction rate; regular infinite-dimensional exponential family; Wasserstein dynamics; weighted Poincaré-Wirtinger constant

1
Introduction

Bayesian consistency guarantees that the posterior distribution concentrates on arbitrarily small neighborhoods of the true model, in a suitable way, as the sample size goes to infinity (
Doob 1949
,
Schwartz 1965
,
Freedman 1963
,
Freedman 1965
,
Diaconis and Freedman 1986
,
Barron et al. 1999
,
Ghosal et al. 2000
,
Walker 2004
). See
Ghosal and van der Vaart 2000
for a general overview on Bayesian consistency. Posterior contractions rates (PCRs) strengthen the notion of Bayesian consistency, as they quantify the speed at which such small neighborhoods of the true model may decrease to zero meanwhile still capturing most of the posterior mass. The problem of establishing optimal PCRs in finite-dimensional (parametric) Bayesian models have been first considered in
Ibragimov and Has’minskiǐ 1981
and
LeCam 1986
. However, it is in the works of
Ghosal et al. 2000
and
Shen and Wasserman 2002
that the problem of establishing PCRs have been investigated in a systematic way, setting forth a general approach to provide PCRs in both finite-dimensional and infinite-dimensional (nonparametric) Bayesian models. Since then, several methods have been proposed to obtain more explicit and also sharper PCRs. Among them, we recall the metric entropy approach, in combination with the definition of specific tests (
Schwartz 1965
,
Ghosal et al. 2000
), the methods based on bracketing numbers and entropy integrals (
Shen and Wasserman 2002
), the martingale approach (
Walker 2004
,
Walker et al. 2007
), the Hausdorff entropy approach
Xing 2010
, and some approaches based on the Wasserstein distance (
Chae et al. 2020
,
Camerlenghi et al. 2022
). See
Ghosal and van der Vaart 2000
, and references therein, for a comprehensive and up-to-date account on PCRs.

1.1
Our contributions

In this paper, we develop a new approach to PCRs, in the spirit of the seminal work of
Ghosal et al. 2000
. We consider a dominated statistical model as a family
\mathscr{M}=\{f_{\theta}\}_{\theta\in\Theta}
of densities, with the parameter space
\Theta
being a (possibly infinite-dimensional) separable Hilbert space. We focus on posterior Hilbert neighborhoods of a given true parameter, say
\theta_{0}
, measuring PCRs in terms of strong norm distances on parameter spaces of functions, such as Sobolev-like norms. This
assumption on
\Theta
yields a stronger metric structure on
\mathscr{M}
, as a subset of the space of densities, usually not equivalent to those considered so far by the literature on nonparametric density estimation (see, e.g.,
Ghosal et al. 1999
,
Giné and Nickl 2011
,
Scricciolo 2006
,
Shen and Wasserman 2002
,
van der Vaart and van Zanten 2008
,
Walker 2004
,
Walker et al. 2007
), based on the choice of (pseudo-)distances such as the
\mathrm{L}^{p}
-norm, the Hellinger, the Kullback-Leibler, and the chi-square. To the best of our knowledge, we are not aware of works in the Bayesian literature that deal with strong PCRs for density estimation by using constructive tests, as prescribed by the standard theory, even if this line of research could be pursued as well. As far as we know, the standard nonparametric approach covers the case of (semi-)metrics which are dominated
by the Hellinger distance (see, e.g.,
Ghosal and van der Vaart 2000
).

We present a theorem on PCRs for the regular infinite-dimensional exponential family of statistical model, and a theorem on PCRs for a general dominated statistical models. The former may be viewed as a special case of the latter, allowing to exploit sufficient statistics arising from the infinite-dimensional exponential family. Critical to our approach is an assumption of local Lipschitz-continuity for the posterior distribution, with respect to the observations or a sufficient statistics of them. Such a property is typically known as “Bayesian well-posedness” (
Stuart 2010
), and it has been investigated in depth in
Dolera and Mainini 2020a
,
Dolera and Mainini 2020b
. By combining the local Lipschitz-continuity with the dynamic formulation of the Wasserstein distance (
Benamou and Brenier 2000
,
Ambrosio et al. 2008
), referred to as Wasserstein dynamics, we set forth a connection between the problem of establishing PCRs and some classical problems arising in mathematical analysis, probability and statistics, e.g., Laplace methods for approximating integrals (
Breitung 1994
,
Wong 2001
), Sanov’s large deviation principle in Wasserstein distance (
Bolley et al. 2007
,
Jing 2020
), rates of convergence of mean Glivenko-Cantelli theorems (
Ajtai et al. 1984
,
Ambrosio et al. 2019
,
Dobrić and Yukic 1995
,
Dolera and Regazzini 2019
,
Fournier and Guillin 2015
,
Talagrand 1994a
,
Talagrand 1994b
,
Bobkov and Ledoux 2019
,
Weed and Bach 2019
,
Jing 2020
), and estimates of weighted Poincaré-Wirtinger constants (
Bakry et al. 2014
,
Heinonen et al. 1993
). In particular, our study leads to introduce new results on Laplace methods for approximating integrals and the estimation of weighted Poincaré-Wirtinger constants in infinite dimension, which are of independent interest.

Some applications of our main theorems are presented for the regular parametric model, the multinomial model, the finite-dimensional and the infinite-dimensional logistic-Gaussian model and the infinite-dimensional linear regression. It turns out that our main results lead to optimal PCRs in finite dimension, whereas in infinite dimension it is shown explicitly how the prior distribution affects PCRs. Among the applications of our results, the infinite-dimensional logistic-Gaussian model is arguably the best setting to motivate the use of strong norm distances. In such a setting our approach is of interest when the ultimate goal of the inferential procedure is the estimation of some functional
\Phi(f_{\theta})
of the density
[
77
, Chapter 6]
for which the mapping
f\mapsto\Phi(f)
is not continuous with respect to the aforesaid metrics on densities, whereas
\theta\mapsto\Phi(f_{\theta})
turns out to be even locally Lipschitz-continuous with respect to the Hilbertian metric on
\Theta
. Thus, strong norms allow to consider larger classes of functionals of density functions, and then possibly a broader range of analyses. Another motivation in the use of strong norms comes from the theory of density estimation under penalized loss functions, with penalizations depending on derivatives of the density, according to the original Good-Gaskins proposal
[
57
,
76
]
. As these penalized loss functions are used to derive smoother estimators, it sounds interesting to derive relative PCRs under the same loss functions.

1.2
Related works

The most popular classical (frequentist) approaches to density estimation are developed within the following frameworks: i) a parameter space that is the space of density functions, typically endowed with the
\mathrm{L}^{p}
norm or the Hellinger distance (
Tsybakov 1994
), usually associated to the notion of “strong consistency”; ii) a parameter space that is the space of density functions endowed with the Wasserstein distance, under which the parameter space is metrized according to a (concrete) metric structure on the space of the observations (
Berthet and Niels-Weed 2021
), usually associated to the notion of “weak consistency”. Both these
frameworks are different from the one we consider in this paper, and therefore a comparison of our PCRs with optimal minimax rates from
Tsybakov 1994
and
Berthet and Niels-Weed 2021
it is not directly possible. Within the classical literature,
Sriperumbudur et al. 2017
considered our statistical framework and provided rates of consistency under the infinite-dimensional exponential family of statistical models, though without any formal statement on their minimax optimality. In principle, our approach to PCRs may be developed within the aforementioned popular statistical frameworks for density estimation. However, since our approach relies on properties of the Wasserstein distance that are well-known for parameter spaces with a linear structure, i.e. Wasserstein dynamics, the framework considered in this paper is the most natural and convenient to start with. As for the other statistical frameworks for density estimation, we conjecture that our approach to PCRs requires a suitable formulation of Wasserstein dynamics for parameter spaces with a nonlinear structure. While such a formulation is available from
Gigli 2009
and
Gigli and Ohta 2012
, it is still not clear to us how to exploit it to deal with PCRs.

1.3
Organization of the paper

The paper is structured as follows. In Section
2
we recall the definition of PCR, presenting an equivalent definition in terms of the Wasserstein distance, and we outline the main steps of our approach to PCRs. Section
3
contains the main results of our work, that is a theorem on PCRs for the regular infinite-dimensional exponential family of statistical models, and a generalization of it for general dominated statistical models. In Section
4
we present some applications of our results for the regular parametric model, the multinomial model, the finite-dimensional and the infinite-dimensional logistic-Gaussian model and the infinite-dimensional linear regression. Section
5
contains a discussion of some directions for future work, especially with respect to the application of our approach to other nonparametric models, such as the popular class of hierarchical (mixture) models. Proofs of our results are deferred to appendices.

2
A new approach to PCRs

We consider
n\geq 1
observations to be modeled as part of a sequence
X^{(\infty)}:=\{X_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
of exchangeable random variables, with the
X_{i}
’s taking values in a measurable space
(\mathbb{X},\mathscr{X})
. Let
(\Theta,\mathrm{d}_{\Theta})
be metric space, referred to as the parameter space, endowed with its Borel
\sigma
-algebra
\mathscr{T}
. Moreover, let
\pi
be a probability measure on
(\Theta,\mathscr{T})
, referred to as the prior measure, and let
\mu(\cdot\,|\,\cdot):\mathscr{X}\times\Theta\rightarrow[0,1]
be a probability kernel, referred to as the statistical model. The Bayesian approach relies on modeling the parameter of interest as a
\Theta
-valued random variable, say
T
, with probability distribution
\pi
. At the core of Bayesian inferences lies the posterior distribution, that is the conditional distribution of
T
given a random sample
(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
, whenever both
T
and the sequence
X^{(\infty)}
are supported on a common probability space
(\Omega,\mathscr{F},\mathds{P})
. The minimal regularity conditions that are maintained, and possibly strengthened, throughout the paper are the following: the set
\mathbb{X}
is a separable topological space, with
\mathscr{X}
coinciding with the ensuing Borel
\sigma
-algebra, and
(\Theta,\mathscr{T})
is a standard Borel space. In this setting, the posterior distribution can be represented through a probability kernel
\pi_{n}(\cdot\,|\,\cdot):\mathscr{T}\times\mathbb{X}^{n}\rightarrow[0,1]
that satisfies the disintegration

\mathds{P}[X_{1}\in A_{1},\dots,X_{n}\in A_{n},T\in B]=\int_{A_{1}\times\dots\times A_{n}}\pi_{n}(B\,|\,x^{(n)})\alpha_{n}(\mathrm{d}x^{(n)})

(1)

for all sets
A_{1},\dots,A_{n}\in\mathscr{X}
and
B\in\mathscr{T}
and
n\geq 1
, where
x^{(n)}:=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
and

\alpha_{n}(A_{1}\times\dots\times A_{n}):=\int_{\Theta}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mu(A_{i}\,|\,\theta)\right]\pi(\mathrm{d}\theta),

(2)

so that
\mathds{P}[T\in B\,|\,X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]=\pi_{n}(B\,|\,X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
is valid
\mathds{P}
-a.s. for any
B\in\mathscr{T}
.

Remark 2.1
.

When the statistical model
\mu(\cdot\,|\,\cdot)
is dominated by some
\sigma
-finite measure
\lambda
on
(\mathbb{X},\mathscr{X})
, with a relative family of
\lambda

\{f(\cdot\,|\,\theta)\}_{\theta\in\Theta}
, then (a version of) the posterior distribution is given by the Bayes formula, that is we write

\pi_{n}(B\,|\,x^{(n)})=\frac{\int_{B}[\prod_{i=1}^{n}f(x_{i}\,|\,\theta)]\pi(\mathrm{d}\theta)}{\int_{\Theta}[\prod_{i=1}^{n}f(x_{i}\,|\,\theta)]\pi(\mathrm{d}\theta)}

for any set
B\in\mathscr{T}
and
\alpha_{n}
-a.e.
x^{(n)}
, while
\alpha_{n}
turns out to be absolutely continuous with respect to the product measure
\lambda^{\otimes_{n}}
with density function of the form

\rho_{n}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}):=\int_{\Theta}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{n}f(x_{i}\,|\,\theta)\right]\pi(\mathrm{d}\theta)\ .

(3)

We say that the posterior distribution is (weakly) consistent at
\theta_{0}\in\Theta
if, as
n\rightarrow+\infty
,
\pi_{n}(U_{0}^{c}\,|\,\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})\rightarrow 0
holds in probability for any neighborhood
U_{0}
of
\theta_{0}
, where
\xi^{(\infty)}:=\{\xi_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
stands for a sequence of
\mathbb{X}
-valued independent random variables identically distributed as
\mu_{0}(\cdot):=\mu(\cdot|\theta_{0})
(
Ghosal and van der Vaart 2000
). The non uniqueness of the posterior distribution
\pi_{n}
requires additional regularity assumptions in order that
\pi_{n}(\cdot\,|\,\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})
is well-defined. PCRs strengthen the notion of Bayesian consistency, in the sense that they quantify the speed at which such neighborhoods may decrease to zero meanwhile still capturing most of the posterior mass. In particular, the definition of PCR can be stated as follows (
Ghosal and van der Vaart 2000
).

Definition 2.2
.

A sequence
\{\epsilon_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
of positive numbers is a PCR at
\theta_{0}
if, as
n\rightarrow+\infty
,

\pi_{n}\left(\left\{\theta\in\Theta:\mathrm{d}_{\Theta}(\theta,\theta_{0})\geq M_{n}\epsilon_{n}\right\}\,|\,\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n}\right)\rightarrow 0

(4)

holds in probability for every sequence
\{M_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
of positive numbers such that
M_{n}\rightarrow\infty
.

Now, we present our approach to PCRs based on the Wasserstein distance. This is a new approach, which relies on four main steps that are outlined hereafter. The first step of our approach originates from a reformulation of Definition
2.2
in terms of the so-called
p

\mathcal{W}_{p}^{(\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{M}))}(\gamma_{1};\gamma_{2}):=\inf_{\eta\in\mathcal{F}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})}\left(\int_{\mathbb{M}^{2}}[\mathrm{d}_{\mathbb{M}}(x,y)]^{p}\ \eta(\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y)\right)^{1/p}

(5)

\mathcal{P}_{p}(\mathbb{M}):=\left\{\gamma\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{M})\ :\ \int_{\mathbb{M}}[\mathrm{d}_{\mathbb{M}}(x,x_{0})]^{p}\gamma(\mathrm{d}x)<+\infty\,\ \text{for\ some\ }x_{0}\in\mathbb{M}\right\}

Lemma 2.3

Assume that

The second step of our approach relies on the assumption of the existence of a suitable sufficient statistics. In particular, we assume the existence of another metric space, say

The third step of our approach relies on the large

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{W}_{p}^{(\mathcal{P}(\Theta))}(\pi_{n}^{\ast}(\cdot\,|\,S_{0});\delta_{\theta_{0}})

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt+L_{0}^{(n)}\mathds{E}[\mathrm{d}_{\mathbb{S}}(\hat{S}_{n},S_{0})]

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt\hskip 9.24994pt+\mathds{E}[\mathcal{W}_{p}^{(\mathcal{P}(\Theta))}(\pi_{n}^{\ast}(\cdot\,|\,S_{0});\pi_{n}^{\ast}(\cdot\,|\,\hat{S}_{n}))\mathds{1}\{\hat{S}_{n}\not\in\mathcal{U}_{\delta_{n}}(S_{0})\}].

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}\int_{\mathbb{M}}\psi(x)\gamma_{s}(\mathrm{d}x)\ _{\big|s=t}=\int_{\mathbb{M}}\langle\mathbf{v}(x),\mathrm{D}\psi(x)\rangle\gamma_{t}(\mathrm{d}x)\hskip 18.49988pt\forall\ \psi\in C^{1}_{b}(\mathbb{M}).

,
yielding the following weaker definition

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{\mathfrak{C}_{2}^{(M)}[\mu]}\right)^{2}=\inf_{\{\psi\in C^{1}_{b}(\mathbb{M})\,:\,\int_{\mathbb{M}}\psi(x)\,\mu(\mathrm{d}x)=0\,\wedge\,\int_{\mathbb{M}}|\psi(x)|^{2}\,\mu(\mathrm{d}x)=1\}}\int_{\mathbb{M}}\|\mathrm{\mathcal{D}}\psi(x)\|^{2}\,\mu(\mathrm{d}x).

We refer to the monographs of
Bogachev 2010
,
Da Prato 2014
,
Da Prato and Zabczyk 2014
for a detailed account of Malliavin calculus and related Sobolev spaces.

3
Main results on PCRs

Following the approach to PCRs outlined in Section
2
, we present two main results: i) a theorem on PCRs for the regular infinite-dimensional exponential family of statistical models; ii) a theorem on PCRs for a general dominated statistical model.

3.1
PCRs for the regular infinite-dimensional exponential family

It is useful to recall the definition and some basic properties of the infinite-dimensional exponential family. In general, classical results on exponential family may be extended to the infinite-dimensional setting through suitable arguments of convex analysis (
Bauschke et al. 2001
,
Bauschke and Combettes 2017
).

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\lambda
be a
\sigma
-finite measure on
(\mathbb{X},\mathscr{X})
,
\mathbb{B}
be a separable Banach space with dual
\mathbb{B}^{\ast}
, and
{}_{\mathbb{B}^{\ast}}\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathbb{B}}
be the pairing between
\mathbb{B}
and
\mathbb{B}^{\ast}
. Also, let
\Gamma
be a nonempty open subset of
\mathbb{B}^{\ast}
, and let
\beta:\mathbb{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{B}
be a measurable map. If the interior
\Lambda
of the convex hull of the support of
\lambda\circ\beta^{-1}
is nonempty and

\int_{\mathbb{X}}\exp\{_{\mathbb{B}^{\ast}}\langle\gamma,\beta_{x}\rangle_{\mathbb{B}}\}\lambda(\mathrm{d}x)<+\infty

(15)

holds for any
\gamma\in\Gamma
, then the regular infinite-dimensional exponential family is a statistical model defined through the family of
\lambda
-densities
\{\varphi(\cdot\,|\,\gamma)\}_{\gamma\in\Gamma}
, where

\varphi(x\,|\,\gamma)=\exp\{_{\mathbb{B}^{\ast}}\langle\gamma,\beta_{x}\rangle_{\mathbb{B}}-M_{\varphi}(\gamma)\}

(16)

with

M_{\varphi}(\gamma):=\log\left(\int_{\mathbb{X}}\exp\{_{\mathbb{B}^{\ast}}\langle\gamma,\beta_{x}\rangle_{\mathbb{B}}\}\lambda(\mathrm{d}x)\right).

(17)

Brown 1986
state that
M_{\varphi}
is a strictly convex function on
\Gamma
, lower semi-continuous on
\mathbb{B}^{\ast}
, of class
C^{\infty}(\Gamma)
and analytic. In addition,
Barndorff-Nielsen 78
implies that
M_{\varphi}
is steep (essentially smooth). Therefore, from
Brown 1986
it holds that

\mathcal{S}:\gamma\mapsto\nabla M_{\varphi}(\gamma)=\int_{\mathbb{X}}\beta_{x}\varphi(x\,|\,\gamma)\lambda(\mathrm{d}x)

(18)

defines a smooth injective map from
\Gamma
into
\mathbb{B}
, with dense range. Finally,
[
24
, Corollary 2.5]
entails the identifiability of the model characterized by the densities (
16
).

To introduce the setting of our theorem on PCR, it is useful to express the statistical model
\mu(\cdot\,|\,\cdot)
in terms of an infinite-dimensional exponential family. In this regard, we introduce a further measurable mapping
g:\Theta\rightarrow\Gamma
and write

\mu(\mathrm{d}x\,|\,\theta)=\varphi(x\,|\,g(\theta))\lambda(\mathrm{d}x)\ .

(19)

In the setting of (
19
), we observe that the identity (
7
) is satisfied with
\mathbb{S}=\mathbb{B}
,
\mathrm{d}_{\mathbb{S}}(s_{1},s_{2})=\|s_{1}-s_{2}\|_{\mathbb{B}}
and
\mathfrak{S}_{n}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=n^{-1}\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}\beta_{x_{i}}
. Therefore, we write

\hat{S}_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\beta_{\xi_{i}}\ .

(20)

Note that Equation (
19
) arises naturally from the assumption that the statistical model
\mu(\cdot\,|\,\cdot)
is dominated, which provides a family
\{f(\cdot\,|\,\theta)\}_{\theta\in\Theta}
of density functions. Accordingly, by assuming that
\mathbb{X}
is endowed of a richer metric structure, if
f(\cdot\,|\,\theta)>0
and
x\mapsto f(x\,|\,\theta)
is continuous for any
\theta\in\Theta
, we write

\log f(x\,|\,\theta)=\int_{\mathbb{X}}\log f(y\,|\,\theta)\delta_{x}(\mathrm{d}y).

The functions
g
and
\beta
then arise from the mapping
y\mapsto\log f(y\,|\,\theta)
and the measure
\delta_{x}
through (some sort of) integration-by-parts, if this is admitted, or through classical Fourier transformation arguments, such as the Plancherel formula.

According to
Ledoux and Talagrand 1991
, under the assumption

\mathds{E}\left[\|\beta_{\xi_{i}}\|_{\mathbb{B}}\right]=\int_{\mathbb{X}}\|\beta_{x}\|_{\mathbb{B}}\ \mu_{0}(\mathrm{d}x)=\int_{\mathbb{X}}\|\beta_{x}\|_{\mathbb{B}}\ \varphi(x\,|\,g(\theta_{0}))\lambda(\mathrm{d}x)<+\infty\ ,

(21)

we set

S_{0}=\int_{\mathbb{X}}\beta_{x}\mu_{0}(\mathrm{d}x)=\int_{\mathbb{X}}\beta_{x}\varphi(x\,|\,g(\theta_{0}))\lambda(\mathrm{d}x)

(22)

in the sense of Bochner integral, and conclude the strong law of large numbers, i.e.
\hat{S}_{n}\rightarrow S_{0}
holds
\mathds{P}
-a.s., as
n\rightarrow+\infty
. Now, we set
M(\theta)=M_{\varphi}(g(\theta))
and then define

\pi_{n}^{\ast}(\mathrm{d}\theta\,|\,b):=\frac{\exp\{n[_{\mathbb{B}^{\ast}}\!\langle g(\theta),b\rangle_{\mathbb{B}}-M(\theta)]\}\pi(\mathrm{d}\theta)}{\int_{\Theta}\exp\{n[\langle g(\tau),b\rangle_{\mathbb{B}}-M(\tau)]\}\pi(\mathrm{d}\tau)},

(23)

provided that

\int_{\Theta}\exp\{n\ _{\mathbb{B}^{\ast}}\!\langle g(\tau),b\rangle_{\mathbb{B}}\}\pi(\mathrm{d}\tau)<+\infty

(24)

for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
b\in\mathbb{B}
. We remark that (
24
) is a necessary assumption for the existence of the posterior distribution.
Now, we state the theorem on PCRs in the setting of infinite-dimensional exponential families; the proof is deferred to Appendix
A.3
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
p\geq 1
be a fixed number.
Let
\{\varphi(\cdot\,|\,\gamma)\}_{\gamma\in\Gamma}
be a regular infinite-dimensional exponential family according to
Definition

3.1
. Let
\Theta
be an open, connected subset of some separable Hilbert
space
\mathbb{H}
. Let
g:\Theta\rightarrow\Gamma
be a measurable mapping for which representation (
19
) is in force. For a fixed
\theta_{0}\in\Theta
, suppose that:

i)

(
21
) is valid;

ii)

(
24
) holds for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
b\in\mathbb{B}
;

iii)

\int_{\Theta}\|\theta\|^{ap}\pi(\mathrm{d}\theta)<+\infty
for some
a>1
;

iv)

there exists a sequence
\{\delta_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
of positive numbers for which (
9
) is valid for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
with
\mathbb{S}=\mathbb{B}
,
\mathrm{d}_{\mathbb{S}}(s_{1},s_{2})=\|s_{1}-s_{2}\|_{\mathbb{B}}
and suitable positive constants
L_{0}^{(n)}
.

Then, for the PCR
\epsilon_{n}
at
\theta_{0}
it holds

\displaystyle\epsilon_{n}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(\int_{\Theta}\|\theta-\theta_{0}\|_{\Theta}^{p}\pi_{n}^{\ast}(\mathrm{d}\theta\,|\,S_{0})\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}

(25)

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt+\|\theta_{0}\|_{\Theta}\ \mathds{P}\left[\hat{S}_{n}\not\in\mathcal{U}_{\delta_{n}}(S_{0})\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt\hskip 9.24994pt+\left(\mathds{E}\left[\int_{\Theta}\|\theta\|_{\Theta}^{ap}\pi_{n}^{\ast}(\mathrm{d}\theta\,|\,\hat{S}_{n})\right]\right)^{\frac{1}{ap}}\left(\mathds{P}\left[\hat{S}_{n}\not\in\mathcal{U}_{\delta_{n}}(S_{0})\right]\right)^{1-\frac{1}{ap}}

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt\hskip 9.24994pt\hskip 9.24994pt+L_{0}^{(n)}\mathds{E}[\|\hat{S}_{n}-S_{0}\|_{\mathbb{B}}]

where
\pi_{n}^{\ast}(\cdot\,|\,\cdot)
is given by (
23
),
S_{0}
and
\hat{S}_{n}
are as in (
22
) and (
20
), respectively, and
\mathcal{U}_{\delta_{n}}(S_{0}):=\{S\in\mathbb{S}\ |\ \|S_{0}-S\|_{\mathbb{B}}<\delta_{n}\}
.

Theorem
3.2
provides an implicit form for PCRs. That is, the large
n
asymptotic behaviour of the terms on the right-hand side of (
25
) must be further investigated to obtain a more explicit expression for the corresponding PCR. In this regard, it is useful to rewrite
\pi^{\ast}_{n}
in terms of the Kullback-Leibler divergence. That is, if
\mathcal{S}\circ g
is injective and
b
belongs to the range of
\mathcal{S}\circ g
, then

\pi_{n}^{\ast}(\mathrm{d}\theta\,|\,b)=\frac{\exp\{-n\mathsf{K}(\theta\,|\,\theta_{b})\}\pi(\mathrm{d}\theta)}{\int_{\Theta}\exp\{-n\mathsf{K}(\tau\,|\,\theta_{b})\}\pi(\mathrm{d}\tau)}

(26)

where
\theta_{b}=(\mathcal{S}\circ g)^{-1}(b)
and

\mathsf{K}(\theta\,|\,\theta^{\prime}):=\int_{\mathbb{X}}\left[\ln\left(\frac{f(x\,|\,\theta^{\prime})}{f(x\,|\,\theta)}\right)\right]f(x\,|\,\theta^{\prime})\mathrm{d}x\ .

(27)

denotes the Kullback-Leibler divergence. See Appendix
A.2
for the proof of Equation (
26
).
It is natural to expect that the main contribution to PCRs arises from the first and the fourth term on the right-hand side of (
25
), which provide general algebraic rates of convergence to zero. Hereafter, we investigate the large
n
asymptotic behaviour of the terms on the right-hand of (
25
). More explicit results in terms of PCRs will be presented in Section
4
with respect to the application of Theorem
3.2
in the context of the regular parametric model, the multinomial model, the finite-dimensional and the infinite-dimensional logistic-Gaussian model and the infinite-dimensional linear regression.

3.1.1
First term on the right-hand of (
25
)

We start by considering the large
n
asymptotic behaviour of the first term on the right-hand side of (
25
). In particular, from (
26
), we can rewrite this terms as

\displaystyle\int_{\Theta}\|\theta-\theta_{0}\|_{\Theta}^{p}\pi_{n}^{\ast}(\mathrm{d}\theta\,|\,S_{0})

(28)

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt=\frac{\int_{\Theta}\|\theta-\theta_{0}\|_{\Theta}^{p}\exp\{n[_{\mathbb{B}^{\ast}}\!\langle g(\theta),S_{0}\rangle_{\mathbb{B}}-M(\theta)]\}\pi(\mathrm{d}\theta)}{\int_{\Theta}\exp\{n[\langle g(\tau),S_{0}\rangle-M(\tau)]\}\pi(\mathrm{d}\tau)}

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt=\frac{\int_{\Theta}\|\theta-\theta_{0}\|_{\Theta}^{p}\exp\{-n\mathsf{K}(\theta\,|\,\theta_{0})\}\pi(\mathrm{d}\theta)}{\int_{\Theta}\exp\{-n\mathsf{K}(\theta\,|\,\theta_{0})\}\pi(\mathrm{d}\theta)}\ .

The last expression of (
28
) shows the ratio of two Laplace integrals, and therefore the Laplace method of approximating integrals can be applied. In the finite-dimensional setting, i.e.
\Theta\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the Laplace approximation method is well-known (
Breitung 1994
,
Wong 2001
), and it leads to the following proposition.

Proposition 3.3
.

In the case that
\Theta\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, assume that
\pi
has a continuous density
q
with respect to the Lebesgue measure, with
q(\theta_{0})>0
, and that
\theta\mapsto\mathsf{K}(\theta\,|\,\theta_{0})
is a
C^{2}
-function
with a strictly positive definite Hessian at
\theta_{0}
, which coincides with the Fisher information matrix
\mathrm{I}[\theta_{0}]
at
\theta_{0}
. Let
\int_{\Theta}|\theta|^{p}\pi(\mathrm{d}\theta)<+\infty
be fulfilled for some

p\geq 1
.
Finally, suppose that for any
\delta>0
there exists
c(\delta)>0
such that

\inf_{|\theta-\theta_{0}|\geq\delta}\mathsf{K}(\theta\,|\,\theta_{0})\geq c(\delta).

(29)

Then, for any
p>0
, there hold

\int_{\Theta}|\theta-\theta_{0}|^{p}e^{-n\mathsf{K}(\theta\,|\,\theta_{0})}\pi(\mathrm{d}\theta)\sim\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{2}{n}\right)^{\frac{d+p}{2}}\!\!\!\!\!\Gamma\left(\frac{d+p}{2}\right)

and

\frac{\int_{S_{d}(1)}\{\langle z,\mathrm{I}[\theta_{0}]^{-1}z\rangle\}^{p/2}\mathrm{d}S(z)}{\sqrt{\mathrm{det}[\mathrm{I}(\theta_{0})]}}\\
\int_{\Theta}e^{-n\mathsf{K}(\theta\,|\,\theta_{0})}\pi(\mathrm{d}\theta)\sim\left(\frac{2\pi}{n}\right)^{d/2}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathrm{det}[\mathrm{I}(\theta_{0})]}}

as
n\rightarrow+\infty
, where
S_{d}(1):=\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\ |\ \|z\|=1\}
,
\mathrm{d}S
denotes the surface measure and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
stands for the standard scalar product in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Thus, under these assumptions,

\int_{\Theta}\|\theta-\theta_{0}\|_{\Theta}^{p}\pi_{n}^{\ast}(\mathrm{d}\theta\,|\,S_{0})=O(n^{-p/2})

(30)

as
n\rightarrow+\infty
.

It is interesting to observe that the inequality (
29
) is a sort of strengthening of the so-called Shannon-Kolmogorov information inequality. See, e.g.,
Ferguson 1996
. In particular, because of (
29
), integrals on the whole
\Theta
can be reduced to integrals over balls centered at
\theta_{0}
, as integration over the complement of any such ball yields exponentially small
quantities with respect to
n
.

According to Proposition
3.3
, in the finite-dimensional setting the prior distribution does not affect the large
n
asymptotic behaviour of the first term on the right-hand side of (
25
). Differently from the standard finite-dimensional setting, the literature on the Laplace approximation method in the infinite-dimensional setting appears to be not well developed. That is, to the best of our knowledge, infinite-dimensional Laplace approximations are limited to the case in which the measure
\pi
is a Gaussian measure (
Albeverio and Steblovskaya 2019
,
Albeverio and Steblovskaya 1999
). Unfortunately, this literature does not cover the case in which the Hessian of the map
\theta\mapsto\mathsf{K}(\theta\,|\,\theta_{0})
at
\theta_{0}
is not coercive (uniformly elliptic), which is precisely the case of interest in our specific problem. The next proposition covers this critical gap; the proof is deferred to Appendix
A.4
. The proposition is of independent interest in the context of the classical Laplace method.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
\Theta
be a separable Hilbert space with scalar product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
, and let
\pi
be the non-degenerate Gaussian measure
\mathcal{N}(m,Q)
, with
m\in\Theta
and

Q
a trace-class operator. For fixed
\theta_{0}\in\Theta
,
assume that
\theta\mapsto\mathsf{K}(\theta\,|\,\theta_{0})
belongs to
C^{2+q}(\Theta)
for some
q\in(0,1]
, and that its Hessian at
\theta_{0}
, which coincides with the Fisher information operator

\mathrm{I}(\theta_{0})
at
\theta_{0}
, is a
compact self-adjoint linear operator from
\Theta
into itself, with trivial kernel. Suppose there exists an orthonormal Fourier basis
\{\mathbf{e}_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
of
\Theta
which diagonalizes simultaneously both
Q
and
\mathrm{I}(\theta_{0})
, so that

Q[\mathbf{e}_{k}]=\lambda_{k}\mathbf{e}_{k}\hskip 18.49988pt\hskip 18.49988pt\mathrm{I}(\theta_{0})[\mathbf{e}_{k}]=\gamma_{k}\mathbf{e}_{k}

(31)

are valid with two suitable sequences
\{\lambda_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
and
\{\gamma_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
of strictly positive numbers that go to zero as
k\rightarrow+\infty
, with
\{\lambda_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}\in\ell_{1}
. Finally, assume there exist two other Hilbert spaces
\mathbb{K}
and
\mathbb{V}
such that

i)

\mathbb{V}\subset\Theta\subset\mathbb{K}
with continuous, dense embeddings;

ii)

an interpolation inequality like

\|\theta\|_{\Theta}\lesssim\|\theta\|_{\mathbb{K}}^{1/r}\|\theta\|_{\mathbb{V}}^{1/s}

(32)

holds for any
\theta\in\mathbb{V}
with conjugate exponents
r,s>1
such that
r<1+q/2
;

iii)

for all
\theta\in\mathbb{V}
, the inequalities

\displaystyle\mathsf{K}(\theta\,|\,\theta_{0})

\displaystyle\geq\phi(\|\theta-\theta_{0}\|_{\mathbb{K}})

(33)

\displaystyle\langle\theta-\theta_{0},\mathrm{I}(\theta_{0})[\theta-\theta_{0}]\rangle

\displaystyle\gtrsim\|\theta-\theta_{0}\|_{\mathbb{K}}^{2}

(34)

are valid with some monotone non-decreasing function
\phi:[0,+\infty)\to[0,+\infty)
such that
\phi(x)=O(x^{2})
as
x\to 0^{+}
;

iv)

\pi(\mathbb{V})=1
and
\int_{\mathbb{V}}e^{t\|\theta\|_{\mathbb{V}}}\pi(\mathrm{d}\theta)<+\infty
for some
t>0
.

Then, as
n\rightarrow+\infty
, the following expansion

\int_{\Theta}\|\theta-\theta_{0}\|_{\Theta}^{2}\pi_{n}^{\ast}(\mathrm{d}\theta\,|\,S_{0})=O\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda_{k}}{n\lambda_{k}\gamma_{k}+1}\right)+O\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\omega_{k}^{2}}{(n\lambda_{k}\gamma_{k}+1)^{2}}\right)

(35)

holds with the sequence
\{\omega_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}\in\ell_{2}
given by
(\theta_{0}-m)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\omega_{k}\mathbf{e}_{k}
.

Remark 3.5
.

In the infinite-dimensional setting, the assumption (
29
) is, in general, too strong. Conditions (
33
)–(
34
), combined with the interpolation (
32
), constitute
a reasonable set of assumptions that allow a quite general treatment in the applications. It is worth noticing that (
29
), as well as (
33
), is expressed in the form of a lower
bound for
\mathsf{K}(\theta\,|\,\theta_{0})
. These bounds are conceptually opposite with respect to the so-called “prior mass condition” required in the standard theory (
Ghosal and van der Vaart 2000
), which
is usually proved by means of upper bounds for
\mathsf{K}(\theta\,|\,\theta_{0})
. See, e.g. the upper bounds for
\mathsf{K}(\theta\,|\,\theta_{0})
in Lemma 2.5 of
Ghosal and van der Vaart 2000
.

Remark 3.6
.

With respect to Proposition
3.3
, the statement of Proposition
3.4
is confined to the case
p=2
. There are no technical limitations for treating the more general case
p\neq 2
, though
p=2
yields to a more readable (conclusive) result.

Remark 3.7
.

Assumption (
31
) is not necessary to obtain PCRs. However, without this assumption, the resulting PCR would have a complicated form, which may be recovered from the proof. For example, let
\Theta_{N}
be the finite-dimensional subspace of
\Theta
obtained by the linear span of
\{\mathbf{e}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{e}_{N}\}
, let
Q_{N}
denote the
N\times N
matrix that represents the restriction of
Q
to
\Theta_{N}
, after projecting the range of such restriction again on
\Theta_{N}
, and let
\mathrm{I}_{N}(\theta_{0})
denote the
N\times N
matrix associated to the restriction just explained of the operator
\mathrm{I}(\theta_{0})
to
\Theta_{N}
. If
Q_{N}
and
\mathrm{I}_{N}(\theta_{0})

are non-singular, then the first term on the right-hand side of (
35
) can be replaced by

\lim_{N\to+\infty}\mathrm{Tr}\left[\left(n\mathrm{I}_{N}(\theta_{0})+Q_{N}^{-1}\right)^{-1}\right],

(36)

which is not as clear as the series
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}/(n\lambda_{k}\gamma_{k}+1)
. An analogous operation can be performed with respect to the second term on the right-hand side of (
35
).

Moreover, the above argument can be reinforced by resorting to some
trace inequalities
, as explained in
[
26
]
. In particular, we assume there exists another compact, self-adjoint operator

\mathrm{I}^{\ast}

such that
\mathrm{I}(\theta_{0})\geq\mathrm{I}^{\ast}
in the sense of quadratic forms, i.e.

\langle\theta,\mathrm{I}(\theta_{0})[\theta]\rangle\geq\langle\theta,\mathrm{I}^{\ast}[\theta]\rangle

for any
\theta\in\Theta
. Whence, upon denoting by
\mathrm{I}^{\ast}_{N}
the restriction of
\mathrm{I}^{\ast}
to
\Theta_{N}
as above, we have
\mathrm{I}_{N}(\theta_{0})\geq\mathrm{I}^{\ast}_{N}

and, consequently,
n\mathrm{I}_{N}(\theta_{0})+Q_{N}^{-1}\geq n\mathrm{I}^{\ast}_{N}+Q_{N}^{-1}
. By the Löwner–Heinz theorem, the mapping
t\mapsto-t^{-1}
is
operator monotone
, yielding that

\mathrm{Tr}\left[\left(n\mathrm{I}_{N}(\theta_{0})+Q_{N}^{-1}\right)^{-1}\right]\leq\mathrm{Tr}\left[\left(n\mathrm{I}^{\ast}_{N}+Q_{N}^{-1}\right)^{-1}\right]\ .

See again
[
26
]
for the details. Therefore, if the orthonormal Fourier basis
\{\mathbf{e}_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
of
\Theta
diagonalizes simultaneously both
Q
and
\mathrm{I}^{\ast}
(instead of

\mathrm{I}(\theta_{0})
), so that

Q[\mathbf{e}_{k}]=\lambda_{k}\mathbf{e}_{k}\hskip 18.49988pt\hskip 18.49988pt\mathrm{I}^{\ast}[\mathbf{e}_{k}]=\gamma_{k}^{\ast}\mathbf{e}_{k}

(37)

are valid with suitable strictly positive
\gamma_{k}^{\ast}
’s that go to zero as
k\rightarrow+\infty
, then by Proposition
3.4

\int_{\Theta}\|\theta-\theta_{0}\|_{\Theta}^{2}\pi_{n}^{\ast}(\mathrm{d}\theta\,|\,S_{0})\lesssim\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda_{k}}{n\lambda_{k}\gamma_{k}^{\ast}+1}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\omega_{k}^{2}}{(n\lambda_{k}\gamma_{k}^{\ast}+1)^{2}}\ .

(38)

Proposition
3.4
shows that the large
n
asymptotic behavior of the first term on the right-hand side of (
25
) is worse than
1/n
, which is the large
n
asymptotic behaviour obtained in Proposition
3.3
with
p=2
. For example, by taking the first term on the right-hand side of (
35
) into account,
if
\lambda_{k}\sim k^{-(1+a)}
and
\gamma_{k}\sim k^{-b}
as
k\rightarrow+\infty
, for some
a,b>0
, a straightforward calculation shows that

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{k^{-(1+a)}}{nk^{-(1+a+b)}+1}\sim n^{-{\frac{a}{1+a+b}}}

(39)

holds as
n\rightarrow+\infty
. As for the second term on the right-hand side of (
35
), it can be made identical to zero by choosing
m=\theta_{0}
, that is by means of centering the Gaussian prior at
\theta_{0}
. However, if
\lambda_{k}\sim k^{-(1+a)}
,
\gamma_{k}\sim k^{-b}
and
\omega^{2}_{k}\sim k^{-(1+c)}
as
k\rightarrow+\infty
, for some choice of
a,b,c>0
with
c<2(1+a+b)
, then

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{k^{-(1+c)}}{(nk^{-(1+a+b)}+1)^{2}}\sim n^{-{\frac{c}{1+a+b}}}

holds as
n\rightarrow+\infty
. Therefore, if
c<a
this second term is slower than the one in (
39
), whilst if
c>a
it is negligible with respect to that
term. Again on (
39
), it is interesting to notice what happens if the eigenvalues
\lambda_{k}
’s approach zero very rapidly, like

\lambda_{k}\sim e^{-k}
, for example. Another straightforward calculation shows that

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-k}}{ne^{-k}k^{-b}+1}\sim\frac{(\log n)^{b+1}}{n}

holds as
n\rightarrow+\infty
. A refinement of this argument entails that the large
n
asymptotic behavior of the right-hand side of (
35
) can be made arbitrarily close to the rate
1/n
, for example by choosing
\lambda_{k}\sim e^{-k^{r}}
and
\gamma_{k}\sim k^{-b}
and
\omega^{2}_{k}\sim k^{-(1+c)}
for some
r,b,c>0
, with arbitrarily large
r
. By recalling that the first term on the right-hand side of (
25
) coincides with the square root of the left-hand side of (
25
), this argument shows that the PCR is arbitrarily close to
1/\sqrt{n}
. It is reasonable to guess that the minimax (classical) risk should go to zero as fast as
1/\sqrt{n}
, though we are not aware of any result proving such a behaviour.

A merit of Proposition
3.4
is to show explicitly that, within the infinite-dimensional setting, PCRs are influenced by three quantities that do not appear in finite-dimensional setting of Proposition
3.3
: i) the rate of approach to zero of the sequence
\{\lambda_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
, which measures the “regularity of the prior”; ii) the rate of approach to zero of the sequence
\{\gamma_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
, which measures the “regularity of the model”; iii) the rate of approach to zero of the sequence
\{\omega_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
, which measures how close is
\theta_{0}
to
m
. Finally, we notice that the space
\mathbb{V}

3.1.2
Second and third term on the right-hand of (

Now, we consider the large

\displaystyle\mathds{P}\left[\hat{S}_{n}\not\in\mathcal{U}_{\delta_{n}}(S_{0})\right]=\mathds{P}\left[\|\hat{S}_{n}-S_{0}\|_{\mathbb{B}}\geq\delta_{n}\right]=\mathds{P}\left[\|\hat{S}_{n}-\mathds{E}[\hat{S}_{n}]\|_{\mathbb{B}}\geq\delta_{n}\right].

(40)

Note that the tail probability in (

Boucheron et al. 2013

25

The third term on the right-hand side of (
25
) includes the posterior moment
\mathds{E}[\int_{\Theta}\|\theta\|_{\Theta}^{ap}\pi_{n}^{\ast}(\mathrm{d}\theta\,|\,\hat{S}_{n})]=\mathds{E}[\int_{\Theta}\|\theta\|_{\Theta}^{ap}\pi_{n}(\mathrm{d}\theta\,|\,\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})]

\displaystyle\left(\int_{\Theta}\|\theta\|^{\rho ap}\pi(\mathrm{d}\theta)\right)^{1/\rho}\left(\int_{\mathbb{X}^{n}}\left[\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{n}f(x_{i}\,|\,\theta_{0})}{\rho_{n}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})}\right]^{\rho^{\prime}}\!\!\!\!\!\rho_{n}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\prod_{i=1}^{n}\lambda(\mathrm{d}x_{i})\right)^{1/\rho^{\prime}}

for conjugate exponents
\rho,\rho^{\prime}>1
, provided that
\int_{\Theta}\|\theta\|^{\rho ap}\pi(\mathrm{d}\theta)<+\infty
. It is useful to recall that the density function
\rho_{n}
has been defined in (
3

Accordingly, the second factor above coincides with the
\rho^{\prime}
-th moment of a martingale, since

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{X}^{n}}\left[\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{n}f(x_{i}\,|\,\theta_{0})}{\rho_{n}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})}\right]^{\rho^{\prime}}\!\!\!\!\!\rho_{n}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\prod_{i=1}^{n}\lambda(\mathrm{d}x_{i})=\mathds{E}\left[\left(\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{n}f(X_{i}\,|\,\theta_{0})}{\rho_{n}(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})}\right)^{\rho^{\prime}}\right]

and

\mathds{E}\left[\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{n+1}f(X_{i}\,|\,\theta_{0})}{\rho_{n+1}(X_{1},\dots,X_{n+1})}\,\Big|\,X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\right]=\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{n}f(X_{i}\,|\,\theta_{0})}{\rho_{n}(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})}\ .

At this stage, a possible resolutive strategy may rely on well-known bounds for moments of martingales (
Dharmadhikari et al. 1968

and the right-hand side typically goes to zero as

. Besides the obvious case in which

coincides with a separable Hilbert space, we refer to

Massart 2007
and
Massart and Rossignol 2013
for the case in which we have

\varepsilon_{n,p}(\mathbb{X},\mu_{0}):=\mathds{E}[\mathcal{W}_{p}^{(\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{X}))}(\mu_{0};\mathfrak{e}_{n}^{(\xi)})]
is the speed of mean Glivenko-Cantelli.

From Theorem
3.13
, we observe that if
V_{0}^{(n)}=\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{X})
makes
L_{0}^{(n)}
finite for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, then the expression on the right-hand side of (
56
) reduces to the first two terms. Similarly to Theorem
3.2
, Theorem
3.13
provides an implicit form for the PCR, thus requiring to further investigate the large
n
asymptotic behaviour of the terms on the right-hand side of (
56
). The posterior distribution appears in (
55
) and (
56
), meaning that further work is required to obtain more explicit terms. In general, it is possible to get rid of
\pi_{n}^{\ast}
in (

) and (

), thus reducing (

) and (

) to expressions that involve only the statistical model and the prior distribution. The first term on the right-hand side of (

3.3
and Proposition
3.4
. With regards to

, we recall from
Fournier and Guillin 2015
that, if
\int_{\mathbb{X}}|x|^{q}\mu_{0}(\mathrm{d}x)<+\infty
for some

, then

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{n,2}(\mathbb{X},\mu_{0})

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt\leq C(q,m)\left(\int_{\mathbb{X}}|x|^{q}\mu_{0}(\mathrm{d}x)\right)^{1/q}

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt\hskip 9.24994pt\times\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}n^{-1/4}+n^{-(q-2)/(2q)}&\mbox{if}\ m=1,2,3\ \mbox{and}\ q\neq 4\\
n^{-1/4}\sqrt{\log(1+n)}+n^{-(q-2)/(2q)}&\mbox{if}\ m=4\ \mbox{and}\ q\neq 4\\
n^{-1/m}+n^{-(q-2)/(2q)}&\mbox{if}\ m>4\ \mbox{and}\ q\neq m/(m-2)\end{array}\right.

). In the dimension

(

. With regards to

Bolley et al. 2007
. In particular, if
\int_{\mathbb{X}}|x|^{q}\mu_{0}(\mathrm{d}x)<+\infty
for some

\mathds{P}[\mathcal{W}_{2}(\mathfrak{e}_{n}^{(\xi)};\mu_{0})>t]\leq B(q,m)t^{-q}\times\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}n^{-q/4}&\mbox{if}\ q>4\\
n^{1-q/2}&\mbox{if}\ q\in[2,4)\end{array}\right.

for any

and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, with some positive constant

. Exponential bounds can be also obtained upon requiring that
\int_{\mathbb{X}}e^{\alpha|x|}\mu_{0}(\mathrm{d}x)<+\infty
for some

. See
Bolley et al. 2007
. In the next corollary we show that, under additional assumptions, similar bounds hold true for the other terms appearing on the right-hand side of (

and some

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}n^{-1/\beta}+L_{0}^{(n)}\varepsilon_{n,2}(\mathbb{X},\mu_{0})+2\|\theta_{0}\|K^{-q}B(q,m)n^{q(a-1/4)}

\displaystyle+C_{2}n^{[q(r-1)(a-1/4)]/r}\mathfrak{M}_{n,r}

From Corollary

4
Applications

4.1
Regular parametric models

Consider the case of dominated Bayesian statistical models with a finite-dimensional parameter
\theta\in\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Accordingly, we start by considering the set of Assumptions
3.12
,
with
d\in\mathbb{N}
, along with the hypotheses of Theorem
3.13
. In this setting, the Kullback-Leibler divergence
\mathsf{K}(\theta\,|\,\theta_{0})
is a
C^{2}
function, whose Hessian at
\theta_{0}
just coincides with the Fisher information
matrix at
\theta_{0}
. Whence,

\mathsf{K}(\theta\,|\,\theta_{0})=\frac{1}{2}\ ^{t}(\theta-\theta_{0})\mathrm{I}[\theta_{0}](\theta-\theta_{0})+o(|\theta-\theta_{0}|^{2})

(60)

as
\theta\rightarrow\theta_{0}
. Finally, we assume (
29
). Therefore, we can apply Proposition
3.3
to get

\mathcal{W}_{p}(\pi_{n}^{\ast}(\mathrm{d}\theta|\mu_{0}),\delta_{\theta_{0}})=\left(\frac{\int_{\Theta}|\theta-\theta_{0}|^{p}e^{-n\mathsf{K}(\theta\,|\,\theta_{0})}\pi(\mathrm{d}\theta)}{\int_{\Theta}e^{-n\mathsf{K}(\theta\,|\,\theta_{0})}\pi(\mathrm{d}\theta)}\right)^{1/p}=O\big(n^{-1/2}\big)

(61)

as
n\rightarrow+\infty
. Now, we discuss the behavior of the constant
L_{0}^{(n)}
, as
n
goes to infinity. First, we would like to stress that there are plenty of conditions that entail

[\mathfrak{C}_{2}(\pi_{n}^{\ast}(\cdot\,|\,\gamma))]^{2}\leq\frac{C(\gamma)}{n}

for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
and some positive constant
C(\gamma)
. We consider the double integral

\int_{\Theta}\int_{\mathbb{X}}\Big\|\mathrm{D}_{\theta}\frac{\nabla_{x}f(x\,|\,\theta)}{f(x\,|\,\theta)}\Big\|^{2}\gamma(\mathrm{d}x)\pi_{n}^{\ast}(\mathrm{d}\theta\,|\,\gamma)\ .

First, if
f(x\,|\,\theta)=\exp\{\langle\theta,T(x)\rangle-M(\theta)\}
, that is the model is an element of the exponential family in the canonical form, then we notice that

D_{\theta}\frac{\nabla_{x}f(x|\theta)}{f(x|\theta)}
reduces to a
d\times m
matrix whose entries are given by
\partial_{x_{j}}T_{i}(x)
, for
j=1,\dots,m
and
i=1,\dots,d
. Therefore, the study of the above double integral boils down to that of the much simpler expressions
\int_{\mathbb{X}}|\partial_{x_{j}}T_{i}(x)|^{2}\gamma(\mathrm{d}x)
, which are independent of
n
. More generally, we can reduce the problem by resorting to the Laplace method for approximating probability integrals, from which we have that

\int_{\Theta}\Big\|\mathrm{D}_{\theta}\frac{\nabla_{x}f(x\,|\,\theta)}{f(x\,|\,\theta)}\Big\|^{2}\pi_{n}^{\ast}(\mathrm{d}\theta|\gamma)\sim\Big\|\mathrm{D}_{\theta}\frac{\nabla_{x}f(x\,|\,\theta)}{f(x\,|\,\theta)}\Big\|^{2}\ _{\Big|\ \theta=\theta^{*}(\gamma)}

as
n\to+\infty
, where
\theta^{*}(\gamma)
denotes a maximum point of the mapping
\theta\mapsto\int_{\mathbb{X}}\log f(y|\theta)\gamma(\mathrm{d}y)
. Therefore, if the above right-hand side proves to be positive,
a reasonable plan to prove global boundedness of
L_{0}^{(n)}
with respect to
n
can be based on the following to steps. First, we check the validity of an inequality like

\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\int_{\Theta}\Big\|\mathrm{D}_{\theta}\frac{\nabla_{x}f(x\,|\,\theta)}{f(x|\theta)}\Big\|^{2}\pi_{n}^{\ast}(\mathrm{d}\theta\,|\,\gamma)\leq C\Big\|\mathrm{D}_{\theta}\frac{\nabla_{x}f(x\,|\,\theta)}{f(x\,|\,\theta)}\Big\|^{2}\ _{\Big|\ \theta=\theta^{*}(\gamma)}

for every
\gamma
belonging to a
\mathcal{W}_{2}^{(\mathcal{P}(\Theta))}
-neighborhood of
\mu_{0}
, where
C
is a positive constant possibly depending on the fixed neighborhood. Second, we prove global boundedness (for
\gamma

varying in the neighborhood) of the following integral

\int_{\mathbb{X}}\Big\|\mathrm{D}_{\theta}\frac{\nabla_{x}f(x\,|\,\theta)}{f(x\,|\,\theta)}\Big\|^{2}\ _{\Big|\ \theta=\theta^{*}(\gamma)}\gamma(\mathrm{d}x)<+\infty\ .

To fix ideas in a more concrete way, we consider the Gaussian case, where
\theta=(\mu,\Sigma)
and

f(x\,|\,\theta)=(2\pi)^{-m/2}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\textrm{det}(\Sigma)}}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}(x-\mu)^{t}\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu)\right\}\hskip 18.49988ptx\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\ .

Note that the mapping
\theta\mapsto\int_{\mathbb{X}}\log f(y\,|\,\theta)\gamma(\mathrm{d}y)
depends on
\gamma
only through its moments of order 1 and 2. Thus, the above strategy reduces to an ordinary
finite-dimensional maximization problem, very similar to the question of finding the maximum likelihood estimator. Finally, the last term in on the right-hand side of (
56
) can be treated as in Corollary
3.14
, by studying the asymptotic behavior of some posterior
r
-moment as in (
58
). We state two propositions that summarize the above considerations. The former result holds when Theorem
3.2
can be applied and gives the optimal rate, while the latter result ensues from Theorem
3.13
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Assume that there exist a separable Banach space
\mathbb{B}
with dual
\mathbb{B}^{\ast}
and two measurable maps
\beta:\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{B}
and
g:\Theta\to\mathbb{B}^{\ast}
for which (
19
) is in force.
If the assumptions of
Theorem

3.2
and
Propositions

3.3
,
3.8
and
3.10
are met, then as
n\rightarrow+\infty

\epsilon_{n}=O\big(n^{-1/2}\big),

which is the optimal rate.

Proposition 4.2
.

Assume that the model
\{f(\cdot|\cdot)\}_{\theta\in\Theta}
and the prior
\pi
satisfy

Assumption

3.12
along with
Propositions

3.3
,
3.8
and
3.10
. Then, as
n\to+\infty

\epsilon_{n}=O\big(\varepsilon_{n,2}(\mathbb{X},\mu_{0})\big),

which is the optimal rate, at least when
m=1
.

4.2
Multinomial models

Consider the case in which the observations, i.e. both the sequence
\{X_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
and the sequence
\{\xi_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
, take values in the finite set, say
\{a_{1},\dots,a_{N}\}
. It is easy to check that
\Theta
can be assumed to coincide with the interior of the
(N-1)
-dimensional simplex

\Delta_{N-1}:=\left\{\theta=(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{N-1})\in[0,1]^{N-1}\ \Big|\ \sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\theta_{i}\leq 1\right\}

and

\pi_{n}(\mathrm{d}\theta\,|\,x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=\frac{\left[\prod_{i=1}^{N}\theta_{i}^{\nu_{n,i}(x)}\right]\pi(\mathrm{d}\theta)}{\int_{\Delta_{N-1}}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{N}t_{i}^{\nu_{n,i}(x)}\right]\pi(\mathrm{d}t)}

where
\theta=(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{N-1})
,
t=(t_{1},\dots,t_{N-1})
,
\theta_{N}:=1-\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\theta_{i}
,
t_{N}:=1-\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}t_{i}
and

\nu_{n,i}(x):=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathds{1}\{x_{j}=a_{i}\}\hskip 18.49988pt\hskip 18.49988pti=1,\dots,N.

Of course, if we put
\mathbb{X}=\{a_{1},\dots,a_{N}\}
, we can not directly apply Theorem
3.13
. Nonetheless, we can resort
to a reinterpretation of the data, in terms of the frequencies
\nu_{n,i}
, that we now explain, that allows the use of our theorem. We consider

\pi_{n}^{\ast}(\mathrm{d}\theta\,|\,p):=\frac{\left[\prod_{i=1}^{N}\theta_{i}^{np_{i}}\right]\pi(\mathrm{d}\theta)}{\int_{\Delta_{N-1}}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{N}t_{i}^{np_{i}}\right]\pi(\mathrm{d}t)}

defined for
p=(p_{1},\dots,p_{N-1})\in\Delta_{N-1}
with the usual proviso that
p_{N}:=1-\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}p_{i}
. Whence,

\pi_{n}(\mathrm{d}\theta\,|\,x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=\pi_{n}^{\ast}\left(\mathrm{d}\theta\,\Big|\,\left(\frac{\nu_{n,1}(x)}{n},\dots,\frac{\nu_{n,N-1}(x)}{n}\right)\right)\ .

The problem of consistency, and the allied question of finding a PCR, can be now reformulated as follows. After fixing
\theta_{0}\in\Delta_{N-1}
, we consider the sequence
\{\xi_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
of i.i.d. random variables,
each taking values in
\{a_{1},\dots,a_{N}\}
, with
\mathds{P}[\xi_{1}=a_{i}]=\theta_{0,i}
, for
i=1,\dots,N
. An analogous version of Lemma
2.3
states that

\epsilon_{n}=\mathds{E}[\mathcal{W}_{2}^{(\mathcal{P}(\Theta))}(\pi_{n}(\mathrm{d}\theta\,|\,\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n});\delta_{\theta_{0}})]

provides a PCR at
\theta_{0}
. Now, we reformulate Theorem
3.13
as follows. First of all, we have that

\mathds{E}\left[\left|\left(\frac{\nu_{n,1}(\xi)}{n},\dots,\frac{\nu_{n,N-1}(\xi)}{n}\right)-\theta_{0}\right|\right]\leq\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\mathds{E}\left[\left|\frac{\nu_{n,1}(\xi)}{n}-\theta_{0,i}\right|^{2}\right]}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}

replaces the speed of the mean Glivenko-Cantelli convergence. Then, we have that

\mathsf{K}(\theta\,|\,\theta_{0})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\theta_{0,i}\log\left(\frac{\theta_{0,i}}{\theta_{i}}\right)\ .

The relation analogous to that in (
56
), which gives a PCR at
\theta_{0}
, reads as follows

\displaystyle\epsilon_{n}

\displaystyle=3\left(\frac{\int_{\Delta_{N-1}}|\theta-\theta_{0}|^{2}e^{-n\mathsf{K}(\theta|\theta_{0})}\pi(\mathrm{d}\theta)}{\int_{\Theta}e^{-n\mathsf{K}(\theta\,|\,\theta_{0})}\pi(\mathrm{d}\theta)}\right)^{1/2}

(62)

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt+L_{0}^{(n)}(\delta_{n})\mathds{E}\left[\left|\left(\frac{\nu_{n,1}(\xi)}{n},\dots,\frac{\nu_{n,N-1}(\xi)}{n}\right)-\theta_{0}\right|\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt+2|\theta_{0}|\mathds{P}\left[\left|\left(\frac{\nu_{n,1}(\xi)}{n},\dots,\frac{\nu_{n,N-1}(\xi)}{n}\right)-\theta_{0}\right|>\delta_{n}\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt+\mathds{E}\Bigg[\left(2\frac{\int_{\Delta_{N-1}}|\theta|^{2}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{N}\theta_{i}^{\nu_{n,i}(\xi)}\right]\pi(\mathrm{d}\theta)}{\int_{\Delta_{N-1}}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{N}\theta_{i}^{\nu_{n,i}(\xi)}\right]\pi(\mathrm{d}\theta)}\right)^{1/2}\times

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt\hskip 9.24994pt\times\mathds{1}\left\{\left|\left(\frac{\nu_{n,1}(\xi)}{n},\dots,\frac{\nu_{n,N-1}(\xi)}{n}\right)-\theta_{0}\right|>\delta_{n}\right\}\Bigg]

where
\{\delta_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
provides a sequence of positive numbers and
L_{0}^{(n)}
is defined as follows

L_{0}^{(n)}:=n\sup_{|p-\theta_{0}|\leq\delta_{n}}[\mathfrak{C}_{2}(\pi_{n}^{\ast}(\cdot\,|\,p))]^{2}\left(\int_{\Delta_{N-1}}\Big|\nabla_{\theta}\nabla_{p}\mathsf{K}(\theta\,|\,p)\Big|^{2}\pi_{n}^{\ast}(\mathrm{d}\theta\,|\,p)\right)^{1/2}\ .

We show that the PCR in (
62
) reduces to a simpler expression. Indeed, the first term on the right-hand side of (
62
) is similar to the one already studied in the previous section. By resorting to the same theorems from
Breitung 1994
, recalling that the mapping
\theta\mapsto\mathsf{K}(\theta\,|\,\theta_{0})
is minimum when

\theta=\theta_{0}
, we get, as
n\to+\infty
,

\left(\frac{\int_{\Delta_{N-1}}|\theta-\theta_{0}|^{2}e^{-n\mathsf{K}(\theta\,|\,\theta_{0})}\pi(\mathrm{d}\theta)}{\int_{\Delta_{N-1}}e^{-n\mathsf{K}(\theta\,|\,\theta_{0})}\pi(\mathrm{d}\theta)}\right)^{1/2}=O\big(n^{-1/2}\big)

provided that
\pi
has full support. As for the second terms on the right-hand side of (
62
), we have already shown that the expectation is controlled by
1/\sqrt{n}
. Apropos of the constant

L_{0}^{(n)}(\delta_{n})
, we can easily show that it is bounded, at least whenever
\theta_{0}
is fixed in the interior of
\Delta_{N-1}
. In fact,
\delta_{n}
can be chosen equal to any positive constant
\delta
less than the
distance between
\theta_{0}
and the boundary of
\Delta_{N-1}
. In particular, by exploiting the convexity of the mapping
\theta\mapsto\mathsf{K}(\theta\,|\,p)
, we can resort to Proposition
3.10
, upon assuming more regularity on the prior distribution
\pi
, in order to obtain
[\mathfrak{C}_{2}(\pi_{n}^{\ast}(\cdot\,|\,p))]^{2}\leq C(\delta)/n
, with a positive constant
C(\delta)
which is independent
of
p
. Then, by means of a direct computation, under the above conditions on
\theta_{0}
and
\delta
we can show that the integral

\int_{\Delta_{N-1}}\Big|\nabla_{\theta}\nabla_{p}\mathsf{K}(\theta\,|\,p)\Big|^{2}\pi_{n}^{\ast}(\mathrm{d}\theta\,|\,p)

can be bounded uniformly in
n
. To conclude the analysis of the terms on the right-hand side of (
62
), we only need to exploit the boundedness of
|\theta|
, as
\theta
varies in
\Delta_{N-1}
,
to show that the third and the fourth terms are both bounded by a multiple of

\mathds{P}\left[\left|\left(\frac{\nu_{n,1}(\xi)}{n},\dots,\frac{\nu_{n,N-1}(\xi)}{n}\right)-\theta_{0}\right|>\delta_{n}\right]\ .

Thus, if
\theta_{0}
is in the interior of
\Delta_{N-1}
and
\delta_{n}=\delta
, for the same
\delta
as above, it is well-known from the theory of large deviations that this probability goes to zero exponentially fast. See
Dembo and Zeitouni 1998
for a detailed account. To conclude, we state a proposition that summarizes the above considerations.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
N\geq 2
be an integer. Let
\pi
be a prior on
\Delta_{N-1}
. If
\pi
has a density
q
(with respect to the Lebesgue measure) such that
q\in\mathrm{C}^{1}(\overline{\Delta_{N-1}})
and
q(\theta)=0
for any
\theta\in\partial\Delta_{N-1}
, then as
n\rightarrow+\infty

\epsilon_{n}=O\big(n^{-1/2}\big),

which is the optimal rate.

4.3
Finite-dimensional logistic-Gaussian model

Consider a class of dominated statistical models specified by density functions of the form

f(x\,|\,\theta)=\frac{e^{\theta\cdot\Gamma_{N}(x)}}{\int_{0}^{1}e^{\theta\cdot\Gamma_{N}(x)}\lambda(\mathrm{d}y)}\hskip 18.49988ptx\in\mathbb{X},\theta\in\Theta

(63)

where, for simplicity, we have fixed
N\in\mathbb{N}
,
\Theta=\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
\mathbb{X}=[0,1]
,
\mathscr{X}=\mathscr{B}([0,1])
,
\lambda=\mathcal{L}^{1}_{[0,1]}
, that is the one-dimensional Lebesgue measure restricted to [0,1], and

\Gamma_{N}(x):=\left(\sin\pi x,\sin 2\pi x,\dots,\sin N\pi x\right)\ .

Of course, the expression
\theta\cdot\Gamma_{N}(x)
represents a Fourier polynomial and, for sufficiently large
N
, can approximate very well any smooth function, in various norm. This model has been studied
in connection with the problem of density estimation (
Crain 1976a
,
Crain 1976b
,
Lenk 1988
,
Lenk 1991
), essentially as a toy model. In the following section, we will analyze its infinite dimensional generalization, which is a more flexible statistical model, even if more complex from a mathematical point of view.

To apply Theorem
3.2
, we start by fixing
\theta_{0}\in\Theta
, so that
\mu_{0}(\mathrm{d}x)=f(x|\theta_{0})\mathrm{d}x
, where
x\mapsto f(x|\theta_{0})
is a continuous and bounded density function on
[0,1]
. Then, we let
\{\xi_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
be a sequence of independent random variables identically distributed as the probability law
\mu_{0}
. The model (
63
) satisfies Definition
3.1
with
\mathbb{B}=\Theta
,
\mathbb{B}^{\ast}=\Theta
(by Riesz’s representation theorem) and
\Gamma=\mathbb{B}^{\ast}
, with
{}_{\mathbb{B}^{\ast}}\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathbb{B}}
being identified with the standard (Euclidean) scalar product of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. The function
\beta
coincides with
\Gamma_{N}(x)
, while
g
is the identity function. Finally, we have that

M(\theta)=\log\left(\int_{0}^{1}e^{\theta\cdot\Gamma_{N}(x)}\mathrm{d}x\right)

which proves to be a convex function, steep, of class
\mathrm{C}^{\infty}(\Theta)
and analytic. Therefore, we have a regular exponential family, in canonical form. As for the prior distribution, besides the multivariate (non-degenerate) Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(m,Q)
, with
m\in\Theta
and
Q
being a symmetric and positive-definite
N\times N
matrix, any other distribution of log-concave form like
\pi(\mathrm{d}\theta)\propto\exp\{-U(\theta)\}\mathrm{d}\theta
fits our assumptions, provided that
U
is of class
\mathrm{C}^{2}(\Theta)
and strongly convex.

Coming back to the application of Theorem
3.2
, we check the validity of the assumptions. First,
|\Gamma_{N}(x)|\leq\sqrt{N}
for all
x\in[0,1]
, so that (
21
) is in force. Then, (
24
) and
\int_{\Theta}\|\theta\|^{ap}\pi(\mathrm{d}\theta)<+\infty
for some
a>1
hold, because of the assumptions on the prior distribution. Thus, the bound (
25
) provides the desired PCR, so that we proceed further by analyzing the various terms as in Section
3.1
. Since

\mathrm{I}(\theta_{0})=\mathrm{Hess}[M](\theta_{0})=\mathsf{Cov}_{\theta_{0}}(\Gamma_{N}(\xi_{1}))

is strictly positive definite, we can apply Proposition
3.3
. We conclude that the first term on the right-hand side of (
25
) goes to zero as
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}
. Then, the boundedness condition
|\Gamma_{N}(x)|\leq\sqrt{N}
for all
x\in[0,1]
entails that the second and the third terms on the right-hand side of (
25
) go to zero exponentially fast by means of classical concentration inequalities, like Bernstein’s inequality for instance (
Boucheron et al. 2013
,
Dembo and Zeitouni 1998
). Finally, we consider the last term on the right-hand side of (
25
). In particular, by Jensen’s inequality

\mathds{E}[\|\hat{S}_{n}-S_{0}\|_{\mathbb{B}}]\leq\left(\mathds{E}[\|\hat{S}_{n}-S_{0}\|_{\mathbb{B}}^{2}]\right)^{1/2}=O\big(n^{-1/2}\big)\ ,

as

, and the square of the weighted Poincaré-Wirtinger constant is asymptotic to

4.4
Infinite-dimensional logistic-Gaussian model

Consider a class of dominated statistical models specified by density functions of the form

The infinite-dimensional logistic-Gaussian model is typically considered in connection with the fundamental problem of density estimation (

4.5
Infinite-dimensional linear regression

Consider a statistical model that arises from the popular linear regression. The observed data are the collection of pairs
(u_{1},v_{1}),\dots,(u_{n},v_{n})
, such that: i) the

vary in an interval

, and are modeled as i.i.d. random variables, say

\mathbb{R}
, and are modeled as i.i.d. random variables

(77)

where

are i.i.d. random variables with Normal

the statistical model is characterized by probability densities

on

(78)

The space

, which is continuously embedded in

On the other hand, from the Bayesian point of view, upon fixing a prior distribution

and resorting to the Bayes formula, the posterior takes on the form

\displaystyle\pi_{n}(\mathrm{d}\theta|x_{1},\dots,x_{n})

\displaystyle=\pi_{n}(\mathrm{d}\theta|(u_{1},v_{1})\dots,(u_{n},v_{n}))

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}[\theta(u_{i})-v_{i}]^{2}\right\}\pi(\mathrm{d}\theta)}{\int_{\Theta}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}[\tau(u_{i})-v_{i}]^{2}\right\}\pi(\mathrm{d}\tau)}\ .

Whence, for any probability measure

\pi_{n}^{\ast}(\mathrm{d}\theta|\gamma)=\frac{\exp\left\{-\frac{n}{2\sigma^{2}}\int_{[a,b]\times\mathbb{R}}[\theta(u)-v]^{2}\gamma(\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}v)\right\}\pi(\mathrm{d}\theta)}{\int_{\Theta}\exp\left\{-\frac{n}{2\sigma^{2}}\int_{[a,b]\times\mathbb{R}}[\tau(u)-v]^{2}\gamma(\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}v)\right\}\pi(\mathrm{d}\tau)}\ .

Lastly, as for the Kullback-Leibler divergence, a straightforward computation yields

\mathsf{K}(\theta|\theta_{0})=\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\int_{a}^{b}[\theta(u)-\theta_{0}(u)]^{2}h(u)\mathrm{d}u\ .

This statistical model is particularly versatile with respect to our theory, because it can be studied as either an infinite-dimensional exponential family or by means of

and Corollary

\displaystyle(\theta(u)-v)^{2}

\displaystyle=-\int_{a}^{b}\int_{\mathbb{R}}[\Delta((\theta(x)-y)^{2})]\mathcal{G}(x,y;u,v)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y

stands for the Green function of the set

\theta

is sufficiently large, then

is still a function, which can be
set equal to

. On the other hand,

\beta
in the theory of exponential families.

As for the assumptions of Theorem

In connection with the model (

5
Discussion

We conclude our work by discussing some directions for future research. The flexibility of the Wasserstein distance is promising when considering non-regular Bayesian statistical models, even in a finite-dimensional setting. One may consider the problem of dealing with dominated statistical models that have moving supports, i.e. supports that depend on
\theta
. The prototypical example is the family of Pareto distributions, which is characterized by a density function

f(x\,|\,\alpha,x_{0})=\frac{\alpha x_{0}^{\alpha}}{x^{1+\alpha}}\mathds{1}\{x\geq x_{0}\},

where
\theta=(\alpha,x_{0})\in(0,+\infty)^{2}
. Under this model, by rewriting the posterior distribution to obtain the representation (
7
), we observe that the empirical distribution can be replaced by the minimum of the observations, which is the maximum likelihood estimator. In doing this, we expect to parallel the proof of Theorem
3.13
, with the minimum playing the role of the sufficient statistic, instead of the empirical measure. In particular, we expect that the term
\varepsilon_{n,p}(\mathbb{X},\mu_{0})
should be replaced by other rates typically involved in limit theorems of order statistics. The theoretical framework for such an extension of our results is developed in the work of
Dolera and Mainini 2020b
, where it is shown how the continuity equation yields a specific boundary-value problem of Neumann type.

As for the infinite-dimensional setting covered by Theorem
3.2
and Theorem
3.13
, an interesting development of our approach to PCRs is represented by the possibility of finding, for general statistical models, explicit sufficient statistics belonging to Banach spaces of functions. To be more precise, we hint at a constructive version of the well-known Fisher-Neyman factorization lemma. This result would pave the way for a suitable rewriting of the statistical model, that allows for the use of our approach. By way of example, one may consider the identity
\log f(x|\theta)=\int_{\mathbb{X}}\log f(y|\theta)\delta_{x}(\mathrm{d}y)
, and exploit an integration-by-part formula to obtain an identity like (
19
), with respect to a suitable measure
\lambda
on
(\mathbb{X},\mathscr{X})
. Such a procedure is at the basis for the development of our approach to PCRs in the context of popular nonparametric models, not considered in this paper, such as the Dirichlet process mixture model (
Ghosal and van der Vaart 2000
), the random histograms (
Ghosal and van der Vaart 2000
) and Pólya trees (
Ghosal and van der Vaart 2000
).

Another promising line of research consists in extending Theorem
3.13
to metric measure spaces. The theoretical ground for this development may be found in the seminal works of
Gigli 2009
,
Gigli and Ohta 2012
,
Ambrosio et al. 2008
,
Otto and Villani 2000
and
von Renesse and Sturm 2009
. In such a context, it is of interest the treatment of the relative entropy-functional in the Wasserstein space. It is well-known that the Hessian of the relative entropy-functional, i.e. the Kullback-Leibler divergence, generalizes by using techniques from infinite-dimensional Riemannian geometry (
Otto and Villani 2000
). From the statistical side, the possibility of choosing a parameter space that coincides with a space of measures allows to re-consider, from a different point of view, popular Bayesian statistical models such as Dirichlet process mixture models, which are defined as

f(x\,|\,\mathfrak{p})=\int\tau(x\,|\,y)\mathfrak{p}(\mathrm{d}y)

where
\tau
is a kernel parameterized by
y
, and
\mathfrak{p}
is a random probability measure with a Dirichlet process prior (
Ferguson 1973
). The goal should be that of considering PCRs relative to Wasserstein neighborhoods of a given true distribution, say
\mathfrak{p}_{0}
. This approach is again different from the nonparametric framework considered in
Berthet and Niels-Weed 2021
, and seems still unexplored.
