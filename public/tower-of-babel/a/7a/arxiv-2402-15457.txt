Introduction

The concept of the asymptotic cut-off phenomenon in the total variation distance was coined by D. Aldous and P. Diaconis in the
context of Markov chains models of card-shuffling, Ehrenfests’ urn and random transpositions, see
[
1
,
35
]
.
Very generally, it refers to the asymptotically abrupt dynamical transition (it is also known as drastic/sharp/step/switch convergence) of the current state out of equilibrium of the Markov chain to its dynamic equilibrium in a cut-off window around the so-called mixing time as a function of the deck size.
In other words, the distance to equilibrium behaves as an approximate step function that remains close to its maximal value (diameter) for a while, and then suddenly drops to zero as the running time parameter reaches a critical threshold, which corresponds to the mixing time.
Since the state space of card-shuffling Markov chains models is finite, the preceding convergence to equilibrium is naturally measured in terms of the total variation distance.
This dynamical phase transition has been observed in a broad spectrum of random models.
For instance, in the discrete state space the asymptotic cut-off phenomenon has been showed for
shuffling cards Markov dynamics
[
1
,
35
]
, random walks on the
n
-dimensional hypercube
[
36
,
61
]
, birth and death Markov chains
[
20
,

]
,
sparse Markov chains
[

[

]
,

[

,

, SSEP dynamics
[

, TASEP dynamics
[

]
, random walks in random regular graphs
[

]
, mean-zero field zero-range process
[
68
]
, averaging processes
[

]
, and
sampling chains
[

,

]
.
For more general Markov processes taking values in

In the present manuscript, we are interested in the asymptotic cut-off phenomenon with complexity positive parameter
\varepsilon
in the weak noise limit
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
for
the uniquely ergodic random dynamics
X^{\varepsilon,x}:=(X^{\varepsilon}_{t}(x))_{t\geqslant 0}
given by the unique strong solution of the stochastic differential equation (for short SDE) with

multiplicative non-linear noise

(1.1)

\mathrm{d}X^{\varepsilon}_{t}(x)=(\mathfrak{b}-\mathfrak{a}X^{\varepsilon}_{t}(x))\mathrm{d}t+\varepsilon\sqrt{X^{\varepsilon}_{t}(x)}\mathrm{d}B_{t},\quad X^{\varepsilon}_{0}(x)=x,\quad t\geqslant 0,\,x\geqslant 0,

where
B:=(B_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
is a one dimensional standard Brownian motion, and
\mathfrak{a}
and
\mathfrak{b}
are positive constants.
The Fokker–Planck equation associated to (
1.1
) is analyzed in 1951 by W. Feller in
[
40
]
to study singular diffusion problems. It is then referred as Feller’s square root process.
The model (
1.1
) is introduced in 1985 by
J. Cox, J. Ingersoll and
S. Ross to describe the evolution of instantaneous interest rate at time
t
,
X^{\varepsilon}_{t}(x)
, with mean reversion
of the interest rate towards the long-term value
\nicefrac{{\mathfrak{b}}}{{\mathfrak{a}}}
with the speed of adjustment
\mathfrak{a}
.
Nowadays, it is known as a Cox–Ingersoll–Ross (CIR) process, see
[
32
]
.
In Section 1.5 in
[
27
]

it is shown that cut-off phenomenon holds true, as the number of sampling increases, for
a particular CIR model (Bessel process), which can be written as a square of an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process.
Furthermore, singular SDEs of the type (
1.1
)
emerge in the model of evolution of chemical reactions and population dynamics as diffusion approximations of res-caled Markov jump processes, see for instance
[
5
,
39
,
75
]
and the references therein.
The stochastic process (
1.1
) belongs to the class of continuous-state branching processes with immigration (a.k.a. CBI processes).
The so-called fluid limit of (
1.1
) (i.e. the deterministic ordinary differential equation obtained as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
) may be interpreted as a mean-field approximation and
the stochastic term
\varepsilon\sqrt{X^{\varepsilon}_{t}(x)}\mathrm{d}B_{t}

may be interpreted as
the demographic or internal noise.
The study of this case will be relevant in the understanding of the cut-off phenomenon for a wide class of SDEs with non-linear coercive deterministic component and driven by multiplicative noise. Moreover, it also shows the complexity that brings to the problem the multiplicative noise.

By Theorem 5.1 in
[
43
]
or Theorem 3.2 in
[
47
]
we have that (
1.1
) has a unique strong solution in a filtered complete probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0},\mathbb{P})
satisfying the usual conditions
where the standard Brownian motion
B
is defined, and denote by
\mathbb{E}
the expectation with respect to the probability measure
\mathbb{P}
.
We stress that the density of the marginal
X^{\varepsilon}_{t}(x)
can be
expressed in terms of Bessel functions,
see
[
32
,
40
]
.
Since we are interested in the limit
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
,
without loss of generality we can assume that the so-called ergodic Feller regime holds true, i.e,
\varepsilon\in(0,\sqrt{2\mathfrak{b}})
.
In the aforementioned ergodic Feller regime, the random dynamics given by (
1.1
) is uniquely ergodic and its invariant probability measure
\mu^{\varepsilon}
possesses a Gamma distribution with parameters

\nicefrac{{2\mathfrak{b}}}{{\varepsilon^{2}}}
and
\nicefrac{{2\mathfrak{a}}}{{\varepsilon^{2}}}
.
Since (
1.1
) belongs to the class of affine diffusions, the marginal
X^{\varepsilon}_{t}(x)
converges in distribution to
X^{\varepsilon}_{\infty}
as
t
tends to infinity. Moreover, the law of
X^{\varepsilon}_{\infty}
does not depend on the initial datum
x
. The preceding convergence can be improved to be valid in the total variation distance.
For more details, we refer to
[
32
,
40
,
42
,
49
]
.

We stress that in general, convergence in distribution does not imply convergence in total variation distance. For instance, the celebrated
DeMoivre–Laplace Central Limit Theorem does not hold true in the total variation distance, nevertheless, it holds true for other distances such as the
Wasserstein distance, see Theorem 1 in
[
24
]
.
Even for absolutely continuous (smooth) sequence of distributions, the convergence in distribution does not imply convergence in total variation distance, see for instance Fig.1 in
[
30
]
or Lemma 1.17 in
[
11
]
.
In fact, the convergence in the total variation distance is very strong and hence it cannot be expected
from just the convergence in distribution without additional structure.

Let
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of random variables defined in a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
.
In addition, let
X
be a random variable defined in
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
.
Assume that
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the distribution of the random variable
X_{n}
is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}
and its density
f_{n}
is unimodal.
Moreover, assume that the random variable
X
has also a unimodal density
f
with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}
.
Then Theorem 2.3 (Ibragimov)
in
[
33
]
or Lemma 3 in
[
46
]
yield that the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

The random sequence
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges in distribution to
X
as
n
tends to infinity.

(ii)

The random sequence
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges in the total variation distance to
X
as
n
tends to infinity.

Nevertheless, showing that the distribution of a random variable has a unimodal density is in general a difficult task, see Khinchin’s Theorem in Theorem 4.5.1
of
[
66
]
.
Without assuming that for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
f_{n}
and
f
are unimodal,
in
[
25
,
80
]
there are sufficient conditions on
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
so that (i) is equivalent to (ii). However, such conditions requires to verify that the sequence
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is equicontinuous, which typically is a challenging task.

In this manuscript,
we also present a Parseval–Plancherel–Fourier based approach to total variation convergence,
which may be of independent interest,
see Proposition
A.3
and Proposition
A.5
in Appendix
A
.
By imposing (a) point-wise convergence of the characteristic functions, (b) integrability of the characteristic functions, and (c) uniform integrability of the characteristic functions, we then have convergence in total variation distance for the corresponding distributions.
We note that (a) is a minimal requirement to study convergence in total variation distance.
To verify (b) and (c) we point out that it is not needed explicit formulas for the characteristic functions, only
(suitable) uppers bounds for the modulus of them are required.
We remark that there are sequences of random variables for which convergence in total variation distance holds true, nevertheless, (b) and (c) are not valid, see Remark
A.7
in Appendix
A
.

Bearing all this in mind, we are interested in the quantitative behavior of the convergence to equilibrium
of the out of equilibrium marginals of the SDE (
1.1
) in the total variation distance and in the Wasserstein distance.
The quantitative analysis of the convergence to equilibrium in a suitable distance or divergence of interest for ergodic Markov processes, in particular SDEs, is typically a difficult task.
By an analytical approach
it typically requires a refined knowledge of the spectrum (roughly speaking, eigenvalues and their eigenfunctions) for the so-called generator of the SDE, which is an infinitely dimensional operator. In addition, Poincaré type inequalities, Log-Sobolev type inequalities, relative entropy inequalities, Lyapunov functions, Kantorovich potentials estimates and/or
Talagrand’s transportation inequalities
are needed to hold true in order to bound the carré du champ in the Bakry–Émery theory, see
[
59
]
.
We note that for short times, the law of the random dynamics starting from a deterministic initial datum is very far from the invariant probability measure and hence estimates using the aforementioned techniques are far to be optimal.
Alternative approaches are offered by the so-called Stein’s method
[
31
,
54
,
62
,
71
]
and probabilistic couplings methods
[
64
]
. Nevertheless, in general only upper bounds for such convergence can be obtained. In addition, coupling techniques for systems driven by multiplicative noises are more involved comparing with systems driven by additive noises as the SDEs aforementioned in the introduction.

Historically, the exponential quantitative analysis of the convergence to equilibrium for Markov processes provides upper bounds of the type

Ce^{-\delta t}
for a suitable distance between the law of the dynamics (marginal) at time
t
and its corresponding invariant probability measure.
The positive
ergodicity constants
(clearly not unique)
C
and
\delta
may depend on the complexity parameter (here denoted by
\varepsilon
), the initial datum of the dynamics and/or the dimension of the underlying state space.
Therefore, generically, it is a difficult task to carry out the dependence with respect to the complexity parameter, initial datum and/or dimension, see
[
69
]
. Numerical computations of ergodicity constants for stochastic dynamics have been studied in
[
63
]
.
The constant
-\delta
is related with the spectral gap of the generator of the Markov process.
As a consequence, the computation and/or estimation of such constants are typically done case by case.
In addition, when the distance can be written in dual-formulation (duality), it is possible to obtain lower bounds by a cunning choice of a distinguish statistic (observable), see for instance the Wilson method for Markov chains given in Proposition 7.7 of
[
61
]
.
Nevertheless, in general, we stress that lower bounds for the convergence to equilibrium are typically out of reach.
Recently,
a criterion to obtain lower bounds on the rate of convergence to equilibrium in the so-called

f
-variation of a continuous-time ergodic Markov process is establishing
in
[
28
]
.

The objective of this manuscript is two-fold.

(1)

Provide a robust technique to show convergence to equilibrium in the total variation distance and in the Wasserstein distance, see Subsection
1.1
for the precise definition of the distances.
We remark that the Parseval–Plancherel–Fourier based approach used in this paper to study the asymptotic cut-off phenomenon in total variation distance for (
1.1
) is robust and it
is not based on explicit formulas for its marginals and/or the aforementioned analytical methods or functional inequalities.
It may be extended to more general stochastic processes driven by general perturbations under suitable information of the characteristic functions, i.e., point-wise convergence, integrability, and uniform integrability when
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
of the spatial Fourier transforms are valid, see Proposition
A.3
and Proposition
A.5
in Appendix
A
.
In particular, after identifying the Central Limit Theorem when
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
for the invariant probability measure, that is, after a suitable shift and scaling, we obtain
the strong notion of asymptotic profile cut-off phenomenon when
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
with
explicit cut-off time, explicit time window, and explicit profile function in terms of the classical error function, see Theorem
1.4
below.
For the Wasserstein distance, the same holds true whenever uniform integrability when
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
of the corresponding
p
-th moments is valid. In addition, the profile function has an explicit exponential shape,
see Theorem
1.10
below.
We point out that
explicit profiles are typically out of reach and that
not all one-dimensional stable stochastic systems exhibit cut-off phenomenon, see
Theorem 1.5 (Gradual convergence) in
[
8
]
.

(2)

Provide explicit asymptotics of the so-called mixing times in the total variation distance and in the Wasserstein distance, see Corollary
1.8
and Corollary
1.12
below.
We emphasize that the asymptotics of the mixing times do not rely on explicit knowledge of optimal ergodicity constants aforementioned, which are generically a difficult task.

1.1.
Preliminaries and asymptotic cut-off phenomenon

In this section, we review the necessary background about Wasserstein distance and the total variation distance.
We point out that the weak convergence in the space of probability measures with finite
p
-moment can be metrized by the Wasserstein distance.
In addition, it is a natural way to compare the discrepancy between the laws of two random elements, even for degenerate cases, where one random element is derived from the other by a small perturbation.
Nowadays, it is an important and well-studied mathematical concept in its own right.
Moreover, it has been ubiquitous in applications
such as
optimal transport theory, probability theory, partial differential equations, machine learning,
for further details and properties of the Wasserstein distance, we refer to the
monographs
[
3
,
41
,
73
,
74
,
82
]
.

We now define the asymptotic cut-off phenomenon in a general setting.
In what follows, we recall the definition
of coupling between two probability measures and then define the Wasserstein distance and the total variation distance. Let
(H,d)
be a Polish space, a complete separable metric space,
equipped with its Borel
\sigma
-algebra

\mathcal{B}(H)
and let
\mathbb{M}_{1}(H)
be the set of probability measures defined in the measurable space
(H,\mathcal{B}(H))
.
Given
\nu_{1}\in\mathbb{M}_{1}(H)
and
\nu_{2}\in\mathbb{M}_{1}(H)
,
we say that a probability measure
\Pi
defined in the product measurable space
(H\times H,\mathcal{B}(H)\otimes\mathcal{B}(H))
is a coupling (a.k.a. transportation plan or joint distribution) between
\nu_{1}
and
\nu_{2}
if and only if

\Pi(B\times H)=\nu_{1}(B)
and

\Pi(H\times B)=\nu_{2}(B)

for any measurable set
B\in\mathcal{B}(H)
.
We then denote by
\mathcal{C}(\nu_{1},\nu_{2})
the set of all couplings between
\nu_{1}
and
\nu_{2}
. We note that the product measure
\nu_{1}\otimes\nu_{2}
is a coupling between
\nu_{1}
and
\nu_{2}
and hence

\mathcal{C}(\nu_{1},\nu_{2})\not=\emptyset
.

For any
p>0
we denote by
\mathbb{M}_{1,p}
the set of Borel probability measures on
(H,\mathcal{B}(H))
with
finite
p
-th moment, that is,

\mathbb{M}_{1,p}\subset\mathbb{M}_{1}
and for some
u_{0}\in H
it follows that

\int_{H}d(u,u_{0})^{p}\nu(\mathrm{d}u)<\infty
for all
\nu\in\mathbb{M}_{1,p}
(the choice of the point
u_{0}
is irrelevant).
Then the Wasserstein distance of order
p>0
,

\mathcal{W}_{p}:\mathbb{M}_{1,p}\times\mathbb{M}_{1,p}\to[0,\infty)
, is defined as

(1.2)

\begin{split}\mathcal{W}_{p}(\nu_{1},\nu_{2})&:=\inf_{\Pi\in\mathcal{C}(\nu_{1},\nu_{2})}\left(\int_{H\times H}d(u,v)^{p}\Pi(\mathrm{d}u,\mathrm{d}v)\right)^{1\wedge(1/p)},\end{split}

where for any real numbers
a
and
b
we denote the minimum between
a
and
b
by
a\wedge b
.
Roughly speaking, (
1.2
) measures how expensive is the transportation of the measure
\nu_{1}
into the measure
\nu_{2}
with respect to the (Monge–Kantorovich) cost function
c(u,v):=d(u,v)^{p}
,
u,v\in H
.
We also define the total variation distance
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}:\mathbb{M}_{1}\times\mathbb{M}_{1}\to[0,1]
by

(1.3)

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}(\nu_{1},\nu_{2}):=\inf_{\Pi\in\mathcal{C}(\nu_{1},\nu_{2})}\int_{H\times H}\mathbf{1}_{\{u\neq v\}}\Pi(\mathrm{d}u,\mathrm{d}v),

where
\mathbf{1}_{U}
denotes the indicator function of a given set
U\subset H
.

Let
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
be two random elements defined on the probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
with finite
p
-th moment and taking values on
H
.
The Wasserstein distance of order
p>0
between
X_{1}
and
X_{2}

is defined by

\mathfrak{W}_{p}(X_{1},X_{2}):=\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mathbb{P}_{X_{1}},\mathbb{P}_{X_{2}})
, where

\mathbb{P}_{X_{1}}
and
\mathbb{P}_{X_{2}}
are the push-forward
probability measures

\mathbb{P}_{X_{1}}(B):=\mathbb{P}(X_{1}\in B)
and

\mathbb{P}_{X_{2}}(B):=\mathbb{P}(X_{2}\in B)

for any
B\in\mathcal{B}(H)
.
For convenience,
we write
\mathcal{W}_{p}(X_{1},X_{2})
in place of
\mathfrak{W}_{p}(X_{1},X_{2})
.
Similarly, we write

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}(X_{1},X_{2})
instead of
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mathbb{P}_{X_{1}},\mathbb{P}_{X_{2}})
.
Moreover, for the sake of intuitive reasoning and in a conscious abuse of notation we write

\mathcal{W}_{p}(X_{1},\nu_{2})

and

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}(X_{1},\nu_{2})
instead of
\mathcal{W}_{p}(X_{1},X_{2})

and

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}(X_{1},X_{2})
, respectively, where
\nu_{2}:=\mathbb{P}_{X_{2}}
. In other words,

(1.4)

\begin{split}\mathcal{W}_{p}(\nu_{1},\nu_{2})&=\inf_{(X_{1},X_{2})}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[d(X_{1},X_{2})^{p}]\right)^{1\wedge(1/p)}\end{split}

and

(1.5)

\begin{split}\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}(\nu_{1},\nu_{2})=\inf_{(X_{1},X_{2})}\mathbb{P}(X_{1}\neq X_{2}),\end{split}

where the infimum is taken over all pairs
(X_{1},X_{2})
satisfying
\mathbb{P}_{X_{j}}=\nu_{j}
,
j=1,2
and
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}
denotes the expectation with respect to
\mathbb{P}
.
While by definition, the Wasserstein distance or the total variation distance are minimizers of an expected value of a given cost function, they are always bounded
above by evaluating the expectation of the cost function in any coupling (for instance, the synchronous coupling), however, lower bounds are typically hard to establish.

In the sequel, we embed our results in the context of cut-off phenomenon in a general framework.
The complexity parameter of the model is denoted by
\varepsilon>0
.
Let
(H_{\varepsilon},d_{\varepsilon})
be a Polish space equipped with its corresponding Borel
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{B}(H_{\varepsilon})
and let
\mathbb{M}_{1}(H_{\varepsilon})
be the set of probability measures defined on
\mathcal{B}(H_{\varepsilon})
.
Let
\widetilde{\mathbb{M}}_{1}(H_{\varepsilon})\subset\mathbb{M}_{1}(H_{\varepsilon})
be
equipped with a distance or divergence
\mathrm{dist}_{\varepsilon}:\widetilde{\mathbb{M}}_{1}(H_{\varepsilon})\times\widetilde{\mathbb{M}}_{1}(H_{\varepsilon})\to[0,\infty]
,
and set

\mathrm{Diam}_{\varepsilon}:=\sup_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\in\widetilde{\mathbb{M}}_{1}(H_{\varepsilon})}\mathrm{dist}_{\varepsilon}(\nu_{1},\nu_{2})

and assume that

(1.6)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\,\mathrm{Diam}_{\varepsilon}=:\mathrm{Diam}\in(0,\infty].

We then consider
\mathcal{X}^{\varepsilon,x_{\varepsilon}}:=(\mathcal{X}^{\varepsilon}_{t}(x_{\varepsilon}))_{t\geqslant 0}
a random dynamical system (including deterministic dynamical system) in continuous time and taking values in
H_{\varepsilon}
, where
x_{\varepsilon}\in H_{\varepsilon}
denotes the initial datum of the process
\mathcal{X}^{\varepsilon,x_{\varepsilon}}
.
In addition, we assume that for any
t\geqslant 0
the law of

\mathcal{X}^{\varepsilon}_{t}(x_{\varepsilon})
, here denoted by
\mathrm{Law}\,\mathcal{X}^{\varepsilon}_{t}(x_{\varepsilon})
, belongs to
\widetilde{\mathbb{M}}_{1}(H_{\varepsilon})
and the existence of
\nu^{\varepsilon}\in\widetilde{\mathbb{M}}_{1}(H_{\varepsilon})
such that

\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}\mathrm{dist}_{\varepsilon}(\mathrm{Law}\,\mathcal{X}^{\varepsilon}_{t}(x_{\varepsilon}),\nu^{\varepsilon})=0.

Since we have already fixed the distance
\mathrm{dist}_{\varepsilon}
for the convergence to the limiting law
\nu^{\varepsilon}
,
we now define the asymptotic cut-off phenomenon.

Definition 1.1

(Asymptotic cut-off phenomenon)
.

We say that the family of random dynamical systems
(\mathcal{X}^{\varepsilon,x_{\varepsilon}},\nu^{\varepsilon},\mathrm{dist}_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}
exhibits:

(i)

asymptotic pre-cut-off
at
(t_{\varepsilon,x_{\varepsilon}})_{\varepsilon>0}
with pre-cut-off time
t_{\varepsilon}:=t_{\varepsilon,x_{\varepsilon}}

if

t_{\varepsilon}\to\infty
, as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}

and there are constants
0<C_{*}<C^{*}<\infty
such that

(1.7)

\lim\limits_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\,\mathrm{dist}_{\varepsilon}(\mathrm{Law}\,\mathcal{X}^{\varepsilon}_{\delta\cdot t_{\varepsilon}}(x_{\varepsilon}),\nu^{\varepsilon})=\begin{cases}\mathrm{Diam}&\quad\textrm{ for }\quad\delta=C_{*},\\

G^{\mathcal{W}_{p}}_{x}(r)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle r\to-\infty}}{{\sim}}\left(\frac{\sqrt{2\mathfrak{b}}}{2\mathfrak{a}}\right)^{p}|C_{x}|^{p}\cdot e^{-r\cdot p},

whereas

G^{\mathcal{W}_{p}}_{x}(r)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle r\to\infty}}{{=}}\mathrm{O}\left(\left(\frac{\sqrt{2\mathfrak{b}}}{2\mathfrak{a}}\right)^{p}|C_{x}|^{p}\cdot e^{-r\cdot p}\right),

where
\mathrm{O}
denotes the classical Bachmann–Landau notation.

As a consequence of Theorem
1.10
with the help of Proposition
1.2
and Remark

we obtain the following corollary, which provides the asymptotic behavior for the mixing times.

Corollary 1.12

(
\eta
-mixing time in the normalized Wasserstein distance)
.

For any
\eta>0
and

it follows that

(1.34)

\tau^{\varepsilon,\mathcal{W}_{p}/\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{mix}}(\eta)=t_{\varepsilon}+\omega_{\varepsilon}\cdot\left(G^{\mathcal{W}_{p}}_{x}\right)^{-1}(\eta)+\mathrm{o}_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}(1),

where

\big(G^{\mathcal{W}_{p}}_{x}\big)^{-1}(\eta)=\ln\left(\left(\frac{\sqrt{2\mathfrak{b}}}{2\mathfrak{a}}\right)\frac{|C_{x}|}{\eta}\right)

and the constant

Lemma 2.6

(Basic properties of the Wasserstein distance of order

)
.

Let

Assume that

\mathcal{W}_{p}(cX,cY)=|c|^{1\wedge p}\mathcal{W}_{p}(X,Y)=\begin{cases}|c|\mathcal{W}_{p}(X,Y)&\textrm{ for }\quad p\in[1,\infty),\\
|c|^{p}\mathcal{W}_{p}(X,Y)&\textrm{ for }\quad p\in(0,1].\end{cases}

Proof.

For details, we refer to Lemma 2.2
in
[

(Limit theorem for

For any
|\lambda|>0

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda Y^{\varepsilon}_{\infty}}]=e^{\lambda^{2}/2}=:K_{\lambda}\quad\textrm{ for any }\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{R}.

\begin{split}\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\mathbb{E}[|Y^{\varepsilon}_{\infty}|^{p}\mathbf{1}_{\{|Y^{\varepsilon}_{\infty}|\geqslant N\}}]&\leqslant\sqrt{C_{\lambda,2p}}\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}(\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda Y^{\varepsilon}_{\infty}}]}+\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[e^{-\lambda Y^{\varepsilon}_{\infty}}]})\sqrt{\mathbb{P}\left(|Y^{\varepsilon}_{\infty}|\geqslant N\right)}\\

&=2\sqrt{C_{\lambda,2p}K_{\lambda}}\sqrt{\mathbb{P}\left(|\mathcal{G}|\geqslant N\right)},\end{split}

where in the last equality we have used that

converges in distribution to
\mathcal{G}
as

.
Now, sending

In the sequel, we
show (
2.25

By (

(2.27)

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda Y^{\varepsilon}_{\infty}}]&=\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda\frac{\Gamma(q_{\varepsilon}+1,1)}{\sqrt{q_{\varepsilon}+1}}}]e^{-\lambda\sqrt{q_{\varepsilon}+1}}=\mathbb{E}[e^{\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{q_{\varepsilon}+1}}\Gamma(q_{\varepsilon}+1,1)}]e^{-\lambda\sqrt{q_{\varepsilon}+1}}\\

Appendix A
General properties of the total variation distance and
Parseval–Plancherel–Fourier approach to total variation convergence

In this section, we provide general properties of the total variation distance.
As we have already discussed in Section
1
, in general, convergence in distribution does not imply convergence in the total variation distance.
We then impose suitable hypotheses on the sequence of characteristic functions that ensure convergence in the total variation distance.
Since the latter could be of independent interest, we state the results in a multidimensional setting.

Along this section,
d
always is a positive fixed integer,
\|\cdot\|
denotes the Euclidean norm in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the standard inner product in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
are the Borelian sets of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Lemma A.1

(General properties of the total variation distance)
.

Let
X
and
Y
be random vectors defined in the probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
and taking values in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then the following is valid.

(i)

Translation invariance: For any deterministic vectors
v_{1},v_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
it follows that

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}(v_{1}+X,v_{2}+Y)=\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}(v_{1}-v_{2}+X,Y)=\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}(X,v_{2}-v_{1}+Y).

(ii)

Scaling invariance: For any non-zero constant
c
it follows that

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}(cX,cY)=\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}(X,Y).

The proof is a direct consequence of Theorem 5.2 in
[
34
]
.

Lemma A.2

(Scheffé’s Lemma)
.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space
and consider
a sequence
(\mathbb{P}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of probability measures on space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
.
For each
n\in\mathbb{N}

assume that
f_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,\infty)
is a density of
\mathbb{P}_{n}
with respect to the Lebesgue measure on

(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
.
In addition, assume that

f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,\infty)
is a density of
\mathbb{P}
with respect to the Lebesgue measure on

(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
.
If
\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}f_{n}(x)=f(x)
for
x
-almost everywhere (w.r.t. the Lebesgue measure), then it follows that

\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mathbb{P}_{n},\mathbb{P})=0.

The proof is well-known and it can be found in Lemma 3.3.2 of
[
77
]
.

Harmonic analysis and representation theory have been applied to bound the total variation distance to equilibrium in compact groups, and in particular, in finite groups, see Chapter 2 in
[
29
]
and Chapter 3 in
[
36
]
.
In Proposition
A.3
and Proposition
A.5
below,
using Fourier inversion and the Parseval–Plancherel isometry, we
obtain total variation convergence in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Proposition A.3

(Fourier approach to total variation convergence)
.

Let
(X_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}
be a family of random vectors defined in the probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
and taking values on
(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
.
Let
X
be a random vector defined in
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
and taking values on
(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
.
Assume that

(i)

Convergence in distribution:

X_{\varepsilon}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}X
as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
.

(ii)

Fourier integrability:

(A.1)

\varphi_{\varepsilon},\varphi\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\quad\textrm{ for each }\quad\varepsilon>0,

where
\varphi_{\varepsilon}
and
\varphi
denote the characteristic function of
X_{\varepsilon}
and
X
, respectively.

(iii)

Tail behavior (uniform integrability):

(A.2)

\limsup_{\ell\to\infty}\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\int_{\{|z|\geqslant\ell\}}|\varphi_{\varepsilon}(z)|\mathrm{d}z=0.

Then it follows that

(A.3)

\lim\limits_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}(X_{\varepsilon},X)=0.

Remark A.4

(
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
-convergence of the Fourier transform)
.

Assume the hypothesis of Proposition
A.3
.
Following step by step the proof of
Proposition
A.3

one can see that

\lim\limits_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\varphi_{\varepsilon}(z)-\varphi(z)|\mathrm{d}z=0.

Hypothesis (
A.1
) with the help of the Fourier Inversion Theorem yields the existence of continuous and bounded densities
f_{\varepsilon}
and
f
such that

(A.4)

\begin{split}f_{\varepsilon}(x)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-\mathsf{i}\langle z,x\rangle}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(z)\mathrm{d}z\quad\textrm{ and }\quad f(x)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-\mathsf{i}\langle z,x\rangle}\varphi(z)\mathrm{d}z,\end{split}

see Proposition 2.5 Item (xii)
in
[
78
]
.
Due to the boundeness of
f_{\varepsilon}
and
f
, for all
p\in[1,\infty]
we have

(A.5)

f_{\varepsilon},f\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\quad\textrm{ for each }\quad\varepsilon>0.

Proof of Proposition
A.3
.

Recall
f_{\varepsilon}
and
f
given in (
A.4
).
For all

we have

(A.6)

\begin{split}(2\pi)^{d}|f_{\varepsilon}(x)-f(x)|&=\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(e^{-\mathsf{i}\langle z,x\rangle}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(z)-e^{-\mathsf{i}\langle z,x\rangle}\varphi(z)\right)\mathrm{d}z\right|\\
&\leqslant\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\varphi_{\varepsilon}(z)-\varphi(z)\right|\mathrm{d}z\\
&=\int_{\{|z|\leqslant\ell\}}\left|\varphi_{\varepsilon}(z)-\varphi(z)\right|\mathrm{d}z+\int_{\{|z|>\ell\}}\left|\varphi_{\varepsilon}(z)-\varphi(z)\right|\mathrm{d}z\end{split}

for all

.
For each

, Item (i) of Theorem 15.24 in
[
52

yields

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\int_{\{|z|\leqslant\ell\}}\left|\varphi_{\varepsilon}(z)-\varphi(z)\right|\mathrm{d}z=0.

By (

\begin{split}(2\pi)^{d}\limsup\limits_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}|f_{\varepsilon}(x)-f(x)|&\leqslant\limsup\limits_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\int_{\{|z|>\ell\}}\left|\varphi_{\varepsilon}(z)-\varphi(z)\right|\mathrm{d}z\\
&\leqslant\limsup\limits_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\int_{\{|z|>\ell\}}\left|\varphi_{\varepsilon}(z)\right|\mathrm{d}z+\int_{\{|z|>\ell\}}\left|\varphi(z)\right|\mathrm{d}z\end{split}

for

and any

Since

Sending

The preceding limit with the help of Lemma

in Appendix

Remark A.7

(Convergence in total variation distance without

-integrability of the characteristic function)

While Proposition

Let

By Scheffé’s Lemma we have

\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(X_{n},X\right)=0.

The corresponding characteristic functions are given by

(A.14)

\varphi_{n}(u)=\left(1-\mathsf{i}\theta^{-1}_{n}u\right)^{-r},\quad u\in\mathbb{R}

and

(A.15)

\varphi(u)=\left(1-\mathsf{i}\theta^{-1}u\right)^{-r},\quad u\in\mathbb{R},

respectively. Clearly,
\varphi_{n},\varphi\not\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
neither
\varphi_{n},\varphi\not\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
.

Proof of
Proposition
A.5
.

Item (ii’) with the help of Lemma 9.2.8 in
[

yields
that the laws of

and

and

corresponding to

and
\varphi
, respectively.
By Parseval–Plancherel Theorem we have

(A.16)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(f_{\varepsilon}(x)-f(x))^{2}\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\varphi_{\varepsilon}(z)-\varphi(z)|^{2}\mathrm{d}z.

We claim that the right-hand side of (
A.16
) tends to zero as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
.
Indeed, let
\ell>0
be fixed and note that

(A.17)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\varphi_{\varepsilon}(z)-\varphi(z)|^{2}\mathrm{d}z=\int_{\{|z|\leqslant\ell\}}|\varphi_{\varepsilon}(z)-\varphi(z)|^{2}\mathrm{d}z+\int_{\{|z|>\ell\}}|\varphi_{\varepsilon}(z)-\varphi(z)|^{2}\mathrm{d}z

For each
\ell>0
, Item (i) of Theorem 15.24 in
[
52
]
with the help of Item (i) gives

(A.18)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\int_{\{|z|\leqslant\ell\}}\left|\varphi_{\varepsilon}(z)-\varphi(z)\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}z=0.

Since

(A.19)

\begin{split}\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\int_{\{|z|>\ell\}}|\varphi_{\varepsilon}(z)-\varphi(z)|^{2}\mathrm{d}z&\leqslant 2\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\int_{\{|z|>\ell\}}|\varphi_{\varepsilon}(z)|^{2}\mathrm{d}z\\
&\qquad+2\int_{\{|z|>\ell\}}|\varphi(z)|^{2}\mathrm{d}z\end{split}

and
\varphi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
we have

(A.20)

\begin{split}\limsup\limits_{\ell\to\infty}\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\int_{\{|z|>\ell\}}|\varphi_{\varepsilon}(z)-\varphi(z)|^{2}\mathrm{d}z&\leqslant 2\limsup\limits_{\ell\to\infty}\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\int_{\{|z|>\ell\}}|\varphi_{\varepsilon}(z)|^{2}\mathrm{d}z.\end{split}

Hence Item (iii’) gives

(A.21)

\lim\limits_{\ell\to\infty}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\int_{\{|z|>\ell\}}|\varphi_{\varepsilon}(z)-\varphi(z)|^{2}\mathrm{d}z=0.

By (
A.16
) we obtain

(A.22)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(f_{\varepsilon}(x)-f(x))^{2}\mathrm{d}x=0.

To conclude we show that the preceding limit yields

(A.23)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f_{\varepsilon}(x)-f(x)|\mathrm{d}x=0.

Indeed, (
A.22
) with the help of
the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality gives
for any
K>0

(A.24)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\int_{\{|x|\leqslant K\}}|f_{\varepsilon}(x)-f(x)|\mathrm{d}x=0.

Recall that
f_{\varepsilon}
and
f
are the densities of the random vectors
X_{\varepsilon}
and
X
, respectively.
Since
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
X_{\varepsilon}\to X
in distribution as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
, we have

(A.25)

\begin{split}&\limsup_{K\to\infty}\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\int_{\{|x|>K\}}|f_{\varepsilon}(x)-f(x)|\mathrm{d}x\leqslant\limsup_{K\to\infty}\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\int_{\{|x|\geqslant K\}}f_{\varepsilon}(x)\mathrm{d}x\\
&\leqslant\limsup_{K\to\infty}\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\mathbb{P}(\|X_{\varepsilon}\|\geqslant K)\leqslant\limsup_{K\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(\|X\|\geqslant K)=0.\end{split}

By (
A.24
)
and (
A.25
) we
deduce (
A.23
).
∎

Appendix B
Scaling property and local central limit theorem for Gamma distribution

In this section, we show a scaling property for the second parameter of the Gamma distribution and a Gaussian local central limit theorem. We recall that for any positive parameters
\alpha
and
\theta
,
\Gamma(\alpha,\theta)
denotes a Gamma distribution with characteristic function given
by (
2.2
).

Lemma B.1

(Scaling property of the Gamma distribution)
.

For any positive constants
\alpha
and
\theta
it follows that

\Gamma(\alpha,\theta)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\theta^{-1}\Gamma(\alpha,1)
.

Proof.

For any
u\in\mathbb{R}
, (
2.2
) yields

(B.1)

\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\mathsf{i}u(\theta^{-1}\Gamma(\alpha,1))}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\mathsf{i}(u\theta^{-1})\Gamma(\alpha,1)}\right]=\left(1-\mathsf{i}u\theta^{-1}\right)^{-\alpha}=\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\mathsf{i}u\Gamma(\alpha,\theta)}\right].

Recall that the distribution of a random variable is characterized by its characteristic function, see for instance Theorem 15.9
in
[
52
]
.
Then (
B.1
) implies the statement.
∎

Lemma B.2

(Local central limit theorem)
.

For any positive
\alpha
set

\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha}:=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha,1)-\alpha}{\sqrt{\alpha}}
.
Then
\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha}
converges in the total variation distance to a standard Gaussian distribution as
\alpha\to\infty
.

Proof.

Let
\alpha>0
be fixed and let
g_{\alpha}
be the density of the random variable
\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha}
. In other words,

(B.2)

g_{\alpha}(z)=\frac{\sqrt{\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}(\sqrt{\alpha}z+\alpha)^{\alpha-1}e^{-\sqrt{\alpha}z-\alpha}\mathbf{1}_{\{z\geqslant-\sqrt{\alpha}\}},

where
\Gamma
denotes the usual Gamma function.
Recall that, for short, we write
F\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\alpha\to\infty}}{{\sim}}G
in a place of
\lim\limits_{\alpha\to\infty}\frac{F(\alpha)}{G(\alpha)}=1
.
The Stirling formula for the function
\Gamma
yields

\Gamma(\alpha)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\alpha\to\infty}}{{\sim}}\sqrt{2\pi(\alpha-1)}\left(\frac{\alpha-1}{e}\right)^{\alpha-1}.

Then we have

(B.3)

\frac{\sqrt{\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\alpha\to\infty}}{{\sim}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\frac{\sqrt{\alpha}}{\sqrt{\alpha-1}}\left(\frac{e}{\alpha-1}\right)^{\alpha-1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\alpha\to\infty}}{{\sim}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\left(\frac{e}{\alpha-1}\right)^{\alpha-1}.

Note that

(B.4)

e\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\alpha\to\infty}}{{\sim}}\frac{1}{\left(1-\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)^{\alpha-1}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\alpha\to\infty}}{{\sim}}\left(\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}\right)^{\alpha-1}.

Hence, for any
z\in\mathbb{R}
fixed, combining (
B.3
), (
B.4
) in (
B.2
) yields

(B.5)

\begin{split}g_{\alpha}(z)&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\alpha\to\infty}}{{\sim}}\frac{e^{-1}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\left(\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}\right)^{\alpha-1}\left(1+\frac{\sqrt{\alpha}z}{\alpha}\right)^{-1}\left(1+\frac{\sqrt{\alpha}z}{\alpha}\right)^{\alpha}e^{-\sqrt{\alpha}z}\mathbf{1}_{\{z\geqslant-\sqrt{\alpha}\}}\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\alpha\to\infty}}{{\sim}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\left(1+\frac{z}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\right)^{\alpha}e^{-\sqrt{\alpha}z}.\end{split}

By Lemma
D.1
in Appendix
D
we have,

(B.6)

\left(1+\frac{z}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\right)^{\alpha}e^{-\sqrt{\alpha}z}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\alpha\to\infty}}{{\sim}}e^{-z^{2}/2}.

Consequently, for any
z\in\mathbb{R}
(
B.5
)
and (
B.6
) imply

\lim\limits_{\alpha\to\infty}g_{\alpha}(z)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-z^{2}/2},

which with the help of Lemma
A.2
in Appendix
A
gives the statement.
∎

Appendix D
Second order Taylor’s expansions

In this section, we show refined asymptotics that were used along the manuscript.

Lemma D.1

(Second order asymptotics I)
.

For any
z\in\mathbb{R}
it follows that

\lim\limits_{\alpha\to\infty}\left(1+\frac{z}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\right)^{\alpha}e^{-\sqrt{\alpha}z}=e^{-z^{2}/2}.

Proof.

Let
z\in\mathbb{R}
be fixed.
It is enough to show that

\lim\limits_{\alpha\to\infty}\left[\alpha\ln\left(1+\frac{z}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\right)-\sqrt{\alpha}z\right]=-\frac{z^{2}}{2}.

By Taylor’s expansion we have for
\alpha\gg 1

\ln\left(1+\frac{z}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\right)=\frac{z}{\sqrt{\alpha}}-\frac{z^{2}}{2\alpha}+H(z,\alpha),

where

(D.1)

\limsup_{r\to\infty}(\alpha^{3/2}|H(z,\alpha)|)<\infty.

Then we have

\begin{split}\alpha\ln\left(1+\frac{z}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\right)-\sqrt{\alpha}z=-\frac{z^{2}}{2}+\alpha H(z,\alpha).\end{split}

By (
D.1
) we deduce

\alpha|G(z,\alpha)|\to 0
as
\alpha\to\infty
and conclude the statement.
∎

Lemma D.2

(Second order asymptotics II)
.

Let
(u_{\alpha})_{\alpha>0}\subset\mathbb{C}
be a function such that

(D.2)

\lim\limits_{\alpha\to\infty}\sqrt{\alpha}(u_{\alpha}-u)=c\quad\textrm{ for some }\quad u\in\mathbb{C}\quad\textrm{ and }\quad c\in\mathbb{C}.

Then it follows that

\lim\limits_{\alpha\to\infty}\left(1+\frac{u_{\alpha}}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\right)^{\alpha}e^{-\sqrt{\alpha}u}=e^{-u^{2}/2+c}.

Proof.

It is enough to show that,

\lim\limits_{\alpha\to\infty}\left[\alpha\ln\left(1+\frac{u_{\alpha}}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\right)-\sqrt{\alpha}u\right]=-\frac{u^{2}}{2}+c.

Here, we are considering the principal branch of the complex logarithmic function
\log:=\ln
.
By Taylor’s expansion we have for
\alpha\gg 1

\ln\left(1+\frac{u_{\alpha}}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\right)=\frac{u_{\alpha}}{\sqrt{\alpha}}-\frac{u^{2}_{\alpha}}{2\alpha}+H(u_{\alpha},\alpha),

where

(D.3)

\limsup_{\alpha\to\infty}(\alpha^{3/2}|H(u_{\alpha},\alpha)|)<\infty.

Then we have

\begin{split}\alpha\ln\left(1+\frac{u_{\alpha}}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\right)-\sqrt{\alpha}u_{\alpha}=-\frac{u^{2}_{\alpha}}{2}+\alpha H(u_{\alpha},\alpha),\end{split}

which can be written as

\begin{split}\alpha\ln\left(1+\frac{u_{\alpha}}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\right)-\sqrt{\alpha}u=\sqrt{\alpha}(u_{\alpha}-u)-\frac{u^{2}_{\alpha}}{2}+\alpha H(u_{\alpha},\alpha).\end{split}

By (
D.3
) we deduce that

\alpha|H(u_{\alpha},\alpha)|\to 0
as
\alpha\to\infty
and
with the help of (
D.2
) we
conclude the statement.
∎

Lemma D.3

(Second order asymptotics III)
.

Let
(u_{\alpha})_{\alpha>0}\subset\mathbb{C}
be a function such that

(D.4)

\lim\limits_{\alpha\to\infty}u_{\alpha}=u\quad\textrm{ for some }\quad u\in\mathbb{C}.

Then it follows that

\lim\limits_{\alpha\to\infty}\left(1+\frac{u_{\alpha}}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\right)^{\alpha}e^{-\sqrt{\alpha}u_{\alpha}}=e^{-u^{2}/2}.

Proof.

It is enough to prove that,

\lim\limits_{\alpha\to\infty}\left[\alpha\ln\left(1+\frac{u_{\alpha}}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\right)-\sqrt{\alpha}u_{\alpha}\right]=-\frac{u^{2}}{2}.

Here, we are considering the principal branch of the complex logarithmic function
\log:=\ln.

By Taylor’s expansion we have for
\alpha\gg 1

\ln\left(1+\frac{u_{\alpha}}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\right)=\frac{u_{\alpha}}{\sqrt{\alpha}}-\frac{u^{2}_{\alpha}}{2\alpha}+H(u_{\alpha},\alpha),

where

(D.5)

\limsup_{\alpha\to\infty}(\alpha^{3/2}|H(u_{\alpha},\alpha)|)<\infty.

Then we have

\begin{split}\alpha\ln\left(1+\frac{u_{\alpha}}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\right)-\sqrt{\alpha}u_{\alpha}=-\frac{u^{2}_{\alpha}}{2}+\alpha H(u_{\alpha},\alpha).\end{split}

By (
D.5
) we deduce that

\alpha|H(u_{\alpha},\alpha)|\to 0
as
\alpha\to\infty
and
with the help of (
D.4
) we
conclude the statement.
∎

Remark D.4
.

We point out that (
D.2
) implies (
D.4
). However, in general (
D.4
) does not imply (
D.2
).

Appendix E
Mixing times equivalence of asymptotic profile cut-off

In this section, we show Item (4) of Proposition
1.2
. For clarity, we state it as a Lemma
E.1
.
The proofs of Item (1), Item (2) and Item (3) are analogous.

Lemma
E.1
establishes the asymptotic relation between the
\eta
-mixing times and the cut-off time in a general framework.
We stress that this result is valid under mild assumptions on the profile function
G
.

Lemma E.1

(Asymptotics of mixing times and profile cut-off phenomenon)
.

Assume that (
1.12
) holds true and
that the function

G:\mathbb{R}\to(0,\mathrm{Diam})
is strictly decreasing and continuous. In addition, assume that

\lim\limits_{r\rightarrow-\infty}\,G(r)=\mathrm{Diam}\quad\textrm{ and }\quad\lim\limits_{r\rightarrow\infty}\,G(r)=0
,
and the existence of a function

((t_{\varepsilon},\omega_{\varepsilon}))_{\varepsilon>0}
satisfying

\lim\limits_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}t_{\varepsilon}=\infty\quad\textrm{ and }\quad\lim\limits_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{\omega_{\varepsilon}}{t_{\varepsilon}}=0.

Then the following statements are equivalent.

(a)

The following limit

(E.1)

\lim\limits_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\,\mathrm{dist}_{\varepsilon}(\mathrm{Law}\,\mathcal{X}^{\varepsilon}_{t_{\varepsilon}+r\cdot\omega_{\varepsilon}},\nu^{\varepsilon})=G(r)\quad\textrm{ for any }\quad r\in\mathbb{R}

holds true.

(b)

For any
\eta\in(0,\mathrm{Diam})

it follows that

(E.2)

\tau^{\varepsilon,\mathrm{dist}_{\varepsilon}}_{\mathrm{mix}}(\eta)=t_{\varepsilon}+G^{-1}(\eta)\cdot\omega_{\varepsilon}+\mathrm{o}(\omega_{\varepsilon}),\quad\textrm{ as }\quad\varepsilon\to 0^{+},

is valid,
where
G^{-1}(\eta)
is the unique
r:=r_{\eta}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
G(r)=\eta
and the function

\mathrm{o}(\omega_{\varepsilon})
satisfies

\frac{\mathrm{o}(\omega_{\varepsilon})}{\omega_{\varepsilon}}\to 0
as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
.

Proof.

We start assuming that the statement of Item (a) is valid.

Let
\eta\in(0,\mathrm{Diam})
be fixed.
We first show that

(E.3)

\limsup\limits_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{\tau_{\text{mix }}^{\varepsilon,x}(\eta)-\left(t_{\varepsilon}+{G}^{-1}(\eta)\cdot\omega_{\varepsilon}\right)}{\omega_{\varepsilon}}\leqslant 0.

We take
\delta^{*}
such that
0<\delta^{*}<\eta
.
Since
G
is a bijection, there exists a unique
r^{*}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
G(r^{*})=\eta-\delta^{*}
, i.e,
r^{*}={G}^{-1}(\eta-\delta^{*})
.
For the preceding choice
\eta
and
\delta^{*}
,
the limit (
E.1
) with the help of (
1.6
) gives the existence of
\varepsilon^{*}=\varepsilon^{*}(\eta,\delta^{*})>0
such that

(E.4)

\begin{split}0&<\eta<\mathrm{Diam}_{\varepsilon}\quad\textrm{ and }\\
\operatorname{dist}_{\varepsilon}\left(t_{\varepsilon}+r^{*}\cdot\omega_{\varepsilon}\right)&<\delta^{*}+G\left(r^{*}\right)=\eta\end{split}

for all
0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon^{*}
.
Thus (
E.4
) with the help of the definition of
\eta
-mixing time yields

(E.5)

\begin{split}\tau_{\text{mix }}^{\varepsilon,x}(\eta)&\leqslant t_{\varepsilon}+r^{*}\cdot\omega_{\varepsilon}=t_{\varepsilon}+G^{-1}(\eta-\delta^{*})\cdot\omega_{\varepsilon}\\
&=t_{\varepsilon}+G^{-1}(\eta)\cdot\omega_{\varepsilon}+\left(G^{-1}(\eta-\delta^{*})-G^{-1}(\eta)\right)\cdot\omega_{\varepsilon}\end{split}

for all
0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon^{*}
. Hence, we obtain

(E.6)

\limsup\limits_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{\tau_{\text{mix }}^{\varepsilon,x}(\eta)-\left(t_{\varepsilon}+{G}^{-1}(\eta)\cdot\omega_{\varepsilon}\right)}{\omega_{\varepsilon}}\leqslant{G}^{-1}(\eta-\delta^{*})-{G}^{-1}(\eta)

for any
\eta>0
and
\delta^{*}\in(0,\eta)
. Since
G
is a strictly decreasing continuous function, then
G^{-1}
is also a continuous function, see Proposition 3.6.6. in
[
58
]
. Moreover, the left-hand of (
E.6
) does not depend on
\delta^{*}
, then tending
\delta^{*}\to 0^{+}
we deduce (
E.3
).

We now prove that

(E.7)

\liminf\limits_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{\tau_{\text{mix }}^{\varepsilon,x}(\eta)-\left(t_{\varepsilon}+{G}^{-1}(\eta)\cdot\omega_{\varepsilon}\right)}{\omega_{\varepsilon}}\geqslant 0.

We take
\delta_{*}>0
such that
\eta+\delta_{*}<\mathrm{Diam}
.
Since
G
is a bijection, there exists a unique
r_{*}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
G(r_{*})=\eta+\delta_{*}
, i.e,
r_{*}={G}^{-1}(\eta+\delta_{*})
.
For the preceding choice
\eta
and
\delta_{*}
,
the limit (
E.1
) with the help of (
1.6
) implies the existence of
\varepsilon_{*}=\varepsilon_{*}(\eta,\delta_{*})>0
such that

(E.8)

\begin{split}0&<\eta<\mathrm{Diam}_{\varepsilon}\quad\textrm{ and }\\
\eta&=-\delta_{*}+G\left(r_{*}\right)<\operatorname{dist}_{\varepsilon}\left(t_{\varepsilon}+r_{*}\cdot\omega_{\varepsilon}\right)\end{split}

for all
0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{*}
.
Thus (
E.8
) with the help of the definition of
\eta
-mixing time yields

(E.9)

\begin{split}\tau_{\text{mix }}^{\varepsilon,x}(\eta)&\geqslant t_{\varepsilon}+r_{*}\cdot\omega_{\varepsilon}=t_{\varepsilon}+G^{-1}(\eta+\delta_{*})\cdot\omega_{\varepsilon}\\
&=t_{\varepsilon}+G^{-1}(\eta)\cdot\omega_{\varepsilon}+\left(G^{-1}(\eta+\delta_{*})-G^{-1}(\eta)\right)\cdot\omega_{\varepsilon}\end{split}

for all
0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon^{*}
, and therefore

(E.10)

\liminf\limits_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{\tau_{\text{mix }}^{\varepsilon,x}(\eta)-\left(t_{\varepsilon}+{G}^{-1}(\eta)\cdot\omega_{\varepsilon}\right)}{\omega_{\varepsilon}}\geqslant{G}^{-1}(\eta+\delta_{*})-{G}^{-1}(\eta)

for any
\eta>0
and
\delta_{*}
such that
\eta+\delta^{*}<\mathrm{Diam}
.
Since
G^{-1}
is a continuous function and
the right-hand of (
E.10
) does not depend on
\delta_{*}
, tending
\delta_{*}\to 0^{+}
we deduce (
E.7
).
By (
E.3
) and (
E.7
) we obtain the statement of Item (b).

In the sequel, we assume that the statement of Item (b) is valid.
Let
r\in\mathbb{R}
be fixed.

We start with the proof of

(E.11)

\limsup\limits_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\mathrm{dist}_{\varepsilon}(\mathrm{Law}\,\mathcal{X}^{\varepsilon}_{t_{\varepsilon}+r\cdot\omega_{\varepsilon}},\nu^{\varepsilon})\leqslant G(r).

Assume that
\delta^{*}>0
and take
\eta^{*}:=G(r-\delta^{*})\in(0,\mathrm{Diam})
.
By hypothesis
G
is a bijection and then we have that
r=G^{-1}(\eta^{*})+\delta^{*}
.
For the preceding choice of
\delta^{*}>0
and
\eta^{*}\in(0,\mathrm{Diam})
,
the asymptotic (
E.2
) with the help of (
1.6
) yields the existence of
\varepsilon^{*}:=\varepsilon^{*}(r,\delta^{*})>0
such that

(E.12)

\begin{split}0&<\eta^{*}<\mathrm{Diam}_{\varepsilon}\quad\textrm{ and }\\
\tau^{\varepsilon,\mathrm{dist}_{\varepsilon}}_{\mathrm{mix}}(\eta^{*})&<t_{\varepsilon}+(G^{-1}(\eta^{*})+\delta^{*})\cdot\omega_{\varepsilon}=t_{\varepsilon}+r\cdot\omega_{\varepsilon}\end{split}

for all
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon^{*})
.
The definition of
\eta^{*}
-mixing time with the help
of (
1.12
) yields

(E.13)

\mathrm{dist}_{\varepsilon}(\mathrm{Law}\,\mathcal{X}^{\varepsilon}_{t_{\varepsilon}+r\cdot\omega_{\varepsilon}},\nu^{\varepsilon})\leqslant\eta^{*}=G(r-\delta^{*})\quad\textrm{ for all }\quad\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon^{*}).

Therefore,

(E.14)

\limsup\limits_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\mathrm{dist}_{\varepsilon}(\mathrm{Law}\,\mathcal{X}^{\varepsilon}_{t_{\varepsilon}+r\cdot\omega_{\varepsilon}},\nu^{\varepsilon})\leqslant G(r-\delta^{*}).

Since the left right-hand
of (
E.14
) does not depend on
\delta^{*}
and the function
G
is continuous, tending
\delta^{*}\to 0^{+}
we
deduce (
E.11
).

We continue with the proof of

(E.15)

\liminf\limits_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\mathrm{dist}_{\varepsilon}(\mathrm{Law}\,\mathcal{X}^{\varepsilon}_{t_{\varepsilon}+r\cdot\omega_{\varepsilon}},\nu^{\varepsilon})\geqslant G(r).

Assume that
\delta_{*}>0
and take
\eta_{*}:=G(r+\delta_{*})\in(0,\mathrm{Diam})
, i.e.,

r=G^{-1}(\eta_{*})-\delta_{*}
.
For the preceding choice of
\delta_{*}>0
and
\eta_{*}\in(0,\mathrm{Diam})
,
the asymptotic (
E.2
) with the help of (
1.6
) yields the existence of
\varepsilon_{*}:=\varepsilon_{*}(r,\delta_{*})>0
such that

(E.16)

\begin{split}0&<\eta_{*}<\mathrm{Diam}_{\varepsilon}\quad\textrm{ and }\\
0&<t_{\varepsilon}+r\cdot\omega_{\varepsilon}=t_{\varepsilon}+(G^{-1}(\eta_{*})-\delta_{*})\cdot\omega_{\varepsilon}<\tau^{\varepsilon,\mathrm{dist}_{\varepsilon}}_{\mathrm{mix}}(\eta_{*})\end{split}

for all
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{*})
.
The definition of
\eta_{*}
-mixing time with the help
of (
1.12
) yields

(E.17)

\mathrm{dist}_{\varepsilon}(\mathrm{Law}\,\mathcal{X}^{\varepsilon}_{t_{\varepsilon}+r\cdot\omega_{\varepsilon}},\nu^{\varepsilon})\geqslant\eta_{*}=G(r+\delta_{*})\quad\textrm{ for all }\quad\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{*}).

Therefore,

(E.18)

\liminf\limits_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\mathrm{dist}_{\varepsilon}(\mathrm{Law}\,\mathcal{X}^{\varepsilon}_{t_{\varepsilon}+r\cdot\omega_{\varepsilon}},\nu^{\varepsilon})\geqslant G(r+\delta_{*}).

Since the left right-hand of (
E.14
) does not depend on
\delta_{*}
and the function
G
is continuous, tending
\delta_{*}\to 0^{+}
we deduce (
E.15
).

By (
E.11
) and (
E.15
) we obtain the statement of Item (a).
∎
