Bosonic and Fermionic Holographic Fluctuation and Dissipation at finite temperature and density
Nathan G. Caldeira, Carlos A. D. Zarro, Henrique Boschi-Filho

Abstract
In this paper we investigate some general aspects of fluctuation and dissipation in the holographic scenario at zero and finite density. We model this situation with a probe string in a diagonal metric representing a black brane. The string stretches from the black brane to a probe brane thus simulating a stochastic driven particle. In this scenario, we compute the admittance, the diffusion coefficient, the correlation functions and the regularized mean square displacement, for bosons and fermions, all from the metric components. We check these calculations with the fluctuation-dissipation theorem. Further, we show that at finite temperature and density, the mean square displacement in the limit of short times reproduces the usual quadratic (ballistic) behavior, for bosons and fermions. For large times, we find ultraslow diffusive processes in various cases, except for bosons at zero chemical potential. We apply this general analysis in two different models: hyperscaling violation at finite temperature and a charged dilatonic AdS black hole, both for bosons and fermions. This is important because we found the fermionic diffusion in systems which allow the appearance of Fermi surfaces and Fermi liquids.



I
Introduction

The study of the dynamics of finite temperature and/or finite chemical potential systems occupies an important part in physical sciences.
Of particular interest is how such systems respond to an external force and its diffusive process. Huge results in this direction can be made studying the linear regime of those processes and the related quantities obtained from this analysis. Thus it is
interesting to explore methods to get relevant quantities in this context.

As a tool for this study we can use the framework of AdS/CFT correspondence in its broader form, applying its dictionary, for example, to analyze and describe aspects
of this kind of phenomenon.

The study of linear response in the context of Brownian motion using holographic models started in
deBoer:2008gu
where it was proposed a set up based on an stretched string that goes from the black hole horizon to a probe brane near the space-time boundary. The presence of a horizon associated with a black hole allows one to calculate the Hawking temperature and to obtain some quantities as functions of temperature. One then interpret the string endpoint as a probe particle in a thermal bath. From the excitation of the string at the boundary one reads the motion of the particle and finds the diffusion coefficient from the admittance. They also calculate the mean square displacement, for short and long times reproducing the ballistic and diffusive regimes, respectively.

This set up was used to study quantum critical points with Lifshitz symmetry in
Tong:2012nf
and with hyperscaling-Lifshitz symmetry in
Edalati:2012tc
. In
Giataganas:2018ekx
a description in terms of a metric written as monomials of the holographic coordinate was presented and applied to many different cases encompassing the previous results
deBoer:2008gu
;
Tong:2012nf
;
Edalati:2012tc
and discussing further cases.
In particular, in Ref.
Edalati:2012tc
a chemical potential at zero temperature was introduced in the case of the extremal black branes. This was also discussed in
Banerjee:2015vmo
where a very low temperature compared to the chemical potential was introduced.

In Ref.
Caldeira:2020sot
an exponential factor was used to deform the AdS-Schwarzschild metric. This deformation was inspired by the soft-wall model where a quadratic exponential dilaton is included in the action
Karch:2006pv
. This exponential factor was introduced in the metric in Ref.
Andreev:2006ct
to guarantee confinement in a quark-antiquark potential. This metric was used in Ref.
Caldeira:2020sot

to obtain the admittance, the diffusion coefficient, compute the regularized mean square displacement and verify the fluctuation-dissipation theorem for this set up. In Ref.
Caldeira:2020rir
, this problem was reanalyzed including a backreaction from the exponential factor altering the horizon function, generalizing the results of Ref.
Caldeira:2020sot
.
In Ref.
Caldeira:2021izy
, this set up with an exponential deformation in the metric was further extended to include a non-zero chemical potential allowing the discussion of bosonic and fermionic mean square displacements and also to verify the fluctuation-dissipation theorem in both (bosonic and fermionic) cases. In particular, the long time regime for both cases present a Sinai-like diffusion logarithm behavior

sinai-like
.

In this work we extend some finite temperature results
deBoer:2008gu
;
Tong:2012nf
;
Edalati:2012tc
;
Giataganas:2018ekx
regarding the linear response and diffusion in the literature to a general diagonal background metric. It is important to mention that this previous results are restricted to bosons.
We include a non-zero chemical potential in this general metric allowing us to distinguish between the bosonic and fermionic cases.
Using the corresponding statistical distributions we calculate and present the values of the mean square displacement for bosonic and fermionic cases. After obtaining closed expressions, we get the limits of short and long times for zero and non-zero chemical potentials for bosons and fermions. This is important because the diffusion and mean square displacement were not discussed previously in the fermionic case, as we present here (we discussed the fermionic case for an exponential deformed metric which is a particular case of the present discussion).

As an application for these general results we apply our findings on two holographic models of interest. First, we consider the hyperscaling-Lifshitz model at finite temperature and zero chemical potential for bosons and fermions. This background was studied as a holographic dual for a series of condensed matter systems and it was used in the holographic study of Fermi surfaces
Huijse:2011ef
. Second, we discuss a charged dilatonic AdS black hole presented in Ref.
Gubser:2009qt
, as another application of our general results. This is a top-down model with finite chemical potential which describes dual Fermi liquids with massless charged fermionic modes. In this model, the entropy is a linear function of the temperature, for low
T
. So, our general results allow us to obtain the diffusion coefficient and mean square displacement of fermionic systems which are relevant to the study of Fermi surfaces and Fermi liquid in the holographic set up.

This work is organized as follows. In Section
II
, we start setting the class of metrics explored here and calculating the Hawking temperature. From the Nambu-Goto action we find the general equation of motion in the linear regime. Then we solve it using a patching method. In Section
III
, we find a general expression for the linear response function and for the diffusion coefficient in terms of the general metric elements.
In Section
IV
, we calculate correlation functions in the bosonic and fermionic cases.
In Section
V
,
we check our results for the admittance and correlation functions with the fluctuation-dissipation theorem for bosons and fermions.
In section
VI
, our goal is to calculate the regularized mean square displacement
s^{2}_{\text{reg}}(t)
for multiple scenarios. We begin establishing the boundary conditions for the solutions and from them we get a general integral representation for
s^{2}_{\text{reg}}(t)
. After that, we specialize to four different cases: bosonic and fermionic at zero and non-zero chemical potentials. For each of these cases we further obtain the regimes of short and large times. At the end of this section we present a summary of this results and compare them.
Going ahead, in section
VII
, we apply the previous results to a hyperscaling violation
d+2
spacetime metric at finite temperature and zero chemical potential presenting some interesting cases for particular values of the parameters of the model.
In section
VIII
,
we look at a top-down finite chemical potential model in
2+1
dimensions introduced in
Gubser:2009qt
. We use it as an illustration for our general finite chemical results in the bosonic and fermionic cases presenting interesting properties.
Finally in the last Section
IX
, we show our conclusions making general comments about the results. Some technical calculations are presented in three appendices.

II
Nambu-Goto action and equations of motion

In this section we study the dynamics of the probe string, finding its equations of motion and their solutions. The set up we are going to use
deBoer:2008gu
considers a probe string in bulk with a black hole. One endpoint of the string interacts with the horizon (IR) and the other to a brane near the boundary (UV). The endpoint near the UV behaves as a particle simulating a stochastic motion. This set up was used to describe Lifshitz as well as hyperscaling violation
Tong:2012nf
;
Edalati:2012tc
;
Giataganas:2018ekx
.

We start with a general diagonal metric

ds^{2}=-g_{tt}dt^{2}+g_{rr}dr^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}g_{ii}dx_{i}^{2},

(1)

where we defined

g_{tt}\equiv a(r)f(r)\,;\qquad g_{rr}\equiv\frac{b(r)}{f(r)}\,;\qquad g_{ii}\equiv c(r)\,;\quad(i=1,\cdots,d).

(2)

Note that
d
is the number of spatial dimensions and
r
is the holographic coordinate. The horizon function
f(r)
of the black hole is assumed to have a simple zero at
r=r_{h}
and goes to 1 for
r\to\infty
.

In the particular case of asymptotically AdS spaces,
a(r)
,
c(r)
and
1/b(r)
go to
r^{2}
as
r\to\infty
, where the boundary is located.
On the other hand, in the case of hyperscaling violation, discussed in Sec.
VII
, the metric coefficients are given by

a(r)=r^{2(z-\theta/d)}\,;\qquad b(r)=r^{-2(1+\theta/d)}\,;\qquad c(r)=r^{2(1-\theta/d)}\,,

(3)

which also reduce to the AdS case when
z\to 1
and
\theta\to 0
.

The Hawking temperature for the general metric, Eq.
(
1
), is given by

T=\frac{1}{4\pi}\sqrt{\frac{a(r_{h})}{b(r_{h})}[f^{\prime}(r_{h})]^{2}}=\frac{|f^{\prime}(r_{h})|}{4\pi}\sqrt{\frac{a(r_{h})}{b(r_{h})}}.

(4)

Let us now describe the motion of the probe string using the Nambu-Goto action:

S_{NG}=-\frac{1}{2\pi\alpha^{\prime}}\int d\tau d\sigma\sqrt{-\gamma}\,,

(5)

where
\alpha^{\prime}
is the string tension,
\gamma={\rm det}(\gamma_{\alpha\beta})
and
\gamma_{\alpha\beta}=g_{mn}\partial_{\alpha}X^{m}\partial_{\beta}X^{n}
is the induced metric on the worldsheet with
m,n=0,1,\cdots,d+1
.

We choose the usual static gauge, where
t=\tau
,
r=\sigma
and
X^{m}=X^{m}(\tau,\sigma)
. By using the general metric Eq.(
1
) and expanding the Nambu-Goto action keeping up to quadratic terms
\dot{X}^{2}
,
X^{\prime 2}
, one gets:

S_{NG}\approx-\frac{1}{4\pi\alpha^{\prime}}\int d\tau d\sigma\left[\;\frac{\dot{X}^{2}\,g_{ii}(r)\sqrt{g_{tt}(r)g_{{rr}}(r)}}{g_{tt}(r)}-\frac{X^{\prime 2}\,g_{ii}(r)\sqrt{g_{tt}(r)g_{{rr}}(r)}}{g_{{rr}}(r)}\;\right]\,,

(6)

where
\dot{X}=\partial_{\tau=t}X
and
X^{\prime}=\partial_{\sigma=r}X
.
From this action one obtains the EoM and define
g_{xx}=g_{ii}
(without sum over
i
from now on) for any
i
,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(\frac{X^{\prime}(t,r)\left(g_{xx}(r)\sqrt{g_{tt}(r)g_{{rr}}(r)}\right)}{g_{{rr}}(r)}\right)+\frac{\ddot{X}(t,r)\left(g_{xx}(r)\sqrt{g_{{tt}}(r)g_{{rr}}(r)}\right)}{g_{{tt}}(r)}=0\,.\qquad

(7)

Then, by using the decomposition
X(t,r)=e^{i\omega t}h_{\omega}(r)
, the EoM reads:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(h_{\omega}^{\prime}(r)\frac{g_{xx}(r)\sqrt{g_{{tt}}(r)}}{\sqrt{g_{{rr}(r)}}}\right)+\frac{\omega^{2}g_{xx}(r)\sqrt{g_{rr}(r)}}{\sqrt{g_{tt}(r)}}h_{\omega}(r)=0\,.

(8)

For a general background metric this equation cannot be solved analytically. Then, we will apply a standard patching method to obtain approximate analytical solutions, to be presented in the next section.

II.1
General solution in the hydrodynamic limit

In this section we are going to consider approximate solutions for Eq. (
8
) considering three different particular cases, following Refs.
deBoer:2008gu
;
Tong:2012nf
;
Edalati:2012tc
;
Giataganas:2018ekx
:

A) Near the horizon (IR);

B) Hydrodynamic limit:
\omega\to 0
;

C) Far from the horizon (UV).

Then, we will match these expressions to get an approximate solution for the Eq. (
8
) near UV, but keeping essential data from the IR and the hydrodynamic limit. This is useful to describe the motion of the string endpoint simulating the stochastic behavior.

First, we consider the solution for region A:

\displaystyle h_{A\omega}(r)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{{A_{1}(\omega)}}{\sqrt{g_{xx}(r_{h})}}\left[1-i\frac{\omega}{f^{\prime}(r_{h})}\sqrt{\frac{b(r_{h})}{a(r_{h})}}\log\left(\frac{r}{r_{h}}-1\right)\right]

(9)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{{A_{1}(\omega)}}{\sqrt{g_{xx}(r_{h})}}\left[1-i\frac{\omega}{4\pi T}\log\left(\frac{r}{r_{h}}-1\right)\right]

(10)

where
{A_{1}(\omega)}
is a normalization factor. This solution is obtained in Appendix
A
.

Now we solve the equation of motion for the hydrodynamic limit which is a general solution in
r
but only up to order
\omega
. In this case one can neglect the term proportional to
\omega^{2}
in Eq. (
8
)

\frac{d}{dr}\left(\frac{g_{xx}\sqrt{g_{tt}}}{\sqrt{g_{rr}}}\frac{dh_{\omega}}{dr}\right)=0,

(11)

which implies that we are considering the condition

\displaystyle\omega^{2}\ll\frac{\sqrt{g_{tt}(r)}}{g_{xx}(r)\sqrt{g_{rr}(r)}}=\frac{\sqrt{a(r)}f(r)}{\sqrt{b(r)}c(r)}\,.

(12)

In general, close to the boundary we can take the metric coefficients as monomials

a(r)\sim r^{a_{t}}\,,\,b(r)\sim r^{a_{r}}\,,\,c(r)\sim r^{a_{x}}\,.

Using the condition in Eq. (
12
) in the UV (
r\to r_{b}
) one gets the constraint

\displaystyle a_{t}-a_{r}-2a_{x}\geq 0\,,

(13)

which is saturated by the asymptotic AdS case with
a(r)
,
c(r)
and
1/b(r)
going to
r^{2}
as
r\to\infty
. Note that in the IR,
r\to r_{h}+\delta
, where
\delta
is a small finite positive quantity (
\delta>0
) usually called the “stretched horizon” (see for instance

deBoer:2008gu
and references therein), the condition Eq. (
12
) implies

\displaystyle\omega^{2}\ll\frac{4\pi T}{g_{xx}(r_{h})}(r-r_{h})=\frac{4\pi T}{g_{xx}(r_{h})}\delta\,.

(14)

This is the hydrodynamic limit considered from now on in this work.

Solving Eq. (
11
), we obtain

\displaystyle h^{B}_{\omega}(r)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle B_{1}(\omega)\int^{r}\frac{\sqrt{g_{rr}}}{g_{xx}\sqrt{g_{tt}}}dr^{\prime}+B_{2}(\omega)

(15)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle B_{1}(\omega)\int^{r}\frac{\sqrt{b(r^{\prime})}}{g_{xx}(r^{\prime})f(r^{\prime})\sqrt{a(r^{\prime})}}dr^{\prime}+B_{2}(\omega)\,,

(16)

where
{B_{1}(\omega)}
and
{B_{2}(\omega)}
are integration constants with respect to
r
but are functions of
\omega
. This can be seen as the general solution up to order
\omega^{2}
of the equation of motion.

One can approximate the integral above in the IR using the fact that
f(r)
has a simple zero at the horizon. Then we can write

\displaystyle h_{\omega\rm(IR)}^{B}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{b(r_{h})}B_{1}(\omega)}{g_{xx}(r_{h})\sqrt{a(r_{h})}}\int^{r}\frac{1}{f(r^{\prime})}dr^{\prime}+B_{2}(\omega)

(17)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{B_{1}(\omega)}{4\pi Tg_{xx}(r_{h})}\log\left(\frac{r}{r_{h}}-1\right)+B_{2}(\omega)\,.

(18)

In order to obtain the functions
{B_{1}(\omega)}
and
{B_{2}(\omega)}
, we compare this expression with the solution Eq. (
9
) for the deep IR region:

B_{1}(\omega)=-i{A_{1}(\omega)}\omega\sqrt{g_{xx}(r_{h})},\quad B_{2}(\omega)=\frac{{A_{1}(\omega)}}{\sqrt{g_{xx}(r_{h})}}\,,

(19)

where
{A_{1}(\omega)}
can be determined by a convenient normalization.

For the case far from the horizon (case C: UV region) we use the solution for case B (
15
)

\displaystyle h_{\omega}^{C}(r)\approx h^{B}_{\omega(\rm UV)}(r)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle B_{1}(\omega)\int^{r}_{r_{h}+\epsilon}\frac{\sqrt{b(r^{\prime})}}{g_{xx}(r^{\prime})f(r^{\prime})\sqrt{a(r^{\prime})}}dr^{\prime}+B_{2}(\omega)

(20)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{{A_{1}(\omega)}}{\sqrt{g_{xx}(r_{h})}}\left(1-i\omega g_{xx}(r_{h})\int^{r}_{r_{h}+\epsilon}\frac{\sqrt{b(r^{\prime})}}{g_{xx}(r^{\prime})f(r^{\prime})\sqrt{a(r^{\prime})}}dr^{\prime}\right).

(21)

In the last expression we are interested mainly in the asymptotic behavior of
h_{\omega}^{C}(r)
for large
r
.

This solution will be used to describe the motion of the string close to the boundary brane
r\to r_{b}
. Please note that this solution is valid in the regime of small frequencies, in particular, it can be seen as an expansion for
\omega/T\ll 1
. This becomes clear when we notice that the integral in Eq. (
20
) is dominated by its near horizon part (
r=r_{h}
) since
f(r)
has a simple zero in this region (
f(r)\approx f^{\prime}(r_{h})(r-r_{h})
), while
a(r)
and
b(r)
are regular functions at
r=r_{h}
. Using these facts one can approximate Eq. (
20
) by

\displaystyle h^{B}_{\omega(UV)}(r\sim r_{b})

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{A_{1}(\omega)}{\sqrt{g_{xx}(r_{h})}}\left[1-i\frac{\omega\sqrt{b(r_{h})}}{f^{\prime}(r_{h})\sqrt{a(r_{h})}}\log\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)-i\,\Xi-i\omega\,\Omega(r_{b})\right]

(22)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{A_{1}(\omega)}{\sqrt{g_{xx}(r_{h})}}\left[1-i\frac{\omega}{4\pi T}\log\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)-i\,\Xi-i\omega\,\Omega(r_{b})\right]\,,

(23)

where
\Xi
and
\Omega(r_{b})
are functions resulting from the integral in Eq. (
20
), which depends on geometry. The function
\Xi
is associated with intermediate values of
r
which is subdominant with respect to the log term and therefore will be ignored in the following. On the other side,
\Omega(r_{b})
depends on the asymptotic behavior of the metric functions for large
r
.
Close to the boundary, the metric coefficients behave as

a(r)\sim r^{a_{t}}\,,\,b(r)\sim r^{a_{r}}\,,\,c(r)\sim r^{a_{x}}\,
, so that the constraint, Eq. (
13
), implies that
\Omega(r_{b})
vanishes in the limit
r_{b}\to\infty
. In particular, if the metric under consideration is asymptotically AdS, then
a(r)
,
c(r)
and
1/b(r)
go to
r^{2}
as
r\to r_{b}
, so that
\Omega(r_{b})\sim 1/r_{b}^{3}
, which is very small for large
r_{b}
.

III
Admittance and diffusion coefficient

In this section we proceed to calculate the admittance from which we find the diffusion coefficient. The admittance is the linear response of the system to an external force. Thus, first we need to introduce an small external force acting on the boundary particle. With this purpose, we turn on an electromagnetic potential
A_{\mu}
on the UV brane. This will not change the bulk dynamics but will introduce an external force to the string endpoint. The linearized Nambu-Goto action, Eq.
(
6
),
then becomes (with unit charge)

\displaystyle S=-\frac{1}{4\pi\alpha^{\prime}}\int dtdr\left[\;\dot{X}^{2}\frac{g_{xx}\sqrt{g_{rr}}}{\sqrt{g_{tt}}}-X^{\prime 2}\frac{g_{xx}\sqrt{g_{tt}}}{\sqrt{g_{rr}}}\right]+\int dt\left(A_{t}+\vec{A}\cdot\vec{\dot{x}}\right)\Big|_{r=r_{b}}\,.\quad

(24)

From this expression we get a modified equation of motion
in the brane position
given by

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{g_{tt}(r_{b})}}{\sqrt{g_{rr}(r_{b})}}g_{xx}(r_{b})X^{\prime}|_{r=r_{b}}-2\pi\alpha^{\prime}F=0\,,

(25)

with
F=\partial_{x}A_{t}-\partial_{t}A_{x}
.
Since
X^{\prime}=\partial_{r}X
and
X(t,r)=e^{i\omega t}h^{C}_{\omega}(r)
,
we can write from Eq. (
20
)

\displaystyle F(\omega)=\frac{-i\omega{A_{1}(\omega)}\sqrt{g_{xx}(r_{h})}}{2\pi\alpha^{\prime}}f(r_{b})\approx\frac{-i\omega{A_{1}(\omega)}\sqrt{g_{xx}(r_{h})}}{2\pi\alpha^{\prime}}.

(26)

Note that for this regime of small frequencies and large string energy (large
r_{b}
,
f(r_{b})\approx 1
) the force on the particle depends only on the IR structure of the metric.
Then, the admittance can be written as

\displaystyle\chi(\omega)=\frac{h^{C}_{\omega}(r_{b})}{F(\omega)}=2\pi\alpha^{\prime}\frac{\left(1-i\omega g_{xx}(r_{h})\int^{r_{b}}\frac{\sqrt{b(r^{\prime})}}{g_{xx}(r^{\prime})f(r^{\prime})\sqrt{a(r^{\prime})}}dr^{\prime}\right)}{-i\omega g_{xx}(r_{h})}\,.

(27)

So, its imaginary part is

\displaystyle\Im{\chi(\omega)}=\frac{2\pi\alpha^{\prime}}{\omega\,g_{xx}(r_{h})}\,.

(28)

The results expressed in Eqs. (
27
)-(
28
) are valid for any metric in the form of Eq. (
1
) in the regime of small frequencies, as proposed in Ref.
Giataganas:2018ekx
.

From the response function
\chi(\omega)
, Eq. (
27
), we can calculate the diffusion coefficient
D
. It is given by
Tong:2012nf
:

\displaystyle D=\frac{1}{\beta}\lim_{\omega\to 0}(-i\omega\chi(\omega))=\frac{2\pi\alpha^{\prime}}{\beta g_{xx}(r_{h})}=\frac{2\pi\alpha^{\prime}T}{g_{xx}(r_{h})},

(29)

where
\beta=1/T
. We can rewrite this result just in terms of the metric using (
4
) as

D=\frac{\alpha^{\prime}|f^{\prime}(r_{h})|}{2g_{xx}(r_{h})}\sqrt{\frac{a(r_{h})}{b(r_{h})}}\,.

(30)

At this stage we have found the linear response and the diffusion coefficient as functions of the black hole radius
r_{h}
. Also we have the temperature as function of the same parameter, so in principle we can invert the equations to get results as functions of temperature. On the other hand, as temperature depends of different parts of the metric compared with for example admittance we can not write at this point a general expression for it as function of temperature. We will need then to specialize to some particular model to get such relation.

Before we conclude this section, let us comment that the real part of the admittance
\chi(\omega)
, Eq. (
27
), is given by

\displaystyle\Re\chi(\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\pi\alpha^{\prime}\int^{r_{b}}_{r_{h}+\epsilon}\frac{\sqrt{b(r^{\prime})}}{g_{xx}(r^{\prime})f(r^{\prime})\sqrt{a(r^{\prime})}}dr^{\prime}

(31)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha^{\prime}}{2Tg_{xx}(r_{h})}\log\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right),

(32)

where we considered the approximation that the integral is dominated by the near horizon (IR) region, as discussed after Eq. (
22
). Note that this expression is independent of the frequencies, at least in the hydrodynamical limit
\omega\to 0
. This result is in consonance with Kramers-Kronig relations such that the real part of the admittance is an even function in
\omega
.

IV
Correlation functions

The mean square displacement is a measure of the variance of the random walk of the particle in the thermal bath from the motion of the probe string. In the following, we are going to calculate in general grounds this quantity using the results from previous sections.

First, in order to obtain the mean square displacement one needs to impose ingoing and outgoing boundary conditions near the horizon

\displaystyle h^{IR}_{\omega}(r)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{{A(\omega)}}{\sqrt{g_{xx}(r_{h})}}\left[e^{i\omega r_{*}}+B(\omega)e^{-i\omega r_{*}}\right]

(33)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{{A(\omega)}}{\sqrt{g_{xx}(r_{h})}}\left[e^{i\frac{\omega}{4\pi T}\log\left(\frac{r}{r_{h}}-1\right)}+B(\omega)e^{-i\frac{\omega}{4\pi T}\log\left(\frac{r}{r_{h}}-1\right)}\right]\,.

(34)

In the UV region the solution for small
\omega
considering these modes is

\displaystyle h^{UV}_{\omega}(r)=\frac{{A(\omega)}}{\sqrt{g_{xx}(r_{h})}}\left[\left(1-i\omega g_{xx}(r_{h})\int^{r}_{r_{h}+\epsilon}\frac{\sqrt{b(r^{\prime})}}{g_{xx}(r^{\prime})f(r^{\prime})\sqrt{a(r^{\prime})}}dr^{\prime}\right)\right.

(35)

\displaystyle\left.+B(\omega)\left(1+i\omega g_{xx}(r_{h})\int^{r}_{r_{h}+\epsilon}\frac{\sqrt{b(r^{\prime})}}{g_{xx}(r^{\prime})f(r^{\prime})\sqrt{a(r^{\prime})}}dr^{\prime}\right)\right]\,.

(36)

where the coefficients
{A(\omega)}
and
{B(\omega)}
are the same as in the IR region.

Matching the UV solution with the Neumann boundary condition in
r=r_{b}
, one obtains that coefficient the
{B(\omega)}
is a pure phase
e^{i\omega\theta}
(see the Appendix
B
). On the other side imposing Neumann b.c. at the IR one obtains a discretization of the frequencies as

\displaystyle\Delta\omega=\frac{\pi f^{\prime}(r_{h})}{\log\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)}\sqrt{\frac{a(r_{h})}{b(r_{h})}}=\frac{4\pi^{2}T}{\log\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)}\,,

(37)

analogous to the result found in
deBoer:2008gu
. For details, see the Appendix
B
.

IV.1
Grand canonical ensemble and correlation functions

In the grand canonical ensemble, the density operator is defined as

\rho_{0}=\frac{e^{-\beta(\sum_{\omega>0}\omega a_{\omega}^{\dagger}a_{\omega}-\mu a_{\omega}^{\dagger}a_{\omega}})}{\text{Tr}\left(\,e^{-\beta(H-\mu N)}\right)}\,,

(38)

where
a_{\omega}^{\dagger}
and
a_{\omega}
are the usual creation and annihilation operators which satisfy

\displaystyle\langle a^{\dagger}_{\omega}a_{\omega}\rangle=\frac{\delta_{\omega\omega^{\prime}}}{e^{\beta\left(\omega-\mu\right)}\pm 1};\qquad\langle a^{\dagger}_{\omega_{n}}a^{\dagger}_{\omega}\rangle=0;\qquad\langle a_{\omega}a_{\omega}\rangle=0\,.

(39)

Note that the plus (minus) sign corresponds to the fermionic (bosonic) case.
With these operators, one can write down the solution for the equation of motion near the boundary

\displaystyle X(t,r)=\sum_{\omega>0}\left(a_{\omega}h_{\omega}^{UV}(r)e^{-i\omega t}+a_{\omega}^{\dagger}(h_{\omega}^{UV}(r))^{*}e^{i\omega t}\right)\,,

(40)

where
we used explicitly the quantization of the frequencies obtained in the previous section. Then, the two point function for the string endpoint reads

\displaystyle\langle x(t)x(0)\rangle

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\langle X(t,r_{b})X(0,r_{b})\rangle

(41)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\omega>0}\sum_{\omega^{\prime}>0}\Big(\frac{h^{UV*}_{\omega}(r_{b})h^{UV}_{\omega^{\prime}}(r_{b})e^{i\omega t}+h^{UV}_{\omega}(r_{b})h^{UV*}_{\omega^{\prime}}(r_{b})e^{-i\omega t}}{e^{\beta\left(\omega-\mu\right)}\pm 1}

(43)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+h^{UV}_{\omega}(r_{b})h^{UV*}_{\omega^{\prime}}(r_{b})e^{-i\omega t}\Big)\delta_{\omega\omega^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\omega>0}|h^{UV}_{\omega}(r_{b})|^{2}\Big(\frac{2\cos(\omega t)}{e^{\beta\left(\omega-\mu\right)}\pm 1}+e^{-i\omega t}\Big).

(44)

Substituting in the above equation the expression for
h_{\omega}^{UV}(r_{b})
given by (
35
), taking into account the fact that
A(\omega)\sim\omega^{-1/2}
from Eq. (
171
), and disregarding terms of order
\omega^{2}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\langle x(t)x(0)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{4\pi^{2}\alpha^{\prime}T}{g_{xx}(r_{h})\log\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)}\sum_{\omega>0}\frac{1}{\omega}\left(\frac{2\cos(\omega t)}{e^{\beta\left(\omega-\mu\right)}\pm 1}+e^{-i\omega t}\right)\,.

(45)

Considering the approximation

\displaystyle d\omega\sim\Delta\omega=\frac{4\pi^{2}T}{\log\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)},

one can rewrite

\displaystyle\langle x(t)x(0)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha^{\prime}}{g_{xx}(r_{h})}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega}{\omega}\left(\frac{2\cos(\omega t)}{e^{\beta\left(\omega-\mu\right)}\pm 1}+e^{-i\omega t}\right)

(46)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle x(0)x(t)\rangle^{*}\,.

(48)

Analogously, one can obtain

\displaystyle\langle x(t)x(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle X(t,r_{b})X(t,r_{b})\rangle

(49)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\omega>0}|h^{UV}_{\omega}(r_{b})|^{2}\Big(\frac{2}{e^{\beta(\omega-\mu)}\pm 1}+1\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha^{\prime}}{g_{xx}(r_{h})}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega}{\omega}\left(\frac{2}{e^{\beta(\omega-\mu)}\pm 1}+1\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle x(0)x(0)\rangle\,.

(51)

Note that in Eqs. (
41
)- (
49
) the sums and integrals span over all positive frequencies
\omega
. On the other side,
the modes present in

|h^{UV}_{\omega}(r_{b})|^{2}
are the first terms of an expansion for
\omega/T\ll 1
, as discussed after Eq.(
20
). As we show in Appendix
C
,
when we substitute
|h^{UV}_{\omega}(r_{b})|^{2}
by Eq. (
35
) in the above equations, the frequencies

\omega\gg T
are exponentially suppressed. Then, it is a good approximation to keep only the first terms in an expansion of
\omega/T
for
|h^{UV}_{\omega}(r_{b})|^{2}
.

V
Fluctuation-dissipation theorem

In this Section, we are going to verify the consistency of our results with the fluctuation-dissipation theorem, using the admittance obtained in Section
III
and the correlation functions in Section
IV
.

Starting from the correlation functions
\langle x(t)x(0)\rangle
and
\langle x(0)x(t)\rangle
, one can define a symmetric Green’s function as:

G_{\rm Sym}(t)\equiv\frac{1}{2}\left(\langle x(t)x(0)\rangle+\langle x(0)x(t)\rangle\right)\,.

(52)

The fluctuation-dissipation theorem in the presence of a chemical potential for the bosonic
Zubarev
and fermionic
Markov:2009ue
cases, can be written as

\displaystyle G_{\rm Sym}^{B,F}(t)=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left[\left(1+2n_{B,F}\right)\Im\chi(\omega)\right],

(53)

where
\mathcal{F}^{-1}[\cdots]
denotes the inverse Fourier transform,
n_{B,F}
are the Bose-Einstein and Fermi-Dirac distributions, and
\Im\chi(\omega)
is the imaginary part of the admittance. Note that the frequencies in this equation are positive physical quantities.

Then, one can rewrite of the r.h.s of equation (
53
) as Fourier transforms as

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left[\left(1+2n_{B,F}\right)\Im\chi(\omega)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega\left(1+\frac{2}{e^{\beta(|\omega|-\mu)}\pm 1}\right)\Im\chi(\omega)e^{i\omega t}

(54)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha^{\prime}}{g_{xx}(r_{h})}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega}{|\omega|}\left(1+\frac{2}{e^{\beta(|\omega|-\mu)}\pm 1}\right)e^{i\omega t}\,,

(55)

where we used the admittance, Eq. (
28
), and
the plus (minis) sign represents fermions (bosons).

On the other hand, from the correlation functions, Eqs. (
46
), the l.h.s. of Eq. (
52
) becomes

\displaystyle G_{\rm Sym}^{B,F}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha^{\prime}}{g_{xx}(r_{h})}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega}{\omega}\left(\frac{4\cos(\omega t)}{e^{\beta\left(\omega-\mu\right)}\pm 1}+e^{-i\omega t}+e^{i\omega t}\right)

(56)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha^{\prime}}{g_{xx}(r_{h})}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega}{|\omega|}\left(\frac{2\left(e^{-i\omega t}+e^{i\omega t}\right)}{e^{\beta\left(|\omega|-\mu\right)}\pm 1}+e^{-i\omega t}+e^{i\omega t}\right).

(57)

Since

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega f(|\omega|)e^{i\omega t}+\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega f(|\omega|)e^{-i\omega t}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega f(|\omega|)e^{i\omega t}\,,

(58)

the Eq.(
57
) becomes

\displaystyle G_{\rm Sym}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha^{\prime}}{g_{xx}(r_{h})}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega}{|\omega|}\left(\frac{2}{e^{\beta\left(|\omega|-\mu\right)}\pm 1}+1\right)e^{i\omega t}\,,

(59)

which coincides with Eq. (
54
). Therefore, it completes the check of our calculations with the fluctuation-dissipation theorem.

VI
Mean square displacements

From the results of Section
IV
, one can calculate the regularized expression for the mean square displacement as:

s^{2}_{\rm reg}(t)=\langle:[x(t)-x(0)]^{2}:\rangle\equiv\langle:[X(t,r_{b})-X(0,r_{b})]^{2}:\rangle\,,

(60)

where the double dots notation
:[\cdots]:
means that we are using normal ordering.
Explicitly,

\displaystyle s^{2}_{\rm reg}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha^{\prime}}{g_{xx}(r_{h})}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega}{\omega}\frac{4(1-\cos(\omega t))}{e^{\beta(\omega-\mu)}\pm 1}

(61)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{8\alpha^{\prime}}{g_{xx}(r_{h})}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega}{\omega}\frac{\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\omega t}{2}\right)}{e^{\beta(\omega-\mu)}\pm 1}.

(62)

In the following, we are going to calculate the regularized mean square displacement in various interesting cases.

VI.1
Zero chemical potential

In this section, we study the particular case of
zero chemical potential from previous results. This is interesting, for instance, in models which describe the superfluid Bose-Mott insulator transition
Zaanen:2015oix
and massless Dirac fermions in graphene
novoselov
;
moriconi
.

For zero chemical potential, the mean square displacement, Eq. (
61
), reads

\displaystyle s^{2}_{\rm reg}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{8\alpha^{\prime}}{g_{xx}(r_{h})}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega}{\omega}\frac{\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\omega t}{2}\right)}{e^{\beta\omega}\pm 1}\,.

(63)

This expression is valid for the bosonic
(-)
and fermionic
(+)
cases. In the following we discuss separately these two cases.

VI.1.1
Bosons

To solve the integral (
63
) for the bosonic case
(-)
, we follow
Caldeira:2020sot
and find

\displaystyle s^{2}_{\rm reg}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2\alpha^{\prime}}{g_{xx}(r_{h})}\log\left(\frac{\sinh(\frac{t\pi}{\beta})}{\frac{t\pi}{\beta}}\right)\,.

(64)

For the short time approximation
t\ll\beta
, we have

\displaystyle s^{2}_{\rm reg}(t)\approx\frac{\pi^{2}\alpha^{\prime}}{3g_{xx}(r_{h})}\frac{t^{2}}{\beta^{2}}\,,

(65)

which is the expected result for the ballistic regime.
Considering now the approximation for large times
t\gg\beta
, one finds

\displaystyle s^{2}_{\rm reg}(t)\approx\frac{2\pi\alpha^{\prime}}{g_{xx}(r_{h})}\frac{t}{\beta}\,,

(66)

which is the standard diffusion result.

VI.1.2
Fermions

Now, considering the fermionic case
(+)
corresponding to the integral (
63
), we have

\displaystyle s^{2}_{\rm reg}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{8\alpha^{\prime}}{g_{xx}(r_{h})}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega}{\omega}\frac{\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\omega t}{2}\right)}{e^{\beta\omega}+1}\,.

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2\alpha^{\prime}}{g_{xx}(r_{h})}\log\left(\frac{\frac{t\pi}{2\beta}}{\tanh\left(\frac{\pi t}{2\beta}\right)}\right)\,.

(67)

In the limit
t\ll\beta
(short times) one can approximate this expression by

\displaystyle s^{2}_{\rm reg}(t)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{\pi^{2}\alpha^{\prime}}{6g_{xx}(r_{h})}\frac{t^{2}}{\beta^{2}}\,,

(68)

in agreement with the well known result for the ballistic regime.

For large times, or
t\gg\beta
, we have that

\displaystyle s^{2}_{\rm reg}(t)

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\frac{2\alpha^{\prime}}{g_{xx}(r_{h})}\log\left(\frac{\pi t}{2\beta}\right)\,,

(69)

which is a Sinai-like subdiffusive regime
sinai-like
.

VI.2
Finite chemical potential

In the case of a finite chemical potential the mean square displacement is given by Eq. (
61
) which we repeat here for convenience

\displaystyle s^{2}_{\rm reg}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{8\alpha^{\prime}}{g_{xx}(r_{h})}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega}{\omega}\frac{\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\omega t}{2}\right)}{e^{\beta(\omega-\mu)}\pm 1}\,.

(70)

In the following, we specialize to the bosonic
(-)
and fermionic
(+)
cases.

VI.2.1
Bosons

In order to calculate the mean square displacement given by the above equation
for the bosonic case, we take
\mu<0
, and consider the series expansion

\frac{1}{e^{\beta(\omega-\mu)}-1}=\frac{e^{-\beta(\omega-\mu)}}{1-e^{-\beta(\omega-\mu)}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}e^{-\beta(\omega-\mu)(n+1)}\,.

(71)

Then,

s^{2}_{\rm Breg}(t)=\frac{8\alpha^{\prime}}{g_{xx}(r_{h})}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega}{\omega}e^{-\beta(\omega-\mu)n}\sin^{2}(\frac{\omega t}{2}).

(72)

Performing the integral, one gets

s^{2}_{\rm Breg}(t)=\frac{2\alpha^{\prime}}{g_{xx}(r_{h})}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}e^{\beta\mu n}\log\left(1+\frac{t^{2}}{n^{2}\beta^{2}}\right)\,.

(73)

This expression can be rewritten in a formal way as

\displaystyle s^{2}_{\rm Breg}(t)=\frac{2\alpha^{\prime}}{g_{xx}(r_{h})}\left\{2{\rm Li_{0}}^{(1,0)}\left(0,e^{\mu}\right)-e^{\mu}\left[{\Phi}^{(0,1,0)}\left(e^{\mu},0,1+i\frac{t}{\beta}\right)\right.\right.

(74)

\displaystyle\left.\left.+{\Phi}^{(0,1,0)}\left(e^{\mu},0,1-i\frac{t}{\beta}\right)\right]\right\}\,.

(75)

In this equation we used the following notation: Li
{}_{n}^{(1,0)}(x,y)
is the first derivative of the polylogarithm function of order
n
with respect to its first argument
x
;
\Phi^{(0,1,0)}(x,y,z)
is the first derivative of the Lerch transcendent function with respect to the second argument
y
.

First we consider
t\ll\beta
, which is the short time approximation, and then from Eq. (
73
), we find

\displaystyle s^{2}_{\rm Breg}(t)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{2\alpha^{\prime}}{g_{xx}(r_{h})}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{e^{\beta\mu n}}{n^{2}}\right)\frac{t^{2}}{\beta^{2}}=\frac{2\alpha^{\prime}}{g_{xx}(r_{h})}\text{Li}_{2}\left(e^{\beta\mu}\right)\frac{t^{2}}{\beta^{2}}\,.

(76)

In the right hand side of this equation we used the polylogarithm function of order 2,
\text{Li}_{2}\left(e^{\beta\mu}\right)
. This equation gives the usual ballistic behavior since it goes like
t^{2}
.

On the other side, in the late time approximation
t\gg\beta
the sum in Eq. (
73
) is dominated by its first term and we have

\displaystyle s^{2}_{\rm Breg}(t)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{4\alpha^{\prime}}{g_{xx}(r_{h})}e^{\beta\mu}\log\left(\frac{t}{\beta}\right)\,.

(77)

This equation corresponds to a subdiffusive regime which is due to the presence of the non-zero chemical potential in this case. This result is analogous to what was found in classical physical systems in
sinai-like
or in Lorentz invariant bosonic theories

Caldeira:2021izy
.

VI.2.2
Fermions

Now, we consider the regularized mean square displacement for the fermionic case. From Eq. (
61
), with
\mu>0
, we have

s^{2}_{\rm Freg}(t)=\frac{8\alpha^{\prime}}{g_{xx}(r_{h})}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega}{\omega}\left(\frac{\sin^{2}(\frac{\omega t}{2})}{e^{\beta(\omega-\mu)}+1}\right)\,.

(78)

In order to evaluate this integral we resort to the Sommerfeld expansion:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega}{\omega}\left(\frac{\sin^{2}(\frac{\omega t}{2})}{e^{\beta(\omega-\mu)}+1}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\mu}\frac{\sin^{2}(\frac{\omega t}{2})}{\omega}d\omega+\cdots

(79)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(-\text{Ci}(t\mu)+\log(t\mu)+\gamma)+\cdots,

(80)

where
\text{Ci}(z)
is the Cosine integral function,
\gamma
is Euler-Mascheroni constant and we disregarded terms of order
{1}/{\mu^{2}\beta^{2}}
and higher, since we are considering the low temperature regime,
\mu\beta=\frac{\mu}{T}\gg 1
.

The mean square displacement, Eq. (
78
), for small times
\mu t\ll 1
, can be approximated as

s^{2}_{\rm Freg}(t)\approx\frac{\alpha^{\prime}}{g_{xx}(r_{h})}\mu^{2}t^{2}\,.

(81)

This expression for
s^{2}
gives the well known ballistic regime
t^{2}
.

On the other hand, taking the large time approximation
\mu t\gg 1
in Eq. (
78
), we obtain

s^{2}_{\rm Freg}(t)\approx\frac{4\alpha^{\prime}}{g_{xx}(r_{h})}\log\left(t\mu\right),

(82)

which corresponds to a subdiffusive behavior
\log t
, as in the bosonic case discussed above. Note that this behavior also appears in classical set-ups as in Ref.
sinai-like
. In Ref.
Caldeira:2021izy
we found a similar fermionic behavior for a Lorentz invariant context.

.

VII
Hyperscaling violation at finite temperature for bosons and fermions

In this section, we apply the general results obtained in the previous parts of the text to a particular system, the Lifshitz-hyperscaling family of metrics:

\displaystyle ds^{2}=r^{-\frac{2\theta}{d}}\left(-r^{2z}f(r)dt^{2}+r^{2}d\vec{x}^{2}+\frac{dr^{2}}{f(r)r^{2}}\right),

(83)

where
z
and
\theta
are the Lifshitz (or dynamical) and hyperscaling violation parameters respectively, and
d
is the spacetime dimension. The horizon function is given by

\displaystyle f(r)=1-\left(\frac{r_{h}}{r}\right)^{d+z-\theta}.

(84)

This problem was studied by the authors in Refs.
Edalati:2012tc
and
Giataganas:2018ekx
for some particular cases without fermions. In our presentation we will discuss the Lifschitz-hyperscaling violation metrics at finite temperature for fermions and bosons at zero chemical potential.

The constraint in equation (
13
) applied for the hyperscaling-Lifshitz metric case becomes

\displaystyle\frac{2\theta}{d}+z+1\geq 0

(85)

It is also important to note as pointed in
Dong:2012se
that the null energy condition imposes additional constraints in the values of the parameters
\theta
and
z
, explicitly

\displaystyle(d-\theta)(d(z-1)-\theta)\geq 0,

(86)

\displaystyle(z-1)(d+z-\theta)\geq 0.

(87)

In order to have a positive specific heat we need to impose one more condition
Dong:2012se

\displaystyle\frac{d-\theta}{z}\geq 0\,.

(88)

Considering that the number
d
of spatial dimensions in the boundary is a positive number and imposing all these conditions results in the parameters assuming the possible values

\displaystyle(i)

\displaystyle\quad z\leq-3,\,\,\theta\geq-\frac{d}{2}(z+1),

(89)

\displaystyle(ii)

\displaystyle\quad-3<z<0,\,\,\theta\geq d,

(90)

\displaystyle(iii)

\displaystyle\quad 1\leq z<2,\,\,-\frac{d}{2}(z+1)\leq\theta\leq d(z-1),\,\,\text{or}\,\,\theta=d,

(91)

\displaystyle(iv)

\displaystyle\quad z\geq 2,\,\,-\frac{d}{2}(z+1)\leq\theta\leq d.

(92)

In this background, we then calculate the response function, the diffusion coefficient, and the mean square displacement from our general discussion of the previous sections. This will allow us to understand the independence of the diffusion process on different hyperscaling parameters, dynamical exponents, and dimensions for bosons and fermions. Also, it will be illustrative to explore some particular configurations of those quantities, presenting some cases in detail. Such Analysis is interesting since those types of gravitational systems are relevant as holographic duals for some condensed matter systems. In some works, for example, they are seen as possessing Fermi surfaces in the boundary theory such as strange metals
Huijse:2011ef
;
Zaanen:2015oix
;
Hartnoll:2018xxg
;
Gubser:2000mm
;
Dong:2012se
;
Ogawa:2011bz
;
Kachru:2008yh
. In other cases, they were used to study the behavior of some (bosonic) theories close to critical point
Edalati:2012tc
;
Tong:2012nf
.

The horizon function Eq. (
84
) implies that the Hawking temperature reads

\displaystyle T=\frac{\left|d+z-\theta\right|}{4\pi}\,r_{h}^{z}\;.

(93)

Then, we can write the admittance from Eq. (
28
) as

\displaystyle\Im{\chi}(\omega)=\frac{2\pi\alpha^{\prime}}{\omega}\left(\frac{4\pi}{\left|d+z-\theta\right|}\right)^{-{2(d-\theta)}/{zd}}T^{-{2(d-\theta)}/{zd}}.

(94)

Note that the condition (
88
) implies a negative exponent for the temperature dependence of the imaginary part of the admittance, which is the usual behavior for holographic systems such as the ones investigated in Ref.
deBoer:2008gu
.

Now, using Eq. (
29
), the diffusion coefficient for this model is

\displaystyle D=2\pi\alpha^{\prime}\left(\frac{4\pi}{\left|d+z-\theta\right|}\right)^{-{2(d-\theta)}/{zd}}T^{(-{2(d-\theta)+zd)}/{zd}}.

(95)

It is interesting to note that this diffusion coefficient could increase or decrease with the temperature depending on the choice of the parameters
z,d,\theta
.

In the particular case where
d=\theta
one has the usual Einstein diffusion proportional to
T
. On the other side, for
d-\theta=zd
one has the diffusion coefficient proportional to
T^{-1}
. This behavior was also found in Ref.
deBoer:2008gu
for pure AdS space. These cases are thermodynamically stable accordingly to the condition given by Eq. (
88
).

VII.1
Bosons

The regularized mean square displacement for the bosonic case without chemical potential (
\mu=0
), Eq. (
64
),
on a Lifshitz and hyperscaling violation metric reduces to

\displaystyle s^{2}_{\rm Breg}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{2\alpha^{\prime}}{\left(\frac{4\pi T}{|d+z-\theta|}\right)^{-{2(d-\theta)}/{zd}}}\log\left(\frac{\sinh({Tt\pi})}{{Tt\pi}}\right)\,.

(96)

For short times one reobtains the ballistic regime

\displaystyle s^{2}_{\rm Breg}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2\alpha^{\prime}}{3}{\left(\frac{4\pi T}{|d+z-\theta|}\right)^{-{2(d-\theta)}/{zd}}}(Tt\pi)^{2}\,

(97)

and for long times we find

\displaystyle s^{2}_{\rm Breg}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{2\alpha^{\prime}}{\left(\frac{4\pi T}{|d+z-\theta|}\right)^{-\frac{2}{d}\frac{d-\theta}{z}}}\left(Tt\pi\right)

(98)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{2\pi\alpha^{\prime}}{\left(\frac{4\pi}{|d+z-\theta|}\right)^{-\frac{2}{d}\frac{d-\theta}{z}}}\left(T\right)^{-\frac{2}{d}\frac{d-\theta}{z}+1}\,t

(99)

which is the usual diffusive behavior in time.

VII.2
Fermions

Now, we are going to analyze the fermionic case with zero chemical potential within a Lifshitz and hypescaling violation metric. So, here we will calculate the mean square displacement for this system. We get from Eq. (
68
) that for short times one has

\displaystyle s^{2}_{\rm Freg}(t)\approx\frac{2\alpha^{\prime}}{g_{xx}(r_{h})}\frac{t^{2}}{\beta^{2}}=2\alpha^{\prime}\left(\frac{4\pi T}{|d+z-\theta|}\right)^{-{2(d-\theta)}/{zd}}\frac{t^{2}}{\beta^{2}}\,,

(100)

which is the typical ballistic behavior while for long times, in turn, from Eq. (
69
), one finds

\displaystyle s^{2}_{\rm Freg}(t)

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\frac{2\alpha^{\prime}}{g_{xx}(r_{h})}\log\left(\frac{\pi t}{2\beta}\right)=2\alpha^{\prime}\left(\frac{4\pi T}{|d+z-\theta|}\right)^{-{2(d-\theta)}/{zd}}\log\left(\frac{\pi t}{2\beta}\right)\,,

(101)

which corresponds to a fermionic subdiffusive regime analogous to the classical (Boltzmann) ones found in Ref.
sinai-like
.

Now, we are going to discuss two special cases:
\theta=d
, which reproduces the Einstein diffusion coefficient, and
\theta=d-1
, in which the admittance goes with the inverse temperature as in the pure AdS case, in the next two sections.

VII.3
The particular case
\theta=d

In this section, we discuss the Lifshitz and hyperscaling violation at zero chemical potential (
\mu=0
) presented above for the particular case of
\theta=d
.
In this case, the admittance, Eq. (
94
), is independent of the temperature

\displaystyle\chi(\omega)|_{\theta=d}=\frac{2\pi\alpha^{\prime}}{\omega}\,,

(102)

and the diffusion coefficient, Eq. (
95
), becomes

\displaystyle D|_{\theta=d}=2\pi\alpha^{\prime}T,

(103)

which is the usual expected behavior for Brownian motion, as obtained by Einstein in his original formulation of the problem.

VII.3.1
Bosons

The regularized mean square displacement for bosons, Eq. (
96
), in this case is given by

Bosons

From Eq. (

VIII
Charged dilatonic AdS black holes

Our objective here is provide an application of ours findings to a non zero chemical potential system. As an example, we consider the Gubser-Rocha model
Gubser:2009qt
;
Zaanen:2015oix
, which is a top-down construction from eleven to four spacetime dimensions for a charged dilatonic AdS black hole. In this model there are fermionic modes which indicate the presence of Fermi surfaces. The lagrangian in four dimensions is given by

\displaystyle{\cal L}=R-\frac{1}{4}e^{\phi/\sqrt{3}}F_{\mu\nu}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{\mu}\phi)^{2}-\frac{6}{L^{2}}\cosh\left(\frac{\phi}{\sqrt{3}}\right)\,,

(116)

and it is a particular case of an Einstein-Maxwell-dilaton system. The solution of the corresponding equations of motion are
Cvetic:1999xp
;
Gubser:2000mm

\displaystyle ds^{2}=\frac{r^{2}}{L^{2}}\left(\frac{Q}{r}+1\right)^{3/2}\left(-f(r)dt^{2}+d\vec{x}^{2}\right)+\frac{L^{2}dr^{2}}{r^{2}f(r)}\left(\frac{Q}{r}+1\right)^{-3/2},

(117)

\displaystyle A_{t}=\frac{\sqrt{3Q\tilde{\mu}}}{r+Q}-\frac{\sqrt{3Q\tilde{\mu}^{1/3}}}{L^{2/3}};\qquad\phi=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\ln\left(1+\frac{Q}{r}\right),\quad f=1-\frac{\tilde{\mu}L^{2}}{(r+Q)^{3}}.

(118)

In the above equations,
Q
is related to the black hole charge,
\tilde{\mu}>0
is related to the black hole mass and
L
is the AdS radius.
The black hole horizon is located at
r=(\tilde{\mu}L^{2})^{1/3}-Q\equiv r_{h}
.
Defining the new coordinate
\tilde{r}=r+Q
so that
\tilde{r}_{h}=r_{h}+Q
, then

f=1-\frac{\tilde{\mu}L^{2}}{{\tilde{r}}^{3}}\,;\qquad A_{t}=-\frac{\sqrt{3Q\tilde{\mu}^{1/3}}}{L^{2/3}}\left[1-\frac{\tilde{r}_{h}}{\tilde{r}}\right]\,,

(119)

which is similar to that of a Reissner-Nordstrom charged black hole with chemical potential given by

\mu=\frac{\sqrt{3Q\tilde{\mu}^{1/3}}}{L^{2/3}}\,.

(120)

The Hawking temperature, using the
r
coordinate defined in Eq. (
117
), is then given by

\displaystyle T=\frac{3}{4\pi L^{2}}\left(\tilde{\mu}L^{2}\right)^{1/6}\sqrt{r_{h}}

(121)

Then, the imaginary part of the admittance can be written from Eq. (
28
) as a function of the temperature as

\displaystyle\Im\chi(\omega)=\frac{3\alpha^{\prime}}{2\omega T}\left(\tilde{\mu}L^{2}\right)^{-1/3}=\frac{9\alpha^{\prime}Q}{2\omega T\mu^{2}L^{2}}\,,

(122)

while the real part from Eq. (
32
) is

\Re\chi\approx-{2\pi\alpha^{\prime}}/{3r_{b}^{3}}\,.

Note that the real part of the admittance is small compared with its
imaginary part since the former does not depend on the frequency
\omega
which is small.

The diffusion coefficient for this model, from Eq.(
29
), reads

\displaystyle D=\frac{9Q\alpha^{\prime}}{2\mu^{2}L^{2}}\,,

(123)

which is temperature independent and decreasing for a growing chemical potential.

VIII.1
Bosons

The bosonic case is characterized by imposing the condition on the chemical potential
\mu<0
. Then,
for short times
t\ll\beta
, Eq. (
76
) gives

\displaystyle s^{2}_{\rm Breg}(t)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{2\alpha^{\prime}}{g_{xx}(r_{h})}\text{Li}_{2}\left(e^{\beta\mu}\right)\frac{t^{2}}{\beta^{2}}=\frac{9\alpha^{\prime}Q}{2\pi\mu^{2}L^{2}T}\text{Li}_{2}\left(e^{\beta\mu}\right)\frac{t^{2}}{\beta^{2}}\,,

(124)

which decreases when
|\mu|
increases.

On the other side, for long times,
t\gg\beta
, from Eq.(
77
), one finds

\displaystyle s^{2}_{\rm Breg}(t)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{4\alpha^{\prime}}{g_{xx}(r_{h})}e^{\beta\mu}\log\left(\frac{t}{\beta}\right)=\frac{9\alpha^{\prime}Q}{\pi\mu^{2}L^{2}T}e^{\beta\mu}\log\left(\frac{t}{\beta}\right)\,.

(125)

Since
\mu<0
the diffusion also drops down when
|\mu|
grows.

VIII.2
Fermions

The fermionic case is described by
\mu>0
. So, for short times, from Eq.(
81
), we obtain:

s^{2}_{\rm Freg}(t)\approx\frac{\alpha^{\prime}}{g_{xx}(r_{h})}\mu^{2}t^{2}=\frac{9\alpha^{\prime}Q}{2\pi L^{2}T}t^{2}\,.

(126)

Note that if one keeps
Q
fixed, there is no dependence on chemical potential for this result.
On the other hand, for long times we can use the results in Eq. (
82
) to obtain:

s^{2}_{\rm Freg}(t)\approx\frac{4\alpha^{\prime}}{g_{xx}(r_{h})}\log\left(t\mu\right)=\frac{9\alpha^{\prime}Q}{\pi\mu^{2}L^{2}T}\log\left(t\mu\right),

(127)

which diminishes for growing

.

IX
Conclusions

In this work we presented some results regarding the dynamics of a particle in a thermal bath at finite density, in the linear regime, using holographic methods. For a general diagonal metric, we obtained expressions for linear response function, mean square displacement, correlations functions and diffusion coefficient, in terms of the metric elements. As we showed here, the mean square displacement differs for fermions and bosons. Also, we have verified the fluctuation-dissipation theorem for zero and non-zero chemical potentials for both statistics. As these results are presented in terms of the metric components, this is a quite general result.

Since we have a general relation between different metric elements and physical quantities as the admittance, the regularized mean square displacement and temperature, it is possible to choose particular backgrounds in order to produce systems with holographic duals that may have some desired physical behavior.

For all these results we see a dependence on the IR part of the metric in the direction of the electrical field on the brane, which acts as an external force for the system. This is quantified by the presence of
g_{xx}(r_{h})
in the general expressions.

For the admittance, we have found the hydrodynamic behavior
\omega^{-1}
of its imaginary part, in accordance with the literature
deBoer:2008gu
;
Tong:2012nf
;
Edalati:2012tc
;
Giataganas:2018ekx
. However, one finds different temperature behaviors with respect to different choices of the metrical components. Note also that the admittance does not depend on the statistics.

For the regularized mean square displacement for bosons we found, in the late times limit, that the effect of turning on the chemical potential makes the diffusion process slower. The usual behavior
s^{2}_{\text{reg}}\sim t
then becomes a Sinai-like diffusion,
s^{2}_{\text{reg}}\sim\log t
. On the other hand, for fermions, we always find
s^{2}_{\text{reg}}\sim\log t
irrespective the value of the chemical potential.

In the first application, we studied the Hyperscaling-Lifshitz background exploring different values for the parameters
z
and
\theta
for varied number of spatial dimensions
d
. It interesting to say that using this freedom of choice it was possible to obtain some interesting cases without violating the thermodynamic stability. For instance, the usual Einstein linear dependence of the temperature in the diffusion constant can be found, for a certain choice of parameters (
d=\theta
).

For the Gubser-Rocha model, Section
VIII
, our second application, we found that the diffusion coefficient does not depend on the temperature and it is inversely proportional to the chemical potential. For bosons, the regularized mean square displacement decreases when the absolute value of chemical potential increases. Also, this quantity is inversely proportional to the temperature. As expected, the presence of a non-null chemical potential makes the diffusion processes becomes slower. The same behavior is found for the fermionic case, but now
s^{2}_{\text{reg}}\sim\log t
and it is inversely proportional to the square of temperature.

We presented here calculations allowing us to determine the diffusion coefficient, the admittance and the mean square distance for fermions which are relevant for systems with Fermi surfaces and Fermi liquids.
We hope that the discussion presented here might help finding new applications for holographic systems, in particular for fermionic ones.

n=0,1,2,...
.
Note that this kind of integral is dominated by the frequencies
\omega<T
, where
T=1/\beta
is the temperature of the system.
Making the change of variables:

\omega\to\frac{x}{t}

the integral becomes

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dx}{t}h(x)\frac{g(x)}{e^{kx}\pm 1}\,,

(173)

with
k\equiv\beta/t
.
The function
\frac{g(x)}{e^{kx}\pm 1}
works like a “filter”, each maximum (minimum) chooses one specific value of
x
of the function
h(x)
. Those values are given by
x=n\pi
. For the
n
-th maximum (minimum)
P_{n}
we have:

\displaystyle P_{n}=\frac{\pm 1}{e^{kn\pi}\pm 1}.

(174)

For
x_{n}=n\pi\gg 1/k
we can approximate:

\displaystyle P_{n}\approx\pm e^{-kn\pi}.

(175)

This means that for large values of
x_{n}
the function
h(x)
is exponentially suppressed. In terms of frequencies we can write that:

\displaystyle x_{n}=\omega_{n}t\gg\frac{1}{k}=\frac{t}{\beta}\implies\omega_{n}\gg\frac{1}{\beta}=T

(176)

Thus, frequencies higher than the temperature are exponentially suppressed in the integral. Then, it is a good approximation to take frequencies smaller than the temperature
T
in the function
h(\omega)
in the integral.
In the case where the chemical potential is non-zero, the relevant integral is

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega h(\omega)\frac{g(\omega t)}{e^{\beta(\omega-\mu)}\pm 1}

(177)

after the change of variables

\displaystyle\omega\to y=t(\omega-\mu)

(178)

this integral becomes

\displaystyle\int_{-\mu t}^{\infty}\frac{dy}{t}h(y)\frac{g(y)}{e^{ky}\pm 1}

(179)

where
k\equiv\beta/t
. Then, in an analogous way we conclude that for

\displaystyle y_{n}=n\pi\gg\frac{1}{k}=\frac{t}{\beta}

(180)

\displaystyle\implies\omega_{n}-\mu\gg T

(181)

the function
h
is exponentially suppressed.

Note that in the case of fermions we have
\mu\geq 0
and then
\omega_{n}\geq\omega_{n}-\mu
making that frequencies satisfying

\displaystyle\omega_{n}\gg T

(182)

Become exponentially suppressed as well.

For bosons, in the limit of
|\mu|\ll\omega_{n}
or
|\mu|\ll T
we have that (
180
) implies that frequencies
\omega_{n}\gg T
are suppressed too. On the other hand, in the case of
|\mu|\gg T
,
z=e^{-\beta\mu}\gg 1
and we can write

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega h(\omega)\frac{g(\omega t)}{e^{\beta(\omega-\mu)}-1}=\frac{1}{z}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega h(\omega)\frac{g(\omega t)}{e^{\beta\omega}-\frac{1}{z}}\,,

(183)

from this we can use the same argument carried out after Eq. (
172
) and conclude that frequencies

\displaystyle\omega_{n}\gg T\,,

(184)

are essentially irrelevant for the integral.
