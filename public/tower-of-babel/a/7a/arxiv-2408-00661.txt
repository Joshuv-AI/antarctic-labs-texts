Neuromorphic detection and cooling of microparticle arrays
Yugang Ren, Benjamin Siegel, Ronghao Yin, Muddassar Rashid, James Millen

Abstract
Micro-objects levitated in a vacuum are an exciting platform for precision sensing due to their low dissipation motion and the potential for control at the quantum level. Arrays of such sensors would allow noise cancellation, directionality, increased sensitivity and in the quantum regime the potential to exploit correlation and entanglement. We use neuromorphic detection via a single event-based camera to record the motion of an array of levitated microspheres. We present a truly scalable method for arbitrary multiparticle control by implementing real-time feedback to cool the motion of three objects simultaneously, the first demonstration of neuromorphic sensing for real-time control at the microscale.

10^{10}

We use neuromorphic detection in a truly scalable method for the control of arbitrary particle arrays across a wide field-of-view. Neuromorphic sensors are highly efficient detectors which mimic neurobiological vision
Liao et al. 2021
. Dynamic vision sensors mimic retinal response

In this work, the number of objects we can simultaneously control is only limited by the number of output channels in our feedback electronics. This single-device method for cooling and controlling particle arrays is readily scalable due to the low data output of neuromorphic detection. Arrays of cooled micro-sensors will lead to enhanced sensitivity

We levitate arrays of charged
5\,\mu m
diameter silica microspheres in a linear Paul trap under vacuum conditions (see Methods) and record their motion using an event-based camers (EBC)
Ren et al. 2022
, see Fig.
1
(a). The charged particles form a stable array due to the coulomb repulsion between them. The EBC uses a neuromorphic sensor, which is an array of independent pixels featuring contrast detectors which output an event in response to light levels on the pixel crossing a user-defined threshold. Pixels which do not experience the required level-change output no signal, removing the data-redundancy present in conventional cameras, while allowing the full sensor to be used at all times, sometimes referred to as a dynamic region-of-interest. The EBC hardware bundles asynchronous events into equal-length frames, and uses filters to identify objects within its field-of-view, afterwhich a propritetary generic tracking algorithm (GTA) tracks the motion of each object independently. For a more detailed explanation in the context of tracking single particles see
Ren et al. 2022
.

The EBC allows the tracking of multiple objects with a high bandwidth and a linear scaling in data output with the number of tracked objects, as compared to a rapid increase in data volume with increased region-of-interest in a conventional camera. Tracking of a single particle using the entire field-of-view of the EBC used
\sim 100
kB s
-1
as compared to 64,800 kB s
-1
using a standard CMOS camera with the same sensor size.

In Fig.
1
(b) we show an example of a single neuromorphic sensor being used to detect 10 levitated particles simultaneously. The EBC identifies each object and tracks it in 2D (illustrated by the coloured box around each image) and assigns each one a stable identification number, allowing us to process the positional data of each particle independently.

Particles levitated by a Paul trap undergo harmonic motion. In Fig.
1
(c) we generate the power spectral density (PSD) for each particle in an array of four to analyze their motion in frequency space. Each particle has a different charge-to-mass ratio and is levitated in a different part of the confining field, meaning each particle has different frequency modes of oscillation, which are well separated under vacuum conditions. Below, we use this fact to independently cool the motion of multiple particles. Interactions between the particles are evident, as shown by the unlabelled peaks in Fig.
1
(c). The signal-to-noise for the different particles varies due to non-uniform illumination and varying coupling to electronic noise. All data in this work is taken at gas pressures of 2.0-

There are many reasons why it is desirable to control the energy of a levitated particle. Although the sensitivity of the levitated sensor does not change through cooling
Monteiro et al. 2020
, rapid damping of the motion increases the stability and measurement bandwidth of the system. By reducing the position fluctuations interactions between the particles can be minimized. Reduction of the particle energy to the ground-state of the levitating potential
Deli“’c et al. 2020
;
Magrini et al. 2021
opens up a toolbox of quantum control
Piotrowski et al. 2023a
and sensitivity enhancement
Ranjit et al. 2016
.

Cold damping is a feedback method whereby a force proportional to the velocity of an oscillator is applied to its motion. Depending on the phase of the feedback signal, this method can cause damping or amplification of the particle motion without adding additional noise, hence the terminology cold damping. In reality, input and output noise of the feedback electronics still limits cooling.

We process the position data from the EBC using a Field Programmable-Gate-Array (FPGA) to generate a feedback signal proportional to velocity, with variable gain and phase. This signal is filtered and applied to an electrode of the levitating apparatus, see Fig.
1
(a).

is the temperature associated with the mode being analyzed and
\Gamma_{\rm t}=\Gamma_{0}+\Gamma_{\rm fb}
is the total momentum damping rate on the particle’s motion, with
\Gamma_{0}

\displaystyle T_{\text{CoM}}=T_{0}\frac{\Gamma_{0}}{\Gamma_{\textrm{t}}}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{m\omega_{z}^{2}}{k_{B}}\frac{\Gamma_{\textrm{fb}}^{2}}{\Gamma_{\textrm{t}}}S_{\textrm{nn}},

(2)

where

The cooling depends on the phase
\phi

\displaystyle T_{\text{CoM}}=\frac{\Gamma_{0}T_{0}}{\Gamma_{0}+\Gamma_{\textrm{{fb}}}\mathrm{cos}\left(\phi+\phi_{0}\right)},

Finally, we combine the optimized

Simultaneous cooling of a microparticle array

Our neuromorphic imaging system gives us 2D readout of the motion of every object it identifies. We are able to process this signal, and make a feedback loop for each degree-of-freedom that is detected. Each feedback loop consists of a dedicated FPGA and set of analogue filters, and we are limited in our experiment to three loops in total. We stress that this is not a limitation of detection or processing power, simply the number of FPGA outputs available to us. For each degree-of-freedom the phase and gain of each feedback loop must be optimized, and filters must be set accordingly.

In Fig.
3
(a) we cool two orthogonal degrees-of-freedom (the
x-
and
z-
axes) of a single particle. We are sensitive to all three degrees-of-freedom due to the angle our imaging system makes to the principal axes of the trap. The geometry of our Paul trap allows the control of all degrees-of-freedom with a single electrode.

We optimize and fix the feedback parameters, then lower the background pressure to reduce
\Gamma_{0}
, hence lowering the temperature
T_{\textrm{CoM}}
. The temperature is limited not by our noise floor, but by imperfect filtering pumping energy from the feedback signal into the
y-
mode, since it is close in frequency to the
z-
mode. At low pressures this causes the particle to become unstable, preventing us from further lowering the pressure.

In Fig.

The cross-talk between modes can be minimized through better filtering, use of phase-locked-loops
Gao et al. 2024
, or further separation of the particle modes to enable more effective filtering.

Conclusions

We have presented a scalable method for the detection and control of arrays of levitated microparticles using a single neuromorphic detector. Neuromorphic imaging is ideally suited to this task, due to its natural affinity with detecting the motion of multiple objects and low data-transfer rate. The tracking speed is limited by the proprietary tracking algorithm of the EBC used in this work, but other work in our lab has achieved neuromorphic imaging at
>100\,
kHz frame-rates
Ren 2024
.

The work presented here can straightforwardly be extended to arrays of 10s of microparticles, with multi-channel FPGA systems with high-quality digital filters being a common tool in research labs. Paul traps are stable at low pressures
Bykov et al. 2019
, where the motional frequencies of levitated particles have sub-Hz linewidths
Pontin et al. 2020
, meaning that the naturally varying charge-to-mass ratio of charged nano- and micro-particles will enable single-particle control and cooling even for large arrays. It’s also worth mentioning that our method is suitable for any object which can carry a charge, unlike optical trapping with intense-beam detection which is limited to low-absorption dielectrics.

Since the motion of levitated sensors is well understood, simple machine-learning
Conangla et al. 2019
could be used to optimize all of the feedback parameters in an array, and optimal tracking algorithms used which have been shown to enable quantum-level control
Magrini et al. 2021
. When combined with the low power-consumption of neuromorphic imaging technology, and great progress in chip-scale particle levitation
Melo et al. 2024
, integrated devices containing arrays of quantum sensors are closer to being a reality.

Methods

Experimental setup

Fig.
4
illustrates the experimental setup surrounding our linear Paul trap. The trap consists of 4 parallel cylindrical trapping electrodes forming a square, with two coaxial cylindrical end-cap electrodes. The distance from trap centre to the surface of the
1\,\textrm{mm}
-diameter trapping electrodes is
r=1.15\,\textrm{mm}
, and one opposing-pair are driven with 360 V amplitude at 1 kHz. The two endcaps are
300\,\mu\textrm{m}
-diameter and separated by
2z_{0}=800\,\mu\textrm{m}
. The proximity of the electrodes to the centre of the trap mean that a voltage applied to either one will create a field with a significant component in all axes, enabling 3D control with a single electrode.

Laser-induced acoustic desorption (LIAD)
Nikkhou et al. 2021
is used to launch micro-particles into the trap at a pressure of
1\,
mbar and then lower the pressure to carry out the experiments presented in this manuscript. We typically trap particles of positive charge, ranging from
2\times 10^{3}\,\textrm{e}
to
2\times 10^{4}\,\textrm{e}
.

To image the particles, 18 mW of laser light of wavelength
520\,
nm is weakly focused
onto the array. The scattered light is imaged onto an event-based camera (Prophesee EVK1 -Gen3.1 VGA (camera sensor: Prophesee PPS3MVCD,
640\times 480
pixels)) using a long-working distance microscope. The camera is precisely calibrated using a method outlined in detail in
Ren et al. 2022
. When dealing with multiple particles, calibration is performed via displacing a translation stage on which our imaging system is mounted by a known amount.

Generation of feedback signals is described below, but a voltage is added to one endcap electrode to control the motion of the particles.

Figure 4:
Experimental setup for levitating, detecting and controlling arrays of microparticles. A pulsed laser beam is used to launch particles into the trap via LIAD
Nikkhou et al. 2021
. They are illuminated with a CW laser from above, and imaged onto an EBC from below. The EBC software runs on a PC, and the tracking algorithm outputs data to a series of FPGAs, which process the data to produce a feedback signal for each degree of freedom of each particle. Each signal is then filtered with analogue filters (not shown), and then all signals are summed together and drive one endcap electrode.

Data processing

A data pipeline is shown in Fig.
5
. The operation of the EBC is described in detail in
Ren et al. 2022
. The generic tracking algorithm (GTA) of the EBC outputs 2D position data for each object it detects. The EBC is communicated with via a Python script, which separates this 2D information into two 1D data streams for each object. The script passes each data stream to one of three FPGA systems (Red Pitaya STEMlab 125-14) to output the position of each particle which we can monitor. Code on the FPGA computes the velocity from the position data, and adds a variable gain and phase to the signal to generate the feedback signal.

Each feedback signal is filtered with an anaolgue filter to isolate each frequency component of motion: a high-pass filter for the
f_{z}
signal and a low-pass filter for the
f_{x}
signal. The filtered feedback signals are combined with a summing amplifier, and sent to one endcap electrode.

Clock synchronization of the FPGA system and the EBC is achieved via enabling the camera clock only.

Data pipeline for feedback control based on neuromorphic detection. Motion data of the levitated microparticles is first tracked by and EBC and then transferred to a computer. The computer streams the data to several FPGAs, which are used to generate feedback signals for particle control.
