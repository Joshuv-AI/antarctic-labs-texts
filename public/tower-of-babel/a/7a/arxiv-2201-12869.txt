A Two-Step Approach to Optimal Dynamic Pricing in Multi-Demand Combinatorial Markets
Kanstantsin Pashkovich and Xinyue Xie

Abstract
Online markets are a part of everyday life, and their rules are governed by algorithms. Assuming participants are inherently self-interested, well designed rules can help to increase social welfare. Many algorithms for online markets are based on prices: the seller is responsible for posting prices while buyers make purchases which are most profitable given the posted prices. To make adjustments to the market the seller is allowed to update prices at certain timepoints. Posted prices are an intuitive way to design a market. Despite the fact that each buyer acts selfishly, the seller’s goal is often assumed to be that of social welfare maximization. Berger, Eden and Feldman recently considered the case of a market with only three buyers where each buyer has a fixed number of goods to buy and the profit of a bought bundle of items is the sum of profits of the items in the bundle. For such markets, Berger et. al. showed that the seller can maximize social welfare by dynamically updating posted prices before arrival of each buyer. Bérczi, Bérczi-Kovács and Szögi showed that the social welfare can be maximized also when each buyer is ready to buy at most two items. We study the power of posted prices with dynamical updates in more general cases. First, we show that the result of Berger et. al. can be generalized from three to four buyers. Then we show that the result of Bérczi, Bérczi-Kovács and Szögi can be generalized to the case when each buyer is ready to buy up to three items. We also show that a dynamic pricing is possible whenever there are at most two allocations maximizing social welfare.

Introduction

We study the dynamic pricing that optimizes social welfare in a multi-demand combinatorial market. A
combinatorial market
consists of three components, namely,
items
,
buyers
, and
valuation functions
. In particular, we consider a market with a set
\mathcal{X}
of
m
items and a set
I
of
n

buyers
, also called
players
. The items are assumed to be heterogeneous and indivisible so that the buyers are not allowed to buy a fractional amount of items. Each buyer
i\in I
is associated with a valuation function
v_{i}:2^{\mathcal{X}}\to\mathcal{R}_{\geq 0}
, which maps subsets of items, also called
bundles
, to non-negative values. In particular, we focus on multi-demand valuation functions. In this framework, every player
i\in I
wants
k_{i}
items and the value of a bundle is the sum of the values of the
k_{i}

\{1,2,3\}
, with the value of each individual item given in Table

. Suppose that player 1 is bi-demand and players 2 and 3 are unit-demand, that is
k_{1}=2
and
k_{2}=k_{3}=1
.

Given a particular pricing of the items, we define the
utility
of a bundle with respect to a player to be the difference between its valuation and price. Of course, a player always picks a bundle that maximizes the utility. Such a bundle is referred to as a
bundle in demand
.
Suppose the four items in the example above are respectively assigned prices
1.5
,
0.1
,
0.5
and
0.9
. Then, for player 1, there are two bundles in demand, namely,
\{\alpha,\beta\}
and
\{\beta,\gamma\}
, that achieve the maximal utility of
1.4
.

The
social welfare
of a market is defined to be the sum of the valuations of the received bundles across all buyers. In the above example, there are also two allocations that maximize the social welfare. We can either assign
\{\alpha,\beta\}
to player 1,
\gamma
to player 2 and
\delta
to player 3; or assign
\{\beta,\gamma\}
to player 1,
\alpha
to player 2 and
\delta
to player 3. Both these allocations yield a social welfare of
5
.

Our goal is to find an optimal pricing so that while the buyers are free to pick any bundle that maximizes their utility, their choices simultaneously maximize the social welfare. We make the following assumptions. The valuation function of each buyer is publicly known. However, there are two unknowns in the problem that increase the difficulty for finding an optimal pricing. Firstly, the buyers arrive one at a time, but the order of their arrival is unknown. Secondly, when two different bundles are both in demand for a player, that player can break the tie arbitrarily.

Due to the two unknowns mentioned above, the well-known Walrasian prices
[
1
]
are insufficient to achieve an optimal allocation
[
2
]
. It can be shown that in general no static pricing can achieve more than
2/3
of the optimal social welfare
[
3
]
. Instead, a
dynamic pricing
is necessary. The dynamic pricing is more powerful since after each buyer has picked a bundle in demand, we are allowed to adjust the prices of the remaining items in the market before the arrival of the next buyer. Since the prices can be updated, to show that a dynamic pricing is optimal, it suffices to check that no matter which player
i\in I
arrives first, any bundle in demand for player
i
can be extended to an optimal allocation. That is, any choice of any player can always be accommodated afterwards by changing the prices of the remaining items.

In our example, one can see that with the assigned prices the two bundles in demand for player 1 can be extend to an optimal allocation. Similarly, one can check that
\{\alpha\}
and
\{\gamma\}
are the only two bundles in demand for player 2 and both extend to an optimal allocation. Similarly,
\{\delta\}
is the only bundle that is in demand for player 3 and indeed
\delta
is assigned to player 3 in all optimal allocations.

Ezra et. al.
[
4
]
proved that dynamic pricing allows to achieve an optimal allocation in markets with homogeneous items and submodular valuations, which is a superclass of multi-demand valuations. As for markets with heterogeneous items, Cohen-Addad et. al.
[
3
]
developed algorithms for computing the dynamic pricing in unit-demand markets and in gross substitutes markets with a unique optimal allocation. A generalization of this result led to another algorithm by Berger et. al. which applies to multi-demand markets with at most three players
[
5
]
. Bérczi et. al. showed that a dynamic pricing is possible in combinatorial multi-demand markets where each player is a bi-demand player
[
6
]
. One can achieve the optimal social welfare by dynamic pricing in the case of two players where both players have valuation functions induced by certain classes of matroids
[
7
]
. The existence of dynamic pricing in a general multi-demand market remains an open problem. In this paper, we show that a dynamic pricing exists in multi-demand markets with four players, multi-demand markets with at most two optimal allocations, and tri-demand markets.

Our technique heavily relies on the concept of legality. An item
x\in\mathcal{X}
is said to be legal to a player
i\in I
if there exists an optimal allocation in which
x
is assigned to
i
. A legal allocation refers to an allocation in which every player
i
receives
k_{i}
legal items. Berger et. al. proved that optimal allocations and legal allocations are equivalent
[
5
]
.

Section
2
provides the precise definitions of relevant concepts, including valuation functions, utility, social welfare, dynamic pricing and legality. Sections
3
and
4
describe first step of the process, where we impose rough prices in order to limit the choices of each player to their legal items. The algorithm for finding rough prices involves weighted directed graphs, which is a generalization of the technique used in
[
3
]
for unit-demand markets.

Section
5
and onward describes second step of the process, where we define fine prices such that the sum of rough prices and fine prices is a dynamic pricing. We do not have an algorithm that finds fine prices in all multi-demand markets. Instead, we focus on three particular types of multi-demand markets. Sections
7
,
8
, and
9
present algorithms for computing dynamic pricing in multi-demand markets with four players, in multi-demand markets with at most two optimal allocations and in tri-demand markets, respectively. The first two algorithms involve induction on the number of items in the market. We use direct graphs that are constructed based on information about legality to find a subset of items whose price can be easily determined and thus can be removed from the market. The last algorithm involves induction on the number of players and items.

1.1
Comparison to Previous Techniques

Our results on rough prices were independently obtained by Bérczi et. al.
[
6
]
, in particular the results guaranteeing the existence of rough prices, see Theorem
4.3
, and positive utility of legal items for rough prices, see Lemma
4.4
. Our techniques to obtain rough prices results are different from the techniques in
[
6
]
. While the paper of Bérczi et. al.
[
6
]
provides a novel approach based on the duality and careful modifications of an optimal dual solution, we do a construction through graphs generalizing the approach of
[
3
]
.

Optimal allocations in a multi-demand market naturally correspond to perfect matchings in some bipartite graph.
In
[
6
]
the techniques for finding fine prices in the case of a bi-demand market are inspired by the ideas from Hall’s marriage theorem
[
8
]
. Similarly our approach for tri-demand market uses similar ideas inspired by Hall’s marriage theorem. However, for our proof we need to combine fine prices in a careful way and for this we need to strengthen our statements.

2
Combinatorial Market

In this section, we provide a formal definition of the studied problem together with the central notions.

We consider a market with a set
\mathcal{X}=[m]
of heterogeneous and indivisible
items
and a set
I=[n]
of
buyers
, also called players. For all
i\in I
, the buyer
i
is
k_{i}
-demand and has a
multi-demand valuation function

v_{i}:2^{\mathcal{X}}\to\real_{\geq 0}
, i.e. for all
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
we have

v_{i}(\mathcal{\mathcal{X}^{\prime}}):=\max\left\{\sum\limits_{x\in\mathcal{\hat{\mathcal{X}}}}v_{i}(x):\mathcal{\hat{\mathcal{X}}}\subseteq\mathcal{\mathcal{X}^{\prime}},|\mathcal{\hat{\mathcal{X}}}|\leq k_{i}\right\}\,.

In the case when
k_{i}=1
, 2 or 3, the buyer
i
is called a unit-demand, bi-demand or tri-demand buyer, respectively. Without loss of generality we can assume that
m=\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{i}
.

Since we work with multi-demand buyers, we encode every valuation function
v_{i}
,
i\in I
as an
m
-dimensional vector
\mathbf{v}^{i}=(v_{x}^{i})_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\in\real_{\geq 0}^{m}
indexed by the items, where
v_{x}^{i}=v_{i}(x)
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
. Then for any subset
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
with
|\mathcal{X}^{\prime}|\leq k_{i}
, we have the relation
v_{i}(\mathcal{X}^{\prime})=\langle\mathbf{v}_{i},\chi(\mathcal{X}^{\prime})\rangle
, where
\chi
is the characteristic vector of
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
on
\mathcal{X}
and
\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\rangle
is the scalar product of vectors
\mathbf{a}
and
\mathbf{b}
.

2.1
Utility and Optimal Allocation

We consider the
prices of the items
to be a vector with positive entries. To encode prices of items, we use the
price vector
, which is a positive
m
-dimensional vector
\mathbf{p}=(p_{x})_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\in\real_{>0}^{m}
indexed by the items in the market.

The
price of a bundle
is the sum of the prices of all items in the bundle. Given a price vector
\mathbf{p}\in\real_{>0}^{m}
, for every player we define the
utility
of a bundle to be the difference between its valuation and price. Formally, the utility of a buyer
i\in I
from a bundle
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
is defined as follows

u_{i}(\mathcal{X}^{\prime},\mathbf{p})\coloneqq v_{i}(\mathcal{X}^{\prime})-\langle\mathbf{p},\chi(\mathcal{X}^{\prime})\rangle.

For simplicity, when
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}=\{x\}
is a singleton, we may omit the set brackets and write
u_{i}(x,\mathbf{p})
.

Every player prefers to pick a
bundle in demand
, i.e. a bundle maximizing the utility. Formally speaking, given a price vector
\mathbf{p}\in\real_{>0}^{m}
, the bundles in demand for a player
i\in I
are exactly the ones in the set

D_{\mathbf{p}}(v_{i})\coloneqq\arg\max\limits_{\mathcal{X}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{X}}\{u_{i}(\mathcal{X}^{\prime},\mathbf{p})\}\,.

In case where multiple bundles are in demand for a given player, the player can break the tie arbitrarily and pick any bundle in demand.

An
allocation
is a collection
\mathbfcal{O}=(\mathcal{O}_{i})_{i\in I}
of disjoint subsets of items in
\mathcal{X}
, where the bundle
\mathcal{O}_{i}
is allocated to player
i
. For each allocation, we can calculate the
social welfare
by summing the valuations corresponding to each player and the bundle assigned to this player. In other words, the social welfare of an allocation
\mathbfcal{O}
is defined as

\SW(\mathbfcal{O})\coloneqq\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}(\mathcal{O}_{i}).

An
optimal allocation
is an allocation that achieves the maximal social welfare.

We assume that in every optimal allocation, every item is assigned to some player.
Thus, we also assume that if an item
x\in\mathcal{X}
is assigned to player
i\in I
in some optimal allocation then
v_{i}(x)>0
. This ensures that it is possible for
x
to contribute positively to the utility of player
i
.

2.2
Legality and Dynamic Pricing

Our goal is to find a
dynamic pricing
that guarantees social welfare optimization, despite the fact that each individual buyer always maximizes their own utility. We introduce the useful concepts of legal items, legal bundles, and legal allocations as in
[
5
]
.

Since we are allowed to update prices after each purchased bundle, to achieve the maximal social welfare, it suffices to ensure that at any point in time, for each buyer every bundle in demand can be completed to an optimal allocation.

Definition 2.1
.

A price vector
\mathbf{p}\in\real_{>0}^{m}
is an (optimal) dynamic pricing if for any player
i\in I
and any bundle
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
that is in demand for player
i
, there exists an optimal allocation in which the bundle
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
is allocated to player
i
.

To formalize the main results we use the notion of
legality
introduced in
[
5
]
. An item
x
is
legal
for a player
i
if there exists an optimal allocation in which
x
is allocated to
i
. A bundle
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
is
legal
for a player
i
if
|\mathcal{X}^{\prime}|=k_{i}
and every item
x\in\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
is legal for player
i
. A
legal allocation

\mathbfcal{O}
is an allocation in which
\mathcal{O}_{i}
is legal for player
i
for all
i\in I
.

A very powerful Theorem
2.2
by Berger et al. states that optimal allocations and legal allocations are equivalent
[
5
]
. Consequently, we use these terms interchangeably.

Theorem 2.2
.

An allocation is legal if and only if it is optimal.

Theorem
2.2
allows us to restate the definition of dynamic pricing in terms of legality. Namely, we can rewrite the definition of dynamic pricing as follows. A price
\mathbf{p}\in\real_{>0}^{m}
is a dynamic pricing if for any player
i\in I
and any bundle
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
that is in demand for player
i
, there exists a legal allocation in which the bundle
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
is allocated to player
i
.

3
Auxiliary Graph

In this section, we define the
auxiliary graph of a market
. Later, we also use existence of Walrasian prices to prove certain properties of the auxiliary graph. Indeed, since multi-demand valuations is a subclass of gross-substitutes valuations, Walrasian prices always exist
[
9
]
. In the next sections, we use auxiliary graph to compute prices.

3.1
Construction of the Auxiliary Graph

The auxiliary graph can be seen as a generalization of the relation graph in Cohen-Addad et. al. algorithm
[
3
]
. The relation graph is constructed based on a single optimal allocation, while our auxiliary graph captures all optimal allocations.

We list all optimal allocations in the market as
\mathbfcal{O}^{1},\mathbfcal{O}^{2},...,\mathbfcal{O}^{k}
and denote the maximum social welfare by
\widehat{\SW}
. For each player
i\in I
, we define a vector

\mathbf{a}^{i}\coloneqq\frac{1}{k}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{k}\chi(\mathcal{O}_{i}^{t})\,,

which averages the bundles assigned to player
i
in all listed optimal allocations. Intuitively, this corresponds to a “fractional bundle” assigned to the player
i
; and thus
\mathbfcal{A}\coloneqq(\mathbf{a}^{i})_{i\in I}
is an average of all optimal allocations. In fact, had we allowed items to be divisible,
\mathbfcal{A}
itself would be a valid “fractional allocation” that achieves the social welfare
\widehat{\SW}
. This intuition is captured by the following lemma. Note that by “fractional allocation”, we mean that all items are assigned completely to the players, i.e.
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{a}^{i}=\mathbf{1}
, and that the total amount of items received by each player is equal to their demand, i.e.
\langle\mathbf{a}^{i},\mathbf{1}\rangle=k_{i}
. We define the value of a “fractional bundle” to be the linear combination of the values of the items in the bundle, i.e. for all players
i\in I
and “fractional bundle”
\mathbf{a}
we have

v_{i}(\mathbf{a})=\langle\mathbf{v}^{i},\mathbf{a}\rangle

whenever
\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{1}\rangle\leq k_{i}
, i.e. whenever the “fractional number” of items allocated to player
i
by
\mathbf{a}
is at most the demand.

Lemma 3.1
.

\mathbfcal{A}
is a fractional allocation with social welfare
\widehat{\SW}
.

Proof.

Each optimal allocation
\mathbfcal{O}^{t}
,
t=1,\ldots,k
, allocates every item to exactly one player, i.e.
\mathbfcal{O}^{t}
is a partition of
\mathcal{X}
. Thus we have

\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\chi(\mathcal{O}_{i}^{t})=\mathbf{1}\,.

Summing over all
k
optimal allocations, we have

\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{a}^{i}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{k}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{k}\chi(\mathcal{O}_{i}^{t})=\frac{1}{k}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{k}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\chi(\mathcal{O}_{i}^{t})=\frac{1}{k}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{k}\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{1}.

Since every player
i
receives exactly
k_{i}
items in any optimal allocation, for every player
i\in I
we have

\langle\mathbf{a}^{i},\mathbf{1}\rangle=\langle\frac{1}{k}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{k}\chi(\mathcal{O}_{i}^{t}),\mathbf{1}\rangle=\frac{1}{k}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{k}\langle\chi(\mathcal{O}_{i}^{t}),\mathbf{1}\rangle=\frac{1}{k}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{k}k_{i}=k_{i}.

So,
\mathbfcal{A}
is a fractional allocation and we can calculate the social welfare of
\mathbfcal{A}
as follows

\displaystyle\SW(\mathbfcal{A})

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\langle\mathbf{v}^{i},\mathbf{a}^{i}\rangle=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\langle\mathbf{v}^{i},\frac{1}{k}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{k}\chi(\mathcal{O}_{i}^{t})\rangle=\frac{1}{k}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{k}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\langle\mathbf{v}^{i},\chi(\mathcal{O}_{i}^{t})\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{k}\SW(\mathbfcal{O}^{t})=\frac{1}{k}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{k}\widehat{\SW}=\widehat{\SW}\,,

finishing the proof.
∎

To facilitate the discussion of legal items, we introduce the following notations. For each player
i\in I
, let us define

\mathcal{K}_{i}:=\bigcup\limits_{t=1}^{k}\mathcal{O}_{i}^{t}\subseteq\mathcal{X}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{R}_{i}:=\bigcap\limits_{t=1}^{k}\mathcal{O}_{i}^{t}\subseteq\mathcal{X}\,.

Then,
\mathcal{K}_{i}
denotes the set of all items that are legal to player
i
and
\mathcal{R}_{i}
denotes the set of all items that are legal only to player
i
.

The fractional allocation
\mathbfcal{A}
is closely related to legality. We can learn whether an item
x
is legal to a player
i
and whether
x
is legal to players other than player
i
by comparing the value of
\mathbf{a}_{x}^{i}
with
0
and
1
.

Remark 3.2
.

For every item
x\in\mathcal{X}
and every player
i\in I
, we have the following equivalences.

1.

x\in\mathcal{R}_{i}
if and only if
\mathbf{a}_{x}^{i}=1
.

2.

x\in\mathcal{K}_{i}
if and only if
0<\mathbf{a}_{x}^{i}\leq 1
.

3.

x\notin\mathcal{K}_{i}
if and only if
\mathbf{a}_{x}^{i}=0
.

Finally, we define the auxiliary graph using the fractional allocation
\mathbfcal{A}
. Intuitively speaking, the edges in the graph represent preferences that we aim to enforce by rough prices. The desired properties of rough prices are discussed in Section
4
.

Definition 3.3
.

An auxiliary graph is a graph
H=(\mathcal{X},E)
, such that for all player
i\in I
and items
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
, whenever
\mathbf{a}_{x}^{i}>0
and
\mathbf{a}_{y}^{i}<1
, we have an edge
e_{i}=x\to y\in E
induced by player
i
with weight

w(x\to y)=v_{i}(x)-v_{i}(y)\,.

Note that for
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
the auxiliary graph
H
can potentially have multiple edges
x\to y
.

3.2
Cycles in the Auxiliary Graph

In this section, we prove that there are no negative-weight cycles in the auxiliary graph. This fact ensures that shortest paths in the auxiliary graph are well defined. Such paths are studied later. We also find the necessary and sufficient condition for the occurrence of a
0
-weight cycle in the auxiliary graph.

To prove that negative-weight cycles do not exist, we consider the utility of bundles and “fractional bundles” with respect to the
Walrasian prices
. The concept of
Walrasian equilibrium
is closely related to optimal allocations and bundles in demand. In particular, Paes Leme and Wong proved the Second Welfare Theorem, which implies that in any optimal allocation the bundle received by each player is a bundle in demand with respect to Walrasian prices
[
10
]
.

Definition 3.4
.

A Walrasian equilibrium consists of a price vector
\mathbf{p}^{W}\in\real^{m}
and an allocation
\mathbfcal{O}
such that for any player
i
the bundle
\mathcal{O}_{i}
is in demand.
\mathbf{p}^{W}
is called a Walrasian price and
\mathbfcal{O}
is called a Walrasian allocation induced by
\mathbf{p}^{W}
.

As mentioned before, for a market with multi-demand players, Walrasian equilibrium always exists
[
9
]
. In the remaining parts of the paper, we denote Walrasian prices by
\mathbf{p}^{W}\in\real^{m}
.

Theorem 3.5

(Second Welfare Theorem)
.

Any Walrasian price and any optimal allocation form a Walrasian equilibrium.

We first show that any small enough compatible perturbation of the “fractional bundle” assigned by
\mathbfcal{A}
to a certain player is a “convex combination” of integral bundles, so that later we can apply the Second Welfare Theorem to compare the utility of these integral bundles with the utility of the bundles that are in demand for the same player.

Lemma 3.6
.

If
a_{x}^{i}>0
and
a_{y}^{i}<1
for some player
i\in I
and items
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
, then
\mathbf{a}^{i}-\delta\boldsymbol{\alpha}
is a convex combination of characteristic vectors of sets with size
k_{i}
, where
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\mathbf{\hat{e}}_{x}-\mathbf{\hat{e}}_{y}\in\real^{m}
and
0\leq\delta\leq\frac{1}{k}
. Here,
\mathbf{\hat{e}}_{x}
and
\mathbf{\hat{e}}_{y}
are vectors in
m
with the entry corresponding to
x
and
y
equal to
1
, respectively, and all other entries equal to
0
.

Proof.

Consider a player
i\in I
such that
a_{x}^{i}>0
and
a_{y}^{i}<1
and consider
0\leq\delta\leq\frac{1}{k}
. Consider the polytope

P:=\{\mathbf{b}\in\real^{m}:\mathbf{0}\leq\mathbf{b}\leq\mathbf{1},\,\sum_{x=1}^{m}b_{x}=k_{i}\}\,.

From Lemma
3.1
, we have
\langle\mathbf{a}^{i},\mathbf{1}\rangle=k_{i}
and so we have

\langle\mathbf{a}^{i}-\delta\boldsymbol{\alpha},\mathbf{1}\rangle=\langle\mathbf{a}^{i},\mathbf{1}\rangle-\delta\langle\boldsymbol{\alpha},\mathbf{1}\rangle=k_{i}-\delta(\langle\mathbf{\hat{e}}_{x},\mathbf{1}\rangle-\langle\mathbf{\hat{e}}_{y},\mathbf{1}\rangle)=k_{i}\,.

By definition of
\mathbf{a}^{i}
, we have that
\mathbf{a}^{i}-\delta\boldsymbol{\alpha}
satisfies the constraints defining the polytope
P
whenever
0\leq\delta\leq\frac{1}{k}
.
Since the polytope
P
is defined by a totally unimodular matrix, all extreme points of
P
are integral. This implies that
\mathbf{a}^{i}-\delta\boldsymbol{\alpha}
can be written as a convex combination of the extreme points of
P
, but the extreme points of
P
are the vectors in
\mathbb{Z}^{m}
with exactly
k_{i}
ones. These extreme points are exactly the characteristic vectors of sets with size
k_{i}
.
∎

When there is a cycle in the auxiliary graph, we can reallocate items along the cycle to obtain another fractional allocation by assigning a larger fraction of item
y
and a smaller fraction of item
x
to player
i
for every edge
e_{i}=x\to y
in the cycle. The total change in utility is given by the weight of the cycle. In particular, in the following lemma, we show that if there is a negative-weight cycle, then we can perturb the fractional allocation
\mathbfcal{A}
to obtain an allocation with a strictly higher utility. We later combine the results from Lemmas
3.6
and
3.7
to show that the auxiliary graph does not contain any negative-weight cycle.

Lemma 3.7
.

If the auxiliary graph
H
has a negative-weight cycle
\mathcal{C}=x_{0}e_{i_{0}}x_{1}e_{i_{1}}x_{2}...x_{l}e_{i_{l}}x_{0}
, where
x_{0},x_{1},...,x_{l}\in\mathcal{X}
are distinct and
e_{i_{0}},...,e_{i_{l}}\in E
are induced by players
i_{0},...,i_{l}
respectively, then

\sum\limits_{j=0}^{l}\langle\mathbf{v}^{i_{j}}-\mathbf{p}^{W},\mathbf{a}^{i_{j}}-\delta\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{j}\rangle>\sum\limits_{j=0}^{l}\langle\mathbf{v}^{i_{j}}-\mathbf{p}^{W},\mathbf{a}^{i_{j}}\rangle,

where
0<\delta\leq\frac{1}{k}
and
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{j}=\mathbf{\hat{e}}_{x_{j}}-\mathbf{\hat{e}}_{x_{j+1}}
for all
j=0,\ldots,l
. Here, we identify
x_{l+1}
with
x_{0}
.

Proof.

Consider the total change in valuation after the allocation is perturbed by
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{j}
for
j=0,\ldots,l
,

\sum\limits_{j=0}^{l}\langle\mathbf{v}^{i_{j}},\delta\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{j}\rangle=\delta\sum\limits_{j=0}^{l}\langle\mathbf{v}^{i_{j}},\mathbf{\hat{e}}_{x_{j}}-\mathbf{\hat{e}}_{x_{j+1}}\rangle=\delta\sum\limits_{j=0}^{l}w(e_{i_{j}})=\delta w(\mathcal{C})<0\,.

Since
\sum\limits_{j=0}^{l}\mathbf{a}^{j}=0
, we get

\sum\limits_{j=0}^{l}\langle\mathbf{p}^{W},\delta\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{j}\rangle=0\,.

Putting these equalities together, we get

\sum\limits_{j=0}^{l}\langle\mathbf{v}^{i_{j}}-\mathbf{p}^{W},\mathbf{a}^{i_{j}}-\delta\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{j}\rangle=\sum\limits_{j=0}^{l}\langle\mathbf{v}^{i_{j}}-\mathbf{p}^{W},\mathbf{a}^{i_{j}}\rangle-\sum\limits_{j=0}^{l}\langle\mathbf{v}^{i_{j}},\delta\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{j}\rangle+\sum\limits_{j=0}^{l}\langle\mathbf{p}^{W},\delta\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{j}\rangle>\sum\limits_{j=0}^{l}\langle\mathbf{v}^{i_{j}}-\mathbf{p}^{W},\mathbf{a}^{i_{j}}\rangle\,,

finishing the proof.
∎

Using Lemma
3.6
and the Second Welfare Theorem, we can show that for any player
i_{j}
,
j=0,\ldots,l
, perturbations by
\delta\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}
cannot achieve higher utility with respect to the prices
\mathbf{p}^{W}
than every integral bundle that is in demand for player
i_{j}
. Together with the result of Lemma
3.7
, this proves that no negative-weight cycles can exist in the auxiliary graph
H
. This fact is captured in the following theorem.

Theorem 3.8
.

There is no negative-weight cycle in the auxiliary graph
H
.

Proof.

Suppose, for contradiction, that there is a negative-weight cycle
\mathcal{C}=x_{0}e_{i_{0}}x_{1}e_{i_{1}}x_{2}...x_{l}e_{i_{l}}x_{0}
. We use the same notation as in Lemma
3.7
. For
j=0,\ldots,l
, let us consider
\mathbf{a}^{i_{j}}-\delta\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{j}
. By Lemma
3.6
, we can write

\mathbf{a}^{i_{j}}-\delta\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{j}=\sum\limits_{q=1}^{u}c_{q}\chi(\mathcal{X}_{q}),

for some
u>1
and
c_{q}\geq 0
, such that
\sum_{q=1}^{u}c_{q}=1
and for each
1\leq q\leq u
,
|\mathcal{X}_{q}|=k_{i_{j}}
. For all
t=1,\ldots,k
, by the Second Welfare Theorem the bundle
\mathcal{O}_{i_{j}}^{t}
is in demand for player
i_{j}
. So,

\langle\mathbf{v}^{i_{j}}-\mathbf{p}^{W},\mathbf{a}^{i_{j}}-\delta\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{j}\rangle=\langle\mathbf{v}^{i_{j}}-\mathbf{p}^{W},\sum\limits_{q=1}^{u}c_{q}\chi(\mathcal{X}_{q})\rangle=\sum\limits_{q=1}^{u}c_{q}\langle\mathbf{v}^{i_{j}}-\mathbf{p}^{W},\chi(\mathcal{X}_{q})\rangle\leq\langle\mathbf{v}^{i_{j}}-\mathbf{p}^{W},\chi(O_{i_{j}}^{t})\rangle

Summing over all
k
optimal allocations, we have

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{v}^{i_{j}}-\mathbf{p}^{W},\mathbf{a}^{i_{j}}-\delta\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{j}\rangle=\frac{1}{k}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{k}\langle\mathbf{v}^{i_{j}}-\mathbf{p}^{W},\mathbf{a}^{i_{j}}-\delta\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{j}\rangle\leq\frac{1}{k}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{k}\langle\mathbf{v}^{i_{j}}-\mathbf{p}^{W},\chi(O_{i_{j}}^{t})\rangle=\langle\mathbf{v}^{i_{j}}-\mathbf{p}^{W},\mathbf{a}^{i_{j}}\rangle\,.

Since the above inequality holds for all
j=1,\ldots,l
, summing over all
j=1,\ldots,l
we get

\sum\limits_{j=1}^{l}\langle\mathbf{v}^{i_{j}}-\mathbf{p}^{W},\mathbf{a}^{i_{j}}-\delta\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{j}\rangle\leq\sum\limits_{j=1}^{l}\langle\mathbf{v}^{i_{j}}-\mathbf{p}^{W},\mathbf{a}^{i_{j}}\rangle\,,

contradicting Lemma
3.7
and finishing the proof.
∎

Let us consider a cycle with weight 0. By a similar argument as in Lemma
3.7
, if we apply similar perturbations to a 0-weight cycle, there should be no change in the utility or the social welfare. Since “fractional bundles” can be considered as convex combinations of integral bundles, this indicates that for every edge
e_{i}=x\to y
in the cycle, both items
x
and
y
are allocated to the player
i
in some optimal allocation, and hence are legal to
i
. This motivates the following theorem, where we show that for edge
e_{i}=x\to y
to be in a 0-weight cycle,
x,y\in\mathcal{K}_{i}\setminus\mathcal{R}_{i}
is a necessary and sufficient condition.

Lemma 3.9
.

For any two items
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
, if in the auxiliary graph
H
there is an edge
e_{\hat{i}}
from
x
to
y
induced by player
\hat{i}
, then
e_{\hat{i}}
is in a 0-weight cycle if and only if
0<\mathbf{a}_{x}^{\hat{i}}<1
and
0<\mathbf{a}_{y}^{\hat{i}}<1
.

Proof.

Let us do a direct proof for both directions. Consider two items
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
and suppose there is an edge
e_{\hat{i}}=x\to y
with weight
v_{\hat{i}}(x)-v_{\hat{i}}(y)
.

Suppose that the edge
e_{\hat{i}}
is in a 0-weight cycle
\mathcal{C}
. Let
\widehat{\SW}
and
\widehat{\SW}_{2}
be the maximal and the second maximal social welfare, respectively. Define

\Delta_{\SW}:=\frac{1}{2}(\widehat{\SW}-\widehat{\SW}_{2})
. Note that if
\widehat{\SW}_{2}
is not well defined then the statement of the lemma holds, so we can assume that
\widehat{\SW}_{2}
is well defined and
\Delta_{\SW}>0
.
By definition of the auxiliary graph
H
, the existence of
e_{\hat{i}}
implies
\mathbf{a}_{x}^{\hat{i}}>0
and
\mathbf{a}_{y}^{\hat{i}}<1
, so it suffices to show
\mathbf{a}_{x}^{\hat{i}}\neq 1
and
\mathbf{a}_{y}^{\hat{i}}\neq 0
. Suppose, for contradiction, that
\mathbf{a}_{x}^{\hat{i}}=1
or
\mathbf{a}_{y}^{\hat{i}}=0
. We consider the following two cases.

Case
\mathbf{a}_{x}^{\hat{i}}=1
.

Consider a new set of valuation functions
(v_{i}^{\prime})_{i\in I}
derived from
(v_{i})_{i\in I}
by defining
v_{\hat{i}}^{\prime}(x):=v_{\hat{i}}(x)-\Delta_{\SW}
and
v_{i}^{\prime}:=v_{i}
otherwise. By the definition of
\Delta_{\SW}
and by the assumption that
\mathbf{a}_{x}^{\hat{i}}=1
, we can prove that the set of optimal allocations is the same for the original and the new valuation functions. So,
\{\mathbfcal{O}^{t}\}_{1\leq t\leq k}
is exactly the set of all optimal allocations corresponding to the new valuation functions
(v_{i}^{\prime})_{i\in I}
. Hence, the new auxiliary graph
H^{\prime}
is the same as
H
and
\mathcal{C}
is a cycle in
H^{\prime}
. The weights of all edges in
\mathcal{C}
remain unchanged, except that the weight on edge
e_{\hat{i}}
becomes

w^{\prime}(e_{\hat{i}})=v_{\hat{i}}^{\prime}(x)-v_{\hat{i}}^{\prime}(y)=v_{\hat{i}}(x)-\Delta_{\SW}-v_{\hat{i}}(y)=w(e_{\hat{i}})-\Delta_{\SW}\,,

where
w^{\prime}
denotes the weights in
H^{\prime}
. So,

w^{\prime}(\mathcal{C})=w(\mathcal{C})-\Delta_{\SW}=0-\Delta_{\SW}<0\,.

So
\mathcal{C}
is a negative-weight cycle in the new auxiliary graph
H^{\prime}
, contradicting Theorem
3.8
.

Case
\mathbf{a}_{y}^{\hat{i}}=0
.

This case is analogous to the above case.
We can consider a new set of valuation functions
(v_{i}^{\prime\prime})_{i\in I}
derived from
(v_{i})_{i\in I}
by defining
v_{\hat{i}}^{\prime\prime}(y):=v_{\hat{i}}(y)+\Delta_{\SW}
and
v_{i}^{\prime\prime}:=v_{i}
otherwise to obtain a similar contradiction.

Conversely, suppose
0<\mathbf{a}_{x}^{\hat{i}}<1
and
0<\mathbf{a}_{y}^{\hat{i}}<1
and let us show that the edge
e_{\hat{i}}
is in a 0-weight cycle
\mathcal{C}
. By the definition of the auxiliary graph, there is an edge from
y
to
x
induced by player
\hat{i}
. Let us denote it by
e_{\hat{i}}^{\prime}
. The cycle
xe_{\hat{i}}ye_{\hat{i}}^{\prime}x
is a 0-weight cycle since

w(xe_{\hat{i}}ye_{\hat{i}}^{\prime}x)=w(e_{\hat{i}})+w(e_{\hat{i}}^{\prime})=v_{\hat{i}}(x)-v_{\hat{i}}(y)+v_{\hat{i}}(y)-v_{\hat{i}}(x)=0\,.

∎

4
Rough Price

In this section, we provide the exact definition of
rough prices
and describe an algorithm for finding rough prices. The algorithm is based on the auxiliary graph discussed in the previous section.

Definition 4.1
.

A price
\mathbf{p}^{R}\in\real_{>0}^{m}
is called a rough price if for every player
i\in I
, it satisfies the following conditions:

1.

For all
x\in\mathcal{R}_{i}
,
y\notin\mathcal{R}_{i}
,
u_{i}(x,\mathbf{p}^{R})>u_{i}(y,\mathbf{p}^{R})
.

2.

For all
x,y\in\mathcal{K}_{i}\setminus\mathcal{R}_{i}
,
u_{i}(x,\mathbf{p}^{R})=u_{i}(y,\mathbf{p}^{R})
.

3.

For all
x\in\mathcal{K}_{i}
,
y\notin\mathcal{K}_{i}
,
u_{i}(x,\mathbf{p}^{R})>u_{i}(y,\mathbf{p}^{R})
.

From this definition, it is more apparent why the edges in the auxiliary graph represent preference constraints. Let us take two items
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
and a player
i\in I
. If
x,y\in\mathcal{K}_{i}\setminus\mathcal{R}_{i}
then we want player
i
to be indifferent between
x
and
y
; and so we include both edges
x\to y
and
y\to x
in
H
. If
x\in\mathcal{R}_{i}
and
y\notin\mathcal{R}_{i}
, or
x\in\mathcal{K}_{i}
and
y\notin\mathcal{K}_{i}
, then we want player
i
to strongly prefer
x
to
y
; and so we only include the edge
x\to y
in
H
. Using Remark
3.2
, one can check that this is equivalent to Definition
3.3
of the auxiliary graph.

The weight of every edge
x\to y
in
H
is defined to be the difference between the values of
x
and
y
for the corresponding player. For each item, we define the rough price to be the negative of the weight of a shortest path from some artificial source vertex to that item. Such a price counterbalances all the difference in the valuation functions. We also introduce a small perturbation of
\epsilon
on certain edges to ensure that the inequalities in
1
and
3
in Definition
4.1
are strict. The details of our construction are outlined in the following algorithm.

Algorithm 1

Finding rough prices in multi-demand markets.

1:

Construct the auxiliary graph
H=(\mathcal{X},E)
.

2:

Define
E^{\prime}:=\{e\in E:e\text{ is in some 0-weight cycle in }H\}
.

3:

Define
\epsilon_{c}:=\min\{w(\mathcal{C}):\text{$\mathcal{C}$ is a cycle in $H$ and }w(\mathcal{C})>0\}>0
.

4:

Define
\epsilon_{v}:=\min\{v_{i}(x):i\in I,x\in\mathcal{X}\}>0
.

5:

Define
\epsilon:=\frac{\min\{\epsilon_{c},\epsilon_{v}\}}{m+1}>0
.

6:

Define a new graph
H^{\prime}
based on
H
by adding a source vertex
s
and adding an edge
s\to x
for each item
x\in\mathcal{X}
.

7:

For every edge
e=x\to y
in
H^{\prime}
, define the weight
w^{\prime}
in
H^{\prime}
by

w^{\prime}(e)=\begin{cases}w(e)\quad&\text{if }e\in E^{\prime}\\
w(e)-\epsilon\quad&\text{if }e\in E\setminus E^{\prime}\\
0\quad&\text{if }x=s\,.\end{cases}

8:

For every
x\in\mathcal{X}
, define
\delta(s,x)
to be the weight of a shortest path from
s
to
x
in
H^{\prime}
.

9:

Define
\mathbf{p}^{R}\in\real^{m}
as
p^{R}_{x}:=-\delta(s,x)+\epsilon
for every
x\in\mathcal{X}
.

10:

return

\mathbf{p}^{R}
.

Through the choice of
\epsilon_{c}
, we ensure that
\epsilon
is small enough so that all cycles in
H^{\prime}
have nonnegative weight as shown in the following lemma. This guarantees the existence of a shortest path from
s
to every item. Through the choice of
\epsilon_{v}
, we ensure that the utility of any legal item remains positive as shown in Lemma
4.4
. We see in the next section that this guarantees that a bundle in demand for player
i
always contains
k_{i}
items.

Lemma 4.2
.

There is no negative-weight cycle in the graph
H^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a simple cycle in
H^{\prime}
. Since the source vertex
s
has no incoming edge,
\mathcal{C}
does not include
s
and hence is also a cycle in
H
. Since there are
m
items,
\mathcal{C}
has length at most
m
.

If
\mathcal{C}
is a
0
-weight cycle in
H
, then it is also a
0
-weight cycle in
H^{\prime}
, because the weights of edges in
\mathcal{C}
are the same in
H
and
H^{\prime}
. Otherwise,
\mathcal{C}
is a positive-weight cycle in
H
. By construction, we have
\epsilon\leq\frac{\epsilon_{c}}{m+1}
where
\epsilon_{c}
is the minimum weight among all positive-weight cycles in
H
. Thus, we have

w^{\prime}(\mathcal{C})\geq w(\mathcal{C})-m\epsilon>w(\mathcal{C})-\epsilon_{c}\geq 0\,.

∎

From Lemma
4.2
we know that the prices
\mathbf{p}^{R}
in Algorithm
1
are well-defined. We now show that these are indeed the desired rough prices.

Theorem 4.3
.

The prices
\mathbf{p}^{R}\in\real^{m}
given by Algorithm
1
are rough prices.

Proof.

For all
x\in\mathcal{X}
,
s\to x
is a path from
s
to
x
, so

p_{x}=-\delta(s,x)+\epsilon\geq-w^{\prime}(s\to x)+\epsilon=0+\epsilon>0

So
\mathbf{p}^{R}
is indeed a vector in
\real{}_{>0}^{m}
.

Now let us show that
\mathbf{p}^{R}
satisfies Definition
4.1
. Consider a player
i\in I
and two items
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
. We consider the following three cases, each corresponding to a property in Definition
4.1
.

Case
x\in\mathcal{R}_{i}
and
y\notin\mathcal{R}_{i}
.

By Remark
3.2
,
\mathbf{a}_{x}^{i}=1
and
\mathbf{a}_{y}^{i}<1
. So, there is an edge
e_{i}=x\to y
with weight
w(e_{i})=v_{i}(x)-v_{i}(y)
in
H
. By Lemma
3.9
we have
e_{i}\notin E^{\prime}
, so

w^{\prime}(e_{i})=w(e_{i})-\epsilon=v_{i}(x)-v_{i}(y)-\epsilon\,.

Notice that a shortest path from
s
to
x
together with the edge
e_{i}
is a path from
s
to
y
, so we have

\left(-p_{x}^{R}+\epsilon\right)+(v_{i}(x)-v_{i}(y)-\epsilon)=\delta(s,x)+w^{\prime}(e_{i})\geq\delta(s,y)=-p_{y}^{R}+\epsilon\,,

and thus

u_{i}(x,\mathbf{p}^{R})=v_{i}(x)-p_{x}^{R}\geq v_{i}(y)-p_{y}^{R}+\epsilon=u_{i}(y,\mathbf{p}^{R})+\epsilon>u_{i}(y,\mathbf{p}^{R})\,.

Case
x,y\in\mathcal{K}_{i}\setminus\mathcal{R}_{i}
.

By Remark
3.2
,
0<\mathbf{a}_{x}^{i}<1
and
0<\mathbf{a}_{y}^{i}<1
. So, there is an edge
e_{i}=x\to y
with weight
w(e_{i})=v_{i}(x)-v_{i}(y)
in
H
. By Lemma
3.9
we have
e_{i}\in E^{\prime}
, so

w^{\prime}(e_{i})=w(e_{i})=v_{i}(x)-v_{i}(y).

By the same argument as in the above case, we have

\left(-p_{x}^{R}+\epsilon\right)+(v_{i}(x)-v_{i}(y))=\delta(s,x)+w^{\prime}(e_{i})\geq\delta(s,y)=-p_{y}^{R}+\epsilon,

and thus

u_{i}(x,\mathbf{p}^{R})=v_{i}(x)-p_{x}^{R}\geq v_{i}(y)-p_{y}^{R}=u_{i}(y,\mathbf{p}^{R})\,.

Similarly, we have
u_{i}(y,\mathbf{p}^{R})\geq u_{i}(x,\mathbf{p}^{R})
; and therefore,
u_{i}(x,\mathbf{p}^{R})=u_{i}(y,\mathbf{p}^{R})
.

Case
x\in\mathcal{K}_{i}
and
y\notin\mathcal{K}_{i}
.

By Remark
3.2
,
0<\mathbf{a}_{x}^{i}\leq 1
and
\mathbf{a}_{y}^{i}=0
. This case is similar to the case
x\in\mathcal{R}_{i}
and
y\notin\mathcal{R}_{i}
.
∎

Now let us show that the found rough prices guarantee positive utilities for all legal items.

Lemma 4.4
.

For every player
i\in I
and for every legal item
x\in\mathcal{K}_{i}
, we have
u_{i}(x,\mathbf{p}^{R})>0
.

Proof.

Take player
i\in I
and item
x\in\mathcal{K}_{i}
. Let
se_{s}x_{1}e_{i_{1}}x_{2}...x_{l-1}e_{i_{l-1}}x_{l}=x
be a shortest path from
s
to
x
.
By Lemma
4.2
, all cycles in
H^{\prime}
have nonnegative weight. So, any cycle in the above path must be a 0-weight cycle. Removing such cycles does not change the weight of the path, so we assume that
x_{1},x_{2},...x_{l}
are all distinct. Therefore, we have
l\leq m
.

For all
j=1,\ldots,l
, we have
\mathbf{a}_{x_{j}}^{i_{j}}>0
since
e_{i_{j}}
exists, so there exists
t_{j}\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
, such that
x_{j}\in\mathcal{O}_{i_{j}}^{t_{j}}
. We consider the following lower bound on the utility of item
x
with respect to player
i
. Note that we identify player
i_{l}
with player
i
and item
x_{l+1}
with item
x_{1}
.

\displaystyle u_{i}(x,\mathbf{p}^{R})

\displaystyle=v_{i}(x)-p_{x}^{R}=v_{i_{l}}(x_{l})+\delta(s,x)-\epsilon=v_{i_{l}}(x_{l})+w^{\prime}(e_{s})+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{l-1}w^{\prime}(e_{i_{j}})-\epsilon

\displaystyle\geq v_{i_{l}}(x_{l})+0+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{l-1}(v_{i_{j}}(x_{j})-v_{i_{j}}(x_{j+1})-\epsilon)-\epsilon

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{l}v_{i_{j}}(x_{j})-\sum\limits_{j=1}^{l-1}v_{i_{j}}(x_{j+1})-l\epsilon

\displaystyle=k\left(\frac{1}{k}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{l}v_{i_{j}}(x_{j})-\frac{1}{k}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{l}v_{i_{j}}(x_{j+1})\right)+v_{i_{l}}(x_{1})-l\epsilon

\displaystyle\geq k(\SW(\mathbfcal{A})-\SW(\mathbfcal{B}))+v_{i_{l}}(x_{1})-m\epsilon,

where
\mathbfcal{B}=(\mathbf{b}^{i})_{i\in I}
is the fractional allocation obtained from
\mathbfcal{A}
by reallocating
\frac{1}{k}
of item
x_{j+1}
to player
i_{j}
instead of player
i_{j+1}
for
j=1,\ldots,l
. By construction, we have
\mathbf{a}_{x_{j}}^{i_{j}}\geq\frac{1}{k}
for all
j=1,\ldots,l
. So
\mathbfcal{B}
is indeed a valid fractional allocation. Formally speaking,
for all
j=1,\ldots,l
we have

\mathbf{b}^{i_{j}}=\mathbf{a}^{i_{j}}-\frac{1}{k}\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{j}\quad\text{where}\quad\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{j}=\mathbf{\hat{e}}_{x_{j}}-\mathbf{\hat{e}}_{x_{j+1}},

and for all other players
i
we have
\mathbf{b}^{i}=\mathbf{a}^{i}
.

Using the same arguments as in Theorem
3.8
, we get

\sum\limits_{j=1}^{l}\langle\mathbf{v}^{i_{j}}-\mathbf{p}^{W},\mathbf{b}^{i_{j}}\rangle=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{l}\langle\mathbf{v}^{i_{j}}-\mathbf{p}^{W},\mathbf{a}^{i_{j}}-\frac{1}{k}\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{j}\rangle\leq\sum\limits_{j=1}^{l}\langle\mathbf{v}^{i_{j}}-\mathbf{p}^{W},\mathbf{a}^{i_{j}}\rangle\,.

Using
\sum\limits_{j=1}^{l}\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{j}=\mathbf{0}
, we get

\sum\limits_{j=1}^{l}\langle\mathbf{p}^{W},\frac{1}{k}\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{j}\rangle=0\,.

Thus, we get

\sum\limits_{j=1}^{l}\langle\mathbf{v}^{i_{j}},\mathbf{b}^{i_{j}}\rangle=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{l}\langle\mathbf{v}^{i_{j}},\mathbf{a}^{i_{j}}-\frac{1}{k}\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{j}\rangle\leq\sum\limits_{j=1}^{l}\langle\mathbf{v}^{i_{j}},\mathbf{a}^{i_{j}}\rangle\,.

Since
\SW(\mathbfcal{B})\leq\SW(\mathbfcal{A})
, we get

\displaystyle u_{i}(x,\mathbf{p}^{R})\geq k(\SW(\mathbfcal{A})-\SW(\mathbfcal{B}))+v_{i_{l}}(x_{1})-m\epsilon\geq v_{i_{l}}(x_{1})-m\epsilon\geq v_{i_{l}}(x_{1})-\frac{m}{m+1}\epsilon_{v}>0\,.

∎

5
Fine Price

We have introduced Algorithm
1
for finding rough prices
\mathbf{p}^{R}
in a multi-demand market. In this section, we define
fine prices
and show that the sum of rough and fine prices is a dynamic pricing. We call the market that we have been working with the original market, or
Market #1
. We now define a market induced by Market #1, which we call
Market #2
.

Market #2 has items
\mathcal{X}^{2}:=\mathcal{X}\setminus\left(\bigcup\limits_{i\in I}\mathcal{R}_{i}\right)
and buyers
I_{2}:=\{i\in I\,:\,\mathcal{R}_{i}\neq\mathcal{K}_{i}\}
, where each player
i\in I_{2}
is a
\hat{k}_{i}
-demand player with
\hat{k}_{i}\coloneqq k_{i}-|\mathcal{R}_{i}|
and valuation function

\hat{v}_{i}(x):=\begin{cases}u_{i}^{*}\quad&\text{if }x\in\widehat{\mathcal{K}}_{i}:=\mathcal{K}_{i}\setminus\mathcal{R}_{i}\\
0\quad&\text{if }x\in\mathcal{X}^{2}\setminus\widehat{\mathcal{K}}_{i}\,,\end{cases}

where
u_{i}^{*}\coloneqq u_{i}(x,\mathbf{p}^{R})
for
x\in\widehat{\mathcal{K}}_{i}
. Note that by
2
in Definition
4.1
of rough prices,
u_{i}^{*}
is well-defined; and by Lemma
4.4
, we have
u_{i}^{*}>0
for every player
i\in I_{2}
.

For
i\in I_{2}
, let us define

\Delta_{i}\coloneqq u_{i}^{*}-\max(\{0\}\cup\{u_{i}(x,\mathbf{p}^{R})\,:\,x\notin\mathcal{K}_{i}\}).

Note that
\Delta_{i}>0
for each
i\in I_{2}
by Lemma
4.4
and
3
in Definition
4.1
of rough prices. Let us also define
\Delta:=\min\{\Delta_{i}:i\in I_{2}\}>0
, where we assume that
I_{2}
is not empty since otherwise Market #2 is trivial.

Definition 5.1
.

A price
\mathbf{p}^{F}\in\real_{>0}^{|\mathcal{X}^{2}|}
is called a fine price of Market #1 if it satisfies the following conditions.

1.

\mathbf{p}^{F}
is a dynamic pricing in Market #2.

2.

For all
x\in\mathcal{X}^{2}
and for all
i\in I_{2}
,
0<p_{x}^{F}<\Delta\leq\Delta_{i}
.

Let us assume that we are given a fine price
\mathbf{p}^{F}
and rough price
\mathbf{p}^{R}
for Market #1, then we can combine them into a dynamic pricing
\mathbf{p}^{D}\in\real_{>0}^{m}
for Market #1 as follows

p_{x}^{D}:=\begin{cases}p_{x}^{R}+p_{x}^{F}\quad&\text{if }x\in\mathcal{X}^{2}\\
p_{x}^{R}\quad&\text{if }x\in\mathcal{X}\setminus\mathcal{X}^{2}\,.\end{cases}

We first note that
\mathbf{p}^{D}
is within the desired range. That is,
\mathbf{p}^{D}
is positive; and from
2
in Definition
5.1
and the construction of
\Delta
, one can show that for each player every legal item yields a positive utility. We now show that
\mathbf{p}^{D}
is indeed a dynamic pricing in Market #1. In the next section, we proceed by finding fine prices in certain types of markets.

Intuitively, the purpose of setting
\mathbf{p}^{F}
is to motivate each player
i
to differentiate the items in
\mathcal{K}_{i}\setminus\mathcal{R}_{i}
. We further note that as long as
\mathbf{p}^{F}
is a dynamic pricing in Market #2 that satisfies
2
in Definition
5.1
, the numerical values of
\mathbf{p}^{F}
are irrelevant. By construction of the rough prices, a player
i\in I_{2}
only picks items from
\widehat{\mathcal{K}}_{i}
and all such items have the same value in Market #2. So the player
i
always picks the
\hat{k}_{i}
items in
\widehat{\mathcal{K}}_{i}
with the lowest prices, regardless of the numerical value of the prices. As a result, it suffices to consider the ordering of the prices in
\mathbf{p}^{F}
. Most importantly, the values of
u_{i}^{*}
,
i\in I_{2}
, are irrelevant, we can simply consider a valuation function that maps any legal item to
2
and any illegal item to
1
. We call such Market #2 a
simplified
multi-demand market. The exact definition is provided in the next section in Definition
6.1
.

Hereafter, we focus on Market #2 and construct an algorithm for finding the dynamic pricing
\mathbf{p}^{F}
in Market #2. In particular, we consider three types of markets, namely, multi-demand markets with four players, multi-demand markets with two optimal allocations, and tri-demand markets.

6
Legality Graph

In this section, we define a weighted directed graph called
legality graph
. As its name suggests, this graph is constructed based on legality information about the items. We also discuss the properties of this graph, especially the existence of cycles. Later, we use legality graphs to find a dynamic pricing in markets with four players and in markets with two optimal allocations.

Firstly, we formalize our observation regarding the Market #2 in the previous section into the following definition. To make the notation clearer, we also define a
legality
function which replaces the role of valuation functions.

Definition 6.1
.

Consider a set
\mathcal{X}
of items and a set
I
of players such that for all
i\in I
, player
i
is
k_{i}
-demand, where
k_{i}\geq 1
. Let the vector of demands be defined as
\mathbf{k}:=(k_{i})_{i\in I}
. Let
\mathscr{L}:I\to 2^{\mathcal{X}}
be the function given by
\mathscr{L}(i)=\{x\in\mathcal{X}\,:\,x\text{ is legal to }i\}
for all
i\in I
. Then,
A:=(\mathcal{X},I,\mathbf{k},\mathscr{L})
is called a simplified multi-demand market. We refer to the function
\mathscr{L}
as the legality function of this simplified market. If
|I|\geq 2
and
|\mathcal{X}|=\sum_{i\in I}k_{i}
, then
A
is said to be saturated.

Hereafter, we assume that the market is a simplified multi-demand market. We consider a saturated market
A=(\mathcal{X}_{A},I_{A},\mathbf{k},\mathscr{L})
. Let
m=|\mathcal{X}_{A}|
and
n=|I_{A}|
. We assume that every item is legal to at least two players. Using the notation from previous sections, we can write
\mathcal{R}_{i}=\varnothing
and
\mathcal{K}_{i}=\mathscr{L}(i)
for all
i\in I
. Whenever necessary, for
i\in I
, we consider every item in
\mathscr{L}(i)
as having valuation 2 and every item in
\mathcal{X}_{A}\setminus\mathscr{L}(i)
as having valuation 1. Notice that these definitions and assumptions are consistent with the properties of Market #2 as discussed in the previous section. Let
\mathbfcal{O}
be a legal allocation in market
A
.

Our approach to finding a dynamic pricing in multi-demand market with four players or two optimal allocation is by induction on the number of items. So, it is useful to make the following definition of a
submarket
.

Definition 6.2
.

Given a subset
\mathcal{X}_{B}\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{A}
. For all
i\in I_{A}
, let
k_{i}^{\prime}:=|O_{i}\cap\mathcal{X}_{B}|
. Let
I_{B}:=\{i\in I_{A}:k_{i}^{\prime}>0\}\subseteq I_{A}
. Define a new market
B=(\mathcal{X}_{B},I_{B},(k_{i}^{\prime})_{i\in I_{B}},\mathscr{L})
. Note that here
\mathscr{L}
represents the legality with respect to market
A
. Suppose market
B
has the same valuation functions as market
A
with the domain restricted to
\mathcal{X}_{B}
. Then we say that market
B
is a submarket of market
A
induced by the legal allocation
\mathbfcal{O}
.

Whenever it is not relevant for our argumentation, we refer to
B
as a submarket of
A
without specifying a particular legal allocation.

We now define a legality graph
G
with respect to a legal allocation
\mathbfcal{O}
. This is related to the preference graph in Cohen-Addad et. al. paper
[
3
]
, but we use legality, instead of valuation functions while constructing this graph.

Definition 6.3
.

The legality graph
G
with respect to
\mathbfcal{O}
is defined to be the directed graph
(\mathcal{X}_{A},E)
, such that for any two items
x,y\in\mathcal{X}_{A}
the edge
x\to y
is in
E
if and only if
x\in\mathcal{O}_{i}
and
y\in\mathscr{L}(i)\setminus\mathcal{O}_{i}
for some player
i\in I_{A}
.

Now that we have introduced the concepts of submarkets and legality graphs, we show how submarkets of
A
relate to market
A
in terms of legality. Remark
6.4
states that if an item is legal to a player in some submarket of
A
, then that item is also legal to the same player in market
A
. Consequently, Remark
6.5
states that the legality graph with respect to the restriction of
\mathbfcal{O}
to a submarket
A
induced by
\mathbfcal{O}
must be a subgraph of
G
.

Remark 6.4
.

Let
B
be a submarket of market
A
with items
\mathcal{X}_{B}
and players
I_{B}
. For each player
i\in I_{B}
, if an item
x\in\mathcal{X}_{B}
is legal to player
i
in market
B
then
x
is also legal to player
i
in market
A
.

Remark 6.5
.

Let market
B
be the submarket of
A
induced by
\mathbfcal{O}
with items
\mathcal{X}_{B}
. Let
\mathbfcal{O}^{B}
be the restriction of
\mathbfcal{O}
to
B
. Let
G^{B}
be the legality graph corresponding to
\mathbfcal{O}^{B}
. Then,
G_{B}
is a subgraph of
G
.

Next, we provide two main results about the cycles in the legality graph. Firstly, we define the
reallocation of items with respect to a cycle
in Definition
6.6
, which always yields another legal allocation. We use this result to move between any two legal allocations and to move between any two legality graphs. Secondly, every edge and vertex in the legality graph is in some cycle. This guarantees the existence of cycles containing a certain edge or a certain vertex. These properties are useful in the next two sections.

Definition 6.6
.

Suppose
\mathcal{C}=x_{1}\to x_{2}\to...\to x_{k}=x_{1}
is a cycle in the legality graph
G
with respect to
\mathbfcal{O}
, and
x_{j}\in\mathcal{O}_{i_{j}}
for
j=1,\ldots,k-1
. The allocation obtained from
\mathbfcal{O}
by assigning item
x_{j+1}
to player
i_{j}
for
j=1,\ldots,k-1
, is called the reallocation of
\mathbfcal{O}
with respect to
\mathcal{C}
.

Using Theorem
2.2
, we see that optimal allocations are related to each other through disjoint simple cycles in the legality graph. As a consequence, we can show that every edge and every vertex in the legality graph are in a cycle.

Remark 6.7
.

Let
\mathbfcal{O}^{\prime}
be an optimal allocation in market
A
different from the optimal allocation
\mathbfcal{O}
. Then, there exists a set of disjoint simple cycles in
G

such that we can obtain
\mathbfcal{O}^{\prime}
from
\mathbfcal{O}
by reallocating items with respect to each of these cycles.

Remark 6.8
.

An allocation is legal if and only if it can be obtained from
\mathbfcal{O}
by reallocations with respect to a disjoint set of simple cycles in
G
.

Using Remark
6.7
, we now show that given an edge
x\to y
, we can either define a bijection on the items that maps
x
to
y
or find two intersecting cycles in the legality graph such that one contains
x
and the other contains
y
. We then conclude that every edge in
G
is in a cycle. It follows directly that every vertex in
G
is in a cycle.

Corollary 6.9
.

Every edge in the legality graph
G
is in some cycle.

Proof.

Consider an edge
x\to y
in the legality graph
G
. Suppose
x\in\mathcal{O}_{i}
for some player
i
, then
y\in\mathscr{L}(i)\setminus\mathcal{O}_{i}
.
If there exists a legal allocation
\mathbfcal{O}^{\prime}
such that
x\in\mathcal{O}_{i}\setminus\mathcal{O}_{i}^{\prime}
and
y\in\mathcal{O}_{i}^{\prime}\setminus\mathcal{O}_{i}
, then we can let
f_{i}:\mathcal{O}_{i}\setminus\mathcal{O}_{i}^{\prime}\to\mathcal{O}_{i}^{\prime}\setminus\mathcal{O}_{i}
be a bijection such that
f_{i}(x)=y
. Similarly we can fix an arbitrary bijection
f_{j}:\mathcal{O}_{j}\setminus\mathcal{O}_{j}^{\prime}\to\mathcal{O}_{j}^{\prime}\setminus\mathcal{O}_{j}
for every player
j
different from
i
. Consider bijections
f_{j}
for every player
j
, we find a set of simple cycles as in Remark
6.7
such that
x\to y
is in one of these simple cycles.

Otherwise, for all legal allocations
\mathbfcal{O}^{\prime}
, we have that
y\in\mathcal{O}_{i}^{\prime}\setminus\mathcal{O}_{i}
implies
x\in\mathcal{O}_{i}\cap\mathcal{O}_{i}^{\prime}
. Suppose
y\in\mathcal{O}_{i}^{1}\setminus\mathcal{O}_{i}
for some legal allocation
\mathbfcal{O}^{1}
. Consider an item
x_{1}\in\mathcal{O}_{i}\setminus\mathcal{O}_{i}^{1}
, then
x_{1}\to y
is an edge in
G
and is in some simple cycle
\mathcal{C}_{1}
, by arguments above. Let
\mathbfcal{O}^{2}
be a legal allocation in which
x\in\mathcal{O}_{i}\setminus\mathcal{O}_{i}^{2}
. Such
\mathbfcal{O}^{2}
exists since
x
is legal to at least two different players. Consider an item
y_{2}\in\mathcal{O}_{i}^{2}\setminus\mathcal{O}_{i}
, then
x\to y_{2}
is an edge in
G
and is in some simple cycle
\mathcal{C}_{2}
.

Suppose, for contradiction that
\mathcal{C}_{1}
and
\mathcal{C}_{2}
do not intersect. Let
\mathbfcal{O}^{\prime}
be the reallocation of
\mathbfcal{O}
with respect to both cycles
\mathcal{C}_{1}
and
\mathcal{C}_{2}
. By Remark
6.8
, the allocation
\mathbfcal{O}^{\prime}
is legal. In the allocation
\mathbfcal{O}^{\prime}
, the item
x
is no longer allocated to
i
, but
y
is allocated to
i
. This contradicts the hypothesis that
y\in\mathcal{O}_{i}^{\prime}\setminus\mathcal{O}_{i}
implies
x\in\mathcal{O}_{i}\cap\mathcal{O}_{i}^{\prime}
for every legal allocation
\mathcal{O}_{i}^{\prime}
.
So, we have that
\mathcal{C}_{1}
and
\mathcal{C}_{2}
intersect at some item
\hat{x}
, then the path from
\hat{x}
to
x
in
\mathcal{C}_{2}
, edge
x\to y
and the path from
y
to
\hat{x}
in
\mathcal{C}_{1}
form a cycle which contains the edge
x\to y
.
∎

Corollary 6.10
.

Every vertex in the legality graph
G
is in some cycle.

Finally, for the sake of exposition we usually consider a cycle in which no two items are assigned to the same player. Therefore, we make the following definition of a
uniquely assiged cycle
and prove in Lemma
6.12
that every item is contained in a cycle with this property.

Definition 6.11
.

Let
\mathcal{C}=x_{0}\to x_{1}\to x_{2}...x_{l-1}\to x_{l}=x_{0}
be a simple cycle in
G
. We say that
\mathcal{C}
is a uniquely assigned cycle if for all
i\in I_{A}
we have

|\{x_{j}\,:\,j=1,\ldots,l\}\cap\mathcal{O}_{i}|\in\{0,1\}\,.

Lemma 6.12
.

Every vertex in the legality graph
G
is contained in some uniquely assigned cycle.

Proof.

Consider an item
x_{0}\in\mathcal{X}_{A}
. By Corollary
6.10
,
x_{0}
is in some cycle
\mathcal{C}=x_{0}\to x_{1}\to x_{2}...x_{l-1}\to x_{l}=x_{0}
in
G
. Let us select such cycle
\mathcal{C}
of minimum possible length. Suppose
x_{j}\in O_{i_{j}}
for
j=0,\ldots,l
. Then, for
j=0,\ldots,l-1
, we have that
x_{j+1}
is legal but not allocated to player
i_{j}
. If all
i_{j}
for
j=0,\ldots,l-1
are distinct, then
\mathcal{C}
is a desired uniquely assigned cycle. Otherwise, suppose
i_{j}=i_{j^{\prime}}
for some
j
and
j^{\prime}
such that
0\leq j<j^{\prime}\leq l-1
. Then
x_{j+1}
and
x_{j^{\prime}+1}
are legal but not allocated to
i_{j}=i_{j^{\prime}}
. This means that the edge
x_{j}\to x_{j^{\prime}+1}
exists in
G
and
x_{0}\to x_{1}\to\ldots\to x_{j}\to x_{j^{\prime}+1}\to x_{j^{\prime}+2}\to\ldots\to x_{l-1}\to x_{l}=x_{0}
is a cycle in
G
. This cycle with length
l-j^{\prime}+j
is strictly smaller than the cycle
\mathcal{C}
we started with and it contains the item
x_{0}
, contradicting the selection of
\mathcal{C}
.
∎

7
Multi-Demand Market with Four Players

To prove that dynamic prices exist for markets with four buyers, we would like to use induction on the number of items in the market. For this, we would like to find an appropriate set of items
\mathcal{X}_{r}\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{A}
to remove from the market. By inductive hypothesis, a market with items
\mathcal{X}_{A}\setminus\mathcal{X}_{r}
has a dynamic pricing; so by assigning either the “lowest” or the “highest” prices to the items in
\mathcal{X}_{r}
, we plan to obtain the dynamic pricing for the original market
A
.

As mentioned at the end of Section
5
, we are not concerned with the numerical values of the prices but rather with their ordering. In particular, we assume that the original market
A
is a
simplified multi-demand market
. We assume that all prices are within the appropriate range given by
2
in Definition
5.1
and focus on the ordering of the prices. Under these assumptions, a bundle in demand is a legal bundle with the lowest fine prices. So, it suffices to find the proper ordering of the items in terms of prices, so that whenever a player picks a lowest priced legal bundle, it is extendable to a legal allocation.

We first consider a special case that is easily solvable by induction on the number of items in the market.

Lemma 7.1
.

Suppose an item
\hat{x}\in\mathcal{X}_{A}
is legal to all players. Let
B
be the submarket of
A
induced by
\mathbfcal{O}
with items
\mathcal{X}_{A}\setminus\{\hat{x}\}
. Then any pricing
\mathbf{p}\in\real_{m}
satisfying the following conditions is a dynamic pricing in market
A
.

1.

For all
x\in\mathcal{X}_{A}\setminus\{\hat{x}\}
,
p_{\hat{x}}>p_{x}
.

2.

(p_{x})_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{A}\setminus\{\hat{x}\}}
is a dynamic pricing in market
B
.

Proof.

Suppose that
\hat{x}\in\mathcal{O}_{\hat{i}}
for some
\hat{i}\in I_{A}
. Every player
i
different from
\hat{i}
is a
k_{i}
-demand player in market
B
. Condition
1
ensures that player
i
only picks items from market
B
and condition
2
ensures that such choices can be extended to an optimal allocation in market
B
. This guarantees that any bundle in demand for player
i
in the market
B
with respect to the price
\mathbf{p}
can be extended to a legal allocation in the market
A
by assigning
\hat{x}
to
\hat{i}
.

On the other hand, any bundle in demand for player
\hat{i}
with respect to the price
\mathbf{p}
has size
k_{\hat{i}}
. Conditions
1
and
2
guarantee that
k_{\hat{i}}^{\prime}=k_{\hat{i}}-1
of these items can be extended to some legal allocation
\mathbfcal{O}^{B}
in market
B
; and the
k_{\hat{i}}
-th item, call it
x^{\prime}
, can be either
\hat{x}
or some item in market
B
. If
x^{\prime}=\hat{x}
, then we finish the proof since this bundle can be extended to a legal allocation in the market
A
. Otherwise, suppose that
x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{O}_{i^{\prime}}^{B}
for some
i^{\prime}
different from
\hat{i}
. We can compensate the player
i^{\prime}
by assigning
\hat{x}
to
i^{\prime}
in order to obtain a legal allocation in market
A
.
∎

Lemma
7.1
allows us to assume that, in market
A
, there does not exist an item that is legal to all players. We define the following two types of
removable subsets
of items. We show that when a removable subset exists, we can always find a dynamic pricing by assuming that a dynamic pricing can be found in a submarket with less than
m
items.

Definition 7.2
.

Suppose there is an item
x_{c}\in\mathcal{O}_{i_{c}}
for some player
i_{c}
such that for every player
i\in I_{A}
who is different from
i_{c}
and for whom
x_{c}
is legal, there exists an item
x_{i}\in\mathcal{X}_{A}\setminus\{x_{c}\}
such that
x_{i}\in\mathcal{O}_{i}\cap\mathscr{L}(i_{c})
. That is, for every player
i
such that
x_{c}
is legal but not allocated to
i
, there is an item
x_{i}
that is allocated to player
i
in
\mathbfcal{O}
and is legal to player
i_{c}
. Let
I_{r}:=\{i\in I_{A}:x_{c}\in\mathscr{L}(i)\}
. Then, the set
\mathcal{X}_{r}=\{x_{c}\}\cup\{x_{i}:i\in I_{r}\setminus\{i_{c}\}\}
is called a removable set of type I with respect to legal allocation
\mathbfcal{O}
; for this removable set the item
x_{c}
is called a central item.

Intuitively, when a removable set of type I exists, we can set the price of
x_{c}
to be “low” and the prices of all other items in
\mathcal{X}_{r}
to be “high” so that no player picks items from
\mathcal{X}_{r}\setminus\{x_{c}\}
. For players not in
I_{r}
the item
x_{c}
is illegal, so their behaviour is solely determined by the dynamic pricing in the submarket with items
\mathcal{X}_{A}\setminus\mathcal{X}_{r}
. For the players in
I_{r}
the item
x_{c}
is legal. By constructing
X_{r}
to contain items that are allocated to
I_{r}
and legal for
i_{c}
, we make sure if a player picks
x_{c}
, we can always compensate by assigning some other item in
\mathcal{X}_{r}
to player
i_{c}
if needed. This leads to the following lemma.

Lemma 7.3
.

Let
\mathcal{X}_{r}\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{A}
be a removable set of type I with a central item
x_{c}\in\mathcal{X}_{r}
. Let
B=(\mathcal{X}_{B},I_{B},(k_{i}^{\prime})_{i\in I_{B}},\mathscr{L})
be the submarket of
A
induced by
\mathbfcal{O}
with
\mathcal{X}_{B}=\mathcal{X}\setminus\mathcal{X}_{r}
. Then any pricing
\mathbf{p}\in\real^{m}
satisfying the following conditions is a dynamic pricing in market
A
.

1.

For all items
x\in\mathcal{X}_{r}\setminus\{x_{c}\}
and
y\in\mathcal{X}_{B}
,
p_{x}>p_{y}>p_{x_{c}}
.

2.

(p_{x})_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{B}}
is a dynamic pricing in market
B
.

Proof.

Let
I_{r}:=\{i\in I_{A}:x_{c}\in\mathscr{L}(i)\}
. Consider a player
\hat{i}\in I_{A}
, by Definition
2.1
it suffices to show that any bundle in demand for this player can be extended to a legal allocation in market
A
.

Case
\hat{i}\notin I_{r}
.

By construction, no item in
\mathcal{X}_{r}
is allocated to
\hat{i}
by the legal allocation
\mathbfcal{O}
. So, we have
\mathcal{O}_{\hat{i}}\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{B}\cap\mathscr{L}(\hat{i})
, which implies
k_{\hat{i}}^{\prime}=|\mathcal{O}_{\hat{i}}\cap\mathcal{X}_{B}|=|\mathcal{O}_{\hat{i}}|=k_{\hat{i}}
.

Since items in
\mathcal{X}_{B}
have strictly lower prices then items in
\mathcal{X}_{r}\setminus\{x_{c}\}
and
x_{c}
is not legal to player
\hat{i}
, a bundle in demand for player
\hat{i}
is a bundle
\mathcal{X}_{\hat{i}}
of
k_{\hat{i}}
items from market
B
. By condition
2
, there exists a legal allocation
\mathbfcal{O}^{B}
in market
B
such that
\mathcal{O}_{\hat{i}}^{B}=\mathcal{X}_{\hat{i}}
. Thus, this bundle
\mathcal{X}_{\hat{i}}
extends to a legal allocation
\mathbfcal{O}^{A}
in market
A
, where
\mathcal{O}_{i}^{A}=\mathcal{O}_{i}^{B}\cup(\mathcal{O}_{i}\cap\mathcal{X}_{r})
for all
i\in I_{A}
.

Case
\hat{i}\in I_{r}
.

Since
|\mathcal{O}_{\hat{i}}\cap\mathcal{X}_{r}|=1
we have
k_{\hat{i}}^{\prime}=k_{\hat{i}}-1
. Since
x_{c}
is legal to
\hat{i}
and has the lowest price among all items, a bundle in demand for player
\hat{i}
consists of
x_{c}
and a bundle
\mathcal{X}_{\hat{i}}
of size
k_{\hat{i}}^{\prime}
that is in demand in market
B
. Since
(p_{x})_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{B}}
a dynamic pricing in market
B
, there exists a legal allocation
\mathbfcal{O}^{B}
in market
B
such that
\mathcal{O}_{\hat{i}}^{B}=\mathcal{X}_{\hat{i}}
.

If
\hat{i}=i_{c}
, then we can define
\mathbfcal{O}^{A}
in the same way as in the first considered case. If
\hat{i}\neq i_{c}
, let
\hat{x}
be the unique item in
\mathcal{X}_{r}\cap\mathcal{O}_{\hat{i}}
. Then we obtain a legal allocation
\mathbfcal{O}^{A}
in market
A
such that
\mathcal{O}_{\hat{i}}^{A}=\mathcal{X}_{\hat{i}}\cup\{x_{c}\}
by defining

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{i}^{A}=\begin{cases}\mathcal{O}_{i}^{B}\cup(\mathcal{O}_{i}\cap\mathcal{X}_{r})\quad&\text{if }i\neq\hat{i}\text{ and }i\neq i_{c}\\
\mathcal{O}_{i}^{B}\cup\{x_{c}\}\quad&\text{if }i=\hat{i}\\
\mathcal{O}_{i}^{B}\cup\{\hat{x}\}\quad&\text{if }i=i_{c}\end{cases}\,.

∎

Now, we consider a different removable set
\mathcal{X}_{r}
in which there is a unique item assigned to every player, according to the allocation
\mathbfcal{O}
. We make sure that the items in
\mathcal{X}_{r}
are “well-connected”, as in
2
in Definition
7.4
, so that when some player takes an extra item in the submarket with items
\mathcal{X}_{A}\setminus\mathcal{X}_{r}
, the player who consequently loses an item can be compensated by taking two items from
\mathcal{X}_{r}
.

Definition 7.4
.

Let
\mathcal{X}_{r}\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{A}
be a subset of items and let
G_{r}
be the subgraph of
G
induced by
\mathcal{X}_{r}
. The set
\mathcal{X}_{r}
is called a removable set of type II with respect to the legal allocation
\mathbfcal{O}
if it satisfies the following conditions.

form a removable set of type III and
\mathcal{C}
is called a removable cycle.

Intuitively, we use a removable set
\mathcal{X}_{r}
of type III formed by
\mathcal{C}
in the following way. If no item is allocated to a player
i
,
i\in I_{A}
, then the choice of player
i
is determined solely by the dynamic pricing in the submarket of
A
with items
\mathcal{X}_{A}\setminus\mathcal{X}_{r}
. On the other hand, if a player
i
has exactly two legal items in
\mathcal{X}_{r}
, then these two items must be adjacent in the cycle. To ensure that an appropriate compensation is possible, we alternately assign “high” and “low” prices to items in the cycle
\mathcal{C}
so that player
i
selects exactly one item from
\mathcal{C}
. This intuition leads to the following lemma.

Lemma 8.3
.

Suppose
\mathcal{X}_{r}\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{A}
is a removable set of type III formed by a cycle
\mathcal{C}

x_{0}\to x_{1}\to x_{2}\to\ldots\to x_{l-1}\to x_{l}=x_{0}\,,

i.e.
\mathcal{X}_{r}=\{x_{j}\,:\,j=1,\ldots,l\}
. Let
B=(\mathcal{X}_{B},I_{B},(k_{i}^{\prime})_{i\in I_{B}},\mathscr{L})
be the submarket of
A
induced by
\mathbfcal{O}
with
\mathcal{X}_{B}=\mathcal{X}\setminus\mathcal{X}_{r}
. Any pricing
\mathbf{p}\in\real^{m}
satisfying the following conditions is a dynamic pricing in market
A
.

1.

For all
y_{1}\in\{x_{j}\,:\,j=1,\ldots,l,\,j\text{ is odd}\}
,
y_{2}\in\mathcal{X}_{B}
, and
y_{3}\in\{x_{j}\,:\,j=1,\ldots,l,\,j\text{ is even}\}
,

p_{y_{1}}>p_{y_{2}}>p_{y_{3}}\,.

2.

(p_{x})_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{B}}
is a dynamic pricing in market
B
.

Proof.

Suppose
x_{j}\in\mathcal{O}_{i_{j}}
for
j=0,\ldots,l
. Let us define
I_{r}:=\{i_{j}\,:\,j=0,\ldots,l\}\subseteq I_{A}
. For all
j=1,\ldots,l
, by construction of the legality graph
G
, we have that the item
x_{j}
is legal to players
i_{j}
and
i_{j-1}
. Since every item is legal to at most two players the item
x_{j}
is only legal to players
i_{j}
and
i_{j-1}
. By definition of uniquely assigned cycles, all
l
players in
I_{r}
are distinct. By Lemma
8.1
, items
x_{j}
and
x_{j+1}
are the only two items in
\mathcal{X}_{r}
that are legal to player
i_{j}
. Note that we identify
i_{l+1}
with
i_{1}
and
x_{l+1}
with
x_{1}
in this proof.

Note that

|\mathcal{O}_{i}\cap\mathcal{X}_{r}|=\begin{cases}1\quad&\text{if }i\in I_{r}\\
0\quad&\text{if }i\notin I_{r}\end{cases}

implies

k_{i}^{\prime}=\begin{cases}k_{i}-1\quad&\text{if }i\in I_{r}\\
k_{i}\quad&\text{if }i\notin I_{r}\end{cases}\,.

Consider a player
\hat{i}\in I_{A}
and let us show that any bundle in demand for player
\hat{i}
with respect to the prices
\mathbf{p}
can be extended to a legal allocation in market
A
. We consider the following three cases.

Case
\hat{i}=i_{\hat{j}}\in I_{r}
, for some
\hat{j}=1,\ldots,l
and
\hat{j}
is even.

In this case,
k_{\hat{i}}^{\prime}=k_{\hat{i}}-1
and the only two items that are legal to
\hat{i}
in
\mathcal{X}_{r}
are
x_{\hat{j}}
and
x_{\hat{j}+1}
. Since
\hat{j}
is even, for all items
x\in\mathcal{X}_{B}
, we have

p_{x_{\hat{j}+1}}>p_{x}>p_{x_{\hat{j}}}.

So for player
\hat{i}
the bundle in demand in the market
A
consists of the item
x_{\hat{j}}
and a bundle
\mathcal{X}_{\hat{i}}
of size
k_{\hat{i}}^{\prime}
that is in demand in market
B
. Since
(p_{x})_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{B}}
is a dynamic pricing in market
B
, there exists a legal allocation
\mathbfcal{O}^{B}
in market
B
, such that
\mathcal{O}_{\hat{i}}^{B}=\mathcal{X}_{\hat{i}}
. We then obtain a legal allocation
\mathbfcal{O}^{A}
in market
A
such that
\mathcal{O}_{\hat{i}}^{A}=\mathcal{X}_{\hat{i}}\cup\{x_{\hat{j}}\}
by defining

\mathcal{O}_{i}^{A}:=\begin{cases}\mathcal{O}_{i}^{B}\cup\{x_{j}\}\quad&\text{if }i\in I_{r}\text{ and }i=i_{j}\text{ for some }j=1,\ldots,l\\
\mathcal{O}_{i}^{B}\quad&\text{if }i\notin I_{r}\,.\end{cases}

Case
\hat{i}=i_{\hat{j}}\in I_{r}
, for some
\hat{j}=1,\ldots,l
and
\hat{j}
is odd.

This case is analogous to the previous case, except that for all items
x\in\mathcal{X}_{B}
, we have

p_{x_{\hat{j}}}>p_{x}>p_{x_{\hat{j}+1}}.

Accordingly, we need to define a legal allocation
\mathbfcal{O}^{A}
in market
A
as

\mathcal{O}_{i}^{A}:=\begin{cases}\mathcal{O}_{i}^{B}\cup\{x_{j+1}\}\quad&\text{if }i\in I_{r}\text{ and }i=i_{j}\text{ for some }j=1,\ldots,l\\
\mathcal{O}_{i}^{B}\quad&\text{if }i\notin I_{r}\,.\end{cases}

Case
\hat{i}\notin I_{r}
.

In this case, no item in
\mathcal{X}_{r}
is legal to
\hat{i}
. So for player
\hat{i}
, the bundle in demand is a set
\mathcal{X}_{\hat{i}}
of
k_{\hat{i}}^{\prime}=k_{\hat{i}}
items, where
\mathcal{X}_{\hat{i}}
is in demand in market
B
. Since
(p_{x})_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{B}}
is a dynamic pricing in market
B
, there exists a legal allocation
\mathbfcal{O}^{B}
in market
B
such that
\mathcal{O}_{\hat{i}}^{B}=\mathcal{X}_{\hat{i}}
. We can extend it to a legal allocation in market
A
in the same way as in either of the previous cases.
∎

This strategy of alternately assigning “high” and “low” prices in a uniquely assigned cycle is possible only when the cycle has an even length. Therefore, it would be desirable to have such cycles in
G
of even length. It turns out that even when such cycles do not exist, as long as we can find two appropriate intersecting odd cycles, it is possible to construct a removable set of type III by reallocating
\mathbfcal{O}
with respect to one of the two odd cycles.

Definition 8.4
.

Suppose there are two odd length cycles
\mathcal{C}_{1}

x_{0}\to x_{1}\to x_{2}\to\ldots\to x_{l_{1}-1}\to x_{l_{1}}=x_{0}

and
\mathcal{C}_{2}

y_{0}\to y_{1}\to y_{2}\to\ldots\to y_{l_{2}-1}\to y_{l_{2}}=y_{0}

in the legality graph
G
, with
x_{s}\in\mathcal{O}_{i_{x,s}}
for all
s=1,\ldots,l_{1}
and
y_{t}\in\mathcal{O}_{i_{y,t}}
for all
t=1,\dots,l_{2}
. The pair
(\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{2})
is called an odd cycle pair if
\mathcal{C}_{1}
and
\mathcal{C}_{2}
satisfy the following conditions

1.

x_{l_{1}-j}=y_{l_{2}-j}
for all
j=0,\ldots,r
, for some
r=0,\ldots,\min\{l_{1},l_{2}\}
.

2.

\{i_{x,s}\,:\,s=1,\ldots,l_{1}\}\cap\{i_{y,t}\,:\,t=1,\ldots,l_{2}\}
has exactly
l_{1}+l_{2}-(r+2)
players.

Lemma 8.5
.

If there is an odd cycle pair in
G
, then there exists a removable set of type III in market
A
when the legality graph is constructed with respect to another optimal allocation.

Proof.

Let
(\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{2})
be an odd cycle pair. Let us use the same notation as in Definition
8.4
. From
1
of Definition
8.4
, we know that
i_{x,l_{1}-j}=i_{y,l_{2}-j}
for
j=0,\ldots,r
. Moreover, since
x_{l_{1}-r}=y_{l_{2}-r}
, we know that
x_{l_{1}-r-1}
and
y_{l_{2}-r-1}
must be allocated to the same player. So, we also have
i_{x,l_{1}-r-1}=i_{y,l_{2}-r-1}
. By
2
of Definition
8.4
, these are exactly the
r+2
players that are common in
\mathcal{C}_{1}
and
\mathcal{C}_{2}
. All other players involved in the two cycles are necessarily distinct.

Let
\mathbfcal{O}^{2}
be the reallocation
\mathbfcal{O}
with respect to
\mathcal{C}_{2}
. By Corollary
6.8
,
\mathbfcal{O}^{2}
is also an optimal allocation in market
A
. Let
G_{2}
be the legality graph corresponding to allocation
\mathbfcal{O}^{2}
. In the graph
G_{2}
, the path
x_{0}\to x_{1}\to\ldots\to x_{l_{1}-r-2}\to x_{l_{1}-r-1}
remains unchanged in comparison to
G_{1}
. However, the cycle
y_{0}\to y_{1}\to\ldots\to y_{l_{2}-1}\to y_{l_{2}}
in
G_{1}
becomes the cycle
y_{l_{2}}\to y_{l_{2}-1}\to\ldots\to y_{1}\to y_{0}
in
G_{2}
; and item
y_{t}
is allocated to player
i_{y,t-1}
by
\mathbfcal{O}^{2}
for all
t=1,\ldots,l_{2}
.

In particular,
y_{l_{2}-r-1}
is no longer allocated to
i_{y,l_{2}-r-1}=i_{x,l_{1}-r-1}
; and
y_{1}
is now allocated to
i_{y,0}=i_{y,l_{2}}=i_{x,l_{1}}=i_{x,0}
. So, edges
x_{l_{1}-r-1}\to y_{l_{1}-r-1}
and
y_{1}\to x_{1}
are in
G_{2}
. Therefore, there is a cycle

x_{1}\to x_{2}\to\ldots\to x_{l_{1}-r-1}\to y_{l_{2}-r-1}\to y_{l_{2}-r-2}\to\ldots\to y_{2}\to y_{1}\to x_{1}

in the graph
G_{2}
and let us denote this cycle by
\mathcal{C}
.
Notice that
\mathcal{C}
is a uniquely assigned cycle in
G_{2}
of length

(l_{1}-r-1)+(l_{2}-r-1)=(l_{1}+l_{2})-2r-2,

which is even since
l_{1}
and
l_{2}
are both odd. By definition,
\mathcal{C}
is a removable cycle and the items in
\mathcal{C}
form a removable set of type III in the graph
G_{2}
.
∎

Lastly, we need to consider the case when there is no removable set formed by a uniquely assigned cycle of even length and no odd cycle pair in the market. So every uniquely assigned cycle has odd length. In this case we can get an additional property about the items in uniquely assigned cycles. In particular, if we remove all but one item
x
in an odd length cycle
\mathcal{C}
from the market, we can show that in the resulting submarket the item
x
is legal to only one player. Using this property, we define a fourth type of removable set that contains all items in
\mathcal{C}
except for one item
x
and show that we can again alternately assign “high” and “low” prices to the items in such a removable set as we did in Lemma
8.3
.

Definition 8.6
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a uniquely assigned odd length cycle in
G
of the form

x_{0}\to x_{1}\to x_{2}\to\ldots\to x_{l-1}\to x_{l}=x_{0}\,.

If there does not exist a uniquely assigned even cycle or an odd cycle pair in the graph
G
, then
\{x_{j}\,:\,j=1,\ldots,l-1\}
is called a removable set of type IV.

Lemma 8.7
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a cycle in
G

x_{0}\to x_{1}\to x_{2}\to\ldots\to x_{l-1}\to x_{l}=x_{0}

such that
\mathcal{X}_{r}\coloneqq\{x_{j}\,:\,j=1,\ldots,l-1\}\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{A}
is a removable set of type IV in
G
. Let
B=(\mathcal{X}_{B},I_{B},(k_{i}^{\prime})_{i\in I_{B}},\mathscr{L})
be the submarket of
A
induced by
\mathbfcal{O}
with items
\mathcal{X}_{B}=\mathcal{X}_{A}\setminus\mathcal{X}_{r}
. Any pricing
\mathbf{p}\in\real^{m}
satisfying the following conditions is a dynamic pricing in market
A
.

1.

For all
y_{1}\in\{x_{j}\,:\,j=1,\ldots,l-1,\,j\text{ is odd}\}
,
y_{2}\in\mathcal{X}_{B}
, and
y_{3}\in\{x_{j}\,:\,j=1,\ldots,l-1,\,j\text{ is even}\}

p_{y_{1}}>p_{y_{2}}>p_{y_{3}}\,.

(p_{x})_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{B}}

Proof.

Suppose
x_{j}\in\mathcal{O}_{i_{j}}

I_{r}=:\{i_{j}\,:\,j=1,\ldots,l-1\}\subseteq I_{A}

|\mathcal{O}_{\hat{i}}\cap\mathcal{X}_{r}|=\begin{cases}1\quad&\text{if }i\in I_{r}\\

Given a multi-demand market with a set

of

3

Theorem 8.9
.

A dynamic pricing exists in a multi-demand market with at most two optimal allocations. It can be computed using Algorithm
3
.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on the number of items
m
in market. As in Theorem
7.7
, the base cases are markets with at most three items. From the induction hypothesis, we may assume that a dynamic pricing can be found in any multi-demand market with less than
m
items and at most two optimal allocations.

We first compute the rough prices as in Algorithm
1
and let market

be the induced simplified market described in Section
5
. We now show that the price vector
\mathbf{p}^{F}
defined in line
18
of Algorithm

is a dynamic pricing in market

.

Let
G
be the legality graph with respect to some legal allocation
\mathbfcal{O}
of market
A
. By Lemma
6.12
and Definition
8.6

has a removable cycle,

∎

9
Market with Tri-Demand Players

We consider a saturated market
A=(\mathcal{X}_{A},I_{A},\mathbf{k},\mathscr{L}\})
. Let us again use the usual notation for the number of items and players in the market
m:=|\mathcal{X}_{A}|
and
n:=|I_{A}|
. Following the ideas in Section
5
, it is enough to consider markets with the following valuation functions for
i\in I_{A}
,
x\in\mathcal{X}_{A}

v_{i}(x)=\begin{cases}1\quad\text{if }x\in\mathscr{L}(i)\\
0\quad\text{if }x\notin\mathscr{L}(i)\,.\end{cases}

In this section, we focus on the special case where the demand of every player is at most three, i.e.
k_{i}
is in
\{1,2,3\}
for all players
i\in I_{A}
. We define the following property:

An item
x\in\mathcal{X}_{A}
is legal to a player
i\in I_{A}
if and only if
v_{i}(x)=1
.

(P)

Notice that market
A
as a whole satisfies property (
P
), but since the converse of Remark
6.4
is not necessarily true, it is not always the case that every submarket of
A
satisfies property (
P
). For saturated tri-demand markets with property (
P
), instead of simply showing the existence of a dynamic pricing, we prove a stronger statement: For every item
x\in\mathcal{X}_{A}
, there exists a dynamic pricing in market
A
such that item
x
is priced “the lowest”.

Definition 9.1
.

Given item
x\in\mathcal{X}_{A}
and dynamic pricing
\mathbf{p}\in\real_{>0}^{m}
of market
A
, we say that
\mathbf{p}
is a dynamic pricing fixed at
x
if
p_{y}>p_{x}
for all
y\in\mathcal{X}_{A}\setminus\{x\}
.

In this section, we work extensively with ordering of items in terms of prices, so we introduce the following notation. Given a price vector
\mathbf{p}\in\real^{m}
, for two disjoint subsets
\mathcal{X}_{1},\mathcal{X}_{2}\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{A}
, we write
\mathcal{X}_{1}<_{p}\mathcal{X}_{2}
to indicate that for all
x\in\mathcal{X}_{1}
and
y\in\mathcal{X}_{2}
we have
p_{x}<p_{y}
.

We often need to define the lowest-priced items in submarkets of market
A
and use them to combine dynamic pricings in different submarkets in a meaningful way. To ensure that such items are well-defined, we make the
assumption that given a dynamic pricing in a submarket of
A
, no two items have the same price. This assumption is
valid because of the following reasoning. Consider a dynamic pricing
\mathbf{p}
where the price
p_{x}
of an item
x
is the same for at least two different items in the market. For all players and all items
y
with strictly lower utility than
x
, we define a positive
\varepsilon
such that
\varepsilon
is less than the smallest difference in utility and
p_{x}+\epsilon
do not coincide with any existing price in the market. Then, we
redefine the price of
x
to be
p_{x}+\varepsilon
. Notice that any item that used to have higher (lower) utility
than
x
still has higher (lower) utility. Consider a player
i
for whom some item
y
previously had
the same utility as
x
. This means that
i
was indifferent between
x
and
y
and
i
is free to pick either
item. So, increasing the price of
x
by
\varepsilon
yields another dynamic pricing. We can repeat this process until every item in the market has a different price.

We would like to use induction on the number of players plus the number of items in the markets. We first prove two base cases, namely a market with two players and a market with two item.

Lemma 9.2
.

If
n=2
then for every
x\in\mathcal{X}_{A}
there exists a dynamic pricing fixed at
x
.

Proof.

It is enough to select prices
\mathbf{p}
such that
\mathscr{L}(1)\setminus\mathscr{L}(2)<_{p}\left(\mathscr{L}(1)\cap\mathscr{L}(2)\right)\setminus\{x\}
and

\mathscr{L}(2)\setminus\mathscr{L}(1)<_{p}\left(\mathscr{L}(1)\cap\mathscr{L}(2)\right)\setminus\{x\}
and
\{x\}<_{p}\mathcal{X}_{A}\setminus\{x\}
.
∎

Corollary 9.3
.

If
m=2
then for every
x\in\mathcal{X}_{A}
there exists a dynamic pricing fixed at
x
.

Proof.

This is immediate from Lemma
9.2
since
m=2
implies that
n\leq 2
.
∎

With the two base cases proved, for the rest of this section, we make the following assumption. In any submarket
B=(\mathcal{X}_{B},I_{B},(k_{i})_{i\in I_{B}},\mathscr{L})
of
A
with
\mathcal{X}_{B}\subset\mathcal{X}_{A}
or
I_{B}\subset I_{A}
, such that property (
P
) holds for
B
, there exists a dynamic pricing fixed at
x
for any
x\in\mathcal{X}_{B}
. We will refer to this assumption as the induction hypothesis.

In this section, we adopt a slightly different notion of submarket, which we use for the rest of this paper.

Definition 9.4
.

A submarket of market
A
is a market
B=(\mathcal{X}_{B},I_{B},(k_{i})_{i\in B},\mathscr{L})
such that
\mathcal{X}_{B}\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{A}
and
I_{B}\subseteq I_{A}
.

We also extend the notion of the legality function to subsets of players as follows.

Definition 9.5
.

We define
\mathscr{L}:2^{I_{A}}\times 2^{\mathcal{X}_{A}}\to 2^{\mathcal{X}_{A}}
to be the function given by

\mathscr{L}(I,\mathcal{X}):=\{x\in\mathcal{X}:x\text{ is legal to }i\text{ for some }i\in I\}

for all
I\subseteq I_{A}
and
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{A}
.

For simplicity, when
I=\{i\}
or
\mathcal{X}=\{x\}
is a singleton, we can omit the set brackets and write
\mathscr{L}(I,\mathcal{X})=\mathscr{L}(i,\mathcal{X})
or
\mathscr{L}(I,\mathcal{X})=\mathscr{L}(I,x)
.
Notice that
\mathscr{L}(i,\mathcal{X}_{A})=\mathscr{L}(i)
for all
i\in I_{A}
. So, this new definition is indeed a generalization of the original legality function.

Note that by construction, the legality function
\mathscr{L}
satisfies the following properties:

9.5.1.

For all
I\subseteq I_{A}
and
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{A}
, we have
\mathscr{L}(I,\mathcal{X})\subseteq\mathcal{X}
.

9.5.2.

For all
I_{1}
,
I_{2}\subseteq I_{A}
and
\mathcal{X}_{1},\mathcal{X}_{2}\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{A}
, we have

\mathscr{L}(I_{1}\cup I_{2},\mathcal{X}_{1}\cup\mathcal{X}_{2})=\mathscr{L}(I_{1},\mathcal{X}_{1})\cup\mathscr{L}(I_{1},\mathcal{X}_{2})\cup\mathscr{L}(I_{2},\mathcal{X}_{1})\cup\mathscr{L}(I_{2},\mathcal{X}_{2})\,.

9.5.3.

For all
I_{1}
,
I_{2}\subseteq I_{A}
, such that
I_{1}\subseteq I_{2}
, and
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{A}
, we have
\mathscr{L}(I_{1},\mathcal{X})\subseteq\mathscr{L}(I_{2},\mathcal{X})
.

9.5.4.

For
I\subseteq I_{A}
and
\mathcal{X}_{1}
,
\mathcal{X}_{2}
, such that
\mathcal{X}_{2}\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{A}
,
\mathcal{X}_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{2}
, we have
\mathscr{L}(I,\mathcal{X}_{1})\subseteq\mathscr{L}(I,\mathcal{X}_{2})
.

A
submarket pair
consists of two related submarkets and can be viewed as a partition of market
A
with additional properties specified in Defintion
9.6
.

Definition 9.6
.

Suppose
B=(\mathcal{X}_{B},I_{B},(k_{i})_{i\in I_{B}},\mathscr{L})
and
C=(\mathcal{X}_{C},I_{C},(k_{i})_{i\in I_{C}},\mathscr{L})
are submarkets of
A
, such that the following conditions are satisfied.

1.

\mathcal{X}_{B}\cup\mathcal{X}_{C}=\mathcal{X}_{A}
,
\mathcal{X}_{B}\cap\mathcal{X}_{C}=\varnothing
, and
\mathcal{X}_{B},\mathcal{X}_{C}\neq\varnothing
.

2.

I_{B}\cup I_{C}=I_{A}
,
I_{B}\cap I_{C}=\varnothing
and
I_{B},I_{C}\neq\varnothing
.

3.

\mathscr{L}(I_{B},\mathcal{X}_{A})\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{B}
.

4.

|\mathcal{X}_{B}|-1=\sum\limits_{i\in I_{B}}k_{i}
and
|\mathcal{X}_{C}|+1=\sum\limits_{i\in I_{C}}k_{i}
.

5.

|\mathscr{L}(I_{C},\mathcal{X}_{B})|\geq 2
and for all
\hat{x}\subseteq\mathscr{L}(I_{C},\mathcal{X}_{B})
, there is a legal allocation
\mathbfcal{O}
in market
A
such that
\bigcup\limits_{i\in I_{B}}\mathcal{O}_{i}=\mathcal{X}_{B}\setminus\{\hat{x}\}
and
\bigcup\limits_{i\in I_{C}}\mathcal{O}_{i}=\mathcal{X}_{C}\cup\{\hat{x}\}

Then, we say that
(B,C)
form a submarket pair in market
A
.

By definition of a legal item, we know that any assignment of a single item can be extended to a legal allocation as long as legality is respected. We define such an assignment as a
legal assignment
.

Definition 9.7
.

A legal assignment of a subset
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{A}
is a way of assigning items in
\mathcal{X}
to players
I_{A}
so that the items received by each player are legal for that player. Such assignment is also called a legal assignment of size
\hat{m}
when
\hat{m}=|\mathcal{X}|
.

We could think of a legal assignment as a vector
\mathbfcal{A}=(\mathcal{A}_{i})_{i\in I_{A}}
of disjoint subsets of
\mathcal{X}
, indexed by the players. When the number of items received by every player
i\in I_{A}
does not exceed the demand
k_{i}
, we say that the legal assignment
respects demand
. If there is a legal allocation
\mathbfcal{O}
where
\mathcal{A}_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{i}
for all
i\in I_{A}
, we say the legal assignment is extendable. Otherwise, we say that the legal assignment is not extendable.

Note that a legal assignment of more than one item is not necessarily extendable. Let us consider a legal assignment of size two that is not extendable.

Suppose there exist two items

\displaystyle|\mathcal{X}_{B}^{*}|=|\mathcal{X}_{B}\cup\hat{\mathcal{X}}_{B}|

\displaystyle=|\mathcal{X}_{B}|+|\hat{\mathcal{X}}_{B}|-|\mathcal{X}_{B}\cap\hat{\mathcal{X}}_{B}|=\sum\limits_{i\in I_{B}}k_{i}+1+\sum\limits_{i\in\hat{I}_{B}}k_{i}+1-|\mathcal{X}_{B}\cap\hat{\mathcal{X}}_{B}|

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i\in I_{B}^{*}}k_{i}+\sum\limits_{i\in I_{B}\cap\hat{I}_{B}}k_{i}+2-|\mathcal{X}_{B}\cap\hat{\mathcal{X}}_{B}|

(2)

\displaystyle|\mathcal{X}_{B}^{*}|=|\mathcal{X}_{B}\cup\hat{\mathcal{X}}_{B}|

\displaystyle|\mathcal{X}_{B}\cap\hat{\mathcal{X}}_{B}|
