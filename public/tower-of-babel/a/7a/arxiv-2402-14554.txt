Abstract
. In this work we prove the Stepanov differentiation theorem for multiple-valued functions. This theorem is proved in the wide generality of metric-space-multiple-valued functions without relying on a Lipschitz extension result. General definitions of differentiability and approximate differentiability for functions between suitable metric spaces are also introduced.

1.
Introduction

Q
-valued functions has been developed by Almgren to study regularity properties of mass-minimizing currents. In particular, in
[
1
]
he generalized the concepts of differential and Sobolev space for
Q
-valued functions, i.e. functions with values in the space
\mathcal{A}_{Q}\left(M\right)
made by unordered
Q
-tuple of points in a metric space
M
, in order to study regularity properties of “Dir-minimizing” functions, which are
Q
-valued functions that minimize a suitable generalization of the Dirichlet energy for
Q
-valued functions. His work about Dir-minimizing functions has been readapted and simplified in
[
3
]
.

When
M={\mathbb{R}}^{k}
, we say that a
Q
-valued function
{f}:{\Omega\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{A}_{Q}\left({\mathbb{R}}^{k}\right)}
is differentiable at
x\in\Omega
with
f(x)=\left\llbracket P_{1}\right\rrbracket+\left\llbracket P_{2}\right\rrbracket+\dotsb+\left\llbracket P_{Q}\right\rrbracket
if and only if there exist
M_{1},M_{2},\dotsc,M_{Q}

(k\times n)
-matrix such that, setting

\displaystyle F(z)=\sum^{Q}_{i=1}\left\llbracket P_{i}+M_{i}z\right\rrbracket\text{,}

we have

\displaystyle\lim_{y\to x}\frac{\mathcal{G}\left[f(y),F(y-x)\right]}{\left\lvert y-x\right\rvert}=0

(where
\mathcal{G}\left[\cdot,\cdot\right]
is the canonical metric on
\mathcal{A}_{Q}\left(M\right)
as defined in section
3
) and if
P_{i}=P_{j}
for some
i,j
then
M_{i}=M_{j}
.

In
[
3
]
the following version of the Rademacher theorem for
Q
-valued functions is proved:

Theorem 1.1
.

If
{f}:{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{A}_{Q}\left({\mathbb{R}}^{k}\right)}
is a Lipschitz
Q
-valued function then
f
is a.e. differentiable on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

To prove it the authors used a Lipschitz extension result for
Q
-valued Lipschitz functions from a closed subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
to all
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. However, we cannot expect a Lipschitz extension result when we replace
{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
with a generic metric space, because it is not always possible to globally extend a continuous function defined on a closed subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
with values on a disconnected metric space.

However, an extension result for
Q
-valued Lipschitz functions is not strictly required, since it is possible to prove theorem
1.1
without using the extension result for Lipschitz
Q
-valued functions at the cost of introducing a slightly weaker notion of differentiable function (which coincides with the classical one on interior points).

The main goal of this work is to prove the the Stepanov theorem for
Q
-valued functions:

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
{f}:{B}\rightarrow{\mathcal{A}_{Q}\left({\mathbb{R}}^{k}\right)}
be a measurable function defined on a measurable set
B\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
without isolated points. Let

\displaystyle\left\lvert\nabla f\right\rvert^{+}(x)=\limsup_{y\in B\to x}\frac{\mathcal{G}\left[f(y),f(x)\right]}{\left\lvert y-x\right\rvert}

\displaystyle A_{f}=\left\{{x\in B}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}{\left\lvert\nabla f\right\rvert^{+}(x)<+\infty}\right\}\text{.}

Then
f
is a.e. differentiable on
A_{f}
.

However, we will generalize these concepts in order to functions defined on doubling metric measure spaces with values on complete metric spaces and not necessarly measurable.

In Section
2
of this work we introduce a broad definition of “differential classes” for functions defined between two metric spaces. This definition generalizes the usual definition of differentiation for single-valued functions defined between euclidean spaces,
Q
-valued functions and real-valued functions defined on
p
-Poincaré metric measuree spaces (see
[
2
]
,
[
5
]
and
[
4
]
). In particular, we say that a function
f
defined on a nonempty subset of a doubling metric measure space with values on a complete metric space is differentiable at some accumulation point
x
if and only if its first-order asymptotical behaviour near
x
belongs to a suitable “differential structure” that makes every Lipschitz function defined on closed subsets differentiable almost everywhere. This Rademacher-type condition is all we need to prove Stepanov’s theorem for functions between metric spaces.

Section
3
is instead entirely devoted to differentiability properties of metric
Q
-valued functions, that is functions with values in
\mathcal{A}_{Q}\left(M\right)
instead of
\mathcal{A}_{Q}\left({\mathbb{R}}^{k}\right)
with
M
any complete metric space, and to find sufficient conditions for the Rademacher-type condition stated in previous section in order to prove a more general version of theorem
1.2
.

2.
Generalized differentiability

2.1.
Differential germs

Let
(X,\mathtt{J},\mu)
be a
K
-doubling metric measure space (
K>0
), that is,
(X,\mathtt{J})
is a complete separable metric space and
{\mu}:{\mathcal{P}(X)}\rightarrow{\left[{0},{+\infty}\right]}
is a Borel-regular outer measure on
X
such that for every
x\in X
and every
r>0
we have

0<\mu\left[B_{2r}(x)\right]\leq K\mu\left[B_{r}(x)\right]<+\infty\text{.}

Remark
.

It is simple to show that for every
x,y\in X
and
r,s>0

\displaystyle\mu\left[B_{r}(x)\right]\leq\mathcal{L}\left(K,\frac{r+\mathtt{J}(x,y)}{s}\right)\mu\left[B_{s}(y)\right]

where
\mathcal{L}(\alpha,\beta)>0
is a suitable funcion. Indeed we have

\mu\left[B_{r}(x)\right]\leq\mu\left[B_{r+\mathtt{J}(x,y)}(y)\right]\leq\mu\left[B_{2^{P\left[\left(r+\mathtt{J}(x,y)\right)/s\right]}s}(y)\right]\\
\leq K^{P\left[\left(r+\mathtt{J}(x,y)\right)/s\right]}\mu\left[B_{s}(y)\right]

where
P(t)=\max\left\{{0,\lceil\log_{2}t\rceil}\right\}
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
E\subseteq X
be any set, there exists a Borel sets
B\supseteq E
such that
\mu\left[B^{\prime}\cap B_{r}(x)\right]\geq\mu\left[B\cap B_{r}(x)\right]
for every
x\in X,r>0
and every other measurable set
B^{\prime}\supseteq E\cap B_{r}(x)
.

Proof.

Fix a point
x_{0}\in X
and set
U_{r}=B_{r}\left(x_{0}\right)
for every
r>0
. By hypothesis for every
r>0
we have
0<\mu\left(U_{r}\right)<+\infty
and there exists
E\cap U_{r}\subseteq B_{r}\subseteq U_{r}
Borel set such that
\mu\left(B_{r}\right)=\mu\left(E\cap U_{r}\right)
since
\mu
is Borel-regular. Then for every
B_{s}(y)\subseteq U_{r}
we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mu\left(E\cap U_{r}\right)&\leq\mu\left(E\cap B_{s}(y)\right)+\mu\left(E\cap U_{r}\setminus B_{s}(y)\right)\\
&\leq\mu\left(B_{r}\cap B_{s}(y)\right)+\mu\left(B_{r}\setminus B_{s}(y)\right)\\
&=\mu\left(B_{r}\right)=\mu\left(E\cap U_{r}\right)\end{split}

which implies
\mu\left(E\cap B_{s}(y)\right)=\mu\left(B_{r}\cap B_{s}(y)\right)
for every
B_{s}(y)\subseteq U_{r}
.

Now let
B=\bigcup^{\infty}_{S=1}\bigcap^{\infty}_{R=S}B_{R}
, clearly
E\subseteq B
and
B
is a Borel set. For every ball
B_{s}(y)
there exists
\tilde{S}\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
B_{s}(y)\subseteq U_{\tilde{S}}
and so

\displaystyle\mu\left(B\cap B_{s}(y)\right)=\lim_{S\to+\infty}\mu\left(\bigcap^{+\infty}_{R=S}\left(B_{R}\cap B_{s}(y)\right)\right)\leq\mu\left(E\cap B_{s}(y)\right)\text{.}

Therefore
\mu\left(B\cap B_{s}(y)\right)=\mu\left(E\cap B_{s}(y)\right)
.
∎

A point
x
of
E
is said to be a
\mu
-interior point (for
E
) if and only if there exists
B\subseteq E
Borel set such that

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}\frac{\mu\left[B_{r}(x)\setminus B\right]}{\mu\left[B_{r}(x)\right]}=0\text{.}

Let also
{E}^{1}\subseteq E
be the set of all the
\mu
-interior points for
E
, and if
x\in{E}^{1}
then we say that
E
is a
\mu
-neighborhood for
x
.

Notice that
E
and
{E}^{1}
may not be
\mu
-measurable sets, however if
E
is
\mu
-measurable then
\mu\left(E\setminus{E}^{1}\right)=0
by using the doubling property of
\mu
(see for example
[
6
]
). Moreover, if
E
and
F
are two
\mu
-neighborhoods for
x
then
E\cap F
is another
\mu
-neighborhood for
x
because

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}\frac{\mu\left[B_{r}(x)\setminus(E\cap F)\right]}{\mu\left[B_{r}(x)\right]}\leq\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}\frac{\mu\left[B_{r}(x)\setminus E\right]}{\mu\left[B_{r}(x)\right]}+\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}\frac{\mu\left[B_{r}(x)\setminus F\right]}{\mu\left[B_{r}(x)\right]}=0\text{.}

Therefore the family of
\mu
-neighborhoods defines a topology on
X
, that we will call the
\mu
-topology. This topology is clearly stronger than the metric topology induced by
\mathtt{J}
on
X
(which we will also call
\mathtt{J}
-topology).

Proposition 2.2
.

For every
x\in X
all the following statements are equivalent:

•

x
is an isolated point (for
X
) with respect to the
\mathtt{J}
-topology;

•

x
is a
\mu
-isolated point;

•

\mu\left(\left\{{x}\right\}\right)>0
.

Proof.

If
x
is an isolated point then there exists
r>0
such that
B_{r}(x)=\left\{{x}\right\}
, and since
B_{r}(x)
is itself a
\mu
-neighborhood for
x
then this would automatically imply that
x
is an isolated point.

Next if
x
is
\mu
isolated then
\left\{{x}\right\}
is a
\mu
-neighborhood for
x
so

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}\frac{\mu\left[B_{r}(x)\setminus\left\{{x}\right\}\right]}{\mu\left[B_{r}(x)\right]}=0

which is possible only if
\mu(\left\{{x}\right\})>0
otherwise
\mu\left[B_{r}(x)\setminus\left\{{x}\right\}\right]=\mu\left[B_{r}(x)\right]
.

Finally we only need to prove that if
\mu(\left\{{x}\right\})=\delta>0
then
x
is an isolated point, that is
B_{r}(x)=\left\{{x}\right\}
for some
r>0
. First to all notice that, since
\mu\left[B_{r}(x)\right]<+\infty
,
\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}\mu\left[B_{r}(x)\setminus\left\{{x}\right\}\right]=0
. Therefore there exists
\tilde{r}>0
such that
\mu\left[B_{r}(x)\setminus\left\{{x}\right\}\right]<\delta/K
for every
0<r<\tilde{r}
.

By contradiction
x
is not an isolated point in
X
, therefore fixed
0<r<\tilde{r}
there exist
y\neq x
and
s>0
such that

•

B_{s}(y)\subseteq B_{r}(x)\setminus\left\{{x}\right\}
;

•

x\in B_{2s}(y)
.

Then

\displaystyle\mu\left[B_{s}(y)\right]\leq\mu\left[B_{r}(x)\setminus\left\{{x}\right\}\right]<\frac{\delta}{K}=\frac{1}{K}\mu\left(\left\{{x}\right\}\right)\leq\frac{1}{K}\mu\left[B_{2s}(y)\right]\leq\mu\left[B_{s}(y)\right]

which is impossible, therefore
\left\{{x}\right\}
must be an isolated point.
∎

Proposition 2.3
.

Consider any nonempty set
U\subseteq X
. Then for every
x\in{U}^{1}
we can find an application
{r_{x}}:{X}\rightarrow{U}
such that
r_{x}(y)=y
on
U
and

\displaystyle\mathtt{J}\left[y,r_{x}(y)\right]=o\left(\mathtt{J}\left(y,x\right)\right)\text{.}

Proof.

We can assume without loss of generality that
x
is an accumulation point (that is,
B_{r}(x)\neq\left\{{x}\right\}
for every
r>0
or equivalently
\mu(\left\{{x}\right\})=0
) and
U
is a Borel set. For every
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
we can find
\delta_{N}>0
such that for every

\displaystyle\frac{\mu\left[B_{r}(x)\setminus U\right]}{\mu\left[B_{r}(x)\right]}\leq\frac{1}{N}\text{.}

In order to prove our statement we need only to prove that for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\delta>0
so that for every
y\in X
with
\left\lvert y-x\right\rvert<\delta
there exists
y^{\prime}\in U
such that

(2.1)

\displaystyle\mathtt{J}\left(y,y^{\prime}\right)<\varepsilon\mathtt{J}\left(y,x\right)\text{.}

If (
2.1
) does not hold then there exists
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that for every
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
there exists
y_{N}
such that
0<r_{N}=\mathtt{J}\left(y_{N},x\right)<\delta_{N}/\left(1+\varepsilon_{0}\right)
but
B_{\varepsilon_{0}r_{N}}\left(y_{N}\right)\cap U=\emptyset
. Therefore, since we have
\left(1+\varepsilon_{0}\right)r_{N}<\delta_{N}
,

\mu\left[B_{\varepsilon_{0}r_{N}}\left(y_{N}\right)\right]\leq\left\lvert B_{\left(1+\varepsilon_{0}\right)r_{N}}(x)\setminus U\right\rvert\leq\frac{1}{N}\mu\left[B_{(1+\varepsilon_{0})r_{N}}(x)\right]\\
\leq\frac{1}{N}\mu\left[B_{(2+\varepsilon_{0})r_{N}}(y_{N})\right]\leq\frac{K_{\varepsilon_{0}}}{N}\mu\left[B_{\varepsilon_{0}r_{N}}(y_{N})\right]

where

is a constant that depends only on
\varepsilon_{0}>0
and
\mu
but not on

∎

Proposition 2.4

Let

\displaystyle\limsup_{y\to x}\frac{f(y)}{\mathtt{J}(y,x)}=\mu-\limsup_{y\to x}\frac{f(y)}{\mathtt{J}(y,x)}\text{.}

Proof.

The inequality
\geq
is trivial, to prove the opposite inequality take any

+\sup_{y\in B_{\delta}(x)\cap B\setminus\left\{{x}\right\}}\frac{\left\lvert f(y)-f[r_{x}(y)]\right\rvert}{\mathtt{J}(y,x)}\leq(1+\varepsilon)\sup_{y\in C}\frac{f(y)}{\mathtt{J}(y,x)}+K\varepsilon

Proof.

3.
Q-valued functions

On a generic metric space

, where

is a positive integer, as the set of all measures
\mathbf{P}
on

that can be represented as the sum of

\left\llbracket P_{1}\right\rrbracket+\left\llbracket P_{2}\right\rrbracket+\dotsb+\left\llbracket P_{Q}\right\rrbracket
not necessarily distinct. Then a
Q
-valued function from a set

to
M
is just a function defined on
A
with values on

, clearly

-valued functions from

to

are equivalent to classical (single-valued) functions from
A
to
M
.

Moreover,
Q

can be used to compose a single
Q
-valued function

, converselt every
Q
-valued function

can be globally decomposed into
Q

M
. However, such decomposition is never useful except when
M
is a really good space (usually

) because many useful properties of
f
will not be inherited by such general decompositions.

On
\mathcal{A}_{Q}\left(M\right)
we define the following
Wassenstein metric
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}\left[\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}\right]=\mathcal{G}\left[\sum^{Q}_{i=1}\left\llbracket A_{i}\right\rrbracket,\sum^{Q}_{i=1}\left\llbracket B_{i}\right\rrbracket\right]=\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{Q}}\sqrt{\sum^{Q}_{i=1}d\left(A_{1},B_{\sigma(i)}\right)^{2}}

where
\mathcal{S}_{Q}
is the set of all the permutations on
\left\{{1,2,\dotsc,Q}\right\}
. Therefore a
Q
-valued function
f
from a topological/metric/measurable space
\Omega
to
M
is
continuous/Lipschitz/measurable
if and only if
{f}:{\Omega}\rightarrow{\mathcal{A}_{Q}\left(M\right)}
is continuous/Lipschitz/measurable. Every measurable
Q
-valued function can be globally decomposed into
Q
measurable single-valued functions (see
[
3
]
), however continuous
Q
-valued functions cannot usually be decomposed into
Q
continuous single-valued functions, for example the following function

\displaystyle{z\in{\mathbb{C}}}\rightarrow{\left\llbracket z^{3/2}\right\rrbracket+\left\llbracket-z^{3/2}\right\rrbracket\in\mathcal{A}_{2}\left({\mathbb{C}}\right)}

cannot be decomposed into two continuous functions in any neighborhood of the origin.

If
\mathcal{D}
is an
X
-distribution for a complete metric space

then we are able to derive another

-distribution
\mathcal{D}^{Q}
but on

: for every

we say
\left[\mathbf{F}\right]\in\mathcal{D}^{Q}_{x_{0},\mathbf{P}}
if and only if there exist
\left[F_{i}\right]\in\mathcal{D}_{x_{0},P_{i}}
for every
i=1,2,\dotsc,Q
such that

(3.1)

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}(x)=\sum^{Q}_{i=1}\left\llbracket F_{i}(x)\right\rrbracket\ \forall x\text{ in a Borel neighborhood of }x_{0}

(3.2)

\displaystyle\text{if }P_{i}=P_{j}\text{ then }\left[F_{i}\right]=\left[F_{j}\right]\text{.}

Notice that
\mathcal{A}_{Q}\left(M\right)
is always a complete metric space whenever
M
is complete, and can be proved by induction on
Q
. This is clearly trivial for
Q=1
, thus assume both
M
and
\mathcal{A}_{Q}\left(M\right)
complete. Let
\mathbf{P}_{l}\in\mathcal{A}_{Q+1}\left(M\right)
be a Cauchy sequence, by induction on
l
we can easily find a subsequence in the form
\mathbf{P}_{h_{l}}=\left\llbracket P_{l}\right\rrbracket+\mathbf{P}^{\prime}_{l}
, where both
P_{l}\in M
and
\mathbf{P}^{\prime}_{l}\in\mathcal{A}_{Q}\left(M\right)
are Cauchy sequences that converge to some
P\in M
and
\mathbf{P}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}_{Q}\left(M\right)
respectively. Therefore
\mathbf{P}_{h_{l}}\to\left\llbracket P\right\rrbracket+\mathbf{P}^{\prime}
which in turn implies
\mathbf{P}_{l}\to\left\llbracket P\right\rrbracket+\mathbf{P}^{\prime}
because
\mathbf{P}_{l}
is Cauchy.

Many authors (see for example
[
3
]
) imposes condition (
3.2
) in their definition of differentiable
Q
-valued functions since it makes easier to work componentwise. For example if
M={\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and
\left[\mathbf{F}\right]\in\mathcal{D}^{Q}
is a differential germ for
f=\sum^{Q}_{i=1}\left\llbracket f_{i}\right\rrbracket
at some
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
with
f_{i}
continuous at
x
then we can always take the
i
-th component
F_{i}\in\mathcal{D}
of
\mathbf{F}
so that
\left[F_{i}\right]
is a
\mathcal{D}
-differential for
f_{i}
at
x
.

Instead other authors (for example
[
7
]
) do not use (
3.2
) in their definition of differentiable
Q
-valued functions, since with (
3.2
) we could have a sequence of
C^{1}

Q
-valued functions
f_{n}
such that both
f_{n}
and
\nabla f_{n}
uniformly converge to some functions
f
and
F
respectively but
f
is not differentiable everywhere! Therefore when one need to work with sequences of
C^{1}

Q
-valued functions it may be useful to require only (
3.1
) without (
3.2
).

However, if one need only a.e. differentiability (for example when dealing with Sobolev functions) then (
3.2
) is unimportant as we will see later. To show this, in this place we will always assume that germs in
\mathcal{D}^{Q}
satisfy both (
3.1
) and (
3.2
).

Theorem 3.1
.

For every
\mathbf{P}\in\mathcal{A}_{Q}\left(M\right)\setminus\left\{{Q\left\llbracket P\right\rrbracket}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}{P\in M}\right\}
there exist
1\leq R<Q
, an open neighborhood
U
of
\mathbf{P}
and two continuous maps
{\pi_{1}^{\mathbf{P}}}:{U}\rightarrow{\mathcal{A}_{R}\left(M\right)}
,
{\pi_{2}^{\mathbf{P}}}:{U}\rightarrow{\mathcal{A}_{Q-R}\left(M\right)}
such that

•

\mathbf{Q}=\pi_{1}^{\mathbf{P}}(\mathbf{Q})+\pi_{2}^{\mathbf{P}}(\mathbf{Q})
for every
\mathbf{Q}\in U
;

•

\mathcal{G}\left[\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{R}\right]^{2}=\mathcal{G}\left[\pi_{1}^{\mathbf{P}}(\mathbf{Q}),\pi_{1}^{\mathbf{P}}(\mathbf{R})\right]^{2}+\mathcal{G}\left[\pi_{2}^{\mathbf{P}}(\mathbf{Q}),\pi_{2}^{\mathbf{P}}(\mathbf{R})\right]^{2}
for every
\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{R}\in U
.

Proof.

Clearly there exist
1\leq R<Q
,
P
,
P_{R+1},P_{R+2},\dotsc,P_{Q}\in M
, and
\varepsilon>0
such that
\mathbf{P}=R\left\llbracket P\right\rrbracket+\sum^{Q}_{i=R+1}\left\llbracket P_{i}\right\rrbracket
and
d\left(P,P_{j}\right)\geq\varepsilon
for every
R+1\leq j\leq Q
. Therefore, the subset
U\subseteq\mathcal{A}_{Q}\left(M\right)
defined so that
\mathbf{A}\in U
if and only if there exists
\sigma\in S_{Q}
such that
d\left(P,A_{\sigma(i)}\right)<\varepsilon/3
for every
i=1,2,\dotsc,R
and
d\left(P_{i},A_{\sigma(i)}\right)<\varepsilon/3
for every
i=R+1,R+2,\dotsc,Q
is open and contains
\mathbf{P}
.

If
\mathbf{A}\in U
we can assume without loss of generality that
\sigma(i)=i
, then we always have

\displaystyle A_{1},A_{2},\dotsc,A_{R}\in B_{\varepsilon/3}(P)

\displaystyle A_{R+1},A_{R+2},\dotsc,A_{Q}\notin B_{2\varepsilon/3}(P)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}\left[\mathbf{P},\mathbf{A}\right]^{2}=\sum^{R}_{i=1}d\left(P,A_{i}\right)^{2}+\sum^{Q}_{i=R+1}d\left(P_{i},A_{\eta(i)}\right)^{2}

where
\eta\in S_{Q}
with
\eta(i)=i
for every
1\leq i\leq R
. We will prove only the last assertion. First of all assume that

\mathcal{G}\left[\mathbf{P},\mathbf{A}\right]^{2}=\sum^{R}_{i=1}d\left(P,A_{\eta(i)}\right)^{2}+\sum^{Q}_{i=R+1}d\left(P_{i},A_{\eta(i)}\right)^{2}

and set

\displaystyle E=\left\{{i=1,2,\dotsc,R}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}{\eta(i)\geq R+1}\right\}

\displaystyle F=\left\{{i=R+1,R+2,\dotsc,Q}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}{\eta(i)\leq R}\right\}\text{.}

Set
P_{i}=P
for
i=1,\dotsc,R
we clearly have
d\left(P_{i},A_{\eta(i)}\right)\geq 2\varepsilon/3
if
i\in E\cup F
. Moreover, both
E
and
F
must have the same cardinality thus if
E
is not empty then we can find another
\eta^{\prime}\in S_{Q}
such that
\eta^{\prime}(i)=\eta(i)
if
i\notin E\cup F
and
d\left(P_{i},A_{\eta^{\prime}(i)}\right)<\varepsilon/3
for every
i
. But this is impossible by our assumption on
\eta
.

Therefore we can set

\displaystyle{\pi_{1}^{\mathbf{P}}}:{\mathbf{A}\in U}\rightarrow{\sum^{R}_{i=1}\left\llbracket A_{i}\right\rrbracket\in\mathcal{A}_{R}\left(M\right)}

\displaystyle{\pi_{2}^{\mathbf{P}}}:{\mathbf{A}\in U}\rightarrow{\sum^{Q}_{i=R+1}\left\llbracket A_{i}\right\rrbracket\in\mathcal{A}_{Q-R}\left(M\right)}\text{.}\qed

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
\Omega\subseteq X
be open nonempty and
{f}:{\Omega}\rightarrow{\mathcal{A}_{Q}\left(M\right)}
be continuous at
x\in\Omega\setminus\Lambda
where

\displaystyle\Lambda=\left\{{x\in\Omega}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}{f(x)=Q\left\llbracket P\right\rrbracket\text{ for some }P\in M}\right\}\text{.}

Then there exist an open neighborhood
U
of
x
and
{g}:{U}\rightarrow{\mathcal{A}_{R}\left(M\right)}
,
{h}:{U}\rightarrow{\mathcal{A}_{S}\left(M\right)}
with
Q=R+S
,
\left.{f}\right|_{U}=\left\llbracket g\right\rrbracket+\left\llbracket h\right\rrbracket
and

\displaystyle\left\lvert\nabla g\right\rvert^{+}(x),\left\lvert\nabla h\right\rvert^{+}(x)\leq\left\lvert\nabla f\right\rvert^{+}(x)\text{.}

Theorem 3.3
.

If an approximate
X
-distribution
\mathcal{D}
for
M
is complete then the
X
-distribution
\mathcal{D}^{Q}
for
\mathcal{A}_{Q}\left(M\right)
satisfying both (
3.1
) and (
3.2
) is complete too.

We use induction on
Q
. If
Q=1
then we can use assumption (RC) on
M
, thus we can assume that theorem
3.3
holds for any
1\leq Q^{\prime}<Q
in order to prove it for
Q
. Let
{f}:{C}\rightarrow{\mathcal{A}_{Q}\left(M\right)}
be a Lipschitz function defined on a closed subset
C
of
X
and let
x\in C
.

If
x\notin\Lambda
then by corollary
3.2
we have
\left.{f}\right|_{V}=\left\llbracket g\right\rrbracket+\left\llbracket h\right\rrbracket
with
x\in A_{g}\cap A_{h}=V
for some open neighborhood
V
of
x
, thus we can use induction on
g
and
h
to prove that
f
is a.e. differentiable on
V
. Since
C
is separable we can cover
C\setminus\Lambda
by countably many of such
V
, thus we immediately get that
f
is a.e. differentiable on
C\setminus\Lambda
.

On
\Lambda
we have instead
f(x)=Q\left\llbracket f_{a}(x)\right\rrbracket
with
{f_{a}}:{\Lambda}\rightarrow{M}
Lipschitz, therefore by induction hypothesis
f_{a}
is a.e. approximately
\mathcal{D}
-differentiable on
A^{\mu}_{f_{a}}
. If
L\subseteq A^{\mu}_{f_{a}}
is the set of points on which
f_{a}
is approximately
\mathcal{D}
-differentiable with differential
[F]\in\mathcal{D}_{x,f_{a}(x)}
then we can assume that
L\subseteq{\Lambda}^{1}\cap A_{f}^{\mu}
. In this way
F
is approximately
\mathcal{D}^{Q}
-differentiable on every point of
L
because

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}\left[f(y),Q\left\llbracket F(y)\right\rrbracket\right]=\sqrt{Q}\ d\left[f_{a}(y),F(y)\right]

for every
y\in\Lambda
.

Combining theorem
3.3
with theorem
2.12
we finally get the following generalized version of Stepanov’s theorem for
Q
-valued functions which generalizes theorem
1.2
:

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
(X,\mathtt{J},\mu)
be a doubling metric measure space,
(M,d)
be a complete metric space and
\mathcal{D}
a complete (approximate)
X
-distribution on
M
. Then every
Q
-valued function
{f}:{X}\rightarrow{\mathcal{A}_{Q}\left(M\right)}
is a.e. (approximately)
\mathcal{D}^{Q}
-differentiable on
A_{f}
(or
A_{f}^{\mu}
).

In particular, these properties hold whenever
M={\mathbb{R}}^{k}
and
X
is
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
or a
p
-Poincaré doubling metric measure space thanks to
[
2
]
.
