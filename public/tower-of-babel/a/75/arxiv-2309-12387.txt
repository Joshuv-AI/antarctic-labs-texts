Simultaneous Resonant and Broadband Detection of Ultralight Dark Matter and High-Frequency Gravitational Waves via Cavities and Circuits
Yifan Chen, Chunlong Li, Yuxin Liu, Jing Shu, Yuting Yang, Yanjie Zeng

Abstract
Electromagnetic resonant systems, such as cavities and LC circuits, are widely used to detect ultralight boson dark matter and high-frequency gravitational waves. However, the narrow bandwidth of single-mode resonators necessitates multiple scan steps to cover broad frequency ranges. By incorporating a network of auxiliary modes via beam-splitter-type and non-degenerate parametric couplings, we enable broadband detection with an effective bandwidth of each scan matching the order of the resonant frequency, while maintaining a strong signal response. In heterodyne upconversion detection, where a background cavity mode transitions into another due to a potential background source, multiple orders of the source frequency can be probed with high sensitivity without tuning the cavity frequency. Consequently, our method allows for significantly deeper exploration of the parameter space within the same integration time compared to single-mode detection.

II.1
Resonant Cavity

Electromagnetic fields can be generated in the presence of an effective current, denoted as
\displaystyle J^{\mu}_{\rm eff}
. A resonant cavity, characterized by a low dissipation quality factor
\displaystyle Q_{\rm int}
, provides a straightforward means to amplify this weak signal. Within the cavity volume
\displaystyle V
, the electromagnetic fields become quantized bound states. Their wavefunctions, parameterized in the Weyl gauge, are expressed as:
follows:

\vec{A}=\sum_{n}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\omega_{\rm rf}^{n}}}\hat{a}_{n}^{\dagger}\vec{\epsilon}_{n}(\vec{r}\,)e^{-\mathrm{i}\omega_{\rm rf}^{n}\,t}+h.c..

(1)

In this equation, the discrete sum over the various modes
\displaystyle n
, with
\displaystyle\hat{a}_{n}
and
\displaystyle\hat{a}_{n}^{\dagger}
representing the annihilation and creation operators, respectively, each mode is characterized by an eigenfrequency
\displaystyle\omega_{\rm rf}^{n}
and a wavefunction
\displaystyle\vec{\epsilon}_{n}(\vec{r}\,)
. With a perfect conductor as the boundary, each resonant mode arises from solving the vacuum Maxwell equations
\displaystyle\Box\vec{A}=0
, subject to the boundary conditions:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\hat{s}\times\vec{\epsilon}_{n}|_{s}=0,\qquad\hat{s}\cdot\left(\vec{\nabla}\times\vec{\epsilon}_{n}\right)|_{s}=0.

(2)

Here, the subscript
\displaystyle s
denotes the surface of the cavity, with
\displaystyle\hat{s}
as the normal vector. Moreover, the wavefunctions must satisfy the orthonormality condition:

\int_{V}\vec{\epsilon}_{m}^{\ *}\,\vec{\epsilon}_{n}\,\mathrm{d}^{3}\vec{r}=\delta_{mn}.

(3)

Using Eq.
1
, the free Hamiltonian for the modes within the cavity is given by:

\begin{split}H_{0}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\text{V}}\left(\vec{E}^{2}+\vec{B}^{2}\right)\,\mathrm{d}^{3}\vec{r}=\sum_{n}\omega_{\rm rf}^{n}\left(\hat{a}_{n}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{n}+\frac{1}{2}\right).\end{split}

(4)

where
\displaystyle\vec{E}=-\partial\vec{A}/\partial t
and
\displaystyle\vec{B}=\vec{\nabla}\times\vec{A}
represent the electric and magnetic fields of the cavity modes, respectively.

We proceed by examining the interaction Hamiltonian concerning the spatial component of the effective current
\displaystyle\vec{J}_{\rm eff}
. A linear coupling with the vector potential
\displaystyle\vec{A}
is established as follows:

\begin{split}H_{\text{int}}=&\int_{\text{V}}\,\vec{A}\cdot\vec{J}_{\rm eff}\,\mathrm{d}^{3}\vec{r}\\
=&\sum_{n}\sqrt{\frac{V}{2\omega_{\rm rf}^{n}}}\,\eta_{n}\,\bar{J}_{\rm eff}\,\hat{a}_{n}^{\dagger}\,e^{-\mathrm{i}\omega_{\rm rf}^{n}\,t}+h.c..\end{split}

(5)

Here,
\displaystyle\eta_{n}
represents the geometric overlap function between
\displaystyle\vec{\epsilon}_{n}
and
\displaystyle\vec{J}_{\rm eff}
, defined as:

\eta_{n}\equiv\frac{{\int}_{V}\,\vec{\epsilon}_{n}\cdot\vec{J}_{\rm eff}\,\mathrm{d}^{3}\vec{r}}{\sqrt{\int_{V}|\vec{J}_{\rm eff}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}^{3}\vec{r}}},

(6)

and
\displaystyle\bar{J}_{\rm eff}
denotes the average current density within the cavity:

\bar{J}_{\rm eff}\equiv\sqrt{\frac{1}{V}\int_{V}|\vec{J}_{\rm eff}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}^{3}\vec{r}}.

(7)

On the other hand, the time component of
\displaystyle J^{\mu}_{\rm eff}
, corresponding to the effective charge density
\displaystyle\rho_{\rm eff}
, exclusively excites the irrotational mode, which does not experience resonant enhancement within the cavity context
Hill 2009
.

In addition to electromagnetic interactions involving the effective current, a cavity mode can also be excited by an external force applied to the cavity when a background mode is present. This force induces a displacement of the cavity walls, thereby instigating a power transition between the background and signal modes. This phenomenon can be characterized by a linear coupling resembling the expression in Eq. (
5
), which will be demonstrated in Sec.
III.2
.

II.2
LC Circuit

The resonant frequency of a cavity is intrinsically linked to its spatial dimensions, which poses a challenge in achieving a wide frequency tuning range outside the GHz regime. This limitation can be mitigated through the utilization of an LC circuit, which allows for precise tuning of the resonant frequency across different orders of magnitude below the GHz range. The LC circuit, comprising an inductor
\displaystyle L
and a capacitor
\displaystyle C
, forms a resonant system with a resonant frequency given by
\displaystyle\omega_{\rm rf}=1/\sqrt{LC}
. The system’s free Hamiltonian, encompassing the energy stored within the inductor and the capacitor, is represented as follows:

H_{0}=\frac{\Phi^{2}}{2L}+\frac{Q^{2}}{2C}=\omega_{\rm rf}\left(\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}+\frac{1}{2}\right).

(8)

Here,
\displaystyle\Phi
denotes the magnetic flux traversing the inductor, and
\displaystyle Q
signifies the charge accumulated in the capacitor. These serve as the canonical coordinate and its conjugate momentum, respectively, and are expressed in terms of annihilation and creation operators.

By introducing a pick-up loop, the LC circuit becomes capable of capturing an external magnetic flux
\displaystyle\Phi_{\Psi}
and resonantly responding when the frequency of
\displaystyle\Phi_{\Psi}
closely aligns with
\displaystyle\omega_{\rm rf}
. The interaction Hamiltonian, arising from a shift of the canonical coordinate
\displaystyle\Phi\rightarrow\Phi+\Phi_{\Psi}
within Eq. (
8
), is presented as follows:

\displaystyle\displaystyle H_{\rm int}=\frac{\Phi\,\Phi_{\Psi}}{L}=\sqrt{\frac{\omega_{\rm rf}}{2L}}\Phi_{\Psi}\,\hat{a}_{n}^{\dagger}\,e^{-\mathrm{i}\omega_{\rm rf}\,t}+h.c..

(9)

The magnetic flux in the pick-up loop originates solely from the spatial component of the effective current. The effective currents induce a magnetic field expressed as:

\vec{B}_{\Psi}(\vec{r}\,)\approx\int\frac{\vec{J}_{\mathrm{eff}}(\vec{r}^{\,\prime})\times\left(\vec{r}-\vec{r}^{\,\prime}\right)}{4\pi\left|\vec{r}-\vec{r}^{\,\prime}\right|^{3}}\mathrm{d}^{3}\vec{r}^{\,\prime},

(10)

where we neglect the time derivative terms, assuming that the Compton wavelength of the source is much larger than the spatial scale of the detector. The existence of a conducting shield modifies the response magnetic field, which can be described as a geometric overlapping between the spatial distribution of
\displaystyle\vec{J}_{\rm eff}
and all the eigenmodes of the shielding cavity
Chaudhuri et al. 2015
. We define a dimensionless geometric overlap function
\displaystyle\eta\sim\mathcal{O}(0.1)
so that
\displaystyle\Phi_{\Psi}=\eta V\bar{J}_{\rm eff}
, with
\displaystyle\bar{J}_{\rm eff}
defined in Eq. (
7
).

II.3
Circuit Representation of Cavity Modes

Encapsulating an antenna within a cavity enables the investigation of specific cavity modes by analyzing the nodal flow across the antenna, expressed as:

\begin{split}\Phi=\int_{\text{Ant}}\vec{A}(\vec{r},t)\cdot\mathrm{d}\vec{l}=\sum_{n}\kappa_{n}\hat{a}_{n}^{\dagger}e^{-\mathrm{i}\omega_{\rm rf}^{n}t}+h.c..\end{split}

(11)

Here, the integration spans the spatial extent of the antenna. The zero-point uncertainty associated with a specific cavity mode is captured by
\displaystyle\kappa_{n}
, defined as:

\kappa_{n}\equiv\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\omega_{\rm rf}^{n}}}\int_{\text{Ant}}\vec{\epsilon}_{n}(\vec{r}\,)\cdot\mathrm{d}\vec{l}.

(12)

Given the harmonic oscillator behavior of the Hamiltonians in Eqs. (
4
) and (
8
), it is feasible to model a cavity mode using an equivalent LC circuit. In this model, the nodal flux is defined as in Eq. (
11
), and the resonant frequency is given by
\displaystyle\omega_{\rm rf}^{n}=1/\sqrt{L_{n}C_{n}}
. This analogy establishes a correspondence between the physical properties of cavity modes and circuit elements:

L_{n}=\frac{2\kappa_{n}^{2}}{\omega_{\rm rf}^{n}},\qquad C_{n}=\frac{1}{2\kappa_{n}^{2}\omega_{\rm rf}^{n}}.

(13)

III
Axion, Dark Photon, and Gravitational Wave-Induced Signals

In this section, we explore the generation of effective current signals by axions, dark photons, and GWs. Additionally, we examine the mechanical vibrations induced by GWs, which can facilitate transitions between background and signal modes within cavities.

III.1
Effective Currents

The interaction Lagrangian between ultralight boson DM or HFGWs and electromagnetic fields can be reformulated as
\displaystyle A_{\mu}J^{\mu}_{\rm eff}
to identify the effective currents
\displaystyle J^{\mu}_{\rm eff}
. An example of this is the axion-photon interaction, which is expressed as follows:

\frac{1}{4}g_{a\gamma}aF_{\mu\nu}\tilde{F}^{\mu\nu}\rightarrow J_{\text{eff}}^{a\,\mu}=g_{a\gamma}\tilde{F}^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\nu}a.

(14)

Here,
\displaystyle g_{a\gamma}
represents the axion-photon coupling constant,
\displaystyle a
is the axion field, and
\displaystyle F_{\mu\nu}
and
\displaystyle\tilde{F}^{\mu\nu}
are the electromagnetic field strength tensor and its dual tensor, respectively. The Bianchi identity ensures that the derivative in Eq. (
14
) affects only the axion field. In scenarios involving non-relativistic axion dark matter, the term with the time derivative primarily contributes to the axion’s mass (
\displaystyle\omega_{a}\simeq m_{a}
), which is approximately
\displaystyle 10^{3}
times greater than that from the spatial derivative
Foster et al. 2018
. Consequently, a background magnetic field
\displaystyle\vec{B}_{0}
is typically employed in electromagnetic resonant detection, leading to the spatial current:

\vec{J}^{a}_{\rm eff}=g_{a\gamma}\omega_{a}a\vec{B}_{0}.

(15)

Here,
\displaystyle\vec{B}_{0}
may be static or oscillating, depending on the experimental setup. The latter corresponds to heterodyne upconversion, typically employing superconducting radio-frequency (SRF) cavities with significantly high quality factors (
\displaystyle Q_{\rm int}\gg 10^{9}
)
Berlin et al. 2020
;
Berlin et al. 2021
;
Berlin et al. 2023
.

The subsequent example involves the kinetic mixing of the dark photon
\displaystyle A^{\prime\mu}
, whose interaction in the interaction basis results in:

\epsilon m^{2}_{A^{\prime}}A^{\prime\mu}A_{\mu}\rightarrow J_{\text{eff}}^{A^{\prime}\,\mu}=\epsilon m^{2}_{A^{\prime}}A^{\prime\mu},

(16)

Here,
\displaystyle\epsilon
represents the kinetic mixing coefficient, and
\displaystyle m_{A^{\prime}}
denotes the mass of the dark photon. Unlike the axion case, the effective current in Eq. (
16
) depends solely on the dark photon field, thus an electromagnetic background field is not relevant. Specifically, the spatial component of the current is directly proportional to
\displaystyle\vec{A}^{\prime}
.

Finally, we consider the GW strain denoted as
\displaystyle h^{\mu\nu}
, which features a universal coupling to matter fields. Its interaction with electromagnetic fields yields:

\frac{1}{2}h^{\mu\nu}T_{\mu\nu}^{\rm EM}\rightarrow J_{\text{eff}}^{h\,\mu}=\partial_{\nu}\left(\frac{1}{2}hF^{\mu\nu}+{h^{\nu}}_{\rho}F^{\rho\mu}-{h^{\mu}}_{\rho}F^{\rho\nu}\right).

(17)

Here,
\displaystyle T_{\mu\nu}^{\rm EM}
corresponds to the energy-momentum tensor of the electromagnetic field, and
\displaystyle h\equiv{h^{\rho}}_{\rho}
. Similar to the axion scenario, the generation of the effective current in Eq. (
17
) requires a background electromagnetic field, established within the proper detector frame. Notably, while the GW strain is often computed in the transverse-traceless (TT) frame characterized by the amplitude
\displaystyle h_{0}
, transitioning from the proper detector frame to
\displaystyle h_{0}
introduces a scaling factor of
\displaystyle\omega_{h}^{2}V^{2/3}

Berlin et al. 2022a
;
Domcke et al. 2022
.

In scenarios characterized by a stationary magnetic field background, the derivative in Eq. (
17
) pertains exclusively to the GW strain
Berlin et al. 2022a
, resulting in:

\vec{J}^{\,h}_{\text{eff}}\simeq\omega_{h}^{2}V^{\frac{1}{3}}B_{0}\,h_{0}\,\hat{j}(\vec{r}\,).

(18)

where we assume
\displaystyle\omega_{h}\sim V^{-1/3}
. The spatially-dependent dimensionless vector
\displaystyle\hat{j}(\vec{r}\,)
is related to the incoming direction and polarization of the GW. In the context of heterodyne upconversion detection,
\displaystyle\omega_{\rm rf}
typically significantly outweighs
\displaystyle\omega_{h}
, consequently rendering the dominant component in Eq. (
17
) proportional to
\displaystyle\omega_{\rm rf}\sim V^{-1/3}
, thus leading to approximately the same expression as in Eq. (
18
).

As elaborated in Sec.
II
, the spatial component of the effective currents exhibits coupling with either a cavity mode or a circuit, expressed in the following Hamiltonian:

\displaystyle\displaystyle{H}_{\alpha}=\alpha\Psi\left(\hat{a}\,e^{\mathrm{i}\omega_{\rm rf}t}+\hat{a}^{\dagger}\,e^{-\mathrm{i}\omega_{\rm rf}t}\right)/\sqrt{2}.

(19)

Here, the coupling coefficients
\displaystyle\alpha
for the cavity and circuit scenarios are given by:

\alpha_{\textrm{cav}}\Psi=\sqrt{\frac{V}{\omega_{\rm rf}}}\eta\bar{J}_{\rm eff},\qquad\alpha_{\textrm{LC}}\Psi=\sqrt{\frac{\omega_{\rm rf}}{L}}\Phi_{\Psi},

(20)

respectively, derived in accordance with Eq. (
5
) and Eq. (
9
).

III.2
Mechanical Deformation

In addition to the microscopic interaction between GWs and electromagnetic fields, an alternative mechanism for inducing cavity mode transitions involves the deformation of the cavity’s inner surface driven by GWs, as explored in Ref.
Berlin et al. 2023
for the SRF cavity configuration. This deformation is characterized by local displacements from the equilibrium position, represented as:

\vec{U}(\vec{r},t)=\sum_{p}x_{p}(t)\vec{U}_{p}(\vec{r}\,),

(21)

where the subscript
\displaystyle p
corresponds to a mechanical normal mode with a time-dependent mode function
\displaystyle x_{p}(t)
, and
\displaystyle\vec{r}
denotes a point within the cavity shell with volume
\displaystyle V_{S}
. The function
\displaystyle\vec{U}_{p}(\vec{r}\,)
describes the dimensionless spatial profile of the
\displaystyle p
-mode, satisfying the normalization condition:

\int_{V_{S}}\,\vec{U}^{*}_{p}(\vec{r}\,)\cdot\vec{U}_{q}(\vec{r}\,)\,\rho_{S}(\vec{r}\,)\,\mathrm{d}^{3}\vec{r}=\delta_{pq}\,M_{S},

(22)

where
\displaystyle\rho_{S}(\vec{r}\,)
and
\displaystyle M_{S}
denote the mass density and total mass of the cavity shell, respectively. These mechanical normal modes can be excited by an external force, described by the following equation of motion:

\ddot{x}_{p}+\frac{\omega_{p}}{Q_{p}}\dot{x}_{p}+\omega_{p}^{2}x_{p}=\frac{1}{M_{S}}\int_{V_{S}}\,\vec{f}(\vec{r},t)\cdot\vec{U}^{*}_{p}(\vec{r\,})\,\mathrm{d}^{3}\vec{r}\equiv\frac{F_{p}}{M_{S}}.

(23)

Here,
\displaystyle\omega_{p}
and
\displaystyle Q_{p}
represent the resonant frequency and mechanical quality factor of the
\displaystyle p
-mode, respectively. The force density is denoted as
\displaystyle\vec{f}(\vec{r},t)
, and
\displaystyle F_{p}
represents the total force applied to the
\displaystyle p
-mode. The solution to this equation in the frequency domain is straightforwardly obtained as
\displaystyle x_{p}(\omega)=L_{p}(\omega)F_{p}(\omega)/M_{S}
, with
\displaystyle L_{p}(\omega)
being the response function given by
\displaystyle(\omega^{2}-\omega_{p}^{2}+\mathrm{i}\omega\omega_{p}/Q_{p})^{-1}
.

GW strain acts as a tidal force on the cavity shell, exerting a force density
\displaystyle f_{i}=-\rho_{S}R_{i0j0}r^{j}
in the long-wavelength regime, where
\displaystyle r_{j}
is defined in a coordinate system originating at the center of mass of the cavity. The Riemann curvature tensor is typically expressed in terms of the strain in the TT gauge,
\displaystyle R_{i0j0}=-\ddot{h}_{ij}^{\mathrm{TT}}/2+\mathcal{O}(h^{2})
. The strain-induced force on the
\displaystyle p
-mode, labeled as
\displaystyle F_{p}^{h}
, is then characterized through an overlapping function
\displaystyle\eta_{p}^{h}
between the strain polarization basis
\displaystyle H_{ij}^{\mathrm{TT}}
and the mechanical mode profile:

\displaystyle F^{h}_{p}(\omega)

\displaystyle=M_{S}V^{1/3}\eta^{h}_{p}\omega^{2}h_{0}(\omega),

(24)

\displaystyle\eta_{p}^{h}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{{H}_{ij}^{\mathrm{TT}}}{2V^{1/3}M_{S}}\left|\int_{V_{S}}\,\rho(\vec{r}\,)\,U_{p}^{i*}(\vec{r}\,)\,r^{j}\,\mathrm{d}^{3}\vec{r}\,\right|.

Here,
\displaystyle h_{0}(\omega)
represents the strain amplitude, satisfying the relation
\displaystyle h_{ij}^{\mathrm{TT}}\equiv h_{0}H_{ij}^{\mathrm{TT}}
.

The deformation of the inner cavity surface leads to deviations from the orthonormality condition of the cavity modes in Eq. (
3
), enabling the transition from the pump mode to the signal mode. The effective coupling between these modes in the frequency domain is identified as per the following equation
Bernard et al. 2002
;
Berlin et al. 2023
:

\alpha\Psi(\omega)=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\frac{\omega_{\text{rf}}^{1/2}B_{0}V^{1/6}}{M_{S}}\sum_{p}\eta^{t}_{p}L_{p}(\omega-\omega_{0})F_{p}^{h}(\omega-\omega_{0}).

(25)

Here,
\displaystyle\eta_{p}^{t}
represents the transition form factor given by

\eta^{t}_{p}=V^{1/3}\int_{S}\,\left(\vec{\epsilon}_{0}\cdot\vec{\epsilon}_{1}{}^{*}-\frac{1}{\omega_{\text{rf}}^{2}}\left(\bm{\mathrm{curl}}\vec{\epsilon}_{0}\right)\cdot\left(\bm{\mathrm{curl}}\vec{\epsilon}_{1}{}^{*}\right)\right)\,\mathrm{d}\vec{s}\cdot\vec{U}_{p},

(26)

where the integral is performed over the inner surface of the cavity. The subscripts
\displaystyle 0
and
\displaystyle 1

IV
Quantum Limit for Single-Mode Resonators

This section begins with a brief introduction to the input-output formalism used to derive the scattering matrix elements of single-mode resonators. From these elements, one can calculate the signal and noise power spectral densities (PSDs). We then explore how to optimize the scan rate by adjusting the readout coupling and distributing the integration time within the
\displaystyle e
-fold time. Additionally, we discuss how the optimized scan rate translates into the physics reaches of various detection schemes for the three sources outlined in Sec.
III
.

IV.1
Input-Output Formalism

The Hamiltonian governing a resonant mode
\displaystyle\hat{a}
is given by

H=H_{\rm free}+H_{\textrm{int}},

(27)

where
\displaystyle H_{\rm free}=\omega_{\rm rf}(\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}+1/2)
represents the free Hamiltonian, and
\displaystyle H_{\textrm{int}}
includes the interaction terms. Utilizing the Heisenberg equation in the interaction picture,

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\hat{a}=-\mathrm{i}\Big[\hat{a},H_{\textrm{int}}\Big],

(28)

leads to the derivation of the system’s dynamics.

Before exploring specific interaction forms, it is essential to consider environmental factors that become relevant when the system is coupled to an external port, as described by the quantum Langevin equation:

-\mathrm{i}\Big[\hat{a},H_{\textrm{env}}^{\textrm{p}}\Big]=-\gamma_{\rm p}\hat{a}+\sqrt{2\gamma_{\rm p}}\hat{u}_{\rm p},

(29)

where
\displaystyle H_{\textrm{env}}^{\textrm{p}}
represents the interaction with the environment via port p,
\displaystyle\gamma_{\rm p}
denotes the dissipation coefficient, and
\displaystyle\hat{u}_{\rm p}
corresponds to incoming noise as dictated by the fluctuation-dissipation theorem. The input-output relation is then derived from the port boundary condition:

\hat{v}_{\rm p}=\hat{u}_{\rm p}-\sqrt{2\gamma_{\rm p}}\hat{a},

(30)

where
\displaystyle\hat{v}_{\rm p}
designates the outgoing mode.

For a single-mode resonant detection system, two noise-contributing ports are relevant: intrinsic dissipation and readout. Together with the interaction with a potential signal,
\displaystyle H_{\alpha}
introduced in Eq. (
19
), the interaction Hamiltonian becomes:

H_{\textrm{int}}=H_{\alpha}+H_{\text{env}}^{\rm\gamma}+H_{\textrm{env}}^{\gamma_{r}}.

(31)

Utilizing Eq. (
28
) and Eq. (
29
), the equation of motion for
\displaystyle\hat{a}
in the frequency domain can be solved as follows:

\hat{a}=\frac{-\mathrm{i}\alpha\Psi e^{-\mathrm{i}\omega_{\rm rf}t}/\sqrt{2}+\sqrt{2\gamma}\hat{u}_{a}+\sqrt{2\gamma_{r}}\hat{u}_{r}}{\gamma+\gamma_{r}-\mathrm{i}\Omega},

(32)

where
\displaystyle\Omega\equiv\omega-\omega_{\rm rf}
represents the frequency shift in the interaction picture, and
\displaystyle\gamma
and
\displaystyle\gamma_{r}
denote the dissipation coefficients of the two ports, with corresponding incoming modes
\displaystyle\hat{u}_{a}
and
\displaystyle\hat{u}_{r}
, respectively. The intrinsic dissipation can alternatively be expressed in terms of the quality factor
\displaystyle Q_{\rm int}
, which is related to
\displaystyle\gamma
by
\displaystyle Q_{\rm int}\equiv\omega_{\rm rf}/(2\gamma)
.

The observable is obtained from the readout port’s outgoing mode, as per the input-output relation in Eq. (
30
):

\hat{v}_{r}=S_{0r}\left(\hat{u}_{a}-\frac{\mathrm{i}\alpha e^{-\mathrm{i}\omega_{\rm rf}t}}{2\sqrt{\gamma}}\Psi\right)+S_{rr}\hat{u}_{r},

(33)

The two scattering matrix elements, characterizing the propagation from the input to the output of different ports, are given by:

S_{0r}=-\frac{2\sqrt{\gamma\gamma_{r}}}{\gamma+\gamma_{r}-\mathrm{i}\Omega},\qquad S_{rr}=\frac{\gamma-\gamma_{r}-\mathrm{i}\Omega}{\gamma+\gamma_{r}-\mathrm{i}\Omega}.

(34)

Here, the subscript
\displaystyle 0
represents the probing sensor, such as a cavity or a circuit discussed in Sec.
III
, and
\displaystyle r
indicates the readout port.

When the integration time significantly exceeds the signal’s correlation time, the PSD of the outgoing mode is typically taken to be the observable, defined by:

S_{v_{r}}(\Omega)\delta(\Omega-\Omega^{\prime})\equiv\braket{\hat{v}_{r}(\Omega)\hat{v}_{r}^{*}(\Omega^{\prime})},

(35)

incorporating PSDs of both the signal and noise.

IV.2
Physics Reach

According to Eq. (
31
), apart from the signal port
\displaystyle H_{\alpha}
, fluctuations are induced by intrinsic dissipation and the readout port. The readout PSD in Eq. (
35
), expressed as
\displaystyle S_{v_{r}}(\Omega)=S_{\text{sig}}+S_{\text{noise}}
, can be separated as:

\begin{split}S_{\text{sig}}&=\left|S_{0r}\right|^{2}\frac{\alpha^{2}}{4\gamma}S_{\Psi},\\
S_{\text{noise}}&=\left|S_{0r}\right|^{2}n_{\textrm{occ}}+\left|S_{rr}\right|^{2}\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}.\end{split}

(36)

Here,
\displaystyle S_{\Psi}
represents the source PSD, which is related to its energy density
\displaystyle\rho_{\Psi}
as follows:

S_{\Psi}=\frac{2\pi}{\omega^{2}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho_{\Psi}}{\mathrm{d}\omega}.

(37)

The three noise terms in Eq. (
36
) represent intrinsic fluctuation noise, the readout port noise, and additional amplifier noise, respectively.
\displaystyle n_{\text{occ}}
represents the intrinsic noise occupation number, for example, the thermal fluctuation of a cavity mode gives
\displaystyle n_{\text{occ}}^{\rm th}=1/2+1/(e^{\omega/T}-1)
, where
\displaystyle\omega
is the frequency and
\displaystyle T
is the temperature. In the zero-temperature limit, the sum of the noise precisely equals one due to unitarity, leading to the standard quantum limit of single-mode resonant detection
Chaudhuri et al. 2018
;
Chaudhuri et al. 2019
.

The sensitivity reach of each scan can be estimated by requiring that the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) be of order one
Chaudhuri et al. 2018
;
Chaudhuri et al. 2019
;
Berlin et al. 2020
;
Lasenby 2020
;
Lasenby 2021
;
Chen et al. 2022a
, as described by the Dicke radiometer equation
Dicke 1946
:

\text{SNR}^{2}=\frac{t_{\text{int}}}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{S_{\text{sig}}}{S_{\text{noise}}}\right)^{2}d\omega.

(38)

Here,
\displaystyle t_{\text{int}}
represents the integration time. The integrand in Eq. (
38
) is decomposed into the product of two distributions: the signal PSD distribution
\displaystyle\alpha^{4}S_{\Psi}^{2}/\gamma^{2}
and the sensitive response function of the detector, characterized by
\displaystyle(|S_{0r}|^{2}n_{\text{occ}}/S_{\text{noise}})^{2}
, which quantifies the ratio of intrinsic fluctuation noise against the total noise.

For convenience, we introduce the concept of the average source frequency as:

\overline{\omega}_{\Psi}\equiv\frac{\int\omega^{2}S_{\Psi}\,\alpha^{2}/\gamma\,d\omega}{\int\omega S_{\Psi}\,\alpha^{2}/\gamma\,d\omega},

(39)

and define the source bandwidth as:

\Delta\omega_{\Psi}\equiv\frac{\int\omega^{2}S_{\Psi}\,\alpha^{2}/\gamma\,d\omega}{\overline{\omega}_{\Psi}^{2}S_{\Psi}(\overline{\omega}_{\Psi})\alpha^{2}(\overline{\omega}_{\Psi})/\gamma(\overline{\omega}_{\Psi})}.

(40)

The quality factor of the source is defined as
\displaystyle Q_{\Psi}\equiv\overline{\omega}_{\Psi}/\Delta\omega_{\Psi}
, which is
\displaystyle 10^{6}
for non-relativistic DM. For HFGWs, we parameterize the PSD as
\displaystyle S_{h}(\omega)=\Theta\left(\Delta\omega_{h}/2-\left|\omega-\omega_{h}\right|\right){h_{0}^{2}}/{\Delta\omega_{h}}
, where
\displaystyle\Theta
is the Heaviside function and
\displaystyle\Delta\omega_{h}
denotes the GW bandwidth.

Due to the common factor
\displaystyle|S_{0r}|^{4}
of the signal and intrinsic fluctuation in Eq. (
38
), the detector’s effective sensitive width is approximately

\Delta\omega_{r}\equiv\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\left|S_{0r}\right|^{2}n_{\textrm{occ}}}{S_{\text{noise}}}\right)^{2}d\omega,

(41)

within which the sensitivity to a source remains approximately the same order.

The integral width in Eq. (
38
) is determined by the minimum of
\displaystyle\Delta\omega_{\Psi}
and
\displaystyle\Delta\omega_{r}
. Moreover, their maximum controls how the integration time
\displaystyle t_{\text{int}}
of each scan is distributed within the total amount of time
\displaystyle t_{e}
spent covering each
\displaystyle e
-fold of
\displaystyle\overline{\omega}_{\Psi}
:

t_{\text{int}}\simeq t_{e}\,\textrm{max}\left[\Delta\omega_{\Psi},\Delta\omega_{r}\right]/\overline{\omega}_{\Psi}.

(42)

By considering only the dominant intrinsic noise within
\displaystyle\Delta\omega_{r}
, SNR
2
for a given hypothesis of
\displaystyle\overline{\omega}_{\Psi}
is simplified to

\text{SNR}^{2}(\overline{\omega}_{\Psi})\simeq\frac{t_{e}}{\overline{\omega}_{\Psi}}\Delta\omega_{\Psi}\Delta\omega_{r}\left.\frac{\alpha^{4}S^{2}_{\Psi}}{32\pi\gamma^{2}n_{\textrm{occ}}^{2}}\right|_{\omega=\overline{\omega}_{\Psi}}.

(43)

V.1
Scattering Matrices For Non-Hermitian Chain

We begin with a chain of resonant modes, where each neighboring pair is linked through both beam-splitter-type interactions and non-degenerate parametric interactions. The system is governed by the Hamiltonian:

\begin{split}H_{\text{ch}}&=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\left(\mathrm{i}|g|e^{\mathrm{i}\varphi^{g}_{k}}\hat{a}_{k}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{k+1}+\mathrm{i}|G|e^{\mathrm{i}\varphi^{G}_{k}}\hat{a}_{k}\hat{a}_{k+1}+h.c.\right)\\
&=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\left[(|g|-|G|)X_{k}\tilde{Y}_{k+1}-(|g|+|G|)\tilde{X}_{k+1}Y_{k}\right],\end{split}

(50)

where
\displaystyle\varphi^{g/G}_{k}
denote the relative phases of the couplings. Quadrature operators are introduced as follows:

\displaystyle X_{k}\equiv\frac{e^{\mathrm{i}\varphi^{+}_{k}}}{\sqrt{2}}\hat{a}_{k}+h.c.,\qquad Y_{k}\equiv\frac{e^{\mathrm{i}\varphi^{+}_{k}}}{\sqrt{2}\mathrm{i}}\hat{a}_{k}+h.c.,

(51)

\displaystyle\tilde{X}_{k}\equiv\frac{e^{\mathrm{i}\varphi^{-}_{k-1}}}{\sqrt{2}}\hat{a}_{k}+h.c.,\quad\,\tilde{Y}_{k}\equiv\frac{e^{\mathrm{i}\varphi^{-}_{k-1}}}{\sqrt{2}\mathrm{i}}\hat{a}_{k}+h.c.,

where
\displaystyle\varphi^{\pm}_{k}\equiv(\varphi^{G}_{k}\pm\varphi^{g}_{k})/2
. As depicted in the top panel of Fig.
3
, the probe sensor mode and the readout port are strategically positioned at the two opposing termini of the chain, specifically labeled as
\displaystyle\hat{a}_{0}
and
\displaystyle\hat{a}_{N}
, respectively.

Assuming zero relative phases
\displaystyle\varphi^{g/G}_{k}=0
, we simplify the system’s dynamics, leading to decoupled equations of motion for
\displaystyle Y_{k}=\tilde{Y}_{k}
:

\displaystyle(\gamma-\mathrm{i}\Omega)Y_{0}+(|g|-|G|)Y_{1}=\sqrt{2\gamma}\hat{u}_{0},

(52)

\displaystyle(\gamma-\mathrm{i}\Omega)Y_{k}+(|g|-|G|)Y_{k+1}-(|g|+|G|)Y_{k-1}=\sqrt{2\gamma}\hat{u}_{k},

\displaystyle(\gamma+\gamma_{r}-\mathrm{i}\Omega)Y_{N}-(|g|+|G|)Y_{N-1}=\sqrt{2\gamma}\hat{u}_{N}+\sqrt{2\gamma_{r}}\hat{u}_{r},

where all intrinsic dissipation coefficients are simplified as
\displaystyle\gamma
. Recursive relations for
\displaystyle Y_{k}
are derived from the first two lines of Eq. (
52
):

\displaystyle Y_{k}=y_{k}

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{k}\prod_{m=j}^{k-1}\mathcal{G}^{k-j}y_{m}\sqrt{2\gamma}\hat{u}_{j}-\mathcal{G}y_{k}Y_{k+1},

(53)

where the series
\displaystyle y_{k}
,
\displaystyle\mathcal{J}
, and
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}
are defined as:

\displaystyle y_{0}\equiv{1}/{\left(\gamma-\mathrm{i}\Omega\right)},\quad y_{k}\equiv{1}/\left(\gamma-\mathrm{i}\Omega+\mathcal{J}^{2}y_{k-1}\right),

(54)

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}\equiv(|g|^{2}-|G|^{2})^{1/2},\quad\mathcal{G}\equiv|g|+|G|.

Consequently, the solution for the last mode
\displaystyle Y_{N}
is expressed as:

\displaystyle Y_{N}=

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{2\gamma_{r}}\,\hat{u}_{r}+\sum_{k=0}^{N}\prod_{j=k}^{N-1}\,\mathcal{G}^{N-k}y_{j}\sqrt{2\gamma}\hat{u}_{k}}{\gamma+\gamma_{r}-\mathrm{i}\Omega+\mathcal{J}^{2}y_{N-1}},

(55)

which provides the scattering matrix elements entering the readout port:

(57)

\displaystyle\lfloor\cdots\rfloor

\displaystyle C_{x-j}^{j}

\displaystyle|g|>|G|

McDonald et al. 2018

\displaystyle\gamma

Similarly, the equations of motion for
\displaystyle X_{k}=\tilde{X}_{k}

\displaystyle Y_{k}

\displaystyle|G|

\displaystyle|G|\rightarrow-|G|

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}

In the presence of nonvanishing relative phases

\displaystyle k

\displaystyle(k+1)
-th mode, denoted as
\displaystyle Y_{k}

Here,
\displaystyle\theta_{k}\equiv(\varphi^{G}_{k-1}-\varphi^{g}_{k-1}-\varphi^{G}_{k}-\varphi^{g}_{k})/2

\displaystyle\Pi_{k=1}^{N-1}\cos\theta_{k}

V.3
Response Width in Multi-Mode Resonators

As discussed in Sec.
IV
, the response width determines the sensitivity reach of detectors. This width can be optimized numerically using Eq (
41
) or approximated by identifying the range where intrinsic noise from
\displaystyle\hat{a}_{0}
dominates over other noise contributions, as expressed in:

\left|S_{0r}\right|^{2}n_{\textrm{occ}}\gtrsim\left|S_{rr}\right|^{2}\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left|S_{kr}\right|^{2}n_{\textrm{occ}}.

(61)

Here, the left-hand side represents intrinsic noise from
\displaystyle\hat{a}_{0}
, while the right-hand side accounts for readout noise and intrinsic noise from higher resonators.

The goal of optimization is to identify the best set of free parameters that maximizes the response width, as specified by Eq. (
61
), for a fixed number of chains,
\displaystyle N
. In principle, this condition depends on five parameters:
\displaystyle|g|
,
\displaystyle\gamma
,
\displaystyle n_{\textrm{occ}}
,
\displaystyle\gamma_{r}
, and
\displaystyle\mathcal{J}
. We assume that
\displaystyle\gamma
and
\displaystyle n_{\textrm{occ}}
are optimized experimentally to be as small as possible, and that
\displaystyle|g|
is maximized to enhance
\displaystyle|S_{0r}|
. The remaining free parameters,
\displaystyle\gamma_{r}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{J}
, are tuned for optimization.

To begin, we examine the properties on the left-hand side of Eq. (
61
), particularly the scattering matrix
\displaystyle|S_{0r}|^{2}
. From Eqs. (
56
) and (
57
), we obtain

\displaystyle|S_{0r}(\Omega=\Delta\omega_{r})|^{2}

(62)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{4\gamma\gamma_{r}\mathcal{G}^{2N}|f_{0}|^{2}}{|\gamma_{r}f_{N}+f_{N+1}|^{2}}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle 4\gamma\gamma_{r}\mathcal{G}^{2N}/\left|\mathcal{J}^{N}\gamma_{r}\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor N/2\rfloor}C_{N-j}^{j}\left(-\mathrm{i}\frac{\Delta\omega_{r}}{\mathcal{J}}\right)^{N-2j}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\,\mathcal{J}^{N+1}\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor(N+1)/2\rfloor}C_{N+1-j}^{j}\left(-\mathrm{i}\frac{\Delta\omega_{r}}{\mathcal{J}}\right)^{N+1-2j}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{4\gamma\gamma_{r}\mathcal{G}^{2N}}{\Delta\omega_{r}^{2N}(\gamma_{r}+\Delta\omega_{r})^{2}}.

In the third line, we neglect
\displaystyle\gamma
in the expression for
\displaystyle f_{N}=\Sigma_{j=0}^{\lfloor N/2\rfloor}C_{N-j}^{j}(\gamma-\mathrm{i}\Omega)^{N-2j}\mathcal{J}^{2j}
, taking
\displaystyle f_{0}=1
, and evaluate the bandwidth at the boundary of the response width,
\displaystyle\Omega=\Delta\omega_{r}
. The last line is derived from the following three regions:

•

When
\displaystyle\mathcal{J}<\Delta\omega_{r}
, the highest-order polynomial term with
\displaystyle j=0
dominates in each sum, leading directly to the final expression.

•

When
\displaystyle\mathcal{J}>\Delta\omega_{r}
, the condition is inconsistent. In this region,
\displaystyle|S_{0r}|^{2}
remains nearly constant for
\displaystyle\Omega<\mathcal{J}
, dominated by the
\displaystyle 0
-th order term in one of the polynomial series with either
\displaystyle j=\lfloor N/2\rfloor
or
\displaystyle\lfloor(N+1)/2\rfloor
.
\displaystyle|S_{0r}|^{2}
only begins to decrease significantly for
\displaystyle\Omega>\mathcal{J}
, ensuring that the bandwidth satisfies
\displaystyle\Delta\omega_{r}>\mathcal{J}
.

•

When
\displaystyle\mathcal{J}\approx\Delta\omega_{r}
, both sums become approximately 1 based on numerical evaluation. Consequently, the term
\displaystyle|\cdots|^{2}
can be approximated as
\displaystyle|\mathcal{J}^{N}\gamma_{r}+\mathcal{J}^{N+1}|^{2}
, which leads to the final expression by setting
\displaystyle\mathcal{J}\approx\Delta\omega_{r}
.

To proceed, we first determine the range of parameters that satisfies the necessary condition from Eq. (
61
), where the dominance of the left-hand side over the readout noise—characterized by a nearly constant PSD of approximately
\displaystyle 1
for sufficiently small
\displaystyle\gamma
—is required. This condition is expressed as

|S_{0r}(\Omega=\Delta\omega_{r})|^{2}n_{\rm occ}\approx\frac{4n_{\rm occ}\gamma\gamma_{r}\mathcal{G}^{2N}}{\Delta\omega_{r}^{2N}(\gamma_{r}+\Delta\omega_{r})^{2}}\geq 1.

(63)

which must be satisfied to achieve an optimal response width. Maximizing
\displaystyle\Delta\omega_{r}
for this relation is achieved by tuning
\displaystyle\gamma_{r}
, which leads to the simple conclusion that
\displaystyle\gamma_{r}\approx\Delta\omega_{r}
. It is important to note that this relation depends only marginally on the value of
\displaystyle\mathcal{J}

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}
affect the flatness of the PSD within
\displaystyle\Delta\omega_{r}

\displaystyle\Omega\gg\mathcal{J}

Given this, we take
\displaystyle\mathcal{J}=\gamma_{r}=\Delta\omega_{r}
as our benchmark parameters. This yields the expression
\displaystyle|S_{0r}|^{2}\sim\gamma\mathcal{G}^{2N}/\Delta\omega_{r}^{2N+1}

63
), becomes

\Delta\omega_{r}^{\rm opt}\simeq\Big(\gamma\,n_{\textrm{occ}}\,\mathcal{G}^{2N}\Big)^{1/(2N+1)}.

(64)

In the limit of large
\displaystyle N

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}
, provided that
\displaystyle\gamma n_{\rm occ}
remains finite. Moreover, we can use
\displaystyle\mathcal{J}=\Delta\omega_{r}^{\rm opt}
to solve for the exact values of
\displaystyle\mathcal{J}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}

\mathcal{J}^{\rm opt}=\left(\gamma n_{\rm occ}(\sqrt{|g|^{2}-\left(\mathcal{J}^{\rm opt}\right)^{2}}+|g|)^{2N}\right)^{1/(2N+1)}.

(65)

For small
\displaystyle N
, the ratio
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}/|g|
is close to
\displaystyle 2

\displaystyle 1
as
\displaystyle N

\displaystyle\Delta\omega_{r}=\mathcal{J}

\displaystyle|g|
as well.

Historically, the goal of achieving a broad bandwidth with a large response originated in the context of gravitational wave detection, particularly in the concept of the white-light cavity

VI
Realization of Multi-Mode Resonators

This section demonstrates the realization of multi-mode resonators that feature significantly broadened response widths, focusing particularly on the beam-splitter-type and non-degenerate parametric interactions between modes, as derived in Sec.
V
and illustrated in Fig.
3
. We will also discuss potential challenges associated with their implementation. Notably, within a multi-mode resonator setup, only one mode at the lowest level needs to function as the probing sensor, as discussed in Secs.
II
and
III
. The remaining auxiliary modes may be circuit modes that do not require pick-up loops and are not embedded in a magnetic field background, or they can be other resonant cavity modes within the same cavity.

One method to achieve the necessary couplings is through three-wave mixing, where one pumping mode’s frequency matches the sum or difference of the other two, thereby enabling non-degenerate parametric and beam-splitter-type interactions, respectively. Feasible implementations of three-wave mixing include the use of a Josephson Ring Modulator (JRM)
Bergeal et al. 2010a
, Superconducting Nonlinear Asymmetric Inductive eLement (SNAIL)
Frattini et al. 2017
, and DC-driven Josephson junction effects
Russer and Russer 2011
. Notably, the JRM structure has recently been utilized to demonstrate response width broadening in a
\displaystyle N=1
resonator composed of two circuit modes
Jiang et al. 2023
. Thus, we use JRMs as the benchmark for constructing multi-mode resonators, as illustrated in the bottom panel of Fig.
3
.

VI.1
Multi-Mode Resonators Connected by Josephson Ring Modulators

\displaystyle|G|
can be engineered to be comparable to, but not exceeding,
\displaystyle\omega_{\rm rf}^{k}
due to the consideration of frequency shifts. We will adopt
\displaystyle\Delta\omega_{r}\approx 2|g|\approx\omega_{\rm rf}
of the probing sensor as the operational limit for multi-mode resonators. Before discussing the physics reach, we briefly outline several challenges to realization and experimental operation.

VI.2
Potential Challenges

The implementation of three-wave mixing, as discussed above, has been previously demonstrated in Ref.
Bergeal et al. 2010a
. Additionally, a prototype that realizes both
\displaystyle g
and
\displaystyle G
types of couplings simultaneously using a Josephson Ring Modulator (JRM) has shown the potential to amplify a mimicking axion signal, achieving a 5.6-fold enhancement in SNR
2
with
\displaystyle N=1

Jiang et al. 2023
. However, constructing a multi-mode resonator capable of significantly broadening the response bandwidth presents several challenges, which we will briefly address along with potential solutions below.

•

The foremost challenge is the compatibility of Josephson junctions with strong magnetic fields. Josephson junctions, being superconducting elements, require isolation from background magnetic fields to maintain superconductivity. One approach involves using a transmission line to connect the cavity or circuit with the Josephson junctions, thereby shielding them from direct magnetic exposure
Wurtz et al. 2021
. Moreover, the operation of Josephson junctions in magnetic fields up to
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(1)\,\mathrm{T}
has been successfully demonstrated
Rokhinson et al. 2012
. Thus, the apparent incompatibility between Josephson junctions and strong magnetic fields can be effectively mitigated.

•

Similarly, the operating temperature of the JRM must be kept below a certain threshold, typically ensuring that the thermal occupation number is dominated by quantum fluctuations. In the benchmark examples shown in Figs.
5
and
6
, we assume
\displaystyle n_{\rm occ}=10
for illustration. However, the environmental temperature surrounding the JRMs may differ from that inside the resonators. For instance, SRF cavities are typically cooled to around
\displaystyle 1.8\,\mathrm{K}
using liquid helium immersion
Tang et al. 2024
. Given that the setup is considerably larger than the cavity itself, there is enough space to accommodate an additional small dilution refrigerator, which can provide the mK-level temperatures required for JRM operation. Alternatively, the SRF cavity could be placed in the K-level stage of a sufficiently large dilution refrigerator to provide adequate cooling power, while the JRM is positioned in the mK stage of the same refrigerator to ensure proper operation.

•

Additionally, when connections are made via JRMs and transmission lines, there is a potential for increased dissipation, which could lower the quality factor of the probing sensors. Consequently, precise noise control and calibration of
\displaystyle\gamma
are necessary.

•

Another potential challenge is the phase fluctuation of the pump mode
\displaystyle\Phi_{p}
, which can deviate from the frequencies at
\displaystyle\omega_{\rm rf}^{k-1}\pm\omega_{\rm rf}^{k}
. In the non-Hermitian chain model discussed in Sec.
V.1
, the resulting phase fluctuations, denoted
\displaystyle\varphi^{g/G}_{k}
, can quench the amplification
Wang et al. 2017
. Misalignment of these phases can suppress the amplification of the quadratures, as described in Eq. (
58
).

For the non-Hermitian chain structure, phase misalignment can be recalibrated
Jiang et al. 2023
. The phase of the JRM can be adjusted by manipulating the phases of the drive currents that generate the beam-splitter-type and non-degenerate parametric interactions (i.e., the pump current
\displaystyle I_{g/G}
). Since phase alignment maximizes the output power, each JRM’s phase can be tuned during calibration to optimize this. This power maximization criterion can make the calibration process automatic.

Typically, the JRM phase remains stable for much longer than the duration of the typical data acquisition cycle. The total integration time can be divided into several cycles, with calibration of all relevant parameters performed before data collection in each cycle.

On the other hand, the binary tree model in Sec.
V.2
completely circumvents this issue, as both quadratures are equally amplified.

•

In implementing three-wave mixing as outlined in Eq. (
70
), with pumping modes defined in Eq. (
73
), higher-order terms such as
\displaystyle\Phi_{k}^{2}\Phi_{p}^{2}
and
\displaystyle\Phi_{k-1}^{2}\Phi_{p}^{2}
could potentially destabilize the resonator. The pumping mode
\displaystyle\Phi_{p}^{2}
oscillates at frequencies
\displaystyle 2\omega_{\rm rf}^{k}
or
\displaystyle 2\omega_{\rm rf}^{k-1}
, resulting in terms under the RWA that involve
\displaystyle\hat{a}_{k}^{2}
and
\displaystyle\hat{a}_{k-1}^{2}

Ruddy et al. 2024
:

\qquad H^{k}_{\rm JRM}\supset\frac{\sqrt{2}I_{0}}{32\varphi_{0}}\tilde{I}_{g}\tilde{I}_{G}\left(\kappa_{k}^{2}\hat{a}_{k}^{2}+\kappa_{k-1}^{2}\hat{a}_{k-1}^{2}+\mathrm{c.c.}\right),

(77)

which are known as the self-squeezing terms. These effects can be suppressed by detuning the pumping frequencies from
\displaystyle\omega_{\rm rf}^{k-1}\mp\omega_{\rm rf}^{k}

Ruddy et al. 2024
. However, a slight deviation from the optimal setting could destabilize the system, particularly when
\displaystyle|g|
and
\displaystyle|G|
are large. Notably, in alternative configurations like the binary tree structure discussed in Sec.
V.2
, self-squeezing issues are naturally avoided by using only one type of coupling in each connection, facilitated by the introduction of auxiliary modes.

•

Another source of nonlinearities arises from the expansion in Eq. (
72
), which requires that
\displaystyle\Phi_{k}\ll\varphi_{0}
to prevent nonlinear effects from JRMs from invalidating our derivation. As shown in Eq. (
11
),
\displaystyle\Phi_{k}
can be approximated by
\displaystyle\Phi_{k}\sim\langle\hat{a}_{k}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{k}\rangle^{1/2}\kappa_{k}
. The steady-state energy in the resonators, from
\displaystyle\hat{a}_{0}
to
\displaystyle\hat{a}_{N}
, grows geometrically with the ratio
\displaystyle(\mathcal{G}/\gamma_{r})^{2}>1
. Therefore, we only need to ensure that the energy in the last mode,
\displaystyle\hat{a}_{N}
, satisfies
\displaystyle\Phi_{N}\ll\varphi_{0}
.

The steady-state energy of
\displaystyle\hat{a}_{N}
can be estimated as:

\langle\hat{a}_{N}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{N}\rangle\approx\frac{1}{\gamma_{r}}\Delta\omega_{r}\,|S_{0r}(\Omega=0)|^{2}n_{\rm occ}\approx 4,

(78)

where we assume that
\displaystyle|S_{0r}|^{2}
is roughly flat within the response bandwidth to simplify the expression, and we use the benchmark response width from Eq. (
64
) in the final estimate.

Meanwhile, from Eqs. (
12
) and (
13
), we see that zero-point fluctuations
\displaystyle\kappa_{k}
can be adjusted by modifying the readout antenna structure in a cavity or by manipulating the inductance and capacitance of an LC circuit. This allows
\displaystyle\kappa_{k}
to be reduced, ensuring that
\displaystyle\Phi_{k}\ll\varphi_{0}\approx 1.65
in natural units. However, excessively reducing
\displaystyle\kappa_{k}
would decrease the magnitudes of
\displaystyle|g|
and
\displaystyle|G|
(as defined in Eq. (
76
)), necessitating a higher Josephson energy
\displaystyle E_{J}
to compensate.

•

JRMs may experience power saturation when the injected power is too large. Since the power injected from the resonator
\displaystyle\hat{a}_{k}
into the JRM is approximately given by
\displaystyle g\,\omega_{\rm rf}^{k}\langle\hat{a}_{k}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{k}\rangle
, and the coupling strength
\displaystyle g
between each resonator remains nearly constant, it suffices to ensure that the JRM connected to
\displaystyle\hat{a}_{N}
does not reach saturation.

In the benchmark case, with
\displaystyle\langle\hat{a}_{N}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{N}\rangle\approx 4
(from Eq. (
78
)), and considering
\displaystyle\omega_{\rm rf}^{N}\approx 2\pi\,\mathrm{GHz}
and
\displaystyle g\approx\omega_{\rm rf}^{N}
, the maximum injected power is approximately
\displaystyle g\,\omega_{\rm rf}^{N}\langle\hat{a}_{N}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{N}\rangle\approx-107.8\,\mathrm{dBm}
.
According to Ref.
Bergeal et al. 2010b
, the saturation power of the JRM can reach around
\displaystyle-105\,\mathrm{dBm}
. Therefore, in the benchmark case considered in this study, the JRMs will not experience power saturation.

•

The optimized response width discussed in Sec.
V
assumes that the intrinsic noise levels across all modes are comparable, allowing the contribution from the lowest mode to dominate. This assumption may not hold if the auxiliary modes have lower quality factors
\displaystyle Q_{\rm int}
than the probing sensor. To maintain dominance of the lowest mode, it is necessary that
\displaystyle(|g|+|G|)^{2}\gamma_{0}n_{\text{occ}}^{0}>\Delta\omega_{r}^{2}\gamma_{1}n_{\text{occ}}^{1}
, where
\displaystyle\gamma_{0/1}
and
\displaystyle n_{\text{occ}}^{0/1}
represent the dissipation coefficients and thermal occupation numbers of the probing sensor and the auxiliary mode, respectively. This condition becomes particularly challenging in an SRF cavity with a high
\displaystyle Q_{\rm int}
and low
\displaystyle\gamma_{0}
, necessitating that the auxiliary modes also maintain low
\displaystyle\gamma_{1}n_{\text{occ}}^{1}
. To achieve this, one might consider employing other SRF cavities or different cavity modes within the same cavity as auxiliary modes.

•

The readout system is connected only to the final, highest-level mode in the network. This connection—typically implemented via an antenna or transmission line—is essentially identical to the standard readout approach used in single-mode resonators. However, a key distinction in the multi-mode setup is the significantly broadened response width, which requires the amplification chain to perform reliably across a wide frequency range. This imposes practical constraints on the choice of amplifiers. In particular, traveling-wave parametric amplifiers are well-suited for this application, as they offer broadband operation while maintaining quantum-limited noise performance.

•

In the binary tree configuration, achieving the full coherent enhancement of the signal—potentially improving the SNR
2
by a factor of
\displaystyle 2^{N}
—requires phase matching across all probing sensors at the lowest level. Several factors can affect the relative phases of signals arriving at the readout port. First, the intrinsic phase coherence of the background source plays a key role. For ultralight DM, the coherence length is typically
\displaystyle\sim 10^{3}
times longer than the Compton wavelength, which is itself comparable to or larger than the detector scale. This ensures phase coherence across the sensor array. A similar argument applies to HFGW detection using SRF cavities or LC circuits, where the GW wavelength is much longer than the detector dimensions. In the case of heterodyne upconversion detection, the background electromagnetic field used for mixing introduces an additional phase contribution. Furthermore, spatial separations between sensors can introduce relative phase shifts.

A viable calibration strategy is to inject a known coherent signal into all probing sensors simultaneously and adjust the relative phases by tuning the lengths of superconducting delay lines, thereby maximizing the resulting signal at the readout port.

VIII
Discussion and Conclusions

This work demonstrates the efficacy of multi-mode resonators in achieving the advantages of both resonant and broadband detection. These resonators exhibit significant responses to signals across a sensitive bandwidth, spanning one or several orders in the frequency domain of the sources. This capability is achieved by employing both beam-splitter and non-degenerate parametric interactions to connect adjacent modes. By tuning these interactions to comparable magnitudes, off-resonant phases are effectively canceled sequentially, thereby enhancing both the bandwidth and the overall response of the system. Consequently, both the peak value and bandwidth of the scattering matrix increase sequentially during propagation towards the readout port, while the readout noise, which sets the standard quantum limit of the sensitive response width, remains unaffected. By upgrading to multi-mode detectors, the scan rate can be increased by a factor of approximately
\displaystyle Q_{\rm int}/n_{\text{occ}}
compared to single-mode detectors. Moreover, the need for frequency tuning and calibration in single-mode resonators is eliminated, saving valuable time and enabling the scanning of large, unexplored regions of axion and dark photon DM, along with HFGWs, within a reasonably short timeframe. Notably, this includes the exploration of the well-motivated QCD axion
Peccei and Quinn 1977
DM mass window above kHz.

The practical implementation of this concept relies on utilizing Josephson junctions, achievable with mature superconducting technology. The stability of the sensitive response width to variations in the two coupling values ensures the robustness of the quantum network. In the chain model described by Eq. (
49
), calibration of the relative phases of the two couplings is necessary, and potential decoherence may arise from self-squeezing and phase fluctuations of the pumping modes
Wang et al. 2017
. However, such issues are circumvented in the binary tree model described in Eq. (
59
), where the two quadratures are equally amplified
Chen et al. 2022a
. Another crucial consideration is the intrinsic dissipation of the probing sensors, which requires precise control once the multi-mode array is formed.

Note that the multi-mode resonators discussed in this work are compatible with squeezing technology employed at the readout port
Zheng et al. 2016
;
Malnou et al. 2019
;
Backes et al. 2021
;
Lehnert 2021
. Both approaches aim to increase the range in which intrinsic noises dominate over readout noise. To further enhance sensitivity, additional probing sensors can be incorporated
Chen et al. 2022a
;
Brady et al. 2022
, which can be naturally embedded into a multi-mode network like the binary tree. Utilizing spatially distributed sensors and sensors with different sensitive directions can reveal both macroscopic properties and the microscopic nature of potential sources, such as angular distribution and polarization
Foster et al. 2021
;
Chen et al. 2022b
.
