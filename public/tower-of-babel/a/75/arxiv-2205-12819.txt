Claire Bruchez, Pierre de la Harpe, and Tatiana Nagnibeda

Abstract
. A graph G = ( V , E ) G=(V,E) of bounded degree has an adjacency operator A A which acts on the Hilbert space ℓ 2 ​ ( V ) \ell^{2}(V) . There are different kinds of measures of interest on the spectrum Σ ⁡ ( A ) \Sigma(A) of A A . In particular, each vector ξ ∈ ℓ 2 ​ ( V ) \xi\in\ell^{2}(V) defines a local spectral measure μ ξ \mu_{\xi} at ξ \xi on Σ ⁡ ( A ) \Sigma(A) ; therefore each vertex v ∈ V v\in V defines a vector δ v ∈ ℓ 2 ​ ( V ) \delta_{v}\in\ell^{2}(V) and the associated measure μ v \mu_{v} on Σ ⁡ ( A ) \Sigma(A) . A vertex v v is dominant if, for all w ∈ V w\in V , the measure μ w \mu_{w} is absolutely continuous with respect to μ v \mu_{v} (it then follows that, for all ξ ∈ ℓ 2 ​ ( V ) \xi\in\ell^{2}(V) , the measure μ ξ \mu_{\xi} is absolutely continuous with respect to μ v \mu_{v} ). The main object of this paper is to show that all possibilities occur: in some graphs, for example in vertex-transitive graphs, all vertices are dominant; in other graphs, only some vertices are dominant; and there are graphs without dominant vertices at all.

A

In the seminal paper by Kesten

2.
Spectral measures, local spectral measures,

and dominant vectors for a bounded self-adjoint operator

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert space (here always over the complex numbers, and not
\{0\}
)
and
X
a bounded self-adjoint operator on
\mathcal{H}
.
The spectrum
\Sigma(X)
is the set of complex numbers
\lambda

such that
\lambda-X
is not invertible; it is a nonempty compact subset of
\mathbf{R}
,
contained in the interval
[-\|X\|,\|X\|]
.
The
spectral measure
of
X
is the projection-valued measure

E_{X}\,\colon\mathfrak{B}_{\Sigma(X)}\to{\rm Proj}(\mathcal{H})
such that

(1)

X=\int_{\Sigma(X)}tdE_{X}(t),

where
\mathfrak{B}_{\Sigma(X)}
is the
\sigma
-algebra of Borel sets on
\Sigma(X)

and
{\rm Proj}(\mathcal{H})
is the set of orthogonal projections of
\mathcal{H}
onto closed subspaces.
A
scalar-valued spectral measure
for
X

is a finite positive measure
\mu\,\colon\mathfrak{B}_{\Sigma(X)}\to\mathbf{R}_{+}

such that
\mu(B)=0
(the zero number)
if and only if
E_{X}(B)=0
(the zero projection on
\mathcal{H}
)
for any Borel subset
B
of
\Sigma(X)
;
this terminology is that of
[
Conw–07
]
.
The existence of a scalar-valued spectral measure for
X

is guaranteed by Proposition
2.1
;
it follows from the definition that two scalar-valued spectral measures for
X

are equivalent to each other.

For any bounded Borel complex-valued function on the spectrum,

f\in\mathcal{B}(\Sigma(X))
,
functional calculus provides a bounded operator
f(X)
on
\mathcal{H}
;
this holds in particular for any continuous function on the spectrum,

f\in\mathcal{C}(\Sigma(X))
.
The local spectral measure of
X
at two vectors
\xi
and
\eta
in
\mathcal{H}

is the complex measure
\mu_{\xi,\eta}
on
\Sigma(X)
defined by

\int_{\Sigma(X)}f(t)d\mu_{\xi,\eta}(t)=\langle f(X)\xi\hskip-1.42271pt\mid\hskip-1.42271pt\eta\rangle\hskip 14.22636pt\text{for all}\hskip 5.69046ptf\in\mathcal{C}(\Sigma(X))

or equivalently by

\mu_{\xi,\eta}(B)=\langle E_{X}(B)\xi\hskip-1.42271pt\mid\hskip-1.42271pt\eta\rangle\hskip 14.22636pt\text{for all Borel subset}\hskip 5.69046ptB\subset\Sigma(X).

In particular
the
local spectral measure
of
X
at a vector
\xi\in\mathcal{H}

is the positive measure on
\Sigma(X)
denoted by
\mu_{\xi}

(rather than
\mu_{\xi,\xi}
) defined by

(2)

\int_{\Sigma(X)}f(t)d\mu_{\xi}(t)=\langle f(X)\xi\hskip-1.42271pt\mid\hskip-1.42271pt\xi\rangle\hskip 14.22636pt\text{for all}\hskip 5.69046ptf\in\mathcal{C}(\Sigma(X)).

The norm of
\mu_{\xi}
is
\mu_{\xi}(\Sigma(X))=\|\xi\|^{2}
.
It follows from the definitions that
\mu_{\xi}
is absolutely continuous
with respect to any scalar-valued spectral measure for
X
.
Note that
\mu_{\xi}
can equally be viewed as a positive measure on
\mathbf{R}

with closed support contained in
\Sigma(X)
.

A vector
\xi\in\mathcal{H}
is
dominant
for
X

if
\mu_{\eta}\prec\hskip-2.84544pt\prec\mu_{\xi}

for all
\eta\in\mathcal{H}
,
in other words if the measure
\mu_{\eta}
is absolutely continuous
with respect to
\mu_{\xi}
for all
\eta\in\mathcal{H}
,
i.e., if the measure
\mu_{\eta}
is dominated by the measure
\mu_{\xi}

for all
\eta\in\mathcal{H}
.

For examples of dominant vectors, see Proposition
2.4
below.
For an easy non-example, consider a self-adjoint operator
X

which has an eigenvalue
\lambda

and which is not
\lambda
times the identity;
an eigenvector of eigenvalue
\lambda
is not a dominant vector for
X
.

Terminology varies from one author to the other.
A dominant vector for
X
is

maximal relative to
X
in
[
DuSc–63
, Chap. X, Section 5, Definition 6]
,
it is a
vector of maximal type
in
[
BoSm–20
]
,
and a
separating vector

for the von Neumann algebra generated by
X
in
[
Dixm–69
]
.
For
\xi
dominant, the measure
\mu_{\xi}

is a scalar-valued spectral measure for
X
;
conversely, any scalar-valued spectral measure
is a measure
\mu_{\xi}
for some dominant
\xi\in\mathcal{H}

[
Conw–07
, Theorem IX.8.9 and Lemma IX.8.6]
.

Proposition 2.1

(
existence and characterizations of dominant vectors
for self-adjoint operators
)
.

Let
X
be a bounded self-adjoint operator on a separable Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
.
Let
\mathcal{B}(\Sigma(X))
and
E_{X}
be as above.

(1)

There exist dominant vectors for
X
.
More precisely, for any
\eta\in\mathcal{H}
, there exists a dominant vector
\xi
for
X

such that
\eta
is in the closed linear span of
\{X^{n}\xi\}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}

(2)

A vector
\xi\in\mathcal{H}
is dominant for

if and only if, for

the equality

(3)

A vector
\xi\in\mathcal{H}
is dominant for

if and only if,
for any Borel subset

\Sigma(X)
, we have

\mu_{\xi}(B)=0
if and only if

i.e., if and only if
\mu_{\xi}
is a scalar-valued spectral measure for

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
,
X
, and
E_{X}
be as in Proposition
2.1
.
Let
\xi\in\mathcal{H}
and let
(\varepsilon_{i})_{i\geq 1}
be an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{H}
.
Denote by
\mu_{\xi}
and
\mu_{i}
the local spectral measures of
X

\xi

\varepsilon_{i}
.

Then the vector
\xi
is dominant for

if and only if
the measure
\mu_{\xi}
dominates

.

Proof.

If
\xi
is dominant,
then
\mu_{i}\prec\hskip-2.84544pt\prec\mu_{\xi}
for all
i\geq 1
,
by definition.
Conversely, suppose that
\mu_{i}\prec\hskip-2.84544pt\prec\mu_{\xi}
for all
i\geq 1
.
Let
B
be a Borel subset of
\Sigma(X)
.
If
\mu_{\xi}(B)=0
,
then
\mu_{i}(B)=\langle E_{X}(B)\varepsilon_{i}\hskip-1.42271pt\mid\hskip-1.42271pt\varepsilon_{i}\rangle=\|E_{X}(B)\varepsilon_{i}\|^{2}=0

for all

, namely
E_{X}(B)\varepsilon_{i}=0

,
hence
E_{X}(B)=0
.
It follows that
\xi
is dominant for
X

(see
(3)
in Proposition

).

Let
X
be a bounded self-adjoint operator on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
.
A vector
\xi\in\mathcal{H}
is
cyclic
for
X

if the closed linear span of
\{X^{n}\xi:n\in\mathbf{N}\}

is the whole of
\mathcal{H}
.
(Some authors choose another definition for more general operators,
but all definitions agree in case
X
is self-adjoint;
see
[
Sim4–15
, Page 289, § 5.1]
.)
Note that, if
X
has any cyclic vector at all,
then the space
\mathcal{H}
is separable.

The following proposition is well-known and straightforward:

Proposition 2.3
.

For a self-adjoint operator
X
, any cyclic vector is dominant.

Proof.

Let
\xi
be a cyclic vector for
X
.
Let
f
in
\mathcal{B}(\Sigma(X))
be a bounded Borel function on the spectrum of
X
.
If
f(X)\xi=0
, then
f(X)X^{n}\xi=X^{n}f(X)\xi=0
for all
n\geq 0
,
hence
f(X)\eta=0
for all
\eta
in the closed linear span of
\{X^{n}\xi:n\in\mathbf{N}\}
,
hence
f(X)=0
.
(We have used the characterization of dominant vectors given in
(2)

of Proposition
2.1
).
∎

There are dominant vectors which are not cyclic,
for example when
X
is a self-adjoint operator on a finite-dimensional space
which has multiple eigenvalues
(more generally when
X
is a self-adjoint operator which is not multiplicity-free).

We will use the following facts on dominant vectors for simple multiplication operators.
Let
\Sigma
be a nonempty compact subset of the real line,

\mathfrak{B}_{\Sigma}
the Borel
\sigma
-algebra of
\Sigma
,
and
\mu
a finite Borel measure on
(\Sigma,\mathfrak{B}_{\Sigma})
,
of closed support
\Sigma
.
The
multiplication operator

M_{\mu}

is here the operator defined on
L^{2}(\Sigma,\mu)
by

(M_{\mu}\xi)(t)=t\xi(t)\hskip 14.22636pt\text{for all}\hskip 5.69046pt\xi\in L^{2}(\Sigma,\mu)\hskip 5.69046pt\text{and}\hskip 5.69046ptt\in\Sigma.

It is a bounded self-adjoint operator
with norm
\max_{t\in\Sigma}|t|

and with spectrum
\Sigma
.
The function
1_{\Sigma}\in L^{2}(\Sigma,\mu)
of constant value
1

is a cyclic vector for
M_{\mu}
.
More is true:

Proposition 2.4

(
cyclic and dominant vectors for multiplication operators
)
.

Let
M_{\mu}
be a multiplication operator as above and
\xi\in L^{2}(\Sigma,\mu)
.
The following three properties are equivalent:

(i)

\xi
is cyclic for
M_{\mu}
,

(ii)

\xi
is dominant for
M_{\mu}
,

(iii)

\xi
is such that
\mu(\{t\in\Sigma:\xi(t)=0\})=0
.

Proof.

Property
(i)
implies Property
(ii)

by Proposition
2.3
.

Suppose that
(iii)
does not hold.
Define
f\in\mathcal{B}(\Sigma)
by

f(t)=1
when
\xi(t)=0
and
f(t)=0
when
\xi(t)\neq 0
;
then
f(M_{\mu})\neq 0
and
f(M_{\mu})\xi=0
,
hence
(ii)
does not hold.
This shows that
(ii)
implies
(iii)
.

Suppose that
(iii)
holds.
Denote by
L^{2}_{\xi}
the closed subspace of
L^{2}(\Sigma,\mu)

spanned by
\{(M_{\mu})^{n}\xi\}_{n\in\mathbf{N}}
.
We have to show that
L^{2}_{\xi}=L^{2}(\Sigma,\mu)
.
For this we consider
\eta\in L^{2}(\Sigma,\mu)

orthogonal to
(M_{\mu})^{n}\xi
for all

,
and we will show that
\eta=0
.
The condition
\eta\perp(M_{\mu})^{n}\xi
reads

\int_{\Sigma}t^{n}\xi(t)\overline{\eta(t)}d\mu(t)=0\hskip 14.22636pt\text{for all}\hskip 5.69046ptn\geq 0,

so that

[formula]

\int_{\Sigma}p(t)\xi(t)\overline{\eta(t)}d\mu(t)=0\hskip 14.22636pt\text{for all}\hskip 5.69046ptp\in\mathcal{P}(\Sigma),

where
\mathcal{P}(\Sigma)
is the algebra of functions on
\Sigma

which are restrictions to
\Sigma

\mathbf{R}
.
Note that
\xi\overline{\eta}
is in

and that

is the dual of the Banach space

.
Note also that
\mathcal{P}(\Sigma)
is w*-dense in

because the algebra
\mathcal{P}(\Sigma)
is dense in the Banach algebra

Consequently, it follows from (

) that

The last equality and Condition

imply that

,

This shows that

Corollary 2.5
.

Let
X
be a bounded self-adjoint operator
on a separable Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
.
Assume that there exists a cyclic vector for
X
.

Then a vector in
\mathcal{H}
is dominant for
X
if and only if it is cyclic for
X
.

Proof.

Let
\Sigma
denote the spectrum of
X
,
let
\xi_{0}
be a cyclic vector for
X
,
and let
\mu
denote the local spectral measure of
X
at
\xi_{0}
.
Then
X
is unitarily equivalent to the operator
M_{\mu}

of Proposition
2.4
; more precisely,
there exists a surjective isometry
U\,\colon\mathcal{H}\to L^{2}(\Sigma,\mu)

such that
X=U^{*}M_{\mu}U

and
U\xi_{0}
is the function on
\Sigma
of constant value
1
.
This can be viewed as a form of the spectral theorem:
see
[
Sim4–15
, Theorem 5.1.7]
.
Note that the equality
UX=M_{\mu}U
determines for each

the image of

by

; indeed

(UX^{n}\xi_{0})(t)=(M_{\mu}^{n}U\xi_{0})(t)=t^{n}(U\xi_{0})(t)

for all

,
so that

is the function

in
L^{2}(\Sigma,\mu)
.

Since a vector
\xi\in\mathcal{H}
is dominant [respectively cyclic] for

if and only if

is dominant [respectively cyclic] for

,
the corollary follows from Proposition

.

(i)

3.
Vertex spectral measures and dominant vertices

for adjacency operators of graphs

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph with
V
countable, and of bounded degree.
Denote by
A
the adjacency operator of
G
and by
\Sigma(A)
its spectrum.
The Hilbert space
\ell^{2}(V)
has a canonical basis
(\delta_{v})_{v\in V}

of characteristic functions of vertices,

\delta_{v}(v)=1
and
\delta_{v}(w)=0
for
w\in V\smallsetminus\{v\}
.
With respect to this basis, the adjacency operator
A
of
G

has matrix coefficients

\langle A\delta_{w}\hskip-1.42271pt\mid\hskip-1.42271pt\delta_{v}\rangle=\left\{\begin{aligned} 1\hskip 5.69046pt&\text{if}\hskip 5.69046pt\{v,w\}\in E\\
0\hskip 5.69046pt&\text{otherwise}\end{aligned}\right.

(we think of
v
as a row index and of
w
as a column index for the matrix of
A
).
Observe that
A=0
if and only if
G
has no edge.

The following proposition describes the convex hull of the spectrum
\Sigma(A)
.
Though most of it is quite standard (see
[
Moha–82
]

and
[
Woes–00
, Lemmas 10.1 and 1.7]
),
we did not find a convenient reference
for the precise inequalities of Claim
(i)
,
indeed for
m_{A}<0<M_{A}=\|A\|
,
so that we decided to include Proposition
3.1
here.

Proposition 3.1

(
smallest and largest spectral values
)
.

Let
G=(V,E)
,
A
, and
\Sigma(A)
be as above.
We assume that
E\neq\emptyset
.

(i)

We have
\|A\|\geq 1
and

-\|A\|\leq\min\{t\in\Sigma(A)\}\leq-1<1\leq\max\{t\in\Sigma(A)\}=\|A\|.

(ii)

If
G
is bipartite, then
\Sigma(A)
is symmetric with respect to zero,
so that in particular
\min\{t\in\Sigma(A)\}=-\|A\|
.

Remarks.

Let
X
be a bounded self-adjoint operator on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
.
Set

\displaystyle M_{X}=\sup\{t\in\mathbf{R}

\displaystyle\mid t=\langle X\xi\mid\xi\rangle\hskip 5.69046pt\text{for some}\hskip 5.69046pt\xi\in\mathcal{H},\hskip 2.84544pt\|\xi\|=1\},

\displaystyle m_{X}=\inf\{t\in\mathbf{R}

\displaystyle\mid t=\langle X\xi\mid\xi\rangle\hskip 5.69046pt\text{for some}\hskip 5.69046pt\xi\in\mathcal{H},\hskip 2.84544pt\|\xi\|=1\}.

It is well-known that

M_{X}=\max\{t\in\Sigma(X)\},\hskip 14.22636ptm_{X}=\min\{t\in\Sigma(X)\},

so that the closed interval
\mathopen{[}m_{X},M_{X}\mathclose{]}

is the convex hull of the spectrum
\Sigma(X)
.
Moreover
-\|X\|\leq m_{X}\leq M_{X}\leq\|X\|

and
\Sigma(X)
contains at least one of
\|X\|,-\|X\|

see for example
[
Halm–67
, Problem 171]
;
but
M_{X}
need not be
\geq 0
and

need not be
\leq 0
.

When

is connected, we have moreover

unless

is in a well-known list of finite graphs, the finite Coxeter graphs;
the list is for example in
[

, Section 3.1.1]

see also Example

Similarly, finite connected graphs with

[

A connected finite graph such that

This does not carry over to infinite graphs; indeed, there exists

is symmetric with respect to

for example the second ladder-like graph in

See however Proposition

(iv)

Proposition 3.2

(

Definition 3.3
.

In a graph
G=(V,E)
,
a vertex
v\in V
is

dominant
if the vector
\delta_{v}\in\ell^{2}(V)
is dominant for
A
,
and
cyclic
if
\delta_{v}
is cyclic for
A
.

By Proposition
2.2
, a vertex
v
is dominant in
G

as soon as the vertex spectral measure
\mu_{v}
dominates
\mu_{w}

for all
w\in V
.

As examples below will show, the vertices of a graph need not be all dominant,
indeed some graphs have no dominant vertex at all.
In other terms, a vertex spectral measure need not be
a scalar-valued spectral measure.

We provide in this section examples of graphs for which
it is easy to check that all vertices are dominant.
There are further examples in Section
7
,
for which the determination of dominant vertices is not so straightforward,
and relies on the analysis of a family of Jacobi operator in Section
6
.

Proposition 3.4

(

)
.

Let

be a graph of bounded degree and

(1)

If

are two vertices such that there exists an automorphism of

to

, the vertex spectral measures
\mu_{v}
and
\mu_{w}
are equal.

(2)

If the group of automorphisms of

then all vertex spectral measures are equal, and all vertices are dominant.

Proof.

Preliminary step.

An automorphism

on

The operator

indeed, for all

\langle U_{\alpha}^{*}AU_{\alpha}\delta_{v}\hskip-1.42271pt\mid\hskip-1.42271pt\delta_{w}\rangle=\langle AU_{\alpha}\delta_{v}\hskip-1.42271pt\mid\hskip-1.42271ptU_{\alpha}\delta_{w}\rangle=\langle A\delta_{\alpha(v)}\hskip-1.42271pt\mid\hskip-1.42271pt\delta_{\alpha(w)}\rangle=\left\{\begin{aligned} 1\hskip 5.69046pt&\hskip 5.69046pt\text{if}\hskip 5.69046pt\{\alpha(v),\alpha(w)\}\in E\\
0\hskip 5.69046pt&\hskip 5.69046pt\text{if not,}\end{aligned}\right.

so that
\langle U_{\alpha}^{*}AU_{\alpha}\delta_{v}\hskip-1.42271pt\mid\hskip-1.42271pt\delta_{w}\rangle=\langle A\delta_{v}\hskip-1.42271pt\mid\hskip-1.42271pt\delta_{w}\rangle

commutes with

Proof of
(1)
.

Let

Proof of

We first recall some facts from linear algebra.
Consider a linear endomorphism

\mathcal{H}
of positive dimension.
For the next three definitions and for Proposition
4.1

need not be self-adjoint,
indeed

can be a vector space over any field, say
\mathbf{K}
.
Let
\mathbf{K}[T]
denote the ring of polynomial in one variable over
\mathbf{K}

The
minimal polynomial

P_{{\rm min}}\in\mathbf{K}[T]
of

is the monic polynomial which generates the ideal
\{Q\in\mathbf{K}[T]:Q(X)=0\}
,
equivalently the monic polynomial of minimal degree such that
P_{{\rm min}}(X)=0

For
\xi\in\mathcal{H}

the

of

is the monic polynomial which generates the ideal

\{Q\in\mathbf{K}[T]:Q(X)\xi=0\}

Observe that

divides

.

is

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
X
be an endomorphism of a finite dimensional vector space
\mathcal{H}

over a field
\mathbf{K}
.
Let
P_{{\rm min}}
be the minimal polynomial of
X
,
and let
s
be its degree.

(1)

There exist dominant vectors for
X
.

(2)

Let
\xi\in\mathcal{H}
and let
P_{\xi}
be the minimal polynomial of
(X,\xi)
.
The following conditions are equivalent:

(i)

\xi
is dominant for
X
,

(ii)

P_{\xi}=P_{{\rm min}}
,
i.e.,
the degree of
P_{\xi}
is
s
,

(iii)

the vectors
\xi,X\xi,X^{2}\xi,\ldots,X^{s-1}\xi
are linearly independent.

Proof.

(1)

Let
P_{{\rm min}}=\prod_{j=1}^{d}(Q_{j})^{\ell_{j}}

be the decomposition of the minimal polynomial
in product of powers of irreducible polynomials in
\mathbf{K}[T]
.
There exists a direct sum decomposition
\mathcal{H}=\bigoplus_{j=1}^{d}V_{j}

in
X
-invariant subspaces such that, for each
j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
,
the minimal polynomial of the restriction of
X
to
V_{j}
is
Q_{j}^{\ell_{j}}
.
For appropriate choices of vectors
\xi_{j}\in V_{j}
,
the sum
\sum_{j=1}^{d}\xi_{j}
is dominant for
X
.
For the details, see
[
Gant–77
, § VII.2]
.

Note that Gantmacher does not have a specific terminology
for the vectors which are called dominant here.

In the particular case of a diagonalisable operator,
the
V_{j}
’s are the eigenspaces of
X

and
\sum_{j=1}^{d}\xi_{j}
is dominant for
X

for any choice of nonzero vectors
\xi_{j}\in V_{j}
.

(2)

It follows from the definitions that
(2)(i)
holds, i.e.,
that the ideals

\{Q\in\mathbf{K}[T]:Q(X)=0\}
and
\{Q\in\mathbf{K}[T]:Q(X)\xi=0\}
coincide,
if and only if
P_{\xi}=P_{{\rm min}}
, i.e.,
if and only if
(2)(ii)
holds.
Also
(2)(ii)
holds, i.e.,

Q(X)\xi\neq 0
for any polynomial

of degree at most

,
if and only if
\xi,X\xi,X^{2}\xi,\ldots,X^{s-1}\xi
are linearly independent, i.e.,
if and only if
(2)(iii)
holds.
∎

The space

The moments of

(3)

\int_{\Sigma(X)}t^{n}d\mu_{\xi}(t)=\langle X^{n}\xi\hskip-1.42271pt\mid\hskip-1.42271pt\xi\rangle=\bigg\langle\sum_{j=1}^{s}\lambda_{j}^{n}\xi_{j}\hskip 2.84544pt\bigg|\hskip 2.84544pt\sum_{k=1}^{s}\xi_{k}\bigg\rangle=\sum_{j=1}^{s}\lambda_{j}^{n}\|\xi_{j}\|^{2}

Let

\mu_{\xi}\big(\{\lambda_{j}\}\big)=\langle P_{j}(X)\xi\hskip-1.42271pt\mid\hskip-1.42271pt\xi\rangle=\langle\xi_{j}\hskip-1.42271pt\mid\hskip-1.42271pt\xi\rangle=\|\xi_{j}\|^{2}\hskip 14.22636pt\text{which is $>0$ if and only if $\xi_{j}\neq 0$.}

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a finite dimensional Hilbert space
and
X
a self-adjoint operator on
\mathcal{H}
.
The
counting measure
for
X

is the probability measure
\tau
on
\Sigma(X)
defined by

\tau(\{\lambda\})=\frac{\text{multiplicity of}\hskip 5.69046pt\lambda}{\dim\mathcal{H}}\hskip 14.22636pt\text{for each}\hskip 5.69046pt\lambda\in\Sigma(X).

The terminology “counting measure” is that of
[
Grig–11
]
;
other authors use
density of states
, or DOS,
a term which refers to solid state physics.
Note that
\tau
is in particular a scalar-valued spectral measure for
X
.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
X
be a self-adjoint operator on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}

of finite dimension
n
.
Let
(\varepsilon_{i})_{1\leq i\leq n}
be an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{H}
.
For
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, denote by
\mu_{i}

the local spectral measure of
X
at
\varepsilon_{i}
.

Then
\tau=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}
is the counting measure for
X
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
denote the algebra of all operators on
\mathcal{H}
;
it is isomorphic to the matrix algebra
{\rm M}_{n}(\mathbf{C})
.
Let
\mathbf{C}[X]
be the subalgebra of
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
generated by
X
;
any
Y\in\mathbf{C}[X]
is of the form
f(X)
for some function
f\,\colon\Sigma(X)\to\mathbf{C}
.
For
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, the measure
\mu_{i}
,
viewed as a linear form on
\mathbf{C}[X]
, extends to the linear form

\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathbf{C},\hskip 5.69046ptY\mapsto\langle Y\varepsilon_{i}\hskip-1.42271pt\mid\hskip-1.42271pt\varepsilon_{i}\rangle,\hskip 14.22636pt\text{again denoted by $\mu_{i}$}.

The linear form
\tau=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}

is the normalized trace on
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
.

Denote by

\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{s}
the eigenvalues
X

and by
m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{s}
their multiplicities.
For
j\in\{1,\ldots s\}
, let
P_{j}
denote the orthogonal projection
of
\mathcal{H}
onto the eigenspace
\ker(\lambda_{j}-X)
.
Let
Y\in\mathbf{C}[X]
;
let
f
be a function
\Sigma(X)\to\mathbf{C}
such that
Y=f(X)
;
we have

\tau(Y)=\tau(f(X))=\tau\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{s}f(\lambda_{j})P_{j}\Big)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{s}f(\lambda_{j})m_{j}.

In the particular case of a characteristic function of the eigenvalue
\lambda_{j}
of
X
,
for some
j\in\{1,\ldots,s\}
, this becomes

\tau(\{\lambda_{j}\})=\frac{m_{j}}{n}>0
;
and this ends the proof.
∎

As a particular case, we obtain the following fact which is well-known,
and explicit for example in
[
Grig–11
, Proposition 10.2]
.

Proposition 4.9

(
the counting measure is the average of the vertex spectral measures
)
.

For the adjacency operator
A
of a finite graph
G=(V,E)
with
n
vertices,

\frac{1}{n}
times the sum of the vertex spectral measures at all vertices

is the counting measure on the spectrum of

Definition 6.1

of a bounded self-adjoint operator on

.
We may view

Let

onto the line generated by

(1,0,0,0,0,\ldots)

.
We denote by

Lemma 6.2
.

The notation is as above.

(1)

For
n\geq 1
, we have
\|S^{n}\|=\max\{a,1\}
.

(2)

The spectral radius of
S
is
1
.

(3)

Any

such that

is an eigenvalue of
S
.

(4)

The spectrum of

is the closed disc

.

(5)

For

such that

, the number
z

(6)

(7)

Proof.

(1)

On the one hand, for

.
Since

,

hence

.
On the other hand, for all
\xi
in

, we have

\displaystyle\|S^{n}\xi\|^{2}

\displaystyle=|a\xi_{n}|^{2}+|\xi_{n+1}|^{2}+|\xi_{n+2}|^{2}+\cdots=a^{2}|\xi_{n}|^{2}+|\xi_{n+1}|^{2}+|\xi_{n+2}|^{2}+\cdots

\displaystyle\leq\big(\max\{a,1\}\big)^{2}\sum_{j=n}^{\infty}|\xi_{j}|^{2}\leq\big(\max\{a,1\}\big)^{2}\|\xi\|^{2},

hence

.

(2)

This follows from

.

(3)

Set

.
Then

(4)

By

(5)

Let

, and

(6)

(7)

Lemma 6.3

(1)

(2)

\frac{1}{z}(1-zS)\hskip 2.84544pt\big[(1-z^{2}(a^{2}-1))P_{0}+1-P_{0}\big]\hskip 2.84544pt(1-zS^{*})=\frac{1}{z}+z-J_{a}.

\displaystyle=\hskip 2.84544pt\frac{1}{z}\big[-z^{2}(a^{2}-1)P_{0}+1-zS+z^{3}(a^{2}-1)P_{0}S^{*}-zS^{*}+z^{2}SS^{*}\big]

\displaystyle=\hskip 2.84544pt\frac{1}{z}\big[1-zJ_{a}+z^{2})\big]\hskip 2.84544pt=\hskip 2.84544pt\frac{1}{z}+z-J_{a}.

8.
Markov operators and graphs without
M
-dominant vertices

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph in which all vertices have finitely many neighbours.
For
u\in V
, denote by
\deg(u)
the number of its neighbour,
and set
\deg_{\sharp}(u)=\max\{\deg(u),1\}
.
Rather than
\ell^{2}(V)
and
A
, consider the Hilbert space

\ell^{2}_{\sharp}(V)=\left\{\eta\,\colon V\to\mathbf{C}\mid\sum_{u\in V}|\eta(u)|^{2}\deg_{\sharp}(u)<\infty\right\},

with scalar product denoted by

\langle\eta_{1}\mid\eta_{2}\rangle_{\sharp}=\sum_{u\in V}\eta_{1}(u)\overline{\eta_{2}(u)}\deg_{\sharp}(u)
.
For
v\in V
, we denote by
\delta_{v}\in\ell^{2}_{\sharp}(V)

the characteristic function of the vertex
v
,
and by
\varepsilon_{v}\in\ell^{2}_{\sharp}(V)
the vector
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\deg_{\sharp}(V)}}\delta_{v}
;
the family
(\varepsilon_{v})_{v\in V}
is an orthonormal basis of
\ell^{2}_{\sharp}(V)
.
The
Markov operator

M
of
G
defined by

(M\eta)(u)=\frac{1}{\deg(u)}\sum_{v\sim u}\eta(v)\hskip 14.22636pt\text{for all}\hskip 5.69046pt\eta\in\ell^{2}_{\sharp}(V)\hskip 5.69046pt\text{and}\hskip 5.69046ptu\in V,

where
\sum_{v\sim u}
is a summation over all vertices
v
which are neighbours of
u
.
(If
\deg(u)=0
, the summation is on an empty set of
v
’s and
(M\eta)(u)=0
.)
It is elementary to check that

\|M\|\leq 1
and that
M
is self-adjoint on
\ell^{2}_{\sharp}(V)

(see
[
Kowa–19
, § 3.2]
).
We have

M\delta_{w}=\sum_{u\sim w}\frac{1}{\deg(u)}\delta_{u}\hskip 14.22636pt\text{and}\hskip 14.22636ptM\varepsilon_{w}=\sum_{u\sim w}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\deg(u)\deg(w)}}\varepsilon_{u},

so that the matrix
\left(M_{v,w}\right)_{v,w\in V}
of
M

with respect to the basis
(\varepsilon_{v})_{v\in V}
is given by

M_{v,w}=\langle M\varepsilon_{w}\mid\varepsilon_{v}\rangle_{\sharp}=\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{1}{\sqrt{\deg(v)\deg(w)}}\hskip 14.22636pt&\text{if}\hskip 5.69046ptv,w\hskip 5.69046pt\text{are neighbours in}\hskip 5.69046ptV\\
0\hskip 36.98866pt\hskip 14.22636pt&\text{otherwise}\end{aligned}\right.

(we think of
v
as a row index and of
w
as a column index for the matrix of
M
).
Observe that
\frac{\sqrt{\deg(w)}}{\sqrt{\deg(v)}}M_{v,w}
,
which is
\frac{1}{\deg(v)}
if
w
is a neighbour of
v
and
0
if not,
is the probability that a random walk starting at
v
is at
w
after one step;
more generally

\frac{\sqrt{\deg(w)}}{\sqrt{\deg(v)}}(M^{n})_{v,w}=\frac{\sqrt{\deg(w)}}{\sqrt{\deg(v)}}\langle M^{n}\varepsilon_{w}\mid\varepsilon_{v}\rangle_{\sharp}=p^{(n)}_{v,w}

is the probability that a random walk starting at
v
is at
w
after
n
steps.
A vector
\eta\in\ell^{2}_{\sharp}(V)
defines
a local spectral measure for
M
, say
\nu_{\eta}
,
and a vertex
v\in V
defines a vertex spectral measure for
M
, say
\nu_{v}=\nu_{\varepsilon_{v}}
.
A vertex
v
is
M
-dominant
if
\nu_{\eta}\prec\hskip-2.84544pt\prec\nu_{v}

for all
\eta\in\ell^{2}_{\sharp}(V)
.

When
G
is a regular graph of some degree
k
,
the space
\ell^{2}_{\sharp}(V)
and
\ell^{2}(V)
are the same as sets,
scalar products are contant multiples of one another,

\langle\eta_{1}\hskip-1.42271pt\mid\hskip-1.42271pt\eta_{2}\rangle_{\sharp}=\frac{1}{k}\langle\eta_{1}\hskip-1.42271pt\mid\hskip-1.42271pt\eta_{2}\rangle
,
and
M=\frac{1}{k}A
.
In this case, a vertex of
G
is
M
-dominant if and only if it is
A
-dominant.

However, this does not hold in general.
Consider for example an integer
n\geq 2
and
the finite path
P_{n}
with vertices
\{v_{0},\ldots,v_{n-1}\}
.
Then
M
has a simple eigenvalue
\cos\frac{k\pi}{n-1}

with eigenvector

\eta_{k}=\big(\cos\frac{kj\pi}{n-1}\big)_{j=0,\ldots,n-1}

for each
k\in\{0,\ldots,n-1\}
.
It follows that,
when
n
is even, all vertices of
P_{n}
are
M
-dominant;
recall from Example
5.1
that,
when
n+1
is not prime, some vertices are not
A
-dominant.
In contrast, when
n=9
, the only
M
-dominant vertices of
P_{9}

are
v_{0}
and
v_{8}
, but the
A
-dominant vertices are
v_{0},v_{2},v_{6},v_{8}
.

For the connected finite graphs we know without
A
-dominant vertices,
a close look shows that these graphs are also without
M
-dominant vertices.
It is open for us whether this holds for all graphs without
A
-dominant vertices.
Note that Propositions 3.4 and 3.7
hold for

without changes.
