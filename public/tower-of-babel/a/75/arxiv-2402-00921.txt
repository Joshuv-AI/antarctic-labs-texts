Allocation of Indivisible Items with a Common Preference Graph: Minimizing Total Dissatisfaction
Nina Chiarelli, Clément Dallard, Andreas Darmann, Stefan Lendl, Martin Milanič, Peter Muršič, Ulrich Pferschy

Abstract
Allocating indivisible items among a set of agents is a frequently studied discrete optimization problem. In the setting considered in this work, the agents’ preferences over the items are assumed to be identical. We consider a very recent measure for the overall quality of an allocation which does not rely on numerical valuations of the items. Instead, it captures the agents’ opinion by a directed acyclic preference graph with vertices representing items. An arc ( a , b ) (a,b) in such a graph means that the agents prefer item a a over item b b . For a given allocation of items the dissatisfaction of an agent is defined as the number of items which the agent does not receive and for which no more preferred item is given to the agent. Our goal is to find an efficient allocation of the items to the agents such that the total dissatisfaction over all agents is minimized. We explore the dichotomy between NP -hard and polynomially solvable instances, depending on properties of the underlying preference graph. While the problem is NP -hard already for three agents even on very restricted graph classes, it is polynomially solvable for two agents on general preference graphs. For an arbitrary number of agents, we derive polynomial-time algorithms for relevant restrictions of the underlying undirected graph. These are trees and, among the graphs of treewidth two, series-parallel graphs and cactus graphs. Keywords: efficient allocation, partial order, preference graph, dissatisfaction, polynomial-time algorithm, computational complexity MSC (2020): 91B32, 90C27, 68Q25, 05C85, 05C20, 05C05 90B10 06A06

Introduction

The effective allocation of tasks, goods or general objects (from now on called
items
) to the members of a group (called
agents
) is a classical topic in Applied Mathematics.
Often these items represent indivisible elements that can only be assigned as a whole, and each item can be assigned to a most one agent.
This property puts the problem into the area of Discrete Optimization where it gave rise to a large body of literature (e.g.,
[
6
,
15
,
23
,
25
]
).

A wide range of models have been put forward to allocate items to agents on basis of their preferences over the items, capturing agents’ preferences in different ways. These include, e.g., ranking all subsets of items (see
[
21
]
), numerical evaluations of the items (see, e.g.,
[
1
,
6
,
3
]
), and ordinal preferences over the items (see, e.g.,
[
2
,
4
,
9
]
).
From a practical point of view, however, ranking all subsets of items becomes very tedious for a small number of items already; also, it can be quite demanding to come up with a numerical value (representing utility or profit) for each item, if an acceptable monetary valuation is not obvious.
Indeed, for many persons it can be very challenging to find meaningful numerical values to express their preferences on a larger set of items.
On the other hand, it seems much easier to compare two items and tell which one is liked better.
Also from a theoretical point of view ordinal valuations have certain advantages over cardinal valuations (see, e.g.,
[
2
]
).
However, if items include very different categories of objects, one may find many pairs of items simply incomparable.
Therefore, a
partial order
can be seen as a very natural way for agents to express their preferences over a set of heterogenous items.
In this paper we will consider the case of identical preferences expressed by a common partial order that is represented by a directed acyclic graph.
Such a group consensus might be reached by agents exchanging their opinions, in particular for sets of less well-known items, or if the agents form a homogenous group with very similar views.
Allocations of items to agents with identical preferences over items were treated in the literature (e.g., in
[
7
,
8
,
18
]
).

For preferences expressed by a partial order the key question remains: How do we evaluate the outcome of an allocation? What is a suitable measure for the utility obtained by an agent from the subset of allocated items?
Recently, in
[
13
]
we introduced a new measure for expressing
satisfaction
or
dissatisfaction
over partial preferences of an agent as follows.
If a certain item is assigned to the agent, then all more preferred items cause dissatisfaction to the agent, unless the agent receives an even more preferred item.
On the other hand, less preferred items contribute to the satisfaction of the agent.

More formally, the preferences of the agents can be represented by a common acyclic directed
preference graph

G=(V,A)
, where
V
is the set of items and an arc
(a,b)\in A
means that all agents prefer item
a
over item
b
.
Since preferences should be transitive, two arcs
(a,b)
and
(b,c)
imply that
a
is also preferred over
c
, even if the arc
(a,c)
is not contained in
A
.
An item
a

dominates
item
b
if there is a directed path from
a
to
b
in
G
.
Allocating a subset of items
S\subseteq V
to an agent incurs a satisfaction value equal to the number of items dominated by any item in
S
plus
|S|
.
On the other hand the resulting dissatisfaction value is given by the number of items neither allocated to that agent nor dominated by any item in
S
.
Clearly, for any allocation satisfaction and dissatisfaction values add up to
|V|
for every agent since every item contributes to exactly one of the two values in any allocation.

In this paper we want to find an allocation with maximal total satisfaction over all agents (i.e., the sum of each agent’s satisfaction), which is equivalent to minimizing total dissatisfaction. In terms of social welfare induced by an allocation, maximizing total satisfaction reflects the idea of maximizing
utilitarian social welfare
, hence aiming at an efficient allocation. We point out that in our companion paper
[
11
]
, the goal is to maximize
egalitarian social welfare
, where the aim is to make the least satisfied agent as happy as possible (see also Section
1.1
).
Our research agenda focuses on the characterization of the boundary between
NP
-hard and polynomially solvable cases of the problem depending on structural properties of the given preference graph.

1.1
Contribution of the paper

At first we give a simple linear-time algorithm for general preference graphs with two agents.
Then we show that the problem is
NP
-hard
for at least three agents, even for a rather simple class of preference graphs with no directed path of length two and no vertex of in-degree greater than
2
.
This hardness result for graphs of “height” two is complemented by a polynomial-time algorithm for preference graphs with width at most two for an arbitrary number of agents.
In our arguments we employ a close connection with the graph coloring problem (Section
3
).

As a main result of the paper we present a fairly involved, but linear-time algorithm for so-called polytrees, i.e., directed graphs whose underlying undirected graph is a tree (Section
4
).
Note that this allows vertices with arbitrary in- and out-degree and implies preferences structures much more complex than simple directed out-trees.
The result also holds for a collection of polytrees, i.e., a polyforest.

Moving beyond tree structures, we then develop polynomial-time algorithms for classes of instances where the underlying undirected graph of the preference graph has treewidth at most two.
For
s,t
-series-parallel graphs as well as for a directed version of cactus graphs the problem can be solved for an arbitrary number of agents in polynomial time (Section
5
).

Some of the earlier results of this paper were included in the proceedings paper
[
12
]
.

1.2
Our related work

In a companion paper
[
11
]
, we recently considered the same preference framework with a common preference graph but for a different objective function.
We studied a fairness criterion which minimizes the maximum dissatisfaction over all agents (or equivalently maximizes the minimum satisfaction).
In this setting, one wants to make the least satisfied agent as happy as possible without considering the total satisfaction.
It is shown in
[
11
]
that the same complexity boundary between
k=2
and
k\geq 3
agents holds for that case.
Other polynomial results were derived for very simple graph structures, such as directed matchings and out-stars.
For out-trees, a polynomial result was given for a constant number of agents.
Furthermore, fixed parameter tractability results for a bounded number of agents were given for preference graphs that can be decomposed into path modules and independent set modules.
Compared to these findings, which employ algorithms quite different from the current paper, we show here that the minimization of total dissatisfaction permits polynomial algorithms for more general graphs.

Sticking to the utilitarian approach of minimizing the total dissatisfaction, it is also interesting to consider the case where each agent reports its own preference graph, as it was done in
[
13
]
.
This general setting with individual preference graphs turned out to be much harder from a computational complexity perspective.

However, we could show that the problem
can be solved in polynomial time in each of the following cases
[
13
]
:

(1)

if each preference graph is a disjoint union of paths,

(2)

for two agents, if each preference graph is a disjoint union of out-stars, and

(3)

for any constant number
k
of agents if the underlying undirected graph of the union of the preference graphs has bounded treewidth
t
(see
[
13
, Theorem 16]
for details); in fact, for this case a fixed-parameter tractable algorithm was given with respect to
k+t
.

A fortiori, these cases are polynomial time solvable also for common preference graphs.
On the other hand, in the case of individual preference graphs the problem of minimizing the total dissatisfaction was shown in
[
13
]
to be
NP
-complete in the case when each preference graph is an out-tree, as well as for two agents, even if the two sets of items are the same and no preference graph contains a two-edge directed path.
As a consequence of the results of the present paper, both of these intractable cases turn out to be solvable in polynomial time in the case of a common preference graph.

2
Preliminaries

For the sake of consistency, definitions and notation are mostly taken from
[
13
]
.
The description coincides with the framework in the companion paper
[
11
]
.

We will consider directed and undirected finite graphs without loops or multiple edges.
For brevity, we will often say
graph
when referring to a directed graph.
A
weakly connected component
of a directed graph
G
is the subgraph of
G
induced by the vertex set of a connected component of the underlying undirected graph of
G
.

Consider a directed graph
G=(V,A)
, where the
n:=|V|
vertices correspond to
n
items.
For
a=(u,v)\in A
, vertex
u
is called the
tail
of
a
and vertex
v
is called the
head
of
a
.
The
in-degree
of a vertex
u
is the number of arcs in
A
for which
u
is the head and the
out-degree
of
u
is the number of arcs in
A
for which
u
is the tail.
The
degree
of a vertex
u
is the number of arcs in
A
for which
u
is either the head or the tail.
A vertex with in-degree
0
is called a
source
.
A vertex with out-degree
0
is called a
sink
.

A sequence
p=(v_{0},v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{\ell})
with
\ell\geq 0

and
(v_{i},v_{i+1})\in A
for each
i\in\{0,\ldots,\ell-1\}
is called a
walk
of length
\ell
from
v_{0}
to
v_{\ell}
; it is a
path
(of length
\ell
from
v_{0}
to
v_{\ell}
) if all its vertices are pairwise distinct.
A walk from
v_{0}
to
v_{\ell}
is
closed
if
v_{0}=v_{\ell}
.
A
cycle
is a closed walk of positive length in which all vertices are pairwise distinct, except that
v_{0}=v_{\ell}
.

A
directed acyclic graph
is a directed graph with no cycle. Observe that in a directed acyclic graph there is always at least one source and at least one sink.
An
out-tree
is a directed acyclic graph
G=(V,A)
with a dedicated vertex
r
(called the
root
) such that for each vertex
v\in V\setminus\{r\}
there is exactly one path from
r
to
v
.
An
out-forest
is a disjoint union of out-trees.
A directed acyclic graph whose underlying undirected graph is a tree (resp., a forest) will be called a
polytree
(resp., a
polyforest
).

A directed acyclic graph
G=(V,A)
induces a binary relation
\succ
over
V
, defined by setting
u\succ v
if and only if
u\neq v
and there is a directed path from
u
to
v
in
G
; in such a case, we say that the agents prefer
u
to
v
.
In particular, a directed acyclic graph induces a strict partial order over
V
.

For
v\in V
let
{\it\textnormal{pred}}(v)
denote the set of predecessors of
v
, i.e., the set of all vertices
u\neq v
such that there is a path from
u
to
v
in
G
.
In addition, let
{\it\textnormal{succ}}(v)\subseteq V
denote the set of successors of
v
in graph
G
, i.e., the set of all vertices
u\neq v
such that there is a path from
v
to
u
in
G
.
We denote by
\textnormal{pred}[v]
the set
\textnormal{pred}(v)\cup\{v\}
, similarly we denote by
\text{succ}[v]
the set
\text{succ}(v)\cup\{v\}
.
For
u,v\in V
we say that item
u
is
dominated
by item
v
if
v\in{\textnormal{pred}}[u]
.

For a directed acyclic graph
G=(V,A)
we denote by
N^{-}(v)
the set of all in-neighbors of
v\in V
, formally
N^{-}(v)=\{u\in V\colon(u,v)\in A\}
and similarly by
N^{+}(v)
the set of all out-neighbors of
v
, that is
N^{+}(v)=\{u\in V\colon(v,u)\in A\}
.

We are given a set of
k
agents denoted by
K=\{1,\ldots,k\}
.
An
allocation

\pi
is a function
K\rightarrow 2^{V}
that assigns to the agents pairwise disjoint sets of items, i.e., for
i,j\in K
,
i\not=j
, we have
\pi(i)\cap\pi(j)=\emptyset
.
Every allocation also implies a
labeling

\lambda_{\pi}
of items, which is a function
V\rightarrow K\cup\{\textsc{null}\}
assigning to every item
v\in V
allocated to agent
i
the label
\lambda_{\pi}(v)=i
and to items
v
not allocated to any agent the label
\lambda_{\pi}(v)=\textsc{null}
.
For a set of items
U\subseteq V
we denote by
\lambda_{\pi}(U)=\{\lambda_{\pi}(v)\in K\colon v\in U\}
the set of agents receiving items from
U
.

As described before, we measure the attractiveness of an allocation by counting the number of items that an agent
does not receive
and for which it receives no other more preferred item.
Formally, for an allocation
\pi
the
dissatisfaction

{\delta}_{\pi}(i)
of agent
i
is defined as the number of items in
G
not dominated by any item in
\pi(i)
.
A
dissatisfaction profile
is a
k
-tuple
(d_{i}\colon i\in K)
with
d_{i}\in\hbox{\rm\vrule\kern-1.0ptN}_{0}
for all
i\in K
such that there is an allocation
\pi
with
{\delta}_{\pi}(i)=d_{i}
for each
i\in K
.

If
\pi
allocates a vertex
v
to agent
i
, it will not change the dissatisfaction
{\delta}_{\pi}(i)
if any vertices in
\text{succ}(v)
are allocated to
i
in addition to
v
.
Thus, we say that if
v\in\pi(i)
, then agent
i

dominates
all vertices in
\text{succ}[v]
. It will also be convenient to define the
satisfaction

s_{\pi}(i)
of agent
i
with respect to allocation
\pi

as the number of items in
V
that are dominated by
i
.

Continuing the work of
[
13
]
we consider the minimization of the
total dissatisfaction among the agents.

Min-Sum Dissatisfaction with a Common Preference Graph

(CG Min-Sum Dissatisfaction)

Input:

A set
K
of agents, a set
V
of items, a directed acyclic graph
G=(V,A)
, and an integer
d
.

Question:

Is there an allocation
\pi
of items to agents such that the total dissatisfaction
\sum_{i\in K}{\delta}_{\pi}(i)
is at most
d
?

Sometimes we will refer to the directed acyclic graph
G
representing the agents’ preferences as the
common graph
.
The corresponding optimization problem asks for an
optimal allocation
, i.e., an allocation that minimizes the total dissatisfaction of all agents.
As pointed out before, minimizing the total dissatisfaction of all agents is equivalent to maximizing the total satisfaction.
We use the former formulation for reasons of consistency with previous works
[
12
,
11
,
13
]
.

Throughout the paper, for our polynomial-time results we provide constructive proofs, i.e., whenever we prove that the decision problem
CG Min-Sum Dissatisfaction
can be decided in polynomial time we actually determine an optimal allocation in polynomial time.

Lemma 1
.

The
CG Min-Sum Dissatisfaction
problem for a common graph
G
with
n
vertices and any set
K
of
k\geq n
agents has a canonical solution independently from
G
.

Proof.

For any allocation
\pi
and any agent
i\in K
, the total satisfaction of
i
is at most
\sum_{v\in\pi(i)}|\textrm{succ}[v]|
and hence the total dissatisfaction is at least

n-\sum_{v\in\pi(i)}|\textrm{succ}[v]|
.
We can thus bound the total dissatisfaction as follows:

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in K}\delta_{\pi}(i)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i\in K}\bigg(n-\sum_{v\in\pi(i)}|\textrm{succ}[v]|\bigg)

\displaystyle=kn-\sum_{i\in K}\bigg(\sum_{v\in\pi(i)}|\textrm{succ}[v]|\bigg)

\displaystyle\geq kn-\sum_{v\in V}|\textrm{succ}[v]|.

Now, if
\pi
is any allocation that assigns each vertex of
G
to a different agent, then each agent
i\in K
that gets assigned some vertex
v\in V
has a dissatisfaction of
n-|\textrm{succ}[v]|
, while each of the remaining
k-n
agents has a dissatisfaction of
n
.
This results in a total dissatisfaction of

\sum_{i\in K}\delta_{\pi}(i)=n^{2}-\sum_{v\in V}|\textrm{succ}[v]|+(k-n)n=kn-\sum_{v\in V}|\textrm{succ}[v]|\,,

hence
\pi
is an optimal allocation.
∎

Following
Lemma

1
, we assume that
k<n
for the rest of the paper.

Observation 2
.

Let
\{G_{1},\ldots,G_{m}\}
be a set of pairwise disjoint and weakly connected graphs. If
CG Min-Sum Dissatisfaction
can be solved in polynomial (resp., linear) time on each graph
G_{j}
, then
CG Min-Sum Dissatisfaction
can be solved in polynomial (resp., linear) time on
G=\bigcup_{j=1}^{m}G_{j}
.

Proof.

Let
\pi_{j}
be an optimal allocation for
CG Min-Sum Dissatisfaction
on
G_{j}
.
Then assigning items
\bigcup_{j=1}^{m}\pi_{j}(i)
to every agent
i\in K
gives an optimal allocation for
CG Min-Sum Dissatisfaction
on
G
since the considered objective function is additive and all
G_{j}
are disjoint.
∎

In some of our results we will make use of the following definitions.
An
antichain
in a directed acyclic graph
G
is a set of vertices that are pairwise unreachable from each other.
The
width
of
G
is the maximum cardinality of an antichain in
G
.

Many of our proofs will employ the following general lemma on the solutions to
CG Min-Sum Dissatisfaction
.
(A similar lemma holds for the problem of minimizing the maximum dissatisfaction, see
[
11
]
.)

Lemma 3
.

When solving the
CG Min-Sum Dissatisfaction
problem for a common graph
G
with
n
vertices and any set
K
of
k
agents such that
k<n
, we may without loss of generality restrict our attention to allocations
\pi
such that for each agent
i\in K
, the set of items allocated to agent
i
forms a nonempty antichain in
G
.

Proof.

Given an agent
i\in K
such that
\pi(i)
does not form an antichain in
G
, from
\pi(i)
we can remove any item that is dominated by some other item in
\pi(i)
without changing the dissatisfaction of agent
i
. Hence, by repeating this process if necessary, we end up with an allocation
\pi
such that for each agent
i\in K
, the set of items
\pi(i)
is an antichain in
G
.

We now argue that we may assume that all these antichains are nonempty.
Let
\pi
be an allocation such that for each agent
i\in K
, the set of items allocated to agent
i
forms an antichain in
G
.
Assume that at least one of these antichains, say
\pi(j)
, for
j\in K
, is empty.
If not all vertices in
G
are allocated, then we can assign any not yet allocated vertex of
G
to agent
j
, thus strictly decreasing the total dissatisfaction.
Repeating this procedure if necessary, we may assume that all vertices in
G
are allocated.
If at least one of these antichains, say
\pi(j)
, is still empty, then, since all the
n
vertices in
G
are allocated to at most
k-1\leq n-1
agents, there exists an agent
i\in K\setminus\{j\}
such that
\{x,y\}\subseteq\pi(i)
for two distinct vertices
x
and
y
of
G
.
Consider now the allocation that removes
y
from
\pi(i)
and assigns
\{y\}
to agent
j
.
Then, the total dissatisfaction of this new allocation does not exceed the total dissatisfaction of the previous allocation.
Repeating this procedure if necessary, we eventually reach an allocation such that the set of items allocated to each agent forms a nonempty antichain in
G
.
∎

3
General results for
CG Min-Sum Dissatisfaction

In
[
12
]
, it was shown that
CG Min-Sum Dissatisfaction
is polynomial-time solvable if there are only two agents and becomes
NP
-complete for
k=3
agents, even if the graph has no directed path of length two and no vertex of in-degree larger than
2
.
This implies that the polynomial-time algorithm for
k=2
is best possible regarding the number of agents, unless
P
=
NP
.
The
NP
-completeness proof for
k=3
in
[
12
]
is based on a fairly involved reduction from
3
-Edge-Coloring
.

We now give shorter proofs of both results.
The proofs are based on the following lower bound on the optimal total dissatisfaction value and a characterization of allocations achieving this lower bound, which we will use in several other proofs as well.

Lemma 4
.

Given a preference graph
G=(V,A)
, for an arbitrary number
k
of agents and any allocation
\pi
the total dissatisfaction is bounded by

\sum_{i\in K}\delta_{\pi}(i)\geq\sum_{v\in V}\max\{k-|\textnormal{pred}[v]|,0\}\,.

(1)

Proof.

We estimate the contribution of each vertex to the dissatisfaction separately.
There are
|\textnormal{pred}[v]|
vertices that dominate vertex
v
, hence at least
k-|\textnormal{pred}[v]|
agents will not be assigned any item that dominates vertex
v
.
In cases where the number of predecessor is large and the above number is negative the bound can clearly be improved to
\max\{k-|\textnormal{pred}[v]|,0\}
.
∎

Using topological sort (see, e.g.,
[
14
]
), we can easily calculate
|\textnormal{pred}[v]|
for all
v\in V
in linear time.
In turn
\sum_{v\in V}\max\{k-|\textnormal{pred}[v]|,0\}
can also be calculated in linear time.

Allocations for which the lower bound from (
1
) is satisfied with equality can be characterized using the following definition.

Definition 5
.

Given a preference graph
G=(V,A)
, any set
K
of agents, and a set
S\subseteq V
, an allocation
\pi
and its corresponding labeling
\lambda_{\pi}
is said to be
S
-good
(with respect to
K
) if for each vertex
v\in S
one of the following two conditions is satisfied:

•

v
is dominated by all agents in
K
, or

•

the labels
\lambda_{\pi}(u)
for
u\in\textnormal{pred}[v]
are pairwise distinct and not
null
.

A
good
allocation is an allocation that is
V
-good.

Lemma 6
.

Given a preference graph
G
for an arbitrary set
K
of agents, an allocation
\pi
achieves the lower bound from (
1
) if and only if it is a good allocation.

Proof.

Let
k=|K|
.
Assume that
\pi
is good and consider an arbitrary vertex
v
in
G
.
If
|\textnormal{pred}[v]|\leq k
, then all vertices in
\textnormal{pred}[v]
are assigned to different agents, since
\pi
is
\{v\}
-good.
Thus, the contribution of
v
to the total dissatisfaction of
\pi
is exactly
k-|\textnormal{pred}[v]|=\max\{k-|\textnormal{pred}[v]|,0\}
.
If
|\textnormal{pred}[v]|>k
, then each agent
i\in K
receives an item that dominates
v
, since
\pi
is
\{v\}
-good.
Therefore, the contribution of
v
to the total dissatisfaction of
\pi
is
0=\max\{k-|\textnormal{pred}[v]|,0\}
.
It follows that the total dissatisfaction of
\pi
meets the lower bound given by
Lemma

4
, and hence
\pi
is optimal.

Conversely, assume an allocation
\pi
meets the bound from
Lemma

4
.
Then it must hold that for every
v\in V
we have
\max\{k-|\textnormal{pred}[v]|,0\}
agents that do not dominate it.
If
\max\{k-|\textnormal{pred}[v]|,0\}=0
, then
v
is dominated by all agents in
K
, implying
\pi
is
\{v\}
-good.
If
\max\{k-|\textnormal{pred}[v]|,0\}\neq 0
, then there are
k-|\textnormal{pred}[v]|>0
agents that do not dominate
v
, implying that
|\textnormal{pred}[v]|<k
agents dominate
v
, hence
\lambda_{\pi}(u)
for
u\in\textnormal{pred}[v]
are pairwise distinct and not
null
, implying
\pi
is
\{v\}
-good.
∎

Lemmas

4
and
6
immediately imply the following.

Corollary 7
.

For any preference graph
G
and any number of agents, any good allocation is optimal for
CG Min-Sum Dissatisfaction
, that is, it minimizes the total dissatisfaction.

The use of good allocations simplifies both proofs of the mentioned results from
[
12
]
.
We first give a short proof that for two agents
CG Min-Sum Dissatisfaction
can be solved in linear time.

Theorem 8
.

For
k=2
and any preference graph

Using the concept of

Lemma 9

•

While in general, even for

In the rest of the paper, we identify some cases when polynomial-time solvability holds also for the case of arbitrary number of agents.
We start with the simplest case, which is a particular case of graphs with treewidth one.

Observation 12
.

For any number

can be solved in linear time if the preference graph

Proof.

The resulting allocation is good (cf.

.
∎

We now present our main result, a linear-time algorithm for

on polytrees, i.e., directed graphs imposed on a tree structure.
Different from out-trees where a level-oriented optimal solution for

The main idea of the algorithm is to process the polytree branch after branch (a branch is a directed path starting from a source vertex) and assign sources and vertices with in-degree

6
Conclusions

This paper studies the efficient allocation of
n
indivisible items among
k
agents with identical preferences.
Here, efficiency is expressed by the goal of maximizing utilitarian social welfare, i.e., maximizing the sum of satisfaction values or, equivalently, minimizing the sum of dissatisfaction values over all agents.
A companion paper
[
11
]
considered the fairness criterion of egalitarian social welfare by minimizing the maximum dissatisfaction among all agents.
Satisfaction and dissatisfaction result from a common preference graph shared between all agents.
In this acyclic digraph satisfaction of an agent counts the number of all vertices assigned to that agent plus all vertices that are dominated by these.
Dissatisfaction is the complement of satisfaction, i.e., the number of undominated vertices without the allocated vertices.

We study the computational complexity of the problem and derive a strong
NP
-hardness result for
k\geq 3
agents even for a highly restricted preference graph.
On the other hand, the problem can be solved in linear time for
k=2
agents on arbitrary graphs and for an arbitrary number of agents on special graph classes, where the underlying undirected graph is a tree. In addition, for an arbitrary number of agents the problem is solvable in polynomial time when the underlying directed graph belongs to certain subclasses of graphs with treewidth two.
In particular, whereas for the class of out-trees the problem is known to be
NP
-complete for agents with individual (non-identical) preference graphs
[
13
]
,
CG Min-Sum Dissatisfaction
, i.e., the problem for agents with a common preference graph, turns out to be solvable in linear time. This also settles the computational complexity for the class of out-stars, for which the computational complexity is still open when agents do not necessarily have a common preference graph.

However, also for agents with a common preference graph, there are several graph classes for which the complexity is still an open problem. Particular examples, when considering an arbitrary number of agents, are the classes of graphs of bounded width or bounded treewidth.

\widehat{G}=(\widehat{V},E)
defined as follows:

1.

The vertex set of
\widehat{G}
is
\widehat{V}=V\cup V^{\prime}
where
V^{\prime}=\{v^{\prime}:v\in V\}
is a set of new vertices.

2.

There are two types of edges in
\widehat{G}
: edges of the form
\{x,y\}\subseteq V
, where
\{x,y\}
is an antichain
in
G
with size two, and edges of the form
\{v,v^{\prime}\}
where
v\in V
.

Note that
\widehat{G}
is bipartite.
Indeed, if
G
has width one, then each of
V
and
V^{\prime}
is an independent set in
\widehat{G}
and their union is
\widehat{V}
.
If
G
has width two, then by Dilworth’s theorem there exists a chain partition
\{P,Q\}
of
G
with size two, and the sets
V(P)\cup\{v^{\prime}:v\in V(Q)\}
and
V(Q)\cup\{v^{\prime}:v\in V(P)\}
are independent sets in
\widehat{G}
with union
\widehat{V}
.
Furthermore, the edges of the form
\{v,v^{\prime}\}
where
v\in V
, form a perfect matching in
\widehat{G}
.

We now assign nonnegative weights to the edges of
\widehat{G}
as follows.
For each edge
e=\{x,y\}\subseteq V
corresponding to an antichain in
G
with size two, we define the weight to
e
to be the dissatisfaction of an agent
i\in K
under any allocation
\pi
such that
\pi(i)=\{x,y\}
, that is, the total number of vertices in
G
that are not reachable by a path from
\{x,y\}
.
Similarly, for each edge of the form
\{v,v^{\prime}\}
where
v\in V
, we define the weight to
e
to be the dissatisfaction of an agent
i\in K
under any allocation
\pi
such that
\pi(i)=\{v\}
, that is, the total number of vertices in
G
that are not reachable by a path from
v
.
Clearly, this weight function can be computed in polynomial time given
G
.

When solving the
CG Min-Sum Dissatisfaction
problem for
G
and
K
,
Lemma

3
implies that each agent
i\in K
will be allocated either only one item or two items forming an antichain.
To each such allocation
\pi
we can associate a matching
M_{\pi}
in
\widehat{G}
as follows.
For each agent
i\in K
receiving only one item, say
\pi(i)=\{v\}
for some
v\in V
, we include in
M_{\pi}
the edge
\{v,v^{\prime}\}
.
For each agent
i\in K
receiving some antichain
\{x,y\}\subseteq V
of size two, i.e.
\pi(i)=\{x,y\}
, we include in
M_{\pi}
the edge
\{x,y\}
.
Since the sets of items assigned to different agents are pairwise disjoint, the obtained set
M_{\pi}
is indeed a matching.
Furthermore, since for each of the
k
agents, the set of items allocated to the agent forms a
nonempty
antichain in
G
, we can associate to each agent
i\in K
a unique edge
e_{i}\in M_{\pi}
; in particular,
M_{\pi}
has cardinality
k
.
By construction, the weight of the edge
e_{i}
equals to the dissatisfaction of agent
i
with respect to the allocation
\pi
.
Note that the above procedure can be reversed: to any matching
M=\{e_{i}:i\in K\}
of cardinality
k
in
\widehat{G}
, we can associate an allocation
\pi_{M}
of items in
G
to the
k
agents such that the dissatisfaction of agent
i
under
\pi_{M}
equals the weight of the edge
e_{i}
.

It follows that the
CG Min-Sum Dissatisfaction
problem for
G
and
K
can be reduced in polynomial time to the problem of computing a matching in
\widehat{G}
with cardinality
k
such that the total weight of the matching edges is as small as possible.
By
Lemma

17
, this problem can be solved in polynomial time.
