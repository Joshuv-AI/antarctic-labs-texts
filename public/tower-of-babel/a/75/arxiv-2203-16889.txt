Abstract.

The paper addresses a conjecture of Shapiro and Tater on the similarity between two sets of points in the complex plane; on one side is the values
of
t\in\mathbb{C}
for which the spectrum of the quartic anharmonic oscillator in the complex plane

\derivative[2]{y}{x}-\left(x^{4}+tx^{2}+2Jx\right)y=\Lambda y,

with certain boundary conditions, has repeated eigenvalues.
On the other side is the set of zeroes of the Vorob’ev-Yablonskii polynomials, i.e. the poles of rational solutions of the second Painlevé equation.
Along the way, we indicate a surprising and deep connection between the anharmonic oscillator problem and certain degenerate orthogonal polynomials.

1
Introduction and results

The second Painlevé equation is an ODE in the complex domain given by

\derivative[2]{u}{t}=2u^{3}+tu+\alpha,

(PII)

with
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
and
t\in\mathbb{C}
.
It admits rational solutions when
\alpha=n\in\mathbb{Z}
and this was recognized by Vorob’ev and Yablonskii in two separate papers
[
Vor65
,
Yab59
]
. For
\alpha=n
there are
n^{2}
poles of the rational solution, of which
n(n-1)/2
correspond to poles with residue
+1
and the remaining
n(n+1)/2
correspond to poles with residue
-1
. Both sets of poles are zeros of certain polynomials defined recursively (see below), and that are referred to as Vorob’ev-Yablonsky (
1
) polynomials.

The study of the asymptotic behaviour of the poles of rational solutions of Painlevé equations has received significant attention both in the community of researchers interested in Painlevé theory
[
CM03
,
Mas18
]
and also due to their occurrence in the description of asymptotic behaviour in the semiclassical limit of the Sine-Gordon equation
[
BM12
,
BM14
,
BM15
,
BB15
]
.
The poles form a very regular “triangular” pattern as it can be seen in Fig.
2
.

A seemingly disconnected problem consists in the study of the spectrum of the following boundary value problem
for the anharmonic oscillator

\displaystyle\derivative[2]{y(x)}{x}-\left(x^{4}+tx^{2}+2Jx\right)y(x)=\Lambda y(x)

(1.1)

\displaystyle y(x)\to 0\mbox{ as $x\to\infty$ and $\arg(x)=\pi,\pm\pi/3$,}

(1.2)

where
t
and
J
are in general complex parameters, and
x
,
y(x)
are the complex independent and dependent variable, respectively. In other words (
1.1
) is an ODE in the complex domain.

The equation is equivalent to a tri-confluent Heun equation by a gauge transformation
y(x)=p(x){\rm e}^{x^{3}/3+t/2x}
.
Following the definition in
[
BB98
]
a quantum mechanical potential is
Quasi Exactly Solvable
(QES)
if a finite portion of the energy spectrum and associated eigenfunctions can be found exactly and in closed form, while the
potential is
Exactly Solvable
(ES) if all the spectrum and associated eigenfunctions can be found exactly and in closed form. In particular
it was shown in
[
ST22
]
that the eigenvalue problem (
1.1
) with only
two out of the three
boundary conditions at infinity is
Quasi Exactly Solvable
.
Here we show that the quartic anharmonic oscillator in (
1.1
), with the boundary conditions (
1.2
) is Exactly Solvable and admits a solution if and only if
J=n+1\in\mathbb{N}
is a positive integer. The corresponding eigenfunctions are quasi–polynomials (i.e. a polynomial times a fixed exponential factor).

These polynomials have been studied in the works of Eremenko and Gabrielov
[
EG11
,
EG12
]
and also Mukhin and Tarasov
[
MT13
]
. We actually find several additional structures relating these polynomials to a special type of orthogonality (see below).
For every given
t\in\mathbb{C}
, the corresponding eigenvalues of these quasi–polynomial eigenfunctions are the zeros of a secular equation of degree
n+1
in the shifted variable
\lambda=\Lambda-\frac{t^{2}}{4}
.
There are particular discrete values of
t\in\mathbb{C}
for which the spectrum of the boundary problem (
1.1
)-(
1.2
) becomes
degenerate
, namely, one of the eigenvalues has higher algebraic multiplicity.

For a fixed
J\in\mathbb{N}
, these particular values of the parameter
t\in\mathbb{C}
form a pattern shown in Fig.
2
that is ostensibly very similar to the pattern of the poles of the rational solutions of the Painlevé II equation (i.e. the zeroes of the Vorob’ev–Yablonskii polynomials).

Figure 1:
Scaled roots of the Vorob’ev-Yablonsky polynomials
Y_{n}(n^{2/3}s)
in red, and roots of the discriminant
D_{n}(n^{2/3}s)
in black, for
n=30
.
This particular scaling was conjectured by Shapiro and Tater in
[
ST22
]
.

This similarity is the object of a conjecture that B. Shapiro and M. Tater floated several years ago, but only recently formalized in
[
ST22
]
(Conjecture 2 ibidem).

The present paper addresses this Shapiro-Tater (ST) conjecture. Further, along the way, we investigate the rather surprisingly deep connections of the boundary value problem (
1.1
)-(
1.2
) with “degenerate” orthogonal polynomials: in fact we show that the polynomials arising from the solutions of (
1.1
)-(
1.2
) satisfy an excess of orthogonality conditions.
We also quantify the conjecture by estimating the discrepancy of the patterns of the two lattices coming from the VY polynomials on one side and the ST boundary problem on the other, explaining also why they have the regular pattern that appears numerically.

For the zeros of the VY polynomials, the analysis of these patterns was extensively already explained in
[
BM14
,
BM15
,
BB15
]
; the rather surprising result is the explanation of the connection between the two seemingly distant problems that we have briefly outlined above.

Detailed results.

We now explain the conjecture in more detail.
Suppose we are looking for
quasi-polynomials
solutions of (
1.1
),
that is, solutions of the form

y(x)=p(x)e^{\theta(x)},\quad\text{ where }\theta(x)=\frac{x^{3}}{3}+\frac{tx}{2}

(1.3)

and
p(x)
is a polynomial.
We may sometimes use the notation
\theta(z;t)
to emphasize the dependence of
\theta
on the parameter t. A substitution into (
1.1
) leads to

\derivative[2]{p}{x}+(2x^{2}+t)\derivative{p}{x}-2(J-1)xp=\lambda\,p,\;\;\text{with }\lambda=\Lambda-\frac{t^{2}}{4}.

Upon setting
J=n+1
, one notices that the ODE

\derivative[2]{p}{x}+(2x^{2}+t)\derivative{p}{x}-2nxp=\lambda p,

(1.4)

preserves the finite dimensional linear space of polynomials of degree at most
n
.
The left hand side of (
1.4
) is a linear operator acting on the space of polynomials of degree at most
n
that can be written as a
(n+1)\times(n+1)
matrix
M_{n}(t)
with respect to the usual basis

(1,x,x^{2},\dots,x^{n})^{t}
, namely

M_{n}(t):=\begin{bmatrix}0&-2n&&&&&\\
t&0&-2(n-1)&&&&\\
2&2t&0&&&&\\
&6&3t&0&\ddots&&\\
&&12&\ddots&\ddots&-4&\\
&&&\ddots&&0&-2\\
&&&&n(n-1)&nt&0\end{bmatrix}\,.

(1.5)

We observe that the matrix
M_{n}(t)
is a
4
-diagonal matrix.
Eigenvalues of this matrix correspond to the eigenvalues
\lambda
in (
1.4
),
and the eigenvectors correspond to quasi-polynomial eigenfunctions of
the differential operator (
1.1
) via the identification (
1.3
).
Therefore we can compute the spectrum of (
1.1
) from the characteristic polynomial

C_{n}(t,\lambda):=\det(M_n(t) - \lambda I).

(1.6)

Now consider the discriminant of
C_{n}(t,\lambda)
with respect to
\lambda
, i.e.

D_{n}(t):=\text{Disc}_{\lambda}(C_{n})(t).

(1.7)

The roots of
D_{n}(t)=0
are precisely the values of
t
such that
the matrix
M_{n}(t)
has repeated eigenvalues (algebraic multiplicity greater than 1).

The Shapiro-Tater conjecture
[
ST22
]
relates the zeros of
D_{n}(t)
to
the poles of rational solutions of
PII
.
The Painlevé II equation has rational solutions
u(t)

if and only if
\alpha=n\in\mathbb{Z}
, and we denote them by
u_{n}(t)
.
Furthermore
u_{n}(t)
is given explicitly in terms of the
Vorob’ev-Yablonsky polynomials
Y_{n}(t)
in the form:

u_{n}(t)=\derivative{t}\log\frac{Y_{n-1}(t)}{Y_{n}(t)}

(1.8)

The VY polynomials are constructed recursively
[
Vor65
,
Yab59
]
as follows:

{Y}_{n+1}(t){Y}_{n-1}(t)=t{Y}_{n}^{2}(t)-4\Big[{Y}_{n}^{\prime\prime}(t){Y}_{n}(t)-\big({Y}_{n}^{\prime}(t)\big)^{2}\Big],\hskip 14.22636ptn\geq 1,\ t\in\mathbb{C}

with
{Y}_{0}(t)=1,{Y}_{1}(t)=t
.
They can also be represented in terms of Schur function indexed by the “staircase partition”
[
KO96
]

Y_{n}(t)=\left(-\frac{4}{3}\right)^{n(n+1)/6}\left(\prod_{k=1}^{n}(2k-1)!!\right)S_{(n,n-1,\dots,1)}\left(\left(-\frac{3}{4}\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}t,0,1,0,0,\dots\right).

(1.9)

The conjecture of Shapiro and Tater
[
ST22
]
is the numerical observation that can be summarized in the conjecture
loosely
formulated below.

Conjecture 1.1
.

The roots of the rescaled discriminant
D_{n}(s)=\text{Disc}_{\lambda}(C_{n})(n^{\frac{2}{3}}s)
in (
1.7
) and the roots of the rescaled Vorob’ev-Yablonsky polynomials
Y_{n}(n^{\frac{2}{3}}s)

form two coinciding lattices as
n\to\infty
.

The evidence leading Shapiro and Tater to make their conjecture was purely numerical, as seen in Fig.

Remark 1.2
.

When
\alpha=n
the rational solutions
u_{n}(t)
of (
PII
) have two types of poles: those of residue
+1
and those of residue
-1
.
This follows from the fact that all the zeroes of
Y_{n}(t)
are simple,

Y_{n}(t),Y_{n+1}(t)
do not share any roots
[
Tan00
]
and that rational solutions have the shape

u_{n}(t)=\derivative{t}\log\frac{Y_{n-1}(t)}{Y_{n}(t)}=\frac{Y^{\prime}_{n-1}(t)}{Y_{n-1}(t)}-\frac{Y^{\prime}_{n}(t)}{Y_{n}(t)}.

(1.10)

Therefore for fixed
n\in\mathbb{N}
the poles of residue
+1
correspond to zeroes of

Y_{n-1}(t)
and poles of residue
-1
correspond to zeroes of
Y_{n}(t)

The results of the paper can be grouped into two categories: the first consists in “structural” analysis of the boundary value problem (
1.1
)-(
1.2
), in Section
3
. The second category involves the asymptotic study for large

and the description of the ST lattice, Sections
4
,
5.1
,
5.2
. The first set of results can be summarized in the following points:

1.

The boundary problem (
1.1
)-(
1.2
) has solutions if and only if
J=n+1\in\mathbb{N}
; in this case
\Lambda
takes at most
n+1
values for each
t\in\mathbb{C}
. See Proposition
3.2
.
For fixed
J\in\mathbb{N}
these values
(t,\Lambda)
are called the
Exactly-Solvable
(ES) spectrum.

2.

For given
J=n+1\in\mathbb{N}
and
(t,\Lambda)
in the ES spectrum, the corresponding solution of (
1.1
) is of the form
y(x)=p_{n}(x){\rm e}^{2\theta(x;t)}
with
p_{n}(x)
a polynomial of degree
n
and
\theta(x;t)=\frac{x^{3}}{3}+\frac{tx}{2}
. This polynomial is
degenerate
orthogonal in the sense that

\displaystyle\left(\varkappa\int_{\infty_{1}}^{\infty_{3}}+\widetilde{\varkappa}\int_{\infty_{3}}^{\infty_{5}}\right)p_{n}(x)x^{j}{\rm e}^{2\theta(x;t)}{\mathrm{d}}x=0,\ \ j=0,\dots,n,

(1.11)

where
\varkappa
and
\tilde{\varkappa}
are some constants. Here
\infty_{k}
denotes a contour that tends to infinity with asymptotic direction of argument
\frac{i\pi k}{3}
with
k=1,3,5
.
Observe that the orthogonality involves all powers including the
n
-th power, and this means that the
(n+1)
-st Hankel determinant of the moments of the above pairing is zero. This result is contained in Section
3.2
and in particular Theorem
3.4
.
These results extend the several properties established for these polynomials in
[
EG11
,
EG12
,
MT13
]
.

3.

In fact more is true: given
t\in\mathbb{C}
suppose that
\varkappa,\widetilde{\varkappa}
are such that (
1.11
) admits a nontrivial polynomial
p_{n}
for solution. Then
y(x)=p_{n}(x){\rm e}^{\frac{x^{3}}{3}+tx}
is a solution of the boundary problem (
1.1
)–(
1.2
). This is proved in Theorem
3.6
.

4.

The two above results, together, yield a complete characterization of the ES spectrum of the quartic anharmonic oscillator with boundary conditions (
1.2
) in terms of degenerate orthogonality. See Corollary
3.8

5.

We then investigate the consequences of the requirement that an eigenvalue is repeated; we prove that this is equivalent to the additional condition that
both
integrals in (
1.11
) for the degenerate orthogonal polynomials vanish independently, that is equivalent to:

\displaystyle\int_{\infty_{2j+1}}^{\infty_{2j+3}}p_{n}(x)^{2}{\rm e}^{2\theta(x;t)}{\mathrm{d}}x=0,\quad j=0,1.

(1.12)

Note that their linear combination in (
1.11
) vanishes due to the degeneracy of the orthogonality pairing, so that this condition implies only one additional constraint on the value of
t
. This is proven in Theorem
3.9
.
Another amusing consequence is that in these cases the antiderivative of
p_{n}(x)^{2}{\rm e}^{\frac{2x^{3}}{3}+tx}
can be shown to be also a quasipolynomial. See Corollary

.

The second set of results involves the asymptotic analysis for large
n
and contains the actual proof of the Shapiro-Tate conjecture. This begins in Section
4
where we introduce the following rescaled variables for the Shapiro-Tater and Jimbo-Miwa cases

\displaystyle\mbox{Shapiro-Tater},\quad z

\displaystyle=\hbar^{\frac{1}{3}}x,\qquad{s}=\hbar^{\frac{2}{3}}t,\qquad E=\hbar^{\frac{4}{3}}\Lambda,\quad\hbar^{-1}=n+1

\displaystyle\mbox{Jimbo-Miwa},\quad z

\displaystyle=\hbar^{\frac{1}{3}}x,\qquad{s}=\hbar^{\frac{2}{3}}a,\qquad\hat{b}=\hbar^{\frac{4}{3}}b,\;\;E=\frac{7s^{2}}{36}+10\hat{b},\quad\hbar^{-1}=n+\frac{1}{2}\,.

\hbar

\displaystyle\hbar^{2}\derivative[2]{y}{z}-Q(z;{s},E)y=0,

\displaystyle Q(z;{s},E)=z^{4}+{s}z^{2}+2z+E\,.

This puts both the ST and JM anharmonic oscillators on the same footing and allows us to use the exact WKB method to compute asymptotic expressions for the Stokes phenomenon of both systems simultaneously.

2
Link to Painlevé

In this section we explain the link between the anharmonic oscillator
(
1.1
)
and the second Painlevé transcendent, following the ideas first pioneered by Its and Novkshenov
[
IN86
]
and later by Masoero
[
Mas10a
,
Mas10b
]
.
The
PII
equation can be expressed as the compatibility condition of two linear systems called Lax pairs.
There are several such Lax pairs but for our purposes we will only need the Jimbo-Miwa Lax pair
[
JM81
,
FIKN06
]
, namely:

\begin{cases}\partialderivative{\Phi}{x}=A(x,t)\Phi,\\
\partialderivative{\Phi}{t}=B(x,t)\Phi,\end{cases}

(2.1)

with the coefficient functions

\displaystyle A(x,t;\alpha)=\left(x^{2}+w+\frac{t}{2}\right)\sigma_{3}+(x-u)v\sigma_{+}-\frac{2}{v}\left(xw+uw+-\alpha+\frac{1}{2}\right)\sigma_{-}

(2.2)

\displaystyle B(x,t)=\frac{x}{2}\sigma_{3}+\frac{v}{2}\sigma_{+}-\frac{w}{v}\sigma_{-}

(2.3)

where
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
is a parameter,
u=u(t),v=v(t),w=w(t)
are meromorphic
functions of
t
and

\sigma_{3}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{bmatrix},\quad\sigma_{+}=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
0&0\end{bmatrix},\quad\sigma_{-}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
1&0\end{bmatrix}.

(2.4)

The compatibility condition
\partial_{x}\partial_{t}\Phi=\partial_{t}\partial_{x}\Phi
of the system
(
2.1
) is equivalent to

\partialderivative{A}{t}-\partialderivative{B}{x}+[A,B]=0,

(2.5)

which in turn yields the following system of ODEs for
u,v,w
:

\derivative{u}{t}=u^{2}+w+\frac{t}{2},\quad\derivative{v}{t}=-uv,\quad\derivative{w}{t}=-2uw+\alpha-\frac{1}{2}.

(2.6)

Eliminating
w
from the system
u(t)
satisfies
(
PII
) with parameter
\alpha
;
similarly eliminating
u(t)

gives that
w(t)
satisfies PXXXIV
[
Inc44
,
FIKN06
]
.

Now we find local solutions of the system (
2.6
) near a pole
of the Painlevé transcent
u(t)
. This will be useful in the upcoming computations.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
t=a
be a pole of residue
+1
of the Painlevé II function
u(t)
with parameter
\alpha
.
Then near
t=a
we have the following Laurent series expansion of (
2.6
):

\displaystyle u(t)

(2.7)

Similarly, if

\displaystyle u(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{-1}{t-a}+\frac{a}{6}(t-a)-\frac{\alpha-1}{4}(t-a)^{2}+b(t-a)^{3}+\mathcal{O}{((t-a)^{3})},

(2.8)

\displaystyle w(t)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{2}-\alpha\right)(t-a)+\left(5b-\frac{a^{2}}{36}\right)(t-a)^{2}+\frac{a(2\alpha-1)}{6}(t-a)^{3}+\mathcal{O}((t-a)^{4}),

Proof.

It is well known that all poles of PII are simple and with residue

2.1
Gauge transformation

With a particular gauge transformation we can convert the matrix ODE

\partialderivative{x}\Phi=A(x,t;\alpha)\Phi
to a scalar ODE; the procedure results in adding an
apparent singularity
in the equation at the position
x=u(t)
. Thus, when the independent variable
t
tends to one of the poles of the solution
u(t)
to the Painlevé equation, the
singularity “escapes” to infinity and in the limit we obtain a
polynomial
ODE. This gives us an eigenvalue problem similar to the one of
Shapiro and Tater (
1.1
).

To implement this idea in detail, first we outline the general gauge transformation as used by

[
Mas10b
]
.
Take a traceless
2\times 2
matrix ODE system
\Phi_{x}=M(x)\Phi
, where

M(x)=\begin{bmatrix}m_{11}(x)&m_{12}(x)\\
m_{21}(x)&-m_{11}(x)\\
\end{bmatrix}.

(2.11)

We wish to turn this into a scalar ODE of the form
y^{\prime\prime}-V(x)y=0
, which we can do by
using the gauge transformation

W(x)=G(x)\Phi(x),\qquad G(x):=\begin{bmatrix}{m_{12}(x)}^{-\frac{1}{2}}&&0\\
&&\\
-\frac{m_{12}^{\prime}(x)}{2m_{12}(x)^{\frac{3}{2}}}+\frac{m_{11}(x)}{m_{12}(x)^{\frac{1}{2}}}&&m_{12}(x)^{\frac{1}{2}}\end{bmatrix}

(2.12)

We find that
W(x)
solves the matrix ODE

\derivative{x}W=\widehat{M}(x)W(x)

where

\widehat{M}(x)=G_{x}G^{-1}+GMG^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
V(x)&0\\
\end{bmatrix},

(2.13)

where the function
V(x)
is:

V(x)=m_{12}m_{21}+m_{11}^{2}+m_{11}^{\prime}-m_{11}\frac{m_{12}^{\prime}}{m_{12}}-\frac{m_{12}^{\prime\prime}}{2m_{12}}+\frac{3}{4}\left(\frac{m_{12}^{\prime}}{m_{12}}\right)^{2}.

(2.14)

Thus the matrix ODE system
W_{x}=\widehat{M}(x)W(x)

is then equivalent to the scalar ODE

y^{\prime\prime}(x)=V(x)y(x)

and
W
is the Wronskian matrix of a pair of independent solutions (whence the choice of symbol).

Let us now apply this transformation to the matrix ODE from the Jimbo-Miwa Lax pair
(
2.1
), i.e. we set
M(x)=A(x,t)
and perform the aforementioned gauge transformation.
Since the entries of
A(x,t)
depend on
t
, our potential
V
will be a function of both
x
and
t
,
namely:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} V(x,t)=&\frac{1}{\left(x-u(t)\right)^{2}}\Bigg[2\,w\left(t\right)u(t)^{4}+\left(-4\,xw\left(t\right)-2\,\alpha+1\right)u(t)^{3}\\
+&\left(w(t)^{2}+\left(2\,{x}^{2}+\,t\right)w(t)+x^{4}+tx^{2}+(6\alpha-1)x+\frac{{t}^{2}}{4}\right)u(t)^{2}\\
+&\left(-2\,xw(t)^{2}+(-2\,tx+1)w(t)-2\,x^{5}-2\,tx^{3}-\frac{1}{2}\,t^{2}x-6\,\alpha\,x^{2}+\frac{t}{2}\right)u(t)\\
+&\,x^{2}w(t)^{2}+\left(\,t{x}^{2}-x\right)w(t)+x^{6}+tx^{4}+\frac{t^{2}x^{2}}{4}+2\,\alpha\,x^{3}-\frac{tx}{2}+\frac{3}{4}\Bigg]\,.\end{aligned}

(2.15)

We are interested in the expression of this potential at a pole
t=a
of PII equation.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
t=a
be a pole with residue
\pm 1
of the
PII
solution
u(t)
with parameter
\alpha
.
Then the
\lim_{t\to a}V(x,t)
exists and we have for a pole of residue
+1

V_{\rm JM}(x;a,b,\alpha):=\lim_{t\to a}V(x,t)=x^{4}+ax^{2}+(2\alpha-1)x+\left(\frac{7a^{2}}{36}-10b\right)

(2.16)

and for a pole of residue
-1

\lim_{t\to a}V(x,t)=x^{4}+ax^{2}+(2\alpha+1)x+\left(\frac{7a^{2}}{36}+10b\right)=V_{\rm JM}(x;a,-b,\alpha+1)

(2.17)

Proof.

The result is obtained from a straightforward computation where we substitute the Laurent series expansions (
2.1
) into (
2.15
) and take the limit as
t\to a
.
The details are left as exercise (it is helpful to use a computer algebra program for this purpose).

\blacksquare

The importance of this transformation for us is the following: the gauge transformation (
2.12
)
introduces only a singularity at the zeros of
m_{1,2}(x)
, which in our case is only at
x=u(t)
, namely, the
value
of the Painlevé transcendent solution
u(t)
. The singularity is clearly only a square-root type singularity with local monodromy
-1
.
Other than this, the Stokes phenomenon of the ODE
y^{\prime\prime}(x)=V(x;t)y(x)
is unchanged and independent of
t
by construction. This is evident from the fact that the transformation of the system effected by (
2.12
) is a
left
multiplication by a simple algebraic matrix
G(x)
with at most square–root singularities around which the whole matrix has a scalar monodromy of multiplication by
-1
. However the Stokes’ phenomenon involves
right
multiplications of the solution by constant matrices, which are thus the same as for the original system.

This important observation has the consequence that as
t
approaches one of the poles of the given solution
u(t)
of the Painlevé equation, the additional singularity moves off to infinity.
Thus we have the following simple but essential statement that we formalize in the proposition below.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
u(t)
be a solution of the Painlevé II equation corresponding to a particular set of Stokes data for the ODE in (
2.1
). Let
t=a
be a pole of
u(t)
with residue
-1
and
b
the coefficient as in (
2.8
).
Then the Stokes phenomenon of the ODE

\displaystyle y^{\prime\prime}(x)=\left(x^{4}+ax^{2}+(2\alpha+1)x+\Lambda\right)y(x),\ \ \ \Lambda:=\frac{7a^{2}}{36}+10b

(2.18)

is the same as the original Stokes phenomenon of the ODE (
2.1
).

A similar statement holds for the poles with residue
+1
but we make the choice of considering only those with negative residue because
as formula (
1.10
) shows the poles with positive residue are the zero of
Y_{n-1}(t)
and the poles with negative residue are the zeros of
Y_{n}(t)
.
The complete proof of the above proposition is equivalent to the one presented in
[
Mas10b
]
Appendix B.

3
A study of quasi-polynomials

In this section we find a characterization of the (quasi)-polynomials corresponding to a repeated eigenvalue for the operator (
1.4
), namely, the Shapiro-Tater eigenvalue problem:

\displaystyle\derivative[2]{y}{x}-(x^{4}+tx^{2}+2Jx+\Lambda)y=0

(3.1)

\displaystyle y(s{\rm e}^{{k}\pi i/3})\to 0,\ \ \ s\to+\infty,\ {k=1,3,5}.

(3.2)

We will call the set
(t,J,\lambda)
for which there is a solution of the problem (
3.1
)-(
3.2
), the
Exactly Solvable
(ES) spectrum.
Our setting is different from
[
ST22
]
, where the authors considered a modified eigenvalue problem (
3.1
) with only
two
boundary conditions. In this case
only a finite portion of the spectrum can be computed explicitly, and for this reason it is a
Quasi Exactly Solvable
(QES) spectrum, the naming comes from
[
BB98
]
.

We will see below that with three boundary conditions at infinity as in (
3.2
), the whole spectrum can be characterised by the vanishing of a finite determinant, and it is therefore
Exactly Solvable
.
The first result in Proposition
3.2
shows that the boundary conditions in (
3.2
) is compatible only with
J
being a positive integer.
We then compute the Stokes phenomenon of the quasi-polynomial solutions explicitly, as shown in (
3.3
).
Finally we relate the problem to (degenerate) orthogonality for a suitable class of non-hermitean orthogonal polynomials, see Theorem
3.4
and Theorem
3.6
which are the main results of this section.

Lemma 3.1
.

The equation (
3.1
) admits quasi–polynomial solutions of the form

y(x)=p(x)e^{\theta(x;t)}\quad\mbox{where $\;\;\theta(x;t)=\frac{x^{3}}{3}+\frac{tx}{2}$},

(3.3)

with
p(x)
a polynomial of degree
n
if and only if
J=n+1
and
\lambda=\Lambda-\frac{t^{2}}{4}
is an eigenvalue of the operator

\mathcal{L}_{J}:=\derivative[2]{x}+2\left(x^{2}+\frac{t}{2}\right)\derivative{x}-2(J-1)x

(3.4)

acting on the space of polynomials of degree up to n.

Proof.

Substituting
y(x)=p(x){\rm e}^{\theta(x)}
in the ODE (
3.1
) gives an equivalent differential equation for the function
p(x)
:

\mathcal{L}_{J}(p(x))=\lambda p(x)\quad\text{ where }\quad\lambda=\Lambda-\frac{t^{2}}{4}\,,

(3.5)

and
\mathcal{L}_{J}
as in (
3.4
).
One can readily see that if
J=n+1
then the operator
\mathcal{L}_{n+1}
in (
3.5
) preserves the space of polynomials of degree at most
n
and then
\Lambda
is, by definition, an eigenvalue of (
3.1
).

Viceversa, if
p(x)
is a polynomial of degree
n
and solves (
3.5
) then one finds by inspection that the l.h.s is a polynomial of degree
n+1
whose leading coefficient is
2(n-J+1)
while the r.h.s. is a polynomial of degree
n
. Thus
J=n+1
. Then
\mathcal{L}_{n+1}
preserves the space of polynomials of degree
n
and
\Lambda
(and the corresponding
\lambda
as per (
3.5
)) is an eigenvalue of the corresponding finite dimensional operator.
\blacksquare

Proposition 3.2
.

The eigenvalue problem (

Proof.

The equation (
3.1
) can be written as a first order system for the Wronkstian matrix

\mathcal{W}(x)
:

\displaystyle\derivative{\mathcal{W}}{x}=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
x^{4}+tx^{2}+2Jx+\Lambda&0\end{bmatrix}\mathcal{W}(x),\ \ \Lambda=\lambda+\frac{t^{2}}{4}.

(3.6)

This equation has a singularity at infinity with Poincaré rank
3
.
Following the ordinary asymptotic analysis
[
Wa87
]
we see that we have formal-series solutions of the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{\text{form}}(x)\sim

\displaystyle x^{-\sigma_{3}}(\mathbb{I}-\sigma_{+}+\sigma_{-})\left(\mathbb{I}+\mathcal{O}(x^{-1})\right)x^{J\sigma_{3}}e^{\theta(x;a)\sigma_{3}},\quad\theta(x;t)=\frac{x^{3}}{3}+\frac{tx}{2}

(3.7)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}x^{J-1}+\mathcal{O}(x^{J-2})&-x^{-J-1}+\mathcal{O}(x^{-J-2})\\
x^{J+1}+\mathcal{O}(x^{J})&x^{-J+1}+\mathcal{O}(x^{-J})\end{bmatrix}{\rm e}^{\theta(x;t)\sigma_{3}},\quad x\to\infty.

(3.8)

We define the Stokes sectors as shown in Figure
3
, i.e.
\mathcal{S}_{k}
is the sector of opening
\pi/3
centered
around the rays of argument
\pi/6+(k-1)\pi/3

\mathcal{S}_{k}=\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\absolutevalue{\arg(z)- \frac{\pi}{6}{ -(k-1)}\frac{\pi}{3}}<\frac{\pi}{6}\right\},\quad k=0,1,2,3,4,5.

(3.9)

Each open sector
\Omega_{k}=\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\absolutevalue{\arg(z)- \frac{\pi}{6}{-(k-1)}\frac{\pi}{3}}<\frac{\pi}{6}+\delta\right\}

.
Then there exists a unique solution

\mathcal{W}^{(k)}(x)\simeq\mathcal{W}_{\text{form}}(x),\quad x\in\Omega_{k},\;\;k=0,\dots,5.

Since
\mathcal{W}^{(k)}(x)

\mathcal{W}^{(k+1)}(x)
have the same asymptotic expansion in
\Omega_{k}\cap\Omega_{k+1}

\mathbb{S}_{k}
(called

), such that

\mathcal{W}^{(k+1)}(x)=\mathcal{W}^{(k)}(x)\mathbb{S}_{k},\quad x\in\Omega_{k}\cap\Omega_{k+1},\quad k=0,1,\dots,5,

\mathbb{S}_{2k}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\

\mathcal{W}^{(6)}(x)=\mathcal{W}^{(0)}(x)\mathbb{S}_{0}\mathbb{S}_{1}\dots\mathbb{S}_{5},\quad\mathcal{W}^{(6)}(x)=\mathcal{W}^{(0)}(x)e^{2\pi iJ\sigma_{3}}

\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\

(3.10)

The first six matrices are the Stokes matrices associated with the directions

The boundary conditions (
3.2

But then the matrix equation (

\displaystyle s_{1}+s_{3}+s_{5}=0

To show that

Given that now the Stokes matrices are all upper triangular, the first column of the solution is an entire function which is asymptotic to the first column of the formal-series solution (

) along all directions.
But then the asymptotic (

) implies that the
(1,1)

\blacksquare

One can also prove the converse of this result, but to do so we first need the following lemma.

Lemma 3.5
.

Consider the second order ODE in the complex plane

\derivative[2]{y}{x}-V(x)y=0.

(3.32)

Suppose that
x=x_{*}
is a (possible) singularity of the potential
V(x)
where it has a pole of order
at most
2
. Additionally, assume that
x=x^{*}
is an apparent singularity ( namely two linearly independent
solutions of the ODE (
3.32
) are analytic at
x=x^{*}
).
Then,
V(x)
is analytic at
x=x^{*}
.

Proof.

We argue by contradiction and consider separately the cases when
V(x)
has a double pole and when
V(x)
has a simple pole at
x=x^{*}
. In both cases we make use of the indicial equation and its basic properties, which can be found in more detail in
[
Olv74
]
.

•

Case 1: Simple pole.

Suppose that near the singular point
x=x_{*}
the potential is of the form

V(x)=\frac{a}{\zeta}+b+\mathcal{O}(\zeta)

(3.33)

with
a\neq 0
and
\zeta=x-x_{*}
.
The indicial equation of the ODE becomes

d(d-1)=0\,.

(3.34)

It has two solutions
d_{1}=1
and
d_{2}=0
differing by a non-zero integer
d_{1}-d_{2}=1
, meaning that that there are two linearly independent solutions
y_{1}(x),y_{2}(x)
such that

\displaystyle y_{1}(x)=\mathcal{O}(\zeta^{d_{1}}),

(3.35)

\displaystyle y_{2}(x)=\mathcal{O}(\zeta^{d_{2}})

(3.36)

as
x\to x_{*}

[
Olv74
, p.151]
.
But a simple computation shows that

y_{2}(x)=1+\mathcal{O}(\zeta)

cannot solve the differential equation (
3.32
) if
V(x)
has a simple pole at
x=x_{*}
. This is a contradiction.

•

Case 2: Double pole
.
Suppose that near the singular point
x=x_{*}
the potential has the shape

V(x)=\frac{a}{\zeta^{2}}+\frac{b}{\zeta}+\mathcal{O}(1)

(3.37)

with
a\neq 0
and again we denote
\zeta=x-x_{*}
.
The indicial equation now gives

\displaystyle a=

\displaystyle d(d-1)\ .

(3.38)

Let
d_{1},d_{2}
be the two solutions of the indicial equation (
3.38
).
By the assumption that the solutions to the ODE are all analytic, we must have that

d_{1}
and
d_{2}
must be non-negative integer and not equal to each other (if
d_{1}=d_{2}
then
one of the solutions has a logarithmic singularity, see
[
Olv74
]
).
Note that
d_{1}=d_{2}
if and only if
a=-1/4
, in which case
d_{1}=d_{2}=1/2
, so we may simply
assume that
d_{1},d_{2}
are non-negative integers.
Rewriting the equation
d_{1}(d_{1}-1)=d_{2}(d_{2}-1)
as

(d_{1}-d_{2})(d_{1}+d_{2}-1)=0

(3.39)

we see that the only non-negative integer solutions are
d_{1}=0
and
d_{2}=1
and vice versa.
In either case from the indicial equation we find that
a=0
, which is a contradiction.
Therefore
V(x)
cannot have a double pole.
\blacksquare

We are ready to prove the converse of Theorem
3.4
, namely
that these degenerate orthogonal polynomials satisfy the Shapiro-Tater ODE (
3.1
).

Theorem 3.6
.

Suppose that
p_{n}(x)
is a
1
-degenerate orthogonal polynomial with respect to the weight

w(x)={\rm e}^{2\theta(x;t)}
on the weighted contour
\Gamma:=\varkappa\gamma+\tilde{\varkappa}\tilde{\gamma}
for
(\varkappa,\tilde{\varkappa})\neq(0,0)
, in the notations of Theorem
3.4
.
Then
F(x)=p_{n}(x){\rm e}^{\theta(x;t)}
is a quasi-polynomial solution of
the boundary problem (
3.1
)-(
3.2
) with
J=n+1
.

Proof.

Suppose that
p_{n}(x)
is a degenerate orthogonal polynomial with respect to the weight

w(x)={\rm e}^{\theta(x;t)}
on the (weighted) contour
\Gamma
, i.e.

\int_{\Gamma}p_{n}(\zeta)z^{k}{\rm e}^{2\theta(\zeta;t)}\differential\zeta=0\quad k=0,1,\cdots,n.

(3.40)

Define the functions

\displaystyle F(x)

\displaystyle:=p_{n}(x){\rm e}^{\theta(x;t)}

(3.41)

\displaystyle G(x)

\displaystyle:=\frac{{\rm e}^{-\theta(x;t)}}{2\pi i}\int_{\Gamma}\frac{p_{n}(\zeta){\rm e}^{2\theta(\zeta;t)}\differential\zeta}{\zeta-x}.

(3.42)

We claim that the Wronskian
W=FG^{\prime}-F^{\prime}G
is constant.
The degenerate orthogonality condition implies that

G(x)\simeq\left(h_{n}x^{-n-2}+\mathcal{O}(x^{-n-3})\right){\rm e}^{-\theta(x;t)},\quad x\to\infty

(3.43)

where the leading factor is

h_{n}:=-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\Gamma}p_{n}(\zeta){\rm e}^{2\theta(\zeta;t)}\zeta^{n+1}\differential\zeta.

(3.44)

Furthermore, by differentiating (denoting
{}^{\prime}=\derivative{x}
) we find

\displaystyle G^{\prime}(x)=

\displaystyle-\theta^{\prime}(x)G(x)+\frac{{\rm e}^{-\theta(x;t)}}{2\pi i}\int_{\Gamma}\frac{p_{n}(\zeta){\rm e}^{2\theta(\zeta;t)}}{(\zeta-x)^{2}}\differential\zeta

(3.45)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\theta^{\prime}(x)G(x)+\frac{1}{x^{2}}\frac{{\rm e}^{-\theta(x;t)}}{2\pi i}\int_{\Gamma}p_{n}(\zeta){\rm e}^{2\theta(\zeta;t)}\left(1+2\frac{\zeta}{x}+3\frac{\zeta^{2}}{x^{2}}+\dots\right)\differential\zeta

(3.46)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\rm e}^{-\theta(x;t)}\left(-h_{n}x^{-n}+\mathcal{O}(x^{-n-1})\right)+\frac{1}{x^{2}}\frac{{\rm e}^{-\theta(x;t)}}{2\pi i}\mathcal{O}(x^{-n-1})

(3.47)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\rm e}^{-\theta(x;t)}\left(-h_{n}x^{-n}+\mathcal{O}(x^{-n-1})\right).

(3.48)

Additionally, since
p_{n}(x)
is a monic polynomial,
we have that
F(x)\sim x^{n}{\rm e}^{\theta(x;t)}
and
F^{\prime}(x)\sim x^{n+2}{\rm e}^{\theta(x;t)}
, which means that the Wronskian is bounded at infinity, i.e.
W(x)=F^{\prime}(x)G(x)-F(x)G^{\prime}(x)=-2h_{n}+\mathcal{O}(x^{-1})
as
x\to\infty
.

The Wronskian
W(x)
has no jump-discontinuities since the boundary values
W_{\pm}(x)
for
x\in\gamma,\widetilde{\gamma}
satisfied

W_{+}=F^{\prime}G_{+}-FG_{+}^{\prime}=F^{\prime}(s_{k}F+G_{-})-F(s_{k}F^{\prime}+G_{-}^{\prime})=F^{\prime}G_{-}-FG_{-}^{\prime}=W_{-}.

(3.49)

Furthermore, since
W
is built from locally analytic functions, it also follows that
W(x)
has no poles.
Thus
W
is an entire function; furthermore it is bounded at infinity since, in each sector,
F^{\prime}G,FG^{\prime}
are bounded. By the Liouville theorem we conclude that
W(x)
must be a constant, i.e.

W(x)=F^{\prime}(x)G(x)-F(x)G^{\prime}(x)\equiv-2h_{n}.

(3.50)

Differentiating this equation gives that
F^{\prime\prime}/F\equiv G^{\prime\prime}/G
. Let us denote by
V(x)
this ratio; then both
F
and
G
satisfy a 2nd order linear ODE of the form:

y^{\prime\prime}-V(x)y=0\quad\text{ with potential }V(x):=\frac{F^{\prime\prime}(x)}{F(x)}.

(3.51)

We can rewrite the potential using the defining expression
F=p_{n}{\rm e}^{\theta}
in terms of the polynomial
p_{n}
, which gives us:

V(x)=\theta^{\prime\prime}(x)+(\theta^{\prime}(x))^{2}+2\theta^{\prime}(x)\frac{p_{n}^{\prime}(x)}{p_{n}(x)}+\frac{p^{\prime\prime}_{n}(x)}{p_{n}(x)}.

(3.52)

Let
c
be one of the zeros of
p_{n}
of multiplicity
d
then we can write
p_{n}(x)=(x-c)^{d}h(x)
and then we expand (
3.52
)
near
x=c
:

V(x)=\frac{d(d-1)}{(x-c)^{2}}+\frac{2d}{x-c}\left(\frac{h^{\prime}(c)}{h(c)}+\theta^{\prime}(c)\right)+\mathcal{O}(1),\ \ \ x\to c.

(3.53)

This shows that
V(x)
may have at most a second-order pole: since now
F(x)
and
G(x)
are both analytic near
x=c
and
both satisfy the ODE
y^{\prime\prime}-V(x)y=0
we deduce that all the singularities of the ODE are apparent.
We can therefore, apply Lemma
3.5
, from which it follows that
V(x)=F^{\prime\prime}/F
is entire.
We conclude that
d=1
, namely all the zeros

x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
of the polynomial
p_{n}(x)
are simple and obtain

V(x)=\theta^{\prime\prime}(x)+(\theta^{\prime}(x))^{2}+2nx+2\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{x-x_{j}}\left[\theta^{\prime}(x_{j})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
k\neq j\end{subarray}}^{n}\frac{1}{x_{j}-x_{k}}\right]

(3.54)

which implies that

\theta^{\prime}(x_{j})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
k\neq j\end{subarray}}^{n}\frac{1}{x_{k}-x_{j}},\quad j=1,\dots,n.

(3.55)

This gives the potential

V(x)=x^{4}+tx^{2}+2x(n+1)+\frac{t^{2}}{4}+2\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}

(3.56)

which coincides with the potential in (
3.1
) with
\Lambda=\frac{t^{2}}{4}+2\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}

\blacksquare

The proof of the above theorem shows that

Recall that the characteristic polynomial

in (

In this section we prove the following crucial result.

Theorem 3.9
.

The following statements are equivalent:

1.

The value of
t\in\mathbb{C}
is such that the Exactly Solvable spectrum of (
3.1
)-(
3.2
) has a repeated eigenvalue;

2.

The value
t\in\mathbb{C}
is such that there exist

and

so that for

the problem (
3.1
), (

of (

)-(

) that satisfies

\int_{\gamma}\left(p_{n}(x)e^{\theta(x;{t})}\right)^{2}\differential x=0,\;\;\;\int_{\widetilde{\gamma}}\left(p_{n}(x)e^{\theta(x;{t})}\right)^{2}\differential x=0

(3.57)

where
\gamma
and
\widetilde{\gamma}
are defined as in Theorem

(contours from
\infty_{1}
to
\infty_{3}
and
\infty_{3}
to
\infty_{5}
respectively, see Fig.

).

Proof.

[
(1.)\Rightarrow(2.)
].
If
y(x)
is a solution of the eigenvalue problem (
3.1
) then

must be a quasipolynomial according to Proposition
3.2
, and we must have that

and
\Lambda
(

) is an eigenvalue.

If this eigenvalue is repeated then the derivative of the characteristic polynomial
C_{n}(t,\lambda)
in (
1.6
) must also vanish.

[
(2.)\Rightarrow(1.)
].
This is immediate consequence of Lemma
3.1
together with the fact that the derivative of a polynomial vanish at each root of multiplicity higher than one.

[
(2.)\Rightarrow(3.)
].

The condition
(2.)
means the eigenvalue has algebraic multiplicity (at least)
2
and so we can consider the generalized eigenvector.
Let us remind the reader here that if
v
is the eigenvector of the matrix
M_{n}(t)
in (

) with eigenvalue
\lambda
, then the generalized eigenvector

satisfies the equation
(M-\lambda I)w=v
. It follows that the generalized eigenvector equation for a
polynomial

(of degree
\leq n
) takes the form of the following differential equation for

:

\derivative[2]{r(x)}{x}+2\left(x^{2}+\frac{t}{2}\right)\derivative{r(x)}{x}-(2(J-1)x+\lambda)r(x)=p_{n}(x).

(3.58)

The fact that the above non-homogeneous equation admits a

is essential to bear in mind: such solution is not unique since we can add to it an arbitrary multiple of

.
The associated homogeneous differential equation has two solutions, one of which is

p_{n}(x)
(a polynomial of degree
n=J-1
) and the other one is obtained from (
3.12
) and can be written as follows:

q_{k}(x)=p_{n}(x)\int_{\infty_{k}}^{x}F(\zeta)^{-2}\differential\zeta,\ \ \ F(x):=p_{n}(x){\rm e}^{\theta(x;{t})},

where we can choose any

for the basepoint of integration.
Consequently, a particular solution
r_{0}(z)
of (

) is found by the standard “variation of parameters” as follows:

r_{0}(x):=p_{n}(x)\underbrace{\int_{\infty_{0}}^{x}F(\zeta)^{-2}\left(\int_{\infty_{1}}^{\zeta}F(s)^{2}\differential s\right)\differential\zeta}_{H(x)}.

(3.59)

Before proceeding with the proof we claim and prove that
H(x)
is bounded by a constant as

.
To see this it is sufficient to show that the inner integral in the defintion of

tends to zero as
\mathcal{O}(\zeta^{2n-2}){\rm e}^{2\theta(\zeta;t)}
(recall that the real part of
\theta
goes to

along the direction
\infty_{1}
and hence this is exponentially small).
To prove this claim we write
p_{n}^{2}(s)=2Q_{2n-2}(s)\theta^{\prime}(s)+R(s)
, with
Q_{2n-2}
the quotient (of degree
2n-2
and
R
the remainder (of degree
1
) polynomials of division by

. Then integrating by parts we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\infty_{1}}^{\zeta}F(s)^{2}{\mathrm{d}}s=Q_{2n-2}(\zeta){\rm e}^{2\theta(\zeta)}+\int_{\infty_{1}}^{\zeta}R(s){\rm e}^{2\theta(s)}\differential s.

(3.60)

To estimate the last integral we “force” again an integration by parts:

\displaystyle\int_{\infty_{1}}^{\zeta}R(s){\rm e}^{2\theta(s)}\differential s=

\displaystyle\frac{R(\zeta)}{2\theta^{\prime}(\zeta)}{\rm e}^{2\theta(\zeta)}-\int_{\infty_{1}}^{\zeta}\left(\frac{R(s)}{2\theta^{\prime}(s)}\right)^{\prime}{\rm e}^{2\theta(s)}\differential s.

(3.61)

In the remaining integral the prefactor to the exponential is
\mathcal{O}(s^{-2})
and thus the integral is easily estimated to be
\mathcal{O}(1){\rm e}^{2\theta}
.

With the claim proved, let us proceed: we know that (
3.58
) has by assumption a polynomial solution and we are going to show now that

itself is such a polynomial.
Indeed, the general solution of (
3.58
) is obtained by adding an arbitrary linear combination of

to

in (
3.59
). We then must show that here are constants

such that

is a polynomial of degree at most
n
; clearly here only the value of
B
is relevant (since
p_{n}
is already a polynomial). Thus the issue boils down to whether there is a value of
B
for which
r_{0}(x)+Bq_{0}(x)
is a polynomial.
We first observe that the only possible value for
B
must be zero.
Indeed consider the asymptotic behaviour for
x\to\infty_{1}
. The integral
H(x)
is bounded by a constant as
x\to\infty_{1}
.
However,
q_{0}(x)
has a dominant exponential growth in this direction and hence the expression
r_{0}(x)+Bq_{0}(x)
has polynomial growth in this direction only if

.

We thus conclude that since (
3.58
) has a (pencil of) polynomial solution(s), this must be given by
r_{0}(x)+Ap_{n}(x)
so that

must itself be a polynomial.

We now establish (
3.57
). To this end we consider the behaviour of
r_{0}(x)
near
\infty_{3}
and
\infty_{5}
. We can write, for example for
\infty_{3}

\displaystyle r_{0}(x)

\displaystyle=p_{n}(x)\int_{\infty_{0}}^{x}F(\zeta)^{-2}\left(\int_{\infty_{1}}^{\infty_{3}}F(s)^{2}\differential s+\int_{\infty_{3}}^{\zeta}F(s)^{2}\differential s\right)\differential\zeta

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{\infty_{1}}^{\infty_{3}}F(s)^{2}\differential s\right)q_{0}(x)+p_{n}(x)\int_{\infty_{0}}^{x}F(\zeta)^{-2}\left(\int_{\infty_{3}}^{\zeta}F(s)^{2}\differential s\right)\differential\zeta

(3.62)

The second term above is polynomially bounded near
\infty_{3}
, by the same argument used to show that
H
is bounded near
\infty_{1}
. But since
q_{0}
is exponentially dominant also near
\infty_{1}
we deduce the condition
\int_{\infty_{1}}^{\infty_{3}}F(s)^{2}\differential s=0
. One similarly deduces
\int_{\infty_{5}}^{\infty_{3}}F(s)^{2}\differential s=0
, which establishes (
3.57
).

[
(3.)\Rightarrow(2.)
].

Consider the expression (
3.59
). It is easy to see directly that it satifies the generalized eigenvector equation (
3.58
); we must only verify that the conditions (
3.57
) guarantee that
r_{0}(z)
is a polynomial. But this follows again from the Liouville theorem and using (
3.3
).

\blacksquare

Remark 3.10
.

The Theorem
3.9
seems at first sight nothing short of a miracle; indeed once we fix
J=n+1\in\mathbb{N}
, then the ODE (
3.1
) has only two continuous parameters
t,\lambda
.

However the multiple eigenvalue condition apparently involves now three equations which are:

(i) the existence of a quasi-polynomial solution which determines
\lambda
as a function of
t
;

(ii) the two equations (
3.57
) for the parameter
t
.

However, the system is actually not overdetermined because of the following reasoning: if, for given
J=n+1\in\mathbb{N}
the pair
(t,\Lambda)
is in the ES spectrum, then we have shown in Theorem
3.4
that
p_{n}(x)
is a
degenerate
orthogonal polynomial, namely,

\displaystyle\varkappa\int_{\gamma}p_{n}^{2}(x){\rm e}^{2\theta(x;t)}{\mathrm{d}}x+\widetilde{\varkappa}\int_{\widetilde{\gamma}}p_{n}^{2}(x){\rm e}^{2\theta(x;t)}{\mathrm{d}}x=0,

(3.63)

with
\gamma,\widetilde{\gamma},\varkappa,\widetilde{\varkappa}
defined in the same theorem.
The coefficients
\varkappa,\widetilde{\varkappa}
cannot be both vanishing for otherwise the Stokes phenomenon of the ODE would be trivial (which is not possible). Then the two equations (
3.57
) yield only one additional constraint.

The following corollary is also a nice property, although it will not be used in the rest.

Corollary 3.11
.

Suppose that
y(x)=p_{n}(x)^{\theta(x;t)}
is a solution of the boundary problem (
3.1
) and hence
t
,
J=n+1
and
\Lambda
are in the ES spectrum. Suppose also that
\Lambda=\lambda+\frac{t^{2}}{4}
is a repeated eigenvalue.
Then the antiderivative of
y^{2}(x)
is also a quasipolynomial,

\displaystyle\int^{x}p_{n}(\zeta)^{2}{\rm e}^{2\theta(\zeta;t)}{\mathrm{d}}\zeta=q_{2n+2}(x){\rm e}^{2\theta(x;t)}.

(3.64)

Proof.

Take the integration basepoint from
\infty_{1}
and consider the function

\displaystyle K(x)=\int_{\infty_{1}}^{x}p_{n}(z)^{2}{\rm e}^{2\theta(z;t)}{\mathrm{d}}z

(3.65)

Standard asymptotic analysis shows that
K(x)
is of the form
\mathcal{O}(x^{2n+2}){\rm e}^{2\theta(x;t)}
along the (dominant) direction towards
\infty_{0},\infty_{2},
and
\infty_{4}
.

Moreover towards
\infty_{1}
we have

\displaystyle q(x):=K(x){\rm e}^{-2\theta(x;t)}=\mathcal{O}(x^{2n+2}).

(3.66)

Thus the function
q(x)=K(x){\rm e}^{-2\theta(x;t)}
is polynomially bounded along the sectors containing
\infty_{j},

j\in\{0,1,2,4\}
. If we can show that it is also polynomially bounded in the sectors containing
\infty_{3}
and
\infty_{5}
then we conclude, by the Liouville’s theorem, that it is a polynomial.

Consider
x
tending to infinity along the sector that contains
\infty_{3}
; we have

\displaystyle q(x)

\displaystyle={\rm e}^{-2\theta(x;t)}\int_{\infty_{1}}^{x}p_{n}(\zeta)^{2}{\rm e}^{2\theta(\zeta;t)}{\mathrm{d}}\zeta=

\displaystyle={\rm e}^{-2\theta(x;t)}\int_{\infty_{3}}^{x}p_{n}(\zeta)^{2}{\rm e}^{2\theta(\zeta;t)}{\mathrm{d}}\zeta+{\rm e}^{-2\theta(x;t)}\int_{\infty_{1}}^{\infty_{3}}p_{n}(\zeta)^{2}{\rm e}^{2\theta(\zeta;t)}{\mathrm{d}}\zeta.

(3.67)

The second term above is just a multiple of
{\rm e}^{-2\theta(x;t)}
which is not polynomially bounded near
\infty_{2j+1}
. However the coefficient is precisely one of the integrals in (
3.57
) which has been proved to vanish. Thus
q(x)
is also polynomially bounded near
\infty_{3}
. Similarly we can prove that it is polynomially bounded at
\infty_{5}
and the proof is thus complete.
\blacksquare

\displaystyle\derivative[2]{y}{z}-\left(n+1\right)^{2}Q(z;{s},E)y=0,\quad\text{for the Shapiro-Tater case;}

4.1
Schrödinger, Riccati and WKB

Keeping with the tradition we will denote by
\hbar^{-1}
the large parameter in either (

\hbar=(n+1)^{-1}

Consider the following Schrödinger equation with small parameter
\hbar

(

\derivative[2]{y}{z}-\hbar^{-2}Q(z;{s},E)y=0.

(4.6)

z
We now explain how to construct the

of (

The formal series ansatz

y(z)=\exp\left(\int^{z}S(\zeta,\hbar)\differential\zeta\right),

S(z,\hbar):=h^{-1}S_{-1}(z)+\sum_{k\geq 0}\hbar^{k}S_{k}(z)

S^{2}(z)+\derivative{S(z)}{z}=\hbar^{-2}Q(z).

(4.9)

Comparing each power of
\hbar
we get a recursive relation for the coefficients

S_{k}(z)
of the formal series:

\displaystyle S_{-1}(z)^{2}=

\displaystyle\quad Q(z),

(4.10)

\displaystyle 2S_{-1}S_{k+1}=

\displaystyle-\derivative{S_k}{z}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n+m=k\\
n,m\geq 0\end{subarray}}S_{n}S_{m}\quad(k\geq-1).

(4.11)

The first three coefficients
S_{k}(z)
are:

\displaystyle S_{-1}(z)=\sqrt{Q(z)},\qquad S_{0}(z)=-\frac{1}{4}\frac{Q^{\prime}(z)}{Q(z)},\qquad S_{1}(z)=\frac{4Q(z)Q(z)^{\prime\prime}-5(Q(z)^{\prime})^{2}}{32Q(z)^{\frac{5}{2}}}.

(4.12)

The choice of sign for
S_{-1}(z)=\pm\sqrt{Q(z)}
gives two
solutions of the Riccati equation (

), denoted
S_{+},S_{-}
respectively; if we change sign in

then all the odd
S_{2k+1}
change sign while the even
S_{2k}
remain unchanged.
These correspond to the two linearly independent solutions of (
4.6
).
We define the odd and even parts of

:

\displaystyle S_{\rm{odd}}(z;\hbar):=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\big(S_{+}(z,\hbar)-S_{-}(z,\hbar)\big)

(4.13)

\displaystyle S_{\rm{even}}(z;\hbar):=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\big(S_{+}(z,\hbar)+S_{-}(z,\hbar)\big)

(4.14)

Since our potential
Q(z)
is independent of
\hbar
, it follows that
S_{\rm{odd}}
only
contains odd powers of
\hbar
, namely:

\displaystyle S_{\rm{odd}}(z;\hbar)=\frac{1}{\hbar}\sqrt{Q(z)}+\hbar S_{1}(z)+\hbar^{3}S_{3}(z)+\dots.

(4.15)

We will only care about
S_{\rm{odd}}
since it can be shown
[
KT05
]

that

can be written in terms of
S_{\rm{odd}}
as:

S_{\rm even}(z,\hbar)=-\frac{1}{2}\derivative{x}\log S_{\rm{odd}}(z,\hbar).

(4.16)

Another important fact is the following:
the differential
S_{\rm{odd}}(z){\mathrm{d}}z
has a pole at

which comes solely from the term
S_{-1}(z)=\sqrt{Q}(z)
; namely,
S_{2k+1}(z)=\mathcal{O}(z^{-2})
as

. This property can also be shown inductively.

These facts motivate the following definition. As is customary in exact WKB theory, we will refer to the zeroes
\mathrm{T}=\{\tau_{0},\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\tau_{3}\}
of

as

.

Definition 4.1

(WKB solutions)
.

The
WKB solutions
to (
4.6
)
are formal power series in
\hbar

S_{\rm{odd}}
in (

).
We give two different normalizations that will be used throughout this paper.

•

Near a turning point

\tau

\psi^{(\tau)}_{\pm}(z,\hbar):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{S_{\rm{odd}}(z,\hbar)}}\exp\left(\pm\int^{z}_{\tau}S_{\rm{odd}}(\zeta,\hbar)\differential\zeta\right)

•

Near infinity
we define the

to be:

\psi_{\pm}^{(\infty)}(z,\hbar):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{S_{\rm{odd}}(z,\hbar)}}\exp\Big( \pm R(z;\hbar) \Big)

\displaystyle R(z;\hbar):=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\hbar}\lim_{p\to\infty}\left[\int_{p}^{z}\sqrt{Q(\zeta)}\differential\zeta-\left(\frac{p^{3}}{3}+\frac{s}{2}p+\log p\right)\right]+\sum_{j\geq 0}\hbar^{2j+1}\int_{\infty}^{z}S_{2j+1}(\zeta){\mathrm{d}}\zeta

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\hbar}\left(\frac{z^{3}}{3}+\frac{s}{2}z+\log(z)\right)+\mathcal{O}(z^{-1})\mathcal{O}(\hbar),\quad(z\to\infty),

that does not have a constant term in the expansion as

.

Remark 4.1
.

The integral in the exponent of

is to be understood term-wise in each coefficient of the powers of
\hbar
.
Additionally,

is multivalued on

with branch points at the zeros

of

. Therefore the integral should be considered
on the elliptic compact Riemann surface

obtained from the affine curve

\Sigma=\Big\{(w,z)\in\mathbb{C}^{2}:w^{2}=Q(z;s,E)\Big\}

by adding two points

at infinity.
The projection
\pi:\overline{\Sigma}\mapsto\overline{\mathbb{C}}
from the Riemann surface
\overline{\Sigma}
to the extended complex plane
\overline{\mathbb{C}}
,
maps
(w,z)\mapsto z
. The projection
\pi
realizes
\overline{\Sigma}

as a double cover of
\overline{\mathbb{C}}
ramified at the zeros of

(turning points). The pre-image of any point

are the two points

on the two sheets of the Riemann surface where the numbering of the sheets is such that

belongs to the first sheet.
Choosing branch cuts and the first sheet of the Riemann surface, we can talk about the integral from a turning point
\tau
to

by defining:

\int_{\tau}^{z}S_{\rm{odd}}(\zeta,\hbar)\differential\zeta:=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\gamma(z)}S_{\rm{odd}}(\zeta,\hbar)\differential\zeta

where the contour
\gamma(z)
lives in the Riemann surface

and goes from

to

,

\widehat{z}

.
This integral is a well defined convergent integral.
Finally, it can be shown by induction that all the correction terms

For more details see
[

]
.

It is useful to relate the periods of
S_{1}
to the periods of

Proposition 4.2
.

If

\displaystyle\oint_{\gamma}S_{1}(z)\differential z=\left(-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial{s}\partial E}-\frac{s}{6}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial E^{2}}\right)I({s},E)

Using (

\displaystyle\partial_{E}\sqrt{Q(z;{s},E)}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{Q(z;{s},E)}}.

Now, we can write (we drop the indication of the dependence on

\displaystyle S_{1}=\frac{1}{48}\frac{Q^{\prime\prime}}{Q^{\frac{3}{2}}}-\frac{5}{24}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{Q}}\right)^{\prime\prime}.

Contribution of the integrals near the turning points

In order to estimate integrals

near turning points we need, as a model, to estimate the corresponding integrals for the solutions of the normal form of the ODE near a simple turning point. Namely, we need to estimate integrals involving squares of solutions of the Airy equation.
This simple lemma will be expedient.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
f=f(x),g=g(x)
be two arbitrary solutions of
y(x)^{\prime\prime}-xy(x)=0
.
There are polynomials
A_{n},B_{n},C_{n}\in\mathbb{C}[x]
of degrees
n+1,n,n-1
, respectively, such that

\displaystyle x^{n}f^{2}(x)=\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}x}\left(A_{n}(x)f(x)^{2}-B_{n}(x)(f^{\prime}(x))^{2}+C_{n}(x)f(x)f^{\prime}(x)\right).

(A.1)

\displaystyle x^{n}f\,g=\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}x}\left(A_{n}\,f\,g-B_{n}\,f^{\prime}g^{\prime}+C_{n}\frac{fg^{\prime}+gf^{\prime}}{2}\right).

(A.2)

They are related to each other by the following equations:

\displaystyle A_{n}(x)=xB_{n}(x)-\frac{1}{2}B_{n}^{\prime\prime}(x),\qquad C_{n}(x)=B_{n}^{\prime}(x),\qquad B_{n}(x)+2xB_{n}^{\prime}(x)-\frac{1}{2}B_{n}^{\prime\prime\prime}(x)=x^{n}.

(A.3)

The first few are:

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{cc|c|c}n&A_{n}&B_{n}\\
\hline\cr 0&x&1\\
\hline\cr 1&\frac{x^{2}}{3}&\frac{x}{3}\\
\hline\cr 2&\frac{x^{3}}{5}-\frac{1}{5}&\frac{x^{2}}{5}\\
\hline\cr 3&\frac{x^{4}}{7}&\frac{x^{3}}{7}-\frac{3}{7}\\
\hline\cr 4&\frac{x^{2}(x^{3}-2)}{9}&\frac{x(x^{3}+4)}{9}\end{array}.

The leading coefficients of
A_{n},B_{n}
are

\displaystyle A_{n}(x)=\frac{x^{n+1}}{2n+1}+\mathcal{O}(x^{n}),\ \ \ B_{n}=\frac{x^{n}}{2n+1}+\mathcal{O}(x^{n-1}).

(A.10)

Proof.

It suffices to prove the first formula since the second is obtained by polarization.
Let
A,B,C
be polynomials of degrees at most
n+1,n,n-1
respectively and consider the Anzatz

\displaystyle J=Af^{2}-B(f^{\prime})^{2}+Cff^{\prime}.

(A.11)

Then, using the ODE, we get

\displaystyle J^{\prime}=\big(A^{\prime}+xC\big)f^{2}+\big(2A-2xB+C^{\prime}\big)ff^{\prime}+\big(C-B^{\prime}\big)(f^{\prime})^{2}.

(A.12)

Thus we seek polynomials such that

\displaystyle A^{\prime}+xC=x^{n},\ \ \ \ C=B^{\prime},\ \ \ 2A-2xB+C^{\prime}=0

(A.13)

\displaystyle A^{\prime}+xB^{\prime}=x^{n},\ \ \ \ C=B^{\prime},\ \ \ 2A-2xB+B^{\prime\prime}=0

(A.14)

\displaystyle A=xB-\frac{1}{2}B^{\prime\prime},\ \ C=B^{\prime},\ \ \ B+2xB^{\prime}-\frac{1}{2}B^{\prime\prime\prime}=x^{n}.

(A.15)

The latter equation for

has a unique polynomial solution obtained by inverting the (finite-dimensional) linear operator on the space of polynomials of degree
\leq n

A.1

\blacksquare
