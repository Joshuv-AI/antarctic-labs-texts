1.
Introduction

1.1.
Background and motivation

The
Riemann zeta function
is a central
object of study in number theory. The zeta function is defined in the
half-plane
\{s\in\mathbb{C}:\sigma\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\Re(s)>1\}
by the equivalent formulas

\zeta(s)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\sum_{n\geqslant 1}n^{-s}=\prod_{p\text{~prime}}(1-p^{-s})^{-1}.

Riemann
[
10
]
showed
that
\zeta(s)
continues analytically to a meromorphic
function in the whole complex plane (the only singularity is a
simple pole at
s=1
), and it
satisfies a functional equation relating its values at

s
and
1-s
.
1
1

1

There are many excellent accounts of the
theory of the Riemann zeta function; we refer the reader to
Titchmarsh
[
11
]
and to Borwein
et al

[
2
]

for essential background.

Because the distribution of prime numbers is determined by the
location of the nontrivial zeros of
\zeta(s)
, the study of such zeros
is of paramount importance in prime number theory. The

Riemann Hypothesis
(RH) asserts that
if
\rho=\beta+i\gamma
is a zero of
\zeta(s)
such that
\beta>0
,
then
\beta=\frac{1}{2}
. If RH is true, then one obtains as a consequence
an optimal estimate for the counting function
\pi(x)

of the primes
p\leqslant x
. However, despite extraordinary advances towards RH by
Hardy, Selberg, Levinson, Conrey, and others, the Riemann Hypothesis
still remains far from settled.
In the words of Selberg, “…there have been very few attempts at proving the Riemann hypothesis, because, simply, no one has ever had any really good idea for how to go about it.”

The Riemann Hypothesis provides a complete description of the horizontal
distribution of the zeros of
\zeta(s)
. However, even assuming
that RH is true, very little is known unconditionally about the vertical distribution of the
zeros on the critical line
\{\sigma=\frac{1}{2}\}
.
In a pioneering paper of great importance,
Montgomery
[
8
]
proposed his celebrated

Pair Correlation Conjecture
(PCC), which (under RH) asserts
that the pair correlation between pairs of zeros of
\zeta(s)

(normalized to have unit average spacing) is

1-((\sin\pi u)/\pi u)^{2}
.
As Dyson had pointed out to Montgomery, this is the same as the pair
correlation function of random Hermitian matrices. More precisely,
PCC asserts that for any
\alpha\leqslant\beta
, one has

\lim\limits_{T\to\infty}\frac{\big|\{(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}):0<\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\leqslant T,~\frac{2\pi\alpha}{\log T}\leqslant\gamma^{\prime}-\gamma\leqslant\frac{2\pi\beta}{\log T}\}\big|}{\frac{1}{2\pi}T\log T}=\intop\nolimits_{\alpha}^{\beta}\bigg\{1-\Big(\frac{\sin\pi u}{\pi u}\Big)^{\!2}\bigg\}\,du.

Although PCC has significantly enhanced our conjectural understanding of
the gaps
\gamma^{\prime}-\gamma
between ordinates
\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}

of different zeros of
\zeta(s)
, and it has led to other conceptual
advances, it says nothing about how often gaps of a particular size
y\neq 0

might occur. Indeed, this question appears to have been overlooked in the
literature thus far. The aim of this paper, therefore, is to establish
some nontrivial results on this question. Our methods are flexible, and
the machinery we use can be adapted to treat other similar questions.

1.2.
Statement of results

As the gaps between zeros of
\zeta(s)

on the critical line form a
countably infinite
set, for any specific

y\neq 0
, it is natural to predict that the relation
\gamma^{\prime}-\gamma=y
holds for
at most finitely many
pairs
(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})
; in other words,
one expects that
\zeta(\tfrac{1}{2}+i(\gamma+y))\neq 0
almost always
when
\zeta(\tfrac{1}{2}+i\gamma)=0
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Assume RH. For any fixed
y\neq 0
and
C>0
, there is a number

T_{*}=T_{*}(y,C)>0
such that for every
T\geqslant T_{*}
we have

\zeta(\tfrac{1}{2}+i\gamma)=0\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\zeta(\tfrac{1}{2}+i(\gamma+y))\neq 0

for at least one
\gamma
in the interval
[T,T(1+\varepsilon)]
, where

\varepsilon\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=T^{-C/\log\log T}
.

To prove the theorem, we study sums of the form

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\rho=\frac{1}{2}+i\gamma\\
T_{1}<\gamma<T_{2}\end{subarray}}x^{\rho}\zeta(\rho+iy),

where
T_{1}
is large and
T_{1}\asymp T_{2}
.
To estimate such sums, we apply the residue theorem with
the meromorphic function

\mathcal{D}_{y}(s)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\frac{\zeta^{\prime}}{\zeta}(s)\zeta(s+iy).

Note that
\mathcal{D}_{y}(s)
can be represented
in the half-plane
\{\sigma>1\}
by
an absolutely convergent Dirichlet series
\sum_{n}D_{y}(n)n^{-s}

whose coefficients are given by

D_{y}(n)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=-\sum_{ab=n}\Lambda(a)b^{-iy}\qquad(n\in\mathbb{N}),

where
\Lambda
is the von Mangoldt function.
For later reference, we note that

(1.1)

D_{y}(p)=-\log p\qquad(p\text{~prime}).

Theorem 1.2
.

Assume RH. For any
y\neq 0
and
A>0
, there are numbers

T_{0}=T_{0}(y,A)>0
and
\Theta=\Theta(A)>0
for which the following holds.
For any
T_{1},T_{2}
satisfying

2T_{1}>T_{2}>T_{1}>T_{0}
, let

\Delta\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=T_{2}-T_{1},\qquad{\bf T}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\tfrac{1}{2}(T_{1}+T_{2}),\qquad\mathscr{L}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\exp\bigg(\frac{\log{\bf T}}{\log\log{\bf T}}\bigg).

Then, for every prime number
x
in the range

(1.2)

{\bf T}\mathscr{L}^{-\Theta}<x<\mathrm{e}^{\pi/|y|}\cdot{\bf T}\mathscr{L}^{-\Theta},

we have

(1.3)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\rho=\frac{1}{2}+i\gamma\\
T_{1}<\gamma<T_{2}\end{subarray}}x^{\rho}\zeta(\rho+iy)=\tfrac{1}{2\pi}(x^{-iy}-1)\Delta\log{\bf T}+O\bigg(\frac{\Delta\log{\bf T}}{(\log\log{\bf T})^{1/2}}+{\bf T}\mathscr{L}^{-A}\bigg),

where the sum in (
1.3
) runs over zeros of
\zeta(s)

(
each zero is summed according to its multiplicity
\,)
,
and the implied constant in (
1.3
) depends only on
y
and
A
.

2.1.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

In this subsection, we show that Theorem
1.1
is a
consequence of Theorem
1.2
coupled
with the following observation.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
y\neq 0
. For every sufficiently large
t
, depending only on
y
,
there is a prime
p
in the range
t<p<\mathrm{e}^{\pi/|y|}t

such that
|p^{-iy}-1|>\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
.

Proof.

Let
\alpha\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\mathrm{e}^{\pi/(4|y|)}
, which exceeds one.
Put
\varepsilon\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=|y|/y\in\{\pm 1\}
. For any

\beta\in(\alpha^{2},\alpha^{4})
, the number
\beta^{-iy}

lies on the unit circle in
\mathbb{C}
between
(\alpha^{2})^{-iy}=(-i)^{\varepsilon}

and
(\alpha^{4})^{-iy}=-1
; therefore,

|\beta^{-iy}-1|>\sqrt{2}
.

By the Prime Number Theorem, for any large

t
(depending on
y
), there exist primes

p_{1}\in(t,\alpha t)
and
p_{2}\in(\alpha^{3}t,\alpha^{4}t)
. The
number
\beta\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=p_{2}/p_{1}
lies in
(\alpha^{2},\alpha^{4})
,
and so by the preceding argument we have

\big|p_{2}^{-iy}-p_{1}^{-iy}\big|=\big|p_{1}^{-iy}(\beta^{-iy}-1)\big|>\sqrt{2}.

This implies that
|p_{j}^{-iy}-1|>\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}

for at least one of the primes
p_{j}
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

We apply Theorem
1.2
with the choices

A\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=2C
,
T_{1}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=T

and
T_{2}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=T(1+\varepsilon)
, where

\varepsilon\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\exp(\tfrac{-C\log T}{\log\log T})
.
Then
\Delta\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\varepsilon T
,
{\bf T}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=T+2\Delta
,
and

\log{\bf T}\sim\log T\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\mathscr{L}^{-A}=\exp\big(\tfrac{(-2C+o(1))\log T}{\log\log T}\big)\quad(T\to\infty).

Clearly,
{\bf T}\mathscr{L}^{-A}=o(\Delta\log{\bf T})

as
T\to\infty
. According to Lemma
2.1
,
there is a prime
x
satisfying (
1.2
) such
that
|x^{-iy}-1|>\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
. For any such prime
x
,
the main term in (
1.3
) dominates the error term,
and thus we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\rho=\frac{1}{2}+i\gamma\\
T_{1}<\gamma<T_{2}\end{subarray}}x^{\rho}\zeta(\rho+iy)\neq 0.

The theorem follows.
∎

2.2.
Bounds on various sums

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
y\in\mathbb{R}
,
x\geqslant 10
, and put

c\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=1+\tfrac{1}{\log x}
. Then

(2.1)

\sum_{n\neq x}\frac{|D_{y}(n)|}{n^{c}|\log(x/n)|}\ll\log^{2}x

Proof.

The proof follows standard arguments.
We begin by observing that

(2.2)

\big|D_{y}(n)\big|=\bigg|-\sum_{ab=n}\Lambda(a)b^{-iy}\bigg|\leqslant\sum_{ab=n}\Lambda(a)=\log n\qquad(n\in\mathbb{N}).

Let
S
denote the sum on the left side of (
2.1
).
For positive integers with
n\leqslant\frac{2}{3}x
or
n\geqslant\frac{4}{3}x
,
we have
|\log(x/n)|\gg 1
; using (
2.2
),
the total contribution to
S
from such integers is

\ll\sum_{n}\frac{\log n}{n^{c}}=-\zeta^{\prime}(c)\asymp(c-1)^{-2}=\log^{2}x,

which is acceptable. For integers
\frac{2}{3}x<n<\frac{4}{3}x
, we have

D_{y}(n)\ll\log x
by (
2.2
), and in this range we also have

n^{c}\asymp n
. Thus, to finish the proof, it suffices to observe that both sums

S_{1}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\sum_{\frac{2}{3}x<n<x}\frac{1}{n\log(x/n)}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad S_{2}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\sum_{x<n<\frac{4}{3}x}\frac{1}{n\log(n/x)}

are of size
O(\log x)
, as one verifies using
standard arguments.
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

Assume RH.
Let
y\neq 0
,
t>t^{\prime}\geqslant 10
, and put
\Delta^{\prime}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=t-t^{\prime}
.
For any
\kappa
in the range
\mathrm{e}\leqslant\kappa\leqslant t/\mathrm{e}
, we have

\sum_{t^{\prime}<n\leqslant t}D_{y}(n)n^{iy}\ll\Delta^{\prime}\big\{|y|^{-1}+(\kappa/t)^{1/2}\log^{2}(t/\kappa+|y|)+\log\kappa\big\}+t/\kappa+(t\kappa)^{1/2}\log^{2}t.

Proof.

A well known result of von Koch
[
7
]

asserts that, under RH, one has

(2.3)

\sum_{n\leqslant x}\Lambda(n)=x+O\big(x^{1/2}(\log x)^{2}\big)\qquad(x\geqslant 2).

More generally, building on work of Gonek, Graham,
and Lee
[
6
]
, the author and Sinha
[
1
]

have shown that the Riemann Hypothesis is true if and only if
the following uniform estimate holds:

\sum_{n\leqslant x}\Lambda(n)n^{iy}=\frac{x^{1+iy}}{1+iy}+O\big(x^{1/2}\log^{2}(x+|y|)\big)\qquad(y\in\mathbb{R},~x\geqslant 2).

By partial summation, this implies

(2.4)

\sum_{n\leqslant x}\frac{\Lambda(n)n^{iy}}{n}\ll|y|^{-1}+x^{-1/2}\log^{2}(x+|y|)+1\qquad(y\neq 0,~x\geqslant 2)

(and the sum is zero for
x\in(0,2]
). Therefore, writing

D_{y}(n)n^{iy}=-n^{iy}\sum_{ab=n}\Lambda(a)b^{-iy}=-\sum_{ab=n}\Lambda(a)a^{iy},

we have

\sum_{t^{\prime}<n\leqslant t}D_{y}(n)n^{iy}=-\sum_{t^{\prime}<ab\leqslant t}\Lambda(a)a^{iy}=-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t^{\prime}<ab\leqslant t\\
a\leqslant t/\kappa\end{subarray}}\Lambda(a)a^{iy}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t^{\prime}<ab\leqslant t\\
a>t/\kappa\end{subarray}}\Lambda(a)a^{iy}.

By (
2.4
) and the Chebyshev bound,

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t^{\prime}<ab\leqslant t\\
a\leqslant t/\kappa\end{subarray}}\Lambda(a)a^{iy}

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\leqslant t/\kappa}\Lambda(a)a^{iy}\bigg({\left\lfloor\frac{t}{a}\right\rfloor}-{\left\lfloor\frac{t^{\prime}}{a}\right\rfloor}\bigg)=\Delta^{\prime}\sum_{a\leqslant t/\kappa}\frac{\Lambda(a)a^{iy}}{a}+O(t/\kappa)

\displaystyle\ll\Delta^{\prime}\big\{|y|^{-1}+(\kappa/t)^{1/2}\log^{2}(t/\kappa+|y|)+1\big\}+t/\kappa.

On other hand, using (
2.3
) we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t^{\prime}<ab\leqslant t\\
a>t/\kappa\end{subarray}}\Lambda(a)a^{iy}

\displaystyle=\sum_{b\leqslant\kappa}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t^{\prime}/b<a\leqslant t/b\\
a>t/\kappa\end{subarray}}\Lambda(a)a^{iy}\ll\sum_{b\leqslant\kappa}\sum_{t^{\prime}/b<a\leqslant t/b}\Lambda(a)

\displaystyle=\sum_{b\leqslant\kappa}\bigg\{\frac{\Delta^{\prime}}{b}+O\big((t/b)^{1/2}\log^{2}(t/b)\big)\bigg\}

\displaystyle\ll\Delta^{\prime}\log\kappa+(t\kappa)^{1/2}\log^{2}(t/\kappa).

Combining the two bounds, the lemma follows.
∎

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
t\geqslant 10\,x\geqslant 100
, and put
c\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=1+\frac{1}{\log x}
. Then

(2.5)

\sum_{n\geqslant 2}\frac{\log n}{n^{c}(|t-2\pi nx|+t^{1/2})}\ll\frac{(x+t^{1/2}\log t)\log t}{t^{c+1/2}}.

Proof.

The sum on the left side of (
2.5
)
is equal to

\bigg(\sum_{n\in S_{1}}+\sum_{n\in S_{2}}+\sum_{n\in S_{3}}\bigg)\frac{\log n}{2\pi xn^{c}(|n-X|+Y)},

where

X\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\frac{t}{2\pi x},\qquad Y\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\frac{t^{1/2}}{2\pi x},

and

\displaystyle S_{1}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\{n\geqslant 2:|n-X|\leqslant Y\},

\displaystyle S_{2}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\{n\geqslant 2:Y<|n-X|\leqslant 2X\},

\displaystyle S_{3}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\{n\geqslant 2:|n-X|>2X\}.

As
n\asymp X
for each
n\in S_{1}
, and
|S_{1}|\ll Y+1
, the
sum over
n\in S_{1}
is

\ll\frac{(\log X)(Y+1)}{xX^{c}Y}\ll\frac{(\log t)(x/t^{1/2}+1)}{t^{c}}.

Since
n\ll X
for each
n\in S_{2}
, the
sum over
n\in S_{2}
is

\ll\frac{\log X}{xX^{c}}\bigg(\sum_{2\leqslant n<X-Y}+\sum_{X+Y<n\leqslant 3X}\bigg)\frac{1}{|n-X|+Y}\ll\frac{\log X\log(X/Y)}{xX^{c}}\ll\frac{\log^{2}t}{t^{c}}.

Finally, since
n>3X
and
n-X>\frac{1}{2}n
for each
n\in S_{3}
,
the sum over
n\in S_{3}
is

\ll\frac{1}{x}\sum_{n>3X}\frac{\log n}{n^{c+1}}\ll\frac{\log X}{xX^{c}}\ll\frac{\log t}{t^{c}}.

Putting everything together, we obtain the stated bound.
∎

Lemma 2.6
.

Assume RH. There is an absolute constant
\lambda>0
such that

(2.6)

\big|\zeta(s)\big|\leqslant\exp\bigg(\frac{\lambda\log|t|}{\log\log|t|}\bigg)

holds uniformly for
\sigma\geqslant\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{\log\log|t|}

with
|t|\geqslant 10
.

Proof.

By
[
9
, Theorem 13.18]
, there is an absolute constant
\lambda_{0}>0

such that

\big|\zeta(s)\big|\leqslant\exp\bigg(\frac{\lambda_{0}}{\log\log|t|}\bigg)\qquad(\sigma\geqslant\tfrac{1}{2},~|t|\geqslant 10).

For
\sigma
in the range
\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{\log\log|t|}\leqslant\sigma\leqslant\frac{1}{2}
,
we apply
[
9
, Corollary 10.5]
:

\big|\zeta(s)\big|\asymp|t|^{1/2-\sigma}\big|\zeta(1-s)\big|.

Since
\tfrac{1}{2}-\sigma\leqslant\tfrac{1}{\log\log|t|}
and

1-\sigma\geqslant\tfrac{1}{2}
, we get that

|t|^{1/2-\sigma}\leqslant\exp\bigg(\frac{\log|t|}{\log\log|t|}\bigg)\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\big|\zeta(1-s)\big|\leqslant\exp\bigg(\frac{\lambda_{0}\log|t|}{\log\log|t|}\bigg).

The lemma is valid with
\lambda\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\lambda_{0}+1
.
∎

2.4.

In §

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
\mathcal{I}
be a bounded interval in

\sigma\in\mathcal{I}

and

(2.10)

\mathcal{X}(1-\sigma+it)=\mathrm{e}^{\pi i/4}\exp\Big(\!-it\log\Big(\frac{|t|}{2\pi\mathrm{e}}\Big)\Big)\Big(\frac{|t|}{2\pi}\Big)^{\sigma-1/2}\big\{1+O_{\mathcal{I}}(|t|^{-1})\big\}.

Proof.

From the definition of

(see (

)) it follows that

\mathcal{X}(1-s)=2^{1-s}\pi^{-s}\Gamma(s)\sin\tfrac{\pi}{2}(1-s).

Suppose

with

.
Using Stirling’s formula for the gamma function

\Gamma(s)=\sqrt{2\pi}\,s^{s-1/2}\mathrm{e}^{-s}\{1+O(t^{-1})\}

(see, e.g., Montgomery and Vaughan
[

along with the estimates

(s-\tfrac{1}{2})\log s=(\sigma-\tfrac{1}{2})\log t+\sigma+(t\log t-\tfrac{\pi}{4})i+\tfrac{\pi is}{2}+O(t^{-1})

Lemma 2.8
.

Uniformly for

Lemma 2.9
.

Define

f_{j}(s)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\begin{cases}1&\quad\hbox{if $j=0$},\\

\end{cases}\qquad g_{j}(v)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\begin{cases}1&\quad\hbox{if $j=0$},\\
\log(\pi v)-\frac{\pi i}{2}&\quad\hbox{if $j=+1$},\\
\log(\pi v)+\frac{\pi i}{2}&\quad\hbox{if $j=-1$}.\\
\end{cases}

For each

, and uniformly for

,

,
and

4.
Concluding remarks

Let us define

N_{0,y}(T)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\big|\big\{\rho=\tfrac{1}{2}+i\gamma:\zeta(\rho)=0\text{~and~}\zeta(\rho+iy)\neq 0\big\}\big|,

where each zero is counted according to its multiplicity.
Theorem
1.1
implies that for any fixed

C^{\prime}>0
one has (under RH) the lower bound

N_{0,y}(T)\gg\exp\bigg(\frac{C^{\prime}\log T}{\log\log T}\bigg)

for all large

. Almost certainly,
N_{0,y}(T)
is much
larger than this. In fact, it is reasonable to expect that

N_{0,y}(T)=N_{0}(T)+O_{y}(1)

for all
y\neq 0
, where

N_{0}(T)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\big|\big\{\rho=\tfrac{1}{2}+i\gamma:\zeta(\rho)=0\big\}\big|=\frac{T}{2\pi}\log\frac{T}{2\pi\mathrm{e}}+O(\log T).

In fact, since the total number of gaps between the zeros of
\zeta(s)

on the line
\{\sigma=\tfrac{1}{2}\}
is countable, for every
y\neq 0

one has
N_{0,y}(T)=N_{0}(T)
for all
T>0
, with at most countably many
exceptions. Under the
Linear Independence Conjecture
, for every

rational

y\neq 0
one has
N_{0,y}(T)=N_{0}(T)
for all
T>0
.
