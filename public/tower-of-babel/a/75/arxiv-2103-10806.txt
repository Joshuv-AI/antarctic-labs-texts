A note on the triameter of graphs
Artem Hak, Sergiy Kozerenko and Bogdana Oliynyk Department of Mathematics, Faculty of Informatics, National University of Kyiv-Mohyla Academy, Skovorody str. 2, 04070 Kyiv, Ukraine.

Abstract
In this note, we give answers to three questions from the paper [A. Das, Triameter of graphs, Discuss. Math. Graph Theory 41 (2021), 601–616.]. Namely, we obtain a tight lower bound for the triameter of trees in terms of order and number of leaves. We show that in a connected block graph any triametral triple of vertices contains a diametral pair and that any diametral pair of vertices can be extended to a triametral triple. We also present several open problems concerning the interplay between triametral triples, diametral pairs and peripheral vertices in median and distance-hereditary graphs.



Keywords:
triameter, diameter, trees, block graphs.

Primary: 05C12, Secondary: 05C05.

Introduction

In
[
2
]
A. Das initiated the study of a new graph parameter

\tr(G)=\max\{d_{G}(a,b)+d_{G}(a,c)+d_{G}(b,c):a,b,c\in V(G)\}

named the
triameter
of a connected (simple, finite) graph
G
. At first, a triameter was used as a parameter in
[
9
]
, but explicitly named only in
[
7
]
. The main motivation for studying
\tr(G)
comes from its appearence in lower bounds on radio
k
-chromatic number of a graph
[
7
,
10
]
and total domination number of a connected graph
[
4
]
.

Among other results, in
[
2
]
it was showed that for any connected graph
G
we have
\tr(G)\geq g(G)
, where
g(G)
is the girth of
G
. Also, the following lower bound for the triameter of a tree
T
with
n
vertices and
l
leaves was presented:

\displaystyle\tr(G)\geq\left\lceil\frac{4(n-1)}{l-1}\right\rceil.

(1)

In the final section of
[
2
]
A. Das raised four open questions concerning the triameter (to which we will refer through this paper as to “Questions 1-4”):

1.

The bound (
1
) is not tight. What is the tight lower bound for
\tr(T)
for any given pair
n,l
?

2.

Is there another lower bound for
\tr(G)
for all connected graphs
G
in terms of parameters different from girth (it is believed that
\delta(G)
,
\Delta(G)
will do)?

3.

Is it true that any triametral triple of vertices in a tree contains a diametral pair?

4.

Is it true that any diametral pair of vertices in a tree can be extended to a triametral triple?

In this paper, we completely answer Question 1 by presenting a tight lower bound for
\tr(T)
in terms of
n,l
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
T
be a tree with
n\geq 4
vertices and
l\geq 3
leaves. Then

\tr(T)\geq 6\left\lfloor\frac{n-1}{l}\right\rfloor+2\min\{(n-1)\bmod{l},3\}.

Moreover, this bound is tight for any given pair
n,l
.

We also give affirmative answers to Questions 3 and 4 not only for trees, but rather for all connected block graphs.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
G
be a connected block graph,
d=d_{G}
and
a,b,c,x,y\in V(G)
such that
d(a,b,c)=\tr(G)
,
d(x,y)=\diam(G)
. Then

\displaystyle\max\{d(a,b),d(a,c),d(b,c)\}=\diam(G)\ \text{and}

\displaystyle\max\{d(a,x,y),d(b,x,y),d(c,x,y)\}=\tr(G).

To our knowledge, Question 2 still remains open. In the last section of the paper, we formulate several similar questions concerning the interplay between the triametral triples, diametral pairs and peripheral vertices in median and distance-hereditary graphs.

2
Preliminaries

All graphs under consideration are simple and finite. A graph is
connected
if any pair of its vertices can be joined by a path. The vertex set of a connected graph
G
is equipped with the “shortest-path” metric
d_{G}
, where
d_{G}(u,v)
equals the length of a shortest
u-v
path in
G
. The
diameter
of a connected graph
G
is the value
\diam(G)=\max\{d_{G}(u,v):u,v\in V(G)\}
. A pair of vertices
u,v\in V(G)
in a connected graph
G
is called
diametral
if
d_{G}(u,v)=\diam(G)
. A vertex
u\in V(G)
is called
peripheral
if it belongs to some diametral pair.

For a triple of vertices
u,v,w\in V(G)
in a connected graph
G
put

d_{G}(u,v,w)=d_{G}(u,v)+d_{G}(u,w)+d_{G}(v,w).

The
triameter
of a connected graph
G
is defined as the value

\tr(G)=\max\{d_{G}(u,v,w):u,v,w,\in V(G)\}.

A triple of vertices
u,v,w\in V(G)
is
triametral
if
d_{G}(u,v,w)=\tr(G)
.

Let
u,v\in V(G)
be a pair of vertices in a connected graph
G
. Put

[u,v]_{G}=\{x\in V(G):d_{G}(u,x)+d_{G}(x,v)=d_{G}(u,v)\}

for the
metric interval
between
u
and
v
. A connected graph is called
median

[
8
]
if
|[u,v]_{G}\cap[u,w]_{G}\cap[v,w]_{G}|=1
for any triple of vertices
u,v,w\in V(G)
.

A
tree
is a connected graph without cycles. Note that any tree is a median graph. A vertex of degree one in a graph is called its
leaf
. By
L(T)
we denote the set of all leaves in a tree
T
. If
u
is a leaf in a tree, then the unique vertex
v
adjacent to
u
is called its
support vertex
.

A
connected component
of a graph is its maximal connected subgraph. A vertex whose deletion increases the number of connected components in a graph is called its
cut-vertex
. A graph is
biconnected
if it has no cut-vertices. A
block
of a graph is its maximal biconnected subgraph. A
block graph
B(G)
of a given graph
G
is the intersection graph of the collection of all blocks in
G
. A graph
H
is called a
block graph
if it is isomorphic to
B(G)
for some
G
. It is well-known that a graph is a block graph if and only if each its block is complete
[
3
]
. As a corollary, we obtain that every tree is a block graph.

The following bounds for the triameter of a graph can be easily derived from the definition.

Proposition 2.1
.

For any connected graph
G
we have

2\diam(G)\leq\tr(G)\leq 3\diam(G).

A connected graph
G
is called
antipodal
if for any its vertex
u\in V(G)
there exists a vertex
u^{\prime}\in V(G)
with
[u,u^{\prime}]_{G}=V(G)
. Note that for any
u
such a vertex
u^{\prime}
is always unique. The vertex
u^{\prime}
is called
antipodal vertex
for
u
. It is clear that for an antipodal graph
G
,
d_{G}(u,u^{\prime})=\diam(G)
for any vertex
u\in V(G)
. We have the following result for the triameter of antipodal graphs.

Proposition 2.2
.

For any antipodal graph
G
it holds
\tr(G)=2\diam(G)
.

Proof.

Let
x,y,z\in V(G)
be a triametral triple of vertices in
G
. Then
2\diam(G)\leq\tr(G)=d_{G}(x,y,z)=d_{G}(x,y)+d_{G}(x,z)+d_{G}(y,z)=d_{G}(x,x^{\prime})-d_{G}(x^{\prime},y)+d_{G}(x,x^{\prime})-d_{G}(x^{\prime},z)+d_{G}(y,z)=2d_{G}(x,x^{\prime})-d_{G}(x^{\prime},y)-d_{G}(x^{\prime},z)+d_{G}(y,z)\leq 2d_{G}(x,x^{\prime})=2\diam(G)
, where
x^{\prime}
is the antipodal vertex for
x
.
∎

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
. The
n
-cube
is a graph
Q_{n}
with
V(Q_{n})=\{0,1\}^{n}
and
E(Q_{n})=\{xy:\ \text{there is a unique}\ i,\ 1\leq i\leq n\ \text{with}\ x_{i}\neq y_{i}\}
. Note that every
n
-cube is a median as well as an antipodal graph.

Corollary 2.3
.

For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have
\tr(Q_{n})=2n
.

Proof.

Since
\diam(Q_{n})=n
and
Q_{n}
is antipodal, the equality
\tr(Q_{n})=2n
immediately follows from Proposition
2.2
. ∎

Note that Corollary
2.3
also can be deduced from the following observation about the triameter of Cartesian product of two connected graphs.

Proposition 2.4
.

[
7
]

For any two connected graphs
G
and
H
,
\tr(G\square H)=\tr(G)+\tr(H)
.

Now, since
Q_{n}
is a Cartesian product of
n
copies of
K_{2}
, the desired equality
\tr(Q_{n})=2n
easily follows.

Corollary 2.5
.

Every pair of vertices in an antipodal graph can be extended to a triametral triple.

Proof.

If
u,v\in V(G)
is a pair of vertices in an antipodal graph
G
, then Proposition
2.2
asserts
d_{G}(u,v,v^{\prime})=d_{G}(u,v)+d_{G}(u,v^{\prime})+d_{G}(v,v^{\prime})\geq 2d_{G}(v,v^{\prime})=2\diam(G)=\tr(G)
, where
v^{\prime}
is the antipodal vertex for
v
. ∎

In particular, Question 4 holds for antipodal graphs. However, Question 3 does not hold for antipodal graphs as the
3
-cube
Q_{3}
has a triametral triple of vertices
x,y,z\in V(Q_{3})
with
d_{Q_{3}}(x,y)=d_{Q_{3}}(x,z)=d_{Q_{3}}(y,z)=2<3=\diam(Q_{3})
.

3.1
An optimal lower bound for the triameter of trees

In this subsection we prove Theorem
1.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

We use induction on
n\geq 4
. If
n=4
, then
T=K_{1,3}
,
l=3
and
\tr(T)=6=6\left\lfloor\frac{n-1}{l}\right\rfloor+2\min\{(n-1)\bmod{l},3\}
. Now suppose that
n\geq 5
. Consider the tree
T^{\prime}=T\backslash L(T)
and put
l^{\prime}=|L(T^{\prime})|
. Note that
l^{\prime}\leq l
. If
l^{\prime}=1
, then
T=K_{1,n-1}
,
l=n-1
and
\tr(T)=6=6\lfloor\frac{n-1}{l}\rfloor+2\min\{(n-1)\bmod{l},3\}
. If
l^{\prime}=2
, then
T
is bistar and
l=n-2
. Since
n\geq 5
,
T\neq P_{4}
and therefore
\tr(T)=8=6\left\lfloor\frac{n-1}{l}\right\rfloor+2\min\{(n-1)\bmod{l},3\}
. Thus, assume
l^{\prime}\geq 3
. By induction assumption,
\tr(T^{\prime})\geq 6\lfloor\frac{n-l-1}{l^{\prime}}\rfloor+2\min\{(n-l-1)\bmod{l^{\prime}},3\}
.

Claim:

\tr(T)=\tr(T^{\prime})+6
.

Let
d_{T^{\prime}}(x,y,z)=\tr(T^{\prime})
for some
x,y,z\in V(T^{\prime})
. Then
x,y,z\in L(T^{\prime})
and hence there is a triple of vertices
x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime}\in L(T)
with
xx^{\prime},yy^{\prime},zz^{\prime}\in E(T)
. We have
\tr(T)\geq d_{T}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime})=d_{T^{\prime}}(x,y,z)+6=\tr(T^{\prime})+6
. Similarly, if
d_{T}(a,b,c)=\tr(T)
, then
a,b,c\in L(T)
. Let
a^{\prime},b^{\prime},c^{\prime}
be the corresponding support vertices in
T
for the leaves
a,b,c
, respectively. Then
\tr(T)=d_{T}(a,b,c)=d_{T^{\prime}}(a^{\prime},b^{\prime},c^{\prime})+6\leq\tr(T^{\prime})+6
. This proves the claim.

Put
m=(n-1)\bmod{l}
and
m^{\prime}=(n-l-1)\bmod{l^{\prime}}
for the sake of simplicity. Note that
n-l-1\geq m
. By Claim,

\displaystyle\tr(T)-(6\lfloor\frac{n-1}{l}\rfloor+2\min\{(n-1)\bmod{l},3\})

\displaystyle=\tr(T^{\prime})+6-6\lfloor\frac{n-1}{l}\rfloor-2\min\{m,3\}

\displaystyle\geq 6\lfloor\frac{n-l-1}{l^{\prime}}\rfloor+2\min\{(n-l-1)\bmod{l^{\prime}},3\}+6-6\lfloor\frac{n-1}{l}\rfloor-2\min\{m,3\}

\displaystyle=6\lfloor\frac{n-l-1}{l^{\prime}}\rfloor+2\min\{m^{\prime},3\}+6-6\lfloor\frac{n-1}{l}\rfloor-2\min\{m,3\}

\displaystyle=6(\lfloor\frac{n-l-1}{l^{\prime}}\rfloor-\lfloor\frac{n-1}{l}\rfloor+1)+2(\min\{m^{\prime},3\}-\min\{m,3\})

\displaystyle=6(\lfloor\frac{n-l-1}{l^{\prime}}\rfloor-\lfloor\frac{n-l-1}{l}\rfloor)+2(\min\{m^{\prime},3\}-\min\{m,3\}).

Since
l^{\prime}\leq l
,
\lfloor\frac{n-l-1}{l^{\prime}}\rfloor\geq\lfloor\frac{n-l-1}{l}\rfloor
. If
\lfloor\frac{n-l-1}{l^{\prime}}\rfloor=\lfloor\frac{n-l-1}{l}\rfloor
, then
\frac{n-l-1-m^{\prime}}{l^{\prime}}=\frac{n-l-1-m}{l}
. Therefore,
m^{\prime}=\frac{(n-l-1)(l-l^{\prime})+ml^{\prime}}{l}\geq\frac{m(l-l^{\prime})+ml^{\prime}}{l}=m
. Hence, in case
\lfloor\frac{n-l-1}{l^{\prime}}\rfloor=\lfloor\frac{n-l-1}{l}\rfloor
, we have
2(\min\{m^{\prime},3\}-\min\{m,3\})\geq 0
and thus
\tr(T)-(6\lfloor\frac{n-1}{l}\rfloor+2\min\{(n-1)\bmod{l},3\})\geq 0
. Finally, if
\lfloor\frac{n-l-1}{l^{\prime}}\rfloor>\lfloor\frac{n-l-1}{l}\rfloor
, then

\displaystyle\tr(T)-(6\lfloor\frac{n-1}{l}\rfloor+2\min\{(n-1)\bmod{l},3\})

\displaystyle\geq 6(\lfloor\frac{n-l-1}{l^{\prime}}\rfloor-\lfloor\frac{n-l-1}{l}\rfloor)+2(\min\{m^{\prime},3\}-\min\{m,3\})

\displaystyle>6+2(\min\{m^{\prime},3\}-\min\{m,3\})\geq 6-2\cdot 3=0

as well. In all cases,
\tr(T)\geq 6\lfloor\frac{n-1}{l}\rfloor+2\min\{(n-1)\bmod{l},3\}
which proves the induction step.

Now we prove that the obtained bound is tight. To do this, fix
n\geq 4
and
3\leq l\leq n-1
. Construct a tree
T_{n,l}
as follows: start with a star
K_{1,l}
, then fix a set of edges
E^{\prime}\subset E(K_{1,l})
with
|E^{\prime}|=m=(n-1)\bmod{l}
and subdivide each of them by
\lfloor\frac{n-l-1}{l}\rfloor+1
new vertices; each other edge in
K_{1,l}
subdivide by
\lfloor\frac{n-l-1}{l}\rfloor
new vertices to obtain
T_{n,l}
. By construction,
T_{n,l}
has

\displaystyle 1+l+\lfloor\frac{n-l-1}{l}\rfloor\cdot l+(n-1)\bmod{l}

\displaystyle=1+l+\lfloor\frac{n-l-1}{l}\rfloor\cdot l+(n-l-1)\bmod{l}

\displaystyle=1+l+n-l-1=n

vertices and
l
leaves.

We must consider four cases depending on the remainder
m
. If
m\geq 3
, then fix a triple of vertices
a,b,c
each incident to some edge from
E^{\prime}
. It holds
\tr(T_{n,l})=d_{T_{n,l}}(a,b,c)=3\cdot 2\cdot(\lfloor\frac{n-l-1}{l}\rfloor+2)=6\lfloor\frac{n-1}{l}\rfloor+6=6\lfloor\frac{n-1}{l}\rfloor+2\min\{m,3\}
. If
m=2
, then fix two leaf vertices
a,b
each incident to some edge from
E^{\prime}
and another leaf vertex
c
from
T_{n,l}
. In this case,

\displaystyle\tr(T_{n,l})=d_{T_{n,l}}(a,b,c)=d_{T_{n,l}}(a,b)+d_{T_{n,l}}(a,c)+d_{T_{n,l}}(b,c)

\displaystyle=2\cdot(\lfloor\frac{n-l-1}{l}\rfloor+2)+2\cdot(2\lfloor\frac{n-l-1}{l}\rfloor+3)

\displaystyle=6\lfloor\frac{n-1}{l}\rfloor+4=6\lfloor\frac{n-1}{l}\rfloor+2\min\{m,3\}.

Further, if
m=1
, then fix a leaf vertex
a
which is incident to the unique edge from
E^{\prime}
and two other leaf vertices
b,c
from
T_{n,l}
. We have

\displaystyle\tr(T_{n,l})=d_{T_{n,l}}(a,b,c)=d_{T_{n,l}}(a,b)+d_{T_{n,l}}(a,c)+d_{T_{n,l}}(b,c)

\displaystyle=2\cdot\lfloor\frac{n-l-1}{l}\rfloor+3+2\cdot 2\cdot(\lfloor\frac{n-l-1}{l}\rfloor+1)

\displaystyle=6\lfloor\frac{n-1}{l}\rfloor+2=6\lfloor\frac{n-1}{l}\rfloor+2\min\{m,3\}.

Finally, for

, then for any triple
a,b,c
of leaf vertices from

\tr(T_{n,l})=3\cdot 2\cdot(\lfloor\frac{n-l-1}{l}\rfloor+1)=6\lfloor\frac{n-1}{l}\rfloor=6\lfloor\frac{n-1}{l}\rfloor+2\min\{m,3\}
. In all four cases the desired equality holds.
∎

To show that any triametral triple of vertices in a block graph contains a diametral pair and that any diametral pair of vertices can be extended to a triametral triple, we will use the next metric characterization of block graphs.

Theorem 3.1
.

[
6
]

A connected graph
G
is a block graph if and only if its metric
d_{G}
satisfies the “
4
-point condition”: for any
x,y,z,t\in V(G)
it holds

d_{G}(x,y)+d_{G}(z,t)\leq\max\{d_{G}(x,z)+d_{G}(y,t),d_{G}(x,t)+d_{G}(y,z)\}.

Now we are ready to prove Theorem
1.2
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Since
G
is a connected block graph, from Theorem
3.1
it follows that
d(x,y)+d(a,b)\leq\max\{d(x,a)+d(y,b),d(x,b)+d(y,a)\}
. Without loss of generality, we can assume that

\displaystyle d(x,y)+d(a,b)\leq d(x,a)+d(y,b).

(2)

If
d(a,y)\geq d(y,b)
, then

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\geq d(a,x,y)-d(a,b,c)=d(a,x)+d(a,y)+d(x,y)-d(a,b)-d(a,c)-d(b,c)

\displaystyle=(d(a,x)-d(a,b))+d(a,y)+d(x,y)-d(a,c)-d(b,c)

\displaystyle\geq(d(x,y)-d(y,b))+d(a,y)+d(x,y)-d(a,c)-d(b,c)

\displaystyle=(d(a,y)-d(y,b))+2d(x,y)-d(a,c)-d(b,c)

\displaystyle\geq 2\diam(G)-d(a,c)-d(b,c)\geq 0.

Hence,
d(a,x,y)=d(a,b,c)=\tr(G)
and
d(a,c)=d(b,c)=\diam(G)
.

If
d(b,x)\geq d(x,a)
, then

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\geq d(b,x,y)-d(a,b,c)=d(b,x)+d(b,y)+d(x,y)-d(a,b)-d(a,c)-d(b,c)

\displaystyle(d(b,y)-d(a,b))+d(b,x)+d(x,y)-d(a,c)-d(b,c)

\displaystyle\geq(d(x,y)-d(x,a))+d(b,x)+d(x,y)-d(a,c)-d(b,c)

\displaystyle=(d(b,x)-d(x,a))+2d(x,y)-d(a,c)-d(b,c)

\displaystyle\geq 2\diam(G)-d(a,c)-d(b,c)\geq 0.

Thus, in this case also
d(b,x,y)=d(a,b,c)=\tr(G)
and
d(a,c)=d(b,c)=\diam(G)
.

Now suppose
d(a,y)<d(y,b)
and
d(b,x)<d(x,a)
. Then
d(a,y)+d(b,x)<d(y,b)+d(x,a)
implying that
d(y,b)+d(x,a)\leq d(x,y)+d(a,b)
(again, see Theorem
3.1
). Combining this inequality with (
2
), we obtain the equality

\displaystyle d(x,y)+d(a,b)=d(x,a)+d(y,b).

(3)

In a similar fashion, we can consider two sums
d(x,y)+d(a,c)
,
d(x,y)+d(a,c)
and apply Theorem
3.1
to each of them. Hence, we restrict ourselves to the case where the following equalities hold:

\displaystyle d(x,y)+d(a,c)=d(a,x)+d(c,y)\ \text{or}

(4)

\displaystyle d(x,y)+d(a,c)=d(a,y)+d(c,x)

(5)

and

\displaystyle d(x,y)+d(b,c)=d(b,x)+d(c,y)\ \text{or}

(6)

\displaystyle d(x,y)+d(b,c)=d(b,y)+d(c,x).

(7)

If (
5
) holds, then using (
3
), we obtain

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\geq d(a,x,y)-d(a,b,c)=d(a,x)+d(a,y)+d(x,y)-d(a,b)-d(a,c)-d(b,c)

\displaystyle=(d(x,y)+d(a,b)-d(y,b))+(d(x,y)+d(a,c)-d(c,x))

\displaystyle+d(x,y)-d(a,b)-d(a,c)-d(b,c)

\displaystyle=3\diam(G)-d(y,b)-d(c,x)-d(b,c)\geq 0.

Therefore,
d(a,x,y)=\tr(G)
and
d(b,c)=\diam(G)
.
If (
6
) holds, then using (
3
), we can similarly obtain
d(b,x,y)=\tr(G)
and
d(a,c)=\diam(G)
.

Finally, assume that (
4
) and (
7
) hold. Then

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\geq d(c,x,y)-d(a,b,c)=d(c,x)+d(c,y)+d(x,y)-d(a,b)-d(a,c)-d(b,c)

\displaystyle=(d(x,y)+d(b,c)-d(b,y))+(d(x,y)+d(a,c)-d(a,x))

\displaystyle+d(x,y)-d(a,b)-d(a,c)-d(b,c)

\displaystyle=3\diam(G)-d(b,y)-d(a,x)-d(a,b)\geq 0.

Hence, in this case
d(c,x,y)=\tr(G)
and
d(a,b)=\diam(G)
.
∎

One natural generalization of block graphs are
distance-hereditary
graphs. These are connected graphs in which every induced connected subgraph is isometric
[
5
]
. It is well-known
[
1
]
that a connected graph

Open questions

Consider the following weakening of Questions 3,4 schemata:

Question 3’:
Is it true that any triametral triple of vertices in a … graph contains a peripheral vertex?

Question 4’:
Is it true that any peripheral vertex in a … graph can be extended to a triametral triple?

These can be formulated for various classes of graphs substituting the corresponding class into the ellipsis. Similarly, in what follows, we will refer to Questions 3,4 also as to shemata of questions for graph classes.
