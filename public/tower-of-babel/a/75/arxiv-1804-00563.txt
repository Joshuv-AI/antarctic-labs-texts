Large 𝒛 \boldsymbol{z} Asymptotics for Special Function Solutions of Painlevé II in the Complex Plane This paper is a contribution to the Special Issue on Painlevé Equations and Applications in Memory of Andrei Kapaev. The full collection is available at https://www.emis.de/journals/SIGMA/Kapaev.html
Alfredo DEAÑO

Abstract
In this paper we obtain large z z asymptotic expansions in the complex plane for the tau function corresponding to special function solutions of the Painlevé II differential equation. Using the fact that these tau functions can be written as n × n n\times n Wronskian determinants involving classical Airy functions, we use Heine’s formula to rewrite them as n n -fold integrals, which can be asymptotically approximated using the classical method of steepest descent in the complex plane.

Introduction and motivation

The six Painlevé differential equations,
{\rm P}_{\rm I}
–
{\rm P}_{\rm VI}
, have attracted a great deal of attention in the last decades. They feature in a large and increasing number of areas in mathematics, ranging from random matrix theory to integrable systems (continuous as well as discrete), orthogonal polynomials, partial differential equations and combinatorics. We refer the reader to standard references such as
[
12
,
17
,
18
,
21
]
, as well as the digital library of mathematical functions
[
30
, Chapter 32]
, for more details and the complete list of the Painlevé equations.

Generic solutions of
{\rm P}_{\rm I}
–
{\rm P}_{\rm VI}
are sometimes called Painlevé transcendents, or nonlinear special functions, and they cannot be expressed in terms of elementary or even classical special functions. However, for specific values of the parameters in the differential equations, it is known that families of rational and special function solutions exist for
{\rm P}_{\rm II}
–
{\rm P}_{\rm VI}
; these appear for instance in the theory of semiclassical orthogonal polynomials, see for example
[
33
]
and the recent monograph
[
34
]
, and in random matrix theory, see
[
18
,
19
,
20
]
.

These rational and special function solutions of Painlevé equations can be constructed from a given
seed function

\varphi(z)
, with a suitable initial value of the parameters; successive application of Bäcklund transformations
[
12
, Section 4]
,
[
30
, Section 32.7]
leads to a sequence of
tau functions
, that as shown by Okamoto
[
28
]
(see also Forrester and Witte
[
19
,
20
]
or Clarkson
[
12
]
) have the form of
n\times n
Wronskian determinants

\displaystyle\tau_{n}(z)=\det\left(\frac{\textrm{D}^{j+k}}{\textrm{D}z^{j+k}}\varphi(z)\right)_{j,k=0,1,\ldots,n-1}

with initial values
\tau_{0}(z)=1
and
\tau_{1}(z)=\varphi(z)
. Here
D
is a differential operator that depends on the particular Painlevé equation that we are considering. The solution of the Painlevé equation (and of other associated equations) can then be written directly in terms of these tau functions.

In this paper we are interested in the special function solutions of the second Painlevé equation, denoted
{\rm P}_{\rm II}
,

\displaystyle q^{\prime\prime}=zq+2q^{3}+\alpha,\qquad\alpha\in\mathbb{C}.

(1.1)

These special function solutions of
{\rm P}_{\rm II}
can be written in terms of standard Airy functions; more precisely, the general seed function is given by

\displaystyle\varphi(z)=C_{1}\operatorname{Ai}\big({-}2^{-1/3}z\big)+C_{2}\operatorname{Bi}\big({-}2^{-1/3}z\big),

(1.2)

where
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are constants, and the tau function is the
n\times n
Wronskian determinant

\displaystyle\tau_{n}(z)=\det\left(\frac{{\rm d}^{j+k}}{{\rm d}z^{j+k}}\varphi(z)\right)_{j,k=0,1,\ldots,n-1},\qquad n\geq 1,

(1.3)

with
\tau_{0}(z):=1
. As shown in
[
13
, Theorems 4 and 7]
, the functions

\displaystyle p_{n}(z)=-2\frac{{\rm d}^{2}}{{\rm d}z^{2}}\log\tau_{n}(z),\qquad q_{n}(z)=\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}z}\log\frac{\tau_{n-1}(z)}{\tau_{n}(z)},\qquad\sigma_{n}(z)=\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}z}\log\tau_{n}(z)

(1.4)

are special function (Airy) solutions of the
{\rm P}_{\rm 34}
equation

\displaystyle p_{n}\frac{{\rm d}^{2}p_{n}}{{\rm d}z^{2}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{{\rm d}p_{n}}{{\rm d}z}\right)^{2}+2p_{n}^{3}-zp_{n}^{2}-\frac{\left(\alpha+\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{2}}{2},

(1.5)

the
{\rm P}_{\rm II}
equation (
1.1
) and the symmetric
\textrm{S}_{\textrm{II}}
equation

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\textrm{d}^{2}\sigma_{n}}{\textrm{d}z^{2}}\right)^{2}+4\left(\frac{\textrm{d}\sigma_{n}}{\textrm{d}z}\right)^{3}+2\frac{\textrm{d}\sigma_{n}}{\textrm{d}z}\left(z\frac{\textrm{d}\sigma_{n}}{\textrm{d}z}-\sigma_{n}\right)=\frac{1}{4}\left(\alpha+\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{2},

(1.6)

respectively, with
\alpha=n-\tfrac{1}{2}
.

The Airy solutions are used in the recent work of Clarkson, Loureiro and Van Assche
[
15
]
, in the asymptotic analysis of the partition function and free energy in the cubic Hermitian random matrix model by Bleher, Deaño and Yattselev
[
3
,
4
,
5
]
, and in the study of multiple orthogonal polynomials with a cubic potential in the complex plane by Van Assche, Filipuk and Zhang
[
35
]
. In random matrix theory, the pure
\operatorname{Ai}
case of
{\rm P}_{\rm II}
special function solutions arises in the calculation of averages of powers of the characteristic polynomial in the GUE (Gaussian unitary ensemble), see
[
19
, Proposition 28]
. The asymptotic behavior of these Airy solutions has been investigated recently by Clarkson in
[
13
]
, but the asymptotic results are restricted to the real line, and only even values of
n
in the oscillatory regime are included. Asymptotic results for the seed case can also be found in
[
17
, Chapter 11]
, and Its and Kapaev in
[
23
, Proposition 4.3]
characterize rational and Airy solutions of
{\rm P}_{\rm II}
as those that do not exhibit asymptotic elliptic behavior in the complex plane.

The aim of this paper is to obtain large
z
asymptotic approximations for
\tau_{n}(z)
corresponding to special function solutions of
{\rm P}_{\rm II}
. As a direct consequence of (
1.4
), the analysis of
\tau_{n}(z)
leads directly to asymptotics for the Painlevé functions
p_{n}(z)
,
q_{n}(z)
and
\sigma_{n}(z)
. The methodology is related to ideas used for the large
n
asymptotics for rational solutions (of
{\rm P}_{\rm II}
–
{\rm P}_{\rm IV}
) by several authors, including Balogh, Bertola and Bothner
[
1
]
, Bertola and Bothner
[
2
]
, Bothner, Miller and Sheng
[
8
]
, Buckingham
[
9
]
, Buckingham and Miller
[
10
,
11
]
. Next we summarise the main steps:

1.

Relate the entries of the Wronskian determinant (
1.3
), which are Airy functions, with the moments of a suitably chosen weight function
w(t,z)
,

\displaystyle\mu_{m}(z)=\int_{\Gamma}t^{m}w(t,z){\rm d}t,\qquad m=0,1,2,\ldots,

where
\Gamma
is a suitable contour on the real line or in the complex plane. Namely, if

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}^{m}}{{\rm d}z^{m}}\varphi(z)=\beta^{m}\int_{\Gamma}t^{m}w(t,z){\rm d}t=\beta^{m}\mu_{m}(z),

for
m\geq 0
and some constant
\beta
, then we can write

\displaystyle\tau_{n}(z)=\det\big(\beta^{j+k}\mu_{j+k}(z)\big)_{j,k=0}^{n-1}=\beta^{n(n-1)}\det\left(\mu_{j+k}(z)\right)_{j,k=0}^{n-1}=\beta^{n(n-1)}D_{n}(z),

for
n\geq 1
, with
D_{0}(z):=1
.

It is worth mentioning that often there are several possible weight functions and contours, and some choices may be simpler and/or impose restrictions on parameters.

2.

Apply the classical Heine’s formula
[
22
, Corollary 2.1.3]
,
[
31
, Section 2.2]
, that gives a multiple integral representation for the Hankel determinant obtained before:

\displaystyle D_{n}(z)=\frac{1}{n!}\int_{\Gamma^{n}}\Delta_{n}(t)^{2}\prod_{k=1}^{n}w(t_{k},z){\rm d}t_{k},\qquad\Delta_{n}(t)=\prod_{1\leq j<k\leq n}(t_{k}-t_{j}).

3.

Apply the (classical) method of steepest descent to this
n
-fold integral, to obtain the leading asymptotic behavior in different sectors of the complex
z
plane. The details of this classical asymptotic method in one variable can be found in many references, for instance
[
7
,
27
,
29
,
32
]
, and in the multivariate context, we refer the reader to
[
6
]
,
[
7
, Chapter 8]
or
[
16
]
. For convenience, we detail the calculation instead of just writing the leading term in the asymptotic expansion given in
[
16
, equation (1.61)]
; the main technical details will depend on the different sectors where the variable
z
grows large in
\mathbb{C}
, which will condition the deformation of
\Gamma
that is needed, as well as the value of the constants in the seed function, that lead to very different asymptotic behaviors.

Remark 1.1
.

We observe that in principle the leading term in the asymptotic expansion could also be obtained using the Toda equation satisfied by the tau functions
\tau_{n}(z)
. This is a general type of identity that relates consecutive tau functions, and that for
{\rm P}_{\rm II}
reads

\displaystyle\frac{\tau_{n+1}(z)\tau_{n-1}(z)}{\tau_{n}(z)^{2}}=\frac{{\rm d}^{2}}{{\rm d}z^{2}}\log\tau_{n}(z),\qquad n\geq 0,

(1.7)

see other examples in
[
19
,
20
]
.

Making a suitable ansatz of the leading term in the large
z
asymptotic expansion allows to construct a proof by induction, see
[
14
, Proposition 5.2]
for an example in
{\rm P}_{\rm IV}
. However, we find that this methodology does not give a very precise estimate of the subleading terms or the order of the error terms, and also it poses problems in the oscillatory regime, where the leading term as
z\to\infty
is usually the result of combinations of different exponential contributions, that are difficult to keep track of in this Toda equation. For this reason, we prefer to calculate the expansions using steepest descent of the integral arising from Heine’s formula. Once the general structure of the asymptotic expansion for
\tau_{n}(z)
is proved, (
1.7
) may be used to identify the coefficients therein.

2
Main results

In the study of the asymptotic behavior of the tau function (
1.3
) we distinguish, much like in the case of classical Airy functions, two regimes: non-oscillatory (exponential) and oscillatory (trigonometric). Furthermore, in the first case it is enough to obtain asymptotics in the sector
|\arg(-z)|<\frac{\pi}{3}
, since we can use the following rotational symmetries of the seed function (
1.2
):

Lemma 2.1
.

The Airy seed function
\varphi(z)
given by (
1.2
) satisfies the following identities:

\displaystyle\varphi\big({\rm e}^{\pm\frac{2\pi{\rm i}}{3}}z\big)=\widetilde{C}_{1\pm}\operatorname{Ai}\big({-}2^{-1/3}z\big)+\widetilde{C}_{2\pm}\operatorname{Bi}\big({-}2^{-1/3}z\big),

with new constants

\displaystyle\widetilde{C}_{1\pm}=\frac{C_{1}}{2}{\rm e}^{\pm\frac{\pi{\rm i}}{3}}+\frac{3C_{2}}{2}{\rm e}^{\mp\frac{\pi{\rm i}}{6}},\qquad\widetilde{C}_{2\pm}=\frac{C_{1}}{2}{\rm e}^{\mp\frac{\pi{\rm i}}{6}}+\frac{C_{2}}{2}{\rm e}^{\pm\frac{\pi{\rm i}}{3}}.

The tau function then satisfies

\displaystyle\tau_{n}\big({\rm e}^{\pm\frac{2\pi{\rm i}}{3}}z\big)={\rm e}^{\pm\frac{2\pi{\rm i}}{3}n(n-1)}\tau_{n}(z).

Proof.

The proof is a straightforward manipulation of standard formulas for Airy functions, in particular

\displaystyle\operatorname{Bi}(z)={\rm e}^{-\frac{\pi{\rm i}}{6}}\operatorname{Ai}\big({\rm e}^{-\frac{2\pi{\rm i}}{3}}z\big)+{\rm e}^{\frac{\pi{\rm i}}{6}}\operatorname{Ai}\big({\rm e}^{\frac{2\pi{\rm i}}{3}}z\big),

\displaystyle\operatorname{Ai}\big({\rm e}^{\mp\frac{2\pi{\rm i}}{3}}z\big)=\tfrac{1}{2}{\rm e}^{\mp\frac{\pi{\rm i}}{3}}\left(\operatorname{Ai}(z)\pm{\rm i}\operatorname{Bi}(z)\right),

see
[
30
, formulas (9.2.10) and (9.2.11)]
. The transformation for
\tau_{n}(z)
follows directly from the properties of the seed function.
∎

Having this result, it is enough to study the tau functions in two different sectors of
\mathbb{C}
:

•

the non-oscillatory sector
|\arg(-z)|<\tfrac{\pi}{3}
,

•

the real axis, where oscillatory behavior occurs.

Once the asymptotic behavior is determined in these sectors, the results in the rotated ones follows directly by changing the constants suitably, according to Lemma
2.1
.

Our main result about the asymptotic behavior of Airy-type solutions of
{\rm P}_{\rm II}
is given in the two theorems below. We present the general result together with a number of consequences, and we highlight the case
C_{2}=0
(when the seed function contains only
\operatorname{Ai}
functions), which is particularly important in applications, because it has a distinguished asymptotic behavior, an extended non-oscillatory sector and particular importance in applications coming from orthogonal polynomials and random matrix theory.

2.1
Non-oscillatory regime

Theorem 2.2
.

For
n\geq 1
, if we define

\displaystyle K_{n}=\frac{2^{-\frac{3n^{2}}{4}-\frac{n}{6}}}{\pi^{\frac{n}{2}}},

(2.1)

then the function
\tau_{n}(z)
has the following asymptotic behavior as
|z|\to\infty
:

1.

If
C_{2}\neq 0
, in the sector
|\arg(-z)|<\frac{\pi}{3}
,

\displaystyle\tau_{n}(z)=K_{n}(-z)^{-\frac{n^{2}}{4}}\sum_{r=0}^{n}\mathbf{A}_{n,r}(z){\rm e}^{\frac{\sqrt{2}}{3}(n-2r)(-z)^{\frac{3}{2}}},

(2.2)

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}_{n,r}(z)\sim(-z)^{\frac{3}{2}r(n-r)}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{a_{n,r}^{(j)}}{(-z)^{3j/2}}

and the leading coefficient is

\displaystyle a_{n,r}^{(0)}=(-1)^{\left\lfloor{r/2}\right\rfloor}2^{(n-r)(\frac{5r}{2}+1)}C_{1}^{r}C_{2}^{n-r}G(r+1)G(n-r+1),

in terms of the Barnes
G
function,
G(n)=\prod\limits_{k=0}^{n-2}k!
, see
[
30
, Section 5.17]
.

2.

If
C_{2}=0
, in the sector
|\arg(-z)|<\pi
,

\displaystyle\tau_{n}(z)=K_{n}(-z)^{-\frac{n^{2}}{4}}\mathbf{A}_{n,n}(z){\rm e}^{-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{3}n(-z)^{\frac{3}{2}}}.

(2.3)

From this theorem and the symmetry relations in Lemma
2.1
, we can draw a number of consequences. Firstly, we can determine asymptotically free of poles regions in the complex plane for the special function solutions of
{\rm P}_{\rm II}
:

Corollary 2.3
.

If
C_{2}\neq 0
, then the Airy solutions of
{\rm P}_{\rm II}
are tronquée solutions
(
asymptotically free of poles
)
in the sectors

\displaystyle S_{k}=\left\{-\frac{\pi}{3}+\frac{2k\pi}{3}<\arg(-z)<\frac{\pi}{3}+\frac{2k\pi}{3},\,k\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}.

If
C_{2}=0
, then the Airy solutions of
{\rm P}_{\rm II}
are tronquée solutions in the sector

\displaystyle S=\{|\arg(-z)|<\pi\}.

This result proves a conjecture by Clarkson
[
13
, p. 99]
. Using these asymptotic expansions, we can determine the asymptotic behavior of the Painlevé functions in (
1.4
).

Corollary 2.4
.

For
n\geq 1
, the functions
q_{n}(z)
,
p_{n}(z)
and
\sigma_{n}(z)
in (
1.4
) admit asymptotic expansions of the following form:

1.

If
C_{2}\neq 0
, as
|z|\to\infty
with
|\arg(-z)|<\frac{\pi}{3}
,

\displaystyle\sigma_{n}(z)=-\frac{n(-z)^{1/2}}{\sqrt{2}}-\frac{n^{2}}{4z}+\frac{\sqrt{2}n(4n^{2}+1)}{32(-z)^{5/2}}+\mathcal{O}\big((-z)^{-4}\big),

\displaystyle p_{n}(z)=-\frac{n}{\sqrt{2}(-z)^{1/2}}-\frac{n^{2}}{2z^{2}}-\frac{5n(4n^{2}+1)\sqrt{2}}{32(-z)^{7/2}}+\mathcal{O}\big((-z)^{-5}\big),

\displaystyle q_{n}(z)=\frac{(-z)^{1/2}}{\sqrt{2}}+\frac{2n-1}{4z}-\frac{\sqrt{2}(12n^{2}-12n+5)}{32(-z)^{5/2}}+\mathcal{O}\big((-z)^{-4}\big).

2.

If
C_{2}=0
, as
|z|\to\infty
with
|\arg(-z)|<\pi
,

\displaystyle\sigma_{n}(z)=\frac{n(-z)^{1/2}}{\sqrt{2}}+\frac{n^{2}}{4z}-\frac{\sqrt{2}n(4n^{2}+1)}{32(-z)^{5/2}}+\mathcal{O}\big((-z)^{-4}\big),

\displaystyle p_{n}(z)=\frac{n}{\sqrt{2}(-z)^{1/2}}-\frac{n^{2}}{2z^{2}}+\frac{5n(4n^{2}+1)\sqrt{2}}{32(-z)^{7/2}}+\mathcal{O}\big((-z)^{-5}\big),

\displaystyle q_{n}(z)=-\frac{(-z)^{1/2}}{\sqrt{2}}+\frac{2n-1}{4z}+\frac{\sqrt{2}(12n^{2}-12n+5)}{32(-z)^{5/2}}+\mathcal{O}\big((-z)^{-4}\big).

2.2
Oscillatory regime

The behavior of
\tau_{n}(z)
in the oscillatory regime is particularly interesting. Figs.
1
and
2
show the
\tau_{n}(z)
functions for different values of
n
, on the positive real axis, with
C_{2}=0
. It is apparent that in the even case there is a leading algebraic term, with oscillations of small amplitude superimposed, whereas in the odd case the leading term is itself oscillatory with increasing amplitude (except when
n=1
). Theorem
2.5
makes this idea more precise.

Theorem 2.5

(oscillatory regime)
.

For
n\geq 1
and
z\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, the function
\tau_{n}(z)
has the following asymptotic behavior as
z\to\infty
:

\displaystyle\tau_{2s}(z)=K_{2s}z^{-s^{2}}\left[\mathbf{B}_{2s,s}(z)+2\sum_{r=0}^{s-1}\left(\mathbf{B}_{2s,r}(z)\cos(\psi_{2s,r}(z))+\mathbf{D}_{2s,r}(z)\sin(\psi_{2s,r}(z))\right)\right],

\displaystyle\tau_{2s-1}(z)=2K_{2s-1}z^{-\frac{(2s-1)^{2}}{4}}\sum_{r=0}^{s-1}\left(\mathbf{B}_{2s-1,r}(z)\cos(\psi_{2s-1,r}(z))+\mathbf{D}_{2s-1,r}(z)\sin(\psi_{2s-1,r}(z))\right),

where
K_{n}
is given by (
2.1
), the phase function is

\displaystyle\psi_{n,r}(z)=(n-2r)\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}z^{3/2}}{3}+\frac{n\pi}{4}\right)

(2.4)

and

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{n,r}(z)\sim M_{n,r}z^{\tfrac{3}{2}r(n-r)}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{b_{n,r}^{(j)}}{z^{\tfrac{3j}{2}}},\qquad\mathbf{D}_{n,r}(z)\sim M_{n,r}z^{\tfrac{3}{2}r(n-r)}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{d_{n,r}^{(j)}}{z^{\tfrac{3j}{2}}},

with

\displaystyle M_{n,r}=(-1)^{r(n-r)}2^{\frac{5}{2}r(n-r)}G(r+1)G(n-r+1),

(2.5)

and leading terms

\displaystyle b_{n,r}^{(0)}=\sum_{p=0}^{\left\lfloor{n/2}\right\rfloor}(-1)^{p}C_{1}^{2p}C_{2}^{n-2p}\sum_{q=\max(0,2p-r)}^{\min(2p,n-r)}(-1)^{q}{r\choose 2p-q}{n-r\choose q},

\displaystyle d_{n,r}^{(0)}=\sum_{p=0}^{\lfloor{n/2}\rfloor-1}(-1)^{p-1}C_{1}^{2p+1}C_{2}^{n-2p-1}\sum_{q=\max(0,2p+1-r)}^{\min(2p+1,n-r)}(-1)^{q}{r\choose 2p+1-q}{n-r\choose q}.

(2.6)

Remark 2.6
.

The general formula for the coefficients
b_{n,r}^{(0)}
and
d_{n,r}^{(0)}
is cumbersome, but it can be easily evaluated with symbolic software, and in many cases the sums reduce to just a few terms. Furthermore, several important simplifications can be made if
C_{2}=0
(pure
\operatorname{Ai}
function in the seed): in this case, only the term
p=\lfloor{n/2}\rfloor
survives,
b_{2s-1,r}^{(0)}=d_{2s,r}^{(0)}=0
, and

\displaystyle b_{2s,r}^{(0)}=(-1)^{s}C_{1}^{2s}\sum_{q=\max(0,2s-r)}^{2s-r}(-1)^{q}{r\choose 2p-q}{n-r\choose q},

\displaystyle d_{2s-1,r}^{(0)}=(-1)^{s}C_{1}^{2s-1}\sum_{q=\max(0,2s-r-1)}^{2s-r-1}(-1)^{q}{r\choose 2p+1-q}{n-r\choose q}.

As before, from this result about the tau function, we can deduce the asymptotic behavior of the Painlevé functions in the oscillatory regime.

Corollary 2.7
.

For
n\geq 1
and
s\geq 1
, the functions
q_{n}(z)
,
p_{n}(z)
and
\sigma_{n}(z)
in (
1.4
) admit the following asymptotic expansions as
z\to\infty
, excluding arbitrarily small but fixed neighborhoods of the poles of the leading terms in the approximations:

1.

If
C_{2}\neq 0
, then

\displaystyle\sigma_{2s}(z)=\frac{s^{2}}{2z}+\frac{s}{2z}\frac{b_{2s,s-1}^{(0)}\sin(\psi_{2s,s-1}(z))-d_{2s,s-1}^{(0)}\cos(\psi_{2s,s-1}(z))}{b_{2s,s}^{(0)}}+\mathcal{O}\big(z^{-5/2}\big),

\displaystyle\sigma_{2s-1}(z)=\sqrt{\frac{z}{2}}\frac{-b_{2s-1,s-1}^{(0)}\sin(\psi_{2s-1,s-1}(z))+d_{2s-1,s-1}^{(0)}\cos(\psi_{2s-1,s-1}(z))}{b_{2s-1,s-1}^{(0)}\cos(\psi_{2s-1,s-1}(z))+d_{2s-1,s-1}^{(0)}\sin(\psi_{2s-1,s-1}(z))}+\mathcal{O}\big(z^{-1}\big),

with coefficients given by (
2.6
) and phase function (
2.4
). Also,

\displaystyle p_{2s}(z)=-\frac{\sqrt{2}s}{\sqrt{z}}\frac{b_{2s,s-1}^{(0)}\cos(\psi_{2s,s-1}(z))+d_{2s,s-1}^{(0)}\sin(\psi_{2s,s-1}(z))}{b_{2s,s}^{(0)}}+\mathcal{O}\big(z^{-2}\big),

\displaystyle p_{2s-1}(z)=z\left[1+\left(\frac{(b_{2s-1,s-1}^{(0)}\sin(\psi_{2s-1,s-1}(z))-d_{2s-1,s-1}^{(0)}\cos(\psi_{2s-1,s-1}(z))}{b_{2s-1,s-1}^{(0)}\cos(\psi_{2s-1,s-1}(z))+d_{2s-1,s-1}^{(0)}\sin(\psi_{2s-1,s-1}(z))}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\hphantom{p_{2s-1}(z) =}{}+\mathcal{O}\big(z^{-1/2}\big),

(2.7)

and

\displaystyle q_{n}(z)=(-1)^{n}\sqrt{\frac{z}{2}}\frac{-b_{2s-1,s-1}^{(0)}\sin(\psi_{2s-1,s-1}(z))+d_{2s-1,s-1}^{(0)}\cos(\psi_{2s-1,s-1}(z))}{b_{2s-1,s-1}^{(0)}\cos(\psi_{2s-1,s-1}(z))+d_{2s-1,s-1}^{(0)}\sin(\psi_{2s-1,s-1}(z))}

\displaystyle\hphantom{q_{n}(z) =}{}+\mathcal{O}\big(z^{-1}\big),

(2.8)

where
s=\left\lceil{\frac{n}{2}}\right\rceil
.

2.

If
C_{2}=0
, then

\displaystyle\sigma_{2s}(z)=\frac{s^{2}}{2z}-\frac{s}{2z}\sin(\psi_{2s,s-1}(z))+\mathcal{O}\big(z^{-5/2}\big),

Proof of Theorem

We recall the classical integral representations of the Airy functions

\displaystyle\operatorname{Ai}(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\int_{\infty{\rm e}^{-\pi{\rm i}/3}}^{\infty{\rm e}^{\pi{\rm i}/3}}\exp\big(\tfrac{1}{3}t^{3}-zt\big)\mathrm{d}t,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Bi}(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty{\rm e}^{-\pi{\rm i}/3}}+\int_{-\infty}^{\infty{\rm e}^{\pi{\rm i}/3}}\right)\exp\big(\tfrac{1}{3}t^{3}-zt\big)\mathrm{d}t,

(3.1)

for

, see
[
30
, formulas (9.5.4) and (9.5.5)]
. Bearing in mind the form of the seed function (
1.2
), we take the weight function

\displaystyle w(t,z)=\exp\big(\tfrac{1}{3}t^{3}+2^{-1/3}zt\big),

(3.2)

and for
m\geq 0
we define the moments

\displaystyle\mu^{\operatorname{Ai}}_{m}(z)=\frac{C_{1}}{2\pi{\rm i}}\int_{\infty{\rm e}^{-\pi{\rm i}/3}}^{\infty{\rm e}^{\pi{\rm i}/3}}t^{m}w(t,z){\rm d}t,

\displaystyle\mu^{\operatorname{Bi}}_{m}(z)=\frac{C_{2}}{2\pi}\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty{\rm e}^{-\pi{\rm i}/3}}+\int_{-\infty}^{\infty{\rm e}^{\pi{\rm i}/3}}\right)t^{m}w(t,z){\rm d}t.

Then, we have
\varphi(z)=\mu^{\operatorname{Ai}}_{0}(z)+\mu^{\operatorname{Bi}}_{0}(z)
, cf. (
1.2
), and as a direct consequence,

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}^{k}}{{\rm d}z^{k}}\varphi(z)=2^{-\frac{k}{3}}\big[\mu^{\operatorname{Ai}}_{k}(z)+\mu^{\operatorname{Bi}}_{k}(z)\big],\qquad k\geq 0.

The Wronskian determinant constructed from the seed function and the Hankel determinant for the weight function (
3.2
) are therefore related as follows:

\displaystyle\tau_{n}(z)=\det\left(\frac{{\rm d}^{j+k}}{{\rm d}z^{j+k}}\varphi(z)\right)_{j,k=0}^{n-1}=2^{-\frac{n(n-1)}{3}}\det\big(\mu^{\operatorname{Ai}}_{j+k}(z)+\mu^{\operatorname{Bi}}_{j+k}(z)\big)_{j,k=0}^{n-1}.

Consider now that we have a total of
r
Airy
\operatorname{Ai}
integrals in the determinant,
0\leq r\leq n
, and consequently
n-r

Airy
\operatorname{Bi}
integrals. Then

\displaystyle\tau_{n}(z)=\frac{2^{-\frac{n(n-1)}{3}}}{(2\pi)^{n}}\sum_{r=0}^{n}\frac{C_{1}^{r}C_{2}^{n-r}}{{\rm i}^{r}}D_{n,r}(z),

(3.3)

where the Hankel determinants

can be written, following the standard theory
[
22
, Corollary 2.1.3]
, as

\displaystyle D_{n,r}(z)=\frac{1}{n!}\int\cdots\int\Delta_{n}(\mathbf{t})^{2}\prod_{k=1}^{n}w(t_{k},z){\rm d}t_{k},\qquad\Delta_{n}(\mathbf{t})=\prod_{1\leq j<k\leq n}(t_{k}-t_{j}),

(3.4)

where
r
integrals are taken along the path corresponding to
\operatorname{Ai}
and
n-r
along the one for
\operatorname{Bi}
, and we denote
\mathbf{t}=(t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{n})
. Note that the integrand is symmetric in the
n
variables (the Vandermonde determinant may change sign when permuting variables, but it appears squared), therefore we can suppose, without loss of generality, that the first
r
integrals correspond to
\operatorname{Ai}
and the last
n-r
integrals to
\operatorname{Bi}
, and then sum over the possible permutations.

Using the scaled variables
\mathbf{t}=2^{-1/6}\sqrt{\rho}\mathbf{u}
, where

\displaystyle z=-\rho{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\alpha},\qquad\rho\geq 0,\qquad|\alpha|<\frac{\pi}{3},

cf.
[
7
, Section 7.3]
, we obtain

\displaystyle D_{n,r}(z)=\frac{\left(2^{-1/6}\rho^{1/2}\right)^{n^{2}}}{n!}{n\choose r}\int_{\Gamma_{\alpha}}\cdots\int_{\Gamma_{\alpha}}\Delta_{n}(\mathbf{u})^{2}\exp\left(\frac{\rho^{3/2}}{\sqrt{2}}\phi(\mathbf{u})\right)\prod_{k=1}^{n}{\rm d}u_{k},

where the phase function is

\displaystyle\phi(\mathbf{u})=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left[\frac{1}{3}u_{k}^{3}-{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\alpha}u_{k}\right],

and
\Gamma_{\alpha}
is any smooth infinite path that joins the sectors
\infty{\rm e}^{-\pi{\rm i}/3}
and
\infty{\rm e}^{\pi{\rm i}/3}
(for the
\operatorname{Ai}
case) and the sectors
-\infty
and
\infty{\rm e}^{\pm\pi{\rm i}/3}
(for the
\operatorname{Bi}
case).

Clearly, the gradient and Hessian of this function are

\displaystyle\nabla\phi(\mathbf{u})=\big(u_{1}^{2}-{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\alpha},\ldots,u_{n}^{2}-{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\alpha}\big),\qquad H\phi(\mathbf{u})=2\operatorname{diag}(u_{k})_{k=1,\ldots,n}.

The stationary points

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}^{*}=(u_{\pm},\ldots,u_{\pm}),\qquad u_{\pm}=\pm{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\alpha/2}.

(with any combination of signs) are non-degenerate, since
\det H\phi(\mathbf{u}^{*})\neq 0
.

For
|\alpha|<\frac{\pi}{3}
, the main contribution to each
\operatorname{Bi}
integral is given by the stationary point
u_{k-}
, since
\textrm{Re}(\phi(u_{k-}))>0
and
\textrm{Re}(\phi(u_{k+}))<0
, and it will appear doubled because of the two paths joining

and
\infty{\rm e}^{\pm\pi{\rm i}/3}
in (
3.1
); for the
\operatorname{Ai}
integrals, the relevant stationary point is
u_{k+}
, since path deformation through

would change the asymptotic behavior (from exponentially decreasing to exponentially increasing). Therefore, for the asymptotic analysis we need to consider stationary points of the form

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}^{(r)}=(\underbrace{u_{+},\ldots,u_{+}}_{r\,\text{times}},\underbrace{u_{-},\ldots,u_{-}}_{n-r\,\text{times}}).

(3.5)

We add and subtract the value of the phase function at this point, to obtain

\displaystyle D_{n,r}(z)=\frac{\left(2^{-1/6}\rho^{1/2}\right)^{n^{2}}}{r!(n-r)!}\exp\left(\frac{\rho^{3/2}}{\sqrt{2}}\phi(\mathbf{u}^{(r)})\right)

\displaystyle\hphantom{D_{n,r}(z) =}{}\times\int_{\Gamma_{\alpha}}\cdots\int_{\Gamma_{\alpha}}\Delta_{n}(\mathbf{u})^{2}\exp\left(\frac{\rho^{3/2}}{\sqrt{2}}\big[\phi(\mathbf{u})-\phi\big(\mathbf{u}^{(r)}\big)\big]\right)\prod_{k=1}^{n}{\rm d}u_{k}.

By analyticity of the integrand in all the variables

, the precise structure of these global paths is not relevant for the analysis, as long as they connect the correct sectors in the complex plane, since we can deform the contours sequentially in the different variables. We will use this freedom to integrate along the paths of steepest descent through the points
\mathbf{u}^{(r)}
. These paths, denoted here by
\Gamma_{\alpha}
, are implicitly given by the equation

\displaystyle\operatorname{Im}\phi(\mathbf{u})=\operatorname{Im}\phi\big(\mathbf{u}^{(r)}\big),

is real-valued on

\alpha
, see the discussion in
[
7
, Section 7.3]
, however, for asymptotic approximations we only need their existence around the stationary points.

We isolate the stationary points by fixing
\delta>0

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\alpha,\delta}=\Gamma_{\alpha}\cap D\big(\mathbf{u}^{(r)},\delta\big),

\displaystyle D_{n,r}(z)=\exp\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{3}(n-2r)(-z)^{3/2}\right)[I_{n,r}(z)+E_{n,r}(z)],

\displaystyle I_{n,r}(z)=\frac{\left(2^{-1/6}\rho^{1/2}\right)^{n^{2}}}{{r!(n-r)!}}\int_{\Gamma_{\alpha,\delta}}\Delta_{n}(\mathbf{u})^{2}\exp\left(\frac{\rho^{3/2}}{\sqrt{2}}\big[\phi(\mathbf{u})-\phi\big(\mathbf{u}^{(r)}\big)\big]\right)\prod_{k=1}^{n}{\rm d}u_{k},

and

It is important to note that we need to choose

in such a way that the remainder

is exponentially small with respect to

the exponential terms that are present in (

), that is,
E_{n,r}(z)=o\big(\exp\big({-}\frac{\sqrt{2}n}{3}(-z)^{3/2}\big)\big)

We apply a final change of variables to transform the exponential terms in the integral into Gaussians, which is a particular case of Morse lemma in the literature

\displaystyle\phi(\mathbf{u})-\phi\big(\mathbf{u}^{(r)}\big)=-\sum_{k=1}^{n}v^{2}_{k}.

This change of variable can be written by components, and as

\displaystyle u_{k}=u_{+}+{\rm i}{\rm e}^{-\frac{\alpha{\rm i}}{4}}v_{k}+\frac{{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}\alpha}}{6}v_{k}^{2}+\mathcal{O}\big(v_{k}^{3}\big),\qquad u_{k}=u_{-}+{\rm e}^{-\frac{\alpha{\rm i}}{4}}v_{k}+\frac{{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}\alpha}}{6}v_{k}^{2}+\mathcal{O}\big(v_{k}^{3}\big).

This maps the contour
\Gamma_{\alpha,\delta}
onto
[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]^{n}
on the real axis, for some

. Then, with an exponentially small error again, we can extend the integrals to the whole real axis, using a standard estimate: for
\varepsilon>1
,
C=\frac{\rho^{3/2}}{\sqrt{2}}>0
, and

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(v){\rm e}^{-Cv^{2}}{\rm d}v-\int_{-\varepsilon}^{\varepsilon}f(v){\rm e}^{-Cv^{2}}{\rm d}v=2\int_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}f(v){\rm e}^{-Cv^{2}}{\rm d}v\leq 2\int_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}f(v){\rm e}^{-Cv}{\rm d}v.

The last integral can be written in terms of incomplete Gamma functions (see
[
30
, Sections 8.2 and 8.11]
for definitions and asymptotics) and a similar argument can be used in
n

3.6

In order to study the Vandermonde determinant, we split it in three parts, separating those terms that combine two

or two

\displaystyle\Delta_{n}(\mathbf{u})^{2}=\prod_{1\leq j<k\leq r}(u_{k}-u_{j})^{2}\prod_{r+1\leq j<k\leq n}(u_{k}-u_{j})^{2}\prod_{1\leq j\leq r,r+1\leq k\leq n}(u_{k}-u_{j})^{2}.

This term, together with the differentials, becomes

\displaystyle\Delta_{n}(\mathbf{u})^{2}\prod_{k=1}^{n}{\rm d}u_{k}=2^{2r(n-r)}{\rm e}^{\frac{\pi{\rm i}}{2}r^{2}-\frac{\alpha{\rm i}}{4}(n^{2}+6r(n-r))}

where

\displaystyle\Xi(\mathbf{v})=1+\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}v_{k}+\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}b_{j,k}v_{j}v_{k}+\cdots,\qquad|v|\to 0,

for some coefficients

and

whose exact form is not relevant for the leading term in the asymptotic expansion. This leads to two decoupled Selberg integrals, and writing everything together we obtain

\displaystyle I_{n,r}(z)=\big(2^{-1/6}\rho^{1/2}\big)^{n^{2}}\exp\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}(n-2r)}{3}(-z)^{3/2}-\left(\frac{n^{2}}{4}+\frac{3r(n-r)}{2}\right)\alpha{\rm i}+\frac{r^{2}\pi{\rm i}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\hphantom{I_{n,r}(z)=}{}\times 2^{(n-r)(2r+1)}\frac{S_{r}(z)S_{n-r}(z)}{r!(n-r)!}\big(1+\mathcal{O}\big(\rho^{-3/2}\big)\big),

(3.7)

where

\displaystyle S_{d}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Delta_{d}(v)^{2}\prod_{k=1}^{d}{\rm e}^{-Cv_{k}^{2}}{\rm d}v_{k}=\frac{(2\pi)^{d/2}}{(2C)^{d^{2}/2}}\prod_{k=1}^{d}k!=\frac{(2\pi)^{d/2}}{(2C)^{d^{2}/2}}G(d+2),

(3.8)

for

and
\operatorname{Re}C>0
, in terms of the Barnes

function, see
[
30
, Section 5.17]
. Identifying

and

, we have

\displaystyle I_{n,r}(z)=2^{-\frac{5n^{2}}{12}+\frac{n}{2}+(n-r)(\frac{5}{2}r+1)}\pi^{\frac{n}{2}}(-z)^{-\frac{n^{2}}{4}-\frac{3}{2}r(n-r)}G(r+1)G(n-r+1)

\displaystyle\hphantom{I_{n,r}(z) =}{}\times\exp\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}(n-2r)}{3}(-z)^{3/2}+\frac{r^{2}\pi{\rm i}}{2}\right)\big(1+\mathcal{O}\big((-z)^{-3/2}\big)\big).

Combining the powers of

\pi

This calculation gives very relevant information about the remainder as well: the order of the error in (

(which integrate to

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}v_{k}^{2}{\rm e}^{-Cv_{k}^{2}}{\rm d}v_{k}=\frac{1}{2C}\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\rm e}^{-Cv_{k}^{2}}{\rm d}v_{k},\qquad\operatorname{Re}C>0,

will contribute to an error of order

as

, since in our situation we have

. This is true for higher order terms as well, so each exponential level in (

, which appears in the coefficients

in (

In the case

\displaystyle I_{n,r}(z)=2^{-\frac{5n^{2}}{12}+\frac{n}{2}}\pi^{\frac{n}{2}}\,z^{-\frac{n^{2}}{4}-\frac{3}{2}r(n-r)}\exp\left[(n-2r){\rm i}\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}z^{3/2}}{3}+\frac{n\pi}{4}\right)\right]M_{n,r}\sum_{p=0}^{n}H_{n,r,p}

Finally, the asymptotic expansion can be written in terms of sines and cosines, instead of complex exponentials, noting that

\displaystyle H_{n,r,p}=\sum_{q=0}^{p}{r\choose p-q}{n-r\choose q}(-1)^{q},

). If

, then

\displaystyle H_{n,r,p}=\sum_{q=p-r}^{p}{r\choose p-q}{n-r\choose q}(-1)^{q},

and

\displaystyle H_{n,n-r,p}=\sum_{q=0}^{r}{n-r\choose p-q}{r\choose q}(-1)^{q}=\sum_{q=0}^{r}{n-r\choose p-r+q}{r\choose r-q}(-1)^{r-q}

\displaystyle\hphantom{H_{n,n-r,p}}{}=\sum_{s=p-r}^{p}{n-r\choose s}{r\choose p-s}(-1)^{p-s}=(-1)^{p}H_{n,r,p},

reversing the sum in the first step and shifting
q=s-p+r
in the second one.

The case
p>n-r
can be proved in a similar way.

5
Proof of Corollaries
2.4
and
2.7

In the non-oscillatory regime, we can derive asymptotic expansions for the Painlevé functions
\sigma_{n}(z)
,
p_{n}(z)
and
q_{n}(z)
in (
1.4
) quite straightforwardly. We will use the asymptotic expansions for
\tau_{n}(z)
and (
1.4
), observing that differentiation is permitted since
\tau_{n}(z)
is an analytic function of
z
inside the relevant sectors, see
[
29
, Section 1.8]
. In the oscillatory regime, one would need to open a sector around the positive real axis; this calculation is similar to the oscillatory case, writing
z=\rho{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\alpha}
,
|\alpha|<\frac{\pi}{3}
and considering both stationary points.

Suppose first that
C_{2}\neq 0
. Instead of working directly with the asymptotic expansion (
2.2
), it is simpler to pick the leading term therein, corresponding to
r=0
, i.e.

\displaystyle\tau_{n}(z)=K_{n}\mathbf{A}_{n,0}(z){\rm e}^{\frac{\sqrt{2}}{3}n(-z)^{\frac{3}{2}}}+\mathcal{O}\Big({\rm e}^{\frac{\sqrt{2}}{3}(n-2)(-z)^{\frac{3}{2}}}\Big)

as
|z|\to\infty
in the sector
|\arg(-z)|<\frac{\pi}{3}
. Using this result, we can deduce the form of the asymptotic expansion for the function
\sigma_{n}(z)
, which is

\displaystyle\sigma_{n}(z)=\frac{\tau_{n}^{\prime}(z)}{\tau_{n}(z)}=\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}s_{n,k}(-z)^{\tfrac{1}{2}-\tfrac{3k}{2}}+\mathcal{O}\Big((-z)^{\tfrac{1}{2}-\tfrac{3K}{2}}\Big),\qquad K\geq 1.

(5.1)

Then, substituting this expansion into the differential equation (
1.6
), we can identify the coefficients
s_{n,k}
. Then, we can compute

\displaystyle p_{n}(z)=-2\sigma^{\prime}_{n}(z)=-2\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}p_{n,k}(-z)^{-\tfrac{1}{2}-\tfrac{3k}{2}}+\mathcal{O}\left((-z)^{\tfrac{1}{2}-\tfrac{3K}{2}}\right),

where the coefficients
p_{n,k}
follow easily from
s_{n,k}
. Finally, from (
1.4
) again, we have

\displaystyle q_{n}(z)=\sigma_{n-1}(z)-\sigma_{n}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}q_{n,k}(-z)^{-\tfrac{1}{2}-\tfrac{3k}{2}}+\mathcal{O}\Big((-z)^{\tfrac{1}{2}-\tfrac{3K}{2}}\Big),

with coefficients that follow from
s_{n,k}
once again.

In the case
C_{2}=0
, we can use a similar argument, but with the form

\displaystyle\tau_{n}(z)=K_{n}\mathbf{A}_{n,n}(z){\rm e}^{-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{3}n(-z)^{\frac{3}{2}}}.

The negative exponential term naturally leads to changes in the coefficients, but the form of the asymptotic expansion is the same.

In the oscillatory regime, calculations are more delicate, and the main difficulty is to establish a general pattern for the asymptotic expansion, as we did before in (
5.1
). The reason for this is that in Theorem
2.5
there is a clear leading term, but as we expand further, different trigonometric functions will be involved. These higher order terms can be computed using symbolic software, but for brevity we will give only the leading terms.

The leading terms in Theorem
2.5
clearly correspond to
r=s
if
n=2s
is even, and to
r=s-1
if
n=2s-1
is odd. For
s\geq 1
, we have

\displaystyle\sigma_{2s}(z)=\frac{\tau_{2s}^{\prime}(z)}{\tau_{2s}(z)}=\frac{s^{2}}{2z}+\frac{s}{2z}\,\frac{b_{2s,s-1}^{(0)}\sin(\psi_{2s,s-1}(z))-d_{2s,s-1}^{(0)}\cos(\psi_{2s,s-1}(z))}{b_{2s,s}^{(0)}}+\mathcal{O}\big(z^{-5/2}\big),

with coefficients given by (
2.6
) and phase function (
2.4
).

If
C_{2}=0
, this expression simplifies considerably, since only the term
p=s
survives, and then
b_{2s,s}^{(0)}=-b_{2s,s-1}^{(0)}=C_{1}^{2s}
and
d_{2s,s-1}^{(0)}=0
, so

\displaystyle\sigma_{2s}(z)=\frac{s^{2}}{2z}-\frac{s}{2z}\sin(\psi_{2s,s-1}(z))+\mathcal{O}\big(z^{-5/2}\big).

This is in agreement with
[
13
, Theorem 9]
, correcting the phase function. In the odd case, we have

\displaystyle\sigma_{2s-1}(z)=\sqrt{\frac{z}{2}}\frac{-b_{2s-1,s-1}^{(0)}\sin(\psi_{2s-1,s-1}(z))+d_{2s-1,s-1}^{(0)}\cos(\psi_{2s-1,s-1}(z))}{b_{2s-1,s-1}^{(0)}\cos(\psi_{2s-1,s-1}(z))+d_{2s-1,s-1}^{(0)}\sin(\psi_{2s-1,s-1}(z))}+\mathcal{O}\big(z^{-1}\big).

If
C_{2}=0
, this expression simplifies again

\displaystyle\sigma_{2s-1}(z)=\sqrt{\frac{z}{2}}\cot(\psi_{2s-1,s-1}(z))+\mathcal{O}\big(z^{-1}\big).

In both cases, the asymptotic approximation is valid away from the zeros of the denominators that appear in the leading terms, we refer the reader to
[
29
, Chapter 1, Section 8.1]
for the general theory.

Similar calculations lead to the asymptotic expansions for

, although care is needed because the leading terms may come from subleading ones before, as a result of differentiation of the trigonometric functions. Straightforward manipulations, using the fact that

and

, which follows from (

), (
2.8
), (

), and (

Higher order terms in these asymptotic expansions can be computed by using the corresponding differential equations. This is quite straightforward in the non-oscillatory regime, but more involved in the oscillatory one, since several trigonometric functions with different phase functions intervene.

Appendix A
Stokes phenomenon for the
\boldsymbol{\operatorname{Ai}}
solution

The case
C_{2}=0
, where the seed function only contains the Airy
\operatorname{Ai}
functions, is especially relevant both because of its asymptotic behavior and in applications. It is interesting to observe that in this case we have strong asymptotics for
\tau_{n}(z)
in the whole cut plane
|\arg(-z)|<\pi
involving only one exponential factor, see (
2.3
); however, we can also use the rotational symmetry given by Lemma
2.1
, to study the asymptotics in the regions
0<\arg z<\frac{2\pi}{3}
and
-\frac{2\pi}{3}<\arg z<0
. This calculation uses a different seed function in the original sector
|\arg(-z)|<\frac{\pi}{3}
and it gives subdominant exponential terms. These do not affect the leading asymptotic behavior, but they give a non-linear Stokes phenomenon for this family of solutions of
{\rm P}_{\rm II}
, in the spirit of Its and Kapaev
[
24
]
, see also
[
17
, Chapter 11]
.

For instance, if we consider the sector
0<\arg z<\frac{2\pi}{3}
and the seed function with
\widetilde{C}_{1}=1
and
\widetilde{C}_{2}=0

(corresponding to the pure Airy
\operatorname{Ai}
function in this rotated sector), we have from (
2.3
) the following asymptotic approximation

\displaystyle\tau_{n}(z)=K_{n}(-z)^{-\frac{n^{2}}{4}}\mathbf{A}_{n,n}(z){\rm e}^{-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{3}n(-z)^{\frac{3}{2}}}

\displaystyle\hphantom{\tau_n(z)}{}=(-1)^{\left\lfloor{n/2}\right\rfloor}K_{n}G(n+1)(-z)^{-\frac{n^{2}}{4}}\big(1+\mathcal{O}\big((-z)^{-\frac{3}{2}}\big)\big){\rm e}^{-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{3}n(-z)^{\frac{3}{2}}}.

However, applying Lemma
2.1
, we can use
\tau_{n}(z)={\rm e}^{\frac{2\pi{\rm i}}{3}n(n-1)}\tau_{n}\big(z{\rm e}^{\frac{2\pi{\rm i}}{3}}\big)
, and then the seed function in the region
|\arg(-z)|<\frac{\pi}{3}
is
\varphi\big(z{\rm e}^{\frac{2\pi{\rm i}}{3}}\big)
, with constants

\displaystyle C_{1}=\tfrac{1}{2}{\rm e}^{-\frac{\pi{\rm i}}{3}},\qquad C_{2}=\tfrac{1}{2}{\rm e}^{\frac{\pi{\rm i}}{6}}.

After simplification, the first two coefficients
\mathbf{A}_{n,0}\big(z{\rm e}^{\frac{2\pi{\rm i}}{3}}\big)
and
\mathbf{A}_{n,1}\big(z{\rm e}^{\frac{2\pi{\rm i}}{3}}\big)
give

\displaystyle\tau_{n}(z)=(-1)^{\left\lfloor{n/2}\right\rfloor}K_{n}G(n+1)(-z)^{-\frac{n^{2}}{4}}{\rm e}^{-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{3}n(-z)^{\frac{3}{2}}}

\displaystyle\hphantom{\tau_n(z)=}{}\times\left[1+(-1)^{n}\frac{2^{\frac{5n-7}{2}}{\rm i}(-z)^{\frac{3}{2}(n-1)}}{\Gamma(n)}{\rm e}^{\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{3}(-z)^{\frac{3}{2}}}\right]\big(1+\mathcal{O}\big((-z)^{-\frac{3}{2}}\big)\big).

Other exponentially small contributions can be calculated in a similar way. As a consequence, we have a similar Stokes phenomenon for the Painlevé functions:

\displaystyle\sigma_{n}(z)=-\frac{n(-z)^{1/2}}{\sqrt{2}}\left[1+(-1)^{n+1}\frac{2^{\frac{5n-5}{2}}{\rm i}}{\Gamma(n+1)}(-z)^{\frac{3}{2}(n-1)}{\rm e}^{\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{3}(-z)^{\frac{3}{2}}}\right]\big(1+\mathcal{O}\big((-z)^{-\frac{3}{2}}\big)\big),

\displaystyle p_{n}(z)=-\frac{n}{\sqrt{2}(-z)^{1/2}}\left[1+(-1)^{n+1}\frac{2^{\frac{5n}{2}-2}{\rm i}}{\Gamma(n)}(-z)^{\frac{3}{2}n}{\rm e}^{\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{3}(-z)^{\frac{3}{2}}}\right]\big(1+\mathcal{O}\big((-z)^{-\frac{3}{2}}\big)\big),

and

\displaystyle q_{n}(z)=\frac{(-z)^{1/2}}{\sqrt{2}}\left[1+(-1)^{n+1}\frac{2^{\frac{5n-5}{2}}{\rm i}}{\Gamma(n)}(-z)^{\frac{3}{2}(n-1)}{\rm e}^{\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{3}(-z)^{\frac{3}{2}}}\right]\big(1+\mathcal{O}\big((-z)^{-\frac{3}{2}}\big)\big),

for
n\geq 1
.

Similar calculations can be carried out in the sector
\frac{4\pi}{3}<\arg z<2\pi
, using the seed function
\varphi\big(z{\rm e}^{-\frac{2\pi{\rm i}}{3}}\big)
with constants

\displaystyle C_{1}=\tfrac{1}{2}{\rm e}^{\frac{\pi{\rm i}}{3}},\qquad C_{2}=\tfrac{1}{2}{\rm e}^{-\frac{\pi{\rm i}}{6}}.

As mentioned before, these extra exponential terms are not strictly needed in the non-oscillatory regime
|\arg(-z)|<\pi
, but they become relevant when one examines the transition between exponential and trigonometric behavior.

An alternative way to calculate these subleading exponential terms would be to extend the asymptotic expansion obtained in the oscillatory regime, with trigonometric functions, to a wider sector in
\mathbb{C}
around the positive real axis. Then in the overlapping region between non-oscillatory and oscillatory behavior the two asymptotic expansions should coincide, and it would be possible to extract the subleading terms.
