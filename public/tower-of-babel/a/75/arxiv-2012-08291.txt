Approximation of BV functions by neural networks A regularity theory approach
Benny Avelin, Vesa Julin

Abstract
. In this paper we are concerned with the approximation of functions by single hidden layer neural networks with ReLU activation functions on the unit circle. In particular, we are interested in the case when the number of data-points exceeds the number of nodes. We first study the convergence to equilibrium of the stochastic gradient flow associated with the cost function with a quadratic penalization. Specifically, we prove a Poincaré inequality for a penalized version of the cost function with explicit constants that are independent of the data and of the number of nodes. As our penalization biases the weights to be bounded, this leads us to study how well a network with bounded weights can approximate a given function of bounded variation (BV). Our main contribution concerning approximation of BV functions, is a result which we call the localization theorem . Specifically, it states that the expected error of the constrained problem, where the length of the weights are less than R R , is of order R − 1 / 9 R^{-1/9} with respect to the unconstrained problem (the global optimum). The proof is novel in this topic and is inspired by techniques from regularity theory of elliptic partial differential equations. Finally we quantify the value of the global optimum by proving a quantitative version of the universal approximation theorem.

1.
Introduction

This paper is concerned with a supervised learning problem, where the goal is to learn a function from data. We adopt the viewpoint of function approximation. Specifically, we consider
x\in\mathbf{S}^{1}
, and a function of bounded variation, or BV-function for short,
y:\mathbf{S}^{1}\to\mathbb{R}
, where
\mathbf{S}^{1}
is the unit circle in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. The goal is to approximate the function
y
in the space of two layer neural networks (single hidden layer) with ReLU activation on the unit circle. To be specific let us define our space of functions as

(1.1)

f_{W,a}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}\sigma(w_{i}\cdot x),

where the vectors
w_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
W=(w_{1},\dots,w_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{2m}
, denote the weights, and the coefficients
a_{i}\in\{-1,1\}
,
a=(a_{1},\dots,a_{m})
, are given and the activation function is
\sigma(t)=\max\{t,0\}
. We denote the set of functions formed in
Eq.

1.1
as
\mathcal{H}_{m,a}
.
This particular set of neural networks on the unit circle (or unit sphere) has previously been studied e.g. in
[
4
,
16
]
. Note that the network in
Eq.

1.1
does not have a bias, which is a simplifying assumption. However, it turns out that this space is still rich enough to approximate any antipodally symmetric function. We assume that the function
y
is in BV, which is a natural assumption as this allows it to take discrete values which is common in classification problems. Moreover, since the Fourier coefficients
a_{k}
of a periodic BV-function decays as
k^{-1}
, this class contains a periodic Barron type space,
[
8
]
.

We define the cost function
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{2m}\to\mathbb{R}
as

(1.2)

\Phi(W):=\int_{\mathbf{S}^{1}}|f_{W,a}(x)-y(x)|^{2}d\mu(x)=\|f_{W,a}-y\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}

and consider the following
L^{2}
approximation problem

(1.3)

\inf_{W}\Phi(W).

In
Eq.

1.2

\mu
is the data-specific measure, which we assume to be a probability measure on
\mathbf{S}^{1}
. In particular,
\mu
can be discrete or absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure. This covers all the interesting cases as we have no restriction on
\mu
.

The paper is divided into two parts. We first consider first-order methods with noise (Langevin dynamics) to find the minimum of
Eq.

1.3
.
It turns out that for a general probability measure
\mu
we need to add a penalization on the cost function
Eq.

1.2
in order to have a uniform convergence rate (see
Example

1.1
). This is in contrast to
[
16
]
where the authors assume a non-degeneracy condition on the empirical measure, i.e. that the points of support are mutually non-parallel. It is clear that when the number of data points grows to infinity we will lose the non-degeneracy condition. In this direction and for a general probability measure
\mu
, we prove in
Theorem

1
the exponential convergence of the stochastic gradient flow associated with
Eq.

1.3
under quadratic penalization.

In the second part we study the value of
Eq.

1.3
, with and without penalization, which we interpret as function approximation problems. As our penalization biases the weights to have bounded length, we first estimate in
Theorem

2
the expected error that we make when we approximate
y
under the constraint that the functions in our network
\mathcal{H}_{m,a}
have uniformly bounded weights. We call this
the Localization theorem
and it is the main result of this paper. To the best of our knowledge the closest related result in the literature is
[
10
]
. Finally in
Theorem

3
we prove a uniform approximation theorem where we estimate the value of
Eq.

1.3
in terms of number of (alternating) nodes. This is a quantitative form of the universal approximation theorem
[
8
]
, see also
[
40
]
.

1.1.
Statement of main results

Convergence of stochastic first order methods

.

Consider the following stochastic optimization of the minimization problem
Eq.

1.3
:

\displaystyle dW_{t}=-\nabla\Phi(W)dt+\sqrt{2}\varepsilon dB_{t},

where
dB_{t}
is the standard isotropic Wiener noise. We remark that the above SDE is basic in many areas of physics and can be considered as a weak approximation to Stochastic Gradient Descent, as in
[
5
,
32
,
33
,
41
]
. The density
\rho(W,t)
of
W_{t}
satisfies the following partial differential equation (PDE), called the Fokker-Planck or forward Kolmogorov equation,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}=\nabla\cdot\left(\rho\nabla\Phi+\varepsilon^{2}\nabla\rho\right).

The smoothness imposed by the isotropic noise term is crucial and it seems the way forward when dealing with general data-measures
\mu
. Naturally the noise with
\varepsilon>0
also helps the flow not to get stuck near local minimum points.

The convergence rate of the density
\rho(\cdot,t)
to an equilibrium measure
\rho_{\infty}
and its relation to Poincaré inequalities (or equivalently spectral gaps) and logarithmic Sobolev inequalities is a well studied problem in both stochastics and in PDEs, see e.g.
[
1
,
2
,
6
,
9
,
11
,
12
,
15
,
20
,
35
,
43
,
45
]
. In particular, if we want to prove an exponential convergence rate for any initial distribution
\rho_{0}
,
\Phi
needs to be a confining potential, i.e. it needs to grow at infinity with at least linear rate
[
43
]
. This is the motivation for studying
\Phi
in depth.

Let us first discuss about the minimization problem
Eq.

1.3
itself, and, in particular, why it is in general not well posed. By this we mean that the problem does not always admit a minimizer and the gradient flow may not converge. The reason for this is that
\Phi
is not coercive, which causes problems as the following example shows.

Example 1.1
.

We consider
\mathcal{H}_{2,a}
, which is the simplest non-trivial neural network, and assume that
\mu
is the normalized Lebesgue measure. Let
a_{1}=1
and
a_{2}=-1
and choose

\displaystyle y(x)=y(x_{1},x_{2})=\mathbb{I}\{x_{2}\geq 0\}x_{1},

where
\mathbb{I}
denotes the indicator function. Note that
y
is not continuous but it is a function of bounded variation. Then
\Phi(W)>0
for all
W\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
but

\displaystyle\inf_{W\in\mathbb{R}^{4}}\Phi(W)=0.

In particular, the function
\Phi
does not have a global minimum. Moreover, there is a point
W_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
such that the gradient flow

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}W_{t}=-\nabla\Phi(W_{t}),

staring from
W_{0}
diverges, i.e.,

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}|W_{t}|=\infty.

Let us briefly explain why the example holds. First, since
y
is not continuous and every
f_{W,a}\in\mathcal{H}_{m,a}
is Lipschitz continuous, it is clear that for every
W
we have
\Phi(W)>0
. To see that the infimum is zero we notice that the following holds:

\displaystyle\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{\sigma(x_{2}+hx_{1})-\sigma(x_{2})}{h}=\sigma^{\prime}(x_{2})x_{1}=\mathbb{I}\{x_{2}\geq 0\}x_{1},

pointwise a.e.
x
. Therefore, by recalling that
f_{W,a}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\sigma(w_{1}\cdot x)-\sigma(w_{2}\cdot x))
we have by choosing
w_{1}=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{h}e_{2}+\sqrt{2}e_{1}
and
w_{2}=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{h}e_{2}
that

\displaystyle f_{W,a}(x)=\sigma(\frac{1}{h}x_{2}+x_{1})-\sigma(\frac{1}{h}x_{2})=\frac{\sigma(x_{2}+x_{1})-\sigma(x_{2})}{h}\to\mathbb{I}\{x_{2}\geq 0\}x_{1}.

The claim
\inf\Phi(W)=0
then follows by the dominated convergence theorem. This shows that the minimization problem
Eq.

1.3
does not, in general, have a minimum.
Example

1.1
also claims that the gradient flow does not in general converge. We prove this in
Lemma

3.2
in
Section

3
. Furthermore, if the target function is a neural network in the class
\mathcal{H}_{m,a}
, then the global minimum is only unique up to certain symmetries, see for instance
[
18
,
17
,
44
]
.

Example

1.1
shows that for a generic probability measure
\mu
the minimization problem
Eq.

1.3
is surprisingly complicated already in the two node case. We do not try to classify all critical points of
Eq.

1.3
but
Example

1.1
shows that the problem is not coercive, does not have global minimum point, and the gradient flow may diverge. This suggests that without any assumptions on the data-points, the exponential convergence proved in
[
16
]
probably fails.

As mentioned before, in order for the density of the Fokker-Planck equation to converge exponentially fast to equilibrium, the potential needs to be confining, but
Example

1.1
shows that this is not the case. Motivated by this, we modify the cost function
Eq.

1.3
by adding a penalization, similar to
[
5
]
, and define for a chosen large parameter
R

(1.4)

\Phi_{R}(W):=\max\{\Phi(W),4(|W|^{2}-R^{2})\}.

Now it is trivial to see that for every
R>0
, the problem

(1.5)

\inf_{W\in\mathbb{R}^{2m}}\Phi_{R}(W)

has a global minimum, and at a minimum point
W_{R}
the weight is bounded by
|W_{R}|\leq R+\|y\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}
.

In the first result we study the convergence of the stochastic gradient flow of the penalized cost function

(1.6)

dW_{t}=-\nabla\Phi_{R}(W)dt+\sqrt{2}\varepsilon dB_{t}

with fixed small
0<\varepsilon\leq 1
. Again we denote the density of
W_{t}
by
\rho(W,t)
.

Theorem 1
.

Let us fix
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
and choose
R\geq 10
. Let
\Phi_{R}
be the penalized cost function defined by
Eqs.

1.2
and
1.4
, and assume
\|y\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}\leq 1
. Assume that
\rho(W,t)
is the density of
W_{t}
which is the solution of
Eq.

1.6
with initial datum
\rho_{0}
. Then
\rho(\cdot,t)\to\rho_{\infty}
, where
\rho_{\infty}(W)=e^{-\Phi_{R}(W)/\varepsilon^{2}}
, exponentially fast and we have the estimate

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2m}}|\rho(\cdot,t)-\rho_{\infty}|^{2}e^{\Phi_{R}/\varepsilon^{2}}\,dW\leq e^{-Ct}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2m}}|\rho_{0}-\rho_{\infty}|^{2}e^{\Phi_{R}/\varepsilon^{2}}\,dW.

When the number of nodes satisfies
m\geq 24R^{2}\varepsilon^{-2}
, the constant
C
is bounded by

\displaystyle C\geq\frac{1}{70}.

Otherwise we have the bound
C\geq 16e^{-(4R^{2}+2)\varepsilon^{-2}}
.

The strength of
Theorem

1
is that we obtain the exponential convergence of the stochastic gradient flow
Eq.

1.6
and the rate is independent of the number of nodes and on the distribution of the data points. The latter follows from the fact that the rate does not depend on the probability measure
\mu
. In addition,
Theorem

1
implies that when the number of nodes is large enough with respect to
\varepsilon^{-1}
and
R
, then the stochastic gradient flow
Eq.

1.6
converges with a uniform speed (in distribution). The convergence rate in
Theorem

1
does however depend on
\varepsilon
and on
R
, but this is necessary. Indeed,
Example

1.1
indicates that the dependence on
R
is necessary, since otherwise it is not clear if the flow even converges.

We prove
Theorem

1
using Aida’s perturbation argument
[
1
]
. Specifically, we consider
\Phi_{R}
as a perturbation of a quadratic potential which satisfies a dimensionally independent log-Sobolev inequality.
Furthermore, when the dimension is high,
\Phi_{R}
can be seen as a local perturbation of the quadratic potential. For this reason we are able to obtain a dimension-free bound on the spectral gap when
m\geq R^{2}\varepsilon^{-2}
. We note that it would be interesting to consider other penalizations, e.g. the linear growth penalization
c|W|
or the quadratic penalization
c|W|^{2}
. Unfortunately, in both of these cases the perturbation proof breaks down when
c
is small. For linear growth potentials the dimensionally independent result is still lacking, and the best known result can be found in
[
6
,
7
,
46
]
.

Function approximation

.

Theorem

1
provides a uniform rate of convergence to equilibrium of
Eq.

1.6
. Furthermore, note that when
\varepsilon>0
is small the equilibrium density
\rho_{\infty}
is concentrated near the global minimum of the cost function
\Phi_{R}
defined in
Eq.

1.4
. However, this still leaves open the question, what is the minimum value
Eq.

1.5
and how close it is to
Eq.

1.3
? We restate this question in an equivalent way as a problem of function approximation, i.e., how well can we approximate a given function
y
using functions in our space
\mathcal{H}_{m,a}
. Note that it is clear from our choice of the penalization
Eq.

1.4
that

\min_{|W|\leq R}\Phi(W)\geq\inf_{W}\Phi_{R}(W)\geq\inf_{W}\Phi(W),

which leads to a further question, how well we can approximate
y
using functions from
\mathcal{H}_{m,a}
under the constraint that the weights are bounded by
|W|\leq R
?

To this aim, in the second part of the paper we study the values of

(1.7)

\displaystyle\inf_{f_{W}\in\mathcal{H}_{m,a}}\|f_{W}-y\|_{2}^{2}

and

(1.8)

\displaystyle\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}f_{W}\in\mathcal{H}_{m,a}\\
|W|\leq R\end{subarray}}\|f_{W}-y\|_{2}^{2},

where
f_{W}=f_{W,a}\in\mathcal{H}_{m,a}
is a function in our network defined in
Eq.

1.1
and
\|\cdot\|_{2}
is the standard
L^{2}
-norm on
\mathbf{S}^{1}
.
We are interested in the following questions:

(1)

How large is the gap between
Eq.

1.8
and
Eq.

1.7
?

(2)

What is the value of
Eq.

1.7
?

Remark 1.2
.

Note that we measure the distance between
f_{W}
and
y
with the standard
L^{2}
-norm, which can be interpreted as the expected error between
f(X)
and
y(X)
when the random variable
X
is uniformly distributed on
\mathbf{S}^{1}
. In particular, if we let
\mu
be the uniform measure, then
\Phi(W)=\|f_{W}-y\|_{2}^{2}
. It would be interesting to answer the Questions
Items

1
and
2
for more general probability measures (see
Remark

1.4
)

The difference between the approximation problems
Eq.

1.7
and
Eq.

1.8
is that under the constraint
|W|\leq R
every function
f\in\mathcal{H}_{m,a}
is Lipschitz continuous with Lipschitz constant
R
. Since we assume
y\in BV(\mathbf{S}^{1})
, then in
Eq.

1.8
we are trying to approximate a given BV-function with uniformly Lipschitz continuous functions given by our network. Therefore, we see
Eq.

1.8
as a regularized version of
Eq.

1.7
. That is, to answer Question
Item

1
, is to study, what is the error that we make when we regularize the problem
Eq.

1.7
by the constraint
|W|\leq R
for
f_{W}\in\mathcal{H}_{m,a}
. We call this
the localization problem
and prove it in
Theorem

2
.

On the other hand, in
Eq.

1.7
, we are trying to approximate BV-functions with functions in our network, but this infimum is not in general attained, see
Example

1.1
. Thus we first characterize the closure of the space
\mathcal{H}_{m,a}
in
Theorem

4
in
Section

4
and denote it by
\overline{\mathcal{H}}_{m,a}
. This turns out to be a subset of BV-functions. To answer Question
Item

2
, we prove in
Theorem

3
that the space
\overline{\mathcal{H}}_{m,a}
asymptotically converges (w.r.t. the number of alternating nodes) to the space of certain BV-functions and quantify this convergence.

The Localization theorem

.

Let us focus on answering Question
Item

1
. Our aim is to estimate the gap between
Eq.

1.8
and
Eq.

1.7
in a quantitative way such that the bound depends only on the
BV
-norm
y
. In particular, we want the estimate to be independent of number of nodes
m
. This turns out to be a difficult and much deeper problem than estimating the size of
Eq.

1.7
.

In order to state the result we define the index sets
I=\{1,\dots,m\}
,
I_{+}:=\{i\in I:a_{i}=1\}
and
I_{-}:=\{i\in I:a_{i}=-1\}
. We define further

(1.9)

\displaystyle\underline{m}:=\min\{\#I_{+},\#I_{-}\},

and

(1.10)

\displaystyle C(m):=\sqrt{m/\underline{m}}\geq\sqrt{2},

with the convention that
C(m)=\infty
if
\underline{m}=0
. The constant
C(m)
measures the ratio between the number of positive and negative coefficients
(a_{i})_{i\in I}
and we do not make any a priori assumptions on this value. Our second main result answers Question
Item

1
and reads as follows.

Theorem 2

(Localization theorem)
.

Assume that
y\in BV(\mathbf{S}^{1})
is such that
\|y\|_{L^{2}(\mathbf{S}^{1})}\leq 1
. Then for all
R\geq R_{0}
the following holds

\displaystyle\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}f_{W}\in\mathcal{H}_{m,a}\\
|W|\leq C(m)R\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle\|f_{W}-y\|_{L^{2}(\mathbf{S}^{1})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\inf_{f_{W}\in\mathcal{H}_{m,a}}\|f_{W}-y\|_{L^{2}(\mathbf{S}^{1})}^{2}+5\cdot 10^{4}(\|y\|_{BV}^{2}+1)\frac{1}{R^{1/9}},

where
C(m)
is defined in
Eq.

1.10
and

R_{0}=\max\{(10\|y\|_{BV})^{6},4\cdot 10^{7}\}.

Let us make a few comments on this result. First,
Theorem

2
shows that when
R
is large with respect to the BV-norm of
y
, the value of
Eq.

1.8
is close to
Eq.

1.7
. The estimate is quantitative and essentially independent of
m
. Indeed, the estimate only depends on the value of
C(m)
, but this is necessary (see
Remark

6.6
). On the other hand, if we choose the coefficients
a_{i}
randomly, then
C(m)\leq C_{0}
for some uniform constant
C_{0}
with high probability and the estimate becomes independent of the number of nodes
m
.

Moreover, the constants depends only on the BV-norm of
y
which is a rather mild regularity assumption (see
Remark

1.3
below). For this reason
Theorem

2
can be applied also to the case when the function
y
takes only discrete values
y(x)\in\{1,2,\dots\}
which is common in classification problems. The drawback is that the proof is rather difficult and as such we obtain a rate
R^{-1/9}
which is not optimal. Indeed, if we would assume that
C(m)
is bounded and that
y
is Lipschitz continuous, the proof would be simpler and it would give a better rate
R^{-\alpha}
for
\alpha
closer to one. As in
Theorem

1
, we give explicit values for the constants, but we do not optimize their values in order to avoid heavy computations.

We find
Theorem

2
the deepest result of our paper and since its proof is rather technical, we give a short outline of its proof in the next section.

Remark 1.3
.

The BV norm in
Theorem

2
is natural, since the closure of
\mathcal{H}_{m,a}
in
L^{2}
belongs to the space of BV functions (see
Theorem

4
). On the other hand, we cannot replace the BV-norm e.g. by
L^{2}
- or even
L^{\infty}
-norm of
y
. The reason for this is that every function
f_{W}\in\mathcal{H}_{m,a}
with
|W|\leq R
is
R
-Lipschitz continuous and it is not possible to approximate
L^{2}
-functions with
R
-Lipschitz functions such that the error depends only on the
L^{2}
-norm of
y
(think of
\sin(kx)
for large
k
on
[0,1]
). From a learning perspective (classification), the BV-norm makes sense as a complexity measure, as it will ”count” the number ”switches” between the class
1
and
0
on the unit circle.

Uniform approximation theorem

.

Let us now finally focus on Question
Item

1
. As mentioned before, Question
Item

1
is related to the universal approximation theorem, which is a well studied problem

[
14
,
21
,
22
,
23
,
25
,
26
,
27
,
29
,
28
,
31
,
34
,
36
,
37
,
38
,
40
,
42
,
47
]
.
We need to quantify the universal approximation theorem (see
Section

5
) in order to give bounds on the size of
Eq.

1.7
. In the context of Barron spaces, see
[
8
]
.
Let us begin with a simple observation that we may write the ReLU function as

\sigma(t)=\max\{t,0\}=\frac{|t|}{2}+\frac{t}{2}=\text{symmetric}+\text{linear}.

Therefore since
x\mapsto w_{i}\cdot x
is linear we deduce that every function
f_{W}\in\mathcal{H}_{m,a}
is of the form

f_{W}=\text{symmetric}+\text{linear}.

Therefore if we have
y\in L^{2}(\mathbf{S}^{1})
, we decompose it as
y=y^{s}+y^{a}
, where
y^{s}(x)=y^{s}(-x)
and
y^{a}(x)=-y^{a}(x)
(antipodally symmetric and antipodally anti-symmetric). Further, we decompose
y^{a}=l+g
, where
l
is linear and
g
is orthogonal to linear functions. Then it is clear from the previous discussion, and we show this in
Lemma

4.7
, that
\mathcal{H}_{m,a}\perp g
and therefore

\displaystyle\|f_{w}-g\|_{2}^{2}=\|g\|_{2}^{2}

for all
f_{W}\in\mathcal{H}_{m,a}
. We thus conclude that in order to study
Eq.

1.7
it is natural to assume that the function
y
satisfies

(1.11)

y=y^{s}+l\qquad\text{for symmetric }\,y^{s}\quad\text{and linear }\,l.

Note that the function
y(x)=\mathbb{I}\{x_{2}\geq 0\}x_{1}
in
Example

1.1
can be written as

\displaystyle y(x)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbb{I}\{x_{2}\geq 0\}x_{1}-\mathbb{I}\{x_{2}\leq 0\}x_{1}\right)+\frac{1}{2}x_{1}

and thus it satisfies
Eq.

1.11
as
x\mapsto\mathbb{I}\{x_{2}\geq 0\}x_{1}-\mathbb{I}\{x_{2}\leq 0\}x_{1}
is (antipodally) symmetric.

Our final result completes our study of the problem

Eq.

1.3
.

Theorem 3

(Uniform approximation theorem)
.

Assume that
y\in BV(\mathbf{S}^{1})
satisfies
Eq.

1.11
. Then

\displaystyle\inf_{f_{W}\in\mathcal{H}_{m,a}}\|f_{W}-y\|_{L^{2}(\mathbf{S}^{1})}^{2}\leq\frac{62\|y\|_{BV}^{2}}{\underline{m}},

where
\underline{m}
is defined in
Eq.

1.9
.

Theorem

3
quantifies the universal approximation theorem, since it states that for a given
y\in BV(\mathbf{S}^{1})
which satisfies
Eq.

1.11
, we may find a function
f_{W}\in\mathcal{H}_{m,a}
which is close to
y
and that this error tends to zero as
\underline{m}\to\infty
. It is rather clear that the convergence depends on
\underline{m}
and not on
m
. Indeed, if
y
is a negative function and
a_{i}=1
for all
i=1,\dots,m
then the best approximation in
\mathcal{H}_{m,a}
is given by the zero function for all
m
.

Theorem

3
is optimal in the sense that we cannot replace the BV-norm by the
L^{2}
-norm of
y
. Indeed, one may see this by considering the symmetric function (in polar coordinates)
y(\theta)=\sin(2k\theta)
. It is rather clear that we cannot approximate
y
by functions in
\mathcal{H}_{m,a}
uniformly when
k
tends to infinity.

1.2.
Outline of the proof of
Theorem

2

As we mentioned above, the proof of
Theorem

2
is rather technical and therefore we prefer to outline it here in order to highlight the key ideas. First, since
y
is a BV-function, we may replace it by a
C^{1}
-function
y_{R}
with an approximation argument from
Lemma

2.7
. We only have to be careful in controlling the error that we make by doing this.

Next we use
Theorem

4
, which we prove in
Section

4
, to find the best approximation function
g\in\overline{\mathcal{H}}_{m,a}
, that is

\|g-y_{R}\|_{2}^{2}=\inf_{f_{W}\in\mathcal{H}_{m,a}}\|f_{W}-y_{R}\|_{2}^{2}.

The idea of the proof is to find a function
f_{W_{R}}\in\mathcal{H}_{m,a}
, with
|W_{R}|\leq C(m)R
, which approximates
g
well. In order to do this we need to study the regularity, or the complexity, of the minimizer
g
.

In order to do this we again use
Theorem

4
which states that
g
is a sum of simple BV-functions and, in particular, it is piecewise linear. We then use the minimality of
g
and the characterization of its symmetric part, given by
Proposition

4.6
, to find the Euler-Lagrange equation for the symmetric part
g^{s}
, which reads as

(1.12)

\displaystyle\int_{S}g^{s}xdx=\int_{S}y^{s}xdx,

where
y^{s}
is the symmetric part of
y
and
S
is any arc where
g
is linear. We note that
Eq.

1.12
is in fact a system of two equations (recall that
x\in\mathbf{S}^{1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
) which contain information on the zeroth and on the first order behavior of
g^{s}
respectively. In
Lemma

6.2
we use an argument inspired by regularity theory of elliptic PDEs to deduce Lipschitz estimates for
g^{s}
in terms of the
C^{1}
-norm of
y
. In
Lemma

6.3
we turn this estimate into information on the weights which define
g
and thus we are able to bound the complexity of
g
. This is the core of the proof, and after that we may approximate
g
in a rather straightforward way by
f_{W_{R}}\in\mathcal{H}_{m,a}
, with
|W_{R}|\leq C(m)R
.

Remark 1.4
.

The proof of
Theorem

2
relies on the fact that the Euler-Lagrange equation
Eq.

1.12
contains information for all small arcs
S
on the unit circle. If we try to generalize
Theorem

2
to more general class of probability measures
\mu
, then
\mu
may not have support on all small arcs and our proof breaks down. What we need for the proof is that the measure is mutually absolutely continuous w.r.t. the Lebesgue measure in a quantifiable sense. We believe for instance that the class of
A^{\infty}
measures would suffice, see
[
13
,
19
]
. We also need the measure to be antipodally symmetric in order to obtain
Eq.

1.12
.

2.
Preliminaries

In this short section we introduce our notation, recall basic results on log-Sobolev inequalities and recall the definition and some basic facts on BV-functions.

2.1.
Notation

Whenever we have a point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
we denote
|x|
as the Euclidean
2
-norm. We denote
\mathbf{S}^{1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
as the unit circle and whenever we have a set
S\subset\mathbf{S}^{1}
we denote with
|S|
the normalized Lebesgue length of that set, i.e.

|S|=\strokedint_{S}dx.

For a discrete set of indices i.e.
I\subset\mathbb{N}
we denote with
|I|
the number of elements in
I
.

We define the
L^{2}
and
L^{\infty}
on norms on
\mathbf{S}^{1}
as

\|f\|_{2}^{2}:=\strokedint_{\mathbf{S}^{1}}f^{2}(x)\,dx\qquad\text{and}\qquad\|f\|_{\infty}:=\text{ess sup}_{x\in\mathbf{S}^{1}}|f(x)|,

and the
C^{1}
-norm of as

\|f\|_{C^{1}}:=\|f\|_{\infty}+\|f^{\prime}\|_{\infty}.

We recall the definition of the ReLU activation function as

\displaystyle\sigma(t)=\max\{t,0\},\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

It has two important properties. First, it is homogeneous
\sigma(\lambda t)=\lambda\sigma(t)
for
\lambda>0
and its derivative is the Heaviside step function
\sigma^{\prime}(t)=\mathbb{I}\{t\geq 0\}
, where
\mathbb{I}
denotes the indicator function.

The function space that we consider in this paper is the following:

Definition 2.1
.

Consider an integer
m>0
, index set
I=\{1,\dots,m\}
and coefficients
a=(a_{i})_{i=1}^{m}\in\{-1,1\}^{m}
. Then the set of all functions of the form

f_{W,a}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}\sigma(w_{i}\cdot x),\quad w_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{2},\,x\in\mathbf{S}^{1}

is denoted by
\mathcal{H}_{m,a}
. We sometimes simplify the notation by
f_{W}=f_{W,a}
, since the coefficients are fixed. We define further
I_{+}=\{i\in I:a_{i}=1\}
,
I_{-}=\{i\in I:a_{i}=-1\}
and recall that

\displaystyle\underline{m}=\min\{|I_{+}|,|I_{-}|\}.

Remark 2.2
.

In the rest of the paper we will assume that
\underline{m}=|I_{-}|\leq|I_{+}|
. We may do this without loss of generality, up to a changing sign.

Furthermore, we will consider the following reordering of the coefficients
a=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m})
such that the first
2\underline{m}
of the
a_{i}
’s alternate in sign, and the remaining
a_{i}
’s are positive. This allows us to consider restrictions of the minimization problem from
\mathcal{H}_{m,a}
to
\mathcal{H}_{m^{\prime},a^{\prime}}
where
a^{\prime}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m^{\prime}})\in\{-1,1\}^{m^{\prime}}
and
0<m^{\prime}<m
. The notation that we will adhere to is

(2.1)

\displaystyle\inf_{f\in\mathcal{H}_{m^{\prime},a^{\prime}}}\|f-y\|_{2}^{2}

Finally we define the following function space

(2.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{al}:=\{f:f\in L^{2},\text{ anti-symmetric part of $f$ is linear}\}.

This is a natural space, since by our earlier discussion before
Theorem

3
it is clear that
\mathcal{H}_{m,a}\subset\mathcal{L}_{al}
. Note also that
\mathcal{L}_{al}^{\perp}
consists of anti-symmetric functions.

2.2.
Log-Sobolev inequalities

In this section we list results related to the Log-Sobolev inequality and Poincaré inequality (or Spectral gap) that we need in the proof of
Theorem

1
. As an introduction to the topic we refer to
[
30
]
and
[
43
]
.

Let
d\mu
be a probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Then we say that
\mu
satisfies the Log-Sobolev inequality (LSI) with constant
C_{LS}
if for all Lipschitz function
u
with
\int u^{2}d\mu=1
the following holds

(2.3)

\displaystyle\int u^{2}\log u^{2}d\mu\leq 2C_{LS}\int|\nabla u|^{2}d\mu.

We say that
\mu
satisfies Poincaré inequality with constant
C_{P}
if for all Lipschitz function
u
with
\int ud\mu=0
the following holds

(2.4)

\displaystyle\int u^{2}d\mu\leq C_{P}\int|\nabla u|^{2}d\mu.

It is well known that LSI implies the Poincaré inequality
[
30
,
43
]
.

Remark 2.3
.

If LSI holds for a constant
C_{LS}
then the Poincaré inequality holds with the constant
C_{P}=C_{LS}
.

We recall the standard result on the Gaussian measure.

Lemma 2.4
.

[
20
,
30
,
43
]

The Gaussian measure
d\mu_{\Lambda}=c_{n,\Lambda}e^{-\frac{\Lambda\|x\|^{2}}{2}}dx
, where
c_{n,\Lambda}
is the normalization factor, satisfies the Log-Sobolev inequality (LSI)
Eq.

2.3
with constant

C_{LS}=\frac{1}{\Lambda}.

By
Lemma

2.4
and
Remark

2.3
we know that the Gaussian measure satisfies the Poincaé inequality
Eq.

2.4
with a constant that is independent of the dimension. This is crucial for us, since our goal is to have estimates which are dimension-free.
The following well-know result enables us to prove LSI for measures that are perturbation of the Gaussian measure.

Lemma 2.5

(Holley-Stroock perturbation lemma)
.

If a LSI holds for the measure
d\mu
with constant
C_{LS}
, then the perturbed measure

\displaystyle Z^{-1}\exp(G(x))d\mu(x)

where
Z
is a normalization constant, satisfies a LSI with constant
C_{LS}\exp(\operatorname{osc}(G))
.

The advantage of
Lemma

2.5
for us is that it can be applied to general class of measures. The disadvantage is that in our setting it provides rather poor bound on the Poincaré constant
C_{P}
. We will use the following perturbation result by Aida
[
1
]
, which fits well in our setting.

Lemma 2.6

(Aida perturbation lemma)
.

Assume LSI holds for the measure
d\mu
with constant
C_{LS}
and assume that for a function
G
and constant
\beta\in(0,1/4]
the following holds

(2.5)

\displaystyle\int\exp(2C_{LS}(1+\beta)|\nabla G|^{2})d\mu

\displaystyle\leq 1+\frac{\beta}{2}

(2.6)

\displaystyle\int\exp(G)d\mu

\displaystyle\geq 1-\frac{\beta}{2}

(2.7)

\displaystyle\int\exp(2G)d\mu

\displaystyle=1.

Then the perturbed measure
\exp(2G(x))d\mu(x)
, satisfies Poincaré inequality with constant

\displaystyle\frac{64C_{LS}(1+\beta)(1-(\beta/2))}{\beta^{2}}.

2.3.
BV Functions

We begin by recalling the definition and basic facts on functions of bounded variation, or simply BV-functions. As an introduction to the topic we refer to
[
3
]
.

Given a function
y\in\mathbf{S}^{1}\to\mathbb{R}
, there is a natural identification of
y
as a
2\pi
-periodic function
\widetilde{y}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
defined as
\widetilde{y}(\theta)=y(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)
. We will assume this identification from now on.
We recall that a
2\pi
-periodic function
y:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a function of bounded variation if

\displaystyle\sup\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}|y(\theta_{i+1})-y(\theta_{i})|:\,n\geq 2,\,0=\theta_{1}<\theta_{2}<\dots<\theta_{n}=2\pi\right)<\infty.

We denote the space of BV-functions on
\mathbf{S}^{1}
by
BV(\mathbf{S}^{1})
. An equivalent definition is to require that the total variation of
y
on
(0,2\pi)

(2.8)

V_{y}:=\sup\left(\strokedint_{(0,2\pi)}y(\theta)\varphi^{\prime}(\theta)\,d\theta:\,\varphi\in C^{1}_{per}(\mathbb{R}),\,\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq 1\right)

is bounded.
The derivative of the
BV
-function
y
is a finite Radon measure which we denote by
\mu_{y}
and we have

\displaystyle\int_{(0,2\pi)}y(\theta)\varphi^{\prime}(\theta)\,d\theta=-\int_{(0,2\pi)}\varphi(\theta)\,d\mu_{y}

for all
\varphi\in C^{1}_{per}(\mathbb{R})
. It is trivial to see that any BV-function is bounded and we define its BV-norm as

\displaystyle\|y\|_{BV}:=\|y\|_{\infty}+V_{y}.

If
y
is a BV-function then it is, in particular,
L^{2}
-function and we may write it in terms of Fourier series

y(\theta)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}\sin(k\theta)+b_{k}\cos(k\theta).

By choosing
\varphi(\theta)=-\cos(k\theta)
we have by
\varphi^{\prime}(\theta)=k\sin(k\theta)
and
Eq.

2.8
that

\displaystyle\|y\|_{BV}\geq k\strokedint_{(0,2\pi)}y(\theta)\sin(k\theta)\,d\theta=k\strokedint_{(0,2\pi)}a_{k}\sin^{2}(k\theta)\,d\theta=\frac{ka_{k}}{2}.

By choosing
\varphi(\theta)=\cos(k\theta)
we obtain by the same argument
-\frac{ka_{k}}{2}\leq\|y\|_{BV}
, and by choosing
\varphi(\theta)=\pm\sin(k\theta)
we obtain the same bound for
b_{k}
. Hence, we conclude

(2.9)

|a_{k}|,|b_{k}|\leq 2\frac{\|y\|_{BV}}{k}.

We may use
Eq.

2.9
to approximate BV-functions with smooth functions in a quantitative way.

Lemma 2.7
.

Assume
y\in BV(\mathbf{S}^{1})
and fix
r\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
. Then there is
y_{r}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbf{S}^{1})
such that
\|y_{r}\|_{\infty}\leq\|y\|_{\infty}
,
\|y_{r}\|_{C^{1}}\leq 5r\,\|y\|_{BV}
and

\|y-y_{r}\|^{2}_{2}\leq 16\|y\|_{BV}^{2}\frac{1}{r}.

Proof.

Let
u
be the solution of the heat equation
\partial_{t}u=u^{\prime\prime}
with initial datum
u(\theta,0)=y(\theta)
. We define
y_{r}(\theta):=u(\theta,r^{-2})
. Let us check that this satisfies all the required conditions. First, by the maximum principle,
\|y_{r}\|_{\infty}\leq\|y\|_{\infty}
.

We proceed by writing
y
in Fourier series as

\displaystyle y(\theta)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}\sin(k\theta)+b_{k}\cos(k\theta)

and the solution to the heat equation as

(2.10)

\displaystyle u(\theta,t)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}\sin(k\theta)e^{-k^{2}t}+b_{k}\cos(k\theta)e^{-k^{2}t}.

By differentiating
Eq.

2.10
and using
Eq.

2.9
we obtain

\displaystyle|\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}u(\theta,t)|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(|a_{k}|k+|b_{k}|k)e^{-k^{2}t}

\displaystyle\leq 4\|y\|_{BV}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}e^{-k^{2}t}

\displaystyle\leq 4\|y\|_{BV}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-x^{2}t}\,dx\leq\frac{2\sqrt{\pi}}{\sqrt{t}}\|y\|_{BV}.

Hence
|y_{r}^{\prime}(\theta)|=|\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}u(\theta,r^{-2})|\leq 4r\,\|y\|_{BV}
and thus
\|y_{r}\|_{C^{1}}=\|y_{r}\|_{\infty}+\|y_{r}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\leq 5r\|y\|_{BV}
for
r\geq 1
.

We continue by estimating
\|y-u(\cdot,t)\|^{2}_{2}
for
t=r^{-2}
by using the Fourier series

\displaystyle\strokedint_{0}^{2\pi}

\displaystyle|y(\theta)-u(\theta,t)|^{2}\,d\theta\leq\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}|a_{k}\left(1-e^{-k^{2}t}\right)|^{2}+|b_{k}\left(1-e^{-k^{2}t}\right)|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{r}|a_{k}\left(1-e^{-k^{2}t}\right)|^{2}+|b_{k}\left(1-e^{-k^{2}t}\right)|^{2}+\sum_{k=r+1}^{\infty}(a_{k}^{2}+b_{k}^{2}).

We first use (
2.9
) the estimate the second term as

\displaystyle\sum_{k=r+1}^{\infty}(a_{k}^{2}+b_{k}^{2})\leq 8\|y\|_{BV}^{2}\sum_{k=r+1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k^{2}}\leq\frac{8\|y\|_{BV}^{2}}{r}.

We use (
2.9
) to estimate the first term as

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{r}|a_{k}\overbrace{\left(1-e^{-k^{2}t}\right)}^{\leq k^{2}t}|^{2}+|b_{k}\left(1-e^{-k^{2}t}\right)|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{r}|t\,k^{2}a_{k}|^{2}+|t\,k^{2}b_{k}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 8\|y\|_{BV}^{2}t^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{r}k^{2}=8\|y\|_{BV}^{2}t^{2}\frac{r(r+1)(2r+1)}{6}

\displaystyle\leq 8\|y\|_{BV}^{2}t^{2}r^{3}\leq 8\|y\|_{BV}^{2}\frac{1}{r}

for
t=r^{-2}
.
Hence we deduce
\|y-u(\cdot,r^{-2})\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{16\|y\|_{BV}^{2}}{r}
.
∎

Recall that every
f_{W}\in\mathcal{H}_{m,a}
belongs to the function space
\mathcal{L}_{al}
defined in
Eq.

2.2
. It is therefore natural to decompose also
y=y_{1}+y_{2}
where
y_{1}\in\mathcal{L}_{al}
and
y_{2}\in\mathcal{L}_{al}^{\perp}
. In the following lemma we make the rather obvious remark that
y
bounds the norm of
y_{1}
.

Lemma 2.8
.

Assume
y\in BV(\mathbf{S}^{1})
and let us write
y=y_{1}+y_{2}
with
y_{1}\in\mathcal{L}_{al}
and
y_{2}\in\mathcal{L}_{al}^{\perp}
, where
\mathcal{L}_{al}
is defined in
Eq.

2.2
. Then

\|y_{1}\|_{BV}\leq 4\|y\|_{BV}.

Proof.

Since
y_{1}\in\mathcal{L}_{al}
it can be written as
y_{1}(x)=y^{s}(s)+l(x)
, where
y^{s}
is the symmetric part of
y
and
l
is a linear function. First, we may write
y^{s}(x)=\frac{y(x)+y(-x)}{2}
and therefore

\|y^{s}\|_{BV}\leq\|y\|_{BV}.

Since
y^{s}\perp l
and
y_{2}\perp l
we have

\displaystyle\strokedint_{\mathbf{S}^{1}}y(x)\frac{l(x)}{\|l\|_{\infty}}dx=\|l\|_{\infty}\strokedint_{\mathbf{S}^{1}}\left(\frac{l}{\|l\|_{\infty}}\right)^{2}dx=\frac{\|l\|_{\infty}}{2},

where the last equality follows from the fact that
\strokedint_{(0,2\pi)}\sin^{2}(\theta)d\theta=1/2
.
From the above equality and from Cauchy-Schwarz we obtain

\displaystyle\|l\|_{\infty}=2\strokedint_{\mathbf{S}^{1}}y(x)\frac{l(x)}{\|l\|_{\infty}}dx\leq 2\frac{\|y\|_{2}}{\sqrt{2}}.

∎

3.
Convergence to equilibrium and properties of the cost function

In this section we assume
\mu
to be merely a probability measure on
\mathbf{S}^{1}
and denote

\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[f]:=\int_{\mathbf{S}^{1}}f(x)\,d\mu(x)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}:=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[f^{2}].

Our goal is to prove
Theorem

1
. To this aim we need first to study the structure of the cost function
\Phi
defined as

\Phi(W):=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[(f_{W}-y)^{2}]=\|f_{W}-y\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2},

where we write
f_{W}=f_{W,a}\in\mathcal{H}_{m,a}
for short. We first study the growth bounds from below and above of the cost function.

Lemma 3.1
.

Assume that
\|y\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}\leq 1
. Then,

\displaystyle\Phi(W)\leq(|W|+1)^{2}

and

\displaystyle|\nabla_{W}\Phi(W)|^{2}\leq 4\Phi(W)\quad\text{and}\quad\langle\nabla_{W}\Phi(W),W\rangle\geq\Phi(W)-1.

Proof.

By Cauchy-Schwarz we have for every
f_{W}\in\mathcal{H}_{m,a}
and every
x\in\mathbf{S}^{1}

\displaystyle|f_{W}(x)|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}|\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}\sigma(w_{i}\cdot x)|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\underbrace{a_{i}^{2}}_{=1}\right)^{1/2}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\underbrace{\sigma(w_{i}\cdot x)^{2}}_{\leq|w_{i}|^{2}}\right)^{1/2}

(3.1)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\left(m\right)^{1/2}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}|w_{i}|^{2}\right)^{1/2}=|W|.

Using
Eq.

3.1
and Cauchy-Schwarz again yields

(3.2)

\big|\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[f_{W}y]\big|\leq\|f_{W}\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}\|y\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}\leq\|f_{W}\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}\leq|W|.

By
Eqs.

3.1
and
3.2
we may now bound the value of the cost function

(3.3)

\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[(f_{W}-y)^{2}]=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[f_{W}^{2}]-2\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[f_{W}y]+\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[y^{2}]\\
\leq|W|^{2}+2|W|+1

which yields the first claim.

By differentiating
f_{W,a}(x)
with respect to the weight
w_{i}
yields

\frac{\partial}{\partial w_{i}}f_{W,a}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}a_{i}\sigma^{\prime}(w_{i}\cdot x)x.

Therefore

(3.4)

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{W}f_{W}\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}^{2}|x|^{2}\sigma^{\prime}(w_{i}\cdot x)^{2}\right]\leq 1.

Hence, we deduce from
Eqs.

3.3
and
3.4
that

\displaystyle|\nabla_{W}\Phi(W)|

\displaystyle=|\nabla_{W}\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[(f_{W}-y)^{2}]|\leq 2\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[|\nabla_{W}f_{W}(f_{W}-y)|]

\displaystyle\leq 2\|\nabla_{W}f_{W}\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}\|f_{W}-y\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{\Phi(W)},

which yields the second claim.

In order to prove the third claim we observe that by homogeneity,
f_{\lambda W}(x)=\lambda f_{W}(x)
for all
\lambda>0
. Therefore we have

\displaystyle\langle\nabla f_{W}(x),W\rangle=\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}\Big|_{\lambda=1}f_{\lambda W}(x)=f_{W}(x).

Thus we deduce

\displaystyle\langle\nabla_{W}\Phi(W),W\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\nabla\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[(f_{W}-y)^{2}],W\rangle

\displaystyle=2\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\langle\nabla f_{W},W\rangle(f_{W}-y)]

\displaystyle=2\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[(f_{W}-y)^{2}]+2\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[y(x)(f_{W}-y)]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[(f_{W}-y)^{2}]-\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[y^{2}]

\displaystyle\geq\Phi(W)-1.

∎

One might be tempted to think that the third inequality in
Lemma

3.1
implies the cost function to be coercive. This is however not true, as the next lemma shows (see also
Example

1.1
). In particular, we show that the gradient flow diverges for certain starting points.

Lemma 3.2
.

Assume that
y(x)=\mathbb{I}\{x_{2}\geq 0\}x_{1}
,
a_{1}=-a_{2}=1
and
\mathcal{H}_{2,a}
and
\Phi
are as in
Example

1.1
. Choose
W_{0}=(w_{1},w_{2})
with
w_{1}=(1/2,b)
and
w_{1}=(-1/2,b)
for
b\geq 1
. Then the gradient flow
(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
of
\Phi
starting from
W_{0}
diverges, i.e.

\lim_{t\to\infty}|W_{t}|=\infty.

The claim follows from a rather simple calculation which we provide for the convenience of the reader.

Proof.

Let us show that the gradient flow
(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
,

\frac{d}{dt}W_{t}=-\nabla_{W}\Phi(W_{t}),

is always of the form
W_{t}=(w_{1}(t),w_{2}(t))\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
with

(3.5)

w_{1}(t)=(1/2,b_{t})\qquad\text{and}\qquad w_{2}(t)=(-1/2,b_{t}),

with
b_{t}\geq 1
. Recall that we assume
\Phi(W)=\|f_{w}-y\|_{2}^{2}
.

Let us assume that
W_{t}=(w_{1},w_{2})
is of the form
Eq.

3.5
and let us calculate
\nabla_{W}\Phi(W_{t})
. We denote
S_{12}=\{x\in\mathbf{S}^{1}:w_{1}\cdot x\geq 0\}\setminus\{x\in\mathbf{S}^{1}:w_{2}\cdot x\geq 0\}
and
S_{21}=\{x\in\mathbf{S}^{1}:w_{2}\cdot x\geq 0\}\setminus\{x\in\mathbf{S}^{1}:w_{1}\cdot x\geq 0\}
and have by a straightforward calculation

\partial_{w_{1}}\Phi(W)\cdot e_{1}=\frac{2}{2\pi}\int_{S_{12}}\left(\frac{1}{2}x_{1}+b_{t}x_{2}-\mathbb{I}_{\{x_{2}\geq 0\}}x_{1}\right)x_{1}\,dx=0

and similarly
\partial_{w_{2}}\Phi(W)\cdot e_{1}=0
. Moreover,

\begin{split}\partial_{w_{1}}\Phi(W)\cdot e_{2}&=\frac{2}{2\pi}\int_{S_{12}}\left(\frac{1}{2}x_{1}+b_{t}x_{2}-\mathbb{I}_{\{x_{2}\geq 0\}}x_{1}\right)x_{2}\,dx\\
&=\frac{2}{2\pi}\int_{S_{21}}\left(\frac{1}{2}x_{1}+b_{t}x_{2}-\mathbb{I}_{\{x_{2}\geq 0\}}x_{1}\right)x_{2}\,dx\\
&=\partial_{w_{2}}\Phi(W)\cdot e_{2}.\end{split}

This means that if
W_{t}
is of the form
W_{t}=(1/2,b_{t},-1/2,b_{t})\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
then the gradient is of the form
\nabla_{W}\Phi(W_{t})=(0,c_{t},0,c_{t})
. This proves
Eq.

3.5
. The argument also yields that
|\nabla_{W}\Phi(W_{t})|^{2}=2c_{t}^{2}>0
when
|b_{t}|<\infty
. Therefore we deduce that necessarily
|b_{t}|\to\infty
and the claim follows.
∎

The fact that the gradient flow may diverge to infinity as shown by
Example

1.1
and
Lemma

3.2
forces us to consider a penalizing term to confine the gradient descent. We recall that we define the penalization of
\Phi
as

(3.6)

\Phi_{R}(W)=\max\{\Phi(W),4(|W|^{2}-R^{2})\}.

The reason we choose this penalization is that it is Gaussian outside a large ball and we wish to utilize the contracting effect of high dimension, where the local non-convexity of
\Phi
matters less and less. As such, when the dimension is large w.r.t.
R
we may expect to have Poincaré inequality with a constant independent of the dimension.

Theorem

1
concerns the density of the stochastic gradient flow (
1.6
). It is well known that the density
\rho
solves the following Fokker-Planck equation

\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}=\nabla\cdot\left(\rho\nabla\Phi_{R}+\varepsilon^{2}\nabla\rho\right).

By setting
u(t,x)=\rho(t,x)e^{\Phi_{R}(x)/\varepsilon^{2}}
we obtain a formulation

(3.7)

\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}=\varepsilon^{2}\Delta u-\nabla\Phi_{R}\cdot\nabla u=\nabla\cdot\left(\varepsilon^{2}\nabla ue^{-\Phi_{R}/\varepsilon^{2}}\right)

In order to quantify the convergence of
u
, and thus in turn of
\rho
, we need to prove the Poincaré inequality (PI)
Eq.

2.4
for the measure
e^{-\Phi_{R}/\varepsilon^{2}}dW
. This is the most important result of the section, and deserves some comments.

The history of Poincaré inequalities for exponential measures is rich and deep. In the Gaussian case, i.e.
e^{-|x|^{2}}dx
, the PI is by
Remark

2.3
a consequence of the log-Sobolev inequality (LSI), which is also called the Gross logarithmic Sobolev inequality, see
[
20
]
. As mentioned before, the crucial fact is that the Gaussian Poincaré constant is independent of the dimension.

Since the cost function
\Phi
defined
Eq.

1.2
and
\Phi_{R}
defined in
Eq.

3.6
are non-convex, we cannot use the well developed theory of PI for log-convex measures
[
43
]
. In order to obtain the PI for measures that are not log-convex, the most well known methods are either by perturbation argument or via Lyapunov functions. In the first case, the idea is to perturb the PI related to a log-concave measure, for which the PI or even LSI holds. In this case the Holley-Stroock perturbation result,
Lemma

2.5
, is essentially sharp for local perturbations, if we know only the oscillation. For global perturbations, the works
[
1
,
2
]
are for our knowledge the best result which gives dimensional-free and explicit constants. The other method, using Lyapunov functions, does not seem to be stable w.r.t. the dimension even for log-concave measures.
It is worth mentioning that if we know more about the geometry of our local perturbation, we could use the tunneling estimates in
[
24
]
to obtain sharper constants.

As for now we do not know enough about the geometry of local minimas of the cost function to apply other methods than perturbation. As such, our proof consists of a combination of the Holley-Stroock perturbation method together with the one by Aida. Finally, it is essential to remark that we trace the constants explicitly.

Proposition 3.3
.

Assume that
0<\varepsilon\leq 1
and
R\geq 10
. Then,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2m}}u^{2}\,e^{-\Phi_{R}(W)/\varepsilon^{2}}\,dW\leq C_{P}\,\varepsilon^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2m}}|\nabla u|^{2}\,e^{-\Phi_{R}(W)/\varepsilon^{2}}\,dW

for every Lipschitz function with
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2m}}u\,e^{-\Phi_{R}(W)}\,dW=0
. When
m\geq 24R^{2}\varepsilon^{-2}
the constant is bounded by

\displaystyle C_{P}\leq 140.

Otherwise we have the bound
C_{P}\leq\frac{1}{8}e^{(4R^{2}+2)\varepsilon^{-2}}
.

Proof.

We will use the perturbation results
Lemma

2.5
and
Lemma

2.6
to prove the Poincaré inequality for the measure
e^{-\Phi_{R}(W)/\varepsilon^{2}}dW
, where
\Phi_{R}(W)
is defined in
Eq.

3.6
. We may write
\Phi_{R}(W)=V(W)-2F(W)
, where
V(W)=4(|W|^{2}-R^{2})
and

\displaystyle F(W)=\frac{1}{2}(V(W)-\Phi(W))\mathbb{I}_{\{\Phi(W)>V(W)\}}.

Since

\displaystyle e^{-V(W)/\varepsilon^{2}}=e^{4R^{2}\varepsilon^{-2}}e^{-4\varepsilon^{-2}|W|^{2}},

the reference measure is the scaled Gaussian measure
d\gamma=c_{2m}e^{-4\varepsilon^{-2}|W|^{2}}dW
, where the normalization factor is

\displaystyle c_{2m}=\left(\frac{4}{\varepsilon^{2}\pi}\right)^{m},

and the perturbed measure is
d\gamma_{F}=e^{2\varepsilon^{-2}F}d\gamma
. By
Lemma

2.4

\gamma
satisfies the log-Sobolev inequality with constant
C_{LS}=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{8}
.

Let us first show that

(3.8)

F(W)=0\qquad\text{for all}\quad|W|^{2}\geq 2R^{2}.

Indeed, by
Lemma

3.1
we have
\Phi(W)\leq(|W|+1)^{2}
. When
R\geq 10
and
|W|^{2}\geq 2R^{2}
then

\displaystyle V(W)=4(|W|^{2}-R^{2})\geq(|W|+1)^{2}.

Therefore
V(W)\geq\Phi(W)
for all
|W|^{2}\geq 2R^{2}
and thus we have
Eq.

3.8
.

By the definition of
F
we have
F\leq 0
. From
Lemma

3.1
we deduce
4|W|^{2}-\Phi(W)\geq 2|W|^{2}-2\geq-2
. Therefore we have

2|F(W)|=(V(W)-\Phi(W))_{-}=(4|W|^{2}-\Phi(W)-4R^{2})_{-}\leq 4R^{2}+2.

By
Lemma

2.5
we deduce that the Poincaré inequality holds for
d\gamma_{F}
with a constant
C_{P,\varepsilon}
, which we may estimate as

(3.9)

C_{P,\varepsilon}\leq\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{8}e^{(4R^{2}+2)\varepsilon^{-2}},

setting
C_{P}=\varepsilon^{-2}C_{P,\varepsilon}
yields the first result.

Let us improve the bound
Eq.

3.9
in the case
m\geq 24R^{2}\varepsilon^{-2}
, by using Aidas result,
Lemma

2.6
. We choose
\beta=\frac{1}{4}
in
Lemma

2.6
. Let us verify the conditions
Eqs.

2.5
,
2.6
and
2.7
. We first claim that

(3.10)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2m}}e^{2C_{LS}(1+\frac{1}{4})|\nabla(\varepsilon^{-2}F)|^{2}}\,d\gamma\leq 1+\frac{1}{8},

which gives
Eq.

2.5
.
To this aim we observe that by
Eq.

3.8
,
F=0
outside the ball
B_{\sqrt{2}R}
. Moreover by differentiating we get
2\nabla F(W)=\nabla V(W)-\nabla\Phi(W)=8W-\nabla\Phi(W)
, when
F(W)\neq 0
. Therefore we estimate by
Lemma

3.1

\displaystyle|\nabla F(W)|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\left(64|W|^{2}-16\langle\nabla\Phi(W),W\rangle+|\nabla\Phi(W)|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4}\left(64|W|^{2}-16\Phi(W)+16+4\Phi(W)\right)

\displaystyle\leq 16|W|^{2}+4.

Therefore we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2m}}e^{2C_{LS}(1+\frac{1}{4})|\nabla(\varepsilon^{-2}F)|^{2}}\,d\gamma

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2m}}e^{\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}(1+\frac{1}{4})|\varepsilon^{-2}\nabla F|^{2}}\,d\gamma=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2m}}e^{\frac{5}{16}\varepsilon^{-2}|\nabla F|^{2}}\,d\gamma

\displaystyle\leq 1+\left(\frac{4}{\varepsilon^{2}\pi}\right)^{m}\int_{B_{\sqrt{2}R}}e^{5\varepsilon^{-2}|W|^{2}+\frac{5}{4}\varepsilon^{-2}}e^{-4\varepsilon^{-2}|W|^{2}}\,dW

\displaystyle\leq 1+e^{\frac{5}{4}\varepsilon^{-2}}\left(\frac{4}{\varepsilon^{2}\pi}\right)^{m}\int_{B_{\sqrt{2}R}}e^{\varepsilon^{-2}|W|^{2}}\,dW

(3.11)

\displaystyle\leq 1+e^{\frac{5}{4}\varepsilon^{-2}}\left(\frac{4}{\varepsilon^{2}\pi}\right)^{m}e^{2R^{2}\varepsilon^{-2}}|B_{\sqrt{2}R}|.

We continue by recalling the volume of the
2m
-dimensional ball of radius
\sqrt{2}R
and using Stirlings formula

(3.12)

|B_{\sqrt{2}R}|=\frac{\pi^{m}}{m!}(\sqrt{2}R)^{2m}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi m}}\left(\frac{2\pi eR^{2}}{m}\right)^{m}.

Therefore, using
Eq.

3.11
,
Eq.

3.12
and the assumptions
m\geq 24R^{2}\varepsilon^{-2}
,
R\geq 10
, we deduce

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2m}}e^{2C_{LS}(1+\frac{1}{4})|\nabla(\varepsilon^{-2}F)|^{2}}\,d\gamma

\displaystyle\leq 1+\frac{e^{\frac{5}{4}\varepsilon^{-2}}}{\sqrt{2\pi m}}e^{2R^{2}\varepsilon^{-2}}\left(\frac{8eR^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}m}\right)^{m}

\displaystyle\leq 1+\frac{1}{100}\left(\frac{e^{\frac{1}{11}}e}{3}\right)^{m}

\displaystyle\leq 1+\frac{1}{8},

since
\frac{e^{\frac{1}{11}}e}{3}<1
. Hence, we have
Eq.

3.10
.

Let us define
G_{\varepsilon}:=\varepsilon^{-2}F+\alpha
, where
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
is chosen such that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2m}}e^{2G_{\varepsilon}}\,d\gamma=1
, in order to satisfy
Eq.

2.7
. Note that
e^{2G_{\varepsilon}}\,d\gamma
is merely
c_{n}e^{2\varepsilon^{-2}F}\,d\gamma
, where
c_{n}
is the normalization factor. We claim that

(3.13)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2m}}e^{G_{\varepsilon}}\,d\gamma\geq 1-\frac{1}{8},

which establishes
Eq.

2.6
.
From
F\leq 0
and from the choice of
\alpha
it follows

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2m}}e^{G_{\varepsilon}}\,d\gamma=e^{\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2m}}e^{\varepsilon^{-2}F}\,d\gamma\geq e^{-\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2m}}e^{2G_{\varepsilon}}\,d\gamma=e^{-\alpha}.

Now since

\displaystyle e^{-\alpha}=\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2m}}e^{2\varepsilon^{-2}F}\,d\gamma\right)^{1/2}.

the claim
Eq.

3.13
follows once we prove

(3.14)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2m}}e^{2\varepsilon^{-2}F}\,d\gamma\geq 1-\frac{1}{10},

because
\sqrt{1-1/10}\geq 1-1/8
.
To this aim we proceed as in
Eq.

3.11
by using
F\leq 0
,
F=0
outside
B_{\sqrt{2}R}
and
Eq.

3.12

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2m}}e^{2\varepsilon^{-2}F}\,d\gamma

\displaystyle=1-\int_{B_{\sqrt{2}R}}(1-e^{2\varepsilon^{-2}F})\,d\gamma

\displaystyle\geq 1-\left(\frac{4}{\varepsilon^{2}\pi}\right)^{m}\int_{B_{\sqrt{2}R}}e^{-4\varepsilon^{-2}|W|^{2}}\,dW

\displaystyle\geq 1-\left(\frac{4}{\varepsilon^{2}\pi}\right)^{m}|B_{\sqrt{2}R}|

\displaystyle\geq 1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi m}}\left(\frac{8eR^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}m}\right)^{m}.

By the assumptions
m\geq 24R^{2}\varepsilon^{-2}
,
\varepsilon\leq 1
and
R\geq 10
we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi m}}\left(\frac{8eR^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}m}\right)^{m}\leq\frac{1}{10}.

Hence,
Eq.

3.14
follows from the two above inequalities and we have proved
Eq.

3.13
.

Now using
Eqs.

3.13
,
3.14
and
3.10
we see that
Eqs.

2.5
,
2.6
and
2.7
are satisfied and
Lemma

2.6
implies that the measure
e^{2G_{\varepsilon}}\,d\gamma
, and thus also
d\gamma_{F}=e^{2\varepsilon^{-2}F}d\gamma
, satisfies the Poincaré inequality
Eq.

2.4
with constant (recall
\beta=\frac{1}{4}
and
C_{LS}=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{8}
)

\displaystyle C_{P,\varepsilon}\leq\frac{64\cdot\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{8}(1+\frac{1}{4})(1-\frac{1}{8})}{(\frac{1}{4})^{2}}=140\,\varepsilon^{2}.

Setting
C_{P}=\varepsilon^{-2}C_{P,\varepsilon}
, the above inequality yields the second result.
∎

Theorem

1
follows from
Proposition

3.3
by a standard argument which we sketch for the convenience of the reader.

Proof of
Theorem

1
.

Let us write
\rho(W,t)=u(W,t)e^{-\Phi_{R}(W)/\varepsilon^{2}}
in which case for
\rho_{\infty}(W)=e^{-\Phi_{R}(W)/\varepsilon^{2}}
we have

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2m}}(\rho(W,t)-\rho_{\infty})^{2}e^{\Phi_{R}/\varepsilon^{2}}\,dW=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2m}}(u(W,t)-1)^{2}e^{-\Phi_{R}/\varepsilon^{2}}\,dW.

Recall that
u
solves the equation
Eq.

3.7
and hence

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2m}}(u-1)^{2}e^{-\Phi_{R}/\varepsilon^{2}}\,dW

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2m}}(u-1)\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}e^{-\Phi_{R}/\varepsilon^{2}}\,dW

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2m}}(u-1)(\varepsilon^{2}\Delta u-\nabla\Phi_{R}\cdot\nabla u)e^{-\Phi_{R}/\varepsilon^{2}}\,dW

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2m}}(u-1)\,\nabla\cdot(\nabla u\,e^{-\Phi_{R}/\varepsilon^{2}})\,dW

(3.15)

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2m}}|\nabla u|^{2}e^{-\Phi_{R}/\varepsilon^{2}}\,dW,

where the last step follows from the self-adjointness of
Eq.

3.7
in
L^{2}(e^{-\Phi_{R}/\varepsilon^{2}}dW)
, see for instance
[
20
,
39
]
.
By
Proposition

3.3
we have

(3.16)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2m}}(u-1)^{2}e^{-\Phi_{R}/\varepsilon^{2}}\,dW\leq C_{P}\varepsilon^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2m}}|\nabla u|^{2}e^{-\Phi_{R}/\varepsilon^{2}}\,dW,

where the constant
C_{P}
is explicitly given in
Proposition

3.3
. Combining
Sections

3
and
3.16
we obtain

\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2m}}(u-1)^{2}e^{-\Phi_{R}/\varepsilon^{2}}\,dW\leq-\frac{2}{C_{P}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2m}}(u-1)^{2}e^{-\Phi_{R}/\varepsilon^{2}}\,dW,

which in turn implies

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2m}}(u(W,t)-1)^{2}e^{-\Phi_{R}/\varepsilon^{2}}\,dW\leq e^{-(2/C_{P})\,t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2m}}(u_{0}(W)-1)^{2}e^{-\Phi_{R}/\varepsilon^{2}}\,dW.

The above statement implies the result.
∎

4.
Closure of
\mathcal{H}_{m,a}
in
L^{2}

The rest of the paper is devoted to study the Questions
Item

1
and
Item

2
. We begin by studying the closure of the space
\mathcal{H}_{m,a}
, defined in
Definition

2.1
, with respect to the
L^{2}(\mathbf{S}^{1})
-norm. We denote this closure by
\overline{\mathcal{H}}_{m,a}
and it contains the functions which we obtain as a limit of sequence of functions
(f_{W_{k}})_{k}
in
\mathcal{H}_{m,a}
, when the weights diverge to infinity
|W_{k}|\to\infty
. We also study the structure of the symmetric part of functions in
\overline{\mathcal{H}}_{m,a}
.

We recall that a function
f_{W,a}=f_{W}\in\mathcal{H}_{m,a}
can be written as

(4.1)

\displaystyle f_{W}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}\sigma(w_{i}\cdot x),

where
I=\{1,\dots,m\}
. As we are interested in the closure when the weight space
\mathbb{R}^{2m}
is fixed, we may disregard the multiplicative factor
1/\sqrt{m}
.

Remark 4.1
.

For further reference, the closure of
\mathcal{H}_{m,a}
with respect to the
L^{2}(\mathbf{S}^{1})
norm is denoted as
\overline{\mathcal{H}}_{m,a}
.

The main result of this section is the following characterization of the space
\overline{\mathcal{H}}_{m,a}
.

Theorem 4
.

A function
g:\mathbf{S}^{1}\to\mathbb{R}
belongs to the space
\overline{\mathcal{H}}_{m,a}
if and only if it is of the form

g(x)=\sum_{i\in J}\mathbb{I}\{\hat{w}_{i}\cdot x\geq 0\}(v_{i}\cdot x)+\sum_{i\in K}a_{i}\sigma(w_{i}\cdot x),

where
\hat{w}_{i}
are unit vectors, the set of indexes
J,K\subset I
are disjoint and
|J|\leq\underline{m}
. Here
\underline{m}
is defined in
Eq.

1.9
.

Remark 4.2
.

In order for a limit to exist in
L^{2}(\mathbf{S}^{1})
when certain nodes
|w_{i}|\to\infty
as
k\to\infty
, some terms in the sum in
Eq.

4.1
needs to cancel. This cancellation is what gives rise to the index set
J
and the remainder of that cancellation gives rise to new functions of type
\mathbb{I}\{\hat{w}_{i}\cdot x\geq 0\}(v_{i}\cdot x)
. Note that these include the original functions
\sigma(w_{i}\cdot x)
by choosing
v=w_{i}
.

Theorem

4
implies that every
g\in\overline{\mathcal{H}}_{m,a}
is a BV-function. In fact,
g
is linear on arcs which we call
sectors
and define as follows.

Definition 4.3
.

Given an index set
I
and a set of directions
\{\hat{w}_{i}:\hat{w}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\}
we denote

\displaystyle S_{i}=\{x\in\mathbf{S}^{1}:\hat{w}_{i}\cdot x\geq 0\}.

Moreover, we define
sectors

\mathcal{S}
as the connected components of
\mathbf{S}^{1}
where the function

x\mapsto\sum_{i\in I}\mathbb{I}\{\hat{w}_{i}\cdot x\geq 0\}

is constant. In particular, the sectors are contained in the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
S_{i}
.

We split the proof of
Theorem

4
into two part in
Lemmas

4.4
and
4.5
. We begin with
Lemma

4.4
which identifies the closure.

Lemma 4.4
.

Assume that
|W_{k}|\to\infty
and
\|f_{W_{k}}\|_{2}\leq C
. Then there is
g\in L^{2}(\mathbf{S}^{1})
and a sub-sequence of
W_{k}
such that
f_{W_{k}}\to g
in
L^{2}(\mathbf{S}^{1})
. Moreover, there exist disjoint sets of indexes
J,K\subset I
such that
|J|\leq\underline{m}
and
g
can be written as

\displaystyle g(x)=\sum_{i\in J}\mathbb{I}\{\hat{w}_{i}\cdot x\geq 0\}(v_{i}\cdot x)+\sum_{i\in K}a_{i}\sigma(w_{i}\cdot x)

for
w_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
i\in K
and
\hat{w}_{i},v_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
for
i\in J
such that
|\hat{w}_{i}|=1
.

Proof.

Let us write

f_{W_{k}}(x)=\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}\sigma(w_{i,k}\cdot x)

and fix
i
. If
\lim\inf_{k\to\infty}|w_{i,k}|=0
then clearly we may ignore these indexes. If
\lim\sup_{k\to\infty}|w_{i,k}|<\infty
we set
i\in K
and define

w_{i}:=\lim_{k\to\infty}w_{i,k},

which exists when we pass to a converging sub-sequence. If neither of these cases happen we set
i\in I_{\infty}
. Then for

f_{k,K}(x):=\sum_{i\in I\setminus I_{\infty}}a_{i}\sigma(w_{i,k}\cdot x)

we have that

f_{k,K}(x)\to\sum_{i\in K}a_{i}\sigma(w_{i}\cdot x).

We thus need to prove the convergence of

f_{k,J}:=\sum_{i\in I_{\infty}}a_{i}\sigma(w_{i,k}\cdot x).

Let us fix
i\in I_{\infty}
and denote
\hat{w}_{i,k}=\frac{w_{i,k}}{|w_{i,k}|}
. By passing to a sub-sequence we may we define

\hat{w}_{i}:=\lim_{k\to\infty}\hat{w}_{i,k}

and assume that
\lim_{k\to\infty}|w_{i,k}|=\infty
. We claim that for each
i\in I_{\infty}
there is
j\in I_{\infty}
such that
\hat{w}_{i}=\hat{w}_{j}
and
a_{i}=-a_{j}
. Indeed, if this is not the case then the limit
\lim_{k\to\infty}\|f_{W_{k}}\|_{2}
would be unbounded. Therefore we in particular deduce that the number of unique directions of
\hat{w}_{i}
for
i\in I_{\infty}
is at most
\underline{m}
. The indices of these directions define the index set
J\subset I_{\infty}
.

Let us write

f_{k,J}(x)=\sum_{i\in I_{\infty}}a_{i}\mathbb{I}\{\hat{w}_{i,k}\cdot x\geq 0\}(w_{i,k}\cdot x).

Let us next fix a small
\varepsilon>0
and define

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\varepsilon}:=\{x\in\mathbf{S}^{1}:|\hat{w}_{i}\cdot x|\leq\varepsilon\,\,\text{for some }\,i\in J\}.

Since
\lim_{k\to\infty}\hat{w}_{i,k}=\hat{w}_{i}
, then for
i\in I_{\infty}
and large
k
we have

\{x\in\mathbf{S}^{1}\setminus\Gamma_{\varepsilon}:(\hat{w}_{i,k}\cdot x)\geq 0\}\subset\{x\in\mathbf{S}^{1}:(\hat{w}_{i}\cdot x)\geq 0\}.

Therefore, for all
x\in\mathbf{S}^{1}\setminus\Gamma_{\varepsilon}
we may write

f_{k,J}(x)=\sum_{i\in I_{\infty}}a_{i}\mathbb{I}\{\hat{w}_{i}\cdot x\geq 0\}(w_{i,k}\cdot x).

To continue we first prove that

(4.2)

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathbf{S}^{1}}|\sum_{i\in I_{\infty}}\mathbb{I}\{\hat{w}_{i}\cdot x\geq 0\}a_{i}w_{i,k}|\leq C^{\prime}

for all
k
. Then we may use compactness to obtain a convergent sub-sequence.

We note that for an arbitrary arc
S\subset\mathbf{S}^{1}
and a vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
we have the following bound

(4.3)

\displaystyle\int_{S}(v\cdot x)^{2}dx\geq c|v|^{2}|S|^{3},

for some
c>0
. We leave this easy bound for the reader to check. To prove
Eq.

4.2
, we let
\mathcal{S}
be all the sectors associated with the directions
\{\hat{w}_{i}\}_{i\in J}
given by
Definition

4.3
. Fix
S\in\mathcal{S}
and let
I_{S}\subset I_{\infty}
be the indices
i\in I_{S}
for which
\hat{w}_{i}\cdot x>0
in that sector. From
Eq.

4.3
and from

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbf{S}^{1}\setminus\Gamma_{\varepsilon}}\left(\sum_{i\in I_{\infty}}a_{i}\mathbb{I}\{\hat{w}_{i}\cdot x\geq 0\}(w_{i,k}\cdot x)\right)^{2}\,dx\leq\|f_{k,J}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq C

we deduce

\displaystyle c|S\setminus\Gamma_{\varepsilon}|^{3}\left|\sum_{i\in I_{S}}a_{i}w_{i,k}\right|^{2}\leq\int_{S\setminus\Gamma_{\varepsilon}}\left(\sum_{i\in I_{S}}a_{i}w_{i,k}\cdot x\right)^{2}\,dx\leq C.

Note that
|S\setminus\Gamma_{\varepsilon}|\geq|S|-2m\varepsilon
. Therefore since the sector
S
was arbitrary we obtain
Eq.

4.2
when
\varepsilon>0
is chosen small enough.

The bound
Eq.

4.2
implies that at each sector
S
and for the associated index set
I_{S}
there is a converging sub-sequence
\sum_{i\in I_{S}}a_{i}w_{i,k}\to\widetilde{v}_{S}
as
k\to\infty
. Therefore, we deduce that
f_{k,J}
converges to
\widetilde{g}
in
L^{2}(\mathbf{S}^{1})
and the limit function can be written as

\displaystyle\widetilde{g}(x)=\sum_{S}\mathbb{I}\{x\in S\}(\widetilde{v}_{S}\cdot x).

Recall that the sectors are by definition the arcs where

x\mapsto\sum_{i\in J}\mathbb{I}\{\hat{w}_{i}\cdot x\geq 0\}

is constant. Therefore it is clear that we may write
g_{1}
as

g_{1}(x)=\sum_{i\in J}\mathbb{I}\{\hat{w}_{i}\cdot x\geq 0\}(v_{i}\cdot x)

for
v_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. This concludes the proof.
∎

Next we prove the other implication of the statement of
Theorem

4
, i.e., that every function of the type stated in
Theorem

4
can be obtained as a limit of functions in
\mathcal{H}_{m,a}
.

Lemma 4.5
.

Assume that a function
g\in L^{2}(\mathbf{S}^{1})
is of the form

\displaystyle g(x)=\sum_{i\in J}\mathbb{I}\{\hat{w}_{i}\cdot x\geq 0\}(v_{i}\cdot x)+\sum_{i\in K}a_{i}\sigma(w_{i}\cdot x),

where
\hat{w}_{i}
are unit vectors and the set of indexes
J,K\subset I
are disjoint and
|J|\leq\underline{m}
. Then
g
is in
\overline{\mathcal{H}}_{m,a}
.

Proof.

We recall that by
Remark

2.2
, we assume that the first
2\underline{m}
coefficients
a_{i}
are alternating, i.e., we assume that
a_{1}=1=-a_{2},\dots
. We may thus construct the following function

f_{W_{h}}(x)=\frac{1}{h}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{2|J|}\sigma((\hat{w}_{i}+hv_{i})\cdot x)-\sigma(\hat{w}_{i}\cdot x)\right)+\sum_{i\in K}a_{i}\sigma(w_{i}\cdot x)

in the space of neural networks
\mathcal{H}_{m,a}
. From the above construction it follows that
f_{W_{h}}(x)
is uniformly bounded and
\lim_{h\to 0}f_{W_{h}}(x)=g(x)
a.e
x
.
∎

Recall that
\mathcal{H}_{m,a}\subset\mathcal{L}_{al}
, where
\mathcal{L}_{al}
is defined in
Eq.

2.2
. Therefore it is trivial that also
\overline{\mathcal{H}}_{m,a}\subset\mathcal{L}_{al}
. This means that we may write any function
g\in\overline{\mathcal{H}}_{m,a}
as
g=g^{s}+l
, where
g^{s}
is symmetric and
l
is linear. In the next lemma we characterize the symmetric and the linear part of
g
.

Proposition 4.6
.

Assume
g\in\overline{\mathcal{H}}_{m,a}
is written as

\displaystyle g(x)=\sum_{i\in I}\mathbb{I}\{w_{i}\cdot x\geq 0\}(v_{i}\cdot x).

Then we may split
g
into a symmetric
g^{s}(x)
and an anti-symmetric
g^{a}(x)
part such that

\displaystyle g(x)=g^{s}(x)+g^{a}(x),

where
g^{a}
is linear. Furthermore, the symmetric part can be written as

\displaystyle g^{s}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in I}\operatorname{sgn}(w_{i}\cdot x)(v_{i}\cdot x)

and the anti-symmetric part
g^{a}(x)
can be written as

\displaystyle g^{a}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\sum_{i\in I}v_{i}\right)\cdot x.

Proof.

We begin by writing the symmetric part of
g
as

\displaystyle g^{s}(x)=\frac{g(x)+g(-x)}{2}.

Applying this to
g
we obtain

\displaystyle 2g^{s}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in I}\mathbb{I}\{w_{i}\cdot x\geq 0\}(v_{i}\cdot x)-\sum_{i\in I}\mathbb{I}\{-w_{i}\cdot x\geq 0\}(v_{i}\cdot x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in I}\operatorname{sgn}(w_{i}\cdot x)(v_{i}\cdot x).

On the other hand, we may write the anti-symmetric part as

\displaystyle g^{a}(x)=\frac{g(x)-g(-x)}{2},

which then becomes

\displaystyle 2g^{a}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in I}\mathbb{I}\{w_{i}\cdot x\geq 0\}(v_{i}\cdot x)+\sum_{i\in I}\mathbb{I}\{-w_{i}\cdot x\geq 0\}(v_{i}\cdot x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in I}\left(\mathbb{I}\{w_{i}\cdot x\geq 0\}+\sum_{i\in I}\mathbb{I}\{-w_{i}\cdot x\geq 0\}\right)(v_{i}\cdot x)

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{i\in I}v_{i}\right)\cdot x.

∎

We conclude this section by noticing that if
y
is anti-symmetric, i.e.,
y(-x)=-y(x)
, then the solution of the minimization problem
Eq.

1.7
is trivial. Indeed, if
y
is linear,
y(x)=w_{0}\cdot x
, then we may easily write it in terms of
f_{W,a}
. Indeed, assuming there are
i,j
such that
a_{i}=-a_{j}=1
, we choose
w_{i}=-w_{j}=\sqrt{m}\,w_{0}
and for all other indexes
w_{k}=0
. For this choice we clearly have

\displaystyle f_{W,a}(x)=\sigma(w_{i}\cdot x)-\sigma(w_{j}\cdot x)=\mathbb{I}_{w_{0}\cdot x\geq 0}(w_{0}\cdot x)-\mathbb{I}_{w_{0}\cdot x\leq 0}(-w_{0}\cdot x)=w_{0}\cdot x.

In case
a_{i}=1
for all
i
, then
f_{W,a}(x)=\sigma(\alpha\cdot x)
is clearly the best approximation of
y
. On the other hand, it turns out that if
y
is anti-symmetric and orthogonal to the set of linear functions, then the best approximation is given by the zero function. We state this in the next lemma.

Lemma 4.7
.

Assume
y\in L^{2}(\mathbf{S}^{1})
, let us write it as
y=y_{1}+y_{2}
, where
y_{1}\in\mathcal{L}_{al}
and
y_{2}\in\mathcal{L}_{al}^{\perp}
. Then, for any
f\in\mathcal{L}_{al}
we have

\displaystyle\|f-y\|_{2}^{2}=\|f-y_{1}\|_{2}^{2}+\|y_{2}\|_{2}^{2}.

In particular, if
y\in\mathcal{L}_{al}^{\perp}
then the best approximation is given by the zero function, i.e.,

\displaystyle\inf_{W\in\mathbb{R}^{2m}}\|f_{W}-y\|_{2}^{2}=\|y\|_{2}^{2}.

Proof.

The claim follows from the orthogonality of the vector spaces
\mathcal{L}_{al}
and
\mathcal{L}_{al}^{\perp}
and the second claim follows from the fact that
\mathcal{H}_{m,a}\subset\mathcal{L}_{al}
.
∎

By
Propositions

4.6
and
4.7
one may also consider the approximation problem only in the space of symmetric functions. Indeed, by
Proposition

4.6
this is equivalent to constraining the nodes
v_{i}
in the representation of
Proposition

4.6
to satisfy

\sum_{i\in I}v_{i}=0.

5.
Uniform approximation theorem

In this section we prove
Theorem

3
which is the uniform approximation theorem for BV functions which are in the space
\mathcal{L}_{al}
. The assumption that
y\in\mathcal{L}_{al}
is natural by
Propositions

4.6
and
4.7
and for the assumption
y\in BV(\mathbf{S}^{1})
we refer to
Remark

1.3
.

The question of universal approximation is fundamental in the theory of neural networks and let us therefore discuss a classical result. Let us consider a more general space of single hidden layer neural networks
\mathcal{H}(m)
, which can be written as

\displaystyle f_{W,a}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}\sigma(w_{i}\cdot x+b_{i}):\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}

where
\sigma
is a general activation function and
b_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
. It is shown, e.g., in
[
31
]
that this space of functions satisfy the so called universal approximation theorem, which we state below.

Theorem

(Universal Approximation Theorem
[
31
]
)
.

Let
\sigma
be an activation function. Set

\displaystyle\Sigma_{n}=\text{span}\{\sigma(w\cdot x+\theta):\quad w\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\,\theta\in\mathbb{R}\}

then
\Sigma_{n}
is dense in
L^{p}(\mu)
for any measure
\mu
iff
\sigma
is non-polynomial and non-constant.

The universal approximation theorem gives us qualitative information about the class of functions that a neural network can represent. However, it does not give information about the quantitative approximation error when the number of nodes is finite.

The key idea of the proof of the above theorem in
[
31
]
is that as the size of the weights diverge to infinity we may construct difference quotients w.r.t. the weights (assuming
\sigma\in C^{\infty}
). This gives rise to polynomials and the result follows from the Weierstrass approximation theorem. As we discussed in
Remark

4.2
, the effect of cancellation is the key observation also in our setting.

We may also study this problem from the point of view of function spaces. By our earlier results we know that the space
\overline{\mathcal{H}}_{m,a}
for every
m
is contained in
BV(\mathbf{S}^{1})\cap\mathcal{L}_{al}
, where
\mathcal{L}_{al}
is defined in
Eq.

2.2
. We would like to know if the space
\overline{\mathcal{H}}_{m,a}
is dense in
BV(\mathbf{S}^{1})\cap\mathcal{L}_{al}
when we let
m
go to infinity. We solve this problem by constructing an explicit family of simple functions in
\overline{\mathcal{H}}_{m,a}
which approximate any symmetric (antipodally) BV-function well.

We begin by showing that in the four node case, we may approximate narrow symmetric step functions well.

Lemma 5.1
.

Assume that
m=4
and
\underline{m}=2
, where
\underline{m}
is defined in
Eq.

1.9
. Let
y
be a symmetric step function, i.e., in polar coordinates
y(\theta)=c\mathbb{I}_{(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})}+c\mathbb{I}_{(\theta_{1}+\pi,\theta_{2}+\pi)}
, where
0<\theta_{2}-\theta_{1}<\pi
and
c\in\mathbb{R}
. There is a function
g\in\overline{\mathcal{H}}_{m,a}
such that in polar coordinates
g(\theta)=0
for all
x\notin[\theta_{1},\theta_{2}]\cup[\theta_{1}+\pi,\theta_{2}+\pi]
, i.e.,
\text{spt}(g)\subset([\theta_{1},\theta_{2}]\cup[\theta_{1}+\pi,\theta_{2}+\pi])
and

\|y-g\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{c^{2}}{1000}(\theta_{2}-\theta_{1})^{5}.

Proof.

By rotation and scaling we may assume that
\theta_{1}=-\theta_{0}
,
\theta_{2}=\theta_{0}
with
\theta_{0}<\pi/2
and
c=1
. Now, using
Theorem

4
we see that there is a function
g\in\overline{\mathcal{H}}_{m,a}
such that
g(x)=\mathbb{I}\{\hat{w}_{1}\cdot x\geq 0\}x_{1}-\mathbb{I}\{\hat{w}_{2}\cdot x\geq 0\}x_{1}
with weights
v_{1}=-v_{2}=e_{1}
,

\displaystyle\hat{w}_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}\sin\theta_{0}\\
\cos\theta_{0}\end{bmatrix}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\hat{w}_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}-\sin\theta_{0}\\
\cos\theta_{0}\end{bmatrix}

and
J=\{1,2\}
. We may write
g
in polar coordinates as

\displaystyle g(\theta)=\mathbb{I}_{(-\theta_{0},\theta_{0})}\cos\theta-\mathbb{I}_{(-\theta_{0}+\pi,\theta_{0}+\pi)}\cos\theta.

Let us check that
g
satisfies the required conditions.

First, it is clear that
\text{spt}(g)\subset([-\theta_{0},\theta_{0}]\cup[-\theta_{0}+\pi,\theta_{0}+\pi])
. Second, by using
|1-\cos\theta|\leq\theta^{2}/2
for
|\theta|<\pi/2
we estimate

\begin{split}\|y-g\|_{2}^{2}&=\frac{1}{2\pi}\left(\int_{(-\theta_{0},\theta_{0})}(1-\cos\theta)^{2}\,d\theta+\int_{(-\theta_{0}+\pi,\theta_{0}+\pi)}(1+\cos\theta)^{2}\,d\theta\right)\\
&=\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\theta_{0}}(1-\cos\theta)^{2}\,d\theta\leq\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\theta_{0}}\theta^{4}\,d\theta\\
&=\frac{1}{10\pi}\theta_{0}^{5}\leq\frac{1}{1000}(2\theta_{0})^{5},\end{split}

which completes the proof.
∎

We are now ready to prove
Theorem

3
. We restate it for the convenience of the reader and recall that the condition
Eq.

1.11
means that
y\in\mathcal{L}_{al}
. Recall also the definition of
\mathcal{H}_{m,a}
in
Definition

2.1
.
See
3

Proof of
Theorem

3
.

Let us begin by approximating the symmetric part
y^{s}
with symmetric simple functions. We claim that given a number
N\in\mathbb{N}
we may find a function, denote it
v_{N}
, which is a sum of
N
many symmetric step functions such that

(5.1)

\|y^{s}-v_{N}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbf{S}^{1})}^{2}\leq\frac{\pi^{2}\|y\|_{BV}^{2}}{N},

furthermore,
v_{N}
can be written as

v_{N}(\theta)=\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}\mathbb{I}_{I_{k}}\text{ for }\theta\in[0,\pi),|c_{k}|\leq\|y^{s}\|_{L^{\infty}},\text{ and }|I_{k}|=\frac{\pi}{N}.

We remark that the function
v_{N}
above is not in the function spaces
\mathcal{H}_{m,a}
or
\overline{\mathcal{H}}_{m,a}
. We postpone the proof of
Eq.

5.1
at the end.

The proof now goes as follows. First, recall that by
Remark

2.2

\underline{m}
is the number of negative coefficients
|\{i\in I:a_{i}=-1\}|
and that the first
2\underline{m}
coefficients are alternating
1=a_{1}=-a_{2}=\dots
. We may also clearly assume that
\underline{m}\geq 1
. By the assumption
y\in\mathcal{L}_{al}
,
y
is of the form
y=y^{s}+l
and thus we use the first two nodes to construct the linear function
l
. If
\underline{m}\leq 2
, we cannot, in general, do better than this and thus we set all other weights to zero. On the other hand if
\underline{m}\geq 3
then we may approximate the simple function
v_{N}
from
Eq.

5.1
with the remaining nodes.

Let us thus assume that
l
is the linear part of
y
. We may choose
g_{l}\in\mathcal{H}_{m,a}
as (recall that
a_{1}=1
and
a_{2}=-1
)

g_{l}(x)=a_{1}\sigma(w_{0}\cdot x)+a_{2}\sigma(-w_{0}\cdot x)=l(x).

If
\underline{m}\leq 2
then we choose all the other weights to be zero and have
\|y-g_{l}\|_{2}^{2}=\|y^{s}\|_{2}^{2}\leq 2\|y\|_{BV}^{2}/\underline{m}
and the result follows.

Assume that
\underline{m}\geq 3
and denote
N:=\lfloor\frac{\underline{m}-1}{2}\rfloor
and observe that
N\geq\underline{m}/3
. Let
v_{N}
be the function given by (
5.1
). Now the coefficients
a_{i}
are alternating for
i=3,\dots,2\underline{m}
and by the choice of
N
we have
2N\leq(\underline{m}-1)
. Therefore we may use
Lemma

5.1
for all
k=1,\dots,N
and find function
g_{s}\in\overline{\mathcal{H}}_{m,a}
of the form

g_{s}(x)=\sum_{i=3}^{2\underline{m}}\mathbb{I}\{\hat{w}_{i}\cdot x\geq 0\}(v_{i}\cdot x)

such that (recall that
|I_{k}|=\pi/N
)

\displaystyle\|v_{N}-g_{s}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{1000}\|y^{s}\|_{\infty}^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{N}|I_{k}|^{5}=\frac{\pi^{5}}{1000}\|y^{s}\|_{\infty}^{2}\frac{1}{N^{4}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3\pi^{5}\|y\|_{BV}^{2}}{1000\underline{m}}\leq\frac{\|y\|_{BV}^{2}}{\underline{m}},

where in the second inequality we have used
N\geq\underline{m}/3\geq 1
. We choose
g\in\overline{\mathcal{H}}_{m,a}
as
g=g_{l}+g_{s}
(note
g_{l}=l
) and deduce by (
5.1
) and
N\geq\underline{m}/3
that

\displaystyle\inf_{W\in\mathbb{R}^{2m}}\|f_{W}-y\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|g-y\|_{2}^{2}=\|y_{s}-g_{s}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\|y_{s}-v_{N}\|_{2}^{2}+2\|v_{N}-g_{s}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\pi^{2}\|y\|_{BV}^{2}}{N}+\frac{2\|y\|_{BV}^{2}}{\underline{m}}\leq\frac{62\|y\|_{BV}^{2}}{\underline{m}}.

Hence, we conclude the proof once we have established
Eq.

5.1
.

To prove
Eq.

5.1
we divide
[0,\pi)
into
N
-many intervals
I_{1},I_{2},\dots,I_{N}
,
I_{k}=[\theta_{k},\theta_{k+1})
, with equal length
|I_{k}|=\frac{\pi}{N}
. First we note that we consider a right continuous representative of
y
, and define the simple function
v_{N}:[0,2\pi)\to\mathbb{R}
as follows

\displaystyle v_{N}(\theta)=\begin{cases}y^{s}(\theta_{k}),&\text{if }\theta\in I_{k}\text{ or }\theta-\pi\in I_{k}\\
0,&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

where we represent the function
y^{s}
in polar coordinates. Since
y^{s}
is a BV-function it has a derivative which is a Radon measure that we denote by
\mu_{y^{s}}
. Recall that by the fundamental theorem for BV-functions and the right continuity of
y
, we have (see
[
3
, p.139]
)

\displaystyle|y^{s}(\theta)-y^{s}(\theta_{k})|=|\int_{\theta_{k}}^{\theta}d\mu_{y^{s}}|\leq|\mu_{y^{s}}|(I_{k})\qquad\text{for a.e. }\,\theta\in I_{k},

where
|\mu_{y^{s}}|
denotes the total variation of
\mu_{y^{s}}
. Therefore, by symmetry

\displaystyle|\mu_{y^{s}}|(I_{k})\leq|\mu_{y^{s}}|([0,\pi))\leq\pi\|y^{s}\|_{BV},

and thus

\displaystyle|y^{s}(\theta)-y^{s}(\theta_{k})|^{2}\leq(|\mu_{y^{s}}|(I_{k}))^{2}\leq\pi\|y^{s}\|_{BV}|\mu_{y^{s}}|(I_{k}),

for all
\theta\in I_{k}
. We use the above inequality to estimate (by symmetry)

\displaystyle\|y^{s}-v_{N}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbf{S}^{1})}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{2\pi}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\int_{I_{k}}|y(\theta)-y(\theta_{k})|^{2}\,d\theta\leq\|y^{s}\|_{BV}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\overbrace{|I_{k}|}^{=\pi/N}|\mu_{y^{s}}|(I_{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi\|y^{s}\|_{BV}}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}|\mu_{y^{s}}|(I_{k})=\frac{\pi\|y^{s}\|_{BV}}{N}|\mu_{y^{s}}|([0,\pi))

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\pi^{2}\|y^{s}\|_{BV}^{2}}{N}.

The inequality
Eq.

5.1
then follows from
\|y^{s}\|_{BV}^{2}\leq\|y\|_{BV}^{2}
. This completes the proof of the theorem.
∎

6.
The Localization theorem

The goal of this section is to prove
Theorem

2
. We begin with the observation that by
Theorem

4
and the identity
\sigma(w_{i}\cdot x)=\mathbb{I}\{\hat{w}_{i}\cdot x\geq 0\}(w_{i}\cdot x)
, we may write every function
g\in\overline{\mathcal{H}}_{m,a}
as

(6.1)

\displaystyle g(x)=\sum_{i\in I}\mathbb{I}\{\hat{w}_{i}\cdot x\geq 0\}(v_{i}\cdot x),

where
\hat{w}_{i}\neq\hat{w}_{j}
with
i\neq j
. In particular, every function
g\in\overline{\mathcal{H}}_{m,a}
is locally linear, i.e., it is linear on arcs which we called sectors. Where the sectors are defined as the components where the function

x\mapsto\sum_{i\in I}\mathbb{I}\{\hat{w}_{i}\cdot x\geq 0\}

is constant (see
Definition

4.3
). We recall also that we denote
S_{i}=\{x\in\mathbf{S}^{1}:\hat{w}_{i}\cdot x\geq 0\}
and that the the sectors, denoted by
\mathcal{S}
, belong to the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
S_{i}
.

Since we have the characterization of the closure of
\mathcal{H}_{m,a}
by
Theorem

4
, we may actually find the function which realizes the infimum in
Eq.

1.7
, i.e., there is
g\in\overline{\mathcal{H}}_{m,a}
which is of the form
Eq.

6.1
such that

(6.2)

\displaystyle\inf_{f_{W}\in\mathcal{H}_{m,a}}\|f_{W}-y\|_{2}^{2}=\|g-y\|_{2}^{2}.

In the next lemma we show that the minimizer
g
solves a kind of Euler-Lagrange equation, which will be useful later when we study the regularity properties of
g
. As we mentioned in the introduction we will turn these regularity estimates into information on the size of the vectors
v_{i}
in
Eq.

6.1
. We stress that unlike the previous section, here we do not assume the function
y
to be in the space
\mathcal{L}_{al}
defined in
Eq.

2.2
.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
y\in L^{2}(\mathbf{S}^{1})
. Then there exists a function
g\in\overline{\mathcal{H}}_{m,a}
of the form
Eq.

6.1
given by
Proposition

4.6
which realizes the infimum
Eq.

6.2
. Furthermore, if
g^{s}
and
y^{s}
are the symmetric part of
g
and
y
respectively, then for each sector
S\in\mathcal{S}
associated with
\{\hat{w}_{i}\}_{i\in I}
in
Eq.

6.1
(see
Definition

4.3
) we have

(6.3)

\int_{S}g^{s}(x)\,x\,dx=\int_{S}y^{s}(x)\,x\,dx.

Proof.

From
Theorem

4
we deduce that there is a function
g\in\overline{\mathcal{H}}_{m,a}
which realizes the infimum in
Eq.

6.2
and we may write it as

\displaystyle g(x)=\sum_{i\in J}\mathbb{I}\{\hat{w}_{i}\cdot x\geq 0\}(v_{i}\cdot x)+\sum_{i\in K}a_{i}\sigma(w_{i}\cdot x),

where
\hat{w}_{i}\neq\hat{w}_{j}
for
i\neq j
. Let us fix
i,j\in I
, with
i\neq j
, let
S_{i}=\{\hat{w}_{i}\cdot x\geq 0\}
and
S_{j}=\{\hat{w}_{j}\cdot x\geq 0\}
and denote the arcs

S_{ij}=S_{i}\setminus S_{j}\qquad\text{and}\qquad S_{ji}=S_{j}\setminus S_{i}.

Let us assume that
i\in J
and
j\in K
. (The other cases follow from a similar argument). As in the proof of
Lemma

4.7
we decompose norm as follows

\displaystyle\|g-y\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|g^{s}-y^{s}\|_{2}^{2}+\|g^{a}-y^{a}\|_{2}^{2},

where
g^{s}
and
g^{a}
are the symmetric and the anti-symmetric part of
g
, and
y^{s},y^{a}
are the symmetric and the anti-symmetric part of y. Let us further fix a vector
u\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Since
g
is a minimizer of
\|f-y\|_{2}^{2}
in
\overline{\mathcal{H}}_{m,a}
we may consider the following variation of

\begin{split}g_{t}(x)=g(x)+t\,\mathbb{I}\{\hat{w}_{i}\cdot x\geq 0\}(u\cdot x)+a_{j}(\sigma((w_{j}-a_{j}tu)\cdot x))-\sigma(w_{j}\cdot x)),\end{split}

for all

. Then, we deduce by
Proposition

4.6
that for the anti-symmetric part of
(g_{t})^{a}
we have

(g_{t})^{a}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\sum_{k\in J\setminus\{i\}}v_{k}+(v_{i}+tu)+\sum_{k\in K\setminus\{j\}}a_{k}w_{k}+a_{j}(w_{j}-a_{j}tu)\right)\cdot x=g^{a}(x),

for all
t
. Therefore, we deduce by the above discussion that the following holds

(6.4)

\displaystyle\|g_{t}-y\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|g_{t}^{s}-y^{s}\|_{2}^{2}+\|g^{a}-y^{a}\|_{2}^{2}.

Now clearly
g_{t}\in\overline{\mathcal{H}}_{m,a}
and by the minimality of
g
together with

6.4
we get

\frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}\|g_{t}^{s}-y^{s}\|_{2}^{2}=0.

By recalling the choice of
g_{t}
and the form of the symmetric part

by

4.6
we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbf{S}^{1}}\left(\operatorname{sgn}(w_{i}\cdot x\geq 0)-\operatorname{sgn}(w_{j}\cdot x\geq 0)\right)(g^{s}(x)-y^{s}(x))(x\cdot u)\,dx=0.

Using
S_{ij}=S_{i}\setminus S_{j}
and

, the above can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\int_{S_{ij}}(g^{s}(x)-y^{s}(x))\,(x\cdot u)\,dx=\int_{S_{ji}}(g^{s}(x)-y^{s}(x))\,(x\cdot u)\,dx.

Since
g^{s}
and

and

\int_{S_{ij}}(g^{s}(x)-y^{s}(x))\,(x\cdot u)\,dx=0.

Since this holds for all

we have

(6.5)

\displaystyle\int_{S_{ij}}g^{s}(x)\,x\,dx=\int_{S_{ij}}y^{s}(x)\,x\,dx.

Again by the fact that

and

\int_{\mathbf{S}^{1}}g^{s}(x)\,x\,dx=\int_{\mathbf{S}^{1}}y^{s}(x)\,x\,dx=0.

Therefore we have by

\int_{\mathbf{S}^{1}\setminus S_{ij}}g^{s}(x)\,x\,dx=\int_{\mathbf{S}^{1}\setminus S_{ij}}y^{s}(x)\,x\,dx.

The claim follows from this and from

6.5
by using the symmetry of
y^{s}
and
g^{s}
and the fact that every sector is in the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the sets
S_{i}
.
∎
