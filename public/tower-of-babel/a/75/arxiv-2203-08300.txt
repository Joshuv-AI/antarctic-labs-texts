Abstract
Sequential Bayesian filters in non-linear dynamic systems require the recursive estimation of the predictive and posterior pdf ( pdf ) . This paper introduces a Bayesian filter called the AKKF ( AKKF ). The AKKF approximates the arbitrary predictive and posterior pdf of hidden states using the KME in RKHS . In parallel with the KME , some particles in the data space are used to capture the properties of the dynamic system model. Specifically, particles are generated and updated in the data space. Moreover, the corresponding kernel weight means vector and covariance matrix associated with the particles’ kernel feature mappings are predicted and updated in the RKHS based on the KKR ( KKR ). Simulation results are presented to confirm the improved performance of our approach with significantly reduced numbers of particles by comparing with the UKF ( UKF ), PF ( PF ), and GPF ( GPF ). For example, compared with the GPF , the AKKF provides around 50 % 50\% LMSE ( LMSE ) tracking performance improvement in the BOT ( BOT ) system when using 50 50 particles.

Introduction

Many problems in the fields of science, including statistical signal processing, target tracking, and satellite navigation, require parameter estimation in non-linear dynamic systems. In order to make
inferences
about a discrete-time dynamic system,
a
DSSM
(

) is required
, including a process model describing the evolution of the hidden states with time, as shown in (
1
), and a measurement model relating the observations to the states, as shown in (
2

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{n}

\displaystyle=\mathit{f}\left(\mathbf{x}_{n-1},\mathbf{u}_{n}\right),

(1)

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}_{n}

\displaystyle=\mathit{h}\left(\mathbf{x}_{n},\mathbf{v}_{n}\right).

(2)

Here,
\mathbf{x}_{n}
represents the hidden state at the
n

n=1,\dots,N
,

\mathbf{y}_{n}
is the corresponding observation. The process and measurement noise are represented as

and

The process function is

I-A

State of the Art — Non-linear Filters

From a Bayesian perspective on
dynamic state estimation, estimation problems are solved by constructing the posterior
pdf
(
pdf
) of hidden states based on all available information, including
DSSM
and received measurements. For problems where a real-time estimate is required after a measurement is received, sequential Bayesian filters are commonly used by recursively computing the posterior
pdf
of the hidden states
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
.
Historically, the main focus of sequential Bayesian filters has been on model-based systems with explicit formulations of
DSSM

[
1
,
4
]
. More recently, data-driven Bayesian filters have been proposed where
DSSM
are unknown or partially known, but training data examples are provided
[
5
,
6
,
7
]
. In both scenarios, the filters are broken down into essentially two stages,
i.e.,
prediction and update. The predictive
pdf
of the states is calculated in the prediction stage, which is then modified to become the posterior
pdf
based on the latest received observation in the update stage
[
8
]
.

For the model-based filters,
the predictive
pdf
of
\mathbf{x}_{n}
is obtained in the prediction stage using the process model via the Chapman–Kolmogorov equation
[
9
]
as

\displaystyle p\left(\mathbf{x}_{n}|\mathbf{y}_{1:n-1}\right)=\int p\left(\mathbf{x}_{n}|\mathbf{x}_{n-1}\right)p(\mathbf{x}_{n-1}|\mathbf{y}_{1:n-1})d\mathbf{x}_{n-1},

(3)

where
p\left(\mathbf{x}_{n}|\mathbf{x}_{n-1}\right)
is the state-transition
pdf
defined by the process model (
1
),
p(\mathbf{x}_{n-1}|\mathbf{y}_{1:n-1})
is the posterior
pdf
at time
n-1
.
Then, the updated posterior
pdf
is proportional to the product of the measurement likelihood and the predictive
pdf
as
[
8
]

\displaystyle p(\mathbf{x}_{n}|\mathbf{y}_{1:n})=\frac{p(\mathbf{y}_{n}|\mathbf{x}_{n})p(\mathbf{x}_{n}|\mathbf{y}_{1:n-1})}{p(\mathbf{y}_{n}|\mathbf{y}_{1:n-1})},

(4)

where
p(\mathbf{y}_{n}|\mathbf{x}_{n})
is the likelihood function defined by the measurement model (
2
) and the denominator is a normalization term given by:

\displaystyle{p(\mathbf{y}_{n}|\mathbf{y}_{1:n-1})=\int p(\mathbf{y}_{n}|\mathbf{x}_{n})p(\mathbf{x}_{n}|\mathbf{y}_{1:n-1})d{\mathbf{x}}_{n}}.

(5)

The
KF
(
KF
)
[
1
]
provides the optimal Bayesian solution for linear DSSMs when the predictive and posterior
pdf
are Gaussian. For state estimation in non-linear systems, the
EKF
(
EKF
)
[
10
]
is
a popular method
to approximate a recursive maximum likelihood (ML) estimate of the hidden state.

The EKF uses the first derivatives to approximate the process and measurement functions by linear equations.

However, this can cause poor approximation performance when the model is highly non-linear or when the posterior distributions are multi-modal.
The
UKF
was proposed in
[
11
]
as an alternative to the
EKF
. The
UKF
uses a weighted set of deterministic particles (so-called sigma points) in the state space to approximate the state distribution rather than the
DSSM
. The sigma points are propagated through the non-linear system to capture the predictive/posterior mean and covariance that is accurate to the third-order of the Taylor expansion
[
2
,
3
]
. The underlying philosophy is that
the approximation of a Gaussian distribution with a finite number of parameters is
more accessible
than the approximation of an arbitrary non-linear function/transformation
[
12
]
.
Compared with the
EKF
, the
UKF
can significantly improve the accuracy of the approximations. However, divergence can still occur in some non-linear problems as the state
pdf
are essentially approximated as Gaussian
[
13
]
[
14
]
.

A more general solution to the non-linear Bayesian filter can be found in the sequential
MC
(
MC
) filter, or the
PF

[
8
,
15
]
. Similarly to the
UKF
, the
PF
represents the hidden state distributions through a weighted set of points or particles. However, unlike the

, the particles of the

are chosen and updated stochastically. Specifically, the popular bootstrap
PF
uses random particles with associated weights,
i.e.,

\{\mathbf{x}_{n}^{\{i\}},w_{n}^{\{i\}}\}_{i=1}^{M}
, to characterize the posterior
pdf
as

\displaystyle p\left(\mathbf{x}_{n}|\mathbf{y}_{1:n}\right)\approx\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{n}^{\{i\}}\delta\left(\mathbf{x}_{n}-\mathbf{x}_{n}^{\{i\}}\right){,}

(6)

where

is the Dirac delta function,

M
represents the number of particles used at a given time. The key steps of the bootstrap
PF
include: 1) Draw particles from the importance density; 2) Update the particles’ weights based on the latest received observation; 3) Particle resampling
[
8
]
.
Resampling is a necessary step to reduce degeneracy. However, it induces an increase in complexity and is hard to parallelize
[
16
,
17
]
.
In
[
16
,
18
,
8
,
19
,
20
]
, various authors proposed specific variants of the bootstrap
PF
to avoid resampling by approximating the hidden state distribution at each time index with a Gaussian.

These variants include the
GPF

[
16
]
, the quasi-Monte Carlo filter
[
18
]
,
the square-root quadrature Kalman filter
[
8
]
, the multiple quadrature Kalman filter
[
19
]
, and the Gauss–Hermite filter
[
20
]
.

Different from the model-based approaches above,

several recent works
[
21
,
22
]
developed nonparametric data-driven Bayesian filters based on the
KBR
(
KBR
).
These papers represented the
pdf
as a weighted sum of feature vectors in
RKHS
owing to the virtue of
KME
.

In
[
21
]
, the
KBR
was used to derive a kernel Bayesian filter where the evolution of hidden states and the measurement model are unknown and need to be inferred from prior training data. Subsequent works
[
5
]
and
[
6
]
have proposed
KBR
-based filters for when the measurement model is only provided through examples of state-observation pairs while the process model is known.
These papers combine the parametric
MC
sampling and the nonparametric measurement model learning.

Specifically, particles are propagated using the process model. Then, the posterior
pdf
is approximated by the
KBR

[
21
]
. A variant of the
KBR
called the
KKR
was formulated in
[
23
]
to overcome some of the instabilities that can be observed in using the
KBR
. These
KME
-based methods can effectively deal with problems that involve unknown measurement models or strong non-linear structures
[
6
]
. However, the feature space for the kernel embeddings remains restricted to the training data set. Therefore, the performance of these data-driven filters relies heavily on the sufficient similarity between the training data and the test data, a problem common to all such methods
[
22
]
.

I-B

Novelties and Contributions

Inspired by the
KBR

[
21
]
and
KKR

[
23
]
, we introduce a full model-based Bayesian filter called the
AKKF
(
AKKF
).
The work presented in this paper has been built on the
preliminary work in
[
24
,
25
]
but presents detailed theoretical explanations, a wider set of applications, and computational complexity analysis.

The main contributions of this paper can be summarized as follows:

1.

We explore the potential of using
KME
within model-based filters.
The proposed
AKKF
provides a means of utilizing nonparametric data-driven filters within a model-driven framework without the need for any training data or an offline training process. Specifically,
the AKKF adaptively draws new particles based on
DSSM
to capture the diversity of the non-linearities.
The kernel embeddings of updated state particles can be seen as providing an adaptive change of basis for the high-dimensional
RKHS
.
Then, the predictive and posterior
pdf
are embedded into the
RKHS
and updated linearly.

2.

We show that, like the
GPF
, the proposed filter can avoid the resampling step found in most
PF
. However, unlike the
GPF
, it is not constrained to approximate the hidden state
pdf
as Gaussian.

3.

The proposed
AKKF
is tested on three different non-linear systems and compared with the
UKF
, an oracle
PF
, and the
GPF
to demonstrate its efficacy. The tracking
performance and computational complexity comparisons show that the
AKKF
achieves higher accuracy while requiring fewer particles.
For example, compared with the
GPF
, the
LMSE
(
LMSE
) tracking performance is improved around by
50\%
in the
BOT
system
with the target moving following the linear
CV
(
CV
) model
, given
50
particles.

Compared with the available filters, there are several significant differences and novelties of the proposed
AKKF
:

1.

The state vector’s mean and covariance (in data space) are extracted from the
KME
of the posterior
pdf
for drawing proposal particles, as shown in the proposal step of Fig.
1
.
Unlike most of the kernel-based methods where the focus is on characteristic kernels
[
21
,
23
]
, we also consider simple quadratic and quartic kernels that provide direct access to the mean and covariance of the hidden state.

2.

The proposal particles can then be precisely propagated through the non-linearity and used to calculate empirical transition operators in the
RKHS
on the fly, as shown in the prediction step of Fig.
1
.
Then, those particles’ feature mappings with associated kernel weights are used in the kernel feature space to approximate the KME of the posterior
pdf
, see the update step in Fig.
1
.
Unlike the bootstrap PF and its extensions, the particle weights can take arbitrary values and are not constrained to be non-negative or to sum to one.

3.

By embedding
pdf
into an
RKHS
, the use of the kernel function allows
the statistical inference in non-linear systems to be solved using linear algebra operations. Here the weighted kernel mean vector and weighted kernel covariance matrix are predicted and updated using the
KKR
,
i.e.,
the
KKR
is used to realize an unbiased update of the
KME

[
23
]
.

Empirical Estimation of KME

. However, these conditions are
very restrictive
and often fail, making the formulation difficult to interpret theoretically and quite unstable practically. Recently an alternative, the
KKR
(
KKR
) has been proposed exploiting the optimal linear interpretation (in the kernel feature space) of the conditional
KME
estimate that enjoys better stability
[
23
]
.

The empirical
KKR
is formulated by executing a
KF
in the kernel feature space.
An illustration of the
KKR
is shown in Fig.
2
.
Specifically, the transition matrix and the transition residual are calculated based on the training data set that is assumed to include
M
sample triples
\mathcal{D}_{\uline{X}XY}=\{(\uline{\mathbf{x}}^{\{1\}},\mathbf{x}^{\{1\}},\mathbf{y}^{\{1\}}),\dots,(\uline{\mathbf{x}}^{\{M\}},\mathbf{x}^{\{M\}},\mathbf{y}^{\{M\}})\}

[
23
]
. Here,
\uline{\mathbf{x}}^{\{i\}}
denotes the predecessor state of
{\mathbf{x}}^{\{i\}},i=1,\dots,M
, and
\mathbf{y}^{\{i\}}
is the corresponding observation of
{\mathbf{x}}^{\{i\}}
. The feature mappings of the training data are represented as
\uline{\Phi}:=\left[\phi_{\mathbf{x}}(\uline{\mathbf{x}}^{\{1\}})\dots,\phi_{\mathbf{x}}(\uline{\mathbf{x}}^{\{M\}})\right]
,

{\Phi}:=\left[\phi_{\mathbf{x}}({\mathbf{x}}^{\{1\}})\dots,\phi_{\mathbf{x}}({\mathbf{x}}^{\{M\}})\right]
and
{\Upsilon}:=\left[\phi_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{y}^{\{1\}})\dots,\phi_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{y}^{\{M\}})\right]
, respectively.
The corresponding kernel weight mean vector and its covariance matrix are
calculated following the prediction and update steps in the weight space.

In the prediction step, the kernel weight vector and covariance matrix from time
n-1
to time
n
are predicted in the weight space as

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}^{-}_{n}=T\mathbf{w}^{+}_{n-1},

(16)

\displaystyle S^{-}_{n}=TS^{+}_{n-1}T^{\rm{T}}+V.

(17)

Here, the kernel transition matrix
T
is calculated based on the training predecessor states and training states data as

T=\left(K_{\uline{\mathbf{x}}\uline{\mathbf{x}}}+{{\lambda}_{\uline{K}}}I\right)^{-1}K_{\uline{\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{x}}
,
and
V
represents the transition residual
[
27
]
.
{K}_{\uline{\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{x}}=\uline{\Phi}^{\rm{T}}\Phi
is the transition Gram matrix, and
{K}_{\uline{\mathbf{x}}\uline{\mathbf{x}}}=\uline{\Phi}^{\rm{T}}\uline{\Phi}
is the Gram matrix of the predecessor states,
{\lambda}_{\uline{K}}
is the regularization parameter to stabilize the inverse of
{K}_{\uline{\mathbf{x}}\uline{\mathbf{x}}}
. The predictive
KME
and covariance operator estimates are then calculated as
\mu_{\mathbf{x}_{n}}^{-}=\Phi\mathbf{w}^{-}_{n}
and
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{x}_{n}\mathbf{x}_{n}}^{-}=\Phi S^{-}_{n}\Phi^{\rm{T}}
, respectively.

Next, the innovation update is executed based the kernel Kalman gain
Q_{n}
calculation in the update step,
i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}^{+}_{n}=\mathbf{w}^{-}_{n}+Q_{n}\left({G_{:,\mathbf{y}_{n}}}-G_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\mathbf{w}^{-}_{n}\right),

(18)

\displaystyle S^{+}_{n}=S^{-}_{n}-Q_{n}G_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}S^{-}_{n},

(19)

\displaystyle Q_{n}=S^{-}_{n}\left(G_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}S^{-}_{n}+\kappa I\right)^{-1},

(20)

where the Gram matrix of the training observations is
G_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}=\Upsilon^{\rm{T}}\Upsilon
. The test observation at time
n
is
\mathbf{y}_{n}
, the kernel function vector between the training observations and the test observation is
G_{:,\mathbf{y}_{n}}=\left[{k}_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{y}^{\{1\}},\mathbf{y}_{n}),\dots,{k}_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{y}^{\{M\}},\mathbf{y}_{n})\right]^{\rm{T}}
.
The updated
KME
and covariance operator estimates are then calculated as
\mu_{\mathbf{x}_{n}}^{+}=\Phi\mathbf{w}^{+}_{n}
and
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{x}_{n}\mathbf{x}_{n}}^{+}=\Phi S^{+}_{n}\Phi^{\rm{T}}
, respectively.

If the
KME
contains linear functions, e.g., when quadratic or quartics kernels are used, we can directly calculate the mean and covariance of the hidden states in the data space as marginal quantities of the estimated
KME
.
Even when this is not possible, e.g., as with Gaussian kernels, a good approximation can be obtained by projecting the estimated
KME
into the data space as (
21
) and (
22
)
[
27
]
where it is implicitly assumed that functions in kernel feature spaces can reasonably approximate the linear and quadratic functions;

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{n}=X_{\mathcal{D}}\mathbf{w}^{+}_{n},

(21)

\displaystyle\hat{\Sigma}_{n}=X_{\mathcal{D}}S^{+}_{n}X_{\mathcal{D}}^{\rm{T}},

(22)

where
X_{\mathcal{D}}=\left[\mathbf{x}^{\{1\}},\dots,\mathbf{x}^{\{M\}}\right]
is the set of the current training states.

One iteration of the
KKR
. Here,
\uline{\mathbf{x}}^{\{i=1:M\}}
,
\mathbf{x}^{\{i=1:M\}}
, and
\mathbf{y}^{\{i=1:M\}}
denotes the deterministic training predecessor hidden states, current hidden states, and observations, respectively. The kernel weight mean vector and covariance matrix are predicted and updated as a KF in kernel spaces. The estimations of hidden states are found by projecting the kernel weights into the training data space. 1. Prediction step: The predictive kernel weight vector and matrix are updated based on the transition matrix
T
. 2. Update step: The posterior kernel weight vector and matrix are updated according to the information of the new observation
\mathbf{y}_{n}
.

The kernel-based filters learn the probabilistic transition and observation dynamics as linear functions on embeddings of the belief state in high-dimensional
RKHS
from training data. Note that existing filters based on the KME or the KKR are entirely data-driven, requiring the training data to provide sufficient statistics of the dynamic systems and, therefore, of use when the
DSSM
is unavailable. The tracking applications of the
KKR
so far include table tennis balls track, human motion activity estimation, and pendulum track
[
23
]
. These applications all have the weakness that the high-dimensional
RKHS
are limited with the training data, which requires high similarities between the test data and the training data.
However, the entirely data-driven filter’s tracking performance is vulnerable and will fail catastrophically when the target moves out of the training space.
This is particularly a problem in the case for the real-time tracking applications that we focus on here. To the best of our knowledge, other investigations have not considered the issue of incorporating a
DSSM
into the
RKHS
setting.

Unlike the
KKR
, this paper proposes a Bayesian filter called the
AKKF
(
AKKF
) that provides a mechanism for applying the data-driven kernel method to model-based systems. Specifically, there is no need for any training data or an offline training process of the
AKKF
.
The
AKKF
adaptively draws new particles whose weighted features match the current
KME
estimate. These particles can then be precisely propagated through the non-linearity
and used to calculate empirical transition operators in the
RKHS
on the fly. The embeddings of updated state particles can be seen as providing an adaptive change of basis for the high-dimensional RKHSs, making the non-linear function approximation more accurate and flexible. Therefore, the
AKKF
has higher efficiency and broader applications.

III
Adaptive Kernel Kalman Filter

The proposed
AKKF
aims to take all the benefits of the
KME
and
KKR
, and adapt them to work in the model-based setup.
I.e.,
the presented
AKKF
is a method incorporating a
DSSM
into
RKHS
.
In a similar manner to
the
selection and propagation of sigma points in the
UKF
, the
AKKF
adaptively updates particles whose weighted features are matched to the
KME
estimate of the current state.
Note that the AKKF chooses particles propagated through the non-linear system randomly, which is different from the
UKF
. Further, the weights of the proposed
AKKF
, unlike
PF
, do not need to be normalized or non-negative and are updated through simple linear regression.

In the proposed AKKF, the empirical KME of the hidden state’s posterior
pdf

requires a set of generated particles’ feature mappings and the corresponding kernel weights.

Fig.
1
shows one iteration of the proposed
AKKF
executed in both the data and kernel feature spaces. Specifically, particles are updated and propagated in the data space based on parametric
DSSM
to capture the diversity of the non-linearities. The corresponding kernel weight mean vector and covariance matrix are predicted and updated
by matching (or approximating in a least squares manner in the feature space) with the state
KME
.
The following presents three main steps of the proposed
AKKF
.

III-A

Embedding the Posterior Distribution at Time
n-1

Given the posterior
KME
estimate at time
n-1
, i.e.,
\hat{\mu}^{+}_{\mathbf{x}_{n-1}}
, we wish to draw new particles that better represent the probability mass of the associated posterior
pdf
.
The posterior
KME
estimate
\hat{\mu}^{+}_{\mathbf{x}_{n-1}}

comprises weighted feature mappings of the particles,
for which we use blue points to represent in Fig.
1
.

While there are sophisticated iterative methods, such as herding
[
28
]
, that can sample from the posterior distribution.
We advocate a much simpler technique in the spirit of importance sampling. Given that we can extract estimates for the mean and covariance of the state
pdf
in data space, we can draw particles in the high probability region of the
pdf
by sampling from a Gaussian distribution with matched mean and covariance. These particles can then be used to generate a new approximation of the
KME
of the
pdf
through appropriate reweighting.

Specifically, the particles and the corresponding kernel feature mappings at time slot
n-1
are represented as
\mathbf{x}^{\{i=1:M\}}_{n-1}
and
\phi_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{x}^{\{i=1:M\}}_{n-1})
, respectively.
And the empirical
KME
and the
covariance operator of
p(\mathbf{x}_{n-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_{0:n-1},\mathbf{y}_{1:n-1})
were calculated as

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}^{+}_{\mathbf{x}_{n-1}}

\displaystyle=\Phi_{n-1}\mathbf{w}^{+}_{n-1},

(23)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathbf{x}_{n},\mathbf{x}_{n}}

\displaystyle=\Phi_{n}S_{n}^{+}\Phi_{n}^{\rm{T}},

(24)

where
\Phi_{n-1}=\left[\phi_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{x}_{n-1}^{\{1\}}),\dots,\phi_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{x}_{n-1}^{\{M\}})\right]
. Then, the state mean and covariance (in data space) of
\mathbf{x}_{n-1}
,
i.e.,

\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{x}_{n-1})
and
\mathrm{Cov}(\mathbf{x}_{n-1})
, are extracted from
\hat{\mu}^{+}_{\mathbf{x}_{n-1}}
and
returned to the data space, as shown by the red arrow in Fig.
1
.

The state vector’s mean and covariance are extracted in two different ways: 1) A suitable kernel choice, i.e., quadratic and quartic kernels, can directly give the state vector’s mean and covariance if the associated
RKHS
contains linear functions.
For example, suppose
\mathbf{x}_{n-1}=\left[{x}_{n-1,1},\dots,{x}_{n-1,d}\right]^{\rm{T}}
is a
d
-dimension vector, with the utilization of quadratic kernel, the empirical
KME

\hat{\mu}_{\mathbf{x}_{n-1}}^{+}
is represented as (
25
) which contains all features of degree zero, degree one, and degree two terms;

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}_{\mathbf{x}_{n-1}}^{+}=\left[\mathrm{vec}\left(\mathbb{E}{\left(\mathbf{x}_{n-1}\mathbf{x}_{n-1}^{\rm{T}}\right)}\right)^{\rm{T}},\left(\mathbb{E}{\left(\mathbf{x}_{n-1}\right)}\right)^{\rm{T}},c\right]^{\rm{T}}.

(25)

Here,
c\geq 0
is a free parameter trading off the influence from higher-order and lower-order terms of the polynomial
[
29
]
. The utilization of quartic kernel can further provide all features of degree zero to degree four terms;
2) Otherwise, such as linear or Gaussian kernels, the state vector’s mean and covariance
can be approximated using (
21
) and (
22
).

Then, the proposal particles, shown as
green points
in Fig.
1
can be randomly sampled from the following normal distribution as

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{n-1}^{\{i=1:M\}}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\mathbb{E}\left(\mathbf{x}_{n-1}\right),\mathrm{Cov}\left(\mathbf{x}_{n-1}\right)\right),

(26)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}\left(\mathbf{x}_{n-1}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(\mathbf{x}_{n-1}\mathbf{x}_{n-1}^{\rm{T}}\right)-\mathbb{E}\left(\mathbf{x}_{n-1}\right)\mathbb{E}\left(\mathbf{x}_{n-1}\right)^{\rm{T}}.

(27)

For convenience, we draw the proposal particles from a Gaussian distribution, although other distributions with matched statistics could also conceivably be used. The proposal particles should therefore capture the location of the significant probability mass of the posterior
pdf
.
In order to use these particles to approximate the KME of the posterior pdf, we need to calculate new kernel weights for them, i.e,
\tilde{\mathbf{w}}^{+}_{n-1}
. Note that this is not equivalent to approximating the posterior pdf by a Gaussian. Instead, it can be thought of as an adaptive change of basis within the feature space which can be achieved through a simple linear mapping that we describe next. Let the proposal particles’ feature mappings be represented as

\Psi_{n-1}=\left[\phi_{\mathbf{x}}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{n-1}^{\{1\}}),\dots,\phi_{\mathbf{x}}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{n-1}^{\{M\}})\right]
,
with the associated weight vector
\tilde{\mathbf{w}}^{+}_{n-1}
and matrix
\tilde{S}^{+}_{n-1}
.
Then, the
KME
and covariance operator in (
23
) and (
24
) are rewritten as

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}^{+}_{\mathbf{x}_{n-1}}

\displaystyle=\Psi_{n-1}\tilde{\mathbf{w}}^{+}_{n-1},

(28)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{C}}^{+}_{\mathbf{x}_{n-1}\mathbf{x}_{n-1}}

\displaystyle=\Psi_{n-1}\tilde{{S}}^{+}_{n-1}\Psi_{n-1}^{\mathrm{T}}.

(29)

The formulas for the proposal kernel weight vector
\tilde{\mathbf{w}}^{+}_{n-1}
and matrix
\tilde{S}^{+}_{n-1}
are (
30
) and (
31
), respectively.

\displaystyle\Psi_{n-1}\tilde{\mathbf{w}}^{+}_{n-1}=\Phi_{n-1}\mathbf{w}^{+}_{n-1}

(30)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\Psi_{n-1}^{\mathrm{T}}\Psi_{n-1}\tilde{\mathbf{w}}^{+}_{n-1}=\Psi_{n-1}^{\mathrm{T}}\Phi_{n-1}\mathbf{w}^{+}_{n-1}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{w}}^{+}_{n-1}=\left(\Psi_{n-1}^{\mathrm{T}}\Psi_{n-1}\right)^{-1}\Psi_{n-1}^{\mathrm{T}}\Phi_{n-1}\mathbf{w}^{+}_{n-1}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{w}}^{+}_{n-1}=\left(K_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}+{\lambda_{\tilde{K}}}I\right)^{-1}K_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{w}^{+}_{n-1}=\Gamma_{n-1}\mathbf{w}^{+}_{n-1},

\displaystyle\Psi_{n-1}\tilde{{S}}^{+}_{n-1}\Psi_{n-1}^{\mathrm{T}}=\Phi_{n-1}{S}^{+}_{n-1}\Phi_{n-1}^{\mathrm{T}}

(31)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\Psi_{n-1}^{\mathrm{T}}\Psi_{n-1}\tilde{{S}}^{+}_{n-1}\Psi_{n-1}^{\mathrm{T}}\Psi_{n-1}=\Psi_{n-1}^{\mathrm{T}}\Phi_{n-1}{S}^{+}_{n-1}\Phi_{n-1}^{\mathrm{T}}\Psi_{n-1}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\tilde{S}^{+}_{n-1}=\left[\left(K_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}+{\lambda_{\tilde{K}}}I\right)^{-1}K_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{x}}\right]{S}^{+}_{n-1}\left[\left(K_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}+{\lambda_{\tilde{K}}}I\right)^{-1}K_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{x}}\right]^{\mathrm{T}}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\tilde{S}^{+}_{n-1}=\Gamma_{n-1}{S}^{+}_{n-1}\Gamma_{n-1}^{\mathrm{T}}.

Here,
\Gamma_{n-1}

represents the change of sample representation from
\Phi_{n-1}
to
\Psi_{n-1}

and is calculated as
\Gamma_{n-1}=\left(K_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}+{\lambda_{\tilde{K}}}I\right)^{-1}K_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{x}}
. The matrix
K_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}=\Psi^{\rm{T}}_{n-1}\Psi_{n-1}
represents the Gram matrix of the proposal particles at time
n-1
. The matrix

K_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{x}}=\Psi^{\rm{T}}_{n-1}\Phi_{n-1}
represents the Gram matrix between the old particles
\mathbf{x}^{\{i=1:M\}}_{n-1}
and the proposal particles
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{\{i=1:M\}}_{n-1}
at time
n-1
. The regularization parameter
\lambda_{\tilde{K}}
is used to stabilize the inverse of
K_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}
.
Note that for small feature spaces,
i.e.,

\Phi
is full rank, and
\mathrm{Dim}\left[\Phi\right]<M
, (
28
)
and
(
29
) are exact. However, to deal with ill-conditioning or where the feature space is larger than the number of samples,
e.g.,
when it is infinite, using the weight vector and covariance matrix from (
30
) and (
31
) make (
28
) and (
29
) approximate.

III-B

Prediction from Time
n-1
to Time
n

In this step, the proposal particles generated in the previous step are propagated through the process model to estimate the transition operator
{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathbf{x}_{n}|\mathbf{x}_{n-1}}
. Then the predictive kernel weight vector and covariance matrix are calculated.

Specifically, the proposal particles at time
n-1
are propagated through the transition function to calculate the
particles at time
n
,
represented as indigo points in Fig.
1
.

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{n}^{\{i\}}=f(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{n-1}^{\{i\}},\mathbf{u}_{n}^{\{i\}}),\quad i=1\dots M,

(32)

where
\mathbf{u}_{n}^{\{i\}}
represents a process noise sample drawn from the process noise
pdf
.
The feature mappings of
\mathbf{x}_{n}^{\{1:M\}}
are
\Phi_{n}=\left[\phi_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{x}_{n}^{\{1\}}),\dots,\phi_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{x}_{n}^{\{M\}})\right]
,
and the predictive KME and covariance operator are calculated by

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}^{-}_{\mathbf{x}_{n}}=\Phi_{n}\mathbf{w}^{-}_{n},

(33)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{C}}^{-}_{\mathbf{x}_{n}\mathbf{x}_{n}}=\Phi_{n}\mathbf{S}^{-}_{n}\Phi_{n}^{\mathrm{T}}.

(34)

Here, the weight vector
\mathbf{w}^{-}_{n}
and matrix
\mathbf{S}^{-}_{n}
are derived in (
35
)–(
41
) as follows.
The conditional KME of the transitional probability
p(\mathbf{x}_{n}|\mathbf{x}_{n-1},\mathbf{y}_{1:n-1})
is approximated as

\displaystyle p(\mathbf{x}_{n}|\mathbf{x}_{n-1},\mathbf{y}_{1:n-1})

\displaystyle\mapsto\hat{\mu}_{\mathbf{x}_{n}}^{-}=\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathbf{x}_{n}|\mathbf{x}_{n-1}}\hat{\mu}_{\mathbf{x}_{n-1}}^{+},

(35)

where the empirical approximations to the
conditional embedding
operator
\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathbf{x}_{n}|\mathbf{x}_{n-1}}
can be derived from a least-squares objective
[
30
]
as

\begin{split}\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathbf{x}_{n}|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{n-1}}&=\Phi_{n}\left(\Psi_{n-1}\Psi^{\rm{T}}_{n-1}+\lambda_{\tilde{K}}I\right)^{-1}\Psi^{\rm{T}}_{n-1}\\
&=\Phi_{n}\left(K_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}+\lambda_{\tilde{K}}I\right)^{-1}\Psi^{\rm{T}}_{n-1}.\end{split}

(36)

Substituting (
28
) and (
36
) into (
35
), we have the estimate of the predictive empirical
KME
of
\mathbf{x}_{n}
as

\begin{split}&\hat{\mu}_{\mathbf{x}_{n}}^{-}=\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathbf{x}_{n}|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{n-1}}\hat{\mu}_{\mathbf{x}_{n-1}}^{+}\\
&=\Phi_{n}\left(K_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}+{\lambda_{\tilde{K}}}I\right)^{-1}\Psi^{\rm{T}}_{n-1}\Phi_{n-1}\mathbf{w}_{n-1}^{+}\\
&=\Phi_{n}\left(K_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}+{\lambda_{\tilde{K}}}I\right)^{-1}K_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{w}_{n-1}^{+}\\
&=\Phi_{n}\mathbf{w}_{n}^{-}.\end{split}

(37)

Thus, the estimate of the predictive kernel weight vector is given by

\mathbf{w}^{-}_{n}=\left(K_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}+{\lambda_{\tilde{K}}}I\right)^{-1}K_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{w}_{n-1}^{+}=\Gamma_{n-1}\mathbf{w}^{+}_{n-1}.

(38)

From (
30
) and (
38
), we see that
\mathbf{w}^{-}_{n}=\tilde{\mathbf{w}}^{+}_{n-1}
.
Next, the empirical predictive covariance operator at time
n
is computed as

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathbf{x}_{n}\mathbf{x}_{n}}^{-}

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathbf{x}_{n}|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{n-1}}\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathbf{x}_{n-1}\mathbf{x}_{n-1}}^{+}\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathbf{x}_{n}|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{n-1}}^{\mathrm{T}}+\mathcal{V}_{n}

(39)

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathbf{x}_{n}|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{n-1}}\Psi_{n-1}\tilde{S}_{n-1}^{+}\Psi_{n-1}^{\mathrm{T}}\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathbf{x}_{n}|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{n-1}}^{\mathrm{T}}+\mathcal{V}_{n}

\displaystyle=\Phi_{n}\tilde{S}_{n-1}^{+}\Phi_{n}^{\mathrm{T}}+\mathcal{V}_{n}.

Here,
\mathcal{V}_{n}
represents the transition residual matrix, which is derived as

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\left(\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathbf{x}_{n}|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{n-1}}\Psi_{n-1}-\Phi_{n}\right)\left(\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathbf{x}_{n}|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{n-1}}\Psi_{n-1}-\Phi_{n}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}

(40)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{M}\left[\Phi_{n}\left(K_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}+\lambda_{\tilde{K}}I\right)^{-1}\Psi_{n-1}^{\mathrm{T}}\Psi_{n-1}-\Phi_{n}\right]

\displaystyle\times\left[\Phi_{n}\left(K_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}+\lambda_{\tilde{K}}I\right)^{-1}\Psi_{n-1}^{\mathrm{T}}\Psi_{n-1}-\Phi_{n}\right]^{\mathrm{T}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Phi_{n}\frac{1}{M}\left[\left(K_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}+\lambda_{\tilde{K}}I\right)^{-1}K_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}-I\right]

\displaystyle\times\left[\left(K_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}+\lambda_{\tilde{K}}I\right)^{-1}K_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}-I\right]^{\mathrm{T}}\Phi_{n}^{\mathrm{T}}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\Phi_{n}V_{n}\Phi_{n}^{\mathrm{T}}.

Here,
V_{n}
is the finite matrix representation of
\mathcal{V}_{n}
. The predictive weight covariance matrix is given by substituting (
39
) and (
40
) into (
34
);

{S}_{n}^{-}=\tilde{S}_{n-1}^{+}+V_{n}.

(41)

III-C

Update at Time
n

This step modifies the predictive kernel weight vector and covariance matrix calculated in the previous step, considering the new observation at time
n
.

The observation particles in Fig.
1
are updated based on the measurement model as

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}_{n}^{\{i\}}=h({\mathbf{x}}_{n}^{\{i\}},\mathbf{v}_{n}^{\{i\}}),\quad i=1\dots M.

(42)

Here,
\mathbf{v}_{n}^{\{i\}}
represents a measurement noise sample drawn from the measurement noise
pdf
.
Then, the kernel mappings of observation particles in the kernel feature space are
\Upsilon_{n}=\left[\phi_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{y}_{n}^{\{1\}}),\dots,\phi_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{y}_{n}^{\{M\}})\right]
. The posterior KME is calculated as

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}_{\mathbf{x}_{n}}^{+}=\hat{\mu}_{\mathbf{x}_{n}}^{-}+\mathcal{Q}_{n}\left[\phi_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{y}_{n})-\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathbf{y}_{n}|\mathbf{x}_{n}}\hat{\mu}_{\mathbf{x}_{n}}^{-}\right],

(43)

where the kernel Kalman gain operator denoted as
\mathcal{Q}_{n}
is applied to the correction term
\phi_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{y}_{n})-\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathbf{y}_{n}|\mathbf{x}_{n}}\hat{\mu}_{\mathbf{x}_{n}}^{-}
and is derived by minimizing the trace of the posterior covariance operator
\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathbf{x}_{n}\mathbf{x}_{n}}^{+}

[
23
]
, as in the (
44
):

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{n}

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}}\mathrm{Tr}\left[{\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathbf{x}_{n}\mathbf{x}_{n}}^{+}}\right]

(44)

\displaystyle=\Phi_{n}S_{n}^{-}\left(G_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}S_{n}^{-}+\kappa I\right)^{-1}\Upsilon_{n}^{\mathrm{T}}.

The Appendix provides the derivation details of
\mathcal{Q}_{n}
. Then, the updated
KME
vector represented in (
43
) is calculated as

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}_{\mathbf{x}_{n}}^{+}=

\displaystyle\Phi_{n}{\mathbf{w}}^{+}_{n}=\Phi_{n}{\mathbf{w}}^{-}_{n}+\mathcal{Q}_{n}\left[\phi(\mathbf{y}_{n})-\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathbf{y}_{n}|\mathbf{x}_{n}}\hat{\mu}_{\mathbf{x}_{n}}^{-}\right]

(45)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Phi_{n}\left[{\mathbf{w}}^{-}_{n}+S_{n}^{-}\left(G_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}S_{n}^{-}+\kappa I\right)^{-1}\left(G_{:,\mathbf{y}_{n}}-G_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}{\mathbf{w}}^{-}_{n}\right)\right],

where the kernel vector of the measurement at time
n
is
G_{:,\mathbf{y}_{n}}=\Upsilon_{n}^{\mathrm{T}}\phi(\mathbf{y}_{n})
, and
the Gram matrix of the observation at time
n
is
G_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}=\Upsilon^{\mathrm{T}}_{n}\Upsilon_{n}
. Hence, the weight vector is updated as

\displaystyle{\mathbf{w}}^{+}_{n}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{w}}^{-}_{n}+S_{n}^{-}\left(G_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}S_{n}^{-}+\kappa I\right)^{-1}\left(G_{:,\mathbf{y}_{n}}-G_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}{\mathbf{w}}^{-}_{n}\right)

(46)

\displaystyle={\mathbf{w}}^{-}_{n}+Q_{n}\left(G_{:,\mathbf{y}_{n}}-G_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}{\mathbf{w}}^{-}_{n}\right),

where
Q_{n}
is the finite matrix representation of
\mathcal{Q}_{n}
;

\displaystyle Q_{n}=S_{n}^{-}\left(G_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}S_{n}^{-}+\kappa I\right)^{-1}.

(47)

Then, the covariance operator can be expressed as:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathbf{x}_{n}\mathbf{x}_{n}}^{+}=\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathbf{x}_{n}\mathbf{x}_{n}}^{-}-\mathcal{Q}_{n}\Upsilon_{n}S_{n}^{-}\Phi_{n}^{\mathrm{T}}.

(48)

The derivation details are shown in Appendix. As the predictive and posterior covariance operators are

\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathbf{x}_{n}\mathbf{x}_{n}}^{-}=\Phi_{n}S_{n}^{-}\Phi_{n}^{\mathrm{T}}
and

\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathbf{x}_{n}\mathbf{x}_{n}}^{+}=\Phi_{n}S_{n}^{+}\Phi_{n}^{\mathrm{T}}
, (
48
) is rewritten as

\displaystyle\Phi_{n}S_{n}^{+}\Phi_{n}^{\mathrm{T}}=\Phi_{n}S_{n}^{-}\Phi_{n}^{\mathrm{T}}-\mathcal{Q}_{n}\Upsilon_{n}S_{n}^{-}\Phi_{n}^{\mathrm{T}}

(49)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\Phi_{n}S_{n}^{+}\Phi_{n}^{\mathrm{T}}

\displaystyle=\Phi_{n}S_{n}^{-}\Phi_{n}^{\mathrm{T}}-\Phi_{n}S_{n}^{-}\Upsilon_{n}^{\mathrm{T}}\left(\Upsilon_{n}S_{n}^{-}\Upsilon_{n}^{\mathrm{T}}+\kappa I\right)^{-1}\Upsilon_{n}S_{n}^{-}\Phi_{n}^{\mathrm{T}}

\displaystyle=\Phi_{n}\left[S_{n}^{-}-S_{n}^{-}\Upsilon_{n}^{\mathrm{T}}\left(\Upsilon_{n}S_{n}^{-}\Upsilon_{n}^{\mathrm{T}}+\kappa I\right)^{-1}\Upsilon_{n}S_{n}^{-}\right]\Phi_{n}^{\mathrm{T}}.

Therefore, the kernel weight covariance matrix is finally updated as

\displaystyle S_{n}^{+}

\displaystyle=S_{n}^{-}-S_{n}^{-}\Upsilon_{n}^{\mathrm{T}}\left(\Upsilon_{n}S_{n}^{-}\Upsilon_{n}^{\mathrm{T}}+\kappa I\right)^{-1}\Upsilon_{n}S_{n}^{-}

(50)

\displaystyle=S_{n}^{-}-S_{n}^{-}\left(G_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}S_{n}^{-}+\kappa I\right)^{-1}G_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}S_{n}^{-}

\displaystyle=S_{n}^{-}-Q_{n}G_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}S_{n}^{-}.

V
Conclusions

In this paper, we provided a new approach to model-driven
Bayesian filters. By embedding the predictive and posterior

pdf
into
RKHS
, classical
KF
calculation can be employed along with an adaptive sampling of the
DSSM
to predict the new data space information. We have observed that more feature information
of the hidden states and the observations can be captured and
recorded with a significantly smaller number of particles than are needed in
PF
-based methods while retaining equivalent estimation accuracy. Furthermore, as the new filters are comprised of standard matrix-vector multiplication operations, the overall
computational complexity is also very favorable and offers an excellent opportunity for parallelization.

This Appendix gives the derivations of kernel Kalman gain
\mathcal{Q}_{n}
and updated kernel covariance operator
\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{x_{n}x_{n}}^{+}
that follow
[
23
]
but are included here for completeness.

The trace of the posteriori covariance operator
\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathbf{x}_{n}\mathbf{x}_{n}}^{+}
is defined as
\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathbf{x}_{n}\mathbf{x}_{n}}^{+}=\mathbb{E}\left[\epsilon_{n}\epsilon_{n}^{\rm{T}}\right]
, where
\epsilon_{n}
is the error of the posteriori
KME
and calculated as

\displaystyle\epsilon_{n}

\displaystyle=\phi_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{x}_{n})-\hat{\mu}_{\mathbf{x}_{n}}^{+}

(59)

\displaystyle=\phi_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{x}_{n})-\left[\hat{\mu}_{\mathbf{x}_{n}}^{-}+\mathcal{Q}_{n}\left(\phi_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{y}_{n})-\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathbf{y}_{n}|\mathbf{x}_{n}}\hat{\mu}_{\mathbf{x}_{n}}^{-}-\mathcal{R}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\left(I-\mathcal{Q}_{n}\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathbf{y}_{n}|\mathbf{x}_{n}}\right)\left(\phi_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{x}_{n})-\hat{\mu}_{\mathbf{x}_{n}}^{-}\right)-\mathcal{Q}_{n}\mathcal{R},

where we have used the fact that
\phi_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{y}_{n})=\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{y}_{n}|\mathbf{x}_{n}}\phi_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{x}_{n})
.
Then, noting that
\phi_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{x}_{n-1})-\hat{\mu}_{\mathbf{x}_{n-1}}^{+}=\epsilon_{n-1}
,
\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathbf{x}_{n}\mathbf{x}_{n}}^{+}
is calculated as

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathbf{x}_{n}\mathbf{x}_{n}}^{+}=\left(I-\mathcal{Q}_{n}\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathbf{y}_{n}|\mathbf{x}_{n}}\right)\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathbf{x}_{n}\mathbf{x}_{n}}^{-}\left(I-\mathcal{Q}_{n}\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathbf{y}_{n}|\mathbf{x}_{n}}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}+\mathcal{Q}_{n}\mathcal{R}\mathcal{Q}_{n}^{\mathrm{T}},

(60)

where
\mathcal{R}
is the covariance matrix of the residual of the observation operator.
The trace of
\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathbf{x}_{n}\mathbf{x}_{n}}^{+}
is minimized when its matrix derivative with respect to the gain matrix is zero.

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathbf{x}_{n}\mathbf{x}_{n}}^{+}}{\partial\mathcal{Q}_{n}}=-2(I-\mathcal{Q}_{n}\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathbf{y}_{n}|\mathbf{x}_{n}})\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathbf{x}_{n}\mathbf{x}_{n}}^{-}\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathbf{y}_{n}|\mathbf{x}_{n}}^{\mathrm{T}}+2\mathcal{Q}_{n}\mathcal{R}=0

(61)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{n}=\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathbf{x}_{n}\mathbf{x}_{n}}^{-}\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{y}_{n}|\mathbf{x}_{n}}^{\mathrm{T}}\left(\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathbf{y}_{n}|\mathbf{x}_{n}}\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathbf{x}_{n}\mathbf{x}_{n}}^{-}\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathbf{y}_{n}|\mathbf{x}_{n}}^{\mathrm{T}}+\mathcal{R}\right)^{-1},
