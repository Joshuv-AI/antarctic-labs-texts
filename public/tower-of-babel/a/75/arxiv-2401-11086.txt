Lattice Boltzmann simulation of non-equilibrium flows using spectral multiple-relaxation-time collision model
Su Yan, Xiaowen Shan

Abstract
Prediction of non-equilibrium flows is critical to space flight. In the present work we demonstrate that the recently developed spectral multiple-relaxation-time (SMRT) lattice Boltzmann (LB) model is theoretically equivalent to Grad’s eigen-system [Grad, H., In Thermodynamics of Gases (1958)] where the eigen-functions obtained by tensor decomposition of the Hermite polynomials are also those of the linearized Boltzmann equation. Numerical results of shock structure simulation using Maxwell molecular model agree very well with those of a high-resolution fast spectral method code up to Mach 7 provided that the relaxation times of the irreducible tensor components match their theoretical values. If a reduced set of relaxation times are used such as in the Shakhov model and lumped-sum relaxation of Hermite modes, non-negligible discrepancies starts to occur as Mach number is raised, indicating the necessity of the fine-grained relaxation model. Together with the proven advantages of LB, the LB-SMRT scheme offers a competitive alternative for non-equilibrium flow simulation.

Introduction

N
on-equilibrium fluid flows are ubiquitous in nature and
engineering applications where the constituent molecules fail to reach thermal
equilibrium on the temporal or spatial scale of interest due to the relatively
large collision interval or
mean-free-path
[
Ivanov1998
,
Gravesen1993
,
Javadpour2021
]
. As the
Naiver-Stokes-Fourier (NSF) equations break down in such limits, the Boltzmann
equation is commonly used to determine the fluid’s behavior. The high
dimensionality of the distribution function and the complexity of the Boltzmann
collision operator (BCO) make the computational solution of the Boltzmann
equation a long-standing challenge in both applied mathematics and computational
physics.

The most popular solution method is perhaps the direct simulation Monte Carlo
(DSMC)
[
Bird1994
]
method which uses a molecular dynamic approach in
simulating the behavior of the Boltzmann equation. DSMC requires the cell size
and time step to be much smaller than the mean free path and mean collision
interval and poses significant limitations on its use in continuous
regime
[
Baker2005
]
. To reduce of the high-dimensional distribution
function, many deterministic numerical methods
[
Dimarco2014
]
have been
proposed. The discrete velocity method
(DVM)
[
goldstein1989investigations
,
bobylev1995approximation
]
uses a fixed
set of discrete velocities to approximate the velocity space and construct
discrete collision mechanism on the velocity nodes to preserve the main physical
properties of BCO. However, the accuracy is limited and computational cost high
when applied to the full BCO
[
Dimarco2014
]
. The spectral
method
[
Bobylev1988
,
Pareschi1996
,
Pareschi2000
]
expands the distribution by
Fourier series in velocity space to achieve spectral accuracy. The fast spectral
method (FSM)
[
Mouhot2006
]
further reduces the computational cost and was
successfully applied to non-equilibrium flows
[
Wu2013
,
Wu2014
]
. In addition
to Fourier expansion, Hermite and Burnett polynomials have recently been used to
approximate the distributions
[
Wang2019
,
Cai2020
]
. Instead of solving for
the distribution function itself, the moment method
[
Grad1949a
]
solves for
the low-order moments of the distribution that dictates the macroscopic
hydrodynamics. The Hermite expansion of the distribution is substituted into
the Boltzmann equation and closed at various orders and
closures
[
Struchtrup2003b
]
.

To simplify the BCO, many models have been suggested among which the simplest is
the Bhatnagar–Gross–Krook (BGK) model
[
Bhatnagar1954
]
in which the
collision is modeled as a uniform relaxation of the distribution towards its
equilibrium. The ellipsoidal statistical BGK (ES-BGK)
[
Holway1966
]
and the
Shakhov model
[
Shakhov1968
]
modifies the target of relaxation to restore
the correct Prandtl number. These kinetic models perform well in near continuous
regime but become less accurate in transition regime
[
Fei2020
]
. To improve
the simulation accuracy of transition flows, an extended Shakhov model was
proposed with incorporating more relaxation of higher-order
moments
[
Yao2023
]
. However, how to consider the relaxation process
of higher-order moments is still an issue worth exploring.

In the past three decades the lattice Boltzmann method
(LBM)
[
Chen1998a
,
Benzi1992
]
, originated from a simple cellular automaton
fluid model
[
Frisch1986
]
, has been developed into a practical computational
fluid dynamics (CFD) tool mostly independent of the classic kinetic theory. Thus
far its development focuses on the recovery of the Navier-Stokes hydrodynamics
using the BGK collision model
[
Chen1992
,
Qian1992
]
, first for
near-incompressible flows and more recently for thermo-compressible flows. After
a formal connection with classical kinetic theory was
established
[
Shan1998
,
Shan2006b
]
, the LBM can be recognized as a special
DVM scheme obtained by first projecting the Boltzmann-BGK equation into the
Hilbert space spanned by the leading Hermite polynomials and then evaluating at
the abscissas of a Gauss-Hermite quadrature so that the kinetic moments of the
truncated distribution are precisely represented by the discrete distribution
function. On the other hand the LBM can also be viewed as a computation scheme
of the moment method. This connection has paved the way for simulating
non-equilibrium flows by allowing more elaborated collision models to be
developed so that the Boltzmann equation itself, rather than its hydrodynamics,
can be simulated by LBM. In recent years, a spectral-space
multiple-relaxation-time (SMRT) collision model was developed where the
distribution is expanded into a finite Hermite series in local frame. Each of
the Hermite polynomials is further decomposed into components corresponding to
an irreducible representation of SO(3)
[
Zee2016
]
which are the minimum
groups closed under spatial rotation. The irreducible tensors are then relaxed
with independent relaxation times without violating rotation
symmetry
[
Shan2007
,
Shan2019
,
Li2019
,
Li2020b
,
Shi2021
,
Shan2021
]
.

A common benchmark problem for non-equilibrium flows is the computation of shock
structure. NSF equations only give acceptable results for very small Mach
number
[
Gilbarg1953
,
Pham-Van-Diep1991
]
. Higher order hydrodynamic models,

e.g.
, Burnett-type equations yield better results
[
Foch1973
]
but
encounter instability and thermodynamic
inconsistency
[
bobylev1982chapman
,
Comeaux1995
]
. For moment method, Grad
first used the 13-moment equations to obtain reasonable results for low Mach
numbers but artifact known as subshock occurs if
\mathit{Ma}>1.65

[
Grad1952
]
.
Hydrodynamic equations based on Ikenberry’s spherical moments and thrid-order
Maxwell iteration
[
Ikenberry1956
,
Truesdell1980a
]
were used to solve the
shock structure problem with no superiority over NSF
observed
[
street1960shock
]
. By combining the moment method and
Chapman-Enskog expansion
[
Chapman1954
]
, the regularized moment
method
[
Struchtrup2003b
]
can provide acceptable results for
\mathit{Ma}<3.0
and
always continuous smooth results over a wide range of Mach
number
[
Torrilhon2004
,
Timokhin2017
]
, but satisfactory quantitative results
cannot be obtained for high Mach number. If the accuracy of high
\mathit{Ma}
shocks is
required, the number of moments must be increased or nonlinear closure must be
used
[
Torrilhon2016
]
. For methods such as DMSC and DVM that directly solve
the Boltzmann equation, accurate solutions of shock wave structures can be
obtained.

The present work has two purposes. First we show that the SMRT model is a
discrete incarnation of the Grad’s eigen-system which, as far as we know, has
not been used as the basis of any computation. This correspondence not only
presents a theoretical framework within which the SMRT model can be scrutinized
for further development, but also provides a comprehensive numerical tool for
moments equations that enables simulation of three-dimensional unsteady
non-equilibrium flows in complex geometry. Secondly we numerically validate the
LB-SMRT scheme for computation of highly non-equilibrium flows using the shock
structure problem as a benchmark. We show that by properly choosing the
reference temperature so that the Holway’s criterion
[
Holway1964
]
is
satisfied, and by using the quadrature rules in a moving reference
frame
[
Frapolli2016
,
Shi2021phd
]
, shock structure can be computed to
\mathit{Ma}=7

while the results remain in excellent agreement with that of a fast spectral
method computation.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Sec.
2
we
briefly review the simplified collision models to which the LB-SMRT model is
related in one way or another. In Sec.
3
the LB-SMRT model is
reviewed with respect to the kinetic models. In Sec.
4
we
present simulation results of shock structure using LB-SMRT. In particular,
dependence on truncation order, comparison with the Shakhov model, and the
necessity of independent relaxation of the irreducible components are discussed.
Finally in Sec.
5
some conclusions and future prospects are
given.

2
Collision models in kinetic theory

The single particle velocity distribution function,
f(\bm{x},\bm{\xi},t)
, is defined
in the phase-space
(\bm{x},\bm{\xi})
, where
\bm{x}
and
\bm{\xi}
are the space and
velocity coordinates respectively, such that
f(\bm{x},\bm{\xi},t)d\bm{\xi}d\bm{x}
is the
probability of finding the particle in the element
d\bm{\xi}d\bm{x}
at time
t
.
Omitting external body force, the Boltzmann equation describing the evolution of

f
can be written as:

\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}+\bm{\xi}\cdot\nabla f=\Omega(f)

(1)

where
\Omega(f)
is the Boltzmann collision operator (BCO) which is a complex
integral-differential operator:

\Omega(f)=\int B(\chi,|\bm{\xi}_{r}|)\left[f(\bm{\xi}^{\prime})f(\bm{\xi}^{\prime}_{\ast})-f(\bm{\xi})f(\bm{\xi}_{\ast})\right]d\chi d\epsilon d\bm{\xi}_{\ast}

(2)

where
\chi
and
\epsilon
are respectively polar and azimuthal angles defining
the unit vector with respect to polar direction.
\bm{\xi}_{r}
is the relative
velocity of collision molecules.
B(\chi,|\bm{\xi}_{r}|)
is the collision kernel
related to inter-molecular potentials.
\bm{\xi}^{\prime}
and
\bm{\xi}^{\prime}_{*}
are
corresponding post-collision velocities. At equilibrium, the distribution takes
the form of the Maxwell-Boltzmann equilibrium distribution:

f^{eq}(\rho,\bm{u},\theta)=\frac{\rho}{(2\pi\theta)^{3/2}}\exp\left[-\frac{|\bm{\xi}-\bm{u}|^{2}}{2\theta}\right]

(3)

where
\rho
,
\bm{u}
and
\theta
are respectively the dimensionless macroscopic
density, velocity, and temperature in “energy units,”
i.e.
, the
temperature is scaled by a constant reference temperature,
T_{0}
, and all
velocities scaled by the corresponding thermal speed
\sqrt{k_{B}T_{0}}
where
k_{B}

is the Boltzmann constant
[
Shan2006b
]
.

2.1
Linearized Boltzmann equation

In the development of simplified kinetic models, the linearized Boltzmann
equation plays a significant role. Assuming the distribution is sufficiently
close to its equilibrium, it can be approximated as

f(\bm{x},\bm{\xi},t)=f^{eq}\left[1+\psi(\bm{x},\bm{\xi},t)\right]

(4)

where
\psi
is the perturbation satisfying
|\psi|\ll 1
, and
\rho
,
\bm{u}
and

\theta
in
f_{eq}
are assumed homogeneous and constant. On substituting into
Eq. (
1
) and neglecting terms quadratic in
\psi
, we have the
linearized Boltzmann equation

\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}+\bm{\xi}\cdot\nabla\psi=L(\psi)

(5)

with the linearized BCO:

L(\psi)=\int B(\chi,|\bm{\xi}_{r}|)\left[\psi(\bm{\xi}^{\prime})+\psi(\bm{\xi}^{\prime}_{\ast})-\psi(\bm{\xi})-\psi(\bm{\xi}_{\ast})\right]f^{eq}d\chi d\epsilon d\bm{\xi}_{\ast}

(6)

Furthermore, for Maxwell molecules which repel with force
\sim r^{-5}
, the
collision kernel becomes independent of
|\bm{\xi}_{r}|
and takes the form of an
eigen system
L(\Psi_{nlm})=-\lambda_{nl}\Psi_{nlm}
where
\lambda_{nl}
is
the eigenvalue
[
WangChang1952
]
. The eigen-function was obtained in
spherical coordinates
(\xi,\vartheta,\varphi)
as:

\Psi_{nlm}\sim S^{(n)}_{l+1/2}(\xi)\xi^{l}Y_{l}^{m}(\vartheta,\varphi)

(7)

which is commonly known as the Burnett polynomial
[
Burnett1936
]
.

S_{l+1/2}^{n}(\xi)
is known as the Sonine polynomials which, up to a constant
factor, is the associated Laguerre polynomials:

L^{a}_{n}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{(-1)^{k}(a+n)!}{k!(n-k)!(a+k)!}z^{k}

(8)

and
Y_{l}^{m}(\vartheta,\varphi)
the
spherical harmonics
. We note that

\xi^{l}Y_{l}^{m}(\vartheta,\varphi)
is the
solid spherical harmonics
, the
solution of the Laplace equation. The first few eigen-functions are related to
the perturbed density, momentum and energy, with the corresponding eigenvalues

\lambda_{00}=\lambda_{01}=\lambda_{10}=0
due to the conservation laws.
Additionally,
\lambda_{02}
and
\lambda_{11}
are related to the perturbed
stress and heat flux respectively. Based on this eigen-system, Gross and
Jackson
[
Gross1959
]
proposed a model for the linearized BCO which gives the
relaxation rate of any distribution,
\psi
, as:

\displaystyle L_{GJ}(\psi)=\lambda_{st}\psi+f^{eq}\sum_{nl}(\lambda_{nl}-\lambda_{st})\frac{2l+1}{4\pi}\int P_{l}(\cos\phi^{\prime})g_{nl}(\xi)g_{nl}(\xi_{\ast})\psi(\xi_{\ast})d\bm{\xi}_{\ast}

(9)

where
P_{l}
is the Legendre polynomial with
\phi^{\prime}
being the angle between

\bm{\xi}
and
\bm{\xi}^{\prime}
,
g_{nl}\equiv S^{(n)}_{l+1/2}(\xi)\xi^{l}
the radial
part of the eigen-function, and
\lambda_{st}
an arbitrary negative number. For
reasonable choosing of
\lambda_{st}
, the linearized BGK, ES-BGK and Shakhov
models can be recovered
[
leiwubook
]
.

2.2
Grad’s Hermite eigen-system

Hereinafter the standard notation of symmetric tensor is used. Defining the

symmetrization
operator:

\mbox{sym}(\mathcal{A})\equiv\frac{1}{r!}\sum A_{i_{1}\cdots i_{r}}

(10)

where
\mathcal{A}
is a rank-
r
tensor and the summation is over the
r!

permutations of the
r
indexes, the
symmetric product
of two tensors

\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
, denoted by
\mathcal{AB}
, is defined as:

\mathcal{AB}=\mbox{sym}\left(\mathcal{A}\otimes\mathcal{B}\right)

(11)

where
\otimes
stands for the normal tensor product. The
n
-th power of

\mathcal{A}
is:

\mathcal{A}^{n}=\underbrace{\mathcal{A}\cdots\mathcal{A}}_{\text{$n$-fold}}

(12)

Grad
[
Grad1949a
]
proposed to expand the dimensionless distribution in the
local reference frame in terms of Hermite orthogonal polynomials
[
Grad1949
]

\frac{\theta^{3/2}}{\rho}f(\bm{x},\bm{\xi},t)\equiv g=\omega(\bm{v})\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\bm{a}^{(n)}(\bm{x},t):\mathcal{H}^{(n)}(\bm{v})

(13)

where
\bm{v}\equiv(\bm{\xi}-\bm{u})/\sqrt{\theta}
,
\mathcal{H}^{(n)}(\bm{v})
is the
n
-th Hermite
polynomial,
\omega(\bm{v})\equiv(2\pi)^{-3/2}\exp\left(-v^{2}/2\right)
the weight
function
w.r.t.
which
\mathcal{H}^{(n)}(\bm{v})
are orthogonal to each other, and
‘:’ denotes full contraction between two tensors. The expansion coefficients
given by

\bm{a}^{(n)}(\bm{x},t)=\int f\mathcal{H}^{(n)}(\bm{v})d\bm{v}

(14)

correspond to the macroscopic hydrodynamic variables
[
Grad1949a
]
. The first
few of them are
\bm{a}^{(0)}=1
,
\bm{a}^{(1)}=0
,
\bm{a}^{(2)}=\bm{P}/p-\bm{\delta}
,

\bm{a}^{(3)}=\bm{Q}/p\sqrt{\theta}
, and
\bm{a}^{(4)}=\bm{R}/p\theta-6\bm{P}\bm{\delta}/p+3\bm{\delta}^{2}
, where

\bm{P}\equiv\int f\bm{c}^{2}d\bm{c},\quad\bm{Q}\equiv\int f\bm{c}^{3}d\bm{c},\quad\mbox{and}\quad\bm{R}\equiv\int f\bm{c}^{4}d\bm{c}

(15)

are all tensorial velocity moments, where
\bm{c}=\bm{\xi}-\bm{u}
is the

peculiar
velocity. Particularly,
\bm{P}
is the pressure tensor,
p=P_{ii}/3
the hydro-static pressure, and
q_{i}=Q_{ikk}/2
the heat flux.
Noticing that
\rho\theta^{-3/2}\omega=f^{eq}
, Eq. (
13
) can be
recognized as an expansion about the
local
Maxwellian:

f=f^{eq}\left[1+\frac{1}{2!}\bm{a}^{(2)}:\mathcal{H}^{(2)}+\frac{1}{3!}\bm{a}^{(3)}:\mathcal{H}^{(3)}+\cdots\right]

(16)

On substituting Eq. (
13
) into Eq. (
2
), Grad obtained
the Hermite expansion of the BCO. The
n
-th expansion coefficient is an
infinite quadratic sum of
\bm{a}^{(n)}
:

\bm{a}^{(n)}_{\Omega}=\sum_{r,s=0}^{\infty}\bm{\beta}^{nrs}:\bm{a}^{(r)}\bm{a}^{(s)}

(17)

where
\bm{\beta}^{nrs}
is a tensor of rank
n+r+s
. Obviously if
f
is
approximated by its first
N
Hermite polynomials,
i.e.
,
\bm{a}^{(n)}=0

for
n>N
, the quadratic sum is finite. Moreover, for Maxwell molecules,

\bm{\beta}^{nrs}=0
for
n\neq r+s
. The quadratic sum is also finite in
this case, and the Hermite polynomials are the eigen-functions of the linearized
BCO
[
Grad1958
]
. Particularly for the third-order (
N=3
) approximation,
Grad obtained a closed system where the state variables are
\bm{a}^{(0)}
,
\bm{a}^{(1)}
,

\bm{a}^{(2)}
and part of
\bm{a}^{(3)}
, which is widely known as the 13-moment system.

Alternative to the expansion about the local Maxwellian, the distribution can
also be expanded about the absolute Maxwellian as
[
Grad1958
]
:

f(\bm{x},\bm{\xi},t)=\omega(\bm{\xi})\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\bm{a}^{(n)}(\bm{x},t):\mathcal{H}^{(n)}(\bm{\xi}),

(18)

where
\omega(\bm{\xi})
is the Maxwellian with homogeneous and constant parameters

\bm{u}=0
and
\theta=1
independent of any macroscopic properties. The
leading few expansion coefficients are:

\bm{a}^{(0)}=\rho,\quad\bm{a}^{(1)}=\rho\bm{u},\quad\bm{a}^{(2)}=\bm{P}+\rho\bm{u}^{2}-\bm{\delta}

(19)

On substituting into Eq. (
6
), Grad
[
Grad1958
]
established
that for Maxwell molecules, given an arbitrary trace-less symmetric tensor

\mathbf{a}
,
\mathbf{a}\bm{\delta}^{m}
is an eigen-function of the linearized
BCO. The complete set of eigen-functions can be obtained by a
“
peeling-off
” procedure which recursively subtracts trace-less
components from
\mathcal{H}^{(n)}
and effectively decomposes it into a sum of the
eigen-functions as

\mathcal{H}^{(n)}(\bm{\xi})=\sum_{m=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\mathcal{H}^{(n,m)}(\bm{\xi})\bm{\delta}^{m}

(20)

where
\mathcal{H}^{(n,m)}
are trace-less symmetric tensors of rank
n-2m
, and the
product is a symmetric product. As
\mathcal{H}^{(n,m)}
are also orthogonal to each other

w.r.t.

\omega(\bm{\xi})
, Eq. (
18
) can be written as

f(\bm{x},\bm{\xi},t)=\omega(\bm{\xi})\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{m=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\bm{a}^{(n,m)}(\bm{x},t):\mathcal{H}^{(n,m)}(\bm{\xi})

(21)

Taking
n=2
as an example, we have
\mathcal{H}^{(2)}=\bm{\xi}^{2}-\bm{\delta}
and
\mbox{tr}\mathcal{H}^{(2)}=\xi^{2}-3
where
\mbox{tr}\mathcal{A}
stands for the trace of tensor
\mathcal{A}
. We
can hence write
\mathcal{H}^{(2)}=\mathcal{H}^{(2,0)}+\mathcal{H}^{(2,1)}\bm{\delta}
with

\mathcal{H}^{(2,0)}=\mathcal{H}^{(2)}-\frac{1}{3}\mbox{tr}\left(\mathcal{H}^{(2)}\right)\bm{\delta}=\bm{\xi}^{2}-\frac{\xi^{2}}{3}\bm{\delta},\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathcal{H}^{(2,1)}=\frac{\xi^{2}}{3}-1

(22)

Using the orthogonality relation, we have
\bm{a}^{(2,0)}=\bm{P}-p\bm{\delta}=\bm{\sigma}
, where
\bm{\sigma}
is the trace-less deviatoric stress tensor and

\bm{a}^{(2,1)}=p-\rho
. Results for
n=3
and
4
are listed in
Tables

and

. We note that although

) is valid in general, for the purposes of

\psi
in Eq. (
4
) be expanded like Eq. (
21
), the linear BCO can then be written as

L(\psi)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\lambda_{nm}\sum_{m=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\bm{a}^{(n,m)}(\bm{x},t):\mathcal{H}^{(n,m)}(\bm{\xi})

(23)

where
\lambda_{nm}
are the eigenvalues of the eigen-function
\psi_{nm}\equiv\bm{a}^{(n,m)}:\mathcal{H}^{(n,m)}
. It is evident from Table
3
that

\lambda_{00}=\lambda_{10}=0
due to conservation of mass and momentum;

\lambda_{20}
and
\lambda_{21}
are related respectively to the shear and bulk
viscosity, the latter of which vanishes in a monatomic gas; and
\lambda_{31}

corresponds to the thermal diffusivity.

The trace-less symmetric tensors,
\mathcal{H}^{(n,m)}(\bm{\xi})
, have been further developed
by a number of authors. Ikenberry
[
Ikenberry1962
]
gave its explicit
expression as

\mathcal{H}^{(n,m)}(\bm{\xi})=\frac{(-1)^{m}n!(2n-4m+1)!!}{(n-2m)!(2n-2m+1)!!}L_{m}^{n-2m+\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{\xi^{2}}{2}\right)\mathcal{Y}^{(n-2m)}(\bm{\xi})

(24)

where
\mathcal{Y}^{(k)}(\bm{\xi})
is the
k
-th solid spherical harmonics expressed in
Cartesian coordinates
[
Ikenberry1961
]
. Noticing Eq. (
7
), this
confirms that
\mathcal{H}^{(n,m)}
are the eigen-functions of the linearized BCO for
Maxwell molecules. Moreover, Ikenberry
[
Ikenberry1962
]
pointed out that the

(n+1)(n+2)/2
distinct components of
\mathcal{H}^{(n)}
, which provide a

reducible
representation of SO(3), can be decomposed into
\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1
groups, corresponding to the
2n-4m+1
linearly independent
components of
\mathcal{H}^{(n,m)}
,
m=0,\cdots,\lfloor n/2\rfloor
which furnish an

irreducible
representation of SO(3), meaning that these groups of
components are the minimum sets closed under spatial rotation. A method to
decompose a generic symmetric tensor into its irreducible components is detailed
in Appendix.

2.3
The relaxation models

Instead of linearization, another class of phenomenological collision model was
developed. Instead of carrying out the collision integrals, the distribution is
simply modeled as a relaxation towards a target distribution. Among them, the
most widely used is the celebrated single-relaxation-time,
a.k.a.
Krook or BGK, model
[
Bhatnagar1954
]
, where the target is simply the
equilibrium

\displaystyle\Omega_{BGK}=-\frac{1}{\tau}\left(f-f^{eq}\right)

(25)

Although retaining most of the essence of BCO in spite of its simplicity, a
notable shortcoming of this model is that all moments are relaxed towards their
equilibrium values at the same rate, resulting in a unphysical unity Prandtl
number among other deviations from reality.

Two well-known remedies exist in the literature. First,
Holway
[
Holway1966
]
proposed the ellipsoidal statistical (ES) model which
replaces
f^{eq}
in Eq. (
25
) with

f_{ES}=\frac{\rho}{\det(\bm{\Lambda})^{1/2}}\exp\left[-\frac{\bm{c}\bm{c}:\bm{\Lambda}^{-1}}{2}\right]

(26)

where
\bm{\Lambda}=\theta\bm{\delta}+b\bm{\sigma}/\rho
, and
\bm{\Lambda}^{-1}

its tensor inverse such that
\bm{\Lambda}\cdot\bm{\Lambda}^{-1}=\bm{\delta}
. The
Prandtl number turns out to depend on the parameter
b
and becomes
2/3
at

b=-0.5
. Another popular model is given by Shakhov
[
Shakhov1968
]
which
replaces
f^{eq}
in Eq. (
25
) with

\displaystyle f_{S}=f^{eq}\left[1+\left(1-\mathit{Pr}\right)\frac{\bm{q}\cdot\bm{c}}{5\rho\theta^{2}}\left(\frac{c^{2}}{\theta}-5\right)\right]

(27)

where
\mathit{Pr}
the desired
Prandtl number. Noticing that the expression multiplied by the factor
1-\mathit{Pr}

is in fact the eigen-function corresponding to heat flux, the effect of
Eq. (
27
) is simply multiplying the eigenvalue by
\mathit{Pr}
and leaving
everything else the same as in the BGK model.

2.4
Ikenberry’s spherical moment system

Using the solid spherical harmonics,
\mathcal{Y}^{(n)}(\bm{c})
, Ikenberry and
Truesdell
[
Ikenberry1956
,
Truesdell1956
]
introduced the functions

\mathcal{Y}^{(n,m)}(\bm{c})\equiv c^{2m}\mathcal{Y}^{(n)}(\bm{c})
and with which the
spherical
moments
as

\bm{P}^{(n,m)}=\int f\mathcal{Y}^{(n,m)}(\bm{c})d\bm{c}

(28)

It can be recognized that
\mathcal{Y}^{(n,m)}(\bm{c})
, linearly independent but necessarily
orthogonal to each other, are linear combinations of
\bm{\delta}^{m}\mathcal{H}^{(n,m)}(\bm{c})

and thus complete. The leading spherical moments are related to macroscopic
hydrodynamics as
\bm{P}^{(0,0)}=\rho
,
\bm{P}^{(1,0)}=0
,
\bm{P}^{(0,1)}=3p

which are conserved, and
\bm{P}^{(2,0)}=\bm{\sigma}
,
\bm{P}^{(1,1)}=2\bm{q}

which are non-conserved. Inspired by the thoughts of Maxwell that for Maxwell
molecules, the collision integral for a distribution in the form of a degree-
n

polynomial can be expressed as a polynomial in the moments of degrees not
greater than
n
, Ikenberry
et al
conjectured and proved the

Ikenberry’s theorem
to evaluate the spherical moments of BCO for
Maxwell molecules
[
Ikenberry1956
,
Truesdell1956
,
Truesdell1980a
]
:

\int\Omega\mathcal{Y}^{(n,m)}(\bm{c})d\bm{c}=-D_{n,m}\bm{P}^{(n,m)}+\sum D_{n_{1},n_{2},m_{1},m_{2}}\bm{P}^{(n_{1},m_{1})}\bm{P}^{(n_{2},m_{2})}

(29)

where
D_{0,0}
and
D_{1,0}
vanish due to mass and momentum conservation,

D_{2,0}
and
D_{1,1}
are related to shear viscosity and thermal diffusivity
respectively. Although formally similar to the eigen-system for linearized BCO,
the collision integral was not linearized here and hence the bilinear form on
the
r.h.s.
of which the coefficients,
D_{n_{1},n_{2},m_{1},m_{2}}
, are
non-zero only if
2m_{1}+n_{1}+2m_{2}+n_{2}=2m+n
. It should be emphasized that the
spherical moments are those of the distribution itself rather than its
perturbation.

3
The spectral multiple-relaxation-time LBM model

Although the LBM was originally developed from the LGA rather independent of
classic kinetic theory, it was later formulated as a velocity-space
discretization of the Boltzmann equation with BGK collision term based on the
absolute Hermite expansion given by
Eq. (
18
)
[
Shan1998
,
Shan2006b
]
. As the early purpose of LBM
was to recover the equations for hydrodynamic variables which are low-order
velocity moments, the expansion was truncated while preserving the dynamics of
these low-order moments
[
Nie2008a
]
. It was further noticed that for a
finite Hermite expansion, Eq. (
14
) can be calculated from values of the
distribution at a set of discrete velocities which form a Gauss-Hermite
quadrature rule in velocity space. Namely, letting
w_{i}
and
\bm{\xi}_{i}
,
i=1,\cdots,d
, be respectively the weights and abscissas of a degree-
2N

Gauss-Hermite quadrature, the expansion coefficients can be obtained as a
weighted sum

\bm{a}^{(n)}(\bm{x},t)=\int f_{N}\mathcal{H}^{(n)}(\bm{\xi})d\bm{\xi}=\int\omega(\bm{\xi})\left[\frac{f_{N}\mathcal{H}^{(n)}(\bm{\xi})}{\omega(\bm{\xi})}\right]d\bm{\xi}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}f_{i}(\bm{x},t)\mathcal{H}^{(n)}(\bm{\xi}_{i})

(30)

where
f_{N}=f_{N}(\bm{x},\bm{\xi},t)
is the order-
N
truncation of
Eq. (
18
), and

f_{i}(\bm{x},t)\equiv\frac{w_{i}f_{N}(\bm{x},\bm{\xi}_{i},t)}{\omega(\bm{\xi}_{i})}=w_{i}\sum_{n=0}^{N}\bm{a}^{(n)}(\bm{x},t):\mathcal{H}^{(n)}(\bm{\xi}_{i})

(31)

The last equality of Eq. (
30
) holds because the term in the brackets is
a polynomial of a degree not exceeding
2N
. Eqs. (
30
) and
(
31
) define an
isomorphism
between
\bm{a}^{(n)}(\bm{x},t)
and

f_{i}(\bm{x},t)
. The dynamic equations of
f_{i}
can be obtained by projecting the
Boltzmann-BGK equation into the Hilbert space spanned by the leading Hermite
polynomials and evaluating at
\bm{\xi}_{i}
:

\frac{\partial f_{i}}{\partial t}+\bm{\xi}_{i}\cdot\nabla f_{i}=-\frac{1}{\tau}\left(f_{i}-f^{eq}_{i}\right)

(32)

where
f^{eq}_{i}
is the truncated Hermite expansion of the Maxwellian evaluated
at
\bm{\xi}_{i}
. After discretizing space and time, the popular LBGK schemes are
arrived at
[
Shan2006b
]
. Two remarks can be made at this point. First, the
expansion about absolute Maxwellian instead of the local one is used because it
results in constant
\bm{\xi}_{i}
which, when aligned with the underlying lattice,
yields the computationally efficient stream-collide LB algorithm. Second,
unlike the original LBGK models obtained
a posteriori
by enforcing
compliance with near-incompressible Navier-Stokes equations, the kinetic
theoretic derivation results in thermal compressible hydrodynamics and those
beyond, essentially providing a potential approach of discretizing the Boltzmann
equation itself.

One of the defects that LBGK inherited from BGK model is the fixed unity Prandtl
number due to coupled viscous and thermal transport. The
multiple-relaxation-time (MRT) LB scheme
[
d'Humieres1992
]
attempted to
solve this problem by assigning separate relaxation times to each eigen modes of
the discrete distribution. However, as the eigen-decomposition depends on the
underlying lattice, and the commonly-used lattices are not sufficient to recover
the energy equation, the issue of coupled viscous-thermal transport was not
resolved.

Based on the Hermite-expansion derivation of LBM, a scheme was proposed to
assign a separate relaxation time to each Hermite term
[
Shan2007
]
. Although
a variable Prandtl number was successfully obtained, the thermal transport was
found to depend on the Mach number when the thermal and viscous relaxation rates
are different. This abnormality was found to be caused by the fact that the
relaxations, essentially local physics, were incorrectly associated with the

raw
moments in the absolute frame. By converting
via
binomial
transform the raw moments to the
central
moments in local frame before
relaxing them, a Galilean invariant multiple-relaxation-time LB collision model
in Hermite spectral space (SMRT) was obtained
[
Shan2019
,
Li2019
]
. Further
realizing that the minimum sets of components of the tensorial moments that can
be relaxed together without violating rotation symmetry correspond to the
irreducible tensors
[
Li2020b
]
, we arrived at the SMRT model
[
Shan2021
]

which is defined
via
the local Hermite expansion
[
Shan2021
]

f(\bm{x},\bm{v},t)=\omega(\bm{v})\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\bm{d}^{(n)}(\bm{x},t):\mathcal{H}^{(n)}(\bm{v})

(33)

The above is otherwise identical to Eq. (
13
) except that the
factor
\rho\theta^{-3/2}
is now absorbed into the expansion coefficients,

\bm{d}^{(n)}
, which are related to the coefficients of the absolute
expansion (
18
)
via
binomial transform and
vice
versa

[
Shan2021
]
. After decomposing
\mathcal{H}^{(n)}
into its irreducible
components, we can write

f(\bm{x},\bm{v},t)=\omega(\bm{v})\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{m=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\bm{d}^{(n,m)}(\bm{x},t):\mathcal{H}^{(n,m)}(\bm{v})

(34)

Denoting by
\bm{d}^{(n,m)}_{eq}
and
\bm{d}^{(n,m)}_{\Omega}
the expansion coefficients of
the Maxwellian and collision term respectively, and noting that
\bm{d}^{(0,0)}_{eq}=\rho
and
\bm{d}^{(n,m)}_{eq}=0
for all
n>0
, the SMRT operator is defined by

\bm{d}^{(n,m)}_{\Omega}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0,&n=0,1\\
-\dfrac{1}{\tau_{n,m}}\left[\bm{d}^{(n,m)}-\bm{d}^{(n,m)}_{eq}\right]=-\dfrac{\bm{d}^{(n,m)}}{\tau_{n,m}},&n\geq 2\end{array}\right.

(35)

where
\tau_{n,m}
is the relaxation time of the mode
\mathcal{H}^{(n,m)}
. Particularly,

\tau_{2,0}
and
\tau_{2,1}
dictate respectively the shear and bulk
viscosity of which the latter vanishes in a monatomic gas, and
\tau_{3,1}

gives the thermal diffusivity. The other relaxation times, although potentially
significant in non-equilibrium flows
[
Shi2021
]
, have no hydrodynamic
correspondence.

The relations between SMRT and the relaxation models can be easily identified.
First of all, when all collision frequencies are the same, Eq. (
35
)
reduces to the BGK model regardless the frame in which the expansion is carried
out. Noticing that the second term on the
r.h.s.
of
Eq. (
27
) is actually
\frac{1}{3!}(1-\mathit{Pr})\bm{d}^{(3,1)}:\mathcal{H}^{(3,1)}
, it
can be recognized that the Shakhov model is the special case of
Eq. (
35
) with
\tau_{3,1}=\tau_{2,0}/\mathit{Pr}
, and

\tau_{n,m}=\tau_{2,0}
for all others. For the ES model, we point out
that
\bm{\Lambda}
is nothing but the weighted sum of two second-order
irreducible tensors with
b
being the proportional constant.

With
\mathcal{H}^{(n,m)}
being the eigen-functions of the linearized BCO for Maxwell
molecules, the SMRT is somewhat theoretically justifiable by the rationale that

\mathcal{H}^{(n,m)}
can serve as a good basis to approximate the BCO in general as
suggested by Grad
[
Grad1958
]
and others. In the rest of the paper we shall
numerically examine the performance of SMRT in the highly non-equilibrium
problem of shock structure.

4
Numerical Implementation and Results

The profile of a normal shock is a typical non-linear and non-equilibrium
benchmark where the variation of macroscopic properties across the shock region
is steep, and the degree of non-equilibrium increases with the Mach number. To
benchmark the ability of SMRT-LB in simulating non-equilibrium flows, we compute
the profile of an one-dimensional (1D) shock in a monatomic gas for Mach numbers
ranging from 1.5 to 7.

4.1
Convergence of Hermite expansion and the shifted-stencil

By the theory of generalized Fourier expansion, any square-integrable function
in the sense that
\int\omega f^{2}d\bm{\xi}
exists can be expanded as the Hermite
series

f=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\bm{a}^{(n)}:\mathcal{H}^{(n)}(\bm{\xi})

(36)

The condition for expansion (
18
) to converge calls for the following
integral to exist:

\int\omega\left[\frac{f(\bm{\xi})}{\omega(\bm{\xi})}\right]^{2}d\bm{\xi}=\int\exp\left(\frac{\xi^{2}}{2}\right)f^{2}(\bm{\xi})d\bm{\xi}

(37)

Assuming that
f\sim f^{eq}
as
\xi\rightarrow\infty
, the convergence
condition is hence
\theta<2
,
i.e.
, the highest temperature in the
domain cannot exceed twice of the reference temperature. Note that in certain
moment-method calculations
[
Grad1952
,
Holway1964
,
Cai2019
]
, the reference
temperature was chosen as the upper-stream (colder) temperature which leads to a
restriction on the down-stream (hotter) temperature and the maximum achievable
Mach number in turn. In the present work, the reference temperature was not
tied to any physical temperature but chosen to ensure that the highest
dimensionless temperature is always
<2
.

Another hidden parameter implicitly dictated by
\omega(\bm{\xi})
is the velocity
of the reference frame. Galilean invariance principle implies that moments in
all inertial frames are equal in representing the macroscopic hydrodynamics –
they are in fact related to each other
via
binomial
transform
[
Shan2021
]
. However, as shown in Appendix
B
, the
Hermite expansion converges faster if the distribution is closer to

\omega(\bm{\xi})
. In the present work, we shift the quadrature abscissas,

\bm{\xi}_{i}
, by a constant
\bar{\bm{u}}=c_{l}\bm{k}
where
c_{l}
is the lattice
constant of the quadrature
[
Shan2016
]
and
\bm{k}
a constant integer
vector chosen to minimize the maximum velocity in the frame moving with

\bar{\bm{u}}
. Obviously, with the same weights
w_{i}
, the abscissas

\bar{\bm{u}}+\bm{\xi}_{i}
constitute a quadrature rule of the same order in the moving
frame, hence the corresponding discrete distributions are isomorphic to the
moments therein and can be used as the state variable in LB. We note that the
shifted-stencil technique was also employed in Ref.
[
Frapolli2016
]
with a
more involved argument.

4.2
Problem Statement and Implementation Details

For a normal shock, the ratios between the up- and down-stream density,
\rho_{1}

and
\rho_{2}
, fluid velocity relative to the shock,
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
, and
temperature,
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}

relations
[
Landau1987
]

\frac{\rho_{2}}{\rho_{1}}=\frac{u_{1}}{u_{2}}=\frac{(\gamma+1)\mathit{Ma}^{2}}{(\gamma-1)\mathit{Ma}^{2}+2},\quad\mbox{and}\quad\frac{\theta_{2}}{\theta_{1}}=\frac{\left(2\gamma\mathit{Ma}^{2}-\gamma+1\right)\left[(\gamma-1)\mathit{Ma}^{2}+2\right]}{(\gamma+1)^{2}\mathit{Ma}}

(38)

where
\gamma

\mathit{Ma}\equiv u_{1}/c_{1}

Conclusion

We demonstrate that the physical foundation of the SMRT model is equivalent to
Grad’s eigen-system where the eigen-state, obtained by irreducible tensor
decomposition of Hermite polynomials, is those of the linearized Boltzmann
equation for Maxwell molecules. The LB-SMRT scheme can be viewed as a fast
discretization of moment method or a DVM scheme with the discrete velocities
chosen to ensure exact recovery of the moments. Numerical results indicate that
retaining moments only up to the fifth order, the strongly non-equilibrium flow
inside a shock at high Mach numbers (
\mathit{Ma}>7
) can be computed to satisfactory
accuracy provided that 1) the reference temperature and velocity are properly
chosen to ensure fast convergence of the Hermite expansion, and 2) all
irreducible tensor components are relaxed with the correct relaxation times. If
a simplified set of relaxation times is used, such as in the Shakhov model or
the lumped-sum Hermite polynomials relaxation, significant discrepancies can
occur, first in high-order moments and then percolating to the lower moments as
Mach number is increased.

We note that the analysis and calculation here are based on Maxwell molecules.
In a more general case of realistic molecules, there should be cross-talk
between moments of different orders. Although Grad has pointed out that the
coupling of this cross-talk is weak
[
Grad1958
]
, its role still needs to be
investigated in strong non-equilibrium situations. Additionally, due to the
boundary-free nature of shock wave structures, the performance of SMRT with
gas-wall interaction remains to be studied.
