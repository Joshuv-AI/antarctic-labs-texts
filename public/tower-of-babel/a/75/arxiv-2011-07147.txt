Elastic Wave Near-Cloaking
Davide Enrico Quadrelli, Richard Craster, Muamer Kadic, Francesco Braghin

Abstract
Cloaking elastic waves has, in contrast to the cloaking of electromagnetic waves, remained a fundamental challenge: the latter successfully uses the invariance of Maxwell’s equations, from which the field of transformational optics has emerged, whereas the elastic Navier equations are not invariant under coordinate transformations. Our aim is to overcome this challenge, at least in practical terms, and thereby unlock applications in mechanics, ultrasound, vibration mitigation, non-destructive evaluation and elastic wave control. We achieve near-cloaking by recognising that, despite the lack of invariance, a decoupling into a system of form invariant potential equations together with a quantifiable approximation, can be used effectively in many cases to control the flow of elastodynamic waves. Here, in particular we focus on the efficiency and practicability of the proposed near-cloaking which is illustrated using carpet cloaks to hide surface defects from incoming compressional and shear in-plane waves and from surface elastic Rayleigh waves.

Several ideas have been advanced to sidestep this restriction and allow for cloaking, for instance using a pentamode assumption which in principle decouples the longitudinal and transverse waves by canceling the latter

2
Theory

2.1
Transformation

We consider a point-wise deformation from the undeformed virtual domain
\boldsymbol{X}\in\Xi
to the deformed
domain

\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{\chi}(\boldsymbol{X})\in\xi
with the deformation gradient defined by
\boldsymbol{F}=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{X}}\boldsymbol{x}
with
\boldsymbol{F}^{-1}=\nabla\boldsymbol{X}
, where
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{X}}
represents the differential operator
for the gradient
in the original undeformed coordinates, whereas
\nabla
refers to the same operator in the deformed configuration
[
4
]
. Similarly capital and plain letters will
henceforth distinguish
undeformed and deformed configurations respectively. The Jacobian
J=\det\boldsymbol{F}
represents the ratio of volume elements between deformed and undeformed configurations. We note that the polar decomposition of the deformation gradient reads
\boldsymbol{F}=\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{R}
, with
\boldsymbol{R}
a local rigid rotation (
\boldsymbol{R}^{T}\boldsymbol{R}=\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{R}^{T}=\boldsymbol{I},\det\boldsymbol{R}=1)
and
\boldsymbol{V}
the symmetric positive definite left stretch tensor satisfying
\boldsymbol{V}^{2}=\boldsymbol{F}\boldsymbol{F}^{T}
.

A conformal map is defined as a map that preserves angles
[
20
,
42
]
and in the language of finite deformation, it can be generally described as a homogeneous stretch superimposed to a local rigid rotation:

\boldsymbol{F}=n\boldsymbol{I}\boldsymbol{R}

(1)

with
\boldsymbol{V}=n\boldsymbol{I}
and
n
being the stretch experienced isotropically by every fiber of space
;
the change of volume
is

J=n^{m}
,
where
m
is
the dimension of the problem. Under the transformation, the Laplacian in the undeformed configuration transforms according to
[
43
]
as:

\nabla_{\boldsymbol{X}}^{2}=J\nabla\cdot(J^{-1}\boldsymbol{V}^{2}\nabla)

(2)

that, for the conformal map, is

\nabla_{\boldsymbol{X}}^{2}=n^{m}\nabla\cdot(n^{2-m}\nabla),

(3)

In the following sections we apply this transformation to the elasticity equations.

2.2
Antiplane Motion (SH waves)

First, we set the scene using anti-plane shear waves
where the displacement
U_{3}(X_{1},X_{2})
is governed by the two-dimensional scalar wave equation:

\bar{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{X}}\cdot(\mu_{0}\bar{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{X}}U_{3})=\rho_{0}\frac{\partial^{2}U_{3}}{\partial t^{2}}

(4)

with
\bar{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{X}}
as the differential operator in two dimensions,
\mu_{0}
and
\rho_{0}
the shear modulus and the mass density, respectively;
for homogeneous media (
4
) is:

\bar{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{X}}^{2}U_{3}=\frac{1}{c_{S0}^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}U_{3}}{\partial t^{2}}

(5)

with shear wavespeed
c_{S0}=\sqrt{\displaystyle\frac{\mu_{0}}{\rho_{0}}}
.
Applying the conformal map (
3
) then gives

\bar{\nabla}^{2}u_{3}=\frac{1}{n^{2}c_{S0}^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}u_{3}}{\partial t^{2}}

(6)

where
u_{3}(\boldsymbol{x}(\boldsymbol{X}))=U_{3}(\boldsymbol{X})
.
It is now possible to deduce a new material, occupying the domain
\xi
, with shear modulus
\mu
and density
\rho
such that it mimics the virtual domain
. The wave equation governing its anti-plane motion reads:

\bar{\nabla}\mu\cdot\bar{\nabla}u_{3}+\mu\bar{\nabla}^{2}u_{3}=\rho\frac{\partial^{2}u_{3}}{\partial t^{2}}

(7)

and if the gradient of
\mu
is null, then it is sufficient that

\displaystyle\frac{\mu}{\rho}=n^{2}c_{S0}^{2}

for the medium to mimic the virtual domain. In particular, selecting material properties
\mu=\mu_{0}
and
\rho={\rho_{0}}/{n^{2}}
gives the perfect conformal cloak and this process mirrors that in electromagnetism or acoustics
[
44
,
45
]
.

2.3
In Plane Motion (P and SV waves)

We now turn our attention to the full
vector equations of elastodynamics that do not allow the neat process in the previous section to be performed exactly. The vector displacement
\boldsymbol{U}
in homogeneous media obeys the elastodynamic Navier wave equation
[
46
]
:

(\lambda_{0}+2\mu_{0})\nabla_{\boldsymbol{X}}(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{X}}\cdot\boldsymbol{U})-\mu_{0}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{X}}\times\nabla_{\boldsymbol{X}}\times\boldsymbol{U}=\rho_{0}\frac{\partial^{2}\boldsymbol{U}}{\partial t^{2}}

(8)

with
\lambda_{0}
and
\mu_{0}
the Lamè parameters of the material. The Helmholtz decomposition

\boldsymbol{U}=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{X}}\Phi+\nabla_{\boldsymbol{X}}\times\boldsymbol{\Psi},\quad\nabla_{\boldsymbol{X}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\Psi}=0

(9)

using the scalar compressional
\Phi
, and vector shear
\boldsymbol{\Psi}
, potentials
gives two wave equations:

\nabla_{\boldsymbol{X}}^{2}\Phi=\frac{1}{c_{P0}^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}\Phi}{\partial t^{2}},\qquad\nabla_{\boldsymbol{X}}^{2}\Psi_{i}=\frac{1}{c_{S0}^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}\Psi_{i}}{\partial t^{2}}\qquad i=1,2,3

(10)

with compressional wavespeed
c_{P0}^{2}=({\lambda_{0}+2\mu_{0}})/{\rho_{0}}
and shear wavespeed
c_{S0}^{2}={\mu_{0}}/{\rho_{0}}
. At first sight these appear as decoupled wave equations and naively applying the same process, using the conformal map, as in anti-plane elasticity appears attractive. In terms of an exact solution this idea is flawed as traction-free boundary conditions implicitly couple the potentials.

We consider plane wave propagation in the
X_{1},X_{2}
plane: an harmonic P-wave and shear waves are obtained from

\Phi(\boldsymbol{X},t)=\Phi_{0}e^{j(\omega t-\boldsymbol{\kappa}\cdot\boldsymbol{X})},\qquad\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\boldsymbol{X},t)=\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{0}e^{j(\omega t-\boldsymbol{\kappa}\cdot\boldsymbol{X})}

(11)

respectively.
We align our axes so that for plane wave propagation no gradients are observed in the third direction, and the problem is then essentially two-dimensional, and the wave equation for the scalar potential becomes:

\bar{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{X}}^{2}\Phi=\frac{1}{c_{P0}^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}\Phi}{\partial t^{2}}

(12)

Moreover, the displacements associated with shear waves, are expressed in terms of
\boldsymbol{\Psi}
as

\boldsymbol{U}=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{X}}\times\boldsymbol{\Psi}=\begin{bmatrix}\Psi_{3,2}\\
-\Psi_{3,1}\\
\Psi_{2,1}-\Psi_{1,2}\end{bmatrix}

(13)

For a SV wave there is no antiplane motion thus:

\Psi_{2,1}-\Psi_{1,2}=0

(14)

and without loss of generality
\Psi_{1}
and
\Psi_{2}
are taken zero, thus the vector wave equation governing plane shear waves is the two-dimensional scalar wave equation in
\Psi_{3}
:

\bar{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{X}}^{2}\Psi_{3}=\frac{1}{c_{S0}^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}\Psi_{3}}{\partial t^{2}}

(15)

To summarise, the equations for in-plane motion are:

\bar{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{X}}^{2}\Phi=\frac{1}{c_{P0}^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}\Phi}{\partial t^{2}},\qquad\bar{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{X}}^{2}\Psi_{3}=\frac{1}{c_{S0}^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}\Psi_{3}}{\partial t^{2}}.

(16)

As previously seen in section
2.1
, after transformation, these equations become

\bar{\nabla}^{2}\phi=\frac{1}{n^{2}c_{P0}^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}\psi}{\partial t^{2}},\qquad\bar{\nabla}^{2}\psi_{3}=\frac{1}{n^{2}c_{S0}^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}\phi_{3}}{\partial t^{2}}.

(17)

To find the corresponding material properties that, in the physical domain, mimic the wave propagation occurring in virtual domain, we now consider the elastodynamic wave equations for general inhomogeneous media:

\begin{split}&\nabla\lambda(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{u})+\nabla\mu\cdot[\nabla\boldsymbol{u}+(\nabla\boldsymbol{u})^{T}]+\\
&+(\lambda+2\mu)\nabla(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{u})-\mu\nabla\times\nabla\times\boldsymbol{u}=\rho\frac{\partial^{2}{\boldsymbol{u}}}{\partial t^{2}}.\end{split}

(18)

If the gradient of the Lamé parameters is null, upon the substitution:

\boldsymbol{u}=\nabla\phi+\nabla\times\boldsymbol{\psi}

(19)

the following is obtained:

\begin{split}&(\lambda+2\mu)\nabla(\nabla^{2}\phi)-\rho\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}\nabla\phi=\\
&=-\left[\mu\nabla\times\nabla^{2}\boldsymbol{\psi}-\rho\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}(\nabla\times\boldsymbol{\psi})\right]\end{split}

(20)

This is rearranged as:

\begin{split}&\nabla\left[(\lambda+2\mu)\nabla^{2}\phi-\rho\frac{\partial^{2}\phi}{\partial t^{2}}\right]+\nabla\rho\frac{\partial^{2}\phi}{\partial t^{2}}=\\
&=\nabla\times\left[\mu\nabla^{2}\boldsymbol{\psi}-\rho\frac{\partial^{2}\boldsymbol{\psi}}{\partial t^{2}}\right]+\nabla\rho\times\frac{\partial^{2}\boldsymbol{\psi}}{\partial t^{2}}\end{split}

(21)

thus, if the gradient of density can be considered negligible, then the identity is satisfied by

\begin{split}&(\lambda+2\mu)\nabla^{2}\phi-\rho\frac{\partial^{2}\phi}{\partial t^{2}}=0\\
&\mu\nabla^{2}\boldsymbol{\psi}-\rho\frac{\partial^{2}\boldsymbol{\psi}}{\partial t^{2}}=0\end{split}

(22)

and the same decomposition in scalar and vector potentials is seen to be applied as in the case of the virtual domain. The cloak for plane waves can then be performed by scaling both longitudinal and transversal wave speeds according to the local metric change of the transformation.
The displacements will be then associated to the gradient of
\phi
and to the curl of
\boldsymbol{\psi}
.

\boldsymbol{u}=\nabla\phi+\nabla\times\boldsymbol{\psi}

(23)

since for the chain rule
\nabla=\boldsymbol{F}^{-T}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{X}}
, then, recalling that
\boldsymbol{V}\in\mathrm{Sym}^{+}
is symmetric and
\boldsymbol{R}
is orthogonal:

\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{F}^{-T}\boldsymbol{U}=\boldsymbol{V}^{-1}\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{U}=\frac{1}{n}\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{U}

(24)

This means that the displacements assume the same local rotation of the medium and are simply scaled according to the local stretch.

Clearly an assumption has been made above regarding the density gradient and we now assess and quantify the impact, and limitations, of doing so, by obtaining a global performance index for the cloak. Turning to Eq. (
21
): considering the term proportional to
\nabla\rho
as being negligible, is also interpreted as adding an extra term to each side of (
20
), such that the the left hand side is rewritten as:

(\lambda+2\mu)\nabla(\nabla^{2}\phi)-\rho\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}\nabla\phi-\nabla\rho\frac{\partial^{2}\phi}{\partial t^{2}}

(25)

while the right hand side becomes:

-\left[\mu\nabla\times\nabla^{2}\boldsymbol{\psi}-\rho\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}(\nabla\times\boldsymbol{\psi})-\nabla\rho\times\frac{\partial^{2}\boldsymbol{\psi}}{\partial t^{2}}\right]

(26)

We use the relation for plane waves in homogeneous media
\phi=\hat{\phi}e^{i(\omega t-\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{P}\cdot\boldsymbol{x})}
to make estimates of the modulus of the last two terms in (
25
):

\left|\rho\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}\nabla\phi\right|\approx\rho_{0}\omega^{2}\kappa_{P}\hat{\phi};\qquad\left|\nabla\rho\frac{\partial^{2}\phi}{\partial t^{2}}\right|\approx<\nabla\rho>\omega^{2}\hat{\phi}

(27)

having introduced
<\nabla\rho>
, i.e. the averaged value of the gradient of density over the surface of the cloak, as an estimate of
\nabla\rho
. The order of magnitude of the ratio between the two is:

O\left(\frac{<\nabla\rho>/\rho_{0}}{\kappa_{P}}\right)=O\left(\frac{<1/n^{2}>c_{P}}{2\pi f}\right)

(28)

which shows this added extra term can be reasonably considered negligible in the limit where
\kappa_{P}
becomes very large and/or the fluctuation in density with respect to background value (i.e. the fluctuations of metric change) are small. A similar approach applies for the last two terms in (
26
): assuming
\boldsymbol{\psi}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}e^{i(\omega t-\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{S}\cdot\boldsymbol{x})}
the modulus of the related terms become:

\left|\rho\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}(\nabla\times\boldsymbol{\psi})\right|\approx\rho_{0}\omega^{2}\kappa_{S}|\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}|;\quad\left|\nabla\rho\times\frac{\partial^{2}\boldsymbol{\psi}}{\partial t^{2}}\right|\approx<\nabla\rho>\omega^{2}|\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}|

(29)

which means the ratio scales as

O\left(\frac{<\nabla\rho>/\rho_{0}}{\kappa_{S}}\right)=O\left(\frac{<1/n^{2}>c_{S}}{2\pi f}\right)

(30)

As a consequence, for a given transformation the cloak is expected to have increasingly good performances for all frequencies above a certain cutoff, which is set from the conditions expressed by requiring the adimensional ratios (
28
) and (
30
) being small. Note that, since the wavelength of P waves is always longer than that of shear waves (
c_{P}>c_{S}
), the condition set by
\kappa_{P}
is the one that is more demanding. The difference between (
28
) and (
30
) only depends on the ratio of wave speeds, that in turn depends only on Poisson’s ratio. Reducing
\nu
results in a reduced difference between
c_{P}
and
c_{S}
, thus reduced difference between the performance of the cloak with respect to the two types of waves.

Note that the same distribution of refraction index could be in principle obtained keeping fixed the density and scaling both the Lamè parameters or even changing all the three material parameters, as proposed in previous works
[
39
]
,
[
40
]
. However, following the previous analysis, it is clearly less convenient to change both Lamé parameters and density at the same time, because further approximations will be introduced in passing from (
18
) to (
20
), other than those that were just analyzed. Moreover, if modulation of the stiffness properties is adopted instead of the density, according to (
7
), some non idealities will be introduced in the anti-plane solution also, which is instead perfect when density modulation only is considered.

The impinging P wave case clearly shows the conversion of polarizations, which can also be seen through the appearance of two strong spots in the FFT of the reference and cloaked cases. 4
Concluding remarks

We have demonstrated that near-cloaking is achievable with just density changes, and have quantified the error in doing so, and this opens the way to designing elastic cloaks for experiments and applications. Moreover, we have demonstrated the effectiveness of the approach that we advocate by showing that it is robust for all angles of incidence and across a broad range of frequencies.

Technically, using the decomposition of the Navier equation into a decoupled set of equations for scalar and vector potentials allows us to clearly isolate and quantify the extraneous terms introduced to marry the equation of the physical domain to the transformed equation.
This leads to a convenient adimensional parameter being introduced to express the global dependence of performance with respect to working frequencies and local stretch imposed by the transformation. The best choice of material parameters that we found, to perform the near-cloak, comprises a gradient in the density only. This then naturally leads to viable materials for experiments as, for instance, metamaterials showing independent control of stiffness properties and density have been successfully manufactured
[
47
]
, and can be employed to design inhomogeneous structures that are easily printed using gray-tone 3D direct laser writing.

Supplementary Material

To supplement the material in the main article we perform a parametric analysis of the performance of the near-cloaking. As explained in the main article, the cloak is achieved by assuming that additional terms connected to the slow variation of density, appearing in the equation of the physical domain are negligible. These terms depend both on the frequency of operation and on the gradient of density inside the cloak. Since the gradient of density is a local property of the cloak, we have introduced a global measure of performance with the following two non-dimensional indicators:

\frac{<\nabla\rho>/\rho_{0}}{\kappa_{P}}=\frac{<1/n^{2}>c_{P}}{2\pi f};\qquad\frac{<\nabla\rho>/\rho_{0}}{\kappa_{S}}=\frac{<1/n^{2}>c_{S}}{2\pi f}

(33)

which are obtained evaluating the ratio between the approximate magnitude of the extra terms that we are neglecting with respect to the other term in the equation. The smaller these ratios are, then the closer the performance of the cloak will be with respect to the reference case. From these equations, it can clearly be seen how the behavior of the cloak basically depends on two main factors: the shape of the defect, that prescribes the density distribution, and the frequency of the incident wave.
