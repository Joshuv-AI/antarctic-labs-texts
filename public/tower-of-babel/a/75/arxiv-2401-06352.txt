Abstract
Reachable sets for a dynamical system describe collections of system states that can be reached in finite time, subject to system dynamics. They can be used to guarantee goal satisfaction in controller design or to verify that unsafe regions will be avoided. However, conventional grid-based methods for computing these sets suffer from the curse of dimensionality, which typically prohibits their use for systems with more than a small number of states, even if they are linear. In this paper, we demonstrate that local viscosity supersolutions and subsolutions of a Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation can be used to generate, respectively, under-approximating and over-approximating reachable sets for time-varying nonlinear systems. Based on this observation, we derive dynamics for a union and intersection of ellipsoidal sets that, respectively, under-approximate and over-approximate the reachable set for linear time-varying systems subject to an ellipsoidal input constraint and an ellipsoidal terminal (or initial) set. The dynamics for these ellipsoids can be selected to ensure that their boundaries coincide with the boundary of the exact reachable set along a collection of solutions of the system. The ellipsoids can be generated with polynomial computational complexity in the number of states, making our approximation scheme computationally tractable for continuous-time linear time-varying systems of relatively high dimension. Keywords: Reachability analysis, Hamilton-Jacobi equations, linear systems, optimal control

Introduction

The development of self-driving cars, the use of collaborative robots in industrial settings, and the deployment of drones in disaster responses are examples of society’s ever-increasing reliance and integration of autonomous systems in day-to-day life.
How can we be certain that these systems will complete their intended task and is it possible for them to behave unsafely?
It is such concerns that give impetus to research into methods of verifying the safety and reliability of these systems, and reachable sets lend themselves well to this task. When computed backwards in time, these sets describe all initial states for which there exists a constraint admissible control that leads the system to a set
\mathcal{X}
at a terminal time
T
(see Fig.
1
). These sets can be used to design controllers with rigorous guarantees of goal satisfaction, which have been previously deployed for maneuvering autonomous cars
[
1
]
and lane merging of unmanned aerial vehicles
[
2
]
. When computed forwards in time, we obtain the set of states that can be reached using an admissible control starting from
\mathcal{X}
at some initial time
t
. This information can be used to verify that an autonomous system will not enter an unsafe region due to an adversarial input, which is useful in developing collision avoidance control strategies
[
3
]
or guaranteeing the existence of collision-free paths
[
4
]
.

Figure 1
:
Diagram depicting the backwards reachable set
\mathcal{G}(t)
and the forwards reachable set
\mathcal{F}(t)
, which enclose all admissible trajectories heading to or away from the set
\mathcal{X}
.

Hamilton-Jacobi (HJ) reachability frames the computation of reachable sets as an optimal control problem. It is a versatile formulation that allows for the direct treatment of nonlinear systems
[
5
]
, two-player game settings
[
6
]
, and handling of state constraints
[
7
]
. To perform reachability analysis, publicly available toolboxes, such as
[
8
]
, provide a conventional and general-purpose method of numerically solving HJ equations over a grid in state-space. However, these approaches exhibit computational times that scale exponentially with the number of system states, rendering such schemes computationally intractable for many engineering applications. Consequently, a large body of research in this field focuses on efficient ways to compute or approximate reachable sets.

When dealing with linear systems, a range of approximation schemes are available. In a discrete-time setting, where the terminal or initial set is a polytope and the input set is also a polytope, the reachable sets have an exact polytopic representation
[
9
, Chapter 10]
. However, the number of linear inequalities required to represent these polytopes grow with each time step, leading to issues with storage capacity and memory requirements. The use of zonotopes in
[
10
]
and
[
11
]
can mitigate some of these issues due to their highly efficient set operations. Ellipsoidal approximation schemes have also garnered popularity in both discrete-time
[
12
]
and continuous-time settings
[
13
]
as ellipsoids can be represented with a fixed number of parameters. Generalisations of these geometries have also been studied, such as ellipsotopes
[
14
]
, which subsume both ellipsoids and zonotopes, and in characterisations based on support functions
[
15
]
. The linchpin in the aforementioned research is that approximations of the Minkowski sum can be used to develop under- and over-approximation (subset and superset) guarantees for reachability of linear systems.

When it comes to nonlinear systems, linearisation-based techniques such as the Taylor series approximation
[
16
]
or Koopman operator linearisation
[
17
]
, are common methods for leveraging existing algorithms for linear systems. Otherwise, efficient algorithms may be available, for instance, when the system can be decomposed into subsystems
[
18
]
or when a mixed monotone embedding exists
[
19
]
. Other approximation methods include implicit storage of the sets using classification-based control laws
[
20
]
, neural network approximations
[
21
]
, and boundary approximations
[
22
]
.

In this paper, we demonstrate that local viscosity supersolutions and subsolutions of a Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman partial differential equation (HJB PDE) can be used to produce under- and over-approximations of reachable sets for time-varying nonlinear systems in continuous-time. This observation is then used to develop under- and over-approximation schemes for linear time-varying (LTV) systems with ellipsoidal input constraints and ellipsoidal terminal/initial sets. We focus on a single player setting, which is a special case of the problem considered in
[
6
]
. Our approach employs the union and intersection of a family of ellipsoids to approximate reachable sets. These results extend our prior work in
[
23
]
, whereby classical supersolutions and subsolutions were used to develop ellipsoidal approximation schemes for linear time-invariant systems. By using viscosity solutions as opposed to classical solutions, approximating sets with non-smooth boundaries can be considered, for example, when the approximating set is constructed via the union or intersection of sets with smooth boundaries. A similar idea is utilised in
[
24
]
, albeit with smooth polynomial supersolutions developed for time-invariant polynomial systems.

A key novelty in our approach, relative to the vast majority of existing literature for linear systems, is that we do not rely on approximations of set operations (see e.g.
[
25
,
26
]
). In particular, we need not deviate from the HJB framework used in nonlinear reachability analysis, thereby allowing for a unified treatment of both nonlinear and linear systems. The approximating sets developed through supersolutions and subsolutions of the HJB PDE possess the same desirable characteristics as existing schemes. For instance, the proposed under- and over-approximating collection of ellipsoids are also, respectively, under- and over-approximating for all intermediate times, which corresponds to the recurrence relation discussed in
[
25
]
. The dynamics of each ellipsoid can be chosen to guarantee that their boundaries coincide with the exact reachable set along a solution of the system, which recovers the tightness property discussed in
[
26
]
. The approach taken in this work yields a different characterisation for the ellipsoidal dynamics compared to the aforementioned references. In all cases, the integration of a matrix differential equation is required. However, this involves only polynomial (in the number of states) computational complexity. In addition to these properties, we demonstrate that the approximating ellipsoids inherit the recursive feasibility and recursive infeasibility properties of the exact reachable set. Specifically, if the system lies within the under-approximating set at a given time, then there exists a constraint admissible control that leads the system to the under-approximating set for any future time. Analogously, if the system is initialised outside the over-approximating set, then it is impossible for the system to be driven into the over-approximating set for any future time. Thus, these approximating sets preserve the intended purpose of reachable sets in control applications.

The remainder of this paper is organised as follows. In Section
2
, we review the necessary preliminaries on HJB reachability analysis. In Section
3
, we generate approximate reachability results for time-varying nonlinear systems. We then specialise to LTV systems in Section
4
, leading to the development of ellipsoidal approximation schemes. Illustrative numerical examples are then considered in Section
5
.

Background Theory

2.1
Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman Reachability Analysis

Fix
T\geq t\geq 0
,
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, and consider a finite dimensional time-varying nonlinear system evolving in continuous time, with trajectories satisfying the Cauchy problem

\begin{split}\dot{x}(s)&=f(s,x(s),u(s)),\quad\text{a.e. }s\in(t,T),\\
x(t)&=x,\end{split}

(1)

where
x(s)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the state and
u(s)\in{\mathbb{U}}
is the input, both at time
s
, with
{\mathbb{U}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
assumed to be compact and non-empty. The control input is selected such that
u\in{\mathscr{U}}[t,T]
, where

{\mathscr{U}}[t,T]\doteq\left\{u:[t,T]\rightarrow\mathbb{U}\;|\;u\text{ measurable}\right\}.

(2)

Additionally, we assume the following for
f
throughout.

Assumption 1
.

The function
f:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfies

i)

f\in{\mathscr{C}}\left([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{U};\,\mathbb{R}^{n}\right)
; and

ii)

f
is Lipschitz continuous in
x
, uniformly in
s
and
u
, that is, there exists a Lipschitz constant
L_{f}\geq 0
such that
\left\lVert f(s,x,u)-f(s,y,u)\right\rVert\leq L_{f}\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert
for all
(s,x,y,u)\in[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{U}}
.

Under Assumption
1
, the dynamics in (
1
) admit a unique and continuous solution for any fixed control
u\in{\mathscr{U}}[t,T]
(see
[
27
, Theorem 3.2, p.93]
). We denote these solutions at time

by
\varphi(s;t,x,u)
. In order to consider closed terminal/initial sets, an additional assumption for compactness of solutions of (
1
) is required, see for example
[
28
, Theorem 7.1.6 and Remarks 7.1.7-7.1.8, pp.190-191]
.

Assumption 2
.

For any
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and
s\in[0,T]
, the set
f(s,x,{\mathbb{U}})\doteq\{f(s,x,u)\,|\,u\in{\mathbb{U}}\}
is convex.

Remark 1
.

If (
1
) is control affine, that is,
f:[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{U}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
can be decomposed as

and

is convex then Assumption
2
is satisfied.

The backwards reachable set for (
1
), which we denote by
\mathcal{G}(t)
, is defined as the set of states at time

for which there exists a control

that leads the system to a set
\mathcal{X}
at the terminal time

. We assume that
\mathcal{X}
can be described by the zero sublevel set of a real-valued function, in particular,

\mathcal{X}\doteq\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,|\,g(x)\leq 0\right\},

(3)

where
g\in{\mathscr{C}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n};\,{\mathbb{R}})
. We define
\mathcal{G}(t)
more precisely below.

Definition 1
.

Consider the terminal set
\mathcal{X}
defined in (
3
). The backwards reachable set
\mathcal{G}(t)=\mathcal{G}(t;\mathcal{X})
of (
1
) at time
t\in[0,T]
is defined by

\mathcal{G}(t)\doteq\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,|\,\exists\,u\in{\mathscr{U}}[t,T]\text{ s.t. }\varphi(T;t,x,u)\in\mathcal{X}\right\}.

(4)

HJB reachability characterises
\mathcal{G}(t)
as the zero sublevel set of a value function

corresponding to the Mayer optimal control problem given by

v(t,x)\doteq\inf_{u\in{\mathscr{U}}[t,T]}g\left(\varphi(T;t,x,u)\right),

(6)

We recall the disturbance-free case of

Theorem 1
.

Let

\begin{split}-v_{t}+H(t,x,\nabla v)&=0,\quad\forall(t,x)\in(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{n},\\

(7)

where

defines the terminal set
\mathcal{X}
in (
3
), and the Hamiltonian

is defined as in (

) admits the unique viscosity solution

given by (

1

\mathcal{G}(t)=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\;|\;v(t,x)\leq 0\right\}.

Note that uniqueness of solutions of (

An analogous set to the backwards reachable set

Definition 2

Consider the terminal set

\mathcal{F}(t)=\mathcal{F}(t;\mathcal{X})
of (

\mathcal{F}(t)\doteq\{\varphi(T;t,x,u)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,|\,u\in{\mathscr{U}}[t,T]\text{ and }x\in\mathcal{X}\}.

It turns out that the forwards reachable set is exactly the backwards reachable set of the time-reversed system given by

\begin{split}\dot{x}(s)&=-f(T+t-s,x(s),u(s)),\quad\text{a.e. }s\in(t,T),\\
x(t)&=x,\end{split}

(9)

where

is given in (

). The following theorem states this relationship. We omit the proof for brevity, but the autonomous case appears in Proposition 6 of

]

Lemma 1

Let

denote the backwards reachable set defined in (

2.2
Viscosity Solutions of HJB Equations

To handle approximating reachable sets that are characterised by potentially non-smooth manifolds, we will need to consider supersolutions and subsolutions of (
7
) in the viscosity sense. Accordingly, we recall the necessary background and definitions associated with viscosity solutions, which we adapt from
[
31
]
. Consider the HJB equation given by

-v_{t}+H(t,x,\nabla v)=0,\hskip 10.00002pt\forall(t,x)\in\Omega,

(10)

where
T>0
is a priori fixed,
\Omega\subseteq(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
is an open set, and
H:\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a continuous function (which corresponds to (
6
) in our work).

Definition 3
.

A function
v\in{\mathscr{C}}\left([0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n};\,\mathbb{R}\right)
is a continuous viscosity supersolution (resp. subsolution) of (
10
) if, for every
\xi\in{\mathscr{C}}^{1}\left(\Omega;\,\mathbb{R}\right)
that results in
v-\xi
attaining a local minimum (resp. local maximum) at
(t_{0},x_{0})\in\Omega
,

-\xi_{t}(t_{0},x_{0})+H(t_{0},x_{0},\nabla\xi(t_{0},x_{0}))\geq 0\hskip 10.00002pt(\text{resp. }\leq 0).

(11)

Additionally, we say that
v
is a viscosity solution of (
10
) if it is both a viscosity supersolution and subsolution of (
10
).

The definitions given in (
11
) utilise a variational description of Fréchet subdifferentials and superdifferentials (whose elements are called Fréchet subgradients and supergradients). Consequently, it is possible to define viscosity supersolutions and subsolutions of (
10
) in terms of these subdifferentials and superdifferentials, which are defined below according to
[
32
]
.

Definition 4
.

A vector
p\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
is a subgradient (resp. supergradient) of
v\in{\mathscr{C}}\left([0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n};\,\mathbb{R}\right)
at
(t_{0},x_{0})\in\Omega
if

\displaystyle\liminf_{(t,x)\rightarrow(t_{0},x_{0})}\frac{v(t,x)-v(t_{0},x_{0})-\left\langle p,(t,x)-(t_{0},x_{0})\right\rangle}{\left\lVert(t,x)-(t_{0},x_{0})\right\rVert}\geq 0,

\displaystyle\limsup_{(t,x)\rightarrow(t_{0},x_{0})}\frac{v(t,x)-v(t_{0},x_{0})-\left\langle p,(t,x)-(t_{0},x_{0})\right\rangle}{\left\lVert(t,x)-(t_{0},x_{0})\right\rVert}\leq 0.\hskip 9.24994pt(\text{resp.})

The set of all such
p\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
satisfying the above is called the subdifferential (resp. superdifferential) of
v
at
(t_{0},x_{0})
, which we denote by
D^{-}v(t_{0},x_{0})
(resp.
D^{+}v(t_{0},x_{0})
).

The subdifferential (resp. superdifferential) of
v
at a point characterise the set of gradients that any continuously differentiable function
\xi
can take at that point if
\xi
were to touch
v
from below (resp. above). This result comes from
[
33
, Proposition 8.5, p.302]
, which we state in the following.

Lemma 2
.

A vector
p\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
is an element of the subdifferential (resp. superdifferential) of
v\in{\mathscr{C}}\left([0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n};\,\mathbb{R}\right)
at
(t_{0},x_{0})\in\Omega
if and only if there exists a function
\xi\in{\mathscr{C}}^{1}\left(\Omega;\,{\mathbb{R}}\right)
such that
\left(\xi_{t}(t_{0},x_{0}),{\nabla}\xi(t_{0},x_{0})\right)=p
and
v-\xi
attains a local minimum (resp. local maximum) at
(t_{0},x_{0})
.

Finally, we recall a classic global comparison result for viscosity supersolutions and subsolutions of (
7
) from
[
29
, Theorem 3.15, p.158]
. We provide the time-varying version below for the backwards HJB PDE (see
[
29
, Remark 3.10, pp.154-155]
and
[
29
, Exercises 3.6-3.7, pp.182-183]
).

Theorem 2
.

Let
f
in (
1
) satisfy Assumption
1
and let
v_{1},v_{2}\in{\mathscr{C}}([0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n};\,{\mathbb{R}})
be, respectively, a viscosity supersolution and subsolution of (
10
) over the domain
\Omega=(0,T)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
with the Hamiltonian
H
given by (
6
). If
v_{1}(T,x)\geq v_{2}(T,x)
for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, then,
v_{1}(t,x)\geq v_{2}(t,x)
for all
(t,x)\in\rm{cl}\left(\Omega\right)
.

3
Approximating Reachable Sets

With the preliminaries introduced, we will now demonstrate that viscosity supersolutions and subsolutions of (
7
) defined over a local domain can be used to produce under- and over-approximating backwards reachable sets for (
1
). Before doing so, let us motivate and highlight the main idea behind this approach. Consider the functions
\overline{v}\in{\mathscr{C}}([0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n};\,{\mathbb{R}})
and
\underline{v}\in{\mathscr{C}}([0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n};\,{\mathbb{R}})
, which are, respectively, a viscosity supersolution and subsolution of

-v_{t}+H(t,x,\nabla v)=0,\hskip 10.00002pt\forall(t,x)\in\Omega,

(12)

where
\Omega\doteq(0,T)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and
H
is given by (
6
). From Theorem
2
, it follows that if
\overline{v}(T,x)\geq g(x)\geq\underline{v}(T,x)
for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, then,
\overline{v}(t,x)\geq v(t,x)\geq\underline{v}(t,x)
for all
(t,x)\in\text{cl}\left(\Omega\right)
where the value function
v:[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
as defined in (
5
) is the continuous viscosity solution of (
12
). Consequently, the zero sublevel set of
x\mapsto\overline{v}(t,x)
and
x\mapsto\underline{v}(t,x)
can then be used to produce under- and over-approximations of the backwards reachable set. In particular,

\displaystyle\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\,|\,\overline{v}(t,x)\leq 0\}\subseteq\overbrace{\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\,|\,v(t,x)\leq 0\}}^{\mathcal{G}(t)}\subseteq\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\,|\,\underline{v}(t,x)\leq 0\}.

However, the issue with using (
12
) is that it imposes the supersolution and subsolution condition for all points in
\Omega
, which can be overly restrictive since
\mathcal{G}(t)
is characterised by points
only
in the zero sublevel set of
v
. The remainder of this section explores the case when
\Omega
is chosen as either of the pre-images
\overline{v}^{-1}((-\infty,0))
or
\underline{v}^{-1}((0,\infty))
. This will mean that
\overline{v}
and
\underline{v}
form upper and lower bounds for
v
only over their zero sublevel set and superlevel set, respectively.

3.1
Under-approximating Backwards Reachable Sets

Let us now demonstrate that viscosity supersolutions

\varphi(t_{2};t_{1},\bar{x},u)\notin\overline{\mathcal{G}}(t_{2})
.

Proof.

First note that a restriction of
\underline{v}
to the domain
[0,t_{2}]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
must also yield a viscosity subsolution of (
12
) on the domain
\Omega=\{(t,x)\in(0,t_{2})\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\,|\,\underline{v}(t,x)>0\}
. Replacing
T
with
t_{2}
in Theorem
4
, it follows that
\overline{\mathcal{G}}(t_{1})
is an over-approximation of the backwards reachable set at time
t=t_{1}
from the terminal set
\overline{\mathcal{G}}(t_{2})=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\;|\;\underline{v}(t_{2},x)\leq 0\right\}
, which is denoted by
\mathcal{G}_{v}(t_{1})
. If
\bar{x}\notin\overline{\mathcal{G}}(t_{1})\doteq\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\,|\,\underline{v}(t_{1},x)\leq 0\}
, then there can not exist a control
u\in{\mathscr{U}}[t_{1},t_{2}]
such that
\varphi(t_{2};t_{1},\bar{x},u)\in\overline{\mathcal{G}}(t_{2})
, else it would be true that
\bar{x}\in\mathcal{G}_{v}(t_{1})
, which can not hold since
\mathcal{G}_{v}(t_{1})\subseteq\overline{\mathcal{G}}(t_{1})
.
∎

3.3
Generating Viscosity Supersolutions and Subsolutions

Before we use the previous findings to develop ellipsoidal approximation schemes for LTV systems, we discuss a possible method of constructing viscosity supersolutions and subsolutions. Consider a collection of
{\mathscr{C}}^{1}([0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n};\,{\mathbb{R}})
functions
\{\overline{v}_{i}\}^{n_{q}}_{i=1}
and
\{\underline{v_{i}}\}^{n_{q}}_{i=1}
. Suppose that every
\overline{v}_{i}
and
\underline{v_{i}}
satisfies the respective partial differential inequalities (PDIs)

\displaystyle-v_{t}+H(t,x,\nabla v)

\displaystyle\geq 0,\hskip 10.00002pt\forall(t,x)\in\overline{v}_{i}^{-1}((-\infty,0)),

(28)

\displaystyle-v_{t}+H(t,x,\nabla v)

\displaystyle\leq 0,\hskip 10.00002pt\forall(t,x)\in\underline{v_{i}}^{-1}((0,\infty)),

(29)

with
H:[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
given by (
6
). Then, every
\overline{v}_{i}
and
\underline{v_{i}}
is, respectively, a classical supersolution and subsolution of (
12
) over the domains
\Omega=\overline{v}_{i}^{-1}((-\infty,0))
and
\Omega=\underline{v_{i}}^{-1}((0,\infty))
. It then follows that the minimisation
over
\{\overline{v}_{i}\}^{n_{q}}_{i=1}
and the maximisation over
\{\underline{v_{i}}\}^{n_{q}}_{i=1}
,

\displaystyle v_{m}(t,x)

\displaystyle\doteq\min_{1\leq i\leq n_{q}}\overline{v}_{i}(t,x),\hskip 10.00002pt\forall(t,x)\in[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n},

(30)

\displaystyle v_{M}(t,x)

\displaystyle\doteq\max_{1\leq i\leq n_{q}}\underline{v_{i}}(t,x),\hskip 10.00002pt\forall(t,x)\in[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n},

(31)

produce a viscosity supersolution and subsolution of (
12
), respectively. This is demonstrated in the following result, which can be seen as an application of
[
29
, Proposition 2.1, p.34]
to the problem setting of this work.

Lemma 7
.

Let
\{\overline{v}_{i}\}^{n_{q}}_{i=1}
(resp.
\{\underline{v_{i}}\}^{n_{q}}_{i=1}
) be a finite collection of
n_{q}\in{\mathbb{N}}
functions belonging to
{\mathscr{C}}^{1}([0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n};\,{\mathbb{R}})
. Let every
\overline{v}_{i}
(resp.
\underline{v_{i}}
) satisfy the PDI in (
28
) (resp. (
29
)). Then,
v_{m}
as defined in (
30
) (resp.
v_{M}
as defined in (
31
)) is a viscosity supersolution (resp. subsolution) of (
12
) with
\Omega=v_{m}^{-1}((-\infty,0))
(resp.
\Omega=v_{M}^{-1}((0,\infty))
).

Proof.

To demonstrate that
v_{m}
is a viscosity supersolution of (
12
) with
\Omega=v_{m}^{-1}((-\infty,0))
, let
\xi\in{\mathscr{C}}^{1}((0,T)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n};\,{\mathbb{R}})
be defined such that
v_{m}-\xi
attains a local minimum at
(t_{0},x_{0})\in v_{m}^{-1}((-\infty,0))
. Since
v_{m}^{-1}((-\infty,0))
is an open set, for some
r>0
the following holds for all
(t,x)\in{\mathbb{B}}_{r}((t_{0},x_{0}))

v_{m}(t,x)-\xi(t,x)\geq v_{m}(t_{0},x_{0})-\xi(t_{0},x_{0}).

(32)

Let
\hat{n}\in\{1,\ldots,n_{q}\}
be any index such that
\overline{v}_{\hat{n}}(t_{0},x_{0})=v_{m}(t_{0},x_{0})
. By Definition,
v_{m}(t,x)\leq\overline{v}_{i}(t,x)
for all
(t,x)\in(0,T)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,n_{q}\}
. Thus, from (
32
),

\overline{v}_{\hat{n}}(t,x)-\xi(t,x)\geq\overline{v}_{\hat{n}}(t_{0},x_{0})-\xi(t_{0},x_{0}),

(33)

for all
(t,x)\in{\mathbb{B}}_{r}((t_{0},x_{0}))
, which means
\overline{v}_{\hat{n}}-\xi
also attains a local minimum at
(t_{0},x_{0})
. Since
\overline{v}_{\hat{n}}
is continuously differentiable, which implies that
\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\overline{v}_{\hat{n}}(t_{0},x_{0}),\nabla\overline{v}_{\hat{n}}(t_{0},x_{0})\right)=\left(\xi_{t}(t_{0},x_{0}),\nabla\xi(t_{0},x_{0})\right)
. Moreover,
(t_{0},x_{0})\in\overline{v}_{\hat{n}}^{-1}((-\infty,0))
since
\overline{v}_{\hat{n}}(t_{0},x_{0})=v_{m}(t_{0},x_{0})<0
. It then follows from (
28
) that
-\xi_{t}(t_{0},x_{0})+H(t_{0},x_{0},\nabla\xi(t_{0},x_{0}))\geq 0
. As
(t_{0},x_{0})\in v_{m}^{-1}((-\infty,0))
is arbitrary, by Definition
3
,
v_{m}
is a viscosity supersolution of (
12
) on
\Omega=v^{-1}_{m}((-\infty,0))
.

With appropriate sign changes, the analogous result for
v_{M}
can be demonstrated. Briefly, consider a function
\xi\in{\mathscr{C}}^{1}((0,T)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n};\,{\mathbb{R}})
, which is defined such that
v_{M}-\xi
attains a local maximum at
(t_{0},x_{0})\in v^{-1}_{M}((0,\infty))
. Since
v_{M}(t,x)\geq\underline{v_{i}}(t,x)
for all
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,n_{q}\}
, this implies that for some
\hat{n}\in\{1,\ldots,n_{q}\}
with
\underline{v_{\hat{n}}}(t_{0},x_{0})=v_{M}(t_{0},x_{0})
,
\underline{v_{\hat{n}}}-\xi
also attains a local maximum at
(t_{0},x_{0})
. Thus,
\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\underline{v_{\hat{n}}}(t_{0},x_{0}),\nabla\underline{v_{\hat{n}}}(t_{0},x_{0})\right)=\left(\xi_{t}(t_{0},x_{0}),\nabla\xi(t_{0},x_{0})\right)
and by (
29
),
-\xi_{t}(t_{0},x_{0})+H(t_{0},x_{0},\nabla\xi(t_{0},x_{0}))\leq 0
. So,
v_{M}
is a viscosity subsolution of (
12
) with
\Omega=v^{-1}_{M}((0,\infty))
.
∎

Remark 3
.

The use of viscosity solutions as opposed to classical solutions (as in
[
23
]
) to characterise under- and over-approximations of reachable sets may seem unnecessary in the case where each individual function
\overline{v}_{i}
and
\underline{v_{i}}
is a classical supersolution and subsolution of (
12
). However, the results of this section generalise to other methods of constructing non-classical solutions and, moreover, provide a unified and rigorous means for understanding how collections of sets can be used in reachability-based problems.

4
Approximate Reachability for LTV Systems

Grid-based approaches are a conventional means of producing accurate characterisations of reachable sets for a broad class of systems and problem settings. However, these approaches scale poorly with the number of system states even when dealing with linear systems. In this section, we exploit the structure of linear systems via the results of Section
3
to yield ellipsoidal approximation schemes for LTV systems. The computational cost of these schemes is dominated by the integration of a matrix differential equation, which has a computational complexity of
\mathcal{O}(n^{3})
.

Definition 6
.

Let
q\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
Q\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{>0}
. An ellipsoid with centre
q
and shape
Q
is defined as the set

\mathcal{E}(q,Q)\doteq\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,|\,\left\langle x-q,Q^{-1}(x-q)\right\rangle\leq 1\right\}.

(34)

Remark 4
.

An ellipsoid
\mathcal{E}(q,Q)
has its axes aligned along the eigenvectors of
Q
with lengths equal to the square root of its corresponding eigenvalues. An ellipsoid is equivalently defined as the affine transformation of the unit ball, in particular,

\mathcal{E}(q,Q)=\left\{Q^{\frac{1}{2}}w+q\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,|\,\lVert w\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq 1\right\}.

(35)

Let us now discuss the problem set up that will be used for the remainder of this paper. We consider a special case of (
1
) where
f:[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{U}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
now describes a linear time-varying system subject to an ellipsoidal input set
{\mathbb{U}}
and an ellipsoidal terminal state set
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. In particular, we consider the continuous-time system given by

\begin{split}&\dot{x}(s)=A(s)x(s)+B(s)u(s),\quad\text{a.e. }s\in(t,T),\\
&u(s)\in{\mathbb{U}}=\mathcal{E}(p,P),\quad x(T)\in\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{E}(x_{e},X_{e}),\end{split}

(36)

where
A\in{\mathscr{C}}([0,T];\,{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n})
,
B\in{\mathscr{C}}([0,T];\,{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times m})
,
p\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
,
P\in\mathbb{S}^{m}_{>0}
,
x_{e}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, and
X_{e}\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{>0}
. In the notation of (
3
), the terminal state set
\mathcal{X}
can be defined via
g(x)=\left\langle x-x_{e},X_{e}^{-1}(x-x_{e})\right\rangle-1
.

Note that this problem setting satisfies the assumptions imposed on the nonlinear system considered in previous sections. To see this, observe that continuity of
A(\cdot)
and
B(\cdot)
ensures item
i
)
of Assumption
1
is satisfied. For any
s\in[0,T]
and
u\in{\mathbb{U}}
, the map
x\mapsto A(s)x+B(s)u
is affine, which ensures the Lipschitz continuity assumption is satisfied, and convexity of
{\mathbb{U}}
then ensures that Assumption
2
is satisfied.

Our proposed approximation scheme utilises a collection of ellipsoidal sets
\left\{\mathcal{E}\left(q_{i}(t),Q_{i}(t)\right)\right\}^{n_{q}}_{i=1}
and defines a corresponding evolution for each center
q_{i}:[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
and shape
Q_{i}:[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbb{S}^{n}_{>0}
such that every ellipsoid is an under-approximation (resp. over-approximation) of
\mathcal{G}(t)
for (
36
). The union of the under-approximating ellipsoids (resp. intersection of the over-approximating ellipsoids) remains an under-approximation (resp. over-approximation) of
\mathcal{G}(t)
. It can be readily verified that the union of ellipsoids can be characterised by the zero sublevel set of the minimum over a collection of quadratic functions. In particular,

\bigcup^{n_{q}}_{i=1}\mathcal{E}(q_{i}(t),Q_{i}(t))=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\,|\,\overline{e}(t,x)\leq 0\},

(37)

where, for all
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
,

\overline{e}(t,x)\doteq\min_{1\leq i\leq n_{q}}\left\langle x-q_{i}(t),Q^{-1}_{i}(t)(x-q_{i}(t))\right\rangle-1.

(38)

When the evolution of all pairs
\{q_{i},Q_{i}\}
are chosen so that the PDI in (
28
) is satisfied for every quadratic function, (
38
) will construct a viscosity supersolution in the manner suggested by Lemma
7
. Correspondingly, the intersection of ellipsoids can be characterised by the zero sublevel set of the maximum over a collection of quadratics, that is,

\bigcap^{n_{q}}_{i=1}\mathcal{E}(q_{i}(t),Q_{i}(t))=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\,|\,\underline{e}(t,x)\leq 0\},

(39)

where, for all
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
,

\underline{e}(t,x)\doteq\max_{1\leq i\leq n_{q}}\left\langle x-q_{i}(t),Q^{-1}_{i}(t)(x-q_{i}(t))\right\rangle-1.

(40)

This will construct a viscosity subsolution in the manner suggested by Lemma
7
. Note that the map
x\mapsto\overline{e}(t,x)
is of class
{\mathscr{Z}}
(see Definition
5
), which is needed in the assumptions of Theorem
3
. We state this below with the proof postponed to the Appendix.

Lemma 8
.

Consider
\overline{e}\in{\mathscr{C}}([0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n};\,{\mathbb{R}})
as defined in (
38
) with
Q_{i}(t)\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{>0}
for all
t\in[0,T]
. Then, the map
x\mapsto\overline{e}(t,x)
is of class
{\mathscr{Z}}
for all
t\in[0,T]
.

Let us present two preliminary results that will be needed to ensure that
\overline{e}
and
\underline{e}
are, respectively, viscosity supersolutions and subsolutions of (
12
) over an appropriate domain.

Lemma 9
.

Let
Q\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{\geq 0}
and
S\in\mathbb{O}^{n}
be an orthogonal matrix. Then, for any
w\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
with
\lVert w\rVert_{2}\leq 1
,

\big\lVert Q^{\frac{1}{2}}w\big\rVert_{2}\geq\tfrac{1}{2}\left\langle w,(Q^{\frac{1}{2}}S+S^{T}Q^{\frac{1}{2}})w\right\rangle.

(41)

Moreover, equality in (
41
) is attained at
w^{\star}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
if
\lVert w^{\star}\rVert_{2}=1
and
Sw^{\star}=Q^{\frac{1}{2}}w^{\star}/\penalty\lVert Q^{\frac{1}{2}}w^{\star}\rVert_{2}
.

Proof.

Fix arbitrary
Q\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{\geq 0}
,
S\in\mathbb{O}^{n}
, and
w\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
such that
\lVert w\rVert_{2}\leq 1
. Noting that
Q^{\frac{1}{2}}
exists, the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality implies that

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}\left\langle w,(Q^{\frac{1}{2}}S+S^{T}Q^{\frac{1}{2}})w\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle Q^{\frac{1}{2}}w,Sw\right\rangle\leq\big\lVert Q^{\frac{1}{2}}w\big\rVert_{2}\big\lVert Sw\big\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle=\big\lVert Q^{\frac{1}{2}}w\big\rVert_{2}\lVert w\rVert_{2}\leq\big\lVert Q^{\frac{1}{2}}w\big\rVert_{2},

which is (
41
). For the second assertion, let
w^{\star}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be such that
\lVert w^{\star}\rVert_{2}=1
and
Sw^{\star}=Q^{\frac{1}{2}}w^{\star}/\penalty\lVert Q^{\frac{1}{2}}w^{\star}\rVert_{2}
. Substituting
w^{\star}
into the right-hand side of (
41
),

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}\left\langle w^{\star},(Q^{\frac{1}{2}}S+S^{T}Q^{\frac{1}{2}})w^{\star}\right\rangle=\left\langle Q^{\frac{1}{2}}w^{\star},Sw^{\star}\right\rangle=\left\langle Q^{\frac{1}{2}}w^{\star},Q^{\frac{1}{2}}w^{\star}/\penalty\big\lVert Q^{\frac{1}{2}}w^{\star}\big\rVert_{2}\right\rangle=\big\lVert Q^{\frac{1}{2}}w^{\star}\big\rVert_{2}.

∎

Lemma 10
.

Let
Q\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{\geq 0}
and
\kappa\in{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}
. Then, for any
w\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
with
\lVert w\rVert_{2}\geq 1
,

\big\lVert Q^{\frac{1}{2}}w\big\rVert_{2}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\left\langle w,\left(\tfrac{1}{\kappa}Q+\kappa{\mathbb{I}}\right)w\right\rangle.

(42)

Moreover, equality in (
42
) is attained at
w^{\star}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
if
\lVert w^{\star}\rVert_{2}=1
and
\kappa=\big\lVert Q^{\frac{1}{2}}w^{\star}\big\rVert_{2}\neq 0
.

Proof.

Fix arbitrary
Q\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{\geq 0}
,
\kappa\in{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}
, and
w\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
such that
\lVert w\rVert_{2}\geq 1
. Then,

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}\left\langle w,\left(\tfrac{1}{\kappa}Q+\kappa{\mathbb{I}}\right)w\right\rangle-\big\lVert Q^{\frac{1}{2}}w\big\rVert_{2}{}

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt=\tfrac{1}{2\kappa}\left(\big\lVert Q^{\frac{1}{2}}w\big\rVert_{2}^{2}-2\kappa\big\lVert Q^{\frac{1}{2}}w\big\rVert_{2}+\kappa^{2}\lVert w\rVert_{2}^{2}\right)

(43)

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt\geq\tfrac{1}{2\kappa}\left(\big\lVert Q^{\frac{1}{2}}w\big\rVert_{2}^{2}-2\kappa\big\lVert Q^{\frac{1}{2}}w\big\rVert_{2}+\kappa^{2}\right){}

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt=\tfrac{1}{2\kappa}\left(\big\lVert Q^{\frac{1}{2}}w\big\rVert_{2}-\kappa\right)^{2}\geq 0,

(44)

which gives (
42
). For the second assertion, let
w^{\star}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be such that
\lVert w^{\star}\rVert_{2}=1
and
\big\lVert Q^{\frac{1}{2}}w^{\star}\big\rVert_{2}\neq 0
. Substitution of
\lVert w^{\star}\rVert_{2}=1
in (
43
) followed by substitution of
\kappa=\big\lVert Q^{\frac{1}{2}}w^{\star}\big\rVert_{2}
in (
44
) yields the desired equality in (
42
). ∎

4.1
Ellipsoidal Under-approximation of Backwards Reachable Sets

We can now present our main result on under-approximating the backwards reachable set of (
36
) via a union of ellipsoidal sets. To ensure that each ellipsoid
\mathcal{E}(q_{i}(t),Q_{i}(t))
in (
37
) is an under-approximation of
\mathcal{G}(t)
, we evolve each pair
\{q_{i},Q_{i}\}
backwards in time according to the final value problem (FVP) defined via (
36
) by

\left\{\;\begin{split}\dot{q}_{i}(t)&=A(t)q_{i}(t)+B(t)p,\\
\dot{Q}_{i}(t)&=A(t)Q_{i}(t)+Q_{i}(t)A^{T}(t)-\underline{{\mathcal{R}}_{i}}(t,Q_{i}(t)),\\
q_{i}(T)&=x_{e},\quad Q_{i}(T)=X_{e},\end{split}\right.

(45)

for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,n_{q}\}
, a.e.
t\in(0,T)
, in which the parameterised operators
\underline{{\mathcal{R}}_{i}}:[0,T]\times\mathbb{S}^{n}_{>0}\rightarrow\mathbb{S}^{n}
and
{\mathcal{Q}}_{\star}:[0,T]\times\mathbb{S}^{n}_{>0}\rightarrow\mathbb{S}^{n}_{\geq 0}
are defined by

\displaystyle\underline{{\mathcal{R}}_{i}}(t,Q)\doteq Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\left({\mathcal{Q}}^{\frac{1}{2}}_{\star}(t,Q)S_{i}(t)+S^{T}_{i}(t){\mathcal{Q}}^{\frac{1}{2}}_{\star}(t,Q)\right)Q^{\frac{1}{2}},

(46)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{Q}}_{\star}(t,Q)\doteq Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}B(t)PB^{T}(t)Q^{-\frac{1}{2}},

(47)

with
S_{i}:[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbb{O}^{n}
being continuous. We use the short-hand notation
{\mathcal{Q}}_{i,\star}(t)\doteq{\mathcal{Q}}_{\star}(t,Q_{i}(t))
where appropriate.

Assumption 3
.

S_{i}\in{\mathscr{C}}\left([0,T];\,\mathbb{O}^{n}\right)
is selected such that a unique positive definite solution
Q_{i}:[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbb{S}^{n}_{>0}
exists.

Existence of solutions for (
45
) and the role of Assumption
3
is further discussed in Remark
5
following the statement of the main result.

The orthogonal matrix-valued function
S_{i}\in{\mathscr{C}}\left([0,T];\,\mathbb{O}^{n}\right)
is a degree of freedom that allows each ellipsoid to evolve under a different set of dynamics. Under appropriate conditions, a solution
x^{\star}(\cdot)
of (
36
) can be contained within an ellipsoid
\mathcal{E}(q_{i}(t),Q_{i}(t))
by selecting
S_{i}
to satisfy

\frac{S_{i}(s)\hat{w}_{i}(s)}{\big\lVert\hat{w}_{i}(s)\big\rVert_{2}}=\frac{{\mathcal{Q}}^{\frac{1}{2}}_{i,\star}(s)\hat{w}_{i}(s)}{\big\lVert{\mathcal{Q}}^{\frac{1}{2}}_{i,\star}(s)\hat{w}_{i}(s)\big\rVert_{2}},

(48)

for all
s\in[t,T]
such that
{\mathcal{Q}}^{\frac{1}{2}}_{i,\star}(s)\hat{w}_{i}(s)\neq 0
, in which

\hat{w}_{i}(s)\doteq Q^{-\frac{1}{2}}_{i}(s)(x^{\star}(s)-q_{i}(s)).

(49)

Theorem 5
.

Let Assumption
3
hold and consider the LTV system described by (
36
). Let
\{q_{i},Q_{i}\}^{n_{q}}_{i=1}
be the set of solutions to (
45
)-(
47
).

(a)

The set

\underline{\mathcal{G}}(t)\doteq\bigcup^{n_{q}}_{i=1}\mathcal{E}\left(q_{i}(t),Q_{i}(t)\right)

(50)

is an under-approximation of
\mathcal{G}(t)
for (
36
) for all
t\in[0,T]
.

(b)

Given
S_{i}\in{\mathscr{C}}\left([0,T];\,\mathbb{O}^{n}\right)
with
i\in\{1,\ldots,n_{q}\}
satisfying (
48
) for all
s\in[t,T]
such that
{\mathcal{Q}}^{\frac{1}{2}}_{i,\star}(s)\hat{w}_{i}(s)\neq 0
and
x^{\star}(\cdot)
is a solution of (
36
) with
x^{\star}(T)\in\mathcal{X}
, then,

x^{\star}(t)\in\mathcal{E}(q_{i}(t),Q_{i}(t))\subseteq\underline{\mathcal{G}}(t)

(51)

for all
t\in[0,T]
.

Proof.

Assertion
(
a
)
. Fix any
i\in\{1,\ldots,n_{q}\}
and note that by Assumption
3
,
Q_{i}(t)\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{>0}
, which implies
Q^{-1}_{i}(t)
exists for all
t\in[0,T]
. Define
\overline{e}_{i}\in{\mathscr{C}}^{1}([0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n};\,{\mathbb{R}})
by

\overline{e}_{i}(t,x)\doteq\left\langle x-q_{i}(t),Q_{i}^{-1}(t)(x-q_{i}(t))\right\rangle-1,

(52)

where
\{q_{i},Q_{i}\}
is the solution to (
45
)-(
47
). Since
Q_{i}(t)\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{>0}
also implies the existence of
Q^{\frac{1}{2}}_{i}(t)
,
{\mathcal{Q}}_{i,\star}(t)
is well-defined. Positive definiteness of
P
implies
{\mathcal{Q}}_{i,\star}(t)\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{\geq 0}
, so
{\mathcal{Q}}^{\frac{1}{2}}_{i,\star}(t)\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{\geq 0}
. Differentiating (
52
) yields

\displaystyle\tfrac{\partial}{\partial t}\overline{e}_{i}

\displaystyle=-2\left\langle\dot{q}_{i}(t),Q_{i}^{-1}(t)(x-q_{i}(t))\right\rangle+\left\langle x-q_{i}(t),\tfrac{d}{dt}Q^{-1}_{i}(t)(x-q_{i}(t))\right\rangle,

(53)

\displaystyle\nabla\overline{e}_{i}

\displaystyle=2Q^{-1}_{i}(t)(x-q_{i}(t)).

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we have demonstrated that viscosity supersolutions and subsolutions of a Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation defined over a local domain can be used to generate under- and over-approximating reachable sets for time-varying nonlinear systems. This observation was then used to develop numerical schemes for approximating either the backwards or forwards reachable set of linear time-varying (LTV) systems.

A union and intersection of ellipsoids, which can be characterised by the minimum and maximum over a collection of quadratic functions, was proposed to under- and over-approximate the reachable set, respectively. The under-approximating ellipsoids admit a generalisation that allows for any solution of the LTV system to be contained within it. This in turn implies that it is possible to have the boundaries of the under-approximating ellipsoids and the exact reachable set coincide along a solution of the system. The over-approximating ellipsoids also admit an analogous generalisation, allowing for a tight over-approximation of the reachable set. The numerical example presented in this paper highlights a marked computational advantage afforded to LTV systems compared to grid-based approaches. Moreover, by utilising a set of conditions that are applicable to nonlinear systems and a general class of sets, the work presented here acts as a demonstration for how approximation schemes may be developed for other system classes and set representations.
