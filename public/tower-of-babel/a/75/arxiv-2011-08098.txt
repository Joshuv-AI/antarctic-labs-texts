Distinct distances on non-ruled surfaces and between circles Thanks: This research project was done as part of the 2019 CUNY Combinatorics REU, supported by NSF awards DMS-1802059 and DMS-1851420.
Surya Mathialagan, Adam Sheffer

Abstract
We improve the current best bound for distinct distances on non-ruled algebraic surfaces in ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} . In particular, we show that n n points on such a surface span Ω ⁡ ( n 32 / 39 − ε ) \Omega\left(n^{32/39-{\varepsilon}}\right) distinct distances, for any ε > 0 {\varepsilon}>0 . Our proof adapts the proof of Székely for the planar case, which is based on the crossing lemma. As part of our proof for distinct distances on surfaces, we also obtain new results for distinct distances between circles in ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} . Consider two point sets of respective sizes m m and n n , such that each set lies on a distinct circle in ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} . We characterize the cases when the number of distinct distances between the two sets can be O ⁡ ( m + n ) O(m+n) . This includes a new configuration with a small number of distances. In any other case, we prove that the number of distinct distances is Ω ⁡ ( min ⁡ { m 2 / 3 ​ n 2 / 3 , m 2 , n 2 } ) \Omega\left(\min\left\{m^{2/3}n^{2/3},m^{2},n^{2}\right\}\right) .

Introduction

Erdős introduced the family of
distinct distances problems
in 1946 and considered it to be his “most striking contribution to geometry”
[
8
]
. For over 50 years, he regularly added more conjectures and problems to this family. For a finite point set
\mathcal{P}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let
\Delta(\mathcal{P})
be the set of distances spanned by pairs of points from
\mathcal{P}
.
In his first seminal paper on the subject, Erdős
[
9
]
asked for the asymptotic value of
D(\mathcal{P})=\min_{|\mathcal{P}|=n}|\Delta(\mathcal{P})|
.
In other words, the problem asks for the minimum number of distances that could be spanned by
n
points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

The answer to the above problem depends on
d
.
In
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, Erdős presented a set of
n
points that spans
\Theta(n/\sqrt{\log n})
distinct distances and conjectured that this was asymptotically optimal.
Over the decades, a large number of works have been dedicated to this problem (such as
[
6
,
25
,
26
,
28
]
).
Recently, Guth and Katz
[
10
]
almost completely settled Erdős’s conjecture, proving that
D(\mathcal{P})=\Omega(n/\log n)
for any set
\mathcal{P}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
of
n
points.

Distinct distances on non-ruled surfaces.
When the breakthrough of Guth and Katz first appeared in 2010, the community was optimistic about using a similar technique to solve the distinct distances problem in every dimension.
This turned out to be more difficult than expected, and so far no progress has been made even in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
One step towards solving the problem in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
may be to consider the problem on an algebraic surface.
That is, we consider the number of distinct distances spanned by
n
points on a specific surface in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

The case of
n
points on a plane in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
is equivalent to the distinct distances problem in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
Tao
[
27
]
showed that the Guth-Katz result can be extended in the following way.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a sphere or a hyperboloid of two sheets.
Then any set
\mathcal{P}
of
n
points on
U
satisfies

D(\mathcal{P})=\Omega\left(n/\log n\right).

No known constructions of
n
points on an algebraic surface in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
span a sublinear number of distinct distances (with the obvious exception of points on a plane).
On the other hand, there are unrestricted point sets
\mathcal{P}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
that satisfy
D(\mathcal{P})=\Theta(n^{2/3})
.
(It is conjectured that no set in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
spans an asymptotically smaller number of distinct distances.)

Sharir and Solomon
[
21
]
proved the following distinct distances result on surfaces in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. (The definition of an irreducible two-dimensional variety of degree
k
can be found in Section
2
.)

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be an irreducible two-dimensional variety of degree
k
.
Let
\mathcal{P}
be a set of
n
points on
U
.
Then for any
{\varepsilon}>0
, we have

D(\mathcal{P})=\Omega_{k}\left(n^{7/9-{\varepsilon}}\right).

In another work, Sharir and Solomon
[
22
]
replace the extra
{\varepsilon}
in the exponent with a polylogarithmic factor.
These bounds can be thought of as adaptations of Chung’s bound for distinct distances in the plane
[
6
]
. These results reveal that the three-dimensional distinct distances problem behaves differently when restricting the point set to a surface.

We improve Theorem 1.2 for non-ruled surfaces.
A surface
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}

is
ruled
if for every point
p\in U
there exists a line that is contained in
U
and incident to
p
.
For example, cylindrical and conical surfaces are ruled.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be an irreducible non-ruled two-dimensional variety of degree
k
.
Let
\mathcal{P}
be a set of
n
points on
U
.
Then for any
{\varepsilon}>0
, we have

D(\mathcal{P})=\Omega_{k}\left(n^{32/39-{\varepsilon}}\right).

The first step of our proof extends Székely’s technique for distinct distances in the plane
[
26
]
. By combining this technique with incidence bounds for curves, we obtain the bound
\Omega(n^{4/5-{\varepsilon}})
.
We further improve this bound by studying perpendicular bisectors of points on surfaces in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
We prove Theorem
1.3
in Section
3
.

While Sharir and Solomon do not state this, their proof of Theorem
1.2
also implies that there exists a point
p\in\mathcal{P}
that spans
\Omega_{k}(n^{7/9-\epsilon})
distances with
\mathcal{P}\setminus\{p\}
.
While this is also the case for Székely’s proof in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, our proof of Theorem
1.3
does not immediately show the existence of such a point.
Some of the new components that extend Székely’s proof to
\mathbb{R}^{3}
do not easily extend in this direction.

Distinct distances between two circles in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
As part of the proof of Theorem
1.3
, we derive a bound on the number of distinct distances between points on two circles in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
We believe that this result is also intrinsically interesting, and not just with respect to Theorem
1.3
.

Consider two finite sets
\mathcal{P}_{1},\mathcal{P}_{2}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
We denote by
D(\mathcal{P}_{1},\mathcal{P}_{2})
the number of distinct distances spanned by pairs in
\mathcal{P}_{1}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}
.
In other words, we ignore distances between pairs of points from the same set.
Many such bipartite distinct distances problems have been studied (for example, see
[
7
,
16
,
19
]
). We are interested in the case where there exist circles
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
such that
\mathcal{P}_{1}\subset C_{1}
and
\mathcal{P}_{2}\subset C_{2}
.

Figure 1
:
The case of two concentric circles.

We begin with the case where
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are concentric circles in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Let
\mathcal{P}_{1}
be the set of vertices of a regular
n
-gon lying on
C_{1}
.
Let
\mathcal{P}_{2}
be a uniform scaling of
\mathcal{P}_{1}
around the center of the circles, such that
\mathcal{P}_{2}\subset C_{2}
.
The case of
n=8
is depicted in Figure
1
.
Arbitrarily fix a point
p\in\mathcal{P}_{1}
.
By symmetry, we have that

D(\mathcal{P}_{1},\mathcal{P}_{2})=D(\{p\},\mathcal{P}_{2})=\Theta(n).

It is not difficult to generalize the above construction to the case where
\mathcal{P}_{1}
and
\mathcal{P}_{2}
are not necessarily of the same size.
When
|\mathcal{P}_{1}|=m
and
|\mathcal{P}_{2}|=n
, we obtain
D(\mathcal{P}_{1},\mathcal{P}_{2})=\Theta(m+n)
. On the other hand,
Pach and de Zeeuw
[
16
]
proved that when
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are not concentric, we have

D(\mathcal{P}_{1},\mathcal{P}_{2})=\Omega\left(\min\left\{m^{2/3}n^{2/3},m^{2},n^{2}\right\}\right).

In the current work, we consider the case where
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
We define the
axis
of a circle
C
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
to be the line incident to the center of
C
and orthogonal to the plane containing
C
.
Note that every point on the axis of
C
is equidistant from all the points of
C
.
On the other hand, a point not on the axis of
C
cannot be equidistant from three points of
C
(every sphere centered at this point intersects
C
in at most two points).
We say that two circles
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
are
aligned
if they have the same line as their axis.
An example is depicted in Figure
2
(a).
Note that the planes that contain aligned circles are parallel.

The above example of concentric circles in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
can be easily extended to the case of aligned circles in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Thus, when
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are aligned, one can find
\mathcal{P}_{1}
and
\mathcal{P}_{2}
such that
D(\mathcal{P}_{1},\mathcal{P}_{2})=\Theta(m+n)
.
Surprisingly, we also discovered a less intuitive family of constructions with a linear number of distances between two circles.
Let
H_{1}
be the plane containing
C_{1}
and let
H_{2}
be the plane containing
C_{2}
.
We say that
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are
perpendicular
if all the following hold:

(1)

The planes
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
are perpendicular (that is, the angle between the two planes is
\pi/2
).

(2)

The center of
C_{1}
lies on
H_{2}
.

(3)

The center of
C_{2}
lies on
H_{1}
.

An example is depicted in Figure
2
(b).

The following is our main contribution to distinct distances between two circles.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
be two circles in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

(a) Assume that
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are aligned or perpendicular.
Then there exist a set
\mathcal{P}_{1}\subset C_{1}
of
m
points and a set
\mathcal{P}_{2}\subset C_{2}
of
n
points, such that

D(\mathcal{P}_{1},\mathcal{P}_{2})=\Theta(m+n).

(b) Assume that
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are neither aligned nor perpendicular.
Let
\mathcal{P}_{1}\subset C_{1}
be a set of
m
points and let
\mathcal{P}_{2}\subset C_{2}
be a set of
n
points.
Then

D(\mathcal{P}_{1},\mathcal{P}_{2})=\Omega\left(\min\left\{m^{2/3}n^{2/3},m^{2},n^{2}\right\}\right).

The proof of Theorem
1.4
(b) relies on Elekes-Szabó type expanding polynomials, similar to an argument of Raz
[
17
]
.
This argument is combined with a new layer of computer calculations.
Part (a) of Theorem
1.4
is proved in Section
4
.
Part (b) is proved in Section
5
.

Future directions.

The main issue of Theorem
1.3
is that it does not hold for ruled surfaces. It seems plausible that our proof could be extended to also hold for ruled surfaces. This could be an interesting direction for future work.

Another direction to explore is distinct distances between two circles in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
. In this case, there exists a simple construction with only one distance between two circles (see
[
14
]
). We wonder what other surprises might exist in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
.

Preliminaries

We now introduce various definitions and tools that are used in the proofs in the following sections.

We briefly survey notation and results from real algebraic geometry.
For references and more information, see for example
[
4
,
11
]
.

For polynomials
f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]
, the
variety
defined by
f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}
is

{\bf V}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{k})=\left\{p\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\ :\ f_{1}(p)=f_{2}(p)=\cdots=f_{k}(p)=0\right\}.

We say that a set
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a variety if there exist
f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]
such that
U={\bf V}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{k})
.
While not true over some other fields, in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
every variety can be defined using a single polynomial.

A variety
U
is
irreducible
if there do not exist two nonempty varieties
U_{1},U_{2}\subset U
such that
U=U_{1}\cup U_{2}
,
U_{1}\neq U
, and
U_{2}\neq U
.
The
dimension
of an irreducible variety
U
is the largest integer
d_{U}
for which there exist non-empty irreducible varieties
U_{0},U_{1},\ldots,U_{d_{U}-1}
such that

U_{0}\subsetneq U_{1}\subsetneq\ldots\subsetneq U_{d_{U}-1}\subsetneq U.

The dimension of a reducible variety
W
is the maximum dimension of an irreducible component of
W
.
We define a
curve
to be an irreducible variety of dimension one.
A
surface
is an irreducible variety of dimension two.

The
ideal
of a variety
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, denoted
{\bf I}(U)
, is the set of polynomials in
\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]
that vanish on every point of
U
.
We say that a set of polynomials
f_{1},\ldots,f_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]

generate

{\bf I}(U)
if every element of
{\bf I}(U)
can be written as
\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}f_{j}g_{j}
for some
g_{1},\ldots,g_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]
.
The
Jacobian matrix
of the polynomials
f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]
is

{\bf J}_{f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}\frac{\partial f_{1}}{\partial x_{1}}&\frac{\partial f_{1}}{\partial x_{2}}&\cdots&\frac{\partial f_{1}}{\partial x_{d}}\\[5.69054pt]
\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial x_{1}}&\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial x_{2}}&\cdots&\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial x_{d}}\\[5.69054pt]
\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots\\[5.69054pt]
\frac{\partial f_{k}}{\partial x_{1}}&\frac{\partial f_{k}}{\partial x_{2}}&\cdots&\frac{\partial f_{k}}{\partial x_{d}}\end{array}\right)

Consider a variety
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
of dimension
k
, and polynomials
f_{1},\ldots,f_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]
that generate
{\bf I}(U)
.
We say that
p\in U
is a
singular
point of
U
if
\mathrm{rank\,}{\bf J}_{f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}}(p)<d-k
.
A point of
U
that is not singular is said to be a
regular
point of
U
.
We denote the set of regular points of a variety
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
as
U_{\text{reg}}
.

We define the
degree
of a surface
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
as the minimum degree of a polynomial
f\in\mathbb{R}[x,y,z]
that satisfies
{\bf V}(f)=U
.
There are several non-equivalent definitions for the degree of a variety in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
that is not a surface.
To avoid this issue, we say that the
complexity
of a variety
U
is the minimum integer
D
that satisfies the following.
There exist
k\leq D
polynomials
f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]
, each of degree at most
D
, such that
U={\bf V}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{k})
. In the past decade, the use of complexity is becoming more common. For example, see
[
5
,
24
]
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a variety of complexity
D
.

(a) The number of irreducible components of
U
is
O_{d,D}(1)
.

(b) The number of connected components of
U
is
O_{d,D}(1)
.

For the following quantitative variant of Theorem
2.1
(b), see Solymosi and Tao
[
24
]
(see also Barone and Basu
[
2
]
).

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a variety of dimension
k
and complexity
C
. Let
f\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]
be a polynomial of degree
D
. Then the number of connected components of
U\setminus{\bf V}(f)
is
O_{C,D,d,k}(D^{k})
.

For more information about the following lemma, see for example
[
4
, Section 3.3]
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a variety of complexity
D
and dimension
d>0
.
Then the set of singular points of
U
is a variety of dimension at most
d-1
and complexity
O_{D}(1)
.

Let
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
The
Zariski closure
of
S
, denoted
\overline{S}
, is the smallest variety that contains
S
.
Specifically, every variety that contains
S
also contains
\overline{S}
.
A set
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is
semi-algebraic
if there exists a boolean function
\Phi(y_{1},\ldots,y_{t})
and polynomials
f_{1},\ldots,f_{t}\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]
such that

p\in S\qquad\text{if and only if }\qquad\Phi(f_{1}(p)\geq 0,\ldots,f_{t}(p)\geq 0)=1.

The dimension of
S
is
\dim\overline{S}
.
The
complexity
of
S
is the minimum
t
such that
S
can be described with at most
t
polynomials of degree at most
t
.
The projection of a real variety may not be a variety.
However, it must be semi-algebraic.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a variety of complexity
k
and of dimension
d^{\prime}
. Let
\pi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{e}
be a
standard projection: a linear map that keeps
e
out of the
d
coordinates of a point in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Then
\pi(U)
is a semi-algebraic set of dimension at most
d^{\prime}
and of complexity
O_{k,d}(1)
.

For more details about Lemma
2.4
, see for example
[
3
, Section 14.2]
.
A projection is equivalent to adding an existential quantifier to some of the variables.
The above reference discusses how to then eliminate such quantifiers.

For more information about the following lemma, see for example
[
3
, Section 16.4]
.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a semi-algebraic set of complexity
t
. Then the number of connected components of
S
is
O_{d,t}(1)
.

Distinct distances: first bounds.

We state a couple of simple observations involving distinct distances in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
For a point
p\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
r>0
, we denote by
S(p,r)
the sphere of radius
r
centered at
p
.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
\gamma
be a one-dimensional variety of complexity
O(1)
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
Let
\mathcal{P}
be a set of
n
points on
\gamma
.
Then
D(\mathcal{P})=\Omega(n)
.

Proof.

By Theorem
2.1
,
\gamma
can be partitioned to
O(1)
irreducible components.
By the pigeonhole principle, there exists an irreducible one-dimensional component
\gamma^{\prime}
of
\gamma
that contains
\Theta(n)
points of
\mathcal{P}
.
Set
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}=\mathcal{P}\cap\gamma^{\prime}
.

Let
p
be an arbitrary point of
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
.
Fix
r>0
, and note that the sphere
S(p,r)
cannot contain
\gamma^{\prime}
.
Since
\gamma^{\prime}
is irreducible, the intersection
S(p,r)\cap\gamma^{\prime}
is of dimension zero (or empty).
By Theorem
2.1
, this intersection consists of
O(1)
points.
That is, the number of points of
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
at distance
r
from
p
is
O(1)
.
This implies that

D(\mathcal{P})\geq D(\mathcal{P}^{\prime})\geq D(\{p\},\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\setminus\{p\})=\Omega(n).

∎

Given two points
a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we denote the distance between
a
and
b
as
|ab|
.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
H_{1}
be the
xy
-plane in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and let
H_{2}
be a plane parallel to
H_{1}
(possibly
H_{1}=H_{2}
).
Let
\mathcal{P}_{1}\subset H_{1}
and
\mathcal{P}_{2}\subset H_{2}
be finite point sets.
Let
\pi:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to H_{1}
be the projection obtained by setting the
z
-coordinate to zero.
Then

D(\mathcal{P}_{1},\mathcal{P}_{2})=D(\mathcal{P}_{1},\pi(\mathcal{P}_{2})).

Proof.

The plane
H_{2}
is defined by an equation of the form
z=c
.
The distance between points
(a_{x},a_{y},0)\in H_{1}
and
(b_{x},b_{y},c)\in H_{2}
is

\sqrt{(a_{x}-b_{x})^{2}+(a_{y}-b_{y})^{2}+c^{2}}.

The distance between
(a_{x},a_{y},0)\in H_{1}
and
\pi(b_{x},b_{y},c)=(b_{x},b_{y},0)
is

\sqrt{(a_{x}-b_{x})^{2}+(a_{y}-b_{y})^{2}}.

Let
a,a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{1}
and
b,b^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}
.
By the above, we note that
|ab|=|a^{\prime}b^{\prime}|
if and only if
|a\pi(b)|=|a\pi(b^{\prime})|
.
Indeed, the square of both distances changes by
c^{2}
.
We conclude that
D(\mathcal{P}_{1},\mathcal{P}_{2})=D(\mathcal{P}_{1},\pi(\mathcal{P}_{2}))
.
∎

In this section we prove Theorem
1.3
.
We first present some additional preliminaries in Section
3.1
. In Section
3.2
, we study some properties of surfaces in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
Finally, we prove Theorem

in Sections
3.3
and

.

3.1
Additional preliminaries

Incidences.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a set of points and let
\mathcal{V}
be a set of varieties, both in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
An incidence is a pair
(p,U)\in\mathcal{P}\times\mathcal{V}
such that the point
p
lies on the variety
U
.
We denote the number of incidences in
\mathcal{P}\times\mathcal{V}
as
I(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{V})
.

A polynomial in
\mathbb{R}[x,y]
of degree at most
k
has at most
\binom{k+2}{2}
monomials.
We can thus define every such polynomial by a set of
\binom{k+2}{2}
real coefficients.
This leads to a bijection between the set of such polynomials and
\mathbb{R}^{\binom{k+2}{2}}
.
Since we are only interested in the zero sets of the polynomials, we think of two polynomials that differ by a constant factor as identical.
Therefore, we can think of the space of polynomials as the projective space
\mathbb{P}\mathbb{R}^{\binom{k+2}{2}}
.

A family of curves in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
is
s
-dimensional
if it corresponds to a variety
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathbb{P}\mathbb{R}^{\binom{D+2}{2}}
of a constant complexity and dimension
s
.
For example, the set of circles in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a 3-dimensional family and the set of circles of radius 1 is a 2-dimensional family.
The following is a result of Sharir and Zahl
[
23
]
.

Crossing numbers.
The

The following is a crossing lemma for graphs with parallel edges (for example, see

The

Let

3.2
Properties of surfaces in
\mathbb{R}^{3}

We now derive several lemmas that are used in the proof of Theorem
1.3
.
These lemmas study properties of surfaces in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
The reader may safely skip these lemmas, returning to them as required when going over the proof of Theorem
1.3
.
Recall that we define a surface as an irreducible variety of dimension two.

Surfaces of revolution.
A surface
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
is a
surface of revolution
if there exists a line
\ell
such that every rotation around
\ell
is a symmetry of
U
.
We refer to
\ell
as the
axis
of
U
.
Note that
U
is a union of disjoint circles with axis
\ell
.
Planes and spheres are surfaces of revolution with infinitely many axes.

Lemma 3.6
.

Excluding planes and sphere, every surface of revolution has exactly one axis.

Proof sketch.

Assume that
U
is a surface of revolution that has distinct axes
\ell
and
\ell^{\prime}
.
If
\ell
and
\ell^{\prime}
are parallel then
U
is a plane orthogonal to both lines.
We may thus assume that
\ell
and
\ell^{\prime}
are not parallel.

Since
\ell
is an axis of
U
, we have that
U
is a union of circles with axis
\ell
.
As we rotate
\mathbb{R}^{3}
around
\ell^{\prime}
, the line
\ell
has infinitely many distinct directions.
Since
U
remains unchanged by such rotations and
\ell
remains an axis of
U
, there are infinitely many directions
v
such that
U
is the union of circles orthogonal to
v
.
Thus, every point of
U
is incident to infinitely many circles that are contained in
U
.
Only planes and spheres have this property (for example, see
[
20
]
).
∎

Lemma 3.7
.

For every integer
k
there exists a constant
\delta_{k}=O_{k}(1)
that satisfies the following.
Let
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a surface of degree
k
.
For a line
\ell\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
, let
\Gamma_{\ell}
be the set of circles with axis
\ell
that are contained in
U
.
If
|\Gamma_{\ell}|>\delta_{k}
then
U
is a surface of revolution with axis
\ell
.

Proof.

By rotating and translating
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, we may assume that
\ell
is the
z
-axis.
Then, every circle of
\Gamma_{\ell}
can be defined using two parameters
h,r
as

C_{h,r}=\left\{\left(r\cdot\frac{2t}{1+t^{2}},r\cdot\frac{1-t^{2}}{1+t^{2}},h\right)\ :\ t\in\mathbb{R}\right\}.

(One point of the circle is missing in this parametrization:
(0,-r,h)
.)

Let
f\in\mathbb{R}[x,y,z]
be a degree
k
polynomial satisfying
{\bf V}(f)=U
(after the above transformation).
Then
C_{h,r}\subset U
if and only if

f\left(r\cdot\frac{2t}{1+t^{2}},r\cdot\frac{1-t^{2}}{1+t^{2}},h\right)

is identically zero.
This expression is a rational function in
t
, which may not be a polynomial.
We define

g(t)=f\left(r\cdot\frac{2t}{1+t^{2}},r\cdot\frac{1-t^{2}}{1+t^{2}},h\right)\cdot(1+t^{2})^{k}.

Note that
g(t)
is a polynomial in
t
of degree at most
2k
.
In addition,
C_{h,r}\subset U
if and only if
g(t)
is identically zero.
That is, all the coefficients of
g(t)
are zero.
Each such coefficient is a polynomial in
h
and
r
.

Consider another plane
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with coordinates
h
and
r
.
Let
W
be the set of points
(h,r)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
that satisfy
C_{h,r}\subset U
. By the preceding paragraph,
W
is a variety of complexity
O_{k}(1)
.
Indeed,
W
is defined by asking each of the coefficients of
g(t)
to vanish.
By Theorem
2.1
, there exists
\delta_{k}
such that
W
consists of at most
\delta_{k}
components.
By assumption
|\Gamma_{\ell}|>\delta_{k}
, so
W
is not zero-dimensional.
That is,
\Gamma_{\ell}
is a one-dimensional family of circles with axis
\ell
.

For
0\leq\alpha<2\pi
, let
R_{\alpha}:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a rotation of angle
\alpha
around
\ell
.
Set
U_{\alpha}=U\cap R_{\alpha}(U)
.
In other words,
U_{\alpha}
is the set of points of
U
that remain in
U
after a rotation of angle
2\pi-\alpha
around
\ell
.
Note that
U_{\alpha}={\bf V}(f,f\circ R_{2\pi-\alpha})
, so it is a variety.
Since
\Gamma_{\ell}
is an infinite family of disjoint circles with axis
\ell
, we get that
U_{\alpha}
is two-dimensional.
Since
U
is irreducible and
U_{\alpha}\subseteq U
, we conclude that
U=U_{\alpha}
.

The preceding paragraph holds for every
0\leq\alpha<2\pi
.
That is, every rotation around
\ell
is a symmetry of
U
.
In other words,
U
is a surface of revolution with axis
\ell
.
∎

Recall that we defined aligned and perpendicular circles in the introduction.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a surface of degree
k
. Let
\Gamma
be a set of circles that are contained in
U
, such that every two circles of
\Gamma
are either aligned or perpendicular.

(a) If
U
is not a surface of revolution then
|\Gamma|=O_{k}(1)
.

(b) Assume that
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
is a surface revolution with a unique axis
\ell
.
If no circle of
\Gamma
has axis
\ell
then
|\Gamma|=O_{k}(1)
.

Proof.

(a) For a plane
H\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
, let
\Gamma_{H}
be the set of circles of
\Gamma
that are contained in
H
.
Since circles in the same plane cannot be perpendicular, every two circles of
\Gamma_{H}
are aligned.
That is, every two circles of
\Gamma_{H}
are concentric.
Since
U
is not a plane,
\gamma=U\cap H
is a variety of dimension at most one and complexity
O_{k}(1)
.
Let
\ell
be a line contained in
H
and incident to the center of the circles of
\Gamma_{H}
.
Then
\ell
intersects every circle of
\Gamma_{H}
in two distinct points.
By Theorem
2.1
, when
|\Gamma_{H}|
is sufficiently large, the intersection
\ell\cap\gamma
cannot be zero dimensional.
That is, we obtain that
\ell\subset\gamma
.
Since this holds for every such line
\ell
, we get that
\gamma=H
.
This implies that
U=H
, contradicting the assumption that
U
is not a surface of revolution.
We conclude that
|\Gamma_{H}|=O_{k}(1)
.

Let
\ell\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a line and let
\Gamma_{\ell}
be the set of circles of
\Gamma
that have
\ell
as their axis.
By Lemma
3.7
, we have that
|\Gamma_{\ell}|=O_{k}(1)
.
That is, the maximum number of circles of
\Gamma
that have a common axis is
O_{k}(1)
.

Assume that
\Gamma
contains two aligned circles
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
that do not lie in the same plane.
No circle can be perpendicular to both
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
, since the center of such a circle would need to lie on two parallel planes.
Thus, all the circles in
\Gamma
are aligned.
By the preceding paragraph, in this case
|\Gamma|=O_{k}(1)
.

No four planes in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
are pairwise perpendicular.
This implies that no four circles in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
are pairwise perpendicular.
Thus,
\Gamma
contains at most three pairwise perpendicular circles.
By the above, the plane of each of these circles contains
O_{k}(1)
circles of
\Gamma
.
We conclude that, if
\Gamma
contains perpendicular circles then
|\Gamma|=O_{k}(1)

We handled the case where
\Gamma
contains two aligned circles that are not coplanar.
We also handled the case where
\Gamma
contains two perpendicular circles.
These complete the proof, since no other case remains.

(b) We claim that the proof of part (a) also holds for part (b).
In that proof, we only used
U
not being a surface of revolution when applying Lemma
3.7
.
Due to the assumption that the circles of
\Gamma
do not have axis
\ell
, we may apply Lemma
3.7
as before.
∎

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a surface of degree

.
Let

If the angle of

Consider a point

Let

By applying

Ruled surfaces.

Let
U
be a surface in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
As stated in the introduction,
U
is
ruled
if for every point
p\in U
there exists a line that is contained in
U
and incident to
p
.
We only require one basic property of non-ruled surfaces.
For more details, see for example
[
10

.

Lemma 3.10
.

A non-ruled surface of degree
D
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
contains
O_{D}(1)
lines.

Cylindrical surfaces.
A surface
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
is a
cylindrical surface
if there exists a curve
\gamma
that satisfies the following.
The curve
\gamma
is contained in a plane
H\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
with normal
\vec{v}
.
The surface
U
is the union of the lines in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
of direction
\vec{v}
that are incident to a point of
\gamma
.
Equivalently,
U
is the union of the translations of
\gamma
in direction
\vec{v}
.
Note that a plane is a cylindrical surface and that every cylindrical surface is also a ruled surface.

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a surface with reflectional symmetries about the distinct planes
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
.
If
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
are parallel then
U
is a cylindrical surface.

Proof.

Let
\delta
denote the distance between
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
.
Let
\vec{v}
be a vector in the direction orthogonal to
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
.
It is not difficult to verify that composing the two reflections leads to a translation
T
of distance
\delta
in direction
\pm\vec{v}
.
By definition,
T
is also a symmetry of
U
.

Let
p
be a point of
U
.
Let
\ell\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be the line incident to
p
and of direction
\vec{v}
.
By repeatedly applying
T
on
p
, we obtain that
\ell
contains infinitely many points of
U
.
By Theorem
2.1
, the intersection
U\cap\ell
is not zero-dimensional.
This implies that
\ell\subseteq U
.
In other words, for every
p\in U
there exists a line
\ell\subset U
incident to
p
and of direction
\vec{v}
.
We conclude that
U
is a cylindrical surface.
∎

3.3
Proof of Theorem
1.3

We are now ready to prove our main result. We first recall the statement of this theorem.

Theorem
1.3
.

Let
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a non-ruled surface of degree
k
.
Let
\mathcal{P}
be a set of
n
points on
U
.
Then for any
{\varepsilon}>0
, we have

D(\mathcal{P})=\Omega_{k}\left(n^{32/39-{\varepsilon}}\right).

Proof.

Since
U
is non-ruled, it is not a plane. By Theorem
1.1
, we may also assume that
U
is not a sphere. (We may also assume that
U
is not a hyperboloid of two sheet, but this is not necessary for our proof.)

By Lemma
3.10
, the surface
U
contains
O_{k}(1)
lines.
If one of these lines contains
\Omega(n/\log n)

\mathcal{P}
, then these points span
\Omega(n/\log n)
distinct distances.
We may thus assume that each of these lines contains
O(n/\log n)
points of
\mathcal{P}
.
We discard from
\mathcal{P}
all points that lie on a line contained in
U
.
After a generic rotation around the origin, we may also assume that
U
does not contain lines that are parallel to the
z

We apply Theorem
3.3
, to obtain a variety
U_{0}
and monotone patches

By definition,

.
We may assume that

contains

points of
\mathcal{P}
.
Otherwise, Lemma

implies a result stronger than required.
Since

U_{j}
that contains
\Theta_{k}(n)
points of
\mathcal{P}
.
We discard from
\mathcal{P}
the points that are not on

.
Abusing notation, we refer to the set of remaining points as
\mathcal{P}
and to
U_{j}
as

.

Constructing a multigraph.

Recall that, for a point

the sphere of radius

We define the

That is,

is the set of points on

at distance

from

Since

is not a patch of a sphere, every level set is a semi-algebraic set of dimension at most one and complexity

.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be the set of level sets

that are incident to at least one point of
\mathcal{P}
and with

.
For every level set
L(p,r)
from
\mathcal{S}
, we discard the zero-dimensional components and the singular points of

.
By Lemma

, this removes

We discard empty sets from
\mathcal{S}
.
Each remaining set is a semi-algebraic set of complexity

We partition every remaining element of
\mathcal{S}
to its connected components.
By Lemma

, each such set consists of
O_{k}(1)
connected components.

After the above process,
\mathcal{S}
consists of segments of curves with no singular points.
Some of those may also be closed.
Since the level sets are contained in spheres, all segments are bounded.
We discard from

\mathcal{P}

Following Székely’s technique

We place a vertex in

We go over the segments of

By “consecutive” we mean that, when traveling along the segment, there are no other points of

Since the segments do not contain singular points, no segment intersects itself.

The crossing number.

The proof of the theorem is based on double counting
\mathrm{cr}(G)
.
Recall that crossing numbers do not change when switching between the plane and a monotone patch.
First, we draw
G
on
U
according to its geometric representation.
That is, every vertex of
V
is placed at the corresponding point of
\mathcal{P}
.
Every edge of
E
is drawn as the arc of the level set that led to it. These edges are disjoint from the boundary of
U
.

The intersection of two level sets
L(p_{1},r_{1})
and
L(p_{2},r_{2})
is
U\cap S(p_{1},r_{1})\cap S(p_{2},r_{2})
.
Set
C=S(p_{1},r_{1})\cap S(p_{2},r_{2})
and note that
C
is either a circle, a single point, or an empty set.
By Theorem
2.1
, either
C\subset U
or
|C\cap U|=O_{k}(1)
.
That is, the intersection of two level sets is either empty, a set of
O_{k}(1)
points, or a circle.

When two level sets intersect at a circle
C
, this may lead to two parallel edges of
E
that are drawn as the same arc of
C
.
In this case, we slightly move the drawing of one of the edges, so that the two edges no longer intersect in their interiors.
We obtain that the interiors of every two edges of
E
intersect in
O_{k}(1)
points.

Set
t=D(\mathcal{P})
.
For every
p\in\mathcal{P}
, the number of level sets of
p
that contain at least one point of
\mathcal{P}
is at most
t
.
Thus, there are
O(nt)
level sets that contain at least one point of
\mathcal{P}
.
By the above, the number of intersections between the edges that originate from two different level sets is
O_{k}(1)
. Slightly moving identical edges may increase the number of intersections, but not by more than a constant factor.
We conclude that

\mathrm{cr}(G)=O_{k}(n^{2}t^{2}).

Lemma 5.1
.

With the above definitions, at least one of the following holds:

•

•

For

Proof of Lemma

Theorem 5.2
.

Let

be a constant-degree irreducible polynomial, such that none of the three first partial derivatives of

is identically zero.
Then at least one of the following two cases holds.

(i)

For all

with
|A|=m
and

, we have

\displaystyle\hskip-31.29802pt\big|\big\{(a,a^{\prime},b,b^{\prime})\in A^{2}\times B^{2}\ :\ \text{ exists }c\in\mathbb{R}\text{ such that }F(a

\displaystyle,b,c)=F(a^{\prime},b^{\prime},c)=0\big\}\big|

\displaystyle=O\left(m^{4/3}n^{4/3}+m^{2}+n^{2}\right).

(ii)

There exists a one-dimensional subvariety

of complexity
O(1)
such that every

satisfies the following:
For each

there exist an open interval

and a real analytic function

with an analytic inverse such that

and for all

we have

(x,y,z)\in{\bf V}(F)\quad\text{ if and only if }\quad\psi_{1}^{p}(x)+\psi_{2}^{p}(y)+\psi_{3}^{p}(z)=0.

We now briefly sketch the remainder of the proof of Lemma
5.1
. We first construct a variety

that in some way describes the distances between

and

.
We then apply Theorem
5.2
on the polynomial

that generates

.
We show that, when we are in case (i) of the theorem,

is large.
Finally, we show that case (ii) of the theorem implies
\rho(s,t)=\varphi_{1}(\varphi_{2}(s)+\varphi_{3}(t))
as in the statement of Lemma
5.1
.

It is not difficult to show that any circle in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
is the zero-set of a polynomial of degree four.
Let
f_{1},f_{2}\in\mathbb{R}[x,y,z]
be polynomials of degree four that satisfy
C_{1}={\bf V}(f_{1})
and
C_{2}={\bf V}(f_{2})
.
Consider points
p=(p_{x},p_{y},p_{z})\in C_{1}
and

at a distance of
\delta\in\mathbb{R}
from each other.
Setting
\Delta=\delta^{2}
leads to

\displaystyle f_{1}(p_{x},p_{y},p_{z})

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle f_{2}(q_{x},q_{y},q_{z})

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle(p_{x}-q_{x})^{2}+(p_{y}-q_{y})^{2}

\displaystyle+(p_{z}-q_{z})^{2}=\Delta.

Considering
p_{x},p_{y},p_{z},q_{x},q_{y},q_{z},\Delta
as variables, the above system defines a variety

of complexity at most four.
At most two points

are contained in the axis of

.
For every other point
p\in C_{1}
and squared distance
\Delta
, there are at most two values of

that satisfy the above system.
This implies that
\dim U\leq 2
.
Since

is the union of infinitely many disjoint one-dimensional varieties (one for every

), we conclude that

.

Let

be the projection defined as

\pi(p_{x},p_{y},p_{z},q_{x},q_{y},q_{z},\Delta)=(p_{x},q_{x},\Delta).

Set

.
By Lemma

, this is a semi-algebraic set of dimension at most two and of complexity

.
Since

is a circle in the

The same holds for

, since it is a circle not contained in a plane parallel to the

Thus, the preimage of every point of

This implies that

.

Consider the set

Note that

and

.
These two missing points yield a one-dimensional constant-complexity semi-algebraic set

.

Let

be a constant-degree polynomial satisfying

.
We claim that no first partial derivative of

is identically zero.
Indeed, from the definition of

must depend on all three coordinates.
We may thus apply Theorem

with

.
We partition the remainder of the proof of Lemma

according to the case of the theorem that holds.

The quadruples case.
First assume that the first case of Theorem
5.2
holds (the case involving the number of quadruples).
Define

Q=\big\{(a,a^{\prime},b,^{\prime}b)\in\mathcal{P}_{1}^{2}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}^{2}\ :\ \text{ exists }c\in\mathbb{R}\text{ such that }F(a,b,c)=F(a^{\prime},b^{\prime},c)=0\big\}.

By Theorem

, we have that

|Q|=O\left(m^{4/3}n^{4/3}+m^{2}+n^{2}\right).

For

, set

.
In other words,

is the number of pairs in

.
Since every pair of

The number of quadruples in

Combining this observation with the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality gives us

Combining the two above bounds for

This completes the first case of the proof of Lemma

.

The case where

has a special form.
We now consider the second case of Theorem

We have that

(9)

To complete the proof of Theorem
1.4
(b), it remains to show that we cannot be in the second case of Lemma
5.1
.
For this, we rely on the following
derivative test
(see for example
[

).
For a function

, we write
f_{x}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
f\in\mathbb{R}[x,y]
be twice differentiable with
f_{y}\not\equiv 0
.
Let
N
be an open neighborhood in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
If there are differentiable
\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2},\varphi_{3}\in\mathbb{R}[z]
satisfying

f(x,y)=\varphi_{1}(\varphi_{2}(x)+\varphi_{3}(y))\ \text{ for every }(x,y)\in N

then

\frac{\partial^{2}(\log|f_{x}/f_{y}|)}{\partial x\partial y}\quad\text{is identically zero in $N$.}

Proof.

Assume that there exist
\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2},\varphi_{3}\in\mathbb{R}[z]
as stated in the lemma.
Then

\displaystyle f_{x}(x,y)=\frac{\partial\varphi_{1}}{\partial x}(x,y)\cdot\frac{\partial\varphi_{2}}{\partial x}(x)\quad\text{ and }\quad f_{y}(x,y)=\frac{\partial\varphi_{1}}{\partial y}(x,y)\cdot\frac{\partial\varphi_{3}}{\partial y}(y).

Combining the above gives

\log\left|f_{x}/f_{y}\right|=\log\left|\frac{\partial\varphi_{2}}{\partial x}(x)/\frac{\partial\varphi_{3}}{\partial y}(y)\right|=\log\left|\frac{\partial\varphi_{2}}{\partial x}(x)\right|-\log\left|\frac{\partial\varphi_{3}}{\partial y}(y)\right|.

Differentiating with respect to both
x
and
y
gives 0, which completes the proof.
∎

Recall that our goal is to show that the second case of Lemma
5.1
cannot happen.
By Lemma
5.3
, it suffices to show that

g(s,t)=\frac{\partial^{2}(\log|\rho_{t}/\rho_{s}|)}{\partial s\partial t}

is not identically zero in any open neighborhood of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
Since
\rho(s,t)
is a rational function in
s
and
t
, so is
g(s,t)
.
Thus, the

is identically zero everywhere if and only if the numerator is identically zero everywhere. That is, if every coefficient in the numerator is 0.

Since (

) includes a logarithm and an absolute value, how can we say that

is rational?
To see that, we set

.
Since
\rho(s,t)
is a rational function in
s
and
t
, so is
h(s,t)
.
For each point
(s,t)
that satisifies
\rho_{s}(s,t)\neq 0
and
\rho_{t}(s,t)\neq 0
, there exists an open neighbourhood where
|h(s,t)|=c\cdot h(s,t)
, where

. In either case, we have that

This implies that, at any point where both
\rho_{t}
and

are nonzero, we have that

.
This is indeed a rational function.
