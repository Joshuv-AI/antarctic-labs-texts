Axioms for retrodiction: achieving time-reversal symmetry with a prior
Arthur J. Parzygnat and Francesco Buscemi

Abstract
We propose a category-theoretic definition of retrodiction and use it to exhibit a time-reversal symmetry for all quantum channels. We do this by introducing retrodiction families and functors, which capture many intuitive properties that retrodiction should satisfy and are general enough to encompass both classical and quantum theories alike. Classical Bayesian inversion and all rotated and averaged Petz recovery maps define retrodiction families in our sense. However, averaged rotated Petz recovery maps, including the universal recovery map of Junge–Renner–Sutter–Wilde–Winter, do not define retrodiction functors, since they fail to satisfy some compositionality properties. Among all the examples we found of retrodiction families, the original Petz recovery map is the only one that defines a retrodiction functor. In addition, retrodiction functors exhibit an inferential time-reversal symmetry consistent with the standard formulation of quantum theory. The existence of such a retrodiction functor seems to be in stark contrast to the many no-go results on time-reversal symmetry for quantum channels. One of the main reasons is because such works defined time-reversal symmetry on the category of quantum channels alone, whereas we define it on the category of quantum channels and quantum states. This fact further illustrates the importance of a prior in time-reversal symmetry. † † Key words: Retrodiction; postdiction; Bayes; Jeffrey; probability kinematics; compositionality; category theory; recovery map; Petz map; inference; prior; time-reversal; monoidal category; dagger; process theory

Retrodiction versus time reversal

As we currently understand them, the laws of physics for closed systems are time-reversal symmetric in both classical and quantum theory.
The associated evolution is reversible in the sense that if one evolves any state to some time in the future,

In order to be agnostic to classical or quantum theory, we try to avoid using language that is only applicable to either. To describe retrodiction, we should first specify the collection of allowed systems (denoted
\mathpzc{A},\mathpzc{B},\mathpzc{C},\dots

{{\mathcal{E}}},{{\mathcal{F}}},\dots
), and states (denoted
\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\dots

\mathcal{E}:\mathpzc{A}\to\mathpzc{B}
from one system
\mathpzc{A}
to another
\mathpzc{B}

\alpha

(\alpha,\mathcal{E})

(\alpha,\mathcal{E})
and whose output is some map
\mathscr{R}_{\alpha,\mathcal{E}}:\mathpzc{B}\to\mathpzc{A}
that only depends on this input (this is sometimes called

The definition of retrodiction that we propose consists of the following
logical
axioms on such an assignment
\mathscr{R}
over all systems
\mathpzc{A},\mathpzc{B},\mathpzc{C},\dots
, evolutions (i.e., processes)
{{\mathcal{E}}},{{\mathcal{F}}},\dots
, and states
\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\dots
(remarks and clarifications on these axioms follow immediately after).

1.

Retrodiction should produce valid processes, i.e.,
the map
\mathscr{R}_{\alpha,\mathcal{E}}
should be a valid process.

2.

The map
\mathscr{R}_{\alpha,\mathcal{E}}
should take the
prediction

\mathcal{E}(\alpha)
back to the prior
\alpha
, i.e.,
\mathscr{R}_{\alpha,\mathcal{E}}(\mathcal{E}(\alpha))=\alpha
.

3.

The retrodiction of the process that does nothing is also the process that does nothing, i.e.,
\mathscr{R}_{\alpha,\mathrm{id}_{\mathpzc{A}}}=\mathrm{id}_{\mathpzc{A}}
.

4.

More generally, the retrodiction of a genuinely reversible process

\mathcal{E}:\mathpzc{A}\to\mathpzc{B}
is the inverse
\mathcal{E}^{-1}
of the original process, i.e.,
\mathscr{R}_{\alpha,\mathcal{E}}=\mathcal{E}^{-1}
, and is therefore independent of the prior.

5.

Retrodiction is involutive in the sense that retrodicting on a retrodiction gives back the original process, i.e.,
\mathscr{R}_{\mathcal{E}(\alpha),\mathscr{R}_{\alpha,\mathcal{E}}}=\mathcal{E}
.

6.

Retrodiction is compositional
in the sense that if one has a prior
\alpha
on system
\mathpzc{A}
and two successive processes
\mathcal{E}:\mathpzc{A}\to\mathpzc{B}
and
\mathcal{F}:\mathpzc{B}\to\mathpzc{C}
, then the retrodiction
\mathscr{R}_{\alpha,\mathcal{F}\circ\mathcal{E}}
associated with the composite process
\mathpzc{A}\xrightarrow{\mathcal{E}}\mathpzc{B}\xrightarrow{\mathcal{F}}\mathpzc{C}
is the composite of the retrodictions
\mathscr{R}_{\mathcal{E}(\alpha),\mathcal{F}}:\mathpzc{C}\to\mathpzc{B}
and
\mathscr{R}_{\alpha,\mathcal{E}}:\mathpzc{B}\to\mathpzc{A}
associated with the constituent components, i.e.,
\mathscr{R}_{\alpha,\mathcal{F}\circ\mathcal{E}}=\mathscr{R}_{\alpha,\mathcal{E}}\circ\mathscr{R}_{\mathcal{E}(\alpha),\mathcal{F}}
.

7.

Retrodiction is tensorial
in the sense that if one has priors
\alpha
and
\alpha^{\prime}
on systems
\mathpzc{A}
and
\mathpzc{A}^{\prime}
, respectively, as well as two processes
\mathcal{E}:\mathpzc{A}\to\mathpzc{B}
and
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}:\mathpzc{A}^{\prime}\to\mathpzc{B}^{\prime}

\mathscr{R}_{\alpha\otimes\alpha^{\prime},\mathcal{E}\otimes\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}:\mathpzc{B}\otimes\mathpzc{B}^{\prime}\to\mathpzc{A}\otimes\mathpzc{A}^{\prime}
associated with the tensor product of the systems and processes is equal to the tensor product
\mathscr{R}_{\alpha,\mathcal{E}}\otimes\mathscr{R}_{\alpha^{\prime},\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}
of the constituent retrodictions, i.e.,
\mathscr{R}_{\alpha\otimes\alpha^{\prime},\mathcal{E}\otimes\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}=\mathscr{R}_{\alpha,\mathcal{E}}\otimes\mathscr{R}_{\alpha^{\prime},\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}

Retrodiction as a monoidal functor

In the setting of quantum theory, we would like to define retrodiction as an assignment that takes a prior, defined on some matrix algebra, together with a process involving another matrix algebra, and produces a map in the opposite direction. However, working with matrix algebras is unnecessarily restrictive and it is more appropriate to include classical and hybrid classical/quantum systems in our analysis.
As such, we will model our
systems
with finite-dimensional unital
C^{*}
-algebras, where commutative algebras correspond to classical systems and general non-commutative algebras correspond to quantum (or hybrid) systems.
We will model our
processes
with completely positive trace-preserving (CPTP) maps between such
C^{*}
-algebras.
Briefly, one benefit of using
C^{*}
-algebras, as opposed to only matrix algebras, is that one can express quantum channels, classical stochastic maps, measurements, preparations, and instruments all as processes between
C^{*}
-algebras (see
[
36

for more details).
We are expressing processes in the Schrödinger picture where the processes describe the action on states.
We emphasize that we do
not
require
\mathcal{E}
to preserve any of the algebraic structure, nor do we require the unit of the algebras to be preserved.
The reader should not be discouraged by our usage of finite-dimensional

We would like retrodiction to satisfy a few more intuitive axioms than just these basic ones. In particular, we have not required a retrodiction family to be a functor. As of now, the only axioms we have incorporated are that the prior is recovered and that retrodiction remains CPTP. We have also assumed the axiom of
universality
, which says that retrodiction is a function depending only on the initial prior and the process (and nothing more).

One other natural axiom to assume for retrodiction is that if one considers the
identity
morphism
(\mathpzc{A},\alpha)\xrightarrow{\mathrm{id}_{\mathpzc{A}}}(\mathpzc{A},\alpha)
in
\mathbf{States}
for any state
\alpha
on
\mathpzc{A}
, then the retrodiction of this should be the identity itself. As an equation, this can be expressed as
\mathscr{R}_{\alpha,\mathrm{id}_{\mathpzc{A}}}=\mathrm{id}_{\mathpzc{A}}
. In words, retrodicting on the process that does nothing is itself the process that does nothing. This axiom is sometimes called
normalization
in the literature
[
54
,
56
,
55
]
.

A second axiom that we believe retrodiction should satisfy is
compositionality
. Namely, if one has two successive processes together with a prior (and successive predictions)

(\mathpzc{A},\alpha)\xrightarrow{\mathcal{E}}(\mathpzc{B},\beta)\xrightarrow{\mathcal{F}}(\mathpzc{C},\gamma),

then one can construct
three
retrodictions associated with these data. On the one hand, one can retrodict on the individual processes
\mathcal{E}
and
\mathcal{F}
to construct two successive retrodictions

(\mathpzc{A},\alpha)\xleftarrow{\mathscr{R}_{\alpha,\mathcal{E}}}(\mathpzc{B},\beta)\xleftarrow{\mathscr{R}_{\beta,\mathcal{F}}}(\mathpzc{C},\gamma).

On the other hand, one can construct the retrodiction associated with the
composite
process

(\mathpzc{A},\alpha)\xrightarrow{\mathcal{F}\circ\mathcal{E}}(\mathpzc{C},\gamma)

in which case one obtains

(\mathpzc{A},\alpha)\xleftarrow{\mathscr{R}_{\alpha,\mathcal{F}\circ\mathcal{E}}}(\mathpzc{C},\gamma).

The axiom of
compositionality
states that the composite of retrodictions for the two individual processes should equal the retrodiction of the composite process (cf.
[
11
,
54
,
56
,
55
]
), i.e.,
4
4

4

If we excluded the prior from our notation for retrodiction, then this would look like
\mathscr{R}_{\mathcal{F}\circ\mathcal{E}}=\mathscr{R}_{\mathcal{E}}\circ\mathscr{R}_{\mathcal{F}}
. We have chosen to include the prior in the notation to be more consistent with the quantum information literature.

\mathscr{R}_{\alpha,\mathcal{F}\circ\mathcal{E}}=\mathscr{R}_{\alpha,\mathcal{E}}\circ\mathscr{R}_{\beta,\mathcal{F}}.

Thus, normalization and compositionality precisely say that retrodiction should be a functor, written
\mathbf{States}\xrightarrow{\mathscr{R}}\mathbf{States}^{\mathrm{op}}
.

But there is another structure we would like retrodiction to preserve, and this is the natural tensor product structure for states. Indeed,
\mathbf{States}
and
\mathbf{CStates}
are (symmetric) monoidal categories, where the tensor product of two objects
(\mathpzc{A},\alpha)
and
(\mathpzc{A}^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime})
is
(\mathpzc{A}\otimes\mathpzc{A}^{\prime},\alpha\otimes\alpha^{\prime})
. The tensor product of two morphisms
(\mathpzc{A},\alpha)\xrightarrow{\mathcal{E}}(\mathpzc{B},\beta)
and
(\mathpzc{A}^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime})\xrightarrow{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}(\mathpzc{B}^{\prime},\beta^{\prime})
is the usual tensor product of CPTP maps, namely

(\mathpzc{A}\otimes\mathpzc{A}^{\prime},\alpha\otimes\alpha^{\prime})\xrightarrow{\mathcal{E}\otimes\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}(\mathpzc{B}\otimes\mathpzc{B}^{\prime},\beta\otimes\beta^{\prime}).

Intuitively, this monoidal (i.e., tensor product) structure on
\mathbf{States}
(and
\mathbf{CStates}
) says that if two systems are prepared independently and undergo independent evolution, then the joint prior and joint process is also a valid prior and process. Due to this independence, we would therefore also expect that if we independently retrodict on each process via

(\mathpzc{A},\alpha)\xleftarrow{\mathscr{R}_{\alpha,\mathcal{E}}}(\mathpzc{B},\beta)\qquad\text{ and }\qquad(\mathpzc{A}^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime})\xleftarrow{\mathscr{R}_{\alpha^{\prime},\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}}(\mathpzc{B}^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}),

then the joint retrodiction

(\mathpzc{A}\otimes\mathpzc{A}^{\prime},\alpha\otimes\alpha^{\prime})\xleftarrow{\mathscr{R}_{\alpha,\mathcal{E}}\otimes\mathscr{R}_{\alpha^{\prime},\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}}(\mathpzc{B}\otimes\mathpzc{B}^{\prime},\beta\otimes\beta^{\prime}),

should be the same as retrodicting on the joint process itself

(\mathpzc{A}\otimes\mathpzc{A}^{\prime},\alpha\otimes\alpha^{\prime})\xleftarrow{\mathscr{R}_{\alpha\otimes\alpha^{\prime},\mathcal{E}\otimes\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}}(\mathpzc{B}\otimes\mathpzc{B}^{\prime},\beta\otimes\beta^{\prime}),

i.e., we expect retrodiction to satisfy
tensoriality
, which states

\mathscr{R}_{\alpha,\mathcal{E}}\otimes\mathscr{R}_{\alpha^{\prime},\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}=\mathscr{R}_{\alpha\otimes\alpha^{\prime},\mathcal{E}\otimes\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}.

The mathematical way to summarize the above discussion is to say that retrodiction should be, at the very least, a monoidal functor
\mathbf{States}\to\mathbf{States}^{\mathrm{op}}

that acts as the identity on objects (we will also require other properties for retrodiction later).
Hence, already from these basic requirements of retrodiction, we are inevitably led to a description of retrodiction in terms of the mathematical discipline of category theory, specifically that of (symmetric) monoidal categories and monoidal functors. Note that these mathematical structures also appear in the settings of resource theories
[

]
, topological field theories
[

,

]
, topological order
[

]
, and many other contexts relevant to physics
[

.

In general, the collection of retrodiction families,

5
Inverting property for retrodiction families

A natural property one might want of a retrodiction family is that the retrodiction of an invertible (reversible) process is the inverse (reverse) process, and is, in particular, independent of the prior.

Definition 5.1
.

A retrodiction family
\mathbf{States}\xrightarrow{\mathscr{R}}\mathbf{States}^{\mathrm{op}}
is
inverting
iff

\mathscr{R}_{\alpha,{{\mathcal{E}}}}={{\mathcal{E}}}^{-1}
whenever
(\mathpzc{A},\alpha)\xrightarrow{{{\mathcal{E}}}}(\mathpzc{B},\beta)
is an isomorphism in
\mathbf{States}
.

Proposition 5.2
.

Every rotated Petz retrodiction family is inverting.

Before proving this, we recall a useful fact, reformulated in algebraic and categorical terms.

Lemma 5.3
.

Isomorphisms in the category
\mathbf{States}
are precisely state-preserving
*
-isomorphisms.

A special case of this lemma is well-known in the quantum information literature. Namely, it says that a CPTP map from one matrix algebra to another has a CPTP inverse if and only if it is of the form
\mathrm{Ad}_{U}
for some unitary
U
. In what follows, we provide a proof applicable to the more general algebraic setting.

Proof of Lemma
5.3
.

Let
(\mathpzc{A},\alpha)\xrightarrow{\mathcal{E}}(\mathpzc{B},\beta)
be an isomorphism in
\mathbf{States}
. This means there exists a morphism
(\mathpzc{A},\alpha)\xleftarrow{\mathcal{F}}(\mathpzc{B},\beta)
in
\mathbf{States}
such that
\mathcal{F}\circ\mathcal{E}=\mathrm{id}_{\mathpzc{A}}
and
\mathcal{E}\circ\mathcal{F}=\mathrm{id}_{\mathpzc{B}}
.
Write the Hilbert–Schmidt adjoints as
E:=\mathcal{E}^{*}
and
F:=\mathcal{F}^{*}
, which are completely positive
unital
maps. Then, for any
A\in\mathpzc{A}
,

E(A)^{{\dagger}}E(A)\leq E(A^{{\dagger}}A)=E\big(F(B)^{{\dagger}}F(B)\big)\leq E\big(F(B^{{\dagger}}B)\big)=B^{{\dagger}}B=E(A)^{{\dagger}}E(A),

where
B:=E(A)
and where the Kadison–Schwarz inequality was used (the first for
E
and the second for
F
). Since the outermost terms in this expression are equal, all intermediate terms are equal. Since this holds for arbitrary
A\in\mathpzc{A}
, the
Multiplication Lemma
(cf.
[
47
, Lemma 4.3]
or
[
86
, Theorem 5]
) then implies
E(A_{1})^{{\dagger}}E(A_{2})=E(A_{1}^{{\dagger}}A_{2})
for all
A_{1},A_{2}\in\mathpzc{A}
, which means that
E
is a
*
-homomorphism. Similarly,
F
is a
*
-homomorphism. Since
E
and
F
are inverses of each other, this proves that
E
is a
*
-isomorphism.
Thus,
E^{*}=\mathcal{E}
is a
*
-isomorphism. This shows that an isomorphism in
\mathbf{States}
is a state-preserving
*
-isomorphism.

Conversely, any state-preserving
*
-isomorphism is automatically a state-preserving CPTP map and therefore defines an isomorphism in
\mathbf{States}
.
∎

Note that the assumption of complete positivity for morphisms in
\mathbf{States}
is crucial in Lemma
5.3
. Indeed, although the transpose map on matrix algebras has itself as an inverse, it is not a
*
-homomorphism.

Proof of Proposition

Let
(\mathpzc{A},\alpha)\xrightarrow{{{\mathcal{E}}}}(\mathpzc{B},\beta)

\mathbf{States}

6
Involutive retrodiction

An interesting property emerges when two averaged rotated Petz retrodiction families are composed in succession. The meaning of composing two retrodiction families in succession is to iterate retrodiction procedures (i.e., retrodicting a retrodiction). Intuitively, we might expect to get back something closely related to the
original
process.
This happens, for example, when taking the Hilbert–Schmidt adjoint of an arbitrary CPTP map. Indeed, in the case of closed system evolution described by a unitary
U
, the Hilbert–Schmidt adjoint is the reverse evolution described by
U^{{\dagger}}
. Applying the adjoint again gives back
U
.
This is part of the reason why the Hilbert–Schmidt adjoint has often been viewed as a time-reversal assignment in the literature
[
88
,
19
,
25
,
28
]
.

From our perspective, however, the Hilbert–Schmidt adjoint is only reasonable in the setting where either one has unitary evolution (cf. Theorem
5.8
) or unital quantum channels. When one also has a prior in the form of a state that is not necessarily uniform, the Hilbert--Schmidt adjoint no longer takes the prediction back to the prior, nor is it necessarily a quantum channel (even upon rescaling)
14
14

14

An example is a discard-and-prepare channel whenever the prepared state is not proportional to the identity, i.e., it is not the maximally mixed state.
.
This is one of the reasons why we have introduced retrodiction families as assignments of the form
\mathbf{States}\to\mathbf{States}^{\mathrm{op}}
, as opposed to sending only quantum channels to quantum channels in the opposite direction without including the data of states. Nevertheless, we might hope a similar involutive property holds for at least some retrodiction families.
In this section, we show that among all averaged rotated Petz retrodiction maps, the original process is always obtained upon iteration only for the (un-rotated) Petz recovery map. In particular, the Petz recovery map bypasses the many no-go theorems in the literature regarding time-reversal symmetry.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
\mu
and
\nu

{{\mathbb{R}}}
and let

\mathbf{States}
. Then
15
15

15

If
\mathscr{R},\mathscr{R}^{\prime}:\mathbf{States}\to\mathbf{States}^{\mathrm{op}}

\big(\mathscr{R}^{\mathrm{P},\nu}\circ\mathscr{R}^{\mathrm{P},\mu}\big)_{\alpha,{{\mathcal{E}}}}:=\mathscr{R}^{\mathrm{P},\nu}_{\beta,\mathscr{R}^{\mathrm{P},\mu}_{\alpha,{{\mathcal{E}}}}}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{Ad}_{\beta^{-ir}}\circ{{\mathcal{E}}}\circ\mathrm{Ad}_{\alpha^{ir}}\,d(\mu\ast\nu)(r),

where

\mu
and

\big(\mathscr{R}^{\mathrm{P},y}\circ\mathscr{R}^{\mathrm{P},x}\big)_{\alpha,{{\mathcal{E}}}}=\mathrm{Ad}_{\beta^{-i(x+y)}}\circ{{\mathcal{E}}}\circ\mathrm{Ad}_{\alpha^{i(x+y)}}

By a direct computation, we obtain

\begin{split}\mathscr{R}^{\mathrm{P},\nu}_{\beta,\mathscr{R}^{\mathrm{P},\mu}_{\alpha,{{\mathcal{E}}}}}&=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathscr{R}^{\mathrm{P},t}_{\beta,\mathscr{R}^{\mathrm{P},\mu}_{\alpha,{{\mathcal{E}}}}}\,d\nu(t)\\

By changing variables

The second claim follows by setting

7
A summary of recovery maps and their properties

We proved that the Petz recovery map provides an inferential time-reversal symmetry for such dynamics, bypassing many previous no-go theorems on time-reversal symmetry.

Appendix A
The Hilbert–Schmidt inner product on
C^{*}
-algebras

In the following, we provide a quick review of traces, the Hilbert–Schmidt inner product, the KMS inner product, and the Petz recovery map for finite-dimensional
C^{*}
-algebras. The reader is referred to
[
91
, Chapter 8]
for more details. Given a finite-dimensional
C^{*}
-algebra,
\mathpzc{A}
, there is a canonical
trace
,
{\rm tr}
. Indeed, since every such
\mathpzc{A}
is
*
-isomorphic to a direct sum of matrix algebras
[
73
, Theorem 5.20 and Proposition 5.26]
, say
\Phi:\mathpzc{A}\xrightarrow{\cong}\bigoplus_{x\in X}\mathbb{M}_{m_{x}}({{\mathbb{C}}})
, the trace on
\mathpzc{A}
is defined by

{\rm tr}(A):={\rm tr}\big(\Phi(A)\big),

where the latter trace is just the sum of the component-wise traces. This is independent of the choice of
*
-isomorphism
\Phi
due to the cyclic property of the trace. Namely, if
\Psi
is another such
*
-isomorphism, then
\Psi\circ\Phi^{-1}
is a
*
-isomorphism of a direct sum of matrix algebras, which leaves the trace invariant. Hence,

{\rm tr}\big(\Phi(A)\big)={\rm tr}\Big(\big(\Psi\circ\Phi^{-1}\big)\big(\Phi(A)\big)\Big)={\rm tr}\big(\Psi(A)\big).

Because of this, there is a canonical
Hilbert–Schmidt inner product
on
\mathpzc{A}
as well.
Namely, this inner product is defined on
(A_{1},A_{2})\in\mathpzc{A}\times\mathpzc{A}
by

\langle A_{1},A_{2}\rangle={\rm tr}\big(A_{1}^{{\dagger}}A_{2}\big)

in terms of the above-defined trace.

With such an inner product on every finite-dimensional
C^{*}
-algebra, if now
\mathpzc{A}\xrightarrow{{{\mathcal{E}}}}\mathpzc{B}
is a linear map, then
{{\mathcal{E}}}^{*}
is the
Hilbert–Schmidt adjoint
of
{{\mathcal{E}}}
and is defined as the adjoint of
{{\mathcal{E}}}
with respect to the Hilbert–Schmidt inner products on
\mathpzc{B}
and
\mathpzc{A}
. Namely,
\mathpzc{A}\xleftarrow{{{\mathcal{E}}}^{*}}\mathpzc{B}
is the unique linear map satisfying

{\rm tr}\big({{\mathcal{E}}}^{*}(B)^{{\dagger}}A\big)\equiv\langle{{\mathcal{E}}}^{*}(B),A\rangle=\langle B,{{\mathcal{E}}}(A)\rangle\equiv{\rm tr}\big(B^{{\dagger}}{{\mathcal{E}}}(A)\big)

for all
A\in\mathpzc{A}
and
B\in\mathpzc{B}
.

Given a state-preserving morphism
(\mathpzc{A},\alpha)\xrightarrow{{{\mathcal{E}}}}(\mathpzc{B},\beta)
of non-degenerate quantum probability spaces, the
Petz recovery map

\mathscr{R}^{\mathrm{P}}_{\alpha,{{\mathcal{E}}}}
is defined as the adjoint, not with respect to the Hilbert–Schmidt inner product, but with respect to the following inner products on
\mathpzc{A}
and
\mathpzc{B}
. The
KMS inner product
on
\mathpzc{A}
associated with the faithful state
\alpha
is defined by sending
(A_{1},A_{2})\in\mathpzc{A}\times\mathpzc{A}
to

\langle A_{1},A_{2}\rangle_{\alpha}:={\rm tr}\Big(A_{1}^{{\dagger}}\alpha^{-1/2}A_{2}\alpha^{-1/2}\Big).

The Petz recovery map
\mathscr{R}^{\mathrm{P}}_{\alpha,{{\mathcal{E}}}}
is therefore the unique map that satisfies

\big\langle B,{{\mathcal{E}}}(A)\big\rangle_{\beta}=\left\langle\mathscr{R}^{\mathrm{P}}_{\alpha,{{\mathcal{E}}}}(B),A\right\rangle_{\alpha}

for all
A\in\mathpzc{A}
and
B\in\mathpzc{B}
. Writing this out explicitly gives

{\rm tr}\Big(B^{{\dagger}}\beta^{-1/2}{{\mathcal{E}}}(A)\beta^{-1/2}\Big)={\rm tr}\Big(\mathscr{R}^{\mathrm{P}}_{\alpha,{{\mathcal{E}}}}(B)^{{\dagger}}\alpha^{-1/2}A\alpha^{-1/2}\Big).

From this expression, the formula for the Petz recovery map in Definition
3.1
follows.

Appendix B
Convex sums of rotated Petz recovery maps

In this appendix, we prove Proposition
4.6
. In fact, we even show that it suffices to work with the class of bit-flip channels on qubits to find counter-examples to compositionality and tensoriality, even in the symmetric case when
\lambda=\frac{1}{2}
and
s=-t
in Proposition
4.6
. Since it is known that the bit flip channel is covariant only with respect to the uniform state
[
82
]
, we need to provide a counter-example where at least two of the states are non-uniform states.

Let
\mathpzc{C}=\mathpzc{B}=\mathpzc{A}=\mathbb{M}_{2}({{\mathbb{C}}})
denote the
C^{*}
-algebra of
2\times 2
matrices. Let
\alpha=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\theta&0\\
0&1-\theta\end{smallmatrix}\right]
,
\beta=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\phi&0\\
0&1-\phi\end{smallmatrix}\right]
and
\gamma=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\psi&0\\
0&1-\psi\end{smallmatrix}\right]
be states on
\mathpzc{A}
,
\mathpzc{B}
, and
\mathpzc{C}
, respectively, for some
\theta,\phi,\psi\in(0,1)
. Let
\Omega_{p}:\mathbb{M}_{2}({{\mathbb{C}}})\to\mathbb{M}_{2}({{\mathbb{C}}})
be the bit-flip channel weighted by
p\in[0,1]
and given explicitly by

\Omega_{p}=(1-p)\mathrm{id}+p\mathrm{Ad}_{\sigma_{x}},\qquad\text{ where }\sigma_{x}=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix}.

Note the set of bit-flip channels is closed under composition, and in fact

\Omega_{q}\circ\Omega_{p}=\Omega_{(1-q)p+q(1-p)}

for all
p,q\in[0,1]
.
Now, set
\mathpzc{A}\xrightarrow{{{\mathcal{E}}}}\mathpzc{B}
and
\mathpzc{B}\xrightarrow{{{\mathcal{F}}}}\mathpzc{C}
to be the bit-flip channels
\Omega_{p}
and
\Omega_{q}
, respectively, where
p,q\in[0,1]
are arbitrary for now. Then
(\mathpzc{A},\alpha)\xrightarrow{{{\mathcal{E}}}}(\mathpzc{B},\beta)\xrightarrow{{{\mathcal{F}}}}(\mathpzc{C},\gamma)
is a composable pair of morphisms in
\mathbf{States}
if and only if

\phi=(1-p)\theta+p(1-\theta)\quad\text{ and }\quad\psi=(1-q)\phi+q(1-\phi).

We will therefore assume these conditions are always met in this example.
The associated rotated Petz recovery maps are given by

\mathscr{R}^{\mathrm{P},t}_{\alpha,{{\mathcal{E}}}}=(1-p)\mathrm{Ad}_{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\phi^{it-1/2}\theta^{-it+1/2}&0\\
0&(1-\phi)^{it-1/2}(1-\theta)^{-it+1/2}\end{smallmatrix}\right]}+p\mathrm{Ad}_{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0&(1-\phi)^{it-1/2}\theta^{-it+1/2}\\
\phi^{it-1/2}(1-\theta)^{-it+1/2}&0\end{smallmatrix}\right]}

and similarly

\mathscr{R}^{\mathrm{P},t}_{\beta,{{\mathcal{F}}}}=(1-q)\mathrm{Ad}_{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\psi^{it-1/2}\phi^{-it+1/2}&0\\
0&(1-\psi)^{it-1/2}(1-\phi)^{-it+1/2}\end{smallmatrix}\right]}+q\mathrm{Ad}_{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0&(1-\psi)^{it-1/2}\phi^{-it+1/2}\\
\psi^{it-1/2}(1-\phi)^{-it+1/2}&0\end{smallmatrix}\right]}\;.

Several simplifications occur when
q=\frac{1}{2}
, so we will henceforth assume this. In particular,
\gamma=\frac{1}{2}\mathds{1}_{2}
, i.e.,
\psi=\frac{1}{2}
, so that

\mathscr{R}^{\mathrm{P},t}_{\beta,{{\mathcal{F}}}}=\mathrm{Ad}_{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\phi^{-it+1/2}&0\\
0&(1-\phi)^{-it+1/2}\end{smallmatrix}\right]}+\mathrm{Ad}_{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0&\phi^{-it+1/2}\\
(1-\phi)^{-it+1/2}&0\end{smallmatrix}\right]}\;.

In addition, since
{{\mathcal{F}}}\circ{{\mathcal{E}}}=\Omega_{1/2}
as a function, we obtain

\mathscr{R}^{\mathrm{P},t}_{\alpha,{{\mathcal{F}}}\circ{{\mathcal{E}}}}=\mathrm{Ad}_{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\theta^{-it+1/2}&0\\
0&(1-\theta)^{-it+1/2}\end{smallmatrix}\right]}+\mathrm{Ad}_{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0&\theta^{-it+1/2}\\
(1-\theta)^{-it+1/2}&0\end{smallmatrix}\right]}\;.

Now, let
\mathscr{R}:=\lambda\mathscr{R}^{\mathrm{P},t}+(1-\lambda)\mathscr{R}^{\mathrm{P},s}
be the convex combination of two rotated Petz recovery maps with
t,s\in{{\mathbb{R}}}
weighted by some
\lambda\in(0,1)
. For our purposes, it suffices to consider the symmetric combination given by
s=-t
and
\lambda=\frac{1}{2}.

Then

\begin{split}\mathscr{R}_{\alpha,{{\mathcal{E}}}}&=\frac{1-p}{2}\mathrm{Ad}_{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\phi^{it-1/2}\theta^{-it+1/2}&0\\
0&(1-\phi)^{it-1/2}(1-\theta)^{-it+1/2}\end{smallmatrix}\right]}+\frac{p}{2}\mathrm{Ad}_{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0&(1-\phi)^{it-1/2}\theta^{-it+1/2}\\
\phi^{it-1/2}(1-\theta)^{-it+1/2}&0\end{smallmatrix}\right]}\\
&+\frac{1-p}{2}\mathrm{Ad}_{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\phi^{-it-1/2}\theta^{it+1/2}&0\\
0&(1-\phi)^{-it-1/2}(1-\theta)^{it+1/2}\end{smallmatrix}\right]}+\frac{p}{2}\mathrm{Ad}_{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0&(1-\phi)^{-it-1/2}\theta^{it+1/2}\\
\phi^{-it-1/2}(1-\theta)^{it+1/2}&0\end{smallmatrix}\right]},\end{split}

\begin{split}\mathscr{R}_{\beta,{{\mathcal{F}}}}&=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Ad}_{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\phi^{-it+1/2}&0\\
0&(1-\phi)^{-it+1/2}\end{smallmatrix}\right]}+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Ad}_{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0&\phi^{-it+1/2}\\
(1-\phi)^{-it+1/2}&0\end{smallmatrix}\right]}\\
&+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Ad}_{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\phi^{it+1/2}&0\\
0&(1-\phi)^{it+1/2}\end{smallmatrix}\right]}+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Ad}_{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0&\phi^{it+1/2}\\
(1-\phi)^{it+1/2}&0\end{smallmatrix}\right]},\end{split}

and

\begin{split}\mathscr{R}_{\alpha,{{\mathcal{F}}}\circ{{\mathcal{E}}}}&=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Ad}_{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\theta^{-it+1/2}&0\\
0&(1-\theta)^{-it+1/2}\end{smallmatrix}\right]}+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Ad}_{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0&\theta^{-it+1/2}\\
(1-\theta)^{-it+1/2}&0\end{smallmatrix}\right]}\\
&+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Ad}_{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\theta^{it+1/2}&0\\

(1-\theta)^{it+1/2}&0\end{smallmatrix}\right]},\end{split}

the last of which is the retrodiction map associated with the composite.
However, the composite of the individual retrodiction maps is given by

\begin{split}\mathscr{R}_{\alpha,{{\mathcal{E}}}}\circ\mathscr{R}_{\beta,{{\mathcal{F}}}}&=\frac{1}{4}\left(\mathscr{R}^{\mathrm{P},t}_{\alpha,{{\mathcal{E}}}}\circ\mathscr{R}^{\mathrm{P},t}_{\beta,{{\mathcal{F}}}}+\mathscr{R}^{\mathrm{P},-t}_{\alpha,{{\mathcal{E}}}}\circ\mathscr{R}^{\mathrm{P},-t}_{\beta,{{\mathcal{F}}}}+\mathscr{R}^{\mathrm{P},-t}_{\alpha,{{\mathcal{E}}}}\circ\mathscr{R}^{\mathrm{P},t}_{\beta,{{\mathcal{F}}}}+\mathscr{R}^{\mathrm{P},t}_{\alpha,{{\mathcal{E}}}}\circ\mathscr{R}^{\mathrm{P},-t}_{\beta,{{\mathcal{F}}}}\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\mathscr{R}_{\alpha,{{\mathcal{F}}}\circ{{\mathcal{E}}}}\\
&+\frac{1-p}{4}\mathrm{Ad}_{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\phi^{-2it}\theta^{it+1/2}&0\\
0&(1-\phi)^{-2it}(1-\theta)^{it+1/2}\end{smallmatrix}\right]}+\frac{p}{4}\mathrm{Ad}_{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0&(1-\phi)^{-2it}\theta^{it+1/2}\\
\phi^{-2it}(1-\theta)^{it+1/2}&0\end{smallmatrix}\right]}\\
&+\frac{1-p}{4}\mathrm{Ad}_{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0&\phi^{-2it}\theta^{it+1/2}\\
(1-\phi)^{-2it}(1-\theta)^{it+1/2}&0\end{smallmatrix}\right]}+\frac{p}{4}\mathrm{Ad}_{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}(1-\phi)^{-2it}\theta^{it+1/2}&0\\
0&\phi^{-2it}(1-\theta)^{it+1/2}\end{smallmatrix}\right]}\\
&+\frac{1-p}{4}\mathrm{Ad}_{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\phi^{2it}\theta^{-it+1/2}&0\\
0&(1-\phi)^{2it}(1-\theta)^{-it+1/2}\end{smallmatrix}\right]}+\frac{p}{4}\mathrm{Ad}_{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0&(1-\phi)^{2it}\theta^{-it+1/2}\\
\phi^{2it}(1-\theta)^{-it+1/2}&0\end{smallmatrix}\right]}\\
&+\frac{1-p}{4}\mathrm{Ad}_{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0&\phi^{2it}\theta^{-it+1/2}\\
(1-\phi)^{2it}(1-\theta)^{-it+1/2}&0\end{smallmatrix}\right]}+\frac{p}{4}\mathrm{Ad}_{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}(1-\phi)^{2it}\theta^{-it+1/2}&0\\
0&\phi^{2it}(1-\theta)^{-it+1/2}\end{smallmatrix}\right]}.\end{split}

This suggests that
\mathscr{R}_{\alpha,{{\mathcal{E}}}}\circ\mathscr{R}_{\beta,{{\mathcal{F}}}}\neq\mathscr{R}_{\alpha,{{\mathcal{F}}}\circ{{\mathcal{E}}}}
, but it may be the case that surprising cancellations occur. To be sure, let us plug in a non-diagonal matrix such as
\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\
0&0\end{smallmatrix}\right]
. This gives

\mathscr{R}_{\alpha,{{\mathcal{F}}}\circ{{\mathcal{E}}}}\left(\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}\right)=\sqrt{\theta(1-\theta)}\cos\left(\ln\left(\frac{\theta}{1-\theta}\right)t\right)\sigma_{x}

and

\begin{split}\left(\mathscr{R}_{\alpha,{{\mathcal{E}}}}\circ\mathscr{R}_{\beta,{{\mathcal{F}}}}\right)\left(\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}\right)=\frac{\sqrt{\theta(1-\theta)}}{2}\Bigg[&\cos\left(\ln\left(\frac{\theta}{1-\theta}\right)t\right)+p\cos\left(\ln\left(\frac{\theta\phi^{2}}{(1-\theta)(1-\phi)^{2}}\right)t\right)+\\

At this point, it may already be clear that these two expressions are different, but just to be very concrete, let us consider the special case where

0&0\end{bmatrix}\right)=0.

This illustrates that
\mathscr{R}_{\alpha,{{\mathcal{E}}}}\circ\mathscr{R}_{\beta,{{\mathcal{F}}}}\neq\mathscr{R}_{\alpha,{{\mathcal{F}}}\circ{{\mathcal{E}}}}
, i.e., compositionality fails in general (in fact, for most values of
t
).

We will also use this example to show that tensoriality fails. In this regard, consider another morphism
(\mathpzc{A}^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime})\xrightarrow{{{\mathcal{E}}}^{\prime}}(\mathpzc{B}^{\prime},\beta^{\prime})
, where
\mathpzc{B}^{\prime}=\mathbb{M}_{2}({{\mathbb{C}}})=\mathpzc{A}^{\prime}
,
{{\mathcal{E}}}^{\prime}=\Omega_{p^{\prime}}
, and
\alpha^{\prime}
and
\beta^{\prime}
are given by the density matrices

\alpha^{\prime}=\begin{bmatrix}\theta^{\prime}&0\\
0&1-\theta^{\prime}\end{bmatrix}\quad\text{ and }\quad\beta^{\prime}=\begin{bmatrix}\phi^{\prime}&0\\
0&1-\phi^{\prime}\end{bmatrix}.

It suffices to set
p=\frac{1}{2}=p^{\prime}
. In this case,
plugging in the matrix
\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\
0&0\end{smallmatrix}\right]\otimes\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\
0&0\end{smallmatrix}\right]
into

\mathscr{R}^{\mathrm{P},t}_{\alpha,{{\mathcal{E}}}}\otimes\mathscr{R}^{\mathrm{P},t^{\prime}}_{\alpha^{\prime},{{\mathcal{E}}}^{\prime}}

gives

\left(\mathscr{R}^{\mathrm{P},t}_{\alpha,{{\mathcal{E}}}}\otimes\mathscr{R}^{\mathrm{P},t^{\prime}}_{\alpha^{\prime},{{\mathcal{E}}}^{\prime}}\right)\left(\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}\otimes\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\

where we temporarily introduced the notation

for
r\in{{\mathbb{R}}}
.
Therefore,

\left(\mathscr{R}_{\alpha,{{\mathcal{E}}}}\otimes\mathscr{R}_{\alpha^{\prime},{{\mathcal{E}}}^{\prime}}\right)\left(\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}\otimes\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}\right)=\sqrt{\theta\theta^{\perp}\theta^{\prime}\theta^{\prime\perp}}\cos\left(\ln\left(\frac{\theta^{\perp}}{\theta}\right)t\right)\cos\left(\ln\left(\frac{\theta^{\prime\perp}}{\theta^{\prime}}\right)t\right)\sigma_{x}\otimes\sigma_{x}\;,

while

These two results are manifestly different since for most values of

and
\theta^{\prime}
the term involving

Hence,
\mathscr{R}_{\alpha,{{\mathcal{E}}}}\otimes\mathscr{R}_{\alpha^{\prime},{{\mathcal{E}}}^{\prime}}\neq\mathscr{R}_{\alpha\otimes\alpha^{\prime},{{\mathcal{E}}}\otimes{{\mathcal{E}}}^{\prime}}

is not tensorial.

In this appendix, we prove Proposition

\begin{split}\mathscr{R}^{\mathrm{JRSWW}}_{\alpha\otimes\alpha^{\prime},{{\mathcal{E}}}\otimes{{\mathcal{E}}}^{\prime}}\left(\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}\otimes\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}\right)=\frac{\pi\sqrt{\theta\theta^{\perp}\theta^{\prime}\theta^{\prime\perp}}}{2}&\bigg[\left(\frac{1}{\pi}+\frac{1}{\cosh(\pi)\sinh(\pi)}\right)\sigma_{x}\otimes\sigma_{x}\\
&+\left(\frac{1}{\pi}-\frac{1}{\cosh(\pi)\sinh(\pi)}\right)\sigma_{y}\otimes\sigma_{y}\bigg],\end{split}

which are not equal to each other. This proves that the JRSWW map is not tensorial.
