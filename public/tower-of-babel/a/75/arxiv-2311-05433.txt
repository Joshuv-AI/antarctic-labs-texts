Dimension growth for affine varieties
Floris Vermeulen

Abstract
. We prove uniform upper bounds on the number of integral points of bounded height on affine varieties. If X X is an irreducible affine variety of degree d ≥ 4 d\geq 4 in 𝔸 n \mathbb{A}^{n} which is not the preimage of a curve under a linear map 𝔸 n → 𝔸 n − dim X + 1 \mathbb{A}^{n}\to\mathbb{A}^{n-\dim X+1} , then we prove that X X has at most O d , n , ε ​ ( B dim X − 1 + ε ) O_{d,n,\varepsilon}(B^{\dim X-1+\varepsilon}) integral points up to height B B . This is a strong analogue of dimension growth for projective varieties, and improves upon a theorem due to Pila, and a theorem due to Browning–Heath-Brown–Salberger. Our techniques follow the p p -adic determinant method, in the spirit of Heath-Brown, but with improvements due to Salberger, Walsh, and Castryck–Cluckers–Dittmann–Nguyen. The main difficulty is to count integral points on lines on an affine surface in 𝔸 3 \mathbb{A}^{3} , for which we develop point-counting results for curves in ℙ 1 × ℙ 1 \mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1} . We also formulate and prove analogous results over global fields, following work by Paredes–Sasyk.



1.
Introduction

If
x\in\mathbb{P}^{n}(\mathbb{Q})
then we may write
x
as
x=(x_{0}:\ldots:x_{n})
where the
x_{i}
are in
\mathbb{Z}
and coprime. Recall that the height of
x
is defined as
H(x)=\max\{|x_{0}|,\ldots,|x_{n}|\}
. If
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
, and
B
is a positive integer, then we denote by
N(X,B)
the number of points on
X\cap\mathbb{P}^{n}(\mathbb{Q})
whose height is bounded by
B
. If
X\subset\mathbb{A}^{n}
, and
B
is a positive integer then we denote by
N_{\mathrm{aff}}(X,B)
the number of points in
[-B,B]^{n}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}
which lie on
X
. This work is concerned with uniform bounds on the quantities
N(X,B)
and
N_{\mathrm{aff}}(X,B)
, for algebraic varieties
X
.

Since the work of Bombieri–Pila
[
1
]
, the determinant method has been a highly influential tool to bound rational points on various geometric objects. This method is especially well-suited to proving uniform upper bounds on rational points on algebraic varieties. For example, Bombieri and Pila prove that if
C\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is an irreducible algebraic curve of degree
d
, then the number of points
(x,y)\in[-B,B]^{2}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}
which lie on
C
is bounded by
O_{d,\varepsilon}(B^{1/d+\varepsilon})
. We stress that the constant here depends only on
d
and
\varepsilon
, and not on
C
itself. By induction on dimension, Pila
[
16
]
showed that if
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is an irreducible algebraic variety of dimension
m
and of degree
d
, then
N_{\mathrm{aff}}(X,B)
is bounded by
O_{d,n,\varepsilon}(B^{m-1+1/d+\varepsilon})
. In the projective setting, the first results were obtained by Heath-Brown
[
12
]
, who developed a
p
-adic analogue of the determinant method. He proved that if
C\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}
is an irreducible projective curve of degree
d
, then the number of points on
C(\mathbb{Q})
of height at most
B
is bounded by
O_{d,\varepsilon}(B^{2/d+\varepsilon})
. Moreover, using this
p
-adic determinant method, Heath-Brown was able to prove that an irreducible projective surface of degree
d\geq 2
has at most
O_{d,\varepsilon}(B^{2+\varepsilon})
points of height at most
B
. In higher dimensions, this led to the
dimension growth conjecture
, a non-uniform version of which was also conjectured by Serre
[
22
]
, which states that if
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
is an irreducible projective variety of degree
d\geq 2
, then

N(X,B)\ll_{d,n,\varepsilon}B^{\dim X+\varepsilon}.

For
d\geq 6
, this conjecture was proven by Browning–Heath-Brown–Salberger
[
5
]
. Their methods are based on an analogue for affine varieties, which is well-suited to induction on dimension. By developing a global
p
-adic determinant method, Salberger
[
20
]
was further able to prove the conjecture when
d\geq 4
, and via different methods in a non-uniform way when
d=3
. For
d=2
, dimension growth is also known by work of Heath-Brown
[
12
]
. Based on an improvement by Walsh
[
25
]
of Heath-Brown’s result for projective curves, Castryck–Cluckers–Dittmann–Nguyen
[
7
]
were able to obtain dimension growth results which are moreover polynomial in
d
, and without
\varepsilon
. The possibility of polynomial dependence on the degree also appears in work by Walkowiak
[
24
]
for projective curves, but with logarithmic factors and a worse exponent than in
[
7
]
.

This article is concerned with a strong version of dimension growth for affine varieties. If
X\subset\mathbb{A}^{n}
is an affine hypersurface of degree
d
, then Pila’s bound
[
16
]

N_{\mathrm{aff}}(X,B)\ll_{d,n,\varepsilon}B^{n-2+1/d+\varepsilon}

is essentially optimal, as the example
x_{2}=x_{1}^{d}
shows. On the other hand, if the intersection of
X
with the hyperplane at infinity is geometrically integral, then one may remove the
1/d
in the exponent, by work of Browning–Heath-Brown–Salberger
[
5
]
. We weaken this condition significantly, and our main result strengthens Pila’s theorem, while generalizing the result by Browning–Heath-Brown–Salberger.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{A}^{n}
be an affine variety of dimension
m
. We say that
X
is
cylindrical over a curve
if there exists a
\mathbb{Q}
-linear map
\pi:\mathbb{A}^{n}\to\mathbb{A}^{n-m+1}
such that
\pi(X)
is a curve.

If
X
is a hypersurface in
\mathbb{A}^{n}
defined by a polynomial
f\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
, then
X
is cylindrical over a curve if and only if there exist linear forms
\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\in\mathbb{Q}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
and a polynomial
g\in\mathbb{Q}[y_{1},y_{2}]
such that
f(x)=g(\ell_{1}(x),\ell_{2}(x))
. Geometrically, one may reformulate
X
being cylindrical over a curve as
X
being a cone whose cone point is an
(n-2)
-dimensional linear space at infinity.

Our main result is then as follows.

Theorem 1.2

(Affine Dimension Growth)
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{A}^{n}
be an irreducible variety of dimension
m
and of degree
d
defined over
\mathbb{Q}
which is not cylindrical over a curve. Then for any positive integer
B

\displaystyle N_{\mathrm{aff}}(X,B)\ll_{d,n,\varepsilon}B^{m-1+\varepsilon},

\displaystyle\quad\text{ if }d\geq 4,

\displaystyle N_{\mathrm{aff}}(X,B)\ll_{n,\varepsilon}B^{m-2+\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}+\varepsilon},

\displaystyle\quad\text{ if }d=3.

For
d\geq 4
this bound is essentially optimal. Indeed, as soon as
X
contains a linear space of maximal dimension defined over
\mathbb{Q}
, we have that
N(X,B)\gg B^{m-1}
. However, it seems reasonable to expect that one may remove the factor
B^{\varepsilon}
, at the cost of a logarithmic factor. Also, the dependence on
d
in these bounds is likely polynomial, as in
[
7
]
. In fact, our methods almost give such improvements, and there is only one very specific case where a factor
B^{\varepsilon}
and the non-explicit dependence on
d
appear. In
[
8
]
, we prove a version of this result which is polynomial in
d
, but with some technical assumptions on
f_{d}
. Let us mention that Salberger
[
18
]
has recently also obtained a weaker version of this theorem, namely with the extra condition that the intersection of
X
with the hyperplane at infinity has an absolutely irreducible component of degree at least
2
. Salberger also studies the case
d=2
via different methods based on work by Heath-Brown
[
11
]
.

Of course, if
X
is cylindrical over a curve, then bounding
N_{\mathrm{aff}}(X,B)
reduces to counting integral points on a curve. Indeed, if
\pi:\mathbb{A}^{n}\to\mathbb{A}^{n-m+1}
is a linear map for which
\pi(X)
is a curve, then

N_{\mathrm{aff}}(X,B)\ll B^{m-1}N_{\mathrm{aff}}(\pi(X),B).

In positive characteristic, analogues of the Bombieri–Pila bound were obtained by Sedunova
[
21
]
, and Cluckers–Forey–Loeser
[
9
]
in large characteristic. In higher dimensions, the first results were obtained by the author
[
23
]
, who proved dimension growth for hypersurfaces over
\mathbb{F}_{q}(t)
of large degree. These methods were subsequently generalized to all global fields and all degree by Paredes–Sasyk
[
15
]
. The results of this paper also naturally adapt to the setting of global fields, in particular also in positive characteristic. In Section
6
we explain how to generalize Theorem
1.2
to any global field, see Theorem
6.1
.

Proof strategy

Let us briefly discuss the proof strategy of Theorem
1.2
. One first reduces to hypersurfaces via a projection argument, and then by cutting with hyperplanes to the case of an affine surface
X
in
\mathbb{A}^{3}
. The most difficult case is now if
X
is ruled, that is,
X
is the union of the lines contained in it. Indeed, if
X
contains only finitely many lines, then Theorem
1.2
follows already from the work of Salberger
[
20
]
. Denote by
X_{\infty}\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}
the intersection of
X
with the plane
\mathbb{P}^{2}
at infinity of
\mathbb{A}^{3}
. If
X_{\infty}
does not contain any linear irreducible components, then an adaptation of work by Browning–Heath-Brown–Salberger
[
5
]
gives the result also in this case, see e.g. the recent
[
18
]
. Therefore, the most difficult case is when
X\subset\mathbb{A}^{3}
is an irreducible ruled surface for which
X_{\infty}
contains a (rational) line. To obtain a good count on integral points in this case, we prove a uniform upper bound for counting integral points on curves in
\mathbb{A}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}
.

Theorem 1.3

(Theorem
3.2
)
.

Let
f(x,t)\in\mathbb{Z}[x,t]
be irreducible with
\deg_{x}f=d_{1},\deg_{t}f=d_{2}
. Then for any positive integers
B_{1},B_{2}\geq 2
, the number of
(x,t)\in\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Q}
with
|x|\leq B_{1},H(t)\leq B_{2}
and
f(x,t)=0
is bounded by

\ll(d_{1}d_{2})^{\frac{9}{2}}B_{1}^{\frac{1}{2d_{2}}}B_{2}^{\frac{1}{d_{1}}}\log(B_{1}B_{2}).

We prove this result in Section
3
via the determinant method, in the spirit of Heath-Brown
[
12
]
, but with the improvements from Walsh
[
25
]
and Castryck–Cluckers–Dittmann–Nguyen
[
7
]
. In Section
4
we then prove Theorem
1.2
for surfaces in
\mathbb{A}^{3}
. Finally, we finish the proof in Section
5
via the aforementioned projection and slicing arguments.

Notation

We use the notation
a=O_{c}(b)
or
a\ll_{c}b
to mean that there exists a constant
\alpha>0
depending on
c
such that
|a|\leq\alpha|b|
. For functions
f,g:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}
we use the notation
f=o(g)
to mean that
\lim f/g=0
.

If
f
is a polynomial, then we denote by
V(f)
the variety defined by the vanishing of
f
. Depending on context, this might be an affine or a projective variety.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section we recall some results about auxiliary polynomials and on bounding rational and integral points on curves.

For many results about irreducible polynomials or varieties, it is typically enough to work with the absolutely irreducible case.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
X
be a projective or affine variety defined over
\mathbb{Q}
, and of degree
d
. If
X
is irreducible but not absolutely irreducible, then there exists a codimension
1
subvariety
Y\subset X
defined over
\mathbb{Q}
of degree at most
d^{2}
such that
X(\mathbb{Q})\subset Y(\mathbb{Q})
.

Proof.

This is established in the first paragraph of
[
25
, Sec. 4]
.
∎

If
f
is a polynomial over
\mathbb{Z}
then we denote by
||f||
the maximum of the absolute values of the coefficients of
f
. The following result by Heath-Brown allows us to replace factors
\log||f||
and
||f||^{\varepsilon}
by factors of the form
O_{d,n}(\log B)
and
O_{d,n,\varepsilon}(B^{\varepsilon})
.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
f\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
be primitive, and absolutely irreducible of degree
d
, and let
B
be a positive integer. Then either
||f||
is bounded by
O_{n}\left(B^{d\binom{d+n}{n}}\right)
, or there exists a polynomial
g\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
of degree at most
d
, not divisible by
f
and vanishing at all integral points of
V(f)
of height at most
B
.

Proof.

This is
[
12
, Thm. 4]
.
∎

We recall the following trivial bound on counting integral points on affine varieties, which is also called the Schwartz–Zippel bound.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{A}^{n}
be a (possibly reducible) affine variety of degree
d
and dimension
m
. Then

N_{\mathrm{aff}}(X,B)\leq d(2B+1)^{m}.

Proof.

See e.g.
[
12
, Sec.  2]
or
[
4
, Thm. 1]
.
∎

3.
Rational points on curves in
\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}

The goal of this section is to establish uniform upper bounds on counting rational points on curves in
\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}
and points in
\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Q}
on curves in
\mathbb{A}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}
. The main results are Theorems
3.1
and
3.2
.

(3.1).
Geometry of
\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}

We recall some geometry of
\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}
. We work over any field
k
. The Picard group of
\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}
is freely generated by the classes
L_{1}=[\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\{0\}]
and
L_{2}=[\{0\}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}]
with intersection product

L_{1}^{2}=L_{2}^{2}=0,\quad L_{1}L_{2}=1.

Hence any curve
C
in
\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}
is linearly equivalent to
d_{1}L_{1}+d_{2}L_{2}
for some integers
d_{1},d_{2}\geq 0
. We call
(d_{1},d_{2})
the
bidegree
of
C
. Equivalently,
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
are the degrees of the maps
C\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
induced by the two coordinate projections
\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
. If one of
d_{1}
or
d_{2}
is zero, say
d_{2}=0
, then
C
is a union of lines of the form
\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\{a_{i}\}
. Therefore, we will always assume that both
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
are non-zero. If
C
is geometrically irreducible, then it is of arithmetic genus
(d_{1}-1)(d_{2}-1)
, e.g. by the adjunction formula. We define
|d|=d_{1}d_{2}
. If
f\in k[X,Y,U,V]
is homogeneous in
X,Y
of degree
d_{1}
, and homogeneous in
U,V
of degree
d_{2}
, then
f=0
defines a curve in
\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}
of bidegree
(d_{1},d_{2})
.

Now assume that
k=\mathbb{Q}
, or more generally any global field as explained in Section
6
. If
P=((x:y),(u:v))\in\mathbb{P}^{1}(\mathbb{Q})\times\mathbb{P}^{1}(\mathbb{Q})
then we define the
biheight of
P
to be

H(P)=(H(x:y),H(u:v))\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

If
C
is a curve in
\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}
defined over
\mathbb{Q}
, and if
B_{1},B_{2}
are positive integers, then we define the counting function

N(C;B_{1},B_{2})=\#\{((x:y),(u:v))\in C(\mathbb{Q})\mid H(x:y)\leq B_{1},H(u:v)\leq B_{2}\}.

If
f\in\mathbb{Z}[X,Y,U,V]
is a bihomogeneous polynomial as above, then we also write
N(f;B_{1},B_{2})
to mean
N(V(f);B_{1},B_{2})
. Note that by dehomogenizing, bounding
N(C;B_{1},B_{2})
is essentially equivalent to counting rational points on an affine curve in
\mathbb{A}^{2}
. Recall that if
f
is a polynomial over
\mathbb{Z}
, then
||f||
denotes the maximum of the absolute values of the coefficients of
f
.

(3.2).
Main theorems

The goal of this section is to prove two uniform upper bounds for counting rational points on curves in
\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}
. The first result is about bounding
N(C;B_{1},B_{2})
. Recall that if
d=(d_{1},d_{2})
then
|d|=d_{1}d_{2}
.

Theorem 3.1

(Curves in
\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}
)
.

Let
f\in\mathbb{Z}[X,Y,U,V]
be primitive irreducible and bihomogeneous of bidegree
d=(d_{1},d_{2})
. For any positive integers
B_{1},B_{2}
we have that

\displaystyle N(f;B_{1},B_{2})

\displaystyle\ll|d|^{\frac{7}{2}}B_{1}^{\frac{1}{d_{2}}}B_{2}^{\frac{1}{d_{1}}}\frac{b(f)}{||f||^{\frac{1}{2|d|}}}+|d|^{3}\log\left(B_{1}^{\frac{1}{d_{2}}}B_{2}^{\frac{1}{d_{1}}}\right)+|d|^{\frac{7}{2}}.

The quantity
b(f)
is related to absolute irreducibility of
f
modulo primes, and will be defined below. It always satisfies

b(f)\ll\max\{|d|^{-1}\log||f||,1\}^{2}.

In particular, in the situation of the above theorem we have that

N(f;B_{1},B_{2})\ll|d|^{\frac{7}{2}}B_{1}^{\frac{1}{d_{2}}}B_{2}^{\frac{1}{d_{1}}}.

The second result is a mixed point counting where we bound points on
C
of the form
((x:1),(u:v))
with
x,u,v\in\mathbb{Z}
and
|x|\leq B_{1},H(u:v)\leq B_{2}
.

Theorem 3.2

(Curves in
\mathbb{A}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}
)
.

Let
f\in\mathbb{Z}[X,U]
be primitive irreducible of bidegree
d=(d_{1},d_{2})
. Then the number of
(x,t)\in\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Q}
with
|x|\leq B_{1},H(t)\leq B_{2}
and such that
f(x,t)=0
is bounded by

\displaystyle\ll|d|^{\frac{9}{2}}B_{1}^{\frac{1}{2d_{2}}}B_{2}^{\frac{1}{d_{1}}}\log(B_{1}B_{2}).

To prove these results, we follow Salberger’s global determinant method
[
20
]
, while incorporating Walsh’
[
25
]
improvement to keep track of the height of
f
. We also crucially need our upper bounds to depend polynomially on the degree, for which we follow the treatment of Castryck, Cluckers, Dittmann and Nguyen
[
7
]
. This polynomial dependence on the degree will be needed in the next section to apply a technique due to Walkowiak
[
24
]
. As an alternative to Theorem
3.1
, one can instead try to prove an analogue of a result due to Broberg
[
3
, Cor. 1]
for counting points in lop-sided boxes on projective curves, but with moreover polynomial dependence on the degree. Such a result would also allow one to prove Theorem
1.2
.

The rest of this section is devoted to a proof of Theorems
3.1
and
3.2
.

(3.3).
A determinant estimate

We have the following determinant estimate by Salberger
[
20
]
, made independent of the degree by following
[
7
]
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
f\in\mathbb{Z}[X,Y,U,V]
be an irreducible bihomogeneous polynomial and let
C=V(f)
in
\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}
. Let
p
be a prime and let
C_{p}
the reduction of
C
modulo
p
. Let
P
be an
\mathbb{F}_{p}
-point of multiplicity
\mu
on
C_{p}
and let
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{s}
be
\mathbb{Z}
-points on
C
with reduction
P
. Let
F_{1},\ldots,F_{s}\in\mathbb{Z}[X,Y,U,V]
be bihomogeneous polynomials over
\mathbb{Z}
. Then
\det(F_{i}(\xi_{j}))_{i,j}
is divisible by
p^{e}
with

e\geq\frac{1}{2\mu}s^{2}-O(s).

Proof.

This is essentially
[
19
, Lem. 2.5]
, as stated in
[
7
, Cor. 2.5]
without the dependence on the degree of
f
. Indeed, note that the proof is local, and so it does not matter whether
C
is a curve in
\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}
, or in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
p
be a prime number. Let
f\in\mathbb{Z}[X,Y,U,V]
be an irreducible bihomogeneous polynomial, let
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{s}
be
\mathbb{Z}
-points on
V(f)
, and let
F_{1},\ldots,F_{s}\in\mathbb{Z}[X,Y,U,V]
be bihomogeneous polynomials. Then
\det(F_{i}(\xi_{j}))_{i,j}
is divisible by
p^{e}
with

e\geq\frac{1}{2}\frac{s^{2}}{n_{p}}-O(s),

where
n_{p}
is the number of
\mathbb{F}_{p}
-points on
C_{p}
, counted with multiplicity.

Proof.

The proof is identical to
[
20
, Lem. 1.4]
. The only difference is that the bound is independent of the degree of
f
, as follows from Lemma
3.3
.
∎

Since we are dealing with curves, bounding
n_{p}
is straightforward.

Lemma 3.5
.

Assume that
C_{p}
is geometrically integral. Then the determinant from the previous lemma is divisible by
p^{e}
with

e\geq\frac{1}{2}\frac{s^{2}}{p+O(|d|\sqrt{p})}-O(s).

Proof.

The curve
C_{p}
is geometrically integral and of genus at most
(d_{1}-1)(d_{2}-1)
. Hence the Weil bound gives that

n_{p}\leq p+1+2(d_{1}-1)(d_{2}-1)\sqrt{p}\leq p+O(|d|\sqrt{p}).

Now use the previous lemma.
∎

If
f\in\mathbb{Z}[X,Y,U,V]
is a polynomial of bidegree
(d_{1},d_{2})
then we define
b(f)=0
if
f
is not absolutely irreducible, and

b(f)=\prod_{p}\exp\left(\frac{\log p}{p}\right),

where the product is over all primes
p>|d|^{2}
such that
f\bmod p
is not absolutely irreducible. Note that our definition differs slightly from
[
7
]
, the reason being that we are only interested in counting points on curves, for which the Weil bound is much stronger than other higher-dimensional effective bounds such as those by Cafure–Matera
[
6
]
.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
f\in\mathbb{Z}[X,Y,U,V]
be primitive, and absolutely irreducible of bidegree
d=(d_{1},d_{2})
. Then

b(f)\ll\max\left\{\frac{\log||f||}{|d|},1\right\}^{2}.

Remark 3.7
.

One can likely remove the exponent
2
in this bound, by using some strong form of effective Noether polynomials in
\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}
as in
[
13
,
17
]
. We will not need such an improvement however.

Proof.

Let
g_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}[X,U,Z]
be the homogenization of
f(X,1,U,1)
with respect to one variable
Z
, and similarly let
g_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}[Y,V,Z]
be the homogenization of
f(1,Y,1,V)
with respect to
Z
. Then both
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
define curves
C_{1},C_{2}
in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
which are birational to
V(f)\subset\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}
of degrees
e_{1},e_{2}
with
\max\{d_{1},d_{2}\}\leq e_{1},e_{2}\leq d_{1}+d_{2}
. Note that
||g_{1}||=||g_{2}||=||f||
. If
p
is a prime such that both
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
are absolutely irreducible modulo
p
, then so is
f
modulo
p
. Hence
b(f)\leq b(g_{1})b(g_{2})
. We now use
[
7
, Cor. 3.2.3]
to bound
b(g_{1})
and
b(g_{2})
, and conclude that
b(f)
is bounded as stated.
∎

The following is the main determinant estimate, and is the analogue of
[
25
, Thm. 2.3]
.

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
f\in\mathbb{Z}[X,Y,U,V]
be bihomogeneous and absolutely irreducible, defining a geometrically irreducible curve
C
in
\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}
. Let
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{s}
be in
C(\mathbb{Q})
, let
F_{\ell i}\in\mathbb{Z}[X,Y,U,V]
be bihomogeneous,
1\leq\ell\leq L,1\leq i\leq s
and denote by
\Delta_{\ell}
the determinant of
(F_{i\ell}(\xi_{j}))_{i,j}
. Let
\Delta
be the greatest common divisor of all the
\Delta_{\ell}
. If
\Delta
is non-zero, then

\log|\Delta|\geq\frac{s^{2}}{2}(\log s-O(1)-2\log|d|-\log b(f)).

Proof.

Let
P
be the set of primes
p
such that either
p\leq|d|^{2}
or
f
is not absolutely irreducible modulo
p
. Applying Lemma
3.5
to all primes
p\leq s
which are not in
P
yields that

\log|\Delta|\geq\frac{s^{2}}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq s\\
p\notin P\end{subarray}}\frac{\log p}{p+O(|d|\sqrt{p})}-O(s)\sum_{p\leq s}\log p.

The last term is bounded by
O(s^{2})
, so we focus on the first term.

For this, we recall the following bounds

\sum_{p\leq n}\frac{\log p}{p}=\log n+O(1),\quad\sum_{p\geq n}\frac{\log p}{p^{3/2}}=O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right),

the first of which is Mertens’ first theorem, while the second follows from the prime number theorem. Now, using that
\frac{1}{p+O(|d|\sqrt{p})}\geq\frac{1}{p}-\frac{O(|d|)}{p^{3/2}}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq s\\
p\notin P\end{subarray}}\frac{\log p}{p+O(|d|\sqrt{p})}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{p\leq s}\frac{\log p}{p}-\sum_{p\in P}\frac{\log p}{p}-O(|d|)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq s\\
p\notin P\end{subarray}}\frac{\log p}{p^{3/2}}

\displaystyle\geq\log s-\sum_{p\leq|d|^{2}}\frac{\log p}{p}-\log b(f)-O(1)-O\left(|d|\sum_{p>|d|^{2}}\frac{\log p}{p^{3/2}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\log s-2\log|d|-\log b(f)-O(1),

as desired.
∎

Proof of Theorem

and Theorem

To prove Theorem

we will need a coordinate transformation to ensure that the coefficient of

of

is large compared to the height of

. If

is bihomogeneous of bidegree
(d_{1},d_{2})
, denote by

the coefficient of

of

.

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
f\in\mathbb{Z}[X,Y,U,V]
be primitive and bihomogeneous of bidegree

. Then there exists a primitive polynomial
f^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}[X,Y,U,V]
which is bihomogeneous of bidegree
(d_{1},d_{2})
with the following properties:

(1)

||f^{\prime}||\leq d_{1}^{d_{1}}d_{2}^{d_{2}}(d_{1}+1)(d_{2}+1)||f||
,

(2)

|c_{f^{\prime}}|\geq\frac{||f^{\prime}||}{3^{d_{1}+d_{2}}d_{1}^{d_{1}}d_{2}^{d_{2}}(d_{1}+1)(d_{2}+1)}
, and

(3)

for any positive integers

, we have
N(f;B_{1},B_{2})\leq N(f^{\prime};d_{1}B_{1},d_{2}B_{2})
.

Proof.

Consider the dehomogenization
f(1,Y,1,V)\in\mathbb{Z}[Y,V]
. By applying
[

, Lem. 3.4.1]
twice, we find integers

with
|a|\leq d_{1},|b|\leq d_{2}
such that
|f(1,a,1,b)|\geq||f||/3^{d_{1}+d_{2}}
. We define

which is indeed primitive and bihomogeneous of bidegree

. Using the boundedness of

By construction, the coefficient of

of

.
∎

.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

We will first prove the theorem when

is large compared to
||f||
. Afterwards we will deduce the theorem in general by using Lemma
3.9
. So assume for now that
|c_{f}|\geq||f||/C_{d}
, where

(1)

C_{d}=3^{d_{1}+d_{2}}d_{1}^{d_{1}}d_{2}^{d_{2}}(d_{1}+1)(d_{2}+1).

Also, by Proposition
2.1
, we may assume that
f
is absolutely irreducible.

Let
S\subset\mathbb{P}^{1}(\mathbb{Q})\times\mathbb{P}^{1}(\mathbb{Q})
be the set of rational points on

of biheight at most
B=(B_{1},B_{2})
. We will show that there exists some auxiliary polynomial
g\in\mathbb{Z}[X,Y,U,V]
not divisible by

(d_{1}M,d_{2}M)
for some integer

of size

(2)

M\ll|d|^{3/2}B_{1}^{\frac{1}{d_{2}}}B_{2}^{\frac{1}{d_{1}}}\frac{b(f)}{||f||^{\frac{1}{2|d|}}}+|d|\log\left(B_{1}^{\frac{1}{d_{2}}}B_{2}^{\frac{1}{d_{1}}}\right)+|d|^{3/2},

and such that

vanishes on all points of

of biheight at most

with
V(g)
inside
\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}
.

Towards a contradiction, let
M
be a positive integer and assume that there is no auxiliary polynomial

of bidegree

as above. We show that

is bounded by the quantity from equation (
2

.

Given integers
D=(D_{1},D_{2})

\mathcal{B}[D]
for the set of bihomogeneous monomials of bidegree
D
. So
|\mathcal{B}[D]|=(D_{1}+1)(D_{2}+1)
. Let
\Xi\subset S
be a maximal subset which is algebraically independent over bihomogeneous monomials of bidegree

\mathcal{B}[dM]
to
\Xi
gives
s=|\Xi|
linearly independent vectors. Let
A
be the
s\times r
matrix whose rows are these vectors, where
r=|\mathcal{B}[dM]|=(d_{1}M+1)(d_{2}M+1)
. Note that each entry of
A
is bounded in absolute value by
B_{1}^{d_{1}M}B_{2}^{d_{2}M}
. By assumption, every polynomial of bidegree
dM
vanishing on
\Xi
is in
f\cdot\mathcal{B}[d_{1}M-d_{1},d_{2}M-d_{2}]
, so that

\displaystyle s=|\mathcal{B}[dM]|-|\mathcal{B}[d_{1}(M-1),d_{2}(M-1)]|=2|d|(M-1)+d_{1}+d_{2}.

Let
\Delta
be the greatest common divisor of all

minors of

. Since every polynomial in
f\cdot\mathcal{B}[d_{1}M-d_{1},d_{2}M-d_{2}]
has a coefficient of size at least
c_{f}\geq||f||/C_{d}

[
2
]
yields that

\Delta\leq\sqrt{|\det(AA^{T})|}\left(||f||/C_{d}\right)^{s-r}.

Now we bound
|\det(AA^{T})|
simply by
s!(rB_{1}^{2d_{1}M}B_{2}^{2d_{2}M})^{s}
and use the determinant estimate from Proposition
3.8
to find that

(3)

\displaystyle\frac{s^{2}}{2}

\displaystyle(\log s-O(1)-2\log|d|-\log b(f))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\log s!}{2}+\frac{s}{2}\log r+sd_{1}M\log B_{1}+sd_{2}M\log B_{2}-(r-s)(\log||f||-\log C_{d})).

By Stirling’s approximation we have that
\log(s!)=s\log(s)-s+O(\log s)
, and hence we may neglect the term
\log(s!)/2
by adjusting the
O(1)
on the left-hand-side. Similarly, since
r=(d_{1}M+1)(d_{2}M+1)
we may also absorb the term
\frac{s}{2}\log r
by adjusting the
O(1)
on the left-hand-side. Now divide by
Ms
in equation (
3
) to get that

\displaystyle\frac{s}{2M}(\log s-O(1)-2\log|d|-\log b(f))

\displaystyle\leq d_{1}\log B_{1}+d_{2}\log B_{2}-\frac{r-s}{Ms}(\log||f||-\log C_{d}).

We first focus on the left-hand-side, for which we have that

\frac{s}{M}=2|d|+O\left(\frac{|d|}{M}\right),

and that

\log s=\log|d|+\log M-O(1).

Hence the left-hand-side becomes

|d|(\log M-O(1)-\log|d|-(1+O(1/M))\log b(f)).

For the right-hand-side, by using that
M\gg|d|
we have that

\frac{r-s}{Ms}=\frac{|d|M^{2}+O(|d|M)}{2|d|M^{2}+O(|d|M)}=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1+O(1/M)}{1+O(1/M)}=\frac{1}{2}+O\left(\frac{1}{M}\right).

Putting this all together, we have that

(4)

\displaystyle|d|(\log M-O(1)-\log|d|-(1+O(1/M))\log b(f))

\displaystyle\leq d_{1}\log B_{1}+d_{2}\log B_{2}-\frac{\log||f||}{2}+O\left(\frac{\log||f||}{M}\right)+\frac{\log C_{d}}{2}+O\left(\frac{\log C_{d}}{M}\right).

We remove the last two terms on the right-hand-side as follows. We compute, using equation (
1
), that

\displaystyle\log C_{d}

\displaystyle=(d_{1}+d_{2})2\log 3+d_{1}\log d_{1}+d_{2}\log d_{2}+\log(d_{1}+1)+\log(d_{2}+1)

\displaystyle\leq|d|\log|d|+O(|d|).

So we can remove the term
\log(C_{d})/2
in equation (
4
), at the cost of replacing the term
-\log|d|
on the left-hand-side by
\frac{-3}{2}\log|d|
. Similarly, since we have assumed that
M\gg|d|
, we may absorb the term
O(\log(C_{d})/M)
in the
O(1)
on the left-hand-side. We end up with

(5)

\displaystyle|d|\left(\log M-O(1)-\frac{3}{2}\log|d|-(1+O(1/M))\log b(f)\right)

\displaystyle\leq d_{1}\log B_{1}+d_{2}\log B_{2}-\frac{\log||f||}{2}+O\left(\frac{\log||f||}{M}\right).

We now distinguish two cases according to
||f||
. Assume first that
||f||\leq B_{1}^{8d_{1}}B_{2}^{8d_{2}}
and
M\geq\log(B_{1}^{1/d_{2}}B_{2}^{1/d_{1}})
. Then we have that

\frac{\log||f||}{M}\ll|d|,

and so we can absorb the term
O(\log(||f||)/M)
in the
O(1)
on the left-hand-side in equation (
5
). Also, by using Lemma
3.6
we can remove the term
O(\log(b(f))/M)
by adjusting the
O(1)
on the left-hand-side. Rearranging, we have achieved that

M\leq|d|^{3/2}B_{1}^{1/d_{2}}B_{2}^{1/d_{1}}\frac{b(f)}{||f||^{1/2|d|}},

which is as desired.

Secondly, assume that
||f||>B_{1}^{8d_{1}}B_{2}^{8d_{2}}

such that the term

on the right-hand-side of equation (

O\left(\frac{\log||f||}{M}\right)\leq\frac{\log||f||}{4}.

Rearranging then yields that

We now use the fact that for any

see e.g.

\displaystyle 2\log b(f)-\frac{\log||f||}{8|d|}

\displaystyle\leq\max\{4\log\log||f||-4\log|d|,0\}-\frac{\log||f||}{8|d|}+O(1)

\displaystyle\leq\max\{\log|d|-4\log|d|+O(1),0\}+O(1)\ll 1.

Hence we have that

3.2

in the range

, which exists by Bertrand’s postulate, and consider

. Let us first assume that

, so that

is primitive. We apply Theorem
3.1
to

Now,

agrees with

\displaystyle\ll|d|^{7/2}B_{1}^{\frac{1}{2d_{2}}}B_{2}^{\frac{1}{d_{1}}}\frac{\log||f_{d_{1}}||+d_{1}\log B_{1}}{||f_{d_{1}}||^{\frac{1}{2|d|}}}+|d|^{3}\log(B_{1}^{\frac{1}{2d_{2}}}B_{2}^{\frac{1}{d_{1}}})+d^{\frac{7}{2}}

4.
Lines on affine surfaces

The main new ingredient for Theorem
1.2
is a more precise count of integral points on lines on affine surfaces, improving
[
5
, Prop. 1]
and
[
7
, Prop. 4.3.3]
. These will typically be the main contribution to
N_{\mathrm{aff}}(X,B)
. The goal of this section is to give strong bounds on this count, assuming that our surface is not cylindrical over a curve. By a line we mean a line defined over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{A}^{3}
be an irreducible affine surface of degree
d
which is not cylindrical over a curve. Let
I
be a finite set of lines on
X
and denote by
Y
the union of all lines in
I
. Then

N_{\mathrm{aff}}(Y,B)\ll_{d,\varepsilon}B^{1+\varepsilon}+\#I.

(4.1).
An auxiliary lemma

The following lemma will be crucial for counting integral points on lines on a surface.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
F(X,T)
be a primitive irreducible polynomial in
\mathbb{Z}[X,T]
of bidegree
d=(d_{1},d_{2})
with
d_{1},d_{2}\geq 1
. Then

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|x|\leq B\\
F(x,t)=0\\
x\in\mathbb{Z},t\in\mathbb{Q}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{H(t)}\ll_{d,\varepsilon}||F||^{\varepsilon}B^{\varepsilon}.

To prove this lemma we reason as follows. Fix a positive integer
B
and for a positive integer
i
let
n_{i}
be the number of
t\in\mathbb{Q}
of height exactly
i
such that the polynomial
F(X,t)
has a root in
\mathbb{Z}
. If
(x,t)\in\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Q}
is such that
F(x,t)=0
, then the bound
|x|\leq B
also implies a bound of the form
O_{|d|}(||F||B^{|d|})
on
H(t)
. Hence the sum we are trying to bound is bounded by

\sum_{i=1}^{O_{|d|}(||F||B^{|d|})}\frac{n_{i}}{i}.

Therefore, to prove Lemma
4.2
we need good bounds on
n_{i}
. We will prove that on average
n_{i}\ll_{d,\varepsilon}i^{\varepsilon}
. The following lemma is reminiscent of Walkowiak’s work on effective Hilbert irreducibility
[
24
]
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
F(X,T)
be a primitive irreducible polynomial in
\mathbb{Z}[X,T]
of bidegree
d=(d_{1},d_{2})
with
d_{1}\geq 2
. Then the number of
t\in\mathbb{Q}
with
H(t)\leq i
for which
F(X,t)
has a root in
\mathbb{Z}
is bounded by

\ll|d|^{\frac{21}{2}}i\log(i)\log(||F||).

Proof.

Write
F(X,T)=F_{d_{1}}(T)X^{d_{1}}+\ldots+F_{0}(T)
. The number of zeroes of
F_{0}(t)
is bounded by
d_{2}
, and so we can always assume below that
F_{0}(t)\neq 0
. If
(x,t/s)\in\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Q}
is a zero of
F
, where
t,s\in\mathbb{Z}
are coprime and
H(t:s)=i
, then the rational root theorem shows that

|x|\leq(d_{2}+1)||F||i^{d_{2}}.

We apply Theorem
3.2
to
F
with
B_{1}=(d_{2}+1)||F||i^{d_{2}}
and
B_{2}=i
to obtain that the number of
t\in\mathbb{Q}
with
H(t)\leq i
such that
F(X,t)
has a root in
\mathbb{Z}
is bounded by

|d|^{\frac{9}{2}}||F||^{\frac{1}{2d_{2}}}i^{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{d_{1}}}\log(||F||)\log i.

If
||F||\leq e^{e}
then the result follows, since
d_{1}\geq 2
. So we may assume that
||F||>e^{e}
. Also, if
d_{2}\geq\frac{\log||F||}{2\log\log||F||}
then

||F||^{\frac{1}{2d_{2}}}\ll\log||F||

and so the result follows too. Hence we may assume that
d_{2}\ll\frac{\log||F||}{\log\log||F||}
. We now use a technique due to Walkowiak
[
24
]
. Take

E=\left\lfloor|d|\frac{\log||F||}{\log\log||F||}\frac{\log i}{\log\log i}\right\rfloor+1

and define
G(X,T)=F(X+T^{E},T)
. Note that
\deg_{X}G=d_{1}
and
\deg_{T}G\in[d_{1}E,d_{1}E+d_{2}]
. If
(x,t)
is a zero of
G
in
\mathbb{Q}^{2}
then
(x+t^{E},t)
is a zero of
F
. If this zero of
F
is in
\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Q}
with

Remark 4.5

This is the only place in the proof of Theorem

where the factor

and the non-explicit dependence on

Proof.

Let

and

be the homogenizations of

and

of degree

and define the rational function

be the homogeneous resultant of

and

.

In this section we prove Lemma
4.1
. We need a little lemma about bounding the degree of a variety.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
be a variety pure of dimension
m
, cut out by equations of degree
d
. Then

\deg X\leq d^{n-m}.

Proof.

Let
f_{1},\ldots,f_{N}
be non-zero homogeneous polynomials of degree
d
such that
X=V(f_{1},\ldots,f_{N})
. We inductively define a sequence of polynomials
g_{1},g_{2},\ldots,g_{k}
, with
k=n-m
and subvarieties
V_{i}=V(g_{1},\ldots,g_{i})
for which

V_{1}\supset\ldots\supset V_{k}\supset X,

and such that
V_{i}
is of dimension
n-i
and has degree at most
d^{i}
. We start by putting
g_{1}=f_{1}
and
V_{1}=V(f_{1})
. Assume that
V_{i},g_{1},\ldots,g_{i}
have been constructed, for
i<k
. Let
\{U_{ij}\}_{j=1,\ldots,M}
be the irreducible components of
V_{i}
, and note that these all have dimension exactly
n-i
. Since
X
is of smaller dimension than
V_{i}
, for each
j
we can find a point
p_{ij}
in
U_{ij}
which is not in
X
. Since the
p_{ij}
are not in
X
, the system of equations

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}f_{1}(p_{i1})+\ldots+\lambda_{N}f_{N}(p_{i1})

\displaystyle\neq 0,

\displaystyle\vdots\quad\qquad\qquad

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}f_{1}(p_{iM})+\ldots+\lambda_{N}f_{N}(p_{iM})

\displaystyle\neq 0

has a solution
(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{N})
. Now put
g_{i+1}=\lambda_{1}f_{1}+\ldots\lambda_{N}f_{N}
and
V_{i+1}=V_{i}\cap V(g_{i+1})
.

We conclude by noting that the dimension of
V_{k}
is the dimension of
X
, and hence the degree of
X
is bounded by the degree of
V_{k}
, which is in turn bounded by
d^{k}=d^{n-m}
.
∎

The following lemma shows that a surface which is not cylindrical over a curve cannot contain too many parallel lines.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{A}^{3}
be an absolutely irreducible surface of degree
d
which is not cylindrical over a curve. Then for each
v\in\mathbb{P}^{2}
,
X
contains at most
d^{2}
lines with direction
v
.

Proof.

The fact that
X
is not cylindrical over a curve shows that the number of lines on
X
with direction
v
is finite. Indeed, if this were infinite then the union of all lines on
X
whose direction is
v
would be an algebraic subvariety of
X
of dimension
2
, hence equal to
X
by irreducibility. But then projecting
\mathbb{A}^{3}\to\mathbb{A}^{2}
along the direction of

maps
X
to a curve, contradicting the fact that
X
is not cylindrical over a curve.

Let
f\in\mathbb{Z}[x,y,z]
be an irreducible polynomial for which
V(f)=X
and for
a\in\mathbb{A}^{3},t\in\mathbb{Q}
consider the expansion

f(a+tv)=g_{d}(a,v)t^{d}+g_{d-1}(a,v)t^{d-1}+\ldots+g_{0}(a,v)

into powers of
t
. We consider the
g_{i}(a,v)
as polynomials in the variable
a
, and look at the variety in
\mathbb{A}^{3}
cut out by these equations. This variety is exactly the union of all lines on
X
with direction
v
, hence of dimension
1
(or empty). Therefore Lemma
4.6
bounds its degree by
d^{2}
.
∎

5.
Affine dimension growth

(5.1).
Affine surfaces

To prove Theorem
1.2
, we will induct on the dimension of our variety
X\subset\mathbb{A}^{n}
. The base case is for surfaces
X\subset\mathbb{A}^{3}
, and so the main result of this section is as follows.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{A}^{3}
be an irreducible surface of degree
d\geq 3
which is not cylindrical over a curve. Then

\displaystyle N_{\mathrm{aff}}(X,B)\ll_{d,\varepsilon}B^{1+\varepsilon},

\displaystyle\quad\text{ if }d\neq 3,

\displaystyle N_{\mathrm{aff}}(X,B)\ll_{\varepsilon}B^{\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}+\varepsilon},

\displaystyle\quad\text{ if }d=3.

Proof.

Let
f\in\mathbb{Z}[x,y,z]
be an irreducible polynomial of degree
d\geq 3
defining the surface
X\subset\mathbb{A}^{3}
. By
[
20
, Thm. 7.2]
there exists a collection of
O_{d}(B^{1/\sqrt{d}}\log(B))
geometrically integral curves
C_{i}
of degree at most
O(\sqrt{d})
such that

N_{\mathrm{aff}}(X_{\mathrm{ns}}\setminus\bigcup_{i}C_{i},B)\ll_{d,\varepsilon}B^{2/\sqrt{d}+\varepsilon},

where
X_{\mathrm{ns}}
denotes the non-singular locus of
X
. By Lemma
4.1
the total contribution to
N_{\mathrm{aff}}(X,B)
coming from those curves
C_{i}
of degree
1
is at most
\ll_{d,\varepsilon}B^{1+\varepsilon}
. On the other hand, by
[
7
, Thm. 3]
the curves
C_{i}
of degree
>1
contribute at most
\ll_{d,\varepsilon}B^{1/2+1/\sqrt{d}+\varepsilon}
to
N_{\mathrm{aff}}(X,B)
. Finally,
X\setminus X_{\mathrm{ns}}
is a possibly reducible curve of degree at most
d^{2}
. Distinguishing in the same way into irreducible components of degree
1
and higher degree shows that
X\setminus X_{\mathrm{ns}}
has at most
\ll_{d}B
integral points of height at most
B
. Putting all these estimates together we conclude that

\displaystyle N_{\mathrm{aff}}(X,B)\ll_{d,\varepsilon}B^{1+\varepsilon},\quad

\displaystyle\text{ if }d\geq 4,

\displaystyle N_{\mathrm{aff}}(X,B)\ll_{\varepsilon}B^{2/\sqrt{3}+\varepsilon},\quad

\displaystyle\text{ if }d=3.

This proves the result.
∎

.

If one can prove Lemma
4.1
with polynomial dependence on

and without factors

, then one can also obtain such a result for Proposition

. In that case, one should follow the proof from
[

.

(5.2).
Affine varieties

The goal of this section is to prove our main result, Theorem
1.2
. The idea is to first prove the result for hypersurfaces by using induction and slicing with hyperplanes, and then use a projection for the general case. Note that our induction is slightly more complicated than in earlier works since we need to preserve the fact that our variety is not cylindrical over a curve. Preserving this condition is taken care of by
[
8
]
, or see
[
18
, Lem. 9]
. However, in
[
8
]
one needs to preserve some more conditions on e.g. the top degree part of the defining polynomial, which is no longer required in the current work.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
for hypersurfaces.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{A}^{n}
be an irreducible hypersurface of degree
d\geq 3
defined by a primitive irreducible polynomial
f\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
, where
n\geq 3
. As usual, Proposition
2.1
shows that we can assume that
X
and

are absolutely irreducible. If
n=3
, then the result follows from Proposition
5.1
, so we assume that
n\geq 4
and that the result holds for hypersurfaces of dimension at most
n-1
. By using Noether forms exactly as in
[
7
, Lem. 4.3.7]
, together with
[
18
, Lem. 9]
there exists a linear form
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
such that

(1)

there are at most
O_{d,n}(1)
values

such that the intersection

is either not absolutely irreducible, or it is cylindrical over a curve, and

(2)

the height of

is bounded by
O_{d,n}(1)
.

Alternatively, one may use
[
8
, Prop. 4.14]
to obtain an effective such linear form

, namely of height at most

and with at most
O_{n}(d^{3})
bad values of

. In any case, if

is a value for which

is absolutely irreducible and not cylindrical over a curve then we use induction to count integral points on

, while for the other values of

we simply use the Schwartz–Zippel bound from Proposition

to obtain that if

then

\displaystyle N_{\mathrm{aff}}(f,B)\leq\sum_{|b|\ll_{n}d^{3}B}N_{\mathrm{aff}}(X\cap\{\ell=b\},B)\ll_{d,n,\varepsilon}B\cdot B^{n-3+\varepsilon}+B^{n-2}\ll_{d,n,\varepsilon}B^{n-2+\varepsilon}.

If

, then the same argument shows that

N_{\mathrm{aff}}(f,B)\ll_{n,\varepsilon}B^{n-3+2/\sqrt{3}+\varepsilon}.\qed

For the general case, we need a little lemma stating that projections preserve being non-cylindrical over a curve.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{A}^{n}
be a variety of dimension
m
which is not cylindrical over a curve and let

-linear map such that

is a hypersurface. Then

is not cylindrical over a curve.

Proof.

Suppose that
f(X)
is cylindrical over a curve, we show that
X
is cylindrical over a curve. There exists a
\mathbb{Q}
-linear map
\pi:\mathbb{A}^{m+1}\to\mathbb{A}^{2}
such that
\pi(f(X))
is a curve. Let
h:\mathbb{A}^{n}\to\mathbb{A}^{n-m-1}
be a complementary
\mathbb{Q}
-linear map to
f
, so that
f\times g:\mathbb{A}^{n}\to\mathbb{A}^{m+1}\times\mathbb{A}^{n-m-1}
is an isomorphism. Then the map
(\pi\circ f)\times h:\mathbb{A}^{n}\to\mathbb{A}^{n-m+1}
is
\mathbb{Q}
-linear and maps
X
to a curve, so that
X
is cylindrical over a curve.
∎

We can now prove Theorem
1.2
in general.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{A}^{n}
be an irreducible variety of degree
d\geq 3
and dimension
m
. By
[
7
, Prop. 4.3.1]
there exists a
\mathbb{Q}
-linear map
\pi:\mathbb{A}^{n}\to\mathbb{A}^{m+1}
such that
\pi(X)
is a hypersurface of degree
d
,
\pi
is birational onto its image, and such that

N_{\mathrm{aff}}(X,B)\leq dN_{\mathrm{aff}}(\pi(X),O_{d,n}(B)).

By Lemma
5.3
the image
\pi(X)
is not cylindrical over a curve, and so Theorem
1.2
for hypersurfaces gives that if
d\geq 4
then

N_{\mathrm{aff}}(X,B)\ll_{d,n,\varepsilon}B^{m-1+\varepsilon}.

If
d=3
then we obtain that

N_{\mathrm{aff}}(X,B)\ll_{n,\varepsilon}B^{m-2+2/\sqrt{3}+\varepsilon}.\qed

6.
Global fields

(6.1).
Definitions and main result

In this section we explain how to adapt the results of this paper to global fields, including positive characteristic fields. In positive characteristic, results about counting rational and integral points on curves are due to Sedunova
[
21
]
and later Cluckers–Forey–Loeser
[
9
]
in large characteristic. In higher dimensions, there are results by the author
[
23
]
, Paredes–Sasyk
[
15
]
, and Cluckers–Dèbes–Hendel–Nguyen–Vermeulen
[
8
]
.

We follow the notation and set-up from
[
23
,
15
]
. Let
K
be a global field, by which we mean a finite separable extension of
\mathbb{Q}
or
\mathbb{F}_{q}(t)
. If
K
is an extension of
\mathbb{F}_{q}(t)
then we assume that
\mathbb{F}_{q}
is the full field of constants of
K
. Denote
k=\mathbb{Q}
or
\mathbb{F}_{q}(t)
and
d_{K}=[K:k]
. Any such global field comes with a height function
H:\mathbb{P}^{n}(K)\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}

[
15
]
(or see
[
8
, Sec. 4]
). For a projective variety
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}_{K}
defined over
K
, we obtain the counting function
N(X,B)
and similarly

for affine varieties. We say that an affine variety
X\subset\mathbb{A}^{n}
of dimension
m
is
cylindrical over a curve
if there exists a
\overline{K}
-linear projection
\pi:\mathbb{A}^{n}\to\mathbb{A}^{n-m+1}
such that
\pi(X)
is a curve.

1.2
then becomes the following.

Let
K
be a global field and let

be an irreducible affine variety of dimension

and degree

defined over

which is not cylindrical over a curve. Then for any positive integer

\displaystyle N_{\mathrm{aff}}(X,B)\ll_{K,d,n,\varepsilon}B^{m-1+\varepsilon},

\displaystyle\quad\text{ if }d\geq 4,

\displaystyle N_{\mathrm{aff}}(X,B)\ll_{K,n,\varepsilon}B^{m-2+\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}+\varepsilon},

\displaystyle\quad\text{ if }d=3.

, but with a worse exponent. The reason for the worse exponent is due to better bounds on degrees of Noether forms in characteristic zero, compare

]
with

]
. Next one can reprove the base of the induction to count
