Thermo-optic hysteresis with bound states in the continuum
D. N. Maksimov 1,2,3 , A. S. Kostyukov 1 , A. E. Ershov 1,3 , M. S. Molokeev 1,2 , E. N. Bulgakov 2,3 and V. S. Gerasimov 1,3

Abstract
We consider thermo-optic hysteresis in a silicon structure supporting bound state in the continuum. Taking into account radiative heat transfer as a major cooling mechanism we constructed a non-linear model describing the optical response. It is shown that the thermo-optic hysteresis can be obtained with low intensities of incident light I 0 ≈ 1 ​ W / m 2 I_{0}\approx 1~\rm{W/m^{2}} at the red edge of the visible under the critical coupling condition.



I
Introduction

Recently, we have seen a surge of interest to bound states
in the continuum (BICs)
Hsu et al. 2016
;
Koshelev et al. 2019a
;
Sadreev 2021
that have grown to an important
tool in nanophotonics paving a
way to optical devices with enhanced light-matter interaction.
BICs do not couple the incident light, however, if the symmetry of the system is broken the
BICs are observed as narrow Fano resonances in the scattering spectrum
Kim et al. 1999
;
Shipman and Venakides 2005
;
Sadreev et al. 2006
;
Blanchard et al. 2016
.
In more detail, the BICs are spectrally surrounded by a leaky band of high-quality resonances (quasi-BICs) which can be excited
from the far-field
Yuan and Lu 2017
. The excitation of the strong resonances results in critical field enhancement
Yoon et al. 2015
;
Mocella and Romano 2015

with the near-field amplitude controlled by the frequency and the angle of incidence of the
incoming monochromatic wave.
The critical field enhancement triggers nonlinear optical effects even with a low intensity of the
incident light.
This resonant enhancement of nonlinear effects can lead to symmetry breaking
Bulgakov et al. 2011
,
channel dropping
Bulgakov et al. 2013
, excitation of non-linear standing waves
Yuan and Lu 2016

as well as self-adaptive robust
Bulgakov and Sadreev 2014
and tunable Fabry-Perot
Bulgakov and Sadreev 2010
BICs.

In the field of nonlinear optics the BICs have been applied
for second harmonic generation
Ndangali and Shabanov 2013
;
Koshelev et al. 2019b
;
Wang and Zhang 2018
.
Quasi-BICs in subwavelength dielectric resonators
Rybin et al. 2017
;
Bogdanov et al. 2019
are also shown
to demonstrate to enhance second harmonic generation
Carletti et al. 2018
;
Koshelev et al. 2020
.
In
Yuan and Lu 2020
it was found that, otherwise decoupled, BICs can be excited via second harmonic generation by illuminating
the structure from the far field.
At the same time it was shown theoretically that quasi-BICs allow for optical bistability
Yuan and Lu 2017
;
Krasikov et al. 2018
;
Bulgakov and Maksimov 2019
;
Maksimov et al. 2020

due to the Kerr effect.

More recently, the research focus shifted towards BICs in lossy structures. In the presence of material absorption
the BIC are shown to acquire finite-life, albeit remain localized and decoupled from the outgoing channels
Hu et al. 2020
.
Quasi-BIC in lossy periodic structures are found to be instrumental for enhancement of light absorption
Saadabad et al. 2021
;
Tan et al. 2022

in the critical coupling regime even in low loss dielectrics. This opens novel opportunities for highly
efficient light absorbers
Zhang and Zhang 2015
;
Wang et al. 2020
;
Sang et al. 2021
;
Xiao et al. 2021
;
Cai et al. 2022
.

Lately, it has been suggested that thermo-optical effects can be the dominating nonlinear effects
in BIC supporting structures due to heating by absorbed radiation
Zograf et al. 2021
.
In this work we investigate resonantly enhanced thermo-optical bistability
Sun and Reano 2010
;
Khandekar and Rodriguez 2017
;
Gao et al. 2017
;
Pottier and Bellon 2021
;
Ryabov et al. 2022
in
a system supporting an optical BICs. Taking into account
radiative heat transfer as a major cooling mechanism we shall construct a non-linear model based on the temporal coupled mode theory (TCMT)
Fan et al. 2003

and theoretically demonstrate thermo-optic hysteresis.

R_{0}

into account both temperature and frequency dependence of the refractive index. In Fig.

By definition a BIC can not couple
to incident light. However, each BIC is a singular point on the dispersion sheet of a leaky band where the
Q
-factor diverges to infinity. The BIC is, thus,
spectrally surrounded by a family of high-
Q
leaky modes (quasi-BICs) with the resonant
Q
-factor controlled by variation of the angle of incidence.
In our case the BIC occurs in the
\Gamma
-point being symmetrically mismatched from the single radiation channel of the zeroth diffraction order. Therefore,
the high-
Q
resonant response is triggered by setting off the angle of incidence from zero. Thus, the similarity between
the BIC and the scattering solution is explained by both BIC and quasi-BIC sitting in the same dispersion band.

Our goal in this section is to set up the optimal regime for enhanced light absorption leading to the most significant change of the refractive index by heating.
The problem of enhanced absorption by quasi-BICs has been previously considered in the literature
Saadabad et al. 2021
. The central result for upside-down symmetric
structures, such as the one in Fig.
1
(a), is that the maximal absorption of
50\%
can be achieved in the critical coupling point
where the radiation and absorption loss rates are equal to one another.

The BIC induced optical response can be understood in the framework of temporal coupled mode theory (TCMT)
Fan et al. 2003
. For the reader’s convenience
we list the most important TCMT formulas below. Let us consider two-channel scattering for TE-polarized light.
The
S
-matrix is implicitly defined
through the following equation

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}s_{1}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\!-\!)}\\
s_{2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\!-\!)}\\
\end{array}\right)=\widehat{S}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}s_{1}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\!+\!)}\\
s_{2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\!+\!)}\\
\end{array}\right),

(1)

where
s_{m}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\!\pm\!)}
are the amplitudes of the plane waves
in the far-field with subscript
m=1,2
corresponding
to the upper and lower half-spaces while superscripts
{}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\!+\!)},^{\scriptscriptstyle(\!-\!)}
stand for incident and outgoing waves respectively. We assume that the system is illuminated
by a monochromatic wave of frequency
\omega
. In what follows we introduce the vectors of incident and outgoing amplitudes
|s^{\scriptscriptstyle(\!\pm\!)}(t)\rangle
which oscillate in time with the harmonic factor
e^{-i\omega t}
.
The TCMT equations
Fan et al. 2003
take the following form

\displaystyle\frac{da(t)}{dt}=-(i\omega_{0}+\gamma+\gamma_{0})a(t)+\langle d^{*}|s^{\scriptscriptstyle(\!+\!)}\rangle,

\displaystyle|s^{\scriptscriptstyle(\!-\!)}\rangle=\widehat{C}|s^{\scriptscriptstyle(\!+\!)}\rangle+a(t)|d\rangle,

(2)

where
\widehat{C}
is the matrix of direct (non-resonant) process,
\omega_{0}
is the resonance center frequency,

\gamma
is the radiation decay rate,
\gamma_{0}
is material loss decay rate,
a
is the amplitude of the resonant eigenmode, and

|d\rangle
is the
2\times 1
vector of coupling constants satisfying

\langle d|d\rangle=2\gamma.

(3)

The solution
for the
S
-matrix reads

\widehat{S}=\widehat{C}+\frac{|d\rangle\langle d^{*}|}{i(\omega_{0}-\omega)+\gamma+\gamma_{0}}.

(4)

In the case of the center-plane mirror symmetry
we have

\widehat{C}=e^{i\phi}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\rho&i\tau\\
i\tau&\rho\end{array}\right),

(5)

where
\phi,\rho,\tau
are real valued parameters such as

\rho^{2}+\tau^{2}=1.

(6)

The following equation also holds true
Fan et al. 2003

\widehat{C}|d^{*}\rangle=-|d\rangle.

(7)

Using Eq. (
3
) together with Eq. (
7
) one finds

|d\rangle=e^{i\frac{\phi}{2}}\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{2(1+\rho)}}\left(\begin{array}[]{l}\tau-i(1+\rho)\\
\tau-i(1+\rho)\end{array}\right),

(8)

for symmetric modes, and

|d\rangle=e^{i\frac{\phi}{2}}\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{2(1+\rho)}}\left(\begin{array}[]{l}\tau+i(1+\rho)\\
-\tau-i(1+\rho)\end{array}\right),

(9)

for anti-symmetric ones.
The reflectance can be found from Eq. (
4
) as

R=\frac{\rho^{2}(\omega-\omega_{0})^{2}\mp 2\rho\tau\gamma(\omega-\omega_{0})+\tau^{2}\gamma^{2}+\rho^{2}\gamma_{0}^{2}}{(\omega-\omega_{0})^{2}+(\gamma+\gamma_{0})^{2}},

(10)

while the transmittance is given by

T=\frac{\tau^{2}(\omega-\omega_{0})^{2}\pm 2\rho\tau\gamma(\omega-\omega_{0})+\rho^{2}\gamma^{2}+\tau^{2}\gamma_{0}^{2}}{(\omega-\omega_{0})^{2}+(\gamma+\gamma_{0})^{2}},

(11)

where the top sign is used for symmetric modes.
Finally, the absorbance is given by

A=\frac{2\gamma\gamma_{0}}{(\omega-\omega_{0})^{2}+(\gamma+\gamma_{0})^{2}}.

(12)

The maximal absorbance is obtained at the critical coupling condition

\gamma=\gamma_{0}
and
\omega=\omega_{0}
. Substituting the above into Eq. (
12
) one finds

A_{\mathrm{max}}=\frac{1}{2}.

(13)

with

as the area of the rod cross section.

III
Heat transfer

Since our system consists of dielectric rods in vacuum, the only mechanism of heat transfer is radiation from the rods.
The total energy lost per unit surface area across all wavelengths per unit time is given by the Stefan-Boltzmann law

j=\alpha\sigma[(\Theta_{0}+\Delta\Theta)^{4}-\Theta_{0}^{4}],

(17)

where
\alpha
is the emissivity,
\sigma=5.670\times 10^{-8}~\mathrm{W}\cdot\mathrm{m}^{-2}\cdot\mathrm{K}^{-4}
is the Stefan-Boltzmann constant,
\Theta_{0}
is the temperature
of the surrounding medium and
\Delta\Theta
is the temperature increase due to absorption of light. The emissivity,
\alpha
, is a quantity of key importance,
that describes the difference in the radiative properties between
the dielectric rod and the black body. In this work we assessed the emissivity by computing the
absorption coefficient of a single dielectric rod in the temperature range
25^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\leq\Theta\leq 300^{\circ}\mathrm{C}
.
The absorption coefficient was found as the average over all possible direction of the incident wave vector for both polarizations and, then,
averaged with the Planck’s distribution

Figure 4:
Emissivity of a single dielectric rod as a function of temperature;
L=428
nm,
R_{0}=128
nm. The average emissivity,
\tilde{\alpha}
, is shown
by the horizontal line.

p(\omega)=\frac{\hbar\omega^{3}}{4\pi^{3}c^{2}}\frac{1}{e^{\hbar\omega/(k_{\mathrm{B}}\Theta)}-1}

(18)

peaked at about
10~\mu{\mathrm{m}}
. The numerical values of the refractive index were taken from
Chandler-Horowitz and Amirtharaj 2005
. The temperature dependance of
the refractive index was neglected in the mid-infrared.
The result is demonstrated in Fig.
4
. One can see that the emissivity
is dependant on temperature of the rods due to the shift of the peak of Planck’s distribution. For further convenience we computed
the temperature average
\tilde{\alpha}=2.8\times 10^{-5}
which will be applied in our simulations. The average emissivity is shown in Fig.
4
as a horizontal line.

The regime of heating can be determined by the value of the Biot number
Bergman et al. 2011

\mathrm{Bi}=\frac{h_{S}}{k}L_{0},

(19)

where
L_{0}
is the characteristic length,
h_{S}
is the surface heat transfer coefficient, and
k
is the thermal conductivity of the body.
Values of the Biot number smaller than
0.1
imply that the heat conduction inside the body is much faster than the heat lost
at the interface between the body and the surrounding medium. In this regime we can assume that the temperature is constant across the body.
By using Eq. (
17
) we find

h_{S}=4\tilde{\alpha}\sigma\Theta_{0}^{3}\Delta\Theta.

(20)

Taking
L_{0}
as the perimeter of the rod, and
k=1.8~\mathrm{W}/(\mathrm{m}\cdot\mathrm{K})
according to
Wada and Kamijoh 1996
we find at the room temperature

\mathrm{Bi}=6.5\cdot 10^{-11}
. This allows us to assume that the temperature is uniform in the cross-section of the rod and avoid simulating heat transfer within the rods. Now our TCMT and heat transfer models can be linked by equating
the energy absorption rate in a unit length of the rod

\frac{dE}{dt}=\gamma_{0}(\Theta)|a|^{2}

(21)

to the radiation rate from the surface per unit length of the same rod

\frac{dE}{dt}=2\pi R_{0}\tilde{\alpha}\sigma[(\Theta_{0}+\Delta\Theta)^{4}-\Theta_{0}^{4}].

(22)

Frequency domain coupled mode equation reads

[i\omega_{0}(\Theta)-i\omega+\gamma(\Theta)+\gamma_{0}(\Theta)]a=d_{1}(\Theta)\sqrt{LI_{0}},

(23)

where
I_{0}
is the intensity of the incident monochromatic light. Notice that according to Eq. (

)

All parameters including the resonant eigenfrequency, absorption and radiation decay rates are dependent on temperature. Hence we write

\displaystyle\omega_{0}(\Theta)=\omega_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(0)}}+\omega_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(1)}}\Delta\Theta,

\displaystyle\gamma_{0}(\Theta)=\gamma_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(0)}}+\gamma_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(1)}}\Delta\Theta,

\displaystyle\gamma(\Theta)_{=}\gamma^{\scriptscriptstyle{(0)}}+\gamma^{\scriptscriptstyle{(1)}}\Delta\Theta.

(24)

As it was mentioned the temperature dependence is due to the temperature dependance of the refractive index. By using

and performing a TCMT fit we found the numerical values of the expansion coefficients in Eq. (

) as follows

\displaystyle L\omega_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(1)}}/c=-2.80\times 10^{-4},~\mathrm{K}^{-1},

\displaystyle L\gamma_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(1)}}/c=2.67\times 10^{-5},~\mathrm{K}^{-1},

\displaystyle L\gamma^{\scriptscriptstyle{(1)}}/c=6.67\times 10^{-6},~\mathrm{K}^{-1}.

(25)

One can see from the above data that the dominating effect is a shift of the resonant frequency. The resonant frequency is at least one
order of magnitude more sensitive to heating than the other TCMT parameters. Also notice
that the absorption loss rate is much more sensitive to heating than the radiative loss rate. This can be understood from the explicit linear dependance
of
\gamma_{0}
on the imaginary part of the refractive index given by Eq. (
15
). In what follows we neglect
the temperature dependance of
\gamma
and, consequently, of
|\bf{d}\rangle
.
From (
23
) and (
3
) we find

|a|^{2}=\frac{\gamma I_{0}L}{[\omega-\omega_{0}(\Theta)]^{2}+[\gamma+\gamma_{0}(\Theta)]^{2}},

which in combination with Eq. (

) and Eq. (

) yields

\displaystyle\frac{(\gamma_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(0)}}+\gamma_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(1)}}\Delta\Theta)\gamma I_{0}L}{(\omega-\omega_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(0)}}-\omega_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(1)}}\Delta\Theta)^{2}+(\gamma+\gamma_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(0)}}+\gamma_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle{(1)}}\Delta\Theta)^{2}}=

\displaystyle 2\pi R_{0}\tilde{\alpha}\sigma[(\Theta_{0}+\Delta\Theta)^{4}-\Theta_{0}^{4}].

(27)

The above formula constitutes a non-linear equation which has to be solved for

exists at

(28)

at the incident wavelength
\lambda=795.9~\rm{nm}
. This in combination with our assessment for the emissivity
\tilde{\alpha}=2.80\cdot 10^{-5}

V
Summary and Conclusions

We considered thermo-optic hysteresis in a system supporting a symmetry protected bound state in the continuum. It is found that
bistability occurs at surprisingly low intensities of incident light, about
I_{0}=0.54~\rm{W/m^{2}}
. Two aspects are of key importance for
obtaining low bistability thresholds. (i) Critical coupling which is obtained by breaking the symmetry of the system by incident light.
With the increase of the angle of incidence the radiation decay rate grows quadratically until it becomes equal to the absorption decay rate,

\gamma=\gamma_{0}
. In this situation, no matter how small the absorption decay rate is,
50\%
of the incident light is absorbed and transformed to heat.
This approach paves a way for engineering effective absorbers with low loss dielectrics. (ii) Low emissivity which is due to a small optical path within the absorbing
dielectric at the peak wavelength of Planck’s distribution at about
10~\rm{\mu m}
. The dielectric array is assembled of rods with a subwavelength cross section
in the visible. Thus, the optical path in the mid-infrared is at least an order of magnitude less than the wavelength of the thermal radiation. Given that the imaginary part
of the refractive index of silicon is vanishingly small in the mid-infrared
Chandler-Horowitz and Amirtharaj 2005
,
n^{\prime\prime}\approx 10^{-4}
, this results in a small value of
the emissivity
\tilde{\alpha}=2.8\cdot 10^{-5}
.

The bistability occurs with intensities eleven orders of magnitude smaller than those needed for hysteresis due to Kerr nonlinearity in silicon

Maksimov et al. 2020
. Thus, that thermo-optic effects should dominate in non-linear response of silicon nano-structures supporting bound states in
the continuum. In this work we have used a rough estimate for the emissivity. Yet, it is worth mentioning that even with
\tilde{\alpha}=1
, i.e. with
a mid-infrared
black body, the bistability thresholds would drop by less than five orders of magnitude and still be dominating in the non-linear response. Here we neglected
diffusive and convective heat transfer. In particular, the system under consideration has no substrate, which is necessary for realistic dielectric
metasurfaces. One would expect that the presence of substrate would further decrease the bistability thresholds as the heat would be leaking into the substrate.
This issue will be addressed in future studies.
