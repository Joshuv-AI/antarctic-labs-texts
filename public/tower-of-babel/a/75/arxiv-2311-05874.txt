Testing Dependency of Unlabeled Databases
Vered Paslev Wasim Huleihel

Abstract
In this paper, we investigate the problem of deciding whether two random databases 𝖷 ∈ 𝒳 n × d \mathsf{X}\in{\cal X}^{n\times d} and 𝖸 ∈ 𝒴 n × d \mathsf{Y}\in{\cal Y}^{n\times d} are statistically dependent or not. This is formulated as a hypothesis testing problem, where under the null hypothesis, these two databases are statistically independent, while under the alternative, there exists an unknown row permutation σ \sigma , such that 𝖷 \mathsf{X} and 𝖸 σ \mathsf{Y}^{\sigma} , a permuted version of 𝖸 \mathsf{Y} , are statistically dependent with some known joint distribution, but have the same marginal distributions as the null. We characterize the thresholds at which optimal testing is information-theoretically impossible and possible, as a function of n n , d d , and some spectral properties of the generative distributions of the datasets. For example, we prove that if a certain function of the eigenvalues of the likelihood function and d d , is below a certain threshold, as d → ∞ d\to\infty , then weak detection (performing slightly better than random guessing) is statistically impossible, no matter what the value of n n is. This mimics the performance of an efficient test that thresholds a centered version of the log-likelihood function of the observed matrices. We also analyze the case where d d is fixed, for which we derive strong (vanishing error) and weak detection lower and upper bounds.

Introduction

Prompted by practical scenarios, such as computational biology applications
[
SXB08
,
KHP12
]
, social network analysis
[
NS08
,
NS09
]
, computer vision
[
BBM05
,
CSS06
]
, and data anonymization/privacy-focused systems, there has been a recent focus on exploring the theoretical underpinnings and algorithmic solutions for database alignment under statistical frameworks. Indeed, quantifying relationships between disparate databases stand as fundamental undertakings in statistics. Modern databases present many challenges: they are high-dimensional, lack labels, contain noise, and appear scrambled. One concrete example of an inference problem, involving a pair of databases, is framed as the following hypothesis testing problem. Under the null hypothesis, no statistical dependency exists between the databases, whereas under the alternative hypothesis, there exists a permutation that reorganizes one database in such a way that the two become dependent. Then, given these databases, under what conditions can we discern whether they are dependent or not?

As a tangible folklore example, consider the following scenario: envision two distinct data sources, such as Netflix and IMDb, each providing feature lists for a set of entities, like users. These features encompass diverse user attributes, such as names, user IDs, and ratings. Frequently, feature labels are either unavailable or intentionally removed to safeguard sensitive personally identifiable information. Consequently, straightforwardly matching feature pairs between the two sources, corresponding to the same user, becomes challenging. Nevertheless, there is optimism that when a substantial correlation exists between the two databases, it becomes feasible to establish connections between them and create a coherent alignment for their respective feature lists
[
NS08
,
NS09
]
.

Recently, there has been a focus on what is known as the
data alignment problem
. This problem can be seen as a straightforward probabilistic model that encapsulates the scenario described above. It was introduced and explored in, e.g.,
[
CMK18
,
DCK19
]
. In essence, this problem involves two databases, denoted as
\mathsf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
and
\mathsf{Y}^{n\times d}
, each comprising
n
users, each with
d
features. The key challenge lies in uncovering an unknown permutation or correspondence that matches users in
\mathsf{X}
with those in
\mathsf{Y}
. When a pair of entries from these databases is matched, their features are dependent according to a known distribution, whereas for unmatched entries, the features are independent. The primary objective is to
recover
the unknown permutation and establish statistical assurances regarding the feasibility and impossibility of this recovery. The feasibility of recovery is contingent upon factors such as the correlation level,
n
, and
d
. The statistical limits of this recovery problem are understood for some specific probability distributions that generate the databases. For instance, in the Gaussian case, where the two databases have independent standard normal entries, with correlation coefficient
\rho
between the entries of matched rows, it has been demonstrated in
[
DCK19
]
that perfect recovery is attainable if
\rho^{2}=1-o(n^{-4/d})
, while it becomes impossible if
\rho^{2}=1-\omega(n^{-4/d})
as both
n
and
d
tend to infinity.

The
detection
counterpart of the recovery problem discussed above has also undergone extensive investigation in
[
KN22a
,
KN22b
,
Tam23
,
EH23
]
, in the Gaussian case. As mentioned earlier, the central question here revolves around determining the correlation level needed to decide whether the two databases are correlated or not. It has been established in
[
KN22b
]
that when
\rho^{2}d\to\infty
, efficient detection becomes feasible (with an exceedingly small error probability) using a straightforward thresholding of the sum of entries in
\mathsf{X}^{T}\mathsf{Y}
. Conversely, it has also been demonstrated that when
\rho^{2}d\sqrt{n}\to 0
and
d=\Omega(\log n)
, detection becomes information-theoretically impossible. Most recently, in
[
EH23
]
, the gap between the lower and upper bounds above has been conclusively addressed, by proving that the upper bound is tight, i.e., if
\rho^{2}d\to 0
as
d\to\infty
, weak detection (performing slightly better than random guessing) is information-theoretically impossible, regardless of the specific value of
n
. In fact, while the main focus in related literature was exclusively on the asymptotic regime where both
n
and
d
tend to infinity,
[
EH23
]
determine sharp thresholds for all possible asymptotic regimes of
n
and
d
.

In this paper, we continue the investigation of the database alignment detection problem. While the results above are neat and interesting, they apply for the Gaussian case only. This is in fact true not just for the detection but also for the recovery problem. This case is, however, restricted in the sense that for jointly Gaussian random variables, the notion of uncorrelatedness and independence are equivalent; the information-theoretic limits depend on the distribution through the correlation parameter solely. Accordingly, in the general case, it is a priori unclear how these thresholds depend on the distributions at hand. Furthermore, as it turns out, many of the techniques in
[
KN22a
,
KN22b
,
Tam23
,
EH23
]
are tailored to the Gaussian case, and the analysis of general distributions becomes challenging. To the best of our knowledge, our paper is the first to provide characterization for the thresholds at which optimal testing is information-theoretically impossible and possible, for two general distributions. Specifically, given two generative distributions, we prove that weak (strong) detection is information-theoretically impossible if a certain function of the eigenvalues of the likelihood function kernel (as defined in (
6
)) is below some threshold; we consider all possible asymptotic regimes as a function of
n
and
d
. We then complement out lower bounds by algorithmic upper bounds, and show that these are tight in a few classical examples. To that end, we propose three detection algorithms and analyze their associated risks and sample complexities.

We will now provide a brief overview of other related works. In
[
DCK20
]
, the problem of partial recovery of the hidden alignment was investigated. In
[
SGE19
]
, necessary and sufficient conditions for successful recovery through a typicality-based framework were established. Additionally,
[
BE21
]
and
[
BE22
]
explored the problem of alignment recovery in cases involving feature deletions and repetitions, respectively. A recent development in this area involved the joint analysis of correlation detection and alignment in Gaussian databases, as presented in
[
Tam23
]
. Finally, it is worth noting that the challenges in database alignment and detection closely relate to various planted matching problems, specifically the graph alignment problem, which has yielded numerous intriguing results and valuable mathematical techniques. Roughly speaking, in this problem the goal is to detect the edge correlation between two random graphs with unlabeled nodes. For further exploration, one can refer to works such as
[
MMX21
,
DWXY21
,
PG11
,
DMWX18
,
WXY23
,

,

,

, and their associated references. In particular, as we mention later on in the outlook of our paper, an interesting open problem mentioned in
[
WXY23

is to investigate the case of general edge weight distributions, which inspired our work for the random databases case.

Setup and Learning Problem

Setup.

As mentioned in the introduction, in this paper we will investigate the following decision problem. We deal with two databases
\mathsf{X}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
and
\mathsf{Y}=(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n})
, where
X_{i}\in{\cal X}^{d}
and
Y_{i}\in{\cal Y}^{d}
, for
i=1,2,\ldots,n
, with
n
being the number of entities (say, users), and
d
is the number of features. Now, under the null hypothesis
{\cal H}_{0}
, the databases
\mathsf{X}
and
\mathsf{Y}
are generated
independently
at random, where
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\sim P_{X}^{\otimes d}
and
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}\sim P_{Y}^{\otimes d}
, with
P_{X}=P_{Y}
(and therefore
{\cal X}={\cal Y}
as well). We denote by
\mathbb{P}_{0}
the null distribution, i.e., the joint probability distribution of
(\mathsf{X},\mathsf{Y})
under
{\cal H}_{0}
. Also, for simplicity of notation, we denote the product measure
Q_{XY}\triangleq P_{X}\times P_{Y}
. Under the alternate hypothesis
{\cal H}_{1}
, the databases
\mathsf{X}
and
\mathsf{Y}
are dependent under some
unknown
alignment/permutation
\sigma\in\mathbb{S}_{n}
, specifically, given
\sigma\in\mathbb{S}_{n}
, a permutation over
[n]
, we have
(X_{1},Y_{\sigma_{1}}),(X_{2},Y_{\sigma_{2}}),\ldots,(X_{n},Y_{\sigma_{n}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}P_{XY}^{\otimes d}
, with the same marginals
P_{X}=P_{Y}
. For a fixed
\sigma\in{\mathbb{S}}_{n}
, we denote the joint probability distribution of
(\mathsf{X},\mathsf{Y})
under the hypothesis
{\cal H}_{1}
by
{\mathbb{P}}_{{\cal H}_{1}|\sigma}
. To summarize, the hypothesis testing problem we deal with is,

\displaystyle\cal{\displaystyle H}_{0}:(X_{1},Y_{1}),\ldots,(X_{n},Y_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{i.i.d}}}{{\sim}}P_{X}^{\otimes d}\times P_{Y}^{\otimes d}

(1)

\displaystyle\cal{\displaystyle H}_{1}:(X_{1},Y_{\sigma_{1}}),\ldots,(X_{n},Y_{\sigma_{n}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{i.i.d}}}{{\sim}}P_{XY}^{\otimes d},\ \ \text{for some }\sigma\in\mathbb{S}_{n}.

Viewing
\mathsf{X}
as an
n\times d
matrix, we denote its
(i,j)
element by
X_{ij}
(and similarly for
\mathsf{Y}
). We remark here that the distributions
Q_{XY}
and
P_{XY}
are allowed to be functions of
n
and
d
. In fact, as we will see later on, this is when the problem becomes interesting and non-trivial. Fig.
1
presents an illustration of our probabilistic model under
{\cal H}_{1}
.

Learning problem.

Given the databases
\mathsf{X}
and
\mathsf{Y}
, a test/detection algorithm
\phi_{n,d}:{\cal X}^{n\times d}\times{\cal Y}^{n\times d}\to\{0,1\}
, for the hypothesis testing problem above, is tasked with outputting a decision in
\{0,1\}
. We define the risk of a detection algorithm
\phi_{n,d}
as the sum of its Type-I and (worst-case) Type-II error probabilities, namely,

\displaystyle\mathsf{R}(\phi_{n,d})\triangleq{\mathbb{P}}_{{\cal H}_{0}}[\phi_{n,d}(\mathsf{X},\mathsf{Y})=1]+\max_{\sigma\in{\mathbb{S}}_{n}}{\mathbb{P}}_{{\cal H}_{1}|\sigma}[\phi_{n,d}(\mathsf{X},\mathsf{Y})=0].

(2)

The
minimax
risk is then defined as,

\displaystyle\mathsf{R}^{\star}\triangleq\inf_{\phi_{n,d}:{\cal X}^{n\times d}\times{\cal Y}^{n\times d}\to\{0,1\}}\mathsf{R}(\phi_{n,d}).

(3)

We remark that
\mathsf{R}
and
\mathsf{R}^{\star}
are in general functions of
n
,
d
,
Q_{XY}
, and
P_{XY}
. However, we omit the dependency on these parameters from our notations for the benefit of readability, as we shall do for our detection algorithms as well.

As in
[
EH23
]
, we study the information-theoretic limits (i.e., impossibility and possibility lower and upper bounds) of the hypothesis testing problem in (
1
), for several possible asymptotic regimes of
n
and
d
. To be more precise, let
{\cal D}
denote the pair of distributions
Q_{XY}
, and
P_{XY}
. Then, the regimes we investigate are characterized by sequences of the parameters
({\cal D},d,n)=({\cal D}_{\ell},d_{\ell},n_{\ell})_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}
. For example, if
P_{X}=P_{Y}={\cal N}(0,1)
and
P_{XY}
denote the joint distribution of two standard normal random variables with correlation coefficient
\rho
, then the triplet
({\cal D},d,n)
reduces to
(\rho,d,n)
. In this paper, the asymptotic regimes we consider correspond to the scenarios where
d_{\ell}
and
n_{\ell}
are either fixed or tend to infinity. Accordingly, asymptotic notations, such as,
f({\cal D})=o(\cdot)
,
f({\cal D})=\Omega(\cdot)
, etc., where
f(\cdot)
is some one-dimensional function of
{\cal D}
, should be interpreted in terms of the sequences above. For example, the condition
f({\cal D})=o(d^{-1})
means that the sequence
({\cal D},d,n)
satisfies
f({\cal D}_{\ell})d_{\ell}\to 0
, as
\ell\to\infty
. Later on, we will give concrete examples which elucidate the notations above. With the above in mind, we are in a position to define the notions of strong and weak detection.

Definition 1
.

A sequence
({\cal D},d,n)=({\cal D}_{k},d_{k},n_{k})_{k}
is said to be:

1.

Admissible for strong detection if

\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathsf{R}^{\star}=0
.

2.

Admissible for weak detection if

\limsup_{k\to\infty}\mathsf{R}^{\star}<1
.

A few comments are in order. First, it should be clear that admissibility of strong detection implies the admissibility of weak detection. More concretely, if a test
\phi
achieves strong detection, i.e.,
\mathsf{R}(\phi)\to 0
, then
\phi
achieves weak detection as well. Also, note that strong detection requires the test statistic to determine with high probability whether
(\mathsf{X},\mathsf{Y})
is is drawn from
{\cal H}_{0}
or
{\cal H}_{1}
, while weak detection only aims at strictly outperforming random
guessing of the underlying hypothesis. Finally, let
\bar{\mathsf{R}}^{\star}
denote the optimal
average
risk, which corresponds to the case where
\sigma
is uniformly drawn over
\mathbb{S}_{n}
(rather being unknown). Mathematically, it is defined similarly to (
2
)–(
3
), but with the maximum in (
2
) over
\sigma\in\mathbb{S}_{n}
replaced by an expectation with respect to
\sigma\sim\mathsf{Unif}(\mathbb{S}_{n})
. It is clear that
\bar{\mathsf{R}}^{\star}
lower bounds the worst-case risk
\mathsf{R}^{\star}
, and it is well-known that
\bar{\mathsf{R}}^{\star}=1-d_{\mathsf{TV}}(\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{0}},\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{1}})
, where with some abuse of notation, in the average case, the distribution
\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{1}}
is defined as the mixture
\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{1}}\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{1}|\sigma}
. Also, the optimal test that achieves the minimal average risk is the likelihood ratio test (a.k.a. the Neyman-Pearson test). Accordingly, weak and strong detection are equivalent to
\liminf d_{\mathsf{TV}}(\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{0}},\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{1}})>0
and
\lim d_{\mathsf{TV}}(\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{0}},\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{1}})=0
, respectively. We discuss this in detail in Section
4
. In the same vein, to rule out the possibility of weak/strong detection, we will use the well-known facts that,

d_{\mathsf{TV}}({\mathbb{P}}_{{\cal H}_{0}},{\mathbb{P}}_{{\cal H}_{1}})=o(1)\implies\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathsf{R}^{\star}=1,

(4)

and

d_{\mathsf{TV}}({\mathbb{P}}_{{\cal H}_{0}},{\mathbb{P}}_{{\cal H}_{1}})\leq 1-\Omega(1)\implies\liminf_{k\to\infty}\mathsf{R}^{\star}>0.

(5)

To wit, the implication in (
4
) correspond to the impossibility of weak detection, while (
5
) corresponds to the impossibility of strong detection, respectively. We discuss further and prove these relations in Section
4
.

3
Main Results

In this section, we present our main results on the impossibility and admissibility of weak and strong criteria of detection, under the setting presented in Section
2
. We differentiate between the various possible asymptotic regimes: (
I
) both
n,d\to\infty
; the regime analyzed in most related past literature, (
II
)
n
is a constant and
d\to\infty
, and (
III
)
d
is a constant and
n\to\infty
. We begin with our impossibility lower-bounds. To that end, we introduce a few important notations.

For any
x\in{\cal X}
and
y\in{\cal Y}
, we let
{\cal L}(x,y)\triangleq\frac{P_{XY}(x,y)}{Q_{XY}(x,y)}
denote the single-letter likelihood function. It turns our beneficial to think of
{\cal L}
as a kernel, which induces an operator as follows. For any square-integrable function
f
under
Q_{XY}
, i.e.,
\int\int f^{2}(x,y)Q_{X}(\mathrm{d}x)Q_{Y}(\mathrm{d}y)<\infty
,

\displaystyle({\cal L}f)(x)\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim Q_{Y}}\left[{\cal L}(x,Y)f(Y)\right].

(6)

Furthermore, the composition
{\cal L}^{2}={\cal L}\circ{\cal L}
is given by
{\cal L}^{2}(x,y)=\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim Q}[{\cal L}(x,Z){\cal L}(Z,y)]
, and
{\cal L}^{k}
is defined similarly. We assume that
{\cal L}(x,y)={\cal L}(y,x)
, and hence
{\cal L}
is self-adjoint. Furthermore, if we assume that
\int\int{\cal L}^{2}(x,y)Q_{X}(\mathrm{d}x)Q_{Y}(\mathrm{d}y)<\infty
, then
{\cal L}
is Hilbert-Schmidt. Thus,
{\cal L}
is diagonazable, and we denote its eigenvalues by
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i\geq 0}
. Accordingly, the trace of
{\cal L}
is given by
\mathsf{trace}({\cal L})=\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim Q_{Y}}[{\cal L}(Y,Y)]=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\lambda_{i}
. Without loss of generality, we assume that the sequence of eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i\geq 0}
is decreasing, namely,
\lambda_{i}\geq\lambda_{i+1}
, for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
. Finally, we prove in Lemma
2
in Section
4
that the largest eigenvalue of
{\cal L}
is unity, i.e.,
\lambda_{0}=1
. We are now in a position to state our main results.

Theorem 1

(Weak detection lower bound)
.

Weak detection is impossible as long as

\displaystyle\sum_{i\geq 1}\frac{\lambda_{i}^{2}}{1-\lambda_{i}^{2}}=o(d^{-1}).

(7)

That is, for a sequence
({\cal D},d,n)=({\cal D}_{k},d_{k},n_{k})_{k}
such that (
7
) holds:

•

If
d
is any function of
k
, and
n\to\infty
then
\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathsf{R}^{\star}=1
.

•

If
n
is constant and
d\to\infty
then
\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathsf{R}^{\star}=1
.

Namely,
({\cal D},d,n)
is not admissible for weak detection.

Based on Theorem
1
, we see that weak detection is impossible when (
7
) holds, for all different asymptotic regimes of
n
and
d
; however, if
d
is fixed then the right-hand-side of (
7
) should be understood as
o(1)
, as
n\to\infty
. Next, we move forward to our strong detection lower bounds.

Theorem 2

(Strong detection lower bounds)
.

A sequence
({\cal D},d,n)
is not admissible for strong detection if:

1.

d\in\mathbb{N}
and
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
are constants, such that

\displaystyle d<-\frac{\log\lambda_{1}^{2}}{\log\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\lambda_{i}^{2}},

(8)

and
n\to\infty
.

2.

n,d\to\infty
, and

\displaystyle\sum_{i\geq 1}\frac{\lambda_{i}^{2}}{1-\lambda_{i}^{2}}<(1-\varepsilon)d^{-1},

(9)

for some
\varepsilon>0
, independent of
n
and
d
.

3.

d\to\infty
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and

\displaystyle\sum_{i\geq 1}\frac{\lambda_{i}^{2}}{1-\lambda_{i}^{2}}=O(d^{-1}).

(10)

It is evident that for strong detection, we can prove slightly stronger results; for example, when
n,d\to\infty
, comparing (
7
) and (
9
) we see that the barriers are the same up to a constant factor. We also note that some of the barriers in Theorems
1
and
2
can be represented in terms of the likelihood kernel. For example, it is easy to check that the condition in (
7
) can be written as,

\displaystyle\mathsf{trace}\left({\cal L}^{2}\cdot(\mathbf{I}-{\cal L}^{2})^{-1}\right)=o(d^{-1}),

(11)

while the condition in (
8
) can be also written as,

\displaystyle d<-\frac{\log\lambda_{1}^{2}}{\log\mathsf{trace}({\cal L}^{2})}.

(12)

We next consider two canonical special cases for which we find explicit formulas for the lower bounds in Theorems
1
and
2
.

Example 1

(Gaussian databases)
.

In the Gaussian case, we assume that
Q_{XY}=P_{X}\times P_{Y}
, with
P_{X}
and
P_{Y}
correspond to the densities of a Gaussian random variable with zero mean and unit variance, while
P_{XY}
is the joint density of two correlated zero mean Gaussian random variables with unit variance, i.e.,

\displaystyle P_{XY}\equiv\mathcal{N}\left(\begin{bmatrix}0\\
0\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}1&\rho\\
\rho&1\end{bmatrix}\right),

(13)

for some known correlation coefficient
\rho\in[-1,1]\setminus\{0\}
. The Gaussian case above was analyzed in
[
KN22a
,
KN22b
,
Tam23
,
EH23
]
. A straightforward calculation reveals that,

\displaystyle{\cal L}(x,y)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}}\exp\left(\frac{-(x^{2}+y^{2})\rho^{2}+2xy\rho}{2(1-\rho^{2})}\right).

(14)

This is well-known as the Mehler’s kernel, and can be diagonalized by the Hermite polynomials as follows
[
Kib45
]
,

\displaystyle{\cal L}(x,y)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{\rho^{\ell}}{\ell!}H_{\ell}(x)H_{\ell}(y),

(15)

where
\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{Z}\sim{\cal N}(0,1)}[H_{i}(\mathsf{Z})H_{j}(\mathsf{Z})]=i!
, for
i=j
, and zero otherwise. As so, we can deduce that the eigenvalues of
{\cal L}
in this case are given by
\lambda_{\ell}=\rho^{\ell}
, for
\ell\geq 0
. Thus, the lower bound in Theorem
1
for weak detection, boils down to,

\displaystyle\sum_{i\geq 1}\frac{\rho^{2i}}{1-\rho^{2i}}=o(d^{-1}).

(16)

To simplify this condition, we note that

\displaystyle\sum_{i\geq 1}\frac{\rho^{2i}}{1-\rho^{2i}}\leq\frac{1}{1-\rho^{2}}\sum_{i\geq 1}\rho^{2i}=\frac{\rho^{2}}{(1-\rho^{2})^{2}}=o(d^{-1}),

(17)

where the last step holds if
\rho^{2}=o(d^{-1})
, which of course implies that (
16
) holds under the same condition. This condition, in turn, coincides with
[
EH23
]
. Similarly, in the regime where
d
is fixed, strong detection is impossible when (
8
) holds, which in the Gaussian setting boils down to

\displaystyle d<\frac{\log\rho^{2}}{\log(1-\rho^{2})},

(18)

which again coincides with the results of
[
EH23
]
. Therefore, our general lower bounds in Theorems
1
and
2
recover the known bounds in the literature.

Example 2

(Bernoulli databases)
.

In the Bernoulli case, we assume that
Q_{XY}=P_{X}\times P_{Y}
, with

P_{X}=P_{Y}=\mathsf{Bernoulli}(\tau p)
, for some
p\in(0,1)
and
\tau\in[0,1]
, and
P_{XY}
denotes the joint distribution of two correlated Bernoulli random variables. Specifically, under
P_{XY}
, we have
X\sim\mathsf{Bernoulli}(\tau p)
, and

\displaystyle Y|X

\displaystyle\sim\begin{cases}\mathsf{Bernoulli}(\tau),\ &\mathsf{if}\;X=1\\
\mathsf{Bernoulli}\left(\frac{\tau p(1-\tau)}{1-\tau p}\right),\ &\mathsf{if}\;X=0.\end{cases}

(19)

Here, Pearson correlation coefficient is given by,

\displaystyle\rho\triangleq\frac{\mathsf{cov}(X,Y)}{\sqrt{\mathsf{var}(X)}\sqrt{\mathsf{var}(Y)}}=\frac{\tau(1-p)}{1-\tau p}.

(20)

The Bernoulli case was not studied in the literature in the context of the database alignment detection problem, but it is the focus in related work on testing correlation of unlabeled random graphs, e.g.,
[
WXY23
]
, where the edges are modelled as Bernoulli random variables. Again, a straightforward calculation reveals that in the above setting,

\displaystyle{\cal L}(x,y)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{p},\ &\mathsf{if}\;(x,y)=(1,1)\\
\frac{1-\tau}{1-\tau p},\ &\mathsf{if}\;(x,y)=(0,0)\\
\frac{1-2\tau p+\tau^{2}p}{(1-\tau p)^{2}},\ &\mathsf{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(21)

Then, the eigenvalues of
{\cal L}
correspond to the eigenvalues of the
2\times 2
matrix
{\cal M}(x,y)\triangleq P_{XY}(x,y)/Q_{X}(x)
, for
x,y\in\{0,1\}
. Explicitly, we have

\displaystyle{\cal M}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1-p\tau(2-\tau)}{1-p\tau}&\frac{p\tau(1-\tau)}{1-p\tau}\\
1-\tau&\tau\end{bmatrix}.

(22)

Accordingly, it can be shown that the eigenvalues of
{\cal M}
are
1
and
\rho
. Thus, the lower bound in Theorem
1
for weak detection, boils down to,

\displaystyle\frac{\rho^{2}}{1-\rho^{2}}=o(d^{-1}).

(23)

which is equivalent to
\rho^{2}=o(d^{-1})
, as in the Gaussian case. Similarly, in the regime where
d
is fixed, strong detection is impossible when (
8
) holds, which in the Bernoulli setting, translates to,

\displaystyle d<\frac{\log(1/\rho^{2})}{\log(1+\rho^{2})},

(24)

similarly to the Gaussian case.

Next, we present our detection algorithms and the corresponding upper bounds. We start with the observation that the composite hypothesis testing problem in (
1
) can be reduced/formulated as a simple hypothesis testing problem where the latent permutation
\sigma
is drawn uniformly at random over
\mathbb{S}_{n}
. Accordingly, as is well-known the optimal decision rule is the Neyman-Pearson test which correspond to thresholding the likelihood ratio,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{1}}(\mathsf{X},\mathsf{Y})}{\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{0}}(\mathsf{X},\mathsf{Y})}=\mathbb{E}_{\sigma\sim\mathsf{Unif}(\mathbb{S}_{n})}\left[\frac{\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{1}|\sigma}(\mathsf{X},\mathsf{Y}|\sigma)}{\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{0}}(\mathsf{X},\mathsf{Y})}\right].

(25)

As is customary to other related testing problems with a latent combinatorial structure, analyzing the above optimal test is difficult due to the averaging over all
n!
possible permutations. Consider, instead, the generalized likelihood ratio test (GLRT), where the averaging is replaced with a maximum, namely,

\displaystyle\max_{\sigma\in\mathbb{S}_{n}}\frac{\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{1}|\sigma}(\mathsf{X},\mathsf{Y}|\sigma)}{\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{0}}(\mathsf{X},\mathsf{Y})}=\max_{\sigma\in\mathbb{S}_{n}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{P^{\otimes d}_{XY}(X_{i},Y_{\sigma_{i}})}{Q^{\otimes d}_{XY}(X_{i},Y_{i})},

(26)

and accordingly, we define the test as,

\displaystyle\phi_{\mathsf{GLRT}}(\mathsf{X},\mathsf{Y})

\displaystyle\triangleq\mathbbm{1}\left\{\max_{\sigma\in\mathbb{S}_{n}}\frac{1}{dn}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\frac{P^{\otimes d}_{XY}(X_{i},Y_{\sigma_{i}})}{Q^{\otimes d}_{XY}(X_{i},Y_{i})}\geq\tau_{\mathsf{GLRT}}\right\},

(27)

for some threshold
\tau_{\mathsf{GLRT}}\in\mathbb{R}
. To present our results, we need a few definitions and notations. Let us denote the log-likelihood ratio (LLR) by
\mathscr{L}(x,y)\triangleq\log{\cal L}(x,y)=\log\frac{P_{XY}(x,y)}{Q_{XY}(x,y)}
, for
(x,y)\in{\cal X}\times{\cal Y}
, i.e., the logarithm of the Radon-Nikodym derivative between
P_{XY}
and
Q_{XY}
. We
assume they are mutually absolutely continuous for simplicity and so
\mathscr{L}
is well-defined. We also
assume that the expectations of the LLR with respect to
P_{XY}
and
Q_{XY}
are finite, or, in other words, that
d_{\mathsf{KL}}(P_{XY}||Q_{XY})
and
d_{\mathsf{KL}}(Q_{XY}||P_{XY})
are finite. For
\theta\in(-d_{\mathsf{KL}}(Q_{XY}||P_{XY}),d_{\mathsf{KL}}(P_{XY}||Q_{XY}))
, we define the Chernoff’s exponents
E_{P},E_{Q}:\mathbb{R}\to[-\infty,\infty)
as the Legendre transforms of the log-moment generating functions, namely,

\displaystyle E_{Q}(\theta)\triangleq\sup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}\lambda\theta-\psi_{Q}(\lambda),\quad,E_{P}(\theta)\triangleq\sup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}\lambda\theta-\psi_{P}(\lambda),

(28)

where
\psi_{Q}(\lambda)\triangleq\log\mathbb{E}_{Q}[\exp(\lambda\mathscr{L})]
and
\psi_{P}(\lambda)\triangleq\log\mathbb{E}_{P}[\exp(\lambda\mathscr{L})]
. Note that
\psi_{P}(\lambda)=\psi_{Q}(\lambda+1)
, and thus
E_{P}(\theta)=E_{Q}(\theta)-\theta
. In particular,
E_{P}
and
E_{Q}
are non-negative convex functions. Moreover, since
\psi^{\prime}_{Q}(0)=-d_{\mathsf{KL}}(Q_{XY}||P_{XY})
and
\psi^{\prime}_{Q}(1)=d_{\mathsf{KL}}(P_{XY}||Q_{XY})
, we have
E_{Q}(-d_{\mathsf{KL}}(Q_{XY}||P_{XY}))=E_{P}(d_{\mathsf{KL}}(P_{XY}||Q_{XY}))=0
and hence
E_{Q}(d_{\mathsf{KL}}(P_{XY}||Q_{XY}))=d_{\mathsf{KL}}(P_{XY}||Q_{XY})
and
E_{P}(-d_{\mathsf{KL}}(Q_{XY}||P_{XY}))=d_{\mathsf{KL}}(Q_{XY}||P_{XY})
. Finally, it is well known that
\lambda\theta-\psi_{Q}(\lambda)
is concave
and has derivative at zero given by
\theta+d_{\mathsf{KL}}(Q_{XY}||P_{XY})
. This implies that the maximizer
\lambda^{\star}
to the concave optimization
E_{Q}(\theta)
can be taken to be non-negative if
\theta\geq-d_{\mathsf{KL}}(Q_{XY}||P_{XY})
. The same is true for
E_{P}(\theta)
if
\theta\leq d_{\mathsf{KL}}(P_{XY}||Q_{XY})
.

Theorem 3

(GLRT strong detection)
.

Consider the GLRT in (
27
), and suppose there is a
\tau_{\mathsf{GLRT}}\in(-d_{\mathsf{KL}}(Q_{XY}||P_{XY}),d_{\mathsf{KL}}(P_{XY}||Q_{XY}))
with

\displaystyle E_{Q}(\tau_{\mathsf{GLRT}})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\log(n/e)}{d}+\frac{1+\log n}{dn}+\omega(d^{-1}n^{-1}),

(29a)

\displaystyle E_{P}(\tau_{\mathsf{GLRT}})

\displaystyle=\omega(d^{-1}n^{-1}).

(29b)

Then,
\mathsf{R}(\phi_{\mathsf{GLRT}})\to 0
, as
n,d\to\infty
, and
d=\omega(\log n)
.

As an alternative test for the GLRT, we propose also the following simple sum test,

\displaystyle\phi_{\mathsf{sum}}(\mathsf{X},\mathsf{Y})\triangleq\mathbbm{1}\left\{\frac{1}{dn^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}{\cal K}(X_{i\ell},Y_{j\ell})\geq\tau_{\mathsf{sum}}\right\},

(30)

where

\displaystyle{\cal K}(X_{i\ell},Y_{j\ell})

\displaystyle\triangleq\log\frac{P_{XY}(X_{i\ell},Y_{j\ell})}{Q_{XY}(X_{i\ell},Y_{j\ell})}-\mathbb{E}_{A\sim P_{X}}\left[\log\frac{P_{XY}(A,Y_{i\ell})}{Q_{XY}(A,Y_{i\ell})}\right]-\mathbb{E}_{B\sim P_{X}}\left[\log\frac{P_{XY}(X_{i\ell},B)}{Q_{XY}(X_{i\ell},B)}\right]

\displaystyle\quad-d_{\mathsf{KL}}(Q_{XY}||P_{XY}),

(31)

is the centered likelihood function, and
\tau_{\mathsf{sum}}\in\mathbb{R}
. In the Gaussian case (as well as in the Bernoulli case) it can be shown that (
30
) is equivalent to thresholding the sum of all entries of the inner product
\mathsf{X}^{T}\mathsf{Y}
, i.e.,
\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}X_{ij}Y_{ij}
. This test was analyzed in
[
KN22b
]
, in the Gaussian case. For the general case, we have the following result.

Theorem 4

(Sum test)
.

Consider the sum test in (
30
), and let

\displaystyle\tau_{\mathsf{sum}}=dn\cdot d_{\mathsf{SKL}}(P_{XY}||Q_{XY}).

(32)

Then,

\displaystyle\mathsf{R}(\phi_{\mathsf{sum}})\leq\frac{4\cdot\mathsf{Var}_{Q_{XY}}\left({\cal K}(A,B)\right)}{d\cdot d^{2}_{\mathsf{SKL}}(P_{XY}||Q_{XY})}.

(33)

In particular, if

\displaystyle d\cdot\frac{d^{2}_{\mathsf{SKL}}(P_{XY}||Q_{XY})}{\mathsf{Var}_{Q_{XY}}\left({\cal K}(A,B)\right)}=\omega(1),

(34)

then,
\mathsf{R}(\phi_{\mathsf{sum}})\to 0
, as
d\to\infty
.

Note that Theorem
4
implies also that weak detection is possible using the sum test if

\displaystyle d\cdot\frac{d^{2}_{\mathsf{SKL}}(P_{XY}||Q_{XY})}{\mathsf{Var}_{Q_{XY}}\left({\cal K}(A,B)\right)}=\Omega(1).

(35)

At this point, it is evident that based on the bounds in Theorems
3
and
4
, the GLRT and the sum test can achieve strong detection (vanishing risk) only if
d\to\infty
, namely, if
d=O(1)
, then the bounds on the risks associated with the GLRT and sum test are not vanishing as
n\to\infty
. Accordingly, pursuing for strong detection in the scenario where
d
is constant, we propose the following testing procedure,

\displaystyle\phi_{\mathsf{count}}(\mathsf{X},\mathsf{Y})\triangleq\mathbbm{1}\left\{\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}\left\{\frac{1}{d}\log\frac{P^{\otimes d}_{XY}(X_{i},Y_{j})}{Q^{\otimes d}_{XY}(X_{i},Y_{j})}\geq\tau_{\mathsf{count}}\right\}\geq\frac{1}{2}n{\cal P}_{d}\right\},

(36)

where
{\cal P}_{d}\triangleq\mathbb{P}_{P_{XY}^{\otimes d}}\left[\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}\mathscr{L}(A_{\ell},B_{\ell})\geq d\cdot\tau_{\mathsf{count}}\right]
, and
\tau_{\mathsf{count}}\in\mathbb{R}
. Roughly speaking,
\phi_{\mathsf{count}}
counts the number of pairs whose likelihood individually exceed a certain threshold. This is similar (but not exactly the same) to a test proposed in
[
EH23
]
in the Gaussian setting, which counts the number of inner products between all possible (normalized) rows in
\mathsf{X}
and
\mathsf{Y}
who individually exceed a certain threshold. We mention here that our result holds for any natural
d\geq 1
, while in
[
EH23
]
it is assumed that
d\geq d_{0}
, for some fixed
d_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
(most notably, excluding the interesting
d=1
case). We have the following result.

Theorem 5

(Count test strong detection)
.

Fix
d\in\mathbb{N}
, and consider the count test in (
36
). Suppose there is a
\tau_{\mathsf{count}}\in(-d_{\mathsf{KL}}(Q_{XY}||P_{XY}),d_{\mathsf{KL}}(P_{XY}||Q_{XY}))
with

\displaystyle E_{Q}(\tau_{\mathsf{count}})

\displaystyle=\omega\left(\log n^{1/d}\right),

(37a)

\displaystyle E_{P}(\tau_{\mathsf{count}})

\displaystyle=\omega(n^{-1}d^{-1}).

(37b)

Then,
\mathsf{R}(\phi_{\mathsf{count}})\to 0
, as
n\to\infty
.

As for the lower bounds, we provide examples for which we derive explicit formulas for the upper bounds above.

Example 3

(Gaussian databases)
.

Consider the same setting as in Example
1
, and let us find the corresponding Chernoff’s exponents in this case. Recall that the likelihood function is given in (
14
). Then,

\displaystyle\psi_{Q}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\log\mathbb{E}_{Q}\left[\frac{1}{(1-\rho^{2})^{\lambda/2}}\exp\left(\lambda\frac{-(x^{2}+y^{2})\rho^{2}+2xy\rho}{2(1-\rho^{2})}\right)\right]

(38)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\lambda}{2}\log(1-\rho^{2})+\log\mathbb{E}_{Q}\left[\exp\left(\lambda\frac{-(x^{2}+y^{2})\rho^{2}+2xy\rho}{2(1-\rho^{2})}\right)\right].

(39)

Using the fact that the moment generating function of Gaussian random variable
\mathsf{W}\sim{\cal N}(\mu,\sigma^{2})
squared is given by,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\exp\left(t\mathsf{W}^{2}\right)]=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-2t\sigma^{2}}}\exp\left(\frac{\mu^{2}t}{1-2t\sigma^{2}}\right),

(40)

for
\mathsf{Real}(t\sigma^{2})<1/2
, it can be shown that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{Q}\left[\exp\left(\lambda\frac{-(x^{2}+y^{2})\rho^{2}+2xy\rho}{2(1-\rho^{2})}\right)\right]=\frac{\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}}{\sqrt{1-(1-2\lambda)^{2}\rho^{2}}},

(41)

for
-\frac{1-\rho^{2}}{\rho^{2}}\leq\lambda
and
\lambda[1-(1-(1-\lambda)\rho^{2})^{-1}]<1
. Thus,

\displaystyle\psi_{Q}(\lambda)=-\frac{\lambda-1}{2}\log(1-\rho^{2})-\frac{1}{2}\log[1-(1-2\lambda^{2})\rho^{2}].

(42)

Let us take
\tau_{\mathsf{GLRT}}=0
. Then,

\displaystyle E_{Q}(0)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}-\psi_{Q}(\lambda)\geq-\psi_{Q}(1/2)=-\frac{1}{4}\log(1-\rho^{2}).

(43)

Therefore, we see that for (
29a
) to hold it is suffice that
d\rho^{2}=\omega(\log n)
. Similarly, it can be shown that

\displaystyle\psi_{P}(\lambda)=-\frac{\lambda}{2}\log(1-\rho^{2})-\frac{1}{2}\log(1-\lambda^{2}\rho^{2}),

(44)

for
-1/\rho^{2}<\lambda<1/\rho^{2}
. Then,

\displaystyle E_{P}(0)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}-\psi_{P}(\lambda)\geq-\psi_{P}(-1/2)=-\frac{1}{4}\log(1-\rho^{2})+\frac{1}{2}\log(1-\rho^{2}/4).

(45)

Accordingly, we see that for (
29b
) to hold it is suffice that
d\rho^{2}=\omega(n^{-1})
. Hence, in the Gaussian special case, for the GLRT to achieve strong detection it is suffice that
d\rho^{2}=\omega(\log n)
. In light of Example
1
, this is tight up to a
\log n
factor. Next, for the sum test in Theorem
4
, a straightforward calculation shows that

\displaystyle d_{\mathsf{KL}}(P_{XY}||Q_{XY})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\log(1-\rho^{2}),

(46)

\displaystyle d_{\mathsf{KL}}(Q_{XY}||P_{XY})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log(1-\rho^{2})+\frac{\rho^{2}}{1-\rho^{2}}.

(47)

Furthermore,

\displaystyle{\cal K}(A,B)=\frac{\rho}{1-\rho^{2}}\cdot AB,

(48)

and thus
\mathsf{Var}_{Q_{XY}}\left({\cal K}(A,B)\right)=\frac{\rho^{2}}{(1-\rho^{2})^{2}}
. Therefore, the condition in (
34
) boils down to
d\rho^{2}=\omega(1)
, which in light of Example
1
is tight up to a constant term. Finally, for fixed
d\in\mathbb{N}
, we see from (
37
) and (
43
) that a sufficient condition for the count test to achieve strong detection is
\rho^{2}=1-o(n^{-4/d})
, as
n\to\infty
. Interestingly, this bound coincided with the threshold for the recovery problem
[
DCK19
]
, achieved by the exhaustive maximum-likelihood estimator, while the count test is clearly efficient.

We can repeat the same calculations for the Bernoulli case. As an example, consider the sum test. Here, a straightforward calculation reveals that,

\displaystyle d_{\mathsf{KL}}(P_{XY}||Q_{XY})

\displaystyle=O(\rho^{2}),

(49)

\displaystyle d_{\mathsf{KL}}(Q_{XY}||P_{XY})

\displaystyle=O(\rho^{2}),

d\rho^{2}=\omega(1)
, which in light of Example
2
is tight up to a constant term. Similarly, when
d
is fixed it can be shown that the count test achieves strong detection if the same condition as in the Gaussian case holds.

4
Proofs of Lower Bounds

As is customary in lower bounds proofs for composite (worst-case) detection problems, it is convenient to reduce to a simple hypothesis testing problem by inducing a uniform prior on the parameter space. Specifically, let
\pi
be the uniform measure on
{\mathbb{S}}_{n}
, and let us denote by
{\mathbb{P}}_{{\cal H}_{1}}
the probability measure obtained by averaging
{\mathbb{P}}_{{\cal H}_{1}|\pi}
with respect to
\pi
. For a sequence of tests
\phi_{n,d}
, we define the average/Bayesian risk by,

\displaystyle\bar{\mathsf{R}}(\phi_{n,d})\triangleq{\mathbb{P}}_{{\cal H}_{0}}[\phi_{n,d}(\mathsf{X},\mathsf{Y})=1]+\mathbb{E}_{\sigma\sim\pi}\left[{\mathbb{P}}_{{\cal H}_{1}|\sigma}[\phi_{n,d}(\mathsf{X},\mathsf{Y})=0]\right],

(51)

and the optimal average risk,

\displaystyle\bar{\mathsf{R}}^{\star}\triangleq\inf_{\phi_{n,d}:{\cal X}^{n\times d}\times{\cal Y}^{n\times d}\to\{0,1\}}\bar{\mathsf{R}}(\phi_{n,d}).

(52)

Clearly, any test
\phi_{n,d}
satisfies
\mathsf{R}(\phi_{n,d})\geq\bar{\mathsf{R}}(\phi_{n,d})
, and therefore
\mathsf{R}^{\star}\geq\bar{\mathsf{R}}^{\star}
. We conclude that lower bounds on the average risk imply lower bounds on the worst-case risk. For example, in order to prove Theorem
1
, it is sufficient to find conditions for which
\bar{\mathsf{R}}^{\star}=1-o(1)
. In particular, using a well-known equivalent characterization of the Bayesian risk function by the total variation distance and Cauchy-Schwartz inequality, we have,

{\mathsf{R}}^{\star}\geq\bar{\mathsf{R}}^{\star}=1-d_{\mathsf{TV}}({\mathbb{P}}_{{\cal H}_{0}},{\mathbb{P}}_{{\cal H}_{1}})\geq 1-\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{{\cal H}_{0}}\left[\mathsf{L}_{n}^{2}\right]-1},

(53)

where
\mathsf{L}_{n}\triangleq\frac{{\mathbb{P}}_{{\cal H}_{1}}}{{\mathbb{P}}_{{\cal H}_{0}}}
is the likelihood ratio, and the expectation is taken with respect to
{\mathbb{P}}_{{\cal H}_{0}}
. Accordingly, using (
53
) we see that in order to prove that
({\cal D},d,n)
is not admissible for weak detection, it is sufficient to show that under the assumptions of Theorem
1
,
\mathbb{E}_{{\cal H}_{0}}\left[\mathsf{L}_{n}^{2}\right]\leq 1+o(1)
. For strong detection, on the other hand, recall that (see, for example,
[
Tsy04
, Lemmata 2.6 and 2.7]
),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\cal H}_{0}}[\mathsf{L}_{n}^{2}]=O(1)\implies d_{\mathsf{TV}}({\mathbb{P}}_{{\cal H}_{0}},{\mathbb{P}}_{{\cal H}_{1}})=1-\Omega(1),

(54)

which implies,

\mathsf{R}^{\star}\geq 1-d_{\mathsf{TV}}({\mathbb{P}}_{{\cal H}_{0}},{\mathbb{P}}_{{\cal H}_{1}})=\Omega(1),

(55)

and thus
({\cal D},d,n)
is not admissible for strong detection (as (
55
) implies
\limsup\mathsf{R}^{\star}>0
). Therefore, our main goal now is to analyze
\mathbb{E}_{{\cal H}_{0}}\left[\mathsf{L}_{n}^{2}\right]
.

4.1
A formula for the second moment

The first step in the calculation calls for a use of Ingster-Suslina method, stating that by Fubini’s theorem,
\mathbb{E}_{{\cal H}_{0}}[\mathsf{L}_{n}^{2}]
may be equivalently written as

\mathbb{E}_{{\cal H}_{0}}[\mathsf{L}_{n}^{2}]=\mathbb{E}_{\pi\perp\!\!\!\perp\pi^{\prime}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{{\cal H}_{0}}\left[\frac{{\mathbb{P}}_{{\cal H}_{1}|\pi}}{{\mathbb{P}}_{{\cal H}_{0}}}\cdot\frac{{\mathbb{P}}_{{\cal H}_{1}|\pi^{\prime}}}{{\mathbb{P}}_{{\cal H}_{0}}}\right]\right],

(56)

where the expectation is taken with respect to the independent coupling of
\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}
, two copies of the uniform measure on
{\mathbb{S}}_{n}
. Note that for fixed permutations
\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}
, in the discrete case, the distributions
{\mathbb{P}}_{{\cal H}_{1}|\pi},{\mathbb{P}}_{{\cal H}_{1}|\pi^{\prime}}
and
{\mathbb{P}}_{0}
, correspond to the underlying probability mass functions under the alternative and null hypotheses. In the continuous case,
{\mathbb{P}}_{{\cal H}_{1}|\pi},{\mathbb{P}}_{{\cal H}_{1}|\pi^{\prime}}
and
{\mathbb{P}}_{0}
are absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue’s measure on
\mathbb{R}^{2\times d\times n}
, and therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathbb{P}}_{{\cal H}_{1}|\pi}}{{\mathbb{P}}_{{\cal H}_{0}}}=\frac{f_{{\cal H}_{1}|\pi}}{f_{{\cal H}_{0}}},\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{{\mathbb{P}}_{{\cal H}_{1}|\pi^{\prime}}}{{\mathbb{P}}_{{\cal H}_{0}}}=\frac{f_{{\cal H}_{1}|\pi^{\prime}}}{f_{{\cal H}_{0}}},

(57)

where
f_{{\cal H}_{i}}
denotes the Radon-Nikodym derivative of
{\mathbb{P}}_{{\cal H}_{i}}
with respect to Lebesgue’s measure, which is the density function
(\mathsf{X},\mathsf{Y})
under the corresponding hypothesis. In the following, with some abuse of notations, we treat and use the same notations for both cases. Specifically, given
\pi,\pi^{\prime}
, we note that

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathbb{P}}_{{\cal H}_{1}|\pi}}{{\mathbb{P}}_{{\cal H}_{0}}}\cdot\frac{{\mathbb{P}}_{{\cal H}_{1}|\pi^{\prime}}}{{\mathbb{P}}_{{\cal H}_{0}}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathsf{L}(X_{i},Y_{\pi_{i}})\mathsf{L}(X_{i},Y_{\pi_{i}^{\prime}})\triangleq\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathsf{Z}_{i},

(58)

where

\displaystyle\mathsf{L}(X_{i},Y_{\pi_{i}})\triangleq\frac{P_{XY}^{\otimes d}(X_{i},Y_{\pi_{i}})}{Q_{XY}^{\otimes d}(X_{i},Y_{\pi_{i}})},

(59)

for any
i\in[n]
.
Thus, substituting (
56
) in (
58
), we get,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\cal H}_{0}}[\mathsf{L}_{n}^{2}]=\mathbb{E}_{\pi\perp\!\!\!\perp\pi^{\prime}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{{\cal H}_{0}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathsf{Z}_{i}\right)\right].

(60)

Next, we find an expression for the inner expectation in (
60
), for fixed permutations
\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}
. Observe that
\mathsf{Z}_{i}
’s may not be independent across different values of
i
. Nonetheless, using the notion of cyclic decomposition, we can decompose
\prod_{i}\mathsf{Z}_{i}
as a product over independent sets of random variables. To that end, define

Lemma 2
.

The largest eigenvalue of
{\cal L}
is 1.

Proof of Lemma
2
.

We first prove the result for the case where
P_{XY}
and
Q_{XY}
are probability mass functions, i.e., the datasets
\mathsf{X}
and
\mathsf{Y}
are discrete and take values over
{\cal X}
and
{\cal Y}
, respectively. The proof for the continuous case, where
P_{XY}
and
Q_{XY}
are probability density functions, is similar. Note that for any square-integrable function
f
under
Q_{Y}
,

\displaystyle({\cal L}f)(x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim Q_{Y}}\left[{\cal L}(x,Y)f(Y)\right]

(74)

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in{\cal Y}}\frac{P_{XY}(x,y)}{Q_{X}(x)Q_{Y}(y)}f(y)Q(y)

(75)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Q_{X}(x)}\sum_{y\in{\cal Y}}f(y)P_{XY}(x,y).

(76)

Thus, the eigenvalues of
{\cal L}
are given by the eigenvalues of the following
|{\cal X}|\times|{\cal Y}|
row-stochastic matrix

\mathbf{M}(x,y)\triangleq\frac{P_{XY}(x,y)}{Q_{X}(x)}
. Since
\mathbf{M}
is a row-stochastic matrix its largest eigenvalue is
1
. Indeed, let
\mathbf{1}
be the all one vector. Then, note that
\mathbf{M}\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{1}
, and thus
1
is an eigenvalue of
\mathbf{M}
. To show that the this is the largest eigenvalue we use Gershgorin circle theorem. Specifically, take row
\ell
in
\mathbf{M}
. The diagonal element will be
\mathbf{M}_{\ell\ell}
and the radius will be
\sum_{i\neq\ell}|\mathbf{M}_{\ell i}|=\sum_{i\neq\ell}\mathbf{M}_{\ell i}
since all
\mathbf{M}_{\ell i}\geq 0
. This will be a circle with its center in
\mathbf{M}_{\ell\ell}\in[0,1]
, and a radius of
\sum_{i\neq\ell}\mathbf{M}_{\ell i}=1-\mathbf{M}_{\ell\ell}
. So, this circle will have
1
on its perimeter. This is true for all Gershgorin circles for this matrix (since
\ell
was chosen arbitrarily). Thus, since all eigenvalues lie in the union of the Gershgorin circles, all eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}
satisfy
|\lambda_{i}|\leq 1
.

In the continuous case, we use similar arguments. Specifically, let
\lambda
be an eigenvalue of
{\cal L}
with corresponding eigenfunction
f_{\lambda}
. Let
x^{\star}=\arg\max_{x\in{\cal X}}|f_{\lambda}(x)|
. Because
f_{\lambda}
is an eigenfunction, we have

\displaystyle({\cal L}f_{\lambda})(x)=\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim Q_{Y}}\left[{\cal L}(x,Y)f_{\lambda}(Y)\right]=\lambda\cdot f_{\lambda}(x),

(77)

for any
x\in{\cal X}
. Substituting
x=x^{\star}
in (
77
) and isolating
\lambda
, we get

\displaystyle|\lambda|

\displaystyle=\frac{\left|\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim Q_{Y}}\left[{\cal L}(x,Y)f_{\lambda}(Y)\right]\right|}{|f_{\lambda}(x^{\star})|}

(78)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim Q_{Y}}\left[{\cal L}(x,Y)\left|\frac{f_{\lambda}(Y)}{f_{\lambda}(x^{\star})}\right|\right]

(79)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim Q_{Y}}\left[{\cal L}(x,Y)\right]=1,

(80)

where the first inequality follows from the triangle inequality, the second inequality is because
\left|\frac{f_{\lambda}(x)}{f_{\lambda}(x^{\star})}\right|\leq 1
, for any
x\in{\cal X}
, and the last equality follows by the definition of
{\cal L}
. Since
\lambda
is arbitrary, the above arguments hold for any eigenvalue of
{\cal L}
. Finally, note that the identity eigenfunction, i.e.,
f(x)=1
, for any
x\in{\cal X}
, corresponds to
\lambda=1
.

\displaystyle\mathsf{cov}_{Q_{XY}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\cal K}(A_{i},B_{j}),\sum_{m=1}^{n}{\cal K}(A_{k},B_{m})\right)=0.

(219)

Combining (
204
), (
205
), (
215
), and (
219
), we get,

\displaystyle\mathsf{Var}_{Q_{XY}}\left(\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}{\cal K}(X_{i\ell},Y_{j\ell})\right)=dn^{2}\cdot\mathsf{Var}_{Q_{XY}}\left({\cal K}(A,B)\right).

(220)

Taking
\tau_{\mathsf{sum}}=dn\frac{d_{\mathsf{KL}}(P_{XY}||Q_{XY})+d_{\mathsf{KL}}(Q_{XY}||P_{XY})}{2}
, we obtain from (
203
),

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{0}}\left[\phi_{\mathsf{sum}}(\mathsf{X},\mathsf{Y})=1\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4\cdot\mathsf{Var}_{Q_{XY}}\left({\cal K}(A,B)\right)}{d\cdot\left(d_{\mathsf{KL}}(P_{XY}||Q_{XY})+d_{\mathsf{KL}}(Q_{XY}||P_{XY})\right)^{2}}.

(221)

Next, we analyze the Type-II error probability. As in the GLRT analysis, under
{\cal H}_{1}
, since our proposed test is invariant to reordering of
\mathsf{X}
and
\mathsf{Y}
, we may assume without loss of generality that the latent permutation is the identity one, i.e.,
\sigma=\mathsf{Id}
. Then, by Chebyshev’s inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{1}}\left[\phi_{\mathsf{sum}}(\mathsf{X},\mathsf{Y})=0\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{1}}\left[\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}{\cal K}(X_{i\ell},Y_{j\ell})<\tau_{\mathsf{sum}}\right]

(222)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathsf{Var}_{P_{XY}}\left(\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}{\cal K}(X_{i\ell},Y_{j\ell})\right)}{\left[dn\cdot(d_{\mathsf{KL}}(P_{XY}||Q_{XY})+d_{\mathsf{KL}}(Q_{XY}||P_{XY}))-\tau_{\mathsf{sum}}\right]^{2}}

(223)

\displaystyle=\frac{4\cdot\mathsf{Var}_{P_{XY}}\left(\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}{\cal K}(X_{i\ell},Y_{j\ell})\right)}{d^{2}n^{2}\cdot\left(d_{\mathsf{KL}}(P_{XY}||Q_{XY})+d_{\mathsf{KL}}(Q_{XY}||P_{XY})\right)^{2}}.

As before, let us find the variance term in (

\displaystyle\mathsf{Var}_{P_{XY}}\left(\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}{\cal K}(X_{i\ell},Y_{j\ell})\right)=d\cdot\mathsf{Var}_{P_{XY}}\left(\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}{\cal K}(A_{i},B_{j})\right),

where each
\{(A_{i},B_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
are i.i.d. pairs of random variables distributed according to
P_{XY}
. Now, we have,

\displaystyle\mathsf{Var}_{P_{XY}}\left(\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}{\cal K}(A_{i},B_{j})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathsf{Var}_{P_{XY}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\cal K}(A_{i},B_{j})\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt+\sum_{i\neq k}\mathsf{cov}_{P_{XY}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\cal K}(A_{i},B_{j}),\sum_{m=1}^{n}{\cal K}(A_{k},B_{m})\right).

(226)

Let us start with the first term at the right-hand-side of (

). We get,

\displaystyle\mathsf{Var}_{P_{XY}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\cal K}(A_{i},B_{j})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathsf{Var}_{P_{XY}}\left({\cal K}(A_{i},B_{j})\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt+\sum_{j\neq m}\mathsf{cov}_{P_{XY}}\left({\cal K}(A_{i},B_{j}),{\cal K}(A_{i},B_{m})\right)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{Var}_{P_{XY}}\left({\cal K}(A,B)\right)+(n-1)\cdot\mathsf{Var}_{Q_{XY}}\left({\cal K}(A,B)\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt+\sum_{j\neq m}\mathsf{cov}_{P_{XY}}\left({\cal K}(A_{i},B_{j}),{\cal K}(A_{i},B_{m})\right).

(228)

Let us find
\mathsf{cov}_{P_{XY}}\left({\cal K}(A_{i},B_{j}),{\cal K}(A_{i},B_{m})\right)
, for the different possible values of
(i,j,m)
. Without loss of generality, we fix
i=1
. For
j=1
and
m\neq 1

\displaystyle\mathsf{cov}_{P_{XY}}\left({\cal K}(A_{1},B_{1}),{\cal K}(A_{1},B_{m})\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{P_{XY}}\left[{\cal K}(A_{1},B_{1})\cdot{\cal K}(A_{1},B_{m})\right]

(229)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{P_{XY}}\left[{\cal K}(A_{1},B_{1})\mathbb{E}_{Q_{XY}}[{\cal K}(A_{1},B_{m})|A_{1}]\right]

(230)

\displaystyle=0,

(231)

where we have used the fact that
A_{1}
and
B_{m}
are statistically independent and thus
\mathbb{E}_{Q_{XY}}[{\cal K}(A_{1},B_{m})|A_{1}]=0
by definition. By symmetry, the above is true also when
m=1
and
j\neq 1
. Finally, for the case where
j,m\neq 1

\displaystyle\mathsf{cov}_{P_{XY}}\left({\cal K}(A_{1},B_{j}),{\cal K}(A_{1},B_{m})\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{P_{XY}}\left[{\cal K}(A_{1},B_{j})\mathbb{E}[{\cal K}(A_{1},B_{m})|A_{1}]\right]

\displaystyle=0.

(234)

Thus,

\displaystyle\mathsf{Var}_{P_{XY}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\cal K}(A_{i},B_{j})\right)

(235)

Next, consider the second term at the right-hand-side of (
226
). For
i\neq k

\displaystyle\mathsf{cov}_{P_{XY}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\cal K}(A_{i},B_{j}),\sum_{m=1}^{n}{\cal K}(A_{k},B_{m})\right)=\sum_{j,m=1}^{n}\mathsf{cov}_{P_{XY}}\left({\cal K}(A_{i},B_{j}),{\cal K}(A_{k},B_{m})\right).

(236)

We split the sum above into two parts; the first correspond to
j=m

j\neq m
. For the former, we have,

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{n}\mathsf{cov}_{P_{XY}}\left({\cal K}(A_{i},B_{m}),{\cal K}(A_{k},B_{m})\right)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{cov}_{P_{XY}}\left({\cal K}(A_{i},B_{i}),{\cal K}(A_{k},B_{i})\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\mathsf{cov}_{P_{XY}}\left({\cal K}(A_{i},B_{k}),{\cal K}(A_{k},B_{k})\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{m\neq i,k}\mathsf{cov}_{P_{XY}}\left({\cal K}(A_{i},B_{m}),{\cal K}(A_{k},B_{m})\right).

(237)

We have,

\displaystyle\mathsf{cov}_{P_{XY}}\left({\cal K}(A_{i},B_{i}),{\cal K}(A_{k},B_{i})\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{P_{XY}}\left({\cal K}(A_{i},B_{i})\cdot{\cal K}(A_{k},B_{i})\right)

(238)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{P_{XY}}\left[{\cal K}(A_{i},B_{i})\cdot\mathbb{E}_{Q_{XY}}\left(\left.{\cal K}(A_{k},B_{i})\right|B_{i}\right)\right]

(239)

\displaystyle=0,

(240)

where we have used the fact that
A_{k}
and
B_{i}
are statistically independent. Similarly, it is clear that
\mathsf{cov}_{P_{XY}}\left({\cal K}(A_{i},B_{k}),{\cal K}(A_{k},B_{k})\right)=0

\mathsf{cov}_{P_{XY}}\left({\cal K}(A_{i},B_{m}),{\cal K}(A_{k},B_{m})\right)=0
. Thus,

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{n}\mathsf{cov}_{P_{XY}}\left({\cal K}(A_{i},B_{m}),{\cal K}(A_{k},B_{m})\right)=0.

(241)

Now, for
j\neq m
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{j\neq m}^{n}\mathsf{cov}_{P_{XY}}\left({\cal K}(A_{i},B_{j}),{\cal K}(A_{k},B_{m})\right)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{cov}_{P_{XY}}\left({\cal K}(A_{i},B_{i}),{\cal K}(A_{k},B_{k})\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\mathsf{cov}_{P_{XY}}\left({\cal K}(A_{i},B_{k}),{\cal K}(A_{k},B_{i})\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{(j,m)\in{\cal G}}\mathsf{cov}_{P_{XY}}\left({\cal K}(A_{i},B_{j}),{\cal K}(A_{k},B_{m})\right),

(242)

where
{\cal G}\triangleq\{(j,m)\in[n]\times[n]:j\neq m,(j,m)\neq(i,k),(j,m)\neq(k,i)\}
. Now, since
\{(A_{i},B_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
are i.i.d. pairs of random variables, we have,

\displaystyle\mathsf{cov}_{P_{XY}}\left({\cal K}(A_{i},B_{i}),{\cal K}(A_{k},B_{k})\right)=0.

(243)

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\mathsf{cov}_{P_{XY}}\left({\cal K}(A_{i},B_{k}),{\cal K}(A_{k},B_{i})\right)=\mathbb{E}_{P_{XY}}\left[{\cal K}(A_{i},B_{k})\cdot{\cal K}(A_{k},B_{i})\right].

242
) for any pair
(j,m)\in{\cal G}
. For any
j\neq i,k
and
m\neq i,k
, we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{cov}_{P_{XY}}\left({\cal K}(A_{i},B_{j}),{\cal K}(A_{k},B_{m})\right)=0,

because the pairs
(A_{i},B_{j})
and
(A_{k},B_{m})
are statistically independent. Next, for
j=i
and
m\neq k
, we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{cov}_{P_{XY}}\left({\cal K}(A_{i},B_{i}),{\cal K}(A_{k},B_{m})\right)

\displaystyle=0,

(246)

because the pairs
(A_{i},B_{i})
and
(A_{k},B_{m})
are statistically independent and
\mathbb{E}_{P_{XY}}[{\cal K}(A_{k},B_{m})]=\mathbb{E}_{Q_{XY}}[{\cal K}(A_{k},B_{m})]=0
because
A_{k}
and
B_{m}
are independent. The same is true for
m=k
and
j\neq i
. For
j=k
and
m\neq i
, we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{cov}_{P_{XY}}\left({\cal K}(A_{i},B_{k}),{\cal K}(A_{k},B_{m})\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{P_{XY}}\left[{\cal K}(A_{i},B_{k})\cdot{\cal K}(A_{k},B_{m})\right]

(247)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{P_{XY}}\left[{\cal K}(A_{i},B_{k})\cdot\mathbb{E}_{P_{XY}}\left(\left.{\cal K}(A_{k},B_{m})\right|A_{i},B_{k},A_{k}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{P_{XY}}\left[{\cal K}(A_{i},B_{k})\cdot\mathbb{E}_{P_{XY}}\left(\left.{\cal K}(A_{k},B_{m})\right|A_{k}\right)\right]

(249)

\displaystyle=0,

(250)

A_{i}
and

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{{\cal H}_{1}}\left(\phi_{\mathsf{count}}=0\right)

6
Conclusions and Outlook

In this paper, we analyzed the problem of detecting the correlation of dependent databases with general distributions. To that end, we derived both lower and upper bounds on the phase transition boundaries at which detection is possible or impossible. Our bounds are tight in the special cases considered in the literature. We hope our work has opened more doors than it closes. Let us mention briefly a few interesting questions for future research:

1.

In this paper we assume that both generative distributions
P_{XY}
and
Q_{XY}
are known to the learner. In practice, it would be interesting to devise universal algorithms which are independent of
P_{XY}
and
Q_{XY}
, but at the same time achieve comparable results to the case where those distributions are known. Furthermore, it would be interesting to devise adaptive probing detection algorithms for scenarios where one is able to probe only part of the data, while the complete databases are not given/expensive to obtain. Finally, it would be interesting to construct differentially private algorithms.

2.

We focused on the case where the data is i.i.d. given the permutation. However, in practice, it is reasonable that there will be some structured dependency over, for example, the feature space. As so, it would be interesting to generalize our results to a model which captures such a dependency. Furthermore, we assumed that the permutation is drawn uniformly over
\mathbb{S}_{n}
. What if there is a different prior, or, a structured set from which the permutation is drawn? As an example, consider the case where there are groups of users with similar features, and the permutation is applied per each group.

3.

We investigated the question of “independent vs. dependent” databases. It would be interesting to analyze the related, but easier, question of “uncorrelated vs. correlated”? Obviously, two databases can be dependent but uncorrelated. Namely, this is a question about first order dependency.

4.

It would be interesting to check whether the techniques in this paper can be used to solve the graph matching detection problem in
[
WXY23
]
, for general weight distributions.
