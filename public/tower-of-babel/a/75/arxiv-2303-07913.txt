Rigorous derivation of Michaelis-Menten kinetics in the presence of diffusion Thanks: Corresponding author.
Bao Quoc Tang, Bao-Ngoc Tran

Abstract
Reactions with enzymes are critical in biochemistry, where the enzymes act as catalysis in the process. One of the most used mechanisms for modeling enzyme-catalyzed reactions is the Michaelis-Menten (MM) kinetic. In the ODE level, i.e. concentrations are only on time-dependent, this kinetic can be rigorously derived from mass action law using quasi-steady-state approximation. This issue in the PDE setting, for instance when molecular diffusion is taken into account, is considerably more challenging and only formal derivations have been established. In this paper, we prove this derivation rigorously and obtain MM kinetic in the presence of spatial diffusion. In particular, we show that, in general, the reduced problem is a cross-diffusion-reaction system. Our proof is based on improved duality method, heat regularisation and a suitable modified energy function. To the best of our knowledge, this work provides the first rigorous derivation of MM kinetic from mass action kinetic in the PDE setting.



Key words—
Enzyme reactions; Michaelis-Menten kinetics; Improved duality method; Modified energy method; Cross-diffusion

1.2
State of the art

The rigorous derivation of MM kinetic in the case homogeneous setting, i.e. for the differential system (
1
), has been extensively investigated in the literature. Different conditions are proposed to validate the MM kinetic, for instance, when small initial enzyme concentrations are, or when fast reaction rate constants are large, see e.g.
[
SS89
]
. It is also worth noting that this research direction belongs to a developed theory of fast-slow systems or multi-time scale dynamics (see e.g.
[
Kue15
]
).

The singular limit as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
of (
1.1
) falls into the problem of fast reaction limits for PDE, which has caught a lot of attention in the past decades. Studies in this direction go back to the works of Evans
[
Eva80
]
and Martin
[
MJ80
]
in the eighties where the former showed the convergence to a nonlinear diffusion problem while the latter proved the convergence to a Stefan free boundary type problem. Already these
two works suggested interesting mathematical structures as well as complexity of fast reaction limits of PDE. Indeed, extensive studies on the subject showed that fast reaction limit leads to many different and interesting
limiting systems, ranging from nonlinear diffusion equations
[
BH03
,
Eva80
]
, Stefan free boundary problems
[
BP12
,
MN11
]
, cross-diffusion systems
[
BPR12
,
DT15
,
DDJ20
,
BCDK21
]
, to a new derivation of the classical dynamical boundary conditions

[
HT16
]
, various behaviour of moving interfaces
[
IMMN17
]
, fractional kinetics
[
CDS18
,
DS19
]
, or systems involving Young measures
[
PS22
]
. On the one hand, this variety of limiting dynamics
shows the close connection of fast reaction limits of (bio-)chemical models to other phenomena of
dynamical systems depending on different scales and situations. For instance, the classical dynamical
boundary condition for parabolic equations, which has its root in modelling heat conduction in solids
(when a solid is in contact with a well-stirred fluid at its surface), can be rigorously derived as
a limit of a volume-surface reaction-diffusion system in which the reaction rate between the volume and surface-concentrations tends to infinity
[
HT16
]
. Another example is the famous SKT cross-diffusion
system, named after Shigesada, Kawasaki, and Teramoto
[
SKT79
]
, can be derived as a formal limit of
a reaction-diffusion system
[
IMN06
]
. On the other hand, the analysis of reduced models can
benefit from viewing it as the limit of a model which possesses useful structures. For instance, in
[
DDJ20
]

a study of fast reaction limit leads to a reduced algebraic-cross-diffusion system whose analysis seem impenetrable
at the first glance, but becomes feasible thanks to the entropic structure of the original system, which
is propagated via the fast reaction limit.

Our present paper contributes to this literature by showing that the classical MM kinetics for enzyme reaction in the presence of diffusion can also be rigorously derived from mass action kinetics by studying a fast reaction limit type problem using a suitable rescaling. We expect that our results have considerable applications, especially in deriving MM kinetics for (bio-)chemical catalytic reactions.

Theorem 2.2
.

Fix

,

the system (

Proof.

It is obvious that the nonlinearities
f_{j}:\mathbb{R}_{+}^{4}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

j=1,\ldots,4
, are locally Lipschitz continuous and have at most quadratic growth. Therefore, by standard fixed-point-arguments, there exists only a local solution
u^{\varepsilon}\in C([0,T_{\max});L^{p}(\Omega))^{4}
,
N/2<p\leq q
, on a maximal interval
[0,T_{\max})
to the integral system

\displaystyle u_{j}^{\varepsilon}(x,t)=e^{td_{j}\Delta}u_{j0}(x)+\int_{0}^{t}e^{(t-s)d_{j}\Delta}f_{j}(u^{\varepsilon}(x,s))ds,\quad j=1,...,4,

(30)

such that

\displaystyle T_{\max}=\infty\quad\text{or}\quad\lim_{t\rightarrow T_{\max}^{-}}\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)^{4}}=\infty\text{ if }T_{\max}<\infty.

(31)

The solution preserves positivity since

,

, are quasi-positive, i.e.

f_{j}(u^{\varepsilon})\geq 0
for all

with

. Moreover, thanks to smoothing effects of the Neumann heat semigroup,

\sum_{j=1}^{4}f_{j}(u^{\varepsilon})\leq l_{1}u^{\varepsilon}_{3}+k_{2}u^{\varepsilon}_{3}\leq\max\{l_{1},k_{2}\}\sum_{j=1}^{4}u^{\varepsilon}_{j},

we can apply
[

]

2.2
Heat regularisation and improved duality method

We first state the classical regularisation by the heat operator, which involves solutions to the following inhomogeneous heat equation

(\mathbf{H}_{d}(u_{0},f)):\quad\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u-d\Delta u=f,&\text{ in }Q_{T},\\
\partial_{\nu}u=0,&\text{ on }\partial\Omega\times(0,T),\\
u(x,0)=u_{0}(x),&\text{ in }\Omega.\end{cases}

Lemma 2.4

(
[
Lam87
]
, Theorem 1)
.

Let
1<q<\infty
. Assume that
f\in L^{q}(Q_{T})
and let
u
be the weak solution to problem
(\mathbf{H}_{1}(0,f))
. Then there is an optimal constant
C_{q}^{\mathsf{MR}}
depending only on
q
, the dimension
N
, and the domain
\Omega
, such that

\|\Delta u\|_{L^{q}(Q_{T})}\leq C_{q}^{\mathsf{MR}}\|f\|_{L^{q}(Q_{T})},

(32)

where the superscript
\mathsf{MR}
indicates the maximal regularity property.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
1<q<\infty
and
d>0
. Assume that
f\in L^{q}(Q_{T})
,
u_{0}\in W^{2,q}(\Omega)
, and
u
be the weak solution to the problem
(\mathbf{H}_{d}(u_{0},f))
. Then, for

\displaystyle p=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lllllll}\dfrac{(N+2)q}{N+2-2q}&\text{if }q<\frac{N+2}{2},\\
\in[1,\infty)\text{ arbitrary}&\text{if }q=\frac{N+2}{2},\\
\infty&\text{if }q>\frac{N+2}{2},\end{array}\right.\quad\text{and}\quad r=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lllllll}\dfrac{(N+2)q}{N+2-q}&\text{if }q<N+2,\\
\in[1,\infty)\text{ arbitrary}&\text{if }q=N+2,\\
\infty&\text{if }q>N+2,\end{array}\right.

there holds

\displaystyle\|u\|_{L^{p}(Q_{T})}+\|\nabla u\|_{L^{r}(Q_{T})}+\|\partial_{t}u\|_{L^{q}(Q_{T})}+\|\Delta u\|_{L^{q}(Q_{T})}

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt\leq C_{1}C_{q,N,\Omega,T}\|f\|_{L^{q}(Q_{T})}+C_{2}C_{q,N,\Omega,T}\|u_{0}\|_{W^{2,q}(\Omega)},

(33)

where
C_{q,N,\Omega,T}
depends only on
q,N,\Omega,T
, continuously depends on
T
, and remains bounded for finite values of
T>0
, and

C_{1}:=(C_{q}^{\mathsf{MR}}+1)(1/d+1+T),\quad C_{2}:=T^{1/q}(2+d+dT).

Proof.

By the substitutions
s=dt
,
\widetilde{u}(s,\cdot)=u(s/d,\cdot)
,
\widetilde{f}(s,\cdot)=f(s/d,\cdot)
, the equation
(\mathbf{H}_{d}(u_{0},f))
of
u
becomes the equation
(\mathbf{H}_{1}(u_{0},\widetilde{f}/d))
of
\widetilde{u}
. Let us consider the decomposition
\widetilde{u}=\widetilde{v}+\widetilde{w}
, where
\widetilde{v}
and
\widetilde{w}
are the solutions to
(\mathbf{H}_{1}(0,\widetilde{f}/d))
and
(\mathbf{H}_{1}(u_{0},0))
, respectively.
By Lemma
2.4
,

\displaystyle\|\Delta\widetilde{v}\|_{L^{q}(Q_{dT})}\leq C_{q}^{\mathsf{MR}}\|\widetilde{f}/d\|_{L^{q}(Q_{dT})},

(34)

On the other hand, since the heat semigroup corresponding to the homogeneous Neumann boundary condition is a contraction semigroup on
L^{q}(\Omega)
, we have

\displaystyle\|\Delta\widetilde{w}(s)\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}=\|\Delta e^{s\Delta}u_{0}\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}=\|e^{s\Delta}\Delta u_{0}\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}\leq\|\Delta u_{0}\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)},

(35)

for all
s\in(0,dT)
, where we note that
e^{s\Delta}
and
\Delta
are commute on
W^{2,q}(\Omega)
since
\{e^{s\Delta}:s\geq 0\}
is a continuous semigroup.
Combining the estimates (
34
) and (
35
) gives

\displaystyle\|\Delta\widetilde{u}\|_{L^{q}(Q_{dT})}\leq C_{q}^{\mathsf{MR}}\|\widetilde{f}/d\|_{L^{q}(Q_{dT})}+(dT)^{\frac{1}{q}}\|\Delta u_{0}\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)},

which is equivalent to

\displaystyle\|\Delta u\|_{L^{q}(Q_{T})}\leq\,

\displaystyle\,C_{q}^{\mathsf{MR}}\|f/d\|_{L^{q}(Q_{T})}+T^{\frac{1}{q}}\|\Delta u_{0}\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}.

and so the equation
(\mathbf{H}_{d}(u_{0},f))
gives

\|\partial_{t}u\|_{L^{q}(Q_{T})}\leq(C_{q}^{\mathsf{MR}}+1)\|f\|_{L^{q}(Q_{T})}+dT^{\frac{1}{q}}\|\Delta u_{0}\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}.

Therefore, by the Hölder inequality,

\displaystyle\|u\|_{L^{q}(Q_{T})}\leq T^{\frac{1}{q}}\|u_{0}\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}+T\|\partial_{t}u\|_{L^{q}(Q_{T})},

and consequently
u\in W^{2,1}_{q}(Q_{T})
with respect to

\displaystyle\|u\|_{W^{2,1}_{q}(Q_{T})}\leq\,

\displaystyle\,(C_{q}^{\mathsf{MR}}+1)(1/d+1+T)\|f\|_{L^{q}(Q_{T})}+T^{1/q}(2+d+dT)\|u_{0}\|_{W^{2,q}(\Omega)}.

(36)

Finally, by applying interpolation inequalities with different spaces of functions (on
\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
) depending on
x
and
t
as in
[
LSU88
, Lemma 3.3]
, there exists a constant
C_{q,N,\Omega,T}
satisfying (
33
).
∎

Remark 2.6
.

At the first glance, the above lemma looks similarly as
[
LSU88
, Corollary of Theorem 9.1]
.
We emphasise, however, that the regularity (

) shows how the constants depend on

, and especially on

. This is crucial to estimate solutions of reaction-diffusion equations with diffusion coefficients

,

, which depend on

. There are similar estimates as (

) in
[
LSU88
, Corollary of Theorem 9.1]
and Cañizo-Desvillettes-Fellner
[

, where the dependence on

was stated.

We will utilise the following improved duality lemma.

Lemma 2.7

(Improved duality estimate,
[
CDF14
,
EMT20
]
)
.

Let
T>0
,
1<q<\infty
,
K\in\mathbb{R}
and
q^{\prime}=q/(q-1)
be Hölder conjugate exponent of
q
. Assume that
X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}
,
m\geq 2
, are nonnegative, smooth functions satisfying the relation

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{lllllll}\displaystyle\partial_{t}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}X_{i}\right)&\leq&\Delta\left(\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\kappa_{i}X_{i}\right)+{K}\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}X_{i}&\text{in }Q_{T},\\
\partial_{\nu}X_{i}&=&0&\text{on }\Gamma_{T},\\
X_{i}(0,x)&=&X_{i,0}(x)&\text{in }\Omega,\end{array}\right.

for some constants
\kappa_{i}>0
,
i=1,\ldots,m
. Let
\kappa_{\min}=\min\{\kappa_{i}\}
and
\kappa_{\max}=\max\{\kappa_{i}\}
. If

\displaystyle C_{q^{\prime}}^{\mathsf{MR}}\frac{|\kappa_{\max}-\kappa_{\min}|}{\kappa_{\max}+\kappa_{\min}}<1,

(40)

and
\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}X_{i,0}\in L^{q}(\Omega)
, then

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\|X_{i}\right\|_{L^{q}(Q_{T})}\leq C(T)\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{m}X_{i,0}\right\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)},

(41)

where

depends continuously on
T
and on
\kappa_{\min}
,
\kappa_{\max}
.

Proof.

With the change of variable
\widetilde{X}_{i}(x,t)=e^{-Kt}X_{i}(x,t)
we have

\partial_{t}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\widetilde{X}_{i}\right)\leq\Delta\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\kappa_{i}\widetilde{X}_{i}\right).

This allows us to apply the same arguments in
[
EMT20
, Lemma 3.9]
to obtain, under the condition (

),

\sum_{i=1}^{m}\|\widetilde{X}_{i}\|_{L^{q}(Q_{T})}\leq C(T)\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{m}\widetilde{X}_{i}(\cdot,0)\right\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}

which implies the desired estimates for

in (

on
\kappa_{\max}
and
\kappa_{\min}
as they are needed afterwards.
∎

2.3
A modified energy function

By observing from the equations of
u_{2}^{\varepsilon}
and
u_{3}^{\varepsilon}
in (
1.1
), it is natural to expect the convergence to the critical manifold, i.e.

\mathcal{M}(u^{\varepsilon}):=(k_{1}u_{1}^{\varepsilon}+l_{2}u_{4}^{\varepsilon})u_{2}^{\varepsilon}-(k_{2}+l_{1})u_{3}^{\varepsilon}\rightarrow 0

in a suitable (preferably strong) topology. This turns out to be a subtle issue, especially in the case where the diffusion coefficients
d_{2}
and
d_{3}
are far away from each other. In the following, we look at the convergence of a perturbed critical manifold

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{\alpha}(u^{\varepsilon}):=(k_{1}u_{1}^{\varepsilon}+l_{2}u_{4}^{\varepsilon}+\alpha(\varepsilon))u_{2}^{\varepsilon}-(k_{2}+l_{1})u_{3}^{\varepsilon}\rightarrow 0,

(42)

where
\alpha=\alpha(\varepsilon)>0
will be chosen later such that
\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\alpha(\varepsilon)=0
. Obviously, the strong convergence of
\mathcal{M}(u^{\varepsilon})
follows from the convergence of
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{\alpha}(u^{\varepsilon})
once
u^{\varepsilon}_{2}
is bounded in a certain norm. For the sake of simplicity, we denote by

A_{2}:=k_{1}u_{1}^{\varepsilon}+l_{2}u_{4}^{\varepsilon}\quad\text{and}\quad A_{3}:=k_{2}+l_{1}.

Note that
A_{2}
depends on
\varepsilon>0
while
A_{3}
is a constant independent of
\varepsilon
. For
t>0
, we define the following function

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{\varepsilon}(t):=\int_{\Omega}((A_{2}+\alpha)u_{2}^{\varepsilon})^{p-1}u_{2}^{\varepsilon}+\int_{\Omega}(A_{3}u_{3}^{\varepsilon})^{p-1}u_{3}^{\varepsilon}=:\mathcal{H}_{2}^{\varepsilon}(t)+\mathcal{H}_{3}^{\varepsilon}(t),\quad p>1.

(43)

By differentiating this function with respect to the temporal variable, and employing the structure of solutions of the problem (
1.1
), we obtain a-priori estimates in the following lemma, which are crucial to derive the limit (
42
) and also gradient estimates.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
and
u^{\varepsilon}
be the solution of (

).

(a)

Let
p\in[2,\infty)
and
\alpha=0
. Then, there exists

such that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\iint_{Q_{T}}\big|A_{2}u_{2}^{\varepsilon}-A_{3}u_{3}^{\varepsilon}\big|^{p}+\sum_{j=2,3}\iint_{Q_{T}}A_{j}^{p-1}(u_{j}^{\varepsilon})^{p-2}|\nabla u_{j}^{\varepsilon}|^{2}

(44)

\displaystyle\leq C\bigg(1+\iint_{Q_{T}}(u_{2}^{\varepsilon})^{p}\Big[A_{2}^{p-2}\partial_{t}A_{2}+A_{2}^{p-3}|\nabla\hskip-1.42271ptA_{2}|^{2}\Big]\bigg).

(b)

Let
p\in(1,2]
and
\alpha=\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{4-p}}
. Then, there exists

for all

. Due to the boundedness of
\{\nabla u^{\varepsilon}_{2}\}
and
\{\nabla u^{\varepsilon}_{3}\}
in
L^{2}(Q_{T})
, we obtain from
\partial_{t}v^{\varepsilon}=d^{\varepsilon}_{2}\Delta v^{\varepsilon}+(d^{\varepsilon}_{3}-d^{\varepsilon}_{2})\Delta u^{\varepsilon}_{3}
that
\{v^{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon}
is relatively compact in
L^{2}(Q_{T})
. This implies that, up to a subsequence,

\frac{(k_{1}u_{1}^{\varepsilon}+l_{2}u_{4}^{\varepsilon})v^{\varepsilon}}{k_{1}u_{1}^{\varepsilon}+l_{2}u_{4}^{\varepsilon}+k_{2}+l_{1}}\rightarrow\frac{(k_{1}u_{1}+l_{2}u_{4})v}{k_{1}u_{1}+l_{2}u_{4}+k_{2}+l_{1}}\quad\text{ in }\quad L^{2}(Q_{T}).

thanks to

\left|\frac{k_{1}u_{1}^{\varepsilon}+l_{2}u_{4}^{\varepsilon}}{k_{1}u_{1}^{\varepsilon}+l_{2}u_{4}^{\varepsilon}+k_{2}+l_{1}}\right|\leq 1.

Therefore,

u^{\varepsilon}_{3}\rightarrow u_{3}=\frac{(k_{1}u_{1}+l_{2}u_{4})v}{k_{1}u_{1}+l_{2}u_{4}+k_{2}+l_{1}}\quad\text{in}\quad L^{2}(Q_{T}).

Thanks to the above convergence of
u^{\varepsilon}_{j}
,
j=1,\ldots,4
and
v^{\varepsilon}
, it is easy to see that

\displaystyle-k_{1}u_{1}^{\varepsilon}u^{\varepsilon}_{2}+l_{1}u_{3}^{\varepsilon}=

\displaystyle(k_{1}u_{1}^{\varepsilon}+l_{1})\bigg(u_{3}^{\varepsilon}-\frac{(k_{1}u_{1}^{\varepsilon}+l_{2}u_{4}^{\varepsilon})v^{\varepsilon}}{k_{1}u_{1}^{\varepsilon}+l_{2}u_{4}^{\varepsilon}+k_{2}+l_{1}}\bigg)-\dfrac{(k_{1}k_{2}u_{1}^{\varepsilon}-l_{1}l_{2}u_{4}^{\varepsilon})v^{\varepsilon}}{k_{1}u_{1}^{\varepsilon}+l_{2}u_{4}^{\varepsilon}+k_{2}+l_{1}}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathchoice{{}\hbox{$\displaystyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\textstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{\meno}$}}}\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\rightarrow-\frac{(k_{1}k_{2}u_{1}-l_{1}l_{2}u_{4})v}{k_{1}u_{1}+l_{2}u_{4}+k_{2}+l_{1}}\;\text{ in }L^{2}(Q_{T}).

Therefore, passing to the limit in the weak formulation

\iint_{Q_{T}}\varphi\partial_{t}u^{\varepsilon}_{1}+d^{\varepsilon}_{1}\iint_{Q_{T}}\nabla u^{\varepsilon}_{1}\cdot\nabla\varphi=\iint_{Q_{T}}(-k_{1}u^{\varepsilon}_{1}u^{\varepsilon}_{2}+l_{1}u^{\varepsilon}_{3})\varphi

we get the weak formulation for

in Definition
2.3
(a). The equations for
u^{\varepsilon}_{4}
and
v
follow the same way, with a remark that for the diffusion term of

, it is sufficient to integrate by parts only once since it holds that

Lemma 3.10
