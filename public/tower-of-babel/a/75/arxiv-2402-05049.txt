Symplectic mechanics of relativistic spinning compact bodies. II. Canonical formalism in the Schwarzschild spacetime
Paul Ramond,, Soichiro Isoyama,

Abstract
This work constitutes the second part of a series of studies that aim to utilise tools from Hamiltonian mechanics to investigate the motion of an extended body in general relativity. The first part of this work [Refs. Ramond 2024 ; P. Ramond 2024 ] constructed a ten-dimensional, covariant Hamiltonian framework encompassing all the linear-in-spin corrections from the secondary spin to the geodesic motion in arbitrary spacetime. This framework was proven to be integrable in the Schwarzschild and Kerr spacetimes, specifically. The present work translates this abstract integrability result into tangible applications for linear-in-spin Hamiltonian dynamics of a compact object in a Schwarzschild spacetime. In particular, a canonical system of coordinates is constructed explicitly, which exploits the spherical symmetry of the Schwarzschild spacetime. These coordinates are based on a relativistic generalization of the classical Andoyer variables of Newtonian rigid body motion. This canonical setup allows us to derive ready-to-use formulae for action-angle coordinates and gauge-invariant Hamiltonian frequencies, which automatically include all linear-in-spin effects. No external parameters or ad hoc choices are necessary, and the framework can be used to find complete solutions by quadrature of generic (bound or unbound), linear-in-spin orbits, including orbital inclination, precession and eccentricity, as well as spin precession. The efficacy of the formalism is demonstrated here in the context of circular orbits with arbitrary spin and orbital precession, with the results validated against known results in the literature.

The main motivation of our efforts comes from extreme mass-ratio inspirals (EMRIs)
– the gradual inspiral of a stellar-mass compact object
(typically a neutron star or black hole or even an “exotic” compact object)
into a supermassive black hole
Amaro-Seoane 2018

These binary inspirals are promising sources of gravitational waves
for an interferometric detector in space
(e.g., the Laser Interferometer Space Antenna
Amaro-Seoane et al. 2017

such as the masses and the spin of the larger central black hole,
can be measured with unparalleled precision

;
Fan et al. 2020
.
However, the gravitational-wave signals from EMRIs will be so intricate
and sufficiently weak that they can only be extracted
from the noisy detector data with modelled waveforms,
that match the phase of the signal over its full duration
(scaling like the inverse of the mass ratio)
across all possible orbital and spin configurations
around a central black hole

.
As a result, the scientific promise described above will be attained
only with a highly accurate and efficient model of relativistic two-body systems,
built on the detailed understanding of their orbital dynamics
in the (multidimensional) EMRI parameter space.

In what follows, we give some more details about the relativistic two-body problem
in the extreme mass-ratio regime,
and motivate our work by emphasising the need for a Hamiltonian treatment

A point mass in general relativity travels on a pre-defined geodesic in the curved geometry of spacetime. However, this feature is only an approximation in the test-particle limit, as revealed by multipolar expansion schemes to a small, extended, compact object (surrounded by a vacuum region)
Dixon 1974
;
Steinhoff and Puetzfeld 2012
;
Harte 2012
;
Harte 2015
.
This modelling approach represents the extended object as a point particle
but endowed with a finite number of multipoles,
encompassing mass, linear and angular momentum, deformability coefficients, among others.
The granularity of this description increases with the number of multipoles included,
providing a more realistic description of the object.

In this multipolar expansion approach to the two-body dynamics,
the associated small parameter is the typical
length scale of the object over the typical length scale of the background spacetime,
which is therefore tailor-made for modelling asymmetric binaries, in particular EMRIs.
At the “zeroth” order, the motion of a smaller compact object is well approximated
by a test body moving along a geodesics of the fixed background spacetime.
However, this is only a crude approximation. Additional effects arise and lead to corrections of this geodesic motion
Harte 2012
. These include (i) conservative and dissipative self-field effects of the small object, owing to the non-linearity of general relativity (GR) and the emission of gravitational waves, and (ii) extended-body effects, due to the compact object not being exactly a point, but rather an extended object which can spin and deform. All these effects are important for measuring EMRIs by space-based gravitational-wave detectors,
as all of them have an impact of quadratic order in the mass ratio in the waveform phase
Pound and Wardell 2021
;
Mathews et al. 2022
,
which is considered to be the minimal accuracy required for parameter estimation
Afshordi et al. 2023a
;
Burke et al. 2024
.

In the present paper (and its foundation
Ramond 2024
;
P. Ramond 2024
),
we leave the self-force effects aside
(i.e. we neglect the back-reaction of the small object’s gravitational field),
and solely focus on the leading order, linear-in-spin corrections
from the secondary’s extended body to the geodesic motion.
At this linear-in-spin order, although it is specific to GR, the equations that describe the evolution of linear and angular momenta along the particle’s worldline are purely kinematical
(no body-specific multipolar forces or torques)
and universal (all bodies move the same way)
Harte 2015
;
Harte and Dwyer 2023
.
Body-specific forces and torques only arise at the next quadrupolar order,
with quadratic-in-spin effects
Harte 2020
.

Analytically solving the relevant linear-in-spin evolution equations,
now known as the Mathisson-Papapetrou-Tulczyjew-Dixon (MPTD) equations,
on an astrophysically relevant Kerr black-hole background has been the subject
of many past studies using a whole spectrum of methods.
Among them, state-of-the-art methods use the powerful formalism of the Marck tetrad
Marck 1983
;
Van de Meent 2020
for the spin to solve the MPTD equations directly in a “perturbed geodesic” philosophy. This method is very natural given the quasi-geodesic nature of linear-in-spin dynamics, especially leading to purely analytical results for generic orbits in the Schwarzschild spacetime
Witzany and Piovano 2024
and simple orbital configurations in Kerr
Abramowicz and Calvani 1979
;
Skoupý and Lukes-Gerakopoulos 2021
;
Skoupý and Lukes-Gerakopoulos 2022
;
Mathews et al. 2022
;
Clemens, S, Dittmer and Josh, Mathews 2024
.
A hybrid numerical/analytical result exists for more generic orbits
Semerák 1999
;
Drummond and Hughes 2022a
;
Drummond and Hughes 2022b
;
Drummond et al. 2024
.
As such, there is a sense in which this Marck formalism offers a simpler implementation
than other means of obtaining the solutions to the linear-in-spin equations of motion.
Having the solution trajectory, however, is not the only way to understand
the linear-in-spin dynamics of the MPTD system.
Many other useful characterizations such as
integrability
of the system,
in which the equations of motion can be solved by quadrature
(as in, e.g., Refs.
Witzany 2019a
;
Witzany and Piovano 2024
) for any orbital configurations,
may require another approach to the MPTD problem.

A Hamiltonian formulation just offers such an approach. In the test-mass approximation (i.e. with no spin), the powerful machinery of Hamiltonian mechanics has been already proven its value
in the context of motion in curved spacetime. A non-exhausted list of examples includes
(but not limited to) the Hamilton-Jacobi equation
Carter 1968
, action-angle variables
Schmidt 2002
;
Witzany 2022
, the phase space analysis
Dean 1999
,
Hamiltonian frequencies and resonances
Schmidt 2002
;
Brink et al. 2015
,
the first law of mechanics
Gralla and Le Tiec 2013
;
Le Tiec 2014b
;
Fujita et al. 2017
,
the perturbation (KAM-type) theory
Hinderer and Flanagan 2008
;
Xue 2020
, Birkhoff normal forms (through Lie series)
Kerachian et al. 2023
symplectic integrators
Wu et al. 2021
;
Wang et al. 2021
.
From the mathematical point of view, these tools are indeed brought by the unique integrability of Kerr geodesic dynamics as a Hamiltonian system
Hughston and Sommers 1973
.

Nonetheless, we are nowhere near having a Hamiltonian understanding of these Kerr, linear-in-spin dynamics as thorough as that of the Kerr geodesics dynamics. The main issue with including the spin is that the treatment becomes necessarily perturbative, since the MPTD equations themselves are always truncated to some multipolar order, for physical consistency (see details in Sec.
I.1
.) This perturbative treatment makes ordinary Hamiltonian concepts such as “an integrable system” or the definition of action-angle variables perturbative as well. We shall follow this approach here as well, and refer to Ref.
P. Ramond and M. Giralt 2025
for details on the mathematical well-posedness of these concepts. On top of this difficulty, the requirement for a spin-supplementary condition (SSC) when the spin is involved in the dynamics implies the existence of algebraic constraints in addition to the differential equations of motion. From a symplectic mechanics point of view, this means that one must reduce the original, non-constrained phase space, to a lower-dimensional one, where the SSC holds. Hamiltonian schemes including linear-in-spin effects exist, for example those constructed in
Refs.
Souriau 1970
;
Damour and Iglesias-Zemmour 2024
;
Souriau 2024
;
Barausse et al. 2009
;
Witzany et al. 2019

(see also Refs.
Ramond 2024
for detailed references). In particular, based on Ref.
Witzany et al. 2019
, the framework presented in Ref.
Witzany 2019a
allowed a well-posed definition of frequencies of motion and (analytical) solutions for spherically symmetric backgrounds in Ref.
Witzany and Piovano 2024
;
Skoupý and Witzany 2024

as well as those for the Kerr background in Ref.
Piovano et al. 2024

under the Tulzyjew-Dixon (TD) spin supplementary condition. It is, however, based on the Marck tetrad formalism in Ref.
Marck 1983
, which intrinsically relies on describing spinning trajectories as perturbation of (non-spinning) geodesics. In a similar vein, Refs.
Barausse et al. 2009
;
Kunst et al. 2016
(and references therein) established the linear-in-spin integrability of the system under another spin supplementary condition, the Newton-Wigner one.

It is not trivial to extend these linear-in-spin formalisms to the quadratic-in-spin case: the Marck tetrad formulation relies on the parallel transport equation for the spin, which is not valid beyond linear order, while the Newton-Wigner spin supplementary condition does not lead to the existence of extra constants of motion, which are key to exploit the quasi-integrability of the dynamics. The present paper, and its foundation in Refs.
P. Ramond 2024
;
Ramond 2024
, aim at providing a rigorous treatment of the linear in spin dynamics, backed up by mathematical theorems on Poisson geometry, for the Hamiltonian analysis at linear order in spin, in a Schwarzschild background. It will provide a basis on which to extend the analysis to quadrupolar, quadratic-in-spin dynamics in subsequent works.

.1.1
This work: the canonical formalism in the Schwarzschild spacetime

With these motivations in mind, as a preliminary analysis, we examine Schwarzschild geometry and consider a
spinning
compact object moving within this fixed background spacetime (i.e., without self-field effects) to the linear order in spin. Future publications will present extensions to the more astrophysically relevant Kerr case.

The classical multipolar expansion schemes (reviewed in Sec.
I.1
) assert that this object can be modelled as a point particle endowed with a linear momentum 1-form
p_{a}
and spin tensor
S^{ab}
. Their evolution along the particle’s worldline
\mathscr{L}
, at linear order in
S^{ab}
and under the Tulczyjew-Dixon (TD) spin supplementary condition (which fixes a unique representative worldline for the extended body), is given
by the following set of (linearized) MPTD equations

\nabla_{u}p_{a}=R_{abcd}S^{bc}u^{d}\,,\quad\nabla_{u}S^{ab}=0\quad\text{and}\quad p_{a}S^{ab}=0\,,

(1)

where
u^{a}=p^{a}/\mu
is the particle’s four-velocity, tangent to
\mathscr{L}
.
The system described in Eq. (
1
) is made of
10
differential equations and
3
algebraic equations, for
13
unknowns in
u^{a},p_{a}
and
S^{ab}
. It encodes everything that we have to know about the object’s dynamics at linear order in spin: its worldline
\mathscr{L}
in
x^{\alpha}
and
how its energy-momentum content evolves along
\mathscr{L}
in
(p_{a},S^{ab})
.

As previously outlined by one of us (PR) in Ref.
Ramond 2024

(hereafter referred to as “Paper I”)
the algebraic-differential system in Eq. (
1
) can be cast
into a covariant Hamiltonian formulation on a
10
dimensional (
10
D) phase space
\mathcal{P}
.
Most importantly, Paper I demonstrates that, when applied to a background Kerr spacetime,
the resulting Hamiltonian system is integrable in the usual, Arnold-Liouville sense [cf Ref.
Liouville 1855
;
Arnold et al. 2006
].

At the level of foundations, the integrability can be proved without any explicit coordinate system in
\mathcal{P}
. At the level of practical implementation, however,
we yet have to identify the coordinate system (particularly the canonical one)
to translate the mathematical property of the integrability
into the powerful and unique tools in Hamiltonian mechanics.

In the present paper we take on that endeavour, and construct canonical coordinates
on
\mathcal{P}
explicitly (see also Ref.
Witzany 2023
for other attempts in this
direction).
Based on these coordinates, we also derive a manifestly integrable Hamiltonian system which is effectively 1D system. This allows us to construct action-angle coordinates in a straightforward way. These action-angle coordinates permit the calculation of frequencies associated with oscillatory or rotational motion without the need to solve the equations of motion. This makes them a valuable tool in practical calculations.

It is important to acknowledge that there are several independent research projects aiming to derive action-angle variables for spinning secondaries orbiting a primary black hole;
see, for example, Refs.
Tanay et al. 2023
;
Witzany 2019a
;
Witzany and Piovano 2024
;
Gonzo and Shi 2024
;
Witzany et al. 2024
.
These works generalize our own in the sense that they are
for primary Kerr black holes, not just for Schwarzschild primaries.
They are also restricted versions of our own,
in the sense that they either are concerned with unbound orbits, assume the weak-field, post-Newtonian gravity, or are based on the Marck tetrad formalism which is limited to the linear-in-spin approximation, while our framework
P. Ramond 2024
;
Ramond 2024
can be extended to the quadratic-in-spin case.
In the context of EMRI modelling, we expect that the Hamiltonian frequencies
will effectively characterize the periodicity of the orbit,
in a manner analogous to that observed in EMRI systems with a non-spinning secondary,
as described in Refs.
Schmidt 2002
;
Hinderer and Flanagan 2008
.

\displaystyle:=\left(D^{1},D^{2},D^{3}\right)=\left(S^{01},S^{02},S^{03}\right)\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad D^{I}=S^{0I}\,.

(20b)

While all these quantities have perfect covariant meaning in general relativity,

Even though we leave the realm of Lorentzian geometry, we shall still use

inherited

I.2.2
The
14
D Poisson manifold
\mathcal{M}

The variables
(x^{\alpha},p_{\alpha},S^{\alpha\beta})

or the variables
y_{\mathcal{M}}:=(x^{\alpha},\pi_{\alpha},S^{I},D^{I})

are two different charts for the same
14

denoted
\mathcal{M}

[cf. TABLE

in Sec.

Using the latter variables

the Poisson brackets for two arbitrary functions

and

on
\mathcal{M}

are defined by [cf. Sec. II of Paper I]

(21)

where the coefficients
\Lambda^{ij}(y_{\mathcal{M}})
are the coordinate expressions
for the
Poisson structure

\Lambda
on
\mathcal{M}
.
Because Eq. (

the following Poisson brackets for

define
\Lambda^{ij}(y_{\mathcal{M}})

\displaystyle\{x^{\alpha},\pi_{\beta}\}_{\mathcal{M}}

\displaystyle=\delta^{\alpha}_{\beta}\,,

(22a)

\displaystyle\{S^{I},S^{J}\}_{\mathcal{M}}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{IJ}_{\phantom{IJ}K}S^{K}\,,

(22b)

\displaystyle\{D^{I},D^{J}\}_{\mathcal{M}}

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon^{IJ}_{\phantom{IJ}K}S^{K}\,,

(22c)

\displaystyle\{D^{I},S^{J}\}_{\mathcal{M}}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{IJ}_{\phantom{IJ}K}D^{K}\,.

(22d)

The
(S^{I},D^{I})

of the

Lorentz algebra.
This is a consequence of the local Lorentz invariance of general relativity,
and is built-in the orthonormal tetrad field that we use.

The Poisson brackets defined in Eqs. (

degenerate,
i.e., the antisymmetric matrix

has (non-maximal) rank

The difference between dim

and rank

identically vanishes.
In our case, these Casimirs are given by [cf. Sec. II.D of Paper I]

(23)

with classical Euclidean notations for three-vectors.
It should be noted that the Casimir invariants are inherent in the phase space
\mathcal{M}

and are not dependent on any choice of the Hamiltonian, which will be introduced below.

A Hamiltonian on
\mathcal{M}
is a scalar field
H_{\mathcal{M}}:\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,
or, equivalently, a function of the
14

\mathcal{M}

For these trajectories to match the solutions of the linear MPTD system in Eq. (

H_{\mathcal{M}}:(x^{\alpha},\pi_{\alpha},S^{I},D^{I})\mapsto\frac{1}{2}g^{\alpha\beta}\pi_{\alpha}\pi_{\beta}+\frac{1}{2}g^{\alpha\beta}\pi_{\alpha}\omega_{\beta CD}S^{CD}\,.

(24)

The three ingredients: the phase space
\mathcal{M}
, the Poisson structure
\{\,,\,\}_{\mathcal{M}}

and the Hamiltonian
H_{\mathcal{M}}
define a
14
D Poisson system.
The phase space trajectories generated by

are described
by the ordinary differential equation (ODE)

\forall F:\mathcal{M}\mapsto\mathbb{R}\,,\quad\frac{\mathrm{d}F}{\mathrm{d}\bar{\tau}}=\{F,H_{\mathcal{M}}\}_{\mathcal{M}}\,,

(25)

where the “time” parameter

Since

When we insert

This is discussed in greater detail in Section II.B of Paper I.

I.2.4
The

In the previous subsection, we defined a Hamiltonian system
on a
12

\mathcal{N}
endowed with canonical coordinates

(x^{\alpha},\sigma,\zeta,\pi_{\alpha},\pi_{\sigma},\pi_{\zeta})

and the Hamiltonian

in Eq. (

However, this system is physically incomplete for two reasons.
First, dim

while the number of independent unknowns in the linear-in-spin MPTD + TD SSC equations is

in Eq. (

The issue here is that the TD SSC in Eq. (
12
)
cannot

16
), even though the latter was originally derived with the help of the TD SSC in Sec.

. It is a one way procedure, and indeed Eqs. (
16
) only imply

\nabla_{u}(p_{a}S^{ab})=0
, not the TD SSC itself.
This is not a problem when working directly at the level of the algebraic-differential system defined by Eqs. (
16

12

Suppose that the TD SSC

is imposed at a point on the worldline
\mathscr{L}
. Then, the parallel transport
\nabla_{u}(p_{a}S^{ab})=0
of the SSC (as implied by Eqs. (
16

However, this is not the case in the Hamiltonian picture, because there is
a priori

no way to “choose an initial condition” of the trajectory in a phase space.
By definition, the phase space is the space of all possible trajectories.
At the level of the phase space
\mathcal{N}
, some of them pass through points where
p_{\alpha}S^{\alpha\beta}=0
, and some do not, making them just physically irrelevant for our purpose.
Instead, one must limit the analysis to the sub-manifold
\mathcal{T}\subset\mathcal{M}

where
p_{\alpha}S^{\alpha\beta}=0
holds
identically
.
To give a precise formulation, let
\mathcal{P}:=\mathcal{T}\cap\mathcal{N}
be the subpart of
\mathcal{N}
where the TD SSC holds identically,
and consider the
constrained Hamiltonian system
on the phase space
\mathcal{P}
,
in which the TD SSC is treated as an algebraic constraint
7
7

7

We are thus in the domain of “singular non-degenerate theory”, in the terminology of Ref.
Deriglazov 2022

.
The properties of
\mathcal{P}
are worked out in Sec. IV.A of Paper I,
and the conclusion there is twofold:

•

\mathcal{P}

under the flow of

will never leave it (see Sec. III.A.1 in Paper I).

•

\mathcal{P}
is
10

, which matches the numbers of unknowns in the linear-in-spin system in Eq. (
16
) (see Sec. III.A.2 in Paper I).

This implies, in particular, that only two out of the four
8
8

8

This was already pointed out in Ref.
Witzany et al. 2019

TD SSCs in Eq. (

.

For the purpose of later computations,

Making use of Eq. (

I.2.5
The
8
D geodesic phase space
\mathcal{G}

Thus far, our Hamiltonian formalism has been concerned exclusively with a spinning body.
Which part of the physical phase space
\mathcal{P}
then corresponds to geodesic motion of a non-spinning body? In the view of the original linearized system in Eq. (
16
)
under the TD SSC in Eq. (
12
), the spin is encoded in the spacelike four-vector
S^{a}_{\text{\tiny{TD}}}
, whose norm is
S_{\circ}
(recall the discussion around Eq. (
13
)).
Thus, the non-spinning limit is defined by
S_{\circ}=0
.

However, in the Hamiltonian formulation, the parameter

S_{\circ}
is a Casimir invariant built into the definition of both the
12
D symplectic leaves
\mathcal{N}
and the
10
D physical phase space
\mathcal{P}
bound to
\mathcal{N}
. Geodesics will thus be confined to the symplectic leaves with
S_{\circ}=0
. On such leaves, the canonical coordinates in Eq. (
29
) admit the well-defined subcase
(\pi_{\sigma},\pi_{\zeta})=(0,0)
, which also make all spin components
S^{I},D^{I}
vanish. The associated angles
\sigma,\zeta
can be chosen freely, setting them to zero being the simplest choice. These four conditions

\left(\sigma,\pi_{\sigma},\zeta,\pi_{\zeta}\right)=\left(0,0,0,0\right)\quad\text{: Non-spinning limit,}

define a well-posed,
8

\mathcal{N}(S_{\circ}=0)
, which we denote
\mathcal{G}
.

By construction,
\mathcal{G}
is invariant under the flow of the Hamiltonian (which becomes the usual geodesic Hamiltonian
H_{\mathcal{G}}(x^{\alpha},p_{\alpha})=\tfrac{1}{2}g^{\alpha\beta}p_{\alpha}p_{\beta}
in this limit, which can be found in, e.g., Refs.
Schmidt 2002
;
Hinderer and Flanagan 2008

In particular,
\mathcal{G}
contains all (and only) the phase space trajectories that correspond to geodesics of the background metric.

I.3
The Schwarzschild spacetime and its symmetries

We now specialize the covariant Hamiltonian formalism, summarized in Sec.
I.2

I.3.1
Geometric generalities

To apply the general formalism reviewed in Sec.
I.1
to the case of the Schwarzschild spacetime, we make a choice of coordinates
x^{\alpha}
covering that spacetime. We choose the Schwarzschild-Droste coordinates
x^{\alpha}=(t,r,\theta,\phi)
, in which the contravariant components of the metric read [cf. Ref.
Gourgoulhon 2021
]

g^{tt}=-\frac{1}{f(r)}\,,\quad g^{rr}=f(r)\,,\quad g^{\theta\theta}=\frac{1}{r^{2}}\,,\quad g^{\phi\phi}=\frac{1}{r^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}\,,

(33)

where
f(r):=1-2M/r
, and
M\geq 0
is the Schwarzschild mass parameter. Because the metric is diagonal, an orthonormal tetrad can be obtained by normalizing the natural basis
(\partial_{\alpha})^{a}
associated with the coordinates
x^{\alpha}
.
With regard to this basis, we identify the tetrad as follows:

(e_{0})^{a}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{f}}(\partial_{t})^{a}\,,\quad(e_{1})^{a}=\sqrt{f}(\partial_{r})^{a}\,,\quad(e_{2})^{a}=\frac{1}{r}(\partial_{\theta})^{a}\,,\quad(e_{3})^{a}=\frac{1}{r\sin\theta}(\partial_{\phi})^{a}\,.

(34)

We shall use this tetrad to define the spin components in Eq. (
20
)
and the connection coefficients
\omega_{\alpha BC}
.
A direct calculation shows that, out of the
48
coefficients of
\omega_{\alpha BC}
,
only eight are not vanishing for the tetrad in Eq. (
34
).
The explicit listing is (see also Ref.
Muller and Grave 2009
but with different conventions):

\omega_{t10}=\frac{M}{r^{2}}\,,\quad\omega_{\theta 21}=\sqrt{f}\,,\quad\omega_{\phi 31}=\sqrt{f}\sin\theta\,,\quad\omega_{\phi 32}=\cos\theta\,,

(35)

and the remaining four coefficients are obtained
by anti-symmetry
\omega_{\alpha BC}=-\omega_{\alpha CB}
.

We can now express the general convariant Hamiltonian on
\mathcal{N}

for a Schwarzschild background.
Inserting all the ingredients Eqs. (
33
) and (
35
)
into Eq. (
28
), we obtain

H_{\mathcal{N}}=-\frac{\pi_{t}^{2}}{2f}+\frac{f\pi_{r}^{2}}{2}+\frac{1}{2r^{2}}\left(\pi_{\theta}^{2}+\frac{\pi_{\phi}^{2}}{\sin^{2}\theta}-\frac{2\pi_{\phi}\cos\theta}{\sin^{2}\theta}S^{1}\right)+\frac{\pi_{\phi}\sqrt{f}}{r^{2}\sin\theta}S^{2}-\frac{\sqrt{f}\pi_{\theta}}{r^{2}}S^{3}+\frac{M\pi_{t}}{r^{2}f}D^{1}\,,

(36)

where, again, it is understood that the components
(S^{I},D^{I})
are functions of
(\sigma,\zeta,\pi_{\sigma},\pi_{\zeta})
using Eqs. (
29
). We note that
\pi_{t},\pi_{r}
have physical dimension
[\text{length}]^{+1}
, while
\pi_{\theta},\pi_{\phi},S^{I},D^{I}
have dimension
[\text{length}]^{+2}
, just like
H_{\mathcal{N}}
.

The two constraints in Eq. (
31
) defining the
10
D physical phase space
\mathcal{P}
can
also be expressed in terms of the canonical coordinates on
\mathcal{N}
as

\displaystyle C^{0}

\displaystyle=-\sqrt{f}\pi_{r}D^{1}-\frac{\pi_{\theta}}{r}D^{2}-\frac{\pi_{\phi}}{r\sin\theta}D^{3}=0\,,

(37a)

\displaystyle C^{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi_{t}}{\sqrt{f}}D^{1}+\frac{\pi_{\phi}}{r\sin\theta}S^{2}-\frac{\pi_{\theta}}{r}S^{3}=0\,.

(37b)

It is now straightforward to derive Hamilton’s equations by inserting Eq. (
36
) into Eq. (
30
). However, we shall not provide them here because their expressions are not required in the context of this paper.

I.3.2
Invariants from Killing vectors

Next we look at the symmetries of the Schwarzschild spacetime, which are in correspondence with four Killing vector fields. Given in the natural vector basis
(\partial_{\alpha})^{a}
associated with
x^{\alpha}=(t,r,\theta,\phi)
, they read [cf. Ref.
Poisson 2004

\displaystyle k_{(t)}^{a}

\displaystyle:=(\partial_{t})^{a}\,,

(38a)

\displaystyle k_{(x)}^{a}

\displaystyle:=-\sin\phi\,(\partial_{\theta})^{a}-\cos\phi\cot\theta\,(\partial_{\phi})^{a}\,,

(38b)

\displaystyle k_{(y)}^{a}

\displaystyle:=\cos\phi\,(\partial_{\theta})^{a}-\sin\phi\cot\theta\,(\partial_{\phi})^{a}\,,

(38c)

\displaystyle k_{(z)}^{a}

\displaystyle:=(\partial_{\phi})^{a}\,.

(38d)

The first one is timelike outside the event horizon (the only place we are interested in for now), and is associated with the invariance under time translations. The last three are spacelike and associated with invariance under spherical (spatial) rotations. Making the substitution within Eq. (
10
), we obtain four Killing-vector invariants
in terms of the canonical coordinates on
\mathcal{N}

\displaystyle\Xi_{(t)}

\displaystyle:=\pi_{t}\,,

(39a)

\displaystyle\Xi_{(x)}

\displaystyle:=-\sin\phi\,\pi_{\theta}-\cos\phi\cot\theta\,\pi_{\phi}+\cos\phi\csc\theta\,S^{1}\,,

(39b)

II
Hamiltonian system on
\mathcal{N}
and Andoyer variables

Andoyer variables, named after the French astronomer Henri Andoyer (1862-1929), are a set of canonical coordinates used in the Hamiltonian mechanics of Newtonian rigid body motion: see, e.g., Refs.
Andoyer 1915
;
Heard 2008
.
They are a generalization of the more well-known Hill and Delaunay variables used in the reduction of the Newtonian two-body problem [cf. Refs.
Laskar 2017
;
Arnold et al. 2006
].
The introduction of Andoyer-type variables in general relativity for spinning dynamics presented here is new, and similar to the definition of orbital elements defined in the geodesic case in Sec.
II.1
, although more geometry is involved.
Quite remarkably, this “classical” change of coordinates can also be applied to the present relativistic context. It is, in essence, the only means by which we are practically able to transition to the physical phase space
\mathcal{P}
.

Note
: Throughout Sec.
II
, we are not dealing with Lorentzian geometry and/or motion within the Schwarzschild spacetime
per se
. As always with interpreting coordinate-dependent expressions, we rely on an abstract Euclidean 3D space where, for example, the angular momentum vector
\vec{L}\,
lives. These Euclidean geometric considerations serve only to obtain a set of formulae that define a symplectic change of coordinates. This will serve as a useful guide in the relativistic generalization of the next section.

II.1
Schwarzschild geodesics

The motion of a non-spinning test particle in a given background spacetime is described by geodesics. These geodesics have a well-known Hamiltonian formulation
[cf. Refs.
Schmidt 2002
;
Hinderer and Flanagan 2008
] on a
8
D phase space
\mathcal{G}
[cf. FIG.
1
],
spanned by the
4
spacetime coordinates
x^{\alpha}
and their
4
conjugated momenta
p_{\alpha}
, corresponding to the particle’s momentum
p_{a}
components. The Hamiltonian generating the geodesic equations is simply
H_{\mathcal{G}}:=\tfrac{1}{2}g^{\alpha\beta}p_{\alpha}p_{\beta}
. For the case of the Schwarzschild spacetime, this reads

H_{\mathcal{G}}\,(t,p_{t},r,p_{r},\theta,p_{\theta},\phi,p_{\phi})=-\frac{p_{t}^{2}}{2f}+\frac{fp_{r}^{2}}{2}+\frac{1}{2r^{2}}\left(p_{\theta}^{2}+\frac{p_{\phi}^{2}}{\sin^{2}\theta}\right)\,.

(53)

where
(t,r,\theta,\phi)
are the Schwarzschild-Droste coordinates and
(p_{t},p_{r},p_{\theta},p_{\phi})
are the components of the
1
-form
p_{a}
in the natural basis
(\partial_{\alpha})^{a}
. Just like the Kepler problem in Newtonian gravity (of which the Hamiltonian is given by the leading order in a
M/r\ll 1
expansion of Eq.(
53
)), Schwarzschild geodesics are always spatially confined to a
2
-dimensional plane, reflecting the spherical symmetry. In terms of Hamiltonian mechanics, this geometric feature is nothing but a
1:1
resonance between the
\theta
and
\phi
motion; this fact is not so obvious by direct inspection of Eq. (
53

A.2
.

Figure 2:
The orbital elements of Schwarzschild geodesics, including the angle
\nu
defining the line of nodes, the true anomaly
\ell
and the orbital inclination
\iota
.

A common approach in relativistic mechanics is to limit the geodesic analysis to the equatorial plane (
\theta=\pi/2
).
However, in the case of an orbit of a spinning body, the spin-curvature coupling will break this resonance and cause orbital precession.
Furthermore, such a method is not aligned with the philosophy of Hamiltonian mechanics, as it relies on adapting the coordinates to a specific orbit rather than incorporating the symmetry for the entire phase space, encompassing all orbits.
It is therefore preferable to use tools that are specifically designed to incorporate spherical symmetry into phase space, with the initial step being a canonical transformation.
In particular, let us consider the following change of coordinates

(\theta,p_{\theta},\phi,p_{\phi})\mapsto(\ell,p_{\ell},\nu,p_{\nu})\,,

(54)

where
\nu
is the angle defining
the line of nodes
, where the orbital plane intersects the equatorial plane, and
\ell
is

, which specifies the location of the particle within the orbital plane; see Fig.
2
. With the help of some Euclidean trigonometry, as detailed in App.
A
, we find the following formulae

\displaystyle\cos\theta

\displaystyle=\sin\iota\sin\ell\,,

(55a)

\displaystyle\sin\theta\cos(\nu-\phi)

\displaystyle=\cos\ell\,,

(55b)

\displaystyle\sin\theta\sin(\nu-\phi)

\displaystyle=-\sin\ell\cos\iota\,.

(55c)

These equations define the new coordinate
\ell

(true anomaly) in terms of the old ones
\theta,\phi
, (polar and azimuthal
angles) and two fixed angles
\nu
(line of nodes) and
\iota
, a constant angle encoding inclination of the orbital angle with respect to the equatorial plane.
The change of coordinates in Eq. (
54

to induce a canonical transformation on
\mathcal{G}
by setting

(56)

noting that

coincides with the norm of the angular-momentum

\vec{L}
defined in Eq. (

Again, the geometry behind this canonical transformation is summarized in Fig.

In terms of the new coordinates,

H_{\mathcal{G}}(t,p_{t},r,p_{r},\ell,p_{\ell},\nu,p_{\nu})=-\frac{p_{t}^{2}}{2f}+\frac{fp_{r}^{2}}{2}+\frac{p_{\ell}^{2}}{2r^{2}}\,.

(57)

By direct inspection, this Hamiltonian admits five integrals of motion, i.e.,
functions

These are

•

•

•

\vec{L}

•

II.5
Andoyer-Hill “effective” formulation

II.5.3
The true anomaly as the Carter-Mino time

The Hill differential equation in Eq. (
71
) parametrizes the pair

(s,\pi_{s})
with respect to the true anomaly
\ell
, instead of the (dimensionless) proper time
{\bar{\tau}}
.
We point out that the true anomaly
\ell
is very closely related to
the (so-named) Carter-Mino time
\lambda
introduced in Ref.
Carter 1968
;
Mino 2003
,
which is widely adopted in the literature on the celestial mechanics of a non-spinning compact object in Kerr spacetime.

In the Schwarzschild limit, the Carter-Mino time
\lambda
is defined by

\mathrm{d}\lambda=\mathrm{d}\tau/r^{2}
.
With Eq. (
69d
), we have

\mathrm{d}\ell=({\mathcal{L}}/{\mu})\,\mathrm{d}\lambda
,
in which
{\mathcal{L}}/{\mu}
is a constant of motion with dimension
[\text{length}]^{+1}
.
Integration of this equation is trivial, and we obtain

\ell=\frac{\mathcal{L}}{\mu}\lambda+\text{cst}\,.

(74)

Note, however, that
\lambda
is not a time parameter in the strict sense
because it has the dimension
[\text{length}]^{-1}
;
recall the dimension of the angle
\ell
, which is
[\text{length}]^{0}
.
In general relativity, it is widely acknowledged that the Carter-Mino time “remarkably” uncouples the radial from angular motion of Kerr or Schwarzschild geodesics.
This is almost “expected” in the context of classical mechanics, where the true anomaly
is related to a conjugate (conserved angular) momentum vector.
This decoupling is achieved through a process that is “by construction” and is explained in detail in Ref.
Laskar 2017
.
However, it remains to explore this analogy further in the context
of the relativistic celestial mechanics in Kerr geometry.

II.5.4
Final remarks for the effective framework

From the preceding paragraphs, it appears that a simple three-steps recipe for solving the effective dynamics would be as follows: (i) Solve the integrable effective Hamiltonian given
in Eq. (
70

into the Hill equation given by Eq. (

While steps (i) and (iii) are tractable analytically, step (ii) should, in principle, rely on numerical integration, as Hill equations like Eq. (

The eccentric configuration, regrettably, necessitates the numerical integration of Hill equation, and this is where our development would stop if we were to study the MPTD system in Eq. (

) as the

\mathcal{N}
.

Nevertheless, these drawbacks of the formulation on

In fact, it was demonstrated that the

III.1.2
Choice of 10 variables on
\mathcal{P}

Our next step is to chose
10

\mathcal{P}
.
Any subset of the
12
Andoyer variables

(or any combination thereof)

\mathcal{N}

Our approach will be to retain the constants of motion

(or, again, any combination thereof)
introduced in Sec.

since we know that the system is integrable on

[cf. Sec. VI in Paper I], these constants of motion can be readily promoted to momenta on
\mathcal{P}

(79)

using the Andoyer spin variables in Eq. (

All attempts to construct such
10

While the set of constants of motion
(E,L_{z},L)
are highly practical on
\mathcal{P}
,
it turns out that
\mathfrak{K}
is not particularly well-adapted for our purposes.
A number of alternative choices were considered as potential starting points,
with the help of the discussion above
and the Hill formulation in Sec.
II.5
.
The issue with
\mathfrak{K}
is that, if it is to serve as the variable that encodes the spin-dependence, it must have the correct dimension. Through trial and error, we find that the quantity
\mu\mathfrak{K}/L
was much more suitable choice than
\mathfrak{K}
: it induces much simpler
\mathcal{P}
-brackets and has the correct spin dimension
[\text{length}]^{+2}

Ultimately, we decided to adopt the following

It should be noted that

12

Our choice in Eq. (

First, Eq. (
80

\mathcal{P}
-brackets
between themselves, i.e., all brackets between any two pair of the set can be expressed
solely in terms of this set.
Second, it leads to a simple parametrization of the orbital quantities of interest,
based on the constants of motions, i.e., Killing invariants
described in Sec.
I.3
.
Third, it includes the invariant
\pi_{\varpi}
with the dimension of spin
[\text{length}]^{+2}

which is directly related to the Rüdiger invaiant
\mathfrak{K}
in Eq. (
79

through the combination

Fourth, similarly, it introduces the “angle” variable
\Gamma

[\text{length}]^{0}

\Gamma=[\pi_{\ell}/(\pi_{t}\mathfrak{K})][\pi_{s}/\tan s]

yet involves the spin Andoyer variables.
The pair of (non-canonical) variables

is very convenient
to parameterize the spin dynamics in
\mathcal{P}

In practice, any one of these motives is crucial in the final reduction
to the canonical coordinates, which we shall develop in Sec.

IV.3
Action-angle variables

A significant implication of our integrable Hamiltonian system is
the existence of a set of canonical coordinates, designated action-angle variables,
(\vartheta^{i},\mathcal{J}_{i})_{i=1\ldots 5}
on
\mathcal{P}
.
This is a consequence of The Liouville–Arnold theorem, as presented
in Refs.
Liouville 1855
;
Arnold et al. 2006
and its subsequent generalisation
in the case of the non-compact level set,
as outlined in Refs.
Fiorani et al. 2003
;
Hinderer and Flanagan 2008
.
The angles
\vartheta^{i}
are cyclic
(in the sense that the Hamiltonian
H_{c}
does not depend on them by definition)
and the actions
\mathcal{J}_{i}
are constants of motion,
which are in a
1
-to-
1
correspondence with the set
(H_{c},E,L_{z},L,\mathfrak{K})
.
In particular, action-angle variables offer a practical means
of obtaining the frequencies of oscillatory (or rotational) motion
in the system.

The Liouville-Arnold theorem guarantees the existence
of action-angle variables
(\vartheta^{i},\mathcal{J}_{i})
,
yet the difficulty remains of determining their explicit functional forms.
Once again, our previously established canonical Hamiltonian system
in Sec.
IV.1
provides a practical method for achieving this objective.
The key point is that four (out of five) canonical momenta
(\pi_{t},\pi_{\nu},\pi_{\ell},\pi_{\varpi})

in Eq. (
115
) are all constants of motion,
and the Hamiltonian
H_{c}
in Eq. (
117
) is already reduced
to the “radial” Hamiltonian for
(r,\,\tilde{\pi}_{r})
essentially.
The overall structure of the Hamiltonian system is therefore identical
to the well-known one for the geodesic dynamics in Schwarzschild
[cf. Appendix
A
],
except for the addition of the spin canonical pair
({\varpi},\,\pi_{\varpi})
.
We can immediately obtain the action-angle variables for the spinning-body
in much the same way as those defined in the case of the non-spinning body
[cf. the Kerr geodesic results in Refs
Schmidt 2002
;
Hinderer and Flanagan 2008
].

In this section, we obtain the action-angle variables
(\vartheta^{i},\mathcal{J}_{i})_{i=1\ldots 5}

of the physical phase space
\mathcal{P}
.
They can be defined in generality in Schwarzschild,
but, as a first application, we restrict ourselves to a bound periodic orbit.
The methods exploited here originates from Schmitt
Schmidt 2002
and Hinderer and Flanagan
Hinderer and Flanagan 2008
,
implemented for geodesic dynamics in Kerr spacetime. Our approach is a generalization of their construction to a spinning body.

IV.3.1
Derivation

To begin we denote the set of five constants of motion as [cf. Sec.
IV.1
]

(P_{i})_{i=1\ldots 5}:=\left(H_{c},E,L_{z},L,S_{\parallel}\right)=\left(-\frac{\mu^{2}}{2},E,L_{z},L,S_{\parallel}\right)\,,

(131)

which can be inverted to express the canonical momenta
\tilde{\pi}_{i}

as a function of
r
and
P_{\alpha}
. We find that

\pi_{t}=-E\,,\quad\tilde{\pi}_{r}=V(r;P_{\alpha})\,,\quad\pi_{\nu}=L_{z}\,,\quad\pi_{\ell}=L\,,\quad\pi_{\varpi}=S_{\parallel}\,,

(132)

where

V(r;P_{\alpha}):=\pm\sqrt{\frac{E^{2}}{f^{2}}-\frac{L^{2}}{fr^{2}}-\frac{\mu^{2}}{f}+\frac{r-3M}{r-2M}\frac{2\mu EL}{E^{2}r^{2}-L^{2}f}S_{\parallel}}\,.

(133)

Following
Schmidt 2002
;
Hinderer and Flanagan 2008
,
we define the generalized action
\mathcal{J}_{i}
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{1}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\pi_{t}\,\mathrm{d}\tilde{t}=-E\,,

(134a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{2}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2\pi}\oint V(r;P_{\alpha})\,\mathrm{d}r=\frac{1}{2\pi}\oint\sqrt{\frac{E^{2}}{f^{2}}-\frac{L^{2}}{fr^{2}}-\frac{\mu^{2}}{f}+\frac{r-3M}{r-2M}\frac{2\mu EL}{E^{2}r^{2}-L^{2}f}S_{\parallel}}\,\mathrm{d}r\,,

(134b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{3}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2\pi}\oint\pi_{\nu}\,\mathrm{d}\nu=L_{z}\,,

(134c)

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{4}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2\pi}\oint\pi_{\ell}\,\mathrm{d}\tilde{\ell}=L\,,

(134d)

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{5}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2\pi}\oint\pi_{\varpi}\,\mathrm{d}{\varpi}=S_{\parallel}\,,

(134e)

where the integral path is given by one periodic orbit in the
(r,\,\tilde{\pi}_{r})
space,
except for
\mathcal{J}_{1}
which involves the non-compact “time” level set
and is integrated over the curve of the length
2\pi
[cf. the usual prescription described in Refs.
Hinderer and Flanagan 2008
;
Schmidt 2002
].
In practice, it would be convenient to only keep
\mathcal{J}_{2}
in Eq. (
134b
)
to first order in the spin expansion; any

terms can be discarded
in our linear-in-spin treatment. After some simple algebra, we have

\mathcal{J}_{2}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\oint\left(\frac{\sqrt{V_{0}}}{fr^{2}}+\frac{r-3M}{r-2M}\frac{\mu EL}{\nu_{0}^{2}\sqrt{V_{0}}}S_{\parallel}\right)\mathrm{d}r\,

(135)

with [cf. Eq. (
110

V_{0}(r;P_{\alpha}):=r^{4}E^{2}-fr^{2}\left(\mu^{2}r^{2}+L^{2}\right)\,.

(136)

It is, however, crucial to distinguish
between the function

and the “radial geodesic potential,”
which is defined, e.g., in Eq. (2.26a) in Ref.
Hinderer and Flanagan 2008

This distinction is important as the former accounts for spin effects implicitly
through corrections to the constants of motion,
E
,
L

\mu

The spin actions
\mathcal{J}_{i}
in Eqs. (

of geodesic actions defined in Refs.
Schmidt 2002
;
Hinderer and Flanagan 2008

see also Refs.
Witzany 2019a
;
Witzany and Piovano 2024

Leo C. Stein 2024

in Eq. (

Because the two sets

and
\mathcal{J}_{i}
are bijective by definition,
we immediately find that

P_{\alpha}=P_{\alpha}\left(\mathcal{J}_{i}\right)\,.

(137)

Next we define the conjugate angle variables

Among the various definitions, a (type-

In our spinning case, we define

from which we obtain the desired canonical transformation as

The first equation is consistent with the definition of

It is easy to verify that the action-angle variables in the form

IV.3.2
Hamiltonian frequencies

As a physical application of our spin action-angle variables,
we here compute the invariant Hamilton’s frequencies
(\omega^{i})_{i=1\ldots 5}

for the spin angle variables
\vartheta^{i}
defined by

\omega^{i}(\mathcal{J}):=\frac{\partial H_{c}}{\partial\mathcal{J}_{i}}\,.

(142)

Physically, they correspond to the oscillation (or rotation) frequencies
of bound orbits.
The formalism described here is adapted from Appendix. A
of Schmidt
Schmidt 2002
, originally developed for geodesic frequencies in Kerr;
see also Appendix A of Ref.
Hinderer and Flanagan 2008
as well as
Sec. IV of Ref.
Tanay et al. 2023

The Key observation of this approach is the bijective map in Eq. (
137
),
which returns the simple chain rule given by

\frac{\partial P_{i}}{\partial{\mathcal{J}}_{k}}\frac{\partial{\mathcal{J}}_{k}}{\partial P_{j}}=\delta^{i}_{j}\,.

(143)

We then take advantage of the fact that the component
P_{1}

in Eq. (
131
) is identical to the canonical Hamiltonian

H_{c}
, and that the
i=0
component of Eq. (
143
)
just becomes

\omega^{k}({\partial{\mathcal{J}}_{k}}/{\partial P_{j}})=\delta^{1}_{j}
.
Inverting this equation, we have

\omega^{1}=\omega^{2}\frac{\partial{\mathcal{J}}_{2}}{\partial E}\,,\quad\omega^{2}=\left(\frac{\partial{\mathcal{J}}_{2}}{\partial H_{c}}\right)^{-1}\,,\quad\omega^{3}=0\,,\quad\omega^{4}=-\omega^{2}\frac{\partial{\mathcal{J}}_{2}}{\partial J}\,,\quad\omega^{5}=-\omega^{2}\frac{\partial{\mathcal{J}}_{2}}{\partial S_{\parallel}}\,.

(144)

This can be solved one step at a time, simply from the partial derivatives of
the radial action
{\mathcal{J}}_{2}
in Eq. (

After linearization, they are given explicitly by [cf. Eq. (
136
)]

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\mathcal{J}}_{2}}{\partial E}

(145a)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\mathcal{J}}_{2}}{\partial H_{c}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\oint\frac{r^{2}}{\sqrt{V_{0}}}\left(1-\frac{ELr\left(r-3M\right)}{\mu V_{0}}S_{\parallel}\right)\mathrm{d}r\,,

(145b)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\mathcal{J}}_{2}}{\partial L}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\oint\frac{L}{\sqrt{V_{0}}}\left(1+\frac{r-3M}{r-2M}\frac{\mu E}{\nu_{0}^{2}L}\left(1-\frac{r^{4}E^{2}}{V_{0}}-\frac{2E^{2}}{f\nu_{0}^{2}}+\frac{r^{4}f\nu_{0}^{2}}{V_{0}}\right)S_{\parallel}\right)\mathrm{d}r\,,

(145c)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\mathcal{J}}_{2}}{\partial S_{\parallel}}

IV.4
A demonstration of the formalism: The circular case

As a preliminary illustration of our canonical formulation,
we examine the model problem of circular orbits,
which are otherwise general orbital and spin configurations.
In this case, the spin precesses, as does the orbital plane.
This will highlight the core ideas of our canonical formulation,

Firstly, we shall set some values of the invariants of motion

IV.4.3
ISCO parameters

The physical stability of circular orbits in response to an arbitrary spacetime perturbation
is directly related to the spectral stability of the corresponding fixed point in phase space,
which is determined by the two eigenvalues of the linearized system around it.
These eigenvalues depend on the parameters
(-E,L,L_{z},S_{\parallel})
of the slice,
but they are always opposite and purely imaginary for circular orbits,
which correspond to elliptic fixed point.
For specific values of the parameters, however, they can be shown to vanish identically.
This corresponds to the two circular orbits (stable and unstable) colliding
as we traverse the two-dimensional slices of constant parameters

\pi_{t}
,
\pi_{\ell}
,
\pi_{\nu}
, and
\pi_{\varpi}
.
The resulting orbit is neither stable nor unstable;
it is semi-stable and corresponds to a parabolic fixed point.

For
2
D canonical systems, the eigenvalues in question are easily seen
to be those of the matrix
\mathbb{J}_{2}{\mathcal{H}}
,
where
\mathbb{J}_{2}
is the canonical
2\times 2
symplectic matrix
and
\mathcal{H}
is the Hessian of the Hamiltonian.
This is the matrix that encodes the linearized dynamics around a given point in phase space.
In order to demonstrate this concept within the context of our problem,
we return to our one-dimensional Hamiltonian
H_{\text{rad}}

in Eq. (
146
), for which we compute its Hessian and evaluate it at a circular orbit,
namely
(r,\tilde{\pi}_{r})=(M\rho,0)
, utilizing also Eq. (
149
).
The result is

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}H_{\text{rad}}}{\partial r^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu^{2}}{M^{2}}\frac{\left(18-9\rho+\rho^{2}\right)\rho^{3/2}-\left(18-3\rho-2\rho^{2}\right)s_{\parallel}}{(\rho-3)^{2}(\rho-2)\rho^{7/2}}\,,

(152a)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}H_{\text{rad}}}{\partial\tilde{\pi}_{r}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(\rho-2)(\rho^{3/2}+s_{\parallel})}{\rho^{5/2}}\,,

(152b)

and the mixed second partial derivative vanishes identically.
The Hessian
\mathcal{H}
is thus diagonal at circular orbits,
and the eigenvalues of the matrix
\mathbb{J}_{2}\mathcal{H}
vanish
if and only if either of the two partial derivatives in Eq. (
146
) vanish.
Clearly, this result can only be derived from the first equation
(because, in any case,
\rho>2
outside the Schwarzschild event horizon),
which, upon solving for
\rho

given by

\rho_{\text{isco}}=6-2\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}s_{\parallel}\,,

(153)

such that

\frac{\partial^{2}H_{\text{rad}}}{\partial r^{2}}=0\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\partial^{2}H_{\text{rad}}}{\partial\tilde{\pi}_{r}^{2}}=\frac{2}{3}-\frac{s_{\parallel}}{9\sqrt{6}}\,.

(154)

These formulae are the ones commonly found in the literature
to define the so-called innermost stable circular orbit (ISCO), whence the notation.
If we insert the value
\rho_{\text{isco}}

into the circular-orbit parameters in Eqs. (
149

which are valid for any circular orbit, we obtain

\varepsilon_{\text{isco}}=\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{3}-\frac{s_{\parallel}}{36\sqrt{3}}\quad\text{and}\quad l_{\text{isco}}=2\sqrt{3}+\frac{\sqrt{2}}{3}s_{\parallel}\,.

(155)

with the results presented in Eqs. (B17) through (B20b) in Ref.
Favata 2011

Appendix A
The non-spinning (geodesic) Hamiltonian

This Appendix presents an analysis of the geodesic Hamiltonian
for the Schwarzschild background.
The results presented here are fairly standard,
but our derivation of these results may offer new insights.

We consider the Schwarzschild spacetime covered
with the Schwarzschild-Droste coordinates
x^{\alpha}=(t,r,\theta,\phi)
.
25
25

25

Technically, we are only interested
in the Schwarzschild-Droste region (i.e. outside the event horizon) of the Schwarzschild spacetime, where
r>2M
, denoted
\mathcal{M}_{\text{I}}
in
Gourgoulhon 2021
.

The equations of motion, whose physical solutions describe timelike geodesics of the Schwarzschild spacetime, are equivalent to Hamilton’s equation for the Hamiltonian

H_{\mathcal{G}}=\tfrac{1}{2}g^{\alpha\beta}(x)p_{\alpha}p_{\beta}
,
where
x^{\alpha}
denote the Schwarzschild-Droste coordinates
and
p_{\alpha}
are the component of the four-momentum
1
-form.
With the contravariant metric coefficients
g^{\alpha\beta}(x)
in Eq. (
33
),
we can write the geodesic Hamiltonian
H_{\mathcal{G}}

in terms of the symplectic coordinates
(x^{\alpha},p_{\alpha})
[recall
f:=1-2M/r
],

H_{\mathcal{G}}=-\frac{p_{t}^{2}}{2f}+\frac{fp_{r}^{2}}{2}+\frac{1}{2r^{2}}\left(p_{\theta}^{2}+\frac{p_{\phi}^{2}}{\sin^{2}\theta}\right)\,.

(156)

Along any solution, the Hamiltonian
H_{\mathcal{G}}
evaluates to the constant
-\mu^{2}/2
, and the “time” parameter conjugated to
H_{\mathcal{G}}
is the proper time per unit mass
\bar{\tau}=\tau/\mu
, a dimensionless affine parameter.

In the following sections, we first perform a reduction of the problem
through the application of the invariants of motion.
We then proceed to analytically solve the dynamics of the system,
with particular focus on the properties
that will persist in our description of a spinning body.

A.1
Schwarzschild spherical symmetry

Let us think for a moment in Euclidean terms and consider a Cartesian orthonormal triad
(\vec{e}_{x},\vec{e}_{y},\vec{e}_{z})
associated with the usual spherical coordinates
(r,\theta,\phi)
. A given orbital plane intersects the equatorial plane
\text{Span}(\vec{e}_{x},\vec{e}_{y})
(or
\theta=\pi/2
in spherical coordinates), thereby defining a particular line, called the
line of nodes
.
Let that line be defined by the relation
\phi=\nu
for some fixed angle
\nu
(this actually defines half that line, the other half being
\phi=\nu+\pi
).
The line of nodes defines a new axis
\vec{f}_{x}
, which can be rotated counterclockwise within the equatorial plane to construct another one, say
\vec{f}_{y}
.
Setting
\vec{f}_{z}=\vec{e}_{z}
, we get a new orthonormal triad
(\vec{f}_{x},\vec{f}_{y},\vec{f}_{z})
, explicitly given by,

\vec{f}_{x}=\cos\nu\,\vec{e}_{x}+\sin\nu\,\vec{e}_{y}\,,\quad\vec{f}_{y}=-\sin\nu\,\vec{e}_{x}+\cos\nu\,\vec{e}_{y}\,,\quad\vec{f}_{z}=\vec{e}_{z}\,.

(157)

Next we perform another rotation, this time leaving the direction
\vec{f}_{x}

unchanged and inclining the (originally) equatorial plane
by an angle
\iota\in[0,\pi]
.
This sends
\vec{f}_{y}
and
\vec{f}_{z}
to

\vec{g}_{y}=\cos\iota\vec{f}_{y}+\sin\iota\vec{f}_{z}
and
\vec{g}_{z}=-\sin\iota\vec{f}_{y}+\cos\iota\vec{f}_{z}
, respectively. Finally, setting
\vec{g}_{x}=\vec{f}_{x}
, and expressing everything
in terms of the original triad
(\vec{e}_{x},\vec{e}_{y},\vec{e}_{z})
,
we arrive at

\displaystyle\vec{g}_{x}

\displaystyle=\cos\nu\,\vec{e}_{x}+\sin\nu\,\vec{e}_{y}\,,

(158a)

\displaystyle\vec{g}_{y}

\displaystyle=-\cos\iota\sin\nu\,\vec{e}_{x}+\cos\iota\cos\nu\,\vec{e}_{y}+\sin\iota\,\vec{e}_{z}\,,

(158b)

\displaystyle\vec{g}_{z}

\displaystyle=\sin\iota\sin\nu\,\vec{e}_{x}-\sin\iota\cos\nu\,\vec{e}_{y}+\cos\iota\,\vec{e}_{z}\,.

(158c)

Now let a unit position vector be defined as usual as

\vec{n}=\cos\phi\sin\theta\vec{e}_{x}+\sin\phi\sin\theta\,\vec{e}_{y}+\cos\theta\,\vec{e}_{z}
.
Using some trigonometric identities, in the new triad, the vector
\vec{n}
reads

\vec{n}=\cos(\phi-\nu)\sin\theta\,\vec{g}_{x}+(\cos\theta\sin\iota+\sin(\phi-\nu)\sin\theta\cos\iota)\,\vec{g}_{y}\\
+(\cos\theta\cos\iota-\sin(\phi-\nu)\sin\theta\sin\iota)\,\vec{g}_{z}\,.

(159)

By construction, the invariant orbital plane is now
\text{Span}(\vec{g}_{x},\vec{g}_{y})

and the Schwarzschild geodesic motion is confined within it. Therefore, one must have
\vec{n}\cdot\vec{g}_{z}=0
, leading to the condition

\sin(\phi-\nu)\sin\theta\sin\iota=\cos\theta\cos\iota\,.

(160)

Since the angles
\iota
and
\nu
are both constant,
this gives an important algebraic relation between
\theta
and
\phi
,
which is the source of the
(\theta-\phi)
-resonance mentioned earlier.
In the orbital plane
\text{Span}(\vec{g}_{x},\vec{g}_{y})
,
let us define an angle
\ell
, called the
true anomaly
,
such that the position vector is
\vec{n}=\cos\ell\vec{g}_{x}+\sin\ell\vec{g}_{y}
.
Then, combining this definition as well as Eqs. (
159
) and (
160
), we obtain the following three relations

\displaystyle\cos\theta

\displaystyle=\sin\iota\sin\ell\,,

(161a)

\displaystyle\sin\theta\cos(\nu-\phi)

\displaystyle=\cos\ell\,,

(161b)

\displaystyle\sin\theta\sin(\nu-\phi)

\displaystyle=-\sin\ell\cos\iota\,.

(161c)

These equations define the new coordinate
\ell
(true anomaly)
in terms of the old ones
\theta
and
\phi
(polar and azimuthal angles),
and two fixed parameters
\iota
and
\Omega

(orbital inclination and an angle defining the line of nodes).
Along with these definitions for the new angles
\ell
and
\nu
,
we define the usual quantities

p_{\ell}^{2}:=p_{\theta}^{2}+p_{\phi}^{2}\csc^{2}\theta\quad\text{and}\quad p_{\nu}:=p_{\phi}\,,

(162)

in which
p_{\ell}
is simply the norm of the angular-momentum.
Together, formulae Eqs. (
161
) and (
162
)
define a canonical change of coordinates

(t,p_{t},r,p_{r},\theta,p_{\theta},\phi,p_{\phi})\mapsto(t,p_{t},r,p_{r},\ell,p_{\ell},\nu,p_{\nu})\,.

(163)

A.2
The orbital plane as a
1:1
resonance

The squared norm of the angular momentum

L^{2}=p_{\theta}^{2}+L_{z}^{2}\csc^{2}\theta\,,

(164)

which is a geodesic integral of motion, places a stringent constraint
on the
(\theta,p_{\theta})
-sector of dynamics.
In a plane where one plots the variables
p_{\theta}
VS
L_{z}\csc\theta
,
this curve depicts a circle of radius
L
.
Therefore,
\theta
varies between
\arcsin(L_{z}/L)
and
\pi-\arcsin(L_{z}/L)
.
This curve is modified slightly in the plane
p_{\theta}
VS
\theta
,
with the circle mapped into a squircle curve
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}(L_{z},L)
.

The Poincaré invariant (i.e., an action)

associated
with the

by this curve
\mathcal{C}

J_{\theta}:=\frac{1}{2{\pi}}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}\,p_{\theta}\mathrm{d}\theta=\frac{|L|}{{\pi}}\int_{\arcsin(L_{z}/L)}^{{\pi}-\arcsin(L_{z}/L)}\sqrt{1-\frac{(L_{z}/L)^{2}}{\sin^{2}\theta}}\,\mathrm{d}\theta\,,

(165)

where
L_{z}/L\in[-1;1]

164
) and the horizontal symmetry of the curve
\mathcal{C}

(we assume that
L_{z}/L\geq 0
; the other case

J_{\theta}=\frac{2L}{{\pi}}\int_{\arcsin(L_{z}/L)}^{{\pi}/2}\sqrt{1-\frac{(L_{z}/L)^{2}}{\sin^{2}\theta}}\mathrm{d}\theta=L-L_{z}

(166)

Therefore, in all cases, we have

J_{\theta}=\sqrt{p_{\theta}^{2}+\frac{p_{\phi}^{2}}{\sin^{2}\theta}}-|p_{\phi}|\quad\text{and}\quad J_{\phi}=p_{\phi}\,.

(167)

Now, let us consider the radial action, defined as the integral
of the radial momentum
p_{r}
over the loop
\mathcal{C}_{r}

in the plane
(r,p_{r})

J_{r}:=\tfrac{1}{2\pi}\oint p_{r}\mathrm{d}r
.
In practice, this quantity can be integrated by inverting the Hamiltonian

H_{\mathcal{G}}=-\frac{E^{2}}{2f}+\frac{fp_{r}^{2}}{2}+\frac{L^{2}}{2r^{2}}\quad\Rightarrow\quad p_{r}=\pm\sqrt{\frac{E^{2}}{f^{2}}+\frac{2H_{\mathcal{G}}}{f}-\frac{L^{2}}{fr^{2}}}\,,

(168)

and we obtain

H_{\mathcal{G}},E^{2}
and

In particular, the energy

expressed as
E=-J_{t}
and

in terms of actions, respectively.
Consequently, the radial action can be expressed as a function of only other actions.
Schematically, we have that

J_{r}=\mathcal{F}(H_{\mathcal{G}},J_{t},J_{\theta}+|J_{\phi}|)\,,

(169)

where the function
\mathcal{F}
would involve some kind of elliptic integrals
and inverse trigonometric functions.
By solving Eq. (
169

we can formally express it in terms of actions of the form

H_{\mathcal{G}}(J_{t},J_{r},J_{\theta},J_{\phi})=\mathcal{G}(J_{t},J_{r},J_{\theta}+|J_{\phi}|)\,,

(170)

for a function
\mathcal{G}
.

What is crucial here is that

depends on the actions
J_{\theta},J_{\phi}

only through the combination
J_{\theta}+|J_{\phi}|
.
Therefore, from the Hamiltonian frequencies defined by
\Omega_{\mathrm{i}}=\partial H_{\mathcal{G}}/\partial J_{\mathrm{i}}

\Omega_{\theta}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\Omega_{\phi}&\text{if}&J_{\phi}\leq 0\,,\\
-\Omega_{\phi}&\text{if}&J_{\phi}\geq 0\,.\end{array}\right.

(171)

In either case, the motion is

there exists a non-trivial linear combination of the frequencies

\Omega_{\theta}
and
\Omega_{\phi}
with integer coefficients that vanishes.
The plus (minus) sign in the right-hand side of Eq. (
171

corresponds to prograde (retrograde) orbits.
This resonance is reminiscent of the existence of an orbital plane.
