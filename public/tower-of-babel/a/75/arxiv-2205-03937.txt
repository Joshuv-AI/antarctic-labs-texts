Measure-valued growth processes in continuous space and growth properties starting from an infinite interface
Apolline Louvet, Amandine Véber

Abstract
The k k -parent and infinite-parent spatial Lambda-Fleming Viot processes (or SLFV), introduced in [ 35 ] , form a family of stochastic models for spatially expanding populations. These processes are akin to a continuous-space version of the classical Eden growth model (but with local backtracking of the occupied area allowed when k k is finite), while being associated with a dual process encoding ancestry. In this article, we focus on the growth properties of the area occupied by individuals of type 1 1 (type 0 0 encoding units of empty space). To do so, we first define the quantities that we shall use to quantify the speed of growth of the occupied area. Using the associated dual process and a comparison with a first-passage percolation problem, we show that the growth of the occupied region in the infinite-parent SLFV is linear in time. Because of the possibility of local backtracking of the occupied area, the result we obtain for the k k -parent SLFV is slightly weaker. It gives an upper bound on the probability that a given location is occupied at time t t , which also shows that growth in the k k -parent SLFV is linear in time. We use numerical simulations to approximate the growth speed for the infinite-parent SLFV, and we observe that the actual speed may be higher than the speed expected from simple first-moment calculations due to the characteristic front dynamics.



easure-valued growth processes

Introduction

Our aim in this work is to connect two worlds: measure-valued stochastic models of population dynamics, and set-valued stochastic growth models. More precisely, we shall be interested in the relation between a family of models introduced in
[
35
]
, called the
k
-parent and
\infty
-parent spatial
\Lambda
-Fleming-Viot processes (SLFV), and the processes describing the dynamics of the region occupied by a given type of individuals in such models, seen as a stochastic growth model in continuous space.

Our motivation for this is twofold. First, we want to show that techniques from (discrete-space) first-passage percolation theory can be used to understand the long term dynamics of the
occupied region
in the
k
-parent and
\infty
-parent SLFV. Second, we want to point out that measure-valued formulations of stochastic growth models provide a natural way of defining these models with initially occupied areas of infinite volume, such as a half-plane, and then studying the impact on the expansion of having an interface of infinite length (and therefore nontrivial fluctuations after some time) between the occupied and unoccupied regions.

1.1
A family of measure-valued models of population expansion

Measure-valued Markov processes are now classical tools to model and analyse the dynamics of spatially structured populations
[
5
,
6
,
16
,
23
]
, in particular when individuals have different genetic or phenotypic characters potentially impacting their reproduction and migration rates. In
[
6
]
, a model called the spatial
\Lambda
-Fleming Viot process was introduced, in which individuals are uniformly spread over some finite dimensional space (
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, say) and take their (genetic) types from a compact set
K
. At each time
t\geq 0
, the local type distribution at each
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is given by a probability distribution
\rho_{t}(z,d\kappa)
on
K
, and the state of the whole population is described by the measure

M_{t}(dz,d\kappa)=\rho_{t}(z,d\kappa)dz

(1.1)

on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times K
. The state of the process
(M_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is locally updated at the times of a Poisson point process of “reproduction” events specifying which region in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is affected, and what is the fraction of the local population which is updated during the event (with the interpretation that a fraction of the individuals in the area is replaced by offspring of an individual chosen to reproduce). This model comes with a set of tools which are extensions of classical tools from population genetics and which make it amenable to analysis. In particular, it is possible to find a
dual
process, interpreted as the genealogical process of a sample of individuals, whose law is linked to the law of
(M_{t})_{t\geq 0}
through a duality relation which is of great use for the analysis of the long term behaviour of
(M_{t})_{t\geq 0}
itself. We refer to Section 4 in
[
6
]
for more details, and to Section
2
below for the precise form of the dual processes and of the duality relations that will be of interest in the present work. While
[
6
]
focuses on the
neutral
case where the single type chosen to “reproduce” is sampled uniformly at random in the area of the region affected by the reproduction event, in
[
20
]
a bias in favour of the reproduction of one type (out of the two possible types
0
and
1
in that work) is introduced by sampling
two
types during the events called
selective
, and deciding that the “offspring” are of type
0
if and only if both sampled types (or “potential parents”) are 0. The corresponding process is called the
SLFV with fecundity selection
, and its dual process can again be considered as tracing back all the potential parents which may have influenced the type of a finite number of individuals sampled in the population at time
t
(see Section 1.2 in
[
20
]
). Observe from this informal description that if we start an SLFV process from a configuration where, at any location in space, we find only one type (that is, each
\rho_{0}(z,d\kappa)
is a Dirac mass at some
\kappa(z)\in K
), and if we assume that the fraction of local population replaced in all events is
1
, then at any time
t\geq 0
we find only one type at any given location. Thereby, we obtain a model in which space is partitioned into regions occupied by different types, and the boundaries of these regions evolve in time due to the Poisson point process of events. This observation yields a natural connection with models of stochastic geometry and set-valued processes, and strongly suggests that methods from these fields can be used to obtain results on the dynamics of the regions encoded by the measures
M_{t}
.

Focusing again on the case
K=\{0,1\}
, assuming that the fraction of local population replaced during all events is
1
and replacing the sampling of
2
types by the sampling of
k\geq 2
types during each reproduction event, we obtain the
k
-parent SLFV introduced in
[
35
]
to model the dynamics of an expanding population (type 1 individuals being “existing individuals” and type 0 individuals being considered as “non-existing”, or “ghosts”). From now on, we restrict our attention to the most biologically relevant dimension
d=2
. The alternative constructions of the process
(M^{(k)})_{t\geq 0}
are recalled in Sections
2.2
and
2.3
; here we satisfy ourselves with the following informal construction. Since
K=\{0,1\}
and since we anticipate that only one type of individuals is present at every location in space at any time, let us write (in the notation of (
1.1
))

w_{t}^{(k)}(z):=\rho_{t}^{(k)}(z,\{1\})\in\{0,1\},\qquad z\in\mathbb{R}^{2},\ t\geq 0.

(1.2)

For each given
t
, the function
w_{t}^{(k)}:z\mapsto w_{t}^{(k)}(z)
can be thought of as the indicator function of the area in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
occupied by individuals of type 1. However, as in all variants of the spatial
\Lambda
-Fleming-Viot process, the kernel
z\mapsto\rho_{t}^{(k)}(z,d\kappa)
(and consequently
w_{t}^{(k)}
) is defined up to Lebesgue null sets (that is, changing the value of
\rho_{t}^{(k)}
at a Lebesgue null set of points does not change the value of the measure
M_{t}^{(k)}
, which is the real object of interest). With this observation, we shall abuse the language and refer to
w_{t}^{(k)}
as a
density
for
M_{t}^{(k)}
. Motivated by experimental work on expanding populations of microorganisms (see in particular Figure 4 in
[
27
]
, in which the geometric properties of the reproduction events impact the diversity observed in the population), we suppose that each event has an elliptical shape encoded by a triplet
(a,b,\gamma)\in(0,+\infty)^{2}\times(-\pi/2,\pi/2)
giving its width, height and angle with respect to the
x
-axis (see Figure
2
). We fix a finite measure
\mu
on
(0,+\infty)^{2}\times(-\pi/2,\pi/2)
with compact support
S
, and we let
\Pi
be a Poisson point process on
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\times S
with intensity measure
dt\otimes dz\otimes\mu(da,db,d\gamma)
. We start from
M_{0}
of the form (
1.1
), and for every
(t,z,a,b,\gamma)\in\Pi
:

•

We sample
k
types
\kappa_{1},\ldots,\kappa_{k}\in\{0,1\}
independently according to the type distribution

\frac{1}{\mathrm{Vol}(\mathfrak{B}_{a,b,\gamma}(z))}\int_{\mathfrak{B}_{a,b,\gamma}(z)}M_{t-}^{(k)}(dy,d\kappa)

at time
t-
in the ellipse
\mathfrak{B}_{a,b,\gamma}(z)
with parameters
(a,b,\gamma)
and centred at
z
.

•

If at least one
\kappa_{i}
is equal to
1
, then for every
z^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{B}_{a,b,\gamma}(z)
we set
w_{t}^{(k)}(z^{\prime}):=1
. That is, we fill the area
\mathfrak{B}_{a,b,\gamma}(z)
with individuals that are all of type 1.

•

If all
\kappa_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq k
, are equal to
0
, then for every
z^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{B}_{a,b,\gamma}(z)
we set
w_{t}^{(k)}(z^{\prime}):=0
. That is, we fill the area
\mathfrak{B}_{a,b,\gamma}(z)
with individuals that are all of type 0.

•

Outside
\mathfrak{B}_{a,b,\gamma}(z)
, nothing changes, and
M_{t}^{(k)}
is set to be the measure with density
w_{t}^{(k)}
.

In the case where all event areas are balls (
i.e.
,
a=b
), it was proved in
[
35
]
that the process
(M_{t}^{(k)})_{t\geq 0}
is well-defined, Markovian and that it can be characterised by means of a well-posed martingale problem. These properties extend to the case where reproduction events are elliptical that we consider here. In the interest of completeness (and to be able to refer to them in future work), we state these results in Section
2
.

Remark 1.1
.

The assumption that
\mu
should have finite support is not necessary to define the
k
-parent SLFV (see
[
35
]
, where a less strict condition is formulated whose analogue for elliptical events applies here too). However, when we study the asymptotic growth of the region occupied by type 1 individuals, which is the main question in this paper, this assumption ensures that “expansion” events cover a bounded area, included in a box of large but fixed size. This fact then allows us to compare the growth process with an appropriate discrete space first-passage percolation model and to derive a lower bound on the expected time needed for the area occupied by type
1
individuals to reach a given point in space. See Section
5
for more details.

Thinking in terms of the area occupied by individuals of type 1 (which we shall call the
occupied area
from now on), we see that this random set has a tendency to expand, but for a fixed value of
k
it can also backtrack during an event in which the region affected contains both types of individuals (and hence overlaps the boundary of the occupied region) but, by chance, only type 0 individuals are sampled. However, the larger
k
, the more likely it is that at least one of the
k
types
\kappa_{i}
is equal to
1
whenever the frequency of type
1
in the area is strictly positive. In the limit as
k\rightarrow\infty
, we therefore obtain a pure growth process, called the
\infty
-parent SLFV and denoted by
(M_{t}^{(\infty)})_{t\geq 0}
, whose dynamics are as follows. Let
\Pi
be the same Poisson point process as earlier. We start from a measure
M_{0}
of the form (
1.1
) and for every
(t,z,a,b,\gamma)\in\Pi
:

•

If
\mathrm{Vol}(\mathfrak{B}_{a,b,\gamma}(z)\cap\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\,w_{t-}^{(\infty)}(y)=1\})>0
, then for every
z^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{B}_{a,b,\gamma}(z)
we set
w_{t}^{(\infty)}(z^{\prime}):=1
. That is, we fill the area
\mathfrak{B}_{a,b,\gamma}(z)
with individuals that are all of type 1.

•

Otherwise, for every
z^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{B}_{a,b,\gamma}(z)
we set
w_{t}^{(\infty)}(z^{\prime}):=0
(which modifies the value of the density at most in a set of locations of volume
0
).

•

Outside
\mathfrak{B}_{a,b,\gamma}(z)
, nothing changes, and
M_{t}^{(\infty)}
is set to be the measure with density
w_{t}^{(\infty)}
.

Again, the process
(M_{t}^{\infty})_{t\geq 0}
with ball-shaped events was proved to be well-defined, Markovian and solution to a well-posed martingale problem in
[
35
]
. For completeness, in Section
2
we state the analogues of these results in the case of elliptical events.

Remark 1.2
.

Until now, the
k
-parent and
\infty
-parent SLFV have been defined with only two types of individuals, existing and non-existing. In this case, it is tempting to conclude that the set-valued dynamics of the occupied region is a sufficient description of the population process. However, to address questions raised by experimental and theoretical work on expanding colonies of microorganisms and gene surfing
[
26
,
27
,
28
]
, in future work we shall define these models with a general, compact, type space
K
and individuals of different types competing to invade space. In this case, the measure-valued formulation is a more natural and flexible framework than the formulation in terms of a random partitioning of space describing the regions occupied by the different subpopulations.

Remark 1.3
.

Although our focus here is on models of expanding populations, let us remark that the
k
-parent and
\infty
-parent SLFV can also be interpreted as modelling spatially homogeneous populations with strong selection in favour of type 1 individuals (the larger
k
, the stronger the selective advantage of type 1 over type 0 individuals). In this perspective, our study follows on from a long series of work focusing mainly on weak selection regimes (see, e.g.,

[
18
,
19
,
20
]

for selection against one type,

[
22
]

for general forms of selection,

[
8
,
10
,
33
]

for fluctuating selection and

[
17
]

for selection against heterozygosity). Note however that in our regime of strong selection, no scaling of time or space is necessary.

1.2
Growth models starting from an occupied area of infinite volume

Formulated as above, the
\infty
-parent SLFV corresponds to a continuous space version of the Eden model
[
15
]
and is very similar to models of continuous-space first-passage percolation, and in particular to the model introduced by Deijfen in
[
12
]
. Apart from their state spaces (Deijfen’s process being set-valued), the main difference between the
\infty
-parent SLFV, and Deijfen’s process and its multitype extensions studied in
[
12
,
13
,
14
]
, is that in the latter, a reproduction event results in the expansion of the area occupied by type 1 individuals only when the centre of the event is already occupied (whereas in the
\infty
-parent SLFV, expansion occurs whenever there is a positive fraction of type 1 individuals in the area of the event). It is not clear how this subtle difference impacts the qualitative behaviour of the random growth, as a significant number of additional events occur in the
\infty
-parent SLFV. Note however that the expansion of the occupied region in the
\infty
-parent SLFV is obviously faster than in Deijfen’s model.

A second, and perhaps more important, difference is that the
\infty
-parent SLFV is defined for very general initial conditions, which are not restricted to occupied regions of finite volume. In particular, this is why the definition provided in the previous section is only informal: when

\mathrm{Vol}(\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\,w_{t}^{(\infty)}(z)=1\})=+\infty,

an infinite number of reproduction events intersect the occupied area in any time interval, and the global jump rate of the process is infinite. This difficulty is handled by endowing the set of measures of the form (
1.1
) with the topology of vague convergence. In this way, only the changes in compact areas of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
are used to define the dynamics of
(M_{t}^{(\infty)})_{t\geq 0}
, and since these changes occur at finite rate, it gives rise to a well-defined process characterised as the solution to a well-posed martingale problem. This construction is detailed in Sections
2.1
and
2.2
. In
[
24
,
25
]
, an alternative construction of Deijfen’s model is proposed, in which the time and centre coordinates of the points in the Poisson point process
\Pi
are used to allocate “contamination times” to all other points than the centre in the area of the events (representing the time needed for an infection to travel from the centre to any other point in the area of the event). A point
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
then lies in the occupied area at time
t
if there is a finite path allowing to travel from the initial occupied area to
y
before time
t
. This construction, very reminiscent of the formulation of discrete-space first-passage percolation models in terms of edge-passing times, allows arbitrary initially occupied areas. It has many merits, in particular it enables us to use the fine techniques from percolation theory to analyse the growth dynamics. However, it is specific to Deijfen’s model and does not seem easily generalisable to other event-based expansion rules (including our own), especially when we want to allow local backtracking of the occupied area as in the
k
-parent SLFV.

In
[
12
,
14
,
25
]
, a shape theorem is proved for Deijfen’s process
(E_{t})_{t\geq 0}
starting from a ball centred at
0
. More precisely, it is shown that when the distribution of the radii of the events has compact support
[
12
]
, or has an exponential moment
[
14
]
, the scaled occupied area
t^{-1}E_{t}
converges almost surely as
t
tends to infinity to an asymptotic shape, which by isotropy is a ball. In
[
25
]
, the authors obtain a necessary and sufficient condition on the distribution of event radii for such a shape theorem to hold true. We shall instead focus on growth starting from a half-plane
H
and on the expansion of the occupied region in the direction transverse to the boundary of
H
. We shall similarly be interested in the asymptotic behaviour of the
k
-parent SLFV, in which the occupied area can also backtrack (which is not the case in the
\infty
-parent SLFV).

To place our work in the more global context already hinted at, first-passage percolation processes are stochastic growth models originally defined on a lattice (generally
\mathbb{Z}^{d},\,d\geq 2
). In these models, each vertex is either occupied, or empty. If vertex
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
becomes occupied at time
t
, and if it is connected to a vertex
y\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
by an edge (denoted
e
), then vertex
y
becomes occupied at time
t+\tau_{e}
, where
\tau_{e}
is independent from one edge to another (but not necessarily identically distributed).
Whether the expansion is linear in time depends on the distribution of the time needed to pass through any given edge of the grid (see,
e.g.
,
[
4
]
) and whether one considers short-range percolation, such as nearest neighbour percolation, or long-range percolation, in which two vertices
x,y\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
are connected by an edge no matter the distance between them
[
9
,
11
,
36
]
. In particular, when edge passing times are distributed as in the Eden growth model, growth is linear in time for short-range percolation and potentially faster for long-range percolation, depending on the relation between the distribution of
\tau_{e}
and the distance between the two vertices it connects
[
4
,
9
]
. Part of the proof of our main results will make use of the linear expansion of the occupied area in an appropriate first-passage percolation model, thanks to a natural coupling argument (see Section
5
). Works like
[
12
,
14
,
30
]

are interesting examples of extensions of first-passage percolation models to a continuous setting.

When the growth is linear in time, in general it is possible to obtain lower and upper bounds on the speed of growth (see,
e.g.
,
[
2
,
40
]
), or to use simulations to approximate it
[
1
]
. For other stochastic growth models, such as the corner growth model
[
38
]
, which belongs to the family of
last-passage percolation models
, it is possible to obtain an explicit speed of growth for specific passage time distributions
[
37
]
. As many other growth models, the Eden model is conjectured to belong to the universality class of the Kardar-Parisi-Zhang (KPZ) equation
[
32
]
. This equation generates rough fronts, whose characteristics are similar to the ones of fronts observed in some expanding biological populations (see,
e.g.
,
[
31
]
). Such a conjecture is notably difficult to establish; to our knowledge, it has only been demonstrated in the case of the solid-on-solid growth model in
[
7
]
.

1.3
Linear growth of the occupied region in the
k
-parent and
\infty
-parent SLFV

As we already explained, in what follows we suppose that, initially, the occupied region is the half-plane

H=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:x<0\}.

(1.3)

That is, the initial value of each
k
-parent SLFV (
k\in\{2,3,\ldots,\infty\}
) is taken to be the measure
M^{H}
defined as follows:

M^{H}(dz,d\kappa):=\big(\mathds{1}_{H}(z)\delta_{1}(d\kappa)+\mathds{1}_{H^{c}}(z)\delta_{0}(d\kappa)\big)dz,

(1.4)

and we write
w^{H}=\mathds{1}_{H}
for its density. We let
(M_{t}^{(k)})_{t\geq 0}
be the
k
-parent SLFV starting from
M^{H}
, and let
(w_{t}^{(k)})_{t\geq 0}
be its density process.

We are interested in the expansion transverse to the interface
\{(0,y),\,y\in\mathbb{R}\}
of the area initially occupied by type 1 individuals and, to measure it, we consider the first time at which the point
(x,0)
is occupied for
x>0
. Because we work with measures, whose densities are defined up to Lebesgue null sets, we need a definition of occupancy of
(x,0)
which does not depend on the choice of density for
M_{t}^{(k)}
. To this end, let
\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}(z)
be the closed ball of radius
\epsilon
centred at
z
, and
V_{\epsilon}
be the volume of this ball.

Definition 1.4
.

Let
M
be a measure of the form (
1.1
), with density
w_{M}
. We say that
z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
is occupied in
M
if

\lim\limits_{\epsilon\to 0}V_{\epsilon}^{-1}\int_{\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}(z)}w_{M}(z^{\prime})dz^{\prime}>0,

and that
z
is empty in
M
otherwise.

The fact that the limit in Definition
1.4
exists is a consequence of the fact that
w_{M}(z^{\prime})dz^{\prime}
is absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure, and so the Lebesgue differentiation theorem applies.

First, we focus on the growth of the occupied region in the
\infty
-parent SLFV. To do so, let us introduce the following random times.

Definition 1.5
.

For all
x>0
, let

\overrightarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,(\infty)}:=\min\left\{t\geq 0:\lim\limits_{\epsilon\to 0}V_{\epsilon}^{-1}\int_{\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}((x,0))}w_{t}^{(\infty)}(z)dz>0\right\}.

The fact that
\overrightarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,(\infty)}
can be defined as a minimum rather than an infimum is a consequence of Lemma
2.14
below (which states that in all
k
-parent SLFV, the value of
M^{(k)}
in a given compact area in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
is updated at a bounded rate), and of the fact that the trajectories of
M^{(\infty)}
are càdlàg by construction. Since a chain of events leading to
(x,0)
does not need to pass through all points
(x^{\prime},0)
with
x^{\prime}<x
, the function
x\to\overrightarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,(\infty)}
is not necessarily increasing. See Figure
1
for an illustration. Our first main result is the following.

Theorem 1.6
.

There exists
\nu^{(\infty)}>0
such that

\lim\limits_{x\to+\infty}\frac{\mathbb{E}\big[\overrightarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,(\infty)}\big]}{x}=\nu^{(\infty)}\quad\text{and}\quad\lim\limits_{x\to+\infty}\frac{\overrightarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,(\infty)}}{x}=\nu^{(\infty)}\quad\text{in probability.}

The proof of Theorem
1.6
requires a series of comparison arguments and estimates that are presented in Sections
3
,
4
and
5
. It is completed at the end of Section
5
. The proof also provides a lower bound on the speed of growth
(\nu^{(\infty)})^{-1}
in terms of the speed of the “express chain” defined in Section
4.2
, see Remark
4.13
. However, the numerical exploration of the growth dynamics of the
\infty
-parent SLFV performed in Section

shows that this lower bound can be highly suboptimal.

Let us now consider the dynamics of the occupied region in the
k
-parent SLFV. Due to the possible local backtracking of the boundary of the occupied region, the dynamics of the
k
-parent SLFV is significantly different from models of continuous first-passage percolation. In particular, we are no longer able to use the fine subadditivity argument of
[
34
]
to show convergence of the normalised hitting time of
(x,0)
. Instead, we obtain the following result.

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
k\geq 2
. There exists
\nu^{(k)}\geq\nu^{(\infty)}
such that for all
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
x(\varepsilon)>0
satisfying that for every
x\geq x(\varepsilon)
and every
t>0
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\lim\limits_{\epsilon\to 0}V_{\epsilon}^{-1}\int_{\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}((x,0))}w_{t}^{(k)}(z)dz\,=\,0\right)\leq\frac{\big(\nu^{(k)}+\varepsilon\big)x}{t}.

Note that the result of Theorem
1.7
is weaker than that of Theorem
1.6
but essentially says that for large
x
, the point
(x,0)
belongs to the occupied area after a time at most of the order of
\nu^{(k)}x
.

). In Section

to reformulate Theorems

linear in time. Combining the results from both sections yields Theorem
1.6
. In Section
6
, we use an analogous strategy to prove Theorem
1.7
. Finally, in Section
7
we use numerical simulations to approximate the growth speed of the area occupied by type
1
individuals, and we compare it to the lower bound obtained in Section
4
. This bound was initially conjectured to be equal to (or at least a good approximation for) the growth speed, but the simulations show that the actual speed can be significantly higher. Moreover, they suggest that the growth is driven by “spikes” which occur at the front and then thicken in all directions. Providing a precise description of the impact of these fluctuations of the interface on the growth speed remains an open question.

2
Spatial
\Lambda
-Fleming Viot processes with elliptical events

In this section, we rigorously define the
k
-parent and
\infty
-parent SLFV with elliptical events and their dual processes, adapting the different constructions carried out in
[
35
]
in the case where all events are ball-shaped.

2.1
State space and notation for ellipses

Let us first formalise the set of measures of the form (
1.1
) in which our SLFV processes will take their values. Let
\mathcal{M}
be the set of all measures on
\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\{0,1\}
whose marginal distribution over
\mathbb{R}^{2}
is Lebesgue measure. Such measures can be decomposed as in (
1.1
):

M(dz,d\kappa)=\rho_{M}(z,d\kappa)dz=\big(w_{M}(z)\delta_{1}(d\kappa)+(1-w_{M}(z))\delta_{0}(d\kappa)\big)dz,

(2.1)

where for almost all
z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
\rho_{M}(z,d\kappa)
is a probability distribution on
\{0,1\}
and
w_{M}(z)=\rho_{M}(z,\{1\})
. In this decomposition, the mapping
z\mapsto\rho_{M}(z,d\kappa)
is defined up to Lebesgue null sets:
\rho_{M}
and
\rho^{\prime}_{M}
are equivalent if and only if they give rise to the same measure
M
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\{0,1\}
, and so are the corresponding
densities

w_{M}=\rho_{M}(\cdot,\{1\})
and
w^{\prime}_{M}=\rho^{\prime}_{M}(\cdot,\{1\})
. In general,
w_{M}
takes its values in the interval
[0,1]
. The state space of our SLFV processes will be the subset of
\mathcal{M}
of all measures admitting a density
w_{M}
taking values in
\{0,1\}
(that is, for every
z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
\rho_{M}(z,d\kappa)
is either a Dirac mass at
0
or a Dirac mass at
1
):

\mathcal{M}_{\lambda}:=\big\{M\in\mathcal{M}\,:\,\exists w_{M}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\{0,1\}\hbox{ satisfying }\eqref{decomposition}\big\}.

(2.2)

We endow
\mathcal{M}_{\lambda}
with the topology of vague convergence. The space of all càdlàg
\mathcal{M}_{\lambda}
-valued trajectories is denoted by
D_{\mathcal{M}_{\lambda}}[0,+\infty)
and we equip this space with the standard Skorokhod topology.

Remark 2.1
.

In
[
35
]
,
w_{M}
was defined as the local proportion of type 0 individuals, so that the occupied region was the set of locations at which
w_{M}(z)=0
. To work with more natural notation, here we define
w_{M}
as the local proportion of type 1 individuals (corresponding to
1-w_{M}
in
[
35
]
) and the occupied region is the set of locations at which
w_{M}(z)=1
.

Let us also set the notation regarding ellipses.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
z_{c}=(x_{c},y_{c})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
(a,b)\in(0,+\infty)^{2}
and
\gamma\in(-\pi/2,\pi/2)
. The ellipse with centre
z_{c}
and parameters
(a,b,\gamma)
, denoted by
\mathfrak{B}_{a,b,\gamma}(z_{c})
, is defined by:

\mathfrak{B}_{a,b,\gamma}(z_{c})=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}x_{c}\\
y_{c}\end{pmatrix}+A_{\gamma}\begin{pmatrix}ar\cos(\theta)\\
br\sin(\theta)\end{pmatrix}:r\in[0,1],\theta\in[0,2\pi)\right\},

where

A_{\gamma}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos(\gamma)&-\sin(\gamma)\\
\sin(\gamma)&\cos(\gamma)\end{pmatrix}.

See Figure
2
for an illustration. We denote the volume of an ellipse with parameters
(a,b,\gamma)
by

V_{a,b}:=\text{Vol}(\mathfrak{B}_{a,b,\gamma}(0)).

(2.3)

Note that this volume is independent of the angle
\gamma
.

:

(a,b,\gamma)
.

2.2
Definition as the unique solution to a martingale problem

2.2.1
Martingale problem

All the random objects we consider in this section are defined on some probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
and we let
\mathbb{E}
denote the expectation with respect to
\mathbb{P}
.

First, let us introduce the test functions on which the martingale problems characterizing the
k
-parent and
\infty
-parent SLFV will be defined.
Let
C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
be the space of all continuously differentiable functions
F:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, and let
C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
be the space of all continuous and compactly supported functions
f:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
. For all
f\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and
F\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, we define
\Psi_{F,f}:\mathcal{M}_{\lambda}\to\mathbb{R}
by

\forall\,M\in\mathcal{M}_{\lambda},\qquad\Psi_{F,f}(M):=F\left(\langle w_{M},f\rangle\right),

(2.4)

where we recall that
w_{M}
denotes a density of
M
(fixed in an arbitrary way), and

\langle w_{M},f\rangle:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f(z)w_{M}(z)dz.

Note that the value of
\Psi_{F,f}(M)
does not depend on the choice of the density
w_{M}
. The set of functions that we shall use in the formulation of the martingale problems is then the set of functions of the form
\Psi_{F,f}
with
F\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
and
f\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

For all
(a,b)\in(0,+\infty)^{2}
,
\gamma\in(-\pi/2,\pi/2)
and
f\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, let

\text{Supp}^{a,b,\gamma}(f):=\left\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\text{Vol}\left(\mathfrak{B}_{a,b,\gamma}(z)\cap\text{Supp}(f)\right)\neq 0\right\},

(2.5)

where
\text{Supp}(f)
stands for the support of
f
. The set
\text{Supp}^{a,b,\gamma}(f)
can be interpreted as the set of all potential centres
z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
for ellipses with parameters
(a,b,\gamma)
overlapping the support of
f
.

For all
z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
w:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\{0,1\}
, let
\Theta_{z}^{a,b,\gamma}(w):\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\{0,1\}
and
\overline{\Theta}_{z}^{a,b,\gamma}(w):\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\{0,1\}
be the functions defined by

\displaystyle\Theta_{z}^{a,b,\gamma}(w)

\displaystyle:=\mathds{1}_{(\mathfrak{B}_{a,b,\gamma}(z))^{c}}w+\mathds{1}_{\mathfrak{B}_{a,b,\gamma}(z)},\qquad\hbox{and}

\displaystyle\text{and}\quad\overline{\Theta}_{z}^{a,b,\gamma}(w)

\displaystyle:=\mathds{1}_{(\mathfrak{B}_{a,b,\gamma}(z))^{c}}w.

(2.6)

That is, if
w
is the indicator function of a set
E
(representing the occupied area in our application), then
\Theta_{z}^{a,b,\gamma}(w)
is the indicator function of the set
E\cup\mathfrak{B}_{a,b,\gamma}(z)
and
\overline{\Theta}_{z}^{a,b,\gamma}(w)
is the indicator function of the set
E\setminus\mathfrak{B}_{a,b,\gamma}(z)
(which is equal to
E
if
E\cap\mathfrak{B}_{a,b,\gamma}(z)=\emptyset
).

Let
\mu
be a finite measure on
(0,+\infty)^{2}\times(-\pi/2,\pi/2)
such that there exists
\mathcal{R}>0
satisfying

\mu\Big(\big((0,\mathcal{R}]\times(0,\mathcal{R}]\big)^{c}\times(-\pi/2,\pi/2)\Big)=0.

Let
\mathcal{R}_{\mu}
be the smallest
\mathcal{R}>0
such that the above condition is satisfied, and set

S_{\mu}:=(0,\mathcal{R}_{\mu}]\times(0,\mathcal{R}_{\mu}]\times(-\pi/2,\pi/2).

(2.7)

We are now ready to introduce the operators
\mathcal{L}_{\mu}^{k}
,
k\geq 2
, and
\mathcal{L}_{\mu}^{\infty}
that we shall use to formulate the martingale problems. For every
F\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
,
f\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and
M\in\mathcal{M}_{\lambda}
, let us define

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mu}^{k}\Psi_{F,f}(M):=\int_{S_{\mu}}\int_{\text{Supp}^{a,b,\gamma}(f)}\int_{\mathfrak{B}_{a,b,\gamma}(z)^{k}}\frac{1}{V_{a,b}^{k}}\,

\displaystyle\left[\left(\prod_{j=1}^{k}\left(1-w_{M}(z_{j})\right)\right)\,F(\langle\overline{\Theta}_{z}^{a,b,\gamma}(w_{M}),f\rangle)\right.

\displaystyle+\left(1-\prod_{j=1}^{k}(1-w_{M}(z_{j}))\right)\,F(\langle\Theta_{z}^{a,b,\gamma}(w_{M}),f\rangle)

\displaystyle-\left.F(\langle w_{M},f\rangle)\vphantom{\prod_{j=1}^{k}}\right]dz_{1}\ldots dz_{k}dz\mu(da,db,d\gamma)

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mu}^{\infty}\Psi_{F,f}(M):=\int_{S_{\mu}}\int_{\text{Supp}^{a,b,\gamma}(f)}

\displaystyle\left(1-\mathds{1}_{\{0\}}\left(\int_{\mathfrak{B}_{a,b,\gamma}(z)}w_{M}(z^{\prime})dz^{\prime}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\times\left(F\left(\langle\Theta_{z}^{a,b,\gamma}(w_{M}),f\rangle\right)-F\left(\langle w_{M},f\rangle\right)\right)dz\mu(da,db,d\gamma).

These operators exactly encode the dynamics described informally in the introduction. Indeed,
\mathcal{L}_{\mu}^{k}
(
resp.
,
\mathcal{L}_{\mu}^{\infty}
) is the generator of a jump process with rates given by the intensity measure of the Poisson point process used in the informal description of the
k
-parent SLFV (
resp.
, the
\infty
-parent SLFV). Moreover, the product
\prod_{j}(1-w_{M}(z_{j}))
in the formulation of
\mathcal{L}_{\mu}^{k}
is the indicator function that none of the
k
‘parental’ locations sampled uniformly at random over
\mathfrak{B}_{a,b,\gamma}(z)
during the event is already occupied, in which case the whole area of the event is subtracted from the occupied region (an operation encoded by
\overline{\Theta}_{z}^{a,b,\gamma}(w_{M})
); otherwise, the area of the event is added to the occupied area, as described by
\Theta_{z}^{a,b,\gamma}(w_{M})
. Likewise, in the formulation of
\mathcal{L}_{\mu}^{\infty}
, if the volume of occupied points in
\mathfrak{B}_{a,b,\gamma}(z)
is zero then no changes occur; otherwise, the area of the reproduction event is added to the occupied area.

The following result enables us to give a rigorous definition of the
k
-parent and
\infty
-parent SLFV.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
M^{0}\in\mathcal{M}_{\lambda}
.

(i)

For all
k\geq 2
, there exists a unique
D_{\mathcal{M}_{\lambda}}[0,+\infty)
-valued process
(M_{t}^{(k)})_{t\geq 0}
such that
M_{0}^{(k)}=M^{0}
and, for all
F\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
and
f\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
,

\left(\Psi_{F,f}\big(M_{t}^{(k)}\big)-\Psi_{F,f}\big(M_{0}^{(k)}\big)-\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{L}_{\mu}^{k}\Psi_{F,f}\big(M_{s}^{(k)}\big)ds\right)_{t\geq 0}

(2.8)

is a martingale. Moreover, the process
(M^{(k)}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is Markovian.

(ii)

There exists a unique
D_{\mathcal{M}_{\lambda}}[0,+\infty)
-valued process
(M_{t}^{(\infty)})_{t\geq 0}
such that
M_{0}^{(\infty)}=M^{0}
and, for all
F\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
and
f\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
,

\left(\Psi_{F,f}\big(M_{t}^{(\infty)}\big)-\Psi_{F,f}\big(M_{0}^{(\infty)}\big)-\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{L}_{\mu}^{\infty}\Psi_{F,f}\big(M_{s}^{(\infty)}\big)ds\right)_{t\geq 0}

(2.9)

is a martingale. Moreover, the process
(M^{(\infty)}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is Markovian.

We then define the
k
-parent SLFV (
resp.
,
\infty
-parent SLFV) associated with
\mu
and with initial condition
M^{0}
as the unique solution to the martingale problem
(\mathcal{L}_{\mu}^{k},\delta_{M^{0}})
(
resp.
,
(\mathcal{L}_{\mu}^{\infty},\delta_{M^{0}})
).

The proof of Theorem
2.3

(i)
is identical to the proof of Theorem 2.1 in
[
35
]
, and therefore we omit it. The key observation is that the shapes of the reproduction events play no critical role in the definition of the process as long as their radii are controlled. It is the case here because we require that
\mu
should be a finite measure with compact support. Theorem
2.3

(ii)
then follows from exactly the same construction of the process of densities
(w_{M_{t}^{(\infty)}})_{t\geq 0}
via the sequential coupling of densities of
M^{(k)}
,
k\geq 2
, as in
[
35
]
(during each event, we use the same
k
-sample of locations as for
w_{M^{(k)}}
, plus a
(k+1)
-th independent sample, to construct
w_{M^{(k+1)}}
– see Theorem 2.9 in
[
35
]
and Section
2.3
below). Again, because the proof in the case of ball-shaped events is long and technical and its adaptation to the case with elliptical events does not require new arguments, we do not repeat it.

2.2.2
Duality relations

As in the case of ball-shaped events studied in
[
35
]
, a key property of the
k
-parent and
\infty
-parent SLFV with elliptical events is the existence of a dual process, which is not only at the core of the strategy to prove uniqueness in law of the
D_{\mathcal{M}_{\lambda}}[0,\infty)
-valued solutions to the martingale problems stated in Theorem
2.3
, but also provides a way to investigate properties of the expansion dynamics. These duality relations are stated in Propositions
2.5
and
2.9
below. As often in such systems, the dual processes consist in tracing back in time the origins of the types contained in some compact area at a given time
t
, using the fact that the time-reversal of a Poisson point process with intensity measure
dt\otimes dz\otimes\mu(da,db,d\gamma)
is again a Poisson point process with the same intensity measure. The duality formulae essentially state that the probability that a given set
E
of points in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
contains no type
1
(“existing”) individuals at time
t
is equal to the probability that, at time
0
, there are no type
1
individuals in the whole set of locations which, at some time
s\in[0,t]
, have influenced the type composition in
E
at time
t
. To see where this interpretation comes from, take
E=\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{l}\}
in Proposition
2.5
, and
E=E^{0}
(a finite union of measurable connected subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
of finite volume) in Proposition
2.9
.

Let us now proceed with the rigorous definition of the dual processes and the statement of the duality relations. These processes will be defined on a new probability space
(\mathbf{\Omega},\mathbf{\mathcal{F}},\mathbf{P})
, and
\mathbf{E}
denotes the expectation with respect to
\mathbf{P}
. Recall that
\mu
is the finite measure with compact support on
(0,\infty)^{2}\times(-\pi/2,\pi/2)
introduced just before (
2.7
).

The
k
-parent ancestral process.

The dual process associated with the
k
-parent SLFV is defined on the set
\mathcal{M}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
of all finite counting measures on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. We equip this state space with the topology of weak convergence. For all
\Xi=\sum_{i=1}^{l}\delta_{\xi_{i}}\in\mathcal{M}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, for all
z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
(a,b)\in(0,+\infty)^{2}
and
\gamma\in(-\pi/2,\pi/2)
, let us define

I_{z,a,b,\gamma}(\Xi):=\big\{i\in\{1,\ldots,l\}:\xi_{i}\in\mathfrak{B}_{a,b,\gamma}(z)\big\}.

In words,
I_{z,a,b,\gamma}(\Xi)
is the set of all indices of the points in
\Xi
which lie in
\mathfrak{B}_{a,b,\gamma}(z)
.

Definition 2.4
.

(
k
-parent ancestral process) Let
k\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0,1\}
and
\Xi^{0}\in\mathcal{M}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. The
k
-parent ancestral process
(\Xi_{t}^{k})_{t\geq 0}
associated with
\mu
and with initial condition
\Xi^{0}
is the
\mathcal{M}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
-valued Markov jump process defined as follows. Let
\overleftarrow{\Pi}
be a Poisson point process on
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\times S_{\mu}
with intensity measure
dt\otimes dz\otimes\mu(da,db,d\gamma)
(defined on
(\mathbf{\Omega},\mathbf{\mathcal{F}},\mathbf{P})
), and let
\Xi_{0}^{k}=\Xi^{0}
. Then, for every
(t,z,a,b,\gamma)\in\overleftarrow{\Pi}
, writing

\Xi_{t-}^{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{t-}^{k}}\delta_{\xi_{t-}^{k,i}}\,,

(where
N_{t-}^{k}
denotes the number of atoms in
\Xi_{t-}^{k}
and
\xi_{t-}^{k,i}
the location in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
of the
i
-th atom):

•

If
I_{z,a,b,\gamma}(\Xi_{t-}^{k})\neq\emptyset
, we sample
k
points
z_{1},...,z_{k}
independently and uniformly at random in
\mathfrak{B}_{a,b,\gamma}(z)
, and we set

\Xi_{t}^{k}:=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{t-}^{k}}\delta_{\xi_{t-}^{k,i}}-\sum_{i\in I_{z,a,b,\gamma}(\Xi_{t-}^{k})}\delta_{\xi_{t-}^{k,i}}+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\delta_{z_{j}}.

That is, we remove all the atoms of
\Xi_{t-}^{k}
sitting in
\mathfrak{B}_{a,b,\gamma}(z)
, and we add
k
atoms at locations that are i.i.d and uniformly distributed over the ellipse
\mathfrak{B}_{a,b,\gamma}(z)
.

•

If
I_{z,a,b,\gamma}(\Xi_{t-}^{k})=\emptyset
, then no jump occurs and
\Xi_{t}^{k}=\Xi_{t-}^{k}
.

This definition is analogous to Definition 2.6 in
[
35
]
in the case of ball-shaped events, and the fact that it gives rise to a well-defined process follows again from the property that the number of atoms in
\Xi^{k}
can be stochastically bounded by a Yule process with
k
offspring and finite reproduction rate.

The
k
-parent SLFV
M^{(k)}
and the
k
-parent ancestral process
\Xi^{k}
associated with
\mu
then satisfy the following duality relation. For
z_{1},\ldots,z_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we write
\Xi[z_{1},...z_{l}]:=\sum_{i=1}^{l}\delta_{z_{i}}
.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
k\geq 2
. Let
M^{0}\in\mathcal{M}_{\lambda}
. Any solution
(M^{(k)}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
to the martingale problem (
2.8
) satisfies that for every
l\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}
and every integrable function
\psi
on
(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{l}
, we have for all
t\geq 0

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{M^{0}}\Bigg[

\displaystyle\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{l}}\psi(z_{1},...,z_{l})\left\{\prod_{j=1}^{l}\left(1-w_{M_{t}^{(k)}}(z_{j})\right)\right\}dz_{1}...dz_{l}\Bigg]

\displaystyle=\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{l}}\psi(z_{1},...,z_{l})\mathbf{E}_{\Xi[z_{1},...,z_{l}]}\Bigg[\prod_{j=1}^{N_{t}^{k}}\left(1-w_{M^{0}}(\xi_{t}^{k,j})\right)\Bigg]dz_{1}...dz_{l}\,,

where
N_{t}^{k}
and
\xi_{t}^{k,j}
,
1\leq j\leq N_{t}^{k}
, are such that
\Xi_{t}^{k}=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{t}^{k}}\delta_{\xi_{t}^{k,j}}
.

Remark 2.6
.

Note that the quantities appearing on both sides of the equality in Proposition
2.5
can be expressed as integrals with respect to
M_{t}^{(k)}
and
M^{0}
, and so they do not depend on the choice of the densities
w_{M_{t}^{(k)}}
and
w_{M^{0}}
.

This result can be seen as the exact analogue of Proposition 2.8 in
[
35
]
once we remember that in
[
35
]
,
w_{M^{(k)}}
denotes the frequency of
type 0
(or “non-existing”) individuals, which is
1-w_{M^{(k)}}
in our notation. Again, we do not repeat the proof here.

The
\infty
-parent ancestral process.

Unlike the processes
\Xi^{k}
taking their values in
\mathcal{M}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, the dual process of the
\infty
-parent SLFV is defined on the set
\mathcal{E}^{cf}
of all finite unions of measurable connected subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with positive and finite Lebesgue measure, that we endow with the topology induced by the Hausdorff distance. Recall the definition of the Poisson point process
\overleftarrow{\Pi}
introduced in Definition
2.4
.

Definition 2.7
.

(
\infty
-parent ancestral process) Let
E^{0}\in\mathcal{E}^{cf}
. The
\mathcal{E}^{cf}
-valued
\infty
-parent ancestral process
(E_{t}^{\infty})_{t\geq 0}
associated with
\mu
and with initial condition
E^{0}
is defined as follows. Let
E_{0}^{\infty}=E^{0}
. Then for every
(t,z,a,b,\gamma)\in\overleftarrow{\Pi}
,

•

if
E_{t-}^{\infty}\cap\mathfrak{B}_{a,b,\gamma}(z)
has non-zero Lebesgue measure, we set

E_{t}^{\infty}=E_{t-}^{\infty}\cup\mathfrak{B}_{a,b,\gamma}(z);

•

otherwise, no jump occurs and
E_{t}^{\infty}=E_{t-}^{\infty}
.

Lemma 2.8
.

The process
(E_{t}^{\infty})_{t\geq 0}
of Definition
2.7
is well-defined and Markovian.

Proof.

Recall that for all
(t,z,a,b,\gamma)\in\overleftarrow{\Pi}
, we have
(a,b)\in(0,\mathcal{R}_{\mu}]^{2}
(by definition of
\mathcal{R}_{\mu}
). Therefore,

\mathfrak{B}_{a,b,\gamma}(z)\subseteq\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}(z)\text{ a.s.,}

where
\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}(z)
is the ball of radius
\mathcal{R}_{\mu}
centred at
z
,
and so we can bound the jump rate of
(E_{t}^{\infty})_{t\geq 0}
from above by that of a
\infty
-parent ancestral process with the same initial condition and associated with
\mu(S_{\mu})\delta_{(\mathcal{R}_{\mu},\mathcal{R}_{\mu},0)}(da,db,d\gamma)
, which is finite (see Section
4
in
[
35
]
).
∎

The
\infty
-parent SLFV and the
\infty
-parent ancestral process associated with the measure
\mu
then satisfy the following duality relation.

Proposition 2.9
.

Let
M^{0}\in\mathcal{M}_{\lambda}
. Any solution
(M^{(\infty)}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
to the martingale problem (
2.9
) satisfies that for every
E^{0}\in\mathcal{E}^{cf}
, we have for all
t\geq 0

\mathbb{P}_{M^{0}}\bigg(\int_{E^{0}}w_{M_{t}^{(\infty)}}(z)dz=0\bigg)=\mathbf{P}_{E^{0}}\bigg(\int_{E_{t}^{\infty}}w_{M^{0}}(z)dz=0\bigg).

(2.10)

As mentioned at the beginning of the section, this duality relation can be interpreted as follows. Whenever a reproduction event affects one area, all the individuals in the area can be considered as
potential parents
. If
\overleftarrow{\Pi}
encodes the reproduction events affecting the population when going
backwards in time
, then
E_{t}^{\infty}
encodes the locations of the potential ancestors at time
0
of the individuals living in
E^{0}
at time
t
. The duality relation then states that a given area
E^{0}
contains only type 0 individuals at time
t
(
i.e.
, is “empty” at time
t
) if and only if all their potential ancestors at time
0
(located in
E_{t}^{\infty}
) are type 0 individuals. Note however that the duality relation only relates the
laws
of the Markov processes
M^{(\infty)}
and
E^{\infty}
, as the two processes for now are not even constructed on the same probability space.

Remark 2.10
.

The careful reader will note that in
[
35
]
, the dual process of the
\infty
-parent SLFV is formulated as an
\mathcal{M}_{\lambda}
-valued process instead of a set-valued process: instead of considering
(E_{t}^{\infty})_{t\geq 0}
, the dual process is taken to be
(m(E_{t}^{\infty}))_{t\geq 0}
, where
m(E)=\mathds{1}_{E}(x)dx
. This choice resulted from the fact that one was interested in the convergence of the

2.3
Poisson point process-based construction

As discussed right after the statement of Theorem

(ii)
in Section

-parent SLFV itself) was first obtained as the limit as

We present this construction here as it will allow us to show

1.

2.

Each (local) update of the process is done in an area shaped as an ellipse.

3.

The occupied area in the

-parent SLFV can only increase as time goes on.

For all
(a,b,\gamma)\in S_{\mu}

Lemma 2.15
.

2.4
The
\infty
-parent SLFV as a random growing set

The informal construction of the
\infty
-parent SLFV suggests that the occupied area is a random growing set. However, this is not a immediate consequence of any of the two possible constructions of the
\infty
-parent SLFV we just introduced. The goal of this section is to show how we can use these two complementary constructions to prove that the
\infty
-parent SLFV corresponds to a random growing set to some extent.

Recall the definition of occupancy of a point given in Definition
1.4
. In the case of the
k
-parent and
\infty
-parent SLFV, by Lemma
2.14
, the occupancy of a location in space is updated at a finite rate. Moreover, the limit appearing in Definition

is generally
\{0,1\}
-valued, in the following sense.

Lemma 2.17
.

Let
M^{0}\in\mathcal{M}_{\lambda}
, and let
z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Assume that there exists
\epsilon_{0}>0
such that

V_{\epsilon_{0}}^{-1}\int_{\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon_{0}}(z)}w_{M^{0}}(z^{\prime})dz^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}.

(2.15)

For
k\in\{2,3,\ldots,+\infty\}
, let
(M_{t}^{(k)})_{t\geq 0}
be the
k
-parent SLFV associated with
\mu
and with initial condition

. Then, for all

,

\lim\limits_{\epsilon\to 0}V_{\epsilon}^{-1}\int_{\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}(z)}w_{M_{t}^{(k)}}(z^{\prime})dz^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}\quad\text{a.s.}

Proof.

By (
2.15
), we can choose
w_{M^{0}}
in such a way that for all
z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon_{0}}(z)
,
w_{M^{0}}(z^{\prime})=w_{M^{0}}(z)
. We can then conclude by using Lemma
2.15
.
∎

Note that, here again, the assumption on

is only used to ensure that the conclusion of the lemma holds true even if

is not overlapped by an event during the interval

(
cf.
Remark
2.16
).

Informally, the following lemma states that, once a location is reached by the expansion in the
\infty
-parent SLFV, it is still occupied at any later time with probability 1. Note that, as local backtracking of the area occupied by type 1 individuals is possible in the
k
-parent SLFV when

, we do not have an equivalent statement for

.

Lemma 2.18
.

Let
M^{0}\in\mathcal{M}_{\lambda}
. Let
(M_{t}^{(\infty)})_{t\geq 0}
be the
\infty
-parent SLFV associated with
\mu
and with initial condition
M^{0}
. For every

and
\epsilon>0
, let

\displaystyle\tau_{z}^{(\epsilon)}

\displaystyle:=\min\left\{s\geq 0:V_{\epsilon}^{-1}\int_{\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}(z)}w_{M_{s}^{(\infty)}}(z^{\prime})dz^{\prime}>0\right\}

\displaystyle\text{and }\quad\tau_{z}

\displaystyle:=\min\left\{s\geq 0:\lim\limits_{\epsilon^{\prime}\to 0}V_{\epsilon^{\prime}}^{-1}\int_{\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon^{\prime}}(z)}w_{M_{s}^{(\infty)}}(z^{\prime})dz^{\prime}>0\right\}.

,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left.V_{\epsilon}^{-1}\int_{\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}(z)}w_{M_{t}^{(\infty)}}(z^{\prime})dz^{\prime}>0\,\right|\tau_{z}^{(\epsilon)}\leq t\right)

\displaystyle\text{and}\quad\mathbb{P}\left(\left.\lim\limits_{\epsilon^{\prime}\to 0}V_{\epsilon^{\prime}}^{-1}\int_{\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon^{\prime}}(z)}w_{M_{t}^{(\infty)}}(z^{\prime})dz^{\prime}>0\,\right|\tau_{z}\leq t\right)

Proof.

Let
\epsilon>0
,
z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
t\geq 0
. Let
\Pi^{+}
be the extended Poisson point process defined in Section
2.3
,

\texttt{w}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\{0,1\}

given by (

and

\forall\,\epsilon<\epsilon_{0}^{x},\qquad E_{t}^{\epsilon,x}=E_{t}^{x}.

Furthermore, we have with probability 1

\lim\limits_{\epsilon\to 0}\mathrm{Vol}\left(E_{t}^{\epsilon,x}\cap H\right)=\mathrm{Vol}\left(E_{t}^{x}\cap H\right)\qquad\hbox{for all }t\geq 0.

Concretely,
\epsilon_{0}^{x}
is the radius of a ball around
(x,0)
small enough to be included in the area of the first event overlapping
(x,0)
, and not to be overlapped by any event in
\overleftarrow{\Pi}
occurring before
t_{0}^{x}
.

Proof.

Let
x>0
. By the same arguments as in the proof of Lemma
2.15
, with probability 1 there exists
\epsilon_{0}^{x}>0
such that

\displaystyle E_{t}^{\epsilon,x}

\displaystyle=E_{0}^{\epsilon,x}\qquad\hbox{for all }0<\epsilon\leq\epsilon_{0}^{x}\hbox{ and all }0\leq t<t_{0}^{x},\hbox{ and}

(3.1)

\displaystyle E_{t_{0}^{x}}^{\epsilon,x}

\displaystyle=E_{t_{0}^{x}}^{x}\qquad\ \hbox{for all }0<\epsilon\leq\epsilon_{0}^{x}.

The first part of the lemma is then a consequence of the strong Markov property of the
\infty
-parent ancestral process applied at time
t_{0}^{x}
.

Then, on the event of probability 1 on which
\epsilon_{0}^{x}
exists, considering radii small enough that
\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}((x,0))\cap H=\emptyset=\{(x,0)\}\cap H
, we can separate two cases according to whether
t<t_{0}^{x}
or
t\geq t_{0}^{x}
.

1.

For
t\in[0,t_{0}^{x})
, for all
\epsilon\in(0,\min\{\epsilon_{0}^{x},x\})
we have by (
3.1
):

\mathrm{Vol}\left(E_{t}^{\epsilon,x}\cap H\right)=\mathrm{Vol}\left(\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}((x,0))\cap H\right)=0=\mathrm{Vol}\left(\{(x,0)\}\cap H\right)=\mathrm{Vol}\left(E_{t}^{x}\cap H\right).

2.

For
t\geq t_{0}^{x}
, the first part of Lemma
3.2
tells us that
E_{t}^{\epsilon,x}=E_{t}^{x}
for all
\epsilon\in(0,\epsilon_{0}^{x})
. Therefore,

\mathrm{Vol}\left(E_{t}^{\epsilon,x}\cap H\right)=\mathrm{Vol}\left(E_{t}^{x}\cap H\right).

This allows us to conclude.
∎

We can now introduce an equivalent of
\overrightarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,(\infty)}
for the
\infty
-parent ancestral process.

Definition 3.3
.

For all
x>0
, let

\tilde{\tau}_{x}^{(\infty)}:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\min\left\{t\geq 0:\lim\limits_{\epsilon\to 0}\mathrm{Vol}\left(E_{t}^{\epsilon,x}\cap H\right)>0\right\}&\mbox{if there exists $\epsilon_{0}^{x}>0$ as in Lemma\penalty\ \ref{lem:lem1},}\\
0&\mbox{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

Lemma 3.4
.

For all
x>0
,
\tilde{\tau}_{x}^{(\infty)}
is well-defined, and is almost surely equal to

\displaystyle\min\left\{t\geq 0:E_{t}^{x}\cap H\neq\emptyset\right\}.

In particular,
\tilde{\tau}_{x}^{(\infty)}\neq 0
a.s.

Proof.

Let
x>0
. By Lemma
3.2
,
\tilde{\tau}_{x}^{(\infty)}
is well-defined and almost surely equal to

\min\Big\{t\geq 0:\mathrm{Vol}\left(E_{t}^{x}\cap H\right)>0\Big\}\geq t_{0}^{x}>0,

The fact that
t_{0}^{x}>0
a.s. is a consequence of the fact that
\mu
is a finite measure with compact support and so the point
(x,0)
is overlapped by an event of
\overleftarrow{\Pi}
at a finite rate. Now, since
H
is an open set we have for all
(a,b,\gamma)\in S_{\mu}

\left\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\mathfrak{B}_{a,b,\gamma}(z)\cap H\neq\emptyset\text{ and }\mathrm{Vol}\left(\mathfrak{B}_{a,b,\gamma}(z)\cap H\right)=0\right\}=\emptyset,

and so no events can lead to a situation where
E_{t}^{x}\cap H\neq\emptyset
and
\mathrm{Vol}(E_{t}^{x}\cap H)=0
. Therefore, we can write

\min\big\{t\geq 0:\mathrm{Vol}\left(E_{t}^{x}\cap H\right)>0\big\}=\min\left\{t\geq 0:E_{t}^{x}\cap H\neq\emptyset\right\}\text{ a.s.,}

which allows us to conclude.
∎

The random variable
\tilde{\tau}_{x}^{(\infty)}
can be interpreted as the equivalent of
\overrightarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,(\infty)}
for the dual process in the following sense.

Lemma 3.5
.

For all
x>0
,
\tilde{\tau}_{x}^{(\infty)}
and
\overrightarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,(\infty)}
have the same distribution.

Proof.

Let
x>0
. For all
\epsilon>0
, let us set

\displaystyle\overrightarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,\epsilon,(\infty)}

\displaystyle:=\min\bigg\{t\geq 0:V_{\epsilon}^{-1}\int_{\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}((x,0))}w_{t}^{(\infty)}(z)dz>0\bigg\},\qquad\hbox{and}

\displaystyle\tilde{\tau}\!_{x}^{\ \epsilon,(\infty)}

\displaystyle:=\min\big\{t\geq 0:\mathrm{Vol}\left(E_{t}^{\epsilon,x}\cap H\right)>0\big\}.

If we can show that

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{\epsilon\to 0}\overrightarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,\epsilon,(\infty)}=\overrightarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,(\infty)}\quad\text{a.s.,}

(3.2)

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{\epsilon\to 0}\tilde{\tau}\!_{x}^{\ \epsilon,(\infty)}=\tilde{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,(\infty)}\quad\text{a.s.,}

(3.3)

\displaystyle\overrightarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,\epsilon,(\infty)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(d)}}{{=}}\tilde{\tau}\!_{x}^{\ \epsilon,(\infty)}\quad\hbox{for all }\epsilon>0,

(3.4)

then we can conclude that
\tilde{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,(\infty)}
and
\overrightarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,(\infty)}
have the same distribution.

Let us start with the proof of (
3.2
). For all
0<\epsilon<\epsilon^{\prime}
, as
w_{\overrightarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,\epsilon,(\infty)}}^{(\infty)}
is
\{0,1\}
-valued, we can write

\int_{\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon^{\prime}}((x,0))}w^{(\infty)}_{\overrightarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,\epsilon,(\infty)}}(z)dz\geq\int_{\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}((x,0))}w^{(\infty)}_{\overrightarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,\epsilon,(\infty)}}(z)dz>0

by definition of
\overrightarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,\epsilon,(\infty)}
. Therefore,
\overrightarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,\epsilon^{\prime},(\infty)}\leq\overrightarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,\epsilon,(\infty)}
. In particular, this means that
\lim\limits_{\epsilon\to 0}\overrightarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,\epsilon,(\infty)}
exists.

Let
t\geq 0
. By Lemma
2.15
, with probability
1
there exists
\tilde{\epsilon}(z,t)>0
such that the function

\epsilon\mapsto V_{\epsilon}^{-1}\int_{\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}((x,0))}w_{t}^{(\infty)}(z)dz

is constant over
(0,\tilde{\epsilon}(z,t)]
, equal to a number
v\in\{0,1\}
. Consequently, we also have

\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}V_{\epsilon}^{-1}\int_{\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}((x,0))}w_{t}^{(\infty)}(z)dz=v.

If
t\geq\overrightarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,(\infty)}
, by the second part of Lemma
2.18
we know that

1=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}V_{\epsilon}^{-1}\int_{\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}((x,0))}w_{t}^{(\infty)}(z)dz=V_{\epsilon}^{-1}\int_{\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}((x,0))}w_{t}^{(\infty)}(z)dz\quad\hbox{for all }\epsilon\in(0,\tilde{\epsilon}(z,t)].

Hence,
t\geq\overrightarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,\epsilon,(\infty)}
for all
\epsilon\in(0,\tilde{\epsilon}(z,t)]
, and using the monotonicity in
\epsilon
of
\overrightarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,\epsilon,(\infty)}
, we obtain that

t\geq\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\overrightarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,\epsilon,(\infty)}.

This implies that
\overrightarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,(\infty)}\geq\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\overrightarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,\epsilon,(\infty)}
. Conversely, if
t<\overrightarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,(\infty)}
then

0=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}V_{\epsilon}^{-1}\int_{\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}((x,0))}w_{t}^{(\infty)}(z)dz=V_{\epsilon}^{-1}\int_{\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}((x,0))}w_{t}^{(\infty)}(z)dz\quad\hbox{for all }\epsilon\in(0,\tilde{\epsilon}(z,t)].

By the first part of Lemma
2.18
,
V_{\epsilon}^{-1}\int_{\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}((x,0))}w_{t}^{(\infty)}(z)dz=0
is equivalent to
t<\overrightarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,\epsilon,(\infty)}
, and so necessarily,
t<\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\overrightarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,\epsilon,(\infty)}
. This gives us that
\overrightarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,(\infty)}\leq\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\overrightarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,\epsilon,(\infty)}
. Combining the two inequalities, we obtain (
3.2
).

Let us now prove (
3.3
). Constructing all the
\infty
-parent ancestral processes with the same Poisson point process ensures that
\epsilon\mapsto\tilde{\tau}\!_{x}^{\ \epsilon,(\infty)}
is a.s. non-increasing and hence that
\lim\limits_{\epsilon\to 0}\tilde{\tau}\!_{x}^{\ \epsilon,(\infty)}
exists with probability 1. By the proof of Lemma
3.2
, with probability 1 there exists
\epsilon_{0}^{x}>0
such that for all
t>0
,

\mathrm{Vol}\left(E_{t}^{\epsilon,x}\cap H\right)=\mathrm{Vol}\left(E_{t}^{x}\cap H\right)\qquad\hbox{for all }0<\epsilon\leq\epsilon_{0}^{x}.

This implies that

\lim\limits_{\epsilon\to 0}\tilde{\tau}\!_{x}^{\ \epsilon,(\infty)}=\tilde{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,(\infty)}\quad\text{a.s.}

Finally, let us prove (
3.4
). Let
\epsilon>0
and
t\geq 0
. Using the first part of Lemma
2.18
, Proposition
2.9
, the fact that
w_{0}^{\infty}=\mathds{1}_{H}
and finally the fact that
(E_{t}^{\epsilon,x})_{t\geq 0}
is non-decreasing for the inclusion, we can write

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\overrightarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,\epsilon,(\infty)}>t\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\int_{\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}((x,0))}w_{t}^{(\infty)}(z)dz=0\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{P}\left(\int_{E_{t}^{\epsilon,x}}w_{0}^{(\infty)}(z)dz=0\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{P}\left(\mathrm{Vol}\left(E_{t}^{\epsilon,x}\cap H\right)=0\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{P}\left(\tilde{\tau}\!_{x}^{\ \epsilon,(\infty)}>t\right).

Since this result is true for any
t\geq 0
, we obtain the desired equality in distribution.
∎

For all
x\geq 0
, let
H^{x}
stand for the half-plane

H^{x}:=\left\{(x^{\prime},y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:x^{\prime}\geq x\right\}.

(3.5)

In particular,
H=(H^{0})^{c}
(this apparent contradiction in our notation will lead to simpler notation in the proofs).

In practice, the set of variables
(\tilde{\tau}_{x}^{(\infty)})_{x>0}
is not very convenient to use. Indeed, for
x<x^{\prime}
, even if
\tilde{\tau}_{x}^{(\infty)}
and
\tilde{\tau}_{x^{\prime}}^{(\infty)}
are constructed using the same underlying Poisson point process related events, we do not necessarily have
\tilde{\tau}_{x}^{(\infty)}\leq\tilde{\tau}_{x^{\prime}}^{(\infty)}
a.s., as the underlying
\infty
-parent ancestral processes have different starting positions and do not necessarily use the same sequence of events to reach
H
. Therefore, we define another sequence
(\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,(\infty)})_{x>0}
such that for all
x>0
,
\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,(\infty)}
and
\tilde{\tau}_{x}^{(\infty)}
have the same distribution, but also such that all underlying
\infty
-parent ancestral processes start from the same location
(0,0)
, thereby ensuring that for all
0<x<x^{\prime}
, we have
\overleftarrow{\tau}_{x}^{\,(\infty)}\leq\overleftarrow{\tau}_{x^{\prime}}^{\,(\infty)}
a.s. Moreover, for every
x>0
, the
\infty
-parent ancestral process associated with
\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,(\infty)}
will have the same distribution as the ancestral process associated with
\tilde{\tau}_{x}^{(\infty)}
reflected with respect to the axis
\{(x/2,y):y\in\mathbb{R}\}
. In other words, we shall be interested in the expansion of the new process “from left to right”, starting from
(0,0)
and reaching abscissa
x
, instead of studying the expansion of
E^{x}
“from right to left”, starting from
(x,0)
and reaching abscissa
0
.

Let
\mu^{\leftarrow}
be the finite measure on
S_{\mu}
defined by the property that for all measurable subsets
I,J
of
(0,+\infty)
and for all
-\pi/2<\gamma_{1}\leq\gamma_{2}<\pi/2
,

\mu^{\leftarrow}(I\times J\times[\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}])=\mu(I\times J\times[-\gamma_{2},-\gamma_{1}]).

(3.6)

Let
(E_{t}^{\epsilon})_{t\geq 0}
,
\epsilon>0
, be a sequence of
\infty
-parent ancestral processes associated with
\mu^{\leftarrow}
with initial condition
\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}((0,0))
, constructed using the same underlying Poisson point process
\overline{\Pi}
(with intensity measure
dt\otimes dz\otimes\mu^{\leftarrow}(da,db,d\gamma)
), and let
(E_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be the
\infty
-parent ancestral process with initial condition
\{(0,0)\}
associated with
\mu^{\leftarrow}
, also constructed using the Poisson point process
\overline{\Pi}
. For definiteness, we suppose that these new objects are also constructed on the probability space
(\mathbf{\Omega},\mathbf{\mathcal{F}},\mathbf{P})
. By the reasoning that led to (
3.1
), there exists almost surely
\epsilon_{0}>0
such that if
t_{0}
is the first time at which
(0,0)
is affected by a reproduction event in
\overline{\Pi}
,

\forall\,\epsilon\in(0,\epsilon_{0}],\qquad E_{t_{0}}^{\epsilon}=E_{t_{0}}\text{ and }\forall\,t\in[0,t_{0}),\ E_{t}^{\epsilon}=E_{0}^{\epsilon}.

(3.7)

Definition 3.6
.

For all
x>0
, let

\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,(\infty)}:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\min\Big\{t\geq 0:\lim\limits_{\epsilon\to 0}\mathrm{Vol}\left(E_{t}^{\epsilon}\cap H^{x}\right)>0\Big\}&\mbox{if $\exists\,\epsilon_{0}>0$ satisfying\penalty\ \eqref{eqn:new_condition_2_3_prime},}\\
0&\mbox{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

As for
(\tilde{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,(\infty)})_{x>0}
, for all
x>0
,
\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,(\infty)}
is well-defined and almost surely equal to

\min\left\{t\geq 0:E_{t}\cap H^{x}\neq\emptyset\right\}.

Let us say that a point
z=(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
is at
horizontal separation

d
of the point
z^{\prime}=(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
if and only if
x-x^{\prime}=d
. Then informally,
\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,(\infty)}
represents the first time the
\infty
-parent ancestral process starting from
(0,0)
reaches points at horizontal separation of at least
x
from the starting location. Moreover, we have the following lemma.

Lemma 3.7
.

The function
x\to\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,(\infty)}
is nondecreasing.

Proof.

We distinguish two cases. If there does not exist
\epsilon_{0}>0
satisfying (
3.7
), then for all
x>0
,
\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{(\infty)}=0
, and we can conclude.

If there exists
\epsilon_{0}>0
satisfying (
3.7
), let
0<x_{1}<x_{2}
. By similar arguments as in Lemma
3.2
and its proof, we can write that

\displaystyle\forall\,0<\epsilon\leq\min(\epsilon_{0},x_{1}/2),\quad\mathrm{Vol}\left(E_{t}^{\epsilon}\cap H^{x_{1}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Vol}\left(E_{t}^{\min(\epsilon_{0},x_{1}/2)}\cap H^{x_{1}}\right)

\displaystyle\text{and }\quad\forall\,0<\epsilon\leq\min(\epsilon_{0},x_{2}/2),\quad\mathrm{Vol}\left(E_{t}^{\epsilon}\cap H^{x_{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Vol}\left(E_{t}^{\min(\epsilon_{0},x_{2}/2)})\cap H^{x_{2}}\right).

Let us set
\epsilon^{\prime}=\min(\epsilon_{0},x_{1}/2,x_{2}/2)
. Then,
H^{x_{2}}\subset H^{x_{1}}
and so

\mathrm{Vol}\left(E_{\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{x_{2}}^{\,(\infty)}}^{\epsilon^{\prime}}\cap H^{x_{1}}\right)\geq\mathrm{Vol}\left(E_{\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{x_{2}}^{\,(\infty)}}^{\epsilon^{\prime}}\cap H^{x_{2}}\right)=\lim\limits_{\epsilon\to 0}\mathrm{Vol}\left(E_{\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{x_{2}}^{\,(\infty)}}^{\epsilon}\cap H^{x_{2}}\right)>0

by definition of
\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{x_{2}}^{\,(\infty)}
, and so
\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{x_{1}}^{\,(\infty)}\leq\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{x_{2}}^{\,(\infty)}
and we can conclude.
∎

Notice that we are now studying the expansion of the backwards-in-time process in the same direction as the occupied area in the forwards-in-time process. On the other hand,
(\tilde{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,(\infty)})_{x>0}
corresponds to the expansion of the
\infty
-parent ancestral process in the
opposite direction
. Moreover, we recall that
(\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,(\infty)})_{x>0}
can be seen as being constructed using an underlying
\infty
-parent ancestral process which is the reflection of the one used to construct
(\tilde{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,(\infty)})_{x>0}
with respect to the axis
\{(x/2,y):y\in\mathbb{R}\}
.
This observation yields the following lemma.

Lemma 3.8
.

For all
x>0
,
\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,(\infty)}
and
\tilde{\tau}_{x}^{\,(\infty)}
have the same distribution.

Proof.

Let
x>0
. For all
t\geq 0
and
\epsilon>0
, let
\mathrm{Ref}(E_{t}^{\epsilon,x})
be the reflection of
E_{t}^{\epsilon,x}
with respect to the axis
\{(x/2,y):y\in\mathbb{R}\}
. Then,
(\mathrm{Ref}(E_{t}^{\epsilon,x}))_{t\geq 0}
,
\epsilon>0
, is a sequence of
\infty
-parent ancestral processes with initial condition
\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}((0,0))
, all constructed using the same Poisson point process having the same distribution as
\overline{\Pi}
(that is, whose intensity measure is
dt\otimes dz\otimes\mu^{\leftarrow}(da,db,d\gamma)
), which can be used to construct
\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{(\infty)}
. Moreover, for all
t\geq 0
and
\epsilon>0
,

\mathrm{Vol}\big(E_{t}^{\epsilon,x}\cap H\big)>0\quad\text{if and only if}\quad\mathrm{Vol}\left(\mathrm{Ref}(E_{t}^{\epsilon,x})\cap H^{x}\right)>0,

which allows us to conclude.
∎

The following result is then a direct consequence of Lemmas
3.5
and
3.8
.

Proposition 3.9
.

For all
x>0
,
\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,(\infty)}
and
\overrightarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,(\infty)}
have the same distribution.

In other words, we can show Theorem
1.6
by proving a similar result for
\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,(\infty)}
.

3.2
A
backwards in time
version of Theorem
1.7

We now turn to the case
k<+\infty
, and rephrase the probability of occupancy appearing in Theorem
1.7
in terms of a reflected version of the
k
-parent ancestral process. Recall the definition of
\mu^{\leftarrow}
given in (
3.6
), of the quenched
k
-parent ancestral process given in Definition
2.11
, of the half-plane
H^{x}
given in (
3.5
), and recall the notation
A(\Xi)
for the set of atoms of the counting measure
\Xi
.

Proposition 3.10
.

Let
k\geq 2
.
Let
\overleftarrow{\Pi}^{+}
be a Poisson point process on
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\times S_{\mu}\times(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{\infty}
with intensity measure
dt\otimes dz\otimes\mu^{\leftarrow}(da,db,d\gamma)\tilde{u}_{(a,b,\gamma)}(d\mathbf{p})
defined on the probability space
(\mathbf{\Omega},\mathbf{\mathcal{F}},\mathbf{P})
. Let
(\overleftarrow{\Xi}_{t}^{(k)})_{t\geq 0}
be the quenched
k
-parent ancestral process with initial condition
\delta_{(0,0)}
constructed using
\overleftarrow{\Pi}^{+}
. Then, for all
x>0
and
t>0
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\lim\limits_{\epsilon\to 0}V_{\epsilon}^{-1}\int_{\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}((x,0))}w_{t}^{(k)}(z)dz=0\right)=\mathbf{P}\Big(A\big(\overleftarrow{\Xi}_{t}^{(k)}\big)\cap\textit{\text{H}}^{x}=\emptyset\Big).

Proof.

Let
x>0
and
t>0
. Let
(w_{k,s}^{H})_{s\geq 0}
be the density of
(M_{s}^{(k)})_{s\geq 0}
obtained when following the Poisson point process construction described in Section
2.3
, and using the Poisson point process
\Pi^{+}
. By Lemma
2.15
, the value of

\lim\limits_{\epsilon\to 0}V_{\epsilon}^{-1}\int_{\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}((x,0))}w_{t}^{(k)}(z)dz

is almost surely equal to

w_{k,t}^{\text{H}}((x,0))=1-\left(\prod_{z\in A\big(\Xi_{k,t}^{\Pi^{+},t,\delta_{(x,0)}}\big)}\big(1-\mathds{1}_{\text{H}}(z)\big)\right),

where
(\Xi_{k,t^{\prime}}^{\Pi^{+},t,\delta_{(x,0)}})_{t^{\prime}\in[0,t]}
is the quenched
k
-parent ancestral process associated with
\Pi^{+}
, started at time
t
and with initial condition
\delta_{(x,0)}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\lim\limits_{\epsilon\to 0}V_{\epsilon}^{-1}\int_{\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}((x,0))}w_{t}^{(k)}(z)dz=0\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(A\Big(\Xi_{k,t}^{\Pi^{+},t,\delta_{(x,0)}}\Big)\cap\text{H}=\emptyset\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{P}\left(A\big(\overleftarrow{\Xi}_{t}^{(k)}\big)\cap\text{H}^{x}=\emptyset\right)

by symmetry with respect to the axis
\{(x/2,y):y\in\mathbb{R}\}
, which is the desired result.
∎

4
Lower bound on the speed of growth of the
\infty
-parent SLFV

In this section, we show that the growth of the occupied area in the
\infty
-parent SLFV, as well as the growth of its dual, is
at least
linear in time. More precisely, we show the following result.

Proposition 4.1
.

There exists
\nu^{(\infty)}\geq 0
such that

\lim\limits_{x\to+\infty}\frac{\mathbf{E}\big[\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,(\infty)}\big]}{x}=\nu^{(\infty)}

(4.1)

and

\lim\limits_{x\to+\infty}\frac{\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,(\infty)}}{x}=\nu^{(\infty)}\quad\text{a.s.}

(4.2)

The proof of this result can be found at the end of Section
4.3
. As
\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,(\infty)}
and
\overrightarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,(\infty)}
are equal in distribution by Proposition
3.9
, the convergence in (
4.1
) also holds with
\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,(\infty)}
replaced by
\overrightarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,(\infty)}
, and the convergence in (
4.2
) only holds in distribution (and therefore in probability, as the limit is a constant) when
\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,(\infty)}
is replaced by
\overrightarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,(\infty)}
. Indeed, the a.s. convergence stated in (
4.2
) exploits the i.i.d. structure of the times needed for
(E_{t})_{t\geq 0}
to reach horizontal separation
n+1
of
(0,0)
starting from a single point at horizontal separation
n
of
(0,0)
. Because there is no analogous subdivision of the reaching time of
(x,0)
by the occupied area in
M^{(\infty)}
(as a path of overlapping events reaching
(0,n+1)
does not necessarily pass by
(0,n)
) and
\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,(\infty)}
and
\overrightarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,(\infty)}
are only equal in distribution, we cannot translate (
4.2
) into the a.s. convergence of
\overrightarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,(\infty)}/x
as
x\rightarrow\infty
.

The key difference with Theorem
1.6
is that Proposition
4.1
does not require
\nu^{(\infty)}
to be non-zero, and hence only means that the growth is at least linear in time (and faster if
\nu^{(\infty)}=0
). In Section
5
, we show that indeed we have
\nu^{(\infty)}\neq 0
, or in other words, that the growth is exactly linear in time.

Remark 4.2
.

Since the limiting speed of growth is given by
(\nu^{(\infty)})^{-1}
, a lower bound on the speed of growth amounts to an upper bound on
\lim\limits_{x\to+\infty}x^{-1}\mathbf{E}\big[\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{(\infty)}\big]
.

In order to prove Proposition
4.1
, we first establish a few auxiliary results.
In all that follows, let again
\overline{\Pi}
be a Poisson point process on
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\times S_{\mu}
with intensity measure
dt\otimes dz\otimes\mu^{\leftarrow}
, and let
(E_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be the
\infty
-parent ancestral process with initial condition
\{(0,0)\}
and associated with
\mu^{\leftarrow}
, constructed using the Poisson point process
\overline{\Pi}
. Here we use the modification of the
\infty
-parent ancestral process introduced earlier, which allows us to take single points as initial conditions.

4.1
A sub-additivity result

We first introduce other
\infty
-parent ancestral processes, coupled to
(E_{t})_{t\geq 0}
via the Poisson point process
\overline{\Pi}
. For all
z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
s\geq 0
, let
(E_{t}^{z,s})_{t\geq 0}
be the
\infty
-parent ancestral process with initial condition
\{z\}
associated with
\mu^{\leftarrow}
, constructed using
only the reproduction events in
\overline{\Pi}
occurring strictly after time
s
. If
s=0
, then
(E_{t}^{z,0})_{t\geq 0}
is the regular
\infty
-parent ancestral process, but if
s\neq 0
, then the process is constant and equal to
\{z\}
during the time interval
[0,s]
, and only after does it start following the dynamics of an
\infty
-parent ancestral process.

Since all the processes
(E_{t}^{z,s})_{t\geq 0}
,
z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
s\geq 0
are constructed using the same underlying Poisson point process, we have the following result.

Lemma 4.3
.

For all
z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
0<s_{1}<s_{2}
, if
z_{2}\in E_{s_{2}}^{z_{1},s_{1}}
, then a.s. for all
t\geq s_{2}
, we have

E_{t}^{z_{2},s_{2}}\subseteq E_{t}^{z_{1},s_{1}}.

We now introduce the following family of random variables. First, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let

\displaystyle T_{0,n}:

\displaystyle=\min\left\{t\geq 0:E_{t}^{(0,0),0}\cap H^{4n\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}\neq\emptyset\right\}=\min\left\{t\geq 0:E_{t}\cap H^{4n\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}\neq\emptyset\right\}=\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{4n\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}^{\,(\infty)}\quad\text{a.s.,}

and let
P_{n}
be sampled uniformly at random among the points in
E_{T_{0,n}}
at horizontal separation of
exactly

4n\mathcal{R}_{\mu}
from
(0,0)
. That is,
P_{n}
is a uniform sample from the compact set

E_{T_{0,n}}\cap\left\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:x=4n\mathcal{R}_{\mu}\right\}.

Moreover, for all
0\leq m\leq n\in\mathbb{N}
, let

T_{m,n}:=\min\left\{t\geq 0:E_{t+T_{0,m}}^{P_{m},T_{0,m}}\cap H^{4n\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}\neq\emptyset\right\},

where
E_{t+T_{0,m}}^{P_{m},T_{0,m}}
corresponds to the
\infty
-parent ancestral process started from
P_{m}
at time
T_{0,m}
.

By construction, the family
(T_{m,n})_{0\leq m\leq n}
satisfies the following lemma.

Lemma 4.4
.

For all
0<m<n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

T_{0,n}\leq T_{0,m}+T_{m,n}.

Proof.

Let
0<m<n\in\mathbb{N}
. By definition,

P_{m}\in E_{T_{0,m}}=E_{T_{0,m}}^{(0,0),0}.

Therefore, using Lemma
4.3
, we have a.s. for all
t\geq 0

E_{t+T_{0,m}}^{P_{m},T_{0,m}}\subseteq E_{t+T_{0,m}}.

In particular, this is true for
t=T_{m,n}
, hence

E_{T_{m,n}+T_{0,m}}^{P_{m},T_{0,m}}\cap H^{4n\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}\subseteq E_{T_{m,n}+T_{0,m}}\cap H^{4n\mathcal{R}_{\mu}},

from which we deduce (by definition of
T_{0,m}
,
P_{m}
and
T_{m,n}
) that

E_{T_{m,n}+T_{0,m}}\cap H^{4n\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}\neq\emptyset.

Therefore,

\displaystyle T_{0,n}

\displaystyle=\min\left\{t>0:E_{t}\cap H^{4n\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}\neq\emptyset\right\}\leq T_{m,n}+T_{0,m}.

∎

By invariance by translation of the underlying Poisson point processes, the following lemmas also hold true.

Lemma 4.5
.

For all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the joint distribution of
(T_{n+1,n+l+1})_{l\geq 1}
is the same as that of
(T_{n,n+l})_{l\geq 1}
.

Lemma 4.6
.

For each
l\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}
, the random variables
(T_{nl,(n+1)l})_{n\geq 1}
are i.i.d. In particular, the sequence
(T_{nl,(n+1)l})_{n\geq 1}
is ergodic.

In order to use Theorem 1.10 from
[
34
]
and conclude, all we need is to show that the following lemma is true.

Lemma 4.7
.

For all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have
\mathbf{E}[T_{0,n}]<+\infty
.

Lemma
4.7
is proved at the end of Section
4.3
, using the so-called
express chain
that we now define. We shall then use it to complete the proof of Proposition
4.1
(again at the end of Section
4.3
).

4.2
Definition of the express chain

The use of what we shall call the
express chain
can be motivated by the following observation. In each ellipse with centre
z=(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and parameters
(a,b,\gamma)
, there exists exactly one point for which the horizontal separation from the centre is maximal: the point with coordinates

(x+a\cos(\theta_{max})\cos(\gamma)-b\sin(\theta_{\max})\sin(\gamma),y+a\cos(\theta_{max})\sin(\gamma)+b\sin(\theta_{max})\cos(\gamma)),

where
\theta_{max}=\arctan(-ba^{-1}\tan(\gamma))
. This point is at horizontal separation

\sqrt{a^{2}\cos^{2}(\gamma)+b^{2}\sin^{2}(\gamma)}

from
z
(see Figure
3
).

Figure 3
:
Ellipse with parameters
(a,b,\gamma)
.

If we take this potential parent, wait until it is affected by a new reproduction event, and repeat, we obtain a Markov jump process, jumping at rate

J_{\mu}:=\int_{S_{\mu}}V_{a,b}\,\mu(da,db,d\gamma),

(4.3)

and going away from
0
at an average
horizontal speed
of
J_{\mu}\mathbf{E}\left[\sqrt{a^{2}\cos^{2}(\gamma)+b^{2}\sin^{2}(\gamma)}\right]
(modulo some stochasticity due to the location of the centre of the reproduction event). See Appendix
A
for the proof of the geometrical properties of ellipses used throughout this section.

Formally, the express chain is defined as follows.

Definition 4.8
.

The express chain associated with
(E_{t})_{t\geq 0}
(constructed using
\overline{\Pi}
), denoted
(C_{t}^{express})_{t\geq 0}
, is the
\mathbb{R}^{2}
-valued Markov process defined as follows.

First, we set
C_{0}^{express}=(0,0)
. Then, for all
(t,z_{c},a,b,\gamma)\in\overline{\Pi}
, if
C_{t-}^{express}\in\mathfrak{B}_{a,b,\gamma}(z_{c})
and
z_{c}=(x_{c},y_{c})
, we set:

C_{t}^{express}=\left(X_{t}^{express},Y_{t}^{express}\right)

where

\displaystyle X_{t}^{express}

\displaystyle=x_{c}+a\cos(\theta_{max})\cos(\gamma)-b\sin(\theta_{\max})\sin(\gamma),

\displaystyle Y_{t}^{express}

\displaystyle=y_{c}+a\cos(\theta_{max})\sin(\gamma)+b\sin(\theta_{max})\cos(\gamma)

and
\theta_{max}=\arctan(-ba^{-1}\tan(\gamma))
.

See Figure
4
for an illustration of how to construct the express chain.

Figure 4
:

\infty
-parent ancestral process at time
t
, for two realisations of the
\infty
-parent ancestral process. The ellipses indicate the reproduction events which affected the
\infty
-parent ancestral process until time
t
. The crosses represent the successive positions of the express chain, and the black point indicates the location reached by the

-parent ancestral process at time

which maximises the horizontal separation from the starting location. This location coincides with the one reached by the express chain in the first case, but not in the second case.

The interest of the express chain lies in the following observation, whose proof is a direct consequence of the definition of
T_{0,n}
.

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}
. For all
t\geq 0
, we have
\{C_{t}^{express}\in H^{4n\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}\}\subset\{T_{0,n}\leq t\}
.

Proof.

Let
t\geq 0
. Since
C_{t}^{express}\in E_{t}
by construction, the fact the
C_{t}^{express}\in H^{4n\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}
implies that
E_{t}\cap H^{4n\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}\neq\emptyset
, and thus that
T_{0,n}\leq t
.
∎

Therefore, if for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we set

T_{0,n}^{express}:=\min\left\{t\geq 0:C_{t}^{express}\in H^{4n\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}\right\},

then for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have

T_{0,n}\leq T_{0,n}^{express}\quad\text{a.s.},\qquad\text{and so}\quad\mathbf{E}[T_{0,n}]\leq\mathbf{E}\big[T_{0,n}^{express}\big].

(4.4)

In other words, in order to show Lemma
4.7
, it is sufficient to obtain a similar result on
\mathbf{E}[T_{0,n}^{express}]
.

Before studying the properties of the express chain in the next section, we introduce the following notation. For all
t\geq 0
, let
N_{t}^{express}
be the number of jumps of the express chain in the time interval
[0,t]
. For all
i\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}
, let
t_{i}
be the instant of the
i
-th jump of the express chain, let
R_{i}=(R_{i}^{X},R_{i}^{Y})
be the coordinates of the centre of the reproduction event triggering this jump, and let
(a_{i},b_{i},\gamma_{i})
be the parameters of the ellipse affected by the reproduction event. We then set for all
i\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}

D_{i}=\sqrt{a_{i}^{2}\cos^{2}(\gamma_{i})+b_{i}^{2}\sin^{2}(\gamma_{i})}.

The random variable
D_{i}
thus encodes the distance between the centre of the reproduction event and the right-most point in the corresponding ellipse. Note that the random variables
(D_{i})_{i\geq 1}
are i.i.d.

4.3
Properties of the express chain

We now study some properties of the express chain, in order to obtain an upper bound on
\mathbf{E}[T_{0,n}^{express}]
.

Let
t\geq 0
. By construction,

\displaystyle X_{t}^{express}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{t}^{express}}\left(R_{i}^{X}-X_{t_{i}-}^{express}+D_{i}\right)

(4.5)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{t}^{express}}\Big(R_{i}^{X}-X_{t_{i}-}^{express}+D_{i}-\mathbf{E}[D_{1}]\Big)+\mathbf{E}[D_{1}]N_{t}^{express}.

The random variables
(R_{i}^{X}-X_{t_{i}-}^{express}+D_{i}-\mathbf{E}[D_{i}])_{i\geq 1}
are i.i.d, bounded and with mean
0
(see Appendix
A
). We can then apply Hoeffding’s inequality
[
29
]
and obtain the following lemma.

Lemma 4.10
.

There exists
C_{1}>0
such that for all
t\geq 0
and
n\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}
, for all

k>4n\mathcal{R}_{\mu}\mathbf{E}[D_{1}]^{-1}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left(X_{t}^{express}<4n\mathcal{R}_{\mu}\Big|N_{t}^{express}=k\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\left(\mathbf{E}[D_{1}]k-4n\mathcal{R}_{\mu}\right)^{2}}{C_{1}k}\right).

Consequently, for all
C^{\prime}>4n\mathcal{R}_{\mu}\mathbf{E}[D_{1}]^{-1}
,

\mathbf{P}\left(X_{t}^{express}<4n\mathcal{R}_{\mu}\Big|N_{t}^{express}>C^{\prime}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\left(C^{\prime}\mathbf{E}[D_{1}]-4n\mathcal{R}_{\mu}\right)^{2}}{C_{1}C^{\prime}}\right).

Proof.

First, notice that the second part of the lemma is a direct consequence of the first part, along with the variations of the function
x\to\exp(-(xc-d)^{2}x^{-1})
,
c,d>0
. As concerns the first part of the lemma, let
t\geq 0
,
n\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}
and
k>4n\mathcal{R}_{\mu}\mathbf{E}[D_{1}]^{-1}
. Using (
4.5
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left(X_{t}^{express}<4n\mathcal{R}_{\mu}\Big|N_{t}^{express}=k\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}(R_{i}^{X}-X_{t_{i}-}^{express}+D_{i}-\mathbf{E}[D_{1}])<4n\mathcal{R}_{\mu}-\mathbf{E}[D_{1}]k\Big|N_{t}^{express}=k\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}(-R_{i}^{X}+X_{t_{i}-}^{express}-D_{i}+\mathbf{E}[D_{1}])>\mathbf{E}[D_{1}]k-4n\mathcal{R}_{\mu}\Big|N_{t}^{express}=k\right).

We conclude by using Hoeffding’s inequality together with the fact that for all
i\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\left|R_{i}^{X}-X_{t_{i}-}^{express}\right|

\displaystyle<4\mathcal{R}_{\mu}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\left|D_{i}-\mathbf{E}[D_{1}]\right|\leq 2\mathcal{R}_{\mu}.

∎

In order to bound
\mathbf{E}[T_{0,n}^{express}]
, we also need to control the number of jumps made by the express chain over the time interval
[0,t]
. Recall the definition of
J_{\mu}
given in (
4.3
).

Lemma 4.11
.

There exists
C_{\otimes}>0
such that for all
t>0
,

\mathbf{P}\left(N_{t}^{express}\leq 0.1J_{\mu}t\right)\leq\exp(-C_{\otimes}J_{\mu}t).

Proof.

The proof relies on the fact that the express chain jumps at rate
J_{\mu}
. Hence,
N_{t}^{express}
is Poisson distributed with parameter
J_{\mu}t
.

Let
t>0
. Using a standard Chernoff bound, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left(N_{t}^{express}\leq 0.1J_{\mu}t\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(eJ_{\mu}t)^{0.1J_{\mu}t}e^{-J_{\mu}t}}{(0.1J_{\mu}t)^{0.1J_{\mu}t}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp(0.1J_{\mu}t)\exp(-J_{\mu}t)}{\exp(0.1J_{\mu}t\,\ln(0.1))}

\displaystyle=\exp(0.1J_{\mu}t-J_{\mu}t-0.1J_{\mu}t\ln(0.1)),

and so taking
C_{\otimes}=J_{\mu}(0.9-0.1\ln(10))
allows us to conclude.
∎

Combining Lemmas
4.10
and
4.11
, we obtain an upper bound for
\mathbf{E}[T_{0,n}^{express}]
.

Lemma 4.12
.

There exists
C_{2}>0
such that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\mathbf{E}\Big[T_{0,n}^{express}\Big]\leq C_{2}n.

Proof.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then, using Lemmas
4.10
and
4.11
to pass from the fourth to the fifth line below, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\big[T_{0,n}^{express}\big]=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbf{P}\left(T_{0,n}^{express}>t\right)dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbf{P}\left(X_{t}^{express}<4n\mathcal{R}_{\mu}\right)dt

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{100n\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}{J_{\mu}\mathbf{E}[D_{1}]}+\int_{\frac{100n\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}{J_{\mu}\mathbf{E}[D_{1}]}}^{\infty}\mathbf{P}\left(X_{t}^{express}<4n\mathcal{R}_{\mu}\right)dt

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{100n\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}{J_{\mu}\mathbf{E}[D_{1}]}+\int_{\frac{100n\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}{J_{\mu}\mathbf{E}[D_{1}]}}^{\infty}\mathbf{P}\left(N_{t}^{express}\leq\frac{J_{\mu}t}{10}\right)dt

\displaystyle+\int_{\frac{100n\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}{J_{\mu}\mathbf{E}[D_{1}]}}^{\infty}\mathbf{P}\left(\left\{X_{t}^{express}<4n\mathcal{R}_{\mu}\right\}\cap\left\{N_{t}^{express}>\frac{J_{\mu}t}{10}\right\}\right)dt

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{100n\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}{J_{\mu}\mathbf{E}[D_{1}]}+\int_{\frac{100n\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}{J_{\mu}\mathbf{E}[D_{1}]}}^{\infty}\exp(-C_{\otimes}J_{\mu}t)dt

(4.6)

\displaystyle+\int_{\frac{100n\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}{J_{\mu}\mathbf{E}[D_{1}]}}^{\infty}\exp\left(-\frac{10}{C_{1}J_{\mu}t}\left(\frac{J_{\mu}t\mathbf{E}[D_{1}]}{10}-4n\mathcal{R}_{\mu}\right)^{2}\right)dt.

Moreover, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\frac{100n\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}{J_{\mu}\mathbf{E}[D_{1}]}}^{\infty}\exp\bigg(-\frac{10}{C_{1}J_{\mu}t}\left(\frac{J_{\mu}t\mathbf{E}[D_{1}]}{10}-4n\mathcal{R}_{\mu}\right)^{2}\bigg)dt

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\frac{2\mathbf{E}[D_{1}]}{C_{1}}4n\mathcal{R}_{\mu}\right)\int_{\frac{100n\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}{J_{\mu}\mathbf{E}[D_{1}]}}^{\infty}\exp\left(-\frac{10}{C_{1}J_{\mu}t}\frac{t^{2}J_{\mu}^{2}\mathbf{E}[D_{1}]^{2}}{100}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{10}{C_{1}J_{\mu}t}16n^{2}\mathcal{R}_{\mu}^{2}\right)dt

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(\frac{8n}{C_{1}}\mathcal{R}_{\mu}\mathbf{E}[D_{1}]\right)\int_{\frac{100n\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}{J_{\mu}\mathbf{E}[D_{1}]}}^{\infty}\exp\left(-\frac{J_{\mu}t}{10C_{1}}\mathbf{E}[D_{1}]^{2}\right)dt

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\frac{8n}{C_{1}}\mathcal{R}_{\mu}\mathbf{E}[D_{1}]\right)\frac{10C_{1}}{J_{\mu}\mathbf{E}[D_{1}]^{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{J_{\mu}\mathbf{E}[D_{1}]^{2}}{10C_{1}}\frac{100n\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}{J_{\mu}\mathbf{E}[D_{1}]}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{10C_{1}}{J_{\mu}\mathbf{E}[D_{1}]^{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{2n}{C_{1}}\mathcal{R}_{\mu}\mathbf{E}[D_{1}]\right).

Since the first term on the r.h.s of (
4.6
) is proportional to
n
, and the second and third terms decrease exponentially fast in
n
, we can conclude.
∎

We can now conclude the proof of Lemma
4.7
.

Proof.

(Lemma
4.7
) Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
. By Lemma
4.12
,

\mathbf{E}\big[T_{0,n}^{express}\big]<+\infty,

and so by (
4.4
),

\mathbf{E}\big[T_{0,n}\big]<+\infty.

∎

We conclude this section with the proof of Proposition
4.1
.

Proof.

(Proposition
4.1
)
Combining Lemmas
4.12
,
4.4
,
4.5
and
4.6
with the fact that
T_{0,n}\geq 0
for all
n
, we obtain that the family
(T_{m,n})_{0\leq m\leq n}
satisfies all the assumptions (1.3), (1.7), (1.8) and (1.9) of Theorem 1.10 from
[
34
]
.
Therefore, there exists
\nu^{(\infty)}\geq 0
such that

\lim\limits_{n\to+\infty}\frac{\mathbf{E}\big[T_{0,n}\big]}{4n\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}=\lim\limits_{n\to+\infty}\frac{\mathbf{E}\big[\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{4n\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}^{\,(\infty)}\big]}{4n\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}=\nu^{(\infty)}.

Moreover, using the ergodicity property stated in Lemma

, we also have

\lim\limits_{n\to+\infty}\frac{T_{0,n}}{4n\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}=\lim\limits_{n\to+\infty}\frac{\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{4n\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}^{\,(\infty)}}{4n\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}=\nu^{(\infty)}\quad\text{ a.s.}

(We have multiplied
n
by the constant

in the denominator so that the limit

x\to\mathbf{E}[\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,(\infty)}]

).
∎

Remark 4.13
.

If we see
(X_{t}^{express})_{t\geq 0}
as a cumulative process (in the sense of Chapter VI.3 in
[

]
), it is possible to use Theorem 3.1 from this chapter and show that the limiting horizontal speed of advance of the express chain is equal to
J_{\mu}\mathbf{E}[D_{1}]
, yielding an explicit lower bound on the speed of growth of the occupied area in the
\infty
-parent SLFV. In particular, if all reproduction ellipses have the same shape parameters
(a,b,\gamma)
and if the total mass of

is

\sqrt{a^{2}\cos^{2}(\gamma)+b^{2}\sin^{2}(\gamma)}
.

5
Upper bound on the speed of growth of the
\infty
-parent SLFV

Recall that
(E_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is the
\infty
-parent ancestral process with initial condition
\{(0,0)\}
associated with
\mu^{\leftarrow}
, constructed using
\overline{\Pi}
.

In this section, we complete the result proved in Section
4
by showing that the growth of the
\infty
-parent SLFV and of its dual counterpart are
at most
linear in time. This can be rewritten as a limiting property of
\mathbf{E}[\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,(\infty)}]
as follows.

Proposition 5.1
.

\lim\limits_{x\to+\infty}\frac{\mathbf{E}\big[\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,(\infty)}\big]}{x}>0.

The proof can be found at the end of Section
5.3
. Combining this result with Propositions
3.9
and
4.1
gives us the result of Theorem
1.6
.

To show the result of Proposition
5.1
, we first observe that it is sufficient to focus on the case in which all reproduction events are balls of fixed radius. Indeed, we have the following comparison result.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\overline{\Pi}^{\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}
be a Poisson point process on
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\times S_{\mu}
with intensity measure

\mu^{\leftarrow}(S_{\mu})dt\otimes dz\otimes\delta_{\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}(da)\otimes\delta_{\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}(db)\otimes\delta_{0}(\gamma).

Let
(E_{t}^{\mathcal{R}_{\mu}})_{t\geq 0}
be the
\infty
-parent ancestral process with initial condition
\{(0,0)\}
constructed using
\overline{\Pi}^{\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}
, and for all
x>0
, let
\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}
be the first time
(E_{t}^{\mathcal{R}_{\mu}})_{t\geq 0}
reaches
H^{x}
, in the sense of Definition
3.6
.
Then, for all
x>0
,

\mathbf{E}\big[\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,(\infty)}\big]\geq\mathbf{E}\left[\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}\right].

Proof.

The proof relies on the following coupling between
(E_{t})_{t\geq 0}
and
(E_{t}^{\mathcal{R}_{\mu}})_{t\geq 0}
. Instead of being independent from
\overline{\Pi}
, the Poisson point process
\overline{\Pi}^{\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}
is constructed using the points from
\overline{\Pi}
, as follows:

\overline{\Pi}^{\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}:=\big\{(t,z,\mathcal{R}_{\mu},\mathcal{R}_{\mu},0):\ (t,z,a,b,\gamma)\in\overline{\Pi}\big\}.

That is, we replace the elliptical area of each reproduction event in
\overline{\Pi}
by a ball of maximal radius
\mathcal{R}_{\mu}
. Since
\mathfrak{B}_{a,b,\gamma}(z)\subseteq\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}(z)
, this coupling ensures that

\forall\,t\geq 0,E_{t}\subseteq E_{t}^{\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}\text{ a.s.}

Therefore, for all
x>0
,

\min\left\{t\geq 0:E_{t}\cap H^{x}\neq\emptyset\right\}\geq\min\left\{t\geq 0:E_{t}^{\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}\cap H^{x}\right\}\text{ a.s.},

which allows us to conclude.
∎

To alleviate the notation, we only provide the proof of Proposition
5.1
when
\mu^{\leftarrow}=\pi^{-1}\delta_{1}\otimes\delta_{1}\otimes\delta_{0}
, so that all reproduction events happen in balls of radius
1
and the rate at which a given point in space is overlapped by an event is equal to
1
. The proof can be easily generalised to balls of arbitrary fixed radius and any intensity of events by a simple scaling of time and space. We can then use Lemma
5.2
to obtain the corresponding result for ellipses with bounded parameters.

5.1
A first-passage percolation problem

We consider the graph
\mathcal{G}
on the vertex set
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, in which
(i,j)
and
(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})
are connected by an edge if and only if

\displaystyle(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\in\big\{(i+1,j),(i-1,j),(i,j+1),(i,j-1),

\displaystyle(i-1,j-1),(i-1,j+1),

\displaystyle\qquad(i+1,j-1),(i+1,j+1)\big\}.

To each edge
e
of
\mathcal{G}
, we associate an independent exponential random variable

\mathcal{E}_{e}\sim Exp(16\times\pi^{-1}).

\mathcal{E}_{e}
corresponds to the time needed to pass through the corresponding edge. Following standard terminology in first-passage percolation, we call it the
passage time
of the edge. The choice of the rate of the exponential distribution ensures we can later compare the growth of the
\infty
-parent ancestral process to the first-passage percolation problem we now introduce.

If
\Gamma
is a (potentially infinite) path formed by the edges
e_{1},...,e_{n},...
, then the passage time of the path
\Gamma
is defined as

\mathcal{E}_{\Gamma}=\sum_{e\in\Gamma}\mathcal{E}_{e}.

If
z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, we define the first-passage time
\mathcal{E}_{z_{1},z_{2}}
from
z_{1}
to
z_{2}
(or equivalently, from
z_{2}
to
z_{1}
since
\mathcal{G}
is not oriented) as the minimum over the passage times of all the (finite) paths going from
z_{1}
to
z_{2}
. We then define

\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{n}^{fpp}:=\min\left\{t\geq 0:\exists m\in\mathbb{Z},\mathcal{E}_{(0,0),(n,m)}\leq t\right\}.

In other words,
\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{n}^{fpp}
is the time needed to reach a point at horizontal separation
n
from the origin, starting from the origin.

The interest of this first-passage percolation problem lies in the fact that since the passage time of any given edge is a.s. strictly positive, generalising Theorem 6.7 from
[
39
]

to our lattice yields the following result.

Lemma 5.3
.

We have

\lim\limits_{n\to+\infty}\frac{\mathbf{E}\big[\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{n}^{fpp}\big]}{n}>0.

In order to use this lemma and show Proposition
5.1
, we need to be able to compare
\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,(\infty)}
and
\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{n}^{\,fpp}
. The main obstacle to this comparison lies in the fact that the
\infty
-parent ancestral process is continuous in space, while the first-passage percolation problem is defined on a graph. Therefore, we now introduce a way to discretise the
\infty
-parent ancestral process.

5.2
Discretisation of the
\infty
-parent ancestral process

In order to discretise the
\infty
-parent ancestral process, we first place a grid on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and associate a cell to each site of the lattice. Let

\mathcal{V}:=\left\{(4i,4j):(i,j)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\right\}

be the underlying grid, and for all
(i,j)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, let
\mathcal{C}_{i,j}
be the square with centre
(4i,4j)
and side length
2
.
That is,

\mathcal{C}_{i,j}:=\left\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:|x-4i|\leq 1\text{ and }|y-4j|\leq 1\right\}.

Each
\mathcal{C}_{i,j}
,
i,j\in\mathbb{Z}
corresponds to the cell associated with the site
(4i,4j)
of the grid
\mathcal{V}
. See Figure
5
for an illustration.

Figure 5
:
Grid used to discretise the
\infty
-parent ancestral process when reproduction events are balls. Each hatched square corresponds to a cell. The grey area around the site
(4i,4j)
, indicated by a black cross, corresponds to the area in which centres of reproduction events have to fall to intersect the cell
\mathcal{C}_{i,j}
. The white areas contain all the centres of reproduction events which do not intersect any cell.

This construction satisfies the two following key properties, that are consequences of the fact that all reproduction events are balls of radius
1
.

1.

For all
z=(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, unless
x=4i+2
,
i\in\mathbb{Z}
or
y=4j+2
,
j\in\mathbb{Z}
, the ball
\mathcal{B}_{1}(z)
intersects at most one cell.

In other words, each reproduction event intersects at most one cell a.s. Moreover, if
\mathcal{B}_{1}(z)
does not intersect any cell, then it means that
z
fell into one of the areas in white on Figure
5
.

2.

If we refer to a sequence of reproduction events occurring in chronological order and such that each reproduction event intersects the previous one as a
path of reproduction events
, then any path of reproduction events for which none of the corresponding balls intersect a cell is almost surely confined in one of the white areas on Figure
5
, in the sense that all the reproduction event centres fall into the same white area almost surely.

The discretised version of the
\infty
-parent ancestral process, denoted by
(D_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, is then defined as follows. For all
t\geq 0
, let

D_{t}:=\left\{(i,j)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:\mathcal{C}_{i,j}\cap E_{t}\neq\emptyset\right\}

be the set of all cells which intersect the
\infty
-parent ancestral process associated with
\mu^{\leftarrow}=\pi^{-1}\delta_{1}\otimes\delta_{1}\otimes\delta_{0}
(and with initial condition
(0,0)
) at time
t
. Let
(\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{n}^{discr})_{n\geq 0}
be the random variables defined for all
n\geq 0
by

\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{n}^{\,discr}:=\min\left\{t\geq 0:\exists m\in\mathbb{Z},(n,m)\in D_{t}\right\}.

Due to the structure of the grid and the size of the cells, we have the following result.

Lemma 5.4
.

For all
n\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}
, we have

\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{n}^{\,discr}\leq\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{4n}^{\,(\infty)}\quad\text{a.s}.

Proof.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}
. Let
z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
be the centre of the reproduction event which occurs at time
\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{4n}^{\,(\infty)}
and makes the
\infty
-parent ancestral process reach for the first time a point at horizontal separation
4n
from the origin. Then, a.s. there exists
j\in\mathbb{Z}
such that

\mathcal{B}_{1}(z)\cap\mathcal{C}_{n,j}\neq\emptyset,

and hence
\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{n}^{\,discr}\leq\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{4n}^{\,(\infty)}
.
∎

In all that follows, for all
(i,j),(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, we say that

•

the cell
\mathcal{C}_{i,j}
is
active
at time
t
if
(i,j)\in D_{t}
;

•

the cell
\mathcal{C}_{i,j}
is
activated
at time
t
if
(i,j)\in D_{t}
and
(i,j)\notin D_{t-}
;

•

the cell
\mathcal{C}_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}

activates

\mathcal{C}_{i,j}
at time
t
if there exists
s<t
such that
\mathcal{C}_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}
is active at time
s
, and if there exists a path of reproduction events starting from
\mathcal{C}_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}
at time
s
, initially overlapping an area of
\mathcal{C}_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}
containing type
1
individuals, and reaching
\mathcal{C}_{i,j}
for the first time at time
t
while not intersecting any other cell in the time interval
[s,t]
.

Notice that under this terminology, a cell can activate another one which is already active. Moreover, with probability one the only cells that the cell
\mathcal{C}_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}
can activate are its nearest neighbours in the graph
\mathcal{G}
, that is, the cells
\mathcal{C}_{i,j}
such that

\sqrt{(i-i^{\prime})^{2}+(j-j^{\prime})^{2}}\leq\sqrt{2}.

5.3
Comparison to the first-passage percolation problem

Let us now compare the growth of the discretised
\infty
-parent ancestral process
(D_{t})_{t\geq 0}
to that of the first-passage percolation problem introduced earlier. Recall that

\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{n}^{fpp}:=\min\left\{t\geq 0:\exists m\in\mathbb{Z},\mathcal{E}_{(0,0),(n,m)}\leq t\right\}

is the time needed by the process associated with the first-passage percolation problem to reach a point at horizontal separation
n
from the origin, starting from the origin.

Proposition 5.5
.

The random variable
\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{n}^{\,discr}
is stochastically bounded from below by
\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{n}^{\,fpp}
. That is, for all
t\geq 0
,

\mathbf{P}\left(\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{n}^{\,discr}\geq t\right)\geq\mathbf{P}\left(\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{n}^{\,fpp}\geq t\right).

Proof.

We recall that for all
e\in\mathcal{G}
,
\mathcal{E}_{e}\sim\mathcal{E}\text{xp}(16\pi^{-1})
is the passage time of edge
e
. In order to show Proposition
5.5
, we show that cells are activated faster in the first-passage percolation problem than in the discretized
\infty
-parent ancestral process, and conclude by induction on the number of cells reached.

First, we observe that for both processes, cells are activated one after another a.s., and a cell cannot be activated if all its neighbours are inactive. Therefore, we can focus on the time needed for a cell to become active once (a.s. exactly) one of its neighbours becomes active. Regarding the first-passage percolation process, this time is bounded from above by
\mathcal{E}_{e}
, where
e
is the edge connecting the active neighbouring cell to the focal cell. Regarding the discretised
\infty
-parent ancestral process, this time is bounded from below by the time needed for the cell to be intersected by a reproduction event (which is a prerequisite for the cell to become active). Such reproduction events occur at a rate bounded from above by
16\pi^{-1}
. Therefore, the time needed for the cell to become active in the discretised
\infty
-parent ancestral process is stochastically bounded from below by
\mathcal{E}_{e}
.
∎

Remark 5.6
.

Due to correlations between activations by neighbouring cells, it would be more difficult to construct a coupling with the first-passage percolation problem. However, the stochastic comparison we obtained is sufficient.

We can now show Proposition
5.1
.

Proof.

(Proposition
5.1
)
By Proposition
5.5
and Lemma
5.4
, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}
,

\mathbf{E}\big[\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{4n}^{\,(\infty)}\big]\geq\mathbf{E}\big[\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{n}^{\,discr}\big]\geq\mathbf{E}\big[\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{n}^{\,fpp}\big].

By Lemma
5.3
, we know that

\lim\limits_{n\to+\infty}\frac{\mathbf{E}\big[\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{n}^{\,fpp}\big]}{4n}>0.

Since we know that
\lim\limits_{n\to+\infty}\mathbf{E}\big[\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{n}^{\,(\infty)}\big]\times n^{-1}
exists by Proposition
4.1
, we obtain that

\lim\limits_{n\to+\infty}\frac{\mathbf{E}\big[\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{4n}^{\,(\infty)}\big]}{4n}>0.

We conclude by using the fact that
x\to\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,(\infty)}
is nondecreasing by Lemma
3.7
.
∎

We conclude this section with the proof of Theorem
1.6
.

Proof.

(Theorem
1.6
)
By Proposition
4.1
, we know that there exists
\nu^{(\infty)}\geq 0
such that

\lim\limits_{x\to+\infty}\frac{\mathbf{E}\big[\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{(\infty)}\big]}{x}=\nu^{(\infty)}.

Moreover, by Proposition
5.1
, we have

\lim\limits_{x\to+\infty}\frac{\mathbf{E}\big[\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{(\infty)}\big]}{x}>0.

Therefore,
\nu^{(\infty)}>0
and we conclude by using Proposition
3.9
.
∎

6
Speed of growth of the
k
-parent SLFV

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.7
. To this end, we combine Proposition
3.10
with estimates on the probability that the reflected ancestral process
\overleftarrow{\Xi}^{(k)}
reaches horizontal distance
x
before time
t
, that are derived using a weaker version of the subadditivity argument developped in Section
4
.

Fix
k\geq 2
. Recall that
\overleftarrow{\Pi}^{+}
is a Poisson point process on
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\times S_{\mu}\times(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{\infty}
with intensity measure
dt\otimes dz\otimes\mu^{\leftarrow}(da,db,d\gamma)\tilde{u}_{(a,b,\gamma)}(d\mathbf{p})
defined on the probability space
(\mathbf{\Omega},\mathbf{\mathcal{F}},\mathbf{P})
, and that
(\overleftarrow{\Xi}_{t}^{(k)})_{t\geq 0}
is the
k
-parent ancestral process with initial condition
\delta_{(0,0)}
constructed using
\overleftarrow{\Pi}^{+}
. For all
x>0
, we set

\displaystyle\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,(k)}

\displaystyle:=\min\left\{t\geq 0:A\left(\overleftarrow{\Xi}_{t}^{(k)}\right)\cap\text{H}^{x}\neq\emptyset\right\}

\displaystyle\text{and }\overleftarrow{T}\!_{x}^{\,(k)}

\displaystyle:=\min\left\{t\geq 0:\forall t^{\prime}\geq t,A\left(\overleftarrow{\Xi}_{t^{\prime}}^{(k)}\right)\cap\text{H}^{x}\neq\emptyset\right\}.

The strategy is as follows. First, we use an approach analogous to that in Section
4
to obtain that:

Lemma 6.1
.

There exists
\nu^{(k)}\geq\nu^{(\infty)}
such that

\lim\limits_{x\to+\infty}\frac{\mathbf{E}\Big[\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,(k)}\Big]}{x}=\nu^{(k)}.

The proof of Lemma
6.1
uses an
express chain
which encodes a special line of ascent (which jumps onto the location of the “right-most parent” each time the current position of the chain is overlapped by an event). We then use this express chain to show that:

Lemma 6.2
.

There exists
C_{1}>0
such that for all
x\geq 0
,

\mathbf{E}\left[\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,(k)}\right]\leq\mathbf{E}\left[\overleftarrow{T}\!_{x}^{\,(k)}\right]\leq\mathbf{E}\left[\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,(k)}\right]+C_{1}.

Using Lemma
6.2
, we shall be able to conclude that
\nu^{(k)}
is also the limit of
x^{-1}\mathbf{E}[\overleftarrow{T}\!_{x}^{\,(k)}]
, and then a simple use of the Markov inequality will give us the desired result. The proof of Theorem
1.7
can be found at the end of this section.

Proof.

(Lemma
6.1
.)
We follow the same strategy as in the proof of Proposition
4.1
and show that the sequence
(\mathbf{E}[\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{4n\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}^{(k)}])_{n\geq 0}
is subadditive. To do so, we introduce the following family of random variables.
For all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let

\displaystyle T_{0,n}^{(k)}

\displaystyle:=\min\left\{t\geq 0:A\left(\overleftarrow{\Xi}_{t}^{(k)}\right)\cap H^{4n\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}\neq\emptyset\right\}=\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{4n\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}^{\,(k)},

and let
P_{n}^{(k)}
be sampled uniformly at random among the points in

A(\overleftarrow{\Xi}_{T_{0,n}^{(k)}}^{(k)})\cap H^{4n\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}.

Note that in contrast to the analogous variable
P_{n}
defined in Section
4.1
, almost surely
P_{n}^{(k)}
is not at horizontal separation of exactly
4n\mathcal{R}_{\mu}
of
(0,0)
(
i.e.
, it has a first coordinate
(P_{n}^{(k)})_{1}>4n\mathcal{R}_{\mu}
a.s.).

Next, for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
, let
(\overleftarrow{\Xi}_{t}^{(k),m})_{t\geq 0}
be the
k
-parent ancestral process started from
\delta_{P_{m}^{(k)}}
, constructed using only the reproduction events in
\overleftarrow{\Pi}^{+}
occurring after time
T_{0,m}^{(k)}
. We then set for all
n\geq m

T_{m,n}^{(k)}:=\min\left\{t\geq 0:A\left(\overleftarrow{\Xi}_{t+T_{0,m}^{(k)}}^{(k),m}\right)\cap H^{4n\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}\neq\emptyset\right\}.

By construction, we have that for all
0\leq m<n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle T_{0,n}^{(k)}

\displaystyle\leq T_{0,m}^{(k)}+T_{m,n}^{(k)}

\displaystyle\text{and}\quad\mathbf{E}\left[T_{m,n}^{(k)}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{E}\left[\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{4(n-m)\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}^{\,(k)}\right].

Therefore,

\mathbf{E}\left[\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{4n\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}^{\,(k)}\right]\leq\mathbf{E}\left[\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{4m\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}^{\,(k)}\right]+\mathbf{E}\left[\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{4(n-m)\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}^{\,(k)}\right],

and the family
(\mathbf{E}[\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{4n\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}^{\,(k)}])_{n\geq 0}
is subadditive.

In order to apply Fekete’s subadditive lemma
[
21
]
and conclude, we now need to show that there exists
c_{k}>0
such that

\forall n\geq 0,\quad\mathbf{E}\left[\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{4n\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}^{\,(k)}\right]\leq c_{k}n.

To do so, we proceed again as in the proof of Proposition
4.1
, and construct an express chain for the
k
-parent SLFV, denoted
(C_{t}^{(k)})_{t\geq 0}
and defined as follows.

•

First we set
C_{0}^{(k)}=(0,0)
.

•

Then, for all
(t,z_{c},a,b,\gamma,(p_{n})_{n\geq 1})\in\overleftarrow{\Pi}^{+}
, if
C_{t-}^{(k)}\in\mathfrak{B}_{a,b,\gamma}(z_{c})
, we write
z_{c}=(x_{c},y_{c})
and
p_{n}=(x_{n},y_{n})
. We define
L
to be the index of the (almost surely unique) point in
p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}
with maximal abscissa (that is, the right-most “potential parent” sampled). Recalling that all
p_{n}
are sampled from
\mathfrak{B}_{a,b,\gamma}((0,0))
, we set

C_{t}^{(k)}=z_{c}+p_{L}
.

•

We do nothing otherwise.

By construction (since
k\geq 2
),
\mathbf{E}[x_{L}-x_{c}]>0
, and for all
n\in\mathbb{N}\backslash\{0\}
and
t\geq 0
, we have

\left\{C_{t}^{(k)}\in\text{H}^{4n\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}\right\}\subseteq\left\{\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{4n\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}^{\,(k)}\leq t\right\}.

For all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we set

T_{0,n}^{\text{express},(k)}:=\min\left\{t\geq 0:C_{t}^{(k)}\in\text{H}^{4n\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}\right\}.

Using the same arguments as in Section
4.3
, we obtain the existence of
c_{k}>0
such that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\mathbf{E}\left[\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{4n\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}^{\,(k)}\right]\leq\mathbf{E}\left[T_{0,n}^{\text{express},(k)}\right]\leq c_{k}n.

We can now apply Fekete’s subadditive lemma and obtain the existence of
\nu^{(k)}\geq 0
such that

\lim\limits_{n\to+\infty}\frac{\mathbf{E}\left[\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{4n\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}^{(k)}\right]}{4n\mathcal{R}_{\mu}}=\nu^{(k)},

from which we deduce that

\lim\limits_{x\to+\infty}\frac{\mathbf{E}\big[\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,(k)}\big]}{x}=\nu^{(k)}

by monotonicity. The inequality
\nu^{(k)}\geq\nu^{(\infty)}
comes from the obvious fact that for all
x>0
,

\mathbf{E}\left[\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,(k)}\right]\geq\mathbf{E}\left[\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,(\infty)}\right].

∎

Proof.

(Lemma
6.2
.) Let
x\geq 0
. By construction,
\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,(k)}\leq\overleftarrow{T}\!_{x}^{\,(k)}
, which gives us the first inequality. For the second inequality, observe first that for all
z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,

\mathbf{P}\Big(\exists t\geq 0:\ \forall t^{\prime}\geq t,\,A\left(\overleftarrow{\Xi}_{t^{\prime}}^{(k)}\right)\cap H^{x}\neq\emptyset\,\Big|\,\overleftarrow{\Xi}_{0}^{(k)}=\delta_{z}\Big)=1,

(6.1)

and there exists
p_{0}\in(0,1)
independent of
x
such that for all
z\in H^{x}
,

\mathbf{P}\Big(\forall t\geq 0,\,A\left(\overleftarrow{\Xi}_{t}^{(k)}\right)\cap H^{x}\neq\emptyset\,\Big|\,\overleftarrow{\Xi}_{0}^{(k)}=\delta_{z}\Big)\geq p_{0}.

(6.2)

Indeed, starting the
express chain

(C_{t}^{(k)})_{t\geq 0}
from
z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we obtain a random walk whose jumps are bounded and have a positive average first coordinate. Therefore, with probability
1
the random walk made of the first coordinate of
C^{(k)}
drifts towards
+\infty
and there exists
t\geq 0
such that for all
t^{\prime}\geq t
,
C^{(k)}_{t^{\prime}}\in H^{x}
. In addition, when
z\in H^{x}
, the probability that this time
t
is equal to
0
(and thus
C^{(k)}
has its whole trajectory in
H^{x}
) is positive and by the invariance properties of its dynamics, this probability is independent of the second coordinate of
z
and is minimal for
z_{1}=x
. We write
p_{0}>0
for this minimal probability. Since
C_{t}^{(k)}
always belongs to the set of atoms of
\overleftarrow{\Xi}^{(k)}_{t}
(by construction), the first part of our argument yields (
6.1
) and the second part yields (
6.2
). In particular, this tells us that
\overleftarrow{T}\!_{x}^{\,(k)}<\infty
almost surely. Pushing the analysis of the
express chain
one step further and decomposing the trajectory of
C^{(k)}
into excursions away from
H^{x}
(whose time lengths are integrable, independently of
x
, since the first coordinate of
C^{(k)}
performs a one-dimensional random walk with bounded jumps of positive mean) until the first time at which the trajectory of
C^{(k)}
remains in
H^{x}
for ever after (the number of such excursions being stochastically bounded by a geometric random variable with parameter
p_{0}
), we obtain that there exists
C_{1}>0
satisfying

\sup_{z\in H^{x}}\mathbf{E}\Big[\inf\Big\{t\geq 0:\ \forall t^{\prime}\geq t,\,C_{t^{\prime}}^{(k)}\in H^{x}\Big\}\,\big|\,C^{(k)}_{0}=z\Big]\leq C_{1}.

(6.3)

Coming back to the case where
\overleftarrow{\Xi}^{(k)}_{0}=\delta_{(0,0)}
, using the strong Markov property of
\overleftarrow{\Xi}^{(k)}
at time
\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,(k)}
and the fact that for every
t
,
C_{t}^{(k)}
belongs to the set of atoms of
\overleftarrow{\Xi}^{(k)}_{t}
, enables us to write that

\mathbf{E}\Big[\overleftarrow{T}\!_{x}^{\,(k)}-\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,(k)}\Big]\leq C_{1},

(6.4)

which gives us the desired result.
∎

Let us conclude this section with the proof of Theorem
1.7
.

Proof.

(Theorem
1.7
.) By Lemmas
6.1
and
6.2
, we have

\lim\limits_{x\to+\infty}\frac{\mathbf{E}\Big[\overleftarrow{T}\!_{x}^{\,(k)}\Big]}{x}=\nu^{(k)}.

Therefore, for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
x_{0}(\varepsilon)>0
such that for all
x\geq x_{0}(\varepsilon)
,

\mathbf{E}\Big[\overleftarrow{T}\!_{x}^{\,(k)}\Big]\leq\big(\nu^{(k)}+\varepsilon\big)x.

(6.5)

Fix
\varepsilon>0
and let
x\geq x_{0}(\varepsilon)
. By Proposition
3.10
, we have for every
t>0

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\lim\limits_{\epsilon\to 0}V_{\epsilon}^{-1}\int_{\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}((x,0))}w_{t}^{(k)}(z)dz=0\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{P}\left(A\left(\overleftarrow{\Xi}_{t}^{(k)}\right)\cap\text{H}^{x}=\emptyset\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{P}\left(\exists t^{\prime}\geq t,A\left(\overleftarrow{\Xi}_{t^{\prime}}^{(k)}\right)\cap\text{H}^{x}=\emptyset\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{P}\left(\overleftarrow{T}\!_{x}^{(k)}>t\right).

We conclude by using the Markov inequality together with (
6.5
).
∎

7
Numerical simulations

The goal of this section is to obtain an approximation for the limiting speed of growth
(\nu^{(\infty)})^{-1}
by means of numerical simulations, and compare it to the lower bound given by the proof of Theorem
1.6
. To do so, we use the fact that

(\nu^{(\infty)})^{-1}=\left(\lim\limits_{x\to+\infty}x^{-1}\mathbf{E}[\overleftarrow{\tau}\!_{x}^{\,(\infty)}]\right)^{-1},

which is a consequence of Theorem
1.6
combined with Proposition
3.9
. Indeed, the
\infty
-parent ancestral process is easier to simulate than its forwards-in-time counterpart, since it jumps at a finite rate at any time, and since there are no border effects to take into account while simulating the process on an appropriately chosen compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

We focus on the case in which all ellipses have the same shape parameters. In order to be able to compare the speed of growth of the occupied regions in
\infty
-parent SLFV with different shape parameters, we assume that any given location
z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
is affected by a reproduction event at rate
1
. Therefore, we take

\mu(da,db,d\gamma)=V_{a_{0},b_{0}}^{-1}\delta_{a_{0}}(da)\otimes\delta_{b_{0}}(db)\otimes\delta_{0}(d\gamma),

where
a_{0}\in(0,+\infty)
and
b_{0}
is chosen such that the volume of the corresponding ellipse is equal to
\pi
.

For
9
different values of
a
ranging from
0.33
to
3
, we simulate

Numerical simulations of the
\infty
-parent SLFV itself suggest that the growth of the process is driven by “spikes” in the expansion direction, see Figure
7
for an example. These spikes are relatively rare, but then they thicken and grow sideways, bridging the gap with the rest of the population. Therefore, they can increase the speed of growth of the process significantly. Moreover, a “spike-driven” growth would have important implications in terms of genetic diversity at the front edge. The study of the contribution of the spikes to the overall growth will be the object of a follow-up study.
