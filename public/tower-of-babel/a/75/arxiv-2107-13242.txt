Type theories in category theory
Tesla Zhang

Abstract
. We introduce basic notions in category theory to type theorists, including comprehension categories, categories with attributes, contextual categories, type categories, and categories with families along with additional discussions that are not very closely related to type theories by listing definitions, lemmata (with detailed, diagram-rich proofs), and remarks. By doing so, this introduction becomes more friendly as a referential material to be read in random order (instead of from the beginning to the end). In the end, we list some mistakes made in the early versions of this introduction. The interpretation of common type formers in dependent type theories are discussed based on existing categorical constructions instead of mechanically derived from their type theoretical definition. Non-dependent type formers include unit, products (as fiber products), and functions (as fiber exponents ), and dependent ones include sigma types, extensional equalities (as equalizers), pi types, and the universe of (all) propositions (as the subobject classifier).

Warning 1.1

(Prerequisites)

We assume the familiarity of the following concepts:

(1)

Set theory: sets, subsets, elements, functions, image, fiber, injectivity, and cartesian products.

(2)

Type theory: dependent types, lambda calculus, (telescopic) contexts, typing judgments, and substitutions.

(3)

Category theory: categories, objects, morphisms (isomorphisms), commutative diagrams,

Typing judgments and substitutions are slightly discussed (in

Notation 1.2
.

We introduce some notational conventions in category theory.

•

We write
A\in\mathcal{C}
to say that “
A
is an object in the category
\mathcal{C}
”, and
f\in\mathcal{C}(A,B)
to say that
“

is a morphism in the category
\mathcal{C}
from
A
towards
B
”. In the literature, the former
is also written as
A\in\text{Ob}(\mathcal{C})
(so that the category
\mathcal{C}
and the set of objects
\text{Ob}(\mathcal{C})
are distinguished)
and the latter is also written as
f\in\text{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(A,B)
.
The
\text{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(A,B)
notation is also (though rarely) used when the category
\mathcal{C}
is a complicated expression.

•

We write
\mathrm{id}_{A}\in\mathcal{C}(A,A)
for the identity morphism,
dom(f)
and
cod(f)
for the domain projection
and the codomain projection of morphisms.
We also say a morphism
f
is a morphism
from

dom(f)
and
towards

cod(f)
.

•

We write
f\circ g\in\mathcal{C}(dom(g),cod(f))
for composition of morphisms.

•

We use dashed lines in commutative diagrams for unique morphisms.

•

We write
A\cong B\in\mathcal{C}
for objects
A
and
B
in the category
\mathcal{C}
are equivalent up to unique isomorphism.

•

We write
F\simeq G
for naturally isomorphic functors
F
and
G
.

∎

Notation 1.3
.

We introduce some notational conventions and terminologies about sets and functions.

•

For two sets

, the “identity” function
\iota:B\to A
is called a

•

We use
-

and the formula itself is the rest of the function body. For example,

(-\circ f)
is a function that maps

(in the literature, people also write
g\mapsto g\circ f
).

•

We write
A\simeq B
for isomorphic sets
A
and
B
.

2.
Background and motivation

Remark 2.1
.

The notion of
substitution
in type theory is the process of replacing a variable
with a term. We sometimes refer to the action of replacement with the verb
apply
,
like “apply this substitution to that term”.
We generalize this notion to lists, so that one substitution deals with multiple replacements.
∎

Definition 2.2
.

The data carried by a substitution is called a
substitution object
–
a list of mapping from variables to terms.
Substitution objects can be
applied
on terms.
∎

Demonstration 2.3
.

Consider a lambda calculus term
(\lambda x.\lambda y.u)~a~b
, we denote its reduced form as
u[a/x,b/y]

(a notation similar to the one used by Curry
[
SubstNotation
]
),
which means the term
u
applied by a substitution object
[a/x,b/y]
(
2.2
).
∎

Remark 2.4

(Nameless)
.

Contexts, types, terms, and substitution objects are considered up to
\alpha
-renaming throughout this document.
That is to say, there are no “names” in these structures –
\alpha
-equivalent terms are considered the same.
We can imagine that we are using a nameless representation of binding structures, such as de Bruijn indices
[
DBI
]
.
∎

Notation 2.5
.

We introduce some notational conventions in type theory.

We use
\Gamma,\Delta
to denote
contexts
,
\sigma,\gamma
(recall
1.5
) for
substitution objects (
2.2
), lowercase letters for terms (preferably
u,v
),
and uppercase letters for types (preferably
A,B
).

We write
u\sigma
for “applying the substitution object
\sigma
to term
u
”.
∎

Remark 2.6
.

In a simple type theory, terms are typed in a context and types are canonical mathematical objects.
However, since we are dealing with a dependent type theory, types will be formed in a context too.
