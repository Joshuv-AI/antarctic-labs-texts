Generalized extremiles and risk measures of distorted random variables
Dieter Debrauwer, Irène Gijbels, Klaus Herrmann

Abstract
Quantiles, expectiles and extremiles can be seen as concepts defined via an optimization problem, where this optimization problem is driven by two important ingredients: the loss function as well as a distributional weight function. This leads to the formulation of a general class of functionals that contains next to the above concepts many interesting quantities, including also a subclass of distortion risks. The focus of the paper is on developing estimators for such functionals and to establish asymptotic consistency and asymptotic normality of these estimators. The advantage of the general framework is that it allows application to a very broad range of concepts, providing as such estimation tools and tools for statistical inference (for example for construction of confidence intervals) for all involved concepts. After developing the theory for the general functional we apply it to various settings, illustrating the broad applicability. In a real data example the developed tools are used in an analysis of natural disasters.



Keywords:

Distortion risk measure, expected loss minimization, extremile, nonparametric estimation, risk measure, statistical functional.

1
Introduction

There is a vast literature on quantiles and expectiles, and their applications in a variety of settings, in particular as risk measures. See
Artzner et al. (1999)
,
Taylor (2008)
,
Bellini and Di Bernardina (2017)
, among others. For a random variable
X
, its
\delta
th quantile (with
\delta\in(0,1)
) can be defined as

\argmin_{c\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{E}[\ell_{\delta}(X,c)]\qquad\mbox{with}\quad\ell_{\delta}(x,c)=\left|\delta-1_{\{x\leq c\}}\right||x-c|,

(1)

see for example
Ferguson (1967)
and
Koenker and Bassett (1978)
. Herein
1_{A}
denotes the indicator function on a set
A
, i.e.,
1_{A}=1
, if
A
holds, and zero otherwise.
Expectiles, introduced and first studied by
Aigner et al. (1967)
and
Newey and Powell (1987)
, are defined as follows: the
\delta
th expectile (with
\delta\in(0,1)
) of
X
is given by

\argmin_{c\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{E}[\ell_{\delta}(X,c)]\qquad\mbox{with}\quad\ell_{\delta}(x,c)=\left|\delta-1_{\{x\leq c\}}\right||x-c|^{2}.

(2)

Quantiles can also be viewed via an alternative optimization problem, namely the
\tau
th quantile (with
\tau\in(0,1)
) of
X
(with cumulative distribution function
F_{X}
) is obtained via

\argmin_{c\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{E}[J_{\tau}(F_{X}(X))\ell_{\delta}(X,c)]\qquad\mbox{with}\quad\ell_{\delta}(x,c)=|x-c|,

(3)

and

J_{\tau}(u)=\begin{cases}s(\tau)(1-u)^{s(\tau)-1}&\text{ if }0<\tau<\frac{1}{2}\\
r(\tau)u^{r(\tau)-1}&\text{ if }\frac{1}{2}\leq\tau<1,\end{cases}

(4)

with
r(\tau)=s(1-\tau)=\ln(1/2)/\ln(\tau)
.
This alternative formulation of quantiles can be found in

Newey and Powell (1987)
. Consequently (
1
) and (
3
) (taking in the latter
\tau=\delta
)
provides two approaches to obtain the
\delta
th quantile of a random variable
X
. Recently
Daouia et al. (2019)
studied the class of extremiles, where the
\tau
th extremile of
X
is obtained via

\argmin_{c\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{E}[J_{\tau}(F_{X}(X))\ell_{\delta}(X,c)]\qquad\mbox{with}\quad\ell_{\delta}(x,c)=(x-c)^{2}\quad\mbox{and}\quad J_{\tau}\,\mbox{as in \eqref{densitytauExtremiles}}.

(5)

Of interest is further to note that the function
J_{\tau}
is in fact the derivative of a function
K_{\tau}
, which is a specific cumulative distribution function defined on the interval
[0,1]
(see expression (
9
) and Figure
1
).

The above observations inspire to look at a more general form of an optimization problem, and to study functionals defined via

\argmin_{c\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{E}[d_{\tau}(F_{X}(X))\,\ell_{\delta}(X,c)],

where
d_{\tau}
is the density associated to a cumulative distribution function
D_{\tau}
, such that
D_{\tau}(0)=0
and
D_{\tau}(1)=1
.
As shown in Section
2
, this minimization problem can also be looked upon as
\argmin_{c\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{E}[\ell_{\delta}(X_{{\scriptsize D}_{\tau}},c)]
, where
X_{{\scriptsize D}_{\tau}}
denotes the random variable which has as cumulative distribution function the transformed function
D_{\tau}\circ F_{X}
, and the expectation is taken with respect to this distributional distorted (shortly distorted) random variable
X_{{\scriptsize D}_{\tau}}
.
Moreover, with
\ell_{\delta}(x,c)=(x-c)^{2}
, i.e. square loss, this leads to

\argmin_{c\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{E}[\ell_{\delta}(X_{{\scriptsize D}_{\tau}},c)]=\mathbb{E}\left[X_{{\scriptsize D}_{\tau}}\right]
and subsequently
\min_{c\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{E}[\ell_{\delta}(X_{{\scriptsize D}_{\tau}},c)]=\mbox{Var}(X_{{\scriptsize D}_{\tau}})
.

In the special case of square loss, the functional thus relates to the mean of the transformed random variable
X_{{\scriptsize D}_{\tau}}
. This is linked to the probability distortions studied by
Liu et al. (2021)
, as a particular setting of distributional transforms. The paper investigates probabilistic properties of these distributional transforms, and discusses (new) risk measures generated from such distributional transforms. In Section
5
we rely on some results of
Liu et al. (2021)
to study risk measures of the distorted random variable
X_{{\scriptsize D}_{\tau}}
.
Distortion risk measures have found widespread application in the literature, for example, in finance, economics, insurance; see
Wang (1996)
,
Wang et al. (1997)
,
Föllmer and Schied (2002)
, and
Cherny and Madan (2017)
,
among others.
Our general functional involves a loss function
\ell_{\delta}(x,c)
. There are numerous examples of loss functions appearing in the literature. Tables
1
and
2
, as well as Section
2.4
review some of these.

As our main contribution we introduce and study a general class of functionals, which cover and generalize these in (
1
), (
2
), (
3
), and (
5
), as special cases. This generalization allows an interesting link to distortion risks as well as to risks of non-distortion type. We call this general class of functionals
generalized extremiles
. The main contribution of this paper consists of developing estimators for generalized extremiles, and establish consistency and asymptotic normality of these estimators, with explicit expression for the asymptotic variance. This general theory can then be applied to a variety of population target quantities. The asymptotic normality result allows to construct asymptotic confidence intervals of estimators in the general framework.

Specific optimization problems capture a number of situations studied in actuarial science, financial mathematics or statistics which are a special case of our framework.
For example, our framework applies if a functional
\rho
can be defined as the expected loss minimizer

\displaystyle\rho(Y)=\argmin_{c\in\mathbb{R}}\,\mathbb{E}[\ell(Y,c)]

(6)

for a suitable loss function
\ell\colon\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
(where we surpressed the index
\delta
), and a random variable
Y
.
In this case
\rho(X_{{\scriptsize D}_{\tau}})
can be the mean, quantile or expectile of the distorted random variable
X_{{\scriptsize D}_{\tau}}
.
Equally, one could for example consider functionals defined as the expected loss minimum

\displaystyle\rho(Y)=\min_{c\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{E}[\ell(Y,c)],

(7)

in which case
\rho(X_{{\scriptsize D}_{\tau}})
can for example be the variance or expected shortfall of the distorted random variable
X_{{\scriptsize D}_{\tau}}
. See
Embrechts et al. (2021)
for details and a discussion
connecting risk measures of the form in (
6
) and (
7
).

While we present some general theory for risk functionals applied to distorted random variables
X_{{\scriptsize D}_{\tau}}
in Section
5
, our study mainly focusses on the specific case of expected loss minimizers as in (
6
), due to their ubiquitous nature in the literature.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we introduce the generalized extremile functionals, establish some basic properties, and explain the link with distortion risks. Section
3
deals with estimation of the broad class of generalized extremiles, and proves consistency and asymptotic normality results. These are then applied to various settings, revealing the breath of the studied functionals. Numerical studies and real data analysis are given in Sections
4
and
6
. The proofs of the main theoretical results are provided in the Appendices. Some further discussions are given in a final section.
Some additional information and explanations are in the Supplementary Material.

2
Generalized extremiles and their properties

In this section we introduce the concept of generalized extremile, study its basic probabilistic properties, highlight some issues toward solving the associated optimization problem, and review and discuss some loss functions. In a final subsection we discuss how generalized extremiles link up to distortion risk measures. In Section
3
we then turn to statistical estimation of generalized extremiles.

2.1
Generalized extremiles and distorted random variables

The class of functionals that we study is based on a pair of key ingredients,
denoted by
(D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\ell_{\delta})
, consisting of a distribution function
D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
and a loss function
\ell_{\delta}
. Their formal definitions are provided below.

Definition 2.1
.

Consider a set
\Delta\subset\mathbb{R}
. For every
\delta\in\Delta
we denote by
\ell_{\delta}
the loss function

\ell_{\delta}(x,c):\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}:(x,c)\mapsto\ell_{\delta}(x,c).

Where appropriate we use
\ell_{\delta}^{\prime}(x,c):=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}c}\ell_{\delta}(x,c)
to denote the partial derivative of
\ell_{\delta}
with respect to
c
.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
\mathrm{T}
be some subset of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
where
k
is some positive integer. For each
\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\mathrm{T}
denote by
D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
an absolutely continuous cumulative distribution function supported on
[0,1]
, with density
d_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
. We denote by
\mathbb{D}
for the set of all such
D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
.

Definition 2.3

(Generalized extremile)
.

Consider a cumulative distribution function
D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\in\mathbb{D}
, and a loss function
\ell_{\delta}
. For a random variable
X\sim F_{X}
we define the generalized extremile of index
(\boldsymbol{\tau},\delta)
, denoted
e_{\boldsymbol{\tau},\delta}(X;D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\ell_{\delta})
, as

e_{\boldsymbol{\tau},\delta}(X;D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\ell_{\delta})\in\argmin_{c\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{E}[d_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(F_{X}(X))(\ell_{\delta}(X,c)-\ell_{\delta}(X,0))].

(8)

Taking
D_{\tau}
, with
\boldsymbol{\tau}=\tau\in(0,1)
, equal to

K_{\tau}(u)=\begin{cases}1-(1-u)^{s(\tau)}&\text{ if }0<\tau<\frac{1}{2}\\
u^{r(\tau)}&\text{ if }\frac{1}{2}\leq\tau<1,\end{cases}

(9)

with
r(\tau)=s(1-\tau)=\ln(1/2)/\ln(\tau)
, in minimization problem (
8
), leads on the one hand to the concept of
\tau
th quantiles when taking as loss function
\ell_{\delta}(x,c)=|x-c|
(see (
3
)), and on the other hand to the concept of
\tau
th extremile with squared loss

\ell_{\delta}(x,c)=(x-c)^{2}
(see (
5
)). We refer to the class of functionals in Definition
2.3
as
generalized extremiles
, since it steps away from the specific choice of
the pair
(D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\ell_{\delta})
in quantiles and extremiles, but having its inspiration strongly influenced by these two concepts.

Note that the choice of the distribution function
D_{\tau}
in (
9
) already reveals why we allow this distribution function to depend on a parameter (vector)
\boldsymbol{\tau}
. From the viewpoint on quantiles expressed in (
1
) it is also clear why we allow the loss function to depend on a parameter
\delta
. Figure
1
depicts the function
K_{\tau}
and its derivative
d_{\tau}
for various values of
\tau
.

When defining generalized extremiles in Definition
2.3
we require the distribution function
D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
to be absolutely continuous admitting a density function
d_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
. The latter can be relaxed, as we illustrate next.

Recall that
X_{{\scriptsize D}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}
denotes the random variable with cumulative distribution function
D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\circ F_{X}
, i.e.,
X_{{\scriptsize D}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}\sim D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\circ F_{X}
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Consider
X_{{\scriptsize D}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}\sim D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\circ F_{X}
. We then have

\argmin_{c\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{E}[d_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(F_{X}(X))(\ell_{\delta}(X,c)-\ell_{\delta}(X,0))]=\argmin_{c\in\mathbb{R}}\ \mathbb{E}[\ell_{\delta}(X_{{\scriptsize D}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}},c)-\ell_{\delta}(X_{{\scriptsize D}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}},0)],

where the expectation on the right-hand side is taken with respect to the random variable
X_{{\scriptsize D}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}
.

Proof.

The proof is straightforward by noting that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[d_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(F_{X}(X))(\ell_{\delta}(X,c)-\ell_{\delta}(X,0)]

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}d_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(F_{X}(x))(\ell_{\delta}(x,c)-\ell_{\delta}(x,0))\mathrm{d}F_{X}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\ell_{\delta}(x,c)-\ell_{\delta}(x,0))\mathrm{d}D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(F_{X}(x))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\ell_{\delta}(X_{{\scriptsize D}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}},c)-\ell_{\delta}(X_{{\scriptsize D}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}},0)].

∎

An alternative would be to define generalized extremiles as stipulated in
Proposition
2.1
, via

\argmin_{c\in\mathbb{R}}\ \mathbb{E}[\ell_{\delta}(X_{{\scriptsize D}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}},c)-\ell_{\delta}(X_{{\scriptsize D}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}},0)],

as such not requiring the existing of the density
d_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
.
Although this would be a slightly broader definition, it would make it more tedious to establish (asymptotic) properties when dealing with statistical inference for the studied functionals. The latter is the main goal of the paper.

2.2
Generalized extremiles: properties

Before stating some probabilistic properties of generalized extremiles, we first define some properties of loss functions, as well as the notion of a dual of a cumulative distribution function
D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
.

Definition 2.4
.

Consider a loss function
\ell_{\delta}:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
. It is called

1.

sign symmetric if
\ell_{\delta}(-x,c)=\ell_{\delta}(x,-c)\text{ for all }x,c\in\mathbb{R}
;

2.

shift invariant if
\ell_{\delta}(x+b,c+b)=\ell_{\delta}(x,c)
for all
x,c,b\in\mathbb{R}
;

3.

positive homogeneous of degree
k>0
if

\ell_{\delta}(ax,ac)=a^{k}\ell_{\delta}(x,c)
for all
a>0
and
x,c\in\mathbb{R}
.

In Tables
1
and
2
we indicate for each of the listed loss functions whether they possess the property of sign symmetry, shift invariance and positive homogeneity.

For a given cumulative distribution function
D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
in the set
\mathbb{D}
it is of interest to consider its dual.

Definition 2.5
.

Given
D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\in\mathbb{D}
we define its dual
\widetilde{D}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
as
\widetilde{D}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(t)=1-D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(1-t)
.

It is clear that the dual function is again part of the set
\mathbb{D}
. Furthermore, the dual of
\widetilde{D}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
is equal to
D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
.

The proofs of all propositions in this section can be found in
Appendix
A
.
Proposition
2.2
provides sufficient conditions under which the expectation in Definition
2.3
is finite. The proof is straightforward and omitted.

Proposition 2.2
.

If the cumulative distribution function
D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
is differentiable with bounded density
d_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
and
\mathbb{E}[\lvert\ell_{\delta}(X,c)-\ell_{\delta}(X,0))\rvert]<\infty
for all
c\in\mathbb{R}
then

\lvert\mathbb{E}[d_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(F_{X}(X))(\ell_{\delta}(X,c)-\ell_{\delta}(X,0))]\rvert<\infty

for all
c\in\mathbb{R}
.

Remark 2.1
.

The condition on the loss function (and
X
) in Proposition
2.2
is very mild, and fulfilled for several commonly used loss functions. In case of the absolute value loss
\ell(x,c)=\lvert x-c\rvert
the reverse triangle inequality yields
\lvert\lvert x-c\rvert-\lvert x\lvert\lvert\leq\lvert c\rvert
and hence
\mathbb{E}[\lvert\ell(X,c)-\ell(X,0)\rvert]<\infty
for all random variables
X
. In case of the square loss
\ell(x,c)=(x-c)^{2}
we have
\lvert(x-c)^{2}-x^{2}\rvert=\lvert c^{2}-2cx\rvert\leq c^{2}+2\lvert c\rvert\cdot\lvert x\rvert
and hence
\mathbb{E}[\lvert\ell(X,c)-\ell(X,0)\rvert]<\infty
for all random variables
X
with
\mathbb{E}[\lvert X\rvert]<\infty
.

From Remark
2.1
it also becomes clear why the term
\ell_{\delta}(X,0)
is included in (
8
), whereas it does not depend on the argument
c
. Due to the inclusion of that term we have, for example, that quantiles obtained by taking
D_{\tau}=K_{\tau}
and
\ell_{\delta}(x,c)=|x-c|
, exist for all random variables. Similarly, expectiles of
X
only require the finiteness of the first absolute moment of
X
.

Propositions
2.3
,
2.4
and
2.5
establish the behaviour of generalized extremiles when the underlying random variable is transformated either by taking the negative of it (Proposition
2.3
), or by an affine transformation (Proposition
2.4
), or by applying a strictly monotone function to it (Proposition
2.5
). From these propositions we can conclude that generalized extremiles have similar properties as quantiles and expectiles, among others.

Proposition 2.3

(Sign symmetry)
.

Denote by
\ell_{\delta}
a sign symmetric loss function. If the distribution function
F_{X}
of
X
is continuous then we have

e_{\boldsymbol{\tau},\delta}\left(X;D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\ell_{\delta}\right)=-e_{\boldsymbol{\tau},\delta}(-X;\widetilde{D}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\ell_{\delta})\qquad\text{ and hence also }\qquad e_{\boldsymbol{\tau},\delta}(X;\widetilde{D}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\ell_{\delta})=-e_{\boldsymbol{\tau},\delta}(-X;D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\ell_{\delta}).

Proposition 2.4
.

Consider scalars
a>0
and
b\in\mathbb{R}
, and a random variable
X\sim F_{X}
such that
\lvert e_{\boldsymbol{\tau},\delta}(X;D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\ell_{\delta})\rvert<\infty
. If the loss function
\ell_{\delta}
is shift invariant, positive homogeneous of degree
k>0
and if

\lvert\mathbb{E}[d_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(F_{X}(X))(\ell_{\delta}(aX,0)-\ell_{\delta}(aX+b,0))]\rvert<\infty

(10)

then

e_{\boldsymbol{\tau},\delta}(aX+b;D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\ell_{\delta})=ae_{\boldsymbol{\tau},\delta}(X;D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\ell_{\delta})+b.

If in addition the loss function
\ell_{\delta}
is sign symmetric,
F_{X}
is continuous and (
10
) holds for
a<0
and
b\in\mathbb{R}
then

e_{\boldsymbol{\tau},\delta}(aX+b;D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\ell_{\delta})=ae_{\boldsymbol{\tau},\delta}(X;\widetilde{D}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\ell_{\delta})+b\qquad\text{ and hence also }\qquad e_{\boldsymbol{\tau},\delta}(aX+b;\widetilde{D}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\ell_{\delta})=ae_{\boldsymbol{\tau},\delta}(X;D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\ell_{\delta})+b.

Proposition 2.5

(Monotone transformations)
.

Suppose
\alpha:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a strictly increasing function and hence measurable function, then

e_{\boldsymbol{\tau},\delta}(\alpha(X);D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\ell_{\delta})=e_{\boldsymbol{\tau},\delta}(X;D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\widetilde{\ell}_{\delta}),

where
\widetilde{\ell}_{\delta}(x,c)=\ell_{\delta}(\alpha(x),c)
.
If
\alpha:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is strictly decreasing and
X
is a random variable with continuous
F_{X}
then

e_{\boldsymbol{\tau},\delta}(\alpha(X);D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\ell_{\delta})=e_{\boldsymbol{\tau},\delta}(X;\widetilde{D}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\widetilde{\ell}_{\delta}).

When a random variable
X
is symmetric and has finite expectation it is possible to express this mean in terms of differences or sums of generalized extremiles, as stated in Proposition
2.6
.

Proposition 2.6

(Mean symmetry)
.

Denote by
\ell_{\delta}
a shift invariant loss function. If
\mathbb{E}[X]
is finite and
X
is symmetric, i.e.,
X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}-X
, then

\frac{1}{2}(e_{\boldsymbol{\tau},\delta}(X;D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\ell_{\delta})-e_{\boldsymbol{\tau},\delta}(-X;D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\ell_{\delta}))=\mathbb{E}[X]

If in addition
\ell_{\delta}
is a sign symmetric loss function and the distribution function
F_{X}
of
X
is continuous then

\frac{1}{2}\left(e_{\boldsymbol{\tau},\delta}(X;D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\ell_{\delta})+e_{\boldsymbol{\tau},\delta}(X;\widetilde{D}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\ell_{\delta})\right)=\mathbb{E}[X].

For two random variables
X
and
Y
, it is interesting to know how the generalized extremile of their sum, i.e.,
e_{\boldsymbol{\tau},\delta}(X+Y;D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\ell_{\delta})
, relates to the sum of the generalized extremiles of each of the random variables. Proposition
2.7
below provides some answer to this question, establishing the property of comonotonic additivity for the absolute value and square loss functions.

Proposition 2.7

(Comonotonic additivity)
.

Denote by
X\sim F_{X}
and
Y\sim F_{Y}
two random variables that have a comonotonic dependence structure, i.e.,
F_{X,Y}(x,y)=\min\{F_{X}(x),F_{Y}(y)\}
. If
\ell_{\delta}
is either the absolute value loss or the square loss we have (assuming the considered generalized extremiles are finite) that

e_{\boldsymbol{\tau},\delta}(X+Y;D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\ell_{\delta})=e_{\boldsymbol{\tau},\delta}(X;D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\ell_{\delta})+e_{\boldsymbol{\tau},\delta}(Y;D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\ell_{\delta}).

A final result states the equivalence of an injective transformation of a generalized extremile and a generalized extremile of the same random variable with a transformed loss function.
It is similar to results in
(
Gneiting, 2011
, see Theorem 4)
and in
(
Osband, 1985
, see p. 9)
known as revelation principle.

Proposition 2.8

(Revelation principle)
.

Let
\beta:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be an injective function. Define
\widetilde{\ell}_{\delta}(x,c)=\ell_{\delta}(x,\beta^{-1}(c))
, then

\beta(e_{\boldsymbol{\tau},\delta}(X;D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\ell_{\delta}))=e_{\boldsymbol{\tau},\delta}(X;D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\widetilde{\ell}_{\delta}).

The result in Proposition
2.8
has some interesting consequences. Consider the square loss function
\ell_{\delta}(x,c)=(x-c)^{2}
. Then we know that

e_{\boldsymbol{\tau},\delta}(X;D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},(x-c)^{2})=\mathbb{E}[X_{{\scriptsize D}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}].

Applying Proposition
2.8
with injective function
\beta:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}:x\mapsto-x
, gives that

e_{\boldsymbol{\tau},\delta}(X;D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},(x+c)^{2})=-\mathbb{E}[X_{{\scriptsize D}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}].

2.3
Towards solving the minimization problem

When it comes to finding a generalized extremile, defined in (
8
), Proposition
2.1
is a good starting point, showing that we need to find a minimizer of

\mathbb{E}[\ell_{\delta}(X_{{\scriptsize D}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}},c)-\ell_{\delta}(X_{{\scriptsize D}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}},0)],
with respect to
c\in\mathbb{R}
.
Proposition
2.9
below provides a way to find such a minimizer, and states sufficient conditions on the pair
(D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\ell_{\delta})
.

Proposition 2.9
.

Let
\ell_{\delta}
be a function such that
c\mapsto\ell_{\delta}(x,c)
is (strictly) convex for every
x
and
c
. Additionally, assume that both
D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
and
F_{X}
are differentiable functions. Under these conditions, the function
c\mapsto\mathbb{E}\left[\ell_{\delta}\left(X_{D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}},c\right)-\ell_{\delta}\left(X_{D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}},0\right)\right]
is (strictly) convex, and the generalized extremile is equal to any value
c
for which

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}c}\left(\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\ell_{\delta}(x,c)\,\mathrm{d}D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\left(F_{X}(x)\right)\right)=0,

(11)

provided the derivative exists. When the mapping
c\mapsto\ell_{\delta}(x,c)
is strictly convex, there exists at most one
c
value fulfilling (
11
). The existence of such a
c
value is ensured when
c\mapsto\ell_{\delta}(x,c)
is convex and
c\mapsto\mathbb{E}\left[\ell_{\delta}\left(X_{D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}},c\right)-\ell_{\delta}\left(X_{D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}},0\right)\right]
is coercive.

Proof.

To solve the minimization problem we want to find the value
c
that minimizes

I(c)=\mathbb{E}\left[\ell_{\delta}\left(X_{D_{\tau}},c\right)\right]=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\ell_{\delta}(x,c)d_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\left(F_{X}(x)\right)f_{X}(x)\mathrm{d}x.

By
Boyd and Vandenberghe (2004)
, p. 79, we know that
I(c)
is convex in
c
if
c\mapsto\ell_{\delta}(x,c)
is convex for every
x
.
It is clear that strict convexity of
c\mapsto\ell_{\delta}(x,c)
implies that
I(c)
is also strict convex and hence the minimum is unique (see Theorem 3.4.4 on p. 114 in
Niculescu and Persson (2006)
).

The existence of a value
c
follows by Theorem 4.4 on p. 638 in
Herrmann et al. (2018)
.
∎

In Proposition
2.9
uniqueness of
c
solving (
11
) is guaranteed when
c\mapsto\mathbb{E}\left[\ell_{\delta}\left(X_{D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}},c\right)-\ell_{\delta}\left(X_{D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}},0\right)\right]
is a coercive function. Some sufficient conditions for this to hold are discussed in the following remark.

Remark 2.2
.

Consider a random variable
Y
(with associated probability measure
\mathbb{P}_{Y}
) such that
E_{0}=\mathbb{E}[\ell_{\delta}(Y,0)]
is finite.
Additional to convexity, assume that
c\mapsto\ell_{\delta}(y,c)
is (i) coercive for
\mathbb{P}_{Y}
almost all
y\in\mathbb{R}
, i.e.,

\lim_{c_{n}\to\pm\infty}\ell_{\delta}(y,c_{n})=\infty
, and (ii) bounded from below, i.e.,
\exists\,C>0
, such that
\ell_{\delta}(y,c)\geq C

\mathbb{P}_{Y}
-a.s.
Then also
c\mapsto\mathbb{E}[\ell_{\delta}(Y,c)-\ell_{\delta}(Y,0)]
is coercive.
To see this consider a sequence
(c_{n})
such that
\lvert c_{n}\rvert\to\infty
and define
Y_{n}:=\ell_{\delta}(Y,c_{n})-C
to have
Y_{n}\geq 0

\mathbb{P}_{Y}
-a.s. and
Y_{n}\to\infty
due to the coercivity of
\ell_{\delta}
.
A slight modification of the monotone convergence theorem, see
Feinstein (2007)
, then implies
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[\ell_{\delta}(Y,c_{n})-\ell_{\delta}(Y,0)]=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[\ell_{\delta}(Y,c_{n})-C]+C-E_{0}=\mathbb{E}[\lim_{n\to\infty}\ell_{\delta}(Y,c_{n})-C]+C-E_{0}=\infty
, showing that
c\mapsto\mathbb{E}[\ell_{\delta}(Y,c)-\ell_{\delta}(Y,0)]
is coercive.
The result is now applicable in the context of Proposition
2.9
if the necessary conditions apply for the choice of
Y=X_{D_{\tau}}
.

As is clear from the above result, convexity of the function
c\mapsto\ell_{\delta}(x,c)
will be an important requirement when it comes to calculating generalized extremiles. In addition it also makes it transparant that interchanging integral and differentiation operators will be helpful. For any random variable
X
(not necessarily absolutely continuous) and any distribution function
D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
(not necessarily admitting a density) the next theorem (stating a type of Leibniz rule) is helpful.

Theorem 2.1

(see
Klenke (2013)
, Theorem 6.28, p. 142)
.

Denote
\mu
the measure induced by
D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\circ F_{X}
. Suppose that a loss function
\ell_{\delta}(x,c)
satisfies the following properties.

•

For every
c
, the map
x\mapsto\ell_{\delta}(x,c)
is integrable (with respect to the measure
\mu
);

•

For
\mu
-almost all
x
, the map
c\mapsto\ell_{\delta}(x,c)
is differentiable with derivative
\ell_{\delta}^{\prime}(x,c)
;

•

There is an integrable function
h:\mathbb{R}\to(0,\infty)
(with respect to the measure
\mu
) such that
\lvert\ell_{\delta}^{\prime}(x,c)\rvert\leq h(x)
for all
c
and
\mu
-almost all
x
.

Then for all
c\in\mathbb{R}
we have

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}c}\Big(\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\ell_{\delta}(x,c)\mathrm{d}D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(F_{X}(x))\Big)=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\ell_{\delta}^{\prime}(x,c)\mathrm{d}D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(F_{X}(x)).

For absolutely continuous
X
with density function
f_{X}
, and
D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
admitting a density
d_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
, the adaptation of this theorem is as follows.

Theorem 2.2
.

Consider an absolutely continuous random variable
X
, and a cumulative distribution function
D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\in\mathbb{D}
with density
d_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
. Suppose that a loss function
\ell_{\delta}(x,c)
satisfies the following properties.

•

For every
c
, the map
x\mapsto\ell_{\delta}(x,c)d_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(F_{X}(x))f_{X}(x)
is integrable;

•

For almost all
x
, the map
c\mapsto\ell_{\delta}(x,c)
is differentiable with derivative
\ell_{\delta}^{\prime}(x,c)
;

•

There is an integrable function
h:\mathbb{R}\to(0,\infty)
(with respect to the measure
\mu
) such that

\lvert\ell_{\delta}^{\prime}(x,c)d_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(F_{X}(x))f_{X}(x)\rvert\leq h(x)
for all
c
and almost all
x
.

Then for all
c\in\mathbb{R}
we have

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}c}\Big(\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\ell_{\delta}(x,c)d_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(F_{X}(x))f_{X}(x)\mathrm{d}x\Big)=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\ell_{\delta}^{\prime}(x,c)d_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(F_{X}(x))f_{X}(x)\mathrm{d}x.

2.4
Examples

Complementary to the loss functions listed in Tables
1
and
2

we review here some loss functions that have been proposed in recent literature, illustrating as such the broad framework we consider.
Some more loss functions are given in Section S1 in the Supplementary Material
(non exhaustive list).

2.4.1
Nonnegative valued loss functions

Adaptive quantile loss function for censoring

In the context of quantile estimation under right random censoring,

De Backer et al. (2019)
established an approach based on considering a loss function that adapts to the cumulative distribution function
F_{C}
of the censoring variable
C
:

\ell_{\delta}(x,c)=\left|\delta-1_{\{x\leq c\}}\right||x-c|-(1-\delta)\displaystyle\int_{0}^{c}F_{C}(s)\mathrm{d}s.

(12)

Note that the mapping
c\mapsto\ell_{\delta}(x,c)
is in general not convex, nor is it monotone. In case of no censoring, i.e. when
P\{C<\infty\}=0
, the adapted loss function reduces to the usual quantile loss function.

M-quantile loss

For a function
\psi:\mathbb{R}\to[0,+\infty)
, consider the loss function

\ell_{\delta}(x,c)=\delta\psi(x-c)1_{\{x>c\}}+(1-\delta)\psi(x-c)1_{\{x\leq c\}}.

This is the M-quantile loss function. Taking
\psi(u)=|u|^{p}
leads to the power
p
absolute loss function, also called
L_{p}-
quantiles as defined in
Chen (1996)
. See also
Jiang et al. (2021)
. Obviously, the qualitative properties of
\ell_{\delta}(x,c)
depend on the qualitative properties of the function
\psi
.

Generalized quantile loss

An extension of the M-quantiles are obtained by involving two different functions
\psi_{1}
and
\psi_{2}
. Namely consider
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}:[0,+\infty)\to[0,+\infty)
, convex and strictly increasing functions satisfying

\psi_{j}(0)=0\text{ and }\psi_{j}(1)=1\text{ for }j=1,2.

The generalized quantile loss function is then

\ell_{\delta}(x,c)=\delta\psi_{1}(x-c)1_{\{x>c\}}+(1-\delta)\psi_{2}(x-c)1_{\{x\leq c\}},

leading to the
\delta
th generalized quantile of a random variable.
See
Bellini et al. (2014)
.
The qualitative properties of the generalized quantile loss depend on these of the functions
\psi_{1}
and
\psi_{2}
.

In
Mao and Cai (2018)
, the authors further generalized this class by using rank-dependent expected utility (RDEU) theory. This corresponding class of generalized quantiles based on RDEU theory is given by

\argmin_{c\in\mathbb{R}}\Big\{\delta\int_{c}^{+\infty}\phi_{1}(x-c)dh_{1}(F_{X}(x))+(1-\delta)\int_{-\infty}^{c}\phi_{2}(c-x)dh_{2}(F_{X}(x))\Big\}.

(13)

The functions
h_{1},h_{2}
are two distortion functions on
[0,1]
, which means they are right-continuous and increasing with
h_{j}(0)=0
and
h_{j}(1)=1
for
j=1,2
, with no jumps at
0
and
1
. Furthermore,
\phi_{1}
and
\phi_{2}
are nondegenerate increasing convex functions on
[0,\infty)
. When
h_{1}=h_{2}:=D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
we can describe (part of) this class using the generalized extremile framework. Indeed, to do so, we have to take

D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}=h_{1}=h_{2}\text{ and }\ell_{\delta}(x,c)=\delta\phi_{1}((x-c)_{+})+(1-\delta)\phi_{2}((c-x)_{-}).

Here we used the notations:
x_{+}=\max\{x,0\}
and
x_{-}=-\min\{x,0\}
.

Generalized shortfall loss

In
Mao and Cai (2018)
and
Mao et al. (2023)
, the authors study generalized shortfall risk measures. These are obtained by replacing
\phi_{1},\phi_{2}
with their derivatives
\phi_{1}^{\prime},\phi_{2}^{\prime}
in (
13
). Just as before, part of these generalized shortfall risk measures can be described in our generalized extremile framework when
h_{1}=h_{2}:=D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
.

Loss function for trimmed mean

The Hampel family of functions (see
Hampel et al. (1986)
) is defined as

\ell(x,c)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}(x-c)^{2}&\text{ if }\lvert x-c\rvert<a\\
a(x-c-a/2)&\text{ if }a\leq\lvert x-c\rvert<b\\
\displaystyle\frac{a(x-c-d)^{2}}{2(b-d)}+a\frac{(b+d-a)}{2}&\text{ if }b\leq\lvert x-c\rvert<d\\
\frac{1}{2}a(b+d-a)&\text{ if }\lvert x-c\rvert\geq d,\\
\end{cases}

where the function
\ell
depends on parameters
a,b
and
d
, with
0<a<b<d
. This is a non-convex function.

A limiting case of this family is obtained by letting
a,b
and
d
taking the limiting value
\delta>0
, leading to the loss function (see p. 79 in
Huber (1964)
)

\ell_{\delta}(x,c)=\begin{cases}(x-c)^{2}&\text{ if }\lvert x-c\rvert<\delta\\
\delta^{2}&\text{ if }\lvert x-c\rvert\geq\delta,\end{cases}

appearing in the context of trimmed means.
Note that the function
c\mapsto\ell_{\delta}(x,c)

is quadratic in
c
around
x
, but attributes always the same constant value (
\delta^{2}
) when
c
is such that
\lvert x-c\rvert\geq\delta
. Consequently the function
c\mapsto\ell_{\delta}(x,c)
is not convex.

2.4.2
Real-valued loss functions

Various functionals involving moments

Let
I\subset\mathbb{R}
be an interval. Consider
r:I\to\mathbb{R}
and
s:I\to(0,+\infty)
measurable functions. Let
\phi
be a strictly convex function with subgradient
\phi^{\prime}
. Let
X_{{\scriptsize D}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}
be a random variable supported on
I
such that
\mathbb{E}[r(X_{{\scriptsize D}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}})]
,
\mathbb{E}[s(X_{{\scriptsize D}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}})]
,
\mathbb{E}[r(X_{{\scriptsize D}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}})\phi^{\prime}(X_{{\scriptsize D}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}})]
,
\mathbb{E}[s(X_{{\scriptsize D}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}})\phi(X_{{\scriptsize D}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}})]
and
\mathbb{E}[X_{{\scriptsize D}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}s(X_{{\scriptsize D}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}})\phi^{\prime}(X_{{\scriptsize D}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}})]
exist and are finite.
Consider the loss function

\ell(x,c)=s(x)(\phi(x)-\phi(c))-\phi^{\prime}(c)(r(x)-cs(x))+\phi^{\prime}(x)(r(x)-xs(x)).

The corresponding generalized extremile equals

\frac{\mathbb{E}[r(X_{{\scriptsize D}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}})]}{\mathbb{E}[s(X_{{\scriptsize D}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}})]}.

We refer to
Gneiting
(see e.g. Theorem 8 on p. 754 in
Gneiting (2011)
) for an in-depth discussion.

Taking
r(x)=x^{2}
,
s(x)=x
and
\phi(x)=x^{2}
leads to the special case of

\frac{\mathbb{E}[\left(X_{{\scriptsize D}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}\right)^{2}]}{\mathbb{E}[X_{{\scriptsize D}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}]}=\frac{\text{var}(X_{{\scriptsize D}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}})}{\mathbb{E}[X_{{\scriptsize D}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}]}+\mathbb{E}[X_{{\scriptsize D}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}].

In this special case the loss function is
\ell(x,c)=x(c-x)^{2}
(see also p. 83 in
Heilmann (1989)
). This is a convex function in
c
for
x>0
, and the derivative of
c\mapsto\ell(x,c)
exists for every
x
and is given by

\ell_{\delta}^{\prime}(x,c)=-2x(x-c)
.

Consider
w:[0,+\infty)\to[0,+\infty)
; and take
r(x)=xw(x)
and
s(x)=w(x)
. The interpretation of the resulting generalized extremile

\frac{\mathbb{E}[X_{{\scriptsize D}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}w(X_{{\scriptsize D}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}})]}{\mathbb{E}[w(X_{{\scriptsize D}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}})]},

for different weighting functions
w
is discussed in
Furman and Zitikis (2009)
.

2.5
Square loss and distortion risk measures

A special subclass of functionals of generalized extremiles is obtained when focussing on the square loss
\ell_{\delta}(x,c)=(x-c)^{2}
. Indeed, as already pointed out in Section
1
, we know that
e_{\boldsymbol{\tau},\delta}(X;D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\ell_{\delta})=\mathbb{E}[X_{{\scriptsize D}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}]
where
X_{{\scriptsize D}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}
is distributed with cumulative distribution function
D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\circ F_{X}
.
This implies that

e_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(X;D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}):=e_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(X,D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},(x-c)^{2})=\int_{-\infty}^{0}[g(1-F_{X}(y))-1]\mathrm{d}y+\int_{0}^{+\infty}g(1-F_{X}(y))\mathrm{d}y,

(14)

where
g(y)=1-D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(1-y)=\widetilde{D}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(y)
.
An expression as in (
14
), with
g:[0,1]\to[0,1]
, a non-decreasing function and
g(0)=0
and
g(1)=1
, is termed a distortion risk of
X
. For this distortion risk to be a coherent risk measure (see for example
Wang (1996)
and
Artzner et al. (1999)
) one needs to require in addition that
g
is a concave function. See, for example,
Wang et al. (1997)
and

CaiWangMao2017
.
Note that generalized extremiles, in case of square loss function, can be seen as a distortion risk measure with distortion function
1-D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(1-y)
. This implies that

D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(y)=1-g(1-y).

Note that concavity of
g
is equivalent with convexity of
D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
. Therefore not every
coherent distortion risk measure is necessarily part of our class. If the function
g
is differentiable then

d_{\tau}(u)=g^{\prime}(1-u)
. In summary, the class of generalized extremiles contains part of the distortion risk measures, but it also contains non-distortion risk measures, for example, expectiles.
Table
3
lists several distortion risk measures that can be seen as part of our general class of functionals.

3
Estimation

Based on an i.i.d. sample
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
from
X
, with unknown distribution function
F_{X}
, the aim is to estimate the generalized extremile

e_{\boldsymbol{\tau},\delta}(X;D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\ell_{\delta})
defined in Definition
2.3
, and this for a general pair
(D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\ell_{\delta})
.
We firstly focus on the setting of a square loss function, but with general
D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
; and secondly treat the setting of a general pair
(D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\ell_{\delta})
. The reason is that in the former setting estimation of the generalized extremile is simpler, and subsequently establishing (asymptotic) properties for it is somewhat less involved.

3.1
Square loss and general distribution function
D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}

As discussed in Section
2.5
in case of square loss the generalized extremile reduces to

e_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(X;D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}})=\mathbb{E}[X_{{{\scriptsize D}}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}]=\int_{0}^{1}F_{X}^{-1}(u)\mathrm{d}D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(u).

(15)

An estimator of
e_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(X;D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}})
(and hence of the corresponding distortion risk) can be easily derived from (
15
).
Denote by
X_{k:n}
the
k
th order statistic in the random sample
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
of size
n
. Let
F_{n}(x)=(n+1)^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}1_{\{X_{i}\leq x\}}
be the empirical cumulative distribution function (with factor
1/(n+1)
instead of
1/n
), and denote by

F_{n}^{-1}(p)
the corresponding empirical quantile function, i.e. for
p\in(0,1)
,
F_{n}^{-1}(p)=X_{k:n}
, where
k
is chosen such that
(k-1)/(n+1)<p\leq k/(n+1)
. A plug-in type of estimator for
e_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(X;D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}})
is then

\widehat{e}_{\boldsymbol{\tau},n}^{\mbox{ {\scriptsize L}}}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(k/(n+1))-D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}((k-1)/(n+1))\right)X_{k:n}:=\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{k=1}^{n}w_{k,n}X_{k:n},

where it is thus readily seen that
\widehat{e}_{\boldsymbol{\tau},n}^{\mbox{ {\scriptsize L}}}
is proportional (with factor
n/(n+1)
) to an
\mathrm{L}
-statistic with weights
w_{k,n}=(n+1)\left(D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(k/(n+1))-D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}((k-1)/(n+1))\right)
.

A second estimator, denoted
\widehat{e}_{\boldsymbol{\tau},n}^{\mbox{ {\scriptsize LM}}}
, relies on the fact that in case of square loss we have

e_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(X;D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}})=\mathbb{E}[Xd_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(F_{X}(X))],

leading to the estimator

\widehat{e}^{\mbox{ {\scriptsize LM}}}_{\boldsymbol{\tau},n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\left(\frac{i}{n+1}\right)X_{i:n}.

A third estimator is obtained by
solving the empirical minimization problem. That is by solving

\argmin_{c\in\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\left(\frac{i}{n+1}\right)\Big((X_{i:n}-c)^{2}-X_{i:n}^{2}\Big)=\argmin_{c\in\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\left(\frac{i}{n+1}\right)\Big(-2cX_{i:n}+c^{2}\Big).

Differentiating the objective function with respect to
c
and equating this to zero gives

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\left(\frac{i}{n+1}\right)(-2X_{i:n}+2c)=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad c=\displaystyle\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\left(\frac{i}{n+1}\right)X_{i:n}}{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\left(\frac{i}{n+1}\right)}.

We thus obtain a new M-type estimator

\widehat{e}^{\mbox{ {\scriptsize M}}}_{\boldsymbol{\tau},n}=\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\left(\frac{i}{n+1}\right)X_{i:n}}{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\left(\displaystyle\frac{i}{n+1}\right)}=\frac{\widehat{e}^{\mbox{ {\scriptsize LM}}}_{\boldsymbol{\tau},n}}{\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\left(\frac{i}{n+1}\right)}.

Note that, since we work with the empirical cumulative distribution function rescaled with the factor
1/(n+1)
, which takes values between
1/(n+1)
and
n/(n+1)
, when evaluated in the observations
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
, we avoid evaluation of the density
d_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
in the endpoints 0 and 1.

All the above three estimators are consistent and have the same (first order) asymptotic distribution. The proofs of the results stated in this section are given in Appendix
B.1
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Given is a fixed index
\boldsymbol{\tau}
. Assume that
D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
is differentiable with density
d_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
. Let
\widehat{e}_{\boldsymbol{\tau},n}
be any of the two estimators
\widehat{e}_{\boldsymbol{\tau},n}^{\mbox{ {\scriptsize LM}}}
or
\widehat{e}_{\boldsymbol{\tau},n}^{\mbox{ {\scriptsize M}}}
.

(i)

[Consistency]
Assume
\mathbb{E}[\lvert X\rvert^{\kappa}]<\infty
for some
\kappa>1
. Let
d_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
be continuous almost everywhere. If for some
0<M<\infty

\lvert d_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(t)\rvert\leq M[t(1-t)]^{-1+1/\kappa+\gamma}\quad\text{ for }t\in(0,1)

for some
\gamma>0
, then
\widehat{e}_{\boldsymbol{\tau},n}\xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}}e_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(X;D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}})
as
n\to\infty
.

If in addition
d_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
is Lipschitz continuous or bounded (uniformly) on
(0,1)
then
\widehat{e}_{\boldsymbol{\tau},n}^{\mbox{ {\scriptsize L}}}\xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}}e_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(X;D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}})
as
n\to\infty

(ii)

[Asymptotic normality result]
Assume
\mathbb{E}[\lvert X\rvert^{\kappa}]<\infty
for some
\kappa>2
. Suppose
d_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\prime}
exists and is continuous on
(0,1)
. Furthermore, suppose there exists some
\gamma>0
such that

\lvert d_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(t)\rvert\leq M[t(1-t)]^{-1/2+1/\kappa+\gamma}\quad\text{ for }t\in(0,1)

and

\lvert d_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\prime}(t)\rvert\leq M[t(1-t)]^{-3/2+1/\kappa+\gamma}\quad\text{ for }t\in(0,1).

Then
\sqrt{n}(\widehat{e}_{\boldsymbol{\tau},n}-e_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(X;D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}))\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(0;\sigma_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{2})
as
n\to\infty
where

\sigma_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{2}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(F_{X}(\min(s,t))-F_{X}(s)F_{X}(t))d_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(F_{X}(s))d_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(F_{X}(t))\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t.

(16)

If in addition
d_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
is Lipschitz continuous or bounded (uniformly) then
\sqrt{n}(\widehat{e}_{\boldsymbol{\tau},n}^{\mbox{ {\scriptsize L}}}-e_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(X;D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}))\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(0;\sigma_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{2})
as
n\to\infty
.

When
d_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
is bounded, we obtain the almost sure convergence for any random variable
X
with a finite absolute
\kappa
th moment (for some
\kappa>1
). In particular, when
D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(u)=K_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(u)
, that is the case of extremiles, we obtain the same result as in Theorem 1(i) on p. 1370 of
Daouia et al. (2019)
. In the case of extremiles, also the function
\lvert d_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\prime}(t)\rvert t(1-t)
is bounded. Hence, also the asymptotic normality result in Theorem 1(ii) on p. 1370 of
Daouia et al. (2019)
is included as a special case of Theorem
3.1
in our more general framework.

3.2
General loss functions and distribution functions

We now turn to the general setting of any pair
(D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\ell_{\delta})
.
From now on we assume that
X
is an absolutely continuous random variable, i.e.,
F_{X}
has an associated density function
f_{X}
.
We only consider loss functions
\ell_{\delta}(x,c)
for which

c\mapsto\ell_{\delta}(x,c)\,\mbox{ is convex for every $x$}.

By Proposition
2.9
we know that solving the minimization problem is equivalent to finding a value
c
for which

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}c}\Big(\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\ell_{\delta}(x,c\,)\mathrm{d}D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(F_{X}(x))\Big)=0.

Since
X
is an absolutely continuous random variable, Theorem
2.2
then ensures the right-hand side equals

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}c}\Big(\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\ell_{\delta}(x,c)d_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(F_{X}(x))f_{X}(x)\mathrm{d}x\Big)=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\ell_{\delta}^{\prime}(x,c)d_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(F_{X}(x))f_{X}(x)\mathrm{d}x.

We are now in the setting of an
M
-functional problem: finding a value
c
such that

\lambda_{F}(c):=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}d_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(F_{X}(x))\ell_{\delta}^{\prime}(x,c)f_{X}(x)\mathrm{d}x=0.

Given a random sample
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
from
X
, the
M
-estimator is defined as the solution
T_{n}
to

\lambda_{F,n}(c):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(F_{X}(X_{i}))\ell_{\delta}^{\prime}(X_{i},c)=0.

However, the appearance of
F_{X}
leads to an extra complication.
Since
F_{X}
is unknown we replace it by a consistent estimator
\widehat{F}_{n}
for it, for example, by its empirical version
F_{n}(x)=(n+1)^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}1_{\{X_{i}\leq x\}}
. The estimator
T_{n}^{*}
is then defined as the value
c
for which

\lambda_{\widehat{F}_{n}}^{*}(c):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(\widehat{F}_{n}(X_{i}))\ell_{\delta}^{\prime}(X_{i},c)=0.

If there is no such value
c
, then we define
T_{n}^{*}
as the value minimizing
\lvert\lambda_{\widehat{F}_{n}}^{*}(c)\rvert
.
If, in practice, there are multiple values of
c
for which
\lambda_{\widehat{F}_{n}}^{*}(c)=0
, we take the smallest of them.

In what follows we establish the consistency and the asymptotic normality of the estimator
T_{n}^{*}
. Before stating the results, we introduce some assumptions, needed in various parts of the theoretical results.

(A1)

1.

X
is an absolutely continuous random variable;

2.

\lambda_{F}(c)
is finite for every
c
and has a unique root
t_{0}
;

3.

c\mapsto\ell_{\delta}(x,c)
is convex for every
x
;

4.

x\mapsto\ell_{\delta}^{\prime}(x,c)
is measurable for every
c
.

(A2)

\mathbb{E}[\lvert\ell_{\delta}^{\prime}(X,c)\rvert]<\infty
.

(A3)

There exist functions
h_{1},h_{2}
with

x\mapsto h_{j}(x,c)
non-decreasing and left continuous for
j=1,2
such that
\ell_{\delta}^{\prime}(x,c)=h_{1}(x,c)-h_{2}(x,c)
for every
c
and
x
.

(A4)

There exists an
r>0
, such that
\mathbb{E}[\lvert h_{j}(X,c)\rvert^{r}]<\infty
for
j=1,2
with
h_{j}
from
(A3)
.

(A5)

There exist constants
M,\gamma>0
such that

\lvert d_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(t)\rvert\leq M[t(1-t)]^{-1+1/r+\gamma}
with
r
from
(A4)
.

(A6)

d_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
has a continuous derivative on
(0,1)
. Furthermore, with
r
from
(A4)
there exist
M,\gamma>0
such that

\lvert d_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(t)\rvert\leq M[t(1-t)]^{-1/2+1/r+\gamma}
and
\lvert d_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\prime}(t)\rvert\leq M[t(1-t)]^{-3/2+1/r+\gamma}
.

(A7)

d_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
is continuous except at possibly a finite number of points.

(A8)

1.

d_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
is bounded on
[0,1]
;

2.

d_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
is locally uniformly continuous except at a finite number of points.

(A9)

\lambda_{F}(c)
is differentiable at
t_{0}
with derivative
\lambda_{F}^{\prime}(t_{0})\neq 0
.

(A10)

For any sequence
\omega_{n}\rightarrow 0
as
n\to\infty

\sup_{\lvert c-t_{0}\rvert\leq\omega_{n}}\frac{\sqrt{n}\lvert\lambda_{\widehat{F}_{n}}^{*}(c)-\lambda_{\widehat{F}_{n}}^{*}(t_{0})-\lambda_{F}(c)\rvert}{1+\sqrt{n}\lvert c-t_{0}\rvert}\xrightarrow{\text{P}}0.

Throughout this section we assume the existence (i.e. finiteness), as well as uniqueness, of the target quantity of interest
e_{\boldsymbol{\tau},\delta}(X;D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\ell_{\delta})
(also shortly denoted as
t_{0}
).

We start by looking into the two loss functions encountered when estimating quantiles. Recall from Section

\displaystyle D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(u)=u\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\ell_{\delta}(x,c)=\left|\delta-1_{\{x\leq c\}}\right||x-c|

\displaystyle\mbox{approach 2}:\qquad

\displaystyle D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(u)=K_{\tau}(u)\quad\mbox{in \eqref{DtauExtremiles}}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\ell_{\delta}(x,c)=|x-c|,

(20)

where in approach 2, one takes

to obtain the

.

Before stating the first corollary we make an important remark.

Remark 3.3
.

Consider the absolute value loss
\ell_{\delta}(x,c)=\lvert x-c\rvert.
The derivative with respect to
c
of this function is not defined in the point
c=x
. In order for
x\mapsto\ell_{\delta}^{\prime}(x,c)
to be left continuous (see Assumption
(A3)
), we will choose

\ell_{\delta}^{\prime}
to be the right-hand side derivative. In the specific case of the absolute value loss, this means
\ell_{\delta}^{\prime}(x,c)=1_{\{x\leq c\}}-1_{\{x>c\}}
. This choice for the right-hand side derivative is justified since we are integrating with respect to
x
. Meaning that the integrand is uniquely defined almost everywhere.

Corollary 3.1
.

Consider the absolute value loss function
\ell_{\delta}(x,c)=\lvert x-c\rvert.
Consider a random variable
X
and a cumulative distribution function
D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
such that the assumptions of Theorem
3.2
are satisfied. Then the asymptotic variance reduces to

\frac{\text{Median}(D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}})(1-\text{Median}(D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}))}{\left[f_{X}\left(\mbox{Median}(X_{{\scriptsize D}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}})\right)\right]^{2}},

(21)

where
\mbox{Median}(D_{\tau})
denotes the median of a random variable with cumulative distribution function
D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
.

Proof.

We have
\ell^{\prime}_{\delta}(x,c)=1_{\{x\leq c\}}-1_{\{x>c\}}
, with thus
x\mapsto\ell^{\prime}_{\delta}(x,c)
non-increasing. This means we can take
h_{1}(x,c)=0
and
h_{2}(x,c)=-\ell^{\prime}_{\delta}(x,c)
for Assumption
(A3)
.
When integrating a function, say
k(s)
, with respect to this measure, we get

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\,k(s)\mathrm{d}\ell^{\prime}_{\delta}(s,t_{0})=-2k(t_{0}).

This implies
\sigma_{t_{0}}^{2}=4[F_{X}(t_{0})-F_{X}^{2}(t_{0})][d_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(F_{X}(t_{0}))]^{2}
.
Further, we have that

\lambda_{F}(c)=\int_{-\infty}^{c}d_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(F_{X}(x))f_{X}(x)\mathrm{d}x-\int_{c}^{+\infty}d_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(F_{X}(x))f_{X}(x)\mathrm{d}x=P\left\{X_{{\scriptsize D}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}\leq c\right\}-P\left\{X_{{\scriptsize D}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}>c\right\}=2D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\left(F_{X}(c)\right)-1,

from which it follows that
t_{0}=\mbox{Median}\left(X_{{\scriptsize D}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}\right)
, and also
F_{X}(t_{0})=\mbox{Median}(D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}})
.

Furthermore
\lambda_{F}^{\prime}(c)=2f_{X}(c)d_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(F_{X}(c))
. In conclusion,

\sigma_{t_{0}}^{2}/\left[\lambda_{F}^{\prime}(t_{0})\right]^{2}
leads to expression (
21
).
∎

Remark 3.4
.

When taking
D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}(u)=K_{\tau}(u)
, expression (
21
) leads to the asymptotic variance for the quantile estimator, obtained via approach 2 (see (
20
)). It is easily seen from (

that
\mbox{Median}(K_{\tau})=\tau
and
\mbox{Median}\left(X_{{\scriptsize D}_{\tau}}\right)=q_{\tau}(X)

∎

Remark 3.6
.

When using the expectile loss function, the corresponding generalized extremile equals the
\delta

. Hence, if we consider

and Corollary

Simulation study 4

The adaptive loss function for quantile estimation in case of censoring was briefly discussed in Section
2.4
. This loss function is not convex, and hence does not satisfy the conditions stated in Theorem
3.2
. Since these are however sufficient conditions, we might wonder whether the finite-sample distribution can nevertheless be well approximated with the asymptotic normal distribution as stated in the theorem.

In the right-random censoring setting, the variable of interest
X
is possibly censored by a variable
C
. We are interested in a
\delta
th quantile of
X
, but only have data of the form
(Y,\Delta_{C})
, where
Y=\min\{X,C\}
and
\Delta_{C}=1_{\{X\leq C\}}
. Given a sample from
(Y,\Delta_{C})
, we can use our estimator to estimate such a
\delta
th quantile of
X
. Table
7
summarizes the various elements of the simulation model.

When calculating the estimator we have to deal with the non-convexity of
\lambda_{F}(c)
and
\lambda_{\widehat{F}_{n}}^{*}(c)
.
Globally minimizing
\lambda_{\widehat{F}_{n}}^{*}(c)
is not feasible, and instead we implemented the following procedure. Based on a sample from
Y
, we construct a sequence
\boldsymbol{c}=(c_{1},\cdots,c_{k})
of values ranging from
\min(Y_{i})
to
\max(Y_{i})
. More precisely we take a equispaced grid of points, with an increment of 0.01, i.e. for
m,m^{*}\in\mathbb{N}
, with
m^{*}>m
, we have
c_{m^{*}}-c_{m}=(m^{*}-m)0.01
. We then search for the smallest value in this sequence, say
c_{m}
, which satisfies the following inequalities

\frac{\lambda_{\widehat{F}_{n}}^{*}(c_{m+j})-\lambda_{\widehat{F}_{n}}^{*}(c_{m})}{j\cdot 0.01}>0.01\quad\text{ for }j=1,\cdots,20.

This requires difference quotients (discrete slopes) to be strictly positive in an interval past a given point. In this manner, we search for a (local) minimum. By considering multiple subsequent difference quotients, we avoid selecting a value due to a small spike in the function
\lambda_{\widehat{F}_{n}}^{*}(c)
.

Recall that
f_{X},F_{X}
denote respectively the density and the cumulative distribution function of
X
. Similarly, we use the notations
f_{C},F_{C}
for these quantities for the censoring variable
C
.
The asymptotic variance stated in Theorem
3.2
equals in this case

\frac{F_{X}(t_{0})(1-F_{X}(t_{0}))}{(f_{X}(t_{0})-(1-\delta)f_{C}(t_{0}))^{2}}.

(26)

When there is no censoring (meaning that
F_{C}(t)=0
for all
t
), this expression reduces to the asymptotic variance of the empirical quantile estimator.

Sander (1975)
estimated a quantile for censored data relying on the Kaplan-Meier estimator for a distribution function. We refer to this quantile estimator as the Kaplan Meier-based (KM-based) estimator. The author established asymptotic normality for the estimator (see Corollary 1 on p. 5 in
Sander (1975)
). The asymptotic variance of this estimator equals

\frac{(1-\delta)^{2}}{\left(f_{X}(t_{0})\right)^{2}}\int_{0}^{t_{0}}\frac{f_{X}(x)}{(1-F_{X}(x))^{2}(1-F_{C}(x))}\mathrm{d}x.

(27)

Again, in case of no censoring, this asymptotic variance reduces to the one of the empirical quantile estimator.

General considerations for risks of distorted random variables

In this section we establish some general properties about risk measures
\rho(X_{{\scriptsize D}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}})
.
In the real data example in Section
6
we refer to these results.
For notational simplicity we simply write
X_{{\scriptsize D}}
instead of
X_{{\scriptsize D}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}
in this section.
Consider a random variable
X
defined on the probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)
, with
\mathcal{F}
the sigma-algebra, and
P
the probability measure. Denote by
\mathcal{X}
the linear space of random variables defined on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)
, and for a given
D\in\mathbb{D}
consider a risk measure

\begin{array}[]{ll}\rho:&\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}\\
&X\to\rho(X_{{\scriptsize D}}).\end{array}

Before stating the main result of this section, we recall some basic properties of a risk measure
\rho
.

\star

A risk measure
\rho
is called
monotone
if:
\forall X,Y\in\mathcal{X}:X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{\leq}}Y\Longrightarrow\rho(X)\leq\rho(Y)
.

\star

A risk measure
\rho
is called
translation invariant
if:
\forall X\in\mathcal{X}
,
c\in\mathbb{R}:\rho(X+c)=\rho(X)+c
.

\star

A risk measure
\rho
is called
positive homogeneous
if:
\forall X\in\mathcal{X}
,
a\in[0,+\infty)
, such that
aX\in\mathcal{X}:\rho(aX)=a\rho(X)
.

\star

A risk measure
\rho
is called
subadditive
if:
\forall X,Y,X+Y\in\mathcal{X}
it holds that
\rho(X+Y)\leq\rho(X)+\rho(Y)
.

A risk measure
\rho
is coherent when it is monotone, translation invariant, positive homogeneous and subadditive.

Theorem
5.1
investigates risk measures for the distorted random variable
X_{{\scriptsize D}}
. In establishing some of these statements, we can rely on results of
Liu et al. (2021)
.
The proof of the theorem is provided in Appendix
C
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Denote by
D
a distortion function and by
\rho
a risk measure defined on
\mathcal{X}
, the linear vector space of random variables.
For two random variables
X
and
Y
in the domain of
\rho
we then have:

(i)

If
\rho
is positive homogeneous, then also
\rho((aX)_{{\scriptsize D}})=a\rho(X_{{\scriptsize D}})
for
a\in[0,\infty)
.

(ii)

If
\rho
is translation invariant, then also
\rho((X+c)_{{\scriptsize D}})=\rho(X_{{\scriptsize D}})+c
for
c\in\mathbb{R}
.

(iii)

If
\rho
is monotone and
X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{\leq}}Y
then
\rho(X_{{\scriptsize D}})\leq\rho(Y_{{\scriptsize D}})
.

(iv)

Suppose
\rho_{\delta}
is coherent. If
D
is convex, then
the composition (distortion) risk measure is also coherent (and thus in particular subadditive), i.e. for all
X
and
Y
, we have
\rho((X+Y)_{{\scriptsize D}})\leq\rho(X_{{\scriptsize D}})+\rho(Y_{{\scriptsize D}})
.

(v)

Denote by
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
two distortion functions such that
D_{1}(u)\leq D_{2}(u)
for all
u\in[0,1]
. If
\rho
is monotone, then
\rho(X_{{\scriptsize D}_{2}})\leq\rho(X_{{\scriptsize D}_{1}})
. Specifically for
D_{1}(u)\leq u\leq D_{2}(u)
we have
\rho(X_{{\scriptsize D}_{2}})\leq\rho(X)\leq\rho(X_{{\scriptsize D}_{1}})
.

(vi)

Suppose that the risk measure
\rho
depends on a parameter
\gamma
, and denote the risk measure by
\rho_{\gamma}
. If
\rho_{\gamma}
is monotone increasing in
\gamma
, then so is the composition risk measure
\rho_{\gamma}(X_{{\scriptsize D}})
.

Risk measures defined as the minimizer of an expected loss minimization procedure as in (
6
) avoid unjustified risk-loading under general conditions.
If
\ell
is such that
\arg\min_{c\in\mathbb{R}}\ell(x,c)=x
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
then we have for an almost surely constant random variable
X\sim\mathds{1}_{[b,\infty)}
, i.e.,
X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{=}}b
for some
b\in\mathbb{R}
, that
\rho(X)=\arg\min_{c\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{E}[\ell(X,c)]=\arg\min_{c\in\mathbb{R}}\ell(b,c)=b
.
This property also carries over to the distorted version
X_{{\scriptsize D}}
, where we have
{\scriptsize D}\circ\mathds{1}_{[b,\infty)}=\mathds{1}_{[b,\infty)}
due to
D(0)=0
and
D(1)=1
.
As such we have
X_{{\scriptsize D}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{=}}b
and hence also
\rho(X_{{\scriptsize D}})=b
.

Concluding remarks and further discussion

This paper studies general functionals, called generalized extremiles, that are solutions to an optimization problem involving as crucial ingredient a pair
(D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\ell_{\delta})
with
D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
a cumulative distribution function with support
[0,1]
, and with
\ell_{\delta}
a loss function. The main contribution in the paper consists of providing estimators for the generalized extremile, and proving their consistency and asymptotic normality.
By allowing for a general pair
(D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\ell_{\delta})
several functionals studied in the literature fall under this framework, and hence we also provide statistical inference for all these. Cases for which estimators and asymptotic normality results for them are available in the literature are limited, and for these cases we showed that our general theorem includes these results as special cases.

Several interesting research questions can be raised. A first question is that one can wonder under which conditions the pair
(D_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\ell_{\delta})
would lead to a coherent risk measure itself. Some partial answers are to be found in Section
2.2
. Furthermore, in Section
5
we derive properties such as e.g. monotonicity or coherence of distorted risk measures based on properties of the base risk measure and the utilized distortion function.
We provide an expression for the asymptotic variance for the estimator of the generalized extremile. Such a result allows to construct approximate confidence intervals for it. This however requires an estimator for the asymptotic variance. Although plug-in estimators are straightforward, it still remains to be established which are sufficient conditions for such a plug-in estimator to be consistent.
Another interesting aspect is the behaviour of the asymptotic variance as a function of the parameters
\boldsymbol{\tau}
and
\delta
. For certain functionals it would be interesting to look into such behaviour. Lastly regarding the asymptotic normality result, this has been established under a set of sufficient conditions. From the simulation study however we know that the result continues to
hold in cases even when some of these conditions are violated. It would be interesting to look for a minimal set of conditions.
A final issue concerns the qualitative properties to be expected from a generalized extremile, such as monotonicity as a function of
\boldsymbol{\tau}
, or non-crossingness for various values of
\delta
. See Section
6
and the discussion therein.
An interesting open research question involves estimation of the generalized extremile which would guarantee these properties to hold for the estimators.
