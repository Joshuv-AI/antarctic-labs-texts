Magnetic slowdown of topological edge states
G. Bal, S. Becker, A. Drouot

Abstract
. We study the propagation of wavepackets along curved interfaces between topological, magnetic materials. Our Hamiltonian is a massive Dirac operator with a magnetic potential. We construct semiclassical wavepackets propagating along the curved interface as adiabatic modulations of straight edge states under constant magnetic fields. While in the magnetic-free case, the wavepackets propagate coherently at speed one, here they experience slowdown, dispersion, and Aharonov–Bohm effects. Several numerical simulations illustrate our results.



1.
Introduction

This paper analyzes wavepackets propagating along an interface between two topologically distinct materials, in the presence of an external magnetic field. It extends constructions carried out in
[
2
]
for magnetic-free models. We represent here the electron dynamics via a two-dimensional Dirac equation:

(1.1)

(\varepsilon D_{t}+\not{D})\Psi(t,x)=0,\quad(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}.

In (
1.1
),
\not{D}
denotes a Dirac operator with sign-changing mass and magnetic field:

(1.2)

\not{D}=(\varepsilon D_{1}-A_{1}(x))\sigma_{1}+(\varepsilon D_{2}-A_{2}(x))\sigma_{2}+\kappa(x)\sigma_{3},\qquad\text{where:}

•

\varepsilon>0
is a small semiclassical parameter and
\varepsilon D_{t}=-i\varepsilon\partial_{t}
,
\varepsilon D_{j}=-i\varepsilon\partial_{j}
denote the self-adjoint semiclassical derivatives;

•

A=(A_{1},A_{2})^{t}\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{2},{\mathbb{R}}^{2})
is a magnetic potential with
\nabla A\in C^{\infty}_{b}
(i.e. smooth with all derivatives uniformly bounded), inducing the magnetic field
B=\partial_{1}A_{2}-\partial_{2}A_{1}
;

•

\kappa\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{2},{\mathbb{R}})
has varying sign and satisfies
\nabla\kappa\in C^{\infty}_{b}
;

•

\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3}
are the standard
2\times 2
Pauli matrices.

The sign of the domain wall
\kappa

(1.3)

\kappa(y)=0\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad\nabla\kappa(y)\neq 0,

the interface
\Gamma=\kappa^{-1}(0)
separates regions of distinct local topology
[
4

2
]
. For analytic reasons, we consider here a uniform version of (
1.3

(1.4)

\inf\{\big|\nabla\kappa(x)\big|:x\in\Gamma\}>0.

The bulk-edge correspondence for Dirac operators
[
4
]
predicts that a tubular neighborhood of
\Gamma=\kappa^{-1}(0)
supports asymmetric currents, hence (some analogue of) edge states for small
\varepsilon
. For vanishing magnetic potentials, we constructed in
[
2
]
long-lived solutions to (
1.1
). These were confined and propagating at speed one along
\Gamma
. We referred to them as dynamical edge states, since we derived them as time-dependent adiabatic modulations of straight edge states. This paper extends the construction to the magnetic case. A non-zero magnetic field induces several new phenomena for dynamical edge states:

(i)

A systematic slowdown, see Figure
3
;

(ii)

A large phase-shift, generating a Aharonov–Bohm effect when
\Gamma
is a loop;

(iii)

In general, a mesoscopic dispersion along
\Gamma
.

1.2.
Simplified main result

For the sake of simplicity, we consider a restricted setup: the domain wall satisfies a geometric condition and the magnetic field is constant. Our main result,
6.2
, takes then a clearer form.

The aforementioned assumptions are:

•

\kappa\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{2},{\mathbb{R}})
with
\nabla\kappa\in C^{\infty}_{b}

satisfies

(1.10)

y\in\kappa^{-1}(0)\quad\Rightarrow\quad\big|\nabla\kappa(y)\big|=1,\quad\Delta\kappa(y)=0;

•

The magnetic field
B
is constant.

While (
1.10
) is analytically restrictive, it is not geometrically restrictive: any one-dimensional submanifold of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
is the zero set of a function
\kappa
satisfying (
1.10
).
For instance, for the straight edge
\Gamma={\mathbb{R}}e_{2}
, we can choose
\kappa(y)=y_{2}
while for the unit circle
\Gamma=\mathbb{S}^{1}
we can choose
\kappa(y)=\ln|y|
(near
|y|=1
). When the two above conditions hold, the quantities
r_{t},B_{t},\rho_{t},\gamma_{t},\varphi_{t},c_{t}
and
s_{t}
do not depend on
t
, and we omit the subscript
t
.

The most elementary setup with these two conditions consists of
\kappa(x)=x_{2}
and
A=-Bx_{2}e_{1}
. The corresponding Dirac operator is:

(1.11)

\not{D}_{0,B}:=(\varepsilon D_{1}+Bx_{2})\sigma_{1}+\varepsilon D_{2}\sigma_{2}+x_{2}\sigma_{3}.

We remark that the magnetic potential vanishes along the interface
{\mathbb{R}}e_{1}
and is parallel to it. The equation
(\varepsilon D_{t}+\not{D}_{0,B})\Psi=0
admits an explicit family of non-dispersive wavepacket solutions that propagate along
\Gamma
at speed
c=(1+B^{2})^{-1/2}
:

(1.12)

\Psi_{0,B}(x):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\psi_{0,B}\left(\dfrac{x-cte_{1}}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\right),\quad\psi_{0,B}(z):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{i\xi z_{1}-\frac{1}{2}\rho(z_{2}+\gamma\xi)^{2}}\hat{f}(\xi)d\xi\cdot U_{2,\varphi}\left[\begin{matrix}1\\
-1\end{matrix}\right],

where
\hat{f}
is any Schwartz-class function,
\rho=\sqrt{1+B^{2}}
,
\gamma=\frac{B}{1+B^{2}}
, and
\varphi=\arctan B
.

We extend this statement to interfaces tilted by an angle
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Let
\not{D}_{\theta,B}
be the Dirac operator with domain wall and magnetic potential

(1.13)

\kappa(x)=n\cdot x,\quad A(x)=B\kappa(x)\tau,\quad n=\left[\begin{matrix}-\sin\theta\\
\cos\theta\end{matrix}\right],\quad\tau=-\left[\begin{matrix}\cos\theta\\
\sin\theta\end{matrix}\right].

The equation
(\varepsilon D_{t}+\not{D}_{\theta,B})\Psi=0
is unitarily equivalent to the case
\theta=0
and admits a family of solutions constructed from
\Psi_{0,B}
:

(1.14)

\Psi_{\theta,B}(x):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\psi_{\theta,B}\left(\dfrac{x-ct\tau}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\right),\qquad\psi_{\theta,B}(z):=U_{3,\theta}\mathcal{R}_{\theta}\psi_{0,B}(z).

Our main result produces approximate solutions to
(\varepsilon D_{t}+\not{D})\Psi=0
as modulations of
\psi_{\theta,B}
. To state it, we need the distribution
g_{t}
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
defined by:

(1.15)

g_{t}(z):=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}e^{i\zeta z+i\gamma(\theta_{t}-\theta_{0})\zeta_{1}^{2}}d\zeta=\dfrac{e^{-i\pi/4}}{|2\pi\gamma(\theta_{t}-\theta_{0})|^{1/2}}e^{i\frac{-z_{1}^{2}}{4\gamma(\theta_{t}-\theta_{0})}}\cdot\delta_{0}(z_{2}).

The second equality is valid for
\theta_{t}\neq\theta_{0}
and should be replaced by a Dirac mass when
\theta_{t}=\theta_{0}
. Our simplified theorem for constant magnetic fields reads then as follows.

Theorem 1.1
.

Assume that
\kappa
satisfies the condition (
1.10
) and that
B
is constant. For any
{\rm T}>0
, the equation
(\varepsilon D_{t}+\not{D})\Psi=0

admits solutions that satisfy, uniformly for
t\in[-{\rm T},{\rm T}]
and
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
:

(1.16)

\Psi(t,x)=\dfrac{e^{\frac{i}{\varepsilon}\chi(t,x)}}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\psi\left(t,\dfrac{x-y_{t}}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\right)+O_{L^{2}}(\varepsilon^{1/2}),\qquad\text{where}\quad\psi(t,z):=\big(\mathcal{R}_{\theta_{t}}g_{t}\big)*\psi_{\theta_{t},B}(z),

(1.17)

\text{and}\quad\chi(t,x)=\int_{0}^{t}\dot{y_{s}}\cdot A(y_{s})ds+A(y_{t})\cdot(x-y_{t})+(x-y_{t})\cdot\left(\nabla A(y_{t})^{\top}-Bn_{t}\tau_{t}^{\top}\right)(x-y_{t}).

The leading order term in (
1.16
):

(i)

Propagates at speed
c=(1+B^{2})^{-1/2}
along
\Gamma
, in the prescribed direction
\tau
.

(ii)

Is semiclassically localized at the phase-space point
(y_{t},A_{t})
with
A_{t}=A(y_{t})
. This is where the eigenvalues of the symbol of
{\not{D}}
are degenerate. While the rapid oscillations generated by
A_{t}
can be locally gauged away, they cannot be globally neglected when
\Gamma
is a closed loop: this is the Aharonov–Bohm effect.

(iii)

Disperses along
\Gamma
at rate prescribed by the difference
\theta_{t}-\theta_{0}
:

(1.18)

\big|\psi(t,z)\big|\leq\dfrac{C}{1+|\theta_{t}-\theta_{0}|^{1/2}}\sup_{z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\big|\psi(0,z)\big|.

In particular, the leading part in (
1.16
) is controlled by
\varepsilon^{-1/2}|\theta_{t}-\theta_{0}|^{-1/2}
.
This is a relatively weak dispersion as it comes from dispersion of the wave envelop
{\mathbb{R}}_{\theta_{t}g_{t}}\star\psi_{\theta_{t},B}
rather than dispersive relations of plane waves. It produces effects for times of order one, in contrast with dispersion in e.g. the semiclassical Schrödinger equation which arises at time
\varepsilon
.

We detail these three effects in §
1.3
. Theorem
6.2
will extend the result of Theorem
1.1
to cover varying magnetic fields, general domain walls and longer times of validity. The corresponding wavepackets will more generally have a variable speed, given by the ODE (
1.6
); a phase with properties identical to (ii) above; and a more complicated rate of dispersion.

Effects of the magnetic field

We comment here on the structure of the wavepacket (

B

\kappa

1.10
); and detail how things change when these conditions are relaxed (see Theorem

).

Turning on a magnetic field systematically slows down the propagation. In the general setup of Theorem

The wavepacket is semiclassically localized at
(y_{t},A_{t})
. This point lies in the crossing set of the semiclassical symbol

(1.19)

\not{D}(x,\xi)=\big(\xi_{1}-A_{1}(x)\big)\sigma_{1}+\big(\xi_{2}-A_{2}(x)\big)\sigma_{2}+\kappa(x)\sigma_{3};

that is, the eigenvalues of
\not{D}(y_{t},A_{t})
are repeated. Hence,
(y_{t},A_{t})
is an exotic semiclassical trajectory, in the sense that it is not among those predicted by standard propagation of singularity, such as
[

The semiclassical action
\dot{y_{t}}\cdot A_{t}
generates a large phase-shift
e^{\frac{i}{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{t}\dot{y_{s}}\cdot A_{s}ds}
that can be locally – but not globally – gauged away. When
\Gamma
is a loop, after a full revolution the phase shift relates to the magnetic flux
\Phi=\int_{\Gamma}A
: it is
e^{\frac{i}{\varepsilon}\Phi}
, see Figure

for the case of the circle. This is the Aharonov–Bohm effect.

The envelop of the wavepacket typically disperses along
\Gamma
. Under the geometric condition (
1.10
), the rate of change of dispersion is
|\theta_{t}-\theta_{0}|^{-1/2}
. Indeed, writing
\psi
in terms of
g_{t}
and
\psi_{0,B}
, and using the formula (
1.15
), we have for
|\theta_{t}-\theta_{0}|\geq 1
:

(1.20)

\displaystyle\sup_{z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\big|\psi(t,z)\big|=\sup_{z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\big|g_{t}*\psi_{0,B}(z)\big|

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\gamma|\theta_{t}-\theta_{0}|}}\sup_{z_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\psi(y_{1},z_{2})|dy_{1}

(1.21)

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{C}{\sqrt{\gamma|\theta_{t}-\theta_{0}|}}=\dfrac{C^{\prime}}{\sqrt{\gamma|\theta_{t}-\theta_{0}|}}\sup_{z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\big|\psi(0,z)\big|.

This yields (
1.18
). While the rate of change of dispersion
|\theta_{t}-\theta_{0}|^{-1/2}
is bounded above if
\Gamma
is asymptotically flat, it can be as small as

when
\Gamma
is a loop or a spiral. In these cases, the wavepacket loses coherence over long times as displayed in Figure
8

, the rate of dispersion
\nu_{t}
takes a more complicated form: see Lemma

, (

) and (
3.42
). We comment that dispersion can have long-time effects. When the dispersion is strongest,
\nu_{t}\sim t
and our construction holds up to times
{\rm T}\ll\varepsilon^{-1/8}
; when it is weakest,
\nu_{t}=O(1)
and we recover the time of validity
{\rm T}\ll\varepsilon^{-1/2}
of
[

When
B
or
|\nabla\kappa|
vary along
\Gamma
(that is, outside the setup of Theorem

), an additional effect emerges: time-dependent anisotropic compression/stretching in the normal and tangent directions of
\Gamma

The compression factors,
\sqrt{\rho_{t}}
(in the normal direction) and

(in the tangent direction), remain bounded above and below in the limit

that controls the dispersion, which can grow like

.

1.4.
Strategy of proof

Conceptually speaking, our approach consists in constructing successive transformations of
{\not{D}}
that bring us closer to the flat Dirac operator
{\not{D}}_{0,B}
of (
1.11
). The main steps are as follows:

1.

In §
2
, we conjugate
{\not{D}}
by a gauge transform
e^{i\chi(t,x)/\varepsilon}
, cooked up so that the resulting magnetic potential
A-\nabla\chi
vanishes along
\Gamma
and is tangent to the level sets of
\kappa
. These are shared features with the flat Dirac operator
{\not{D}}_{0,B}
. The expansion of the gauge term
e^{i\chi(t,x)/\varepsilon}
near
y_{t}
produces the large oscillatory phase of (
1.16
).

2.

In §
3.1
we look for solutions to the gauge-modified Dirac equation
(\varepsilon D_{t}+\tilde{\not{D}})\tilde{\Psi}=0
as semiclassical wavepackets localized at
(y_{t},0)
. A formal Taylor expansion produces a hierarchy of equations for the envelops.

3.

In §
3.2
, we perform a series of spatial and spinorial rotations on the leading equation. The first two rotations (which already appear in
[
2
]
) flatten the interface; the last is magnetically induced and is among the new ingredients. The result is the leading equation that one would get starting from
\not{D}_{0,B}
.

4.

Up to a partial shifted Fourier transform, the leading operator takes the same form as in the absence of magnetic fields
[
2
]
. In §
4
, we compute explicitly its kernel and prove stability estimates – later needed for the subleading transport equation.

5.

In §
5
we explicitly integrate the transport equation. In Fourier variables, the solutions have a quadratic phase with Hessian
\theta_{t}-\theta_{0}
(in the setup of §
1.2
). In physical space, this transfers to dispersion at rate
|\theta_{t}-\theta_{0}|^{-1/2}
.

6.

Higher-order approximations are constructed iteratively in §
6
. Combining them with the unitarity of
e^{-it{\not{D}}}
and a Duhamel argument, we obtain our main result, Theorem
6.2
. It gives an approximate solution to
(\varepsilon D_{t}+{\not{D}})\Psi=0
that propagates along
\Gamma
, slowed down by the magnetic field, and explicitly expressed through the above transformations.

We then illustrate our findings with a series of numerical simulations in §
7
.

1.5.
Related literature

For systems of semiclassical PDEs, the symbols that govern the macroscopic transport are the eigenvalues of the (matrix-valued) symbol. A symbolic diagonalization argument shows that their Hamiltonian flow governs the leading-order dynamics.
When the eigenvalues of the symbol are degenerate, the classical equations of motion break down. The situation studied here is among the simplest such cases. Our analysis show that the phase-space crossing set
\{\big(x,A(x)\big),x\in\Gamma\}
support wavepackets with the dynamics (
1.6
). As mentioned above, (
1.6
) is an exotic semiclassical trajectory not predicted by the standard results on propagation of singularities
[
16
]
.
In other setups, wavepackets may e.g. start away from the crossing set, reach it, and undergo a Landau–Zener transition; see e.g.
[
23
,
22
,
19
,
10
]
.

From a physical point of a view, our motivation stems from the ubiquity of
{\not{D}}
in the field of topological phases of matter
[
29
,
24
]
, and in particular one-particle models of topological insulators and topological superconductors
[
28
,
9
,
25
,
4
,
5
]
, which generically come with conical points
[
15
]
. The domain wall
\kappa(x)
models the interface between two topologically distinct insulating phases
[
18
,
12
,
11
]
. This in turn generates an asymmetric transport along the interface
\Gamma
by a principle called the bulk-interface correspondence; see e.g.
[
17
,
20
,
25
,
13
,
6
,
7
,
14
,
3
]
. The wavepackets analyzed here encode this asymmetry; see
[
2
, §1.4]
for a discussion when
A=0
. The operator
{\not{D}}
also emerges in the effective analysis of graphene and its pseudomagnetic (strained) analogues, see for instance
[
21
]
.

Note that the magnetic field is essential in the integer quantum Hall effect
[
27
,
1
,
8
]
, which was the first observed example of topologically non-trivial state of matter.
There, the insulating gaps are obtained from the degenerate Landau levels associated to a constant magnetic field. This contrasts with the situation considered here: the non-trivial topology imposed by the domain wall is stable against magnetic contributions; see
[
7
]
. This means that the magnetic field does not influence the existence of edge states. It however affect their quantitative features, see §
1.3
.

Our results in Theorems
1.1
and
6.2
concern weakly dispersive wavepackets with a macroscopic center
y_{t}
propagating along
\Gamma=\kappa^{-1}(0)
. Changing the metric (i.e. replacing
D
by
\big(D_{j}+\Gamma_{j}(x))g^{jk}(x)\big)\sigma_{k}
) would likely preserve this structure. However, adding an electric potential
V(x)
to
{\not{D}}
modifies the energy landscape, hence the interface
\Gamma=\kappa^{-1}(0)
between the two topological media cannot properly support wavepackets. These are likely to propagate instead within a thicker strip close to
\Gamma
, with splitting according to Landau–Zener transition rules.

2.
Gauge transformation

This section constructs a gauge function that reduces
{\not{D}}
to an operator with magnetic properties closer to those of the model operator (
1.11
).

2.1.
Equivalent tangent magnetic potential

We construct first a magnetic potential
\tilde{A}
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
such that
\nabla\times\tilde{A}=B
near
\Gamma
, and
\tilde{A}
vanishes along
\Gamma=\kappa^{-1}(0)
and is carried by the vector field
\tau
. In particular, the operator

(2.1)

{\tilde{\not{D}}}=\big(\varepsilon D_{1}-\tilde{A}_{1}(x)\big)\sigma_{1}+\big(\varepsilon D_{2}-\tilde{A}_{2}(x)\big)\sigma_{2}+\kappa(x)\sigma_{3}

will share many of the characteristics of the model (
1.11
): the magnetic potential is tangent to
\Gamma
and vanishes along
\Gamma
. Moreover, from magnetic equivalence between
A
and
\tilde{A}
,
{\not{D}}
and
{\tilde{\not{D}}}
are locally conjugated. We use below the notation (with Euclidean distance
d
)

(2.2)

U_{\eta}=\big\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}:d(x,\Gamma)<\eta\big\}.

Lemma 2.1
.

There exist
\eta>0
and a function
\beta\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{2},{\mathbb{R}})
vanishing on
\mathbb{R}^{2}\backslash U_{2\eta}
such that, defining
\tilde{A}:=\beta\kappa\tau
, we have

(2.3)

\nabla\times(A-\tilde{A})=\nabla\times(A-\beta\kappa\tau)=0\quad\text{ on }U_{\eta};\qquad\beta=\dfrac{B}{|\nabla\kappa|}\quad\text{ on }\Gamma.

Proof.

We first note that it suffices to construct
\beta
on
U_{\eta}
and extend it to
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
as a smooth function with support in
U_{2\eta}
. For
f
a smooth function, we observe that

(2.4)

\nabla\times(f\tau)=\nabla f\times\tau+f\nabla\times\tau=\partial_{n}f+f\nabla\times\tau,

where we used
\nabla f\times\tau=\partial_{1}f\tau_{2}-\partial_{2}f\tau_{1}=\partial_{n}f
. Let
B=\nabla\times A
. To find
\beta
such that
B=\nabla\times(\beta\kappa\tau)
, we first solve

(2.5)

\partial_{n}f+f\nabla\times\tau=B,\ \ \ \ f|_{\Gamma}=0.

We note that the coefficients of
\partial_{n}
are in
C^{\infty}_{b}
. In particular, the flow
e^{s\partial_{n}}(x)
is defined for all times. We now define the map
\Phi:\Gamma\times{\mathbb{R}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{2}

(2.6)

\Phi(x,s)=e^{s\partial_{n}}(x).

If

solves (
2.5
), then
{\tilde{f}}=f\circ\Phi
solves

(2.7)

\partial_{s}{\tilde{f}}+{\tilde{f}}\cdot(\nabla\times\tau)\circ\Phi=B\circ\Phi,\ \ \ \ {\tilde{f}}|_{\Gamma}=0.

This equation clearly admits a solution
{\tilde{f}}\in C^{\infty}(\Gamma\times{\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{R}})
. Moreover, note that
\partial_{n}
is transverse to
\Gamma
; that
|\nabla\kappa|
is uniformly bounded above and below; and that
\kappa\in C^{\infty}_{b}
,
\Phi
is a diffeomorphism from
\Gamma\times(-\delta,\delta)
to its range, which contains a neighborhood of the form
U_{\eta}
. Therefore,
{\tilde{f}}
induces the solution
f={\tilde{f}}\circ\Phi^{-1}\in C^{\infty}(U_{\eta},{\mathbb{R}})
of (
2.5
).

Because
f
vanishes on
\Gamma
and
\kappa
vanishes transversely on
\Gamma
(with
|\nabla\kappa|
bounded below), after potentially reducing
\eta

f=\beta\kappa
for some smooth function
\beta
on
U_{\eta}
. From the equation (
2.5
) and the identity (
2.4
), we conclude that

(2.8)

\nabla\times A=B=\partial_{n}f+f\nabla\times\tau=\nabla\times(f\tau)=\nabla\times(\beta\kappa\tau).

Moreover, using that
f
and
\kappa
vanish on
\Gamma
, we have along
\Gamma
:

(2.9)

B=\partial_{n}f=\partial_{n}(\beta\kappa)=\beta\partial_{n}\kappa=\beta|\nabla\kappa|.

This completes the proof.∎

Thanks to (
2.3

A
and
\tilde{A}
give rise to the same magnetic field on
U_{\eta}
, hence the difference
A-\tilde{A}
is locally a gradient field:

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\eta>0
given by Lemma
2.1
and
U\subset U_{\eta}
be a simply connected set. For any
(y_{0},\eta_{0})\in U\times{\mathbb{R}}
, there exists a unique
\chi_{0}\in C^{\infty}(U,{\mathbb{R}})
such that

(2.10)

\tilde{A}=A-\nabla\chi_{0}\quad\text{ in }U;\quad\text{ and }\quad\chi_{0}(y_{0})=\eta_{0}.

Proof of Lemma
2.2
.

Thanks to (
2.3
), we have
\nabla\times(A-\beta\kappa\tau)=0
on
U_{\eta}
, hence on
U
. Since
U
is simply connected, by Poincaré’s lemma there exists a unique
\chi_{0}\in C^{\infty}(U,{\mathbb{R}})
such that
\nabla\chi_{0}=A-\beta\kappa\tau
and
\chi(y_{0})=\eta_{0}
. ∎

It follows from Lemma

that the operators
{\not{D}}
and

defined in (

(2.11)

e^{-i\chi_{0}/\varepsilon}{\not{D}}e^{i\chi_{0}/\varepsilon}={\tilde{\not{D}}}.

2.2.
Global gauge

When
\Gamma
is simply connected, we can pick
U=U_{\eta}
in Lemma
2.2
, and the conjugation relation (
2.11
) holds on a full neighborhood of
\Gamma
. This however fails when
\Gamma
is a loop:
\chi_{0}
is only defined on part of
\Gamma
. We circumvent this obstacle by using instead a time-dependent gauge that follows the center of mass
y_{t}
of our wavepacket, i.e. defined on a set of the form

(2.12)

\Omega_{\delta}=\big\{(t,x),\ t\in{\mathbb{R}},\ |x-y_{t}|<\delta\big\}.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
\tilde{A}
be as defined in Lemma
2.1
. There exist
\delta>0
and
\chi\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2},{\mathbb{R}})
with support in
\Omega_{2\delta}
with
\chi(0,y_{0})=0
and such that for
(t,x)\in\Omega_{\delta}
:

(2.13)

\tilde{A}(x)=A(x)-\nabla\chi(t,x),\qquad\partial_{t}\chi(t,x)=0.

Proof.

As in the proof of Lemma
2.1
, it suffices to construct
\chi
on
\Omega_{\delta}
and to extend it to
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
as a smooth function with support in
\Omega_{2\delta}
. Without loss of generality, we can assume that
\Gamma
is connected. If
\Gamma
is also simply connected, then we simply take
\delta=\eta
and
\chi(t,x)=\chi_{0}(x)
. If
\Gamma
is not simply connected, then it is a loop; in particular it is compact.

Fix
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
. According to Lemma
2.2
, there exists
\delta_{t}>0
and a smooth function
\chi(t,\cdot)
defined on the ball
B(y_{t},\delta_{t})
such that

(2.14)

\tilde{A}(x)=A(x)-\nabla\chi(t,x),\quad x\in B(y_{t},\delta_{t});\qquad\chi(t,y_{t})=\int_{0}^{t}\dot{y_{s}}\cdot A(y_{s})ds.

Since
\Gamma
is compact, we can pick
\delta_{t}
independent of
t
; we write below
\delta=\delta_{t}
. Varying
t
, we obtain a uniquely defined function
\chi
on
\Omega_{\delta}
. We take time-derivative of both identities in (
2.14
). The first one yields
\nabla\partial_{t}\chi(t,x)=0
for
x\in B(y_{t},\delta)
. The second one gives

(2.15)

\partial_{t}\chi(t,y_{t})=\partial_{t}\big(\chi(t,y_{t})\big)-\dot{y_{t}}\cdot\nabla\chi(t,y_{t})=\dot{y_{t}}\cdot A(y_{t})-\dot{y_{t}}\cdot A(y_{t})=0,

where we used
\nabla\chi=\beta\kappa\tau-A
hence
\nabla\chi(t,y_{t})=A(y_{t})
. From these identities, we deduce that
\partial_{t}\chi(t,x)=0
for
x\in B(y_{t},\delta)
; in particular
\partial_{t}\chi
is smooth. Moreover,
\nabla\chi=A-\tilde{A}
is also smooth. This implies that
\chi
is smooth, which completes the proof. ∎

Thanks to (
2.13
), we have the relation

(2.16)

e^{-i\chi/\varepsilon}\big(\varepsilon D_{t}+{\not{D}}\big)e^{i\chi/\varepsilon}=\varepsilon D_{t}+{\tilde{\not{D}}}+R,\qquad\text{where:}

(2.17)

R=\partial_{t}\chi+(\partial_{1}\chi-A_{1}+\tilde{A}_{1})\sigma_{1}+(\partial_{2}\chi-A_{2}+\tilde{A}_{2})\sigma_{2}.

It follows from (
2.16
) that
\Psi
solves (
1.1
) if and only if
\tilde{\Psi}=e^{-i\chi/\varepsilon}\Psi
solves

(2.18)

\big(\varepsilon D_{t}+\tilde{\not{D}}+R\big)\tilde{\Psi}=0.

While
R
looks like a leading-order term, it will effectively be of order
\varepsilon^{\infty}
because it vanishes on
\Omega_{\delta}
– a domain where our wavepacket is concentrated. We will eventually treat it as a small source term in §
6
.

2.3.
Gauge expansion

We conclude this section with an expansion of
\chi
near
(t,y_{t})
, which will serve to prove Theorem
1.1
.

Lemma 2.4
.

We have:

(2.19)

\chi(t,y_{t})=\int_{0}^{t}\dot{y_{s}}\cdot A_{s}ds;\qquad\nabla\chi(t,y_{t})=A_{t};\qquad\nabla^{2}\chi(t,y_{t})=\nabla A^{\top}(y_{t})-B_{t}n_{t}\tau_{t}^{\top}.

Proof.

The first formula of (
2.19
) comes from the equation (
2.14
) defining
\chi
. The second one follows from

(2.20)

\nabla\chi(t,y_{t})=A(y_{t})-\beta(y_{t})\kappa(y_{t})\tau(y_{t})=A_{t}.

In order to see the last one, we notice that

(2.21)

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\chi(t,y_{t})

\displaystyle=\nabla\cdot\big(\nabla^{\top}\chi\big)(y_{t})=\nabla\big(A^{\top}-\beta\kappa\tau^{\top}\big)(y_{t})

(2.22)

\displaystyle=\nabla A^{\top}(y_{t})-\beta(y_{t})\nabla\kappa(y_{t})\tau^{\top}(y_{t})=\nabla A^{\top}(y_{t})-B_{t}n_{t}\tau_{t}^{\top},

where we used
\beta_{t}=B_{t}/r_{t}
on
\Gamma
, see (
2.3
). This completes the proof.
∎

3.
Local normal form

We now start our construction of approximate solutions to
(\varepsilon D_{t}+\tilde{\not{D}})\tilde{\Psi}=0
. This section describes a series of transformations that allows us to explicitly invert an operator that governs the leading dynamics: a rescaling of
\tilde{\Psi}
in natural coordinates, spatial and spinorial rotations, and finally a shifted partial Fourier transform.

3.1.
Spatial rescaling

We first write the wavepacket in natural coordinates:

(3.1)

\psi(t,z)=\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}S\tilde{\Psi}(t,z),

with the scaling transformation defined as

(3.2)

Sf(z)=f(y_{t}+\sqrt{\varepsilon}z),\qquad S^{-1}f(x)=f\left(\frac{x-y_{t}}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\right).

We observe that
\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}S
is an isometry on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, while formally,

(3.3)

Sf(z)=\sum_{\alpha}\frac{\varepsilon^{\frac{|\alpha|}{2}}z^{\alpha}}{\alpha!}\partial^{\alpha}f(y_{t})=\displaystyle\sum_{j\geq 0}\varepsilon^{\frac{j}{2}}S_{j}f(z),\quad S_{j}f(z):=\displaystyle\sum_{|\alpha|=j}\frac{z^{\alpha}}{\alpha!}\partial^{\alpha}f(y_{t}).

This scaling emerges from the following consideration. For
\kappa(x)=x_{2}
, the wavepacket is confined by a harmonic-like oscillator
\varepsilon D_{2}\sigma_{2}+x_{2}\sigma_{3}
and hence comes with a natural scale
\sqrt{\varepsilon}x_{2}
. This scale is then imposed to all directions when
\kappa
admits more sophisticated variations, i.e. when
\Gamma
is curved.

We then verify that
\psi(t,z)
solves

(3.4)

L\psi=0;\qquad L=\varepsilon^{-\frac{1}{2}}S(\varepsilon D_{t}+\tilde{\not{D}})S^{-1}=\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}D_{t}-\dot{y}_{t}\cdot D+(D+\varepsilon^{-\frac{1}{2}}Sh(z))\cdot\sigma

with
D=(D_{1},D_{2},0)^{t}
,
\sigma=(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3})^{t}
, and
h=(-\tilde{A}_{1},-\tilde{A}_{2},\kappa)^{t}=(-\beta\kappa\tau,\kappa)^{t}
. We still use
\dot{y}_{t}
for
(\dot{y}_{t},0)^{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
Using (
3.3
), we find formally
L=\sum_{j\geq 0}\varepsilon^{\frac{j}{2}}L_{j}
, where we observe that
L_{-1}=0
since
\tilde{A}
and hence
h
vanish on
\Gamma=\kappa^{-1}(0)
, and

(3.5)

\displaystyle L_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\dot{y}_{t}\cdot D+D\cdot\sigma+S_{1}h\cdot\sigma

(3.6)

\displaystyle L_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D_{t}+S_{2}h\cdot\sigma

(3.7)

\displaystyle L_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle S_{j+1}h\cdot\sigma=\displaystyle\sum_{|\alpha|=j+1}\frac{1}{\alpha!}z^{\alpha}\partial^{\alpha}h(y_{t})\cdot\sigma,\qquad j\geq 2.

Our next objective is to transform
L_{0}
in an appropriate basis so that its infinite dimensional kernel and its inverse on the orthogonal complement may be written explicitly.

3.2.
Rotations

We now introduce spatial and spinorial rotations already mentioned in the introduction. We define, for
1\leq j\leq 3
and
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
, the spinor rotation acting on
\mathbb{C}^{2}
:

(3.8)

U_{j,\theta}=e^{-i\frac{\theta}{2}\sigma_{j}}=\cos\left(\tfrac{\theta}{2}\right)-i\sin\left(\tfrac{\theta}{2}\right)\sigma_{j}.

Associated to it is a standard three-dimensional rotation acting on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
of angle
\theta
about the
j
-axis (with usual orientation)
\tilde{R}_{j,\theta}
such that for
c\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
:

(3.9)

\tilde{R}_{j,\theta}c\cdot\sigma=c\cdot U_{j,\theta}^{*}\sigma U_{j,\theta},\qquad U_{j,\theta}^{*}\sigma U_{j,\theta}=:\tilde{R}_{j,\theta}^{*}\sigma.

Note that

(3.10)

U_{k,\theta}^{*}\sigma_{j}U_{k,\theta}=\delta_{j\not=k}\cos\theta\sigma_{j}+\epsilon_{jkl}\sin\theta\sigma_{l}+\delta_{jk}\sigma_{j}.

From this, we deduce two formulas that we will use later:

(3.11)

\tilde{R}_{2,\varphi_{t}}=\left[\begin{matrix}\cos\varphi_{t}&0&-\sin\varphi_{t}\\
0&1&0\\
\sin\varphi_{t}&0&\cos\varphi_{t}\end{matrix}\right]=\left[\begin{matrix}c_{t}&0&-s_{t}\\
0&1&0\\
s_{t}&0&c_{t}\end{matrix}\right],\qquad\tilde{R}_{3,\theta_{t}}=\left[\begin{matrix}R_{\theta_{t}}&0\\
0&1\end{matrix}\right],

where
R_{\theta_{t}}
is the spatial rotation defined in (
1.8
).
We finally define the
L^{2}
-unitary transform related to the spatial rotations

(3.12)

\mathcal{R}_{\theta_{t}}f=f\circ R_{\theta_{t}}

and the operator

(3.13)

\operatorname{U}_{t}=U_{2,\varphi_{t}}U_{3,\theta_{t}}\mathcal{R}_{\theta_{t}}.

When
\varphi_{t}\equiv 0
(i.e., when
B=0
), this transformation already appears in
[
2
]
.
We now compute the operators
\operatorname{U}_{t}^{*}L_{j}\operatorname{U}_{t}
. We start with preliminary relations:

Lemma 3.1
.

We find:

(3.14)

\operatorname{U}_{t}^{*}(-\dot{y}_{t}\cdot D)\operatorname{U}_{t}=c_{t}D_{1},\quad\operatorname{U}_{t}^{*}D\cdot\sigma\operatorname{U}_{t}=(c_{t}\sigma_{1}+s_{t}\sigma_{3})D_{1}+\sigma_{2}D_{2},\quad

(3.15)

\operatorname{U}_{t}^{*}D_{t}\operatorname{U}_{t}=D_{t}-\frac{\dot{\varphi}_{t}}{2}\sigma_{2}-\frac{\dot{\theta}_{t}}{2}(-s_{t}\sigma_{1}+c_{t}\sigma_{3})-\dot{\theta_{t}}(z_{1}D_{2}-z_{2}D_{1}).

Moreover
\operatorname{U}_{t}^{*}S_{j}h\cdot\sigma\operatorname{U}_{t}
is a multiplication operator by a time-dependent homogeneous polynomial of degree
j
in
z
; and for
j=1,2
:

(3.16)

\operatorname{U}_{t}^{*}S_{1}h\cdot\sigma\operatorname{U}_{t}=\rho_{t}z_{2}\sigma_{3},\quad\operatorname{U}_{t}^{*}S_{2}h\cdot\sigma\operatorname{U}_{t}=-r_{t}c_{t}z_{2}\left\langle z,\nabla\beta(y_{t})\right\rangle+q_{t}(z)\cdot\sigma

where
q_{t}\cdot\sigma
is a matrix-valued quadratic form in
z
carried by
\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3}
.

Proof.

1. Space differentiation. We first observe that

(3.17)

\mathcal{R}_{\theta_{t}}^{*}D\mathcal{R}_{\theta_{t}}=\tilde{R}_{3,{\theta_{t}}}^{*}D,\quad\mathcal{R}_{\theta_{t}}^{*}D\cdot v\mathcal{R}_{\theta_{t}}=D\cdot\tilde{R}_{3,{\theta_{t}}}v.

Applied to
v=-\dot{y}_{t}
with
R_{\theta_{t}}\dot{y}_{t}=-c_{t}e_{1}
by construction, we find
-\tilde{R}_{3,{\theta_{t}}}\dot{y}_{t}\cdot D=c_{t}D_{1}
. Since spinorial rotations commute with scalars, we obtain the first relation in (
3.14
).

We now apply (
3.17
) to
v=\sigma
and find

(3.18)

(\mathcal{R}_{\theta_{t}}U_{3,{\theta_{t}}})^{*}D\cdot\sigma\mathcal{R}_{\theta_{t}}U_{3,{\theta_{t}}}=D\cdot\sigma.

Therefore, using (
3.11
), we find

(3.19)

\operatorname{U}_{t}^{*}D\cdot\sigma\operatorname{U}_{t}=U_{2,\varphi_{t}}^{*}D\cdot\sigma U_{2,\varphi_{t}}=D\cdot\tilde{R}_{2,\varphi_{t}}^{*}\sigma=(c_{t}\sigma_{1}+s_{t}\sigma_{3})D_{1}+\sigma_{2}D_{2}.

This proves the second identity in (
3.14
).

2. Time differentiation. We note that

(3.20)

\mathcal{R}_{\theta_{t}}^{*}D_{t}\mathcal{R}_{\theta_{t}}=D_{t}-\dot{\theta_{t}}Jz\cdot D_{z},\quad U_{3,\theta_{t}}^{*}D_{t}U_{3,\theta_{t}}=D_{t}-\frac{\dot{\theta}_{t}}{2}\sigma_{3},\quad U_{2,\varphi_{t}}^{*}D_{t}U_{2,\varphi_{t}}=D_{t}-\frac{\dot{\varphi}_{t}}{2}\sigma_{2}.

We deduce that

(3.21)

\displaystyle\operatorname{U}_{t}^{*}D_{t}\operatorname{U}_{t}

\displaystyle=U_{2,\varphi_{t}}^{*}\Big(D_{t}-\frac{\dot{\theta}_{t}}{2}\sigma_{3}\Big)U_{2,\varphi_{t}}-\dot{\theta_{t}}(z_{1}D_{2}-z_{2}D_{1})

(3.22)

\displaystyle=D_{t}-\frac{\dot{\varphi}_{t}}{2}\sigma_{2}-\frac{\dot{\theta}_{t}}{2}(-s_{t}\sigma_{1}+c_{t}\sigma_{3})-\dot{\theta_{t}}(z_{1}D_{2}-z_{2}D_{1}).

This proves (
3.15

3. Multiplicative operators. We observe that
\mathcal{R}^{*}_{\theta_{t}}Sh\mathcal{R}_{\theta_{t}}=h(y_{t}+\sqrt{\varepsilon}R_{\theta_{t}}z)
as a multiplication operator so that

(3.23)

\operatorname{U}_{t}^{*}Sh\cdot\sigma\operatorname{U}_{t}=\tilde{R}_{2,\varphi_{t}}\tilde{R}_{3,\theta_{t}}h(y_{t}+\sqrt{\varepsilon}R_{\theta_{t}}z)\cdot\sigma=\sum_{j\geq 1}\varepsilon^{\frac{j}{2}}\displaystyle\sum_{|\alpha|=j}z^{\alpha}\nu_{\alpha}\cdot\sigma,

where we have defined the
3-
vectors
\nu_{\alpha}=\frac{1}{\alpha!}\tilde{R}_{2,\varphi_{t}}\tilde{R}_{3,\theta_{t}}(R_{-\theta_{t}}\nabla)^{\alpha}h(y_{t})
. Indeed,

(3.24)

h(y_{t}+\sqrt{\varepsilon}R_{\theta}z)=\displaystyle\sum_{j\geq 1}\frac{1}{j!}(\sqrt{\varepsilon}z\cdot R_{-\theta}\nabla)^{j}h(y_{t})=\displaystyle\sum_{j\geq 1}\varepsilon^{\frac{j}{2}}\displaystyle\sum_{|\alpha|=j}\frac{z^{\alpha}}{\alpha!}(R_{-\theta}\nabla)^{\alpha}h(y_{t})

using multinomial coefficients. Therefore,
S_{j}h\cdot\sigma
is a multiplicative operator by a homogeneous polynomial of degree
j
depending on
t

\operatorname{U}_{t}^{*}S_{j}h\cdot\sigma\operatorname{U}_{t}
is also a homogeneous polynomial of degree
j
.

We now focus on
j=1
. Define
\tilde{h}=(-\beta\tau,1)^{t}
so that
h=\kappa\tilde{h}
.
Since
\kappa(y_{t})=0
, we have
S_{1}h=\tilde{h}(y_{t})\cdot S_{1}\kappa

(3.25)

\operatorname{U}_{t}^{*}S_{1}h\cdot\sigma\operatorname{U}_{t}=\mathcal{R}_{\theta_{t}}^{*}S_{1}\kappa\mathcal{R}_{\theta_{t}}\ \operatorname{U}_{t}^{*}\tilde{h}(y_{t})\cdot\sigma\operatorname{U}_{t}.

We now compute separately
\mathcal{R}_{\theta_{t}}^{*}S_{1}\kappa\mathcal{R}_{\theta_{t}}
and
\operatorname{U}_{t}^{*}\tilde{h}(y_{t})\cdot\sigma\operatorname{U}_{t}
(note that
\mathcal{R}_{\theta_{t}}
does not technically affect that last term since
\tilde{h}(y_{t})
does not depend on
z
). We have:

(3.26)

\mathcal{R}_{\theta_{t}}^{*}S_{1}\kappa\cdot\sigma\mathcal{R}_{\theta_{t}}=\mathcal{R}_{\theta_{t}}^{*}z\cdot\nabla\kappa(y_{t})\mathcal{R}_{\theta_{t}}=z\cdot R_{\theta_{t}}\nabla\kappa(y_{t})=r_{t}z_{2}.

Moreover, since
B_{t}=\beta_{t}r_{t}
, we have
\tilde{h}(y_{t})=(-r_{t}^{-1}B_{t}\tau_{t},1)^{t}
hence
R_{3,\theta_{t}}\tilde{h}=(r_{t}^{-1}B_{t},0,1)
. We deduce

(3.27)

U_{3,\theta_{t}}^{*}\tilde{h}(y_{t})\cdot\sigma U_{3,\theta_{t}}=R_{3,\theta_{t}}\tilde{h}(y_{t})\cdot\sigma=\dfrac{B_{t}\sigma_{1}}{r_{t}}+\sigma_{3}=\dfrac{\rho_{t}}{r_{t}}(s_{t}\sigma_{1}+c_{t}\sigma_{3}).

By (
3.11

U_{2,\varphi_{t}}^{*}(s_{t}\sigma_{1}+c_{t}\sigma_{3})U_{2,\varphi_{t}}=\sigma_{3}
, which is the main motivation for the definition of

(3.28)

\operatorname{U}_{t}^{*}\tilde{h}(y_{t})\cdot\sigma\operatorname{U}_{t}=U_{2,\varphi_{t}}^{*}U_{3,\theta_{t}}^{*}\tilde{h}(y_{t})\cdot\sigma U_{3,\theta_{t}}U_{2,\varphi_{t}}=\dfrac{\rho_{t}}{r_{t}}\sigma_{3}.

Multiplying (
3.26

3.28

3.25

(3.29)

\operatorname{U}_{t}^{*}S_{1}h\cdot\sigma\operatorname{U}_{t}=\rho_{t}z_{2}\sigma_{3}.

We go on with the case
j=2
. We compute
\operatorname{U}_{t}^{*}S_{2}h\cdot\sigma\operatorname{U}_{t}
. We recall that
h=\kappa\tilde{h}
, therefore (using again

(3.30)

S_{2}h=2(S_{1}\kappa)(S_{1}\tilde{h})+(S_{2}\kappa)\tilde{h}(y_{t}).

We start with the contribution of
(S_{2}\kappa)\tilde{h}(y_{t})
, i.e. the term
\operatorname{U}_{t}^{*}(S_{2}\kappa)\tilde{h}(y_{t})\cdot\sigma\operatorname{U}_{t}
. Using that

(3.31)

\operatorname{U}_{t}^{*}(S_{2}\kappa)\tilde{h}(y_{t})\operatorname{U}_{t}=\mathcal{R}_{\theta_{t}}^{*}(S_{2}\kappa)\mathcal{R}_{\theta_{t}}\ \operatorname{U}_{t}^{*}\tilde{h}(y_{t})\cdot\sigma\operatorname{U}_{t}=\mathcal{R}_{\theta_{t}}^{*}(S_{2}\kappa)\mathcal{R}_{\theta_{t}}\dfrac{\rho_{t}}{r_{t}}\sigma_{3},

where we used (
3.28

\sigma_{3}
.

We now focus on
\operatorname{U}_{t}^{*}(S_{1}\kappa)(S_{1}\tilde{h}(y_{t})\cdot\sigma)\operatorname{U}_{t}
.
Using (
3.26

(3.32)

\operatorname{U}_{t}^{*}(S_{1}\kappa)(S_{1}\tilde{h})\cdot\sigma\operatorname{U}_{t}=\mathcal{R}_{\theta_{t}}^{*}(S_{1}\kappa)\mathcal{R}_{\theta_{t}}\,\operatorname{U}_{t}(S_{1}\tilde{h})\cdot\sigma\operatorname{U}_{t}=r_{t}z_{2}\,\operatorname{U}_{t}(S_{1}\tilde{h})\cdot\sigma\operatorname{U}_{t}.

It remains to compute
\operatorname{U}_{t}(S_{1}\tilde{h})\cdot\sigma\operatorname{U}_{t}
. We recall that
\tilde{h}=(-\beta\tau,1)^{t}
. Moreover,
\tau
has unit norm:
\left\langle\tau,\tau\right\rangle=1
. Taking derivatives of this expression shows that
\partial_{j}\tau
is normal to
\tau

\partial_{j}\tau=\alpha_{j}n

(3.33)

\displaystyle(S_{1}\tilde{h})\cdot\sigma

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j,k=1}^{2}z_{j}\partial_{j}(\beta\tau_{k})\sigma_{k}=-\sum_{j,k=1}^{2}z_{j}(\partial_{j}\beta)\tau_{k}\sigma_{k}-\beta\sum_{j,k=1}^{2}z_{j}\partial_{j}\tau_{k}\sigma_{k}

(3.34)

\displaystyle=-(S_{1}\beta)\tau\cdot\sigma-\beta\sum_{j,k=1}^{2}z_{j}\alpha_{j}n_{k}\sigma_{k}=-(S_{1}\beta)\tau\cdot\sigma-\left\langle z,\alpha\right\rangle n\cdot\sigma.

We observe that:

(3.35)

U_{3,\theta_{t}}^{*}\tau\cdot\sigma U_{3,\theta_{t}}=R_{3,\theta_{t}}\tau\cdot\sigma=-\sigma_{1},\qquad U_{2,\varphi_{t}}^{*}\sigma_{1}U_{2,\varphi_{t}}=R_{2,\varphi_{t}}e_{1}\cdot\sigma=c_{t}\sigma_{1}-s_{t}\sigma_{3};

(3.36)

U_{3,\theta_{t}}^{*}n\cdot\sigma U_{3,\theta_{t}}=R_{3,\theta_{t}}n\cdot\sigma=\sigma_{2},\qquad U_{2,\varphi_{t}}^{*}\sigma_{2}U_{2,\varphi_{t}}=\sigma_{2}.

Therefore, the following equality is valid modulo terms carried by
\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3}
:

(3.37)

\operatorname{U}_{t}(S_{1}\tilde{h})\cdot\sigma\operatorname{U}_{t}=\mathcal{R}_{\theta_{t}}^{*}S_{1}\beta\mathcal{R}_{\theta_{t}}c_{t}\sigma_{1}=c_{t}\left\langle z,R_{\theta_{t}}\nabla\beta(y_{t})\right\rangle\sigma_{1}.

Going back to (
3.30
), (
3.31
) and (
3.32
), we conclude that – again with equality valid modulo terms carried by
\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3}
:

(3.38)

\displaystyle\operatorname{U}_{t}^{*}S_{2}h\cdot\sigma\operatorname{U}_{t}=\operatorname{U}_{t}^{*}(S_{1}\kappa)(S_{1}\tilde{h})\cdot\sigma\operatorname{U}_{t}=-c_{t}r_{t}z_{2}\left\langle z,R_{\theta_{t}}\nabla\beta(y_{t})\right\rangle\sigma_{1}.

This completes the proof of (
3.16
), hence of the lemma.
∎

Thanks to Lemma
3.1
, we can describe
\operatorname{U}_{t}^{*}L_{j}\operatorname{U}_{t}
. For
j\geq 2
,
\operatorname{U}_{t}^{*}L_{j}\operatorname{U}_{t}
is a multiplication operator by a homogeneous polynomial of degree
j
. For
j=0,1
, we have explicit expressions:

(3.39)

\displaystyle\operatorname{U}_{t}^{*}L_{0}\operatorname{U}_{t}

\displaystyle=c_{t}(1+\sigma_{1})D_{1}+\sigma_{2}D_{2}+(\rho_{t}z_{2}+s_{t}D_{1})\sigma_{3};

(3.40)

\displaystyle\operatorname{U}_{t}^{*}L_{1}\operatorname{U}_{t}

\displaystyle=D_{t}-\dot{\theta_{t}}(z_{1}D_{2}-z_{2}D_{1})+\left(\dfrac{\dot{\theta_{t}}s_{t}}{2}+j_{t}z_{1}z_{2}+k_{t}z_{2}^{2}\right)\sigma_{1}+E_{1},

where
E_{1}
is a multiplication operator by a polynomial of degree two and carried by
\sigma_{2}
and
\sigma_{3}
(we will see later that it does not contribute to leading order). The coefficients
j_{t}
and
k_{t}
are explicitly given in terms of
\beta
:

(3.41)

\left[\begin{matrix}j_{t}\\
k_{t}\end{matrix}\right]=r_{t}c_{t}R_{\theta_{t}}\nabla\beta(y_{t})=r_{t}c_{t}\left[\begin{matrix}-\partial_{\tau}\beta(y_{t})\\
\partial_{n}\beta(y_{t})\end{matrix}\right].

We end this section with a few important relations:

(3.42)

j_{t}=-r_{t}\dot{\beta_{t}};\quad j_{t}\gamma_{t}=\dfrac{d\ln c_{t}}{dt};\quad k_{t}=\dfrac{c_{t}}{2}\left(\partial_{n}B(y_{t})-B_{t}\dfrac{\Delta\kappa(y_{t})}{r_{t}}\right).

Proof of (
3.42
).

We start with the first identity in (
3.42
). We recall that
\dot{y_{t}}=c_{t}\tau_{t}
. Since
\partial_{\tau}
is tangent to
\Gamma
and
\beta=B/|\nabla\kappa|
along
\Gamma
:

(3.43)

j_{t}=-r_{t}c_{t}\partial_{\tau}\beta(y_{t})=-r_{t}c_{t}\tau_{t}\cdot\nabla\left(\dfrac{B}{|\nabla\kappa|}\right)(y_{t})=-r_{t}\dot{y_{t}}\cdot\nabla\left(\dfrac{B}{|\nabla\kappa|}\right)(y_{t})=-r_{t}\dot{\beta_{t}}.

Regarding the second identity: using (
3.43
) and
\beta_{t}=B_{t}/r_{t}
on
\Gamma
, we obtain:

(3.44)

j_{t}\gamma_{t}=-\dfrac{r_{t}B_{t}}{r_{t}^{2}+B_{t}^{2}}\dot{\beta_{t}}=-\dfrac{\beta_{t}\dot{\beta_{t}}}{1+\beta_{t}^{2}}=-\dfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{d}{dt}\ln\big(1+\beta_{t}^{2}\big)=-\dfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{d}{dt}\ln\frac{1}{c_{t}^{2}}=\dfrac{d\ln c_{t}}{dt}.

For the last identity, we first note that
|\nabla\kappa|=\partial_{n}\kappa
. Since
f=\beta\kappa
solves (
2.5
):

(3.45)

B=\partial_{n}f+f\nabla\times\tau=\partial_{n}(\beta\kappa)+\beta\kappa\nabla\times\tau=\kappa\partial_{n}\beta+\beta\partial_{n}\kappa+\beta\kappa\nabla\times\tau.

Therefore, since
\kappa
vanishes on
\Gamma
, we deduce that on
\Gamma
:

(3.46)

\partial_{n}B=2\partial_{n}\kappa\partial_{n}\beta+\beta\big(\partial_{n}^{2}\kappa+\partial_{n}\kappa\nabla\times\tau\big)=2|\nabla\kappa|\partial_{n}\beta+B\left(\dfrac{\partial_{n}^{2}\kappa}{|\nabla\kappa|}+\nabla\times\tau\right).

Moreover, using
|\nabla\kappa|=\partial_{n}\tau
and
\nabla f\times J\nabla g=\nabla f\cdot\nabla g
:

(3.47)

\nabla\times\tau=\dfrac{\nabla\times J\nabla\kappa}{|\nabla\kappa|}+\nabla\dfrac{1}{|\nabla\kappa|}\cdot J\nabla\kappa=\dfrac{\Delta\kappa}{|\nabla\kappa|}-\dfrac{\partial_{n}^{2}\kappa}{|\nabla\kappa|}.

Plugging (
3.47
) into (
3.46
) and using the defining equation
\kappa_{t}=r_{t}c_{t}\partial_{n}\beta(y_{t})
yield (
3.42
).
∎

3.3.
Shifted Fourier transform

We finally introduce the
L^{2}-
unitary transformation

(3.48)

\mathcal{V}_{t}a(z)=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{iz_{1}\xi}a(\xi,z_{2}+\gamma_{t}\xi)d\xi.

Its inverse is given explicitly by

(3.49)

\mathcal{V}_{t}^{*}a(\xi,\zeta)=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-iz_{1}\xi}a(z_{1},\zeta-\gamma_{t}\xi)dz_{1}.

Above,
\xi
is the one-dimensional dual Fourier variable to
z_{1}
while the one-dimensional variable
\zeta
is a shift of
z_{2}
given by
\zeta=z_{2}+\gamma_{t}\xi
.
This shifted partial Fourier transform, which reduces to a partial Fourier transform in the first variable
z_{1}\to\xi
when
B=0
and introduces an additional shift
\gamma_{t}\xi
to
z_{2}
otherwise is reminiscent of the parametrization of Landau level eigenfunctions in a Landau gauge when
B\not=0
.
The construction of our wavepackets involves many functions of the form
a(t,\xi,\zeta)=f(t,\xi)\phi(\zeta)
,
with
f
describing the wavepacket profile along
\Gamma
while
\phi
describes the wavepacket profile across
\Gamma
.

The global transformation we need to consider is the composition of
\operatorname{U}_{t}
introduced in the preceding section and
\mathcal{V}_{t}
given above. We thus set
\mathfrak{U}_{t}=\operatorname{U}_{t}\mathcal{V}_{t}
. The operator in this new set of variables is
T:=\mathfrak{U}_{t}^{*}L\mathfrak{U}_{t}
with
L
defined in (
3.4
). We similarly define
T_{j}=\mathfrak{U}_{t}^{*}L_{j}\mathfrak{U}_{t}
for
j\geq 0
. We will also be using
D_{\xi}=-i\partial_{\xi}
and
D_{\zeta}=-i\partial_{\zeta}
.

In
T_{1}
, it will turn out that the terms carried by
\sigma_{2}
or
\sigma_{3}
and the terms that are odd in
(D_{\zeta},\zeta)
do not appear to leading order in our expansion. Therefore, we use the notation

(3.50)

P_{1}\equiv P_{2}

if
P_{1}-P_{2}
is a differential operator made of odd terms in
(\zeta,D_{\zeta})
and terms carried by
\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

With
\mathfrak{U}_{t}=\operatorname{U}_{t}{\mathcal{V}}_{t}
and
T_{j}=\mathfrak{U}_{t}^{*}L_{j}\mathfrak{U}_{t}
, we have:

(3.51)

\displaystyle T_{0}

\displaystyle=c_{t}(1+\sigma_{1})\xi+D_{\zeta}\sigma_{2}+\rho_{t}\zeta\sigma_{3},

(3.52)

\displaystyle T_{1}

\displaystyle\equiv D_{t}+\dot{\theta_{t}}\gamma_{t}\big(D_{\zeta}^{2}-\xi^{2}\big)+\Big(\dfrac{\dot{\theta_{t}}s_{t}}{2}+j_{t}\gamma_{t}(D_{\xi}\xi-D_{\zeta}\zeta)+k_{t}\big(\zeta^{2}+\gamma_{t}^{2}\xi^{2}\big)\Big)\sigma_{1}.

Moreover
T_{j}
is a linear combination of differential operators of the form
\xi^{k}\zeta^{\ell}D_{\xi}^{m}D_{\zeta}^{j+1-k-\ell-m}
, with
k,l\geq 0
and coefficients bounded with respect to
t
.

Proof.

We note that
\mathcal{V}_{t}
satisfies the canonical relations

(3.53)

{\mathcal{V}}_{t}^{*}(z_{2}+\gamma_{t}D_{1}){\mathcal{V}}_{t}=\zeta,\quad{\mathcal{V}}_{t}^{*}D_{2}{\mathcal{V}}_{t}=D_{\zeta},\quad{\mathcal{V}}_{t}^{*}D_{1}{\mathcal{V}}_{t}=\xi,\quad\mathcal{V}_{t}^{*}z_{1}\mathcal{V}_{t}=-(D_{\xi}+\gamma_{t}D_{\zeta}).

We deduce (
3.51
) for
T_{0}=\mathfrak{U}_{t}^{*}L_{0}\mathfrak{U}_{t}
from the expression (
3.39
). Thanks to (
3.53
) we obtain
{\mathcal{V}}_{t}^{*}z_{2}{\mathcal{V}}_{t}=\zeta-\gamma_{t}\xi
, hence using the notation
\equiv
:

(3.54)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{V}}_{t}^{*}z_{1}z_{2}{\mathcal{V}}_{t}

\displaystyle=-(D_{\xi}+\gamma_{t}D_{\zeta})(\zeta-\gamma_{t}\xi)\equiv\gamma_{t}(D_{\xi}\xi-D_{\zeta}\zeta)

(3.55)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{V}}_{t}^{*}z_{2}^{2}{\mathcal{V}}_{t}

\displaystyle=(\zeta-\gamma_{t}\xi)^{2}\equiv\zeta^{2}+\gamma_{t}^{2}\xi^{2}

(3.56)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{V}}_{t}^{*}(z_{1}D_{2}-z_{2}D_{1}){\mathcal{V}}_{t}

\displaystyle=-(D_{\xi}+\gamma_{t}D_{\zeta})D_{\zeta}-(\zeta-\gamma_{t}\xi)\xi\equiv-\gamma_{t}(D_{\zeta}^{2}-\xi^{2}).

Moreover, we observe that

(3.57)

\mathcal{V}_{t}^{*}D_{t}\mathcal{V}_{t}=D_{t}+\dot{\gamma}_{t}\xi D_{\zeta}\equiv D_{t}.

We deduce from (
3.40
) the expression for
T_{1}
:

(3.58)

\displaystyle T_{1}\equiv D_{t}+\dot{\theta_{t}}\gamma_{t}(D_{\zeta}^{2}-\xi^{2})+\Big(\dfrac{\dot{\theta_{t}}s_{t}}{2}+j_{t}\gamma_{t}(D_{\xi}\xi-D_{\zeta}\zeta)+k_{t}\big(\zeta^{2}+\gamma_{t}^{2}\xi^{2}\big)\Big)\sigma_{1}.

Finally, since
L_{j}
is multiplication operator by a homogeneous polynomial of degree
j+1
,
T_{j}
is a linear combination of differential operators of the form
\xi^{k}\zeta^{\ell}D_{\xi}^{m}D_{\zeta}^{j+1-k-\ell-m}
, with coefficients bounded with respect to
t
. This completes the proof.
∎

3.4.
Conjugation and asymptotic expansion

Introducing
a=\mathfrak{U}_{t}^{*}\psi
and recalling that
T=\mathfrak{U}_{t}^{*}L\mathfrak{U}_{t}
, equation (
3.4
) is equivalent to
Ta=0
. With
L_{j}
given in (
3.5
)–(
3.7
) and
T_{j}=\mathfrak{U}_{t}^{*}L_{j}\mathfrak{U}_{t}
, we decompose
a
as
a=\sum_{j\geq 0}\varepsilon^{\frac{j}{2}}a_{j}
to reduce the equation
Ta=0
to the triangular system

(3.59)

T_{0}a_{0}=0,\quad T_{1}a_{0}+T_{0}a_{1}=0,\quad\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{j}T_{j-k}a_{k}=0,\ j\geq 2.

In §
4
we identify the kernel of
T_{0}
and we produce explicit formula for its inverse on the orthogonal complement. We describe how to solve the subleading equation
T_{1}a_{0}+T_{0}a_{1}=0
in §
5
. We finally present the higher-order asymptotic expansions and corresponding error estimates in §
6
.

4.
Inversion of leading operator

To study the kernel of the operator

(4.1)

T_{0}=c_{t}(1+\sigma_{1})\xi+D_{\zeta}\sigma_{2}+\rho_{t}\zeta\sigma_{3},

and invert it on the orthogonal complement, we first bring it to a normal form. Thanks to the change of variables
(\xi,\zeta)\mapsto(\rho_{t}^{1/2}c_{t}^{-1}\xi,\rho_{t}^{-1/2}\zeta)
, we can assume that
\rho_{t}=c_{t}=1
. Moreover, with
Q=Q^{*}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{3})
, we have

(4.2)

Q\sigma_{1}Q=\sigma_{3},\quad Q\sigma_{2}Q=-\sigma_{2},\quad Q\sigma_{3}Q=\sigma_{1}.

It suffices then to work with the model operator

(4.3)

H=\xi(1+\sigma_{3})+\sigma_{1}\zeta-D_{\zeta}\sigma_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}2\xi&\mathfrak{a}_{\zeta}\\
\mathfrak{a}_{\zeta}^{*}&0\end{pmatrix},\quad\mathfrak{a}_{\zeta}=\partial_{\zeta}+\zeta,

instead of
T_{0}
. Multiplication by
2\xi
may also be written as
\mathfrak{a}_{\xi}+\mathfrak{a}_{\xi}^{*}
with
\mathfrak{a}_{\xi}=\partial_{\xi}+\xi
.

4.1.
Functional setting.

To study the operator
H
, we use the standard basis of Hermite functions on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
given by

(4.4)

h_{0}(\zeta)=\pi^{-\frac{1}{4}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\zeta^{2}},\qquad h_{n}(\zeta)=\dfrac{1}{2^{\frac{n}{2}}\sqrt{n!}}(\mathfrak{a}_{\zeta}^{*})^{n}h_{0}(\zeta),\ n\geq 1.

They satisfy the relations

(4.5)

\mathfrak{a}_{\zeta}h_{0}=0;\qquad\mathfrak{a}_{\zeta}h_{n}=\sqrt{2n}h_{n-1},\ n\geq 1;\qquad\mathfrak{a}_{\zeta}^{*}h_{n}=\sqrt{2n+2}h_{n+1},\ n\geq 0.

With the decomposition
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\ni\psi(\zeta)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\psi_{n}h_{n}(\zeta)
, we may then define

(4.6)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{a}_{\zeta}^{-1}\psi(\zeta)=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2n}}\psi_{n-1}h_{n}(\zeta),\quad

\displaystyle\psi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})

(4.7)

\displaystyle(\mathfrak{a}_{\zeta}^{*})^{-1}\psi(\zeta)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2n+2}}\psi_{n+1}h_{n}(\zeta),\quad

\displaystyle\psi\in({\rm Ker}\,\mathfrak{a}_{\zeta})^{\perp}=\overline{{\rm Ran}\,\mathfrak{a}_{\zeta}^{*}}=\{\psi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R});\ \psi_{0}=0\}.

A natural scale of Hilbert spaces for
p\in\mathbb{N}
associated to this decomposition is
\mathcal{S}_{p}(\mathbb{R})=\{\psi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R});(\mathfrak{a}_{\zeta}^{*})^{p}\psi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\}
endowed with the norm

(4.8)

\displaystyle\|\psi\|^{2}_{p}=\|(\mathfrak{a}_{\zeta}^{*})^{p}\psi\|^{2}_{0}\ \cong\ \|\psi\|^{2}_{0}+\|D_{\zeta}^{p}\psi\|_{0}^{2}+\|\zeta^{p}\psi\|^{2}_{0},

where
\|\cdot\|_{0}
is the usual
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})-
norm and where
a\cong b
when there exists
C_{p}>0
such that
C_{p}^{-1}a\leq b\leq C_{p}a
. That the two above expressions for the norm are equivalent may be obtained by induction from the classical result

(4.9)

\displaystyle\|\mathfrak{a}_{\zeta}^{*}\psi\|^{2}_{0}=\|D_{\zeta}\psi\|^{2}_{0}+\|\zeta\psi\|^{2}_{0}+\|\psi\|^{2}_{0}

for
\psi\in\mathcal{S}_{1}(\mathbb{R})
. Note that
\mathcal{S}_{0}(\mathbb{R})\equiv L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.

With the decomposition
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\ni\psi(\xi,\zeta)=\sum_{m,n\geq 0}\psi_{mn}h_{m}(\xi)h_{n}(\zeta)
in two-dimensional spaces, we similarly define
\mathcal{S}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})=\{\psi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2});(\mathfrak{a}_{\xi}^{*})^{p}\psi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\mbox{ and }(\mathfrak{a}_{\zeta}^{*})^{p}\psi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\}
endowed with the norms

(4.10)

\displaystyle\|\psi\|^{2}_{p}=\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{p}\|(\mathfrak{a}_{\xi}^{*})^{j}(\mathfrak{a}_{\zeta}^{*})^{p-j}\psi\|^{2}_{0}\ \cong\ \|(1+|\xi|^{p}+|\zeta|^{p})\psi\|^{2}_{0}+\|D_{\xi}^{p}\psi\|_{0}^{2}+\|D_{\zeta}^{p}\psi\|_{0}^{2}.

For vector-valued functions, we also define the spaces
\mathcal{S}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{C}^{q})
component-wise. Below we simply write
\mathcal{S}_{p}
when the domain
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and range
\mathbb{C}^{q}
are clear and
\|\cdot\|_{p}
the associated norm.

4.2.
Inversion of model operator.

As the lemma below demonstrates, the kernel
N\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathbb{C}^{2})
of
H
and its orthogonal complement are given by

(4.11)

\displaystyle N=

\displaystyle\left\{\psi(\xi,\zeta)=f(\xi)h_{0}(\zeta)\left[\begin{matrix}0\\
1\end{matrix}\right],\ f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\right\},

(4.12)

\displaystyle N^{\perp}=

\displaystyle\left\{\psi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathbb{C}^{2}),\ \big(\psi_{2}(\xi,\cdot),h_{0}(\cdot)\big)_{2}=0,\ \forall\xi\in\mathbb{R}\right\},

where
(\cdot,\cdot)_{2}
is the standard
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})-
inner product in the second variable
\zeta
and
\psi=(\psi_{1},\psi_{2})^{t}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

For
p\in\mathbb{N}
, we define the bounded linear operator
H^{-1}
from
N^{\perp}\cap\mathcal{S}_{p+1}
to
N^{\perp}\cap\mathcal{S}_{p}
as

(4.13)

\displaystyle H^{-1}=\left[\begin{matrix}0&(\mathfrak{a}_{\zeta}^{*})^{-1}\\
\mathfrak{a}_{\zeta}^{-1}&-\mathfrak{a}_{\zeta}^{-1}2\xi(\mathfrak{a}_{\zeta}^{*})^{-1}\end{matrix}\right].

All solutions
\psi\in\mathcal{S}_{p}
of the equation
H\psi=g
for
g\in N^{\perp}\cap\mathcal{S}_{p+1}
are of the form
\psi=H^{-1}g+\psi_{0}
with
\psi_{0}
arbitrary in
N\cap\mathcal{S}_{p}
. In particular
{\rm Ker}\,H=N
on
\mathcal{S}_{0}
.

Proof.

The system
H\psi=g
is equivalent to
\mathfrak{a}^{*}_{\zeta}\psi_{1}=g_{2}
and
\mathfrak{a}_{\zeta}\psi_{2}+2\xi\psi_{1}=g_{1}
. Using (
4.6
), we find for
(g_{2},h_{0}(\zeta))_{2}=0
, i.e.,
g\in N^{\perp}
, that
\psi_{1}=(\mathfrak{a}^{*}_{\zeta})^{-1}g_{2}
and then
\psi_{2}=\mathfrak{a}_{\zeta}^{-1}(g_{1}-2\xi\psi_{1})
and hence the above result. We then note that
\mathfrak{a}_{\zeta}^{-1}
has range in
N^{\perp}
.

That
\psi\in\mathcal{S}_{p}
when
g\in\mathcal{S}_{p+1}
follows directly from the fact that the following operators are bounded:
\mathfrak{a}_{\zeta}^{-1}:\mathcal{S}_{p+1}\to\mathcal{S}_{p+1}\cap N^{\perp}
,
(\mathfrak{a}_{\zeta}^{*})^{-1}:\mathcal{S}_{p+1}\cap N^{\perp}\to\mathcal{S}_{p+1}
, as well as
\xi:\mathcal{S}_{p+1}\to\mathcal{S}_{p}
.

Any function
\psi\in\mathcal{S}_{p}
solution of
H\psi=g
may be decomposed as
\psi_{0}+\psi_{1}
with
\psi_{0}\in N
and
\psi_{1}\in N^{\perp}
. Since
H\psi_{0}=0
and hence
\psi_{1}=H^{-1}g
,
\psi_{0}
is arbitrary in
{\rm Ker}\,H=N
.
∎

4.3.
Microscopic balance.

Our geometric assumptions impose that
C^{-1}\geq\rho_{t}\geq C>0
and that
0<c_{0}\leq c_{t}<1
for constants
C
and
c_{0}
independent of
t\geq 0
.

For a fixed time
t
, we now solve the equation
T_{0}a=b
.
We introduce

(4.14)

\phi_{t}(\zeta):=\left(\frac{\rho_{t}}{4\pi}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}e^{-\frac{\rho_{t}}{2}\zeta^{2}}\left[\begin{matrix}1\\
-1\end{matrix}\right],\qquad N_{t}={\rm Ker}\ T_{0}=\big\{f(\xi)\phi_{t}(\zeta);\ f\in\mathcal{S}_{0}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{C})\big\}.

The normalization implies that
\|\phi_{t}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}^{2})}=1
. That
N_{t}
is the kernel of
T_{0}
comes from Lemma
4.1
and the change of variables given in the last section.
We still denote by
\mathcal{S}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathbb{C}^{2})
the spaces of functions in the
(\xi,\zeta)
variables, which are equivalent to the corresponding spaces in the variables
(\rho_{t}^{-1}c_{t}\xi,\rho_{t}^{1/2}\zeta)
by assumption on
(c_{t},\rho_{t})
.

The leading order equation is then solved as follows.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
b\in N_{t}^{\perp}\cap\mathcal{S}_{p+1}
. The equation
T_{0}a=b
admits a unique solution
T_{0}^{-1}b:=a\in N_{t}^{\perp}\cap\mathcal{S}_{p}
with inverse operator given explicitly by

(4.15)

\displaystyle T_{0}^{-1}=Q\left[\begin{matrix}0&(\rho_{t}\zeta-\partial_{\zeta})^{-1}\\
(\rho_{t}\zeta+\partial_{\zeta})^{-1}&-2\xi c_{t}(\rho_{t}\zeta+\partial_{\zeta})^{-1}(\rho_{t}\zeta-\partial_{\zeta})^{-1}\end{matrix}\right]Q,\quad Q=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{3}).

All solutions of that equation in
\mathcal{S}_{p}
are of the form
a=T_{0}^{-1}b+f(\xi)\phi(\zeta)
for arbitrary
f(\xi)\in\mathcal{S}_{p}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C})
.

Proof.

This is a corollary of Lemma
4.1
and the invertible transform from
T_{0}
to
H
.
The operator
T_{0}
is thus invertible on the orthogonal complement of
\phi_{t}(\zeta)
with
T_{0}a=b
solvable if and only if
(b,\phi_{t})_{2}=0
in which case all solutions are given by
a=T_{0}^{-1}b+f(\xi)\phi_{t}
for
f
arbitrary in
\mathcal{S}_{p}
.
∎

The solution to the leading equation
T_{0}a_{0}=0
is therefore given by

(4.16)

a_{0}(t,\xi,\zeta)=f_{0}(t,\xi)\phi_{t}(\zeta)

with
f_{0}(t,\xi)
arbitrary at this level.

5.
Transport equation

Lemma
4.2
states that the subleading equation in (
3.59
),
T_{0}a_{1}=-T_{1}a_{0}
, admits a solution if and only if
T_{1}a_{0}(t,\cdot)\in N_{t}^{\perp}
for every
t
. From the expressions (
4.16
) of
a_{0}
and (
4.14
) of
N_{t}
and
\phi_{t}(\zeta)
, we deduce that
T_{0}a_{1}=-T_{1}a_{0}
is solvable if and only if
\mathcal{T}f_{0}=0
, where

(5.1)

\mathcal{T}f(t,\xi):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi_{t}(\zeta)\cdot T_{1}[f(t,\xi)\phi_{t}(\zeta)]\ d\zeta.

In this section we provide an explicit expression for
\mathcal{T}
and for the solutions to
\mathcal{T}f_{0}=0
and more generally
\mathcal{T}f_{0}=g
. We also provide a functional setting to analyze the map
g\to f_{0}
when the dispersion is strongest.

5.1.
Derivation of the transport operator

Lemma 5.1
.

We have the identity

(5.2)

\mathcal{T}=D_{t}-\dfrac{k_{t}}{2\rho_{t}}-j_{t}\gamma_{t}\dfrac{\xi D_{\xi}+D_{\xi}\xi}{2}-\left(\dot{\theta_{t}}\gamma_{t}+k_{t}\gamma_{t}^{2}\right)\xi^{2}.

Proof.

We first observe that

(5.3)

\left\langle\left[\begin{matrix}1\\
-1\end{matrix}\right],\sigma_{j}\left[\begin{matrix}1\\
-1\end{matrix}\right]\right\rangle=-2\delta_{1j}.

Therefore, the terms in
T_{1}
that are carried by
\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3}
do not contribute to
\mathcal{T}
. Likewise, we observe that

(5.4)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi(\zeta)\cdot\zeta\phi(\zeta)d\zeta=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi(\zeta)\cdot D_{\zeta}\phi(\zeta)d\zeta=0.

Hence, for the purpose of computing
\mathcal{T}
, we can ignore terms in
T_{1}
carried by
\sigma_{2}
or
\sigma_{3}
, and terms linear in
(\zeta,D_{\zeta})
. In other words, we can replace
T_{1}
in (
5.1
) by the right hand side of (
3.52
):

(5.5)

D_{t}+\dot{\theta_{t}}\gamma_{t}\big(D_{\zeta}^{2}-\xi^{2}\big)+\left(\dfrac{\dot{\theta_{t}}s_{t}}{2}+j_{t}\gamma_{t}(D_{\xi}\xi-D_{\zeta}\zeta)+k_{t}\big(\zeta^{2}+\gamma_{t}^{2}\xi^{2}\big)\right)\sigma_{1}.

We note moreover that

(5.6)

D_{\zeta}\zeta\phi=i(\rho_{t}\zeta^{2}-1)\phi,\quad D_{\zeta}^{2}\phi(\zeta)=(\rho_{t}-\rho_{t}^{2}\zeta^{2})\phi(\zeta),\ \ \ \ D_{t}\phi(\zeta)=i\dfrac{\dot{\rho_{t}}}{2}\zeta^{2}\phi(\zeta)-i\dfrac{\dot{\rho_{t}}}{4\rho_{t}}\phi(\zeta).

Therefore, we deduce that

(5.7)

\begin{array}[]{l}\mathcal{T}=\Big(\dfrac{\rho_{t}}{\pi}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-\rho_{t}\zeta^{2}}\Big(D_{t}+i\dfrac{\dot{\rho_{t}}}{2}\zeta^{2}-i\frac{1}{4}\frac{\dot{\rho}_{t}}{\rho_{t}}+\dot{\theta_{t}}\gamma_{t}\big(\rho_{t}-\rho_{t}^{2}\zeta^{2}-\xi^{2}\big)\\[8.53581pt]
\qquad-\Big(\dfrac{\dot{\theta_{t}}s_{t}}{2}+j_{t}\gamma_{t}(D_{\xi}\xi-i\rho_{t}\zeta^{2}+i)+k_{t}\big(\zeta^{2}+\gamma_{t}^{2}\xi^{2}\big)\Big)\Big)d\zeta.\end{array}

Furthermore, we have

(5.8)

\left(\frac{\rho_{t}}{\pi}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-\rho_{t}\zeta^{2}}d\zeta=1,\ \ \ \ \left(\frac{\rho_{t}}{\pi}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\zeta^{2}e^{-\rho_{t}\zeta^{2}}d\zeta=\dfrac{1}{2\rho_{t}}.

Hence, after performing the integration and realizing that the coefficients involving
\dot{\rho}_{t}
cancel out, we obtain the formula

(5.9)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}

\displaystyle=D_{t}+\dot{\theta_{t}}\gamma_{t}\left(\dfrac{\rho_{t}}{2}-\xi^{2}\right)-\dfrac{\dot{\theta_{t}}s_{t}}{2}-j_{t}\gamma_{t}\left(D_{\xi}\xi+\dfrac{i}{2}\right)-k_{t}\left(\dfrac{1}{2\rho_{t}}+\gamma_{t}^{2}\xi^{2}\right)

(5.10)

\displaystyle=D_{t}-\dot{\theta_{t}}\gamma_{t}\xi^{2}-j_{t}\gamma_{t}\dfrac{\xi D_{\xi}+D_{\xi}\xi}{2}-k_{t}\left(\dfrac{1}{2\rho_{t}}+\gamma_{t}^{2}\xi^{2}\right)

(5.11)

\displaystyle=D_{t}-\dfrac{k_{t}}{2\rho_{t}}-j_{t}\gamma_{t}\dfrac{\xi D_{\xi}+D_{\xi}\xi}{2}-\left(\dot{\theta_{t}}\gamma_{t}+k_{t}\gamma_{t}^{2}\right)\xi^{2},

where in the second line we used
\gamma_{t}\rho_{t}=s_{t}
.
∎

5.2.
Solving the transport equation

To produce an explicit solution of the transport equation
\mathcal{T}f=0
we define
(\lambda_{t},\mu_{t},\nu_{t})
such that

(5.12)

\lambda_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\dfrac{k_{s}}{2\rho_{s}}ds,\qquad\nu_{t}=2\int_{0}^{t}\dfrac{c_{0}^{2}}{c_{s}^{2}}\left(\dot{\theta_{s}}\gamma_{s}+k_{s}\gamma_{s}^{2}\right)ds,\qquad e^{\mu_{t}}=\frac{c_{t}}{c_{0}}.

Lemma 5.2
.

The solution to
\mathcal{T}f=0
is given by

(5.13)

f(t,\xi)=\exp\left(i\lambda_{t}+i\dfrac{\nu_{t}}{2}\left(e^{\mu_{t}}\xi\right)^{2}\right)e^{\frac{\mu_{t}}{2}}f\left(0,e^{\mu_{t}}\xi\right).

Proof.

1. We recall that the self-adjoint operator
\frac{1}{2}(\xi D_{\xi}+D_{\xi}\xi)
generates the semigroup of (
L^{2}-
unitary) dilations; that is, for
F
independent of
t
:

(5.14)

\left(D_{\mu}-\dfrac{\xi D_{\xi}+D_{\xi}\xi}{2}\right)U_{\mu}F=0,\quad U_{\mu}F(\xi)=e^{\frac{\mu}{2}}F\big(e^{\mu}\xi\big).

We note that
e^{\mu_{0}}=1
; moreover, thanks to (
3.42
), we have
\dot{\mu_{t}}=\partial_{t}\ln(c_{t})=j_{t}\gamma_{t}
. Therefore, by the chain rule and (
5.14
), we have

(5.15)

U_{\mu_{t}}^{-1}\left(D_{t}-j_{t}\gamma_{t}\dfrac{\xi D_{\xi}+D_{\xi}\xi}{2}\right)U_{\mu_{t}}=D_{t}.

2. We deduce, using
U_{\mu_{t}}^{-1}\xi^{2}U_{\mu_{t}}=e^{-2\mu_{t}}\xi^{2}
, that

(5.16)

\displaystyle U_{\mu_{t}}^{-1}\mathcal{T}U_{\mu_{t}}

\displaystyle=D_{t}-\dfrac{k_{t}}{2\rho_{t}}-e^{-2\mu_{t}}\left(\dot{\theta_{t}}\gamma_{t}+k_{t}\gamma_{t}^{2}\right)\xi^{2}=D_{t}-\dot{\lambda_{t}}-\dfrac{1}{2}\dot{\nu_{t}}\xi^{2}=e^{i\lambda_{t}+i\nu_{t}\frac{\xi^{2}}{2}}D_{t}e^{-i\lambda_{t}-i\nu_{t}\frac{\xi^{2}}{2}},

where we used the relations (
5.12
) for
\lambda_{t}
and
\nu_{t}
. For the formula (
5.14
) for
U_{\mu}
, we deduce that the solution to
\mathcal{T}f=0=\mathcal{T}U_{\mu_{t}}e^{i\lambda_{t}+i\frac{\nu_{t}}{2}\xi^{2}}g
is
D_{t}g=0
and hence

(5.17)

f(t,\cdot)=U_{\mu_{t}}e^{i\lambda_{t}+i\frac{\nu_{t}}{2}\xi^{2}}f(0,\cdot).

Since
e^{\mu_{t}}=c_{t}/c_{0}
, we conclude that

(5.18)

f(t,\xi)=e^{\frac{\mu_{t}}{2}}\exp\left(i\lambda_{t}+i\frac{\nu_{t}}{2}\left(e^{\mu_{t}}\xi\right)^{2}\right)f\left(0,e^{\mu_{t}}\xi\right).

This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 5.3

(i) With

Hence,

(5.24)

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{t}\tilde{a}_{0}(e^{\mu_{t}}z_{1},\frac{z_{2}}{e^{\mu_{t}}})

\displaystyle=\left(\dfrac{\rho_{t}}{4\pi}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{iz_{1}\xi}\exp\left(i\dfrac{\nu_{t}}{2}\xi^{2}-\frac{\rho_{t}e^{-2\mu_{t}}}{2}(z_{2}+\gamma_{t}\xi)^{2}\right)\hat{f}\left(\xi\right)\dfrac{e^{-\frac{\mu_{t}}{2}}d\xi}{\sqrt{2\pi}}.

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{t}\tilde{a}_{0}(e^{\mu_{t}}z_{1},z_{2})=(f*_{1}G_{t})(z_{1},e^{\mu_{t}}z_{2})

\displaystyle G_{t}(z_{1},e^{\mu_{t}}z_{2})

\displaystyle=\left(\dfrac{\rho_{t}}{4\pi}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}e^{\frac{-\mu_{t}}{2}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\rho_{t}z_{2}^{2}}\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\tilde{Q}_{t}\xi^{2}}e^{i\xi(z_{1}+i\rho_{t}\gamma_{t}e^{-\mu_{t}}z_{2})}\dfrac{d\xi}{\sqrt{2\pi}},\qquad\tilde{Q}_{t}=\rho_{t}\gamma_{t}^{2}e^{-2\mu_{t}}-i\nu_{t}

\displaystyle\sup_{z\in{\mathbb{R}}}\big|\mathcal{V}_{t}\tilde{a}_{0}(z)\big|

5.4.
Dispersion-dependent functional setting

The solution of
\mathcal{T}f=0
with given initial condition admits an explicit expression as we saw in Lemma
5.2
. In the analysis of the asymptotic expansion of
\psi(t,z)
in powers of
\varepsilon
, we need to solve transport equations of the form
\mathcal{T}f=g
with time-dependent source terms. To quantify the stability of the inverse transport operators and that of other relevant transforms, we introduce the following functional setting.

We recall the functional spaces
\mathcal{S}_{p}
were defined in section
4.1
. To handle the time-dependence for
t\in[0,{\rm T}]
of the wavepackets, we introduce the spaces for
p\in\mathbb{N}
and
k=\lfloor\frac{p}{2}\rfloor\in\mathbb{N}
(i.e.,
p=2k
or
p=2k+1
) defined for an interval
{\rm I}\subset\mathbb{R}
by

(5.34)

\mathbb{S}_{p}({\rm I},\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{C}^{q})=\cap_{r=0}^{k}C^{r}({\rm I};\mathcal{S}_{p-2r}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{C}^{q}))

with norm given by the sum of the natural norms for the above spaces (see (
5.37
) below). The spaces are constructed so that any derivative in time corresponds to a loss of order
2
in the remaining variable.

To quantify the effects of dispersion, we define

(5.35)

\displaystyle\langle\nu\rangle_{{\rm T}}=1+\sup_{0\leq t\leq{\rm T}}|\nu_{t}|\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\varsigma=\langle\nu\rangle_{{\rm T}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

We saw in Lemma
5.2
that dispersion resulted in a multiplication operator of the form
\phi_{t}(\xi)=e^{i\frac{1}{2}\nu(t)\xi^{2}}
.
The operator
D_{t}\phi_{t}=\phi_{t}(D_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\nu^{\prime}(t)\xi^{2})
. This explains why the spaces
\mathbb{S}_{p}
are constructed so that both
\partial_{t}
and
\xi^{2}
map
\mathbb{S}_{p+2}
to
\mathbb{S}_{p}
.

Similarly,
\partial_{\xi}\phi_{t}=\phi_{t}(\partial_{\xi}+i\nu(t)\xi)
, so that (the operator of multiplication by)
\phi_{t}
is large as an operator on
\mathbb{S}_{1}
when
\langle\nu\rangle_{{\rm T}}
is.
Yet clearly,
\phi_{t}^{2}
is comparable to
\phi_{t}
in the same sense. Since the construction of our wavepackets requires repeated application of operators of the form
\phi_{t}
, we introduce scaled metrics on
\mathcal{S}_{p}
and
\mathbb{S}_{p}
so that application of
\phi_{t}
results in a bounded operation independent of
\langle\nu\rangle_{{\rm T}}
.

This is simply achieved by replacing
\mathfrak{a}_{\xi}^{*}=-\partial_{\xi}+\xi
by
\mathfrak{a}_{\xi\varsigma}^{*}=-\varsigma\partial_{\xi}+\varsigma^{-1}\xi
and endowing the spaces
\mathcal{S}_{p}(\mathbb{R})
and
\mathcal{S}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
respectively with the norms

(5.36)

\displaystyle\|\psi\|^{2}_{p\varsigma}=\|(\mathfrak{a}_{\xi\varsigma}^{*})^{p}\psi\|^{2}_{0}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\|\psi\|_{p\varsigma}^{2}=\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{p}\|(\mathfrak{a}_{\xi\varsigma}^{*})^{j}(\mathfrak{a}_{\zeta}^{*})^{p-j}\psi\|^{2}_{0}.

We call
\mathcal{S}_{p\varsigma}
the spaces
\mathcal{S}_{p}
endowed with these dispersion-scaled norms.

The space
\mathbb{S}_{p}({\rm I},\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{C})
are similarly endowed for
d=1,2
with the norms (and called
\mathbb{S}_{p\varsigma}
)

(5.37)

\displaystyle\|\psi\|^{2}_{p\varsigma}=\sup_{t\in{\rm I}}\displaystyle\sum_{r=0}^{\lfloor\frac{p}{2}\rfloor}\|(\mathfrak{a}_{\xi\varsigma}^{*})^{p-2r}\partial_{t}^{r}\psi\|^{2}_{0}\ \mbox{ and }\ \|\psi\|_{p\varsigma}^{2}=\sup_{t\in{\rm I}}\displaystyle\sum_{r=0}^{\lfloor\frac{p}{2}\rfloor}\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{p-2r}\|(\mathfrak{a}_{\xi\varsigma}^{*})^{j}(\mathfrak{a}_{\zeta}^{*})^{p-2r-j}\partial_{t}^{r}\psi\|^{2}_{0}.

Spaces of vector-valued functions are similarly constructed componentwise.

Note that
\varsigma^{p}\|f\|_{p}\lesssim\|f\|_{p\varsigma}\lesssim\varsigma^{-p}\|f\|_{p}
. Here and below, we use the notation
a\lesssim b
to mean the existence of a
\varsigma-
independent constant
C
such that
a\leq Cb
.
Here is a number of dispersion-dependent estimates we will be using.

Lemma 5.4
.

All operator bounds below are meant to be
\varsigma
-independent bounds.

Any operator
B\in\{\varsigma D_{\xi},\varsigma^{-1}\xi,D_{\zeta},\zeta\}
is bounded from
\mathcal{S}_{p+1,\varsigma}
to
\mathcal{S}_{p\varsigma}
and from
\mathbb{S}_{p+1,\varsigma}
to
\mathbb{S}_{p\varsigma}
. The operator
\partial_{t}
is bounded from
\mathbb{S}_{p+2,\varsigma}
to
\mathbb{S}_{p\varsigma}
.

Let
\phi(\xi)=e^{i\frac{1}{2}\nu\xi^{2}}
with
|\nu|\lesssim\langle\nu\rangle_{{\rm T}}
. Then the operator of multiplication by
\phi(\xi)
is bounded from
\mathcal{S}_{p\varsigma}
to itself.

Let
\phi(t,\xi)=e^{i\frac{1}{2}\nu(t)\xi^{2}}
with
\sup_{t\in{\rm I}}|\nu(t)|\lesssim\langle\nu\rangle_{{\rm T}}
and
\sup_{t\in{\rm I}}|\nu^{(j)}(t)|\leq C_{j}
for
j\geq 1
. Then the operator of multiplication by
\phi(t,\xi)
is bounded from
\mathbb{S}_{p\varsigma}
to itself.

Proof.

The first statement comes from the construction of the spaces since
\mathfrak{a}^{*}_{\xi\varsigma}
controls
\varsigma\partial_{\xi}
and
\varsigma^{-1}\xi
in the sense that

(5.38)

\displaystyle\|\mathfrak{a}^{*}_{\xi\varsigma}\psi\|_{0}^{2}=\varsigma^{2}\|\partial_{\xi}\psi\|_{0}^{2}+\varsigma^{-2}\|\xi\psi\|_{0}^{2}+\|\psi\|^{2}_{0}.

One obtains from (
5.37
) for
p\geq 2
that
\|\partial_{t}\psi\|^{2}_{p\varsigma}\lesssim\|\psi\|^{2}_{p-2,\varsigma}
and hence the bound on
\partial_{t}
.

Consider now the operator of multiplication by
\phi(\xi)
in one dimension
d=1
. We wish to show that

(5.39)

\displaystyle\|\phi\psi\|_{p\varsigma}^{2}=\|(\mathfrak{a}_{\xi\varsigma}^{*})^{p}(\phi\psi)\|^{2}_{0}\lesssim\|(\mathfrak{a}_{\xi\varsigma}^{*})^{p}\psi\|^{2}_{0}=\|\psi\|_{p\varsigma}^{2}.

This holds when
p=0
. Assume it holds for
p-1\geq 0
. Then

(5.40)

\displaystyle\|\phi\psi\|_{p\varsigma}^{2}=\|(\mathfrak{a}_{\xi\varsigma}^{*})^{p-1}(\mathfrak{a}_{\xi\varsigma}^{*})(\phi\psi)\|^{2}_{0}=\|(\mathfrak{a}_{\xi\varsigma}^{*})^{p-1}(\phi\psi_{1})\|^{2}_{0}\lesssim\|\psi_{1}\|_{p-1,\varsigma}^{2}

by induction hypothesis, where
\psi_{1}(\xi)=(\mathfrak{a}_{\xi\varsigma}^{*}-i\varsigma\nu\xi)\psi
. By construction of the functional spaces and the above result for the operator
\varsigma^{-1}\xi
knowing that
\varsigma|\nu|\lesssim\varsigma^{-1}
, we find
\|\psi_{1}\|_{p-1,\varsigma}^{2}\lesssim\|\psi\|_{p,\varsigma}^{2}
and the result is proved. The same proof applies in two dimensions
d=2
as well using the norm for
\mathcal{S}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
in (
5.36
) and that the commutator
[\mathfrak{a}_{\zeta}^{*},\phi]=0
.

The proof in the time-dependent setting uses that

(5.41)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{r}(\phi\psi)=\phi\big(\partial_{t}+i\frac{1}{2}\nu^{\prime}(t)\xi^{2}\big)^{r}\psi=\phi\psi_{r},\qquad\psi_{r}=\big(\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{r}\nu_{j}(t)\xi^{2(r-j)}\partial_{t}^{j}\big)\psi

for smooth and bounded functions
\nu_{j}(t)
independent of
\langle\nu\rangle_{{\rm T}}
by assumption on
\nu(t)
. Assume dimension
d=1
as
d=2
is treated similarly. Using that
\xi^{2(r-j)}\partial_{t}^{j}
maps
\mathbb{S}_{p\varsigma}
to
\mathbb{S}_{p-2r,\varsigma}
for each
0\leq j\leq r
and the bounds proved above in the time-independent setting, we find

(5.42)

\displaystyle\|(\mathfrak{a}_{\xi\varsigma}^{*})^{p-2r}\partial_{t}^{r}\phi\psi\|_{0}=\|(\mathfrak{a}_{\xi\varsigma}^{*})^{p-2r}\phi\psi_{r}\|_{0}\lesssim\|\psi_{r}\|_{p-2r,\varsigma}\lesssim\|\psi\|_{p\varsigma}.

The explicit expression of the norms in (
5.37
) and the above estimate conclude the proof of the lemma.
∎

5.5.
Stability of the transport and other operators

We then have the following stability result for the transport equation
\mathcal{T}f=g
. We recall that
\varsigma=\langle\nu\rangle_{{\rm T}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}
.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
p\in\mathbb{N}
. The solution
f_{0}(t,\xi)
of
\mathcal{T}f_{0}=g
on
[0,{\rm T}]\times\mathbb{R}
with initial condition
f_{0}(0,\xi)=\hat{f}(\xi)\in\mathcal{S}_{p\varsigma}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C})
and source term
g\in\mathbb{S}_{p\varsigma}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C})
satisfies the estimate

(5.43)

\|f_{0}\|_{p\varsigma}\leq C_{p}\big(\|\hat{f}\|_{p\varsigma}+\langle{\rm T}\rangle\|g\|_{p\varsigma}\big)

where
\langle{\rm T}\rangle=1+{\rm T}
and
C_{p}
is independent of
{\rm T}
.

Proof.

We first adapt Lemma
5.2
to handle volume sources and define

(5.44)

\lambda_{s,t}=\int_{s}^{t}\frac{k_{\tau}}{2\rho_{\tau}}d\tau,\qquad\tilde{\nu}_{s,t}=2\displaystyle\int_{s}^{t}\frac{c_{t}^{2}}{c_{\tau}^{2}}\big(\dot{\theta}_{\tau}\gamma_{\tau}+k_{\tau}\gamma_{\tau}^{2})d\tau,\qquad e^{\mu_{s,t}}=\frac{c_{t}}{c_{s}},

to obtain that the solution to
\mathcal{T}f=0
with initial condition

is given by

(5.45)

f(t,\xi)=\exp\left(i\lambda_{s,t}+i\frac{\tilde{\nu}_{s,t}}{2}\xi^{2}\right)e^{\mu_{s,t}/2}f\left(s,e^{\mu_{s,t}}\xi\right).

By an application of the Duhamel principle, the solution to
\mathcal{T}f=g
for
0\leq t\leq T
with
f(0,\xi)=0
is thus given explicitly by

(5.46)

f(t,\xi)=i\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\exp\Big(i\lambda_{s,t}+i\frac{1}{2}\tilde{\nu}_{s,t}\xi^{2}\Big)e^{\mu_{s,t}/2}g(s,e^{\mu_{s,t}}\xi)ds.

Consider the solution in (
5.45
) at a fixed time
s
with the explicit time-dependence
\lambda(t)=\lambda_{s,t}
,
e^{\mu(t)}=e^{\mu_{s,t}}
and
\nu(t)=\tilde{\nu}_{s,t}
to simplify notation. We prove the lemma for the operator

(5.47)

\displaystyle f(t,\xi)\to e^{i\lambda(t)}e^{\frac{\mu(t)}{2}}e^{i\frac{1}{2}\nu(t)\xi^{2}}f(t,e^{\mu(t)}\xi).

The term
e^{i\lambda(t)}
generates a smooth in time modulation. Since
\mu(t)
is smooth and bounded above and below by positive constants independent of
t
,
e^{\mu(t)/2}
is also smooth. The operator
f(t,\xi)\to e^{\frac{\mu(t)}{2}}e^{i\lambda(t)}f(t,\xi)
is therefore bounded in the norms
\|\cdot\|_{p\varsigma}
.

All other time dependent coefficients are smooth and uniformly bounded in time independent of
{\rm T}
except for
\nu(t)
that may grow linearly with
\nu^{\prime}(t)
uniformly bounded. Consider the operator
f(t,\xi)\mapsto h(t,\xi)=f(t,e^{\mu(t)}\xi)
with
\hat{f}\in\mathbb{S}_{p\varsigma}
. Plugging
h(t,\xi)
into the definition of the norms (
5.37
), we directly obtain that
\|h\|_{p\varsigma}\lesssim\|f\|_{p\varsigma}
.

The final transformation (with
\nu(t)
replaced by
\nu(t)e^{-2\mu(t)}
)

(5.48)

\displaystyle f(t,\xi)\mapsto h(t,\xi)=f(t,\xi)e^{i\frac{1}{2}\nu(t)\xi^{2}}

was analyzed in Lemma
5.4
. This concludes the analysis of the map from
\hat{f}(\xi)
to
f_{0}(t,\xi)
.

The volume source term
g(t,\xi)
is treated similarly by the Duhamel principle, with an additional possible integration in time that provides the extra multiplication by
\langle{\rm T}\rangle
. This concludes the proof of the lemma.
∎

6.
Asymptotic expansion and error estimates

We recall that our objective is to construct approximations of solutions
\Psi(t,x)
of the Dirac equation (
1.1
). Our first step was to perform a gauge transformation
\tilde{\Psi}(t,x)=e^{-i\chi(t,x)/\varepsilon}\Psi(t,x)
replacing the Dirac operator
{\not{D}}
by
\tilde{\not{D}}+R
in (
2.18
). Since
R
in (
2.17
) is a negligible perturbation to arbitrary order in
\varepsilon
, our second step was to look for wavepackets
\tilde{\Psi}(t,x)
in the kernel of
\varepsilon D_{t}+\tilde{\not{D}}
.

Wavepackets in natural coordinates
\psi(t,z)=\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}S\tilde{\Psi}(t,z)
with the scaling
S
defined in (
3.2
) then solve
L\psi=0
in (
3.4
). Further transformations resulted in the definition of
a(\xi,\zeta)=\mathfrak{U}_{t}^{*}\psi(\xi,\zeta)
, where
\mathfrak{U}_{t}=\operatorname{U}_{t}\mathcal{V}_{t}
with
\operatorname{U}_{t}
defined in (
3.13
) and
\mathcal{V}_{t}
in (
3.48
). The problem
L\psi=0
is then equivalent to
Ta=0
for
T=\mathfrak{U}_{t}^{*}L\mathfrak{U}_{t}
. Writing
L=\sum_{j\geq 0}\varepsilon^{\frac{j}{2}}L_{j}
with
L_{j}
presented in (
3.5
)-(
3.7
), we have a corresponding expansion
T=\sum_{j\geq 0}\varepsilon^{\frac{j}{2}}T_{j}
with
T_{j}=\mathfrak{U}_{t}^{*}L_{j}\mathfrak{U}_{t}
.

6.1.
Construction of the asymptotic wavepacket.

Using the notation recalled above, we now construct approximations
a^{J}=\sum_{j=0}^{J}\varepsilon^{\frac{j}{2}}a_{j}
of
a(t,\xi,\zeta)
solution of
Ta=0
. Plugging the expansion for
a^{J}
in the equation
Ta^{J}=0
, using
T=\sum_{j\geq 0}\varepsilon^{\frac{j}{2}}T_{j}
, and equating like powers of
\varepsilon
gives the sequence of equations (
3.59
), which we recall here:

(6.1)

\sum_{k=0}^{j}T_{k}a_{j-k}=0,\qquad 0\leq j\leq J.

We solve these equations in turn.

The leading order equation
T_{0}a_{0}=0
combined with Lemma
4.2
shows that
a_{0}(t,\xi,\zeta)=f_{0}(t,\xi)\phi_{t}(\zeta)
with
\phi_{t}
defined in (
4.14
). The compatibility condition for the next equation
T_{0}a_{1}=-T_{1}a_{0}
implies
\mathcal{T}f_{0}=0
with the transport operator
\mathcal{T}
defined in Lemma
5.2
. For a fixed initial condition
f_{0}(0,\xi)=\hat{f}(\xi)
, Lemma
5.5
provides a unique solution
f_{0}(t,\xi)
of
\mathcal{T}f_{0}=0
and a leading term
a_{0}(t,\xi,\zeta)=f_{0}(t,\xi)\phi_{t}(\zeta)
.

Consider next the construction of
a_{1}
, solution to
T_{0}a_{1}+T_{1}a_{0}=0
. It is given according to Lemma
4.2
by

(6.2)

a_{1}=-T_{0}^{-1}T_{1}a_{0}+f_{1}\phi_{t}

with
f_{1}
arbitrary at this stage. We will consider regularity properties in a lemma below.

We now extend the construction to higher-order approximations.
Let
j\geq 2
and assume
a_{k}
for
0\leq k\leq j-1
constructed except for
f_{j-1}
. In order to define
a_{j}
, we impose the compatibility condition

(6.3)

\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{j}T_{k}a_{j-k},\phi_{t}\Big)_{2}=0\quad\mbox{ or equivalently }\quad\mathcal{T}f_{j-1}=g_{j-1},\quad g_{j-1}=-\Big(\sum_{k=2}^{j}T_{k}a_{j-k},\phi_{t}\Big)_{2}.

We recall that
(\cdot,\cdot)_{2}
is the standard inner product in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}^{2})
in the
\zeta
variable. By lemma
5.5
, this is a well-posed transport equation for
f_{j-1}
. Then, by lemma
4.2
,

(6.4)

a_{j}=T_{0}^{-1}\big(-\sum_{k=1}^{j}T_{k}a_{j-k}\big)+f_{j}\phi_{t}.

This constructs
a_{j}
for
0\leq j\leq J
iteratively and we set
f_{J}=0
for concreteness.
This concludes the construction of the approximation

(6.5)

\displaystyle a^{J}=\sum_{j=0}^{J}\varepsilon^{\frac{j}{2}}a_{j}

of formal order
\varepsilon^{\frac{J+1}{2}}
of
a
solution of
Ta=0
.

Note that the initial conditions for
a_{j}(0,\xi,\zeta)
for
j\geq 1
are defined implicitly by the above construction. Only
\hat{f}(\xi)
in the initial condition
a_{0}(0,\xi,\zeta)=\hat{f}(\xi)\phi_{t}(\zeta)
is prescribed. Our construction aims to propagate wavepackets that belong to an appropriate (non-dispersive) branch of continuous spectrum of
\tilde{\not{D}}
. The initial condition for
a^{J}
ensures that the latter belongs to that branch with sufficient accuracy.

The terms of the above expansion satisfy the following estimate:

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
a_{j}
be constructed as above for
0\leq j\leq J
(with
f_{J}=0
) and for
p\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\hat{f}\in\mathcal{S}_{p+3J}
. Then we have the estimates for
0\leq j\leq J-1
,

(6.6)

\displaystyle\|a_{j}\|_{p}\leq C_{pJ}\big(\langle{\rm T}\rangle\langle\nu\rangle_{{\rm T}}^{3}\big)^{j}\langle\nu\rangle_{{\rm T}}^{p}\|\hat{f}\|_{p+3j},\qquad\|a_{J}\|_{p}\leq C_{pJ}\big(\langle{\rm T}\rangle\langle\nu\rangle_{{\rm T}}^{3}\big)^{J-1}\langle\nu\rangle_{{\rm T}}^{p+1}\|\hat{f}\|_{p+3J},

with constants
C_{pJ}
independent of
{\rm T}
and
\langle\nu\rangle_{{\rm T}}
.

Proof.

Consider
0<j<J
. The estimate for
a_{J}
is a bit different since
f_{J}=0
.

We first observe that by construction of the terms
a_{j}
for
1\leq j\leq J-1
,

(6.7)

\displaystyle a_{j}=-T_{0}^{-1}\sum_{k=0}^{j-1}T_{j-k}a_{k}-\phi_{t}(\zeta)\mathcal{T}^{-1}\sum_{k=0}^{j-1}(T_{j+1-k}a_{k},\phi_{t})_{2}.

We wish to prove by induction that

(6.8)

\displaystyle\|a_{j}\|_{p\varsigma}\lesssim(\langle T\rangle\varsigma^{-3})^{j}\|a_{0}\|_{p+3j,\varsigma},\qquad\|a_{J}\|_{p\varsigma}\lesssim(\langle T\rangle\varsigma^{-3})^{J-1}(\varsigma\|a_{0}\|_{p+3J,\varsigma}+\|a_{0}\|_{p+3J-1,\varsigma}).

Using that
\|a_{0}\|_{p\varsigma}=\|f_{0}\phi_{t}\|_{p\varsigma}\lesssim\|\hat{f}\|_{p\varsigma}
thanks to Lemma
5.5
, then
\varsigma^{p}\|f\|_{p}\lesssim\|f\|_{p\varsigma}\lesssim\varsigma^{-p}\|f\|_{p}
and
\varsigma=\langle\nu\rangle_{{\rm T}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}
thus provide the results stated in the lemma. It remains to verify (
6.8
).

From (
6.7
) for
j=1
, we obtain
a_{1}=-T_{0}^{-1}T_{1}a_{0}-\phi_{t}\mathcal{T}^{-1}(T_{2}a_{0},\phi_{t})_{2}
so that using the result of Lemma
5.6
, we find the second term to be the least regular and

(6.9)

\displaystyle\|a_{1}\|_{p\varsigma}\lesssim\langle T\rangle\varsigma^{-3}\|a_{0}\|_{p\varsigma+3}

so that (
6.8
) holds when
j=1
. Assume it holds for
j-1\geq 1
. Then using (
6.7
) and Lemma
5.6
, we find

(6.10)

\displaystyle\|a_{j}\|_{p\varsigma}\lesssim\langle T\rangle\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{j-1}\varsigma^{-(j+2-k)}\|a_{k}\|_{p+j+2-k,\varsigma}\lesssim\langle T\rangle\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{j-1}\varsigma^{-(j+2-k)}(\langle T\rangle\varsigma^{-3})^{k}\|a_{0}\|_{p+2k+j+2,\varsigma},

which is largest when
k=j-1
and provides the sought estimate when
0\leq j<J
. It remains to consider the term

(6.11)

\displaystyle a_{J}=-T_{0}^{-1}\sum_{k=0}^{J-1}T_{J-k}a_{k}=-T_{0}^{-1}\Pi_{t}\sum_{k=0}^{J-1}T_{J-k}a_{k}=-T_{0}^{-1}\Pi_{t}T_{1}a_{J-1}-T_{0}^{-1}\Pi_{t}\sum_{k=0}^{J-2}T_{J-k}a_{k},

by construction of the wavepackets, where
\Pi_{t}
projects onto
N_{t}^{\perp}
. This operator has a smooth Schwartz kernel in
(t,\zeta,\zeta^{\prime})
that is independent of
\xi
. Therefore
\Pi_{t}
is bounded from
\mathbb{S}_{p\varsigma}
to itself with
\varsigma-
independent bound.

Since
T_{0}^{-1}
is bounded from
N^{\perp}\cap\mathbb{S}_{p+1,\varsigma}
to
\mathbb{S}_{p\varsigma}
, we find for
0\leq k\leq J-2

(6.12)

\displaystyle\|T_{0}^{-1}\Pi_{t}T_{J-k}a_{k}\|_{p\varsigma}\lesssim\|a_{k}\|_{p+2+J-k,\varsigma}\lesssim(\langle{\rm T}\rangle\varsigma^{-3})^{k}\|a_{0}\|_{p+2+J+2k,\varsigma}\lesssim(\langle{\rm T}\rangle\varsigma^{-3})^{J-1}\|a_{0}\|_{p+3J-1,\varsigma}

since
2+J+2k\leq 3J-1
.

It remains to consider
T_{0}^{-1}\Pi_{t}T_{1}a_{J-1}
.
We decompose
T_{0}^{-1}\Pi_{t}T_{1}=A+B
with
A=T_{01}^{-1}\Pi_{t}T_{11}+T_{02}^{-1}\Pi_{t}T_{12}
with bound
\langle T\rangle
from
\mathbb{S}_{p+2,\varsigma}\to\mathbb{S}_{p\varsigma}
and
B=T_{01}^{-1}\Pi_{t}T_{12}+T_{02}^{-1}\Pi_{t}T_{11}
with bound
\langle T\rangle\varsigma
from
\mathbb{S}_{p+3,\varsigma}\to\mathbb{S}_{p\varsigma}
as per Lemma
5.6
. This implies

(6.13)

\displaystyle\|a_{J}\|_{p\varsigma}\lesssim(\langle T\rangle\varsigma^{-3})^{J-1}(\varsigma\|a_{0}\|_{p+3J,\varsigma}+\|a_{0}\|_{p+3J-1,\varsigma})

as was to be shown. Thus (
6.8
) holds and this concludes the proof of the lemma.
∎

6.2.
Main approximation result

We can now state our main result.

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
\Psi
be the solution on
[0,{\rm T}]\times\mathbb{R}^{2}
of
(\varepsilon D_{t}+{\not{D}})\Psi=0
with initial condition
\Psi(0,x)=\Psi^{J}(0,x)
where
\Psi^{J}(t,x)=e^{\frac{i}{\varepsilon}\chi(t,x)}(\varepsilon^{-\frac{1}{2}}S^{-1}\mathfrak{U}_{t}a^{J})(t,x)
with
a^{J}
constructed in section
6.1
based on an initial condition
\hat{f}(\xi)\in\mathcal{S}_{3J+2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C})
.

Then there exists
C_{J}>0
independent of
{\rm T}
such that for every
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
,

(6.14)

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,{\rm T}]}\|\Psi-\Psi^{J}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathbb{C}^{2})}\leq C_{J}\left(\langle{\rm T}\rangle\langle\nu\rangle_{{\rm T}}^{3}\right)^{J}\varepsilon^{\frac{J}{2}}.

We recall that
\langle\nu\rangle_{{\rm T}}
is defined in (
5.35
) and
\langle{\rm T}\rangle=1+{\rm T}
. In particular, the approximation error is controlled so long as
\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}\langle T\rangle\langle\nu\rangle_{{\rm T}}^{3}\ll 1
. When
\langle\nu\rangle_{{\rm T}}
is uniformly bounded, errors are controlled up to times
{\rm T}\ll\varepsilon^{-\frac{1}{2}}
as in
[
2
]
. However, when
\langle\nu\rangle_{{\rm T}}
is of order
{\rm T}
, then errors are controlled up to times
{\rm T}\ll\varepsilon^{-\frac{1}{8}}
.

Note that such results are qualitatively reasonable: in the presence of dispersion, it becomes more difficult to control spatial moments (necessitated by the Taylor expansion of the coefficients
h
) of the solution.

The rest of the section is devoted to the proof of this theorem. It is based on a similar approximation result in the local variables
z=(z_{1},z_{2})
, which we state as a result of independent interest.

Proposition 6.3
.

Let
\psi
be the solution on
[0,{\rm T}]\times\mathbb{R}^{2}
of
L\psi=0
with initial condition
\psi(0,z)=\psi^{J}(0,z)
where
\psi^{J}(t,z)=\mathfrak{U}_{t}a^{J}(t,z)
with
a^{J}
constructed in section
6.1
based on an initial condition
\hat{f}(\xi)\in\mathcal{S}_{3J+2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C})
.

Then there exists
C_{J}>0
independent of
{\rm T}
such that for every
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
,

(6.15)

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,{\rm T}]}\|\psi-\psi^{J}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathbb{C}^{2})}\leq C_{J}\left(\langle{\rm T}\rangle\langle\nu\rangle_{{\rm T}}^{3}\right)^{J}\varepsilon^{\frac{J}{2}}.

Proof.

Solutions of the local problems
L\psi=0
and
Ta=0
are equivalent via the relation
\psi=\mathfrak{U}_{t}a
.
We now show that
\psi^{J}=\sum_{j=0}^{J}\varepsilon^{\frac{j}{2}}\psi_{j}
with
\psi_{j}=\mathfrak{U}_{t}a_{j}
approximately solves
L\psi=0
when
J\geq 1
. Define for
J\geq 1
the remainder operator

(6.16)

\displaystyle L_{\geq J}=\varepsilon^{-\frac{J}{2}}\Big(L-\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{J-1}\varepsilon^{\frac{j}{2}}L_{j}\Big)

and the reduced operator
\tilde{L}_{\geq J}=L_{\geq J}-\delta_{1J}D_{t}
involving only the coefficients
h
.

We deduce from (
6.1
) that
\sum_{k=0}^{j}L_{k}\psi_{j-k}=0
for
0\leq j\leq J
and hence

(6.17)

L\psi^{J}=\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{J}\varepsilon^{\frac{j}{2}}L\psi_{j}=\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{J}\Big(\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{J-j}\varepsilon^{\frac{j+k}{2}}L_{k}\psi_{j}+\varepsilon^{\frac{J+1}{2}}L_{\geq J+1-j}\psi_{j}\Big)=\varepsilon^{\frac{J+1}{2}}\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{J}L_{\geq J+1-j}\psi_{j},

since

(6.18)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{J}\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{J-j}\varepsilon^{\frac{j+k}{2}}L_{k}\psi_{j}=\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{J}\displaystyle\sum_{l=j}^{J}\varepsilon^{\frac{l}{2}}L_{l-j}\psi_{j}=\displaystyle\sum_{l=0}^{J}\varepsilon^{\frac{l}{2}}\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{l}L_{l-j}\psi_{j}=0.

We thus obtain

(6.19)

L\psi^{J}=\varepsilon^{\frac{J+1}{2}}\Big(D_{t}\psi_{J}+\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{J}\tilde{L}_{\geq J+1-j}\psi_{k}\Big)=:r_{J}.

We now derive a uniform bound in time of order
\varepsilon^{\frac{J+1}{2}}
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathbb{C}^{2})
for
r_{J}
.

We observe that
\mathcal{V}_{t}\mathcal{S}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{C}^{q})\mathcal{V}_{t}^{*}\cong\mathcal{S}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{C}^{q})
as spaces of functions (of
z
for
d=2
). In other words,
\|g\|_{p}\cong\|\mathcal{V}_{t}^{*}g\|_{p}
define equivalent norms for each
p
. The reason is that the above spaces based on Hermite functions are invariant by conjugation by Fourier transforms as well as invariant under invertible linear transforms
(\xi,z_{2})\mapsto(\xi,z_{2}+\gamma_{t}\xi)
of the base variables (uniformly in time since
\gamma_{t}
is bounded). Since
\gamma_{t}
is smooth, we also obtain the equivalence of the norms of
\mathcal{V}_{t}\mathbb{S}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{C}^{q})\mathcal{V}_{t}^{*}\cong\mathbb{S}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{C}^{q})
. Note that there is no meaningful notion of anisotropic space
\mathbb{S}_{p\varsigma}
in the variable
z
.

The regularity results of lemma
6.1
therefore apply to
\psi_{j}=\mathfrak{U}_{t}a_{j}
since conjugation by
\operatorname{U}_{t}
also preserves norms as the rotation angles
\theta_{t}
and
\varphi_{t}
are smooth in
t
. As a consequence,

(6.20)

\displaystyle\|\psi_{J}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\langle T\rangle^{J-1}\langle\nu\rangle_{{\rm T}}^{3J}\|\hat{f}\|_{3J+2},

(6.21)

\displaystyle\|\psi_{j}\|_{J-j+2}

\displaystyle\lesssim(\langle T\rangle\langle\nu\rangle_{{\rm T}}^{3})^{j}\langle\nu\rangle_{{\rm T}}^{J-j+2}\|\hat{f}\|_{J+2j+2}\lesssim\langle T\rangle^{J-1}\langle\nu\rangle_{{\rm T}}^{3J}\|\hat{f}\|_{3J+2}

for
0\leq j\leq J-1
.

Since
D_{t}
is bounded from
\mathbb{S}_{p+2}
to
\mathbb{S}_{p}
, we obtain that
\|D_{t}\psi_{J}\|_{0}\lesssim\langle T\rangle^{J-1}\|\hat{f}\|_{3J+2}
.

Using Taylor expansions of the coefficients for
1\leq j\leq J
,

(6.22)

\displaystyle h(y_{t}+\sqrt{\varepsilon}z)=\sum_{|\alpha|\leq j}\frac{1}{\alpha!}\partial^{\alpha}h(y_{t})\varepsilon^{\frac{|\alpha|}{2}}z^{\alpha}+\varepsilon^{\frac{j+1}{2}}\sum_{|\beta|=j+1}z^{\beta}R_{\beta}(y_{t}+\sqrt{\varepsilon}z),

with smooth functions
R_{\beta}
by regularity assumptions on
\kappa
and
A
, we observe that
\tilde{L}_{\geq j}
has the same regularity properties as
\tilde{L}_{j}
. In particular, it maps
\mathbb{S}_{p+j+1}
to
\mathbb{S}_{p}
. Therefore, for
0\leq j\leq J
,
\|\tilde{L}_{\geq J+1-j}\psi_{j}\|_{0}\lesssim\|\psi_{j}\|_{J+2-j}\lesssim\langle T\rangle^{J-1}\langle\nu\rangle_{{\rm T}}^{3J}\|\hat{f}\|_{3J+2}
thanks to (
6.20
).

By definition (
6.19
), this shows that

(6.23)

\displaystyle\|r_{J}\|_{0}\lesssim\varepsilon^{\frac{J+1}{2}}\langle T\rangle^{J-1}\langle\nu\rangle_{{\rm T}}^{3J}\|\hat{f}\|_{3J+2}.

Define
\tilde{L}=L-\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}D_{t}
, which we verify is self adjoint
[
26
]
. We thus observe that
L(\psi^{J}-\psi)=r^{J}
is equivalent to

(6.24)

(D_{t}+\varepsilon^{-\frac{1}{2}}\tilde{L})(\psi^{J}-\psi)=\varepsilon^{-\frac{1}{2}}r_{J}.

By unitarity of the above Dirac operator
[
26
]
, we obtain an error on
\|\psi^{J}-\psi\|_{0}
of order
\langle T\rangle\varepsilon^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|r_{J}\|_{0}\lesssim\varepsilon^{\frac{J}{2}}\langle T\rangle^{J}\langle\nu\rangle_{{\rm T}}^{3J}
. This concludes the proof of the proposition.
∎

Proof.

(Theorem
6.2
)
Let
\tilde{\Psi}
be the solution of
(\varepsilon D_{t}+\tilde{\not{D}})\tilde{\Psi}=0
with initial conditions
\tilde{\Psi}(0,x)=\tilde{\Psi}^{J}(t,x)=(\varepsilon^{-\frac{1}{2}}S^{-1}\mathfrak{U}_{t}a^{J})(t,x)
. Since
\tilde{\Psi}=\varepsilon^{-\frac{1}{2}}S^{-1}\psi
and
\tilde{\Psi}^{J}=\varepsilon^{-\frac{1}{2}}S^{-1}\psi^{J}
for
\varepsilon^{-\frac{1}{2}}S^{-1}
an
L^{2}-

6.3
that

(6.25)

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,{\rm T}]}\|\tilde{\Psi}-\tilde{\Psi}^{J}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathbb{C}^{2})}\leq C_{J}\left(\langle{\rm T}\rangle\langle\nu\rangle_{{\rm T}}^{3}\right)^{J}\varepsilon^{\frac{J}{2}}.

By construction,
\Psi^{J}(t,x)=e^{\frac{i}{\varepsilon}\chi(t,x)}\tilde{\Psi}^{J}(t,x)=e^{\frac{i}{\varepsilon}\chi(t,x)}\varepsilon^{-\frac{1}{2}}S^{-1}\psi^{J}(t,x)
so that

(6.26)

(\varepsilon D_{t}+{\not{D}})\Psi^{J}=e^{\frac{i}{\varepsilon}\chi(t,x)}(\varepsilon D_{t}+\tilde{\not{D}}+R)\tilde{\Psi}^{J}=e^{\frac{i}{\varepsilon}\chi(t,x)}\varepsilon^{-\frac{1}{2}}S^{-1}[\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}r_{J}+(\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}SR)\psi^{J}],

where we used
\varepsilon D_{t}+\tilde{\not{D}}=S^{-1}L\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}S
and where
r_{J}
defined in (
6.19
) and estimated in (
6.23
).

By unitarity for
(\varepsilon D_{t}+{\not{D}})
, the statement of the theorem follows if we show that

satisfy a bound similar to
r_{J}
as in (
6.23
).

Since
R
vanishes on
\Omega_{\delta}
and is uniformly bounded on
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}
by construction, we find that for every
p\geq 0
,

(6.27)

\displaystyle\|(1+|z|^{2})^{\frac{p}{2}}R(t,y_{t}+\sqrt{\varepsilon}z)\|_{\infty}\lesssim\varepsilon^{\frac{p}{2}}.

Therefore, thanks to (
6.20
),

(6.28)

\displaystyle\|R(t,y_{t}+\sqrt{\varepsilon}z)\psi^{J}\|_{0}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{j=0}^{J}\varepsilon^{\frac{j}{2}}\|R(t,y_{t}+\sqrt{\varepsilon}z)\psi_{j}\|_{0}\lesssim\sum_{j=0}^{J}\varepsilon^{\frac{J+1}{2}}\|(1+|z|^{2})^{\frac{J-j+1}{2}}\psi_{j}\|_{0}

(6.29)

\displaystyle\lesssim\ \varepsilon^{\frac{J+1}{2}}\sum_{j=0}^{J}\|\psi_{j}\|_{J-j+1}\lesssim\varepsilon^{\frac{J+1}{2}}\langle T\rangle^{J-1}\langle\nu\rangle_{{\rm T}}^{3J}\|\hat{f}\|_{3J+2}.

Thus,

. Since

6.2
by the same unitarity principle as in Proposition

.

Proof.

(Theorem

)
The above result with

when the gauge transformation is based on
\chi(t,x)
. Indeed, in this simplified setting with

constant and
\Delta\kappa=0
, we observe that

is given by (

) with
\lambda_{t}=0
,

, and
\tilde{\nu}_{t}=\nu_{t}=2\gamma(\theta_{t}-\theta_{0})
with

An additional error in
\Psi^{1}(t,x)=e^{\frac{i}{\varepsilon}\chi(t,x)}\varepsilon^{-\frac{1}{2}}S^{-1}\psi^{1}(t,x)

This term is multiplied by
\psi^{1}=\psi_{0}+\sqrt{\varepsilon}\psi_{1}

\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}

and when

as required for

in Theorem

with a bound

7.
Numerical simulations

In this section, we illustrate numerically the main effects of the magnetic field and the curved interface on the propagating wavepackets: (i) slowdown; (ii) Aharonov–Bohm phase-shift; (iii) dispersion.

The leading term in the asymptotic expansion provided by Theorem
6.2
is given by

(7.1)

\Psi_{0}(t,x)=\varepsilon^{-\frac{1}{2}}e^{\frac{i}{\varepsilon}\chi(t,x)}(\mathfrak{U}_{t}a_{0})\Big(t,\frac{x-y_{t}}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\Big),\qquad\text{where:}

•

\mathfrak{U}_{t}a_{0}(t,z)=\operatorname{U}_{t}\mathcal{V}_{t}a_{0}(t,z)
with
\operatorname{U}_{t}
a spatial and spinorial rotation that does not quantitatively affect the amplitude landscape of the wavepacket;

•

\mathcal{V}_{t}
is a Fourier-like transform given explicitly in (
3.48
).

•

a_{0}(t,\xi,\zeta)=f_{0}(t,\xi)\phi_{t}(\zeta)
with
f_{0}(t,\xi)
given respectively by (
5.13
) and (
4.14
).

For concreteness, we work below with the Gaussian initial condition
f_{0}(0,\xi)=e^{-\frac{1}{2}\sigma\xi^{2}}
for
\sigma>0
. Thanks to (
5.13
) and (
5.29
), we obtain

(7.2)

\mathcal{V}_{t}a_{0}(t,z)=\Big(\frac{\rho_{t}}{4\pi}\Big)^{\frac{1}{4}}e^{\frac{1}{2}\mu_{t}}(Q_{\sigma})^{-\frac{1}{2}}e^{i\lambda_{t}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\rho_{t}z_{2}^{2}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}Q_{\sigma}^{-1}(z_{1}+is_{t}z_{2})^{2}}\left[\begin{matrix}1\\
-1\end{matrix}\right],

with
Q_{\sigma}=e^{2\mu_{t}}\sigma+s_{t}\gamma_{t}-ie^{2\mu_{t}}\nu_{t}
. The various constants that appear in these formulas are collected as follows

(7.3)

\displaystyle\quad r_{t}=|\nabla\kappa(y_{t})|,\quad\rho_{t}=\sqrt{r_{t}^{2}+B_{t}^{2}},\quad c_{t}=\frac{r_{t}}{\rho_{t}},\quad s_{t}=\frac{B_{t}}{\rho_{t}},\quad\gamma_{t}=\frac{B_{t}}{\rho_{t}^{2}},\quad j_{t}\gamma_{t}=\frac{d\ln c_{t}}{dt},

(7.4)

\displaystyle k_{t}=\frac{c_{t}}{2}\Big(\partial_{n}B(y_{t})-B_{t}\frac{\Delta\kappa(y_{t})}{r_{t}}\Big),\quad\lambda_{t}=\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\frac{k_{s}}{2\rho_{s}}ds,\quad e^{\mu_{t}}=\frac{c_{t}}{c_{0}},\quad\nu_{t}=2\int_{0}^{t}\frac{c_{0}^{2}}{c_{s}^{2}}(\dot{\theta}_{s}\gamma_{s}+k_{s}\gamma_{s}^{2})ds,

and
n(y_{t})
and
\tau(y_{t})
are the normal and tangent vectors to
\Gamma
at
y_{t}
.

The rate of change of dispersion
\dot{\nu}_{t}
is, up to the multiplicative constant
2\gamma_{t}e^{-2\mu_{t}}
given by the two contributions
\dot{\theta}_{t}+\gamma_{t}k_{t}
, which we write explicitly as

(7.5)

\dot{\theta}_{t}+\gamma_{t}k_{t}=c_{t}\Big(\epsilon_{t}K_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{B\partial_{n}B-B^{2}r_{t}^{-1}\Delta\kappa}{B^{2}+r_{t}^{2}}(y_{t})\Big),

with
\epsilon_{t}=\pm 1
when
\{\mp\kappa>0\}
is convex in a neighborhood of
y_{t}
. This provides an expression to assess how the geometry of
\kappa
and that of
B
combine to amplify or suppress dispersion.

7.1.
Magnetic slowdown in constant B-field

The most visible impact of the magnetic field is the slowdown of the wavepacket: it propagates at speed
c_{t}=(1+B_{t}^{2})^{-\frac{1}{2}}
, which is strictly less than
1
whenever
B_{t}\neq 0
. This is confirmed by the results shown in Figure
9
.

In this simulation, a constant magnetic field for a straight interface given by
\kappa(x)=x_{2}
is modeled by
\tilde{A}=A=-Bx_{2}e_{1}=Bx_{2}\tau
(with therefore
\chi=0
and
\beta=B
). This implies
j_{t}=k_{t}=0
so that
e^{\mu_{t}}=1
and
\nu_{t}=0
. The terms
\rho=\sqrt{1+B^{2}}
and
\gamma=\frac{B}{1+B^{2}}
are constant. The wavepacket velocity is constant and given by

(7.6)

\dot{y}_{t}=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+B^{2}}}e_{1}=-ce_{1}.

The wavepacket in (
7.2
) takes the simpler form

(7.7)

\mathcal{V}_{t}a_{0}(t,z)=\Big(\frac{\rho}{4\pi}\Big)^{\frac{1}{4}}\Big(\frac{1}{\sigma+\rho\gamma^{2}}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\left(\rho z_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{\sigma+\rho\gamma^{2}}(z_{1}+isz_{2})^{2}\right)}\left[\begin{matrix}1\\
-1\end{matrix}\right].

The oscillations of the spinor components are clearly visible in Figure
9
.

7.2.
Aharonov–Bohm effect for a circular interface

This effect emerges in the phase
\chi(t,x)/\varepsilon
in (
7.1
) when
\Gamma
is a loop.
Once
y_{t}
completes an exact full rotation around
\Gamma
, the wavepacket acquires the phase shift

(7.8)

\displaystyle\dfrac{\chi(t,y_{t})}{\varepsilon}=\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{t}\dot{y_{s}}A(y_{s})ds=\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Gamma}A,

see (
2.19
). This is a gauge-independent quantity involving the magnetic flux
\Phi=\int_{\Gamma}A
enclosed by
\Gamma
, and of order
1/\varepsilon
.

Consider a magnetic vector potential with flux

and a circular interface

7.3.
Dispersive and phase effects in closed interfaces

The coefficient
Q_{\sigma}
in (
7.2
) controls the dispersion. The only term there that can grow with
t
is

(7.10)

\nu_{t}e^{2\mu_{t}}=2\int_{0}^{t}\dfrac{c_{t}^{2}}{c_{s}^{2}}\left(\dot{\theta_{s}}\gamma_{s}+k_{s}\gamma_{s}^{2}\right)ds.

Consider now a circle of radius
R
and a choice of domain wall
\kappa(x)=\frac{|x|^{m}-R^{m}}{mR^{m-1}}
for
m>0
with
|\nabla\kappa|=1=r
and
\Delta\kappa=m|x|^{-1}
equal to
mR^{-1}
on
\Gamma
. Assume
B
constant so that all coefficients are independent of time and given by

(7.11)

c=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+B^{2}}},\quad k=-\frac{cBm}{2R},\quad\dot{\theta}=\frac{c}{R},\quad\dot{\theta}+\gamma k=\frac{c}{R}\left(1-\frac{mB^{2}}{2(1+B^{2})}\right).

We thus observe that
\nu_{t}=2t\gamma(\dot{\theta}+\gamma k)
grows linearly in time provided that
\dot{\theta}+\gamma k\neq 0
. In this case, the resulting wavepacket decreases like
t^{-1/2}
, in a way depending on
B
(higher magnetic fields, however, do not necessarily enhance dispersion). This was predicted in Lemma
5.3
and (
7.2
) and is numerically confirmed in Figure
11
.

In the other hand, when
m=\frac{2(1+B^{2})}{B^{2}}
, the resulting domain wall prevents dispersion; see Figure
12
. This can be of interest in application where one wants to slow down propagation without losing on coherence.

Figure 12
.
Homogeneous magnetic field strength

with

. Snapshots showing an almost dispersion-free propagation around the circle.

In this scenario we can explicitly construct the local gauge
\chi
. We have
n=e_{r}
and
\tau=-e_{\theta}
, by using the defining relations
\partial_{\tau}\chi=A_{\tau}
on
\Gamma
followed by
\partial_{n}\chi=A_{n}
across it. We have

(7.12)

A=Bx_{1}e_{2}=\frac{Br}{2}\big((1+\cos 2\theta)e_{\theta}+\sin 2\theta e_{r}\big)

so that integrating along
\Gamma
, then across
\Gamma
, we obtain

(7.13)

\chi(R,\theta)=\frac{BR^{2}}{2}\left(\theta+\frac{\sin 2\theta}{2}\right),\qquad\chi(r,\theta)=BR^{2}\frac{\theta}{2}+Br^{2}\frac{\sin 2\theta}{4}.

This shows that
\chi(r,\theta)
is not globally defined as a continuous function on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
: the term
BR^{2}\frac{\theta}{2}
jumps after each revolution. The increment
\pi BR^{2}
is the magnetic flux: we retrieve a Aharonov–Bohm effect.
