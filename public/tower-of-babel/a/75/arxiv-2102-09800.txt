A short characterization of the Octonions
Erwin Kleinfeld Yoav Segev

Abstract
. In this paper we prove that if R R is a proper alternative ring whose additive group has no 3 3 -torsion and whose non-zero commutators are not zero-divisors, then R R has no zero-divisors. It follows from a theorem of Bruck and Kleinfeld that if, in addition, the characteristic of R R is not 2 , 2, then the central quotient of R R is an octonion division algebra over some field. We include other characterizations of octonion division algebras and we also deal with the case where ( R , + ) (R,+) has 3 3 -torsion.

2.
Preliminaries on alternative rings

Our main references for alternative rings are
[
5
,
6
]
.
Let
R
be a ring, not necessarily with
{\bf 1}
and not
necessarily associative.

Definitions 2.1
.

Let
x,y,z\in R.

(1)

The
associator

(x,y,z)
is defined to be

(x,y,z)=(xy)z-x(yz).

(2)

The
commutator

(x,y)
is defined to be

(x,y)=xy-yx.

(3)

R
is an
alternative ring
if

(x,y,y)=0=(y,y,x),

for all
x,y\in R.

(4)

The
nucleus
of
R
is denoted
N
and defined by

N=\{n\in R\mid(n,R,R)=0\}.

Note that in an alternative ring the associator is skew symmetric in its
3

variables (
[
6
, Lemma 1]
). Hence
(R,n,R)=(R,R,n)=0,
for
n\in N.

(5)

The
center
of
R
is denoted
C
and defined

C=\{c\in N\mid(c,R)=0\}.

(6)

The
commutative center
of
R
is denoted here by
Z
and defined by

Z=\{z\in R\mid(z,R)=0\}.

In the remainder of this paper
R
is an alternative ring

which is
not associative
.

N
denotes the nucleus of
R,

C
its center and
Z
its commutative center.

Facts 2.2
.

Let
R
be an alternative ring.

(1)

If
v\in R
is a commutator, then
v^{4}\in N.

(2)

If
v\in R
is a commutator, then
[v^{2},R,R]v=0.

(3)

Any subring of
R
generated by two elements is associative.

Proof.

For (1)&(2) see
[
5
, Theorem 3.1]
. Part (3) is a theorem of E. Artin.
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

(1)

Let
x,y\in R,
with
(x,y)=0,
and let
z\in Z.

Then
xz
commutes with
y.

(2)

The commutative center
Z
of
R
is a subring of
R.

(3)

If
3x=0\implies x=0,
for all
x\in R,
then
Z=C.

Proof.

(1) By
[
6
, equation (6), p. 131]
we have

(2.1)

(xy,r)-x(y,r)-(x,r)y=3(x,y,r),\qquad\text{for all $x,y,r\in R$.}

Taking
r=z,
we get

3(x,y,z)=0.

But also

(zx,y)=(zx,y)-z(x,y)-(z,y)x=3(z,x,y)=3(x,y,z)=0.

(2) By (1), if
x,z\in Z
and
y\in R,
then
xz
commute with
y,

so
xz\in Z,
and
Z
is closed under multiplication. Hence
Z
is a subring of
R.

(3) We already saw in the proof of part (1) that
3(x,y,z)=0,

for all
x,y\in R,
and
z\in Z,
so
(x,y,z)=0,
and
z\in C.

Of course
C\subseteq Z.

∎

The following lemma gives some properties of
R
which we will require later.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
w,x,y,z\in R,
and
n,n^{\prime}\in N,
then

(1)

[N,R]\subseteq N.

(2)

Nuclear elements commute with associators.

(3)

(w,n)(x,y,z)=-(x,n)(w,y,z).
In particular,
(n^{\prime},n)(x,y,z)=0.

(4)

If
N
contains no non-zero elements which are zero divisors in
R,
then
N
is commutative.

(5)

(w,n)(w,n)(x,y,z)=0.

Proof.

(1)&(2) By
[
6
, equation (2), p. 129]
,

(2.2)

(nx,y,z)=n(x,y,z).

Also, by the beginning of the proof of Lemma 4, p. 132 in
[
6
]
,

(2.3)

(nx,y,z)=(x,y,z)n\quad\text{and}\quad(xn,y,z)=n(x,y,z).

So
(nx,y,z)=(xn,y,z),
hence (1) holds, and also
n(x,y,z)=(x,y,z)n,
so (2) holds.

(3) By
[
6
, equation (6), p. 131]
,

(2.4)

(wx,n)=w(x,n)+(w,n)x.

Now

\displaystyle(w,n)(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=((w,n)x,y,z)

\displaystyle\quad(\text{by (1) and equation \eqref{n(x,y,z)1}})

\displaystyle=-(w(x,n),y,z)

\displaystyle\quad\text{(by equation \eqref{wx,n} and (1)})

\displaystyle=-(x,n)(w,y,z)

\displaystyle\quad\text{(by equation \eqref{n(x,y,z)2} and (1))}.

Taking
w=n^{\prime}\in N,
we get the last statement of (3).

(4) This is immediate from the last statement of (3) and the hypothesis that
R
is not associative.

(5) We have

\displaystyle(w,n)(w,n)(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=-(w,n)(x,n)(w,y,z)

(by (3))

\displaystyle=-(w,n)(w,y,z)(x,n)

(by (2))

\displaystyle=(w,n)(y,w,z)(x,n)

(associators are skew-symmetric)

\displaystyle=-(y,n)(w,w,z)(x,n)

(by (3))

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\quad\text{(because $R$ is alternative)}.

∎

We also need the following three lemmas.

Lemma 2.5
.

Assume that

(i)

no non-zero commutator of
R
is nilpotent;

(ii)

non-zero elements of the nucleus of
R
are not divisors of zero in
R.

Then a non-zero commutator in
R
is not a divisor of zero in
R.

Proof.

Let
v
be a non-zero commutator. Then, by Facts
2.2
(1),
0\neq v^{4}\in N.

If
t\in R
is such that
vt=0,
then since the subring generated by
v
and
t
is associative,

v^{4}t=0,
so
t=0.

∎

Lemma 2.6
.

Assume that

(1)

no non-zero commutator of
R
is nilpotent;

(2)

R
has an identity;

(3)

the nucleus of
R
is a simple ring.

Then the nucleus of
R
is a field, and
a non-zero commutator in
R
is not a divisor of zero in
R.

Proof.

First we show that
N
is a field.
Recall from Lemma
2.4
that nuclear elements commute with associators. Set

I:=\{n\in N\mid n(x,y,z)=0,\text{ for all }x,y,z\in R\}.

Then
(n^{\prime}n)(x,y,z)=n^{\prime}(n(x,y,z)=0,
and
(nn^{\prime})(x,y,z)=(x,y,z)(nn^{\prime})=((x,y,z)n)n^{\prime}=0,

for
n\in I,\ n^{\prime}\in N,\ x,y,z\in R.
So
I
is an ideal of
N.

Hence
I=0,
since
{\bf 1}\notin I.

However by Lemma
2.4
(3), any commutator of elements in
N
is in
I,

so
N
must be commutative. This plus simple is enough to make
N
into a field.

Now
let
0\neq n\in N,
such that
nt=0,
for some
t\in R.

Then
t={\bf 1}t=(n^{-1}n)t=n^{-1}(nt)=0.
Note that
the identity of
R
is the identity of
N.

Hence
R
satisfies the hypotheses of Lemma
2.5
,
and we are done.
∎

Lemma 2.7
.

Assume that
2x=0
implies
x=0,
for all
x\in R.

Then the subring of
R
generated by a triple
a,b,c

of commuting elements is commutative.

Proof.

We claim that for any triple of commuting elements
x,y,z\in R
we have

(xy,z)=0.

Indeed, by
[
6
, equation (6), p. 131]
,

(xy,z)=3(x,y,z).

But also
(yx,z)=3(y,x,z)=-3(x,y,z).

Hence
2(3(x,y,z))=0,
so

3(x,y,z)=0.
Hence
(xy,z)=0.

A standard argument using
(*)
and the length of
an element in the generators
\{a,b,c\}
yields the lemma.
∎

Proof of Proposition

Part (1) follows from Lemmas

, respectively.
Part (2) follows from Lemma

. Suppose

3.
The proof of Theorem 1.1

In this section we prove Theorem
1.1
. So we let
R

be an alternative ring which is not associative.
We consider the following hypotheses.

Non-zero commutators are not zero-divisors in R.

\displaystyle\text{If $x\in R,$ and $y,z$ are in the commutative center of $R,$}

\displaystyle\text{then $(xy)z=x(yz)$.}

Note that if hypothesis (B) holds in
R
then
R
is not commutative.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
x,y,t\in R.
Then

(1)

((x,y),x,t)=(y,x,(x,t);

(2)

(x,y)x=(x,yx);

(3)

if hypothesis (A) holds, and
xt=0,
then
(x,yx)t=0.

Proof.

(1) By
[
6
, equation (4), p. 130]
(and the fact that
R
is alternative),

(xy,x,t)=(y,x,t)x=(y,x,xt),

and by
[
6
, equation (5), p. 130]
,

(yx,x,t)=x(y,x,t)=(y,x,tx).

Sustracting we get (1).

(2) This is immediate from Fact
2.2
(3).

(3) Assume that hypothesis (A) holds,
and that
xt=0.
We claim that
(x,t)=0.

Indeed, by Fact
2.2
(3),
(x,t)^{2}=(-tx)^{2}=t(xt)x=0,

so, by hypothesis (A),
(x,t)=0.

By part (1),
((x,y),x,t)=0.
Since
xt=0,
we see that

((x,y)x)t=0,
so (3) follows from (2).
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Assume that hypothesis (A) holds in
R.

Let
x\in R,
with
x\notin Z.
Then
x

is not a zero divisor in
R.

Proof.

Let
x\in R
such that
x\notin Z.
Then
there exists
y\in R,
with
(x,y)\neq 0.
Suppose that

xt=0,
for some
t\in R.
By Lemma
3.1
(3),

(x,yx)t=0,
and by Lemma
3.1
(2),
(x,yx)=(x,y)x.

By hypothesis (A),
(x,yx)\neq 0,
so by hypothesis (A)
again,
t=0.

∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Assume that hypotheses (A) and (B) hold in
R.

Let
0\neq z\in Z,
then
z
is not a zero divisor in
R.

Proof.

Let
0\neq z\in Z,
and suppose that
z
is a divisor of zero.
By Lemma
3.2
,
there exists non-zero
w\in Z,
with
zw=0.

Let
(x,y)
be a non-zero commutator in
R.

By Lemma
3.2
,

xz\neq 0.
By hypothesis (B),

(xz)w=x(zw)=0.
Hence by Lemma
3.2
again,

xz\in Z.

By equation (
2.1
),

3(x,y,z)=(xy,z)-x(y,z)-(x,z)y=0

But then also
3(x,z,y)=0,
so by equation (
2.1
) again,

0=3(x,z,y)=(xz,y)-x(z,y)-(x,y)z.

Since
(xz,y)=0=x(z,y),
we see that
(x,y)z=0,
contradicting hypothesis (A).
∎

.

Assume that hypotheses (A) and (B) hold in

By Lemmas
3.2
and

,

R
contains no divisors of zero. This show part (a).
Part (b) follows
from part (a) and

.

Remark 3.4
.

As usual, a non-zero element
x\in R
is a left zero divisor if there
exists a non-zero
y\in R

such that
xy=0.
Right zero divisors are similarly defined.

In this paper, when we say that a non-zero element
r\in R
is
not a zero divisor

we mean that it is
either not a left zero divisor, or not a right zero divisor
.

Note that if a non-zero
r\in R
is not a zero divisor, then
r^{2}\neq 0.

Suppose now that we assume that non-zero commutators in
R
are not zero divisors.
Then
(x,y)^{2}\neq 0,
for any non-zero commutator
(x,y).
Now let
r\in R
such
that
r
is not a zero divisor. Suppose that
r
is not a left zero divisor.
We show that
r
is not a right zero divisor either. Assume
sr=0,
for some non-zero

s\in R.
Then
rs=(r,s)\neq 0.
But (since subrings generated
by two elements are associative),
(r,s)^{2}=r(sr)s=0,

a contradiction. Similarly any non-zero element which is not a right zero divisor
is also not a left zero divisor.

We thus see that in this paper, a non-zero element which is not a zero divisor (as defined
above) is
not a right and not a left zero divisor
.
