The strong data processing inequality under the heat flow
B. Klartag, O. Ordentlich

Abstract
Let ν \nu and μ \mu be probability distributions on ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} , and ν s , μ s \nu_{s},\mu_{s} be their evolution under the heat flow, that is, the probability distributions resulting from convolving their density with the density of an isotropic Gaussian random vector with variance s s in each entry. This paper studies the rate of decay of s ↦ D ( ν s ∥ μ s ) s\mapsto D(\nu_{s}\|\mu_{s}) for various divergences, including the χ 2 \chi^{2} and Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergences. We prove upper and lower bounds on the strong data-processing inequality (SDPI) coefficients corresponding to the source μ \mu and the Gaussian channel. We also prove generalizations of de Brujin’s identity, and Costa’s result on the concavity in s s of the differential entropy of ν s \nu_{s} . As a byproduct of our analysis, we obtain new lower bounds on the mutual information between X X and Y = X + s ​ Z Y=X+\sqrt{s}Z , where Z Z is a standard Gaussian vector in ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} , independent of X X , and on the minimum mean-square error (MMSE) in estimating X X from Y Y , in terms of the Poincaré constant of X X .

Main results on

For

Theorem 1.1

\displaystyle 1-\frac{\mathrm{mmse}(\mu,s)}{\mathbb{E}_{\mu}|X-\mathbb{E}X|^{2}}\leq\eta_{\chi^{2}}(\mu,s)=S^{2}(X,X+\sqrt{s}Z)\leq\frac{1}{1+\frac{s}{C_{\mathrm{P}}(\mu)}}.

Inequality (

Corollary 1.3

\displaystyle\mathrm{mmse}(\mu,s)\geq\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\mu}|X-\mathbb{E}X|^{2}}{1+\frac{C_{\mathrm{P}}(\mu)}{s}}=\frac{nP}{1+\frac{P}{s}\cdot\bar{C}_{\mathrm{P}}(\mu)}

\displaystyle P=\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}_{\mu}|X-\mathbb{E}X|^{2},

(23)

\displaystyle\bar{C}_{\mathrm{P}}(\mu)=\frac{C_{\mathrm{P}}(\mu)}{P}.

Let us compare Corollary

Assume that
\mu

[

\displaystyle\mathrm{mmse}(\mu,s)\geq\frac{n^{2}s}{n+s\mathcal{J}(\mu)}=\frac{nP}{\bar{\mathcal{J}}(\mu)+\frac{P}{s}},

(25)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(\mu)=\int_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{|\nabla\rho(x)|^{2}}{\rho(x)}dx.

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{J}}(\mu)=\frac{\mathcal{J}(\mu)\cdot P}{n}.

is finite, the bound from Corollary

We remark that there are also examples where

is finite and

\displaystyle I(\mu,s)=I(X;X+\sqrt{s}Z)\geq\frac{n}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{\bar{C}_{\mathrm{P}}(\mu)}\log\left(1+\frac{P}{s}\cdot\bar{C}_{\mathrm{P}}(\mu)\right),

1.2
Main results on KL-divergence

We now move on to present our results concerning the KL divergence. Our first result is a generalization of de Bruijn’s identity.

Proposition 1.5

Let
\mu

\nu

\displaystyle J(\nu\,\,||\,\,\mu)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{|\nabla f|^{2}}{f}d\mu.

Proposition

Some of the proceeding results rely on the correctness of a certain conjecture, namely Conjecture
4.2
, stated in Section
4

4.2

The only gap between our “proof” and a fully rigorous proof is the justification of order exchange between integration and differentiation, as well as justifying several integration by parts (that is, showing that the involved functions vanish at infinity). While it is not trivial to rigorously justify these steps, we are quite certain they are correct under some minimal regularity assumptions. In fact, the justification of such steps is often ignored in the literature. For example, to the best of our knowledge, the first time a fully rigorous proof was given for de Bruijn’s identity was by Barron in 1984
[
6
]
, while prior proofs, such as that of Blachman
[
8
]
did not justify the exchange of differentiation and integration. Whenever a statement relies on the correctness of Conjecture

, we explicitly emphasize it.

Main results on general divergences

Fix a smooth, convex function
\varphi:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
with
\varphi(1)=0
and set
\varphi(0)=\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\varphi(t)
.
In
[
11
]
, Chafaï introduced
the concepts of
\varphi
-Sobolev inequalities and
\varphi
-entropy,
which turn out to fit nicely with our study of the evolution of
the
\varphi
-divergence
D_{\varphi}(\nu_{s}\,\,||\,\,\mu_{s})
.

The
\varphi
-Sobolev constant of
a random vector
X
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, denoted by
C_{\varphi}(X)
, is the infimum over all
C\geq 0
such that for any locally-Lipschitz function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow(0,\infty)
with
\mathbb{E}\varphi(X)=1
,

\mathbb{E}\varphi(f(X))\leq\frac{C}{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}\varphi^{\prime\prime}(f(X))|\nabla f(X)|^{2}.

(37)

This generalized the definition of the Poincaré constant and the log-Sobolev constant. Indeed,
in the case where
\varphi(x)=x\log x
is the
KL
-divergence, we have
C_{\varphi}(X)=C_{\mathrm{LS}}(X)
, and a simple argument
reveals that when
\varphi(x)=(x-1)^{2}
we have
C_{\varphi}(X)=C_{\mathrm{P}}(X)
. When
X\sim\mu
we set
C_{\varphi}(\mu)=C_{\varphi}(X)
. In the general case, our definition (
37
) is slightly different from the one in Chafaï in that we impose the additional requirement that
\mathbb{E}f(X)=1
, which implies that
\varphi(\mathbb{E}f(X))=0
.
In Chafaï the left-hand side of (
37
) is replaced by
\mathbb{E}\varphi(f(X))-\varphi(\mathbb{E}f(X))
. In the cases of interest just described, the two definitions coincide by homogeneity.

Given two probability distributions
\mu
and
\nu
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
with
\nu
absolutely continuous with a smooth density with respect to
\mu
, we defined the
\varphi
-Fisher information via

J_{\varphi}(\nu\,\,||\,\,\mu)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi^{\prime\prime}(f)|\nabla f|^{2}d\mu,

where
f=d\nu/d\mu
.
Thus we have the defining inequality of the
\varphi
-Sobolev constant,

D_{\varphi}(\nu\,\,||\,\,\mu)\leq\frac{C_{\varphi}(\mu)}{2}\cdot J_{\varphi}(\nu\,\,||\,\,\mu).

(38)

Some of the properties of the Poincaré and log-Sobolev constants generalize to the more general context of the
\varphi
-Sobolev constant.
The
\varphi
-Sobolev constant is
2
-homogeneous: For any fixed number
\lambda>0
,

C_{\varphi}(\lambda X)=\lambda^{2}C_{\varphi}(X).

This follows from the definition (
37
), by substituting
g(x)=f(\lambda x)
.
The Poincaré constant is the smallest of all
\varphi
-Sobolev constants:

Proposition 1.8
.

Assume that
\varphi^{\prime\prime}(1)>0
. Then for any random vector
X
for which
C_{\varphi}(X)<\infty
,

C_{P}(X)\leq C_{\varphi}(X).

Proposition
1.8
must be known, yet for lack of concise reference we provide its proof below.
The function
1/\varphi^{\prime\prime}
plays an important role in the sequel. When it is a concave function on
(0,\infty)
– this is equivalent to condition (H1) from Chafaï
[
11
]
– it is possible to compute
the
\varphi
-Sobolev constant of the Gaussian measure. The following proposition follows from the results of Chafaï
[
11
]
and Proposition
1.8
. For convenience we provide a proof.

Proposition 1.9
.

Let
\varphi:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
be a smooth, convex function with
\varphi(1)=0
such that
1/\varphi^{\prime\prime}
is concave. Let
X
be a standard
Gaussian random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then,

C_{\varphi}(X)=1.

Proposition
1.5
, the de Bruijn identity, may be generalized even further:

Proposition 1.10
.

Let
\varphi(x)=\varphi_{\lambda}(x)=x^{\lambda}-1
, for
\lambda>1
. Let
\mu
and
\nu
be probability distributions on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
D_{\varphi}(\nu\,\,||\,\,\mu)<\infty
, and assume
\mathbb{E}_{\mu}|X|^{4}<\infty
. Then for any
s>0
,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{ds}D_{\varphi}(\nu_{s}\,\,||\,\,\mu_{s})=-\frac{1}{2}J_{\varphi}(\nu_{s}\,\,||\,\,\mu_{s}).

(39)

This proposition formally implies Proposition
1.5
since
D_{\text{KL}}(\nu\|\mu)=\lim_{\lambda\to 1}\frac{1}{\lambda-1}\log(1+D_{\varphi_{\lambda}}(\nu\,\,||\,\,\mu))
. While we have only proved Proposition
1.10
for
\varphi(x)=\varphi_{\lambda}(x)
,
\lambda>1
, we believe it should be valid for any smooth, convex function
\varphi:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
with
\varphi(1)=0
. We have only used the assumption that
\varphi(x)=\varphi_{\lambda}(x)
,
\lambda>1
, for justifying integration under the integral sign, and for justifying integration by parts.
The following is a generalization of Theorem
1.6
.

Theorem 1.11
.

Let
\varphi(x)=\varphi_{\lambda}(x)=x^{\lambda}-1
, for
1<\lambda\leq 2
, such that
1/\varphi^{\prime\prime}
is concave. Then,
for any probability measure
\mu
and
s>0
,

\displaystyle 1-\frac{\mathrm{mmse}(\mu,s)}{\mathbb{E}_{\mu}|X-\mathbb{E}X|^{2}}\leq\eta_{\varphi}(\mu,s)\leq\frac{1}{1+\frac{s}{C_{\varphi}(\mu)}}.

(40)

If additionally
\mu
is log-concave, then, assuming the validity of Conjecture
4.2
, we also have that

\displaystyle 1-\frac{s}{C_{\varphi}(\mu)}\leq\eta_{\varphi}(\mu,s).

(41)

The left-hand side inequality in (
40
) immediately follows from
(
5
) and (
19
).
We proceed with a generalization of Theorem
1.7
.

Theorem 1.12
.

Let
\varphi(x)=\varphi_{\lambda}(x)=x^{\lambda}-1
, for
1<\lambda\leq 2
, such that
1/\varphi^{\prime\prime}
is concave, such that
1/\varphi^{\prime\prime}
is concave.
Then, assuming the validity of Conjecture
4.2
, for any probability measure
\nu
and any log-concave probability measure
\mu
, the mapping
s\mapsto D_{\varphi}(\nu_{s}\|\mu_{s})
is convex.
In particular, whenever
\mu
is log-concave,
s\mapsto\eta_{\varphi}(\mu,s)

For the geometric meaning of the
\varphi
-Sobolev constant, and for the relations to concentration of measure, we refer the reader to
[

, Section 3.4]
.

1.4
Related Work

Contraction coefficients for the Gaussian channel were studied in
[
35
]
(see also
[
21
]
), and it was shown that there exist probability distributions
\mu
with bounded second moment
\mathbb{E}_{\mu}|X-\mathbb{E}X|^{2}
for which
\eta_{\chi^{2}}(\mu,s)=\eta_{\text{KL}}(\mu,s)=1
. Our results show that while a bounded second moment does not imply non-trivial
\eta_{\chi^{2}}(\mu,s)
or
\eta_{\text{KL}}(\mu,s)
, bounded Poincarè constant
C_{\mathrm{P}}(\mu)
or log-Sobolev constant
C_{\mathrm{LS}}(\mu)
, respectively, do imply non-trivial contraction coefficient. Moreover, in the log-concave
case the Poincarè constant or log-Sobolev constant are essentially equivalent to the corresponding contraction coefficient.

For discrete channels, Raginsky
[
38
]
has estimated the SDPI constants
\eta_{\chi^{2}}(\mu,K_{Y|X})
and
\eta_{\text{KL}}(\mu,K_{Y|X})
, as well as for other choices of
\varphi
, of a source
\mu
and a general discrete channel
K_{Y|X}
as a function of
a Poincarè constant
C_{\mathrm{P}}(\mu,K_{Y|X})
or a log-Sobolev constant
C_{\mathrm{LS}}(\mu,K_{Y|X})
, respectively, corresponding to
both
the source
\mu
and
the channel
K_{Y|X}
. See
[
38
]

for the precise definitions of those constants and the relations to the contraction coefficients. We stress that the Poincarè constant
C_{\mathrm{P}}(\mu)
and log-Sobolev constant
C_{\mathrm{LS}}(\mu)
used in our bounds depend only on the source
\mu
. Furthermore, our bounds only address the Gaussian channels, while the estimates in
[
38
]
hold for any discrete channel (though this class does not include the Gaussian channel).

The general problem of developing lower bounds, amenable to evaluation, on the MMSE in estimating a random variable
X
from a dependent random variable
Y
is a classic topic in information theory, signal processing, and statistics, and so is the special case of estimation in Gaussian noise. Classic references include
[
46
,
44
,
48
,
50
]
. See also
[
37
, Chapter 30]
. Our lower bound from Corollary
1.3
varies smoothly with the noise level
s
, similarly to the Bayesian CRLB, and therefore, cannot capture threshold phenomena/phase transitions of the MMSE in
s
. However, to the best of our knowledge, no previous bounds have estimated
\mathrm{mmse}(\mu,s)
in terms of the Poincarè constant
C_{\mathrm{P}}(\mu)
. A recent work by Zieder, Dytso and Cardone
[
49
]
develops a lower bound on the MMSE for a class of additive channels from
X
to
Y
, including the Gaussian one. Their bound is expressed in terms of the random variable
\kappa(X)
, where
\kappa(x)=(C_{\mathrm{P}}(W_{Y|X=x}))^{-1}
is the inverse of the Poincarè constant corresponding to the conditional distributions of the channel’s output given the input
x
, as well as the variance of the conditional information density between
X
and
Y
. Our bound from Corollary
1.3
(which holds only for the Gaussian channel), on the other hand, depends
only
on Poincarè constant of the
source

C_{\mathrm{P}}(\mu)
.

The function
s\mapsto h(X+\sqrt{s}Z)
for isotropic Gaussian
Z
statistically independent of
X\sim\mu
, has been studied intensively, from the first days of information theory. de Bruijn’s identity
[
43
,
13
]
(see also
[
16
, Theorem 17.7.2]
) shows that the derivative of this function with respect to
s
is
\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{J}(\mu_{s})
.
As explained here, our Proposition
1.5
is a stronger and more general identity. de Bruijn’s identity was instrumental for proving the entropy power inequality
[
43
,
8
]
. Decades later, Costa used de Bruijn’s identity for showing that
s\mapsto e^{\frac{2}{n}h(X+\sqrt{s}Z)}
is concave, which immediately also implies the concavity of
s\mapsto h(X+\sqrt{s}Z)
. Several alternative proofs for Costa’s EPI were given in
[
20
,
47
,
25
,
40
,
14
]
.
An important point of view on the evolution of curvature under the heat flow, involving curvature and dimension was developed by Bakry, Émery and the Toulouse school, see the book
[
5
]
.

In Theorem
1.7
we establish the convexity of
s\mapsto D(\nu_{s}\|\mu_{s})
for log-concave
\mu
, which generalizes the latter, and weaker, statement of Costa. Unfortunately, we were not able to prove convexity of
s\mapsto\log D(\nu_{s}\|\mu_{s})
. Further improvements of the entropy power inequality for the random variable
X+\sqrt{s}Z
were established by Courtade
[
14
]
. The relation between
I(\mu,s)
and
\mathrm{mmse}(\mu,s)
, called the I-MMSE relation, which is intimately related to de Bruijn’s identity, was discovered by Guo, Shamai and Verdú
[
24
]
.
To the best of our knowledge, our Corollary
1.4
is the first lower bound on
I(\mu,s)
in terms of
C_{\mathrm{P}}(\mu)
. An upper bound in a somewhat similar spirit was derived in
[
3
]
. Inequalities interpolating between the Poincaré and the log-Sobolev inequality appear in Latała and Oleszkiewicz
[
30
]
. This is a family of inequalities parameterized by a parameter
1\leq p\leq 2
.

In the remainder of this paper, we prove the theorems stated above.

2.1
Proof of the lower bound in Theorem
20

We show that for any pair of random variables
X,Y
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathcal{A}
, where
\mathcal{A}
is some abstract alphabet, it holds that

\displaystyle S^{2}(X,Y)\geq 1-\frac{\mathrm{mmse}(X|Y)}{\mathbb{E}|X-\mathbb{E}X|^{2}},

(42)

where

\displaystyle\mathrm{mmse}(X|Y)=\mathbb{E}|X-\mathbb{E}[X|Y]|^{2}.

(43)

The idea is to use linear test functions in
the definition (
9
) of the maximal correlation
S(X,Y)
.
For the case
n=1
, the lower bound was already observed by Reǹyi
[
39
]
, see also
[
4
, eq. 17]
. Here we give a proof for the general case.
Let

\displaystyle i^{*}=\argmax_{i\in[n]}\frac{\mathrm{Var}(\mathbb{E}[X_{i}|Y])}{\mathrm{Var}(X_{i})},

(44)

and set
\tilde{f}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
as

\tilde{f}(X)=\frac{X_{i^{*}}-\mathbb{E}X_{i^{*}}}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(X_{i^{*}})}},

such that
\mathbb{E}\tilde{f}(X)=0
and
\mathrm{Var}(\tilde{f}(X))=1
.
Since for non-negative numbers
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n},b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}
it holds that
\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}}\leq\max_{i\in[n]}\frac{a_{i}}{b_{i}}
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(\mathbb{E}[\tilde{f}(X)|Y])

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{Var}(\mathbb{E}[X_{i^{*}}|Y])}{\mathrm{Var}(X_{i^{*}})}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{Var}(\mathbb{E}[X_{i}|Y]}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{Var}(X_{i})}

(45)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{Var}(X_{i})-\mathbb{E}(\mathrm{Var}[X_{i}|Y)]}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{Var}(X_{i})}

(46)

\displaystyle=1-\frac{\mathrm{mmse}(X|Y)}{\mathbb{E}_{\mu}|X-\mathbb{E}[X]|^{2}}.

(47)

The statement follows by invoking (
9
)

\displaystyle S^{2}(X;Y)=\sup_{f}\mathrm{Var}(\mathbb{E}[f(X)|Y])\geq\mathrm{Var}(\mathbb{E}[\tilde{f}(X)|Y]).

(48)

2.2
Proof of Corollary
1.4

Let

\displaystyle\tilde{I}(\mu,\rho)

\displaystyle=I(X;\sqrt{\rho}X+Z),

(49)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathrm{mmse}}(\mu,\rho)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}|X-\mathbb{E}[X|\sqrt{\rho}X+Z]|^{2}.

(50)

Clearly,
I(\mu,s)=\tilde{I}(\mu,\frac{1}{s})
and
\mathrm{mmse}(\mu,s)=\widetilde{\mathrm{mmse}}(\mu,\frac{1}{s})
. By Corollary
1.3
, we therefore have that

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathrm{mmse}}(\mu,\rho)=\mathrm{mmse}\left(\mu,\frac{1}{\rho}\right)\geq\frac{nP}{1+\rho\bar{C}_{\mathrm{P}}(\mu)P}.

(51)

By the I-MMSE identity
[
24
, Theorem 2]
,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\rho}\tilde{I}(\mu,\rho)=\frac{1}{2}\widetilde{\mathrm{mmse}}(\mu,\rho).

(52)

Thus,

\displaystyle\tilde{I}(\mu,\rho)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\rho}\widetilde{\mathrm{mmse}}(\mu,t)dt\geq\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\rho}\frac{nP}{1+t\bar{C}_{\mathrm{P}}(\mu)P}dt=\frac{n}{2}\frac{1}{\bar{C}_{\mathrm{P}}(\mu)}\log\left(1+\bar{C}_{\mathrm{P}}(\mu)P\cdot\rho\right).

(53)

where in the last equality we have used the identity
\int_{0}^{\rho}\frac{a}{1+bt}dt=\frac{a}{b}\log(1+b\rho)
for
a,b>0
. The claimed result follows by recalling that
I(\mu,s)=\tilde{I}(\mu,\frac{1}{s})
.

3

-divergence and proofs for blurring time bounds

In this section we complete the proof of
Theorem
20
. Let
X
be a random vector attaining values in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
When proving Theorem
20
we may assume that
C_{P}(X)<\infty
as otherwise
the conclusion is vacuous. Recall from above that a log-concave random vector has a finite Poincaré constant, and that
for any additional random vector
Y
,

S^{2}(X,Y)=\sup_{f}\mathrm{Var}(\mathbb{E}[f(X)|Y])

(54)

where the supreumum runs over all measurable functions
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with
\mathbb{E}f(X)=0
and
\mathrm{Var}(f(X))=1
.
The requirement that
\mathbb{E}f(X)=0
is actually not necessary. Let
Z
be a standard Gaussian random
vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, independent of
X
. Recall that we are interested in the
\chi^{2}

contraction coefficient

\eta_{\chi^{2}}(\mu,s)=S^{2}(X,X+\sqrt{s}Z).

Following Klartag and Putterman
[
29
]
, for a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with
\mathbb{E}|f(X)|<\infty
and for
s>0
we write

Q_{s}f(X+\sqrt{s}Z)=\mathbb{E}[f(X)|X+\sqrt{s}Z].

Let
\mu
be the probability distribution on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
that is the distribution law of the random vector
X
,
and let
\mu_{s}
be the distribution law of
X+\sqrt{s}Z
. The operator

Q_{s}:L^{2}(\mu)\rightarrow L^{2}(\mu_{s})

is of norm at most one, since

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|Q_{s}f|^{2}d\mu_{s}=\mathbb{E}\left|Q_{s}f(X+\sqrt{s}Z)\right|^{2}=\mathbb{E}\left|\mathbb{E}[f(X)|X+\sqrt{s}Z]\right|^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}|f(X)|^{2}.

Lemma 3.1
.

For any
s>0
, the quantity
S^{2}(X,X+\sqrt{s}Z)
is the square of the operator norm of
Q_{s}:L^{2}(\mu)\rightarrow L^{2}(\mu_{s})
restricted
to the subspace of functions of
\mu
-average zero. In other words,
S^{2}(X,X+\sqrt{s}Z)
is the minimal number
M\geq 0
such that for any
f\in L^{2}(\mu)
with
\int fd\mu=0
,

\|Q_{s}f\|_{L^{2}(\mu_{s})}^{2}\leq M\cdot\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}.

(55)

Proof.

By (
54
), for any
s>0
,

S^{2}(X,X+\sqrt{s}Z)=\sup_{f}\mathrm{Var}(\mathbb{E}[f(X)|X+\sqrt{s}Z])=\sup_{f}\mathrm{Var}(Q_{s}f(X+\sqrt{s}Z)),

where the supremum runs over all
f
with
\mathbb{E}f(X)=0
and
\mathrm{Var}f(X)=1
. Therefore,

S^{2}(X,X+\sqrt{s}Z)=\sup_{f}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|Q_{s}f|^{2}d\mu_{s}=\sup_{f}\|Q_{s}f\|_{L^{2}(\mu_{s})}^{2},

where the supremum runs over all
f
with
\int fd\mu=0

\int f^{2}d\mu=1
.

The operator

\gamma_{s}(x)=(2\pi s)^{-n/2}\exp(-|x|^{2}/(2s))

for the density in
\mathbb{R}^{n}

By considering the joint distribution of

Q_{s}f(y)=\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\gamma_{s}(x-y)f(x)d\mu(x)}{(\rho*\gamma_{s})(y)}.

(57)

The proof of Theorem
20
requires two differentiations of the expression on the left-hand side

As in Klartag and Putterman

f
with
\mathbb{E}|f(X)|^{2}<\infty
,

\mathrm{Var}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[f(X)|X_{s}\right]\right)=\mathrm{Var}(Q_{s}f(X_{s}))\leq\frac{1}{1+s/C_{P}(\mu)}\cdot\mathrm{Var}f(X).

(65)

Recall that
\mu
is the distribution law of
X
.
Since
Q_{s}:L^{2}(\mu)\rightarrow L^{2}(\mu_{s})
is a bounded operator and
\mathcal{F}_{\mu}
is dense in
L^{2}(\mu)
,
it suffices to prove (
65
) under the additional assumption that
f\in\mathcal{F}_{\mu}
. Abbreviate
f_{s}=Q_{s}(f)
.
By using (
60
) and the Poincaré inequality, we get

\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\mathrm{Var}(f_{s}(X_{s}))\leq-\frac{1}{C_{\mathrm{P}}(X_{s})}\mathrm{Var}(f_{s}(X_{s}))\leq-\frac{1}{s+C_{\mathrm{P}}(X)}\mathrm{Var}(f_{s}(X_{s})),

where we used subadditivity in the last passage. Therefore

\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\log\mathrm{Var}(f_{s}(X_{s}))\leq-\frac{1}{s+C_{\mathrm{P}}(X)}.

By integrating from
0
to
s
we conclude that

\log\frac{\mathrm{Var}(f_{s}(X_{s}))}{\mathrm{Var}f(X)}\leq-\int_{0}^{s}\frac{dx}{x+C_{\mathrm{P}}(X)}=\log\frac{1}{1+s/C_{\mathrm{P}}(X)},

proving (
65
). This proves the right-hand side inequality in (
19
).
Let us now assume that
X
is log-concave and prove (
20
). This is in fact
proven in
[
29
]
, but let us briefly repeat the argument here for convenience. Let
0<\varepsilon<C_{\mathrm{P}}(\mu)
. By the definition of
C_{\mathrm{P}}(\mu)
,
there exists a non-constant function
f
such that

\mathrm{Var}f(X)\geq(C_{\mathrm{P}}(\mu)-\varepsilon)\cdot\mathbb{E}|\nabla f(X)|^{2}.

(66)

Thanks to the appendix of
[
7
]
, we may assume that
f
is smooth and compactly-supported in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and in particular
f\in\mathcal{F}_{\mu}
.
According to (
60
) and (
61
) we may differentiate the Rayleigh quotient

\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\frac{\mathbb{E}|\nabla f_{s}(X_{s})|^{2}}{\mathrm{Var}(f_{s}(X_{s}))}=-\mathbb{E}|\nabla^{2}\tilde{f}_{s}(X_{s})|^{2}-2\mathbb{E}(\nabla^{2}\psi_{s})(X_{s})\nabla\tilde{f}_{s}(X_{s})\cdot\nabla\tilde{f}_{s}(X_{s})-\mathbb{E}|\nabla\tilde{f_{s}}(X_{s})|^{4}

where we normalize
\tilde{f}_{s}=f_{s}/\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(f_{s}(X_{s})}
. As explained in the proof of
[
29
, Theorem 2.4]
, it follows from the spectral theorem that

\langle L_{\mu_{s}}^{2}\tilde{f}_{s},\tilde{f}_{s}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mu_{s})}\geq\langle L\tilde{f}_{s},\tilde{f}_{s}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mu_{s})}^{2}=\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\nabla\tilde{f}_{s}|^{2}d\mu_{s}\right)^{2}.

Therefore, from the Bochner formula (
64
),

\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\frac{\mathbb{E}|\nabla f_{s}(X_{s})|^{2}}{\mathrm{Var}(f_{s}(X_{s}))}\leq-\mathbb{E}(\nabla^{2}\psi_{s})(X_{s})\nabla\tilde{f}_{s}(X_{s})\cdot\nabla\tilde{f}_{s}(X_{s})\leq 0.

(67)

where the last inequality is the only place where we use log-concavity; indeed,
since
X
is log-concave, so is the random vector
X+\sqrt{s}Z
by the Prékopa-Leindler
inequality (see e.g. the first pages of Pisier
[
34
]
, or Davidovič, Korenbljum and Hacet
[
18
]
). Thus
e^{-\psi_{s}}
is a log-concave density, which amounts to the fact that the symmetric matrix
\nabla^{2}\psi_{s}
is positive semi-definite. Hence the term involving the Hessian
\nabla^{2}\psi_{s}
in (
67
) is non-positive.
Consequently,

\mathbb{E}|\nabla f_{s}(X_{s})|^{2}/\mathrm{Var}(f_{s}(X_{s}))
is non-increasing in
s
and

\log\frac{\mathrm{Var}(f_{s}(X_{s}))}{\mathrm{Var}f(X)}=-\int_{0}^{s}\frac{\mathbb{E}|\nabla f_{t}(X_{t})|^{2}}{\mathrm{Var}(f_{t}(X_{t}))}dt\geq-s\cdot\frac{\mathbb{E}|\nabla f(X)|^{2}}{\mathrm{Var}(f(X))}\geq-\frac{s}{C_{\mathrm{P}}(\mu)-\varepsilon}.

Hence for any
\varepsilon>0
we found
f
such that

\mathrm{Var}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[f(X)|X_{s}\right]\right)=\mathrm{Var}(f_{s}(X_{s}))\geq\exp\left(-\frac{s}{C_{\mathrm{P}}(\mu)-\varepsilon}\right)\mathrm{Var}f(X).

Since
\varepsilon>0
is arbitrary, and in view of Lemma
55
this proves that
S^{2}(X,X+\sqrt{s}Z)\geq\exp(-s/C_{\mathrm{P}}(\mu))
.
Inequality (
20
) is thus proven.

Thomas Courtade and Joseph Lehec communicated to us an alternative proof of the right-hand side inequality in (
19
). Their proof does not require differentiation with respect to the parameter
s
.

In this section we prove Propositions

We keep the notation

f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

\mu

As in the previous section, some care is needed when differentiating under the integral sign and when integrating by parts and neglecting the boundary terms. As opposed to the previous section analyzing the case of the

, the basic idea is to introduce a suitable class
\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\varphi}
of smooth functions, which allow for integrations by parts without boundary terms, and is preserved by the

From Klartag and Putterman

s>0
,

\frac{d}{ds}Q_{s}f=\Box_{s}Q_{s}f

(68)

where

\Box_{s}=L_{s}-\frac{\Delta}{2}

and
L_{s}=L_{\mu_{s}}
is the Laplace operator associated with the measure
\mu_{s}

We write
\rho_{s}
for the density of
\mu_{s}

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\varphi(x)=\varphi_{\lambda}(x)=x^{\lambda}-1
, for
\lambda>1
. Let
\mu
and
\nu
be probability distributions on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
D_{\varphi}(\nu\,\,||\,\,\mu)<\infty
, and assume
\mathbb{E}_{\mu}|X|^{4}<\infty
. Denote
f=d\nu/d\mu
such that
f_{s}=Q_{s}f=d\mu_{s}/d\nu_{s}
, for
s>0
. we have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{ds}\mathbb{E}\varphi(f_{s}(X_{s}))=-\frac{1}{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}\varphi^{\prime\prime}(f_{s}(X_{s}))|\nabla f_{s}(X_{s})|^{2}=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi^{\prime\prime}(f_{s})|\nabla f_{s}|^{2}d\mu_{s}.

(69)

In particular, if
D_{\text{KL}}(\nu\,\,||\,\,\mu)<\infty

\frac{d}{ds}\mathbb{E}f_{s}(X_{s})\log f_{s}(X_{s})=-\frac{1}{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}\frac{|\nabla f(X_{s})|^{2}}{f_{s}(X_{s})}

(70)

Proof.

We prove (
69
) below for any smooth, convex function
\varphi:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
with
\varphi(1)=0
, without justifying differentiation under the integral sign, and without justifying integration by parts. The justifications for these steps for
\varphi(x)=\varphi_{\lambda}(x)=x^{\lambda}-1
, for
\lambda>1
, under the assumptions that
D_{\varphi}(\nu\,\,||\,\,\mu)<\infty
, and
\mathbb{E}_{\mu}|X|^{4}<\infty
, are given in the appendix. We also prove in the appendix that (
69
) implies (
70
).

Recall that
\partial\rho_{s}/\partial s=\Delta\rho_{s}/2
by the heat equation, as
\mu_{s}=\mu*\gamma_{s}
and
\rho_{s}
is the density of
\mu_{s}
.
This and (
68
) are the main places where we use the heat equation in our arguments. Let us compute:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{ds}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi(f_{s})\rho_{s}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi^{\prime}(f_{s})\Box_{s}f_{s}\cdot\rho_{s}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi(f_{s})\frac{\Delta\rho_{s}}{2}.

Integrating by parts we continue with

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left[\varphi^{\prime}(f_{s})\left(L_{s}f_{s}-\frac{\Delta f_{s}}{2}\right)+\frac{\Delta(\varphi(f_{s}))}{2}\right]\rho_{s}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left[\varphi^{\prime}(f_{s})L_{s}f_{s}-\varphi^{\prime}(f_{s})\frac{\Delta f_{s}}{2}+\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(f_{s})\Delta f_{s}+\varphi^{\prime\prime}(f_{s})|\nabla f_{s}|^{2}}{2})\right]\rho_{s}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left[-\varphi^{\prime\prime}(f_{s})|\nabla f_{s}|^{2}+\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime}(f_{s})|\nabla f_{s}|^{2})}{2}\right]\rho_{s},

where we used the integration by parts
(
63
) in the last passage.

Proof of Proposition
1.5
and Proposition
1.10
.

Follow directly from Lemma
4.1
, and the definition of
D_{\varphi}
and
J_{\varphi}
.

We move on to computing the second derivative of
\mathbb{E}\varphi(f(X_{s}))
.

Conjecture 4.2
.

Denoting

\kappa=-\frac{1}{2\varphi^{\prime\prime}}\left(\frac{1}{\varphi^{\prime\prime}}\right)^{\prime\prime},

and

g_{s}=\varphi^{\prime}(f_{s})

we have, under mild regularity assumptions, the “Bochner-type” formula

\displaystyle\frac{d}{ds}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi^{\prime\prime}(f_{s})|\nabla f_{s}|^{2}d\mu_{s}=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left[2(\nabla^{2}\psi_{s})\nabla g_{s}\cdot\nabla g_{s}+\left|\nabla^{2}g_{s}\right|^{2}+\kappa(f_{s})|\nabla g_{s}|^{4}\right]\frac{d\mu_{s}}{\varphi^{\prime\prime}(f_{s})},

where we recall that
\rho_{s}=e^{-\psi_{s}}
.

Sketch of proof.

We assume below that differentiation under the integral sign is valid, and that all functions involved in integration in parts vanish. Justifying these assumptions is the missing step in proving this conjecture. We now prove the “Bochner-typ” formula above, under the assumption that those steps are indeed valid (i.e., ignoring all regularity issues).

Our approach follows the
M
-function of Ivanishvili and Volberg
[
26
]
and the dynamic approach to the
\Gamma
-calculus is presented in
[
29
]
.
We consider a function
M(x,y)
of two real variables of the form

M(x,y)=y\varphi^{\prime\prime}(x).

(71)

Thus
M(x,y)=y/x
in the case where
\varphi(x)=x\log x
and
M(x,y)=2y
if
\varphi(x)=(x-1)^{2}
. In view of Lemma
4.1
, we would need to compute

\frac{d}{ds}\mathbb{E}M(f_{s}(X_{s}),|\nabla f_{s}(X_{s})|^{2}).

By the chain rule, for any functions
f,g
,

\Delta M(f,g)=M_{x}\Delta f+M_{y}\Delta g+M_{xx}|\nabla f|^{2}+2M_{xy}\nabla f\cdot\nabla g+M_{yy}|\nabla g|^{2},

(72)

where we abbreviate
M_{x}=\partial M/\partial x,M_{y}=\partial M/\partial y
etc. Let us also introduce the dynamic
\Gamma
-calculus notation from
[
29
]
. We set

\Gamma_{1}(u,v)=\nabla u\cdot\nabla v

and

\Gamma_{2}(u,v):=\Box_{s}\Gamma_{1}(u,v)-\Gamma_{1}(\Box_{s}u,v)-\Gamma_{1}(u,\Box_{s}v)

which satisfies

\Gamma_{2}(u,u)=\|\nabla^{2}u\|_{HS}^{2}-2\nabla^{2}(\log\rho_{s})\nabla u\cdot\nabla u.

(73)

We abbreviate
\Gamma_{1}(u)=\Gamma_{1}(u,u)
and
\Gamma_{2}(u)=\Gamma_{2}(u,u)
.

We first prove that for a general function
M
of two variables,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{ds}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}M(f_{s}(X_{s}),|\nabla f_{s}(X_{s})|^{2})

(74)

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left[M_{y}\Gamma_{2}(f_{s})+\frac{M_{xx}}{2}\Gamma_{1}(f_{s})+M_{xy}\Gamma_{1}(f_{s},\Gamma_{1}(f_{s}))+\frac{M_{yy}}{2}\Gamma_{1}(\Gamma_{1}(f_{s}))\right]d\mu_{s}.

To this end, abbreviate
M=M(f_{s},|\nabla f_{s}|^{2}),M_{x}=M_{x}(f_{s},|\nabla f_{s}|^{2})

etc. Then,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{ds}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}M(f_{s},|\nabla f_{s}|^{2})\rho_{s}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left[\frac{\Delta M}{2}+M_{x}\Box_{s}f_{s}+2M_{y}\Gamma_{1}(\Box_{s}f_{s},f_{s})\right]\rho_{s}

(75)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left[\frac{\Delta M}{2}+M_{x}\Box_{s}f_{s}+M_{y}\left(\Box_{s}\Gamma_{1}(f_{s},f_{s})-\Gamma_{2}(f_{s},f_{s})\right)\right]\rho_{s}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left[\frac{\Delta M}{2}+M_{x}\left(L_{s}-\frac{\Delta}{2}\right)f_{s}+M_{y}\left(\left(L_{s}-\frac{\Delta}{2}\right)\Gamma_{1}(f_{s})-\Gamma_{2}(f_{s})\right)\right]\rho_{s}.

By (
72
) with
f=f_{s},g=\Gamma_{1}(f_{s})
,

\Delta M=M_{x}\Delta f_{s}+M_{y}\Delta\Gamma_{1}(f_{s})+M_{xx}\Gamma_{1}(f_{s})+2M_{xy}\Gamma_{1}(f_{s},\Gamma_{1}(f_{s}))+M_{yy}\Gamma_{1}(\Gamma_{1}(f_{s})).

Therefore,

\frac{1}{2}\left(\Delta M-M_{x}\Delta f_{s}-M_{y}\Delta\Gamma_{1}(f_{s})\right)=\frac{M_{xx}}{2}\Gamma_{1}(f_{s})+M_{xy}\Gamma_{1}(f_{s},\Gamma_{1}(f_{s}))+\frac{M_{yy}}{2}\Gamma_{1}(\Gamma_{1}(f_{s})).

Consequently, the integral in (
75
) equals

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left[M_{x}L_{s}f_{s}+M_{y}L_{s}\Gamma_{1}(f_{s})-M_{y}\Gamma_{2}(f_{s})+\frac{M_{xx}}{2}\Gamma_{1}(f_{s})+M_{xy}\Gamma_{1}(f_{s},\Gamma_{1}(f_{s}))+\frac{M_{yy}}{2}\Gamma_{1}(\Gamma_{1}(f_{s}))\right]\rho_{s}

Integrate by parts the two terms involving
L_{s}
using (
63
) to obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}

\displaystyle\left[M_{x}L_{s}f_{s}+M_{y}L_{s}\Gamma_{1}(f_{s})\right]\rho_{s}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left[-\nabla M_{x}\cdot\nabla f_{s}-\nabla M_{y}\cdot\nabla\Gamma_{1}(f_{s})\right]\rho_{s}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left[-(M_{xx}\nabla f_{s}+M_{xy}\nabla\Gamma_{1}(f_{s}))\cdot\nabla f_{s}-(M_{xy}\nabla f_{s}+M_{yy}\nabla\Gamma_{1}(f_{s}))\cdot\nabla\Gamma_{1}(f_{s})\right]\rho_{s}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left[-M_{xx}\Gamma_{1}(f_{s})-2M_{xy}\Gamma_{1}(\Gamma_{1}(f_{s}),f_{s})-M_{yy}\Gamma_{1}(\Gamma_{1}(f_{s}))\right]\rho_{s}.

Hence, the integral in (
75
) equals

-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left[M_{y}\Gamma_{2}(f_{s})+\frac{M_{xx}}{2}\Gamma_{1}(f_{s})+M_{xy}\Gamma_{1}(f_{s},\Gamma_{1}(f_{s}))+\frac{M_{yy}}{2}\Gamma_{1}(\Gamma_{1}(f_{s}))\right]\rho_{s}

and (
74
) is proven. In the specific case where
M
is the function given by (
71
) we conclude from (
74
) that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{ds}

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi^{\prime\prime}(f_{s})|\nabla f_{s}|^{2}d\mu_{s}

(76)

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left[\varphi^{\prime\prime}(f_{s})\Gamma_{2}(f_{s})+\frac{\varphi^{(4)}(f_{s})}{2}\Gamma_{1}(f_{s})^{2}+\varphi^{(3)}(f_{s})\Gamma_{1}(f_{s},\Gamma_{1}(f_{s}))\right]d\mu_{s}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left[2\varphi^{\prime\prime}(\nabla^{2}\psi_{s})\nabla f_{s}\cdot\nabla f_{s}+\varphi^{\prime\prime}|\nabla^{2}f_{s}|^{2}+\frac{\varphi^{(4)}}{2}|\nabla f_{s}|^{4}+2\varphi^{(3)}(\nabla^{2}f_{s})\nabla f_{s}\cdot\nabla f_{s}\right]d\mu_{s}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left[2\varphi^{\prime\prime}(\nabla^{2}\psi_{s})\nabla f_{s}\cdot\nabla f_{s}+\varphi^{\prime\prime}\left|\nabla^{2}f_{s}+\frac{\varphi^{(3)}}{\varphi^{\prime\prime}}\nabla f_{s}\otimes\nabla f_{s}\right|^{2}+\left\{\frac{\varphi^{(4)}}{2}-\frac{\left(\varphi^{(3)}\right)^{2}}{\varphi^{\prime\prime}}\right\}|\nabla f_{s}|^{4}\right]d\mu_{s}

Note that

\frac{\varphi^{(4)}}{2}-\frac{\left(\varphi^{(3)}\right)^{2}}{\varphi^{\prime\prime}}=-\frac{(\varphi^{\prime\prime})^{2}}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\varphi^{\prime\prime}}\right)^{\prime\prime}=(\varphi^{\prime\prime})^{3}\kappa.

Consequently,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{ds}

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi^{\prime\prime}(f_{s})|\nabla f_{s}|^{2}d\mu_{s}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left[2\varphi^{\prime\prime}(\nabla^{2}\psi_{s})\nabla f_{s}\cdot\nabla f_{s}+\varphi^{\prime\prime}\left|\nabla^{2}f_{s}+\frac{\varphi^{(3)}}{\varphi^{\prime\prime}}\nabla f_{s}\otimes\nabla f_{s}\right|^{2}+\kappa(\varphi^{\prime})^{3}|\nabla f_{s}|^{4}\right]d\mu_{s}.

Since
g_{s}=\varphi^{\prime}(f_{s})
we have
\nabla g_{s}=\varphi^{\prime\prime}(f_{s})\nabla f_{s}
and
\nabla^{2}g_{s}=\varphi^{\prime\prime}(f_{s})\nabla^{2}f_{s}+\varphi^{(3)}(f_{s})\nabla f_{s}\otimes\nabla f_{s}
, and the lemma is proven.

5
Proofs of inequalities related to
\varphi
-Sobolev constants

We keep the notation and assumptions of the previous section.
We need to use the subadditivity property of the
\varphi
-Sobolev constants, proven by Chafaï
[
11
, Corollary 3.1]
in greater generality. For the convenience of the reader we include here a statement and a proof
of the following proposition, as well as of Proposition
1.9
, which also goes back to Chafaï
[
11
]
.

Theorem 5.1

(“Subadditivity of the
\varphi
-Sobolev constants”, Chafaï
[
11
]
)
.

Let
X
and
Y
be independent random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Let
\varphi:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
be a smooth, convex function with
\varphi(1)=0
such that
1/\varphi^{\prime\prime}
is concave. Then,

C_{\varphi}(X+Y)\leq C_{\varphi}(X)+C_{\varphi}(Y).

Proof.

for any locally-Lipschitz function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow[0,\infty)

with
\mathbb{E}|f(X+Y)|<\infty
, denoting
g(x)=\mathbb{E}f(x+Y)
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle\varphi(f(X+Y))-\varphi(\mathbb{E}(f(X+Y)))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\mathbb{E}_{Y}\varphi(f(X+Y))-\varphi(\mathbb{E}_{Y}\varphi(f(X+Y)))\right]+\mathbb{E}_{X}\varphi(g(X))-\varphi(\mathbb{E}_{X}g(X))

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{\varphi}(Y)}{2}\mathbb{E}_{X}\mathbb{E}_{Y}\varphi^{\prime\prime}(f(X+Y))|\nabla f(X+Y)|^{2}+\frac{C_{\varphi}(X)}{2}\mathbb{E}_{X}\varphi^{\prime\prime}(g(X))|\nabla g(X)|^{2}.

To conclude the proof it remains to show that for any fixed
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\varphi^{\prime\prime}(g(x))|\nabla g(x)|^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}_{Y}\varphi^{\prime\prime}(f(x+Y))|\nabla f(x+Y)|^{2}.

Indeed, this would imply that

\mathbb{E}\varphi(f(X+Y))-\varphi(\mathbb{E}(\varphi(X+Y)))\leq\frac{C_{\varphi}(Y)+C_{\varphi}(X)}{2}\mathbb{E}\varphi^{\prime\prime}(f(X+Y))|\nabla f(X+Y)|^{2},

and hence
C_{\varphi}(X+Y)\leq C_{\varphi}(Y)+C_{\varphi}(X)
. Since
\nabla g(x)=\mathbb{E}\nabla f(x+Y)
, all that remains is to show that

\varphi^{\prime\prime}(\mathbb{E}f(x+Y))|\mathbb{E}\nabla f(x+Y)|^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}_{Y}\varphi^{\prime\prime}(f(x+Y))|\nabla f(x+Y)|^{2}.

By Jensen’s inequality, this would follow once we show that the function

(x,y)\mapsto\varphi^{\prime\prime}(x)|y|^{2}\qquad\qquad(x>0,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n})

(77)

is jointly convex in
x
and
y
. Since
\varphi^{\prime\prime}(x)|y|^{2}=\sum_{j}\varphi^{\prime\prime}(x)y_{j}^{2}
, it suffices to show that
the function

(x,y)\mapsto\varphi^{\prime\prime}(x)y^{2}\qquad\qquad(x>0,y\in\mathbb{R})

(78)

is jointly convex in
(x,y)
. The Hessian of this function is the matrix

\left(\begin{matrix}\varphi^{(4)}(x)y^{2}&2\varphi^{(3)}(x)y\\
2\varphi^{(3)}(x)y&2\varphi^{\prime\prime}(x)\end{matrix}\right)

Since this symmetric
2\times 2
matrix has a non-negative entry on the diagonal, namely
2\varphi^{\prime\prime}(x)
, by the Sylvester criterion it is
positive semi-definite if and only if its determinant is non-negative. The determinant equals

2y^{2}\left[\varphi^{\prime\prime}\varphi^{(4)}-2(\varphi^{(3)})^{2}\right]=-2y^{2}(\varphi^{\prime\prime})^{3}\left(\frac{1}{\varphi^{\prime\prime}}\right)^{\prime\prime},

where we used the formula
(1/\varphi^{\prime\prime})^{\prime\prime}=-(\varphi^{\prime\prime}\varphi^{(4)}-2(\varphi^{(3)})^{2})/(\varphi^{\prime\prime})^{3}
.
Since
\varphi^{\prime\prime}\geq 0
and
1/\varphi^{\prime\prime}
is concave, the determinant is non-negative, and the function in (
78
) is convex. This completes the proof.

Proof of Proposition
1.8
.

For any locally-Lipschitz function
f
,

\mathbb{E}\varphi(f(X))-\varphi(\mathbb{E}f(X))\leq\frac{C_{\varphi}(X)}{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}\varphi^{\prime\prime}(f(X))|\nabla f(X)|^{2}.

Let
g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a Lipschitz, bounded function. Then there exists
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that for any
0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}
, the function
g_{0}=1+\varepsilon g
attains values at
I
. In fact, since
\varphi
is smooth, there exists
M>0
and
\varepsilon_{1}<\varepsilon_{0}
such that for
\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{1}
,

\left|\varphi(g_{0}(x))-\varepsilon\varphi^{\prime}(1)g(x)-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\varphi^{\prime\prime}(1)g^{2}(x)\right|\leq\varepsilon^{3}M

and

\varphi^{\prime\prime}(g_{0}(x))\geq(1-\varepsilon M)\cdot\varphi^{\prime\prime}(1).

Consequently, for any
0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{1}
,

\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\varphi^{\prime\prime}(1)Var(g(X))-2M\varepsilon^{3}\leq\mathbb{E}\varphi(f(X))-\varphi(\mathbb{E}f(X))

and

\mathbb{E}\varphi^{\prime\prime}(f(X))|\nabla f(X)|^{2}\geq(1-\varepsilon M)\varepsilon^{2}\varphi^{\prime\prime}(1)\cdot\mathbb{E}|\nabla g(X)|^{2}.

By using the
\varphi
-Sobolev inequality and dividing by
\varepsilon^{2}\varphi^{\prime\prime}(1)>0
, and then letting
\varepsilon
tends to zero we obtain

Proof of Proposition
1.9
.

Let
Z
be a standard Gaussian random vector, independent of
X
.
When
X
is a standard Gaussian, the operator
Q_{s}
has a pleasant form given by the Mehler formula. Recall that in general,

Q_{s}f(X+\sqrt{s}Z)=\mathbb{E}\left[f(X)|X+\sqrt{s}Z\right]

and hence

\mathbb{E}Q_{s}f(X+\sqrt{s}Z)=\mathbb{E}f(X).

In the case where
X
is Gaussian, the density of
X
conditioned on
X+\sqrt{s}Z=y
is

\frac{\gamma_{1}(x)\gamma_{s}(y-x)}{\gamma_{1+s}(y)}=\gamma_{\frac{s}{s+1}}\left(x-\frac{y}{s+1}\right)

is the Gaussian density, as in (

is that of a Gaussian random vector of mean

and covariance
\frac{s}{s+1}{\rm Id}
. Therefore, for any

,

Q_{s}f(y)=\mathbb{E}f\left(\frac{y}{s+1}+\sqrt{\frac{s}{s+1}}Z\right)

(80)

and

\nabla Q_{s}f(y)=\frac{1}{s+1}\mathbb{E}\nabla f\left(\frac{y}{s+1}+\sqrt{\frac{s}{s+1}}Z\right).

Consequently, in all of

\varphi^{\prime\prime}(Q_{s}f)|\nabla Q_{s}f|^{2}=\frac{1}{(s+1)^{2}}\cdot\varphi^{\prime\prime}(Q_{s}f)|Q_{s}(\nabla f)|^{2}

(81)

where
Q_{s}
is acting on a vector field entry by entry, i.e.,
Q_{s}(\nabla f)=(Q_{s}(\partial_{1}f),\ldots Q_{s}(\partial_{n}f))
.
Since the function in (
77
) is convex in
x
and
y
, and since
Q_{s}
is an averaging operator as we see from (

), we may use Jensen’s inequality.
We obtain from (

\varphi^{\prime\prime}(Q_{s}f)|\nabla Q_{s}f|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{(s+1)^{2}}\cdot Q_{s}\left[\varphi^{\prime\prime}(f)|\nabla f|^{2}\right]

(82)

Abbreviate

, and observe that

\mathbb{E}\varphi(f(X))-\varphi(\mathbb{E}f(X))=-\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\mathbb{E}\varphi(\mathbb{E}\left[f(X)|X+\sqrt{s}Z\right])ds=-\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\mathbb{E}\varphi(f_{s}(X_{s}))ds.

From the generalized de Bruijn identity in the form of Lemma
4.1
above, and from (

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\varphi(f(X))-\varphi(\mathbb{E}f(X))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\varphi^{\prime\prime}(f_{s}(X_{s}))|\nabla f_{s}(X_{s})|^{2}ds

\displaystyle D_{\varphi^{2}}(\nu_{s}\|\mu_{s})

\displaystyle D_{\varphi}(\nu_{s}\|\mu_{s})\leq D_{\varphi}(\nu\|\mu)<\infty.

[
