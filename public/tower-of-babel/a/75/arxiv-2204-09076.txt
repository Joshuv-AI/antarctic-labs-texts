Spatial Search via Memoryless Walk with Selfloop
Peter Høyer, Janet Leahy

Abstract
The defining feature of memoryless quantum walks is that they operate on the vertex space of a graph, and therefore can be used to produce search algorithms with minimal memory. We present a memoryless walk that can find a unique marked vertex on a two-dimensional grid. Our walk is based on the construction proposed by Falk, which tessellates the grid with squares of size 2 × 2 2\times 2 . Our walk uses minimal memory, O ⁡ ( N ​ log ⁡ N ) O(\!\sqrt{N\log N}) applications of the walk operator, and outputs the marked vertex with vanishing error probability. To accomplish this, we apply a selfloop to the marked vertex—a technique we adapt from interpolated walks. We prove that with our explicit choice of selfloop weight, this forces the action of the walk asymptotically into a single rotational space. We characterize this space and as a result, show that our memoryless walk produces the marked vertex with a success probability asymptotically approaching one.



1
Introduction

Search problems are one of the foundational applications of quantum algorithms, and are one of the situations in which quantum algorithms are proven to provide a speedup over their classical counterparts.
For example, Grover’s search algorithm
[
Gro96
]
can find a single element in an
N
-element database with
\Theta(\sqrt{N})
quantum queries, while any classical algorithm requires
\Omega(N)
queries for the same task.

In spatial search, the goal is to find a “marked” element on a graph, with the restriction that in a single time step, amplitude can only be moved between adjacent vertices on the graph. Such restrictions can emerge from some underlying physical structure
[
AA05
]
, or from the computational cost of moving from one vertex to another
[
Amb07
]
. Quantum spatial search was first considered by Benioff
[
Ben02
]
, who showed that direct application of Grover’s search on the grid does not yield a speedup over classical algorithms. A near-quadratic speed-up was then discovered using a divide-and-conquer quantum algorithm
[
AA05
]
and quantum walks
[
AKR05
,
CG04
,
San08
]
.

Quantum walks are the quantum counterparts of classical random walks. Memoryless, or coinless, walks are a less commonly studied type of quantum walk, but have several advantages over other models. As the name suggests, they operate directly on the vertex space of the graph. This differentiates them from coined or Szegedy-style quantum walks, which use extra registers to encode the previous location of the walker. Memoryless walks can therefore be used to produce search algorithms with optimal memory requirements. They also have a simple structure, alternating two or more non-commuting reflections that can be derived from tessellations of the underlying graph. A proposal for implementing memoryless walks using superconducting microwave resonators is given by
[
MdOP17
]
, and an implementation of memoryless walks on IBM quantum computers is in
[
AAMP20
]
.

Staggered walks are a class of memoryless walks and were defined by Portugal et al. in
[
PSFG16
]
. Staggered walks are based on graph tessellations into cliques, which enforces the spatial search constraint and gives a method for constructing memoryless walks on general graphs. Ref.
[
PSFG16
]
shows that any quantum walk on a graph
G
in the standard Szegedy model
[
Sze04
]
can be converted to a staggered walk on the underlying line graph of
G
. The conversion preserves the asymptotic cost, the success probability, and the space requirement. Staggered walks have also been used to derive relationships between memoryless, coined, Szegedy, and continuous quantum walks
[
PBF15
,
Por16a
,
Por16b
,
PdOM17
,
CP18
,
KPSS18
]
.

A memoryless walk on the line was given by Patel, Raghunathan, and Rungta in
[
PRR05
]
, who give and analyse a walk with Hamiltonians, and they note that their walk operator resembles the staggered fermion formalism. In
[
PRR10
]
, Patel, Raghunathan and Rahaman present numerical simulations on the extension of this walk to the
N
-vertex grid. Their results show that it finds a unique marked vertex in
\Theta(\sqrt{N\log N})
applications of the walk operator with success probability
\Theta(\frac{1}{\log N})
, and that by using an ancilla qubit, the success probability can be improved to
\Theta(1)
.

Falk
[
Fal13
]
gives a memoryless walk on the two-dimensional grid, constructing a discrete walk operator by reflecting about two alternating tessellations of the grid. The walk by Falk was analysed by Ambainis, Portugal and Nahimovs
[
APN15
]
, who proved that it finds a unique marked vertex on the grid using
\Theta(\sqrt{N\log N})
applications of the walk operator with success probability
\Theta(\frac{1}{\log N})
. Portugal and Fernandes
[
PF17
]
give a memoryless walk with Hamiltonians that also finds a unique marked vertex on the two-dimensional grid with
\Theta(\sqrt{N\log N})
applications of the walk operator and success probability
\Theta(\frac{1}{\log N})
.

The success probability of
[
PRR05
,
PRR10
]
,
[
Fal13
]
and
[
PF17
]
is sub-constant. The success probability can be improved to
\Theta(1)
by applying amplitude amplification
[
BHMT02
]
, but that would increase the number of steps by a factor of
\Theta(\sqrt{\log N})
and introduce additional operators in an implementation. Two alternative methods
[
APN15
,
PF17
]
for increasing the success probability are the post-processing local neighborhood search in
[
ABN+12
]
and Tulsi’s proposal of adding an ancilla qubit to the grid
[
PRR10
,
Tul08
]
.

In this paper, we give a memoryless walk that finds the marked vertex in
\Theta(\sqrt{N\log N})
steps with vanishing error probability. Our walk uses minimal memory and preserves the simple structure given by alternating tessellations of the grid. To do this, we augment the basic memoryless construction by showing how the interpolated walks of Krovi et al.
[
KMOR16
]
can be adapted to the vertex space of a graph. We define our memoryless walk using the tessellations shown in Figure
1
, which divide the grid into squares of size
2\times 2
. This is the same tessellation structure analysed by
[
APN15
]
, who proved that using Falk’s construction, the maximum success probability of the corresponding memoryless walk scales with
\Theta(\frac{1}{\log N})
. By using a selfloop to force the action of the walk into a single two-dimensional subspace, we modify their walk so that the maximum success probability asymptotically approaches one.

Classically, the interpolated version of a random walk is constructed by adding weighted selfloop edges to marked vertices. Applying Szegedy’s isometry
[
Sze04
]
to interpolated walks produces quantum walks that can find a unique marked vertex on any graph
[
KMOR16
]
. This approach has recently been used to solve the spatial search problem on any graph with a quadratic speedup over classical random walks, even for the case of multiple marked vertices. Solutions of this form have been obtained for both the discrete
[
AGJK20
,
AGJ21
]
and continuous
[
ACNR21
]
models using quantum walks that operate on the edge space of a graph. Quantum interpolated walks can be simulated by controlled quantum walks
[
DH17
]
, preserving the asymptotic cost, the success probability, and the space requirement.

To extend the approach of
[
KMOR16
]
to the memoryless setting, we introduce a new state corresponding to a selfloop on the marked vertex. Rather than reflecting about the marked vertex, we reflect about an interpolation between the selfloop state and the marked vertex. The result is a memoryless walk parametrized by the weight of the selfloop,
s
. We prove in our analysis that with our explicit choice of weight, this forces the evolution of the initial state into a single rotational subspace of our walk operator. As a result, our walk achieves a success probability
1-O(\frac{1}{\log N})
with
\Theta(\sqrt{N\log N})
steps and minimal memory.

Most of our analysis considers the eigenvector of an operator with the smallest positive eigenphase. We use the term “slowest eigenvector” to refer to this eigenvector, and “slowest rotational subspace” to refer to the subspace spanned by this eigenvector and its conjugate.

To prove our main result, we present a set of techniques for analysing memoryless walks. We analyse an operator
\mathsf{W}
, whose spectra are completely known, composed with a two-dimensional rotation
\mathsf{F}
. As part of our proof, we determine the asymptotic behaviour of both the smallest positive eigenphase of
\mathsf{W}\mathsf{F}
and its associated eigenvector. The result is a precise asymptotic description of the slowest rotational subspace of our walk operator.

The techniques we use to obtain this description are general enough that they could be applied in other contexts as well.

We begin by defining our walk in Section
2
. An overview of the paper layout and proof structure is given in Section
2.1
.

Walk construction and main result

We consider the task of finding a unique marked vertex on a two-dimensional grid with
{n_{r}}

{n_{c}}

{n_{r}}

(Main result)

Fix

2.1
Proof strategy

Our proof of Theorem
1
is based on the analysis of an intermediate walk operator, which we define below.

To simplify the analysis, we also introduce a change of basis. This will allow us to compute necessary properties of the walk spectra, as the eigenvectors of
\mathsf{B}\mathsf{A}
factor into product states under this basis change. Let
\mathsf{cz}
be an operator acting on the pair of least significant bits in a tensor product space, with the action defined by
{|{i,j}\rangle}\mapsto-{|{i,j}\rangle}
if both
i
and
j
are even, and being the identity otherwise. Let
\mathsf{cz}
act trivially on
{|{\circlearrowleft}\rangle}

\mathsf{X}

\mathsf{X}_{z}=\mathsf{cz}\circ\mathsf{X}\circ\mathsf{cz}

{|{{\pi_{z}}}\rangle}=\mathsf{cz}{|{\pi}\rangle}

We define

\displaystyle\mathsf{W}

\displaystyle=\normalsize(\mathsf{B}\mathsf{A}\normalsize)_{z},

(5)

\displaystyle\mathsf{F}

\displaystyle=\normalsize(\mathsf{A}\mathsf{\tilde{G}}\mathsf{A}\mathsf{\tilde{G}}\normalsize)_{z},

(6)

so that

(7)

This is analogous to the decomposition of

Note that

is input-independent, while

is a two-dimensional rotation, and therefore can be decomposed as the product of two reflections, which we write as

Our intermediate walk consists of
\mathsf{W}

\mathsf{F}_{1}

This choice of intermediate walk has the advantage that it consists of a real operator whose spectra are completely known, composed with a one-dimensional reflection. This allows us to apply existing results about operators of this type, including the eigenvector analysis of
[

]
and the flip-flop theorem from
[

Our proof is based on a tight characterization of the slowest rotational subspace of the intermediate walk

We show that with our choice of selfloop, the action of
\mathsf{W}\mathsf{F}_{1}
on
{|{{\pi_{z}}}\rangle}
can be reduced asymptotically to a Grover-like rotation in the slowest two-dimensional rotational subspace of
\mathsf{W}\mathsf{F}_{1}

To prove our main result, we relate the slowest rotational subspaces of
\mathsf{W}\mathsf{F}_{1}
and
\mathsf{W}\mathsf{F}
in Section
6
. We show that composing
\mathsf{W}\mathsf{F}_{1}
with the reflection
\mathsf{F}_{2}
does not significantly alter the slowest rotational subspace, and therefore that
\mathsf{W}\mathsf{F}
has the same asymptotic behaviour as
\mathsf{W}\mathsf{F}_{1}
when applied to
{|{{\pi_{z}}}\rangle}
. The proof of Theorem
1
follows from the basis-change relationship between
\mathsf{U}
and
\mathsf{W}\mathsf{F}
given in
equation (
7
).

With our approach, we are able to derive precise statements about the behaviour of an operator composed with a two-dimensional rotation. This addresses a more general challenge in analysing quantum algorithms, and may have applications outside of memoryless walks.

5
Reduction to the slowest subspace

Our memoryless walk, given in Theorem
1
, achieves a success probability asymptotically close to 1. This is possible because our choice of
s
reduces the walk asymptotically to a rotation in a single two-dimensional subspace. As we show, this subspace is exactly the slowest rotational subspace of the applied walk operator.
We prove this by giving a tight description of the smallest positive eigenvalue of the walk operator and its associated eigenvector. We also show that the two-dimensional rotation induced by the walk maps the initial state to the desired state,
{|{\circlearrowleft}\rangle}
.

We consider two walk operators. The first consists of the real operator
\mathsf{W}
composed with the one-dimensional reflection
\mathsf{F}_{1}
. The second consists of
\mathsf{W}
composed with the two-dimensional rotation
\mathsf{F}
. We discuss the first operator in this section, and then use the results to derive properties of the second operator in Section
6
.

First, we prove three key lemmas about the slowest rotational subspace of
\mathsf{W}\mathsf{F}_{1}
. Lemma
8
gives a tight bound on the smallest positive eigenphase of
\mathsf{W}\mathsf{F}_{1}
, and Lemmas
10
and
11
characterize the asymptotic behaviour of its associated eigenvector. These three lemmas show that the action of
\mathsf{W}\mathsf{F}_{1}
on
{|{{\pi_{z}}}\rangle}
can be reduced to a rotation in the slowest rotational subspace. These results are the basis of the methods used in Section
6
to prove our main result.

The intermediate operator
\mathsf{W}\mathsf{F}_{1}
is fundamentally a tool for analysis. However, it is interesting to note that
\mathsf{W}\mathsf{F}_{1}
can be used directly to find the marked state. The proof of the corollary below follows from a similar argument to our proof of the main theorem.

Corollary 6

Fix
s=1-\frac{1}{N+1}
and suppose
{n_{r}}={n_{c}}
. Then there exists a constant
c>0
such that after
c\sqrt{N\log N}
applications of
\mathsf{W}\mathsf{F}_{1}
to
{|{{\pi_{z}}}\rangle}
, measuring the state will produce
{|{\circlearrowleft}\rangle}
with probability
1-e(N)
, where
e(N)\in O(\frac{1}{\log N})
.

Comparing Corollary
6
with Theorem
1
shows that most of the finding behaviour of the memoryless walk comes from the first reflection
\mathsf{F}_{1}
. In Section
6
, we show that composing
\mathsf{W}\mathsf{F}_{1}
with the second reflection
\mathsf{F}_{2}
only changes the behaviour of the walk slightly, leading to our proof of Theorem
1
.

Our proofs of both Corollary
6
and our main theorem rely on the following observation.

Lemma 7

The (unnormalized) vector

\displaystyle{|{{\mathsf{U}_{0}}}\rangle}={|{{\pi_{z}}}\rangle}-\sqrt{\frac{s}{(1-s)N}}{|{\circlearrowleft}\rangle}

(+1)
-eigenvector for each of
\mathsf{W}

\mathsf{F}_{2}

Proof

Both
{|{{\pi_{z}}}\rangle}

{|{\circlearrowleft}\rangle}

(+1)
-eigenvectors of
\mathsf{W}
, so
{|{{\mathsf{U}_{0}}}\rangle}
is a
(+1)
-eigenvector of
\mathsf{W}

{|{{\mathsf{U}_{0}}}\rangle}
is a
(+1)
-eigenvector of
\mathsf{F}_{1}
, we compute

\displaystyle\langle{{\mathsf{U}_{0}}}|{{f_{1}}}\rangle=\frac{2\sqrt{s}}{\sqrt{N(4-3s)}}+\frac{\sqrt{s}}{\sqrt{4-3s}}\langle{{\pi_{z}}}|{-}\rangle=0.

5.1
Smallest eigenphase of
\mathsf{W}\mathsf{F}_{1}

We choose the operator
\mathsf{W}\mathsf{F}_{1}

An overview of the applied results is given in Appendix

Lemma 8

{\varphi_{1}}
of
\mathsf{W}\mathsf{F}_{1}
satisfies
{\varphi_{1}}\in\Theta(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N\log N}})

Proof

First, we note that the smallest positive eigenvalue of
\mathsf{W}
is given by

\displaystyle\theta_{10}=\,\textup{acos}\bigg(2\cos^{2}\!\bigg(\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{N}}\bigg)-1\bigg)=\frac{4\pi}{\sqrt{N}}.

We know by Lemma

{|{{f_{1}}}\rangle}
satisfies

\displaystyle\|\Pi_{00}{|{{f_{1}}}\rangle}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{8}{N+4},

\displaystyle\|\Pi_{kl}{|{{f_{1}}}\rangle}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\dim(\mathsf{W}_{kl})}{N+4}

\displaystyle\textup{ for all $k,l$ not both $0$}.

In particular,
{|{{f_{1}}}\rangle}
overlaps all eigenspaces of
\mathsf{W}
, so by Theorem
17

{\varphi_{1}}<\theta_{10}=\frac{4\pi}{\sqrt{N}}
.

By Lemma
16
,
{\varphi_{1}}
must also satisfy the equation

\displaystyle\|\Pi_{00}{|{{f_{1}}}\rangle}\|^{2}\cot\!\bigg(\frac{{\varphi_{1}}}{2}\bigg)+\sum_{{kl}\neq 00}\|\Pi_{kl}{|{{f_{1}}}\rangle}\|^{2}\cot\!\bigg(\frac{{\varphi_{1}}-\theta_{kl}}{2}\bigg)=0,

\mathsf{W}
except the

-eigenspace,

Note that the
(-1)
-eigenspace is included this sum.
Because

\displaystyle\|\Pi_{00}{|{{f_{1}}}\rangle}\|^{2}\cot\!\bigg(\frac{{\varphi_{1}}}{2}\bigg)\in\Theta\Big(\frac{1}{{\varphi_{1}}N}\Big).

Therefore, for equation (

\displaystyle\sum_{{kl}\neq 00}\|\Pi_{kl}{|{{f_{1}}}\rangle}\|^{2}\cot\!\bigg(\frac{\theta_{kl}-{\varphi_{1}}}{2}\bigg)\in\Theta\Big(\frac{1}{{\varphi_{1}}N}\Big).

7
Conclusion

We give a quantum walk that uses minimal memory and
\Theta(\sqrt{N\log N})
steps to find a unique marked vertex on a two-dimensional grid. In doing so, we show how interpolated walks can be adapted to the memoryless setting. By adding a selfloop to the marked vertex, our walk boosts the probability of measuring the marked state from
O(\frac{1}{\log N})
to
1-O(\frac{1}{\log N})
, while preserving the simplicity of the tessellation-based structure.

We give a precise analysis of how the selfloop affects the walk dynamics by showing that our walk asymptotically reduces to a rotation in a single two-dimensional subspace. Applying this rotation evolves the initial state to the selfloop state, from which the marked state can be obtained by straightforward amplitude amplification.

As part of our proof, we give a precise description of the slowest rotational subspace of our memoryless walk operator. This is done using its decomposition into a real operator composed with a two-dimensional rotation. The techniques we use to analyse such an operator are general enough they have the potential to be used in the analysis of other walks as well. This includes developing and analysing memory-optimal spatial search algorithms for other types of graph, as well as for handling graphs with multiple marked vertices.

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{N}=\|\langle{{a_{00}}}|{w_{kl}^{00}}\rangle\|^{2}+\|\langle{{a_{00}}}|{w_{kl^{\prime}}^{11}}\rangle\|^{2}+\|\langle{{a_{00}}}|{w_{k^{\prime}l}^{11}}\rangle\|^{2}+\|\langle{{a_{00}}}|{w_{k^{\prime}l^{\prime}}^{00}}\rangle\|^{2}.

Therefore, for any subspace
\mathsf{W}_{kl}
with
kl\neq 00
, we have
{\langle{g}|}\Pi_{kl}{|{g}\rangle}={\langle{{a_{00}}}|}\Pi_{kl}{|{{a_{00}}}\rangle}=\frac{\dim(\mathsf{W}_{kl})}{N}
. Next, recall that
\mathsf{W}_{00}
refers to the
(+1)
-eigenspace of
\mathsf{W}
. We know that both
{|{g}\rangle}
and
{|{{a_{00}}}\rangle}
are normalized, so

\displaystyle{\langle{g}|}\Pi_{00}{|{g}\rangle}={\langle{{a_{00}}}|}\Pi_{00}{|{{a_{00}}}\rangle}=1-\sum_{{kl}\neq 00}\frac{\dim(\mathsf{W}_{kl})}{N}=\frac{N+4}{2N}.

Applying Fact
18
, this implies that

\displaystyle\sqrt{3}{\langle{-}|}\Pi_{kl}{|{+}\rangle}={\langle{g}|}\Pi_{kl}{|{g}\rangle}+{\langle{g}|}\Pi_{kl}{|{{a_{00}}}\rangle}-{\langle{{a_{00}}}|}\Pi_{kl}{|{g}\rangle}-{\langle{{a_{00}}}|}\Pi_{kl}{|{{a_{00}}}\rangle}=0,

for any subspace
\mathsf{W}_{kl}
. This proves equation (
20
).

To prove equation (
21
), we compute

\displaystyle{\langle{+}|}\Pi_{kl}{|{+}\rangle}=\frac{1}{3}\Big[{\langle{g}|}\Pi_{kl}{|{g}\rangle}-{\langle{g}|}\Pi_{kl}{|{{a_{00}}}\rangle}-{\langle{{a_{00}}}|}\Pi_{kl}{|{g}\rangle}+{\langle{{a_{00}}}|}\Pi_{kl}{|{{a_{00}}}\rangle}\Big]=\frac{2\dim(\mathsf{W}_{kl})}{3N},

and similarly for equation (
22
).

Using the property that
{|{+}\rangle}
and
{|{-}\rangle}
are normalized, this implies that

\displaystyle\|\Pi_{00}{|{+}\rangle}\|^{2}=1-\sum_{kl\neq 00}\frac{2\dim(\mathsf{W}_{kl})}{3N}=\frac{2(N+2)}{3N},

which proves equation (
23
). We can similarly compute that
\|\Pi_{00}{|{-}\rangle}\|^{2}=\frac{4}{N}
, proving equation (
24
).

8.3
Sums

In this section, we prove asymptotic bounds on a set of sums over the spectra of
\mathsf{W}
. We assume a square grid, with
{n_{r}}={n_{c}}=\sqrt{N}
.

Fact 19

Suppose
0<\alpha<\theta_{kl}
for all
k,l
, and consider the sum

\displaystyle\sum_{kl\neq 00}\dim(\mathsf{W}_{kl})\cot\!\bigg(\frac{\theta_{kl}-\alpha}{2}\bigg).

(52)

1.

If
\alpha\in\Theta(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}})
, then the sum has order
\Omega(\sqrt{N}\log N)
.

2.

If
\alpha\in o(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}})
, then the sum has order
\Theta(\alpha N\log N)
.

Proof

Instead of taking the sum over the subspaces
\mathsf{W}_{kl}
, which partition the domain of
\mathsf{W}
, we convert to a sum over
k
and
l
. Recall that each pair
0\leq k,l\leq\sqrt{N}/2-1
corresponds to two eigenvectors of
\mathsf{W}
:
{|{w_{kl}^{00}}\rangle}
with eigenphase
\theta_{kl}
and
{|{w_{kl}^{11}}\rangle}
with eigenphase
-\theta_{kl}
. Using this property, we rewrite the sum as

\displaystyle\sum_{kl\neq 00}\dim(\mathsf{W}_{kl})\cot\!\bigg(\frac{\theta_{kl}-\alpha}{2}\bigg)

(53)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\frac{\sqrt{N}}{2}-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l=0\\
\text{not both 0}\end{subarray}}^{\frac{\sqrt{N}}{2}-1}\cot\!\bigg(\frac{\theta_{kl}-\alpha}{2}\bigg)-\cot\!\bigg(\frac{\theta_{kl}+\alpha}{2}\bigg)

\displaystyle=2\sum_{k=0}^{\frac{\sqrt{N}}{2}-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l=0\\
\text{not both 0}\end{subarray}}^{\frac{\sqrt{N}}{2}-1}\frac{\cot(\frac{\alpha}{2})\Big(\cot^{2}(\frac{\theta_{kl}}{2})+1\Big)}{\cot^{2}(\frac{\alpha}{2})-\cot^{2}(\frac{\theta_{kl}}{2})},

where the final equality follows from angle sum identities.

Next, observe that by the definition of
\theta_{kl}
,

\displaystyle\cot^{2}\!\bigg(\frac{\theta_{kl}}{2}\bigg)+1=\frac{2}{1-\cos\theta_{kl}}=\frac{1}{1-\cos^{2}\tilde{k}\cos^{2}\tilde{l}}.

We therefore consider the sum

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\frac{\sqrt{N}}{2}-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l=0\\
\text{not both 0}\end{subarray}}^{\frac{\sqrt{N}}{2}-1}\frac{1}{1-\cos^{2}\tilde{k}\cos^{2}\tilde{l}}.

For the terms where
l=0
, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\frac{\sqrt{N}}{2}-1}\frac{1}{1-\cos^{2}\tilde{k}}\in\Theta(N),

and similarly for
k=0
. The remaining terms satisfy

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\frac{\sqrt{N}}{2}-1}\sum_{l=1}^{\frac{\sqrt{N}}{2}-1}\frac{1}{1-\cos^{2}\tilde{k}\cos^{2}\tilde{l}}\in\Theta(N\log N).

Now, consider equation (
53
) in the case where
\alpha\in\Theta(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}})
. We have

\displaystyle 2\sum_{k=0}^{\frac{\sqrt{N}}{2}-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l=0\\
\text{not both 0}\end{subarray}}^{\frac{\sqrt{N}}{2}-1}\frac{\cot(\frac{\alpha}{2})\Big(\cot^{2}(\frac{\theta_{kl}}{2})+1\Big)}{\cot^{2}(\frac{\alpha}{2})-\cot^{2}(\frac{\theta_{kl}}{2})}

\displaystyle\geq 2\sum_{k=0}^{\frac{\sqrt{N}}{2}-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l=0\\
\text{not both 0}\end{subarray}}^{\frac{\sqrt{N}}{2}-1}\frac{\cot(\frac{\alpha}{2})\Big(\cot^{2}(\frac{\theta_{kl}}{2})+1\Big)}{\cot^{2}(\frac{\alpha}{2})}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\cot(\frac{\alpha}{2})}\sum_{k=0}^{\frac{\sqrt{N}}{2}-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l=0\\
\text{not both 0}\end{subarray}}^{\frac{\sqrt{N}}{2}-1}\frac{1}{1-\cos^{2}\tilde{k}\cos^{2}\tilde{l}}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{kl\neq 00}\dim(\mathsf{W}_{kl})\cot\!\bigg(\frac{\theta_{kl}-\alpha}{2}\bigg)\in\Omega(\sqrt{N}\log N).

In the case where
\alpha\in o(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}})
, the denominator in equation (
53
) is dominated by the term
\cot^{2}(\frac{\alpha}{2})
. Therefore, the expression has the same asymptotic order as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\cot(\frac{\alpha}{2})}\sum_{k=0}^{\frac{\sqrt{N}}{2}-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l=0\\
\text{not both 0}\end{subarray}}^{\frac{\sqrt{N}}{2}-1}\frac{1}{1-\cos^{2}\tilde{k}\cos^{2}\tilde{l}}\in\Theta(\alpha N\log N),

proving the second clause.

Fact 20

Suppose
0<\alpha<\theta_{kl}
for all
k,l
, and that
\alpha\in o(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}})
. Then

\displaystyle\sum_{{kl}\neq 00}\dim(\mathsf{W}_{kl})\Bigg[\cot\!\bigg(\frac{\theta_{kl}+\alpha}{2}\bigg)-\cot\!\bigg(\frac{\theta_{kl}-\alpha}{2}\bigg)\Bigg]^{2}\in\Theta(\alpha^{2}N^{2}).

(54)

Proof

\displaystyle\sum_{{kl}\neq 00}\dim(\mathsf{W}_{kl})\Bigg[\cot\!\bigg(\frac{\theta_{kl}+\alpha}{2}\bigg)-\cot\!\bigg(\frac{\theta_{kl}-\alpha}{2}\bigg)\Bigg]^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\frac{\sqrt{N}}{2}-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l=0\\
\text{not both 0}\end{subarray}}^{\frac{\sqrt{N}}{2}-1}\Bigg[\cot\!\bigg(\frac{\theta_{kl}+\alpha}{2}\bigg)-\cot\!\bigg(\frac{\theta_{kl}-\alpha}{2}\bigg)\Bigg]^{2}

By a similar derivation as in Fact (
19
), this sum has the same order as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\cot^{2}(\frac{\alpha}{2})}\sum_{k=0}^{\frac{\sqrt{N}}{2}-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l=0\\
\text{not both 0}\end{subarray}}^{\frac{\sqrt{N}}{2}-1}\!\bigg(\frac{1}{1-\cos^{2}\tilde{k}\cos^{2}\tilde{l}}\bigg)^{2}.

Using the Taylor expansion of cosine, for
l=0
we get

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\frac{\sqrt{N}}{2}-1}\!\bigg(\frac{1}{1-\cos^{2}\tilde{k}}\bigg)^{2}\in\Theta(N^{2}),

and similarly for
k=0
.
Finally,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\frac{\sqrt{N}}{2}-1}\sum_{l=1}^{\frac{\sqrt{N}}{2}-1}\!\bigg(\frac{1}{1-\cos^{2}\tilde{k}\cos^{2}\tilde{l}}\bigg)^{2}\in\Theta(N^{2}).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{{kl}\neq 00}\dim(\mathsf{W}_{kl})\Bigg[\cot\!\bigg(\frac{\theta_{kl}+\alpha}{2}\bigg)-\cot\!\bigg(\frac{\theta_{kl}-\alpha}{2}\bigg)\Bigg]^{2}\in\Theta(\alpha^{2}N^{2}).
