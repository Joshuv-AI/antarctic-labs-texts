Entangled subspaces and generic local state discrimination with pre-shared entanglement
Benjamin Lovitz, Nathaniel Johnston

Abstract
Walgate and Scott have determined the maximum number of generic pure quantum states that can be unambiguously discriminated by an LOCC measurement [ article ] . In this work, we determine this number in a more general setting in which the local parties have access to pre-shared entanglement in the form of a resource state. We find that, for an arbitrary pure resource state, this number is equal to the Krull dimension of (the closure of) the set of pure states obtainable from the resource state by SLOCC. Surprisingly, a generic resource state maximizes this number. Local state discrimination is closely related to the topic of entangled subspaces, which we study in its own right. We introduce r r -entangled subspaces, which naturally generalize previously studied spaces to higher multipartite entanglement. We use algebraic-geometric methods to determine the maximum dimension of an r r -entangled subspace, and present novel explicit constructions of such spaces. We obtain similar results for symmetric and antisymmetric r r -entangled subspaces, which correspond to entangled subspaces of bosonic and fermionic systems, respectively.

Introduction

An
LOCC measurement
is a quantum measurement that can be implemented by local operations and classical communication (LOCC). We say an
n
-tuple of pure quantum states
([v_{1}],\dots,[v_{n}])
is
locally (unambiguously) discriminable
if there exists an LOCC measurement with
n+1
outcomes
\{1,\dots,n,?\}
that, when performed on any
[v_{a}]
, outputs either
a
or
?
, with non-zero probability to output
a
. More generally, we say that
([v_{1}],\dots,[v_{n}])
is
locally (unambiguously) discriminable with (a pure resource state)

[w]
if
([v_{1}],\dots,[v_{n}])
can be locally discriminated using an LOCC measurement with pre-shared entanglement
[w]
. Our notation for pure states
[w]
is projective, and can be thought of as
\frac{1}{\lVert\mspace{1mu}w\mspace{1mu}\rVert^{2}}ww^{*}
for a non-zero vector
w
, where
\lVert\mspace{1mu}\cdot\mspace{1mu}\rVert
denotes the Euclidean norm and
(\cdot)^{*}
denotes the conjugate-transpose.

For an algebraic variety
Y,
we say that a property holds for a
generic
element of
Y
if there exists a Zariski-open-dense subset of
Y
on which that property holds. Zariski-open-dense sets are massive. In particular, they are full-measure with respect to any absolutely continuous measure, e.g. the unitary Haar measure. One should intuitively think of a property holding for a generic element of
Y
if it holds with probability one when an element of
Y
is picked at random.

Walgate and Scott determined that, for
m

d_{1},\dots,d_{m}

n
-tuple of
{\sum_{j=1}^{m}(d_{j}-1)+1}
pure states is locally discriminable, and this is the largest number for which this holds
[
article
]

[w]

{r(\sum_{j=1}^{m}(d_{j}-1)+1)}
generic pure states are locally discriminable with the tensor-rank-
r
GHZ state
{[\tau_{r,m}]=\left[\sum_{a=1}^{r}e_{a}^{\otimes m}\right]}

e_{1},\dots,e_{r}
are standard basis vectors, and this bound is often achieved (Corollary
9
). For
m=2
, this bound cannot be achieved: precisely
{d_{1}d_{2}-(d_{1}-\min\{d_{1},r\})(d_{2}-\min\{d_{2},r\})}
generic pure states are locally discriminable with
[\tau_{r,2}]
(or any other Schmidt rank
r

11
).

More generally, we characterize this number for an arbitrary pure resource state
[w]
(Theorem
7
). The
SLOCC image
of
[w]
, denoted by
\Ima([w])
, is the set of pure states obtainable from
[w]

{d=\dim(\overline{\Ima([w])})}

\Ima([w])

(d+1)
-tuple of pure states is locally discriminable with
[w]
. We also prove a “strong converse:” A Zariski-open-dense set of
(d+2)
-tuples of pure states are
not
locally discriminable with
[w]
. To recover Walgate and Scott’s result, observe that the SLOCC image of a trivial resource state is the set of unentangled (or,
product
) pure states. This set is already closed, and has dimension
d=\sum_{j=1}^{m}(d_{j}-1)
.

Our characterization extends Walgate and Scott’s result in two directions beyond the obvious addition of a resource state: First, our results hold under the algebraic-geometric notion of “generic” introduced above, which yields stronger statements than the measure-theoretic notion used by Walgate and Scott. Second, our “strong converse,” mentioned above, is much stronger than the converse statement proven by Walgate and Scott (see the discussion following Corollary
10

It is natural to quantify “how useful” a given resource state
[w]
is for LUSD in terms of the number of generic pure states that can be locally discriminated with
[w]
. We use algebraic group theory to prove that, under this barometer, a generic resource state is maximally useful for LUSD. This is quite surprising, because in many other contexts the most useful quantum states form a measure zero subset of the Hilbert space. For example, in bipartite space the maximally entangled states form a measure zero set, and in multipartite space the set of graph states (a class of states often regarded as the most useful states) has measure zero
[
PhysRevA.69.062311
]
.
As one more example, it is known that most multipartite states are, in a sense, useless for measurement-based quantum computation
[
PhysRevLett.102.190501
]
.

We now sketch a proof of one direction (the “strong converse”) of our characterization of generic LUSD with a resource state. We invoke a recent observation of Bandyopadhyay et al., that an

-tuple of pure states
([v_{1}],\dots,[v_{n}])

({u_{a}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}v_{b}\neq 0\iff a=b})

[
PhysRevA.94.022311
]

{\Ima([w])\cap\spn\{[v_{1}],\dots,[v_{n-1}]\}^{\perp}\neq\{\}}

We use similar algebraic-geometric techniques to study entangled subspaces. The
tensor rank
of a pure state is the minimum number
r
for which that state can be written as a superposition of
r
product states. It is known that the set of pure states of tensor rank at most
r
is precisely
\Ima([\tau_{r,m}])
. We define an
r
-entangled subspace
to be a projective linear subspace that avoids
\overline{\Ima([\tau_{r,m}])}
(this closure is known as the set of pure states of
border rank
at most
r
). In bipartite space, the tensor rank is equal to the Schmidt rank, and
\Ima([\tau_{r,2}])
is already closed. Parthasarathy determined the maximum dimension of a 1-entangled subspace
[
Parthasarathy:2004aa
]
, and Bhat explicitly constructed a 1-entangled subspace of maximum dimension
[
BHAT_2006
]
. Cubitt et al. proved analogous results for
r
-entangled subspaces of bipartite space
[
Cubitt_2008
]
. A theorem in algebraic geometry states that if
X
is a projective variety, then the minimum codimension of a projective linear subspace disjoint from
X
is
\dim(X)+1
(and, by the algebraic geometry result of the previous paragraph, almost all subspaces of this codimension avoid
X
). It follows, from a standard upper bound on
\dim(\overline{\Ima([\tau_{r,m}])}),
that there always exists an
r
-entangled subspace of dimension

\displaystyle d_{1}\cdots d_{m}-r\sum_{j=1}^{m}(d_{j}-1)-r-1,

(1)

whenever this quantity is non-negative. Furthermore, this is often the maximum dimension of such a subspace.
Using this bound, we explicitly construct
2
-entangled subspaces of maximum dimension in tripartite space with local (affine) dimensions
d_{1},d_{2}\in\{2,3\}
and
d_{3}=2
(i.e. qubits and qutrits); and in quadripartite space with local (affine) dimensions
d_{1}=d_{2}=d_{3}=d_{4}=2
(i.e. all qubits). To show that these subspaces are indeed 2-entangled, we solve an equivalent ideal membership problem using the Macaulay2 software package
[
M2
,
entangled_subspaces_code
]
. While ideal membership problems are notoriously intractable in general, our positive results reveal that this may not be the case for verifying
r
-entangled subspaces (at least for small
r
).

We also define
r
-entangled subspaces of the symmetric and antisymmetric spaces, which correspond to bosonic and fermionic entangled subspaces, respectively
[
Grabowski_2012
]
. We explicitly construct maximal symmetric and antisymmetric
r
-entangled subspaces of bipartite space for arbitrary
r
, and of multipartite space for
r=1
, which matches the cases of standard
r
-entangled subspace constructions presented in
[
BHAT_2006
,
Cubitt_2008
]
.

It is known that, under various notions of entanglement, the maximum dimension of an entangled subspace is precisely the maximum number of negative eigenvalues of an entanglement witness
[
Augusiak_2011
,
Johnston_2013
,
Johnston_2019
]
. The number of negative eigenvalues quantifies “how good” the witness is at detecting entanglement. We prove that this connection between subspaces and negative eigenvalues holds under a much more general notion of witness, including multipartite
r
-entanglement witnesses.

There are other types of entangled subspaces that have been studied in previous works:
non-positive partial transpose subspaces
, for which every mixed state supported on that subspace has non-positive partial transpose
[
Johnston_2013
,
Johnston_2019
]
;
genuinely entangled subspaces
, for which every element is non-product with respect to every bipartition
[
Cubitt_2008
,
PhysRevA.98.012313
]
; and subspaces of bipartite space with high entropy of entanglement
[
Hayden_2006
]
. Entangled subspaces are connected to unextendible product bases, and have found applications, for example, in quantum error correction
[
PhysRevA.76.042309
,
PhysRevA.69.052330
]
and quantum tomography
[
Heinosaari_2013
]
.

2.1
Notation and Terminology

For a non-zero vector space
\mathcal{V}
(which we always take to be over
\mathbb{C}
), we use
\langle\cdot|\cdot\rangle:\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{V}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
to denote the standard Hermitian inner product given by
{\langle u|v\rangle=u^{*}v}
, where
(\cdot)^{*}
denotes the conjugate-transpose. We use
\Sp(\mathcal{V})\subseteq\mathcal{V}
to denote the unit sphere of
\mathcal{V}
with respect to the norm induced by the inner product
\langle\cdot|\cdot\rangle
.

We use
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{V})
(or more briefly,
\mathbb{P}\mathcal{V}
) to denote the set of
1
-dimensional linear subspaces of
\mathcal{V}
, and we write
{[v]\in\mathbb{P}\mathcal{V}}
for the span of a non-zero vector
v\in\mathcal{V}
. While this projective notation is perhaps a bit non-standard in the field of quantum information theory, it is particularly important for us here: our work relies
heavily
on results from algebraic geometry, where this notation is standard. Readers who are unfamiliar with projective geometry can still interpret our results and proofs as long as they keep a few points in mind:

•

In quantum physics, a “pure state” of
\mathcal{V}
is typically represented by a unit vector
\lvert\mspace{1mu}v\mspace{1mu}\rangle\in\Sp(\mathcal{V})
, with the understanding that
\lvert\mspace{1mu}v\mspace{1mu}\rangle
and
\lvert\mspace{1mu}w\mspace{1mu}\rangle
represent the same state if there exists
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\lvert\mspace{1mu}w\mspace{1mu}\rangle=e^{i\theta}\lvert\mspace{1mu}v\mspace{1mu}\rangle
. In this paper, we instead say that the pure states of
\mathcal{V}
are represented by the
1
-dimensional linear subspaces
[v]\in\mathbb{P}\mathcal{V}
, which is equivalent via the bijection which identifies a 1-dimensional linear subspace with some representative unit vector in that subspace.

Identifying pure quantum states in this way as subspaces, rather than as unit vectors, is useful since it allows us to regard certain sets of pure quantum states as algebraic varieties, and study them using the machinery of algebraic geometry. However, not much will be lost by a reader making the mental substitution
[v]\rightarrow\lvert\mspace{1mu}v\mspace{1mu}\rangle
when reading the main results of this paper.

For brevity, we will refer to pure quantum states simply as “states,” and we will refer to mixed quantum states (defined and treated only in Section
) as “mixed states.”

•

Instead of considering a linear subspace of vectors
Z\subseteq\mathcal{V}
, we typically consider the corresponding
projective
linear subspace
\check{Z}\subseteq\mathbb{P}\mathcal{V}
defined by

\displaystyle\check{Z}=\big\{[z]\in\mathbb{P}\mathcal{V}:z\in Z\setminus\{0\}\big\}.

(2)

By convention, a projective linear subspace has dimension one less than the corresponding non-projective subspace (after all, we regard a
1
-dimensional line
\spn\{v\}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
as a single point
[v]\in\mathbb{P}\mathcal{V}
). For this reason, a
projective
(n-1)
-plane
(or alternatively, a
projective linear subspace of dimension
n-1
) in
\mathbb{P}\mathcal{V}
is the projectivization of an
n
-dimensional linear subspace of
\mathcal{V}
. Similarly, we use the shorthand
\mathbb{P}^{D}=\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{C}^{D+1})
, with the understanding that this is a projective
D
-dimensional space.

As a result of considering projective subspaces instead of non-projective ones, the numbers that we present as the maximum dimension of a (projective) entangled subspace will be one less than the numbers presented in previous works (e.g.,
[
Parthasarathy:2004aa
,
Cubitt_2008
]
).

We can also projectivize a set
Z\subseteq\mathcal{V}
even if it is just an affine cone (i.e., closed under multiplication by complex scalars), rather than a linear subspace. In this case, the projectivization
\check{Z}
is still given by Equation (
2
), but we note that it will no longer necessarily be a (projective) subspace—it will just be a set, potentially with no additional structure. Conversely, given a subset
X\subseteq\mathbb{P}\mathcal{V}
, we define
\hat{X}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
to be the affine cone over
X
:

\hat{X}=\big\{x\in\mathcal{V}:[x]\in X\big\}\cup\{0\}.

For vector spaces
\mathcal{W}
and
\mathcal{V}
, we use
\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{W},\mathcal{V})
to denote the space of linear maps from
\mathcal{W}
to
\mathcal{V}
, and let
\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{V})=\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V})
. If
m
is a positive integer and we let
[m]=\{1,\dots,m\}
, then every vector space
\mathcal{V}=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{m}\mathcal{V}_{j}
is canonically isomorphic to
\mathrm{L}(\bigotimes_{j\in S}\mathcal{V}_{j}^{*},\bigotimes_{j\in[m]\setminus S}\mathcal{V}_{j})
for any subset
S\subseteq[m]
. The
flattening rank
of a state
[v]\in\mathbb{P}\mathcal{V}
is the maximum, taken over all subsets
S\subseteq[m]
of size
1\leq\lvert S\rvert\leq m-1
, of the matrix rank of
[v]
as an element of (the projectivization of)
\mathrm{L}(\bigotimes_{j\in S}\mathcal{V}_{j}^{*},\bigotimes_{j\in[m]\setminus S}\mathcal{V}_{j})
.

Let
\mathrm{Pos}(\mathcal{V})\subseteq\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{V})
be the set of positive semidefinite operators on
\mathcal{V}
, let
\Un(\mathcal{V})\subseteq\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{V})
be the set of unitary operators on
\mathcal{V}
, and let
\GL(\mathcal{V})\subseteq\mathrm{L}(\mathcal{V})
be the set of invertible operators on
\mathcal{V}
. Let
\{e_{1},\dots,e_{d}\}
be the standard basis of
\mathbb{C}^{d}
.

2.2
The Segre, Veronese, and Grassmannian varieties (and their secants)

We will be particularly interested in the algebraic varieties known as the Segre, Veronese, and Grassmannian varieties, as these correspond to the sets of unentangled states in a space of distinguishable, bosonic, and fermionic particles, respectively
[
Grabowski_2012
]
. In this section, we briefly describe these varieties, as well as their
r
-th secants, which we will use to study
r
-entangled subspaces. We refer the reader to
[
10.1112/S0024610706022630
,
landsberg2012tensors
,
harris2013algebraic
,
Bernardi_2018
]
for more in-depth treatments of these objects.

The Segre variety

\displaystyle Y=\mathrm{Seg}(\mathbb{P}^{d_{1}-1}\times\dots\times\mathbb{P}^{d_{m}-1})\subseteq\textstyle{\mathbb{P}(\bigotimes_{j=1}^{m}\mathbb{C}^{d_{j}})}

(3)

is the image of the Segre embedding, and is equal to the set of states of the form
{[x_{1}\otimes\dots\otimes x_{m}]}
, where
x_{j}\in\mathbb{C}^{d_{j}}
for each
j\in[m]
. The Segre variety corresponds to the unentangled (or,
product
) states in the space
\mathbb{P}(\bigotimes_{j=1}^{m}\mathbb{C}^{d_{j}})
of distinguishable particles.

For each permutation
\sigma\in S_{m}
, let
P_{\sigma}\in\mathrm{L}(\bigotimes^{m}\mathbb{C}^{d})
be the linear map defined on product vectors as

\displaystyle P_{\sigma}(x_{1}\otimes\dots\otimes x_{m})=x_{{\sigma^{-1}(1)}}\otimes\dots\otimes x_{{\sigma^{-1}(m)}},

(4)

and extended linearly. Let
\mathbb{P}(\bigvee^{m}\mathbb{C}^{d})\subseteq\mathbb{P}(\bigotimes^{m}\mathbb{C}^{d})
denote the symmetric subspace, i.e, the set of states
{[x]\in\mathbb{P}(\bigotimes^{m}\mathbb{C}^{d})}
such that
P_{\sigma}x=x
for all
\sigma\in S_{m}
. This space is spanned by states of the form
[x_{1}\vee\dots\vee x_{m}]
, where

\displaystyle[x_{1}\vee\dots\vee x_{m}]=\left[\sum_{\sigma\in S_{m}}x_{\sigma(1)}\otimes\dots\otimes x_{\sigma(m)}\right].

(5)

In quantum physics,
\mathbb{P}(\bigvee^{m}\mathbb{C}^{d})
represents a bosonic space of indistinguishable particles. (This space can also be thought of as the set of homogeneous polynomials of degree
m
in
d
variables.) The Veronese variety

\displaystyle\nu_{m}(\mathbb{P}^{d-1})\subseteq\textstyle{\mathbb{P}({\bigvee}^{m}\mathbb{C}^{d})}

(6)

is the image of the
m
-th Veronese embedding, and is equal to the set of states in
\mathbb{P}(\bigvee^{m}\mathbb{C}^{d})
of the form
[x^{\vee m}]
. The Veronese variety corresponds to the set of unentangled states in the bosonic space
\mathbb{P}({\bigvee}^{m}\mathbb{C}^{d})
.

Let
\mathbb{P}(\bigwedge^{m}\mathbb{C}^{d})\subseteq\mathbb{P}(\bigotimes^{m}\mathbb{C}^{d})
denote the antisymmetric subspace, i.e. the set of states
[x]\in\mathbb{P}(\bigotimes^{m}\mathbb{C}^{d})
such that
P_{\sigma}x=(-1)^{\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma)}x
for all
\sigma\in S_{m}
. This space is spanned by the set of states of the form
[x_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge x_{m}]
, where

\displaystyle[x_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge x_{m}]=\left[\sum_{\sigma\in S_{m}}(-1)^{\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma)}x_{\sigma(1)}\otimes\dots\otimes x_{\sigma(m)}\right].

(7)

In quantum physics, the antisymmetric subspace represents a fermionic space of indistinguishable particles. The Grassmannian variety

\displaystyle\Gr(m-1,\mathbb{P}^{d-1})\subseteq\textstyle{\mathbb{P}({\bigwedge}^{m}\mathbb{C}^{d})}

(8)

is the set of states in
\mathbb{P}(\bigwedge^{m}\mathbb{C}^{d})
of the form
[x_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge x_{m}]
. The Grassmannian variety corresponds to the set of unentangled states in the fermionic space
\mathbb{P}({\bigwedge}^{m}\mathbb{C}^{d})
. The Grassmannian can also be viewed as the variety of projective
(m-1)
-planes in
\mathbb{P}^{d-1}
. We expound on this persective in Section
2.3
, as we will make frequent use of it.

Now we introduce secant varieties. For a projective variety
X\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{D}
, let

\displaystyle\sigma_{r}(X)=\overline{\bigcup_{[x_{1}],\dots,[x_{r}]\in X}\spn\{[x_{1}],\dots,[x_{r}]\}}\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{D}

(9)

be the
r-th secant variety
to
X
, where the closure can equivalently be taken with respect to either the Zariski or Euclidean topology. It is a standard result that

\displaystyle\dim(\sigma_{r}(X))\leq\min\{D,r\dim(X)+r-1\}.

(10)

If equality holds in this expression, then
\sigma_{r}(X)
is said to have the
expected dimension
, and otherwise it is said to be
defective
.

For the Segre variety
Y\subseteq\mathbb{P}(\bigotimes_{j=1}^{m}\mathbb{C}^{d_{j}})
, we have
\dim(Y)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}(d_{j}-1)
and
\dim(\mathbb{P}(\bigotimes_{j=1}^{m}\mathbb{C}^{d_{j}}))=d_{1}\cdots d_{m}-1
, so

\displaystyle\dim(\sigma_{r}(Y))\leq\min\bigg\{d_{1}\cdots d_{m}-1,r\sum_{j=1}^{m}(d_{j}-1)+r-1\bigg\}.

(11)

The
border rank
of a state
[v]\in\mathbb{P}(\bigotimes_{j=1}^{m}\mathbb{C}^{d_{j}})
is the smallest positive integer
r
for which
[v]\in\sigma_{r}(Y)
. The
tensor rank
of
[v]
is the smallest
r
for which
[v]
is in the span of
r
elements of
Y
(i.e.
[v]
is contained in the pre-closure of the set defined in (
9
), with
X=Y
). In the case of a bipartite quantum system (i.e., when
m=2
), the border rank and tensor rank coincide, and are often referred to as the
Schmidt rank
.

A conjecturally complete set of defective
\sigma_{r}(Y)
have been proposed in
[
Abo_2008
]
, which are nicely summarized in
[
Bernardi_2018
, Conjecture 6]
. For example,

\displaystyle\dim(\sigma_{r}(\mathrm{Seg}(\mathbb{P}^{d_{1}-1}\times\mathbb{P}^{d_{2}-1})))=d_{1}d_{2}-(d_{1}-\min\{d_{1},r\})(d_{2}-\min\{d_{2},r\})-1,

(12)

so
\sigma_{r}(\mathrm{Seg}(\mathbb{P}^{d_{1}-1}\times\mathbb{P}^{d_{2}-1}))
is defective in many cases. Under the identification
{\mathbb{C}^{d_{1}}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d_{2}}\cong\mathrm{L}((\mathbb{C}^{d_{1}})^{*},\mathbb{C}^{d_{2}})}
, the variety
\sigma_{r}(\mathrm{Seg}(\mathbb{P}^{d_{1}-1}\times\mathbb{P}^{d_{2}-1}))
corresponds to the set of (projective)
d_{2}\times d_{1}
matrices of rank at most
r
.

For the Veronese variety
\nu_{m}(\mathbb{P}^{d-1})\subseteq\mathbb{P}({\bigvee}^{m}(\mathbb{C}^{d}))
, we have
\dim(\nu_{m}(\mathbb{P}^{d-1}))=d-1
and
\dim(\mathbb{P}({\bigvee}^{m}(\mathbb{C}^{d})))=\binom{d+m-1}{m-1}-1
, so

\displaystyle\dim(\sigma_{r}(\nu_{m}(\mathbb{P}^{d-1})))\leq\min\left\{\binom{d-1+m}{m}-1,rd-1\right\}.

(13)

A complete set of defective Veronese secants are known
[
alexander1995polynomial
]
; see also Theorem 2 in
[
Bernardi_2018
]
. In particular,
\sigma_{r}(\nu_{2}(\mathbb{P}^{d-1}))
is defective whenever
2\leq r\leq d-1
, in which case

\displaystyle\dim(\sigma_{r}(\nu_{2}(\mathbb{P}^{d-1})))=\min\left\{\binom{d+1}{2}-1,rd-\binom{r}{2}-1\right\}.

(14)

Note that there is a typo in the expression of this dimension in Theorem 2 of
[
Bernardi_2018
]
. Under the identification
\mathbb{C}^{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d}\cong\mathrm{L}((\mathbb{C}^{d})^{*},\mathbb{C}^{d})
, the variety
\sigma_{r}(\nu_{2}(\mathbb{P}^{d-1}))
corresponds to the set of (projective) symmetric
d\times d
matrices of rank at most
r
.

For the Grassmannian variety
\Gr(m-1,\mathbb{P}^{d-1})\subseteq\mathbb{P}({\bigwedge}^{m}(\mathbb{C}^{d}))
, we have

\displaystyle{\dim(\Gr(m-1,\mathbb{P}^{d-1}))=m(d-m)},

(15)

and
\dim(\mathbb{P}({\bigwedge}^{m}(\mathbb{C}^{d})))=\binom{d}{m}-1
, so

\displaystyle\dim(\sigma_{r}(\Gr(m-1,\mathbb{P}^{d-1})))\leq\min\left\{\binom{d}{m}-1,rm(d-m)+r-1\right\}.

(16)

As with the Segre variety, there are a conjecturally complete set of defective Grassmannian secants; see
[
Bernardi_2018
, Conjecture 7]
and
[
doi:10.1080/10586458.2007.10128997
,
boralevi2013note
,
bernardi2018new
]
. Similarly to the Segre and Veronese varieties,
\sigma_{r}(\Gr(1,\mathbb{P}^{d-1}))
is defective whenever
2\leq r<\lfloor\frac{d}{2}\rfloor
, in which case

\displaystyle\dim(\sigma_{r}(\Gr(1,\mathbb{P}^{d-1})))=\binom{d}{2}-\binom{d-2r}{2}-1=2r(d-r)-r-1.

(17)

Under the identification
\mathbb{C}^{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d}\cong\mathrm{L}((\mathbb{C}^{d})^{*},\mathbb{C}^{d})
, the variety
\sigma_{r}(\Gr(1,\mathbb{P}^{d-1}))
corresponds to the set of projective antisymmetric
d\times d
matrices of rank at most
2r
.

2.3
The set of projective
(n-1)
-planes as a projective variety

In this subsection, we recall the canonical bijection between the Grassmannian variety and the set of projective planes of a fixed dimension, which endows the latter set with the structure of a projective variety. We then review several facts about the Grassmannian that we will use to prove our characterization of generic LUSD with a resource state (Theorem
7
).

Recall the canonical bijection between the set of projective
(n-1)
-planes and
{\Gr(n-1,\mathbb{P}\mathcal{V})}
, which identifies
[v_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge v_{n}]
with
\spn\{[v_{1}],\dots,[v_{n}]\}
for any linearly independent set
\{[v_{1}],\dots,[v_{n}]\}\subseteq\mathbb{P}\mathcal{V}
. This identification endows the set of projective
n-1
planes with the structure of a projective variety.
In a slight abuse of notation, we will use
{\Gr(n-1,\mathbb{P}\mathcal{V})}
to refer to both the projective variety of decomposable elements of
\mathbb{P}({\bigwedge}^{n}\mathcal{V})
and the projective variety of projective
(n-1)
-planes in
\mathbb{P}\mathcal{V}
.

It is important to keep in mind the canonical bijection between the set of projective
(n-1)
-planes in
\mathbb{P}\mathcal{V}
(namely,
\Gr(n-1,\mathbb{P}\mathcal{V})
), and the set of
n
-dimensional linear subspaces of
\mathcal{V}
(typically denoted
\Gr(n,\mathcal{V})
). While
\Gr(n,\mathcal{V})
is perhaps more standard, we prefer
\Gr(n-1,\mathbb{P}\mathcal{V})
, as we would like the elements of each subspace to be states. Note that we have replaced the symbol
m
with the symbol
n
in this section, to match later notation in which the Grassmannian is viewed as a space of projective
(n-1)
-planes.

In accordance with our definition of
generic
, we say that a property holds for a generic projective
(n-1)
-plane if there exists a Zariski-open-dense subset of
\mathrm{Gr}(n-1,\mathbb{P}\mathcal{V})
on which the property holds. We say a property holds for a generic
n
-tuple of states in
\mathbb{P}\mathcal{V}
(or when
n=1
, simply a generic state in
\mathbb{P}\mathcal{V}
) if there exists a Zariski-open-dense subset
U\subseteq\mathbb{P}(V)^{\times n}
such that the property holds for every
([v_{1}],\dots,[v_{n}])\in U
. (Here,
\mathbb{P}(V)^{\times n}
is viewed as a projective variety via the Segre embedding.)

In the remainder of this subsection, we review two facts that we will use to prove our characterization of generic LUSD with a resource state. First, a generic
n
-tuple of states spans a generic projective
(n-1)
-plane, and vice versa (Fact
1
). Second, the bijection
{\Gr(n-1,\mathbb{P}^{d})\cong\Gr(d-n,\mathbb{P}^{d})}
, which sends a subspace to its orthogonal complement, defines an isomorphism of projective varieties (Fact
2
). We defer the proofs of these facts to Appendix
.

Fact 1
.

A generic
n
-tuple of states spans a generic projective
(n-1)
-plane, and vice versa. In more details, let
n
be a positive integer, let
\mathcal{V}
be a
\mathbb{C}
-vector space, and let

\displaystyle\tilde{\pi}:\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{V})^{\times n}\dashrightarrow\mathrm{Gr}(n-1,\mathbb{P}\mathcal{V})

(18)

be the rational map defined by
\tilde{\pi}([v_{1}],\dots,[v_{n}])=[v_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge v_{n}]
. Then a subset
{U\subseteq\mathrm{Gr}(n-1,\mathbb{P}\mathcal{V})}
is open-dense if and only if
\tilde{\pi}^{-1}(U)\subseteq\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{V})^{\times n}
is open-dense.

Fact 2
.

The bijection
\Gr(n-1,\mathbb{P}^{d})\cong\Gr(d-n,\mathbb{P}^{d})
, which sends a subspace to its orthogonal complement with respect to some non-degenerate bilinear form
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
, defines an isomorphism of projective varieties.

2.4
Projective linear subspaces disjoint from a variety

The following algebraic-geometric result will be instrumental in proving our characterization of generic LUSD with a resource state. We will also use it to
determine the maximum dimension of an entangled subspace.

Theorem 3
.

Let
X\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{D}
be a quasiprojective variety. Then

\displaystyle\dim(X)=

The smallest non-negative integer
d
for which a generic projective
D-d-1

(19)

plane is disjoint from
X
.

(20)

\displaystyle=

The largest non-negative integer
d
for which a generic projective
D-d

(21)

plane intersects
X
.

(22)

If
X
is projective, then

\displaystyle\dim(X)=

The largest non-negative integer
d
for which every projective
D-d
plane

(23)

intersects

.

(24)

This characterization is taken to be the definition of the dimension of an irreducible variety in
[

, Definition 11.2]
, and is shown to be equivalent to other standard notions of dimension, e.g. the Krull dimension. The above extension to the reducible case is straightforward.

2.5
The SLOCC image

LOCC channels
are quantum channels (completely positive, trace preserving maps) that can be implemented by local operations and classical communication (LOCC).
SLOCC maps
are completely positive, trace non-increasing maps that can be implemented with non-zero probability by an LOCC channel. In other words, SLOCC maps represent LOCC channels with postselection
[
watrous_2018
]
.

Let
\mathcal{V}=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{m}\mathbb{C}^{d_{j}}
and
\mathcal{W}=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{m}\mathbb{C}^{c_{j}}
be vector spaces. For a state
[w]\in\mathbb{P}{\mathcal{W}}
, we define the
SLOCC image
of
[w]
in
\mathbb{P}{\mathcal{V}}
, denoted
\Ima_{\mathbb{P}{\mathcal{V}}}([w])\subseteq\mathbb{P}{\mathcal{V}}
, to be the set of states in
\mathbb{P}{\mathcal{V}}
obtainable from
[w]
by SLOCC. This set is also known as the
downward closure of
[w]
with respect to SLOCC
, and can alternatively be characterized as the set of pure states that have a Tucker decomposition with core state
[w]

[
Tucker:1966aa
,
rabanser2017introduction
]
. A related notion is the
SLOCC orbit
of a state
[v]\in\mathbb{P}\mathcal{V}
, denoted
\mathcal{O}_{[v]}\subseteq\mathbb{P}{\mathcal{V}}
, which is the set of states in
\Ima_{\mathbb{P}{\mathcal{V}}}([v])
that can be converted back to
[v]
by SLOCC, i.e.

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{[v]}=\{[u]\in\Ima_{\mathbb{P}{\mathcal{V}}}([v]):[v]\in\Ima_{\mathbb{P}{\mathcal{V}}}([u])\}.

(25)

In this subsection, we observe several properties of
\Ima_{\mathbb{P}{\mathcal{V}}}([w])
and
\mathcal{O}_{[v]}
that we will use in Section
3
. We can describe these sets mathematically as

\displaystyle\Ima_{\mathbb{P}{\mathcal{V}}}([w])=\{[(A_{1}\otimes\dots\otimes A_{m})w]:A_{i}\in\mathrm{L}(\mathbb{C}^{c_{i}},\mathbb{C}^{d_{i}})\quad\!\text{for all}

\displaystyle\quad\!i\in[m]

(26)

\displaystyle\text{and}\quad\!(A_{1}\otimes\dots\otimes A_{m})w\neq 0\},

(27)

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{[v]}=\{[(A_{1}\otimes\dots\otimes A_{m})v]:A_{i}\in\mathrm{GL}(\mathbb{C}^{d_{i}})\quad\text{for all}\quad i\in[m]\},

(28)

see
[
PhysRevA.62.062314
]
. True to its name,
\mathcal{O}_{[v]}
is the orbit of
[v]
under the standard action of the product (projective) general linear group.

If
\mathcal{V}=\mathbb{C}^{d_{1}}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d_{2}}
and
\mathcal{W}=\mathbb{C}^{c_{1}}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{c_{2}}
are bipartite spaces, then
\Ima_{\mathbb{P}\mathcal{V}}([w])
is the set of states in
\mathbb{P}\mathcal{V}
of Schmidt rank less than or equal to the Schmidt rank of
[w]
, and
\mathcal{O}_{[v]}
is the set of states of Schmidt rank equal to the Schmidt rank of
[v]
. In multipartite space, consider the tensor-rank-
r
GHZ state

\displaystyle[\tau_{r,m}]=\left[\sum_{a=1}^{r}e_{a}^{\otimes m}\right]\subseteq\mathbb{P}((\mathbb{C}^{r})^{\otimes m}).

(29)

It is straightforward to verify that
\Ima_{\mathbb{P}{\mathcal{V}}}([\tau_{r,m}])
is the set of states in
\mathbb{P}{\mathcal{V}}
of tensor rank at most
r
, and hence
{\overline{\Ima_{\mathbb{P}{\mathcal{V}}}([\tau_{r,m}])}=\sigma_{r}}(Y)
, where
Y
is the Segre variety of
\mathbb{P}\mathcal{V}
defined in (
3
). It is clear that
\mathcal{O}_{[\tau_{r,m}]}
is the set of states of the form
[\sum_{a=1}^{r}x_{a,1}\otimes\dots\otimes x_{a,m}]
, where
\{x_{1,j},\dots,x_{r,j}\}\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{r}
is linearly independent for all
j\in[m]
.

We conclude this subsection by proving that
\Ima_{\mathbb{P}{\mathcal{V}}}([w])
and
\mathcal{O}_{[v]}
are both irreducible and constructible (Fact
4
), and that in many cases a generic state
[w]\in\mathbb{P}\mathcal{W}
maximizes
\dim(\overline{\Ima_{\mathbb{P}\mathcal{V}}([w])})
(Fact
5
). We will use Facts
4
and
5
, respectively, to prove our characterization of generic LUSD with a resource state, and to prove that a generic resource state is maximally useful for LUSD (see Theorem
7
and the subsequent discussion). We defer the proofs of these facts to Appendix
.

Fact 4
.

Let
\mathcal{V}=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{m}\mathbb{C}^{d_{j}}
and
\mathcal{W}=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{m}\mathbb{C}^{c_{j}}
be vector spaces, and let
[w]\in\mathbb{P}{\mathcal{W}}
and
{[v]\in\mathbb{P}\mathcal{W}}
be states. Then the sets
\Ima_{\mathbb{P}{\mathcal{V}}}([w])
and
\mathcal{O}_{[v]}
are both irreducible and constructible in the Zariski topology.

Since both
\Ima_{\mathbb{P}{\mathcal{V}}}([w])
and
\mathcal{O}_{[v]}
are constructible, each contains an open-dense subset of its closure
[
An:2012aa
, Lemma 2.1]
. In fact,
\mathcal{O}_{[v]}
is itself an open-dense subset of its closure (i.e.
\mathcal{O}_{[v]}
is
locally closed
)
[
humphreys2012linear
, Proposition 8.3]
. Observe that
\overline{\mathcal{O}_{[v]}}=\overline{\Ima_{\mathbb{P}{\mathcal{V}}}([v])}
.

Fact 5
.

Let
\mathcal{V}=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{m}\mathbb{C}^{d_{j}}
and
\mathcal{W}=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{m}\mathbb{C}^{c_{j}}
be vector spaces with
c_{j}\leq d_{j}
for all
j\in[m]
. Then
\dim(\overline{\Ima_{\mathbb{P}\mathcal{V}}([w])})
is maximized for a generic state
[w]\in\mathbb{P}\mathcal{W}
.

It would be nice to know if the condition that
c_{j}\leq d_{j}
for all
j\in[m]
is necessary for Fact
5
to hold.

2.6
Local unambiguous state discrimination (LUSD)

We conclude this section by reviewing unambiguous state discrimination (USD), and its local counterpart, local unambiguous state discrimination (LUSD). We use these notions in Section
3
to characterize generic LUSD with a resource state.

An
n
-tuple of states
([v_{1}],\dots,[v_{n}])\in\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{V})^{\times n}
is
(unambiguously) discriminable
if there exists a quantum measurement with
n+1
outcomes
\{1,\dots,n,?\}
that, when performed on any
[v_{a}]
, outputs either
a
or
?
, with non-zero probability to output
a
. Mathematically, this is equivalent to the existence positive semidefinite operators
M_{1},\dots,M_{n},M_{?}\in\mathrm{Pos}(\mathcal{V})
for which
M_{1}+\dots+M_{n}+M_{?}=\mathds{1}
and
(\langle v_{b}|M_{a}v_{b}\rangle\neq 0\iff a=b)
. Note that
([v_{1}],\dots,[v_{n}])
is discriminable if and only if it is linearly independent.

Let
\mathcal{V}=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{m}\mathbb{C}^{d_{j}}
be a vector space. We say that an
n
-tuple of states

\displaystyle{([v_{1}],\dots,[v_{n}])\in\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{V})^{\times n}}

(30)

is
locally (unambiguously) discriminable
if it is discriminable by a measurement implementable by an LOCC channel, with local subsystems
\mathbb{P}\mathbb{C}^{d_{j}}
. We say that an
n
-tuple of states
([v_{1}],\dots,[v_{n}])
is
locally (unambiguously) discriminable with (resource state)

{[w]\in\mathbb{P}{\mathcal{W}}}
if
([v_{1}\otimes w],\dots,[v_{n}\otimes w])
is locally discriminable, where the local subsystem
j\in[m]
is now the composite system
\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{C}^{d_{j}}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{c_{j}})
. This is equivalent to
([v_{1}],\dots,[v_{n}])
being locally discriminable via an LOCC measurement with pre-shared entanglement
[w]

[
PhysRevA.94.022311
]
.

3
Generic local state discrimination with pre-shared entanglement

In this section, we characterize the maximum number of generic pure states that can be locally discriminated with a fixed resource state
[w]
, and observe that a generic resource state
[w]
maximizes this number. To prove this characterization, we require the following mathematical description of LUSD with a resource state.

Theorem 6

(
[
PhysRevA.94.022311
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{V}=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{m}\mathbb{C}^{d_{j}}
and
\mathcal{W}=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{m}\mathbb{C}^{c_{j}}
. An
n
-tuple of states
([v_{1}],\dots,[v_{n}])\in\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{V})^{\times n}
is locally (unambiguously) discriminable with resource state

if and only if there exist states

\displaystyle[u_{1}],\dots,[u_{n}]\in\Ima_{\mathbb{P}\mathcal{V}}([w])

(31)

for which
(u_{a}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}{v_{b}}\neq 0\iff a=b)
, where the transpose can equivalently be taken with respect to any product basis of
\mathcal{V}
.

Note that this statement indeed does not depend on the choice of product basis over which the transpose is taken, since any two product bases are related by a product change of basis
A=A_{1}\otimes\dots\otimes A_{m}\in\GL(\mathcal{V})
, and

\displaystyle[u_{a}]\in\Ima_{\mathbb{P}\mathcal{V}}([w])\iff[Au_{a}]\in\Ima_{\mathbb{P}\mathcal{V}}([w]).

(32)

Also note that, if the Hermitian inner product is preferred, an alternative (equivalent) statement is that there exist
[u_{1}],\dots,[u_{n}]\in\Ima_{\mathbb{P}\mathcal{V}}([\overline{w}])
such that
{({\langle u_{a}|v_{b}\rangle}\neq 0\iff a=b)}
, where
\overline{w}
denotes the complex conjugate of
w
with respect to any product basis of
\mathcal{W}
.

The SLOCC image of a trivial (i.e. non-existent) resource state is simply the Segre variety
Y
of product states, defined in (
3
). This case of Theorem
6
, first proven by Chefles, was used to characterize generic LUSD in
[
article
]
, and we use the above generalization of Bandyopadhyay et al. to characterize generic LUSD with a resource state.

Theorem 7
.

Let
n
be a positive integer, let
\mathcal{V}=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{m}\mathbb{C}^{d_{j}}
and
\mathcal{W}=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{m}\mathbb{C}^{c_{j}}
be vector spaces, let
[w]\in\mathbb{P}\mathcal{W}
be a quantum state, let

be the closure of the SLOCC image of

n
-tuple of states in

is locally (unambiguously) discriminable with

n
-tuple of states in

is not locally (unambiguously) discriminable with

By Fact

can be used to discriminate the maximum number of generic states in this setting. It would be nice to know if the condition that

We emphasize that the third sentence of Theorem

.

We remark that similar characterizations of generic LUSD can be obtained in symmetric and antisymmetric space, but we omit stating such results explicitly.

. For any open-dense subset

, there exists

is open-dense, so

(37)

is open-dense. Thus,

. Since

intersects every open-dense subset

Corollary 10

The known dimensions of secant varieties in bipartite space allow us to quantify exactly how many generic states are locally discriminable with an arbitrary resource state:

Corollary 11

Let

and

be positive integers, let

For example, if

is large and

4
Entangled subspaces of maximum dimension

For a vector space
\mathcal{V}=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{m}\mathbb{C}^{d_{j}}
, we define a
(standard)

r
-entangled (projective linear) subspace
of
\mathbb{P}\mathcal{V}
to be a projective linear subspace disjoint from
\sigma_{r}(Y)
, where
Y
is the Segre variety of product states (
3
). In other words, an
r
-entangled subspace is one that does not contain any states of border rank at most
r
. Similarly, we define a
symmetric
r
-entangled (projective linear) subspace
of
\mathbb{P}({\bigvee}^{m}\mathbb{C}^{d})
to be a projective linear subspace disjoint from
\sigma_{r}(\nu_{m}(\mathbb{P}^{d-1}))
, and an
antisymmetric
r
-entangled (projective linear) subspace
of
\mathbb{P}({\bigwedge}^{m}\mathbb{C}^{d})
to be a projective linear subspace disjoint from
\sigma_{r}(\Gr(m-1,\mathbb{P}^{d-1}))
. These three types of
r
-entangled subspaces correspond to entangled subspaces in systems of distinguishable, bosonic, and fermionic particles, respectively
[
Grabowski_2012
]
.

In Appendix
we use Theorem
3
to determine the maximum dimensions of these entangled subspaces. In particular, Corollaries
,
, and
establish the maximum possible dimension of standard
r
-entangled, symmetric
r
-entangled, and antisymmetric
r
-entangled subspaces, respectively, and show that a generic subspaces of these dimensions are
r
-entangled. Despite the abundance of entangled subspaces of maximum dimension, explicit constructions of them are only known for standard
r
-entangled subspaces in the
r=1
case
[
BHAT_2006
]
and the
m=2
case
[
Cubitt_2008
]
. In this section, we match these results for symmetric and antisymmetric
r
-entangled subspaces by providing explicit constructions in these two cases. We also construct maximal
2
-entangled subspaces of
\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{C}^{d_{1}}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d_{2}}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{2})
for
d_{1},d_{2}\in\{2,3\}
, and of
\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{C}^{2}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{2}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{2}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{2})
.

Many of these constructions (and indeed, many of the already known constructions of similar subspaces from
[
Cubitt_2008
,
Heinosaari_2013
,
CDJ13
]
) are based on
totally non-singular matrices
, which are matrices with the property that all of their minors (i.e., determinants of square submatrices) are non-zero. For example, every Vandermonde matrix

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}1&\alpha_{1}&\alpha_{1}^{2}&\cdots&\alpha_{1}^{d-1}\\
1&\alpha_{2}&\alpha_{2}^{2}&\cdots&\alpha_{2}^{d-1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
1&\alpha_{d}&\alpha_{d}^{2}&\cdots&\alpha_{d}^{d-1}\end{bmatrix}

(38)

with
\alpha_{i}\neq\alpha_{j}\neq 0
for all
i\neq j
is totally non-singular (a slightly stronger property of these matrices, called total positivity, was proved in the case when

in
[
Fal01
]
, but the same proof works for total non-singularity in general). In fact, total non-singularity is a generic phenomenon: the set of totally non-singular matrices is open-dense.

The following result, which was proved in
[
Cubitt_2008
, Lemma 9]
, provides the reason that totally non-singular matrices are of use to us.

Lemma 12
.

Let
M
be an
n\times n
totally non-singular matrix with
n\geq k
, and let
v\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
be a linear combination of
k
of the columns of
M
. Then
v
contains at least
n-k+1
non-zero entries.

Maximal symmetric
r
-entangled subspaces of bipartite space

To construct a symmetric

We construct such a subspace by placing columns of totally non-singular matrices along the super- and sub-diagonals of those symmetric matrices. More specifically, for each

\displaystyle B=\Big\{\left[M_{j}^{i}+\big(M_{j}^{i}\big)^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}\right]:0\leq i\leq d-r-1,1\leq j\leq d-r-i\Big\}.

The fact that the set

All that remains is to count the number of vectors in

\displaystyle|B|=\sum_{i=0}^{d-r-1}\big(d-r-i\big)

\displaystyle=(d-r)^{2}-\sum_{i=0}^{d-r-1}i

\displaystyle=(d-r)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}(d-r)(d-r-1)=\binom{d-r+1}{2}.

Since the projective dimension of the subspace is
