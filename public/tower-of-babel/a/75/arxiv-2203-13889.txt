The Distribution of Rational Points on Conics
D.R. Heath-Brown

Abstract
. We examine the counting function for rational points on conics, and show how the point where the asymptotic behaviour begins depends on the size of the smallest zero.

Introduction

Let
q(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=q(\mathbf{x})\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}]
be a quadratic form, and
write

\mathbb{Z}_{\mathrm{prim}}^{n}=\{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}-\{\mathbf{0}\}:\,\mathrm{g.c.d.}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=1\}.

The
purpose of this paper is to investigate the behaviour as

B\rightarrow\infty
of the counting function

N(B)=N(B;q)=\#\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\mathrm{prim}}^{3}:\,q(\mathbf{x})=0,\,\max\{|x_{1}|,|x_{2}|,|x_{3}|\}\leq B\},

and of its weighted form

N(B,w)=N(B,w;q)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\mathrm{prim}}^{3}\\
q(\mathbf{x})=0\end{subarray}}w(B^{-1}\mathbf{x}).

Here we take
w:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}
to be infinitely differentiable, with
compact support. With this notation, the conic
q=0
has

\tfrac{1}{2}N(B)
rational points of height at most
B
.

Provided
q
is isotropic over
\mathbb{Q}
(in other words, if

q(\mathbf{x})=0
has at least one non-zero integral solution), one has

N(B)\sim\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma_{\infty}\mathfrak{S}(q)B\;\;\;\mbox{as}\;\;B\to\infty

where
\sigma_{\infty}>0
is the real density of solutions, and

\mathfrak{S}(q)>0
may be given
explicitly in terms of the usual product of local densities. (The
factor
\tfrac{1}{2}
is the “alpha constant” in Peyre’s terminology
[
4
]
)
Indeed one has

N(B,w)=\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma_{\infty}(q;w)\mathfrak{S}(q)B+O_{q,w}(B\exp\{-c\sqrt{\log B}\}),

(1)

for some absolute constant
c>0
. These
results follow from work of the author
[
3
, Corollary 2]

We stress that the error term in (
1
) contains an unspecified
dependence on

q
. Our main aim in this paper is to obtain a good explicit
dependence, so as to show how large
B
has to be, in terms of
q
,
before one sees the true asymptotics for
N(B)
.

In order to see the phenomena that
N(B)
can display we present a
numerical example. Let
q_{0}
be the form

q_{0}(\mathbf{x})=-61x_{1}^{2}-22x_{1}x_{3}-38x_{2}^{2}+99x_{2}x_{3}+39x_{3}^{2}.

(2)

Then the following graph shows values of

for

. Indeed for

the graph

. It is this strange behaviour that we aim to explain —

We begin by introducing some notation and terminology. In general we
will want to allow our form
q
to have odd cross-terms. We
therefore write it in the asymmetric shape

q(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{1\leq i\leq j\leq 3}q_{ij}x_{i}x_{j},

associate with
q
the matrix

Q=\left(\begin{array}[]{rrr}2q_{11}&q_{12}&q_{13}\\
q_{12}&2q_{22}&q_{23}\\
q_{13}&q_{12}&2q_{33}\end{array}\right).

(3)

Moreover, we define the determinant, somewhat
unconventionally, by

\Delta=\Delta(q)=\tfrac{1}{2}\det(Q).

Thus
\Delta\in\mathbb{Z}
for any integral form, and

\Delta\big(q(M\mathbf{x})\big)=\det(M)^{2}\Delta(q)

for any
3\times 3
matrix
M
. By changing the sign of
q
if necessary we can
arrange that
\Delta(q)\geq 0
. We
recall that
q
is said to be primitive if the coefficients
q_{ij}

have no common factor.
With this notation our first result is the following.

Theorem 1
.

Let
q
be a primitive integral isotropic form
with
\Delta>0
.
Then there is a positive integer
K\leq\tau(\Delta)
, and there are
nonsingular
3\times 3
integer matrices
M_{1},\ldots,M_{K}
having the
following properties.

(i)

If
\Delta
is square-free then
K=\tau(\Delta)
.

(ii)

The determinant
\det(M_{k})
is a positive divisor of
\Delta
.

(iii)

For any
primitive integral solution
\mathbf{x}
of the equation
q(\mathbf{x})=0
, there is a
unique index
k
such that
\mathbf{x}\in M_{k}(\mathbb{Z}^{3})
.

(iv)

For each
k
there is a corresponding
D_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

q(M_{k}\mathbf{x})=D_{k}(x_{1}x_{3}-x_{2}^{2}),

(4)

identically in
\mathbf{x}
.

(v)

We have

\Delta\det(M_{k})^{2}=D_{k}^{3},

(5)

so that
D_{k}\mid\Delta
and
\det(M_{k})\mid D_{k}
. Moreover

D_{k}\mid\det(M_{k})^{2}
.

(vi)

A prime
p
can divide
\Delta\det(M_{k})^{-1}
only if

v_{p}(\Delta)\geq 4
.

(vii)

If
\Delta
is cube-free then for every index

k\leq\tau(\Delta)
the set
M_{k}(\mathbb{Z}^{3})
contains a primitive zero
of
q
.

Here
\tau(\ldots)
is the usual divisor function, and
v_{p}(\Delta)

is the
p
-adic valuation. In addition to this
notation we will find it convenient to write
J(\mathbf{x})
for the quadratic form

x_{1}x_{3}-x_{2}^{2}
, so that
q(M_{k}\mathbf{x})=D_{k}J(\mathbf{x})
.

The theorem shows that we can partition the primitive integer zeros of

q
into
K
classes
\mathcal{C}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{C}_{K}
,
corresponding to the different matrices
M_{k}
. Specifically, we
define

\mathcal{C}_{k}=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\mathrm{prim}}^{3}\cap M_{k}(\mathbb{Z}^{3}):\,q(\mathbf{x})=0\}.

Moreover, since the
primitive integer zeros of
J
are given exactly twice each by

\pm(u_{1}^{2},u_{1}u_{2},u_{2}^{2})
, the theorem shows that we can
produce the primitive integer solutions of
q(\mathbf{x})=0
exactly twice
each as

\mathbf{x}=\pm M_{k}(u_{1}^{2},u_{1}u_{2},u_{2}^{2}).

Here
(u_{1},u_{2})

must be primitive if
\mathbf{x}

is, but unfortunately it is not true that
\mathbf{x}
is primitive whenever

u_{1}
and
u_{2}
are coprime.

Part (vi) of the theorem shows that if
\Delta
has no
fourth-power divisors then we have
\det(M_{k})=D_{k}=\Delta
for every
index
k
. In what follows, it may help the reader if they first
restrict attention to this simplified case.

Part (vii) of the theorem shows
that if
\Delta
is cube-free then each of the
classes
\mathcal{C}_{k}
is non-empty. For other values of
\Delta

we may discard any values
of
k
for which
\mathcal{C}_{k}
is empty, without affecting the claims in
the theorem. Thus we will suppose in what follows that each class

\mathcal{C}_{k}
is non-empty.

The form
q_{0}
given by (
2
) has
\Delta(q_{0})=977861=p_{0}
, say,
which is prime. Hence part (i) of the theorem shows that
K=2
, and
parts (v) and (vi) yield

\det(M_{1})=D_{1}=\det(M_{2})=D_{2}=p_{0}.

In fact we may take

M_{1}=\left(\begin{array}[]{rrr}1&-45&3426\\
0&100&3339\\
-1&-54&3047\end{array}\right)\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;M_{2}=\left(\begin{array}[]{rrr}39&-21&-98\\
0&-100&-1\\

(6)

Indeed

q_{0}(\mathbf{x})=L_{1}(\mathbf{x})L_{2}(\mathbf{x})-p_{0}L_{3}(\mathbf{x})^{2}
with

L_{1}(\mathbf{x})=100x_{1}+99x_{2}+100x_{3},\;\;L_{2}(\mathbf{x})=9778x_{1}+9877x_{2}+9779x_{3},

and

L_{3}(\mathbf{x})=x_{1}+x_{2}+x_{3},

and it turns out that the two classes are

\mathcal{C}_{1}=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\mathrm{prim}}^{3}:\,q(\mathbf{x})=0\;\mbox{and}\;p_{0}\mid L_{1}(\mathbf{x})\}

and

\mathcal{C}_{2}=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\mathrm{prim}}^{3}:\,q(\mathbf{x})=0\;\mbox{and}\;p_{0}\mid L_{2}(\mathbf{x})\}.

In order to use Theorem
1
for quantitative results we will need
information on the size of the entries in
M_{k}
. However for each
k

there are infinitely many choices for
M_{k}
, since the automorphism
group

\Aut(J)=\Aut_{\mathbb{Z}}(J)=\{U\in{\rm M}_{3}(\mathbb{Z}):J(M\mathbf{x})=J(\mathbf{x})\}

is infinite. (Note that
\det(U)=\pm 1
for every
U\in\Aut(J)
. Thus

U^{-1}
will be automatically be integral.) Our next result shows that we can
always make a good choice for
M_{k}
. We will write
||\mathbf{x}||
for the

L^{2}
-norm of the vector
\mathbf{x}
, and

||q||
for the
L^{2}
-norm of the coefficients of the
matrix
Q
of

, as given by (

). Specifically, we have

||q||=||Q||=\{4q_{11}^{2}+2q_{12}^{2}+2q_{13}^{2}+4q_{22}^{2}+2q_{23}^{2}+4q_{33}^{2}\}^{1/2}.

Theorem 2
.

In Theorem
1
we may choose
M_{k}
so that if
M_{k}^{-1}
has
rows
\mathbf{r}_{1},\mathbf{r}_{2},\mathbf{r}_{3}
, then

||\mathbf{r}_{1}||\cdot||\mathbf{r}_{3}||\leq 9D_{k}^{-1}||q||\;\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;\;||\mathbf{r}_{2}||^{2}\leq 10D_{k}^{-1}||q||.

(7)

Moreover if
M_{k}
has columns
\mathbf{c}_{1},\mathbf{c}_{2},\mathbf{c}_{3}

we will have
||\mathbf{c}_{1}||\leq||\mathbf{c}_{3}||
,

||\mathbf{c}_{1}||\cdot||\mathbf{c}_{3}||\leq 90\det(M_{k})^{2}D_{k}^{-2}||q||^{2}\leq 90||q||^{2}

(8)

and

||\mathbf{c}_{2}||\leq 9\det(M_{k})D_{k}^{-1}||q||\leq 9||q||.

(9)

The constants can certainly be improved, but for our purposes it
suffices to know that there is at least one set of numerical values that is
valid. From now on we will assume that the matrices
M_{k}
are as
described in Theorem
2
. For the form
(
2
) we have

||q_{0}||=\sqrt{39872}=199.679\ldots,

and one sees that the columns of the matrices (
6
) amply fulfil the
conditions above.

Since
q(\mathbf{c}_{1})=q(\mathbf{c}_{3})=0
, we find that for any isotropic form
q

and any index
k
there are two linearly independent zeros

\mathbf{c}_{1},\mathbf{c}_{3}\in M_{k}(\mathbb{Z}^{3})
with

||\mathbf{c}_{1}||\cdot||\mathbf{c}_{3}||\leq 90||q||^{2}
; in particular there is at least
one vector in
\mathcal{C}_{k}
of length at most
10||q||
. (The reader
should note that
\mathbf{c}_{1}
and
\mathbf{c}_{3}
need not be primitive, while

\mathcal{C}_{k}
is defined as the set of primitive zeros in
M_{k}(\mathbb{Z}^{3})
.)
Thus Theorem
2
recovers (in the case of ternary forms) the
results of Davenport
[
2

and Cassels
[
1
]
,
which were weaker in as much as they referred only to the complete
set of zeros of
q
, rather than individual classes
\mathcal{C}_{k}
.

Our next result, which is rather easy, explains how
\mathbf{c}_{1}
and

\mathbf{c}_{3}
are related to the smallest and second smallest zeros of
q

in
\mathcal{C}_{k}
. It is phrased in terms of the quantity

\rho=\rho(q)=\frac{||q||^{3}}{\Delta},

which we will refer to as the “aspect ratio” of
q
. We will see that

\rho\geq 2
in all cases, and we may expect that
\rho(q)\approx 1
for
“typical” forms
q
. We therefore think of forms with large aspect
ratio as having untypically small determinant. For the form
(
2
) we have
\rho(q_{0})=8.141\ldots
.

Theorem 3
.

We have
\rho(q)\geq 2
for any
q
.

Let
\mathbf{z}_{1}
be an element of
\mathcal{C}_{k}
of minimal length, and let
\mathbf{z}_{2}

be an element of
\mathcal{C}_{k}
of minimal length subject to the condition
that
\mathbf{z}_{2}\not=\pm\mathbf{z}_{1}
. Then

||\mathbf{z}_{1}||\cdot||\mathbf{z}_{2}||\geq D_{k}/||q||.

(10)

If

||\mathbf{c}_{1}||<\rho^{-1/2}\det(M_{k})D_{k}^{-1}||q||,

then
\mathbf{c}_{1}
must be a scalar multiple of the shortest vector
\mathbf{z}_{1}
.
In general

||\mathbf{c}_{1}||\leq 90\rho||\mathbf{z}_{1}||\;\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;\;||\mathbf{c}_{3}||\leq 90\rho||\mathbf{z}_{2}||.

When
\rho\ll 1
, as we usually expect, we may interpret Theorem

3
as saying that the lengths of
\mathbf{c}_{1}
and
\mathbf{c}_{3}
are
within a constant factor of the shortest possible lengths, namely

||\mathbf{z}_{1}||
and
||\mathbf{z}_{2}||
. Moreover, suppose we write
c
for the
constant
c=(91\rho)^{-1/2}
and

\ell=\sqrt{90}\det(M_{k})D_{k}^{-1}||q||

for the maximum length for
\mathbf{c}_{1}
permitted by (
8
). Then
whenever
\mathbf{c}_{1}
has length at most
c\ell
, the vector
\mathbf{c}_{1}

must actually
be the minimal zero
\mathbf{z}_{1}
, or a scalar multiple of it. For the form
q_{0}
,
the first column is a minimal zero, so that any other zero in

\mathcal{C}_{1}
must have length
at least
2^{-1/2}p_{0}/||q_{0}||=3462.805\ldots
The third column of

M_{1}
gives a zero of length
5671.913\ldots
. In contrast, the first
and third columns of
M_{2}
give relatively small zeros in
\mathcal{C}_{2}
.

In fact Theorem
2
follows from the following more general
result.

Theorem 4
.

Suppose
A
is a
3\times 3
integer matrix, and that
J(A\mathbf{x})=q(\mathbf{x})
.
Then there is a matrix
U\in\Aut(J)
such that the rows

\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c}
of
UA
satisfy

||\mathbf{a}||\cdot||\mathbf{c}||\leq 9||q||\;\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;\;||\mathbf{b}||^{2}\leq 10||q||.

We may use the previous theorems to count primitive zeros of
q(\mathbf{x})
.
Our eventual aim is to give a sharp explicit version of the asymptotic
formula (
1
). We begin by estimating the number of zeros in
each of the classes
\mathcal{C}_{k}
with height at most
B
, using the
counting function

N(B;\mathcal{C}_{k})=\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{C}_{k}}w(B^{-1}\mathbf{x}).

Previously we had said that
:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}
should be infinitely
differentiable, with compact support. We shall now be more specific
and require that
w(\mathbf{x})=0
whenever
||\mathbf{x}||>1
.
Since it is possible that
w(\mathbf{x})
might vanish on
the zero locus of
q
we introduce a second weight function
w_{0}(x)

defined as

w_{0}(\mathbf{x})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{1-||\mathbf{x}|^{2}}\right\},&||\mathbf{x}||<1,\\
0,&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

(11)

This has the properties required for
w
itself, and its support
includes non-trivial points of the conic
q(\mathbf{x})=0
. We now define the
real density of points on the conic
q=0
,relative to the weight
w

by setting

\sigma_{\infty}(q;w)=\lim_{T\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}w(\mathbf{y})K_{T}(q(\mathbf{y}))dy_{1}dy_{2}dy_{3},

(12)

with

K_{T}(t)=T\max\{1-T|t|\,,\,0\}.

(13)

This coincides with the constant occuring in (
1
), see
Theorem 3 of
[
3
]
which has a mild variant of (
12
).
We shall show in Lemma
10
that the above limit does indeed exist.
The following asymptotic formula for
N(B;\mathcal{C}_{k})
then holds.

Theorem 5
.

For each class
\mathcal{C}_{k}
there is a square-free divisor
\Delta_{1}\Delta_{2}

of
\Delta
such that
p\mid\Delta_{1}
for every prime for which

p||\Delta
, and such that

\displaystyle N(B;\mathcal{C}_{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta^{1/2}}{2D_{k}^{1/2}}\sigma_{\infty}(q,w)\kappa B\left\{1+O_{w}\left(\psi(B)\left(\frac{||\mathbf{z}_{2}||}{B}\right)^{1/4}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt{}+O_{w}(1),

with

\kappa=\frac{6}{\pi^{2}}\prod_{p\mid\Delta_{1}}\frac{1}{1+p^{-1}}\prod_{p\mid\Delta_{2}}\frac{1-p^{-1}}{1+p^{-1}},

and

\psi(B)=4^{\omega(\Delta)}\rho\,\frac{\sigma_{\infty}(q,w_{0})}{\sigma_{\infty}(q,w)}\log B.

Here
\mathbf{z}_{2}
is the second smallest element of
\mathcal{C}_{k}
, as
described precisely in Theorem
3
.

A number of comments should be made here. Firstly, in interpreting
the theorem one should think of the factor
\psi(B)
as being roughly of
order 1, or more generally as not being too large. We will see in
Lemma
11
that
\sigma_{\infty}(q,w)\ll\sigma_{\infty}(q,w_{0})

when
\sup|w|\leq 1
. However we have no estimate in the reverse direction
since it is possible that the zero locus of
q
only just enters the
support of
w
, making
\sigma_{\infty}(q,w)
small. None the less it
is reasonable to think that
\sigma_{\infty}(q,w_{0})\ll\sigma_{\infty}(q,w)

in most cases of interest.

Viewing
\psi(B)
as being small we may
interpret the theorem as giving a linear asymptotic formula for

N(B;\mathcal{C}_{k})
which takes effect when
B
is not much larger
than
||\mathbf{z}_{2}||
. Indeed one can easily show that the error term

O_{w}(1)
is insignificant when
B\geq\rho\,||\mathbf{z}_{2}||
. Of course when

B<||\mathbf{z}_{2}||
the function
N(B;\mathcal{C}_{k})
counts at most the
zeros
\pm\mathbf{z}_{1}
. Thus
N(B;\mathcal{C}_{k})
is
O_{w}(1)

from
B=1
to
B=||\mathbf{z}_{2}||
, and then begins to display its typical
linear growth.

When
\Delta
is square-free we have
D_{k}=\Delta
for every
k
, by
parts (v) and (vi) of Theorem
1
. Moreover we will have

\Delta_{1}=\Delta
and
\Delta_{2}=1
, so that

\kappa=\frac{6}{\pi^{2}}\prod_{p\mid\Delta}\frac{1}{1+p^{-1}},

for each index
k
. Thus when
\Delta
is square-free the leading
constant in Theorem
5
is the same for each value of
k
, but
the point at which linear growth begins is potentially different.

We are now in a position to explain the observed kink in our graph
of
N(B)
for the quadratic
q_{0}
. The correspondence between
N(B)

and the counting functions
N(B;\mathcal{C}_{k})
is not precise since the
former is defined using the condition
||\mathbf{x}||_{\infty}\leq B
while the
latter use
||\mathbf{x}||=||\mathbf{x}||_{2}
. For the class
\mathcal{C}_{1}
we may take

\mathbf{z}_{1}=(1,0,-1)
. The zero of second smallest sup-norm in
\mathcal{C}_{1}

is
(3426,3339,3047)
whence
N(B;\mathcal{C}_{1})=2
for
1\leq B<3426
.
However as soon as
B
is somewhat larger than
3500
we will have

N(B;\mathcal{C}_{1})\sim cB
,
for a certain constant
c>0
. For
\mathcal{C}_{2}
the two zeros of
smallest sup-norm are
(39,0,61)
and
(-98,-1,99)
(or

(-38,-99,38)
, which has the same sup-norm) so that we will have

N(B;\mathcal{C}_{2})\sim cB
, as soon as
B
is somewhat larger than a
few hundred, with
the same constant
c
. Thus the initial section of the graph for

N(B,q_{0})
, up to
B=3500
or so, reflects the range in which

N(B;\mathcal{C}_{1})=2
but
N(B;\mathcal{C}_{2})
is already growing like
cB
,
and the later values of
B
are in the range where both

N(B;\mathcal{C}_{1})
and
N(B;\mathcal{C}_{2})
are growing like
cB
.

Some remarks on the shape of
\psi(B)
are also in order. It would be
interesting to know to what extent the various factors involved could
be reduced, or indeed removed. Although this seems possible to some
extent, we hope that the present form of
\psi(B)
will be sufficient
for applications.

We can produce an asymptotic formula for
N(B,w;q)
by summing up the
formulae for
N(B;\mathcal{C}_{k})
. Since
||\mathbf{z}_{2}||\leq||\mathbf{c}_{3}||

and
||\mathbf{z}_{1}||\leq||\mathbf{c}_{1}||
for each index
k
the inequality
(
8
) yields

||\mathbf{z}_{2}||\leq||\mathbf{c}_{3}||\ll||q||^{2}/||\mathbf{c}_{1}||\leq||q||^{2}/||\mathbf{z}_{1}||.

Thus Theorem
5
has the following immediate corollary, in light
of part (i) of Theorem
1
.

Theorem 6
.

Let
\mathbf{z}_{0}
be a non-trivial integer zero of
q
with
||\mathbf{z}_{0}||

minimal. Then

\displaystyle N(B,w;q)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma_{\infty}(q;w)\mathfrak{S}(q)B\left\{1+O_{w}\left(\psi(B)\left(\frac{||q||^{2}}{||\mathbf{z}_{0}||B}\right)^{1/4}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt{}+O_{w}(\tau(\Delta)),

with
\psi(B)
as in Theorem
5
.

This is the promised improvement of (
1
), with a good explicit
dependence on
q
. It produces a linear asymptotic growth as soon as

B
is a little larger than
||q||^{2}/||\mathbf{z}_{0}||
. Since
||\mathbf{z}_{0}||
is typically
of order around
||q||
this is essentially best possible. We should also
comment on the quality of the error term, which has a power saving in
B
.
In (
1
) there is a saving of order
\exp\{-c\sqrt{\log B}\}
, which
has its origins in the error term for the Prime Number Theorem. Thus
one could replace
\sqrt{\log B}
in the exponent by some slightly larger
power of
\log B
, but one
cannot hope to establish (
1
) with a power saving in
B
by the
methods of
[
3
]
.

The reader may compare our work with that of Sofos
[
5
]
. The latter gives an asymptotic formula for an unweighted counting function, and has an error term which has a better dependence on
B
(of order
B^{1/2}\log B
) and an explicit dependence on
q
, though a much weaker one.

In future work we plan to apply Theorem
6
to count rational
points on certain varieties that can be fibred into conics. Indeed
such applications provide the natural motivation for the present
paper. In work in preparation (jointly with Dan Loughran) we look at the counting function for Del Pezzo surfaces of degree 5, in the case where there is a conic fibration.
Another example, which we plan to examine in due course, is the variety

V\in\mathbb{P}^{2}\times\mathbb{P}^{2}
cut out by the equation

X_{0}Y_{0}^{2}+X_{1}Y_{1}^{2}+X_{2}Y_{2}^{2}=0,

Proof of Theorem

We begin with a result that will allow us to work with matrices over

\mathbb{Z}/mZ
, rather than
\mathbb{Z}
.

Lemma 1
.

Let
M
be an
n\times n
integer matrix, with determinant coprime
to some positive integer
r
. Then there is a matrix

M^{\prime}\equiv M\;(\text{mod}\;r)
with prime determinant.
Moreover, if
\det(M)\equiv 1\;(\text{mod}\;r)

there is an
M^{\prime\prime}\equiv M\;(\text{mod}\;r)
in
\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
.

Proof.

We can write
M
in Smith Normal Form as
M=UDV
with

U,V\in\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
and
D
diagonal. One then sees that it
suffices to prove the lemma when
M
is diagonal, which we do by
induction on
n
. The case
n=1
is immediate, by Dirichlet’s
Theorem. If the result is true for matrices of size
n-1
, and

M=\mathrm{Diag}(m_{1},\ldots,m_{n})=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}M_{0}&0\\
\hline\cr 0&m_{n}\end{array}\right),

say, then
\det(M_{0})
will be coprime to
r
so that

M_{0}\equiv M_{0}^{\prime}\;(\text{mod}\;r)
with
\det(M_{0}^{\prime})
prime. It follows that
we may write
M_{0}^{\prime}
in Smith Normal Form as
U_{0}D_{0}V_{0}
, with

D_{0}=\mathrm{Diag}(1,\ldots,1,p)
say. Thus

M\equiv M_{1}\;(\text{mod}\;r)
with

M_{1}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}M^{\prime}_{0}&0\\
\hline\cr 0&m_{n}\end{array}\right)=U_{1}\mathrm{Diag}(1,\ldots,1,p,m_{n})V_{1},

where

U_{1}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}U_{0}&0\\
\hline\cr 0&1\end{array}\right),

and similarly for
V_{1}
. To complete the induction step it
remains to show that the lemma holds for the matrix

\mathrm{Diag}(p,m_{n})
. However

\mathrm{Diag}(p,m_{n})\equiv\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}p&sr\\
r&m_{n}+tr\end{array}\right)\;(\text{mod}\;r)

and the matrix on the right has determinant

pm_{n}+tpr-sr^{2}
. Since
pm_{n}
will be coprime to
r
we
can make this determinant prime by taking
t=0
and
choosing
s
suitably. Moreover, if
pm_{n}=1+kr
, we can
make the determinant equal to 1 by choosing
s
and
t

so that
sr-tp=k
. This completes the induction
argument.
∎

Our next result describes the reduction of ternary forms modulo
a prime
p
and its powers. We do not assume that
p
is odd.

Lemma 2
.

Let
p
be prime and let
q(\mathbf{x})
be an integral ternary quadratic
form, not divisible by
p
but with

p^{e}||\Delta(q)
for some exponent

.
Then there is a matrix
M\in\mathrm{SL}_{3}(\mathbb{Z})

(i)

q(M\mathbf{x})\equiv\kappa x_{3}^{2}\;(\text{mod}\;p)

p\nmid\kappa

(ii)

q(M\mathbf{x})\equiv x_{1}x_{2}+\kappa p^{e}x_{3}^{2}\;(\text{mod}\;p^{e+1})

\kappa
coprime to

(iii)

q(M\mathbf{x})\equiv q_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})+\kappa p^{e}x_{3}^{2}\;(\text{mod}\;p^{e+1})

\kappa

p

irreducible modulo

Proof.

In view of Lemma
1
it suffices to find a suitable
p
-adic matrix

M\in\mathrm{SL}_{3}(\mathbb{Z}_{p})
. When
p
is odd we can diagonalize over

\mathbb{Z}_{p}
to give
Ax_{1}^{2}+Bx_{2}^{2}+Cx_{3}^{2}
, say. Since
q
has determinant
divisible by
p
, but does not vanish modulo

we see
that either case (i) of the lemma holds, or that we may take

p\nmid AB
and
p^{e}||C
. We then have case (ii) if
-AB
is
a quadratic residue of
p
, and case (iii) otherwise.

For
p=2
we consider the reduction of
q
over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
. Since

2\mid\Delta
we find that
q(\mathbf{x})
is equivalent to one of
x_{3}^{2}
, or

x_{1}x_{2}
, or
x_{1}^{2}+x_{1}x_{2}+x_{2}^{2}
over
\mathbb{F}_{2}

\mathrm{SL}_{3}(\mathbb{F}_{2})
. (This can be shown by considering all
possible forms

modulo 2, if necessary.) The first case leads
immediately to case (i) of the lemma. In the remaining cases, Lemma

1
shows that

is equivalent to

\tilde{q}(x_{1},x_{2})+\ell(x_{1},x_{2})x_{3}+\mu x_{3}^{2}
over
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, where

\ell(x_{1},x_{2})
is a linear form, and

\tilde{q}(x_{1},x_{2})\equiv x_{1}x_{2}
or

x_{1}^{2}+x_{1}x_{2}+x_{2}^{2}\;(\text{mod}\;2)
. Replacing
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
by

x_{1}-\xi_{1}x_{3}
and
x_{2}-\xi_{2}x_{3}
respectively eliminates the term

\ell(x_{1},x_{2})x_{3}
provided that

\ell(x_{1},x_{2})=\xi_{1}\frac{\partial\tilde{q}(x_{1},x_{2})}{\partial x_{1}}+\xi_{2}\frac{\partial\tilde{q}(x_{1},x_{2})}{\partial x_{2}}.

Suitable
\xi_{1},\xi_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}
can always be found, since the linear

\partial\tilde{q}/\partial x_{1}
and

\partial\tilde{q}/\partial x_{2}
are congruent modulo 2 to
x_{2}
and

x_{1}
respectively. We then conclude that
q
is equivalent to

\tilde{q}(x_{1},x_{2})+\mu^{\prime}x_{3}^{2}
over
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
. Computing the
determinant of this we find that
2^{e}||\mu^{\prime}
. When

\tilde{q}(x_{1},x_{2})\equiv x_{1}^{2}+x_{1}x_{2}+x_{2}^{2}\;(\text{mod}\;2)

\tilde{q}(x_{1},x_{2})\equiv x_{1}x_{2}\;(\text{mod}\;2)

\mathbb{Z}_{2}

∎

We next have the following lemma, which shows how we remove powers of

Lemma 3

Suppose that

Thirdly, if
