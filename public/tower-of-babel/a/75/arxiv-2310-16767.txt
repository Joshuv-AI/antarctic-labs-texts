Inversion Sets and Quotient Root Systems
Ivan Dimitrov, Cole Gigliotti, Etan Ossip, Charles Paquette, David Wehlau

Abstract
. The main result of this paper is a recursive description of all decompositions Δ + = Φ 1 ⊔ Φ 2 ⊔ ⋯ ⊔ Φ k \Delta^{+}=\Phi_{1}\sqcup\Phi_{2}\sqcup\dots\sqcup\Phi_{k} of the positive roots Δ + \Delta^{+} of an arbitrary root system Δ \Delta into a disjoint union of inversion sets. Such decompositions play a central role in geometric invariant theory (GIT) in connection with studying the Littlewood-Richardson cone and related problems. This work can be considered as a continuation of [ D-W ] in which similar questions were studied for root systems of type 𝔸 \mathbb{A} . The methods of [ D-W ] rely on properties of permutations and are not transferable to an arbitrary root system. In order to develop a type-independent approach, we go beyond root systems and consider quotient root systems (QRSs for short). We study subsets of positive roots in an arbitrary QRS R R . We prove that every Φ ⊆ R + \Phi\subseteq R^{+} can be represented in a canonical way as an inflation and develop methods to study recursively properties of such subsets. We extend the notion of an inversion to subsets of any QRS, i.e., beyond the case when a Weyl group is associated with R R . If Φ ⊆ R + \Phi\subseteq R^{+} is an inversion set, we introduce a graph G ⁡ ( Φ ) G(\Phi) and endow the set Comp ⁡ ( Φ ) {\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)} of connected components of G ⁡ ( Φ ) G(\Phi) with a partial addition. The resulting monoid-like structure ( Comp ⁡ ( Φ ) , + ) ({\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)},+) is a further generalization of root systems beyond QRSs. We study in detail the properties of ( Comp ⁡ ( Φ ) , + ) ({\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)},+) and their applications to studying the properties of Φ \Phi . In particular we investigate the relationship between Φ \Phi being primitive and Φ \Phi being irreducible. Apart from describing recursively all decompositions of Δ + \Delta^{+} into the disjoint union of inversion sets, we provide applications to geometric invariant theory and derive enumerative results which may be of independent interest. Keywords: Root system, Quotient root system, Inversion set, Biconvex set.

1.
Introduction

Let
\Delta
be a root system with Weyl group
W
and set of positive roots
\Delta^{+}
. The inversion
set of

is the set

\Phi(w):=\{\alpha\in\Delta^{+}\,|\,w(\alpha)\in\Delta^{-}\}=\Delta^{+}\cap w^{-1}(\Delta^{-})\ ,

where
\Delta^{-}=-\Delta^{+}
. If
\Delta
is of type
\mathbb{A}_{n}
, then
\Delta^{+}
can be identified with the set

\{(i,j)\in\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}\,|\,1\leqslant i<j\leqslant n+1\}\ ,

W=S_{n+1}
is the symmetric group on
n+1
elements, and the inversion set of
\sigma\in S_{n+1}

is the set

\Phi(\sigma)=\{(i,j)\in\Delta^{+}\,|\,\sigma(i)>\sigma(j)\}\ .

Inversion sets we first introduced by Kostant,
[
K1
]
, in the context of Lie algebra cohomology.
Because of their relationship to cohomologies of homogeneous varieties, inversion sets
have recently arisen in various problems in geometric invariant theory (GIT), e.g. when
describing faces of the Littlewood-Richardson cone or studying the Belkale-Kumar product on full flag
varieties, see for example
[
BK
]
and
[
DR
]
.

Describing the decompositions of
\Delta^{+}
as a disjoint union of inversion sets arises
in several of the problems mentioned above, see
[
D-W
]
and the references therein for details.
In
[
D-W
]
a complete description of such decompositions was obtained in terms of inflations of permutations.
The results for type
\mathbb{A}
are also carried over to types
\mathbb{B}
and
\mathbb{C}
by exploiting a realization of each
of the latter root systems in terms of root systems of type
\mathbb{A}
with additional symmetries. Unfortunately, the methods
developed there do not apply to root systems of type
\mathbb{D}
and to exceptional root systems.

The motivation and starting
point for this paper was to provide a uniform approach to studying decompositions of
\Delta^{+}
as
into a disjoint union of inversion sets, i.e., as

(1)

\Delta^{+}=\Phi(w_{1})\sqcup\Phi(w_{2})\sqcup\dots\sqcup\Phi(w_{k})\ .

We develop machinery which allows us to provide in Theorem
9.1
a recursive
description of all decompositions (
1
). This description is then used to prove some statements
which have appeared recently in works related to GIT.
In
[
FR
]
a property of inversion sets

\Phi(w_{1}),\Phi(w_{2}),\Phi(w_{3})
that satisfy (
1
) for
k=3

is central for the main result. In the first
version of the paper the authors provided a very long case-by-case proof of that property. In the latest
version of
[
FR
]
a much simpler proof based on the first version of this paper is provided. As an illustration of
the methods and results we develop, we provide two short proofs of the same result, see
Proposition
9.6
. In
[

]
the authors discuss the conjecture that the sum of the numbers of right
descents of elements

of the Weyl group

of
\Delta

which satisfy (

\Delta
. They provide a long
proof in the case when
\Delta
is a root system of type
\mathbb{A}
. Propostion

establishes the same result for all finite Weyl groups. (In the case when
\Delta
is of type

\mathbb{A}
, Propostion
9.4
was proved in
[

]
.)

In order to extend the results of
[
D-W
]
, we introduce the notion of inflation of
inversion sets which generalizes inflations of permutations to inflations of arbitrary subsets of
positive roots in a (quotient) root system.
The operation of taking an inflation of permutations,
which plays a central role in
[
D-W
]
, is nothing but the composition
in the operad whose
n
-ary operations are the permutations of
n
elements. This operad structure yields
a canonical expression of every permutation as a composition (in the sense of operads) of permutations. In
particular, one can introduce the notion of a primitive permutation — a permutation which cannot be represented as a
non-trivial composition (in
[
D-W
]
the term “simple permutation” is used instead of primitive permutation).
It turns out that the property of a permutation being primitive is closely related (though not identical) to
the corresponding inversion set being irreducible, i.e., one that does not decompose into the disjoint union
of two non-empty inversion sets. Following this point of view, it is natural to look for an operad structure
on root systems beyond these of type
\mathbb{A}
and to explore the relationship between compositions in the operad and
decompositions of inversion sets. The first major obstacle to realizing the idea outlined above is that the
class of root systems is not large enough to define the corresponding operad. It turns out that the natural extension
to the class of quotient root systems (QRSs for short) allows us to extend the notion of inflation
to subsets of arbitrary QRSs. In this paper we do not use the language of operads but the interested reader will
be able to fill in the details.

Roughly speaking, if
\Delta
is a root system with
a base
\Sigma
and
I
is a subset of
\Sigma
, the QRS
\Delta/I
is the image of
\Delta

under the natural projection
\pi_{I}:\mathrm{span\,}\Delta\to\mathrm{span\,}\Delta/\mathrm{span\,}I
. QRSs have been studied extensively
in connection with Lie theory,
[
K2
]
, and simplicial hyperplane arrangements,
[
Cu
]
and the references therein,
to mention a few. The notions of positive roots, bases, etc.
extend to QRSs and the counterparts of Weyl groups are certain groupoids called Weyl groupoids.
Quotients of QRSs are also well-defined and taking quotients of QRSs is functorial. In particular,
for any QRS
R
and any subset
I
of a given base
S
of
R
we have the quotient
R/I
, the
subsystem
R_{I}
, and the surjection
\pi_{I}:R\to R/I
.

Let
\Psi\subseteq(R/I)^{+}
and

, where
(R/I)^{+}
and
R_{I}^{+}
denote the positive roots
of
R/I
and
R_{I}

of

4.1
)
the
inflation of

by

as the subset

(2)

\mathrm{inf}_{I}^{S}(\Psi,X)=\{\alpha\in R^{+}\backslash R_{I}\,|\,\pi_{I}(\alpha)\in\Psi\}\cup\{\alpha\in R\,|\,\alpha\in X\}\subseteq R^{+}\ .

Every
\Phi\subseteq R^{+}
can be expressed as an inflation with
I=\emptyset
and
I=S
as follows:

\Phi=\mathrm{inf}_{\emptyset}^{S}(\Phi,\emptyset)=\mathrm{inf}_{S}^{S}(\emptyset,\Phi)
.
We call these two expressions
trivial inflations
. A subset
\Phi\subseteq R^{+}
is

if it cannot be represented as a non-trivial inflation. In Theorem
4.12

we prove that every
\Phi\subseteq R^{+}
can be expressed uniquely
as
\Phi=\mathrm{inf}_{I}^{S}(\Psi,X)
, where
\emptyset\subseteq I\subsetneq S
and

(i)

\Psi
is primitive

or

(ii)

and

is minimal with this property.

Let

be a QRS with positive roots
R^{+}
. By definition, a subset
\Phi\subseteq R^{+}
is an inversion set
in

if
\Phi
is both closed and co-closed. If

is a root system,
\Phi\subseteq R^{+}
is an inversion set
if and only if it is the inversion set of an element

of the Weyl group

of
\Delta

Inflation preserves inversion sets:
\Phi=\mathrm{inf}_{I}^{S}(\Psi,X)
is an inversion set in

if and only if

\Psi
and
X
are inversion sets in
R/I
and
R_{I}

In order to study various properties and attributes of inversion sets like their supports, canonical form
(
2
), decompositions into a disjoint union of inversion sets, etc., we introduce the graph

G(\Phi)
associated with an inversion set
\Phi\subseteq R^{+}
. The vertices of
G(\Phi)
are the elements of
\Phi
.
Two vertices
\alpha,\beta\in\Phi
are connected with an edge if and only if
\alpha-\beta\in\pm\Phi^{c}
.
Let
{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
denote the set of connected components of
G(\Phi)
. It turns out that the set
{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
inherits
an order and a partial addition from
R^{+}
. Remarkably, the order and the partial addition enjoy many of the
properties of the order and the addition on
R^{+}
. Since
{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}=R^{+}
when
\Phi=R^{+}
, we can consider

{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
as a further extension of the notion of a root system beyond QRSs. Despite the similarities, there
are some important distinctions between
R^{+}
and
{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
. The first one is that the addition is only partially
defined. This is actually not too surprising — the addition in
R^{+}
is also only partially defined. However,
in the case of
{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
, we do not know of a natural lattice that contains
{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
and such that the partial addition
on
{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
is inherited from the addition on the lattice.
The second (and related)
problem is that cancellation rules do not always hold in
{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
: we may have
A+B=A+C
without
B=C

or even
A+A=A
for
A\neq 0
. The core of the paper is devoted to studying the properties of addition in
{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}

and their applications to representing
\Phi
as an inflation and to decomposing
\Phi
into a disjoint union of
inversion sets. In particular, we establish the relationship between two important properties of an inversion set:
being primitive and being irreducible.

We complete the paper with two sections on applications of the machinery developed earlier. The first
set of applications is to three problems arising in geometric representation theory — we are
able to prove statements that have arisen in other works and up until now have been beyond reach.
The second set of applications is to derive some enumerative results which may be of interest to combinatorialists.
For example, we arrive at an analog of Catalan numbers arising from roots systems of types
\mathbb{D}
.

We expect that the methods developed in this paper may find further applications. For example,
the notion of inflation is not limited to inversion sets and it is reasonable to ask what other
classes of subsets of positive roots will yield interesting combinatorial and/or algebraic structures similar
to
{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
. Another interesting direction is to understand whether the partial addition on
{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
can
be understood as the restriction of an additive structure on an appropriate lattice.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Quotient root systems

Quotient root systems (QRSs for short), originally introduced by Kostant, provide the
natural setting in which inversion sets of roots can be studied recursively. Below we provide the
necessary notation and background on QRSs; for more details, we refer the reader to
[
K2
]
and
[
DF
]
.

Recall,
[
B
]
and
[
H
]
, that a
root system
is a finite set
\Delta
of nonzero vectors in a Euclidean space

E
with inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
that satisfies the following properties:

(i)

\Delta
spans
E
;

(ii)

If
\alpha\in\Delta
and
r\in\mathbb{R}
satisfy
r\alpha\in\Delta
, then
r=\pm 1
;

(iii)

If
\alpha,\beta\in\Delta
, then
\sigma_{\alpha}(\beta)\in\Delta
, where
\sigma_{\alpha}

denotes the reflection about
\alpha
;

(iv)

For
\alpha,\beta\in\Delta
,

2\,\frac{\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle}\in\mathbb{Z}
.

The elements of
\Delta
are called
roots
.

Remark 2.1
.

It is convenient to modify slightly the definition above by requiring that the zero vector belongs to
\Delta
.
For the rest of the paper we will work with the assumption that all root systems (and quotient root systems, see below)
contain the zero vector. This convention requires obvious minor adjustments in the definitions and statements about root systems.
For example, in conditions (ii) – (iv) of the definition of a root system, we need to assume that
\alpha\neq 0
.
Note that, with this convention,
\Delta=\{0\}
is a root system of rank zero.

A subset
\Sigma\subseteq\Delta
is a
base
of
\Delta
if it is a basis of
E
and every nonzero element of
\Delta

is an integer combination of elements of
\Sigma
with non-negative or non-positive coefficients only. A base
\Sigma

yields the partition
\Delta=\Delta^{+}\sqcup\{0\}\sqcup\Delta^{-}
of
\Delta
into
positive roots
, the
zero root, and
negative roots
. In other words,
the positive and negative roots are the nonzero roots which are expressed respectively as
non-negative and non-positive combinations of elements of
\Sigma
.

Given a subset
I\subseteq\Sigma
, we introduce the following notation:

–

E_{I}
and
E/I
denote respectively the span of
I
and its orthogonal complement in
E
;

–

\pi_{i}:E\to E/I
is the projection defined by the decomposition
E=E_{I}\oplus E/I
;

–

\Delta_{I}:=\Delta\cap E_{I}
,
\Delta/I:=\pi_{I}(\Delta)\subseteq E/I
.

Clearly,
\Delta_{I}\subseteq E_{I}
is a root system with a base
\Sigma_{I}:=I
and positive roots

\Delta_{I}^{+}=\Delta_{I}\cap\Delta^{+}
with respect to
\Sigma_{I}
.

A
quotient root system
(or QRS for short) is a set of the form
R=\Delta/I
for some root system
\Delta
and
some subset
I
of a base of
\Delta
. We call the elements of a QRS
roots
.
Note that the quotient
\Delta/I
depends on
I
but not on
\Sigma
as long as
I
is a subset of a base of
\Delta
.
The
rank
of
R
, denoted
\mathrm{rk}\,R
, is the dimension of the ambient vector space
E/I
.
Furthermore, a given QRS
R
may be the quotient of different root systems, more precisely,
quotients of two different root systems may be isomorphic,
cf.
[
DF
]
.
The subset
\Delta/I
of
E/I
is the
quotient of
\Delta
with respect to
I
.
Finally,
\Delta/\emptyset=\Delta
and
\Delta/\Sigma=\{0\}
.
In particular, every root system is a QRS.

QRSs exhibit many properties similar to properties of root systems but also
differ in some aspects, e.g. QRSs allow for multiples of roots which are also roots, for example, a QRS
may contain both
\alpha
and
2\alpha
. For a detailed discussion on the properties of QRSs, see
[
K2
]
or
[
DF
]
.

As for root systems, a
base

S
of a QRS
R

is a subset of
R
that forms a basis of the underlying vector space
E/I
,
and such that every root can be written
as a non-negative or non-positive integral combination of elements of
S
. A base
S
of
R

determines a set of
positive
roots
R^{+}
, namely the nonzero elements of
R
which are
non-negative integral combinations of elements of
S
; the
negative
roots
R^{-}
are the nonzero elements of
R
which are
non-positive integral combinations of elements of
S
.
As in the case of root systems, we declare
0\in R
to be neither positive nor negative and
we have the disjoint union decomposition
R=R^{+}\sqcup\{0\}\sqcup R^{-}
.
A
positive system

P
in
R
is a subset which is closed under addition, i.e.,
(P+P)\cap R\subseteq P
and

R=P\sqcup\{0\}\sqcup(-P)
. Equivalently,
P\subseteq R
is a positive system if and only
if
P
consists of all elements of
R
in an open half-subspace of
E
whose boundary hyperplane
does not contain any nonzero roots.
Positive systems in
R
are in a bijection with bases of
R
,
see
[
DF
]
for a detailed discussion of positive systems in
R
and bases of
R
.

It is easy to see that, given a base
\Sigma
of
\Delta
and
I\subseteq\Sigma
,

S:=\Sigma/I:=\{\pi_{I}(\beta)\,|\,\beta\in\Sigma\backslash I\}

is a base of
R
. Moreover,

R^{+}=\pi_{I}(\Delta^{+})\backslash\{0\}
and
R^{-}=\pi_{I}(\Delta^{-})\backslash\{0\}
.

Throughout the paper we will use
R
and
S\subseteq R
to denote respectively a QRS

and a base

of
R
,
while we will reserve

\Delta
and
\Sigma
for root systems.
If
R,S,\Delta
, and
\Sigma
are used in the same discussion,

is assumed to be the quotient of
\Delta
with
respect to some
I\subseteq\Sigma
and
S=\Sigma/I
.

If
R
is a QRS with a base
S
and
I\subseteq S
, we can define the
quotient
R/I
of

with respect to

in the same way we defined quotients of root systems,
Taking quotients of QRSs is functorial:
If
I\subseteq J\subseteq S
, then

(R/I)/(J/I)\cong R/J\ ,

where
J/I\subseteq S/I
in the QRS

.
Similarly, we can define a subsystem

of

. Clearly,

also is a QRS
and taking subsystems is functorial.

Let
R
be a QRS in a Euclidean space

and let
E
be decomposed orthogonally
as
E=E^{1}\oplus\dots\oplus E^{s}
. We say that
R=R^{1}\times\dots\times R^{s}

if
R=R^{1}\cup\dots\cup R^{s}
, where
R^{i}:=E^{i}\cap R
.
If
R=R^{1}\times\dots\times R^{s}
and

is a base of
R^{i}
,
then
S=S^{1}\cup\dots\cup S^{s}
is a base of
R
. Moreover, every base of

is of this form.
We say that
R
is

if it cannot be written as
R=R^{\prime}\times R^{\prime\prime}
for two
nontrivial subsystems
R^{\prime}
and
R^{\prime\prime}
. Every nontrivial QRS decomposes uniquely
as
R=R^{1}\times\dots\times R^{s}
, where each
R^{i}
is irreducible.
Since the decomposition of a QRS into irreducible components interplays well with most properties,
throughout the paper we will implicitly assume that the QRSs we consider are irreducible, unless otherwise stated. Whenever necessary,
we will comment explicitly on the differences that arise for reducible QRSs.

The irreducible root systems
fall into the infinite series
\mathbb{A}_{l},\mathbb{B}_{l},\mathbb{C}_{l},\mathbb{D}_{l}
and the exceptional ones
\mathbb{E}_{6},\mathbb{E}_{7},\mathbb{E}_{8},\mathbb{F}_{4},\mathbb{G}_{2}
.
The irreducible QRSs are described in
[
DF
]
.
If
R
is an irreducible QRS, then as observed below, any quotient of
R
is itself an irreducible QRS.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
R
be a QRS with base
S=\{\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{r}\}
. We define a partial order on the roots by declaring
for two roots
\alpha=\sum_{i=1}^{r}a_{i}\theta_{i}
and

\beta=\sum_{i=1}^{r}b_{i}\theta_{i}
, that

\alpha\leqslant\beta
if
a_{i}\leqslant b_{i}
for
1\leqslant i\leqslant r
.

Remark 2.3
.

If
\Delta
is a root system, we can display a root in
\Delta
by labeling the nodes of the
Coxeter-Dynkin diagram with its coefficients in the given base.
For instance, for the root system
\mathbb{D}_{5}
, every root can be represented in the form

a\ b\ c\ ^{\displaystyle d}_{\displaystyle e}\

and the highest root of
\mathbb{E}_{6}
is represented as

{\begin{array}[]{@{}c@{}c@{}c@{}c@{}c@{\hskip 1pt}}&&2&&\hfil\hskip 1.0pt\\[-4.0pt]
1&2&3&2&1\hfil\hskip 1.0pt\end{array}}\ .

Definition 2.4
.

The
support
of a root
\alpha=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}a_{i}\theta_{i}
, with respect to a given base
S
, is

\operatorname{supp}(\alpha)=\{\theta_{i}\in S\mid a_{i}\neq 0\}.

If
E
is a set of roots, then we define its
support

\operatorname{supp}(E)
to be the union of the supports of the roots in
E
.

As in the case of root systems, if
R
is a QRS and
\alpha\in R
, then

\operatorname{supp}(\alpha)
is the base of an irreducible subsystem. Moreover,
R
is
irreducible if and only if there is a unique maximal root in
R
; in that case
its support is the corresponding base of
R
. In particular, the quotient of an
irreducible QRS is itself irreducible.

We finish this subsection by recording two results that will be useful in the sequel.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
\alpha,\beta\in R
. If
\left\langle\alpha,\beta\right\rangle<0
, then
\alpha+\beta\in R
.
If
\left\langle\alpha,\beta\right\rangle>0
, then
\alpha-\beta\in R
.

Proof.

This is part of Theorem 2.3 in
[
K2
]
.
∎

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
I\subseteq S
and
\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta},\bar{\gamma}
be non-zero elements in
R/I
such that

\bar{\gamma}=\bar{\alpha}+\bar{\beta}
. Then

(i)

If
\gamma\in\pi_{I}^{-1}(\bar{\gamma})
, then
there are
\alpha\in\pi_{I}^{-1}(\bar{\alpha})
and

\beta\in\pi_{I}^{-1}(\bar{\beta})
with
\gamma=\alpha+\beta
.

(ii)

If
\alpha\in\pi_{I}^{-1}(\bar{\alpha})
, then
there are

\beta\in\pi_{I}^{-1}(\bar{\beta})
and

\gamma\in\pi_{I}^{-1}(\bar{\gamma})

with
\gamma=\alpha+\beta
.

Proof.

Statement (i) follows from
[
K2
, Theorems 1.9 and 2.3]
and statement (ii) follows from (i) by considering the
elements
\bar{\alpha}=\bar{\gamma}+(-\bar{\beta})
.
∎

2.2.
Inversion sets

In this subsection, we fix a QRS
R
with base
S
. For a subset
\Phi
of
R^{+}
, we denote by
\Phi^{c}
its complement in
R^{+}
.

Definition 2.7
.

Let
\Phi\subseteq R^{+}
. We say that

(1)

\Phi
is
closed
if
\alpha+\beta\in\Phi
whenever
\alpha,\beta\in\Phi
.

(2)

\Phi
is
co-closed
if
\Phi^{c}
is closed, i.e., if

\alpha+\beta\in\Phi^{c}
whenever
\alpha,\beta\in\Phi^{c}
.

(3)

\Phi
is an
inversion set
if it is both closed and co-closed.

Remark 2.8
.

It is easy to see that
intersections of closed sets are closed and unions of co-closed sets are co-closed.

For roots systems, the notion of inversion set takes its origin from the fact that
\Phi
is an inversion set precisely
when there is an element
w
in the Weyl group of the root system such that

\Phi=\{\alpha\in R^{+}\mid w(\alpha)\in R^{-}\}.

Remark 2.9
.

A set
\Phi\subseteq R^{+}
is an inversion set if and only if

\Phi=R^{+}\cap P

for some positive system
P\subseteq R
. Moreover, the assignment
P\mapsto R^{+}\cap P
is a bijection
between the positive systems in
R
and inversion sets in
R^{+}
.

As we will be interested in decomposing inversion sets, we need the following two definitions.

Definition 2.10
.

Let
\Phi\subseteq R^{+}
be a non-empty inversion set. We say that
\Phi
is
irreducible
if, whenever

\Phi=\Phi_{1}\sqcup\Phi_{2}
as a disjoint union of inversion sets, then
\Phi_{1}=\Phi
or
\Phi_{2}=\Phi
.

Definition 2.11
.

Let
\Phi
be an inversion set in
R^{+}
. A
decomposition
of
\Phi
is an expression

\Phi=\Phi_{1}\sqcup\dots\sqcup\Phi_{r}

where all
\Phi_{i}
are pairwise disjoint inversion sets. Such a decomposition is
fine
if each
\Phi_{i}

contains exactly one simple root. In particular, the number of inversion sets in a fine decomposition of
R^{+}

equals the rank of
R
.

Proposition 2.12
.

Let
\Phi=\Phi_{1}\sqcup\dots\sqcup\Phi_{r}
be a decomposition of an inversion set
\Phi
.
Then for
\emptyset\neq I\subseteq\{1,\dots,r\}
, the union
\cup_{i\in I}\Phi_{i}
is an inversion set.

Proof.

It suffices to prove that
\Phi_{1}\sqcup\Phi_{2}
is closed and co-closed. It is clear that
\Phi_{1}\sqcup\Phi_{2}

is co-closed as the union of co-closed sets.
Further,
\Phi_{1}\sqcup\Phi_{2}
is closed as the complement of
\Phi_{3}\sqcup\dots\sqcup\Phi_{r}\sqcup\Phi^{c}
,
which is co-closed.
∎

3.
Paths in QRSs

One difficulty when working with QRSs, including root systems, is that sums of roots are not in general
roots. In this section we study the following question: Given roots
\alpha
and
\beta
in a QRS
R

and a set
K\subseteq R
with

\beta-\alpha\in\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{Z}}K
, is there a way to add roots in
K
to get from
\alpha
to
\beta
without
leaving
R
?
Through Sections
3.1
and
3.2
, we
build toward an affirmative answer to this question.
Section
3.1
introduces an identity for roots which will be used throughout the paper.
Section
3.2
then introduces paths, and Proposition
3.5

guarantees their existence.
In Section
3.3

we consider how freely one may choose roots from
K
, and define an especially flexible class of paths.

In this section, although the focus is still on irreducible QRSs, the results we present
apply to general QRSs. Remark
3.4
below explains how paths
in reducible QRSs are built from paths in irreducible components.

3.1.
Two-out-of-three rule

We start by establishing the “two-out-of-three” rule for sums of roots.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in R
be such that
\alpha+\beta+\gamma\in R

but
\beta+\gamma\notin R
. If
\alpha+\gamma\neq 0
, then
\alpha+\beta\in R
;
if
\alpha+\beta\neq 0
, then
\alpha+\gamma\in R
.
In particular, if
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in R
,
\alpha+\beta+\gamma\in R
,
and none of
\alpha+\beta,\alpha+\gamma,\beta+\gamma
equals zero, then at least
two of
\alpha+\beta,\alpha+\gamma,\beta+\gamma
belong to
R
.

Proof.

We repeatedly use Proposition
2.5
.
We assume that
\alpha+\gamma\neq 0
and show that
\alpha+\beta\in R
. It suffices to show that
either
\left\langle\alpha,\beta\right\rangle<0
or
\left\langle\alpha+\beta+\gamma,\gamma\right\rangle>0
. For that reason, we will
assume
\left\langle\alpha,\beta\right\rangle\geqslant 0
and show that
\left\langle\alpha+\beta+\gamma,\gamma\right\rangle>0
. Note that
because
\beta+\gamma\notin R
, both
\left\langle\beta,\gamma\right\rangle\geqslant 0

and
\left\langle\alpha+\beta+\gamma,\alpha\right\rangle\leqslant 0
.
We extract from these inequalities that
\left\langle\alpha+\gamma,\alpha\right\rangle\leqslant-\left\langle\beta,\alpha\right\rangle\leqslant 0
.
Because
\left\langle\alpha+\gamma,\alpha+\gamma\right\rangle>0
and
\left\langle\alpha+\gamma,\alpha\right\rangle\leqslant 0
,
we now have that
\left\langle\alpha+\gamma,\gamma\right\rangle>0
. Finally, we note that

\left\langle\alpha+\beta+\gamma,\gamma\right\rangle=\left\langle\alpha+\gamma,\gamma\right\rangle+\left\langle\beta,\gamma\right\rangle>0
,
and so we conclude that
\alpha+\beta\in R
. By the symmetry of the statement,
\alpha+\gamma

is a root provided
\alpha+\beta\neq 0
, proving the first part of the statement. The second part follows
immediately from the first one.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

For root systems we can prove the result above by passing to the corresponding complex Lie algebra
and using properties of its root decomposition and Jacobi’s identity. Such an argument carries over
to the case of QRSs by using the eigenspace decomposition of a Lie algebra with respect to a
toral subalgebra which is not maximal, see
[
K2
]
.

3.2.
Paths in
R

Definition 3.3
.

A
path
from a root
\alpha
to a root
\beta
consists of the pair
(\alpha,\beta)

and a possibly empty sequence of roots
\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2},\dots,\kappa_{n}

such that
\alpha+\kappa_{1}+\dots+\kappa_{n}=\beta

and for all
i
, we have
\alpha+\kappa_{1}+\dots+\kappa_{i}\in R
.
We denote this path
[\alpha;\kappa_{1},\dots,\kappa_{n};\beta]
and call each
\kappa_{i}
a
step
.
The roots
\alpha+\kappa_{1}+\dots+\kappa_{i}
are called the
partial sums
of the path.
We say that
[\alpha;\kappa_{1},\dots,\kappa_{n};\beta]
passes through
\gamma
if
\gamma
is such
a partial sum.

The path
[\alpha;-\alpha,\beta;\beta]
is called the
trivial path from
\alpha
to
\beta
.

Remark 3.4
.

If
\alpha
and
\beta
are nonzero roots in different components of
R
, then any path from
\alpha

to
\beta
necessarily passes through zero. Consequently, any path in
R
that does not pass through zero is
contained in a single component of
R
.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
\beta=\alpha+\kappa_{1}+\dots+\kappa_{n}
, where
\alpha,\beta,\kappa_{i}\in R
for
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
.
There is a path

[\alpha;\kappa_{i_{1}},\kappa_{i_{2}},\dots,\kappa_{i_{m}};\beta]

from
\alpha
to
\beta
for some (possibly empty) subset

\{i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{m}\}
of
\{1,2,\dots,n\}
.

Proof.

Set
\mu:=\kappa_{1}+\dots+\kappa_{n}
. If
\mu=0
, then there is nothing to prove. Thus we assume that
\mu\neq 0
.
First we prove that
there is
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
such that either
\alpha+\kappa_{i}\in R
or
\alpha+\mu-\kappa_{i}\in R
.
For this we consider two cases for
\mu
:

(i)

\mu\not\in R
. In this case
\alpha+\kappa_{i}\in R
for some
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
.
Indeed, since
\beta\in R
but
\mu=\beta-\alpha\not\in R
, we conclude that
\alpha\neq 0
and

0\geqslant\left\langle\alpha,\beta\right\rangle=\left\langle\alpha,\alpha\right\rangle+\left\langle\alpha,\kappa_{1}\right\rangle+\dots+\left\langle\alpha,\kappa_{n}\right\rangle\ .

Since
\left\langle\alpha,\alpha\right\rangle>0
, there is
1\leqslant i\leqslant n

such that
\left\langle\alpha,\kappa_{i}\right\rangle<0
and thus
\alpha+\kappa_{i}\in R
.

(ii)

\mu\in R
.
Since
\left\langle\mu,\kappa_{1}\right\rangle+\left\langle\mu,\kappa_{2}\right\rangle+\dots+\left\langle\mu,\kappa_{n}\right\rangle=\left\langle\mu,\mu\right\rangle>0
,
there is
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
such that
\left\langle\mu,\kappa_{i}\right\rangle>0
and hence
\mu-\kappa_{i}\in R
.
Applying
Proposition
3.1
to the expression
\beta=(\mu-\kappa_{i})+\alpha+\kappa_{i}
, we conclude
that if
\alpha+\kappa_{i}\notin R
, then
\mu-\kappa_{i}+\alpha\in R
, since

\mu=(\mu-\kappa_{i})+\kappa_{i}\neq 0
.

In proving the proposition,
we may and will assume that no non-empty subcollection of
\kappa_{1},\dots,\kappa_{n}
sums to 0.
With this assumption in mind, the statement of the proposition follows by induction on
n
from
the observation above.
∎

3.3.
Reduced paths

Given a path between two roots, there are often multiple
ways to rearrange its steps without breaking the condition that partial sums be roots. Having the freedom to permute
the steps in a path may allow us to ensure that all partial sums satisfy additional properties, e.g.
that they are positive or that they stay within an inversion set.
It turns out that, for a certain class of paths, every
permutation of the steps of such a path defines another path, see
Proposition
3.8
below.

Definition 3.6
.

A path
[\alpha;\kappa_{1},\dots,\kappa_{n};\beta]
is
reduced
if
no sum of two or more steps
\kappa_{i}
is a root.

Remark 3.7
.

(i)
Proposition
3.5
implies that the path

[\alpha;\kappa_{1},\dots,\kappa_{n};\beta]
is reduced if and only if
\kappa_{i}+\kappa_{j}\not\in R

for
1\leqslant i\neq j\leqslant n
. Indeed, assume that

\kappa_{i}+\kappa_{j}\not\in R
for
1\leqslant i\neq j\leqslant n
but

\kappa_{i_{1}}+\dots+\kappa_{i_{s}}\in R
for some
i_{1},\dots,i_{s}
. Assume
furthermore, that
s\geqslant 3
is the smallest integer for which such
\kappa_{i_{1}},\dots,\kappa_{i_{s}}

exist. Then
\kappa_{i_{2}}+\dots+\kappa_{i_{s}}\neq 0
by the minimality of
s

and, by Proposition
3.5
, there is a path between

\kappa_{i_{1}}
and
\kappa_{i_{1}}+\dots+\kappa_{i_{s}}
. In particular,
\kappa_{i_{1}}+\kappa_{i_{l}}\in R

for some
2\leqslant l\leqslant s
, contrary to the assumption that
\kappa_{i}+\kappa_{j}\not\in R

for
1\leqslant i\neq j\leqslant n
.

(ii)

It is clear that, given a path
[\alpha;\kappa_{1},\dots,\kappa_{n};\beta]
,
there exists a reduced path
[\alpha;\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{m};\beta]
, where each

\mu_{i}
is the sum of a subcollection of the steps
\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2},\dots,\kappa_{n}
of the original path
(and the subcollections corresponding to different steps among
\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{m}
are disjoint).
Of course, there may be several reduced paths
[\alpha;\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{m};\beta]
as above.

(iii)
If
\alpha,\beta
are nonzero roots in different components of
R
, then the only
reduced path from
\alpha
to
\beta
is the trivial one, see Remark
3.4
.

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
[\alpha;\kappa_{1},\dots,\kappa_{n};\beta]
be a non-trivial reduced path.
Then for any permutation
i_{1},\dots,i_{n}
of
1,\dots,n
,
[\alpha;\kappa_{i_{1}},\dots,\kappa_{i_{n}};\beta]
is a reduced path.

Proof.

If
\kappa_{i}=-\alpha
for some
i
, then
\beta=\sum_{j\neq i}\kappa_{i}
and the assumption that

[\alpha;\kappa_{1},\dots,\kappa_{n};\beta]
is reduced implies that
n\leqslant 2
. The paths

[\alpha;\kappa_{1},\dots,\kappa_{n};\beta]
with
n\leqslant 2
and
\kappa_{i}=-\alpha
for some
i
are

[\alpha;-\alpha;0]
,

[\alpha;\beta,-\alpha;\beta]
(if
\alpha+\beta\in R
), and

[\alpha;-\alpha,\beta;\beta]
. The statement is true for the first two paths and the last path is the trivial path.

Now assume that
n\geqslant 1
and
\alpha\neq-\kappa_{i}

for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
.
We prove that any transposition of adjacent steps in
[\alpha;\kappa_{1},\dots,\kappa_{n};\beta]

results in another reduced path, from which the desired result follows. To that end, we take some
1\leqslant i<n
and
consider swapping the positions of
\kappa_{i}
and
\kappa_{i+1}
. The resulting sequence of partial sums is the
same as that of the original path, except
\alpha+\kappa_{1}+\dots+\kappa_{i-1}+\kappa_{i}
is replaced
by
\alpha+\kappa_{1}+\dots+\kappa_{i-1}+\kappa_{i+1}
. We therefore need only to show that this is a root.
To that end, we apply Proposition
3.1
to the expression

(\alpha+\kappa_{1}+\dots+\kappa_{i-1})+\kappa_{i}+\kappa_{i+1}
to conclude
that
\alpha+\kappa_{1}+\dots+\kappa_{i-1}+\kappa_{i+1}\in R
provided

\alpha+\kappa_{1}+\dots+\kappa_{i-1}+\kappa_{i}\neq 0
. If
i=1
, this is guaranteed by the condition
that
\alpha\neq-\kappa_{j}
for any
1\leqslant j\leqslant n
. If
i>1
, then we would have a non-trivial sum of steps in a
reduced path equal to
-\alpha
, which is impossible.
We therefore conclude that

[\alpha;\kappa_{1},\dots,\kappa_{i-1},\kappa_{i+1},\kappa_{i},\kappa_{i+2},\dots,\kappa_{n};\beta]

is another reduced path.
∎

Example 3.9
.

In the root system
\mathbb{D}_{5}
we have the following equation

010^{\displaystyle 0}_{\displaystyle 0}-000^{\displaystyle 0}_{\displaystyle 1}+001^{\displaystyle 0}_{\displaystyle 1}+001^{\displaystyle 1}_{\displaystyle 1}+100^{\displaystyle 0}_{\displaystyle 0}=112^{\displaystyle 1}_{\displaystyle 1}\ .

(Here roots have been identified with their coefficients as in
Remark
2.3
.) The equation above yields the path

\left[010^{\displaystyle 0}_{\displaystyle 0};100^{\displaystyle 0}_{\displaystyle 0},001^{\displaystyle 1}_{\displaystyle 1},-000^{\displaystyle 0}_{\displaystyle 1},001^{\displaystyle 0}_{\displaystyle 1};112^{\displaystyle 1}_{\displaystyle 1}\right]\ ,

which is not reduced but gives rise to the reduced path

\left[010^{\displaystyle 0}_{\displaystyle 0};100^{\displaystyle 0}_{\displaystyle 0},001^{\displaystyle 1}_{\displaystyle 0},001^{\displaystyle 0}_{\displaystyle 1};112^{\displaystyle 1}_{\displaystyle 1}\right]\ .

To verify Proposition
3.8
, we
check that each permutation of the steps in the reduced path
above properly defines a path.

\begin{array}[]{ccc}\left[010^{\displaystyle 0}_{\displaystyle 0};100^{\displaystyle 0}_{\displaystyle 0},001^{\displaystyle 1}_{\displaystyle 0},001^{\displaystyle 0}_{\displaystyle 1};112^{\displaystyle 1}_{\displaystyle 1}\right]&\left[010^{\displaystyle 0}_{\displaystyle 0};100^{\displaystyle 0}_{\displaystyle 0},001^{\displaystyle 0}_{\displaystyle 1},001^{\displaystyle 1}_{\displaystyle 0};112^{\displaystyle 1}_{\displaystyle 1}\right]&\left[010^{\displaystyle 0}_{\displaystyle 0};001^{\displaystyle 1}_{\displaystyle 0},100^{\displaystyle 0}_{\displaystyle 0},001^{\displaystyle 0}_{\displaystyle 1};112^{\displaystyle 1}_{\displaystyle 1}\right]\\[8.0pt]
\left[010^{\displaystyle 0}_{\displaystyle 0};001^{\displaystyle 1}_{\displaystyle 0},001^{\displaystyle 0}_{\displaystyle 1},100^{\displaystyle 0}_{\displaystyle 0};112^{\displaystyle 1}_{\displaystyle 1}\right]&\left[010^{\displaystyle 0}_{\displaystyle 0};001^{\displaystyle 0}_{\displaystyle 1},100^{\displaystyle 0}_{\displaystyle 0},001^{\displaystyle 1}_{\displaystyle 0};112^{\displaystyle 1}_{\displaystyle 1}\right]&\left[010^{\displaystyle 0}_{\displaystyle 0};001^{\displaystyle 0}_{\displaystyle 1},001^{\displaystyle 1}_{\displaystyle 0},100^{\displaystyle 0}_{\displaystyle 0};112^{\displaystyle 1}_{\displaystyle 1}\right]\end{array}

It is not too difficult to see that every partial sum along each of these paths is a root in

.

3.4.
An application

The following proposition will be useful in Section
7
.

Proposition 3.10
.

Let
\Phi
be a co-closed subset (for instance, an inversion set) of
R^{+}
. Then

\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{Z}}\Phi=\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{Z}}\operatorname{supp}\Phi\ .

Proof.

It follows from the definition of
\operatorname{supp}\Phi
that
\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{Z}}\Phi\subseteq\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{Z}}\operatorname{supp}\Phi
.
To demonstrate the reverse inclusion, we take
\theta\in\operatorname{supp}\Phi
and show that it is in
\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{Z}}\Phi
.
This is obvious if
\theta\in\Phi
, so we consider the case where
\theta\notin\Phi
. Since
\theta\in\operatorname{supp}\Phi
,
there is some
\alpha\in\Phi
such that
\theta<\alpha
. We may therefore, appealing to
Proposition
3.5
, form a path from
\theta
to
\alpha
with positive simple roots as steps.
By Remark
3.7
(ii), we may replace
this path by the reduced path
[\theta;\kappa_{1},\dots,\kappa_{n};\alpha]

with positive steps. Using Proposition
3.8
, we choose an
ordering of the
\kappa_{i}
’s with those steps in
\Phi
preceding those in
\Phi^{c}
:

[\theta;\underbrace{\kappa_{1},\dots,\kappa_{m}}_{\kappa_{i}\in\Phi},\underbrace{\kappa_{m+1},\dots,\kappa_{n}}_{\kappa_{i}\in\Phi^{c}};\alpha]\ .

Co-closure implies that the partial sums
\alpha-\kappa_{n}
,
\alpha-\kappa_{n}-\kappa_{n-1}

down to
\theta+\kappa_{1}+\dots+\kappa_{m}
are all in
\Phi
,
from which it follows that
\theta\in\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{Z}}\Phi
.
∎

4.
Inflation on subsets of positive roots

In this section, we introduce the notion of inflation in a QRS. The notion of
inflation was first studied in the context of the symmetric group (see for example
[
AAK
]
),
by giving a natural way to decompose every inversion set in a unique way, and in particular
giving rise to some elements that cannot be decomposed non-trivially (that we will call primitive,
although other authors have used the term simple). On the one hand, the notion of inflation that we
present generalizes the corresponding notion for the symmetric group. On the other hand,
inflation will allow us to better understand inversion sets and their decompositions by providing some inductive methods
to study QRSs.

Throughout this section
\Phi
denotes a subset of
R^{+}
and,
for a given
I\subseteq S
,
\Psi
denotes a subset of
(R/I)^{+}
.

4.1.
Definition and basic properties

Definition 4.1
.

Let
I\subseteq S
and
\Phi\subseteq R^{+}
. We say that
\Phi
is an
inflation
from
I

of
\Psi\subseteq(R/I)^{+}
by
X\subseteq R_{I}^{+}
if

\Phi
consists of all preimages under the canonical projection
\pi_{I}:V\to V/\mathrm{span\,}I

of the elements of
\Psi
along with the elements of
X
.
Formally,

\Phi=(\pi_{I}^{-1}(\Psi)\cap R)\cup X\ .

We write
\Phi=\mathrm{inf}_{I}^{S}(\Psi,X)
.

Remark 4.2
.

Note that Definition
4.1
is meaningful regardless of whether
R
is assumed to be reducible or
irreducible. If
R
is reducible, write
R=R^{1}\times\cdots\times R^{s}
where each
R^{i}
is irreducible with base
S^{i}
.
We can write
I=I^{1}\cup\cdots\cup I^{s}
with
I^{i}\subseteq S^{i}
. Observe that
R/I
can be identified with

R^{1}/I^{1}\times\cdots\times R^{s}/I^{s}
. For any
\Phi\subseteq R^{+}
, we let
\Phi^{i}=\Phi\cap R^{i}
and for

\Psi\subseteq(R/I)^{+}
, we let
\Psi^{i}=\Psi\cap R^{i}/I^{i}
. Note that
\Phi^{i}\subseteq(R^{i})^{+}
and

\Psi^{i}\subseteq(R^{i}/I^{i})^{+}
.

Then for
\Phi\subseteq R^{+}
, we have that
\Phi=\mathrm{inf}_{I}^{S}(\Psi,X)
if and only if

\Phi^{i}=\mathrm{inf}_{I^{i}}^{S^{i}}(\Psi^{i},X^{i})
, where
X^{i}=X\cap R_{I^{i}}
.
This justifies restricting our attention to irreducible QRSs.

Remark 4.3
.

We will often use the following simple properties of inflation without explicit reference.

(i)

\mathrm{inf}_{I}^{S}(\Psi,X)
consists of all positive roots in
R
which project to
roots in
\Psi
along with the elements of
X
(which are some of the positive roots that project to 0).

(ii)

Every
\Phi\subseteq R^{+}
is an inflation
\Phi=\mathrm{inf}_{S}^{S}(\emptyset,\Phi)
as
well as
\Phi=\mathrm{inf}_{\emptyset}^{S}(\Phi,\emptyset)
.

(iii)

4.2.
Canonical form

The aim of this subsection is to provide a canonical way to describe a subset of
R^{+}
as an inflation.
Recalling from Remark
4.2
that any inflation in a reducible system

R
can be viewed naturally as a disjoint union of inflations within the irreducible components of
R
,
we persist with the global assumption that
R
is irreducible.

Definition 4.10
.

Let
R
be an irreducible QRS.
A subset
\Phi\subseteq R^{+}
is
primitive
if
\Phi\neq\emptyset,\Phi\neq R^{+}
, and
\mathrm{Gen}(\Phi)=\{\emptyset,S\}
.

Remark 4.11
.

(i)

If
\Phi
is primitive, then
\Phi=\mathrm{inf}_{\emptyset}^{S}(\Phi,\emptyset)

and
\Phi=\mathrm{inf}_{S}^{S}(\emptyset,\Phi)
are the only presentations of
\Phi
as an inflation.

(ii)

\Phi
is primitive if and only if
\Phi^{c}
is primitive.

(iii)

If
R
is reducible, then every subset
\Phi\subseteq R^{+}
can be written as some non-trivial inflation
because every set is an inflation with respect to any quotient that sends an entire
irreducible component to
0
. Hence the obvious
extension of Definition
4.10
to subsets of reducible
QRSs would yield no primitive sets.

Theorem 4.12
.

Let
R
be an irreducible QRS, and let
\Phi\subseteq R^{+}
. Then
\Phi=\mathrm{inf}_{I}^{S}(\Psi,X)
where
I\subsetneq S

and one of the following mutually exclusive alternatives holds:

(i)

\Psi
is primitive

(ii)

\Psi=\emptyset
or
\Psi=(R/I)^{+}
.

Moreover, the set
I
is uniquely determined in case (i) and in case (ii) there is a minimum such
I
.

Definition 4.13
.

Following Theorem
4.12
, we call the expression
\Phi=\mathrm{inf}_{I}^{S}(\Psi,X)
where either

(i)

\Psi
is primitive

or

(ii)

\Psi=\emptyset
or
(R/I)^{+}
and
I
is the minimal
subset of
S
with this property

the
canonical form of
\Phi
. In this case, we also say that
\Phi
is
canonically inflated from

I
.

Note that in
[
D-W
]
, the notion of a “simple form of a permutation” was studied.
The canonical form of an inversion set in type
\mathbb{A}
is the counterpart of the
simple form of the corresponding permutation.

Before proving the theorem, we establish the following useful result.

Lemma 4.14
.

Assume that
I\cap J=\emptyset
and
I\cup J=S
.
Define

I^{\prime}:=I\cap J^{\perp}=\{\theta\in I\,|\,\theta\perp\lambda\ \forall\lambda\in J\}\quad{\text{and}}\quad J^{\prime}:=J\cap I^{\perp}=\{\theta\in J\,|\,\theta\perp\lambda\ \forall\lambda\in I\}\ .

If
\Phi=\mathrm{inf}_{I}^{S}(\Psi,X)=\mathrm{inf}_{J}^{S}(\Theta,Y)
, then
\Phi=\mathrm{inf}_{I^{\prime}\cup J^{\prime}}^{S}(\Xi,Z)
,
where
\Xi=\emptyset
or
\Xi=(R/(I^{\prime}\cup J^{\prime}))^{+}
.

Proof.

For
\alpha,\beta\in R^{+}
and
\Phi\subseteq R^{+}
we write
\alpha\sim_{\Phi}\beta
if either
both
\alpha,\beta\in\Phi
or both
\alpha,\beta\notin\Phi
.
Let
\tau
denote the highest root of
R^{+}
.

Since
I^{\prime}\subseteq I
and
J^{\prime}\subseteq I^{\perp}
, we have
R_{I^{\prime}\cup J^{\prime}}=R_{I^{\prime}}\times R_{J^{\prime}}
.
Noting that
\tau\not\in R_{I^{\prime}\cup J^{\prime}}
(unless
I=\emptyset
or
J=\emptyset
when there is nothing to prove),
we conclude that the statement of the lemma is equivalent to proving that
\gamma\sim_{\Phi}\tau
for
every
\gamma\in R^{+}\backslash(R_{I^{\prime}}\cup R_{J^{\prime}})
. To prove this we use the fact that

R^{+}\backslash(R_{I^{\prime}}\cup R_{J^{\prime}})=(R^{+}\backslash(R_{I}\cup R_{J}))\cup((R_{I}\cup R_{J})\backslash(R_{I^{\prime}}\cup R_{J^{\prime}}))\ ,

and consider two cases:

(i)

\gamma\in R^{+}\backslash(R_{I}\cup R_{J})
.

In this case there is a path
[\gamma;\kappa_{1},\dots,\kappa_{n};\tau]
with
\kappa_{i}\in S=I\cup J
.
Since
\gamma\not\in R_{I}\cup R_{J}
, we see that
\gamma+\kappa_{1}+\dots+\kappa_{i}\not\in R_{I}\cup R_{J}

for every
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
. Moreover, for
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
,

\gamma+\kappa_{1}+\dots+\kappa_{i-1}\,\sim_{\Phi}\gamma+\kappa_{1}+\dots+\kappa_{i-1}+\kappa_{i}

because
\Phi
is inflated both from
I
and
J
and
S=I\cup J
. Using that
\sim_{\Phi}
is an equivalence
relation, we conclude that
\gamma\sim_{\Phi}\tau
.

(ii)

\gamma\in(R_{I}\cup R_{J})\backslash(R_{I^{\prime}}\cup R_{J^{\prime}})
.

Without loss of generality we may assume that
\gamma\in R_{I}\backslash R_{I^{\prime}}
.
Then
\operatorname{supp}\gamma
is contained in
I
but not in
I^{\prime}
.
The definition of
I^{\prime}
implies that there are
\beta\in J
and
\theta\in\operatorname{supp}\gamma
such
that
\langle\beta,\theta\rangle\neq 0
. Hence
\langle\beta,\theta\rangle<0
and,
moreover,
\langle\gamma,\beta\rangle<0
. Thus
\gamma+\beta\in R^{+}
and since

\pi_{J}(\gamma)=\pi_{J}(\gamma+\beta)\neq 0
, we conclude that

\gamma\sim_{\Phi}\gamma+\beta
. Noting that
\gamma+\beta\not\in R_{I}\cup R_{J}

and using case (i), we conclude that
\gamma+\beta\sim_{\Phi}\tau
. Combining the two
equivalences, we arrive at
\gamma\sim_{\Phi}\tau
which completes the proof. ∎

If

We next describe the set

Proposition 4.18
.

Let
\Phi=\mathrm{inf}_{I}^{S}(\Psi,X)
be the canonical form of
\Phi\subseteq R^{+}
where
\Psi
is primitive.
Then
\mathrm{Gen}(\Phi)=\mathrm{Gen}(X)\cup\{S\}
.

Proof.

First we prove that
\mathrm{Gen}(\Phi)\backslash\{S\}
contains a unique maximal element.
Assume, by way of contradiction,
that
\mathrm{Gen}(\Phi)\backslash\{S\}
contains two distinct maximal elements
K
and
L
.
Then
K\cap L
and
K\cup L
are both elements
of
\mathrm{Gen}(\Phi)
by Proposition
4.9
and

by the maximality of

and

. Write

\Phi=\mathrm{inf}_{K}^{S}(\Psi^{\prime},X^{\prime})=\mathrm{inf}_{L}^{S}(\Psi^{\prime\prime},X^{\prime\prime})=\mathrm{inf}_{K\cap L}^{S}(\Gamma,Y)\ .

We define
K^{\prime\prime}:=K/(K\cap L)
and

and

. Then

and

4.6
, there exist

\alpha

4.4.
Inflations and inversion sets

Proposition 4.19
.

Let
\Phi=\mathrm{inf}_{I}^{S}(\Psi,X)
. Then
\Phi
is an inversion set if and only if
both
\Psi
and
X
are inversion sets.

Proof.

Assume that
\Psi,X
are inversion sets.
To prove that
\Phi
is closed,
take roots
\alpha,\beta\in\Phi
with

\alpha+\beta\in R
and show that

\alpha+\beta\in\Phi
. If
\alpha,\beta\in X
,
then
\alpha+\beta\in X\subseteq\Phi
by closure of
X
. Assume that
\alpha\in X

and
\pi_{I}(\beta)\in\Psi
. Then
\pi_{I}(\alpha+\beta)=\pi_{I}(\beta)\in\Psi
.
Hence,
\alpha+\beta\in\Phi
. Finally, if
both
\pi_{I}(\alpha),\pi_{I}(\beta)\in\Psi
, then closure of
\Psi

implies that
\pi_{I}(\alpha+\beta)=\pi_{I}(\alpha)+\pi_{I}(\beta)\in\Psi
.
Hence,
\alpha+\beta\in\Phi
. This proves that
\Phi
is closed. The fact that
\Phi
is co-closed follows from
the identity

\Phi^{c}=\mathrm{inf}_{I}^{S}(\Psi^{c},X^{c})
and the proof above.

Conversely, assume that
\Phi
is an inversion set. We first prove that
X
is also. The
closure property of
X
follows from the closure property of
\Phi
and that

X=\Phi\cap{\rm span}(I)
. For the co-closure, assume that
\alpha,\beta\in X^{c}

and
\alpha+\beta
is a root. If
\alpha,\beta\in\Phi^{c}
, then the co-closure of
\Phi

implies that
\alpha+\beta\in X^{c}
. But if
\alpha\in\pi_{I}^{-1}(\Psi)
,
then
\alpha+\beta\not\in{\rm span}(I)
so
\alpha+\beta\in X^{c}
. Hence,

X
is also co-closed and thus an inversion set. It remains to prove that
\Psi
is an
inversion set. For the closure, let
\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta}\in\Psi
with

\bar{\gamma}:=\bar{\alpha}+\bar{\beta}
a root. It follows from
Proposition
2.6
that for a given

\gamma\in\pi_{I}^{-1}(\bar{\gamma})
, there are
\alpha\in\pi_{I}^{-1}(\bar{\alpha})
and

\beta\in\pi_{I}^{-1}(\bar{\beta})
with
\gamma=\alpha+\beta
. If
\bar{\gamma}\not\in\Psi
, then

\gamma\in\Phi^{c}
. As
\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta}\in\Psi
, we have that
\alpha,\beta\in\Phi
.
The closure property of
\Phi
implies that
\gamma\in\Phi
, which is a contradiction. Similarly,
for the co-closure, if
\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta}\in\Psi^{c}
with
\bar{\alpha}+\bar{\beta}
a root
in
\Psi
, then we get an expression
\gamma=\alpha+\beta
of three roots with

\alpha,\beta\in\Phi^{c}
and
\gamma\in\Phi
, which contradicts the co-closure of
\Phi
.
Thus,
\Psi
is an inversion set.
∎

We complete this section with a description of
\mathrm{Gen}(\Phi)
different from the one in Proposition
4.18

in the case when
\Phi
is an inversion set. Before stating the result, we introduce a notion related to a positive system
P
in
R
,
see Remark
2.9
. Let

be the base of

.
A subspace

is
a

-Levi subspace
if it is spanned by a subset of

.

Proposition 4.20
.

Let
P
be a positive system in

and let
\Phi:=R^{+}\cap P
be the corresponding inversion set.
Then
I\in\mathrm{Gen}(\Phi)
if and only if
W:=\mathrm{span\,}I
is a
P
-Levi subspace of
E
.

Proof.

Assume first that
W
is a
P
-Levi subspace of
E
. To prove that
I\in\mathrm{Gen}(\Phi)
, we need to show that,
for any
\beta,\gamma\in R^{+}\backslash W
such that
\beta-\gamma\in W
, either both
\beta
and
\gamma

belong to
\Phi
or both belong to
\Phi^{c}
. Since
\Phi=R^{+}\cap P
, this is equivalent to saying that

\beta
and
\gamma
are on the same side of a hyperplane defining
P
.
Consider the hyperplane
\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}:=\ker\varphi_{\varepsilon}
,
where
\varphi_{\varepsilon}:E\to\mathbb{R}
is defined by its values on the simple roots of
P
as follows:

\varphi_{\varepsilon}(\theta)=\varepsilon
for
\theta\in W

and
\varphi_{\varepsilon}(\theta)=1
if
\theta\not\in W
. The hyperplane
\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}
determines
P
.
Since
W
is
P
-Levi, there is
c>0
such that
\varphi_{\varepsilon}(\alpha)\leqslant c\varepsilon
if
\alpha\in R\cap W
and

\varphi_{\varepsilon}(\alpha)\geqslant 1
for
\alpha\in P\backslash W
. Thus,
for small enough positive values of
\varepsilon
,
any two roots
\beta,\gamma\in R\backslash W
with
\beta-\gamma\in W

lie on the same side of
\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}
. This completes the argument that
I\in\mathrm{Gen}(\Phi)
.

Next we assume that
I\in\mathrm{Gen}(\Phi)
but
W
is not a
P
-Levi subspace of
E
. Label the
roots in the base
S^{\prime}
of
P
as
\theta_{1}^{\prime},\dots,\theta_{n}^{\prime}
so that
\theta_{1}^{\prime},\dots,\theta_{k}^{\prime}\in W

and
\theta_{k+1}^{\prime},\dots,\theta_{n}^{\prime}\not\in W
. The assumption that
W
is not a
P
-Levi subspace of
E

implies that
\theta_{1}^{\prime},\dots,\theta_{k}^{\prime}
do not span
W
. Then there is
w^{\prime}\in P\cap W
such that

w^{\prime}\not\in\mathrm{span\,}\{\theta_{1}^{\prime},\dots,\theta_{k}^{\prime}\}
. (For example we can take
w^{\prime}=\pm\theta
where
\theta

is a simple root of
R^{+}
not contained in
\mathrm{span\,}\{\theta_{1}^{\prime},\dots,\theta_{k}^{\prime}\}
.) Climbing down from
w^{\prime}
towards
0

with steps among
\theta_{1}^{\prime},\dots,\theta_{k}^{\prime}
, we can replace
w^{\prime}
with
w
which satisfies the following conditions:

w\in W\cap P
and
w=\beta+\gamma
, where
\beta,\gamma\in P\backslash W
.

Assume further that
w\in R^{+}
. Then one of
\beta
and
\gamma
is in
R^{+}
and the other one is in
R^{-}
. Indeed,
they cannot both be in
R^{-}
because
w\in R^{+}
. If both
\beta
and
\gamma
are in
R^{+}
, since
\beta+\gamma\in W=\mathrm{span\,}I
,
we would conclude that
\beta,\gamma\in W
which contradict the way they were chosen. Say,
\beta\in R^{+}
and
\gamma\in R^{-}
.
Then
\beta,-\gamma\in R^{+}\backslash W
,
\beta\in\Phi
, and
\beta-(-\gamma)=w\in W
imply that
-\gamma\in\Phi
.
The last then implies that
-\gamma\in P
which contradicts the assumption that
\gamma\in P
. This completes the proof
in the case when
w\in R^{+}
. The case when
w\in R^{-}
is dealt in the same way.
∎

5.
The graph
G(\Phi)

For the rest of the paper, the symbol
\Phi
will denote an inversion set of
R
, and in particular, a
subset of
R^{+}
. We recall that by
\Phi^{c}
we mean the complement of
\Phi
in
R^{+}
.

While Section
3
deals with the existence and properties of arbitrary paths between roots in
R
,
when discussing an inversion set
\Phi
, it is often useful to focus on paths
whose partial sums belong to
\Phi
and steps belong to
\pm\Phi^{c}
. To that end,
we introduce the graph
G(\Phi)
and study a partial order on its connected components,
which resembles the partial order on
R^{+}
.
In fact, if
\Phi=R^{+}
, then each component of
G(\Phi)
consists of a single
element of
\Phi
and thus
{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
is identified with
R^{+}
and the partial orders on these
two sets are the same.

5.1.
Definition of
G(\Phi)

Definition 5.1
.

Let
\Phi\subseteq R^{+}
be an inversion set. We define
G(\Phi)
to be the graph whose vertices are the elements of
\Phi
,
where two vertices
\alpha
and
\alpha^{\prime}
are connected by an edge if
\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}\in\pm\Phi^{c}
.

Note that in this definition we allow for
R
to be reducible. It is not difficult to see that if

R=R^{1}\times\dots\times R^{s}

is a reducible QRS
and
\Phi\subseteq R^{+}
, then
\Phi=\Phi^{1}\sqcup\dots\sqcup\Phi^{s}
, where
\Phi^{i}:=\Phi\cap(R^{i})^{+}

and
G(\Phi)
is the union of
G(\Phi^{1}),\dots,G(\Phi^{s})
in the sense that its sets of vertices and edges are the
(disjoint) unions of the respective sets of vertices and edges of
G(\Phi^{1}),\dots,G(\Phi^{s})
.

We will be mainly interested in the connected components of the graph
G(\Phi)
as opposed to
the graph itself. We denote by
{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
the set of connected components
of
G(\Phi)
. In the case when
\Phi=R^{+}
, the components are the singleton sets, each containing
a single positive root.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
\Phi
be an inversion set and let
C\in{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
.
If
\Phi=\Phi_{1}\sqcup\Phi_{2}
, then
C\subseteq\Phi_{1}
or
C\subseteq\Phi_{2}
.
In particular, if
\Phi=\mathrm{inf}_{I}^{S}(\Psi,X)
, then
C\subseteq\mathrm{inf}_{I}^{S}(\Psi,\emptyset)
or
C\subseteq X
.

Proof.

Suppose to the contrary that neither
C\cap\Phi_{1}
nor
C\cap\Phi_{2}
is empty. By definition
we would have
\alpha+\kappa=\beta
for some
\alpha\in\Phi_{1},\beta\in\Phi_{2},

and
\kappa\in\pm\Phi^{c}
. By symmetry, we may assume that
\kappa\in R^{+}
.
Then
\alpha,\kappa\in\Phi_{2}^{c}
, which
contradicts the co-closure of
\Phi_{2}
.
∎

When we have expressed an inversion set
\Phi=\mathrm{inf}_{I}^{S}(\Psi,X)
as an inflation,
we usually will denote components contained in
X
by
X_{1},X_{2},\dots
and
components outside of
X
by

Z_{1},Z_{2},\dots
.

5.2.
Partial order of components.

Proposition
5.2
suggests that when studying
decompositions of an inversion set
\Phi
, the relevant objects to examine are not arbitrary
subsets of
\Phi
, but rather collections of components of
G(\Phi)
. For that reason,
understanding the structure of the set
{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
is the focus of the remainder of this and the following section.

We begin by defining a partial order on the set
{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
. First, we need the
following lemma, which will also be useful for defining component addition in Section
6
.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
\Phi\subseteq R^{+}
and
A,B,C\in{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
with
\alpha\in A,\beta\in B
, and
\alpha+\beta\in C
.

(i)

If
A\neq C
, then for all
\alpha^{\prime}\in A
there exists
\beta^{\prime}\in B
such that
\alpha^{\prime}+\beta^{\prime}\in C
.

(ii)

If
A\neq C
and
B\neq C
, then for all
\gamma^{\prime}\in C
,
there exist
\alpha^{\prime}\in A
and
\beta^{\prime}\in B
such that
\gamma^{\prime}=\alpha^{\prime}+\beta^{\prime}
.

Proof.

First we prove (i). It suffices to verify (i) for every neighbour
\alpha^{\prime}\in A
of
\alpha
. Write

\alpha^{\prime}=\alpha-\kappa
where
\kappa\in\pm\Phi^{c}
.
Then
\alpha^{\prime}+\kappa+\beta=\alpha+\beta
.
Since the right hand side is a root, either
\alpha^{\prime}+\beta\in R
or

\beta+\kappa\in R
by Proposition
3.1
.
If
\alpha^{\prime}+\beta
is a root then it lies in
\Phi
by the closure of

\Phi
. Since
(\alpha^{\prime}+\beta)-(\alpha+\beta)=-\kappa\in\pm\Phi^{c}

we see that
\alpha^{\prime}+\beta\in C
verifying (i).
In the second case, suppose that
\beta+\kappa\in R
.
If
\beta+\kappa\in\pm\Phi^{c}
then
(\alpha+\beta)-\alpha^{\prime}\in\pm\Phi^{c}

and thus
C=A
.
This contradiction shows that
\beta+\kappa\notin\pm\Phi^{c}
.
If
\beta+\kappa\in-\Phi
then

-\kappa=\beta+(-\beta-\kappa)\in\Phi
.
This contradiction shows that
\beta+\kappa\notin-\Phi
.
Therefore
\beta+\kappa\in\Phi
and thus
\beta+\kappa\in B
.
Then
\alpha^{\prime}+(\beta+\kappa)=\alpha+\beta\in C
again verifying (i).

To prove (ii), it suffices show that (ii) holds for every neighbour

\gamma^{\prime}\in C
of
\gamma:=\alpha+\beta
.
Write
\gamma^{\prime}=\gamma+\kappa
where

\kappa\in\pm\Phi^{c}
. Consider the equation

\alpha+\beta+\kappa=\gamma^{\prime}
. Since the right hand side is a root either

\alpha+\kappa\in R
or
\beta+\kappa\in R
again by Proposition
3.1
. Without loss of generality we
may suppose that
\alpha+\kappa\in R
.
If
\alpha+\kappa\in\pm\Phi^{c}
then

\gamma^{\prime}-\beta=\alpha+\kappa\in\pm\Phi^{c}

and thus
C=B
.
This contradiction shows that
\alpha+\kappa\notin\pm\Phi^{c}
.
If
\alpha+\kappa\in-\Phi
then

-\kappa=\alpha+(-\alpha-\kappa)\in\Phi
.
This contradiction shows that
\alpha+\kappa\notin-\Phi
.
Therefore
\alpha+\kappa\in\Phi
and thus
\alpha+\kappa\in A
.
Then
(\alpha+\kappa)+\beta=\gamma^{\prime}
as required.
∎

Remark 5.5
.

Note that in Lemma
5.4
, the assumptions that
A\neq C
in part (i) and

A\neq C
,
B\neq C
in part (ii) are necessary. For instance, in
Example
5.3
(i), if we take
A=Z_{1},B=X_{1},C=Z_{1}
,
then we can find roots
\alpha,\beta
with
\alpha\in A,\beta\in B
and
\alpha+\beta\in C
.
However, taking
\alpha^{\prime}
maximal in
A
, there is no
\beta^{\prime}\in B
with
\alpha^{\prime}+\beta^{\prime}\in C
.
Similarly, in the same example, if we take
\gamma^{\prime}
minimal in
C
then it is not possible
to write
\gamma^{\prime}=\alpha^{\prime}+\beta^{\prime}
with
\alpha^{\prime}\in A
and
\beta^{\prime}\in B
.

Next we introduce a partial order on
{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
inherited from the partial order on
R^{+}
from
Definition
2.2
.

Definition 5.6
.

Let
A,B\in{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
. We write
A\leqslant B
if there exist
\alpha\in A
,
\beta\in B

with
\alpha\leqslant\beta
. As usual,
A<B
means
A\leqslant B
and
A\neq B
.

Proposition 5.7
.

Let
A
and
B
be two distinct elements of
{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
.
Then the following conditions are all equivalent:

(i)

A<B
.

(ii)

there exist components
A=C_{0},C_{1},\dots,C_{m}=B
and a sequence
\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{m}
with

\gamma_{i}\in C_{i}
for all
i
and
\gamma_{i+1}-\gamma_{i}\in R^{+}
.

(iii)

there exist components

A=D_{0}<D_{1}<\dots<D_{n}=B
and for every
\alpha\in A
, a sequence

\alpha=\delta_{0},\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{n}
with

\delta_{i}\in D_{i}
for all
i
, and

\delta_{i+1}-\delta_{i}\in\Phi
.

(iv)

for every
\alpha\in A
there exists
\beta\in B
with

\alpha<\beta
.

Proof.

Assume
A<B
, i.e.,
A\neq B
and there exists

\alpha\in A
and
\beta\in B
with
\alpha<\beta
. We will show (ii).
Then,
\alpha+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\theta_{i}=\beta
for
\theta_{i}\in S
.
We then form a reduced path
[\alpha;\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2},\dots,\kappa_{m};\beta]

from
\alpha
to
\beta
with
\kappa_{i}>0
. By
Proposition
3.8
,
we may rearrange this path so that the
\kappa_{i}
in
\Phi
come first and those
in
\Phi^{c}
after. Write
\gamma_{i}=\alpha+\kappa_{1}+\dots\kappa_{i}

for
1\leq i\leq m
, and define
\gamma_{0}=\alpha
. We assume that

\kappa_{1},\dots,\kappa_{s}\in\Phi
and
\kappa_{s+1},\dots,\kappa_{m}\not\in\Phi

where
0\leqslant s\leqslant m
. Our convention is that when
s=0
, no
\kappa_{i}
lie in
\Phi

and when
s=m
, no
\kappa_{i}
lie in
\Phi^{c}
.

We claim that all
\gamma_{i}
are in
\Phi
.
If
\gamma_{i}\in\Phi
and
\kappa_{i+1}\in\Phi
, then
\gamma_{i+1}\in\Phi
by closure.
Using that
\alpha=\gamma_{0}\in\Phi
, this shows that
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{s}\in\Phi
.
Now, assume that for some
t>s
, we have that
\gamma_{t}\not\in\Phi
. By co-closure,
that implies that
\gamma_{j}\not\in\Phi
for all
j\geqslant t
. In particular,

\beta=\gamma_{m}\not\in\Phi
, which is a contradiction. This proves our claim.

Thus, define
C_{i}
to be the component containing
\gamma_{i}
.
Since
\gamma_{i+1}-\gamma_{i}=\kappa_{i+1}>0
for all
i
, this sequence
satisfies the desired conditions. This completes the proof of (ii).

Now assume that (ii) is true. We will show that (iii) follows.
Let
D_{0}=C_{0}=A
, then set
D_{1}
to be the next
C_{i}
in the sequence which is different
from
D_{0}
, carrying on in this manner gives a sequence
A=D_{0}<D_{1}<\dots<D_{n}=B
of components.
Take
\alpha\in A
, and set
\delta_{0}=\alpha\in D_{0}
.
Now suppose we have a sequence of
\delta_{i}\in D_{i}
which satisfy the result for all
i\leqslant k
.
By the way we defined
D_{k+1}
, there is a
C_{i}=D_{k}
, such that
C_{i+1}=D_{k+1}
, thus,
\gamma_{i+1}-\gamma_{i}\in R^{+}
.
Since the components
D_{k}
and
D_{k+1}
are distinct,
\gamma_{i+1}-\gamma_{i}
cannot be in
\Phi^{c}
,
thus, we can use Lemma
5.4
(i) to
find a
\rho_{k+1}\in\Phi
such that
\delta_{k+1}:=\delta_{k}+\rho_{k+1}\in D_{k+1}
.
This proves (iii).

Now, assume that (iii) holds. Let
\alpha\in A
. Statement (iii) implies the existence of a
sequence
\alpha=\delta_{0},\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{n}
such that
\delta_{n}\in B

and
\delta_{i}<\delta_{i+1}
for all
i
. In particular,
\alpha=\delta_{0}<\dots<\delta_{n}
,
which proves (iv). Clearly, (iv) implies (i).
∎

As a consequence of the equivalence of (i) and (iv) in Proposition
5.7
, we have the following.

Corollary 5.8
.

Let
A,B\in{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
with
A\leqslant B
. Then
\operatorname{supp}A\subseteq\operatorname{supp}B
. ∎

We are now ready to prove that the relation
\leqslant
is a partial order.

Proposition 5.9
.

The relation
\leqslant
is a partial order on the set
{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
.

Proof.

Reflexivity is clear. Transitivity follows from part (iv) of
Proposition
5.7
. To see antisymmetry,
assume by way of contradiction that
A<B
and
B<A
. Let
\alpha\in A
be a
maximal root in
A
. Then, by part (iv) of Proposition
5.7
,
there exists
\beta\in B
satisfying
\alpha<\beta
and some
\alpha^{\prime}\in A

satisfying
\beta<\alpha^{\prime}
. Together these imply that
\alpha<\alpha^{\prime}
, contradicting maximality of
\alpha
.
∎

6.
Addition of components

In this section we introduce a partial addition of elements
of
{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
which is compatible with the partial order discussed above
and exhibits many properties similar to the properties of addition in a QRS
R
.
When
{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}=R^{+}
, i.e., when
\Phi=R^{+}
,
the partial addition on
{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
coincides with the (partial) addition on
R^{+}
.

There are, however, some substantial differences between addition in
R
and partial addition in
{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
.
If
R
is a QRS, then addition in
R
is the restriction of the addition in the “root lattice of
R
”, i.e.,
the lattice generated by
R
. As a consequence, for any
\alpha,\beta\in R
,
\alpha+\beta
is a well-defined
element of the aforementioned lattice, which may or may not belong to
R
. Since addition in the root lattice is
commutative and associative and
R=-R
, addition in
R
is commutative and associative and we only need to worry whether
the elements involved in an identity like
(\alpha+\beta)+\gamma=\alpha+(\beta+\gamma)
belong to
R
or not.
In contrast, the partial
addition in
{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
does not extend naturally to addition in a lattice generated by
{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
. As a consequence,
we need to study carefully properties such as commutativity, associativity, cancellation, etc. for addition in
{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
.

By the very definition, the partial addition is commutative:
A+B
is defined if and only if
B+A
is defined and in
that case they are equal. Associativity is more delicate:
the existence of
A+B
and
(A+B)+C
does not imply the existence of
(A+C)+B
. Nevertheless, we show that
if
A+B
and
(A+B)+C
exist, then at least one of
(A+C)+B
or
(B+C)+A
exists and equals
(A+B)+C
.
This property is the “component” analog of the two-out-of-three rule for addition in a QRS.
Similarly, cancellation rules do not apply to the partial addition in
{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
, i.e.,
the equality
A+B=A+B^{\prime}
does not necessarily imply
B=B^{\prime}
. However, the cancellation rule holds
under the condition that
A+B=A+B^{\prime}\neq A
, see Proposition
6.16
.

Apart from discussing the questions stated above, we introduce the notion of a simple component.
This a component that is not the sum of two other components or, equivalently, a component that contains
a simple root of
R
. We then show that the properties of simple components resemble those of simple roots.

We complete the section by studying components of the form
kA
for
A\in{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
and
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
.

6.1.
Definition and basic properties

We are now ready to define the addition on
{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
.

Definition 6.1
.

Consider two elements
A,B\in{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
.
If there exist
\alpha\in A
and
\beta\in B
such that

\alpha+\beta\in R
then we define
A+B=C
where
C
is the element
of
{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
containing
\alpha+\beta
. If no such pair

(\alpha,\beta)\in A\times B
exists then the sum
A+B
is undefined.

Given
k\geqslant 3
and
A_{1},A_{2},\dots,A_{k}\in{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
, we define inductively

A_{1}+A_{2}+\dots+A_{k}
as the component
(A_{1}+A_{2}+\dots+A_{k-1})+A_{k}

if both
A_{1}+A_{2}+\dots+A_{k-1}
and
(A_{1}+A_{2}+\dots+A_{k-1})+A_{k}
are defined
and say that
A_{1}+A_{2}+\dots+A_{k}
is undefined otherwise.

Remark 6.2
.

(i)

It is not obvious that when
A+B
is defined it is uniquely determined. This is established by
Proposition
6.3
.

(ii)

It follows from
Proposition
5.7
(iii) that
A\leqslant B
if
and only if there exists a possibly empty sequence of components

C_{1},C_{2},\dots,C_{n}
such that
B=A+C_{1}+C_{2}+\dots+C_{n}
.

(iii)

The sum
A_{1}+A_{2}+\dots+A_{k}
may be thought of as an extension of the notion of paths in QRSs.
Indeed,
[\alpha;\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2},\dots,\kappa_{n};\beta]
is a path in
R
if each of the partial sums

\alpha,\alpha+\kappa_{1},\dots,\alpha+\kappa_{1}+\dots+\kappa_{n}
belongs to
R
and

\beta=\alpha+\kappa_{1}+\dots+\kappa_{n}
. In other words,
B=A+K_{1}+K_{2}+\dots+K_{n}
for components
is the counterpart of the path
[\alpha;\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2},\dots,\kappa_{n};\beta]
.

Proposition 6.3
.

Suppose that
A_{1},A_{2},\dots,A_{n}
and
C

are elements of
{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
.
Let
\alpha_{i},\alpha^{\prime}_{i}\in A_{i}
for
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
with
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\in C
and

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}^{\prime}\in R
. Then
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}^{\prime}\in C
.

Proof.

It follows from Proposition
3.5
that
the expression
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}^{\prime}\in R
gives us a path

[\alpha_{1}^{\prime};\kappa_{1},\ldots,\kappa_{s},\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}^{\prime}]
where each

\kappa_{i}
is one of the
\alpha_{j}^{\prime}
. By closure of
\Phi
, all partial
sums of that path are in
\Phi
. In particular,
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}^{\prime}\in\Phi
.
Let
C^{\prime}
denote the component of
G(\Phi)
containing

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha^{\prime}_{i}
. Then
C\geqslant A_{i}
and
C^{\prime}\geqslant A_{i}
for all
i
. If
C=A_{j}
and
C^{\prime}=A_{k}

then
A_{j}\geqslant A_{k}
and
A_{k}\geqslant A_{j}
which implies
C=A_{j}=A_{k}=C^{\prime}
. Hence (interchanging
C
and
C^{\prime}

if necessary) we may suppose, without loss of generality, that
C>A_{i}
for all
i
.

For each
i
with
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
there exists a path

[\alpha_{i};\kappa_{i,1},\kappa_{i,2},\dots,\kappa_{i,k_{i}};\alpha_{i}^{\prime}]
in
A_{i}
with

\kappa_{i,j}\in\pm\Phi^{c}
for all
i,j
. By Proposition
3.5
, there exists a path

[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i};\nu_{1},\nu_{2},\dots,\nu_{m};\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}^{\prime}]
where each
\nu_{k}=\kappa_{i,j}

for some
i,j
. We claim that all partial sums along this path are in
\Phi
(and therefore in
C
). It is clear that this
path begins in
\Phi
, since
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\in C
. Suppose that

\rho_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\nu_{j}\in\Phi
is a partial sum along the path.
We claim that the next partial sum
\rho_{k}+\nu_{k+1}
is also in
\Phi
.
Note that
\rho_{k}+\nu_{k+1}=\rho_{k}+\kappa_{i,j}
for some
i,j
.
Since
\nu_{k+1}=\kappa_{i,j}
appears as a step on a path in
A_{i}
, we may write

\nu_{k+1}=\gamma-\gamma^{\prime}
where
\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\in A_{i}
.
Thus,
\rho_{k}+\gamma-\gamma^{\prime}\in R
. Observe that
\gamma\in R^{+}
implies that

\rho_{k}+\gamma\neq 0
. Also, if
\rho_{k}-\gamma^{\prime}=0
, then
\rho_{k}\in A_{i}
, which is
impossible since
\rho_{k}
lies in a
component that is strictly greater than
A_{i}
. Hence, by Proposition
3.1

either
\rho_{k}+\gamma\in R

or
\rho_{k}-\gamma^{\prime}\in R
.

If
\rho_{k}+\gamma\in R
, then
\rho_{k}+\gamma\in\Phi
by the closure of
\Phi
.
But then
\rho_{k}+\gamma-\gamma^{\prime}
cannot be in
R^{-}
, since if it were then
\gamma^{\prime}>\rho_{k}+\gamma
, which is a contradiction because

\gamma^{\prime}\in A_{i}
and
\rho_{k}\in C
and
A_{i}<C
.
The root
\rho_{k}+\gamma-\gamma^{\prime}
also cannot be in
\Phi^{c}
, since then
\rho_{k}+\gamma
and
\gamma^{\prime}

would belong to the same component, which again yields a contradiction in the order
of components. Thus, in this case,
\rho_{k}+\gamma-\gamma^{\prime}=\rho_{k}+\nu_{k+1}
must lie in
\Phi
.

If instead
\rho_{k}-\gamma^{\prime}
is a root, it cannot be in
R^{-}
since that would imply
\rho_{k}<\gamma^{\prime}

contradicting the fact that
C>A_{i}
. The root
\rho_{k}-\gamma^{\prime}
cannot be in
\Phi^{c}
,
as otherwise
\rho_{k}
and
\gamma^{\prime}
would lie in the same component. Therefore
\rho_{k}-\gamma^{\prime}\in\Phi.

Then the closure of
\Phi
implies that

\rho_{k}+\nu_{k+1}=\rho+\gamma-\gamma^{\prime}\in\Phi
,
which in turn implies that the next partial sum
\rho_{k}+\nu_{k+1}\in\Phi
.

Hence we see that the entire path
[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i};\nu_{1},\nu_{2},\dots,\nu_{m};\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}^{\prime}]

remains in
C
.
∎

Proposition 6.4
.

Let
A,B,C\in{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
such that
(A+B)+C
is defined. Then at least one of the two
sums
B+(A+C)
or
A+(B+C)
is defined and is equal to
(A+B)+C
. When all three sums are defined, they are all equal.

Proof.

We begin by proving the first assertion. Let
D=A+B
.

First we consider the situation when
(A+B)+C\in\{A,B\}
,
say
(A+B)+C=A
. Then
A=D=A+B
since
A\leqslant A+B\leqslant A
.
Furthermore
D+C=A+C
is defined and

(A+B)+C=A+C=A=A+B=(A+C)+B
.
The case where
(A+B)+C=B
is entirely similar.

Suppose then that
(A+B)+C>A
and
(A+B)+C>B
.
Then either
D+C>D
or both
D>A
and
D>B
. We show that there exist

\alpha\in A,\beta\in B
, and
\gamma\in C
such that
\alpha+\beta+\gamma
is a root.
If
D+C\neq D
, then we pick
\alpha\in A
and
\beta\in B
such that
\alpha+\beta\in D
.
Then, applying Lemma
5.4
(i), there exists
a
\gamma\in C
such that
\alpha+\beta+\gamma\in(A+B)+C
. If
D\neq A,B
,
we pick
\delta\in D
and
\gamma\in C
such that
\delta+\gamma\in(A+B)+C
.
By Lemma
5.4
(ii),
\delta=\alpha+\beta

for some
\alpha\in A
,
\beta\in B
, so that
\alpha+\beta+\gamma\in(A+B)+C
.

Then applying Proposition
3.1
we see that
one of
\alpha+\gamma
or
\beta+\gamma
is a root. Hence either

A+C
or
B+C
is defined. Moreover, because

(\alpha+\gamma)+\beta=(\beta+\gamma)+\alpha=\alpha+\beta+\gamma
,
it follows that
(A+B)+C=(A+C)+B
when
A+C
is defined, and
(A+B)+C=(B+C)+A

when
B+C
is defined.

Finally we prove the last statement of the proposition. Suppose then that say
(A+B)+C=(A+C)+B
and that

(B+C)+A
is defined. Applying the first assertion of
this proposition
to the sum
(B+C)+A
we see that this sum is equal to
either
(A+B)+C
or to
(A+C)+B
. Thus all three sums
are equal in this case.
∎

Clearly, Proposition
6.4
is analogous to Proposition
3.1
.
In the same way, the following proposition
for components is the analog of Proposition
3.8

for roots. The proof is also analogous to the proof of
Proposition
3.8
, and we omit it.

Proposition 6.5
.

Assume that the sum
A+K_{1}+\dots+K_{n}
is defined and
K_{i}+K_{j}
is undefined for all
1\leqslant i\neq j\leqslant n
.
Then, for any permutation
i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{n}
of
1,2,\dots,n
, the sum

A+K_{i_{1}}+\dots+K_{i_{n}}
is defined and equal to
A+K_{1}+\dots+K_{n}
.
\square

6.2.
Simple components

We can define a concept analogous to simple roots for components as well.

Proposition 6.7
.

Let
C\in{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
. The following are equivalent.

(i)

The component
C
contains a simple root.

(ii)

Whenever
C=A+B
for two components
A,B\in{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
, either
A=C
or
B=C
.

Definition 6.8
.

A component
C
is
simple
if either (i) or (ii) of Proposition
6.7
holds.

Proof of Proposition
6.7
.

(i)
\implies
(ii)
: Suppose that
C
is a component that contains a
simple root but does not satisfy (ii). Let
\theta\in C
be a simple root and

A,B\in{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}

both not equal to
C
such that
A+B=C
. By
Lemma
5.4
(ii),
there exists
\alpha\in A
and
\beta\in B
such that
\alpha+\beta=\theta
, a contradiction.

(ii)
\implies
(i)
: Suppose
C
is a component satisfying (ii) but not (i).
Let
\gamma\in C
be minimal. By our assumption,
\gamma
is not simple. Hence,
there exist positive roots
\alpha,\beta
such
that
\alpha+\beta=\gamma
. If one of
\alpha,\beta
is in
\Phi^{c}
,
we would have that the other is in
\Phi
, and in particular, in
C
. This contradicts the minimality of
\gamma
in
C
.
If
\alpha,\beta
are both in
\Phi
, they belong to their own
components
A,B
respectively, such that
A+B=C
. Since
C
is simple,
either
A
or
B
is
C
. Either way, there is a root in
C
strictly
smaller than
\gamma
, which contradicts minimality.
∎

Remark 6.9
.

It follows from Proposition
6.7
(ii) that components
minimal with respect to
\leqslant
are simple. This is useful in inductive arguments on the
poset
({\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)},\leqslant)
, since simple components are nicely characterized by the equivalence
outlined in Proposition
6.7
and are in general easy to work with.

Proposition 6.10
.

There is at most one simple component
C\in{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
with
\operatorname{supp}C=\operatorname{supp}\Phi
.

Proof.

Suppose
C_{1},C_{2}\in{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
are both simple
with
\operatorname{supp}C_{1}=\operatorname{supp}C_{2}=\operatorname{supp}\Phi
.
By definition,
there is a simple root
\theta\in C_{1}
, and since
C_{2}
is of full
support in
\Phi
, there exists a root
\alpha\in C_{2}
supported on
\theta
.
Because
\theta\leqslant\alpha
, we have
C_{1}\leqslant C_{2}
. Symmetrically,
C_{2}\leqslant C_{1}
, and we conclude that
C_{1}=C_{2}
.
∎

Proposition 6.11
.

Any element
A\in{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
can be written as
A=T_{1}+T_{2}+\dots+T_{n}
for simple components
T_{i}
.

Proof.

It suffices to prove that if
A
is not simple, then
A
can be written as
A=B+C
where
B<A
and
C
is simple.
The result then follows by induction in the poset
{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
.
To that end, suppose
A
is not simple and let
\alpha\in A
be minimal.
The root
\alpha
is not simple since
A
is not simple.
Thus,
\alpha=\beta+\theta
with
\theta
a simple root and
\beta\in R^{+}
.
If
\theta\in\Phi^{c}
, then
\beta\in A
which contradicts minimality of
\alpha
.
If
\beta\in\Phi^{c}
, then
\alpha-\theta\in\Phi^{c}
and so
A
contains a simple root, which is a contradiction.
Thus, writing
C
for the component of
\theta
and
B
for the component of
\beta
, we have
A=B+C
where
B<A
and

C
is simple.
∎

We can always derive the partial order on
{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
from its addition table.
Reasonably, we might ask what information about the addition table we can learn from the partial order.
For example, the statement
A+B=B
implies
A\leqslant B
.
The following proposition provides a partial converse.

Proposition 6.12
.

Let
A,T\in{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
with
T
simple. If
A<T
, then
A+T
is defined and is equal to
T
.

Proof.

By Remark
6.2
,
there is a sequence of components
B_{1},B_{2},\dots,B_{n}
such that

A+B_{1}+\dots+B_{n}=T
.
Since
T
is simple, either
A+B_{1}+\dots+B_{n-1}=T
or
B_{n}=T
.
Induction on
n
allows us to find a
k<n
such that
A+B_{1}+\dots+B_{k}+T=T
.
If
A+B_{1}+\dots+B_{k-1}+T
is defined and

(A+B_{1}+\dots+B_{k-1}+T)+B_{k}=T,

then
T\leqslant A+B_{1}+\dots+B_{k-1}+T\leqslant T
. Otherwise, if
B_{k}+T
is defined and

(A+B_{1}+\dots+B_{k-1})+(B_{k}+T)=T,

then
T\leqslant B_{k}+T\leqslant T
.
In both cases,
A+B_{1}+\dots+B_{k-1}+T=T
.
By Proposition
6.4
, at least one of these two conditions must hold.
Induction on
k
gives
A+T=T
.
∎

Proposition 6.13
.

Let
T
be a simple component. If
A,B<T
and
A+B
is defined, then
A+B<T
.

Proof.

It suffices to prove that
A+B\leqslant T
, since the possibility that
A+B=T

is excluded by the fact that
T
is simple. By
Proposition
6.12
,

A+T=B+T=T
. Thus, we may choose
\alpha\in A
and
\beta\in B

such that
\alpha+\beta\in A+B
and
\tau,\tau^{\prime}\in T

such that
\alpha+\tau=\tau^{\prime}
. We note that
(\alpha+\beta)+\tau-\tau^{\prime}=\beta

so that by Proposition
3.1
either
\alpha+\beta+\tau

or
\alpha+\beta-\tau^{\prime}=\beta-\tau
is a root. If
\alpha+\beta+\tau
is a root,
then
A+B+T
is defined and by Proposition
6.4
it is equal to
T
,
yielding the desired result. If instead
\beta-\tau
is a root, it must not be in
\pm\Phi^{c}
,
as
B\neq T
. It further must not be in
\Phi
, since
B<T
. Thus,
\beta-\tau\in-\Phi

and
\tau-\beta\in\Phi
. Noting then that
(\alpha+\beta)+(\tau-\beta)=\tau^{\prime}\in T
,
we conclude that
\alpha+\beta\leqslant\tau^{\prime}
and that
A+B\leqslant T
.
∎

Corollary 6.14
.

Let
T
be a simple component with
\operatorname{supp}T=\operatorname{supp}\Phi
. If
A\ngeq T
, then
A+T=T
.

Proof.

By Proposition
6.11
,

A=T_{1}+T_{2}+\dots+T_{n}
where
T_{i}
is simple. Since
A\ngeq T
,
T_{i}\neq T

for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
, and because
T
is of full support in
\Phi
,
T_{i}<T
for all
i
.
The result then follows from Propositions
6.13

and
6.12
.
∎

6.3.
Cancellation rules

The partial addition of components does not necessarily satisfy the property that

A+B=A+C
implies
B=C
, i.e., cancellation does not hold in general.
Below we study the question of when does the cancellation rule apply.

Lemma 6.15
.

Suppose
A,B,C,D\in{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
satisfy
A+B=A+C=D
and
A\neq D
.
Then there exist roots
\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\in A
,
\beta\in B
, and
\gamma\in C
such that
\alpha+\beta=\alpha^{\prime}+\gamma\in D
.

Proof.

If
B=C
, this is immediate. Otherwise, since
B\neq C
, either

B\neq D
or
C\neq D
, so we will assume without loss of generality that
C\neq D

and choose some
\alpha\in A
and
\beta\in B
such that
\alpha+\beta\in D
. Then,
by Lemma
5.4
,
\alpha+\beta=\alpha^{\prime}+\gamma

for some
\alpha^{\prime}\in A
and
\gamma\in C
, as desired.
∎

Proposition 6.16
.

Let
A,B,C\in{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
.
If
A+B=A+C\neq A
, then
B=C
.

Proof.

Let
D:=A+B=A+C
. If
A=B
and
A=C
then
B=C
and we are done.
Thus we may assume, without loss of generality, that
A\neq B
.
Applying Lemma
6.15
, we choose roots

\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\in A
,
\beta\in B
and
\gamma\in C
such that
\alpha+\beta=\alpha^{\prime}+\gamma\in D
.
We next fix a path
[\alpha^{\prime};\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2},\dots,\kappa_{m};\alpha]
with
\kappa_{i}\in\pm\Phi^{c}
.
Noting that
\gamma-\beta=\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}
and applying
Proposition
3.5
, we may construct the
path
[\beta;\nu_{1},\nu_{2},\dots,\nu_{n};\gamma]
where
\nu_{i}
is chosen from
\{\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2},\dots,\kappa_{m}\}
.
We show that this path remains in
\Phi
and thus in
B
.

Clearly the path begins in
\Phi
. We claim that if the
k
-th partial
sum
\rho_{k}
along the path is in
\Phi
, then
\rho_{k+1}
is as well.
Indeed, if we suppose
\rho_{k+1}=\rho_{k}+\nu_{k+1}\in-\Phi
,
then
-\rho_{k}-\nu_{k+1}\in\Phi
, and so by closure
-\nu_{k+1}\in\Phi
,
which is a contradiction since
\nu_{k+1}\in\pm\Phi^{c}
. Next we show that
\rho_{k+1}\notin\pm\Phi^{c}
.
Writing
\nu_{k+1}=\eta-\eta^{\prime}
for
\eta,\eta^{\prime}\in A
, we apply
Proposition
3.1
to conclude that either
\rho_{k}+\eta\in R
or
\rho_{k}-\eta^{\prime}\in R
.
If
\rho_{k}+\eta\in R
, then
\rho_{k}+\eta\in D=A+B
, so
\rho_{k+1}=\rho_{k}+\eta-\eta^{\prime}\notin\pm\Phi^{c}

since
\rho_{k+1}
would otherwise link
A
and
D
.
If
\rho_{k}-\eta^{\prime}\in R
, then either (i)
\rho_{k}-\eta^{\prime}\in\Phi

or (ii)
\rho_{k}-\eta^{\prime}\in-\Phi
or (iii)
\rho_{k}-\eta^{\prime}\in\pm\Phi^{c}
.

(i)

In the first case,
\rho_{k}-\eta^{\prime}\in\Phi
implies
\rho_{k+1}\in\Phi
by closure of
\Phi
.

(ii)

In the second case,

\rho_{k}-\eta^{\prime}\in-\Phi
implies that
\eta^{\prime}-\rho_{k}
belongs to a component
E

satisfying
E+B=A
. If
\rho_{k+1}\in\pm\Phi^{c}
,
then
(\eta^{\prime}-\rho_{k})-\eta=-\rho_{k+1}\in\pm\Phi^{c}
, so
E=A
.
We obtain from here the contradiction that
D=A+B=E+B=A
.

(iii)

Finally, if
\rho_{k}-\eta^{\prime}\in\pm\Phi^{c}
, then
A=B
, which contradicts the initial assumption that
A\neq B
.

Since
\rho_{k+1}\notin-\Phi
and
\rho_{k+1}\notin\pm\Phi^{c}
, we
conclude that
\rho_{k+1}\in\Phi
, completing the proof. ∎

Next we provide a necessary condition for the failure of the cancellation property for
A+B=B
.

Lemma 6.17
.

Suppose
A,B\in{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
such that
A+B=B
. Then there exists a simple component
T\in{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}

such that
A\leqslant T\leqslant B
. As a consequence, if
A+A=A
, then
A
is simple.

Proof.

If
B
is simple,
A\leqslant B
and there is nothing to prove, so assume
B
is not simple.
Proposition
6.11

implies the existence of a component
C<B
and a simple component
T
such that
B=C+T
.
Plugging this into
A+B=B
gives
A+(C+T)=B=C+T
.
Proposition
6.4
implies either
A+T
is defined and
(A+T)+C=T+C
,
or
A+C
is defined and
(A+C)+T=C+T
.
Since
B\neq T
and
B\neq C
, we can cancel a term in both of the previous equations by
Proposition
6.16
to see that either
A+T=T
or
A+C=C
.
In the first of the two cases,
A\leqslant T
and so we are done.
In the second case,
C<B
and so we can use induction on the poset
{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
to obtain the desired result.
∎

Next we state a condition which ensures that cancellation holds in
{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
.

Proposition 6.18
.

If
U+T\neq T
for all pairs of simple components
T,U\in{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
, then cancellation holds for
the partial addition in
{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
.

Proof.

Proposition
6.16
implies that
if
A+B\neq B
for all pairs of components
A,B\in{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
, then cancellation holds for
the partial addition in
{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
. Hence it remains to show that

U+T\neq T
for all pairs of simple components
T,U\in{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
implies that

A+B\neq B
for all pairs of components
A,B\in{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
.

Assume that
U+T\neq T
for all pairs of simple components
T,U\in{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
but
there are
A,B\in{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
with
A+B=B
. The proof of
Lemma
6.17
implies that there exists a
simple component
T
such that
A\leqslant T\leqslant B
and
A+T=T
. Then
A<T
since
otherwise
T+T=T
contradicting the assumption.
Hence there is a simple
U
such that
U\leqslant A
and
U+T=T
in contradiction to the assumption.
∎

6.4.
Multiples of a component

Proposition
6.16
and
Lemma
6.17
allow us to characterize the
multiples of a component in
{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
.

If
r
is a positive integer and
A\in{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
, we define
rA
inductively as follows:

1A:=A
and, for
r\geqslant 2
,

rA:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(r-1)A+A,&\text{\it{if this sum is defined}};\\
{\text{{\it undefined}}},&{\text{\it{otherwise}}.}\end{array}\right.

There are three mutually exclusive alternatives for
A+A
:

A+A
is undefined,
A+A=A
, or
A<A+A=2A
.
The following lemma shows that these alternatives for
A+A
determine how all multiples of
A
behave.

Proposition 6.19
.

Let
\langle A\rangle=\{nA\mid n\geqslant 1,nA\text{ is defined}\}
.
There is
k
such that
A,2A,\dots,kA
are defined and distinct, and

\langle A\rangle=\{A,2A,\dots,kA\}
.
Moreover, one of the following three mutually exclusive alternatives holds:

(i)

k=1
and
nA
is undefined for all
n\geqslant 2
.

(ii)

k=1
and
nA
is defined and equal to
A
for all
n\geqslant 2
. In this case, by
Lemma
6.17
,
A
is necessarily simple.

(iii)

k\geqslant 2
,
nA
is defined if and only if
1\leqslant n\leqslant k
. Furthermore,
for
1\leqslant m,n\leqslant k
,

mA+nA=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}(m+n)A,&\text{ if }&m+n\leqslant k;\\
\text{undefined},&\text{ if }&m+n>k.\end{array}\right.

Proof.

We let
k
denote the cardinality of
\langle A\rangle
.
If
nA
and
(n+1)A
are both defined and distinct, then
nA<(n+1)A
.
Hence
\langle A\rangle
consists of the
k
distinct elements
A,2A,\dots,kA
.
Then
(k+1)A
is either undefined or
(k+1)A=kA
.
In the latter case
k=1
. Indeed, otherwise we would have
kA+A=(k-1)A+A
and
Proposition
6.16
would imply
kA=(k-1)A
, which contradicts
the assumption that
A,2A,\dots,kA
are distinct.
To complete the proof, it remains to check the addition formula in (iii).

Every
\alpha\in rA=(r-1)A+A
may be written as

\alpha=\alpha^{\prime}+\alpha^{\prime\prime}
with
\alpha^{\prime}\in(r-1)A
and

\alpha^{\prime\prime}\in A
for all
r=1,2,\dots,k
.
It follows that every root
\alpha\in rA

may be expressed as
\alpha=\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+\dots+\alpha_{r}

with
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{r}\in A
.
From this, it is easy to see using Proposition
6.3
that
mA+nA=(m+n)A
whenever

m+n\leqslant k
.

Consider now the case where
m+n>k
. By contradiction, assume that
mA+nA
is defined and let

t\geqslant k+1
be minimal such that there exist

1\leqslant m,n\leqslant k
with
m+n=t
and

mA+nA
is defined.
Further suppose
n
is minimal such that

(t-n)A+nA
is defined.

If
n=1
, we already have an expression for
mA+A
, and there is nothing to show.
Thus
n\geqslant 2
and
mA+((n-1)A+A)
is defined.
By Proposition
6.4

either
(mA+(n-1)A)+A
or

(mA+A)+(n-1)A
is defined.
In the former case, we again must have
m+n-1=k

which cannot happen.
The latter case violates the minimality of

n
. Either way we arrive at a contradiction.
∎

.

7.
Primitive and irreducible inversion sets

In this section we continue to assume that
R
is an irreducible QRS,
\Phi\subseteq R^{+}
is an inversion set,
and we retain the notation from Sections
5
and

6
.
We investigate the relationship between the set
\mathrm{Gen}(\Phi)
and the supports of elements of
{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
.
In particular we prove that when
\Phi
is written canonically as
\Phi=\mathrm{inf}_{I}^{S}(\Psi,X)
,
then all elements of
{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
are inflated from
I
.
We then use this result to prove that
an inversion set
\Phi
is primitive if and only if both
\Phi
and
\Phi^{c}
are irreducible.
The results of this section are used in Section
9
below.

7.1.
The set
\mathrm{Gen}(A)
for
A\in{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}

Proposition 7.1
.

Let
A,B\in{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
such that
A+B
is defined. If
I\in\mathrm{Gen}(A)\cap\mathrm{Gen}(B)
, then
I\in\mathrm{Gen}(A+B)
.

Proof.

If
A+B
is equal to
A
or
B
the result is immediate.
Otherwise, for any
\gamma\in A+B
such that
\pi_{I}(\gamma)\neq 0
,
\pm\theta\in I
, and
\gamma+\theta\in R
,
we would like to show that
\gamma+\theta\in A+B
.
To that end, we apply Lemma
5.4
(ii)
and write
\gamma=\alpha+\beta
where
\alpha\in A,\beta\in B
.
Then
\gamma+\theta=\alpha+\beta+\theta
.

We note that
\pi_{I}(\alpha)\neq 0
. Otherwise
\beta
and
\alpha+\beta
would be in
the same fibre, and since
I\in\mathrm{Gen}(B)
, we would have
A+B\subseteq B

which would imply that
A+B=B
. Similarly,
\pi_{I}(\beta)\neq 0
.
In particular,
\alpha+\theta
and
\beta+\theta
are all non-zero.
Note also that
\alpha+\beta\neq 0
, because both
\alpha,\beta
are positive.
Therefore, using Proposition
3.1
, we conclude that either
\alpha+\theta
is
a root or
\beta+\theta
is a root.
Say
\alpha+\theta\in R
.
Since
I\in\mathrm{Gen}(A)
, this means that
\alpha+\theta\in A
and therefore
(\alpha+\theta)+\beta\in A+B
.
We thus conclude that
I\in\mathrm{Gen}(A+B)
.
∎

Proposition 7.2
.

For all
C\in{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
,
\operatorname{supp}C\in\mathrm{Gen}(\Phi)
.

Proof.

Let
I=\operatorname{supp}C
and
D=\bigcup_{C^{\prime}\leqslant C}C^{\prime}
. It follows from
Corollary
5.8
that
\operatorname{supp}D=I
.
Next, suppose
\alpha+\beta\in D
for some
\alpha,\beta\in R^{+}
.
We will show that either
\alpha
or
\beta
is in
D
to prove that
D
is co-closed.
Since
C\subseteq\Phi
and
\Phi
is co-closed, either
\alpha
or
\beta
is in
\Phi
,
so without loss of generality we let
\alpha\in\Phi
. We then give the labels

C_{\alpha+\beta}
and
C_{\alpha}
to the components respectively containing
\alpha+\beta

and
\alpha
. Since
\alpha\leqslant\alpha+\beta
, we have
that
C_{\alpha}\leqslant C_{\alpha+\beta}\leqslant C
, so
C_{\alpha}\subseteq D
,
\alpha\in D
, and
D
is co-closed.

Having constructed the co-closed set
D
, we use it to prove our result.
We suppose toward a contradiction that there exist
\gamma,\delta\in R^{+}

with
\pi_{I}(\gamma)=\pi_{I}(\delta)\neq 0
,
\gamma\in\Phi
, and
\delta\in\Phi^{c}
.
By Proposition
3.10
,
\delta-\gamma\in\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{Z}}(D)
, and so
Proposition
3.5
guarantees a path
[\gamma;\kappa_{1},\dots,\kappa_{n};\delta]

with
\kappa_{i}\in\pm D
. Both
\gamma
and
\delta
belong to the same fibre
of
R/I
, and since
\kappa_{i}\in\pm D\subseteq\mathrm{span\,}I
, every partial sum along
this path belongs to that same fibre and hence must be a positive root. Since

\gamma\in\Phi
,
\delta\in\Phi^{c}
, and all partial sums are positive, there will be
some
i
such that the
i
-th partial sum
\gamma_{i}
is in
\Phi
while the
(i+1)
-th
partial sum
\gamma_{i+1}
is in
\Phi^{c}
. We would then have
\gamma_{i}+\kappa_{i+1}=\gamma_{i+1}
.
We note that
\kappa_{i+1}
must be negative since since
\Phi
is closed and
\gamma_{i+1}\in\Phi^{c}
.
But then
\gamma_{i}-(-\kappa_{i+1})=\gamma_{i+1}
, so that
\gamma_{i}
and
-\kappa_{i+1}
are
linked in
G(\Phi)
. Since
-\kappa_{i+1}\in D
and
D
is a union of components of
G(\Phi)
,
this implies
\gamma^{\prime}\in D
, which is not possible because
\pi_{I}(\gamma^{\prime})\notin\pi_{I}(D)=\{0\}
.
∎

Proposition 7.3
.

Let
\Phi=\mathrm{inf}_{I}^{S}(\Psi,X)
expressed canonically with
\Psi
primitive. There is a
unique simple component
Z
in
{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
not contained in

X
with
\operatorname{supp}Z=S
.
All other simple components are contained in
X
.

Proof.

Pick a simple root
\theta\in\mathrm{inf}_{I}^{S}(\Psi,\emptyset)
and let
Z\in{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
be the component
containing
\theta
. By Proposition
7.2
,
\operatorname{supp}Z\in\mathrm{Gen}(\Phi)
,
and by Proposition
4.18
, either
\operatorname{supp}Z\subseteq I
or
\operatorname{supp}Z=S
.
Since
\theta\in\operatorname{supp}Z
but
\theta\notin I
, we conclude that
\operatorname{supp}Z=S
.
Because we chose
\theta
arbitrarily, it follows that any component containing a simple root
of
\mathrm{inf}_{I}^{S}(\Psi,\emptyset)
has support
S
, and by
Proposition
6.10
there is only one such component.
All other simple components therefore must be contained in
X
.
∎

The following corollary is useful in the proof of Proposition
7.6
.

Corollary 7.4
.

Let
\Phi=\mathrm{inf}_{I}^{S}(\Psi,X)
written in canonical form with
\Psi
primitive.
Let
X^{\prime},Z\in{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
be simple components where
X^{\prime}\subseteq X
and
\operatorname{supp}Z=S
. Then
X^{\prime}+Z=Z
.

Proof.

Let
\theta\in X^{\prime}
be simple. Since
\operatorname{supp}Z=S
, there exists some
z\in Z
such
that
\theta\leqslant z
, so that
X^{\prime}\leqslant Z
. The result then follows from
Proposition
6.12
.
∎

While this is all that is required for the proof of
Proposition
7.6
, one might
reasonably wonder whether
Y+Z=Z
for any (not necessarily simple component)

Y\subseteq X
. This is implied by the following statement.

Remark 7.5
.

Let
T\in{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
be a simple component with
\operatorname{supp}T=S
. Then,

(i)

All components are comparable to
T
.

(ii)

All components greater than
T
are equal to
kT
for some
k>1
.

(iii)

Letting
\Phi=\mathrm{inf}_{I}^{S}(\Psi,X)
be the canonical form of
\Phi
,
\Psi

is either primitive or
(R/I)^{+}
where
R/I
is a rank-one system.

Proposition 7.6
.

Let
\Phi
be an inversion set canonically inflated from
I
. Then all components of
G(\Phi)
are inflated from
I
.

Proof.

We write
\Phi=\mathrm{inf}_{I}^{S}(\Psi,X)
and split the argument into three mutually exclusive cases.

(i)

\Psi=\emptyset
:

Here the result is trivial, since
\operatorname{supp}C\subseteq\operatorname{supp}\Phi=I
for every component
C
of
G(\Phi)
, so that
I\in\mathrm{Gen}(C)
.

(ii)

\Psi=(R/I)^{+}
:

Since
I
is canonical,
\operatorname{supp}\Phi^{c}=I
. This implies that each component of
G(\Phi)
is
contained in a single fibre of
\pi_{I}
. To show that each is inflated from
I
we need only
to show that those components that are contained in a fibre over a non-zero root of
R/I

are exactly the fibre that contains them. This is a consequence of
Propositions
3.10
and
3.5
,
that state respectively that roots in
\Phi^{c}
span the difference between any two roots
\alpha,\beta

in the same fibre, and that roots of
\Phi^{c}
can therefore be used to construct a path between
\alpha

and
\beta
. This path stays in the same fibre and therefore in
\Phi
, so that components of
G(\Phi)

are either fibres of
\pi_{I}
over non-zero elements or contained in the zero fibre. In both cases, they are inflated from
I
.

(iii)

\Psi
is primitive:

Let
Z
be the unique simple component with
\operatorname{supp}(Z)=S
defined in
Proposition
7.3
and let
X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{n}
denote the
other simple components, which lie in
X
.
Every
X_{i}
is clearly inflated from
I
. We will now show that
Z
is also inflated from
I
.
It follows from Corollary
7.4
that

X_{i}+Z=Z
for all
i
.

Let
\gamma\in Z

and
\theta\in\pm I
such that
\gamma+\theta\in R
. Since
\Phi
is inflated from
I
,
\gamma+\theta\in\Phi
.
If
\theta\in\pm\Phi^{c}
, then
\gamma+\theta
is adjacent to
\gamma
in
G(\Phi)
,
so
\gamma+\theta\in Z
. If
\theta\in\Phi
, then the component
X_{\theta}
of
G(\Phi)

containing
\theta
is simple and contained in
X
, so that
\gamma+\theta\in X_{\theta}+Z=Z
.
Otherwise, if
\theta\in-\Phi
, then
\gamma+\theta\leqslant\gamma
and so the component containing
it is less than or equal to
Z
. Since
Z
is minimal among components not contained in
X

and
\pi_{I}(\gamma+\theta)=\pi_{I}(\gamma)\neq 0
, it follows that
\gamma+\theta\in Z
.
This shows that
I\in\mathrm{Gen}(Z)
. With this, we have that all simple components are inflated from
I
.

Since all components are sums of simple components by
Proposition
6.11
and sums of
inflations from
I
are inflations from
I
by Proposition
7.1
,
every component is inflated from
I
.

Since the three preceding cases cover all possibilities for
\Psi
in the canonical expression of

\Phi
, the proof is complete. ∎

Corollary 7.7
.

Let
\Phi,\Phi_{1},\dots,\Phi_{k}
be inversion sets such that
\Phi=\Phi_{1}\sqcup\dots\sqcup\Phi_{k}
.
Assume that
\Phi=\mathrm{inf}_{I}^{S}(\Psi,X)
is the canonical form of

.

.

7.2.
Irreducibility of
\Phi
and
{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}

We start with establishing necessary and sufficient conditions for an inversion set
\Phi

to be irreducible in terms of its associated graph
G(\Phi)
.

Theorem 7.8
.

Let
\Phi
be an inversion set. The following are equivalent:

(i)

\Phi
is irreducible.

(ii)

Every component
C\in{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
satisfies
\operatorname{supp}C=\operatorname{supp}\Phi
.

(iii)

The set
{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
contains a unique simple component.

(iv)

There exists a component
Z\in{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}
such that
{\operatorname{Comp}(\Phi)}=\{Z,2Z,\dots,kZ\}
for some
k\geqslant 1
.

Proof.

We show that
(i)
\implies
(ii)
by contrapositive. If there were a component
C
with
\operatorname{supp}C\subsetneq\operatorname{supp}\Phi
,
then
\Phi=\mathrm{inf}_{\operatorname{supp}C}^{S}(\Psi,X)
by Proposition
7.2
.
Because
\operatorname{supp}C\neq\operatorname{supp}\Phi
,
\Psi
is non-empty. Since
C\subseteq X
,
X
is also non-empty,
and thus
\Phi
is reducible.
That
(ii)
\implies
(iii)
follows immediately from
Proposition
6.10
.
(iii)
\implies
(iv)
because by
Proposition
6.11
every component can be written as
the sum of simple components, and by
(iii)
there is a unique simple component
Z
.
To see that
(iv)
\implies
(i)
,
suppose that
\Phi=\Phi_{1}\sqcup\Phi_{2}
. Assume
Z\subseteq\Phi_{i}
.
By the closure of
\Phi_{i}
, every other component is also contained in
\Phi_{i}
,
so
\Phi_{i}=\Phi
. Since we began with an arbitrary decomposition,
\Phi
must be irreducible.
∎

Corollary 7.9
.

Every primitive inversion set is irreducible.
Conversely, if
\mathrm{rk}\,R>2
,
then every irreducible inversion set
whose complement is also irreducible is necessarily primitive. ∎

Proof.

The first assertion is clear by Proposition
7.3

combined with Theorem
7.8
. For the second assertion,
suppose that
\Phi
and
\Phi^{c}
are irreducible but not primitive.
Then there is an
I\subsetneq S
with
\Phi=\mathrm{inf}_{I}^{S}(\Psi,X)
, and
\Phi^{c}=\mathrm{inf}_{I}^{S}(\Psi^{c},X^{c})
.
Without loss of generality, assume
\Psi
is not empty.
Then since
\Phi
is irreducible,
X
is empty.
If
I
is empty, then the fact that
\Phi
is not primitive implies that
\Psi=R^{+}/I=R^{+}
.
In the case that
I
is not empty, irreducibility of
\Phi^{c}
implies that one of

\Psi^{c},X^{c}=R_{I}^{+}
is empty, therefore
\Psi^{c}=\emptyset

We see that in every case,
\Phi=\mathrm{inf}_{I}^{S}(R^{+}/I,\emptyset)
and
\Phi^{c}=\mathrm{inf}_{I}^{S}(\emptyset,R_{I}^{+})
.
The inversion sets

, and

are irreducible only if they are both quotient
root systems of rank no more than

\mathrm{rk}\,R=\mathrm{rk}\,R_{I}+\mathrm{rk}\,R/I

.
∎

Remark 7.10
.

The condition appearing in the above corollary that

Take

Theorem

implies that, using the

. This is a significant improvement

, since, for a root

is at least

.

Corollary 7.11
.

If

is a QRS and

is an inversion set, then the time required

.∎

Proposition 8.1

Proposition 8.5

Proposition 8.6
.

Consider a collection of (not necessarily distinct) components

.
Suppose

.

Proof.

The proof is by (strong) induction on

So assume that

. If

Now suppose that

.
Also, if

Write

Proposition 8.7
.

Let

(i)

If

or

.

(ii)

If

Induction on

.
As in the proof of Proposition

, (ii) if

for the canonical

. Since

If

9.
Applications to GIT

If
\Delta
is a root system and
w
is an element of the Weyl group of
\Delta
,
the inversion set
\Phi(w)
of
w
is defined as

\Phi(w):=\{\alpha\in\Delta^{+}\,|\,w(\alpha)\in\Delta^{-}\}=\Delta^{+}\cap w^{-1}(\Delta^{-})\ .

Taking inversion sets of elements of
W
establishes a bijection between the Weyl group
W
and
the inversion sets in
\Delta^{+}
:
\Phi(w)
is an
inversion set in
\Delta^{+}
and, conversely, every inversion set in
\Delta^{+}
equals
\Phi(w)

for a unique
w\in W
. In this section we show how several problems which have recently arisen
in geometric invariant theory can be studied using the methods developed in this paper.

9.1.
Decomposing inversion sets

The problem of decomposing a given inversion set
\Phi(w)
into the disjoint union of inversion
sets
\Phi(w_{1}),\dots,\Phi(w_{k})
arises naturally in connection with different problems
in geometric invariant theory, e.g. the description of the faces of the Littlewood-Richardson cone or
the Belkale-Kumar product on
H^{*}(G/B,\mathbb{Z})
, see
[
BK
]
,
[
DR
]
,
[
FR
]
for details.
In
[
D-W
]
decompositions

(4)

\Phi(w)=\Phi(w_{1})\sqcup\Phi(w_{2})\sqcup\dots\sqcup\Phi(w_{k})

were studied for roots systems of types
\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B}
, and
\mathbb{C}
. The approach in

[
D-W
]
is based on the fact that the Weyl group of the root system
\mathbb{A}_{n}
is the symmetric
group on
n+1
elements and the Weyl groups of the root systems
\mathbb{B}_{n}
and
\mathbb{C}_{n}
can
be realized as groups of “symmetric” permutations. Consequently, the methods of
[
D-W
]
do not
extend beyond these three types of root systems. In this section we develop a type-independent
approach to studying the analogs of the decomposition (
4
) and other related problems.
More precisely, we provide a description of decompositions like (
4
) which is recursive
in terms of subsystems and quotients of
\Delta
. Consequently, the natural setting in which we
study (
4
) and related topics is provided by quotient root systems.

Let
R
be a QRS. First we characterize all decompositions

(5)

R^{+}=\Phi_{1}\sqcup\Phi_{2}\sqcup\dots\sqcup\Phi_{k}\ ,

where
\Phi_{i}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{+}
is an inversion set for every
1\leqslant i\leqslant k
.
Both inflations and the decomposition (
5
)
are compatible with the decomposition of a QRS into its irreducible components. More precisely,
if
R
is a QRS with irreducible components
R^{1},R^{2},\dots,R^{s}
, then every decomposition of
R^{+}
into
inversion sets yields a decomposition of each
R^{i}
into inversion sets and vice-versa. As a consequence,
it is sufficient to describe the decompositions (
5
) for irreducible QRSs.

Theorem 9.1
.

Let
R
be an irreducible QRS and let

R^{+}=\Phi_{1}\sqcup\Phi_{2}\sqcup\dots\sqcup\Phi_{k}\ ,

where
\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2},\dots,\Phi_{k}
are inversion sets. Assume that
\Phi_{1}
contains the highest root of
R
.
If
\Phi_{1}=\mathrm{inf}_{I}^{S}(\Psi,X_{1})
when expressed canonically, then up to a relabelling,

\Phi_{2}=\mathrm{inf}_{I}^{S}(\Psi^{c},X_{2})
and
\Phi_{i}=\mathrm{inf}_{I}^{S}(\emptyset,X_{i})
for
i>2
.

Proof.

We note that
\Psi\neq\emptyset
, since otherwise
\operatorname{supp}\Phi_{1}=I\subsetneq S
,
and
\Phi_{1}
would not contain the highest root of
R
. We therefore split our argument into the two remaining cases:

(i)

\Psi=(R/I)^{+}
:
In this case the result is clear, since
\Phi_{i}\subseteq\Phi_{1}^{c}=\mathrm{inf}_{I}^{S}(\emptyset,X_{1}^{c})
for each
i\geqslant 2
.

(ii)

\Psi
is primitive:
Note that
\mathrm{inf}_{I}^{S}(\Psi^{c},X_{1}^{c})=\Phi_{1}^{c}=\Phi_{2}\sqcup\Phi_{3}\sqcup\dots\sqcup\Phi_{k}
,
so by Corollary
7.7
,

I\in\mathrm{Gen}(\Phi_{i})
for each
i\geqslant 2
. Writing
\Phi_{i}=\mathrm{inf}_{I}^{S}(\Xi_{i},X_{i})
,
we find that
\Psi^{c}=\Xi_{2}\sqcup\Xi_{3}\sqcup\dots\sqcup\Xi_{k}
. Since
\Psi^{c}
is
primitive and therefore irreducible by Corollary
7.9
,
all but one
\Xi_{i}
is empty. Labelling the non-empty set
\Xi_{2}
, we find that

\Phi_{2}=\mathrm{inf}_{I}^{S}(\Psi^{c},X_{2})
and
\Phi_{i}=\mathrm{inf}_{I}^{S}(\emptyset,X_{i})
for
i>2
. ∎

Corollary 9.2
.

Let, as in Theorem
9.1
,
R
be an irreducible QRS and

R^{+}=\Phi_{1}\sqcup\Phi_{2}\sqcup\dots\sqcup\Phi_{k}\ .

Then
\operatorname{supp}\Phi_{i}=S
for either one or two values of
i
. More precisely,

\operatorname{supp}\Phi_{i}=S
for a unique

if one of the sets
\Phi_{1},\dots,\Phi_{k}
is inflated from
(R/I)^{+}
and

\operatorname{supp}\Phi_{i}=S
for two values of
i
if two of the sets
\Phi_{1},\dots,\Phi_{k}
are inflated from
(complementary) primitive inversion sets in
(R/I)^{+}
.
\square

Remark 9.3
.

Theorem

allows us to describe the decompositions of a given inversion set

\Phi\subseteq R^{+}
into a disjoint union of inversion sets.
Let
\Phi\subseteq R^{+}
,
\Phi=\mathrm{inf}_{I}^{S}(\Psi,X)
when expressed canonically.
We want to describe all decompositions

\Phi=\Phi_{1}\sqcup\Phi_{2}\sqcup\dots\sqcup\Phi_{k}\ .

First we note that, by Corollary

,

\Phi_{i}=\mathrm{inf}_{I}^{S}(\Psi_{i},X_{i})

. We consider three cases depending on

and (

.

9.2.
A basis of
E
related to a decomposition (
6
)

Let
\Delta
be a root system in the Euclidean space
E
and
w\in W
. Set

S(w):=\{\alpha\in\Delta^{+}\,|\,-w(\alpha){\text{ is a simple root}}\}\ .

In the case when
\Delta
is of type
\mathbb{A}
, it was proved in
[
D-W
, Proposition 8.1]
that, if

\Delta^{+}=\Phi(w_{1})\sqcup\Phi(w_{2})\sqcup\Phi(w_{3})\ ,

then
S(w_{1}),S(w_{2}),S(w_{3})
are disjoint and their union is a basis of
E
. This result was then used to
show that the corresponding face of the Littlewood-Richardson cone is simplicial and to provide
an efficient algorithm for determining its generating rays. Next we will prove that the analog of

[
D-W
, Proposition 8.1]
holds true for every root system. It is not difficult then to show that
this analog implies the analogs of the results derived as consequences of
[
D-W
, Proposition 8.1]
.
We will not discuss these application here as the interested reader will be able to fill in the details
following the exposition of
[
D-W
]
.

Let
R
be a QRS and let
\Phi\subseteq R^{+}
be an inversion set. To define the set

S(\Phi)
we note that
\Phi=R^{+}\cap P
for
a (unique) positive system
P\subseteq R
. Set

S(\Phi):=\{\alpha\in\Phi\,|\,\alpha{\text{ is a simple root of }}P\}\ .

If
R
is a root system and
w\in W
, then
S(w)=S(\Phi(w))
because
\Phi(w)=R^{+}\cap w^{-1}(R^{-})
.

Proposition 9.4
.

Let
R^{+}=\Phi_{1}\sqcup\Phi_{2}\sqcup\dots\sqcup\Phi_{k}
. Then
S(\Phi_{1}),S(\Phi_{2}),\dots,S(\Phi_{k})
are disjoint sets whose union is a basis of

.

Proof.

Since
\Phi_{1},\dots,\Phi_{k}
are disjoint and
S(\Phi_{i})\subseteq\Phi_{i}
, the sets
S(\Phi_{1}),\dots,S(\Phi_{k})

are also disjoint. We prove that their union is a basis of
E
by induction on the rank of
R
. If
{\mathrm{rk\,}}R=1

the statement is obvious. For the rest of the proof we assume that the statement is true
for every QRS of rank less than
r
and consider a QRS

of rank

.

Let
R^{+}=\Phi_{1}\sqcup\Phi_{2}\sqcup\dots\sqcup\Phi_{k}
where

\Phi_{i}=\mathrm{inf}_{I}^{S}(\Psi_{i},X_{i})
as in Theorem
9.1
and let
W:=\mathrm{span\,}I
.
For every
1\leqslant i\leqslant k

S^{\prime}(\Phi_{i}):=S(\Phi_{i})\cap W\quad{\text{and}}\quad S^{\prime\prime}(\Phi_{i})=S(\Phi_{i})\backslash S^{\prime}(\Phi_{i})\ .

Since
R_{I}^{+}=X_{1}\sqcup X_{2}\sqcup\dots\sqcup X_{k}
, we have

X_{i}=(R^{+}\cap W)\cap P=R_{I}^{+}\cap(P\cap W)
and hence
S(X_{i})=S^{\prime}(\Phi_{i})
.
Thus, by induction,

S^{\prime}(\Phi_{1})\cup\dots\cup S^{\prime}(\Phi_{k})
is a basis of
W
.

Next we consider the two possible cases for
\Phi_{1}
:

(i)

\Psi_{1}=(R/I)^{+}
:

Then
\Psi_{i}=\emptyset
for
i>1
and hence
S^{\prime\prime}(\Phi_{i})=\emptyset
for
i>1
.

10.
Enumerative results

In this section we show how the methods and results developed so far can be applied to obtain various statistics
related to inversion sets in root systems.
For example, one can ask how many primitive (inversions) sets does a given root system (or a given QRS) have.
In the cases of root systems of types
\mathbb{A}
,
\mathbb{B}
, and
\mathbb{C}
, functional equations for
the generating functions of the number of primitive inversion sets are known,
[
AAK
]
and
[
D-W
]
.
However, these equations do not yield explicit formulas for the their numbers. On the other hand, an estimate
in the case of root systems of type
\mathbb{A}
is known.

In Sections
10.1
–
10.3
we discuss three enumerative problems:
The first one concerns the number of
fine decompositions
of a root system, i.e., decompositions in which
every inversion set contains a single simple root, see below.
The second one discusses the number of primitive inversion sets of a root system.
The third one discusses the number of inversion sets which occur in fine decompositions.
These are just a couple of examples of integral sequences arising from inversion sets.
We have chosen to include them because they illustrate the applicability of our methods and results and
because some of the sequences already appear in the OEIS in different contexts. In particular, we propose
an analog of Catalan numbers related to root systems of type
\mathbb{D}
(the analog related to root
systems of types
\mathbb{B}
and
\mathbb{C}
already appeared in
[
D-W
]
).

10.1.

Recall that a decomposition

of the

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{n-4}\sum_{i=0}^{j}a_{i}a_{j-i}d_{n-j-2}=\sum_{j=0}^{n-4}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{j}a_{i}a_{j-i}\right)d_{n-j-2}=\sum_{j=0}^{n-4}a_{j+1}d_{n-j-2}\ .

Therefore

\displaystyle d_{n}=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n-2}a_{i-1}d_{n-i}+2a_{n-1}+\sum_{i=1}^{n-3}\sum_{j=i+1}^{n-2}a_{i-1}a_{j-i-1}d_{n-j}+\sum_{i=2}^{n-2}a_{i-1}a_{n-i-1}+\sum_{i=2}^{n-2}a_{i-1}a_{n-i-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a_{0}d_{n-1}+\sum_{i=2}^{n-2}a_{i-1}d_{n-i}+2a_{n-1}+\sum_{j=0}^{n-4}a_{j+1}d_{n-j-2}+2\sum_{i=2}^{n-2}a_{i-1}a_{n-i-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d_{n-1}+\sum_{i=0}^{n-4}a_{i+1}d_{n-i-2}+2a_{n-1}+\sum_{j=0}^{n-4}a_{j+1}d_{n-j-2}+2\sum_{i=2}^{n-2}a_{i-1}a_{n-i-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d_{n-1}+2\sum_{i=0}^{n-4}a_{i+1}d_{n-i-2}+2a_{n-1}+2\sum_{i=1}^{n-3}a_{i}a_{n-i-2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d_{n-1}-2a_{n-2}d_{1}-2a_{n-1}d_{0}+2\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}a_{i}d_{n-i-1}+2a_{n-1}-2a_{0}a_{n-2}-2a_{n-2}a_{0}+2\sum_{i=0}^{n-2}a_{i}a_{n-i-2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d_{n-1}+2\sum_{i=0}^{n-2}a_{i+1}d_{n-i-2}-6a_{n-2}+2\sum_{i=0}^{n-2}a_{i}a_{n-i-2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d_{n-1}+2\sum_{i=0}^{n-2}a_{i+1}d_{n-i-2}+2a_{n-1}-6a_{n-2}\ .

The first few values of the sequence
(d_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
are: 1, 1, 5, 19, 69, 249.

The generating function
D(z):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}d_{n}z^{n}
of this sequence is

D(z)=\frac{2z^{2}-5z+2+(3z-1)\sqrt{1-4z}}{z+\sqrt{1-4z}}\ .

10.2.
Primitive inversion sets

In Table 1 are some data on the number of primitive inversion sets. Albert, Atkinson and Klazar
gave a generating function, defined recursively for the
\mathbb{A}_{n}
case (see
[
AAK
]
).

We wish to count the number of primitive inversion sets in
some QRS
R
. Using the canonical form of an inflation we may
relate the number of inversion sets in
R
to the number
of primitive inversion sets in a quotient of
R
.

Given a QRS
R
we write
\Inv(R)
to denote the number of inversion sets in
R
,
\Pr(R)
to denote the number of
primitive inversion sets in
R
and
\D(R)
to denote the number of
inversions sets in
R
of full support. (Note that in Section
10.1
above we
introduced the notation
\Pi(R)
to denote one-half of
the number of primitive inversion sets in
R
i.e.,
\Pr(R)=2\Pi(R)
.)
Here

\Inv(\emptyset)=\D(\emptyset)=1
.

It is clear that
if
R=R^{1}\times R^{2}
is reducible then

\Inv(R)=\Inv(R^{1})\Inv(R^{2})
and
\D(R)=\D(R^{1})\D(R^{2})
.
Thus it will be sufficient to consider irreducible QRSs.

Since every inversion set has full support in exactly one subsystem of R, i.e.,
for exactly one choice of
I
, we have

\Inv(R)=\sum_{\emptyset\subseteq I\subseteq S}\D(R_{I})\ .

Proposition 10.3
.

\Inv(R)=\sum_{\emptyset\subseteq I\subsetneq S}\left(\Inv(R_{I})\Pr(R/I)+2\D(R_{I})\right)\ .

Proof.

Let
\phi\subseteq R
be an inversion set and write

\phi=\mathrm{inf}_{I}^{S}(\Psi,X)
for the canonical form of
\phi
.
If
\psi
is primitive then
X
is any inversion set contained
in
I
. There are
\Pr(R/I)\Inv(R_{I})
such inversion sets.
Otherwise either
\psi=(R/I)^{+}
or
\psi=\emptyset
.
One of these
two possibilities occurs for
\phi
and the other for
\phi^{c}
.
If
\psi=\emptyset
then
\operatorname{supp}X=I
and thus there are

D(I)
choices for
X
. Accounting for
both
\phi
and
\phi^{c}
we see that there are
2D(I)

inversion sets inflated from
I
with
\psi
not primitive.
∎

Isolating one term in each of the above equations we have

\D(R)=\Inv(R)-\sum_{\emptyset\subseteq I\subsetneq S}\D(R_{I})

and

\Pr(R)=\Inv(R)-2-\sum_{\emptyset\subsetneq I\subsetneq S}\left(\Inv(R_{I})\Pr(R/I)+2\D(R_{I})\right)\ .

Using the above two equations we may recursively solve for

\D(R_{I})
and
\Pr(R/I)
for every
I
if we know
each
\Inv(R_{I})
. The difficulty lies in working with
subsystems of quotients. Fortunately we have the
following isomorphism of QRSs which allows us to replace
a subsystem of a quotient by a quotient of a subsystem.

Lemma 10.4
.

Assume that
I
and
J
are disjoint subsets of
S
. Then

(R/I)_{\overline{J}}\cong\left(R_{I\cup J}\right)/I\ ,

where
\overline{J}\subseteq S/I
is the image of
J
under the projection
S\twoheadrightarrow S/I
.

Proof.

Set
K:=(I\cup J)^{c}
and let

I=\{\theta_{1}^{\prime},\dots,\theta_{l}^{\prime}\}\ ,\quad J=\{\theta_{1}^{\prime\prime},\dots,\theta_{m}^{\prime\prime}\}\ ,\quad K=\{\theta_{1}^{\prime\prime\prime},\dots,\theta_{s}^{\prime\prime\prime}\}\ .

For
\gamma\in R
we denote the image of
\gamma
under the surjection
R\twoheadrightarrow R/I
by
\bar{\gamma}
.
Then

R/I=\{\bar{\gamma}=\sum_{j}a_{j}^{\prime\prime}\bar{\theta}_{j}^{\prime\prime}+\sum_{k}a_{k}^{\prime\prime\prime}\bar{\theta}_{k}^{\prime\prime\prime}\,|\,{\text{ there is }}\gamma=\sum_{i}a_{i}^{\prime}\theta_{i}^{\prime}+\sum_{j}a_{j}^{\prime\prime}\theta_{j}^{\prime\prime}+\sum_{k}a_{k}^{\prime\prime\prime}\theta_{k}^{\prime\prime\prime}\in R\}

and hence

(R/I)_{\overline{J}}=\{\bar{\gamma}=\sum_{j}a_{j}^{\prime\prime}\bar{\theta}_{j}^{\prime\prime}\,|\,{\text{ there is }}\gamma=\sum_{i}a_{i}^{\prime}\theta_{i}^{\prime}+\sum_{j}a_{j}^{\prime\prime}\theta_{j}^{\prime\prime}\in R\}\ .

Similarly,

R_{I\cup J}=\{\gamma\in R\,|\,\gamma=\sum_{i}a_{i}^{\prime}\theta_{i}^{\prime}+\sum_{j}a_{j}^{\prime\prime}\theta_{j}^{\prime\prime}\}

and hence

(R_{I\cup J})/I=\{\bar{\gamma}=\sum_{j}a_{j}^{\prime\prime}\bar{\theta}_{j}^{\prime\prime}\,|\,{\text{ there is }}\gamma\in R_{I\cup J},\gamma=\sum_{i}a_{i}^{\prime}\theta_{i}^{\prime}+\sum_{j}a_{j}^{\prime\prime}\theta_{j}^{\prime\prime}\}\ .

Comparing the expressions above, we conclude that
(R/I)_{\overline{J}}=\left(R_{I\cup J}\right)/I
.
∎

[

\Inv(R)

10.3.
Fine inversion sets

Next we study some statistics concerning fine inversion sets, i.e., inversion sets which contain
only one simple root.

Let
\Delta
be an irreducible root system and
let
S
be the fixed base of
\Delta
.
Let
\Phi=\mathrm{inf}_{I}^{S}(\Psi,X)
be an inversion set. Since

|\Phi\cap S|=|\Psi\cap(S/I)|+|X\cap I|\ ,

\Phi
is fine if and only if one of the sets
\Psi
and
X
is fine and the other one is empty.
Let
S^{\prime}:=\operatorname{supp}\Phi
and let
\Delta^{\prime}
be the subsystem of
\Delta
generated by
S^{\prime}
. Since
X

is empty or fine, we conclude that
\Delta^{\prime}
is an irreducible subsystem of
\Delta
.
Moreover,
\Phi\subseteq\Delta^{\prime}
and

(9)

\Phi=\mathrm{inf}_{J}^{S^{\prime}}(\Psi^{\prime},\emptyset)\ ,

where
\Psi^{\prime}
is fine and primitive or
{\mathrm{rk\,}}(\Delta^{\prime}/J)=1
and
\Psi^{\prime}=(\Delta^{\prime}/J)^{+}
. In short, we have the following.

Proposition 10.5
.

Equation (
9
) establishes a bijection between the fine inversion sets in
\Delta
and
the pairs
(\Delta^{\prime}/J,\Psi^{\prime})
, where
\Delta^{\prime}/J
is a subquotient of an irreducible subsystem
\Delta^{\prime}
of
\Delta

and
\Psi^{\prime}\subseteq(\Delta^{\prime}/J)^{+}
is fine and primitive or
{\mathrm{rk\,}}(\Delta^{\prime}/J)=1
and
\Psi^{\prime}=(\Delta^{\prime}/J)^{+}
.

\square

Proposition
10.5
reduces any problem of counting fine inversion sets in
\Delta

to counting fine and primitive inversion sets in (irreducible) subquotients of
\Delta
. With the exception of
root systems of type
\mathbb{A}
, see Remark
10.7
, the latter problem is difficult.
Restricting our attention to fine inversion sets which occur in a fine decomposition simplifies the problem
because it imposes the additional condition

{\mathrm{rk\,}}(\Delta^{\prime}/J)\leqslant 2

to the pairs
(\Delta^{\prime}/J,\Psi^{\prime})
in Proposition
10.5
,
cf. Proposition
10.1
.
Hence, to count the fine inversion sets which occur in a fine decomposition, we need to
understand all rank 2 subquotients of
\Delta
. If
\Delta
is a root system
and
R
is an irreducible rank 2 subquotient of
\Delta
, then
R^{+}
is one of the following three sets:

\{\alpha,\beta,\alpha+\beta\},\quad\{\alpha,\beta,\alpha+\beta,\alpha+2\beta\},\quad\{\alpha,\beta,\alpha+\beta,\alpha+2\beta,2\alpha+2\beta\}\ .

If
\Delta
is a root system, Propositions
10.1

and
10.5
combined
with the discussion above, allow us to count the inversions sets that occur in at least one fine decomposition
of
\Delta^{+}
sorted by the respective simple roots they contain as well as the number of such inversion sets which,
in addition, contain the highest root of
\Delta
. We leave the details to the interested reader and provide the results below.
The simple roots
\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{n}
of
\Delta
are labeled as in
[
B
]
.

If
\Delta
is of type
\mathbb{A}_{n}
, let
a_{n,k}
denote the number of fine
inversion sets that occur in a fine decomposition and contain the root
\theta_{k}
and let

\tilde{a}_{n,k}
denote the fine inversion set that, in addition, contain the highest root of
\Delta
.
Then

a_{n,k}=k(n-k+1)\quad\quad{\text{and}}\quad\quad\tilde{a}_{n,k}=1\ .

If
\Delta
is of type
\mathbb{B}_{n}

b_{n,k}
and
\tilde{b}_{n,k}

If

and

Remark 10.6
.

Note that

as one can expect since the Weyl groups of the root systems

Remark 10.7
.

If
\Delta
is of type
\mathbb{A}_{n}
, counting fine inversion sets is simpler because all irreducible
subquotients of
\Delta
are of type
\mathbb{A}
as well. Here are a few results:

(i)

The number of fine inversion sets in
\mathbb{A}_{n}
containing
\theta_{k}
is
\binom{n+1}{k}-1
.

(ii)

The number of all fine inversion sets in
\mathbb{A}_{n}
is
2^{n+2}-(n+2)
.

(iii)

If
n>1
is odd, there is one fine and primitive inversion set in
\mathbb{A}_{n}
;
if
n
is even, there are no fine and primitive inversion sets in
\mathbb{A}_{n}
.

These results can be derived either using Propositions
10.1

and
10.5
or directly by interpreting the elements of the Weyl group of
\mathbb{A}_{n}

as permutations of
1,2,\dots,n+1
, cf.
[
D-W
]
.
