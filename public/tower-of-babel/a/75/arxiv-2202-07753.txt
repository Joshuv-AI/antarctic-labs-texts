Abstract
. We consider a fully-coupled slow-fast system of McKean-Vlasov SDEs with full dependence on the slow and fast component and on the law of the slow component and derive convergence rates to its homogenized limit. We do not make periodicity assumptions, but we impose conditions on the fast motion to guarantee ergodicity. In the course of the proof we obtain related ergodic theorems and we gain results on the regularity of Poisson type of equations and of the associated Cauchy-Problem on the Wasserstein space that are of independent interest.

\displaystyle\dot{\tilde{u}}(t,x)

\displaystyle=\tilde{L}\tilde{u}(t,x)\coloneqq\tilde{\gamma}(t,x)\cdot\partial_{x}\tilde{u}(t,x)+\tilde{D}(t,x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\partial^{2}_{x}\tilde{u}(t,x),t\in(0,T],x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

\displaystyle u(0,x)

The first is that the PDE results regarding differentiability of the fundamental solution of the Cauchy problem depends on having regularity (differentiability and Hölder continuity in

\tilde{\gamma}(t,x),\tilde{D}(t,x)

\bar{\gamma}
and
\bar{D}
, which a priori may have any form of dependence on their measure argument as long as it is smooth enough. Recently, such regularity was studied in
[
32

14

and
[

through the use of Malliavin Calculus and a parametrix method respectively. However, the first two results don’t extend completely to cover the derivatives that we need since they are formed specifically for functions of the form

, and thus some extra work would be needed to use these for the required bounds on mixed derivatives in time and space of
\tilde{u}
. In addition, in the latter two, the authors are only interested in proving enough regularity in order to establish strong solutions of the Cauchy problem on Wasserstein Space (see Equation (

[

, where such regularity is studied via an alternative method using the Linear Functional Derivative, and the associated McKean-Vlasov SDE has additive noise and is posed on the torus.

The other issues are related to the following remark regarding the “flow” property of solutions to McKean-Vlasov SDEs with respect to their initial conditions:

Remark 5.2
.

It is important that we parameterize the initial condition in Equations (
1
) and (
8
) by
\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
rather than
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, since due to the dependence of the coefficients on their law, McKean-Vlasov SDEs do not enjoy the same flow property on the reals as standard SDEs. In other words, while for
\tilde{X}^{\epsilon,x}
corresponding to Equation (
1
) with all coefficients independent of
\mu
(that is, when Equation (
1
) is a standard SDE and not a McKean-Vlasov SDE) and deterministic initial condition
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\tilde{X}^{\epsilon,y}_{t}\bigg|_{y=\tilde{X}^{\epsilon,x}_{s}}=\tilde{X}^{\epsilon,x}_{s+t}
, in general
X^{\epsilon,\delta_{y}}_{t}\bigg|_{y=X^{\epsilon,\delta_{x}}_{s}}\neq X^{\epsilon,\delta_{x}}_{s+t}
, where here we mean equality in distribution. We do, however, have via strong existence and uniqueness (see Remark
2.5
) that
X^{\epsilon,\bar{\nu}}_{t}\bigg|_{\bar{\nu}=\mathcal{L}(X^{\epsilon,\nu}_{s})}=X^{\epsilon,\nu}_{s+t}
for all
\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. In other words, the flow property for solutions of the McKean-Vlasov SDE holds viewed as a flow of measures, not as a flow on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

To illustrate why one cannot hope to have a flow property on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we note that for a random variable
\eta\sim\nu
,
X^{\epsilon,\delta_{x}}_{t}\bigg|_{x=\eta}\neq X^{\epsilon,\nu}_{t}
. This becomes evident by setting all coefficients equal to
0
but
c
, and letting
c(x,y,\mu)=c(\mu)=\langle\mu,\phi\rangle
for some deterministic, non-constant
\phi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then we no longer have
\epsilon
dependence, and

\displaystyle X^{\delta_{x}}_{t}

\displaystyle=x+\int_{0}^{t}\langle\mathcal{L}(X^{\delta_{x}}_{s}),\phi\rangle ds

\displaystyle=x+\int_{0}^{t}\langle\delta_{X^{\delta_{x}}_{s}},\phi\rangle ds

\displaystyle=x+\int_{0}^{t}\phi(X^{\delta_{x}}_{s})ds

is deterministic, with

\displaystyle X^{\delta_{x}}_{t}\bigg|_{x=\eta}=\eta+\int_{0}^{t}\phi(X^{\delta_{x}}_{s})\bigg|_{x=\eta}ds,

while

\displaystyle X^{\nu}_{t}

\displaystyle=\eta+\int_{0}^{t}\langle\mathcal{L}(X^{\nu}_{s}),\phi\rangle ds

\displaystyle=\eta+\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}[\phi(X^{\nu}_{s})]ds,

so

\displaystyle X^{\delta_{x}}_{t}\bigg|_{x=\eta}=X^{\nu}_{t}\Leftrightarrow\int_{0}^{t}\phi(X^{\delta_{x}}_{s})\bigg|_{x=\eta}ds=\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}[\phi(X^{\nu}_{s})]ds,

the left of which is a random variable and the right of which is deterministic. Thus, this is requiring that
\eta
be deterministic to hold in general. Thus, via the aforementioned flow property on measures,
X^{\epsilon,\delta_{x}}_{s+t}=X^{\epsilon,\nu}_{t}\bigg|_{\nu=\mathcal{L}(X^{\epsilon,\delta_{x}}_{s})}\neq X^{\epsilon,\delta_{y}}_{t}\bigg|_{y=X^{\epsilon,\delta_{x}}_{s}}
. See
[
26
]
p.3-4 for a good further exposition on this, and how it relates to the non-linearity of the generator of McKean-Vlasov SDEs.

This leads us to the next major disadvantage of using the standard Cauchy Problem over the Cauchy Problem on Wasserstein space for studying convergence of McKean-Vlasov SDEs. Where in the standard averaging case, we have for
\xi\sim\nu
,

\displaystyle\biggl|\mathbb{E}\biggl[\phi(\tilde{X}^{\epsilon,\xi}_{t})-\phi(\tilde{X}^{\xi}_{t})\biggr]\biggr|

\displaystyle=\biggl|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{E}\biggl[\phi(\tilde{X}^{\epsilon,x}_{t})-\phi(\tilde{X}^{x}_{t})\biggr]\nu(dx)\biggr|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\biggl|\mathbb{E}\biggl[\phi(\tilde{X}^{\epsilon,x}_{t})-\phi(\tilde{X}^{x}_{t})\biggr]\biggr|\nu(dx)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}C\epsilon|\phi|_{C^{4}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\nu(dx)

\displaystyle=C\epsilon|\phi|_{C^{4}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})},

so the result established via the standard Cauchy Problem for deterministic initial conditions
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
also holds in the case of random initial conditions. Meanwhile, for the McKean-Vlasov setting, due to the non-linearity of the Fokker-Plank equations associated to
X^{\epsilon,\nu}
and
X^{\nu}
(see p.4 in
[
26
]
):

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\biggl[\phi(X^{\epsilon,\nu}_{t})-\phi(X^{\nu}_{t})\biggr]\neq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{E}\biggl[\phi(X^{\epsilon,\delta_{x}}_{t})-\phi(X^{\delta_{x}}_{t})\biggr]\nu(dx)

in general, so the result does not have the same upshot, and a proof of convergence of
\mathcal{L}(X^{\epsilon,\delta_{x}}_{t})
to
\mathcal{L}(X^{\delta_{x}}_{t})
for each
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which is what would be obtained via the standard Cauchy problem, does not immediately lift to the case of random initial conditions.
Lastly, even assuming sufficient regularity on the coefficients to apply Itô’s formula to
\tilde{u}(t,X^{\epsilon,\delta_{x}}_{t})
, we would end up needing to control terms of the form
[\bar{\gamma}(X^{\epsilon,\delta_{x}}_{t},\mathcal{L}(X^{\delta_{y}}_{t}))|_{y=X^{\epsilon,\delta_{x}}_{t}}-\gamma(X^{\epsilon,\delta_{x}}_{t},Y^{\epsilon,\delta_{x}}_{t},\mathcal{L}(X^{\epsilon,\delta_{x}}_{t}))]\cdot\partial_{x}\tilde{u}(t,X^{\epsilon,\delta_{x}}_{t})
(see the last equation on p.1230 of
[
22

) rather than the term

[\bar{\gamma}(X^{\epsilon,\delta_{x}}_{t},\mathcal{L}(X^{\epsilon,\delta_{x}}_{t}))-\gamma(X^{\epsilon,\delta_{x}}_{t},Y^{\epsilon,\delta_{x}}_{t},\mathcal{L}(X^{\epsilon,\delta_{x}}_{t}))]\cdot\partial_{\mu}U(t,\mathcal{L}(X^{\epsilon,\delta_{x}}_{t}))[X^{\epsilon,\delta_{x}}_{t}]
. The latter is what appears when using the Cauchy-Problem on Wasserstein space (see the term

in the proof of Theorem
3.1

4.5

\bar{\gamma}(X^{\epsilon,\delta_{x}}_{t},\mathcal{L}(X^{\epsilon,\delta_{x}}_{t}))-\bar{\gamma}(X^{\epsilon,\delta_{x}}_{t},\mathcal{L}(X^{\delta_{x}}_{t}))
(note the difference in the measure argument). This is do to an effective decoupling of the evolution of the measure which appears in the coefficients
\tilde{\gamma},\tilde{D}
from the process whose generator is given by the right hand side of the equation which
\tilde{u}
satisfies. In fact, this is the very decoupling used in

37

These issues reflect that if we were going to attempt to use
\tilde{u}
as above, we would be attempting to re-frame the McKean-Vlasov SDE (

\mathbb{R}^{d}

\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Using instead the Cauchy Problem on Wasserstein space, we are “lifting” our view of the process to a deterministic evolution of a measure on
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, that is, its Law. This is reflected by the fact that in Equation (

7.
Conclusions and Future Work

In this paper we have derived an averaging principle for fully coupled McKean-Vlasov SDEs, along with an associated rate of weak convergence.

In this paper, due to limitations in the literature regarding regularity of solutions to the Cauchy Problem on Wasserstein space, we made strong assumptions on the regularity of the coefficients of Equation (
1
). It is known that under weaker assumptions, similar rates of convergence can be derived in case of fully coupled standard SDEs (without coefficients which have explicit dependence on the law of the process). See Remarks
2.6
and
3.3
for further discussion of this. An interesting avenue of future research would be to see if the method proposed in this paper can be extended to weaker assumptions via improving the existing regularity results for PDEs of the type (
10
).

Another interesting extension would be to establish an averaging principle for fully-coupled SDEs in the setting where the coefficients the fast and slow process in Equation (
1
) depend on the law of the fast process,
\mathcal{L}(Y^{\epsilon,\nu}_{t})
. In this setting, the solution of the Poisson Equation
\Phi
whose derivatives appear in the coefficients of limiting equation (
8
) will have to solve a PDE on Wasserstein Space, since the generator obtained from considering the
O(1/\epsilon^{2})
terms from the generator of
(X^{\epsilon,\nu}_{t},Y^{\epsilon,\nu}_{t})
and “freezing” the terms associated to the slow process will be that of a McKean-Vlasov SDE. See
[
36
,
38
,
53
]
for related results in this direction.

Now we show the equality (

). We have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}h(x,y,\mu_{1})-h(x,y,\mu_{2})-[\mathcal{L}_{x,\mu_{1}}-\mathcal{L}_{x,\mu_{2}}]v(x,y,\mu_{2})\pi(dy;x,\mu_{1})

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}h(x,y,\mu_{1})\pi(dy;x,\mu_{1})-\int_{\mathbb{R}}h(x,y,\mu_{2})\pi(dy;x,\mu_{2})=

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{\mathbb{R}}h(x,y,\mu_{1})-h(x,y,\mu_{2})-[\mathcal{L}_{x,\mu_{1}}-\mathcal{L}_{x,\mu_{2}}]v(x,y,\mu_{2})\pi(dy;x,\mu_{1})

and

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{L}_{x,\mu_{1}}v(x,y,\mu_{2})\pi(dy;x,\mu_{1})=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{f(x,y,\mu_{1})-f(x,y,\mu_{2})a(x,y,\mu_{1})/a(x,y,\mu_{2})}{a(x,y,\mu_{2})}\frac{\pi(y;x,\mu_{1})}{\pi(y;x,\mu_{2})}\times

\displaystyle\hskip 113.81102pt\times\biggl[\int_{-\infty}^{y}h(x,\bar{y},\mu_{2})\pi(\bar{y};x,\mu_{2})d\bar{y}-\bar{h}(x,\mu_{2})\int_{-\infty}^{y}\pi(\bar{y};x,\mu_{2})d\bar{y}\biggr]dy

Lemma A.5
.

Consider
B\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}B(x,y,\mu)\pi(dy;x,\mu)=0,\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

Suppose that for some complete collection of multi-indices
\bm{\zeta}
that
B\in\mathcal{M}_{p}^{\bm{\zeta}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d});\mathbb{R}),f\in\mathcal{M}_{p}^{\bm{\zeta}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d});\mathbb{R}^{d}),a\in\mathcal{M}_{p}^{\bm{\zeta}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d});\mathbb{R}^{d\times d})
.
Then for the unique classical solution
u\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
to

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{x,\mu}u(x,y,\mu)=B(x,y,\mu)

such that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u(x,y,\mu)\pi(dy;x,\mu)=0
, and

has at most polynomial growth as
|y|\rightarrow\infty
(which exists by Lemma
A.1
):
u,\partial_{y_{i}}u,\partial_{y_{i}}\partial_{y_{j}}u\in\mathcal{M}_{p}^{\bm{\zeta}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d});\mathbb{R})

.

Proof.

The proof essentially uses the same tools and a similar method to Lemma
A.2
here and Theorem 2.1 Step 3 in
[
22
]
, so we will only check this in the case for
(n,l,\bm{\beta})=(0,1,0)
and then comment on how the rest of the terms follow. Importantly, Lemma
A.2
only assumes existence and polynomial growth of derivatives up to one order less than the derivative we obtain from Equation (
35
).

The result for
(n,l,\bm{\beta})=(0,0,0)
is just another way of writing Lemma
A.1
, once we establish continuity of
u,\partial_{y}u,\partial^{2}_{y}u
in
x,\mu

[
22
]
.

We first note that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{x,\mu_{1}}[u(x,y,\mu_{1})-u(x,y,\mu_{2})]

\displaystyle=B(x,y,\mu_{1})-\mathcal{L}_{x,\mu_{1}}u(x,y,\mu_{2})

\displaystyle=B(x,y,\mu_{1})-B(x,y,\mu_{2})-[\mathcal{L}_{x,\mu_{1}}-\mathcal{L}_{x,\mu_{2}}]u(x,y,\mu_{2}).

By the equality (
A.2
) from Lemma
A.2
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}B(x,y,\mu_{1})-B(x,y,\mu_{2})-[\mathcal{L}_{x,\mu_{1}}-\mathcal{L}_{x,\mu_{2}}]u(x,y,\mu_{2})\pi(dy,x,\mu_{1})=

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}B(x,y,\mu_{1})\pi(dy;x,\mu_{1})-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}B(x,y,\mu_{2})\pi(dy;x,\mu_{2})=0,

so in fact the inhomogeneity in the above Poisson equation is centered. Now, via the assumptions on
B,f,a
and the fact that the bound on the growth of
u
in Lemma
A.1
depends linearly on the local Hölder semi-norm of the Poisson Equation’s inhomogeneity as per the Proof of Theorem 2.1 i) in
[
22
]
, we have:

\displaystyle|u(x,y,\mu_{1})-u(x,y,\mu_{2})|

\displaystyle\leq C\mathbb{W}_{2}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})(1+|y|)^{p}

\displaystyle|\partial_{y}u(x,y,\mu_{1})-\partial_{y}u(x,y,\mu_{2})|

\displaystyle\leq C\mathbb{W}_{2}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})(1+|y|)^{p^{\prime}}

\displaystyle|\partial^{2}_{y}u(x,y,\mu_{1})-\partial^{2}_{y}u(x,y,\mu_{2})|

\displaystyle\leq C\mathbb{W}_{2}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})(1+|y|)^{p^{\prime\prime}},

for some

and

.

The proof with
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}
replaced by
x_{1},x_{2}
follows in the same way, and thus the desired continuity in
x,\mathbb{W}_{2}
is established.

To obtain continuity of and a rate of polynomial growth for

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{x,\mu}D^{(0,1,0)}u(x,y,\mu)

\displaystyle=D^{(0,1,0)}B(x,y,\mu)-D^{(0,1,0)}f(x,y,\mu)\cdot\partial_{y}u(x,y,\mu)-D^{(0,1,0)}a(x,y,\mu)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\partial^{2}_{y}u(x,y,\mu)

\displaystyle=D^{(0,1,0)}B(x,y,\mu)-\mathcal{L}^{(0,1,0)}_{x,\mu}u(x,y,\mu)

A.2

in Lemma

\displaystyle=D^{(0,1,0)}\biggl[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}B(x,y,\mu)\pi(dy;x,\mu)\biggr]=0,

and the inhomogeneity of the elliptic PDE that

solves in fact obeys the centering condition, and since we already know that

applies. This establishes that

, and
D^{(0,1,0)}u
in the place of

.

\displaystyle=D^{(1,0,0)}B(x,y,\mu)[z_{1}]-\mathcal{L}^{(1,0,0)}_{x,\mu}[z_{1}]u(x,y,\mu)

\displaystyle-\biggl[D^{(1,0,0)}B(x,y,\mu)[z_{2}]-\mathcal{L}^{(1,0,0)}_{x,\mu}[z_{2}]u(x,y,\mu)\biggr].

35

All of the remaining bounds work in the same way, with the inhomogeneity of the elliptic PDE of the desired derivative of

solves being the integrand of the expression for the corresponding derivative of

from Equation (

A.2

(41)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{x,\mu}D^{(n,l,\bm{\beta})}u(x,y,\mu)[z_{1},...,z_{n}]

\mu

Lemma A.6
.

Suppose that for some complete collection of multi-indices
\bm{\zeta}
that

For the Lipschitz property when

, we have via Equations (
A.2
) and (
A.2
), using the polynomial growth in
y
of

and
\partial^{2}_{y}v

\displaystyle\leq C

from the fact that

In addition, from Equation (

\mu_{1},\mu_{2}
.

We say
u
is
L-differentiable
or
Lions-differentiable
at
\mu_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
if there exists a random variable
X_{0}
on some
(\tilde{\Omega},\tilde{\mathcal{F}},\tilde{\mathbb{P}})
satisfying the above assumptions such that
\mathcal{L}(X_{0})=\mu_{0}
and
\tilde{u}
is Fréchet differentiable at
X_{0}
.

The Fréchet derivative of
\tilde{u}
can be viewed as an element of
L^{2}(\tilde{\Omega},\tilde{\mathcal{F}},\tilde{\mathbb{P}};\mathbb{R}^{d})
by identifying
L^{2}(\tilde{\Omega},\tilde{\mathcal{F}},\tilde{\mathbb{P}};\mathbb{R}^{d})
and its dual. From this, one can find that if
u
is L-differentiable at
\mu_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, there is a deterministic measurable function
\xi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
D\tilde{u}(X_{0})=\xi(X_{0})
, and that
\xi
is uniquely defined
\mu_{0}
-almost everywhere on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We denote this equivalence class of
\xi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mu_{0};\mathbb{R}^{d})
by
\partial_{\mu}u(\mu_{0})
and call
\partial_{\mu}u(\mu_{0})[\cdot]\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
the
Lions derivative
of
u
at
\mu_{0}
. Note that this definition is independent of the choice of
X_{0}
and
(\tilde{\Omega},\tilde{\mathcal{F}},\tilde{\mathbb{P}})
. See
[
5
]
Section 5.2.

To avoid confusion when
u
depends on more variables than just
\mu
, if
\partial_{\mu}u(\mu_{0})
is differentiable at
z_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we denote its derivative at
v_{0}
by
\partial_{z}\partial_{\mu}u(\mu_{0})[z_{0}]
.

Definition B.2
.

(
[
5
]
Definition 5.83) We say
u\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
is
Fully

\mathbf{C^{2}}
if the following conditions are satisfied:

(1)

u
is
C^{1}
in the sense of L-differentiation, and its first derivative has a jointly continuous version
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\mathbb{R}^{d})\ni(\mu,z)\mapsto\partial_{\mu}u(\mu)[z]\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

(2)

For each fixed
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the version of
\mathbb{R}^{d}\ni z\mapsto\partial_{\mu}u(\mu)[z]\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
from the first condition is differentiable on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
in the classical sense and its derivative is given by a jointly continuous function
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\mathbb{R}^{d})\ni(\mu,z)\mapsto\partial_{z}\partial_{\mu}u(\mu)[z]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
.

(3)

For each fixed
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the version of
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\ni\mu\mapsto\partial_{\mu}u(\mu)[z]\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
in the first condition is continuously L-differentiable component-by-component, with a derivative given by a function
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\ni(\mu,z,\bar{z})\mapsto\partial^{2}_{\mu}u(\mu)[z][\bar{z}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
such that for any
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
X\in L^{2}(\tilde{\Omega},\tilde{\mathcal{F}},\tilde{\mathbb{P}};\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\mathcal{L}(X)=\mu

\partial^{2}_{\mu}u(\mu)[z][X]
gives the Fréchet derivative at
X
of

for every

. Denoting
\partial^{2}_{\mu}u(\mu)[z][\bar{z}]

\partial^{2}_{\mu}u(\mu)[z,\bar{z}]

\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\ni(\mu,z,\bar{z})\mapsto\partial^{2}_{\mu}u(\mu)[z,\bar{z}]
