Classical gases with singular densities
Luca Di Persio, Yuri Kondratiev, Viktorya Vardanyan

Abstract
We study classical continuous systems with singular distributions of velocities. Radon measures with the infinite mass give these distributions. Positions of particles in such systems are no longer usual configurations in the location space, leading to the necessity of developing new analytical tools to study considered models.



Keywords:
Classical gases; Poisson point process; Correlation functions; Discrete measures

1
Introduction

Let
\Gamma(\mathbb{R}_{0}^{d}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
denote the space of configurations for an interacting particle system, where each particle is described by a pair
(v_{x},x)
, with
v_{x}\in\mathbb{R}_{0}^{d}=\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
representing the velocity and
x\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
the position. The phase space is then
\mathbb{R}_{0}^{d}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
, and locally finite subsets of this space give the configurations. Specifically, a configuration
\gamma\in\Gamma(\mathbb{R}_{0}^{d}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
is a set of pairs
\gamma=\{(v_{x},x)\}\subset\mathbb{R}_{0}^{d}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
, where each particle is characterized by its velocity
v_{x}
and position
x
.

We focus on a subset of configurations, denoted
\Gamma_{p}(\mathbb{R}_{0}^{d}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
, called
pinpointed configurations
. A configuration is pinpointed if no two particles are positioned at the same location in
{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
unless they have the same velocity, i.e., each position
x
in the configuration corresponds to at most one velocity
v_{x}
.

Further, we define the
Plato space
, denoted
\Pi(\mathbb{R}_{0}^{d}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
, as the subset of
\Gamma_{p}(\mathbb{R}_{0}^{d}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
consisting of configurations where the total velocity within any compact region of
{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
is bounded. This constraint ensures that the system’s behaviour remains physically plausible by avoiding the occurrence of unbounded velocities within finite spatial regions.

We define the cone of vector-valued discrete Radon measures
{\mathbb{K}}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
as a subset of the space of Radon measures
{\mathcal{M}}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})

{\mathbb{K}}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})=\bigg\{\eta=\sum_{x\in\tau(\eta)}v_{x}\delta_{x}\in{\mathcal{M}}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})\bigg|x\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}},\ v_{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\bigg\},

where the support of
\eta
is defined as

\tau(\eta)=\{x\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\;|\;\eta(\{x\})=:v_{x}(\eta)\}.

This paper is dedicated to the in-depth exploration and analysis of the cone
{\mathbb{K}}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
, which serves as a significant framework for modelling particle systems in real-world scenarios. Defining mathematical structures on
{\mathbb{K}}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
, however, presents substantial challenges. To address these, we employ the Plato space
\Pi({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
, where the elements of this space, termed *ideas*, are mapped through the reflection mapping
{\mathcal{R}}
to corresponding elements in
{\mathbb{K}}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
, which represent observed objects (i.e., images of ideas). The properties of the object space in
{\mathbb{K}}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
are intrinsically tied to the structure of the idea space in the Plato space. This conceptual framework is inspired by Plato’s theory of forms, which asserts that the observable world is a projection of a higher realm of ideal forms. The relationship between Plato space
\Pi({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
and the cone of vector-valued discrete Radon measures
{\mathbb{K}}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
, mediated by the reflection mapping, forms the basis for the analysis of
{\mathbb{K}}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
.

The nonlinearity and complexity of the infinite-dimensional spaces involved present significant challenges in directly analyzing the dynamics modelled on
\Pi({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
. To overcome this, we propose reinterpreting the equations within the finite configuration space
\Pi_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
, a subset of
\Pi({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
. This reformulation simplifies the analysis, and the
K
-transform plays a central role in enabling this transition.

In this paper, we perform harmonic analysis on the space
\Pi({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
and extend this analysis to the cone of vector-valued discrete Radon measures
{\mathbb{K}}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
using the reflection mapping
{\mathcal{R}}
. Additionally, we investigate the correlation measures and correlation functions associated with probability measures on the cone. This area of study was previously developed by Kondratiev, Lytvynov, and Vershik in
[
7
]
for the cone of positive discrete Radon measures on a Riemannian manifold, and by Finkelshtein, Kondratiev, Kuchling, Lytvynov, and Oliveira in
[
4
]
,
[
11
]
for the cone of positive discrete Radon measures on
{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
. Harmonic analysis on configuration spaces over Riemannian manifolds was developed in
[
8
]
by Kondratiev and Kuna, and we apply the corresponding approach to the cone
{\mathbb{K}}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
.

2
Vector Valued Random Discrete Measures

2.1
From Configuration Spaces to Discrete Measures

Consider the Euclidean space
{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
and the locally compact pointed
space
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}={{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\setminus\{0\}
.
Let
\Gamma({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
denote the configuration space over

{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
, i.e., the set of all locally finite subsets

\gamma=\{(v,x)\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
.
A subset is considered locally finite if it contains only finitely many points within any compact set. We equip this space with
the vague topology as described in
[
1
]
, and the corresponding
\sigma
-algebra is denoted by

{\mathcal{B}}(\Gamma({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}))
.

Within all configurations, we focus on a specific subspace
\Gamma_{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
, referred to as the space of pinpointed configurations.
By definition,
\Gamma_{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
consists of configurations

\gamma\in\Gamma({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
satisfying the condition that if
(v_{1},x_{1}),(v_{2},x_{2})\in\gamma

and
(v_{1},x_{1})=(v_{2},x_{2})
with
x_{1}=x_{2}
, then
v_{1}=v_{2}
. This means we cannot have two points with the same location in
{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
but different values in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}
. As easily seen

\Gamma_{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})\in{\mathcal{B}}(\Gamma({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}))
. For any
\gamma\in\Gamma_{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
,
we can write the configuration as

\gamma=\{(v_{x},x)\},\;\tau(\gamma)=\{x\;|\;(v_{x},x)\in\gamma\}\subset{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}},

where
\tau(\gamma)
denotes the projection of the configuration onto its spatial positions in
{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
.
We can also examine smaller subsets of configurations where
\tau(\gamma)
remains a configuration. These subsets correspond to
marked point processes
, which offer a more tractable framework for study and analysis.

For any configuration
\gamma\in\Gamma_{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
and any compact set
\Lambda\in{\mathcal{B}}_{c}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
, we define the
local velocity functional as:

V_{\Lambda}(\gamma)=\sum_{x\in\tau(\gamma)\cap\Lambda}|v_{x}|\leq\infty.

This functional measures the sum of the magnitudes of velocities associated with points in
\gamma
whose spatial positions lie within the compact set
\Lambda
. Using this functional, we define the Plato space

\Pi({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
as a subset of the pinpointed configuration space
\Gamma_{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
. Specifically,

\Pi({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})=\{\gamma\in\Gamma_{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})\;\forall\Lambda\in{\mathcal{B}}_{c}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})\;|\;V_{\Lambda}(\gamma)<\infty\}.

Let
{\mathcal{M}}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}},{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
denote the set of all vector-valued Radon measures defined on the Borel subsets
{\mathcal{B}}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
, such that

{\mathcal{M}}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}},{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})\ni\mu:{\mathcal{B}}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})\to{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}},\;\forall\Lambda\in{\mathcal{B}}_{c}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})\;\;|\mu(\Lambda)|<\infty.

We introduce a mapping from the Plato space
\Pi({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
to the space of vector-valued Radon measures:

\Pi({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})\ni\gamma=\sum_{(v_{x},x)\in\gamma}\delta_{(v_{x},x)}\to\eta={\mathcal{R}}\gamma=\sum_{x\in\tau(\gamma)}v_{x}\delta_{x}\in{\mathcal{M}}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}),

where the image of this mapping is denoted as
{\mathbb{K}}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
:

{\mathbb{K}}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})={\mathcal{R}}(\Pi({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}))\subset{\mathcal{M}}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}).

The topology of
{\mathbb{K}}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
is derived from the topology of the configuration space
\Gamma({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
.

In this framework, the points of
\Pi({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
are identified as *ideas*, while their images under the reflection mapping
\mathcal{R}:\Pi({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})\to{\mathbb{K}}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
are interpreted as *observed objects* (i.e., the mapped representations of the ideas). The structure and properties of the object space
{\mathbb{K}}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
are entirely governed by the underlying Plato space
\Pi({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
, which serves as the foundational framework for all further analyses of
{\mathbb{K}}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
.

From a physical perspective, this construction models a system of particles in the space
{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
, where each spatial position
x\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
is associated with a velocity (or mark)
v_{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}
. This representation encapsulates the particle system’s spatial configuration and dynamic behaviour.

2.2
Poisson Measure on
\Gamma({\mathbb{R}}_{0}^{d}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})

The classical ideal gas is described by a pair
(v_{x},x)
where
x\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
represents the position and
v_{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{0}^{d}={{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\setminus\{0\}
represents the velocity of a particle. The phase space of the gas is then given by
{\mathbb{R}}_{0}^{d}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
. Microscopic states of the gas are represented by phase space configurations, which are locally finite subsets of
{\mathbb{R}}_{0}^{d}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
. The space of such configurations is denoted by:

\Gamma({\mathbb{R}}_{0}^{d}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})\ni\gamma=\{(v_{x},x)\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}_{0}^{d}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}.

A macroscopic state of the gas shall be a probability measure on
\Gamma({\mathbb{R}}_{0}^{d}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
. For the ideal gas, such a measure is a Poisson point measure.
Let us take a Radon measure
\lambda
on
{\mathbb{R}}_{0}^{d}
and a non-atomic Lebesgue measure
m
on
{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
. Consider an intensity measure

\lambda\otimes m
on
\Gamma({\mathbb{R}}_{0}^{d}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
and the Poisson measure
\pi_{\lambda\otimes m}
on
\Gamma({\mathbb{R}}_{0}^{d}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
.
The following Laplace transform may characterize that measure:

\int_{\Gamma({\mathbb{R}}_{0}^{d}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}\exp(\sum_{(v_{x},x)\in\gamma}\psi(v_{x},x))\pi_{\lambda\otimes m}(d\gamma)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{0}^{d}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}(\exp\psi(v_{x},x)-1)\lambda(dv)m(dx)

for any continuous
\psi
with bounded support.
The support of this measure is essentially smaller and may be restricted to the pinpointed configurations:

\gamma=\{(v_{x},x)\},\;\tau(\gamma)=\{x\;|\;(v_{x},x)\in\gamma\}\subset{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}.

Note that the set
\tau(\gamma)
, in general, is a discrete subset of
{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
but is not
locally finite, i.e., is not a configuration in
{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
.

To have a physically relevant property of finite local energy,
we need to assume an additional property of the measure
\lambda
.
Namely, we consider measures with finite first moments

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{0}^{d}}|v|\lambda(dv)<\infty.

This situation will be at the centre of our considerations.

Note that in the case of finite measures
\lambda
, the Poisson measure

\pi_{\lambda\otimes m}
may be considered in the framework of marked point
processes
[
6
]
. The latter allows for an easier technical analysis.

We are interested in defining the following mapping:

\gamma\mapsto\eta=\sum_{x\in\tau(\gamma)}v_{x}\delta_{x}

which is a transformation mapping a configuration
\gamma
to a discrete vector-valued Radon measure on
{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
. The space of such measures, which becomes random when
\gamma
is random, exhibits rich mathematical structures involving complex analysis and geometry elements. For the simpler case of real-valued discrete measures, refer to
[
7
]
.

The scenario most analogous to the classical case in statistical physics arises with probability measures
\lambda
, particularly when dealing with Maxwellian (Gaussian) distributions. In these cases, integrating concerning velocities yields a Poisson distribution
\pi_{m}
on the configuration space of positions
\Gamma({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
. However, this result is unattainable when the intensity measure satisfies
\lambda({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})=\infty
. The distinction between these two cases is fundamental, as the properties of the velocity distribution play a critical role in determining the spatial structure of the model in the case of an infinite measure
\lambda
.”

2.3
Measures on
{\mathbb{K}}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})

Probability measures on the space of compact subsets
{\mathbb{K}}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
are fundamental tools for modelling various phenomena in mathematical physics, biology, and the representation theory of infinite-dimensional groups. Our objective is to construct such measures by utilizing the reflection map
{\mathcal{R}}
applied to carefully selected measures on the Plato space
\Pi({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
. To exemplify this method, we begin with elementary cases related to Poisson measures. Specifically, we first define a non-atomic Radon measure
\lambda
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}
that satisfies the following conditions:

\lambda({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0})=\infty,

\forall n\in{\mathbb{N}}\;\;\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}}|v|^{n}\lambda(dv)<\infty.

As a concrete example, we take

\lambda(dv)=\frac{1}{|v|^{d}}e^{-|v|^{2}}dv,

which represents a modified version of the Maxwell distribution for velocities in statistical physics, incorporating a singularity at
v=0
. A broader class of such measures can be expressed in the following form

\lambda(dv)=\frac{1}{|v|^{\alpha}}e^{-|v|^{\beta}}dv,

where
\alpha\in[d,d+1)
and
\beta>0
. These generalizations allow for flexibility in modelling systems with different velocity distributions.

Let
m(dx)
denote a non-atomic Lebesgue measure on
{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
, and define the intensity measure:

\sigma(dv,dx)=\lambda(dv)m(dx)

on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
. This measure combines the velocity distribution
\lambda
with the spatial measure
m
.

For any
\Lambda\in{\mathcal{B}}_{c}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
we have the following disjoint decomposition:

\Gamma(\Lambda)=\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty}\Gamma^{n}(\Lambda),

where
\Gamma(\Lambda):=\{\gamma\in\Gamma({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}):\gamma\subset\Lambda\}
is the set of all configurations supported in
\Lambda
and
\Gamma^{n}(\Lambda):=\{\gamma\in\Gamma(\Lambda):|\gamma|=n\}
is the set of
n
-point configurations. The Lebesgue-Poisson measure on
\Gamma(\Lambda)
is given by:

\mathcal{L}_{\sigma}:=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\sigma^{(n)},

where
\sigma^{(n)}
is the symmetric product measure on
\Gamma^{n}(\Lambda)
, defines as
\sigma^{(n)}:=(\lambda\otimes m)^{\otimes n}\circ sym_{n}^{-1}
. Poisson measure on
\Gamma(\Lambda)
is defined as
\pi_{\sigma}^{\Lambda}:=e^{-\sigma(\Lambda)}\mathcal{L}_{\sigma}
.

There is a standard definition of a Poisson measure

\pi_{\sigma}

on
\Gamma({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
, therefore we can proceed as follows: for any
\psi\in C_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
(continuous functions with compact supports) define

<\psi,\gamma>=\sum_{(v,x)\in\gamma}\psi(v,x),\;\;\gamma\in\Gamma({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}).

The Poisson measure
\pi_{\sigma}
is defined via its Laplace transform:

\int_{\Gamma({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}e^{<\psi,\gamma>}\pi_{\sigma}(d\gamma)=\exp{\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}(e^{\psi(v,x)}-1)}\lambda(dv)m(dx).

As a consequence of this definition, we have

\int_{\Gamma({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}<\psi,\gamma>\pi_{\sigma}(d\gamma)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}\psi(v,x)\lambda(dv)m(dx)

and this relation can be extended to the case of any
\psi\in L^{1}(\lambda\otimes m)
.

Lemma 2.1
.

The Poisson measure
\pi_{\sigma}
in concentrated on
\Pi({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
, i.e.,

\pi_{\sigma}(\Pi({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}))=1.

Proof.

Take any
\Lambda\in{\mathcal{B}}_{c}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
and define

\psi(v,x)=|v|1_{\Lambda}(x).

Then,

\int_{\Gamma({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}\sum_{(v,x)\in\gamma}|v|1_{\Lambda}(x)\pi_{\sigma}(d\gamma)=m(\Lambda)\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{0}^{d}}|v|\lambda(dv)<\infty.

It means that
V_{\Lambda}<\infty

\pi_{\sigma}
-a.s.,
i.e.,
\pi_{\sigma}(\Pi({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}))=1
.

∎

To obtain measures on
{\mathbb{K}}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
, we can employ the
pushforward
of measures on
\Pi(R^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
through the mapping
{\mathcal{R}}
. One crucial step is demonstrating the measurable structures’ compatibility on
\Pi(oR^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
and
{\mathbb{K}}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
.
Having already established the connection between
{\mathbb{K}}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
and
\Pi(R^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
, it is natural to explore further the relationship induced by the mapping

{\mathcal{R}}
. For instance, one could examine the relationship between the

\sigma
-algebras
{\mathcal{B}}(\Pi(R^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}))
and
{\mathcal{B}}({\mathbb{K}}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}))
.

Theorem 2.2
.

The image
\sigma
-algebra of
{\mathcal{B}}(\Pi(R^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}))
under
{\mathcal{R}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}({\mathbb{K}}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}))
coincide, i.e.,

{\mathcal{B}}({\mathbb{K}}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}))=\left\{{\mathcal{R}}(A\cap\Pi(R^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}))\mid A\in{\mathcal{B}}(\Gamma(R^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}))\right\}.

Proof.

The proof follows directly from the topological considerations presented in
[
7
]
for the case of real-valued discrete measures.

∎

Denote
\mu_{\lambda}
the image measure on
{\mathbb{K}}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
under
the reflection map corresponding to
\pi_{\sigma}
.
For
h\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
and
\phi\in C_{0}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
introduce a function

L_{h,\phi}:{\mathbb{K}}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})\to{\mathbb{R}}
via:

L_{h,\phi}(\eta)=<h\otimes\phi,\eta>=\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}\phi(x)<h,\eta(dx)>=\sum_{x\in\tau(\eta)}\phi(x)<h,v_{x}>.

The definition of the Poisson measure implies

\int_{{\mathbb{K}}}e^{<h\otimes\phi,\eta>}\mu_{\lambda}(d\eta)=\exp(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}(e^{<h,v>\phi(x)}-1)\lambda(dv)m(dx)).

Define the function:

\Phi_{\lambda}^{h}(r)=\exp({\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}}(e^{<h,v>r}-1)\lambda(dv)}),\;\;r\in{\mathbb{R}},

within the integral can be rewritten as

\int_{{\mathbb{K}}}e^{<h\otimes\phi,\eta>}\mu_{\lambda}(d\eta)=e^{\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}}\log(\Phi_{\lambda}^{h}(\phi(x)))m(dx)}.

The definition of the measure
\mu_{\lambda}

through its Laplace transform can be
understood in terms of the equation above.

3
Harmonic Analysis on
\Pi({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})

The inherent nonlinearity of infinite-dimensional spaces presents significant challenges in directly analyzing the dynamics formulated on
\Pi({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
. To address this, we propose reformulating the equations within the space of finite configurations,
\Pi_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
, which is a proper subset of
\Pi({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
. Applying the
K
-transform facilitates this reformulation. A comparable methodology was utilized by Kondratiev and Kuna in
[
8
]
for conducting harmonic analysis on configuration spaces over Riemannian manifolds.

3.1
The
K
-Transform

In what follows, we will introduce auxiliary space connected to the Plato space
\Pi({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
via the
K
-transform. Moreover we will show relations between functions on
\Pi({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
and
\Pi_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
.

Definition 3.1
.

The Plato space of finite configurations
\Pi_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
is defined as:

\Pi_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}):=\{\gamma\in\Pi({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})\mid|\gamma|<\infty\},

where
|\cdot|
denotes the number of elements in a set. Its topology is induced by the set
\Gamma_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
.

The spaces
\Pi({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
and
\Pi_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
play completely different roles.
As mentioned before,
\Pi({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
represents the space of ideas, whereas
\Pi_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
is regarded as a mathematical construct that exists alongside
\Pi({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
. Moreover, these spaces are also topologically different.

We will introduce
n
-point configurations, which are used to decompose the space.

Definition 3.2
.

1.

For
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, the set of
n
-point configurations is defined as:

\Pi_{0}^{(n)}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}):=\left\{\gamma\in\Pi_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})\colon|\gamma|=n\right\}.

2.

For a set
\Lambda\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
, the set of all configurations supported in
\Lambda
is defined as:

\Pi_{0}(\Lambda):=\left\{\gamma\in\Pi_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})\colon\gamma\subset\Lambda\right\}.

3.

A Borel set
A\subset\Pi_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
is called bounded if there exists
\Lambda\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
compact and
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that

A\subset\bigcup_{n=0}^{N}\Pi_{0}^{(n)}(\Lambda).

Denote the system of all such sets by
{\mathcal{B}}_{b}(\Pi_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}))
.

We have the following decompositions:

\Pi_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})=\bigsqcup_{n=0}^{\infty}\Pi_{0}^{(n)}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})=\bigcup_{\Lambda\in{\mathcal{B}}_{c}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}\Pi_{0}(\Lambda),

where the first union is disjoint and
{\mathcal{B}}_{c}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
denotes all Borel subsets of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
with compact closure.

To introduce the
K
-transform between
\Pi_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
and
\Pi({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
, we first need to define well-defined classes of functions on which this transform can be applied. Additionally, we will introduce a specific class of measures that can extend the
K
-transform to a wider range of functions. We use the notation
B^{0}(\Pi_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}))
to represent the set of all measurable functions
G\colon\Pi_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})\to{\mathbb{R}}
.

Definition 3.3
.

1.

A function
G\colon\Pi_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})\to{\mathbb{R}}
is said to be bounded with local support if there exists
C>0
and
\Lambda\in{\mathcal{B}}_{c}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
such that the following estimate holds for all
\eta\in{\mathbb{K}}_{0}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
:

|G(\gamma)|\leq C\mathbbm{1}_{\Pi_{0}(\Lambda)}(\gamma).

(1)

It is important to note that this implies that
G(\gamma)=0
if
\gamma\cap\Lambda^{c}\neq\emptyset
. We denote by
B_{\mathrm{ls}}(\Pi_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}))
all measurable functions
G\colon\Pi_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})\to{\mathbb{R}}
which are bounded with local support.

2.

A function
G\colon\Pi_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})\to{\mathbb{R}}
is called bounded with bounded support, if there exists
\Lambda\in\mathcal{B}_{c}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}),N\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
C>0
such that

|G(\gamma)|\leq C\mathbbm{1}_{\Pi_{0}(\Lambda)}(\gamma)\mathbbm{1}_{\{|\gamma|\leq N\}}(\gamma),

(2)

i.e.
G(\gamma)=0
whenever
|\gamma|>N
or
\gamma\cap\Lambda^{c}\neq\emptyset
. Denote the space of all such measurable functions by
B_{\mathrm{bs}}(\Pi_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}))
. Evidently, it follows that we have
B_{\mathrm{bs}}(\Pi_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}))\subset B_{\mathrm{ls}}(\Pi_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}))
.

3.

A measure
\rho
on
\Pi_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
is called locally finite if for any
\Lambda\in{\mathcal{B}}_{c}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
and for any
m\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, the value of
\rho(\Pi_{0}^{(m)}(\Lambda))
is finite. Similarly,
\rho(A)
is finite for all bounded measurable sets
A\subset\Pi_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
. The space of all locally finite measures on
\Pi_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
is denoted by
{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathrm{lf}}(\Pi_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}))
.

In the following, we define the
K
-transform and its main properties.

Definition 3.4

(
[
8
]
)
.

Let
G\in B_{\mathrm{ls}}(\Pi_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}))
. The
K
-transform of

is the function
KG\colon\Pi({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})\to{\mathbb{R}}
defined by:

(K_{\Pi}G)(\gamma)=(KG)(\gamma):=\sum_{\xi\Subset\gamma}G(\xi),

where the inclusion
\xi\Subset\gamma
means that the sum is taken over all finite subsets of
\gamma
.

To avoid any confusion, the dependence on
\Pi
is omitted. We can see that by the definition of
B_{\mathrm{ls}}(\Pi_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}))
, the
K
-transform is well-defined on such functions.

We recall some results similar to the results which can be found in the theory of homogeneous configuration spaces
[

]
.

Proposition 3.5

(
[

]
)
.

1.

The

-transform maps functions from

\mathcal{F}L^{0}(\Pi({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}))

The

and

3.

The mapping

K^{-1}F(\gamma)=\sum_{\xi\subset\gamma}(-1)^{|\gamma\setminus\xi|}F(\xi),\ \gamma\in\Pi_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}).

4.

K
is linear and positivity preserving.

5.

If
G\in B_{\mathrm{ls}}(\Pi_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}))
and continuous, then

3.2
Correlation Measures on
\Pi_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})

Our objective is to establish categories of measures on
\Pi_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
that correspond to probability measures on
\Pi({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
, utilized to model the state of our system. This approach is based on
[
8
,
12
]
. Additionally, we will demonstrate that the group of measures on
\Pi({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
with limited local moments allows us to extend the
K
-transform to
L^{1}
-spaces. To define a measure on
\Pi_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
, we first introduce the pre-kernel
\mathcal{K}
.

Definition 3.9
.

Define the following pre-kernel based on the
K
-transform by:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{K}&\colon{\mathcal{B}}_{b}(\Pi_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}))\times\Pi({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})\to[0,\infty)\\
&(A,\gamma)\mapsto\mathcal{K}(A,\gamma):=(K\mathbbm{1}_{A})(\gamma).\end{split}

(3)

We show that
\mathcal{K}
is in fact a pre-kernel. The property
\mathcal{K}(\emptyset,\gamma)=0
for any
\gamma\in\Pi_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
is obvious. For
\sigma
-additivity, let
A_{i}\in{\mathcal{B}}_{b}(\Pi_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})),\ i\in{\mathbb{N}}
be disjoint sets such that their countable union also belongs to
{\mathcal{B}}_{b}(\Pi_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}))
. Then there exist
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
\Lambda\in{\mathcal{B}}_{c}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
such that

\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}A_{i}\subset\bigcup_{k=0}^{N}\Pi^{(k)}_{0}(\Lambda).

This implies that for
\gamma\in\Pi_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
,

\mathcal{K}\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}A_{i},\gamma\right)=\sum_{\xi\Subset\gamma}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\mathbbm{1}_{A_{i}}(\xi)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\xi\Subset\gamma\\
|\gamma|\leq N\end{subarray}}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\mathbbm{1}_{A_{i}}(\xi)\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\xi\Subset\gamma\\
|\xi|\leq N\end{subarray}}\mathbbm{1}_{A_{i}}(\xi)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{K}(A,\gamma),

which completes the proof of the claim. Moreover,
{\mathcal{K}}
can indeed be extended.

Lemma 3.10
.

The pre-kernel
\mathcal{K}
has a unique extension to a kernel on
{\mathcal{B}}(\Pi_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}))\times\Pi({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
.

Proof.

Since
{\mathcal{B}}_{b}(\Pi_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}))
is a ring, it suffices to demonstrate the
\sigma
-finiteness of
\mathcal{K}(\cdot,\gamma)
n order to obtain a unique extension to
{\mathcal{B}}(\Pi_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}))
. For
A\in{\mathcal{B}}_{b}(\Pi_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}))
, the sum

\mathcal{K}(A,\gamma)=\sum_{\xi\Subset\gamma}\mathbbm{1}_{A}(\xi)

is finite. Therefore, by Carathéodory’s theorem,
{\mathcal{K}}
can be uniquely extended to a kernel on
{\mathcal{B}}(\Pi_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}))\times\Pi({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
.
∎

We can further extend Relation (
3

Proposition 3.11
.

Let
G\colon\Pi_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a measurable function with

or

\int_{\Pi_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}G(\xi){\mathcal{K}}(d\xi,\gamma)=\sum_{\xi\Subset\gamma}G(\xi)=(KG)(\gamma).

Proof.

The function
G
can be approximated by a sequence of simple functions, namely:

G(\gamma)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}a_{k}\mathbbm{1}_{A_{k}}(\gamma),

where
a_{k}\in{\mathbb{R}},A\in{\mathcal{B}}_{b}(\Pi_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})),\gamma\in\Pi_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
. The identity can then be derived by taking monotone limits. For further details, see
[
8
,
12
]
.
∎

Definition 3.12

(4)

4

For a measure

(6)

3.3
Correlation Functions on
\Pi_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})

Our goal is to establish the existence of a correlation function associated with a specific subset of measures, denoted by
{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathrm{fm}}^{1}(\Pi({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}))
. This correlation function acts as the density function for the corresponding correlation measure
\rho_{\mu}
. Such functions are of particular mathematical interest due to their widespread use in applications where they characterize the behavior of the system under consideration.

Definition 3.21
.

A measure
\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}^{1}_{\mathrm{fm}}(\Pi({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}))
is locally absolutely continuous with respect to the Poisson measure
\pi_{\sigma}
iff the measure
\mu^{\Lambda}
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\pi_{\sigma}^{\Lambda}
for all
\Lambda\in{\mathcal{B}}_{c}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
, where
\mu^{\Lambda}:=\mu\circ p^{-1}_{\Lambda}
.

Proposition 3.22

(
[
8
]
)
.

For a measure
\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}^{1}_{\mathrm{fm}}(\Pi({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}))
which is locally absolutely continuous with respect to
\pi_{\sigma}
, the correlation measure
\rho_{\mu}
is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue-Poisson measure
\mathcal{L}_{\sigma}
introduced in Section 2.1. The density function has the following representation for any
\gamma\in\Pi_{0}(\Lambda)
:

k_{\mu}(\gamma)=\frac{d\rho_{\mu}}{d\mathcal{L}_{\sigma}}(\gamma)=\int_{\Pi(\Lambda)}\frac{d\mu^{\Lambda}}{d\pi^{\Lambda}_{\sigma}}(\gamma\cup\xi)\pi_{\sigma}^{\Lambda}(d\xi).

Definition 3.23
.

The function
k_{\mu}\colon\Pi_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})\to{\mathbb{R}}
defined by the previous proposition is called the correlation function corresponding to
\mu
. Moreover, we have the decomposition
k_{\mu}\simeq\{k_{\mu}^{(n)}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
, where for any
n\in{\mathbb{N}},k_{\mu}^{(n)}\colon({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is a symmetric function with

k_{\mu}^{(n)}(v_{1},x_{1},\dotsc,v_{n},x_{n}):=\begin{cases}k_{\mu}(\{(v_{1},x_{1}),\dotsc,(v_{n},x_{n})\}),&\text{ if }|\{(v_{1},x_{1}),\dotsc,(v_{n},x_{n})\}|=n,\\
0,&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

The functions
k_{\mu}^{(n)}
are called
n
-point correlation functions.

In what follows, we present the Bogoliubov functional, which we will employ to define correlation functions on the cone
{\mathbb{K}}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
. Bogoliubov originally developed this category of functionals
[
2
]
to establish correlation functions for systems in statistical mechanics. The Bogoliubov functional approach also investigates continuum interacting particle systems in
[
9
]
.

Definition 3.24
.

Let
\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}^{1}_{\mathrm{fm}}(\Pi({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}))
. The Bogoliubov functional
L^{\Pi}_{\mu}
corresponding to
\mu
is a functional defined at each measurable function
\varphi\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
by

L^{\Pi}_{\mu}(\varphi):=\int_{\Pi({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}\prod_{(v,x)\in\gamma}(1+\varphi(v,x))\mu(d\gamma),

provided, the right-hand side exists for
|\varphi|
.

The following proposition will be useful to define the correlation functions on
{\mathbb{K}}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
later.

Proposition 3.25

(
[
9
]
)
.

Under some assumptions, the Bogoliubov functional is the generating functional of the correlation function. In other words, for any
\varphi\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
such that
L_{\mu}^{\Pi}(\varphi)
is well-defined, we have

\displaystyle L_{\mu}^{\Pi}(\varphi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\int_{({\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{0}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})^{n}}\varphi(v_{1},x_{1})\dotsb\varphi(v_{n},x_{n})\times

\displaystyle\hskip 100.0pt\vphantom{\int}\times k_{\mu}^{(n)}(v_{1},\dotsc,x_{n})\lambda(dv_{1})m(dx_{1})\dotso\lambda(dv_{n})m(dx_{n}).

4
Harmonic Analysis on
K({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})

We aim to present harmonic analysis on the cone of vector-valued discrete Radon measures on
{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
, denoted by
{\mathbb{K}}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
, after introducing harmonic analysis on the Plato space
\Pi({\mathbb{R}}_{0}^{d}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
. To establish a relationship between
\Pi({\mathbb{R}}_{0}^{d}\times{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
and
{\mathbb{K}}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
, we employ the reflection mapping
{\mathcal{R}}
.

4.1
The
K
-Transform

Initially, we focus on the space
{\mathbb{K}}_{0}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
and, similar to the previous section, introduce subspaces of
{\mathbb{K}}_{0}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
to decompose the space. Subsequently, we proceed to introduce classes of functions in these spaces.

Definition 4.1
.

1.

The set of discrete Radon measures with finite support is defined as:

{\mathbb{K}}_{0}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}):=\left\{\eta\in{\mathbb{K}}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})\colon|\tau(\eta)|<\infty\right\}.

2.

For
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, the set of
n
-points measures is defined as:

{\mathbb{K}}_{0}^{(n)}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}):=\left\{\eta\in{\mathbb{K}}_{0}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})\colon|\tau(\eta)|=n\right\},\ n\in{\mathbb{N}}

and
{\mathbb{K}}_{0}^{(0)}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})=\{0\}
the set consisting of the zero measure.

3.

For a compact set
\Lambda\subset{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
, the set of all measures supported in
\Lambda
is defined as:

{\mathbb{K}}_{0}(\Lambda):=\left\{\eta\in{\mathbb{K}}_{0}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})\colon\tau(\eta)\subset\Lambda\right\}.

4.

A set
A\subset{\mathbb{K}}_{0}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
is called bounded if there exists a compact set
\Lambda\subset{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
and
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that

A\subset\bigcup_{n=0}^{N}{\mathbb{K}}^{(n)}_{0}(\Lambda).

Denote the collection of all bounded Borel subsets of
{\mathbb{K}}_{0}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
by
{\mathcal{B}}_{b}({\mathbb{K}}_{0}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}))
.

5.

A bounded set
A\subset{\mathbb{K}}_{0}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
is said to have compact velocities if, additionally, there exists a compact set

Lemma 4.12
.

For any locally finite measure
\rho
, the space
B_{\mathrm{bs}}({\mathbb{K}}_{0}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}))
is dense in
L^{1}(\rho)
.

Proof.

Let
G\in L^{1}({\mathbb{K}}_{0}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}),\rho)
for some measure
\rho
on
{\mathbb{K}}_{0}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
. We begin by approximating unbounded functions with bounded support. Define

\displaystyle G_{n}(\eta)

\displaystyle:=\left[G(\eta)\mathbbm{1}_{{\mathbb{K}}_{0}(B_{n})}(\eta)\mathbbm{1}_{\{|\tau(\eta)|\leq n\}}(\eta)\right]\land n

\displaystyle G^{\prime}_{n}(\eta)

\displaystyle:=\left[G(\eta)\mathbbm{1}_{{\mathbb{K}}_{0}(B_{n})}(\eta)\mathbbm{1}_{\{|\tau(\eta)|\leq n\}}(\eta)\right],

where
B_{n}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is the ball with radius
n
centered at
0
. Then
G_{n}\in B_{\mathrm{bs}}({\mathbb{K}}_{0}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}))
and

\displaystyle\|G_{n}(\eta)-G^{\prime}_{n}(\eta)\|_{L^{1}(\rho)}

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{K}}_{0}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}|G_{n}(\eta)-G^{\prime}_{n}(\eta)|\rho(d\eta)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{K}}_{0}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}|G_{n}^{\prime}(\eta)|\mathbbm{1}_{\{|G_{n}(\eta)|\geq n\}}(\eta)\rho(d\eta)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{{\mathbb{K}}_{0}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}|G(\eta)|\mathbbm{1}_{\{|G(\eta)|\geq n\}}(\eta)\rho(d\eta).

Since
G\in L^{1}({\mathbb{K}}_{0}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}),\rho)
, the last term converges to
0
for
n\to\infty
. Next, define

G^{\prime\prime}_{n}(\eta)=G(\eta)\mathbbm{1}_{{\mathbb{K}}_{0}(B_{n})}(\eta).

Recall that
{\mathbb{K}}_{0}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})
can be decomposed into disjoint n-point configurations, i.e.,

{\mathbb{K}}_{0}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})=\bigcup_{m=0}^{\infty}{\mathbb{K}}_{0}^{(m)}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}),\ {\mathbb{K}}_{0}^{(m)}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})=\left\{\eta\in{\mathbb{K}}_{0}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})\colon|\tau(\eta)|=m\right\}.

\displaystyle\|G^{\prime}_{n}(\eta)-G^{\prime\prime}_{n}(\eta)\|

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{K}}_{0}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}|G^{\prime}_{n}(\eta)-G^{\prime\prime}_{n}(\eta)|\rho(d\eta)

and since

, the series is absolutely convergent. Therefore, the last expression tends to
0
as
n\to\infty
. For the final step, observe that the increasing sequence

approximates
{\mathbb{K}}_{0}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})

which converges to
0
as

Corollary 4.13
.

Define the density function:

f(\eta)=\prod_{x\in\tau(\eta)}\frac{1}{|v_{x}|}.

Then the space
\widetilde{B}_{\mathrm{bs}}({\mathbb{K}}_{0}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}))
is dense in
L^{1}({\mathbb{K}}_{0}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}),f\rho)
.

Proof.

Let
G\in L^{1}(f\rho)
. By definition, we have

\|G\|_{L^{1}(f\rho)}=\int_{{\mathbb{K}}_{0}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}|G(\eta)|\prod_{x\in\tau(\eta)}\frac{1}{|v_{x}|}\rho(d\eta)<\infty,

which implies that
G\cdot f\in L^{1}(\rho)
. Since
B_{\mathrm{bs}}({\mathbb{K}}_{0}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}))
is dense in
L^{1}(\rho)
, there exists a sequence
\{G_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\subset B_{\mathrm{bs}}({\mathbb{K}}_{0}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}))
such that

\|G_{n}-G\cdot f\|_{L^{1}(\rho)}\to 0,\ n\to\infty.

On the other hand, the sequence
\{\widetilde{G}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is in
\widetilde{B}_{\mathrm{bs}}({\mathbb{K}}_{0}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}))
, where
\widetilde{G}_{n}:=\frac{G_{n}}{f}
and satisfies

\widetilde{G}_{n}(\eta)=\frac{G_{n}(\eta)}{f(\eta)}\leq C\mathbbm{1}_{{\mathbb{K}}_{0}(\Lambda)}(\eta)\mathbbm{1}_{\{|\tau(\eta)|\leq N\}}(\eta)\prod_{x\in\tau(\eta)}v_{x}.

Moreover,
\widetilde{G}_{n}
converges to
G
in
L^{1}(f\rho)
:

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{G}_{n}-G\|_{L^{1}(f\rho)}

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{K}}_{0}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}\left|\frac{G_{n}}{f}-G\right|fd\rho

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{K}}_{0}({{\mathbb{R}}^{d}})}|G_{n}-Gf|d\rho=\|G_{n}-Gf\|_{L^{1}(\rho)}\to 0\ n\to\infty.

This completes the proof.
∎

5
Discussion

We developed a rigorous mathematical framework to analyze classical continuous systems with singular velocity distributions, addressing a significant gap in modelling such systems. These distributions, represented by Radon measures with infinite mass, introduce challenges beyond standard approaches in configuration spaces. In particular, we focused on constructing and analyzing the phase space of configurations and measures, with specific emphasis on the Plato space
\Pi(\mathbb{R}_{0}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
and the cone of vector-valued discrete Radon measures
{\mathbb{K}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, connected via the reflection mapping
{\mathcal{R}}
. This relationship is the foundation for developing harmonic analysis and studying associated measures and dynamics. Let us underline that the conceptualization of the Plato space introduces an idealized setting where configurations are locally finite subsets of
\mathbb{R}_{0}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which are required to satisfy the condition that, for any compact subset
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the total velocity of particles within
\Lambda
remains finite, expressed as:

V_{\Lambda}(\gamma)=\sum_{x\in\tau(\gamma)\cap\Lambda}|v_{x}|<\infty,\hskip 9.24994pt\forall\gamma\in\Pi(\mathbb{R}_{0}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}),

\tau(\gamma)
being the projection of the configuration
\gamma
onto spatial positions. This constraint ensures the system avoids unbounded velocities within finite spatial regions, aligning with physical plausibility.
Moreover, since the reflection mapping
{\mathcal{R}}
connects the Plato space
\Pi(\mathbb{R}_{0}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
with the cone
{\mathbb{K}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
by mapping configurations
\gamma\in\Pi(\mathbb{R}_{0}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
to vector-valued Radon measures:

{\mathcal{R}}\gamma=\sum_{x\in\tau(\gamma)}v_{x}\delta_{x},\hskip 9.24994pt\text{where }v_{x}\in\mathbb{R}_{0}^{d},

then it defines
{\mathbb{K}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
as the image of
\Pi(\mathbb{R}_{0}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
under
{\mathcal{R}}
. The latter gives the possibility of forming the space of observable objects derived from the ideal configurations. Consequently, the topology of
{\mathbb{K}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
inherits its structure from the vague topology on the configuration space.
In the latter setting, a cornerstone of the analysis lies in the use of Poisson measures on
\Pi(\mathbb{R}_{0}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
, defined via their Laplace transform:

\int_{\Pi(\mathbb{R}_{0}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})}\exp\left(\sum_{(v,x)\in\gamma}\psi(v,x)\right)\pi_{\sigma}(d\gamma)=\exp\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(e^{\psi(v,x)}-1\right)\sigma(dv,dx),

where
\sigma=\lambda\otimes m
is an intensity measure combining a Radon measure
\lambda(dv)
on
\mathbb{R}_{0}^{d}
and a Lebesgue measure
m(dx)
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, hence encapsulating the statistical properties of the system and forms the basis for deriving probability measures on
{\mathbb{K}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
We also implemented the associated harmonic analysis via the K-transform to facilitate the transition between finite configurations in
\Pi_{0}(\mathbb{R}_{0}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
and infinite configurations in
\Pi(\mathbb{R}_{0}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Indeed, it allows the extension of functions and measures from finite to infinite configurations, providing a powerful tool for analyzing the system’s dynamics.
Moreover, introducing the measures on
{\mathbb{K}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
via the pushforward of measures on
\Pi(\mathbb{R}_{0}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
under
{\mathcal{R}}
, allows us to study complex systems with singular velocity distributions. Accordingly, we derive a central result of the paper, i.e.: we proved that the image
\sigma
-algebra of
\Pi(\mathbb{R}_{0}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
under
{\mathcal{R}}
coincides with the
\sigma
-algebra of
{\mathbb{K}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, ensuring the measurability of the reflection mapping and the compatibility of the mathematical structures.
The results discussed above notably extend the scope of prior research on configuration spaces and Radon measures, especially those concentrating on positive measures in Riemannian contexts. Classical studies, such as those by Kondratiev et al., have explored the harmonic analysis of positive discrete Radon measures on Riemannian manifolds, representing particle configurations with purely spatial characteristics. These configurations are typically modelled as locally finite sets with well-behaved measures, facilitating the development of analytical techniques such as correlation functions and generating functionals. In contrast, the framework developed here addresses a broader and more complex class of systems: vector-valued Radon measures with singular distributions. These singularities arise when measures have infinite mass or exhibit significant anisotropy in velocity space. Specifically, velocity distributions of the form
\lambda(dv)=|v|^{-\alpha}e^{-|v|^{\beta}}dv
, with
\alpha\in[d,d+1)
and
\beta>0
, present deep mathematical and physical consequences. These distributions enable the inclusion of systems where velocities can become arbitrarily large near specific regions of phase space, a scenario that cannot be described using traditional finite measures.
In particular, the parameter
\alpha
determines the degree of singularity near
v=0
. For
\alpha=d
, the measure
\lambda(dv)
transitions to a marginally finite scenario. In contrast, values of
\alpha
approaching
d+1
emphasize the singular nature of the distribution, leading to configurations that challenge standard assumptions of local finiteness. On the other hand, the parameter
\beta
controls the distribution’s decay rate at large
|v|
, with larger values corresponding to faster decay. The interplay between
\alpha
and
\beta
broadens the modelling flexibility, accommodating a wide variety of physical systems, e.g.:
when
\beta=2
, the measure approximates modified Maxwellian distributions relevant in statistical physics, while, for
\beta<2
, the distributions model systems with heavy-tailed velocities characteristic of certain astrophysical or turbulent systems.

The shift from positive measures to vector-valued measures introduces additional complexity due to the need to account for the vectorial nature of the marks (velocities) attached to spatial configurations. This extension requires new mathematical tools to handle the coupled spatial and velocity dependencies. For instance, the harmonic analysis developed for positive measures relies on scalar-valued generating functions and correlation measures, whereas vector-valued measures necessitate functionals and transforms that respect the vector structure.
Let us also underline that the introduction of singular velocity distributions also enhances the physical realism of the model, enabling the study of systems where unbounded velocities naturally occur such, e.g.:
Astrophysical Systems, where stars and galaxies often exhibit velocity distributions with heavy tails due to gravitational interactions. The singular measures proposed here can model such systems without artificial truncations of velocity magnitudes; Kinetic Theory: namely in the Boltzmann framework, velocity distributions often develop singular features due to collision dynamics, especially in dilute gases; Turbulence and Fluids: where singular velocity distributions appear in the statistical description of turbulent flows, where small-scale dynamics generate extreme velocities; etc.

The generalizations discussed above introduce additional complexity in the definition and analysis of measures on configuration spaces. Specifically, the transition from scalar-valued to vector-valued measures on
{\mathbb{K}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
requires a careful consideration of both topological and algebraic structures. Key to this is ensuring the measurability of the reflection mapping
{\mathcal{R}}:\Pi(\mathbb{R}_{0}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})\to{\mathbb{K}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and the compatibility of the corresponding
\sigma
-algebras. The K-transform, originally developed for scalar measures, is extended here to accommodate vector-valued measures, providing robust harmonic analysis and probabilistic modeling. This extension enables the handling of singular and anisotropic velocity distributions, thus broadening the framework’s applicability to various physical and mathematical problems. This includes exploring systems where classical assumptions, such as bounded velocities or locally finite configurations, do not hold, opening new research directions in studying complex systems.

Future work will aim to extend this framework to interacting particle systems, where pairwise or higher-order interactions introduce additional complexities. Additionally, generalizing the framework to non-Euclidean geometries, such as Riemannian or hyperbolic spaces, will shed light on systems with curvature-dependent dynamics. Numerical implementations of the K-transform and associated measures could facilitate the application of these methods to real-world systems. Lastly, investigating stochastic evolution on
{\mathbb{K}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
in the context of (stochastic) mean-field games is a promising direction, linking the theoretical framework to dynamic processes.
