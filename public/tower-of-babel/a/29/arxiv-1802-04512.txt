The principle of pointfree continuity
Kawai Tatsuji, Giovanni Sambin

Abstract
. In the setting of constructive pointfree topology, we introduce a notion of continuous operation between pointfree topologies and the corresponding principle of pointfree continuity. An operation between points of pointfree topologies is continuous if it is induced by a relation between the bases of the topologies; this gives a rigorous condition for Brouwer’s continuity principle to hold. The principle of pointfree continuity for pointfree topologies 𝒮 \mathcal{S} and 𝒯 \mathcal{T} says that any relation which induces a continuous operation between points is a morphism from 𝒮 \mathcal{S} to 𝒯 \mathcal{T} . The principle holds under the assumption of bi-spatiality of 𝒮 \mathcal{S} . When 𝒮 \mathcal{S} is the formal Baire space or the formal unit interval and 𝒯 \mathcal{T} is the formal topology of natural numbers, the principle is equivalent to spatiality of the formal Baire space and formal unit interval, respectively. Some of the well-known connections between spatiality, bar induction, and compactness of the unit interval are recast in terms of our principle of continuity. We adopt the Minimalist Foundation as our constructive foundation, and positive topology as the notion of pointfree topology. This allows us to distinguish ideal objects from constructive ones, and in particular, to interpret choice sequences as points of the formal Baire space.

Introduction

In a number of writings, Brouwer analysed functions from
choice sequences to the natural numbers, and claimed that every total
function on choice sequences is continuous in a very strong sense (see
e.g. Brouwer
[
3
]
).
As a corollary, he obtained the continuity theorem of real numbers: every total function on the unit interval is uniformly
continuous.
In more recent accounts of Brouwer’s intuitionism (e.g. Kleene and
Vesley
[
16
]
), it is common to decompose Brouwer’s
analysis into two principles: continuity principle and bar
induction.
1
1

1

There are many variations of these principles,
depending on the strength of continuity and complexity of bars. The
versions of continuity principle and bar induction that we present
here are deliberately chosen to suite our aims.
Continuity principle
says that every total function from choice sequences to the natural
numbers is pointwise continuous, while bar induction says that every
monotone bar is an inductive bar (cf. Troelstra and van Dalen

[
26
, Chapter 4]
).

The aim of this paper is to show that continuity principle can be maintained if
we take seriously the constructive and pointfree approach to topology

[
22
,
25
]
, while bar
induction is the principle which is exactly needed to bridge the gap
between pointwise continuity and pointfree continuity.

As to the first claim, in the constructive pointfree topology, a
point appears as a set of its formal neighbourhoods.
Therefore, it is reasonable
to assume that every constructively defined operation on the
collection of points must be induced by a relation between the formal
neighbourhoods of the relevant pointfree topologies.
Actually, one of
the main points of this paper is that every pointwise continuous operation
between points of pointfree topologies is induced
by a relation between formal neighbourhoods, and conversely if a
relation between formal neighbourhoods gives rise to a total
operation between points, then such operation is
automatically pointwise continuous. Hence, we obtain continuity for free if we
start from a relation between formal neighbourhoods; see Theorem
2.12
.

Given two pointfree topologies
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{T}
,
it is then natural to ask whether a relation
from formal neighbourhoods of
\mathcal{S}

to those of
\mathcal{T}
which gives rise to a total operation
between points is actually a pointfree
map from
\mathcal{S}
to
\mathcal{T}
.
This is not necessarily the case unless the topology on points of

\mathcal{S}
coincides with
\mathcal{S}
. Hence, we formulate this
transition to a pointfree map as a
principle, called
the principle of pointfree continuity

for
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{T}

(
\mathrm{PoPC}_{\mathcal{S},\mathcal{T}}
); see Definition
2.19
for the precise formulation.
We discuss some sufficient conditions on
\mathcal{S}
and

\mathcal{T}
under which
\mathrm{PoPC}_{\mathcal{S},\mathcal{T}}
holds in
Section
2.4
.

The above claim and the principle of pointfree continuity
make sense for arbitrary pointfree topologies — not just for the Baire
space and the natural numbers for which continuity principle and bar
induction are usually formulated.
However, the principle
\mathrm{PoPC}_{\mathcal{B},\mathcal{N}}

for the formal (i.e. pointfree) Baire space
\mathcal{B}
and the formal
topology of natural numbers
\mathcal{N}
is equivalent to
monotone bar induction,
and hence to spatiality of the formal Baire space.
Moreover, some well-known notions such as neighbourhood function and
decidable bar induction
[
26
, Chapter 4]
naturally
arise when we consider a variant of
\mathrm{PoPC}_{\mathcal{B},\mathcal{N}}
formulated with
respect to a restricted class of relations
from the formal neighbourhoods of
\mathcal{B}
to
\mathcal{N}
; see
Section
3
.
Furthermore, the principle
\mathrm{PoPC}_{\mathcal{I}[0,1],\mathcal{N}}
for the formal unit
interval
\mathcal{I}[0,1]
and the formal topology of natural numbers
\mathcal{N}
is shown to
be equivalent to Heine–Borel covering theorem, and thus to spatiality
of the formal topology of real numbers; see Section
4
.

Remark on foundations

In this paper, we adopt the Minimalist
Foundation
[

,
18

as our constructive foundation and positive

[

as the notion of pointfree topology
(see Section

For the details of the Minimalist Foundation, we refer the reader to

It should be noted, however, that the results in

essential use of the structure of positive topology, and thus they can be
understood in the setting of formal topology
[

In what follows, we elaborate on why working in the Minimalist
Foundation could be relevant to this work.
The Minimalist Foundation distinguishes collections from sets and
logic from type theory. In particular, points of a positive
topology usually form a collection rather than a set, and hence we can
regard a point of a positive topology as an ideal object.
For example, points of the formal Baire space, which are equivalent to
functions between the natural numbers, form a collection rather than a
set. This is due to the separation of logic from type theory, which
allows us to keep the logical notion of function distinct from the type
theoretic notion of constructive operation. Because of this, we can view
a point of the formal Baire space as a choice sequence which is not
necessarily lawlike (see also the discussion following Proposition

3.3
).

It is clear from Brouwer’s writings that not only the notion of choice
sequence but also that of lawlike operation (which he called
algorithm in
[
3
]
) plays a crucial role in
his analysis of continuity on the Baire space. Hence, any satisfactory
account of Brouwer’s intuitionism requires both the notion of choice sequence
and that of lawlike sequence which are kept separate.
The Minimalist Foundation can serve as a practical foundation of
intuitionism where we can talk about choice sequences as a
‘‘figure of speech’’. For example, it might be possible to postulate
bar induction on choice sequences
while maintaining the view that every type
theoretic operation is lawlike.

Another candidate for a
practical foundation of Brouwer’s intuitionism is the theory of
choice sequences

by Kreisel and

[

the treatment in the Minimalist Foundation may be more coherent and easier to understand, since it

2.
Continuity principle for positive topologies

The Minimalist Foundation, in particular its distinction
between sets and collections, leads us to introduce
two different notions of topology which replace the classical notion
of topological space.
When points form a set, we reach the notion of concrete space.
When points form a collection, it is more appropriate
to understand them as ideal points of a pointfree structure, which we
call positive topology.

We briefly review these notions in the next two subsections,
and refer the reader to
[
24
]
or the forthcoming book

[
25
]
for further details.

2.3.

The collection

s\subseteq S\times T
is a
relation which makes the following diagram commute:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathit{IP}t(\mathcal{S})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces S}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathit{IP}t(\mathcal{T})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\backelm}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces g}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces s}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\backelm}

The above definition
is motivated by the following observation on representable topologies:
let
(r,s)\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
be a convergent relation pair.
Then
s
is a formal map from
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{X}}
to
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{Y}}

and hence (as we will see in Corollary
2.15
below)

s_{*}
is an operation from

\mathit{IP}t(\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{X}})
to
\mathit{IP}t(\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{Y}})
.
Since
(s_{*},s)
trivially makes the above square commute,
it is a continuous map from
\mathit{IP}t(\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{X}})

to
\mathit{IP}t(\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{Y}})
.

Every continuous map
(g,s)
is pointwise continuous, that is

b\elm g(\alpha)\imp\exists a\elm\alpha\left[\left(\forall\beta\colon\mathit{IP}t(\mathcal{S})\right)a\elm\beta\imp b\elm g(\beta)\right]

for all
\alpha\colon\mathit{IP}t(\mathcal{S})
and
b\in T
. More
specifically, from
the open neighbourhood
\mathsf{Ext}(b)
of
g(\alpha)
,
by commutativity one can trace
b
backward along
s

and find an open neighbourhood
\mathsf{Ext}(a)
of
\alpha
whose image
under
g
falls within
\mathsf{Ext}(b)
. Thus, the relation
s
acts as a

modulus
of continuity for the operation
g
.

Commutativity of the square means that

b\elm g(\alpha)\leftrightarrow s^{-}b\meets\alpha\leftrightarrow b\elm{s}_{*}(\alpha)

for all
b\in T
and
\alpha\colon\mathit{IP}t(\mathcal{S})
,
which is equivalent to saying that
g
is equal to the image
operation
{s}_{*}\colon\mathcal{P}(S)\to\mathcal{P}(T)
on

\mathit{IP}t(\mathcal{S})
. Hence, every continuous map
from
\mathcal{I}p(\mathcal{S})
to
\mathcal{I}p(\mathcal{T})
is of the form

({s}_{*},s)
where
s\subseteq S\times T
is a relation which
maps
\alpha\colon\mathit{IP}t(\mathcal{S})
to
{s}_{*}(\alpha)\colon\mathit{IP}t(\mathcal{T})
. Conversely, if
s\subseteq S\times T
is a
relation which induces a well-defined operation
{s}_{*}\colon\mathit{IP}t(\mathcal{S})\to\mathit{IP}t(\mathcal{T})
, then the pair
({s}_{*},s)

clearly makes the square commute.
In summary, we have the following theorem.

Theorem 2.12

(Continuity theorem)
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{T}
be positive topologies, and
let
s\subseteq S\times T
be a relation.
If
{s}_{*}
is a
mapping from

\mathit{IP}t(\mathcal{S})
to
\mathit{IP}t(\mathcal{T})
, then
({s}_{*},s)
is a continuous map from

\mathcal{I}p(\mathcal{S})
to
\mathcal{I}p(\mathcal{T})
. Moreover, every
continuous map from
\mathcal{I}p(\mathcal{S})
to
\mathcal{I}p(\mathcal{T})
is
induced by a relation from
S
to
T
in this way.

Theorem
2.12
should be compared to the familiar form of
continuity principle “every full function is continuous”. The
theorem articulates a condition on operations between ideal points in
which the continuity principle holds, i.e. that of being induced by a
relation between bases. This condition is reasonable from the
constructive point of view since in order to define an operation on an
infinite object like an ideal point, we can
only rely on finite information about it, i.e. its formal
neighbourhoods and a relation between them (cf. Vickers

[
28
]
). From a predicative point of
view, we believe that the notion of continuous map is one of the
simplest way of characterising continuous operations between
ideal points.

2.4.
Principle of pointfree continuity

Our next aim is to relate the notion of continuous map to that of
formal map.

Given a positive topology
\mathcal{S}
, let

\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{I}p}=(S,\cov_{\mathcal{I}p},\pos_{\mathcal{I}p})
be the
large

positive topology associated with the concrete space

\mathcal{I}p(\mathcal{S})
(cf. (
2.3
)).
Note that
\cov_{\mathcal{I}p}
and
\pos_{\mathcal{I}p}
are defined by
quantifications over
\mathit{IP}t(\mathcal{S})
, which is not
necessarily a set, and hence they are
large
structures in general.
There is a formal map
\varepsilon_{\mathcal{S}}\colon\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{I}p}\to\mathcal{S}
represented by the identity
relation on
S
. In particular, we have

\displaystyle a\cov U\imp a\cov_{\mathcal{I}p}U

\displaystyle a\pos_{\mathcal{I}p}U\imp a\pos U

for all
a\in S
and
U\subseteq S
.

Lemma 2.13
.

Let
s\subseteq S\times T
be a relation between the bases
of positive topologies
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{T}
. Then

(1)

S\cov_{\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{I}p}}s^{-}T
if and only if

{s}_{*}(\alpha)
is inhabited for all
\alpha\colon\mathit{IP}t(\mathcal{S})
;

(2)

s^{-}b\downarrow s^{-}c\cov_{\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{I}p}}s^{-}(b\downarrow c)
for all
b,c\in T
if and only if

{s}_{*}(\alpha)
is filtering for all
\alpha\colon\mathit{IP}t(\mathcal{S})
;

(3)

b\cov_{\mathcal{T}}V\imp s^{-}b\cov_{\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{I}p}}s^{-}V
for all
b\in T
and
V\subseteq T
if and
only if
{s}_{*}(\alpha)
splits
\cov_{\mathcal{T}}
for all

\alpha\colon\mathit{IP}t(\mathcal{S})
;

(4)

s^{-}b\pos_{\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{I}p}}s^{*}V\imp b\pos_{\mathcal{T}}V
for all

b\in T
and
V\subseteq T
if and only if
{s}_{*}(\alpha)
enters

\pos_{\mathcal{T}}
for all
\alpha\colon\mathit{IP}t(\mathcal{S})
.

Proof.

(
1
)
Since every ideal point is inhabited,
S\cov_{\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{I}p}}s^{-}T

is equivalent to
\alpha\meets s^{-}T
for all
\alpha\colon\mathit{IP}t(\mathcal{S})
, that is
{s}_{*}(\alpha)\meets T
for
all
\alpha\colon\mathit{IP}t(\mathcal{S})
.

(
2
) Similar to (
1
) using
the fact that every ideal point is filtering.

(
3
)
By unfolding the definition,

b\cov_{\mathcal{T}}V\imp s^{-}b\cov_{\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{I}p}}s^{-}V
for all
b\in T
and
V\subseteq T

if and only if

b\cov_{\mathcal{T}}V\imp\forall\alpha\colon\mathit{IP}t(\mathcal{S})\left[\alpha\meets s^{-}b\imp\alpha\meets s^{-}V\right]
for all
b\in T
and
V\subseteq T
. This is easily seen to be equivalent to

b\cov_{\mathcal{T}}V\amp b\elm{s}_{*}(\alpha)\imp{s}_{*}(\alpha)\meets V
for all
b\in T
,
V\subseteq T

and
\alpha\colon\mathit{IP}t(\mathcal{S})
, that is

{s}_{*}(\alpha)
splits
\cov_{\mathcal{T}}
for all
\alpha\colon\mathit{IP}t(\mathcal{S})
.

(
4
) By unfolding the definition, we have

s^{-}b\pos_{\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{I}p}}s^{*}V\imp b\pos_{\mathcal{T}}V

for all
b\in T
and
V\subseteq T

if and only if

\exists\alpha\colon\mathit{IP}t(\mathcal{S})\left[\alpha\meets s^{-}b\amp\alpha\subseteq s^{*}V\right]\imp b\pos_{\mathcal{T}}V

for all
b\in T
and
V\subseteq T
. This is equivalent to

b\elm{s}_{*}(\alpha)\amp{s}_{*}(\alpha)\subseteq V\imp b\pos_{\mathcal{T}}V

for all
b\in T
,
V\subseteq T
and
\alpha\colon\mathit{IP}t(\mathcal{S})
, that is

{s}_{*}(\alpha)
enters
\pos_{\mathcal{T}}
for all

\alpha\colon\mathit{IP}t(\mathcal{S})
.
∎

Proposition 2.14
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{T}
be positive topologies
and
s\subseteq S\times T
be a relation. The following are
equivalent:

(1)

{s}_{*}(\alpha)\colon\mathit{IP}t(\mathcal{T})
for all
\alpha\colon\mathit{IP}t(\mathcal{S})
;

(2)

({s}_{*},s)
is a continuous
map from
\mathcal{I}p(\mathcal{S})
to

\mathcal{I}p(\mathcal{T})
;

(3)

s
is a formal map from
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{I}p}
to
\mathcal{T}
.

Proof.

(
1

\Leftrightarrow

2
)
This is the content of Theorem
2.12
.

(
1

\Leftrightarrow

3
)
Immediate from Lemma
2.13
.
∎

Corollary 2.15
.

If
s\colon\mathcal{S}\to\mathcal{T}
is a formal map,
then
({s}_{*},s)
is continuous, i.e.
{s}_{*}
is a mapping from

\mathit{IP}t(\mathcal{S})
to
\mathit{IP}t(\mathcal{T})
.

Proof.

If
s\colon\mathcal{S}\to\mathcal{T}
is a formal map,
then composition with the canonical map
\varepsilon_{\mathcal{S}}\colon\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{I}p}\to\mathcal{S}
gives a formal map
s\colon\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{I}p}\to\mathcal{T}
. Then, the conclusion
follows from Proposition
2.14
.
∎

If the pointwise cover and positivity coincide with the pointfree
ones, i.e. the canonical map
\varepsilon_{\mathcal{S}}\colon\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{I}p}\to\mathcal{S}
is an isomorphism, then we could
have replaced item (
3
) of Proposition

2.14
with a formal map
s\colon\mathcal{S}\to\mathcal{T}
. Here, the notion of bi-spatiality is exactly what is
required.

Definition 2.16
.

A positive topology
\mathcal{S}
is
\emph{bi-spatial}
if
the canonical formal map
\varepsilon_{\mathcal{S}}\colon\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{I}p}\to\mathcal{S}
is an isomorphism, i.e.

\displaystyle a\cov_{\mathcal{I}p}U\imp a\cov U

(spatiality)

\displaystyle a\pos U\imp a\pos_{\mathcal{I}p}U

(reducibility)

for all
a\in S
and
U\subseteq S
.
A positive topology is
spatial
if
it satisfies spatiality.

The following proposition corresponds to the embedding
of the category of sober topological spaces into that of spatial
locales (Johnstone
[
13
, Chapter II, 1.7]
).

Proposition 2.17
.

Every representable topology is bi-spatial.

Proof.

This follows from the fact that in any concrete space

\mathcal{X}
, the subset
\Diamond x
is an ideal point of

\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{X}}
for each
x\in X
.
∎

From Definition
2.16
and Proposition

2.12
, we obtain our second continuity theorem:

Theorem 2.18
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{T}
be positive topologies, and
s\subseteq S\times T
be a relation. If
\mathcal{S}
is bi-spatial, then

s
is a formal map
s\colon\mathcal{S}\to\mathcal{T}
if and only if

{s}_{*}(\alpha)\colon\mathit{IP}t(\mathcal{T})
for all
\alpha\colon\mathit{IP}t(\mathcal{S})
.

Our new principle of continuity is then obtained by omitting the
assumption of bi-spatiality from Theorem
2.18
.

Definition 2.19
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{T}
be positive topologies.
The
principle of pointfree continuity for
\mathcal{S}
and

\mathcal{T}
is the statement:

(
\mathrm{PoPC}_{\mathcal{S},\mathcal{T}}
):

For any relation
s\subseteq S\times T
, if

{s}_{*}
is a mapping from
\mathit{IP}t(\mathcal{S})
to
\mathit{IP}t(\mathcal{T})
,
then
s
is a formal map
s\colon\mathcal{S}\to\mathcal{T}
.

After this definition,
the content of Theorem
2.18
may be expressed by:

\mathrm{PoPC}_{\mathcal{S},\mathcal{T}}
holds whenever
\mathcal{S}
is bi-spatial.

Remark 2.20
.

\mathrm{PoPC}_{\mathcal{S},\mathcal{T}}
is concerned with the property of a relation
s\subseteq S\times T
, and not with the property of the operation

{s}_{*}\colon\mathit{IP}t(\mathcal{S})\to\mathit{IP}t(\mathcal{T})
. Another
possible formulation of the continuity principle would be to say that
every continuous operation
(g,s)\colon\mathcal{I}p(\mathcal{S})\to\mathcal{I}p(\mathcal{T})

is induced by a formal map
s^{\prime}\colon\mathcal{S}\to\mathcal{T}
, namely
g={s^{\prime}}_{*}
, or equivalently

{s}_{*}={s^{\prime}}_{*}
. This formulation of continuity principle is
analogous to the uniform continuity theorem for the Cantor space, saying
that every pointwise continuous function from the Cantor space to
the discrete space of natural numbers is uniformly continuous. This latter form is
weaker than the one given in Definition
2.19
(cf. Theorem

3.21
).

With no restriction on
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{T}
,
the principle
\mathrm{PoPC}_{\mathcal{S},\mathcal{T}}
is false.
The following examples are not surprising, given the fact
that
\mathrm{PoPC}_{\mathcal{S},\mathcal{T}}
is a kind of completeness principle.

Example 2.21

(Non-spatial topologies)
.

(1)

Consider a positive topology
\mathcal{S}
with

S=\left\{*\right\}
, and

a\cov U\defeqiv a\elm U
and
a\pos U\defeqiv\bot

for all
a\in S
and
U\subseteq S
.
The topology
\mathcal{S}
has no points,
i.e.
\mathit{IP}t(\mathcal{S})=\emptyset
.
For any positive topology
\mathcal{T}
, if the principle

\mathrm{PoPC}_{\mathcal{S},\mathcal{T}}
holds, then any relation
s\subseteq S\times T
is a formal map from
\mathcal{S}
to

\mathcal{T}
. But the empty relation cannot be a formal map since
it does not satisfy
1
.

(2)

There is a counterexample which is more natural than the
previous one, the geometric theory of surjective functions
between sets (cf. Fox
[
10
, Section 4.1.4]
). This
topology, call it
\mathcal{S}
, is a positive topology with no
points, but there exists
a\in S
such that
a\pos S
.
Then, the same argument as in the first example leads to a
contradiction.

In the light of the counterexamples above, we give some sufficient
conditions on

\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{T}
under which

\mathrm{PoPC}_{\mathcal{S},\mathcal{T}}
holds. Note that by Theorem

2.18
if
\mathcal{S}
is bi-spatial
then
\mathrm{PoPC}_{\mathcal{S},\mathcal{T}}
holds.
For example,

\mathrm{PoPC}_{\mathcal{S},\mathcal{T}}
holds whenever

\mathcal{S}
is representable (cf. Proposition

2.17
).

Let
s\subseteq S\times T
be a relation between the underlying
bases of positive topologies
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{T}
.
Define relations
\cov_{s}
and
\pos_{s}
from
T
to
\mathcal{P}(T)
as

\displaystyle b\cov_{s}V

\displaystyle\defeqiv s^{-}b\cov_{\mathcal{S}}s^{-}V

\displaystyle b\pos_{s}V

\displaystyle\defeqiv s^{-}b\pos_{\mathcal{S}}s^{*}V.

(2.5)

We write
\mathrm{Im}[s]
for the structure
(T,\cov_{s},\pos_{s})
.

Lemma 2.22
.

(1)

\mathrm{Im}[s]
satisfies all the properties of
positive topology except (
\mathord{\downarrow}
-right).

(2)

If
s
satisfies
2
and

3
, then
\mathrm{Im}[s]
is a positive topology.

(3)

If
s
satisfies
2

and
3
, then the relation
s
is a
formal map from
\mathcal{S}
to
\mathrm{Im}[s]
if and only if

s
satisfies
1
.

(4)

If
s
satisfies
2
and
3
,
then the identity relation
\id_{T}
on

is a formal map from

\mathrm{Im}[s]
to
\mathcal{T}
if and only if
s
satisfies

4
.

Proof.

(
1
)
It is straightforward to show that
\mathrm{Im}[s]

satisfies
all the properties of positive topology except (
\mathord{\downarrow}
-right)
using the corresponding properties of
\mathcal{S}
.
For example, to see that
\cov_{s}
and
\pos_{s}
satisfy
(compatibility), suppose we have
b\cov_{s}V
and

. Then there exists

such that
a\pos_{\mathcal{S}}s^{*}W
. Since

,
we have

by

. Hence

.

(
2
)
Assume that
s
satisfies
2
and
3
.
By (

),
it suffices to show that
\mathrm{Im}[s]
satisfies (
\mathord{\downarrow}
-right).
Suppose that
b\cov_{s}V
and

. By (
\mathord{\downarrow}
-right)
of
\mathcal{S}
, we have

.
Then
s^{-}V\downarrow_{\mathcal{S}}s^{-}W\cov_{\mathcal{S}}s^{-}(V\downarrow_{\mathcal{T}}W)
by

. Hence by

and

, we have

.

(

)
Assume that

satisfies
2
and
3
.
It is easy to see that

(as a formal map from

to

,

, and

. Then condition

is equivalent
to that of

(as a formal map from
\mathcal{S}
to
\mathcal{T}
).

(

)
Assume that

satisfies

and

.
Then
\id_{T}
trivially satisfies

.
For

, let

. Then
s^{-}d\cov_{\mathcal{S}}s^{-}b
and

. By

of

, we have

, i.e.

{\id_{T}}^{-}b\downarrow_{\mathrm{Im}[s]}{\id_{T}}^{-}c\cov_{s}{\id_{T}}^{-}(b\downarrow_{\mathcal{T}}c)
.
Lastly, conditions

and

for

Definition 2.23
.

The
image
of a formal map
s\colon\mathcal{S}\to\mathcal{T}
is the positive topology
\mathrm{Im}[s]=(T,\cov_{s},\pos_{s})
.

As a corollary of Lemma
2.22
, we have an image
factorisation.

Proposition 2.24
.

Any formal map
s\colon\mathcal{S}\to\mathcal{T}
between
positive topologies factors as
s\colon\mathcal{S}\to\mathrm{Im}[s]
and
\id_{T}\colon\mathrm{Im}[s]\to\mathcal{T}
.

Proposition 2.25
.

For any formal map
s\colon\mathcal{S}\to\mathcal{T}

and a positive topology
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
, the principle

\mathrm{PoPC}_{\mathcal{S},\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}
implies

\mathrm{PoPC}_{\mathrm{Im}[s],\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}
.

Proof.

Fix a formal map
s\colon\mathcal{S}\to\mathcal{T}
and
a positive topology
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
.
It is easy to see that a relation
s^{\prime}\subseteq T\times S^{\prime}
is a formal map from
\mathrm{Im}[s]
to
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
if
and only if the composition
s^{\prime}\circ s
is a formal map from

\mathcal{S}
to
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
.

Let
s^{\prime}\subseteq T\times S^{\prime}
be a relation such that
{s^{\prime}}_{*}
is a mapping from

\mathit{IP}t(\mathrm{Im}[s])
to
\mathit{IP}t(\mathcal{S}^{\prime})
.
Since
{s}_{*}
is a mapping from
\mathit{IP}t(\mathcal{S})
to

\mathit{IP}t(\mathrm{Im}[s])
, we have that

{(s^{\prime}\circ s)}_{*}
is a mapping from
\mathit{IP}t(\mathcal{S})
to

\mathit{IP}t(\mathcal{S}^{\prime})
. Thus
s
is a formal map from

\mathcal{S}
to
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
by

\mathrm{PoPC}_{\mathcal{S},\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}
. Therefore

is a formal map
from
\mathrm{Im}[s]
to
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
.
∎

Given a cover
\cov
on a set
S
, we can impredicatively define a
positivity
\pos_{\cov}
which is compatible with
\cov
as follows:

\displaystyle a\pos_{\cov}U\defeqiv\left(\exists V\colon\mathcal{P}(S)\right)\left[a\elm V\subseteq U\amp\left(\forall W\colon\mathcal{P}(S)\right)V\meets\mathcal{A}W\imp V\meets W\right]

(2.6)

where

\mathcal{A}W\defeql\left\{a\in S\mid a\cov W\right\}.

It is easy to see that
\pos_{\cov}
is the
greatest positivity
compatible with
\cov
.
If a cover
\cov
is generated inductively (cf. Coquand et al.
[
6
]
), then the positivity
\pos_{\cov}
can be constructed by
coinduction
[
25
]
, and hence its construction can be
done predicatively. This is the case for the positive topologies
treated in Section
3
and Section
4
, whose
covers are generated inductively.

Lemma 2.26
.

Let
\cov
and
\cov^{\prime}
be two covers on a set
S
, and let

\pos
be a positivity on
S
. If
\cov^{\prime}\subseteq\cov
and
\pos
is
compatible with
\cov
, then
\pos
is compatible with
\cov^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Obvious from the definition of compatibility.
∎

Corollary 2.27
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{T}
be positive topologies,
and
s\subseteq S\times T
be a relation which satisfies

1
,
2
, and
3
.
If
\pos_{\mathcal{T}}
is the
greatest positivity compatible with

\cov_{\mathcal{T}}
, then
s
is a formal map from
\mathcal{S}
to

\mathcal{T}
.

Proof.

Condition
4

is equivalent to saying that
the positivity
\pos_{s}
is smaller than

\pos_{\mathcal{T}}
. By
3
, we have

\cov_{\mathcal{T}}\subseteq\cov_{s}
. Thus

\pos_{s}
is compatible with
\cov_{\mathcal{T}}
by Lemma

2.26
. Therefore
\pos_{s}\subseteq\pos_{\mathcal{T}}
.
∎

Proposition 2.28
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{T}
be positive topologies.
If
\mathcal{S}
is spatial and

\mathcal{T}
has the greatest
positivity compatible with its cover,
then
\mathrm{PoPC}_{\mathcal{S},\mathcal{T}}
holds.

Proof.

Immediate from Corollary
2.27
and
Proposition
2.14
.
∎

Most of the positive topologies which arise in practice have
greatest
compatible positivities, so the assumption on the topology

\mathcal{T}
in Proposition
2.28
is often
satisfied. Thus, it is the spatiality of
\mathcal{S}

that is crucial for the continuity principle

\mathrm{PoPC}_{\mathcal{S},\mathcal{T}}
to hold.

At this point, it is natural to ask whether (bi-)spatiality of

\mathcal{S}
is actually necessary for the principle

\mathrm{PoPC}_{\mathcal{S},\mathcal{T}}
to hold. In the following sections, we
answer this question in some specific cases which occupy a central
place in Brouwer’s intuitionism.

3.
Continuity on the Baire space

The continuity principle for the Baire space deserves special attention.
Since this is the context in which Brouwer introduced his principle of
continuity, it is of our particular interest to see in what sense our
continuity principle is related to the principles of intuitionism.

3.1.
Formal Baire space

We recall the pointfree definition of the Baire space,
whose ideal points can
be considered as free choice sequences.
Let
\mathbb{N}^{*}
denote
the set of finite sequences of natural numbers.
We write
a*b
for the concatenation of finite sequences
a

and
b
. By an abuse of notation, we write
a*n
for the
concatenation of a finite sequence
a
and the singleton sequence of

n\in\mathbb{N}
.
The order
\leq_{\mathcal{B}}
on
\mathbb{N}^{*}
is defined by the reverse prefix
ordering:

a\leq_{\mathcal{B}}b\defeqiv\text{$b$ is an initial segment of $a$.}

In particular, we have
a*n\leq_{\mathcal{B}}a
.

Definition 3.1
.

The
formal Baire space
is a positive topology
\mathcal{B}=(\mathbb{N}^{*},\cov_{\mathcal{B}},\pos_{\mathcal{B}})
where the cover
\cov_{\mathcal{B}}

is inductively generated by the following rules

\displaystyle\frac{a\elm U}{a\cov_{\mathcal{B}}U}\,(\eta)\qquad\frac{a\leq_{\mathcal{B}}b\cov_{\mathcal{B}}U}{a\cov_{\mathcal{B}}U}\,(\zeta)\qquad\frac{\left(\forall n\in\mathbb{N}\right)a*n\cov_{\mathcal{B}}U}{a\cov_{\mathcal{B}}U}\,(\digamma)

and
\pos_{\mathcal{B}}
is the greatest positivity compatible with

\cov_{\mathcal{B}}
.

We recall some well-known properties of
\mathcal{B}
that
we shall use in this section.
Let
\mathord{\downarrow}_{\mathcal{B}}{U}
denote the
downward closure of a subset
U\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{*}
with respect to

\leq_{\mathcal{B}}
:

\mathord{\downarrow}_{\mathcal{B}}{U}\defeql\left\{a\in\mathbb{N}^{*}\mid\left(\exists b\elm U\right)a\leq_{\mathcal{B}}b\right\}.

The
\zeta
-inference can be eliminated in the
following sense; see e.g. Troelstra and van Dalen
[
26
, Chapter 4, Exercise 4.8.10]
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\bcov_{\mathcal{B}}
be the relation from
\mathbb{N}^{*}
to
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N}^{*})

inductively defined by
\eta
and
\digamma
-rules. Then

a\cov_{\mathcal{B}}U\leftrightarrow a\bcov_{\mathcal{B}}\mathord{\downarrow}_{\mathcal{B}}{U}

for all
a\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and
U\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{*}
.

Proof.

By induction on
\cov_{\mathcal{B}}
and
\bcov_{\mathcal{B}}
.
∎

Using Lemma
3.2
, it is straightforward to show

a\leq_{\mathcal{B}}b\leftrightarrow a\cov_{\mathcal{B}}\left\{b\right\}

(3.1)

for all
a,b\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
. From Lemma
3.2
and
(
3.1
), we can obtain the following elementary characterisation of
an ideal point of
\mathcal{B}
.

Proposition 3.3
.

A subset
\alpha\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{*}
is an ideal point of
\mathcal{B}
if and only if

(1)

\alpha\meets\alpha
;

(2)

a,b\elm\alpha\imp\alpha\meets(a\downarrow b)
for all

a,b\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
;

(3)

{a}*{x}\elm\alpha\imp a\elm\alpha
for all

a\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and
x\in\mathbb{N}
;

(4)

a\elm\alpha\imp\left(\exists x\in\mathbb{N}\right){a}*{x}\elm\alpha
for all

a\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
.

The above characterisation
provides us with a geometric intuition of an ideal point of

\mathcal{B}
as an infinitely proceeding sequence

\mathbf{\mathit{nil}},x_{0},x_{0}*x_{1},x_{0}*x_{1}*x_{2},\cdots

of one-step extensions of elements of
\mathbb{N}^{*}
starting from the empty
sequence
\mathbf{\mathit{nil}}
; at each
“stage”
x_{0}*\cdots*x_{n}
of this sequence, the next step

x_{0}*\cdots*x_{n}*x
is constructed by choosing an arbitrary
element
x\in\mathbb{N}
without any restriction (cf. condition
(
4
) of Proposition
3.3
).
Thus, the notion of ideal point of
\mathcal{B}
can be considered as
a possible manifestation of free choice sequences
[
8
, Chapter 3]
. This led us
to identify choice sequences with ideal points of

\mathcal{B}
.

Definition 3.4
.

A
choice sequence
is an ideal point of
\mathcal{B}
.

We here leave the reader to check that in the Minimalist Foundation
(in particular, without assuming any form of axiom of choice) one can prove that:

Theorem 3.5
.

There is a bijective correspondence between choice sequences, i.e. ideal points of
\mathcal{B}
,
and functions from

\mathbb{N}
to
\mathbb{N}
, i.e. total and single-valued relations from
\mathbb{N}

to
\mathbb{N}
.

3.2.
Continuity principles for the Baire space

The principle of bar induction and the principle of continuity for functions from
choice sequences to the natural numbers are closely related.
In particular, Brouwer introduced bar induction in his analysis of the
computation tree of such a function. Hence, we focus on the
continuity principle
between the Baire space and the natural numbers in the pointfree
setting, and study its connection to various forms of bar induction.
The standard reference of bar induction is Troelstra and van Dalen

[
26
, Chapter 4, Section 8]
.

Definition 3.6
.

For every set
S
, we define the
discrete positive topology

on

to be a positive topology

\mathcal{D}S=(S,\cov_{\mathcal{D}S},\pos_{\mathcal{D}S})

where cover and positivity are defined by

\displaystyle a\cov_{\mathcal{D}S}U

\displaystyle\defeqiv a\elm U,

\displaystyle a\pos_{\mathcal{D}S}U

\displaystyle\defeqiv a\elm U.

It is easy to see that
\pos_{\mathcal{D}S}
is the
greatest positivity compatible with

\cov_{\mathcal{D}S}

\mathcal{D}S
are
singletons of

.
The
formal topology of natural numbers
is the discrete positive topology

\mathcal{D}\mathbb{N}
on the set
\mathbb{N}
of natural numbers,
which we denote by

\mathcal{N}=\left(\mathbb{N},\cov_{\mathcal{N}},\pos_{\mathcal{N}}\right)
.

A relation

from a poset
(X,\leq)
to a set

if

\downarrow s^{-}y=s^{-}y

for all
y\in Y
. The
monotonisation
of a relation
s\subseteq X\times Y
is the composition
s\mathop{\circ}\leq
, which
is obviously monotone.

Lemma 3.7

Every formal map

Immediate from Lemma

Lemma 3.8

For every monotone relation

(

(

\Rightarrow

For each

(3.2)

Now assume (

We show that

Suppose that

Then (

\cov_{\mathcal{B}}\subseteq\cov_{\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{I}p}}

(

\Rightarrow

1

Assume (

a\mathrel{s}n

and

a\cov_{\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{I}p}}s^{-}n\downarrow_{\mathcal{I}p}s^{-}m\cov_{\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{I}p}}s^{-}(n\downarrow m).

Since

\alpha_{a}\meets s^{-}(n\downarrow m)
. Hence

.
∎

Recall from
[
26
, Chapter 4, Section 8]
that a subset

is a

if every choice sequence has a
neighbourhood which is in

\left(\forall\alpha\colon\mathit{IP}t(\mathcal{B})\right)\alpha\meets U.

The

Proposition 3.9
.

For every monotone

(2)

to

Fix a monotone relation

if and only if

By Lemma

We say that a subset

The

The following are equivalent.

(1)

(2)

\mathrm{PoPC}_{\mathcal{B},\mathcal{N}}

(3)

Proof.

Obviously (

(

Thus, it suffices to show

(

\Rightarrow

3.4.
Relativisation to spreads

In this section, we relativise our continuity
principle to spreads. This is in accord
with Brouwer
[
3
]
,
who considered continuity of functions on
choice sequences which belong to a fixed spread.

Definition 3.22
.

A
spread
is a decidable tree of natural numbers in which every node
has an extension. Specifically, a spread is an inhabited decidable subset

U\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{*}
such that

(1)

a\elm U\amp a\leq_{\mathcal{B}}b\imp b\elm U
,

(2)

a\elm U\imp\left(\exists n\in\mathbb{N}\right){a}*{n}\elm U

for all
a,b\in S
and
U\subseteq S
.
Note that every spread enters
\pos_{\mathcal{B}}
.
We say that a choice sequence
\alpha\colon\mathit{IP}t(\mathcal{B})

belongs to
a
spread
U
if
\alpha\subseteq U
, i.e. every choice made by
\alpha
is in the spread.

Every spread
U
determines a cover
\cov_{U}
and a
positivity
\pos_{U}
on
\mathbb{N}^{*}
by

\displaystyle a\cov_{U}V

\displaystyle\defeqiv a\cov_{\mathcal{B}}\neg U\cup V

\displaystyle a\pos_{U}V

\displaystyle\defeqiv a\pos_{\mathcal{B}}U\cap V

(3.8)

where
\neg U\defeql\left\{a\in\mathbb{N}^{*}\mid\neg\left(a\elm U\right)\right\}
.

Lemma 3.23
.

Let
U
be a spread.

(1)

\mathcal{B}_{U}\defeql(\mathbb{N}^{*},\cov_{U},\pos_{U})
is a positive topology.

(2)

\pos_{U}
is the

positivity that is compatible with
\cov_{U}
.

(3)

\alpha\colon\mathit{IP}t(\mathcal{B}_{U})

if and only if
\alpha
is a choice sequence belonging to

Proof.

(
1
) We leave the reader to check that

\cov_{U}
is a cover and
\pos_{U}
is a positivity. We show that

\cov_{U}
and
\pos_{U}
are compatible. Let
a\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and

. Suppose that
a\cov_{U}V
and
a\pos_{U}W
. Since
\cov_{\mathcal{B}}
and
\pos_{\mathcal{B}}

have
(\neg U\cup V)\pos_{\mathcal{B}}U\cap W

\neg U\pos_{\mathcal{B}}U\cap W

V\pos_{\mathcal{B}}U\cap W

V\pos_{\mathcal{B}}U\cap W

V\pos_{U}W

(

\pos^{\prime}
be a positivity on

\mathbb{N}^{*}
which is compatible with
\cov_{U}
.
We show that

\frac{a\pos^{\prime}V\quad\left(\forall b\in\mathbb{N}^{*}\right)\left(b\pos^{\prime}V\imp b\elm W\right)}{a\pos_{\mathcal{B}}W}

To this end, fix
V,W\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{*}
, and put
V^{\prime}=\left\{a\in\mathbb{N}^{*}\mid a\pos^{\prime}V\right\}
. Suppose that
a\pos^{\prime}V
and

\left(\forall b\in\mathbb{N}^{*}\right)b\pos^{\prime}V\imp b\elm W
.
This is equivalent to

and

.
Since
\cov_{\mathcal{B}}\subseteq\cov_{U}

\pos^{\prime}
is compatible with

. Thus
\pos^{\prime}\subseteq\pos_{\mathcal{B}}
. Since

a\pos_{\mathcal{B}}V^{\prime}

a\pos_{\mathcal{B}}W
.

Now, to see that

for all

and

. Then, trivially

. Moreover, either

or

a\elm\neg U

a\cov_{U}\emptyset
, and

Hence
a\elm U

a\elm U\cap V
.

(

) First, suppose that

\alpha

is an ideal point of
\mathcal{B}
. Moreover, since

, we have

. As

\alpha

belongs to
U

\alpha
enters

\pos_{U}

a\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and
V\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{*}
, and suppose
that

The other properties of ideal points are easily checked.
∎

We often identify a spread

Lemma 3.24
.

Every spread is a retract of
\mathcal{B}

This is well known in point-set topology (cf. Troelstra and van

[

Fix a spread

\displaystyle\frac{}{\mathbf{\mathit{nil}}\mathrel{s}\mathbf{\mathit{nil}}}\qquad\frac{a\mathrel{s}b\quad b*n\elm U}{a*n\mathrel{s}b*n}\qquad\frac{a\mathrel{s}b\quad\neg(b*n\elm U)}{a*n\mathrel{s}b*l}

We consider the continuity principle for the formal

Definition 4.1

Define a preorder

and a strict order

\displaystyle(p,q)

\displaystyle\leq_{\mathcal{R}}(p^{\prime},q^{\prime})\defeqiv p^{\prime}\leq p\amp q\leq q^{\prime},

\displaystyle(p,q)

\displaystyle<_{\mathcal{R}}(p^{\prime},q^{\prime})\defeqiv p^{\prime}<p\amp q<q^{\prime}.

The

is a positive topology

\mathcal{R}=(S_{\mathcal{R}},\cov_{\mathcal{R}},\pos_{\mathcal{R}})

\cov_{\mathcal{R}}
