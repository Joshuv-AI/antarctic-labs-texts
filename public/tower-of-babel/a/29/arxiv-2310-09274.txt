Geometry of unimodular systems
I.V.Artamkin

Abstract
A collection of vectors in a real vector space is called a unimodular system if any of its maximal linearly independent subsets generates the same free abelian group. This notion is closely connected with totally unimodular matrices : rows or columns of a totally unimodular matrix form a unimodular system and the matrix of coefficients of expansions of all vectors of a unimodular system with respect to its maximal linearly independent subset is totally unimodular. In this paper we show that a unimodular system defines the following geometric data: a Euclidean space, an integral lattice in it, and a reflexive lattice zonotope. The discriminant of the lattice is equal to the number of maximal linearly independent subsystems, and we call this number the complexity of the unimodular system. For a unimodular system Ω \Omega we also define the Gale dual unimodular system Ω ⊥ \Omega^{\bot} which has the same complexity. These notions may be illustrated by the well-known graphic and cographic unimodular systems of a graph. Both graphic and cographic unimodular systems have the same complexity which is equal to the complexity of the graph. For graphs without loops and bridges the graphic and the cographic unimodular systems are Gale dual to each other. We describe this geometric data for certain examples: for the graphic and the cographic unimodular systems of a generalized theta-graph, consisting of two vertices connected by N N edges, for the cographic system of the complete graph K N K_{N} , and for the famous Bixby-Seymour unimodular system, which is neither graphic nor cographic.

In the case of planar graphs, the connection between these two

turns out to be the cographic system of the dual graph

\widehat{\Gamma}
and vice versa.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
\Gamma
be a planar graph embedded in a sphere
S^{2}
, and

\widehat{\Gamma}
be the dual embeded graph.
Then the graphic
system of the graph

\Gamma
is isomorphic to the cographic system of the dual graph

\widehat{\Gamma}
and the cographic system of the graph

\Gamma
is isomorphic to the graphic
system of the dual graph

\widehat{\Gamma}
.

Embedded graph
\Gamma
defines a cellular decomposition of the
sphere
S^{2}
, which provides the chain complex

C_{2}(\Gamma,\mathbb{R})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\partial_{2}(\Gamma)}}{{\to}}C_{1}(\Gamma,\mathbb{R})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\partial_{1}(\Gamma)}}{{\to}}C_{0}(\Gamma,\mathbb{R}).

(3.15)

(Here

C_{2}(\Gamma,\mathbb{R})=\{\sum_{\mbox{\tiny faces }f\ \mbox{\tiny of the embeded graph }\Gamma}c_{f}\cdot f,\quad c_{f}\in\mathbb{R}\},

(3.16)

orientation of all the 2-cells is induced from the orientation of

S^{2}
, and
\partial_{2}(\Gamma)
is the standard border mapping.)

Since
H_{1}(S^{2})=0
the sequence (
3.15
) is
exact, i.e.

\operatorname{Ker}(\partial_{1}(\Gamma))=\operatorname{Im}(\partial_{2}(\Gamma)).

(3.17)

We may fix
the scalar product on
C_{2}(\Gamma,\mathbb{R})
in the same way as it was
done on
C_{0}(\Gamma,\mathbb{R})
and
C_{1}(\Gamma,\mathbb{R})
(the 2-cells form an
orthonormal basis) and identify

with its dual.
Then we get the dual sequence to (

)

C_{0}(\Gamma,\mathbb{R})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\partial_{1}(\Gamma)^{*}}}{{\to}}C_{1}(\Gamma,\mathbb{R})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\partial_{2}(\Gamma)^{*}}}{{\to}}C_{2}(\Gamma,\mathbb{R})

(3.18)

which is nothing else but the sequence
(
3.15
) for the dual graph
\widehat{\Gamma}

C_{2}(\widehat{\Gamma},\mathbb{R})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\partial_{2}(\widehat{\Gamma})}}{{\to}}C_{1}(\widehat{\Gamma},\mathbb{R})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\partial_{1}(\widehat{\Gamma})}}{{\to}}C_{0}(\widehat{\Gamma},\mathbb{R})

and therefore
\partial_{2}(\Gamma)=\partial_{1}(\widehat{\Gamma})^{*}
and

\operatorname{Ker}(\partial_{1}(\Gamma))=\operatorname{Im}(\partial_{1}(\widehat{\Gamma})^{*}).

This implies that the graphic system for
\Gamma
is the
cographic system for
\widehat{\Gamma}
and vice versa.
\square

4
The lattice.

Let
\Omega=(U,\Xi)
be an unimodular system,
\Xi=[\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{N}]
,
\xi_{i}\in U^{*}
, and
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{N}

generate over
\mathbb{Z}
a free abelian group
M\subset U^{*}
of rank
n
.
Consider the linear mapping

\Phi_{\Omega}:U\to\mathbb{R}^{N}\ \mbox{ defined by }\ \Phi_{\Omega}(u)=(\xi_{1}(u),\ldots,\xi_{N}(u)).

(4.1)

Put

W_{\Omega}=\Phi_{\Omega}(U)\ \mbox{ and }\ L_{\Omega}=W_{\Omega}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{N}

(4.2)

where
\mathbb{Z}^{N}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}

is the standard lattice consisting of vectors with integer coordinates.

Proposition 4.1
.

The mapping
\Phi_{\Omega}
is injective (i.e.
\dim W_{\Omega}=n
) and

{L_{\Omega}=\Phi_{\Omega}(M^{*})}
.

Corollary 4.1
.

The
rank of the lattice

L_{\Omega}
is equal to
n
.

Since
\Xi
generates
U^{*}
the mapping
\Phi_{\Omega}
is
injective. Choose a basis of M in
\Xi
, say
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n}
. (We may change the numeration of elements of

\Xi
if necessary.) Denote by
u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}
the dual
basis in
U
. Then the vectors

w_{1}=\Phi_{\Omega}(u_{1}),\ldots,w_{n}=\Phi_{\Omega}(u_{n})

(4.3)

are integer and
linearly independent in
W_{\Omega}
.

Consider any vector
w\in L_{\Omega}
then
w=\Phi_{\Omega}(u)
for
certain
u\in U
, then
\xi_{i}(u)

is integer for any
i
,
1\leq i\leq N
. Thus
u\in M^{*}
.
\square

Remark 4.1
.

Denote by
h_{1},\ldots,h_{N}
the standard basis in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
(i.e.
the
k
-th coordinate of
h_{k}
is 1, and the rest are zero) then
the matrix of the linear mapping
\Phi_{\Omega}
with respect to the
basis
u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}
is exactly the totally unimodular matrix

A_{\Omega}
from (
2.2
).

Let us fix the standard scalar product on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
:
\langle x,y\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}y_{i}
(
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})
,

y=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{N})
,
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
).

Proposition 4.2
.

The discriminant of the lattice

L_{\Omega}
is equal to the complexity of the unimodular system
c(\Omega)
.

The proof repeats a well-known argument proving the classical
Kirhhoff’s theorem (
[
4
]
): the bases of
U^{*}
consisting of
vectors from
\Xi
are in one-to-one correspondence with bases of
the matrix
A_{\Omega}
and all these non-zero minors are equal to

\pm 1
. Therefore the number of such non-zero minors by
Cauchy-Binet theorem is equal to
\det(A_{\Omega}^{t}A_{\Omega})
. And the matrix
A_{\Omega}^{t}A_{\Omega}
is nothing but the
Gram matrix of the basis (
4.3
) of the lattice
L_{\Omega}
.
\square

We can also define scalar product on
U

\langle u,v\rangle_{U}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\xi(u_{i})\xi(v_{i}),

(4.4)

then
\Phi_{\Omega}
becomes an isometry of Euclidian spaces
U
and

W_{\Omega}
and isomorphism of lattices
L_{\Omega}
and
M^{*}
.
Thus we can identify the spaces
U
and
W_{\Omega}
by means of the
isomorphism
\Phi_{\Omega}
. The linear functions
\xi_{i}
on
U

may be expressed by

\xi_{i}(u)=\langle\Phi_{\Omega}(u),h_{i}\rangle,\qquad u\in U,

(4.5)

and we shall denote the corresponding linear functions on
W_{\Omega}
by the same letter:

\xi_{i}(w)=\langle w,h_{i}\rangle,\qquad w\in W_{\Omega},

(4.6)

obtaining the same unimodular system
\Omega=(W_{\Omega},\Xi)
.

Note that in Proposition
3.2
we have
already constructed the lattice
L_{\Omega}
in the case when
\Omega

is graphic or cographic system for a connected graph
\Gamma
. Namely
for the graphic system
\mathcal{F}_{\Gamma}=(F_{\Gamma},E\setminus E_{b})
(we use the notations of Proposition

3.2
)

W_{\mathcal{F}_{\Gamma}}=F_{\Gamma},

(4.7)

and

L_{\mathcal{F}_{\Gamma}}=F_{\Gamma}(\mathbb{Z}).

(4.8)

while for cographic system

\mathcal{G}_{\Gamma}=(G_{\Gamma},E\setminus E_{l})

W_{\mathcal{G}_{\Gamma}}=G_{\Gamma}

(4.9)

and

L_{\mathcal{G}_{\Gamma}}=G_{\Gamma}(\mathbb{Z}).

(4.10)

In addition to the bases constructed in the proof of the
Proposition
3.2
the lattice

L_{\mathcal{G}_{\Gamma}}
has a remarkable basis
which is used in the proof of the Kirhhoff’s theorem. Fix a vertex

v_{0}\in V
, then the elements (
3.8
)

{\partial^{*}(v)=\sum_{e\in E\setminus E_{l}}i(e,v)\cdot e}

(4.11)

for all

,

, form a basis
of

.
The Gram matrix of this basis is obtained from the Laplacian matrix

I_{\Gamma}^{T}I_{\Gamma}
by deleting the row and the column
corresponding to the vertex

. Using Proposition

3.2
we get the Kirhhoff’s theorem:
the determinant of this Gram matrix is equal to the complexity
of the graph
\Gamma
.

Corollary 4.2
.

The lattice

for the
complete graph
K_{N}
on
N
vertices is

(see
[

]
)
— integer lattice congruent to the lattice

.

-1&-1&N-1&\ldots&-1\\
\ldots&\ldots&\ldots&\ldots&\ldots\\
-1&-1&-1&\ldots&N-1\\
\end{array}\right).

(4.12)

(see

[
3
]
, chapter 4, (77)).
\square

Note that the complexity of the complete graph is the number of
of trees on

labeled vertices and the determinant of

, so Propositions

and

The dual system.

In this section we construct the Gale dual unimodular system
\Omega^{\bot}
. Let
\Omega=(U,\Xi)
, consider the data (
4.1
)
and (
4.2
). Take the orthogonal complement to
W_{\Omega}
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, namely

W^{\bot}_{\Omega}=\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{N},\ \langle w,z\rangle=0\ \forall w\in W_{\Omega}\}.

(6.1)

Each vector
h_{i}
from the standard basis of
\mathbb{R}^{N}

(see Remark
4.1
) provides a linear function

\eta_{i}(z)=\langle h_{i},z\rangle
on

. Of course the function
\eta_{i}
may not be
non-zero on

. Suppose that some
\eta_{i}
, say

\eta_{1}
, vanishes on

. This means that
\langle h_{1},z\rangle=0
for all

and

, so

and this
is just the case described in Propositions

and

.

Proposition 6.1
.

\eta_{i}
vanishes on

if and only if
\xi_{i}

generates a direct summand of
\Omega
isomorphic to
\Upsilon
.
\square

Proposition 6.2
.

Let
\Omega=\Omega^{\prime}\oplus\Upsilon^{s}
, where
\Omega^{\prime}=(U^{\prime},\Xi^{\prime})

\Upsilon
. Then
V_{\Omega}^{\bot}=V_{\Omega^{\prime}}^{\bot}
.
\square

We have seen that
\eta_{i}
vanishes on

if and
only if

corresponds to a trivial direct summand
\Upsilon
. Let us collect al non-zero functions

(6.2)

.

Put

is a unimodular system of

It is sufficient to prove this statement for the case when

has no direct summand

. Let

,

, where

. According to

we need to prove that all linearly

the same

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
\Omega=(U,\Xi)
be
an unimodular system having no direct summands
\Upsilon
and

and

defined by (

)
and (

). Then

a) Linear functions

\eta_{j_{1}},\ldots\eta_{j_{N-n}}

and only if

are linearly

.

\Xi^{\bot}
, in b) and c) we describe the abelian group generated by

, and then d) is just the statement we

\square

Proof of Lemma
6.1

. For simplicity we may
renumber the elements of
\Xi
so that

and

.

Let’s assume that

\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n}
are not linearly independent. Then

. Therefore
{a=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n},0,\ldots,0)\in W_{\Omega}^{\bot}}
is nonzero, but
\eta_{j}(a)=0
for

and hence
\eta_{n+1},\ldots,\eta_{N}

is not a basis of
(W_{\Omega}^{\bot})^{*}
.

Next let’s assume that

\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n}
are linearly independent, then
they form a basis of
U^{*}
. Let
u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}
be the dual
basis of
U
; put

. Then

z_{i}=h_{i}+\sum_{q>n}a_{q,i}h_{q},\quad 1\leq i\leq n,

(6.3)

where

are the entries of the matrix

from (

). (Recall that

is the matrix of the mapping
\Phi_{\Omega}
, see remark

. Put

then

\langle z_{i},w_{j}\rangle=0
for

,

. The elements

for

are linearly

W_{\Omega}^{\bot}
over

\mathbb{R}
, which proves item a). Note that these elements are integer:

, therefore rank of the lattice

is

, which proves item b).

Let’s prove that
w_{n+1},\ldots,w_{N}
also form a basis of the

W_{\Omega}^{\bot}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{N}

be an integer element of
W_{\Omega}^{\bot}
, i.e. all the

are

and since

all

.
Therefore

and
{w=-\sum_{j>n}x_{j}w_{j}}
is the

with integer

w_{n+1},\ldots,w_{N}
