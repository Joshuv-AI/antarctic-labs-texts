Abstract
. Partition functions are an important research object in combinatorics and mathematical physics [Barvinok, 2016]. In this work, we consider the partition function of the Ising antiferromagnet on random regular graphs and characterize its limiting distribution in the replica symmetric phase up to the Kesten-Stigum bound. Our proof relies on a careful execution of the method of moments, spatial mixing arguments and small subgraph conditioning.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Motivation

The Ising model, invented by Lenz in 1920 to explain magnetism, is a cornerstone in statistical physics.
Consider any graph
G
with vertex set
V
and edge set
E
. Each vertex carries one of two possible spins
\pm 1
and the interactions between vertices are represented by
E
. For a spin configuration
\sigma\in\left\{{\pm 1}\right\}^{V}
on
G
, we can consider the Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}_{G}

\mathcal{H}_{G}(\sigma)=\sum_{(v,w)\in E}\frac{1+\sigma_{v}\sigma_{w}}{2}.

Together with a real parameter
\beta>0
the Hamiltonian gives rise to a distribution on spin configurations defined by

(1.1)

\mu_{G,\beta}(\sigma)=\frac{\exp\left({-\beta\mathcal{H}_{G}(\sigma)}\right)}{Z_{G,\beta}}\quad\left({\sigma\in\left\{{\pm 1}\right\}^{V}}\right)\quad\text{where}\quad Z_{G,\beta}=\sum_{\tau\in\left\{{\pm 1}\right\}^{V}}\exp\left({-\beta\mathcal{H}_{G}(\tau)}\right).

The probability measure
\mu_{G,\beta}
is known as the Boltzmann distribution with the normalizing term
Z_{G,\beta}
being the partition function.
\mu_{G,\beta}
favors configurations with few edges between vertices of the same spin which is known as the antiferromagnetic Ising model. There is a corresponding formulation of (
1.1
) where edges between vertices of the same spin are preferred - the ferromagnetic Ising model. Both models are of great interest in combinatorics and physics and the literature on each is vast
[
7
]
.

In this paper, we study the Ising antiferromagnet on the random
d
-regular graph
\mathbb{G}=\mathbb{G}(n,d)
. One might be tempted to think that the regularities of this graph model provide a more amenable study object than its well-known Erdős-Rényi counterpart with fluctuating vertex degrees. However, for the Ising model the reverse seems to be true.
Indeed, the independence of edges in the Erdős-Rényi-model greatly facilitates deriving the distribution of short cycles in the planted model and simplifies the calculation of both the first and second moment.

Clearly,
\mu_{\mathbb{G},\beta}
gives rise to correlations between spins of nearby vertices. The degree of such correlations is governed by the choice of
\beta
. A question which is of keen interest in combinatorics and statistical physics is whether such correlations persist for two uniformly sampled (and thus likely distant) vertices. According to physics predictions, for small values of
\beta
we should observe a rapid decay of correlation
[
10
]
and thus no
long-range correlations
. This regime is known as the
replica symmetric phase
. It is suggested that there exists a specific
\beta
which marks the onset of long-range correlations in
\mathbb{G}
. This value is conjectured to be at the combinatorially meaningful Kesten-Stigum bound
[
3
]

\beta_{\text{KS}}=\log\left({\frac{\sqrt{d-1}+1}{\sqrt{d-1}-1}}\right).

The question of long-range correlations is tightly related to the partition function
Z_{\mathbb{G},\beta}
from which also various combinatorially meaningful observables can be derived. The
Max Cut
on random
d
-regular graphs is a case in point due to the well-known relation

\mbox{\sc MaxCut}(G)=\frac{dn}{2}+\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta}\log Z_{G,\beta}.

for any graph
G
. Thus, it is of key interest to understand the behavior of
Z_{\mathbb{G},\beta}
.

1.2.
Result

In recent work,
[
3
]
were able to pinpoint the replica symmetry breaking phase transition at the Kesten-Stigum bound, thus charting the replica symmetric phase for the Ising antiferromagnet on random
d
-regular graphs.
The key feature of the replica-symmetric phase is that w.h.p. two independent samples
\bm{\sigma}_{1},\bm{\sigma}_{2}
from the Boltzmann distribution
\mu_{\mathbb{G},\beta}
exhibit an almost flat overlap in the sense that
\left|{\bm{\sigma}_{1}\cdot\bm{\sigma}_{2}}\right|=o(n)
.
To be precise,
[
3
]
determined
Z_{\mathbb{G},\beta}
up to an error term
\exp(o(n))
for
\beta<\beta_{\text{KS}}
.
In this paper, we move beyond this crude approximation. By deriving the limiting distribution in the replica-symmetric phase, we show that
Z_{\mathbb{G},\beta}
is tightly concentrated with bounded fluctuations which we can quantify and attribute to short cycles in
\mathbb{G}
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Assume that
0<\beta<\beta_{\text{KS}}
and
d\geq 3
. Let
(\Lambda_{i})_{i}
be a sequence of independent Poisson variables with
\mathbb{E}\left[{\Lambda_{i}}\right]=\lambda_{i}
where
\lambda_{i}=\frac{\left({d-1}\right)^{i}}{2i}
.
Then as
n\to\infty
we have

\displaystyle\log\left({Z_{\mathbb{G}(n,d),\beta}}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\log\left({\frac{1+e^{\beta}}{2+de^{\beta}-d}}\right)-n\left({\left({1-\frac{d}{2}}\right)\log\left({2}\right)+\frac{d}{2}\log\left({1+e^{-\beta}}\right)}\right)+\frac{d-1}{2}\frac{e^{-\beta}-1}{e^{-\beta}+1}+\frac{\left({d-1}\right)^{2}}{4}\left({\frac{e^{-\beta}-1}{e^{-\beta}+1}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\xrightarrow{d}\log\left({W}\right):=\sum_{i=3}^{\infty}\Lambda_{i}\log\left({1+\left({\frac{e^{-\beta}-1}{e^{-\beta}+1}}\right)^{i}}\right)-\frac{\left({d-1}\right)^{i}}{2i}\left({\frac{e^{-\beta}-1}{e^{-\beta}+1}}\right)^{i}.

The infinite product defining
W
converges a.s. and in
L^{2}
.

Taking the expectation of this distribution readily recovers the first part of the result by
[
3
]
.
The proof of Theorem
1.1
relies on the combination of the method of moments and small subgraph conditioning enriched in our case by spatial mixing arguments to make the calculation of the second moment tractable.

2.
Techniques

2.1.
Notation

Let
\mathbb{G}=\mathbb{G}(n,d)
denote a random
d
-regular graph on
n
vertices. We consider sparse graphs with constant
d
as
n\to\infty
. Throughout the paper, we will employ standard Landau notation with the usual symbols
o(\cdot),O(\cdot),\Theta(\cdot),\omega(\cdot)
, and
\Omega(\cdot)
to refer to the limit
n\to\infty
. We say that a sequence of events
(\mathcal{E}_{n})_{n}
holds
with high probability
(w.h.p.) if
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left[{\mathcal{E}_{n}}\right]=1
. When the context is clear we might drop the index of the expectation. Moreover, we will use the proportional
\propto
to hide necessary normalisations.

2.2.
Outline

To get a handle on the distribution of
Z_{\mathbb{G},\beta}
in the replica symmetric phase, we need to identify the sources of fluctuations of
Z_{\mathbb{G},\beta}
. One obvious source is the number of short cycles. Since
\mathbb{G}
is sparse and random, standard arguments reveal that
\mathbb{G}
contains only few short cycles. In the following, let
C_{i}(G)
denote the number of short cycles of length
i
in a graph
G
and
\mathcal{F}_{\ell}
the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the random variables
C_{i}(\mathbb{G})
for
i\leq\ell
. A key quantity to consider is the variance of
Z_{\mathbb{G},\beta}
. By standard decomposition, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[{Z_{\mathbb{G},\beta}^{2}}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[{Z_{\mathbb{G},\beta}}\right]^{2}=\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbb{E}\left[{Z_{\mathbb{G},\beta}\mid\mathcal{F}_{\ell}}\right]^{2}-\mathbb{E}\left[{Z_{\mathbb{G},\beta}}\right]^{2}}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbb{E}\left[{Z_{\mathbb{G},\beta}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{\ell}}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[{Z_{\mathbb{G},\beta}\mid\mathcal{F}_{\ell}}\right]^{2}}\right]

for any
\ell\geq 1
.
Note that the first term of the r.h.s. describes the contribution to the variance by the fluctuations in the number of short cycles, while the second term accounts for the conditional variance given the number of short cycles. It turns out that as
\ell\to\infty
after taking
n\to\infty
, the second summand vanishes. In other words, the entire variance of
Z_{\mathbb{G},\beta}
is due to fluctuations in the number of short cycles.

To show this property formally, we leverage a result by
[
8
]
that stipulates conditions under which one is able to describe the limiting distribution of
Z_{\mathbb{G},\beta}
(see Theorem
4.1
in the appendix).
One ingredient is the distribution of short cycles in
\mathbb{G}
and a planted model
\mathbb{G}^{*}
. In
\mathbb{G}^{*}
, we first select a spin configuration
\sigma
uniformly at random and subsequently sample a graph
G
with probability proportional to
\exp\left({-\beta\mathcal{H}_{G}(\sigma)}\right)
. While the distribution of short cycles in
\mathbb{G}
is well established, the distribution of short cycles in the planted model
\mathbb{G}^{*}
is a key contribution of this paper. The second ingredient is a careful application of the method of moments. Unfortunately, standard results on the first and second moment on random regular graphs (see i.e.
[
3
]
), do not suffice in our case and we have to sharpen our pencils to yield an error term of order
O\left({\exp(1/n)}\right)
. While the need for this lower error term prolongs calculations, it also poses some challenges that we resolve by a careful application of the Laplace’s method as suggested by
[
5
]
and spatial mixing arguments.

2.3.
Short cycles

To get started, let us write

(2.1)

\delta_{i}=\left({\frac{e^{-\beta}-1}{e^{-\beta}+1}}\right)^{i}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lambda_{i}=\frac{\left({d-1}\right)^{i}}{2i}.

The first item on the agenda is to derive the distribution of short cycles in
\mathbb{G}
. This is a well-established result.

Fact 2.1

(Theorem 9.5 in
[
9
]
)
.

Let
\Lambda_{i}\sim{\rm Po}(\lambda_{i})
be a sequence of independent Poisson random variables for
i\geq 3
. Then jointly for all
i
we have
C_{i}(\mathbb{G})\xrightarrow{d}\Lambda_{i}
as
n\to\infty
.

Deriving the distribution of short cycles in the planted model
\mathbb{G}^{*}
informally introduced above requires some more work. Let us start with the definitions. Given
\sigma\in\left\{{\pm 1}\right\}^{V}
and for any
\beta>0
, let us define the distribution of
\mathbb{G}^{*}(\sigma)
for any event
\mathcal{A}
as

(2.2)

\mathbb{P}\left[{\mathbb{G}^{*}(\sigma)\in\mathcal{A}}\right]\propto\mathbb{E}\left[{\exp\left({-\beta\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{G}}(\sigma)}\right)\bm{1}\left\{{\mathbb{G}\in\mathcal{A}}\right\}}\right].

This definition gives rise to the following experiment. First, draw a spin configuration
\bm{\sigma}^{*}
uniformly at random among all configurations
\left\{{\pm 1}\right\}^{V}
. In the next step, draw
\mathbb{G}^{*}=\mathbb{G}^{*}(\bm{\sigma}^{*})
according to (
2.2
). Hereafter,
\mathbb{G}^{*}
will be denoted the planted model.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let

\Xi_{i}\sim{\rm Po}\left({\lambda_{i}\left({1+\delta_{i}}\right)}\right)

be a sequence of independent Poisson random variables for
i\geq 3
. Then jointly for all
i
we have
C_{i}(\mathbb{G}^{*})\xrightarrow{d}\Xi_{i}
as
n\to\infty
.

Establishing the distribution of short cycles in
\mathbb{G}^{*}
is one of the main contributions of this paper. To this end, we start off with similar arguments as used in
[
11
]
, but need to diligently account for the subtle dependencies introduced by the regularities in
\mathbb{G}^{*}
.

Applying Fact
2.1
and Proposition
2.2
to Theorem 1 in
[
8
]
requires a slight detour via the Nishimori property. To this end, note that the random graph
\mathbb{G}
induces a reweighted graph distribution
\hat{\mathbb{G}}
which for any event
\mathcal{A}
is defined by

(2.3)

\mathbb{P}\left[{\hat{\mathbb{G}}\in\mathcal{A}}\right]\propto\mathbb{E}\left[{Z_{\mathbb{G},\beta}\bm{1}\left\{{\mathbb{G}\in\mathcal{A}}\right\}}\right].

Moreover, consider the distribution
\hat{\bm{\sigma}}
on spin configurations defined by

(2.4)

\mathbb{P}\left[{\hat{\bm{\sigma}}=\sigma}\right]\propto\mathbb{E}\left[{\exp\left({-\beta\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{G}}(\sigma)}\right)}\right]

for any
\beta>0
.

\hat{\mathbb{G}},\mathbb{G}^{*},\hat{\bm{\sigma}},\bm{\sigma}^{*}
, and the Boltzmann distribution from (

) are connected via the well-known Nishimori property.

Fact 2.3

(Proposition 3.2 in
[
4
]
)
.

For any graph
G
and spin configuration
\sigma\in\left\{{\pm 1}\right\}^{V}
we have

\mathbb{P}\left[{\hat{\mathbb{G}}=G}\right]\mu_{G}(\sigma)=\mathbb{P}\left({\hat{\bm{\sigma}}=\sigma}\right)\mathbb{P}\left({\mathbb{G}^{*}=G\mid\bm{\sigma}^{*}=\sigma}\right).

The first and second moment

The second key ingredient towards the proof of Theorem

is the method of moments. As standard random regular graph results are too crude, we need a more precise calculation. Fortunately, with some patience and equipped with Laplace’s method as stated in
[
5
]

Proposition 2.4
.

Assume that
0<\beta<\beta_{\text{KS}}
and

\mathbb{E}\left[{Z_{\mathbb{G},\beta}}\right]=\exp\left({-\lambda_{1}\delta_{1}-\lambda_{2}\delta_{2}+O\left({\frac{1}{n}}\right)}\right)\sqrt{\frac{1+e^{\beta}}{2+de^{\beta}-d}}\exp\left({n\left({\left({1-d/2}\right)\log\left({2}\right)+d\log\left({1+e^{-\beta}}\right)/2}\right)}\right)

3.
Discussion

Studying partition functions has a long tradition in combinatorics and mathematical physics.
k
-SAT,
q
-coloring or the stochastic block model are just some noteworthy examples where the partition function reveals fundamental and novel combinatorial insights. Due to its connection to the
Max Cut
problem and the disassortative stochastic block model, the Ising antiferromagnet fits nicely into this list.
For random
d
-regular graphs, Coja-Oghlan et al.
[
3
]
pinpointed its replica symmetry breaking phase transition at the Kesten-Stigum bound. Using the method of moments and spatial mixing arguments, they they determine
Z_{\mathbb{G},\beta}
up to
\exp(o(n))
.
In this paper, we move beyond this approximation and derive the limiting distribution of
Z_{\mathbb{G},\beta}
in the replica symmetric regime. We note that the distribution of
Z_{\mathbb{G},\beta}
above the Kesten-Stigum bound is fundamentally different.
A similar analysis for the Erdős-Rényi-model was carried out in
[
11
]
.

Using the combination of the method of moments and small subgraph conditioning underlying our proof was initially pioneered by Robinson & Wormald
[
12
]
to prove that cubic graphs are w.h.p. Hamiltonian. Janson
[
8
]
subsequently showed that small subgraph conditioning can be used to obtain limiting distributions. This strategy was successfully applied, among others, to the stochastic block model
[
11
]
and the Viana-Bray model
[
6
]
.
For other problems, the second moment appears to be too crude for the entire replica symmetric phase and enhanced techniques are needed
[
2
]
. In this work, we enrich the classical strategy of the method of moments and small subgraph conditioning by spatial mixing arguments to cover the entire replica symmetric phase.

An interesting remaining question is to throw a bridge between the properties of the partition function
Z_{\mathbb{G},\beta}
and long-range correlations in
\mathbb{G}
. While it should be a small step from Theorem
1.1
to vindicate the absence of long-range correlations in the replica symmetric phase, proving the presence of long-range correlations above the Kesten-Stigum bound is a more challenging, yet important endeavour.

4.
Getting started

Before moving to the proofs of Propositions
2.2
,
2.4
and
2.6
, let us introduce some additional notation.
With
\mathcal{P}
denoting the set of all probability distributions on a finite set
\Omega\neq\emptyset
and two probability measures
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
, let us introduce the entropy
H(\mu)
and Kullback-Leibler divergence
D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left({{{\mu}\|{\nu}}}\right)

H(\mu)=-\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}\mu(\omega)\log\mu(\omega)\qquad\text{and}\qquad D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left({{{\mu}\|{\nu}}}\right)=\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}\mu(\omega)\log\frac{\mu(\omega)}{\nu(\omega)}\in[0,\infty].

Note the convention
0\cdot\log\left(\frac{0}{0}\right)=0
and furthermore that if there exists some
\omega\in\Omega
such that
\mu(\omega)>0
and
\nu(\omega)=0
, this implies
D_{\textrm{KL}}(\mu\|\nu)=\infty
.
When we consider the product measure between two probability distribution
\mu
and
\nu
, we will use the notation
\mu\otimes\nu
.

Next, let us state a fundamental result by Janson
[
8
]
which stipulates conditions under which one is able to obtain the limiting distribution of the partition function.

Theorem 4.1

(Theorem 1 in
[
8
]
)
.

Let
\lambda_{i}>0
and
\delta_{i}\geq-1,i=1,2,\dots,
be constants and suppose that for each
n
there are random variables
C_{in},i=1,2,\dots,
and
Z_{n}
(defined on the same probability space) such that
X_{in}
is non-negative integer valued and
\mathbb{E}\left[{Z_{n}}\right]\neq 0
(at least of large n), and furthermore the following conditions are satisfied:

(1)

C_{in}\xrightarrow{d}\Lambda_{i}
as
n\to\infty
, jointly for all
i
where
\Lambda_{i}\sim{\rm Po}\left({\lambda_{i}}\right)
are independent Poisson random variables;

(2)

For any finite sequence
c_{1},\dots,c_{m}
of non-negative integers,

\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[{Z_{n}\mid C_{1n}=c_{1},\dots,C_{mn}=c_{m}}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[{Z_{n}}\right]}\to\prod_{i=1}^{m}(1+\delta_{i})^{x_{i}}\exp\left({-\lambda_{i}\delta_{i}}\right)\qquad\text{as}\quad n\to\infty;

(3)

\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\delta_{i}^{2}<\infty;

(4)

\mathbb{E}\left[{Z_{n}^{2}}\right]/\left({\mathbb{E}\left[{Z_{n}}\right]}\right)^{2}\to\exp\left({\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\delta_{i}^{2}}\right)\qquad\text{as}\quad n\to\infty.

Then, we have

\frac{Z_{n}}{\mathbb{E}\left[{Z_{n}}\right]}\xrightarrow{d}W=\prod_{i\geq 1}(1+\delta_{i})^{\Lambda_{i}}\exp\left({-\lambda_{i}\delta_{i}}\right);

moreover, this and the convergence in (1) hold jointly. The infinite product defining
W
converges a.s. and in
L^{2}
, with
\mathbb{E}\left({W}\right)=1
and
\mathbb{E}\left({W^{2}}\right)=\exp\left({\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{i}\delta_{i}^{2}}\right)
. Hence, the normalized variables
Y_{n}/\mathbb{E}\left({Y_{n}}\right)
are uniformly square integrable. Furthermore, the event
W>0
equals, up to a set of probability zero, the event that
Z_{i}>0
for some
i
with
\delta_{i}=-1
. In particular,
W>0
a.s. if and only if every
\delta_{i}>-1
.

A substantial part of this paper is devoted to determining the first and second moment of

. As we will see in due course, this task requires a special version of the well-known Laplace’s method, which is usually formulated in terms of integrals. In contrast to that, the model considered here is discrete and therefore requires a variation of Laplace’s method which is applicable to countable sums. Fortunately,

5
]
provides an adaptation that we can leverage here. Let us start by providing the result of interest:

Theorem 4.2

(Theorem 2.3 in
[

)

Suppose the following:

(1)

\mathcal{L}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
is a lattice with rank
r\leq N
.

(2)

V\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
is the
r
-dimensional subspace spanned by
\mathcal{L}
.

(3)

W=V+w
is an affine subspace parallel to
V
, for some

.

(4)

K\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
is a compact convex set with non empty interior
K^{\circ}
.

(5)

\phi:K\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a continuous function and the restriction of
\phi
to

has a unique maximum at some point

.

(6)

\phi
is twice continuously differentiable in a neighbourhood of

and

is its Hessian at
x_{0}
.

(7)

\psi:K_{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a continuous function on some neighbourhood
K_{1}\subseteq K
of
x_{0}
with
\psi\left(x_{0}\right)>0
.

(8)

For each positive integer

there is a vector
\ell_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
with
\frac{\ell_{n}}{n}\in W
.

(9)

For each positive integer

there is a positive real number

and a function

such that, as

\displaystyle a_{n}\left(\ell\right)=O\left(b_{n}e^{n\phi\left(\ell/n\right)+o\left(n\right)}\right),\hskip 50.00008pt\ell\in\left(\mathcal{L}+\ell_{n}\right)\cap nK,

and

\displaystyle a_{n}\left(\ell\right)=b_{n}\left(\psi\left(\frac{\ell}{n}\right)+o(1)\right)e^{n\phi\left(\ell/n\right)},\hskip 40.00006pt\ell\in\left(\mathcal{L}+\ell_{n}\right)\cap nK_{1},

uniformly for
\ell
in the indicated sets.

Then, provided
\det\left(-H|_{V}\right)\neq 0
, as

,

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell\in\left(\mathcal{L}+\ell_{n}\right)\cap nK}a_{n}\left(\ell\right)\sim\frac{\left(2\pi n\right)^{r/2}\psi\left(x_{0}\right)b_{n}e^{n\phi\left(x_{0}\right)}}{\det\left(\mathcal{L}\right)\sqrt{\det\left(-H|_{V}\right)}}.

Theorem
4.2
is largely self-explanatory. The concept of lattices, however, is not obvious from the theorem itself. Therefore, we briefly revisit the idea of lattices and how they are connected to our model. In general, lattices are discrete subgroups of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
where each lattice is isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}^{r}
for some
0\leq r\leq N
. In this context,
discrete
simply means that the intersection of a lattice with an arbitrary, bounded set in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
is finite. Furthermore,
r
is commonly called the rank of the respective lattice. This means that each lattice has a (not necessarily unique) basis consisting of the vectors
x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}
. The crucial characteristics of these basis vectors are on the one hand that they are independent. On the other hand, every element of the respective lattice has a unique representation of the form
\sum_{i=1}^{r}k_{i}\cdot x_{i}
where
k_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
for all
i\in[r]
.

In applying Theorem
4.2
we are especially interested in understanding the determinant
\det\left(\mathcal{L}\right)
for a given lattice
\mathcal{L}
. Formally,
\det\left(\mathcal{L}\right)
is simply obtained by calculating the determinant of the matrix that consists of the basis vectors
x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}
mentioned above. Intuitively, the determinant provides the
r
-dimensional volume of a unit cell of the lattice
\mathcal{L}
. Note that the term
\left(\det\left(\mathcal{L}\right)\right)^{-1}
in Theorem
4.2
is the key difference compared to more common versions of Laplace’s method for integrals.

from the distribution

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[{\mathbb{G}^{*}=G|\bm{\sigma}^{*}=\sigma}\right]\propto\exp\left(-\beta\mathcal{H}_{G}\left(\sigma\right)\right).

For some graph

on the nodes of

(5.1)

\displaystyle\mu_{++}(G,\sigma):=\frac{2}{dn}\sum_{(u,v)\in E}\bm{1}{\left\{\sigma\left(v\right)=\sigma\left(u\right)=+1\right\}}.

Since

\displaystyle\mu_{--}(G,\sigma)

(5.3)

\displaystyle\mu_{+-}(G,\sigma)

\displaystyle=\mu_{-+}(G,\sigma):=\frac{1}{dn}\sum_{(u,v)\in E}\bm{1}{\left\{\sigma\left(v\right)\neq\sigma\left(u\right)\right\}}.

Due to the fact that our model is built on undirected edges, we just count all the edges connecting vertices with different spins and evenly ’split’ them between

and
\mu_{-+}

(5.4)

\displaystyle\rho_{+}(G,\sigma):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{v\in V}\bm{1}{\left\{\sigma\left(v\right)=+1\right\}}\hskip 30.00005pt\textrm{ and }\hskip 30.00005pt\rho_{-}(G,\sigma):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{v\in V}\bm{1}{\left\{\sigma\left(v\right)=-1\right\}}

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}_{++}=\hat{\mu}_{--}=\frac{e^{-\beta}}{2\left(1+e^{-\beta}\right)}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\hat{\mu}_{+-}=\hat{\mu}_{-+}=\frac{1}{2\left(1+e^{-\beta}\right)}.

To determine the distribution of short cycles in the RSBM, we start by considering the event

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\mu}:=\left\{{\left\|{\mu^{\prime}-\mu}\right\|=O\left({n^{-1/2}\log n}\right)}\right\}.

In the next lines, we establish that

is a high probability event.

Lemma 5.2

Let

(5.7)

\displaystyle\frac{\binom{dn\rho_{+}-2l_{++}-M}{dn\mu_{++}-2l_{++}}\binom{dn\rho_{-}-2l_{--}-M}{dn\mu_{--}-2l_{--}}}{\binom{dn\rho_{+}}{dn\mu_{++}}\binom{dn\rho_{-}}{dn\mu_{--}}}

\hat{\mu}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}_{\hat{\mu}}
. Summing up over all

yields the desired statement

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[X^{\prime\prime}|\mathcal{A}_{\hat{\mu}}\right]=O\left(n^{-1}\right).

This same argumentation can be extended to arbitrary higher moments
\mathbb{E}\left[C_{l}\left(\mathbb{G}^{*}\right)^{j}|\mathcal{A}_{\hat{\mu}}\right]
with
j\in\mathbb{N}
. Thus, the method of moments provides the desired statement.
∎

Proof of Proposition
2.2
.

The Proposition results from combining Lemmas
5.1
and
5.2
.
∎

6.3.

Lemma 6.4

(Hessian for the first moment)

We have

\displaystyle\det\left(-\textrm{Hes}_{\psi}\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{e^{-\beta}}{2\left(1+e^{-\beta}\right)}\right)\right)=4d\left(1+e^{-\beta}\right)^{2}e^{\beta}\left(2+d\left(e^{\beta}-1\right)\right).

Proof.

Let us get started simple and state the partial derivatives of the Kullback-Leibler divergence from (

\displaystyle\frac{\partial D_{\textrm{KL}}\left(\mu||\rho\otimes\rho\right)}{\partial\mu_{++}}

\displaystyle=\log\left(\mu_{++}\right)+\log\left(1+\mu_{++}-2\rho_{+}\right)-2\log\left(\rho_{+}-\mu_{++}\right)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}D_{\textrm{KL}}\left(\mu||\rho\otimes\rho\right)}{\partial\mu_{++}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mu_{++}}+\frac{1}{1+\mu_{++}-2\rho_{+}}+\frac{2}{\rho_{+}-\mu_{++}}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial D_{\textrm{KL}}\left(\mu||\rho\otimes\rho\right)}{\partial\rho_{+}}

\displaystyle=-2\log\left(1+\mu_{++}-2\rho_{+}\right)+2\log\left(1-\rho_{+}\right)+2\log\left(\rho_{+}-\mu_{++}\right)-2\log\left(\rho_{+}\right)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}D_{\textrm{KL}}\left(\mu||\rho\otimes\rho\right)}{\partial\rho_{+}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{1+\mu_{++}-2\rho_{+}}-\frac{2}{1-\rho_{+}}+\frac{2}{\rho_{+}-\mu_{++}}-\frac{2}{\rho_{+}}.

Furthermore, for the entropy we recall

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\textrm{H}\left(\rho_{+}\right)}{\partial\rho_{+}}=\log\left(1-\rho_{+}\right)-\log\left(\rho_{+}\right)\hskip 20.00003pt\textrm{and}\hskip 20.00003pt\frac{\partial^{2}\textrm{H}\left(\rho_{+}\right)}{\partial\rho_{+}^{2}}=-\frac{1}{1-\rho_{+}}-\frac{1}{\rho_{+}}.

Keeping these auxiliary calculations in mind, the first derivatives of
\psi
turn out to be

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\psi\left(\mu_{++},\rho_{+}\right)}{\partial\mu_{++}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{d}{2}\left(\log\left(\mu_{++}\right)+\log\left(1+\mu_{++}-2\rho_{+}\right)-2\log\left(\rho_{+}-\mu_{++}\right)+2\beta\right)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\psi\left(\mu_{++},\rho_{+}\right)}{\partial\rho_{+}}

\displaystyle=\log\left(1-\rho_{+}\right)-\log\left(\rho_{+}\right)-d\left(-\log\left(1+\mu_{++}-2\rho_{+}\right)+\log\left(1-\rho_{+}\right)+\log\left(\rho_{+}-\mu_{++}\right)-\log\left(\rho_{+}\right)-\beta\right)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}\psi\left(\mu_{++},\rho_{+}\right)}{\partial\mu_{++}^{2}}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}\psi\left(\mu_{++},\rho_{+}\right)}{\partial\mu_{++}\partial\rho_{+}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial^{2}\psi\left(\mu_{++},\rho_{+}\right)}{\partial\rho_{+}\partial\mu_{++}}=-\frac{d}{2}\left(-\frac{2}{1+\mu_{++}-2\rho_{+}}-2\frac{1}{\rho_{+}-\mu_{++}}\right)=\frac{d\left(1-\rho_{+}\right)}{\left(1+\mu_{++}-2\rho_{+}\right)\left(\rho_{+}-\mu_{++}\right)}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}\psi}{\partial\mu_{++}^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{e^{-\beta}}{2\left(1+e^{-\beta}\right)}\right)

Lemma 6.5

(Maximum for the First Moment Calculation)
.

With the definitions of
\hat{\rho}_{+}
and
\hat{\mu}_{++}
from (

\displaystyle\arg\max_{\left(\mu_{++},\rho_{+}\right)\in\mathcal{Q}}\psi\left(\mu_{++},\rho_{+}\right)=\left(\hat{\mu}_{++},\hat{\rho}_{+}\right)

Proof.

As a starting point, we set the first derivatives equal to zero, resulting in

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\psi\left(\hat{\mu}_{++},\hat{\rho}_{+}\right)}{\partial\hat{\mu}_{++}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{d}{2}\left(\log\left(\hat{\mu}_{++}\right)+\log\left(1+\hat{\mu}_{++}-2\hat{\rho}_{+}\right)-2\log\left(\hat{\rho}_{+}-\hat{\mu}_{++}\right)+2\beta\right)=0

which is equivalent to

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\log\left(\hat{\mu}_{++}\right)+\log\left(1+\hat{\mu}_{++}-2\hat{\rho}_{+}\right)-2\log\left(\hat{\rho}_{+}-\hat{\mu}_{++}\right)+2\beta

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow 1

\displaystyle=\frac{\hat{\mu}_{++}\left(1+\hat{\mu}_{++}-2\hat{\rho}_{+}\right)}{\left(\hat{\rho}_{+}-\hat{\mu}_{++}\right)^{2}}e^{2\beta}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow 0

\displaystyle=\hat{\mu}_{++}^{2}\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)+\hat{\mu}_{++}\left(1-2\hat{\rho}_{+}+e^{-2\beta}2\hat{\rho}_{+}\right)-e^{-2\beta}\hat{\rho}_{+}^{2}.

Then, the quadratic formula yields two candidates for the solution, namely

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}_{++,1/2}

\displaystyle=\frac{-1+2\hat{\rho}_{+}-e^{-2\beta}2\hat{\rho}_{+}\pm\sqrt{\left(1-2\hat{\rho}_{+}\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)\right)^{2}+4\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)e^{-2\beta}\hat{\rho}_{+}^{2}}}{2\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)}

\displaystyle=\hat{\rho}_{+}-\frac{1\mp\sqrt{\left(1-2\hat{\rho}_{+}\right)^{2}+4\hat{\rho}_{+}e^{-2\beta}\left(1-\hat{\rho}_{+}\right)}}{2\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)}.

This result immediately poses the question of possible extrema. First we note that

\displaystyle\left(1-2\hat{\rho}_{+}\right)^{2}+4\hat{\rho}_{+}e^{-2\beta}\left(1-\hat{\rho}_{+}\right)>0

since both summands are positive.
This in turn enables us to rule out
\hat{\mu}_{++,2}=\hat{\rho}_{+}-\frac{1+\sqrt{\left(1-2\hat{\rho}_{+}\right)^{2}+4\hat{\rho}_{+}e^{-2\beta}\left(1-\hat{\rho}_{+}\right)}}{2\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)}
as a solution since that would imply

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}_{-+}=\hat{\mu}_{+-}=\hat{\rho}_{+}-\hat{\mu}_{++,2}=\frac{1+\sqrt{\left(1-2\hat{\rho}_{+}\right)^{2}+4\hat{\rho}_{+}e^{-2\beta}\left(1-\hat{\rho}_{+}\right)}}{2\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)}>\frac{1}{2\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)}>\frac{1}{2}

which contradicts the fact that
\hat{\mu}
is a probability measure. As a consequence, the only solution that is consistent with our model assumptions is

(6.8)

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}_{++}=\hat{\rho}_{+}-\frac{1-\sqrt{\left(1-2\hat{\rho}_{+}\right)^{2}+4\hat{\rho}_{+}e^{-2\beta}\left(1-\hat{\rho}_{+}\right)}}{2\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)}=\hat{\rho}_{+}-\frac{1-\eta}{2\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)}

where

\displaystyle\eta:=\sqrt{\left(1-2\hat{\rho}_{+}\right)^{2}+4\hat{\rho}_{+}e^{-2\beta}\left(1-\hat{\rho}_{+}\right)}=\sqrt{1-4\hat{\rho}_{+}\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)+4\hat{\rho}_{+}^{2}\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)}

In the next step, we plug (
6.8
) into the first derivative of
\psi

X
. Basically, there are four different types of self-loops in our model,
X_{++},X_{+-},X_{-+},
and
X_{--}
. The index in each of the four cases just refers to the spin pair assigned to the vertex of the self-loop. Then, the expectation of
X_{++}
can be formulated as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{X_{++}|\mathcal{G}\left({\sigma,\mu}\right)}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho_{++}n\binom{d}{2}\binom{dn\rho_{++}-2}{dn\mu_{++++}-2}\left({dn\mu_{++++}-3}\right)!!}{\binom{dn\rho_{++}}{dn\mu_{++++}}\left({dn\mu_{++++}-1}\right)!!}=\frac{\rho_{++}n\frac{d!}{2(d-2)!}\frac{\left({dn\rho_{++}-2}\right)!}{\left({dn\mu_{++++}-2}\right)!\left({dn\rho_{++}-dn\mu_{++++}}\right)!}}{\frac{\left({dn\rho_{++}}\right)!}{\left({dn\mu_{++++}}\right)!\left({dn\rho_{++}-dn\mu_{++++}}\right)!}\left({dn\mu_{++++}-1}\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{n\mu_{++++}d(d-1)}{2\left({dn\rho_{++}-1}\right)}\sim\frac{\mu_{++++}(d-1)}{2\rho_{++}}

where, in the first step, we already cancelled out the factors that appeared both in the numerator and denominator. By almost identical calculations, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{X_{+-}|\mathcal{G}\left({\sigma,\mu}\right)}\right]

\displaystyle\sim\frac{\mu_{+-+-}(d-1)}{2\rho_{+-}},\qquad\mathbb{E}\left[{X_{-+}|\mathcal{G}\left({\sigma,\mu}\right)}\right]\sim\frac{\mu_{-+-+}(d-1)}{2\rho_{-+}},

\displaystyle\text{and}\qquad\mathbb{E}\left[{X_{--}|\mathcal{G}\left({\sigma,\mu}\right)}\right]\sim\frac{\mu_{----}(d-1)}{2\rho_{--}}.

Bringing these four results together and plugging in the optimal point
\left({\mu^{*},\rho^{*}}\right)
, we arrive at

(7.4)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{X|\mathcal{G}\left({\sigma,\mu}\right)}\right]\sim\left({d-1}\right)\frac{2}{\left({1+e^{\beta}}\right)^{2}}.

With a similar argument, we determine the expectation of the number of double edges
Y
. More precisely, we decompose
Y
into the random variables
Y_{\sigma_{1},\tau_{1},\sigma_{2},\tau_{2}}
with
\left({\sigma_{1},\tau_{1},\sigma_{2},\tau_{2}}\right)\in\left\{{\pm 1}\right\}^{4}
. Each
Y_{\sigma_{1},\tau_{1},\sigma_{2},\tau_{2}}
is just the number of double edges between two vertices where the first vertex is assigned to the spin-pair
\left({\sigma_{1},\tau_{1}}\right)
and the second to the pair
\left({\sigma_{2},\tau_{2}}\right)
. Let us start with the four spin configurations with
\left({\sigma_{1},\tau_{1}}\right)=\left({\sigma_{2},\tau_{2}}\right)
. In order to keep the calculations simple, we focus on
Y_{++++}
and then extend the results to
Y_{+-+-}
,
Y_{-+-+}
, and
Y_{----}
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{Y_{++++}|\mathcal{G}\left({\sigma,\mu}\right)}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{2\binom{\rho_{++}n}{2}\binom{d}{2}^{2}\binom{dn\rho_{++}-4}{dn\mu_{++++}-4}\left({dn\mu_{++++}-5}\right)!!}{\binom{dn\rho_{++}}{dn\mu_{++++}}\left({dn\mu_{++++}-1}\right)!!}

\displaystyle\sim\frac{\left({\rho_{++}n}\right)^{2}\left({\frac{d(d-1)}{2}}\right)^{2}\left({dn\mu_{++++}}\right)^{4}}{\left({\rho_{++}dn}\right)^{4}\left({dn\mu_{++++}}\right)^{2}}=\frac{(d-1)^{2}}{4}\frac{\mu_{++++}^{2}}{\rho_{++}^{2}}

where, in the first step, we already cancelled out the factors that occured both in the numerator and denominator. Following this line of thought, we can also state

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{Y_{+-+-}|\mathcal{G}\left({\sigma,\mu}\right)}\right]

\displaystyle\sim\frac{(d-1)^{2}}{4}\frac{\mu_{+-+-}^{2}}{\rho_{+-}^{2}},\qquad\mathbb{E}\left[{Y_{-+-+}|\mathcal{G}\left({\sigma,\mu}\right)}\right]\sim\frac{(d-1)^{2}}{4}\frac{\mu_{-+-+}^{2}}{\rho_{-+}^{2}},

\displaystyle\text{and}\qquad\mathbb{E}\left[{Y_{----}|\mathcal{G}\left({\sigma,\mu}\right)}\right]\sim\frac{(d-1)^{2}}{4}\frac{\mu_{----}^{2}}{\rho_{--}^{2}}.

For the next calculation, we consider the sum of
Y_{+---}
and
Y_{--+-}
. Since the edges in our model are undirected, it is not suitable to make a distinction between the two.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{Y_{+---}+Y_{--+-}|\mathcal{G}\left({\sigma,\mu}\right)}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{2\rho_{+-}\rho_{--}n^{2}\binom{d}{2}^{2}\binom{dn\rho_{+-}-2}{dn\mu_{+---}-2}\binom{dn\rho_{--}-2}{dn\mu_{+---}-2}\left({dn\mu_{+---}-2}\right)!}{\binom{dn\rho_{+-}}{dn\mu_{+---}}\binom{dn\rho_{--}}{dn\mu_{+---}}\left({dn\mu_{+---}}\right)!}

\displaystyle\sim\frac{2\rho_{+-}\rho_{--}n^{2}\left({\frac{d(d-1)}{2}}\right)^{2}\left({dn\mu_{+---}}\right)^{4}}{\left({\rho_{+-}dn}\right)^{2}\left({\rho_{--}dn}\right)^{2}\left({dn\mu_{+---}}\right)^{2}}=\frac{(d-1)^{2}}{4}\frac{\mu_{+---}^{2}}{\rho_{+-}\rho_{--}}

Here, we once again tacitly cancelled out the factors in the first expression that are included in both the numerator and denominator. The same approach can be iteratively applied to the remaining types of double edges, which eventually yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{Y_{-+--}+Y_{---+}|\mathcal{G}\left({\sigma,\mu}\right)}\right]

\displaystyle\sim\frac{(d-1)^{2}}{4}\frac{\mu_{-+--}^{2}}{\rho_{-+}\rho_{--}},\qquad\mathbb{E}\left[{Y_{+++-}+Y_{+-++}|\mathcal{G}\left({\sigma,\mu}\right)}\right]\sim\frac{(d-1)^{2}}{4}\frac{\mu_{+++-}^{2}}{\rho_{++}\rho_{+-}},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{Y_{-+++}+Y_{++-+}|\mathcal{G}\left({\sigma,\mu}\right)}\right]

\displaystyle\sim\frac{(d-1)^{2}}{4}\frac{\mu_{++-+}^{2}}{\rho_{++}\rho_{-+}},\qquad\mathbb{E}\left[{Y_{++--}+Y_{--++}|\mathcal{G}\left({\sigma,\mu}\right)}\right]\sim\frac{(d-1)^{2}}{4}\frac{\mu_{++--}^{2}}{\rho_{++}\rho_{--}},

\displaystyle\text{and}\qquad\mathbb{E}\left[{Y_{+--+}+Y_{-++-}|\mathcal{G}\left({\sigma,\mu}\right)}\right]\sim\frac{(d-1)^{2}}{4}\frac{\mu_{+--+}^{2}}{\rho_{+-}\rho_{-+}}.

Taking the sum of all these findings and plugging in the optimal point
\left({\mu^{*},\rho^{*}}\right)
, we finally obtain

(7.5)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{Y|\mathcal{G}\left({\sigma,\mu}\right)}\right]\sim\left({d-1}\right)^{2}\frac{\left({1+e^{2\beta}}\right)^{2}}{\left({1+e^{\beta}}\right)^{4}}.

With the statements (
7.4
) and (
7.5
) in mind, we claim that for all
k,\ell\geq 1

(7.6)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\prod_{i=1}^{k}\left({X-i+1}\right)\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}\left({Y-j+1}\right)}\right]\sim\left({\left({d-1}\right)\frac{2}{\left({1+e^{\beta}}\right)^{2}}}\right)^{k}\left({\left({d-1}\right)^{2}\frac{\left({1+e^{2\beta}}\right)^{2}}{\left({1+e^{\beta}}\right)^{4}}}\right)^{\ell}

holds. This can be seen as follows. In (
7.4
) and (
7.5
), we placed just one loop or double edge, respectively. To obtain (
7.6
), we now have to place some fixed numbers
k
and
\ell
of self-loops and double edges. Since
n
approaches infinity, the probability that any choices of self-loops and double-edges overlap is bounded by
O(1/n)
. Thus, the desired result can be leveraged from (
7.4
) and (
7.5
).

With (
7.6
) in place, we immediately see

\mathbb{P}\left[{\mathbf{G}^{*}_{2}\in\mathcal{S}}\right]=\mathbb{P}\left[{X=Y=0}\right]\sim\exp\left({-\left({d-1}\right)\frac{2}{\left({1+e^{\beta}}\right)^{2}}-\left({d-1}\right)^{2}\frac{\left({1+e^{2\beta}}\right)^{2}}{\left({1+e^{\beta}}\right)^{4}}}\right).

which concludes the proof.
∎

Now, we are equipped to prove Proposition
2.6
.

Proof of Proposition
2.6
.

We again use the asymptotic equality

(7.7)

\mathbb{E}\left[Z_{\mathbb{G},\beta}^{2}\bm{1}{\{\mathcal{O}\}}\right]\sim\frac{\mathbb{P}\left[{\mathbf{G}^{*}_{2}\mathrm{\ is\ simple}}\right]}{\mathbb{P}\left[{\mathbf{G}\textrm{ is simple}}\right]}\mathbb{E}\left[Z_{\mathbf{G},\beta}^{2}\bm{1}{\{\mathcal{O}\}}\right].

Thus, the desired result is obtained by combining equation (
7.7
), Proposition
7.1
, Lemma
7.6
, and Fact
6.7
.
∎

8.
Second Moment Optimization / Proof of Lemma
7.4

In this section we solve the maximization problem

\displaystyle\max_{\mu\in\mathcal{O}^{\prime}}\delta\left(\mu,\rho\right)

where

\displaystyle\delta\left(\mu,\rho\right)

\displaystyle:=\textrm{H}\left(\rho\right)-\frac{d}{2}\left(D_{\textrm{KL}}\left(\mu||\rho\otimes\rho\right)+\beta\sum_{\sigma\in A_{1}}\mu(\sigma)+2\beta\sum_{\sigma\in A_{2}}\mu(\sigma)\right)\qquad\text{and}

\displaystyle A_{1}

\displaystyle:=\left\{x\in\{\pm 1\}^{4}:\sigma_{1}=\sigma_{2}\textrm{ and }\tau_{1}\neq\tau_{2}\right\}\cup\left\{x\in\{\pm 1\}^{4}:\sigma_{1}\neq\sigma_{2}\textrm{ and }\tau_{1}=\tau_{2}\right\}\qquad\text{and}

\displaystyle A_{2}

\displaystyle:=\left\{x\in\{\pm 1\}^{4}:\sigma_{1}=\sigma_{2}\textrm{ and }\tau_{1}=\tau_{2}\right\}.

with vectors of the form
\sigma=(\sigma_{1},\tau_{1},\sigma_{2},\tau_{2})\in\{\pm 1\}^{4}
. Furthermore,
\mathcal{O}^{\prime}
denotes the set of all
\mu
that are conceivable given that the event
\mathcal{O}
from (
2.5
) holds. At this point, we exploit the spatial mixing argument. Keeping Lemma
2.5
in mind, we limit our attention to the event
\mathcal{O}
. This is a crucial step for the following calculations because it allows us to perform the reparametrization

(8.1)

\displaystyle\rho_{\alpha}(\sigma_{1},\tau_{1}):=\frac{1+\alpha\cdot\bm{1}\left\{{\sigma_{1}=\tau_{1}}\right\}-\alpha\cdot\bm{1}\left\{{\sigma_{1}\neq\tau_{1}}\right\}}{4}

where
\alpha\in(-1,+1)
and
(\sigma_{1},\tau_{1})\in\{\pm 1\}^{2}
.
Now, the proof strategy is as follows. First, we minimize
\delta
with respect to
\mu
. This will provide us with a solution of
\mu
formulated in terms of
\rho
or
\alpha
, respectively. In a second step, all that remains to do is to maximize the function
\delta
with respect to
\alpha
.

8.1.
Minimization with respect to
\mu

Instead of solving the optimization in one step, we start by considering

\displaystyle g(\mu,\rho_{\alpha}):=D_{\textrm{KL}}(\mu||\rho_{\alpha}\otimes\rho_{\alpha})+\beta\cdot\sum_{\sigma\in A_{1}}\mu(\sigma)+2\cdot\beta\cdot\sum_{\sigma\in A_{2}}\mu(\sigma)

where
A_{1}
is defined by

\displaystyle A_{1}:=\left\{x\in\{\pm 1\}^{4}:\sigma_{1}=\sigma_{2}\textrm{ and }\tau_{1}\neq\tau_{2}\right\}\cup\left\{x\in\{\pm 1\}^{4}:\sigma_{1}\neq\sigma_{2}\textrm{ and }\tau_{1}=\tau_{2}\right\}

and
A_{2}
is given by

\displaystyle A_{2}:=\left\{x\in\{\pm 1\}^{4}:\sigma_{1}=\sigma_{2}\textrm{ and }\tau_{1}=\tau_{2}\right\}.

Note that the entropy term
H(\rho)
is independent of
\mu
and thus not relevant for optimizing with respect to
\mu
.
The above formulation immediately brings us to the constrained minimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{\mu\in\mathcal{O}^{\prime}}g(\mu,\rho_{\alpha})

\displaystyle\textrm{ s.t. }\forall\left(\sigma_{1},\tau_{1}\right)\in

\displaystyle\{\pm 1\}^{2}:\sum_{\left(\sigma_{2},\tau_{2}\right)\in\{\pm 1\}^{2}}\mu(\sigma_{1},\tau_{1},\sigma_{2},\tau_{2})=\rho_{\alpha}\left(\sigma_{1},\tau_{1}\right)

\displaystyle\forall\left(\sigma_{2},\tau_{2}\right)\in

\displaystyle\{\pm 1\}^{2}:\sum_{\left(\sigma_{1},\tau_{1}\right)\in\{\pm 1\}^{2}}\mu(\sigma_{1},\tau_{1},\sigma_{2},\tau_{2})=\rho_{\alpha}\left(\sigma_{2},\tau_{2}\right)

where
\mathcal{P}\left(\{\pm 1\}^{4}\right)
denotes the set of all probability distributions on
\{\pm 1\}^{4}
. For ease of notation, we will drop the index
\alpha
and just write
\rho
. As a first step, we point out that due to symmetry the optimal
\mu^{*}
will have the following properties:

\displaystyle\mu^{*}_{++++}

\displaystyle=\mu^{*}_{----},\qquad\mu^{*}_{++--}=\mu^{*}_{--++},

\displaystyle\mu^{*}_{+-+-}

\displaystyle=\mu^{*}_{-+-+},\qquad\mu^{*}_{+--+}=\mu^{*}_{-++-},

\displaystyle\mu^{*}_{+++-}=\mu^{*}_{++-+}

\displaystyle=\mu^{*}_{+-++}=\mu^{*}_{-+++}=\mu^{*}_{---+}=\mu^{*}_{--+-}=\mu^{*}_{-+--}=\mu^{*}_{+---}.

From the above reparametrization, we additionally emphasize that both

\displaystyle\rho_{++}=\rho_{--}\hskip 30.00005pt\textrm{ and }\hskip 30.00005pt\rho_{+-}=\rho_{-+}

hold irrespective of the chosen
\alpha
. This fact directly entails that setting up the Lagrangian function for our minimization problem will only require two distinct Lagrangian multipliers, namely
\lambda_{++}
and
\lambda_{+-}
. Put differently, we are going to consider the following Lagrangian function
\mathcal{L}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\left(\mu,\lambda_{++},\lambda_{+-}\right):=

\displaystyle g(\mu,\rho_{\alpha})-\lambda_{++}\cdot\left(\sum_{\left(\sigma_{1},\tau_{1}\right)\in\left\{(-1,-1),(+1,+1)\right\}}\left[\sum_{\left(\sigma_{2},\tau_{2}\right)\in\{\pm 1\}^{2}}\mu\left(\sigma_{1},\tau_{1},\sigma_{2},\tau_{2}\right)\right]-\rho_{\alpha}\left(\sigma_{1},\tau_{1}\right)\right)

\displaystyle-\lambda_{++}\cdot\left(\sum_{\left(\sigma_{2},\tau_{2}\right)\in\left\{(-1,-1),(+1,+1)\right\}}\left[\sum_{\left(\sigma_{1},\tau_{1}\right)\in\{\pm 1\}^{2}}\mu\left(\sigma_{1},\tau_{1},\sigma_{2},\tau_{2}\right)\right]-\rho_{\alpha}\left(\sigma_{2},\tau_{2}\right)\right)

\displaystyle-\lambda_{+-}\cdot\left(\sum_{\left(\sigma_{1},\tau_{1}\right)\in\left\{(-1,+1),(+1,-1)\right\}}\left[\sum_{\left(\sigma_{2},\tau_{2}\right)\in\{\pm 1\}^{2}}\mu\left(\sigma_{1},\tau_{1},\sigma_{2},\tau_{2}\right)\right]-\rho_{\alpha}\left(\sigma_{1},\tau_{1}\right)\right)

\displaystyle-\lambda_{+-}\cdot\left(\sum_{\left(\sigma_{2},\tau_{2}\right)\in\left\{(-1,+1),(+1,-1)\right\}}\left[\sum_{\left(\sigma_{1},\tau_{1}\right)\in\{\pm 1\}^{2}}\mu\left(\sigma_{1},\tau_{1},\sigma_{2},\tau_{2}\right)\right]-\rho_{\alpha}\left(\sigma_{2},\tau_{2}\right)\right)

Keeping the symmetry in mind, it suffices to consider the following derivatives of the Lagrangian function

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}\left(\mu,\lambda_{++},\lambda_{+-}\right)}{\partial\mu_{++++}}

\displaystyle=1+\log\left(\frac{\mu_{++++}}{\rho_{++}^{2}}\right)+2\beta-2\lambda_{++}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}\left(\mu,\lambda_{++},\lambda_{+-}\right)}{\partial\mu_{++--}}

\displaystyle=1+\log\left(\frac{\mu_{++--}}{\rho_{++}^{2}}\right)-2\lambda_{++}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}\left(\mu,\lambda_{++},\lambda_{+-}\right)}{\partial\mu_{+-+-}}

\displaystyle=1+\log\left(\frac{\mu_{+-+-}}{\rho_{+-}^{2}}\right)+2\beta-2\lambda_{+-}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}\left(\mu,\lambda_{++},\lambda_{+-}\right)}{\partial\mu_{+--+}}

\displaystyle=1+\log\left(\frac{\mu_{+--+}}{\rho_{+-}^{2}}\right)-2\lambda_{+-}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}\left(\mu,\lambda_{++},\lambda_{+-}\right)}{\partial\mu_{+++-}}

\displaystyle=1+\log\left(\frac{\mu_{+++-}}{\rho_{++}\cdot\rho_{+-}}\right)+\beta-\lambda_{++}-\lambda_{+-}

Setting these derivatives equal to zero, we instantly obtain

(8.2)

\displaystyle\mu_{++++}^{*}

\displaystyle=\rho_{++}^{2}\exp\left(2\lambda_{++}-2\beta-1\right)=\rho_{++}^{2}x_{1}^{2}e^{-2\beta}

(8.3)

\displaystyle\mu_{++--}^{*}

\displaystyle=\rho_{++}^{2}\exp\left(2\lambda_{++}-1\right)=\rho_{++}^{2}x_{1}^{2}

(8.4)

\displaystyle\mu_{+-+-}^{*}

\displaystyle=\rho_{+-}^{2}\exp\left(2\lambda_{+-}-2\beta-1\right)=\rho_{+-}^{2}x_{2}^{2}e^{-2\beta}

(8.5)

\displaystyle\mu_{+--+}^{*}

\displaystyle=\rho_{+-}^{2}\exp\left(2\lambda_{+-}-1\right)=\rho_{+-}^{2}x_{2}^{2}

(8.6)

\displaystyle\mu_{+++-}^{*}

\displaystyle=\rho_{++}\rho_{+-}\exp\left(\lambda_{++}+\lambda_{+-}-\beta-1\right)=\rho_{++}\rho_{+-}x_{1}x_{2}e^{-\beta}

where the second equalities in each line represent a notational simplification by introducing
x_{1}:=e^{\lambda_{++}-\frac{1}{2}}
and
x_{2}:=e^{\lambda_{+-}-\frac{1}{2}}
.

Lemma 8.1
.

The above system of equations (
8.2
)-(
8.6
) and therefore the minimization problem of
g\left(\mu,\rho_{\alpha}\right)
has the unique solution

\displaystyle x_{1}=2\sqrt{\frac{\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}+\alpha\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-2e^{-\beta}z}{\left(1+\alpha\right)^{2}\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}}}.

and

\displaystyle x_{2}=2\sqrt{\frac{\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-\alpha\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}\pm 2e^{-\beta}z}{\left(1-\alpha\right)^{2}\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}}}.

Proof.

In order to solve this system of equations, we recall two of the initial constraints of our minimization problem, i.e.

\displaystyle\mu_{++++}+\mu_{+++-}+\mu_{++-+}+\mu_{++--}

\displaystyle=\rho_{++}

\displaystyle\mu_{+-++}+\mu_{+---}+\mu_{+--+}+\mu_{+-+-}

\displaystyle=\rho_{+-}.

Plugging in the
\mu^{*}
we derived above and once again keeping in mind the symmetry of the problem, the two constraints can be reformulated into

\displaystyle\rho_{++}x_{1}^{2}e^{-2\beta}+2\rho_{+-}x_{1}x_{2}e^{-\beta}+\rho_{++}x_{1}^{2}

\displaystyle=1

\displaystyle\rho_{+-}x_{2}^{2}e^{-2\beta}+2\rho_{++}x_{1}x_{2}e^{-\beta}+\rho_{+-}x_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=1.

which in turn yields

x_{1}=\frac{1-x_{2}^{2}\rho_{+-}\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)}{2\rho_{++}e^{-\beta}x_{2}}.

Substituting
x_{1}
into the first constraint, we arrive at

\displaystyle\frac{\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)\left(1-x_{2}^{2}\rho_{+-}\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)\right)^{2}}{4\rho_{++}e^{-2\beta}x_{2}^{2}}+\frac{\rho_{+-}}{\rho_{++}}\cdot\left(1-x_{2}^{2}\rho_{+-}\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)\right)=1

For notational convenience, we substitute
x:=x_{2}^{2}
and
\kappa:=1+e^{-2\beta}
. As a consequence, the previous equation can be expressed as

\displaystyle x^{2}\left(\rho_{+-}^{2}\kappa^{3}-4\rho_{+-}^{2}\kappa e^{-2\beta}\right)+x\left(4\rho_{+-}e^{-2\beta}-2\rho_{+-}\kappa^{2}-4\rho_{++}e^{-2\beta}\right)+\kappa=0.

Now, we are able to apply the quadratic formula which yields

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle=\frac{-4\rho_{+-}e^{-2\beta}+2\rho_{+-}\kappa^{2}+4\rho_{++}e^{-2\beta}\pm\sqrt{\left(4\rho_{+-}e^{-2\beta}-2\rho_{+-}\kappa^{2}-4\rho_{++}e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-4\rho_{+-}^{2}\kappa^{2}\left(\kappa^{2}-4e^{-2\beta}\right)}}{2\left(\rho_{+-}^{2}\kappa^{3}-4\rho_{+-}^{2}\kappa e^{-2\beta}\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\alpha\right)\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}+\left(1+\alpha\right)e^{-2\beta}-\left(1-\alpha\right)e^{-2\beta}\pm\sqrt{\left(2\alpha e^{-2\beta}+\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\alpha\right)\kappa^{2}\right)^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\left(1-\alpha\right)^{2}\kappa^{4}+\left(1-\alpha\right)^{2}\kappa^{2}e^{-2\beta}}}{\frac{1}{8}\left(1-\alpha\right)^{2}\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)\left(\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-4e^{-2\beta}\right)}

\displaystyle=4\frac{\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-\alpha\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}\pm 2\sqrt{4\alpha^{2}e^{-4\beta}+2\alpha e^{-2\beta}\left(1-\alpha\right)\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}+\left(1-\alpha\right)^{2}\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}e^{-2\beta}}}{\left(1-\alpha\right)^{2}\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}}

Focusing on the square root term, we note

\displaystyle 4\alpha^{2}e^{-4\beta}+2\alpha e^{-2\beta}\left(1-\alpha\right)\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}+\left(1-\alpha\right)^{2}\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}e^{-2\beta}

\displaystyle=4\alpha^{2}e^{-4\beta}-\alpha^{2}e^{-2\beta}\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}+\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}e^{-2\beta}=e^{-2\beta}\left(\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-\alpha^{2}\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}\right)

which leads us to

\displaystyle x=4\frac{\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-\alpha\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}\pm 2e^{-\beta}\sqrt{\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-\alpha^{2}\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}}}{\left(1-\alpha\right)^{2}\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}}=4\frac{\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-\alpha\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}\pm 2e^{-\beta}z}{\left(1-\alpha\right)^{2}\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}}

where we have introduced
z:=\sqrt{\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-\alpha^{2}\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}}
for notational convenience. At this point, we recall that
x=x_{2}^{2}
to arrive at

\displaystyle x_{2}=2\sqrt{\frac{\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-\alpha\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}\pm 2e^{-\beta}z}{\left(1-\alpha\right)^{2}\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}}}

where we discard the negative square root since
x_{2}
by definition is of the form
x_{2}=e^{\lambda_{+-}-\frac{1}{2}}
and thereby always non-negative. This leaves us with two potential solutions for
x_{2}
which only differ in the
\pm
sign in the above equation. Leaving out the detailed calculation, it is easy to show that choosing
+
at the
\pm
sign would result in a negative
x_{1}
. However, similar to
x_{2}
,
x_{1}=e^{\lambda_{++}-\frac{1}{2}}
also cannot become negative by construction. As a consequence, the only remaining and suitable candidate for
x_{2}
and thereby the solution is

\displaystyle x_{2}=2\sqrt{\frac{\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-\alpha\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-2e^{-\beta}z}{\left(1-\alpha\right)^{2}\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}}}.

With this solution for
x_{2}
we are now able to calculate the optimal
x_{1}
. More specifically, we recall the formula we have derived a few steps back

\displaystyle x_{1}=\frac{1-x_{2}^{2}\rho_{+-}\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)}{2\rho_{++}e^{-\beta}x_{2}}.

Plugging in the optimal
x_{2}
, we arrive at the expression

(8.7)

\displaystyle x_{1}=\frac{1-\frac{\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-\alpha\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-2e^{-\beta}z}{\left(1-\alpha\right)\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}}}{\left(1+\alpha\right)e^{-\beta}\sqrt{\frac{\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-\alpha\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-2e^{-\beta}z}{\left(1-\alpha\right)^{2}\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}}}}=\frac{\left(-4e^{-\beta}+2z\right)\sqrt{1+e^{-2\beta}}}{\left(1+\alpha\right)\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)\sqrt{\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-\alpha\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-2e^{-\beta}z}}.

Next, we claim that

\displaystyle x_{1}=2\sqrt{\frac{\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}+\alpha\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-2e^{-\beta}z}{\left(1+\alpha\right)^{2}\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}}}.

Indeed, we find starting at (
8.7
) that

\displaystyle x_{1}=\frac{\left(-4e^{-\beta}+2z\right)\sqrt{1+e^{-2\beta}}}{\left(1+\alpha\right)\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)\sqrt{\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-\alpha\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-2e^{-\beta}z}}.

Thus, our claim is equivalent to

(8.8)

\displaystyle\left(z-2e^{-\beta}\right)^{2}\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}=\left(\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}+\alpha\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-2e^{-\beta}z\right)\left(\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-\alpha\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-2e^{-\beta}z\right).

To see that (
8.8
) is indeed true, we execute the following auxiliary calculation:

\displaystyle\left(\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}+\alpha\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-2e^{-\beta}z\right)\left(\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-\alpha\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-2e^{-\beta}z\right)

\displaystyle=\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{4}-4e^{-\beta}z\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}+4e^{-2\beta}\left(\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-\alpha^{2}\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}\right)-\alpha^{2}\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{4}

\displaystyle=\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}\left(z^{2}-4e^{-\beta}z+4e^{-2\beta}\right)=\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}\left(z-2e^{-\beta}\right)^{2}.

Hence, we established our claim and thus know

\displaystyle x_{1}=2\sqrt{\frac{\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}+\alpha\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-2e^{-\beta}z}{\left(1+\alpha\right)^{2}\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}}}.

∎

Let us bring together our findings of this subsection. Due to the well-known fact that the Kullback-Leibler divergence is convex in its input parameters, we immediately see that the function
g(\mu,\rho_{\alpha})
is convex as well. As a consequence, the
\mu^{*}
we have just calculated is indeed the minimum. Put differently, we are now able to state

\displaystyle\min_{\mu\in\mathcal{O}^{\prime}}g(\mu,\rho_{\alpha})=g(\mu^{*},\rho_{\alpha}).

Although this statement is satisfactory, we would favor a more explicit expression. This is achieved by the following Lemma.

Lemma 8.2
.

We have

\displaystyle g(\mu^{*},\rho_{\alpha})

\displaystyle=2\log\left(2\right)-\log\left(\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}\right)-\left(1+\alpha\right)\log\left(1+\alpha\right)

\displaystyle-\left(1-\alpha\right)\log\left(1-\alpha\right)+\frac{1+\alpha}{2}\log\left(\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}+\alpha\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-2e^{-\beta}z\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{1-\alpha}{2}\log\left(\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-\alpha\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-2e^{-\beta}z\right).

Proof.

In order to get to the desired expression, we take a closer look at the Kullback-Leibler divergence for the optimal
\mu^{*}

\displaystyle D_{\textrm{KL}}(\mu^{*}||\rho_{\alpha}\otimes\rho_{\alpha})

\displaystyle=2\mu_{++++}^{*}\log\left(x_{1}^{2}e^{-2\beta}\right)+2\mu_{++--}^{*}\log\left(x_{1}^{2}\right)+2\mu_{+-+-}^{*}\log\left(x_{2}^{2}e^{-2\beta}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+2\mu_{+--+}^{*}\log\left(x_{1}^{2}\right)+8\mu_{+++-}^{*}\log\left(x_{1}x_{2}e^{-\beta}\right)

\displaystyle=-\beta\left(4\mu_{++++}^{*}+4\mu_{+-+-}^{*}+8\mu_{+++-}^{*}\right)+\log\left(x_{1}\right)\left(4\mu_{++++}^{*}+4\mu_{++--}^{*}+8\mu_{+++-}^{*}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\log\left(x_{2}\right)\left(4\mu_{+-+-}^{*}+4\mu_{+--+}^{*}+8\mu_{+++-}^{*}\right)

Using the reformulation of
D_{\textrm{KL}}(\mu^{*}||\rho_{\alpha}\otimes\rho_{\alpha})
,
g(\mu^{*},\rho_{\alpha})
can be formulated as

\displaystyle g(\mu^{*},\rho_{\alpha})=\log\left(x_{1}\right)\left(4\mu_{++++}^{*}+4\mu_{++--}^{*}+8\mu_{+++-}^{*}\right)+\log\left(x_{2}\right)\left(4\mu_{+-+-}^{*}+4\mu_{+--+}^{*}+8\mu_{+++-}^{*}\right).

This expression in turn is suitable for inserting
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
leading to

\displaystyle g(\mu^{*},\rho_{\alpha})

\displaystyle=2\log\left(2\right)-\log\left(\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\left[\log\left(\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}+\alpha\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-2e^{-\beta}z\right)-2\log\left(1+\alpha\right)\right]\cdot\left(2\mu_{++++}^{*}+2\mu_{++--}^{*}+4\mu_{+++-}^{*}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\left[\log\left(\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-\alpha\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-2e^{-\beta}z\right)-2\log\left(1-\alpha\right)\right]\cdot\left(2\mu_{+-+-}^{*}+2\mu_{+--+}^{*}+4\mu_{+++-}^{*}\right).

Since
\mu^{*}
is a probability measure by definition, we can exploit the identity

\displaystyle 2\mu_{+-+-}^{*}+2\mu_{+--+}^{*}+4\mu_{+++-}^{*}=1-2\mu_{++++}^{*}-2\mu_{++--}^{*}-4\mu_{+++-}^{*}

to rearrange
g(\mu^{*},\rho_{\alpha})
as

\displaystyle g(\mu^{*},\rho_{\alpha})

\displaystyle=2\log\left(2\right)-\log\left(\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}\right)+\log\left(\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-\alpha\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-2e^{-\beta}z\right)-2\log\left(1-\alpha\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\left[\log\left(\frac{\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}+\alpha\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-2e^{-\beta}z}{\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-\alpha\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-2e^{-\beta}z}\right)-2\log\left(\frac{1+\alpha}{1-\alpha}\right)\right]\cdot\left(2\mu_{++++}^{*}+2\mu_{++--}^{*}+4\mu_{+++-}^{*}\right).

To keep the terms relatively brief, we define

\displaystyle T_{1}

\displaystyle:=2\log\left(2\right)-\log\left(\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}\right)+\log\left(\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-\alpha\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-2e^{-\beta}z\right)-2\log\left(1-\alpha\right)

\displaystyle T_{2}

\displaystyle:=2\mu_{++++}^{*}+2\mu_{++--}^{*}+4\mu_{+++-}^{*}

\displaystyle T_{3}

\displaystyle:=\log\left(\frac{\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}+\alpha\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-2e^{-\beta}z}{\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-\alpha\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-2e^{-\beta}z}\right)-2\log\left(\frac{1+\alpha}{1-\alpha}\right)

which implies
g(\mu^{*},\rho_{\alpha})=T_{1}+T_{2}\cdot T_{3}
. In the next step, we will plug in
\mu^{*}
in order to simplify

\displaystyle T_{2}

\displaystyle=2\mu_{++++}^{*}+2\mu_{++--}^{*}+4\mu_{+++-}^{*}=2x_{1}^{2}\rho_{++}^{2}\left(1+e^{-2\beta}+2e^{-\beta}\frac{\rho_{+-}x_{2}}{\rho_{++}x_{1}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}+\alpha\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-2e^{-\beta}z}{2\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad+e^{-\beta}\frac{\sqrt{\left(\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}+\alpha\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-2e^{-\beta}z\right)\left(\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-\alpha\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-2e^{-\beta}z\right)}}{\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}}

Applying (
8.8
) to the term in the square root yields

\displaystyle T_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}+\alpha\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-2e^{-\beta}z}{2\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}}+e^{-\beta}\frac{\left(z-2e^{-\beta}\right)\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)}{\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}+\alpha\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-4e^{-2\beta}}{2\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}}=\frac{1+\alpha}{2}.

Coming back to
g(\mu^{*},\rho_{\alpha})
, we obtain the expression that Lemma
8.2
promised

\displaystyle g(\mu^{*},\rho_{\alpha})

\displaystyle=2\log\left(2\right)-\log\left(\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}\right)-\left(1+\alpha\right)\log\left(1+\alpha\right)-\left(1-\alpha\right)\log\left(1-\alpha\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1+\alpha}{2}\log\left(\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}+\alpha\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-2e^{-\beta}z\right)+\frac{1-\alpha}{2}\log\left(\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-\alpha\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-2e^{-\beta}z\right).

∎

Maximization with respect to
\alpha

In this subsection we focus on the function

which results from plugging in the definition of
\rho
in terms of
\alpha
from (
8.1
). More specifically, we are interested in solving the optimization

\displaystyle\max_{-1<\alpha<1}f_{d}\left(\alpha,\beta\right).

which will immediately yield the answer to our initial optimization problem over
\delta\left(\mu,\rho\right)
. Note that we tacitly exploit the results of both Lemma
8.1
and Lemma

to be able to state a function

that only depends on

and

. As a consequence, we have to prove the following statement.

Lemma 8.3
.

Assume that
0<\beta<\beta_{\text{KS}}
. Then we have

\displaystyle\arg\max_{-1<\alpha<1}f_{d}\left(\alpha,\beta\right)=0.

Proof.

To solve the maximization with respect to
\alpha
, we calculate the derivatives. Let us start with the simpler ones, namely the first and second derivative of the entropy with respect to
\alpha
:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\textrm{H}\left(\frac{1+\alpha}{2}\right)}{\partial\alpha}=\frac{1}{2}\log\left(1-\alpha\right)-\frac{1}{2}\log\left(1+\alpha\right)

and

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}\textrm{H}\left(\frac{1+\alpha}{2}\right)}{\partial\alpha^{2}}=\dfrac{1}{2}\left(\frac{-1}{1-\alpha}-\frac{1}{1+\alpha}\right)=-\frac{1}{1-\alpha^{2}}.

Before we continue with our main task, let us state a useful observation which will be helpful in the following calculations. Let

\displaystyle z=\sqrt{\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-\alpha^{2}\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}}

Then, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial z}{\partial\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{-\alpha\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}}{\sqrt{\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-\alpha^{2}\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}}}=-\alpha\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}z^{-1}.

Next, we determine the first two derivatives for
g(\mu^{*},\rho_{\alpha})
. Starting with the first derivative, we find

\displaystyle\frac{\partial g(\mu^{*},\rho_{\alpha})}{\partial\alpha}=-\frac{1+\alpha}{1+\alpha}-\log\left(\frac{1+\alpha}{2}\right)+\frac{1-\alpha}{1-\alpha}+\log\left(\frac{1-\alpha}{2}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\left[\log\left(\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}+\alpha\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-2e^{-\beta}z\right)-\log\left(\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-\alpha\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-2e^{-\beta}z\right)\right]

\displaystyle+\frac{1+\alpha}{2}\cdot\frac{\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}+2e^{-\beta}\alpha\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}z^{-1}}{\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}+\alpha\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-2e^{-\beta}z}+\frac{1-\alpha}{2}\cdot\frac{-\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}+2e^{-\beta}\alpha\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}z^{-1}}{\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-\alpha\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-2e^{-\beta}z}

For the next simplification, we focus on the last two summands of the previously stated derivative, i.e.

\displaystyle\frac{1+\alpha}{2}\cdot\frac{\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}+2e^{-\beta}\alpha\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}z^{-1}}{\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}+\alpha\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-2e^{-\beta}z}+\frac{1-\alpha}{2}\cdot\frac{-\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}+2e^{-\beta}\alpha\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}z^{-1}}{\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-\alpha\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-2e^{-\beta}z}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}}{2z}\cdot\frac{\left(1+\alpha\right)\left(z+2e^{-\beta}\alpha\right)\left(\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-\alpha\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-2e^{-\beta}z\right)}{\left(\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}+\alpha\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-2e^{-\beta}z\right)\left(\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-\alpha\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-2e^{-\beta}z\right)}

\displaystyle+\frac{\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}}{2z}\cdot\frac{\left(1-\alpha\right)\left(-z+2e^{-\beta}\alpha\right)\left(\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}+\alpha\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-2e^{-\beta}z\right)}{\left(\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}+\alpha\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-2e^{-\beta}z\right)\left(\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-\alpha\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-2e^{-\beta}z\right)}

Again, we restrict our attention to one term, namely

\displaystyle\left(1+\alpha\right)\left(z+2e^{-\beta}\alpha\right)\left(\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-\alpha\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-2e^{-\beta}z\right)

\displaystyle+\left(1-\alpha\right)\left(-z+2e^{-\beta}\alpha\right)\left(\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}+\alpha\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-2e^{-\beta}z\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-2e^{-\beta}z\right)\left(4e^{-\beta}\alpha+2\alpha z\right)+\alpha\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}\left(-2z-4e^{-\beta}\alpha^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=2\alpha z4e^{-2\beta}-8e^{-2\beta}\alpha z=0.

As a result, the first derivative can be reduced to

\displaystyle\frac{\partial g(\mu^{*},\rho_{\alpha})}{\partial\alpha}

\displaystyle=-\log\left(1+\alpha\right)+\log\left(1-\alpha\right)+\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}+\alpha\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-2e^{-\beta}z}{\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-\alpha\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-2e^{-\beta}z}\right).

Based on this result, we can instantly compute the second derivative

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}g(\mu^{*},\rho_{\alpha})}{\partial\alpha^{2}}=-\frac{2}{1-\alpha^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\cdot\left[\frac{\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}+2e^{-\beta}\alpha\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}z^{-1}}{\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}+\alpha\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-2e^{-\beta}z}-\frac{-\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}+2e^{-\beta}\alpha\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}z^{-1}}{\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-\alpha\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-2e^{-\beta}z}\right].

Once again, we apply (
8.8
) to get to

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}g(\mu^{*},\rho_{\alpha})}{\partial\alpha^{2}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{1-\alpha^{2}}+\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}\frac{2z\left(\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-2e^{-\beta}z\right)-4e^{-\beta}\alpha^{2}\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}}{2z\left(z-2e^{-\beta}\right)^{2}\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{1-\alpha^{2}}+\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}\frac{\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}\left(z-2e^{-\beta}\right)}{z\left(z-2e^{-\beta}\right)^{2}\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}}=\frac{\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}}{z\left(z-2e^{-\beta}\right)}-\frac{2}{1-\alpha^{2}}.

Finally, combining the derivatives of the entropy and
g(\mu^{*},\rho_{a})
we arrive at

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f_{d}\left(\alpha,\beta\right)}{\partial\alpha}=\frac{\partial\textrm{H}\left(\frac{1+\alpha}{2}\right)}{\partial\alpha}-\frac{d}{2}\cdot\frac{\partial g(\mu^{*},\rho_{\alpha})}{\partial\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log\left(1-\alpha\right)-\frac{1}{2}\log\left(1+\alpha\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{d}{2}\left(\log\left(1+\alpha\right)-\log\left(1-\alpha\right)-\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}+\alpha\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-2e^{-\beta}z}{\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-\alpha\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-2e^{-\beta}z}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{d-1}{2}\log\left(1+\alpha\right)-\frac{d-1}{2}\log\left(1-\alpha\right)-\frac{d}{4}\log\left(\frac{\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}+\alpha\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-2e^{-\beta}z}{\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-\alpha\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-2e^{-\beta}z}\right)

and

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}f_{d}\left(\alpha,\beta\right)}{\partial\alpha^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial^{2}\textrm{H}\left(\frac{1+\alpha}{2}\right)}{\partial\alpha^{2}}-\frac{d}{2}\cdot\frac{\partial^{2}g(\mu^{*},\rho_{\alpha})}{\partial^{2}\alpha}=-\frac{1}{1-\alpha^{2}}-\frac{d}{2}\cdot\left(\frac{\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}}{z\left(z-2e^{-\beta}\right)}-\frac{2}{1-\alpha^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=d\cdot\frac{z^{2}-4ze^{-\beta}+\left(1+e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-\alpha^{2}\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}-\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}+\alpha^{2}\left(1-e^{-2\beta}\right)^{2}}{\left(1-\alpha^{2}\right)2z\left(z-2e^{-\beta}\right)}-\frac{1}{1-\alpha^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{d-2}{2\left(1-\alpha^{2}\right)}-\frac{de^{-\beta}}{\left(1-\alpha^{2}\right)z}=\frac{d-2}{2\left(1-\alpha^{2}\right)}-\frac{d}{\left(1-\alpha^{2}\right)\sqrt{\left(e^{\beta}+e^{-\beta}\right)^{2}-\alpha^{2}\left(e^{\beta}-e^{-\beta}\right)^{2}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{d-2}{2\left(1-\alpha^{2}\right)}-\frac{d}{\left(1-\alpha^{2}\right)\sqrt{\left(1-\alpha^{2}\right)\cdot\left(e^{2\beta}+e^{-2\beta}\right)+2+2\alpha^{2}}}.

Furthermore, we note that for every
\beta
we have for
\alpha=0

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f_{d}}{\partial\alpha}\left(0,\beta\right)=0.

Now, to complete the maximization with respect to
\alpha
, we claim is that the global maximum of
f_{d}\left(\alpha,\beta\right)
is at
\alpha=0
as long as
\beta<\beta_{\text{KS}}
. We prove this claim in two steps. First, we show that
\frac{\partial^{2}f_{d}}{\partial\alpha^{2}}\left(\alpha,\beta\right)
is increasing in
\beta
. Subsequently, we establish that
\frac{\partial^{2}f_{d}}{\partial\alpha^{2}}\left(\alpha,\beta^{*}\right)
is smaller than zero for all
\alpha\in(-1,1)
.
As a consequence,
\frac{\partial^{2}f_{d}}{\partial\alpha^{2}}\left(\alpha,\beta\right)<0
holds for all
\beta\in\left(0,\beta^{*}\right)
and
\alpha\in(-1,1)
and thereby implies that the maximum of
f_{d}\left(\alpha,\beta\right)
is attained at
\alpha=0
for
\beta<\beta^{*}
.
The previously performed technical rearrangements are helpful for calculating the next derivative in a straightforward manner.

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}f_{d}}{\partial\alpha^{2}}\right)\left(\alpha,\beta\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{2\left(1-\alpha^{2}\right)}\frac{\left(1-\alpha^{2}\right)\cdot\left(2\beta e^{2\beta-1}-2\beta e^{-2\beta-1}\right)}{\left[\left(1-\alpha^{2}\right)\cdot\left(e^{2\beta}+e^{-2\beta}\right)+2+2\alpha^{2}\right]^{\frac{3}{2}}}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\frac{d}{2}2\beta e^{2\beta-1}\left(1-e^{-4\beta}\right)}_{>0}\underbrace{\left[\underbrace{\left(1-\alpha^{2}\right)}_{>0}\cdot\left(e^{2\beta}+e^{-2\beta}\right)+2+2\alpha^{2}\right]^{-\frac{3}{2}}}_{>0}>0

where we restrict our attention to
-1<\alpha<1
. All that remains to do is to plug in the Kesten-Stigum bound into the second derivative with respect to alpha which yields

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}f_{d}}{\partial\alpha^{2}}\left(\alpha,\beta^{*}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{d-2}{2\left(1-\alpha^{2}\right)}-\frac{d}{1-\alpha^{2}}\cdot\left[\left(1-\alpha^{2}\right)\cdot\left(\frac{\left(\sqrt{d-1}+1\right)^{2}}{\left(\sqrt{d-1}-1\right)^{2}}+\frac{\left(\sqrt{d-1}-1\right)^{2}}{\left(\sqrt{d-1}+1\right)^{2}}\right)+2+2\alpha^{2}\right]^{-\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{d-2}{2\left(1-\alpha^{2}\right)}-\frac{d}{1-\alpha^{2}}\cdot\left[\left(1-\alpha^{2}\right)\cdot\left(\frac{\left(\sqrt{d-1}+1\right)^{4}+\left(\sqrt{d-1}-1\right)^{4}}{\left(d-1-1\right)^{2}}\right)+2+2\alpha^{2}\right]^{-\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{d-2}{2\left(1-\alpha^{2}\right)}-\frac{d}{1-\alpha^{2}}\cdot\left[\frac{4d^{2}+\alpha^{2}\left(16-16d\right)}{\left(d-2\right)^{2}}\right]^{-\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{d-2}{2\left(1-\alpha^{2}\right)}\cdot\underbrace{\left(1-\frac{d}{\sqrt{d^{2}-4\alpha^{2}\left(d-1\right)}}\right)}_{<0}<0

where we assume both
d>2
and
-1<\alpha<1
. This concludes the maximization problem.
∎

What remains is to bring all the findings of this section together.

Proof of Lemma
7.4
.

Substituting
\alpha=0
from Lemma
8.3
into the previous reformulations, we can state that
\delta\left(\mu,\rho\right)
obtains its optimum at
\mu^{*}
where

\displaystyle\mu_{++++}^{*}

\displaystyle=\mu_{----}^{*}=\mu_{+-+-}^{*}=\mu_{-+-+}^{*}=\frac{e^{-2\beta}}{4\left(1+e^{-\beta}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle\mu_{+--+}^{*}

\displaystyle=\mu_{--++}^{*}=\mu_{-++-}^{*}=\mu_{++--}^{*}=\frac{1}{4\left(1+e^{-\beta}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle\mu_{+++-}^{*}

\displaystyle=\mu_{++-+}^{*}=\mu_{+-++}^{*}=\mu_{-+++}^{*}=\mu_{---+}^{*}=\mu_{--+-}^{*}=\mu_{-+--}^{*}=\mu_{+---}^{*}=\frac{e^{-\beta}}{4\left(1+e^{-\beta}\right)^{2}}

which also implies

\displaystyle\rho_{++}^{*}=\rho_{+-}^{*}=\rho_{-+}^{*}=\rho_{--}^{*}=\frac{1}{4}

and
