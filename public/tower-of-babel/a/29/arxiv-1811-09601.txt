The formalism of Segal sections
Edouard Balzin

Abstract
. Given a family of model categories ℰ → 𝒞 {\mathcal{E}}\to{\mathcal{C}} , we associate to it a homotopical category of derived, or Segal, sections 𝖣𝖲𝖾𝖼𝗍 ⁡ ( 𝒞 , ℰ ) {\sf DSect}({\mathcal{C}},{\mathcal{E}}) that models the higher-categorical sections of the localisation L ​ ℰ → 𝒞 L{\mathcal{E}}\to{\mathcal{C}} . The derived sections provide an alternative, strict model for various higher algebra objects appearing in the work of Lurie. We prove a few results concerning the properties of the homotopical category 𝖣𝖲𝖾𝖼𝗍 ⁡ ( 𝒞 , ℰ ) {\sf DSect}({\mathcal{C}},{\mathcal{E}}) , and as an example, study its behaviour with respect to the base-change along a select class of functors.

Introduction

Segal objects

The formalism presented in this paper was developed in the study of homotopy algebraic
structures as described by Segal and generalised by Lurie. We begin the introduction
by describing this context.

Denote by
\Gamma
the category whose objects are finite sets and morphisms
are given by partially defined set maps. Each such morphism between
S
and
T

can be depicted as
S\supset S_{0}\to T
. A
\Gamma
-space
is simply a functor

X:\Gamma\to{\rm\bf Top}
taking values in the category of topological spaces. Each such functor
can be evaluated on the maps in
\Gamma
that have the form
S\supset{s}\to 1
, where

s\in S
and
1
is the one-element set. As a result, for each
S\in\Gamma
we get the map

(i)

X(S)\longrightarrow X(1)^{S}

and the
\Gamma
-space is called
Segal

[
23
]
if the map (
i
) is a homotopy
equivalence for all
S\in\Gamma
.

For each
S
, we also have the map
S\stackrel{{\scriptstyle=}}{{\supset}}S\to 1
, and evaluating
X

on it, combined with (
i
), gives the following span in
{\rm\bf Top}
:

(ii)

X(1)^{S}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\longleftarrow}}X(S)\longrightarrow X(1);

choosing an inverse equivalence to the left map, we get a non-canonical operation
X(1)^{S}\to X(1)
.
There is no ambiguity in choice of the inverse in the homotopy category of topological spaces

{\rm Ho}\,{\rm\bf Top}
: the result is a commutative monoid structure on
X(1)
in the homotopy category.
The full structure that
X(1)
carries is that of an
{\mathrm{E}}_{\infty}
, or a homotopy commutative monoid.

Instead of
\Gamma
one can work with other categories. For example, take the category

\Delta
which consists of finite categories
[n]=0\to 1\to...\to n
(
n\geq 0
)
and functors between them.
We can then consider functors
Y:\Delta^{\sf op}\to{\rm\bf Top}
such that

Y([0])=*
and for each
n\geq 1
, the map
Y([n])\to Y([1])^{n}
, induced by all the
consecutive interval inclusions
[1]\to[n]
, is a homotopy equivalence. Such Segal

\Delta
-spaces are known to describe homotopy associative, or
{\mathrm{E}}_{1}
-monoids. The
functor
\Delta^{\sf op}\to\Gamma
which maps
[n]
to the set of its
n
generating arrows,
allows us to pull
\Gamma
-spaces back to
\Delta^{\sf op}
: every commutative monoid is also
an associative one.
There are further examples of small categories that parametrise operations up to homotopy.
For instance,
[
5
]
introduces the notion of an operator category and gives
examples of categories that model
{\mathrm{E}}_{m}
-algebras. The case
m=2
can also be approached
geometrically using the exit path category
[
27
]
of the
2
-disk. One can also
introduce categories that permit to describe an
{\mathrm{E}}_{m}
-algebra over another algebra. All such categories

{\mathrm{O}}
come with a functor
{\mathrm{O}}\to\Gamma
, so that we can pull Segal
\Gamma
-spaces back to
{\mathrm{O}}
,
reflecting the “initial” property of homotopy commutative structures.

Let us attempt to replace
{\rm\bf Top}
with another homotopical category (by which we simply mean
a category with a distinguished class of weak equivalences) possessing a monoidal
structure. For example, take the category
{\rm\bf DVect}_{k}
of chain complexes of vector spaces over a field
k
.
The maps like (
i
) will take the form
X(S)\to\oplus_{S}X(1)
, which
is not very interesting for the purposes of algebra. Indeed, the tensor product
\otimes_{k}

is not Cartesian. The observation of
[
20
]
addresses this issue by making the following construction.

Algebras as sections

Given any symmetric monoidal category
({\mathcal{M}},\otimes,{\mathbb{I}})
, let us define the category
{\mathcal{M}}^{\otimes}
. Its
objects are pairs
(S,\{X_{s}\}_{s\in S})
where
S\in\Gamma
and each
X_{s}
is an object of
{\mathcal{M}}
.
A morphism
(S,\{X_{s}\}_{s\in S})\to(T,\{Y_{t}\}_{t\in T})
consists of a partially
defined map
f:S\to T
, and for each
t\in T
, of a morphism
\otimes_{s\in f^{-1}(t)}X_{s}\to Y_{t}
.
When
f^{-1}(t)
is empty, the monoidal product over it equals
{\mathbb{I}}
.
The compositions can then be defined with the help of the coherence isomorphisms
for the product
\otimes
and the unit object.

The forgetful functor
p:{\mathcal{M}}^{\otimes}\to\Gamma
is a
Grothendieck opfibration

[
15
]
:
the assignment
S\mapsto{\mathcal{M}}^{\otimes}(S):=p^{-1}(S)={\mathcal{M}}^{S}
is functorial, but in a weak way. Each map
f:S\to T

corresponds to a functor
f_{!}:{\mathcal{M}}^{\otimes}(S)\to{\mathcal{M}}^{\otimes}(T)
, sending
(S,\{X_{s}\}_{s\in S})
to

(T,\{(\otimes_{s\in f^{-1}(t)}X_{s})\}_{t\in T})
. We see that for composable maps

f,g
, one has
g_{!}f_{!}\cong(gf)_{!}
and such isomorphisms, while suitably coherent, are nonetheless
not equalities. It is more convenient to work with Grothendieck (op)fibrations since
this allows to keep all these isomorphisms implicit.

Denoting for simplicity two objects of
{\mathcal{M}}^{\otimes}
as
X,Y
, any map
\alpha:X\to Y

factors as
X\to f_{!}X\to Y
where
f=p(\alpha)
; the map
\alpha
is called
cocartesian

if the induced map
f_{!}X\to Y
is an isomorphism. Consider now a section of
p
, that is
a functor
{\mathcal{A}}:\Gamma\to{\mathcal{M}}^{\otimes}
with
p{\mathcal{A}}=id
. Demand further that given any
inert
map
in
\Gamma
, that is a map of the form
S\supset T\stackrel{{\scriptstyle=}}{{\to}}T
, the induced
map
{\mathcal{A}}(S)\to{\mathcal{A}}(T)
is cocartesian. It is elementary to verify that each
{\mathcal{A}}(S)

is isomorphic to
S
copies of
{\mathcal{A}}(1)\in{\mathcal{M}}^{\otimes}(1)={\mathcal{M}}
. The remaining information provided by the section
{\mathcal{A}}
equips
{\mathcal{A}}(1)

with the structure of a commutative monoid in
{\mathcal{M}}
: the value of
{\mathcal{A}}
on the map

S\stackrel{{\scriptstyle=}}{{\supset}}S\to 1
gives multiplication
{\mathcal{A}}(1)^{S}\to{\mathcal{A}}(1)
, the composition
property insures that everything is determined by this map for
|S|=2
(which is forced to be associative)
and the action of automorphisms in
\Gamma
forces the multiplication to be commutative.

Setting
{\mathcal{M}}={\rm\bf DVect}_{k}
, it is well known that the homotopy theory of commutative

dg
-algebras is an inadequate model for
{\mathrm{E}}_{\infty}
-algebras in
{\rm\bf DVect}_{k}
if

chark\neq 0
. From the fibrational perspective, the reason for this is that
the transition functors of the opfibration
{\mathcal{M}}^{\otimes}\to\Gamma
lack adjoints
(they are multifold tensor products). One cannot thus apply the model structure results of

[
3
]
or pull back
to a suitable Reedy category and use
[
1
]
.
Another problem arises if we try different parametrising categories
{\mathrm{O}}
described
above. We can consider functors
{\mathrm{O}}\to{\mathcal{M}}^{\otimes}
that commute with the projection to

\Gamma
, subject to some cocartesian conditions. This way one can indeed obtain associative algebra
in the case of
\Delta^{\sf op}\to\Gamma
, but the operator categories parametrising
{\mathrm{E}}_{m}
-structures
will give commutative algebras for
m\geq 2
, due to the Eckmann-Hilton argument.

In order to deal with the problems described above,
[
20
]
passes to
the world of symmetric monoidal infinity-categories,
and performs all the constructions on that level. More precisely the machinery of
[
20
]

is developed for the infinity-operads,
and is not immediately applicable to arbitrary operation-indexing categories
{\mathrm{O}}\to\Gamma
some of which
[
2
]
have no operadic
analogues (technically speaking, one can attempt taking a fibrant replacement of
{\mathrm{O}}
in the category of infinity-operads,
but this will result in the loss of control over the combinatorics of
{\mathrm{O}}
).
This approach is also, arguably, quite
technical: the chapters of
[
20
]
devoted to colimits of algebras and adjoint functors
contain heavy combinatorics necessary even for colimits of algebras in the presentable setting (in the sense of
[
19
, 5.5]
).
These issues motivated us to look for an alternative approach.

Segal sections

Let us pass from
{\mathcal{M}}^{\otimes}\to\Gamma
to more general
Grothendieck opfibrations
{\mathcal{E}}\to{\mathcal{C}}
over small categories
{\mathcal{C}}
. For each
c\in{\mathcal{C}}

we thus have the fibre category
{\mathcal{E}}(c)
, and each map
f:c\to c^{\prime}
produces a transition
functor
f_{!}:{\mathcal{E}}(c)\to{\mathcal{E}}(c^{\prime})
(the compositions are respected up to an isomorphism).
Define
{\sf Sect}({\mathcal{C}},{\mathcal{E}})

to be the category of sections of
{\mathcal{E}}\to{\mathcal{C}}
; it is defined as the fibre of the post-composition
functor
\operatorname{\mathsf{Fun}}({\mathcal{C}},{\mathcal{E}})\to\operatorname{\mathsf{Fun}}({\mathcal{C}},{\mathcal{C}})
(with
\operatorname{\mathsf{Fun}}
denoting the functor categories) over the identity functor.
Any section
X:{\mathcal{C}}\to{\mathcal{E}}
associates to a map
f:c\to c^{\prime}
the map
f_{!}X(c)\to X(c^{\prime})
in
{\mathcal{E}}(c^{\prime})
;
if the latter map is an isomorphism, the section
X
is cocartesian along
f
. We can
impose the cocartesian condition along a subset
{\mathcal{S}}
of maps of
{\mathcal{C}}
, and denote
the resulting subcategory as
{\sf Sect}_{\mathcal{S}}({\mathcal{C}},{\mathcal{E}})
. This category is a generalisation
of the algebra objects category in the case of
{\mathcal{M}}^{\otimes}\to\Gamma
.

Assume now that each
{\mathcal{E}}(c)
has weak equivalences
{\mathcal{W}}(c)
.
For simplicity we ask here that the transition functors
f_{!}

preserve weak equivalences. We can then take the higher-categorical localisation
of
{\mathcal{E}}
with respect to all
{\mathcal{W}}(c)
, and as observed in
[
16
,
21
]
,
the resulting infinity-functor
L{\mathcal{E}}\to{\mathcal{C}}
is a cocartesian fibration in infinity-categories
as defined in
[
19
]
. One can use the language of
[
19
]
to define the higher categories

{\sf Sect}({\mathcal{C}},L{\mathcal{E}})

and
{\sf Sect}_{\mathcal{S}}({\mathcal{C}},L{\mathcal{E}})
. We would like to understand the properties of these higher categories
as a function of the properties of
{\mathcal{E}}
, and ideally have convenient strict models for them.

Our answer to this problem is the notion of a
derived
, or
Segal
, section (we will use
both names interchangeably
in this text).
Informally, such an object
X

associates to
c\in{\mathcal{C}}
an object
X(c)\in{\mathcal{E}}(c)
, and to each map
f:c\to c^{\prime}
,
a diagram

(iii)

f_{!}X(c)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\longleftarrow}}X_{f}\longrightarrow X(c^{\prime})

with
f_{!}:{\mathcal{E}}(c)\to{\mathcal{E}}(c^{\prime})
being the transition functor and the left map in
(
iii
) belonging to
{\mathcal{W}}(c)
. If we were to invert it, we would
get a map
f_{!}X(c)\to X(c^{\prime})
: in this sense, a Segal section is a derived version
of the concept of a section.
There are also additional data that ensure the coherence
with respect to the compositions in
{\mathcal{C}}
. Finally, if
f\in{\mathcal{S}}
, then both
maps in the diagram (
iii
) are required to be weak equivalences.

Below we explain more formally our definition of Segal sections;
those readers that are only interested in the properties of the homotopical category of derived
sections are invited to skip further.

The start is to replace the base category
{\mathcal{C}}
. The well-known construction associates to
it the category
\Delta/{\mathcal{C}}
, whose objects are functors
[n]\to{\mathcal{C}}
(in other words,
strings of arrows
c_{0}\to...\to c_{n}
), and the morphisms are functors
[n]\to[m]
over
{\mathcal{C}}
.
The
simplicial replacement

{\mathbb{C}}
of
{\mathcal{C}}
is defining by simply putting

{\mathbb{C}}:=(\Delta/{\mathcal{C}})^{\sf op}
.

Among the maps in
\Delta
, we can consider those interval inclusions
[m]\hookrightarrow[n]

that send
0
to
0
. The maps in
{\mathbb{C}}
with such underlying
\Delta
-map look like

(c_{0}\to...\to c_{n})\longrightarrow(c_{0}\to...\to c_{m})

and are called Segal maps in this paper. The reason for this terminology is the known
fact that the functor
h:{\mathbb{C}}\to{\mathcal{C}}
sending
c_{0}\to...\to c_{n}
to
c_{0}
, is the
(higher-categorical) localisation of
{\mathbb{C}}
along Segal maps (Proposition
1.15
).
As a result, given a category
{\mathcal{M}}
with weak equivalences
{\mathcal{W}}
, we can represent
the functors from
{\mathcal{C}}
to the localisation of
{\mathcal{M}}
with respect to
{\mathcal{W}}
(in ordinary or higher
sense) as functors from
{\mathbb{C}}
to
{\mathcal{M}}
sending the Segal maps to
{\mathcal{W}}
. Given
such functor
X
, its evaluation on the span
c_{0}\leftarrow(c_{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle f}}{{\to}}c_{1})\to c_{1}

gives a diagram

(iv)

X(c_{0})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\in{\mathcal{W}}}}{{\longleftarrow}}X(c_{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle f}}{{\to}}c_{1})\longrightarrow X(c_{1})

that can be compared with (
ii
) and is a version of (
iii
)
when no non-trivial opfibration is involved. In this way,
X:{\mathbb{C}}\to{\mathcal{M}}
can be viewed as
a “Segal functor” from
{\mathcal{C}}
to
{\mathcal{M}}
.

The next step of the construction concerns the opfibration
{\mathcal{E}}\to{\mathcal{C}}
. Its extension
to
{\mathbb{C}}
can be done using the functor
h:{\mathbb{C}}\to{\mathcal{C}}
. While this technique is useful when dealing with
Quillen presheaves
[
1
]
, the result is not illuminating
in the case of
{\mathcal{M}}^{\otimes}\to\Gamma
due to the already mentioned lack of adjoints.
Instead, one has to turn to the
transpose

fibration
{\mathcal{E}}^{\top}\to{\mathcal{C}}^{\sf op}
(Definition
1.17
): it has the same fibres
{\mathcal{E}}(c)
, and has the same
transition functors inherited from
{\mathcal{E}}\to{\mathcal{C}}
, but read in a contravariant direction
over
{\mathcal{C}}^{\sf op}
. Now, any object
c_{0}\to...\to c_{n}\in{\mathbb{C}}
, viewed as a functor
\mathbf{c}:[n]\to{\mathcal{C}}
,
allows us to write

\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{c}):={\sf Sect}([n],\mathbf{c}^{{\sf op},*}{\mathcal{E}}^{\top}),

in other words, we consider the sections of the transpose fibration over the string

c_{0}\to...\to c_{n}
. Moreover the assignment
\mathbf{c}\mapsto\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{c})
defines a functor: for each map
\alpha:\mathbf{c}\to\mathbf{c}^{\prime}

in
{\mathbb{C}}
, we have the restriction
\alpha_{!}:\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{c})\to\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{c}^{\prime})
. Using the Grothendieck construction

[
26
]
we can take the associated opfibration denoted
\mathbf{E}\to{\mathbb{C}}
.

Given an object
\mathbf{c}=c_{0}\to...\to c_{n}
, the functor
\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{c})\to\mathbf{E}(c_{0})={\mathcal{E}}(c_{0})

of evaluation at
c_{0}
admits a right adjoint that induces an equivalence

\mathbf{E}(c_{0})\cong{\sf Cart}([n],\mathbf{c}^{{\sf op},*}{\mathcal{E}}^{\top})\subset\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{c})
of
\mathbf{E}(c_{0})
with the full subcategory
of
{\sf Sect}([n],\mathbf{c}^{{\sf op},*}{\mathcal{E}}^{\top})
consisting of sections taking all maps of
[n]
to cartesian maps. A similar
situation happens along any Segal map
\alpha:\mathbf{c}\to\mathbf{c}^{\prime}
,
and the existing adjunctions
\alpha_{!}:\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{c})\rightleftarrows\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{c}^{\prime}):\alpha^{*}

mean that
\mathbf{E}\to{\mathbb{C}}
is also a fibration along Segal maps.

The following is Proposition
2.2
in the main text.

Proposition 1
.

The category
{\sf Sect}({\mathcal{C}},{\mathcal{E}})
is equivalent
to the full subcategory of
{\sf Sect}({\mathbb{C}},\mathbf{E})
consisting of those
X
such that for each Segal
map
\alpha:\mathbf{c}\to\mathbf{c}^{\prime}
, the image
X(\alpha)
is cartesian, meaning
X(\mathbf{c})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\to}}\alpha^{*}X(\mathbf{c}^{\prime})
.

If
{\mathcal{E}}\to{\mathcal{C}}
has the weak equivalences as before, each category
\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{c})
also comes with weak equivalences, defined value-by-value. The category of
derived sections

{\sf DSect}({\mathcal{C}},{\mathcal{E}})
is then defined as the full subcategory of
{\sf Sect}({\mathbb{C}},\mathbf{E})
consisting of all
X

such that each Segal
\alpha:\mathbf{c}\to\mathbf{c}^{\prime}
induces a weak equivalence

X(\mathbf{c})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\mathcal{W}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\alpha^{*}X(\mathbf{c}^{\prime})
(more generally, in the precedent
expression
we will replace
\alpha^{*}
by its right derived functor
{\mathbb{R}}\alpha^{*}
). The weak equivalences
of derived sections are defined value-by-value, giving a homotopical structure on
{\sf DSect}({\mathcal{C}},{\mathcal{E}})
.
Without further explanation, we also mention that one can take the subset
{\mathcal{S}}
of maps of
{\mathcal{C}}

into account (Definition
2.16
), resulting in a full homotopical
subcategory
{\sf DSect}_{\mathcal{S}}({\mathcal{C}},{\mathcal{E}})\subset{\sf DSect}({\mathcal{C}},{\mathcal{E}})
of
{\mathcal{S}}
-locally constant
derived sections.

Summary of properties

The summary of the discussion above is that given
an opfibration
{\mathcal{E}}\to{\mathcal{C}}
, there exists another opfibration
\mathbf{E}\to{\mathbb{C}}
that
inherits weak equivalences from
{\mathcal{E}}
, and the homotopical category of derived sections
{\sf DSect}({\mathcal{C}},{\mathcal{E}})

is defined as a full subcategory of
{\sf Sect}({\mathbb{C}},\mathbf{E})
using a weakened version of cartesian section condition.
Let us now describe some of its properties.

We first study the case when
the fibres of
{\mathcal{E}}\to{\mathcal{C}}
are model categories, and the transition functors preserve
fibrations and trivial fibrations (but not necessarily have adjoints): such opfibrations
are called
model
in this text. One important example is the case of
{\rm\bf DVect}_{k}^{\otimes}\to\Gamma
with
k
any field, whose
infinity-sections model
{\mathrm{E}}_{\infty}
-algebras over
k
(one can use operation-indexing categories to include

{\mathrm{E}}_{n}
-algebras over
k
and other structures). Another example is

{\rm\bf DVect}_{k}^{{\sf op},\otimes}\to\Gamma
, corresponding to the monoidal structure on the
opposite category of chain complexes over
k
: studying infinity-sections
in this case corresponds to higher coalgebra objects in
L{\rm\bf DVect}_{k}
.

The following proposition is the summary of subsection
2.2
:

Proposition 2
.

Let
{\mathcal{E}}\to{\mathcal{C}}
be a model opfibration. Then the functor
\mathbf{E}\to{\mathbb{C}}
is a bifibration in
model categories and Quillen pairs, its sections
{\sf Sect}({\mathbb{C}},\mathbf{E})
is a model category for the
Reedy structure of
[
1
]
,
and the full subcategory
{\sf DSect}({\mathcal{C}},{\mathcal{E}})\subset{\sf Sect}({\mathbb{C}},\mathbf{E})
is closed under homotopy
limits.

Moreover when the model categories
{\mathcal{E}}(c)
are combinatorial and the transition functors
f_{!}

are accessible, there is a Bousfield localisation of the Reedy model structure on
{\sf Sect}({\mathbb{C}},\mathbf{E})

whose fibrant objects are satisfy the derived section condition. In particular, there exist homotopy
colimits of derived sections.

If the transition functors along the maps from the subset
{\mathcal{S}}
preserve limits,
then the inclusion
{\sf DSect}_{\mathcal{S}}({\mathcal{C}},{\mathcal{E}})\subset{\sf Sect}({\mathbb{C}},\mathbf{E})
is also stable under limits,
yet the existence of a localised model structure is not guaranteed (the situation is similar
to that encountered in
[
4
]
). Nonetheless Proposition
2

allows to establish the following important result. Given a model opfibration
{\mathcal{E}}\to{\mathcal{C}}
,
denote by
{\mathcal{E}}_{f}\subset{\mathcal{E}}
the subopfibration spanned by the fibrewise-fibrant objects.
We can apply the infinity-localisation of
[
16
]
to get a cocartesian fibration in quasicategories

L{\mathcal{E}}_{f}\to{\mathcal{C}}
. The following result is a consequence of the comparison theorem
given in
[
1
]
and is a combination of Theorem
3.3
and Corollary

3.5

in the main text:

Theorem 3
.

Let
{\mathcal{E}}\to{\mathcal{C}}
be a model opfibration and
{\mathcal{S}}
a (possibly empty) subset of maps of
{\mathcal{C}}
.
The infinity-category
{\sf Sect}_{\mathcal{S}}({\mathcal{C}},L{\mathcal{E}}_{f})
consisting of sections
{\mathcal{C}}\to L{\mathcal{E}}_{f}

that send the maps of
{\mathcal{S}}
to cocartesian maps of
L{\mathcal{E}}_{f}
is equivalent
to the infinity-localisation
L{\sf DSect}_{\mathcal{S}}({\mathcal{C}},{\mathcal{E}})
, in a manner compatible with the base-change.

Assume
that the fibres of
{\mathcal{E}}\to{\mathcal{C}}
are combinatorial model categories, the transition functors are accessible,
and the transition functors along the maps in
{\mathcal{S}}
preserve limits. Then both

L{\sf DSect}_{\mathcal{S}}({\mathcal{C}},{\mathcal{E}})
and
{\sf Sect}_{\mathcal{S}}({\mathcal{C}},L{\mathcal{E}}_{f})
are presentable infinity-categories.

We remark that even in the non-homotopical context, it is not obvious why the sections
of
{\mathcal{E}}\to{\mathcal{C}}
have colimits, as they are indeed not given fibrewise. One can view the
idea of a Segal section to be a smarter way to represent the information encoded by a
higher-categorical section
{\mathcal{C}}\to L{\mathcal{E}}_{f}
. Returning to the case of
{\rm\bf DVect}_{k}^{\otimes}\to\Gamma

with inert maps denoted as
In
, the homotopical category
{\sf DSect}_{In}(\Gamma,{\rm\bf DVect}_{k}^{\otimes})

provides a strictification of the infinity-category of
{\mathrm{E}}_{\infty}
-algebras in
{\rm\bf DVect}_{k}
and brings with
it an alternative to
[
20
]
’s proof of the existence of (homotopy) colimits. The same
is true for more general operation-indexing categories
{\mathrm{O}}\to\Gamma
, whether or not they have
corresponding operadic analogues.

The procedure of associating
\mathbf{E}\to{\mathbb{C}}
to the opfibration
{\mathcal{E}}\to{\mathcal{C}}
can be also
carried out in the situation when
{\mathcal{E}}
is an infinity-category (and the functor to
{\mathcal{C}}
is a
cocartesian fibration), and that is explained in subsection
3.2
.
One then has that the infinity-category
{\sf Sect}({\mathcal{C}},{\mathcal{E}})
is identified with a full subcategory
of
{\sf Sect}({\mathbb{C}},\mathbf{E})
in a way similar to that explained in Proposition
1
.
This allows to establish the presentability part of Theorem
3

more generally, as stated in Theorem
3.13
in the main text.

Theorem 4
.

Let
{\mathcal{E}}\to{\mathcal{B}}
be a cocartesian fibration over a small infinity category
{\mathcal{B}}
, such
that each fibre
{\mathcal{E}}(b)
is presentable and each transition functor is accessible.
Let
{\mathcal{S}}
be a subset of maps of
{\mathcal{B}}
such that each transition functor
f_{!}
along

f\in{\mathcal{S}}
preserves limits (and thus satisfies the right adjoint functor theorem).

Then the infinity-category of
{\mathcal{S}}
-cocartesian sections
{\sf Sect}_{\mathcal{S}}({\mathcal{B}},{\mathcal{E}})
is presentable, with limits
and (sufficiently large) filtered colimits calculated fibrewise.

This result does not appear in
[
19
, 5.5]
, and is relatively immediate
if one adapts the Segal section perspective. It gives, again, an alternative to
[
20
]

the categories of algebras over infinity-operads in presentably symmetric monoidal

Resolutions

The final section can be viewed as an exercise in the calculus of simplicial replacements,
and involves proving a certain descent statement. Given a functor
F:{\mathcal{D}}\to{\mathcal{C}}
,
one can readily induce the pullback
F^{*}:{\sf DSect}({\mathcal{C}},{\mathcal{E}})\to{\sf DSect}({\mathcal{D}},F^{*}{\mathcal{E}})
. If

F
presents
{\mathcal{C}}
as a higher-categorical localisation of
{\mathcal{D}}
with respect to
F
-isomorphisms,
denoted
F-iso
, then Theorem
3
implies that
F^{*}

is homotopically fully faithful, and its image consists of
{\sf DSect}_{F-iso}({\mathcal{D}},F^{*}{\mathcal{E}})
.
When
F
belongs to a special class of localisations (called resolutions in this paper, a condition
due to
[
16
, Key Lemma]
), the same statement can be proven without passing by
the comparison with the higher-categorical sections, which reveals interesting simplicial
combinatorics on the way (and that we shall use in our subsequent work).

Organisation of the paper

The first section introduces the combinatorics
relevant to the procedure of replacing
{\mathcal{E}}\to{\mathcal{C}}
with its “simplicial extension”

\mathbf{E}\to{\mathbb{C}}
, and we proceed to define and study the first properties of derived/Segal
sections, proving Propositions
1
and
2
in the second section.
The third section compares the Segal sections with the higher-categorical sections,
which leads to Theorem
3
, and then gives a version of
the purely higher-categorical Segal section construction, resulting in Theorem
4
.
The resolutions example is treated in the last, fourth section.

One way to produce new factorisation categories out of existing ones

Definition 1.1

The importance of Segal maps is summarised by the following:

Proposition 1.15
.

For a small category
{\mathcal{C}}
, the functor

is a higher-categorical

{\mathbb{C}}
along

Proof.

Lemma

establishes that

is a localisation along the

{\mathbb{C}}
that project to the identities of

. It will suffice to show that

-identities.

Let
\alpha:\mathbf{c}\to\mathbf{c}^{\prime}
be a map in
{\mathbb{C}}

.
This implies that

\alpha:(c\to c^{\prime}\to...\to c\to c_{0}\to...)\longrightarrow(c\to c^{\prime}_{0}\to...)

. Since Segal maps are inverted by

-invertible.
It will thus suffice to show that the following map

-invertible:

The map

The latter is a one-sided inverse of the Segal map

∎

1.3.
Families over the simplicial replacement

We now turn to the question of extending an opfibration
{\mathcal{E}}\to{\mathcal{C}}
to
{\mathbb{C}}
.
This can be done in a few ways. Since we have a functor
h_{\mathcal{C}}:{\mathbb{C}}\to{\mathcal{C}}
,
there is a pull-back opfibration
h_{\mathcal{C}}^{*}{\mathcal{E}}\to{\mathbb{C}}
. However, unless
{\mathcal{E}}\to{\mathcal{C}}

is a Quillen presheaf, it is not apparent why the category of sections of
h_{\mathcal{C}}^{*}{\mathcal{E}}\to{\mathbb{C}}

carries a model structure. In particular, we cannot use this approach to study higher algebra
in the language of model categories.

Any opfibration
p:{\mathcal{E}}\to{\mathcal{C}}
can be described, up to an
equivalence, as a Grothendieck construction of a covariant functor from
{\mathcal{C}}
to categories.
The latter is the same thing as a contravariant functor
from
{\mathcal{C}}^{\sf op}
to categories. The way to capture this duality without
passing to category-valued functors, is the following.

Definition 1.17
.

Fix an opfibration
p:{\mathcal{E}}\to{\mathcal{C}}
. Define a category denoted as
{\mathcal{E}}^{\top}
as follows:

(1)

Ob({\mathcal{E}}^{\top})=Ob({\mathcal{E}})

(2)

A morphism from

in
{\mathcal{E}}^{\top}
is an isomorphism class of cospans in
{\mathcal{E}}

such that the left arrow is fibrewise,
p(x\to y)=id_{p(x)}

There is an evident functor
p^{\top}:{\mathcal{E}}^{\top}\to{\mathcal{C}}^{\sf op}
which sends a map

x\longrightarrow y\longleftarrow z
to
p(y\longleftarrow z)
.
A morphism of
{\mathcal{E}}^{\top}
is
p^{\top}
-cartesian iff it can be represented
by a span of the form
y\stackrel{{\scriptstyle=}}{{\longrightarrow}}y\longleftarrow z
.
The functor
p^{\top}
is a fibration, which we call
the
transpose fibration
of

.

If
{\mathcal{E}}\to{\mathcal{C}}
equals
\int E\to{\mathcal{C}}
for a functor
E:{\mathcal{C}}\to{\rm\bf Cat}

{\mathcal{E}}^{\top}\to{\mathcal{C}}^{\sf op}
is equivalent to the (fibrational) Grothendieck construction applied to

and the opfibration

-projection, then

is simply

The construction of a transpose fibration that we outlined can be also done higher-categorically,

-locally constant derived section,

(2)

if
X\in{\sf DSect}_{\mathcal{S}}({\mathcal{C}},{\mathcal{E}})
is fibrant as a section in

{\sf PSect}({\mathcal{C}},{\mathcal{E}})={\sf Sect}({\mathbb{C}},\mathbf{E})
and
f:x\to y
is a Segal map in
{\mathbb{C}}
,
then the induced morphism

X(x)\to f^{*}X(y)
is a trivial fibration of fibrant objects,

(3)

if
X_{\bullet}:I\to{\sf DSect}({\mathcal{C}},{\mathcal{E}})
is a diagram of derived sections,
then its homotopy limit in
{\sf PSect}({\mathcal{C}},{\mathcal{E}})
is a derived section. If the transition
functors along
{\mathcal{S}}
preserve limits, then the same result is true for the diagrams
valued in
{\sf DSect}_{\mathcal{S}}({\mathcal{C}},{\mathcal{E}})
.

Proof.

The first assertion is a direct consequence of Lemma
2.11
.

For the second assertion, we know that
X
fibrant implies
X(x)
fibrant for each
x\in{\mathcal{X}}
of degree
0
. In general, we know that
X(x)\to\mathscr{Mat}_{x}X
is a fibration. Similarly as for simplicial objects in a model category, one can prove that for any
\alpha:x\to y
covering an injection
[n]\hookleftarrow[m]
in
\Delta
, the map
\mathscr{Mat}_{x}X\to\alpha^{*}X(y)
is a fibration. This implies that
X(x)
is fibrant and any Segal map
x\to y
goes to
X(x)\to X(y)
, a fibration and a weak equivalence.

The final assertion is easily checked due to the fact that the (homotopy) limits
are calculated fibrewise
[
17
]
in
{\sf Sect}({\mathbb{C}},\mathbf{E})
and the commutativity between
{\mathbb{R}}f^{*}

for a Segal map
f:x\to y
and homotopy colimits:

\mathbb{R}\operatorname{\varprojlim}X_{\bullet}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\to}}\mathbb{R}\operatorname{\varprojlim}\mathbb{R}f^{*}X_{\bullet}(y)\cong\mathbb{R}f^{*}\mathbb{R}\operatorname{\varprojlim}X_{\bullet}(y).

When the transition functors along
{\mathcal{S}}
preserve limits, Lemma

and a dual calculation permit to check the same for the local constancy condition.
∎

The colimits of derived sections, if exist,
are usually not calculated in a fibrewise way. However, some abstract existence results can
be established if the original model opfibration
{\mathcal{E}}\to{\mathcal{C}}
has combinatorial fibres
and accessible transition functors. For example, one can state the following:

Proposition 2.20
.

Let
{\mathcal{E}}\to{\mathcal{C}}
be a model opfibration such that each
{\mathcal{E}}(c)
is left proper combinatorial,
and each transition functor
f_{!}:{\mathcal{E}}(c)\to{\mathcal{E}}(c^{\prime})
is accessible. Then
there exists a left proper combinatorial model structure on
{\sf PSect}({\mathcal{C}},{\mathcal{E}})
with
Reedy cofibrations and fibrant objects given by Reedy-fibrant objects of
{\sf DSect}({\mathcal{C}},{\mathcal{E}})
.

Proof.

According to
[
1
, Proposition 2.30]
, each model category
\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{c})
,
being the category of sections over a Reedy category, is combinatorial. It is moreover
left proper, as is easily checked using the fact that the colimits are computed fibrewise.
Thus the Quillen presheaf
\mathbf{E}\to{\mathbb{C}}
has left proper combinatorial fibres. This
again implies that the category
{\sf PSect}({\mathcal{C}},{\mathcal{E}})={\sf Sect}({\mathbb{C}},\mathbf{E})
is left proper combinatorial
for the Reedy model structure.

The rest is done similarly to
[
3
, Theorem 2.42]
. As explained there or at
[
1
, Lemma 2.31]
,
for each object
X\in\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{c})
there exists a section
i(X)
such that

{\sf PSect}({\mathcal{C}},{\mathcal{E}})(i(X),Y)=\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{c})(X,Y(\mathbf{c}))
: indeed,
i(X)(\mathbf{c}^{\prime})\cong\coprod_{\alpha:\mathbf{c}\to\mathbf{c}^{\prime}}\alpha_{!}X
.
For each map
f:\mathbf{c}\to\mathbf{c}^{\prime}
, one has a canonical map
f_{!}X\to i(X)(\mathbf{c}^{\prime})
,
and hence
i(f_{!}X)\to i(X)
. The required model structure is then obtained by
left Bousfield-localising the Reedy structure on
{\sf PSect}({\mathcal{C}},{\mathcal{E}})
along the set

\{i(f_{!}X)\to i(X)\,\,|\,\,f:\mathbf{c}\to\mathbf{c}^{\prime}\in\mathscr{S}_{\mathbb{C}},\,X\in G(\mathbf{c})\}

with
G(\mathbf{c})
denoting, as in
[
3
]
, the set of cofibrant homotopy generators of
\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{c})
. ∎

Proposition

implies in particular the existence of homotopy
colimits of derived sections, even if the latter are calculated inexplicitly.
To see the same for
{\mathcal{S}}
-locally constant derived sections, one can attempt
to use the formalism of right model categories of Barwick
[

]
.
However, the question of homotopy colimits can be approached differently, using
the language of higher categories.

Let

Remark 3.7

relative nerve functor of Lurie, recalled below. We can also assume that there is a functor

taking value in fibrant

for

is equivalent
over

to the covariant relative nerve of

.

Let

{diagram}

an inverse of the equivalence between the cartesian sections of the
contravariant
relative nerve

\int{\mathrm{F}}_{i}\to{\mathcal{D}}(i)^{\sf op}
, and
{\mathrm{F}}_{i}(d_{i})
.

Note that the assignment

defines a covariant functor, since
a map

induces a unique map

in
{\mathcal{D}}(i)

of the marked version of

{diagram}

that induces the corresponding map of cartesian sections.

Proof.

Let us recall the construction of

[

, Definition 3.2.5.2]

a map

Theorem 3.13

Let

Proof.

We already know that

[

Choose an infinity-localisation

-cocartesian since any map in

Having a cocartesian fibration

By

is a presentable fibration. All that remains is

-coloured anti-Segal maps lead to limit-preserving transition functors.

The following will suffice. Let
{\mathcal{F}}\to[n]
be a cartesian fibration in presentable infinity-categories

4.
Resolutions

Given a functor
F:{\mathcal{D}}\to{\mathcal{C}}
and a model opfibration
{\mathcal{E}}\to{\mathcal{C}}
, we get an induced pullback functor
\mathbb{F}^{*}:{\sf PSect}({\mathcal{C}},{\mathcal{E}})={\sf Sect}({\mathbb{C}},\mathbf{E})\to{\sf Sect}({\mathbb{D}},\mathbf{E})={\sf PSect}({\mathcal{D}},{\mathcal{E}})
on the categories of presections. This functor trivially preserves weak equivalences, and also the condition of being a derived section. We thus get the functor

\mathbb{F}^{*}:{\rm Ho}\,{\sf DSect}({\mathcal{C}},{\mathcal{E}})\to{\rm Ho}\,{\sf DSect}({\mathcal{D}},{\mathcal{E}})

on the level of localisations.
In some cases, the functor
\mathbb{F}^{*}
is full and faithful, and its essential image is easy to characterise.

Definition 4.1
.

For a functor

and

of

the category

•

with objects being pairs of

so that the vertical maps are isomorphisms,

•

with morphisms given by commutative diagrams

{diagram}

such that for each

{diagram}

commutes.

The categories

are extensions of the notion of an essential fibre of a functor.

Definition 4.2

(1)

A functor

(2)

A functor

if

•

over
[0]\in\Delta
, the category
{\mathcal{D}}(c)
is contractible, and

•

for each

in
{\mathbb{C}}
over

and

given by the (strict) fibre of

over

(3)

A functor
F:{\mathcal{D}}\to{\mathcal{C}}
is a
left resolution
if

•

for each
c\in{\mathbb{C}}
over
[0]\in\Delta
, the category
{\mathcal{D}}(c)
is contractible, and

•

for each

in
{\mathbb{C}}
over

Lemma 4.3
.

If
F:{\mathcal{D}}\to{\mathcal{C}}
is a right or left resolution, then
F
is also a resolution.

Proof.

We prove the right part, the left part being dual, Inductively, assume we have proven the resolution property for each
\mathbf{c}^{\prime}_{[k]}
with
0\leq k<n
. Then for an object
\mathbf{c}_{[n]}=c_{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle f}}{{\to}}c_{1}\to...\to c_{n}
we have the associated functor
{\mathcal{D}}(c_{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle f}}{{\to}}c_{1}\to...\to c_{n})\to{\mathcal{D}}(c_{1}\to...\to c_{n})
. This is an opfibration over a contractible category, with fibres equivalent to
F(f,d)
for some
d\in{\mathcal{D}}(c_{1})
. Quillen’s Theorem A implies then the contractibility of
{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbf{c}_{[n]})
. ∎

Lemma 4.4
.

If
F:{\mathcal{D}}\to{\mathcal{C}}
is a prefibration (and, by convention, an isofibration) with contractible fibres, then it is a right resolution.

Dually, if
F:{\mathcal{D}}\to{\mathcal{C}}
is a preopfibration (and, by convention, an isofibration) with contractible fibres, then it is a left resolution.

Proof.

Since
F
is an isofibration, the categories
{\mathcal{D}}(c)
and
{\mathcal{D}}(c^{\prime}\to c)
are equivalent to their strict analogues: the strict fibre
F^{-1}(c)
and the category of arrows
d^{\prime}\to d
with
F(d^{\prime}\to d)
equal to
c^{\prime}\to c
. It is then easy to see that the fibres of
{\mathcal{D}}(c^{\prime}\to c)\to{\mathcal{D}}(c)
have terminal objects for a prefibration, and the fibres of
{\mathcal{D}}(c^{\prime}\to c)\to{\mathcal{D}}(c^{\prime})
have initial objects for a preopfibration, and hence are contractible. Quillen’s Theorem A, again, implies the result. ∎

This example motivates the intuition behind left (respectively right) resolutions as covariant (respectively contravariant) families of contractible homotopy types, represented in categories, indexed by the base category
{\mathcal{C}}
.

Lemma 4.5
.

If
p:{\mathcal{D}}\rightleftarrows{\mathcal{C}}:i
is an adjunction and

is full and faithful, then
p
is a resolution. An equivalence of categories is a resolution.

Proof.

Every fibre
{\mathcal{D}}(c_{0}\to...\to c_{n})
has a terminal object given by
ic_{0}\to...\to ic_{n}
(note that

is isomorphic to

).
∎

.

Let

Remark 4.8
.

For many uses it is already sufficient to know the weaker statement, that the functor

However proving such a weaker statement often gives more for free.
To verify that a given relative functor is a weak equivalence, one can often use the following
criterion. Let

preserving all weak equivalences,

and the homotopy category adjunction
