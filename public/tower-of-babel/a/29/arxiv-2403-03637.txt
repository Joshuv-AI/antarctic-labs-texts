Exact objectives of random linear programs and mean widths of random polyhedrons
Mihailo Stojnic

Abstract




Abstract

We consider
random linear programs
(rlps) as a subclass of
random optimization problems
(rops) and study their typical behavior. Our particular focus is on appropriate linear objectives which connect the rlps to the mean widths of random polyhedrons/polytopes. Utilizing the powerful machinery of
random duality theory
(RDT)
[
64
]
, we obtain, in a large dimensional context, the exact characterizations of the program’s objectives. In particular, for any
\alpha=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{m}{n}\in(0,\infty)
, any unit vector
\mathbf{c}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, any fixed
\mathbf{a}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, and
A\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n}
with iid standard normal entries, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{P}}_{A}\left((1-\epsilon)\xi_{opt}(\alpha;\mathbf{a})\leq\min_{A\mathbf{x}\leq\mathbf{a}}\mathbf{c}^{T}\mathbf{x}\leq(1+\epsilon)\xi_{opt}(\alpha;\mathbf{a})\right)\longrightarrow 1,

where

\xi_{opt}(\alpha;\mathbf{a})\triangleq\min_{x>0}\sqrt{x^{2}-x^{2}\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(\left(\frac{\mathbf{a}_{i}}{x}\right)^{2}+1\right)\mbox{erfc}\left(\frac{\mathbf{a}_{i}}{x\sqrt{2}}\right)-\frac{\mathbf{a}_{i}}{x}\frac{e^{-\frac{\mathbf{a}_{i}^{2}}{2x^{2}}}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\right)}{n}}.

For example, for
{\bf a}=\mathbf{1}
, one uncovers

\xi_{opt}(\alpha)=\min_{x>0}\sqrt{x^{2}-x^{2}\alpha\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{x^{2}}+1\right)\mbox{erfc}\left(\frac{1}{x\sqrt{2}}\right)-\frac{1}{x}\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2x^{2}}}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\right)}.

Moreover,
2\xi_{opt}(\alpha;\mathbf{1})
is precisely the concentrating point of the mean width of the polyhedron
\{{\bf x}|A{\bf x}\leq\mathbf{1}\}
.

Index Terms: Random linear programs; Polyhedron/polytope width; Random duality theory
.

1
Introduction

Given a function
f({\bf x}):{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
a generic linearly constrained optimization problems have the following form:

\displaystyle\min_{{\bf x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}

\displaystyle f({\bf x})

subject to

\displaystyle A{\bf x}\leq{\bf a}

(1)

\displaystyle B{\bf x}={\bf b},

where

is an
m_{1}\times n

is an
m_{2}\times n
matrix,
{\bf a}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{1}\times 1}
an
m_{1}\times 1
vector, and
{\bf b}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{2}\times 1}
is an
m_{2}\times 1
vector. If, additionally, one also has that the objective is linear, i.e. for some
{\bf c}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
,

\displaystyle f({\bf x})={\bf c}^{T}{\bf x},

(2)

then the problem in (
1
) is called
linear
program. Given that (
1
) is one of the most famous classical optimization problems, it has a rich history and has been thoroughly studied for over a century (see, e.g.,
[
15
,
51
,
45
,
39
,
40
,
9
,
11
,
10
]
and references therein). Depending on the context, types of applications, and overall usefulness many aspects of (
1

[

,

,

,

,

,

,

,
10
]
), position just finding the minimal value of

and
{\bf x}

Our interest here is slightly different and is not primarily motivated by the classical algorithmic point of view. Instead of trying to algorithmically find the minimal
f({\bf x})
, we will be interested in scenarios where

can be analytically characterized without actually explicitly solving (

(rlps). We also restrict the focus to fully polyhedral/polytopal rlps, i.e. to rlps with inequality constraints. This basically means that the following version of (

\displaystyle\xi\triangleq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\min_{{\bf x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}

\displaystyle{\bf c}^{T}{\bf x}

\displaystyle A{\bf x}\leq{\bf a},

where

. In scenarios where

are random, we view

Random optimization problems, and in particular the linear ones, have played an important role in various scientific and engineering fields and, consequently, have been the subject of an intensive studying over the last several decades. We below briefly discuss some of the most relevant known results.

Probably among the first random feasibility problems (rfps) that gained a strong prominence are the machine learning pattern recognition instances and in particular the so-called perceptrons. Perceptrons are often viewed as the most fundamental units of any neural network concept. 1.2
Our contribution

We set
m\triangleq m_{1}
and consider large
n

linear
regime, i.e. the regime where
\alpha=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{m_{1}}{n}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{m}{n}
remains constant as
n
grows. We then consider (
3
) in a typical statistical scenario with elements of
A
being iid standard normals. Such scenarios are precisely the ones considered in
[
64
,
58
,
59
]
where the foundational principles of the
random duality theory
(RDT) were introduced. We here rely on these principles, utilize the RDT, and create a generic framework for the analysis of (
3
). As a consequence, we obtain the precise closed form characterization of
\xi
(the objective value of (
3
)) as a function of any real number
\alpha\in(0,\infty)
. Moreover, we show that for
\alpha\rightarrow\infty
our results precisely match those obtained in
[
4
]
.

In sections that follow below, we will slowly introduce all the needed RDT ingredients. We, however, find it useful to note here before starting the technical presentation, that our exposition will in no way use or rely on any analytical/algorithmic concepts particularly related to (
1
) or (
3
). In other words, besides a few well known generic optimization concepts, no extensive or advanced knowledge of the optimization theory is required.

2
The problem setup

As emphasized earlier, we focus on programs (
1
) that have a linear objective and linear inequalities. In other words, we consider

\displaystyle\xi=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\min_{{\bf x}}

\displaystyle{\bf c}^{T}{\bf x}

subject to

\displaystyle A{\bf x}\leq{\bf a}.

(4)

To avoid overwhelming the presentation with a tone of tiny details and special cases and to ultimately make the writing neater and easier, we assume throughout the paper that the objective in (
4
) exists and is either deterministically bounded (i.e., bounded for any realization of
A
) or that it is bounded with a probability (over the randomness of
A
) going
1
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Along the same lines, to further facilitate and make less cumbersome the overall exposition, we also assume boundedness (deterministic or random) of all other key quantities appearing in the derivations below. Equipped with such assumptions, we proceed by transforming (
4
)

\displaystyle\xi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\min_{{\bf x}}\max_{\lambda\geq 0}\left({\bf c}^{T}{\bf x}+\lambda^{T}A{\bf x}-\lambda^{T}{\bf a}\right).

(5)

The above holds generically, i.e., for any
A
,
{\bf a}
, and
{\bf c}
. It therefore automatically applies to the random instances as well.
Given that such instances are of our particular interest here, the following summarizes the probabilistic characterization of
\xi
that we are looking for.

Ultimate goal:

Given:

\displaystyle\alpha=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{m}{n}\in(0,\infty)

find

\displaystyle\xi_{opt}(\alpha;{\bf a})

such that

\displaystyle\forall\epsilon>0,\quad\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{P}}_{A}\left((1-\epsilon)\xi_{opt}(\alpha;{\bf a})\leq\xi\leq(1+\epsilon)\xi_{opt}(\alpha;{\bf a})\right)\longrightarrow 1.

As expected,
\alpha
and
\epsilon
can be arbitrarily large or small but they do not change as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Throughout the paper we adopt a usual convention where the subscripts next to
{\mathbb{P}}
and
{\mathbb{E}}
denote the randomness with respect to which the statistical evaluation is taken. When clear from the contexts, the subscripts are left unspecified.

3
Handling random linear programs via RDT

We start by recalling on the main RDT principles and then continue by showing, step-by-step, how each of them applies to the problems of our interest here.

Summary of the RDT’s main principles

[
58
,
64
]

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{ll}\hskip-13.73148pt\mbox{1) \emph{Finding underlying optimization algebraic representation}}&\hskip 0.0pt\mbox{2) \emph{Determining the random dual}}\\
\hskip-13.73148pt\mbox{3) \emph{Handling the random dual}}&\hskip 0.0pt\mbox{4) \emph{Double-checking strong random duality.}}\end{array}

To make the presentation neat, we formalize all the key results (including both simple to more complicated ones) as lemmas and theorems.

1)
Algebraic objective characterization:
The following lemma summarizes the algebraic discussions from previous sections and ultimately provides a convenient optimization representation of the objective
\xi
. Given that the trivial scaling by
\|{\bf c}\|_{2}
of the objective in (
3
) introduces no conceptual changes, we below, without loss of generality, assume
\|{\bf c}\|_{2}=\sqrt{n}
.

Lemma 1
.

(Algebraic optimization representation) Let
A\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n}
be any given (possibly random) matrix. For a given
{\bf c}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
,
\|{\bf c}\|_{2}=\sqrt{n}
and a vector
{\bf a}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
with the components being fixed real numbers that do not change as
n\rightarrow\infty
, let
\xi
be as in (
3
) and set

\displaystyle f_{rp}(A)

\displaystyle\triangleq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\min_{{\bf x}}\max_{\lambda\geq 0}\left({\bf c}^{T}{\bf x}+\lambda^{T}A{\bf x}-\lambda^{T}{\bf a}\right)\hskip 57.81621pt({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\textbf{random primal}})

\displaystyle\xi_{rp}

\displaystyle\triangleq

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{A}f_{rp}(A).

(8)

Then

\xi=f_{rp}(A)\quad\mbox{and}\quad\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{A}\xi=\xi_{rp}.

(9)

Proof.

Follows immediately via the Lagrangian from (
5
).
∎

The above lemma holds for any given matrix
A
. The RDT proceeds by imposing a statistics on
A
.

2)
Determining the random dual:
As is standard within the RDT, we utilize the concentration of measure, which here basically means that for any fixed
\epsilon>0
, one can write (see, e.g.
[
58
,
64
,
59
]
)

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{P}}_{A}\left(\frac{|f_{rp}(A)-{\mathbb{E}}_{A}(f_{rp}(A))|}{{\mathbb{E}}_{A}(f_{rp}(A))}>\epsilon\right)\longrightarrow 0.

(10)

Another key RDT ingredient is the following so-called random dual theorem.

Theorem 1
.

(Objective characterization via random dual) Assume the setup of Lemma
9
and let the components of
A
be iid standard normals. Let
{\bf g}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
and
{\bf h}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be vectors comprised of iid standard normals and set

\displaystyle f_{rd}({\bf g},{\bf h})

\displaystyle\triangleq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\min_{{\bf x}}\max_{\lambda\geq 0}\left({\bf c}^{T}{\bf x}+\|{\bf x}\|_{2}\lambda^{T}{\bf g}+\|\lambda\|_{2}{\bf h}^{T}{\bf x}-\lambda^{T}{\bf a}\right)\hskip 57.81621pt({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\textbf{random dual}})

\displaystyle\xi_{rd}

\displaystyle\triangleq

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{{\bf g},{\bf h}}f_{rd}({\bf g},{\bf h}).\vskip 0.0pt

(11)

One then has

\displaystyle\xi_{rd}

\displaystyle\triangleq

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{{\bf g},{\bf h}}f_{rd}({\bf g},{\bf h})\leq\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{A}f_{rp}(A)\triangleq\xi_{rp}.\vskip 0.0pt

(12)

and

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{P}}_{{\bf g},{\bf h}}\left(f_{rd}({\bf g},{\bf h})\geq(1-\epsilon)\xi_{rd}\right)\leq\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{P}}_{A}\left(f_{rp}(A)\geq(1-\epsilon)\xi_{rd}\right).

(13)

Proof.

Follows as a direct application of the Gordon’s probabilistic comparison theorem (see, e.g., Theorem B in
[
32
]
as well as Theorem 1, Corollary 1, and Section 2.7.2 in
[
65
]
).
∎

3)
Handling the random dual:
After solving the inner maximization over
\lambda
we have

\displaystyle f_{rd}({\bf g},{\bf h})

\displaystyle\triangleq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\min_{{\bf x}}\max_{\lambda\geq 0}\left({\bf c}^{T}{\bf x}+\|{\bf x}\|_{2}\lambda^{T}{\bf g}+\|\lambda\|_{2}{\bf h}^{T}{\bf x}-\lambda^{T}{\bf a}\right)

(14)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\min_{{\bf x}}\max_{\|\lambda\|_{2}}\left({\bf c}^{T}{\bf x}+\|\lambda\|_{2}{\bf h}^{T}{\bf x}+\|\lambda\|_{2}\|\max\left(\|{\bf x}\|_{2}{\bf g}-{\bf a},0\right)\|_{2}\right).

Keeping the
\|\lambda\|_{2}
fixed and minimizing over
{\bf x}
, we further find

\displaystyle f_{rd}({\bf g},{\bf h})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\min_{{\bf x}}\max_{\|\lambda\|_{2}}\left({\bf c}^{T}{\bf x}+\|\lambda\|_{2}{\bf h}^{T}{\bf x}+\|\lambda\|_{2}\|\max\left(\|{\bf x}\|_{2}{\bf g}-{\bf a},0\right)\|_{2}\right)

(15)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\min_{\|{\bf x}\|_{2}}\max_{\|\lambda\|_{2}}\left(-\|{\bf x}\|_{2}\|{\bf c}+\|\lambda\|_{2}{\bf h}\|_{2}+\|\lambda\|_{2}\|\max\left(\|{\bf x}\|_{2}{\bf g}-{\bf a},0\right)\|_{2}\right).

\lambda_{f}=\|\lambda\|_{2}

x
and
\lambda_{f}

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{{\bf h}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\|{\bf c}+\|\lambda\|_{2}{\bf h}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sqrt{\frac{{\mathbb{E}}_{{\bf h}}\|{\bf c}+\|\lambda\|_{2}{\bf h}\|_{2}^{2}}{n}}

(16)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sqrt{\frac{{\mathbb{E}}_{{\bf h}}\left(\|{\bf c}\|_{2}^{2}+2\|\lambda\|_{2}{\bf c}^{T}{\bf h}+\|\lambda\|_{2}^{2}\|{\bf h}\|_{2}^{2}\right)}{n}}

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sqrt{\frac{\|{\bf c}\|_{2}^{2}+2\|\lambda\|_{2}{\mathbb{E}}_{{\bf h}}\left({\bf c}^{T}{\bf h}\right)+\|\lambda\|_{2}^{2}{\mathbb{E}}_{{\bf h}}\|{\bf h}\|_{2}^{2}}{n}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{1+\lambda_{f}^{2}}.

Moreover, we also have

\displaystyle f^{(1)}(x;{\bf a})

\displaystyle\triangleq

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{{\bf g}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\|\max\left(\|{\bf x}\|_{2}{\bf g}-{\bf a},0\right)\|_{2}

(17)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{{\bf g}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\|\max\left(x{\bf g}-{\bf a},0\right)\|_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{{\bf g}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left\|\max\left({\bf g}-\frac{{\bf a}}{x},0\right)\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle x\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sqrt{\frac{{\mathbb{E}}_{{\bf g}}\left\|\max\left({\bf g}-\frac{{\bf a}}{x},0\right)\right\|_{2}^{2}}{n}}

\displaystyle x\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\mathbb{E}}_{{\bf g}_{i}}\max\left({\bf g}_{i}-\frac{{\bf a}_{i}}{x},0\right)^{2}}{n}}

\displaystyle x\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\frac{{\bf a}_{i}}{x}}^{\infty}\left({\bf g}_{i}-\frac{{\bf a}_{i}}{x},0\right)^{2}e^{-\frac{{\bf g}_{i}^{2}}{2}}d{\bf g}_{i}}{n}}

\displaystyle\triangleq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\frac{{\bf a}_{i}}{x}}^{\infty}\left({\bf g}_{i}-\frac{{\bf a}_{i}}{x},0\right)^{2}e^{-\frac{{\bf g}_{i}^{2}}{2}}d{\bf g}_{i}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\left(\frac{{\bf a}_{i}}{x}\right)^{2}+1\right)\mbox{erfc}\left(\frac{{\bf a}_{i}}{x\sqrt{2}}\right)-\frac{{\bf a}_{i}}{x}\frac{e^{-\frac{{\bf a}_{i}^{2}}{2x^{2}}}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}.

(18)

15

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{{\bf g},{\bf h}}f_{rd}({\bf g},{\bf h})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{{\bf g},{\bf h}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\min_{\|{\bf x}\|_{2}}\max_{\|\lambda\|_{2}}\left(-\|{\bf x}\|_{2}\|{\bf c}+\|\lambda\|_{2}{\bf h}\|_{2}+\|\lambda\|_{2}\|\max\left(\|{\bf x}\|_{2}{\bf g}-{\bf a},0\right)\|_{2}\right)

\displaystyle\min_{x>0}\max_{\lambda_{f}>0}\left(-x\sqrt{1+\lambda_{f}^{2}}+\lambda_{f}f^{(1)}(x;{\bf a})\right),

\displaystyle f^{(1)}(x;{\bf a})\triangleq x\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m}f^{(1)}_{i}(x,{\bf a}_{i})}{n}}=x\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(\left(\frac{{\bf a}_{i}}{x}\right)^{2}+1\right)\mbox{erfc}\left(\frac{{\bf a}_{i}}{x\sqrt{2}}\right)-\frac{{\bf a}_{i}}{x}\frac{e^{-\frac{{\bf a}_{i}^{2}}{2x^{2}}}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\right)}{n}}.

(20)

\displaystyle\frac{d\left(-x\sqrt{1+\lambda_{f}^{2}}+\lambda_{f}f^{(1)}(x;{\bf a})\right)}{d\lambda_{f}}

\displaystyle-\frac{2x\lambda_{f}}{2\sqrt{1+\lambda_{f}^{2}}}+f^{(1)}(x;{\bf a}).

Setting the above derivative to zero then gives

\displaystyle x^{2}\lambda_{f}^{2}=\left(1+\lambda_{f}^{2}\right)\left(f^{(1)}(x;{\bf a})\right)^{2},

which implies that one has for the optimal

\displaystyle\hat{\lambda}_{f}=\frac{f^{(1)}(x;{\bf a})}{\sqrt{x^{2}-\left(f^{(1)}(x;{\bf a})\right)^{2}}},

(23)

Plugging the above
\hat{\lambda}_{f}

), we obtain

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{{\bf g},{\bf h}}f_{rd}({\bf g},{\bf h})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min_{x>0}\max_{\lambda_{f}>0}\left(-x\sqrt{1+\lambda_{f}^{2}}+\lambda_{f}f^{(1)}(x;{\bf a})\right)

(24)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min_{x>0}\left(-x\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{f^{(1)}(x;{\bf a})}{\sqrt{x^{2}-\left(f^{(1)}(x;{\bf a})\right)^{2}}}\right)^{2}}+\frac{\left(f^{(1)}(x;{\bf a})\right)^{2}}{\sqrt{x^{2}-\left(f^{(1)}(x;{\bf a})\right)^{2}}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min_{x>0}\left(-x\sqrt{\frac{x^{2}}{x^{2}-\left(f^{(1)}(x;{\bf a})\right)^{2}}}+\frac{\left(f^{(1)}(x;{\bf a})\right)^{2}}{\sqrt{x^{2}-\left(f^{(1)}(x;{\bf a})\right)^{2}}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min_{x>0}\sqrt{x^{2}-\left(f^{(1)}(x;{\bf a})\right)^{2}}

.

(Characterization of random dual) Assume the setup of Theorem

\xi_{rd}
be as in (

\displaystyle\xi_{rd}

\displaystyle\triangleq

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{{\bf g},{\bf h}}f_{rd}({\bf g},{\bf h})=\min_{x>0}\sqrt{x^{2}-\left(f^{(1)}(x;{\bf a})\right)^{2}}

(25)

\displaystyle=

4
Conclusion

We considered
random linear programs
(rlps) which are a subclass of
random optimization problems
(rops). It was demonstrated how the powerful machinery of
random duality theory
(RDT)
[
64
]
can be utilized to create a generic framework for the analysis of rlps. We then applied the framework for the analysis of the optimization of a linear objective over the standard Gaussian polyhedrons. For a typically hardest to analyze, so-called large
n
linear (proportional) regime, we obtained the closed form exact characterization of the program’s objective value as a function of a single system parameter
\alpha
which represents the ratio of the number of polyhedral constraints and the problem’s ambient dimension. We also uncovered that the result is insensitive with respect to the particular linearity of the objective, which, due to the rotational symmetry of the matrix of constraints, is to be expected. Moreover, if the vector defining the hyperplane of the objective is chosen uniformly at random from the unit sphere, the corresponding averaged objective value is precisely the mean width of the random polyhedron/polytope. This effectively means that our results automatically provide the characterization of the mean width of the Gaussian’s polytopes. Our characterizations are valid for any number of cconstraint/dimension ratios
\alpha
, including the limiting
\alpha\rightarrow\infty
one. Moreover, in the
limiting scenario, our results take a particularly simple
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\log(\alpha)}}
form and precisely match those obtained for
\alpha\rightarrow\infty
in
[
4
]
.

The presented methodology is very generic and many extensions and/or generalizations are possible. For example, besides optimal values of the objective various other properties of optimization problems are of interest. These may relate to behavior of the optimal solutions or critical constraints. Moreover, the randomness can appear in different forms (perturbation or full scale) and can affect different parts of the problem infrastructure. Moreover, generalization are in no way restricted to
random linear problems
(rlps). They apply equally well to general
random optimization problems
(rops). Various other random structures can be analyzed as well, including, for example,
random feasibility problems
(rfps) (although pretty detailed, the list of random structures discussed in, e.g.,
[
66
]
is only a small sample of a large collection of problems that can be handled through the machinery presented here). The associated technical details are often problem specific, and we discuss them in separate papers.

As pointed out in
[
64
]
, the RDT considerations do not require the standard Gaussianity assumption. Utilizing the assumption, however, made the presentation smoother. While the writing would necessarily be more cumbersome, the needed conceptual adjustments as one deviates from the Gaussianity are rather minimal. In particular, the Lindeberg central limit theorem variant (see, e.g.,
[
41
]
) can be utilized to extend the RDT results to various different statistics. We consider the approach of
[
17
]
as particularly elegant in that regard.
