Effect of a magnetic field on the thermodynamic properties of a high-temperature hadron resonance gas with van der Waals interactions
Bhagyarathi Sahoo, Kshitish Kumar Pradhan, Dushmanta Sahu, Raghunath Sahoo

Abstract
We study the behavior of a hadronic matter in the presence of an external magnetic field within the van der Waals hadron resonance gas model, considering both attractive and repulsive interactions among the hadrons. Various thermodynamic quantities like pressure ( P P ), energy density ( ε \varepsilon ), magnetization ( ℳ \mathcal{M} ), entropy density ( s s ), squared speed of sound ( c s 2 c_{\rm s}^{2} ), and specific-heat capacity at constant volume ( c v c_{v} ) are calculated as functions of temperature ( T T ) and static finite magnetic field ( e ​ B eB ). We also consider the effect of baryochemical potential ( μ B \mu_{B} ) on the above-mentioned thermodynamic observables in the presence of a magnetic field. Further, we estimate the magnetic susceptibility ( χ M 2 \chi_{\rm M}^{2} ), relative permeability ( μ r \mu_{\rm r} ), and electrical susceptibility ( χ Q 2 \chi_{\rm Q}^{2} ) which can help us to understand the system better. Through this model, we quantify a liquid-gas phase transition in the T-eB- μ B \mu_{B} phase space.

Introduction

In the early stages of the evolution of the Universe, it was supposed to be extremely hot and dense, possibly filled with a unique state of matter called quark-gluon plasma (QGP). We explore the ultrarelativistic heavy-ion collisions in laboratories to probe such initial conditions. At extreme temperatures and/or baryon densities, the hadronic degrees of freedom transform into partonic degrees of freedom, resulting in QGP formation. Quantum chromodynamics (QCD) is the widely used theory to describe the behavior of QGP. In addition, studying its thermodynamic properties is of utmost importance to understand the behavior and evolution of hot and dense QCD matter. Various thermodynamic properties of strongly interacting nuclear matter have been estimated from the first-principle lattice QCD (lQCD) approach. However, the applicability of lQCD breaks down at high baryochemical potential due to the fermion sign problem

(43)

For this work, the parameters in the model are taken as

GeV fm

, where the hard-core radius

III
Renormalization of vacuum pressure

As we discussed in the previous section, the total pressure (negative of the total free-energy density) of the system is due to both the thermal and vacuum components, i.e.

P_{total}=P_{th}(T,eB)+\Delta P_{vac}(T=0,eB)

(44)

where
P_{th}(T,eB)
is the thermal part of the pressure, which is the sum of the pressure due to both charged and neutral particles. In the presence of a magnetic field, the thermal parts of the pressure for charged and neutral particles are calculated using Eqs. (
II
), and (
6
), respectively.
In this section, we will calculate the vacuum contribution of pressure in the presence of an external magnetic field using a dimensional regularization method. The vacuum pressure term is ultraviolet divergent, and it requires appropriate regularization to extract meaningful physical information
Kadam:2019rzo
;
Endrodi:2013cs
;
Menezes:2008qt
. As a result, magnetic field-dependent and independent components must be distinguished using an appropriate regularization technique.

In the presence of an external magnetic field, the vacuum pressure for a charged spin-
\frac{1}{2}
particle is given by
Kadam:2019rzo
;
Endrodi:2013cs
;
Menezes:2008qt

P_{\text{vac}}(S=1/2,B)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}g_{k}\frac{|Q|B}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dp_{z}}{2\pi}E_{p,k}(B),

(45)

where
g_{k}=2-\delta_{k0}
is the degeneracy of the
kth
Landau level. We have added and subtracted the lowest Landau-level contribution (i.e.,
k=0
) from the above equation, and we get

\displaystyle P_{\text{vac}}(S=1/2,B)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}2\frac{|Q|B}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dp_{z}}{2\pi}

\displaystyle\bigg[E_{p,k}(B)-\frac{E_{p,0}(B)}{2}\bigg].

(46)

A dimensional regularization method
Peskin:1995
is used to regularize the ultraviolet divergence of vacuum pressure. In
d-\varepsilon
dimension Eq. (
III
) can be written as

\displaystyle P_{\text{vac}}(S=1/2,B)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{|Q|B}{2\pi}\mu^{\varepsilon}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d^{1-\varepsilon}p_{z}}{(2\pi)^{1-\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle\bigg[\sqrt{p_{z}^{2}+m^{2}-2|Q|Bk}-\sqrt{p_{z}^{2}+m^{2}}\bigg],

(47)

In the preceding equation,
\mu
sets the dimension to 1. The integration can be carried out using the usual
d-
dimensional formulas
Peskin:1995
;
Ramond:2001
.

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d^{d}p}{(2\pi)^{d}}\>\bigg[p^{2}+m^{2}\bigg]^{-A}=\frac{\Gamma[A-\frac{d}{2}]}{(4\pi)^{d/2}\Gamma[A](m^{2})^{(A-\frac{d}{2})}}.

(48)

Integration of the first term in Eq. (
III
) gives

\displaystyle I_{1}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{|Q|B}{2\pi}\mu^{\varepsilon}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d^{1-\varepsilon}p_{z}}{(2\pi)^{1-\varepsilon}}\bigg[p_{z}^{2}+m^{2}-2|Q|Bk\bigg]^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{(|Q|B)^{2}}{4\pi^{2}}\bigg(\frac{2|Q|B}{4\pi\mu}\bigg)^{-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}\Gamma\bigg[-1+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\bigg]\zeta\bigg[-1+\frac{\varepsilon}{2},x\bigg],

(49)

where we denote
x\equiv\frac{m^{2}}{2|Q|B}
. The Landau infinite sum has been illustrated in terms of the Riemann-Hurwitz
\zeta
function

\zeta[z,x]=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{[x+k]^{z}},

(50)

with the expansion
Digital:mathematica
;
Elizalde:1986
,

\zeta\bigg[-1+\frac{\varepsilon}{2},x\bigg]\approx-\frac{1}{12}-\frac{x^{2}}{2}+\frac{x}{2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\zeta^{{}^{\prime}}(-1,x)+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2})

(51)

and the asymptotic behavior of the derivative
Digital:mathematica
;
Elizalde:1986
,

\displaystyle\zeta^{\prime}(-1,x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{12}-\frac{x^{2}}{4}+\left(\frac{1}{12}-\frac{x}{2}+\frac{x^{2}}{2}\right)ln(x)+\mathcal{O}(x^{-2}).

The expansion of the
\Gamma
function around some negative integers is given by

\Gamma\bigg[-1+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\bigg]=-\frac{2}{\varepsilon}+\gamma-1+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon),

(53)

and,

\Gamma\bigg[-2+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\bigg]=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}-\frac{\gamma}{2}+\frac{3}{4}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon).

(54)

Here,
\gamma
is the Euler constant. The limiting expression for natural is,

\lim_{\varepsilon\longrightarrow 0}a^{-\varepsilon/2}\approx 1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\text{ln}(a).

(55)

Equation (
III
) can be written as expressed as using the expansion of the
\Gamma
function and
\zeta
function:

\displaystyle I_{1}=-\frac{(|Q|B)^{2}}{4\pi^{2}}\bigg[-\frac{2}{\varepsilon}+\gamma-1+\text{ln}\bigg(\frac{2|Q|B}{4\pi\mu^{2}}\bigg)\bigg]

\displaystyle\bigg[-\frac{1}{12}-\frac{x^{2}}{2}+\frac{x}{2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\zeta^{{}^{\prime}}(-1,x)+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2})\bigg]

(56)

The second term in Eq. (
III
) can be simplified in the same way, and we obtain,

\displaystyle I_{2}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{|Q|B}{2\pi}\mu^{\varepsilon}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d^{1-\varepsilon}p_{z}}{(2\pi)^{1-\varepsilon}}\bigg[p_{z}^{2}+m^{2}\bigg]^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(|Q|B)^{2}}{4\pi^{2}}\bigg[-\frac{x}{\varepsilon}-\frac{(1-\gamma)}{2}x+\frac{x}{2}\text{ln}\bigg(\frac{2|Q|B}{4\pi\mu^{2}}\bigg)+\frac{x}{2}\text{ln}(x)\bigg].

Hence, the vacuum pressure in the presence of an external magnetic field becomes

\displaystyle P_{\text{vac}}(S=1/2,B)=\frac{(|Q|B)^{2}}{4\pi^{2}}\bigg[\zeta^{{}^{\prime}}(-1,x)-\frac{2}{12\varepsilon}-\frac{(1-\gamma)}{12}

\displaystyle-\frac{x^{2}}{\varepsilon}-\frac{(1-\gamma)}{2}x^{2}+\frac{x}{2}\text{ln}(x)

\displaystyle+\frac{x^{2}}{2}\text{ln}\bigg(\frac{2|Q|B}{4\pi\mu^{2}}\bigg)+\frac{1}{12}\text{ln}\bigg(\frac{2|Q|B}{4\pi\mu^{2}}\bigg)\bigg].

Divergence is still evident in the preceding expression. As a result, we add and deduct the
B=0
contribution from it. To carry out the renormalization of the
B>0
pressure, the
B=0
contribution must be determined. The vacuum pressure in
d=3-\varepsilon
dimensions at
B=0
is given by

\displaystyle P_{\text{vac}}(S=1/2,B=0)=\mu^{\varepsilon}\int\frac{d^{3-\varepsilon}p}{(2\pi)^{3-\varepsilon}}\>(p^{2}+m^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(|Q|B)^{2}}{4\pi^{2}}\bigg(\frac{2|Q|B}{4\pi\mu^{2}}\bigg)^{-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}\Gamma\bigg(-2+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\bigg)x^{2-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}.

Above Eq. (
III
) can be further simplified by using
\Gamma
-function expansion from Eq. (
53
),

\displaystyle P_{\text{vac}}(S=1/2,B=0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{(|Q|B)^{2}}{4\pi^{2}}x^{2}\bigg[\frac{1}{\varepsilon}+\frac{3}{4}-\frac{\gamma}{2}

(60)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\text{ln}\bigg(\frac{2|Q|B}{4\pi\mu^{2}}\bigg)-\frac{1}{2}\text{ln}(x)\bigg]

Now, we add and subtract Eq. (
60
) from (
III
), we get the regularized pressure with the vacuum part, and the magnetic field-dependent part separated as

\displaystyle P_{\text{vac}}(S=1/2,B)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P_{\text{vac}}(1/2,B=0)+\Delta P_{\text{vac}}(1/2,B),

where,

\displaystyle\Delta P_{\text{vac}}(S=1/2,B)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(|Q|B)^{2}}{4\pi^{2}}\bigg[-\frac{2}{12\varepsilon}+\frac{\gamma}{12}

(62)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{12}\text{ln}\bigg(\frac{m^{2}}{4\pi\mu^{2}}\bigg)+\frac{x}{2}\text{ln}(x)-\frac{x^{2}}{2}\text{ln}(x)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{x^{2}}{4}-\frac{\text{ln}(x)+1}{12}+\zeta^{{}^{\prime}}(-1,x)\bigg].

The field contribution given by the Eq. (
62
) is, however, divergent due to the existence of the magnetic field-dependent term
\frac{B^{2}}{\varepsilon}

Schwinger:1951nm
;
Elmfors:1993bm
;
Andersen:2011ip
. We eliminate this divergence by redefining field-dependent pressure contribution to include magnetic field contribution.

\Delta P_{\text{vac}}^{r}=\Delta P_{\text{vac}}(B)-\frac{B^{2}}{2}.

(63)

The divergences are absorbed into the renormalization of the electric charge and the magnetic field strength
Endrodi:2013cs
,

B^{2}=Z_{e}B_{r}^{2};\hskip 14.22636pte^{2}=Z_{e}^{-1}e_{r}^{2};\hskip 14.22636pte_{r}B_{r}=|Q|B,

(64)

where the electric charge renormalization constant is

\displaystyle Z_{e}\bigg(S=\frac{1}{2}\bigg)=1+\frac{1}{2}e_{r}^{2}\bigg[-\frac{2}{12\varepsilon}+\frac{\gamma}{12}+\frac{1}{12}\text{ln}\bigg(\frac{m_{*}}{4\pi\mu^{2}}\bigg)\bigg].

We fix
m_{*}=m
, i.e. the particle’s physical mass. Thus, the contribution of the renormalized field-dependent pressure in the absence of a pure magnetic field (
\frac{B^{2}}{2}
) is,

\displaystyle\Delta P_{\text{vac}}^{r}(S=1/2,B)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(|Q|B)^{2}}{4\pi^{2}}\bigg[\zeta^{{}^{\prime}}(-1,x)+\frac{x}{2}\text{ln}(x)

(66)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\frac{x^{2}}{2}\text{ln}(x)+\frac{x^{2}}{4}-\frac{\text{ln}(x)+1}{12}\bigg]

Using a similar technique, the renormalized magnetic field-dependent pressure for spin-zero and spin-one particles can be calculated. These terms are crucial in determining the magnetization of hadronic matter. The vacuum pressure is affected by the charge, mass, and spin of the particles. As a result, the total vacuum pressure of a hadron gas is calculated by adding the vacuum pressures of all particles taken into account.

For spin-zero particles, the regularized vacuum pressure is

\displaystyle\Delta P_{\text{vac}}^{r}(s=0,B)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{(|Q|B)^{2}}{8\pi^{2}}\bigg[\zeta^{{}^{\prime}}(-1,x+1/2)-\frac{x^{2}}{2}\text{ln}(x)

(67)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{x^{2}}{4}+\frac{\text{ln}(x)+1}{24}\bigg].

Similarly, for spin-one particles,

\displaystyle\Delta P_{\text{vac}}^{r}(s=1,B)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{3}{8\pi^{2}}(|Q|B)^{2}\bigg[\zeta^{{}^{\prime}}(-1,x-1/2)

(68)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{(x+1/2)}{3}\text{ln}(x+1/2)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{2}{3}(x-1/2)\text{ln}(x-1/2)-\frac{x^{2}}{2}\text{ln}(x)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{x^{2}}{4}-\frac{7}{24}(\text{ln}(x)+1)\bigg].

So, the total magnetic field-dependent vacuum pressure becomes

\displaystyle\Delta P_{\text{vac}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Delta P_{\text{vac}}^{r}(s=0,B)+\Delta P_{\text{vac}}^{r}(S=1/2,B)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\Delta P_{\text{vac}}^{r}(s=1,B).

After computing the total vacuum pressure, the system’s vacuum magnetization can be computed as follows:

\Delta\mathcal{M}_{\text{vac}}=\frac{\partial(\Delta P_{\text{vac}})}{\partial(|Q|B)}.

(70)

V
Summary

In this work, we explore the effect of a magnetic field on the thermodynamic properties of an interacting hadron resonance gas model at zero and finite chemical potential. The static finite magnetic field significantly affects pressure, energy density, trace anomaly, magnetization, and second-order conserved charge fluctuations such as electric and magnetic susceptibility. However, this effect is less significant on entropy density, specific heat, etc. We found that all thermodynamic quantities are suppressed because of interactions. The effect of higher baryon chemical potential on the thermodynamic variable is interesting. The magnetization, entropy density, specific heat, and speed of sound may indicate discontinuity behavior approaching a higher baryochemical potential, which suggests a phase transition in the VDWHRG model. A clear observation of diamagnetic to paramagnetic transitions happens in our study. The electrical susceptibility is found to be suppressed because of the magnetic field at lower temperatures, and it slowly increases at higher temperatures. A possible liquid-gas phase transition is also explored in the presence of a finite magnetic field and baryochemical potential.

Appendix A
Squared speed of sound

The squared speed of sound is given by,

c_{s}^{2}(T,\mu,QB)=\bigg(\frac{\partial P}{\partial\epsilon}\bigg)_{s/n}.

(71)

Using the variables
T
,
\mu
and
QB
this can be rewritten as

c_{s}^{2}(T,\mu,QB)=\frac{dP}{d\epsilon}=\frac{\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}+\frac{\partial P}{\partial\mu}\frac{d\mu}{dT}+\frac{\partial P}{\partial(QB)}\frac{d(QB)}{dT}}{\frac{\partial\epsilon}{\partial T}+\frac{\partial\epsilon}{\partial\mu}\frac{d\mu}{dT}+\frac{\partial\epsilon}{\partial(QB)}\frac{d(QB)}{dT}}

(72)

Number density (
n
) and entropy density (
s
) of a system is a function of
(T,\mu,QB)

The first condition is keeping the ratio
(s/n)
constant. From the derivative, one obtains

d\bigg(\frac{s}{n}\bigg)=0,

(73)

which implies

nds=sdn.

(74)

Divide both sides by dT so that the above Eq. (
) can be modified as,

n\bigg(\frac{ds}{dT}\bigg)=s\bigg(\frac{dn}{dT}\bigg).

(75)

One can write
n(T,\mu,QB)
and
s(T,\mu,QB)
in the form of differential as,

dn=\frac{\partial n}{\partial T}dT+\frac{\partial n}{\partial\mu}d\mu+\frac{\partial n}{\partial(QB)}d(QB)

(76)

So if we divide both sides of the Eq. (
76
) by dT, then we have,

\frac{dn}{dT}=\frac{\partial n}{\partial T}+\frac{\partial n}{\partial\mu}\frac{d\mu}{dT}+\frac{\partial n}{\partial(QB)}\frac{d(QB)}{dT}

(77)

Similarly for
s(T,\mu,QB)
we can write,

ds=\frac{\partial s}{\partial T}dT+\frac{\partial s}{\partial\mu}d\mu+\frac{\partial s}{\partial(QB)}d(QB)

(78)

\frac{ds}{dT}=\frac{\partial s}{\partial T}+\frac{\partial s}{\partial\mu}\frac{d\mu}{dT}+\frac{\partial s}{\partial(QB)}\frac{d(QB)}{dT}

(79)

Substituting Eq. (
77
) and Eq. (
79
) in Eq. (
75
) we get,

n\bigg(\frac{\partial s}{\partial T}+\frac{\partial s}{\partial\mu}\frac{d\mu}{dT}+\frac{\partial s}{\partial(QB)}\frac{d(QB)}{dT}\bigg)=s\bigg(\frac{\partial n}{\partial T}+\frac{\partial n}{\partial\mu}\frac{d\mu}{dT}+\frac{\partial n}{\partial(QB)}\frac{d(QB)}{dT}\bigg).

(80)

\displaystyle\frac{d\mu}{dT}\bigg(n\frac{\partial s}{\partial\mu}-s\frac{\partial n}{\partial\mu}\bigg)=s\bigg(\frac{\partial n}{\partial T}+\frac{\partial n}{\partial(QB)}\frac{d(QB)}{dT}\bigg)-n\bigg(\frac{\partial s}{\partial T}+\frac{\partial s}{\partial(QB)}\frac{d(QB)}{dT}\bigg)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\frac{d\mu}{dT}=\frac{\bigg(s\frac{\partial n}{\partial T}-n\frac{\partial s}{\partial T}\bigg)+\bigg(s\frac{\partial n}{\partial(QB)}-n\frac{\partial s}{\partial(QB)}\bigg)\frac{d(QB)}{dT}}{n\frac{\partial s}{\partial\mu}-s\frac{\partial n}{\partial\mu}}

(81)

Similarly, one can evaluate

\frac{d(QB)}{dT}=\frac{\bigg(s\frac{\partial n}{\partial T}-n\frac{\partial s}{\partial T}\bigg)+\bigg(s\frac{\partial n}{\partial\mu}-n\frac{\partial s}{\partial\mu}\bigg)\frac{d\mu}{dT}}{n\frac{\partial s}{\partial(QB)}-s\frac{\partial n}{\partial(QB)}}

(82)

For a finite baryon chemical potential and finite external magnetic field, the above two transcendental equations can be solved numerically to find the speed of sound of the system.
