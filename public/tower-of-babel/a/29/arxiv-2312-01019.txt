Suk-Geun Hwang, Woo Jeon, Ki-Bong Nam, Tung T. Nguyen

Abstract
We define the finite number ring ℤ n ​ [ r m ] {\mathbb{Z}}_{n}[\sqrt[m]{r}] where m , n m,n are positive integers and r r in an integer akin to the definition of the Gaussian integer ℤ ⁡ [ i ] {\mathbb{Z}}[i] . This idea is also introduced briefly in [ 9 ] . By definition, this finite number ring ℤ n ​ [ r m ] {\mathbb{Z}}_{n}[\sqrt[m]{r}] is naturally isomorphic to the ring ℤ n ​ [ x ] / ⟨ x m − r ⟩ {\mathbb{Z}}_{n}[x]/{\langle x^{m}-r\rangle} . From an educational standpoint, this description offers a straightforward and elementary presentation of this finite ring, making it suitable for readers who do not have extensive exposure to abstract algebra. We discuss various arithmetical properties of this ring. In particular, when n = p n=p is a prime number and ℤ p \mathbb{Z}_{p} contains a primitive m m -root of unity, we describe the structure of ℤ n ​ [ r m ] \mathbb{Z}_{n}[\sqrt[m]{r}] explicitly.



Keywords: Radical Extension, Unital Determinant, Finite Fields, Polynomial Factorization

\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}x_{i}(\sqrt[m]{r})^{i}+\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}x_{i}(\sqrt[m]{r})^{i}=\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}(x_{i}+y_{i})(\sqrt[m]{r})^{i}

\mathbb{Z}_{n}[\sqrt[m]{r}]

(\sqrt[m]{r})^{i}(\sqrt[m]{r})^{j}=\begin{cases}(\sqrt[m]{r})^{i+j}&\text{if
$i+j<m$}\\
{r}(\sqrt[m]{r})^{k}&\text{if
$i+j>m$ and $k\equiv i+j$ mod $m$}\\
{r}&\text{if
$i+j=m$}\end{cases}

(1)

We can see that
\mathbb{Z}_{n}[\sqrt[m]{r}]
is a commutative ring which is naturally isomorphic to the quotient ring
\mathbb{Z}_{n}[x]/\langle x^{m}-r\rangle
. The goal of this paper is to prove some fundamental properties of these finite rings using the explicit description mentioned above.

2
Ring theoretic properties of
\mathbb{Z}_{n}[\sqrt[m]{r}]

We begin with a lemma about finite commutative rings.

Lemma 1
.

Let
R
be a finite commutative ring. Then
R
is an integral domain if and only if
R
is a field.

Proof.

If
R
is a field then clearly
R
is an integral domain. Conversely, suppose that
R
is an integral domain. We claim that each element
a\in R
such that
a\neq 0
has an inverse. In fact, let
m_{a}:R\to R
be the multiplication map

m_{a}(x)=ax.

Because
R
is an integral domain, the map
m_{a}
is injective. Since
R
is finite,
m_{a}
must be surjective as well. Therefore, we can find
b\in R
such that
ab=1.
This shows that
a
is invertible in
R.
Since this is true for all
a\in R
such that
a\neq 0
, we conclude that
R
is a field.
∎

Suppose that
n=p_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\ldots p_{s}^{\alpha_{s}}
be the prime factorization of
n.
Then, by the Chinese remainder theorem

\mathbb{Z}_{n}\cong\prod_{i=1}^{s}\mathbb{Z}_{p_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}}.

Consequently

\mathbb{Z}_{n}[\sqrt[m]{r}]\cong\prod_{i=1}^{s}\mathbb{Z}_{p_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}}[\sqrt[m]{r}].

A direct consequence of this observation is the following

Proposition 1
.

If
n
is not a prime integer, then the ring
{\mathbb{Z}}_{n}[\sqrt[m]{r}]
is not an integral domain.

Proof.

If
n
is not prime, then
{\mathbb{Z}}_{n}
is not an integral domain. Since the ring
{\mathbb{Z}}_{n}[\sqrt{r}]
contains
{\mathbb{Z}}_{n}
, we conclude that is also not an integral domain.
∎

Proposition 2
.

If
x^{m}-r
is reducible over
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
; namely there exists
f,g\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}[x]
such that
\deg(f)>1
and
\deg(g)>1
and

x^{m}-r=f(x)g(x),

then
\mathbb{Z}_{n}[\sqrt[m]{r}]
is not an integral domain.

Proof.

By our assumption

f(\sqrt[m]{r})g(\sqrt[m]{r})=(\sqrt[m]{r})^{m}-r=0.

Since
\deg(f),\deg(g)<m
, we conclude that
f(\sqrt[m]{r})
must be a non-zero divisor in
\mathbb{Z}_{n}[\sqrt[m]{r}].
Therefore,
\mathbb{Z}_{n}[\sqrt[m]{r}]
is not an integral domain.
∎

We have the following condition, which provides a necessary and sufficient condition for
\mathbb{Z}_{n}[\sqrt[m]{r}]
to be a field.

Theorem 1
.

The finite number ring
\mathbb{Z}_{n}[\sqrt[m]{r}]
is a field if and only the following two conditions are satisfied

1.

n
is a prime number.

2.

x^{m}-r
is irreducible in
\mathbb{Z}_{n}[x].

Proof.

The necessary part of this Theorem follows from Lemma
1
, Proposition
1
, and Proposition
2
. Conversely, suppose that
n
is a prime number and
x^{m}-r
is irreducible over
\mathbb{Z}_{n}[r]
, we claim that
\mathbb{Z}_{n}[\sqrt[m]{r}]
is a field. Let
a=a_{0}+a_{1}\sqrt[m]{r}+a_{2}(\sqrt[m]{r})^{2}+\cdots+a_{m-1}(\sqrt[m]{r})^{m-1}
be a non-zero element in
\mathbb{Z}_{n}[\sqrt[m]{r}]
. Let

h(x)=a_{0}+a_{x}+\cdots+a_{m-1}x^{m-1}\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}[x].

Since
p
is prime,
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
is a field, and
\mathbb{Z}_{p}[x]
is a Euclidean domain. By the Euclidean algorithm and the fact that
x^{m}-r
is irreducible of degree
m
, we can find
f_{1},f_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
such that

h(x)f_{1}(x)+(x^{m}-r)f_{2}(x)=1.

We then see that

af_{1}(\sqrt[m]{r})=h(\sqrt[m]{r})f_{1}(\sqrt[m]{r})=1.

This shows that
a
is invertible as required.
∎

2.1
Units in
\mathbb{Z}_{n}[\sqrt[m]{r}]

In this section, we study units in the ring
\mathbb{Z}_{n}[\sqrt[m]{r}]
by embedding it in a matrix ring. We start with the following lemma.

Lemma 2
.

A non-zero element
a=a_{0}+a_{1}\sqrt[m]{r}+a_{2}(\sqrt[m]{r})^{2}+\cdots+a_{m-1}(\sqrt[m]{r})^{m-1}
of the
ring
{\mathbb{Z}}_{n}[\sqrt[m]{r}]
is a unit, if and only if
A
is invertible where

A=\begin{bmatrix}a_{0}&a_{m-1}r&a_{m-2}r&a_{m-3}r&\cdots&a_{1}r\\
a_{1}&a_{0}&a_{m-1}r&a_{m-2}r&\cdots&a_{2}r\\
a_{2}&a_{1}&a_{0}&a_{m-1}r&\cdots&a_{3}r\\
&&&\cdots&\\
a_{m-1}&a_{m-2}&a_{m-3}&a_{m-4}&\cdots&a_{0}\end{bmatrix}.

Equivalently,
a_{0}+a_{1}\sqrt[m]{r}+a_{2}(\sqrt[m]{r})^{2}+\cdots+a_{m-1}(\sqrt[m]{r})^{m-1}
is a unit if and only if
\Delta=\det(A)\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}^{\times}.

Proof.

Suppose that
A
is invertible. We want to find a non-zero element

b_{0}+b_{1}\sqrt[m]{r}+b_{2}(\sqrt[m]{r})^{2}+\cdots+b_{m-1}(\sqrt[m]{r})^{m-1},

of the ring
\mathbb{Z}_{n}[\sqrt[m]{r}]
such that

\displaystyle(a_{0}+a_{1}\sqrt[m]{r}+\cdots+a_{m-1}(\sqrt[m]{r})^{m-1})(b_{0}+b_{1}\sqrt[m]{r}+\cdots+b_{m-1}(\sqrt[m]{r})^{m-1})=1.

This is equivalent to

\displaystyle a_{0}b_{0}+a_{m-1}rb_{1}+a_{m-2}rb_{2}+a_{m-3}rb_{3}+\cdots+a_{1}rb_{m-1}=1

\displaystyle a_{1}b_{0}+a_{0}b_{1}+a_{m-1}rb_{2}+a_{m-2}rb_{3}+\cdots+a_{2}rb_{m-1}=0

\displaystyle a_{2}b_{0}+a_{1}b_{1}+a_{0}b_{2}+a_{m-1}rb_{3}+\cdots+a_{3}rb_{m-1}=0

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\cdots

\displaystyle a_{m-1}b_{0}+a_{m-2}b_{1}+a_{m-3}b_{2}+a_{m-4}b_{3}+\cdots+a_{0}b_{m-1}=0.

(2)

Since
A
is invertible, the system (
2.1
) has a unique solution.
This implies that the system of linear equations (
2.1
) has a unique non-zero solution. We conclude that
a
is invertible. Conversely, suppose that
a
is invertible. Then the multiplication
m_{a}:\mathbb{Z}_{n}[\sqrt[m]{r}]\to\mathbb{Z}_{n}[\sqrt[m]{r}]
is invertible. In particular, for each
0\leq i\leq m-1
, we can find

v_{i}=b_{0i}+b_{1i}\sqrt[m]{r}+\ldots+b_{(m-1)i}(\sqrt[m]{r})^{m-1},

such that
av_{i}=(\sqrt[m]{r})^{i}.
In other words, we have

\displaystyle A\begin{bmatrix}b_{0i}\\
b_{1i}\\
\vdots\\
b_{ii}\\
\vdots\\
b_{(m-1)i}\end{bmatrix}=e_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
0\\
\vdots\\
1\\
\vdots\\
0\end{bmatrix}.

Therefore, if we let
B=(b_{ij})_{0\leq i,j\leq m-1}
then
AB=I_{m}
. This shows that
A
is invertible.

∎

Corollary 2

An element

of the ring

2.3
Gaussian Integers over
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}

A prime
p
is called Pythagorean if there are integers
a
and
b

such that
a^{2}+b^{2}=p
. It is well-known that this condition is equivalent to the condition that either

or
p
is a prime of the form

(see

]
).

Proposition 7
.

{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}[i]
is a field if and only if
p
is not an Pythagorean prime.

Proof.

If
p
is Pythagorean then there are integers
a,b
such that
a^{2}+b^{2}=p^{2}
. This implies that
(a+bi)((a-bi)=a^{2}+b^{2}\equiv p
(mod
p
) i.e.,
a^{2}+b^{2}\equiv 0
mod
p
. This
implies that
a+bi
has a zero divisor. Thus
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}[i]
is not an integral domain, i.e., it is not a field.

Conversely, suppose that
\mathbb{Z}_{p}[i]
is not a field. By Corollary
3
, there exists
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
such that
a^{2}+b^{2}=0.
This is equivalent to the condition that

or

In other words,

\Box

3
Finite rings over finite fields

In this section, we study the finite ring
\mathbb{F}_{q}[\sqrt[m]{r}]
where
\mathbb{F}_{q}
is the finite field with
q
elements. This section is a bit less elementary than the previous section in the sense that we need Galois theory for some of our arguments. In fact, we have the following statement about the Galois group of a finite field.

Proposition 8
.

Let
\mathbb{F}_{q^{n}}/\mathbb{F}_{q}
be a finite extension. Then the Galois group of
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}/\mathbb{F}_{q}
is a cyclic group of order
n
generated by the Frobenius automorphism
\text{Frob}_{q}
defined by
\text{Frob}(x)=x^{q}.
Note that
\text{Frob}_{q}
is exactly the
q
-power map
f^{[q]}.

Definition 1
.

Let
a,b
be two relatively prime integers. The order of
a
modulo
b
, denoted by
\text{ord}_{b}(a)
is the smallest number
h
such that
a^{h}\equiv 1\pmod{b}
.

We also need the following definition.

Definition 2
.

Let
\mathbb{F}_{p}
be the prime finite field of characteristics
p
and
r\in\overline{\mathbb{F}_{p}}^{\times}.
The order of
r
, denoted by
\text{ord}(r)
is defined to be the smallest positive integer
h
such that
r^{h}=1.

We are now ready to discuss the factorization of
x^{m}-r
over a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
under the assumption that
\mathbb{F}_{q}
contains a primitive
m
-roots of unity. We first observe the following phenomenon.

Theorem 7
.

Suppose that
\mathbb{F}_{q}
contains a primitive
m
-root of unity. Then
x^{m}-r
can be factorized into a product of
\frac{m}{t}
irreducible polynomials of degree
t
where
t=\text{ord}_{\text{ord}(r)m}(q)
; namely,
t
is the smallest number such that
\text{ord}(r)m|q^{t}-1.

Proof.

Since
\gcd(q,m)=1
, the polynomial
x^{m}-r
is separable over
\mathbb{F}_{q}.
Let
\alpha
be any root of
x^{m}-r
over
\overline{\mathbb{F}_{q}}.
Then, by definition
\alpha^{m}=r.
The finite extension
\mathbb{F}_{q}(\alpha)/\mathbb{F}_{q}
is a Galois extension whose Galois group is generated by the Frobenius map
a\mapsto a^{q}
. Consequently, the Galois conjugates of
\alpha
are
\{\alpha,\alpha^{q},\ldots,\alpha^{q^{t-1}}\}
where
t
is the smallest positive integer such that
\alpha^{q^{t}}=\alpha.
Equivalently,
\alpha^{q^{t}-1}=1.
We remark that since the Galois orbit of
\alpha
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
has
t
elements, the minimal polynomial of
\alpha
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
must have degree
t.
We claim that

t=\text{ord}_{\text{ord}(r)m}(q)
, independent of
\alpha
. If this is true, our theorem is proved. In fact, since
\mathbb{F}_{q}
contains a primitive
m
-root of unity, we know that
m|q-1.
We then see that

1=\alpha^{q^{t}-1}=(\alpha^{m})^{\frac{q^{t}-1}{m}}=r^{\frac{q^{t}-1}{m}}.

The above equality implies that
\text{ord}(r)|\frac{q^{t}-1}{m}
or equivalently
\text{ord}(r)m|q^{t}-1.
Since
t
is the smallest positive integer with this property, we conclude that
t=\text{ord}_{\text{ord}(r)m}(q).

∎

Let

\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]
(by Theorem
7
,
t
is independent of the choice of the irreducible factor). We claim that
t=m.
We know that

t=\text{ord}_{\text{ord}(r)m}(q)=\text{ord}_{(q-1)m}(q).

By definition,
t
is the smallest positive integer such that

\text{ord}(r)m|q^{t}-1.
Put it equivalently

m|\frac{q^{t}-1}{\text{ord}(r)}=\frac{q-1}{\text{ord}(r)}(1+q+\ldots+q^{t-1}).

Since
\mathbb{F}_{q}
contains a primitive
m
-root of unity, we know that
m|q-1.
Consequently

0\equiv\frac{q-1}{\text{ord}(r)}(1+q+\ldots+q^{t-1})\equiv\frac{q-1}{\text{ord}(r)}t\pmod{m}.

(4)

Let
d=\gcd(m,\frac{q-1}{\text{ord}(r)})=1.
We claim that

. Indeed, since
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
is a cyclotomic group of order
q-1
, there exists a primitive root in

such that

r=g^{\frac{q-1}{\text{ord}(r)}}.
Since
d|\frac{p-1}{\text{ord}(r)}

By our assumption on

From this fact and Equation

t|m

t=m

is irreducible.
∎

We discuss two corollaries Theorem

Corollary 10
.

If
r
is a primitive root in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
and
\mathbb{F}_{q}
contains a primitve
m
-root of unity, then
x^{m}-r
is irreducible over
\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]
.

Proof.

Since
r
is a primitive root and
m|q-1
,
r
can not belong to the image of
f^{[k]}
for all
k|m
and
k>1.
By Theorem
9
,
x^{m}-r
must be irreducible.
∎

The next corollary is a direct consequence of Theorem

.

Corollary 11
.

Suppose that
m
is a prime number and
\mathbb{F}_{q}
contains a primitive
m
-root of unity. Then
x^{m}-r
is irreducible if and only
r
is not an
m
-power.

Corollary 12
.

Suppose that
\mathbb{F}_{q}
contains a primitive
m
-root of unity. Let
M
be the positive integer such that

1.

m|M
and
\text{rad}(M)=\text{rad}(m)
. Here, for a positive integer
a
,
\text{rad}(a)
is the product of all distinct prime factors of
a.

2.

\gcd(M,\frac{q-1}{M})=1.

Then, the number of
r\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
such that
x^{m}-r
is irreducible is
\varphi(M)\dfrac{q-1}{M}.

Proof.

By Theorem
9
, we know that
x^{m}-r
is irreducible if and only if
r
does not belong to the image of
f^{[k]}
for each
k|m
and
k>1
. By the proof of this theorem, this is equivalent to saying that
\gcd(m,\frac{q-1}{\text{ord}(r)})=1.
Because
m|q-1
, this is also equivalent to
M|\text{ord}(r).
Let us write
\text{ord}(r)=Mh
where
h|\frac{q-1}{M}.
Since the group
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
is cyclic, the number of elements of order
Mh
is exactly
\varphi(Mh)=\varphi(M)\varphi(h).
We conclude that the number of irreducible polynomials of the form
x^{m}-r
is exactly

\sum_{h|\frac{q-1}{M}}\varphi(M)\varphi(h)=\varphi(M)\sum_{h|\frac{q-1}{M}}\varphi(h)=\varphi(M)\frac{q-1}{M}.

The last equality follows from the fact that for any positive integer
n

\sum_{d|n}\varphi(d)=n.

∎

Remark 3.1
.

If we assume that
m
is a squarefree number then we can relax the condition that
\mathbb{F}_{q}
contains a primitive
m
-root of unity in Corollary
12
. In fact, suppose that
\mathbb{F}_{q}
does not contain a primitive
m
-root of unity. This implies that
m\nmid q-1.
Because
m
is a squarefree number, there exists a prime divisor
d
of
m
such that
d\nmid q-1.
This implies that the
f^{[d]}
power map is surjective. In other words, we can write
r=b^{d}
for some
b\in\mathbb{F}_{q}.
This implies that
x^{m/d}-b
is a factor of
x^{m}-r.
As a corollary,
x^{m}-r
is always reducible.
