Synthetic projective lines, geometric closure and AB-sets
Koen Thas

Abstract
. In this note, we introduce a new approach to abstract “synthetic” projective lines. We discuss various aspects of our approach, and compare these aspects with the classical one. A number of intriguing questions arise. Amongst these aspects, we discuss geometric closures, and introduce automorphism-blocking sets . We also construct a number of (counter) examples in infinite cases.



1.
Introduction

There exists a long and rich history in defining the concept of a projective line, both over coordinatizing algebraic structures and combinatorially.
The classical approach is to consider a field (or division ring)
k
, and define the projective line
\mathbb{P}^{1}(k)
over
k
as the geometry of the vector lines in the
2
-dimensional (left or right, in case of a division ring) vector space
V(2,k)
. (One can see the line as the set
k\cup\{\infty\}
, which comes with the natural
3
-transitive action of
\mathsf{PGL}_{2}(k)
, extended with field automorphisms.) This local viewpoint is a property we want to incorporate in our approach.

Some known approaches

One first, naive synthetic approach is the rather “flat” incidence-geometrical way in which one sees a line as a set of points
\Omega
, which then comes with the automorphism
group
\mathsf{Sym}(\Omega)
. This is obviously not what we want: their is no projective aspect whatsoever, and the automorphism group is boring. Buekenhout
[
1
]
, under the influence of the school of Libois,
took an entire different turn, and modeled projective lines after projective lines seen as subgeometries of Desarguesian projective spaces
\mathbb{P}^{n}(k)
(with
k
a field). More specifically, he molded the geometry by using specific automorphisms (which in the Desarguesian examples are induced by central collineations in the line stabilizers in
\mathsf{P\Upgamma L}_{n+1}(k)
), as such creating an interesting geometric object which comes with an interesting automorphism group. We recall Buekenhout’s definition. A
projective line
is a set
\mathcal{L}
of cardinality
\geq 3
, satisfying the following axioms:

(a)

Each
p,q\in\mathcal{L}
, comes with a group
\lambda(p,q)
whose elements are called
central collineations
with
center

p
and
axis

q
. Note that
q
need not be distinct from
p
. The group
\lambda(p,q)
acts on
\mathcal{L}
as follows: it fixes
p
and
q
, and if an element fixes a point
r\not\in\{p,q\}
, then it is the identity. The action is faithful, and each
\lambda(p,q)
is a subgroup of
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathcal{L})
.

(b)

If
p\neq q
, then
\lambda(p,q)
and
\lambda(q,p)
commute with each other.

(c)

For any
\gamma\in\lambda(r,s)
and any
p,q
, we have
\gamma\lambda(p,q)\gamma^{-1}=\lambda(\gamma(p),\gamma(q))
.

(d)

The composite of two collineations with center
p
(possibly with different axes) is again a collineation with center
p
, and dually the composite of two collineations with axis
q
(possibly with different centers) is again a collineation with axis
q
. Thus we have two groups
\lambda(p)=\cup_{q}\lambda(p,q)
and
\lambda(q)=\cup_{p}\lambda(p,q)
.

A projective line is
Desarguesian
if in addition
\lambda(p,q)
acts (sharply) transitively on
\mathcal{L}\setminus\{p,q\}
.
In this approach, any set is a projective line (by putting all
\lambda(p,q)=\{\mathrm{id}\}
). The natural examples are the lines
U
in any (not necessarily Desarguesian) projective plane
\mathcal{P}
: if
u
and
v
are points of
U
, then let
\lambda(u,v)
be induced by the group of central collineations of
\mathcal{P}
with center
u
and axis some line
V
incident with
v
where
V
varies over these lines.
Buekenhout’s theory is just one of many quite similar approaches which endow a set with specific permutations modeled after lines in certain classes of projective planes. Still, it only models the automorphism groups of projective lines (in projective planes), and not the geometry. In our approach, projective lines will come with an embedding; this viewpoint includes a geometric theory, a relative angle and heavily restricted automorphism groups.

One other way to see projective lines in the incidence-geometrical context is through Tits’s theory of (split) BN-pairs, in this case of dimension
1
. Unfortunately this is essentially an approach to classical lines, and if
U
is a line in a projective line
\mathcal{P}
, then if
H
is the group induced on
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathcal{P})_{U}
, the permutation group
(H,U)
(where we see
U
as a point set) is generically not reached by a (split) BN-pair of rank
1
, since the latter are always doubly transitive.
We feel that this approach also lacks an underlying geometry, and in this paper we want to take quite a different view on the subject which entertains both more geometry and more general automorphism groups.

Remark 1.1
.

One of the most interesting viewpoints is the algebro-geometric approach, which puts a Zariski topology on the set of prime ideals of a commutative ring (this
defines the geometry), and then comes with a sheaf of rings. For our purposes, especially the geometrical (topological) aspect is very interesting, and the structure of the commutative ring endows the projective line with a highly nontrivial geometry. We give two examples. Consider the real projective line
\mathsf{Proj}(\mathbb{R}[x,y])
. Its prime ideals (closed points) are generated by either polynomials of degree
1
or irreducible polynomials of degree
2
. Those of degree
1
, such as
(x-a)
gives a “classical rational point” (
(1,a)
) and those of the second type, such as
(x^{2}+1)
“become” two rational points
(x+i)
(
(1,i)
) and
(x-i)
(
(1,-i)
) in the projective line
\mathsf{Proj}(\mathbb{C}[x,y])
, and
\mathsf{Gal}(\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R})
acts transitively on these “conjugate points.”
Second example
: consider the algebro-geometric projective line
L=\mathsf{Proj}({\mathbb{F}_{q}}[x,y])
, where
\mathbb{F}_{q}
is any finite field. Its closed points are the “usual” points, but it contains much more information. Let
x
be any of its closed points (which we regard as the point at infinity), and consider the affine line
L\setminus\{x\}=\mathsf{Spec}(\mathbb{F}_{q}[x])
. Let
f(x)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]
be an irreducible polynomial of degree
m
. Then the point corresponding to the prime ideal
(f(x))
defines
m
different points in
\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}\cong\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]/(f(x))
on which the Galois group
\mathsf{Gal}(\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}/\mathbb{F}_{q})
acts transitively. In some sense, the affine line
\mathsf{Spec}(\overline{\mathbb{F}_{q}}[x])
contains the same information (and its Galois group captures the information of all the separate finite Galois groups).

The present paper

In this paper, a projective line comes with a full imbedding in an axiomatic projective plane, and morphisms between lines must take the embeddings into account. But there is more: if
U
is a line in a projective plane
\mathcal{P}
, then the actual projective line is determined by a point
x
not incident with
U
, and that line consists of the data
\mathcal{L}=(\mathcal{P},U,x)
. The points of the line
\mathcal{L}
are given by the lines incident with
x
, and automorphism must respect the structure of the plane, while fixing
U
and
x
. We compare two approaches:
the “projective” approach in which
U
itself is defined as a line, and the aforementioned “affine” approach, which looks at the line from the viewpoint of
x
. In the Desarguesian case, those approaches are essentially the same, but in general they are very different. Generically, what one sees locally from the point
x
depends on the location of
x
, and only when there is an automorphism of the plane which fixes
U
while carrying
x
to another point
x^{\prime}
, the local geometries will be the same. On the other hand, it could be that nonisomorphic lines in this formalism define the exact same automorphism group as a permutation group, and that is different in the synthetic approached we mentioned (where the isomorphism class of the line is determined by the isomorphism class of the permutation group it defines).

Our main viewpoint is essentially the following. If one wants to define abstract synthetic projective planes
\Upgamma
, one cannot use an embedding into a higher-dimensional projective space such as a
3
-space (
2
), since the classical Veblen-Young result states that such as space is isomorphic to a space
\mathbb{P}^{3}(\dj)
which is defined
over a division ring
\dj
. But since planes do not have this property, we
can
and will use embeddings of lines in axiomatic planes (
1
) to do the job.

2.
Some initial definitions

A point-line incidence structure
\Upgamma=(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{L},\mathbf{I})
(where
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{L}
are the point set and line set, and
\mathbf{I}
. is a symmetric incidence relation) is a
projective plane
if it satisfies the following axioms:

(a)

any two distinct points are incident with one unique line;

(b)

any two distinct lines are incident with one unique point;

(c)

there exist four distint points of which no three are incident with the same line.

Each projective plane has an
order

N
; any of its points are then incident with
N+1
lines and any line is incident with
N+1
points.

A point-line incidence structure
\Upgamma=(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{L},\mathbf{I})
(where
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{L}
are the point set and line set, and
\mathbf{I}
. is a symmetric incidence relation) is an
affine plane
if it satisfies the following axioms:

(a)

any two distinct points are incident with one unique line;

(b)

if
A
is a line and
a
is a point not incident with
A
, then
a
is incident with a unique line
B
which does not meet
A
;

(c)

there exist three distint points of which no three are incident with the same line.

Automorphisms and isomorphisms of and between projective and affine planes are defined in the standard way
[
8
]
. By
\mathrm{Aut}(\Upgamma)
, we denote the automorphism group of a geometry
\Upgamma
.

If
\Upgamma
is a projective plane and
U
is any line of
\Upgamma
, then by deleting the line
U
and the points incident with
U
, one obtains an affine plane
\Upgamma_{U}
. On the other hand, if
\mathcal{A}
is an affine plane, then there exists a projective plane
\mathcal{P}
and a line
V
in
\mathcal{P}
such that
\mathcal{P}_{V}
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{A}
. The couple
(\mathcal{P},V)
is unique up to isomorphism with respect to this property. Any automorphism of
\mathcal{P}
which fixes
V
induces an automorphism of
\mathcal{P}_{V}
, and every automorphism of
\mathcal{A}
defines an automorphism of
\mathcal{P}
which fixes
V
. If
\mathcal{A}
is an affine plane, then by
\overline{\mathcal{A}}
we denote its projective completion.

3.
Synthetic projective lines

We are guided by the analogy between vector spaces (over division rings) and axiomatic affine planes with a distinguished point (which we compare to the zero vector). So consider any axiomatic affine plane
\mathcal{A}
, and fix one arbitrary point
\omega
. Based on the discussion in section
2
, we define the projective line
(\mathcal{A},\omega)
as the set of lines
\mathcal{L}_{\omega}
of
\mathcal{A}
incident with
\omega
. Note that we do not consider
\mathcal{L}_{\omega}
merely as a set: it comes with an embedding in
\mathcal{A}
. The definition of the automorphism group of
(\mathcal{A},\omega)
clearly illustrates this viewpoint.

3.1.
Automorphism group

An
automorphism
of
(\mathcal{A},\omega)
is an element
\alpha
of
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathcal{A})_{\omega}
, taking into account that
\alpha=\alpha^{\prime}
in
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathcal{A},\omega)
with
\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\in\mathrm{Aut}(\mathcal{A})_{\omega}
if and only if they induce the same action on
\mathcal{L}_{\omega}
.
It follows that

(2)

\mathrm{Aut}(\mathcal{A},\omega)\ \cong\ \mathrm{Aut}(\mathcal{A})_{\omega}/N

with
N
the kernel of the action of
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathcal{A})_{\omega}
on
\mathcal{L}_{\omega}
—that is, the group of homologies with center
\omega
and axis the line at infinity of
\mathcal{A}
.

The notion of isomorphism is now obvious: we say that two synthetic lines
(\mathcal{A},\omega)
and
(\mathcal{A},\omega^{\prime})
are
isomorphic
if there exists an isomorphism of planes

(3)

\gamma:\ A\ \mapsto A^{\prime}

such that
\gamma(\omega)=\omega^{\prime}
.

In section
5
we will formally define the category of projective lines.

3.2.
Projective planes versus affine planes

One could also consider the variation of the definition of synthetic projective line, in which the affine plane
\mathcal{A}
is replaced by a projective plane
\mathcal{P}
. In that case, we could also dualize the definition, and see a projective type line as a line of a projective plane, which comes with an automorphism group which is induced by the group of the plane.

We prefer the affine approach, on the one hand because of the vector space analogy, and on the other hand because it depends on the location of the base point (relative to the line at infinity, which is a natural model for the line). Also, a number of very interesting problems arise in the affine approach, and these are among our motivations to study the problem. In fact, the very problems which arise when comparing the affine approach to the projective approach already are very promising, as we will see in this section. Let us remark that if
(\mathcal{A},\mu)
is a projective line (
\mathcal{A}
an affine plane), then the embeddings

(4)

(\mathcal{A},\mu)\ \hookrightarrow\ \mathcal{A}\ \hookrightarrow\ \overline{\mathcal{A}}

implies that
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathcal{A},\mu)
is a subgroup of
\mathrm{Aut}(\overline{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
.

The main two questions we ask are the following. Let
\mathcal{P}
be a projective plane, and consider, in the same way as above, the synthetic line (projective version)
(\mathcal{P},\mu)
and
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathcal{P})_{\mu}/N
, with
N
the kernel of the action of
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathcal{P})_{\mu}
on the lines incident with
\mu
. For each line
U
in
\mathcal{P}
which is not incident with
\mu
we can consider the synthetic line
(\mathcal{P}_{U},\mu)
, with
\mathcal{P}_{U}
the affine plane arising from deleting
U
.

Question
3.1

(Inheritance)
.

Is
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathcal{P})_{\mu}/N=\bigcup_{U\mathop{\not\mathbf{I}}\mu}\Big({\mathrm{Aut}(\mathcal{P})}_{U,\mu}\Big/M_{U}\Big)
, with
M_{U}
the kernel of the action of
{\mathrm{Aut}(\mathcal{P})}_{U,\mu}
on the lines incident with
\mu
.

In other words: can we recover each automorphism of the projective type line
(\mathcal{P},\mu)
from some automorphism of an affine type line
(\mathcal{P}_{U},\mu)
?

We will construct counter examples in the infinite case, which will indicate that many counter examples exist. In the finite case, many counter examples exist as well, and we will explain some thoughts on that matter. A second, highly related but altogether different question which we need to understand is the following:

Question
3.2

(Generation)
.

Is
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathcal{P})_{\mu}/N=\Big\langle\Big({\mathrm{Aut}(\mathcal{P})}_{U,\mu}\Big/M_{U}\Big)\ |\ U\mathop{\not\mathbf{I}}\mu\Big\rangle
, with
M_{U}
the kernel of the action of
{\mathrm{Aut}(\mathcal{P})}_{U,\mu}
on the lines incident with
\mu
.

Here we ask whether each automorphism of the projective type line
(\mathcal{P},\mu)
is
generated
by automorphisms of affine type lines
(\mathcal{P}_{U},\mu)
.

3.3.
Some general remarks

We first recall the next theorem:

Theorem 3.3

(Hughes and Piper
[
8
]
, Theorem 13.3)
.

An automorphism of a finite projective plane has an equal number of fixed points and lines.

Now any automorphism
\alpha\in\mathrm{Aut}(\mathcal{P})
which fixes
\mu
, also fixes some line
M
. If
M
is not incident with
\mu
, then
\alpha
, as an automorphism of
(\mathcal{P},\mu)
, is inherited from an automorphism of
(\mathcal{P}_{M},\mu)
. But theoretically, automorphisms which only fix a flag of
\mathcal{P}
, or more generally which fix
m<0
lines on a point
\mathcal{U}
and
n>0
points on exactly one these lines (and no other lines and points), could exist, and would not be inherited. (Call such automorphisms “of projective origin.”) We will now construct counter examples which work both in the finite and infinite case for Desarguesian planes, and more generally for semifield planes. In the next subsection, we will construct a rather different class of counter examples.

Call a projective plane
\mathcal{P}
a
semifield plane
if there exists a flag
(x,Y)
for which
x
is a translation point (with translation group
T(x)
), and
Y
a translation line (with translation group
T(Y)
). Now let
t\in T(Y)^{\times}
be such that it fixes the point
u\neq x
on
Y
linewise (an element of
T(Y)
which is not an
(x,Y)
-elation has this property). Let
\widetilde{y}
be an element in
T(x)^{\times}
which does not fix
Y
pointwise (again, any element which is not an
(x,Y)
-elation is such an element). Note that
T(Y)\cap T(x)
is precisely the complete group of all
(x,Y)
-elations. Then obviously
t\widetilde{t}
fixes the flag
(x,Y)
and no other points and lines.

For Desarguesian projective planes, the converse is also true in case of linear automorphisms. For, let
(x,Y)
be a flag in such a plane
\mathcal{P}
, and let
\alpha
be a linear automorphism fixing
x
,
Y
and no other points and lines. Suppose
\alpha
induces the (linear) automorphism
\alpha_{Y}
on
Y
; then it is easy to see that
T(x)
contains an element

\widehat{\alpha}
which induces
\alpha_{1}
. In the same way, if
\alpha_{x}
is the linear automorphism induced by
\alpha
on the lines incident with
x
,
we take an element
\widetilde{\alpha}
in
T(Y)
which induces
\alpha_{x}
on these lines. Now consider
\epsilon:=\alpha(\widehat{\alpha}\widetilde{\alpha})^{-1}
; this is
an automorphism which fixes
x
linewise and
Y
pointwise, so
\epsilon\in T(x)\cap T(Y)\leq\langle T(x),T(Y)\rangle=T(x)T(Y)
. It follows that
\alpha
is also contained
in
T(x)T(Y)
. If
\alpha
is not linear, this might depend on the field over which the plane is defined. Suppose that
\mathcal{P}
is finite and defined over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, with
q=p^{h}
and

p
a prime. Suppose that
(p,h)=1
, so that
p
does not divide
|\texttt{Gal}(\mathbb{F}_{q}/\mathbb{F}_{p})|
. As the order of
\alpha
must be a power of
p
(otherwise we
have more fixed points and fixed lines), and since
[\mathsf{P\Upgamma L}_{3}(q):\mathsf{PGL}_{3}(q)]=h=|\mathsf{Gal}(\mathbb{F}_{q}/\mathbb{F}_{p})|
, it follows that
\alpha
is linear.

3.4.
Infinite case

In the infinite case, we need a different approach. For, suppose that
\gamma
is an automorphism of the infinite projective plane
\mathcal{P}
.
Let
\mathcal{P}=\{x_{i}\ |\ i\in\mathcal{I}\}
be the set of points of the plane, and
\mathcal{L}=\{M_{i}\ |\ i\in\mathcal{I}\}
be the set of lines. Let
A
be an incidence matrix for
\mathcal{P}
(
a_{ij}=1
if and only if
x_{i}
is incident with
M_{j}
, otherwise it is
0
).
Define a matrix
P=(p_{ij})
as follows:
p_{ij}=1
if
x_{i}^{\gamma}=x_{j}
; (otherwise
0
) and define a matrix
L=(l_{ij})
in a similar way:
l_{ij}=1
if
l_{i}^{\gamma}=l
(otherwise
0
). Note that
P
and
L
are infinite permutation matrices. Now consider
(PA)_{ij}=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{I}}p_{ik}a_{kj}=:a_{uj}
, where
u
is uniquely determined by the fact that
p_{iu}=1
:
x_{i}^{\gamma}=x_{u}
. Next, we look at the
(i,j)
-entry
(AL)_{ij}
of
AL
, which is given by
\sum_{k\in\mathcal{I}}a_{ik}l_{kj}=a_{iv}
, where
v
is uniquely determined by the fact that
M_{v}^{\gamma}=M_{j}
. Now
a_{uj}=1
if and only if
x_{u}\mathbf{I}M_{j}
if and only if
x_{u}^{\gamma^{-1}}\mathbf{I}M_{j}^{\gamma^{-1}}
if and only if
x_{i}\mathbf{I}M_{v}
if and only if
a_{iv}=1
, implying that

(5)

PA\ =\ AL.

In the finite case one now easily concludes that
P
and
L
have the same trace, translated into the fact
\gamma
has the same number of fixed points as fixed lines. But in the infinite case this is essentially where the story ends. In the next subsection, we construct counter examples to Theorem
3.3
in the infinite case.

Counter examples

Consider the Fano plane
\Pi
, and let
(x,M)
be an anti-flag. Let
\alpha
be an automorphism of
\Pi
of order
3
which fixes
x
,
M
and no other point or line, so that point-orbits and line-orbits of
\langle\alpha\rangle
which do not contain
x
nor
L
all have size
3
(and note that such automorphisms exist). Now remove
M
and the points incident with
M
(which form one point-orbit) to obtain a point-line configuration
\mathcal{C}
(affine plane of order
2
), and
\alpha
induces an automorphism of
\mathcal{C}
which we denote by
\alpha_{0}
.

We now perform a free construction on
\mathcal{C}
to obtain a countably infinite free projective plane
\overline{\mathcal{C}}
.
Put
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}_{0}
. In step one, we add new points to
\mathcal{C}_{0}
for each pair of nonconcurrent lines in
\mathcal{C}_{0}
to obtain
\mathcal{C}_{1}
. In the next step, we add new lines for each pair of noncollinear points to obtain
\mathcal{C}_{2}
. And so on. The free plane
\overline{\mathcal{C}}
is

(6)

\cup_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{C}_{i}.

In each step, we also construct an automorphism of the newly obtained point-line configuration. This is easy. For instance, one we have constructed
\mathcal{C}_{1}
, the new points naturally form an orbit of size
3
(by looking at the image of the lines) and we obtain
\alpha_{1}
. The same is true for
\mathcal{C}_{2}
, and we obtain
\alpha_{2}
. By induction, we obtain that each
\alpha_{i}
is well defined, and its “union”
\overline{\alpha}
is an automorphism of
\overline{\mathcal{C}}
which fixes the point
x
, and all other points and lines live in orbits of
\langle\overline{\alpha}\rangle
of size
3
. (Note that if the new line
N
is added in stage
\mathcal{C}_{m}
, then it only contains two points which both live in separate orbits of size
3
, so that

cannot be fixed, and it lives in an
\langle\alpha\rangle
-orbit of size
3
.)

So there are no fixed lines.

Note that the automorphism
\overline{\alpha}
cannot be inherited by some affine type projective line automorphism based at

:

Theorem 3.4
.

The automorphism
\overline{\alpha}
defines a nontrivial automorphism of the projective type line
(\overline{\mathcal{C}},x)
which is not inherited by any affine type line automorphism.

Proof
. Suppose there is some line
U
in
\overline{\mathcal{C}}
not incident with
x
, so that there is an automorphism of
\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{U}
(which we see as an automorphism of
\overline{\mathcal{C}}
which fixes
U
) which induces
\overline{\alpha}
on the lines of
\overline{\mathcal{C}}
incident with
x
. Then
\beta:=\overline{\alpha}\gamma^{-1}
is a central collineation of
\overline{\mathcal{C}}
with center
x
. Now let
\Delta
be a triangle with sides
A,B,C
, such that

is not incident with
A,B
and

. Then

define a Desargues configuration

. Now
\mathcal{D}
is a finite point-line configuration with three points per line and three lines per point, so by definition the configuration is

[

, it follows that
\mathcal{D}^{\overline{\alpha}}
is a subgeometry of the begin configuration
\mathcal{C}_{0}
, which is the contradiction we were seeking. So

indeed defines a nontrivial automorphism of the projective type line
(\overline{\mathcal{C}},x)
which is not inherited by any affine type line automorphism in
\overline{\mathcal{C}}
.
\blacksquare

Applied to Question

, Theorem

yields counter examples in the infinite case.

Of course, this method immediately generalizes to the following theorem (using the same notation).

3.5.
Group theoretical discussion

On the abstract group-theoretical level, we pose the following related problem.

Question
3.6
.

Let

be a permutation group. When is

generated by (all) its subgroups

, where

In

generated by all the point-stabilizers

. If

, then clearly

cannot act transitively on

(as it contains point-stabilizers). If

is an

and

act transitively on

, so that

for some

fixes all

-orbits. Conversely, let

be a proper system of imprimitivity upon which

4.
Orbits and lines

Many nonisomorphic synthetic projective lines can arise from one and the same plane
\mathcal{P}
, and the isomorphism classes are obviously determined precisely by the orbits of
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathcal{P})
on the set
\mho(\mathcal{P})
of anti-flags in
\mathcal{P}
. Rigid planes (see also section
8
), for example, yield the maximal number of nonisomorphic projective lines.
Here is the extremal case on the other end of the spectrum.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a finite projective plane. Then all lines
(\mathcal{P}_{U},\omega)
(with
U
a line in
\mathcal{P}
and
\omega
not incident with
\omega
) are isomorphic
if and only if
\mathcal{P}
is Desarguesian.

Proof
. Obviously this is the case if and only if
\mathcal{P}
acts transitively on anti-flags. In that case, it follows from Ostrom and Wagner
[
12
]
(see also Cameron and Kantor
[
2
]
).

\blacksquare

In the infinite case, no such conclusion can hold due to model-theoretic considerations. In
[
20
]
Tent shows the following theorem (which we only state for projective planes, but which she obtains for all generalized polygons). We refer to her paper for the notational use.

Theorem 4.2

(Tent
[
20
]
)
.

There exists a countable projective plane
\Upgamma
which is not isomorphic to
\mathbb{P}^{2}(\dj)
for any division ring
\dj
, which contains all finite (known and unknown) projective planes (up to isomorphism).
Its automorphism group
\mathrm{Aut}(\Upgamma)
acts transitively on all finitely generated
\mathcal{L}_{3}
-structures (in
\Upgamma
) of given isomorphism type. In particular,

(a)

\mathrm{Aut}(\Upgamma)
is a group with a BN-pair;

(b)

\mathrm{Aut}(\Upgamma)
acts highly transitively on (all lines incident with) every point, and on (all point incident with) every line;

(c)

if
\upgamma=(x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{6}=x_{0})
is a
6
-cycle in the incidence graph of
\Upgamma
, then the elementwise stabilizer of
\upgamma
acts highly transitively on
\Upgamma_{1}(x_{1})\setminus\{x_{0},x_{2}\}
(and obviously
x_{1}
is arbitrary in this context).

Here,
\Upgamma_{1}(x_{1})
denotes the set of vertices adjacent to (and different from)
x_{1}
(in the incidence graph).

The fact that
\mathrm{Aut}(\Upgamma)
is a group with a BN-pair translates into the fact that its acts transitively on the ordered triangles in
\Upgamma
. As a by-product, it acts
transitively on the anti-flags, so that
\Upgamma
is a nonclassical projective plane for which all projective lines
(\Upgamma^{U},\omega)
(where
(\omega,U)
is any anti-flag in

\mathcal{A}=\Upgamma^{U}
is the affine plane obtained by deleting the line
U
.

Projective planes
\mathcal{P}
in which all projective-type projective lines
(\mathcal{P},\omega)
are isomorphic are precisely those planes with a point-transitive automorphism group. In the finite case, there are several long-standing conjectures which imply that such planes are Desarguesian. In the infinite case such results cannot be true, as we have seen in Theorem
4.2
.

Note that if we fix some line
U
in a projective plane
\mathcal{P}
, the isomorphism class of
(\mathcal{P},\omega)
, with
\omega
a point not on
U
solely depends on the point-orbits of
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathcal{P})_{U}
. So one line in the plane
\mathcal{P}
can define many nonisomorphic affine-type projective lines. In general, this relative viewpoint is very different than in the case of
“classical” projective lines.

6.
Extensions and embeddings

Let
(A,\omega)
be a synthetic projective line. We say that
(A^{\prime},\omega^{\prime})
is an

of
(A,\omega)
, or that

is a

of

if there is an injective morphism

\rho:\ A\ \mapsto A^{\prime}

such that
\rho(\omega)=\omega^{\prime}
. Since

, we can see

as a subplane of

. Note that there is no reason to think that
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathcal{A},\omega)
is a subgroup
of
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathcal{A}^{\prime},\omega^{\prime})

is a Desarguesian plane over

and
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}

, we indeed have a natural inclusion of subgroups.

6.1.
Desarguesian sublines

In the classical context, the notion of subline coincides with a line over a subfield.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
(A,\omega)
be a subline of
(A^{\prime},\omega^{\prime})
, where
A^{\prime}=\mathbb{A}^{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
is a Desarguesian affine plane over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Then
U
(line of infinity of
\rho(A)
) is a line
defined over a subfield of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, that is, a projective of
U^{\prime}
(line of infinity of
A^{\prime}
) in the classical sense.

Proof
. Let

\rho:\ A\ \mapsto A^{\prime}

be an injective morphism such that
\rho(\omega)=\omega^{\prime}
. We have that
\rho(A)
is a subplane of
A^{\prime}\cong\mathbb{A}^{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
, so
\rho(A)^{U}
is a projective subplane of

{A^{\prime}}^{U^{\prime}}\cong\mathbb{P}^{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
. It follows immediately that
\rho(A)^{U}\cong\mathbb{P}^{2}(\ell)
with
\ell
some subfield of

, and the statement follows.
\blacksquare

Note that in the infinite case, we can easily “self-embed” projective lines. Here is an example.

\mathbb{P}^{2}(\ell)
,

Geometric closure

Call a line
(\mathcal{A},\omega)

finite
if
\mathcal{A}
is finite. Call an affine plane
\mathcal{A}

if its collections of points and lines are sets; otherwise

Let
(\mathcal{A},\omega)
be a set-like synthetic projective line, and let

be the category of all set-like synthetic projective lines which contain

. (Note that all the planes have the same line at infinity.)
In a natural way we can make

into a direct system (by taking natural embeddings as connection morphisms). If
(\mathcal{B},\omega)
and
(\mathcal{C},\omega)
are elements of

We call

Note that if

\overline{(\mathcal{A},\omega)}\ =\ \overline{(\,B,\omega)},

showing a striking similarity with the notion of algebraic closure. Also note that

has the same line at infinity as

.

However, geometric closures of lines of type

are obviously much “larger” than lines of type
\mathbb{P}^{2}(\overline{k})
, where
\overline{k}
is an algebraic closure of

as follows:

•

•

is not finite, we only consider extensions

have the same order.

Even in the modified variation, if we would start from

\overline{(\mathbb{A}^{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q}),\omega)}\ \not\cong\ (\mathbb{A}^{2}(\overline{\mathbb{F}_{q}}),\omega).

If

is a finite sharply
2
-transitive permutation group, then
|X|
is a prime power
q
, and either
H
acts as a one-point-stabilizer in
\mathbf{P\Upgamma L}_{2}(q)
in its natural action on the projective line
\mathbb{P}^{1}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
, or
q\in\{5^{2},7^{2},11^{2},23^{2},29^{2}\}
.

It follows that if
(H,X)
is a finite (faithful) sharply
2
-transitive permutation group, then
|X|
is a prime power, so if
(\mathcal{A},\omega)
is a finite projective line with a sharply
2
-transitive automorphism group, where
\mathcal{A}
has order
N
, then
N+1=p^{m}
for some prime
p
and positive integer
m
. If the prime power conjecture would be true, we end up with couples
(N,N+1)
where both entries are prime powers. It is well known that for such couples either
N
or
N+1
are powers of
2
, and if
(N,N+1)\neq(8,9)
, the other one is a prime number. In the infinite case there is much more elbow room.

The general linear groups

Obviously, the groups
\mathsf{P\Gamma L}_{2}(\dj)
, with
\dj
a division ring, are realizable.

Highly transitive lines

Let
\Upgamma
be the plane constructed by Tent as in Theorem
4.2
. Then for any point
\omega
, we have that
\mathrm{Aut}(\Upgamma,\omega)
acts highly transitively on the points of
(\Upgamma,\omega)
. Now let
U
be any line of
\Upgamma
, and
v
any point not incident with
U
. As
\mathrm{Aut}(\Upgamma)
is a group with a BN-pair, it follows that
\mathrm{Aut}(\Upgamma)_{(U,v)}
acts
2
-transitively on the lines incident with
v
. Let
\mathcal{A}
be the affine plane obtained by deleting
U
from
\Upgamma
. Then by Theorem

(c), it follows that
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathcal{A},v)
acts highly transitively on the points of
(\mathcal{A},v)
.

We now pass to the other side of the spectrum.

8.1.
Rigid lines

Call a projective line
(\mathcal{A},\omega)

rigid
if its automorphism group is the trivial group. This immediately translates into the fact that
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathcal{A})_{\omega}
is a subgroup of the group of homologies of
\mathcal{A}
with center
\omega
(and axis the line at infinity). Although it is a wide-open question whether finite rigid projective planes exist, we will show that as a direct corollary of known results, finite (and infinite) rigid affine and projective type lines exist. Especially in the finite case, this is particularly interesting.

8.2.
Kantor’s translation planes

In
[
9
]
, Kantor constructed infinite classes of finite translation planes
(\pi,U,T)
of which the full automorphism group
\mathrm{Aut}(\pi)
coincides with the translation group
T
(and hence have kernel isomorphic to
\mathbb{F}_{2}
). More precisely, we have such planes for each prime power
q=2^{h}
with
h
odd, composite and
h\geq 3
.
For each point
\omega
in
\pi\setminus T
,
(\pi_{U},\omega)
defines a rigid projective line. The line
U
itself defines a projective type projective line (it is the only line in
\pi
with this property).

The following problem might be interesting:

Problem 8.3
.

Study affine planes
\mathcal{A}
(with line at infinity
U
) which yield a non-rigid projective type line
U
, but for every point
\omega
a rigid projective line
(\mathcal{A},\omega)
.

This translates into the fact that
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathcal{A})
is nontrivial and does not fix every parallel class, but if
\alpha\in\mathrm{Aut}(\mathcal{A})
fixes an affine point, then all parallel classes
are
fixed.

8.3.
Rigid planes, symmetry breaking and AB-sets

Obviously it is much harder to produce rigid projective (and affine) planes than rigid lines. Note that a rigid projective plane yields a rigid affine plane after deleting any line;
the projective completion of a rigid affine plane is not necessarily rigid though (the Kantor planes of the previous section are finite examples).
In this subsection we have a closer look at an easy construction of infinite rigid projective planes through so-called “AB-sets.”

It is an open question as to whether finite rigid projective planes exist. In the infinite case, things are very different: Mendelsohn
[
11
]
showed that for every group
G
, there is a projective plane
\mathcal{P}
such that its full automorphism group is isomorphic to
G
. The case
G=\{\mathrm{id}\}
yields rigid projective planes, and hence for every anti-flag
(a,A)
in such a plane
\mathcal{P}
, we have that
(\mathcal{P}_{A},a)
, respectively
(\mathcal{P},A)
, is a rigid affine type, respectively projective type, projective line.

It is not hard to construct infinite rigid projective planes, and so we will describe a procedure with which one can do this.

•

Start with a point-line geometry
\Upgamma
which does not violate the axioms of a projective plane. (In this context, it will be most convenient to have
\Upgamma
finite.)

•

Leave away a point-line subconfiguration
\mathcal{C}
of
\Upgamma
such that the new geometry
\Upgamma\setminus\mathcal{C}=:\mathcal{C}_{0}
is confined and rigid.

•

Free complete
\mathcal{C}_{0}
to obtain a rigid projective plane
\overline{\mathcal{C}_{0}}
.

Why is the plane
\overline{\mathcal{C}_{0}}
rigid? First note that if an automorphism of
\overline{\mathcal{C}_{0}}
fixes
\mathcal{C}_{0}
elementwise, then by its very definition,
\overline{\mathcal{C}_{0}}
is fixed elementwise by the completion of
\alpha
. Now let
\gamma
be an automorphism of
\overline{\mathcal{C}_{0}}
; then
\mathcal{C}_{0}^{\gamma}
is also confined, so by
[
3
]
we must have
\mathcal{C}_{0}^{\gamma}\leq\mathcal{C}_{0}
. As
\mathcal{C}_{0}
is finite by definition, it follows that
\gamma
induces an automorphism, hence the identity, on
\mathcal{C}_{0}
.

We propose to define
automorphism-blocking sets
(“AB-sets”) as follows. With
\Upgamma
a point-line geometry, we say that
\mathcal{C}
is an
automorphism-blocking set
of
\Upgamma
(or that
\mathcal{C}

blocks the automorphisms
of
\Upgamma
, or that it
breaks the symmetry
of
\Upgamma
), if the following conditions are satisfied:

AB1

every automorphism of
\Upgamma\setminus\mathcal{C}
extends to an automorphism of
\Upgamma
;

AB2

the elementwise stabilizer
\mathrm{Aut}(\Upgamma)_{[\mathcal{C}]}
of
\mathcal{C}
in
\mathrm{Aut}(\Upgamma)
is trivial;

AB3

\mathrm{Aut}(\Upgamma)_{\mathcal{C}}=\mathrm{Aut}(\Upgamma)_{[\mathcal{C}]}
.

Note that (AB1) is very natural: many interesting subgeometries of many point-line geometries meet this property.
Famous example
: let
\mathcal{C}
be a line plus
all its points in any projective plane
\Upgamma
: then all automorphisms of the affine plane
\Upgamma\setminus\mathcal{C}
extend uniquely to automorphisms of
the projective completion
\Upgamma
. Clearly AB-sets survive the procedure described above if the complements are confined: let
\beta
be an automorphism of
\Upgamma\setminus\mathcal{C}
; then by (AB1) it extends to an automorphism
\overline{\gamma}
of
\Upgamma
. Since
\overline{\gamma}
stabilizes
\Upgamma\setminus\mathcal{C}
, it also stabilizes
\mathcal{C}
, so by (AB3), it fixes
\mathcal{C}
elementwise. By (AB2),
\overline{\gamma}
is trivial, so
\gamma
also is. So what we want to construct infinite rigid projective planes, is AB-sets for which the complement is confined.

Remark 8.4
.

We strongly suspect that (general, minimal and maximal) AB-sets (in various types of geometries) are interesting in their own right to study. Note that they are subgeometries which see the geometry of the ambient geometry, but
don’t see its symmetry
.

8.4.
Examples

There are may interesting AB-sets (with confined complement), starting from various interesting geometries. We provide some classes of examples, starting from Kantor’s planes (cf. subsection
8.2
).

In Kantor planes

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a projective Kantor plane of order
N=2^{n}
with translation line
U
. Let
\mathcal{C}=(\{u\},\emptyset,\mathbf{I})
be the subgeometry defined
by the point
u
which is not incident with
U
. Then obviously all automorphisms of
\mathcal{P}\setminus\{u\}
extend to automorphisms of
\mathcal{P}
, but
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathcal{P})_{\{u\}}=\{\mathrm{id}\}
since
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathcal{P})
acts sharply transitively on the points not incident with
U
. Since
\mathcal{P}\setminus\mathcal{C}
is confined,
it follows that the free completion of
\mathcal{P}\setminus\mathcal{C}
is a rigid projective plane. Many other AB-sets can obviously be found in Kantor planes.

Note that in general (say, in geometries with more symmetry), AB-sets usually are much more complicated to find, and will need a good understanding of the fixed elements structures of elements in automorphism groups.

From rigid ovals

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a finite projective plane of order
N\geq 3
, and let
\mathcal{O}
be a rigid oval in
\mathcal{P}
(a set of
N+1
points no three of which are collinear, for which
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathcal{P})_{\mathcal{O}}
induces the identity on
\mathcal{O}
). Then
\mathcal{O}
is an AB-set in
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{P}\setminus\mathcal{O}
is confined. For, let
\gamma
be an automorphism of
\mathcal{P}\setminus\mathcal{O}
. We only have to show that
\alpha
naturally defines an action on
\mathcal{O}
. So let
U,V
be two different lines which meet in a point
x\in\mathcal{O}
; if
U^{\gamma}
and
V^{\gamma}
do not meet in a point of
\mathcal{O}
, then

U^{\gamma}\cap V^{\gamma}=\{v\}
with
v
a point in
\mathcal{P}\setminus\mathcal{O}
. But then
v^{\gamma^{-1}}
must be a point of
U\cap V
, while it is not a point of
\mathcal{O}
, contradiction.
It follows that the action on
\mathcal{O}
is well defined, and so
\gamma
extends to an automorphism
\overline{\gamma}
of
\mathcal{P}
. Finally, let
\alpha\in\mathrm{Aut}(\mathcal{P})_{\mathcal{O}}
. As
\mathcal{O}
is rigid, it fixes every point of
\mathcal{O}
. Now let
u
be a point of
\mathcal{P}\setminus\mathcal{O}
, and let
U,V
be two different lines on
u
which meet
\mathcal{O}
. If
U
or
V
is a tangent line
of
\mathcal{O}
at a point
w
of
\mathcal{O}
, then obviously it is fixed (tangent lines are unique); if
U
or
V
meet
\mathcal{O}
in distinct points, then obviously it is also fixed. So
u
is also fixed, and
\alpha
is the identity.
It follows that
\mathcal{O}
is an AB-set.

The principle used here is very interesting: starting from a simple rigid structure, one constructs a more complex one. Of course, rigid ovals form just one set of rigid objects which yield AB-sets and hence rigid planes. One could also have started from rigid geometric hyperplanes in finite generalized quadrangles and use Pralle’s theory of affine quadrangles
[
16
]
to show that these form AB-sets.

in
\Upgamma_{1}
