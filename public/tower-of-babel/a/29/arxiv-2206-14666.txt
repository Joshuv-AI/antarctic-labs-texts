Conditionally Elicitable Dynamic Risk Measures for Deep Reinforcement Learning Thanks: August 8, 2026.
Anthony Coache, Sebastian Jaimungal, Álvaro Cartea

Abstract
We propose a novel framework to solve risk-sensitive reinforcement learning (RL) problems where the agent optimises time-consistent dynamic spectral risk measures. Based on the notion of conditional elicitability, our methodology constructs (strictly consistent) scoring functions that are used as penalizers in the estimation procedure. Our contribution is threefold: we (i) devise an efficient approach to estimate a class of dynamic spectral risk measures with deep neural networks, (ii) prove that these dynamic spectral risk measures may be approximated to any arbitrary accuracy using deep neural networks, and (iii) develop a risk-sensitive actor-critic algorithm that uses full episodes and does not require any additional nested transitions. We compare our conceptually improved reinforcement learning algorithm with the nested simulation approach and illustrate its performance in two settings: statistical arbitrage and portfolio allocation on both simulated and real data.

1
Introduction

One principled model-free framework for learning-based control is reinforcement learning (RL)
[
60
]
.
In RL, the agent observes feedback in the forms of costs from interactions with an environment, uses this information to update its current behaviour, and aims to discover the best course of action based on a certain objective.
In recent years, deep learning – i.e. relying on neural network structures to approximate complex functions – has shown remarkable success in RL applications, ranging from mastering Atari 2600 video games
[
44
]
to developing autonomous image-learning robots
[
38
]
and defeating world champions Go players
[
58
]
.
It also has become an appealing alternative in several financial decision making problems, where one wishes to learn optimal strategies with no explicit assumptions about the environment.
For a thorough survey of recent advances in RL applied to financial problems, see e.g.
[
25
,
29
,
27
]
.

In RL, the agent’s optimisation problem must take into account the additional randomness due to the uncertainty in the environment.
One can think of, for instance, a trader concerned by the risks associated with financial assets, an autonomous vehicle which must pay attention to weather and road conditions, or a medical worker whose actions and treatments impact the life of its patients.
In most (if not all) real-life applications, there exists inherent uncertainty in the environment, and the agent must adapt its strategy to avoid potentially catastrophic consequences.
For an overview and outlook of safety concerns in RL algorithms, see e.g.
[
22
]
.

There are numerous proposals that accounts for risk sensitivity in the literature, often called risk-aware or risk-sensitive RL frameworks (see e.g.
[
14
,
45
,
31
,
5
,
30
]
).
Risk-aware RL proposes to quantify risk of random costs through risk measures (instead of the usual expectation) to account for environmental uncertainties.
This risk-awareness provides robustness to low-probability but high-cost outcomes in the environment, and allows more flexibility than traditional approaches because the agent may choose the risk measure considering its goals and risk tolerances.

In the extant literature, several authors address risk evaluation for sequential decision making problems by applying risk measures recursively to a sequence of cost random variables, and by optimising the risk in a time-consistent and model-free dynamic framework as additional information becomes available.
For instance,
[
53
]
evaluates the risk at each period using
dynamic Markov coherent risk measures
,
[
8
]
and
[
4
]
propose iterated coherent risk measures, where they both derive risk-aware
dynamic programming (DP) equations
and provide
policy iteration algorithms
,
[
2
]
investigate bounded policy iteration algorithms for
partially observable Markov decision processes
,
[
35
]
prove the convergence of
temporal difference algorithms
optimising dynamic Markov coherent risk measures, and
[
7
]
derive a DP principle for
Kusuoka-type conditional risk mappings
.
However, these works require computing the value function for every possible state of the environment, limiting their applicability to problems with a small number of state-action pairs.
In contrast, here we aim to develop computational approaches to optimise RL problems that allows for both continuous or discrete states and actions.

A recent development in risk-aware RL is that in
[
9
]
.
The authors use
dynamic convex risk measures
and devise a model-free approach to solve
finite-horizon
RL problems in a
time-consistent manner
.
This extends the work from
[
63
]
that studies optimal stationary policies under dynamic coherent risk measures.
They also demonstrate the performance and flexibility of their approach on several benchmark examples, which, by generating strategies that mitigate risk and not simply maximising expectation, effectively accounts for uncertainty in the data-generating processes.
In both works, one downside of the proposed actor-critic algorithms is the use of a
nested simulation
or
simulation upon simulation
approach.
Such nested simulation approaches, where one generates (outer) episodes and (inner) transitions for every visited state, are computationally expensive, as it requires a large number of simulations to obtain accurate results.
In many real-world applications, simulations are costly – the acquisition of new observations may not be possible, for instance in trading markets and medical trials – making this methodology highly ineffective (in terms of memory and speed) or even impracticable.
One of our motivations is to develop a novel framework which circumvents simulating additional transitions.

If we restrict the broad class of convex risk measures to a narrower class of risk mappings that are
elicitable
, one can achieve significant improvements in the estimation of the risk.
Although the term “elicitability” was established recently by
[
37
]
, the general idea originates from the seminal work of
[
46
]
.
Elicitable (and conditionally elicitable) statistical functions have a corresponding loss function, often called
scoring function
, that can be used as a penalizer when updating its point estimate – a common example is the expectation which can be estimated using the mean squared error.
In the RL literature,
[
57
]
suggest a Q-learning algorithm for a class of elicitable mappings, i.e.
utility-based shortfall risk measures
, in case of tabular Markovian decision processes, while
[
41
]
propose a modification for
dynamic expectile risk measures
and continuous spaces.
Recently,
[
19
]
provided characterisations of those scoring functions for many risk measures, including a class of static spectral risk measures.
To the best of our knowledge, there is no work that explores the notion of (strictly consistent) scoring functions for a large class of conditionally elicitable risk measures in a dynamic framework.

In this paper, we develop a practical and efficient framework to estimate dynamic spectral risk measures, which allows us to devise a conceptually improved methodology for actor-critic algorithms in risk-aware RL problems.
Our contributions are:
(i) devise a composite model regression framework using artificial neural networks (ANNs) to estimate a class of elicitable dynamic spectral risk measures;
(ii) prove that our estimation method can approximate the dynamic risk to an arbitrary accuracy given sufficiently large neural network structures;
(iii) implement a risk-aware actor-critic algorithm using our proposed framework that exclusively uses full (outer) episodes, i.e. does not require additional (inner) transitions; and
(iv) validate our approach on a benchmark example, and illustrate its performance on a portfolio allocation problem using both simulated and real data.

2
Dynamic Risk Setting

In this section, we provide a brief overview of dynamic risk measures, which, as additional information becomes available, assess the risk of sequences of random variables, such as cash-flows, in a dynamic framework.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space.
A
static risk measure
is a mapping
\rho:\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow\bar{\mathbb{R}}
, quantifying the risk of a certain random variable
Y\in\mathcal{Y}
, that satisfies additional assumptions.
In what follows, we assume that
\mathcal{Y}
is the space of bounded
p
-integrable
\mathcal{F}
-measurable random variables, with
p\in[1,\infty]
, and all random variables are interpreted as random costs.
Next, we introduce some well-known risk measures commonly used in the literature.

Definition 1
.

The
value-at-risk

[
3
]
with threshold
\alpha\in(0,1)
of
Y
, denoted
\text{VaR}_{\alpha}(Y)
, is given by the
\alpha
-quantile of the distribution of
Y
.

Definition 2
.

The
conditional value-at-risk

[
51
]
with threshold
\alpha\in(0,1)
of
Y
is given by

\text{CVaR}_{\alpha}(Y)=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\int_{\alpha}^{1}\text{VaR}_{u}(Y)\,\mathrm{d}u.

(1)

Definition 3
.

A
spectral risk measure

[
36
]
of the random variable of
Y
, denoted
\rho^{\varphi}
, is defined as

\rho^{\varphi}(Y)=\int_{[0,1]}\text{CVaR}_{\alpha}(Y)\,\varphi(\mathrm{d}\alpha),

(2)

where
\varphi
is a nonnegative, nonincreasing measure such that
\int_{[0,1]}\varphi(\mathrm{d}\alpha)=1
(also known as the spectrum).

Spectral risk measures satisfy several properties shared with other risk measures in the literature.
We list some properties in the following proposition.

Proposition 4
.

Let
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
,
\beta>0
and
Y,Z\in\mathcal{Y}
. A spectral risk measure
\rho^{\varphi}
is said to be

1.

monotone
, i.e.
Y\leq Z
implies
\rho^{\varphi}(Y)\leq\rho^{\varphi}(Z)
;

2.

translation invariant
, i.e.
\rho^{\varphi}(Y+\gamma)=\rho^{\varphi}(Y)+\gamma
;

3.

positive homogeneous
, i.e.
\rho^{\varphi}(\beta Y)=\beta\,\rho^{\varphi}(Y)
; and

4.

subadditive
, i.e.
\rho^{\varphi}(Y+Z)\leq\rho^{\varphi}(Y)+\rho^{\varphi}(Z)
.

Any risk measure satisfying these four properties is said to be
coherent

[
3
]
.
Spectral risk measures are coherent, while the converse does not always hold.

Employing static risk measures as an objective function in sequential decision making problems does not account for the temporal structure of intermediate costs that generates the terminal costs, and moreover leads to time-inconsistent solutions – we give an illustrative example below.
Therefore, to properly monitor random variables at different times, we must adapt risk assessment to a dynamic framework.
Dynamic risk measures have the advantage of being time-consistent, a property that has a general appeal especially in finance applications, so optimising them leads to time-consistent solutions.

Various classes of risk measures have been extended to the dynamic case, such as coherent risk measures
[
50
]
, distribution-invariant risk measures
[
66
]
, convex risk measures
[
21
]
, and dynamic assessment indices
[
6
]
, among others.
There also exists an equivalence between backward stochastic differential equations (BSDEs) and dynamic risk measures (see e.g.
[
47
,
10
]
), which is exploited by some authors to compute dynamic risk measures via a deep BSDE method (see e.g.
[
26
]
).
However, the dual representation from BSDEs does not directly help to optimise a dynamic risk measure over policies in a model-agnostic manner.
Here, we closely follow the work of
[
53
]

which derives a recursive equation for dynamic risk from general principles.
For a thorough exploration of dynamic risk measures, see e.g.
[
1
]
.

Let
\mathcal{T}:=\{0,\ldots,T\}
denote a sequence of periods.
Consider a filtration
\mathcal{F}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{1}\subseteq\ldots\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{T}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
on a filtered probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\in\mathcal{T}},\mathbb{P})
, with
\mathcal{F}_{0}=\{\emptyset,\Omega\}
being the trivial
\sigma
-algebra, and
(\mathcal{Y}_{t})_{t\in\mathcal{T}}
the spaces of bounded
p
-integrable
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-measurable random variables.
Define
\mathcal{Y}_{t_{1},t_{2}}:=\mathcal{Y}_{t_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{Y}_{t_{2}}
and
\rho_{t_{1},t_{2}}:\mathcal{Y}_{t_{1},t_{2}}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}_{t_{1}}
.

Definition 5
.

A
dynamic risk measure

[
53
]
is a sequence
\{\rho_{t,T}\}_{t\in\mathcal{T}}
, where each
\rho_{t,T}
satisfies the monotonicity property
\rho_{t,T}(Y)\leq\rho_{t,T}(Z)
for all
Y,Z\in\mathcal{Y}_{t,T}
such that
Y\leq Z
.

Here, inequalities are taken almost surely, and inequalities between sequences of costs are to be understood component-wise, i.e.
Y_{\tau}\leq Z_{\tau}
for all
t\leq\tau\leq T
.
The mappings
\rho_{t,T}
, often referred to as
conditional risk measures
, can be interpreted as
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-measurable charges one would be willing to incur at time
t
instead of the sequence of future costs.
A crucial property of dynamic risk measures relies on the notion of
time-consistency
.
Indeed, one wishes to evaluate the risk of future outcomes, but it must not lead to inconsistencies at different points in time.

Definition 6
.

A dynamic risk measure
\{\rho_{t,T}\}_{t\in\mathcal{T}}
is said to be
time-consistent

[
53
]
iff for any sequence
Y,Z\in\mathcal{Y}_{t_{1},T}
and any
t_{1},t_{2}\in\mathcal{T}
such that
0\leq t_{1}<t_{2}\leq T
,

Y_{\tau}=Z_{\tau},\,\forall\tau=t_{1},\ldots,t_{2}-1\quad\text{and}\quad\rho_{t_{2},T}(Y_{t_{2}},\ldots,Y_{T})\leq\rho_{t_{2},T}(Z_{t_{2}},\ldots,Z_{T})

implies that
\rho_{t_{1},T}(Y_{t_{1}},\ldots,Y_{T})\leq\rho_{t_{1},T}(Z_{t_{1}},\ldots,Z_{T})
.

definition

6
may be interpreted as follows: if
Y
will be at least as good as
Z
(in terms of the dynamic risk
\rho_{t,T}
) at time
t_{2}
and they are identical between
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
, then
Y
should not be worse than
Z
(in terms of
\rho_{t,T}
) at time
t_{1}
.
A key result to derive a recursive relationship for time-consistent dynamic risk measures is the following characterisation (see Theorem 1 of
[
53
]
).

Theorem 7
.

Let
\{\rho_{t,T}\}_{t\in\mathcal{T}}
be a dynamic risk measure satisfying
\rho_{t,T}(Y_{t},Y_{t+1},\ldots,Y_{T})=Y_{t}+\rho_{t,T}(0,Y_{t+1},\ldots,Y_{T})
, and
\rho_{t,T}(0,\ldots,0)=0
, for any
Y\in\mathcal{Y}_{t,T},\ t\in\mathcal{T}
.
Then
\{\rho_{t,T}\}_{t\in\mathcal{T}}
is time-consistent iff for any
0\leq t_{1}\leq t_{2}\leq T
and
Y\in\mathcal{Y}_{0,T}
, we have

\rho_{t_{1},T}(Y_{t_{1}},\ldots,Y_{T})=\rho_{t_{1},t_{2}}\Big(Y_{t_{1}},\ldots,Y_{t_{2}-1},\rho_{t_{2},T}\big(Y_{t_{2}},\ldots,Y_{T}\big)\Big).

A time-consistent dynamic risk measure at time
t_{1}
can be seen to include the sequence of costs up to a certain time
t_{2}
, and the
\mathcal{F}_{t_{2}}
-measurable charge for the remaining costs.
As a consequence of
theorem

7
, for any
t\in\mathcal{T}
, we have the recursive relationship

\rho_{t,T}(Y_{t},\ldots,Y_{T})=Y_{t}+\rho_{t}\Bigg(Y_{t+1}+\rho_{t+1}\bigg(Y_{t+2}+\cdots+\rho_{T-2}\Big(Y_{T-1}+\rho_{T-1}\big(Y_{T}\big)\Big)\cdots\bigg)\Bigg),

(3)

where the
one-step conditional risk measures

\rho_{t}:\mathcal{Y}_{t+1}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}_{t}
satisfy
\rho_{t}(Y)=\rho_{t,t+1}(0,Y)
for any
Y\in\mathcal{Y}_{t+1}
.

Instead of assuming that the one-step conditional risk measures
\rho_{t}
are convex (see e.g.
[
9
]
) or coherent (see e.g.
[
53
,
63
]
), we impose stronger properties to focus on a narrower class of risk measures, so that we can develop more efficient learning methodologies that do not require nested simulations.
In what follows, we assume that the one-step conditional risk measures
\rho_{t}
are static spectral risk measures as in
definition

3
.
Furthermore, we define the one-step conditional spectral risk measure, with spectrum
\varphi
, for any
Y\in\mathcal{Y}_{t+1}
as
\rho_{t}^{\varphi}(Y|\mathcal{F}_{t})
, which outputs an
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-measurable random variable obtained by conditioning on
\mathcal{F}_{t}
.

A well-known issue with static risk measures is their time-inconsistency, i.e. an optimal behaviour planned for a future state of the environment when optimising a static risk measure may not be optimal anymore once the agent visits the state.
We provide a motivational example to illustrate the consequences of using static risk measures in sequential decision making problems.

Suppose we face the two-period optimisation problem shown in
fig.

1
with transition probabilities indicated on each branch, where we start at
s_{0}
, choose between moving up or down at each period, and obtain a terminal cost
Y
.
If we aim to minimise the static
CVaR
with threshold
\alpha=0.9
of the terminal cost, the optimal actions are to move up and then down, which lead to

\displaystyle\text{CVaR}_{0.9}(Y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-0.9}\int_{0.9}^{1}\text{VaR}_{u}(Y)\,\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{0.1}\Big(2\times 0.01-1\times 0.09\Big)=-0.7,

while the second contender is to move up twice with a
CVaR
of
0
.
The time-consistency issue arises if we land at
s_{1}^{\text{up}^{\prime}}
, because the
CVaR
with threshold
\alpha=0.9
of moving up (resp. down) is now
0
(resp.
2
).
In this scenario, the optimal action at
s_{1}^{\text{up}^{\prime}}
is to move up, which contradicts the initial optimal strategy starting from
s_{0}
.
If we instead aim to minimise the dynamic
CVaR
(i.e. one-step conditional risk measures are static
CVaR
s at threshold
\alpha
), it is clear that the optimal actions are to move up twice; in fact, these are the optimal actions for any
\alpha\in[0.7,1)
.

To overcome this issue with static risk measures, one must solve optimisation problems for every possible state of the environment, which is highly ineffective when dealing with sequential decision making problems of several periods with a large number of states.
This motivates the need to consider dynamic risk measures.

3
Reinforcement Learning

In this section, we introduce the RL problems we study, and give the motivation behind our work.
We describe each problem as an
agent
who tries to learn an optimal behavior, or
agent’s policy
, by interacting with a certain
environment
in a model-agnostic manner.

Let
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{A}
be arbitrary state and action spaces respectively, and let
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathbb{R}
be a cost space.
The environment is often represented as a
Markov decision process
(MDP) with the tuple
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},c,\mathbb{P})
, where
c(s,a,s^{\prime})\in\mathcal{C}
is a cost function and
\mathbb{P}
characterises the transition probabilities
\mathbb{P}(s_{t+1}=s^{\prime}\;|\;s_{t}=s,a_{t}=a)
.
The transition probability is assumed stationary, although time may be a component of the state.
In what follows, we assume that time is embedded in the state space, which can be interpreted as an augmented state space where the first dimension corresponds to the time.
An
episode
consists of a sequence of
T+1

periods
, denoted
\mathcal{T}:=\{0,\ldots,T\}
, where
T\in\mathbb{N}
is known and finite.
At each period
t\in\mathcal{T}
, the agent begins in a state
s_{t}\in\mathcal{S}
and takes an action
a_{t}\in\mathcal{A}
according to a randomised time-dependent
policy

\pi^{\theta}:\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{A})
parameterised by some
\theta
.
Here,
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{A})
represents the space of probability measures on a certain
\sigma
-algebra generated by the action space
\mathcal{A}
, and therefore the agent selects the action
a
with probability
\pi^{\theta}(a|s_{t}=s)
when being in state
s
at time
t
.
The agent then moves to the next state
s_{t+1}\in\mathcal{S}
, and receives a cost
c_{t}=c(s_{t},a_{t},s_{t+1})<\infty
.
We view the cost function as a deterministic mapping of the states and actions, but we can easily generalise to include other sources of randomness.

We consider time-consistent, Markov, dynamic risk measures
\{\rho_{t,T}\}_{t\in\mathcal{T}}
where we assume the one-step conditional risk measures
\rho_{t}
are one-step conditional spectral risk measures.
Using
eq.

3
, we aim to solve
(T+1)
-period risk-sensitive RL problems of the form

\min_{\theta}\,\rho_{0,T}\Big(\{c^{\theta}_{t}\}_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\Big)=\min_{\theta}\,\rho_{0}\Bigg(c^{\theta}_{0}+\rho_{1}\bigg(c^{\theta}_{1}+\cdots+\rho_{T-1}\Big(c^{\theta}_{T-1}+\rho_{T}\big(c^{\theta}_{T}\big)\Big)\cdots\bigg)\Bigg),

(P)

where
c^{\theta}_{t}=c(s_{t}^{\theta},a^{\theta}_{t},s_{t+1}^{\theta})
is a bounded
\mathcal{F}_{t+1}
-measurable random cost modulated by the policy
\pi^{\theta}
– that is why we include a
\theta
index. Note that the policy dependence on
s_{t}^{\theta}
holds for all periods except the initial state at
t=0
. One might incorporate a deterministic discount factor in
eq.

P
, which can be moved in and out of the risk measures
\rho_{t}
due to the positive homogeneity property.
It differs from: (i) standard risk-neutral RL, which typically deals with an expectation of the cumulative (discounted) costs as the objective function; and (ii) risk-aware RL with static risk measures, which provides optimal precommitment strategies.

Next, we derive dynamic programming (DP) equations for the class of problems of the form
eq.

P
.
We define the
value function
as the running risk-to-go

V_{t}(s;\theta):=\rho_{t}\bigg(c_{t}^{\theta}+\rho_{t+1}\Big(c_{t+1}^{\theta}+\dots+\rho_{T}\big(c_{T}^{\theta}\big)\Big)\biggm|s_{t}=s\bigg),

(4)

for all
s\in\mathcal{S}
and
t\in\mathcal{T}
.
The value function outputs the dynamic spectral risk at a certain time
t
when being in a specific state
s
and behaving according to the policy
\pi^{\theta}
; it represents the performance criterion that the agent seeks to minimise.
Using
eq.

4
, the DP equations for a specific policy
\pi^{\theta}
are

\displaystyle V_{T}(s;\theta)

\displaystyle=\rho_{T}\Big(c_{T}^{\theta}\Bigm|s_{T}=s\Big),\quad\text{and}

(5a)

\displaystyle V_{t}(s;\theta)

\displaystyle=\rho_{t}\Big(c_{t}^{\theta}+V_{t+1}(s_{t+1}^{\theta};\theta)\Bigm|s_{t}=s\Big).

(5b)

The DP equations for the value function allow the agent to evaluate the risk of a fixed policy
\pi^{\theta}
recursively.

One common approach for solving RL problems of the form
eq.

P
is to optimise the value function over policies.
It then requires an accurate estimation of the value function in
eqs.

5a
and
5b
at every period and state of the environment, which is not straightforward at first sight.
A proposed methodology in the class of actor-critic algorithms consists of approximating the value function at a certain state by simulating several transitions, which we refer to as
nested approach
(see e.g.
[
9
]
).
In that work, the authors develop a RL algorithm that estimates the value function by generating additional (inner) transitions for every visited state of an (outer) episode – e.g. given a random variable, simulating many realisations and computing the average of the worst realisations gives a naive estimator of its tail expectation.
However, those nested simulations are costly, and the acquisition of new observations may not be possible when working with real datasets.
We instead wish to find an alternative methodology that alleviates this curse of dimensionality, i.e. that is
improved memory-wise
,
comparable in computational performance
, and more importantly
using only full episodes
.

At this point, our research question is twofold:
(i) how to devise an efficient approach to estimate the value function, which is effectively a dynamic risk measure; and
(ii) how to adapt a RL algorithm that avoids simulating transitions for every visited state to solve the class of RL problems described above.
We use the notion of
conditional elicitability
as a tool to answer the former question, and take advantage of the
gradient formulae for spectral risk measures with a finite support spectrum
to solve the latter, which leads us to a computationally efficient risk-aware RL algorithm.

4
Elicitability and Consistent Scoring Functions

In this section, we introduce the necessary theoretical background for point estimation using scoring functions, following the work from
[
23
]
.
We keep the results as general as possible, even though we often consider
\mathbb{F}
as the set of cumulative distribution functions (CDFs), and
\mathbb{A},\mathbb{Y}
as (subset of) the reals, i.e.
\mathbb{A}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{k}
and
\mathbb{Y}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Suppose we have a
d
-dimensional
random variable

Y
with support on
\mathbb{Y}
and a CDF
F:=F_{Y}\in\mathbb{F}
.
We are interested in finding a
k
-dimensional
point approximation

\mathfrak{a}\in\mathbb{A}
of a certain
statistical mapping
of the random variable
M(Y)
, where
M:\mathbb{Y}\rightarrow\mathbb{A}
.
The main goal is to find a
scoring function

S:\mathbb{A}\times\mathbb{Y}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that when observing a realisation
y\in\mathbb{Y}
, our current point forecast
\mathfrak{a}\in\mathbb{A}
is penalized by
S(\mathfrak{a},y)
.
The score function can be interpreted as a loss function.

To draw parallels between this notation and our objective, recall that we aim to provide an estimation (i.e. point approximation
\mathfrak{a}
) of the value function
V_{t}(s;\theta)
at every state
s\in\mathcal{S}
and time
t\in\mathcal{T}
(i.e. statistical mapping
M(Y)
) for sequences of costs
\{c^{\theta}_{t}\}_{t\in\mathcal{T}}
induced by the agent’s policy
\pi^{\theta}
(i.e. random variable
Y
).
We wish to update our estimate of the value function using a single realisation of costs from an episode and an appropriate loss function (i.e. scoring function
S
).

Next, we define a key property of scoring functions that makes them appealing for estimating mappings
M
.

Definition 8
.

A scoring function
S:\mathbb{A}\times\mathbb{Y}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is said to be
\mathbb{F}
-consistent for
M

iff for any
F\in\mathbb{F}
and
\mathfrak{a}\in\mathbb{A}
, we have

\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim F}\Big[S\big(M(Y),Y\big)\Big]\leq\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim F}\Big[S\big(\mathfrak{a},Y\big)\Big],

(6)

where
\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim F}[Y]=\int y\,\mathrm{d}F(y)
.
Furthermore, the scoring function
S
is
strictly

\mathbb{F}
-consistent for
M
if the equality in
eq.

6
implies that
\mathfrak{a}=M(Y)
.

Here, the choice of a scoring function
S
is closely related to the choice of a mapping
M
, as demonstrated by
[
23
]
.
For instance, one can show that if the mapping of interest is the mean, then
S(\mathfrak{a},y)=(\mathfrak{a}-y)^{2}
is (resp. strictly) consistent for the class of CDFs with finite first moments (resp. second moments).

definition

9
describes all mappings
M
admitting strictly consistent scoring functions.

Definition 9
.

A
k
-dimensional mapping
M:\mathbb{Y}\rightarrow\mathbb{A}
is said to be
k
-elicitable
wrt
\mathbb{F}

iff there exists a scoring function
S
that is strictly
\mathbb{F}
-consistent for
M
.

Therefore, a mapping
M
is elicitable iff there exists a scoring function such that its estimate is the unique minimiser of the expected score, i.e.

M(Y)=\argmin_{\mathfrak{a}\in\mathbb{A}}\;\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim F}\Big[S(\mathfrak{a},Y)\Big].

Much research has been done to determine which mappings are elicitable.
Under certain regularity assumptions, all moments, quantiles, and expectiles are
1
-elicitable (see e.g.
[
55
,
64
,
54
]
).
For instance, a strictly consistent scoring function
S
for the mean is necessarily of the form

S(\mathfrak{a},y)=G(y)-G(\mathfrak{a})+G^{\prime}(\mathfrak{a})(\mathfrak{a}-y),

where
G:\mathbb{Y}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is strictly convex with subgradient
G^{\prime}
; taking
G(x)=x^{2}
leads to the squared error.
For the
\alpha
-quantile, and thus the value-at-risk
\text{VaR}_{\alpha}
as in
definition

1
, any strictly consistent
S
must be of the form

S(\mathfrak{a},y)=\Big({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathds{1}_{y\leq\mathfrak{a}}}-\alpha\Big)\Big(G(\mathfrak{a})-G(y)\Big),

where
G:\mathbb{Y}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a nondecreasing function.
On the other hand, several researchers proved the non
1
-elicitability of well-known functionals, such as the variance, expected-shortfall, also known as conditional value-at-risk (
CVaR
), and any spectral risk measure (see e.g.
[
23
]
).

4.1
Conditional Elicitability

The value function we wish to estimate is a (dynamic) spectral risk measure, which is not
1
-elicitable
[
68
]
.
However,
[
16
]
proved that despite not being elicitable on its own, the
CVaR
is elicitable conditionally on the
VaR
.
It originates from the work of
[
46
]
and the concept of
higher order elicitability
or
conditional elicitability
, where components of a
k
-elicitable vector-valued mapping can fail to be
1
-elicitable.
As a common example, the variance is not
1
-elicitable, but the vector composed of the mean and variance is
2
-elicitable using the so-called revelation principle.
It relies heavily on the fact that the variance can be connected to a mapping with two
1
-elicitable components, i.e. the first two moments, via a bijection.

[
19
]
characterises (with necessary and sufficient conditions) the class of strictly consistent scoring functions for several statistical mappings.
One of their main results (see Theorem 5.2 of
[
19
]
) states that spectral risk measures having a spectrum
\varphi
with finite support can be a component of a
k
-elicitable mapping, although a spectral risk measure is not elicitable on its own.
We thus reformulate their result and adjust the notation for dynamic risk measures.
First, we add the conditioning on the
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{F}_{t}
, since we are working in a dynamic framework.
We also consider random variables that represent costs, and hence
CVaR
s that are upper tail expectations.

Theorem 10
(Conditional elicitability of spectral risk measures).

Let CDFs of
Y\,|_{\mathcal{F}_{t}}
for
Y\in\mathcal{Y}_{t+1}
, denoted by
\mathbb{F}
, have finite first moments and be supported on
\mathbb{Y}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
.
Let
\rho^{\varphi}
be a spectral risk measure, where
\varphi
is given by

\varphi=\sum_{m=1}^{k-1}p_{m}\delta_{\alpha_{m}},

with
p_{m}\in(0,1]
,
\sum_{m=1}^{k-1}p_{m}=1
, and
0<\alpha_{1}<\alpha_{2}<\cdots<\alpha_{k-1}<1
.
Define the mapping

M(Y)=\Big(\text{VaR}_{\alpha_{1}}(Y),\ldots,\text{VaR}_{\alpha_{k-1}}(Y),\,\rho^{\varphi}(Y)\Big).

If the CDFs in
\mathbb{F}
have unique
\alpha_{m}
-quantiles, then the mapping
M
is
k
-elicitable wrt
\mathbb{F}
.
Furthermore, define the set of estimates
\mathbb{A}=\{\mathfrak{a}\in\mathbb{Y}^{k}\;|\;\mathfrak{a}_{1}\leq\ldots\leq\mathfrak{a}_{k-1}\}
.
Then a scoring function
S:\mathbb{A}\times\mathbb{Y}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
of the form

\begin{split}&S(\mathfrak{a}_{1},\ldots,\mathfrak{a}_{k-1},\mathfrak{a}_{k},y)\\
&\quad=\Bigg[\sum_{m=1}^{k-1}\Big(\mathds{1}_{y\leq\mathfrak{a}_{m}}-\alpha_{m}\Big)\Big(G_{m}(\mathfrak{a}_{m})-G_{m}(y)\Big)\Bigg]-G_{k}(\mathfrak{a}_{k})+G_{k}(y)\\
&\qquad\quad+G_{k}^{\prime}(\mathfrak{a}_{k})\Bigg[\mathfrak{a}_{k}+\sum_{m=1}^{k-1}\frac{1}{1-\alpha_{m}}\bigg(\mathfrak{a}_{m}\Big(\mathds{1}_{y>\mathfrak{a}_{m}}-(1-\alpha_{m})\Big)-y\,\mathds{1}_{y>\mathfrak{a}_{m}}\bigg)\Bigg]\end{split}

(7)

is (resp. strictly)
\mathbb{F}
-consistent for
M
if (i)
G_{k}:\mathbb{Y}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is (resp. strictly) convex with subgradient
G_{k}^{\prime}
such that for a specific choice of mappings
G_{1},\ldots,G_{k-1}:\mathbb{Y}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, the functions

x\mapsto G_{m}(x)-\frac{xp_{m}}{1-\alpha_{m}}G_{k}^{\prime}(\mathfrak{a}_{k}),\quad m=1,\ldots,k-1

(8)

are (resp. strictly) increasing for any
\mathfrak{a}_{k}\in\mathbb{Y}
; and (ii)
\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim F}[|G_{m}(Y)|]<\infty
,
\forall\,m=1,\ldots,k
.

This result states that, under some regularity assumptions, for any spectral risk measure with a finite support spectrum of a random variable conditioned on a
\sigma
-algebra, there exists a strictly consistent scoring function for the mapping
M
composed of the spectral risk measure
\rho^{\varphi}
and all
\alpha_{m}
-quantiles with nonzero weights in its spectrum.
One particularly interesting spectral risk measure is the
CVaR
, where the spectrum
\varphi
has a weight on a single quantile.

corollary

11
(see Corollary 5.5 of
[
19
]
) which follows from
theorem

10
, gives the characterisation of (strictly) consistent scoring functions for the
CVaR
.

Corollary 11
(Conditional elicitability of the
CVaR
).

Let CDFs of
Y\,|_{\mathcal{F}_{t}}
for
Y\in\mathcal{Y}_{t+1}
, denoted by
\mathbb{F}
, have finite first moments, and be supported on
\mathbb{Y}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
.
Define the mapping

M(Y)=\Big(\text{VaR}_{\alpha}(Y),\,\text{CVaR}_{\alpha}(Y)\Big).

If the CDFs in
\mathbb{F}
have unique
\alpha
-quantiles, then the mapping
M
is
2
-elicitable wrt
\mathbb{F}
.
Furthermore, define the set of estimates
\mathbb{A}=\{\mathfrak{a}\in\mathbb{Y}^{2}\;|\;\mathfrak{a}_{1}\leq\mathfrak{a}_{2}\}
.
Then a scoring function
S:\mathbb{A}\times\mathbb{Y}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
of the form

\begin{split}S(\mathfrak{a}_{1},\mathfrak{a}_{2},y)&=\Big(\mathds{1}_{y\leq\mathfrak{a}_{1}}-\alpha\Big)\Big(G_{1}(\mathfrak{a}_{1})-G_{1}(y)\Big)-G_{2}(\mathfrak{a}_{2})+G_{2}(y)\\
&\qquad+G_{2}^{\prime}(\mathfrak{a}_{2})\Bigg[\mathfrak{a}_{2}+\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\bigg(\mathfrak{a}_{1}\Big(\mathds{1}_{y>\mathfrak{a}_{1}}-(1-\alpha)\Big)-y\,\mathds{1}_{y>\mathfrak{a}_{1}}\bigg)\Bigg]\end{split}

(9)

is (resp. strictly)
\mathbb{F}
-consistent for
M
if (i)
G_{2}:\mathbb{Y}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is (resp. strictly) convex with subgradient
G_{2}^{\prime}
such that for a specific choice of mapping
G_{1}:\mathbb{Y}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, the function

x\mapsto G_{1}(x)-\frac{x}{1-\alpha}G_{2}^{\prime}(\mathfrak{a}_{2})

(10)

is (resp. strictly) increasing for any
\mathfrak{a}_{2}\in\mathbb{Y}
; and (ii)
\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim F}[|G_{1}(Y)|],\,\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim F}[|G_{2}(Y)|]<\infty
.

We restrict the set of estimates to
\{\mathfrak{a}\in\mathbb{Y}^{2}\;|\;\mathfrak{a}_{1}\leq\mathfrak{a}_{2}\}
because
\text{CVaR}_{\alpha}
must be greater than
\text{VaR}_{\alpha}
.

corollary

11
is a key result as it states that one can find the
CVaR
using the
VaR
as an intermediate step in the optimisation problem

\Big(\text{VaR}_{\alpha}(Y),\text{CVaR}_{\alpha}(Y)\Big)=\argmin_{(\mathfrak{a}_{1},\mathfrak{a}_{2})\in\mathbb{A}}\;\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim F}\Big[S(\mathfrak{a}_{1},\mathfrak{a}_{2},Y)\Big],

with
S
given in
eq.

9
.

We emphasise here that there exist infinitely many characterisations for the scoring function
S
, and the choice of
G_{1},G_{2}
strongly depends on the support
\mathbb{Y}
.
For instance, assume that
Y
are bounded costs, i.e.
|Y|<C
, for some constant
C>0
.
Then if we use
G_{1}(x)=C
and
G_{2}(x)=-\log(x+C)
, which leads to
G_{2}^{\prime}(x)=\frac{-1}{x+C}
in the strictly consistent scoring function in
eq.

9
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\begin{split}S(\mathfrak{a}_{1},\mathfrak{a}_{2},y)&=\Big(\mathds{1}_{y\leq\mathfrak{a}_{1}}-\alpha\Big)\Big(C-C\Big)+\log(\mathfrak{a}_{2}+C)-\log(y+C)\\
&\qquad\quad-\frac{1}{\mathfrak{a}_{2}+C}\Bigg[\mathfrak{a}_{2}+\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\bigg(\mathfrak{a}_{1}\Big(\mathds{1}_{y>\mathfrak{a}_{1}}-(1-\alpha)\Big)-y\,\mathds{1}_{y>\mathfrak{a}_{1}}\bigg)\Bigg]\end{split}

\displaystyle=\log\left(\frac{\mathfrak{a}_{2}+C}{y+C}\right)-\frac{\mathfrak{a}_{2}}{\mathfrak{a}_{2}+C}+\frac{\mathfrak{a}_{1}\left(\mathds{1}_{y\leq\mathfrak{a}_{1}}-\alpha\right)+y\,\mathds{1}_{y>\mathfrak{a}_{1}}}{(\mathfrak{a}_{2}+C)(1-\alpha)}.

(11)

This choice respects the condition in
eq.

10
, as
x\mapsto C+\frac{x}{(\mathfrak{a}_{2}+C)\alpha}
is strictly increasing wrt
x
.
This specific characterisation is a canonical choice in the literature, as the first term of
eq.

9
vanishes.

If one is interested in nonnegative random variables, a more appropriate choice is
G_{2}(x)=-\log(x)
, which leads to

S(\mathfrak{a}_{1},\mathfrak{a}_{2},y)=\log\left(\frac{\mathfrak{a}_{2}}{y}\right)-1+\frac{\mathfrak{a}_{1}\left(\mathds{1}_{y\leq\mathfrak{a}_{1}}-\alpha\right)+y\,\mathds{1}_{y>\mathfrak{a}_{1}}}{\mathfrak{a}_{2}(1-\alpha)}.

(12)

Although we do not focus here on the choice of these functions, it would be interesting from a theoretical perspective to understand if characterisations of scoring functions affect optimisation performances.
In our experiments, we use scoring functions of the form
eq.

11
as we work with bounded random costs.

5
Deep Composite Model Regression

Assume the framework outlined in
section

4
, but now the random variable
Y\,|_{\mathcal{F}_{t}}
is explained by
q

covariates
or
features

x\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
.
In our RL problem, recall that the sequences of costs
\{c^{\theta}_{t}\}_{t\in\mathcal{T}}
induced by the agent’s policy
\pi^{\theta}
(i.e. random variable
Y
) are explained by the states
s\in\mathcal{S}
and times
t\in\mathcal{T}
(i.e. observable features
x
).
We then take an interest in
k
-elicitable mappings of the
conditional
CDF
F:=F_{Y\mid X=x}\in\mathbb{F}
, such as
M(Y|X=x)=\text{CVaR}_{\alpha}(Y|X=x)
.
The objective is to use strictly consistent scoring functions to approximate these mappings.
In this section, we work with a
1
-elicitable mapping
M
and a corresponding strictly consistent scoring function
S
for readability purposes.
However, since the setting is valid for any strictly consistent scoring function, we can replace
M
with any
k
-elicitable mapping.

We wish to find a function of the features
h:\mathbb{R}^{q}\rightarrow\mathbb{A}
, as opposed to a point approximate
\mathfrak{a}\in\mathbb{A}
, that minimises the expectation of the strictly consistent scoring function, i.e.

M(Y|X=x)=\argmin_{h\,:\,\mathbb{R}^{q}\rightarrow\mathbb{A}}\;\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim F}\Big[S(h(x),Y)\Big].

(13)

As the space of all functions is infinite dimensional, the usual approach to obtaining approximations for
M(Y|X=x)
consists of assuming a certain parametric form for the function
h
, and optimising over its parameters.
It is closely related to the notion of quantile and composite model regression.

Quantile regression and composite models have shown a lot of potential in the machine learning (ML) community (see e.g.
[
42
,
62
,
67
,
52
,
24
]
).
These approaches provide more desirable alternatives to the previous methods, which utilise the expectation-maximisation (EM) algorithm in the modelling procedure and require specific distribution assumptions.

The original quantile regression approach in
[
33
]
assumes there exists a prespecified link function
h:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that

M(Y|X=x)=h^{-1}\Big(\beta_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{q}\beta_{j}x_{j}\Big).

(14)

After determining the appropriate scoring function, one finds the optimal coefficients of
eq.

14
by solving for

\beta^{*}=\argmin_{\beta\,\in\,\mathbb{R}^{q+1}}\;\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim F}\Bigg[S\bigg(h^{-1}\Big(\beta_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{q}\beta_{j}X_{j}\Big),Y\bigg)\Bigg].

(15)

As the true distribution is often unknown, the expectation in
eq.

15
is replaced by the empirical mean based on observed data
(x^{(i)},y^{(i)})
,
i=1,\ldots,n
, to obtain the estimator

\hat{\beta}=\argmin_{\beta\,\in\,\mathbb{R}^{q+1}}\;\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Bigg[S\bigg(h^{-1}\Big(\beta_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{q}\beta_{j}x_{j}^{(i)}\Big),y^{(i)}\bigg)\Bigg].

(16)

This approach with a strictly consistent scoring function gives an M-estimator of
M(Y|X=x)
that converges in probability to the true value under certain regularity assumptions (see Theorem 1 of
[
15
]
for relations between consistency and conditional model-consistency of scoring functions).

Instead of the generalised linear model assumption in
eq.

16
, which is quite restrictive,
[
17
]
generalises this idea and uses a more powerful function approximator to perform deep quantile regression: neural network structures.
The authors model the mapping
M
as a linear combination of outputs from a feed-forward ANN, denoted
H^{\psi}:\mathbb{R}^{q}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{q}
, and update the weights and biases of the ANN as well as the coefficients
\beta
to optimise the expected score. They solve

(\hat{\beta},\hat{\psi})=\argmin_{\beta,\psi}\;\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Bigg[S\bigg(h^{-1}\Big(\beta_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{q}\beta_{j}H^{\psi}(x^{(i)})_{j}\Big),y^{(i)}\bigg)\Bigg],

(17)

where they use a linear combination of the outputs of
H
, and then perform a transformation using the link function
h
.
This framework is implemented for deep composite model regression where the mapping
M
is the tuple consisting of a
\alpha
-quantile, its lower and upper tail expectations.
They illustrate the applicability of their approach on a real dataset, and show better performance than that of classical approaches.
Their works extend the paper from
[
49
]
, in which joint estimation of quantiles and the expectation is performed using neural network structures.

[
17
]
do not, however, study how their framework can be applied dynamically.
Also, although this deep modelling is quite powerful, their approach can be improved.
Indeed, using a linear combination with outputs of an ANN in
eq.

17
is unnecessary and adds more parameters in the optimisation process.
ANNs are known to be universal approximators, and a sufficiently large neural network structure can adequately approximate any linear or nonlinear relationship.
One could instead directly model the statistical mapping
M
with an ANN, e.g.
H^{\psi}:\mathbb{R}^{q}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, and only optimise its weights and biases:

\hat{\psi}=\argmin_{\psi}\;\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigg[S\Big(H^{\psi}(x^{(i)}),y^{(i)}\Big)\bigg].

(18)

The approach in
eq.

18
is applied by
[
18
]
to estimate (static) sensitivity measures.
We make use of this form in our subsequent analysis.

\displaystyle\argmin_{\psi_{t}}\;\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{\theta}(\cdot,\cdot|s_{t}=s)}\Bigg[S\bigg(\underbrace{H_{1,t}^{\psi_{1,t}}\Big(s;\theta\Big)}_{\text{VaR}_{\alpha}(\cdot|s_{t})};\ \underbrace{V_{t}^{\psi_{t}}\Big(s;\theta\Big)}_{\text{CVaR}_{\alpha}(\cdot|s_{t})};\ \underbrace{c_{t}^{\theta}+V_{t+1}^{\psi_{t+1}}\Big(s_{t+1}^{\theta};\theta\Big)}_{\text{running risk-to-go}}\bigg)\Bigg],

where

We also assume that the CDFs of the costs have finite first moments and unique

Here, we view

To overcome stability issues often arising in actor-critic approaches, we replace the parameters

The critic procedure to estimate the value function is summarised as follows.
For each epoch of the training loop, we initially set to zero the accumulated gradients in the ANNs

We then simulate a mini-batch of full episodes induced by the policy

We also note that any bounded one-step conditional risk measure

In the proof of

6.2
Policy Update

Next, we provide details regarding the gradient of the value function which is used to update the policy.

Theorem 14
.

Suppose the logarithm of transition probabilities
\log\mathbb{P}^{\theta}(a,s^{\prime}|s)
is a differentiable function in
\theta
when
\mathbb{P}^{\theta}(a,s^{\prime}|s)\neq 0
, and its gradient wrt
\theta
is bounded for any
(a,s)\in\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{S}
.
Then, for any state
s\in\mathcal{S}
, the gradient of the value function at period
T
given in
eq.

20a
is

\nabla_{\theta}V_{T}(s;\theta)=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{\theta}(\cdot,\cdot|s_{T}=s)}\bigg[\Big(c_{T}^{\theta}-\lambda^{*}\Big)_{+}\bigg(\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi^{\theta}(a|s_{T})\Big\rvert_{a=a_{T}^{\theta}}\bigg)\bigg],

(34a)

and the gradient of the value function at periods
t\in\mathcal{T}\setminus\{T\}
given in
eq.

20b
is

\begin{split}\nabla_{\theta}V_{t}(s;\theta)&=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{\theta}(\cdot,\cdot|s_{t}=s)}\bigg[\Big(c_{t}^{\theta}+V_{t+1}(s_{t+1}^{\theta};\theta)-\lambda^{*}\Big)_{+}\bigg(\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi^{\theta}(a|s_{t})\Big\rvert_{a=a_{t}^{\theta}}\bigg)\bigg]\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad+\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{\theta}(\cdot,\cdot|s_{t}=s)}\bigg[\bigg(\nabla_{\theta}V_{t+1}(s^{\prime};\theta)\Big\rvert_{s^{\prime}=s_{t+1}^{\theta}}\bigg)\;\xi^{*}(a_{t}^{\theta},s_{t+1}^{\theta})\bigg],\end{split}

(34b)

where
(\xi^{*},\lambda^{*})
is any saddle-point of the Lagrangian function of
eqs.

20a
and
20b
respectively.

Proof.

We provide a proof for the gradient at the last period – the gradient for the others periods is obtained in an analogous manner.
First, recall that using the representation theorem for coherent risk measures (see Theorem 6.4 of
[
56
]
), the value function at period
t=T
in
eq.

20a
can be written as

V_{T}(s;\theta)=\sup_{\xi\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{P}^{\theta}(\cdot,\cdot|s_{T}=s))}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{\theta}(\cdot,\cdot|s_{T}=s)}\Big[c_{T}^{\theta}\,\xi\Big],

(35)

where the set
\mathcal{U}
is the (convex) risk envelope

\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{P}^{\theta}(\cdot,\cdot|s)):=\left\{\xi:\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{\theta}(\cdot,\cdot|s)}\Big[\xi\Big]=1,\ \xi\in\left[0,\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\right]\right\}.

(36)

For any state
s\in\mathcal{S}
, the Lagrangian associated with the optimisation problem in
eq.

35
is

\displaystyle L^{\theta}(\xi,\lambda)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{\theta}(\cdot,\cdot|s_{T}=s)}\Big[c_{T}^{\theta}\,\xi\Big]-\lambda\left(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{\theta}(\cdot,\cdot|s_{T}=s)}\Big[\xi\Big]-1\right)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&=\sum_{(a,s^{\prime})}c(s_{T},a,s^{\prime})\,\mathbb{P}^{\theta}(a,s^{\prime}|s_{T}=s)\,\xi(a,s^{\prime})\\
&\qquad-\lambda\left(\sum_{(a,s^{\prime})}\mathbb{P}^{\theta}(a,s^{\prime}|s_{T}=s)\,\xi(a,s^{\prime})-1\right).\end{split}

(37)

One can show that there exists at least one saddle-point of the Lagrangian function in
eq.

37
due to the convexity of the
CVaR
and its risk envelope
\mathcal{U}
.
Furthermore, any saddle-point
(\xi^{*},\lambda^{*})
satisfies
\xi^{*}(a,s^{\prime})=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}
if
c_{T}^{\theta}>\lambda^{*}
and
\xi^{*}(a,s^{\prime})=0
otherwise, where
\lambda^{*}
is any
\alpha
-quantile of
c_{T}^{\theta}
.
Using Slater’s condition and the convexity of the problem, strong duality holds, i.e.

V_{T}(s;\theta)=\max_{\xi\geq 0}\,\min_{\lambda}\,L^{\theta}(\xi,\lambda).

(38)

Next, we apply the Envelope theorem for saddle-point problems (see Theorem 4 in
[
43
]
) on
eq.

38
, which gives us that the gradient of the maximisation problem equals to the gradient of the Lagrangian evaluated at one of its saddle-points.
It relies on the equidifferentiability of the objective function and the absolute continuity of its gradient – these properties are satisfied as we work with a spectral (and thus coherent and convex) risk measure.

We remark that the transition probabilities
\mathbb{P}^{\theta}(a,s^{\prime}|s)
depends on
\theta
only through the policy
\pi^{\theta}(a|s)
.
By the assumptions in the Theorem statement,
\mathbb{P}^{\theta}(a,s^{\prime}|s)
is differentiable and bounded, hence policy gradient can be applied. This assumption is standard among policy gradient methods (see e.g.
[
61
,
34
,
40
]
).
Using the Envelope theorem on
eq.

7.1
Statistical Arbitrage Example

Suppose an agent begins each episode with zero inventory, and at each period the agent wishes to trade quantities of an asset, whose price fluctuates according to some data-generating processes.
There are quadratic costs associated with each transaction, and the market imposes a terminal penalty encouraging the agent to liquidate their inventory by the end of the episode.
For each period
t\in\mathcal{T}
, the agent observes the asset’s price
S_{t}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and their inventory
q_{t}\in(q_{\min},q_{\max})
, performs a trade
a_{t}^{\theta}\in(a_{\min},a_{\max})
, resulting in wealth
y_{t}\in\mathbb{R}
according to

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} y_{0}&=0,\\
y_{t}&=y_{t-1}-a_{t-1}^{\theta}S_{t-1}-(a_{t-1}^{\theta})^{2}\phi_{1},\qquad t=1,\ldots,T\\
y_{T+1}&=y_{T}-a_{T}^{\theta}S_{T}-(a_{T}^{\theta})^{2}\phi_{1}+q_{T+1}S_{T+1}-q_{T+1}^{2}\phi_{2},\end{aligned},\end{cases}

with coefficients
\phi_{1}=0.005
and
\phi_{2}=0.5
for the transaction costs and terminal penalty, respectively.
We suppose that
T=4
,
q_{\max}=-q_{\min}=5
,
a_{\max}=-a_{\min}=2
, and the asset price follows an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process given by the stochastic differential equation (SDE)

\mathrm{d}S_{t}=\kappa(\mu-S_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\mathrm{d}W_{t},

where
\kappa=2
,
\mu=1
,
\sigma=0.2
and
W_{t}
is a standard Brownian motion.
The statistical arbitrage consists in taking positions in the stock to take advantage of temporary price deviations from the mean-reverting value
\mu
.
The risk-aware agent tries to optimise the RL problem stated in
eq.

P
, where for every period, the actions are determined by the trades
a_{t}
, the costs by the differences in wealth
c_{t}=y_{t}-y_{t+1}
, and the states by the tuples
(t,S_{t},q_{t})
.

We compare the optimal policies learnt with both the elicitable and nested simulation (proposed by
[

We first observe that our proposed approach gives a similar approximation to the time-dependent optimal solution.
We remark that the estimation differs for extreme prices – we believe it is due to the fact that when randomising the initial states of the episodes, most of them do not have major price changes as time progresses.
Also, our algorithm is more efficient in terms of memory and time.
Indeed, the approach does not require additional transitions for every visited state, and the training procedure takes approximately half of the time compared to the nested simulation approach.
We suspect that this time improvement is mostly due to the actor procedure, where the nested approach must repeatedly simulate outer episodes and inner transitions.

We acknowledge that this set of experiments is not an exhaustive comparison between the two approaches, as there are many components to consider: i.e. the code implementation, number of inner transitions, dimensions of the state space, initialisation of the ANNs, etc.

Portfolio Allocation Example

In this set of experiments, we highlight the flexibility of our novel approach on a portfolio allocation problem setting.
Suppose an agent faces a market with
I
risky assets, and can allocate its wealth between these financial instruments during
T=12
periods over a one year horizon. First, we conduct two sets of experiments: for every risky asset denoted
i=1,\ldots,I
, the price dynamics
(S_{t}^{(i)})_{t}
are given by either one of the SDEs

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}S_{t}^{(i)}

\displaystyle=\mu^{(i)}S_{t}^{(i)}\mathrm{d}t+\sigma^{(i)}S_{t}^{(i)}\mathrm{d}W_{t}^{(i)},\quad\text{or}

(41a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}X_{t}^{(i)}

\displaystyle=-\kappa X_{t}^{(i)}\mathrm{d}t+\sigma^{(i)}\mathrm{d}W_{t}^{(i)}\quad\text{with}\quad S_{t}^{(i)}=e^{X_{t}^{(i)}+\vartheta^{(i)}_{t}},

(41b)

where
\mu^{(i)}

\sigma^{(i)}
the volatility scale parameters,

\vartheta^{(i)}_{t}=\mu^{(i)}t-(\sigma^{(i)})^{2}(1-e^{-2\kappa t})/4\kappa
the (time-dependent) mean-reversion levels, and
(W_{t}^{(i)})_{t}
are
\mathbb{P}
-Brownian motions.
One can show that the expectation of the price dynamics is identical with both SDEs, i.e.
\mathbb{E}[S_{t}^{(i)}]=e^{\mu^{(i)}t}
for all
i=1,\ldots,I

eq.

41b
.

(W_{t}^{(i)})_{t}

i=1,\ldots,I

\mathbb{P}
-Brownian motions with correlation
\rho

\mathrm{d}[W^{(i)},W^{(j)}]_{t}=\rho_{i,j}\mathrm{d}t

CVaR

Here, we focus on a few features: states are given by the time
t

\{S_{t}^{(i)}\}_{i=1,\ldots,I}

I=3

The policy is characterised by a
I

More specifically, the outputs of an ANN

If short-selling is allowed in the market, one can, for instance, obtain the investment proportions for all assets except one (e.g. with a hyperbolic tangent output activation function to control the maximum leverage allowed) and then impose the
\sum_{i}\pi_{t}^{(i)}=1

7.3
Other Examples

In this paper, we apply our proposed methodology on statistical arbitrage and portfolio optimisation problems.
Risk-awareness plays a central role in various mathematical finance applications, which clearly motivates the need to account for uncertainty in those situations.
Indeed, one may apply our algorithm in other contexts, to solve sequential decision making problems where the objective involves a dynamic risk measure of the costs induced by a certain policy – e.g., in credit risk, visual recognition, video games, robot control and trading problems, among others.

Next, we describe some settings for which a risk-aware RL algorithm is relevant or more appropriate than the usual risk-neutral approaches.
This is by no means exhaustive, but rather an illustration of RL problems that could benefit from risk-sensitive behaviours.

An important problem in mathematical finance consists of hedging and pricing contingent claims.
Suppose we have a financial market of multiple risky securities and risk-free bonds following some asset dynamics, and all assets can be traded over a time horizon with trading frictions.
The agent aims to determine either: (i) the price it should charge at each period for selling a certain (path-dependent) contingent claim; or (ii) the strategy it should adopt for hedging a certain (path-dependent) contingent claim.
The observable features that dictates the agent’s decisions may include, but are not restricted to, the market volatility, asset price history, or inventories of the different assets held by the agent.
It is obvious why applying risk-aware RL methods on this class of problems is sensible – indeed, the agent wishes to protect itself from high-cost outcomes in the market.
Our proposed approach also allows the agent to tweak the threshold of the dynamic
CVaR
to fulfil its own tolerance to risk.
We note that the setup described above is applicable to other sequential decision making problems in finance, e.g. in market making, entry-exit problems, goal-based portfolio management, and placement of cryptocurrency limit orders.

Uncertainty in the environment also affects performances of agents in domains outside of finance, for instance in robot control.
Consider an autonomous rover exploring an unknown terrain.
Its objective is to reach a final destination while avoiding different obstacles along the way that could cause substantial damages.
At each period, the rover evaluates its position and environment conditions and chooses to move in a certain direction, but its movements are altered by meteorological/topographical events, e.g. facing strong winds, slipping on sand, or crossing shallow water.
The randomness may be modeled through the randomised policy with an atypical distribution and/or directly within the transition probability distributions.
The costs observed by the rover may be influenced by the travelled distance, the consumption of energy and the severity of any impairments.
In this scenario, a risk-neutral rover would typically take the most direct route to reach the destination as quickly as possible, ignoring the randomness in its movements and potentially causing expensive damages.
Instead, a more conservative behaviour would account for uncertainty by keeping distance from obstacles to avoid inadvertent collisions, even if it increases the costs in terms of travelled distance.
A similar setting may be used in video games applications, where the agent tries to accomplish different missions.

Generalisation to Spectral Risk Measures

Our algorithm is described in detail for the dynamic
CVaR
; however, it is interesting and important to consider generalising the framework to dynamic risk measures which are
k
-elicitable.
This section presents the modifications to our approach to incorporate any dynamic spectral risk measure having a spectrum with finite support.

In what follows, we consider problems of the form
eq.

P
, and assume that the one-step conditional risk measures are one-step conditional spectral risk measures
\rho^{\varphi}
, where
\varphi
is given by

\varphi=\sum_{m=1}^{k-1}p_{m}\delta_{\alpha_{m}},

with
p_{m}\in(0,1]
,
\sum_{m=1}^{k-1}p_{m}=1
, and
0<\alpha_{1}<\alpha_{2}<\cdots<\alpha_{k-1}<1
.
In this setting, the value function in
eqs.

5a
and
5b
is written as

\displaystyle V_{T}(s;\theta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{k-1}p_{m}\;\text{CVaR}_{\alpha_{m}}\Big(c_{T}^{\theta}\Bigm|s_{T}=s\Big),\quad\text{and}

(42a)

\displaystyle V_{t}(s;\theta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{k-1}p_{m}\;\text{CVaR}_{\alpha_{m}}\Big(c_{t}^{\theta}+V_{t+1}(s_{t+1}^{\theta};\theta)\Bigm|s_{t}=s\Big).

(42b)

Similarly to the
CVaR
case, we define an ANN
\pi^{\theta}:\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{A})
for the agent’s policy.
We decompose the value function when in state
s\in\mathcal{S}
at period
t\in\mathcal{T}
and behaving according to
\pi^{\theta}
as

V_{t}(s;\theta)=H_{k,t}(s;\theta)+\sum_{m=1}^{k-1}p_{m}\sum_{l=1}^{m}H_{l,t}(s;\theta),

(43)

where we define for
m=1,\ldots,k-1

\displaystyle\begin{split}H_{m,t}(s;\theta)&:=\text{VaR}_{\alpha_{m}}\Big(c_{t}^{\theta}+V_{t+1}(s_{t+1}^{\theta};\theta)\Bigm|s_{t}=s\Big)-\sum_{l=1}^{m-1}H_{l,t}(s;\theta),\quad\text{and}\end{split}

(44a)

\displaystyle\begin{split}H_{k,t}(s;\theta)&:=\rho^{\varphi}\Big(c_{t}^{\theta}+V_{t+1}(s_{t+1}^{\theta};\theta)\Bigm|s_{t}=s\Big)-\sum_{m=1}^{k-1}p_{m}\sum_{l=1}^{m}H_{l,t}(s;\theta).\end{split}

(44b)

We further denote the estimation of the value function in
eq.

43
by

V_{t}^{\psi}(s;\theta)=H_{k,t}^{\psi_{k}}(s;\theta)+\sum_{m=1}^{k-1}p_{m}\sum_{l=1}^{m}H_{l,t}^{\psi_{l}}(s;\theta),

(45)

where
\psi=\{\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{k}\}
and
H_{1,t}^{\psi_{1}}(\cdot;\theta):\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,
H_{2,t}^{\psi_{2}}(\cdot;\theta),\ldots,H_{k,t}^{\psi_{k}}(\cdot;\theta):\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
estimate respectively
H_{1,t},H_{2,t},\ldots,H_{k,t}
.
Again, note that we characterise the difference between
VaR
s to ensure
\text{VaR}_{\alpha_{m}}
is greater than
\text{VaR}_{\alpha_{m-1}}
for every
m=2,\ldots,k-1
, and we constrain the ANN

9
Conclusion

In this paper, we developed a novel setting to solve finite-horizon RL problems where the objective function consists of a time-consistent, dynamic spectral risk measure of costs induced by a randomised policy.
Our proposed approach, which makes use of the conditional elicitability of spectral risk functionals with a finite support spectrum, provides an improvement over the existing nested simulation framework.
Indeed, it requires less memory and computation resources by estimating the risk and performing a policy gradient method using only full episodes.
It also gives a risk-aware RL algorithm for solving this class of sequential decision making problems in scenarios where simulating additional transitions is too costly or even infeasible.

Part of our future work consists of extending this framework and providing risk-aware RL alternatives.
For instance, from a risk-aware perspective, an agent may wish to consider deterministic policies to avoid introducing extra randomness in the optimisation problem, or robustify its actions against the uncertainty from the environment.
It is also of great interest to understand how such algorithms perform in real-life applications from direct interactions with real environments, and quantify the gain in performance of time-consistent policies as opposed to precommitment strategies.
Thus providing insights on how to devise a universal model-agnostic approach for solving RL problems with time-consistent dynamic risk measures.

a
d
