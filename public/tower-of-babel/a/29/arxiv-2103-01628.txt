Improving EFX Guarantees through Rainbow Cycle Number
Bhaskar Ray Chaudhury, Jugal Garg, Kurt Mehlhorn, Ruta Mehta, Pranabendu Misra

Abstract
We study the problem of fairly allocating a set of indivisible goods among n n agents with additive valuations. Envy-freeness up to any good (EFX) is arguably the most compelling fairness notion in this context. However, the existence of EFX allocations has not been settled and is one of the most important problems in fair division [ 36 ] . Towards resolving this problem, many impressive results show the existence of its relaxations. In particular, [ 6 ] shows the existence of 0.618 0.618 -EFX allocations, and [ 19 ] shows that EFX allocation exists if we do not allocate at most n − 1 n-1 goods. The latter result was recently improved for three agents in [ 18 ] , in which the two unallocated goods are allocated through an involved procedure. Reducing the number of unallocated goods for an arbitrary number of agents is a systematic way to settle the big question. In this paper, we develop a new approach, and show that for every ε ∈ ( 0 , 1 / 2 ] \varepsilon\in(0,1/2] , there always exists a ( 1 − ε ) (1-\varepsilon) -EFX allocation with sublinear number of unallocated goods and high Nash welfare. For this, we reduce the EFX problem to a novel problem in extremal graph theory. We introduce the notion of rainbow cycle number R ⁡ ( ⋅ ) {\mathit{R}}(\cdot) . For all d ∈ ℕ d\in\mathbb{N} , R ⁡ ( d ) {\mathit{R}}(d) is the largest k k such that there exists a k k -partite graph G = ( ∪ i ∈ [ k ] V i , E ) G=(\cup_{i\in[k]}V_{i},E) , in which • each part has at most d d vertices, i.e., | V i | ≤ d |V_{i}|\leq d for all i ∈ [ k ] i\in[k] , • for any two parts V i V_{i} and V j V_{j} , each vertex in V i V_{i} has an incoming edge from some vertex in V j V_{j} and vice-versa, and • there exists no cycle in G G that contains at most one vertex from each part. We show that any upper bound on R ⁡ ( d ) {\mathit{R}}(d) directly translates to a sublinear bound on the number of unallocated goods. We establish a polynomial upper bound on R ⁡ ( d ) {\mathit{R}}(d) , yielding our main result. Furthermore, our approach is constructive, which also gives a polynomial-time algorithm for finding such an allocation.

Today the issue of fair division arises in division of labor, inheritance, or computing resources, divorce settlements, partnership dissolutions, splitting rent among tenants, splitting taxi fare among passengers, dividing household tasks, air traffic management, frequency allocation, and so on.
In the internet age, the existence of several centralized platforms and more computational power has triggered substantial interest from the economics and computer science community to find computationally tractable protocols to allocate resources fairly; see Spliddit

EFX Allocations.

A quintessential notion of fairness is that of envy-freeness (EF): an allocation

Finding

Further Related Work

Fair division has received significant attention since the seminal work of Steinhaus

[
31
]
. While EF1 and EFX are fairness notions that relax envy-freeness, the most popular notion of fairness that relaxes proportionality for indivisible items is

(MMS), which was introduced by Budish
[

]
. While MMS allocations do not always exist

]

,

,

,

. Some works assume ordinal ranking over the goods, as opposed to cardinal values, e.g.,

Alongside fairness, the efficiency of an allocation is also a desirable property. Two common measures of efficiency is that of Pareto-optimality and Nash welfare. Caragiannis et al.

A fair division instance is given by the three tuple

An agent envies (heavily envies, strongly envies) an agent

strongly Pareto-dominates
