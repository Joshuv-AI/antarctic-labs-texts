1.
Introduction

The object of this paper is the study of

\displaystyle U_{t}(a,q):=\sum_{1\leq n_{1}<n_{2}<\cdots<n_{t}}\frac{q^{n_{1}+n_{2}+\cdots+n_{t}}}{\prod_{k=1}^{t}(1+aq^{n_{k}}+q^{2n_{k}})}=\sum_{n\geq 0}MO(a,t;n)q^{n},

(1)

where
a
is among
0,\pm 1,\pm 2
. As will become clear, the
U_{t}(a;q)
are most interesting when the roots of

1+ax+x^{2}=0

are also roots of unity, and this occurs precisely for
a=0,\pm 1,\pm 2
.

We shall prove that these functions are quasi-modular forms. Thus we can expect interesting arithmetic consequences some of which we describe in detail. In Section 2, we prove a necessary theorem connecting
U_{t}(a,q)
with modified Chebychev polynomials and prove that
MO(1,t;3n+2)=0
. In Sections 3, we prove analytically the following result:

Theorem 1.1
.

We have that

\displaystyle U_{2}(1,q)=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{q^{3n}}{(1-q^{3n})^{2}}.

(2)

In Section 4, we give a combinatorial proof of the result in (
2
). Section 5 provides alternative forms of
U_{t}(a,q)
necessary to discerning the quasi-modular nature of these functions. Section 6 treats quasi-modularity in the cases
a=\pm 2
. Section 7 explores the cases
t=1
, paving the way for Section 8 where we reveal quasi-modularity of the remaining cases
a=0,\pm 1
. The paper concludes with two appendices, the first uses the Wilf-Zeilberger (WZ) method to treat some of the binomial coefficient summations needed in this paper, and the second utilizes Riordan arrays for the same objective.

2.
The Chebychev connection

In this section, we begin by recalling the Andrews-Rose identity
[
4
]
which involves the
Chebychev polynomials of the first kind
defined by

\displaystyle T_{n}(\cos\theta)=\cos(n\theta).

In
[
4
, Theorem 1]
, it is proved that

\displaystyle 2\sum_{n\geq 0}T_{2n+1}\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)q^{n^{2}+n}

\displaystyle=x(q^{2};q^{2})^{3}_{\infty}\prod_{n\geq 1}\left(1+\frac{x^{2}q^{2n}}{(1-q^{2n})^{2}}\right)

(3)

\displaystyle=(q^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}^{3}\sum_{t\geq 0}U_{t}(-2,q)x^{2t+1},

where
U_{t}(a,q)
is defined in (
1
), and

(A;q)_{n}=(1-A)(1-Aq)\cdots(1-Aq^{n-1})\qquad\text{for $0\leq n\leq\infty$}.

We note that
T_{n}(x)
is alternately an even and odd function, depending on
n
. To make (
3
) more manageable for our purposes, we write

\displaystyle to_{n}(x)=\frac{T_{2n+1}(\sqrt{x})}{\sqrt{x}}.

(4)

Thus equation (
3
) now becomes

\sum_{n\geq 0}to_{n}\left(\frac{x}{4}\right)q^{\binom{n+1}{2}}=(q;q)^{3}_{\infty}\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(1+\frac{xq^{n}}{(1-q^{n})^{2}}\right)=(q;q)_{\infty}^{3}\sum_{t\geq 0}U_{t}(-2,q)x^{t}.

Consequently, we obtain the following.

Theorem 2.1
.

We have

\displaystyle\sum_{t\geq 0}U_{t}(a,q)x^{t}=

\displaystyle\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(1+aq^{n}+q^{2n})(1-q^{n})}\cdot\sum_{n\geq 0}to_{n}\left(\frac{x+a+2}{4}\right)q^{\binom{n+1}{2}}.

(5)

Proof.

We proceed as follows,

\displaystyle\sum_{t\geq 0}U_{t}(a,q)x^{t}=

\displaystyle\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(1+\frac{xq^{n}}{1+aq^{n}+q^{2n}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{1+aq^{n}+q^{2n}}\cdot\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(1+(x+a)q^{n}+q^{2n}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(1-q^{n})^{2}}{1+aq^{n}+q^{2n}}\cdot\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(1+\frac{(x+a+2)q^{n}}{(1-q^{n})^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(1+aq^{n}+q^{2n})(1-q^{n})}\cdot\sum_{n\geq 0}to_{n}\left(\frac{x+a+2}{4}\right)q^{\binom{n+1}{2}}.\qed

This core identity allows us an immediate congruence.

Theorem 2.2
.

We have
MO(1,t;3n+2)=0
.

Proof.

By (
5
),

\displaystyle\sum_{t\geq 0}U_{t}(1,q)x^{t}

\displaystyle=\prod_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{(1+q+q^{2n})(1-q^{n})}\sum_{n\geq 0}to_{n}\left(\frac{x+3}{4}\right)q^{\binom{n+1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(q^{3};q^{3})_{\infty}}\sum_{n\geq 0}to_{n}\left(\frac{x+3}{4}\right)q^{\binom{n+1}{2}}.

Now,
\binom{n+1}{2}
is never congruent to
2
modulo
3
. Hence there are no powers of
q
in
U_{t}(1,q)
congruent to
2
modulo
3
. This establishes our theorem.
∎

3.
Analytic proof of Theorem
1.1

We start with the following preliminary lemma.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\omega(n):=\frac{n(3n+1)}{2}
. Then,

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{q^{3n}}{(1-q^{3n})^{2}}=\frac{1}{(q^{3};q^{3})_{\infty}}\cdot\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}(-1)^{n-1}\omega(n)\,q^{3\omega(n)}.

Proof.

Consider

\displaystyle q\frac{d}{dq}(q^{3};q^{3})_{\infty}

\displaystyle=-q\cdot(q^{3};q^{3})_{\infty}\cdot\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{3nq^{3n-1}}{1-q^{3n}}

\displaystyle=-3\cdot(q^{3};q^{3})_{\infty}\cdot\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}nq^{3nm}

\displaystyle=-3\cdot(q^{3};q^{3})_{\infty}\cdot\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{q^{3m}}{(1-q^{3m})^{2}}.

On the other hand, the identity
(q;q)_{\infty}=\sum_{\mathbb{Z}}(-1)^{n}q^{\omega(n)}
implies that

\displaystyle q\frac{d}{dq}(q^{3};q^{3})_{\infty}

\displaystyle=q\frac{d}{dq}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}q^{3\omega(n)}=3\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\omega(n)q^{3\omega(n)}.

The conclusion is immediate from here.
∎

3.1.
Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Let us now recall
[
1
]
one of the representations of
T_{n}(x)
adjusted to what we defined (
4
) as
to_{n}(x)
. Namely,

\displaystyle to_{n}(x)=\frac{T_{2n+1}(\sqrt{x})}{\sqrt{x}}=(2n+1)\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{n+k}\binom{n+k+1}{2k+1}\frac{(4x)^{k}}{n+k+1}.

(6)

Next we note that
U_{2}(1,q)
is the coefficient of
x^{2}
in the expansion (see Theorem
5
)

\displaystyle\sum_{t\geq 0}U_{t}(1,q)x^{t}=\prod_{n\geq 1}\left(1+\frac{xq^{n}}{1+q^{n}+q^{2n}}\right)=\frac{1}{(q^{3};q^{3})_{\infty}}\cdot\sum_{n\geq 0}to_{n}\left(\frac{x+3}{4}\right)q^{\binom{n+1}{2}}.

Based on the expression

\displaystyle to_{n}\left(\frac{x+3}{4}\right)

\displaystyle=(2n+1)\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{n+k}\binom{n+k+1}{2k+1}\frac{(x+3)^{k}}{n+k+1}

(7)

\displaystyle=(2n+1)\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{n+k}\frac{\binom{n+k+1}{2k+1}}{n+k+1}\sum_{i=0}^{k}\binom{k}{i}3^{k-i}x^{i},

we want the coefficient of
x^{2}
in (
7
), which is

c_{n}:=(2n+1)\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{n+k}\frac{\binom{n+k+1}{2k+1}}{n+k+1}\binom{k}{2}3^{k-2}.

We claim that

c_{n}=\begin{cases}(-1)^{j-1}\frac{j(3j+1)}{2}&\text{if $n=3j$,}\\
0&\text{if $n=3j+1$,}\\
(-1)^{j-1}\frac{j(3j-1)}{2}&\text{if $n=3j-1$.}\end{cases}

This is a standard binomial coefficient identity that can easily be verified (see Example
9.2
in Appendix 1 or Example
10.1
in Appendix 2).
That means,

U_{2}(1,q)=\frac{1}{(q^{3};q^{3})_{\infty}}\cdot\left(\sum_{j\geq 0}(-1)^{j-1}\frac{j(3j+1)}{2}q^{\binom{3j+1}{2}}+\sum_{j\geq 0}(-1)^{j-1}\frac{j(3j-1)}{2}q^{\binom{3j}{2}}\right).

The proof is completed after comparing this formula with that of Lemma
3.1
.

As a companion for Theorem
2.2
, we are now in a position to prove the next result.

Theorem 3.1
.

The following congruence holds true:

MO(1,3;3n+1)\equiv 0\pmod{3}.

Proof.

We will borrow a result for
U_{3}(1,q)=\sum_{n\geq 0}MO(1;3,n)q^{n}
from Section 5:

\displaystyle U_{3}(1,q)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(q^{3};q^{3})_{\infty}}\sum_{n\geq 0}q^{\binom{n+1}{2}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{(-1)^{n+k}(2n+1)\binom{n+k+1}{2k+1}}{n+k+1}\binom{k}{3}3^{k-3}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(q^{3};q^{3})_{\infty}}\sum_{n\geq 0}q^{\binom{n+1}{2}}\sum_{k=3}^{n}(-1)^{n+k}\left(\binom{n+k+1}{2k+1}+\binom{n+k}{2k+1}\right)\binom{k}{3}3^{k-3}.

Since we are interested in the coefficients of
q^{3n+1}
in
U_{3}(1,q)
, and all the powers of
q
in
\frac{1}{(q^{3};q^{3})_{\infty}}
are multiple of
3
, it suffices to check the case when

\binom{n+1}{2}\equiv 1\pmod{3}
, that is
n=3m+1
, and show that the inner sum

\sum_{k=3}^{3m+1}(-1)^{3m+k+1}\left(\binom{3m+k+2}{2k+1}+\binom{3m+k+1}{2k+1}\right)\binom{k}{3}3^{k-3}

is divisible by
3
.
Actually, each summands is immediately divisible by
3
due to the term
3^{k-3}
unless (perhaps) when
k=3
.
Replacing this value to compute the corresponding term, we obtain

\displaystyle(-1)^{3m}\left(\binom{3m+5}{7}+\binom{3m+4}{7}\right)\binom{3}{3}3^{0}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{m}\frac{3(2m+1)}{7}\binom{3m+4}{6}

\displaystyle\equiv 0\pmod{3}.

This completes the proof.
∎

4.
A combinatorial proof of Theorem
1.1

Let us recall this alternative representations of
U_{2}(1,q)
,

\displaystyle U_{2}(1,q)

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq n_{1}<n_{2}}\frac{q^{n_{1}+n_{2}}}{(1+q^{n_{1}}+q^{2n_{1}})(1+q^{n_{2}}+q^{2n_{2}})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq n_{1}<n_{2}}\frac{q^{n_{1}+n_{2}}(1-q^{n_{1}})(1-q^{n_{2}})}{(1-q^{3n_{1}})(1-q^{3n_{2}})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{q^{3n}}{(1-q^{3n})^{2}}.

We use the following notation

n_{1}^{f_{1}}n_{2}^{f_{2}}

to denote the partition of
f_{1}n_{1}+f_{2}n_{2}
into different parts
n_{1}
and
n_{2}
wherein
n_{1}
appears
f_{1}
times and
n_{2}
appears
f_{2}
times.

Let
P_{0}(n)
denotes the number of partitions of
n
involving two different parts (each may occur any number of time)

n_{1}^{f_{1}}n_{2}^{f_{2}}

where neither
f_{1}
nor
f_{2}
is divisible by
3
, and
f_{1}\equiv f_{2}\pmod{3}
.

Let
P_{1}(n)
denotes the number of partitions of
n
involving two different parts

n_{1}^{f_{1}}n_{2}^{f_{2}}

where neither
f_{1}
nor
f_{2}
is divisible by
3
, and
f_{1}\not\equiv f_{2}\pmod{3}
.

Thus we may reformulate Theorem
1.1
as

\displaystyle P_{0}(n)-P_{1}(n)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if $n\not\equiv 0\pmod{3}$,}\\
\sigma\big(\frac{n}{3}\big)&\text{if $n\equiv 0\pmod{3}$.}\end{cases}

(8)

The analytic form of (
8
) is clearly

\sum_{n\geq 1}(P_{0}(n)-P_{1}(n))q^{n}=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{q^{3n}}{(1-q^{3n})^{2}}.

To prove this, we begin with a proposed bijection between the partitions enumerated by
P_{0}(n)
and those enumerated by
P_{1}(n)
.

First we map
P_{1}(n)
partitions into
P_{2}(n)
partitions. We begin with the partitions

n_{1}^{f_{1}}n_{2}^{f_{2}}

where
f_{1}\not\equiv f_{2}\pmod{3}
and
3
divides neither
f_{1}
nor
f_{2}
.
Without loss of generality we take
f_{2}>f_{1}
(equality is impossible because
f_{1}\not\equiv f_{2}\pmod{3}
)

n_{1}^{f_{1}}n_{2}^{f_{2}}\mapsto(n_{1}+n_{2})^{f_{1}}n_{2}^{f_{2}-f_{1}}.

Clearly the image is a
P_{2}
partition because
f_{2}-f_{1}\not\equiv f_{2}\pmod{3}
and thus must be congruent to
f_{1}
(keep in mind there are only
2
non-zero residue classes modulo
3
).

This is evidently an injection of the
P_{1}
partitions into
P_{0}
partitions.

For the reverse mapping we assume
n_{1}>n_{2}
(and
f_{1}\not\equiv f_{2}\pmod{3}
, neither
\equiv 0\pmod{3}
)

n_{1}^{f_{1}}n_{2}^{f_{2}}\mapsto(n_{1}-n_{2})^{f_{1}}n_{2}^{f_{1}+f_{2}}.

The reverse image clearly has
f_{1}\not\equiv f_{1}+f_{2}\pmod{3}
(and neither
f_{1}
nor
f_{1}+f_{2}
is congruent to
0
modulo
3
). Also both
n_{1}-n_{2}
and
n_{2}
are positive integers. However, the image is not a
P_{0}
partition precisely when
n_{1}=2n_{2}
because then there are not two different parts.

So let us examine partitions of the form

(2d)^{f_{1}}d^{f_{2}}

with
f_{1}\equiv f_{2}\pmod{3}
. The number being partitioned is

f_{1}(2d)+f_{2}d=d(2f_{1}+f_{2})=n.

Now
2f_{1}+f_{2}\equiv-f_{1}+f_{2}\equiv 0\pmod{3}
; so
3\mid n
and
d
must be a divisor of
n/3
.

Thus if
n\not\equiv 0\pmod{3}
, we have a bijection and
P_{1}(n)=P_{0}(n)
. Finally we may take
n=3\nu
. In how many ways can we solve

f_{1}(2d)+f_{2}d=3\nu,

or equivalently

2f_{1}+f_{2}=3\frac{\nu}{d}?

This is solvable for
f_{1}
provided
3\frac{\nu}{d}-f_{2}
is even. Thus
f_{2}
may be chosen from

\text{$1$ or $2$, $4$ or $5$, $7$ or $8$, $\dots$, $3\frac{\nu}{d}-2$ or $3\frac{\nu}{d}-1$}.

Therefore, there are
\frac{\nu}{d}
choices possible for
f_{2}
(and
f_{1}
is uniquely determined once
f_{2}
is chosen).
Hence for each divisor
d
of
\nu
(recall
n=3\nu
), there are
\frac{\nu}{d}
partitions in
P_{0}(n)
without an image in
P_{1}(n)
. Hence

P_{0}(3\nu)-P_{1}(3\nu)=\sum_{d\mid\nu}\frac{\nu}{d}=\sum_{d\mid\nu}d=\sigma(\nu)=\sigma\Big(\frac{n}{3}\Big)

and Theorem
1.1
is proved.

5.
A combined setup for all
U_{t}(a,q)

By adopting the convention
U_{0}(a,q):=1
, let us write a generating series for the family of functions
U_{t}(a,q)
as

F(x;a,q):=\sum_{t\geq 0}U_{t}(a,q)\,x^{t}.

Also recall (
5
),

F(x;a,q)=\prod_{m\geq 1}\frac{1}{(1+aq^{m}+q^{2m})(1-q^{m})}\cdot\sum_{n\geq 0}to_{n}\left(\frac{x+a+2}{4}\right)q^{\binom{n+1}{2}}.

As a result, we may extract the coefficient, denoted
\mathbf{[x^{t}]}
, of
x^{t}
from both sides:

U_{t}(a,q)=\prod_{m\geq 1}\frac{1}{(1+aq^{m}+q^{2m})(1-q^{m})}\cdot\sum_{n\geq 0}q^{\binom{n+1}{2}}\mathbf{[x^{t}]}\,\,to_{n}\left(\frac{x+a+2}{4}\right).

From (
7
), we know that

\displaystyle\mathbf{[x^{t}]}\,\,to_{n}\left(\frac{x+a+2}{4}\right)=(2n+1)\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{n+k}\frac{\binom{n+k+1}{2k+1}}{n+k+1}\binom{k}{t}(a+2)^{k-t}.

(9)

The case
a=-2
goes back to Andrews-Rose
[
4
, Corollary 2]
:

\displaystyle U_{t}(-2,q)=\frac{1}{(q;q)^{3}_{\infty}}\cdot\sum_{n\geq 0}(-1)^{n+t}\frac{2n+1}{2t+1}\binom{n+t}{2t}q^{\binom{n+1}{2}}.

(10)

For
a=2
, the coefficient of
x^{t}
in (
5
) is

c_{n}:=(2n+1)\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{n+k}\frac{\binom{n+k+1}{2k+1}}{n+k+1}\binom{k}{t}4^{k-t}=\binom{n+t}{2t}.

This is known as
Moriarty identity
, see Gould’s collection
[
8
, 3.161]
or Appendix 2 or Example
9.1
in Appendix 1 below.
Therefore, we have

\displaystyle U_{t}(2,q)=\frac{(q;q)_{\infty}}{(q^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}^{2}}\cdot\sum_{n\geq 0}\binom{n+t}{2t}q^{\binom{n+1}{2}}.

(11)

The case
a=-1
is:

U_{t}(-1,q)=\frac{(q^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}(q^{3};q^{3})_{\infty}}{(q;q)_{\infty}^{2}(q^{6};q^{6})_{\infty}}\cdot\sum_{n\geq 0}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{(-1)^{n+k}(2n+1)\binom{n+k+1}{2k+1}}{n+k+1}\binom{k}{t}q^{\binom{n+1}{2}}.

The case
a=1
is:

U_{t}(1,q)=\frac{1}{(q^{3};q^{3})_{\infty}}\cdot\sum_{n\geq 0}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{(-1)^{k}(2n+1)\binom{n+k+1}{2k+1}}{n+k+1}\binom{k}{t}3^{k-t}q^{\binom{n+1}{2}}.

For
a=0
, by the computations made in the Appendix 2 below, we may write

\displaystyle U_{t}(0,q)=

\displaystyle\frac{(q^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}}{(q;q)_{\infty}(q^{4};q^{4})_{\infty}}\cdot\sum_{n\geq 0}(-1)^{n+\lfloor(n+t)/2\rfloor}\binom{\lfloor(n+t)/2\rfloor}{t}q^{\binom{n+1}{2}}.

6.
Quasimodular structure for
a=\pm 2

We introduce the rational functions

Q_{m}(a,q):=\frac{q^{m}}{1+aq^{m}+q^{2m}}

so that we may write the above generating function as

F(x;a,q)=\prod_{m\geq 1}(1+Q_{m}(a,q)\,x)=\sum_{t\geq 0}U_{t}(a,q)\,x^{t}.

One also has that

-\log\left(F(-x;a,q)\right)=\sum_{r\geq 1}H_{r}(a,q)\frac{x^{r}}{r}\quad\text{where}\quad H_{r}(a,q):=\sum_{m\geq 1}Q_{m}^{r}(a,q).

6.1.
The case
a=-2

Let’s revive the umbral expansion
[
2
, Example 7.1]

\displaystyle(2r-1)!\,H_{r}(-2,q)=\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{S}^{2}-1^{2})(\mathbf{S}^{2}-2^{2})\cdots(\mathbf{S}^{2}-(r-1)^{2})

(12)

where

H_{r}(-2,q)=\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{q^{rk}}{(1-q^{k})^{2r}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbf{S}_{j}(q):=\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{k^{j}q^{k}}{1-q^{k}}.

From Andrews-Rose
[
4
]
, we know that the
U_{t}(-2,q)
lie in the ring of quasimodular forms and hence freely generated by the Eisenstein series
E_{2}=24D(\eta)/\eta,E_{4}
and
E_{6}
where

\eta(\tau)=q^{1/24}\prod_{n\geq 1}(1-q^{n})
denotes the
Dedekind eta-function
. So, the derivative
\partial_{E_{2}}
is meaningful here.

Proposition 6.1
.

It is true that

\partial_{E_{2}}U_{t}(-2,q)=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{t}\frac{U_{t-j}(-2,q)}{j^{2}\binom{2j}{j}}.

Proof.

Operating with
\partial_{E_{2}}
on
-\log\left(F(-x;-2,q)\right)=\sum_{r\geq 1}H_{r}(-2,q)\frac{x^{r}}{r}
leads to

-\sum_{t}(-x)^{t}\partial_{E_{2}}U_{t}(-2,q)=F(-x;-2,q)\sum_{r}\frac{x^{r}}{r}\partial_{E_{2}}H_{r}(-2,q)

from which we obtain (by comparing powers of
x^{t}
)

\displaystyle\partial_{E_{2}}U_{t}(-2,q)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j=1}^{t}(-1)^{j}\frac{U_{t-j}(-2,q)}{j}\cdot\partial_{E_{2}}H_{j}(-2,q)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j=1}^{t}(-1)^{j}\frac{U_{t-j}(-2,q)}{j(2j-1)!}\cdot\partial_{E_{2}}\left[\mathbf{S}\prod_{\ell=1}^{j-1}(\mathbf{S}^{2}-\ell^{2})\right]

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j=1}^{t}(-1)^{j}\frac{U_{t-j}(-2,q)}{j(2j-1)!}\cdot(-1)^{j}\cdot\frac{1^{2}2^{2}\cdots(j-1)^{2}}{24}

where the two facts
E_{2}=1-24\,\mathbf{S}_{1}
and the identity in (
12
) have been utilized. So, we infer that

\partial_{E_{2}}U_{t}(-2,q)=-\frac{1}{12}\sum_{j=1}^{t}\frac{U_{t-j}(-2,q)}{j^{2}\binom{2j}{j}}.

∎

6.2.
The case
a=2

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
D:=q\frac{d}{dq}
. If
A_{t}(q):=\sum_{n\geq 0}(2n+1)^{t}q^{\binom{n+1}{2}},B(q):=\sum_{n\geq 0}q^{\binom{n+1}{2}}
and

Definition 8.1

A holomorphic function

(1) For all

c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\Gamma,
we have the modular transformation

\phi\left(\frac{a\tau+b}{c\tau+d},\frac{z}{c\tau+d}\right)\ =\ (c\tau+d)^{k}\exp\left(2\pi i\cdot\frac{mcz^{2}}{c\tau+d}\right)\phi(\tau,z).

(2) For all integers
a,b
, we have the elliptic transformation

\phi(\tau,z+a\tau+b)\ =\ \exp\big(-2\pi im(a^{2}\tau+2az)\big)\phi(\tau,z).

(3) For each
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\rm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
, we have the Fourier expansion

(c\tau+d)^{-k}\exp\left(-2\pi i\cdot\frac{mcz^{2}}{c\tau+d}\right)\phi\left(\frac{a\tau+b}{c\tau+d},\frac{z}{c\tau+d}\right)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\sum_{r^{2}\leq 4mn}b(n,r)q^{n}u^{r};

where
b(n,r)
are complex numbers and
u:=e^{2\pi iz}
.

We also recall a result on Jacobi forms

Let
\phi
be a Jacobi form on
\Gamma
of weight

It’s our convention that

In this section, we opt to verify at least two of the binomial coefficient identities which appeared in the earlier sections of this paper. Our proof is the so-called Wilf-Zeilberger (WZ) method of automated procedure

, effective for identities of hypergeometic type.

Example 9.1
.

We have

(2n+1)\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{n+k}\frac{\binom{n+k+1}{2k+1}}{n+k+1}\binom{k}{t}4^{k-t}=\binom{n+t}{2t}.

Proof.

Define two functions

\displaystyle f_{1}(n,k):

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{n+k}(2n+1)}{n+k+1}\frac{\binom{n+k+1}{2k+1}\binom{k}{t}}{\binom{n+t}{2t}}4^{k-t},\qquad\text{and}

\displaystyle g_{1}(n,k):

\displaystyle=f_{1}(n,k)\cdot\frac{2(n+1)(k-t)(2k+1)}{(2n+1)(n+t+1)(n-k+1)}

where the second function is generated by
Zeilberger’s algorithm
. Then, one checks (using symbolic software!) that

f_{1}(n+1,k)-f_{1}(n,k)=g_{1}(n,k+1)-g_{1}(n,k)
. Next, sum both sides of the last equation over all integers
k
. The outcome is the right-hand side vanishes and hence the sum
\sum_{k}f_{1}(n,k)
is a constant (independent of
n
). Keep in mind that actually these sums have “finite support”, namely the summands are zero outside of a finite interval. To complete the argument, evaluate say at
n=t
to obtain the value
1
. That means
\sum_{k}f_{1}(n,k)=1
hence the desired claim follows.
∎

\displaystyle g_{2}(n,k):

\displaystyle=f_{2}(n,k)\cdot R(n,k)

where

\displaystyle=(-1)^{n-t}[z^{n}]\frac{z^{t}(1+z)}{(1+az+z^{2})^{t+1}}.

If
a=2
then we immediately obtain

[x^{t}]\,to_{n}\left(\frac{x+4}{4}\right)=(-1)^{n-t}[z^{n}]\frac{z^{t}}{(1+z)^{2t+1}}=\binom{n+t}{2t}.

In a similar way, for
a=-2
,

[x^{t}]\,to_{n}\left(\frac{x}{4}\right)=(-1)^{n-t}[z^{n}]\frac{z^{t}(1+z)}{(1-z)^{2t+2}}=(-1)^{n-t}\left(\binom{n+t+1}{2t+1}+\binom{n+t}{2t+1}\right).

Moreover, for
a=0
,

[x^{t}]\,to_{n}\left(\frac{x+2}{4}\right)=(-1)^{n-t}[z^{n}]\frac{z^{t}(1+z)}{(1+z^{2})^{t+1}}=\begin{cases}(-1)^{n+j}\binom{j}{t}&\text{if $n+t=2j$,}\\
(-1)^{n+j}\binom{j}{t}&\text{if $n+t=2j+1$.}\end{cases}

Finally, if
a=1
then

\displaystyle[x^{t}]\,to_{n}\left(\frac{x+3}{4}\right)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{n-t}[z^{n}]\frac{z^{t}(1+z)(1-z)^{t+1}}{(1-z^{3})^{t+1}}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{n-t}\left([z^{n-t}]h(z)+[z^{n-t-1}]h(z)\right)

where
h(z)=(\frac{1-z}{1-z^{3}})^{t+1}
.

Example 10.1
.

If
a=1
and

then

\displaystyle[x^{2}]\,to_{n}\left(\frac{x+3}{4}\right)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{n}\left([z^{n-2}]\left(\frac{1-z}{1-z^{3}}\right)^{3}+[z^{n-3}]\left(\frac{1-z}{1-z^{3}}\right)^{3}\right)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}(-1)^{j-1}\frac{j(3j+1)}{2}&\text{if $n=3j$,}\\
0&\text{if $n=3j+1$,}\\
(-1)^{j-1}\frac{j(3j-1)}{2}&\text{if $n=3j-1$.}\end{cases}

where
