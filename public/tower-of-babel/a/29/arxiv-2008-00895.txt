On second-order and fourth-order elliptic systems consisting of bulk and surface PDEs: Well-posedness, regularity theory and eigenvalue problems
Patrik Knopf, Chun Liu

Abstract
In this paper, we study second-order and fourth-order elliptic problems which include not only a Poisson equation in the bulk but also an inhomogeneous Laplace–Beltrami equation on the boundary of the domain. The bulk and the surface PDE are coupled by a boundary condition that is either of Dirichlet or Robin type. We point out that both the Dirichlet and the Robin type boundary condition can be handled simultaneously through our formalism without having to change the framework. Moreover, we investigate the eigenvalue problems associated with these second-order and fourth-order elliptic systems. We further discuss the relation between these elliptic problems and certain parabolic problems, especially the Allen–Cahn equation and the Cahn–Hilliard equation with dynamic boundary conditions. Keywords: Poisson equation, Laplace–Beltrami equation, bulk-surface coupling, Robin boundary condition, Dirichlet boundary condition, regularity theory, eigenvalue problem. Introduction

In this paper,
\Omega
denotes a bounded domain in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(with
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
d\geq 2
) whose boundary is denoted by
\Gamma:=\partial\Omega
and is supposed to have at least Lipschitz regularity. Moreover,
\mathbf{n}
denotes the outer unit normal vector field on
\Gamma
.

A second-order problem with bulk-surface coupling.

We first consider the following second-order elliptic system consisting of a Poisson equation in the bulk and an inhomogeneous Laplace–Beltrami equation on the surface:

\displaystyle-\omega\Delta u

\displaystyle=f

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in}\;\Omega,

(1.1a)

\displaystyle-\gamma\Delta_{\Gamma}v+\alpha\omega\partial_{\mathbf{n}}u

\displaystyle=g

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on}\;\Gamma,

(1.1b)

\displaystyle K\partial_{\mathbf{n}}u

\displaystyle=\alpha v-u

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on}\;\Gamma.

(1.1c)

The pair
(f,g)
stand for a generic pair of source terms whose exact properties will be specified in Section 3. Moreover,
\omega,\gamma>0
,
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and
K\geq 0
are given constants.
If
\alpha\neq 0
, the equation (
1.1a
) in the bulk (i.e., in
\Omega
) and the equation (
1.1b
) on the surface (i.e., on
\Gamma
) are coupled through the boundary condition (
1.1c
).
In the degenerate case
\alpha=0
, the subproblems ((
1.1a
),(
1.1c
)) and (
1.1b
) are completely decoupled.
If
K>0
, (
1.1c
) can be regarded as a Robin type boundary condition, which is sometimes also referred to as a Fourier type boundary condition. (It is worth mentioning that from a historical point of view, the term Fourier boundary condition would be more precise as it seems that Robin never used this type of boundary condition himself. We refer to
[
30
,
31
]
for a detailed discussion of this issue.)
In the case
K=0
, this boundary condition is to be interpreted as the Dirichlet type boundary condition

\displaystyle u|_{\Gamma}=\alpha v\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on}\;\Gamma.

By our approach, both cases
K>0
and
K=0
can be handled simultaneously.

For simplicity of the notation and to provide a cleaner presentation, we will set the constants
\omega
and
\gamma
to one in the analysis. We will see that the choice
\omega=1
does not even mean any loss of generality due to a rescaling argument. We establish the existence and uniqueness of weak solutions to (
1.1
) provided that the source terms belong to suitable spaces. Moreover, we develop a regularity theory for such solutions depending on the regularity of the domain and the source terms.

A second-order eigenvalue problem.

Associated with (
1.1
) is the following eigenvalue problem:

\displaystyle-\omega\Delta u

\displaystyle=\lambda u

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in}\;\Omega,

(1.2a)

\displaystyle-\gamma\Delta_{\Gamma}v+\alpha\omega\partial_{\mathbf{n}}u

\displaystyle=\lambda v

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on}\;\Gamma,

(1.2b)

\displaystyle K\partial_{\mathbf{n}}u

\displaystyle=\alpha v-u

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on}\;\Gamma.

(1.2c)

It can formally be regarded as a generalization of the

Wentzell eigenvalue problem

\displaystyle-\Delta u

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in}\;\Omega,

(1.3a)

\displaystyle-\gamma\Delta_{\Gamma}u+\partial_{\mathbf{n}}u

\displaystyle=\lambda u

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on}\;\Gamma,

(1.3b)

or the
Steklov eigenvalue problem

\displaystyle-\Delta u

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in}\;\Omega,

(1.4a)

\displaystyle\partial_{\mathbf{n}}u

\displaystyle=\lambda u

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on}\;\Gamma.

(1.4b)

In contrast to classical eigenvalue problems, the eigenvalue does not appear in the equation itself but in the boundary condition instead.
After its introduction in
[
43
]
, the Steklov eigenvalue problem has already been extensively investigated in the literature from many different perspectives. We refer the reader to
[
3
,
4
,
8
,
13
,
19
,
21
,
2
,
28
,
41
]
to name but a few.
There are also several works on the Wentzell eigenvalue problem of which we want to mention
[
33
,
16
,
46
,
17
]
.

To understand the connection of our system (
1.1
) to the Wentzell problem and the Steklov problem, we choose
K=0
and
\alpha=\omega^{-1/2}
for any
\omega>0
. In particular this means that

\omega^{1/2}\,u|_{\Gamma}=v
on
\Gamma
due to (
1.1c
).
Multiplying (
1.2a
) by
\omega^{-1}
and (
1.2b
) by
\omega^{-1/2}
then yields

\displaystyle-\Delta u

\displaystyle=\lambda\omega^{-1}u

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in}\;\Omega,

(1.5a)

\displaystyle-\gamma\Delta_{\Gamma}u+\partial_{\mathbf{n}}u

\displaystyle=\lambda u

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on}\;\Gamma.

(1.5b)

Now, by formally passing to the limit
\omega\to\infty
, we obtain the Wentzell problem (
1.3
) as the limit system. Choosing first
\gamma=\omega^{-1}
in (
1.5
) and passing to the formal limit
\omega\to\infty
afterwards, we arrive at the Steklov problem (
1.4
).

For the analysis of the eigenvalue problem (
1.2
) we will set the constants
\omega
and
\gamma
to one again. We prove that there exists a positive unbounded sequence of eigenvalues whose corresponding eigenfunctions form an orthonormal basis of a suitable linear subspace of
L^{2}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Gamma)
. Moreover, we conclude regularity properties for the eigenfunctions and we show that the eigenvalues can be characterized by a variational minimax principle.

A fourth-order problem with bulk-surface coupling.

We next investigate the following fourth-order elliptic problem with bulk-surface coupling:

\displaystyle\Delta^{2}\phi

\displaystyle=f

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in}\;\Omega,

(1.6a)

\displaystyle\Delta_{\Gamma}^{2}\psi-\alpha\Delta_{\Gamma}\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\phi-\beta\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\Delta\phi

\displaystyle=g

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on}\;\Gamma,

(1.6b)

\displaystyle K\,\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\phi

\displaystyle=\alpha\psi-\phi

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on}\;\Gamma,

(1.6c)

\displaystyle L\,\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\Delta\phi

\displaystyle=\beta\Delta_{\Gamma}\psi-\Delta\phi-\alpha\beta\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\phi

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on}\;\Gamma.

(1.6d)

Here,
K,L\geq 0
and
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
are given constants, and
(f,g)
denotes a pair of generic source terms whose properties will be specified in Section 5. We further suppose that
{\alpha}
and
{\beta}
satisfy

\displaystyle{\alpha}{\beta}|\Omega|+|\Gamma|\neq 0,

which will be crucial for the analysis. We will see that the fourth-order system (
1.6
) can be decoupled into two second-order systems which are both of the type (
1.1
):

\displaystyle-\Delta\phi

\displaystyle=\mu

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in}\;\Omega,

(1.7a)

\displaystyle-\Delta_{\Gamma}\psi+\alpha\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\phi

\displaystyle=\nu

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on}\;\Gamma,

(1.7b)

\displaystyle K\,\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\phi

\displaystyle=\alpha\psi-\phi

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on}\;\Gamma,

(1.7c)

\displaystyle-\Delta\mu

\displaystyle=f

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in}\;\Omega,

(1.7d)

\displaystyle-\Delta_{\Gamma}\nu+\beta\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\mu

\displaystyle=g

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on}\;\Gamma,

(1.7e)

\displaystyle L\,\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\mu

\displaystyle=\beta\nu-\mu

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on}\;\Gamma.

(1.7f)

For that reason, the theory developed for the problem (
1.1
) can be used to establish weak well-posedness and higher regularity for the system (
1.6
).

A fourth-order eigenvalue problem.

Inspired by the Steklov eigenvalue problem, also fourth-order eigenvalue problems, in which the eigenvalue appears in the boundary condition, have been extensively investigated in the literature. We refer the reader to
[
5
,
9
,
10
,
11
,
20
,
27
,
39
,
40
]
just to mention a few of them. Because of their relation to the Steklov problem, these models are sometimes referred to as
biharmonic Steklov eigenvalue problems
.

In this paper, we study the following eigenvalue problem:

\displaystyle\Delta^{2}\phi

\displaystyle=\lambda\phi

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in}\;\Omega,

(1.8a)

\displaystyle\Delta_{\Gamma}^{2}\psi-\alpha\Delta_{\Gamma}\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\phi-\beta\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\Delta\phi

\displaystyle=\lambda\psi

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on}\;\Gamma,

(1.8b)

\displaystyle K\,\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\phi

\displaystyle=\alpha\psi-\phi,

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on}\;\Gamma,

(1.8c)

\displaystyle L\,\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\Delta\phi

\displaystyle=\beta\Delta_{\Gamma}\psi-\Delta\phi-\alpha\beta\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\phi

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on}\;\Gamma.

(1.8d)

As stated above,
K,L\geq 0
and
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
are given constants with
{\alpha}{\beta}|\Omega|+|\Gamma|\neq 0
.

The novelty of this eigenvalue problem is that it comprises not only a boundary condition but a fourth-order elliptic equation on the surface. It can thus be regarded as a
bulk-surface biharmonic eigenvalue problem
.
In contrast to the fourth-order Steklov type problems mentioned above, the eigenvalue appears both in the Poisson equation (
1.8a
) in the bulk and in the Laplace–Beltrami equation (
1.8b
) on the surface but not in the coupling conditions (
1.8c
) and (
1.8d
).

As in the second-order case, we prove the existence of a positive unbounded sequence of eigenvalues whose associated eigenfunctions form an orthonormal basis of a suitable linear subspace of
(H^{1}(\Omega))^{*}\times(H^{1}(\Gamma))^{*}
(with the asterisk indicating the dual space). We further establish regularity properties for the eigenfunctions and we show that the eigenvalues can be characterized by a variational minimax principle.

Relation to elliptic and parabolic problems with dynamic boundary conditions.

We further want to mention that the problems studied in this paper are not only interesting from the perspective of pure analysis but can also be used in the treatment of parabolic problems (especially phase-field models) with dynamic boundary conditions.

The second order problem (
1.1
) is closely related to the Allen–Cahn equation subject to a dynamic boundary condition that is also of Allen–Cahn type:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u-\Delta u

\displaystyle=F^{\prime}(u)

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in}\;\Omega\times(0,T),

(1.9a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v-\Delta_{\Gamma}v+\alpha\partial_{\mathbf{n}}u

\displaystyle=G^{\prime}(u)

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on}\;\Gamma\times(0,T),

(1.9b)

\displaystyle K\partial_{\mathbf{n}}u

\displaystyle=\alpha v-u

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on}\;\Gamma\times(0,T),

(1.9c)

\displaystyle(u,v)|_{t=0}

\displaystyle=(u_{0},v_{0})

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on}\;\Omega\times\Gamma.

(1.9d)

In this phase-field model,
u=u(x,t)
and
v=v(x,t)
(the so-called phase-field variables) describe the difference in volume fractions of two different materials located in the bulk
\Omega
and on the surface
\Gamma
, respectively. This means that the functions
u
and
v
are expected to attain values close to
1
or
-1
in the regions where only one of the materials is present.
To describe phase separation processes, the bulk potential
F
and the surface potential
G
usually exhibit a double-well structure with minima at
\pm 1
and a local maximum at
0
.

In the Dirichlet case (
K=0
), the problem was investigated, for instance, in
[
42
,
12
,
14
]
. The Robin case (
K>0
) was studied in
[
15
,
37
]
. We further refer to
[
24
]
where a problem similar to (
1.9
) was discussed.

In the analysis of models like (
1.9
) a deeper understanding of the elliptic system (
1.1
) is very beneficial. Although different strategies have been used in the literature to prove well-posedness, the analysis of the second order eigenvalue problem offers a new possibility to approach systems of the type (
1.9
). Namely, the orthonormal basis of eigenfunctions to the problem (
1.2
) can be used to approximate equations like (
1.9
) by means of a Faedo–Galerkin scheme.

We also want to mention some further works on second order elliptic or parabolic problems subject to dynamic boundary conditions that are related to the elliptic problem (
1.1
). In
[
45
]
, the Laplace equation with dynamic boundary conditions of reaction-diffusion type was studied, and in
[
25
]
, nonlinear problems with parabolic dynamic boundary conditions were investigated. An overview about certain classes of elliptic and parabolic problems with dynamic boundary conditions of Wentzell type is given in
[
23
]
.

Similar to the second-order case, the fourth-order elliptic problem (
1.6
) (or its decoupled equivalent (
1.7
)) is closely related to the Cahn–Hilliard equation subject to a dynamic boundary condition that also exhibits a Cahn–Hilliard structure:

\displaystyle F^{\prime}(\phi)-\Delta\phi

\displaystyle=\mu

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in}\;\Omega\times(0,T),

(1.10a)

\displaystyle G^{\prime}(\psi)-\Delta_{\Gamma}\psi+\alpha\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\phi

\displaystyle=\nu

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on}\;\Gamma\times(0,T),

(1.10b)

\displaystyle K\,\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\phi

\displaystyle=\alpha\psi-\phi

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on}\;\Gamma\times(0,T),

(1.10c)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi-\Delta\mu

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in}\;\Omega\times(0,T),

(1.10d)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\psi-\Delta_{\Gamma}\nu+\beta\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\mu

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on}\;\Gamma\times(0,T),

(1.10e)

\displaystyle L\,\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\mu

\displaystyle=\beta\nu-\mu

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on}\;\Gamma\times(0,T),

(1.10f)

\displaystyle(\phi,\psi)|_{t=0}

\displaystyle=(\phi_{0},\psi_{0})

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on}\;\Omega\times\Gamma.

(1.10g)

As in the Allen-Cahn equation (
1.9
), the functions
\phi=\phi(x,t)
and
\psi=\psi(x,t)
denote phase-field variables, and
F
and
G
denote the bulk and the surface potential, respectively. Usually both
F
and
G
exhibit a double-well structure as described above.
Moreover,
\mu=\mu(x,t)
stands for the chemical potential in the bulk whereas
\nu=\nu(x,t)
denotes the chemical potential on the surface.

The system (
1.10
) with
K=L=0
was introduced and analyzed in
[
22
,
29
]
. In
[
38
]
, the model (
1.10
) with
K=0
and
L=\infty
(meaning
\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\mu=0
on
\Gamma\times(0,T)
) was derived by an energetic variational approach. This system was further generalized in
[
34
]
by also allowing
K>0
. The asymptotic limit
K\to 0
was also studied in
[
34
]
. The case
K=0
and
0<L<\infty
and its asymptotic limits
L\to 0
and
L\to\infty
were investigated in
[
35
]
. A similar nonlocal Cahn–Hilliard model was proposed and analyzed in
[
36
]
.

In the analysis of these models the second-order elliptic problem (
1.1
) plays a crucial role. For instance in
[
26
,
34
,
35
]
, where well-posedness of (
1.10
) was established based on a gradient-flow approach, the system (
1.1
) was essential to define the underlying inner product. However, we point out that the cases
K=0
(or
L=0
) and
K>0
(or
L>0
) always had to be handled separately, whereas in this paper we establish a formalism to approach all these cases simultaneously. We are further convinced that the orthonormal basis of eigenfunctions to the second-order problem (
1.2
) or the fourth-order problem (
1.8
) could potentially be used to discretize the system (
1.10
) by a Faedo–Galerkin scheme, which would provide a new approach to tackle such problems.

Notation and preliminaries

In this Section we introduce some notation and preliminaries that will be used throughout this paper.

\mathrm{(P1)}

In this paper,
\mathbb{N}
denotes the set of natural numbers excluding zero, and
\mathbb{N}_{0}=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
.
In general,
\Omega
will denote a bounded domain in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for some
d\in\mathbb{N}
with
d\geq 2
whose boundary
\Gamma:=\partial\Omega
has at least Lipschitz regularity. The case
d=1
is excluded as the Laplace–Beltrami operator does not make sense on a boundary consisting only of single points.

\mathrm{(P2)}

For any Banach space
X
, its norm will be denoted by
\|\cdot\|_{X}
and its dual space is denoted by
X^{*}
. For any
\varphi\in X^{*}
and
\zeta\in X
, we write

\left<\varphi\hskip 1.0pt{,}\hskip 1.0pt\zeta\right>_{X}
to denote their dual pairing. If
X
is a Hilbert space, its inner product is denoted by
(\cdot,\cdot)_{X}
.

\mathrm{(P3)}

For any
1\leq p\leq\infty
,
L^{p}(\Omega)
and

L^{p}(\Gamma)
stand for the Lebesgue spaces that are equipped with
the standard norms
\|\cdot\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}
and

\|\cdot\|_{L^{p}(\Gamma)}
. For
s\geq 0
and
1\leq p\leq\infty
, the
symbols
W^{s,p}(\Omega)
and
W^{s,p}(\Gamma)
denote the Sobolev
spaces with corresponding norms
\|\cdot\|_{W^{s,p}(\Omega)}
and

\|\cdot\|_{W^{s,p}(\Gamma)}
. Note that
W^{0,p}
can be identified
with
L^{p}
. All Lebesgue spaces and Sobolev spaces are Banach spaces
and if
p=2
, they are even Hilbert spaces. In this case we will
write
H^{s}(\Omega)=W^{s,2}(\Omega)
and

H^{s}(\Gamma)=W^{s,2}(\Gamma)
.

\mathrm{(P4)}

Let
C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
and
C^{\infty}(\Gamma)
denote the spaces of smooth functions on
\overline{\Omega}
or
\Gamma
, respectively.
For brevity, we will use the notation

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{\infty}

\displaystyle:=C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})\times C^{\infty}(\Gamma).

\mathrm{(P5)}

For any functions
\zeta\in H^{1}(\Omega)^{*}
and
\xi\in H^{1}(\Gamma)^{*}
, we define their generalized mean by the duality pairings

\displaystyle\left<\zeta\right>_{\Omega}:=\left<\zeta\hskip 1.0pt{,}\hskip 1.0pt1\right>_{H^{1}(\Omega)^{*}},\hskip 20.00003pt\left<\xi\right>_{\Gamma}:=\left<\xi\hskip 1.0pt{,}\hskip 1.0pt1\right>_{H^{1}(\Gamma)^{*}}.

If additionally
\zeta\in L^{1}(\Omega)
or
\xi\in L^{1}(\Gamma)
, the mean can be expressed as

\displaystyle\left<\zeta\right>_{\Omega}:=\frac{1}{\left|\Omega\right|}\int_{\Omega}\zeta\;\mathrm{d}x,\hskip 20.00003pt\left<\xi\right>_{\Gamma}:=\frac{1}{\left|\Gamma\right|}\int_{\Gamma}\xi\;\mathrm{d}S,

respectively.

\mathrm{(P6)}

For any integer
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, we introduce the space

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{k}

\displaystyle:=H^{k}(\Omega)\times H^{k}(\Gamma)

which is endowed with the standard inner product

\displaystyle\big((u,v)\hskip 1.0pt{,}\hskip 1.0pt(\zeta,\xi)\big)_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}:=\big(u\hskip 1.0pt{,}\hskip 1.0pt\zeta\big)_{H^{k}(\Omega)}+\big(v\hskip 1.0pt{,}\hskip 1.0pt\xi\big)_{H^{k}(\Gamma)},\hskip 10.00002pt(u,v),(\zeta,\xi)\in\mathcal{H}^{k}

and the induced norm

\displaystyle\left\|(u,v)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}:=\big((u,v)\hskip 1.0pt{,}\hskip 1.0pt(u,v)\big)^{1/2}_{\mathcal{H}^{k}},\hskip 10.00002pt(u,v)\in\mathcal{H}^{k}.

This means that

\big(\mathcal{H}^{k},\left(\cdot\hskip 1.0pt{,}\hskip 1.0pt\cdot\right)_{\mathcal{H}^{k}},\left\|\,\cdot\,\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}\big)

is a Hilbert space.

\mathrm{(P7)}

For any
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
K\geq 0
, we define the closed linear subspaces

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{K,\alpha}^{m}

\displaystyle:=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\mathcal{H}^{m},&&\text{if}\;K>0,\\
&\big\{(u,v)\in\mathcal{H}^{m}\;|\;u|_{\Gamma}=\alpha v\;\;\text{a.e. on}\;\Gamma\big\},&&\text{if}\;K=0.\end{aligned}\right.

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{\beta}^{k}

\displaystyle:=\big\{(u,v)\in\mathcal{H}^{m}\;|\;\beta\left|\Omega\right|\left<u\right>_{\Omega}+\left|\Gamma\right|\left<v\right>_{\Gamma}=0\big\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{K,\alpha,\beta}^{m}

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{H}_{K,\alpha}^{m}\cap\mathcal{V}_{\beta}^{m}.

Note that these subspaces are Hilbert spaces with respect to the inner product
\left(\cdot\hskip 1.0pt{,}\hskip 1.0pt\cdot\right)_{\mathcal{H}^{r}}
and its induced norm
\left\|\,\cdot\,\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{r}}
for
r=k
or
r=m
, respectively.

\mathrm{(P8)}

Let
K\geq 0
and
{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}
be any real numbers. We set

\displaystyle\sigma(K):=\begin{cases}K^{-1},&\text{if}\;K>0,\\
0,&\text{if}\;K=0,\end{cases}

and we define a bilinear form on
\mathcal{H}^{1}\times\mathcal{H}^{1}
by

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \big((\phi,\psi)\hskip 1.0pt{,}\hskip 1.0pt(\zeta,\xi)\big)_{K,\alpha}&:=\int_{\Omega}\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla\zeta\;\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma}\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}\xi\;\mathrm{d}S\\
&\qquad+\sigma(K)\int_{\Gamma}({\alpha}\psi-\phi)({\alpha}\xi-\zeta)\;\mathrm{d}S,\end{aligned}

for all
(\phi,\psi),(\zeta,\xi)\in\mathcal{H}^{1}
.
Moreover, we set

\displaystyle\left\|(\phi,\psi)\right\|_{K,\alpha}:=\big((\phi,\psi)\hskip 1.0pt{,}\hskip 1.0pt(\phi,\psi)\big)_{K,\alpha}^{1/2}.

Now, let
{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}
with
{\alpha}{\beta}|\Omega|+|\Gamma|\neq 0
be arbitrary.
Then the bilinear form
\left(\cdot\hskip 1.0pt{,}\hskip 1.0pt\cdot\right)_{K,\alpha}
defines an inner product on
\mathcal{W}_{K,\alpha,\beta}^{1}\,
, and
\left\|\,\cdot\,\right\|_{K,\alpha}
defines a norm on
\mathcal{W}_{K,\alpha,\beta}^{1}
that is equivalent to the norm
\left\|\,\cdot\,\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}
(see Corollary
A.2
in the appendix).

The space

\big(\mathcal{W}_{K,\alpha,\beta}^{1},\left(\cdot\hskip 1.0pt{,}\hskip 1.0pt\cdot\right)_{K,\alpha},\left\|\,\cdot\,\right\|_{K,\alpha}\big)

is a Hilbert space. Unless stated otherwise, we understand the space
\mathcal{W}_{K,\alpha,\beta}^{1}
to be standardly endowed with the inner product
\left(\cdot\hskip 1.0pt{,}\hskip 1.0pt\cdot\right)_{K,\alpha}
and the norm
\left\|\,\cdot\,\right\|_{K,\alpha}
.

\mathrm{(P9)}

For any
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, we define the space

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}^{-1}_{\beta}:=\big\{(u,v)\in(\mathcal{H}^{1})^{*}\;|\;\beta\left|\Omega\right|\left<u\right>_{\Omega}+\left|\Gamma\right|\left<v\right>_{\Gamma}=0\big\}.

This entails the chain of inclusions

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{K,\alpha,\beta}^{1}\subset\mathcal{V}^{1}_{\beta}\subset\mathcal{V}^{-1}_{\beta}\subset(\mathcal{H}^{1})^{*}\subseteq(\mathcal{H}_{K,\alpha}^{1})^{*}

for all
K\geq 0
and
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
.

3
Second-order elliptic problems with bulk-surface

coupling of Robin or Dirichlet type

In this section, we want to investigate the second-order elliptic system (
1.1
). For simplicity of the notation and to provide a cleaner presentation, we set
\omega=\gamma=1
. The system (
1.1
) is thus restated as:

\displaystyle-\Delta u

\displaystyle=f

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in}\;\Omega,

(3.1a)

\displaystyle-\Delta_{\Gamma}v+\alpha\partial_{\mathbf{n}}u

\displaystyle=g

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on}\;\Gamma,

(3.1b)

\displaystyle K\partial_{\mathbf{n}}u

\displaystyle=\alpha v-u

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on}\;\Gamma,

(3.1c)

where
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and
K\geq 0
are given constants.

In fact, the choice
\omega=1
means no loss of generality due to the following rescaling argument:
Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
,
\omega,\gamma>0
and
K\geq 0
be arbitrary and let
(u,v)
be any solution to the system (
1.1
).
It is then straightforward to check that
(\tilde{u},\tilde{v}):=\omega(u,v)
is a solution to the system (
1.1
) with
\omega
and
\gamma
being replaced by
\tilde{\omega}:=1
and
\tilde{\gamma}:=\gamma\omega^{-1}
, respectively. Hence, if the solution
(u,v)
is known, the solution
(\tilde{u},\tilde{v})
can directly be recovered.

Although it can not be justified by rescaling, we confine ourselves to investigate the problem for
\gamma=1
. We point out that the case
\gamma\neq 1
can be handled by the same analytical methods. That is, in the case
\gamma\neq 1
, the definition of the inner product
\left(\cdot\hskip 1.0pt{,}\hskip 1.0pt\cdot\right)_{K,\alpha}
would have to be modified slightly (see Remark
3.2
(d)).

As already pointed out in the introduction, for
K>0
, the coupling equation (
3.1c
) can be regarded as the
Robin type boundary condition

\displaystyle\partial_{\mathbf{n}}u|_{\Gamma}=\frac{1}{K}\big(\alpha v-u\big)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{a.e. on $\Gamma$}.

(3.2)

For
K=0
, (
3.1c
) is to be interpreted as the
Dirichlet type boundary condition

\displaystyle u|_{\Gamma}=\alpha v\hskip 10.00002pt\text{a.e. on $\Gamma$}.

(3.3)

Our approach allows to handle both cases simultaneously.

We first consider the system (
3.1
) formally and we assume that the functions
u
,
v
,
f
and
g
are sufficiently regular. After testing (
3.1a
) and (
3.1b
) with test functions
\zeta
and
\xi
, respectively, integration by parts leads to the equation

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\nabla\zeta\;\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma}\nabla_{\Gamma}v\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}\xi\;\mathrm{d}S+\int_{\Gamma}\partial_{\mathbf{n}}u(\alpha\xi-\zeta)\;\mathrm{d}S\\[4.30554pt]
&\quad=\int_{\Omega}f\zeta\;\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma}g\xi\;\mathrm{d}S.\end{aligned}

(3.4)

Invoking the boundary condition (
3.1c
), we find that

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}\partial_{\mathbf{n}}u(\alpha\xi-\zeta)\;\mathrm{d}S=\sigma(K)\int_{\Gamma}(\alpha v-u)(\alpha\xi-\zeta)\;\mathrm{d}S.

Hence, in view of (

P8

), the equation (
3.4
) can be expressed as

\displaystyle\big((u,v)\hskip 1.0pt{,}\hskip 1.0pt(\zeta,\xi)\big)_{K,\alpha}=\big((f,g)\hskip 1.0pt{,}\hskip 1.0pt(\zeta,\xi)\big)_{\mathcal{H}^{0}}=\big\langle(f,g)\hskip 1.0pt{,}\hskip 1.0pt(\zeta,\xi)\big\rangle_{\mathcal{H}^{1}_{K,\alpha}}.

This motivates the following definition.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
K\geq 0
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
be arbitrary, let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain
and let
(f,g)\in\mathcal{V}^{-1}_{\alpha}
be arbitrary.

Then a pair
(u,v)\in\mathcal{H}^{1}_{K,\alpha}
is called a weak solution of the system (
3.1
) if the weak formulation

\displaystyle\big((u,v)\hskip 1.0pt{,}\hskip 1.0pt(\zeta,\xi)\big)_{K,\alpha}=\big\langle(f,g)\hskip 1.0pt{,}\hskip 1.0pt(\zeta,\xi)\big\rangle_{\mathcal{H}^{1}_{K,\alpha}}

(3.5)

is satisfied for all test functions
(\zeta,\xi)\in\mathcal{H}^{1}_{K,\alpha}
.

Remark 3.2
.

\mathrm{(a)}

Suppose that the functions
(u,v)\in\mathcal{H}^{1}_{K,\alpha}
and
(f,g)\in\mathcal{V}^{-1}_{\alpha}
satisfy the weak formulation (
3.5
). Choosing the test functions
(\zeta,\xi)=(\alpha,1)
in (
3.5
), we obtain the compatibility condition

\displaystyle\alpha\left|\Omega\right|\left<f\right>_{\Omega}+\left|\Gamma\right|\left<g\right>_{\Gamma}=0.

(3.6)

For that reason, this constraint is incorporated in the space of admissible source terms
\mathcal{V}^{-1}_{\alpha}
.
Moreover, in the case
K>0
, we may choose
(\zeta,\xi)=(1,0)
and
(\zeta,\xi)=(0,1)
. This leads to

\displaystyle-\left|\Omega\right|\left<f\right>_{\Omega}=\frac{1}{K}\int_{\Gamma}(\alpha v-u)\;\mathrm{d}S,\hskip 10.00002pt\left|\Gamma\right|\left<g\right>_{\Gamma}=\alpha\,\frac{1}{K}\int_{\Gamma}(\alpha v-u)\;\mathrm{d}S

(3.7)

if
K>0
.

\mathrm{(b)}

Suppose that
(u,v)\in\mathcal{H}^{1}_{K,\alpha}
is a weak solution of the system (
3.1
) to the source terms
(f,g)\in\mathcal{V}^{-1}_{\alpha}
. One can easily see that then the pair

(u+\alpha c,v+c)\in\mathcal{H}^{1}_{K,\alpha}

is also a weak solution to the source terms
(f,g)
for any constant
c\in\mathbb{R}
.
Hence, in order to discuss unique weak solutions, this constant
c
needs to be fixed. This can be done, for instance, by demanding that
(u,v)\in\mathcal{W}_{K,\alpha,\beta}^{1}
for some suitable
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
.

\mathrm{(c)}

We want to mention that a second-order elliptic equation similar to ours has been investigated in
[
18
]
. The system studied there reads as follows:

\displaystyle-\Delta u+u

\displaystyle=f

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in}\;\Omega,

(3.8a)

\displaystyle-\Delta_{\Gamma}v+v+\partial_{\mathbf{n}}u

\displaystyle=g

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on}\;\Gamma,

(3.8b)

\displaystyle\partial_{\mathbf{n}}u

\displaystyle=\beta v-\alpha u

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on}\;\Gamma,

(3.8c)

where
\alpha
and
\beta
are positive constants. Although we will use similar techniques to tackle the problem (
3.1
), it is not possible to just resort to the results established in
[
18
]
.
For instance, because of the additional terms “
+u
” in (
3.8a
) and “
+v
” in (
3.8b
), there is no compatibility condition (such as (
3.6
) for our model).

\mathrm{(d)}

To investigate the system (
1.1
) with
\gamma\neq 1
, the inner product
\left(\cdot\hskip 1.0pt{,}\hskip 1.0pt\cdot\right)_{K,\alpha}
would have to be replaced by

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \big((\phi,\psi)\hskip 1.0pt{,}\hskip 1.0pt(\zeta,\xi)\big)_{K,{\alpha},\gamma}&:=\int_{\Omega}\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla\zeta\;\mathrm{d}x+\gamma\int_{\Gamma}\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}\xi\;\mathrm{d}S\\
&\qquad+\sigma(K)\int_{\Gamma}({\alpha}\psi-\phi)({\alpha}\xi-\zeta)\;\mathrm{d}S,\end{aligned}

for all
(\phi,\psi),(\zeta,\xi)\in\mathcal{H}^{1}
. However, as
\gamma
is just a positive constant, this modification would not have any crucial impact on the analysis. Thus, the choice
\gamma=1
is not a real loss of generality.

We now intend to establish existence and uniqueness as well as regularity results for weak solutions of the system (
3.1
). In view of Remark
3.2
(b), we require that the weak solution belongs to
\mathcal{W}^{1}_{K,\alpha,\beta}
for any given
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
with
{\alpha}{\beta}|\Omega|+|\Gamma|\neq 0
. This is stated by the following theorem.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
K\geq 0
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
be arbitrary and let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain. Then the following holds:

\mathrm{(a)}

For any
{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}
with
{\alpha}{\beta}|\Omega|+|\Gamma|\neq 0
and any pair of source terms
(f,g)\in\mathcal{V}^{-1}_{\alpha}
, there exists a unique weak solution
(u_{(f,g)},v_{(f,g)})\in\mathcal{W}_{K,\alpha,\beta}^{1}
of the system (
3.1
).

This means, we can define a solution operator

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\mathcal{S}_{K,\alpha,\beta}=\big(\mathcal{S}_{K,\alpha,\beta}^{\Omega},\mathcal{S}_{K,\alpha,\beta}^{\Gamma}\big):\mathcal{V}^{-1}_{\alpha}\to\mathcal{W}_{K,\alpha,\beta}^{1}\subset\mathcal{V}^{-1}_{\beta},\\
&\mathcal{S}_{K,\alpha,\beta}(f,g):=(u_{(f,g)},v_{(f,g)})\end{aligned}

(3.9)

mapping any pair of source terms
(f,g)\in\mathcal{V}^{-1}_{\alpha}
onto the corresponding weak solution
(u_{(f,g)},v_{(f,g)})\in\mathcal{W}_{K,\alpha,\beta}^{1}
of the system (
3.1
).

Moreover, it holds that
\mathcal{S}_{K,\alpha,\beta}
is injective and continuous with

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{S}_{K,\alpha,\beta}(f,g)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}\leq C\left\|(f,g)\right\|_{(\mathcal{H}^{1}_{K,\alpha})^{*}}

(3.10)

for a constant
C\geq 0
depending only on
\Omega
,
K
,
\alpha
and
\beta
.

\mathrm{(b)}

Suppose that
\Omega
is of class
C^{k+2}
and that
(f,g)\in\mathcal{V}^{k}_{\alpha}
for any
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
Then it holds that
\mathcal{S}_{K,\alpha,\beta}(f,g)\in\mathcal{H}^{k+2}_{K,\alpha}
with

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{S}_{K,\alpha,\beta}(f,g)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{k+2}}\leq C\left\|(f,g)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}

(3.11)

for a constant
C\geq 0
depending only on
\Omega
,
K
,
\alpha
,
\beta
and
k
.

This means that
\mathcal{S}_{K,\alpha,\beta}(f,g)
is a strong solution of the system (
3.1
), i.e., the equations of (
3.1
) are satisfied (at least) almost everywhere in
\Omega
and on
\Gamma
, respectively.

\mathrm{(c)}

Suppose that
\Omega
is of class
C^{\infty}
and that
(f,g)\in\mathcal{V}_{\alpha}^{m}
for every
m\in\mathbb{N}
.
Then it holds that
\mathcal{S}_{K,\alpha,\beta}(f,g)\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
.

In this proof, let
C>0
denote generic constants depending only on
\Omega
,
K
,
\alpha
and
\beta
.

Proof of
\mathrm{(a)}
.

Recall that
\mathcal{W}^{1}_{K,\alpha,\beta}
is standardly endowed with the inner product
\left(\cdot\hskip 1.0pt{,}\hskip 1.0pt\cdot\right)_{K,\alpha}
and its induced norm
\left\|\,\cdot\,\right\|_{K,\alpha}
,
and that

\displaystyle(f,g)\in\mathcal{V}^{-1}_{\alpha}\subset(\mathcal{H}^{1}_{K,\alpha})^{*}.

Invoking Corollary
A.2
, we obtain the estimate

\displaystyle\big\langle(f,g)\hskip 1.0pt{,}\hskip 1.0pt(\bar{\zeta},\bar{\xi})\big\rangle_{\mathcal{H}^{1}_{K,\alpha}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|(f,g)\right\|_{(\mathcal{H}^{1}_{K,\alpha})^{*}}\left\|(\bar{\zeta},\bar{\xi})\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\|(f,g)\right\|_{(\mathcal{H}^{1}_{K,\alpha})^{*}}\left\|(\bar{\zeta},\bar{\xi})\right\|_{K,\alpha}

(3.12)

for all
(\bar{\zeta},\bar{\xi})\in\mathcal{W}^{1}_{K,\alpha,\beta}
.
This means that the mapping

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}^{1}_{K,\alpha,\beta}\ni(\bar{\zeta},\bar{\xi})\mapsto\big\langle(f,g)\hskip 1.0pt{,}\hskip 1.0pt(\bar{\zeta},\bar{\xi})\big\rangle_{\mathcal{H}^{1}_{K,\alpha}}\in\mathbb{R}

defines a continuous linear functional which thus belongs to
(\mathcal{W}^{1}_{K,\alpha,\beta})^{*}
.
Hence, the Lax-Milgram theorem implies the existence of a unique pair
(u_{(f,g)},v_{(f,g)})\in\mathcal{W}^{1}_{K,\alpha,\beta}
such that

\displaystyle\big((u_{(f,g)},v_{(f,g)})\hskip 1.0pt{,}\hskip 1.0pt(\bar{\zeta},\bar{\xi})\big)_{K,\alpha,\beta}=\big\langle(f,g)\hskip 1.0pt{,}\hskip 1.0pt(\bar{\zeta},\bar{\xi})\big\rangle_{\mathcal{H}^{1}_{K,\alpha}}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all}\;(\bar{\zeta},\bar{\xi})\in\mathcal{W}^{1}_{K,\alpha,\beta}.

(3.13)

It remains to show that (
3.13
) holds true for all test functions in
\mathcal{H}^{1}_{K,\alpha}
. To this end, let
(\zeta,\xi)\in\mathcal{H}^{1}_{K,\alpha}
be arbitrary. We choose

\displaystyle\bar{\zeta}:=\zeta-\beta c\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\bar{\xi}:=\xi-c\hskip 10.00002pt\text{where}\hskip 10.00002ptc:=\frac{\beta\int_{\Omega}\zeta\;\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma}\xi\;\mathrm{d}S}{\beta^{2}\left|\Omega\right|+\left|\Gamma\right|}.

By this construction, we have
(\bar{\zeta},\bar{\xi})\in\mathcal{H}^{1}_{K,\alpha}
with

\displaystyle\beta\int_{\Omega}\bar{\zeta}\;\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma}\bar{\xi}\;\mathrm{d}S=\beta\int_{\Omega}\zeta\;\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma}\xi\;\mathrm{d}S-(\beta^{2}\left|\Omega\right|+\left|\Gamma\right|)c=0.

This means that
(\bar{\zeta},\bar{\xi})\in\mathcal{W}^{1}_{K,\alpha,\beta}
. Plugging
(\bar{\zeta},\bar{\xi})
into (
3.13
) we observe that the constant terms cancel out. Hence, since
(\zeta,\xi)\in\mathcal{H}^{1}_{K,\alpha}
was arbitrary, we conclude that
(
3.13
) holds true for all test functions
(\zeta,\xi)\in\mathcal{H}^{1}_{K,\alpha}
. This means that
(u_{(f,g)},v_{(f,g)})
is the unique weak solution of the system (
3.1
) in the space
\mathcal{W}^{1}_{K,\alpha,\beta}
.
In particular, the solution operator
\mathcal{S}_{K,\alpha,\beta}
is well-defined.

Testing the weak formulation (
3.5
) with
(\zeta,\xi)=(u_{(f,g)},v_{(f,g)})
, and using the estimate (
3
) as well as Young’s inequality, we conclude that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \left\|(u_{(f,g)},v_{(f,g)})\right\|_{K,\alpha}^{2}&=\big\langle(f,g)\hskip 1.0pt{,}\hskip 1.0pt(u_{(f,g)},v_{(f,g)})\big\rangle_{\mathcal{H}^{1}_{K,\alpha}}\\
&\leq C\left\|(f,g)\right\|_{(\mathcal{H}^{1}_{K,\alpha})^{*}}\left\|(u_{(f,g)},v_{(f,g)})\right\|_{{K,\alpha}}\end{aligned}

(3.14)

which proves (
3.10
). Thus, (a) is established.

In the following we write
(u,v):=\mathcal{S}_{K,\alpha,\beta}(f,g)
for brevity. The generic constants denoted by
C
may now also depend on
k
.

Proof of
\mathrm{(b)}
in the case
K>0
.

We first prove the assertion for
k=0
. Fixing
\zeta=0
the weak formulation (
3.5
) reduces to

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}\nabla_{\Gamma}v\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}\xi\;\mathrm{d}S=\int_{\Gamma}g\xi-\frac{\alpha}{K}(\alpha v-u)\xi\;\mathrm{d}S,\hskip 20.00003pt\xi\in H^{1}(\Gamma).

This means that
v
is a weak solution of the elliptic equation

\displaystyle-\Delta_{\Gamma}v=G\;\;\text{on}\;\Gamma\hskip 20.00003pt\text{with}\hskip 20.00003ptG:=g-\frac{1}{K}\alpha(\alpha v-u).

As
u\in H^{1}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow H^{1/2}(\Gamma)
, we know that
G\in L^{2}(\Gamma)
. Hence, we can apply regularity theory for elliptic equations on submanifolds (see, e.g.,
[
44
, s. 5, Thm 1.3]
and recall that
\Gamma
is a compact submanifold of class
C^{2}
without boundary) to infer that
v\in H^{2}(\Gamma)
with

\displaystyle\left\|v\right\|_{H^{2}(\Gamma)}\leq C\left\|G\right\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}+C\left\|v\right\|_{H^{1}(\Gamma)}\leq C\left\|g\right\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}+C\left\|(u,v)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}.

Proceeding as in (
3.14
) and using the equivalence of the norms
\left\|\,\cdot\,\right\|_{K,\alpha}
and
\left\|\,\cdot\,\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}
(see Corollary
A.2
), we conclude that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \left\|(u,v)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}\leq C\left\|(u,v)\right\|_{K,\alpha}\leq C\left\|(f,g)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{0}}\end{aligned}

(3.15)

and thus,

\displaystyle\left\|v\right\|_{H^{2}(\Gamma)}\leq C\left\|(f,g)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{0}}.

(3.16)

Now, we choose
\xi=0
in (
3.5
). This leads to

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\nabla\zeta\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}f\zeta\;\mathrm{d}x+\sigma(K)\int_{\Gamma}(\alpha v-u)\zeta\;\mathrm{d}S,\hskip 20.00003pt\zeta\in H^{1}(\Omega).

This means that
u
is a weak solution to the Poisson-Neumann problem

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta u&=f&&\text{in}\;\Omega,\\
\partial_{\mathbf{n}}u&=F&&\text{on}\;\Gamma,\\
\end{aligned}\right.\hskip 20.00003pt\text{with}\hskip 20.00003ptF:=\frac{1}{K}(\alpha v-u).

From
u\in H^{1}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow H^{1/2}(\Gamma)
and (
3.7
), it follows that

\displaystyle F\in H^{1/2}(\Gamma)\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and}\hskip 20.00003pt\int_{\Gamma}F\;\mathrm{d}S=-\int_{\Omega}f\;\mathrm{d}x.

This allows us to apply regularity theory for Poisson’s equation with inhomogeneous Neumann boundary condition (see, e.g.,
[
44
, s. 5, Prop. 7.7]
) to infer that
u\in H^{2}(\Omega)
with

\displaystyle\left\|u\right\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+C\left\|u\right\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+C\left\|F\right\|_{H^{1/2}(\Gamma)}.

Using the continuous embeddings
H^{1}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow H^{1/2}(\Gamma)
and
H^{1}(\Gamma)\hookrightarrow H^{1/2}(\Gamma)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\|F\right\|_{H^{1/2}(\Gamma)}\leq C\left\|u\right\|_{H^{1/2}(\Gamma)}+C\left\|v\right\|_{H^{1/2}(\Gamma)}\leq C\left\|u\right\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+C\left\|v\right\|_{H^{1}(\Gamma)},

and thus,

\displaystyle\left\|u\right\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+C\left\|u\right\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+C\left\|v\right\|_{H^{1}(\Gamma)}.

(3.17)

Combining (
3.15
), (
3.16
) and (
3.17
), we eventually conclude that

\displaystyle\left\|(u,v)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{2}}\leq C\left\|(f,g)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{0}}.

This proves the assertion if
k=0
.

The result for
k>0
can be established inductively as the regularity results cited above hold true for any integer
k\geq 0
. Assuming that
(u,v)\in\mathcal{V}_{\alpha}^{k}
is already established for some
k\geq 0
, we can proceed analogously to the case
k=0
to conclude that
(u,v)\in\mathcal{H}^{k+2}
with

\displaystyle\left\|(u,v)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{k+2}}\leq C\left\|(f,g)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}.

This means that (b) is established if
K>0
.

Proof of
\mathrm{(b)}
in the case
K=0
.
As in the case
K>0
, we first prove the assertion for
k=0
. Choosing an arbitrary test function
\zeta\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
and fixing
\xi=0
, it obviously holds that
(\zeta,\xi)\in\mathcal{H}^{1}_{K,\alpha}
since
\zeta|_{\Gamma}=0=\alpha\xi
is satisfied almost everywhere on
\Gamma
. Plugging
(\zeta,\xi)
into the weak formulation (
3.5
), we infer that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\nabla\zeta\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}f\zeta\;\mathrm{d}x.

In particular, as
\zeta\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
was arbitrary, this holds true for all test functions
\zeta\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
.
This implies that the distributional derivative
\Delta u
belongs to
L^{2}(\Omega)
and satisfies

\displaystyle-\Delta u=f\hskip 10.00002pt\text{a.e. in}\;\Omega.

We further know that
u|_{\Gamma}=\alpha v\in H^{1}(\Gamma)
. Hence, we can apply elliptic regularity theory for the Poisson–Dirichlet problem (see, e.g.,
[
7
, Thm. 3.2]
or
[
15
, Thm. A.2]
) to conclude that
u\in H^{3/2}(\Omega)
with

\displaystyle\left\|u\right\|_{H^{3/2}(\Omega)}\leq C\big(\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\left\|v\right\|_{H^{1}(\Gamma)}\big).

(3.18)

Now, since
\Delta u\in L^{2}(\Omega)
and
u\in H^{3/2}(\Omega)
, we can use a variant of the elliptic trace theorem (see, e.g.,
[
7
, Thm. 2.27]
or
[
15
, Thm. A.1]
) to conclude that
\partial_{\mathbf{n}}u\in L^{2}(\Gamma)
with

\displaystyle\left\|\partial_{\mathbf{n}}u\right\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}\leq C\left\|u\right\|_{H^{3/2}(\Omega)}.

(3.19)

Consequently, integration by parts gives

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\nabla\zeta\;\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}f\zeta\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Gamma}\partial_{\mathbf{n}}u\zeta\;\mathrm{d}S\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $\zeta\in H^{1}(\Omega)$}.

(3.20)

Let now
\xi\in H^{1}(\Gamma)
be arbitrary. According to the inverse trace theorem (see, e.g.,
[
32
, Thm. 4.2.3]
) there exists a function
\bar{\xi}\in H^{3/2}(\Omega)
such that
\bar{\xi}|_{\Gamma}=\xi
. We choose
\zeta=\alpha^{-1}\bar{\xi}
and thus
(\zeta,\xi)\in\mathcal{H}^{1}_{K,\alpha}
. Plugging this pair of test functions into the weak formulation (
3.5
) and using the identity (
3.20
), we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}\nabla_{\Gamma}v\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}\xi\;\mathrm{d}S=\int_{\Gamma}(g-\alpha\partial_{\mathbf{n}}u)\xi\;\mathrm{d}S.

As
\xi\in H^{1}(\Gamma)
was arbitrary, this implies that
v
is a weak solution of the elliptic equation

\displaystyle-\Delta_{\Gamma}v=G\;\;\text{on}\;\Gamma\hskip 20.00003pt\text{with}\hskip 20.00003ptG:=g-\alpha\partial_{\mathbf{n}}u.

Since
G\in L^{2}(\Gamma)
, we can apply regularity theory for elliptic equations on submanifolds (see, e.g.,
[
44
, s. 5, Thm 1.3]
and recall that
\Gamma
is a compact submanifold of class
C^{2}
without boundary) to conclude that
v\in H^{2}(\Gamma)
with

\displaystyle\left\|v\right\|_{H^{2}(\Gamma)}\leq C\left\|G\right\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}\leq C\left\|g\right\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}+C\left\|\partial_{\mathbf{n}}u\right\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}.

Using the estimate (
3.15
) (which obviously holds true for
K=0
), (
3.18
) and (
3.19
), we thus get

\displaystyle\left\|v\right\|_{H^{2}(\Gamma)}\leq C\left\|(f,g)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{0}}.

(3.21)

As
u|_{\Gamma}=\alpha v
almost everywhere on
\Gamma
, we further deduce that
u|_{\Gamma}\in H^{2}(\Gamma)
. Recalling that
-\Delta u=f
almost everywhere in
\Omega
, and invoking
elliptic regularity theory for the Poisson–Dirichlet problem (see, e.g.,
[
7
, Thm. 3.2]
or
[
15
, Thm. A.2]
), we eventually conclude that
u\in H^{2}(\Omega)
with

\displaystyle\left\|u\right\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\big(\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\left\|v\right\|_{H^{2}(\Gamma)}\big)\leq\left\|(f,g)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{0}}.

(3.22)

Hence, in combination with (
3.21
), the estimate

\displaystyle\left\|(u,v)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{2}}\leq C\left\|(f,g)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{0}}

directly follows.
This proves the assertion if
k=0
.

The result for
k>0
can be established inductively as the regularity results cited above hold true for any integer
k\geq 0
.
Assuming that
(u,v)\in\mathcal{W}^{k}_{K,\alpha,\beta}
is already established for some
k\geq 0
, we can proceed analogously to the case
k=0
to conclude that
(u,v)\in\mathcal{H}^{k+2}
with

\displaystyle\left\|(u,v)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{k+2}}\leq C\left\|(f,g)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}.

This means that (b) is established if
K=0
.

In summary, this completes the proof of (b).

Proof of
\mathrm{(c)}
.
The claim follows by a simple induction exploiting Sobolev’s embedding theorem.

Hence, the proof of Theorem
3.3
is complete.
∎

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
K\geq 0
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
be arbitrary, let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain, and let
\mathcal{S}_{K,\alpha,\alpha}
denote the solution operator that was introduced in Theorem
3.3
(a) (with
\beta:=\alpha
). Then the operator

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{K,\alpha,\alpha}^{0}:=\mathcal{S}_{K,\alpha,\alpha}|_{\mathcal{V}^{0}_{\alpha}}:\mathcal{V}^{0}_{\alpha}\to\mathcal{V}^{0}_{\alpha}

(3.23)

has the following properties:

\mathrm{(a)}

\mathcal{S}_{K,\alpha,\alpha}^{0}
is linear, continuous and compact.

\mathrm{(b)}

\mathcal{S}_{K,\alpha,\alpha}^{0}
is injective and thus, it holds that
\textnormal{ker}(\mathcal{S}_{K,\alpha,\alpha}^{0})=\{(0,0)\}
.

\mathrm{(c)}

\mathcal{S}_{K,\alpha,\alpha}^{0}
is self-adjoint with respect to the inner product
\left(\cdot\hskip 1.0pt{,}\hskip 1.0pt\cdot\right)_{\mathcal{H}^{0}}
on
\mathcal{V}^{0}_{\alpha}
.

Proof of
\mathrm{(a)}
.
It directly follows from Theorem
3.3
(a) that the operator
\mathcal{S}_{K,\alpha,\alpha}^{0}
is well-defined and linear. Let now
(f,g)\in\mathcal{V}^{0}_{\alpha}
be arbitrary. Testing the weak formulation (
3.5
) written for
(u,v)=\mathcal{S}_{K,\alpha,\alpha}^{0}(f,g)
with
(\zeta,\xi)=\mathcal{S}_{K,\alpha,\alpha}^{0}(f,g)
, and using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{S}_{K,\alpha,\alpha}^{0}(f,g)\right\|_{K,\alpha}^{2}=\big((f,g)\hskip 1.0pt{,}\hskip 1.0pt\mathcal{S}_{K,\alpha,\alpha}^{0}(f,g)\big)_{\mathcal{H}^{0}}\leq\left\|(f,g)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{0}}\left\|\mathcal{S}_{K,\alpha,\alpha}^{0}(f,g)\right\|_{K,\alpha}.

and the continuity of the operator
\mathcal{S}_{K,\alpha,\alpha}^{0}
follows immediately.
Since

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{K,\alpha,\alpha}^{0}(\mathcal{V}^{0}_{\alpha})\subset\mathcal{W}^{1}_{K,\alpha,\alpha},

and as the embedding
\mathcal{W}^{1}_{K,\alpha,\alpha}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{V}^{0}_{\alpha}
is compact, we conclude that
\mathcal{S}_{K,\alpha,\alpha}^{0}
is a compact operator.

Proof of
\mathrm{(b)}
.
Theorem
3.3
(a) states that the operator
\mathcal{S}_{K,\alpha,\alpha}^{0}
is injective and thus, its kernel is trivial.

Proof of
\mathrm{(c)}
.
Let
(f_{1},g_{1}),(f_{2},g_{2})\in\mathcal{V}^{0}_{\alpha}
be arbitrary.
Recalling the definition of the operator
\mathcal{S}_{K,\alpha,\alpha}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{S}_{K,\alpha,\alpha}(f_{1},g_{1})\hskip 1.0pt{,}\hskip 1.0pt(f_{2},g_{2})\right)_{\mathcal{H}^{0}}=\left(\mathcal{S}_{K,\alpha,\alpha}(f_{1},g_{1})\hskip 1.0pt{,}\hskip 1.0pt\mathcal{S}_{K,\alpha,\alpha}(f_{2},g_{2})\right)_{K,\alpha}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt=\left((f_{1},g_{1})\hskip 1.0pt{,}\hskip 1.0pt\mathcal{S}_{K,\alpha,\alpha}(f_{2},g_{2})\right)_{\mathcal{H}^{0}},

which proves the assertion of (c).

Thus, the proof is complete.
∎

Corollary 3.5
.

Let
K\geq 0
and
{\alpha},{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}
with
{\alpha}{\beta}|\Omega|+|\Gamma|\neq 0
be arbitrary, and let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain.
Then the bilinear form

\displaystyle\left(\cdot\hskip 1.0pt{,}\hskip 1.0pt\cdot\right)_{K,\alpha,\beta,\ast}:\mathcal{V}^{-1}_{\alpha}\times\mathcal{V}^{-1}_{\alpha}\to\mathbb{R},

\displaystyle\left((f_{1},g_{1})\hskip 1.0pt{,}\hskip 1.0pt(f_{2},g_{2})\right)_{K,\alpha,\beta,\ast}:=\big(\mathcal{S}_{K,\alpha,\beta}(f_{1},g_{2})\hskip 1.0pt{,}\hskip 1.0pt\mathcal{S}_{K,\alpha,\beta}(f_{1},g_{2})\big)_{K,\alpha}

defines an inner product on the space
\mathcal{V}^{-1}_{\alpha}
. The induced norm

\displaystyle\left\|(f,g)\right\|_{K,\alpha,\beta,\ast}:=\left((f,g)\hskip 1.0pt{,}\hskip 1.0pt(f,g)\right)_{K,\alpha,\beta,\ast}^{1/2},\hskip 10.00002pt(f,g)\in\mathcal{V}^{-1}_{\alpha},

is equivalent to the norm
\left\|\,\cdot\,\right\|_{(\mathcal{H}^{1}_{K,\alpha})^{*}}
on
\mathcal{V}^{-1}_{\alpha}
and thus, the space

\displaystyle\big(\mathcal{V}^{-1}_{\alpha},\left(\cdot\hskip 1.0pt{,}\hskip 1.0pt\cdot\right)_{K,\alpha,\beta,\ast},\left\|\,\cdot\,\right\|_{K,\alpha,\beta,\ast}\big)

is a Hilbert space.

The mapping
\left(\cdot\hskip 1.0pt{,}\hskip 1.0pt\cdot\right)_{K,\alpha,\beta,\ast}
is obviously well-defined, bilinear and symmetric. Moreover, it holds that

\displaystyle\left((f,g)\hskip 1.0pt{,}\hskip 1.0pt(f,g)\right)_{K,\alpha,\beta,\ast}\geq 0\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all}\;(f,g)\in\mathcal{V}^{-1}_{\alpha}.

Recalling that
\left(\cdot\hskip 1.0pt{,}\hskip 1.0pt\cdot\right)_{K,\alpha}
is an inner product on
\mathcal{W}^{1}_{K,\alpha,\beta}
, and that the solution operator
\mathcal{S}_{K,\alpha,\beta}
is linear and injective, we conclude that

\displaystyle\left((f,g)\hskip 1.0pt{,}\hskip 1.0pt(f,g)\right)_{K,\alpha,\beta,\ast}=0\hskip 10.00002pt\Leftrightarrow\hskip 10.00002pt\mathcal{S}_{K,\alpha,\beta}(f,g)=(0,0)\hskip 10.00002pt\Leftrightarrow\hskip 10.00002pt(f,g)=(0,0).

This means that
\left(\cdot\hskip 1.0pt{,}\hskip 1.0pt\cdot\right)_{K,\alpha,\beta,\ast}
is positive definite and thus, it defines an inner product on the space
\mathcal{V}^{-1}_{\alpha}
.

To prove the equivalence of the norms
\left\|\,\cdot\,\right\|_{K,\alpha,\beta,\ast}
and
\left\|\,\cdot\,\right\|_{(\mathcal{H}^{1}_{K,\alpha})^{*}}
, let
(f,g)\in\mathcal{V}^{-1}_{\alpha}
be arbitrary, and let
C>0
denote generic constants depending only on
\Omega
,
K
,
\alpha
and
\beta
. We first infer from Theorem
3.3
(a) and Corollary
A.2
that

\displaystyle\left\|(f,g)\right\|_{K,\alpha,\beta,\ast}=\left\|\mathcal{S}_{K,\alpha,\beta}(f,g)\right\|_{K,\alpha}\leq C\left\|\mathcal{S}_{K,\alpha,\beta}(f,g)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}\leq C\left\|(f,g)\right\|_{(\mathcal{H}^{1}_{K,\alpha})^{*}}.

Let now
(\zeta,\xi)\in\mathcal{H}^{1}_{K,\alpha}
with
\left\|(\zeta,\xi)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}\leq 1
be arbitrary. Recalling the definition of the operator
\mathcal{S}_{K,\alpha,\beta}
and using Lemma
A.1
we get

\displaystyle\big\langle(f,g)\hskip 1.0pt{,}\hskip 1.0pt(\zeta,\xi)\big\rangle_{\mathcal{H}^{1}_{K,\alpha}}=\big(\mathcal{S}_{K,\alpha,\beta}(f,g)\hskip 1.0pt{,}\hskip 1.0pt(\zeta,\xi)\big)_{K,\alpha}\leq\left\|\mathcal{S}_{K,\alpha,\beta}(f,g)\right\|_{K,\alpha}\left\|(\zeta,\xi)\right\|_{K,\alpha}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq C\left\|\mathcal{S}_{K,\alpha,\beta}(f,g)\right\|_{K,\alpha}\left\|(\zeta,\xi)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}\leq C\left\|\mathcal{S}_{K,\alpha,\beta}(f,g)\right\|_{K,\alpha}

and thus,

\displaystyle\left\|(f,g)\right\|_{(\mathcal{H}^{1}_{K,\alpha})^{*}}\leq C\left\|\mathcal{S}_{K,\alpha,\beta}(f,g)\right\|_{K,\alpha}.

This means that the equivalence of the norms is established and thus, the proof is complete.
∎

4
A second-order eigenvalue problem

For
K\geq 0
,
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, we now consider the following second-order eigenvalue problem with bulk-surface coupling of Robin/Dirichlet type:

\displaystyle-\Delta u

\displaystyle=\lambda u

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in}\;\Omega,

(4.1a)

\displaystyle-\Delta_{\Gamma}v+\alpha\partial_{\mathbf{n}}u

\displaystyle=\lambda v

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on}\;\Gamma,

(4.1b)

\displaystyle K\partial_{\mathbf{n}}u

\displaystyle=\alpha v-u

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on}\;\Gamma.

(4.1c)

We immediately notice that for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
there exists at least one weak solution of the system (
4.1
) in the space
\mathcal{W}^{1}_{K,\alpha,\alpha}
, as the pair of null functions
(u,v)=(0,0)\in\mathcal{W}^{1}_{K,\alpha,\alpha}
trivially solves the equations. However, weak solutions of (
4.1
) are generally not unique. In the following we will of course be interested in nontrivial solutions. The following proposition provides some important properties of weak solutions to the problem (
4.1
).

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
K\geq 0
,
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
be arbitrary, and let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain. Then the following holds:

\mathrm{(a)}

Let
(u,v)\in\mathcal{W}^{1}_{K,\alpha,\alpha}
be any weak solution of (
4.1
) in the sense of Definition
3.1
. Then
(u,v)
satisfies the relation

\displaystyle(u,v)=\mathcal{S}_{K,\alpha,\alpha}(\lambda u,\lambda v)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{a.e. in $\Omega$,}

(4.2)

as well as the identity

\displaystyle\left\|(u,v)\right\|_{K,\alpha}^{2}=\lambda\left\|(u,v)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{0}}^{2}.

(4.3)

\mathrm{(b)}

Suppose that
\Omega
is of class
C^{k+2}
for any
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
and let
(u,v)
be any weak solution of (
4.1
).
Then it holds that
(u,v)\in\mathcal{W}^{k+2}_{K,\alpha,\alpha}
with

\displaystyle\left\|(u,v)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{k+2}}\leq C\lambda\,\left\|(u,v)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}

for a constant
C\geq 0
depending only on
\Omega
,
K
,
\alpha
and
k
.

This means that
(u,v)
is a strong solution of the eigenvalue problem (
4.1
).

\mathrm{(c)}

Suppose that
\Omega
is of class
C^{\infty}
,
and let
(u,v)
be any weak solution.
Then it holds that
(u,v)\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
.

Let
(u,v)\in\mathcal{W}^{1}_{K,\alpha,\alpha}
be any weak solution of the system (
4.1
), and let us fix
(f,g):=(\lambda u,\lambda v)\in\mathcal{V}^{-1}_{\alpha}
. This means that
(u,v)
is a weak solution of the system (
3.1
) to the source terms
(f,g)
.
However, according to Theorem
3.3
(a),
\mathcal{S}_{K,\alpha,\alpha}\big(f,g\big)
is the unique weak solution of the problem (
3.1
) in the space
\mathcal{W}^{1}_{K,\alpha,\alpha}
to the source terms
(f,g)
.
We thus conclude that

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{K,\alpha,\alpha}\big(\lambda u,\lambda v\big)=\mathcal{S}_{K,\alpha,\alpha}\big(f,g\big)=(u,v)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{a.e. in $\Omega$},

which proves (
4.2
). In particular, this means that the theory developed in Theorem
3.3
can be applied on
(u,v)
.
Choosing the test functions
(\zeta,\xi)=(u,v)
in the weak formulation (
3.5
) written for
(f,g)=(\lambda u,\lambda v)
, we directly conclude the identity (
4.3
).
Moreover, the regularity assertions (b) and (c) are a direct consequence of the corresponding results stated in Theorem
3.3
.
∎

An eigenvalue of (
4.1
) and its corresponding eigenfunctions are defined as follows:

Definition 4.2
.

Let
K\geq 0
,
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
be arbitrary, and let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain.

We call
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
an
eigenvalue
if the system (
4.1
) possesses at least one nontrivial weak solution
(u,v)\in\mathcal{W}^{1}_{K,\alpha,\alpha}
.
In this case, the pair
(u,v)
is referred to as an
eigenfunction
to the eigenvalue
\lambda
.

We can easily see that eigenvalues must be strictly positive.

Corollary 4.3
.

Let
K\geq 0
,
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
be arbitrary, let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain,
and let
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
be an eigenvalue. Then it holds that
\lambda>0
.

We argue by contradiction and assume that
\lambda\leq 0
. Let
(u,v)
be a corresponding eigenfunction. It then follows from (
4.3
) that

\displaystyle\left\|(u,v)\right\|_{K,\alpha}^{2}=0

which directly implies that
(u,v)=(0,0)
. However, this is a contradiction since eigenfunctions are nontrivial by definition.
∎

The eigenvalues of the problem (
4.1
) and their corresponding eigenfunctions can be characterized as follows:

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
K\geq 0
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
be arbitrary, and let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain. Then the following holds:

\mathrm{(a)}

The problem (
4.1
) has countably many eigenvalues and each of them has a finite-dimensional eigenspace. Repeating each eigenvalue according to its multiplicity, we can write them as a sequence
(\lambda_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{R}
with

\displaystyle 0<\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\lambda_{3}\leq...\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and}\hskip 20.00003pt\lambda_{k}\to\infty\hskip 10.00002pt\text{as}\;k\to\infty.

\mathrm{(b)}

There exists an orthonormal basis
\big((u_{k},v_{k})\big)_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of
\mathcal{V}^{0}_{\alpha}
with respect to the inner product
\left(\cdot\hskip 1.0pt{,}\hskip 1.0pt\cdot\right)_{\mathcal{H}^{0}}
where for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the pair
(u_{k},v_{k})
is an eigenfunction to the eigenvalue
\lambda_{k}
.

In particular, any pair
(u,v)\in\mathcal{V}^{0}_{\alpha}
can be expressed as

\displaystyle(u,v)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}c_{k}\,(u_{k},v_{k})\hskip 10.00002pt\text{with}\hskip 10.00002ptc_{k}:=\big((u,v)\hskip 1.0pt{,}\hskip 1.0pt(u_{k},v_{k})\big)_{\mathcal{H}^{0}},\;\;k\in\mathbb{N}.

As the solution operator
\mathcal{S}^{0}_{K,\alpha,\alpha}:\mathcal{V}^{0}_{\alpha}\to\mathcal{V}^{0}_{\alpha}
to the problem (
3.1
) satisfies the properties established in Corollary
3.4
, the spectral theorem for compact normal operators (see, e.g.,
[
1
, s. 12.12]
) can be applied and proves all assertions. Note that the sequence of eigenvalues is strictly positive due to Corollary
4.3
.
∎

Furthermore, the eigenvalues and the corresponding eigenfunctions can be characterized by the following variational principle.

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
K\geq 0
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
be arbitrary, and let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain.
Moreover, let
(\lambda_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
denote the sequence of eigenvalues from Theorem
4.4
.
For any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let
S_{k-1}
denote the collection of all
(k-1)
-dimensional linear subspaces of
\mathcal{V}^{0}_{\alpha}
.

Then, for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the eigenvalue
\lambda_{k}
can be represented by the variational principle

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}=\underset{V\in S_{k-1}}{\max}\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}(\zeta,\xi)\in V^{\bot},\\
\left\|(\zeta,\xi)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{0}}=1\end{subarray}}{\min}\;\left\|\mathcal{S}^{0}_{K,\alpha,\alpha}(\zeta,\xi)\right\|_{K,\alpha}^{2}

The assertion follows immediately from the minimax principle for self-adjoint operators (see, e.g.,
[
6
, Thm. 6.1.2]
).

5
Fourth-order elliptic problems with bulk-surface coupling of Robin or Dirichlet type

We now consider the following fourth-order elliptic system with bulk-surface coupling of Robin/Dirichlet type and general source terms
(f,g)
:

\displaystyle\Delta^{2}\phi

\displaystyle=f

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in}\;\Omega,

(5.1a)

\displaystyle\Delta_{\Gamma}^{2}\psi-\alpha\Delta_{\Gamma}\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\phi-\beta\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\Delta\phi

\displaystyle=g

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on}\;\Gamma,

(5.1b)

\displaystyle K\,\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\phi

\displaystyle=\alpha\psi-\phi

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on}\;\Gamma,

(5.1c)

\displaystyle L\,\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\Delta\phi

\displaystyle=\beta\Delta_{\Gamma}\psi-\Delta\phi-\alpha\beta\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\phi

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on}\;\Gamma.

(5.1d)

Here
K,L\geq 0
and
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
are given constants with
{\alpha}{\beta}|\Omega|+|\Gamma|\neq 0
.

Let us first make some formal considerations.
Assuming that the solution is sufficiently regular, we can introduce the auxiliary variables

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle:=-\Delta\phi

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in}\;\;\Omega,

(5.2)

\displaystyle\nu

\displaystyle:=-\Delta_{\Gamma}\psi+\alpha\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\phi

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on}\;\;\Gamma.

(5.3)

Then the system (
5.1
) can be equivalently formulated as

\displaystyle-\Delta\phi

\displaystyle=\mu

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in}\;\Omega,

(5.4a)

\displaystyle-\Delta_{\Gamma}\psi+\alpha\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\phi

\displaystyle=\nu

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on}\;\Gamma,

(5.4b)

\displaystyle K\,\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\phi

\displaystyle=\alpha\psi-\phi

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on}\;\Gamma,

(5.4c)

\displaystyle-\Delta\mu

\displaystyle=f

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in}\;\Omega,

(5.4d)

\displaystyle-\Delta_{\Gamma}\nu+\beta\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\mu

\displaystyle=g

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on}\;\Gamma,

(5.4e)

\displaystyle L\,\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\mu

\displaystyle=\beta\nu-\mu

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on}\;\Gamma.

(5.4f)

We observe that the subsystem (
5.4d
)-(
5.4f
) decouples and that both subsystems (
5.4a
)-(
5.4c
) and (
5.4d
)-(
5.4f
) are of the same type as the second-order system (
3.1
).
Recalling the solution operator of the second order problem (
3.1
) that was introduced in Theorem
3.3
, we can express the pair
(\mu,\nu)
as

\displaystyle(\mu,\nu)=\mathcal{S}_{L,\beta,\alpha}(f,g)\in\mathcal{W}^{1}_{L,\beta,\alpha}\subset\mathcal{V}^{-1}_{\alpha}.

(5.5)

Consequently, since the pair
(\phi,\psi)
satisfies the subsystem (
5.4a
)-(
5.4c
), we infer that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\big((\phi,\psi)\hskip 1.0pt{,}\hskip 1.0pt(\zeta,\xi)\big)_{K,\alpha}=\big((\mu,\nu)\hskip 1.0pt{,}\hskip 1.0pt(\zeta,\xi)\big)_{\mathcal{H}^{0}}=\big(\mathcal{S}_{L,\beta,\alpha}(f,g)\hskip 1.0pt{,}\hskip 1.0pt(\zeta,\xi)\big)_{\mathcal{H}^{0}}\\
\end{aligned}

(5.6)

for all
(\zeta,\xi)\in\mathcal{H}^{1}_{K,\alpha}
.

This motivates the following definition.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
K,L\geq 0
and
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
with
{\alpha}{\beta}|\Omega|+|\Gamma|\neq 0
be arbitrary, let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain,
and let
(f,g)\in\mathcal{V}^{-1}_{\beta}
be arbitrary.

Then a pair
(\phi,\psi)\in\mathcal{W}^{1}_{K,\alpha,\beta}
is called a weak solution of the system (
5.1
) if the weak formulation

\displaystyle\big((\phi,\psi)\hskip 1.0pt{,}\hskip 1.0pt(\zeta,\xi)\big)_{K,\alpha}=\big(\mathcal{S}_{L,\beta,\alpha}(f,g)\hskip 1.0pt{,}\hskip 1.0pt(\zeta,\xi)\big)_{\mathcal{H}^{0}}

(5.7)

is satisfied for all test functions
(\zeta,\xi)\in\mathcal{H}^{1}_{K,\alpha}
.

In view of (
5.5
) and (
5.6
), the theory developed in Section 3 can now be used to prove well-posedness and regularity results for solutions of the system (
5.1
).

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
K,L\geq 0
and
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
with
{\alpha}{\beta}|\Omega|+|\Gamma|\neq 0
be arbitrary and let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain. Then the following holds:

\mathrm{(a)}

For any pair of source terms
(f,g)\in\mathcal{V}^{-1}_{\beta}
there exists a unique weak solution
(\phi_{(f,g)},\psi_{(f,g)})\in\mathcal{W}^{1}_{K,\alpha,\beta}
of the system (
5.1
).

This means, we can define a solution operator

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\mathcal{F}_{K,\alpha,L,\beta}=(\mathcal{F}_{K,\alpha,L,\beta}^{\Omega},\mathcal{F}_{K,\alpha,L,\beta}^{\Gamma}):\mathcal{V}^{-1}_{\beta}\to\mathcal{V}^{-1}_{\beta},\\
\quad&\mathcal{F}_{K,\alpha,L,\beta}(f,g):=(\phi_{(f,g)},\psi_{(f,g)})\end{aligned}

(5.8)

mapping any pair of source terms
(f,g)\in\mathcal{V}^{-1}_{\beta}
onto the corresponding weak solution
(\phi_{(f,g)},\psi_{(f,g)})\in\mathcal{W}^{1}_{K,\alpha,\beta}
of (
5.1
). In particular, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{K,\alpha,L,\beta}=\mathcal{S}_{K,\alpha,\beta}\circ\mathcal{S}_{L,\beta,\alpha}.

Moreover, we obtain the estimate

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{F}_{K,\alpha,L,\beta}(f,g)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}\leq C\left\|(f,g)\right\|_{(\mathcal{H}^{1}_{K,\alpha})^{*}},

(5.9)

for a constant
C\geq 0
depending only on
\Omega
,
K
,
L
,
{\alpha}
and
{\beta}
.

\mathrm{(b)}

Suppose that
\Omega
is of class
C^{k+2}
for any
k\in\{0,1\}
, and that
(f,g)\in\mathcal{V}^{-1}_{\beta}
.
Then it even holds that

\mathcal{F}_{K,\alpha,L,\beta}(f,g)\in\mathcal{W}_{K,\alpha,\beta}^{k+2}

with

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{F}_{K,\alpha,L,\beta}(f,g)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{k+2}}\leq C\left\|(f,g)\right\|_{(\mathcal{H}^{1}_{K,\alpha})^{*}}\,,

for a constant
C\geq 0
depending only on
\Omega
,
K
,
L
,
{\alpha}
,
{\beta}
and
k
.

\mathrm{(c)}

Suppose that
\Omega
is of class
C^{k+4}
, and that
(f,g)\in\mathcal{V}^{k}_{\beta}
for any
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
Then it even holds that

\mathcal{F}_{K,\alpha,L,\beta}(f,g)\in\mathcal{W}_{K,\alpha,\beta}^{k+4}

with

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{F}_{K,\alpha,L,\beta}(f,g)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{k+4}}\leq C\left\|(f,g)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{k}},

for a constant

Corollary 5.3

\mathrm{(b)}

\mathrm{(c)}

\mathrm{(a)}

6
A fourth-order eigenvalue problem

For
K,L\geq 0
,
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
with
{\alpha}{\beta}|\Omega|+|\Gamma|\neq 0
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, we now consider the following fourth-order eigenvalue problem with bulk-surface coupling of Robin/Dirichlet type:

\displaystyle\Delta^{2}\phi

\displaystyle=\lambda\phi

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in}\;\Omega,

(6.1a)

\displaystyle\Delta_{\Gamma}^{2}\psi-\alpha\Delta_{\Gamma}\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\phi-\beta\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\Delta\phi

\displaystyle=\lambda\psi

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on}\;\Gamma,

(6.1b)

\displaystyle K\,\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\phi

\displaystyle=\alpha\psi-\phi,

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on}\;\Gamma,

(6.1c)

\displaystyle L\,\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\Delta\phi

\displaystyle=\beta\Delta_{\Gamma}\psi-\Delta\phi-\alpha\beta\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\phi

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on}\;\Gamma.

(6.1d)

The existence of at least one weak solution in
\mathcal{W}^{1}_{K,\alpha,\beta}
is trivial, as the pair of null functions
(\phi,\psi)=(0,0)\in\mathcal{W}^{1}_{K,\alpha,\beta}
obviously solves the equations. In general, weak solutions of (
6.1
) are not unique, and we are of course interested in nontrivial solutions. The following proposition provides some important properties of weak solutions.

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
K,L\geq 0
,
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
with
{\alpha}{\beta}|\Omega|+|\Gamma|\neq 0
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
be arbitrary, and let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain. Then the following holds:

\mathrm{(a)}

Let
(\phi,\psi)\in\mathcal{W}^{1}_{K,\alpha,\beta}
be any weak solution of (
6.1
) in the sense of Definition
5.1
. Then
(\phi,\psi)
satisfies the relation

\displaystyle(\phi,\psi)=\mathcal{F}_{K,\alpha,L,\beta}(\lambda\phi,\lambda\psi)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{a.e. in $\Omega$,}

(6.2)

as well as the identity

\displaystyle\left\|(\phi,\psi)\right\|_{K,\alpha}=\lambda\left\|\mathcal{S}_{L,\beta,\alpha}(\phi,\psi)\right\|_{L,\beta}.

(6.3)

\mathrm{(b)}

Suppose that
\Omega
is of class
C^{k+2}
for any
k\in\{0,1\}
,
and let
(\phi,\psi)
be any weak solution of (
6.1
).
Then it holds that
(\phi,\psi)\in\mathcal{W}^{k+2}_{K,\alpha,\beta}
with

\displaystyle\left\|(\phi,\psi)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{k+2}}\leq C\lambda\,\left\|(\phi,\psi)\right\|_{(\mathcal{H}^{1}_{K,\alpha})^{*}}\,,

for a constant
C\geq 0
depending only on
\Omega
,
K
,
L
,
{\alpha}
,
{\beta}
and
k
.

\mathrm{(c)}

Suppose that
\Omega
is of class
C^{k+4}
for any
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
and let
(\phi,\psi)
be any weak solution of (
6.1
).
Then it holds that
(\phi,\psi)\in\mathcal{W}^{k+2}_{K,\alpha,\beta}
with

\displaystyle\left\|(\phi,\psi)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{k+4}}\leq C\lambda\,\left\|(\phi,\psi)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}.

for a constant
C\geq 0
depending only on
\Omega
,
K
,
L
,
{\alpha}
,
{\beta}
and
k
.

\mathrm{(d)}

Suppose that
\Omega
is of class
C^{\infty}
,
and let
(\phi,\psi)
be any weak solution of (
6.1
).
Then it holds that
(\phi,\psi)\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
.

Let
(\phi,\psi)\in\mathcal{W}^{1}_{K,\alpha,\beta}
be an arbitrary weak solution of the system (
6.1
), and let us fix
(f,g):=(\lambda\phi,\lambda\psi)\in\mathcal{V}^{-1}_{\beta}
. This means that
(\phi,\psi)
is a weak solution of the system (
5.1
) to the source terms
(f,g)
.
According to Theorem
5.2
(a),
\mathcal{F}_{K,\alpha,L,\beta}\big(f,g\big)

) in the space
\mathcal{W}^{1}_{K,\alpha,\beta}
to the source terms

.
We thus infer that

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{K,\alpha,L,\beta}\big(\lambda\phi,\lambda\psi\big)=\mathcal{F}_{K,\alpha,L,\beta}\big(f,g\big)=(\phi,\psi)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{a.e. in $\Omega$},

which verifies (

). This means that the theory developed in Theorem

is applicable for the weak solution

) written for

\displaystyle\big((\phi,\psi)\hskip 1.0pt{,}\hskip 1.0pt(\phi,\psi)\big)_{K,\alpha}

An eigenvalue of (
6.1
) and its corresponding eigenfunctions are defined as follows:

Definition 6.2
.

Let
K,L\geq 0
,
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
with
{\alpha}{\beta}|\Omega|+|\Gamma|\neq 0
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
be arbitrary, and let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain.

We call
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
an

if the system (
6.1
) possesses at least one nontrivial weak solution
(\phi,\psi)\in\mathcal{W}^{1}_{K,\alpha,\beta}
.
In this case, the pair
(\phi,\psi)
is referred to as an
eigenfunction
to the eigenvalue
\lambda
.

We immediately observe that eigenvalues must be strictly positive.

Corollary 6.3
.

Let
K,L\geq 0
,
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
with
{\alpha}{\beta}|\Omega|+|\Gamma|\neq 0
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
be arbitrary, let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain,
and let
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
be an eigenvalue. Then it holds that
\lambda>0
.

We argue by contradiction and assume that
\lambda\leq 0
. Let
(\phi,\psi)
be a corresponding eigenfunction. It then follows from (
6.3
) that

\displaystyle\left\|(\phi,\psi)\right\|_{K,\alpha}^{2}=0

which directly yields
(\phi,\psi)=(0,0)
. Since eigenfunctions are nontrivial this is a contradiction and thus, the assertion is established.
∎

The eigenvalues of the problem (
6.1
) and their corresponding eigenfunctions can be characterized as follows:

Theorem 6.4
.

and
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}

\mathrm{(a)}

\displaystyle 0<\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\lambda_{3}\leq...\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and}\hskip 20.00003pt\lambda_{k}\to\infty\hskip 10.00002pt\text{as}\;k\to\infty.

We present a Poincaré type inequality with respect to the norm
\left\|\,\cdot\,\right\|_{K,\alpha}
for functions in
\mathcal{W}^{1}_{K,\alpha,\beta}
.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
K\geq 0
and
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
with
\alpha\beta|\Omega|+|\Gamma|\neq 0
be arbitrary, and let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain
\Omega
with boundary
\Gamma
. Then there exists a constant
c_{P}>0
depending only on
K
,
\alpha
,
\beta
and
\Omega
such that

\displaystyle\left\|(u,v)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{0}}\leq c_{P}\left\|(u,v)\right\|_{K,\alpha}

(A.1)

for all pairs
(u,v)\in\mathcal{W}^{1}_{K,\alpha,\beta}
.

We prove the assertion by contradiction. Therefore, we assume that the estimate is false. Consequently, for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
there exists a pair
(u_{k},v_{k})\in\mathcal{W}^{1}_{K,\alpha,\beta}
such that

\displaystyle\left\|(u_{k},v_{k})\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{0}}>k\left\|(u_{k},v_{k})\right\|_{K,\alpha}

(A.2)

Thus, the sequence
(\tilde{u}_{k},\tilde{v}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
defined by

\displaystyle\tilde{u}_{k}:=\frac{u_{k}}{\left\|(u_{k},v_{k})\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{0}}},\hskip 20.00003pt\tilde{v}_{k}:=\frac{v_{k}}{\left\|(u_{k},v_{k})\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{0}}},\hskip 20.00003ptk\in\mathbb{N}

satisfies

\displaystyle(\tilde{u}_{k},\tilde{v}_{k})\in\mathcal{W}^{1}_{K,\alpha,\beta},\hskip 20.00003pt\left\|(\tilde{u}_{k},\tilde{v}_{k})\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{0}}=1,\hskip 20.00003ptk\in\mathbb{N}.

(A.3)

Moreover, (
A.2
) implies that

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla\tilde{u}_{k}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\left\|\nabla_{\Gamma}\tilde{v}_{k}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}^{2}+\sigma(K)\left\|\alpha\tilde{v}_{k}-\tilde{u}_{k}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}^{2}=\left\|(\tilde{u}_{k},\tilde{v}_{k})\right\|_{K,\alpha}^{2}<\frac{1}{k^{2}},

(A.4)

for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
. In particular, (
A.3
) and (
A.4
) imply that the sequence
(\tilde{u}_{k},\tilde{v}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded in
\mathcal{H}^{1}
. Hence, according to the Banach-Alaoglu theorem, there exists a pair
(u,v)\in\mathcal{H}^{1}
such that
(\tilde{u}_{k},\tilde{v}_{k})\rightharpoonup(u,v)
in
\mathcal{H}^{1}
after extraction of a subsequence.
It thus follows that

\displaystyle\beta\left|\Omega\right|\left<u\right>_{\Omega}+\left|\Gamma\right|\left<v\right>_{\Gamma}=0.

From the compact embedding
\mathcal{H}^{1}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{H}^{0}
, we deduce that

\displaystyle(\tilde{u}_{k},\tilde{v}_{k})\to(u,v)\hskip 20.00003pt\text{in}\;\mathcal{H}^{0},

after another subsequence extraction. In particular, this implies that
\left\|(u,v)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{0}}=1
.

If
K>0
, we infer from (
A.4
) that

\displaystyle\alpha\tilde{v}_{k}-\tilde{u}_{k}\to 0\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in}\;L^{2}(\Gamma)\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and thus,}\hskip 20.00003pt\alpha v-u=0\hskip 10.00002pt\text{a.e. on}\;\Gamma.

(A.5)

This obviously holds true for
K=0
, since then

As the
