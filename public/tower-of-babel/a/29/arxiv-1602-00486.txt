On Time Correlations for KPZ Growth in One Dimension This paper is a contribution to the Special Issue on Asymptotics and Universality in Random Matrices, Random Growth Processes, Integrable Systems and Statistical Physics in honor of Percy Deift and Craig Tracy. The full collection is available at http://www.emis.de/journals/SIGMA/Deift-Tracy.html
Patrik L. FERRARI † and Herbert SPOHN ‡

Abstract
Time correlations for KPZ growth in 1 + 1 1+1 dimensions are reconsidered. We discuss flat, curved, and stationary initial conditions and are interested in the covariance of the height as a function of time at a fixed point on the substrate. In each case the power laws of the covariance for short and long times are obtained. They are derived from a variational problem involving two independent Airy processes. For stationary initial conditions we derive an exact formula for the stationary covariance with two approaches: (1) the variational problem and (2) deriving the covariance of the time-integrated current at the origin for the corresponding driven lattice gas. In the stationary case we also derive the large time behavior for the covariance of the height gradients.

Introduction

Because of novel experiments
[
48
,

,

]
and exact solutions (see surveys and lecture notes
[
10
,
14
,
26
,
41
,

), there is a continuing interest in growing surfaces in the Kardar–Parisi–Zhang (KPZ) universality class
[
35
]
, in particular for the case of
1+1
dimensions. The object of interest is a height function
h(x,t)
over the one-dimensional substrate space,
x\in\mathbb{R}
, at time
t\geq 0
, which evolves by a stochastic evolution. Examples are the KPZ equation itself, the single step model, polynuclear growth, Eden type growth, and more. The spatial statistics,
x\mapsto h(x,t)
at large, but fixed time
t
is fairly well understood. The typical size of the height fluctuations is of order
t^{1/3}
and the correlation length grows as
t^{2/3}
. The precise spatial statistics depends on the initial conditions. Three canonical cases have been singled out, which are flat, step (also curved), and stationary. On the other hand, our understanding of the correlations in time is more fragmentary. For the point-to-point semi-discrete
directed polymer, which corresponds to curved initial data, Johansson
[
33
]
recently derived the long time asymptotics of the joint
distribution of
(h(0,\tau t),h(0,t))
,
\tau
fixed,
t\to\infty
. In an earlier work on the same quantity
[
20
]
Dotsenko obtains a replica solution of the KPZ equation. In both cases the final result is an infinite series, from which it seems to be difficult to extract more explicit information
1
1

1

In
[
21
]
progress has been achieved recently at the level of joint distribution functions for curved initial data in the limit
\tau\to 1
.
. For us, this state of affairs is one motivation to reconsider the issue of the KPZ time correlations.

The most basic observable is the temporal correlation function

\displaystyle C^{\diamond}(t_{0},t)={\rm Cov}(h(0,t_{0}),h(0,t))=\mathbbm{E}(h(0,t_{0})h(0,t))-\mathbbm{E}(h(0,t_{0}))\mathbbm{E}(h(0,t)).

(1.1)

Here the superscript
⋄
stands for the initial conditions, which are denoted by either “flat”, “step”, or “stat”. In the stationary case the covariance depends only on
t-t_{0}
. But for flat and curved both arguments have to be kept.

The correlation (
1.1
) has been measured in the turbulent liquid crystal experiment by Takeuchi and Sano
[
50
]
and is also determined numerically by Singha
[
47
]
(for the step case) and Takeuchi
[
48
]
in the closely related Eden cluster growth. The large
t
scaling behavior is reported as

\displaystyle C^{\mathrm{f\/lat}}(t_{0},t)\simeq(t_{0})^{4/3}t^{-2/3},\qquad C^{\mathrm{step}}(t_{0},t)\simeq(t_{0})^{2/3},

(1.2)

where we ignored the model-dependent prefactors, see
[
50
, Section 2.6]
for more details. Thus in the curved case the correlation of the unscaled height function does not decay to
0
for large
t
, which is surprising at first sight. The rough explanation is as follows (see also
[
34
]
): In the flat case the height
h(0,t)
depends on the nucleation events in the backward light cone with base points
x
such that
|x|\leq t^{2/3}
and so does
h(0,t_{0})
with
|x|\leq(t_{0})^{2/3}
. On the other side, in the curved case the domain of dependence has the form of a cigar of width
t^{2/3}
, resp.
(t_{0})^{2/3}
, since at short times only the few nucleation events close to the initial seed are available. Estimating the overlap in each case results in the distinct behavior as stated in (
1.2
).

In our contribution we consider the covariance

\displaystyle C_{t}^{\diamond}(\tau)=t^{-2/3}C^{\diamond}(\tau t,t)

rescaled according to the KPZ scaling theory. Thus one expects the limit

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}C_{t}^{\diamond}(\tau)=C^{\diamond}(\tau)

to exist. Without loss of generality one may set
0\leq\tau\leq 1
. To study
C^{\diamond}(\tau)
, we consider last passage percolation (LPP) as a particular model in the KPZ universality class. In this model at zero temperature, the height function is represented through the energy of an optimal directed polymer in a random medium, which is tightly related with the totally asymmetric simple exclusion process (TASEP), see Section
2
. We first obtain an expression for
C^{\diamond}(\tau)
based on a variational problem involving two independent Airy processes. This looks complicated, but we succeed in studying the power law behavior of
C^{\diamond}(\tau)
for
\tau
close to
0
and
1
, see (
2.4
), (
2.5
). In the first limit our result is in agreement with the behavior stated in (
1.2
). For stationary initial conditions we even obtain the entire limiting
C^{\mathrm{stat}}(\tau)
. Proving our result mathematically rigorously is technically difficult and goes beyond the scope of this paper.

An alternative approach comes from switching to local slopes,
\partial_{x}h(x,t)
, which are then governed by a type of stochastic particle dynamics. For example, the slope of the single step model is equivalent to the TASEP. The process
t\mapsto\partial_{t}h(0,t)=\mathsf{j}(t)
is stationary and the covariance
{\rm Cov}(\mathsf{j}(t),\mathsf{j}(t^{\prime}))
depends only on
t-t^{\prime}
. In the particle picture
{\rm Cov}(\mathsf{j}(t),\mathsf{j}(0))
is the correlation of the current (density) across the origin. We argue that
\int_{\mathbb{R}}dt{\rm Cov}(\mathsf{j}(t),\mathsf{j}(0))=0
and
{\rm Cov}(\mathsf{j}(t),\mathsf{j}(0))\simeq-|t|^{-4/3}
for large
|t|
, see (
3.9
). Thereby we arrive at an expression for
C^{\mathrm{stat}}(\tau)
which is identical to the one obtained by the LPP method. In fact,
C^{\mathrm{stat}}(\tau)
equals the covariance of fractional Brownian motion with Hurst exponent
\tfrac{1}{3}
. However, since the rescaled height function is expected to converge to a limit with Baik–Rains distribution, the limiting height process cannot be Gaussian (this is proven for a few models
[
7
,
25
,
27
,
40
]
).

Our contribution consists of three parts. In Section
2
we investigate
C^{\diamond}(\tau)
in the framework of directed polymers. In Section
3
we study the current time correlations for stationary lattice gases and in Section
4
we report on Monte-Carlo simulations of the TASEP in support of our theoretical findings.

Variational formulas for the universal part

of the two-time distribution

As a model in the KPZ universality class we consider the totally asymmetric simple exclusion process (TASEP). Particle configurations are denoted by
\eta\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}}

c_{j,j+1}(\eta)=\eta_{j}(1-\eta_{j+1})
. The particle configuration at time

is denoted by
\eta(t)
. Of central interest is the height function,
h(j,t)
, defined through
2
2

2

In the literature the height function is mostly defined to be twice the one defined in this paper. As we will discuss also the particle current, in our context it seems to be more natural to avoid unnecessary factors of
2
relating the two quantities.

\displaystyle h(j,t)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle J(t)+\sum_{i=1}^{j}\tfrac{1}{2}(1-2\eta_{i}(t)),&\mbox{if $j\geq 1$},\\
J(t),&\mbox{if $j=0$},\\
\displaystyle J(t)-\sum_{i=j+1}^{0}\tfrac{1}{2}(1-2\eta_{i}(t)),&\mbox{if $j\leq-1$},\end{cases}

(2.1)

where

is the particle current across the bond

integrated over the time interval
[0,t]
. Note that

. We study the TASEP because it allows for a simple mapping to last passage percolation (LPP), which will be the main technical tool in this section.

We will study the three different initial conditions mentioned in the introduction:

(i)

step initial conditions,
\eta=\mathbbm{1}_{\mathbb{Z}_{-}}
,

(ii)

flat initial conditions with density
\tfrac{1}{2}
,
\eta=\mathbbm{1}_{2\mathbb{Z}}
,

(iii)

stationary initial conditions with density
\tfrac{1}{2}
, i.e.,

, where

For these three initial conditions we would like to understand the scaling limit

\displaystyle\tau\mapsto{\cal X}^{\diamond}(\tau)=\lim_{t\to\infty}-2^{4/3}t^{-1/3}\big(h(0,\tau t)-\tfrac{1}{4}\tau t\big),

(2.2)

which defines
{\cal X}^{\diamond}(\tau)
,

, as a stochastic process in
\tau
(provided the limit exists).

is a fraction of the physical time

and the asymptotic mean has been subtracted. The fact that the scaling (

) should give a non-trivial limit process is due to the slow-decorrelation phenomenon, namely that along special space-time paths, fluctuations of order

occurs only over a macroscopic time scale. The special paths are the characteristics of the PDE describing the macroscopic evolution of the particle density
[
16
,
22
]
.

Up to model dependent scale factors, the limit processes are expected to be universal, meaning that the limit is the same for any model
in the KPZ universality class. In case the particular initial condition has to be specified, a superscript is added as

For the spatial argument, the corresponding scaling limit reads

\displaystyle w\mapsto\mathcal{Y}^{\diamond}(w)=\lim_{t\to\infty}-2^{4/3}t^{-1/3}\big(h\big(w2^{1/3}t^{2/3},t\big)-\tfrac{1}{4}t\big)

In Section

\displaystyle C^{\rm step}(\tau)=\Theta\big(\tau^{2/3}\big),\qquad C^{\rm f\/lat}(\tau)=\Theta\big(\tau^{4/3}\big),

2.1
TASEP and LPP

Let us first recall the relation between TASEP and LPP. A last passage percolation (LPP) model on
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
with independent random variables
\{\omega_{i,j},\,i,j\in\mathbb{Z}\}
is the following. An
up-right path

\pi=(\pi(0),\pi(1),\ldots,\pi(n))
on
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
from a point
A
to a point
E
is a sequence of points in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
with
\pi(k+1)-\pi(k)\in\{(0,1),(1,0)\}
, with
\pi(0)=A
and
\pi(n)=E
, and where
n
is called the length
\ell(\pi)
of
\pi
. Now, given a set of points
S_{A}
, one defines the last passage time
L_{S_{A}\to E}
as

\displaystyle L_{S_{A}\to E}=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\pi\colon A\to E\\
A\in S_{A}\end{subarray}}\sum_{1\leq k\leq\ell(\pi)}\omega_{\pi(k)}.

(2.7)

Finally, we denote by
\pi^{\max}_{S_{A}\to E}
any maximizer of the last passage time
L_{S_{A}\to E}
. For continuous random variables, the maximizer is a.s. unique.

For the TASEP the ordering of particles is preserved. If initially one orders from right to left as

\displaystyle\cdots<x_{2}(0)<x_{1}(0)<0\leq x_{0}(0)<x_{-1}(0)<\cdots,

then for all times
t\geq 0
also
x_{n+1}(t)<x_{n}(t)
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. The
\omega_{i,j}
in the LPP is the waiting time of particle
j
to jump from site
i-j-1
to site
i-j
.
By definition
\omega_{i,j}
are
{\rm exp}(1)
i.i.d. random variables. Let
S_{A}=\{(u,k)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\colon u=k+x_{k}(0),\,k\in\mathbb{Z}\}
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(L_{{S_{A}}\to(m,n)}\leq t)=\mathbb{P}(x_{n}(t)\geq m-n).

Further, for
m=n
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(L_{{S_{A}}\to(n,n)}\leq t)=\mathbb{P}(x_{n}(t)\geq 0)=\mathbb{P}(J(t)\geq n).

In particular, for the initial conditions under consideration, the set
S_{A}
is given by

(i)

Step initial conditions:
S_{A}=\{(0,0)\}
.

(ii)

Flat initial conditions with density
\tfrac{1}{2}
:
S_{A}={\cal L}=\{(i,j)\,|\,i+j=0\}
.

(iii)

Stationary initial conditions with density
\tfrac{1}{2}
:
S_{A}=\tilde{\cal L}
is a two-sided simple symmetric random walk passing through the origin and rotated by
\pi/4
. Using Burke’s property
[
11
]
one can equivalently replace all the randomness which is above the random line
\tilde{\cal L}
but outside the first quadrant by exponentially distributed random variables with parameter
\tfrac{1}{2}
only along the bordering lines
\{(i,-1),\,i\geq 0\}
and
\{(-1,i,),\,i\geq 0\}
, see
[
40
]
for more details.

See Fig.
1
for an illustration.

\exp(1)
i.i.d., while in the dark gray they are

In (iii)-(b) the blank regions at the boundary have a length which is i.i.d. geometric of mean 1.

Step initial conditions

TASEP with step initial conditions corresponds to the point-to-point problem in the LPP picture, see Fig.

\displaystyle{\cal X}^{\rm step}(1)=(1-\tau)^{1/3}\max_{v\in\mathbb{R}}\big\{\hat{\tau}^{1/3}{\cal A}_{2}\big(v\hat{\tau}^{-2/3}\big)-v^{2}\hat{\tau}^{-1}+\big(\tilde{\cal A}_{2}(v)-v^{2}\big)\big\}.

(2.12)

To argue about the behavior for

2

\displaystyle C^{\rm step}(\tau)=\tfrac{1}{2}{\rm Var}({\cal X}^{\rm step}(1))+\tfrac{1}{2}{\rm Var}({\cal X}^{\rm step}(\tau))-\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbbm{E}\big(({\cal X}^{\rm step}(\tau)-{\cal X}^{\rm step}(1))^{2}\big)

\displaystyle\hphantom{C^{\rm step}(\tau)}{}=\tfrac{1}{2}(1+\tau^{2/3}){\rm Var}({\cal X}^{\rm step}(1))-\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbbm{E}\big(({\cal X}^{\rm step}(\tau)-{\cal X}^{\rm step}(1))^{2}\big).

Now, by (
2.12
) and
{\cal X}^{\rm step}(\tau)=(1-\tau)^{1/3}\hat{\tau}^{1/3}{\cal A}_{2}(0)
, we have

\displaystyle{\cal X}^{\rm step}(1)-{\cal X}^{\rm step}(\tau)=(1-\tau)^{1/3}\max_{v\in\mathbb{R}}\big\{\hat{\tau}^{1/3}\big[{\cal A}_{2}\big(v\hat{\tau}^{-2/3}\big)\!-{\cal A}_{2}(0)\big]+\tilde{\cal A}_{2}(v)-v^{2}\big(1+\hat{\tau}^{-1}\big)\big\},

where
{\cal A}_{2}
and
\tilde{\cal A}_{2}
are independent Airy
2

) the first term becomes

and since the maximum is obtained for

should be at most a correction of order

. (

\displaystyle C^{\rm step}(\tau)\simeq\tfrac{1}{2}\big(1+\tau^{2/3}\big){\rm Var}({\cal X}^{\rm step}(1))-\tfrac{1}{2}(1-\tau)^{2/3}{\rm Var}\big({\cal X}^{\rm stat}(1)\big)+\mathcal{O}(1-\tau),

where we used the property that
{\cal A}_{\rm stat}(0)
has mean zero.

To make the present result into a theorem one has to control the convergence of the Airy process to Brownian motion. In recent work in progress, Corwin and Hammond establish rigorously the behavior close to

and

3
Current covariance for stationary lattice gases

The height function
h(0,t)
of the TASEP is identical to the time-integrated current across the bond
(0,1)
, denoted by
J(t)
in (
2.1
). This suggests to study the covariance of the same observable for a more general class of one-dimensional lattice gases. The mapping to LPP is then lost. On the other hand, in case of stationary initial conditions, one can exploit the local conservation law for the particle number together with space-time stationarity to obtain some information on the current covariance. Thereby we extend the validity of (
2.14
). The covariance of
J(t)
is identical to the one of fractional Brownian motion in the scaling limit. For reversible models the Hurst parameter is
H=\tfrac{1}{4}
, while for non-reversible lattice gases
H=\tfrac{1}{3}
. In fact, for reversible models it is expected, and proved for particular cases
[
19
,
38
]
, that as a stochastic process
J(t)
converges under the appropriate scaling to fractional Brownian motion, which is a Gaussian process. Such a result cannot hold in the non-reversible case, since the large
t
distribution of
J(t)
is Baik–Rains, as proved for a few models
[
7
,
25
,
27
,
40
]
.

We consider exclusion processes on
\mathbb{Z}
, for simplicity with nearest neighbor jumps only. They are defined as a generalization
of the TASEP by allowing for an arbitrary exchange rate
c_{j,j+1}(\eta)>0
. For the ASEP the exchange rates are
c_{j,j+1}(\eta)=p\eta_{j}(1-\eta_{j+1})+q(1-\eta_{j})\eta_{j+1}
with
p+q=1
,
p=\tfrac{1}{2}
being the reversible SSEP. We assume that
c_{j,j+1}
has finite range and is invariant under lattice translations. The generator,
L
, of the corresponding Markov jump process is then defined through

\displaystyle Lf(\eta)=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}c_{j,j+1}(\eta)\big(f\big(\eta^{j,j+1}\big)-f(\eta)\big)

acting on local functions
f
, where
\eta^{j,j+1}
denotes the configuration
\eta
with occupancies at sites
j
and
j+1
exchanged.

We start the dynamics in the steady state. For reversible models, by definition there is a finite range translation invariant energy function,
H
, such that

\displaystyle c_{j,j+1}(\eta)=c_{j,j+1}\big(\eta^{j,j+1}\big)\mathrm{e}^{-[H(\eta^{j,j+1})-H(\eta)]}.

For given average density,
0\leq\rho\leq 1
, there is a unique stationary measure,
\mu_{\rho}
, satisfying
\mu_{\rho}=\mu_{\rho}\mathrm{e}^{Lt}
.
\mu_{\rho}
is the Gibbs measure for
H-\bar{\mu}\sum_{j}\eta_{j}
, where the chemical potential
\bar{\mu}
has to be adjusted such that the average density equals
\rho
. On the other hand, for non-reversible lattice gases one immediately encounters the long-standing problem to prove the existence of a unique stationary measure at fixed
\rho
. Here we simply assume such a property to be valid, including the exponential space-mixing of
\mu_{\rho}
. We use
\mathbbm{E}(\cdot)
as a generic symbol for the process expectation and
\langle\cdot\rangle_{\rho}
as expectation with respect to
\mu_{\rho}
. For the ASEP the steady state is Bernoulli and obviously our assumptions hold.

Let us consider the empirical current across the bond
(j,j+1)
, denoted by
\mathsf{j}_{j,j+1}(t)
. This is a sequence of
\delta
-functions with weight
1
for a jump from
j
to
j+1
and weight
-1
for the reverse jump. The time-integrated current across the bond
(j,j+1)
is then

\displaystyle J_{j,j+1}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}ds\,\mathsf{j}_{j,j+1}(s)

with the convention
J(t)=J_{0,1}(t)
,
\mathsf{j}(t)=\mathsf{j}_{0,1}(t)
. The average current reads
\mathbb{E}(\mathsf{j}(t))\!=\!\langle c_{0,1}(\eta)(\eta_{0}-\eta_{1})\rangle_{\rho}=j(\rho)
.
We also introduce the stationary covariance

\displaystyle S(j,t)={\rm Cov}(\eta_{j}(t),\eta_{0}(0))=\mathbb{E}\big(\eta_{j}(t)\eta_{0}(0)\big)-\rho^{2}.

There is a sum rule which connects
S
with the variance of
J(t)
,

\displaystyle{\rm Var}(J(t))=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}|j|S(j,t)-\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}|j|S(j,0).

(3.1)

The proof is deferred to Appendix
B
.

Since
J(t)
has stationary increments, it is convenient to study the correlations of the increments
dJ(t)=\mathsf{j}(t)dt
. As discussed in Appendix
B
, the covariance is given by

\displaystyle{\rm Cov}\big(\mathsf{j}(t),\mathsf{j}(t^{\prime})\big)=\langle c_{0,1}\rangle_{\rho}\delta(t-t^{\prime})+h(t-t^{\prime}).

For the continuous part we first define the generator of time reversed process,
L^{\mathrm{R}}
, through
\langle f(Lg)\rangle_{\rho}=\langle(L^{\mathrm{R}}f)g\rangle_{\rho}
. Its exchange rates are given by

\displaystyle c^{\mathrm{R}}_{j,j+1}(\eta)=\frac{\mu_{\rho}(\eta^{j,j+1})}{\mu_{\rho}(\eta)}c_{j,j+1}\big(\eta^{j,j+1}\big).

Hence the current function across the bond
(j,j+1)
equals

\displaystyle r_{j,j+1}(\eta)=c_{j,j+1}(\eta)(\eta_{j}-\eta_{j+1})

(3.2)

and the time-reversed current function equals

\displaystyle r^{\mathrm{R}}_{j,j+1}(\eta)=c^{\mathrm{R}}_{j,j+1}(\eta^{j,j+1})(\eta_{j}-\eta_{j+1}).

They satisfy
\langle r_{j,j+1}\rangle_{\rho}=-\langle r^{\mathrm{R}}_{j,j+1}\rangle_{\rho}
. Then

\displaystyle h(t)=-\big\langle\big(r^{\mathrm{R}}_{0,1}-j(\rho)\big)\mathrm{e}^{L|t|}(r_{0,1}-j(\rho))\big\rangle_{\rho}.

(3.3)

3.1
Reversible models

While our focus is on non-reversible models, it is still instructive to first explain how fractional Brownian motion appears for reversible lattice gases. Then
r^{\mathrm{R}}_{j,j+1}=r_{j,j+1}(\eta)
and the smooth part
h(t)
simplifies to

\displaystyle h(t)=-\langle c_{0,1}(\eta)(\eta_{0}-\eta_{1})\mathrm{e}^{L|t|}c_{0,1}(\eta)(\eta_{0}-\eta_{1})\rangle_{\rho},

see Appendix
B
. Since
L
is a symmetric operator in the Hilbert space
L^{2}(\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}},\mu_{\rho})
, there exists a spectral measure
\nu
of finite mass such that

\displaystyle h(t)=-\int_{0}^{\infty}\nu(d\lambda)\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda|t|}.

(3.4)

In particular,
h
is monotonically increasing with
h(0)=-\langle c_{0,1}(\eta)^{2}(\eta_{0}-\eta_{1})^{2}\rangle_{\rho}
and
h(\infty)=0
.

From hydrodynamic fluctuation theory
[
12
,
13
]
, one knows that
S(j,t)
broadens diffusively as

\displaystyle S(j,t)\simeq\chi(Dt)^{-1/2}f_{\mathrm{G}}\big((Dt)^{-1/2}j\big)

(3.5)

with
f_{\mathrm{G}}
the standard Gaussian,
D
a diffusion constant depending on
\rho
, and the susceptibility

\displaystyle\chi=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}S(j,0).

Hence, using (
3.5
) for large
t
,

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}|j|S(j,t)\simeq\chi(Dt)^{1/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}dx|x|f_{\mathrm{G}}(x).

Now,

\displaystyle{\rm Var}(J(t))=\langle c_{0,1}\rangle_{\rho}t+\int_{0}^{t}ds\int_{0}^{t}ds^{\prime}h(s-s^{\prime}).

The sum rule (
3.1
) implies a variance of order
\sqrt{t}
. Thus to cancel the leading behavior proportional to
t
, one must have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}dth(t)=-\langle c_{0,1}\rangle_{\rho}.

Substituting in (
3.1
), one arrives at

\displaystyle\chi\int_{\mathbb{R}}dx|x|f_{\mathrm{G}}(x)(Dt)^{1/2}\simeq-2\int_{0}^{t}ds\int_{s}^{\infty}duh(u),

which implies

\displaystyle h(t)\simeq-c_{0}t^{-3/2},\qquad c_{0}=\tfrac{1}{8}D^{1/2}\chi\int_{\mathbb{R}}dx|x|f_{\mathrm{G}}(x).

(3.6)

The current correlation is negative and decays as
-|t|^{-3/2}
.

With this information, one can now determine the covariance of
J(t)
,

\displaystyle{\rm Cov}\big(J(t)J(\tau t)\big)=-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{\tau t}dsds^{\prime}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}du\,h(u)\delta(s-s^{\prime})-h(s-s^{\prime})\right)

\displaystyle\hphantom{{\rm Cov}\big(J(t)J(\tau t)\big)}{}=-\int_{0}^{\tau t}ds\left(2\int_{s}^{\infty}ds^{\prime}h(s^{\prime})-\int_{\tau t-s}^{t-s}ds^{\prime}h(s^{\prime})\right)

(3.7)

with
0\leq\tau\leq 1
. We insert the asymptotics from (
3.6
) in the form
-c_{0}(c_{1}+Dt)^{-3/2}
. Then

\displaystyle{\rm Cov}\big(J(t)J(\tau t)\big)\simeq\big(1+\tau^{1/2}-(1-\tau)^{1/2}\big)(Dt)^{1/2}\chi\int_{0}^{\infty}dxxf_{\mathrm{G}}(x)

for large
t
, which one recognizes as the covariance of fractional Brownian motion with Hurst parameter
H=\tfrac{1}{4}
.

3.2
Non-reversible models, zero propagation speed

For reversible lattice gases the average current
j(\rho)
vanishes and a localized perturbation stays centered, compare with (
3.5
). For non-reversible models the average current does not vanish, in general. A small perturbation of the steady state will propagate with velocity
v(\rho)=j^{\prime}(\rho)
, which generically will be non-zero. The correlator is centered at
v(\rho)t
. If
v(\rho)\neq 0
, then the sum rule implies that
{\rm Var}(J(t))\sim\sqrt{t}
, indicating that
J(t)
will be close to a Brownian motion. Fractional Brownian motion can be seen only when the current is integrated along the ray
\{x=v(\rho)t\}
. To properly implement such a notion requires extra considerations, which will be explained in the next subsection. For this part we assume
v(\rho)=0
. For the ASEP
j(\rho)=(p-q)\rho(1-\rho)
and our condition holds only at
\rho=\tfrac{1}{2}
.

Secondly non-reversible models are in the KPZ universality class and the covariance is expected to scale as

\displaystyle S(j,t)\simeq\chi(\Gamma t)^{-2/3}f_{\mathrm{KPZ}}\big((\Gamma t)^{-2/3}j\big)

(3.8)

with
\Gamma=\tfrac{1}{2}\chi^{2}|j^{\prime\prime}(\rho)|
according to KPZ scaling theory
[
36
]
. From the sum rule (
3.1
), again we infer that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}dth(t)=-\langle c_{0,1}\rangle_{\rho}

with
h(t)
given by equation (
3.3
). Thus, substituting (
3.8
), one arrives at

\displaystyle\chi\int_{\mathbb{R}}dx|x|f_{\mathrm{KPZ}}(x)(\Gamma t)^{2/3}\simeq-2\int_{0}^{t}ds\int_{s}^{\infty}duh(u),

which implies

\displaystyle h(t)\simeq-c_{0}t^{-4/3},\qquad c_{0}=\tfrac{1}{9}\Gamma^{2/3}\chi\int_{\mathbb{R}}dx|x|f_{\mathrm{KPZ}}(x).

(3.9)

The current correlation is negative and decays as
-|t|^{-4/3}
. The full covariance is obtained by the same scheme as above, see (
3.7
), with the result

\displaystyle{\rm Cov}\big(J(t),J(\tau t)\big)\simeq\tfrac{1}{2}\big(1+\tau^{2/3}-(1-\tau)^{2/3}\big)(\Gamma t)^{2/3}\chi\int_{\mathbb{R}}dx|x|f_{\mathrm{KPZ}}(x)

(3.10)

valid for large
t
. We recognize the covariance of fractional Brownian motion with Hurst parameter
H=\tfrac{1}{3}
.
Note that the Hurst exponent for the driven lattice gas is larger than the reversible value
\tfrac{1}{4}
. Nevertheless, the process
{\cal X}^{\rm stat}(\tau)
is not a fractional Brownian motion, since its one-point distribution is known to be non-Gaussian. The non-universal prefactors in (
2.6
) and (
3.10
) look different. But they have to agree because of the sum rule (
3.1
). As explained in Corollary
A.6
, their equivalence can also be verified directly from the definition.

Our argument is on less secure grounds than in the reversible case. Firstly, the scaling (
3.8
) of the correlator is proved only for the TASEP. Even then, no spectral theorem in the form (
3.4
) is available. But if for TASEP at density
\tfrac{1}{2}
the current correlator
h(t)
is assumed to be increasing, then (
3.10
) holds in the limit
t\to\infty
. In Section
4
we display the results of Monte Carlo simulations for the TASEP at density
\tfrac{1}{2}
. They very convincingly confirm
h(t)<0
, strict increase, and
-t^{-4/3}
asymptotics, see Figs.
7
and
8
. For density
\tfrac{1}{2}
the theoretically predicted parameters are
\Gamma=\sqrt{2}
and
c_{0}=0.02013\ldots
.

3.3
Non-reversible models, non-zero propagation speed

We first have to generalize the sum rule to a current integrated along the ray
\{x=vt\}
, where for notational simplicity we assume
v>0
. As a start-up this will be done for the more transparent case of a continuum stochastic field
u(x,t)
, which is stationary in time and, for each realization, satisfies the conservation law

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u(x,t)+\partial_{x}\mathcal{J}(x,t)=0.

(3.11)

The random current field
\mathcal{J}(x,t)
is also space-time stationary. Without loss of generality we assume
\mathbbm{E}(u(x,t))=0
,
\mathbbm{E}(\mathcal{J}(x,t))=0
. (
3.11
) implies that
(-u,\mathcal{J})
is a curl-free vector field on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Thus there is a potential, resp. height function, defined by

\displaystyle h(y,t)=\int_{0}^{t}ds\mathcal{J}(0,s)-\int_{0}^{y}dxu(x,t),

(3.12)

where
y\geq 0
in accordance with
v>0
.
h(y,t)
does not depend on the choice of the integration path. In particular, one can integrate along the ray
\{x=vt\}
. Then

\displaystyle h(vt,t)=\int_{0}^{t}ds\big(\mathcal{J}(vs,s)-vu(vs,s)\big).

Along the ray
\{x=vt\}
the current is given by
s\mapsto\mathcal{J}(vs,s)-vu(vs,s)
, which is a stationary process in
s
and integrates to
h(vt,t)
.

As before, we define
S(x,t)={\rm Cov}(u(x,t),u(0,0))
. Then the sum rule (
3.1
) generalises to

\displaystyle{\rm Var}(h(y,t))=\int dx|y-x|S(x,t)-\int dx|x|S(x,0),

(3.13)

see Appendix
B
. If
S(x,t)
is peaked at
vt
, then the variance of the time-integrated current with end-point
(vt,t)
reflects the anomalous peak broadening.

For lattice gases the position space is discrete and one has to adjust the scheme. We denote by
J_{j,j+1}([t^{\prime},t])
the current across the bond
j,j+1
integrated over the time-interval
[t^{\prime},t]
. The height
h(y,t)
,
y\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, is defined in analogy to (
3.12
) as

\displaystyle h(y,t)=J_{0,1}([0,t])-\sum_{j=1}^{y}\eta_{j}(t).

The path from
(0,0)
to
(0,t)
to
(y,t)
is deformed into a staircase with step width
1
. Then

\displaystyle h(y,\tfrac{1}{v}y)=\sum_{j=1}^{y}X_{j},\quad X_{j}=J_{j-1,j}\big(\big[\tfrac{1}{v}(j-1),\tfrac{1}{v}j\big]\big)-\eta_{j}\big(\tfrac{1}{v}j\big).

\{X_{j},j\in\mathbb{Z}\}
is a stationary process and sums up to
h(y,\tfrac{1}{v}y)
.

The sum rule (
3.13
) remains valid in the form

\displaystyle{\rm Var}(h(y,t))=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}|j-y|S(j,t)-\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}|j|S(j,0).

The covariance has the scaling form

\displaystyle S(j,t)\simeq\chi(\Gamma t)^{-2/3}f_{\mathrm{KPZ}}\big((\Gamma t)^{-2/3}(j-v(\rho)t)\big).

(3.14)

Now all pieces are assembled. In the definition of
X_{j}
we set
v=v(\rho)
. Then the sum rule yields

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}{\rm Cov}(X_{0},X_{j})=0,

and using the scaling form (
3.14
) of
S(j,t)
one arrives at

\displaystyle{\rm Cov}(X_{0},X_{j})\sim-|j|^{-4/3}

for large
|j|
. Then as before one concludes that

\displaystyle{\rm Cov}\big(h(v(\rho)t,t),h(v(\rho)\tau t,\tau t)\big)\simeq\tfrac{1}{2}\big(1+\tau^{2/3}-(1-\tau)^{2/3}\big)(\Gamma t)^{2/3}\chi\int_{\mathbb{R}}dx|x|f_{\mathrm{KPZ}}(x)

in the scaling regime.

Considering arbitrary space-time rays provides a more complete picture of the current fluctuations than merely considering the current across the origin. There is a special direction of slope
v(\rho)^{-1}
, along which the covariance is the same as that of fractional Brownian motion with Hurst parameter
H=\tfrac{1}{3}
. For any
v\neq v(\rho)
, the time-integrated current behaves like a Brownian motion.

Appendix A
Scaling functions and limiting distributions

We recall the definitions of the GUE/GOE Tracy–Widom and the Baik–Rains distribution functions as well as the scaling function
f_{\rm KPZ}
used for the two-point function.

Definition A.1
.

The GUE Tracy–Widom distribution function is defined by

\displaystyle F_{\rm GUE}(s)=\det(\mathbbm{1}-K_{2,s})_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{n!}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}dx_{1}\cdots\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}dx_{n}\det\left[K_{2,s}(x_{i},x_{j})\right]_{1\leq i,j\leq n}

with the kernel
K_{2,s}(x,y)=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}d\lambda\mathrm{Ai}(x+s+\lambda)\mathrm{Ai}(y+s+\lambda)
.

Definition A.2
.

The GOE Tracy–Widom distribution function is defined by

\displaystyle F_{\rm GOE}(s)=\det(\mathbbm{1}-K_{1})_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{n!}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}dx_{1}\cdots\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}dx_{n}\det\left[K_{1,s}(x_{i},x_{j})\right]_{1\leq i,j\leq n}

with the kernel
K_{1,s}(x,y)=\mathrm{Ai}(x+y+s)
.

Definition A.3
.

The Baik–Rains distribution function is defined by

\displaystyle F_{\rm BR}(s)=\frac{\partial}{\partial s}(F_{\rm GUE}(s)g(s)),

where
g(s)
is given as follows

\displaystyle g(s)=s+\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}dxdy\mathrm{Ai}(x+y+s)-\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}dxdy\Phi_{s}(x)(\mathbbm{1}-P_{0}K_{2,s}P_{0})^{-1}(x,y)\Psi_{s}(y),

where
P_{s}
is the projection onto
(s,\infty)
and

\displaystyle\Phi_{s}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}dyK_{2,s}(x,y),\qquad\Psi_{s}(y)=1-\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}dx\mathrm{Ai}(x+y+s).

Definition A.4
.

The KPZ scaling function
f_{\rm KPZ}
is defined by

\displaystyle f_{\rm KPZ}(w)=\frac{1}{4}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial w^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}s^{2}dF_{w}(s),

where

\displaystyle F_{w}(s)=\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\big(F_{\rm GUE}\big(s+w^{2}\big)g\big(s+w^{2},w\big)\big),

where
g(s,w)
is given by

\displaystyle g(s,w)=e^{-w^{3}/3}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}_{-}}dxdye^{w(x+y)}\mathrm{Ai}(x+y+s)+\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}dxdy\Phi_{w,s}(x)\rho_{s}(x,y)\Psi_{w,s}(y)\right).

Here,
\rho_{s}(x,y)=(\mathbbm{1}-P_{0}K_{2,s}P_{0})^{-1}(x,y)
, and

\displaystyle\Phi_{w,s}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{-}}dze^{w(z+s)}K_{2,s}(z,x),\qquad\Psi_{w,s}(y)=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{-}}dze^{wz}\mathrm{Ai}(y+z+s).

The scaling function
f_{\rm KPZ}(w)
is even with
\int_{\mathbb{R}}dwf_{\rm KPZ}(w)|w|=0.287599\ldots
. Remark that
g(s,0)=g(s)
.

Here is another identity that allows us to compare the two formulas obtained for the stationary case.

Lemma A.5
.

It holds

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}dx|x|f_{\rm KPZ}(x)=\frac{1}{2}{\rm Var}(\xi_{\rm BR}).

Proof.

Using the above definitions and the fact that
f_{\rm KPZ}
is an even function, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}dx|x|f_{\rm KPZ}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}dxx\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}dss^{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial s^{2}}\big(F_{\rm GUE}\big(s+x^{2}\big)g\big(s+x^{2},x\big)\big)

\displaystyle\hphantom{\int_\mathbb{R}dx |x| f_{\rm KPZ}(x)}{}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}dss^{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial s^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dxx\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}\big(F_{\rm GUE}\big(s+x^{2}\big)g\big(s+x^{2},x\big)\big)

\displaystyle\hphantom{\int_\mathbb{R}dx |x| f_{\rm KPZ}(x)}{}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}dss^{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial s^{2}}F_{\rm GUE}(s)g(s,0)=\frac{1}{2}{\rm Var}(\xi_{\rm BR}),

where in the third step we use integration by parts twice and in the last step the fact that
\mathbbm{E}(\xi_{\rm BR})=0
.
∎

.

Proof.

For TASEP with stationary initial conditions and density

\displaystyle\hphantom{{\rm Cov}\big({\cal X}^{\rm stat}(\tau),{\cal X}^{\rm stat}(1)\big)}{}=\frac{1}{2}\big(1+\tau^{2/3}-(1-\tau)^{2/3}\big){\rm Var}(\xi_{\rm BR})=(\ref{eq2.10}),

Appendix B
Sum rule and current-current correlations

B.1
The sum rule

We prove that

\displaystyle{\rm Var}(h(y,t))=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}|j-y|S(j,t)-\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}|j|S(j,0).

We use the definition (
3.12
). Expanding out the square yields the terms (I), (II), and (III). Let us introduce the short hand

\sum\limits_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}g_{j}\eta_{j}(t)=\eta(g,t)
and correspondingly
\sum\limits_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}f_{j}J_{j,j+1}(t)=J(f,t)
. By the conservation law,

\displaystyle{\rm Cov}(\eta(f,t),\eta(g,t))={\rm Cov}\big(J\big(\partial^{\mathrm{T}}\partial f,t\big),J(g,t)\big)

with
(\partial f)_{j}=f_{j+1}-f_{j}
.

Choosing
g_{j}=\delta_{0j}
and
f_{j}=|j|
, hence
(\partial^{\mathrm{T}}\partial f)_{j}=-2\delta_{0j}
, one arrives at

\displaystyle\mathrm{(I)}={\rm Var}(J_{0,1}(t))=\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}|j|\big(S(j,t)+S(-j,t)-2S(j,0)\big),

where we used stationarity in
j
. Next we consider the cross term starting from

\displaystyle{\rm Cov}(\eta(f,t),\eta(g,0))=-{\rm Cov}\big(J(f,t),\eta\big(\partial^{\mathrm{T}}g,0\big)\big).

Choosing
f_{j}=\delta_{jy}
and
-\partial^{\mathrm{T}}g
as the indicator function of
[1,\dots,y]
yields

\displaystyle\mathrm{(II)}=-2{\rm Cov}(J_{y,y+1}(t),\eta(\partial^{\mathrm{T}}g,0))=2\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}g_{j}\big(S(y-j,t)-S(y-j,0)\big).

Finally

\displaystyle\mathrm{(III)}=\sum_{j=1}^{y}\sum_{i=1}^{y}S(j-i,0).

Summing all three terms establishes the claim.

B.2
Current-current correlation

One can think of
\mathsf{j}_{j,j+1}(t)
as a point process with weights
\pm 1
. Then the covariance has a self-part, proportional to
\delta(t-t^{\prime})
, and a continuous part. Such a decomposition holds also for current correlations. We first consider the self-part and introduce the short hands
q_{j}=\eta_{j}(1-\eta_{j+1})
,
\bar{q}_{j}=(1-\eta_{j})\eta_{j+1}
,
q_{j}(\eta(t))=q_{j}(t)
,
J_{j,j+1}([s,t])=J_{j,j+1}(t)-J_{j,j+1}(s)
for
0\leq s<t
. Note that
q_{j}-\bar{q}_{j}=\eta_{j}-\eta_{j+1}
. Then

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\to 0}\delta^{-1}\mathbbm{E}\big(J_{i,i+1}([t,t+\delta])J_{j,j+1}([t,t+\delta])\big)

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\delta^{-1}\mathbbm{E}\big((q_{i}(t)\bar{q}_{i}(t+\delta)-\bar{q}_{i}(t)q_{i}(t+\delta))(q_{j}(t)\bar{q}_{j}(t+\delta)-\bar{q}_{j}(t)q_{j}(t+\delta))\big)

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\langle q_{i}q_{j}L\bar{q}_{i}\bar{q}_{j}+\bar{q}_{i}\bar{q}_{j}Lq_{i}q_{j}-q_{i}\bar{q}_{j}L\bar{q}_{i}q_{j}-\bar{q}_{i}q_{j}Lq_{i}\bar{q}_{j}\rangle_{\rho}=\delta_{i,j}\langle c_{j,j+1}\rangle_{\rho}.

By the same method the continuous part is obtained as

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\to 0}\delta^{-2}\mathbbm{E}\big(J_{i,i+1}([s,s+\delta]J_{j,j+1}([t,t+\delta])\big)

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\delta^{-1}\mathbbm{E}\big((q_{i}(s)\bar{q}_{i}(s+\delta)-\bar{q}_{i}(s)q_{i}(s+\delta))\mathrm{e}^{L(t-s-\delta)}r_{j,j+1}(\eta(s+\delta))\big)

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\big\langle(\eta_{i}-\eta_{i+1})c_{i,i+1}(\mathrm{e}^{L(t-s)}r_{j,j+1})(\eta^{j,j+1})\big\rangle_{\rho}=-\big\langle r^{\mathrm{R}}_{i,i+1}\mathrm{e}^{L(t-s)}r_{j,j+1}\big\rangle_{\rho}.

Here
r^{\mathrm{R}}_{i,i+1}
is the reverse current defined by

\displaystyle r^{\mathrm{R}}_{j,j+1}(\eta)=\frac{\mu_{s}(\eta^{j,j+1})}{\mu_{s}(\eta)}c_{j,j+1}\big(\eta^{j,j+1}\big)(\eta_{j}-\eta_{j+1}).

Hence

\displaystyle\mathbbm{E}\big(\mathsf{j}_{i,i+1}(s)\mathsf{j}_{j,j+1}(t)\big)=\langle c_{j,j+1}\rangle_{\rho}\delta_{i,j}\delta(s-t)-\big\langle r^{\mathrm{R}}_{i,i+1}\mathrm{e}^{L(t-s)}r_{j,j+1}\big\rangle_{\rho}.

In particular,

\displaystyle{\rm Cov}\big(\mathsf{j}(s),\mathsf{j}(t)\big)=\mathbbm{E}\big(\mathsf{j}(s)\mathsf{j}(t)\big)-j(\rho)^{2}=\langle c_{0,1}\rangle_{\rho}\delta(s-t)+h(s-t),

where

\displaystyle h(t)=-\big\langle\big(r^{\mathrm{R}}_{0,1}-j(\rho)\big)\mathrm{e}^{L|t|}(r_{0,1}-j(\rho))\big\rangle_{\rho}.
