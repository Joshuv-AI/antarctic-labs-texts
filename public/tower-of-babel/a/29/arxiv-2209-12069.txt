Heat transfer mediated by the Berry-phase in non-reciprocal many-body systems
S.-A. Biehs, P. Ben-Abdallah

Abstract
We investigate the adiabatic evolution of thermal state in non-reciprocal many-body systems coupled to their environment and subject to periodic drivings. In such systems we show that besides the dynamical phase a geometrical phase can exist and it drives the relaxation dynamic of the system. On the contrary to the dynamical phase which always pushes the system toward its equilibrium state we show that the geometric phase can speed up or reduce the speed of relaxation process. These results could have applications in the field of thermal management of complex systems.

II
Time evolution of thermal state in
N
-body systems

To start let us consider a generic many-body system made with
N
bodies in mutual interaction and in interaction with an external bath at temperature
T_{b}
. The time evolution of thermal state
\mathbb{T}=(T_{1},...,T_{N})
of this system under temporal driving is governed by an energy balance (master) equation of the general form

C_{i}\frac{d{T}_{i}}{dt}=\underset{j\neq i}{\sum}\mathscr{P}_{j\rightarrow i}(\mathbb{T};T_{b},t),\>\>i={1,...,N}.

(1)

Here
\mathscr{P}_{j\rightarrow i}
denotes the power received by the
i^{th}
element from the
j^{th}
element within the system and
C_{i}
its heat capacity. Close to the equilibrium state
\mathbb{T}_{eq}=(T_{b},...,T_{b})^{t}
the net power can be linearized and expressed in term of pairwise thermal conductance

G_{ij}=\underset{T_{j}\rightarrow T_{i}}{\rm lim}\frac{\mathscr{P}_{j\rightarrow i}}{T_{j}-T_{i}}

(2)

and of conductance

G_{ib}=\underset{T_{b}\rightarrow T_{i}}{\rm lim}\frac{\mathscr{P}_{b\rightarrow i}}{T_{b}-T_{i}}

(3)

between the bath and each element. In this approximation equations (
1
) can be recast in the matrix form

\mathds{C}(t)\frac{d\mathbb{T}}{dt}=\hat{\mathds{G}}(t)\mathbb{T}+\mathbb{S}(t),

(4)

where
\hat{\mathds{G}}(t)
denotes the conductances matrix with

\hat{G}_{ij}=-\underset{j\neq i}{\sum}(G_{ij}+G_{ib})\delta_{ik}-(1-\delta_{ik})G_{ik},

(5)

\mathds{C}={\rm diag}(C_{1},...,C_{N})
is the heat capacity matrix and
\mathbb{S}(t)
is the source vector induced by the bath (i.e.
S_{i}=G_{ib}T_{b}
). The time evolution of thermal state is given by the following expression

\mathbb{T}(t)=\mathds{U}(t_{0},t)\mathbb{T}_{0}+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathds{U}(\tau,t)\mathds{C}^{-1}(\tau)\mathbb{S}(\tau)d\tau.

(6)

Here
\mathds{U}
denotes the propagator of differential system (
4
) and
\mathbb{T}_{0}=(T_{1}(t_{0}),...,T_{N}(t_{0}))^{t}
is the initial thermal state. In reciprocal systems (i.e.
\hat{G}_{ij}=\hat{G}_{ji}
) the fundamental matrix can be expressed in term of the exponential of the conductance matrix so that the thermal state (
6
) can easily be calculated. On the other hand, in non-reciprocal systems (i.e.
\hat{G}_{ij}\neq\hat{G}_{ji}
), the situation is more tricky and no simple expression of thermal state can be derived. Nevertheless, following Garrisson and Wright
Garrison
we can, in the adiabatic appoximation, seek a solution of this system by expanding it over the basis
\{\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{\perp}\}_{n}
of eigenstates of
\hat{\mathds{G}}
. To proceed we first recast the master equation as

\frac{d\mathbb{\tilde{T}}}{dt}=\tilde{\mathds{G}}(t)\mathbb{\tilde{T}},

(7)

by setting
\mathbb{\tilde{T}}=(\mathbb{T},T_{b})^{t}
and

\tilde{\mathds{G}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}\hat{\mathds{G}}&\vdots&\left(\begin{array}[]{c}G_{1b}\\
\vdots\\
G_{Nb}\end{array}\right)\\
\cdots&\cdots&\cdots\\
\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&\cdots&0\end{array}\right)&\vdots&0\end{array}\right).

(8)

Note, that for convenience
\hat{\mathds{G}}
is now the conductance matrix normalized by the heat capacities.
Then we seek a solution of Eq.(
7
) using the following expansion

\mathbb{\tilde{T}}(t)=\overset{N+1}{\underset{i=1}{\sum}}c_{i}(t)e^{\gamma_{di}(t)}\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{i}(t),

(9)

where
\gamma_{di}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\lambda_{i}(\tau)d\tau
is the dynamical phase associated the eigenvalue
\lambda_{i}
of
\tilde{\mathds{G}}
while
\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{i}
is its eigenvector. It is easy to check that
\lambda_{N+1}=0
is an eigenvalue with the corresponding eigenvector
\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{N+1}=\mathbb{1}_{N+1}=(1,...,1)^{t}
. It turns out that the temperatures vector takes the form

\mathbb{\tilde{T}}(t)=\overset{N}{\underset{i=1}{\sum}}c_{i}(t)e^{\gamma_{di}(t)}\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{i}(t)+c_{N+1}\mathbb{1}_{N+1}.

(10)

Since
\mathbb{\tilde{T}}\rightarrow(\mathbb{T}_{eq},T_{b})^{t}
after a sufficiently long time (
\hat{\mathds{G}}
being strictly definite negative) we can write the solution of Eq.(
7
) as

\mathbb{\tilde{T}}(t)=\overset{N}{\underset{i=1}{\sum}}c_{i}(t)e^{\gamma_{di}(t)}\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{i}(t)+T_{b}\mathbb{1}_{N+1},

(11)

Taking the projection of this temperature vector on the subspace
\mathbf{e}_{1}\otimes...\otimes\mathbf{e}_{N}
generated by the basis vectors
\mathbf{e}_{i}
of the canonic base, we get finally the thermal state

\mathbb{T}(t)=\overset{N}{\underset{i=1}{\sum}}c_{i}(t)e^{\gamma_{di}(t)}\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{\perp i}(t)+T_{b}\mathbb{1}_{N},

(12)

where according to the form of the block matrix defined in (
8
)
\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{\perp i}
is the
i^{th}
eigenvector of
\hat{\mathds{G}}
.

III
Adiabatic limit and geometrical phase

Inserting the solution in Eq. (
12
) into the master equation (
4
) we obtain, after removing the subscript
\perp
for readability reasons, the relations

\overset{N}{\underset{i=1}{\sum}}\partial_{t}(c_{i}e^{\gamma_{di}}\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{i})=\overset{N}{\underset{i=1}{\sum}}c_{i}(t)e^{\gamma_{di}}\hat{\mathds{G}}\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{i}+T_{b}\hat{\mathds{G}}\mathbb{1}_{N}+\mathbb{S}.

(13)

As
T_{b}\tilde{\mathds{G}}\mathbb{1}_{N}=-\mathbb{S}
and
\hat{\mathds{G}}\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{i}=\lambda_{i}\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{i}
we have equivalently

\overset{N}{\underset{i=1}{\sum}}(\dot{c}_{i}e^{\gamma_{di}}\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{i}+c_{i}e^{\gamma_{di}}\dot{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{i})=0.

(14)

Multiplying this relation by
\boldsymbol{\psi}_{j}
the
j^{th}
eigenvector of the transpose matrix
\hat{\mathds{G}}^{t}
and using the biorthogonality relations
\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{j}=\delta_{ij}
between the eigenvectors of
\hat{\mathds{G}}
and
\hat{\mathds{G}}^{t}
we find

\dot{c}_{j}e^{\gamma_{dj}}+\overset{N}{\underset{i=1}{\sum}}c_{i}e^{\gamma_{di}}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{j}\cdot\dot{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{i}=0.

(15)

Now, assuming a slow (adiabatic) variation of external control parameters with respect to time this system simplifies into

\dot{c}_{j}+c_{j}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{j}\cdot\dot{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{j}=0,

(16)

whose the solution reads

c_{j}(t)=\alpha_{j}e^{\gamma_{gj}(t)},

(17)

with

\gamma_{gj}(t)=-\int_{0}^{t}\!\!{\rm d}\tau\,\boldsymbol{\psi}_{j}\cdot\dot{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{j}(\tau)

(18)

the so called geometric phase. Hence, in the adiabatic limit the thermal state can be written as

\mathbb{T}(t)=\overset{N}{\underset{i=1}{\sum}}\alpha_{i}e^{\gamma_{di}(t)}e^{\gamma_{gi}(t)}\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{i}(t)+T_{b}\mathbb{1}_{N}.

(19)

This expression is the classical analog of the Berry’s result to arbitrary non-reciprocal many body systems.
The constants
\alpha_{i}
can be readily calculated from the initial thermal state
\mathbb{T}_{0}
.

IV
A simple toy model

To show to what extent the flux is affected by the geometrical phase we consider below a generic system as sketched in (Fig.1-a) made of two objets in mutual interaction and in interaction with the external bath. In this case the conductance matrix takes the form

\hat{\mathds{G}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}-a&G_{12}\\
G_{21}&-b\\
\end{array}\right),

(20)

with
a=G_{12}+G_{1b}
and
b=G_{21}+G_{2b}
. The eigenvectors of
\hat{\mathds{G}}
and of its transpose are

\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{i}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
\frac{G_{21}}{b+\lambda_{i}}\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}=\frac{1}{\beta_{i}}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
\frac{G_{12}}{b+\lambda_{i}}\end{pmatrix},

(21)

where

\lambda_{i}=\frac{1}{2}{\rm tr}\hat{\mathds{G}}\pm\biggl[\frac{1}{4}{\rm tr}^{2}\hat{\mathds{G}}-{\rm det}\hat{\mathds{G}}\biggr]^{1/2}

(22)

denotes the (real) eigenvalues of
\hat{\mathds{G}}
,
\beta_{i}
being calculated from the normalization relation
\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{i}\cdot\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}=1
. By setting
x_{i}=\frac{G_{21}}{b+\lambda_{i}}
and
y_{i}=\frac{G_{12}}{G_{21}}
it follows that the geometric phase accumulated between the initial instant up to time
t
is

\gamma_{gi}(t)=-\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\!\!{\rm d}\tau\,\frac{x_{i}\dot{x_{i}}y_{i}}{1+x_{i}^{2}y_{i}}.

(23)

Now, suppose that
\hat{\mathds{G}}(t)=\hat{\mathds{G}}(\mathbb{R}(t))
, where
\mathbb{R}=(R_{1},R_{2})^{t}
denotes a set of control parameters and that there is a time
T
for which
\mathbb{R}(t_{0}+T)=\mathbb{R}(t_{0})
. Hence, we consider a closed trajectory in parameter space. Then the accumulated geometrical phase in Eq. (
23
) during this interval
T
can be written as

\gamma_{gi}(T)=\oint_{\partial S}{\rm d}\mathbb{R}\cdot\mathbb{A}_{i},

(24)

where

\mathbb{A}_{i}=(\nabla_{\mathbb{R}}\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{i})^{t}\cdot\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}

(25)

is analog to a vector potential whose associated magnetic field is
\mathbb{B}_{i}=\nabla\times\mathbb{A}_{i}
. Here
\partial S
is the path of the trajectory in parameter space. Using the Stoke theorem with the oriented surface
S
enclosed by the countour
\partial S
generated by the change of parameter
\mathbb{R}
during the interval
[t_{0},t_{0}+T]
we can express the geometric phase in term of
\mathbb{B}_{i}
as

\gamma_{gi}(T)=\int_{S}{\rm d}\mathbb{S}\cdot\mathbb{B}_{i}

(26)

where
{\rm d}\mathbf{S}
is the infinitesimal oriented surface element on
S
.

These expressions allow us to determine the vector potential and magnetic field in our two-body system for any trajectory
\mathbf{R}
. With Eq. (
23
) for one period
T
we find

\mathbf{A}_{i}=\frac{x_{i}y_{i}}{\beta_{i}}\bigl(\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}x_{i}\bigr).

(27)

Note that this expression is not gauge invariant as it is typical for vector potentials. For example, also the expression

\mathbf{A}_{i}^{\prime}=-\frac{x_{i}^{2}}{2\beta_{i}}\bigl(\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}y_{i}\bigr)

(28)

is a valid representation of the vector potential. On the other hand, the magnetic field is gauge invariant, and consequently we obtain for both
\mathbf{A}_{i}
and
\mathbf{A}_{i}^{\prime}
the magnetic field

\mathbf{B}=-(\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}x_{i})\times\bigl(\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}y_{i}\bigr)\frac{x_{i}}{\beta_{i}^{2}}.

(29)

Interestingly, from this expression it is obvious that
\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{0}
if
y_{i}=1
, i.e. if
G_{12}=G_{21}
. Hence, non-reciprocity is a necessary condition for the appearance of a Berry phase. Furthermore, the magnetic field vanishes if
\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}x_{i}
and
\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}y_{i}
are parallel or antiparallel. This happens for a constant background conductance if
\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}G_{12}
and
\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}G_{21}
are parallel or antiparallel, i.e. if for example
G_{12}
and
G_{21}
change in phase with the external parameter change. Therefore, if
G_{12}
and
G_{21}
are in phase or phase-shifted by
\pi
, then there is no Berry phase.

The above statements are very general. Now, we take a conrete path in the parameter space by choosing the two natural parameters
x_{i}
and
y_{i}
for a path embedded in
\mathds{R}^{3}
, i.e.
\mathbf{R}(t)=(x_{i}(t),y_{i}(t),0)^{t}
. Then the magnetic field is

\mathbf{B}=-\frac{x_{i}}{\beta_{i}^{2}}\mathbf{e}_{z}.

(30)

The numerical results show clearly the sensitivity of geometric effect to the multiperiodicity of driving. When
\Lambda\sim\tau
and
\Lambda\rightarrow\infty
the integration surface reduces to a single point so that the Berry phase vanishes. On the contrary, with a finite period
\Lambda
the closed countour delimits a non-vanishing area and this countour is simple (without crossing point) provide that
\Lambda=\tau
whereas in the case of multiperiodic driving (i.e.
\Lambda\neq\tau
) this countour presents, in general, several crossing points and it can be decomposed into several loops which are traveled either in clockwise or anti-clockwise direction. In the first case if a loop is in the first quadrant (i.e.
x_{i}>0
and
y_{i}>0
) and the countour is browsed in clockwise direction resulting in a positive contribution to the geometric phase
\gamma_{i}
. In other words, during this period the geometric phase tends to insulate the different parts of the system. On the other hand, if the loop is browsed in the opposite direction the generated geometric phase is negative and the relaxation process is accelerated. This phenomenon is analog to the coiled light mechanism discovered by Chiao et al.
Chiao1
;
Chiao2
where the parameters space corresponds to the light polarization state.

Notice that, the cumulated dynamic phases (always negative) being, at large time scale, larger than the cumulated geometric phase as shown in Fig. 2-c and Fig. 2-d it always pushes the system toward its equilibrium state. On the other hand, the geometrical phase, which can be either positive or negative, has an oscillatory character and its accumulation after one or several periods can be either positive or negative so that they can speed up or speed down the relaxation process. Unfurtunatly, this geometric effect cannot be persistent in time and the system finishes at the end to be driven only by the dynamical phase. In our toy model the two Berry phases

V
conclusion

In summary, although the physics of non-reciprocal systems remain today largely ellusive, the results introduced in this work highlight the peculiarities of relaxation processs for this systems when they are driven by periodic external actuations. On the contrary to reciprocal systems, the presence of a geometrical phase superimposes to the dynamical phase and has the potential to significantly alter the relaxation dynamic of systems. We have shown that this phase can be used either to accelerate or reduce the speed of relaxation. We hope that these preliminary results will stimulate research on the thermal control of non-reciprocal systems. On a theoretical point of view it would be interesting to explore the role played by the dissipation mechanisms induced by the external driving as well as the potential of multispectral drivings on the relaxation dynamics. The non-adiabatic control of these systems remains also a challenging problem. These problems will be addressed in subsequent studies.
