Fermion coupling to loop quantum gravity: canonical formulation.
Jerzy Lewandowski, Cong Zhang

Abstract
In the model of a fermion field coupled to loop quantum gravity, we consider the Gauss and the Hamiltonian constraints. According to the explicit solutions to the Gauss constraint, the fermion spins and the gravitational spin networks intertwine with each other so that the fermion spins contribute to the volume of the spin network vertices. For the Hamiltonian constraint, the regularization and quantization procedures are presented in detail. By introducing an adapted vertex Hilbert space to remove the regulator, we propose a diffeomorphism covariant graph-changing Hamiltonian constraint operator of the fermion field. This operator shows how fermions move in the loop quantum gravity spacetime and simultaneously influences the background quantum geometry. Moreover, as an innovation of our work, introducing the vertex Hilbert space also fixes issues so that a densely defined symmetric Hamiltonian constraint operator can be obtained.

The usual Hamiltonian analysis tells

introduces the Grassman-valued half-densities

to define the phase space of fermion. Moreover, in order to do quantization, the smeared version of

by
\sqrt{\delta(x,y)}

\int\differential^{3}y\sqrt{\delta(x,y)}\tilde{\Psi}(y)

thiemann1998quantumV
. With the smeared variables, the fermion sector is quantized and the resulting quantum theory carries out the dieffeomorphsm-invariance feature.

It will be useful to compare our work with the known models of fermions in LQG. Among these models, a typical one is to introduce the path observables (see, e.g.,

We not only give the exact solutions to the Gauss constraint but also study the physical pictures resulting from these solutions. Second, for the Hamiltonian constraint of the fermion field, we propose a different regularization strategy than that in
thiemann1998quantumV
. Additionally, the vertex Hilbert space is introduced, not only for removing the regulator in the regularized Hamiltonian constraint but also for fixing the issues on the adjoint of

will be denoted by
{\mathbf{Cyl_{F}}}
.

According to (
24
), each cylindrical function with respect to
\gamma_{F}
can be identical with a vector

f_{\gamma_{F}}=\left(f_{0},\{f_{i_{1}\cdots i_{n}}\}_{1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{n}\leq{2|\gamma_{F}|}}\right)\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{2|\gamma_{F}|}}.

Moreover, for a cylindrical function
\Psi
expressed via
f_{\gamma_{F}}\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{2|\gamma_{F}|}}
, there always exists a lager graph
\gamma_{F}^{\prime}\supset\gamma_{F}
such that
\Psi
is rewritten via some
f^{\prime}_{\gamma_{F}^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{2|\gamma_{F}^{\prime}|}}
. Thus, given two functions
\Psi_{1},\Psi_{2}\in{\mathbf{Cyl_{F}}}
on
\gamma_{F}{}_{1}
and
\gamma_{F}{}_{2}
respectively, we can find another graph
\gamma_{F}^{\prime}
containing both
\gamma_{F}{}_{1}
and
\gamma_{F}{}_{2}
to rewrite
\Psi_{1}
and
\Psi_{2}
with
f_{\gamma_{F}^{\prime}}^{(1)},f_{\gamma_{F}^{\prime}}^{(2)}\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{2|\gamma_{F}^{\prime}|}}
. Then the inner product of
\Psi_{1}
and
\Psi_{2}
is given by

\langle\Psi_{1},\Psi_{2}\rangle=\int\differential\mu(\rho_{1}\rho_{1}^{\dagger})\differential\mu(\rho_{2}\rho_{2}^{\dagger})\cdots\differential\mu(\rho_{2|\gamma_{F}^{\prime}|}\rho_{2|\gamma_{F}|^{\prime}}^{\dagger})\Psi_{1}^{\dagger}\Psi_{2}

(25)

with
\differential\mu(\rho_{n}\rho_{n}^{\dagger})=\differential\rho_{n}^{\dagger}\differential\rho_{n}e^{\rho_{n}\rho_{n}^{\dagger}}
. By applying (
25
), one can verify

\langle\Psi_{1},\Psi_{2}\rangle=(f_{\gamma_{F}^{\prime}}^{(1)})^{\dagger}f_{\gamma_{F}^{\prime}}^{(2)}.

(26)

Moreover, even though the graphs
\gamma_{F}^{\prime}
containing both
\gamma_{F}{}_{1}
and
\gamma_{F}{}_{2}
are not unique,
Eq. (
25
) is independent of the choice of
\gamma_{F}^{\prime}
since
\int\differential\mu(\rho_{n}\rho_{n}^{\dagger})=1
. Indeed, Eq. (
25
) defines a measure
\differential\mu_{H}(\zeta^{\dagger}\zeta)
on
{\mathbf{Cyl_{F}}}^{\dagger}\otimes{\mathbf{Cyl_{F}}}
, and will be rewritten as

\langle\Psi_{1},\Psi_{2}\rangle=\int\differential\mu_{H}(\zeta\zeta^{\dagger})\Psi_{1}^{\dagger}\Psi_{2}.

(27)

The fermion Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{F}
is the completion of
{\mathbf{Cyl_{F}}}
with the inner product defined by (
27
), i.e.,

\mathcal{H}_{F}=\overline{{\mathbf{Cyl_{F}}}}.

(28)

On
{\mathbf{Cyl_{F}}}
, a type of operators are the multiplication operators
\widehat{\zeta_{v_{F},A}^{\dagger}}
given by

(\widehat{\zeta_{v_{F},A}^{\dagger}}\Psi)(\zeta^{\dagger})=\zeta_{A}^{\dagger}(v_{F})\Psi(\zeta^{\dagger}),\ \forall\Psi\in{\mathbf{Cyl_{F}}}.

(29)

Another type of operators are the derivative operators
\hat{\zeta}_{v_{F},A}
, acting on
\Psi\in{\mathbf{Cyl_{F}}}
as

(\hat{\zeta}_{v_{F},A}\Psi)(\zeta^{\dagger})=\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\zeta_{A}^{\dagger}(v_{F})}\Psi\right)(\zeta^{\dagger}),\ \forall\Psi\in{\mathbf{Cyl_{F}}}.

(30)

It is easy to verify that
\widehat{\zeta_{v_{F},A}^{\dagger}}
and
\hat{\zeta}_{v_{F},A}
are adjoint to each other, i.e.,

\widehat{\zeta_{v_{F},A}^{\dagger}}=\hat{\zeta}_{v_{F},A}^{\dagger},

(31)

which realizes the real condition. Moreover, one has

[{\hat{\zeta}}_{v_{F},A},{\hat{\zeta}}^{\dagger}_{v_{F}^{\prime},B}]_{+}={\hat{\zeta}}_{v_{F},A},{\hat{\zeta}}^{\dagger}_{v_{F}^{\prime},B}+{\hat{\zeta}}^{\dagger}_{v_{F}^{\prime},B}{\hat{\zeta}}_{v_{F},A}=\delta_{AB}\delta_{v_{F}v_{F}^{\prime}},

(32)

which implements the Poisson brackets (
20
) by defining

\hat{\theta}_{A}(v_{F})=\sqrt{\hbar}\,\hat{\zeta}_{v_{F},A}.

(33)

III.2.2
the spin network states of fermion field

Given a graph
\gamma_{F}
, the space of the fermionic cylindrical functions with respect to
\gamma_{F}
is a finite-dimensional Hilbert space, denoted by
\mathcal{H}_{{F,}\gamma_{F}}
.
Considering a graph
\gamma_{F}=\{v_{F}\}
which is compronent of a single vertex, one has the the space
\mathcal{H}_{{F,}\{v_{F}\}}\equiv\mathcal{H}_{v_{F}}
consisting of functions

\Psi(\zeta^{\dagger})=a_{00}+a_{10}\zeta_{\frac{1}{2}}^{\dagger}(v_{F})+a_{01}\zeta_{-\frac{1}{2}}^{\dagger}(v_{F})+a_{11}\zeta_{\frac{1}{2}}^{\dagger}(v_{F})\zeta_{-\frac{1}{2}}^{\dagger}(v_{F})

(34)

The inner product of
\Psi^{(i)}(\zeta^{\dagger})=a_{00}^{(i)}+a_{10}^{(i)}\zeta_{\frac{1}{2}}^{\dagger}(v_{F})+a_{01}^{(i)}\zeta_{-\frac{1}{2}}^{\dagger}(v_{F})+a_{11}^{(i)}\zeta_{\frac{1}{2}}^{\dagger}(v_{F})\zeta_{-\frac{1}{2}}^{\dagger}(v_{F})
with
i=1,2
is

\langle\Psi^{(1)},\Psi^{(2)}\rangle=\sum_{i,j\in\{0,1\}}(a_{ij}^{(1)})^{*}a_{ij}^{(2)}

(35)

For convenience, we introduce the Dirac bra-ket notation
|i,j\rangle_{v_{F}}
(
i,j=0,1
) to denote the state
\Psi_{ij}
, where
\Psi_{ij}
are the states given by
\Psi_{00}(\zeta^{\dagger})=1
,
\Psi_{10}(\zeta^{\dagger})=\zeta_{\frac{1}{2}}^{\dagger}(v_{F})
,
\Psi_{01}(\zeta^{\dagger})=\zeta_{-\frac{1}{2}}^{\dagger}(v_{F})
and
\Psi_{11}(\zeta^{\dagger})=\zeta_{\frac{1}{2}}^{\dagger}(v_{F})\zeta_{-\frac{1}{2}}^{\dagger}(v_{F})
. Then the states
|i,j\rangle_{v_{F}}
form an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{H}_{v_{F}}
, i.e.,

{}_{v_{F}}\langle i_{1},j_{1}|i_{2},j_{2}\rangle_{v_{F}}=\delta_{i_{1}i_{2}}\delta_{j_{1}j_{2}}.

(36)

The action of
\hat{\zeta}_{v_{F},A}
and
\hat{\zeta}_{v_{F},A}^{\dagger}
for
A=\pm\frac{1}{2}
on
\mathcal{H}_{v_{F}}
reads

\begin{array}[]{lll}\hat{\zeta}_{v_{F},\frac{1}{2}}^{\dagger}|0,i_{2}\rangle_{v_{F}}=|1,i_{2}\rangle_{v_{F}},&\hat{\zeta}_{v_{F},\frac{1}{2}}^{\dagger}|1,i_{2}\rangle_{v_{F}}=0,&\forall i_{2}=0,1,\\
\hat{\zeta}_{v_{F},\frac{1}{2}}|0,i_{2}\rangle_{v_{F}}=0,&\hat{\zeta}_{v_{F},\frac{1}{2}}|1,i_{2}\rangle_{v_{F}}=|0,i_{2}\rangle_{v_{F}},&\forall i_{2}=0,1,\\
\hat{\zeta}_{v_{F},-\frac{1}{2}}^{\dagger}|i_{1},0\rangle_{v_{F}}=(-1)^{i_{1}}|i_{1},1\rangle_{v_{F}},&\hat{\zeta}_{v_{F},-\frac{1}{2}}^{\dagger}|i_{1},1\rangle_{v_{F}}=0,&\forall i_{1}=0,1,\\
\hat{\zeta}_{v_{F},-\frac{1}{2}}|i_{1},0\rangle_{v_{F}}=0,&\hat{\zeta}_{v_{F},-\frac{1}{2}}|i_{1},1\rangle_{v_{F}}=(-1)^{i_{1}}|i_{1},0\rangle_{v_{F}},&\forall i_{1}=0,1.\end{array}

(37)

For the general case where
\gamma_{F}\subset\Sigma
consists of more than one vertices, we first associate to each
v_{F}\in\gamma_{F}
the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{v_{F}}
. Then, fixing an orientation of
\gamma_{F}
, one has the tensor product space

\mathcal{H}_{F,\gamma_{F}}=\mathcal{H}_{v_{F}^{(1)}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{v_{F}^{(2)}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathcal{H}_{v_{F}^{|\gamma_{F}|}}.

(38)

An orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{H}_{F,\gamma_{F}}
is composed of the vectors

|i_{1},i_{2},\cdots,i_{2|\gamma_{F}|}\rangle:=|i_{1},i_{2}\rangle_{v_{F}^{(1)}}\otimes|i_{3},i_{4}\rangle_{v_{F}^{(2)}}\otimes\cdots\otimes|i_{2|\gamma_{F}|-1},i_{2|\gamma_{F}|}\rangle_{v_{F}^{(|\gamma_{F}|)}}

(39)

with
i_{k}\in\{0,1\}
for all
1\leq k\leq 2|\gamma_{F}|
. Indeed, the vector
|i_{1},i_{2},\cdots,i_{2|\gamma_{F}|}\rangle
refers to the cylindrical function
\Psi_{\vec{i}}=\rho_{i_{1}}^{\dagger}\rho_{i_{2}}^{\dagger}\cdots\rho_{i_{n}}^{\dagger}
with respect to
\gamma_{F}
.
It is worth noting that the definition of
\mathcal{H}_{F,\gamma_{F}}
depends on the orientation of
\gamma_{F}
, since
\zeta_{A}^{{\dagger}}(v_{F})
are Grassmann numbers. This fact can be illustrated more explicitly with the following examples.
Consider another orientation
n\to\tilde{v}_{F}^{(n)}
of
\gamma_{F}
such that

\tilde{v}_{F}^{(1)}=v_{F}^{(2)},\tilde{v}_{F}^{(2)}=v_{F}^{(1)},\tilde{v}_{F}^{(k)}=v_{F}^{(k)},\ \forall k\geq 3.

(40)

Then, under the new orientation, we have the tensor-product Hilbert space
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{F,\gamma_{F}}
possessing the basis

\widetilde{|i_{1},i_{2},\cdots,i_{2|\gamma_{F}|}\rangle}:=|i_{1},i_{2}\rangle_{\tilde{v}_{F}^{(1)}}\otimes|i_{3},i_{4}\rangle_{\tilde{v}_{F}^{(2)}}\otimes\cdots\otimes|i_{2|\gamma_{F}|-1},i_{2|\gamma_{F}|}\rangle_{\tilde{v}_{F}^{(\gamma_{F})}}.

(41)

By definition,
\widetilde{|i_{1},i_{2},\cdots,i_{2|\gamma_{F}|}\rangle}
referes to the cylindrical funciton

\tilde{\Psi}_{\vec{i}}=\rho_{i_{3}}^{\dagger}\rho_{i_{4}}^{\dagger}\rho_{i_{1}}^{\dagger}\rho_{i_{2}}^{\dagger}\cdots\rho_{i_{n}}^{\dagger}=(-1)^{(i_{1}+i_{2})(i_{3}+i_{4})}\Psi_{\vec{i}^{\prime}},

(42)

where
\vec{i}^{\prime}=\{i_{3},i_{4},i_{1},i_{2},\cdots,i_{2|\gamma_{F}|}\}
. Eq. (
42
) gives the equivalence relation between
\mathcal{H}_{F,\gamma_{F}}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{F,\gamma_{F}}

\widetilde{|i_{1},i_{2},\cdots,i_{2|\gamma_{F}|}\rangle}=(-1)^{(i_{1}+i_{2})(i_{3}+i_{4})}|i_{3},i_{4},i_{1},i_{2},\cdots,i_{2|\gamma_{F}|}\rangle.

(43)

This equivalence relation can be analogously defined between the tensor product spaces with different orientations. The fermionic Hilbert space with respect to
\gamma_{F}
is actually the space of equivalence classes associated with this equivalence relation.

Indeed, the extra sign in (
43
) can be systematically obtained by introducing the notion of graded objects. One can refer to Appendix
B
and the reference therein for more details on the this notion.
In our work, the Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{H}_{v_{F}}
are graded. The degree
{\mathfrak{d}}(i_{1},i_{2})
of each
|i_{1},i_{2}\rangle_{v_{F}}
is

{\mathfrak{d}}(i_{1},i_{2})=i_{1}+i_{2}\mod 2.

(44)

The operator algebra on
\mathcal{H}_{v_{F}}
is also graded. By definition, the degrees of the operators
\hat{\zeta}_{v_{F},A}
and
\hat{\zeta}_{v_{F},A}^{\dagger}
are

{\mathfrak{d}}(\hat{\zeta}_{v_{F},A})=1={\mathfrak{d}}(\hat{\zeta}_{v_{F},A}^{\dagger}).

(45)

A general principle to deal with these graded objects is that, whenever we swap two items, an additional sign appears by the rule
xy=(-1)^{{\mathfrak{d}}(x){\mathfrak{d}}(y)}yx
.
Following this rule, we can obtain the identity (
43
) manifestly.

Given a vertex
v_{F}
, let
\mathcal{H}_{v_{F}}^{\rm irr}
be the subspace of
\mathcal{H}_{v_{F}}
spanned by
|0,1\rangle_{v_{F}}
,
|1,0\rangle_{v_{F}}
and
|1,1\rangle_{v_{F}}
. Then the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{F,\gamma_{F}}^{\rm irr}
with respect to
\gamma_{F}
is defined as

\mathcal{H}_{F,\gamma_{F}}^{\rm irr}=\bigotimes_{v_{F}\in\gamma_{F}}\mathcal{H}_{v_{F}}^{\rm irr},

(46)

such that
{\mathbf{Cyl}}_{F}
can be decomposed as

{\mathbf{Cyl}}_{F}=\bigoplus_{\gamma_{F}}\mathcal{H}_{F,\gamma_{F}}^{\rm irr}\oplus\mathbb{C}.

(47)

The kinematical Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
of the entire system is the tensor product of
\mathcal{H}_{G}
and
\mathcal{H}_{F}
, i.e.,

\mathcal{H}=\overline{\mathcal{H}_{G}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{F}}.

(48)

A densely subspace
{\mathbf{Cyl_{tot}}}
of
\mathcal{H}
is

{\mathbf{Cyl_{tot}}}={\mathbf{Cyl}}\otimes{\mathbf{Cyl_{F}}}.

(49)

The states in
{\mathbf{Cyl_{tot}}}
will be called the cylindrical states. To obtain a cylindrical state, one needs a graph
\gamma=\gamma_{G}\cup\gamma_{F}
, where the gravitational graph
\gamma_{G}
is constituted of edges and their ending points as vertices, and the fermionic graph
\gamma_{F}
contains only vertices . To define a state with respect to
\gamma
, besides the data for a (gauge variant) LQG spin network state, one also needs to assign to each fermionic vertex
v_{F}
a state
|i_{1}(v_{F}),i_{2}(v_{F})\rangle_{v_{F}}
with
i_{1}(v_{F}),i_{2}(v_{F})\in\{0,1\}
. In principle, a fermionic vertex
v_{F}
can be located anywhere, regardless of the given gravitational graph
\gamma_{G}
. However, if
v_{F}
is chosen as point in
e\in E(\gamma_{G})
but
v_{F}\notin V(\gamma_{G})
, we can always split
e
at
v_{F}
to define a new graph
\tilde{\gamma}_{G}
. Then
v_{F}
becomes a vertex of
\tilde{\gamma}_{G}
. Moreover, because of
\gamma_{G}\subset\tilde{\gamma}_{G}
, every cylindrical function with respect to
\gamma_{G}
can be rewritten by using
\tilde{\gamma}_{G}
. Thus, it is sufficient to consider those graphs
\gamma=\gamma_{G}\cup\gamma_{F}
where each fermionic vertex
v_{F}
satisfies either
v_{F}\in V(\gamma_{G})
or
v_{F}\notin\gamma_{G}
. Then, in
V(\gamma_{G})
, there could be bivalent vertices which is a fermionic vertex. These vertices serve to split edge. Thus, by (
15
), they are just spurious vertices. However, in contrast to (
15
), we can put trivial SU(2) representation at these vertices to decompose the Hilbert space with respect to graphs. Moreover precisely, let
\Gamma_{o}
be the set of graphs
\gamma=\gamma_{G}\cup\gamma_{F}
where each fermionic vertex
v_{F}
satisfies either
v_{F}\in V(\gamma_{G})
or
v_{F}\notin\gamma_{G}
. Consider the assignments
\vec{j}^{\prime}
to
E(\gamma)
such that each representation is nontrivial. Besides, let
\vec{l}^{\prime}
denote assignments of irreducible representations to vertices of
\gamma
which are non-trivial at each fake vertex of
\gamma_{G}
where a vertex
v\in V(\gamma_{G})
is fake if
v\notin\gamma_{F}
and it is spurious as defined in (
15
). Then, we define

\mathcal{H}_{G,\gamma_{G}}^{\rm irr}:={\bigoplus_{\vec{j}^{\prime},\vec{l}^{\prime}}}\mathcal{H}_{G,\gamma_{G}}^{(\vec{j}^{\prime},\vec{l}^{\prime})},

(50)

which gives us the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{\gamma}^{\rm irr}
as

\mathcal{H}_{\gamma}^{\rm irr}=\mathcal{H}_{G,\gamma_{G}}^{\rm irr}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{F,\gamma_{F}}^{\rm irr}.

(51)

Then we have the decomposition

{\mathbf{Cyl_{tot}}}=\bigoplus_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{o}}\mathcal{H}_{\gamma}^{\rm irr}\oplus\mathbb{C}.

(52)

IV
The constraint operators for gravity coupled to fermion field

IV.1
the Gauss constraint

Classically, the Gauss constraint
G[\lambda]
reads

G[\lambda]=\int_{\Sigma}\differential^{3}x\lambda^{m}\left(\frac{1}{\kappa\beta}D_{a}E^{a}_{m}+\frac{1}{2}\xi^{\dagger}\sigma_{m}\xi\right).

(53)

It is straightfoward to quantize it as the operator

\widehat{G[\lambda]}=\sum_{v}\lambda^{m}(v)\hat{G}_{v,m}

(54)

with

\hat{G}_{v,m}=\hbar\sum_{[e]}\hat{J}_{m}^{v,[e]}+\hbar\,\hat{\zeta}_{v,A}^{\dagger}\frac{(\sigma_{m})_{AB}}{2}\hat{\zeta}_{v,B}

(55)

Let us use
\hat{\mathcal{J}}_{v,m}
to denote the second term in (
55
), namely

\hat{\mathcal{J}}_{v,m}=\hat{\zeta}_{v,A}^{\dagger}\frac{(\sigma_{m})_{AB}}{2}\hat{\zeta}_{v,B}.

(56)

On the fermionic Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{v}
at vertex
v
, the action of
\hat{\mathcal{J}}_{v,m}
reads

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{J}}_{v,m}|0,0\rangle_{v}

\displaystyle=0,\ \hat{\mathcal{J}}_{v,m}|1,1\rangle_{v}=0

(57)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{J}}_{v,m}\left(|1,0\rangle_{v},|0,1\rangle_{v}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(|1,0\rangle_{v},|0,1\rangle_{v}\right)\frac{\sigma_{m}}{2}.

According to (
57
), the operators
\hat{\mathcal{J}}_{v,m}
for all
m=1,2,3
behave as the angular moment operators. Thus, the operator
\hat{\mathcal{J}}_{v,m}
generates an SU(2) action on
\mathcal{H}_{v}
as

u\triangleright|\phi\rangle_{v}=\begin{pmatrix}|1,0\rangle_{v},|0,1\rangle_{v}\end{pmatrix}u\begin{pmatrix}\phi_{10}\\
\phi_{01}\end{pmatrix}+\phi_{00}|0,0\rangle_{v}+\phi_{11}|1,1\rangle_{v}.

(58)

where
|\phi\rangle_{v}=\sum_{ij}\phi_{ij}|i,j\rangle_{v}
and
u\in{\mathrm{SU(2)}}
.
Therefore,
\mathcal{H}_{v}
becomes a reducible representation space of SU(2). The 1-dimensional space spanned by either
|0,0\rangle_{v}
or
|1,1\rangle_{v}
is the trivial representation space, and the 2-dimensional space spanned by
|0,1\rangle_{v}
and
|1,0\rangle_{v}
is the
1/2
-representation space where
|0,1\rangle_{v}
and
|1,0\rangle_{v}
serve as the standard basis according to (
57
). This fact leads to the decomposition

\mathcal{H}_{v}\equiv\mathcal{H}_{0}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{0}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{1/2},

(59)

where
\mathcal{H}_{j}
denote the
j
-representation space of SU(2).

For a graph
\gamma=\gamma_{G}\cup\gamma_{F}
, a spin
j_{e}
is assigned to the edge
e\subset\gamma_{G}
. Then at each vertex
v\in V(\gamma)
there is the Hilbert space

\mathcal{H}_{v}^{\rm tot}=\bigotimes_{e\text{ starts from }v}\mathcal{H}_{j_{e}}\otimes\bigotimes_{e^{\prime}\text{ targets }v}\mathcal{H}_{j_{e^{\prime}}}^{*}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{v}

(60)

where
\mathcal{H}_{j}^{*}
denotes the dual space of
\mathcal{H}_{j}
. On
\mathcal{H}_{v}^{\rm tot}
, the infinitesimal SU(2) action gives the Gauss constraint. Thus, the solution space to the Gauss constraint is

\mathcal{H}^{\rm Gau}=\bigotimes_{x\in V(\gamma)}{\rm Inv}\left(\mathcal{H}_{v}^{\rm tot}\right),

(61)

where
{\rm Inv}\left(\mathcal{H}_{v}^{\rm tot}\right)\subset\mathcal{H}_{v}^{\rm tot}
is the SU(2)-invariant subspace.
To see
{\rm Inv}(\mathcal{H}_{v}^{\rm tot})
more precisely, let us assume all edges at
v
are outgoing without loss of generality.
Then we have

\mathcal{H}_{v}^{\rm tot}=\bigotimes_{e\text{ at }v}\mathcal{H}_{j_{e}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{v}.

(62)

Given an order of the edges at
v
, one can choose an orthonormal basis of
\bigotimes\limits_{e\text{ at }v}\mathcal{H}_{j_{e}}
composed of vectors
|k_{2},k_{3},\cdots,k_{n},M\rangle
satisfying

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{3}(L_{i}^{(l)})^{2}|k_{2},k_{3},\cdots,k_{n},M\rangle=

\displaystyle k_{l}(k_{l}+1)|k_{2},k_{3},\cdots,k_{n},M\rangle,\ \forall l=2,\cdots,n

(63)

\displaystyle L_{3}^{(n)}|k_{2},k_{3},\cdots,k_{n},M\rangle=

\displaystyle M|k_{2},k_{3},\cdots,k_{n},M\rangle,

\displaystyle L_{1}^{(n)}|k_{2},k_{3},\cdots,k_{n},M\rangle=

\displaystyle\sum_{s=\pm 1}\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{(k_{n}-sM)(k_{n}+sM+1)}|k_{2},k_{3},\cdots,k_{n},M+s\rangle

\displaystyle L_{2}^{(n)}|k_{2},k_{3},\cdots,k_{n},M\rangle=

\displaystyle\sum_{s=\pm 1}\frac{-is}{2}\sqrt{(k_{n}-sM)(k_{n}+sM+1)}|k_{2},k_{3},\cdots,k_{n},M+s\rangle

with
\hat{L}_{i}^{(l)}:=\sum_{k=1}^{l}\hat{J}_{i}^{v,e_{k}}.

Let us define
\mathrm{Inv}\left(\mathcal{H}_{G}^{(v)}\right)\subset\bigotimes\limits_{e\text{ at }v}\mathcal{H}_{j_{e}}
as the subspace spanned by
|k_{2},k_{3},\cdots,k_{n-1},0,0\rangle
for all possible
k_{2},k_{3},\cdots,k_{n-1}
. One has

L_{i}^{(n)}|k_{2},k_{3},\cdots,k_{n-1},0,0\rangle=0,\ \forall i=1,2,3.

(64)

Moreover, with the vectors
|k_{2},k_{3},\cdots,k_{n-1},1/2,M\rangle
, we define

|k_{2},k_{3},\cdots,k_{n-1}\rangle_{\rm tot}={\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}}\left|k_{2},k_{3},\cdots,k_{n-1},\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right\rangle\otimes|0,1\rangle_{v}-{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}}\left|k_{2},k_{3},\cdots,k_{n-1},\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2}\right\rangle\otimes|1,0\rangle_{v}.

(65)

Then one has

(L_{i}^{(n)}+\hat{\mathcal{J}}_{v,i})|k_{2},k_{3},\cdots,k_{n-1}\rangle_{\rm tot}=0,\ \forall i=1,2,3.

(66)

Let
\mathcal{H}_{\rm inv}\subset\mathcal{H}_{v}^{\rm tot}
denote the subspace spanned by
|k_{2},k_{3},\cdots,k_{n-1}\rangle_{\rm tot}
for all possible
k_{2},k_{3},\cdots,k_{n-1}
. Then
{\rm Inv}(\mathcal{H}_{v}^{\rm tot})
can be decomposed as

{\rm Inv}(\mathcal{H}_{v}^{\rm tot})=\left(\mathrm{Inv}\left(\mathcal{H}_{G}^{(v)}\right)\otimes|0,0\rangle_{v}\right)\oplus\left(\mathrm{Inv}\left(\mathcal{H}_{G}^{(v)}\right)\otimes|1,1\rangle_{v}\right)\oplus\mathcal{H}_{\rm inv},

(67)

where
\mathrm{Inv}\left(\mathcal{H}_{G}^{(v)}\right)\otimes|i_{1},i_{2}\rangle_{v}
is the space composed of vectors
|\psi\rangle\otimes|i_{1},i_{2}\rangle
for all
|\psi\rangle\in\mathrm{Inv}\left(\mathcal{H}_{G}^{(v)}\right)
.

Let
v
be a
n
-valence gauge invariant fermionic vertex, where the
i
th edge
e_{i}
is assigned to a spin
j_{i}
.
According to the decomposition (
67
), the gauge invariant Hilbert space
{\rm Inv}(\mathcal{H}_{v}^{\rm tot})
contains the subspace
\mathcal{H}_{\rm inv}
, isometric to the gauge invariant Hilbert space of a
(n+1)
-valence pure-gravity vertex where the
i
th edge for
1\leq i\leq n
is assigned to spin
j_{i}
, and the
(n+1)
th, the spin
1/2
.
Then once we consider the volume operator at
v
, this extra spin
1/2
will also have contribution.
Since the extra spin
1/2
originates from the fermion filed, one gets an intuitive picture that fermion field contributes to the volume of a vertex. Moreover, a
n
-valence vertex in pure-LQG is always regarded as a polyhedron whose faces are dual to the edges. The flux operators
\hat{J}^{v,e}_{i}
associated to each edge
e
have the geometric interpretation of the area vector of the dual face. Then the pure-LQG Gauss constraint is just the closure condition
\sum_{e}\hat{J}^{v,e}_{i}=0
ensuring that the faces can form a closed polyhedron. Now, the fermion field is involved. Then the Gauss constraint (
55
) implies

\sum_{e}\hat{J}^{v,e}_{i}=-\hat{\mathcal{J}}_{v,i}

(68)

where the right hand side does not vanish in general. Thus, the faces dual to the edges could not give a closed polyhedron for states in
\mathcal{H}_{\rm inv}
. By (
68
), the area defect of this unclosed polyhedron is filled by
\hat{\mathcal{J}}_{v,i}
, i.e. the fermion spin at the vertex (see
mansuroglu2021fermion
for more details on the fermion spin). A direct consequence of the above discussion is that the volume of a 3-valence vertex with fermion does not vanish any more for states in
\mathcal{H}_{\rm inv}
. Let
j_{i}
with

\widehat{H(\delta)}

\gamma

\displaystyle\langle\widehat{H^{(1)}(v;\delta)}{}^{\dagger}\Psi_{\gamma}|\Phi_{\gamma^{\prime}}\rangle=\langle\Psi_{\gamma}|\widehat{H^{(1)}(v;\delta)}\Phi_{\gamma^{\prime}}\rangle

(103)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\in E(\gamma^{\prime})\\
e\text{ at }v\end{subarray}}\kappa\hbar\beta N(v)\left\langle(\hat{\mathfrak{h}}_{1}(v,e;\delta)^{\dagger}-\hat{\theta}(v)\sigma^{i}\hat{\theta}(v)\hat{J}_{i}^{v,e})\Psi_{\gamma}\middle|\Phi_{\gamma^{\prime}}\right\rangle

with

\gamma

\Psi_{\gamma}
with
\gamma
shown in Fig.

. Moreover, the fermion state of

. Then, for each graph

obtained from

.
Similar argument can be done for all of states in

at

. Besides, it is manifest that there are infinitely many such Hilbert spaces that are excluded from the domain of

(see (

Finally, let us discuss why we choose

cannot be promoted to a well-defined operator in the diffeomorphism invariant Hilbert space.

IV.2.1
the vertex Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{\rm vtx}

Given a graph
\gamma
, we will consider the subgroup of
C^{m}
semianalytic diffeomorphisms which act on
V(\ker(\gamma))
trivially, i.e. the diffeomorphisms preserving every vertex of
\ker(\gamma)
. This subgroup will be denoted by
{\rm Diff}_{V(\ker(\gamma))}
. There are two subgroups of
{\rm Diff}_{V(\ker(\gamma))}
. The first one, denoted by
{\rm Diff}_{\gamma}
, preserves
\gamma
. The other one, denoted by
{\rm Diff}^{0}_{\gamma}
, preserves every edge of
\gamma
. Hence
{\rm Diff}^{0}_{\gamma}
is a subgroup of
{\rm Diff}_{\gamma}
.
The quotient

{\rm GS}_{\gamma}={\rm Diff}_{\gamma}/{\rm Diff}^{0}_{\gamma}

(104)

is the group of graph symmetries of
\gamma
.
{\rm GS}_{\gamma}
is a finite group with order
|{\rm GS}_{\gamma}|
. Given a state
\Psi_{\gamma}
with respect to
\gamma
and a diffeomorphism
\phi
,
\phi\star\Psi_{\gamma}
denotes the pullback of
\Psi
under
\phi
. The averaging with respect to
{\rm GS}_{\gamma}
defines a projection
P_{\gamma}:\mathcal{H}_{\gamma}^{\rm irr}\to\mathcal{H}_{\gamma}^{\rm irr}
,

P_{\gamma}:\Psi_{\gamma}\mapsto\frac{1}{|{\rm GS}_{\gamma}|}\sum_{\phi\in{\rm GS}_{\gamma}}\phi\star\Psi_{\gamma}.

(105)

The averaging with respect to the remaining diffeomorphisms
{\rm Diff}_{V(\ker(\gamma))}/{\rm Diff}_{\gamma}
defines

\eta(\Psi_{\gamma}):=\sum_{\phi\in{\rm Diff}_{V(\ker(\gamma))}/{\rm Diff}_{\gamma}}\phi\star P_{\gamma}(\Psi_{\gamma})=\frac{1}{|{\rm GS}_{\gamma}|}\sum_{\phi\in{\rm Diff}_{V(\ker(\gamma))}/{\rm Diff}_{\gamma}^{0}}\phi\star\Psi_{\gamma}.

(106)

Obviously,
\eta(\Psi_{\gamma})
belongs to the algebraic dual space
{\mathbf{Cyl_{tot}}}^{*}
of
{\mathbf{Cyl_{tot}}}
. Taking advantage the decomposition (
52
), one can extend
\eta
to a well-defined operation on
{\mathbf{Cyl_{tot}}}
. Let
\eta({\mathbf{Cyl_{tot}}})\subset{\mathbf{Cyl_{tot}}}^{*}
denote the image of
{\mathbf{Cyl_{tot}}}
under
\eta
. The group averaging procedure naturally endows
\eta({\mathbf{Cyl_{tot}}})
with an inner product

\left(\eta(\Psi_{\gamma}),\eta(\Psi_{\gamma^{\prime}}^{\prime})\right)=(\eta(\Psi_{\gamma})|\Psi_{\gamma^{\prime}}^{\prime}\rangle,

(107)

where
(\eta(\Psi_{\gamma})|\Psi_{\gamma^{\prime}}^{\prime}\rangle
, the action of
\eta(\Psi_{\gamma})\in{\mathbf{Cyl_{tot}}}^{*}
on
\Psi_{\gamma^{\prime}}
, is given by

(\eta(\Psi_{\gamma})|\Psi_{\gamma^{\prime}}^{\prime}\rangle=\sum_{\phi\in{\rm Diff}_{V(\ker(\gamma))}/{\rm Diff}_{\gamma}^{0}}\langle\phi\star\Psi_{\gamma},\Psi_{\gamma^{\prime}}^{\prime}\rangle.

(108)

Here,
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is the inner product in
\mathcal{H}
. Then the vertex Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{\rm vtx}
is the completion of
\eta({\mathbf{Cyl_{tot}}})
, i.e,

\mathcal{H}_{\rm vtx}=\overline{\eta({\mathbf{Cyl_{tot}}})}.

(109)

Given a finite subset
W\subset\Sigma
, let
\Gamma_{\ker}(W)
be the collection of graphs
\gamma
satisfying
V(\ker(\gamma))=W
. Two graphs
\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\in\Gamma_{\ker}(W)
are said to be equivalent, denoted by
\gamma\sim_{d}\gamma^{\prime}
, if there exists a diffeomorphism
\phi\in{\rm Diff}_{W}
, i.e., a diffeomorphism preserving
W
, such that
\phi(\gamma)=\gamma^{\prime}
. Let
[\Gamma_{\ker}(W)]
be the quotient space
\Gamma_{\ker}(W)/\sim_{d}
,
[\gamma]\in[\Gamma_{\ker}(W)]
be the equivalence class of
\gamma
, and
\mathcal{S}_{\gamma}^{\rm irr}
be the image of
P_{\gamma}
.
Then
\eta:\mathcal{S}_{\gamma}^{\rm irr}\to{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm vtx}
is isometric. By
\eta(\mathcal{S}_{\gamma}^{\rm irr})
denoting the image of
\mathcal{S}_{\gamma}^{\rm irr}
under
\eta
, one has

\eta(\mathcal{S}_{\gamma}^{\rm irr})=\eta(\mathcal{S}_{\gamma^{\prime}}^{\rm irr}),\ \forall\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\in[\gamma].

(110)

Thus, we can define

\eta(\mathcal{S}_{[\gamma]}^{\rm irr}):=\eta(\mathcal{S}_{\gamma^{\prime}}^{\rm irr})

(111)

by choosing arbitrary
\gamma^{\prime}\in[\gamma]
.
Let
{\rm FS}(\Sigma)
be the set of finite subsets of
\Sigma
. Then we have

\eta({\mathbf{Cyl_{tot}}})=\bigoplus_{W\in{\rm FS}(\Sigma)}\bigoplus_{[\gamma]\in{[\Gamma_{\ker}(W)]}}\eta({\mathcal{S}}_{[\gamma]}^{\rm irr})\oplus\mathbb{C}.

(112)

The factor
1/|{\rm GS}_{\gamma}|
in (
107
) ensures that
\eta
is an isometric between
\mathcal{H}_{\gamma}^{\rm irr}
and
\eta(\mathcal{H}_{\gamma}^{\rm irr})
, i.e.,

\eta({\mathbf{Cyl_{tot}}})\cong\bigoplus_{W\in{\rm FS}(\Sigma)}\bigoplus_{[\gamma]\in{[\Gamma_{\ker}(W)]}}{\mathcal{S}}_{\sigma([\gamma])}^{\rm irr}\oplus\mathbb{C}.

(113)

where
\sigma([\gamma])\in[\gamma]
is a representative of
[\gamma]
fixed once and for all.

IV.2.2
the Hamiltonian operator on
\mathcal{H}_{\rm vtx}

Given
\Psi_{\gamma}\in\mathcal{S}_{\gamma}^{\rm irr}
, let
\widehat{H(\delta)}{}^{*}
be the dual of
\widehat{H(\delta)}
acting on
\eta(\Psi_{\gamma})
, i.e.,

\left(\widehat{H(\delta)}{}^{*}\eta(\Psi_{\gamma})\middle|\Phi_{\gamma^{\prime}}\right\rangle=\left(\eta(\Psi_{\gamma})\middle|\widehat{H(\delta)}\Phi_{\gamma^{\prime}}\right\rangle,\forall\gamma^{\prime}\in\Gamma_{o},\ \Phi_{\gamma^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{H}_{\gamma^{\prime}}^{\rm irr}.

(114)

The operator
\widehat{H(\delta)}
takes the form
\widehat{H(\delta)}=\sum_{v}N(v)\widehat{\sqrt{V_{v}^{-1}}}\hat{O}(v)\widehat{\sqrt{V_{v}^{-1}}}
with
\hat{O}(v)
representing some operator. Due to the inverse volume operators, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\to 0}\left(\widehat{H(\delta)}{}^{*}\eta(\Psi_{\gamma})\middle|\Phi_{\gamma^{\prime}}\right\rangle=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\sum_{v\in V(\ker(\gamma))}N(v)\left(\eta(\Psi_{\gamma})\middle|\widehat{\sqrt{V_{v}^{-1}}}\hat{O}(v)\widehat{\sqrt{V_{v}^{-1}}}\Phi_{\gamma^{\prime}}\right\rangle.

(115)

Recalling (
98
), we will consider the right hand term by term.

Let us begin with the operator
\widehat{H^{(1)}(v;\delta)}
with
v\in V(\ker(\gamma))
. By (
115
), we need to investigate

I_{v}(\gamma^{\prime},\delta)=\left(\eta(\Psi_{\gamma})\middle|\widehat{H^{(1)}(v;\delta)}\Phi_{\gamma^{\prime}}\right\rangle

(116)

for all graphs
\gamma^{\prime}
taking
v
as a vertex. By definition, we can split
\widehat{H^{(1)}(v;\delta)}
into two parts such that

\widehat{H^{(1)}(v;\delta)}\Phi_{\gamma^{\prime}}=\kappa\hbar\beta N(v)\hat{\mathfrak{h}}_{1}(v;\delta)\Phi_{\gamma^{\prime}}-\kappa\hbar\beta N(v)\hat{\mathfrak{h}}_{2}(v)\Phi_{\gamma^{\prime}}

(117)

with
\hat{\mathfrak{h}}_{1}(v;\delta)\Phi_{\gamma^{\prime}}=\sum_{e\text{ at }v}\hat{\mathfrak{h}}_{1}(v,e;\delta)\Phi_{\gamma^{\prime}}
and
\hat{\mathfrak{h}}_{2}(v)=\sum_{[e]\text{ at }v}\hat{\theta}^{\dagger}(v)\sigma^{i}\hat{\theta}(v)\hat{J}_{i}^{v,[e]}
. Substituting (
117
) into (
116
), we will be concerned about the term

I_{v}^{(1)}(\gamma^{\prime},\delta)=\left(\eta(\Psi_{\gamma})\middle|\hat{\mathfrak{h}}_{1}(v;\delta)\Phi_{\gamma^{\prime}}\right\rangle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\in E(\gamma^{\prime})\\
e\text{ at }v\end{subarray}}\left(\eta(\Psi_{\gamma})\middle|\hat{\mathfrak{h}}_{1}(v,e;\delta)\Phi_{\gamma^{\prime}}\right\rangle.

(118)

Substituting (
106
) into (
118
), we get

\displaystyle I_{v}^{(1)}(\gamma^{\prime},\delta)=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\in E(\gamma^{\prime})\\
e\text{ at }v\end{subarray}}\sum_{\phi\in{\rm Diff}^{\prime}}\left\langle\hat{\mathfrak{h}}_{1}(v,e;\delta)^{\dagger}(\phi\star\Psi_{\gamma})\middle|\Phi_{\gamma^{\prime}}\right\rangle

(119)

with
{\rm Diff}^{\prime}:={\rm Diff}_{V(\ker(\gamma))}/{\rm Diff}_{\gamma}
for abbreviation,
where we employ
\hat{\mathfrak{h}}_{1}(v,e;\delta)^{\dagger}=\hat{J}_{i}^{v,e}\hat{\theta}^{\dagger}(v)\sigma^{i}h_{e(v,\delta)}^{-1}\hat{\theta}(t_{e(v,\delta)})
. Note that the summation in the right hand side of (
119
) is over edges of
\gamma^{\prime}
, while the operator
\hat{\mathfrak{h}}_{1}(v,e;\delta)^{\dagger}
acts on states associated to graphs diffeomorphism to
\gamma
. Thus, we
cannot
get
I^{(1)}_{v}(\gamma^{\prime},\delta)=\left(\hat{\mathfrak{h}}_{1}(v;\delta)^{\dagger}\eta(\Psi_{\gamma})|\Phi_{\gamma^{\prime}}\right\rangle
naively from (
119
).

Consider the action of
\hat{\mathfrak{h}}_{1}(v,e;\delta)^{\dagger}
on
D^{j_{e}}_{m_{e}n_{e}}(h_{e(v,\delta)})\otimes D^{j_{e}^{\prime}}_{m_{e}^{\prime}n_{e}^{\prime}}(h_{e\setminus e(v,\delta)})\otimes|k_{1},k_{2}\rangle_{t_{e(v,\delta)}}
, a general state associated to the graph
\{e\}\cup\{t_{e(v,\delta)}\}
, where
e\setminus e(v,\delta)
is the other segment of
e
complementing
e(v,\delta)
.
Here and in the rest, by a state associated to a graph
\alpha
, we refer to a state in
\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}^{\rm irr}
. Acting on the state, the holonomy
h_{e(v,\delta)}^{-1}
in
\hat{\mathfrak{h}}_{1}(v,e;\delta)^{\dagger}
changes the spin
j_{e}
on
e(v,\delta)
to
j_{e}\pm\frac{1}{2}
. Then, for
j_{e}=\frac{1}{2}
, the spin on
e(v,\delta)
is changed to
0
, i.e., the segment
e(v,\delta)
is erased. Thus, for
j_{e}=\frac{1}{2}
, acted by the holonomy, the state becomes a combination of one state associated
\{e\}
and the other state associated to
\{e\setminus e(v,\delta)\}
. However, the operator
\hat{J}_{i}^{v,e}
in
\hat{\mathfrak{h}}_{1}(v;\delta)^{\dagger}
will annihilate this one associated to
\{e\setminus e(v,\delta)\}
. Consequently, acted by
\hat{\mathfrak{h}}_{1}(v;\delta)^{\dagger}
, the resulting state is still a state associated to
\{e\}
, regardless of the change of the fermionic vertices by
\hat{\theta}^{\dagger}(v)
and
\hat{\theta}(t_{e(v,\delta)})
.

\phi_{o}\star\Psi_{\gamma}
for some diffeomorphism
\phi_{o}
is a state associated to the graph
\phi_{o}(\gamma)
. Let
\tilde{\gamma}_{G}
and
\tilde{\gamma}_{F}
denote the gravitational sector and the fermionic sector of
\phi_{o}(\gamma)
, i.e.,
\phi_{o}(\gamma)=\tilde{\gamma}_{G}\cup\tilde{\gamma}_{F}
. Then, the discussion in the last paragraph tells that
\hat{\mathfrak{h}}_{1}(v;\delta)^{\dagger}\phi_{o}\star\Psi_{\gamma}
is a state associated to some graph whose gravitational sector is
\tilde{\gamma}_{G}\cup e(v,\delta)
. Thus,
\left\langle\hat{\mathfrak{h}}_{1}(v,e;\delta)^{\dagger}(\phi_{o}\star\Psi_{\gamma})\middle|\Phi_{\gamma^{\prime}}\right\rangle\neq 0
for
\Phi_{\gamma^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{H}_{\gamma^{\prime}}^{\rm irr}
implies
e\subset\tilde{\gamma}_{G}\cup e(v,\delta)
, which means either
e\subset\phi_{o}(\gamma)
, or
e(v,\delta)\not\subset\phi_{o}(\gamma)
but
e\setminus e(v,\delta)\subset\phi_{o}(\gamma)
.

Let us first consider the case where
e(v,\delta)\not\subset\phi_{o}(\gamma)
but
e\setminus e(v,\delta)\subset\phi_{o}(\gamma)
such that
\left\langle\hat{\mathfrak{h}}_{1}(v,e;\delta)^{\dagger}(\phi_{o}\star\Psi_{\gamma})\middle|\Phi_{\gamma^{\prime}}\right\rangle\neq 0
. The hypothesis
\left\langle\hat{\mathfrak{h}}_{1}(v,e;\delta)^{\dagger}(\phi_{o}\star\Psi_{\gamma})\middle|\Phi_{\gamma^{\prime}}\right\rangle\neq 0
implies
\tilde{\gamma}_{G}\cup e(v,\delta)=\gamma^{\prime}_{G}
where
\gamma^{\prime}_{G}
is the gravitational sector of
\gamma^{\prime}
. Together with
e(v,\delta)\not\subset\phi_{o}(\gamma)
, one gets
\tilde{\gamma}_{G}=\gamma^{\prime}_{G}\setminus e(v,\delta)
. Since
e(v,\delta)
is a segment of an edge
e\subset\gamma^{\prime}_{G}
,
t_{e(v,\delta)}
is a 1-valence vertex in
\tilde{\gamma}_{G}
. Furthermore,
t_{e(v,\delta)}
is a 1-valence fermionic vertex because of
\hat{\mathfrak{h}}_{1}(v,e;\delta)^{\dagger}(\phi_{o}\star\Psi_{\gamma})\neq 0
. Thus,
t_{e(v,\delta)}
is
not
a removable vertex, i.e., all of the diffeomorphisms in
{\rm Diff}^{\prime}
preserve this vertex. Now, let us come to
\delta^{\prime}<\delta
. If
\sum_{\phi\in{\rm Diff}^{\prime}}\left\langle\hat{\mathfrak{h}}_{1}(v,e;\delta^{\prime})^{\dagger}(\phi\star\Psi_{\gamma})\middle|\Phi_{\gamma^{\prime}}\right\rangle=\sum_{\phi\in{\rm Diff}^{\prime}}\left\langle\hat{\mathfrak{h}}_{1}(v,e;\delta^{\prime})^{\dagger}(\phi\star\phi_{o}\star\Psi_{\gamma})\middle|\Phi_{\gamma^{\prime}}\right\rangle
still does not vanish, there have to exist another diffeomorphism
\phi_{o}^{\prime}
which can move a fermionic vertex in
\phi_{o}(\gamma)
to
t_{e(v,\delta^{\prime})}
such that
\hat{\mathfrak{h}}_{1}(v,e;\delta^{\prime})^{\dagger}(\phi_{o}^{\prime}\star\phi_{o}\star\Psi_{\gamma})\neq 0
, and simultaneously preserve all of the other vertices but not necessarily
t_{e(v,\delta)}
in
\phi_{o}(\gamma)
such that
\left\langle\hat{\mathfrak{h}}_{1}(v,e;\delta^{\prime})^{\dagger}(\phi_{o}^{\prime}\star\phi_{o}\star\Psi_{\gamma})\middle|\Phi_{\gamma^{\prime}}\right\rangle\neq 0
. This can be done only if
\phi_{o}^{\prime}
can move
t_{e(v,\delta)}
to
t_{e(v,\delta^{\prime})}
which is impossible because
t_{e(v,\delta)}
is preserved by all diffeomorphisms in
{\rm Diff}^{\prime}
. Therefore,
\sum_{\phi\in{\rm Diff}^{\prime}}\left\langle\hat{\mathfrak{h}}_{1}(v,e;\delta^{\prime})^{\dagger}(\phi\star\Psi_{\gamma})\middle|\Phi_{\gamma^{\prime}}\right\rangle=0
for all
\delta^{\prime}<\delta
. This discussion tells that for sufficiently small
\delta
, the case with
e(v,\delta)\not\subset\phi_{o}(\gamma)
but
e\setminus e(v,\delta)\subset\phi_{o}(\gamma)
can be excluded. Because we finally need to consider the limit as
\delta\to 0
, choosing a sufficiently small
\delta
can be done without loss of generality.

For the case with
e\subset\phi_{o}(\gamma)
,
\left\langle\hat{\mathfrak{h}}_{1}(v,e;\delta)^{\dagger}(\phi_{o}\star\Psi_{\gamma})\middle|\Phi_{\gamma^{\prime}}\right\rangle\neq 0
requires that the graph of
\hat{\mathfrak{h}}_{1}(v,e;\delta)^{\dagger}(\phi_{o}\star\Psi_{\gamma})
is equal to
\gamma^{\prime}
. Since
\delta
is chosen to be sufficiently small, in the graph
\gamma^{\prime}
there is no fermionic vertex on the segment
e(v,\delta)\subset e\subset\gamma^{\prime}
, and
t_{e(v,\delta)}\in\gamma^{\prime}
is also not a fermionic vertex. Therefore, for
\left\langle\hat{\mathfrak{h}}_{1}(v,e;\delta)^{\dagger}(\phi_{o}\star\Psi_{\gamma})\middle|\Phi_{\gamma^{\prime}}\right\rangle\neq 0
, there cannot exist any fermionic vertices in
e(v,\delta)\cup\{t_{e(v,\delta)}\}
, where
e(v,\delta)
and
t_{e(v,\delta)}
now are thought of as a segment and a vertex in the graph of
\hat{\mathfrak{h}}_{1}(v,e;\delta)^{\dagger}(\phi_{o}\star\Psi_{\gamma})
. This conclusion has two meanings. At first,
\phi_{o}\in{\rm Diff}^{\prime}
must be the diffeomorphism which moves the closest fermionic vertex to
v
in
e\in\gamma
to
t_{e(v,\delta)}
so that
\hat{\theta}(t_{e(v,\delta)})
can kill it. The closest fermionic vertex to
v
in
e\in\gamma
will be denoted by
v_{F}^{(v,e)}
, and Fig.
3
gives an illustration of
v_{F}^{(v,e)}
. Second, it means that the fermion state at
v_{F}^{(v,e)}
cannot be
|1,1\rangle_{v_{F}^{(v,e)}}
because it cannot be annihilated completely by
\hat{\theta}(t_{v,\delta})
.
For the second point, we introduce a projection
\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{v_{F}}
on the fermion Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{v_{F}}
such that

\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{v_{F}}|i_{1},i_{2}\rangle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}0,&i_{1}=1=i_{2},\\
|i_{1},i_{2}\rangle,&\text{ otherwise. }\end{array}\right.

(120)

The first point makes us decompose
\phi_{o}
as follows. Let
\gamma_{\star}
be the graph of
\gamma^{\prime}
with
v_{F}^{(v,e)}
dropped, and
v\in\gamma
promoted to a new fermionic vertex if it is not. Then, we find a diffeomorphism
\phi_{1}\in{\rm Diff}_{V(\ker(\gamma))}/{\rm Diff}_{\gamma_{\star}}={\rm Diff}_{V(\ker(\gamma_{\star}))}/{\rm Diff}_{\gamma_{\star}}
such that
\phi_{1}(\gamma_{\star})=\gamma^{\prime}
. With
\phi_{1}
, we find
\phi_{2}\in{\rm Diff}_{\phi_{1}(\gamma_{\star})}/{\rm Diff}_{\gamma}
which moves
v_{F}^{(v,e)}
to
t_{e(v,\delta)}
. One thus gets
\phi_{o}=\phi_{2}\circ\phi_{1}
. Introducing
\widehat{\mathfrak{H}}_{v}(e)
as

\widehat{\mathfrak{H}}_{v}(e):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\hat{J}_{i}^{v,e}\hat{\theta}^{\dagger}(v)\sigma^{i}h_{e[v_{F}^{(v,e)},v]}^{-1}\hat{\theta}(v_{F}^{(v,e)})\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{v_{F}^{(v,e)}},&\text{ if }v_{F}^{(v,e)}\text{ exists}.\\
0&\text{\ otherwise}\end{array}\right.

(121)

where

denotes the edge from
v
to
v_{F}^{(v,e)}
. We thus get

\left\langle\hat{\mathfrak{h}}_{1}(v,e;\delta)^{\dagger}(\phi_{o}\star\Psi_{\gamma})\middle|\Phi_{\gamma^{\prime}}\right\rangle=\left\langle\phi_{1}\star(\widehat{\mathfrak{H}}_{v}(e)\Psi_{\gamma})\middle|\Phi_{\gamma^{\prime}}\right\rangle,

(122)

where the right hand side is independent of

V
summation and outlook

This work is concerned about the model of fermion field coupled to LQG. The Gauss and Hamiltonian constraints in this model are studied in details. In the solution to the Gauss constraint, fermion spins and the gravitational spin network intertwine with each other so that the fermion spins contribute to the volume of the spin network vertices. Consequently, the closure condition encoded in the Gauss constraint will no longer be satisfied for the gauge invariant state with non-vanishing fermion spins. In other words, the faces dual to the edges at a fermionic vertex with non-vanishing fermion spin could not form a closed polyhedron, and the area defect of this unclosed polyhedron is filled by the fermion spin. Consequently, in contract to pure-gravity case, a 3-valence gauge invariant vertex with non-vanishing fermion spin will get non-vanishing volume from the fermion spin. The volume of this type of vertices is computed in details.

For the Hamiltonian constraint, the regularization and quantization procedures are presented in details. There are several remarkable issues on the Hamiltonian constraint operator. At first, in order to take the limit of the regularized expression as the regulator approaches 0, we introduce the vertex Hilbert space. By definition, the vertex Hilbert space is the space of cylindrical functions averaged with the diffeomorphisms preserving the unremovable vertices. Thus, the states in the vertex Hilbert space are partially diffeomorphism invariant. The vertex Hilbert space is the dual space to the space of cylindrical functions. Then, the regularized Hamiltonian operator
\widehat{H(\delta)}
can be promoted as an operator
\widehat{H(\delta)}{}^{*}
therein by duality.
Due to the diffeomorphism invariant feature of the vertex Hilbert space, the operators
\widehat{H(\delta)}{}^{*}
for different values of the regulator
\delta
are identical. Thus, the limit of
\widehat{H(\delta)}{}^{*}
as
\delta
approaches 0 can be taken. Moreover, by introducing the vertex Hilbert space, we fix several other problematic issues. Classically, the Hamiltonian can be divided into two parts as
H_{\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}}
plussing its complex conjugate
\overline{H_{\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}}}
.
H(\delta)
can be promoted to the operator
\widehat{H(\delta)}
which is cylindrical consistent and diffeomorphism covariant. However, its adjoint
\widehat{H(\delta)}{}^{\dagger}
, as a candidate of the operator corresponding to
\overline{H_{\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}}}
, is not densely defined, because
\widehat{H(\delta)}
could change graphs by erasing some segments of edges in graphs. These problems on
\widehat{H(\delta)}{}^{\dagger}
are finally solved by introducing the vertex Hilbert space to define limit. In the vertex Hilbert space, the operator
\hat{A}_{F}^{\dagger}
, as the limit of
H(\delta)
, performs in a way such that
a projection is left multiplied in
\hat{A}_{F}^{\dagger}
as a factor. Then, if
\hat{A}_{F}^{\dagger}
erases segments of edges, the projection will annihilate the resulting state, so that the adjoint of
\hat{A}_{F}^{\dagger}
is densely defined.
Finally, in the Hamiltonian constraint operator, there are the operators
\widehat{\mathfrak{H}}_{v}^{\#}(e)
and
\widehat{\mathfrak{H}}_{v}(e)
involved. These two operators tell how a fermion moves in LQG spacetime and influences the background loop quantum geometry. According to our results, the operator
\widehat{\mathfrak{H}}_{v}^{\#}(e)
moves a fermion at the vertex
v
to a point
v_{F}\in e
so that
v_{F}
becomes the closest fermionic vertex to
v
, and simultaneously changes the spin on the segment connecting
v
to
v_{F}
of
e
as well as the intertwiner at
v
. This procedure will be reversed by the operator
\widehat{\mathfrak{H}}_{v}(e)
, which moves a fermion, located at the fermionic vertex
v_{F}^{(v,e)}
closest to
v
in
e
, to the vertex
v
, and simultaneously changes the spin on the segment connecting
v
to
v_{F}^{(v,e)}
in
e
as well as the intertwiner at
v
. In addition,
\widehat{\mathfrak{H}}_{v}(e)
is defined to contain a projection operator
\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{v_{F}^{(e,v)}}
. As a consequence of this operator, the fermion located at, says,
s_{e}
will be confined around
s_{e}
by the state
|1,1\rangle_{v_{F}^{(v,e)}}
located at
v_{F}^{(v,e)}\in e
.

Even though the current work is concerned about the graph changing feature, the framework can be easily adapted to define a graph preserving version of the Hamiltonian constraint operator. Then one can apply this graph preserving operator to the lattice model of fermion coupled to LQG, so that some open issues in lattice fermion field theory can be employed and studied. Moreover, the properties of the fermion Hamiltonian operator are still not well understood although we have discussed some of them. All of these will be left as our future works.

Appendix B
graded vector space and graded algebra

We follow the notions given in
kostant1975graded
.
A vector space
V
over
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
is graded (over
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
) if there are fixed subspaces
V_{0}
and
V_{1}
such that
V=V_{0}\oplus V_{1}
.
An element
v\in V
is homogeneous if
v
is either in
V_{0}
or in
V_{i}
. For all
v\in V_{i}
with
v\neq 0
, we define their degree as

{\mathfrak{d}}(v)=i.

(158)

Given two graded vector space
V
and
W
, the space
{\rm Hom}(V,W)
of homomorphism from
V
to
W
is graded. An element
\alpha\in{\rm Hom}(V,W)
is said to be homogeneous and of
{\mathfrak{d}}(\alpha)
provided

\alpha[V_{i}]\subset W_{i+{\mathfrak{d}}(\alpha)\,{\rm mod}\,2}.

(159)

with
\alpha[V_{i}]
denotes the image of
\alpha
acting on
V_{i}
.

An algebra
(A,\cdot)
is a graded algebra if
A
is a graded vector space and
A_{i}\cdot A_{j}\subset A_{i+j\,\mathrm{mod}\,2}
where
A_{i}\cdot A_{j}
denotes the space of elements
a_{i}\cdot a_{j}
for all
a_{i}\in A_{i}
and
a_{j}\in A_{j}
. A graded algebra
A
is a graded commutative algebra if the product satisfies

x\cdot y=(-1)^{{\mathfrak{d}}(x){\mathfrak{d}}(y)}y\cdot x

(160)

where
x,y\in A
are homogeneous. Any commutative algebra
\mathbb{A}
is a graded commutative algebra with the grade
\mathbb{A}_{1}=\mathbb{A}
and
\mathbb{A}_{0}=\{0\}
.
An example of the graded commutative algebra is the exterior algebra of some finite vector space
V
, i.e.

A=\mathbb{R}\oplus V\oplus(V\wedge V)\oplus(V\wedge V\wedge V)\oplus\cdots\oplus\bigwedge^{n}V.

(161)

A
is graded as

A_{0}=\bigoplus_{k=0}\bigwedge^{2k}V,\ A_{0}=\bigoplus_{k=0}\bigwedge^{2k+1}V.

(162)

A graded algebra
(\mathfrak{a},[\cdot,\cdot])
is a graded Lie algebra if the Lie bracket satisfies

(1)

[x,y]=(-1)^{1+{\mathfrak{d}}(x){\mathfrak{d}}(y)}[y,x]
;

(2)

(-1)^{{\mathfrak{d}}(x){\mathfrak{d}}(z)}[[x,y],z]+(-1)^{{\mathfrak{d}}(y){\mathfrak{d}}(x)}[[y,z],x]+(-1)^{{\mathfrak{d}}(z){\mathfrak{d}}(y)}[[z,x],y]=0
.

An operation
\partial
on a graded algebra
A
is called a derivative if it satisfies

\partial(xy)=(\partial x)y+(-1)^{{\mathfrak{d}}(\partial){\mathfrak{d}}(x)}x(\partial y)

(163)

where
{\mathfrak{d}}(\partial)
is defined by thinking of it as a homomorphism on
A
. It can be checked that the operator
[x,\cdot]
on a graded Lie algebra
A
for all
x\in A
is a derivative.
