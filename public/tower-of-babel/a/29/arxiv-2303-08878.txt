A Non- ℝ \mathbb{R} -Factorizable Product of ℝ \mathbb{R} -Factorizable Groups
Ol’ga Sipacheva

Abstract
. An example of two ℝ \mathbb{R} -factorizable groups whose product is not ℝ \mathbb{R} -factorizable is constructed. One of these groups is second-countable.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
22A05, 54F45

In
[
1
]
and
[
2
]
we constructed an example of two groups, one Lindelöf and the
other cosmic, whose product is not
\mathbb{R}
-factorizable. In this paper we show that cosmic group can
be made second-countable, which gives a negative solution of Problem 8.5.2 in
[
3
]
.

A topological group G is said to be
\mathbb{R}
-factorizable
if, for every continuous function
f\colon G\to\mathbb{R}
, there exists a continuous epimorphism
h\colon G\to H
onto a second-countable topological group
H
and a
continuous function
g\colon H\to\mathbb{R}
such that
f=g\circ h
. It is known that any Lindelöf group is

\mathbb{R}
-factorizable
[
4
, Assertion 1.1]
and that
\dim_{0}H\leqslant\dim_{0}G
for any topological group
G
and
any
\mathbb{R}
-factorizable subgroup
H
of
G
.

The heart of the construction in
[
1
]
and
[
2
]
is two spaces
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
with
the following properties:

(1)

\dim_{0}C_{i}=0
for
i=1,2
;

(2)

\dim_{0}(C_{1}\times C_{2})>0
;

(3)

C_{1}^{n}
is Lindelöf for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
;

(4)

the underlying set of the space
C_{1}
is the Cantor set
C
, its topology is finer than that of
C
, and it has a
base consisting of sets closed in
C
;

(5)

C_{2}
is second-countable; in fact,
C_{2}
is a subspace of the Cantor set
C
.

According to Theorem 7 of
[
5
]
, both spaces
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are retracts of certain topological groups

G_{1}
and
G_{2}
, respectively. Therefore,
C_{1}\times C_{2}
is a retract of (and hence
C
-embedded in)
G_{1}\times G_{2}
, which implies that
\dim_{0}(G_{1}\times G_{2})>0
. In
[
1
]
and
[
2
]
for
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
the free Abelian topological groups

A(C_{1})
and
A(C_{2})
were taken. It was shown that
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are Lindelöf, zero-dimensional,
and topologically isomorphic to closed subgroups of groups
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
, each of which is a
product of zero-dimensional second-countable topological groups. Since any product of zero-dimensional
second-countable topological spaces is strongly zero-dimensional
[
6
]
, it follows that so is the product

M_{1}\times M_{2}
, and it contains
G_{1}\times G_{2}
as a subgroup. Therefore,
G_{1}\times G_{2}
is not

\mathbb{R}
-factorizable, although both
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are.

In this paper we show that the group
G_{2}
can be made second-countable. To be more precise, we show that
C_{2}

is a retract of a zero-dimensional second-countable group
G_{2}
. This does not affect the above argument (except
that we can set
M_{2}=G_{2}
) but gives an example of an
\mathbb{R}
-factorizable group which ceases to be

\mathbb{R}
-factorizable when multiplied by a second-countable group.

Recall that the Boolean group
B(X)
with basis
X
is the set
[X]^{<\omega}
of finite subsets of
X

endowed with the operation
\vartriangle
of symmetric difference. Each point
x\in X
is identified with the
singleton
\{x\}
. We use the notation
\mathop{+_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}}
for the group operation of
B(X)
and occasionally write

\vartriangle
instead of
\mathop{+_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}}
. Thus, if
x\in X
,
F,G\in B(X)
, and
\mathbf{A}\subset B(X)
, then

\displaystyle x\mathop{+_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}}F=\{x\}\mathop{+_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}}F=\{x\}\vartriangle F,\qquad F\mathop{+_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}}G=F\vartriangle G,

\displaystyle F\mathop{+_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}}\mathbf{A}=\{F\mathop{+_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}}A:A\in\mathbf{A}\}=\{F\vartriangle A:A\in\mathbf{A}\}.

When
X
is a Tychonoff space, there exists a group topology on
B(X)
such that
B(X)
with this topology
contains
X
as a subspace. The finest group topology with this property is called the
free group topology

on
B(X)
, and the group
B(X)
endowed with this topology is called the
free Boolean topological group
of

X
; see
[
7
]
for details. In what follows, we use the same notation
B(X)
for the abstract Boolean
group with basis
X
and for the free Boolean topological group of
X
.

In Theorem 3 (version 2) of
[
5
]
, for a non-Archimedean space
X
, a retraction of the clopen subset

B_{*}(X)=\{F\in B(X):\text{$|F|$ is odd}\}

of the free Boolean topological group

B(X)
onto
X
was constructed. In the particular case of the Cantor
set
C
, the construction (slightly modified) is as follows.

Recall that
C
can be represented as the subset of
[0,1]
consisting of those numbers whose ternary expansions
do not contain 1. This suggests the natural base
\mathscr{B}
for the topology of
C
:

\mathscr{B}=\{U_{n_{1}\dots n_{k}}:k\in\mathbb{N},n_{i}\in\{0,2\}\text{ for }i\leqslant k\},

where
U_{n_{1}\dots n_{k}}
denotes the set of all numbers in
[0,1]
whose ternary expansions begin with

0.n_{1}\dots n_{k}
. Clearly, the elements of
\mathscr{B}
form a tree with respect to reverse inclusion and every element
of
\mathscr{B}
is clopen.

There are two natural orders on the set of subsets of
C
, the order by inclusion and the order induced by the
usual order on
C\subset\mathbb{R}
. In what follows, when writing, say, “
A<B
,” “
\min A
,” or “
A
is on the
left of
B
,” we always mean the latter, unless otherwise is explicitly stated.

Let
F
be any finite subset of
C
. We say that a set
A\subset C
is
F
-void
if
A\cap C=\varnothing
,
F
-even
if
|A\cap F|
is even and positive, and
A
-odd
if
|A\cap F|
is odd. Clearly, each
F
-even element of
\mathscr{B}
is contained in a maximal (by inclusion)
F
-even element
of
\mathscr{B}
, and the union of these maximal
F
-even elements is equal to the union of all
F
-even elements
of
\mathscr{B}
. Moreover, this union is itself
F
-even.

For
F\in B_{*}(C)
, we set

r(F)=\min(F\setminus\bigcup\{B\in\mathscr{B}:\text{$B$ is $F$-even or $F$-void}\})\\
=\min(F\setminus\bigcup\{B:\text{$B$ is an inclusion-maximal $F$-even element of
$\mathscr{B}$}\}).

The map
r\colon B_{*}(C)\to C
thus defined is a retraction
[
5
, Theorem 7
(version 1)]
. Extending it by, say,
0
to
B(C)
, we obtain a retraction
\hat{r}\colon B(C)\to C
(because

B_{*}(C)
is clopen in
B(X)
). The rest of this paper is devoted to the proof that
\hat{r}
is continuous with
respect to a second-countable zero-dimensional group topology
\tau
on
B(C)
defined below.

Given a disjoint open cover
\Gamma
of
C
, we set

\mathbf{H}_{\Gamma}=\{F\in B(C):\text{$|F\cap U|$ is even for each $U\in\Gamma$}\}.

Clearly,
\mathbf{H}_{\Gamma}
is a subgroup of
B(C)
. It is open in the free group topology of

B(C)
(see
[
7
]
).

The countable family

\mathscr{H}=\{\mathbf{H}_{\Gamma}:\text{$\Gamma$ is a (finite) disjoint cover of $C$ by elements of $\mathscr{B}$}\}

of subgroups is a local base at zero for a group topology
\tau
of
B(C)
, which is coarser that the free
group topology. It is easy to check that
C
is contained in
(B(C),\tau)
as a
subspace. Indeed, take any point
x\in C
and any base neighborhood
V_{x}
of
x
. Let

\Gamma
be a disjoint cover of
C
consisting of
V_{x}
and some other elements of
\mathscr{B}
.
If
F\in\mathbf{H}_{\Gamma}
and
x\mathop{+_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}}F=\{x\}\vartriangle F\in C
, then either
F=\varnothing
or
F=\{x,y\}
. In the
latter case,
x\mathop{+_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}}F=y
and by the definition of
\mathbf{H}_{\Gamma}
the point
y
must belong to the same element of

\Gamma
as
x
, that is, to
V_{x}
. Thus,
(x\mathop{+_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}}\mathbf{H}_{\Gamma})\cap C\subset V_{x}
. This shows that the topology
induced by
\tau
on
C
is not coarser than the original topology of
C
. On the other hand, it cannot be finer,
because
\tau
is coarser than the free group topology. Obviously,
(B(C),\tau)
is
T_{0}
and hence Tychonoff.

Note that all elements in any
\mathbf{H}_{\Gamma}
are of even cardinality. Therefore, for every
F\in B_{*}(C)
, we
have
F\mathop{+_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}}\mathbf{H}_{\Gamma}=\{F\vartriangle H:H\in\mathbf{H}_{\Gamma}\}\subset B_{*}(C)
.

Let us show that the map
r
is continuous with respect to the topology
\tau|_{B_{*}(C)}
. Suppose that

x=r(F)
for
F\in B_{*}(X)
. By construction
x\in F
. Take any neighborhood
U
of
x
. Let
V_{1},\dots,V_{m}
be
all inclusion-maximal
F
-even elements of
\mathscr{B}
; their number is finite because they are pairwise
disjoint (since
\mathscr{B}
is a tree) and each of them intersects the finite set
F
. None of
these elements contains
x
(because
x=r(F)
), and all of them are clopen. Choose a base neighborhood
V_{x}\in\mathscr{B}
of
x
satisfying the conditions
V_{x}\subset U
,
V_{x}\cap F=\{x\}
, and
V_{x}\cap V_{i}=\varnothing
for
i\leqslant m
. Consider the cover of
C
consisting of the sets
V_{1},\dots,V_{m}
and
V_{x}
and of all elements of
\mathscr{B}

disjoint from them. Let
\Gamma
be a disjoint refinement of this cover. Clearly, it is finite. We claim that

r(F\mathop{+_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}}\mathbf{H}_{\Gamma})\subset V_{x}
.

Indeed, take an
H\in\mathbf{H}_{\Gamma}
. We must show that
r(F\vartriangle H)\in V_{x}
.
Note that an element
V
of
\Gamma
is (
F\vartriangle H
)-odd if and only if it is
F
-odd,
because each element of
\Gamma
is either
H
-even or
H
-void and a point of
F
can be
cancelled in
F\vartriangle H
only by some point of
H
.

Let
V
be the leftmost
F
-odd (= (
F\vartriangle H
)-odd) element of
\Gamma
. Then
V\cap F
is not covered by any
inclusion-maximal
F
-even elements of
\mathscr{B}
, because all such elements are included in
\Gamma
and
V
is not
among them. By the definition of the map
r
we have
x=r(F)\leqslant\min(V\cap F)
, and
V_{x}
is
F
-odd. Therefore,

V=V_{x}
.

The point
r(F\vartriangle H)
cannot belong to an (
F\vartriangle H
)-even element
W
of
\Gamma
, because

\Gamma\subset\mathscr{B}
and so all points occurring in such a
W
are discarded when defining
r(F\vartriangle H)
.
Therefore, the element
V
of
\Gamma
containing
r(F\vartriangle H)
either coincides with
V_{x}
or is on the right of

V_{x}
. According to the definition of
r
, there exists a family
\mathscr{B}^{\prime}
of
(F\vartriangle H)
-even elements of
\mathscr{B}
such
that
\bigcup\mathscr{B}^{\prime}\supset\{y\in F\vartriangle H:y<V\}
.

Suppose that
V\neq V_{x}
. Note that
V_{x}
is
(F\vartriangle H)
-odd, because it contains precisely one point of
F
and
an even number (or none) of the points of
H
. In particular,
(F\vartriangle H)\cap V_{x}\neq\varnothing
. Let

W_{1},\dots,W_{k}
be all inclusion-maximal elements of
\mathscr{B}^{\prime}
intersecting
(F\vartriangle H)\cap V_{x}
. Each

W_{i}
, being an element of
\mathscr{B}
, either is contained in some element of
\Gamma
or is a union of some elements
of
\Gamma
, because
\mathscr{B}
is a tree and
\Gamma\subset\mathscr{B}
is a cover of
C
. By maximality
the sets
W_{1},\dots,W_{k}
are pairwise disjoint. Therefore, if
k\geqslant 2
, then all of them are contained in

V_{x}
and
V_{x}\cap(F\vartriangle H)=\bigcup_{i\leqslant k}W_{i}\cap(F\vartriangle H)
. This is impossible, because
|V_{x}\cap(F\vartriangle H)|
is odd and all
|W_{i}\cap(F\vartriangle H)|
are even. Thus, some element
W
of
\mathscr{B}^{\prime}
contains
V_{x}\ni x
.
Moreover, this
W
is a union of some elements of
\Gamma
. This means that
|W\cap H|
is even and hence so is

|W\cap F|
, because
W
is
(F\vartriangle H)
-even. However,
x
equals
r(F)
and hence does not belong to any

F
-even or
F
-void element of
\mathscr{B}
. This contradiction proves that
V=V_{x}
, i.e.,
r(F\vartriangle H)\in V_{x}
.

Thus,
r(F\mathop{+_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}}\mathbf{H}_{\Gamma})\subset V_{x}
. We have shown that, for any
F\in B_{*}(C)
and any neighborhood
U
of

x=r(F)
in
C
, there exists an
\mathbf{H}_{\Gamma}\in\mathscr{H}
such that the image of the open neighborhood
F\mathop{+_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}}\mathbf{H}_{\Gamma}
of
F
in
(B(C),\tau)
under
r
is contained in
U
. This means that
r
is continuous with
respect to the topology
\tau|_{B_{*}(C)}
.

To obtain a retraction
\hat{r}\colon(B(C),\tau)\to C
, it remains to extend
r
over
B(C)\setminus B_{*}(C)
,
e.g., by 0; this extension is continuous, because
B_{*}(C)
is clopen in
(B(C),\tau)
.

Restricting
\hat{r}
to the subgroup
G_{2}
of
(B(C),\tau)
generated by
C_{2}\subset C
, we obtain a retraction
of
G_{2}
onto
C_{2}
, because
r(F)\in F
for any
F\in B(C)
and hence
r(G_{2})=C_{2}
. The group

(B(C),\tau)
is metrizable and zero-dimensional, since its local base at zero consists of countably many
open (and hence closed) subgroups, and it is separable, since its topology is coarser than the separable free
group topology. Therefore,
G_{2}
is a second-countable zero-dimensional group.
