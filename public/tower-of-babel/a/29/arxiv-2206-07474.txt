Priori Error Estimate of Deep Mixed Residual Method for Elliptic PDEs Thanks: Submitted to the editors DATE.
Lingfeng Li, Xue-Cheng Tai, Jiang Yang, Quanhui Zhu

Abstract
In this work, we derive a priori error estimate of the mixed residual method when solving some elliptic PDEs. Our work is the first theoretical study of this method. We prove that the neural network solutions will converge if we increase the training samples and network size without any constraint on the ratio of training samples to the network size. Besides, our results suggest that the mixed residual method can recover high order derivatives better than the deep Ritz method, which has also been verified by our numerical experiments.



keywords

Mixed residual method, Quadratic error, Neural networks approximation

Introduction

Using neural networks (NNs) to solve partial differential equations (PDEs) is very popular in these years. Since NNs are very powerful in approximating complicated functions, they are commonly used to solve PDEs in high dimension
[
6
,
26
,
25
,
23
,
15
]
. Besides, the neural networks can also be applied to approximate the solution operators
[
14
,
10
]
and learn PDEs from data
[
12
,
11
]
.

To approximate solutions to PDEs, we need first to define a subspace (subset) of functions and then search for a good solution in this subspace (subset). The classical finite element method (FEM) usually works with the finite elements space. To construct a finite element space, we need to discretize the domain and then define finite elements based on the mesh. The solution is then parameterized as a linear combination of all finite elements. FEM is very powerful in 2 and 3 dimensional problems. However, it suffers from the curse of dimensionality. Some mesh-free methods, e.g., the smoothed particle hydrodynamics
[
5
]
, may be considered to solve high-dimensional problems, but these methods are very inaccurate near the boundary. NNs are used as a new mesh-free tool for PDEs in recent years. It is accurate for various problems, easy to implement and efficient to train.

When solving PDEs with networks, we first parameterize the solution as a network with specified structures. Some commonly used networks are fully connected networks (FCN), residual networks (ResNet) and convolutional networks (CNN). A special case of the FCN is called two-layer network (TLN) which is often used in theoretical analysis
[
1
,
18
,
13
]
. The TLN is closely related to the Barron space
[
1
]
and we will also briefly introduce it in this paper. The training of PDE problems falls into the category of unsupervised training or semi-supervised training, where no ground truth or very few ground truth labels are known.

Let us consider a general boundary value problem with the form:

\displaystyle f(u,\nabla u,\Delta u)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle x\in\Omega,

\displaystyle B(u,\nabla u)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle x\in\partial\Omega,

where
\Omega
is a bounded domain. The DGM
[
25
]
and PINN
[
23
]
consider replacing
u
with a neural network
\phi
and then minimize the squared residual loss:

\|f(\phi,\nabla\phi,\Delta\phi)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\lambda\|B(\phi,\nabla\phi)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)},

where
\lambda
is the penalty coefficient. Derivatives of
\phi
with respect to
x
are computed by the backpropagation. This type of method is simple and effective, but the evaluation of high order derivatives requires more computational efforts. To avoid the evaluation of high order derivatives, we may consider its variational form. If the equations is equivalent to a variational problem in a weak sense:

\min_{u}J(u):=a(u,u)-l(u),

where
a(\cdot,\cdot)
is a bilinear form and
l
is a linear functional, then we may use
J
as the training loss and minimize
J(\phi)
. This is called deep Ritz method (DRM)
[
26
]
. In some cases, we may add a penalty term for the boundary condition as well
[
22
]
.
[
28
]
also proposed an adversarial network based on the variational form of PDEs. Another way to avoid higher order derivatives is to introduce an auxiliary vector-valued network
\psi
to approximate
\nabla u
. Then, we combine the squared residual loss with an extra penalty term to form the mixed residual loss:

\|f(\phi,\boldsymbol{\psi},\text{div}(\boldsymbol{\psi}))\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\lambda_{1}\|B(\phi,\boldsymbol{\psi})\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\lambda_{2}\|\nabla\phi-\boldsymbol{\psi}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

This idea has been studied in
[
17
,
2
,
27
]
.
[
16
]
further tried to enforce the boundary condition exactly to networks. This formulation is also called the first order system least square and has been applied to the finite element method
[
3
]
. These loss functions represented in the form of integration are called expected loss functions. In practice, we would approximate these integrals with quadrature and this approximation is called the empirical loss function.

In the study of numerical PDE methods, we are interested in the error estimates. However, for NNs, the error analysis is generally difficult, because of the non-linearity and non-convexity of the problem. The error of neural networks usually consists of two parts: approximation error and generalization error, which is also called quadrature error sometimes. The approximation error measures how well a given function can be approximated by a set of neural networks and the quadrature error measures the deviation between the expected loss and the empirical loss. So far, the error of DRM is well-studied in the literature
[
13
,
22
,
9
]
. Like the finite element methods, the error
\|\phi-u^{*}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}
is controlled by the
H^{1}
approximation error of neural networks.
[
20
,
21
]
also gives a posteriori error for PINNs. For the Mixed residual method, there is no error analysis so far.

Main contribution:

In this work, we provide a priori error analysis for the mixed residual method when solving some elliptic PDEs.

1.

This work is the first attempt at numerical analysis for the deep mixed residual method.

2.

Our result suggests that the mixed residual method can recover high order derivatives better than the deep Ritz method. We have also observed this property in numerical experiments.

3.

Our analysis covers both homogeneous and non-homogeneous boundary conditions while the analysis of deep Ritz method
[
13
,
22
]
only considers homogeneous cases.

4.

In this work, we estimate the approximation error and the quadrature error of two-layer networks equipped with a non-smooth activation function: rectified quadratic unit (ReQU) activation function.

5.

In
[
13
]
, the convergence of deep Ritz method requires that the number of training samples increases cubically with the network size. In our result, the increase of training samples is independent of network size.

This paper is organized as follow. In Section
2
, we first introduce some preliminary results and describe our main theorems. In Section
3
, we will derive the error analysis for some elliptic PDEs. Then, we estimate the approximation error and quadrature error in Section
4
. Lastly, we conduct some numerical experiments in Section
5
to partially verify our analysis.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Neural networks

A neural network is a parametric function that maps an input
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
to an output
y
. The output could be a scalar or a vector. Let us consider a simple FCN with scalar output:

\displaystyle h_{j}

\displaystyle=\sigma(W_{j-1}h_{j-1}+b_{j-1}),\quad j=2,\dots,L,

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle=\omega^{T}h_{L}+b_{L},

where
h_{0}=x
,
\sigma
is a non-linear activation function and
W_{j}
,
b_{j}
,
\omega
are parameters to be learned. If the output
y
is a vector, then we just replace
\omega
with a matrix and replace
b_{L}
with a vector. Another polular network structure is the ResNet
[
7
]
:

\displaystyle h_{j}

\displaystyle=\sigma(W_{j-1}h_{j-1}+b_{j-1})+h_{j-1},\quad j=2,\dots,L,

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle=\omega^{T}h_{L}+b_{L}.

The main difference here is that an extra
h_{j-1}
is added in each
h_{j}
update, so the map
\sigma(W_{j-1}h_{j-1}+b_{j-1})
equals to the residual
h_{j}-h_{j-1}
. This structure can avoid the so-called gradient vanishing problem in the training of very deep networks. One can refer to
[
4
]
for more theoretical explanation of ResNet.

Let us denote a network as
\phi(x)
. To train this network, we need to define an expected loss function
L(\phi)
of the form:

L(\phi)=\int_{\Omega}l(x,\phi(x))\mathrm{d}\mu(x),

where
l(x,y)
is a function that measures how well the network output
y=\phi(x)
fits a given criterion, and
\mu(x)
is a probability measure defined on
\Omega
. We then aim to minimize the loss
L(\phi)
and find the optimal
\phi
from a given set of networks:
\hat{\phi}=\arg\min_{\phi}L(\phi)
.
In practice, the integral form
L
is usually approximated by a quadrature, i.e., we randomly sample some points from the distribution
\mu
and approximate
L
by

L_{n}(\phi)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}l(x_{i},\phi(x_{i})).

L_{n}
is also called the empirical loss. The quadrature error is then defined as
|L_{n}(\phi)-L(\phi)|
.

2.2
Deep mixed residual methods for elliptic PDEs

Let
\Omega
be a Lipschitz domain in the
d
-dimensional space. We consider the following second order PDEs with Neumann boundary condition

\displaystyle-\Delta u+u=f\text{ for }x\in\Omega,\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\partial u}{\partial\boldsymbol{n}}=g_{1}\text{ for }x\in\partial\Omega,

or Dirichlet boundary condition

\displaystyle-\Delta u=f\text{ for }x\in\Omega,\quad\text{and}\quad u=g_{2}\text{ for }x\in\partial\Omega.

To solve the PDE problems, we define two neural networks
\phi(x;\theta_{1}):\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
\boldsymbol{\psi}(x;\theta_{2}):\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
\phi
simulates the true solution
u^{*}
and
\boldsymbol{\psi}
simulates
\nabla u^{*}
. Let
\phi\in V
and
\boldsymbol{\psi}\in W
, where
V
and
W
are sets of scalar-valued and vector-valued networks. The expected loss functions are given as below respectively. For the Neumann problem:

L^{N}(\phi,\boldsymbol{\psi})=R_{g}(\phi,\boldsymbol{\psi})^{2}+\lambda_{1}R_{e}^{N}(\phi,\boldsymbol{\psi})^{2}+\lambda_{2}R_{b}^{N}(\phi,\boldsymbol{\psi}),^{2}

(1)

and for the Dirichlet problem:

L^{D}(\phi,\boldsymbol{\psi})=R_{g}(\phi,\boldsymbol{\psi})^{2}+\lambda_{1}R_{e}^{D}(\phi,\boldsymbol{\psi})^{2}+\lambda_{2}R_{b}^{D}(\phi,\boldsymbol{\psi}),^{2}

(2)

where

\displaystyle R_{g}(\phi,\boldsymbol{\psi})=\|\nabla\phi-\boldsymbol{\psi}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

\displaystyle R_{e}^{N}(\phi,\boldsymbol{\psi})=\|-\text{div}(\boldsymbol{\psi})+\phi-f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

\displaystyle R_{e}^{D}(\phi,\boldsymbol{\psi})=\|-\text{div}(\boldsymbol{\psi})-f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

\displaystyle R_{b}^{N}(\phi,\boldsymbol{\psi})=\|\boldsymbol{\psi}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}-g_{1}\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)},

\displaystyle R_{b}^{D}(\phi,\boldsymbol{\psi})=\|\phi-g_{2}\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}.

The corresponding empirical loss are respectively denoted by

\displaystyle L_{n}^{N}(\phi,\boldsymbol{\psi})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(|(\nabla\phi-\boldsymbol{\psi})(x_{i})|^{2}+\lambda_{1}|(-\text{div}(\boldsymbol{\psi})+\phi-f)(x_{i})|^{2})

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\bar{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{\bar{n}}\lambda_{2}|(\boldsymbol{\psi}\cdot\mathbf{n}-g_{1})(\bar{x}_{i})|^{2},

and

\displaystyle L_{n}^{D}(\phi,\boldsymbol{\psi})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(|(\nabla\phi-\boldsymbol{\psi})(x_{i})|^{2}+\lambda_{1}|(-\text{div}(\boldsymbol{\psi})-f)(x_{i})|^{2})

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\bar{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{\bar{n}}\lambda_{2}|(\phi-g_{2})(\bar{x}_{i})|^{2},

where
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
is randomly sampled from
\Omega
and
\{\bar{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\bar{n}}
is the sample from
\partial\Omega
. In following discussions,
\bar{n}
is an integer greater than or equals to
n/d^{2}
.
The error of neural network solutions are measured by
\|\phi-u^{*}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}
and
\|\boldsymbol{\psi}-\nabla u^{*}\|_{H_{\text{div}}(\Omega)}^{2}
respectively.

2.3
Barron space and two-layer neural networks

In this work, we assume that solutions to PDEs belong to the Barron space. Originally, the Barron space was introduced by
[
1
]
.

Definition 1
.

For a function
u
defined on
\Omega
, if there exists a complex measure
F(d\omega)=e^{i\theta(\omega)}|\tilde{u}(\omega)|d\omega
such that

u(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{i\omega\cdot x}F(d\omega),\quad x\in\Omega,\quad\text{and}\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1+|\omega|_{1})^{s}|\tilde{u}(\omega)|d\omega<+\infty,

then
u
belongs to the Barron space
\mathcal{B}^{s}(\Omega)
associated with the Barron norm

\|u\|_{\mathcal{B}^{s}}:=\inf_{\tilde{u}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1+|\omega|_{1})^{s}|\tilde{u}(\omega)|d\omega.

The infimum in the definition is taken over all the possible complex measure
F(d\omega)=e^{i\theta(\omega)}|\tilde{u}(\omega)|d\omega
.
When
s=3
, we simply denote
\mathcal{B}^{s}
as
\mathcal{B}
. It is easy to see that
\mathcal{B}^{s+1}\subset\mathcal{B}^{s}
for
s\geq 1
and
[
24
]
proves that
\mathcal{B}^{s}(\Omega)\subset H^{s}(\Omega)
for any positive integer
s
and bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Here we also generalize the definition of Barron space to vector-valued functions.

Definition 2
.

For a vector-valued function
\boldsymbol{u}=(u_{1},\dots,u_{d})
defined on
\Omega
, if
u_{j}\in\mathcal{B}^{s}(\Omega)
for each
j=1,\dots,d
, we say
\boldsymbol{u}
belongs to the Barron space
\mathcal{B}^{s}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
associated with the Barron norm
\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{\mathcal{B}^{s}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})}=(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\|u_{j}\|_{\mathcal{B}^{s}(\Omega)}^{2})^{1/2}
.

Lemma 3
.

Given a function
u\in\mathcal{B}^{s+1}(\Omega)
, we have
\nabla u\in\mathcal{B}^{s}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
and

\|\nabla u\|_{\mathcal{B}^{s}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq\|u\|_{\mathcal{B}^{s+1}(\Omega)}
.

Proof.

Given any
\eta>0
, there exist a
F(d\omega)=e^{i\theta(\omega)}|\tilde{u}(\omega)|d\omega
such that
u(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{i\omega\cdot x}F(d\omega)
in
\Omega
and
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1+|\omega|_{1})^{s+1}|\tilde{u}(\omega)|d\omega<\|u\|_{\mathcal{B}^{s+1}(\Omega)}+\eta
. Since the gradient of
u
has the representation
\nabla u(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{i\omega\cdot x}i\omega F(d\omega)
, then

\displaystyle\|\nabla u\|_{\mathcal{B}^{s}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1+|\omega|_{1})^{s}|\omega|_{1}|\tilde{u}(\omega)|d\omega\bigg)^{2}

\displaystyle<\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1+|\omega|_{1})^{s+1}|\tilde{u}(\omega)|d\omega\bigg)^{2}<\bigg(\|u\|_{\mathcal{B}^{s+1}(\Omega)}+\eta\bigg)^{2}.

Let
\eta
go to
0
and we complete the proof.

For functions in the Barron space
\mathcal{B}(\Omega)
, we can use two-layer ReLU activated networks to approximate them to arbitrary actuary.
[
9
]
gives an estimation of the approximation error with the order
O(\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}})
, where
m
is width of two-layer ReLU networks.
In our discussions, the activation function needs to be secondly differentiable. Instead, we use the rectified quadratic unit(ReQU) function
\text{ReQU}(x)=\text{ReLU}(x)^{2}=(\max\{x,0\})^{2}
.
We further define the set of ReQU activated networks associated with a Barron space function
u
by

\displaystyle V^{m}_{u}=\bigg\{

\displaystyle c+\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}\text{ReQU}(\omega_{i}^{\top}x+b_{i})\bigg|

\displaystyle|c|\leq 2\|u\|_{\mathcal{B}},|a_{i}|\leq 8\|u\|_{\mathcal{B}},|\omega_{i}|_{2}\leq 1,|b_{i}|\leq 1,i=1,\dots,m\bigg\}.

and

\displaystyle W^{m}_{\boldsymbol{u}}=\bigg\{

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{c}+\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\boldsymbol{a}_{i}\circ\text{ReQU}(\boldsymbol{W}_{i}x+\boldsymbol{b}_{i})\bigg||\boldsymbol{c}_{j}|\leq 2\|\boldsymbol{u}_{j}\|_{\mathcal{B}},|(\boldsymbol{a}_{i})_{j}|\leq 8\|\boldsymbol{u}_{j}\|_{\mathcal{B}},

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{W}_{i}\|_{2}\leq 1,\|\boldsymbol{b}_{i}\|_{\infty}\leq 1,i=1,\dots,m,j=1,\dots,d\bigg\}.

2.4
Main results

We first give our main results in this section. If we assume the domain
\Omega=[0,1]^{d}
, the errors of the mixed residual method for two PDE problems are given in the following theorems. Basically, the errors of neural networks can be decomposed into two parts: the approximation error and the quadrature error.

Theorem 4
.

Suppose the solution to Neumann problem (
2.2
)
u^{*}_{N}
belongs to the Barron space
\mathcal{B}^{4}(\Omega)
. Let
\hat{\phi},\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}
be the neural network solutions:

(\hat{\phi},\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}})=\mathop{\arg\min}\limits_{\phi\in V^{m}_{u^{*}_{N}},\boldsymbol{\psi}\in W^{m}_{u^{*}_{N}}}L^{N}_{n}(\phi,\boldsymbol{\psi}).

Then, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\hat{\phi}-u^{*}_{N}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}\lesssim\frac{\|u^{*}_{N}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{B}^{4}(\Omega)}}{m}+\frac{\|u^{*}_{N}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{B}^{4}(\Omega)}}{\sqrt{n}},

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}-\nabla u^{*}_{N}\|_{H_{div}(\Omega)}^{2}\lesssim\frac{\|u^{*}_{N}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{B}^{4}(\Omega)}}{m}+\frac{\|u^{*}_{N}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{B}^{4}(\Omega)}}{\sqrt{n}},

where the expectation is taken on the random sampling of training data in
\Omega
and
\partial\Omega
.

Theorem 5
.

Suppose the solution to Dirichlet problem (
2.2
)
u^{*}_{D}
belongs to the Barron space
\mathcal{B}^{4}(\Omega)
. Let
\hat{\phi},\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}
be the neural network solutions:

(\hat{\phi},\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}})=\mathop{\arg\min}\limits_{\phi\in V^{m}_{u^{*}_{D}},\boldsymbol{\psi}\in W^{m}_{u^{*}_{D}}}L^{D}_{n}(\phi,\boldsymbol{\psi}).

Then, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\hat{\phi}-u^{*}_{D}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}\lesssim\frac{\|u^{*}_{D}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{B}^{4}(\Omega)}}{\sqrt{m}}+\frac{\|u^{*}_{D}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{B}^{4}(\Omega)}}{n^{1/4}},

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}-\nabla u^{*}_{D}\|_{H_{\text{div}}(\Omega)}^{2}\lesssim\frac{\|u^{*}_{D}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{B}^{4}(\Omega)}}{\sqrt{m}}+\frac{\|u^{*}_{D}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{B}^{4}(\Omega)}}{n^{1/4}},

where the expectation is taken on the random sampling of training data.

3
Error analysis for elliptic problems

In this section, we will show how the error of neural networks can be controlled by the approximation error and quadrature error for elliptic PDEs with different boundary conditions.

3.1
Neumann boundary condition

Suppose
V
and
W
be sets of scalar-valued and vector-valued neural networks with fixed structures and activation functions in
H^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Then, for the PDE problem (
2.2
) with Neuman boundary condition, we have the following error estimate.

Theorem 6
.

Let
u^{*}_{N}
be the classical solution to the Neumann problem (
2.2
) and

(\hat{\phi},\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}})=\underset{\phi\in V,\boldsymbol{\psi}\in W}{\arg\min}L_{n}^{N}(\phi,\boldsymbol{\psi}).

Then, we have

\|\hat{\phi}-u^{*}_{N}\|^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\lesssim E_{1}+E_{2},\quad\text{and}\quad\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}-\nabla u^{*}_{N}\|_{H_{\text{div}}(\Omega)}^{2}\lesssim E_{1}+E_{2},

where

E_{1}=2\sup_{\phi\in V,\boldsymbol{\psi}\in W}|L^{N}(\phi,\boldsymbol{\psi})-L^{N}_{n}(\phi,\boldsymbol{\psi})|,

and

\displaystyle E_{2}=\underset{\boldsymbol{\psi}\in W}{\min}\left(\|\boldsymbol{\psi}-\nabla u^{*}_{N}\|_{H_{\text{div}}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{\psi}-\nabla u^{*}_{N}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}\right)+\underset{\phi\in V}{\min}\|\phi-u^{*}_{N}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}.

To prove Theorem
6
, we first need to show the errors of two networks can be bounded by some multiples of the expected loss functions.

Lemma 7
.

Let
\phi\in V
and
\boldsymbol{\psi}\in W
be two networks, and
u^{*}_{N}
be the classical solution to the Neumann problem (
1
). Then we have

\|\phi-u^{*}_{N}\|^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq 4C_{\Omega}(R^{N}_{b})^{2}+2R_{g}^{2}+2(R^{N}_{e})^{2},

and

\|\boldsymbol{\psi}-\nabla u^{*}_{N}\|_{H_{\text{div}}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq 6R_{g}^{2}+6(R_{e}^{N})^{2}+8C_{\Omega}(R_{b}^{N})^{2}.

Proof.

Since
u^{*}_{N}
is the classical solution to the PDE problem (
2.2
), it satisfy the variational form:

\int_{\Omega}\nabla u^{*}_{N}\nabla v+u^{*}_{N}v\ dx=\int_{\Omega}fv\ dx+\int_{\partial\Omega}g_{1}v\ ds,

for any
v\in H^{1}(\Omega)
. Let
v=\hat{\phi}=\phi-u^{*}_{N}
, we have

\int_{\Omega}\nabla u^{*}_{N}\nabla\hat{\phi}+u^{*}_{N}\hat{\phi}\ dx=\int_{\Omega}f\hat{\phi}\ dx+\int_{\partial\Omega}g_{1}\hat{\phi}\ ds.

Then, by adding
\int_{\Omega}(\text{div}(\boldsymbol{\psi})-\phi)\hat{\phi}\ dx-\int_{\partial\Omega}\hat{\phi}\nabla\phi\cdot\mathbf{n}\ ds
on both sides,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(f+\text{div}(\boldsymbol{\psi})-\phi)\hat{\phi}\ dx-\int_{\partial\Omega}\hat{\phi}(\nabla\phi-\nabla u^{*}_{N})\cdot\mathbf{n}\ ds

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(\nabla u^{*}_{N}-\boldsymbol{\psi})\cdot\nabla\hat{\phi}+(u^{*}_{N}-\phi)\hat{\phi}\ dx-\int_{\partial\Omega}\hat{\phi}(\nabla\phi-\boldsymbol{\psi})\cdot\mathbf{n}\ ds

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(\nabla u^{*}_{N}-\nabla\phi+\nabla\phi-\boldsymbol{\psi})\cdot\nabla\hat{\phi}+(u^{*}_{N}-\phi)\hat{\phi}\ dx+\int_{\partial\Omega}\hat{\phi}(\boldsymbol{\psi}-\nabla\phi)\cdot\mathbf{n}\ ds

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\hat{\phi}|^{2}\ dx-\int_{\Omega}|\hat{\phi}|^{2}\ dx+\int_{\Omega}(\nabla\phi-\boldsymbol{\psi})\cdot\nabla\hat{\phi}\ dx+\int_{\partial\Omega}\hat{\phi}(\boldsymbol{\psi}-\nabla\phi)\cdot\mathbf{n}\ ds.

By rearranging the equation, we get

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\hat{\phi}|^{2}\ dx+\int_{\Omega}|\hat{\phi}|^{2}\ dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}(f+\text{div}(\boldsymbol{\psi})-\phi)\hat{\phi}+\int_{\Omega}(\nabla\phi-\boldsymbol{\psi})\cdot\nabla\hat{\phi}\ dx+\int_{\partial\Omega}\hat{\phi}(\boldsymbol{\psi}-\nabla u^{*}_{N})\cdot\mathbf{n}\ ds

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega}\hat{\phi}(\boldsymbol{\psi}\cdot\mathbf{n}-g_{1})\ ds+\int_{\Omega}(\nabla\phi-\boldsymbol{\psi})\cdot\nabla\hat{\phi}\ dx+\int_{\Omega}(-\text{div}(\boldsymbol{\psi})+\phi-f)\hat{\phi}\ dx

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{\Omega}(R^{N}_{b})^{2}+\frac{\|\hat{\phi}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}}{4}+\frac{R_{g}^{2}}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\hat{\phi}|^{2}\ dx+\frac{(R^{N}_{e})^{2}}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\hat{\phi}|^{2}\ dx,

where the last inequality is derived by applying the trace theorem and
C_{\Omega}
is a constant depends on the domain
\Omega
. Then, we combine like terms and get

\|\phi-u^{*}_{N}\|^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}=\|\hat{\phi}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq 4C_{\Omega}(R_{b}^{N})^{2}+2R_{g}^{2}+2(R_{e}^{N})^{2}.

For
\|\boldsymbol{\psi}-\nabla u^{*}_{N}\|_{H_{\text{div}}(\Omega)}^{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\psi}-\nabla u^{*}_{N}\|_{H_{\text{div}}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\boldsymbol{\psi}-\nabla\phi+\nabla\phi-\nabla u^{*}_{N}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\text{div}(\boldsymbol{\psi})+f-\phi+\phi-u^{*}_{N}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq 2R_{g}^{2}+2(R_{e}^{N})^{2}+2\|\hat{\phi}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq 6R_{g}^{2}+6(R_{e}^{N})^{2}+8C_{\Omega}(R_{b}^{N})^{2}.

Next, we are going to prove the expected loss can be bounded by the approximation error of function classes
V
and
W
to the solution
u^{*}_{N}
.

Lemma 8
.

Let
(\phi^{*},\boldsymbol{\psi}^{*}):=\underset{\phi\in V,\boldsymbol{\psi}\in W}{\arg\min}L^{N}(\phi,\boldsymbol{\psi}).
Then,

\displaystyle L^{N}(\phi^{*},\boldsymbol{\psi}^{*})\lesssim\underset{\boldsymbol{\psi}\in W}{\min}\left(\|\boldsymbol{\psi}-\nabla u^{*}_{N}\|_{H_{\text{div}}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{\psi}-\nabla u^{*}_{N}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}\right)+\underset{\phi\in V}{\min}\|\phi-u^{*}_{N}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}.

Proof.

For any
\tilde{\phi}\in V
and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}\in W
, we have

\displaystyle L^{N}

\displaystyle(\phi^{*},\boldsymbol{\psi}^{*})\leq L^{N}(\tilde{\phi},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}-\nabla\tilde{\phi}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\lambda_{1}\|-\text{div}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}})+\tilde{\phi}-f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\lambda_{2}\|(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}-\nabla u^{*}_{N})\cdot\mathbf{n}\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}-\nabla u^{*}_{N}+\nabla u^{*}_{N}-\nabla\tilde{\phi}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\lambda_{2}\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}-\nabla u^{*}_{N}\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle+\lambda_{1}\|-\text{div}(\boldsymbol{\psi})+\text{div}(\nabla u^{*}_{N})+\tilde{\phi}-u^{*}_{N}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}-\nabla u^{*}_{N}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+2\|\nabla\tilde{\phi}-\nabla u^{*}_{N}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\lambda_{2}C_{\Omega}\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}-\nabla u^{*}_{N}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle+2\lambda_{1}\|\text{div}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}})-\text{div}(\nabla u^{*}_{N})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+2\lambda_{1}\|\tilde{\phi}-u^{*}_{N}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle c_{1}(\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}-\nabla u^{*}_{N}\|_{H_{\text{div}}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}-\nabla u^{*}_{N}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2})+c_{2}\|\tilde{\phi}-u^{*}_{N}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}

where
c_{1}=\max\{1,2\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}C_{\Omega}\}
and
c_{2}=\max\{2,2\lambda_{1}\}
. Then we can take minimum on the right hand side of the inequality and finish the proof.

Now, we can prove the error estimates in Theorem

using Lemma
7
and Lemma
8
directly.

Proof.

Suppose

(\phi^{*},\boldsymbol{\psi}^{*})=\underset{\phi\in V,\boldsymbol{\psi}\in W}{\arg\min}L^{N}(\phi,\boldsymbol{\psi}).

By Lemma
7
, we have

\|\hat{\phi}-u^{*}_{N}\|^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\lesssim L^{N}(\hat{\phi},\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}),\quad\text{and}\quad\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}-\nabla u^{*}_{N}\|_{H_{\text{div}}(\Omega)}^{2}\lesssim L^{N}(\hat{\phi},\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}).

Since

\displaystyle L^{N}(\hat{\phi},\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}})

\displaystyle=L^{N}(\hat{\phi},\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}})-L_{n}^{N}(\hat{\phi},\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}})+L_{n}^{N}(\hat{\phi},\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}})

\displaystyle\leq L^{N}(\hat{\phi},\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}})-L_{n}^{N}(\hat{\phi},\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}})+L_{n}^{N}(\phi^{*},\boldsymbol{\psi}^{*})

\displaystyle=L^{N}(\hat{\phi},\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}})-L_{n}^{N}(\hat{\phi},\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}})+L_{n}^{N}(\phi^{*},\boldsymbol{\psi}^{*})-L^{N}(\phi^{*},\boldsymbol{\psi}^{*})+L^{N}(\phi^{*},\boldsymbol{\psi}^{*})

\displaystyle\leq 2\sup_{\phi\in V,\boldsymbol{\psi}\in W}|L^{N}(\phi,\boldsymbol{\psi})-L^{N}_{n}(\phi,\boldsymbol{\psi})|+L^{N}(\phi^{*},\boldsymbol{\psi}^{*}).

Then we just apply Lemma
8
and we finish the proof.

Dirichlet boundary condition

Theorem 9
.

Suppose the solution

Similar to the proof of Theorem
6
, we also need to show the error of neural networks can be bounded by the expected loss.

Lemma 10
.

Let
\phi\in V
and
\boldsymbol{\psi}\in W
be two networks, and
u^{*}_{D}
be the classical solution to the Dirichlet problem (
2.2
). Then

\|\phi-u^{*}_{D}\|^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq 2\tilde{C}_{\Omega}\left(C_{B}R_{b}^{D}+(R^{D}_{b})^{2}+\frac{\tilde{C}_{\Omega}}{2}R_{g}^{2}+\frac{\tilde{C}_{\Omega}}{2}(R^{D}_{e})^{2}\right),

and

\|\psi-\nabla u^{*}_{D}\|_{H_{\text{div}}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq 4C_{B}\tilde{C}_{\Omega}R^{D}_{b}+4\tilde{C}_{\Omega}(R^{D}_{b})^{2}+(2+2\tilde{C}_{\Omega}^{2})R^{2}_{g}+2\tilde{C}_{\Omega}^{2}(R^{D}_{e})^{2}+(R_{e}^{D})^{2},

where
C_{B}=\sqrt{|\partial\Omega|}\sup_{\boldsymbol{\psi}\in W,x\in\partial\Omega}|\boldsymbol{\psi}(x)|+C_{\Omega}\|\nabla u^{*}_{D}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}
is a constant.

Proof.

We first need to introduce the Poincaré-Friedrichs inequality:
For any
u\in H^{1}(\Omega)
, there exist a constant
\tilde{C}_{\Omega}
such that

\|u\|^{2}_{H^{1}}\leq\tilde{C}_{\Omega}\left(\int_{\partial\Omega}|u|^{2}\ ds+\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}\ dx\right).

Since
u^{*}_{D}
is the classical solution to the Dirichlet problem (
2.2
), we multiply
\hat{\phi}
on both sides of (
2.2
) and integrate them over
\Omega
, we get

-\int_{\partial\Omega}\hat{\phi}\nabla u^{*}_{D}\cdot\mathbf{n}\ ds+\int_{\Omega}\nabla u^{*}_{D}\nabla\hat{\phi}\ dx=\int_{\Omega}f\hat{\phi}\ dx.

By adding
\int_{\Omega}\text{div}(\boldsymbol{\psi})\hat{\phi}\ dx
on both sides,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(f+\text{div}(\boldsymbol{\psi}))\hat{\phi}\ dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{\partial\Omega}\hat{\phi}\nabla u^{*}_{D}\cdot\mathbf{n}\ ds+\int_{\Omega}\nabla u^{*}_{D}\nabla\hat{\phi}+\text{div}(\boldsymbol{\psi})\hat{\phi}\ dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{\partial\Omega}\hat{\phi}(\nabla u^{*}_{D}-\boldsymbol{\psi})\cdot\mathbf{n}\ ds+\int_{\Omega}(\nabla u^{*}_{D}-\boldsymbol{\psi})\nabla\hat{\phi}\ dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{\partial\Omega}(\phi-g_{2})(\nabla u^{*}_{D}-\boldsymbol{\psi})\cdot\mathbf{n}\ ds+\int_{\Omega}(\nabla u^{*}_{D}-\nabla\phi+\nabla\phi-\boldsymbol{\psi})\nabla\hat{\phi}\ dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega}(\phi-g_{2})(\boldsymbol{\psi}-\nabla u^{*}_{D})\cdot\mathbf{n}\ ds+\int_{\Omega}(\nabla u^{*}_{D}-\nabla\phi+\nabla\phi-\boldsymbol{\psi})\nabla\hat{\phi}\ dx.

By rearranging the equation, we get

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\hat{\phi}|^{2}\ dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega}(\phi-g_{2})(\boldsymbol{\psi}-\nabla u^{*}_{D})\cdot\mathbf{n}\ ds+\int_{\Omega}(\nabla\phi-\boldsymbol{\psi})\nabla\hat{\phi}\ dx-\int_{\Omega}(f+\text{div}(\boldsymbol{\psi}))\hat{\phi}\ dx

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\psi}-\nabla u^{*}_{D}\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}R_{b}^{D}+\frac{\tilde{C}_{\Omega}}{2}R_{g}^{2}+\frac{\|\nabla\hat{\phi}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}}{2\tilde{C}_{\Omega}}+\frac{\tilde{C}_{\Omega}}{2}(R^{D}_{e})^{2}+\frac{\|\hat{\phi}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}}{2\tilde{C}_{\Omega}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{B}R_{b}^{D}+\frac{\tilde{C}_{\Omega}}{2}R_{g}^{2}+\frac{\|\nabla\hat{\phi}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}}{2\tilde{C}_{\Omega}}+\frac{\tilde{C}_{\Omega}}{2}(R^{D}_{e})^{2}+\frac{\|\hat{\phi}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}}{2\tilde{C}_{\Omega}},

where
C_{B}=\sqrt{|\partial\Omega|}\sup_{\boldsymbol{\psi}\in W,x\in\partial\Omega}|\boldsymbol{\psi}(x)|+C_{\Omega}\|\nabla u^{*}_{D}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}
.

Using the Poincaré-Friedrichs inequality,

\displaystyle\|\hat{\phi}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{C}_{\Omega}\left((R^{D}_{b})^{2}+\|\nabla\hat{\phi}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{C}_{\Omega}\left((R^{D}_{b})^{2}+C_{B}R_{b}^{D}+\frac{\tilde{C}_{\Omega}}{2}R_{g}^{2}+\frac{\tilde{C}_{\Omega}}{2}(R^{D}_{e})^{2}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\|\hat{\phi}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2},

which implies that

\|\hat{\phi}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq 2\tilde{C}_{\Omega}\left((R^{D}_{b})^{2}+C_{B}R_{b}^{D}+\frac{\tilde{C}_{\Omega}}{2}R_{g}^{2}+\frac{\tilde{C}_{\Omega}}{2}(R^{D}_{e})^{2}\right),

and

\displaystyle\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}\|_{H_{\text{div}}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(R_{g}+\|\nabla\hat{\phi}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right)^{2}+(R_{e}^{D})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2R_{g}^{2}+(R_{e}^{D})^{2}+2\|\hat{\phi}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 4C_{B}\tilde{C}_{\Omega}R^{D}_{b}+4\tilde{C}_{\Omega}(R^{D}_{b})^{2}+(2+2\tilde{C}_{\Omega}^{2})R^{2}_{g}+2\tilde{C}_{\Omega}^{2}(R^{D}_{e})^{2}+(R^{D}_{e})^{2}.

Next, we can show the expected loss can be bounded by the approximation error.

Let

For any

3.3
Convergence Rate and Comparison with DRM

Suppose the the approximation error of neural networks in the set
V
to any function
u
in a specific space
X
, equipped with the norm
\|\cdot\|_{X}
, has the following estimates

\min_{\phi\in V}\|\phi-u\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}\lesssim m^{-\gamma}\|u\|_{X}^{2},

where
m
is a parameter proportional to the total number of parameters in networks of
V
and
\gamma>0
is a positive constant. Similarly, we can expect the approximation error of networks in
W
to
\nabla u
has the same rate if
\nabla u
has desired regularity:

\min_{\boldsymbol{\psi}\in W}\|\boldsymbol{\psi}-\nabla u\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}\lesssim m^{-\gamma}\|\nabla u\|_{X^{d}}^{2},

where
\|\nabla u\|_{X^{d}}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\|\partial_{i}u\|_{X}^{2}.
Besides,
\|\boldsymbol{\psi}-\nabla u\|_{H_{\text{div}}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq d\|\boldsymbol{\psi}-\nabla u\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}
, so we have

\min_{\boldsymbol{\psi}\in W}\|\boldsymbol{\psi}-\nabla u\|_{H_{\text{div}}(\Omega)}^{2}\lesssim\frac{1}{m^{\gamma}}\|\nabla u\|_{X^{d}}^{2}.

Then we can derive the convergence rate for the mixed residual method when solving the Neumann problem (
5.2
) and the Dirichlet problem (
5.2
). Let us neglect the quadrature error part here, since the estimates of quadrature errors are quite similar for different methods. For the Neumann problem, let
(\phi^{*},\boldsymbol{\psi}^{*}):=\underset{\phi\in V,\boldsymbol{\psi}\in W}{\arg\min}L^{N}(\phi,\boldsymbol{\psi})
and
u^{*}_{N}\in X
. The following convergence rate can be derived from Theorem
6

\|\phi^{*}-u^{*}_{N}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}\lesssim\frac{1}{m^{\gamma}}\text{ and }\|\boldsymbol{\psi}^{*}-\nabla u^{*}_{N}\|_{H_{\text{div}}(\Omega)}^{2}\lesssim\frac{1}{m^{\gamma}}.

For the Dirichlet problem, by abusing the notation, let
(\phi^{*},\boldsymbol{\psi}^{*}):=\underset{\phi\in V,\boldsymbol{\psi}\in W}{\arg\min}L^{D}(\phi,\boldsymbol{\psi})
and
u^{*}_{D}\in X
. Then, from Theorem
9
, we can derive the convergence rate:

\|\phi^{*}-u^{*}_{D}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}\lesssim\frac{1}{m^{\gamma/2}}\text{ and }\|\boldsymbol{\psi}^{*}-\nabla u^{*}_{D}\|_{H_{\text{div}}(\Omega)}^{2}\lesssim\frac{1}{m^{\gamma/2}}.

Let us compare the above convergence rate with DRM. The priori error of DRM with homogeneous boundary conditions has been well-studied in the literature
[
13
,
9
,
22
]
.
For the Neumann problem with homogeneous boundary condition,
let
\phi^{*}
be DRM solution. Then, the error of
\phi^{*}
can be estimated by

\|\phi^{*}-u^{*}_{N}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq\min_{\phi}\|\phi-u^{*}_{N}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}\lesssim\frac{1}{m^{\gamma}}\|u\|_{X}^{2}.

For the Dirichlet problem with homogeneous boundary condition,
let
\phi^{*}_{\lambda}
be the DRM solution where
\lambda
is the weight of boundary penalty. Then it has the following estimates in
[
22
]
:

\displaystyle\|\phi^{*}_{\lambda}-u^{*}_{D}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}\lesssim\left(\sqrt{\frac{1+\lambda}{m^{\gamma}}\|u^{*}_{D}\|_{X}^{2}+\frac{1}{\lambda}}+\frac{1}{\lambda}\right)^{2}.

The best rate is then
\frac{1}{m^{\gamma/2}}
when choosing
\lambda=m^{\gamma/2}
.

Generally, the mixed residual method achieve the same convergence rate with respect to
m
with DRM in both problems. Our result suggests that the mixed residual method can also recover the Laplacian of the solution by taking divergence of the
\boldsymbol{\psi}
network. Moreover, our result can be applied to both homogeneous boundary and non-homogeneous boundary problems.

4.2
Estimation of the Rademacher Complexity

In this section, we will illustrate that the quadrature error trained on a finite dataset
\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
can be estimated by the Rademacher complexity. For simplicity, we consider
X_{i}\in\Omega=[0,1]^{d}
in the following estimation.

Definition 14
.

Let
\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be a set of random variables independently distributed and
\{\varepsilon_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be an i.i.d sequence of Rademacher variables (i.e. taking the values of
{
1,-1
}
equiprobably). Then the
empirical Rademacher Complexity
of the function class
\mathcal{F}
is a random variable given by

\hat{R}_{n}(\mathcal{F}):=\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}[\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}f(X_{i})|].

Taking its expectation yields the
Rademacher Complexity
of the function class
\mathcal{F}

R_{n}(\mathcal{F}):=\mathbb{E}_{X}[\hat{R}_{n}(\mathcal{F})]=\mathbb{E}_{X}\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}[\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}f(X_{i})|].

The definition gives some basic calculation rules:

Lemma 15
.

Let
\mathcal{F},\mathcal{G}
be function classes and
a,b
be constants. Then

(i)

R_{n}(\mathcal{F+G})\leq R_{n}(\mathcal{F})+R_{n}(\mathcal{G})
.

(ii)

R_{n}(a\mathcal{F})=|a|R_{n}(\mathcal{F}).

(iii)

Assume
g
is a fixed function and
\|g\|_{\infty}\leq b
, then
R_{n}(g)\leq\frac{b}{\sqrt{n}}.

(iv)

(Ledoux-Talagrand contraction lemma
[
8
]
) Assume that
\sigma:\mathbb{R}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is
l
-Lipschitz with
\sigma(0)=0
, then
R_{n}(\sigma(\mathcal{F}))\leq 2lR_{n}(\mathcal{F})
.

(v)

R_{n}(\mathcal{F}^{2})\leq 4\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\|f\|_{\infty}R_{n}(\mathcal{F})
.

(vi)

R_{n}(\mathcal{F}\mathcal{G})\leq 6\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{G}}\|f\|_{\infty}(R_{n}(\mathcal{F})+R_{n}(\mathcal{G}))
.

The proof can be found in Appendix
B
.
With the calculation rules prepared, we are ready to estimate the complexity of the neural network function classes and the loss function classes.

Lemma 16
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be the linear transformation function class defined by

\mathcal{G}:=\{\omega\cdot x+b|\|\omega\|_{2}=1,|b|\leq 1\}.

Then we have

R_{n}(\mathcal{G})\leq\frac{\sqrt{2d\log d}+1}{\sqrt{n}}.

(3)

Proof.

It is obvious that the Rademacher complexity of the constant is bounded by
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}
. Then we consider the function class

\hat{\mathcal{G}}:=\{\omega\cdot x|\|w\|_{2}=1\}.

Dividing the Rademacher complexity into each dimensional yields

R_{n}(\hat{\mathcal{G}})\leq\frac{\|w\|_{1}}{n}\mathbb{E}_{X}\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}X_{i}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{\sqrt{d}\|w\|_{2}}{n}\mathbb{E}_{X}\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}X_{i}\|_{\infty}.

Then we use the Massart lemma
[
19
]
:

\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}[\max_{a\in A}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}a_{i}]\leq\max_{a\in A}\|a\|_{2}\frac{\sqrt{2\log(|A|)}}{n}.

(4)

View each fearture
X_{i,j}
and
-X_{i,j}
for
j=1,\cdots,d
as a member of the finite hypothesis class, i.e.,

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=\{Y_{1},\cdots,Y_{d},-Y_{1},\cdots,-Y_{d}\},

\displaystyle Y_{j}

\displaystyle=(X_{1,j},\cdots,X_{n,j}),\qquad j=1,\cdots,d.

Applying (
4
) yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X}\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}X_{i}\|_{\infty}=\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}_{X}\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}\max_{a\in A}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}a_{i}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sqrt{2\log 2d}}{n}\mathbb{E}_{X}\max_{a\in A}\|a\|_{2}\leq\frac{\sqrt{2\log 2d}}{\sqrt{n}}.

The last step is finished by
\|Y_{i}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{n}\|Y_{i}\|_{\infty}=\sqrt{n}
, for all
j=1,\cdots,d
. Summarizing the result, we have the inequality

R_{n}(\mathcal{G})\leq\frac{\sqrt{2d\log 2d}+1}{\sqrt{n}}.

The estimation of the complexity of a two-layer neural network depends on the activation function, so we make the following assumptions:
\sigma\in H^{2}(\Omega)
with
\sigma(0)=0

\sup_{x}|\sigma^{(k)}(x)|\leq l_{k},\ k=0,1,2.

Lemma 17
.

The Rademacher complexity of

where

l_{1}

Using properties (i) and (iv), the Rademacher complexity of two layer neural network is broken down into the sum of the Rademacher complexity of each neuron, that is

\displaystyle R_{n}(V^{m}_{u})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\lVert u\rVert_{\mathcal{B}(\Omega)}}{\sqrt{n}}+\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}|a_{i}|R_{n}(\sigma(\mathcal{G}))

Proof.

According to the definition of the Rademacher complexity, it follows directly that the upper bound of

can be dividide into the following parts:

1.

\mathcal{L}_{g}=\{|\nabla\phi-\psi|^{2}|\phi\in V^{m}_{u},\psi\in W^{m}_{\nabla u}\}
,

2.

\mathcal{L}^{N}_{e}=\{(-\nabla\cdot\psi+\phi-f)^{2}|\phi\in V^{m}_{u},\psi\in W^{m}_{\nabla u}\}
,

3.

\mathcal{L}^{D}_{e}=\{((-\nabla\cdot\psi-f)^{2}|\psi\in W^{m}_{\nabla u}\}
.

The first term appears as a gradient penalty term in the loss function of both problems. By expanding the first term, we obtain

|\nabla\phi-\psi|^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}(\phi_{x_{j}}+\psi_{j})^{2}.

Viewing each dimension yields

\phi_{x_{j}}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}w_{i,j}\sigma^{\prime}(w_{i}\cdot x+b_{i}),\ \psi_{j}\in V^{m}_{u},\ j=1,\cdots,d.

It follows that

R_{n}(\mathcal{L}_{g})\leq 4\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sup_{\phi\in V^{m}_{u},\psi\in W^{m}_{\nabla u}}\|\nabla\phi-\psi\|_{\infty}(R_{n}(V^{m}_{u})+\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}|a_{i}w_{i,j}|R_{n}(\sigma^{\prime}(\mathcal{G}))),

where

\displaystyle\sup_{\phi\in V^{m}_{u},\psi\in W^{m}_{\nabla u}}\|\nabla\phi-\psi\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq(8l_{1}+2+8l_{0})\|u\|_{\mathcal{B}^{4}(\Omega)},

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{i=1}^{m}|a_{i}w_{i,j}|R_{n}(\sigma^{\prime}(\mathcal{G}))

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}|a_{i}|\sqrt{d}\|w_{i}\|_{2}R_{n}(\sigma^{\prime}(\mathcal{G}))

\displaystyle\leq 8\sqrt{d}\|u\|_{\mathcal{B}^{4}(\Omega)}R_{n}(\mathcal{\sigma^{\prime}(G)})

\displaystyle\leq 16\sqrt{d}l_{2}\|u\|_{\mathcal{B}^{4}(\Omega)}R_{n}(\mathcal{G}).

According to lemma
3
and
17
, it follows that

R_{n}(\mathcal{L}_{g})\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{d^{3}\log d}{n}}\|u\|_{\mathcal{B}^{4}(\Omega)}^{2}.

(8)

The estimation of the second part follows in a similar manner.

\displaystyle R_{n}(\mathcal{L}_{e}^{N})

\displaystyle\leq 4\sup_{\phi\in V^{m}_{u},\psi\in W^{m}_{\nabla u}}\|-\nabla\cdot\psi+\phi-f\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\cdot(8d\|u\|_{\mathcal{B}^{4}(\Omega)}R_{n}(\mathcal{\sigma^{\prime}(G)})+R_{n}(V_{u}^{m})+R_{n}(f))

By the fact that
\psi_{i}\in V_{u}^{m}
, the estimation of
\nabla\cdot\psi
is almost equivalent to that of
\phi_{x_{i}}
, which gives

\sup_{\phi\in V^{m}_{u},\psi\in W^{m}_{\nabla u}}\|-\nabla\cdot\psi+\phi-f\|_{\infty}\leq(8dl_{1}+3+8l_{0})\|u\|_{\mathcal{B}^{4}(\Omega)}.

Then the second part can be proved in the same way as shown before, and it is easy to show

R_{n}(\mathcal{L}_{e}^{N})\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{d^{5}\log d}{n}}\|u\|_{\mathcal{B}^{4}(\Omega)}^{2}.

(9)

\mathcal{L}^{D}_{e}
and
\mathcal{L}^{N}_{e}
have almost the same form. Consequently, the complexity differs by a constant factor.

The last step is to estimate the function classes with respect to the boundary conditions. With the assumption that the domain
\Omega
is a rectangle, it follows

\displaystyle R_{\bar{n}}(\mathcal{L}_{b}^{N})

\displaystyle\leq 4\sup_{\psi\in W^{m}_{\nabla u}}\|\psi\cdot\mathbf{n}-g_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial\Omega)}(R_{\bar{n}}(V^{m}_{u})+R_{\bar{n}}(g_{1}))\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{d\log d}{{\bar{n}}}}\|u\|_{\mathcal{B}^{4}(\Omega)}^{2}.

(10)

In general, the number of sample points on the boundary is different from that in the interior domain. Comparing with (
8
) and (
9
), taking
\bar{n}=\frac{n}{d^{2}}
will not change the upper bound of the Rademacher complexity.

The formula (
8
), (
9
) and (
10
) conclude a upper bound of the Rademacher complexity.

R_{n}(\mathcal{L}^{N})+R_{\bar{n}}(\mathcal{L}^{N}_{b})\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{d^{5}\log d}{n}}\|u\|_{\mathcal{B}^{4}(\Omega)}^{2}.

(11)

The estimation for the Dirichlet problem is almost identical. Although these are two different problems, they have the same upper bound, which is determined by Laplacian, dimensionality, and size of the dataset.

It remains to show that the Rademacher complexity can bound the quadrature error.
The following lemma fills up the gap between the Rademacher complexity and the quadrature error.

Lemma 19
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a set of functions,
\{X_{1},\cdots,X_{n}\}
be i.i.d. random variables, and
\mathbb{E}_{X}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}l(u(X_{i}))=L(u)
. Then

\mathbb{E}_{X}\sup_{u\in\mathcal{F}}|L(u)-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}l(u(X_{i}))|\leq 2R_{n}(\mathcal{L}),

(12)

where
\mathcal{L}:=\{l(u)|u\in\mathcal{F}\}
.

Proof.

We first show the inequality that

\sup_{f}\mathbb{E}_{X}f(X)\leq\mathbb{E}_{X}\sup_{f}f(X).

It follows directly from

\mathbb{E}_{X}f(x)\leq\mathbb{E}_{X}\sup_{f}f(X)
for all
f
. Recall that

L(u)=\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}_{X}\sum_{i=1}^{n}l(u(X_{i})),

then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X}\sup_{u\in\mathcal{F}}|L(u)-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}l(u(X_{i}))|

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{X}\sup_{u\in\mathcal{F}}|\mathbb{E}_{Y}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(l(u(Y_{i}))-l(u(X_{i})))|

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{X}\mathbb{E}_{Y}\sup_{u\in\mathcal{F}}|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(l(u(Y_{i}))-l(u(X_{i})))|.

Here
X_{i}
and
Y_{i}
have the same distribution, which yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X}\mathbb{E}_{Y}\sup_{u\in\mathcal{F}}|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(l(u(Y_{i}))-l(u(X_{i})))|

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{X}\mathbb{E}_{Y}\mathbb{E}_{\epsilon}\sup_{u\in\mathcal{F}}|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\epsilon_{i}(l(u(Y_{i}))-l(u(X_{i})))|

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{Y}\mathbb{E}_{\epsilon}\sup_{u\in\mathcal{F}}|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\epsilon_{i}l(u(Y_{i}))|+\mathbb{E}_{X}\mathbb{E}_{\epsilon}\sup_{u\in\mathcal{F}}|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\epsilon_{i}l(u(X_{i}))|

\displaystyle=2R_{n}(\mathcal{L}).

4.3
Proof of the main theorems

Now we can use the previous results to prove the main theorems. The proof of Theorem
4
is given below.

Proof.

From Theorem
6
, we know that the error
\|\hat{\phi}-u^{*}_{N}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}
and
\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}-\nabla u^{*}_{N}\|_{H_{div}(\Omega)}^{2}
can be bounded by quadrature error:

E_{1}=2\sup_{\phi\in V,\boldsymbol{\psi}\in W}|L^{N}(\phi,\boldsymbol{\psi})-L^{N}_{n}(\phi,\boldsymbol{\psi})|

and approximation error:

\displaystyle E_{2}=\underset{\boldsymbol{\psi}\in W}{\min}\left(\|\boldsymbol{\psi}-\nabla u^{*}_{N}\|_{H_{\text{div}}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{\psi}-\nabla u^{*}_{N}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}\right)+\underset{\phi\in V}{\min}\left(\|\phi-u^{*}_{N}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}\right).

By Lemma
12
and
13
, since
u^{*}_{N}\in\mathcal{B}^{4}(\Omega)
and
\nabla u^{*}_{N}\in\mathcal{B}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we can derive an estimate for the approximation error:
E_{2}\lesssim\frac{\|u^{*}_{N}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{B}^{4}(\Omega)}}{m}.

For the quadrature error, it is easy to check the activation function
\text{ReQU}(x)
satisfies

\displaystyle|\text{ReQU}(\omega\cdot x+b)|

\displaystyle\leq(\sqrt{d}+1)^{2},

\displaystyle|\text{ReQU}^{\prime}(\omega\cdot x+b)|

\displaystyle\leq 2(\sqrt{d}+1),

\displaystyle|\text{ReQU}^{\prime\prime}(\omega\cdot x+b)|

\displaystyle\leq 2,

for all
x\in\Omega
. Then we can apply Lemma
18
and
19
to derive the estimate hold for any
\phi\in V^{m}_{u^{*}_{N}}
and
\boldsymbol{\psi}\in W^{m}_{u^{*}_{N}}
:

\mathbb{E}|L^{N}_{n}(\phi,\boldsymbol{\psi})-L^{N}(\phi,\boldsymbol{\psi})|\leq 2(R_{n}(\mathcal{L}^{N})+R_{\bar{n}}(\mathcal{L}^{N}_{b}))\lesssim\frac{\|u^{*}_{N}\|_{\mathcal{B}^{4}(\Omega)}^{2}}{\sqrt{n}},

if we take
\bar{n}
no less than
n/d^{2}
and the constant is at most a polynomial of
d
. Then,
E_{1}
also has the same estimate. Therefore, we have proved the theorem.

The Theorem
5
can also be proved in the same way.

(iii)
First use the definition of the Rademacher complexity

\displaystyle R_{n}(g)=\mathbb{E}_{X}\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}\sup_{g\in\{g\}}|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}g(X_{i})|=\mathbb{E}_{X}\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}g_{i}|,

where
g_{i}=g(X_{i})
,
i=1,\cdots,n
. Next we expand part of this summation

\displaystyle(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}g_{i})^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}^{2}g_{i}^{2}+\sum_{i\neq j}\varepsilon_{i}\varepsilon_{j}g_{i}g_{j}\leq b^{2}(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i})^{2}+\sum_{i\neq j}\varepsilon_{i}\varepsilon_{j}(g_{i}g_{j}-b^{2}).

(19)

Taking the expectation of
\varepsilon
yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}g_{i})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq b^{2}\mathbb{E}(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i})^{2}+\mathbb{E}[\sum_{i\neq j}\varepsilon_{i}\varepsilon_{j}(g_{i}g_{j}-b^{2})]

\displaystyle=b^{2}\mathbb{E}(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i})^{2}+\sum_{i\neq j}\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{i}\varepsilon_{j}](g_{i}g_{j}-b^{2})

\displaystyle=b^{2}\mathbb{E}(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i})^{2}+\sum_{i\neq j}\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{i}]\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{j}](g_{i}g_{j}-b^{2})

\displaystyle=b^{2}\mathbb{E}(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i})^{2}.

Let

, and it satisfies

\mathbb{E}[Y]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{i}]=0,\ \text{Var}[Y]=n\text{Var}[\varepsilon]=n.

With the above estimation, the proof is completed

\displaystyle R_{n}(g)\leq\frac{1}{n}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}g_{i})^{2}}\leq\frac{b}{n}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]}=\frac{b}{n}\sqrt{\text{Var}[Y]}=\frac{b}{\sqrt{n}}.

(vi)

\displaystyle R_{n}(\mathcal{F}\mathcal{G})
