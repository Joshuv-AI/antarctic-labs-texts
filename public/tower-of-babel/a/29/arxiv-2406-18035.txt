Local Linear Recovery Guarantee of Deep Neural Networks at Overparameterization
Yaoyu Zhang zhyy.sjtu@sjtu.edu.cn, Leyang Zhang leyangz_hawk@outlook.com, Zhongwang Zhang 0123zzw666@sjtu.edu.cn, Zhiwei Bai bai299@sjtu.edu.cn

Abstract
Determining whether deep neural network (DNN) models can reliably recover target functions at overparameterization is a critical yet complex issue in the theory of deep learning. To advance understanding in this area, we introduce a concept we term “local linear recovery” (LLR), a weaker form of target function recovery that renders the problem more amenable to theoretical analysis. In the sense of LLR, we prove that functions expressible by narrower DNNs are guaranteed to be recoverable from fewer samples than model parameters. Specifically, we establish upper limits on the optimistic sample sizes, defined as the smallest sample size necessary to guarantee LLR, for functions in the space of a given DNN. Furthermore, we prove that these upper bounds are achieved in the case of two-layer tanh neural networks. Our research lays a solid groundwork for future investigations into the recovery capabilities of DNNs in overparameterized scenarios.



keywords

deep learning theory, recovery at overparameterization,
local linear recovery guarantee, optimistic sample size.

1
Introduction

Determining the requisite number of data points for a model to recover a target function is a fundamental problem in regression. This issue is particularly pertinent for deep neural network (DNN) models, where the question arises: Can these models recover target functions using fewer data points than their parameter count, thereby operating effectively in an overparameterized regime? Traditional wisdom suggests that a linear model with
M
parameters typically needs
M
data points to reconstruct a linear function. Particularly, in band-limited signal recovery, the Nyquist-Shannon sampling theorem posits that a periodic signal with a maximum frequency of
f
(represented by
M=2f
coefficients) can be perfectly reconstructed from
n\geqslant 2f
uniformly sampled points
(
Shannon, 1984
)
. These precedents imply that linear models lack recovery guarantee when overparameterized. In contrast, nonlinear DNN models have been empirically shown to generalize effectively even when overparameterized
(
Zhang et al., 2017
)
. Subsequent experiments reinforce this observation, indicating that DNNs can achieve zero generalization error in recovering target functions under overparameterization
(
Zhang et al., 2023b
)
. This study delves into the theoretical underpinnings of target function recovery for DNNs, with the goal of establishing a recovery guarantee in overparameterized scenarios.

Within the spectrum of recovery guarantees, the global recovery guarantee—ensuring target recovery through comprehensive training—is the most robust and practically relevant, especially in overparameterized contexts. However, the intricate and highly nonlinear training dynamics of DNNs render this global guarantee theoretically elusive. To navigate this complexity, we propose a more attainable goal: establishing a weaker form of recovery guarantee for DNNs in overparameterized conditions. Specifically, we introduce the concept of local linear recovery (LLR) guarantee, which scales back the expectation from global recovery from a random initialization to local recovery in the vicinity of an optimal point within the parameter space. While local linear fitting of data is not practically feasible in general, the LLR-guarantee sheds light on the optimistic (i.e., best-possible) performance for global recovery of DNNs, suggesting that without an LLR-guarantee at overparameterization, stronger forms of recovery are unlikely.

Our main contributions are threefold: (i) We introduce the LLR-guarantee and formulate its theoretical framework for both differentiable and analytic models, demonstrating that linear models do not possess an LLR-guarantee when overparameterized. (ii) Employing the Embedding Principle
(
Zhang et al., 2021b
;
Zhang et al., 2022
)
, we derive upper bounds for the optimistic sample sizes—defined as the smallest sample sizes that ensure an LLR guarantee—for general DNNs. These bounds affirm the LLR-guarantee at overparameterization for all functions expressible by narrower DNNs. (iii) We pinpoint the exact optimistic sample sizes for two-layer fully-connected and convolutional tanh neural networks, which meet their respective upper bounds, thereby illustrating the exactitude of our upper bounds.

2
Related works

In our research, we introduce the concept of optimistic sample size, which quantifies the minimum number of training samples necessary to recover a target function under a best-possible condition. This approach diverges significantly from traditional sample complexity, which typically estimates the sample requirement for achieving a specified performance level in the worst-case scenarios
(
Shalev-Shwartz and Ben-David, 2014
)
. Recognizing that DNNs often perform substantially better in practice than theoretical worst-case predictions suggest
(
Zhang et al., 2017
;
Zhang et al., 2021a
)
, our LLR analysis pioneers a framework for estimating sample sizes under the best-possible conditions. Furthermore, our empirical findings reveal that the practical performance of a finely-tuned DNN can approach this optimistic threshold, even in scenarios of substantial overparameterization. Moreover, as demonstrated in
Zhang and Xu (2024)
, techniques such as dropout can further enhance the network’s performance to recover target functions.

Our theoretical LLR framework, which is predicated on the linearization of DNNs, stands in stark contrast to linear analyses based on the NTK/lazy training/linear regime
(
Jacot et al., 2018
;
Arora et al., 2019
;
Chizat et al., 2019
;
Luo et al., 2021
)
. The main differences are as follows: (i) NTK analysis linearizes around a random initial point, whereas LLR analysis considers linearization around a target point, i.e., a global minimizer with zero generalization error. (ii) NTK pertains to the linear training behavior of DNNs with large initial weights, whereas the optimistic sample sizes from LLR analysis are empirically linked to the performance of nonlinear training dynamics exhibited at small initializations. According to the phase diagrams in Refs.
(
Luo et al., 2021
;
Zhou et al., 2022
)
, NTK analysis corresponds to the linear regimes, while our LLR results associate to the condensed regimes, where nonlinear condensation dynamics are instrumental in achieving near-optimism performance.

The discovery of the embedding principle
(
Zhang et al., 2021b
;
Zhang et al., 2022
;
Fukumizu et al., 2019
;
Simsek et al., 2021
;
Bai et al., 2022
)
has shed light on the analysis of critical points within the loss landscapes of DNNs and has forged connections between the loss landscapes of networks of varying widths. Notably,
Zhang et al. (2022)
introduces a comprehensive suite of critical embedding operators that map the parameter space of a narrower DNN to that of a wider one while preserving both the output function and the criticality of the network. This powerful analytical tool further illuminates the hierarchical model rank structure inherent in DNNs. Leveraging these critical embeddings, which inherently maintain the model rank, we derive in this work an upper bound for the optimistic sample size applicable to general DNNs.

3
Theory of local linear recovery

3.1
Assumptions and definitions

Assumption
.

(i) We consider differentiable (w.r.t. both inputs and parameters) models with
1
-d output
f_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot)=f(\cdot;\bm{\theta}):\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{M}\to\mathbb{R}
.

(ii) We consider target functions
f^{*}
expressible by the model
f_{\bm{\theta}}
, i.e.,
f^{*}\in\mathcal{F}:=\{f_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot)|\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}\}
. And the training data
S=\{(\bm{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},y_{i}=f^{*}(\bm{x}_{i})\in\mathbb{R})\}_{i=1}^{n}
is sampled from
f^{*}
.

(iii) The loss function
\ell(\cdot,\cdot)
is a continuously differentiable distance function and the empirical loss
L_{S}(\bm{\theta})=\mathbb{E}_{S}\ell(f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}),y)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(f(\bm{x}_{i};\bm{\theta}),y_{i})
.

Under the above general assumptions, this work focuses on the recovery of target function
f^{*}
for the following regression problem when
n\leqslant M
:

\mathrm{min}_{\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}_{i}),f^{*}(\bm{x}_{i})).

(1)

We say
f^{*}
is successfully recovered when the solution
\bm{\theta}^{*}
obtained by an algorithm satisfies
f_{\bm{\theta}^{*}}=f^{*}
. For convenience, we define the target set
\mathcal{M}_{f^{*}}
as follows, by which
f^{*}
is recovered when
\bm{\theta}^{*}\in\mathcal{M}_{f^{*}}
.

Definition 1

(target set)
.

Suppose we have a model
f_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot)=f(\cdot;\bm{\theta}):\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{M}\to\mathbb{R}
with model function space
\mathcal{F}=\{f_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot)|\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}\}
. For any target function
f^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}
, the target set
\mathcal{M}_{f^{\prime}}:=\{\bm{\theta}|f(\cdot;\bm{\theta})=f^{\prime}\}
.

We note that analyzing the recovery of
f^{*}
at overparameterization through global training presents significant difficulties due to two primary challenges: (i) the optimization challenge, where it remains uncertain whether the training process can avoid local minima and saddle points to converge to a global minimum
(
Sun et al., 2020
)
; (ii) the challenge of infinite solutions, where the target set
\mathcal{M}_{f^{*}}
is embedded within an approximately
(M-n)
-dimensional manifold of global minima with complex geometry
(
Cooper, 2021
;
Zhang et al., 2023a
)
, making it difficult to ascertain if global training can precisely reach
\mathcal{M}_{f^{*}}
. To bypass these obstacles, we introduce a more theoretically tractable variant of recovery as follows.

Definition 2

(local linear recovery (LLR) guarantee)
.

Suppose we have a differentiable model
f_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot)
with
M
parameters, a target function
f^{*}\in\mathcal{F}:=\{f_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot)\}
and training data
S=\{(\bm{x}_{i},f^{*}(\bm{x}_{i}))\}_{i=1}^{n}
.

(a)
LLR-guarantee:
We say
f^{*}
has local linear recovery (LLR) guarantee (by model
f_{\bm{\theta}}
from
S
) if the following condition holds: There exists
\bm{\theta}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}_{f^{*}}
such that

f^{*}=\mathrm{argmin}_{g\in\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{\bm{\theta}^{\prime}}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(g(\bm{x}_{i}),f^{*}(\bm{x}_{i})),

(2)

where
\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{\bm{\theta}^{\prime}}=\{f(\cdot;\bm{\theta}^{\prime})+\bm{a}^{\text{T}}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}f(\cdot;\bm{\theta}^{\prime})|\bm{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}\}
is the tangent function hyperplane at
\bm{\theta}^{\prime}
. If the above condition holds, we say
f^{*}
has LLR-guarantee at
\bm{\theta}^{\prime}
(by model
f_{\bm{\theta}}
from
S
).

(b)
n
-sample LLR-guarantee:
We say a function
f^{*}\in\mathcal{F}
has
n
-sample LLR-guarantee if there exists a
n
-sample dataset
S=\{(\bm{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},f^{*}(\bm{x}_{i})\in\mathbb{R})\}_{i=1}^{n}
such that
f^{*}
has LLR-guarantee. Furthermore, if
n<M
, then we say
f^{*}
has LLR-guarantee at overparameterization.

(c)
n
-sample LLR-guarantee a.e.:
We say a function
f^{*}\in\mathcal{F}
has
n
-sample LLR-guarantee a.e. (almost everywhere) if the following condition holds: For inputs
X=[\bm{x}_{1},\cdots,\bm{x}_{n}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}
a.e. with respect to
\mathcal{L}^{d\times m}
Lebesgue measure,
f^{*}
has LLR-guarantee from
S=\{(\bm{x}_{i},f^{*}(\bm{x}_{i}))\}_{i=1}^{n}
.

Remark 3

(LLR-guarantee vs. LLR-guarantee a.e.)
.

(i) Although
n
-sample LLR-guarantee seems to be significantly weaker than
n
-sample LLR-guarantee a.e., we will show later that any
n
-sample LLR-guarantee automatically upgrades to
n
-sample LLR-guarantee a.e. for analytic models, e.g., neural networks with tanh, sigmoid or GELU activation. For the pursuit of generality, we mainly focus on the
n
-sample LLR-guarantee in this work. (ii) In general,
n
-sample LLR-guarantee informs the potential of recovering the target from
n
samples in practice, whereas no
n
-sample LLR-guarantee is a strong indication that
n
samples are not sufficient to recover the target.

Proposition 1
.

Suppose we have a differentiable model
f_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot)
with
M
parameters. For any target function
f^{*}\in\mathcal{F}
, if it has
n
-sample LLR-guarantee, then it has
n^{\prime}
-sample LLR-guarantee for any
n^{\prime}\geqslant n
.

Proof.

When
f^{*}
has
n
-sample LLR-guarantee, there exists
S=\{(\bm{x}_{i},f^{*}(\bm{x}_{i}))\}_{i=1}^{n}
and
\bm{\theta}^{*}\in\mathcal{M}_{f^{*}}
such that the solution set

\phi_{S}^{*}:=\mathrm{argmin}_{f\in\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{\bm{\theta}^{*}}}\mathbb{E}_{(\bm{x},y)\in S}\ell(f(\bm{x}),y)=\{f^{*}\}.

For
S^{\prime}=S\cup\{(\bm{x}^{\prime}_{i},f^{*}(\bm{x}^{\prime}_{i}))\}_{i=n+1}^{n^{\prime}}
with any
\bm{x}^{\prime}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
for
i\in[n+1:n^{\prime}]
, let

\phi_{S^{\prime}}^{*}:=\mathrm{argmin}_{f\in\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{\bm{\theta}^{*}}}\mathbb{E}_{(\bm{x},y)\in S^{\prime}}\ell(f(\bm{x}),y).

Obvious,
f^{*}\in\phi_{S^{\prime}}^{*}
, by which the empirical loss attains
0
on
S^{\prime}
. Then, any global minimizer
f\in\phi_{S^{\prime}}^{*}
also attains
0
empirical loss on
S^{\prime}
, thus
0
empirical loss on
S
. Therefore,
\phi_{S^{\prime}}^{*}\subset\phi_{S}^{*}=\{f^{*}\}
, which yields

\phi_{S^{\prime}}^{*}=\phi_{S}^{*}=\{f^{*}\}.

By Definition
2
,
f^{*}
has
n^{\prime}
-sample LLR-guarantee.
∎

Proposition.
1
signifies the importance of understanding the minimum sample size with LLR-guarantee for a target function, which is rigorously defined below as an optimistic sample size.

Definition 4

(optimistic sample size)
.

Suppose we have a differentiable model
f_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot)
. For any function
f^{*}\in\mathcal{F}
, if
f^{*}
has LLR-guarantee from
n
samples but not
n-1
samples, then its optimistic sample size

O_{f_{\bm{\theta}}}(f^{*})=n.

3.2
General LLR theory

In this section, we present the theoretical results of LLR for the regression problem of general differentiable models. In particular, we establish the quantitative relation between the LLR-guarantee and the model rank, by which estimating the optimistic sample size of a target converts to an estimation of its model rank.

Definition 5

(model rank)
.

Given any differentiable (in parameters) model
f_{\bm{\theta}}
, the model rank for any
\bm{\theta}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
is defined as

\textnormal{rank}_{f_{\bm{\theta}}}(\bm{\theta}^{*}):=\textnormal{dim}\left(\textnormal{span}\left\{\partial_{\theta_{i}}f(\cdot;\bm{\theta}^{*})\right\}_{i=1}^{M}\right),

(3)

where
\textnormal{span}\left\{\phi_{i}(\cdot)\right\}_{i=1}^{M}=\{\sum_{i=1}^{M}a_{i}\phi_{i}(\cdot)|a_{1},\cdots,a_{M}\in\mathbb{R}\}
and
\textnormal{dim}(\cdot)
returns the dimension of a linear function space.

Definition 6

(empirical tangent matrix and empirical model rank)
.

Given any differentiable model
f_{\bm{\theta}}
and training data
S=\{(\bm{x}_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
, at any parameter point
\bm{\theta}^{*}
,
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}f(\bm{X};\bm{\theta}^{*})=[\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}f(\bm{x}_{1};\bm{\theta}^{*}),\cdots,\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}f(\bm{x}_{n};\bm{\theta}^{*})]
is referred to as the empirical tangent matrix. Then the empirical model rank is defined as follows

\textnormal{rank}_{S}(\bm{\theta}^{*})=\textnormal{rank}(\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}f(\bm{X};\bm{\theta}^{*})).

Lemma 7

(LLR condition)
.

f^{*}
has local linear recovery guarantee at
\bm{\theta}^{*}
by model
f_{\bm{\theta}}
from
S
if and only if
\textnormal{rank}_{S}(\bm{\theta}^{*})=\textnormal{rank}_{f_{\bm{\theta}}}(\bm{\theta}^{*})
.

Proof.

Define

\tilde{R}_{S}(\bm{a})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(f(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{\theta}^{*})+\bm{a}^{\text{T}}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}f(\bm{x}_{i};\bm{\theta}^{*}),f^{*}(\bm{x}_{i})).

First assume that
f^{*}
has LLR guarantee at
\bm{\theta}^{*}
by model
f_{\bm{\theta}}
from
S
. Then
f(\cdot;\bm{\theta}^{*})=f^{*}
by Definition
2
, and this function is unique. This uniqueness implies that for any
\bm{a}\in\ker\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}f(\bm{X};\bm{\theta}^{*})
we must have

f(\cdot;\bm{\theta}^{*})+\bm{a}^{\text{T}}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}f(\cdot;\bm{\theta}^{*})=f^{*}.

Equivalently,
\bm{a}\in\ker\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}f(\cdot;\bm{\theta}^{*})
. This shows
\ker\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}f(\bm{X};\bm{\theta}^{*})\subseteq\ker\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}f(\cdot;\bm{\theta}^{*})
. But clearly
\ker\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}f(\cdot;\bm{\theta}^{*})\subseteq\ker\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}f(\bm{X};\bm{\theta}^{*})
, so the two kernels are equal. It follows that

\textnormal{rank}_{S}(\bm{\theta}^{*})=\textnormal{rank}\left(\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}f(\bm{X};\bm{\theta}^{*})\right)=\textnormal{rank}_{f_{\bm{\theta}}}(\bm{\theta}^{*})

Conversely, assume that
\textnormal{rank}_{S}(\bm{\theta}^{*})=\textnormal{rank}_{f_{\bm{\theta}}}(\bm{\theta}^{*})
. Then, similar as above, we have
\ker\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}f(\cdot;\bm{\theta}^{*})=\ker\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}f(\bm{X};\bm{\theta}^{*})
. Because
\ell
is a distance function, for any
\bm{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
with
\tilde{R}_{S}(\bm{a})=R_{S}(\bm{\theta}^{*})=0
, we must have

\bm{a}^{\text{T}}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}f(\bm{x}_{i};\bm{\theta}^{*})=0,\quad\forall\,1\leqslant i\leqslant n.

Equivalently,
\bm{a}\in\ker\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}f(\bm{X};\bm{\theta}^{*})
and thus
\bm{a}^{\text{T}}f(\cdot;\bm{\theta}^{*})=0
. This shows

f^{*}=f(\cdot;\bm{\theta}^{*})=\mathrm{argmin}_{g\in\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{\bm{\theta}^{\prime}}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(g(\bm{x}_{i}),f^{*}(\bm{x}_{i}))

is well-defined, i.e.,
f^{*}
has LLR guarantee at
\bm{\theta}^{*}
.

∎

Corollary 1

(phase transition of LLR-guarantee at a target point)
.

For any
\bm{\theta}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}_{f^{*}}
, if training data size
n<\textnormal{rank}_{f_{\bm{\theta}}}(\bm{\theta}^{\prime})
,
f^{*}
has no local linear recovery guarantee at
\bm{\theta}^{\prime}
. Otherwise, if
n\geqslant\textnormal{rank}_{f_{\bm{\theta}}}(\bm{\theta}^{\prime})
,
f^{*}
has
n
-sample LLR-guarantee, i.e., there exits an
n
-sample dataset
S^{\prime}=\{(\bm{x}_{i},f^{*}(\bm{x}_{i}))\}_{i=1}^{n}
such that
f^{*}
has local linear recovery guarantee at
\bm{\theta}^{\prime}
.

Proof.

For
n<\textnormal{rank}_{f_{\bm{\theta}}}(\bm{\theta}^{\prime})
,
\textnormal{rank}_{S}(\bm{\theta}^{\prime})\leqslant n<\textnormal{rank}_{f_{\bm{\theta}}}(\bm{\theta}^{\prime})
. By Lemma
7
,
f^{*}
has no local linear recovery guarantee at
\bm{\theta}^{\prime}
. On the other hand, for
n\geqslant\textnormal{rank}_{f_{\bm{\theta}}}(\bm{\theta}^{\prime})
, we claim that there exist
\bm{X}=(\bm{x}_{1},...,\bm{x}_{n})
with
\textnormal{rank}\left(\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}f(\bm{X};\bm{\theta}^{*})\right)=\textnormal{rank}_{f_{\bm{\theta}}}(\cdot;\bm{\theta}^{\prime})
. Suppose this is not true. Let

M^{\prime}=\max_{\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}}\textnormal{rank}\left(\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}f(\bm{X};\bm{\theta}^{\prime})\right)

and let
\bm{x}_{1},...,\bm{x}_{M^{\prime}}
be such that

\textnormal{rank}\left[\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}f((\bm{x}_{1},...,\bm{x}_{M^{\prime}});\bm{\theta}^{\prime})\right]=M^{\prime}.

So in particular
M^{\prime}<\textnormal{rank}_{f_{\bm{\theta}}}(\cdot;\bm{\theta}^{\prime})\leqslant M
. Given
a\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
such that
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}f(\bm{x}_{i};\bm{\theta})=0
for all
1\leq i\leq M^{\prime}
, we must have
a\in\ker\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}f(\bm{\theta}^{\prime};\cdot)
. Therefore,

\ker\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}f(\bm{\theta}^{\prime};\cdot)\subseteq\ker\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}f((\bm{x}_{1},...,\bm{x}_{M^{\prime}});\bm{\theta}^{\prime})\subseteq\ker\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}f(\bm{\theta}^{\prime};\cdot).

This means

\dim\ker\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}f(\bm{\theta}^{\prime};\cdot)=\dim\ker\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}f(\bm{X};\bm{\theta}^{\prime})=M-M^{\prime}

and thus
\textnormal{rank}_{f_{\bm{\theta}}}(\bm{\theta}^{\prime})=M^{\prime}
, a contradiction. So there must exist
\bm{X}=(\bm{x}_{1},...,\bm{x}_{n})
with
\textnormal{rank}\left(\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}f(\bm{X};\bm{\theta}^{*})\right)=\textnormal{rank}_{f_{\bm{\theta}}}(\cdot;\bm{\theta}^{\prime})
. By Lemma
7
,
f^{*}
has
n
-sample guarantee.

∎

We address above the LLR-guarantee at a target point. In the following, we further address the LLR-guarantee for recovering a target function.

Definition 8

(model rank for function)
.

The model rank for any function
f^{*}\in\mathcal{F}
is defined as

\textnormal{rank}_{f_{\bm{\theta}}}(f^{*}):=\min_{\bm{\theta}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}_{f^{*}}}\textnormal{rank}_{f_{\bm{\theta}}}(\bm{\theta}^{\prime}).

(4)

Theorem 1

(optimistic sample size estimate)
.

Suppose we have a differentiable model
f_{\bm{\theta}}
. For any function
f^{*}\in\mathcal{F}
, we have

O_{f_{\bm{\theta}}}(f^{*})=\textnormal{rank}_{f_{\bm{\theta}}}(f^{*}).

Proof.

When the sample size
n<\textnormal{rank}_{f_{\bm{\theta}}}(f^{*})
, for any
\bm{\theta}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}_{f^{*}}
,
n<\textnormal{rank}_{f_{\bm{\theta}}}(\bm{\theta}^{\prime})
. By Corollary
1
,
f^{*}
has no LLR-guarantee at
\bm{\theta}^{\prime}
. Therefore,
f^{*}
has no
n
-sample LLR-guarantee. When the sample size
n\geqslant\textnormal{rank}_{f_{\bm{\theta}}}(f^{*})
, there exists
\bm{\theta}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}_{f^{*}}
, such that
n\geqslant\textnormal{rank}_{f_{\bm{\theta}}}(f^{*})=\textnormal{rank}_{f_{\bm{\theta}}}(\bm{\theta}^{\prime})
. By Corollary
1
,
f^{*}
has
n
-sample LLR-guarantee. By Definition
4
, we have

O_{f_{\bm{\theta}}}(f^{*})=\textnormal{rank}_{f_{\bm{\theta}}}(f^{*}).

∎

The subsequent two corollaries provide a rigorous formulation of two intuitive assertions about the best-possible cases of regression problem (
1
): (i)
M
samples are sufficient for recovery; (ii) linear models generally cannot be recovered at overparameterization.

Corollary 2

(generic upper bound for optimistic sample size)
.

Suppose we have a differentiable model
f_{\bm{\theta}}
. For any function
f^{*}\in\mathcal{F}
, we have

O_{f_{\bm{\theta}}}(f^{*})\leqslant M.

Proof.

Because
\textnormal{rank}_{f_{\bm{\theta}}}(\bm{\theta})\leqslant M
for any
\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
,
\textnormal{rank}_{f_{\bm{\theta}}}(f^{*})\leqslant M
for any
f^{*}\in\mathcal{F}
. Therefore, by Theorem
1
,
O_{f_{\bm{\theta}}}(f^{*})=\textnormal{rank}_{f_{\bm{\theta}}}(f^{*})\leqslant M
.
∎

Corollary 3

(no LLR-guarantee for linear models at overparameterization)
.

For a linear model
f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\theta_{i}\phi_{i}(\bm{x})
with
\bm{\theta}=[\theta_{1},\cdots,\theta_{M}]^{\text{T}}
, if its basis functions are linearly independent, i.e.,
\textnormal{dim}(\textnormal{span}\{\phi_{i}(\cdot)\}_{i=1}^{M})=M
, then

O_{f_{\bm{\theta}}}(f^{*})\equiv M,

i.e., none of the functions in the model function space
\mathcal{F}
has LLR-guarantee at overparameterization.

Proof.

For any
\bm{\theta}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
, we have

\textnormal{rank}_{f_{\bm{\theta}}}(\bm{\theta}^{*})=\textnormal{dim}\left(\textnormal{span}\left\{\partial_{\theta_{i}}f(\cdot;\bm{\theta}^{*})\right\}_{i=1}^{M}\right)=\textnormal{dim}\left(\textnormal{span}\left\{\phi_{i}(\bm{x})\right\}_{i=1}^{M}\right)=M.

Therefore,
O_{f_{\bm{\theta}}}(f^{*})=\textnormal{rank}_{f_{\bm{\theta}}}(f^{*})=M
for any
f^{*}\in\mathcal{F}
.
∎

3.3
LLR-guarantee for analytic models

In this subsection, we exploit the characteristics of analytic functions to enhance the LLR guarantee, extending it to an almost everywhere (a.e.) guarantee for analytic models.

Lemma 9
.

Given
m
linearly independent analytic functions
\phi_{1}(\bm{x}),\cdots,\phi_{m}(\bm{x})
with
\phi_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
for all
i\in[m]
,
\textnormal{rank}(\bm{\Phi}(\bm{X}))=m
almost everywhere (a.e.) with respect to
\mathcal{L}^{d\times m}
Lebesgue measure, where

\bm{\Phi}(\bm{X}):=\left[\begin{matrix}\phi_{1}(\bm{x}_{1})&...&\phi_{m}(\bm{x}_{1})\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\phi_{1}(\bm{x}_{m})&...&\phi_{m}(\bm{x}_{m})\end{matrix}\right].

Proof.

Clearly,
\det(\bm{\Phi}(\cdot)):\mathbb{R}^{d\times m}\to\mathbb{R}
is an analytic function over
\mathbb{R}^{d\times m}
. In addition, because
\{\phi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
are linearly independent, there exists
\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times m}
such that
\det(\bm{\Phi}(\cdot))\neq 0
, i.e.,
\det(\bm{\Phi}(\cdot))
is not constant zero. By the property of real analytic function
(

4
Upper bounds of optimistic sample sizes for DNNs

Building on the LLR theory, the task of estimating optimistic sample sizes for target functions necessitates the elucidation of the model rank across the space of functions. This task is notably challenging, particularly for DNNs with three or more layers, due to the complexity inherent in disentangling the linear dependencies among the compositional tangent functions. Fortunately, we observe that the critical embedding operators introduced in Refs.
(
Zhang et al., 2021b
;
Zhang et al., 2022
)
can preserve the tangent function space and the model rank when transitioning from a parameter point in a narrower DNN to one in a wider DNN. This observation allows us to constrain the model rank of a target function within a wide DNN by referencing the smallest DNN capable of representing it regardless of the depth. The formal mathematical exposition of these results is provided below.

4.1
DNNs and the Embedding Principle

In this subsection, we briefly recapitulate the key elements of Embedding Principle in Refs.
(

;
Zhang et al., 2022
)

The cornerstone of the Embedding Principle’s proof lies in constructing a series of critical embeddings that map any given DNN to a wider one. Figure

Additionally,

Definition 11

(critical mapping)

\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\alpha_{i}+\frac{1}{p_{i}}\sum_{t=1}^{d}n_{j}\beta_{it}e_{t}\right)p_{i}^{n_{j}}=0
, which yields

\alpha_{1}+\frac{1}{p_{1}}\sum_{t=1}^{d}n_{j}\beta_{1t}e_{t}=-\sum_{i=2}^{m}\left(\alpha_{i}+\frac{1}{p_{i}}\sum_{t=1}^{d}n_{j}\beta_{it}e_{t}\right)\frac{p_{i}^{n_{j}}}{p_{1}^{n_{j}}}.

If
m=1
, by taking limits
j\to\infty
, we have
\alpha_{1}=\sum_{t=1}^{d}\beta_{1t}e_{t}=0
.

Otherwise, since
|p_{1}|>|p_{i}|
for any
2\leqslant i\leqslant m
, it follows that, by taking limits
j\to\infty
,

\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}\left(\alpha_{1}+\frac{1}{p_{1}}\sum_{t=1}^{d}n_{j}\beta_{1t}e_{t}\right)=\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}-\sum_{i=2}^{m}\left(\alpha_{i}+\frac{1}{p_{i}}\sum_{t=1}^{d}n_{j}\beta_{it}e_{t}\right)\frac{p_{i}^{n_{j}}}{p_{1}^{n_{j}}}=0.

Thus, we also have
\alpha_{1}=\sum_{t=1}^{d}\beta_{1t}e_{t}=0
. For
m>2
, we may rewrite Eq. (
7
) as

\alpha_{2}+\frac{1}{p_{2}}\sum_{t=1}^{d}n_{j}\beta_{2t}e_{t}=-\sum_{i=3}^{m}\left(\alpha_{i}+\frac{1}{p_{i}}\sum_{t=1}^{d}n_{j}\beta_{it}e_{t}\right)\frac{p_{i}^{n_{j}}}{p_{2}^{n_{j}}}

for each
j\geq 2
, and take limits as we do above to deduce that
\alpha_{2}+\frac{1}{p_{2}}\sum_{t=1}^{d}n_{j}\beta_{2t}e_{t}=0
. By repeating this procedure for at most
m
times, we conclude that
\alpha_{i}+\frac{1}{p_{i}}\sum_{t=1}^{d}n_{j}\beta_{it}e_{t}=0
for all
i\in[m]
. Then,
\alpha_{i}=\sum_{t=1}^{d}\beta_{it}e_{t}=0
for any
i\in[m]
. For each
i
,
\sum_{t=1}^{d}\beta_{it}e_{t}^{\prime}
is a linear function of
\bm{e}^{\prime}
on the open set
\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash E
which vanishes on a neighborhood of
\bm{e}
, we must have
\alpha_{i}=\beta_{it}=0
for any
i\in[m],t\in[d]
. Therefore,
\{\sigma(\bm{w}_{i}^{\text{T}}\bm{x}),\sigma^{\prime}(\bm{w}_{i}^{\text{T}}\bm{x})x_{1},...,\sigma^{\prime}(\bm{w}_{i}^{\text{T}}\bm{x})x_{d}\}_{i=1}^{m}
must be a linearly independent set.
∎

Corollary 7

(model rank estimate for two-layer NNs)
.

Let
\sigma=\tanh
. Given
d\in\mathbb{N}
, weights
\bm{w}_{1},...,\bm{w}_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
a_{1},...,a_{m}\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

\textnormal{dim}(\textnormal{span}\{\sigma(\bm{w}_{i}^{\text{T}}\bm{x}),a_{i}\sigma^{\prime}(\bm{w}_{i}^{\text{T}}\bm{x})x_{1},...,a_{i}\sigma^{\prime}(\bm{w}_{i}^{\text{T}}\bm{x})x_{d}\}_{i=1}^{m})=m_{\bm{w}}+m_{a}d,

where
m_{\bm{w}}=\frac{1}{2}|\{\bm{w}_{i},-\bm{w}_{i}|\bm{w}_{i}\neq\bm{0},i\in[m]\}|
indicating the number of independent neurons,
m_{a}=\frac{1}{2}|\{\bm{w}_{i},-\bm{w}_{i}|\bm{w}_{i}\neq\bm{0},a_{i}\neq 0,i\in[m]\}|+|\{\bm{w}_{i}|\bm{w}_{i}=\bm{0},a_{i}\neq 0,i\in[m]\}|
indicating the number of independent effective neurons. Here,
|\cdot|
is the cardinality of a set, i.e., number of different elements in a set.

Proof.

Note that
\sigma=\tanh
is analytic and
\sigma^{(2n+1)}(0)\neq 0
for all
n
. Because
\tanh
is an odd function, we have
\tanh(x)=-\tanh(-x)
and
\tanh(0)=0
. Therefore, given
\bm{w}_{i},\bm{w}_{j}\neq\bm{0}
with
\bm{w}_{i}=\pm\bm{w}_{j}
,
\textnormal{span}\{\sigma(\bm{w}_{i}^{\text{T}}x)\}=\textnormal{span}\{\sigma(\bm{w}_{j}^{\text{T}}x)\}
and
\textnormal{span}\{\sigma^{\prime}(\bm{w}_{i}^{\text{T}}x)x_{1},...,\sigma^{\prime}(\bm{w}_{i}^{\text{T}}x)x_{d}\}=\textnormal{span}\{\sigma^{\prime}(\bm{w}_{j}^{\text{T}}x)x_{1},...,\sigma^{\prime}(\bm{w}_{j}^{\text{T}}x)x_{d}\}
. Since there are
m_{\bm{w}}
different non-zero weights, by Proposition
2
we have

\dim\left(\textnormal{span}\{\sigma(\bm{w}_{i}^{\text{T}}x)\}_{i=1}^{m}\right)=m_{\bm{w}}.

Furthermore, note that

\textnormal{span}\{a_{i}\sigma^{\prime}(\bm{w}_{i}^{\text{T}}x)x_{1},...,a_{i}\sigma^{\prime}(\bm{w}_{i}^{\text{T}}x)x_{d}\}_{i=1}^{m}=\textnormal{span}\{\sigma^{\prime}(\bm{w}_{i}^{\text{T}}x)x_{1},...,\sigma^{\prime}(\bm{w}_{i}^{\text{T}}x)x_{d}:a_{i}\neq 0,i\in[m]\}.

Thus, by Proposition
2
,

\displaystyle\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\dim\left(\textnormal{span}\{\sigma^{\prime}(\bm{w}_{i}^{\text{T}}x)x_{1},...,\sigma^{\prime}(\bm{w}_{i}^{\text{T}}x)x_{d}:\bm{w}_{i}\neq\bm{0},a_{i}\neq 0,i\in[m]\}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}|\{\bm{w}_{i},-\bm{w}_{i}|\bm{w}_{i}\neq\bm{0},a_{i}\neq 0,i\in[m]\}|\cdot d.

Now suppose that
\bm{w}_{j}=\bm{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
for some
j\in[m]
. Since
\sigma^{\prime}(\bm{w}^{\text{T}}x)=\sigma^{\prime}(0)\neq 0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\textnormal{span}\{\sigma^{\prime}(\bm{w}_{j}^{\text{T}}x)x_{1},...,\sigma^{\prime}(\bm{w}_{j}^{\text{T}}x)x_{d}\}=\textnormal{span}\{x_{1},...,x_{d}\}

which consists only of linear functions. By the nonlinearity of
\tanh
, we conclude that

\dim\left(\textnormal{span}\{\sigma^{\prime}(\bm{w}_{i}^{\text{T}}x)x_{1},...,\sigma^{\prime}(\bm{w}_{i}^{\text{T}}x)x_{d}\}\right)=m_{a}d

and thus
\dim\left(\textnormal{span}\{\sigma(\bm{w}_{i}^{\text{T}}x),\sigma^{\prime}(\bm{w}_{i}^{\text{T}}x)x_{1},...,\sigma^{\prime}(\bm{w}_{i}^{\text{T}}x)x_{d}\}\right)=m_{\bm{w}}+m_{a}d
as desired.
∎

The above corollary directly gives rise to the following result.

Corollary 8
.

Let
\sigma=\tanh
. Given distinct weights
\bm{w}_{1},...,\bm{w}_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash\{\bm{0}\}
satisfying
\bm{w}_{k}\neq\pm\bm{w}_{j}
for
k\neq j
, and
a_{1},...,a_{m}\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}
, we have

\textnormal{dim}(\textnormal{span}\{\sigma(\bm{w}_{i}^{\text{T}}\bm{x}),a_{i}\sigma^{\prime}(\bm{w}_{i}^{\text{T}}\bm{x})x_{1},...,a_{i}\sigma^{\prime}(\bm{w}_{i}^{\text{T}}\bm{x})x_{d}\}_{i=1}^{m})=m(d+1).

Next we consider the estimate of the model rank for CNNs which are widely used in practice. Here we consider the case where the input has two-dimensional indices, which is the most general case for the image input. The following two propositions can be directly generalized to the model rank estimate of CNNs with an input of one index dimension.

Definition 13

(ineffective neurons/kernels)
.

For two-layer tanh NNs, we say a neuron/kernel is output-ineffective if its output weight array is zero but input weight array is nonzero. We say a neuron/kernel is input-ineffective if its input weight array is zero but output weight array is nonzero. We say a neuron/kernel is null if both input and out weight arrays are zero. All these neurons/kernels are ineffective. Neurons/kernels that are not ineffective are effective.

Remark 14
.

Estimating the model rank at parameter points with input-ineffective neurons is complicated for CNNs. Luckily, we notice that model rank of a target function
f^{*}\in\mathcal{F}
can be obtained by considering only
\bm{\theta}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}_{f^{*}}
with no input-ineffective neurons. This is because any
\bm{\theta}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}_{f^{*}}
with input-ineffective neurons associates to a
\bm{\theta}^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{M}_{f^{*}}
such that (i) input-ineffective neurons at
\bm{\theta}^{\prime}
are replaced by null neurons (ii)
\textnormal{rank}_{f_{\bm{\theta}}}(\bm{\theta}^{\prime\prime})\leqslant\textnormal{rank}_{f_{\bm{\theta}}}(\bm{\theta}^{\prime})
. Therefore, we only estimate the model rank for CNNs at the parameter points with no input-ineffective neurons in the following propositions.

Proposition 3

(model rank estimate for CNNs (with weight sharing))
.

Given
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
s\in[d]
. For any
l\in[m]

\bm{K}_{l}
be a
(s\times s)

7
Conclusion

Figure 4:
Schematic overview of our theoretical results and interconnections.

In this study, we have established a local linear recovery (LLR) guarantee for deep neural networks (DNNs), demonstrating that all functions expressible by narrower DNNs possess an LLR-guarantee at overparameterization. Fig.
4
presents a schematic overview of our theoretical results and their interconnections. Our work lays a solid groundwork for advancing the recovery theory of DNNs. Suggested by our results for two-layer NNs (Section
5
), the linear independence of neurons as in Proposition
2
plays a key role in the exact calculation of model rank. For future research, three significant challenges remain open: (i) determining how to recover a target function with a sample size that approaches the optimistic estimate; (ii) investigating whether DNNs can offer stronger forms of recovery guarantees at overparameterization.
(iii) exploring neuron independence in deeper networks to exactly estimate their model ranks.

More recently, progress has been made on these fronts.
Zhang and Xu (2024)
presents empirical evidence suggesting that dropout techniques may enable the recovery of a target function with a sample size that aligns with its optimistic one. Furthermore,
Zhang et al. (2023a)
introduces a stronger form of recovery guarantee, namely the local recovery guarantee, for two-layer NNs at overparameterization. As we progress, we anticipate a significant deepening of our theoretical grasp of target recovery in DNNs.
