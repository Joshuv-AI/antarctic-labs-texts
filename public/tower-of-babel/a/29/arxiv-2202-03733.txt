Phase retrieval of entire functions and its implications for Gabor phase retrieval
Matthias Wellershoff

Abstract
We characterise all pairs of finite order entire functions whose magnitudes agree on two arbitrary lines in the complex plane by means of the Hadamard factorisation theorem. Building on this, we also characterise all pairs of second order entire functions whose magnitudes agree on infinitely many equidistant parallel lines. Furthermore, we show that the magnitude of an entire function on three parallel lines, whose distances are rationally independent, uniquely determines the function up to global phase, and that there exists a first order entire function whose magnitude on the lines ℝ + i n ​ ℤ \mathbb{R}+\tfrac{\mathrm{i}}{n}\mathbb{Z} does not uniquely determine it up to global phase, for all positive integers n n . Our results have direct implications for Gabor phase retrieval. Keywords Phase retrieval, finite order entire functions, Hadamard factorisation theorem, Gabor transform, time-frequency analysis Mathematics Subject Classification (2020) 30D20, 94A12

Introduction

Let us consider the recovery of entire functions
f
from partial knowledge of their magnitudes

\lvert f(z)\rvert,\qquad z\in\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{C}.

The above problem is called
phase retrieval
of entire functions. Clearly,
f
cannot be recovered completely since the entire function
\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\alpha}f
, with
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, has the same magnitudes. To capture this, we will consider the equivalence relation
\sim
defined by

f\sim g:\iff\exists\alpha\in\mathbb{R}.f=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\alpha}g.

(1)

Recovering an equivalence class under
\sim
is referred to as recovery
up to global phase
.

The interest in the phase retrieval problem of entire functions arises from its direct connection to various other phase retrieval problems encountered in applications. A notable example is
Gabor phase retrieval
, where the objective is to reconstruct a square-integrable function (up to global phase) from the magnitude of its Gabor transform. This particular problem finds applications in audio processing: in particular, the Griffin–Lim algorithm
[
8
]
, designed for Gabor phase retrieval, is integrated into PyTorch’s torchaudio library and TensorFlow’s I/O module.

The main result of this paper is the
characterisation all finite order entire functions which agree on two arbitrary lines
in the complex plane (cf. Theorems
6
,
8
, and
10
), which is proven using the well-known Hadamard factorisation theorem. This characterisation includes a theorem proven in
[
15
]
addressing the unique recovery (up to global phase) of finite order entire functions from magnitude information on two intersecting lines.

Additionally, we consider entire functions whose magnitudes agree on three or more parallel lines in the complex plane. This leads us to a new uniqueness result on lines whose distances are rationally independent.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}\subset\mathbb{C}
be three parallel lines. Let
a>0
denote the distance between
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
and let
b>0
denote the distance between
\ell_{2}
and
\ell_{3}
. Assume that
\tfrac{b}{a}
is irrational and let
f
as well as
g
be entire functions. Then,
\lvert f\rvert=\lvert g\rvert
holds on
\ell_{1}\cup\ell_{2}\cup\ell_{3}
if and only if
f\sim g
.

Moreover, our characterisation allows for the construction of entire functions which do not agree up to global phase but whose magnitudes agree on infinitely many parallel lines; such as,

f(z)=\cosh z+\mathrm{i}\sinh z,\qquad g(z)=\cosh z-\mathrm{i}\sinh z.

(2)

These functions have matching magnitudes on
\mathbb{R}+\tfrac{\pi\mathrm{i}}{2}\mathbb{Z}
and share no common zeroes. This construction was used to generate counterexamples to uniqueness for sampled Gabor phase retrieval in
[
5
]
(which was later generalised in
[
3
,
9
]
).

Finally, our characterisation leads to the construction of an entire function
f_{0}
for which there exist parallel lines that are arbitrarily close together on which magnitude information of
f_{0}
does not suffice for a recovery up to global phase.

Theorem 2
.

There exists a sequence of entire functions
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
of order one such that
f_{0}\not\sim f_{n}
and
\lvert f_{0}\rvert=\lvert f_{n}\rvert
on
\mathbb{R}+\tfrac{\mathrm{i}}{n}\mathbb{Z}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Notation, preliminaries and overview of existing literature

2.1
Notation

The space of entire functions is denoted by
\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C})
. Complex polynomials of degree at most
d\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
are denoted by
\mathbb{C}_{d}[z]
, and the space of all complex polynomials is
\mathbb{C}[z]
. The equivalence relation
\sim
defined in (
1
) is primarily associated with functions in
\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C})
in this paper, though occasionally also used for functions in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.

An entire function
f
is of
finite order
if it satisfies
f(z)=\mathcal{O}(\exp(\lvert z\rvert^{a}))
as
z\to\infty
for some positive
a>0
. The infimum of all such
a
is called the
order

\rho\geq 0
of the function
f
. Similarly, an entire function
f
of order
\rho>0
is of
type

\sigma\geq 0
if
\sigma
is the infimum of all positive
k>0
such that
f(z)=\mathcal{O}(\exp(k\lvert z\rvert^{\rho}))
as
z\to\infty
.

The zeroes of an entire function
f
are described using the multiset notation
M_{f}:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{N}_{0}
,

M_{f}(z):=\begin{cases}m&\mbox{if }z\mbox{ is a zero of }f\mbox{ of multiplicity }m,\\
0&\mbox{else}.\end{cases}

The expressions

E(z;0)=1-z,\qquad E(z;p)=(1-z)\exp\Bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{p}z^{j}/j\Bigg),

for
p\in\mathbb{N}
, are called
primary factors
. If
f
is a finite order entire function, then there exists an integer
p\in\mathbb{N}
such that (see e.g.
[
21
, Subsection 8.23 on p. 250]
)

\sum_{a\in\supp(M_{f})\setminus\{0\}}\frac{M_{f}(a)}{\lvert a\rvert^{p+1}}<\infty.

If
p
is the smallest such integer, we call

P(z):=z^{M_{f}(0)}\prod_{a\in\supp(M_{f})\setminus\{0\}}E\Big(\frac{z}{a};p\Big)^{M_{f}(a)}

the
canonical product
formed with the zeroes of
f
, and we call
p
its
genus
. Here and throughout the rest of this paper,
\supp
denotes the support of a function. Finally, we denote the complex phase or argument by
\arg:\mathbb{C}\to(-\pi,\pi]
.

2.2
Preliminaries

It is not difficult to see (and widely known) that phase retrieval of entire functions enjoys uniqueness when magnitude information on an open set is available.

Lemma 3
.

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{C}
be an open set, and let
f,g\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C})
be entire functions. Then,
\lvert f\rvert=\lvert g\rvert
on
\Omega
if and only if
f\sim g
.

We include a very short proof for the convenience of the reader.

Proof of Lemma
3
.

The lemma follows from the identity theorem if
g=0
. So, let us assume that
g\neq 0
and
\lvert f\rvert=\lvert g\rvert
on
\Omega
. Let
Z(g)\subset\mathbb{C}
denote the set of zeroes of
g
. By assumption,
\lvert f/g\rvert=1
on
\Omega\setminus Z(g)
such that the open mapping theorem implies that
f/g
is constant on
\Omega\setminus Z(g)
. Hence,
f/g=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\alpha}
on
\Omega\setminus Z(g)
for some
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
. Now, the identity theorem implies that
f\sim g
.
∎

Therefore, phase retrieval of entire functions is unique when magnitude information on a set
containing an open set
is available. It remains to consider sets which do
not
contain open sets. We start by considering individual straight lines.

Let
f,g\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C})
be two non-zero entire functions and split the multiset of zeroes of
f
into two smaller multisets: the common zeroes of
f
and
g
, and the zeroes of
f
that are no zeroes of
g
. The former are described by the multiset
M_{\text{c}}:=\min\{M_{f},M_{g}\}
while the latter are described by the multiset
M_{\text{d}}:=\min\{M_{f}-M_{g},0\}
.

Using this notation, we present the following theorem from
[
7
]
which will be applied repeatedly in the rest of this paper. An earlier reference,
[
22
]
, establishes the result for Fourier transforms of functions in
L^{2}([-1/2,1/2])
. The proof notably also covers the more general case presented below. Additionally, in
[
2
]
, the theorem is proven using Blaschke-type products instead of canonical products.

Theorem 4
.

Let
f,g\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C})\setminus\{0\}
be of finite order. Then,
\left\lvert f\right\rvert=\left\lvert g\right\rvert
on
\mathbb{R}
if and only if
f
and
g
factor as

\displaystyle f(z)=\mathrm{e}^{Q_{f}(z)}\cdot z^{m}\prod_{a\in\operatorname{supp}(M_{\text{c}})\setminus\{0\}}E\left(\frac{z}{a};p\right)^{M_{\text{c}}(a)}\prod_{a\in\operatorname{supp}(M_{\text{d}})}E\left(\frac{z}{a};p\right)^{M_{\text{d}}(a)},

\displaystyle g(z)=\mathrm{e}^{Q_{g}(z)}\cdot z^{m}\prod_{a\in\operatorname{supp}(M_{\text{c}})\setminus\{0\}}E\left(\frac{z}{a};p\right)^{M_{\text{c}}(a)}\prod_{a\in\operatorname{supp}(M_{\text{d}})}E\left(\frac{z}{\overline{a}};p\right)^{M_{\text{d}}(a)}.

Here,
p\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
is the genus of the canonical product formed with the zeroes of
f
,
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
is the multiplicity of the zero at the origin of
f
, and
Q_{f},Q_{g}\in\mathbb{C}[z]
are polynomials of the same order which have coefficients whose real parts agree.

The reader is referred to
[
7
,
22
]
for the proof of the theorem above, which is based on the well-known Hadamard factorisation theorem (cf. e.g.
[
21
, Subsection 8.24 on p. 250]
). For the remainder of this paper, the following core idea is especially important: if
\left\lvert f\right\rvert=\left\lvert g\right\rvert
on
\mathbb{R}
, then

f(z)\overline{f(\overline{z})}=g(z)\overline{g(\overline{z})},\qquad z\in\mathbb{C},

by the identity theorem of complex analysis. Therefore, the multisets
M_{f}
and
M_{g}
satisfy

M_{f}(z)+M_{f}(\overline{z})=M_{g}(z)+M_{g}(\overline{z}),\qquad z\in\mathbb{C}.

(3)

Remark 5
.

Let us make three further remarks:

1.

Equation (
3
) immediately shows that
\left\lvert M_{f}-M_{g}\right\rvert
, the symmetric difference of the multisets of the zeroes of
f
and
g
, is invariant under the action of complex conjugation.

2.

The theorem generalises to entire functions
f
and
g
whose magnitudes agree on a general line
\ell
in
\mathbb{C}
by composing
f
and
g
with a rotation and a translation. In this case, if
\sigma_{\ell}:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
denotes the reflection of
\mathbb{C}
along
\ell
, then

M_{f}(z)+M_{f}(\sigma_{\ell}(z))=M_{g}(z)+M_{g}(\sigma_{\ell}(z)),\qquad z\in\mathbb{C},

(4)

and
\left\lvert M_{f}-M_{g}\right\rvert
is invariant under the action of
\sigma_{\ell}
.

3.

In the context of Theorem
4
, we define the function

P_{\mathrm{c}}(z):=z^{m}\prod_{a\in\operatorname{supp}(M_{\text{c}})\setminus\{0\}}E\left(\frac{z}{a};p\right)^{M_{\text{c}}(a)}

and call it the
canonical product formed with the common zeroes of
f
and
g
.

2.3
Background and overview of existing literature

The results presented in this paper were established to answer open questions in the Gabor phase retrieval community. Following the findings in
[
6
]
, which demonstrated that real-valued bandlimited functions are uniquely determined by samples of their Gabor transform magnitude, there was interest in exploring whether the same holds for general complex-valued (bandlimited) functions. While this question was later resolved in
[
10
]
and
[
24
]
using different techniques, at the time, there was hope that the connection between the Gabor and Bargmann transforms could be used in this pursuit. Since the range of the Bargmann transform is the Fock space of second order entire functions
[
11
, Section 3.4 on pp. 53–58]
, one is naturally led to questions about phase retrieval of entire functions.

The approach adopted here draws inspiration from
[
19
]
, where one of the key elements is a result establishing the uniqueness of recovering certain functions holomorphic on the upper half-plane from magnitude measurements on two parallel lines. This result is then applied to show that measurements of the Cauchy wavelet transform on two scales are sufficient for the recovery of the analytic representations
1
1

1

The
analytic representation

f_{+}
of
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
is defined by
\widehat{f}_{+}:=2\chi_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\cdot\widehat{f}
, where
\widehat{\cdot}
denotes the Fourier transform and
\chi_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}
is the indicator function of the positive real numbers
\mathbb{R}_{+}
.
of square-integrable functions (up to global phase). Consequently, the analytic representations of bandlimited functions can be reconstructed from samples of the Cauchy wavelet transform
[
4
]
. These ideas motivated the related problem addressed in this paper: the recovery of second order entire functions from their magnitudes on two parallel lines.

Finally, we note that techniques analogous to those employed in this paper were used in
[
12
]
to address the problem of phase retrieval in shift-invariant spaces with Gaussian generator. Similarly, in
[
23
]
, comparable methods were applied to demonstrate the unique recovery (up to global phase) of bandlimited functions from Gabor transform magnitudes sampled at twice the Nyquist rate in two frequency bins.

3
Characterisation of finite order entire functions with common magnitudes on two lines

Theorem
4
states that the magnitude of an entire function
f
on a single line does not uniquely determine
f
(up to global phase). Two ambiguities arise: first, magnitude information on the real line
\mathbb{R}
does not suffice to distinguish between functions of the form
\exp(iQ)
, where
Q\in\mathbb{R}[z]
is a polynomial with real coefficients; secondly, and potentially more importantly, magnitude information on any line is insufficient to uniquely determine zeroes of entire functions.

In this section, we investigate whether magnitude information on
two
lines uniquely determines entire functions (up to global phase). The insights presented in the following can be generalised to cases in which we have magnitude information on an arbitrary number of lines under certain conditions.

3.1
Intersecting lines

Let us consider two arbitrary lines in the complex plane. There are two possible scenarios: either the lines intersect at some point, or they are parallel. In this subsection, we focus on intersecting lines, and assume, without loss of generality
2
2

2

Otherwise, we can translate and rotate the lines.
, that they meet at the origin, one of the lines is
\mathbb{R}
and the other intersects
\mathbb{R}
at an angle
\theta\in(0,\tfrac{\pi}{2}]
. We further categorise our analysis into two cases: first, when
\theta\in(0,\tfrac{\pi}{2}]\setminus\pi\mathbb{Q}
(we call such angles
irrational
), and secondly, when
\theta\in(0,\tfrac{\pi}{2}]\cap\pi\mathbb{Q}
(we call such angles
rational
).

Let us begin by considering a general angle
\theta\in(0,\tfrac{\pi}{2}]
, however. For two non-zero entire functions
f,g\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C})
with
\lvert f\rvert=\lvert g\rvert
on
\mathbb{R}\cup\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}\mathbb{R}\subset\mathbb{C}
, the function
\lvert M_{f}-M_{g}\rvert
is invariant under both the reflection
\sigma_{\theta}
along
\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}\mathbb{R}
and complex conjugation (which we may denote by
\sigma_{0}
since it coincides with reflection along
\mathbb{R}
) according to Remark
5
. Consequently,
\lvert M_{f}-M_{g}\rvert
is invariant under the action of the group
G
generated by
\sigma_{0}
and
\sigma_{\theta}
. Notably, this group contains the rotation
\rho_{2\theta}=\sigma_{0}\sigma_{\theta}
by angle
2\theta
.

Irrational angles

If
\theta\in(0,\tfrac{\pi}{2}]\setminus\pi\mathbb{Q}
is an irrational angle, then the invariance of the function
\lvert M_{f}-M_{g}\rvert
under the rotation
\rho_{2\theta}
allows us to conclude that
M_{f}=M_{g}
. Indeed, remember that equation (
3
) implies that
M_{f}(0)=M_{g}(0)
, and suppose by contradiction that there exists a number
a\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
such that
\lvert M_{f}(a)-M_{g}(a)\rvert>0
. Then,
\lvert M_{f}-M_{g}\rvert
is non-zero on the countably infinite set
\rho_{2\theta}^{\mathbb{Z}}a
contained in the circle of radius
\lvert a\rvert
around the origin. Therefore, either
f
or
g
must have a non-isolated set of zeroes and cannot be non-zero.

Hence, magnitude information on two lines intersecting at an irrational angle uniquely determines the zeroes of an entire function. One can now show that this enables us to completely recover the entire function up to global phase (which was first proven in
[
15
]
).

Theorem 6

(
[
15
, Theorem 3.3 on p. 419]

.

Let
\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\subset\mathbb{C}
be two lines intersecting at an irrational angle
\theta\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\pi\mathbb{Q}
, and let
f,g\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C})
be two entire functions of finite order. Then,
\lvert f\rvert=\lvert g\rvert
on
\ell_{1}\cup\ell_{2}
if and only if
f\sim g
.

The proof of the above theorem can be found in
[
15
]
which also contains the insight that it actually suffices to assume that
\lvert f\rvert=\lvert g\rvert
on
u_{1}\cup u_{2}
, where
u_{1}\subseteq\ell_{1}
and
u_{2}\subseteq\ell_{2}
are sets of uniqueness for finite order entire functions.

Remark 7
.

Theorem
6
remains true for general entire functions. Indeed, the argument that shows that the zeroes of
f
and

agree remains true. Then, Weierstrass factorisation implies that
f=\mathrm{e}^{\alpha}P
and
g=\mathrm{e}^{\beta}P
, where

is a product of primary factors and
\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C})
. Since
\lvert f\rvert=\lvert g\rvert
on
\ell_{1}\cup\ell_{2}
, we have that

and

\ell_{1}\cup\ell_{2}

\rho_{2\theta}^{\mathbb{Z}}\ell_{1}

\rho_{2\theta}^{\mathbb{Z}}\ell_{1}\subset\mathbb{C}\cong\mathbb{R}^{2}
is dense, it follows that

and thus that
\alpha-\beta=\mathrm{i}c
for some

where we used

4.1
Uniqueness for three parallel lines

Consider three distinct parallel lines
\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}\subset\mathbb{C}
on which the magnitudes of two non-zero entire functions
f
and
g
agree. We denote the distance between
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
by

and the distance between
\ell_{2}
and
\ell_{3}
by

. Assume, without loss of generality, that the three lines are parallel to the real numbers
\mathbb{R}
. Then, the zeroes of

that are

If

is irrational, then

and

have the same zeroes: indeed, if we assume that there exists a

f=0
: a contradiction.

We can now conclude that
f
and
g
agree up to global phase.

Theorem 12
.

Let
\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}\subset\mathbb{C}
be three distinct parallel lines. Let
a>0
denote the distance between
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
and let
b>0
denote the distance between
\ell_{2}
and
\ell_{3}

\tfrac{b}{a}
is irrational and let

be of finite order. Then,

if and only if

.

Proof.

Suppose that
\lvert f\rvert=\lvert g\rvert
on
\ell_{1}\cup\ell_{2}\cup\ell_{3}
. As explained before the statement of the theorem, we have that
M_{\mathrm{d}}=0
. Let us assume, without loss of generality, that one of the lines coincides with the real numbers
\mathbb{R}
. Then, Theorem
4
shows that
f=\mathrm{e}^{Q_{f}}P_{\mathrm{c}}
and
g=\mathrm{e}^{Q_{g}}P_{\mathrm{c}}
, with polynomials
Q_{f},Q_{g}\in\mathbb{C}[z]
whose coefficients have the same real parts.

Denote the imaginary parts of those coefficients by
(\lambda_{k})_{k=0}^{d},(\mu_{k})_{k=0}^{d}\in\mathbb{R}
and let
y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
be such that
\mathbb{R}+\mathrm{i}y_{0}
is one of the parallel lines. Then,
\lvert f\rvert=\lvert g\rvert
on
\mathbb{R}+\mathrm{i}y_{0}
implies
\operatorname{Re}Q_{f}=\operatorname{Re}Q_{g}
on
\mathbb{R}+\mathrm{i}y_{0}
. Expressed in terms of the imaginary parts of the coefficients, this is

\sum_{k=0}^{d}(\lambda_{k}-\mu_{k})\operatorname{Im}[(x+\mathrm{i}y_{0})^{k}]=0,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

We expand the power
(x+\mathrm{i}y_{0})^{k}
and obtain

\sum_{\ell=0}^{d}x^{\ell}\sum_{k=\ell}^{d}\binom{k}{\ell}\sigma_{k-\ell}y_{0}^{k-\ell}(\lambda_{k}-\mu_{k})=0,

where

\sigma_{k}:=\operatorname{Im}[\mathrm{i}^{k}]=\begin{cases}(-1)^{\ell}&\text{if }k=2\ell+1,\\
0&\text{else}.\end{cases}

Comparing coefficients yields

\sum_{k=\ell}^{d}\binom{k}{\ell}\sigma_{k-\ell}y_{0}^{k-\ell}(\lambda_{k}-\mu_{k})=0,\qquad\ell=0,\dots,d.

(5)

We finally show
\lambda_{k}=\mu_{k}
for all

inductively, which proves the theorem. As base case, we show that
\lambda_{d}=\mu_{d}
, which follows from equation (
5

\ell=d-1

for all

) and obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{k=j-1}^{d}\binom{k}{j-1}\sigma_{k-j+1}y_{0}^{k-j+1}(\lambda_{k}-\mu_{k})

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle jy_{0}(\lambda_{j}-\mu_{j})+\sum_{k=j+2}^{d}\binom{k}{j-1}\sigma_{k-j+1}y_{0}^{k-j+1}(\lambda_{k}-\mu_{k})

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle jy_{0}(\lambda_{j}-\mu_{j})=0,

such that

\operatorname{Im}a\in(-\pi/2,\pi/2]\end{subarray}}\left[\frac{\sinh(\overline{a}-z)}{\sinh\overline{a}}\mathrm{e}^{R_{\bar{a}}(z)}\right]^{M_{\text{d}}(a)}.

Q_{f}
and
Q_{g}
: let
x\in\mathbb{R}
and compute

\frac{\lvert f(x-\frac{\pi\mathrm{i}}{2})\rvert}{\lvert g(x-\frac{\pi\mathrm{i}}{2})\rvert}=\mathrm{e}^{\operatorname{Re}[Q_{f}(x-\frac{\pi\mathrm{i}}{2})-Q_{g}(x-\frac{\pi\mathrm{i}}{2})]}\\
\cdot\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in\operatorname{supp}(M_{\text{d}})\\
\operatorname{Im}a\in(-\pi/2,\pi/2]\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{\lvert\sinh\overline{a}\rvert}{\lvert\sinh a\rvert}\frac{\lvert\sinh(a-z+\frac{\pi\mathrm{i}}{2})\rvert}{\lvert\sinh(\overline{a}-z+\frac{\pi\mathrm{i}}{2})\rvert}\right)^{M_{\mathrm{d}}(a)}\\
\cdot\mathrm{e}^{M_{\mathrm{d}}(a)\operatorname{Re}[R_{a}(x-\frac{\pi\mathrm{i}}{2})-R_{\bar{a}}(x-\frac{\pi\mathrm{i}}{2})]}.

Since

\sinh\overline{a}=\overline{\sinh a},\qquad\sinh(\overline{a}-x+\tfrac{\pi\mathrm{i}}{2})=-\overline{\sinh(a-x+\tfrac{\pi\mathrm{i}}{2})},

and
\lvert f\rvert=\lvert g\rvert
on
\mathbb{R}-\tfrac{\pi\mathrm{i}}{2}
, the above simplifies to

\operatorname{Re}\left[Q_{f}\left(x-\frac{\pi\mathrm{i}}{2}\right)-Q_{g}\left(x-\frac{\pi\mathrm{i}}{2}\right)\right]\\
=-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in\operatorname{supp}(M_{\text{d}})\\
\operatorname{Im}a\in(-\pi/2,\pi/2]\end{subarray}}M_{\mathrm{d}}(a)\operatorname{Re}\left[R_{a}\left(x-\frac{\pi\mathrm{i}}{2}\right)-R_{\bar{a}}\left(x-\frac{\pi\mathrm{i}}{2}\right)\right].

We may now use the following simple claim

Claim.

Let
p,q\in\mathbb{C}_{2}[z]
be two polynomials whose coefficients have the same real parts. Denote the imaginary parts of their coefficients by
(a_{j})_{j=0}^{2},(b_{j})_{j=0}^{2}\in\mathbb{R}
. Then,

\operatorname{Re}[p(x-\mathrm{i}y)-q(x-\mathrm{i}y)]=2(a_{2}-b_{2})xy-(a_{1}-b_{2})y,\qquad x,y\in\mathbb{R}.

∎

It follows that

\pi(\lambda_{2}-\mu_{2})x-\frac{\pi}{2}(\lambda_{1}-\mu_{2})\\
=-\pi\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in\operatorname{supp}(M_{\text{d}})\\
\operatorname{Im}a\in(-\pi/2,\pi/2]\end{subarray}}M_{\mathrm{d}}(a)(x\operatorname{Im}\csch^{2}a-\operatorname{Im}\coth a)

and the factorisation is complete. The reverse implication is shown in a similar way.
∎

4.3
Construction of universal counterexamples

Given a set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}
of infinitely many equidistant parallel lines, we have constructed entire functions
f,g\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C})
of order one which depend on
\Omega
, do
not
agree up to global phase, and whose magnitudes agree on
\Omega
. We call such functions counterexamples to uniqueness for phase retrieval of entire functions; or, simply
counterexamples
.

This raises the question whether
f
can be constructed independently on
\Omega
as well; i.e., whether we can form an entire function
f\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C})
such that, for all sets
\Omega
of infinitely many equidistant parallel lines in some family
\mathcal{F}
, we can find
g_{\Omega}\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C})
such that
f\not\sim g_{\Omega}
while
\lvert f\rvert=\lvert g_{\Omega}\rvert
on
\Omega
. We call the function
f

universal counterexample
for
\mathcal{F}
if it exists.

For the family
\mathcal{F}=\{\mathbb{R}+\mathrm{i}a\mathbb{Z}\,|\,a>0\}
,
no
universal counterexamples of finite order exist (if we require
g
to be of finite order as well).

Lemma 17
.

For all
f\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C})
of finite order, there exists a positive number
a>0
, such that, for all
g\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C})
of finite order,
\lvert f\rvert=\lvert g\rvert
on
\mathbb{R}+\mathrm{i}a\mathbb{Z}
implies
f\sim g
.

Proof.

Suppose by contradiction that the lemma is untrue at
f\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C})
. Fix
a>0
and consider
g\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C})
of finite order such that
f\not\sim g
while
\lvert f\rvert=\lvert g\rvert
on
\mathbb{R}+\mathrm{i}a\mathbb{Z}
. Then, the multiset
M_{\mathrm{d}}
, describing the zeroes of
f
that are
no
zeroes of
g
, is invariant under the translation
\tau_{2a}
. At the same time,
M_{\mathrm{d}}
is
not
identically zero. Indeed, if it were, then Hadamard factorisation of
f
and
g
would yield
f=\mathrm{e}^{Q_{f}}P
and
g=\mathrm{e}^{Q_{g}}P
, where
Q_{f},Q_{g}\in\mathbb{C}[z]
and
P
is the canonical product formed with the zeroes of
f
. It then follows that
Q_{f}=Q_{g}+\mathrm{i}c
for some
c\in\mathbb{R}
as in the proof of Theorem
12
, which implies that
f\sim g
: a contradiction.

Hence, we can find
z_{a}\in\mathbb{C}
such that
M_{\mathrm{d}}(\tau_{2a}^{k}z_{a})>0
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. It follows that
\tau_{2a}^{\mathbb{Z}}z_{a}
is a subset of the zeroes of
f
. Since
a>0
is arbitrary, it follows that
f
has uncountably many zeroes which implies that
f=0
and thus
g=0
. We have reached a contradiction again and the lemma is proven.
∎

Remark 18
.

The proof above does not use that
\mathcal{F}=\{\mathbb{R}+\mathrm{i}a\mathbb{Z}\,|\,a>0\}
contains sets of parallel lines that are arbitrarily close. Instead only the uncountability of
\mathbb{R}_{+}
is utilised. It follows that it is also true that, for all uncountable
A\subset\mathbb{R}_{+}
and all
f\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C})
of finite order, there exists an
a\in A
, such that, for all
g\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C})
of finite order,
\lvert f\rvert=\lvert g\rvert
on
\mathbb{R}+\mathrm{i}a\mathbb{Z}
implies
f\sim g
.

This raises the question whether the same remains true if we replace
A
by a countable set containing arbitrarily small numbers such as
\mathbb{N}^{-1}
, for instance. We answer this question by considering a direct construction.

Theorem 19
.

Let
f:\mathbb{N}_{0}\to(0,\infty)
be such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}f(n)=\infty
and define
a_{n}:=\exp(\pi nf(n))
for
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. The functions

g_{n}(z):=\frac{a_{n}-\mathrm{i}\mathrm{e}^{\pi nz}}{a_{n}-\mathrm{i}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathbb{N}\\
k\neq n\end{subarray}}\frac{a_{k}+\mathrm{i}\mathrm{e}^{\pi kz}}{a_{k}+\mathrm{i}},\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

are well-defined entire functions and satisfy
\lvert g_{0}\rvert=\lvert g_{n}\rvert
on
\mathbb{R}+\tfrac{\mathrm{i}}{n}\mathbb{Z}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
. If
f
is injective, then
g_{0}\not\sim g_{n}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and, if
f=\exp
, then
g_{n}
is of order one for
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

Proof.

Let
R>0
and let
K\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
k\geq K
implies
f(k)\geq R+\pi^{-1}
. Then,

\sum_{k\geq K}\left\lvert\frac{a_{k}+\mathrm{i}\mathrm{e}^{\pi kz}}{a_{k}+\mathrm{i}}-1\right\rvert=\sum_{k\geq K}\frac{\lvert\mathrm{e}^{\pi kz}-1\rvert}{\lvert a_{k}+\mathrm{i}\rvert}\leq\sum_{k\geq K}\mathrm{e}^{\pi k(\lvert z\rvert-f(k))}\leq\sum_{k\geq K}\mathrm{e}^{-k}<\infty,

for all
\lvert z\rvert\leq R
, such that the infinite product converges locally uniformly to an entire function.

Next, let
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
m\in\mathbb{Z}
, and
x\in\mathbb{R}
. We can directly compute that
g_{0}(f(n)+\tfrac{\mathrm{i}}{2n})=0
and

\frac{\lvert g_{0}(x+\frac{m\mathrm{i}}{n})\rvert}{\lvert g_{n}(x+\frac{m\mathrm{i}}{n})\rvert}=\frac{\left\lvert\frac{a_{n}+\mathrm{i}\mathrm{e}^{\pi n(x+m/n\mathrm{i})}}{a_{n}+\mathrm{i}}\right\rvert}{\left\lvert\frac{a_{n}-\mathrm{i}\mathrm{e}^{\pi n(x+m/n\mathrm{i})}}{a_{n}-\mathrm{i}}\right\rvert}=\frac{\left\lvert a_{n}+\mathrm{i}(-1)^{m}\mathrm{e}^{\pi nx}\right\rvert}{\left\lvert a_{n}-\mathrm{i}(-1)^{m}\mathrm{e}^{\pi nx}\right\rvert}=1.

If we assume that
g_{n}(f(n)+\tfrac{\mathrm{i}}{2n})=0
, then there exists
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
k\neq n
, such that

a_{k}+\mathrm{i}\mathrm{e}^{\pi k(f(n)+\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2n})}=0\iff f(k)=f(n)\mbox{ and }\frac{k+n}{4n}\in\mathbb{Z}.

If
f
is injective, the above is impossible and we can conclude that
g_{0}\not\sim g_{n}
.

Finally suppose that
f=\exp
. We show that
g_{0}
is of order one by splitting the product over
k\in\mathbb{N}
into a finite and an infinite product:

g_{0}(z)=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathbb{N}\\
k\leq\log(\lvert z\rvert+\pi^{-1})\end{subarray}}\frac{a_{k}+\mathrm{i}\mathrm{e}^{\pi kz}}{a_{k}+\mathrm{i}}\cdot\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathbb{N}\\
k>\log(\lvert z\rvert+\pi^{-1})\end{subarray}}\frac{a_{k}+\mathrm{i}\mathrm{e}^{\pi kz}}{a_{k}+\mathrm{i}}.

For the finite product, we note that

\left\lvert\frac{a_{k}+\mathrm{i}\mathrm{e}^{\pi kz}}{a_{k}+\mathrm{i}}\right\rvert\leq a_{k}+\mathrm{e}^{\pi k\lvert z\rvert}\leq(\lvert z\rvert+\pi^{-1}+1)\exp(\pi\lvert z\rvert\log(\lvert z\rvert+\pi^{-1})).

For the infinite product, we rewrite

\frac{a_{k}+\mathrm{i}\mathrm{e}^{\pi kz}}{a_{k}+\mathrm{i}}=1-\frac{\mathrm{i}(1-\mathrm{e}^{\pi kz})}{a_{k}+\mathrm{i}}=:1-\phi_{k}(z).

Note that
\lvert\phi_{k}(z)\rvert\leq\mathrm{e}^{\pi k(\lvert z\rvert-\exp(k))}<\mathrm{e}^{-k}<1
such that an application of the Taylor series for
\log(1-z)
yields

\log\Bigg\lvert\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathbb{N}\\
k>\log\lvert z\rvert\end{subarray}}\frac{a_{k}+\mathrm{i}\mathrm{e}^{\pi kz}}{a_{k}+\mathrm{i}}\Bigg\rvert=-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathbb{N}\\
k>\log\lvert z\rvert\end{subarray}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{\operatorname{Re}[\phi_{k}(z)^{n}]}{n}\leq\sum_{k,n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathrm{e}^{-nk}=:c<\infty.

It follows that

\lvert g_{0}(z)\rvert\leq(\lvert z\rvert+\pi^{-1}+1)^{\log(\lvert z\rvert+\pi^{-1})}\exp(\pi\lvert z\rvert\log^{2}(\lvert z\rvert+\pi^{-1}))\cdot\mathrm{e}^{c}

such that
g_{0}
is of first order. Therefore, the functions
(g_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are of first order as well.
∎

Remark 20
.

The function
g_{0}
was initially constructed by choosing the zeroes
z_{n,k}:=\mathrm{e}^{n}+\tfrac{\mathrm{i}}{2n}+\tfrac{2\mathrm{i}k}{n}

4.4
A remark on Gabor transform phase retrieval

Introducing the Gabor and Bargmann transform

Gabor transform phase retrieval and phase retrieval of entire functions are related by the connection of the Gabor and Bargmann transforms. Specifically, the
Gabor transform
of a square-integrable function
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
is

\mathcal{G}f(x,\omega):=2^{1/4}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(t)\mathrm{e}^{-\pi(t-x)^{2}}\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}t\omega}\,\mathrm{d}t,\qquad(x,\omega)\in\mathbb{R}^{2},

while the
Bargmann transform
is

\mathcal{B}f(z):=2^{1/4}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(t)\mathrm{e}^{2\pi tz-\pi t^{2}-\frac{\pi}{2}z^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}t,\qquad z\in\mathbb{C}.

The Bargmann transform is a unitary map from
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
onto the Fock space
\mathcal{F}^{2}(\mathbb{C})
of entire functions which are square-integrable with respect to the Gaussian probability measure
[
11
, Theorem 3.4.3 on p. 56]
, given by

\gamma(A):=\int_{A}\mathrm{e}^{-\pi\lvert z\rvert^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}z,\qquad A\subset\mathbb{C}.

Interestingly, the Fock space is a reproducing kernel Hilbert space with the property
[
11
, Theorem 3.4.2 on p. 54]

\left\lvert F(z)\right\rvert\leq\left\lVert F\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{C},\gamma)}\mathrm{e}^{\pi\left\lvert z\right\rvert^{2}/2},\qquad z\in\mathbb{C},

from which we can immediately conclude that
F\in\mathcal{F}^{2}(\mathbb{C})
is of order less than two and type less than
\tfrac{\pi}{2}
. Additionally, it is clear that any entire function of order strictly less than two (or of order two and type strictly less than
\tfrac{\pi}{2}
) is in the Fock space.

Finally, the Gabor and Bargmann transforms satisfy
[
11
, Proposition 3.4.1 on p. 54]

\mathcal{G}f(x,-\omega)=\mathrm{e}^{\pi\mathrm{i}x\omega}\mathcal{B}f(z)\mathrm{e}^{-\pi\left\lvert z\right\rvert^{2}/2},\qquad z=x+\mathrm{i}\omega\in\mathbb{C}.

This relates Gabor transform phase retrieval — which is the recovery of square-integrable signals
f
from partial knowledge of the magnitudes of their Gabor transforms
\lvert\mathcal{G}f\rvert
on
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
— to the phase retrieval of entire functions of order at most two and type at most
\tfrac{\pi}{2}
.

Three results on Gabor transform phase retrieval

It follows that all the results that we have proven so far continue to hold for Gabor phase retrieval after suitable modifications. In the following, we illustrate this with three interesting examples.

First, we see that Theorem
12
implies the following counterpart.

Theorem 21
.

Let
\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be three parallel lines. Let
a>0
denote the distance between
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
and let
b>0
denote the distance between
\ell_{2}
and
\ell_{3}
. Assume that
\tfrac{b}{a}
is irrational and let
f,g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Then,
\lvert\mathcal{G}f\rvert=\lvert\mathcal{G}g\rvert
holds on
\ell_{1}\cup\ell_{2}\cup\ell_{3}
if and only if
f\sim g
.

Secondly, note that the entire functions defined in equation (
2
) are of first order and thus in the Fock space
\mathcal{F}^{2}(\mathbb{C})
. Taking the inverse Bargmann transform yields

\displaystyle f(t)=2^{1/4}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{2\pi}-\pi t^{2}}\left(\cosh(2t)+\mathrm{i}\sinh(2t)\right),

\displaystyle g(z)=2^{1/4}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{2\pi}-\pi t^{2}}\left(\cosh(2t)-\mathrm{i}\sinh(2t)\right).

It follows from the relation of the Gabor and the Bargmann transform that
\lvert\mathcal{G}f\rvert=\lvert\mathcal{G}g\rvert
on
\mathbb{R}\times\tfrac{\pi}{2}\mathbb{Z}
while
f\not\sim g
. The functions above inspired the paper
[
5
]
.

Thirdly, there exist universal counterexamples for Gabor phase retrieval.

Theorem 22
.

There exists a sequence
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
such that
f_{0}\not\sim f_{n}
and
\lvert\mathcal{G}f_{0}\rvert=\lvert\mathcal{G}f_{n}\rvert
on
\mathbb{R}\times\tfrac{\mathbb{Z}}{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

A bound on the number of zeroes in certain disks for functions in the Fock space

In some applications of the results presented so far, one might want to construct a square-integrable function, whose Gabor transform has a specified set of zeroes
Z\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
. This boils down to the construction of an entire function in the Fock space with the related set of zeroes
\{z=x+\mathrm{i}y\in\mathbb{C}\,|\,(x,-y)\in Z\}
. In order for the latter to be achievable, we need to make sure that
Z
is sufficiently spread out.

Let us illustrate this with an interesting example: suppose we are given
a,b>0
and want to construct a non-zero function
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
such that
\mathcal{G}f=0
on
a\mathbb{Z}\times b\mathbb{Z}
. A natural way of approaching this problem is to define

F(z)=z\prod_{(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\setminus\{(0,0)\}}E\left(\frac{z}{am+\mathrm{i}bn};2\right),\qquad z\in\mathbb{C},

(6)

which is a well-defined entire function of order two according to
[
21
, Subection 8.25 on pp. 251–252]
since

\sum_{m,n\geq 2}\frac{1}{(a^{2}m^{2}+b^{2}n^{2})^{1+\epsilon}}\leq\int_{1}^{\infty}\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(a^{2}x^{2}+b^{2}y^{2})^{1+\epsilon}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y<\infty,

for
\epsilon>0
.

If we could show that the type of
F
is strictly smaller than
\tfrac{\pi}{2}
, then
F\in\mathcal{F}^{2}(\mathbb{C})
and we could write
f=\mathcal{B}^{-1}F\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Upper bounding the type of a product of primary factors is generally hard, however, and we will instead present a bound on the number of zeroes in disks centered at the origin for functions in the Fock space. This bound can be used to give a rough idea about how densely one can choose the set of zeroes of
F\in\mathcal{F}^{2}(\mathbb{C})
.

Lemma 23
.

Let
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
be non-zero. Let
n:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{N}_{0}
be such that
n(r)
denotes the number of zeroes of
\mathcal{B}f
(counted with multiplicity) in the disc of radius
r>0
centered at the origin. Let

m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
denote the multiplicity of the zero at the origin of
\mathcal{B}f
and let
c:=2\log\lVert f\rVert_{2}-2\log\lvert\lim_{z\to 0}z^{-m}\mathcal{B}f(z)\rvert-m
.
Then,

n(r)\leq\pi\mathrm{e}r^{2}-2m\log r+c,\qquad r>0.

Proof.

Let
R\geq r>0
be such that
R^{2}=\mathrm{e}r^{2}
and define

F(z):=\frac{\mathcal{B}f(z)}{z^{m}},\qquad z\in\mathbb{C}.

Denote by
n_{F}(t)
the number of zeroes of
F
(counted with multiplicity) in the disc of radius
t>0
centered at the origin. Note that
F
is an entire function with removable singularity at
z=0
. Therefore, Jensen’s formula implies

\int_{0}^{R}\frac{n_{F}(t)}{t}\,\mathrm{d}t=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\log\left\lvert F(R\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta})\right\rvert\,\mathrm{d}\theta-\log\left\lvert\lim_{z\to 0}F(z)\right\rvert.

We can use the reproducing property of
\mathcal{F}^{2}(\mathbb{C})
along with the fact that
\mathcal{B}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\to\mathcal{F}^{2}(\mathbb{C})
is an isometry to see that

\left\lvert F(R\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta})\right\rvert=\frac{\left\lvert\mathcal{B}f(R\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta})\right\rvert}{R^{m}}\leq\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\pi}{2}R^{2}}}{R^{m}}\left\lVert\mathcal{B}f\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{C},\gamma)}=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\pi\mathrm{e}}{2}r^{2}}}{\mathrm{e}^{\frac{m}{2}}r^{m}}\lVert f\rVert_{2}.

In addition, we can use that
n_{F}(t)
is increasing in
t
to estimate

\int_{r}^{R}\frac{n_{F}(t)}{t}\,\mathrm{d}t\geq n_{F}(r)\int_{r}^{R}\frac{1}{t}\,\mathrm{d}t=\frac{n_{F}(r)}{2}

and thus

n_{F}(r)\leq 2\int_{r}^{R}\frac{n_{F}(t)}{t}\,\mathrm{d}t\leq 2\int_{0}^{R}\frac{n_{F}(t)}{t}\,\mathrm{d}t.

We conclude that

n_{F}(r)\leq\pi\mathrm{e}r^{2}-2m\log r+2\log\lVert f\rVert_{2}-2\log\left\lvert\lim_{z\to 0}\frac{\mathcal{B}f(z)}{z^{m}}\right\rvert-m.

∎

We apply Lemma
23
in our example: let us use the estimate
[
16
, Theorem I on pp. 15–16]

n(r)=\left\lvert(a\mathbb{Z}+\mathrm{i}b\mathbb{Z})\cap\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}\,\middle|\,\left\lvert z\right\rvert\leq r\right\}\right\rvert=\frac{\pi r^{2}}{ab}+\mathcal{O}(r^{2/3}),\qquad r\to\infty.

A comparison with Lemma
23
yields that
ab\geq\mathrm{e}^{-1}
is a necessary condition for
F\in\mathcal{F}^{2}(\mathbb{C})
, where
F
is defined in equation (
6
). We have therefore proven the following sampling result for the Gabor transform.

Corollary 24
.

Let
a,b>0
such that
ab<\mathrm{e}^{-1}
and let
f,g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. If

\mathcal{G}f=\mathcal{G}g\qquad\mbox{on }a\mathbb{Z}\times b\mathbb{Z},

then
f=g
.

Remark 25
.

The assumption
ab<\mathrm{e}^{-1}
is known to be suboptimal. In fact, Corollary
24
holds under the weaker assumption
ab\leq 1
according to
[
18
,
20
]
.

Finally, we note that we can directly estimate the type of
F
defined in equation (
6
). Indeed,
F
is a special case of the Weierstrass
\sigma
-function and one may use its quasi-periodicity to show that its type
\sigma
satisfies
\sigma\leq\tfrac{\pi}{2ab}

[
13
,
14
]
. Hence,
F\in\mathcal{F}^{2}(\mathbb{C})
and it follow that we can construct a non-zero function
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
such that
\mathcal{G}f=0
on
a\mathbb{Z}\times b\mathbb{Z}
if
ab>1
.

Appendix A
Expansion of a function as an infinite product

The following expansion is used in the proof of Theorem
14
.

Proposition 26
.

For all
a,z\in\mathbb{C}
with
a\not\in\pi\mathrm{i}\mathbb{Z}
, the following product formula holds:

\frac{\sinh(a-z)}{\sinh a}\exp(z\coth a)=\prod_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(1-\frac{z}{a+\pi\mathrm{i}k}\right)\exp\left(\frac{z}{a+\pi\mathrm{i}k}\right).

Proof.

We want to rearrange the infinite product. To justify this, we show that the product converges absolutely. Let us fix
a,z\in\mathbb{C}
with
a\not\in\pi\mathrm{i}\mathbb{Z}
and introduce the notation
u_{k}:=z/(a+\pi\mathrm{i}k)
for
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. If
u_{k}
satisfies
\lvert u_{k}\rvert\leq\tfrac{1}{2}
, then

\lvert s_{k}\rvert:=\Bigg\lvert\sum_{\ell=2}^{\infty}\frac{u_{k}^{\ell}}{\ell}\Bigg\rvert\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell=2}^{\infty}\left\lvert u_{k}\right\rvert^{\ell}=\frac{\lvert u_{k}\rvert^{2}}{2-2\lvert u_{k}\rvert}\leq\lvert u_{k}\rvert^{2}\leq\frac{1}{4}.

If
\lvert k\rvert
is sufficently large, then
\lvert u_{k}\rvert\leq\tfrac{1}{2}
as well as
\pi\lvert k\rvert>\lvert a\rvert
. Summing over all such
k
yields

\displaystyle\sum_{k}\lvert 1-E(u_{k};1)\rvert

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\lvert 1-\exp(-s_{k})\rvert\leq\sum_{k}(\exp\lvert s_{k}\rvert-1)\leq 2\sum_{k}\lvert s_{k}\rvert

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{k}\lvert u_{k}\rvert^{2}\leq 2\lvert z\rvert^{2}\sum_{k}(\pi\lvert k\rvert-\lvert a\rvert)^{-2}<\infty

by the integral test.

We rearrange the infinite product

\prod_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}E(u_{k};1)=E(u_{0};1)\prod_{n\in\mathbb{N}}E(u_{n};1)E(u_{-n};1).

It is useful for us to note that

1-u_{n}=\frac{1+\frac{a-z}{\pi\mathrm{i}n}}{1+\frac{a}{\pi\mathrm{i}n}},\qquad(1-u_{n})(1-u_{-n})=\frac{1+\frac{(a-z)^{2}}{\pi^{2}n^{2}}}{1+\frac{a^{2}}{\pi^{2}n^{2}}},

which allows us to write our infinite product as

\frac{(a-z)\prod_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\big(1+\frac{(a-z)^{2}}{\pi^{2}n^{2}}\big)}{a\prod_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\big(1+\frac{a^{2}}{\pi^{2}n^{2}}\big)}\exp\left(\frac{z}{a}+2az\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{1}{a^{2}+\pi^{2}n^{2}}\right).

By the well-known expansion of the sine as an infinite product (cf. e.g.
[
21
, Section 3.23 on pp. 113–114]
) and the partial fraction expansion of the cotangent (cf. e.g.
[
21
, Example (i) on p. 113]
), the above is equal to

\frac{\sin(\mathrm{i}(a-z))}{\sin(\mathrm{i}a)}\exp(z\mathrm{i}\cot(\mathrm{i}a)),

which proves the proposition.
∎

Appendix B
Characterisation of finite order entire functions with common magnitudes on equidistant parallel lines

We prove the following characterisation.

Theorem 27
.

Let
y_{0}>0
, and let
f,g\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{C})\setminus\{0\}
be of order less than two. Then,
\left\lvert f\right\rvert=\left\lvert g\right\rvert
on
\mathbb{R}+\mathrm{i}y_{0}\mathbb{Z}
if and only if
f
and
g
factor as

\displaystyle f(z)=r\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\alpha}\mathrm{e}^{Q(z)}P_{\mathrm{c}}(z)\prod_{a\in W_{-}}\left[\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\pi}{y_{0}}(a-z)}-1}{\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\pi}{y_{0}}a}-1}\right]^{M_{\mathrm{d}}(a)}\prod_{a\in W_{+}}\left[\frac{1-\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\pi}{y_{0}}(a-z)}}{1-\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\pi}{y_{0}}a}}\right]^{M_{\mathrm{d}}(a)},

\displaystyle g(z)=r\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\beta}\mathrm{e}^{Q(z)}P_{\mathrm{c}}(z)\prod_{a\in W_{-}}\left[\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\pi}{y_{0}}(\overline{a}-z)}-1}{\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\pi}{y_{0}}\overline{a}}-1}\right]^{M_{\mathrm{d}}(a)}\prod_{a\in W_{+}}\left[\frac{1-\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\pi}{y_{0}}(\overline{a}-z)}}{1-\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\pi}{y_{0}}\overline{a}}}\right]^{M_{\mathrm{d}}(a)}.

Here,
r>0
,
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
,
Q\in\mathbb{C}_{2}[z]
is a polynomial with
Q(0)=0
,
P_{\mathrm{c}}
is the canonical product formed with the common zeroes of
f
and
g
, and

\displaystyle W_{+}:=\left\{a\in[0,\infty)+\mathrm{i}(-y_{0},y_{0}]\,\middle|\,M_{\mathrm{d}}(a)>0\right\},

\displaystyle W_{-}:=\left\{a\in(-\infty,0)+\mathrm{i}(-y_{0},y_{0}]\,\middle|\,M_{\mathrm{d}}(a)>0\right\}.

Proof.

As in the proof of Theorem
14
, we can assume that
y_{0}=\tfrac{\pi}{2}
without loss of generality. Suppose that
\lvert f\rvert=\lvert g\rvert
on
\mathbb{R}+\tfrac{\pi\mathrm{i}}{2}\mathbb{Z}
. Then, Theorem
14
implies that
f
and
g
factor as

\displaystyle f(z)=\mathrm{e}^{Q_{f}(z)}P_{\mathrm{c}}(z)\prod_{a\in W_{-}\cup W_{+}}\left[\frac{\sinh(a-z)}{\sinh a}\mathrm{e}^{R_{a}(z)}\right]^{M_{\mathrm{d}}(a)},

\displaystyle g(z)=\mathrm{e}^{Q_{g}(z)}P_{\mathrm{c}}(z)\prod_{a\in W_{-}\cup W_{+}}\left[\frac{\sinh(\overline{a}-z)}{\sinh a}\mathrm{e}^{R_{\bar{a}}(z)}\right]^{M_{\mathrm{d}}(a)}.

Here,
Q_{f},Q_{g}\in\mathbb{C}_{2}[z]
are polynomials,
P_{\mathrm{c}}
is the canonical product formed with the common zeroes of
f
and
g
, and
R_{a}(z)=z\coth a+z^{2}/2\csch^{2}a
.

We want to rearrange the infinite products over
W_{-}
and
W_{+}
which we will justify by their absolute convergence (just as in the proof of Proposition
26
). Let us note that almost all
a\in W_{-}\cup W_{+}
satisfy
\lvert\operatorname{Re}a\rvert\geq 2\lvert z\rvert
and focus on those that do in the following. We introduce the notation
u_{a,k}:=z/(a+\pi\mathrm{i}k)
and find that
\lvert u_{a,k}\rvert\leq\tfrac{1}{2}
. Applying the well-known Taylor series expansion of
\log(1-z)
, it follows that

\log\left(\frac{\sinh(a-z)}{\sinh a}\mathrm{e}^{R_{a}(z)}\right)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\log E(u_{a,k};2)=-\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{\ell=3}^{\infty}\frac{u_{a,k}^{\ell}}{\ell}=:s_{a},

and that

\lvert s_{a}\rvert\leq\frac{1}{3}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{\ell=3}^{\infty}\lvert u_{a,k}\rvert^{\ell}\leq\frac{2}{3}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\lvert u_{a,k}\rvert^{3}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{\lvert\operatorname{Re}a\rvert\geq 2\lvert z\rvert}\left\lvert\frac{\sinh(a-z)}{\sinh a}\mathrm{e}^{R_{a}(z)}-1\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lvert\operatorname{Re}a\rvert\geq 2\lvert z\rvert}\lvert\mathrm{e}^{s_{a}}-1\rvert\leq\sum_{\lvert\operatorname{Re}a\rvert\geq 2\lvert z\rvert}(\mathrm{e}^{\lvert s_{a}\rvert}-1)

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{\lvert\operatorname{Re}a\rvert\geq 2\lvert z\rvert}\lvert s_{a}\rvert\leq\frac{4}{3}\sum_{\lvert\operatorname{Re}a\rvert\geq 2\lvert z\rvert}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\lvert u_{a,k}\rvert^{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{4\lvert z\rvert}{3}\sum_{\lvert\operatorname{Re}a\rvert\geq 2\lvert z\rvert}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\lvert a+\pi\mathrm{i}k\rvert^{-3}<\infty,

where we use that the exponent of convergence of the zeroes of
f
is smaller than its order (cf. e.g.
[
21
, Section 8.22 on pp. 249–250]
) and thus smaller than two.

We have shown that the products over
W_{-}\cup W_{+}
converge absolutely, which implies that they converge unconditionally. We can thus rearrange them to

\prod_{a\in W_{-}}\left[\frac{\sinh(a-z)}{\sinh a}\mathrm{e}^{R_{a}(z)}\right]^{M_{\mathrm{d}}(a)}\prod_{a\in W_{+}}\left[\frac{\sinh(a-z)}{\sinh a}\mathrm{e}^{R_{a}(z)}\right]^{M_{\mathrm{d}}(a)}\\
=\prod_{a\in W_{-}}\left[\frac{\mathrm{e}^{2(a-z)}-1}{\mathrm{e}^{2a}-1}\mathrm{e}^{z(\coth a+1)+\frac{z^{2}}{2}\csch^{2}a}\right]^{M_{\mathrm{d}}(a)}\\
\cdot\prod_{a\in W_{+}}\left[\frac{1-\mathrm{e}^{-2(a-z)}}{1-\mathrm{e}^{-2a}}\mathrm{e}^{z(\coth a-1)+\frac{z^{2}}{2}\csch^{2}a}\right]^{M_{\mathrm{d}}(a)}.

As in
[
12
, Section 3 on pp. 11–14]
, we can show that the above simplifies to

\mathrm{e}^{R_{f}(z)}\prod_{a\in W_{-}}\left[\frac{\mathrm{e}^{2(a-z)}-1}{\mathrm{e}^{2a}-1}\right]^{M_{\mathrm{d}}(a)}\prod_{a\in W_{+}}\left[\frac{1-\mathrm{e}^{-2(a-z)}}{1-\mathrm{e}^{-2a}}\right]^{M_{\mathrm{d}}(a)},

where
R_{f}\in\mathbb{C}_{2}[z]
, because all of the sums

\displaystyle\sum_{a\in W_{-}}\left\lvert\frac{\mathrm{e}^{2(a-z)}-1}{\mathrm{e}^{2a}-1}-1\right\rvert,\qquad\sum_{a\in W_{+}}\left\lvert\frac{1-\mathrm{e}^{-2(a-z)}}{1-\mathrm{e}^{-2a}}-1\right\rvert,

\displaystyle\sum_{a\in W_{-}}\left\lvert\coth a+1\right\rvert,\qquad\sum_{a\in W_{+}}\left\lvert\coth a-1\right\rvert,\qquad\sum_{a\in W_{-}\cup W_{+}}\lvert\csch a\rvert^{2}

converge. We conclude that

\displaystyle f(z)=\mathrm{e}^{Q_{f}(z)+R_{f}(z)}P_{\mathrm{c}}(z)\prod_{a\in W_{-}}\left[\frac{\mathrm{e}^{2(a-z)}-1}{\mathrm{e}^{2a}-1}\right]^{M_{\mathrm{d}}(a)}\prod_{a\in W_{+}}\left[\frac{1-\mathrm{e}^{-2(a-z)}}{1-\mathrm{e}^{-2a}}\right]^{M_{\mathrm{d}}(a)},

\displaystyle g(z)=\mathrm{e}^{Q_{g}(z)+R_{g}(z)}P_{\mathrm{c}}(z)\prod_{a\in W_{-}}\left[\frac{\mathrm{e}^{2(\overline{a}-z)}-1}{\mathrm{e}^{2\overline{a}}-1}\right]^{M_{\mathrm{d}}(a)}\prod_{a\in W_{+}}\left[\frac{1-\mathrm{e}^{-2(\overline{a}-z)}}{1-\mathrm{e}^{-2\overline{a}}}\right]^{M_{\mathrm{d}}(a)}.

It remains for us to consider the polynomials
p_{f}:=Q_{f}+R_{f},p_{g}:=Q_{g}+R_{g}\in\mathbb{C}_{2}[z]
: since
\lvert f\rvert=\lvert g\rvert
on
\mathbb{R}
, we have
\operatorname{Re}p_{f}=\operatorname{Re}p_{g}
on
\mathbb{R}
, which implies that the coefficients of
p_{f}
and
p_{g}
have the same real parts. Finally,
\lvert f\rvert=\lvert g\rvert
on
\mathbb{R}+\tfrac{\pi\mathrm{i}}{2}
implies that
p_{f}=p_{g}+\mathrm{i}c
for
c\in\mathbb{R}
as in the proof of Theorem
12
. Therefore, the factorisation has been proven. The reverse implication follows by a direct computation.
∎
