Method of Moments Estimation for Affine Stochastic Volatility Models
Yan-Feng Wu, Xiangyu Yang, Jian-Qiang Hu

Abstract
We develop moment estimators for the parameters of affine stochastic volatility models. We first address the challenge of calculating moments for the models by introducing a recursive equation for deriving closed-form expressions for moments of any order. Consequently, we propose our moment estimators. We then establish a central limit theorem for our estimators and derive the explicit formulas for the asymptotic covariance matrix. Finally, we provide numerical results to validate our method.



keywords
: affine jump diffusion; stochastic volatility; Heston model; method of moments.

1
Introduction

Volatility plays a crucial role in the pricing of options and other derivative securities. Empirical evidence from financial
markets, such as volatility clustering, the dependence between increments, and volatility smiles, shows that the assumption of constant volatility used in the Black-Scholes European options pricing model is inappropriate (see
Fama,, 1965
). Therefore, several
stochastic volatility (SV) models, such as
Heston, (1993)
,
Bates, (1996)
and
Barndorff-Nielsen and Shephard, (2001)
, have been proposed to better capture the time-varying features of volatility. However, parameter estimation for these SV models poses a formidable computational challenge for practical implementation. Likelihood-based inference and moment-based inference are two major types of estimation methods. Our work in this paper offers a new and direct method of moments (MM) estimation for affine SV models.

There is a large body of literature on likelihood-based inference, in which Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) and maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) are the two main approaches used. An MCMC model assumes a prior distribution of parameters, and then iteratively collects samples in a Bayesian framework until certain convergence is achieved
(
Eraker,, 2001
;
Jacquier et al.,, 2002
;
Eraker et al.,, 2003
;
Roberts et al.,, 2004
;
Griffin and Steel,, 2006
;
Jasra et al.,, 2011
)
. In some cases, this approach may become computationally prohibitive because of its slow convergence to a Markov chain equilibrium distribution. On the other hand, the MLE approach adopts a frequentist perspective and often requires a closed-form expression of the state transition density function; however, this is hard to obtain in SV models, let alone optimization. Hence, the MLE approach often involves some sort of approximation or simulation methods. Two prevail schemes are simulated MLE (SMLE) (see, e.g.,
Durham and Gallant, (2002)
) and quasi-MLE (QMLE) (see, e.g.,
Ruiz, (1994)
,
Harvey and Shephard, (1996)
, and
Feunou and Okou, (2018)
).
Hurn et al., (2015)
design an MLE estimate using a particle filter to approximate the likelihood.
Aït-Sahalia and Kimmel, (2007)
propose a closed-form approximation scheme based on the extracted volatility from observed option prices.
Andersen et al., (2015)
employ optimization tools such as penalized nonlinear least squares to develop a new parametric estimation procedure. A gradient-based simulated MLE estimate is proposed in
Peng et al., (2014)
and
Peng et al., (2016)
. Nevertheless, approximations used in these MLE methods require enormous computational effort, and many assumptions may be difficult to satisfy in practice.

Modern financial markets give rise to numerous real-time decision-making instances (e.g. high-frequency trading) that request computationally fast and less restrictive estimators. The moment-based method is a case in point but has yet to be recognized fully. A few important contributions include the simulated MM (SMM); see
Duffie and Singleton, (1993)
), efficient MM (EMM); see
Bansal et al., (1994)
and
Gallant and Tauchen, (1996)
, and generalized MM (GMM); see
Singleton, (2001)
,
Bollerslev and Zhou, (2002)
,
Jiang and Knight, (2002)
, and
Chacko and Viceira, (2003)
. SMM simulates sequences from the target models and estimates parameters by matching simulated data moments with actual data moments numerically. GMM can be applied when we have more sample moments than the number of parameters. EMM combines the efficiency of MLE with the flexibility of GMM, which is a variant of SMM. In general, the major issue of the moment-based method is the so-called statistical inefficiency in the sense that the higher order moments are used, the greater likelihood of estimation bias occurs. Fortunately, we can mitigate this problem by using better computational techniques (e.g., see
Wu et al., (2019)
and
Wu et al., (2022)
). For instance,
Yang et al., (2021)
recently develop the first closed-form MM parameter estimators for a class of Lévy-driven Ornstein-Uhlenbeck stochastic volatility models.

In this paper, we consider affine SV models, and develop explicit MM estimators for the parameters of these models. To this end, we first derive the analytical formulas for the required moments and covariances of the affine SV models and then use them to develop our MM estimators.
The MM estimators can be expressed explicitly in terms of the sample moments and sample covariances of the observed asset prices.
In fact, we have established a recursive equation for deriving closed-form expressions for moments of any order, while our MM estimators only require those moments of relatively low order.
We also prove that the large-sample behaviors of our MM estimators satisfy the central limit theorem and derive the explicit formulas for the asymptotic covariance matrix. In addition, numerical experiments are provided to test the efficiency of our method. The advantage of our estimators is that they are simple and easy to implement in practice and do not require the availability of high-frequency data or option price data.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we present the affine SV model and its baseline SV model, then calculate the moments and covariances of the returns of the asset price. In Section 3, we derive our MM estimators and establish the central limit theorem. Some numerical experiments are provided in Section 4. Section 5 concludes this paper. The appendices contain some of the detailed calculations which are omitted in the main body of the paper and also some extensions of the baseline affine SV model.

2
Affine Stochastic Volatility Model

We first delineate the general framework of affine SV models. Subsequently, we employ the Heston model, a prototypical example of an affine SV model, to illustrate the derivation of closed-form expressions for moments and covariances of any order. We then extend this moment derivation methodology to a broader array of affine SV models, encompassing those with jump components in either the price or variance processes, as well as models incorporating multiple latent volatility factors. These extensions are detailed in the appendices.

Consider
s(t)
as the price of a certain asset at time
t
. The general affine SV model explored in this study is governed by the following stochastic differential equations (SDEs):

\displaystyle\frac{ds(t)}{s(t)}

\displaystyle=\mu(\boldsymbol{v}(t))dt+\sigma(\boldsymbol{v}(t))dw^{s}_{t}+dz^{s}(t),

(1)

\displaystyle d\boldsymbol{v}(t)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\mu_{v}}(\boldsymbol{v}(t))dt+\boldsymbol{\sigma_{v}}(\boldsymbol{v}(t))d\boldsymbol{w^{v}}(t)+d\boldsymbol{z^{v}}(t),

(2)

where
\boldsymbol{v}(t)
denotes a vector of latent variance factors,
w^{s}(t)
and
\boldsymbol{w^{v}}(t)
are potentially correlated Wiener processes,
z^{s}(t)
and
\boldsymbol{z^{v}}(t)
are possibly correlated compound Poisson processes. The drift functions —
\mu(\cdot),\boldsymbol{\mu_{v}}(\cdot)
and the variance
\sigma^{2}(\cdot)
and the covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\sigma_{v}}^{T}(\cdot)\boldsymbol{\sigma_{v}}(\cdot)
—all have affine dependence on the hidden state vector
\boldsymbol{v}(t)

(
Duffie et al.,, 2000
)
. It is assumed that the drifts, diffusions and compound Poisson processes are well-behaved enough to ensure the unique strong solution of the SDEs.

This general model includes several common SV models as special cases, such as the Heston model, the stochastic volatility with jumps (SVJ) model, the stochastic volatility with independent jumps (SVIJ) model, the stochastic volatility with contemporaneous jumps (SVCJ) model, and even the BNS model. Within the framework of the Heston model
(
Heston,, 1993
)
, the vector
\boldsymbol{v}(t)
simplifies to a single variance factor
v(t)
, with drift and diffusion functions defined as:

\displaystyle\mu(v(t))

\displaystyle=\mu,

\displaystyle\sigma(v(t))

\displaystyle=\sqrt{v(t)},

\displaystyle\mu_{v}(v(t))

\displaystyle=k(\theta-v(t)),

\displaystyle\sigma_{v}(v(t))

\displaystyle=\sigma_{v}\sqrt{v(t)},

and without any jump components
z^{s}(t)
and
z^{v}(t)
in both the price and variance equations. The SVJ model, as described by
(
Bates,, 1996
)
, extends the Heston framework by including jumps in the price process. The SVIJ and SVCJ models, detailed in
Eraker et al., (2003)
, further incorporate jumps into the variance process, either independently or contemporaneously with price jumps. An additional variant is the multi-factor Heston model, which characterizes the latent variance as a vector of square-root diffusions, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{v}(t)=(v_{1}(t),v_{2}(t))^{T}
, and

\displaystyle\mu(\boldsymbol{v}(t))

\displaystyle=\mu,

\displaystyle\sigma(\boldsymbol{v}(t))

\displaystyle=\sqrt{v_{1}(t)+v_{2}(t)},

\displaystyle v_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=k_{1}(\theta_{1}-v_{1}(t)),

\displaystyle\sigma_{v1}(v_{1}(t))

\displaystyle=\sigma_{v1}\sqrt{v_{1}(t)},

\displaystyle v_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=k_{2}(\theta_{2}-v_{2}(t)),

\displaystyle\sigma_{v2}(v_{2}(t))

\displaystyle=\sigma_{v2}\sqrt{v_{2}(t)}.

In contrast, the BNS model, proposed by
Barndorff-Nielsen and Shephard, (2001)
posits a one-dimensional non-Gaussian Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) process for the latent variance, driven solely by a compound Poisson process
z^{v}(t)
, devoid of a diffusion component. The drift and diffusion functions are defined as:

\displaystyle\mu(v(t))

\displaystyle=\mu,

\displaystyle\sigma(v(t))

\displaystyle=\sqrt{v(t)},

\displaystyle\mu_{v}(v(t))

\displaystyle=-\lambda v(t),

\displaystyle\sigma_{v}(v(t))

\displaystyle=0,

and there is no jump components in the price process.

It is notable that these affine SV models do not possess closed-form solution for their transition density functions. However, for the general affine SV model, closed-form expressions for their conditional Characteristic Functions (CFs) can be derived
(
Duffie et al.,, 2000
;
Singleton,, 2001
;
Chacko and Viceira,, 2003
)
.
Jiang and Knight, (2002)
extended this work by developing a closed-form CF for a simplified version of the Heston model.

Our work in this paper aims to extend the analytical tractability of these models to include closed-form formulas for moments and covariances of any (positive integer) order, which can then be used to construct moment estimators for the affine SV model.

Since the Heston model is the baseline affine SV model, we will focus on its moment calculations which will lay the foundation for the more complex moment computations of other affine SV models. The methodology developed for the Heston model can be generalized and applied to estimate other affine SV models. The detailed moment-derivation procedures for these other affine SV models are provided in the appendices.

2.1
Baseline Affine Stochastic Volatility Model

We use the following Heston model
(
Heston,, 1993
)
as our baseline affine SV model :

\displaystyle\frac{ds(t)}{s(t)}

\displaystyle=\mu dt+\sqrt{v(t)}dw^{s}(t),

(3)

\displaystyle dv(t)

\displaystyle=k(\theta-v(t))dt+\sigma_{v}\sqrt{v(t)}dw^{v}(t),

(4)

where
v(t)
is a scalar affine diffusion, and
w^{s}(t)
and
w^{v}(t)
are two Wiener processes with correlation
\rho
.
Assume that the initial values
s(0)
and
v(0)
are independent of each other and also independent of
w^{s}(t)
and
w^{v}(t)
. The variance process (
4
) is a Cox-Ingersoll-Ross process
(
Cox et al.,, 1985
)
, which is also called square-root diffusion. The parameters
\theta>0
,
k>0
, and
\sigma_{v}
determine the long-run mean, the mean reversion velocity, and the volatility of the variance process
v(t)
, respectively, and satisfy the condition
\sigma_{v}^{2}\leq 2k\theta
.

Based on Itô formula, the log price process can be written as:

d\log s(t)=(\mu-\frac{1}{2}v(t))dt+\sqrt{v(t)}dw^{s}(t).

(5)

Though modeled as a continuous-time process, the asset price is observed at discrete-time instances. Assume we have observations of
s(t)
at discrete-time
ih
(
i=0,1,\cdots,N
), and let
s_{i}\triangleq s(ih)
. Similarly, let
v_{i}\triangleq v(ih)
, however, we should note that
v_{i}
is not observable. Finally, we define the return
y_{i}
as

y_{i}\triangleq\log s_{i}-\log s_{i-1}.

We can decompose
w^{s}(t)
as

w^{s}(t)=\rho w^{v}(t)+\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}w(t),

where
w(t)
is another Wiener process independent of
w^{v}(t)
. For notational simplicity, we define:

\displaystyle I_{s,t}\triangleq

\displaystyle\int_{s}^{t}\sqrt{v(u)}dw^{v}(u),

\displaystyle I_{s,t}^{*}\triangleq

\displaystyle\int_{s}^{t}\sqrt{v(u)}dw(u),

\displaystyle I\!E_{s,t}\triangleq

\displaystyle\int_{s}^{t}e^{ku}\sqrt{v(u)}dw^{v}(u),

\displaystyle IV_{s,t}\triangleq

\displaystyle\int_{s}^{t}v(u)du,

and

\displaystyle I_{i}

\displaystyle\triangleq I_{(i-1)h,ih},

\displaystyle I_{i}^{*}

\displaystyle\triangleq I^{*}_{(i-1)h,ih},

\displaystyle I\!E_{i}

\displaystyle\triangleq I\!E_{(i-1)h,ih},

\displaystyle IV_{i}

\displaystyle\triangleq IV_{(i-1)h,ih},

\displaystyle I_{i,t}

\displaystyle\triangleq I_{ih,t},

\displaystyle I^{*}_{i,t}

\displaystyle\triangleq I^{*}_{ih,t},

\displaystyle I\!E_{i,t}

\displaystyle\triangleq I\!E_{ih,t},

\displaystyle IV_{i,t}

\displaystyle\triangleq IV_{ih,t}.

IV_{s,t}
,
IV_{i}
, and
IV_{i,t}
defined above are also referred as integrated variance (volatility). We can then express
y_{i}
as

y_{i}=\mu h-\frac{1}{2}IV_{i}+\rho I_{i}+\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}I_{i}^{*}.

(6)

Based on (
4
), the variance process
v(t)
can be re-written as:

v(t)=e^{-k(t-s)}v(s)+\theta\left[1-e^{-k(t-s)}\right]+\sigma_{v}e^{-kt}I\!E_{s,t},

(7)

which is a Markov process that has a steady-state gamma distribution with mean
\theta
and variance
\theta\sigma_{v}^{2}/(2k)
, e.g., see
Cox et al., (1985)
. Without loss of generality, throughout this paper we assume that
v(0)
is distributed according to the steady-state distribution of
v(t)
, which implies that
v(t)
is strictly stationary and ergodic
(
Overbeck and Rydén,, 1997
)
.
However, all the results we shall derive in the rest of this paper, including the formulas for all the moments/covariances and the MM estimators, remain the same for any non-negative
v(0)
. Since
v(t)
is stationary, we have

\displaystyle\E[v(t)^{m}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{j=0}^{m-1}\left(\theta+\frac{j\sigma_{v}^{2}}{2k}\right),

(8)

\displaystyle\var(v(t))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\theta\sigma_{v}^{2}}{2k},

(9)

for
m=1,2,\ldots
and
t>0
, and

\displaystyle\E[v(t)v(s)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-k(t-s)}\frac{\theta\sigma_{v}^{2}}{2k}+\theta^{2},

(10)

\displaystyle\cov(v(t),v(s))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-k(t-s)},

(11)

for
s<t
.
Furthermore, we have

\displaystyle IV_{s,t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\theta(t-s)-\frac{v(t)-v(s)}{k}+\frac{\sigma_{v}}{k}I_{s,t},

(12)

\displaystyle IV_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\theta(h-\tilde{h})+\tilde{h}v_{i-1}-\frac{\sigma_{v}}{k}e^{-kih}I\!E_{i}+\frac{\sigma_{v}}{k}I_{i},

(13)

where
\tilde{h}\triangleq(1-e^{-kh})/k
. The above formulas will be useful in calculating the moments and covariances of
y_{i}
in the next section.
Hereafter, we will use a generic subscript
n
to denote variance and return (i.e.,
v_{n},y_{n}
) in their steady states.

2.2
Moments and covariances of returns

There are five parameters in the Heston model, therefore, to estimate them we need to obtain at least five moments/covariances of
y_{n}
. In this section, we show specifically how
\E[y_{n}]
,
\var(y_{n})
,
\cov(y_{n},y_{n+1})
,
\cov(y_{n},y_{n+2})
, and
\cov(y_{n}^{2},y_{n+1})
can be calculated and derive explicit formulas for them in terms of the five parameters involved in the Heston model, which can then be used to estimate these parameters.

Based on (
6
), together with
\E[I_{n}]=0
,
\E[I^{*}_{n}]=0
, and
\E[IV_{n}]=\theta h
, we have

\E[y_{n}]=(\mu-\theta/2)h.

(14)

To calculate
\var(y_{n})
, we first note
\cov(IV_{n},I_{n}^{*})=0
,
\cov(v_{n-1},I_{n}^{*})=0
,
\cov(I\!E_{n},I_{n}^{*})=0
, and
\cov(I_{n},I_{n}^{*})=0
. Hence,

\var(y_{n})=\frac{1}{4}\var(IV_{n})-\rho\cov(IV_{n},I_{n})+\rho^{2}\var(I_{n})+(1-\rho^{2})\var(I_{n}^{*}).

Let us take
\var(IV_{n})
as an example, it can be further expanded as

\displaystyle\var(IV_{n})

\displaystyle=\tilde{h}^{2}\var(v_{n-1})+\frac{\sigma_{v}^{2}}{k^{2}}\left(e^{-2knh}\var(I\!E_{n})+\var(I_{n})-2e^{-knh}\cov(I\!E_{n},I_{n})\right),

and we have

\var(IV_{n})=\frac{\theta\sigma_{v}^{2}}{k^{2}}(h-\tilde{h}).

Terms

\cov(IV_{n},I_{n}),\var(I_{n})
and
\var(I_{n}^{*})
can be computed similarly. In summary, we have

\var(y_{n})=\theta h+\left(\frac{\sigma_{v}^{2}}{4k^{2}}-\frac{\rho\sigma_{v}}{k}\right)\theta(h-\tilde{h}).

(15)

For
\cov(y_{n},y_{n+1})
,
\cov(y_{n},y_{n+2})
, we have

\displaystyle\cov(y_{n},y_{n+1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\theta\tilde{h}^{2}\left(\frac{\sigma_{v}^{2}}{8k}-\frac{\rho\sigma_{v}}{2}\right),

(16)

\displaystyle\cov(y_{n},y_{n+2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-kh}\cov(y_{n},y_{n+1})=e^{-kh}\theta\tilde{h}^{2}\left(\frac{\sigma_{v}^{2}}{8k}-\frac{\rho\sigma_{v}}{2}\right).

(17)

In fact, (
17
) can be generalized as

\cov(y_{n},y_{n+m})=e^{-(m-1)kh}\cov(y_{n},y_{n+1}),

(18)

for
m=2,3,\cdots
.

Finally, we consider
\cov(y_{n}^{2},y_{n+1})
. Since the computation is fairly straightforward but tedious, we omit all the details and just provide the following final result:

\displaystyle\cov(y_{n}^{2},y_{n+1})

\displaystyle=\frac{\theta\sigma_{v}^{4}}{8k^{3}}\tilde{h}(he^{-kh}-\tilde{h})+\left(\frac{\theta\sigma_{v}^{2}}{4k}\mu h-\frac{\theta^{2}\sigma_{v}^{2}}{8k}h-\frac{\theta\sigma_{v}^{2}}{4k}\right)\tilde{h}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{\rho\sigma_{v}}{2}\tilde{h}\bigg[\left(\frac{3\sigma_{v}^{2}}{2k^{2}}-\frac{2\rho\sigma_{v}}{k}\right)\theta(he^{-kh}-\tilde{h})+(2\mu\theta-\theta^{2})h\tilde{h}\bigg].

(19)

All the detailed calculations are provided in Appendix A.

Before closing, we point out that we can in fact compute the moments and covariances of
any
(positive integer) order and a recursive calculation procedure is provided in Appendix B.
And the calculation of the moments and covariances for other affine SV models is presented in Appendix D.

3
Parameter estimation

In this section, we derive our MM estimators for the five parameters in the Heston model based on asset price samples and the moments/covariances obtained in the previous section. We prove both the moments/covariances estimators and the MM estimators satisfy the central limit theorem and also derive the explicit formulas for their covariance matrices.

Assume we are given a sequence of asset price samples,
S_{0},S_{1},\cdots,S_{N}
, based on which we can calculate return samples:

Y_{i}\triangleq\log S_{i}-\log S_{i-1},

i=1,\cdots,N
.
These samples can be used to compute the sample moments and covariances of
y_{n}
, which can be used to estimate their population counterparts:

\displaystyle\E[y_{n}]

\displaystyle\approx\overline{Y}\triangleq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}Y_{i},

\displaystyle\var(y_{n})

\displaystyle\approx S^{2}\triangleq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(Y_{i}-\overline{Y})^{2},

\displaystyle\cov(y_{n},y_{n+1})

\displaystyle\approx\hat{\cov}(y_{n},y_{n+1})\triangleq\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}(Y_{i}-\overline{Y})(Y_{i+1}-\overline{Y}),

\displaystyle\cov(y_{n},y_{n+2})

\displaystyle\approx\hat{\cov}(y_{n},y_{n+2})\triangleq\frac{1}{N-2}\sum_{i=1}^{N-2}(Y_{i}-\overline{Y})(Y_{i+2}-\overline{Y}),

\displaystyle\cov(y_{n}^{2},y_{n+1})

\displaystyle\approx\hat{\cov}(y_{n}^{2},y_{n+1})\triangleq\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}(Y_{i}^{2}-\overline{Y^{2}})(Y_{i+1}-\overline{Y}),

where
\overline{Y^{2}}\triangleq(1/N)\sum_{i=1}^{N}Y_{i}^{2}
.
Let

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\gamma}

\displaystyle=(\E[y_{n}],\var(y_{n}),\cov(y_{n},y_{n+1}),\cov(y_{n},y_{n+2}),\cov(y_{n}^{2},y_{n+1}))^{T},

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}

\displaystyle=(\overline{Y},S^{2},\hat{\cov}(y_{n},y_{n+1}),\hat{\cov}(y_{n},y_{n+2}),\hat{\cov}(y_{n}^{2},y_{n+1}))^{T}.

where
T
denotes the transpose of a vector or matrix. We also define

\displaystyle z_{i}^{1}

\displaystyle\triangleq(y_{i}-\E[y_{n}])^{2},

\displaystyle z_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle\triangleq(y_{i}-\E[y_{n}])(y_{i+1}-\E[y_{n}]),

\displaystyle z_{i}^{3}

\displaystyle\triangleq(y_{i}-\E[y_{n}])(y_{i+2}-\E[y_{n}]),

\displaystyle z_{i}^{4}

\displaystyle\triangleq(y_{i}^{2}-\E[y_{n}^{2}])(y_{i+1}-\E[y_{n}]),

\displaystyle z_{i}

\displaystyle\triangleq(z_{i}^{1},z_{i}^{2},z_{i}^{3},z_{i}^{4}).

Let

\displaystyle\sigma_{11}

\displaystyle=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{N}\boldsymbol{1}^{T}_{N}[\cov(y_{i},y_{j})]_{N\times N}\boldsymbol{1}_{N},

\displaystyle\sigma_{1m}

\displaystyle=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{N}\boldsymbol{1}^{T}_{N}[\cov(y_{i},z_{j}^{m-1})]_{N\times N_{m}}\boldsymbol{1}_{N_{m}},~~~~~~~\mbox{$2\leq m\leq 5$},

\displaystyle\sigma_{lm}

\displaystyle=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{N}\boldsymbol{1}^{T}_{N_{k}}[\cov(z_{i}^{l-1},z_{j}^{m-1})]_{N_{l}\times N_{m}}\boldsymbol{1}_{N_{c}},~~~2\leq l\leq m\leq 5,

where
(N_{2},N_{3},N_{4},N_{5})=(N,N-1,N-2,N-1)
and
\boldsymbol{1}_{n}=(1,\cdots,1)^{T}
with
n
elements.
We are now ready to present the following central limit theorem for our moment/covariance estimators.

Theorem 3.1
.

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\sqrt{N}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}-\boldsymbol{\gamma})\overset{d}{=}\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)

(20)

where
\overset{d}{=}
denotes equal in distribution and
\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)
is a multivariate normal distribution with mean 0 and symmetric covariance matrix

\Sigma=[\sigma_{lm}]_{5\times 5}
, with

\displaystyle\sigma_{11}

\displaystyle=\theta h+\theta h\left(\frac{\sigma_{v}^{2}}{4k^{2}}-\frac{\rho\sigma_{v}}{k}\right),

(21)

\displaystyle\sigma_{22}

\displaystyle=\var(z_{n}^{1})+2C_{2}\cov(z_{n}^{1},v_{n})+2C\cov(z_{n}^{1},v_{n}^{2}),

(22)

\displaystyle\sigma_{33}

\displaystyle=\var(z_{n}^{2})+2\cov(z_{n}^{2},z_{n+1}^{2})+2C_{3}\cov(z_{n}^{2},v_{n+1})

\displaystyle\quad+2e^{-kh}C\cov(z_{n}^{2},v_{n+1}^{2}),

(23)

\displaystyle\sigma_{44}

\displaystyle=\var(z_{n}^{3})+2\cov(z_{n}^{3},z_{n+1}^{3})+2\cov(z_{n}^{3},z_{n+2}^{3})+2C_{4}\cov(z_{n}^{3},v_{n+2})

\displaystyle\sigma_{55}

\displaystyle=\var(z_{n}^{4})+2\cov(z_{n}^{4},z_{n+1}^{4})+2C_{51}\cov(z_{n}^{4},v_{n+1})+2C_{52}\cov(z_{n}^{4},v_{n+1}^{2})

and off-diagonal entries

\displaystyle\sigma_{12}

\displaystyle=\cov(y_{n},z_{n}^{1})+C_{y}\cov(v_{n},z_{n}^{1})+C_{2}\cov(y_{n},v_{n})+C\cov(y_{n},v_{n}^{2}),

\displaystyle\sigma_{13}

\displaystyle=\cov(y_{n},z_{n}^{2})+\cov(y_{n+1},z_{n}^{2})+C_{y}\cov(v_{n+1},z_{n}^{2})+C_{3}\cov(y_{n},v_{n})

\displaystyle\quad+e^{-kh}C\cov(y_{n},v_{n}^{2}),

\displaystyle\sigma_{14}

\displaystyle=\cov(y_{n},z_{n}^{3})+\cov(y_{n+1},z_{n}^{3})+\cov(y_{n+2},z_{n}^{3})+C_{y}\cov(v_{n+2},z_{n}^{3})

\displaystyle\quad+C_{4}\cov(y_{n},v_{n})+e^{-2kh}C\cov(y_{n},v_{n}^{2}),

\displaystyle\sigma_{15}

\displaystyle=\cov(y_{n},z_{n}^{4})+\cov(y_{n+1},z_{n}^{4})+C_{y}\cov(v_{n+1},z_{n}^{4})

\displaystyle\quad+C_{51}\cov(y_{n},v_{n})+C_{52}\cov(y_{n},v_{n}^{2})+C_{53}\cov(y_{n},v_{n}^{3}),

\displaystyle\sigma_{23}

\displaystyle=\cov(z_{n}^{1},z_{n}^{2})+C_{3}\cov(z_{n}^{1},v_{n})+e^{-kh}C\cov(z_{n}^{1},v_{n}^{2})

\displaystyle\quad+\cov(z_{n+1}^{1},z_{n}^{2})+C_{2}\cov(v_{n+1},z_{n}^{2})+C\cov(v_{n+1}^{2},z_{n}^{2}),

\displaystyle\sigma_{24}

\displaystyle=\cov(z_{n}^{1},z_{n}^{3})+C_{4}\cov(z_{n}^{1},v_{n})+e^{-2kh}C\cov(z_{n}^{1},v_{n}^{2})+\cov(z_{n+1}^{1},z_{n}^{3})

\displaystyle\quad+\cov(z_{n+2}^{1},z_{n}^{3})+C_{2}\cov(v_{n+2},z_{n}^{3})+C\cov(v_{n+2}^{2},z_{n}^{3}),

\displaystyle\sigma_{25}

\displaystyle=\cov(z_{n}^{1},z_{n}^{4})+C_{51}\cov(z_{n}^{1},v_{n})+C_{52}\cov(z_{n}^{1},v_{n}^{2})+C_{53}\cov(z_{n}^{1},v_{n}^{3})

\displaystyle\quad+\cov(z_{n+1}^{1},z_{n}^{4})+C_{2}\cov(v_{n+1},z_{n}^{4})+C_{3}\cov(v_{n+1}^{2},z_{n}^{4}),

\displaystyle\sigma_{34}

\displaystyle=\cov(z_{n}^{2},z_{n}^{3})+\cov(z_{n}^{2},z_{n+1}^{3})+C_{4}\cov(z_{n}^{2},v_{n+1})

\displaystyle\quad+e^{-2kh}C\cov(z_{n}^{2},v_{n+1}^{2})+\cov(z_{n+1}^{2},z_{n}^{3})+\cov(z_{n+2}^{2},z_{n}^{3})

\displaystyle\quad+C_{3}\cov(v_{n+2},z_{n}^{3})+e^{-kh}C\cov(v_{n+2}^{2},z_{n}^{3}),

\displaystyle\sigma_{35}

\displaystyle=\cov(z_{n}^{2},z_{n}^{4})+\cov(z_{n}^{2},z_{n+1}^{4})

\displaystyle\quad+C_{51}\cov(z_{n}^{2},v_{n+1})+C_{52}\cov(z_{n}^{2},v_{n+1}^{2})+C_{53}\cov(z_{n}^{2},v_{n+1}^{3})

\displaystyle\quad+\cov(z_{n+1}^{2},z_{n}^{4})+C_{3}\cov(v_{n+1},z_{n}^{4})+e^{-kh}C\cov(v_{n+1}^{2},z_{n}^{4}),

\displaystyle\sigma_{45}

\displaystyle=\cov(z_{n}^{3},z_{n}^{4})+\cov(z_{n}^{3},z_{n+1}^{4})+\cov(z_{n}^{3},z_{n+2}^{4})

\displaystyle\quad+C_{51}\cov(z_{n}^{2},v_{n+2})+C_{52}\cov(z_{n}^{2},v_{n+2}^{2})+C_{53}\cov(z_{n}^{2},v_{n+2}^{3})

\displaystyle\quad+\cov(z_{n+1}^{3},z_{n}^{4})+C_{4}\cov(v_{n+1},z_{n}^{4})+e^{-2kh}C\cov(v_{n+1}^{2},z_{n}^{4}),

where
C_{2},C_{3},C_{4},C,C_{y},C_{51},C_{52},C_{53}
are provided in Appendix C.

Proof.

The variance process
v(t)
is a Markov process, so is the discrete-time process
\{v_{n}\}
. Conditioning on
v_{n-1}
,
IV_{n}
and
I_{n}
are independent of
\{v_{i},0\leq i\leq n-2\}
.
Hence,
\{(v_{n},IV_{n},I_{n})\}
is also a Markov process.
The return process
\{y_{n}\}
can be viewed as a stochastic process driven by the hidden Markov chain
\{(v_{n},IV_{n},I_{n})\}
. Conditioning on
(v_{n},IV_{n},I_{n})
,
y_{n}
’s are independent and normally distributed with mean
\mu h-IV_{n}/2+\rho I_{n}
and variance
(1-\rho^{2})IV_{n}
, i.e.,

y_{n}|(v_{n},IV_{n},I_{n})\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\mu h-\frac{1}{2}IV_{n}+\rho I_{n},(1-\rho^{2})IV_{n}\right).

Under the condition
\sigma_{v}^{2}\leq 2k\theta
, the strictly stationary variance process
v(t)
(i.e., Cox-Ingersoll-Ross process
(
Cox et al.,, 1985
)
) is
\rho
-mixing; see Proposition 2.8 in
Genon-Catalot et al., (2000)
. Hence,
v(t)
is ergodic and strong mixing (also known as
\alpha
-mixing) with an exponential rate; see
Bradley, (2005)
. The discrete-time process
\{v_{n}\}
is also ergodic and inherits the exponentially fast strong mixing properties of
v(t)
. It can be verified that the hidden chain
\{(v_{n},IV_{n},I_{n})\}
is also ergodic and strong mixing with an exponential rate, noting that
I_{n}=(v_{n}-v_{n-1}-k\theta h+kIV_{n})/\sigma_{v}
.
Furthermore, according to Proposition 3.1 in
Genon-Catalot et al., (2000)
, the observable process
\{y_{n}\}
is strictly stationary and strong mixing with an exponential rate.
Applying the central limit theorem for strictly stationary strong mixing sequences (see Theorem 1.7 in
Ibragimov, (1962)
), we can prove (
20
) as Corollary 3.1 in
Genon-Catalot et al., (2000)
.

Next, we provide the calculations for the diagonal entries of
\Sigma
, but omit those for the off-diagonal entries since they are very similar. We now calculate the five diagonal entries of
\Sigma
.

\sigma_{11}
:

Based on (
18
), we have

\displaystyle\sigma_{11}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\left(N\var(y_{n})+2\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq N}\cov(y_{i},y_{j})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\var(y_{n})+2\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq N}e^{-(j-i-1)kh}\cov(y_{n},y_{n+1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\var(y_{n})+2\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\left[\frac{N-1}{1-e^{-kh}}-\frac{e^{-kh}-e^{-Nkh}}{(1-e^{-kh})^{2}}\right]\cov(y_{n},y_{n+1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\var(y_{n})+\frac{2}{1-e^{-kh}}\cov(y_{n},y_{n+1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\theta h+\theta h\left(\frac{\sigma_{v}^{2}}{4k^{2}}-\frac{\rho\sigma_{v}}{k}\right).

\sigma_{22}
:

We have

\displaystyle\sigma_{22}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\left(N\var(z_{n}^{1})+2\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq N}\cov(z_{i}^{1},z_{j}^{1})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\var(z_{n}^{1})+\frac{\tilde{h}^{2}}{2(1-e^{-2kh})}\cov(z_{n}^{1},v_{n}^{2})

\displaystyle+2\left[\frac{\tilde{h}-he^{-kh}}{1-e^{-kh}}\left(\frac{\sigma_{v}^{2}}{2k^{2}}-\frac{\rho\sigma_{v}}{k}\right)+\frac{1}{k}-\frac{\tilde{h}^{2}(\sigma_{v}^{2}/k+2\theta)}{4(1-e^{-2kh})}\right]\cov(z_{n}^{1},v_{n}),

where the off-diagonal entries of
[\cov(z_{i}^{1},z_{j}^{1})]_{N\times N}
can be calculated as:

\displaystyle\cov(z_{i}^{1},z_{j}^{1})

\displaystyle=\frac{\tilde{h}^{2}}{4}e^{2k(j-i-1)h}\cov(z_{n}^{1},v_{n}^{2})-\frac{\tilde{h}^{2}}{4}e^{2k(j-i-1)h}\left(\frac{\sigma_{v}^{2}}{k}+2\theta\right)\cov(z_{n}^{1},v_{n})

\displaystyle\quad+e^{-k(j-i-1)h}\left[\left(\frac{\sigma_{v}^{2}}{2k^{2}}-\frac{\rho\sigma_{v}}{k}\right)(\tilde{h}-he^{-kh})+\tilde{h}\right]\cov(z_{n}^{1},v_{n}).

\sigma_{33}

We have

\displaystyle\sigma_{33}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\left((N-1)\var(z_{n}^{2})+2\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq N-1}\cov(z_{i}^{2},z_{j}^{2})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\var(z_{n}^{2})+2\cov(z_{n}^{2},z_{n+1}^{2})+2\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{1<i+1<j\leq N-1}\cov(z_{i}^{2},z_{j}^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\var(z_{n}^{2})+2\cov(z_{n}^{2},z_{n+1}^{2})+\frac{\tilde{h}^{2}e^{-kh}}{2(1-e^{-2kh})}\cov(z_{n}^{2},v_{n+1}^{2})

\displaystyle+2\left[\frac{e^{-kh}h\tilde{h}}{1-e^{-kh}}\left(\frac{\sigma_{v}^{2}}{4k}-\frac{\rho\sigma_{v}}{2}\right)-\frac{e^{-kh}\tilde{h}^{2}}{1-e^{-2kh}}\left(\frac{\sigma_{v}^{2}}{4k}+\frac{\theta}{2}\right)\right]\cov(z_{n}^{2},v_{n+1}),

\displaystyle=\frac{\tilde{h}^{2}}{4}e^{-k(2j-2i-3)h}\cov(z_{n}^{2},v_{n+1}^{2})

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{\tilde{h}^{2}}{4}e^{-k(2j-2i-3)h}\left(\frac{\sigma_{v}^{2}}{k}+2\theta\right)\cov(z_{n}^{2},v_{n+1})

\displaystyle\quad+e^{-k(j-i-2)h}e^{-kh}\left(\frac{\sigma_{v}^{2}}{4k}-\frac{\rho\sigma_{v}}{2}\right)h\tilde{h}\cov(z_{n}^{2},v_{n+1}).

We have

\displaystyle\sigma_{44}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\left((N-2)\var(z_{n}^{3})+2\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq N-2}cov(z_{i}^{3},z_{j}^{3})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\var(z_{n}^{3})+2\cov(z_{n}^{3},z_{n+1}^{3})+2\cov(z_{n}^{3},z_{n+2}^{3})

\displaystyle+2\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{2<i+2<j\leq N-2}\cov(z_{i}^{3},z_{j}^{3})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\var(z_{n}^{3})+2\cov(z_{n}^{3},z_{n+1}^{3})+2\cov(z_{n}^{3},z_{n+2}^{3})+\frac{e^{-2kh}\tilde{h}^{2}}{2(1-e^{-2kh})}\cov(z_{n}^{3},v_{n+2}^{2})

\displaystyle+2\left[\frac{e^{-2kh}h\tilde{h}}{1-e^{-kh}}\left(\frac{\sigma_{v}^{2}}{4k}-\frac{\rho\sigma_{v}}{2}\right)-\frac{\tilde{h}^{2}}{1-e^{-2kh}}\left(\frac{\sigma_{v}^{2}}{4k}+\frac{\theta}{2}\right)\right]\cov(z_{n}^{3},v_{n+2}),

\displaystyle\quad-e^{-2k(j-i-3)h}\left(\frac{\sigma_{v}^{2}}{4k}+\frac{\theta}{2}\right)\tilde{h}^{2}\cov(z_{n}^{3},v_{n+2})

\displaystyle\quad+e^{-k(j-i-1)h}\left(\frac{\sigma_{v}^{2}}{4k}-\frac{\rho\sigma_{v}}{2}\right)h\tilde{h}\cov(z_{n}^{3},v_{n+2}).

Remark 3.1
.

Though the final explicit formulas for the entries of
\Sigma
(except
\sigma_{11}
) are not given, they are not difficult to compute, as shown in Appendix B. The complete derivations are quite tedious and the final formulas are lengthy.

Having obtained the moment and covariance estimates, we can now develop the estimators for the parameters. First, let us consider
k
, the mean reversion velocity parameter in the variance (volatility) process. Note that (
18
) leads to

k=\frac{1}{(m-1)h}\ln\left(\frac{\cov(y_{n},y_{n+1})}{\cov(y_{n},y_{n+m})}\right),

for
m=2,3,\cdots
. Therefore, we construct the following estimator for
k
:

\hat{k}=\frac{1}{M-1}\sum_{m=2}^{M}\frac{1}{(m-1)h}\ln\left(\frac{\hat{\cov}(y_{n},y_{n+1})}{\hat{\cov}(y_{n},y_{n+m})}\right),

(26)

where
2\leq M<N
(usually
M
takes a small value, e.g.,
M\leq 10
) and

\displaystyle\hat{\cov}(y_{n},y_{n+m})\triangleq\frac{1}{N-m}\sum_{n=1}^{N-m}(Y_{n}-\overline{Y})(Y_{n+m}-\overline{Y}).

The estimators for the other four parameters are given by

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}

\displaystyle=S^{2}/h-\frac{2(h-\tilde{h}_{\hat{k}})}{h\hat{k}\tilde{h}_{\hat{k}}^{2}}\hat{\cov}(y_{n},y_{n+1}),

(27)

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}

\displaystyle=\overline{Y}/h+\hat{\theta}/2,

(28)

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}_{v}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{4\hat{k}\overline{Y}+[8\hat{d}_{h}/(\hat{\theta}\tilde{h}_{\hat{k}}^{3})]\hat{\cov}(y_{n},y_{n+1})-2\hat{k}\frac{\hat{\cov}(y_{n}^{2},y_{n+1})}{\hat{\cov}(y_{n},y_{n+1})}}{\hat{\theta}\tilde{h}_{\hat{k}}^{2}/(2\hat{\cov}(y_{n},y_{n+1}))-\hat{d}_{h}/(\hat{k}\tilde{h}_{\hat{k}})},

(29)

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}

\displaystyle=\frac{\hat{\sigma}_{v}}{4\hat{k}}-\frac{2}{\hat{\theta}\hat{\sigma}_{v}\tilde{h}_{\hat{k}}^{2}}\hat{\cov}(y_{n},y_{n+1}),

(30)

where
\tilde{h}_{\hat{k}}\triangleq(1-e^{-\hat{k}h})/\hat{k}
and
\hat{d}_{h}\triangleq he^{-\hat{k}h}-\tilde{h}_{\hat{k}}
.

In what follows, we let
M=2
in
\hat{k}
and
denote the corresponding estimators as a continuous mapping
g:\mathbb{R}^{5}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{5}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle g(\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})=(\hat{k},\hat{\theta},\hat{\sigma}_{v},\hat{\mu},\hat{\rho})^{T}.

(Our analysis remains the same for
M>2
.)
We now present the following central limit theorem for the parameter estimators.

Theorem 3.2
.

Suppose
M=2
. Then,

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\sqrt{N}((\hat{k},\hat{\theta},\hat{\sigma}_{v},\hat{\mu},\hat{\rho})^{T}-(k,\theta,\sigma_{v},\mu,\rho)^{T})\overset{d}{=}\mathcal{N}(0,J_{g}\Sigma J_{g}^{T})

(31)

where
J_{g}
is the Jacobian of
g
.

Proof.

It follows directly by applying the delta method (Theorem 3.1 in
Van der Vaart, (2000)
) and Theorem 4.1.
J_{g}
is a
5\times 5
matrix of partial derivatives with respect to the entries of
g
. For instance, the first row of
J_{g}
is the gradient of
k
with respect to
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
, i.e.,

\left(0,0,\frac{1}{h\cov(y_{n},y_{n+1})},-\frac{1}{h\cov(y_{n},y_{n+2})},0\right).

∎

Conclusion

In this paper, we consider the problem of parameter estimation for affine SV models. We first obtain the moments and covariances of the asset price based on which we develop MM estimators. A key of our method is to develop a recursive procedure to calculate the moments and covariances of the asset price. Our MM estimators are simple and easy to implement. We also establish the central limit theorem for our estimators in which the explicit formulas for the asymptotic covariance matrix are given. Finally, we provide numerical results to test and validate our method. The method proposed in this paper can potentially be applied to other SV models. This is a possible future research direction.

Appendix A: Covariance computation

Here we provide some intermediate results omitted in Section 2.

To calculate
\var(y_{n})
, we first note
\cov(IV_{n},I_{n}^{*})=0
,
\cov(v_{n-1},I_{n}^{*})=0
,
\cov(I\!E_{n},I_{n}^{*})=0
, and
\cov(I_{n},I_{n}^{*})=0
. Hence,

\var(y_{n})=\frac{1}{4}\var(IV_{n})-\rho\cov(IV_{n},I_{n})+\rho^{2}\var(I_{n})+(1-\rho^{2})\var(I_{n}^{*}).

First, we note
\var(I_{n}^{*})=\E[(I_{n}^{*})^{2}]=\E[IV_{n}]=\theta h.

We also have

\displaystyle\var(IV_{n})

\displaystyle=\tilde{h}^{2}\var(v_{n-1})+\frac{\sigma_{v}^{2}}{k^{2}}\left(e^{-2knh}\var(I\!E_{n})+\var(I_{n})-2e^{-knh}\cov(I\!E_{n},I_{n})\right).

The four components in the above equation are given by

\displaystyle\var(v_{n-1})

\displaystyle=\frac{\theta\sigma_{v}^{2}}{2k},

\displaystyle\var(I\!E_{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{\theta[e^{2knh}-e^{2k(n-1)h}]}{2k},

\displaystyle\var(I_{n})

\displaystyle=\theta h,

\displaystyle\cov(I_{n},I\!E_{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{\theta[e^{knh}-e^{k(n-1)h}]}{k}.

In what follows, we illustrate how
\cov(I_{n},I\!E_{n})
can be computed in detail. Since

\displaystyle dI_{n-1,t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{v(t)}dw^{v}(t),

\displaystyle dI\!E_{n-1,t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{kt}\sqrt{v(t)}dw^{v}(t),

we have by Itô formula

d(I_{n-1,t}I\!E_{n-1,t})=\left(I\!E_{n-1,t}\sqrt{v(t)}+I_{n-1,t}e^{-kt}\sqrt{v(t)}\right)dw^{v}(t)+e^{kt}v(t)dt.

Note

\int_{(n-1)h}^{t}\left(I\!E_{n-1,s}\sqrt{v(s)}+I_{n-1,s}e^{-ks}\sqrt{v(s)}\right)dw^{v}(s)

is a martingale with zero mean, so

\E[I_{n-1,t}I\!E_{n-1,t}]=\int_{(n-1)h}^{t}e^{ks}\E[v(s)]ds=\frac{\theta[e^{kt}-e^{k(n-1)h}]}{k}.

The interchange of expectation and integration above is guaranteed by Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem. By letting
t=nh
, we can obtain

\cov(I_{n},I\!E_{n})=\E[I_{n}I\!E_{n}]-\E[I_{n}]E[I\!E_{n}]=\E[I_{n}I\!E_{n}]=\frac{\theta[e^{knh}-e^{k(n-1)h}]}{k}.

In summary, we have

\var(IV_{n})=\frac{\theta\sigma_{v}^{2}}{k^{2}}(h-\tilde{h}).

For
\cov(y_{n},y_{n+1})
,
\cov(y_{n},y_{n+2})
, we have

\displaystyle\cov(y_{n},y_{n+1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\cov(IV_{n},IV_{n+1})-\frac{1}{2}\rho\cov(I_{n},IV_{n+1}),

\displaystyle\cov(y_{n},y_{n+2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\cov(IV_{n},IV_{n+2})-\frac{1}{2}\rho\cov(I_{n},IV_{n+2}),

since
\cov(IV_{n},I_{n+i}^{*})=\cov(I_{n}^{*},IV_{n+i})=\cov(I_{n},I_{n+i}^{*})=0
for
i\geq 0
and
\cov(I_{n}^{*},I_{n+i}^{*})=0
for
i\geq 1
.
After some calculations, we can obtain

\displaystyle\cov(IV_{n},IV_{n+1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tilde{h}\cov(IV_{n},v_{n})=\frac{\theta\tilde{h}^{2}\sigma_{v}^{2}}{2k},

\displaystyle\cov(I_{n},IV_{n+1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tilde{h}^{2}\theta\sigma_{v},

\displaystyle\cov(IV_{n},IV_{n+2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-kh}\cov(IV_{n},IV_{n+1}),

\displaystyle\cov(I_{n},IV_{n+2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-kh}\cov(I_{n},IV_{n+1}),

hence, we have (
16
) and (
17
).

Finally, we consider
\cov(y_{n}^{2},y_{n+1})
. First, we have

\displaystyle\cov(y_{n}^{2},y_{n+1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{8}\tilde{h}\cov(IV_{n}^{2},v_{n})-\frac{1}{2}\tilde{h}(1-\rho^{2})\cov((I_{n}^{*})^{2},v_{n})

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\mu h\tilde{h}\cov(IV_{n},v_{n})-\frac{1}{2}\tilde{h}\rho^{2}\cov(I_{n}^{2},v_{n})

\displaystyle-\mu h\tilde{h}\rho\cov(I_{n},v_{n})+\frac{1}{2}\tilde{h}\rho\cov(IV_{n}I_{n},v_{n}).

We have already computed
\cov(IV_{n},v_{n})
earlier. The other covariances can be computed based on the following variances and covariances:

\displaystyle\var(v_{n-1}),

\displaystyle\var(I\!E_{n}),

\displaystyle\cov(I_{n},I\!E_{n}),

\displaystyle\cov(v_{n-1}^{2},v_{n-1}),

\displaystyle\cov(v_{n-1}I\!E_{n},v_{n-1}),

\displaystyle\cov(v_{n-1}I_{n},v_{n-1}),

\displaystyle\cov(v_{n-1}I\!E_{n},I\!E_{n}),

\displaystyle\cov(v_{n-1}I_{n},I\!E_{n}),

\displaystyle\cov(I\!E_{n}^{2},v_{n-1}),

\displaystyle\cov(I\!E_{n}^{2},I\!E_{n}),

\displaystyle\cov(I\!E_{n}I_{n},v_{n-1}),

\displaystyle\cov(I\!E_{n}I_{n},I\!E_{n}),

\displaystyle\cov(I_{n}^{2},v_{n-1}),

\displaystyle\cov(I_{n}^{2},I\!E_{n}),

\displaystyle\cov((I_{n}^{*})^{2},v_{n-1}).

In what follows, we use
\cov(I\!E_{n}I_{n},I\!E_{n})
as an illustrative example to show how these terms can be computed.
Using Itô formula, we have

d(I\!E_{n-1,t}^{2}I_{n-1,t})=\alpha(t)dw^{v}(t)+\beta(t)dt,

where

\displaystyle\alpha(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle I\!E_{n-1,t}^{2}\sqrt{v(t)}+2I_{n-1,t}I\!E_{n-1,t}e^{kt}\sqrt{v(t)},

\displaystyle\beta(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle I_{n-1,t}e^{2kt}v(t)+2I\!E_{n-1,t}e^{kt}v(t).

Therefore,
I\!E_{n-1,t}^{2}I_{n-1,t}
can be expressed as

I\!E_{n-1,t}^{2}I_{n-1,t}=\int_{(n-1)h}^{t}\alpha(s)dw^{v}(s)+\int_{(n-1)h}^{t}\beta(s)ds.

The expectation of the first integral on the right hand side of the above equation is zero, hence

\E[I\!E_{n-1,t}^{2}I_{n-1,t}]=\int_{(n-1)h}^{t}\E[\beta(s)]ds.

(We have again interchanged the expectation and integration operations in the above equation by applying Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem.) We can easily compute
\E[I_{n-1,t}v(t)]
and
\E[I\!E_{n-1,t}v(t)]
in
\E[\beta(t)]
by expanding
v(t)
via (
7
), which gives us

\displaystyle\E[I\!E_{n-1,t}^{2}I_{n-1,t}]

\displaystyle=\frac{\theta\sigma_{v}}{k}\left[\frac{1}{k}e^{2kt}-\frac{1}{k}e^{kt+k(n-1)h}-e^{2k(n-1)h}[t-(n-1)h]\right].

By letting
t=nh
and also noting
E[I\!E_{n}]=0
, we have

\cov(I\!E_{n}I_{n},I\!E_{n})=\E[I\!E_{n}^{2}I_{n}]=\frac{\theta\sigma_{v}}{k^{2}}e^{2knh}(1-e^{-kh}-khe^{-2kh}).

All other variances and covariances can be computed in a similar way.
Moreover, we point out that we can in fact compute the moments and covariances of
any
(positive integer) order and a recursive calculation procedure is provided in Appendix B.

Appendix B: A recursive procedure for computing moments and covariances

Here we discuss how the moments and covariances of
y_{n}
can be calculated. Define

\displaystyle y_{n-1,t}

\displaystyle\triangleq\mu[t-(n-1)h]-\frac{1}{2}IV_{n-1,t}+\rho I_{n-1,t}+\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}I_{n-1,t}^{*},

then

\displaystyle y_{n-1,t}-\E[y_{n-1,t}]

\displaystyle=\beta_{n-1,t}\theta-\beta_{n-1,t}v_{n-1}+\frac{\sigma_{v}}{2k}e^{-kt}I\!E_{n-1,t}+\left(\rho-\frac{\sigma_{v}}{2k}\right)I_{n-1,t}+\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}I_{n-1,t}^{*},

where
\beta_{n-1,t}\triangleq(1-e^{-k[t-(n-1)h]})/(2k)
.
The
l
th central moment of
y_{n-1,t}
, denoted by
\cm_{l}(y_{n-1,t})
, can be computed based on the following quantities:

\E[\theta^{n_{1}}v_{n-1}^{n_{2}}(e^{-kt}I\!E_{n-1,t})^{n_{3}}I_{n-1,t}^{n_{4}}I_{n-1,t}^{*n_{5}}],

(32)

where
n_{i}\geq 0
(
i=1,2,3,4,5
) and
\sum_{i=1}^{5}n_{i}=l
. We can compute (
32
) as in the following two steps:

\E[\theta^{n_{1}}v_{n-1}^{n_{2}}\E[(e^{-kt}I\!E_{n-1,t})^{n_{3}}I_{n-1,t}^{n_{4}}I_{n-1,t}^{*n_{5}}|v_{n-1}]],

i.e., first take expectation conditioning on
v_{n-1}
, and then take expectation w.r.t.
v_{n-1}
. We will show later that the conditional moment
\E[I\!E_{n-1,t}^{n_{3}}I_{n-1,t}^{n_{4}}I_{n-1,t}^{*n_{5}}|v_{n-1}]
is a polynomial of
v_{n-1}
, which implies that (
32
) can be expressed as the moments of
v_{n-1}
.
By using (
8
), we can compute the moment of any order of
v_{n-1}
, hence we can compute (
32
) as well.

Next, we consider
\E[I\!E_{n-1,t}^{n_{3}}I_{n-1,t}^{n_{4}}I_{n-1,t}^{*n_{5}}|v_{n-1}]
. We separate
I\!E_{n-1,t}^{n_{3}}I_{n-1,t}^{n_{4}}I_{n-1,t}^{*n_{5}}
into two parts:
I\!E_{n-1,t}^{n_{3}}I_{n-1,t}^{n_{4}}
and
I_{n-1,t}^{*n_{5}}
, since they are driven by two different Wiener processes
w^{v}(t)
and
w(t)
, respectively. For
I\!E_{n-1,t}^{n_{3}}I_{n-1,t}^{n_{4}}
, we have

\displaystyle d(I\!E_{n-1,t}^{n_{3}}I_{n-1,t}^{n_{4}})

\displaystyle=c_{w}(t)dw^{v}(t)+c(t)dt

where

\displaystyle c_{w}(t)

\displaystyle\triangleq n_{3}I\!E_{n-1,t}^{n_{3}-1}I_{n-1,t}^{n_{4}}\sqrt{v(t)}+n_{4}I\!E_{n-1,t}^{n_{3}}I_{n-1,t}^{n_{4}-1}e^{kt}\sqrt{v(t)},

\displaystyle c(t)

\displaystyle\triangleq\bigg[\frac{1}{2}n_{3}(n_{3}-1)I\!E_{n-1,t}^{n_{3}-2}I_{n-1,t}^{n_{4}}e^{2kt}+\frac{1}{2}n_{4}(n_{4}-1)I\!E_{n-1,t}^{n_{3}}I_{n-1,t}^{n_{4}-2}

\displaystyle\qquad+n_{3}n_{4}I\!E_{n-1,t}^{n_{3}-1}I_{n-1,t}^{n_{4}-1}e^{kt}\bigg]v(t).

The conditional expectation

\E[I\!E_{n-1,t}^{n_{3}}I_{n-1,t}^{n_{4}}|v_{n-1}]=\int_{(n-1)h}^{t}\E[c(s)|v_{n-1}]ds.

Since

\displaystyle v(t)

\displaystyle=e^{k(n-1)h}(v_{n-1}-\theta)e^{-kt}+\theta+\sigma_{v}e^{-kt}I\!E_{n-1,t},

\E[I\!E_{n-1,t}^{n_{3}}I_{n-1,t}^{n_{4}}|v_{n-1}]
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\E[I\!E_{n-1,t}^{n_{3}}I_{n-1,t}^{n_{4}}|v_{n-1}]

\displaystyle=f_{1}(\E[I\!E_{n-1,t}^{n_{3}\boldsymbol{-2}}I_{n-1,t}^{n_{4}}|v_{n-1}],\E[I\!E_{n-1,t}^{n_{3}}I_{n-1,t}^{n_{4}\boldsymbol{-2}}|v_{n-1}],

\displaystyle\qquad~\E[I\!E_{n-1,t}^{n_{3}\boldsymbol{-1}}I_{n-1,t}^{n_{4}\boldsymbol{-1}}|v_{n-1}],\E[I\!E_{n-1,t}^{n_{3}\boldsymbol{-1}}I_{n-1,t}^{n_{4}}|v_{n-1}],

\displaystyle\qquad~\E[I\!E_{n-1,t}^{n_{3}\boldsymbol{+1}}I_{n-1,t}^{n_{4}\boldsymbol{-2}}|v_{n-1}],\E[I\!E_{n-1,t}^{n_{3}}I_{n-1,t}^{n_{4}\boldsymbol{-1}}|v_{n-1}]),

(33)

where
f_{1}
is some function whose details have been left out for simplicity.

For
I_{n-1,t}^{*n_{5}}
, we have

\displaystyle dI_{n-1,t}^{*n_{5}}

\displaystyle=n_{5}I_{n-1,t}^{*n_{5}-1}\sqrt{v(t)}dw(t)+\frac{1}{2}n_{5}(n_{5}-1)I_{n-1,t}^{*n_{5}-2}v(t)dt.

Note that
d(I\!E_{n-1,t}^{n_{3}}I_{n-1,t}^{n_{4}})dI_{n-1,t}^{*n_{5}}=0
because
dw^{v}(t)dw(t)=0
.
Hence,

\displaystyle d(I\!E_{n-1,t}^{n_{3}}I_{n-1,t}^{n_{4}}I_{n-1,t}^{*n_{5}})

\displaystyle=(I\!E_{n-1,t}^{n_{3}}I_{n-1,t}^{n_{4}})dI_{n-1,t}^{*n_{5}}+I_{n-1,t}^{*n_{5}}d(I\!E_{n-1,t}^{n_{3}}I_{n-1,t}^{n_{4}})

\displaystyle=n_{5}I\!E_{n-1,t}^{n_{3}}I_{n-1,t}^{n_{4}}I_{n-1,t}^{*n_{5}-1}\sqrt{v(t)}dw^{s}(t)+c_{w}(t)I_{n-1,t}^{*n_{5}}dw^{v}(t)

\displaystyle\quad+\left[\frac{1}{2}n_{5}(n_{5}-1)I\!E_{n-1,t}^{n_{3}}I_{n-1,t}^{n_{4}}I_{n-1,t}^{*n_{5}-2}v(t)+c(t)I_{n-1,t}^{*n_{5}}\right]dt.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\E[I\!E_{n-1,t}^{n_{3}}I_{n-1,t}^{n_{4}}I_{n-1,t}^{*n_{5}}|v_{n-1}]

\displaystyle=\int_{(n-1)h}^{t}\E\left[\frac{1}{2}n_{5}(n_{5}-1)I\!E_{n-1,s}^{n_{3}}I_{n-1,s}^{n_{4}}I_{n-1,s}^{*n_{5}-2}v(s)+c(s)I_{n-1,s}^{*n_{5}}|v_{n-1}\right]ds.

Again, there exists some function
f_{2}
based on which
\E[I\!E_{n-1,t}^{n_{3}}I_{n-1,t}^{n_{4}}I_{n-1,t}^{*n_{5}}|v_{n-1}]
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\E[I\!E_{n-1,t}^{n_{3}}I_{n-1,t}^{n_{4}}I_{n-1,t}^{*n_{5}}|v_{n-1}]

\displaystyle=f_{2}(\E[I\!E_{n-1,t}^{n_{3}}I_{n-1,t}^{n_{4}}I_{n-1,t}^{*n_{5}\boldsymbol{-2}}|v_{n-1}],\E[I\!E_{n-1,t}^{n_{3}\boldsymbol{+1}}I_{n-1,t}^{n_{4}}I_{n-1,t}^{*n_{5}\boldsymbol{-2}}|v_{n-1}],

\displaystyle\qquad~\E[I\!E_{n-1,t}^{n_{3}\boldsymbol{-2}}I_{n-1,t}^{n_{4}}I_{n-1,t}^{*n_{5}}|v_{n-1}],\E[I\!E_{n-1,t}^{n_{3}}I_{n-1,t}^{n_{4}\boldsymbol{-2}}I_{n-1,t}^{*n_{5}}|v_{n-1}],

\displaystyle\qquad~\E[I\!E_{n-1,t}^{n_{3}\boldsymbol{-1}}I_{n-1,t}^{n_{4}\boldsymbol{-1}}I_{n-1,t}^{*n_{5}}|v_{n-1}],\E[I\!E_{n-1,t}^{n_{3}\boldsymbol{-1}}I_{n-1,t}^{n_{4}}I_{n-1,t}^{*n_{5}}|v_{n-1}],

\displaystyle\qquad~\E[I\!E_{n-1,t}^{n_{3}\boldsymbol{+1}}I_{n-1,t}^{n_{4}\boldsymbol{-2}}I_{n-1,t}^{*n_{5}}|v_{n-1}],\E[I\!E_{n-1,t}^{n_{3}}I_{n-1,t}^{n_{4}\boldsymbol{-1}}I_{n-1,t}^{*n_{5}}|v_{n-1}]),.

(34)

It should be noted that

\E[I_{n-1,t}^{*n_{5}}|v_{n-1}]=\E[I_{n-1,t}^{n_{5}}|v_{n-1}].

(
33
) and (
34
) can be used to compute the central moment of any order of
y_{n-1,t}
recursively, from lower order ones to high order ones. For example, we can start with the combinations
\{(n_{3},n_{4},n_{5}),l=1\}
, then
\{(n_{3},n_{4},n_{5}),l=2\}
, and so on, where
n_{3}+n_{4}+n_{5}=l
. The computations are fairly straightforward but computationally intensive, which can be automated, see
Wu and Hu, (2023)
for a Python implementation.

Appendix D: Extensions to other affine stochastic volatility models

Here we discuss three extensions of the baseline affine SV model studied in the main body of this paper.

Appendix D1: SV model with jumps in the return process

The first extension is the following SV model, which adds a jump component in the log price process of the Heston model:

\displaystyle d\log s(t)

\displaystyle=(\mu-v(t)/2)dt+\sqrt{v(t)}dw^{s}(t)+dz^{s}(t),

\displaystyle dv(t)

\displaystyle=k(\theta-v(t))dt+\sigma_{v}\sqrt{v(t)}dw^{v}(t),

where
z^{s}(t)
is a compound Poisson process and independent of everything else,
N(t)
is the associated Poisson process with rate
\lambda
, and
j
is the jump which is a random variable distributed according to
F_{j}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j})
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j}
is a parameter. For this model,

y_{n}=y_{o,n}+J_{n},

where

\displaystyle y_{o,n}

\displaystyle\triangleq

\displaystyle\mu h-\frac{1}{2}IV_{n}+\rho I_{n}+\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}I_{n}^{*},

\displaystyle J_{n}

\displaystyle\triangleq

\displaystyle\sum_{i=N((n-1)h)+1}^{N(nh)}j_{i}.

In this model, we have two more parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j}
and
\lambda
to estimate. Therefore, we need the following seven equations:

\displaystyle\E[y_{n}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E[y_{o,n}]+E[J_{n}],

\displaystyle\var(y_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\var(y_{o,n})+\var(J_{n}),

\displaystyle\cov(y_{n},y_{n+1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\cov(y_{o,n},y_{o,n+1}),

\displaystyle\cov(y_{n},y_{n+2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-kh}\cov(y_{n},y_{n+1}),

\displaystyle\cov(y_{n}^{2},y_{n+1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\cov(y_{o,n}^{2},y_{o,n+1})+2E[J_{n}]\cov(y_{o,n},y_{o,n+1}),

\displaystyle\cov(y_{n},y_{n+1}^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\cov(y_{o,n},y_{o,n+1}^{2})+2E[J_{n+1}]\cov(y_{o,n},y_{o,n+1}),

\displaystyle\cm_{3}[y_{n}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\cm_{3}[y_{o,n}]+\cm_{3}[J_{n}],

where
\cm_{3}[\cdot]
denotes the third central moment. If
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j}
contains more than one parameter, then more moment equations can be computed.

Appendix D2: SV model with jumps in the variance process

The second extension adds a jump component in the variance process of the Heston model:

\displaystyle d\log s(t)

\displaystyle=(\mu-v(t)/2)dt+\sqrt{v(t)}dw^{s}(t),

\displaystyle dv(t)

\displaystyle=k(\theta-v(t))dt+\sigma_{v}\sqrt{v(t)}dw^{v}(t)+dz^{v}(t),

where
z^{v}(t)
denotes a compound Poisson process with rate
\lambda
and is independent of all other processes. The jump sizes are determined by a random variable,
j
, with distribution
F_{j}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j})
, where
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j}
symbolizes the parameter vector. As in the Heston model, initial variance
v(0)
is assumed to follow the steady state distribution of
v(t)
to ensure the process is strictly stationary.

Applying Itô’s lemma, we derive:

d(e^{kt}v(t))=\theta de^{kt}+\sigma_{v}e^{kt}\sqrt{v(t)}dw^{v}(t)+e^{kt}dz(t).

This leads to:

\displaystyle v(t)-\theta=e^{-kt}(v(0)-\theta)+\sigma_{v}e^{-kt}\int_{0}^{t}e^{ks}\sqrt{v(s)}dw^{v}(s)+e^{-kt}\int_{0}^{t}e^{ks}dz(s).

Given the stationarity of
v(t)
, we have
v(0)\overset{d}{=}v(t)
. Consequently, the distributional equality holds:

(1-e^{-kt})(v(t)-\theta)\overset{d}{=}\sigma_{v}e^{-kt}\int_{0}^{t}e^{ks}\sqrt{v(s)}dw^{v}(s)+e^{-kt}\int_{0}^{t}e^{ks}dz^{v}(s).

Defining the integrated exponential jump terms as
I\!E\!Z_{0,t}\triangleq\int_{0}^{t}e^{ks}dz^{v}(s)
, we can rewrite the equation as:

(1-e^{-kt})(v(t)-\theta)\overset{d}{=}\sigma_{v}e^{-kt}I\!E_{0,t}+e^{-kt}I\!E\!Z_{0,t}.

For an interval
((n-1)h,nh]
, this relationship becomes:

(1-e^{-knh})(v_{n}-\theta)\overset{d}{=}\sigma_{v}e^{-knh}I\!E_{n}+e^{-knh}I\!E\!Z_{n},

where
I\!E\!Z_{n}\triangleq I\!E\!Z_{(n-1)h,nh}
.

The
m
-th moment of
(v_{n}-\theta)
can be calculated using:

\mathbb{E}[(v_{n}-\theta)^{m}]=(1-e^{-knh})^{-m}e^{-mknh}\sum_{i=0}^{m}\binom{m}{i}\sigma_{v}^{i}\mathbb{E}[I\!E_{n}^{i}]\mathbb{E}[I\!E\!Z_{n}^{m-i}],~~m=1,2,\cdots,

(35)

due to the independence between
I\!E_{n}
and
I\!E\!Z_{n}
. The expectation
\mathbb{E}[I\!E_{n}^{i}]
can be computed as detailed in Appendix A. In what follows we demonstrate how to compute the expectation
\mathbb{E}[I\!E\!Z_{n}^{m}]
.

By definition, we have

I\!E\!Z_{n}=\sum_{i=N((n-1)h)+1}^{N(nh)}e^{ks_{i}}j_{i}.

To illustrate, consider the expected value
\E[I\!E\!Z_{1}]
for the interval
(0,h]
. Assume that the compound Poisson process
z(t)
experiences jumps at times
s_{1},s_{2},\cdots,s_{N(h)}
, corresponding to jump sizes
j_{1},j_{2},\cdots,j_{N(h)}
. It is important to note that
s_{1},s_{2},\cdots,s_{N(h)}
are not ordered. Given
N(h)
, these times are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) uniform random variables over the interval
(0,h]
; this is detailed in Theorem 5.2 by
Ross, (2010)
.

Representing a generic uniform random variable over
(0,h]
with
s
, one can interpret
I\!E\!Z_{0,t}
as a variant of the process
z^{v}(t)
with identical Poisson dynamics but with modified jump sizes
e^{ks}j
instead of
j
.

The
m
-th moment of
\E[I\!E\!Z_{1}]
is calculated via:

\E[I\!E\!Z_{1}^{m}]=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda h}\frac{(\lambda h)^{n}}{n!}\E\left[\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{ks_{i}}j_{i}\right)^{m}\right].

(36)

Computing the first moment is straightforward:
\E[I\!E\!Z_{1}]=\lambda h\E[e^{ks}]\E[j]
. The calculation of the second and higher moments is feasible provided the moments of
j
exist, as demonstrated in studies by
Wu et al., (2019)
,
Wu et al., (2022)
and
Yang et al., (2021)
. Consequently, the moments of
v_{n}-\theta
of any (integer) order are determinable through Equations (
35
) and (
36
).

Employing Equation (
35
), the
m
-th moment of
v_{n}
can be calculated as follows:

\E[v_{n}^{m}]=\E[[(v_{n}-\theta)+\theta]^{m}]=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\binom{m}{i}\E[(v_{n}-\theta)^{i}]\theta^{m-i}.

Likewise, the
n
-th interval return
y_{n}
can be decomposed as:

y_{n}=\mu h-\frac{1}{2}IV_{n}+\rho I_{n}+\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}I_{n}^{*}.

The moments and covariances of the returns are computable using the methodology described in Appendix B. We omit the detailed intermediary steps here due to their complexity.

Appendix D3: Multi-factor SV model

We consider the SV model in which the volatility consists of two independent component processes,

\displaystyle d\log s(t)

\displaystyle=(\mu-v(t)/2)dt+\sqrt{v(t)}dw(t),

\displaystyle v(t)

\displaystyle=v_{1}(t)+v_{2}(t),

\displaystyle dv_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=k_{1}(\theta_{1}-v_{1}(t))dt+\sigma_{1v}\sqrt{v_{1}(t)}dw_{1}(t),

\displaystyle dv_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=k_{2}(\theta_{2}-v_{2}(t))dt+\sigma_{2v}\sqrt{v_{2}(t)}dw_{2}(t),

w_{2}(t)

We define

\displaystyle I_{1,n}

\displaystyle\triangleq\int_{(n-1)h}^{nh}\sqrt{v_{1}(t)}dw_{1}(t),

\displaystyle I_{2,n}

\displaystyle\triangleq\int_{(n-1)h}^{nh}\sqrt{v_{2}(t)}dw_{2}(t),

\displaystyle IV_{1,n}

\displaystyle\triangleq\int_{(n-1)h}^{nh}v_{1}(t)dt,

\displaystyle IV_{2,n}

\displaystyle\triangleq\int_{(n-1)h}^{nh}v_{2}(t)dt,

\displaystyle IV_{n}

\displaystyle\triangleq IV_{1,n}+IV_{2,n},

\displaystyle I_{n}^{*}

\displaystyle\triangleq\int_{(n-1)h}^{nh}\sqrt{v(t)}dw(t).

\displaystyle cov(y_{n},y_{n+2})

\displaystyle\cm_{3}[y_{n}]

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{8}\cm_{3}[IV_{1,n}]-\frac{1}{8}\cm_{3}[IV_{2,n}],

\displaystyle\cov(y_{n}^{2},y_{n+1})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{8}\cov(IV_{1,n}^{2},IV_{1,n+1})-\frac{1}{4}(2-2\mu h+\theta_{2}h)\cov(IV_{1,n},IV_{1,n+1})

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1}{8}\cov(IV_{2,n}^{2},IV_{2,n+1})-\frac{1}{4}(2-2\mu h+\theta_{1}h)\cov(IV_{2,n},IV_{2,n+1}),

\displaystyle\cov(y_{n},y_{n+1}^{2})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{8}\cov(IV_{1,n},IV_{1,n+1}^{2})-\frac{1}{4}(2-2\mu h+\theta_{2}h)\cov(IV_{1,n},IV_{1,n+1})

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1}{8}\cov(IV_{2,n},IV_{2,n+1}^{2})-\frac{1}{4}(2-2\mu h+\theta_{1}h)\cov(IV_{2,n},IV_{2,n+1}).

) can be used to compute the intermediate terms
