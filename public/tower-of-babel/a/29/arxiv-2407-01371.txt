Binary Losses for Density Ratio Estimation
Werner Zellinger

Abstract
Estimating the ratio of two probability densities from a finite number of observations is a central machine learning problem. A common approach is to construct estimators using binary classifiers that distinguish observations from the two densities. However, the accuracy of these estimators depends on the choice of the binary loss function, raising the question of which loss function to choose based on desired error properties. For example, traditional loss functions, such as logistic or boosting loss, prioritize accurate estimation of small density ratio values over large ones, even though the latter are more critical in many applications. In this work, we start with prescribed error measures in a class of Bregman divergences and characterize all loss functions that result in density ratio estimators with small error. Our characterization extends results on composite binary losses from ( Reid & Williamson, 2010 ) and their connection to density ratio estimation as identified by ( Menon & Ong, 2016 ) . As a result, we obtain a simple recipe for constructing loss functions with certain properties, such as those that prioritize an accurate estimation of large density ratio values. Our novel loss functions outperform related approaches for resolving parameter choice issues of 11 deep domain adaptation algorithms in average performance across 484 real-world tasks including sensor signals, texts, and images.

The following Theorem

Theorem 1
.

Let
\phi:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
be strictly convex and twice differentiable and let

Then,
\ell:\{-1,1\}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(f)-\mathcal{R}(f^{\ast})=\frac{1}{2}B_{\phi}(\beta,g\circ f),\quad\forall P,Q\in\mathcal{M}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{X}):P\ll Q,\forall f\in L^{1}(Q),

if and only if

\displaystyle\ell(y,\widehat{y})=\begin{cases}\gamma\!\left(\Psi^{-1}(\widehat{y})\right)+\left(1-\Psi^{-1}(\widehat{y})\right)\gamma^{\prime}\!\left(\Psi^{-1}(\widehat{y})\right)&\text{if}\ y=1\\

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{x}\leq\frac{\phi^{\prime\prime\prime}\!\left(x\right)}{\phi^{\prime\prime}\!\left(x\right)}-\frac{\left(g^{-1}\right)^{\prime\prime}\!\left(x\right)}{\left(g^{-1}\right)^{\prime}\!\left(x\right)}\leq 0,\quad\forall x\in[0,\infty).

(9)

Corollary
1
is proven in Subsection
B.3
.
Corollary
1
suggests a
canonical

\displaystyle g_{\mathrm{can}}^{-1}(\widehat{y}):=\phi^{\prime}(\widehat{y})

(10)

for constructing strictly convex loss functions by Eq.
8
.

Remark 3
.

The canonical link
\Psi_{\mathrm{can}}^{\prime}(\weta):=w(\weta)=\weta\ell^{\prime}_{1}(\Psi(\weta))
of
Buja et al. (2005)
(i.e., the matching loss of
Kivinen & Warmuth (1997)
;
Helmbold et al. (1999)
as discussed in
(
Reid & Williamson, 2010
, Section 6.1)
) and the canonical
g_{\mathrm{can}}
do not lead to the same loss function.
To see this, note that the equation
\Psi_{\mathrm{can}}^{-1}(\widehat{y})=\frac{g(\widehat{y})}{1+g(\widehat{y})}
in Theorem
1
leads to

\left(g^{-1}\right)^{\prime}\!(z)=\Psi_{\mathrm{can}}^{\prime}\!\left(\frac{z}{1+z}\right)=w\!\left(\frac{z}{1+z}\right).

Buja et al. (2005)
show that differentiating a second time Eq.
20
gives
w(\weta)=-\BR^{\prime\prime}(\weta)
and therefore

\left(g^{-1}\right)^{\prime}\!(z)=-\BR^{\prime\prime}\!\left(\frac{z}{1+z}\right)=-\gamma^{\prime\prime}\!\left(\frac{z}{1+z}\right)=\phi^{\prime\prime}(z)(1+z)^{3}

which satisfies Eq.
9
but differs from
\left(g^{-1}_{\mathrm{can}}\right)^{\prime}=\phi^{\prime\prime}(z)
by a factor
(1+z)^{3}
.

Thanks to Theorem
1
and Corollary
1
, given a strictly convex Bregman generator
\phi
, we are now able to express the Bregman divergence
B_{\phi}(\beta,g_{\mathrm{can}}\circ\widehat{f})
as excess risk of a
canonical
convex loss function
\ell_{\mathrm{can}}
defined by Eq.
8
and
g_{\mathrm{can}}
.
Instantiating Algorithm
1
by
\ell_{\mathrm{can}}
and
\Psi^{-1}(\widehat{y})=\frac{g_{\mathrm{can}}(\widehat{y})}{1+g_{\mathrm{can}}(\widehat{y})}
, we arrive at minimizing
B_{\phi}(\beta,\widehat{\beta})
for
\widehat{\beta}=g_{\mathrm{can}}\circ\widehat{f}
.

5
Loss Functions for Estimating Large Density Ratios

It has been observed by
Menon & Ong (2016)
that no loss function in Example
1
prioritizes an accurate estimation of large density ratio values over smaller values.
We show this behavior in Subsection
A.1
which uses the fact that the second derivative
\phi^{\prime\prime}
of the Bregman generator
\phi
satisfies the relation

\displaystyle B_{\phi}(\beta,\widehat{\beta})=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim Q}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi^{\prime\prime}(c)\phi_{c}(\beta(x),\widehat{\beta}(x))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}c\right]

(11)

for the piecewise linear function
\phi_{c}
defined in Eq.
16
that does not depend on
\phi
.
It can be seen that the weight functions
\phi^{\prime\prime}(c)=\frac{1}{c(1+c)}
(LR),
\phi^{\prime\prime}(c)=c^{-1}
(KLest),
\phi^{\prime\prime}(c)=c^{-3/2}
(Exp) and
\phi^{\prime\prime}(c)=1
(KuLSIF) of Example
1
are non-increasing and therefore do not realize a prioritization of estimating large values.
In contrast, we propose the following novel Bregman divergences as error measures for Algorithm
1
, which prioritize an accurate estimation of large values, see Figure
1
for an illustration.

The exponential weight (EW)
\phi^{\prime\prime}(c):=e^{2c}
and
g_{\mathrm{can}}^{-1}:=\phi^{\prime}
realize the novel Bregman divergence

\displaystyle B_{\phi}(\beta,\widehat{\beta})=\frac{1}{4}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim Q}\!\left[e^{2\beta(x)}+e^{2\widehat{\beta}(x)}(-1-2\beta(x)+2\widehat{\beta}(x))\right].

(12)

Plugging
\phi
and
g_{\mathrm{can}}
in Eq.
8
gives the loss functions
\ell(1,y)=-y
,
\ell(-1,y)=\frac{1}{2}y(\log(2y)-1)
and
\widehat{\beta}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\log(2\widehat{f}(x))
, which satisfy Eq.
7
according to Theorem
1
.

The polynomial weight functions
\phi^{\prime\prime}(c):=c^{2k}
for
k\in\{0,1,2,3,\ldots\}
and
g_{\mathrm{can}}^{-1}:=\phi^{\prime}
in Eq.
10
realize the Bregman divergences

\displaystyle B_{\phi}(\beta,\widehat{\beta})=\frac{1}{(1+k)(2+k)}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim Q}\!\left[\beta(x)^{2+k}-(2+k)\beta(x)\widehat{\beta}(x)^{1+k}+(1+k)\widehat{\beta}(x)^{2+k}\right].

(13)

Plugging
\phi
and
g_{\mathrm{can}}
in Eq.
8
gives the loss functions
\ell(1,y)=-y
and
{\ell(-1,y)=\frac{((1+k)y)^{\frac{2+k}{1+k}}}{2+k}}
and the estimators
{\widehat{\beta}(x)=((1+k)\widehat{f}(x))^{\frac{1}{1+k}}}
.
Note that the class of divergences in Eq.
13
can be interpreted to extend KuLSIF, which realizes Eq.
13
with
k=0
, to larger exponents.

6.1
Numerical Illustrations

Before showing the potential of our novel loss functions on real world problems in Subsection
6.2
, we illustrate the behavior of our methods on two experiments: Experiment (a) on the form of kernel estimators for a one-dimensional Gaussian density ratio and Experiment (b) on the behavior of the (analytically computed) density ratio estimators used as importance weights in kernel regression.

Experiment (a):
Figure

instantiated with the loss functions for KuLSIF (see Example

The function class
\mathcal{F}
was fixed to a Gaussian RKHS with bandwidth parameter computed by the median heuristic.
We also added a Tikhonov penalty

to Eq.

and computed the solution for different values of

by the Broyden-Fletcher-Goldfarb-Shanno (BFGS) algorithm, see, e.g.,
(

.

The equal weighting of all density ratio values in KuLSIF leads to exploding estimates for small sample size
m+n=10
with small regularization parameter
\alpha\in\{10^{-6},10^{-4}\}
.
This problem does not appear for our loss function, which comes, however, at the cost of ”flatter” estimates due to a higher penalization of errors in larger density ratio values.

Experiment (b):
Figure

extends the setting of Figure

to unsupervised domain adaptation under covariate shift
(

)
:

from

together with labels

corrupted by iid Gaussian noise observations
(\epsilon_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
and unlabeled iid

observations
(x_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
from

, the goal is to estimate the unknown Bayes predictor
f_{P}(x)=\sin(3x^{4})

This is done by importance weighted kernel least squares regression

\displaystyle\widehat{f}:=\argmin_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\frac{1}{m+n}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\widehat{\beta}(x_{i}^{\prime})\left\lVert y_{i}^{\prime}-f(x_{i}^{\prime})\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\alpha\lVert f\rVert_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}

(14)

with
\widehat{\beta}
of Figure

for LR and our method with exponential weight
\phi^{\prime\prime}(c)=e^{2c}
.
We used a degree-five polynomial RKHS
\mathcal{F}

\alpha=10^{-32}
and computed the solution by BFGS optimizing the weights of the solution granted by the representer theorem
Kimeldorf & Wahba (1970)
.

Our weight
\phi^{\prime\prime}(c)=e^{2c}
is increasing, which leads to an error smaller than
0.040
in squared

-norm.

In contrast, the weight function of LR is decreasing, which leads to an error larger than

L^{2}(P)
-norm.
The higher performance of our method in squared
L^{2}(P)
-norm comes at the cost of a higher squared
L^{2}(Q)
-norm (
0.798
) than for LR (
0.778
).
However, we note that in unsupervised domain adaptation we are interested in the target excess risk on
P
which is nothing but the squared
L^{2}(P)
-norm.

6.2
Real-World Datasets

In the following, we test the performance of our exponential weighted Bregman divergence in Eq.
12
compared to the performace of LR, KuLSIF and Boost (see Example
1
) for calculating importance weights for parameter selection methods in unsupervised domain adaptation.
In total, we test the performance on 484 parameter selection tasks; one for each combination of a parameter selection method, a domain adaptation method (from three datasets) and a domain adaptation tasks (originating from three datasets). We trained 9174 neural networks in total, see Section
C
for details.

The unsupervised domain adaptation setting:
given iid
source
observations
(x_{i}^{\prime})_{i=1}^{m}
from
Q
together with labels
y_{i}^{\prime}=f_{P}(x_{i}^{\prime})+\epsilon_{i}
corrupted by
general
iid noise observations
(\epsilon_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
and unlabeled iid
target
observations
(x_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
from
P
, the goal is to estimate the
generally unknown
Bayes predictor
f_{P}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{R}^{d_{y}}
,
d_{y}\in\mathbb{N}
.

Domain adaptation methods:

The goal is to select parameters from the methods Adversarial Spectral Kernel Matching (AdvSKM)
(
Liu & Xue, 2021
)
, Deep Domain Confusion (DDC)
(
Tzeng et al., 2014
)
, Correlation Alignment (DCoral)
(
Sun et al., 2017
;
Nikzad-Langerodi et al., 2019
)
, Central Moment Discrepancy (CMD)
(
Zellinger et al., 2017
;
Zellinger et al., 2019
)
, Higher-order Moment Matching (HoMM)
(
Chen et al., 2020
)
, Minimum Discrepancy Estimation for Deep Domain Adaptation (MMDA)
(
Rahman et al., 2020
)
, Deep Subdomain Adaptation (DSAN)
(
Zhu et al., 2021
)
, Domain-Adversarial Neural Networks (DANN)
(
Ganin et al., 2016
)
, Conditional Adversarial Domain Adaptation (CDAN)
(
Long et al., 2018
)
, DIRT-T (DIRT)
(
Shu et al., 2018
)
and Convolutional Deep Domain Adaptation specifically suitable for time-series (CoDATS)
(
Wilson et al., 2020
)
.

Parameter selection methods:

We select these parameters by
Importance Weighted (Cross-)Validation (IWV)
(
Sugiyama et al., 2007
)
and Importance Weighted Aggregation (IWA)
(
Dinu et al., 2023
)
.
Both methods, IWV and IWA, first compute several models
f_{1},\ldots,f_{l}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}
, one for each parameter setting to try, and second compute the final model
\widehat{f}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}
by Eq.
14
for
\mathcal{F}=\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{l}\}
and
\mathcal{F}=\{\sum_{k=1}^{l}c_{k}\cdot f_{k}|c_{1},\ldots,c_{k}\in\mathbb{R}\}
for IWV and IWA, respectively.
Since Eq.
14
relies on an accurate estimate of
\beta=\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}P}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}Q}
also IWV and IWA crucially depend on such an estimate.

Datasets and tasks:

We rely on three domain adaptation datasets with several domains and tasks.
The first dataset is the
AmazonReviews
dataset from
Blitzer et al. (2006)
which consists of text reviews from four domains: books (B), DVDs (D), electronics (E), and kitchen appliances (K).
The reviews are encoded in feature vectors of bag-of-words unigrams and bigrams with binary labels indicating the sentiment of the rankings.
From the four domains we
obtain twelve domain adaptation tasks (B
\rightarrow
D, D
\rightarrow
E,…) where each domain serves once as source domain and once
as target domain.
The second dataset is the
Heterogeneity Human Activity Recognition dataset
(HHAR) from
Stisen et al. (2015)
which investigates
sensor-, device- and workload-specific heterogeneities from 36 smartphones and smartwatches,
consisting of 13 different device models from four manufacturers.
We follow
Ragab et al. (2023)
and obtain five domain adaptation tasks.
Our third dataset is the
MiniDomainNet
dataset from
Zellinger et al. (2021)
which is a reduced version of the large scale
DomainNet-2019
from
Peng et al. (2019)
consisting of six
different image domains (Quickdraw, Real, Clipart, Sketch, Infograph, and Painting).
MiniDomainNet reduces the number of classes of DomainNet-2019 to the
top-five largest representatives in the training set of each class across all six domains.
MiniDomainNet is a multi-source single target dataset for which we obtain five tasks, one task for each domain chosen as target domain (and all others used as source domains).

Density ratio estimation methods:

1
initialized by our loss function corresponding to Eq.
12
and the other loss functions from Example

excluding SQ which did not perform well in our experiments and also not in the one of
Gruber et al. (2024)
.
Eq.
2
is optimized with the BFGS algorithm (100 iterations) in a Gaussian RKHS with bandwidth chosen by the median heuristic and Tikhonov regularization with parameter
\alpha\in\{10,0.1,10^{-3}\}
chosen by cross-validation following
Gruber et al. (2024)
.

7
Conclusion and Future Work

A large class of methods for density ratio estimation minimizes a Bregman divergence between the true density ratio and an estimator.
However, the Bregman divergence is only implicitly determined and depends on the choice of a binary loss function used to instantiate the algorithm that underlies this class of methods.
In this work, we derived the
unique
formula of a (strictly proper composite convex) loss function for an explicitly given Bregman divergence that we want to minimize.
Our formula allows us to construct novel loss functions with the property of focusing on estimating large density ratio values, which is in contrast to related approaches.
We provide numerical illustrations and applications in deep domain adaptation.
The sample complexity of our methods is, to the best of our knowledge, an open problem.

A.1
Weight Representation of Bregman Divergences

The second derivative
\phi^{\prime\prime}
of the Bregman generator
\phi
of Eq.
1
can be seen as a weight of the Bregman divergence as follows.

Lemma 2

(
Reid & Williamson (2009)
)
.

Let
\phi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be strictly convex and twice differentiable.
Then

\displaystyle B_{\phi}(\beta,\widehat{\beta})=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim Q}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi^{\prime\prime}(c)\phi_{c}(\beta(x),\widehat{\beta}(x))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}c\right]

(15)

with

\displaystyle\phi_{c}(r,\widehat{r}):=\begin{cases}(r-c)&\text{if}\ \widehat{r}<c\leq r\\
(c-r)&\text{if}\ r<c\leq\widehat{r}\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}.

(16)

For the convenience of the reader, we provide a proof of Lemma
2
based on Taylor’s theorem.

Proof of Lemma
2
.

For any
s_{0},s\in[a,b]\subset\mathbb{R}
and
\phi:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}
, Taylor’s theorem grants us the expansion (cf.
Reid & Williamson (2009, Corollary 7)
)

\displaystyle\phi(s)=\phi(s_{0})+\phi^{\prime}(s_{0})(s-s_{0})+\int_{a}^{b}\phi_{c}(s,s_{0})\phi^{\prime\prime}(c)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}c.

(17)

It follows that

\displaystyle B_{\phi}(\beta,\widehat{\beta})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim Q}\left[\phi(\beta(x))-\phi(\widehat{\beta}(x))-\phi^{\prime}(\widehat{\beta}(x))(\beta(x)-\widehat{\beta}(x))\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim Q}\left[\int_{\widehat{\beta}(x)\wedge\beta(x)}^{\widehat{\beta}(x)\vee\beta(x)}\phi_{c}(\beta(x),\widehat{\beta}(x))\phi^{\prime\prime}(c)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}c\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim Q}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi_{c}(\beta(x),\widehat{\beta}(x))\phi^{\prime\prime}(c)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}c\right].

∎

Lemma
2
illustrates a prioritization of large values
r_{2}\geq r_{1}
over smaller ones for increasing weight function
\phi^{\prime\prime}
:

\displaystyle d_{\phi}(r_{2},r_{2}-\Delta)\geq d_{\phi}(r_{1},r_{1}-\Delta)

(18)

for the distance

\displaystyle d_{\phi}(s,s_{0}):=\phi(s)-\phi(s_{0})-\phi^{\prime}(s_{0})(s-s_{0})

that determines
B_{\phi}(\beta,\widehat{\beta})=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim Q}[d_{\phi}(\beta(x),\widehat{\beta}(x))]
.
Eq.
18
follows from Lemma
2
and the substitution
\varphi(x):=x+r_{1}-r_{2}
,

\displaystyle d_{\phi}(r_{1},r_{1}-\Delta)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi_{c}(r_{1},r_{1}-\Delta)\phi^{\prime\prime}(c)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}c

\displaystyle=\int_{r_{1}-\Delta}^{r_{1}}(r_{1}-c)\phi^{\prime\prime}(c)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}c

\displaystyle=\int_{\varphi(r_{2}-\Delta)}^{\varphi(r_{2})}(r_{1}-c)\phi^{\prime\prime}(c)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}c

\displaystyle=\int_{r_{2}-\Delta}^{r_{2}}\phi^{\prime\prime}(\varphi(c))(r_{1}-\varphi(c))\varphi^{\prime}(c)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}c

\displaystyle=\int_{r_{2}-\Delta}^{r_{2}}\phi^{\prime\prime}(c+r_{1}-r_{2})(r_{2}-c)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}c

\displaystyle\leq\int_{r_{2}-\Delta}^{r_{2}}\phi^{\prime\prime}(c)(r_{2}-c)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}c

\displaystyle=d_{\phi}(r_{2},r_{2}-\Delta),

where the inequality holds for increasing
\phi^{\prime\prime}(c-(r_{2}-r_{1}))\leq\phi^{\prime\prime}(c)
.

A.2
Characterization of Proper Losses

For convenience of the reader, in the following, we provide self-inclusive proofs of all mathematical statements used to prove our results.

Menon & Ong (2016)
discovered a connection
8
8

8

The relation
\phi^{\prime\prime}(c)=\frac{1}{(1+c)^{3}}w\!\left(\frac{c}{1+c}\right)
is proven in
(
Menon & Ong, 2016
, Lemma 4)
with
w
as defined in our Theorem
2
.

between
\phi^{\prime\prime}
in Lemma
2
and the weight in a fundamental characterization of proper loss functions due to
Shuford Jr et al. (1966, Theorem 1)
(cf.
Aczél & Pfanzagl (1967)
;
Staël von Holstein (1970)
), which we re-state here following
Buja et al. (2005)
;
Reid & Williamson (2010)
.

Theorem 2

(
Shuford Jr et al. (1966, Theorem 1)
)
.

A loss function
\lambda:\{-1,1\}\times[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
with differentiable partial losses
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{-1}
is strictly proper if and only if for all
\eta\in(0,1)

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{1}^{\prime}(\eta)}{\eta-1}=\frac{\lambda^{\prime}_{-1}(\eta)}{\eta}=w(\eta)

(19)

for some weight function
w:(0,1)\to[0,\infty)
such that
\int_{\epsilon}^{1-\epsilon}w(c)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}c<\infty
for all
\epsilon>0
.

Proof of Theorem
2
.

If
\lambda
is proper, then
\CR(\eta,\eta)=\inf_{\widehat{\eta}\in(0,1)}\CR(\eta,\widehat{\eta})
, which implies that

0=\frac{\partial}{\partial\widehat{\eta}}\CR(\eta,\widehat{\eta})\Bigr|_{\widehat{\eta}=\eta}=(1-\eta)\lambda_{-1}^{\prime}(\eta)+\eta\lambda_{1}^{\prime}(\eta)

and consequently also Eq.
19
.

Eq.
19
gives

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\widehat{\eta}}\CR(\eta,\widehat{\eta})

\displaystyle=(1-\eta)\lambda_{-1}^{\prime}(\widehat{\eta})+\eta\lambda_{1}^{\prime}(\widehat{\eta})

\displaystyle=\eta(\widehat{\eta}-1)w(\widehat{\eta})+(1-\eta)\widehat{\eta}w(\widehat{\eta})

\displaystyle=(\widehat{\eta}-\eta)w(\widehat{\eta}),

(20)

which provides us a unique minimum
\CR(\eta,\eta)=\inf_{\widehat{\eta}\in(0,1)}\CR(\eta,\widehat{\eta})
since
w(\eta)>0
.
∎

Another fundamental characterization of proper loss functions is due to
Savage (1971)
, which we re-state here in the notation of
(
Reid & Williamson, 2010
, Theorem 4)
.

Theorem 3

(
Savage (1971)
)
.

A loss function
\lambda:\{-1,1\}\times[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
is proper if and only if its pointwise Bayes risk
\BR
is concave and for each
\eta\in[0,1]
and
\widehat{\eta}\in(0,1)

\displaystyle\CR(\eta,\widehat{\eta})=\BR(\widehat{\eta})+(\eta-\widehat{\eta})\BR^{\prime}(\widehat{\eta}).

(21)

The next proof of Theorem
3
is given by
Reid & Williamson (2009)
.

Proof of Theorem
3
.

To prove the first direction, note that the properness of the loss
\lambda
means that

\CR(\eta,\eta)=\BR(\eta):=\inf_{\widehat{\eta}\in[0,1]}\CR(\eta,\widehat{\eta})

for all
\eta\in[0,1]
.
Expanding the derivative of the pointwise Bayes risk gives

\displaystyle\BR^{\prime}(\eta)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}\CR(\eta,\eta)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}\left((1-\eta)\lambda(-1,\eta)+\eta\lambda(1,\eta)\right)

\displaystyle=-\lambda(-1,\eta)+(1-\eta)\lambda^{\prime}(-1,\eta)+\lambda(1,\eta)+\eta\lambda^{\prime}(1,\eta)

\displaystyle=-\lambda(-1,\eta)+\lambda(1,\eta),

where the last equality follows from the fact that

\displaystyle 0=\frac{\partial}{\partial\widehat{\eta}}\CR(\eta,\widehat{\eta})\Bigr|_{\widehat{\eta}=\eta}=(1-\eta)\lambda^{\prime}(-1,\eta)+\eta\lambda^{\prime}(1,\eta).

Eq.
21
can now be obtained as follows:

\displaystyle\BR(\widehat{\eta})+(\eta-\widehat{\eta})\BR^{\prime}(\widehat{\eta})

\displaystyle=(1-\widehat{\eta})\lambda(-1,\widehat{\eta})+\widehat{\eta}\lambda(1,\widehat{\eta})+(\eta-\widehat{\eta})(-\lambda(-1,\widehat{\eta})+\lambda(1,\widehat{\eta}))

\displaystyle=(1-\eta)\lambda(-1,\widehat{\eta})+\eta\lambda(1,\widehat{\eta})

where the last line is just the definition of
\CR(\eta,\widehat{\eta})
.

To prove the other direction, assume that
\phi:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
is a concave function and

\displaystyle\CR(\eta,\widehat{\eta})=\phi(\widehat{\eta})+(\eta-\widehat{\eta})\phi^{\prime}(\widehat{\eta}).

Taylor’s theorem grants us the expansion (cf.
(
Reid & Williamson, 2009
, Corollary 7)
)

\displaystyle\phi(\eta)=\phi(\widehat{\eta})+\phi^{\prime}(\widehat{\eta})(\eta-\widehat{\eta})+\int_{0}^{1}\phi_{c}(\eta,\widehat{\eta})\phi^{\prime\prime}(c)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}c.

for
\phi_{c}
as defined in Lemma
2
.
It follows that

\displaystyle\CR(\eta,\widehat{\eta})=\phi(\eta)-\int_{0}^{1}\phi_{c}(\eta,\widehat{\eta})\phi^{\prime\prime}(c)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}c\geq\phi(\widehat{\eta})

(22)

since
\phi
is concave and
\phi^{\prime\prime}(\eta)\leq 0
. The integral in Eq.
22
is minimized to zero in the case of
\widehat{\eta}=\eta
.
∎

A.3
Bregman Divergence Representation of Excess Risk

To prove Lemma
1
, we will need the following Bregman identity.

Lemma 3

(
Menon & Ong (2016, Lemma 2)
)
.

Let
\phi:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
be twice differentiable strictly convex and denote by
d_{\phi}(y,\widehat{y}):=\phi(y)-\phi(\widehat{y})-\phi^{\prime}(\widehat{y})(y-\widehat{y})
.
Then, for all
x,y\in[0,\infty)
, it holds that

\displaystyle(1+x)\cdot d_{\phi}\!\left(\frac{x}{1+x},\frac{y}{1+y}\right)=d_{\phi^{\diamond}}(x,y),

(23)

where
\phi^{\diamond}(z):=(1+z)\cdot\phi\!\left(\frac{z}{1+z}\right)
.

Proof of Lemma
3

(
Menon & Ong, 2016
, Section 5.1)
.

Eq.
17
gives, for any
s_{0},s\in\mathbb{R}
:

\displaystyle d_{\phi}(s,s_{0})=\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi_{c}(s,s_{0})\phi^{\prime\prime}(c)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}c

with
\phi_{c}(s,s_{0})=|s-c|\cdot\llbracket(s-c)(s_{0}-c)<0\rrbracket
, where
\llbracket\circ\rrbracket
is
1
if the statement
\circ
is true and it is
0
otherwise.
For
s_{0}:=\frac{y}{1+y}
and
s:=\frac{x}{1+x}
, we obtain

\displaystyle d_{\phi}\!\left(\frac{x}{1+x},\frac{y}{1+y}\right)=\int^{\frac{x\vee y}{1+x\vee y}}_{\frac{y\wedge x}{1+y\wedge x}}\left|\frac{x}{1+x}-z\right|\cdot\phi^{\prime\prime}(z)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z.

Applying the substitution
z=\frac{u}{1+u}
with
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z=\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u}{(1+u)^{2}}
gives

\displaystyle d_{\phi}\!\left(\frac{x}{1+x},\frac{y}{1+y}\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{y\wedge x}^{x\vee y}\left|\frac{x}{1+x}-\frac{u}{1+u}\right|\cdot\phi^{\prime\prime}\!\left(\frac{u}{1+u}\right)\frac{1}{(1+u)^{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\int_{y\wedge x}^{x\vee y}\frac{\left|x-u\right|}{(1+x)(1+u)}\cdot\phi^{\prime\prime}\!\left(\frac{u}{1+u}\right)\frac{1}{(1+u)^{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+x}\int_{y\wedge x}^{x\vee y}\left|x-u\right|\cdot\phi^{\prime\prime}\!\left(\frac{u}{1+u}\right)\frac{1}{(1+u)^{3}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+x}d_{\phi^{\diamond}}(x,y),

where the last line uses

\displaystyle\left(\phi^{\diamond}\right)^{\prime}(z)=\frac{1}{1+x}\phi^{\prime}\!\left(\frac{z}{1+z}\right)+\phi\!\left(\frac{z}{1+z}\right)~\text{and}~\left(\phi^{\diamond}\right)^{\prime\prime}(z)=\phi^{\prime\prime}\!\left(\frac{z}{1+z}\right)\frac{1}{(1+z)^{3}}.

∎

We are now ready to provide a proof of Lemma
1
.
For convenience of the reader, we don’t just repeat the arguments of
Menon & Ong (2016, Proposition 3)
, but we also include the required basis arguments of
Reid & Williamson (2009)
and
Reid & Williamson (2010)
.

Proof of Lemma
1
.

The properness of
\ell
and Theorem
3
imply

\displaystyle\CR(\eta,\widehat{y})=\BR(\Psi^{-1}(\widehat{y}))+(\eta-\Psi^{-1}(\widehat{y}))\BR^{\prime}(\Psi^{-1}(\widehat{y})).

(24)

Denoting by
\eta(x):=\rho(y=1|x)
and following
(
Reid & Williamson, 2010
, Corollary 13)
, the excess risk can be expressed as an expected Bregman divergence as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(f)\,-

\displaystyle\,\mathcal{R}(f^{\ast})=

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\rho}\left[\ell\!\left(y,f(x)\right)\right]-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\rho}\left[\ell(y,f^{\ast}(x))\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\rho_{\mathcal{X}}}\left[\CR\!\left(\eta(x),f(x)\right)\right]-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\rho_{\mathcal{X}}}\left[\BR(\eta(x))\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\rho_{\mathcal{X}}}\left[\BR\!\left(\Psi^{-1}(f(x))\right)+(\eta(x)-\Psi^{-1}(f(x)))\BR^{\prime}\!\left(\Psi^{-1}(f(x))\right)-\BR(\eta(x))\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\rho_{\mathcal{X}}}\left[d_{-\BR}\!\left(\eta(x),\Psi^{-1}(f(x))\right)\right]

(25)

with
d_{\phi}
as defined in Lemma
3
.
Note that the Bregman generator
-\BR
is strictly convex, since
\BR
is concave due to Theorem
3
and it is strictly concave due to the uniqueness of the minimum
\BR(\eta)=\min_{\widehat{y}\in\mathbb{R}}\CR(\eta,\widehat{y})
.

Following the proof of
Menon & Ong (2016, Proposition 3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(f)-\mathcal{R}(f^{\ast})=\frac{1}{2}B_{-\BR^{\diamond}}(\beta,g\circ f)=\frac{1}{2}B_{-\gamma^{\diamond}}(\beta,g\circ f)=\frac{1}{2}B_{\phi}(\beta,g\circ f)

because
\gamma^{\diamond}(z)=\phi(z)-c_{2}z-c_{1}
, the fact that Bregman divergences are unique up to affine terms of the generator and the equality
g(y)=\frac{\Psi^{-1}(y)}{1-\Psi^{-1}(y)}
which follows from the definition of
\Psi^{-1}
.

For proving necessity of Eq.
8
assume
\ell
is a strictly proper composite loss function with invertible link function
\Psi:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying Eq.
7
.
From
\Psi^{-1}(\widehat{y})=\frac{g(\widehat{y})}{1+g(\widehat{y})}
we obtain
g(\widehat{y})=\frac{\Psi^{-1}(\widehat{y})}{1-\Psi^{-1}(\widehat{y})}
.
That is, all requirements for Lemma
5
are satisfied and it’s application gives, for all
\widehat{\eta}\in[0,1]
,

\displaystyle\BR(\widehat{y})=\gamma\!\left(\Psi^{-1}(\widehat{y})\right).

(43)

Next, we construct a strictly proper loss function in the same way as it is done in
(
Reid & Williamson, 2010
, Theorem 7)
and
(
Bao, 2023
, Corollary 3)
.
We use Theorem
3
to obtain

\displaystyle\CR\!\left(\eta,\widehat{y}\right)=\gamma(\Psi^{-1}(\widehat{y}))+\left(\eta-\Psi^{-1}(\widehat{y})\right)\gamma^{\prime}\!\left(\Psi^{-1}(\widehat{y})\right)

for all
\eta\in[0,1]
and
\widehat{y}\in\mathbb{R}
, which can be applied since
\Psi^{-1}(\widehat{y})\in(0,1)
as
g
is strictly monotonically increasing.
The form of the loss function in Eq.
8
follows now from the definition

\displaystyle\CR\!\left(\eta,\widehat{y}\right)

\displaystyle=\eta\ell(1,\widehat{y})+(1-\eta)\ell(-1,\widehat{y}),

for
\eta=0
and
\eta=1
.
∎

B.3
Characterization of Convex Losses

Next, we prove our characterization of convex losses by properties of
\phi,g
.

Proof of Corollary
1
.

Our aim is to apply Theorem
4
.
In the following denote by
\weta:=\Psi^{-1}(\widehat{y})
.
Applying Theorem
2
with
w
as defined in Eq.
19
gives

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\widehat{y}}\CR(\eta,\widehat{y})

\displaystyle=(1-\eta)\ell_{-1}^{\prime}(\widehat{y})+\eta\ell_{1}^{\prime}(\widehat{y})

\displaystyle=\eta\left(\weta-1\right)w\!\left(\weta\right)+(1-\eta)\weta w\!\left(\weta\right)

\displaystyle=(\weta-\eta)w\!\left(\weta\right)

and, together with Theorem
3
, we have

\displaystyle w(\weta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\eta-\weta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\widehat{y}}\CR(\eta,\widehat{y})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\eta-\weta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\widehat{y}}\left(\BR(\weta)+(\eta-\weta)\BR^{\prime}(\weta)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\eta-\weta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\widehat{y}}\left(\BR^{\prime}(\weta)-\BR^{\prime}(\weta)+(\eta-\weta)\BR^{\prime\prime}(\weta)\right)

\displaystyle=-\BR^{\prime\prime}(\weta)

\displaystyle=-\gamma^{\prime\prime}(\weta),

where the last equality follows from Lemma
5
.
Note that

\displaystyle\gamma^{\prime}(\weta)

\displaystyle=\phi\!\left(\frac{\weta}{1-\weta}\right)+\frac{1}{\weta-1}\phi^{\prime}\!\left(\frac{\weta}{1-\weta}\right)+c_{1}

\displaystyle\gamma^{\prime\prime}(\weta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(\weta-1)^{3}}\phi^{\prime\prime}\!\left(\frac{\weta}{1-\weta}\right)

\displaystyle\gamma^{\prime\prime\prime}(\weta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(\weta-1)^{5}}\phi^{\prime\prime\prime}\!\left(\frac{\weta}{1-\weta}\right)-\frac{3}{(\weta-1)^{4}}\phi^{\prime\prime}\!\left(\frac{\weta}{1-\weta}\right)

and

\displaystyle\Psi(\weta)

\displaystyle=g^{-1}\!\left(\frac{\weta}{1-\weta}\right)

\displaystyle\Psi^{\prime}(\weta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1-\weta)^{2}}\left(g^{-1}\right)^{\prime}\!\left(\frac{\weta}{1-\weta}\right)

\displaystyle\Psi^{\prime\prime}(\weta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1-\weta)^{4}}\left(g^{-1}\right)^{\prime\prime}\!\left(\frac{\weta}{1-\weta}\right)-\frac{2}{(1-\weta)^{3}}\left(g^{-1}\right)^{\prime}\!\left(\frac{\weta}{1-\weta}\right),

which gives

\displaystyle\frac{w^{\prime}(\weta)}{w(\weta)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\phi^{\prime\prime\prime}\!\left(\frac{\weta}{1-\weta}\right)}{\phi^{\prime\prime}\!\left(\frac{\weta}{1-\weta}\right)(\weta-1)^{2}}-\frac{3}{\weta-1}

\displaystyle\frac{\Psi^{\prime\prime}(\weta)}{\Psi^{\prime}(\weta)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(g^{-1}\right)^{\prime\prime}\!\left(\frac{\weta}{1-\weta}\right)}{\left(g^{-1}\right)^{\prime}\!\left(\frac{\weta}{1-\weta}\right)(\weta-1)^{2}}-\frac{2}{\weta-1}.

It holds that
\Psi^{-1}(z)=\frac{g^{-1}(z)}{g^{-1}(z)+1}
for
g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
being strictly monotonically increasing.
That is,
\left(\Psi^{-1}\right)^{\prime}(z)>0
and Theorem
4
grants convexity of losses if and only if

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\weta}\leq\frac{\phi^{\prime\prime\prime}\!\left(\frac{\weta}{1-\weta}\right)}{\phi^{\prime\prime}\!\left(\frac{\weta}{1-\weta}\right)(\weta-1)^{2}}-\frac{\left(g^{-1}\right)^{\prime\prime}\!\left(\frac{\weta}{1-\weta}\right)}{\left(g^{-1}\right)^{\prime}\!\left(\frac{\weta}{1-\weta}\right)(\weta-1)^{2}}-\frac{1}{\weta-1}\leq\frac{1}{1-\weta},\quad\forall\weta\in(0,1).

Substituting
x:=\frac{\weta}{1-\weta}
such that
\weta=\frac{x}{1+x}
,
-\frac{1}{\weta}=-\frac{1+x}{x}
and
-\frac{1}{\weta-1}=1+x
gives

\displaystyle-\frac{1+x}{x}\leq\frac{\phi^{\prime\prime\prime}\!\left(x\right)}{\phi^{\prime\prime}\!\left(x\right)}(1+x)^{2}-\frac{\left(g^{-1}\right)^{\prime\prime}\!\left(x\right)}{\left(g^{-1}\right)^{\prime}\!\left(x\right)}(1+x)^{2}+(1+x)\leq 1+x,\quad\forall x\in[0,\infty)

and consequently

\displaystyle-\frac{(1+x)^{2}}{x}\leq\frac{\phi^{\prime\prime\prime}\!\left(x\right)}{\phi^{\prime\prime}\!\left(x\right)}(1+x)^{2}-\frac{\left(g^{-1}\right)^{\prime\prime}\!\left(x\right)}{\left(g^{-1}\right)^{\prime}\!\left(x\right)}(1+x)^{2}\leq 0,\quad\forall x\in[0,\infty).

∎
