On the monotonicity of discrete entropy for log-concave random vectors on ℤ d \mathbb{Z}^{d}
Matthieu Fradelizi, Lampros Gavalakis, Martin Rapaport

Abstract
We prove the following type of discrete entropy monotonicity for sums of isotropic, log-concave, independent and identically distributed random vectors X 1 , … , X n + 1 X_{1},\dots,X_{n+1} on ℤ d \mathbb{Z}^{d} : H(X_1+⋯+X_n+1) ≥H(X_1+⋯+X_n) + d 2 log ( n+1 n ) +o(1), where o ⁡ ( 1 ) o(1) vanishes as H ⁡ ( X 1 ) → ∞ H(X_{1})\to\infty . Moreover, for the o ⁡ ( 1 ) o(1) -term, we obtain a rate of convergence O ⁡ ( H ⁡ ( X 1 ) ​ e − 1 d ​ H ​ ( X 1 ) ) O\Bigl({H(X_{1})}{e^{-\frac{1}{d}H(X_{1})}}\Bigr) , where the implied constants depend on d d and n n . This generalizes to ℤ d \mathbb{Z}^{d} the one-dimensional result of the second named author (2023). As in dimension one, our strategy is to establish that the discrete entropy H ⁡ ( X 1 + ⋯ + X n ) H(X_{1}+\cdots+X_{n}) is close to the differential (continuous) entropy h ⁡ ( X 1 + U 1 + ⋯ + X n + U n ) h(X_{1}+U_{1}+\cdots+X_{n}+U_{n}) , where U 1 , … , U n U_{1},\dots,U_{n} are independent and identically distributed uniform random vectors on [ 0 , 1 ] d [0,1]^{d} and to apply the theorem of Artstein, Ball, Barthe and Naor (2004) on the monotonicity of differential entropy. In fact, we show this result under more general assumptions than log-concavity, which are preserved up to constants under convolution. Namely, we consider families of random variables for which, as the determinant of the covariance matrix increases, the probability mass function i) is bounded above in terms of the the determinant of the covariance matrix, ii) has subexponential tails, iii) has (discrete) bounded variation. In order to show that log-concave distributions satisfy our assumptions in dimension d ≥ 2 d\geq 2 , more involved tools from convex geometry are needed because a suitable position is required. We show that, for a log-concave function on ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} in isotropic position, its integral, barycenter and covariance matrix are close to their discrete counterparts. Moreover, in the log-concave case, we weaken the isotropicity assumption to what we call almost isotropicity . One of our technical tools is a discrete analogue to the upper bound on the isotropic constant of a log-concave function, which extends to dimensions d ≥ 1 d\geq 1 a result of Bobkov, Marsiglietti and Melbourne (2022) in dimension one and may be of independent interest.

Introduction

1.1
Monotonicity of entropy

The
differential entropy
of an
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued random vector
X
with density
f
is defined as

h(X)=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)\log f(x)dx,

if the integral exists. When
X
is supported on a strictly lower-dimensional
set than
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(and hence does not have a density with respect to the

d
-dimensional Lebesgue measure), one sets
h(X)=-\infty
. The
entropy power
of
X
is defined by

N(X)=e^{2h(X)/d}
.

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be i.i.d. continuous random variables. A generalization of the classical Entropy Power Inequality (EPI) of Shannon
[
22
]
and Stam
[
24
]
, due to Artstein, Ball, Barthe and Naor,
[
1
]
states that

h\Bigl(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n+1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}X_{i}\Bigr)\geq h\Bigl(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\Bigr),

which by the scaling property of differential entropy is equivalent to

h\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}{X_{i}}\Bigr)\geq h\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{n}{X_{i}}\Bigr)+\frac{d}{2}\log{\Bigl(\frac{n+1}{n}\Bigr)}.

(1)

It can also be interpreted as the monotonic increase of entropy along the Central Limit Theorem (CLT)
[
3
]
. Later, other proofs and generalizations were given by Shlyakhtenko
[
23
]
, Tulino and Verdú
[
26
]
and Madiman and Barron
[
17
]
.

Let
A
be a discrete (finite or countable) set and let
X
be a random variable supported on
A
with probability mass function (p.m.f.)
p
on
A
. The discrete (Shannon) entropy of
X
is

H(X)=-\sum_{x\in A}{p(x)\log{p(x)}}.

An exact analogue of (
1
) cannot be true for discrete random variables as can be seen by taking
n=1,d=1
and considering deterministic or even close to deterministic random variables. Nevertheless, Tao
[
25
]
, using ideas from additive combinatorics, proved that for any independent and identically distributed random variables
X_{1},X_{2}
taking values in a finite subset of a torsion-free (abelian) group

H(X_{1}+X_{2})\geq H(X_{1})+\frac{1}{2}\log{2}-o(1),

(2)

where
o(1)\to 0
as
H(X_{1})\to\infty
. This can be seen as a discrete analogue of (
1
) for
d=n=1
.
Besides (
2
), other discrete versions of the EPI have been studied by several authors. In
[
11
,
12
]
, a discrete EPI similar to (
2
) was proved, with a worse constant than
\frac{1}{2}{\log{2}}
, but with sharp quantitative bounds for the
o(1)
-term (see also
[
9
]
for a detailed discussion on connections with that work). An EPI for the binomial family was proved in
[
13
, Theorem 1.1]
; in
[
20
, Theorem 4.6]
it was shown that

e^{2H(X)}-1

is superadditive with respect to convolution on the class of uniform distributions on finite subsets of the integers (see also
[
18
]
for extensions to Rényi entropy and
[
19
]
for dimensional extensions). Finally the constant
\frac{1}{2}\log{2}
was shown to be improvable for the class of uniform distributions in
[
27
]
.

In addition to (
2
), Tao conjectured that for any independent and identically distributed random variables
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n+1}
taking values in a finite subset of a torsion-free group

H\Bigl(X_{1}+\cdots+X_{n+1}\Bigr)\geq H\Bigl(X_{1}+\cdots+X_{n}\Bigr)+\frac{1}{2}\log{\Bigl(\frac{n+1}{n}\Bigr)}-o(1),

(3)

as
H(X_{1})\to\infty
depending on
n
. A special case of this conjecture was recently proven in
[
8
]
, where it is shown that (
3
) is satisfied by log-concave random variables on the integers with explicit rate for the
o(1)
that is exponential in
H(X_{1})
:

Theorem 1

(
[
8
]
)
.

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be i.i.d. log-concave random variables on
\mathbb{Z}
. Then

H\Biggl(\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}{X_{i}}\Biggr)\geq H\Biggl(\sum_{i=1}^{n}{X_{i}}\Biggr)+\frac{1}{2}\log{\Bigl(\frac{n+1}{n}\Bigr)}-O\Bigl(H(X_{1})e^{-H(X_{1})}\Bigr),

as
H(X_{1})\to\infty
.

This statement can be interpreted as a type of “approximate” monotonicity in the
discrete
entropic CLT
[
10
]
. We recall here that an integer valued random variable
X
with p.m.f.
p
is said to be
log-concave
if

p(k)^{2}\geq p(k-1)p(k+1),

(4)

for every
k\in\mathbb{Z}.

1.2
Main results and paper outline

Our goal is to extend Theorem
1
to higher dimensions and to the following larger class of probability distributions.

Definition 2
.

Let
\mathcal{C}(c_{1},c_{2},c_{3})
be the class of p.m.f.s
p
on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
for which, denoting
\sigma:=\det{\Bigl(\mathrm{Cov}{(p)}}\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{2d}}
,

1.

\max_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}{p(k)}\leq\frac{c_{1}}{\sigma^{d}},

2.

for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
with
\|k\|_{\infty}\geq c_{2}\sigma
,

p(k)\leq\frac{c_{2}}{\sigma^{d}}e^{-\frac{\|k\|_{1}}{c_{2}\sigma}}\quad\text{ and}

3.

\sum_{i=1}^{d}{\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}}{|p(k)-p(k-e_{i})|}\leq\frac{c_{3}}{\sigma}.

Our first main result is the following one:

Theorem 3
.

For any i.i.d. random vectors
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n+1}
on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
such that their common p.m.f. belongs to the class
\mathcal{C}(c_{1},c_{2},c_{3})
, for some absolute constants
c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}
that depend on the dimension only,

H(X_{1}+\cdots+X_{n+1})\geq H(X_{1}+\cdots+X_{n})+\frac{d}{2}\log{\Bigl(\frac{n+1}{n}\Bigr)}-o(1)

(5)

as
H(X_{1})\to\infty
. Moreover, we have the rate of convergence
o(1)=O_{d,n}\Bigl({H(X_{1})}{e^{-\frac{1}{d}H(X_{1})}}\Bigr)
. In particular, (
5
) holds for any i.i.d. log-concave random vectors
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n+1}
on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
with almost isotropic extension.

Note that regarding Tao’s conjecture
3
, our method for this class of random variables improves the result by a constant
d
, where
d
is the dimension. In the following subsection, we will explicitly detail the definitions we use for
discrete log-concavity in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
as well as the definition of
almost isotropic extension
.
Theorem
3
readily follows from the next Theorem together with the generalized EPI in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and can be interpreted as an approximation result of discrete and continuous entropies in higher dimensions:

Theorem 4
.

For any i.i.d. random vectors
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n+1}
on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
such that their common p.m.f. belongs to the class
\mathcal{C}(c_{1},c_{2},c_{3})
, for some absolute constants
c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}
that depend on the dimension only,

h(X_{1}+\cdots+X_{n}+U_{1}+\cdots+U_{n})=H(X_{1}+\cdots+X_{n})+o(1),

(6)

where
o(1)\to 0
as
H(X_{1})\to\infty
,
h
stands for the differential entropy,
H
denotes the discrete Shannon entropy and
U_{1},\ldots,U_{n}
are independent continuous uniforms on
[0,1]^{d}
. Moreover, we have the rate of convergence
o(1)=O_{d,n}\Bigl({H(X_{1})}{e^{-\frac{1}{d}H(X_{1})}}\Bigr)
. In particular, (
6
) holds for any i.i.d. log-concave random vectors
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n+1}
on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
with almost isotropic extension.

Remark 5
.

Let
X
be a log-concave random vector on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
with almost isotropic extension. Denote the covariance matrix of
X
by
K_{X}
,
\sigma^{2}:=\det{(K_{X})}^{\frac{1}{d}}
and let
U
be a uniform on the open box
[0,1]^{d}
. Then

H(X)=h(X+U)\leq\frac{d}{2}\log{\biggl(\det{(K_{X}+\frac{1}{12}I_{\mathrm{d}})^{\frac{1}{d}}}2\pi e\biggr)}\simeq\frac{d}{2}\log{\biggl(\sigma^{2}2\pi e\biggr)},

(7)

by the fact that the Gaussian maximizes the entropy under fixed covariance matrix, which implies that for any random variable
X
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
one has

h(X)\leq\frac{d}{2}\log(2\pi e(\det\mathrm{Cov}(X))^{1/d}).

(8)

Thus,
H(X)\to\infty
implies
\sigma\to\infty
and therefore it suffices to prove (
6
) with
o(1)\to 0
as
\sigma\to\infty
.

We prove Theorem
4
in Section
2
. First we use the second property of Definition
2
, which is an exponential tail bound, in order to control the
entropy tails
. We use the third property, which is a type of smoothness, to control the error term arising from the approximation of the density of
\sum_{i}{X_{i}+U_{i}}
by the p.m.f. of
\sum_{i}{X_{i}}
. Combined with the Taylor-type estimate of Lemma
17
, which is analogous to an estimate used in
[
25
]
, this approximation is the main technical step in the proof of Theorem
4
. The first property of Definition
2
ensures that Lemma
17
is applicable.

In Section
3
we will show that the assumption that the p.m.f. of
X_{1}
satisfies these three properties is weaker than almost isotropic log-concavity in the sense that any almost isotropic log-concave p.m.f. on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
satisfies these properties with appropriate constants. We remark here that, since our definition of log-concavity reduces to the usual one for the one-dimensional case, Theorems
3
and
4
are also relaxed versions of the corresponding results obtained in
[
8
]
. We prove the following theorem, which allows us to establish that if
p
is log-concave with almost isotropic extension then
p
belongs to the class
\mathcal{C}\left(c_{1}(d),c_{2}(d),c_{3}(d)\right)
for some absolute constants
c_{1}(d),c_{2}(d),c_{3}(d)
that depend on the dimension only (in particular, this theorem is essential for proving item
1
in Theorem

):

Theorem 6
.

Suppose
p
is a log-concave p.m.f. on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
with almost isotropic extension and covariance matrix
\mathrm{Cov}{(p)}
. Then there exists a constant
C^{\prime}_{d}
depending on the dimension only, such that

\max_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}{p(k)}\leq\frac{C^{\prime}_{d}}{\det\Bigl(\mathrm{Cov}_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}(p)\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{2}}}

provided that
\det\left(\mathrm{Cov}_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}(p)\right)
is large enough depending on
d
.

The above Theorem can be seen as
d
-dimensional analogue of the following result, due to Bobkov, Marsiglietti and Melbourne
[
4
]
, who also studied discrete versions of the EPI (up to multiplicative constants) for Rényi entropies of log-concave distributions and which is an important tool in the one-dimensional case:

Theorem 7
.

[
4
, Theorem 1.1]

If a random variable
X
follows a discrete log-concave p.m.f.
f
on
\mathbb{Z}
, then

\max_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}f(k)\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+4\sigma^{2}}},

(9)

where
\sigma^{2}=\mathrm{Var}(X)
.

Theorem
6
may also be seen as a discrete analogue of a dimensional upper bound on the isotropic constant
L_{f}
.
The corresponding (continuous) lower bound on
L_{f}
can be deduced from (
8
) and the fact that
e^{-h(X)}\leq\max(f)
:

L_{f}=\left(\max_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}{f}\right)^{\frac{1}{d}}\mathrm{det}(\mathrm{Cov}(f))^{\frac{1}{2d}}\geq e^{-\frac{h(X)}{d}}\mathrm{det}(\mathrm{Cov}(f))^{\frac{1}{2d}}\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi e}}.

It is well known that a dimensional upper bound also exists and we denote by
L_{d}
the maximum of the isotropic constants
L_{f}
among log-concave functions
f
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The latter is related to the famous hyperplane conjecture (or slicing problem) from convex geometry, one of the central questions in the field. The hyperplane conjecture is equivalent to the isotropic constant conjecture
[
5
, Theorem 3.1.2]
.

Hyperplane conjecture:

there exists a universal constant
c>0
such that, for any dimension
d
and for any convex body
K
in isotropic position and of volume
1
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and any direction
\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, one has
|K\cap\theta^{\perp}|_{d-1}\geq c
.

Isotropic constant conjecture:

there exists a universal constant
C
such that for any dimension
d
, one has
L_{d}\leq C
.

A recent result of Klartag
[
16
]
gives the best known constant depending on the dimension
d
for the slicing problem and thus also the best upper bound known for
L_{d}
:

L_{d}\leq C\sqrt{\log{d}}\hskip 2.84544pt,

(10)

for some absolute constant
C
.

Our method for proving Theorem
6
is to use the corresponding continuous result.
To this end, we obtain the approximations

\displaystyle\Bigl|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}{f}-\sum_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}{f}\Bigr|

\displaystyle=o_{d}(1),\quad\text{as }\sigma\to\infty,

\displaystyle\Bigl|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}xf-\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}kf(k)\Bigr|

\displaystyle=O_{d}(1),\quad\text{as }\sigma\to\infty,

\displaystyle\Bigl|\det\Bigl(\mathrm{Cov}_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}{(f)}\Bigr)-\det\Bigl(\mathrm{Cov}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}{(f)}\Bigr)\Bigr|

\displaystyle=O_{d}(\sigma^{2d-1}),\quad\text{as }\sigma\to\infty,

for any isotropic log-concave density
f
. This is done in Section
3.4
. Before that, after recalling some know facts about Ball’s bodies in Section
3.1
, we prove the exponential tails bound in Section
3.2
. Although our results hold under the more general almost isotropicity assumption, for better illustration of the ideas, we assume first in Sections
3.1
—

that the continuous extension

is isotropic. In Section
3.5
we show how to relax this assumption (see Remark
36
).

Finally, in Section
4
we conclude with a brief discussion of our assumptions as well as open questions regarding fundamental issues of log-concavity in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.

1.3
Notations and definitions

In this subsection, we will detail the notations as well as key definitions.

Big- and small-
O
notation.
Let
f
be a real-valued function and
g
another strictly positive function.
We write
f=O(g)
if there exist positive absolute constants
N,C
such that
|f(x)|\leq Cg(x)
for every
x\geq N
.
If
N,C
are absolute up to a parameter
d
, we write
f=O_{d}(g)
.
Analogously, we write
f=\Omega(g)
, if
|f(x)|\geq Cg(x)
for every
x\geq N
.
If
f=\Omega(g)
and
f=O(g)
, we write
f=\Theta(g)
(with the analogous definitions for
\Omega_{d}
and
\Theta_{d}
).
When it is more convenient, we will write
f\lesssim_{d}g
for
f=O_{d}(g)
and
f\simeq_{d}g
for
f=\Theta(g)
. We write
f(x)=o(g(x))
if
\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}=0
.

Convex bodies.
A
convex body
is a convex set that is compact and has a non-empty interior. For a convex body
K\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, one denotes by
|K|
its volume. The
convex hull
of a set
A
, denoted by
\mathrm{conv(A)}
, is the smallest convex set containing the set
A
.

Log-concavity and convexity in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.

For
d>1
, there are more than one definitions of log-concavity that have been used in different contexts. We will use a quite general definition that implies several other notions of log-concavity, the one of
extensible log-concave functions
. The reader is referred to Murota
[
21
]
for an extensive discussion of discrete convexity in higher dimensions.
A function
f:\mathbb{Z}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is said to be
convex-extensible
if there exists a convex function
\bar{f}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
such that

\bar{f}(z)=f(z)\hskip 8.5359pt(\forall z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d})\hskip 2.84544pt.

We refer to
\bar{f}
as the
extension
of
f
.
Based on the above, we define

Definition 8

(Log-concave extensible functions)
.

A function
f:\mathbb{Z}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R_{+}}
is said to be log-concave extensible if there exists
V:\mathbb{Z}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}

convex-extensible such that

f(z)=e^{-V(z)}.

Throughout when we say that
f
is log-concave, we mean that
f
is log-concave extensible. We say that a random vector
X
, taking values in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and p.m.f.
p:\mathbb{Z}^{d}\to[0,1]
is log-concave, if
p
is log-concave.

Similarly, we define a
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
-convex set (commonly referred in the literature as
hole-free
set
[
21
]
):

Definition 9

(
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
-convexity)
.

A set
A\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
is said to be
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
-convex if and only if its indicator function
\chi_{A}
, defined by
\chi_{A}(x)=1
if

x\in A
and
0
otherwise, is log-concave-extensible or equivalently if

A=\mathrm{conv}(A)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}\hskip 2.84544pt.

Notice that the support of a log-concave function
f
on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
is
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
-convex.

Remark 10
.

For
d=1
our definition of log-concavity is equivalent to the usual definition
p(k)^{2}\geq p(k-1)p(k+1),k\in\mathbb{Z}
, which is preserved under convolution (e.g.
[
14
]
). However, for
d>1
log-concavity may not be preserved in general, as pointed out by Murota
[
21
, Example 3.15]
by considering two log-concave distributions supported on

S_{1}=\{(0,0),(1,1)\}
and
S_{2}=\{(0,1),(1,0)\}
respectively. This is because the Minkowski sum
S_{1}+S_{2}
is not
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
-convex.
On the other hand, in the i.i.d. case, we shall see in our Proposition
38
below that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}A
is
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
-convex for any
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
-convex set
A
. We do not know whether our definition of log-concavity is preserved under self-convolution.

Notion of isotropicity
. The isotropic constant of a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is defined by

L_{f}:=\left(\frac{\max_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}{f}}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}{f}}\right)^{\frac{1}{d}}\mathrm{det}(\mathrm{Cov}(f))^{\frac{1}{2d}},

(11)

where
\mathrm{Cov}{(f)}
is the
inertia
or
covariance
matrix defined, for
1\leq i,j\leq d
, by

[\mathrm{Cov}{(f)}]_{ij}:=\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}x_{i}x_{j}f(x)dx}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)dx}-\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}x_{i}f(x)dx\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}x_{j}f(x)dx}{\Bigl(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)dx\Bigr)^{2}}\hskip 2.84544pt.

We say that
f
is
isotropic
if
\mathrm{Cov}{(f)}=\sigma^{2}\mathrm{I}_{d}
, for some
\sigma>0,
where
\mathrm{I}_{d}
is the
d\times d
identity matrix. Let
K
be a convex body in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then its covariance matrix is
\mathrm{Cov}(K):=\mathrm{Cov}(\mathds{1}_{K})
and its isotropic constant is
L_{K}:=L_{\mathds{1}_{K}}
. The convex body
K
is called
isotropic
if
\mathds{1}_{K}
is isotropic.

Similarly, in the discrete case
p:\mathbb{Z}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
, we define the covariance matrix
\mathrm{Cov}{(p)}

by

[\mathrm{Cov}{(p)}]_{ij}:=\frac{\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}k_{i}k_{j}p(k)}{\sum_{k}{p(k)}}-\frac{\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}k_{i}p(k)\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}k_{j}p(k)}{\Bigl(\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}p(k)\Bigr)^{2}}\hskip 2.84544pt.

Definition 11
.

A family
\{f_{\sigma}\}_{\sigma\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}
of non-negative functions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is
almost isotropic
if, as
\sigma\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}{(f_{\sigma})}_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}+O(\sigma)\quad\text{for }i=j,

\displaystyle=O(\sigma)\quad\text{for }i\neq j.

Remark 12
.

It is straightforward to check that
the family
\{f_{\sigma}\}_{\sigma\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}
is almost isotropic if and only if

||\mathrm{Cov}{(f_{\sigma})}-\sigma^{2}\mathrm{I}_{d}||_{\rm op}=O(\sigma),

where
||A||_{\rm op}
is the operator norm.

We are interested in log-concave densities
f
, for which
\det{\mathrm{Cov}{(f)}}\to\infty
. Thus, when we write that
f
is almost isotropic, it is meant that
f
represents a family of densities with the underlying parameter
\sigma:=\det{\mathrm{Cov}{(f)}}^{\frac{1}{2d}}
growing large.
When its not clear from the context, we write
\mathrm{Cov}_{\mathbb{R}}(f)
and
\mathrm{Cov}{{}_{\mathbb{Z}}(f)}
to distinguish between the continuous and discrete covariance matrix of
f
.

2
Proofs of main results

Our first observation is that the class
\mathcal{C}(c_{1},c_{2},c_{3})
defined in Definition
2
is closed under self-convolution up to constants, as shown by the following proposition:

Proposition 13
.

Suppose

p\in\mathcal{C}(c_{1}(d),c_{2}(d),c_{3}(d))
for some constants
c_{1}(d),c_{2}(d)\geq 1,c_{3}(d)
depending on the dimension only. Then the convolution of
p
with itself
n
times,

p_{n}:=p*\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n}}{{\ldots}}*p
, satisfies

p_{n}\in\mathcal{C}({n^{\frac{d}{2}}}c_{1}(d),n^{\frac{d}{2}}c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n),\sqrt{n}c_{3}(d))

for some possibly different constant
c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n)
that depends on the dimension and
n
.

That is, denoting
\sigma:=\det{\Bigl(\mathrm{Cov}{(p)}}\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{2d}}
, we have

1.

\max_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}{p_{n}(k)}\leq\frac{c_{1}(d)}{\sigma^{d}}.

2.

For all
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
with
\|k\|_{\infty}\geq c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n)\sigma
,

p_{n}(k)\leq\frac{c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n)}{\sigma^{d}}e^{-\frac{\|k\|_{1}}{c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n)\sigma}},

where in fact we may take

c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n)=\bigl(3\cdot 4^{d+1}c_{2}(d)\bigr)^{2^{n}-1}.

3.

\sum_{i=1}^{d}{\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}}{|p_{n}(k)-p_{n}(k-e_{i})|}\leq\frac{c_{3}(d)}{\sigma}.

Proof.

First we note that, since
\mathrm{Cov}{(p_{n})}=n\mathrm{Cov}{(p)}
, one has
\det{\Bigl(\mathrm{Cov}{(p_{n})}}\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{2d}}=\sqrt{n}\det{\Bigl(\mathrm{Cov}{(p)}}\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{2d}},
so
p_{n}\in\mathcal{C}(n^{\frac{d}{2}}c_{1}(d),n^{\frac{d}{2}}c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n),\sqrt{n}c_{3}(d))
is indeed equivalent to the three statements.

For
1
we have

\max_{k}p_{n}(k)=\max\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}p_{n-1}(j)p(k-j)\leq\max_{k}p(k)\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}{p_{n-1}(j)}=\max_{k}p(k)\leq\frac{c_{1}(d)}{\sigma^{d}}.

To prove that property
2
is satisfied, we proceed by induction. Assume that
p,
as well as
p_{n}
, satisfy condition
2
of Definition
2
with the constant
c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n)
. Let
k\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
\|k\|_{\infty}\geq c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n+1)\sigma.
We have

\displaystyle p_{{n+1}}(k)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}p(j)p_{n}(k-j)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\|j\|_{\infty}\leq c_{2}(d)\sigma}p(j)p_{n}(k-j)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\|j\|_{\infty}>c_{2}(d)\sigma,\\
\|k-j\|_{\infty}>c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n)\sigma\end{subarray}}p(j)p_{n}(k-j)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\|j\|_{\infty}>c_{2}(d)\sigma,\\
\|k-j\|_{\infty}\leq c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n)\sigma\end{subarray}}p(j)p_{n}(k-j).

(12)

For the first sum in (
12
), we note that for
\|j\|_{\infty}\leq c_{2}(d)\sigma
,

\|k-j\|_{1}\geq\|k\|_{1}-\|j\|_{1}\geq\|k\|_{1}-\sqrt{d}\|j\|_{\infty}\geq\|k\|_{1}-\sqrt{d}c_{2}(d)\sigma\geq\|k\|_{1}-c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n)\sigma
and
\|k-j\|_{\infty}\geq\|k\|_{\infty}-\|j\|_{\infty}\geq c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n+1)\sigma-c_{2}(d)\sigma\geq c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n)\sigma
, by the elementary bound
\|x\|_{\infty}\leq\|x\|_{1}\leq\sqrt{d}\|x\|_{\infty},x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Therefore we can bound that sum as

\sum_{\|j\|_{\infty}\leq c_{2}(d)\sigma}p(j)p_{n}(k-j)\leq\frac{c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n)}{\sigma^{d}}\sum_{\|j\|_{\infty}\leq c_{2}(d)\sigma}p(j)e^{-\frac{\|k-j\|_{1}}{c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n)\sigma}}\leq\frac{c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n)}{\sigma^{d}}e^{-\frac{\|k\|_{1}}{c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n)\sigma}}.

(13)

For the second sum in (
12
), using
c_{2}(d)\leq c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n)
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\|j\|_{\infty}>c_{2}(d)\sigma,\|k-j\|_{\infty}>c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n)\sigma}p(j)p_{n}(k-j)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{c_{2}(d)c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n)}{\sigma^{2d}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\|j\|_{\infty}>c_{2}(d)\sigma,\\
\|k-j\|_{\infty}>c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n)\sigma\end{subarray}}{e^{\frac{-\|j\|_{1}-\|k-j\|_{1}}{c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n)\sigma}}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{c_{2}(d)c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n)}{\sigma^{2d}}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}{e^{\frac{-\|j\|_{1}-\mid\|k\|_{1}-\|j\|_{1}\mid}{c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n)\sigma}}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{c_{2}(d)c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n)}{\sigma^{2d}}\Bigl[\sum_{0\leq m\leq\|k\|_{1}}{\sum_{\|j\|_{1}=m}{e^{\frac{-m+\|k\|_{1}+m}{c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n)\sigma}}}}+\sum_{m\geq\|k\|_{1}}{\sum_{\|j\|_{1}=m}{e^{\frac{-2m+\|k\|_{1}}{c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n)\sigma}}}}\Bigr].

(14)

The first double sum in (
14
) is

\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq m\leq\|k\|_{1}}{\sum_{\|j\|_{1}=m}{e^{\frac{-m+\|k\|_{1}+m}{c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n)\sigma}}}}

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\frac{\|k\|_{1}}{c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n)\sigma}}\big|\{j:\|j\|_{1}\leq\|k\|_{1}\}\big|

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\frac{\|k\|_{1}}{c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n)\sigma}}\big|\{j:\|j\|_{\infty}\leq\|k\|_{1}\}\big|

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\frac{\|k\|_{1}}{c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n)\sigma}}(2\|k\|_{1}+1)^{d}\leq 4^{d}\|k\|_{1}^{d}e^{-\frac{\|k\|_{1}}{c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n)\sigma}}\leq 4^{d}\sigma^{d}e^{-\frac{\|k\|_{1}}{2c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n)\sigma}},

(15)

where the last inequality holds at least as long as

\|k\|_{1}\geq 2c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n)d\sigma\Bigl(\log{(2c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n)d)}\Bigr)^{2},

(16)

since
x^{d}\leq e^{\frac{x}{2c}},
for
x\geq 2cd\bigl(\log{(2cd)}\bigr)^{2},d\geq 1,c\geq 2
.
On the other hand, the second double sum in (
14
) is

\displaystyle\sum_{m\geq\|k\|_{1}}{\sum_{\|j\|_{1}=m}{e^{\frac{-2m+\|k\|_{1}}{c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n)\sigma}}}}

\displaystyle\leq e^{\frac{\|k\|_{1}}{c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n)\sigma}}\sum_{m\geq\|k\|_{1}}{\big|\{j:\|j\|_{\infty}\leq m\}\bigr|e^{-\frac{2m}{\sigma c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n)}}}

\displaystyle\leq 4^{d}e^{\frac{\|k\|_{1}}{c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n)\sigma}}\sum_{m\geq\|k\|_{1}}{m^{d}e^{-\frac{2m}{\sigma c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n)}}}

(18)

\displaystyle\leq 4^{d}\sigma^{d}e^{\frac{\|k\|_{1}}{c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n)\sigma}}\sum_{m\geq\|k\|_{1}}{e^{-\frac{3m}{2\sigma c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n)}}}

(19)

\displaystyle=4^{d}\sigma^{d}e^{\frac{\|k\|_{1}}{c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n)\sigma}}\frac{e^{-\frac{3\|k\|_{1}}{2\sigma c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n)}}}{1-e^{-\frac{3\|k\|_{1}}{2\sigma c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n)}}}\leq 2\cdot 4^{d}\sigma^{d}e^{-\frac{\|k\|_{1}}{2\sigma c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n)}},

(20)

where (
19
) holds at least as long as (
16
) is satisfied since
m\geq\|k\|_{1}
and similarly for the last inequality.
Plugging the bounds (
15
), (
20
) in (
14
) we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{\|j\|_{\infty}>c_{2}(d)\sigma,\|k-j\|_{\infty}>c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n)\sigma}p(j)p_{n}(k-j)

\displaystyle\leq 4^{d+1}\frac{c_{2}(d)c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n)^{2}}{\sigma^{d}}e^{-\frac{\|k\|_{1}}{2\sigma c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n)}},

(21)

provided that (
16
) holds.

For the third sum in (
12
), we observe that
\|k-j\|_{\infty}\leq c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n)\sigma
implies
\|j\|_{1}\geq\|k\|_{1}-\|k-j\|_{1}\geq\|k\|_{1}-c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n)\sqrt{d}\sigma
, and therefore

\displaystyle\sum_{\|j\|_{\infty}>c_{2}(d)\sigma,\|k-j\|_{\infty}\leq c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n)\sigma}p(j)p_{n}(k-j)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\|j\|_{\infty}>c_{2}(d)\sigma,\|k-j\|_{\infty}\leq c_{2}(d)\sigma}\frac{c_{2}(d)}{\sigma^{d}}e^{-\frac{\|j\|_{1}}{c_{2}(d)\sigma}}p_{n}(k-j)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n)}{\sigma^{d}}e^{\frac{-\|k\|_{1}}{c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n)\sigma}+\sqrt{d}}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}p(k-j)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{e^{\sqrt{d}}c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n)}{\sigma^{d}}e^{\frac{-\|k\|_{1}}{c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n)\sigma}}.

(22)

Clearly, the worst bound out of (
13
), (
21
) and (
22
) is (
21
) and the dominating condition for
k
is (
16
).
By (
12
), we can therefore bound

p_{n+1}(k)\leq 3\cdot 4^{d+1}\frac{c_{2}(d)c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n)^{2}}{\sigma^{d}}e^{-\frac{\|k\|_{1}}{2\sigma c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n)}}\leq\frac{c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n+1)}{\sigma^{d}}e^{-\frac{\|k\|_{1}}{\sigma c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n+1)}},

(23)

with
c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n+1)=3\cdot 4^{d+1}c_{2}(d)c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n)^{2}=\bigl(3\cdot 4^{d+1}c_{2}(d)\bigr)^{2^{n}-1}
by the inductive hypothesis. Note that this choice of
c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n+1)
also ensures that (
16
) is satisfied and therefore the proof of
2
. is complete.

For property
3
, let
X
be a random vector with p.m.f.
p_{X}=p
. Then
S_{n}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}{X_{i}}
, where
X_{i}
are i.i.d. copies of
X
, has p.m.f.
p_{n}
. We observe that

\sum_{i=1}^{d}{\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}}{|p_{n}(k)-p_{n}(k-e_{i})|}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\|p_{S_{n}}-p_{S_{n}+e_{i}}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}.

For any probability measures
\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\nu_{1}
and
\nu_{2}
,
the total variation distance satisfies the convolution inequality

\|\mu_{1}\ast\mu_{2}-\nu_{1}\ast\nu_{2}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq\|\mu_{1}-\nu_{1}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}+\|\mu_{2}-\nu_{2}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}.

This can be proved either directly or by the fact that total variation is an
f-
divergence and therefore satisfies a data processing inequality.
Choosing
\mu_{1}=\nu_{1}=p_{S_{n-1}},

\mu_{2}=p_{X}
and
\nu_{2}=p_{X+e_{i}}
, we get

\|p_{S_{n}}-p_{S_{n}+e_{i}}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq\|p_{X}-p_{X+e_{i}}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}

Using the assumption on
X
, the last inequality yields

\sum_{i=1}^{d}\|p_{S_{n}}-p_{S_{n}+e_{i}}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{d}\|p_{X}-p_{X+e_{i}}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq\frac{c_{3}(d)}{\sigma}.

∎

Lemma 14
.

Let
X
be a random vector with p.m.f.
p
on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
satisfying conditions
2
,
3
of Definition
2
.
Let
U^{(n)}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}U_{i}
where
U_{i}
are i.i.d. uniform random vectors on the cube
[0,1]^{d}
.
Denote by
h_{n}^{(d)}(x)=\prod_{i=1}^{d}h_{n}(x_{i})
the density of
U_{n}
and by
f_{n}^{(d)}
the density of
U^{(n)}+X
. Then,

1.

\forall z\in[0,1]^{d},\forall n\geq 1
,

\sum_{j\in\{0,\ldots,n-1\}^{d}}h_{n}^{(d)}(j+z)=1
.

2.

For
x=k+z
where
z\in[0,1]^{d}
and
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,
|f_{n}^{(d)}(x)-p(k)|\leq\frac{2^{d-1}}{d}\frac{c_{3}}{\sigma}
with
c_{3}
the constant that appears in condition
3
of Definition
2
.

3.

For
x=k+z
where
z\in[0,1]^{d}
and
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, with
\|k\|_{\infty}\geq c_{2}\sigma+n
,

f_{n}^{(d)}(x)\leq\Bigl(n^{d}(e^{\frac{nd}{c_{2}\sigma}}+1)+1\Bigr)\frac{c_{2}}{\sigma^{d}}e^{-\frac{\|k\|_{1}}{c_{2}\sigma}}.

Proof.

First, we prove 1.
Let us prove that the one-dimensional case holds i.e.

\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}h_{n}(j+z)=1
, for any
z\in[0,1]
. For this, one does an induction on
n
.
For
n=1
, since
S_{1}=U_{1}
,
h_{1}(z)=1_{[0,1]}
so the base case is immediate.
Now let us suppose by induction that the claim holds for
n
. Noticing that the support of
h_{n}
is
[0,n]
and using the induction hypothesis, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{n}h_{n+1}(j+z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\int_{0}^{1}h_{n}(j+z-u)du=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\left(\int_{0}^{z}h_{n}(j+z-u)du+\int_{z}^{1}h_{n}(j+z-u)du\right)

\displaystyle=z+\int_{0}^{z}h_{n}(n+z-u)du+\int_{z}^{1}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}h_{n}(k+1+z-u)\right)du=z+1-z=1.

For the
d
-dimensional case, observe that since
h_{n}^{(d)}
is a product, for all
z\in[0,1]^{d}
,

\sum_{j\in\{0,\ldots,n-1\}^{d}}h_{n}^{(d)}(j+z)=\sum_{j\in\{0,\ldots,n-1\}^{d}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{d}h_{n}(z_{i}+j_{i})\right)=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left(\sum_{j_{i}=0}^{n-1}h_{n}(z_{i}+j_{i})\right)=1,

where, for the last equality, we have used the above one-dimensional argument.

Let us prove the second item. Using item
1
, one has

f_{n}^{d}(x)-p(k)=f_{n}^{(d)}(k+z)-p(k)=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}p(k-i)h_{n}^{(d)}(i+z)-p(k)=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}u(i)v(i),

(24)

where
u(i)=p(k-i)-p(k)
,
v(i)=h_{n}^{(d)}(z+i)
. Note that the sum actually ranges over
i\in\{0,\ldots,n-1\}^{d}
, since
h_{n}^{(d)}(i+z)=0
outside this discrete cube. Also, for
i=(i_{1},\dots,i_{d})\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, denote

W(i):=\left(T(v)\right)_{i}=\sum_{j\geq i}v_{j},

where
j\geq i
means that
j_{l}\geq i_{l}
for all
1\leq l\leq d
. By a straightforward induction,

v(i)=(T^{-1}(W))_{i}=\sum_{\epsilon\in\{-1,1\}^{d}}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{d}\left(-\epsilon_{j}\right)\right)W\left(i+\sum_{k=1}^{d}\frac{\epsilon_{k}+1}{2}e_{k}\right).

Let us introduce the standard dot product
in
l^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
as

\langle z,h\rangle_{l^{2}(\mathbb{Z})}:=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}z(i)h(i)
for any
z,h\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
. Then the sum

\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}u(i)v(i)

can be rewritten as
\langle u,T^{-1}W\rangle_{l^{2}(\mathbb{Z})}
with

T^{-1}:=\sum_{\epsilon\in\{-1,1\}^{d}}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{d}(-\epsilon_{j})\right)S_{\epsilon},

where
S_{\epsilon}
is the left-shift operator, that is,
\left(S_{\epsilon}W\right)_{i}=W\left(i+\sum_{k=1}^{d}\frac{\epsilon_{k}+1}{2}e_{k}\right)
. It is well known that the adjoint
S_{\epsilon}^{\star}
of
S_{\epsilon}
,
is the right-shift operator or in another words
(S_{\epsilon}^{\star}(W))_{i}=W(i-\sum_{k=1}^{d}\frac{\epsilon_{k}+1}{2}e_{k})
. Thus

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}u(i)v(i)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\sum_{\epsilon\in\{-1,1\}^{d}}u_{i}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{d}\left(-\epsilon_{j}\right)\right)W\left(i+\sum_{k=1}^{d}\frac{\epsilon_{k}+1}{2}e_{k}\right)

\displaystyle=\langle u,T^{-1}(W)\rangle_{l^{2}(\mathbb{Z})}=\langle\left(T^{-1}\right)^{\star}u,W\rangle_{l^{2}(\mathbb{Z})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}W(i)\sum_{\epsilon\in\{-1,1\}^{d}}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{d}(-\epsilon_{j})\right)u\left(i-\sum_{k=1}^{d}\frac{\epsilon_{k}+1}{2}e_{k}\right)

Thus, by the triangular inequality, summing up to the last coordinate which can be either
1
or
-1
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}u(i)v(i)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\sum_{\epsilon\in\{-1,1\}^{d-1}}\left|u\left(i-\sum_{k=1}^{d}\frac{\epsilon_{k}+1}{2}e_{k}\right)-u\left(i-\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}\frac{\epsilon_{k}+1}{2}e_{k}-e_{d}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2^{d-1}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\left|u(i)-u(i-e_{d})\right|.

Now, using the same argument for
j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
, instead of
j=d
and summing over
j
one gets

\left|f_{n}(x)-p(k)\right|=\left|\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}u(i)v(i)\right|\leq\frac{2^{d-1}}{d}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left|p(i)-p(i+e_{j})\right|\leq\frac{2^{d-1}}{d}\frac{c_{3}}{\sigma},

where, in the last inequality, we have used that
X
satisfies condition
3
of Definition
2
.

Finally, for item
3
, we have by (
24
), since
|h_{n}|\leq 1
,

\displaystyle|f_{n}^{(d)}(x)-p(k)|

\displaystyle=\bigl|\sum_{i\in\{0,\ldots,n-1\}^{d}}{(p(k-i)-p(k))h_{n}(z+i)}\bigr|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\in\{0,\ldots,n-1\}^{d}}{p(k-i)}+n^{d}p(k).

But, using hypothesis
2
, the assumption
\|k\|_{\infty}\geq c_{2}\sigma+n
and the observations that
\|k-i\|_{\infty}\geq\|k\|_{\infty}-n\geq c_{2}\sigma
and
\|k-i\|_{1}\geq\|k\|_{1}-nd,
we have

\displaystyle|f_{n}^{(d)}(x)-p(k)|

\displaystyle\leq n^{d}\frac{c_{2}}{\sigma^{d}}e^{-\frac{(\|k\|_{1}-nd)}{c_{2}\sigma}}+n^{d}\frac{c_{2}}{\sigma^{d}}e^{-\frac{\|k\|_{1}}{c_{2}\sigma}}

and the claimed bound follows since
f_{n}^{(d)}(x)\leq|f_{n}^{(d)}(x)-p(k)|+p(k)
.
∎

.

We are now ready to prove Theorem

Proof of Theorem

.

Let

. Denote
S_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}{X_{i}},

\displaystyle h(X_{1}+\cdots+X_{n}+U_{1}+\cdots+U_{n})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k:\|k\|_{\infty}\leq\sigma^{2}}{\int_{k+[0,1)^{d}}{F(f_{S_{n}+U^{(n)}}(x))dx}}+\sum_{k:\|k\|_{\infty}>\sigma^{2}}{\int_{k+[0,1)^{d}}{F(f_{S_{n}+U^{(n)}}(x))dx}}.

, in that range of

(26)

for some constants

\displaystyle\sum_{k:\|k\|_{\infty}>\sigma^{2}}{\int_{k+[0,1)^{d}}{F(f_{S_{n}+U^{(n)}}(x))dx}}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{d,n}\sum_{k:\|k\|_{\infty}>\sigma^{2}}{\int_{k+[0,1)^{d}}{F\Bigl(\frac{C^{\prime}_{d,n}}{\sigma^{d}}e^{-\frac{\|k\|_{1}}{c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n)\sigma}}\Bigr)dx}}

(27)

\displaystyle\lesssim_{d,n}\sum_{k:\|k\|_{\infty}>\sigma^{2}}{\frac{1}{\sigma^{d}}e^{-\frac{\|k\|_{\infty}}{c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n)\sigma}}\frac{\|k\|_{\infty}}{\sigma}}

\displaystyle+\sum_{k:\|k\|_{\infty}>\sigma^{2}}{\frac{1}{\sigma^{d}}e^{-\frac{\|k\|_{\infty}}{c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n)\sigma}}\log{\sigma}}.

(28)

Observing that the first term in (
28
) dominates and using a similar calculation as the one leading to (
20
), we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{k:\|k\|_{\infty}>\sigma^{2}}{\int_{k+[0,1)^{d}}{F(f_{S_{n}+U^{(n)}}(x))dx}}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{d,n}\frac{1}{\sigma^{d+1}}\sum_{k:\|k\|_{\infty}>\sigma^{2}}{e^{-\frac{\|k\|_{\infty}}{c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n)\sigma}}\|k\|_{\infty}}

(29)

\displaystyle\lesssim_{d,n}\frac{1}{\sigma^{d+1}}\sum_{m>\sigma^{2}}{m^{d+1}e^{-\frac{m}{c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n)\sigma}}}

(30)

\displaystyle\lesssim_{d,n}\sum_{m>\sigma^{2}}{e^{-\frac{m}{2c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n)\sigma}}}\lesssim_{d,n}\frac{e^{-\frac{\sigma}{2c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n)}}}{1-e^{-\frac{1}{2c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n)\sigma}}}\lesssim_{d,n}\sigma e^{-\frac{\sigma}{2c_{2}^{\prime}(d,n)}}=O(\sigma e^{-\Theta(\sigma)}).

(31)

Now we show that the first term in (
25
) is approximately
H(S_{n})
.
To that end we apply the estimate (
38
) of Lemma
17
to the integrand with
\mu=\Theta_{d,n}(\frac{1}{\sigma}),D=\Theta_{d,n}(\sigma^{2d-1}),M=\sigma^{2d},a=f_{S_{n}+U^{(n)}}(x)
and
b=p_{S_{n}}(k).
The assumption of the lemma is satisfied for an appropriate choice of the implied constants since by Lemma
14
and part
1
of Proposition
13
,

a,b\lesssim_{d,n}\max_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}{p_{S_{n}}(k)}+O_{d,n}\Bigl(\frac{1}{\sigma}\Bigr)\lesssim_{d,n}\frac{1}{\sigma}\simeq_{d,n}\frac{D}{M}.

Thus, since there are at most

elements in the set

,

\displaystyle\sum_{k:\|k\|_{\infty}\leq\sigma^{2}}{\Bigl|\int_{k+[0,1)^{d}}{F(f_{S_{n}+U^{(n)}}(x))dx}}-{F(p_{S_{n}}(k))}\Bigr|

\displaystyle\lesssim_{d,n}\sigma^{2d}\frac{\log{\sigma}}{\sigma^{2d+1}}+\log{\sigma}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}{\int_{k+[0,1)^{d}}|f_{S_{n}+U^{(n)}}(x)-p_{S_{n}}(k)|dx}

(32)

\displaystyle\lesssim_{d,n}\frac{\log{\sigma}}{\sigma}+\log{\sigma}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}{\sup_{x\in k+[0,1)^{d}}|g_{n}(k,x)|}

(33)

\displaystyle\lesssim_{d,n}\frac{\log{\sigma}}{\sigma},

(34)

where

S_{n}
, which satisfies the assumption

Therefore, by (

\displaystyle\Bigl|H(S_{n})-\sum_{k:\|k\|_{\infty}\leq\sigma^{2}}{\int_{k+[0,1)^{d}}{F(f_{S_{n}+U^{(n)}}(x))dx}}\Bigr|

\displaystyle\lesssim_{d,n}\Bigl|H(S_{n})-\sum_{k:\|k\|_{\infty}\leq\sigma^{2}}{F(p_{S_{n}}(k))}\Bigr|+\frac{\log{\sigma}}{\sigma}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{d,n}\sum_{k:\|k\|_{\infty}>\sigma^{2}}{F(p_{S_{n}}(k))}+\frac{\log{\sigma}}{\sigma}.

(35)

But, in view of (
26
) and (
27

\sum_{k:\|k\|_{\infty}>\sigma^{2}}{F(p_{S_{n}}(k))}\lesssim_{d,n}\frac{\sigma}{e^{\Theta_{d,n}(\sigma)}}.

(36)

Plugging the bound (

) into (

\displaystyle\Bigl|H(S_{n})-\sum_{k:\|k\|_{\infty}\leq\sigma^{2}}{\int_{k+[0,1)^{d}}{F(f_{S_{n}+U^{(n)}}(x))dx}}\Bigr|

\displaystyle\leq O_{d,n}\Bigl(\frac{\sigma}{e^{\Theta_{d,n}(\sigma)}}\Bigr)+O_{d,n}\Bigl(\frac{\log{\sigma}}{\sigma}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=O_{d,n}\Bigl(\frac{\log{\sigma}}{\sigma}\Bigr).

Finally, by (

\displaystyle\bigl|h(S_{n}+U^{(n)})-H(S_{n})\bigr|

\displaystyle=O_{d,n}\Bigl(\frac{\log{\sigma}}{\sigma}\Bigr)+O_{d,n}\Bigl(\frac{\sigma}{e^{\Theta_{d,n}(\sigma)}}\Bigr)

(37)

\displaystyle=O_{d,n}\Bigl(\frac{\log{\sigma}}{\sigma}\Bigr),

completing the proof.

3.1
Ball’s bodies

In what follows, we set
\omega_{d}
to be the volume of the unit Euclidean ball denoted by
B_{2}^{d}
. For a given convex body
K\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
0
in its interior, its
inradius

r(K)
is defined as the largest
r>0
for which
rB_{2}^{d}\subset K
and its
circumradius

R(K)
is defined as the smallest
R>0
such that
K\subset RB_{2}^{d}
. The support function of
K
is defined as
h_{K}(x)=\max_{y\in K}\langle x,y\rangle
and the radial function of
K
is
\rho_{K}(x)=\inf\{t>0;\ tx\in K\}=1/\|x\|_{K}
, where
\|x\|_{K}
denotes the gauge associated to
K
.

For most results in this section we will assume that
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a centered, isotropic, log-concave density, that is

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f=1
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}xf=0
and

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}x^{T}xf=\sigma^{2}\mathrm{I}_{d}.

To this function
f
, we attach its Ball’s bodies.
This important family of bodies was introduced by Ball
[
2
]
. We refer to the book
[
5
]
for the properties of these.

Definition 19
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
be an integrable, centered log-concave function.
For any
p>0
, the set
K_{p}(f)
is defined as follows

K_{p}(f):=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\int_{0}^{\infty}pr^{p-1}f(rx)dr\geq f(0)\right\}.

Ball
[
2
]
established that the set
K_{p}(f)
is a convex body. Moreover, its radial function is

\rho_{K_{p}(f)}(x)=\Bigl(\frac{1}{f(0)}\int_{0}^{\infty}pr^{p-1}f(rx)dr\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{p}}\hskip 5.69046pt\text{for}\hskip 5.69046ptx\neq 0.

From integration in polar coordinates (see
[
5
, Proposition 2.5.3]
), it follows that
K_{d+1}(f)
is centered and, for any
p\geq 0
and
u\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
,

\int_{K_{d+p}(f)}|\langle x,u\rangle|^{p}dx=\frac{1}{f(0)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\langle x,u\rangle|^{p}f(x)dx.

(39)

Finally, the following inclusion relations between
K_{p}(f)
and
K_{q}(f)
hold
[
5
, Proposition 2.5.7]
, for
p<q
,

\frac{\Gamma(p+1)^{\frac{1}{p}}}{\Gamma(q+1)^{\frac{1}{q}}}K_{q}(f)\subset K_{p}(f)\subset e^{\frac{d}{p}-\frac{d}{q}}K_{q}(f).

Applying these inclusions first for
p=d
and
q=d+1
then for
p=d+1
and
q=d+2
and using classical inequalities on Gamma functions, it follows that there exist universal constants
0<c_{1}<c_{2}
such that for any dimension
d\geq 1
, one has

c_{1}K_{d+1}(f)\subset K_{d}(f)\subset c_{2}K_{d+1}(f)\quad\hbox{and}\quad c_{1}K_{d+2}(f)\subset K_{d+1}(f)\subset c_{2}K_{d+2}(f).

(40)

We shall apply to
K_{d+1}(f)
the following theorem due to Kannan, Lovász and Simonovits
[
15
, Theorem 4.1]
to which we give a new and simple proof.

Theorem 20
.

Let
K
be a centered convex body in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
u\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. Then

\frac{h_{K}(u)^{2}}{d(d+2)}\leq\frac{1}{|K|}\int_{K}\langle x,u\rangle^{2}dx=\langle\mathrm{Cov}(K)u,u\rangle\leq\frac{d}{d+2}h_{K}(u)^{2}.

(41)

Proof.

Let
f(t)=|\{x\in K;\langle x,u\rangle=t\}
. Denote by
[-b,a]
its support. Then one has
a=h_{K}(u)
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(t)dt=|K|,\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}}tf(t)dt=\int_{K}\langle x,u\rangle dx\quad\hbox{and}\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}}t^{2}f(t)dt=\int_{K}\langle x,u\rangle^{2}dx.

Now we define
g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
by

g(t)=\alpha\left(1+\frac{t}{da}\right)^{d-1}\mathds{1}_{[-da,a]}(t),\quad\hbox{where}\ \alpha=\frac{1}{a\left(1+\frac{1}{d}\right)^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(t)dt.

And let
h:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
be defined by
h(t)=dh(-dt)
. Then, it is not difficult to see that

\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(t)dt=\int_{\mathbb{R}}h(t)dt=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(t)dt\quad\hbox{and}\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}}tg(t)dt=\int_{\mathbb{R}}th(t)dt=\int_{\mathbb{R}}tf(t)dt=0.

(42)

We shall prove that

\int_{\mathbb{R}}t^{2}g(t)dt\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}t^{2}f(t)dt\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}t^{2}h(t)dt,

from which the inequality (
41
) follows immediately by a simple calculation.
If
f\neq g
, it follows from (
42
) that there exist at least two points
t_{1}(g)<t_{2}(g)
such that
g-f
changes sign at these points and, in the same way, if
f\neq h
there exists at least two points
t_{1}(h)<t_{2}(h)
such that
f-h
changes sign at these points.
Since the function
f^{1/(d-1)}
is concave on its support
[-b,a]
and the functions
g^{1/(d-1)}
and
h^{1/(d-1)}
are affine on their respective supports
[-da,a]
and
[-\frac{a}{d},a]
, it follows that the functions
g-f
and
f-h
have exactly two sign changes and that they are non positive on
[t_{1},t_{2}]
and non negative outside
[t_{1},t_{2}]
. Let us define
v
to be either
g-f
or
f-h
. Then, for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
, one has
(t-t_{1})(t-t_{2})v(t)\geq 0
.
Integrating this inequality, we deduce that
\int t^{2}v(t)dt\geq 0
, which is the result.
∎

We shall deduce from the preceding theorem the following important technical lemma. We note that Lemma
21
(and its consequences) is the only part, where the isotropicity assumption is used.

Lemma 21
.

Let
d\geq 1
be an integer. There exist two constants
0<C_{d}^{\prime}<C_{d}
such that for any
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R_{+}}
centered, isotropic, log-concave density and for every
\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
,

C_{d}^{\prime}\leq\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}dr^{d-1}f(r\theta)dr\right)^{\frac{1}{d}}\leq C_{d},

(43)

where
C_{d}
and
C_{d}^{\prime}
are constants depending only on the dimension
d
. In fact, we may take

C_{d}^{\prime}=\frac{c_{1}^{d+2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}e^{\frac{3}{2}}}\quad\hbox{and}\quad C_{d}=(d+1)c_{2}^{d+2}L_{d},

(44)

where
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
are the absolute constants appearing in (
40
).

Proof.

The function
f
being isotropic, we have
\mathrm{Cov}(f)=\sigma^{2}I_{d}
, for some
\sigma>0
and
\int f=1
, thus
L_{f}=\max(f)^{\frac{1}{d}}\sigma
.
Note that proving (
43
) is equivalent to proving that

C_{d}^{\prime}B_{2}^{d}\subset f(0)^{\frac{1}{d}}K_{d}(f)\subset C_{d}B_{2}^{d}.

Applying Theorem
20
to the centered body
K_{d+1}(f)
, we get that for any
u\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
,

\frac{h_{K_{d+1}(f)}(u)^{2}}{d(d+2)}\leq\frac{1}{|K_{d+1}(f)|}\int_{K_{d+1}(f)}\langle x,u\rangle^{2}dx\leq\frac{d}{d+2}h_{K_{d+1}(f)}(u)^{2}.

(45)

Then, we use (
39
) for
p=2
to get that

\int_{K_{d+2}(f)}\langle x,u\rangle^{2}dx=\frac{1}{f(0)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\langle x,u\rangle^{2}f(x)dx=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{f(0)}.

This gives

c_{1}^{d+2}\frac{\sigma^{2}}{f(0)}\leq\int_{K_{d+1}(f)}\langle x,u\rangle^{2}dx\leq c_{2}^{d+2}\frac{\sigma^{2}}{f(0)}.

Using again the inclusion relations (
40
), we get upper and lower bound of
|K_{d+1}(f)|
by
|K_{d}(f)|=1/f(0)
, by (
39
) for
p=0
. Thus, altogether we have

c_{1}^{2d+2}\sigma^{2}\leq\frac{1}{|K_{d+1}(f)|}\int_{K_{d+1}(f)}\langle x,u\rangle^{2}dx\leq c_{2}^{2d+2}\sigma^{2}.

Using these inequalities, (
45
) and the inclusion relations (
40
), we get

\sqrt{\frac{d+2}{d}}c_{1}^{d+2}\sigma B_{2}^{d}\subset K_{d}(f)\subset\sqrt{d(d+2)}c_{2}^{d+2}\sigma B_{2}^{d}.

Now, from
[
6
, Theorem 4]
, we have
f(0)\geq e^{-d}\max(f)
hence

\frac{L_{f}}{e}\leq f(0)^{\frac{1}{d}}\sigma=\left(\frac{f(0)}{\max(f)}\right)^{\frac{1}{d}}L_{f}\leq L_{d}.

Using that

L_{f}\geq 1/\sqrt{2\pi e}
, we conclude.
∎

3.2
A concentration lemma

The following concentration lemma will be required to establish the concentration property 2.

Lemma 22

(Concentration Lemma)
.

Let
c_{d}:=3^{\frac{1}{d}}C_{d}
, where
C_{d}
is the constant from (
44
). Then, for every log-concave, isotropic, centered density function
f
and for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\|x\|_{2}\geq c_{d}/f(0)^{\frac{1}{d}}
,

f(x)\leq f(0)2^{-\|x\|_{2}\frac{f(0)^{\frac{1}{d}}}{c_{d}}}.

(46)

Proof.

Let
x_{\max}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a value where the maximum of
f
is attained and for every
\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, let
r_{\max}(\theta)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a value where the maximum of
r\mapsto f(r\theta)
is attained. Since
f
is log-concave, for every
\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, the function
r\mapsto f(r\theta)
is non-increasing for
r\geq r_{\max}(\theta)
. Therefore, for every
r_{0}(\theta)\geq r_{\max}(\theta)
,

\int_{0}^{\infty}dr^{d-1}f(r\theta)dr\geq\int_{r_{\max}(\theta)}^{r_{0}(\theta)}dr^{d-1}f(r\theta)dr\geq f(r_{0}(\theta))(r_{0}(\theta)^{d}-r_{\max}(\theta)^{d}).

(47)

We choose
r_{0}=r_{0}(\theta)
as

r_{0}(\theta)=\Bigl(2d\int_{0}^{\infty}{\frac{r^{d-1}f(r\theta)}{f(0)}dr}+r_{\max}^{d}\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{d}}\hskip 2.84544pt,

(48)

so that,
by (
47
), one has

f(r_{0}(\theta))\leq\frac{f(0)}{2}
.
Since
r\mapsto f(r\theta)
is non-decreasing on
[0,r_{\max}(\theta)]
, one has

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{+\infty}dr^{d-1}f(r\theta)dr\geq\int_{0}^{r_{\max}(\theta)}dr^{d-1}f(0)dr=f(0)r_{\max}(\theta)^{d},

and therefore, by Lemma
21
,

r_{\max}(\theta)\leq\frac{\big(d\int_{0}^{+\infty}r^{d-1}f(r\theta)dr\big)^{\frac{1}{d}}}{f(0)^{\frac{1}{d}}}\leq\frac{C_{d}}{f(0)^{\frac{1}{d}}},

where
C_{d}
is given by (
44
).
Thus, by (
48
) and the definition of
c_{d}
,

r_{0}(\theta)\leq\left(\frac{3}{f(0)}\right)^{\frac{1}{d}}C_{d}=\frac{c_{d}}{f(0)^{\frac{1}{d}}}.

Since for every
r\geq r_{0}(\theta)
, one has
r_{0}(\theta)=\frac{r_{0}(\theta)}{r}\cdot r+(1-\frac{r_{0}(\theta)}{r})\cdot 0
, by
log-concavity, we deduce

\displaystyle f(r)\leq f(0)\left(\frac{f(r_{0}(\theta))}{f(0)}\right)^{\frac{r}{r_{0}(\theta)}}\leq f(0)2^{-{\frac{r}{r_{0}(\theta)}}}\leq f(0)2^{-{\frac{rf(0)^{\frac{1}{d}}}{c_{d}}}}.

Hence, if
\|x\|_{2}\geq c_{d}/f(0)^{\frac{1}{d}}
, then

f(x)\leq f(0)2^{-\|x\|_{2}\frac{f(0)^{\frac{1}{d}}}{c_{d}}}.

∎

3.3
Sum of maxima of isotropic log-concave functions

The following lemma bounds the sum of the maxima of a log-concave density using our previous concentration result.

Lemma 23
.

Let
f
be a centered, isotropic, log-concave density on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with covariance
\sigma^{2}\mathrm{I}_{\mathrm{d}}
. Let
0\leq i\leq 2
and
0\leq j\leq d-1
. Then, as
\sigma\to\infty
,

\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}^{d-j}}|l_{1}|^{i}\max_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{j}}f(k_{1},\dots,k_{j},l_{1},\dots,l_{d-j})={O_{d}}\Bigl(\frac{1}{\sigma^{j-i}}\Bigr).

(49)

Proof.

Set
\lambda=c_{d}f(0)^{-1/d}
, with
c_{d}
is given in Lemma
22
. Then
f(x)\leq f(0)2^{-\|x\|_{2}/\lambda}
, for
\|x\|_{2}\geq\lambda
. Moreover, from the definition of
L_{f}
, its bounds and the inequality
f(0)\geq e^{-d}\max(f)
, we have

\frac{c_{d}\sigma}{L_{d}}\leq\lambda=c_{d}f(0)^{-1/d}\leq\frac{c_{d}e\sigma}{L_{f}}\leq c_{d}\sqrt{2\pi e^{3}}\sigma.

Note that

\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}^{d-j}}|l_{1}|^{i}\max_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{j}}f(k,l)=\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}^{d-j},\|l\|_{\infty}\leq\lambda}|l_{1}|^{i}\max_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{j}}f(k,l)+\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}^{d-j},\|l\|_{\infty}>\lambda}|l_{1}|^{i}\max_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{j}}f(k,l),

The first sum is easily upper bounded by
{O_{d}}(\frac{1}{\sigma^{j-i}})
. Indeed,

\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}^{d-j},\|l\|_{\infty}\leq\lambda}|l_{1}|^{i}\max_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{j}}f(k,l)\leq(\max f)(2\lambda)^{d+i-j}=\frac{L_{f}^{d}}{\sigma^{d}}(2\lambda)^{d+i-j}\leq\frac{L_{d}^{d}(2c_{d}\sqrt{2\pi e^{3}})^{d+i-j}}{\sigma^{j-i}}={O_{d}}\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{j-i}}\right).

Using the tails estimates of Lemma
22
and the fact that for
l\in\mathbb{Z}^{d-j}
, one has
\|l\|_{2}\geq\|l\|_{\infty}
and
\|l\|_{2}\geq\frac{\|l\|_{1}}{\sqrt{d-j}}\geq\frac{\|l\|_{1}}{\sqrt{d}}
the second sum can be expressed as follows

\displaystyle\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}^{d-j},\|l\|_{\infty}>\lambda}|l_{1}|^{i}\max_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{j}}f(k,l)

\displaystyle\leq f(0)\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}^{d-j},\|l\|_{\infty}>\lambda}|l_{1}|^{i}2^{-\frac{\|l\|_{1}}{\lambda\sqrt{d}}}

\displaystyle=f(0)\left(\sum_{|l_{1}|>\lambda}|l_{1}|^{i}2^{-\frac{|l_{1}|}{\lambda\sqrt{d}}}\right)\left(\sum_{|n|>\lambda}2^{-\frac{|n|}{\lambda\sqrt{d}}}\right)^{d-j-1}.

Putting
x=2^{-1/\lambda\sqrt{d}}
and assuming that
\lambda\sqrt{d}\geq 1
, we have
x\in[\frac{1}{2},1)
and
\frac{1}{2}\leq\lambda\sqrt{d}(1-x)\leq\ln(2)
. Thus

\sum_{|n|>\lambda}2^{-\frac{|n|}{\lambda\sqrt{d}}}=2\sum_{n>\lambda}x^{n}\leq\frac{2}{1-x}\leq 4\lambda\sqrt{d}.

Hence, for
i=0
, replacing
\lambda
by its value, for
\sigma\geq\frac{L_{d}}{\sqrt{d}c_{d}}
, we have
\lambda\geq 1/\sqrt{d}
and thus

\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}^{d-j},\|l\|_{\infty}>\lambda}\max_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{j}}f(k,l)\leq f(0)\left(4\lambda\sqrt{d}\right)^{d-j}=\left(4\sqrt{d}c_{d}\right)^{d-j}f(0)^{\frac{j}{d}}\leq\frac{(4\sqrt{d}c_{d})^{d-j}L_{d}^{j}}{\sigma^{j}}={O_{d}}\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{j}}\right).

And, for
i=1
, it is not difficult to see that, for
\lambda\geq 1/\sqrt{d}
, we have

\sum_{|l_{1}|>\lambda}|l_{1}|2^{-\frac{|l_{1}|}{\lambda\sqrt{d}}}=2\sum_{n>\lambda}nx^{n}=2x\left(\sum_{n>\lambda}x^{n}\right)^{\prime}\leq\frac{6}{(1-x)^{2}}\leq 24d\lambda^{2}.

Thus, again for
\sigma\geq\frac{L_{d}}{\sqrt{d}c_{d}}
, which ensures that
\lambda\geq 1/\sqrt{d}
, we deduce

\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}^{d-j},\|l\|_{\infty}>\lambda}|l_{1}|\max_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{j}}f(k,l)\leq f(0)\times 24d\lambda^{2}\left(4\lambda\sqrt{d}\right)^{d-j-1}\leq 2f(0)\left(4\lambda\sqrt{d}\right)^{d-j+1}={O_{d}}\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{j-1}}\right).

In the same way, for
i=2
, we easily get that, for
\lambda\geq 1/\sqrt{d}
,

\sum_{|l_{1}|>\lambda}l_{1}^{2}2^{-\frac{|l_{1}|}{\lambda\sqrt{d}}}=2\sum_{n>\lambda}n^{2}x^{n}=2x^{2}\left(\sum_{n>\lambda}x^{n}\right)^{\prime\prime}+2\sum_{n>\lambda}nx^{n}\leq\frac{14}{(1-x)^{3}}+\frac{12}{(1-x)^{2}}\leq 26(2\lambda\sqrt{d})^{3}.

Thus, again for
\sigma\geq\frac{L_{d}}{\sqrt{d}c_{d}}
, which ensures that
\lambda\geq 1/\sqrt{d}
, we deduce

\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}^{d-j},\|l\|_{\infty}>\lambda}l_{1}^{2}\max_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{j}}f(k,l)\leq f(0)\times 26(2\lambda\sqrt{d})^{3}\left(4\lambda\sqrt{d}\right)^{d-j-1}\leq 4f(0)\left(4\lambda\sqrt{d}\right)^{d-j+2}={O_{d}}\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{j-2}}\right).

∎

3.4
Approximating the integral, mean and covariance discretely

In this section, we will approximate the isotropic constant of an isotropic, log-concave density
f\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
in a discrete way. To do so, we will first approximate the integral as a sum and subsequently approximate the continuous covariance of
f
by its discrete covariance.

Proposition 24
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a log-concave isotropic density function with covariance matrix of the form
\sigma^{2}\mathrm{I}_{\mathrm{d}}
. Then

\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}fdx-\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}f(k)\right|={O_{d}}\Bigl(\frac{1}{\sigma}\Bigr)\hskip 2.84544pt.

Proof.

Let us proceed by induction. Let us first consider the one dimensional base case. Let
f:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a log-concave function and let suppose that the maximum of
f
is attained at
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
. Let
k_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
k_{0}\leq x_{0}<k_{0}+1
. Then, one has

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}fdx

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{k}^{k+1}f(x)dx

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{k<k_{0}}f(k)+\sum_{k\geq k_{0}+1}f(k+1)+\min\{f(k_{0}),f(k_{0}+1)\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}f(k)-f(k_{0})-f(k_{0}+1)+\min\{f(k_{0}),f(k_{0}+1)\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}f(k)-\max\{f(k_{0}),f(k_{0}+1)\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}f(k)-\max_{\mathbb{Z}}f.

The reverse inequality is analogous and thus one gets

\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}fdx-\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}f(k)\right|\leq\max_{\mathbb{Z}}f\leq\max_{\mathbb{R}}f.

(50)

For
d=1
, we get
\max_{\mathbb{R}}(f)=\frac{L_{f}}{\sigma}\leq\frac{1}{\sigma}
, by
[
7
]
, which proves the base case. Notice that the inequality (
50
) holds as soon as
f
is quasi-concave on
\mathbb{R}
, i.e. if
f
is first non-decreasing then non-increasing and not necessarily a density.

Now, let
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
and let

F(y):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x,y)dx
.
Note that
F
is log-concave as the marginal of a log-concave function. Moreover
F
is an isotropic density and
\mathrm{Cov}_{\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}(F)=\sigma^{2}I_{d-1}
. One has

\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x,y)dxdy-\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}f(k)\right|\leq\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}F(y)dy-\sum_{y\in\mathbb{Z}^{d-1}}F(y)\right|+\sum_{y\in\mathbb{Z}^{d-1}}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x,y)dx-\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}f(k,y)\right|.

By the inductive hypothesis the first term is upper bounded by
C_{d-1}/\sigma
for some constant
C_{d-1}>0
. Since for every
y\in\mathbb{Z}^{d-1}
, the function
x\mapsto f(x,y)
is quasi-concave, one may use (
50
) to get that the second term is upper bounded by

\sum_{y\in\mathbb{Z}^{d-1}}\max_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}f(x,y).

We conclude by Lemma
23
.
∎

A remark from convex geometry: the case of isotropic convex bodies.
Applying the previous proposition to
f=\mathds{1}_{K}/|K|_{d}
, where
K
is an isotropic convex body in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and using that, in this case,
\sigma=L_{K}|K|^{1/d}
, we deduce the following proposition.

Proposition 25
.

For any isotropic convex body
K
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\left|\frac{\#(K\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d})}{|K|_{d}}-1\right|\leq\frac{M_{d}}{|K|^{\frac{1}{d}}}\hskip 2.84544pt,

for some constant
M_{d}>0
, depending only on the dimension,
where
\#K
denotes the cardinality of a discrete set
K
.

Remark 26
.

The hypothesis of isotropicity is necessary, since in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, for
d\geq 2
,
it is easy to construct convex bodies that do not contain integer points, but whose volumes are increasingly large.

Proposition 27
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a centered, isotropic, log-concave density. Then, for
1\leq i\leq d
,

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}k_{i}f(k)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}x_{i}f(x)dx+O_{d}(1)=O_{d}(1).\hskip 2.84544pt

In particular, for
d=1
, the following finite bound holds for any log-concave centered integrable function

\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}xf(x)dx-\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}kf(k)\right|\leq(e+1)\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}f(k)\hskip 2.84544pt.

Proof.

The proof is by induction again. Let us make a one dimensional remark that will be used on the base case of the induction. Let
f:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a centered log-concave function and let suppose that the maximum of
f
is attained at
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
and let us suppose without loss of generality that
x_{0}>0
. Then since
f
is log-concave and by the inequality
[
6
, Theorem 4]
one has

\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)dx\geq\int_{0}^{x_{0}}f(x)dx\geq f(0)x_{0}\geq\frac{\max f}{e}x_{0}\hskip 2.84544pt.

(51)

Hence, we get
x_{0}\max f\leq e\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)dx
. The case of the summation is analogous. Let
k_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}
be such that
k_{0}\leq x_{0}<k_{0}+1
. Then, we deduce

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}f(k)\geq\sum_{k=0}^{k_{0}}f(k)\geq(k_{0}+1)f(0)\geq\frac{\max f}{e}(k_{0}+1)\hskip 2.84544pt.

(52)

Let us start the induction. Let
f
be a centered, isotropic, log-concave density and let suppose that the maximum of
f
is attained at
x_{0}
. We decompose the integral
\int_{\mathbb{R}}xf(x)dx
as follows

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}xf(x)dx

\displaystyle=\sum_{k<0}\int_{k}^{k+1}xf(x)dx+\sum_{k=0}^{k_{0}-1}\int_{k}^{k+1}xf(x)dx+\int_{k_{0}}^{k_{0}+1}xf(x)dx+\sum_{k\geq k_{0}+1}\int_{k}^{k+1}xf(x)dx

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{k<0}kf(k+1)+\sum_{k=0}^{k_{0}-1}kf(k)+k_{0}\min\{f(k_{0}),f(k_{0}+1)\}+\sum_{k\geq k_{0}+1}kf(k+1)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}kf(k)-k_{0}f(k_{0})-k_{0}f(k_{0}+1)+k_{0}\min\{f(k_{0}),f(k_{0}+1)\}-\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}f(k).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}xf(x)dx

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}kf(k)-\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}f(k)-k_{0}\max f

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}kf(k)-\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}f(k)-e\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}f(k)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}kf(k)-(e+1)\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}f(k),

where in the last line we have used the inequality (
52
). Bounding from the above
\int_{\mathbb{R}}xf(x)dx
we obtain a similar inequality.
Thus, we get

\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}xf(x)dx-\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}kf(k)\right|\leq(e+1)\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}f(k)\hskip 2.84544pt,

and the one-dimensional case follows by Proposition
24
.

For the inductive step, let
F:\mathbb{R}^{d-1}\to\mathbb{R}
be defined, for
x\in\mathbb{R},y\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
, by

F(y):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x,y)dx.

Then
F
is log-concave, centered and isotropic as the marginal of a log-concave, centered and isotropic density and, for
i=1,\ldots,d-1

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}y_{i}f(x,y)dxdy=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}y_{i}F(y)dy.

We have

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}y_{i}f(x,y)dxdy-\sum_{k_{1}\in\mathbb{Z},k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d-1}}k_{i}f(k_{1},k)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}y_{i}f(x,y)dxdy-\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d-1}}k_{i}F(k)\right|+\left|\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d-1}}k_{i}F(k)-\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}k_{i}f(k)\right|.

But the first term is
O_{d}(1)
by inductive hypothesis and for the second we note that
f(\cdot,k)
is log-concave for every
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d-1}
, so we may apply (
50
) to get, for any
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d-1}
,

\left|F(k)-\sum_{k_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}}f(k_{1},k)\right|=\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}{f(x,k)dx}-\sum_{k_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}}{f(k_{1},k)}\right|\leq\max_{x\in\mathbb{R}}f(x,k),

which gives

\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}y_{i}f(x,y)dxdy-\sum_{k_{1}\in\mathbb{Z},k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d-1}}k_{i}f(k_{1},k)\right|\leq O_{d}(1)+\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d-1}}k_{i}\max_{x\in\mathbb{R}}f(x,k)=O_{d}\bigl({1}\bigr),

where the last equality follows by Lemma
23
.
∎

Now we wish to show that a continuous isotropic, log-concave density
f
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is almost isotropic in the discrete sense, meaning that its discrete covariance matrix
\mathrm{Cov}_{\mathbb{Z}}{(f)}
has
O(\sigma)
off-diagonal elements and
\sigma^{2}+O(\sigma)
diagonal elements.

Proposition 28
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a centered, isotropic, log-concave density with
\mathrm{Cov}{(f)}=\sigma^{2}\mathrm{I}_{\mathrm{d}}
. Then

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}f(k)k_{i}^{2}=\sigma^{2}+O_{d}\big(\sigma\big),\hskip 2.84544pt\text{for every }\ 1\leq i\leq d.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on the dimension.
For the one-dimensional case let

x_{0}=\inf\{x\in\mathbb{R}:f(x)=\max{f}\}
.
Then

\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)x^{2}=\int_{x\leq x_{0}}f(x)x^{2}dx+\int_{x>x_{0}}f(x)x^{2}dx.

Noting that
f
is non-decreasing on
(-\infty,x_{0}]
and non-increasing on
[x_{0},+\infty)
by log-concavity, we get

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)x^{2}dx

\displaystyle=\sum_{k<\lfloor x_{0}\rfloor}\int_{[k,k+1)}f(x)x^{2}dx+\sum_{k>\lfloor x_{0}\rfloor}\int_{[k,k+1)}f(x)x^{2}dx+\int_{\lfloor x_{0}\rfloor}^{\lfloor x_{0}\rfloor+1}f(x)x^{2}dx

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k<\lfloor x_{0}\rfloor}f(k+1)(k^{2}+k+\frac{1}{3})+\sum_{k>\lfloor x_{0}\rfloor}f(k)(k^{2}+k+\frac{1}{3})+\int_{\lfloor x_{0}\rfloor}^{\lfloor x_{0}\rfloor+1}f(x)x^{2}dx

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}f(k)k^{2}-\sum_{k<\lfloor x_{0}\rfloor}f(k+1)k-\sum_{k>\lfloor x_{0}\rfloor}f(k)k+f(x_{0})\Bigl(\lfloor x_{0}\rfloor^{2}+\lfloor x_{0}\rfloor+\frac{1}{3}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}f(k)k^{2}-\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}f(k)k+f(x_{0})\Bigl(\lfloor x_{0}\rfloor^{2}+\lfloor x_{0}\rfloor+\frac{1}{3}\Bigr)

(53)

Now we observe that
f(x_{0})=\max{f}\leq\frac{1}{\sigma}
and, by (
51
),
x_{0}=O({\sigma})
. Hence, the last term in (
53
)
is
O(\sigma)
.
Furthermore, by Proposition
24
, the second term in (
53
) is
O(1)
. Thus,

\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)x^{2}dx\leq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}f(k)k^{2}+O(\sigma).

Similarly, using the piecewise monotonicity of
f
in the reverse way, we obtain

\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)x^{2}dx\geq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}f(k)k^{2}+O(\sigma).

This proves the base case.

Let
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
and let

F(y):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x,y)dx
. Then, by the inductive hypothesis applied to
F
, for
i=1,\ldots,d-1,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x,y)y_{i}^{2}dxdy=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}F(y)y_{i}^{2}dy=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d-1}}F(k)k_{i}^{2}+O_{d}(\sigma)\hskip 2.84544pt.

(54)

Since, for every
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d-1}
, the function
f(\cdot,k)
is log-concave in
\mathbb{R}
, we have, by (
50
),

\left|F(k)-\sum_{k^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}}f(k^{\prime},k)\right|=\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x,k)dx-\sum_{k^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}}f(k^{\prime},k)\right|\leq\max_{x}f(x,k)\hskip 2.84544pt.

(55)

So by (
54
) and (
55
) we get, for
i=1,\ldots,d-1

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x,y)y_{i}^{2}dxdy

\displaystyle=\sum_{k^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z},k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d-1}}f(k^{\prime},k)k_{i}^{2}+\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d-1}}O(\max_{x}f(x,k))k_{i}^{2}+O_{d}(\sigma)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z},k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d-1}}f(k^{\prime},k)k_{i}^{2}+O_{d}(\sigma),

by Lemma
23
, and the result follows.

∎

We will need the following lemma for the base case of the induction of Proposition
30
.

Lemma 29
.

Let
f
be a centered, isotropic, log-concave density in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, with
\mathrm{Cov}{(f)}=\sigma^{2}\mathrm{I}_{\mathrm{2}}
. For
x\in\mathbb{R}
, let

N_{\max}(x):=\inf\{y\in\mathbb{R}:f(x,y)=\max_{y}{f(x,y)}\}.

Then

\int_{\mathbb{R}}xN_{\max}(x)\max_{y}f(x,y)dx=O(\sigma)

(56)

and

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}{kN_{\max}(k)\max_{y}f(k,y)}=O(\sigma).

(57)

Proof.

Let
c
be an absolute constant to be determined later and denote the set
A_{\sigma}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}:|x|,|N_{\max}(x)|\leq c\sigma\}.
We have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}xN_{\max}(x)\max_{y}f(x,y)dx=\int_{A_{\sigma}}xN_{\max}(x)\max_{y}f(x,y)dx+\int_{A_{\sigma}^{\mathrm{C}}}xN_{\max}(x)\max_{y}f(x,y)dx

The first term can be trivially bounded as

\Bigl|\int_{A_{\sigma}}xN_{\max}(x)\max_{y}f(x,y)dx\Bigr|\leq O(\sigma^{3})\max{f}=O(\sigma).

For the second term we have, for some absolute constant
C^{\prime}_{2}
and for
c
large enough,

\displaystyle\Bigl|\int_{A_{\sigma}^{\mathrm{C}}}|x|N_{\max}(x)\max_{y}f(x,y)dx\Bigr|

\displaystyle\leq\max(f)\int_{A_{\sigma}^{\mathrm{C}}}{xN_{\max}(x)e^{-\frac{|x|+N_{\max}(x)}{\sigma}C^{\prime}_{2}}dx}

\displaystyle\leq O\Bigl(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\Bigr)\sigma\int_{x>c\sigma}{xe^{-\frac{x}{\sigma}C^{\prime}_{2}}dx}

(58)

\displaystyle=O(\sigma).

(59)

Here (
58
) follows by using the fact that
ye^{-y}\leq 1
. This gives (
56
),
(
57
) follows in a similar way.
∎

Proposition 30
.

Let
f
be a centered, continuous, isotropic, log-concave density in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with
\mathrm{Cov}{(f)}=\sigma^{2}\mathrm{I}_{\mathrm{d}}
. Then, for all
i\neq j

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}f(k)k_{i}k_{j}=O_{d}(\sigma).

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
d
. Consider first the base case
d=2
.
Then, letting
N_{\max}(x):=\inf\{y\in\mathbb{R}:f(x,y)=\max_{y}{f(x,y)}\}
and
M_{\max}(y):=\inf\{x\in\mathbb{R}:f(x,y)=\max_{x}{f(x,y)}\}
and assuming without loss of generality that the infima are achieved,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}{f(x,y)xydx}=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}{x\int_{0\leq y\leq N_{\max}(x)}{f(x,y)ydy}+\int_{y>N_{\max}(x)}{f(x,y)ydy}dx}.

Now

\displaystyle{\int_{0\leq y\leq N_{\max}(x)}{f(x,y)ydy}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{0\leq k\leq N_{\max}(x)}{\int_{[k,k+1)}{f(x,y)ydy}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{0\leq k\leq N_{\max}(x)-1}{(k+1)f(x,k+1)}+\max_{y}f(x,y)(N_{\max}(x)+1)

(60)

and

\displaystyle{\int_{y>N_{\max}(x)}{f(x,y)ydy}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\geq N_{\max}(x)}{(k+1)f(x,k)}.

(61)

By (
60
) and (
61
)

\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}{f(x,y)xydx}\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}{x\Bigl(\sum_{k\geq 0}{kf(x,k)}+\sum_{k\geq N_{\max}(x)}{f(x,k)}+\max_{y}f(x,y)(N_{\max}(x)+1)\Bigr)dx}

(62)

For the first term in (
62
) we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}{x\sum_{k\geq 0}{kf(x,k)}dx}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\geq 0}{\sum_{0\leq m\leq M_{\max}(k)-1}{k(m+1)f(m+1,k)}}+\sum_{k\geq 0}{\sum_{m>M_{\max}(k)}{k(m+1)f(m,k)}}

\displaystyle+\sum_{k\geq 0}{k(M_{\max}(k)+1)\max_{x}f(x,k)}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\geq 0}\sum_{m\geq 0}{kmf(m,k)}+\sum_{k\geq 0}{\sum_{m\geq 0}{kf(m,k)}}+\sum_{k\geq 0}{k(M_{\max}(k)+1)\max_{x}f(x,k)}

(63)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\geq 0}\sum_{m\geq 0}{kmf(m,k)}+\sum_{k\geq 0}{\sum_{m\geq 0}{kf(m,k)}}+O(\sigma),

(64)

where the last inequality follows by Lemma
29
.
On the other hand, for the second term in (
62
) we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\sum_{k\geq N_{\max}}xf(x,k)dx}\leq\Bigl(\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}x^{2}f(x,k)dx}\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{2}}=O(\sigma)

(65)

by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and Proposition
28
.
Finally, the third term in (
62
) is

\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\max_{y}f(x,y)(N_{\max}(x)+1)dx}=O(\sigma)

(66)

by Lemma
29
.
Plugging (
64
), (
65
) and (
66
) into (
62
) we obtain

\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}{f(x,y)xydx}\leq\sum_{k\geq 0}\sum_{m\geq 0}{kmf(m,k)}+\sum_{k\geq 0}{\sum_{m\geq 0}{kf(m,k)}}+O(\sigma).

(67)

Using the reverse bounds to those in (
60
) and (
61
) obtained by the monotonicity of
f
, we get
the lower bound

\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}{f(x,y)xydx}\geq\sum_{k\geq 0}\sum_{m\geq 0}{kmf(m,k)}+\sum_{k\geq 0}{\sum_{m\geq 0}{kf(m,k)}}+O(\sigma).

In a completely analogous way, we may bound the integral on the rest of the quadrants and obtain the approximations

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}_{-}^{2}}{f(x,y)xydx}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\leq 0}\sum_{m\leq 0}{kmf(m,k)}+\sum_{k\leq 0}{\sum_{m\leq 0}{kf(m,k)}}+O(\sigma),

(68)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}_{-}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}}{f(x,y)xydx}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 0}\sum_{m\leq 0}{kmf(m,k)}+\sum_{k\geq 0}{\sum_{m\leq 0}{kf(m,k)}}+O(\sigma)\quad\text{and}

(69)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{-}}{f(x,y)xydx}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\leq 0}\sum_{m\geq 0}{kmf(m,k)}+\sum_{k\leq 0}{\sum_{m\geq 0}{kf(m,k)}}+O(\sigma).

(70)

Since, by Proposition
27
,
\sum_{k}{\sum_{m}{kf(m,k)}}=O(1)
, by adding (
67
)–(
70
) we obtain

0=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}{f(x,y)xydx}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}{kmf(m,k)}+O(\sigma).

This completes the base case
d=2
.

The inductive step is similar to that in the proof of Proposition
28
. Let
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
y=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{d-1})\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
and let

F(y):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x,y)dx
. Then, by the inductive hypothesis, for
1\leq i,j\leq d-1,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x,y)y_{i}y_{j}dy=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}F(y)y_{i}y_{j}dy=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d-1}}F(k)k_{i}k_{j}+O_{d}(\sigma)\hskip 2.84544pt.

(71)

Since for every
k=(k_{1},\ldots,k_{d-1})\in\mathbb{Z}^{d-1}
, the function
f(\cdot,k)
is log-concave
\mathbb{R}
, we have by Proposition
24

F(k)-\sum_{k^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}}f(k^{\prime},k)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x,k)dx-\sum_{k^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}}f(k^{\prime},k)=O_{d}\bigl(\max_{x}f(x,k)\bigr)\hskip 2.84544pt.

(72)

So by (
71
) and (
72
) we get, for
1\leq i,j\leq d-1

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x,y)y_{i}y_{j}dxdy=\sum_{k^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z},k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d-1}}f(k^{\prime},k)k_{i}k_{j}+\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d-1}}O\Bigl(\max_{x}f(x,k)\Bigr)k_{i}k_{j}+O_{d}(\sigma).

(73)

But by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and Lemma
23
,
\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d-1}}O\Bigl(\max_{x}f(x,k)\Bigr)k_{i}k_{j}=O_{d}(\sigma)
and the result follows.
∎

Corollary 31
.

Let
f
be a centered, isotropic, log-concave density in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with
\mathrm{Cov}{(f)}=\sigma^{2}\mathrm{I}_{d}.
Then

\det\Bigl(\mathrm{Cov}_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}{(f)}\Bigr)=\sigma^{2d}+O_{d}(\sigma^{2d-1}).

Proof.

By Proposition
30

[\mathrm{Cov}_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}(f)]_{ij}=O_{d}(\sigma),\quad\text{for }i\neq j

and

[\mathrm{Cov}_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}(f)]_{ij}=\sigma^{2}+O_{d}(\sigma),\quad\text{for }i=j.

Thus,

\displaystyle\det\Bigl(\mathrm{Cov}_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}{(f)}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\tau}\mathrm{sgn}(\tau)\prod_{i=1}^{d}\mathrm{Cov}_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}(f)_{i\tau(i)}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2d}+O(\sigma^{2d-1}),

since for
\tau
being the identity permutation
\mathrm{sgn}(\tau)\prod_{i=1}^{d}{\mathrm{Cov}_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}{(f)}_{i\tau(i)}}=\bigl(\sigma^{2}+O(\sigma)\bigr)^{d}=\sigma^{2d}+O(\sigma^{2d-1})
and for any other permutation
\tau^{\prime}

\prod_{i=1}^{d}{\mathrm{Cov}_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}{(f)}_{i\tau^{\prime}(i)}}=O((\sigma^{2})^{d-2}\sigma^{2})=O_{d}(\sigma^{2d-2}).

∎

Finally, Corollary
32
below is a version of Theorem
6
with the assumption that the continuous function
f
is isotropic. It can be observed from the proof that the dependence of of
C^{\prime}_{d}
on
d
can be taken to be the same as in the continuous case (cf. (
10
)). It can also be seen that due to use of Corollary
31
,
\sigma
needs to be taken at least
\Omega(d!)
. We do not know whether this is the best (lowest) rate.

Corollary 32

(
Theorem
6
for isotropic
f
).

Let
p
be log-concave p.m.f. on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, whose continuous log-concave extension, say
f
, is isotropic. Then there exists a constant
C^{\prime}_{d}
that depends on the dimension only, such that

\max_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}{p(k)}\leq\frac{C^{\prime}_{d}}{\det\Bigl(\mathrm{Cov}(p)\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{2}}}

provided that
\sigma:=\det{\Bigl(\mathrm{Cov}_{\mathbb{R}}{(f)}\Bigr)}
is large enough depending on
d
.

Proof.

Since
p
is extensible log-concave, there exists a continuous log-concave function
f
(not necessarily a density) such that
f(k)=p(k)
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and by assumption
f
is isotropic. Thus

\max_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}p(k)\leq\max_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)=\frac{L_{f}^{d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f}{\sigma^{d}}\leq\frac{C_{d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}{f}}{\sigma^{d}}\hskip 2.84544pt,

(74)

where
C_{d}
is an upper bound of
L_{f}^{d}
.
Noting that by definition
\mathrm{Cov}{(Zp)}=\mathrm{Cov}{(p)}
for any normalising constant
Z\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and applying Proposition
24
and Corollary
31
to
\frac{f(\cdot)}{\int{f}}
, we obtain

\max_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}p(k)\leq\frac{C_{d}(1+O_{d}(\frac{1}{\sigma}))}{\Bigl(\det\Bigl(\mathrm{Cov}{(p)}\Bigr)+O_{d}(\sigma^{2d-1})\Bigr)^{1/2}}\leq\frac{2C_{d}}{\det\Bigl(\mathrm{Cov}{(p)}\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{2}}}

provided that
\sigma
is large enough depending on

, using that
O_{d}(\sigma^{2d-1})\geq-\frac{1}{2}\det\Bigl(\mathrm{Cov}{(p)}\Bigr)\simeq-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2d}
as
\sigma\to\infty
.
∎

Proposition 33
.

Fix
d\geq 1
and let
p
be a log-concave p.m.f. on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
with almost isotropic extension. Then, for every
1\leq i\leq d
,

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}|p(k)-p(k-e_{i})|=O_{d}\left(\frac{1}{\det{\left(\mathrm{Cov}{(p)}\right)^{\frac{1}{2d}}}}\right),

where
e_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
is defined as the vector with the
i
-th coordinate
1
and all the other coordinates
0
.

Proof.

For any one-dimensional quasi-concave non-negative real sequence
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
,
\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}|a_{n}-a_{n-1}|\leq 2\max_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}a_{n}
. Thus, by log-concavity, denoting
\overline{k_{i}}=\sum_{j\neq i}k_{j}e_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d-1}
, one has

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}|p(k)-p(k-e_{i})|\leq 2\sum_{\overline{k_{i}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d-1}}\max_{k_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}}p(k_{1},\ldots,k_{i-1},k_{i},k_{i+1},\ldots,k_{d}),

Hence, as in (
49
), we have

\sum_{\overline{k_{i}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d-1}}\max_{k_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}}p(k_{1},\ldots,k_{i-1},k_{i},k_{i+1},\ldots,k_{d})=O_{d}\Bigl(\frac{1}{\det{\bigl(\mathrm{Cov}{(p)}\bigr)^{\frac{1}{2d}}}}\Bigr)

and the result follows.
∎

3.5
Relaxing the isotropicity assumption on
f

In this section, we relax the assumptions of Lemma
21
, so that we obtain conclusions of the same kind under some weaker assumptions on the eigenvalues of the continuous covariance matrix
\mathrm{Cov}_{\mathbb{R}}{(f)}
. Specifically, we will prove:

Proposition 34
.

For every centered and log-concave density
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and for every
\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
one has

c_{1}^{d+2}f(0)^{\frac{1}{d}}\sqrt{\lambda_{\min}(\mathrm{Cov}(f))}\leq\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{d-1}f(r\theta)dr\right)^{\frac{1}{d}}\leq(d+1)c_{2}^{d+2}f(0)^{\frac{1}{d}}\sqrt{\lambda_{\max}(\mathrm{Cov}(f))}.

where
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
are the constants appearing in (
40
).

Before giving the proof, we mention the consequences of Proposition
34
.

Corollary 35
.

Let
f
be a a centered, almost isotropic log-concave density. Then for every
\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}

\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{d-1}f(r\theta)dr=\Theta_{d}(1),

as
\sigma:=\det{(\mathrm{Cov}{(f)})}\to\infty
.

Proof.

Since
f
is almost isotropic, we have

\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}\big(\mathrm{Cov}(f)\big)\simeq_{d}\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}\big(\mathrm{Cov}(f)\big)\simeq_{d}\sigma^{2}

as
\sigma\to\infty
. The result follows from Proposition
34
since
f(0)\simeq_{d}\max{f}\simeq_{d}\frac{1}{\sigma^{d}}
.
∎

Remark 36
.

The isotropicity assumption was only used in Lemma
21
, which in turn allowed us to prove the concentration Lemma
22
which was repeatedly evoked in Section
3
to bound the sums of maxima of log-concave functions and thus also the error terms. By Corollary
35
, a version of Lemma
22
holds true for almost isotropic densities, although with possibly different constants and for large enough
\sigma
. Therefore, all the previous results of Section
3
hold true and thus also the statements of Theorem
3
and
4
under the almost isotropic log-concave assumption.

Remark 37
.

Note that if the function
f
is in addition isotropic i.e.

\mathrm{Cov}{(f)}=\sigma^{2}\mathrm{I}_{d}
, one has that
f(0)\simeq_{d}\frac{1}{\sigma^{d}}
and so we recover the result of Lemma
21
.

For the above-mentioned proposition, the following lemma which gives bounds for the inradius and circumradius of convex bodies in not necessarily isotropic position will be essential .

Proof of Proposition
34
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a centered log-concave density. For simplicity, we denote
\lambda_{\min}=\lambda_{\min}(\mathrm{Cov}(f))
and analogously for
\lambda_{\max}
. Following the same steps as in the proof of Lemma
21
, we get that, for any
u\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
,

\frac{\lambda_{\min}}{f(0)}\leq\int_{K_{d+2}(f)}\langle x,u\rangle^{2}dx=\frac{1}{f(0)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\langle x,u\rangle^{2}f(x)dx\leq\frac{\lambda_{\max}}{f(0)}.

Using the inclusion relations (
40
), this gives

c_{1}^{2d+2}\lambda_{\min}\leq\frac{1}{|K_{d+1}(f)|}\int_{K_{d+1}(f)}\langle x,u\rangle^{2}dx\leq c_{2}^{2d+2}\lambda_{\max}.

Using these inequalities, (
45
) and again the inclusion relations (
40
), we get

\sqrt{\frac{d+2}{d}}c_{1}^{d+2}\sqrt{\lambda_{\min}}B_{2}^{d}\subset K_{d}(f)\subset\sqrt{d(d+2)}c_{2}^{d+2}\sqrt{\lambda_{\max}}B_{2}^{d},

which gives inequality (
34
).

∎

4
Concluding Remarks and Open Questions

1.

As noted in the Introduction, for
d=1
, our definition of log-concavity is equivalent to the usual definition
p(k)^{2}\geq p(k-1)p(k+1),k\in\mathbb{Z}
, which is preserved under convolution (e.g.
[
14
]
). However, for
d>1
log-concavity may not be preserved in general as we have already observed in Remark
10
.

On the other hand in the i.i.d. case, Proposition
38
below shows that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}A
is convex for any discrete convex set
A
, meaning that
\mathrm{conv}(A)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}=A
. We do not know whether our definition of log-concavity is preserved under self-convolution.

Proposition 38
.

Let
A\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
be
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
convex. Then, for any integer
n\geq 2
,
A_{n}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}A
is
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
- convex.

Proof.

It is clear that

A_{n}\subset\mathrm{conv}(A_{n})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}.

For the inverse inclusion, we first note that
\mathrm{conv}(A_{n})=n\mathrm{conv}(A)
. Thus, if
a\in\mathrm{conv}(A_{n})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, then there are
a_{1},a_{2}\in A
and
\lambda\in[0,1]
such that
a=n\bigl((1-\lambda)a_{1}+\lambda a_{2}\bigr)\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}.

We distinguish
n
cases depending on the value of
\lambda
. If
\frac{k}{n}\leq\lambda\leq\frac{k+1}{n}
for
k
integer such that
0\leq k\leq n-1
, we express
a
conveniently as follows

a=(n\lambda-k)a_{2}+\big(1-(n\lambda-k)\big)a_{1}+(n-k-1)a_{1}+ka_{2}.

Thus, we get that
a\in\mathrm{conv}(A)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}+A_{n-k-1}+A_{k}
. By
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
-convexity,

\mathrm{conv}(A)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}=A
and
A_{n-k-1}+A_{k}=A_{n-1}
. Therefore,
a\in A+A_{n-1}=A_{n}
.
∎

It is therefore natural to ask the following question:

Question 39
.

Let
X_{1},X_{2}
be i.i.d. log-concave random vectors on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. Is
X_{1}+X_{2}
log-concave? Furthermore, if
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
are i.i.d. log-concave, is
\sum_{i=1}^{n}{X_{i}}
log-concave for every
n
?

We note here that our assumptions of Definition
2
are preserved up to constants under convolution as shown in Proposition
13
and therefore, assuming that the p.m.f. of
X_{1}
is log-concave ensures that the p.m.f. of the sum also satisfies these assumptions.

Answering Question
39
even for quantized multivariate Gaussians seems to be non-trivial. Nevertheless, the following simple example shows that the answer is positive for quantized isotropic multivariate Gaussians.

Example 40
.

Let
X_{1}
be a random variable with p.m.f. on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
proportional to
e^{-\frac{\|k\|_{2}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}
; that is
X_{1}
is a multivariate centered isotropic Gaussian quantized on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. Then
X_{1}
has the distribution of
(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{d})
, where
Z_{i}
are i.i.d. one-dimensional quantized centered Gaussians with variance
\sigma^{2}
.

Note that if
p
is a p.m.f. on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
which is a product of log-concave p.m.f.s
\{p_{i}\}_{i=1}^{d}
on
\mathbb{Z}

p(k)=\prod_{i=1}^{d}p^{(i)}(k_{i}),\quad k=(k_{1},\ldots,k_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},

then
p
is clearly log-concave on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.

Therefore, since log-concavity is preserved under convolution in dimension one
[
4
]
and the coordinates of
\sum_{i=1}^{n}{X_{i}}
are independent, it follows that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}{X_{i}}
is log-concave for every
n
.

2.

For Theorem
18
and therefore for Theorems
3
and
4
as well, we have assumed that there exists a continuous extension
f
, which is almost isotropic. We have then shown that
f
is also almost isotropic in the discrete sense. It would be more natural to start with the assumption that the discrete p.m.f. is isotropic. However, in this case we would not be able to use the continuous toolkit to prove our concentration lemma in Section
3.2
. Nevertheless, we suspect that if the discrete p.m.f. is isotropic, then there exists a continuous extension that is almost isotropic and therefore our results for discrete log-concave distributions would still hold under this assumption:

Question 41
.

Let
X
be a log-concave random vector with p.m.f.
p
on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. Assume that
p
is almost isotropic. Is there a continuous log-concave extension of
p
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
which is almost isotropic?
