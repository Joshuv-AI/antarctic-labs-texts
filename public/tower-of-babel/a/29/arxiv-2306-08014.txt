Realising Synthetic Active Inference Agents, Part I: Epistemic Objectives and Graphical Specification Language
Magnus Koudahl, Thijs van de Laar, Bert de Vries

Abstract
The Free Energy Principle is a theoretical framework for describing how (intelligent) systems self-organise into coherent, stable structures by minimising a free energy functional. Active Inference is a corollary of the Free Energy Principle that specifically details how systems that are able to plan for the future (agents) function by minimising particular free energy functionals that incorporate information seeking components. This paper is the first in a series of two where we derive a synthetic version of Active Inference on free form factor graphs. The present paper focuses on deriving a local version of the free energy functionals used for Active Inference. This enables us to construct a version of Active Inference which applies to arbitrary graphical models and interfaces with prior work on message passing algorithms. The resulting messages are derived in our companion paper. We also identify a gap in the graphical notation used for factor graphs. While factor graphs are great at expressing a generative model, they have so far been unable to specify the full optimisation problem including constraints. To solve this problem we develop constrained Forney-style factor graph notation which permits a fully graphical description of variational inference objectives. We then proceed to show how constrained Forney-style factor graphs can be used to reconstruct prior algorithms for Active Inference as well as derive new ones. The latter is demonstrated by deriving an algorithm that permits direct policy inference for Active Inference agents, circumventing a long standing scaling issue that has so far hindered the application of Active Inference in industrial settings. We demonstrate our algorithm on the classic T-maze task and show that it reproduces the information seeking behaviour that is a hallmark feature of Active Inference.



1
Introduction

Active Inference (AIF) is an emerging framework for modelling intelligent agents interacting with an environment. Originating in the field of computational neuroscience, it has since been spread to numerous other fields such as modern machine learning. At its core, Active Inference relies on variational inference techniques to minimise a free energy functional. A key differentiator of Active Inference compared to other approaches is the use of custom free energy functionals such as Expected and Generalised free energies (expected free energy and generalised free energy, respectively). These functionals are specifically constructed so as to elicit epistemic, information-seeking behaviour when used to infer actions.

Optimisation of these functionals have so far relied on custom algorithms that require evaluating very large search trees which has rendered upscaling of Active Inference difficult. Many recent works, such as Branching Time Active Inference
[
3
]
and sophisticated inference
[
7
]
have investigated algorithmic ways to prune the search tree in order to solve this problem.

This paper is the first in a series of two parts where we take a different approach and formulate a variation of the generalised free energy optimisation problem using a custom Lagrangian derived from constrained Bethe free energy on factor graphs. Using variational calculus we then derive a custom message passing algorithm that can directly solve for fixed points of local generalised free energy terms, removing the need for a search tree. This allows us to construct a purely optimisation-based approach to Active Inference which we name Lagrangian Active Inference.

Lagrangian Active Inference applies to arbitrary graph topologies and interfaces with generic message passing algorithms which allow for scaling up of Active Inference using off-the-shelf tools. We accomplish this by constructing a node-local generalised free energy on generic factor graphs.

The present paper is structured as follows: In section
2
, we review relevant background material concerning Forney-style factor graphs (FFGs) and Bethe free energy (BFE). In section
3
we formalise what we mean by "epistemics" and construct an objective that is local to a single node on an Forney-style factor graph and possesses an epistemic, information-seeking drive. This objective turns out to be a local version of the generalised free energy which we review in section
3.2
.

Once we start modifying the free energy functional, we recognise a problem with current Forney-style factor graph notation. Forney-style factor graphs visualise generative models but fail to display a significant part of the optimisation problem, namely, the variational distribution and the functional to be optimised.

To remedy this, we develop the constrained Forney-style factor graph graphical notation in section
5
as a method for visualising both the variational distribution and any adaptations to the free energy functional that are needed for Active Inference. These tools form the basis of the update rules derived in our companion paper
[
15
]
.

With the constrained Forney-style factor graph notation in hand, in section
6
we then proceed to demonstrate how to recover prior algorithms for Active Inference as message passing on a constrained Forney-style factor graph. Active Inference is often described as message passing on a probabilistic graphical model, see
[
4
,
6
,
13
,
26
]
for examples. However, this relationship has not been properly formalised before, in part because adequate notation has been lacking. Using constrained Forney-style factor graphs it is straightforward to accurately write down this relation. Further, due to the modular nature of constrained Forney-style factor graphs it becomes easy to devise extensions to prior Active Inference algorithms that can be implemented using off-the-shelf message passing tools.

Finally, section
7
demonstrates a new algorithm for policy inference using Lagrangian Active Inference that scales linearly in the planning horizon, providing a solution to a long-standing barrier for scaling Active Inference to larger models and more complex tasks.

2
The Lagrangian approach to message passing

In this section, we review the Bethe free energy along with Forney-style factor graphs. These concepts form the foundation from which we will build towards local epistemic, objectives.

As a free energy functional, the Bethe free energy is unique because stationary points of the Bethe free energy correspond to solutions of the belief propagation algorithm
[
20
,
25
,
28
]
which provides exact inference on tree-structured graphs.

Furthermore, by adding constraints to the Bethe free energy using Lagrange multipliers, one can form a custom Lagrangian for an inference problem. Taking the first variation of this Lagrangian, one can then solve for stationary points and obtain message passing algorithms that solve the desired problem. Prior work
[
25
,
28
]
have shown that adding additional constraints to the Bethe free energy allows for deriving a host of different message passing algorithms including variational message passing
[
27
]
and expectation propagation
[
18
]
among others. We refer interested readers to
[
25
]
for a comprehensive overview of this technique and how different choices of constraints can lead to different algorithms.

Constraints are specified at the level of nodes and edges, meaning this procedure can produce hybrid message passing algorithms, foreshadowing the approach we are going to take for deriving a local, epistemic objective for Active Inference.

3
Defining epistemic objectives

Now we move on to the central topic of Active Inference, namely agents that interact with the world they inhabit. Under the heading of Active Inference, an agent entails a generative model of its environment and is engaged in the process of achieving future goals (specified by a target distribution or goal prior) through actions. This task can be cast as a process of free energy minimisation
[
5
,
7
,
8
]
. A natural question to ask is then, what should this free energy functional look like and why? Here we wish to highlight a core feature of Active Inference that sets it apart from other approaches: Systematic information gathering by targeted exploration of an environment. Whatever the form of our free energy functional, it should lead to agents that possess an exploratory drive consistent with Active Inference.

While it is tempting to default to a standard variational free energy, prior work
[
24
]
has shown that directly optimising Bethe free energy or variational free energy when inferring a sequence of actions (which we will refer to as a
policy
) does not lead to agents that systematically explore their environment. Instead, directly inferring a policy by minimising a variational free energy/Bethe free energy leads to Kullback-Leibler divergence-control
[
12
]

1
1

1

Other works will sometimes use
\pi
as a symbol to denote a policy
. This means that variational free energy/Bethe free energy is not the correct choice when we desire an agent that actively samples its environment with the explicit purpose of gathering information.

Instead a hallmark feature of Active Inference is the use of alternative functionals in place of either the variational free energy or the Bethe free energy, specifically for inferring policies. The goal of these alternative functionals is often specifically to induce an epistemic, explorative term that drives Active Inference agents to seek out information.

Epistemics, epistemic behaviour or "foraging for information" are commonly used terms in the Active Inference literature and related fields. While we have used the term colloquially until this point, we now clarify formally how we use the term in the present paper and how it relates to the objective functionals we consider. A core problem is that epistemics is most often defined in terms of the behaviour of agents ("what does my agent
do
?") rather than from a mathematical point of view. Prior work on this point includes
[
10
,
17
]
.

We take the view that epistemics arise from the optimisation of either an mutual information term or a bound thereon. The mutual information (between two variables
x
and
z
) is defined as
[
16
]

\displaystyle\operatorname{I}\!\left[\,{x,z}\,\right]

\displaystyle=\iint p(x,z)\log\frac{p(x,z)}{p(x)p(z)}\differential x\differential z\,.

(10)

To gain an intuition for why maximising MI leads to agents that seek out information, we can rewrite MI as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\operatorname{I}\!\left[\,{x,z}\,\right]&=\iint p(x,z)\log\frac{p(z\mid x)}{p(z)}\differential x\differential z\\
&=\operatorname{H}\!\left[{z}\right]-\operatorname{H}\!\left[{z\mid x}\right]=\operatorname{H}\!\left[{x}\right]-\operatorname{H}\!\left[{x\mid z}\right]\end{split}

(11)

Since mutual information is symmetric in its arguments, Eq. (
11
) can equally well be written in terms of
x
rather than
z
. Eq. (
11
) shows that mutual information decomposes as the difference between the marginal entropy
z
and the expected entropy of
z
conditional on
x
.

If we let
z
denote an internal state of an agent and
x
an observation and allow our agent to choose
x
- for instance through acting on an environment - we can see why maximising Eq. (
10
) biases the agent towards seeking our observations that reduce entropy in
z
. Maximising Eq. (
10
) means the agent will prefer observations that provide useful (in the sense of reducing uncertainty) information about its internal states
z
. For this reason mutual information is also known as Information Gain.

Actually computing Eq. (
10
) is often intractable and in practice, a bound is often optimised instead.

3.1
Constructing a local epistemic objective

At this point, we have seen that the Bethe free energy is defined over arbitrary Forney-style factor graphs, yet does not lead to epistemic behaviour. On the other hand, maximising mutual information leads to the types of epistemic behaviour we desire, yet is not distributed like the Bethe free energy.
The question becomes whether there is a way to merge the two and obtain a distributed functional - like the Bethe free energy- that includes an epistemic term?

We will now show how to construct such a functional. Our starting point will be the Bethe free energy given by Eq. (
8
). We will focus on a single node
a
and its associated local energy term and partition the incoming edges into two sets,
\bm{x}
and
\bm{z}
. This gives the node local free energy

\displaystyle F[q_{a}]

\displaystyle=\iint q(\bm{x},\bm{z})\log\frac{q(\bm{x},\bm{z})}{f(\bm{x},\bm{z})}\differential\bm{x}\differential\bm{z}

(12)

Now we need to add on an mutual information term to induce epistemics. Since we are minimising our free energy functional and want to maximise mutual information, we augment the free energy with a negative mutual information term as

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}\!\left[\,{q_{a}}\,\right]

\displaystyle=\overbrace{\iint q(\bm{x},\bm{z})\log\frac{q(\bm{x},\bm{z})}{f(\bm{x},\bm{z})}\differential\bm{x}\differential\bm{z}}^{\text{Variational free energy}}+\overbrace{\iint q(\bm{x},\bm{z})\log\frac{q(\bm{x})q(\bm{z})}{q(\bm{x},\bm{z})}\differential\bm{x}\differential\bm{z}}^{\text{Negative mutual information}}

(13a)

\displaystyle=\iint q(\bm{x},\bm{z})\log\frac{\cancel{q(\bm{x},\bm{z})}}{f(\bm{x},\bm{z})}\differential\bm{x}\differential\bm{z}+\iint q(\bm{x},\bm{z})\log\frac{q(\bm{x})q(\bm{z})}{\cancel{q(\bm{x},\bm{z})}}\differential\bm{x}\differential\bm{z}

(13b)

\displaystyle=\iint q(\bm{x},\bm{z})\log\frac{q(\bm{x})q(\bm{z})}{f(\bm{x},\bm{z})}\differential\bm{x}\differential\bm{z}

(13c)

which we can recognise as a node-local generalised free energy
[
19
]
. In section
3.2
we review the results of
[
19
]
to expand upon this statement.
Eq. (
13a
) provides a straightforward explanation of the kinds of behaviour that we can expect out of agents optimising a generalised free energy. Namely, minimising Bethe free energy with a goal prior corresponds to performing Kullback-Leibler divergence-control
[
12
]
while the mutual information term adds an epistemic, information-seeking component. Viewed in this way, there is nothing mysterious about the kind of objective optimised by Active Inference agents: It is simply the sum of two well-known and established objectives that are each widely used within the control and reinforcement learning communities.

Generalised free energy

A core issue with the expected free energy is that it is strictly limited to planning over future timesteps. This means that Active Inference agents that utilise the expected free energy functional need to maintain two separate models: One for inferring policies (using expected free energy) and one for state inference (using variational free energy/Bethe free energy) that updates as observations become available. The key advantage of expected free energy is that it induces the epistemic drive that we desire from Active Inference agents.

The goal of
[
19
]
was to extend upon the expected free energy by introducing a functional that could induce similar epistemic behaviour when used to infer policies while at the same time reducing to a variational free energy when dealing with past data points. In this way, an agent would no longer have to maintain two separate models and could instead utilise only one.

The generalised free energy as introduced by
[
19
]
is tied to a specific choice of generative model. The model introduced by
[
19
]
is given by

\displaystyle p(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{z}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{\hat{u}}})

\displaystyle\propto p(\bm{\mathrm{z}}_{0})\prod_{k=1}^{T}p(\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{k}|\bm{\mathrm{z}}_{k})p(\bm{\mathrm{z}}_{k}|\bm{\mathrm{\hat{u}}}_{k},\bm{\mathrm{z}}_{k-1})\tilde{p}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{k})

Given a current time step

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{T}\iint q(\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{k}|\bm{\mathrm{z}}_{k})q(\bm{\mathrm{z}}_{k}|\bm{\mathrm{\hat{u}}}_{k})\log\frac{q(\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{k}|\bm{\mathrm{\hat{u}}}_{k})q(\bm{\mathrm{z}}_{k}|\bm{\mathrm{\hat{u}}}_{k})}{\tilde{p}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{k})p(\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{k},\bm{\mathrm{z}}_{k}|\bm{\mathrm{\hat{u}}}_{k})}\differential\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{k}\differential\bm{\mathrm{z}}_{k}

where

. This partitions the connected variables into two sets: A set of variables where the expectation is modified and any remainder that is not modified.
p(s_{i}\mid\bm{s}_{a\backslash i})
denotes a conditional probability distribution. We write
p
here instead of
f
to emphasise that the conditional needs to be normalised in order for us to be able to take the expectation.

We refer to this move as a
P-substitution
. Once the mean field factorisation is enforced, we can recognise Eq. (
21
) as a node-local generalised free energy
G[q_{a}]
.

As an example of constructing a node-local generalised free energy with this approach, we can consider a node with two connected variables,
\{x,z\}
.

The local free energy becomes

\displaystyle F[q_{a}]

\displaystyle=\iint q(x,z)\log\frac{q(x,z)}{p(x,z)}\differential x\differential z

(22)

Now we apply a mean field factorisation

\displaystyle F[q_{a}]

\displaystyle=\iint q(x)q(z)\log\frac{q(x)q(z)}{p(x,z)}\differential x\differential z

(23)

And finally, perform P-substitution to obtain a node-local generalised free energy

\displaystyle\iint{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}q(x)}q(z)\log\frac{q(x)q(z)}{p(x,z)}\differential x\differential z

\displaystyle\implies\iint{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}p(x\mid z)}q(z)\log\frac{q(x)q(z)}{p(x,z)}\differential x\differential z

(24)

We denote the set of nodes for which we want to perform P-substitution with
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
. When performing P-substitution, we are replacing a local
variational
free energy
F[q_{a}]
with a local
generalised
free energy
\mathcal{G}\!\left[\,{q_{a}}\,\right]
. Armed with P-substitution, we can now write the simplest instance of a Lagrangian for Active Inference

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{L}[q]&=\underbrace{\sum_{a\in\mathcal{P}}\mathcal{G}\!\left[\,{q_{a}}\,\right]}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{P-substituted subgraph}\\
\text{w / naive mean field}\end{subarray}}+\underbrace{\sum_{b\in\mathcal{V}\backslash\mathcal{P}}F[q_{b}]}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{Node local}\\
\text{free energies}\end{subarray}}+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{E}}(1-d_{i})\underbrace{\operatorname{H}\!\left[{q(s_{i})}\right]}_{\text{Edge entropy}}\\
&\quad\quad+\underbrace{\sum_{a\in\mathcal{V}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{E}}\int\lambda_{ia}(s_{i})\Bigg[q(s_{i})-\int q(\bm{s}_{a})\differential s_{a\backslash i}\Bigg]\differential s_{i}}_{\text{Marginalisation}}\\
&\quad\quad+\underbrace{\sum_{a\in\mathcal{V}}\lambda_{a}\Bigg[\int q(\bm{s}_{a})\differential\bm{s}_{a}-1\Bigg]}_{\text{Normalisation of node marginals}}+\underbrace{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{E}}\lambda_{i}\Bigg[\int q(s_{i})\differential s_{i}-1\Bigg]}_{\text{Normalisation of edge marginals}}\,.\end{split}

(25)

With the Active Inference Lagrangian in hand, we can now solve for stationary points using variational calculus and obtain message passing algorithms for Lagrangian Active Inference. The key insight is that messages flowing out of
\mathcal{P}
derived from stationary points of Eq. (
25
) will correspond to stationary points of the local generalised free energy rather than Bethe free energy, meaning the result will include an epistemic component. This paves the way for a localised version of Active Inference that applies to arbitrary graph structures, does not suffer from scaling issues as the planning horizon increases, and can be solved efficiently and asynchronously using message passing.

Constrained Forney-style factor graphs

While Forney-style factor graphs are a useful tool for writing down generative models, we have by now established the importance of knowing the exact functional to be minimised. This requires specifying not just the model

but also the family

through constraints and any potential P-substitutions. This is important if we want to be able to succinctly specify not just the model but also the exact inference problem we aim to solve.

We will now develop just such a new notation for writing constraints directly as part of the Forney-style factor graph. We refer to Forney-style factor graphs with added constraints specification as Constrained Forney-style factor graphs.

5.6
CFFG Compression

The value of constrained Forney-style factor graph notation is measured by how much it aids other researchers and practitioners in expressing their ideas accurately and succinctly. We envision two main groups for whom constrained Forney-style factor graphs might be of particular interest. The first group is comprised of mathematical researchers working on constrained free energy optimisation on Forney-style factor graphs For this group we expect that the notation developed so far will be both useful and practically applicable since work is often focused on the intricacies of performing local optimisation. Commonly an Forney-style factor graph in this tradition is small but a very high level of accuracy is desired in order to be mathematically rigorous.

However there is a second group composed of applied researchers for whom the challenge is to accurately specify a larger inference problem and its solution to solve an auxiliary goal - for instance controlling a drone, transmitting a coded message or simulating some phenomenon using AIF. For this group of researchers in particular, constrained Forney-style factor graph notation as described so far might be too verbose and the overhead of using it may not outweigh the benefits gained. To this end, we now complete constrained Forney-style factor graph notation by a mandatory compression step.

The compression step is designed to remove redundant information by enforcing an emphasis on
deviations from a default Bethe free energy
. By default, we mean no constraints other than normalisation and marginalisation and with a joint factorisation around every node. Recall that default normalisation constraints are denoted by empty, round beads and marginalisation by connected lines. A joint factorisation means all incoming edges are connected and the node only has a single, internal bead.

To provide a recipe for compressing a constrained Forney-style factor graph, we will need the concept of a
bead chain
. A bead chain is simply a series of beads connected by edges. In the following recipe, a bead will only be summarised as part of a chain if it contains no additional information, meaning if it is round and empty. To compress a constrained Forney-style factor graph, we follow a series of four steps:

1.

Summarise every bead chain by their terminating beads.

2.

For nodes with no factorisation constraints, remove empty, internal beads.

3.

For nodes with no factorisation constraints, remove all internal edges.

4.

For all factor nodes, push remaining internal beads to the border of the corresponding factor node.

After performing these steps, we are left with a compressed version of the original constrained Forney-style factor graph where each node can be much smaller and more concise. To exemplify, we apply the recipe to the constrained Forney-style factor graph in Fig.

7
LAIF for policy inference

The tools presented in this chapter are not limited to restating prior work. Indeed, Lagrangian Active Inference offers several advantages over prior methods, one of which is the ability to directly infer a policy instead of relying on a post hoc comparison of energy terms. To demonstrate, we solve two variations of the classic T-maze task
[
6
]
. This is a well-studied setting within the Active Inference literature and therefore constitutes a good minimal benchmark. In the T-maze experiment, the agent lives in a maze with four locations as depicted in Fig.
25
. The agent (☺) starts in position 1 and knows that a reward is present at either position 2 or 3, but not which one. At position 4 is a cue that informs the agent which arm contains the reward. The optimal action to take is therefore to first visit 4 and learn which arm contains the reward before going to the rewarded arm. Because this course of action requires delaying the reward, an agent following a greedy policy behaves sub-optimally. The T-maze is therefore considered a reasonable minimal example of the epistemic, information-seeking behaviour that is a hallmark of Active Inference agents. We implemented our experiments in the reactive message passing toolbox

8
Conclusions

In this paper we have proposed a novel approach to Active Inference based on lagrangian optimisation which we have named Lagrangian Active Inference (LAIF). We demonstrated Lagrangian Active Inference on a classic benchmark problem from the Active Inference literature and found that it inherits the epistemic drive that is a hallmark feature of Active Inference. Lagrangian Active Inference presents three main advantages over previous algorithms for Active Inference.

Firstly, an advantage of Lagrangian Active Inference is the computational efficiency afforded by being able to pass backward messages instead of needing to perform forwards rollouts for every policy. While Lagrangian Active Inference is still an iterative procedure, the computational complexity of each iteration scales linearly in the size of the planning horizon
T
instead of exponentially.

A second advantage is that Lagrangian Active Inference allows for directly inferring posteriors over control signals instead of relying on a model comparison step based on expected free energy/generalised free energy. This means that Lagrangian Active Inference unifies inference for perception, learning, and actions into a single procedure without any overhead - it all becomes part of the same inference procedure. See our companion paper
[
15
]
for more details.

Thirdly, Lagrangian Active Inference is inherently modular and consequently works for freely definable constrained Forney-style factor graphs, while prior work has focused mostly on specific generative models. Extensions to hierarchical or heterarchical models are straightforward and only require writing out the corresponding constrained Forney-style factor graph.

We have also introduced constrained Forney-style factor graph notation for writing down constraints and P-subtitutions on an Forney-style factor graph. constrained Forney-style factor graphs are useful not just for Active Inference but for specifying free energy functionals in general. constrained Forney-style factor graphs accomplish this through a simple and intuitive graphical syntax. Our hope is that constrained Forney-style factor graphs can become a standard tool similar to Forney-style factor graphs when it is desirable to write not just a model
f
but also a family of approximating distributions
\mathcal{Q}
. Specifically in the context of Active Inference we have also introduced P-substitution as a way to modify the underlying free energy functional. In doing so we have formalised the relation between Active Inference and message passing on a constrained Forney-style factor graph, paving the way for future developments.

In future work, we plan to extend Lagrangian Active Inference to more node constructions to further open up the scope of available problems that can be attacked using Active Inference.

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{I}\sum_{j=1}^{I}\log[\bm{\mathrm{A}}_{ij}^{\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{i}\bm{\mathrm{z}}_{j}}\Bigg]

(65b)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{I}\sum_{j=1}^{I}\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{i}\bm{\mathrm{z}}_{j}\log\bm{\mathrm{A}}_{ij}

(65c)

Now, we are ready to start derivations for the transition mixture node. The transition mixture node has the node function

\displaystyle f(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{y}},\bm{\mathrm{z}},\bm{\mathrm{A}})

\displaystyle=\prod_{k}\mathcal{C}at\big(\bm{\mathrm{x}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{A}}_{k}\bm{\mathrm{z}}\big)^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{k}}

(66a)

\displaystyle=\prod_{k}\Bigg[\prod_{i}\prod_{j}\big[\bm{\mathrm{A}}_{k}\big]_{ij}^{\bm{\mathrm{z}}_{i}\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{j}}\Bigg]^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{k}}

(66b)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i,j,k}\bm{\mathrm{A}}_{ijk}^{\bm{\mathrm{z}}_{i}\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{j}\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{k}}

(66c)

Where we use
i
to index over columns,
j
to index over rows and
k
to index over factors.
\bm{\mathrm{A}}
is a 3-tensor that is normalised over columns. In other words, each slice of
\bm{\mathrm{A}}
corresponds to a valid transition matrix and slices are indexed by
k
. We assume a structured mean-field factorisation such that

\displaystyle q(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{y}},\bm{\mathrm{z}},\bm{\mathrm{A}})

\displaystyle=q(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{y}},\bm{\mathrm{z}})\prod_{k}q(\bm{\mathrm{A}}_{k})

(67)

We now define

\displaystyle\log f_{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{y}},\bm{\mathrm{z}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\mathrm{A}})}\Big[\log f(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{y}},\bm{\mathrm{z}},\bm{\mathrm{A}})\Big]

(68a)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\mathrm{A}})}\Big[\sum_{ijk}\bm{\mathrm{z}}_{i}\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{j}\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{k}\log\bm{\mathrm{A}}_{ijk}\Big]

(68b)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{k}\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\mathrm{A}}_{k})}\Big[\sum_{ij}\bm{\mathrm{z}}_{i}\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{j}\log\bm{\mathrm{A}}_{ijk}\Big]

(68c)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{k}\sum_{ij}\bm{\mathrm{z}}_{i}\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{j}\Big[\overline{\log\bm{\mathrm{A}}_{k}}\Big]_{ij}

(68d)

which implies that

\displaystyle f_{A}(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{y}},\bm{\mathrm{z}})

\displaystyle=\exp\Bigg( \sum_{k} \vect{y}_k \sum_{ijk} \vect{z}_i \vect{x}_j \Big[ \overline{\log\matr{A}_{k}} \Big]_{ij} \Bigg)

(69a)

\displaystyle=\prod_{ijk}\exp\Big( \overline{\log\matr{A}_{k}} \Big)^{\bm{\mathrm{z}}_{i}\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{j}\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{k}}

(69b)

\displaystyle=\sum_{ijk}\bm{\mathrm{z}}_{i}\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{j}\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{k}\underbrace{\exp\Big( \overline{\log\matr{A}_k} \Big)_{ij}}_{\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}_{ijk}}

(69c)

Now, we are ready to derive the desired messages. The first message
\nu(\cdot)
will be towards
\bm{\mathrm{x}}
. If we use
\mu(\bm{\mathrm{y}})
to denote the incoming message on the edge
\bm{\mathrm{y}}
(similar for
\mu{\bm{\mathrm{z}}}
), then the message towards
\bm{\mathrm{x}}
is given by

\displaystyle\nu(\bm{\mathrm{x}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{z}}}\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}\mu(\bm{\mathrm{z}})\mu(\bm{\mathrm{y}})\exp\Big( \mathbb{E}_{q(\matr{A})} \log f(\vect{x},\vect{y},\vect{z},\matr{A}) \Big)

(70a)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{z}}}\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}\mu(\bm{\mathrm{z}})\mu(\bm{\mathrm{y}})f_{A}(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{y}},\bm{\mathrm{z}})\Big)

(70b)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{z}}}\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}\mu(\bm{\mathrm{z}})\mu(\bm{\mathrm{y}})\sum_{ijk}\bm{\mathrm{z}}_{i}\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{j}\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{k}\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}_{ijk}

(70c)

Assuming the incoming messages are Categorically distributed, we can continue as

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{C}at(\bm{\mathrm{z}}\mid\pi_{z})}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{C}at(\bm{\mathrm{y}}\mid\pi_{y})}\sum_{ijk}\bm{\mathrm{z}}_{i}\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{j}\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{k}\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}_{ijk}

(71a)

\displaystyle=\sum_{ijk}\pi_{zi}\pi_{yk}\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{j}\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}_{ijk}

(71b)

\displaystyle=\prod_{j}\Big[\sum_{ik}\pi_{zi}\pi_{yk}\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}_{ijk}\Big]^{\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{j}}

(71c)

where the last line follows from
\bm{\mathrm{x}}
being one-hot encoded. At this point, we can recognise the message

\displaystyle\nu(\bm{\mathrm{x}})

\displaystyle\propto\mathcal{C}at(\bm{\mathrm{x}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{\rho}})\text{ where }\bm{\mathrm{\rho}}_{j}=\frac{\sum_{ik}\pi_{zi}\pi_{yk}\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}_{ijk}}{\sum_{ijk}\pi_{zi}\pi_{yk}\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}_{ijk}}

(72)

By symmetry, similar results hold for messages
\nu(\bm{\mathrm{z}})
and
\nu(\bm{\mathrm{y}})
.
\nu(\bm{\mathrm{z}})
is given by

\displaystyle\nu(\bm{\mathrm{z}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{x}\sum_{y}\mu(x)\mu(\bm{\mathrm{y}})f_{A}(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{y}},\bm{\mathrm{z}})

(73a)

\displaystyle\propto\mathcal{C}at(\bm{\mathrm{z}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{\rho}})\text{ where }\bm{\mathrm{\rho}}_{i}=\frac{\sum_{jk}\pi_{xj}\pi_{yk}\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}_{ijk}}{\sum_{ijk}\pi_{xj}\pi_{yk}\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}_{ijk}}\,.

(73b)

Similarly,
\nu(\bm{\mathrm{y}})
evaluates to

\displaystyle\nu(\bm{\mathrm{y}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{x}\sum_{z}\mu(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\mu(\bm{\mathrm{z}})f_{A}(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{y}},\bm{\mathrm{z}})

(74a)

\displaystyle\propto\mathcal{C}at(\bm{\mathrm{y}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{\rho}})\text{ where }\bm{\mathrm{\rho}}_{k}=\frac{\sum_{ij}\pi_{x,j}\pi_{z,i}\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}_{ijk}}{\sum_{ijk}\pi_{x,j}\pi_{z,i}\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}_{ijk}}\,.

(74b)

To compute the message towards the
n
’th candidate transition matrix
\bm{\mathrm{A}}_{n}
, we will need to take an expectation with respect to
q(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{y}},\bm{\mathrm{z}})
. It is given by

\displaystyle q(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{y}},\bm{\mathrm{z}})

\displaystyle=\mu(\bm{\mathrm{y}})\mu(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\mu(\bm{\mathrm{z}})f_{A}(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{y}},\bm{\mathrm{z}})

(75a)

\displaystyle=\prod_{ijk}\pi_{xj}^{\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{j}}\pi_{zi}^{\bm{\mathrm{z}}_{i}}\pi_{yk}^{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{k}}\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}_{ijk}^{\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{j}\bm{\mathrm{z}}_{i}\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{k}}

(75b)

\displaystyle=\prod_{ijk}\Big[\pi_{xj}\pi_{zi}\pi_{yk}\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}_{ijk}\Big]^{\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{j}\bm{\mathrm{z}}_{i}\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{k}}

(75c)

\displaystyle\propto\mathcal{C}at(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{y}},\bm{\mathrm{z}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{B}})

(75d)

where
\bm{\mathrm{B}}
is a three-dimensional contingency tensor with entries

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{ijk}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi_{xj}\pi_{zi}\pi_{yk}\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}_{ijk}}{\sum_{ijk}\pi_{xj}\pi_{zi}\pi_{yk}\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}_{ijk}}

(76)

Now we can compute the message towards a transition matrix
\bm{\mathrm{A}}_{n}
as

\displaystyle\log\nu(\bm{\mathrm{A}}_{n})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\mathrm{A}}_{k\backslash n})q(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{y}},\bm{\mathrm{z}})}\Big[\log f(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{y}},\bm{\mathrm{z}},\bm{\mathrm{A}})\Big]

(77a)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\mathrm{A}}_{k\backslash n})q(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{y}},\bm{\mathrm{z}})}\Big[\sum_{ijk}\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{j}\bm{\mathrm{z}}_{i}\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{k}\log\bm{\mathrm{A}}_{ijk}\Big]

(77b)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\mathrm{A}}_{k\backslash n})}\Big[\sum_{ijk}\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{ijk}\log\bm{\mathrm{A}}_{ijk}\Big]

(77c)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\sum_{k\backslash n}\Big[\sum_{ijk}\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{ijk}\log\bm{\mathrm{A}}_{ijk}\Big]}_{\text{Constant w.r.t $\bm{\mathrm{A}}_{n}$}}+\sum_{ij}\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{ijn}\log\bm{\mathrm{A}}_{ijn}

(77d)

\displaystyle\propto tr\Big(\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{ijn}\log\bm{\mathrm{A}}_{ijn}\Big)

(77e)

which implies that

\displaystyle\nu(\bm{\mathrm{A}}_{n})

\displaystyle\propto\mathcal{D}ir(\bm{\mathrm{A}}_{n}\mid\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{n}+1)\,.

(78)

We see that messages towards each component in
\bm{\mathrm{A}}
are distributed according to a Dirichlet distribution with parameters
\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{n}+1
. The final expression we need to derive is the average energy term for the transition mixture node. It is given by

\displaystyle U_{x}[q]

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\mathrm{A}})q(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{y}},\bm{\mathrm{z}})}\Big(\log f(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{y}},\bm{\mathrm{z}},\bm{\mathrm{A}})\Big)

(79a)

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{y}},\bm{\mathrm{z}})}\Big(f_{A}(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{y}},\bm{\mathrm{z}})\Big)

(79b)

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{y}},\bm{\mathrm{z}})}\Big[\sum_{ijk}\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{j}\bm{\mathrm{z}}_{i}\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{k}\big[\overline{\log\bm{\mathrm{A}}_{k}}\big]_{ij}\Big)

(79c)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{ijk}\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{ijk}\big[\overline{\log\bm{\mathrm{A}}_{k}}\big]_{ij}

(79d)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{k}tr\Big(\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{k}^{T}\overline{\log\bm{\mathrm{A}}_{k}}\Big)

(79e)
