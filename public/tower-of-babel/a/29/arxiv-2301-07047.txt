Closed-Form Formulae for Inflation Correlators
Zhehan Qin, Zhong-Zhi Xianyu, [5mm] Department of Physics, Tsinghua University, Beijing 100084, China

Abstract
We derive exact and closed-form expressions for a large class of two-point and three-point inflation correlators with the tree-level exchange of a single massive particle. The intermediate massive particle is allowed to have arbitrary mass, spin, chemical potential, and arbitrary nonderivative or derivative couplings to external inflaton modes. We also allow the coupling coefficients to have arbitrary complex power dependences on the conformal time. Our results feature closed-form expressions involving only familiar special functions and without any infinite sums. This is achieved by an improved bootstrap method with a suitable change of variables. Our results cover a wide range of cosmological collider models and can be directly used for future phenomenological studies. Our results can also be used as basic building blocks for constructing more complicated inflation correlators.

A particular aspect of studying inflation correlators is to find precise and explicit results, either numerical or analytical, to a range of key processes that were identified in the recent studies of CC phenomenologies. Until recently, many phenomenological studies have relied on very often unjustified approximations. In general, the CC processes involve inflation correlators mediated by massive fields at both the tree and the loop levels, and the on-shell production of these massive particles during inflation can leave oscillatory signatures in the inflation correlators. In this work, we present new analytical results for a wide range of three-point and two-point inflation correlators with a single massive exchange at the tree level, shown in Fig.

Our results feature exact and closed-form formulae. In contrast to previously obtained results, our expressions for the three-point and two-point correlators do not contain any Taylor series. Instead, all the momentum dependences are fully captured by familiar special functions whose analytical properties are well understood. This makes our result particularly suitable for understanding the analytic properties of inflation correlators. Also, our compact results can also be used as handy building blocks when constructing more complicated inflation correlators, a topic we shall explore in a separate work

The strategy we adopt in this paper is similar in spirit to many previous works on cosmological bootstraps. That is, we begin with a tree-level four-point correlator
\langle\varphi_{\mathbf{k}_{1}}\varphi_{\mathbf{k}_{2}}\varphi_{\mathbf{k}_{3}}\varphi_{\mathbf{k}_{4}}\rangle^{\prime}
with a single massive exchange in the
s
-channel, with momentum
\mathbf{k}_{s}\equiv\mathbf{k}_{1}+\mathbf{k}_{2}
. The result for the three-point and two-point correlators can be obtained by taking soft limits
\mathbf{k}_{4}\rightarrow 0
and
\mathbf{k}_{2},\mathbf{k}_{4}\rightarrow 0
, which we call the single folded limit and the double folded limit, respectively. (See Fig.
2
.) The regularity of folded limits of four-point functions follows from the Bunch-Davies initial condition for all the fields involved
[
56
]
. So, there is no conceptual difficulty in taking the folded limits. In practice, however, the four-point functions usually contain terms singular in folded limits, and these singularities must cancel out in the full expression. The matter is further complicated by the fact that the double folded limit is usually at the boundary of convergent regions of the Taylor series for the background. All these complications make the folded limit less trivial than it seems.

In this work, we circumvent these complications by making a proper change of variables. The advantage of adopting a new set of variables for the bootstrap equations has already been observed in
[
64
]
. Here we shall make fuller use of it. The key idea is simple: after stripping off trivial external momentum factors, an
s
-channel four-point correlator depends on various momenta only through two independent momentum ratios. In previous works, the two independent momentum ratios are normally taken as
r_{1}\equiv k_{s}/(k_{1}+k_{2})
and
r_{2}\equiv k_{s}/(k_{3}+k_{4})
, where
k_{i}\equiv|\mathbf{k}_{i}|
. Our new observation is that it is more advantageous to use
u_{i}\equiv 2r_{i}/(1+r_{i})

(i=1,2)
instead of
r_{1,2}
. For physical parameters
0\leq r_{1,2}\leq 1
, the new variable
u_{i}
is a monotonic function of
r_{i}
and also takes its value from
[0,1]
. The key advantage of
u
variables is that the inhomogeneous bootstrap equation can be solved by an ansatz of single-layer Taylor series in the single folded limit
u_{2}\rightarrow 1
, and it turns out that this Taylor series can be easily summed to give a closed-form special function, which in our case is always a generalized hypergeometric function
{}_{3}\text{F}_{2}
. With the closed-form expression for the three-point functions, the double folded limit
u_{1,2}\rightarrow 1
is also easily taken, and the result is again a closed-form expression.

where
\text{W}_{\kappa,\nu}(z)
is the Whittaker W function. The bulk propagators for particles of nonzero chemical potential can then be constructed from their mode functions in the same way as shown above for a massive scalar. It is then clear that the cases with nonzero chemical potential require a separate treatment. Therefore, we classify the correlators considered in this work into two categories, one corresponds to the Hankel-type correlators, and includes all massive fields with dS covariant dispersion; the other is the Whittaker-type correlators, which covers the cases of massive spinning fields with nonzero chemical potential.

With all bulk-to-boundary propagators and bulk propagators specified in Fig.
1
, it remains to include the appropriate couplings for all the internal vertices. In this work, we allow the massive field
\sigma
to have very general couplings to the external inflaton lines. The couplings can be either nonderivative or derivative, and they can contain an arbitrary number of uncontracted time derivatives or contracted spatial derivatives. Normally, if one imposes scale invariance, the time dependence in the coupling coefficient is fully determined by the number of fields and derivatives. However, we do not have to make the assumption of scale invariance, which is broken at least by the inflaton potential. Indeed, it is even possible to include complicated time dependences in the coupling coefficients, such as a component oscillatory in physical time,
c(\tau)\supset(-\tau)^{p}\cos\omega t\sim(-\tau)^{p\pm\mathrm{i}\omega}
, which may appear in some resonant models.

One notable difference in our definition of seed integrals from the previously defined seed integrals (including our earlier work
[
64
]
) is that the SK indices
\mathsf{a},\mathsf{b}
are left unsummed. As we shall see later, when writing correlators as linear combinations of seed integrals, the coefficients of the linear combination usually involve additional SK indices, and this fact necessitates the results of the seed integrals for each individual SK branch. In previous works, this complication was usually overcome by acting appropriate differential operators on a fully summed seed integral. Acting on such differential operators has the effect of raising the powers
p_{1,2}
by integer units. However, when we want to change
p_{1,2}
continuously on their complex planes, the method of acting differential operators no longer works. Also, for the three-point and two-point functions, there is simply no sufficient momentum dependence to act the differential operator. For example, to raise the power of
p_{2}
in a four-point seed integral, one needs to act a differential operator that contains
\partial_{u_{2}}
. However, for the three-point function,
u_{2}
has been set to a constant, so the differential operator is no longer applicable. It is for these two reasons that we choose to keep the SK indices explicit in the definition of the seed integrals. We shall explain this point more clearly with an example below.

2.2
Examples

In this subsection, we provide various examples, showing how to reduce the correlators to seed integrals. We choose a range of examples that often appear in models of CC physics. We also provide sufficient details and mid-steps to illustrate the procedure.

2.2.1
Scalar exchange

The first group of examples all involve tree-level exchange of a massive scalar field
\sigma
, but the massive scalar can couple to the external inflaton
\varphi
in many different ways. The first three examples below consider three special choices of couplings, all coming from CC model buildings. The fourth example considers the most general coupling one can write down between
\sigma
and
\varphi
.

Time-derivative coupling.

The presence of a rolling inflaton background breaks the time diffeomorphism, and thus allows us to introduce uncontracted temporal indices when writing the effective Lagrangian for the fluctuating fields. In particular, one can consider couplings between
\sigma
and the conformal time derivative of
\varphi^{\prime}\equiv{\mathrm{d}}\varphi/{\mathrm{d}}\tau
. Therefore, for the trilinear and bilinear couplings shown in Fig.
1
, we can choose:

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{\varphi\varphi\sigma}=\displaystyle\frac{\,{1}\,}{2}(-\tau)^{-2}(\varphi^{\prime})^{2}\sigma,

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{\varphi\sigma}=(-\tau)^{-3}\varphi^{\prime}\sigma.

(12)

The explicit
\tau
dependence in the couplings is dictated by the scale invariance, and we do not include the constant coefficients for clarity. It is trivial to put them back in the final expression. The above couplings are perhaps the simplest choice from a purely technical point of view, owing to the fact that the bulk-to-boundary propagator
G_{\mathsf{a}}(k,\tau)
in (
1
) becomes simpler when acting on a conformal time derivative:
G_{\mathsf{a}}^{\prime}(k,\tau)=\tau e^{+\mathrm{i}\mathsf{a}k\tau}/(2k)
. The SK integral thus takes the following form: (See
[
18
]
for the diagrammatic rules in the SK formalism.)

\displaystyle\langle\varphi_{\mathbf{k}_{1}}\varphi_{\mathbf{k}_{2}}\varphi_{\mathbf{k}_{3}}\rangle_{\sigma_{\mathbf{k}_{3}}}^{\prime}=

\displaystyle-\sum_{\mathsf{a},\mathsf{b}=\pm}\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}\int\displaystyle\frac{\,{{\mathrm{d}}\tau_{1}}\,}{(-\tau_{1})^{2}}\displaystyle\frac{\,{{\mathrm{d}}\tau_{2}}\,}{(-\tau_{2})^{3}}G_{\mathsf{a}}^{\prime}(k_{1};\tau_{1})G_{\mathsf{a}}^{\prime}(k_{2};\tau_{1})G_{\mathsf{b}}^{\prime}(k_{3};\tau_{2})D_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}(k_{3};\tau_{1},\tau_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\displaystyle\frac{\,{1}\,}{8k_{1}k_{2}k_{3}}\sum_{\mathsf{a},\mathsf{b}=\pm}\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}\int{\mathrm{d}}\tau_{1}\displaystyle\frac{\,{{\mathrm{d}}\tau_{2}}\,}{(-\tau_{2})^{2}}e^{\mathrm{i}\mathsf{a}k_{12}\tau_{1}+\mathrm{i}\mathsf{b}k_{3}\tau_{2}}D_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}(k_{3};\tau_{1},\tau_{2}).

(13)

Here the notation
\langle\varphi_{\mathbf{k}_{1}}\varphi_{\mathbf{k}_{2}}\varphi_{\mathbf{k}_{3}}\rangle_{\sigma_{\mathbf{k}_{3}}}^{\prime}
means that we have only included the graph with the massive propagator carrying the momentum
\mathbf{k}_{3}
. There are also other two permutations to be included in the full result
\langle\varphi_{\mathbf{k}_{1}}\varphi_{\mathbf{k}_{2}}\varphi_{\mathbf{k}_{3}}\rangle_{\sigma}^{\prime}
. Now, comparing the above expression with the Hankel seed integral defined in (
10

\displaystyle\langle\varphi_{\mathbf{k}_{1}}\varphi_{\mathbf{k}_{2}}\varphi_{\mathbf{k}_{3}}\rangle_{\sigma}^{\prime}=\displaystyle\frac{\,{-1}\,}{8k_{1}k_{2}k_{3}^{4}}\sum_{\mathsf{a},\mathsf{b}=\pm}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{I}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}^{0,-2}(u,1)+(\text{2 perms}),

(14)

where
u=2k_{3}/k_{123}

\delta\langle\varphi_{\mathbf{k}}\varphi_{-\mathbf{k}}\rangle_{\sigma}^{\prime}
with two insertions of bilinear couplings
\mathcal{O}_{\varphi\sigma}
introduced above:

\displaystyle\delta\langle\varphi_{\mathbf{k}}\varphi_{-\mathbf{k}}\rangle_{\sigma}^{\prime}=

\displaystyle-\sum_{\mathsf{a},\mathsf{b}=\pm}\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}\int\displaystyle\frac{\,{{\mathrm{d}}\tau_{1}}\,}{(-\tau_{1})^{3}}\displaystyle\frac{\,{{\mathrm{d}}\tau_{2}}\,}{(-\tau_{2})^{3}}G_{\mathsf{a}}^{\prime}(k;\tau_{1})G_{\mathsf{b}}^{\prime}(k;\tau_{2})D_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}(k;\tau_{1},\tau_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\displaystyle\frac{\,{1}\,}{4k^{3}}\sum_{\mathsf{a},\mathsf{b}=\pm}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{I}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}^{-2,-2}(1,1).

(15)

In this simple example, we have only one seed integral with its SK indices directly summed, thanks to the simple form of the bulk-to-boundary propagator with one time derivative. We will see more complicated combinations in the following examples where the combination coefficients themselves contain SK indices.

dS-invariant coupling.

Our second example is a slight modification of the first example. Instead of time-derivative coupling for the trilinear coupling, we make the following dS invariant choice:

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{\varphi\varphi\sigma}=\displaystyle\frac{\,{1}\,}{2}(-\tau)^{-2}\eta^{\mu\nu}(\partial_{\mu}\varphi)(\partial_{\nu}\varphi)\sigma,

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{\varphi\sigma}=(-\tau)^{-3}\varphi^{\prime}\sigma.

(16)

At the same time, we still keep the time derivative coupling for the bilinear vertex. We note in passing that dS invariant couplings for the bilinear vertex, such as
\partial_{\mu}\sigma\partial^{\mu}\varphi
and
\sigma\varphi
, are in a sense trivial, since such couplings can always be rotated away by a simple linear field redefinition. Consequently, dS invariant two-point mixings normally do not lead to physically interesting effects in CC physics.

It is straightforward to find the following SK integral for the three-point correlator constructed with the above two couplings:

\displaystyle\langle\varphi_{\mathbf{k}_{1}}\varphi_{\mathbf{k}_{2}}\varphi_{\mathbf{k}_{3}}\rangle_{\sigma_{\mathbf{k}_{3}}}^{\prime}=

\displaystyle~\sum_{\mathsf{a},\mathsf{b}=\pm}\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}\int\displaystyle\frac{\,{{\mathrm{d}}\tau_{1}}\,}{(-\tau_{1})^{2}}\displaystyle\frac{\,{{\mathrm{d}}\tau_{2}}\,}{(-\tau_{2})^{3}}G_{\mathsf{b}}^{\prime}(k_{3};\tau_{2})D_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}(k_{3};\tau_{1},\tau_{2})

\displaystyle~\times\Big[G_{\mathsf{a}}^{\prime}(k_{1};\tau_{1})G_{\mathsf{a}}^{\prime}(k_{2};\tau_{1})+\mathbf{k}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{k}_{2}G_{\mathsf{a}}(k_{1};\tau_{1})G_{\mathsf{a}}(k_{2};\tau_{1})\Big].

in (

\displaystyle\langle\varphi_{\mathbf{k}_{1}}\varphi_{\mathbf{k}_{2}}\varphi_{\mathbf{k}_{3}}\rangle_{\sigma}^{\prime}=

\displaystyle~\displaystyle\frac{\,{1}\,}{16(k_{1}k_{2}k_{3})^{2}}\sum_{\mathsf{a},\mathsf{b}=\pm}\Big[(\varrho_{12}^{2}-1)\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{I}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}^{0,-2}(u,1)+\mathrm{i}\mathsf{a}\displaystyle\frac{\,{\varrho_{12}(1-\varrho_{1}^{2}-\varrho_{2}^{2})}\,}{\varrho_{1}\varrho_{2}}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{I}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}^{-1,-2}(u,1)

\displaystyle~+\displaystyle\frac{\,{1-\varrho_{1}^{2}-\varrho_{2}^{2}}\,}{\varrho_{1}\varrho_{2}}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{I}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}^{-2,-2}(u,1)\Big]+(\text{2 perms}),

(18)

where
u=2k_{3}/k_{123}
as before, and we have defined
\varrho_{1}\equiv k_{1}/k_{3}

for simplicity. Also, we have used the momentum conservation to write

In this particular example, one can get rid of the SK-indices-dependent coefficients by acting appropriate differential operators. One can directly check that the following equation holds:

\displaystyle\langle\varphi_{\mathbf{k}_{1}}\varphi_{\mathbf{k}_{2}}\varphi_{\mathbf{k}_{3}}\rangle_{\sigma}^{\prime}=

\displaystyle~\displaystyle\frac{\,{1}\,}{8(k_{1}k_{2}k_{3})^{2}}\Big[-k_{1}k_{2}\partial_{k_{12}}^{2}+(\mkern 2.0mu\widehat{\mkern-2.0mu\mathbf{k}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{1}\cdot\mkern 2.0mu\widehat{\mkern-2.0mu\mathbf{k}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{2})(1-k_{1}\partial_{k_{12}})(1-k_{2}\partial_{k_{12}})\Big]\sum_{\mathsf{a},\mathsf{b}=\pm}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{I}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}^{-2,-2}(u,1)

\displaystyle~+(\text{2 perms}).

(19)

When acting the differential operator
\partial_{k_{12}}
, the
u

\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{I}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}^{-2,-2}(u,1)
should be understood as
u=2k_{3}/(k_{12}+k_{3})
. Thus we see that it is sometimes possible to avoid SK-index-dependent coefficients by acting appropriate differential operators on the fully summed seed integrals. But this is not always possible, as we shall see in a later example with the most general couplings.

Oscillating coupling.

Now we consider an example with explicitly time-dependent couplings. In particular, we consider a trilinear coupling that is oscillatory in
physical
time
t
.

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{\varphi\varphi\sigma}=\displaystyle\frac{\,{1}\,}{2}(-\tau)^{-2}\big[1+\lambda(-\tau)^{p}\cos(\omega t+\delta)\big](\varphi^{\prime})^{2}\sigma,

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{\varphi\sigma}=(-\tau)^{-3}\varphi^{\prime}\sigma.

(20)

Here
\lambda
is a small dimensionless parameter,
\delta
is a phase, and the real exponent
p
describes how the oscillation amplitude changes with time. Such a trilinear coupling can appear when there is a background field oscillating with a physical frequency
\omega
.

Now let us focus on the three-point correlator contributed by the oscillatory term in
\mathcal{O}_{\varphi\varphi\sigma}
, namely, the term proportional to
\lambda
. With the same procedure, we write down the SK integral and compare it with the Hankel seed integral, which shows that the
\mathcal{O}({\lambda})
part of the correlator can be written in the following way:

\displaystyle\langle\varphi_{\mathbf{k}_{1}}\varphi_{\mathbf{k}_{2}}\varphi_{\mathbf{k}_{3}}\rangle_{\lambda}^{\prime}=

\displaystyle~\displaystyle\frac{\,{-\lambda}\,}{16k_{1}k_{2}k_{3}^{4}}\sum_{\mathsf{a},\mathsf{b}=\pm}\Big[e^{-\mathrm{i}\delta}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{I}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}^{\,p+\mathrm{i}\omega,-2}(u,1)+e^{+\mathrm{i}\delta}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{I}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}^{\,p-\mathrm{i}\omega,-2}(u,1)\Big]+(\text{2 perms}).

(21)

Here we have used the fact that the physical time is related to the conformal time via
\tau=-e^{-t}
.

General couplings.

Finally we consider the most general coupling between the massive scalar field
\sigma
and the external inflaton mode
\varphi
respecting the spatial translation and rotation. Using integration by parts, we can move all the derivatives on
\sigma
to
\varphi
. After doing this, the most general trilinear and bilinear couplings take the following form:

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{\varphi\varphi\sigma}=

\displaystyle~\displaystyle\frac{\,{1}\,}{2}(-\tau)^{R}\Big[\partial_{\tau}^{J}\partial_{i_{1}}\cdots\partial_{i_{M}}(\partial_{j}\partial^{j})^{N}\varphi\Big]\Big[\partial_{\tau}^{K}\partial^{i_{1}}\cdots\partial^{i_{M}}(\partial_{k}\partial^{k})^{L}\varphi\Big]\sigma,

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{\varphi\sigma}=

\displaystyle~(-\tau)^{S}\Big[\partial_{\tau}^{P}(\partial_{i}\partial^{i})^{Q}\varphi\Big]\sigma.

(22)

Here
R
and
S

\text{Re}\,R,S>-5/2

and

and

\displaystyle\langle\varphi_{\mathbf{k}_{1}}\varphi_{\mathbf{k}_{2}}\varphi_{\mathbf{k}_{3}}\rangle_{\sigma_{\mathbf{k}_{3}}}^{\prime}=-\displaystyle\frac{\,{1}\,}{2}(-\mathbf{k}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{k}_{2})^{M}(-k_{1}^{2})^{N}(-k_{2}^{2})^{L}(-k_{3}^{2})^{Q}\sum_{\mathsf{a},\mathsf{b}=\pm}\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}\int{\mathrm{d}}\tau_{1}{\mathrm{d}}\tau_{2}(-\tau_{1})^{R}(-\tau_{2})^{S}

\displaystyle~\times\Big[\partial_{\tau_{1}}^{J}G_{\mathsf{a}}(k_{1};\tau_{1})\Big]\Big[\partial_{\tau_{1}}^{K}G_{\mathsf{a}}(k_{2};\tau_{1})\Big]\Big[\partial_{\tau_{2}}^{P}G_{\mathsf{b}}(k_{3};\tau_{2})\Big]D_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}(k_{s};\tau_{1},\tau_{2})+(\mathbf{k}_{1}\leftrightarrow\mathbf{k}_{2}).

(23)

To compare this with the Hankel seed integral (
10
), it is useful to note the following expression for the bulk-to-boundary propagator with

\partial_{\tau}^{J}G_{\mathsf{a}}(k_{1};\tau_{1})=\displaystyle\frac{\,{1}\,}{2k^{3}}(\mathrm{i}\mathsf{a}k)^{J}(1-J-\mathrm{i}\mathsf{a}k\tau)e^{+\mathrm{i}\mathsf{a}k\tau}.

(24)

Then it is straightforward to find the following expression:

\displaystyle~\langle\varphi_{\mathbf{k}_{1}}\varphi_{\mathbf{k}_{2}}\varphi_{\mathbf{k}_{3}}\rangle_{\sigma}^{\prime}=\displaystyle\frac{\,{(-1)^{M+N+L+Q}(\mathbf{k}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{k}_{2})^{M}k_{1}^{2N+J-1}k_{2}^{2L+K-1}k_{3}^{2Q+P-R-S-6}}\,}{16k_{1}^{2}k_{2}^{2}k_{3}^{2}}\sum_{\mathsf{a},\mathsf{b}=\pm}(\mathrm{i}\mathsf{a})^{J}(\mathrm{i}\mathsf{a})^{K}(\mathrm{i}\mathsf{b})^{P}

\displaystyle~\times\bigg[(1-J)(1-K)(1-P)\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{I}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}^{R,S}(u,1)-\mathrm{i}\mathsf{a}\big((1-K)\varrho_{1}+(1-J)\varrho_{2}\big)(1-P)\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{I}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}^{R+1,S}(u,1)

\displaystyle~-(1-P)\varrho_{1}\varrho_{2}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{I}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}^{R+2,S}(u,1)-\mathrm{i}\mathsf{b}(1-J)(1-K)\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{I}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}^{R,S+1}(u,1)

\displaystyle~-\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}\big((1-K)\varrho_{1}+(1-J)\varrho_{2}\big)\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{I}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}^{R+1,S+1}(u,1)-\mathrm{i}\mathsf{b}\varrho_{1}\varrho_{2}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{I}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}^{R+2,S+1}(u,1)\bigg]+(\text{5 perms}).

(25)

Again, this expression contains coefficients that depend on SK indices. In this example, it is no longer possible to eliminate this dependence by acting on differential operators on a summed seed integral, in particular because the
u_{2}
variable has been set to 1. Therefore it seems most convenient to leave the SK indices unsummed in the definition of the seed integral, in order to accommodate the most general couplings as presented here.

2.2.2
Spinning exchange

Next we consider the exchange of massive particles with nonzero spin
s
. The angular momentum conservation at each of the two-point mixing vertices in Fig.
1
implies that only the longitudinal component of the spinning field can mix with the external scalar mode, and that only the helicity-2 component of the spinning field can mix with the external graviton. Therefore, in the following examples, we will only consider longitudinal polarizations for the three-point and two-point scalar correlators with spinning exchange. In the last example, we shall also consider an example of graviton correlators, in which we will have a helicity-2 exchange, possibly with a nonzero chemical potential.

The main purpose of the following examples is to show how to reduce the SK integrals with spinning field into seed integrals defined with spin-0 or spin-1 massive propagators. Therefore we will no longer consider the most general couplings. Instead, we will focus on the problems such as how to relate the longitudinal mode function of a spinning particle with the mode function of a massive scalar field. We shall begin with the simplest example, namely the spin-1 case, and then consider the general spin-
s
field.

Spin-1.

The three-point function with longitudinal spin-1 exchange has been extensively studied in
[
64
]
, and here we show the main results together with some important mid-steps for illustration. A massive spin-1 particle can be obtained after canonically quantizing a vector field
A_{\mu}
with mass parameter
\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu
. For this field, we choose the following simple couplings to the external inflaton modes:

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{\varphi\varphi A}=\displaystyle\frac{\,{1}\,}{2}(-\tau)^{-1}\varphi^{\prime}(\partial_{i}\varphi)A_{i},

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{\varphi A}=(-\tau)^{-1}(\partial_{i}\varphi^{\prime})A_{i}

(26)

Then, the SK integral for the three-point function mediated by the longitudinal mode of
A
reads:

\displaystyle\langle\varphi_{\mathbf{k}_{1}}\varphi_{\mathbf{k}_{2}}\varphi_{\mathbf{k}_{3}}\rangle_{A}^{\prime}=

\displaystyle~\bigg\{\displaystyle\frac{\,{1}\,}{2}\Big[\mathbf{k}_{2}\cdot\bm{\epsilon}^{(L)}(\mkern 2.0mu\widehat{\mkern-2.0muk\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{3})\Big]\Big[\mathbf{k}_{3}\cdot\bm{\epsilon}^{(L)*}(\mkern 2.0mu\widehat{\mkern-2.0muk\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{3})\Big]\sum_{\mathsf{a},\mathsf{b}=\pm}\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}\int\displaystyle\frac{\,{{\mathrm{d}}\tau_{1}}\,}{-\tau_{1}}\displaystyle\frac{\,{{\mathrm{d}}\tau_{2}}\,}{-\tau_{2}}

\displaystyle~\times G_{\mathsf{a}}^{\prime}(k_{1},\tau_{1})G_{\mathsf{a}}(k_{2},\tau_{1})G_{\mathsf{b}}^{\prime}(k_{3},\tau_{2})D^{(L)}_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}(k;\tau_{1},\tau_{2})\bigg\}+(\text{5 perms}).

(27)

Here we have introduced the longitudinal polarization vector
\bm{\epsilon}^{(L)}(\mkern 2.0mu\widehat{\mkern-2.0muk\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu)=\mkern 2.0mu\widehat{\mkern-2.0mu\mathbf{k}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu\equiv\mathbf{k}/k
, as well as the longitudinal bulk propagator
D_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}^{(L)}(k_{3};\tau_{1},\tau_{2})
. This bulk propagator is built from the longitudinal mode function
B^{(L)}(k,\tau)
in (
7
) following the standard procedure. Now, by a careful examination of the equation of motion of
A_{\mu}
as well as the constraint
\nabla^{\mu}A_{\mu}=0
, one can show that the longitudinal mode function
B^{(L)}(k,\tau)
can be built from a massive scalar mode function of Hankel-type
[
13
,
64
]
. Here we directly quote the result. It is convenient to define the following differential operator
\mathcal{L}_{\tau}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\tau}=\partial_{\tau}-\displaystyle\frac{\,{2}\,}{\tau}.

(28)

Then, one can show that the longitudinal component in
A_{i}
is related to the temporal polarization
B^{(L)}_{0}
in
A_{0}
via an action of
\mathcal{L}_{\tau}
operator. In addition, the mode function for the temporal polarization

is identical to that of the massive scalar
\sigma(k,\tau)
up to a constant factor:

\displaystyle B^{(L)}(k,\tau)=\displaystyle\frac{\,{1}\,}{\mathrm{i}k}\mathcal{L}_{\tau}B_{0}^{(L)}(k,\tau),

(29)

\displaystyle B^{(L)}_{0}(k,\tau)=\displaystyle\frac{\,{\sqrt{\pi}k}\,}{2m}e^{-\pi\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu/2}(-\tau)^{3/2}\mathrm{H}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{i}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu}(-k\tau)=\displaystyle\frac{\,{k}\,}{m}\sigma(k,\tau).

(30)

From the above relations, we can immediately find a relation between the longitudinal propagator
D_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}^{(L)}(k;\tau_{1},\tau_{2})
and the massive scalar propagator
D_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}(k;\tau_{1},\tau_{2})
:

\displaystyle D_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}^{(L)}(k;\tau_{1},\tau_{2})=\displaystyle\frac{\,{1}\,}{m^{2}}\Big[\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{1}}\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{2}}D_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}(k;\tau_{1},\tau_{2})-\mathrm{i}\mathsf{a}\tau_{1}\tau_{2}\delta_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}\delta(\tau_{1}-\tau_{2})\Big].

(31)

There is a notable contact term proportional to
\delta_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}\delta(\tau_{1}-\tau_{2})
in this expression. It is nonzero only for the two same-sign propagators
D_{\pm\pm}^{(L)}
. This term arises when we commute the
\mathcal{L}_{\tau}
operator, originally acting on the mode function, with the step function in
D_{\pm\pm}^{(L)}
; See (
5
).

Now we substitute (
31
) in (
2.2.2
), which then leads to the following SK integral involving the massive scalar propagator
D_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}(k;\tau_{1},\tau_{2})
:

\displaystyle\langle\varphi_{\mathbf{k}_{1}}\varphi_{\mathbf{k}_{2}}\varphi_{\mathbf{k}_{3}}\rangle_{A}^{\prime}=

\displaystyle~\displaystyle\frac{\,{\mathbf{k}_{2}\cdot\mathbf{k}_{3}}\,}{2m^{2}}\bigg\{\sum_{\mathsf{a},\mathsf{b}=\pm}\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}\int\displaystyle\frac{\,{{\mathrm{d}}\tau_{1}}\,}{-\tau_{1}}\displaystyle\frac{\,{{\mathrm{d}}\tau_{2}}\,}{-\tau_{2}}G_{\mathsf{a}}^{\prime}(k_{1};\tau_{1})G_{\mathsf{a}}(k_{2};\tau_{1})G_{\mathsf{b}}^{\prime}(k_{3};\tau_{2})\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{1}}\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{2}}D_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}(k;\tau_{1},\tau_{2})

\displaystyle-\sum_{\mathsf{a}=\pm}\mathrm{i}\mathsf{a}\int{\mathrm{d}}\tau_{1}\,G_{\mathsf{a}}^{\prime}(k_{1};\tau_{1})G_{\mathsf{a}}(k_{2};\tau_{1})G_{\mathsf{a}}^{\prime}(k_{3};\tau_{1})\bigg\}+(\text{5 perms}).

(32)

The first term in the second line comes from the contact term in (
31
). Then, we perform integration by parts, to move
\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{1}}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{2}}
to external modes. There is no boundary term since the integrand vanishes at both
\tau=-\infty
(by
\mathrm{i}\epsilon

\tau=0
(by explicitly counting the powers of
\tau_{1,2}
in the integrand). After this is done, we are ready to write the above SK integral as a linear combination of the Hankel seed integral:

\displaystyle\langle\varphi_{\mathbf{k}_{1}}\varphi_{\mathbf{k}_{2}}\varphi_{\mathbf{k}_{3}}\rangle_{A}^{\prime}=

\displaystyle-\displaystyle\frac{\,{1}\,}{m^{2}}\displaystyle\frac{\,{\mkern 2.0mu\widehat{\mkern-2.0mu\mathbf{k}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{2}\cdot\mkern 2.0mu\widehat{\mkern-2.0mu\mathbf{k}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{3}}\,}{16(k_{1}k_{2}k_{3})^{2}}\bigg\{\sum_{\mathsf{a},\mathsf{b}=\pm}\bigg[4\varrho_{1}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{I}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}^{-1,-1}(u,1)-2\mathrm{i}\mathsf{b}\varrho_{1}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{I}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}^{-1,0}(u,1)

\displaystyle-2\mathrm{i}\mathsf{a}\varrho_{1}(\varrho_{1}-2\varrho_{2})\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{I}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}^{0,-1}(u,1)-\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}\varrho_{1}(\varrho_{1}-2\varrho_{2})\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{I}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}^{0,0}(u,1)+2\varrho_{1}\varrho_{2}\varrho_{12}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{I}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}^{1,-1}(u,1)

\displaystyle-\mathrm{i}\mathsf{b}\varrho_{1}\varrho_{2}\varrho_{12}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{I}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}^{1,0}(u,1)\bigg]+\displaystyle\frac{\,{4\varrho_{1}\varrho_{3}^{2}(\varrho_{123}+3\varrho_{2})}\,}{\varrho_{123}^{4}}\bigg\}+(\text{5 perms}).

(33)

Here again
u=2k_{3}/k_{123}
, and we define the same momentum ratios
\varrho_{i}
as in (
2.2.1
). Therefore, we see that the computation of three-point correlators with spin-1 exchange can also be reduced to that of the Hankel seed integral.

General integer spin.

The procedure described above for spin-1 field can be readily applied to fields with general integer spin
s
, although the algebra can be quite tedious. A spin-
s
particle can be described by a transverse, traceless, and totally symmetric tensor
\Psi_{\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{s}}
of rank-
s
.
There are again a lot of possible choices for the trilinear and bilinear vertices in Fig.
1
. We do not pursue the most general possibilities, but only illustrate the method with the following simple example:

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{\varphi\varphi\sigma}=(-\tau)^{-3+2s}\varphi^{\prime}(\partial_{i_{1}}\cdots\partial_{i_{s}}\varphi)\Psi_{i_{1}\cdots i_{s}},

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{\varphi\sigma}=(-\tau)^{-3+2s}(\partial_{i_{1}}\cdots\partial_{i_{s}}\varphi^{\prime})\Psi_{i_{1}\cdots i_{s}},

(34)

where the explicit time dependence is again fixed by the scale symmetry. With the couplings given, the SK integral for the three-point correlator with
\Psi
-exchange reads:

\displaystyle\langle\varphi_{\mathbf{k}_{1}}\varphi_{\mathbf{k}_{2}}\varphi_{\mathbf{k}_{3}}\rangle_{\Psi}^{\prime}=

\displaystyle~\bigg\{\displaystyle\frac{\,{(-1)^{s+1}}\,}{2}\Big[k_{2}^{i_{1}}\cdots k_{2}^{i_{s}}{\epsilon}_{i_{1}\cdots i_{s}}^{(L)}({\mkern 2.0mu\widehat{\mkern-2.0muk\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu}_{3})\Big]\Big[k_{3}^{j_{1}}\cdots k_{3}^{j_{s}}{\epsilon}_{j_{1}\cdots j_{s}}^{(L)*}({\mkern 2.0mu\widehat{\mkern-2.0muk\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu}_{3})\Big]\sum_{\mathsf{a},\mathsf{b}=\pm}\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}\int\displaystyle\frac{\,{{\mathrm{d}}\tau_{1}}\,}{(-\tau_{1})^{3-2s}}\displaystyle\frac{\,{{\mathrm{d}}\tau_{2}}\,}{(-\tau_{2})^{3-2s}}

\displaystyle~\times G_{\mathsf{a}}^{\prime}(k_{1},\tau_{1})G_{\mathsf{a}}(k_{2},\tau_{1})G_{\mathsf{b}}^{\prime}(k_{3},\tau_{2})D^{(s,L)}_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}(k;\tau_{1},\tau_{2})\bigg\}+(\text{5 perms}).

(35)

Here
\epsilon_{i_{1}\cdots i_{s}}^{(L)}(\mkern 2.0mu\widehat{\mkern-2.0muk\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu)
is the longitudinal polarization tensor. Up to a normalization factor,
\epsilon_{i_{1}\cdots i_{s}}^{(L)}(\mkern 2.0mu\widehat{\mkern-2.0muk\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu)
is the unique combination of
\mkern 2.0mu\widehat{\mkern-2.0muk\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{i}
and
\delta_{ij}
with the property of being transverse, traceless, and totally symmetric with all indices. Therefore, in the above expression, the factor
k_{3}^{j_{1}}\cdots k_{3}^{j_{s}}{\epsilon}_{j_{1}\cdots j_{s}}^{(L)*}({\mkern 2.0mu\widehat{\mkern-2.0muk\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu}_{3})
contributes a constant factor, while
k_{2}^{i_{1}}\cdots k_{2}^{i_{s}}{\epsilon}_{i_{1}\cdots i_{s}}^{(L)}({\mkern 2.0mu\widehat{\mkern-2.0muk\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu}_{3})
contributes to a angular dependent factor
\propto\mathrm{P}_{s}(\cos\theta_{23})
where
\mathrm{P}_{n}(z)
is the Legendre polynomial and
\theta_{23}
is the angle between
\mathbf{k}_{2}
and
\mathbf{k}_{3}
. The factor
\mathrm{P}_{n}(z)
is well known in CC physics as the signal of a spin-
s
exchange. However, let us emphasize that this factor also depends on the form of the couplings. Had we coupled the spin-
s
in a different way to the external mode, this factor will change accordingly.

The propagator
D_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}^{(s,L)}
corresponding to the longitudinal polarization tensor
\epsilon_{i_{1}\cdots i_{s}}^{(L)}
with
s
spatial indices is again built from the mode function for the helicity-0 component of the spin-
s
field with all-spatial indices
\Psi_{i_{1}\cdots i_{s}}
. Similar to the previous spin-1 case, the mode function
\Psi^{(s,L)}_{s}(k,\tau)
can also be constructed from the scalar mode function
\sigma(k,\tau)
of the same mass parameter
\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu
by the action of a differential operator. For spin-
s
, this differential operator is defined recursively. Here we quote the result, and the details can be found in, e.g., App. A of
[
13
]
. First, similar to spin-1 case, the temporal mode function
\Psi^{(s,L)}_{0}(k,\tau)
is identical to the scalar mode function up to a constant factor:

\displaystyle\Psi^{(s,L)}_{0}(k,\tau)=\displaystyle\frac{\,{\pi^{3/4}\text{sech}^{1/2}(\pi\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu)}\,}{2^{s/2}}\Gamma^{1/2}\begin{bmatrix}1+s\\
\mbox{$\frac{\,{1}\,}{2}$}+s,\mbox{$\frac{\,{1}\,}{2}$}+s+\mathrm{i}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu,\mbox{$\frac{\,{1}\,}{2}$}+s-\mathrm{i}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu\end{bmatrix}k^{s}\sigma(k,\tau),

(36)

where we have used the compact notation for the Euler-
\Gamma
product, as defined in (
133
). Then, the longitudinal mode function
\Psi^{(s,L)}_{s}(k,\tau)
with respect to the longitudinal polarization of
s
spatial indices is constructed recursively from the following relation:

\displaystyle\Psi^{(s,L)}_{n}(k,\tau)=\displaystyle\frac{\,{1}\,}{\mathrm{i}k}\mathcal{L}_{\tau}\Psi^{(s,L)}_{n-1}(k,\tau)-\sqrt{\pi}\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}\Gamma\begin{bmatrix}1+n,\mbox{$\frac{\,{1+m+n}\,}{2}$}\\
1+m,1-m+n,\mbox{$\frac{\,{1}\,}{2}$}+n,\mbox{$\frac{\,{1+m-n}\,}{2}$}\end{bmatrix}\Psi^{(s,L)}_{m}(k,\tau).

(37)

For example, the all-spatial longitudinal mode functions,
\Psi_{2}^{(2,L)}(k,\tau)
for a spin-2 particle and
\Psi_{3}^{(3,L)}(k,\tau)
for a spin-3 particle, are respectively given by:

\displaystyle\Psi_{2}^{(2,L)}(k,\tau)=

\displaystyle~\sqrt{\displaystyle\frac{\,{2}\,}{3(\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu^{2}+\mbox{$\frac{\,{1}\,}{4}$})(\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu^{2}+\mbox{$\frac{\,{9}\,}{4}$})}}\bigg(\mathcal{L}_{\tau}^{2}-\displaystyle\frac{\,{k^{2}}\,}{3}\bigg)\sigma(k,\tau),

(38)

\displaystyle\Psi_{3}^{(3,L)}(k,\tau)=

\displaystyle~\sqrt{\displaystyle\frac{\,{2}\,}{5(\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu^{2}+\mbox{$\frac{\,{1}\,}{4}$})(\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu^{2}+\mbox{$\frac{\,{9}\,}{4}$})(\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu^{2}+\mbox{$\frac{\,{25}\,}{4}$})}}\bigg(\mathcal{L}_{\tau}^{3}-\displaystyle\frac{\,{14k^{2}}\,}{15}\mathcal{L}_{\tau}\bigg)\sigma(k,\tau),

(39)

where we have dropped unimportant overall phases. Then, in parallel with the previous
spin-1
example, we can move the differential operator on
\sigma(k,\tau)
to the external modes by using the integration by parts, and thereby reduce the correlator of spin-
s
exchange to a linear combination of the Hankel seed integral.

Tensor and mixed correlators.

In all examples considered above, the correlators are reduced to linear combinations of Hankel seed integral. The Whittaker seed integral has not been used until now. The reason is that the Whittaker mode function appears only for spinning field with helicity-dependent chemical potentials. Such chemical potentials usually vanish for the longitudinal polarization, and therefore it does not play a role in the scalar two-point and three-point correlators at the tree level. (However, the chemical-potential-enhanced mode does play a role in scalar three-point function at the one-loop level or the scalar four-point function at the tree level.) To see the effect of the chemical potential in tree-level three-point functions, we should consider spinning external states, among which the graviton correlators are most relevant to realistic CC physics. Indeed, it was shown in
[
48
]
that a helical chemical potential can be introduced to a massive spin-2 particle
\Sigma
. When
\Sigma
linearly mixes with the massless graviton mode
\gamma_{\mathbf{k}}^{(\pm 2)}
, it can mediate a mixed three-point function
\langle\varphi_{\mathbf{k}_{1}}\varphi_{\mathbf{k}_{2}}\gamma_{\mathbf{k}_{3}}^{(\pm 2)}\rangle^{\prime}
, described again by the left diagram of Fig.
1
, but with the right external mode replaced by
\gamma_{\mathbf{k}_{3}}
. There is also a two-point function of graviton modes
\langle\gamma_{\mathbf{k}}^{(\pm 2)}\gamma_{-\mathbf{k}}^{(\pm 2)}\rangle^{\prime}
contributed by the massive spin-2 mode, similar to the right diagram of Fig.
1
. The corresponding couplings can be chosen as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{\varphi\varphi\Sigma}=\displaystyle\frac{\,{1}\,}{2}(\partial_{i}\varphi)(\partial_{j}\varphi)\sigma_{ij},

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{\gamma\Sigma}=(-\tau)^{-1}\gamma_{ij}^{\prime}\sigma_{ij}.

(40)

To write down the SK integral, we also need the bulk-to-boundary propagator
T_{\mathsf{a}}(k;\tau)
for the external graviton, as well as the mode function
\Sigma^{(\pm 2)}(k,\tau)
for the helicity-2 component of the massive spin-2 field
\Sigma
. They are respectively given by:

\displaystyle T_{\mathsf{a}}(k,\tau)=\displaystyle\frac{\,{1}\,}{4k^{3}}(1-\mathrm{i}\mathsf{a}k\tau)e^{\mathrm{i}\mathsf{a}k\tau},

(41)

\displaystyle\Sigma^{(\pm 2)}(k,\tau)=-\displaystyle\frac{\,{e^{\mp\pi\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu/2}}\,}{2\sqrt{k}\tau}\mathrm{W}_{\pm\mathrm{i}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu,\mathrm{i}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu}(2\mathrm{i}k\tau)=-\displaystyle\frac{\,{1}\,}{\sqrt{2}\tau}B^{(\pm 1)}(k,\tau),

(42)

where
B^{(\pm 1)}(k,\tau)
is the mode function for the massive spin-1 field with helicity
h=\pm 1
, given in (
7
). From this one can build the SK integral, and then rewrite it as a linear combination of Whittaker seed integrals. The details have been spelled out in
[
64
]
. Here we show the final result:

\displaystyle\langle\varphi(\mathbf{k}_{1})\varphi(\mathbf{k}_{2})\gamma^{(\pm 2)}(\mathbf{k}_{3})\rangle^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\displaystyle\frac{\,{\mkern 2.0mu\widehat{\mkern-2.0muk\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{1i}\mkern 2.0mu\widehat{\mkern-2.0muk\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{2j}\epsilon_{ij}^{(\pm 2)}(\mkern 2.0mu\widehat{\mkern-2.0muk\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{3})}\,}{32(k_{1}k_{2}k_{3})^{2}}\sum_{\mathsf{a},\mathsf{b}=\pm}\Big[\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{I}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}^{(\pm)-1,-1}(u,1)+\mathrm{i}\mathsf{a}\varrho_{12}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{I}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}^{(\pm)0,-1}(u,1)-\varrho_{1}\varrho_{2}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{I}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}^{(\pm)1,-1}(u,1)\Big],

(43)

where, again,
u=2k_{3}/k_{123}
, and the various momentum ratios
\varrho_{i}
are defined in the same way as in (
2.2.1
). It turns out that this correlator can also be obtained by acting an appropriate differential operator on a fully summed SK integral. Interested readers can find more details in
[
64
]
.

It seems that the Whittaker seed integral is of limited use in representing inflation correlators. As we shall show in a separate work
[
91
]
, this seed integral will be more useful when we treat the two-point or three-point functions considered here as subgraphs of more complicated diagrams. In that case, the Whittaker seed integral will be a very convenient building block for constructing more complicated processes at the loop level.

3  Bootstrapping Hankel Seed Integrals

In this section, we provide the details of computing the three-point and two-point functions with Hankel-type tree-level mediation. We will derive the bootstrap equations satisfied by the seed integrals, first in
r
-variables, and then in
u
-variables. We then solve the bootstrap equations and obtain both the particular solution to the inhomogeneous equation and the general solution to the homogeneous equation. The final answer is determined by proper boundary conditions, which we choose to impose from the squeezed limit. Then, we take the single and double folded limit, to get the desired result for the seed integrals with one or both of
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
taken to 1. Readers uninterested in the technical details can directly go to Sec.
3.4
for the final results.

3.1
Hankel seed integral and its bootstrap equation

As mentioned above, our starting point is the Hankel seed integral
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}^{p_{1}p_{2}}(u_{1},u_{2})
defined in (
10
). Here we shall first write the seed integrals as functions of
r_{1}=k_{s}/k_{12}
and
r_{2}=k_{s}/k_{34}
, and transform to
(u_{1},u_{2})
later:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{p_{1}p_{2}}_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}(r_{1},r_{2})\equiv-\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}\,k_{s}^{5+p_{12}}\int_{-\infty}^{0}{\mathrm{d}}\tau_{1}{\mathrm{d}}\tau_{2}\,(-\tau_{1})^{p_{1}}(-\tau_{2})^{p_{2}}e^{\mathrm{i}\mathsf{a}k_{12}\tau_{1}+\mathrm{i}\mathsf{b}k_{34}\tau_{2}}D_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}(k_{s};\tau_{1},\tau_{2}).

(44)

where
D_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}(k;\tau_{1},\tau_{2})
is the bulk propagator for a massive scalar field, given in (
5
) and (
6
). An important and well-known property of the Schwinger-Keldysh propagators is that they satisfy the equation of motion for the massive scalar field with or without a
\delta
-source:

\displaystyle(\tau_{1}^{2}\partial_{\tau_{1}}^{2}-2\tau_{1}\partial_{\tau_{1}}+k_{s}^{2}\tau_{1}^{2}+m^{2})D_{\pm\mp}(k_{s};\tau_{1},\tau_{2})=0,

(45)

\displaystyle(\tau_{1}^{2}\partial_{\tau_{1}}^{2}-2\tau_{1}\partial_{\tau_{1}}+k_{s}^{2}\tau_{1}^{2}+m^{2})D_{\pm\pm}(k_{s};\tau_{1},\tau_{2})=\mp\mathrm{i}\tau_{1}^{2}\tau_{2}^{2}\delta(\tau_{1}-\tau_{2}).

(46)

This will be the key property we shall make use of when deriving the bootstrap equation for the Hankel seed integral.

Now, we start to derive the bootstrap equation. It turns out convenient to redefine several variables. First, we shall use
z_{1}\equiv-k_{12}\tau_{1}
and
z_{2}\equiv-k_{34}\tau_{2}
instead of the conformal time
\tau_{1}
and
\tau_{2}
. Notice that the negative sign in the definition of
z_{1,2}
makes them real and positive:
z_{1,2}\in(0,+\infty)
in the physical regime. Second, we shall define a “hatted” massive propagator
\mkern 2.0mu\widehat{\mkern-2.0muD\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}(z_{1},z_{2})
:

\mkern 2.0mu\widehat{\mkern-2.0muD\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}(z_{1},z_{2})=k^{3}D_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}(k;\tau_{1},\tau_{2})=\displaystyle\frac{\,{\pi e^{-\pi\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu}}\,}{4}(z_{1}z_{2})^{3/2}\mathrm{H}_{\mathrm{i}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu}^{(1)}(z_{1})\mathrm{H}_{-\mathrm{i}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu}^{(2)}(z_{2}).

(47)

The nice thing about the hatted propagator is that it depends on the time and the momentum only through the combination
k\tau_{1}
and
k\tau_{2}
, and therefore, we can rewrite it as a function of two independent variables
z_{1}
and
z_{2}
. With these redefinitions, we can rewrite the Hankel seed integral (
44
) as

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}^{p_{1}p_{2}}(r_{1},r_{2})=

\displaystyle~(-\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b})r_{1}^{1+p_{1}}r_{2}^{1+p_{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\mathrm{d}}z_{1}{\mathrm{d}}z_{2}\,z_{1}^{p_{1}}z_{2}^{p_{2}}e^{-\mathrm{i}\mathsf{a}z_{1}-\mathrm{i}\mathsf{b}z_{2}}\mkern 2.0mu\widehat{\mkern-2.0muD\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}(r_{1}z_{1},r_{2}z_{2}).

(48)

It now becomes manifest that the Hankel seed integral depends on external momenta only through
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
.

Recall that the massive propagator
D_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}
satisfies the equation of motion (
45
) and (
46
). This pair of equations can be rewritten in terms of the hatted propagator as

\displaystyle(z_{1}^{2}\partial_{z_{1}}^{2}-2z_{1}\partial_{z_{1}}+z_{1}^{2}+m^{2})\mkern 2.0mu\widehat{\mkern-2.0muD\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\pm\mp}(z_{1},z_{2})=0,

(49)

\displaystyle(z_{1}^{2}\partial_{z_{1}}^{2}-2z_{1}\partial_{z_{1}}+z_{1}^{2}+m^{2})\mkern 2.0mu\widehat{\mkern-2.0muD\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\pm\pm}(z_{1},z_{2})=\mp\mathrm{i}z_{1}^{2}z_{2}^{2}\delta(z_{1}-z_{2}).

(50)

For the hatted propagator
\mkern 2.0mu\widehat{\mkern-2.0muD\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}(r_{1}z_{1},r_{2}z_{2})
in (
48
), the two variables
z_{1}
and
z_{2}
are multiplied by the momentum ratios
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
, respectively. Therefore, for this particular propagator, we can change the variables in the equations (
49
) and (
50
), and rewrite them as differential equations with respect to
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
:

\displaystyle(r_{1}^{2}\partial_{r_{1}}^{2}-2r_{1}\partial_{r_{1}}+r_{1}^{2}z_{1}^{2}+m^{2})\mkern 2.0mu\widehat{\mkern-2.0muD\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\pm\mp}(r_{1}z_{1},r_{2}z_{2})=0,

(51)

\displaystyle(r_{1}^{2}\partial_{r_{1}}^{2}-2r_{1}\partial_{r_{1}}+r_{1}^{2}z_{1}^{2}+m^{2})\mkern 2.0mu\widehat{\mkern-2.0muD\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\pm\pm}(r_{1}z_{1},r_{2}z_{2})=\mp\mathrm{i}r_{1}^{2}z_{1}^{2}r_{2}^{2}z_{2}^{2}\delta(r_{1}z_{1}-r_{2}z_{2}),

(52)

Now, we can insert the differential operator
(r_{1}^{2}\partial_{r_{1}}^{2}-2r_{1}\partial_{r_{1}}+r_{1}^{2}z_{1}^{2}+m^{2})
in front of the hatted propagator in (
48
), which reduces the massive propagator into 0 or a term proportional to
\delta
function. Either way, the integral is trivialized, and this will become the right-hand side of the final bootstrap equation.

To derive the left-hand side of the bootstrap equation, we commute the differential operator
(r_{1}^{2}\partial_{r_{1}}^{2}-2r_{1}\partial_{r_{1}}+r_{1}^{2}z_{1}^{2}+m^{2})
with the
z
-integrals. It is clear that the only obstacle in our attempt of pulling the differential operator to the left of the integral sign is the
r_{1}^{2}z_{1}^{2}
term, which depends on the integration variable
z_{1}
. This obstacle can be removed by integration by parts. That is, for any well-behaved function
f(rz)
, we have:

0=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\mathrm{d}}z\,\partial_{z}\big[z^{p+1}e^{-\mathrm{i}\mathsf{a}z}f(rz)\big]=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\mathrm{d}}z\,z^{p}e^{-\mathrm{i}\mathsf{a}z}(p+1-\mathrm{i}\mathsf{a}z+r\partial_{r})f(rz),

(53)

which then gives

\int_{0}^{\infty}{\mathrm{d}}z\,z^{p}e^{-\mathrm{i}\mathsf{a}z}zf(rz)=-\mathrm{i}\mathsf{a}(r\partial_{r}+p+1)\int_{0}^{\infty}{\mathrm{d}}z\,z^{p}e^{-\mathrm{i}\mathsf{a}z}f(rz).

(54)

A repetition of the same procedure then gives:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}{\mathrm{d}}z\,z^{p}e^{-\mathrm{i}\mathsf{a}z}z^{2}f(rz)=-(r\partial_{r}+p+2)(r\partial_{r}+p+1)\int_{0}^{\infty}{\mathrm{d}}z\,z^{p}e^{-\mathrm{i}\mathsf{a}z}f(rz),

(55)

which shows that the
z^{2}
term can be as well transformed into a differential operator with respect to
r
. Then, it is straightforward to get the following differential equations:

\displaystyle\Big[(r_{1}^{2}-r_{1}^{4})\partial_{r_{1}}^{2}-\big(2r_{1}+(4+2p_{1})r_{1}^{3}\big)\partial_{r_{1}}+\big((\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu^{2}+\mbox{$\frac{\,{9}\,}{4}$})-(p+1)(p+2)r_{1}^{2}\big)\Big]

\displaystyle\times\big[r_{1}^{-1-p_{1}}r_{2}^{-1-p_{2}}\mathcal{I}_{\pm\mp}^{p_{1}p_{2}}(r_{1},r_{2})\big]=0,

(56)

\displaystyle\Big[(r_{1}^{2}-r_{1}^{4})\partial_{r_{1}}^{2}-\big(2r_{1}+(4+2p_{1})r_{1}^{3}\big)\partial_{r_{1}}+\big((\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu^{2}+\mbox{$\frac{\,{9}\,}{4}$})-(p+1)(p+2)r_{1}^{2}\big)\Big]

\displaystyle\times\big[r_{1}^{-1-p_{1}}r_{2}^{-1-p_{2}}\mathcal{I}_{\pm\pm}^{p_{1}p_{2}}(r_{1},r_{2})\big]=e^{\mp\mathrm{i}p_{12}\pi/2}\displaystyle\frac{\,{r_{1}^{4+p_{2}}r_{2}^{4+p_{1}}}\,}{(r_{1}+r_{2})^{5+p_{12}}}\Gamma(5+p_{12}).

(57)

After slight simplifications, we arrived at the bootstrap equations for the Hankel seed integral in terms of
r
-variables:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{r_{1}}^{p_{1}}\mathcal{I}_{\pm\mp}^{p_{1}p_{2}}(r_{1},r_{2})=0,

(58)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{r_{1}}^{p_{1}}\mathcal{I}_{\pm\pm}^{p_{1}p_{2}}(r_{1},r_{2})=e^{\mp\mathrm{i}p_{12}\pi/2}\Gamma(5+p_{12})\Big(\displaystyle\frac{\,{r_{1}r_{2}}\,}{r_{1}+r_{2}}\Big)^{5+p_{12}};

(59)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{r}^{p}\equiv(r^{2}-r^{4})\partial_{r}^{2}-\big[(4+2p)r+2r^{3}\big]\partial_{r}+\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu^{2}+\displaystyle\frac{\,{(5+2p)^{2}}\,}{4}.

(60)

One can well work with this set of equations and solve for the Hankel seed integral
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}
, as in most previous works on this topic. However, a crucial observation made in
[
64
]
is that it is much easier to take the three-point and two-point limits if we choose in instead to work with the following new variables:

u_{i}\equiv\displaystyle\frac{\,{2r_{i}}\,}{1+r_{i}},\quad i=1,2.

(61)

Correspondingly, we rewrite the Hankel seed integral as functions of
u_{1,2}
, namely, we define:

\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{I}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}^{p_{1}p_{2}}(u_{1},u_{2})\equiv\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}^{p_{1}p_{2}}\big(r_{1}(u_{1}),r_{2}(u_{2})\big).

(62)

Then, the bootstrap equations for
\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{I}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}^{p_{1}p_{2}}(u_{1},u_{2})
are:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{u_{1}}^{p_{1}}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{I}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\pm\mp}^{p_{1}p_{2}}(u_{1},u_{2})=0,

(63)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{u_{1}}^{p_{1}}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{I}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\pm\pm}^{p_{1}p_{2}}(u_{1},u_{2})=e^{\mp\mathrm{i}p_{12}\pi/2}\Gamma(5+p_{12})\Big(\displaystyle\frac{\,{u_{1}u_{2}}\,}{2(u_{1}+u_{2}-u_{1}u_{2})}\Big)^{5+p_{12}};

(64)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{u}^{p}\equiv(u^{2}-u^{3})\partial_{u}^{2}-\Big[(4+2p)u-(1+p)u^{2}\Big]\partial_{u}+\Big[\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu^{2}+\big(p+\mbox{$\frac{\,{5}\,}{2}$}\big)^{2}\Big].

(65)

This completes the derivation of the bootstrap equations for the Hankel seed integral in
u
-variables. Below we are going to solve these equations to get the four-point function and especially its folded limit.

3.2
Solving the bootstrap equation

Now we start to solve the bootstrap equations (
63
) and (
64
) for the Hankel seed integrals.

For the homogeneous equation (
63
), the solution is a proper linear combination of two independent solutions:

\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{I}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\pm\mp}^{p_{1}p_{2}}(u_{1},u_{2})=\sum_{\mathsf{a},\mathsf{b}=\pm}\alpha_{\pm\mp|\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{a}}^{p_{1}}(u_{1})\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{b}}^{p_{2}}(u_{2}).

(66)

Here
\mathcal{Y}_{\pm}^{p}(u)
is a pair of linearly independent solutions to the homogeneous equation (
63
), which we choose to be:

\mathcal{Y}_{\pm}^{p_{1}}(u_{1})=2^{\mp\mathrm{i}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu}\Big(\displaystyle\frac{\,{u_{1}}\,}{2}\Big)^{5/2+p_{1}\pm\mathrm{i}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu}\Gamma\Big[\displaystyle\frac{\,{5}\,}{2}+p_{1}\pm\mathrm{i}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu,\mp\mathrm{i}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu\Big]{}_{2}\mathrm{F}_{1}\left[\begin{matrix}\mbox{$\frac{\,{5}\,}{2}$}+p_{1}\pm\mathrm{i}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu,\mbox{$\frac{\,{1}\,}{2}$}\pm\mathrm{i}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu\\
1\pm 2\mathrm{i}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu\end{matrix}\middle|u_{1}\right].

(67)

Here we have included some numerical factors in
\mathcal{Y}_{\pm}^{p_{1}}(u_{1})
for later convenience. Note that we have used the fact that the seed integral
\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{I}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\pm\mp}^{p_{1}p_{2}}(u_{1},u_{2})
has a symmetry with respect to the exchange
u_{1}\leftrightarrow u_{2}
, and that the seed integrals satisfy an identical set of bootstrap equations with respect to
u_{2}
. Therefore, we expect that the final result for
\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{I}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\pm\mp}^{p_{1}p_{2}}(u_{1},u_{2})
should be bilinear in
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{a}}^{p_{1}}(u_{1})
and
\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{b}}^{p_{2}}(u_{2})
. The coefficients
\alpha_{\pm\mp|\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}
should be determined by imposing a proper boundary condition. In this work, we impose the boundary condition by matching the result to an explicit calculation in the squeezed limit
u_{1}\ll u_{2}\ll 1
, as will be elaborated below.

For the inhomogeneous equation (
64
), the solution is the sum of two parts: one particular solution
\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{I}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\pm\pm,\text{Inh}}^{p_{1}p_{2}}(u_{1},u_{2})
with the presence of the source term, and one homogeneous solution which is again a linear combination of two independent solutions to the homogeneous equation:

\displaystyle\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{I}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\pm\pm}^{p_{1}p_{2}}(u_{1},u_{2})=\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{I}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\pm\pm,\text{Inh}}^{p_{1}p_{2}}(u_{1},u_{2})+\sum_{\mathsf{a},\mathsf{b}=\pm}\alpha_{\pm\pm|\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{a}}^{p_{1}}(u_{1})\mathcal{Y}_{\mathsf{b}}^{p_{2}}(u_{2}).

(68)

The coefficients
\alpha_{\pm\pm;\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}
are again determined by matching the result to an explicit calculation in the squeezed limit.

Below, we shall first work out the particular solution
\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{I}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\pm\pm,\text{Inh}}^{p_{1}p_{2}}(u_{1},u_{2})
, and then construct the homogeneous solution. To find the particular solution, we do not work with arbitrary momentum configuration; Instead, we shall directly work with the three-point limit by taking
u_{2}\rightarrow 1
, since we are concerned only with the three-point and two-point functions in this work, and the single folded limit
u_{2}\rightarrow 1
is regular for the particular solution. The general four-point correlator for arbitrary momentum configuration can be calculated following the same procedure in our previous work
[
64
]
.

On the other hand, to find the homogeneous solution, we still work with the 4-point function, and impose the boundary conditions in the squeezed limit
u_{1,2}\rightarrow 0
. It turns out that this limit is enough to fix all the coefficients in the homogeneous solution. Only after this is done, we take the single folded limit
u_{2}\rightarrow 1
to get the result for the three-point function. The reason that we work with the four-point function for the homogeneous solution is that the single folded limit is singular for each term in the homogeneous solution, and the regular three-point limit is achieved only when we take the “correct” linear combination of all these terms.

Particular solution.

As said, we solve the inhomogeneous equation (
64
) in the single folded limit
u_{2}=1
. Also writing
u_{1}=u
, the equation becomes:

\mathcal{D}_{u}^{p_{1}}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{I}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\pm\pm,\text{Inh}}^{p_{1}p_{2}}(u,1)=e^{\mp\mathrm{i}p_{12}\pi/2}\Gamma(5+p_{12})\Big(\displaystyle\frac{\,{u}\,}{2}\Big)^{5+p_{12}}.

(69)

Note that
\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{I}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{++,\text{Inh}}^{p_{1}p_{2}}
and
\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{I}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{--,\text{Inh}}^{p_{1}p_{2}}
satisfy the equation with the same differential operator on the left-hand side but with the source term differed by a phase.

We use the following Taylor series as an ansatz for the solution:

\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{I}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\pm\pm,\text{Inh}}^{p_{1}p_{2}}(u,1)=2^{-5-p_{12}}e^{\mp\mathrm{i}p_{12}\pi/2}\Gamma(5+p_{12})\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{X}_{n}u^{5+p_{12}+n}.

(70)

Then, the equation (
69
) gives rise to the following recursion relation for the Taylor coefficients
\mathcal{X}_{n}
:

\mathcal{X}_{0}=\displaystyle\frac{\,{1}\,}{(\mbox{$\frac{\,{5}\,}{2}$}+p_{2})^{2}+\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu^{2}},\qquad\mathcal{X}_{n+1}=\displaystyle\frac{\,{(n+3+p_{2})(n+5+p_{12})}\,}{(n+\mbox{$\frac{\,{7}\,}{2}$}+p_{2})^{2}+\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu^{2}}\mathcal{X}_{n}.

(71)

Substituting this back into the ansatz (
70
), we see that the summation is a standard (generalized) hypergeometric series, which can be directly finished, and we get the following result for the particular solution:

\mathcal{X}_{n}=\displaystyle\frac{\,{(3+p_{2})_{n}(5+p_{12})_{n}}\,}{(p_{2}+\mbox{$\frac{\,{5}\,}{2}$}-\mathrm{i}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu)_{n+1}(p_{2}+\mbox{$\frac{\,{5}\,}{2}$}+\mathrm{i}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu)_{n+1}},

(72)

where
(a)_{n}\equiv\Gamma(a+n)/\Gamma(a)
is the Pochhammer symbol. Thus

\displaystyle\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{I}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\pm\pm,\text{Inh}}^{p_{1}p_{2}}(u,1)=\displaystyle\frac{\,{e^{\mp\mathrm{i}p_{12}\pi/2}\Gamma(5+p_{12})u^{5+p_{12}}}\,}{2^{5+p_{12}}[(\mbox{$\frac{\,{5}\,}{2}$}+p_{2})^{2}+\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu^{2}]}{}_{3}\mathrm{F}_{2}\left[\begin{matrix}1,3+p_{2},5+p_{12}\\
\mbox{$\frac{\,{7}\,}{2}$}+p_{2}-\mathrm{i}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu,\mbox{$\frac{\,{7}\,}{2}$}+p_{2}+\mathrm{i}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu\end{matrix}\middle|u\right].

(73)

This completes our derivation for the particular solution to the bootstrap equation.

Homogeneous solutions.

As mentioned above, the problem of finding the homogeneous solution boils down to a determination of coefficients
\alpha_{\pm\mp|\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}
and
\alpha_{\pm\pm|\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}
, as given in (
66
) and (
68
).

We impose the boundary condition in the hierarchical squeezed limit
u_{1}\ll u_{2}\ll 1
. In this limit, the tree-seed integral can be directly done. We put this calculation in App.
B.1
, and here we quote the result:

\displaystyle\lim_{u_{1}\ll u_{2}\ll 1}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{I}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu^{p_{1}p_{2}}_{\pm\mp}(u_{1},u_{2})=

\displaystyle~\displaystyle\frac{\,{e^{\mp\mathrm{i}\bar{p}_{12}\pi/2}}\,}{4\pi}\Big[\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{Y}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{+}^{p_{1}}(u_{1})+\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{Y}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{-}^{p_{1}}(u_{1})\Big]\Big[\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{Y}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{+}^{p_{2}}(u_{2})+\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{Y}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{-}^{p_{2}}(u_{2})\Big],

(74)

\displaystyle\lim_{u_{1}\ll u_{2}\ll 1}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{I}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu^{p_{1}p_{2}}_{\pm\pm}(u_{1},u_{2})=

\displaystyle~\displaystyle\frac{\,{\pm\mathrm{i}e^{\mp\mathrm{i}p_{12}\pi/2}}\,}{4\pi}\Big[e^{\pi\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{Y}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\pm}^{p_{1}}(u_{1})+e^{-\pi\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{Y}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\mp}^{p_{1}}(u_{1})\Big]\Big[\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{Y}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{+}^{p_{2}}(u_{2})+\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{Y}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{-}^{p_{2}}(u_{2})\Big].

(75)

where
\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{Y}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\pm}^{p}(u)
is the two independent solutions
\mathcal{Y}_{\pm}^{p}(u)
given in (
67
) in the squeezed limit
u\rightarrow 0
:

\displaystyle\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{Y}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\pm}^{p}(u)=2^{\mp\mathrm{i}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu}\Big(\displaystyle\frac{\,{u}\,}{2}\Big)^{5/2+p\pm\mathrm{i}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu}\Gamma\Big[\displaystyle\frac{\,{5}\,}{2}+p+\mathrm{i}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu,-\mathrm{i}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu\Big].

(76)

Given the form of the squeezed-limit solution, we do not have to write down all the coefficients
\alpha_{\mathsf{cd}|\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}
explicitly. Rather, we only need to make the replacement
\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{Y}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\pm}^{p}(u)\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}_{\pm}^{p}(u)
in (
74
) and (
75
), and the results are guaranteed to be the correct homogeneous solutions:

\displaystyle\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{I}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu^{p_{1}p_{2}}_{\pm\mp}(u_{1},u_{2})=

\displaystyle~\displaystyle\frac{\,{e^{\mp\mathrm{i}\bar{p}_{12}\pi/2}}\,}{4\pi}\Big[{\mathcal{Y}}_{+}^{p_{1}}(u_{1})+{\mathcal{Y}}_{-}^{p_{1}}(u_{1})\Big]\Big[{\mathcal{Y}}_{+}^{p_{2}}(u_{2})+{\mathcal{Y}}_{-}^{p_{2}}(u_{2})\Big],

(77)

\displaystyle\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{I}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu^{p_{1}p_{2}}_{\pm\pm}(u_{1},u_{2})=

\displaystyle~\displaystyle\frac{\,{\pm\mathrm{i}e^{\mp\mathrm{i}p_{12}\pi/2}}\,}{4\pi}\Big[e^{\pi\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu}{\mathcal{Y}}_{\pm}^{p_{1}}(u_{1})+e^{-\pi\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu}{\mathcal{Y}}_{\mp}^{p_{1}}(u_{1})\Big]\Big[{\mathcal{Y}}_{+}^{p_{2}}(u_{2})+{\mathcal{Y}}_{-}^{p_{2}}(u_{2})\Big].

(78)

At this point, an important observation is that the squeezed-limit results (
74
) and (
75
) scale with the momentum ratio
u_{1}
as
u_{1}^{5/2+p_{1}\pm\mathrm{i}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu}
, which is identical to the scaling of the general homogeneous solution (
67
). On the contrary, the particular solution (
73
) scales as
u_{1}^{5+p_{12}}
in the squeezed limit
u_{1}\ll 1
. [Note that
u_{1}=u
in (
73
).] Therefore, for positive real
\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu
, as long as
p_{2}>-5/2
, the particular solution (
73
) is always subdominant, and the general solutions in (
66
) and (
68
) can be matched to the squeezed-limit result (
74
) and (
75
), respectively. On the other hand, when
p_{2}\leq-5/2
, the particular solution will dominate the squeezed limit, and there is no way to match the squeezed-limit result. In fact, the Hankel seed integral
\mathcal{I}^{p_{1}p_{2}}
is divergent when
p_{1,2}\leq-5/2
, and the divergence does not cancel out among different SK branches. Physically, the powers
p_{1,2}\leq-5/2
appear because of too nonlocal interactions, due to which the perturbation theory actually breaks down. Therefore, we shall always assume
p_{1,2}>-5/2
in this work when
\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu
is positive real.

3.3
Folded limit

Single folded limit.

Now we consider the single folded limit
u_{2}\rightarrow 1
. The general solution (
67
) can become singular in this limit through the hypergeometric factor
{}_{2}\text{F}_{1}
, and the singular terms must cancel themselves in (
77
) and (
78
), so that only the finite part of the hypergeometric function contributes to the folded limit. Therefore, we only keep the finite part of
{}_{2}\text{F}_{1}
function. In the folded limit
u\rightarrow 1
,

\text{Fin}\bigg\{\lim_{u\rightarrow 1}{}_{2}\mathrm{F}_{1}\left[\begin{matrix}a,b\\
c\end{matrix}\middle|u\right]\bigg\}=\Gamma\left[\begin{matrix}c,s\\

4  Bootstrapping Whittaker Seed Integrals

Now we consider the case where the intermediate massive particle has a boost-breaking chemical potential. This scenario is particularly interesting for CC phenomenology due to its exponentially enhanced signal and also its parity-violating nature. Mathematically, the mode functions for such fields involve the Whittaker W function, which needs a separate treatment from the previously considered Hankel type. In this section, we shall consider the Whittaker case and go through the same procedure as the last section. We shall be briefer in this section since most of the intermediate steps are in parallel with the previous section.

4.1
Whittaker seed integral and its bootstrap equation

As introduced in Sec.
2
, the Whittaker seed integral (
11
) is defined based on the SK integral for the tree-level four-point correlator with the exchange of a spin-1 particle of mass parameter
\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu
and helicity-dependent chemical potential
\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu
. In parallel with the previous section, we first derive the bootstrap equations for this seed integral with
r
-variables, and then change to
u
-variables. Thus we rewrite the Whittaker seed integral (
11
) in the following way:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{(h)p_{1}p_{2}}_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}(r_{1},r_{2})=-\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}\,r_{1}^{1+p_{1}}r_{2}^{1+p_{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\mathrm{d}}z_{1}{\mathrm{d}}z_{2}\,z_{1}^{p_{1}}z_{2}^{p_{2}}e^{-\mathrm{i}\mathsf{a}z_{1}-\mathrm{i}\mathsf{b}z_{2}}\mkern 2.0mu\widehat{\mkern-2.0muD\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}^{(h)}(r_{1}z_{1},r_{2}z_{2}).

(94)

In the seed integral above,
\mkern 2.0mu\widehat{\mkern-2.0muD\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}^{(h)}(z_{1},z_{2})
is again a hatted propagator, this time built from the helicity-
h
component (
h=\pm 1
) of the massive spin-1 propagator
D_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}^{(h)}(k;\tau_{1},\tau_{2})
:

\mkern 2.0mu\widehat{\mkern-2.0muD\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}^{(h)}(z_{1},z_{2})=kD_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}^{(h)}(k;\tau_{1},\tau_{2}).

(95)

Again, we use the dimensionless and positive variables
z_{i}=-k\tau_{i}

(i=1,2)
. Here the four Schwinger-Keldysh propagators
D_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}^{(h)}(k;\tau_{1},\tau_{2})
are again related to the Wightman functions
D_{>}^{(h)}
and
D_{<}^{(h)}=D_{>}^{(h)*}
, and the “greater” Wightman function is given by

\displaystyle D^{(h)}_{>}(k;\tau_{1},\tau_{2})=

\displaystyle~\displaystyle\frac{\,{e^{-\pi h\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu}}\,}{2k}\mathrm{W}_{\mathrm{i}h\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu,\mathrm{i}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu}(2\mathrm{i}k\tau_{1})\mathrm{W}_{-\mathrm{i}h\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu,\mathrm{i}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu}(-2\mathrm{i}k\tau_{2}).

(96)

Let us emphasize that, although we are building the Whittaker seed integral from the spin-1 propagators, this seed integral can be used to compute correlators involving higher spin states. Very often, the boost-breaking chemical potential changes the dispersion of a spin-
s
field in such a way that its highest or lowest helicity component (
h=\pm s
) is most enhanced. For such states, the mode function is identical to the
h=\pm 1
states of the spin-1 field up to a prefactor, and thus above seed integral is perfectly applicable to these cases. One only needs to be careful that the mass parameter
\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu
is related to the mass
m
of a spin-
s
state via
\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu=\sqrt{m^{2}-(s-1/2)^{2}}
.

The equations of motion satisfied by the propagator
D_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}^{(h)}(k;\tau_{1},\tau_{2})
then lead to the following set of equations satisfied by the hatted propagators:

\displaystyle(r_{1}^{2}\partial_{r_{1}}^{2}+r_{1}^{2}z_{1}^{2}+2h\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mur_{1}z_{1}+m^{2})\mkern 2.0mu\widehat{\mkern-2.0muD\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\pm\mp}^{(h)}(r_{1}z_{1},r_{2}z_{2})=0,

(97)

\displaystyle(r_{1}^{2}\partial_{r_{1}}^{2}+r_{1}^{2}z_{1}^{2}+2h\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mur_{1}z_{1}+m^{2})\mkern 2.0mu\widehat{\mkern-2.0muD\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\pm\pm}^{(h)}(r_{1}z_{1},r_{2}z_{2})=\mp\mathrm{i}r_{1}r_{2}z_{1}z_{2}\delta(r_{1}z_{1}-r_{2}z_{2}).

(98)

Therefore, we insert the differential operator
r_{1}^{2}\partial_{r_{1}}^{2}+r_{1}^{2}z_{1}^{2}+2h\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mur_{1}z_{1}+m^{2}
in front of the hatted propagator in the Whittaker seed integral (
94
), to derive the bootstrap equation. On one hand, the differential operator reduces the integral to either 0 or a local term, which becomes the right-hand side of the bootstrap equation. On the other hand, we commute the differential operator with the integrals over
z_{1,2}
with the help of (
54
) and (
55
), and end up with a new differential operator acting on the whole seed integral, which is the left-hand side of the bootstrap equation. The result is:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\pm,r_{1}}^{p_{1}}\mathcal{I}_{\pm\mp}^{(h)}(r_{1},r_{2})=0,

(99)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\pm,r_{1}}^{p_{1}}\mathcal{I}_{\pm\pm}^{(h)}(r_{1},r_{2})=-e^{\mp\mathrm{i}p_{12}\pi/2}\Gamma(3+p_{12})\Big(\displaystyle\frac{\,{r_{1}r_{2}}\,}{r_{1}+r_{2}}\Big)^{3+p_{12}};

(100)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\pm,r}^{p}\equiv(r^{2}-r^{4})\partial_{r}^{2}-\Big[2(1+p)r\pm 2\mathrm{i}h\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mur^{2}+2r^{3}\Big]\partial_{r}+\Big[\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu^{2}+\mbox{$\frac{\,{(3+2p)^{2}}\,}{4}$}\Big].

(101)

Next we use the new variables
u_{i}=(2r_{i})/(1+r_{i})
in place of
r_{i}

(i=1,2)
, in terms of which the seed integral is rewritten as

\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{I}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}^{(h)p_{1}p_{2}}(u_{1},u_{2})\equiv\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}^{(h)p_{1}p_{2}}\big(r_{1}(u_{1}),r_{2}(u_{2})\big)

(102)

Then we can derive the following set of equations with respect to the
u
-variable:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\pm,u_{1}}^{p_{1}}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{I}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\pm\mp}^{(h)}(u_{1},u_{2})=0,

(103)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\pm,u_{1}}^{p_{1}}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{I}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\pm\pm}^{(h)}(u_{1},u_{2})=-e^{\mp\mathrm{i}p_{12}\pi/2}\Gamma(3+p_{12})\Big(\displaystyle\frac{\,{u_{1}u_{2}}\,}{2(u_{1}+u_{2}-u_{1}u_{2})}\Big)^{3+p_{12}};

(104)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\pm,u}^{p}=(u^{2}-u^{3})\partial_{u}^{2}-\Big[(2+2p)u+(\pm\mathrm{i}h\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu-p)u^{2}\Big]\partial_{u}+\Big[\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu^{2}+\big(p+\mbox{$\frac{\,{3}\,}{2}$}\big)^{2}\Big].

(105)

These are the bootstrap equations for the Whittaker seed integral, from which we shall bootstrap the three-point and two-point functions in the following subsections.

4.2
Solving the bootstrap equation

In parallel with the procedure in the last section, we now solve the bootstrap equations (
103
) and (
104
). Again, the solution to the homogeneous equation (
103
) can be written as a linear combination of the two independent solutions

\displaystyle\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{I}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\pm\mp}^{(h)p_{1}p_{2}}(u_{1},u_{2})=\sum_{\mathsf{a},\mathsf{b}=\pm}\beta_{\pm\mp|\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}\,\mathcal{U}_{\pm|\mathsf{a}}^{p_{1}}(u_{1})\,\mathcal{U}_{\mp|\mathsf{b}}^{p_{2}}(u_{2}),

(106)

with
\mathcal{U}^{p}_{\mathsf{a}|\mathsf{b}}(u)
(
\mathsf{b}=\pm 1
) the two independent solutions to the following equations:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\mathsf{a},u}^{p}\mathcal{U}^{p}_{\mathsf{a}|\mathsf{b}}(u)=0.

(107)

Here
\mathcal{D}_{\mathsf{a},u}^{p}
are the differential operators in (
105
). Note that we have two distinct operators
\mathcal{D}_{\mathsf{a},u}^{p}
, labeled by
\mathsf{a}=\pm
. For each fixed choice of
\mathsf{a}=\pm
, there are a pair of independent solutions, labeled by
\mathsf{b}=\pm
, hence the notation
\mathcal{U}^{p}_{\mathsf{a}|\mathsf{b}}
for the solutions. Explicitly, we can choose
\mathcal{U}^{p}_{\mathsf{a}|\mathsf{b}}
to be:

\mathcal{U}_{\mathsf{a}|\mathsf{b}}^{p}(u)=\mathrm{i}\,\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}\,2^{\mathrm{i}\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu}\pi\text{csch}(2\pi\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu)\Big(\displaystyle\frac{\,{u}\,}{2}\Big)^{3/2+p+\mathrm{i}\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu}{}_{2}\mathcal{F}_{1}\left[\begin{matrix}\mbox{$\frac{\,{3}\,}{2}$}+p+\mathrm{i}\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu,\mbox{$\frac{\,{1}\,}{2}$}+\mathrm{i}\mathsf{a}h\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu+\mathrm{i}\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu\\
1+2\mathrm{i}\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu\end{matrix}\middle|u\right].

(108)

Here, again, we have included some numerical factors for later convenience. The coefficients
\beta_{\pm\mp|\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}
in (
106
) should be determined by the boundary conditions in the squeezed limit, as will be detailed below.

For the inhomogeneous equation (
104
), the solution is a sum of a particular solution
\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{I}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\pm\pm,\text{Inh}}^{(h)p_{1}p_{2}}(u_{1},u_{2})
and a homogeneous solution which is again a linear combination of independent solutions:

\displaystyle\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{I}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\pm\pm}^{(h)p_{1}p_{2}}(u_{1},u_{2})=\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{I}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\pm\pm,\text{Inh}}^{(h)p_{1}p_{2}}(u_{1},u_{2})+\sum_{\mathsf{a},\mathsf{b}=\pm}\beta_{\pm\pm|\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}}\,\mathcal{U}^{p_{1}}_{\pm|\mathsf{a}}(u_{1})\,\mathcal{U}^{p_{2}}_{\pm|\mathsf{b}}(u_{2}).

(109)

Below we will first find the particular solution
\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{I}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\pm\pm,\text{Inh}}^{(h)p_{1}p_{2}}(u_{1},u_{2})
, and then determine the homogeneous solutions by matching the results in the squeezed limit.

Particular solution.

Similar to the previous section, we do not pursue the most general result for the particular solution. Rather, we shall set
u_{2}=1
and write
u_{1}=u
in the inhomogeneous equation (
104
):

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\pm,u}^{p_{1}}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{I}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu^{(h)p_{1}p_{2}}_{\pm\pm,\text{Inh}}(u,1)=-e^{\mp\mathrm{i}p_{12}\pi/2}\Gamma(3+p_{12})\Big(\displaystyle\frac{\,{u}\,}{2}\Big)^{3+p_{12}}.

(110)

This equation can again be solved by trying the following series ansatz:

\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{I}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu^{(h)p_{1}p_{2}}_{\pm\pm,\text{Inh}}(u,1)=-2^{-3-p_{12}}e^{\mp\mathrm{i}p_{12}\pi/2}\Gamma(3+p_{12})\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{V}_{n,\pm}u^{3+p_{12}+n}.

(111)

The differential equation then leads to the following recursion relations for the series coefficients:

\mathcal{V}_{0,\pm}=\displaystyle\frac{\,{1}\,}{(\mbox{$\frac{\,{3}\,}{2}$}+p_{2})^{2}+\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu^{2}},\qquad\mathcal{V}_{n+1,\pm}=\displaystyle\frac{\,{(n+2+p_{2}\pm\mathrm{i}h\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu)(n+3+p_{12})}\,}{(n+\mbox{$\frac{\,{5}\,}{2}$}+p_{2})^{2}+\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu^{2}}\mathcal{V}_{n,\pm},

(112)

and the recursion relation can be directly solved to get a general term formula:

\mathcal{V}_{n,\pm}=\displaystyle\frac{\,{(2+p_{2}\pm\mathrm{i}h\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu)_{n}(3+p_{12})_{n}}\,}{(p_{2}+\mbox{$\frac{\,{3}\,}{2}$}-\mathrm{i}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu)_{n+1}(p_{2}+\mbox{$\frac{\,{3}\,}{2}$}+\mathrm{i}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu)_{n+1}}.

(113)

This shows that the series ansatz is again a standard hypergeometric series. Finishing the summation, we get:

\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{I}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu^{(h)p_{1}p_{2}}_{\pm\pm,\text{Inh}}(u,1)=-\displaystyle\frac{\,{e^{\mp\mathrm{i}p_{12}\pi/2}\Gamma(3+p_{12})u^{3+p_{12}}}\,}{2^{3+p_{12}}[(\mbox{$\frac{\,{3}\,}{2}$}+p_{2})^{2}+\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu^{2}]}{}_{3}\mathrm{F}_{2}\left[\begin{matrix}1,2+p_{2}\pm\mathrm{i}h\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu,3+p_{12}\\
\mbox{$\frac{\,{5}\,}{2}$}+p_{2}-\mathrm{i}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu,\mbox{$\frac{\,{5}\,}{2}$}+p_{2}+\mathrm{i}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu\end{matrix}\middle|u\right].

(114)

This completes our computation of the particular solution.

and
\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{I}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\pm\pm}^{(h)p_{1}p_{2}}(u_{1},u_{2})

(115)

\displaystyle\beta_{\pm\pm|++}=\beta_{\pm\pm|+-}=\displaystyle\frac{\,{\mp\mathrm{i}e^{\mp\mathrm{i}p_{12}\pi/2}e^{-\pi(h\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu-\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu)}\cosh[\pi(h\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu+\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu)]}\,}{\pi\Gamma\big[\mbox{$\frac{\,{1}\,}{2}$}\pm\mathrm{i}h\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu-\mathrm{i}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu,\mbox{$\frac{\,{1}\,}{2}$}\pm\mathrm{i}h\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu+\mathrm{i}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu\big]},

(116)

\displaystyle\beta_{\pm\pm|-+}=\beta_{\pm\pm|--}=\displaystyle\frac{\,{\mp\mathrm{i}e^{\mp\mathrm{i}p_{12}\pi/2}e^{-\pi(h\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu+\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu)}\cosh[\pi(h\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu-\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu)]}\,}{\pi\Gamma\big[\mbox{$\frac{\,{1}\,}{2}$}\pm\mathrm{i}h\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu-\mathrm{i}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu,\mbox{$\frac{\,{1}\,}{2}$}\pm\mathrm{i}h\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu+\mathrm{i}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu\big]}.

(117)

4.3
Folded limit

Single-folded limit.

Now we consider the folded limits of the Whittaker seed integrals. First, to obtain the three-point function, we take the single-folded limit
u_{2}\rightarrow 1
. As in the previous section, each of the solutions
\mathcal{U}_{\mathsf{a}|\mathsf{b}}^{p}(u)
is singular when

. However, the singular terms must cancel out in the final expression, as a result of choosing the Bunch-Davies initial condition. Therefore, we only need to retain the finite parts of the solutions
\mathcal{U}_{\mathsf{a}|\mathsf{b}}^{p}(u)
in the folded limit
u\rightarrow 1
. For notational simplicity, we define the finite part of
\mathcal{U}_{\mathsf{a}|\mathsf{b}}^{p}(1)
as:

U_{\mathsf{a}|\mathsf{b}}^{p}\equiv\text{Fin}\Big\{\mathcal{U}_{\mathsf{a}|\mathsf{b}}^{p}(1)\Big\}=\displaystyle\frac{\,{\mathrm{i}\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}\pi}\,}{2^{3/2+p}\sinh(2\pi\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu)}\Gamma\left[\begin{matrix}\mbox{$\frac{\,{3}\,}{2}$}+p+\mathrm{i}\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu,\mbox{$\frac{\,{1}\,}{2}$}+\mathrm{i}\mathsf{a}h\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu+\mathrm{i}\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu,-1-p-\mathrm{i}\mathsf{a}h\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu\\
-\mbox{$\frac{\,{1}\,}{2}$}-p+\mathrm{i}\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu,\mbox{$\frac{\,{1}\,}{2}$}-\mathrm{i}\mathsf{a}h\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu+\mathrm{i}\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu\end{matrix}\right].

(118)

Looking at the structure of the coefficients
\beta_{\mathsf{a}\mathsf{b}|\mathsf{c}\mathsf{d}}
in (
115
) - (
117
), we see that the

-dependence in the full homogeneous solution always appears in the combination
{\mathcal{U}}_{\pm|+}^{p_{2}}(u_{2})+{\mathcal{U}}_{\pm|-}^{p_{2}}(u_{2})

\displaystyle\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{I}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\pm\mp}^{(h)p_{1}p_{2}}(u,1)=

Double-folded limit.

5  Conclusions and Outlooks

Inflation correlators play central roles in the study of Cosmological Collider physics, and it is also of central importance for a better understanding of quantum field theories in dS. Exact and analytical results for inflation correlators are useful for understanding the analytic structure of dS correlation functions in general, for phenomenological studies of CC physics, and also for efficient numerical implementation.

In this work, we have found exact and closed-form formulae for a wide range of two-point and three-point correlation functions of massless modes in inflationary spacetime mediated by a single massive field at the tree level. As we have shown, tree-level correlators can be easily reduced to (combinations of) seed integrals, either Hankel-type or Whittaker-type. In particular, three/two-point functions correspond to the single/double-folded limit of seed integrals. We calculated the seed integrals using an improved bootstrap method. First, our start point of deriving the bootstrap equation is the equations of motion for the massive propagator, rather than the bulk symmetry, so our method does not depend on full dS symmetry and also applies to dS-boost-breaking models. Second, we found it more convenient to express the bootstrap equations with variables
u_{1,2}
, since the inhomogeneous solution in the single-folded limit can then be summed to a generalized hypergeometric function. With the coefficients of homogeneous solutions appropriately determined, closed-form expressions of seed integrals in the single-folded limit are derived. It is then straightforward to go to the double-folded limit, where all the spurious divergences should be canceled due to the Bunch-Davis initial condition.

Apart from building phenomenological models and constructing efficient templates for practical data analysis, our results also find wide applications for theoretical studies of inflation correlators.
On one hand, low-point tree-level correlators can be subgraphs of more complicated processes, and our expressions can be used as building blocks. This topic will be explored in detail in
[
91
]
, where the two-point correlators found in this work act as effective vertices in a loop diagram.
On the other hand, closed-form expressions explicitly show the analytical structure of the correlators and thus make the analytic continuation easier to do. The analytic structure of inflation correlators encodes rich physical information, and can provides us useful insights into the physical processes happening in the inflationary universe. They may also pave the way for efficient computation methods for more complicated correlation functions. We leave these topics for future studies.

\displaystyle\times\Gamma\Big[\mbox{$\frac{\,{5}\,}{2}$}+p_{1}-2s_{1},\mbox{$\frac{\,{5}\,}{2}$}+p_{2}-2s_{2},s_{1}-\mbox{$\frac{\,{\mathrm{i}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu}\,}{2}$},s_{1}+\mbox{$\frac{\,{\mathrm{i}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu}\,}{2}$},s_{2}-\mbox{$\frac{\,{\mathrm{i}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu}\,}{2}$},s_{2}+\mbox{$\frac{\,{\mathrm{i}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu}\,}{2}$}\Big],

\displaystyle\times{}_{2}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathrm{F}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{1}\left[\begin{matrix}\mbox{$\frac{\,{5}\,}{2}$}+p_{2}-2s_{2},5+p_{12}-2s_{12}\\
\mbox{$\frac{\,{7}\,}{2}$}+p_{2}-2s_{2}\end{matrix}\middle|\,-\displaystyle\frac{\,{r_{1}}\,}{r_{2}}\right].

(144)

The last step is to complete the above Barnes-type integrals using the residue theorem. Since we focus on the region
r_{1}<r_{2}<1
, we should close the contour from left, and sum over residues at the left poles for both
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
:

s_{i}=-n_{i}\mp\mathrm{i}\displaystyle\frac{\,{\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu}\,}{2},\qquad n_{i}\in\mathbb{N},\quad i=1,2,

(145)

then we can obtain the full result.

However, we only need the leading order result in the squeezed limit
r_{1}\ll r_{2}\ll 1
, which obviously corresponds to the poles with
n_{1,2}=0
. Furthermore, we find both
\mathcal{I}_{\pm\mp}^{p_{1}p_{2}}
and
\mathcal{I}_{\pm\pm,\text{F},>}^{p_{1}p_{2}}
are of order
\mathcal{O}(r_{1}^{5/2+p_{1}}r_{2}^{5/2+p_{2}})
, but the time-ordered integral
\mathcal{I}_{\pm\pm,\text{TO},>}^{p_{1}p_{2}}
is of order
\mathcal{O}(r_{1}^{5+p_{12}})
and thus can be neglected since we are working in the region
r_{1}\ll r_{2}
and we assume
\text{Re}\,p_{2}>-5/2
as explained below (
10
). So finally we can obtain the leading order results by summing the residues at four poles

:

\displaystyle\lim_{r_{1}\ll r_{2}\ll 1}\mathcal{I}_{\pm\mp}^{p_{1}p_{2}}(r_{1},r_{2})=

\displaystyle~\displaystyle\frac{\,{e^{\mp\mathrm{i}\bar{p}_{12}\pi/2}}\,}{4\pi}\Big[\mkern 2.0mu\widehat{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{Y}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{+}^{p_{1}}(r_{1})+\mkern 2.0mu\widehat{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{Y}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{-}^{p_{1}}(r_{1})\Big]\Big[\mkern 2.0mu\widehat{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{Y}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{+}^{p_{2}}(r_{2})+\mkern 2.0mu\widehat{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{Y}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{-}^{p_{2}}(r_{2})\Big],

(146)

\displaystyle\lim_{r_{1}\ll r_{2}\ll 1}\mathcal{I}_{\pm\pm}^{p_{1}p_{2}}(r_{1},r_{2})=

\displaystyle~\displaystyle\frac{\,{\pm\mathrm{i}e^{\mp\mathrm{i}p_{12}\pi/2}}\,}{4\pi}\Big[e^{\pi\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu\widehat{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{Y}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\pm}^{p_{1}}(r_{1})+e^{-\pi\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu\widehat{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{Y}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\mp}^{p_{1}}(r_{1})\Big]\Big[\mkern 2.0mu\widehat{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{Y}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{+}^{p_{2}}(r_{2})+\mkern 2.0mu\widehat{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{Y}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{-}^{p_{2}}(r_{2})\Big],

(147)

where

\mkern 2.0mu\widehat{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{Y}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\pm}^{p}(r)=2^{\mp\mathrm{i}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu}r^{5/2+p\pm\mathrm{i}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu}\Gamma\Big[\displaystyle\frac{\,{5}\,}{2}+p\pm\mathrm{i}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu,\mp\mathrm{i}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu\Big].

(148)

One can straightforwardly write down the above results in terms of variables

:

\displaystyle\lim_{u_{1}\ll u_{2}\ll 1}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{I}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu^{p_{1}p_{2}}_{\pm\mp}(u_{1},u_{2})=

\displaystyle~\displaystyle\frac{\,{e^{\mp\mathrm{i}\bar{p}_{12}\pi/2}}\,}{4\pi}\Big[\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{Y}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{+}^{p_{1}}(u_{1})+\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{Y}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{-}^{p_{1}}(u_{1})\Big]\Big[\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{Y}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{+}^{p_{2}}(u_{2})+\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{Y}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{-}^{p_{2}}(u_{2})\Big],

(149)

\displaystyle\lim_{u_{1}\ll u_{2}\ll 1}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{I}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu^{p_{1}p_{2}}_{\pm\pm}(u_{1},u_{2})=

\displaystyle~\displaystyle\frac{\,{\pm\mathrm{i}e^{\mp\mathrm{i}p_{12}\pi/2}}\,}{4\pi}\Big[e^{\pi\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{Y}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\pm}^{p_{1}}(u_{1})+e^{-\pi\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{Y}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\mp}^{p_{1}}(u_{1})\Big]\Big[\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{Y}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{+}^{p_{2}}(u_{2})+\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{Y}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{-}^{p_{2}}(u_{2})\Big].

(150)

where

\displaystyle\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\mathcal{Y}\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu_{\pm}^{p}(u)=2^{\mp\mathrm{i}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu}\Big(\displaystyle\frac{\,{u}\,}{2}\Big)^{5/2+p\pm\mathrm{i}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu}\Gamma\Big[\displaystyle\frac{\,{5}\,}{2}+p\pm\mathrm{i}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu,\mp\mathrm{i}\mkern 2.0mu\widetilde{\mkern-2.0mu\nu\mkern-2.0mu}\mkern 2.0mu\Big].
