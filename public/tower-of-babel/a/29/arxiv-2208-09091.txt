A Hausdorff dimension analysis of sets with the product of consecutive vs single partial quotients in continued fractions
Mumtaz Hussain, Bixuan Li, Nikita Shulga

Abstract
. We present a detailed Hausdorff dimension analysis of the set of real numbers where the product of consecutive partial quotients in their continued fraction expansion grow at a certain rate but the growth of the single partial quotient is at a different rate. We consider the set ℱ ⁡ ( Φ 1 , Φ 2 ) = def ℰ ⁡ ( Φ 1 ) \ ℰ ⁡ ( Φ 2 ) = { x ∈ [ 0 , 1 ) : a n ​ ( x ) ​ a n + 1 ​ ( x ) ≥ Φ 1 ​ ( n ) ​ for infinitely many ​ n ∈ ℕ a n + 1 ​ ( x ) < Φ 2 ​ ( n ) ​ for all sufficiently large ​ n ∈ ℕ } , \mathcal{F}(\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\mathcal{E}(\Phi_{1})\backslash\mathcal{E}(\Phi_{2})=\left\{x\in[0,1):\begin{split}a_{n}(x)a_{n+1}(x)&\geq\Phi_{1}(n)\text{\,\, for infinitely many }n\in\mathbb{N}\\ a_{n+1}(x)&<\Phi_{2}(n)\text{\,\, for all sufficiently large }n\in\mathbb{N}\end{split}\right\}, where Φ i : ℕ → ( 0 , ∞ ) \Phi_{i}:\mathbb{N}\to(0,\infty) are any functions such that lim n → ∞ Φ i ​ ( n ) = ∞ \lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\Phi_{i}(n)=\infty . We obtain some surprising results including the situations when ℱ ⁡ ( Φ 1 , Φ 2 ) \mathcal{F}(\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2}) is empty for various non-trivial choices of Φ i \Phi_{i} ’s. Our results contribute to the metrical theory of continued fractions by generalising several known results including the main result of [Nonlinearity, 33(6):2615–2639, 2020]. To obtain some of the results, we consider an alternate generalised set, which may be of independent interest, and calculate its Hausdorff dimension. One of the main ingredients is in the usage of the classical mass distribution principle; specifically a careful distribution of the mass on the Cantor subset by introducing a new idea of two different types of probability measures.



1.
Introduction

It is well-known that every irrational number
x\in(0,1)
has a unique infinite continued fraction expansion. This expansion can be induced by the Gauss map
T:[0,1)\to[0,1)
defined by

T(0)=0,~T(x)=\frac{1}{x}-\left\lfloor\frac{1}{x}\right\rfloor\textmd{ for }x\in(0,1),

where
\lfloor x\rfloor
denotes the integer part of
x
. We write
x:=[a_{1}(x),a_{2}(x),a_{3}(x),\ldots]
for the continued fraction of
x
where
a_{1}(x)=\lfloor 1/x\rfloor
,
a_{n}(x)=a_{1}(T^{n-1}(x))
for
n\geq 2
are called the partial quotients of
x
.

The metric theory of continued fractions concerns the quantitative study of properties of partial quotients for almost all
x\in(0,1)
. This area of research is closely connected with metric Diophantine approximation, for example, fundamental theorems in this field by Khintchine (1924) and Jarník (1931) can be represented in terms of the growth of partial quotients. The classical Borel-Bernstein theorem (1912) states that the Lebesgue measure of the set

\mathcal{E}_{1}(\Phi):=\left\{x\in[0,1):a_{n}(x)\geq\Phi(n)\ {\rm for\ infinitely\ many}\ n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}

is either zero or one depending upon the convergence or divergence of the series
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\Phi(n)^{-1}
respectively. Here and throughout
\Phi:\mathbb{N}\to[1,\infty)
will be an arbitrary function such that
\Phi(n)\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
. For rapidly increasing functions
\Phi
, the Borel-Bernstein (1911, 1912) theorem gives no further information than the zero Lebesgue measure of
\mathcal{E}_{1}(\Phi)
. To distinguish between the sets of zero Lebesgue measure, the Hausdorff dimension is an appropriate tool.
When the function
\Phi(n)
tends to infinity at a polynomial rate
n^{a}
, the Hausdorff dimension of
\mathcal{E}_{1}(\Phi)
was calculated by Good
[
7
]
, and when
\Phi(n)
tends to infinity at a super-exponential rate
a^{b^{n}},
the Hausdorff dimension was established by Łuczak
[
16
]
. For an arbitrary function
\Phi
the dimension of
\mathcal{E}_{1}(\Phi)
was computed by Wang-Wu
[
23
]
.

Theorem 1.1

(
[
23
, Wang-Wu]
)
.

Let
\Phi:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{[}1,\infty)
. Suppose

\log B=\liminf\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\log\Phi(n)}{n}\ {and}\ \log b=\liminf\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\log\log\Phi(n)}{n}.

(i)

When
B=1
,
\dim_{H}\mathcal{E}_{1}(\Phi)=1.

(ii)

When
B=\infty
,
\dim_{H}\mathcal{E}_{1}(\Phi)=1/(1+b).

(iii)

When
1<B<\infty
,
\dim_{H}\mathcal{E}_{1}(\Phi)=s_{B}:=\inf\{s\geq 0:\mathsf{P}(T,-s(\log B+\log|T^{\prime}|))\leq 0\},

where
T^{\prime}
denotes the derivative of the Gauss map
T
, and
\mathsf{P}
represents the pressure function defined in the Subection
2.3
.

Recently, Kleinbock-Wadleigh
[
13
]
observed that the growth of the product of consecutive partial quotients gives a characterisation for a real number to be
\Phi
-Dirichlet improvable or not. In fact, in
[
13
]
the Lebesgue measure of the set of
\Phi
-Dirichlet non-improvable numbers

\mathcal{E}_{2}(\Phi):=\left\{x\in[0,1):a_{n}(x)a_{n+1}(x)\geq\Phi(n)\ {\rm for\ infinitely\ many}\ n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}

was established. Soon after that the Hausdorff dimension of this set (in a generalised form) was established in
[
9
,
10
]
.

Theorem 1.2

(
[
9
, Huang-Wu-Xu]
)
.

Let
\Phi:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{[}1,\infty)
. Suppose

\log B=\liminf\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\log\Phi(n)}{n}\ {and}\ \log b=\liminf\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\log\log\Phi(n)}{n}.

(i)

When
B=1
,
\dim_{H}\mathcal{E}_{2}(\Phi)=1.

(ii)

When
B=\infty
,
\dim_{H}\mathcal{E}_{2}(\Phi)=1/(1+b).

(iii)

When
1<B<\infty
,
\dim_{H}\mathcal{E}_{2}(\Phi)=s_{0}:=\inf\{s\geq 0:\mathsf{P}(T,-s^{2}\log B-s\log|T^{\prime}|))\leq 0\}.

Note that the set
\mathcal{E}_{1}(\Phi)
is properly contained in
\mathcal{E}_{2}(\Phi)
raising a natural question of the size of the set

\mathcal{F}(\Phi):=~\mathcal{E}_{2}(\Phi)\setminus\mathcal{E}_{1}(\Phi)=\left\{x\in[0,1):\begin{array}[]{r}a_{n+1}(x)a_{n}(x)\geq\Phi(n)\text{ for infinitely many }n\in\mathbb{N}\text{ and}\\
a_{n+1}(x)<\Phi(n)\text{ for all sufficiently large }n\in\mathbb{N}\end{array}\right\}.

The Hausdorff dimension of
\mathcal{F}(\Phi)
was established in
[
1
]
.

Theorem 1.3

(
[
1
, Bakhtawar-Bos-Hussain]
)
.

Let
\Phi:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{[}1,\infty)
. Suppose

\log B=\liminf\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\log\Phi(n)}{n}\ {\rm and}\ \log b=\liminf\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\log\log\Phi(n)}{n}.

Then

\dim_{H}\mathcal{F}(\Phi)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}s_{0}&\mathrm{if}\ \ 1<B<\infty;\\[12.91663pt]
\frac{1}{1+b}&\mathrm{if}\ \ B=\infty,\end{array}\right.

where
s_{0}
is from Theorem
1.2
.

Note that the Hausdorff dimension proved in Theorem
1.3
is the same as in Theorem
1.2
, that is,
\dim_{H}\mathcal{F}(\Phi)=\dim_{H}\mathcal{E}_{2}(\Phi)
. The main reason that the subset
\mathcal{F}(\Phi)
has the same dimension as the superset
\mathcal{E}_{2}(\Phi)
, is that the set
\mathcal{E}_{2}(\Phi)
is much larger than
\mathcal{E}_{1}(\Phi)
. However, if we consider different approximation functions then the problem becomes more intricate and challenging. The complications arises in constructing a suitable Cantor type subset supporting a probability measure. To overcome this difficulty we introduce two types of probability measures – an idea that we believe can lend itself in resolving problems of similar nature. Indeed our results below highlight situations when
\mathcal{F}(\Phi)
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}(\Phi)
have different dimensions for different
\Phi
. To be precise, define the set

\mathcal{F}(\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\mathcal{E}_{2}(\Phi_{1})\backslash\mathcal{E}_{1}(\Phi_{2})=\left\{x\in[0,1):\begin{split}a_{n}(x)a_{n+1}(x)&\geq\Phi_{1}(n)\text{\,\, for infinitely many }n\in\mathbb{N}\\
a_{n+1}(x)&<\Phi_{2}(n)\text{\,\, for all sufficiently large }n\in\mathbb{N}\end{split}\right\}.

We fully characterise the Hausdorff dimension of
\mathcal{F}(\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2})
as well as prove some more results regarding general sets, in some way connected to
\mathcal{F}(\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2})
, which are also of their own independent interest.

It is worth noting that when
\Phi
is a function of the denominator of the
n^{\rm th}
convergent
q_{n}
, the Hausdorff dimension (and measure) study is carried out in a sequence of papers

[
2
,
4
,
14
]
. In particular, these papers give insight into comparing the uniform Diophantine approximation (Dirichlet non-improvable numbers) to the asymptotic Diophantine approximation (Jarník set).

1.1.
Main results

The first result of this paper gives a full description of the Hausdorff dimension of
\mathcal{F}(\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2})
.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
\Phi_{1}
and
\Phi_{2}
be positive functions such that

\displaystyle\log B_{1}=\liminf\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log\Phi_{1}(n)}{n}

\displaystyle\text{ \,\,\,\,\,\, and \,\,\,\,\,}\log b_{1}=\liminf\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log\log\Phi_{1}(n)}{n}.

\displaystyle\log B_{2}=\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log\Phi_{2}(n)}{n}

\displaystyle\text{ \,\,\,\,\,\, and \,\,\,\,\,}\log b_{2}=\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log\log\Phi_{2}(n)}{n}.

Then

\dim_{H}\mathcal{F}(\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}s_{0}&{\rm if}\ \ B_{1}^{s_{0}}\leq B_{2}\ {\rm and}\ B_{1}\ {\rm is\ finite};\\[12.91663pt]
g_{B_{1},B_{2}}&{\rm if}\ \ B_{1}^{s_{0}}\geq B_{2}>B_{1}^{1/2};\\[12.91663pt]
{\diameter}&{\rm if}\ \ B_{1}^{1/2}>B_{2}\ {\rm and}\ B_{2}\ {\rm is\ finite};\\[12.91663pt]
\frac{1}{1+b_{1}}&{\rm if}\ \ B_{1}=B_{2}=\infty,\ 1\leq b_{1}<b_{2},\ {\rm and}\ b_{1}\ {\rm is\ finite};\\[12.91663pt]
{\diameter}&{\rm if}\ \ B_{1}=B_{2}=\infty,\ b_{1}>b_{2}\geq 1,\ {\rm and}\ b_{2}\ {\rm is\ finite};\\[12.91663pt]
{\diameter}\text{\,\, or\,\, }0&{\rm if}\ \ B_{1}=B_{2}=\infty,\ b_{1}=b_{2}=\infty,\par\end{array}\right.

where
s_{0}
is from Theorem
1.1
and

g_{B_{1},B_{2}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\inf\{s\geq 0:P(T,-s\log|T^{\prime}|-s\log B_{1}+(1-s)\log B_{2})\leq 0\}.

The reason behind the slight variation in the definitions of

and

compared to

and

respectively, is in the nature of the conditions of our set. To get the proper classification, we need to bound

from both sides for all

is sufficient. Reader might also notice that this theorem does not cover two cases, namely

Preliminaries and auxiliary results

For completeness we give a brief introduction to Hausdorff measures and dimension. For further details we refer to the beautiful texts

,

]
.

2.1.
Hausdorff measure and dimension

Let
0<s\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
let

E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Then, for any
\rho>0
a countable collection
\{B_{i}\}
of balls in

\mathbb{R}^{n}
with diameters
\mathrm{diam}(B_{i})\leq\rho
such that

E\subset\bigcup_{i}B_{i}
is called a
\rho
-cover of
E
.
Let

\mathcal{H}_{\rho}^{s}(E)=\inf\sum_{i}\mathrm{diam}(B_{i})^{s},

where the infimum is taken over all possible
\rho
-covers
\{B_{i}\}
of
E
. It is easy to see that
\mathcal{H}_{\rho}^{s}(E)
increases as
\rho
decreases and so approaches a limit as
\rho\rightarrow 0
. This limit could be zero or infinity, or take a finite positive value. Accordingly, the
s
-Hausdorff measure
\mathcal{H}^{s}
of
E
is defined to be

\mathcal{H}^{s}(E)=\lim_{\rho\to 0}\mathcal{H}_{\rho}^{s}(E).

It is easy to verify that Hausdorff measure is monotonic and countably sub-additive, and that
\mathcal{H}^{s}(\varnothing)=0
. Thus it is an outer measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

For any subset
E
one can verify that there exists a unique critical value of
s
at which
\mathcal{H}^{s}(E)
‘jumps’ from infinity to zero. The value taken by
s
at this discontinuity is referred to as the
Hausdorff dimension of
E
and is denoted by
\dim_{\mathrm{H}}E
; i.e.,

\dim_{\mathrm{H}}E:=\inf\{s\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\;:\;\mathcal{H}^{s}(E)=0\}.

When
s=n
,
\mathcal{H}^{n}
coincides with standard Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Computing Hausdorff dimension of a set is typically accomplished in two steps: obtaining the upper and lower bounds separately.
Upper bounds often can be handled by finding appropriate coverings. When dealing with a limsup set, one usually applies the Hausdorff measure version of the famous Borel–Cantelli lemma (see Lemma 3.10 of
[
3
]
):

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\{B_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
be a sequence of measurable sets in
\mathbb{R}
and suppose that,

\sum_{i}\mathrm{diam}(B_{i})^{s}\,<\,\infty.

Then

\mathcal{H}^{s}({\limsup_{i\to\infty}B_{i}})=0.

The main tool in establishing the lower bound for the dimension of
E(A_{1},A_{2})
will be the following well-known mass distribution principle
[
5
]
.

Proposition 2.2

(Mass Distribution Principle
[
5
]
)
.

Let
\mu
be a probability measure supported on a measurable set
F
. Suppose there are positive constants
c
and
r_{0}
such that

\mu(B(x,r))\leq cr^{s}

for any ball
B(x,r)
with radius
r\leq r_{0}
and center
x\in F
. Then
\dim_{\mathrm{H}}F\geq s
.

2.2.
Continued fractions and Diophantine approximation

Recall that the Gauss map
T:[0,1)\to[0,1)
is defined by

T(0)=0,~T(x)=\frac{1}{x}-\left\lfloor\frac{1}{x}\right\rfloor\textmd{ for }x\in(0,1),

where
\lfloor x\rfloor
denotes the integer part of
x
.
We write
x:=[a_{1}(x),a_{2}(x),a_{3}(x),\ldots]
for the continued fraction of
x
where
a_{1}(x)=\lfloor 1/x\rfloor
,
a_{n}(x)=a_{1}(T^{n-1}(x))
for
n\geq 2
are called the partial quotients of
x
.
The sequences
p_{n}=p_{n}(x)
,
q_{n}=q_{n}(x)
, referred to as
n^{\rm th}
convergents, has the recursive relation

(2.1)

p_{n+1}=a_{n+1}(x)p_{n}+p_{n-1},\ \ q_{n+1}=a_{n+1}(x)q_{n}+q_{n-1},\ \ n\geq 0.

Thus
p_{n}=p_{n}(x),q_{n}=q_{n}(x)
are determined by the partial quotients
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
, so we may write
p_{n}=p_{n}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n}),q_{n}=q_{n}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
. When it is clear which partial quotients are involved, we denote them by
p_{n},q_{n}
for simplicity.

For any integer vector
(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
with
n\geq 1
, write

I_{n}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n}):=\left\{x\in[0,1):a_{1}(x)=a_{1},\dots,a_{n}(x)=a_{n}\right\}

for the corresponding ‘cylinder of order
n
’, i.e. the set of all real numbers in
[0,1)
whose continued fraction expansions begin with
(a_{1},\dots,a_{n}).

We will frequently use the following well known properties of continued fraction expansions. They are explained in the standard texts
[
11
,
12
]
.

Proposition 2.3
.

For any positive integers
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
, let
p_{n}=p_{n}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
and
q_{n}=q_{n}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
be defined recursively by (
2.1
). Then:

(
\rm P_{1}
)

\displaystyle I_{n}(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\left[\frac{p_{n}}{q_{n}},\frac{p_{n}+p_{n-1}}{q_{n}+q_{n-1}}\right)&{\rm if}\ \ n\ {\rm{is\ even}};\\
\left(\frac{p_{n}+p_{n-1}}{q_{n}+q_{n-1}},\frac{p_{n}}{q_{n}}\right]&{\rm if}\ \ n\ {\rm{is\ odd}}.\end{array}\right.

Thus, its length is given by

\frac{1}{2q_{n}^{2}}\leq|I_{n}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})|=\frac{1}{q_{n}(q_{n}+q_{n-1})}\leq\frac{1}{q_{n}^{2}},

since

p_{n-1}q_{n}-p_{n}q_{n-1}=(-1)^{n},\ {\rm for\ all}\ n\geq 1.

(
\rm P_{2}
)

For any
n\geq 1
,
q_{n}\geq 2^{(n-1)/2}
and

1\leq\frac{q_{n+m}(a_{1},\cdots,a_{n},b_{1},\cdots,b_{m})}{q_{n}(a_{1},\cdots,a_{n})\cdot q_{m}(b_{1},\cdots,b_{m})}\leq 2.

(
\rm P_{3}
)

\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\leq q_{n}\leq\prod_{i=1}^{n}(a_{i}+1)\leq 2^{n}\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}.

(
\rm P_{4}
)

\frac{1}{3a_{n+1}(x)q^{2}_{n}(x)}\,<\,\Big|x-\frac{p_{n}(x)}{q_{n}(x)}\Big|=\frac{1}{q_{n}(x)(q_{n+1}(x)+T^{n+1}(x)q_{n}(x))}\,<\,\frac{1}{a_{n+1}q^{2}_{n}(x)}.

(
\rm P_{5}
)

there exists a constant
K>1
such that for almost all
x\in[0,1)
,

q_{n}(x)\leq K^{n},\ {\text{for all $n$ sufficiently large}}.

Let
\mu
be the Gauss measure given by

d\mu=\frac{1}{(1+x)\log 2}dx.

It is clear that
\mu
is
T
-invariant and equivalent to Lebesgue measure
\mathcal{L}
.

The next proposition concerns the position of a cylinder in
[0,1)
.

Proposition 2.4

(
[
12
]
, Khintchine)
.

Let
I_{n}=I_{n}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
be a cylinder of order
n
, which is partitioned into sub-cylinders
\{I_{n+1}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n},a_{n+1}):a_{n+1}\in\mathbb{N}\}
. When
n
is odd, these sub-cylinders are positioned from left to right, as
a_{n+1}
increases from 1 to
\infty
; when
n
is even, they are positioned from right to left.

The following result is due to Łuczak
[
16
]
.

Lemma 2.5

(
[
16
]
, Łuczak)
.

For any
b,c>1
, the sets

\displaystyle\left\{x\in[0,1):a_{n}(x)\geq c^{b^{n}}\ {\text{for infinitely many}}\ n\in\mathbb{N}\right\},

\displaystyle\left\{x\in[0,1):a_{n}(x)\geq c^{b^{n}}\ {\text{for all }}\ n\geq 1\right\},

have the same Hausdorff dimension
\frac{1}{b+1}
.

We will also need the following lemma from

Lemma 2.6

(

6

Let

be a sequence of positive integers tending to infinity with

2.3.
Pressure function

When dealing with dimension problems in non-linear dynamical system, pressure function and other concepts from thermodynamics are good tools. The concept of a general pressure function was introduced by Walters
[
22
]
as a generalization of entropy. It describes exponential growth rate of ergodic sum which determine the whole system in some sense. We are interested in a way of calculating Hausdorff dimensions using pressure functions.

A method in
[
21
, Theorem 2.2.1]
can be used to calculate the Hausdorff dimension of self-similar sets for linear system. As for the non-linear setting, the relation between Hausdorff dimension and pressure functions is given in
[
21
]
as the corresponding generalization of Moran
[
20
]
. The main required ingredient is the following

Definition 2.7
.

Given any continuous function
f:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
we define its
pressure

\mathsf{P}\left(f\right)
, with respect to
T
, as

\mathsf{P}\left(f\right):=\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\lim\sup}\frac{1}{n}\log{{\left(\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}T^{n}x=x\\
x\in X\end{subarray}}e^{f\left(x\right)+f\left(Tx\right)+\cdots+f\left(T^{n-1}x\right)}\right)}}

where the summation is over all periodic points.

We note that we can write
``\lim"
instead of
``\limsup"
since the above limit actually exists. Combined this with dimension theory, we are concerned with a family of functions
f_{t}\left(x\right)=-t\log\left|T^{\prime}\left(x\right)\right|
,
x\in X
and
0\leq t\leq d
. Substituting these functions into the above definition leads to a real-valued map

\begin{array}[]{l}\left[0,d\right]\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\qquad t\mapsto\mathsf{P}\left(f_{t}\right)=\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\lim\sup}\frac{1}{n}\log\left(\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}T^{n}x=x\\
x\in X\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\left|\left(T^{n}\right)^{\prime}\left(x\right)\right|^{t}}\right).\end{array}

We observe that a function
t\mapsto\mathsf{P}\left(f_{t}\right)
satisfies interesting properties:

(i)

\mathsf{P}\left(0\right)=\log k
;

(ii)

t\mapsto\mathsf{P}\left(f_{t}\right)
is strictly
decreasing;

(iii)

t\mapsto\mathsf{P}\left(f_{t}\right)
is analytic on
\left[0,d\right]
.

Proof can be found on page 32 of
[
21
]
. Moreover, property (i) is easily obtained by the definition.

Bowen and Ruelle had given key results for calculating the Hausdorff dimension from the pressure function. Bowen
showed the result in the context of quasi-circles, and Bowen-Ruelle developed the
method for the case of hyperbolic Julia sets. More precisely,

Theorem 2.8

(Bowen-Ruelle)
.

Let
T:X\rightarrow X
be a
C^{1+\alpha}

conformal expanding map, for some
\alpha>0
. There is a unique solution
0\leq s\leq d
to

\mathsf{P}\left(-s\log\left|T^{\prime}\right|\right)=0,

which occurs precisely at
s=\dim_{H}X
.

More details and context on pressure functions can be found in
[
17
,
18
,
19
]
. We use the fact that the pressure function with a continuous potential can be approximated by the pressure function restricted to the sub-systems in continued fractions.

Let
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{N}
be a finite or infinite set
and define

X_{\mathcal{A}}=\{x\in[0,1):{\text{for all}}\ n\geq 1,a_{n}(x)\in\mathcal{A}\}.

Then
(X_{\mathcal{A}},T)
is a subsystem of
([0,1),T)
where
T
is a
Gauss map. Given any real function
\psi:[0,1)\rightarrow\mathbb{R},
the pressure function restricted to
the system

(2.3)

\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{A}}(T,\psi):=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\sum_{a_{1},\cdots,a_{n}\in\mathcal{A}}\sup_{x\in X_{\mathcal{A}}}e^{S_{n}\psi([a_{1},\cdots,a_{n}+x])},

where
S_{n}\psi(x)
denotes the ergodic sum
\psi(x)+\cdots+\psi(T^{n-1}x)
. For simplicity, we denote
\mathsf{P}_{\mathbb{N}}(T,\psi)
by

when

. We note that the supremum in equation (
2.3

For each

variation of

{\mathop{\rm{Var}}}_{n}(\psi):=\sup\Big\{|\psi(x)-\psi(y)|:I_{n}(x)=I_{n}(y)\Big\}.

The following results
[
15
, Proposition 2.4]
ensure the existence of the limit in the equation (
2.3
).

Proposition 2.9

(
[
15
]
, Li-Wang-Wu-Xu)
.

Let
\psi:[0,1)\to\mathbb{R}
be a real function with

\mathrm{Var}_{n}(\psi)\to 0

\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{A}}(T,\psi)

The system
([0,1),T)

Proposition 2.10

(
[

]

Let

and
\mathrm{Var}_{n}(\psi)\to 0
as

. We have

\mathsf{P}_{\mathbb{N}}(T,\psi)=\sup\{\mathsf{P}_{\mathcal{A}}(T,\psi):\mathcal{A}\ \mathrm{is\ a\ finite\ subset\ of}\ \mathbb{N}\}.

Now we consider the specific potentials,

\displaystyle\psi_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=-s(\log B+\log|T^{\prime}(x)|)

\displaystyle\psi_{2}(x)

As before, we obtain the pressure function with potential

3.
Hausdorff dimension of
E(A_{1},A_{2})
.

The proof of Theorem
1.7
consists of two parts, the upper bound and the lower bound.
For notational simplicity, we take
c_{1}=c_{2}=1
and the other case can be done with obvious modifications. That is we will deal with the set

E(A_{1},A_{2})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\left\{x\in[0,1):\,A_{1}^{n}\leq a_{n}(x)<2A_{1}^{n},\,\,A_{2}^{n}\leq a_{n+1}(x)<2A_{2}^{n},\,\,\text{for i.m. }n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}.

3.1.
Upper bound.

It is known that the covering of an upper limit set is natural, so the natural covering of
E(A_{1},A_{2})
is found routinely as follows.

\displaystyle E(A_{1})

\displaystyle=\bigcap\limits_{N=1}^{\infty}\bigcup\limits_{n=N}^{\infty}\left\{x\in[0,1):\,A_{1}^{n}\leq a_{n}(x)<2A_{1}^{n},\,\,A_{2}^{n}\leq a_{n+1}(x)<2A_{2}^{n}\right\}

\displaystyle\subset\bigcup\limits_{n=N}^{\infty}\left\{x\in[0,1):\,A_{1}^{n}\leq a_{n}(x)<2A_{1}^{n},\,\,A_{2}^{n}\leq a_{n+1}(x)<2A_{2}^{n}\right\}:=\bigcup\limits_{n=N}^{\infty}E_{n}

\displaystyle=\bigcup\limits_{n=N}^{\infty}\bigcup\limits_{a_{1},\ldots,a_{n-1}\in\mathbb{N}}\left\{x\in[0,1)\,:\,a_{i}(x)=a_{i},1\leq i<n,\ A_{1}^{n}\leq a_{n}(x)<2A_{1}^{n},\ A_{2}^{n}\leq a_{n+1}(x)<2A_{2}^{n}\right\}.

There are two potential optimal covers for
E_{n}
for each
n\geq N
.
For any integers
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n-1}\in\mathbb{N}
, define

J_{n-1}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n-1})=\bigcup\limits_{A_{1}^{n}\leq a_{n}<2A_{1}^{n}}I_{n}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}).

That is one type of suitable coverings of
E(A_{1})
, which is specifically represented as

E(A_{1})\subset\bigcup\limits_{n=N}^{\infty}\bigcup\limits_{a_{1},\ldots,a_{n-1}\in\mathbb{N}}J_{n-1}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n-1}).

Then, by using Proposition
2.3
and Proposition
2.4
recursively, we obtain

|J_{n-1}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n-1})|=\sum\limits_{A_{1}^{n}\leq a_{n}<2A_{1}^{n}}\left|\frac{p_{n}}{q_{n}}-\frac{p_{n}+p_{n-1}}{q_{n}+q_{n-1}}\right|\asymp\frac{1}{A_{1}^{n}q_{n-1}^{2}}.

Therefore, for any
\varepsilon>0
, an
(s_{A_{1}}+2\varepsilon)
-dimensional Hausdorff dimension of
E(A_{1},A_{2})
can be estimated as

\begin{split}\mathcal{H}^{s_{A_{1}}+2\varepsilon}(E(A_{1},A_{2}))&\leq\liminf_{N\to\infty}\sum\limits_{n=N}^{\infty}\sum\limits_{a_{1},\ldots,a_{n-1}}|J_{n-1}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n-1})|^{s_{A_{1}}+2\varepsilon}\\
&\leq\liminf_{N\to\infty}\sum\limits_{n=N}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{(n-1)\varepsilon}}\sum\limits_{a_{1},\ldots,a_{n-1}}\left(\frac{1}{A_{1}^{n}q_{n-1}^{2}}\right)^{s_{A_{1}}}\\
&\leq\liminf_{N\to\infty}\sum\limits_{n=N}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{(n-1)\varepsilon}}<\infty.\end{split}

Hence, from the definition of Hausdorff dimension, it follows that

\dim_{H}E(A_{1},A_{2})\leq s_{A_{1}}.

As for another covering type of
E(A_{1})
, similar with the former, for any integers
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n-1}\in\mathbb{N}
and
A_{1}^{n}\leq a_{n}(x)<2A_{1}^{n}
, define

J_{n}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})=\bigcup\limits_{A_{2}^{n}\leq a_{n+1}<2A_{2}^{n}}I_{n+1}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n+1})

Then

|J_{n}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})|\asymp\frac{1}{A_{2}^{n}q_{n}^{2}},

and

E(A_{1})\subset\bigcup_{n=N}^{\infty}\bigcup\limits_{a_{1},\ldots,a_{n-1}}\bigcup\limits_{A_{1}^{n}\leq a_{n}<2A_{1}^{n}}J_{n}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}).

Therefore, for any
\varepsilon>0
, a
(g_{(A_{1}A_{2}),A_{1}}+2\varepsilon)
-dimensional Hausdorff dimension of
E(A_{1},A_{2})
can be estimated as

\begin{split}\mathcal{H}^{g_{(A_{1}A_{2}),A_{1}}+2\varepsilon}(E(A_{1},A_{2}))&\leq\liminf_{N\to\infty}\sum\limits_{n=N}^{\infty}\sum\limits_{a_{1},\ldots,a_{n-1}}\sum\limits_{A_{1}^{n}\leq a_{n}<2A_{1}^{n}}|J_{n}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})|^{g_{(A_{1}A_{2}),A_{1}}+2\varepsilon}\\
&\leq\liminf_{N\to\infty}\sum\limits_{n=N}^{\infty}\sum\limits_{a_{1},\ldots,a_{n-1}}\sum\limits_{A_{1}^{n}\leq a_{n}<2A_{1}^{n}}\left(\frac{1}{A_{2}^{n}q_{n}^{2}}\right)^{g_{(A_{1}A_{2}),A_{1}}+2\varepsilon}\\
&\leq\liminf_{N\to\infty}\sum\limits_{n=N}^{\infty}\sum\limits_{a_{1},\ldots,a_{n-1}}A_{1}^{n}\left(\frac{1}{(A_{2}A_{1}^{2})^{n}q_{n-1}^{2}}\right)^{g_{(A_{1}A_{2}),A_{1}}+2\varepsilon}\\
&\leq\liminf_{N\to\infty}\sum\limits_{n=N}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{(n-1)\varepsilon}}<\infty.\end{split}

The third inequality holds by recursive relation (
2.1
). Thus, the upper bound is obtained immediately by

\dim_{H}E(A_{1},A_{2})\leq g_{(A_{1}A_{2}),A_{1}}.

Hence,

\dim_{H}E(A_{1},A_{2})\leq\min\left\{s_{A_{1}},g_{(A_{1}A_{2}),A_{1}}\right\}.

\displaystyle E=\{x\in[0,1):\,A_{1}^{n_{k}}\leq a_{n_{k}}(x)<2A_{1}^{n_{k}},\

\dim_{H}\mathcal{F}_{B_{1},B_{2}}\geq g_{B_{1},B_{2}}.

Hence, together with the upper bound, when

.

4.3.
Case

In this case let us investigate the conditions on partial quotients in the definition of

\mathcal{F}(\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2})\supset\left\{x\in[0,1):\begin{split}a_{n_{j}}(x)a_{n_{j}+1}(x)&\geq\widetilde{B_{1}}^{n_{j}}&\text{for all }j\in\mathbb{N}\\

) we required that

.

\displaystyle\widetilde{E}=\Bigg\{x\in[0,1):\,

(2)

For the lower bound, we can completely repeat the argument from the lower bound in last case, but for a slightly different set, namely

\displaystyle\widetilde{E}=\Bigg\{x\in[0,1):

\displaystyle\widetilde{B_{2}}^{n_{j}}c_{1}\leq a_{n_{j}}(x)<2c_{1}\widetilde{B_{2}}^{n_{j}},c_{2}\left(\frac{\widetilde{B_{1}}}{\widetilde{B_{2}}}\right)^{n_{j}}\leq a_{n_{j}+1}(x)<2c_{2}\left(\frac{\widetilde{B_{1}}}{\widetilde{B_{2}}}\right)^{n_{j}},

\displaystyle a_{n}(x)=2\text{\,\, for all }y_{j}<n<n_{j},j\geq 1,\text{\,\, and }1\leq a_{n}(x)\leq M\text{\,\, for other }n\in\mathbb{N}\Bigg\}.

By the continuity of

\dim_{\mathrm{H}}\mathcal{F}(\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2})=g_{B_{1},B_{2}}

(3)

B_{1}^{1/2}>B_{2}
and
B_{2}
is finite.

If

, then obviously our set is empty. Hence we can assume that
B_{1}<\infty
. By definition of
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
we know that
\Phi_{1}(n)\geq(B_{1}-\varepsilon_{1})^{n}
for

large enough with any

and
\Phi_{2}(n)\leq(B_{2}+\varepsilon_{2})^{n}
for

.

By conditions of our set we know that

a_{n+1}<\Phi_{2}(n)\leq(B_{2}+\varepsilon_{2})^{n}\text{\,\,\, and \,\,\,}a_{n}<\Phi_{2}(n)\leq(B_{2}+\varepsilon_{2})^{n-1}.

Combining these together, we have that
a_{n}a_{n+1}<(B_{2}+\varepsilon_{2})^{2n-1}
. On the other hand, the second condition of our set is

a_{n+1}a_{n}\geq\Phi_{1}(n)\geq(B_{1}-\varepsilon_{1})^{n}.

In our case we know that
B_{1}>B_{2}^{2}
, or we can rewrite it as
B_{1}=B^{2}_{2}+\delta
for some fixed
\delta>0
. Let
\delta^{\prime}=\sqrt{B_{2}^{2}+\delta/2}-B_{2}
and take
\varepsilon_{1}<\delta/2
and
\varepsilon_{2}<\delta^{\prime}
. Now we easily come to a contradiction between conditions, since

(B_{1}-\varepsilon_{1})^{n}=(B_{2}^{2}+\delta-\varepsilon_{1})^{n}>(B_{2}^{2}+\delta/2)^{n}=(B_{2}+\delta^{\prime})^{2n}>(B_{2}+\varepsilon_{2})^{2n-1},

(4)

B_{1}=B_{2}=\infty
,
1\leq b_{1}<b_{2}
and
b_{1}
is finite.

First, let us assume that
b_{1}=1
. Note that
B=\infty
, therefore for any

for all sufficiently large

. Thus one can see that

\mathcal{F}(\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2})\subset\{x\in[0,1):a_{n}(x)\geq C^{n}\text{ \,\, i.m. }n\in\mathbb{N}\}.

Then by Theorem
1.1
we have

\dim_{\mathrm{H}}\mathcal{F}(\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2})\leq\lim_{C\to\infty}s_{C}=\frac{1}{2}.

Now for the lower bound notice that
b_{2}=1+\delta
for some fixed
\delta>0
and that for every

one has

\Phi_{1}(n)\leq e^{(1+\epsilon)^{n}}\text{\,\, and \,\, }\Phi_{2}(n)\geq e^{(1+\delta-\epsilon)^{n}}.

Now it is obvious that

\mathcal{F}(\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2})\supset\left\{x\in[0,1):\begin{split}a_{n}(x)a_{n+1}(x)&\geq e^{(1+\epsilon)^{n}}&\text{for i.m. }n\in\mathbb{N}\\
a_{n+1}(x)&<e^{(1+\delta-\epsilon)^{n}}&\text{for all sufficiently large }n\in\mathbb{N}\end{split}\right\}.

At this point we will make use of Lemma
2.6
. Set
s_{n}=\frac{1}{2}e^{(1+\epsilon)^{n}}
. One can easily check that

Applying Lemma
2.6

\displaystyle\dim_{\mathrm{H}}\mathcal{F}(\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2})

\displaystyle\geq\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\left(\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log(s_{1}s_{2}\cdots s_{n})}{2\log(s_{1}s_{2}\cdots s_{n})+\log s_{n+1}}\right)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\left(\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1-\frac{1}{(1+\epsilon)^{n}}}{2+\epsilon-\frac{2}{(1+\epsilon)^{n}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{2+\epsilon}=\frac{1}{2}.

. By the definition of

, for any

that is
\Phi_{1}(n)\leq e^{(b_{1}+\epsilon)^{n}}
for infinitely many

, whereas
\Phi_{1}(n)\geq e^{(b_{1}-\epsilon)^{n}}
for all sufficiently large

.
Note that

\mathcal{F}(\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2})\subset\left\{x\in[0,1):a_{n}(x)\geq e^{\frac{1}{2(b_{1}-\epsilon)}(b_{1}-\epsilon)^{n}}\text{ \,\, for i.m. }n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}.

Therefore, by using Lemma
2.5

Now for the lower bound we use Lemma
2.6

. One can easily check that

U_{4}=\{x\in[0,1):s_{n}\leq a_{n+1}(x)<2s_{n}\,\,\,\forall n\in\mathbb{N}\}

is a subset of our original set. Applying Lemma
2.6
to this choice of the sequence
s_{n}
, we get

\displaystyle\dim_{\mathrm{H}}\mathcal{F}(\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2})

\displaystyle\geq\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\left(\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log(s_{1}s_{2}\cdots s_{n})}{2\log(s_{1}s_{2}\cdots s_{n})+\log s_{n+1}}\right)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\left(\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1-\frac{1}{(b_{1}+\epsilon)^{n}}}{b_{1}+\epsilon+1-\frac{2}{(b_{1}+\epsilon)^{n}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{1+b_{1}+\epsilon}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+b_{1}}.

(5)

if
B_{1}=B_{2}=\infty
,
b_{1}>b_{2}\geq 1
and
b_{2}
is finite.

We want to show that in this case our set is empty. Let us first assume that

is finite. Then

Combining these together, we have that

On the other hand, the second condition of our set is that for every

We can take
\epsilon_{1}

\displaystyle>2(b_{2}+\epsilon_{2})^{n}>(b_{2}+\epsilon_{2})^{n-1}+(b_{2}+\epsilon_{2})^{n}>\log a_{n}a_{n+1}.

This clearly gives us a contradiction for
n

a_{n}a_{n+1}\geq\Phi_{1}(n)\geq e^{(b_{1}^{\prime}-\epsilon_{1})^{n}},

(6)

,

then the set

6.1.
Proof of Proposition
1.5

Note that this proposition concerns the case
B_{1}=B_{2}^{2}
. Recall that

E_{1}=\left\{x\in[0,1):\begin{split}a_{n}(x)a_{n+1}(x)&\geq B_{2}^{2n}\text{\,\, for infinitely many }n\in\mathbb{N}\\
a_{n+1}(x)&<B_{2}^{n}\text{\,\, for all sufficiently large }n\in\mathbb{N}\end{split}\right\}.

Then simply by Theorem
1.8
, this set is empty.

Our second set is

E_{2}=\left\{x\in[0,1):\begin{split}a_{n}(x)a_{n+1}(x)&\geq B_{2}^{2n-1}\text{\,\, for infinitely many }n\in\mathbb{N}\\
a_{n+1}(x)&<3B_{2}^{n+1}\text{\,\, for all sufficiently large }n\in\mathbb{N}\end{split}\right\}.

To get the lower bound on the Hausdorff dimension, we take the subset

\displaystyle F=\{x\in[0,1)\,:\,B_{2}^{n}\leq a_{n}(x)<2B_{2}^{n},

\displaystyle\ B_{2}^{n}\leq a_{n+1}(x)<2B_{2}^{n},\,\,\text{i.m. }n\in\mathbb{N};

\displaystyle\text{ and }1\leq a_{n}(x)\leq M\text{\,\, for all other\,\,}n\in\mathbb{N}\}.

It is indeed a subset of
E_{2}
, because for the first condition of
E_{2}
we have

a_{n}(x)a_{n+1}(x)\geq B_{2}^{n}B_{2}^{n}=B_{2}^{2n}>B_{2}^{2n-1},

and for the second one we have

a_{n+1}(x)<2B_{2}^{n}<3B_{2}^{n+1},

a_{n}(x)<2B_{2}^{n}<3B_{2}^{n}.

We dealt with set
F
in Theorem
1.7
, which tells us that its dimension is
g_{B_{1},B_{2}}
. So, we have that

\dim_{\mathrm{H}}E_{2}\geq g_{B_{1},B_{2}}.

The upper bound of dimension for the set
E_{2}
is clear by the result for the case
B_{1}^{1/2}<B_{2}
in Theorem

, since
E_{2}
is becoming larger when we decrease the value of
B_{1}
.
And so
\dim_{\mathrm{H}}E_{2}=g_{B_{1},B_{2}}.

6.2.
Proof of Proposition
1.6

Note that this proposition concerns the case
B_{1}=B_{2}=\infty
,
b_{1}=b_{2}<\infty
. The first set in consideration is

P_{1}=\left\{x\in[0,1):\begin{split}a_{n}(x)a_{n+1}(x)&\geq e^{b_{1}^{n}}\text{\,\, for infinitely many }n\in\mathbb{N}\\
a_{n+1}(x)&<e^{b_{1}^{n}}\text{\,\, for all sufficiently large }n\in\mathbb{N}\end{split}\right\}.

This set is a particular case of the set
\mathcal{F}(\Phi)
from Theorem
1.3
and its Hausdorff dimension by this theorem is equal to
\frac{1}{b_{1}+1}
.

The second set is

P_{2}=\left\{x\in[0,1):\begin{split}a_{n}(x)a_{n+1}(x)&\geq e^{5b_{1}^{n}}\text{\,\, for infinitely many }n\in\mathbb{N}\\
a_{n+1}(x)&<e^{b_{1}^{n-1}}\text{\,\, for all sufficiently large }n\in\mathbb{N}\end{split}\right\}.

This set is empty, because the conditions are incompatible. Indeed, on the one hand we have

a_{n}(x)a_{n+1}(x)<e^{b_{1}^{n-1}+b_{1}^{n-2}}.

On the other hand,

a_{n}(x)a_{n+1}(x)\geq e^{5b_{1}^{n}}.

Combining this together, we get
5b_{1}^{2}<b_{1}+1
, which can happen only when
b_{1}
is less than 1. But we know that
b_{1}\geq 1
, hence the set
P_{2}
is empty.
