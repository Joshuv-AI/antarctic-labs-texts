Multipass Quantum Process Tomography: Precision and Accuracy Enhancement
Stancho G. Stanchev and Nikolay V. Vitanov

Abstract
We introduce a method to enhance the precision and accuracy of Quantum Process Tomography (QPT) by mitigating the errors caused by state preparation and measurement (SPAM), readout and shot noise. Instead of performing QPT solely on a single gate, we propose performing QPT on a sequence of multiple applications of the same gate. The method involves the measurement of the Pauli transfer matrix (PTM) by standard QPT of the multipass process, and then deduce the single-process PTM by two alternative approaches: an iterative approach which in theory delivers the exact result for small errors, and a linearized approach based on solving the Sylvester equation. We examine the efficiency of these two approaches through simulations on IBM Quantum using ibmq_qasm_simulator . Compared to the Randomized Benchmarking type of methods, the proposed method delivers the entire PTM rather than a single number (fidelity). Compared to standard QPT, our method delivers PTM with much higher accuracy and precision because it greatly reduces the SPAM, readout and shot noise errors. We use the proposed method to experimentally determine the PTM and the fidelity of the CNOT gate on the quantum processor ibmq_manila (Falcon r5.11L).



I
Introduction

Quantum Process Tomography (QPT) is a crucial technique in quantum information processing, which provides valuable insights into the behavior of quantum systems and their constituent quantum operations
NielsenChuang2012
;
ChuangNielsen1997
;
Poyatos1997
;
O'Brien2004
.
In the pursuit of large-scale quantum computers, it plays a vital role in characterizing the fidelity of quantum gates and quantum circuits
Riebe2006
;
Tinkey2021
;
Bialczak2010
.
However, QPT and in particular Standard QPT (SQPT), face significant challenges.
They typically require a large number of measurements in order to accurately characterize a quantum process.
As the size of the quantum system grows, the number of required measurements increases exponentially, making the process resource-intensive and time-consuming.
Moreover, in order to achieve high precision and accuracy, a huge number of measurement shots is required, with minimal SPAM and readout errors.

Recent advancements in both theory and practical applications have significantly improved the quality of quantum process tomography (QPT).
From the perspective of how the characterized process is applied within the circuit, prior to the measurement, we can categorize these techniques into two groups: those that employ single process applications and those that utilize multiple applications, often referred to as long-gate sequence, gate repetition, concatenation, multipass, etc.

The single-process application techniques include, for example, Standard QPT
NielsenChuang2012
;
ChuangNielsen1997
;
Poyatos1997
;
O'Brien2004
;
Fiurasek2001
, Ancilla-Assisted QPT (AAPT)
Altepeter2003
, Direct Characterization of Quantum Dynamics (DCQD)
Mohseni2008
;
Roncallo2024
, Compressed sensing QPT
Rodionov2014
, Gradient-Descent QPT
Ahmed2023
, Self-Consistent QPT
Merkel2013
;
Sugiyama2021
, Projected Least-Squares QPT
Surawy-Stepney2022
, etc. These methods, especially SQPT, are known to be relatively susceptibility to SPAM and readout errors.
The methods that greatly improve the precision of tomography are those that use gate repetitions.
The repetitions amplify the structured and randomized gate errors and reduce the effects of systematic and/or statistical measurement errors caused by SPAM, readout and shot noise.
The most widely adopted such protocols are Randomized Benchmarking (RB), Interleaved RB, and Gate Set Tomography (GST).
Some other examples are discussed in Refs.
Chow2012a
;
Kunjummen2023
;
Gulliksen2015
.

Randomized benchmarking
Knill2008
;
Magesan2011
;
Proctor2017
;
Magesan2012
;
Emerson2005
;
Erhard2019
;
Corcoles2013
;
Cross2019
entails executing prolonged sequences of random Clifford group gates that combine to create the identity, enabling the observation of performance degradation as the circuit depth increases.
However, RB serves as a protocol for characterizing the average performance.
It demonstrates efficient scalability with respect to the number of qubits affected by the characterized gate set and remains resilient in the presence of noise during state preparation and measurement.
Interleaved RB
Magesan2012a
is designed to characterize a particular gate, which is ‘interleaved’ throughout the standard RB sequence. The protocol is a simple extension of standard RB which involves running sequences of random gates.
In GST
Nielsen2021
;
Greenbaum2015
, the key distinction in comparison to older QPT methods is its calibration-free nature.
Unlike its predecessors, GST does not rely on pre-calibrated state preparations and measurements. However, obtaining a GST estimate entails solving a highly nonlinear optimization problem. Moreover, the scaling with system size is polynomially worse than QPT due to the necessity to characterize multiple gates simultaneously. In order to estimate a complete gate set, approximately 80 experiments are required for single-qubit gates and over 4000 for two-qubit gates.
This is far worse than QPT, which only requires 12 experiments for a single-qubit gate and 144 experiments for a two-qubit gate
Greenbaum2015
.
There are also methods, as the one discussed in
Chow2012b
that utilize gate application of a sequence of identical repetitions.

In this paper, we introduce a method called
Multipass Quantum Process Tomography
(MQPT ) that combines the methods based on repetitions with those that use single-gate application only.
Contrary to most repetition methods, our method uses only repetitions of
the same gate
.
The repetitions lead to the amplification of the single-pass errors in the
multipass
errors, which are then measured with high accuracy and precision by standard QPT.
There are two clear advantages of using only the same gate (see Fig.
1
): (i) we do not introduce additional errors stemming from other gates, and (ii) it is possible to design a simple and very fast post-processing procedure that derives the individual process from the multipass process.
Our method is distinguished by its intuitiveness and simplicity, as it uses the already developed single-pass techniques, such as SQPT, and reduced mathematical complexity, making it both theoretically accessible and practically feasible.
Yet, our method features significant accuracy and precision enhancement compared to single-pass SQPT and potentially to other single-pass methods.

II
Multipass Quantum Process Tomography (MQPT)

II.1
Motivation

As noted in Introduction, a sequence of repetitions is widely used in the process characterization due to the fact that the errors are amplified (accumulated) as the number of repetitions
N
increases.
These larger multipass errors are more easily measurable with high precision and accuracy than the small single-pass errors.
The single-pass errors can be deduced from the multipass signal provided that we have an unambiguous connection between the single-pass and multipass processes.
The latter requirement imposes some limitations on the formalism which can be used to describe the multipass quantum process.

In the unitary case, due to the absence of decoherence, the error amplification can even be quadratic
Vitanov2020
.
Then it is relatively easy to find the
N
th power of a given parameterized propagator, as has been done in several works
Vitanov1995
;
Stanchev2023
;
Stanchev2024
;
Vitanov2018
;
Baldwin2014
;
Zhou2015
.
It is also possible to find the single propagator from the
N
-pass propagator (its
N
th power), which makes it possible to accurately and precisely deduce the single-pass propagator errors from the amplified multi-pass errors.
If the propagator
U
is explicitly defined with respect to time during a time period from 0 to the gate time
T
, then exponentiating
U^{N}
can be easily obtained by scaling
T
to
NT
, for example, in the Mølmer-Sørensen gate
Sorensen2000
.

However, if the evolution is not unitary and it is instead represented by a Completely Positive and Trace-Preserving (CPTP) map, as it occurs when considering open quantum system noise, then finding the
N
th power of a process by parameterizing the single process becomes a challenging task.
For the sake of process tomography, we are very interested in the inverse task, i.e., finding the first power from the known
N
th power, which is also a daunting task.
The extraction of the single CPTP process from the amplified multi-pass process will be referred to as
Multipass Quantum Process Tomography
(MQPT).

In this paper, we will treat the terms “propagator”, “gate”, “process”, and “channel” as synonymous concepts, recognizing that they are often used interchangeably or may refer to similar aspects of quantum information and quantum computing.

II.2
Single process and distance metrics

II.2.1
Channel characteristics

Before delving into multipass processes, let us first describe the overall concept of a process.
A quantum process (channel) is a quantum operation represented by a CPTP map.
Various CPTP map representations ensure the physicality of quantum processes by preserving properties such as trace and positivity
Choi1972
;
Choi1975
;
Jamiolkowski1972
and adhere to Markovian dynamics
Lindblad1976
;
Gorini1976
.
Examples of such representations are the Choi matrix,
\chi
-Process matrix, Kraus (Operator sum representation)
Kraus1983
, Stinespring
Stinespring1955
, Liouville (Superoperator), Pauli Transfer matrix (PTM)
Merkel2013
;
Gambetta2013
, etc.
All of them are equivalent and can be converted into each other
Wood2015
.
In our analysis, we use PTM (
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}
) and Liouville (
\boldsymbol{{S}}
), because if a given process is repeated
N
times then the resultant multipass process is simply
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}^{N}
and
\boldsymbol{{S}}^{N}
, respectively.
These two representations govern the evolution of the vectorized density matrix
\ket{\rho}\!\rangle
.
The density matrix vectorization in Liouville representation is simply the column stacking of
\rho
, i.e.

|\rho\rangle\rangle:=\left(\rho_{11},\rho_{21},\ldots,\rho_{k1},\rho_{12},\rho_{22},\ldots,\rho_{k2},\rho_{kk}\right)^{T},

(1)

while the corresponding channel
\boldsymbol{{S}}
is a complex matrix.
On the other hand, the PTM
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}
is a real matrix with elements in the range from
-1
to
1
and the respective density matrix vectorization is in the
n
-qubit Pauli basis, denoted by
\mathcal{P}^{\otimes n}
.
It forms an operator basis for a
d=2^{n}
dimensional Hilbert space, encompassing a total of
d^{2}=4^{n}
operators.
These operators can be conveniently labeled using a single index, such as
P_{1}=I^{\otimes n}
,
P_{2}
,
P_{3}
, up to
P_{d^{2}}=Z^{\otimes n}
. These operators are Hermitian and unitary, satisfying the property
P_{k}^{2}=I^{\otimes n}
.
In the case of a single qubit (
n=1
), the basis is represented by
\mathcal{P}^{\otimes 1}=\{{I},{X},{Y},{Z}\}
, which correspond to the familiar Pauli matrices.
Expanding to two qubits (
d=4
), we form a set of 16 operators:
\{II,IX,IY,IZ,XI,XX,XY,\ldots,ZZ\}
, where
II
should be interpreted as
I\otimes I
, and so on.
These operators are succinctly denoted as
\left\{P_{1},P_{2},\ldots,P_{16}\right\}

Forest2019
.

The density matrix vectorization for the single qubit reads:

\rho=\frac{1}{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1+z&x-iy\\
x+iy&1-z\end{array}\right)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad|\rho\rangle\rangle=\left(\begin{array}[]{l}1\\
x\\
y\\
z\end{array}\right),

(2)

where
x,y,z
are the Bloch vector components.
In the PTM representation, the evolution of the state can be expressed as

\ket{\rho}\!\rangle=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}\ket{\rho_{0}}\!\rangle,

(3)

where
\ket{\rho_{0}}\!\rangle
is the initial vectorized state.

The conversion from PTM to Liouville representation is done by the transformation

\boldsymbol{{S}}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}},

(4)

where
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}
is the unitary operator

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}=\sum_{k=1}^{d^{2}}|c_{k}\rangle\langle\langle P_{k}|,

(5)

where all
|c_{k}\rangle
represent the computational basis and they are all possible strings from
|c_{1}\rangle=|0,0,\ldots,0\rangle
to
|c_{d^{2}}\rangle=|1,1,\ldots,1\rangle
.

For the purposes of quantum computation, the channels must have unitary targets
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}
(or
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}_{L}
), represented in PTM and Liouville representations, respectively.
These targets can be expressed by the corresponding
d\times d
target unitary operator
\bm{T}
as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}\Big(\bm{T}^{*}\otimes\bm{T}\Big)\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}^{\dagger},

(6a)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}_{L}

\displaystyle=\bm{T}^{*}\otimes\bm{T}.

(6b)

For the ensuing analysis, we will employ the PTM representation, as it is equivalent to the Liouville representation but it is more convenient for our objectives.

We define the discrepancy between the actual process
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}
and the target process
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}
as the error matrix

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}},

(7)

where
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}
in general is not a CPTP matrix.
The characterization and analysis of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}
play a central role in evaluating the accuracy and precision of the process tomography methods discussed hereafter.

II.2.2
Distance metrics

Given the matrices
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}
,
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}
, we can calculate the process distance metrics.
In this paper, we utilize the
diamond norm
alongside process infidelity metrics.
The diamond norm (known also as
completely bounded trace norm
)
Aharonov1998
;
Kitaev1997
is one of the most effective measures to assess the proximity of two processes due to its enhanced rigor.
In our analysis, we formally express it as

\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\|_{\diamond}=\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}\|_{\diamond}.

(8)

For more comprehensive details about the diamond norm, its definition and calculation, we refer the reader to Refs.
Watrous2009
;
Watrous2013
.
In our work, for the numerical calculation of
\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\|_{\diamond}
and the CPTP channel representations and transformations, we utilize the following Python ready-to-use tools:

qiskit.quantum_info
,
Qiskit
,

forest.benchmarking.operator_tools

Forest2019
, and

qutip.superop_reps

Qutip2013
.

The process fidelity
F
and infidelity
e_{F}
, (when
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}
is unitary
Chow2012b
) are

\displaystyle F

\displaystyle=\frac{\operatorname{Tr}[\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}]}{d^{2}}=1+\frac{\operatorname{Tr}[\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}]}{d^{2}},

(9a)

\displaystyle e_{F}

\displaystyle=1-F=-\frac{\operatorname{Tr}[\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}]}{d^{2}}.

(9b)

Measurement of
e_{F}
is achievable through Interleaved RB.
However, there is currently no established scalable method for determining
\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\|_{\diamond}
.
Tomographic methods, such as GST, can be utilized to estimate
\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\|_{\diamond}
, but they become exponentially expensive as the number of qubits
n
increases.
As discussed in Ref.
Hashim2023
, if only
e_{F}
is known, it is possible to bound
\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\|_{\diamond}
using

e_{F}\leq\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\|_{\diamond}\leq d\sqrt{e_{F}},

(10)

which means that the larger number of qubits
n

(d=2^{n})
leads to larger inaccuracy of
\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\|_{\diamond}
.
The objective of the MQPT method is not only to measure
\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\|_{\diamond}
more accurately, but also to find the individual elements of the error matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}
.
Therefore, for our simulations, we introduce an additional metric called
differential diamond norm
that is determined by the difference between the measured error matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{N}
and the actual one, i.e.
\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{N}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\|_{\diamond}
.

II.3
MQPT: conceptual framework

The basic structure of MQPT is shown schematically in Fig.
1
and it assumes in general two stages.
In stage I,
N
processes (passes) are applied instead of a single process
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}
, so that the sequence
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}^{N}
, which represents the multipass process, is the object of characterization.
The aim is to amplify the gate error
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}
and use the enhanced precision and accuracy of the measured multipass process in order to enhance the precision and accuracy of the deduced single-pass process.
The multipass process can be characterized by a conventional method, such as SQPT, AAPT, or any other methods that in principle apply the characterizing process only once.
Because the multipass process features large (accumulated) errors, the latter can be determined with high accuracy and precision.
Subsequently, stage II is a post-processing calculation procedure used to obtain the single process
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}
from the multipass process
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}^{N}
.
Stage I includes measurement errors (SPAM, readout, shot noise), introduced by the preparation and projective measurement operations
\rho_{j}
and
M_{k}
.
All measurement errors remain constant at both ends of the entire circuit, i.e. they do not accumulate as
N
increases and they are presumed to be the same for single-pass and multipass processes.
This leads to an improvement in the measurement of the multipass process
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}^{N}
, since only the gate error
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}
accumulates over its course.

Before proceeding to stage II, let us make a rough estimate of the resource increase with such an approach. In contemporary quantum superconducting processors, the time for a single shot
t_{s}
(even in the absence of any gate in the circuit) is about 0.3 ms, whereas the time for a single pass (gate)
t_{g}
is on the order of
\sim 100
ns and
\sim 600
ns for single-qubit and two-qubit gates, respectively.
This means that with a number of passes
N\approx 20
, the time for a single shot (for the two-qubit case) will be
t^{\prime}_{s}=t_{s}+Nt_{g}\approx 1.04t_{s}
, so that it will increases by only about 4% compared to the application of a single pass.
This implies that the method does not significantly increase the cost of the experiment in practice.

For performing stage II — deducing the single-pass PTM from the multipass PTM — we use two approaches:

•

an iterative method;

•

a linear approximation.

The iterative method delivers a solution that is practically exact regarding the input data derived from the measured matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}}}^{N}
.
In this context, the term ’exact’ refers solely to the correspondence between the input and the output, rather than indicating precision in identifying the actual error matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}
.
However, if
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}}}^{N}
is considered to be measured with small errors, i.e., with a large number of shots, then initial measurement errors (such as SPAM and readout) are mitigated without introducing any additional post-processing errors.
This leads to a significant enhancement in both precision and accuracy in the deduced single-pass process.

The linear method introduces some post-processing errors due to considering only the linear terms in the expansion of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}}}^{N}
with respect to the error matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}
.
Hence, it applies to small errors only.
Despite this, the method also proves to be useful, especially when the tomography is performed with relatively small number of shots, i.e. in the presence of large shot noise.
In this scenario, it positively affects precision and accuracy, showing some benefits over the iterative method.
This can be likened to the principles of a Wiener filter in signal and image processing
Boulfelfel1994
, where a linear approach is favored for its ability to effectively reduce noise and enhance the clarity of imaging in noisy environments.

III
Iterative and Linear methods

As discussed above, the second stage of MQPT is the inverse task, in which the objective is to extract the single process
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}
, and respectively, the error matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}
, from the multipass process
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}}}^{N}=(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}})^{N}
.
Generally speaking, this problem has many solutions.
However, assuming that
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}
is a high-fidelity gate, i.e.,
\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\|\ll\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}\|
, and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}
is a known target matrix, then we can find a definitive single solution.
We will do that first considering the practically exact iterative solution and then we present the linear method.

III.1
Post-processing iterative method

The method is based on an iterative approach.
It combines the principles of perturbative methods with iterative refinement, akin to gradient descent methods.
At its core, the algorithm employs a perturbative strategy, introducing
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}
as a minor modification to
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}
. This perturbation, significantly smaller in magnitude compared to
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}
(
\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\|\ll\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}\|
), ensures the preservation of the fundamental characteristics of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}
, providing stability to the solution process.
The method unfolds in an iterative cycle, where each iteration corrects
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}
based on the difference between
(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}})^{N}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}}}^{N}
. This iterative refinement directs the solution towards a high degree of precision, typically reducing the error to less than
10^{-12}
, achieving what is called a ’practically exact’ solution in the field of numerical computations.

The iterative method proceeds as follows:

•

Initialization:
Start with an initial guess for
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}
, usually
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}=0
.

•

Iterative Update Steps:

(i) Calculate
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}}}^{N}_{\text{current}}=(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}})^{N}
.

(ii) Determine the difference:
\bm{\delta}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}}}^{N}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}}}^{N}_{\text{current}}
.

(iii) Update
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}
using:
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}+\alpha\bm{\delta}
, where
\alpha
is a small positive learning coefficient (e.g., 0.01).

•

Convergence Criterion:
The process repeats until the norm of
(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}})^{N}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}}}^{N}
falls below a specified tolerance (e.g.,
10^{-12}
), indicating that a practically exact solution has been found.

The code is presented in appendix
B
.

It is important to note that for the CNOT gate, the method works with
N=2m+1
passes, while for the
\sqrt{X}
gate it works for
N=4m+1
passes, where
m=0,1,2,\ldots

In section
IV
, we demonstrate the performance of this method for the
\sqrt{X}
and CNOT gates by simulation on IBM Quantum
ibmq_simulator
.

III.2
Linear method for involutary gates

Now we will derive the linear approximation for the solution of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}=f(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}}}^{N},\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}},N)
, which is valid for involutary gates, i.e., gates which satisfy the relation

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}^{2}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}},

(11)

Some examples for involutory gates are

•

single qubit gates:
X
,
Y
,
Z
and Hadamard;

•

two-qubit gates: CNOT, SWAP, Controlled Z;

•

three-qubit gates: Toffoli and Fredkin.

For these gates, it is convenient to choose the number of the passes to be
N=2m+1

(m=0,1,2,\ldots)
, so we have
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}^{2m+1}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}
. Then the multipass process

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}^{2m+1}=(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}})^{2m+1}

(12)

reads, when expanded up to the second power of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}
, as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}^{2m+1}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}+(m+1)\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}+m\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}

(13a)

\displaystyle+\frac{m(m+1)}{2}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}^{2}_{a}}+\frac{m(m-1)}{2}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}^{2}_{b}}+O(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}^{3}),

(13b)

with

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}^{2}_{a}}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}^{2}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}^{2}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}},

(14a)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}^{2}_{b}}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}},

(14b)

where we have used the non-commutative rules and we have accounted for Eq. (
11
).

In order to find a unique linear solution, we retain linear terms, i.e. Eq. (
13a
).
The nonlinear terms
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}^{2}_{a}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}^{2}_{b}}
can be viewed as post-processing errors; their significance grows quadratically with
m
.
This fact sets an upper limit of how many passes we can apply before our solution deviates too much from the actual one.

By multiplying Eq. (
13a
) with
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}
from the left and taking into account Eq. (
11
), we obtain

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}^{2m+1}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}=(m+1)\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}+m\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}.

(15)

This is the Sylvester equation,

\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{C},

(16)

with

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}

\displaystyle=(m+1)\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}},

(17a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}

\displaystyle=m\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}},

(17b)

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}^{2m+1}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}},

(17c)

which are known matrices.

Equation (
16
) has a unique solution for
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}
if and only if the matrices
\mathbf{A}
and
-\mathbf{B}
do not share any eigenvalue.
In our case, this condition is always fulfilled because
m+1\neq-m
.
This means that by solving Eq. (
16
) we can calculate the error matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}
and then the single process
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}
.

III.3
Linear approximation for arbitrary target gates

As for the involutary target gates, we proceed with the same linear approximation for the generalization for any arbitrary target gate.
By keeping only the linear terms in
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}
, the multipass process
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}}}^{N}
takes the form

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}}}^{N}\approx\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}^{N}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}^{N-1}\sum_{s=0}^{N-1}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}^{-s}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}^{s}.

(18)

We find from here

\boldsymbol{E}^{(N)}=\sum_{s=0}^{N-1}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}^{-s}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}^{s},

(19)

where
\boldsymbol{E}^{(N)}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}^{1-N}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}}}^{N}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}
is a known matrix and our aim is to find the error matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}
on the right-hand side.
Equation (
19
) is a special case of a general extended Sylvester equation.
According to Ref.
Song2011
it can be numerically solved for
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}
by the iterations

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{k+1}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{k}+\mu\sum_{q=0}^{N-1}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}^{q}\Big(\boldsymbol{E}^{(N)}-\sum_{s=0}^{N-1}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}^{-s}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{k}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}^{s}\Big)\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}^{-q},

(20)

where
\mu
is a convergence factor that controls the convergence of the iterative algorithm (
20
), which takes values in the range of
0<\mu\ll 1
.
Larger
\mu
leads to fewer iterations; however, above a critical value there is no convergence of the algorithm.
More details about the choice of
\mu
can be found in Ref.
Song2011
.
In our demonstration for single-qubit non-involutary gates, we choose
\mu=0.003
.
The initial matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{0}
could be any arbitrary matrix, having the same dimension as
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}
.

In Sec.
IV
, we demonstrate the linear method for the
\sqrt{X}
and CNOT gates by simulations on
qasm_simulator
.
We will illustrate also how the linear method leads to improved accuracy for small number of shots
n_{s}
compared to the iterative method.
However, for large
n_{s}
, the post-processing errors become significant and then the iterative method outperforms the linear method.

IV
Performance of the MQPT method: simulation

The simulations discussed in this section were performed with
qasm_simulator
backend of Qiskit, IBM’s open-source quantum computing software framework
Qiskit
.

IV.1
Description of the simulation

In order to examine the precision and the accuracy of the proposed method and its capability to identify individual error matrix elements, we have chosen a purely simulation-based approach.
This choice is due to the fact that in simulations, we are able to know a priori the actual process we wish to characterize and use it as a reference.
In order to obtain reliable statistics, we apply the iterative method to the diamond norm by performing 50 tomographies for various passes
N
and numbers of shots
n_{s}
.
We perform such simulations on two basic quantum gates — the single-qubit gate
\sqrt{X}
and the two-qubit gate CNOT.
For both of them, we have pre-generated PTM matrices
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}
that have similar gate infidelities as the respective real quantum processors of IBM Quantum.
The PTM’s were generated using the Qutip’s master equation
mesolve
program
Qutip2013
with the incorporation of collapse operators and unitary errors that were randomly selected.

For the simulation of measurement, it is necessary also to simulate the measurement errors — specifically, SPAM, readout, and shot noise errors.
As for the gate error matrix, the measurement errors are chosen by considering typical values for the current real IBM Quantum processors.
In our model, the SPAM error is introduced by the so-called ”noise model”
Noise2024
.
The SPAM error is modeled only for the
\sqrt{X}
gate, as it is the only imperfect single qubit gate that is used in SPAM.
The phase gates, which are also basic gates and used in SPAM, are practically perfect due to the usage of the so-called ”Frame change” technique
McKay2017
.
We assume a typical SPAM error (infidelity) of
2\times 10^{-4}
for the
\sqrt{X}
gate, while the readout error is set to
3\times 10^{-3}
.
We note that, in reality, the readout error could be larger, in the range of
10^{-2}
; however, we assume that we have also performed in advance the so-called ”readout mitigation” technique
MPT2024
.
This technique is widely used and is available in Qiskit in the module
MitigatedProcessTomography
. Nevertheless, this technique cannot completely mitigate the readout error, and a smaller but still significant part remains.
The shot noise error, that scales proportionally to
1/\sqrt{n_{s}}
is automatically introduced when specifying the number of shots
n_{s}
in the Qiskit
ProcessTomography
module.
In the simulation discussed in this section, we use various numbers of shots
n_{s}=
4k, 10k, 40k, 100k etc., where, for simplicity, k
=10^{3}
hereafter.

In IBM Qiskit, the output of the
ProcessTomography
measurement is presented by the measured Choi matrix
\mathcal{C}
.
In the first stage of MQPT, when performing QPT on
N
gates we have a measured Choi matrix
\mathcal{C}^{(N)}
, that we convert to the respective PTM
\mathcal{R}^{N}
by the transformation

\left(\mathcal{R}^{N}\right)_{i,j}=\frac{1}{d}\operatorname{Tr}\left[\mathcal{C}^{(N)}P_{j}^{T}\otimes P_{i}\right].

(21)

Subsequently, in the post-processing MQPT procedure, having found
\mathcal{R}^{N}
, we determine the measured error matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{N}
and the measured single process
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}_{N}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{N}
.
Since we know the actual error
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}
, we can visualize the precision and accuracy enhancement by using the diamond norms of the measured error matrix
\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{N}\|_{\diamond}
and the difference between the measured and actual error matrices
\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{N}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\|_{\diamond}
.

IV.2
MQPT simulation of
\sqrt{X}

We begin with a simulation of the single-qubit gate
\sqrt{X}
, which is a basic gate in IBM Quantum processors.
The target
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}
and the actual error matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}
are plotted in Fig.
2
(top row), and the explicit form of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}
is given in Appendix
A
.
In each shot, we applied the
\sqrt{X}
gate
N=4m+1
times (
N
passes) and then performed standard QPT in order to generate the PTM matrix of the process.
Because each shot has a statistical shot noise error, we used
n_{s}
shots in order to reduce this error.
In the simulations we used
n_{s}=
4k to 1000k.
For each combination of
N
and
n_{s}
, we executed 50 tomographies, followed by the application of the iterative method with some comparisons with the linear one.
Each of the 50 tomographies had its own “shot noise” statistical error and hence their distribution is presented in histograms.

Figure
2
provides a visual representation of the enhanced MQPT by displaying the error matrices
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{N}
for various numbers of passes (
N=1,5,17
) and numbers of shots (10k, 100k, 1000k).
When compared to the actual matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}
, as shown in Fig.
2
(top/right), it becomes evident how the matrices
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{N}
progressively approximate
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}
more closely with the increase in both
N
and
n_{s}
,
while the single-pass matrices
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{1}
differ significantly, even for 1000k shots.

In order to conduct a more thorough examination of the precision and accuracy of our method, we analyzed the distributions of the diamond norms
\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{N}\|_{\diamond}
and
\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{N}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\|_{\diamond}
derived by the iterative method, which are depicted in Fig.
3
.
The left-hand-side matrix of plots reveals that for SQPT (
N=1
) only the precision is improved as the number of shots
n_{s}
increases, while the accuracy is still low.
The reason is that the SPAM and readout errors add up to the gate errors and prevent SQPT from retrieving the latter.
On the contrary, for MQPT (
N=5,17
), the distributions squeeze (indicating improved precision) and shift closer to the exact values (indicating improved accuracy) when
N
and
n_{s}
increase.

The right-hand-side matrix of plots in Fig.
3
shows the distribution of the diamond norm difference,
\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{N}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\|_{\diamond}
, representing the distance between the measured error matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{N}
and the actual error matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}
, for various numbers of passes
N=4m+1
and numbers of shots
n_{s}
.
This figure demonstrates how effectively MQPT can measure the actual matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}
, going beyond merely assessing the actual diamond norm
\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\|_{\diamond}
, as compared to Fig.
3
.
Indeed, different matrices
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{N}
can have the same diamond norm
\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{N}\|_{\diamond}
but they can be at different distances
\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{N}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\|_{\diamond}
from the actual error matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}
.
However, when the diamond distance
\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{N}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\|_{\diamond}
approaches 0 then the calculated error matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{N}
approaches the actual error matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}
with certainty.
The left column in the right-hand-side matrix of plots in Fig.
3
demonstrates that this cannot be achieved with a single pass regardless of the number of shots.
The multipass MQPT method, even for the relatively low values of
N
in the figure, make it possible to approach very closely the actual error matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}
because the detrimental SPAM and readout effects are greatly reduced.

While distribution plots for the linear method have been omitted due to their qualitative resemblance to those of the iterative method, Fig.

presents a comparison of the two methods. It is noteworthy that, for a range of shots (4k and 40k), both
\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{N}\|_{\diamond}
and
\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{N}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\|_{\diamond}
yield smaller values by the linear method.
This observation seems to contradict expectations, as the iterative method typically offers a practically exact solution for finding
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}
given a multi-pass process
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{R}}}}^{N}
.
However, the linear method mixes the post-processing with the shot noise errors, which statistically leads to a random error compensation.
This implicit feature is quite similar to Ridge regression
Gruber1998
, which prevents overfitting to noisy data, and to the Wiener filter
Boulfelfel1994
, suggesting that the linear method might provide a more stable solution in the presence of noise.
However, this trend is reversed for the higher shot count of 1000k,
making the data clearer of shot noise error, the inherent ability of the iterative method to precisely adjust to the signal without being distorted by noise comes to the forefront.
At the same time, the linear method begins to exhibit its limitations, as at reduced levels of shot noise, an increasingly uncompensated post-processing error becomes evident, and the accuracy of the linear method falls behind.

(right) displays these results on a logarithmic scale.

are implemented.
Another noteworthy observation is the precise infidelity measurement achieved by the IRB method.
While the IRB method accurately determines infidelity

In contrast, the strength of MQPT lies in its ability to deliver these metrics, although with lower accuracy in the measurement of

Following the approach taken for the
\sqrt{X}
gate, we examine the diamond norm distributions for the CNOT gate in order to assess the precision and accuracy of our MQPT approach.
The diamond norms
\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{N}\|_{\diamond}
and
\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{N}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\|_{\diamond}
, calculated with the iterative method, are plotted in Fig.
6

As for the
\sqrt{X}
gate, the right-hand-side matrix of plots, which shows the difference norm
\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{N}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\|_{\diamond}
between the simulated
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{N}
and actual
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}
error matrices, is the unambiguous measure of success or failure of the simulation, because different simulated matrices
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{N}
may have the same diamond norm
\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{N}\|_{\diamond}
but they may be far from the actual error matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}
.

Figure
6
demonstrates that, as the number of passes
N
increases, the distribution becomes both more precise (its width shrinks) and more accurate (it approaches the vertical dashed line, which shows the actual error stemming from the generated random error matrix).
On the other hand, when we increase the number of shots
n_{s}
and hence reduce the statistical shot-noise error, the precision increases (according to the statistical square-root law), but the change in accuracy depends on the number of passes used: it quickly saturates for 1 pass, while for 5 and 17 passes the distribution moves steadily toward the dashed line (the actual error).

The reason is that the SPAM and readout errors shift the single-pass simulations, whereas these errors are greatly reduced by the multipass approach.
This simulation clearly conveys the enhancements in both precision and accuracy with the MQPT repetition method.

Fig.

It is a test of how the two methods perform relative to each other.
For 4k shots, the linear method yields better accuracy than the iterative method.

This suggests that at higher shot counts, the iterative method more effectively mitigates the SPAM and readout errors.
Additionally, at 40k and 1000k shots, there is a monotonic decrease in the iterative norms as the number of passes
N
increases, further emphasizing the iterative method’s advantage in the high-shot regime.

\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{N}\|_{\diamond}

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}

N

This observation does not show up in the differential diamond norm

VI
Conclusions

In this paper, we introduced a technique called Multipass Quantum Process Tomography (MQPT) that greatly improves the precision and accuracy of Standard Quantum Process Tomography by employing gate repetitions (passes).
This approach serves as a complement to other non-repetitive methods such as SQPT and AAPT, offering a more straightforward and resource-efficient alternative to the more intricate and demanding repetitive methods.
We explored both iterative and linear versions of MQPT and demonstrated their application to the
\sqrt{X}
and CNOT gates.
Our simulation models assessed the method’s accuracy and precision before their implementation on the real IBM Quantum processor
ibmq_manila
.
The findings indicate that while each of the linear and iterative MQPT methods has specific advantages depending on the tomography conditions, both consistently alleviate the effects of SPAM and readout errors, and shot noise.

We emphasize that, compared to other quantum tomography methods, such as randomized benchmarking, which only deliver the gate fidelity, our method provides the entire quantum process matrix, the Pauli transfer matrix (PTM).
It contains the complete information for the specific process, which can be used to deduce the contributions of the various error mechanisms.
We achieve this with far better precision and accuracy than the (non-repetitive) Standard Quantum Process Tomography.
