Discrete Laplace and transition operators over non-Archimedean ordered fields Thanks: Keywords : weighted graphs, differential operators, Laplace operator, ordered field, transition operator, Cheeger constant. Mathematics Subject Classification 2010: 05C50, 47A75, 47A10, 05C22, 12J15
Anna Muranova

Abstract
. We investigate properties of spectrum of normalized Laplacian ℒ \mathcal{L} for finite graphs over non-Archimedean ordered fields. We prove a Cheeger’s inequality for first non-zero eigenvalue. Then we describe properties of the operator 𝒫 = I − ℒ \mathcal{P}=I-\mathcal{L} , which is a generalization of transition operator. We show that Cheeger estimate α 1 ⪯ 1 − h 2 \alpha_{1}\preceq\sqrt{1-h^{2}} for the second largest eigenvalue of 𝒫 \mathcal{P} is crucial for investigation of the convergence of analogue of random walk to equilibrium over a non-Archimedean ordered fields. We consider examples over the Levi-Civita field.



1.
Introduction

The eigenvalues of normalized Laplacian
\mathcal{L}
on weighted graphs is a classical widely investigated topic.
For finite graphs the results are presented in the classical books and papers on graph theory, like e.g.
[
1
]
,
[
6
]
,
[
9
]
. The probability (transition) operator
\mathcal{P}
is the difference of identity operator and Laplacian. It uniquely describes a random walk on graph. The most important results in classical case are that all eigenvalues of Laplacian lie in
[0,2]
, that
0
is always an eigenvalue and that the eigenvalues are symmetric with respect to
1
if and only if the graph is bipartite. The branch of results for probability operator follows from the above mentioned facts. Further one can consider the Hilbert space of functions from vertices of graph to
\mathbb{R}
and investigate properties of
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{L}
as operators on this space. It is known, that random walk convergence to an equilibrium for any given function on finite graph (see e.g.
[
6
]
,
[
9
]
).

We investigate the Laplace and transition operators on graphs, whose weighted belongs to a non-Archimedean real-closed ordered field
\mathbb{K}
, following the previous works by author (
[
15
]
,
[
16
]
). We show, that the results, arising from linear algebra hold also in this settings. More precisely, we present the prove, that eigenvalues of Laplacian belong to the same field, are between
0
and
2
. Further, they are symmetric with respect to
1
if and only if the graph is bipartite.
Moreover, some inequalities in terms of number of vertices also hold for them (see e.g. Proposition
1
). We present the direct proofs of all these facts.

Since we do not use non-standard probability theory (e.g.
[
17
]
), to avoid a confusion we call the operator
\mathcal{P}

transition operator
in this paper, keeping in mind, that its matrix has values between
0
and
1
in
\mathbb{K}
. The details about operators are described in Section
3
.

In non-Archimedean fields the fact that eigenvalues of
\mathcal{P}
are between
-1
and
1
is not enough, to show the convergence of
\mathcal{P}^{m}f,m\in\mathbb{N}
,
f
is a function on vertices, taking values in
\mathbb{K}
to an equilibrium (function on vertices, depended on
f
). Moreover, the convergence it not always the case. One of the possible ways to investigate the convergence is to use Cheeger inequality (see Theorem
2
):

\lambda_{1}\succeq 1-\sqrt{1-h^{2}}

for the smallest non-zero eigenvalue of Laplacian, which leads to
\alpha_{1}\preceq\sqrt{1-h^{2}}
for the second smallest eigenvalue of
\mathcal{P}
. Further we show, that in case of non-Archimidean ordered field there is a crucial difference between two classical formulations of Cheeger inequality:

\lambda_{1}\succeq{\frac{h^{2}}{2}}\;\;\;\;\;\;\mbox{ and }\;\;\;\;\;\;\lambda_{1}\succeq 1-\sqrt{1-h^{2}}

since only the latest one can be used to prove the convergence.

In Section
5.1
we investigate the convergence of
\mathcal{P}^{m}f
or
\mathcal{P}^{2m}f
for some
f
to equilibrium, using inequalities, which in the classical (real) case are used to calculate the rate of convergence. In Theorem
5
we show that, on the contrary to real case, for any non-bipartite non-complete graph over non-Archimedean field there is always a subspace of functions, for which
\mathcal{P}^{m}f
does not converge. Complete graphs show different behavior due to Lemma
5
and we do not describe them in this note.

The largest non-Archimedean ordered field is the field of surreal numbers (
[
7
]
,
[
2
, Theorem 24.29]
), and it is known (e.g.
[
18
]
) that the convergent sequences in classical sense are exclusively constant sequences there. Therefore, it makes sense to investigate convergence only in some particular non-Archimedean ordered fields. Following out previous work (
[
16
]
), which shows that the Levi-Civita field appears naturally in theory of electrical networks, in the last section we investigate the convergence of
\mathcal{P}^{m}
over the Levi-Civita field. Theorem
6
gives the sufficient condition for convergence for any function on bipartite graph in terms of Cheeger constant. For non-bipartite non-complete graphs the Theorem
7
describes sufficient conditions for existence of function
f
on vertices, such that
\mathcal{P}^{m}f
converges to an equilibrium.

2.
Preliminaries

Let
\mathbb{K}
be an arbitrary real-closed non-Archimedean ordered field (in fact, an order can be introduced in any real-closed field
[
12
, Theorem 2.2]
). If the ordered field is not real closed, we should consider one of its real closures, which are all isomorphic due to Artin-Schreier Theorem (see e.g.
[
4
]
) with isomorphism, preserving the order. A field is called
ordered
if the property of positivness (
\succ 0
) is defined for its elements, and if it satisfies the folowing postulates:

(1)

For every element
a\in\mathbb{K}
, just one of the relations

a\succ 0,a=0,-a\succ 0

is valid.

(2)

If
a\succ 0
and
b\succ 0
, then
a+b\succ 0
and
ab\succ 0
. We write
a\succ b
(“
a
greater than
b
”) if
a-b\succ 0
. We write
a\prec b
if
b\succ a
(“
a
smaller than
b
”). This is a total order in
\mathbb{K}
. The signs
\preceq
and
\succeq
correspond to “great or equal” and “smaller or equal”
(see
[
23
]
).

The set
\mathbb{K}^{+}=\{a\in\mathbb{K}\mid a\succ 0\}
is called a
set of positive elements
.

An ordered field is
non-Archimedean
, if there exist an
infinitesimal

\tau\in\mathbb{K}^{+}
, i.e,
\tau
such that
\tau\preceq 1/m
for any
m\in\mathbb{N}
, where
m=\underbrace{1+\cdots+1}_{m}\in\mathbb{K}^{+}
.

An ordered field
\mathbb{K}
is
real-closed
if it has no proper algebraic extension to an ordered field, or, equivalently, if the complexification of
\mathbb{K}
is algebraically closed, or, again equivalently, if every positive element in
\mathbb{K}
has a square root and every polynomial over
\mathbb{K}
of odd degree has a root in
\mathbb{K}

[
4
, p. 38]
.

Let
\mathbb{K}
be a real-closed field. Further we need a complexification of
\mathbb{K}
, i.e a field extension
\mathbb{K}(\sqrt{-1})
, which is algebraically closed. Let us agree, that we pick up one square root of
-1
and denote it by
\mathbf{i}
. Then we can consider the automorphism of this field, which maps elements of
\mathbb{K}
into themselves, and maps
\mathbf{i}
to
-\mathbf{i}
. We call this automorphism a
complex conjugation
and we denote it further in text by overlining the element (in the same way as case of usual complex conjugation). We denote
\mathbb{K}(\mathbf{i})
by
\mathbb{K}_{C}
.

Definition 1
.

[
15
]

Let
\mathbb{K}
be an ordered field. A
graph (over an oredred field
\mathbb{K}
)
is a couple
(V,b)
, where
V
is a set of vertices (i.e. arbitrary set)
and
b:V\times V\rightarrow\mathbb{K}
satisfies the following properties:

(1)

b(x,y)\succeq 0
for any
x,y\in V
,

(2)

b(x,y)=b(y,x)
for any
x,y\in V
,

(3)

b(x,x)=0
for any
x\in V
.

If
b(x,y)\neq 0
, we say that there is an
edge
between
x,y
and write
x\sim y
.

The third condition means, that we consider graphs without loops.
When the field
\mathbb{K}
is relevant, we will say
graph over the field
\mathbb{K}
.
Note that if instead of
\mathbb{K}
we use
\mathbb{R}
, then we obtain a classical definition of a
weighted graph
.

The
path
in graph is any sequence of vertices
x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}
such that

x_{1}\sim x_{2}\sim\dots\sim x_{n}.

A graph is called
connected
if there exist a path between any two of its vertices. A graph is called
finite
if its set of vertices is finite (
\#V<\infty
).
In this note we consider exclusively finite connected graphs.

A graph is called
complete
if
x\sim y
for any
x,y\in V
. Otherwise it is called
non-complete
.

A graph is called
bipartite
, if there exist a partition of its vertices
V=~V_{1}\cup~V_{2}
such that if
x\sim y
for
x,y\in V
then either
x\in V_{1},y\in V_{2}
or
x\in V_{2},y\in V_{1}
. Otherwise, the graph is called
non-bipartite
.

Let us consider the
normalized
weights

(1)

p(x,y)=\dfrac{b(x,y)}{b(x)},

where
b(x)=\sum_{y}b(x,y)
. Note, that
p(x,y)\neq p(y,x)
in general.

It is known that weighted graph can be considered as Hilbert space (e. g.
[
9
]
,
[
11
]
). We would like to introduce an analogue of scalar product on graphs over ordered fields.

Let us consider a set of functions on vertices of a graph
(V,b)
:

\mathfrak{F}=\{f\mid f:V\rightarrow\mathbb{K}\}

As an analogue of
scalar
product for any
f,g\in\mathfrak{F}
we consider

\langle f,g\rangle=\sum_{x\in V}f(x)g(x)b(x).

We list below the properties of this scalar product, which are crucial for us. These properties coincide with the properties of usual scalar product for order
>
on
\mathbb{R}
.

(1)

for any
f,g\in\mathfrak{F}
holds

\langle f,g\rangle=\langle g,f\rangle;

(2)

for any
a,b\in\mathbb{K}
and for any
f,g\in\mathfrak{F}
holds

\langle af+bf,g\rangle=a\langle f,g\rangle+b\langle f,g\rangle;

(3)

for any
f\in\mathfrak{F}

\langle f,f\rangle\succeq 0

and
\langle f,f\rangle=0
if and only if
f\equiv 0
on
V
.

Moreover, the analogue of complex-valued scalar product can be considered for the functions, taking values in
\mathbb{K}_{C}
. We denote the set of functions on vertices with values from
\mathbb{K}_{C}
by

\mathfrak{F}_{C}=\{f\mid f:V\rightarrow\mathbb{K}_{C}\}.

Then the ‘complex’ scalar product for
f,g\in\mathfrak{F}_{C}
is defined by

\langle f,g\rangle_{C}=\sum_{x\in V}f(x)\overline{g(x)}b(x).

It posses properties (2) – (3) and is anti-symmetric, i.e.

(1enumi)

for any
f,g\in\mathfrak{F}_{C}

\langle f,g\rangle_{C}=\overline{\langle g,f\rangle}_{C}.

Obviously,
\langle f,g\rangle_{C}=\langle f,g\rangle
for any
f,g\in\mathfrak{F}
.

These things are usually described in frameworks of symmetric and Hermitian quadratic forms (see e.g.
[
12
]
), but we would like to bring here together several things and introduce an analogue of a pre-Hilbert space on finite graphs over ordered fields.

We define

\|f\|=\sqrt{\langle f,f\rangle}\in\mathbb{K}^{+}

for any
f\in\mathfrak{F}_{c}
.

3.
Laplace and transition operators, their eigenvalues

Our main point of investigation will be a normalized Laplace operator (Laplacian) and transition operator on
\mathfrak{F}
.

Laplacian
on a graph
(V,b)
over
\mathbb{K}
is defined as
\mathcal{L}:\mathfrak{F}\to\mathfrak{F}
:

(2)

\mathcal{L}f(x)=\sum_{y\in V}(f(x)-f(y))\dfrac{b(x,y)}{b(x)}=\sum_{y\in V}(f(x)-f(y))p(x,y)

for any
f\in\mathfrak{F}
.

Further, we define a
transition operator
as
\mathcal{P}=I-\mathcal{L}
on
f\in\mathfrak{F}
. The entries of the corresponding matrix over
\mathbb{K}
lie between
0
and
1
, moreover, elements of each row/column sum up to
1\in\mathbb{K}
.

Note that for the classical weighted graphs
\mathcal{P}
is a stochastic matrix of the corresponding Markov chain (see e.g.
[
14
]
,
[
24
]
just to mention some among many).

The Laplacian is a symmetric operator with respect to the introduced scalar product (i.e.
\langle\mathcal{L}f,g\rangle=\langle f,\mathcal{L}g\rangle
for
f,g\in\mathfrak{F}
), due to the
Green formula

[
15
, Thm. 22]
:

\sum_{x\in V}\mathcal{L}f(x)g(x)b(x)=\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{x,y\in V}(\nabla_{xy}f)(\nabla_{xy}g)b(x,y),

where
\nabla_{xy}f=f(y)-f(x)
for any
f,g\in\mathfrak{F}
.

Moreover, it is symmetric for the scalar product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{C}
and all the functions from
\mathfrak{F}_{C}
, since the analogue of Green formula for function from
\mathfrak{F}_{c}
can be proven in the classical way (see e.g proof in
[
9
]
or proof in
[
15
, Thm. 22]
). Therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in V}\mathcal{L}f(x)\overline{g(x)}b(x)=\sum_{x\in V}\mathcal{L}f(x)\overline{g(x)}b(x).

Now we present here several lemmas, which are reformulations of known algebraic facts for the Laplacian as a linear operator on
\mathfrak{F}
. Moreover, we present proofs for some of them, since not all linear algebra techniques can be used over an arbitrary non-Archimedean ordered field.

Lemma 1
.

All eigenvalues of the Laplacian belong to
\mathbb{K}
. Moreover, all its eigenfunctions belong to
\mathfrak{F}
.

Proof.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{K}_{C}
be an eigenvalue of
\mathcal{L}
with an eigenfunction
f
, i.e. there exists
f\in\mathfrak{F}_{c}
,
f\not\equiv 0
such that
\mathcal{L}f(x)=\lambda f(x)
for any
x\in V
. Then

\displaystyle\lambda\langle f,f\rangle_{C}=\langle\lambda f,f\rangle_{C}=\langle\mathcal{L}f,f\rangle_{C}=\langle f,\mathcal{L}f\rangle_{C}=\langle f,\lambda f\rangle_{C}=\overline{\lambda}\langle f,f\rangle_{C}

due to anti-symmetry. Therefore, dividing by
\langle f,f\rangle_{C}
we obtain
\overline{\lambda}=\lambda
from where follows, that
\lambda\in\mathbb{K}
.

Let
\lambda
has an eigenfunction
f\in\mathfrak{F}_{C}
. Then, there exists
f_{1},f_{2}\in\mathfrak{F}
such that
f=f_{1}+\mathbf{i}f_{2}
, and due to the linearity of
\mathcal{L}
,

\mathcal{L}f=\mathcal{L}f_{1}+\mathbf{i}\mathcal{L}f_{2}=\lambda f_{1}+\mathbf{i}\lambda f_{2}

from where follows

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}f_{1}

\displaystyle=\lambda f_{1}

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}f_{2}

\displaystyle=\lambda f_{2}.

Moreover, since
f\not\equiv 0
, at least one of the functions
f_{1},f_{2}
is not constantly zero and, therefore, it is an eigenfunction of
\mathcal{L}
.

∎

Lemma 2
.

All the eigenvalues of the Laplacian are precisely the roots of characteristic polynomial of its matrix.

Proof.

Follows from more general theorem, see e.g.
[
12
, Theorem 3.2 on p.562]

∎

Lemma 3
.

There exists an orthonormal basis (
\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle=0
and
\|v_{i}\|=1
for any
i,j=\overline{1,n},i\neq j
) of eigenfunctions of the Laplacian, i.e. the matrix of Laplacian is diagonilazable over
\mathbb{K}
.

Proof.

Follows from the proof of Lemma 6.3 in
[
12
, p. 582]
, and proof of the Spectral Theorem 6.4 there.
∎

Therefore, the Laplacian has exactly
n=\#V
eigenvalues

\lambda_{0}\preceq\lambda_{1}\preceq\dots\preceq\lambda_{n-1}

and there are
n
orthonormal eigenfunctions
v_{0},v_{1}\dots,v_{n-1}
, forming a basis of
\mathfrak{F}
. These notations for eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of the Laplacian will be used through the entire paper.

Lemma 4
.

Set

\mathcal{R}(f)=\dfrac{\langle\mathcal{L}f,f\rangle}{\langle f,f\rangle}

for
f\in\mathfrak{F}\setminus\{0\}
(the function
\mathcal{R}
is called the
Rayleigh quotient
of
\mathcal{L}
). The following is true for all
k=0,\dots,n-1
:

(3)

\lambda_{k}\preceq\mathcal{R}(f)\mbox{ for any }f\in\mathfrak{F}\mbox{ with }\langle f,v_{i}\rangle=0\;\mbox{ for all }\;i=\overline{0,k-1},

(4)

\lambda_{k}\succeq\mathcal{R}(f)\mbox{ for any }{f\in\mathfrak{F}\mbox{ with }\langle f,v_{i}\rangle=0\;\mbox{ for all }\;i=\overline{k+1,n-1}}.

Moreover,

(5)

\lambda_{k}=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}f:\langle f,v_{i}\rangle=0\\
\forall\;i=0,\dots,k-1\end{subarray}}\mathcal{R}(f)=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}f:\langle f,v_{i}\rangle=0\\
\forall\;i=k+1,\dots,n-1\end{subarray}}\mathcal{R}(f),

where maximum and minimum are attained on the corresponding eigenfunction
v_{k}
.

Proof.

The fact that
\lambda_{k}=\mathcal{R}(v_{k})
follows from the definitions of Rayleigh quotient and an eigenvalue.

The inequalities (
3
) and (
4
) follow from considering
Rayleigh quotient for the basis decomposition
f=f_{k}v_{k}+\dots+f_{n-1}v_{n-1}
and
f=f_{0}v_{0}+\dots+f_{k}v_{k}
for any function from the given subspaces.

∎

4.
Estimates on eigenvalues

In this Section we present estimates on eigenvalues of Laplace and transition operaror for finite graph
(V,b)
over non-Archimedean ordered field. The results are also known to hold for the field
\mathbb{R}
(see e.g.
[
1
]
,
[
9
]
).
In the classical case of weighted graphs the absolute values of eigenvalues
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{n-1}
(the latest plays role in the case of non-bipartite graphs) determine the rate of convergence of random walk, i.e. the speed of convergence of
\mathcal{P}^{m}f,m\in\mathbb{N}
(or
\mathcal{P}^{2m}f
for bipartite graphs) for
f\in\mathfrak{F}
to a function
\overline{f}\in\mathfrak{F}
, depending on
f
and is related to a mixing time (see e.g.
[
9
]
,
[
14
]
).
In this Section, we present estimates on these eigenvalues for graphs over ordered fields. The main result here is the Cheeger’s inequality in its stronger formulation for graphs over ordered fields (Theorem
2
).

By
absolute value of
k\in\mathbb{K}
we mean the value
|k|\in\mathbb{K}^{+}\cup\{0\}
defined as follows

|k|=\begin{cases}k,\mbox{ if }k\succeq 0\\
-k,\mbox{otherwise.}\end{cases}

4.1.
Cheeger’s inequality

Lemma 5
.

For any non-complete graph
\alpha_{1}\succeq 0
.

Proof.

The proof follows the classical outline for the proof of fact, that
\lambda_{1}\leq 1
, see e.g.
[
9
, p.44]
. We present the proof here in a shorter form.

Let us take two vertices
z_{1},z_{2}
such that
z_{1}\not\sim z_{2}
(it is possible, since the graph is non-complete) and consider function

f(x)=\begin{cases}c_{1},\mbox{ for }x=z_{1}\\
c_{2},\mbox{ for }x=z_{2}\\
0,\mbox{ otherwise},\end{cases}

where
c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathbb{K}
, such that
\langle f,1\rangle=0
. Let us denote
V_{12}=V\setminus\{z_{1},z_{2}\}
. Then
f(x)=0
for
x\in V_{12}
.

We have
\langle f,f\rangle=c_{1}^{2}b(z_{1})+c_{2}^{2}b(z_{2})
and, due to the Green formula,

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{L}f,f\rangle

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{x,y\in V}(f(y)-f(x))^{2}b(x,y)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x=z_{1},\\
y\in V_{12}\end{subarray}}(f(y)-f(x))^{2}b(x,y)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in V_{12},\\
y=z_{1}\end{subarray}}(f(y)-f(x))^{2}b(x,y)\right)

\displaystyle+\dfrac{1}{2}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x=z_{2},\\
y\in V_{12}\end{subarray}}(f(y)-f(x))^{2}b(x,y)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in V_{12},\\
y=z_{2}\end{subarray}}(f(y)-f(x))^{2}b(x,y)\right)

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\dfrac{1}{2}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x=z_{1},\\
y=z_{2}\end{subarray}}(f(y)-f(x))^{2}b(x,y)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x=z_{2},\\
y=z_{1}\end{subarray}}(f(y)-f(x))^{2}b(x,y)\right)}_{=0\mbox{ since }z_{1}\not\sim z_{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x=z_{1},\\
y\in V_{12}\end{subarray}}(f(y)-f(x))^{2}b(x,y)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x=z_{2},\\
y\in V_{12}\end{subarray}}(f(y)-f(x))^{2}b(x,y)+0

5.2.
Property of non-convergence for non-bipartite graphs

In classical case the estimates from Theorem
3
and Theorem
4
are mainly used to prove the convergence of random walk to equilibrium and to estimate mixing times (see
[
9
]
,
[
14
]
). Here we investigate the convergence over non-Archimedean field, since any Archimedean field is isomorphic to a subfield of real numbers and therefore is not interesting for us.

The convergence of sequence
\{k_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
to
k
in ordered field means by definition, that for any element
a\in\mathbb{K}
there exists
N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for any
n\geq N_{0}
we have

|k_{n}-k|\preceq a.

To state any result on convergence for an arbitrary ordered field is impossible, since in surreal numbers (which is the largest possible ordered field, containing all the others, see e.g.
[
7
]
,
[
2
, Theorem 24.29]
) only constant sequences converge, if one consider the above mentioned classical notion of convergence (
[
18
, Theorem 16]
). Therefore, we will present the theorem on convergence for the important case of the Levi-Civita field in the next section.

However, for non-bipartite graphs we have the following surprisingly interesting property of
\mathcal{P}^{m}
over arbitrary non-Archimedean ordered field
\mathbb{K}
.

Theorem 5
.

Let
V
be a non-bipartite graph over a non-Archimedean field
\mathbb{K}
. Then there is no limit
\mathcal{\alpha}_{n-1}^{m}v_{n-1}(x)
for
m\to\infty
for any vertex
x\in V
with
v_{n-1}(x)\neq 0
over any non-Archimedean ordered field.
There are no partial limits.

In other words, there always exists a function
f\in\mathfrak{F}
and a vertex
x\in V
such that
\mathcal{P}^{m}f(x)
has no (partial) limit in
\mathbb{K}
.

Proof.

For any two elements of the sequence
\alpha_{n-1}^{m}v_{n-1}(x)
and
\alpha_{n-1}^{m+l}v_{n-1}(x)
, where
l\in\mathbb{N}

we have

|\alpha_{n-1}^{m}v_{n-1}(x)-\alpha_{n-1}^{m+l}v_{n-1}(x)|=|\alpha_{n-1}^{m}||1-\alpha_{n-1}^{l}||v_{n-1}(x)|\\
\succ{\tau}|1+\alpha_{n-1}|{|v_{n-1}(x)|},

where
\tau\in\mathbb{K}
is any infinitesimal, since
|\alpha_{n-1}^{m}|\succeq 1/(n-1)^{m}\succ\tau
due to Lemma
7
and for
|1-\alpha_{n-1}^{l}|
we have two possibilities:

•

if
l
is odd, then
|1-\alpha_{n-1}^{l}|\succ 1\succ|1+\alpha_{n-1}|
, since
-1\prec\alpha_{n-1}\prec 0
for non-bipartite graphs.

•

If
l
is even, then
\alpha_{n-1}^{l}=|\alpha_{n-1}|^{l}\prec|\alpha_{n-1}|\prec 1
, since graph is non-bipartite, and

|1-\alpha_{n-1}^{l}|\succ|1-|\alpha_{n-1}||=|1+\alpha_{n-1}|
.

Since
-1\prec\alpha_{n-1}
for non-bipartite graph we have
|1+\alpha_{n-1}|\neq 0
and
non-convergence follows from the fact that any subsequence is not a Cauchy sequence.

∎

The Levi-Civita field is the smallest non-Archimedean real-closed ordered field, which is Cauchy complete in the order topology. Moreover, it has a subfield, isomorphic to the field of rational functions, whose elements naturally appear as edge weights in the theory of electrical networks (see e.g.

]
).

Firstly, we recall the definition and main notations for the Levi-Civita field
\mathcal{R}

]
,

]
,

Definition 3
.

A subset
Q
of the rational numbers
\mathbb{Q}
is called
left-finite
if for every number
r\in\mathbb{Q}
there are only finitely many elements of
Q
that are smaller than
r
.

Definition 4
.

[
13
]

We define the Levi-Civita field
\mathcal{R}
as a field of formal power series on
\epsilon
, where any non-zero element
a\in\mathcal{R}
can be written as

(12)

a=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}a_{i}\epsilon^{q_{i}},

with
a_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
,
a_{0}\neq 0
,
Q=\{q_{i}\}_{i=0}^{\infty}\subset\mathbb{Q}
being left-finite and
q_{i}
are pairwise different.

Addition and multiplication are defined naturally as for formal power series. The order is defined as follows:
a\succ 0
if
a_{0}>0
.

For

(13)

a=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}a_{i}\epsilon^{q_{i}}\mbox{ and }d=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}d_{i}\epsilon^{p_{i}}\

we write
a\approx d
(“
a
is
comparable
to
d
”) if
q_{0}=p_{0}
and
a_{0}=d_{0}
.
For the element
a=0
we put
q_{0}=\infty
. Therefore, two non-zero elements are comparable, if the first terms of their series coincide. Zero is comparable only to itself.

For the convenience, we state the following lemma, which follows immediately from the notion of comparabless.

Lemma 8
.

The following two statements hold:

(1)

Let
a\approx d
for
a,d\in\mathcal{R}\setminus\{0\}
, then
|a|\succ\epsilon|d|
. Moreover, from
a\approx d
follows
|a|\approx|d|
and
a^{m}\approx d^{m}
for any
m\in\mathbb{N}
.

(2)

Let
a\approx r
for
a\in\mathcal{R},r\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
. Then
|a|\succ\epsilon
and there exists
q>0
such that
|a-r|\prec\epsilon^{q}
.

(3)

If
a_{1}\approx d_{1}
and
a_{2}\approx d_{2}
, where
a_{1},a_{2},d_{1},d_{2}\in\mathcal{R}
, then
a_{1}a_{2}\approx d_{1}d_{2}
.

6.2.
Properties of
\mathcal{P}^{m}f
in the Levi-Civita field

Let us consider finite graphs over the Levi-Civita field. Two following Propositions describe completely the behaviour of eigenfunctions of
\mathcal{P}
under the action of
\mathcal{P}^{m},m\in\mathbb{N}
depending on eigenvalues.

Proposition 3
.

If
\alpha_{i}
is an eigenvalue of transition operator such that
\alpha_{i}\approx r
for some
r\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
and
\alpha_{i}\neq 1
then there is no limit
\mathcal{\alpha}_{i}^{m}v_{i}(x)
for
m\to~\infty,m\in\mathbb{N}
whenever
v_{i}(x)\neq 0
.
Moreover, if in addition
\alpha_{i}\neq-1
, then there are no partial limits.

Proof.

Firstly the case
\alpha_{i}=~-1
is obvious, since partial limits for
\alpha_{i}=~-1
exist for the subsequences
\mathcal{\alpha}_{i}^{2m}v_{i}(x)
and
\mathcal{\alpha}_{i}^{2m+1}v_{i}(x)
and limit does not exists whenever
v_{i}(x)\neq 0
. Therefore, let further in the proof
a_{i}\neq\pm 1
.

Let us point out that
|r|\leq 1
follows from
|\alpha_{i}|\preceq 1
.

For any two elements of sequence
\alpha_{i}^{m}v_{i}(x)
and
\alpha_{i}^{m+l}v_{i}(x)
, where
l\in\mathbb{N}

we have

|\alpha_{i}^{m}v_{i}(x)-\alpha_{i}^{m+l}v_{i}(x)|=|\alpha_{i}^{m}||1-\alpha_{i}^{l}||v_{i}(x)|.

For the first multiplayer
|\alpha_{i}^{m}|\approx|r^{m}|
holds and
|\alpha_{i}^{m}|\succ\epsilon
due to Lemma
8
. Further we have several cases:

(1)

for
r^{l}\neq 1
, we have
|1-\alpha_{i}^{l}|\approx|1-r^{l}|\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
and we obtain

(14)

|\alpha_{i}^{m}v_{i}(x)-\alpha_{i}^{m+l}v_{i}(x)|\succ{\epsilon}^{2}|v_{i}(x)|.

due to Lemma
8
(2).

(2)

for
r^{l}=1
(i. e.
r=1
or
r=-1
with
l
being even) we have

|1-~\alpha_{i}^{l}|\approx~|\alpha_{i,1}lr^{l-1}\epsilon^{q_{1}}|\mbox{ for }\alpha_{i}=r+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{i,j}\epsilon^{q_{j}},\mbox{ with }\alpha_{i,1}\neq 0

and

|1-~\alpha_{i}^{l}|\succ\epsilon^{q_{1}+1}|\alpha_{i,1}lr^{l-1}|
due to Lemma
8
(1),. Therefore,

|\alpha_{i}^{m}v_{i}(x)-\alpha_{i}^{m+l}v_{i}(x)|\succ{\epsilon}^{2+q_{1}}|\alpha_{i,1}lr^{l-1}||v_{i}(x)|

and again since
\alpha_{i,1}\neq 0
we obtain

(15)

|\alpha_{i}^{m}v_{i}(x)-\alpha_{i}^{m+l}v_{i}(x)|\succ{\epsilon}^{3+q_{1}}|v_{i}(x)|.

From (
14
) and (
15
) we obtain that for any
m,l\in\mathbb{N}
holds

|\alpha_{i}^{m}v_{i}(x)-\alpha_{i}^{m+l}v_{i}(x)|\succ{\epsilon}^{3+q_{1}}|v_{i}(x)|,

i.e. the sequence
\alpha_{i}^{m}v_{i}(x)
has no Cauchy subsequences.
∎

Proposition 4
.

If
\alpha_{i}
is an eigenvalue of transition operator such that
\alpha_{i}\approx~r\epsilon^{q}
, for some
r\in\mathbb{R},q>0,q\in\mathbb{Q}\cup\{\infty\}
then there is a limit
\mathcal{\alpha}_{i}^{m}v_{i}(x)
for
m\to~\infty,m\in\mathbb{N}
for any vertex
x\in V
.

Proof.

Note that we also include here the case
\alpha_{i}=0
(
\Leftrightarrow\alpha_{i}\approx 0
), since in this case
\alpha_{i}^{m}v_{i}(x)=0\to 0
for
m\in\mathbb{N}
.

Therefore, further in the proof we assume that
r\neq 0
. We have

|\alpha_{i}^{m}v_{i}(x)|\approx|r^{m}\epsilon^{qm}v_{i}(x)|.

Let
\displaystyle v_{i}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}v_{i,j}\epsilon^{t_{j}}
. Then

|\alpha_{i}^{m}v_{i}(x)|=|r^{m}||v_{i,0}(x)|\epsilon^{qm+t_{0}}+o(\epsilon^{qm+t_{0}}),

where
o(\epsilon^{p})
stays for terms with powers of
\epsilon
, higher then
p
. Therefore, for any
a\succ 0,a=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}a_{i}\epsilon^{q_{i}}\in\mathcal{R}

we obtain
{|\alpha_{i}^{m}v_{i}(x)|\prec a}
taking
m
large enough (such that
qm+t_{0}>q_{0}
).
∎

Now we present convergence results in terms of Cheeger constant
h
defined by (
9
).

Theorem 6
.

Let
V
be a bipartite graph with
\#V>2
over the Levi-Civita field.
Let
h\approx 1
. Then for any
f\in\mathfrak{F}
we have
\mathcal{P}^{2m}f\to\overline{f}
, where

\overline{f}=\dfrac{2}{b(V)}\begin{cases}\sum_{y\in V_{1}}f(y)b(y),\;x\in V_{1},\\
\sum_{y\in V_{2}}f(y)b(y),\;x\in V_{2}.\end{cases}

Proof.

Since
\#V>2
, the bipartite graph is non-complete and
\alpha_{1}\succeq 0
. Since
h\preceq 1
, from
h^{2}\approx 1
we get
1-h^{2}=|1-h^{2}|\prec\epsilon^{q}
for some
q>0
due to Lemma
8
(2) and the convergence follows from Corollary
4
, since

\|\mathcal{P}^{2m}-\overline{f}\|\prec\epsilon^{qm}\|f\|\prec\epsilon^{qm+t_{0}}\varphi_{0}\mbox{ for }\|f\|=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\varphi_{i}\epsilon^{t_{i}}.

Therefore, for any
a\succ 0,a=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}a_{i}\epsilon^{q_{i}}\in\mathcal{R}

we obtain
{\|\mathcal{P}^{2m}-\overline{f}\|\prec a}
taking
m
large enough (such that
qm+t_{0}>q_{0}
).
∎

Theorem 7
.

Let
V
be a non-complete non-bipartite graph with
\#V=n>1
over the Levi-Civita field. Let
h\approx 1
. Then for any
f\in\lspan(\{v_{i}\mid\alpha_{i}\succ 0,i=\overline{0,n-1}\})
we have
\mathcal{P}^{m}f\to\widetilde{f}
, where

\widetilde{f}(x)=\dfrac{1}{b(V)}\sum_{y\in V}f(y)b(y),\;x\in V.

Proof.

Follows from Theorem
3
by Corollary
2
, similarly to the proof of Theorem
6
.
∎
