Concave tents: a new tool for constructing concave reformulations of a large class of nonconvex optimization problems
Markus Gabl

Abstract
Optimizing a nonlinear function over nonconvex sets is challenging since solving convex relaxations may lead to substantial relaxation gaps and infeasible solutions that must be "rounded" to feasible ones, often with uncontrollable losses in objective function performance. For this reason, these convex hulls are especially useful if the objective function is linear or even concave, since concave optimization is invariant to taking the convex hull of the feasible set. Motivated by this observation, we propose the notion of concave tents, which are concave approximations of the original objective function that agree with this objective function on the feasible set, and allow for concave reformulations of the problem. Concave tents, therefore, are special cases of concave extensions, which were originally introduced by Crama 1993. In this text, we derive such concave tents for a large class of objective functions as the optimal value functions of conic optimization problems. Hence, evaluating our concave tents requires solving a conic problem. Interestingly, we can find supergradients by solving the conic dual problem, so that differentiation is of the same complexity as evaluation. For feasible sets that are contained in the extreme points of their convex hull, we construct these concave tents in the original space of variables. For general feasible sets, we propose a double lifting strategy, where the original optimization problem is lifted into a higher-dimensional space in which the concave tent can be constructed with a similar effort. We investigate the relation of the so-constructed concave tents to concave envelopes and naive concave tents based on concave quadratic updates. Based on these ideas, we propose a primal heuristic for a class of robust discrete quadratic optimization problems that can be used instead of classical rounding techniques. Numerical experiments suggest that our techniques can be beneficial as an upper-bounding procedure in a branch-and-bound solution scheme.



Keywords:
Nonconvex Optimization, Conic Optimization, Copositive Optimization, Global Optimization

Introduction

In this paper, we consider the optimization problem

\displaystyle\inf_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{f(\mathbf{x})\colon\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{X}}\right\},

(1)

where
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{\infty,-\infty\right\}
is a nonlinear function and
{\mathcal{X}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a compact set, and both may be nonconvex. This is an important yet challenging problem class. Usually, it is tackled via global optimization techniques, that vary depending on the structure of
{\mathcal{X}}
and
f
. If
f
were linear, one could solve the problem as a convex optimization problem if the convex hull of
{\mathcal{X}}
, which is also a compact set denoted by
\conv({\mathcal{X}})
, can be handled in a tractable manner. For example, if
{\mathcal{X}}\subseteq\left\{0,1\right\}^{n}
, then the convex hull has a tractable polyhedral description in many interesting cases and there are many techniques to approximate it algorithmically. However, already in the nonlinear convex case, and even if a full description of the convex hull of
{\mathcal{X}}
is available, the resulting convex relaxation can in general only deliver a lower bound, since a convex nonlinear function may attain solutions at points in
\conv({\mathcal{X}})\setminus{\mathcal{X}}
. In addition to the problem of potentially weak bounds, projecting the optimal solution of the relaxation onto
{\mathcal{X}}
, for example via rounding, may introduce a loss in performance that can be hard to control.

However, in this text, we want to go another route, where we replace
f
with a function
g
that is concave on
\conv({\mathcal{X}})
but agrees with
f
on
{\mathcal{X}}
, which is called a
concave tent
(see
[
20
,
46
]
). Concave functions attain their optimum over compact convex sets at extreme points of these sets, so that for a general concave function
\hat{g}
that overestimates
f
on
{\mathcal{X}}
we have that

\displaystyle\inf_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{f(\mathbf{x})\colon\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{X}}\right\}\leq\inf_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{\hat{g}(\mathbf{x})\colon\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{X}}\right\}=\inf_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{\hat{g}(\mathbf{x})\colon\mathbf{x}\in\conv({\mathcal{X}})\right\},

and we will show that the mere existence of a concave tent
g
of
f
over
{\mathcal{X}}
guarantees that the gap can be closed for
\hat{g}=g
. Hence, we reformulate nonconvex minimization over a nonconvex set into concave minimization over a convex set. Both, of course, can be hard. Nonetheless, the transformation allows for a different set of solution strategies to be applied that can take advantage of optimization techniques over
\conv({\mathcal{X}})
and certain properties of
g
. We will particularly be interested in using these reformulations for the sake of producing upper bounds, which are very useful in global optimization procedures since they often allow for the elimination of potential solution candidates. Obtaining such a concave tent seems elusive at first, but we will show that it can be constructed for a large class of choices of
f
and
{\mathcal{X}}
by exploiting conic optimization theory and convex geometry.

Concave tents are special cases of so-called
concave extensions
. These are concave overestimators of
f
which are exact on pre-specified subsets of a convex domain. They were already introduced in
[
20
,
46
]
and in
Section

3
will discuss more closely how concave tents relate to concave extensions. However, our exposition offers a new perspective on this topic that differs significantly from earlier treatments in
[
20
,
46
]
. Indeed, their approach sought to generate relaxations and, therefore, lower bounds of (
1
), by replacing

with a convex function that is identical to
f
on certain subsets of its domain. Hence,
[
46
]
spoke of convex (rather than concave) extensions (i.e. convex underestimators that are exact on a pre-specified subset of a convex domain), while
[
20
]
dealt with concave extensions but considered maximization problems. Their idea was, that a convex underestimator that is exact on certain points of the domain should yield strong relaxations by design. To the contrary, we build concave reformulations in an effort to construct upper bounds, harnessing the useful properties of concave functions that let us exploit knowledge on
\conv({\mathcal{X}})
.

This shift in perspective opens up a new set of techniques to approach (
1
), namely concave optimization, i.e. minimizing a concave function over a convex set. This class of problems is NP-hard, as it contains NP-hard special cases (see e.g.
[

31

[

]
concerns zero-one integer programming, where the binarity constraint on the decision

can be relaxed to the unit box constraint after the objective is augmented by a nonnegative concave quadratic term which vanishes at the extreme points of the unit box, hence preserving the global optimum. In
[

,

[

An important consideration that merits the shift to a concave optimization perspective is the elevated role of convex hulls of nonconvex sets. These feature prominently in the global optimization and, specifically, mixed integer linear optimization literature, see e.g.
[

Approaches involving convex hulls are regularly used in conjunction with linearization techniques. One of the most popular instances of such machinery is given by copositive reformulations of nonconvex quadratically constrained quadratic problems. These rest on the identity

\displaystyle\inf_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{Q}}\mathbf{x}+2{\mathbf{q}}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}\colon\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{X}}\right\}=\inf_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\,{\mathsf{X}}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{n}}\left\{\mathrm{Tr}({\mathsf{Q}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{X}})+2{\mathbf{q}}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}\colon\begin{bmatrix}1&\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}\\

\displaystyle{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{X}})\coloneqq\mathrm{clconv}\left\{\left[\begin{array}[]{c}1\\
\mathbf{x}\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{c}1\\
\mathbf{x}\end{array}\right]^{\mathsf{T}}:\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{X}}\right\}\subseteq{\mathcal{S}}^{n+1}\,,

yields the same optimal value so that the relaxation gap is nullified. The key difficulty of this approach is the characterization of
{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{X}})
, which is only known for special instances of
{\mathcal{X}}
, see for example
[
5
,
14
,
15
,
16
,
22
,
17
,

,
51
]
. These results have been applied, for example, to fractional optimization
[
3
,
30
]
and stochastic 0-1 optimization
[
37
]
.

Recently,
{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{X}})
has also been used to generate underestimators of optimal value functions of quadratically constrained quadratic problems in
[
25
]
, which presents a continuation of ideas earlier proposed in
[
52
,
4
]
. We want to highlight at this point, that this set will play a central role in our exposition as well. We will several times invoke
[

{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{X}})
is compact even if the "

" operator were omitted from its definition so that in that case every element in
{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{X}})
is a convex combination of matrices of the form
\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}^{\mathsf{T}}\colon\mathbf{y}\in\left\{1\right\}\times{\mathcal{X}}
, and not merely a clusterpoint of such convex combinations.

2
Contribution

This text aims at achieving the following contributions:

•

We formally introduce the notion of concave tents and discuss some necessary and sufficient conditions for their existence, as well as their relations to general concave extensions. We show that nonconvex optimization over a nonconvex set is in a certain sense equivalent to optimizing the respective concave tent, if it exists, over the convex hull of that set.

•

We will construct concave tents of functions of a certain class of sets that are subsets of the extreme points of their convex hull. These concave tents will be characterized as the optimal value functions of conic optimization problems related to copositive optimization theory.

•

For the case of general feasible sets we employ a double lifting strategy, which can be interpreted as a generalization of the traditional copositive optimization paradigm.

•

We discuss the quality of our concave tents in comparison to the concave envelope and a classical construction of concave tents based on concave quadratic updates, and show that our concave tents are sandwiched between the two and that the lower bound that is the concave envelope can be attained.

•

We will discuss obtaining
\epsilon
-supergradients of our concave tents based on conic duality theory.

•

We then use our theory to construct a primal heuristic for a class of robust quadratic discrete optimization problems. Numerical experiments demonstrate that this heuristic can be useful in a branch and bound (B&B) solution scheme.

3
Definition, existence, and implication for optimization

We start by formally defining what we mean by a concave tent.

Definition 1
.

Let
{\mathcal{X}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
and let
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a function. Then a concave tent of
f
over
{\mathcal{X}}
is any function
g\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that

a)

g
is concave over
\conv({\mathcal{X}})
, and

b)

f(\mathbf{x})=g(\mathbf{x})
for all
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{X}}
.

The above definition is a specialization of the definition of so-called concave extensions, originally introduced in
[
20
,
46
]
, which are defined as follows. Let
{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a convex set and let
{\mathcal{X}}\subseteq{\mathcal{C}}
. A concave extension of a function
f\colon{\mathcal{X}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
over
{\mathcal{C}}
is any concave function
g\colon{\mathcal{C}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that
g(\mathbf{x})=f(\mathbf{x}),\ \forall\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{X}}
(see
[
46
, Definition 1.]
). The definition of a concave tent can be recovered by requiring
{\mathcal{C}}=\conv({\mathcal{X}})
. In addition, we only require
g
to be concave on
\conv({\mathcal{X}})
, but that is a nuance of little consequence. Similarly, our nomenclature differs slightly as we speak of concave tents over
{\mathcal{X}}
, rather than over
{\mathcal{C}}=\conv({\mathcal{X}})
. However, this seemingly minor specialization is important since concave tents allow us to construct concave reformulations of (
1
). This represents a significant departure from the originally intended use of concave extensions, whose convex counterparts were introduced to build convex relaxations, where the idea was to have a relaxation that is exact at least in some parts of the domain. Nonetheless, many theoretical aspects of concave extensions, mainly explored in
[
46
]
, can be transferred to the realm of concave tents. Still, in the remainder of this section, we will provide a self-contained discussion of some of those aspects that is geared towards preparing the reader for the rest of the present text.

Firstly, note that concave tents are not necessarily concave functions, as they can be nonconcave outside of
\conv({\mathcal{X}})
, and that they overestimate
f
on
{\mathcal{X}}
since there they are equal, but not necessarily over
\conv({\mathcal{X}})
unless
f
is convex.
Now, we want to convince the reader that the above concept is meaningfully distinct from general concave overestimators as well as from the concave envelope, i.e. the smallest concave overestimator of that function over
{\mathcal{X}}
. Indeed, an arbitrary concave overestimator may fail to meet the requirement b) in
Definition

1
. Take as an example the function that is
\infty
everywhere, which is concave over any set
{\mathcal{X}}
but differs from any function
f
finite on that set. On the other hand, consider the concave envelope of
f(\mathbf{x})=x^{2}
over the set
{\mathcal{X}}=\left\{-1,1\right\}
, which is just the function that is a constant equal to one within the interval
[0,1]
and
-\infty
everywhere else. It is a concave tent by
Definition

1
, but that is also the case for the function that we obtain by adding to this concave envelope the function
1-x^{2}
, which also demonstrates that concave tents are not unique. However, we still can show the following simple relation between concave tents and concave envelopes.

Proposition 1
.

If a concave tent of a function
f
over a set
{\mathcal{X}}
exists, then the concave envelope of
f
over
{\mathcal{X}}
is a concave tent.

Proof.

Let
h
be the hypothesized concave envelope and
g
be an arbitrary concave tent, then for any
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{X}}
we have
f(\mathbf{x})\leq h(\mathbf{x})\leq g(\mathbf{x})=f(\mathbf{x})
, since
h
is the smallest concave overestimator of
f
on
{\mathcal{X}}
, hence also smaller than
g
on that domain. Thus,
h
is a concave function that agrees with
f
over
{\mathcal{X}}
, i.e. it is a concave tent.
∎

It is, however, easy to see that a concave tent does not necessarily exist. For example, consider
f(x)=\max\left\{-x,x-1\right\},\ {\mathcal{X}}\coloneqq\left\{0,1/2,1\right\}
. We see that a concave tent is impossible since there is a point
x=1/2
so that any function
g
that agrees with
f
on
{\mathcal{X}}
fulfills
-0.5=g(1/2)=g((1/2)0+(1/2)1)<1/2g(0)+1/2g(1)=0
so that
g
cannot be concave over
\conv({\mathcal{X}})
.

Later in the text, we will derive concave tents for various configurations of
{\mathcal{X}}
and
f
so that existence is guaranteed in interesting cases. It is nonetheless fruitful to examine on a more fundamental level what the necessary and sufficient conditions for its existence are. Our example in the previous paragraph illustrates the critical necessary condition for the existence of a concave tent discussed in the result below:

Proposition 2
.

Let
f
and
{\mathcal{X}}
be as in
Definition

1
, and consider the following condition

\displaystyle{\mathcal{X}}\ni\mathbf{x}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i},\ \bm{\lambda}\in\Delta_{k},\ \mathbf{x}_{i}\in{\mathcal{X}},\ i\in[1\!:\!k]\implies f(\mathbf{x})\geq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}f(\mathbf{x}_{i}).

(3)

Then, (
3
) is necessary for the existence of a concave tent of
f
over
{\mathcal{X}}
. If
{\mathcal{X}}
is compact and
f
is continuous over
{\mathcal{X}}
, then (
3

Proof.

To prove necessity, assume there was such an

that is a convex combination of

with weights
\bm{\lambda}\in\Delta_{k}

. Then, a function that agrees with
f

{\mathcal{X}}
cannot be concave, so a concave tent cannot exist.
To argue sufficiency, we consider the graph of
f

{\mathcal{X}}
, i.e.

{\mathcal{F}}\coloneqq\left\{[f(\mathbf{x}),\ \mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}]^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\colon\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{X}}\right\}.

By compactness of

and continuity of

\displaystyle\hskip-14.22636ptg(\mathbf{x})\coloneqq\sup_{\mu}\left\{\mu\colon\begin{bmatrix}\mu\\

where we can set

and the supremum is attained by the compactness of

. Assume to the contrary that there is an

so that

We always have

while

by attainment of the supremum, in contradiction to (

and hence a concave tent.
∎

The requirements on
f
and
{\mathcal{X}}
for the necessary condition to be sufficient are quite drastic, and we hypothesize that much weaker assumptions could suffice. Indeed,

discusses a much broader necessary and sufficient condition for the existence of a concave extensions of

(not necessarily equal to

). However, for the purposes of this text the above conditions suffice. Also, in the context of optimization such regularizations are quite common and we do not wish to delve too deep into convex analysis at this point. Now, we merely like to mention that the necessary condition is trivially fulfilled if there is no point in
{\mathcal{X}}
that is a convex combination of other points in
{\mathcal{X}}
. This is the case if
{\mathcal{X}}
is contained in the set of extreme points in

, which is the setup we will be concerned with exclusively. However, in

Theorem 3
.

Let
f
and
{\mathcal{X}}
be as in
Definition

1
. If a concave tent of
f
over
{\mathcal{X}}
, say

, exists, then for the optimization problems

\inf_{\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{X}}}\left\{f(\mathbf{x})\right\}\mbox{ and }\inf_{\mathbf{x}\in\conv({\mathcal{X}})}\left\{g(\mathbf{x})\right\}
the following hold:

a)

Every strict local minimizer of the latter problem is a strict local minimizer of the former.

b)

We have
\inf_{\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{X}}}\left\{f(\mathbf{x})\right\}=\inf_{\mathbf{x}\in\conv({\mathcal{X}})}\left\{g(\mathbf{x})\right\}
, and they have the same global minimizers.

Proof.

We first prove a), so let
\mathbf{x}\in\conv({\mathcal{X}})
be a local minimizer of the concave optimization problem, i.e.
g(\mathbf{x})<g(\mathbf{y}),\ \forall\mathbf{y}\in\left(\conv({\mathcal{X}})\cap{\mathcal{B}}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})\right)\setminus\left\{\mathbf{x}\right\}

\varepsilon>0

\mathbf{x}
is an extreme point of
\conv({\mathcal{X}})
, then
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{X}}
and we get

as desired, since
f(\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{y}),\ \forall\mathbf{y}\in{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{X}}\cap{\mathcal{B}}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})\subseteq\conv({\mathcal{X}})\cap{\mathcal{B}}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})
. So assume
\mathbf{x}
is not an extreme point. Then there are
\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\conv({\mathcal{X}})\cap{\mathcal{B}}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})\setminus\left\{\mathbf{x}\right\},\ i\in[1\!:\!k],\ \bm{\lambda}\in\Delta_{k}
such that
\mathbf{x}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}
(such points exist in
\conv({\mathcal{X}})\setminus\left\{\mathbf{x}\right\}
and can be contracted towards
\mathbf{x}
until they become members of
{\mathcal{B}}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})
).
We have
\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x})\geq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}g(\mathbf{x}_{i})\geq g(\mathbf{x}_{i^{*}})
for some
i^{*}\in[1\!:\!k]
since an average cannot be smaller than its smallest constituent, but this is in contradiction to strict local optimality. To prove b), if
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{X}}
then
f(\mathbf{x})=g(\mathbf{x})
and
\mathbf{x}\in\conv({\mathcal{X}})
so that "
\geq
" is immediate. Conversely, if
\mathbf{x}\in\conv({\mathcal{X}})\setminus{\mathcal{X}}
then
\mathbf{x}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i},\ \bm{\lambda}\in\Delta_{k},\ \mathbf{x}_{i}\in{\mathcal{X}},\ i\in[1\!:\!k]
and
g(\mathbf{x})=g(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i})\geq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}g(\mathbf{x}_{i})
and there must be at least one
\mathbf{x}_{i}\in{\mathcal{X}}
so that
g(\mathbf{x})\geq g(\mathbf{x}_{i})=f(\mathbf{x}_{i})
. Thus, the infima are identical and the final statement follows since global minimizers of the concave problem, if they exist, are attained in
{\mathcal{X}}
by the above argument.
∎

Remark
.

In case
{\mathcal{X}}\subseteq\left\{0,1\right\}^{n}
, the definition of strict local minimizer can be applied, but is not meaningful as every point in
{\mathcal{X}}
would be a strict local minimizer. However, the above theorem is not restricted to such sets and point b) is still meaningful even for discrete sets, so we chose not to exclude, nor treat separately the discrete case.

The core message of this theorem is the following: concave tents allow us to employ methods from concave minimization to tackle
\inf_{\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{X}}}f(\mathbf{x})
, where we may exploit knowledge on
\conv({\mathcal{X}})
. Optimizing the concave tent globally solves the original problem, and strict local solutions yield upper bounds that cannot be improved locally. Thus, well-studied concave optimization tools as well as results on
\conv({\mathcal{X}})
can potentially be applied in a new area.

4
A versatile class of functions

The mere existence of a concave tent does not imply that it can be easily constructed. Indeed, for this purpose, we have to make further assumptions on the feasible set
{\mathcal{X}}
and the function
f
, the latter of which will be discussed in this section. Namely, henceforth we focus on functions that fulfill the following:

Assumption 1
.

The objective function
f
can be written as

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{x})=h(\mathbf{x})+\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{A}}\mathbf{x}+2\mathbf{a}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}+\max_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}}\left\{2\mathbf{u}^{\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{B}}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{u}^{\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{C}}\mathbf{u}+2\mathbf{c}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{u}\colon\mathbf{u}\in{\mathcal{U}}\right\},

for a closed proper concave function
h
, a compact convex nonempty set
{\mathcal{U}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{q}
, matrices
{\mathsf{A}}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{n},\ {\mathsf{B}}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times n},\ {\mathsf{C}}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{q}
and vectors
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\ \mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
.

Let us briefly discuss the versatility of this class of functions. Firstly, note that the supremum term is always a closed convex function of
\mathbf{x}
since it is the pointwise supremum of functions that are linear in
\mathbf{x}
. Indeed, by
[
42
, Theorem 12.1.]
any closed, convex function can be written as a pointwise supremum of linear functions so that
Assumption

1
excludes convex functions that are not closed, which play a minor role in convex optimization anyway. This also might give the appearance that including the term
\mathbf{u}^{\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{C}}\mathbf{u}
is spurious, however, some convex functions are naturally given is in this form, e.g., those that appear in robust optimization as discussed shortly.

For an arbitrary closed convex function
c
, we can often recover a representation that conforms with
Assumption

1
. From the convex conjugate of a convex function
c
given by
c^{*}(\mathbf{u})=\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{\mathbf{u}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}-c(\mathbf{x})\right\}
we get its biconjugate characterization

\displaystyle\hskip-28.45274ptc(\mathbf{x})=\sup_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{u}-c^{*}(\mathbf{u})\right\}=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\\
u_{0}\in\mathbb{R}\end{subarray}}\left\{\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{u}-u_{0}\colon\left[\mathbf{u}^{\mathsf{T}},u_{0}\right]\in{\mathcal{U}}\right\},\ {\mathcal{U}}\coloneqq\left\{\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{u}\\
u_{0}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\colon f^{*}(\mathbf{u})\leq u_{0}\right\},

so that

f(\mathbf{x})\coloneqq h(\mathbf{x})+\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{A}}\mathbf{x}+2\mathbf{a}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}+c(\mathbf{x})
can be rewritten in the required form. However, the set
{\mathcal{U}}
as defined above is not compact since
u_{0}
is not constrained from above and even if it were, the effective domain of
c^{*}
may be unbounded. We address this issue in greater detail in
Appendix

A
, where we will show how to replace
c
by surrogate function, the Pasch-Hausdorff envelope, that agrees with
f
on
{\mathcal{X}}
and conforms with
Assumption

1
. Consequently, the model in
Assumption

1
encompasses many instances of difference-of-convex optimization.

The second source of minimization problems where the functions conform with
Assumption

1
stems from robust optimization especially robust optimization with quadratic indices. In such models,
\mathbf{u}
would take the role of the uncertainty parameter and
{\mathcal{U}}
the role of the uncertainty set. The special case
f(\mathbf{x})\coloneqq\sup_{\mathbf{u}\in{\mathcal{U}}}\left\{(\mathbf{a}+{\mathsf{B}}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{u})\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{c}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{u}\right\}
is the standard worst-case evaluation in robust linear optimization (see e.g.
[
8
]
). Examples of robust optimization models where the uncertainty parameter appears quadratically are given by adjustable robust optimization under quadratic decision rules or under affine decision rules but with uncertain recourse and by robust quadratic optimization (see e.g.
[
9
]
and
[
13
]
and references therein).

To the best of our knowledge an exact characterization of functions that fulfill
Assumption

1
is not known and we defer an exhaustive investigation to future research and close this section with the following useful result:

Proposition 4
.

Under
Assumption

1
, the supremum in the definition of
f
is locally Lipschitz in
\mathbf{x}
, and
f
itself is upper semicontinuous and nowhere equal to
\infty
.

Proof.

The first statement follows from
[
19
, Theorem (2.1)]
. Thus,
f
is the sum of a continuous function, a quadratic function and the closed proper concave function
h
, hence itself upper semicontinuous and smaller than
\infty
everywhere.
∎

5
Constructing concave tents via conic optimization

In this section, we will derive concave tents as optimal value functions of certain conic optimization problems. We divide our discussion into three parts:
to ease the reader into the subject, we will present a construction of concave tents for functions that are simpler than the ones presented in
Assumption

1
and
{\mathcal{X}}\subseteq\left\{0,1\right\}^{n}
. After that, we will derive concave tents where
f
conforms with
Assumption

1
and
{\mathcal{X}}
is, more generally, a set that contains only extreme points of its convex hull. Finally, we will present a double lifting strategy that will allow us to bypass the restrictions of the necessary condition in
Proposition

2
so that we can construct concave reformulations over general sets
{\mathcal{X}}
.

5.1
Concave tents of convex functions over subsets of
\left\{0,1\right\}^{n}

In this subsection, we will discuss concave tents over
{\mathcal{X}}\subseteq\left\{0,1\right\}^{n}
and functions that are of the form
f(\mathbf{x})=\sup_{\mathbf{u}\in{\mathcal{U}}}\mathbf{u}^{\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{B}}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{c}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{u}
for some compact convex
{\mathcal{U}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{q}
, i.e. we restrict the discussion to closed convex functions. Note, that due to compactness we can assume w.l.o.g. that
\|\mathbf{u}\|^{2}\leq 1,\ \forall\mathbf{u}\in{\mathcal{U}}
so that
{\mathsf{I}}\bullet{\mathsf{U}}\leq 1
whenever
(\mathbf{u},{\mathsf{U}})\in{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{U}})
. We will make use of the following two lemmas:

Lemma 5
.

Let
g\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be given by

g(\mathbf{x})\coloneqq\sup_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\left\{\mathbf{p}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}+{\mathbf{q}}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{y}\colon\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}},&\mathbf{y}^{\mathsf{T}}\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}}\in{\mathcal{P}}\right\},

for some vectors
\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
{\mathbf{q}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
and a convex set
{\mathcal{P}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n+k}
, then
g
is a concave function.

Proof.

Assume that
\left\{\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2}\right\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

\exists\mathbf{y}_{i}\colon\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}},&\mathbf{y}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}}\in{\mathcal{P}},\ i=1,2.

Then, by convexity
\mathbf{y}(\lambda)\coloneqq\lambda\mathbf{y}_{1}+(1-\lambda)\mathbf{y}_{2}
for
\lambda\in[0,1],
is a feasible solution for
g(\mathbf{x}(\lambda))
where
\mathbf{x}(\lambda)\coloneqq\lambda\mathbf{x}_{1}+(1-\lambda)\mathbf{x}_{2}
and, thus, yields a lower bound so that

g(\mathbf{x}(\lambda))=g(\lambda\mathbf{x}_{1}+(1-\lambda)\mathbf{x}_{2})\geq\mathbf{p}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}(\lambda)+{\mathbf{q}}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{y}(\lambda)=\lambda g(\mathbf{x}_{1})+(1-\lambda)g(\mathbf{x}_{2}).

If at least one of
\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2}
is such that no suitable
\mathbf{y}_{i},\ i=1,2
exists then the inequality holds trivially, since the respective value for
g
is
-\infty
. As an alternative geometrical argument one can construct the hypograph of
g
defined by the inequality
t\leq g(\mathbf{x})
(where
t
is the vertical coordinate) as the hypograph
t\leq\mathbf{p}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}+{\mathbf{q}}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{y}
of the objective function over
{\mathcal{P}}
projected onto the
(\mathbf{x},t)
-coordinates. The details are left to the reader.
∎

Lemma 6
.

The following holds

a)

For
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\ \left\{{\mathsf{A}},{\mathsf{B}}\right\}\subset{\mathcal{S}}_{+}^{n}
we have that
\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}={\mathsf{A}}+{\mathsf{B}}
implies
{\mathsf{A}}=\alpha\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}},\ {\mathsf{B}}=\beta\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}
where
\alpha,\ \beta
are nonnegative and
\alpha+\beta=1
.

b)

For
\left\{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\right\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
we have that
\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}=\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}^{\mathsf{T}}
implies
\mathbf{y}\in\left\{\mathbf{x},-\mathbf{x}\right\}
.

Proof.

These are well-known facts, that are proved in, for example,
[
13
, Proposition 11.]
.
∎

We are now ready to state the main theorem of this section.

Theorem 7
.

Let
f(\mathbf{x})=\sup_{\mathbf{u}\in{\mathcal{U}}}\mathbf{u}^{\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{B}}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{c}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{u}
, such that
{\mathcal{U}}\subseteq{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(\mathbf{o})\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{q}
is convex and compact and let
{\mathcal{X}}\subseteq\left\{0,1\right\}^{n}
. Further, let
{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq{\mathcal{S}}^{n+q+1}
be a closed convex set such that

{\mathcal{S}}_{+}^{n+q+1}\supseteq{\mathcal{C}}\supseteq{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{U}}\times{\mathcal{X}})
.
Define

\displaystyle g(\mathbf{x})\coloneqq\sup_{\Psi,\mathbf{u},{\mathsf{U}},{\mathsf{X}}}\left\{{\mathsf{B}}^{\mathsf{T}}\bullet\Psi+\mathbf{c}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{u}\colon\begin{pmatrix}1&\mathbf{u}^{\mathsf{T}}&\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}\\
\mathbf{u}&{\mathsf{U}}&\Psi^{\mathsf{T}}\\
\mathbf{x}&\Psi&{\mathsf{X}}\end{pmatrix}\in{\mathcal{C}},\ \mathbf{u}\in{\mathcal{U}},\ \begin{array}[]{r}\diag({\mathsf{X}})=\mathbf{x},\\
{\mathsf{I}}\bullet{\mathsf{U}}\leq 1,\end{array}\right\}.

Then
g
is a concave tent of
f
over
{\mathcal{X}}
.

Proof.

The concavity of
g
is a direct consequence of
Lemma

5
. To prove that
g
and
f
agree on
{\mathcal{X}}
, we will use the fact that both functions are given in terms of optimization problems, which we will show to be equivalent whenever
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{X}}
.
To prove "
\leq
", let
\mathbf{u}\in{\mathcal{U}}
and
\mathbf{y}\coloneqq\left(1,\mathbf{u}^{\mathsf{T}},\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}\right)
. Then
\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}^{\mathsf{T}}
is a feasible matrix for the problem defining
g
, where
\diag({\mathsf{X}})=\diag(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}})=\mathbf{x}
since
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{X}}
,
{\mathsf{I}}\bullet{\mathsf{U}}=\mathbf{u}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{u}\leq 1
since
\mathbf{u}\in{\mathcal{U}}\subseteq{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(\mathbf{o})
and
\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}^{\mathsf{T}}\in{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{U}}\times{\mathcal{X}})\subseteq{\mathcal{C}}
be the same inclusions. Finally,
\Psi=\mathbf{x}\mathbf{u}^{\mathsf{T}}
so that
for the objective function we get
{\mathsf{B}}^{\mathsf{T}}\bullet\Psi+\mathbf{c}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{u}={\mathsf{B}}^{\mathsf{T}}\bullet\mathbf{x}\mathbf{u}^{\mathsf{T}}+\mathbf{c}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{u}^{\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{B}}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{c}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{u}
as desired.

For the other direction let
\left(\Psi,\mathbf{u},{\mathsf{U}},{\mathsf{X}}\right)
be feasible for the conic problem for our chosen
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{X}}
. By feasibility, we know that

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}1&\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}\\
\mathbf{x}&{\mathsf{X}}\end{bmatrix}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{n+1}_{+}\implies{\mathsf{X}}-\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{+}^{n}\implies{\mathsf{X}}=\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}+{\mathsf{M}},\ {\mathsf{M}}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{+}^{n}.

Further, since
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{X}}\subseteq\left\{0,1\right\}^{n}
we have
\diag(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}})-\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{o}
. But then

\displaystyle\mathbf{o}=\diag({\mathsf{X}})-\mathbf{x}=\diag(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}+{\mathsf{M}})-\mathbf{x}=\diag(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}})-\mathbf{x}+\diag({\mathsf{M}})=\mathbf{o}+\diag({\mathsf{M}}),

so that
\diag({\mathsf{M}})=\mathbf{o}
, which together with
{\mathsf{M}}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{+}^{n}
implies
{\mathsf{M}}={\mathsf{O}}
and we get
{\mathsf{X}}=\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}
.
Further, since
{\mathcal{S}}_{+}^{n}=\conv\left(\left\{\mathbf{w}\mathbf{w}^{\mathsf{T}}\colon\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\right\}\right)
we know that

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}1&\mathbf{u}^{\mathsf{T}}&\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}\\
\mathbf{u}&{\mathsf{U}}&\Psi^{\mathsf{T}}\\
\mathbf{x}&\Psi&{\mathsf{X}}\end{bmatrix}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{i}^{2}&\lambda_{i}\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}&\lambda_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\\
\lambda_{i}\mathbf{u}_{i}&\mathbf{u}_{i}\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}&\mathbf{u}_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\\
\lambda_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}&\mathbf{v}_{i}\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}&\mathbf{v}_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\end{bmatrix}\mbox{ for some }\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{i}\\
\mathbf{u}_{i}\\
\mathbf{v}_{i}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+q+1},\ i\in[1\!:\!k].

(4)

After plugging in
{\mathsf{X}}=\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}
, inspecting the respective submatrix yields

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}1\\
\mathbf{x}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1\\
\mathbf{x}\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{i}\\
\mathbf{v}_{i}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{i}\\
\mathbf{v}_{i}\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}}\ \implies\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{i}\\
\mathbf{v}_{i}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{i}\\
\mathbf{v}_{i}\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}}=\mu_{i}\begin{bmatrix}1\\
\mathbf{x}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1\\
\mathbf{x}\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}},\ \bm{\mu}\in\Delta_{k},

by
Lemma

6
a). Then, we can see from
Lemma

6
b) that

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{i}\\
\mathbf{v}_{i}\end{bmatrix}=\pm\begin{bmatrix}\sqrt{\mu_{i}}\\
\sqrt{\mu_{i}}\mathbf{x}\end{bmatrix},\mbox{ so that }\ \mathbf{u}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\pm\sqrt{\mu_{i}}\mathbf{u}_{i},\mbox{ by (\ref{eqn:SDPDecomposition}),}

and we finally get
\Psi=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbf{v}_{i}\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\pm\sqrt{\mu_{i}}\mathbf{x}\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}=\mathbf{x}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\pm\sqrt{\mu_{i}}\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}=\mathbf{x}\mathbf{u}^{\mathsf{T}},

and
\mathbf{u}\in{\mathcal{U}}
by feasibility so that it is feasible for the problem defining
f
, with

{\mathsf{B}}^{\mathsf{T}}\bullet\Psi+\mathbf{c}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{u}={\mathsf{B}}^{\mathsf{T}}\bullet\mathbf{x}\mathbf{u}^{\mathsf{T}}+\mathbf{c}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{u}^{\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{B}}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{c}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{u}
, which completes the proof. ∎

We will now give an illustration of the above theorem in a simple example.

Example 1
.

Consider the following nonconvex optimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{x\in{\mathcal{X}}}\max_{(u_{1},u_{2})^{\mathsf{T}}\in{\mathcal{U}}}u_{1}+u_{2}x,\mbox{ with }{\mathcal{X}}\coloneqq\left\{0,1\right\},\ {\mathcal{U}}\coloneqq\left\{\begin{bmatrix}1\\
-8\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}-2\\
2\end{bmatrix}\right\}.

(5)

The inner maximization problem constitutes a convex function in
x
, say
f
, that is the pointwise maximum of two affine functions. It evaluates to
f(0)=1,\ f(1)=0
and
f(3/10)=-7/5
, which is its global minimum over
\conv({\mathcal{X}})=[0,1]
. By linearity with respect to
(u_{1},u_{2})
we can replace
{\mathcal{U}}
with its convex hull

\displaystyle\conv({\mathcal{U}})=\left\{\begin{bmatrix}u_{1}\\
u_{2}\end{bmatrix}=u\begin{bmatrix}1\\
-8\end{bmatrix}+(1-u)\begin{bmatrix}-2\\
2\end{bmatrix}\colon u\in[0,1]\right\},

so that we can substitute
(u_{1},u_{2})
via an affine expression in
u
after which (
5
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\left\{0,1\right\}}\max_{u\in[0,1]}3u+2x-10ux-2,

To construct a concave tent of
f
over
\left\{0,1\right\}
we need an appropriate subset
{\mathcal{C}}
of
{\mathcal{S}}^{3}_{+}
that is also a superset of
{\mathcal{G}}([0,1]\times\left\{0,1\right\})
. We propose

\displaystyle{\mathcal{C}}\coloneqq\left\{\begin{bmatrix}1&u&x\\
u&U&\psi\\
x&\psi&X\end{bmatrix}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{3}_{+},\colon(x,X)\in{\mathcal{G}}(\left\{0,1\right\}),\ (u,U)\in{\mathcal{G}}([0,1]),\ \psi\geq 0\right\},

since we have the simple characterizations (which are discussed for example in
[
16
]
):

\displaystyle{\mathcal{G}}(\left\{0,1\right\})=\left\{\begin{bmatrix}1&x\\
x&X\end{bmatrix}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{2}_{+}\colon\begin{array}[]{l}X\leq x\leq 1\\
X=x\end{array}\right\},\ {\mathcal{G}}([0,1])=\left\{\begin{bmatrix}1&u\\
u&U\end{bmatrix}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{2}_{+}\colon U\leq u\leq 1\right\},

and
\psi\geq 0
whenever
\psi=xu
with
x\in\left\{0,1\right\}
and
u\in[0,1]
so that, clearly, we have
{\mathcal{S}}^{3}_{+}\supseteq{\mathcal{C}}\supseteq{\mathcal{G}}([0,1]\times\left\{0,1\right\})
as required. In addition we have that
(u,U)\in{\mathcal{G}}([0,1])
implies
1\bullet U\leq 1
as required but the constraint is redundant on
{\mathcal{C}}
. We are now ready to construct our concave tent as

\displaystyle g(x)\coloneqq\sup_{u,U,\psi}\left\{3u+2x-10\psi-2\colon\begin{bmatrix}1&u&x\\
u&U&\psi\\
x&\psi&x\end{bmatrix}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{3}_{+},\ U\leq u\leq 1,\ 0\leq\psi\right\}.

(6)

Note, that due to the equality constraint in the description of
{\mathcal{G}}(\left\{0,1\right\})
we were able to eliminate
X
, rendering the respective constraints vacuous. In addition, the constraint
x\leq 1
is then implied by the psd (positive semidefinite) constraint, so that it can be omitted as well.

We will show that
g
is indeed a concave tent as proclaimed in
Theorem

7
. First, note that for any feasible solution, we can always increase
U
until it equals
u
since the matrix will not change definiteness. But then, the constraint
u\leq 1
is redundant since the psd constraint already implies
u\geq u^{2}
, as all principal minors must be nonnegative. We claim that the optimal solution is given by
u=1-x,\ \psi=0
with optimal value
1-x
. We only need to check the psd constraint to argue feasibility, which is easily done by observing that all principal minors are indeed nonnegative. To prove optimality we inspect the dual problem given by

\displaystyle\inf_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta,\epsilon,\eta}\left\{x(2\gamma+\eta+2)+\alpha-2\colon\begin{bmatrix}\alpha&\beta&\gamma\\
\beta&\delta&\epsilon\\
\gamma&\epsilon&\eta\end{bmatrix}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{3}_{+},\ 3+2\beta+\delta=0,\ 2\epsilon\leq 10\right\}.

(7)

By weak conic duality, it is sufficient to find a feasible dual solution that attains the proclaimed optimal value. Indeed, for
\alpha=\delta=\epsilon=\eta=3,\ \beta=\gamma=-3
, we find the constraints fulfilled, where positive definiteness can again be certified by the principal minors, and the optimal value is indeed
1-x
. This is not only a concave tent but it tracks the concave envelope of
f
over the unit interval so we could not improve the upper bounds if we strengthened
{\mathcal{C}}
. It is, however, interesting to see what would happen if the constraints were relaxed instead.

Indeed, if we merely omit the constraints
1\geq u\geq U
, a weaker concave tent would be given by

\displaystyle\tilde{g}(x)\coloneqq\sup_{u,U,\psi}\left\{3u+2x-10\psi-2\colon\begin{bmatrix}1&u&x\\
u&U&\psi\\
x&\psi&x\end{bmatrix}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{3}_{+}\colon U\leq 1,\ 0\leq\psi\right\}.

(8)

To solve the sdp, again consider, that it is always possible to set
U=1
without changing the optimal value. Also, since
\psi=0
was optimal for
g
, and the eliminated constraint did not involve
\psi
we can infer that this choice for
\psi
is still optimal. Then, we relax the psd condition to merely requiring that the determinant be nonnegative, i.e.
x-u^{2}x-x^{2}\geq 0
.
Since
x\geq 0
, the latter constraint reduces to
1-x\geq u^{2}
,
so that the optimal choice for the relaxation is given by
u=\sqrt{1-x}
. However, it is easily checked, again by enumeration of all principal minors, that this solution is feasible for (
8
) and, hence, optimal. In total, we have shown that
\tilde{g}(x)=3\sqrt{1-x}+2x-2
, which is indeed concave but strictly greater than
g
over the unit interval, except for the endpoints, where both functions are equal to
f
.

5.2
Concave tents over sets of extreme points

In this section, we will generalize and strengthen our results from the previous section. The sufficient conditions in the theorem below and their relation to the conditions in
Proposition

2
are discussed in the sequel.

Theorem 8
.

Let
f
be such that
Assumption

1
holds and let
{\mathcal{X}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Further, let
{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq{\mathcal{S}}^{n+q+1}
be a closed convex set such that

{\mathcal{S}}^{n+q+1}_{+}\supseteq{\mathcal{C}}\supseteq{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{U}}\times{\mathcal{X}}).

Finally let
{\mathcal{A}}\colon{\mathcal{S}}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a linear function and
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, such that

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{bmatrix}1&\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}\\
\mathbf{x}&{\mathsf{X}}\end{bmatrix}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{n+1}_{+}\colon{\mathcal{A}}({\mathsf{X}},\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{b}\right\}

\displaystyle\supseteq{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{X}}),

(9)

5.2.1

Lemma 9

Let

5.3
A double lifting approach for general
{\mathcal{X}}

By
Proposition

2
, a concave tent may fail to exist if the set
{\mathcal{X}}
is not a set of extreme points of
\conv({\mathcal{X}})
. In this section, we are going to discuss how to circumvent this issue by employing a double lifting strategy. The theorem below can be interpreted as a generalization of (
2
).

Theorem 12
.

Let
{\mathcal{X}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a compact and let
f
be a function conforming to
Assumption

1
, where
h
is equal to zero everywhere. Assume, the function
g\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\times{\mathcal{S}}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is given by

\displaystyle g(\mathbf{x},{\mathsf{X}})\coloneqq\sup_{\Psi,\mathbf{u},{\mathsf{U}}}\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}{\mathsf{A}}\bullet{\mathsf{X}}+2\mathbf{a}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}+2{\mathsf{B}}^{\mathsf{T}}\bullet\Psi+{\mathsf{C}}\bullet{\mathsf{U}}+2\mathbf{c}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{u}\colon\\
\begin{bmatrix}1&\mathbf{u}^{\mathsf{T}}&\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}\\
\mathbf{u}&{\mathsf{U}}&\Psi^{\mathsf{T}}\\
\mathbf{x}&\Psi&{\mathsf{X}}\end{bmatrix}\in{\mathcal{C}},\ \begin{bmatrix}1&\mathbf{u}^{\mathsf{T}}\\
\mathbf{u}&{\mathsf{U}}\end{bmatrix}\in{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{U}}),\end{array}\right\},

where
{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq{\mathcal{S}}^{n+q+1}
is as in
Theorem

8
.
Also, let
\bar{h}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\times{\mathcal{S}}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\ \left(\mathbf{x},{\mathsf{X}}\right)\mapsto\bar{h}(\mathbf{x},{\mathsf{X}})
be a closed concave function. Then

\displaystyle v^{*}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\inf_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{\bar{h}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}})+f(\mathbf{x})\colon\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{X}}\right\}=\inf_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\,{\mathsf{X}}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{n}}\left\{\bar{h}(\mathbf{x},{\mathsf{X}})+g(\mathbf{x},{\mathsf{X}})\colon\begin{bmatrix}1&\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}\\
\mathbf{x}&{\mathsf{X}}\end{bmatrix}\in{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{X}})\right\}.

Proof.

First, we observe that
g
is concave by
Lemma

5
and it is closed function by similar arguments as in
Theorem

8
, where boundedness follows again from
(\mathbf{u},{\mathsf{U}})
being bounded on
{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{U}})
,
(\mathbf{x},{\mathsf{X}})
is fixed and so
\Psi
is bounded since
{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq{\mathcal{S}}_{+}^{n+q+1}
. In addition
g
is finite on
{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{X}})
since
{\mathcal{C}}\supseteq{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{U}})
. Define
{\mathcal{X}}_{lift}\coloneqq\left\{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}})\in{\mathcal{S}}^{n+1}\colon\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{X}}\right\}\subseteq{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{X}})
and
f_{lift}\colon{\mathcal{X}}_{lift}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\ (\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}})\mapsto f(\mathbf{x})
. Then

. We also see that

for all

by repeating the arguments
Theorem

(once

is a concave tent of

over
{\mathcal{X}}_{lift}
so that

by
Theorem

3
and since

is closed, i.e. upper semicontinuous (see
[

) we can replace the feasible set with its closure
\cl\conv({\mathcal{X}}_{lift})={\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{X}})
.
∎

We can think of

as the result of a double lifting. First, we lift the

into a higher dimensional space to obtain a function

defined on the extreme points of
{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{X}})
, for which we build, in a second lifting step, the function

, which is a concave tent of

over
{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{X}})
. The necessary condition in

corresponds to a point
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}})
in the lifted space that is an extreme point of
{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{X}})
. Also, the boundedness condition on
{\mathcal{X}}
is alleviated since in the proof of

8
it is merely needed (in the guise of (

{\mathsf{X}}=\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}
whenever
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{X}}
, but this feature is absorbed by the structure of

in the theorem above.

The function
h
is replaced by
\bar{h}
which can have a more complicated structure since it only needs to be concave in the lifted space. For example,
\bar{h}
can be the pointwise infimum of linear functions
\bar{h}(\mathbf{x},{\mathsf{X}})\coloneqq\inf_{i\in{\mathcal{I}}}\left\{{\mathsf{Q}}_{i}\bullet{\mathsf{X}}+2{\mathbf{q}}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}+q_{i}\right\}
in the lifted space so that in under the restriction
{\mathsf{X}}=\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}
the function becomes the pointwise infimum of (not necessarily convex) quadratic functions in the original space of variables. If we set
\bar{h}
to be a quadratic function and
f
to be constant zero then the above theorem recovers (
2
). Thus, our theory can be seen as an expansion of the classical copositive optimization framework discussed in the introduction.

It is well known that even linear optimization over
{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{X}})
can be very difficult let alone nonlinear optimization. We will defer investigations on how to navigate this difficulty to future research. Therefore, our discussion henceforth will focus on the concave tents described in
Theorem

8
.

5.4
Some guidance on
{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{U}})
and its relaxations

In order to build the our concave tents an exact representation of
{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{U}})
is not necessary in principle, since any instance of
{\mathcal{C}}
(defined as in
Theorem

8
) that is a subset of the positive semidefinite cone will suffice. However, the tighter we approximate
{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{U}})
, the smaller the concave tent will be (since we restrict the feasible set of the defining supremum problem), which reduces the risk of creating spurious local minima in the concave reformulation. As stated in the introduction, the literature on the transformation
{\mathcal{G}}(.)
is vast, so many tools exist for tackling this challenging object. However, if this transformation is applied to the Cartesian product
{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{U}}
the situation becomes even more complicated, despite the usefulness of akin objects, that appear in for example
[
48
,
49
]
to name a few.

Characterizations of
{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{F}})
for any arbitrary
{\mathcal{F}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
often involve the so-called set-completely positive cone
\mathcal{CPP}({\mathcal{K}})\coloneqq\cl\conv\left\{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}\colon\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{K}}\right\}\subseteq{\mathcal{S}}^{n}
, where
{\mathcal{K}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a closed convex cone, and are regularly given as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{F}})\coloneqq\left\{(\mathbf{x},{\mathsf{X}})\in\mathcal{CPP}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times{\mathcal{K}}_{{\mathcal{F}}})\colon{\mathcal{A}}_{{\mathcal{F}}}(\mathbf{x},{\mathsf{X}})=\mathbf{b}_{{\mathcal{F}}}\right\},

(17)

where
{\mathcal{K}}_{{\mathcal{F}}},\ {\mathcal{A}}_{{\mathcal{F}}},\ \mathbf{b}_{{\mathcal{F}}}
are a suitably chosen closed convex cone, linear map, and vector, respectively (see
[
13
, Section 2.1]
for a thorough introduction to such constructions). This construction shifts the difficulty into the conic constraint. In general, certifying membership in such a cone is NP-hard. An extensive list of references to results on these cones and their approximations schemes can be found in
[
13
, Section 2.4]
.

If we have such characterizations of
{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{X}})
and
{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{U}})
than it is immeadiate that

\displaystyle{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{U}})=\left\{{\mathsf{M}}\in\mathcal{CPP}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times{\mathcal{K}}_{{\mathcal{X}}}\times{\mathcal{K}}_{{\mathcal{U}}})\colon{\mathcal{A}}_{{\mathcal{X}}}(\mathbf{x},{\mathsf{X}})=\mathbf{b}_{{\mathcal{X}}},\ {\mathcal{A}}_{{\mathcal{U}}}(\mathbf{u},{\mathsf{U}})=\mathbf{b}_{{\mathcal{U}}}\right\},

with
{\mathsf{M}}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{n+q+1}
is a matrix with the same subdivisions as those in the description of (
8
), which likewise shifts the difficulty to the characterization of
\mathcal{CPP}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times{\mathcal{K}}_{{\mathcal{X}}}\times{\mathcal{K}}_{{\mathcal{U}}})
. In case both
{\mathcal{K}}_{{\mathcal{X}}}\times{\mathcal{K}}_{{\mathcal{U}}}
are the nonnegative orthant, one can work with the approximation strategies mentioned above. However, outside this special case, this matrix cone is notoriously difficult to work with. Since
\mathcal{CPP}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times{\mathcal{K}}_{{\mathcal{X}}}\times{\mathcal{K}}_{{\mathcal{U}}})\implies(\mathbf{x},{\mathsf{X}})\in\mathcal{CPP}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times{\mathcal{K}}_{{\mathcal{X}}}),\ (\mathbf{u},{\mathsf{U}})\in{\mathcal{G}}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times{\mathcal{K}}_{{\mathcal{U}}})
a reasonable startingpoint for an outer approximation of
{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{U}})
is given by

\displaystyle{\mathcal{C}}_{1}\coloneqq

\displaystyle\left\{{\mathsf{M}}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{+}^{n+q+1}\colon\begin{array}[]{l}{\mathcal{A}}_{{\mathcal{X}}}(\mathbf{x},{\mathsf{X}})=\mathbf{b}_{{\mathcal{X}}},\ (\mathbf{x},{\mathsf{X}})\in\mathcal{CPP}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times{\mathcal{K}}_{{\mathcal{X}}}),\\
{\mathcal{A}}_{{\mathcal{U}}}(\mathbf{u},{\mathsf{U}})=\mathbf{b}_{{\mathcal{U}}},\ (\mathbf{u},{\mathsf{U}})\in\mathcal{CPP}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times{\mathcal{K}}_{{\mathcal{U}}})\end{array}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{{\mathsf{M}}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{+}^{n+q+1}\colon(\mathbf{x},{\mathsf{X}})\in{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{X}}),\ (\mathbf{u},{\mathsf{U}})\in{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{U}})\right\},

which is still not tractable, due to the set-completely positive constraint. Still we have some takeaways from these two representations. The first tells us that even if we have exact representations of of
\mathcal{CPP}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times{\mathcal{K}}_{{\mathcal{X}}})
and
\mathcal{CPP}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times{\mathcal{K}}_{{\mathcal{U}}})
we cannot exactly represent
\mathcal{CPP}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times{\mathcal{K}}_{{\mathcal{X}}}\times{\mathcal{K}}_{{\mathcal{U}}})
, but all knowledge on the former two cones can be used to strengthen relaxations of
{\mathcal{C}}_{1}
. Also, a strengthening can be obtained by adding valid constraints on
\Psi
. For example, if
{\mathcal{K}}_{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n_{x}}
, then
\mathrm{Rows}(\Psi)\subseteq{\mathcal{K}}_{{\mathcal{U}}}
. Later, in
Section

8.2
, we will use results from
[
1
]
for generating further valid constraints on
\Psi
. Other ways to tighten this representation are discussed in
[
13
, Section 2.3.]
and references therein. The second representation of
{\mathcal{C}}_{1}
show us that, likewise, all knowledge on
{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{X}})
and
{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{U}})
can be used to strengthen its relaxations; again, we refer to
[
13
, Section 2.1]
and references therein.

6
Quality of our concave tents and comparisons

We will now investigate the quality of the concave tents constructed above in the sense that we will compare them to two important classical instances of concave tents: the concave envelope and concave reformulations based on concave quadratic updates. We will show that, under some technical assumptions, our concave tents are sandwiched between the two and that they attain the lower bound, which is the concave envelope.

Remark
.

In
Theorem

13
and
15
below we assume that the concave term
h
in the definition of
f
is removed. We conjecture that similar statements can be proved with
h
included, most likely after a thorough modification of the definition of
g
. However, the arguments, if valid at all, would be significantly more complicated. To keep the present text concise, we have therefore decided to defer such proofs to a future short communication in case our conjecture turns out to be true.

6.1
Concave envelope characterization

Based on recent results in
[
25
]
we can show that in case
{\mathcal{C}}={\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{U}}\times{\mathcal{X}})
, a respective concave tent constructed in
Theorem

8
is actually the concave envelope of
f
over
{\mathcal{X}}
. Thus, our family of concave tents includes the best concave tent in the sense that it coincides with the smallest concave overestimator of
f
over
{\mathcal{X}}
. Larger concave tents may have an increased number of local minima which is computationally undesirable, so the result below is encouraging.

Theorem 13
.

Maintain the assumptions of
Theorem

8
and assume that
h
is the zero function,
{\mathcal{X}}
is closed, and
{\mathcal{C}}={\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{U}}\times{\mathcal{X}})
. Then the function
g
is the concave envelope of
f
over
{\mathcal{X}}
.

Proof.

Denote the concave envelope of
f
over
{\mathcal{X}}
as
\hat{f}
.
Note, that by (
9
) the linear constraint can be omitted in the description of
g
given the present choice of
{\mathcal{C}}
, so that
g
is of a form such that it falls under the regime of
[
25
, Theorem 6.]
. In the language of that theorem, the triple
(f,g,{\mathcal{U}}\times{\mathcal{X}})
takes the role of
(\phi,\hat{\phi},{\mathcal{F}})
discussed there.
We already argued in
Theorem

8
that the supremum defining
g
has zero duality gap. This implies by point 5. of
[
25
, Theorem 6.]
that
g=\cl(\hat{f})
. However, from the proof of
Proposition

4
we know that
f
is continuous if
h
is continuous, which is the case for the zero function. The concave envelope of continuous functions over compact sets is itself closed by
[
42
, Corollary 17.2.1.]
. Thus,
g=\cl(\hat{f})=\hat{f}
as desired.
∎

While the theorem states that it is in principle possible to recover the concave envelope, the assumptions are much more restrictive than what is needed for the derivation of weaker concave tents, since the set
{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{U}}\times{\mathcal{X}})
is highly intractable for many instances of
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{U}}
. Thus, a trade-off has to be made between the quality of the concave tent and the computational effort caused by strengthening the cone
{\mathcal{C}}
. In
Section

8.2
we give an example based on semidefinite and second-order cone constraints that performs reasonably well in the numerical experiments discussed later in
Section

9
.

6.2
Comparison to a classic construction

For
{\mathcal{X}}\coloneqq\left\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\colon{\mathcal{A}}(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}},\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{b}\right\}
, where
{\mathcal{A}}
is of the form (
16
) and conforms with (
10
) and for a function
f
that conforms with
Assumption

1
there is a classical way to construct concave tents by considering

\displaystyle g_{c}(\mathbf{x},\bm{\lambda})\coloneqq f(\mathbf{x})-\bm{\lambda}^{\mathsf{T}}({\mathcal{A}}(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}},\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{b})=f(\mathbf{x})-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}({\mathsf{A}}_{i}\bullet\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}+\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}-b_{i}).

(18)

Whenever
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{X}}
we have that
g_{c}(\mathbf{x},\bm{\lambda})=f(\mathbf{x})
, and by
Lemma

9
there is a choice for
\bm{\lambda}
such that
-\bm{\lambda}^{\mathsf{T}}{\mathcal{A}}(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}},\mathbf{x})
becomes a concave quadratic function, which opens up the possibility that
g_{c}(\mathbf{x},\bm{\lambda})
becomes concave in
\mathbf{x}
(perhaps after a scaling of
\bm{\lambda}
) and therefore a concave tent of
f
over
{\mathcal{X}}
. Such approaches have for example been used in
[
39
,
40
]
as discussed in the introduction. However, outside of such special cases, it is nontrivial to find the required value for
\bm{\lambda}
if such values exist at all. If the Hessian of
f
, say
{\mathsf{H}}_{f}
, exists for all
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{X}}
, then it suffices to choose
\bm{\lambda}
such that the hessian of
g_{c}
fulfills
-({\mathsf{H}}_{f}(\mathbf{x})-\sum_{i=1}^{m}2\lambda_{i}{\mathsf{A}}_{i})\in{\mathcal{S}}_{+}^{n},\ \forall\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{X}}
, which may be an intractable choice unless
{\mathsf{H}}_{f}(\mathbf{x})
depends on
\mathbf{x}
in a very simple manner. In addition,
{\mathsf{H}}_{f}
might be difficult to find or be nonexistent. It may be even more difficult to find the value of
\bm{\lambda}
that gives the smallest concave tent based on
g_{c}
.
Finally, even if an appropriate choice of
\bm{\lambda}
does exist, finding supergradients of
g_{c}
may be challenging, for example, if the supremum term in
f
does not allow for a simple construction of derivatives. By comparison, our approach allows for super-differentiation under mild assumptions as will be discussed in
Section

7
.

On the other hand, if an appropriate choice for
\bm{\lambda}
does exist and the resulting concave tent can be differentiated easily, then the classical construction could be computationally much cheaper than the ones we proposed in the prior sections. Still, this hypothetical advantage is paid for in terms of the quality of the so-constructed concave tent. Indeed, in the main result of this section, we will show that even the weakest of the concave tents introduced in
Theorem

8
underestimates any of the concave tents based on
g_{c}
.

Lemma 14
.

Let
\mathbb{R}^{q}\supseteq{\mathcal{U}}\neq\emptyset
be compact. Then for
{\mathsf{U}}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{q+1},\ \Psi\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(q+1)},\ {\mathsf{X}}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{n}
we have

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}{\mathsf{U}}&\Psi^{\mathsf{T}}\\
\Psi&{\mathsf{X}}\end{bmatrix}\in{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{U}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}),\quad\mbox{ if and only if }\quad\begin{bmatrix}{\mathsf{U}}&\Psi^{\mathsf{T}}\\
\Psi&{\mathsf{X}}\end{bmatrix}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{q+n+1},\ {\mathsf{U}}\in{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{U}}).

Proof.

If
{\mathsf{M}}\in{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{U}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
then it is also psd since matrices of the form
\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}^{\mathsf{T}}
are psd and the psd-cone is closed. Further, it is the limit point of
{\mathsf{M}}_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}\mathbf{y}^{j}_{i}(\mathbf{y}^{j}_{i})^{\mathsf{T}}
, with
\mathbf{y}^{j}_{i}=[1,(\mathbf{u}^{j}_{i})^{\mathsf{T}},(\mathbf{x}^{j}_{i})^{\mathsf{T}}]\in\left\{1\right\}\times{\mathcal{U}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n},\ i\in[1\!:\!k],\ \bm{\lambda}^{j}\in\Delta_{k},\ \forall j\in\mathbb{N}
so that the
{\mathsf{U}}
-block of
{\mathsf{M}}
is the limit point of convex combinations of rank one matrices recruited from
\left\{1\right\}\times{\mathcal{U}}
, which certifies
{\mathsf{U}}\in{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{U}})
.

Conversely, from the psd constraint we get a factorization
{\mathsf{U}}={\mathsf{V}}{\mathsf{V}}^{\mathsf{T}},\ \Psi={\mathsf{V}}{\mathsf{Z}}^{\mathsf{T}},\ {\mathsf{X}}={\mathsf{Z}}{\mathsf{Z}}^{\mathsf{T}}
from some
{\mathsf{V}}\in\mathbb{R}^{q+1\times k},\ {\mathsf{Z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}
. Also, since
{\mathcal{U}}
is compact we get from
{\mathsf{U}}\in{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{U}})
that
{\mathsf{U}}=\sum_{i=1}^{l}\lambda_{i}\mathbf{w}_{i}\mathbf{w}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}={\mathsf{W}}\Diag(\bm{\lambda}){\mathsf{W}}
for
\mathbf{w}_{i}\in\left\{1\right\}\times{\mathcal{U}},\ i\in[1\!:\!l],\ \bm{\lambda}\in\Delta_{l}
and
{\mathsf{W}}\coloneqq[\mathbf{w}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{w}_{l}]
. From Caratheodory’s Theorem, we can always set
k=q+n+1
and
l=q+1<k
, so that w.l.o.g. we can assume
k=l
since if
l
is too small we can split summands in the convex combinations until
l
is large enough. We can also assume
\bm{\lambda}>0
since otherwise we can just drop the summands with zero weight. Therefore
\Lambda\coloneqq\Diag(\bm{\lambda})
, the diagonal matrix whose diagonal entries are the entries of
\bm{\lambda}
, has an invertible square root. Set
{\overline{{\mathsf{W}}}}\coloneqq{\mathsf{W}}\Lambda^{1/2}
, then the fact that
{\overline{{\mathsf{W}}}}{\overline{{\mathsf{W}}}}^{\mathsf{T}}=\mathsf{V}\mathsf{V}^{\mathsf{T}}
implies that
{\overline{{\mathsf{W}}}}={\mathsf{V}}{\mathsf{Q}}
for some orthonormal
{\mathsf{Q}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}
by
[
29
, Lemma 2.6.]
. Set
{\mathsf{Y}}\coloneqq{\mathsf{Z}}{\mathsf{Q}}\Lambda^{\nicefrac{{-1}}{{2}}}
, then we have

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{l}{\mathsf{U}}={\mathsf{V}}{\mathsf{Q}}{\mathsf{Q}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{V}}^{\mathsf{T}}={\overline{{\mathsf{W}}}}{\overline{{\mathsf{W}}}}^{\mathsf{T}}={\mathsf{W}}\Lambda{\mathsf{W}}^{\mathsf{T}},\\
\Psi={\mathsf{Z}}{\mathsf{Q}}{\mathsf{Q}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{V}}^{\mathsf{T}}={\mathsf{Z}}{\mathsf{Q}}{\overline{{\mathsf{W}}}}^{\mathsf{T}}={\mathsf{Z}}{\mathsf{Q}}\Lambda^{\nicefrac{{-1}}{{2}}}\Lambda{\mathsf{W}}^{\mathsf{T}}={\mathsf{Y}}\Lambda{\mathsf{W}}^{\mathsf{T}},\\
{\mathsf{X}}={\mathsf{Z}}{\mathsf{Q}}{\mathsf{Q}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{Z}}^{\mathsf{T}}={\mathsf{Z}}{\mathsf{Q}}\Lambda^{\nicefrac{{-1}}{{2}}}\Lambda\Lambda^{\nicefrac{{-1}}{{2}}}{\mathsf{Q}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{Z}}^{\mathsf{T}}={\mathsf{Y}}\Lambda{\mathsf{Y}}^{\mathsf{T}},\end{array}\mbox{ so that }\begin{bmatrix}{\mathsf{U}}&\Psi^{\mathsf{T}}\\
\Psi&{\mathsf{X}}\end{bmatrix}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{w}_{i}\\
\mathbf{y}_{i}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{w}_{i}\\
\mathbf{y}_{i}\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}}\hskip-8.5359pt,

where
\mathbf{y}_{i}
is the
i
-th column of
{\mathsf{Y}}
and
[\mathbf{w}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}},\mathbf{y}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}]^{\mathsf{T}}\in\left\{1\right\}\times{\mathcal{U}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n},\ i\in[1\!:\!k]
, so that the matrix-block is indeed an element of
{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{U}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
∎

Theorem 15
.

Let
{\mathcal{X}}\coloneqq\left\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\colon{\mathcal{A}}(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}},\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{b}\right\}
such that
{\mathcal{A}}
is of the form (
16
) and fulfills (
10
) and let
f
fulfill
Assumption

1
with
h
being the zero function. Let
g
be as in
Theorem

8
and define
{\mathcal{T}}\coloneqq\left\{\bm{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\colon g_{c}(.,\bm{\lambda})\mbox{ is concave}\right\}
. Then
g(\mathbf{x})\leq g_{c}(\mathbf{x},\bm{\lambda}),\ \forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\ \forall\bm{\lambda}\in{\mathcal{T}}
.

Proof.

Since
g
becomes larger with
{\mathcal{C}}
it suffices to discuss the case
{\mathcal{C}}={\mathcal{S}}_{+}^{n+q+1}
.
If
{\mathcal{T}}=\emptyset
the statement is trivially true, so assume otherwise that
\bar{\bm{\lambda}}\in{\mathcal{T}}
. By
Proposition

4
,
f
is upper semicontinuous and nowhere infinity so that
g_{c}(\,.\,,\bar{\bm{\lambda}})
is closed proper concave function. Thus, by
[
42
, Theorem 12.1]
we have
g_{c}(\,.\,,\bar{\bm{\lambda}})=\inf_{[\mathbf{v}^{\mathsf{T}},v_{0}]^{\mathsf{T}}\in{\mathcal{V}}}\left\{\mathbf{v}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}+v_{0}\right\}
, for some set
{\mathcal{V}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. We will now show that in fact
g_{c}(\mathbf{x},\bar{\bm{\lambda}})=\hat{g}_{c}(\mathbf{x},\bar{\bm{\lambda}})
where

\displaystyle\hat{g}_{c}(\mathbf{x},\bar{\bm{\lambda}})\coloneqq\sup_{\Psi,\mathbf{u},{\mathsf{U}},{\mathsf{X}}}\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}{\mathsf{A}}\bullet{\mathsf{X}}+2\mathbf{a}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}+2{\mathsf{B}}^{\mathsf{T}}\bullet\Psi+{\mathsf{C}}\bullet{\mathsf{U}}+2\mathbf{c}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{u}-\bar{\bm{\lambda}}^{\mathsf{T}}\left({\mathcal{A}}({\mathsf{X}},\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{b}\right)\colon\\
\begin{bmatrix}1&\mathbf{u}^{\mathsf{T}}&\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}\\
\mathbf{u}&{\mathsf{U}}&\Psi^{\mathsf{T}}\\
\mathbf{x}&\Psi&{\mathsf{X}}\end{bmatrix}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{+}^{n+q+1},\ \begin{bmatrix}1&\mathbf{u}^{\mathsf{T}}\\
\mathbf{u}&{\mathsf{U}}\end{bmatrix}\in{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{U}}),\end{array}\right\}.

We have "
\leq
" since for any
\mathbf{x}
we have a solution
\mathbf{u}\in{\mathcal{U}}
to the inner supremum defining
f
and
{\mathsf{X}}=\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}},\ \Psi=\mathbf{x}\mathbf{u}^{\mathsf{T}},\ {\mathsf{U}}=\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}^{\mathsf{T}}
is a feasible solution to the supremum defining
\hat{g}_{c}
. For the converse, consider that
g_{c}(\mathbf{x},\bar{\bm{\lambda}})=\inf_{[\mathbf{v}^{\mathsf{T}},v_{0}]^{\mathsf{T}}\in{\mathcal{V}}}\left\{\mathbf{v}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}+v_{0}\right\}
implies

\displaystyle\ g_{c}(\mathbf{x},\bar{\bm{\lambda}})\leq\mathbf{v}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}+v_{0},\quad\forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\ [\mathbf{v}^{\mathsf{T}},v_{0}]^{\mathsf{T}}\in{\mathcal{V}},

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{g_{c}(\mathbf{x},\bar{\bm{\lambda}})-\mathbf{v}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}\right\}\leq v_{0},\quad\forall[\mathbf{v}^{\mathsf{T}},v_{0}]^{\mathsf{T}}\in{\mathcal{V}},

and by (
2
) we can reformulate

\displaystyle\hskip-28.45274pt\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{g_{c}(\mathbf{x},\bar{\bm{\lambda}})-\mathbf{v}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}\right\}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\mathbf{x},\Psi,\mathbf{u},{\mathsf{U}},{\mathsf{X}}}\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}{\mathsf{A}}\bullet{\mathsf{X}}+2\mathbf{a}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}+2{\mathsf{B}}^{\mathsf{T}}\bullet\Psi+{\mathsf{C}}\bullet{\mathsf{U}}+2\mathbf{c}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{u}-\bar{\bm{\lambda}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\mathcal{A}}({\mathsf{X}},\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{v}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}\colon\\
\begin{bmatrix}1&\mathbf{u}^{\mathsf{T}}&\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}\\
\mathbf{u}&{\mathsf{U}}&\Psi^{\mathsf{T}}\\
\mathbf{x}&\Psi&{\mathsf{X}}\end{bmatrix}\in{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{U}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}),\end{array}\right\}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{\hat{g}_{c}(\mathbf{x},\bar{\bm{\lambda}})-\mathbf{v}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}\right\},

where the second equality is valid since the matrix constraint involving
{\mathcal{G}}({\mathcal{U}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
can be replaced by the matrix constraints in the definition of
\hat{g}_{c}(\mathbf{x},\bar{\bm{\lambda}})
by
Lemma

14
. Thus, we get

\displaystyle\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{\hat{g}_{c}(\mathbf{x},\bar{\bm{\lambda}})-\mathbf{v}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}\right\}=\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{g_{c}(\mathbf{x},\bar{\bm{\lambda}})-\mathbf{v}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}\right\}\leq v_{0},\quad\forall[\mathbf{v}^{\mathsf{T}},v_{0}]^{\mathsf{T}}\in{\mathcal{V}},

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\ \hat{g}_{c}(\mathbf{x},\bar{\bm{\lambda}})\leq\mathbf{v}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}+v_{0},\quad\forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\ [\mathbf{v}^{\mathsf{T}},v_{0}]^{\mathsf{T}}\in{\mathcal{V}},

so that indeed
\hat{g}_{c}(\mathbf{x},\bar{\bm{\lambda}})\leq\inf_{(\mathbf{v}^{\mathsf{T}},v_{0})^{\mathsf{T}}\in{\mathcal{V}}}\left\{\mathbf{v}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}+v_{0}\right\}=g_{c}(\mathbf{x},\bar{\bm{\lambda}})
. In addition we can also see that the supremum defining
\hat{g}_{c}
is unbounded if there is an
{\mathsf{X}}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{+}^{n}
such that
{\mathsf{A}}\bullet{\mathsf{X}}-\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\bar{\bm{\lambda}})_{i}{\mathsf{A}}_{i}\bullet{\mathsf{X}}>0
, as this
{\mathsf{X}}
would span an improving ray. But since
\hat{g}_{c}\leq g_{c}<\infty
as
g_{c}
is proper, we see that our assumptions imply
-({\mathsf{A}}-\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\bar{\bm{\lambda}})_{i}{\mathsf{A}}_{i})\in{\mathcal{S}}_{+}^{n}
. Thus, for any
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we get

\displaystyle\hskip-14.22636ptg_{c}(\mathbf{x},\bar{\bm{\lambda}})\geq

\displaystyle\inf_{\bm{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\left\{g_{c}(\mathbf{x},\bm{\lambda})\colon-({\mathsf{A}}-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}{\mathsf{A}}_{i})\in{\mathcal{S}}_{+}^{n}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf_{\bm{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\left\{\sup_{\Psi,\mathbf{u},{\mathsf{U}},{\mathsf{X}}}\left\{{\mathsf{A}}\bullet{\mathsf{X}}+\dots\right\}\colon-({\mathsf{A}}-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}{\mathsf{A}}_{i})\in{\mathcal{S}}_{+}^{n}\right\}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\sup_{\Psi,\mathbf{u},{\mathsf{U}},{\mathsf{X}}}\left\{{\mathsf{A}}\bullet{\mathsf{X}}+\dots+\inf_{\bm{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\left\{-\bm{\lambda}^{\mathsf{T}}\left({\mathcal{A}}({\mathsf{X}},\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{b}\right)\colon\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}{\mathsf{A}}_{i}-{\mathsf{A}}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{+}^{n}\right\}\colon\dots\right\},

where the first inequality is due to the feasibility of
\bar{\bm{\lambda}}
in the right-hand side infimum. The next equation is due to
g_{c}=\hat{g}_{c}
and the definition of the latter. Finally, the second inequality is due to the min-max inequality.
The inner infimum in the last problem can, by weak conic duality, be underestimated via its dual given by
\sup_{{\mathsf{Y}}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{+}^{n}}\left\{{\mathsf{A}}\bullet{\mathsf{Y}}\colon{\mathcal{A}}({\mathsf{X}}+{\mathsf{Y}},\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{b}\right\}
, which we can plug in to obtain the underestimator

\displaystyle g_{c}(\mathbf{x},\bar{\bm{\lambda}})

\displaystyle\geq\sup_{\Psi,\mathbf{u},{\mathsf{U}},{\mathsf{X}},{\mathsf{Y}}}\left\{{\mathsf{A}}\bullet({\mathsf{X}}+{\mathsf{Y}})+\dots\colon{\mathcal{A}}({\mathsf{X}}+{\mathsf{Y}},\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{b},\ {\mathsf{Y}}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{+}^{n},\ \dots\right\}

\displaystyle\geq\sup_{\Psi,\mathbf{u},{\mathsf{U}},{\mathsf{X}}}\left\{{\mathsf{A}}\bullet{\mathsf{X}}+\dots\colon{\mathcal{A}}({\mathsf{X}},\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{b},\ \dots\right\}=g(\mathbf{x}),

where the second inequality stems from the fact that we effectively forced the constraint
{\mathsf{Y}}={\mathsf{O}}
, thus restricting the feasible set. In total we get
g(\mathbf{x})\leq g_{c}(\mathbf{x},\bar{\bm{\lambda}})
as desired.
∎

Supergradients based on conic duality

A powerful feature of the convex tents we introduced in previous sections is that obtaining a supergradient at a certain point comes for free with evaluating the convex tent at that point by conic duality theory, although there are some caveats to this statement. In this section, we will discuss this matter in greater detail. First, let us introduce the conic duals of the concave tents we have discussed so far explicitly.

For convenience of notation, we define the closed convex cone

\displaystyle\bar{{\mathcal{C}}}\coloneqq\left\{\begin{bmatrix}x_{0}&\mathbf{u}^{\mathsf{T}}&\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}\\
\mathbf{u}&{\mathsf{U}}&\Psi^{\mathsf{T}}\\
\mathbf{x}&\Psi&{\mathsf{X}}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathrm{cone}\left({\mathcal{C}}\right)\colon\ \begin{bmatrix}x_{0}&\mathbf{u}^{\mathsf{T}}\\
\mathbf{u}&{\mathsf{U}}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathrm{cone}({\mathcal{G}}_{{\mathcal{U}}})\ \right\}\subseteq{\mathcal{S}}^{n+q+1}_{+},

so that the different versions of

discussed before can be cast as a linear conic optimization with a single conic constraint involving
\bar{{\mathcal{C}}}
. In addition we need to define two linear maps

and

such that

and we denote their adjoints by
{\mathcal{A}}_{X}^{*}
and
{\mathcal{A}}_{x}^{*}
respectively.
Replacing the supremum in

with its dual yields its dual characterization:

\displaystyle g(\mathbf{x})=h(\mathbf{x})+\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\,\alpha\in\mathbb{R},\\
\bm{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\end{subarray}}\left\{\alpha+\mathbf{b}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\lambda}+(2\mathbf{a}+2\mathbf{w}-{\mathcal{A}}_{x}^{*}(\bm{\lambda}))^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}\colon\begin{bmatrix}\alpha&-\mathbf{c}^{\mathsf{T}}&\mathbf{w}^{\mathsf{T}}\\
-\mathbf{c}&-{\mathsf{C}}&-{\mathsf{B}}^{\mathsf{T}}\\
\mathbf{w}&-{\mathsf{B}}&{\mathcal{A}}_{X}^{*}(\bm{\lambda})-{\mathsf{A}}\end{bmatrix}\in\bar{{\mathcal{C}}}^{*}\right\}

where
\bar{{\mathcal{C}}}^{*}\coloneqq\left\{{\mathsf{M}}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{n+q+1}\colon{\mathsf{M}}\bullet{\mathsf{Y}}\geq 0,\ \forall{\mathsf{Y}}\in\bar{{\mathcal{C}}}\right\}
denotes the dual cone of
{\mathcal{C}}

Note that under the assumptions of

8

as discussed in the proof of

-supergradient from the respective coefficients of
\mathbf{x}
in the dual representation. We again restrict ourselves to the case where

is the zero function. Otherwise, one can use the theorem in combination with standard supergradient calculus (specifically linearity of the supergradient map, see
[

Theorem 16
.

Consider the primal and dual characterizations of
g
and assume that
h
is the zero function. Let
\bar{\mathbf{x}}\in\dom(g)
and let
(\mathbf{w},\alpha,\bm{\lambda})
be a feasible solution for the dual characterization of

. Then
\bar{\mathbf{y}}\coloneqq(2\mathbf{a}+2\mathbf{w}-{\mathcal{A}}_{x}^{*}(\bm{\lambda}))\in\partial_{\epsilon}\mathbf{g}(\bar{\mathbf{x}})
, the set of
\epsilon
-supergradients of

at
\bar{\mathbf{x}}

\epsilon=\alpha+\mathbf{b}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\lambda}+\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{\mathsf{T}}\bar{\mathbf{y}}-g(\bar{\mathbf{x}})\geq 0

Proof.

For an arbitrary

we have

g(\mathbf{x})\leq\alpha+\mathbf{b}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\lambda}+\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}\bar{\mathbf{y}}

by weak conic duality and dual feasibility of

, which is independent of
\mathbf{x}
. Then, the following holds:

\displaystyle g(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}\bar{\mathbf{y}}+\alpha+\mathbf{b}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\lambda}=\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}\bar{\mathbf{y}}+\alpha+\mathbf{b}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\lambda}+g(\bar{\mathbf{x}})-g(\bar{\mathbf{x}})+\bar{\mathbf{y}}^{\mathsf{T}}\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\bar{\mathbf{y}}^{\mathsf{T}}\bar{\mathbf{x}}

\displaystyle=g(\bar{\mathbf{x}})+\bar{\mathbf{y}}^{\mathsf{T}}(\mathbf{x}-\bar{\mathbf{x}})+\bar{\mathbf{y}}^{\mathsf{T}}\bar{\mathbf{x}}+\alpha+\mathbf{b}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\lambda}-g(\bar{\mathbf{x}})=g(\bar{\mathbf{x}})+\bar{\mathbf{y}}^{\mathsf{T}}(\mathbf{x}-\bar{\mathbf{x}})+\epsilon,\ \forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

∎

There are open questions regarding
\epsilon
-supergradients obtained by solving the dual problem. Neither is the optimal solution necessarily unique nor is

always a singleton and neither is the case for
\epsilon\neq 0
. It is unclear how the multiplicity of
\epsilon
-optimal solutions relates to the multiplicity of
\epsilon
-supergradients and, more importantly, there is no straightforward method of obtaining multiple solutions from a conic problem, if they exist. We will not investigate this ordeal any further here as it is a challenging one. Hopefully, we will be able to pursue it in future research.

Remark
.

We gave a presentation of the dual in relatively abstract terms and although we will discuss an application of our theory in the next section, the dual characterization of the concave tent employed there will not be stated explicitly. For readers who want to learn the mechanics of deriving duals, we refer to

. A small example of such a dual was already provided as (

Thus, a dual solution can be extracted from the primal formulation without the need to dualize it explicitly. We found that not only is this more convenient, but in our experiments eliciting the dual values in this manner was numerically much more stable, as working with the dual directly often led to Mosek reporting numerical problems.

8
A primal heuristic for robust quadratic discrete optimization

In this section, we will apply concave tents to a certain type of robust quadratic binary problems with quadratic index, to derive a primal heuristic for this problem. We will first introduce the problem and derive a finite convex relaxation. The latter will typically yield fractional solutions and we will discuss a classical rounding scheme for obtaining close feasible solutions. As an alternative to this scheme, we then introduce a primal heuristic that exploits concave tents. In the succeeding section, we compare the performance of both "rounding" methods in a B&B framework, where they are used to produce upper bounds the quality of which impacts the computational effort of the B&B approach.

8.1
Robust quadratic optimization over discrete sets

The problem we are interested in is given by

In the following section, we propose an alternative procedure based on concave tents where data on the objective function is utilized and calculations are cheaper at least in theory.

Remark
.

Based on the discussion in
[
14

, there is an immediate strengthening of

where nonnegative slack variables for the redundant constraints

, and parts of the lifted blocks can be subjected to nonnegativity constraints. In our experiments, we found that the computational burden of this construction would outweigh the benefit of obtaining tighter bounds substantially. This is not surprising, as modern conic solvers scale relatively poorly with the size of the psd block and often struggle if matrix variables are subject to both psd constraints and nonnegativity constraints at the same time. For this reason, we do not discuss this approach here any further. However, more powerful conic solvers might be able to tip the scale in favor of the more involved construction, which would be an interesting field of future investigations.

Remark
.

In an earlier attempt to apply our methods to discrete robust optimization problems, we considered network flow problems with uncertain objective and ellipsoidal uncertainty sets, which are NP-hard even if the nominal problem is not (see

In contrast, the present application reliably exhibits poor performance of the lower bounding procedure, so that primal heuristics were much more impactful. Remark

Since

This procedure is designed in the spirit of
Theorem

3

\mathbf{x}_{rel}
over
{\mathcal{X}}

{\mathcal{X}}

\conv({\mathcal{X}})

\conv({\mathcal{X}})

{\mathcal{X}}
.

We also see that in contrast to (
24

Numerical evidence in a B&B framework

In this section, we will present results from numerical experiments where we tested the procedure described in the previous section. The experiments were carried out on a laptop with an Intel Core i5-9300H CPU with 2.40GHz and 16 GB of RAM. As a modeling interface we used YALMIP
[
33
]
, as a conic solver we used Mosek 9.2 and as a MILP- and MIQP-solver we used Gurobi 9.1. We made heavy use of Mosek’s ability to report optimal dual variables from primal formulations to
avoid working with the duals explicitly. This reduced the complexity of the implementation but
also aided numerical stability in our experience.

In our experiments, we solve (
19
) via a B&B procedure which we implemented ourselves. For the lower bounds, we use (
23
), which typically yields fractional solutions. The idea is to ’round’ these via our primal heuristic and our experiment aims at finding an advantage of this procedure compared to the traditional rounding described in (
24
). The question is whether there is a difference in the number of nodes explored and, as a possible consequence, in the overall running time of the solver. Better upper bounds can lead to earlier pruning so that at least the former is plausible, while the latter is additionally affected by the computational burden of evaluating the concave tent and solving the MILP in
Algorithm

1
versus solving the MIQP in (
24
).

As a branching strategy we used a "last-in-first-out" principle, where after branching and solving the two new subproblems, we choose the leave with the better lower bound to enter the list of open nodes last. Whenever a subproblem was solved, the so obtained fractional solution was used to construct a feasible integer solution either via our primal heuristic or via traditional rounding.

In the first group of instances, the classic rounding approach mostly outperforms our approach in terms of both measures. We suspect that this is because the objective function seems to be relatively symmetrical around the origin so that the concave tents were relatively flat over
\conv({\mathcal{X}})
. In effect, our Frank-Wolfe step was not propelled in beneficial directions. In the second group, the situation is more even in terms of nodes explored with the concave tent approach doing a little better, but the computation time mostly favored the classical approach even in instances where the node count favored the concave tent approach. In the third group node count favors the concave tents while computation time is mixed. The results of both of these groups demonstrate that, even though the rounding procedure is based on an MIQP, solving the SDP for the concave tent approach can inflict a higher computational burden. Note, that the larger
q
the bigger the matrix block in the concave tent computation, which shows in the overall computation time as well, which favored our primal heuristic for instances where
q
was small. For example, in the fourth group computation time favored the concave tent approach while node counts were similar except for instance 4. This behavior continues in the fifth group where our heuristic performs well in both measures. We suspect that the looser linear constraints in the last group made it difficult for the Gurobi to solve the MIQPs since the linear relaxations it employs for globally solving it are weakened substantially. On the other hand, Gurobi’s MILP solver and Mosek’s barrier SDP solver were not affected by this as much in terms of computation time. Overall we can see that our approach can be beneficial depending on the problem at hand and in our estimations merits further investigation in future research.

Conclusion

In this article, we introduced concave tents and studied some theoretical properties as well as their practical construction for a large class of use cases. We investigated some useful aspects of these constructions and used these insights to develop a rounding heuristic for a certain type of robust optimization problem. When compared to a traditional rounding scheme this approach performs promisingly in a B&B framework. We hope we can expand on this theory in the future.

Appendix A
Regularizing convex functions

As stated in
Section

4
, the bidual characterization of a closed, proper convex function, say
c
, almost fulfills
Assumption

1
, but the resulting instance of
{\mathcal{U}}
may lack the critical property of compactness. We will therefore switch to a regularized version of
c
given by its so-called Pasch-Hausdorff envelope:

\displaystyle c_{\beta}(\mathbf{x})\coloneqq\inf_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{c(\mathbf{y})-\beta\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|_{2}\right\}=\sup_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{u}-c_{\beta}^{*}(\mathbf{u})\right\}=\sup_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{u}-c^{*}(\mathbf{u})\colon\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2}\leq\beta\right\},

where the definition follows
[
6
, Section 12.3]
and the equations are due to
[
7
, Lemma 2.1]
. Since
\dom c^{*}\cap\left\{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\colon\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2}\leq\beta\right\}
is compact (as closed convex functions have closed effective domains) there is a
\hat{u}
such that

\displaystyle c_{\beta}(\mathbf{x})=\sup_{\mathbf{u},u_{0}}\left\{\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{u}-u_{0}\colon\colon\left[\mathbf{u}^{\mathsf{T}},u_{0}\right]\in{\mathcal{U}}\right\},\quad{\mathcal{U}}\coloneqq\left\{\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{u}\\
u_{0}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\colon\begin{array}[]{l}c^{*}(\mathbf{u})\leq u_{0}\leq\hat{u},\\
\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2}\leq\beta,\end{array}\right\},

so that
c_{\beta}
fulfills all the requirements of
Assumption

1
, since
c^{*}
is closed and proper. While
c_{\beta}
is a minorant in
c
in general the following result states that for any compact
{\mathcal{X}}\subseteq\dom c
we can find
\beta
such that
c_{\beta}(\mathbf{x})=c(\mathbf{x}),\ \forall\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{X}}
.

Theorem 18
.

Let
c
be a closed proper convex function and let
{\mathcal{X}}
be a compact subset of
\dom c
. Then there is a
\beta>0
such that
c_{\beta}=c
on
{\mathcal{X}}
.

Proof.

This is a direct consequence of
[
6
, Corollary 12.19]
, after realizing that all closed proper convex functions are Lipschitz continuous relative to any compact subset of their effective domain.
∎

The downside of this derivation is that it requires calculating an upper estimate
\beta
for the Lipshitz constant. This can be difficult in general and a poor estimation might lead to weaker concave tents. We will defer the investigation of cases where this can be done sufficiently accurately to future research.
