Large amplitude radially symmetric spots and gaps in a dryland ecosystem model
Eleanor Byrnes, Paul Carter, Arjen Doelman, Lily Liu

Abstract
We construct far-from-onset radially symmetric spot and gap solutions in a two-component dryland ecosystem model of vegetation pattern formation on flat terrain, using spatial dynamics and geometric singular perturbation theory. We draw connections between the geometry of the spot and gap solutions with that of traveling and stationary front solutions in the same model. In particular, we demonstrate the instability of spots of large radius by deriving an asymptotic relationship between a critical eigenvalue associated with the spot and a coefficient which encodes the sideband instability of a nearby stationary front. Furthermore, we demonstrate that spots are unstable to a range of perturbations of intermediate wavelength in the angular direction, provided the spot radius is not too small. Our results are accompanied by numerical simulations and spectral computations.

Remark 1.4
.

The methods used in this paper to construct radially symmetric spot and gap solutions can be applied in a similar manner for the construction of solutions such as rings, targets, or other radially symmetric profiles, with different, perhaps more complex, conditions on parameters which ensure their existence. We provide some numerical evidence for the existence of such solutions in §
5
and describe how one might go about constructing these orbits, but do not provide the lengthy technical details here.

The spot and gap solutions of Theorems
1.2
–
1.3
will be constructed as heteroclinic orbits for
\displaystyle r\in[0,\infty)
using geometric singular perturbation theory. The main idea, following
[
41
]
, is to find the orbits as intersections of a core manifold of solutions which remain bounded at
\displaystyle r\to 0
, and a far-field manifold of solutions which decay to one of the states
\displaystyle P_{0}
or
\displaystyle P_{2}
as
\displaystyle r\to\infty
. These manifolds are each three-dimensional, and, unlike in
[
41
]
, it is not possible to obtain an explicit description of these manifolds due to the fact that the flow on (one of) the slow manifolds of (
1.4
) is nonlinear. This introduces complications which are handled through the use of an intermediate scaling and careful qualitative analysis of the nonlinear non-autonomous reduced flow on this slow manifold. The non-autonomous nature of the equation (
1.4
) makes this a somewhat challenging construction. To demonstrate how these orbits are constructed using the slow/fast geometry of (
1.4
), it is helpful to first consider the simpler construction of traveling or stationary planar front solutions of (
1.1
), which manifest as heteroclinic orbits in an appropriate traveling equation with a similar geometry to that of (
1.4
).

It is shown in companion paper
[
4
]
that the invasion fronts in (
1.1
) – that can be seen as spots or gaps with radius
\displaystyle r_{I}\to\infty
– are unstable with respect to a sideband/finger instability. Therefore, we next consider the stability of spots. Without going into the details of a fully rigorous analysis, we first consider the spectral stability problem for large spots. By a careful asymptotic analysis we recover the sideband instability mechanism and conclude that large spots are unstable and will typically form finger-like patterns – like the planar invasion fronts. By similarity, we conclude that the same is true for large gaps. These observations are confirmed by direct simulations: in Fig.
9
in §
5
we show snapshots of a spot and a gap that both evolve towards labyrinthine patterns after undergoing such an instability. Moreover, we subsequently conclude by considering perturbations within a range of intermediate wave numbers that spots (and gaps) with
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(1)
radius
\displaystyle r_{I}
must also be unstable: only sufficiently small spots and gaps may possibly be stable – see again §
5
and especially Fig.
10
for a brief numerical study. These small gaps correspond to fairy circles
[
14
]
and also appear as stable vegetation patterns in numerical simulations of the dryland ecosystem model of
[
46
]
– a model that can be seen as a slightly more extended version of (
1.1
)
[
4
]
(and that has been deduced from more extended models to study fairy circles).

The set-up of the paper is as follows. In §
2
, we consider the construction of traveling fronts in (
1.1
), while in §
3
, we treat the radially symmetric case and provide the proofs of Theorems
1.2
–
1.3
. The spectral stability of the radial spot and gap solutions is considered in §
4
using formal asymptotic methods, and we include numerical simulations and a brief discussion in §
5
.

Stationary and traveling planar fronts

To motivate the approach for the existence analysis of radially symmetric solutions of (
1.1

\displaystyle(u,v)(x,y,t)=(u,v)(\xi)

\displaystyle\xi=x-\delta ct

\displaystyle c=0

\displaystyle c\neq 0

\displaystyle\delta c

Using a geometric singular perturbation approach, we can construct bistable front solutions as perturbations from slow/fast heteroclinic orbits between the desert state
\displaystyle(U,V)=(a,0)
and the vegetated state
\displaystyle(U,V)=(U_{2},V_{2})
in the traveling wave ODE

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{split}0&=u_{\xi\xi}+\delta cu_{\xi}+a-u-uv^{2}\\
0&=\delta^{2}v_{\xi\xi}+\delta cv_{\xi}-mv+uv^{2}(1-bv).\end{split}

(2.1)

We note that in the case
\displaystyle c=0
, the system (
2.1
) corresponds to (
1.3
) in the far field limit
\displaystyle r\to\infty
. Thus, it is natural to first consider the geometry of (
2.1

\displaystyle r=0

\displaystyle r\to\infty

Slow/fast analysis

We can write (

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{split}u_{\xi}&=p\\
p_{\xi}&=-\delta cp-a+u+uv^{2}\\

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{split}u_{\zeta}&=\delta p\\

Setting
\displaystyle\delta=0

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{split}v_{\zeta}&=q\\

2.4
Sideband (in)stability of planar fronts

Given a front solution constructed as in §
2.2

\displaystyle P_{2}

\displaystyle P_{0}
(the case of a desert-to-vegetation front is similar), and has been constructed as a perturbation from one of the singular slow/fast fronts in §

\displaystyle c=c_{\mathrm{h}}(\delta)=c_{\mathrm{vd}}(u_{*})+\mathcal{O}(\delta)

We linearize (

\displaystyle(U,V)=(u_{\mathrm{h}},v_{\mathrm{h}})(\xi;\delta)+e^{\lambda t+i\ell y}(u,v)(\xi)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{split}\lambda u&=u_{\xi\xi}+\delta c_{\mathrm{h}}u_{\xi}-\ell^{2}u-\left(1+v_{\mathrm{h}}(\xi)^{2}\right)u-2u_{\mathrm{h}}(\xi)v_{\mathrm{h}}(\xi)v\\

Due to translation invariance, this eigenvalue problem has a solution when
\displaystyle\lambda=\ell=0

\displaystyle(u_{\mathrm{h}}^{\prime},v_{\mathrm{h}}^{\prime})(\xi;\delta)

\displaystyle\ell=0
(that is, the spectrum corresponding to 1D longitudinal perturbations in the direction of propagation) is bounded away from the imaginary axis in the left half plane.

Focusing on this critical eigenvalue, we consider its continuation for small
\displaystyle|\ell|

\displaystyle\ell
through terms of
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(\ell^{2})
, we anticipate that this critical translation eigenvalue expands as

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lambda_{\mathrm{c}}(\ell)=\lambda_{\mathrm{c},2}\ell^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\ell^{4}).

(2.20)

This eigenvalue describes the stability of the front to long wavelength perturbations transverse to the front, and the stability is thus determined by the sign of the coefficient
\displaystyle\lambda_{\mathrm{c},2}
. In a companion paper
[
4
]
, we have developed a procedure to compute this coefficient in a general class of two-component singularly perturbed reaction diffusion systems, which includes (
1.1
) as an example, and we briefly describe the results here.

We rewrite the stability problem (

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbb{L}\begin{pmatrix}u\\
v\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\lambda\begin{pmatrix}u\\
v\end{pmatrix}+\ell^{2}\begin{pmatrix}u\\

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbb{L}

(2.22)

We now expand

v\end{pmatrix}(\xi;\ell)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=0,

where
\displaystyle<U,V>=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}U(\xi)V(\xi)\mathrm{d}\xi
, and
\displaystyle(u^{A}_{\mathrm{h}},v^{A}_{\mathrm{h}})(\xi;\delta)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbb{L}^{A}\begin{pmatrix}u\\

(2.25)

From this we obtain an expression for
\displaystyle\lambda_{2,\mathrm{c}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lambda_{2,\mathrm{c}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=-\frac{\left\langle\begin{pmatrix}u_{\mathrm{h}}^{\prime}\\

(2.27)

In

\displaystyle\displaystyle>0,

so that the traveling planar fronts are always unstable to long wavelength transverse perturbations. This has implications for the (in)stability of spot/gap solutions, as we will show in §

3
Existence of radially symmetric spots and gaps

With the construction of the front solutions in §
2
in mind, we now focus on the construction of a vegetation spot solution, consisting of a single vegetation patch localized near
\displaystyle r=0
, with a single interface at some radius
\displaystyle r=r_{I}
(to be specified), at which the profile transitions from the vegetated state in the core to the desert state in the far-field. The case of gaps is similar, and we will briefly outline the differences in §

Throughout the analysis we treat

as a singular perturbation parameter. At times, it will also be convenient to consider (

We refer to (
3.1

The spot solution will be constructed as a perturbation from the singular limit structure associated with (
3.1

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{0}_{\delta},\mathcal{M}^{+}_{\delta}

3.1

2

\displaystyle r=0

\displaystyle r=r_{I}
can be thought of as a free parameter which is chosen in such a way in order to ensure that the stable manifold of the far-field desert equilibrium and the unstable manifold associated with the core vegetated states precisely intersect transversely across the fast jump.

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{dv}}=\bigcup_{|p|\leq\bar{p},k\in[0,\bar{k}]}\phi_{\mathrm{dv}}.

(3.13)

2.

The manifolds
\displaystyle\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{u}}(\mathcal{M}^{+}_{0})
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{s}}(\mathcal{M}^{0}_{0})
intersect transversely along the three-dimensional manifold

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{vd}}=\bigcup_{|p|\leq\bar{p},k\in[0,\bar{k}]}\phi_{\mathrm{vd}}.

(3.14)

Proof.

We focus on the statement
1
, as the proof of
2
is nearly identical. To prove transversality, it remains to show that the intersection breaks transversely when varying the remaining slow variable
\displaystyle u
near
\displaystyle u=u_{\mathrm{f}}=\frac{9bm}{2}
. We accomplish this by computing the splitting of the manifolds
\displaystyle\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{u}}(\mathcal{M}^{0}_{0})
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{s}}(\mathcal{M}^{+}_{0})
to leading order in
\displaystyle|u-u_{\mathrm{f}}|
via a Melnikov-type computation.

Given
\displaystyle(p,k)\in[-\bar{p},\bar{p}]\times[0,\bar{k}]
, the front
\displaystyle\phi_{\mathrm{dv}}(s)=(v_{\mathrm{dv}},q_{\mathrm{dv}})(s)
which lies in the intersection
\displaystyle\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{u}}(\mathcal{M}^{0}_{0})\cap\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{s}}(\mathcal{M}^{+}_{0})
is a solution of the fast layer equation (
3.5
) at
\displaystyle u=u_{\mathrm{f}}
. The adjoint equation associated with the linearization of (
3.5
) about
\displaystyle\phi_{\mathrm{dv}}
at
\displaystyle u=u_{\mathrm{f}}
is given by

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{split}\begin{pmatrix}v_{s}\\
q_{s}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-m+u_{\mathrm{f}}\left(2v_{\mathrm{dv}}(s)-3bv_{\mathrm{dv}}(s)^{2}\right)\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}v\\
q\end{pmatrix},\end{split}

(3.15)

and admits a unique bounded solution (up to multiplication by a constant)
\displaystyle\psi_{\mathrm{dv}}(s):=\left(-q_{\mathrm{dv}}(s),v_{\mathrm{dv}}(s)\right)
. To leading order, the splitting distance of the manifolds
\displaystyle\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{u}}(\mathcal{M}^{0}_{0})\cap\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{s}}(\mathcal{M}^{+}_{0})
is determined to leading order in
\displaystyle|u-u_{\mathrm{f}}|
by the Melnikov integral

\displaystyle\displaystyle M_{\mathrm{dv}}^{u}:=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}D_{u}F(\phi_{\mathrm{dv}}(s);u_{\mathrm{f}})\cdot\psi_{\mathrm{dv}}(s)\mathrm{d}s,

(3.16)

where
\displaystyle F(v,q;u)

2

\displaystyle\displaystyle M_{\mathrm{vd}}^{u}:=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}D_{u}F(\phi_{\mathrm{vd}}(s);u_{\mathrm{f}})\cdot\psi_{\mathrm{vd}}(s)\mathrm{d}s,

\displaystyle\psi_{\mathrm{vd}}(s):=\left(-q_{\mathrm{vd}}(s),v_{\mathrm{vd}}(s)\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle M_{\mathrm{vd}}^{u}:=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}-v_{\mathrm{vd}}(s)^{3}(1-bv_{\mathrm{vd}}(s))\mathrm{d}s<0.

(3.19)

3.2
The core region

In this section, we construct a three-dimensional manifold of orbits which remain bounded and converge to a set of uniformly vegetated states as
\displaystyle r\to 0
.

3.2.1
The center-unstable core manifold
\displaystyle\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{cu}}_{\delta}(\mathcal{C})

For the core region, we consider
\displaystyle r\in[0,r_{\mathrm{c}}]
, where
\displaystyle r_{\mathrm{c}}=\delta s_{\mathrm{c}}
, or equivalently
\displaystyle s\in[0,s_{\mathrm{c}}]
, for some
\displaystyle s_{\mathrm{c}}>0
fixed independently of
\displaystyle\delta>0
. We use a blow-up rescaling
\displaystyle z=\log s
to obtain the dynamics in the core region as

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{split}u_{z}&=\delta sp\\
p_{z}&=-p-\delta s(a-u-uv^{2})\\
v_{z}&=sq\\
q_{z}&=-q+s\left(mv-uv^{2}(1-bv)\right)\\
s_{z}&=s.\end{split}

(3.25)

Note that this system admits a two-dimensional manifold of equilibria at
\displaystyle s=0
defined by
\displaystyle\mathcal{E}=\{p=q=s=0\}
. When
\displaystyle\delta=0
, (
3.25
) becomes

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{split}u_{z}&=0\\
p_{z}&=-p\\
v_{z}&=sq\\
q_{z}&=-q+s\left(mv-uv^{2}(1-bv)\right)\\
s_{z}&=s,\end{split}

(3.26)

and near values of
\displaystyle(u,v)
satisfying
\displaystyle mv-uv^{2}(1-bv)=0
, solutions of (
3.26
) which are bounded as
\displaystyle z\to-\infty
can be expanded in terms of modified Bessel functions as follows. For given
\displaystyle u>4bm
, there are three solutions
\displaystyle v=v_{*}(u)
of
\displaystyle mv-uv^{2}(1-bv)=0
, namely
\displaystyle v_{*}(u)=0
or
\displaystyle v_{*}(u)=v_{\pm}(u)
. Fixing one of these choices, we consider
\displaystyle v\approx v_{*}(u)
, so that
\displaystyle v=v_{*}(u)+\bar{v}
, where
\displaystyle|\bar{v}|\ll 1
is assumed small.

In the case of spots, we are interested in solutions near the vegetated state in the core, hence we take
\displaystyle v_{*}(u)=v_{+}(u)
. Substituting into (
3.26
), we obtain

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{split}u_{z}&=0\\
p_{z}&=-p\\
\bar{v}_{z}&=sq\\
q_{z}&=-q+s\left(\kappa\bar{v}+\mathcal{O}(\bar{v}^{2})\right)\\
s_{z}&=s,\end{split}

(3.27)

where
\displaystyle\kappa:=ubv_{+}(u)(v_{+}(u)-v_{-}(u))
. Our aim is to construct solutions of (
3.25
) which remain bounded as
\displaystyle z\to-\infty
(
\displaystyle s\to 0
). In view of (
3.26
), when
\displaystyle\delta=0
, any such solution must satisfy
\displaystyle p\equiv 0
, while the equation for
\displaystyle(v,q)
can be re-expressed in terms of the fast variable
\displaystyle s
as

\displaystyle\displaystyle\bar{v}_{ss}+\frac{\bar{v}_{s}}{s}-\kappa\bar{v}+\mathcal{O}(\bar{v}^{2})=0,

(3.28)

which, at the linear level, is a zero-order Bessel-type equation whose solutions can be expressed as linear combinations of the modified Bessel functions
\displaystyle I_{0}(\sqrt{\kappa}s),K_{0}(\sqrt{\kappa}s)
of the first and second kind. The function
\displaystyle I_{0}(\zeta)
is bounded at
\displaystyle\zeta=0
, while
\displaystyle K_{0}
diverges logarithmically.

Therefore, given
\displaystyle u_{0}>4mb
and
\displaystyle s_{\mathrm{c}}>0
, we linearize (
3.25
) about the solution
\displaystyle(u,p,v,q,s)=(u_{0},0,v_{+}(u_{0}),0,e^{z})
, integrate, and solve the resulting fixed-point equation in terms of the Bessel functions
\displaystyle I_{0}(\cdot),K_{0}(\cdot)
. Defining the subset
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}:=\{p=q=s=0,v=v_{+}(u),u>4bm\}\subset\mathcal{E}
, this allows us to construct a local three-dimensional center-unstable manifold
\displaystyle\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{cu}}_{\delta}(\mathcal{C})
of solutions which are bounded as
\displaystyle s\to 0
(
\displaystyle z\to-\infty
), and in particular converge to
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
as
\displaystyle s\to 0
. This manifold admits the expansion

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{cu}}_{\delta}(\mathcal{C})=\left\{(u,p,v,q,s)\in\mathbb{R}^{5}:\begin{pmatrix}u\\
p\\
v\\
q\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}u_{0}+\mathcal{O}(\delta)\\
\mathcal{O}(\delta)\\
v_{+}(u_{0})+cI_{0}\left(\sqrt{\kappa}s\right)+\mathcal{O}(\delta+c^{2})\\
c\sqrt{\kappa}I_{1}\left(\sqrt{\kappa}s\right)+\mathcal{O}(\delta+c^{2})\end{pmatrix},u_{0}>4bm,0\leq s\leq s_{\mathrm{c}},|c|\leq c_{0}\right\}

(3.29)

for sufficiently small
\displaystyle|c_{0}|
and
\displaystyle\delta\ll 1
. Here
\displaystyle I_{1}=I_{0}^{\prime}
is the first-order modified Bessel function of the first kind. The manifold
\displaystyle\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{cu}}_{\delta}(\mathcal{C})
is parameterized by
\displaystyle u_{0}
and
\displaystyle c
, both of which will be selected by intersecting with the three-dimensional far-field manifold
\displaystyle\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{s}}(\mathcal{B}_{\delta}^{\mathrm{far}})
to obtain a spot solution in the full five-dimensional phase space.

3.2.2
Core transition region

In the previous subsection, we constructed the core center-unstable manifold
\displaystyle\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{cu}}_{\delta}(\mathcal{C})
of solutions which remain bounded as
\displaystyle s\to 0

\displaystyle s_{\mathrm{c}}>0
, we obtained expansions for the manifold
\displaystyle\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{cu}}_{\delta}(\mathcal{C})
valid for
\displaystyle 0\leq s\leq s_{\mathrm{c}}

\displaystyle\delta
is taken sufficiently small. We now aim to track this manifold into the region

Hence we consider
\displaystyle r\in[\delta s_{\mathrm{c}},r_{0}]
where
\displaystyle s_{\mathrm{c}}
is as above, fixed large independently of
\displaystyle\delta

\displaystyle r_{0}>0

\displaystyle\delta

\displaystyle\delta
is taken sufficiently small. We define the quantity

\displaystyle s_{\mathrm{c}}\gg 1

3.5
Rings, targets, and other radially symmetric solutions

The techniques used in §
3.1
–
3.4
to construct spot and gap solution could be used to construct other localized solutions with radial symmetry as follows: The general strategy is the same, in that to construct a solution which is asymptotically constant, and bounded as
\displaystyle r\to 0
, as in the case of spots/gaps, we construct a core manifold
\displaystyle\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{u}}_{\delta}(\mathcal{C})
of states originating near one of the desert or vegetated steady states
\displaystyle(U_{0},V_{0})
or
\displaystyle(U_{2},V_{2})
. We similarly construct a far-field stable manifold
\displaystyle\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{s}}_{\delta}(\mathcal{B}^{\mathrm{far}}_{\delta})
of solutions bounded as
\displaystyle r\to\infty
consisting of stable fibers of one of the steady states
\displaystyle(U_{0},V_{0})
or
\displaystyle(U_{2},V_{2})
, which could be the same or different from the state near the core.

Then, to construct a radially symmetric profile with a desired number of interfaces, the core manifold
\displaystyle\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{u}}_{\delta}(\mathcal{C})
is tracked along a number of fast jumps alternating between
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{0}_{\delta}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{+}_{\delta}
as a sequence of radii
\displaystyle r_{j},j=1,2,3,\ldots
, in between which
\displaystyle\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{u}}_{\delta}(\mathcal{C})
follows the slow flow of the corresponding slow manifold
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{0}_{\delta}
or
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{+}_{\delta}
, entering along its fast stable fibers, and exiting aligned along its fast unstable fibers according to the exchange lemma.

This procedure could be used, in principle, to construct ring or target profiles with any desired (finite) number of interfaces. Some examples obtained numerically are presented in §
5
.

\displaystyle(\bar{u}^{0},\bar{p}^{0})(r)=(\alpha K_{\ell},\alpha K_{\ell}^{\prime})(r)
in the slow region
\displaystyle r>r_{I}
.

Near
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{+}_{0}
, the slow reduced equations are not as straightforward, due to the nonlinear reduced flow on
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{+}_{0}
. In particular inspecting (
4.1
), to leading order we have that
\displaystyle v
satisfies

\displaystyle\displaystyle v=\frac{v_{+}(r)^{2}(1-bv_{+}(r))}{m-u_{+}(r)(2v_{+}(r)-3bv_{+}(r)^{2})}u,

(4.14)

where
\displaystyle v_{+}(r):=v_{+}(u_{+}(r))
and
\displaystyle u_{+}(r)
is the solution in the slow region for
\displaystyle r<r_{I}
. Substituting into (
4.1
), we obtain the leading order equation for
\displaystyle u
in the slow region

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{split}0&=u_{rr}+\frac{1}{r}u_{r}-\frac{\ell^{2}}{r^{2}}u-\left(1+v_{+}(r)^{2}\right)u-\frac{2u_{+}(r)v_{+}(r)^{3}(1-bv_{+}(r))}{m-u_{+}(r)(2v_{+}(r)-3bv_{+}(r)^{2})}u\\
&=u_{rr}+\frac{1}{r}u_{r}-\frac{\ell^{2}}{r^{2}}u-u-\frac{v_{+}(r)^{2}(m+u_{+}(r)v_{+}(r)^{2})}{m-u_{+}(r)(2v_{+}(r)-3bv_{+}(r)^{2})}u.\end{split}

(4.15)

The solutions of this equation do not appear to have a nice representation in terms of special functions. However, this equation is of the form

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{split}0&=u_{rr}+\frac{1}{r}u_{r}-\frac{\ell^{2}}{r^{2}}u-u-f_{+}(r)u,\end{split}

(4.16)

where
\displaystyle f_{+}(r)
has a well-defined limit as
\displaystyle r\to 0
. As
\displaystyle r\to 0
, the equation behaves like a Bessel-type equation, and it is possible to show that there is a unique (up to a constant multiple) solution
\displaystyle u=u^{*}_{\ell}(r)
for each
\displaystyle\ell
which is bounded as
\displaystyle r\to 0
. To see this, we rewrite (
4.16
) as

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{split}u_{\eta}&=d\\
d_{\eta}&=\ell^{2}u+r^{2}u+r^{2}f_{+}(r)u\\
r_{\eta}&=r,\end{split}

(4.17)

where
\displaystyle\eta=\log r
. The system (
4.17
) has a fixed point at the origin which admits a two-dimensional unstable manifold corresponding to a one-dimensional space of solutions of the nonautonomous linear system (
4.16
) which are bounded as
\displaystyle\eta\to-\infty
(
\displaystyle r\to 0
). This space is spanned by a nontrivial solution, which we denote by
\displaystyle u=u^{*}_{\ell}(r)
, which will serve as a candidate eigenfunction in the slow region
\displaystyle r<r_{I}
. We therefore obtain the leading order solution
\displaystyle(\bar{u}^{+},\bar{p}^{+})(r)=(\beta u^{*}_{\ell},\beta(u^{*}_{\ell})^{\prime})(r)
in the slow region
\displaystyle r<r_{I}
. Using the conditions (
4.12
), we obtain

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{split}\alpha K_{\ell}(r_{I})&=\beta u^{*}_{\ell}(r_{I})\\
\alpha K_{\ell}^{\prime}(r_{I})&=\beta(u^{*}_{\ell})^{\prime}(r_{I})-u_{\mathrm{f}}v_{+}(u_{\mathrm{f}})^{2},\end{split}

(4.18)

which we can solve to determine

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{split}\bar{u}_{1}&=\alpha K_{\ell}(r_{I})=\frac{u_{\mathrm{f}}v_{+}(u_{\mathrm{f}})^{2}K_{\ell}(r_{I})u^{*}_{\ell}(r_{I})}{K_{\ell}(r_{I})(u^{*}_{\ell})^{\prime}(r_{I})-K_{\ell}^{\prime}(r_{I})u^{*}_{\ell}(r_{I})},\end{split}

(4.19)

and so

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{1}(\ell)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\left(\frac{(a-u_{\mathrm{f}})K_{1}(r_{I})}{K_{0}(r_{I})}-\frac{u_{\mathrm{f}}v_{+}(u_{\mathrm{f}})^{2}K_{\ell}(r_{I})u^{*}_{\ell}(r_{I})}{K_{\ell}(r_{I})(u^{*}_{\ell})^{\prime}(r_{I})-K_{\ell}^{\prime}(r_{I})u^{*}_{\ell}(r_{I})}\right)\frac{-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}v_{\mathrm{vd}}^{2}\left(1-bv_{\mathrm{vd}}\right)v_{\mathrm{vd}}^{\prime}\mathrm{d}s}{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}v_{\mathrm{vd}}^{\prime 2}\mathrm{d}s}.

(4.20)

In general, we require information about the solution
\displaystyle u^{*}_{\ell}
to be able to determine the sign of this quantity as a function of
\displaystyle\ell
. This is nontrivial to do in general, as
\displaystyle u^{*}_{\ell}
likely does not have a direct representation in terms of special functions. However, in certain limiting cases we can approximate (
4.20
). For sufficiently large spots
\displaystyle r_{I}\gg 1
, we argue in §
4.3
that such spots inherit instabilities from nearby planar front solutions (see §
2.4
). We consider the case of large wavenumbers
\displaystyle|\ell|\gg 1
in §
4.4
.

4.3
Large spots: recovering the sideband instability

In this section, we consider the critical eigenvalue expression (
4.20
) in the case of a very large radial spot solution, that is
\displaystyle r_{I}\gg 1
. Near the core, such a solution is approximately constant, while at the interface, the solution resembles a stationary planar front between the desert and vegetated equilibrium states. In the limit
\displaystyle r_{I}\to\infty
, in the far field the solution approaches the stationary planar front and inherits the (in)stability properties of the front. To see this, in this section we estimate the expression (
4.20
) in the asymptotic limit
\displaystyle r_{I}\to\infty
.

Remark 4.2
.

To investigate this limit, one option would be to apply the approach from §
2.4
to (
4.3
) under the assumption
\displaystyle r_{I}\gg 1
, as for the stability of traveling fronts. However, since we do not intend to repeat the analysis from
[
4
]
which results in the expression (
2.28

4.20
) in the asymptotic limit
\displaystyle r_{I}\to\infty
.

We estimate the expression (
4.20
) for finite values of
\displaystyle\ell\in\mathbb{Z}
as
\displaystyle r_{I}\to\infty

\displaystyle u^{*}_{\ell}(r_{I})
, where
\displaystyle u^{*}_{\ell}(r)
is the unique bounded solution (up to a constant) of (
4.15

\displaystyle r\to 0

\displaystyle r_{I}\gg 1

\displaystyle u^{*}_{\ell}(r_{I})
as follows. Note that when
\displaystyle\ell=\pm 1

4.15

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{split}0&=u_{rr}+\frac{1}{r}u_{r}-\frac{1}{r^{2}}u-u-\frac{v_{+}(r)^{2}(m+u_{+}(r)v_{+}(r)^{2})}{m-u_{+}(r)(2v_{+}(r)-3bv_{+}(r)^{2})}u,\end{split}

which admits a solution bounded at
\displaystyle r=0

\displaystyle u_{+}^{\prime}(r)
, where
\displaystyle u_{+}(r)
is the core solution on
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{+}_{0}
which satisfies
\displaystyle u_{+}(r_{I})=u_{\mathrm{f}}
and
\displaystyle u_{+}^{\prime}(r_{I})=\frac{(a-u_{\mathrm{f}})K_{1}(r_{I})}{K_{0}(r_{I})}

\displaystyle\displaystyle u_{2}(r):=u_{+}^{\prime}(r)\int_{r_{I}}^{r}\frac{\mathrm{d}s}{su_{+}^{\prime}(s)^{2}},

and the Wronskian of
\displaystyle u_{1}:=u_{+}^{\prime}
and
\displaystyle u_{2}
is given by
\displaystyle W(u_{1},u_{2})(r)=r^{-1}
. We now assume
\displaystyle r_{I}\gg 1
and attempt to construct the leading-order bounded solution of (
4.15

\displaystyle[0,r_{I}]
as
\displaystyle r_{I}\to\infty

\displaystyle[0,r_{I}]=[0,r_{I}-R]\cup[r_{I}-R,r_{I}]

\displaystyle r_{I}\gg R\gg 1

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{+}_{0}
for large
\displaystyle r

3.43

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle(u_{+},u_{+}^{\prime})(r_{I}-R)=(U_{2},0)+\mathcal{O}(e^{-\nu R})
for some
\displaystyle\nu>0

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle[0,r_{I}-R]

4.15

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{split}0&=u_{rr}+\frac{1}{r}u_{r}-\frac{\ell^{2}}{r^{2}}u-\kappa_{0}u,\end{split}

(4.22)

where

\displaystyle\displaystyle\kappa_{0}=1+\frac{v_{+}(U_{2})^{2}(m+U_{2}v_{+}(U_{2})^{2})}{m-U_{2}(2v_{+}(U_{2})-3bv_{+}(U_{2})^{2})},

(4.23)

which admits a unique bounded solution as
\displaystyle r\to 0

\displaystyle I_{\ell}(\sqrt{\kappa_{0}}r)

\displaystyle r_{I}\gg R

\displaystyle I_{\ell}(\sqrt{\kappa_{0}}(r_{I}-R))\sim\frac{e^{\sqrt{\kappa_{0}}(r_{I}-R)}}{\sqrt{2\pi r_{I}}}

On the interval
\displaystyle[r_{I}-R,r_{I}]

\displaystyle\frac{\ell^{2}-1}{r^{2}}=\frac{\ell^{2}-1}{r_{I}^{2}}\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{R^{2}}{r_{I}^{2}}\right)\right)\ll 1

4.15

\displaystyle u^{*}_{\ell}=u_{1}(r)+\frac{\ell^{2}-1}{r_{I}^{2}}\tilde{u}(r)
where
\displaystyle\tilde{u}
satisfies

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{split}0&=\tilde{u}_{rr}+\frac{1}{r}\tilde{u}_{r}-\frac{1}{r^{2}}\tilde{u}-\tilde{u}-\frac{v_{+}(r)^{2}(m+u_{+}(r)v_{+}(r)^{2})}{m-u_{+}(r)(2v_{+}(r)-3bv_{+}(r)^{2})}\tilde{u}=u_{1}\end{split}

(4.24)

to leading order in
\displaystyle\frac{\ell^{2}-1}{r_{I}^{2}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\tilde{u}(r)=C_{1}u_{1}(r)+C_{2}u_{2}(r)-u_{1}(r)\int_{r_{I}}^{r}su_{1}(s)u_{2}(s)\mathrm{d}s+u_{2}(r)\int_{r_{I}-R}^{r}su_{1}(s)^{2}\mathrm{d}s.

(4.25)

Recalling
\displaystyle u^{*}_{\ell}(r)=u_{1}(r)+\frac{\ell^{2}-1}{r_{I}^{2}}\tilde{u}(r)
,
in order to construct a bounded solution as
\displaystyle r\to 0

\displaystyle(u,u^{\prime})(r)\approx(C_{3}I_{\ell},C_{3}I_{\ell}^{\prime})(\kappa_{0}r)
at
\displaystyle r=r_{I}-R

\displaystyle C_{3}

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\ell}
on the interval
\displaystyle[0,r_{I}-R]

\displaystyle u_{1}(r_{I}-R)

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle R\to\infty

\displaystyle u_{2}(r_{I}-R)

\displaystyle R\to\infty

\displaystyle I_{\ell}(\sqrt{\kappa_{0}}(r_{I}-R))\sim e^{-\sqrt{\kappa_{0}}R}I_{\ell}(\sqrt{\kappa_{0}}r_{I})

\displaystyle C_{2}\approx 0

It remains to determine the coefficient (

\displaystyle\displaystyle u^{*}_{\ell}(r_{I})

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sim K_{\ell}(r_{I})u_{\mathrm{f}}v_{+}(u_{\mathrm{f}})^{2}+\frac{(\ell^{2}-1)}{r_{I}^{2}}\left(\frac{K_{\ell}(r_{I})}{u_{+}^{\prime}(r_{I})}\int_{r_{I}-R}^{r_{I}}u_{+}^{\prime}(s)^{2}\mathrm{d}s+\frac{\left(a-u_{\mathrm{f}}\right)\pi}{4r_{I}K_{0}(r_{I})}e^{-2r_{I}}\right),

(4.27)

4.4
Point spectrum for
\displaystyle|\ell|\gg 1

When
\displaystyle|\ell|\gg 1
(but
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(1)
with respect to
\displaystyle\delta
), we can obtain asymptotic approximations for
\displaystyle K_{\ell}
and
\displaystyle u^{*}_{\ell}
in (
4.20
). For the former, we can employ standard asymptotic results for modified Bessel functions
[
8
]
. For the latter, we consider (
4.15
) in the limit of large
\displaystyle|\ell|
. Considering (
4.20
) and rearranging the term involving
\displaystyle K_{\ell}
and
\displaystyle u^{*}_{\ell}
, we see that

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(\ell)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\left(\frac{(a-u_{\mathrm{f}})K_{1}(r_{I})}{K_{0}(r_{I})}-\frac{u_{\mathrm{f}}v_{+}(u_{\mathrm{f}})^{2}}{\frac{(u^{*}_{\ell})^{\prime}(r_{I})}{u^{*}_{\ell}(r_{I})}-\frac{K_{\ell}^{\prime}(r_{I})}{K_{\ell}(r_{I})}}\right)\frac{-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}v_{\mathrm{vd}}^{2}\left(1-bv_{\mathrm{vd}}\right)v_{\mathrm{vd}}^{\prime}\mathrm{d}s}{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}v_{\mathrm{vd}}^{\prime 2}\mathrm{d}s}.

(4.29)

Defining
\displaystyle w^{*}_{\ell}(r)=\frac{r}{|\ell|}\frac{(u^{*}_{\ell})^{\prime}(r)}{u^{*}_{\ell}(r)}
, and using (
4.15

\displaystyle w=w^{*}_{\ell}(r)
satisfies the equation

\displaystyle\displaystyle w_{r}=\frac{|\ell|}{r}\left(1-w^{2}\right)+\frac{r}{|\ell|}\left(1+f_{+}(r)\right),

(4.30)

where

\displaystyle\displaystyle f_{+}(r)=\frac{v_{+}(r)^{2}(m+u_{+}(r)v_{+}(r)^{2})}{m-u_{+}(r)(2v_{+}(r)-3bv_{+}(r)^{2})}.

(4.31)

Appending an equation for
\displaystyle r
and rescaling the spatial coordinate, we have the equivalent autonomous system

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{split}w^{\prime}&=\left(1-w^{2}\right)+\frac{r^{2}}{\ell^{2}}\left(1+f_{+}(r)\right)\\
r^{\prime}&=\frac{r}{|\ell|},\end{split}

which, for large
\displaystyle|\ell|\gg 1
, admits two invariant manifolds which are defined up to

\displaystyle w_{\pm}(r;\ell)=\pm 1+\mathcal{O}(|\ell|^{-1})
. The manifold
\displaystyle w=1
is attracting while
\displaystyle w=-1
is repelling. For large
\displaystyle|\ell|

\displaystyle u^{*}_{\ell}(r)
to be bounded as
\displaystyle r\to 0

\displaystyle w^{*}_{\ell}(r)

\displaystyle w_{+}(r;\ell)

for large
\displaystyle|\ell|
. Therefore,
\displaystyle\frac{(u^{*}_{\ell})^{\prime}(r)}{u^{*}_{\ell}(r)}\sim\frac{|\ell|}{r}

as
\displaystyle|\ell|\to\infty
. A similar argument (or using the asymptotic expressions in
[
8

) shows that
\displaystyle\frac{K_{\ell}^{\prime}(r)}{K_{\ell}(r)}\sim-\frac{|\ell|}{r}
. Finally, returning to (

), we have that

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(\ell)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\left(\frac{(a-u_{\mathrm{f}})K_{1}(r_{I})}{K_{0}(r_{I})}-\frac{u_{\mathrm{f}}v_{+}(u_{\mathrm{f}})^{2}r_{I}}{2|\ell|}\right)\frac{-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}v_{\mathrm{vd}}^{2}\left(1-bv_{\mathrm{vd}}\right)v_{\mathrm{vd}}^{\prime}\mathrm{d}s}{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}v_{\mathrm{vd}}^{\prime 2}\mathrm{d}s}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sim\left(\frac{(a-u_{\mathrm{f}})K_{1}(r_{I})}{K_{0}(r_{I})}\right)\frac{-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}v_{\mathrm{vd}}^{2}\left(1-bv_{\mathrm{vd}}\right)v_{\mathrm{vd}}^{\prime}\mathrm{d}s}{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}v_{\mathrm{vd}}^{\prime 2}\mathrm{d}s}

\displaystyle\displaystyle>0

for
\displaystyle|\ell|\gg 1
sufficiently large (but
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(1)
with respect to
\displaystyle\delta
), so that spots of radius
\displaystyle r_{I}=\mathcal{O}(1)
are always unstable. In particular, the spots we have constructed in §
3
are unstable to (suitably) large wave numbers
\displaystyle\ell
. However, for sufficiently small spots (
\displaystyle r_{I}=o(1)
with respect to
\displaystyle\delta
), the above argument is no longer valid, and it is not possible to rule out stable spots; see §
5
for some solutions obtained numerically. However, the regime
\displaystyle r_{I}=o(1)
lies outside the scope of the existence analysis in §
3
.

Next, we note that if
\displaystyle\ell=\bar{\ell}\delta^{-1/2}
for some
\displaystyle 0<\bar{\ell}=\mathcal{O}(1)
, a similar analysis as in §
4.2
results in the solvability condition

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lambda_{1}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=-\frac{\bar{\ell}^{2}}{r_{I}^{2}}+\left(\frac{(a-u_{\mathrm{f}})}{K_{0}(r_{I})}K_{1}(r_{I})\right)\frac{-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}v_{\mathrm{vd}}^{2}\left(1-bv_{\mathrm{vd}}\right)v_{\mathrm{vd}}^{\prime}\mathrm{d}s}{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}v_{\mathrm{vd}}^{\prime 2}\mathrm{d}s}

(4.33)

(in place of (
4.20
)) so that spots are always unstable to wavenumbers of the form
\displaystyle\ell=\bar{\ell}\delta^{-1/2}
where
\displaystyle\bar{\ell}
is small but
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(1)
. For larger wavenumbers, the first term in (
4.33
) dominates so that
\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(\ell)<0
. Thus a switch from unstable to stable wavenumbers occurs at some critical
\displaystyle\ell=\bar{\ell}\delta^{-1/2}
.

Finally, if
\displaystyle\ell=\frac{\bar{\ell}}{\delta}
for some
\displaystyle 0<\bar{\ell}=\mathcal{O}(1)
, to construct a bounded solution of (

), to leading order we must have
\displaystyle u\equiv 0
, and the fast equation in (
4.3
) reduces to

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{split}\lambda v+\frac{\bar{\ell}^{2}}{r_{I}^{2}}v&=v_{ss}-mv+u_{\mathrm{f}}\left(2v_{\mathrm{vd}}-3bv_{\mathrm{vd}}^{2}\right)v,\end{split}

(4.34)

which is just the Sturm-Liouville problem (

\displaystyle\lambda

\displaystyle\frac{\bar{\ell}^{2}}{r_{I}^{2}}

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle v_{\mathrm{vd}}

\displaystyle\left\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}:\mathrm{Re}\lambda\leq-\frac{\bar{\ell^{2}}}{r_{I}^{2}}\right\}

for
\displaystyle|\ell|=\mathcal{O}(1/\delta)
or larger.

1.3

A natural question concerns the nature of these linear instabilities in the nonlinear dynamics of the spots/gaps. In the large radius limit, we expect such solutions to inherit the sideband instability of the nearby stationary front; in
[
4
]
, it was demonstrated that this sideband instabilty can lead to the appearance of finger-like patterns along the front interface, which can in turn lead to labyrinthine patterns which expand spatially into the homogeneous states. By performing direct numerical simulations using the unstable spot and gap solutions from Figure
8
as initial data, we see a similar instability manifest along the (circular) interface; see Figure
9
for snapshots of these simulations. We leave a more detailed study of the appearance of such finger-like patterns, and the relation to the corresponding instabilities in the stationary front interface to future work.

Obtaining the stability of spots of smaller radii rigorously appears to be a challenging problem, and we leave this to future work; in particular our existence analysis does not immediately extend to this regime. Additionally, unlike prior works which considered the stability of radially symmetric solutions using singular perturbation methods
[
41
,
42
]
in a
\displaystyle 3
-component FitzHugh–Nagumo system, the solutions of the linearized equations (
4.15
) do not have explicit representations in terms of special functions, which makes it difficult to determine
\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(\ell)
for smaller values of
\displaystyle r_{I}
. This is related to the challenges which arise in the existence analysis in §
3
due to the nonlinear reduced flow on the slow manifold
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{+}_{\delta}
.

Another natural line of further research concerns investigating whether the present insights obtained on specific model (
1.1
) can be lifted to the general setting of the 2-component singularly perturbed reaction diffusion systems considered in the companion paper
[
4
]
. In that paper, we study the (in)stability of planar fronts with respect to longitudinal perturbations, as we did for (
1.1
) in section §
2
, and derive two general, and relatively simple, criteria on the emergence of the sideband instability mechanism (that typically leads to finger-like patterns). The present analysis indicates that the same mechanism may drive the (in)stability of large spots and gaps in the general setting of
[
4
]
, although one should not underestimate the technicalities involved in establishing the counterparts of Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
for the general model. It is less clear from the combination of the present insights and those of
[
4
]
, under which conditions spots and gaps with a radius
\displaystyle r_{I}
of
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(1)
will be unstable (as is the case for (
1.1
)). The nature of the analysis in section §
4.4
suggests that it is possible to derive a general (in)stability result for spots and gaps with radius
\displaystyle r_{I}=\mathcal{O}(1)
against perturbations with
\displaystyle|\ell|\gg 1
. This suggests that also in the general setting, spots and gaps with radius
\displaystyle r_{I}
‘sufficiently small’ are potentially the ‘most stable’ (radially symmetric) localized patterns. Thus, the issue of the existence and stability of spots and gaps of sufficiently small radius is a central question and resolving that question may explain the abundance of ‘spikes’ in the literature on localized patterns in singularly perturbed reaction-diffusion systems – see
[
5
,
23
,
44
]
and the references therein and Remark
1.1
. These spikes have a fully homoclinic nature, in the sense that they are not close to a concatenation of almost heteroclinic orbits: away from the slow manifold
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{0}_{\delta}
they only follow the fast (spatial) dynamics, they do not follow the slow flow on a second slow manifold
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{+}_{\delta}
– as is the case for the patterns constructed here (with
\displaystyle r_{I}=\mathcal{O}(1)
). Thus, by studying spot and gap patterns with radius
\displaystyle r_{I}
decreasing from being
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(1)
to asymptotically small, one needs to zoom in on the subtle process through which a localized pattern detaches from
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{+}_{\delta}
during its jump away from and back to
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{0}_{\delta}
– see also
[
21
]
.
