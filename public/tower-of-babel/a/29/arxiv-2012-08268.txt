Monoidal Categories for Formal Concept Analysis
Sean Tull

Abstract
We investigate monoidal categories of formal contexts in which states correspond to formal concepts. In particular we examine the category of bonds or Chu correspondences between contexts, which is known to be equivalent to the *-autonomous category of complete sup-lattices. We show that a second monoidal structure exists on both categories, corresponding to the direct product of formal contexts defined by Ganter and Wille, and discuss the use of these categories as compositional models of meaning.



Introduction

Formal concept analysis (FCA)
is a highly successful framework for reasoning about collections of objects and their properties, initiated by Wille
[
Wil92
]
.
Starting from a system described by a
formal context
of
objects
and the
attributes
these attain, the central feature of FCA is the construction of its hierarchy of
formal concepts
, which form a complete lattice known as the
concept lattice
. FCA has found many successful applications in semantics, including data mining, machine learning, the semantic web, and linguistics
[
GW99
,
GSW05
]
.

A more recently developed framework is that of
Categorical Distributional Compositional Models of Meaning (DisCo)
, initiated by Coecke, Clark and Sadrzadeh
[
CSC10
]
. Typically drawing on Lambek’s theory of
pregroup grammars

[
Lam08
]
, this provides a structured recipe for deriving the meaning of a sentence in terms of the meaning of its individual words when these exist in an autonomous category. More generally, Delpeuch has extended the framework to any monoidal category
[
Del19
]
.

Though vector spaces are most commonly used, more semantic categories have recently been explored in the DisCo framework, including the use of density matrices for word meanings
[
BSC15
]
, and convex relational spaces modelling Gardenfors’ framework of
conceptual spaces

[
Gär04
,
BCG+19
]
.

In this work, we investigate monoidal categories of formal contexts, to serve as new models of meaning in frameworks for compositional semantics such as DisCo. Conversely, one may hope that category theory may provide new tools for FCA, as argued by Mori
[
Mor08
]
and Pavlovic
[
Pav12
,
Pav20
]
.

Since word meanings in the DisCo formalism are represented by states, we wish to consider categories whose objects are formal contexts and states correspond to their formal concepts. However, beyond this there is freedom in both our choice of morphism and tensor product of formal contexts, and several have been proposed for each
[
Wil85
,
GW99
,
KHZ05
,
Mor08
,
Ern14
]
.

The morphisms we consider are equivalent to the notion of
bond
between formal contexts introduced by Ganter and Wille
[
GW99
]
, which Mori has studied in detail via the equivalent notion of
Chu correspondence

[
Mor08
]
, and which we show also coincide with the morphisms of contexts studied by Moshier
[
Mos16
]
. These form our category of interest
\mathbf{Cxt}
. Taking the concept lattice is known to provide an equivalence of categories between
\mathbf{Cxt}
and the category
\mathbf{SupLat}
of complete sup-lattices. Since the latter is known to have a *-autonomous structure given by the tensor product
\boxtimes
of sup-lattices, this yields the *-autonomous monoidal structure
(\mathbf{Cxt},\boxtimes)
described by Mori in
[
Mor08
]
.

However, one may prefer a tensor structure motivated by formal concepts themselves, rather than lattices. Wille has in fact introduced a notion of
direct product
of formal contexts
[
Wil85
]
. Here we show these provide an alternative symmetric monoidal structure
(\mathbf{Cxt},\otimes)
. Wille has also shown the direct product to correspond to an alternative tensor product
\otimes
of complete lattices. We extend Wille’s results to merely sup-complete homomorphisms, to show that this tensor in fact provides a second symmetric monoidal structure
(\mathbf{SupLat},\otimes)
which makes the concept lattice a monoidal equivalence.
In summary then, for each of the corresponding tensors
\underline{\otimes}\in\{\otimes,\boxtimes\}
on
\mathbf{Cxt}
and
\mathbf{SupLat}
, taking concept lattices provides an equivalence of symmetric monoidal categories
(\mathbf{Cxt},\underline{\otimes})\simeq(\mathbf{SupLat},\underline{\otimes})
. Here we briefly discuss the potential use of each monoidal structure in the DisCo framework, which would be desirable to explore in future work.

1
Formal Concept Analysis

Let us now introduce the basic ingredients of Formal Concept Analysis (FCA). Throughout we follow the presentation of
[
GW99
]
.

Definition 1
.

A
formal context
is a tuple

\mathbb{K}=(G,M,\models)

consisting of a set
G
of
objects
, a set
M
of
attributes
, and a relation
\models\subseteq G\times M
. For each
g\in G
and
m\in M
, whenever
\models(g,m)
we instead write
g\models m
and say that
the object
g
has the attribute
m
.

More generally, for any such context
\mathbb{K}
, for any subsets
A\subseteq G
and
B\subseteq M
we write
A\models B
whenever
a\models b
for all
a\in A
and
b\in B
. We define

\displaystyle A^{\prime}

\displaystyle:=\{m\in M\mid a\models m\ \forall a\in A\}\subseteq M

\displaystyle B^{\prime}

\displaystyle:=\{\ g\in G\ \mid g\models b\ \ \ \forall b\in B\}\subseteq G

We then have
A\subseteq B^{\prime}\iff B\subseteq A^{\prime}
. This means that the mappings
A\mapsto A^{\prime}
and
B\mapsto B^{\prime}
form a
Galois connection
between the partially ordered sets
\mathbb{P}(G)
and
\mathbb{P}(M)
, or in other words an adjunction

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{P}(G)^{\mathrm{\rm op}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\bot}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{P}(M)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(-)^{\prime}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(-)^{\prime}}

As a result we obtain (idempotent and order-preserving)
closure operators
on
\mathbb{P}(G)
and
\mathbb{P}(M)
given by

A\mapsto\overline{A}:=A^{\prime\prime}
and
B\mapsto\overline{B}:=B^{\prime\prime}
. For each subset
A
of
G
we call
\overline{A}
the
closure
of
A
, and say
A
is
closed
when
A=\overline{A}
, and similarly for
B\subseteq M
. For any
g\in G
we define
g^{\prime}=\{g\}^{\prime}
and
\overline{g}=\overline{\{g\}}
, and similarly for
m\in M
. We may now define concepts themselves.

Definition 2
.

A
(formal) concept
of a context
\mathbb{K}
is a pair
(A,B)
where
A\subseteq G
and
B\subseteq M
, satisfying
A=B^{\prime}
and
B=A^{\prime}
. We call
A
the
extent
and
B
the
intent
of the concept, respectively.

By definition, the extent of a concept is precisely the set of all objects which satisfy all the attributes of its intent. Conversely its intent describes precisely the attributes these objects all share. We can define an ordering on concepts by

(A_{1},B_{1})\leq(A_{2},B_{2}):\iff A_{1}\leq A_{2}\ \ (\iff B_{2}\leq B_{1})

The key result of FCA is now the following.

Theorem 3

(
Basic theorem of formal concept analysis
)
.

[
Wil92
]
For any context
\mathbb{K}
, the set of concepts
\mathbb{B}(\mathbb{K})
forms a complete lattice under
\leq
, with

\displaystyle\bigwedge_{i\in I}(A_{i},B_{i})

\displaystyle=\left(\bigcap_{i\in I}A_{i},\overline{\bigcup_{i\in I}B_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle\bigvee_{i\in I}(A_{i},B_{i})

\displaystyle=\left(\overline{\bigcup_{i\in I}A_{i}},\bigcap_{i\in I}B_{i}\right)

Example 5
.

Any set
A
determines a formal context

\underline{A}:=(A,A,\neq)

Here every subset
B\subseteq A
is closed, with
B^{\prime}=A\setminus B
, so that
\mathbb{B}(\underline{A})\simeq\mathbb{P}(A)
, the power set of
A
. In particular we define the
trivial context
to be
\mathbb{I}:=\underline{\{\star\}}
.

Example 6
.

For any partially ordered set
P
we can define a formal context

F(P):=(P,P,\leq)

The lattice
\mathbb{B}(F(P))
is the smallest complete lattice in which
P
can be order-embedded, known as the
Dedekind-MacNeille completion
of
P

[

. In particular, when
V
is a complete lattice we have an isomorphism
V\simeq\mathbb{B}(F(V))
. Thus every complete lattice arises as a concept lattice.

Example 7
.

Any Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
determines a formal context

(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{H},\bot)

where
\bot
is its orthogonality relation. The concept lattice of this context is isomorphic to the orthomodular lattice of subspaces
V\leq\mathcal{H}
, via
V\mapsto(V,V^{\bot})
.

1.1
Notation

We will shortly describe morphisms of formal contexts based on relations and so fix some conventions about these. For any sets
A,B
and any relation
R\colon A\to B
, meaning a subset
R\subseteq A\times B
, we denote the converse relation by
R^{\dagger}\colon B\ \to A
. For each subset
X\subseteq A
we set

R(X):=\{b\in B\mid(\exists x\in X)R(x,b)\}

We will often equate
R
with its induced mapping
A\to\mathbb{P}(B)
, and so define
R
by specifying the subsets
R(a):=R(\{a\})\subseteq B
for each
a\in A
. The map
X\mapsto R(X)
has an adjoint
R^{\bullet}\colon\mathbb{P}(B)\to\mathbb{P}(A)
given by

R^{\bullet}(Y):=\{a\in A\mid R(a)\subseteq Y\}

for each
Y\subseteq B
. Finally, we also define a map
R_{\bullet}\colon\mathbb{P}(A)\to\mathbb{P}(B)
by

R_{\bullet}(X):=\{b\in B\mid(\forall x\in X)\ R(x,b)\}

(1)

for each
X\subseteq A
.

2
A Category of Formal Contexts

We now introduce morphisms of contexts. In fact we will give four equivalent ways of describing such a morphism, with most of the results of this section being essentially due to Mori who studied these maps in
[
Mor08
]
. Throughout, let
\mathbb{K}_{1}=(G_{1},M_{1},\models)
,
\mathbb{K}_{2}=(G_{2},M_{2},\models)
,
\dots
be contexts.

Definition 8
.

In the category
\mathbf{Cxt}
, the objects are formal contexts
\mathbb{K}
and the morphisms
\mathbb{K}_{1}\to\mathbb{K}_{2}
are relations
R\colon G_{1}\to G_{2}
which are
closed
, meaning that

1.

R(g)\subseteq G_{2}
is closed, for all
g\in G_{1}
;

2.

R^{\bullet}\colon\mathbb{P}(G_{2})\to\mathbb{P}(G_{1})
preserves closed sets.

The composition of
R\colon\mathbb{K}_{1}\to\mathbb{K}_{2}
and
S\colon\mathbb{K}_{2}\to\mathbb{K}_{3}
is defined by

(S\circ R)(g):=\overline{(S(R(g))}\quad(\forall g\in G_{1})

The identity morphism on
\mathbb{K}
is the relation
g\mapsto\overline{g}
for all
g\in G
.

To establish that
\mathbf{Cxt}
is a valid category, we will use the following.

Lemma 9
.

A relation
R\colon G_{1}\to G_{2}
is closed iff
R(g)\subseteq G_{2}
is closed for all
g\in G_{1}
, and for all subsets
A\subseteq G_{1}
we have

\overline{R(A)}=\overline{R(\overline{A})}

(2)

In fact for all
R\colon\mathbb{K}_{1}\to\mathbb{K}_{2}
and
S\colon\mathbb{K}_{2}\to\mathbb{K}_{3}
in
\mathbf{Cxt}
and
A\subseteq G_{1}
we have

\overline{(S\circ R)(A)}=\overline{S(R(A))}

(3)

Proof.

For the first point, note that for any closed relation
R\colon G_{1}\to G_{2}
and
A\subseteq G_{1}
,
B\subseteq M_{2}
we have

\displaystyle R(A)\models B

\displaystyle\iff R(A)\subseteq B^{\prime}

\displaystyle\iff A\subseteq R^{\bullet}(B^{\prime})

\displaystyle\iff\overline{A}\subseteq R^{\bullet}(B^{\prime})\iff R(\overline{A})\models B

using that
R^{\bullet}(B^{\prime})
is closed since
B^{\prime}
is. It follows that (
2
) holds. Conversely if this is the case then for all
A\subseteq G_{1}
and
B\subseteq M_{2}
we have

\displaystyle A\subseteq R^{\bullet}(B^{\prime})

\displaystyle\iff R(A)\subseteq B^{\prime}

\displaystyle\iff\overline{R(A)}\subseteq B^{\prime}

\displaystyle\iff\overline{R(\overline{A})}\subseteq B^{\prime}\iff\overline{A}\subseteq R^{\bullet}(B^{\prime})

using that
B^{\prime}
is closed in the second step, and so
R^{\bullet}(B^{\prime})
is closed as required.

For (
3
) note that by definition

\displaystyle\overline{(S\circ R)(A)}=\overline{\bigcup_{a\in A}\overline{S(R(a))}}=\overline{\bigcup_{a\in A}S(R(a))}=\overline{S(R(A))}

using that in any context closure operators satisfy
\overline{\bigcup_{i\in I}A_{i}}=\overline{\bigcup_{i\in I}\overline{A_{i}}}
.
∎

Corollary 10
.

\mathbf{Cxt}
is a well-defined category.

Proof.

That composite of
R\colon\mathbb{K}_{1}\to\mathbb{K}_{2}
and
S\colon\mathbb{K}_{2}\to\mathbb{K}_{3}
is indeed a closed relation by Lemma
9
since for all
A\subseteq G_{1}
we have

\overline{(S\circ R)(A)}=\overline{S(R(A)}=\overline{S(\overline{R(A)})}=\overline{S(\overline{R(\overline{A})})}=\overline{S(R(\overline{A}))}=\overline{(S\circ R)(\overline{A})}

We further have
R\circ\mathrm{id}=R
since
R(g)=\overline{R(g)}=\overline{R(\overline{g})}
for all
g\in G
and
\mathrm{id}\circ R=R
again follows from Lemma
9
.
∎

Though our definition of morphism refers only to objects, and not attributes,
we see shortly that each closed relation
R\colon G_{1}\to G_{2}
is equivalently described by another
R^{*}\colon M_{2}\to M_{1}
in the opposite direction, related to
R
in the following manner studied by Mori.

Definition 11
.

[
Mor08
]

A
Chu correspondence

(R,S)\colon\mathbb{K}_{1}\to\mathbb{K}_{2}
is a pair of relations
R\colon G_{1}\to G_{2}
and
S\colon M_{2}\to M_{1}
for which each of the sets
R(g_{1})\subseteq G_{2}
and
S(m_{2})\subseteq M_{1}
are closed and we have

R(g_{1})\models m_{2}\iff g_{1}\models S(m_{2})

for all
g_{1}\in G_{1}
,
m_{1}\in M_{2}
.

Another notion of morphism of contexts was put forward by Ganter and Wille directly in the context of formal concept analysis
[
GW99
]
.

Definition 12
.

A
bond
is a relation
B\colon G_{1}\to M_{2}
for which
B(g_{1})\subseteq M_{2}
and
B^{\dagger}(m_{2})\subseteq G_{1}
are closed, for all
g_{1}\in G_{1}
and
m_{2}\in M_{2}
.

Thus a bond is simply a relation from
G_{1}
to
M_{2}
whose rows and columns are closed. We can now show that all of these notions of morphism are equivalent.

Proposition 13
.

For any contexts
\mathbb{K}_{1},\mathbb{K}_{2}
there are bijections between:

1.

Closed relations
R\colon G_{1}\to G_{2}
;

2.

Closed relations
R^{*}\colon M_{2}\to M_{1}
;

3.

Chu correspondences
(R,R^{*})\colon\mathbb{K}_{1}\to\mathbb{K}_{2}
;

4.

Bonds
B\colon G_{1}\to M_{2}
;

given by

R(g_{1})={R^{*}}^{\bullet}(g_{1}^{\prime})^{\prime}=B(g_{1})^{\prime}\qquad R^{*}(m_{2})=R^{\bullet}(m_{2}^{\prime})^{\prime}\qquad B(g_{1})=R(g_{1})^{\prime}\\

(4)

for each
g_{1}\in G_{1}
and
m_{2}\in M_{2}
.

Proof.

2

\iff

3
.
For any closed relation
R\colon G_{1}\to G_{2}
define
R^{*}\colon M_{2}\to M_{1}
as above. Since each set
R^{\bullet}(m_{2}^{\prime})
is closed we have
R^{\bullet}(m_{2}^{\prime})=R^{\bullet}(m_{2}^{\prime})^{\prime\prime}=R^{*}(m_{2})^{\prime}
and so

\displaystyle R(g_{1})\models m_{2}

\displaystyle\iff R(g_{1})\subseteq m_{2}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\iff g_{1}\in R^{\bullet}(m_{2}^{\prime})=R^{*}(m_{2})^{\prime}

\displaystyle\iff g_{1}\models R^{*}(m_{2})

and so
(R,R^{*})
is a Chu correspondence.

Conversely, let
(R,S)
be any Chu correspondence. Then it is easy to see that

. Then if
A\subseteq G_{2}

R^{\bullet}(A)=R^{\bullet}(A^{\prime\prime})=S(A^{\prime})^{\prime}

\displaystyle\iff g_{1}\in B^{\dagger}(m_{2})=\overline{B^{\dagger}(m_{2})}

\displaystyle\iff g_{1}\models B^{\dagger}(m_{2})^{\prime}=S(m_{2})

making
(R,S)

Each of the above correspondences may be made functorial. Firstly, for any context
\mathbb{K}=(G,M,\models)
define the
dual context

\mathbb{K}^{*}:=(M,G,\models^{\dagger})

by swapping objects and attributes. Let us say that a category
\mathbf{C}
is
self-dual
when it comes with an equivalence
(-)^{*}\colon\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{\rm op}}\simeq\mathbf{C}
satisfying
A^{**}=A
for all objects
A

f^{**}=f

Lemma 14
.

The assignment
\mathbb{K}\mapsto\mathbb{K}^{*}
and
R\mapsto R^{*}
defines a self-duality
(-)^{*}\colon\mathbf{Cxt}^{\mathrm{\rm op}}\simeq\mathbf{Cxt}
.

Proof.

For any
R\colon\mathbb{K}_{1}\to\mathbb{K}_{2}
, Proposition
13
tells us that
R^{*}
is the unique morphism for which
(R,R^{*})
forms a Chu correspondence. It follows easily that

and that

since

is a Chu correspondence. Moreover if

and

is also, and so

∎

\displaystyle=(R_{2}(R_{1}(g)))^{\prime\prime\prime}=(R_{2}\circ R_{1})(g)^{\prime}

as required.

3
Monoidal Structures on Formal Contexts

We will now define two distinct monoidal structures on
\mathbf{Cxt}
, each sharing the same tensor unit
\mathbb{I}
, but with different tensor operations.

The first tensor has been described in the context of Chu correspondences
[
Mor08
]
, and is motivated by its close connection to the tensor product of sup-lattices, as we see in Section
4
.

Definition 17
.

For any contexts
\mathbb{K}_{1},\mathbb{K}_{2}
we define their
lattice tensor
as

\mathbb{K}_{1}\boxtimes\mathbb{K}_{2}:=(G_{1}\times G_{2},\mathbf{Cxt}(\mathbb{K}_{1},\mathbb{K}_{2}^{*}),\models)

where for any morphism
\mathbb{K}_{1}\to\mathbb{K}_{2}^{*}
corresponding to a relation
R\colon G_{1}\to G_{2}
we set
(g_{1},g_{2})\models R
whenever
R(g_{1})\models g_{2}
. Equivalently, we have that
(g_{1},g_{2})\in B
where
B
is the bond induced by
R
.

Another tensor of contexts has been introduced by Wille directly for FCA.

Definition 18
.

For any contexts
\mathbb{K}_{1},\mathbb{K}_{2}
we define their
concept tensor
as

4
Categories of Lattices

We now study how our category
\mathbf{Cxt}
and its monoidal structures relate to those of complete lattices, via the concept lattice construction.

Throughout, we write
\mathbf{SupLat}
for the category of complete sup-lattices. That is, the objects are complete lattices (with

) and the morphisms are mappings

\mathbf{InfLat}
for the category of complete inf-semilattices.

preserves suprema,

preserves infima

. We denote the 2-element complete lattice by

Recall that any context

5
Outlook: Applications

We close by briefly discussing potential applications of the category
\mathbf{Cxt}
as a compositional model of natural language meaning, which we hope to expand on in future work.

Before considering each of our tensors, we note that
\mathbf{Cxt}
itself naturally models order relations on words. In detail, following
[
CSC10
]
, we choose a formal context
\mathbb{K}
to represent each basic word type, e.g. nouns. The semantics of a noun
w
is then a state
\llbracket w\rrbracket\colon\mathbb{I}\to\mathbb{K}
, which by Lemma
25
corresponds to a concept of
\mathbb{K}
. The ordering on concepts allows one to capture entailment, as is treated using density matrices in
[
BSC15
]
.

5.1
DisCo in Closed Categories

The DisCo framework is typically applied to autonomous (i.e. rigid) monoidal categories
\mathbf{C}
, in which each object comes with a dual object with a ‘cup’ and ‘cap’
[
CSC10
]
. Any pregroup forms such a category, allowing one to interpret pregroup grammars in
\mathbf{C}
.

Though neither of our monoidal structures on
\mathbf{Cxt}
is autonomous, a known result due to Lambek tells us that in fact a simpler structure than pregroups is required in practice.

Definition 35
.

[
Lam97
]

A
protogroup
is a partially ordered monoid
(P,\leq,\cdot)
such that for every
a\in P
there are chosen elements
a^{l},a^{r}\in P
with

a^{l}\cdot a\leq 1\qquad a\cdot a^{r}\leq 1\qquad\text{(contractions)}

(16)

P
is a
pregroup
when additionally for all
a\in A
we have

1\leq a\cdot a^{l}\qquad 1\leq a^{r}\cdot a\qquad\text{(expansions)}

(17)

Lemma 36

(Switching Lemma,
[
Lam08
]
)
.

For any terms
t,t^{\prime}
in the free pregroup
\mathcal{P}_{B}
generated by basic types
B
, if
t\leq t^{\prime}
then there exists
t^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{B}
such that
t\leq t^{\prime\prime}
without expansions and
t^{\prime\prime}\leq t^{\prime}
without contractions.

In particular each inequality
t\leq s
for the sentence type
s
, determining that a phrase of type
t
is a valid sentence, requires contractions only, e.g.

N
S
Alice
N^{r}
likes
N^{l}
N
Bob

Hence for such applications protogroup grammars are sufficient, requiring our category to only have ‘cups’ (and not ‘caps’).

Any closed symmetric monoidal category
(\mathbf{C},\otimes,\multimap)
can model protogroup grammars as follows. For each object
A
we set
A^{l}=A^{r}=A^{*}:=(A\multimap I)
, and we interpret
a^{l}\cdot a\leq 1
as the canonical morphism

A^{*}\otimes A\to I
given by

A^{*}
A
:=
\mathsf{eval}
A\multimap I
A

(18)

and
a\cdot a^{r}\leq 1
by applying the symmetry to the above.

5.2
The Lattice Tensor

Since the category
(\mathbf{Cxt},\boxtimes)
is *-autonomous, it is in particular closed. Hence as above it can model protogroup grammars and all the sentence-parsing aspects of the DisCo framework. We may use the cups defined in (
18
) to interpret any valid sentence as a single concept.

5.3
The Concept Tensor

In contrast
(\mathbf{Cxt},\otimes)
is merely a symmetric monoidal category, with no apparent canonical cups or caps. However, Antonin Delpeuch has shown how the DisCo framework may be extended even to bare monoidal categories, thanks to the following result.

Theorem 37
.

[
Del19
]

For any monoidal category
\mathbf{C}
there is a free autonomous category
L(\mathbf{C})
with a strong monoidal full and faithful embedding
\mathbf{C}\hookrightarrow L(\mathbf{C})
.

Since the embedding is full, this means that any interpretation of a sentence as a state
I\to S
involving formal cups and caps can be re-arranged to a valid morphism in
\mathbf{C}
, and so be rewritten without them.

In future it would be desirable to fully explore the usefulness of both tensors on
\mathbf{Cxt}
when modelling sentence meanings.
