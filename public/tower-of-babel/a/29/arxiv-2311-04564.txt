Free fermions, KdV charges, generalised Gibbs ensembles, modular transforms and line defects
Max Downing, and Gérard M. T. Watts

Abstract
In this paper we return to the question of the modular properties of a generalised Gibbs ensemble of a single free fermion. We extend our previous proposals to a GGE containing an arbitrary number of conserved charges and provide a physical interpretation of the result in terms of a line defect. The defect description perfectly explains the product formula for the modular transformation we found previously. We also give a proposal for a Hamiltonian approach to the line defect.



1
Introduction

In this paper we return to the interpretation and generalisation of the results we obtained in
Downing:2021mfw
. In that paper we proposed an exact formula for the modular transform of a generalised Gibbs ensemble (GGE) of a single massless free fermion in the presence of a single extra conserved quantity.
In this paper we provide a physical interpretation for this formula (which was lacking in
Downing:2021mfw
) and we extend the results to an arbitrary finite combination of conserved charges. The physical interpretation is given by introducing a defect into the system. The presence of this defect can be seen in the TBA equations which were used in
Downing:2021mfw
to derive our conjecture for the modular transform of the GGE and provides an explanation for the peculiar product form of the modular transform. This defect can also be constructed explicitly in a Hamiltonian formalism.

We also note that a specialisation of our results to the case
q=1
and a single conserved quantity had already been proven in the mathematical literature
hrj:8932
, and that a complete proof of these results for a finite collection of charges can be found in the companion paper
downing2023modular
. For the background to and motivation for this work, we refer the reader to
Downing:2021mfw
.

We start in section
2
with a quick recap of the main result in
Downing:2021mfw
and in section
3
we explain its interpretation in terms of a system with a defect.
This leads to an interpretation of the modular transform formulae in terms of an altered quantisation condition on the fermion modes which we explain in section
4
.
In section
5
we then generalise the results in
Downing:2021mfw
for a single charge of spin 3 to an arbitrary but finite combination of charges. In section
6
we extend the conjecture to the case with an infinite number of charges and in section
7
to the “(R,-)” sector which generalises the modular properties of the eta function.
Finally, in section
8
, we present a construction of the defect in a Hamiltonian formalism based on the methods of
Toth:2006tj
, and then conclude with some conjectures and observations.

3
Physical interpretation in terms of defects

In our original paper we did not have a satisfactory physical understanding of the modular transformed formulae. We now have one proposal which is both simple and elegant, which is to consider the original GGE not as a change in the action or Hamiltonian of the free fermion, but instead as due to the insertion of a line defect. We now present evidence for this interpretation.

The original picture as envisaged in
Downing:2021mfw
was that the GGE could be considered as the partition function for some system on a torus, expressed as a trace, as in figure
1
.

H=\frac{2\pi}{R}(L_{0}-c/24)-\frac{\alpha}{L}I_{3}&&H^{\prime}=\;\;?\end{array}

Original interpretation of the modular transformed GGE traces.
We call (I) the direct channel and (II) the crossed channel.

H^{\prime}

\displaystyle E_{0}^{{\text{NS}}/{\text{R}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi}{L}\,h_{0}^{{\text{NS}}/{\text{R}}}(\tau,\alpha)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\pi L}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{t}{1\pm e^{t}}f(-\alpha\tau^{3}t^{2}/4{\pi^{2}})\,{\rm d}t

(24)

(25)

4
Fermion quantisation condtion

Comparing the ground state energy (
19

Downing:2021mfw
with the TBA expression in the presence of a defect (
24

\displaystyle T(u)=\pm\exp({i\alpha u^{3}R^{3}/8{\pi^{3}}L^{3}})=\pm\exp({i\alpha R^{3}p^{3}/8{\pi^{3}}})\;.

(27)

We consider the unperturbed system (without the defect) to be the fermion in the NS sector for which the momentum
p_{n}=(2\pi/L)k_{n}
of the modes
\psi_{-k_{n}}
satisfy

\displaystyle e^{ip_{n}L}=e^{2\pi ik_{n}}=-1\;,\;\;k_{n}=\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{3}{2},\ldots\;.

(28)

When the defect with transmission factor
T(u)
is introduced into the system, the quantisation condition is altered to

\displaystyle e^{ipL}T(u)=\pm e^{ipL+i\alpha R^{3}p^{3}/8{\pi^{3}}}=-1

(29)

so that

\displaystyle ip_{n}L+i\alpha(Rp_{n}/2\pi)^{3}=2n\pi i\;,\;\;\;\;\begin{cases}n\in\mathbb{Z}+\tfrac{1}{2}&{\text{NS}}\;,\\
n\in\mathbb{Z}&{\text{R}}\;.\end{cases}

(30)

In terms of
x=ipL
this is precisely the condition (
14
)

\displaystyle x_{n}-\alpha\left(\frac{Rx_{n}}{2\pi L}\right)^{3}=2n\pi i\;,\;\;\;\;\begin{cases}n\in\mathbb{Z}+\tfrac{1}{2}&{\text{NS}}\;,\\
n\in\mathbb{Z}&{\text{R}}\;,\end{cases}

(31)

and the condition that the fermion is actually right-moving is that the real part of
p
is positive, so that the imaginary part of
x
is positive.

This means that in the presence of the defect, the fermion is quantised with modes
\psi_{-k_{n}}
for each solution of (
14
) with positive imaginary part. Taking the standard expression for the partition function where each mode of energy
E_{n}
contributes a factor

\displaystyle(1+e^{-RE_{n}})=(1+e^{-Rp_{n}})=(1+e^{iRx_{n}/L})=(1+e^{\tau x_{n}})

(32)

to the partition function
Z=\Tr(e^{-RH})
, we get exactly the results (
9
), (
10
) and (
11
).

These formulae do not represent the trace over the product of three separate sets of fermion modes, as we conjecture in
Downing:2021mfw
, but instead the trace over the set of modes of a single fermion subject to the deformed quantisation condition (
29

Equation (
46
) is the quantisation condition for the one particle energies in the presence of the line defect.
In section
8
we reproduce the quantisation condition (
46
) by constructing the defect Hamiltonian explicitly.

6
Infinite collection of charges

We can extend the conjectured transform even further to the case where we have an infinite number of charges inserted. This case was also commented on in
downing2023modular
but the transformation was not proved there. We will discuss the issues involved with proving these transforms at the end of this section.

Formally the GGE with all charges inserted is

\Tr_{\text{NS/R},\pm}\left(e^{\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\alpha_{2n-1}I_{2n-1}}q^{I_{1}}\right)=e^{-\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\alpha_{2n-1}c_{2n-1}^{\text{NS/R}}}q^{-c_{1}^{{\text{NS}}/{\text{R}}}}\prod_{k\geq 0}\left(1\pm e^{\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\alpha_{2n-1}k^{2n-1}}q^{k}\right)\;.

(47)

Since this contains both infinite series and products there are potential convergence issues. We must restrict the domain of the chemical potentials,
\alpha_{2n-1}
, so that the right hand side of (
47
) is well defined. We have to consider the convergence of the two series

2\pi i\tau k+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\alpha_{2n-1}k^{2n-1}\quad\text{and}\quad 2\pi i\tau c_{1}^{{\text{NS}}/{\text{R}}}+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\alpha_{2n-1}c_{2n-1}^{\text{NS/R}}\;,

(48)

and the convergence of the product. Since the product in (
47
) is over all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
or
\mathbb{Z}+\frac{1}{2}
with
k\geq 0
, the radius of convergence of the series
2\pi i\tau k+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{2n-1}k^{2n-1}
must be infinite. Additionally, as
k
increases, the value of the exponential of the series must decay sufficiently fast for the product to converge. We also need the series
2\pi i\tau c_{1}^{{\text{NS}}/{\text{R}}}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{2n-1}c_{2n-1}^{\text{NS/R}}
to converge. These conditions together constrain the allowed values of the chemical potentials if we want (
47
) to be well defined.

Alternatively if the right hand side is not well defined for our choice of
\alpha_{2n-1}
we can obtain a well defined expression through a regularisation process. We first define the function
f_{\tau,{\bm{\alpha}}}(k)
as the power series expansion

f_{\tau,{\bm{\alpha}}}(k)=2\pi i\tau k+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\alpha_{2n-1}k^{2n-1}\;,\quad|k|<R\;,

(49)

which has radius of convergence
R
. If
R
is finite then as we take the product over
k
in (
47
) there is a point where
k>R
so the power series will no longer converge. However if the function
f_{\tau,{\bm{\alpha}}}(k)
is defined for all
k>0
when we can replace the power series with
f
in the product. We still need to check that the product converges.

There is also the series
2\pi i\tau c_{1}^{{\text{NS}}/{\text{R}}}+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\alpha_{2n-1}c^{\text{NS/R}}_{2n-1}
to consider. If this series does not converge for our choice of chemical potentials we can again regularise this series as follows. Using the integral representation (
2
) for
c^{\text{NS/R}}_{2n-1}
the series can be written as

\begin{split}&\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{2n-1}c^{\text{R}}_{2n-1}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dt}{2\pi i}\frac{f_{\tau,{\bm{\alpha}}}\left(\frac{t}{2\pi i}\right)}{e^{t}-1}\;,\\
&\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{2n-1}c^{\text{NS}}_{2n-1}=-\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dt}{2\pi i}\frac{f_{\tau,{\bm{\alpha}}}\left(\frac{t}{2\pi i}\right)}{e^{t}+1}\;.\end{split}

(50)

Again this is a formal relation since if the power series of
f_{\tau,{\bm{\alpha}}}(t)
has a finite radius of convergence and we are integrating over
t>0
there will be a point where the expansion is no longer valid and the integral and sum cannot be swapped. We need the integrals to converge for the GGE to be defined but this is a weaker condition then the convergence of the original sum.

Using these regularisations we will take the GGEs to be defined as

\begin{split}&\chi^{{\text{R}},+}(\tau,{\bm{\alpha}})=\Tr_{\text{R},+}\left(e^{\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\alpha_{2n-1}I_{2n-1}}q^{I_{1}}\right)=\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dt}{2\pi i}\frac{f_{\tau,{\bm{\alpha}}}\left(\frac{t}{2\pi i}\right)}{e^{t}-1}\right)\prod_{k=0}\left(1+e^{f_{\tau,{\bm{\alpha}}}(k)}\right)\;,\\
&\chi^{{\text{NS}},\pm}(\tau,{\bm{\alpha}})=\Tr_{\text{NS},\pm}\left(e^{\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\alpha_{2n-1}I_{2n-1}}q^{I_{1}}\right)=\exp\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dt}{2\pi i}\frac{f_{\tau,{\bm{\alpha}}}\left(\frac{t}{2\pi i}\right)}{e^{t}+1}\right)\prod_{k=\frac{1}{2}}\left(1\pm e^{f_{\tau,{\bm{\alpha}}}(k)}\right)\;.\end{split}

(51)

We can now extend the transformation for the finite case (
42
) to a conjectured transform for the infinite case

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\chi^{{\text{NS}},+}({\widehat{\tau}},{\bm{\alpha}})\\
\chi^{{\text{NS}},-}({\widehat{\tau}},{\bm{\alpha}})\\
\chi^{{\text{R}},+}({\widehat{\tau}},{\bm{\alpha}})\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&0&\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\\
0&{\sqrt{2}}&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\widehat{\chi}^{{\text{NS}},+}(\tau,{\bm{\alpha}})\\
\widehat{\chi}^{{\text{NS}},-}(\tau,{\bm{\alpha}})\\
\widehat{\chi}^{{\text{R}},+}(\tau,{\bm{\alpha}})\end{pmatrix}\;,\;\;{\widehat{q}}=\exp(2\pi i{\widehat{\tau}})\;,\;\;{\widehat{\tau}}=-1/\tau\;,

(52)

where

\displaystyle\widehat{\chi}^{{\text{NS}},\pm}(\tau,{\bm{\alpha}})

\displaystyle=q^{h_{0}^{{\text{NS}}}(\tau,{\bm{\alpha}})}\prod_{k\in\mathbb{Z}+\frac{1}{2}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{j}(k)\\
\text{Im}(x_{j}(k))>0\end{subarray}}\left(1\pm e^{\tau x_{j}(k)}\right)\;,

(53)

\displaystyle\widehat{\chi}^{{\text{R}},+}(\tau,{\bm{\alpha}})

\displaystyle=2^{N}q^{h_{0}^{{\text{R}}}(\tau,{\bm{\alpha}})}\,\prod_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{j}(k)\\
\text{Im}(x_{j}(k))>0\end{subarray}}\left(1+e^{\tau x_{j}(k)}\right)\;.

(54)

The ground state eigenvalues
h_{0}^{{\text{NS}}/{\text{R}}}(\tau,\alpha)
are given by

\displaystyle h_{0}^{{\text{NS}}/{\text{R}}}(\tau,\alpha)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}u\log(1\pm e^{-f_{\widehat{\tau},{\bm{\alpha}}}(iu\tau/2\pi)})\;,

(55)

the functions
x_{i}(k)
satisfy

f_{\widehat{\tau},{\bm{\alpha}}}\left(\frac{i\tau x}{2\pi}\right)=2\pi ik\;,

(56)

where
k\in\mathbb{Z}
for the R sector and
k\in\mathbb{Z}+\frac{1}{2}
for the NS sector and
N
is the number of
x_{i}(k)
that vanish at
k=0
.

Since we do not have an explicit expression for the function
f_{\tau,{\bm{\alpha}}}(x)
we cannot say much about the solutions
x_{i}(k)
. When proving the transform in
downing2023modular
for the finite case the
x_{i}(k)
are roots of a polynomial. This allowed us to derive several properties of the roots that were then used in the proof. It would be interesting to consider the cases where we have a set of the chemical potentials were infinitely many of the
\alpha_{2n-1}
are non-zero so that we have a known function
f_{\tau,{\bm{\alpha}}}(x)
. If the
x_{i}(k)
are also known functions then we can potentially prove the transform in these special cases.

7
The “
(R,-)
” sector

In this section we will also consider the sector “(R,
-
)” in which the fermion field is periodic on both cycles of the torus and so its contribution is calculated by taking a trace in the Ramond sector with the insertion of
(-1)^{F}
. Since the periodicity is the same on both cycles, the sector
is invariant under
\tau\to-1/\tau
which interchanges the cycles.

While this sector does contribute to correlation functions in the conformally invariant Ising model, for example the one-point function of the energy operator, it gives zero contribution to the partition function in the Ising model.
The reason is that the the full Ising model is constructed from left- and right- moving fermions. The relevant state space is then in the
(R,R)
sector, with both left- and right- moving fermions. The presence of two anticommuting fermion zero modes
\{\psi_{0},\bar{\psi}_{0}\}=0
means the ground state space is two-dimensional with equal numbers of bosonic and fermionic states. The same is true at every level in the
(R,R)
sector and so the trace
\Tr_{(R,R)}(\,(-1)^{F}e^{-RH})
contributes zero to the partition function.

The same equality of bosonic and fermion states also occurs when we consider just the left-moving sector but want to include both
(-1)^{F}
and
\psi_{0}
. Since these also anticommute,
\{\psi_{0},(-1)^{F}\}=0
, there must be at least a two-dimensional highest weight space with equal numbers of bosonic and fermionic states and so

\displaystyle\Tr_{{\text{R}}}\left(\,(-1)^{F}q^{L_{0}-c/24}\right)=0\;.

(57)

We can, however, consider a restricted fermion algebra which does not include the fermion zero mode, and a corresponding state space with a one-dimensional highest weight space
R^{\prime}
spanned by a bosonic state of conformal weight
1/16
. In this case the traces are simply

\displaystyle\Tr_{{\text{R}}^{\prime}}\left(\,q^{L_{0}-c/24}\,\right)

\displaystyle=q^{1/24}\prod_{n>0}(1+q^{n})=\tfrac{1}{2}\chi^{{\text{R}},+}(q)\;,

(58)

\displaystyle\Tr_{{\text{R}}^{\prime}}\left(\,(-1)^{F}\,q^{L_{0}-c/24}\,\right)

\displaystyle=q^{1/24}\prod_{n>0}(1-q^{n})=\eta(\tau)\;,

(59)

where
\eta(\tau)
is the usual Dedekind eta function which transforms as a modular form of weight 1/2:

\displaystyle\eta(-1/\tau)=\sqrt{-i\tau}\,\eta(\tau)\;.

(60)

While this function is not invariant, as might have been hoped for from the previous discussion of periodicities – a fact we can attribute to the omission of the fermion zero mode – it certainly has well-defined modular properties.
It is then straightforward to extend all the considerations of the previous sections to traces in the
(R^{\prime},-)
sector, with the result (again proven in
downing2023modular
):

\displaystyle\chi^{{\text{R}},-}({\widehat{\tau}},{\bm{\alpha}})

\displaystyle=\sqrt{-i\tau}\,\widehat{\chi}^{{\text{R}},-}(\tau,{\bm{\alpha}})\;,\;\;

(61)

where the functions
\chi
and
\widehat{\chi}
are

\displaystyle\chi^{{\text{R}},-}(\tau,{\bm{\alpha}})

\displaystyle=q^{1/24}\prod_{n=1}\left(1-q^{n}e^{p^{({\bm{\alpha}})}(n)}\right)\;,

(62)

\displaystyle\widehat{\chi}^{{\text{R}},-}(\tau,{\bm{\alpha}})

\displaystyle=q^{h_{0}^{{\text{R}}}(\tau,{\bm{\alpha}})}\,\prod_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{j}(k)\\
\text{Im}(x_{j}(k))>0\end{subarray}}\left(1-e^{\tau x_{j}(k)}\right)\;,

(63)

and the roots
x_{i}(k)
satisfy
(
46
).

8
Defect Hamiltonian

In section
3
, we stated that formally we can consider the Hamiltonian in the crossed channel to be the perturbation of the usual conformal Hamiltonian by a local field
D(0)
such that the spectrum of the Hamiltonian matches the quantisation condition on the fermion modes.
In this section we present one construction of just such a modified Hamiltonian.

If we consider, as before, just the insertion of
I_{3}
, then we meet the immediate problem that the line defect in the original (direct) channel can be considered as the integral of an irrelevant field,

\displaystyle e^{\alpha I_{3}}\;,\;\;I_{3}=\left(\tfrac{L}{2\pi}\right)^{3}\int_{0}^{L}\tfrac{1}{2}(\psi^{\prime\prime\prime}(x)\psi(x))\,\frac{{\rm d}x}{2\pi}=\left(\tfrac{L}{2\pi}\right)^{3}\int_{0}^{L}\tfrac{6}{7}(T(x)T(x))\,\frac{{\rm d}x}{2\pi}\;.

(64)

Our first guess for the perturbing field
D(x)
will be that same irrelevant field. However, perturbations by irrelevant fields are well known to be plagued by divergences and so we have used an approach which seems to avoid such problems: this is the method of G.Zs. Tóth
Toth:2006tj
which proved very effective for the boundary perturbation of the free fermion by the irrelevant field
T(x)
without the need for any further explicit regularisation or renormalisation. The effect of the perturbation was to induce a boundary reflection factor which (as with the defect transmission factor here) modified the fermion quantisation condition. The construction of
Toth:2006tj
added a boundary field to the Hamiltonian which led to altered equations of motion for the fermion which in turn led to altered boundary conditions and reproduced precisely the correct quantisation condition. In this section we attempt to repeat that idea for the defect transmission factor.

Throughout this section we will be working with space,
x
, and time,
t
, coordinates rather than the complex coordinates
(z,\bar{z})
used previously.

We start with a free right moving fermion
\psi(x,t)
. The field
\psi
is defined on the cylinder with
x\in[0,L]
and
t\in\mathbb{R}
. We impose either periodic
(+)
or anti periodic
(-)
boundary conditions in the spatial direction

\psi(0,t)=\pm\psi(L,t)\;,

(65)

and the equal time anti-commutator between two fields is

\{\psi(x,t),\psi(y,t)\}=-2\pi i\,\delta(x-y)\;.

(66)

Recall that the Hamiltonian for the free fermion is

H_{0}=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{0}^{L}{{\rm d}x}\,\partial_{x}\psi\,\psi=-\int_{0}^{L}\frac{{{\rm d}x}}{2\pi}T(x)=(L_{0}-c/24)\;.

(67)

We could introduce the line defect directly into the right-moving fermion on the line at
x=L/2
, but instead we use the folding trick to map it to an equivalent system of a left- and a right-moving fermion on a strip with a trivial boundary condition at
x=0
and a non-trivial boundary condition at
L/2
. We do this since it enables us to use precisely the same method as
Toth:2006tj
, as follows:

First, fold the line
\frac{L}{2}<x<L
over onto the line
0<x<\frac{L}{2}
, so the point
x
is identified with
L-x
. Now we define two new fermion fields
\Phi_{i}(x,t)
,
i=1,2
, that both live on the line
0<x<\frac{L}{2}
, in terms
of the original field
\psi(x,t)

\Phi_{1}(x,t)=\sqrt{\frac{iL}{\pi}}\psi(x,t)\;,\quad\Phi_{2}(x,t)=\sqrt{\frac{iL}{\pi}}\psi(L-x,t)\;.

(68)

We have chosen the normalisation of
\Phi_{i}
to match the conventions in
Toth:2006tj
. Note that in
Toth:2006tj
the fields
\Phi_{i}(x,t)
are defined on the cylinder
(x,t)\in[0,L]\times\mathbb{R}
while our
\Phi_{i}(x,t)
are defined on
(x,t)\in[0,L/2]\times\mathbb{R}
so the commutators and Hamiltonian differ from those in
Toth:2006tj
by factors of 2. Since
\psi
is right-moving on the full line, after the folding
\Phi_{1}
is right-moving and
\Phi_{2}
is left-moving. From (
65
) the new fields have the boundary conditions

\Phi_{1}(0,t)=\pm\Phi_{2}(0,t)\;,\quad\Phi_{1}\left(\tfrac{L}{2},t\right)=\Phi_{2}\left(\tfrac{L}{2},t\right)\;.

(69)

Additionally the anti-commutator (
66
) becomes

\begin{split}&\{\Phi_{1}(x,t),\Phi_{1}(y,t)\}=\{\Phi_{2}(x,t),\Phi_{2}(y,t)\}=2L\delta(x-y)\;,\\
&\{\Phi_{1}(x,t),\Phi_{2}(y,t)\}=2L(\delta(x+y-L)\pm\delta(x+y))\;.\end{split}

(70)

The second anti-commutation relation comes from the two boundaries where the fermion fields are identified as in (
69
). The Hamiltonian for the free fermions is now

H_{0}=-\frac{i}{4L}\int_{0}^{L/2}{{\rm d}x}\,(\Phi_{1}\partial_{x}\Phi_{1}-\Phi_{2}\partial_{x}\Phi_{2})\;.

(71)

We will use the Hamiltonian formalism to compute the equations of motion

\partial_{t}\Phi_{i}(x,t)=i[H_{0},\Phi_{i}(x,t)]\;.

(72)

However before we do this we introduce our conventions for the Dirac delta function and the Heaviside step function. When integrated against a test function the Dirac delta function gives the following results for
a>0

\int_{-a}^{a}{{\rm d}x}\,\delta(x)f(x)=f(0)\;,\quad\int_{0}^{a}{{\rm d}x}\,\delta(x)f(x)=\int_{-a}^{0}{{\rm d}x}\,\delta(x)f(x)=\frac{1}{2}f(0)\;.

(73)

The Heaviside step function is defined as the integral of the Dirac delta function

\Theta(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}{{\rm d}x}^{\prime}\,\delta(x^{\prime})\;,

(74)

so it takes the values

\Theta(x)=\begin{cases}0,&x<0\;,\\
\tfrac{1}{2},&x=0\;,\\
1,&x>0\;.\end{cases}

(75)

Hence if we integrate
\delta(x-y)
against a test function
f(x)
in the interval
[a,b]
then

\int_{a}^{b}{{\rm d}x}f(x)\delta(x-y)=f(y)(1-\Theta(a-y)-\Theta(y-b))\;,

(76)

and if we integrate
\delta^{\prime}(x-y)
against a test function then

\int_{a}^{b}{{\rm d}x}f(x)\delta^{\prime}(x-y)=\delta(y-b)f(b)-\delta(y-a)f(a)-f^{\prime}(y)(1-\Theta(a-y)-\Theta(y-b))\;.

(77)

We use these results and the anti-commutators (
70
) to compute the equations of motion (
72
)

\begin{split}&\partial_{t}\Phi_{1}(x,t)=-\partial_{x}\Phi_{1}(x,t)-\frac{1}{2}\delta(x)(\Phi_{1}(0,t)\mp\Phi_{2}(0,t))+\frac{1}{2}\delta\left(x-\tfrac{L}{2}\right)\left(\Phi_{1}\left(\tfrac{L}{2},t\right)-\Phi_{2}\left(\tfrac{L}{2},t\right)\right)\\
&+\Theta(-x)(\partial_{x}\Phi_{1}(0,t)\pm\partial_{x}\Phi_{2}(0,t))+\Theta\left(x-\tfrac{L}{2}\right)\left(\partial_{x}\Phi_{1}\left(\tfrac{L}{2},t\right)+\partial_{x}\Phi_{2}\left(\tfrac{L}{2},t\right)\right)\;,\end{split}

(78)

and

\begin{split}&\partial_{t}\Phi_{2}(x,t)=\partial_{x}\Phi_{2}(x,t)+\frac{1}{2}\delta(x)(\Phi_{2}(0,t)\mp\Phi_{1}(0,t))+\frac{1}{2}\delta\left(x-\tfrac{L}{2}\right)\left(\Phi_{1}\left(\tfrac{L}{2},t\right)-\Phi_{2}\left(\tfrac{L}{2},t\right)\right)\\
&-\Theta(-x)(\partial_{x}\Phi_{2}(0,t)\pm\partial_{x}\Phi_{1}(0,t))-\Theta\left(x-\tfrac{L}{2}\right)\left(\partial_{x}\Phi_{1}\left(\tfrac{L}{2},t\right)+\partial_{x}\Phi_{2}\left(\tfrac{L}{2},t\right)\right)\;,\end{split}

(79)

The delta function terms
\delta(x)
and
\delta\left(x-\frac{L}{2}\right)
in the above equations vanish by the boundary conditions (
69
). So we have

\begin{split}\partial_{t}\Phi_{1}(x,t)=&-\partial_{x}\Phi_{1}(x,t)+\Theta(-x)(\partial_{x}\Phi_{1}(0,t)\pm\partial_{x}\Phi_{2}(0,t))\\
&+\Theta\left(x-\tfrac{L}{2}\right)\left(\partial_{x}\Phi_{1}\left(\tfrac{L}{2},t\right)+\partial_{x}\Phi_{2}\left(\tfrac{L}{2},t\right)\right)\;,\\
\partial_{t}\Phi_{2}(x,t)=&\partial_{x}\Phi_{2}(x,t)-\Theta(-x)(\partial_{x}\Phi_{2}(0,t)\pm\partial_{x}\Phi_{1}(0,t))\\
&-\Theta\left(x-\tfrac{L}{2}\right)\left(\partial_{x}\Phi_{1}\left(\tfrac{L}{2},t\right)+\partial_{x}\Phi_{2}\left(\tfrac{L}{2},t\right)\right)\;,\end{split}

(80)

Solving these equations will give the usual free fermion solution and quantisation condition
(as we will see below).

Now we add a line defect at
x=\frac{L}{2}
. We do this by adding terms
H_{D}^{(2n-1)}
,
n=1,2,\dots
, to the Hamiltonian
H_{0}
to get
the full Hamiltonian for the system with a line defect:

H=H_{0}+\sum_{n=1}\lambda_{2n-1}H_{D}^{(2n-1)}\;,

(81)

and the equations of motion for the full system are

\partial_{t}\Phi_{i}(x,t)=i[H,\Phi_{i}(x,t)]\;.

(82)

We take the additional terms
H_{D}^{(2n-1)}
to be

H_{D}^{(2n-1)}=i\left(\Phi_{1}\left(\tfrac{L}{2},0\right)+\Phi_{2}\left(\tfrac{L}{2},0\right)\right)\lim_{y\rightarrow\frac{L}{2}}\partial_{y}^{2n-1}(\Phi_{2}(y,0)-\Phi_{1}(y,0))\;.

(83)

The limit regularises later expressions that would otherwise contain delta function singularities. It removes these singularities by using the prescription

\lim_{y\rightarrow\frac{L}{2}}\delta^{(n)}\left(y-\tfrac{L}{2}\right)=0\;,\quad n=0,1,\dots\;.

(84)

Throughout the calculation we will keep the limit explicit and only take it at the end. The equations of motion of the full system contain the commutator of the defect terms
H_{D}^{(2n-1)}
with the fields
\Phi_{i}

[H_{D}^{(2n-1)},\Phi_{i}(x,t)]=-4iL\delta\left(x-\tfrac{L}{2}\right)\lim_{y\rightarrow\frac{L}{2}}\partial_{y}^{2n-1}(\Phi_{2}(y,t)-\Phi_{1}(y,t))\;,\quad i=1,2\;.

(85)

This means the equations of motion for
\Phi_{i}

are now

\begin{split}&\partial_{t}\Phi_{1}(x,t)+\partial_{x}\Phi_{1}(x,t)\\
&=\Theta(-x)(\partial_{x}\Phi_{1}(0,t)\pm\partial_{x}\Phi_{2}(0,t))+\Theta(x-L/2)(\partial_{x}\Phi_{1}(L/2,t)+\partial_{x}\Phi_{2}(L/2,t))\\
&+4L\delta(x-L/2)\sum_{n=1}\lambda_{2n-1}\lim_{y\rightarrow L/2}\partial_{y}^{2n-1}(\Phi_{2}(y,t)-\Phi_{1}(y,t))\;,\\
\end{split}

(86)

\begin{split}&\partial_{t}\Phi_{2}(x,t)-\partial_{x}\Phi_{2}(x,t)\\
&=-\Theta(-x)(\partial_{x}\Phi_{2}(0,t)\pm\partial_{x}\Phi_{1}(0,t))-\Theta(x-L/2)(\partial_{x}\Phi_{1}(L/2,t)+\partial_{x}\Phi_{2}(L/2,t))\\
&+4L\delta(x-L/2)\sum_{n=1}\lambda_{2n-1}\lim_{y\rightarrow L/2}\partial_{y}^{2n-1}(\Phi_{2}(y,t)-\Phi_{1}(y,t))\;.\end{split}

(87)

We will use the equations of motion (
86
) and (
87
) to derive the quantisation condition for the single particle energies. Since the Hamiltonian is quadratic in the fields this is still a “free” fermion and hence the full spectrum is composed from the sum of multiple single particle states. Hence if we know the quantisation condition on the single particle states we know the full spectrum.

To find the one particle energies start with the eigenstates
|E_{1}\rangle
and
|E_{2}\rangle
of the full Hamiltonian
H
(
81
). We will derive the quantisation condition from the matrix elements

\langle E_{1}|\Phi_{i}(x,t)|E_{2}\rangle\;,\quad i=1,2\;.

(88)

In the Heisenberg picture, the time evolution of the fields is

\Phi_{i}(x,t)=e^{itH}\Phi_{i}(x,0)e^{-itH}\;.

(89)

Since the two states are eigenstates of
H
we find

\langle E_{1}|\Phi_{i}(x,t)|E_{2}\rangle=\langle E_{1}|\Phi_{i}(x,0)|E_{2}\rangle e^{ikt}=f_{i}(x)e^{ikt}\;,

(90)

for single particle energies.

We now solve the equations of motion and derive the quantisation condition (
97
). Plugging (
90
) into the equations of motion we get two coupled differential equations

\begin{split}ikf_{1}(x)=&-f_{1}^{\prime}(x)+\Theta(-x)(f_{1}^{\prime}(0)\pm f_{2}^{\prime}(0))+\Theta\left(x-\tfrac{L}{2}\right)\left(f_{1}^{\prime}\left(\tfrac{L}{2}\right)+f_{2}^{\prime}\left(\tfrac{L}{2}\right)\right)\\
&+4L\delta\left(x-\tfrac{L}{2}\right)\sum_{n=1}\lambda_{2n-1}\lim_{y\rightarrow\frac{L}{2}}(f_{2}^{(2n-1)}(y)-f_{1}^{(2n-1)}(y))\;,\end{split}

(91)

\begin{split}ikf_{2}(x)=&f_{2}^{\prime}(x)-\Theta(-x)(f_{2}^{\prime}(0)\pm f_{1}^{\prime}(0))-\Theta\left(x-\tfrac{L}{2}\right)\left(f_{1}^{\prime}\left(\tfrac{L}{2}\right)+f_{2}^{\prime}\left(\tfrac{L}{2}\right)\right)\\
&+4L\delta\left(x-\tfrac{L}{2}\right)\sum_{n=1}\lambda_{2n-1}\lim_{y\rightarrow L/2}(f_{2}^{(2n-1)}(y)-f_{1}^{(2n-1)}(y))\;,\end{split}

(92)

with boundary conditions from (
69
)

f_{1}(0)=\pm f_{2}(0)\;,\quad f_{1}\left(\tfrac{L}{2}\right)=f_{2}\left(\tfrac{L}{2}\right)\;.

(93)

In the interval
x\in[0,L/2)
the solution (up to an overall multiplicative constant) is

f_{1}(x)=e^{-ikx}\;,\quad f_{2}(x)=\pm e^{ikx}\;.

(94)

Since the first order differential equations contain a delta function supported at the boundary
x=\frac{L}{2}
, the solution will have a finite discontinuity there. Hence the solutions on the interval
x\in[0,L/2]
take the form

\begin{split}&f_{1}(x)=e^{-ikx}+\Theta\left(x-\tfrac{L}{2}\right)D_{1}(E_{1},E_{2})\;,\\
&f_{2}(x)=\pm e^{ikx}+\Theta\left(x-\tfrac{L}{2}\right)D_{2}(E_{1},E_{2})\;.\end{split}

(95)

The prescription (
84
) means the defect terms in the equations of motion don’t contain additional delta functions. The equations of motion reduce to a finite term and one proportional to a delta function. The finite piece fixes
D_{1}
and
D_{2}

D_{1}(k)=-D_{2}(k)=\pm e^{ikL/2}-e^{-ikL/2}\;,

(96)

which is compatible with the boundary conditions (
69
). The term proportional to the delta function gives the quantisation condition

\pm e^{ikL/2}-e^{-ikL/2}=-4iL(\pm e^{ikL/2}+e^{-ikL/2})\sum_{n=1}(-1)^{n}\lambda_{2n-1}k^{2n-1}\;.

(97)

More explicitly if we have periodic fermions (
+
) we find

\displaystyle D_{1}(k)=-D_{2}(k)=2i\sin\left(\tfrac{kL}{2}\right)\;,

(98)

\displaystyle\sin\left(\tfrac{kL}{2}\right)=4L\cos\left(\tfrac{kL}{2}\right)\sum_{n=1}(-1)^{n-1}\lambda_{2n-1}k^{2n-1}\;,

(99)

and if we have anti periodic fermions (
-
)

\displaystyle D_{1}(k)=-D_{2}(k)=-2\cos\left(\tfrac{kL}{2}\right)\;,

(100)

\displaystyle\cos\left(\tfrac{kL}{2}\right)=4L\sin\left(\tfrac{kL}{2}\right)\sum_{n=1}(-1)^{n}\lambda_{2n-1}k^{2n-1}\;.

(101)

Note that if we set
\lambda_{2n-1}=0
for all
n
then the quantisation conditions (
99
) and (
101
) become
\sin\left(\tfrac{kL}{2}\right)=0
and
\cos\left(\tfrac{kL}{2}\right)=0
respectively which lead to the one particle spectra
k=\frac{2\pi}{L}n
and
k=\frac{2\pi}{L}\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Hence the quantisation condition (
99
) corresponds to the R sector and (
101
) corresponds to the NS sector.

In order to obtain the quantisation conditions (
99
) and (
101
) we inserted the defect at the point
x=\frac{L}{2}
. However if we insert the defect at any other point then we would obtain the same quantisation conditions. We can write the defect operators (
83
) in terms of the original fermion field
\psi(x,t)
using (
68
)

H_{D}^{(2n-1)}=-\frac{2L}{\pi}\psi\left(\tfrac{L}{2},0\right)\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\left(\psi^{(2n-1)}\left(\tfrac{L}{2}+\epsilon,0\right)+\psi^{(2n-1)}\left(\tfrac{L}{2}-\epsilon,0\right)\right)\;,

(102)

where
\psi^{(n)}(x,t)=\partial_{x}^{n}\psi(x,t)
. If we want to place the defect at
x=aL
,
a\in[0,1]
rather than at
x=\frac{L}{2}
the defect operators are

H_{D}^{(2n-1)}=-\frac{2L}{\pi}\psi\left(aL,0\right)\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\left(\psi^{(2n-1)}\left(aL+\epsilon,0\right)+\psi^{(2n-1)}\left(aL-\epsilon,0\right)\right)\;.

(103)

We can again fold the line
x\in[0,L]
at the point where the defect has been placed and introduce the new fields

\Phi_{1}(x,t)=\sqrt{\frac{iL}{\pi}}\,\psi(2ax,t)\;,\quad\Phi_{2}(x,t)=\sqrt{\frac{iL}{\pi}}\,\psi(L-2(1-a)x,t)\;,\quad x\in[0,L/2]\;.

(104)

If the free Hamiltonian (
67
) and the defect operators (
103
) are written in terms of
\Phi_{1}
and
\Phi_{2}
we can again find the equations of motion and solving the equations of motion will lead to the same quantisation conditions (
99
) and (
101
).

Before describing how to reproduce the quantisation condition (
46
) from the defect quantisation conditions (
99
) and (
101
) we show that this formalism can be used to formally reproduce the spectrum of the transformed theory under the usual modular transform of the characters i.e. the GGE with only the
I_{1}
charge inserted.

8.1
Inserting
I_{1}

We first check that this defect Hamiltonian reproduces the energy spectrum for the transformed GGE with just an additional
I_{1}
charge inserted. Adding just an additional
I_{1}
charge is equivalent to changing the length of the system. In appendix
B
we show via the TBA equations that a change in the system length can naturally be interpreted as a defect. This is just the usual partition functions so the modular transform is known exactly. We will first look at the case

\Tr_{\text{NS},+}\left(e^{2\pi i(\alpha+\tau)I_{1}}\right)=\Tr_{\text{NS},+}\left(e^{-\frac{2\pi i}{\alpha+\tau}I_{1}}\right)\;.

(105)

Consider a rectangular torus with side lengths
R
and
L
so the modular parameter is
\tau=i\frac{L}{R}
. We also take
\alpha
to be pure imaginary and set
\alpha=i\frac{L\beta}{R}
. Hence the energies in the transformed theory satisfy

\displaystyle\cos\left(\frac{L(1+\beta)}{2\pi}k\right)=0

(106)

and take the form

\frac{2\pi}{L}\frac{1}{1+\beta}\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)\;,\quad n\in\mathbb{N}\;.

(107)

The question is: how to reproduce this from the
quantisation condition (
101
)?

The initial guess would be that including just
I_{1}
ought to correspond to only
\lambda_{1}
being non-zero.
If we
set
\lambda_{1}=-\frac{\beta}{8}
and
\lambda_{2n-1}=0
for
n>1
, then the
perturbing operator is

D=-\frac{\beta}{8}H_{D}^{(1)}=\frac{L\beta}{2\pi}\,\psi(L/2)\psi^{\prime}(L/2)=-\frac{L\beta}{4\pi}T(L/2)\;,

(108)

and the quantisation condition is

\cos\left(\tfrac{kL}{2}\right)=\frac{L\beta}{2}\sin\left(\tfrac{kL}{2}\right)k\;.

(109)

This is not the desired relation (
106
). Instead,
it matches (46) in
Toth:2006tj
, and we see that the perturbation cannot just be
T(0)
.

However we will see below that we need to add an infinite set of irrelevant operators of higher weight to our Hamiltonian in order to reproduce the required spectrum (
107
). In
Toth:2006tj
these higher weight operators aren’t added because they are considering a different physical systems where the spectrum is reproduced without including them.

We now assume
k
has a power series expansion in
\beta
with constant term
\frac{2\pi}{L}\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)
. Solving the quantisation condition perturbatively gives

k=\frac{2\pi}{L}\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)\left(1-\beta+\beta^{2}-\left(1-\frac{(1+2n)^{2}\pi^{2}}{12}\right)\beta^{3}+\left(1-\frac{(1+2n)^{2}\pi^{2}}{3}\right)\beta^{4}+\dots\right)\;.

(110)

We want to remove the additional terms that start at order
\beta^{3}
to reproduce (
107
). We do this by adding a term of the form
\beta^{3}k^{3}
to the quantisation condition. This corresponds to adding an
H_{D}^{(3)}
term to the Hamiltonian. The new quantisation condition is

\cos\left(\tfrac{kL}{2}\right)=\sin(\pi k)\left(\frac{L\beta}{2}k+\frac{1}{3}\left(\frac{L\beta}{2}\right)^{3}k^{3}\right)\;,

(111)

which has solutions

k=\frac{2\pi}{L}\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)\left(1-\beta+\beta^{2}-\beta^{3}+\beta^{4}-\left(1-\frac{(2n+1)^{4}\pi^{4}}{120}\right)\beta^{5}+\dots\right)\;.

(112)

We can continue to add
\beta^{2n-1}H_{D}^{(2n-1)}
terms to the Hamiltonian to get the required solution for
k
. If we do this order by order the quantisation condition becomes

\cos\left(\frac{Lk}{2}\right)=\sin\left(\frac{Lk}{2}\right)\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}2^{2n}(2^{2n}-1)B_{2n}}{(2n)!}\left(\frac{L\beta k}{2}\right)^{2n-1}\;,

(113)

where
B_{2n}
are the Bernoulli numbers. For
|L\beta k|<\pi
this series converges

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}2^{2n}(2^{2n}-1)B_{2n}}{(2n)!}\left(\frac{L\beta k}{2}\right)^{2n-1}=\tan\left(\frac{L\beta k}{2}\right)\;,

(114)

and the quantisation condition becomes

\cos\left(\frac{Lk}{2}\right)=\sin\left(\frac{Lk}{2}\right)\tan\left(\frac{L\beta k}{2}\right)\;,

(115)

or equivalently

\frac{\cos\left(\frac{L(1+\beta)k}{2}\right)}{\cos\left(\frac{L\beta k}{2}\right)}=0\;,

(116)

which is in turn equivalent to the desired relation (
106
) with the desired spectrum

k=\frac{2\pi}{L}\frac{1}{1+\beta}\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)\;,\quad n\in\mathbb{Z}\;.

(117)

Recall that the series in the quantisation condition (
113
) converges for
|L\beta k|<\pi
. For a fixed
n
this requires
\beta
to be in the interval

\beta\in\left(\frac{-1}{|2n+1|+1},\frac{1}{|2n+1|-1}\right)\;.

(118)

But if we want the series to converge for all
n
then the radius of convergence is 0. This means that our result for the quantised energies (
117
) is a formal result.

We now return to the Hamiltonian (
81
). In order to get the required quantisation condition, the
\lambda_{2n-1}
in (
81
) are

\lambda_{2n-1}=-\frac{2(2^{2n}-1)B_{2n}}{(2n)!}L^{2n-2}\beta^{2n-1}\;.

(119)

Hence the defect Hamiltonian can be written as

\sum_{n=1}\lambda_{2n-1}H_{I}^{(2n-1)}=-\frac{i}{L}\left(\Phi_{1}\left(\tfrac{L}{2},0\right)+\Phi_{2}\left(\tfrac{L}{2},0\right)\right)\lim_{y\rightarrow\frac{L}{2}}\tanh\left(\tfrac{L\beta}{2}\partial_{y}\right)(\Phi_{2}(y,0)-\Phi_{1}(y,0))\;.

(120)

This is an infinite sum of irrelevant operators which means the Hamiltonian may not be well defined.

We can also reproduce the energies for the Ramond sector as well using the quantisation condition (
99
). Again the coefficients in the defect Hamiltonian are

\lambda_{2n-1}=-\frac{2(2^{2n}-1)B_{2n}}{(2n)!}L^{2n-2}\beta^{2n-1}\;,

(121)

so the quantisation condition becomes

\sin\left(\frac{Lk}{2}\right)=-\cos\left(\frac{Lk}{2}\right)\tan\left(\frac{L\beta k}{2}\right)\;\Rightarrow\;\frac{\sin\left(\frac{L(1+\beta)k}{2}\right)}{\cos\left(\frac{L\beta k}{2}\right)}=0

(122)

and hence the one particle energies are

k=\frac{2\pi}{L}\frac{n}{1+\beta}\;,\quad n\in\mathbb{Z}\;.

(123)

These again match the results coming from the modular properties of the the characters.

8.2
Arbitrary finite collection of
I_{2n-1}

We can also use this defect formalism to reproduce the polynomial quantisation condition (
46
) we get from transforming a GGE with multiple charges inserted. Recall that we have the two quantisation conditions for the energy differences
k
, depending on if we are in the NS or R sectors respectively

\begin{split}&\cos\left(\frac{Lk}{2}\right)=4L\sin\left(\frac{Lk}{2}\right)\sum_{n=1}(-1)^{n}\lambda_{2n-1}k^{2n-1}\;,\\
&\sin\left(\frac{Lk}{2}\right)=4L\cos\left(\frac{Lk}{2}\right)\sum_{n=1}(-1)^{n-1}\lambda_{2n-1}k^{2n-1}\;.\end{split}

(124)

The required quantisation conditions are polynomials of the form

\sum_{n=1}^{N}\alpha_{2n-1}k^{2n-1}+\frac{kL}{2}=m\pi\;,

(125)

where
m\in\mathbb{Z}+\frac{1}{2}
for the NS sector and
m\in\mathbb{Z}
for the R sector. In order to get these quantisation conditions we need to tune the
\lambda_{2n-1}
such that

L\sum_{n=1}(-1)^{n}\lambda_{2n-1}k^{2n-1}=\tan\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}\alpha_{2n-1}k^{2n-1}\right)\;.

(126)

Formally this can always be done, we just expand the right hand side as a power series in
k
and find the coefficients
\lambda_{2n-1}
in terms of the
\alpha_{2n-1}
. Once we have fixed the
\lambda_{2n-1}
in terms of the
\alpha_{2n-1}
the quantisation conditions (
124
) become

\begin{split}&\cos\left(\frac{Lk}{2}\right)=\sin\left(\frac{Lk}{2}\right)\tan\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}\alpha_{2n-1}k^{2n-1}\right)\;,\\
&\sin\left(\frac{Lk}{2}\right)=-\cos\left(\frac{Lk}{2}\right)\tan\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}\alpha_{2n-1}k^{2n-1}\right)\;.\end{split}

(127)

These then lead to the required quantisation conditions (
125
). Again, the defect Hamiltonians that give rise to these quantisation conditions contain an infinite number of irrelevant operators and so the question of whether they are well defined is an issue.

9
Summary and Outlook

We have reconsidered the expressions for the modular transform of free-fermion GGEs we discussed in
Downing:2021mfw
. We first showed how our original conjectures could be reinterpreted in terms of a defect. This is not an especially new idea - it was possibly first discussed in
Negro
- but does explain the exact results we have in a very appealing physical way.

The same TBA equation (
19
) has also arisen previously in
caselle2013quantisation
in a discussion of
T\bar{T}
perturbations.
The context is a bit different - the transmission factor here arises there as the reflection factor for the reflection off a boundary for a theory with a non-trivial bulk scattering. As a result, the spectrum is not the same although formally the integrals are the same. Since the form of the defect transmission factor is exactly the same as the change in the bulk S-matrix for a
T\bar{T}
perturbation and since the
T\bar{T}
field does approach the
TT
field as it nears the boundary, it seems very likely that this is more than just a coincidence.

We also generalised the conjectures in
Downing:2021mfw
for the modular transform of a GGE with just the
I_{3}
charge to an arbitrary finite number of charges. This leads to very similar expressions over fermion modes which are given by the solutions to higher degree polynomial equations. These, together with the original conjectures, have been proven in
downing2023modular
.

It is straightforward to see that this result also has the interpretation as a defect, and this must be the case for a generic GGE on physical grounds. It is also easy to see directly from the TBA equations - we show this in appendix
B
in the case of a massive purely elastic scattering theory.

It is worth noting that this does not yet lead to an in principle closed action of the modular group since the result of a modular transform on a system with a finite number of conserved charges will usually lead to one with an infinite set of charges. Again the sets of infinite charges for which the GGE transform exists and can be defined are also worth investigating further.

We have also found a formal expression for the defect operator
D(0)
in the crossed channel so that the Hamiltonian
H_{0}+D(0)
reproduces the spectrum in the defect theory. However the operator used contains an infinite number of irrelevant operators so isn’t very natural.

Having said this, in
LeClair:2021wfd
;
Ahn:2022pia
theories deformed by an infinite number of irrelevant operators are considered. This irrelevant operators are the
T\bar{T}
operator and its higher weight analogues. There it was found that in order for the theories to have a UV completion an infinite number of irrelevant operators must be included in the deformation. This suggests that actually it is natural that our defect Hamiltonian must include an infinite set of irrelevant terms. Perhaps a more natural defect description can be found.

In
hrj:8932
more general modular transformations
\tau\rightarrow\widehat{\tau}=\frac{a\tau+b}{c\tau+d}
were considered. These allow an expansion as
\tau\to i\infty
, that is for small
q
, and take a similar product form to (
43
) and (
44
) (although the more general modular transformation formula is not explicitly given in
hrj:8932
, it is contained in the results in that paper).

Here we were concerned with finding a physical interpretation for the modular S transform
\tau\rightarrow\widehat{\tau}=-1/\tau
and so didn’t consider such more general modular transformations.

In this paper the defect was placed horizontally in the original GGE and vertically in the transformed GGE, as in figure
2
, but physically one would expect a general modular transform to correspond to placing the defect at a different angle. Understanding how a general modular transformation acts on the GGE is obviously an interesting question and we are currently investigating it using these ideas of defects placed at an angle.
