Rate Splitting in FDD Massive MIMO Systems Based on the Second Order Statistics of Transmission Channels Thanks: This work was supported by the German Research Foundation under Grant UT 36/18-1
Donia Ben Amor ID, Michael Joham ID

Abstract
In this work, we present new results for the application of rate splitting multiple access (RSMA) to the downlink (DL) of a massive multiple-input-multiple-output system (MIMO) operating in frequency-division-duplex (FDD) mode. We propose a statistical precoding design relying on the channels’ second-order information when one-layer RS is implemented. The advantage of the statistical precoding lies in simplifying the precoder design by basing the optimization objective on the slowly-varying channel covariance matrices instead of the fast-changing instantaneous channel estimates. To this end, we use the so-called bilinear precoder, which was shown in [ 1 ] and [ 2 ] to have limited performance in the high SNR regime due to the imperfect channel state information (CSI) available at the base station (BS). We formulate the DL throughput maximization problem based on a widely used lower bound on the achievable sum rate and propose an iterative approach to solve the underlying optimization problem. Numerical results demonstrate the benefit of implementing one-layer RS. Furthermore, the proposed iterative approach achieves excellent results in terms of spectral efficiency compared to the state-of-the-art techniques.

I
Introduction

Massive multiple-input-multiple-output (MaMIMO) systems are a prominently used technology in current wireless communication standards due to the several advantages ranging from increased spectral efficiency to improved energy efficiency and reliability to name a few
[
3
]
,
[
4
]
. These benefits are closely related to the presence of an accurate channel state information (CSI) at the base station (BS). The acquisition of the CSI is, however, often a challenging task due to the typically very small channel coherence interval
[
5
]
. An imperfect CSI can tremendously impact the system throughput especially in the high SNR regime where the system becomes interference limited due to the CSI systematic estimation errors. While the channels’ second-order statistics could be exploited to mitigate the interference, the latter cannot be completely removed and it has been observed in several works on statistical precoding/equalization, e.g.,
[
1
]
and
[
2
]
that the achievable rate saturates in the high SNR regime due to pilot contamination. The latter effect results from pilot reuse among users during channel probing of a time-division-duplex (TDD) system. A similar effect is observed in FDD systems, when the number of orthogonal pilots used for training the DL channel is less than the number of BS antennas. In either cases, the channel estimates suffer from a systematic error, even when investing high transmit power during training
[
6
]
.

In this work, we focus on a MaMIMO system operating in FDD mode, where the CSI acquisition is more challenging than in TDD systems. This mainly results from the lack of channel reciprocity between the uplink (UL) and DL channels due to UL-DL frequency gap being larger than the channel coherence bandwidth. Hence, explicit training of the DL channel and subsequent feedback are inevitable. On the one hand, the length of the training period should be at least equal to the number of BS antennas to ensure a contamination-free estimation, which on the other hand results in a huge training overhead considering the assumed massive number of BS antennas
M\gg 1
. We, therefore, shrink the length of the training phase to
T_{\text{dl}}<M
. This obviously comes at the cost of a contaminated channel observation at the user side, i.e., interference between the channel coefficients. The goal is to design a transmit strategy at the BS that takes into account the underlying systematic error in the channel estimates.

One of the promising approaches to achieve higher data rates under imperfect CSI is RSMA. This technique combines the advantages of conventional broadcasting and multicasting systems, where the transmitter (BS) splits the message intended to each user into common and private parts. Each user has then to decode the common messages first and then apply successive interference cancellation to remove the interference from the common part. Depending on how many common streams are sent, an SIC layer per common stream is required at the receiver’s side. In order to reduce the user’s hardware complexity, we assume that one-layer RS is implemented, such that only one common stream is transmitted to all users. This implies that only one SIC layer is required at the user’s side. In
[
7
]
, an RS approach is proposed for a system with imperfect CSI at the transmitter where an adaptive power allocation strategy is developed. Furthermore, the authors suggest a hierarchical RS method where the long term CSI (channel covariance matrices) and the short-term CSI (instantaneous imperfect channel estimate) are exploited to design a two-stage precoder. The work
[
8
]
considers a setup with user mobility. A lower bound on the ergodic sum rate is derived for this setup and a power allocation strategy is proposed for the 1-layer RS based system. In
[
9
]
, the authors show the ability of RS to mitigate the pilot contamination effect resulting from pilot reuse among users during the channel probing of a TDD massive MIMO system. Another application for RS in the case of imperfect CSI was investigated in
[
10
]
, where RS is used to alleviate the effect of hardware impairments on MaMIMO systems. In
[
11
]
and
[
12
]
, a MaMIMO system with imperfect CSI at the transmitter is considered. The authors propose to use the so-called iterative weighted minimum mean squared error (IWMMSE) approach to solve the sum rate maximization problem for a linearly precoded DL system featuring one-layer RS. Although this approach has been shown to achieve very good performance under imperfect CSI at the transmitter, it remains computationally expensive, considering that one has to solve the underlying optimization problem in each channel coherence interval to find the precoders for the given instantaneous channel estimate.

Contributions:

We propose an alternative approach to the IWMMSE method in order to solve the throughput maximization problem, where we formulate the optimization problem depending on the channel statistics by combining the one-layer RS strategy with the bilinear precoding approach proposed in
[
2
]
. This precoder consists of a linear transformation of the received training signal, where the transformation is a deterministic matrix that is designed only based on the channel distribution information (CDI). Due to the slower variation of the channel statistics compared to the channel itself, one has hence to solve the optimization problem for the deterministic matrices only when the covariance matrices change, which tremendously reduces the computational complexity related to the precoder design. To this end, it is assumed that the channel covariance matrices or at least estimates thereof are available at the BS. We refer the reader to the works
[
13
]
,
[
2
]
, where an extrapolation approach is used to acquire the DL channel covariance matrices from the UL channel covariance matrices by assuming the reciprocity of the so-called angular scattering function. As for the UL channel covariance matrices, those can be estimated using the UL training samples that are sent anyway for the purpose of UL channel estimation. In Section
VI
, we study through numerical simulations the impact of imperfect channel covariance matrices on the system performance.

The formulated DL throughput maximization problem is involved and thus we opt for a decomposition of the underlying problem into three parts that are solved consecutively. We propose efficient solution approaches for each subproblem and use results on fractional programming proposed in
[
14
]
,
[
15
]
to make the optimization more tractable.
Despite the suboptimality of the proposed approach, it still leads to a satisfactory performance in terms of the achievable data rates, as the simulation results show in a later part of this work.

Paper Organization:
This paper is organized as follows. In Section
II
, we present the preliminaries and the system setup considered in this work. In Section
III
, we derive expressions for the considered utility function and the different constraints and formulate the optimization problem. Section
IV
deals with the solution approach for the underlying optimization problem. In Section
V
, we present the performance of the proposed approach in terms of the spectral efficiency. A conclusion for this work is drawn in Section
VI
.

II
System Model

We consider the DL of a single-cell massive multi-user multiple-input-single-output (MISO) system operating in FDD mode, where the BS is equipped with
M\gg 1
antennas and serves
K
single-antennas users. We denote by

k

T_{\text{ch}}

T_{\text{dl}}<M

T_{\text{dl}}

Here,

orthogonal pilot sequences. This implies that
\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{\Phi}=\boldsymbol{I}_{T_{\text{dl}}}
, but
\boldsymbol{\Phi}\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\mathrm{H}}\neq\boldsymbol{I}_{M}
. The noise vector
\boldsymbol{n}_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}\left(\boldsymbol{0},\sigma^{2}_{n}\boldsymbol{I}_{T_{\text{dl}}}\right)
comprises the portion of noise resulting from the noisy DL training. Following the model in
[
13
]
, we assume that the observation vector
\boldsymbol{y}_{k}
is fed back to the BS in the UL through an analog feedback channel
[
16
]
, such that
\boldsymbol{n}_{k}
also contains the noise portion modeling the feedback errors.

At the receiver side, we assume that SIC is implemented. Each user decodes firstly the common message

, then subtracts the common message from the received signal through SIC and decodes its own private message

where

Given the common precoder

and the private precoders

, the transmit vector

\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{c}}s_{\text{c}}+\sum_{i=1}^{K}\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{p},i}s_{\text{p},i}

(3)

where

and
s_{\text{p},i}\sim\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}(0,1),i=1,\dots,K
.

Our aim is to design the common precoder
\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{c}}
and the private precoder
\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{p},k}
for some user
k
in the following form

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{c}}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{A}_{\text{c}}\boldsymbol{y}

(4)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{p},k}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{A}_{\text{p},k}\boldsymbol{y}_{k}.

(5)

In (
4
), we introduced the vector
\boldsymbol{y}
of all users’ channel observations, i.e.,

\boldsymbol{y}=[\boldsymbol{y}_{1}^{\mathrm{T}},\boldsymbol{y}_{2}^{\mathrm{T}},\dots,\boldsymbol{y}_{K}^{\mathrm{T}}]^{\mathrm{T}}

(6)

where the

are defined in (

).
\boldsymbol{A}_{\text{c}}
and
\boldsymbol{A}_{\text{p},k}
in (

) and (

), respectively, are deterministic transformation matrices which depend solely on the second-order channel statistics and on the training matrix

. These matrices are our design variables throughout this work.

The structure of the common precoder in (
4
) implies that the latter has to be designed as the superposition of ”individual precoders”, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{c}}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\boldsymbol{A}_{\text{c},k}\boldsymbol{y}_{k}
with
\boldsymbol{A}_{\text{c}}=[\boldsymbol{A}_{\text{c},1},\boldsymbol{A}_{\text{c},2},\dots,\boldsymbol{A}_{\text{c},K}]

]
.

) as bilinear precoders. We use this term because the received signal

at user

[cf. (
2
)] is not only linear in the data signals
s_{\text{c}}
and
s_{\text{p},k}
, but also in the channel observation vector
\boldsymbol{y}_{k}
[cf. (
1
)] due to (

) and (
5
).

Due to the limited power budget available at the BS denoted by
P_{\text{dl}}
, the transmit vector
\boldsymbol{x}
must satisfy the average transmit power constraint

\mathbb{E}[\|\boldsymbol{x}\|^{2}]=\|\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{c}}\|^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{K}\|\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{p},i}\|^{2}\leq P_{\text{dl}}.

(7)

Now, let
\alpha_{\text{c}}
denote the power fraction allocated to the common stream transmission. Hence, the power constraint in (

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{c}}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\alpha_{\text{c}}P_{\text{dl}}

(8)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{K}\|\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{p},i}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(1-\alpha_{\text{c}})P_{\text{dl}}.

As we aim at optimizing the precoders based on the second-order statistics of the channel, we relax the power constraints by taking the expectation with respect to the channels over the expressions in (

4
) and (

) into the relaxed power constraints, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{c}}\|^{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{A}_{\text{c}}\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{y}}\boldsymbol{A}_{\text{c}}^{\mathrm{H}}\right)\leq\alpha_{\text{c}}P_{\text{dl}}

(9)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}[\|\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{p},i}\|^{2}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\mathrm{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{A}_{\text{p},i}\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{y}_{i}}\boldsymbol{A}_{\text{p},i}^{\mathrm{H}}\right)\leq(1-\alpha_{\text{c}})P_{\text{dl}}

(10)

where we introduced
\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{y}}
in (
9
), the covariance matrix of
\boldsymbol{y}
given by

\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{y}}=\text{blkdiag}\left(\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{y}_{1}},\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{y}_{2}},\dots,\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{y}_{K}}\right)

(11)

and
\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{y}_{k}}
is defined as the covariance matrix of
\boldsymbol{y}_{k}
, i.e.,

\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{y}_{k}}=\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{C}_{k}\boldsymbol{\Phi}+\sigma_{n}^{2}\boldsymbol{I}_{T_{\text{dl}}}.

(12)

The block-diagonal structure of
\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{y}}
follows from the independence of the users’ channel observations.

III
Problem Formulation

Throughout this work, our goal is to optimize the system throughput with respect to the deterministic transformations
\boldsymbol{A}_{\text{c}}
and
\boldsymbol{A}_{\text{p},k},\forall k
, introduced in (
4
) and (
5
), respectively. Note that since these transformations only depend on the channel covariance matrices, the considered optimization problem has to be only run, whenever the channel covariance matrices change, which happens much less frequently than the channel itself changes. Hence, the computation of each precoder consists simply of a matrix-vector multiplication in each channel coherence interval [cf. (
4
), (
5
)].

We start this section by specifying the design objective. To this end, we derive closed-form expressions, which will allow us to formulate the optimization problem to be solved in Section
IV
.

Considering the RS setup, we deal with two kinds of messages: common and private messages. We, therefore, differentiate between the common rate
R_{\text{c},k}
and the private rate
R_{\text{p},k}
for each user
k
.

\displaystyle R_{\text{c},k}=I(r_{\text{c},k};s_{\text{c}}),\quad

\displaystyle R_{\text{p},k}=I(r_{\text{p},k};s_{\text{p},k})

(13)

where
I(r;s)
denotes the mutual information between the two signals
r
and
s
.

In (
13
), we introduced
r_{\text{c},k}
, the received signal for the common message at user
k
, which is given by

\displaystyle r_{\text{c},k}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{h}_{k}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{c}}s_{\text{c}}+\sum_{i=1}^{K}\boldsymbol{h}_{k}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{p},i}s_{\text{p},i}+v_{k}

(14)

\displaystyle=b_{\text{c},k}s_{\text{c}}+\sum_{i=1}^{K}b_{\text{p},k,i}s_{\text{p},i}+v_{k}.

Here, the scalars
b_{\text{c},k}=\boldsymbol{h}_{k}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{c}}
and
b_{\text{p},k,i}=\boldsymbol{h}_{k}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{p},i}
are the effective channels when incorporating the common and the private precoders, respectively.

In this work, we assume that user
k
knows perfectly the effective channel coefficient
b_{\text{c},k}
. This can be achieved by sending beamformed dedicated pilot symbols in the DL. This means, in addition to the matrix
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
, the BS sends precoded pilots to the users, which can then estimate the combination of the channel with precoder given by the scalars
b_{\text{c},k}
and
b_{\text{p},k,i}
. The knowledge of
b_{\text{c},k}
at the user’s side is necessary to be able to perform perfect SIC, i.e., in order to decode the common message and subtract it entirely from the received signal.
After performing perfect SIC, we can write the received signal for the private message
r_{\text{p},k}
as follows

\displaystyle r_{\text{p},k}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{h}_{k}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{p},k}s_{\text{p},k}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1,\\
i\neq k\end{subarray}}^{K}\boldsymbol{h}_{k}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{p},i}s_{\text{p},i}+v_{k}

(15)

\displaystyle=b_{\text{p},k,k}s_{\text{p},k}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1,\\
i\neq k\end{subarray}}^{K}b_{\text{p},k,i}s_{\text{p},i}+v_{k}.

Due to the imperfect channel state information at the transmitter side [cf. (
1
)], no closed-form expressions can be found for the achievable rates of the
R_{\text{c},k}
and
R_{\text{p},k}
.

For such a setup, it is very common to consider a lower bound on the mutual information as the design objective. One widely used lower bound in the MaMIMO literature is the so-called hardening bound, which first appeared in
[
19
]
and was then also investigated in
[
20
]
.

For this lower bound, we assume that the BS only knows the statistics of the effective channels
b_{\text{c},k}
and
b_{\text{p},k,i}
[cf. (
14
)]. The estimates of these effective channels are simply given by their means

\hat{b}_{\text{c},k}=\mathbb{E}[b_{\text{c},k}],\quad\hat{b}_{\text{p},k,i}=\mathbb{E}[b_{\text{p},k,i}].

(16)

The effective channels can thus be written as

b_{\text{c},k}=\hat{b}_{\text{c},k}+\tilde{b}_{\text{c},k},\quad b_{\text{p},k,i}=\hat{b}_{\text{p},k,i}+\tilde{b}_{\text{p},k,i}

(17)

with the zero-mean random parts
\tilde{b}_{\text{c},k}
and
\tilde{b}_{\text{p},k,i}
.

Inserting the definitions of
b_{\text{c},k}
and
b_{\text{p},k,i}
into (
14
) and (
15
), we obtain the two portions of the received signal

\displaystyle r_{\text{c},k}

\displaystyle=\hat{b}_{\text{c},k}s_{\text{c}}+\underbrace{\tilde{b}_{\text{c},k}s_{\text{c}}+\sum_{i=1}^{K}b_{\text{p},k,i}s_{\text{p},i}+v_{k}}_{\eta_{\text{c},k}}

(18)

\displaystyle r_{\text{p},k}

\displaystyle=\hat{b}_{\text{p},k,k}s_{\text{p},k}+\underbrace{\tilde{b}_{\text{p},k,k}s_{\text{p},k}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\neq k\end{subarray}}^{K}b_{\text{p},k,i}s_{\text{p},i}+v_{k}}_{\eta_{\text{p},k}}.

The lower bound is essentially based on treating the effective noise terms
\eta_{\text{c},k}
and
\eta_{\text{p},k}
constituted by the interference and noise as a Gaussian uncorrelated additive noise, which corresponds to the worst-case noise. In several previous works (e.g.,
[
21
]
), such a lower bound for MaMIMO systems has been rigorously derived and with similar steps, the lower bounds for the common and private rates are given by

\displaystyle I(r_{\text{c},k};s_{\text{c}})

\displaystyle\geq R_{\text{c},k}^{\text{LB}}=\log_{2}(1+\gamma_{\text{c},k})

(19)

\displaystyle I(r_{\text{p},k};s_{\text{p},k})

\displaystyle\geq R_{\text{p},k}^{\text{LB}}=\log_{2}(1+\gamma_{\text{p},k})

where
\gamma_{\text{c},k}
and
\gamma_{\text{p},,k}
denote, respectively, the effective common and private signal-to-interference-and-noise-ratios (SINRs) of user
k
and are given by (see
[
19
]
)

\displaystyle\gamma_{\text{c},k}

\displaystyle=\frac{|\hat{b}_{\text{c},k}|^{2}}{\text{var}(\eta_{\text{c},k})}

\displaystyle=\frac{|\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{h}_{k}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{c}}]|^{2}}{\text{var}(\boldsymbol{h}_{k}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{c}})+\sum_{i=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}[|\boldsymbol{h}_{k}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{p},i}|^{2}]+1}

(20)

\displaystyle\gamma_{\text{p},k}

\displaystyle=\frac{|\hat{b}_{\text{p},k,k}|^{2}}{\text{var}(\eta_{\text{p},k})}

\displaystyle=\frac{|\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{h}_{k}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{p},k}]|^{2}}{\text{var}(\boldsymbol{h}_{k}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{p},k})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\neq k\end{subarray}}^{K}\mathbb{E}[|\boldsymbol{h}_{k}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{p},i}|^{2}]+1}.

After inserting the expressions for the bilinear precoders [see (
4
) and (
5
)] into (
20
), we obtain the following common and private SINRs of user
k

\displaystyle\gamma_{\text{c},k}

\displaystyle=\frac{|\boldsymbol{z}_{k}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{c}}|^{2}}{\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{c}}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{Z}_{k}\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{c}}+|\boldsymbol{q}_{k}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{p},k}|^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{K}\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{p},i}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{Q}_{i,k}\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{p},i}+1}

(21)

\displaystyle\gamma_{\text{p},k}

\displaystyle=\frac{|\boldsymbol{q}_{k}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{p},k}|^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{K}\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{p},i}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{Q}_{i,k}\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{p},i}+1}.

(22)

A detailed derivation of both closed-form expressions is presented in Appendix

, where the definitions of the
\boldsymbol{z}_{k}
,
\boldsymbol{Z}_{k}

\boldsymbol{Q}_{i,k}
and
\boldsymbol{q}_{k}
are given. Note that
\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{c}}=\text{vec}(\boldsymbol{A}_{\text{c}})
and
\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{p},i}=\text{vec}(\boldsymbol{A}_{\text{p},i})
[see (
73
)].

Now that we derived the SINR expressions, we can formulate sum rate optimization problem under the transmit power constraints as follows

\displaystyle\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha_{\text{c}}\in[0,1]\\
R_{\text{c}},\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{c}},\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{p},1},\dots,\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{p},K}\end{subarray}}{\max}\>R_{\text{c}}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}R_{\text{p},k}^{\text{LB}}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad R_{\text{c}}

\displaystyle\leq R_{\text{c},k}^{\text{LB}},\forall k

(23)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}[\|\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{p},k}\|^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq(1-\alpha_{\text{c}})P_{\text{dl}}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{c}}\|^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq\alpha_{\text{c}}P_{\text{dl}}

where the first constraint ensures that the common rate is not larger than the minimum common rate of all users, which allows the common message to be be decoded by all users.

The optimization problem in (
23
) is non-convex and hence intractable. To reduce the computational cost, we propose a solution approach similar to
[
22
]
, which mainly consists of splitting (
23
) into three parts.

(I)

First, assuming a fixed power fraction
\alpha_{\text{c}}
, we formulate the private rate optimization problem

\underset{\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{p},1},\dots,\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{p},K}}{\max}\>\sum_{k=1}^{K}R_{\text{p},k}^{\text{LB}}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}[\|\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{p},k}\|^{2}]\leq(1-\alpha_{\text{c}})P_{\text{dl}}.

(24)

Here, we implicitly assume that the private precoder optimization does not affect the common rate, which is of course not the case in general, since the common SINR depends on the private precoders as well. But this assumption eases the solving procedure by decoupling the private and common rate optimization. Note that this assumption is only considered to make the optimization problem (
23
) more tractable. The system model and the SINR expressions for the performance evaluation do take into account the effect of the private precoders on the common rate.

(II)

Now, for fixed private precoders and some given power fraction
\alpha_{\text{c}}
, we recast the common precoder optimization problem as a max-min beamforming problem

\underset{\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{c}}}{\max}\>\underset{k}{\min}\>R_{\text{c},k}^{\text{LB}}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\mathbb{E}[\|\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{c}}\|^{2}]\leq\alpha_{\text{c}}P_{\text{dl}}.

(25)

Since the rate expression
R_{\text{c},k}^{\text{LB}}
is monotonically increasing in
\gamma_{\text{c},k}
, we can reformulate (
25
) as a max-min problem of the common SINRs, that is,

\underset{\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{c}}}{\max}\>\underset{k}{\min}\>\gamma_{\text{c},k}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\mathbb{E}[\|\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{c}}\|^{2}]\leq\alpha_{\text{c}}P_{\text{dl}}.

(26)

(III)

We are now left with the optimization over the power fraction
\alpha_{\text{c}}
that is allocated to the common stream transmission. Finding an optimal power fraction would require a joint optimization over the precoders such that the overall sum rate is maximized. In order to circumvent the complexity of such an approach, we opt for a line search method that returns the scalar
\alpha_{\text{c}}
that maximizes the overall rate.

IV
Solution Approach

In this section, we present a detailed description of our solution approach. Our contribution here consists of adapting and combining several methods, which were shown to have a satisfactory performance for a different setup than ours. As mentioned in the previous section, we divide the sum rate optimization problem into 3 subproblems consisting of an outer loop for the power fraction optimization and an inner loop with 2 optimization steps: the private precoder optimization and the common precoder optimization.

For the private precoder optimization, we use an efficient method which was proposed in
[
14
]
and used in
[
15
]
to solve the weighted sum rate maximization problem. The method makes use of the so-called
Lagrangian dual transform
and
quadratic transform

[
14
]
. Both transforms consist of introducing auxiliary variables to reformulate the underlying optimization problem. On the one hand, it is ensured such that the newly formulated problem is equivalent to the original one in the optimum. On the second hand, the solving procedure of the new optimization problem becomes much easier. This is because each variable can be computed in closed-form when the remaining ones are fixed and one needs to alternate between the different optimization variables until convergence is reached.

As for the common precoder optimization, we again exploit the quadratic transform
[
14
]
to reformulate the underlying maximization problem by introducing some auxiliary variables. To solve the so-obtained optimization problem, we propose two different approaches. One is to use an interior-point solver, e.g., CVX
[
23
]
. The other approach is an iterative algorithm inspired by
[
18
]
. It consists of iterativetly updating the common transformation vector, while ensuring that the minimum common SINR does not decrease after each update, which gives rise to the name
”Common SINR increasing algorithm”
.

For both, the private and the common precoder optimization problems, we assumed that the power fraction allocated to the common stream is fixed. Now, in order to find the power fraction that maximizes the overall sum rate, we consider an outer loop for the power fraction optimization and perform a line search in the interval
[0,1]
. To this end, we use a simple and efficient approach for finding the maximum of a function, namely the golden section method
[
24
]
. The inner loop consists thus of the first two optimization problems stated before: the private precoder optimization and the common precoder optimization.

IV-A

(I) Private Precoder Optimization

Before we proceed to the solution approach for the private precoder optimization problem, we rewrite (
24
) as a function of
\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{p}}=[\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{p},1}^{\mathrm{T}},\dots,\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{p},K}^{\mathrm{T}}]^{\mathrm{T}}
containing the vectorized forms of the transformation matrices
\boldsymbol{A}_{\text{p},1},\dots,\boldsymbol{A}_{\text{p},K}
of the private precoders

\underset{\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{p}}}{\max}\>\sum_{k=1}^{K}\log_{2}(1+\gamma_{\text{p},k})\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\,\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{p}}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{F}\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{p}}\leq(1-\alpha_{\text{c}})P_{\text{dl}}

(27)

where we used the reformulation of the power constraint in (
10
) and applied the identities in (
74
) resulting in the following expression

\sum_{i=1}^{K}\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{p},i}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{F}_{i}\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{p},i}\leq(1-\alpha_{\text{c}})P_{\text{dl}}.

(28)

The matrices
\boldsymbol{F}_{i}
and the matrix
\boldsymbol{F}
are given by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{F}_{i}=\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{y}_{i}}^{\mathrm{T}}\otimes\boldsymbol{I}_{M}

(29)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{F}=\text{blkdiag}\left(\boldsymbol{F}_{1},\boldsymbol{F}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{F}_{K}\right).

(30)

Due to the sum of the logarithms and the dependence of the private SINR of each user on the transformation matrices of all users, the optimization problem in (
27
) is non-convex and therefore intractable. To address this challenge, we proceed by decoupling the optimization variables in the objective function and splitting (
27
) into several subproblems, that are much easier to solve. To this end, we employ the Lagrangian dual transform
[
14
]
.

The first step of this transform is to introduce a vector of slack variables
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=[\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{K}]
and formulate the following optimization problem

\underset{\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{p}},\boldsymbol{\alpha}}{\max}\>f_{1a}(\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{p}},\boldsymbol{\alpha})\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\,\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{p}}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{F}\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{p}}\leq(1-\alpha_{\text{c}})P_{\text{dl}}

(31)

where the new objective function for the private precoder optimization is given by

f_{1a}(\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{p}},\boldsymbol{\alpha})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\log_{2}(1+\alpha_{k})-\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{k}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{(1+\alpha_{k})\gamma_{\text{p},k}}{1+\gamma_{\text{p},k}}.

(32)

In this formulation of the objective function, the private SINR
\gamma_{\text{p},k}
lies outside of the logarithm function, which eases the optimization with respect to the transformations
\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{p},k}
as we shall see in the upcoming steps.

One further observation is that for fixed
\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{p}}
, the optimal
\alpha_{k}
is given by

\alpha_{k}^{\text{opt}}=\gamma_{\text{p},k}.

(33)

The equivalence of (
27
) and (
31
) can be seen by inserting (
33
) into (
32
), which leads to the same objective of (
27
).

If we now fix
\alpha_{k}
, optimizing over
\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{p}}
reduces to maximizing the third term of (
32
) while taking into account the transmit power constraint. The optimization problem therefore reads as

\underset{\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{p}}}{\max}\>f_{2}(\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{p}})\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{p}}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{F}\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{p}}\leq(1-\alpha_{\text{c}})P_{\text{dl}}

(34)

where the objective function is given by

f_{2}(\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{p}})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{(1+\alpha_{k})|\boldsymbol{q}_{k}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{p},k}|^{2}}{1+\sum_{i=1}^{K}\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{p},i}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{Q}_{i,k}\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{p},i}+|\boldsymbol{q}_{k}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{p},k}|^{2}}

(35)

after incorporating (
22
).
The optimization problem in (
34
) is a multi-ratio fractional program, to which we apply the quadratic transform as proposed in
[
14
]
. This transform allows to decouple the numerators and denominators of the individual fractions while guaranteeing the equivalence of the transformed problem to the original one.

In order to apply the quadratic transform, we need to introduce an auxiliary variable
\beta_{k}
for each of the
K
ratios in (
35
). To this end, we formulate the following optimization problem

\underset{\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{p}},\boldsymbol{\beta}}{\max}\>f_{2a}(\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{p}},\boldsymbol{\beta})\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{p}}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{F}\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{p}}\leq(1-\alpha_{\text{c}})P_{\text{dl}}

(36)

where
\boldsymbol{\beta}=[\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\dots,\beta_{K}]
and the objective function
f_{2a}
is given by

\displaystyle f_{2a}(\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{p}},\boldsymbol{\beta})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}2\sqrt{1+\alpha_{k}}\Re\{\beta_{k}^{*}\boldsymbol{q}_{k}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{p},k}\}

\displaystyle-\sum_{k=1}^{K}|\beta_{k}|^{2}\left(|\boldsymbol{q}_{k}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{p},k}|^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{K}\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{p},i}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{Q}_{i,k}\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{p},i}+1\right).

(37)

The quadratic transform guarantees that solving (
36
) for
\boldsymbol{\beta}
and
\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{p}}
is equivalent to solving (
34
).

The optimization problem in (
36
) is convex in each of the variables
\boldsymbol{\beta}
and
\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{p}}
, when the other one is fixed. A straightforward solution approach is to alternately solve for one of the variables while maintaining the other one constant until convergence of the objective is reached. Here, convergence is attained, when the absolute change of the objective function drops below some threshold
\varepsilon
.

Starting by fixing
\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{p}}
, the optimal
k
th element in
\boldsymbol{\beta}
that maximizes the objective in (
36
) results from setting the derivative of
f_{2a}
with respect to
\beta_{k}
to zero (the power constraint is independent of the auxiliary variable
\beta_{k}
) and is given by

\beta_{k}^{\text{opt}}=\frac{\sqrt{1+\alpha_{k}}\boldsymbol{q}_{k}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{p},k}}{1+\sum_{i=1}^{K}\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{p},i}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{Q}_{i,k}\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{p},i}+|\boldsymbol{q}_{k}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{p},k}|^{2}}.

(38)

The second step is to fix
\boldsymbol{\beta}
and solve for the transformations
\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{p},k}
. In this case, (
36
) is convex in
\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{p},k}
and the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions are not only necessary but also sufficient for global optimality. Hence, based on setting the derivative of the Lagrangian function corresponding to (
36
) w.r.t.
\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{p},k}
to zero, we obtain

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{p},k}(\lambda)=

\displaystyle\sqrt{1+\alpha_{k}}\beta_{k}

\displaystyle\left(|\beta_{k}|^{2}\boldsymbol{q}_{k}\boldsymbol{q}_{k}^{\mathrm{H}}+\sum_{i=1}^{K}|\beta_{i}|^{2}\boldsymbol{Q}_{k,i}+\lambda\boldsymbol{F}_{k}\right)^{-1}\boldsymbol{q}_{k}.

(39)

with the Lagrangian multiplier
\lambda\geq 0
associated to the transmit power constraint.
The optimal Lagrangian dual variable
\lambda^{\text{opt}}
can be obtained via bisection such that the transmit power constraint is fulfilled with a certain accuracy
\epsilon>0
. Note that
\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{p},k}(\lambda)
is monotonically decreasing in
\lambda
, therefore the function defining the power constraint
\sum_{i=1}^{K}\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{p},i}^{\mathrm{H}}(\lambda)\boldsymbol{F}_{i}\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{p},i}(\lambda)
is also monotonically decreasing in
\lambda
. Hence, searching for the optimal
\lambda
that satisfies the power constraint corresponds to finding the zero of the monotonic function
\sum_{i=1}^{K}\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{p},i}^{\mathrm{H}}(\lambda)\boldsymbol{F}_{i}\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{p},i}(\lambda)-(1-\alpha_{\text{c}})P_{\text{dl}}

[
25
]
.

The solution approach to the private precoder optimization problem (
27
) is given in Algorithm

.

IV-B

(II) Common Precoder Optimization

To tackle the max-min beamforming problem stated in (
26
), we apply the quadratic transform employed in the previous section and propose two approaches to solve the reformulated optimization problem.

First, we express (
26
) depending on the common transformation vector
\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{c}}

\underset{\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{c}}}{\max}\>\underset{k}{\min}\>\gamma_{\text{c},k}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{c}}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{F}\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{c}}\leq\alpha_{\text{c}}P_{\text{dl}}

(40)

where we performed similar manipulations of the power constraint in (
9
) as in the previous section, so that the matrix
\boldsymbol{F}
is again given by

\boldsymbol{F}=\text{blkdiag}\left(\boldsymbol{F}_{1},\boldsymbol{F}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{F}_{K}\right)=\boldsymbol{C}_{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\mathrm{T}}\otimes\boldsymbol{I}_{MT_{\text{dl}}}.

(41)

A common strategy to circumvent the max-min optimization is to introduce a slack variable
z
such that the minimization with respect to the user index is recast as a constraint and where the resulting optimization problem is equivalent to the original max-min problem. Introducing the slack variable
z
yields the following optimization problem

\displaystyle\underset{\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{c}},z}{\max}\>z\quad\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{c}}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{F}\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{c}}\leq\alpha_{\text{c}}P_{\text{dl}}

(42)

\displaystyle\gamma_{\text{c},k}\geq z,\forall k.

Since
\gamma_{\text{c},k}
, the common SINR of the
k
th user, is a fraction where the numerator and denominator depend on the optimization variable
\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{c}}
, we use the quadratic transform in order to decouple the numerator and the denominator.

By defining an auxiliary variable
\eta_{k}
for each of the
K
common SINRs, where
\boldsymbol{\eta}=[\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{K}]
, we can reformulate an optimization equivalent to problem (
42
)

\displaystyle\underset{\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{c}},z,\boldsymbol{\eta}}{\max}\>z\quad\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{c}}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{F}\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{c}}\leq\alpha_{\text{c}}P_{\text{dl}}

(43)

\displaystyle 2\Re\{\eta_{k}^{*}\boldsymbol{z}_{k}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{c}}\}-|\eta_{k}|^{2}(\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{c}}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{Z}_{k}\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{c}}+\sigma_{k}^{2})\geq z,\forall k

where we defined
\sigma_{k}^{2}=|\boldsymbol{q}_{k}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{p},k}|^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{K}\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{p},i}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{Q}_{i,k}\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{p},i}+1
, which obviously does not depend on the common transformation vector
\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{c}}
.

For fixed
\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{c}}
, the optimal auxiliary variables
\eta_{k}^{\text{opt}}
can be determined by setting the derivative of the left-hand side of the SINR constraint in (
43
) to zero, which yields the following result

\eta_{k}^{\text{opt}}=\frac{\boldsymbol{z}_{k}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{c}}}{\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{c}}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{Z}_{k}\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{c}}+\sigma_{k}^{2}}.

(44)

We note that plugging this expression into (
43
) leads to the same optimization problem in (
42
).

If we now fix the auxiliary variables
\eta_{k}
, we end up with a convex optimization problem, which can be solved via CVX
[
23
]
.

Alternatively, we propose an iterative approach inspired by
[
18
]
that aims to increase the common SINR in each of its iterations.

Consider again the optimization problem in (
43
) where the auxiliary variables
\eta_{k}
are kept constant. If we denote by

the user with the least common SINR after iteration

, the update rule for the common transformation vector

at iteration

where

denotes the step-size and hence controls how much from the previous update is included in the current update and

is defined in (

The second term in (

is a complex scalar that we have to tune together with the vector

for the update in (

and

Together with the conditions implied by the power constraint, the parameters

and

\displaystyle v=\sqrt{\alpha_{\text{c}}P_{\text{dl}}(2u-u^{2})}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}\angle\left(\boldsymbol{t}_{\text{c}}^{(n),\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{c}}^{(n),\perp}\right)}

(46)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{c}}^{(n),\perp,\prime}=\boldsymbol{T}^{(n)}\boldsymbol{t}_{\text{c}}^{(n)}

(47)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{c}}^{(n),\perp}=\frac{\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{c}}^{(n),\perp,\prime}}{\|\boldsymbol{F}^{\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{Z}_{\ell(n)}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{c}}^{(n),\perp,\prime}\|_{2}}

The matrix
\boldsymbol{T}^{(n)}

Suppose that
\ell(n)
is the user with the least SINR at the beginning of iteration

and

IV-C

(III) Power Fraction Optimization

In order to find
\alpha_{\text{c}}\in[0,1]
, the power fraction allocated to the common stream, we use a one-dimensional search approach, known as the golden section method. It consists of finding the maximum/minimum of a certain function over some interval
[a,b]
by efficiently shrinking the search interval after each step
[
24
]
.

For our setup, the function of interest is the sum rate
R_{\text{sum}}^{\text{LB,RS}}=R_{\text{c}}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}R_{p,k}^{\text{LB}}
, i.e., the objective of (
23
). The search interval is given by
[a,b]=[0,1]
and the method operates as follows. First, two intermediate values
\alpha_{\text{c}1}
and
\alpha_{\text{c}2}
are found according to

\displaystyle\alpha_{\text{c}1}=a+(1-g)(b-a)

(50)

\displaystyle\alpha_{\text{c}2}=a+g(b-a)

(51)

where
g
is the inverse of the golden ratio
\frac{\sqrt{5}+1}{2}
which gives rise to the name of this method.

Now, for the given
\alpha_{\text{c}1}
and
\alpha_{\text{c}2}
, the two potential values of
\alpha_{\text{c}}
, we run the private precoder optimization approach described in Sec.
IV-A
for a fixed common precoder. We then proceed with solving the common precoder optimization problem following the approach presented in Sec.
IV-B
.

We hence obtain two values for the sum rate
R_{\text{sum,1}}^{\text{LB,RS}}=R^{\text{sum}}(\alpha_{\text{c}1})
and
R_{\text{sum,2}}^{\text{LB,RS}}=R^{\text{sum}}(\alpha_{\text{c}2})
corresponding to
\alpha_{\text{c}1}
and
\alpha_{\text{c}2}
.

If
R_{\text{sum,1}}^{\text{LB,RS}}>R_{\text{sum,2}}^{\text{LB,RS}}
, then the search interval is reduced and the new boundaries are given by
a
and
b=\alpha_{\text{c}2}
, otherwise the new interval boundaries are
a=\alpha_{\text{c}1}
and
b
.

Given the new search interval, we can recompute
\alpha_{\text{c}1}
and
\alpha_{\text{c}2}
according to (
50
) and (
51
) and then go through the previously described steps until the size of the interval given by
|b-a|
drops below a certain threshold or the maximum number of iterations is reached.

V
Iterative Weighted MSE Approach

To compare the performance of the proposed approach, we briefly present the iterative weighted minimum mean squared error (IWMMSE) precoding approach proposed in
[
12
]
,
[
11
]
. This method is based on establishing a relationship between the sum rate maximization problem and the augmented weighted MSE minimization problem by introducing weights and equalizing filters. The latter optimization problem turns out to be convex in each of its variables when the other ones are kept constant and hence an alternating solution approach is pursued.

Let
g_{\text{c},k}
and
g_{\text{p},k}
be the equalizing filters for the
k
th user’s common and private messages, respectively.
Given the expressions in (
14
) and in (
15
), the estimates for the
k
th user common message and private message read as

\displaystyle\hat{s}_{\text{c},k}

\displaystyle=g_{\text{c},k}r_{\text{c},k}

\displaystyle=g_{\text{c},k}\boldsymbol{h}_{k}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{c}}s_{\text{c}}+g_{\text{c},k}\sum_{j=1}^{K}\boldsymbol{h}_{k}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{p},j}s_{\text{p},j}+g_{\text{c},k}v_{k}

(52)

\displaystyle\hat{s}_{\text{p},k}

\displaystyle=g_{\text{p},k}r_{\text{p},k}

\displaystyle=g_{\text{p},k}\sum_{j=1}^{K}\boldsymbol{h}_{k}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{p},j}s_{\text{p},j}+g_{\text{p},k}v_{k}

(53)

respectively.

The MSEs of the common message and the private message for user
k
can respectively be written as

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\text{c},k}=\mathbb{E}[|\hat{s}_{\text{c},k}-s_{\text{c}}|^{2}]=

\displaystyle|g_{\text{c},k}|^{2}(|\boldsymbol{h}_{k}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{c}}|^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{K}|\boldsymbol{h}_{k}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{p}_{j}|^{2}+1)+1-2\Re\{g_{\text{c},k}^{*}\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{c}}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{h}_{k}\}

(54)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\text{p},k}=\mathbb{E}[|\hat{s}_{\text{p},k}-s_{\text{p},k}|^{2}]=

\displaystyle|g_{\text{p},k}|^{2}(\sum_{j=1}^{K}|\boldsymbol{h}_{k}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{p},j}|^{2}+1)+1-2\Re\{g_{\text{p},k}^{*}\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{p},k}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{h}_{k}\}.

(55)

Minimizing the MSEs with respect to the equalizing filters
g_{\text{c},k}
and
g_{\text{p},k}
, yields the MMSE optimal filters

g_{\text{c},k}^{\text{MMSE}}=\frac{\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{c}}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{h}_{k}}{T_{\text{c},k}},\qquad g_{\text{p},k}^{\text{MMSE}}=\frac{\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{p},k}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{h}_{k}}{T_{\text{p},k}}

(56)

respectively, where
T_{\text{c},k}=|\boldsymbol{h}_{k}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{c}}|^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{K}|\boldsymbol{h}_{k}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{p},j}|^{2}+1
and
T_{\text{p},k}=\sum_{j=1}^{K}|\boldsymbol{h}_{k}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{p},j}|^{2}+1
.

Applying the MMSE equalizing filters from (
56
) yields the following MMSE expressions, which are closely related to the instantaneous common and private SINRs

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\text{c},k}^{\text{MMSE}}=1-\frac{|\boldsymbol{h}_{k}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{c}}|^{2}}{T_{\text{c},k}}=\frac{1}{1+\gamma_{\text{c},k}^{\text{inst}}}

(57)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\text{p},k}^{\text{MMSE}}=1-\frac{|\boldsymbol{h}_{k}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{p},k}|^{2}}{T_{\text{p},k}}=\frac{1}{1+\gamma_{\text{p},k}^{\text{inst}}}

(58)

where

\displaystyle\gamma_{\text{c},k}^{\text{inst}}

\displaystyle=\frac{|\boldsymbol{h}_{k}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{c}}|^{2}}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}|\boldsymbol{h}_{k}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{p},j}|^{2}+1}

\displaystyle\gamma_{\text{p},k}^{\text{inst}}

\displaystyle=\frac{|\boldsymbol{h}_{k}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{p},k}|^{2}}{\sum_{j\neq k}|\boldsymbol{h}_{k}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{p},j}|^{2}+1}

Now, introducing the common and private weights
u_{\text{c},k}
and
u_{\text{p},k}
, we consider the augmented weighted MSEs

\displaystyle\xi_{\text{c},k}=u_{\text{c},k}\varepsilon_{\text{c},k}-\log_{2}(u_{\text{c},k})

(59)

\displaystyle\xi_{\text{p},k}=u_{\text{p},k}\varepsilon_{\text{p},k}-\log_{2}(u_{\text{p},k}).

When the MSEs are fixed, the optimal weights that minimize
\xi_{\text{c},k}
and
\xi_{\text{p},k}
are given by
u_{\text{c},k}^{*}=1/\varepsilon_{\text{c},k}
and
u_{\text{p},k}^{*}=1/\varepsilon_{\text{p},k}
(a scaling factor of
1/\log(2)
was ignored, since it does not affect the solution).

Note that inserting these expressions into (
59
) and applying the MMSE equalizing filters yields the following augmented weighted MMSEs

\xi_{\text{c},k}^{\text{MMSE}}=1-R_{\text{c},k}^{\text{inst}},\quad\xi_{\text{p},k}^{\text{MMSE}}=1-R_{\text{p},k}^{\text{inst}}

(60)

where
R_{\text{c},k}^{\text{inst}}
and
R_{\text{p},k}^{\text{inst}}
are the common and private rates of user
k
, respectively, given by
R_{\text{c},k}^{\text{inst}}=\log_{2}(1+\gamma_{\text{c},k}^{\text{inst}})
and
R_{\text{p},k}^{\text{inst}}=\log_{2}(1+\gamma_{\text{p},k}^{\text{inst}})
.

Since the BS has only access to an observation of the channel from which the estimate
\hat{\boldsymbol{H}}
can be computed, the instantaneous achievable rates are not known to the BS. In
[
11
]
, the authors thus define the conditional average common and private rates of user
k
, namely
\bar{R}_{\text{c},k}=\mathbb{E}[R_{\text{c},k}^{\text{inst}}|\hat{\boldsymbol{H}}]
and
\bar{R}_{\text{p},k}=\mathbb{E}[R_{\text{p},k}^{\text{inst}}|\hat{\boldsymbol{H}}]
, to which the BS has access. The average common rate is then given by
\bar{R}_{\text{c}}=\underset{k}{\min}\>\bar{R}_{\text{c},k}
.

The average sum rate maximization problem hence reads as

\displaystyle\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}\bar{R}_{\text{c}},\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{c}}\\
\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{p},1},\dots,\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{p},K}\end{subarray}}{\max}

\displaystyle\bar{R}_{\text{c}}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\bar{R}_{\text{p},k}

(61)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}\quad\bar{R}_{\text{c}}\leq\bar{R}_{\text{c},k},\forall k

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{c}}\|^{2}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\|\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{p},k}\|^{2}\leq P_{\text{dl}}.

Note that contrarily to (
23
), the optimization problem (
61
) exhibits an instantaneous power constraint and has to be solved in every channel coherence interval.

Due to the intractability of (
61
), the so-called sample average approximation (SAA) is used in order to evaluate the average rates
[
11
]
. Given the channel estimate
\hat{\boldsymbol{h}}_{k}
and the known error variance
\sigma_{e,k}^{2}
, the BS generates
N_{s}
samples of the channel such that the
n
th sample is given by

\boldsymbol{h}_{k}^{(n)}=\sqrt{1-\sigma_{e,k}^{2}}\hat{\boldsymbol{h}}_{k}+\sqrt{\sigma_{e,k}^{2}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}}_{k}

where
\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}}_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{I}_{M})
.

The average common and private rates are then approximated using these samples according to
\bar{R}_{\text{c},k}^{(N_{s})}=\frac{1}{N_{s}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{s}}R_{\text{c},k}^{\text{inst}}(\boldsymbol{H}^{(n)})
and
\bar{R}_{\text{c},k}^{(N_{s})}=\frac{1}{N_{s}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{s}}R_{\text{p},k}^{\text{inst}}(\boldsymbol{H}^{(n)})
, respectively, with
\boldsymbol{H}^{(n)}=[\boldsymbol{h}_{1}^{(n)},\dots,\boldsymbol{h}_{K}^{(n)}]
.

Similar to (
60
), a relationship between the approximated rates and the approximated augmented weighted MMSEs can be established

\bar{\xi}_{\text{c},k}^{\text{MMSE}(N_{s})}=1-\bar{R}_{\text{c},k}^{(N_{s})},\quad\bar{\xi}_{\text{p},k}^{\text{MMSE}(N_{s})}=1-\bar{R}_{\text{p},k}^{(N_{s})}

(62)

where

\displaystyle\bar{\xi}_{\text{c},k}^{\text{MMSE}(N_{s})}=\frac{1}{N_{s}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{s}}\xi_{\text{c},k}^{\text{MMSE}}(\boldsymbol{H}^{(n)})

\displaystyle\bar{\xi}_{\text{p},k}^{\text{MMSE}(N_{s})}=\frac{1}{N_{s}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{s}}\xi_{\text{p},k}^{\text{MMSE}}(\boldsymbol{H}^{(n)}).

We can hence formulate the optimization problem based on the approximated augmented weighted MSEs

\displaystyle\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}\bar{\xi}_{\text{c}},\boldsymbol{U},\boldsymbol{G}\\
\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{c}},\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{p},1},\dots,\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{p},K}\end{subarray}}{\min}

\displaystyle\bar{\xi}_{\text{c}}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\bar{\xi}_{\text{p},k}^{(N_{s})}

(63)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle\bar{\xi}_{\text{c}}\geq\bar{\xi}_{\text{c},k}^{(N_{s})},\forall k

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{c}}\|^{2}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\|\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{p},k}\|^{2}\leq P_{\text{dl}}

where the weights and equalizing filters corresponding to the
N_{s}
samples are collected respectively in
\boldsymbol{U}
and
\boldsymbol{G}
.

The optimization problem (
63
) is convex in each of its blocks (
\boldsymbol{U}
,
\boldsymbol{G}
, and
\boldsymbol{P}=[\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{c}},\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{p},1},\dots,\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{p},K}]
) if the other two are fixed. Therefore, an alternating solution approach is used. For fixed precoders,
\boldsymbol{U}
and
\boldsymbol{G}
are computed according to the MMSE solution as explained in the beginning of this section. The weights and equalizing filters are calculated for each of the
N_{s}
samples and subsequently averaged out. Then for fixed
\boldsymbol{U}
and
\boldsymbol{G}
, the optimization problem with respect to
\boldsymbol{P}
reduces to a quadratically constrained convex problem, which can be solved using standard interior-point solvers (e.g. CVX). We refer the interested reader to
[
11
]
for a detailed description of this approach.

We consider the 3GPP spatial channel model and specifically the urban microcell environment
[
26
]
. The BS is equipped with
M=64
antennas and serves

users distributed inside the cell with radius
250~\text{m}
. The users’ positions are generated uniformly at random inside the cell, around the BS located at the cell center. The channels are non-line-of-sight (NLOS) and sampled from the Gaussian distribution with zero mean and covariance matrix generated according to the multi-path channel model. Assuming
N_{\text{path}}=6
main clusters and
N_{\text{rays}}=20
rays in each cluster, the covariance matrix of user
k
at carrier frequency
f
is given by

\boldsymbol{C}_{k}=p_{k}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\text{paths}}}\frac{\beta_{n}}{N_{\text{rays}}}\sum_{m=1}^{N_{\text{rays}}}\boldsymbol{a}(\theta_{k,n,m},f)\boldsymbol{a}^{\mathrm{H}}(\theta_{k,n,m},f)

where
p_{k}
depends on the path-loss and, therefore, on the distance between the BS and the mobile station (MS) and
\beta_{n}
denotes the power of the
n
th cluster.

\boldsymbol{a}(\theta_{k,n,m},f)
is the array steering vector. We assume that the BS antennas are in a uniform linear array (ULA) configuration and that the antenna spacing is given by half the wavelength of the UL channel. The ratio of the DL and UL carrier frequencies is denoted by
\nu=\frac{f_{\text{dl}}}{f_{\text{ul}}}=1.1
.
The
i
th component of the array steering vector evaluated at the DL carrier frequency is given by

\displaystyle[\boldsymbol{a}^{\text{dl}}(\theta_{k,n,m})]_{i}

\displaystyle=[\boldsymbol{a}(\theta_{k,n,m},f_{\text{dl}})]_{i}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\mathrm{j}\pi\nu(i-1)\sin(\theta_{k,n,m})\right)

where the angle
\theta_{k,n,m}
is is the angle-of-arrival (AOA) of the
m
th ray inside the
m
th cluster for the
k
th user.

We assume that
T_{\text{dl}}=8
out of
T_{\text{coh}}=200
channel accesses in each channel coherence interval are used for the DL training and that the noise variance of the training signal is given by
\sigma_{n}^{2}=\frac{1}{P_{\text{tr,dl}}}
. Here,
P_{\text{tr,dl}}
denotes the DL training power given by
P_{\text{dl}}T_{\text{dl}}
.

In the first part of this section, we assume that the BS disposes perfect knowledge of the channel covariance matrices. In the latter part, we will assess the performance obtained when only an estimate of the second-order information is available at the BS.

For the private precoder optimization using the proposed iterative solution approach, a maximum of
20

\boldsymbol{\Phi}
such that the power constraints are fulfilled

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{p},k}=\text{vec}(\boldsymbol{A}_{\text{p},k})=\text{vec}(\boldsymbol{\Phi})

(64)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{p}}^{\prime}=[\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{p},1}^{\mathrm{T}},\dots,\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{p},K}^{\mathrm{T}}]^{\mathrm{T}}

\displaystyle R_{\text{sum}}^{\text{RS}}=\tau\left(\mathrm{E}[\log_{2}\left(1+\gamma_{\text{c}}^{\text{inst}}\right)]+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathrm{E}[\log_{2}\left(1+\gamma^{\text{inst}}_{\text{p},k}\right)]\right)

(65)

VI-A

Comparison to the IWMMSE Approach

For the IWMMSE approach, we use the MMSE channel estimate
\hat{\boldsymbol{h}}_{k}^{\text{MMSE}}
that we obtain from the channel observation
\boldsymbol{y}_{k}
according to

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{h}}_{k}^{\text{MMSE}}=\boldsymbol{C}_{k}\boldsymbol{\Phi}(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{C}_{k}\boldsymbol{\Phi}+\sigma_{n}^{2}\boldsymbol{I}_{T_{\text{dl}}})^{-1}\boldsymbol{y}_{k}.

In order to generate the
N_{s}=200

\sigma_{e,k}^{2}=\text{tr}(\boldsymbol{C}_{k}-\boldsymbol{C}_{k}\boldsymbol{\Phi}(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{C}_{k}\boldsymbol{\Phi}+\sigma_{n}^{2}\boldsymbol{I}_{T_{\text{dl}}})^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{C}_{k})/M
. A maximum of
30

As for the common precoder, we use the dominant left singular vector of the MMSE estimate of the channel matrix. The power fraction obtained using the bilinear approach is taken as initial value for the IWMMSE, where uniform power allocation is performed among the private streams.

Obviously, the bilinear precoding approach outperforms the IWMMSE method in both cases, i.e., with or without RS. Both methods seem to benefit from RS in the medium to high power regime. While for the bilinear approach the gains are visible starting from

0.33

0.27

We need to mention here, that the optimization problem for the bilinear precoding approach considers a relaxed power constraint, whereas the power constraint for the IWMMSE approach is an instantaneous one. This offers more degrees of freedom for the bilinear precoder design. However, even after scaling the bilinear precoders to satisfy the instantaneous total power constraint, the change in terms of the achievable sum rate was negligible.

Regarding the runtimes of both algorithms, the overall optimization of the transformation matrices of the bilinear precoders converges after
1431
s
for a given realisation of the channel covariance matrices
at a DL transmit power of
P_{\text{dl}}=40
dB. On the other hand, the runtime of the IWMMSE approach
for a given channel realisation
is about
184
s at
P_{\text{dl}}=40
dB. Obviously, the runtime needed for the bilinear approach is much larger than the one required for the IWMMSE method. However,
we need to emphasize, that the optimization problem proposed for finding the deterministic transformations defining the bilinear precoder has only to be solved once the covariance matrices change and therefore only a matrix-vector multiplication is performed to find the precoding vector for each channel realization. Contrary, the IWMMSE method has to be run in each channel coherence interval. It is also noteworthy that the IWMMSE approach is very sensitive to the initialization of the precoders especially in the high power regime.

VI-B

Comparison of the Performance for Perfectly Known vs. Estimated Covariance Matrices

The performance of the system with perfect knowledge of the covariance matrices shall now be compared to the one when the BS disposes not the true DL channel covariance matrices but an estimate thereof. For the estimation procedure, we use the extrapolation approach proposed in our previous work
[
2
]
. In a few words, this approach consists of exploiting a reciprocity relationship between the UL and DL covariance matrices in order to estimate the DL covariance matrices using the UL channel observations.
From Fig.
10
, one can see that when estimated covariance matrices are used, a performance degradation of about
1
bpcu in the low power regime is experienced and the gap becomes about
2
bpcu in the medium to high power range.

Nevertheless, we observe that the slope of the curves remains the same for high power values, which confirms the robustness of the applied covariance estimation approach.

VII
Conclusion

In this work, we presented a framework for the application of rate splitting to an FDD massive multi-user MIMO system. Despite the suboptimality of the proposed approach and the incomplete channel knowledge at the BS, numerical results have shown that the combination of the bilinear precoding based on the second-order channel information and rate splitting leads to a drastic improvement in terms of the achievable sum rate when compared to the system without rate splitting. The transmission of the common message is mainly beneficial when it comes to medium to high transmit powers. While the conventional DL system (usual broadcast channel, without rate splitting) saturates in the high transmit power regime due to the inter-user interference and the imperfect channel knowledge, introducing the common stream yields a rate of the system that increases when more transmit power is used. Furthermore, our method significantly outperforms the commonly used iterative weighted MMSE approach. While the optimization problem related to the latter has to be solved in each and every channel coherence interval, the bilinear approach depending on the channel covariance matrices requires a simple matrix-vector multiplication in every channel coherence interval. Finding the deterministic transformation matrices that constitute the bilinear precoder has only to be performed in the beginning of the covariance coherence interval which is given by several channel coherence intervals.

A future research direction would be to extend the proposed approach to a multi-cell setup where the challenges related to the system design become more significant due to the inter-cell interference. Furthermore, since the benefits of RS decrease as the number of served users increases, one could optimize the set of users that have to decode the common stream, which could increase the minimum common rate. This user selection can be based on the channel second-order statistics.
