Neural Simulation via Bootstrapping
Xin Li

Abstract
Learning is easy; representation is hard. Representation and simulation are two sides of the same coin. How are the external environment, internal states, and the mind of a person represented by the brain based on a simple Hebbian learning principle? Conceptually similar to the cloning of DNA for genetics, we suggest a bootstrapping-based constructive framework for representation learning via neural simulation. The key insight is to understand how the evolution of the brain drives the repurposing of old structures for new applications via alternating between temporal exploitation and spatial exploration. For temporal exploitation, a simple dopamine-based reinforcement learning algorithm offers a principled solution to the credit assignment problem and its variations in space and time. For spatial exploration, new structures are invented to generate more powerful representations of the agent’s external environment and internal state by neural simulation. Unlike Monte Carlo simulation, the neural simulation uses delta measures as the seed and implements four levels of predictive processing by the neocortex via bootstrapping, including integral transform (fast thinking), conditional sampling (slow thinking), latent quantization (abstract thinking), and flow maximization (social thinking). From reptiles and mammals to primates and humans, our simulation-based framework offers a unified perspective for understanding the evolution of natural intelligence.

More importantly, as Von Neumann said, “Anyone who attempts to generate random numbers by deterministic means is, of course, living in a state of sin.”. Brain-based simulation (which we call neural simulation in this paper) does not appear to suffer from the curse of dimensionality and demonstrates amazing generalization performance in real-world scenarios. Shedding novel insight into the mechanism of neural simulation is likely to help understand the organizational principles underlying human brains.

In this paper, we draw inspiration from the rhythms of brain cycles

, a thousand brain theory

[

; 2) the column organization discovered by V. Mountcastle

suggests the existence of a universal predictive processing algorithm for implementing neural simulation at different levels: from transform (0th-order) and sampling (1st-order) to quantization (2nd-order) and projection (3rd-order); 3) Bootstrapping is a strategy that has been recursively exploited by the evolution to achieve higher intelligence by inventing new structures under the dogma of “structures defining functions”.

Inspired by the phylogenetic continuity hypothesis of navigation and memory

(zeroth-order or without simulation). Based on the principle of least resistance

, the hippocampus solves the shortest path problem by a novel representation of distance in the environment (i.e., synaptic resistance of reciprocal synaptic strength). After two great deaths, the hippocampus is repurposed by mammalian brains for indexing/retrieval (pp. 285
[

). Instead, cortical columns in the neocortex (new brain) inherit and expand the functionality of the old brain for predictive processing purposes

.

Built upon Ballard’s deictic codes
[

]
and Friston’s active inference

, we show how the bootstrapping strategy can be recursively applied to implement neural simulation for more flexible behavior and higher intelligence, leading to a sequence of brain models with increasing complexity. Following the evolutionary path of the brain, we organize these models into the following four (from zeroth-order to third-order neural simulation):

•

Integral transform for fast thinking (modeling world) - The sensory cortex repurposes the where algorithm (originally for spatial navigation) for what (object recognition) based on the change of coordinates (from egocentric to allocentric). The navigation-memory continuity offers a path integral-based interpretation of episodic and semantic memory. We construct a hierarchical extension of memory-based perception to mirror the nested organization of the physical world without simulation involved (a deterministic process).

•

Conditional sampling for slow thinking (modeling self) - The frontal cortex repurposes the external simulation of the world for modeling the self to predict the intention inwardly. This represents the paradigm shift from passive to active/causal inference. Intention or goal is constructed as a computational trick to facilitate the calculation of path integral, which enlarges the prediction cycle by including the self in the world model. Causality is a unidirectional navigational path in the latent space. First-order simulation implements a neural diffusion process-based sampling strategy in the latent space (a stochastic process).

•

Latent quantization for abstract thinking (modeling mind) - Based on the social brain hypothesis, we connect the world/external model (content) with the self/internal model (context) to form a larger cycle based on the neural simulation of the granular prefrontal cortex (gPFC). This bootstrapping trick plays the role in scaling up the simulation in space (e.g., involving social interaction) and time (e.g., predicting future needs). The self-referential bootstrapping in the theory of mind (ToM) model is implemented by a quantization strategy in the latent space of path integral capable of both discrimination and generalization. Second-order simulation facilitates the understanding of others’ minds by bootstrapping and modeling one’s own mind.

•

Flow maximization for social thinking (modeling communication) - As an intellectually appealing application, we hypothesize that the social model supports another bootstrapping strategy for facilitating social communication from non-verbal to verbal. From this perspective, language is the product of constructing a bigger cycle (Broca’s area and Wernicke’s area) that breaks the physical barrier between two brains. Shannon’s information theory offers a maximum information-flow interpretation for the hierarchy of languages. We show how the agnostic model of languages can be implemented by the Dirichlet Process in the latent space.

II
Synchronization of Neural Groups via Hebbian Learning

Hebbian learning
[
15
]
is a mechanism of synaptic plasticity where the connection strength between two neurons increases if they are activated simultaneously. A neural group is a collection of consistently co-activated neurons to represent a specific pattern or function. Hebbian learning facilitates the creation of such groups by 1) Strengthening Intra-Group Connections: Neurons that repeatedly fire together form stronger synaptic connections;
Over time, this forms a tightly coupled group of neurons that reliably encode specific information. 2) Pruning Unrelated Connections:
Neurons that do not fire together weaken their connections (anti-Hebbian learning or synaptic pruning), isolating the group from unrelated activity. 3)
Stabilizing Activity Patterns:
The synchronized firing within the group reinforces the group’s identity and functionality.

Conceptually similar to the concept of cloning in genetics, neural groups can create similar or redundant groups across the brain. This can happen via Hebbian learning in several ways: 1) Input Duplication -
If similar stimuli are processed in different parts of the brain, Hebbian learning can cause the formation of similar neural groups in these regions. 2) Redundancy for Robustness -
The brain often creates multiple neural groups for the same function to ensure robustness;
Hebbian learning facilitates this by replicating activity patterns in different networks exposed to the same stimuli. 3) Distributed Representations -
Hebbian learning propagates activity patterns across connected networks, leading to the formation of distributed yet coherent representations. 4) Functional Specialization -
Neural groups in one region may teach another region via synchronized activation and Hebbian processes.

More specifically, we focus on the temporal synchrony of neural groups
[
16
]
and its implications for neural computation (e.g., the binding problem). There are two ways of extending the mechanism of temporal synchrony to support structural representations of the external environment and internal states in space and time: parallel and sequential. 1) Parallel refers to the growth of synchronous cell assemblies within a specific temporal window (e.g., the smallest resolution to distinguish temporally segmented events). Such parallel extension of cell assemblies is suitable for content representation of the external environment that aims to deliver a coherent description (“oneness”) of the salient object in the foreground from the background. 2) Sequential extension refers to the concatenation of cell assemblies in a timely sequential order (before the phase resetting). The sequence of cell assemblies
[
17
]
naturally defines a trajectory/path of the neural system in the state space. This extension is suitable for a) context representation of internal states that refer to a set of conditions in which an input is uniquely coupled with an output; and b) cause-effect representation because the unidirectional navigational path naturally defines the causation (the broken symmetry in time).

Supported by temporal synchrony, the mechanism of functional specialization is closely related to the strategy of bootstrapping because it allows complex animals to exploit constructive mechanisms invented by nature in simple organisms
[
4
]
. This principle reflects an evolutionary trend where foundational biological mechanisms, once established in simple organisms, are repurposed and extended into more complex ones. For example, the repurposing of dopamine for reinforcement learning
[
18
]
in primitive brains has been preserved in more complex animals. But “more is different”
[
19
]
; what is less obvious is how the bootstrapping strategy is recursively applied by nature to drive the evolution of animal brains toward intelligent and adaptive behavior. A principled understanding of the constructive mechanisms at the system level sets up the stage for this research.

III
Bootstrapping for Exploitation-Exploration Dilemma

The evolution of the brain can be formulated as a continual learning or decision-making problem - i.e., how can an adaptive system learn across non-stationary tasks in a dynamically changing environment, preserving knowledge while efficiently integrating new information? Such a problem is closely related to the
fundamental exploration-exploitation dilemma in reinforcement learning
[
20
]
and decision-making
[
21
]
. The explore-exploit tradeoff can be broken into two subproblems: 1) Exploitation - the credit assignment problem
[
22
]
refers to the challenge of identifying which components or actions are responsible for the observed outcomes (e.g., intelligent behavior) of a system/agent, especially in dynamic and complex environments; 2) Exploration - the inductive bias problem
[
23
]
refers to the challenge of how to choose an agent’s hypothesis space to ensure reliable generalization? Note that inductive bias can impact exploration strategies which in turn affect the efficiency of credit assignment.

Historically, logic and statistics have been competing approaches to understanding intelligence
[
24
]
. In logician schools, Cantor’s diagonalization argument has been reinvented several times (e.g., Godel’s incompleteness theorem and Turing’s halting problem) to show the fundamental limit of computation and reasoning with a fixed formal system. Decision-making in brain evolution is no different, implying the existence of undecidable situations no matter how advanced the evolved learning system is. Alternatively, the Bayesian brain hypothesis
[
25
]
and the principle of free energy
[
26
]
have emerged as top-down proxy models. In Bayesian terms, inductive bias and data observation jointly manifest as the choices of the prior distribution and the likelihood function. These components recursively encode the model’s assumptions about the nature of the data, the relationships between variables, and the likely hypotheses, which influence posterior beliefs after observing evidence.

In this paper, we formalize Minsky’s position on “learning is easy; representation is hard.”
[
27
]
by constructing a bootstrapping-based solution to the exploration-exploitation dilemma under a reinforcement learning framework. The motivation behind our approach is two-fold. On the one hand, we generalize the idea of replacing the actual reward with the expected reward in temporal-difference (TD) learning
[
28
]
by conditioning. Conditioning based on contextual information
C
helps reduce the variance in predicting the future reward during exploitation. On the other hand, we solve the inductive bias problem by invoking the law of total expectation
E[X]=E[E(X|C)]
, which offers a principled solution to generalization. Putting conditioning and generalization into a closed loop serves as the building block for our bootstrapping strategy.

The bootstrapping strategy is closely related to Godel’s layered truth systems. Based on the diagonalization argument, one can always construct a new formal system or update the prior by introducing additional mathematical structures (e.g., irrational numbers and distance metrics). We highlight the introduction of the delta function as a new representation to facilitate reasoning in space (e.g., spatial position). The mixing of integrable and delta functions supports the use of Stieltjes integrals for bottleneck problems in dynamic programming
[
29
]
. Following the evolution of mammalian brains, we show how the family of delta functions (a.k.a. generalized functions) leads to a universal representation of space for both spatial navigation and object recognition, supporting the phylogenetic continuity of navigation and memory
[
9
]
.

III-A

Principle of Conditioning: Learning is Easy

The key insight underlying dopamine’s repurposing for TD-learning is to replace the actual reward
X
with an expectation of future reward
E[X]
. The benefit of such a replacement is to handle the unpredictable uncertainty in a dynamically varying environment. In simulation
[
30
]
, conditioning has been widely recognized as an effective strategy for variance reduction. Replacing
E[X]
with
E[X|C]
can be interpreted as a natural extension of dopamine’s repurposing by considering spatiotemporal context
C
. The key benefit of conditioning is that the additional information contained in context variable
C
can help resolve the uncertainty about the outcome/reward
X
. For example, encoding head-direction response into place cells makes them unidirectional, which contains less uncertainty than omnidirectional.

Cloned neural groups provide distributed neural representations of the same sensory/motor variables, each associated with a different spatiotemporal context. These redundant groups enable robust predictions because 1) predictions can be averaged across groups to reduce noise and therefore variance in the prediction outcome; 2) different neural groups can specialize in encoding specific contextual variations, improving prediction accuracy. Note that conditioning can be readily extended recursively to support hierarchical structure and inference. Neural group cloning also facilitates hierarchical organization, where lower-level groups encode fine-grained details in unstructured observation data, and higher-level groups encode abstract predictions into structured representations.
Conditional predictions flow top-down, while sensory evidence flows bottom-up, enabling context-dependent active inference across scales.

III-B

Principle of Generalization: Representation is Hard

Inductive bias is the set of assumptions that a learning algorithm uses to prioritize one solution over others when the training data alone is insufficient to determine the solution uniquely. From the perspective of bias-variance tradeoff
[
31
]
, a well-chosen inductive bias aligns the model’s assumptions with the true data distribution, striking a balance between bias and variance to achieve good generalization. The innovative design of inductive bias by nature through evolution seems to be in favor of built-in neural architectures that enhance survival and adaptations to the organism’s specific environment. Meta-learning and Bayesian updating have emerged as favored mechanisms due to the evolutionary pressures in dynamic environments.

In parallel to conditioning, the law of total expectation
E[X]=E[E(X|C)]
offers a principled solution to generalization, which can be conveniently implemented by the aliasing or integration operators of neural systems
[
32
]
. At the core of the generalization problem is the universal representation to support goal-directed behavior in neural systems. An important new insight advocated by this work is to efficiently focus the learning process on informative parts of the data distribution (e.g., the head part) via implicit sampling, which is often in a way that aligns with the target task. However, implicit sampling must balance between exploiting known regions and exploring underrepresented areas to ensure continual yet comprehensive learning. To tackle such exploitation-exploration dilemma, we argue that a representation suitable for ordered systems (e.g., delta measures) is more appropriate than noise measures (e.g., Gaussian) characterizing disordered systems, which is consistent with Friston’s principle of free energy
[
26
]
.

Putting conditioning and generalization together, we observe that brain evolution follows a pattern of alternating between temporal exploitation and spatial exploration. For temporal exploitation, a simple dopamine-based reinforcement learning algorithm offers a principled solution to the credit assignment problem. For spatial exploration, new structures are invented to generate a more powerful representation of the agent’s external environment and internal state. Next, we will organize the evolution from reptile to mammalian and primate brains into four thinking problems and show how bootstrapping offers a unified solution. Note that “if a constructive mechanism is invented by nature in simple organisms,
more complex animals tend to exploit it” (pp. 259,
[
4
]
). The unifying theme is that under the dogma of “structure defining function”
[
4
]
, more adaptive and flexible behavior can be learned by iteratively bootstrapping new brain structures (e.g., hippocampus
\rightarrow
sensory and motor cortex
\rightarrow
prefrontal cortex) to support more functionally specialized representations.

IV
Fast Thinking Without Simulation

IV-A

Credit Assignment: Bootstrapping via Temporal Difference

In Minsky’s original thinking
[
22
]
, credit assignment was formulated as a problem of associating an action state with an observed reward. Under the reinforcement learning framework, the actor-critic model
[
28
]
serves as a simulation-based solution to the multi-stage decision process (MDP) by leveraging interactions with the environment to estimate and improve policies and value functions iteratively. The critic evaluates the expected returns through sampled trajectories and guides the actor’s policy updates, allowing learning without explicit knowledge of the environment’s dynamics. This approach efficiently focuses on relevant parts of the state-action space, facilitating scalability, adaptability, and generalization.
As demonstrated in
[
18
]
, dopaminergic neurons are responsible for solving the temporal credit assignment problem by reinforcement learning such as temporal-difference (TD) learning
[
33
]
.

TD-learning
[
28
]
can be interpreted as the combination of Monte Carlo (MC) and dynamic programming (DP) methods. The key idea underlying MC is to sample sequences of states, actions, and rewards from interaction with the environment and average the complete returns at the end of each episode. By contrast, bootstrapping, the key innovation behind DP, is based on Bellman’s principle of optimality
[
29
]
that states “within an optimal sequence of decisions, any subsequence of decisions starting from any point in time must also be optimal with respect to the state reached at that point”. Conceptually similar to the actor-critic model in TD-learning, the basal ganglia and hypothalamus (responsible for dopamine release) play a similar role of student-teacher in their interaction. The outcome of this magical bootstrapping strategy is to allow an agent to learn arbitrary sequences of actions to achieve a certain objective defined in the latent space of the internal states (initialized by the hypothalamus).

An agent’s ability to learn arbitrary sequences of actions (represented by a path
L
in the state space) can be exploited to grasp the concept of space. The objective of space learning can be formulated as a problem of learning a latent graph from its actions and observations while navigating in the physical world. Suppose an agent performs a sequence of actions
\{a_{1},...,a_{n}\}
; the corresponding states and rewards are denoted by
L:\{s_{1}\rightarrow s_{2},...,s_{n-1}\rightarrow s_{n}\}
and
\{x_{1},...,x_{n}\}
, respectively. The key idea behind TD-learning is to replace the actual (deterministic) reward
x
by its expectation
E[x]=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}
. Instead of maximizing the reward at the end of the episode (like in MC-based supervised learning), TD-learning solves the MDP problem by simulating the dynamically varying environment on the fly. The equivalence between TD-learning and supervised learning has been established in
[
33
]
.

IV-B

Space is a Latent Sequence

How is the concept of space represented by the dynamics of neural groups? For reptiles, the task of spatial navigation is closely related to their homing behavior
[
34
]
. It is well known that animals count on path integration
[
7
]
to navigate in the environment by relying on self-motion cues only.
The task of spatial navigation is the inductive bias for an agent’s learning about the spatial representation from cognitive graphs in topology to cognitive maps in geometry.
Recent works
[
35
,
36
]
have shown it is possible to recover the topology of the environment represented by a cognitive graph from the sequences of actions and observations via the expectation-maximization (EM) algorithm. The aliasing problem with sensory observation
[
32
]
can be tackled by cloning states of cognitive graphs. As shown in
[
37
]
, the max-product belief propagation algorithm, which is based on Bellman’s duality of dynamic programming
[
29
]
, can be shown to be optimal for inference on arbitrary graphs with the cloning trick.

The build-up of cognitive maps
[
38
]
from cognitive graphs requires the introduction of distance metric. Hebbian plasticity converts the Euclidean distance into the synaptic strengths between sequentially activated and unidirectionally connected neuronal assemblies
[

]
. As shown in
[

, the problem of finding the shortest path on cognitive graphs can be solved by the principle of least resistance if the synaptic resistance (reciprocal of synaptic strength) encodes the Euclidean distance between the nodes representing places. Computationally, the goal of finding the shortest paths for all pairs can be achieved by Floyd-Warshall algorithm that incrementally improves an estimate of the shortest path. With a cognitive map, it is then possible to associate a landmark (what) with a position (where) in the first world model. Conceptually similar to the simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM)
[
39
]
, a Kalman filtering algorithm (a close relative of the expectation-maximization algorithm
[
40
]
) is sufficient for the reconstruction of the world model as a cognitive map.

The positions of landmarks in the SLAM model are represented by the delta function (refer to Fig.
2
). Such a pointer-based representation (a.k.a., deictic codes
[
13
]
) is supported by the encoding of spatial coordinates into grid cells and place cells
[
41
,
42
]
. Topologically, the torus captures the repeating periodic structure of the hexagonal grid, abstracting away specific measurements and focusing on the connectivity of the space. Note that the hexagonal lattice not only represents connectivity but also encodes precise distances and directions. According to Heisenberg’s uncertainty principle, there is a fundamental tradeoff between resolution and accuracy. A fine-resolution hexagonal lattice improves the accuracy at the price of increased cost (e.g., the energy required for representing the grid codes
[
41
]
). The product of cyclic groups on positive integers is well known for the modular arithmetic result (Chinese Remainder Theorem). Linear combination of grid codes generates a delta-like response of place codes
[
43
]
.

Kalman filtering-based world model offers a strong inductive bias because it is essentially a relational representation connecting where (positions) with what (landmarks). Its fundamental limitation arises from the assumption about a stationary external environment or a single map. Perfecting the task of exploitation cannot solve the continual learning problem; to accommodate a dynamically changing environment, an agent needs to learn many maps by exploring the unknown territory and efficiently integrating new information. To reach a new layer via bootstrapping, nature discovered the duality between navigation and memory (i.e., their phylogenetic continuity
[
9
]
). By repurposing the hippocampus from a cognitive graph to an indexing map, the brain incorporates new inductive bias about a constantly changing environment into the dialogue between the hippocampus and the neocortex
[
44
]
.

IV-C

Sensorimotor Knowledge Automation

It has been hypothesized in
[
5
]
that nature compresses the old brain into cortical columns, which have become the building block of the new brain. By abstracting each cortical column into a SLAM model, we observe the emergence of a generative model capable of predictive coding
[
12
]
and processing
[
11
]
. Such generative models directly facilitate the simulation of the external environment through sensorimotor interaction. We call it the zeroth-order simulation because it is based on observing only (the first ladder of causal reasoning according to Pearl
[

) and does not involve any higher-level cognition. Without the need for a new learning mechanism, the neocortex can solve the problem of recognition by simulation or seeking a new representation (i.e., change of coordinates). From spatial navigation to landmark/object recognition, we will see that it only requires the change of coordinate from egocentric to allocentric.

From spatial navigation to object recognition, the goal of computation has shifted from positional to landmark recognition. Accordingly, the ideal representation of the content (object of interest) varies from egocentric to allocentric coordinates
[

\delta(\rho-xcos\theta-ysin\theta)

To summarize, fast or habitual thinking is a deterministic process that transforms sensorimotor knowledge into a delta-measure-based representation in the latent space. This process does not require any neural simulation because it solves the temporal credit assignment problem by bootstrapping the reward function and spatial navigation by transforming it into a latent space learning problem. Sensorimotor knowledge is transformed into delta functions in the latent space by integral transforms (path integration and GHT). The repurposing of the hippocampus from a mapping device to an indexing machine efficiently exploits the hypothesis space. Moreover, it helps build up the foundation for neocortical simulation/sampling, which is required for an optimal decision in a dynamically varying environment (i.e., to address the challenge of spatial uncertainty). Since an SLAM agent can computationally abstract each cortical column, the neocortex as a generative model plays the critical role of neural simulation.

V
Slow Thinking: First-order Simulation

To understand the limitation of automation without simulation, we can consider a similar construction to the Chinese room experiment. Imagine an agent capable of making all predictions about the flow of sensory observations from the external environment, just like a non-playing character (NPC) in a simulation game. What is the fundamental difference between an NPC and a real player? It is the presence of intention or goal, that also distinguishes the old brain from the new brain. Using Godel’s truth system as an analogy, the old brain (fast thinking with automation) or all model-free RL systems are incomplete because they lack flexibility (due to deterministic logic) and can never answer the question of why
[
45
]
. Coping with uncertainty requires the construction/simulation of not only the external world but also the agent’s own intention. In mammalian brains, the prefrontal cortex tries to explain and predict an animal’s behavior in the same way that the sensory neocortex tries to explain and predict sensory observations. From this perspective, causality is a by-product of neural simulation; causal inference
[
45
]
can be unified with active inference
[

because intervening and counterfactual reasoning simply represent two parallel paths of neural simulation.

More specifically, the principle of conditioning allows us to extend the strategy of variance reduction from TD (zeroth-order simulation:
X\rightarrow E[X]
) to path integration in space (first-order simulation:
E[X]\rightarrow E[X|\mathbf{\Phi}]
) where
\mathbf{\Phi}
denotes the spatial context represented by an ensemble of paths

defined by the action sequences. The objective of first-order simulation is to determine the optimal path from the episodic memory. It follows that a computational implementation of first-order simulation requires two stages: 1) encoding of episodic memory which will form the basis for path enumeration; 2) searching for the optimal path via goal simulation. The first step is a natural extension of sensorimotor knowledge automation by hippocampal-neocortical dialogue

V-A

Mirroring the Physical: Navigation and Memory

Based on the phylogenetic continuity hypothesis of navigation and memory
[

In the old brain, the hippocampus and entorhinal cortex solve the spatial navigation problem by the path of least resistance; in the new brain, a similar computational mechanism is repurposed for encoding episodically linked items. From a representation learning perspective, an important connection is that activating any position in the physical space as required by spatial navigation is analogous to recalling an episode in the mental space. According to the principle of conditioning, it is the temporal context that facilitates the separation and completion of navigating paths in both spaces.

For spatial navigation, temporal context is encoded into the orientation (head-direction cell) as required by the double integration in dead reckoning. Accordingly, unidirectional place cells are context-dependent; they are generalized into context-independent omnidirectional place cells
\delta(x,y)=\int\delta(x,y,\theta)d\theta
through the hippocampal-neocortical dialogue (i.e., sleep-wake cycle). For memory formation, common items of multiple overlapping episodes are analogous to the junctions of navigating paths. It follows that the consolidation of semantic memory from episodic memory can be computationally abstracted by
\delta(L)=\int\delta(\mathbf{\Theta},L)d\mathbf{\Theta}
, where
\mathbf{\Theta},L
denote the context variables and content (non-overlapping path), respectively.

The interaction between the hippocampus and the neocortex

V-B

Modeling Oneself: Goal Representation via Abstraction

Just like the credit assignment problem formulation in the temporal domain, decision-making in a space of unbounded uncertainty has been formulated into the search problem in the latent space.
In Minsky’s search problem
[
22
]
, the number of different paths in the latent/solution space grows exponentially as the parameters (e.g., number of stages and the number of choices for each stage) scale up. This problem is closely related to Bellman’s curse of dimensionality in dynamic programming. As the duality principle of dynamic programming suggests (pp. 88,
[
29
]
), the optimal reward and optimal policy mutually determine each other thanks to the recurrent construction of DP. If this is the case, the fundamental difficulty with the search problem in space (physical or mental) can be overcome by the simulation strategy in time conceptually similar to how AlphaGo simulates its future moves by Monte Carlo tree search
[
64
]
. The difference between brain evolution and game-playing lies in the ultimate goal of simulation.

In game-playing, the goal is well defined by the game rule. By contrast, there is no such pre-defined goal in the search problem. The curse of dimensionality is intrinsically related to the lack of a goal (destination for navigation or intention for behavior). Note that for the old brain (fast thinking with automation), the goal was implicitly specified by the maximization of a reward function (i.e. dopamine-based regulation). An important new insight for solving the search problem is brought by “intelligence without representation”
[
65
]
that argues for using the external environment itself as the world model. This line of reasoning makes sense because of the locality constraint (i.e., an agent can only occupy a local position in space and time). In other words, decision-making by an agent is analogous to GPS-based navigation: one only needs to know where to make the next turn based on a local map without knowing the global atlas. A moment of thought with ”use the world as its own model” reveals that one can not escape from modeling oneself (e.g., one still needs to know the relative position in a local map for deciding where to make the next turn).

An important new insight brought by our approach is that modeling oneself is not more difficult than modeling the external environment. Just like one portion of the neocortex (sensory neocortex)trying to predict the flow of sensory observations from the external world, another portion of the neocortex (the prefrontal cortex) can be repurposed to predict an animal’s behavior including internal states. Conceptually similar to the representation of objects by the sensory cortex, the representation of goal by the prefrontal cortex is based on the binding of several internal sources including value signals (dopamine from the BG), contextual signals (other prefrontal areas), emotional signals (amygdala) and episodic memory (hippocampus). An abstract representation in the latent space is given by
\delta(\mathbf{\Theta},\mathbf{\Phi})
, where
\mathbf{\Theta},\mathbf{\Phi}
corresponds to the abstraction of external stimuli and internal states, respectively.

With an abstract representation of both the world and oneself, we can reformulate the search problem as a dual credit assignment problem in space as follows. Conceptually similar to temporal credit assignment in the old brain, we replace the expected reward
E[X]
by its conditional expectation
E[X|\mathbf{\Phi}]
where the context variable
C
encodes the enumeration of “navigation paths” from episodic memory in the latent space. Since conditioning reduces the variance, computing
E[X|\mathbf{\Phi}]
via neural simulation is beneficial to decision-making. Note that the conditioning strategy can be recursively applied to minimize the risk of decision-making (i.e., delay the decision until the last moment)
[
58
]
. The generalization performance of the above simulation-based approach is guaranteed by the law of total expectation
E[X]=E[E[X|\mathbf{\Phi}]]
where the context dependency is eliminated by the integration.

Neural Simulation: Inverse Diffusion Process in the Latent Space

A key insight underlying Monte Carlo (MC) simulation is to use random numbers for evaluating integrals. Alternatively, the evaluation of path integrals is facilitated by the principle of the least resistance in neural simulation. Carrying this insight, we will show how to implement various sampling techniques via neural simulation - an analog computer. Conceptually, neural sampling techniques presented here (refer to Fig.

Cognitive Graph-based Sampling.

The evaluation of path integrals in the Euclidean space can be conveniently implemented by the calculation of the shortest path on cognitive graphs. As shown in

Generalization into unvisited positions can be achieved by interpolation on cognitive maps with the introduction of distance metrics. In summary, spatial sampling (for where representation) is computationally efficient with the implementation of grid and place cells.

Inverse GHT-based Sampling.

As a generative model, the sensory neocortex implements object sampling (for what representation) along the hierarchy. Computationally, inverse GHT represents an abstraction of the sampling in the latent space - i.e., the generation of a new object from a delta response function. Fig.

SLAM-based Conditional Sampling.

Where-and-what representations such as deictic codes
[

are based on two fundamental assumptions of the physical world, known as locality constraints: 1) no object can simultaneously occupy two different locations; 2) no physical location can simultaneously accommodate two different objects. These constraints have subtle implications for conditional sampling as follows.

Lemma 1 (Linear-time Correspondence)
.
Given
N

N
different objects, it requires
O(N)

Proof
. Brute-force correspondence requires
O(N^{2})
computation; but based on the locality constraint, the correspondence between objects and positions can be formulated as an assignment problem with uniform cost. It is well-known that such cost matrix with a simplified structure admits a linear-time solution. ∎

An important corollary of Lemma 1 is the cognitive offloading strategy by deictic codes
[
13
]
. Using pointer-based mechanism, deictic representations divide the problem of where-what correspondence problem into two parallel tasks: 1) identification (what sampling) - i.e., given where, determine what; and 2) localization (where sampling) - i.e., given what, determine where. Such a divide-and-conquer strategy allows an organism to develop a unified representation of what and where using SLAM models. It facilitates the task of conditional sampling by greatly reducing the variance based on the locality constraints.

Goal Simulation via Bootstrapping.

Just like the mirroring of the world by sensorimotor knowledge in the latent space, the evolution of the first goal follows a similar pattern - to mirror the interaction between the basal ganglia (BG) and sensorimotor cortex. Note that this task is achieved by repurposing a portion of the neocortex - agranular prefrontal cortex (aPFC) - without inventing a new learning mechanism. The trick is to bootstrap the original actor-critic model by a teacher-student model: BG originally serves the teacher and aPFC the student. As the learning proceeds, the role between BG and aPFC swaps. Eventually, aPFC becomes capable of predicting the intent of the animal and making sure that the animal’s behavior is consistent with the intent/goal.

One computational solution to implement the teacher-student model is via apprentice learning. We note that there is a dual problem to RL - inverse reinforcement learning (IRL)
[
66
]
that learns the reward function from observing the behavior of the agent following the RL policy. Putting RL and IRL into a closed loop, we observe that imitation learning represents a computational abstraction of functional cloning. Just like genetics-based structural cloning, functional cloning by neural circuits is an example of self-replicating machines that escape the curse of diagonalization argument by self-referential construction. Bootstrapping allows the agent to learn an abstract goal representation from experience (interaction between BG and sensorimotor cortex).
Note that the simulation of goal is resource-demanding, which explains why it is a process of slow thinking. The activation of goal simulation is controlled by aPFC in the situation of uncertainty - i.e., different paths
L
’s lead to divergent outcomes (a manifest of spatial uncertainty).

Intentional Causality is Unidirectional Navigation Path

According to
[
63
]

Repeated observation of correlation corresponds to physical causality, which can be formulated as the binding problem solved by the temporal synchrony hypothesis. Other than observing associations (first level of Ladder of Causation), intervention and counterfactuals have been placed as the second and the third levels in

P(Y|do(X))

P(Y|do(\bar{X}))

Neural simulation offers a computational abstraction of
do(X)

by navigational paths in the latent space. Such an approach to causation can be interpreted as a generalization of temporal credit assignment - instead of assigning the credit based on the prediction of future reward by the critic as in TD-learning, neural simulation allows an agent to assign the credit based on the causal past that is not observable due to the broken symmetry of time. More specifically, the expected reward
E[X]

do(X)

Note that the goal is hierarchically represented by mammalian brains (i.e., goal
\rightarrow
subgoals
\rightarrow
sub-subgoals). Simulation of hierarchical goals allows an agent to decompose the problem of credit assignment both spatially (multi-path) and temporally (multi-stage).
Similar to goal hierarchy, causality can be understood as a computational trick for delaying informed decisions from the causal past. Even though it is empirically out of reach, we can appreciate the significance of causation by understanding its evolutionary advantages. Generalized credit assignment is consistent with the computational abstraction of bootstrapping strategies in space and time. Both spatial and temporal contexts contribute to the task of variance reduction by conditioning in a bottom-up fashion (i.e., a goal at a high level is conditioned on the simulated goals at lower levels along the hierarchy).

To summarize, slow or goal-directed thinking is a stochastic process that generates multiple samples associated with different hypotheses in the latent space. This process requires neural simulation in the latent space as an efficient exploration tool. Sensorimotor knowledge is interpolated by pre-wiring the bias (i.e., introducing spatiotemporal bias from the episodic memory or counterfactual reasoning). Inductive bias via conditioning effectively reduces the variance of neural simulation in the latent space. However, given the fundamental tradeoff between bias and variance, further variance reduction by quantization/classification is needed, which calls for the bootstrapping trick again.

Abstract Thinking: Second-Order Simulation

Who am I or can the brain understand itself? Such a question is only ecologically meaningful when the animals do not need to strive for survival (e.g., the foraging of fruits gives primates easy access to calories) so they can have more free time for thinking. Based on Cantor’s diagonalization argument, no formal system is complete; bootstrapping via self-reference is the key to constructing a new layer of truth system with the goal of generalization in mind (note that pre-wiring inductive bias is introduced by the first-order simulation). Despite the incompleteness theorem, constructing an explanation of the neural simulation itself has evolutionary advantages different from the first-order simulation. The self-referential bootstrapping allows an agent to build an abstract
quantum
model for the thinking process itself, which is implemented by the repurposing of the neocortex again. According to William James

VI-A

Social Brain Hypothesis: Bootstrapping for Quantization

Swarm intelligence is essentially an application or branch of collective behavior that uses the emergent dynamics from local interactions to achieve global goals without centralized guidance.
Despite the evolutionary benefits offered by the swarm, its representational power is limited (i.e., collective behaviors in invertebrates lack diversity) due to a lack of communication among individual agents.
Social communication requires an advanced (reliable and accurate) understanding of each other’s intentions and behavior at the group level. Just like the swap between BG and aPFC in goal construction for first-order simulation, modeling one’s own mind and others’ minds follows a similar teacher-student model in mind construction for second-order simulation. If one portion of the frontal cortex can be repurposed to abstract the sensorimotor knowledge distilled from the physical interaction with the environment, another portion of the frontal cortex can also be repurposed to predict the abstract knowledge needed for sophisticated social interaction. Under the social brain hypothesis (i.e., social interaction has evolutionary advantages), the problem of credit assignment can be reformulated with a new twist - i.e., how can the conditioning variable
\mathbf{\Phi}
be redefined within the social context? Instead of optimizing the individual reward
E(X|\mathbf{\Phi})
, nature finds it beneficial to optimize the quantitative reward
Q[E(X|\mathbf{\Phi})]
, where
Q
denotes a nonlinear quantization operator for generalization purposes.

Unlike interaction with the physical environment, social interaction requires a new reference frame for calibrating the collective reward at the group level. Mentalizing or social projection provides such a reference frame by distinguishing one’s own mind (selfishness) from interacting with others (altruism vs. competition). Conceptually similar to the excitation and inhibition of neuronal groups, individual behavior can have positive and negative projections on other members of a social group. For example, sharing the skill of tool-making can greatly improve the group’s survival by disseminating the knowledge broadly; while stealing or grabbing food from someone else to satisfy one’s need can have a detrimental impact on the group. To reliably calibrate positive/negative projections, quantization is needed as a stone for striking two birds (discrimination and generalization).

Second-order simulation implements the calibration of both positive and negative projections for social interaction.
First, we note that predictive processing at the cortical columns
[
11
]
supports both nonparametric inference tasks: regression (first-order simulation) and classification (second-order simulation). To tackle the exploitation-exploration dilemma, nature discovered that prewiring the bias (inductive bias) is an effective strategy with the assumption of effective variance reduction strategies. Conditioning based on spatiotemporal context can facilitate the task of discrimination or classification; but without generalization, an agent cannot distinguish between the true surprise and the perturbed routine. Second, quantization represents the simplest yet effective solution to generalization and discrimination. It is a bit unintuitive to abstract think-about-thinking into a nonlinear quantization operator. Therefore, we provide a toy example from lower-level simulations such as spatial navigation (unidirectional vs. omnidirectional place cells) and object recognition (cats vs. dogs) to facilitate our illustration. In spatial navigation and object recognition, generalization is achieved by integrating the contextual variable
\mathbf{\Phi}
(i.e., subspace projection). In addition to dimensionality reduction, quantization implements a nonlinear projection onto convex sets with well-defined properties such as stability and error robustness.

Like aPFC for first-order simulation, the granular prefrontal cortex (gPFC) for second-order simulation is also a repurposing of the frontal cortex for achieving more abstract goals. Unlike aPFC where simulation is for sampling in the latent space (i.e., generating multiple hypotheses), gPFC-based simulation implements the projection onto a pre-defined constraint set by a pointing mechanism such as delta measure (i.e., quantization by deictic codes). Such a sequential bootstrapping strategy makes evolutionary sense because quantization or abstract thought is only required by primates with a more demanding need for social interaction. In other words, the social brain hypothesis is a necessary but not sufficient condition for developing second-order simulation. Quantum psychology is also needed for probing into the origin of language, as we will elaborate next.

VI-B

Language is Quantized Representation of Sensorimotor Knowledge

Quantization via second-order simulation offers a reliable representation of sensorimotor knowledge with abstraction. Such encoding allows an agent to not only reliably reproduce one’s own mind but also infer other peers’. Conceptually similar to the extension of temporal credit assignment into space by path integrals, we can further generalize it from the physical to social space. That is, how can social interaction further optimize the solution to the credit assignment problem by transmissibility? Instead of optimizing
Q[E(X|\mathbf{\Phi})]
, can we optimize the collective reward
E_{g}(Q[E(X|\mathbf{\Phi})])
, where
E_{g}
denotes the expectation over a social group?

The answer is affirmative. We can offer two intuitive arguments before presenting the formal solution. First, imitation or observational learning clones another’s behavior by simulating it within one’s own mind. Primates are known for exaggerating their behavior when teaching youngsters. Such exaggeration facilitates the task of imitation learning by divisive normalization
[
68
]
- a useful nonlinear strategy that has been widely observed in sensory perception. Second, the Bischof-Kohler hypothesis states the evolutionary benefits of anticipating future needs. Under the framework of credit assignment, future needs can be understood as the nonlocal extension of the temporal scale. The solution will require 1) long-term memory for simulating bigger cycles in the physical world (e.g., seasonal changes for frugivores); and 2) a communication channel among peers to share the plan for coordinating the collective behavior.

Formally, LDA posits that each document’s topic proportions are drawn from a Dirichlet prior, and each topic–word distribution is similarly drawn from a Dirichlet prior at the vocabulary level. Given a corpus, the algorithm infers both the topic–word distributions and the per-document topic mixtures, effectively clustering semantically related words together into topics. By treating text as a result of topic combinations, LDA enables unsupervised discovery of underlying thematic structures, improving tasks like document classification, recommendation, and information retrieval by providing interpretable, compressed representations of language.

VI-C

The evolution of natural languages has followed its own path for tens of thousands of years. Despite the superficial difference (e.g., ideogram vs. phonogram), all languages can be abstracted by the quantized representation of sensorimotor knowledge. Declaration by labels (i.e., every object has a name) is a neural simulation of quantizing one class of sensorimotor knowledge into another (usually more compact and easily reproducible).

That is, LLM needs to simulate a goal first by modeling itself, just like how aPFC models the sensory observation data. It also needs a leap of faith to simulate its own mind (like gPFC), maybe through interacting with humans. In other words, second-order simulation might be the most essential step in building superintelligent AI systems. It requires the abstraction of sensorimotor knowledge under the framework of embodied cognition.

Likely not if nature’s creation has involved bootstrapping on so many different levels. If humans can overcome the barrier between brains and computers by building an interface for neural decoding or an intelligent machine obeying the brain’s organizational principle, social communication among people can go through the machine without the need for language (conceptually similar to the Santi civilization in the sci-fi fiction “the three body problem”

VII
Conclusion

Unlike Monte Carlo simulation, we define neural simulation as a class of computational models accounting for the evolution of animal brains. From reptiles to mammals, the simulation or representation of the external environment by the internal states marks the first construction of a simplified world model by the old brain (hippocampus and entorhinal cortex). The compression of the old brain to cortical columns provides the mammals’ neocortex cloning capability via Hebbian learning similar to DNAs in genetics. By extending discrete to continuous attractors, the evolution first solved the problem of spatial navigation by path integration in two steps: angular path integration for
S^{1}
and toroidal mapping
S^{1}\rightarrow R^{2}
. Then this simple constructive mechanism is repeatedly exploited by evolution to generate more and more adaptive behavior via bootstrapping. First, sensorimotor knowledge automation complicates the world model by associating landmarks (what) with locations (where). With the change of coordinates, integral transforms can solve both perception and action problems by neural diffusion processes along opposite directions to form a closed loop for sensorimotor control. Second, the prefrontal cortex of mammalian brains solves the search problem by goal (first-order) simulation. By modeling oneself, the bootstrapping allows the animal to solve a generalized temporal credit assignment problem by conditional sampling. Third, a portion of the prefrontal cortex (gPFC) solves the identity problem by the mind (second-order) simulation. The abstraction of both self and goal by quantization in the latent space offers an inductive bias to understanding other people’s minds in social interaction. Fourth, the credit assignment problem is reformulated into the flow maximization problem at a social scale. Languages were invented as the ultimate bootstrapping solution to match the hierarchical organization of one’s abstract thought. The ultimate interface between humans and computers might not involve language at all when the singularity is near.
