The atomic Leibniz rule
Ben Elias, Hankyung Ko, Nicolas Libedinsky, Leonardo Patimo

Abstract
. The Demazure operator associated to a simple reflection satisfies the twisted Leibniz rule. In this paper we introduce a generalization of the twisted Leibniz rule for the Demazure operator associated to any atomic double coset. We prove that this atomic Leibniz rule is equivalent to a polynomial forcing property for singular Soergel bimodules.



1.
Introduction

We introduce a generalization of the twisted Leibniz rule which applies to Demazure operators associated with certain double cosets. We call it the atomic Leibniz rule, and it should play an important role in an eventual description of the singular Hecke category by generators and relations. This result is the algebraic heart which we extract from singular Soergel bimodules and transplant to their diagrammatic calculus.

1.1.

In this paper, we work with general Coxeter systems and quite general actions of these groups on polynomial rings. For ease of exposition, in this section, we assume that
R=\mathbb{Q}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
, with the standard action of the symmetric group
W=\mathbf{S}_{n}
. Let
s_{i}
be the simple reflection swapping
i
and
i+1
, and let
S=\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{n-1}\}
be the set of simple reflections. There is a
Demazure operator

\partial_{s_{i}}\colon R\to R
defined by the formula

(1)

\partial_{s_{i}}(f)=\frac{f-s_{i}f}{x_{i}-x_{i+1}}.

This operator famously satisfies a
twisted Leibniz rule

(2)

\partial_{s_{i}}(fg)=s_{i}(f)\partial_{s_{i}}(g)+\partial_{s_{i}}(f)g.

Because the operators
\partial_{s_{i}}
satisfy the braid relations, one can unambiguously define an operator
\partial_{w}
associated with any
w\in\mathbf{S}_{n}
by composing the operators
\partial_{s_{i}}
along a reduced expression. When computing
\partial_{w}(fg)
, one could apply the twisted Leibniz rule repeatedly to obtain a complicated generalization of (
2
) for
\partial_{w}
. We discuss this in §
2.2
.

What we provide in this paper is the natural generalization of the twisted Leibniz rule to the setting of double cosets. In double coset combinatorics, the analogues of simple reflections are called atomic cosets. We prove a version of (
2
) for Demazure operators attached to atomic cosets.

1.2.

We recall some definitions so as to precisely state our first theorem. Let
(W,S)
be any Coxeter system. For any subset
I\subset S
, let

be the parabolic subgroup of

is
finitary

-coset

(I,J)
-coset

In

(I,J)
-coset

as a map

In

Briefly stated,

•

•

In particular, the subsets

When

-cosets have the form
\{s\}

. Atomic cosets in

can be described using cabled crossings. For example, the permutation with one-line notation

crosses the first two numbers past the next five while fixing the remaining numbers. It is a prototypical example of
\underline{{\mathtt{a}}}
for an atomic coset
{\mathtt{a}}
; in this example

.

Example 1.1
.

We let

Remark 1.2
.

Though we can prove the existence and uniqueness of such a formula, we do not have an explicit description of the operators
T_{q}
. We consider this an interesting open problem. A more accessible problem is to compute
T_{q}
on a (carefully chosen) set of generators of
R^{J}
, which we accomplish in type
A
in Theorem

to apply the atomic Leibniz rule for any pair of elements in

(see the discussion of multiplicativity in §

Remark 1.3
.

In §
3.3
we prove that the atomic Leibniz rule for one realization implies the atomic Leibniz rule for related realizations (e.g. obtained via base change, enlargement, or quotient). The atomic Leibniz rule also depends only on the restriction of the realization to the finite parabolic subgroup
W_{M}
associated to
{\mathtt{a}}

A
, and in finite characteristic as well, see Example

.

1.4.

Our second main result is a connection between the atomic Leibniz rule and the theory of singular Soergel bimodules
[
Wil11
]
. Like Soergel bimodules, singular Soergel bimodules are ubiquitous in geometric representation theory, appearing e.g. in the geometric Satake equivalence and other situations where partial flag varieties play a role.
Specifically, we connect the atomic Leibniz rule to a property called polynomial forcing, whose motivation we postpone a little longer.

Let us first be more precise. Singular Soergel bimodules are graded bimodules over graded rings, but we ignore all gradings in this paper.
To an atomic coset
{\mathtt{a}}
as above, one can associate the
(R^{I},R^{J})
-bimodule
B_{\mathtt{a}}:=R^{I}\otimes_{R^{M}}R^{J}
. This is an indecomposable bimodule; there are also indecomposable
(R^{I},R^{J})
-bimodules
B_{q}
associated to any
(I,J)
-coset
q
, which we will not try to describe here. Then
B_{\mathtt{a}}
has a submodule called the
submodule of lower terms
, spanned by the images of all bimodule endomorphisms of
B_{\mathtt{a}}
which factor through
B_{q}
for
q<{\mathtt{a}}
.

Atomic polynomial forcing
is the statement that
1\otimes f
and
\underline{{\mathtt{a}}}(f)\otimes 1
are equal modulo lower terms in
B_{\mathtt{a}}
, for any
f\in R^{J}
. Williamson has proven for SW-realizations
[
Wil11
, Theorem 6.4]
that singular Soergel bimodules have “standard filtrations,” which implies atomic polynomial forcing for SW-realizations.
Polynomial forcing
(Definition
5.7
) is a generalization of atomic polynomial forcing for a bimodule
B_{p}
associated with an arbitrary double coset
p
. Using
[
EKLP23b
,
EKLP24b
]
we prove Theorem
5.13
, which says that polynomial forcing for any double coset is a consequence of atomic polynomial forcing.

The restrictions imposed on SW-realizations are significant as they rule out some examples of great importance to modular representation theory, e.g. affine Weyl groups in finite characteristic. An
almost SW-realization
(Definition
4.21
) is a realization over a domain
\Bbbk
such that one obtains an SW-realization after base change to the fraction field of
\Bbbk
. An example is
\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
with its action of
\mathbf{S}_{n}
. The second main result of this paper is the following equivalence (
Theorem

5.5
):

Theorem B
.

Let
(W,S)
be a Coxeter system equipped with an almost SW-realization. Then the atomic Leibniz rule is equivalent to atomic polynomial forcing.

We deduce the SW-realization case of Theorem
A
from Theorem
B
and Williamson’s theory of standard filtrations.

1.5.

Let us now explain the motivation for our results.

The diagrammatic presentation for the category of Soergel bimodules, developed by Elias, Khovanov, Libedinsky, and Williamson
[
Lib10
]
,
[
EK10
]
,
[
Eli16
]
,
[
EW16
]
has proven to be an important tool for both abstract and computational reasons. This diagrammatically constructed category is typically called the
Hecke category
, and is equivalent to the category of Soergel bimodules for SW-realizations. For non-SW-realizations the category of Soergel bimodules need not behave well, but the diagrammatic category does behave well, and continues to provide a categorification of the Hecke algebra. The ability to compute within the Hecke category has led to advances such as
[
EW14
]
,
[
EW21
]
,
[
Wil17
]
,
[
RW18
]
,
[
LW18
]
,
[
Ach+19
]
.

The authors are in the midst of a concerted effort to define the
singular Hecke category
, a diagrammatic presentation of singular Soergel bimodules. The framework for such a presentation was developed in
[
ESW17
]
, but this framework lacks some of the relations needed. In
[
EKLP24a
]
we described what should be the basis for morphisms in the diagrammatic category, called the
double leaves basis
, and proved that it descends to a basis for morphisms between singular Soergel bimodules for SW-realizations.

Once the remaining relations are understood, proving that the diagrammatic category is correctly presented reduces to proving that double leaves span. An important part of that proof will be
diagrammatic polynomial forcing
, which is like polynomial forcing except that the submodule of lower terms is replaced by the span of double leaves associated to smaller elements in the Bruhat order. We abbreviate diagrammatic polynomial forcing to
\PF
-forcing
. It is
\PF
-forcing which is our true goal in this paper.

For an atomic coset
{\mathtt{a}}
one can identify
\End(B_{{\mathtt{a}}})
with
B_{{\mathtt{a}}}
as bimodules. In Section
4.2.3
, we explicitly compute the submodule
\PF_{<{\mathtt{a}}}\subset B_{\mathtt{a}}
corresponding to the
\Bbbk
-linear
subspace of
\mathrm{End}(B_{\mathtt{a}})
spanned by double leaves factoring through

q<{\mathtt{a}}
. The following theorem (Corollary
4.33
and Theorem
5.5
)
is proven by direct computation and is the reason we discovered the atomic Leibniz rule in the first place.

Theorem C
.

Let
(W,S)
be a Coxeter system equipped with an almost SW-realization. Then the atomic Leibniz rule is equivalent to atomic
\PF
-forcing.

In other words, for a given
f\in R^{J}
, we prove that
1\otimes f-\underline{{\mathtt{a}}}(f)\otimes 1\in\PF_{<{\mathtt{a}}}
if and only if the atomic Leibniz rule holds for
f
and any
g\in R^{J}
.

For an SW-realization, the results of
[
EKLP24a
]
can be used to prove that
\PF_{<{\mathtt{a}}}
agrees precisely with the submodule of lower terms (see Corollary
4.19
). In §
4.4
we use some novel localization tricks to extend this statement to almost-SW-realizations. Thus
\PF
-forcing agrees with polynomial forcing for almost-SW-realizations, thus Theorem
C
implies Theorem
B
.

1.6.

One is still motivated to prove
\PF
-forcing (or equivalently, the atomic Leibniz rule) for almost-SW-realizations and more general realizations. We now discuss how this process is simplified by Theorem
B
.

Let us say the atomic Leibniz rule
holds for
f\in R^{J}
if (
3
) holds for that specific
f
and all
g\in R^{J}
. It is obvious that if the rule holds for
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
then it holds for
f_{1}+f_{2}
. It is not obvious that if it holds for
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
then it holds for
f_{1}\cdot f_{2}
, thus the formula (
3
) is not obviously
multiplicative
in
f
. However, atomic polynomial forcing is obviously multiplicative in
f
. Our equivalence in Theorem
B
is proven on an element-by-element basis, from which we conclude that the atomic Leibniz rule is actually multiplicative.

As a consequence, the atomic Leibniz rule can be proven without relying on Williamson’s results, by checking it on each generator
f
of
R^{J}
. We perform this computation in type
A
in §
6
, for carefully chosen generators
f
, via a direct and elementary proof. The operators
T_{q}
are simplified dramatically when applied to these generators.

On the one hand, this proves Theorem
A
for the symmetric group over
\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
, and consequently for other realizations (see Remark
1.3
). Via Theorem
C
, this implies
\PF
-forcing in the same generality. On the other hand, it also gives a computationally effective way to apply the atomic Leibniz rule (or polynomial forcing), even if one does not know the operators
T_{q}
in general: decompose
f
as a linear combination of products of generators, and apply the atomic Leibniz rule for generators one term at a time.

2.
Examples and remarks

In this chapter we give some examples of atomic Leibniz rules, in the relatively small Coxeter group
W=\mathbf{S}_{4}
. The reader should not be disheartened if these examples are still quite difficult and technical to verify by hand! Indeed, one purpose of this chapter is to showcase the complexity involved in even the simplest examples of the atomic Leibniz rule.

The reader will not miss out on anything important if they skip directly to
Section

3
.

2.1.
Examples

Our examples take place in
W=\mathbf{S}_{4}
. We let
s=(12)
,
t=(23)
, and
u=(34)
denote the simple reflections. We give the examples without justification first, and then discuss the verification afterwards. We require that
T_{q}(f)\in R^{K}
for some

depending on

(precisely:
K=\underline{q}^{-1}I\underline{q}\cap J

Example 2.1
.

There are three
(su,su)
-cosets in
\mathbf{S}_{4}
: the maximal one
p
which is atomic, the submaximal coset
q
with
\underline{q}=t
, and the minimal coset
r
containing the identity. We claim that for

we have

(5)

\partial_{tsut}(fg)=tsut(f)\cdot\partial_{tsut}(g)+\partial_{sut}(T_{q}(f)\cdot g)+T_{r}(f)\cdot g,

or in other words

(6)

\partial_{p}(fg)=\underline{p}(f)\cdot\partial_{p}(g)+\partial_{q}(T_{q}(f)\cdot g)+\partial_{r}(T_{r}(f)\cdot g),

where

T_{q}(f)=su\partial_{t}(f),\qquad T_{r}(f)=\partial_{tsut}(f)-\partial_{sut}(T_{q}(f)).

Note that (obviously)

while (less obviously)

. These are the invariance requirements.

Remark 2.2
.

Iterating the ordinary twisted Leibniz rule, it is not hard to deduce the existence of a formula of the form

\partial_{tsut}(fg)=tsut(f)\cdot\partial_{tsut}(g)+\sum_{x<tsut}\partial_{x}(T_{x}(f)g)

. What is not obvious is that, when

, this formula will simplify so that only the terms where

Example 2.3
.

There are two
(tu,st)

\partial_{stu}(fg)=stu(f)\partial_{stu}(g)+\partial_{tu}(T_{q}(f)\cdot g),

Example 2.4
.

We return to the notation of Example

\partial_{r}

\partial_{sut}(f\cdot g)=sut(f)\partial_{sut}(g)+T(f)\cdot g

Note that

\displaystyle\quad\partial_{sut}(f\cdot g)=

Lemma 2.5
.

We have

(14)

\partial_{s}(s(f))=-\partial_{s}(f),\quad s\partial_{s}(f)=\partial_{s}(f),\qquad\partial_{s}(\partial_{s}(f))=0,

(15)

st\partial_{s}(f)=\partial_{t}(stf),\quad s\partial_{t}(sf)=t\partial_{s}(tf),\quad s\partial_{u}(f)=\partial_{u}(sf),

(16)

\alpha_{s}\partial_{s}(f)=f-sf,

(17)

\partial_{st}(sf)+\partial_{ts}(f)=t\partial_{st}(f).

Note that
\alpha_{s_{i}}=x_{i}-x_{i+1}
above.

Proof.

The reader can verify these relations directly from (
1

Here are some example computations using (

(18)

\partial_{s}(t\partial_{s}(f))=-\partial_{s}(st\partial_{s}(f))=-\partial_{s}\partial_{t}(stf)=\partial_{s}\partial_{t}(tstf).

(19)

\partial_{s}(t\partial_{s}(f))=\partial_{s}(ts\partial_{s}(f))=ts\partial_{t}\partial_{s}(f).

4.
Lower terms

In this section we give an explicit description of the ideal of lower terms for an atomic coset using the technology of singular light leaves.

4.1.
Definition of lower terms

Definition 4.1
.

Let

I_{\bullet}=[[I=I_{0}\subset K_{1}\supset I_{1}\subset\ldots K_{m}\supset I_{m}=J]]

be a multistep
(I,J)
expression. To this expression we associate a
(singular) Bott-Samelson bimodule

\BS(I_{\bullet}):=R^{I_{0}}\otimes_{R^{K_{1}}}R^{I_{1}}\otimes_{R^{K_{2}}}\cdots\otimes_{R^{K_{m}}}R^{I_{m}}.

This is an
(R^{I},R^{J})
-bimodule.
The collection of all Bott-Samelson bimodules is closed under tensor product, and forms (the set of objects in) a full sub-2-category of the 2-category of bimodules. This sub-2-category is denoted
\mathbf{SBSBim}
.

For two
(R^{I},R^{J})
-bimodules
B
and
B^{\prime}
,
\Hom(B,B^{\prime})
denotes the space of bimodule maps. Moreover,
\Hom(B,B^{\prime})
is itself an
(R^{I},R^{J})
-bimodule in the usual way.

Inside any linear category, given a collection of objects, their identity maps generate a two-sided ideal. This ideal consists of all morphisms which factor through one of those objects, and linear combinations thereof. In the context of
(R^{I},R^{J})
-bimodules, the actions of
R^{I}
and
R^{J}
commute with any morphism, and thus preserve the factorization of morphisms. Hence the morphisms within any such ideal form a sub-bimodule of the original Hom space.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
p
be an
(I,J)
-coset. Consider the set of reduced expressions
M_{\bullet}
for any
(I,J)
-coset

with

. Let

denote the ideal in the category of
(R^{I},R^{J})
-bimodules generated by the identity maps of

for such expressions. Then
\Hom_{<p}
is a two-sided ideal, the
ideal of lower terms
relative to
p
. The ideal
\Hom_{\leq p}
is defined similarly.

So

is a subset of
\Hom(B,B^{\prime})
, and is a sub-bimodule for
(R^{I},R^{J})
.
We write

instead of

.

We now focus on the case of atomic cosets. We use the letter
{\mathtt{a}}
to denote an atomic coset and let

denote the unique reduced expression of

. We let

Because

as

as
(R^{I},R^{J})
-bimodules

4.3.
Double leaves and lower terms: part I

The main result of

is that double leaves form a basis for morphisms between Bott-Samelson bimodules. However,

Definition 4.16

We call a realization a

•

•

The ring
\Bbbk
is an infinite field of characteristic not equal to

.

Remark 4.17
.

Abe

]
has recently developed a theory of singular Soergel bimodules that works for Frobenius realizations, without the extra restrictions of an SW-realization. One expects that the results of

can be straightforwardly generalized to Abe’s setting. For simplicity and because of the current state of the literature, we will work with Williamson’s category of bimodules.

4.4.
Double leaves and lower terms: part II

Definition 4.21
.

An
almost-SW realization
is a Frobenius realization, together with the following assumptions.

•

The ring
\Bbbk
is a domain with fraction field
{\mathbb{F}}
.

•

After base change to
{\mathbb{F}}
, the result is an SW-realization.

•

Finitely-generated projective modules over
\Bbbk
are free.

Example 4.22
.

The defining representation of
\mathbf{S}_{n}
over
\mathbb{Z}
is an almost-SW realization
[
Dem73
, Lemma 5]
.

Example 4.23
.

The root realization of a Weyl group is almost-SW when defined over
\Bbbk=\mathbb{Z}[1/N]
for small
N
(
N=30
will suffice for all Weyl groups by
[
Dem73
, Proposition 8]
).

Lemma 4.24
.

Let
I\subset S
be finitary. Then
R^{I}
is a free
\Bbbk
-module.

Proof.

As a polynomial ring over a free
\Bbbk
-module
V
,
R
is a free
\Bbbk
-module. By the assumption of generalized Demazure surjectivity,
R
is free as an
R^{I}
-module when

is finitary. Thus
R^{I}
is a direct summand of
R
, and is therefore projective as a
\Bbbk
-module. Since both
R
and
R^{I}
are finitely-generated as
\Bbbk
-modules in each graded degree, we deduce that
R^{I}
is also a free module over
\Bbbk
. ∎

Our goal in this section is to generalize the results of the previous section to almost-SW realizations. In all the lemmas in this section, we assume an almost-SW realization. First we note the compatibility of base change with most of the constructions above.

We let
R
be the polynomial ring of the realization over
\Bbbk
, and let
R_{\mathbb{F}}:=R\otimes_{\Bbbk}{\mathbb{F}}
be the polynomial ring of the realization after base change. Let
R^{I}_{\mathbb{F}}\subset R_{{\mathbb{F}}}
be the invariant subring.

Lemma 4.25
.

We have
R^{I}_{{\mathbb{F}}}\cong R^{I}\otimes_{\Bbbk}{\mathbb{F}}
.

Proof.

There is a natural map
R^{I}\otimes_{\Bbbk}{\mathbb{F}}\to R_{{\mathbb{F}}}
, and since scalars are
W
-invariant, the image lies within
R_{{\mathbb{F}}}^{I}
. The map is injective since
{\mathbb{F}}
is flat over
\Bbbk
. We now argue that the map
R^{I}\otimes_{\Bbbk}{\mathbb{F}}\to R_{{\mathbb{F}}}^{I}
is surjective. If
f\in R_{{\mathbb{F}}}^{I}
, then there is some
c\in\Bbbk
such that
cf\in R
(e.g. letting
c
be the product of the denominators of each monomial in
f
). Clearly
cf\in R^{I}
, whence
f
is the image of
cf\otimes\frac{1}{c}
.

∎

Let
B_{{\mathtt{a}},{\mathbb{F}}}=R^{I}_{\mathbb{F}}\otimes_{R^{M}_{\mathbb{F}}}R^{J}_{\mathbb{F}}
. If

I_{\bullet}=[[I=I_{0}\subset K_{1}\supset I_{1}\subset\ldots K_{m}\supset I_{m}=J]]
is a multistep
(I,J)
-expression, let

\BS_{\mathbb{F}}(I_{\bullet}):=R^{I_{0}}_{\mathbb{F}}\otimes_{R_{\mathbb{F}}^{K_{1}}}R_{\mathbb{F}}^{I_{1}}\otimes_{R_{\mathbb{F}}^{K_{2}}}\cdots\otimes_{R_{\mathbb{F}}^{K_{m}}}R_{\mathbb{F}}^{I_{m}}.

Lemma 4.26
.

The natural inclusion map
\BS(I_{\bullet})\to\BS(I_{\bullet})\otimes_{\Bbbk}{\mathbb{F}}
is injective.
We have
\BS_{\mathbb{F}}(I_{\bullet})\cong\BS(I_{\bullet})\otimes_{\Bbbk}{\mathbb{F}}
. As a consequence we have an injective map

(57)

\Hom(\BS(I_{\bullet}),\BS(I^{\prime}_{\bullet}))\to\Hom(\BS_{{\mathbb{F}}}(I_{\bullet}),\BS_{{\mathbb{F}}}(I^{\prime}_{\bullet})).

Proof.

By our assumptions from §
3.1
,
R^{I}
is free over
R^{K}
whenever
I\subset K
. We fix a basis
\{b_{i}^{I,K}\}
for this extension.
Hence the Bott-Samelson bimodule
\BS(I_{\bullet})
is free as a right
R^{I_{m}}
-module with basis

(58)

\left\{b_{i_{1}}^{I_{0},K_{1}}\otimes\ldots\otimes b_{i_{m}}^{I_{m-1},K_{m}}\otimes 1\right\}.

It is also free as a right
\Bbbk
-module by
Lemma

4.24
. So base change is injective on bimodules.
Notice that
\{b_{i}^{I,K}\otimes 1\}
is a basis of
R^{I}_{\mathbb{F}}
over
R^{K}_{\mathbb{F}}
. Hence
(
58
)
gives also a basis of
\BS_{{\mathbb{F}}}(I_{\bullet})
over
R^{I_{m}}_{\mathbb{F}}
.
Since it sends a basis to a basis, we deduce that the natural map
\BS(I_{\bullet})\otimes_{\Bbbk}{\mathbb{F}}\to\BS_{\mathbb{F}}(I_{\bullet})
is an isomorphism.

The localization functor gives the map in (
57
). For a morphism
\phi
between bimodules over
\Bbbk
, let
\phi\otimes 1
denote its image, a morphism between bimodules over
{\mathbb{F}}
. The restriction of

to the subset

is the original morphism
\phi
. Hence if
\phi\otimes 1
is the zero morphism, so is
\phi
.
∎

Lemma 4.27
.

For
q\leq{\mathtt{a}}
and
b\in R^{\rightred(q)}
, let us temporarily write
\mathbb{DLL}_{r,\Bbbk}(q,b)
for the double leaf as a morphism between Bott-Samelson bimodules over
\Bbbk
, and
\mathbb{DLL}_{r,{\mathbb{F}}}(q,b)
for the double leaf as a morphism between Bott-Samelson bimodules over
{\mathbb{F}}
, where for the latter we identify
b
with its image in
R_{{\mathbb{F}}}^{\rightred(q)}
. Under the map (
57
), we have

\mathbb{DLL}_{r,\Bbbk}(q,b)\mapsto\mathbb{DLL}_{r,{\mathbb{F}}}(q,b).

Proof.

The calculus from
[
ESW17
]
for interpreting diagrams is invariant under base change. Alternatively,
B_{{\mathtt{a}}}
is generated as a bimodule by
1\otimes 1
, and elementary light leaves
\mathbb{ELL}([n,q])
are determined uniquely in their Hom space by the fact that they send
1\otimes 1
to
1\otimes\ldots\otimes 1
. This property is preserved by base change. ∎

Lemma 4.28
.

Let
\mathbb{B}_{q}
be a basis of
R^{\rightred(q)}
over
\Bbbk
. Then the set
\{\mathbb{DLL}_{r,\Bbbk}(q,b)\ |\ q\leq{\mathtt{a}},b\in\mathbb{B}_{q}\}
is linearly independent. Let
\PF_{<{\mathtt{a}},\Bbbk}
and
\PF_{<{\mathtt{a}},{\mathbb{F}}}
be defined as before for their respective realizations. The map (
57
) induces an isomorphism

(59)

\PF_{<{\mathtt{a}},\Bbbk}\otimes_{\Bbbk}\,{\mathbb{F}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\to}}\PF_{<{\mathtt{a}},{\mathbb{F}}}.

Proof.

By definition
\PF_{<{\mathtt{a}}}
is the span (over
\Bbbk
or
{\mathbb{F}}
) of the double leaf morphisms. The map (
57
) restricts to a map
\PF_{<{\mathtt{a}},\Bbbk}\to\Hom(\BS_{{\mathbb{F}}}(I_{\bullet}),\BS_{{\mathbb{F}}}(I^{\prime}_{\bullet}))
. By the previous lemma, the image of this map is contained in

. Thus one has an induced map
\PF_{<{\mathtt{a}},\Bbbk}\otimes_{\Bbbk}{\mathbb{F}}\to\PF_{<{\mathtt{a}},{\mathbb{F}}}
.

Note that
\mathbb{B}_{q}
is sent by base change to a basis of

over
{\mathbb{F}}
. The elements
\{\mathbb{DLL}_{r,\Bbbk}(q,b)\otimes 1\}
(ranging over the appropriate index set) form an

-spanning set for the left-hand side of (

), and are sent to
\{\mathbb{DLL}_{r,{\mathbb{F}}}(q,b)\}
, which form a

-basis for

by Proposition

. Thus the map (

\{\mathbb{DLL}_{r,\Bbbk}(q,b)\}
are linearly independent over

. ∎

Lemma 4.29
.

We have

\PF_{<{\mathtt{a}},\Bbbk}=\bigoplus_{q<{\mathtt{a}}}\partial_{\overline{q}w_{J}^{-1}}(R^{\rightred(q)}\cdot\Delta_{M,(1)}^{J})\otimes\Delta_{M,(2)}^{J}.

Proof.

The span in Definition
4.13
in indeed a direct sum of subspaces, by the linear independence shown in the previous lemma. ∎

Henceforth we return to the
\Bbbk
-linear setting by default (i.e. in the absence of a subscript). Now the question remains: is the inclusion
\PF_{<{\mathtt{a}}}\subset\End_{<{\mathtt{a}}}(B_{{\mathtt{a}}})
an equality over
\Bbbk
, knowing that the result holds over
{\mathbb{F}}
? In Corollary
4.33
below, we prove that the answer is yes.

Lemma 4.30
.

For any
\phi\in\End_{<{\mathtt{a}}}(B_{{\mathtt{a}}})
there is some
n\in\Bbbk
such that
n\phi\in\PF_{<{\mathtt{a}}}
.

Proof.

In view of
Lemma

4.26
and (
57
), we can regard both
\PF_{<{\mathtt{a}}}
and
\End(B_{{\mathtt{a}}})
as
\Bbbk
-submodules of
\End(B_{{\mathtt{a}},{\mathbb{F}}})
, which we identify with
B_{{\mathtt{a}},{\mathbb{F}}}
. We have
\End_{<{\mathtt{a}}}(B_{{\mathtt{a}}})\subset\End_{<{\mathtt{a}}}(B_{{\mathtt{a}},{\mathbb{F}}})
by definition. Meanwhile,
Corollary

4.19
holds over
{\mathbb{F}}
and thus

(60)

\End_{<{\mathtt{a}}}(B_{{\mathtt{a}},{\mathbb{F}}})\cong\bigoplus_{q<{\mathtt{a}}}\partial_{\overline{q}w_{J}^{-1}}(R_{\mathbb{F}}^{\rightred(q)}\cdot\Delta_{M,(1)}^{J})\otimes\Delta_{M,(2)}^{J}.

So any
\phi\in\End_{<{\mathtt{a}}}(B_{\mathtt{a}})
is a
{\mathbb{F}}
-multiple of an element of
\PF_{<{\mathtt{a}}}
. Multiplying by the denominator, there exists
n\in\Bbbk
such that
n\phi\in\PF_{<{\mathtt{a}}}
. ∎

We continue with a divisibility lemma on Demazure operators, which ensures that Demazure operators do not “produce” additional divisibility by elements of
\Bbbk
.

Lemma 4.31
.

Let
b\in R
and let
n\in\Bbbk
. If
n\mid\partial_{w_{M}w_{J}^{-1}}(bg)
for all
g\in R^{J}
, then
n\mid b
.

Proof.

Assume that
n\nmid b
. We need to find
g\in R^{J}
such that
n\nmid\partial_{w_{M}w_{J}^{-1}}(bg)
.

Recall from Lemma
2.6
that for
w\in W
and
f,g\in R
we have

\partial_{w}(fg)=\sum_{x\leq w}T^{\prime}_{x}(f)\partial_{x}(g)

for certain operators
T^{\prime}_{x}
defined over
\Bbbk
, where
T^{\prime}_{w}(f)=w(f)
.
Let
W^{J}
be the subset of elements in
W
which are minimal (for the Bruhat order) in their right
W_{J}
-coset. We have
\partial_{x}(g)=0
when
x\not\in W^{J}
and
g\in R^{J}
, so we have

\partial_{w}(fg)=\sum_{x\leq w,x\in W^{J}}T^{\prime}_{x}(f)\partial_{x}(g).

We apply this formula when
w=w_{M}w_{J}^{-1}
. Let
W^{J}_{M}=W^{J}\cap W_{M}
. Since
n\nmid b
, then also
n\nmid w_{M}w_{J}^{-1}(b)=T_{w_{M}w_{J}^{-1}}^{\prime}(b)
. So there exists some
y\in W_{M}^{J}
(not necessarily unique) which is minimal with respect to the property that
n\nmid T^{\prime}_{y}(b)
. Now we have

(61)

\partial_{w_{M}w_{J}^{-1}}(bg)=\sum_{x\in W_{M}^{J}}T^{\prime}_{x}(b)\partial_{x}(g)\equiv\sum_{x\not<y,x\in W_{M}^{J}}T^{\prime}_{x}(b)\partial_{x}(g)\pmod{n}.

Recall that
\ell(xw_{M})=\ell(w_{M})-\ell(x)
for all
x\in W_{M}
.
Let
z=y^{-1}w_{M}
so that
y.z=w_{M}
. We have

\ell(w_{J}y^{-1}w_{M})=\ell(w_{M})-\ell(w_{J}y^{-1})=\ell(w_{M})-\ell(y^{-1})-\ell(w_{J})=\ell(z)-\ell(w_{J}),

thus the left descent set of
z
contains
J
. By
[
EKLP23a
, Lemma 3.2]
we have
\operatorname{Im}(\partial_{z})\subset R^{J}
. By our assumption of generalized Demazure surjectivity, we can choose
P_{M}\in R
such that
\partial_{w_{M}}(P_{M})=1
. Set
g=\partial_{z}(P_{M})\in R^{J}
and note that
\partial_{y}(g)=1
.

Let
x\in W_{M}
. If
x.z
is not reduced, then
\partial_{x}(g)=\partial_{x}\partial_{z}(P_{M})=0
by
[
EKLP23a
, (27))]
. If
x.z
is reduced, then

\ell(w_{M})-\ell(yx^{-1})=\ell(x.y^{-1}w_{M})=\ell(x)+\ell(w_{M})-\ell(y).

It follows that
\ell(yx^{-1})+\ell(x)=\ell(y)
, so
yx^{-1}.x=y
and, in particular,
x\leq y
.
This means that
\partial_{x}(g)\neq 0
only if
x\leq y
.

Finally, we plug
g=\partial_{z}(P_{M})
into (
61
) and we observe that
\partial_{w_{M}w_{J}^{-1}}(bg)\equiv T^{\prime}_{y}(b)\not\equiv 0\pmod{n}
.
∎

Now we can prove that
\PF_{<{\mathtt{a}}}
, as a submodule of
\End(B_{\mathtt{a}})
, is closed under division by elements in
\Bbbk
(when that makes sense).

Proposition 4.32
.

Let
\phi\in\PF_{<{\mathtt{a}}}
and assume there exists
n\in\Bbbk
such that
\frac{1}{n}\phi\in\End(B_{\mathtt{a}})
. Then
\frac{1}{n}\phi\in\PF_{<{\mathtt{a}}}
.

Proof.

If
n
is a unit in
\Bbbk
the result is trivial, so assume otherwise.

Let
\phi\in\PF_{<{\mathtt{a}}}
and assume that
\frac{1}{n}\phi\in\End(B_{\mathtt{a}})\cong R^{I}\otimes_{R^{M}}R^{J}
. We can write

\phi=\sum_{q<{\mathtt{a}}}\partial_{\overline{q}w_{J}^{-1}}(b_{q}\cdot\Delta_{M,(1)}^{J})\otimes\Delta_{M,(2)}^{J}

for some unique
b_{q}\in R^{\rightred(q)}
. For clarity we choose to unravel Sweedler’s notation. Choose dual bases
\{c_{i}\}
and
\{d_{i}\}
for
R^{J}
over
R^{M}
relative to the Frobenius trace map
\partial^{J}_{M}
. We have

\phi=\sum_{i}\sum_{q<{\mathtt{a}}}\partial_{\overline{q}w_{J}^{-1}}(b_{q}\cdot c_{i})\otimes d_{i}.

Since
d_{i}
is a basis of
R^{J}
over
R^{M}
, any element of
R^{I}\otimes_{R^{M}}R^{J}
is uniquely expressible as
\sum_{i}f_{i}\otimes d_{i}
for
f_{i}\in R^{I}
. In particular, if
n
divides
\sum_{i}f_{i}\otimes d_{i}
we have

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}f_{i}\otimes d_{i}=\sum_{i}f^{\prime}_{i}\otimes d_{i}

for some unique
f^{\prime}_{i}
. Then
\sum f_{i}\otimes d_{i}=\sum_{i}nf^{\prime}_{i}\otimes d_{i}
, and by the unicity mentioned before, this implies that
nf_{i}^{\prime}=f_{i}
for all
i.

Consequently,
\phi
is divisible by
n
if and only if for all
i
we have

n\mid\sum_{q<{\mathtt{a}}}\partial_{\overline{q}w_{J}^{-1}}(b_{q}\cdot c_{i}).

We want to show that all the
b_{q}
are actually divisible by
n
, so that
\frac{1}{n}\phi\in\PF_{<{\mathtt{a}}}
.

Assume for contradiction that there exists a minimal
r
such that
n\nmid b_{r}
. Let
z
be such that
z.\overline{r}w_{J}^{-1}=w_{M}w_{J}^{-1}
. Then by similar arguments to the previous lemma we have

0\equiv\partial_{z}\left(\sum_{q<{\mathtt{a}}}\partial_{\overline{q}w_{J}^{-1}}(b_{q}\cdot c_{i})\right)\equiv\partial_{w_{M}w_{J}^{-1}}(b_{r}\cdot c_{i})\pmod{n}

for each
i
.
The subset of
R^{J}
consisting of those
c
for which
\partial_{w_{M}w_{J}^{-1}}(b_{r}\cdot c)\equiv 0\pmod{n}
is evidently an
R^{M}
-submodule. Since this submodule contains a basis
\{c_{i}\}
for
R^{J}
over
R^{M}
, it must contain all of
R^{J}
. Thus

n\mid\partial_{w_{M}w_{J}^{-1}}(b_{r}\cdot g)

for all
g\in R^{J}
. By
Lemma

4.31
we deduce
n\mid b_{r}
, leading to a contradiction.
∎

Corollary 4.33
.

For an almost SW-realization we have
\PF_{<{\mathtt{a}}}=\End_{<{\mathtt{a}}}(B_{\mathtt{a}})
. In particular,
\PF_{<{\mathtt{a}}}
is an
(R^{I},R^{J})
-bimodule.

Proof.

The containment
\PF_{<{\mathtt{a}}}\subset\End_{{\mathtt{a}}}(B_{{\mathtt{a}}})
was already shown in
Lemma

4.14
. Now pick an arbitrary element
\psi\in\End_{<{\mathtt{a}}}(B_{\mathtt{a}})
. By
Lemma

4.30
, there is some
n\in\Bbbk
such that
n\psi\in\PF_{<{\mathtt{a}}}
. Then
\psi=\frac{1}{n}(n\psi)\in\End_{<{\mathtt{a}}}(B_{\mathtt{a}})
, so by
Proposition

4.32
we deduce that
\psi\in\PF_{<{\mathtt{a}}}
.
∎

5.
Polynomial forcing and atomic Leibniz

5.1.
Polynomial forcing for atomic cosets

Now we explain the concept of polynomial forcing. We consider first the case of an atomic coset
{\mathtt{a}}\leftrightharpoons[[I\subset M\supset J]]
.

Recall that
\underline{{\mathtt{a}}}J=I\underline{{\mathtt{a}}}
and
\leftred({\mathtt{a}})=I
since
{\mathtt{a}}
is core. Thus there is an isomorphism

(62)

R^{J}\to R^{I},\qquad f\mapsto\underline{{\mathtt{a}}}(f).

Definition 5.1
.

Let
{\mathtt{a}}
be an atomic coset with reduced expression
[[I\subset M\supset J]]
and let
f\in R^{J}
. We say that
polynomial forcing
holds for
f
and
{\mathtt{a}}
if we have

(63)

1\otimes f-\underline{{\mathtt{a}}}(f)\otimes 1\in\End_{<{\mathtt{a}}}(B_{\mathtt{a}}).

We say that
polynomial forcing
holds for
{\mathtt{a}}
if (
63
) holds for all
f\in R^{J}
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Suppose that (
63
) holds for
f_{1}
and for
f_{2}
, with both
f_{1},f_{2}\in R^{J}
. Then it holds for
f_{1}+f_{2}
and
f_{1}f_{2}
.

Proof.

Additivity is trivial, because
\Hom_{<{\mathtt{a}}}
is closed under addition. Now consider the following:

(64)

1\otimes f_{1}\cdot f_{2}-\underline{{\mathtt{a}}}(f_{1}\cdot f_{2})\otimes 1=(1\otimes f_{1}-\underline{{\mathtt{a}}}(f_{1})\otimes 1)\cdot f_{2}+\underline{{\mathtt{a}}}(f_{1})\cdot(1\otimes f_{2}-\underline{{\mathtt{a}}}(f_{2})\otimes 1).

Since
\End_{<{\mathtt{a}}}(B_{\mathtt{a}})
is closed under right and left multiplication, both terms on the right-hand side above are in
\End_{<{\mathtt{a}}}(B_{\mathtt{a}})
, and the result is proven. ∎

Definition 5.3
.

Consider

\PF

5.2.
Equivalence

Now we prove the equivalence between atomic Leibniz rules and polynomial forcing. To formulate an intermediate condition in the proof, which is also of importance for the next section, we agree to say the following.
Given an atomic
(I,J)
-coset
{\mathtt{a}}
and an element
f\in R^{J}
,
an atomic Leibniz rule for
{\mathtt{a}}
and
f
is said to hold if there exist elements
T_{q}(f)
such that equation (
30

g\in R^{J}
. Since this condition is stated for one polynomial

at a time, there is no requirement that

is an
R^{M}
-linear operator.

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
{\mathtt{a}}\leftrightharpoons[I,M,J]
be an atomic
(I,J)
-coset, and
f\in R^{J}
.
We have a rightward atomic Leibniz rule for
{\mathtt{a}}
and
f
if and only if
\PF
-forcing holds for
{\mathtt{a}}
and
f
. Moreover, for an almost-SW realization, if
\PF
-forcing holds for
{\mathtt{a}}
and
f
, then
the atomic Leibniz rule is unique, i.e., the elements
T_{q}(f)\in R^{\rightred(q)}
in (
30
) are uniquely determined.

Proof.

Note that
R^{J}\subset R^{M}
is a Frobenius extension, see
[
EMTW20
, Section 24.3.2]
. The trace map is

\partial^{J}_{M}:=\partial_{w_{M}w_{J}^{-1}}=\partial_{\mathtt{a}}.

Let
\Delta^{J}_{M}\in R^{J}\otimes_{R^{M}}R^{J}
denote the coproduct element (the image of
1\in R^{J}
under the coproduct map), which we often denote using Sweedler notation. Then
[

f=1
]
implies that

(65)

1\otimes 1=\partial_{\mathtt{a}}(\Delta^{J}_{M,(1)})\otimes\Delta^{J}_{M,(2)}.

Multiplying
\underline{{\mathtt{a}}}(f)
on the left
we get

\underline{{\mathtt{a}}}(f)\otimes 1=\underline{{\mathtt{a}}}(f)\cdot\partial_{\mathtt{a}}(\Delta^{J}_{M,(1)})\otimes\Delta^{J}_{M,(2)}.

Meanwhile,
[
ESW17
, (2.2)]
implies that

(66)

1\otimes f=\partial_{\mathtt{a}}(f\cdot\Delta^{J}_{M,(1)})\otimes\Delta^{J}_{M,(2)}.

Thus we have

(67)

1\otimes f-\underline{{\mathtt{a}}}(f)\otimes 1=\left[\partial_{\mathtt{a}}(f\cdot\Delta^{J}_{M,(1)})-\underline{{\mathtt{a}}}(f)\cdot\partial_{\mathtt{a}}(\Delta^{J}_{M,(1)})\right]\otimes\Delta^{J}_{M,(2)}.

Letting

, a rightward atomic Leibniz rule for
{\mathtt{a}}
and

(68)

\partial_{{\mathtt{a}}}(f\cdot g)-\underline{{\mathtt{a}}}(f)\cdot\partial_{{\mathtt{a}}}(g)=\sum_{q<{\mathtt{a}}}\partial_{\overline{q}w_{J}^{-1}}(T_{q}(f)\cdot g).

Thus we have

(69)

1\otimes f-\underline{{\mathtt{a}}}(f)\otimes 1=\sum_{q<{\mathtt{a}}}\partial_{\overline{q}w_{J}^{-1}}\left(T_{q}(f)\cdot\Delta^{J}_{M,(1)}\right)\otimes\Delta^{J}_{M,(2)}.

which lies in
\PF_{<{\mathtt{a}}}
by definition.

We prove now the other direction. We have

1\otimes f-\underline{{\mathtt{a}}}(f)\otimes 1\in\PF_{<{\mathtt{a}}}.

By (
67
), we obtain

\left[\partial_{\mathtt{a}}(f\cdot\Delta^{J}_{M,(1)})-\underline{{\mathtt{a}}}(f)\cdot\partial_{\mathtt{a}}(\Delta^{J}_{M,(1)})\right]\otimes\Delta^{J}_{M,(2)}\in\End_{<{\mathtt{a}}}(B_{\mathtt{a}}).

By definition of
\PF_{<{\mathtt{a}}}
we deduce that

\displaystyle\partial_{\mathtt{a}}(f\cdot\Delta^{J}_{M,(1)})

\displaystyle\otimes\Delta^{J}_{M,(2)}=

(70)

\displaystyle\underline{{\mathtt{a}}}(f)\cdot\partial_{\mathtt{a}}(\Delta^{J}_{M,(1)})\otimes\Delta^{J}_{M,(2)}+\sum_{q<{\mathtt{a}}}\partial_{\overline{q}w_{J}^{-1}}(T_{q}(f)\cdot\Delta_{M,(1)}^{J})\otimes\Delta_{M,(2)}^{J}

for some
T_{q}(f)\in R^{\rightred(q)}
. For an almost-SW realization, Lemma
4.29
implies that the
T_{q}(f)
are unique.

Note that
\Delta^{J}_{M,(1)}
and
\Delta^{J}_{M,(2)}
run over dual bases of
R^{J}
over
R^{M}
. The elements

form a basis for

, when viewed as a left

-module. Thus in order for the equation (

. Hence we conclude

(71)

\partial_{\mathtt{a}}(f\cdot\Delta)=\underline{{\mathtt{a}}}(f)\cdot\partial_{\mathtt{a}}(\Delta)+\sum_{q<{\mathtt{a}}}\partial_{q}(T_{q}(f)\cdot\Delta)

for all
\Delta

R^{J}
over
R^{M}
.

Using the linearity of (

∎

5.3.
Polynomial forcing for general cosets

Now let
p
be an arbitrary
(I,J)
-coset, with a reduced expression
I_{\bullet}
.
We wish to avoid the technicalities of changing the reduced expression

in this paper. Instead we focus on the special case when
I_{\bullet}
is an
atomic-factored reduced expression
, i.e. it has the following form:

(72)

I_{\bullet}=[[I\supset\leftred(p)]]\circ I^{\prime}_{\bullet}\circ[[\rightred(p)\subset J]]

where
I^{\prime}_{\bullet}
is an
atomic reduced expression
(see below) for
p^{\core}

An atomic reduced expression for a core coset

is a reduced expression of the form

(73)

I^{\prime}_{\bullet}=[[\leftred(p)=N_{0}\subset M_{1}\supset N_{1}\subset\cdots\subset M_{m}\supset N_{m}=\rightred(p)]],

where each
[[N_{i}\subset M_{i+1}\supset N_{i+1}]]

. In particular,

. Any core coset has an atomic reduced expression, see
[
EKLP24b
, Cor. 2.17]
and thus any coset has an atomic-factored reduced expression by
[

, Proposition 4.28]

We have

(74)

\BS(I_{\bullet})=R^{\leftred(p)}\otimes_{R^{M_{1}}}R^{N_{1}}\otimes_{R^{M_{2}}}\cdots\otimes_{R^{M_{m}}}R^{\rightred(p)}

viewed as an
(R^{I},R^{J})
-bimodule. Meanwhile,
\BS(I^{\prime}_{\bullet})
is the same abelian group,
but is viewed as an
(R^{\leftred(p)},R^{\rightred(p)})
-bimodule. There is an action of each
R^{N_{i}}
on

by multiplication in the
i

74

which is not surjective in general.

An arbitrary reduced expression for

\leftred(p)
or
\rightred(p)

R^{\leftred(p)}
on
\BS(I_{\bullet})
by left-multiplication, and an obvious action of
R^{\rightred(p)}
by right-multiplication.
The goal is to prove that these two actions agree up to a twist by
\underline{p}
, modulo lower terms.

We denote by
\Id_{I_{\bullet}}
the identity morphism of
\BS(I_{\bullet})
.

Definition 5.7
.

Let
I_{\bullet}
be an atomic-factored reduced expression as in (

holds for

if for all

, within

as described in (

) and (

), we have

\underline{p}(f)\cdot\Id_{I_{\bullet}}\equiv\Id_{I_{\bullet}}\cdot f\text{ modulo }\End_{<p}(\BS(I_{\bullet})).

We say that

holds for a double coset

if it holds for all atomic-factored reduced expressions

satisfying
I_{\bullet}\leftrightharpoons p
.

This definition generalizes Definition

because atomic cosets have only one reduced expression.

Let
p
be an arbitrary
(I,J)
-coset. Since
\leftred(p)\subset I
, there is an inclusion of rings

and

via

. In this way,

-bimodule.

Definition 5.8

Let

(I,J)
-coset. The

We identify

Let

Let

The right action of

5.4.
Polynomial forcing: atomic and general

In the diagrammatic category, we intend to use the atomic Leibniz rule to prove polynomial forcing, and not vice versa. In that context, polynomial forcing is to be interpreted as the morphism-theoretic statement that (
78
) is a well-defined morphism, when
I_{\bullet}
is an atomic-factored reduced expression. The goal of this section is to prove that atomic polynomial forcing implies general polynomial forcing.

In
[
EKLP23b
]
, a compatibility between the Bruhat order and concatenation of reduced expression is proven, which implies the following result.

Proposition 5.12

(
[
EKLP23b
, Prop. 3.7]
)
.

Let
P_{\bullet}\leftrightharpoons p
and
Q_{\bullet}\leftrightharpoons q
and
R_{\bullet}\leftrightharpoons r
be reduced expressions such that
P_{\bullet}\circ Q_{\bullet}\circ R_{\bullet}\leftrightharpoons p.q.r
is reduced. Then

(82)

\id_{P_{\bullet}}\otimes\End_{<q}(Q_{\bullet})\otimes\id_{R_{\bullet}}\subset\End_{<p.q.r}(P_{\bullet}\circ Q_{\bullet}\circ R_{\bullet}).

Theorem 5.13
.

Assume an SW-realization. Then polynomial forcing holds for all double cosets.

Proof.

We first treat the case where
p=p^{\core}
is a core
(I,J)
-coset. Consider an atomic reduced expression
I_{\bullet}
for
p
, yielding atomic cosets

such that

. Since

are core cosets, by
[

, Lem. 2.10]
we have
\underline{p}=\underline{{\mathtt{a}}_{1}}\cdot\underline{{\mathtt{a}}_{2}}\cdots\underline{{\mathtt{a}}_{m}}
. Now within

we have

(83)

\Id_{{\mathtt{a}}_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\Id_{{\mathtt{a}}_{m}}f\equiv\Id_{{\mathtt{a}}_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\underline{{\mathtt{a}}_{m}}(f)\Id_{{\mathtt{a}}_{m}}\equiv\ldots\equiv\underline{{\mathtt{a}}_{1}}(\cdots(\underline{{\mathtt{a}}_{m}}(f)))\Id_{{\mathtt{a}}_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\Id_{{\mathtt{a}}_{m}},

where

indicates equality modulo lower terms.
At each step we applied polynomial forcing for an atomic coset as proved in
Theorem

5.10
, and used Proposition
5.12
to argue that lower terms for
{\mathtt{a}}_{i}
embed into lower terms for
p
. Thus polynomial forcing holds for
p
.

If
p
is not a core coset, let
I_{\bullet}
be a special reduced expression for
p
as in (

). Polynomial forcing for
p^{\core}
implies that

\underline{p}(f)\cdot\Id_{I_{\bullet}}\equiv\Id_{I_{\bullet}}\cdot f

modulo

\id_{[[I\supset\leftred(p)]]}\otimes\End_{<p^{\core}}(I^{\prime}_{\bullet})\otimes\id_{[[\rightred(p)\subset J]]}.

By Proposition

\End_{<p}(I_{\bullet})
, as desired. ∎

The reader familiar with Soergel bimodules might be familiar with the following example, which showcases how atomic polynomial forcing implies the general case. It also relates our new concept of polynomial forcing to the concept previously in the literature.

Example 5.14
.

Consider the
(\emptyset,\emptyset)
-coset
p=\{s\}
for a simple reflection
s
, and the reduced expression
I_{\bullet}=[\emptyset,s,\emptyset]
. Inside
B_{s}:=\BS(I_{\bullet})=R\otimes_{R^{s}}R
we have

(84)

1\otimes f-s(f)\otimes 1=\partial_{s}(f)\cdot\frac{1}{2}(\alpha_{s}\otimes 1+1\otimes\alpha_{s}).

The term on the right hand side is in
\End_{<s}(I_{\bullet})
, a consequence of the so-called
polynomial forcing relation
in the Hecke category, see e.g.
[
EW16
, (5.2)]
.

Now consider the
(\emptyset,\emptyset)
coset
p=\{w\}
for some
w\in W
, and a reduced expression
I_{\bullet}=[\emptyset,s_{1},\emptyset,s_{2},\ldots,\emptyset,s_{d},\emptyset]
. By applying the polynomial forcing relation for
B_{s_{d}}
, we see that
1\otimes\cdots\otimes 1\otimes f\equiv 1\otimes\cdots\otimes s_{d}(f)\otimes 1
modulo maps which factor through
[\emptyset,s_{1},\emptyset,\ldots,\emptyset,s_{d-1},\emptyset]
. Continuing, we can apply polynomial forcing for each
B_{s_{i}}
to force
f
across all the tensors, at the cost of maps which factor through subexpressions of
s_{1}\cdots s_{d}
. By the subexpression property of the Bruhat order, such maps consist of lower terms.

6.

6.2.
Complete symmetric polynomials

Fix
a,b\geq 1
and continue to use the notation from the previous section. The standard action of
\mathbf{S}_{n}
on
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
(with the standard choice of roots and coroots) we call the
permutation realization
. Let
R=\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
. All Demazure operators preserve
R
. By a result of Demazure
[
Dem73
, Lemme 5]
, Frobenius surjectivity holds in type
A
over
\mathbb{Z}
, that is, for any
I\subset M
we can find
P_{I}\in R
such that
\partial_{I}(P_{I})=1
.

Moreover, for any
J\subset I
the ring
R^{I}
is Frobenius over
R^{J}
and we can choose dual bases
\Delta^{J}_{I,(1)}
and
\Delta^{J}_{I,(1)}
accordingly.

It is well-known that the subring
R^{J}=R^{\mathbf{S}_{a}\times\mathbf{S}_{b}}
is generated over
R^{\mathbf{S}_{n}}
by the complete symmetric polynomials

h_{i}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{a})=\sum_{1\leq k_{1}\leq k_{2}\leq\ldots\leq k_{i}\leq a}x_{k_{1}}x_{k_{2}}\cdots x_{k_{i}}

in the first
a
variables. In this section, we directly prove the atomic Leibniz rule for
{\mathtt{a}}
when
f=h_{i}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{a})
.

One of the great features of complete symmetric polynomials is their behavior under Demazure operators. For example, we have

(91)

\partial_{3}(h_{i}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}))=h_{i-1}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4}).

As a consequence,
\partial_{2}\partial_{3}(h_{i}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}))=0
, a fact which is false if
h_{i}
is replaced by some general polynomial inside
R^{\mathbf{S}_{3}\times\mathbf{S}_{n-3}}
. Indeed, the only elements
w\leq s_{1}s_{2}s_{3}
for which
\partial_{w}(h_{i}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}))\neq 0
are
w=s_{3}
and
w=e
. This will simplify the computation considerably.

Below we shall use letters like
X
,
Y
, and
Z
to denote subsets of
\{1,\ldots,n\}
. We write
h_{i}(X)
for the
i
-th complete symmetric polynomial in the variables
x_{j}
for
j\in X
.

Lemma 6.2
.

We have

(92)

\partial_{j}(h_{i}(X))=\begin{cases}h_{i-1}(X\cup\{j+1\})&\text{if }j\in X\text{and }j+1\not\in X\\
-h_{i-1}(X\cup\{j\})&\text{if }j+1\in X\text{and }j\not\in X\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Proof.

Clearly,
h_{i}(X)
is
s_{j}
-invariant if both
j
and
j+1
are inside or outside
X
, so
\partial_{j}(h_{i}(X))=0
.
Assume now that
j\in X
and
j+1\not\in X
.
We have
h_{i}(X)=h_{i}(X\setminus\{j\})+h_{i-1}(X)x_{j}
. Hence,

\partial_{j}(h_{i}(X))=\partial_{j}(h_{i-1}(X)x_{j}).

Applying the twisted Leibniz rule, by induction on
|X|
, we obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{j}(h_{i}(X))

\displaystyle=h_{i-2}(X\cup\{j+1\})x_{j}+s_{j}(h_{i-1}(X))\partial_{j}(x_{j})

\displaystyle=h_{i-2}(X\cup\{j+1\})x_{j}+h_{i-1}(X\cup\{j+1\}\setminus\{j\})

\displaystyle=h_{i-1}(X\cup\{j+1\}).

The case where
j+1\in X
and
j\notin X
follows because
\partial_{j}(s_{j}(f))=-\partial_{j}(f)
, so we have

\partial_{j}(h_{i}(X))=-\partial_{j}(h_{i}(X\cup\{j\}\setminus\{j+1\}))=-h_{i-1}(X\cup\{j\}).\qed

Theorem 6.3
.

Use the notation of §
6.1
. Fix
a,b\geq 1
, and let
n=a+b.
Let
X=\{1,\ldots,a\}
and
Y=\{n,n-1,\ldots,n+1-a\}
. Recall
y_{0},y_{1}\in\mathbf{S}_{n}
from (
90

Then for any
i\geq 0
and any

we have

(93)

\partial_{y_{0}}(h_{i}(X)\cdot g)=h_{i}(Y)\cdot\partial_{y_{0}}(g)+\partial_{y_{1}}(h_{i-1}(X\cup n)\cdot g).

As
\partial_{p}=\partial_{y_{0}},

\underline{p}=y_{0},
and
y_{0}(h_{i}(X))=h_{i}(Y)
,
this is compatible with (
30
), where
T_{q_{1}}(h_{i}(X))=h_{i-1}(X\cup n)
, and
T_{q_{k}}(h_{i}(X))
is zero for all
k>1
. Most of the terms in (

Proof.

We will do a proof by example, for the example
a=3
and
b=4
. The general proof is effectively the same only the notation is more cumbersome.

In this proof, we write
\partial_{123}
for
\partial_{1}\circ\partial_{2}\circ\partial_{3}
(and not the Frobenius trace associated to the longest element
w_{123}
). We use parenthesization for emphasis, so that
\partial_{(12)3}
is the same thing as
\partial_{123}
, but emphasizes that
\partial_{(12)3}=\partial_{12}\circ\partial_{3}
.

Remember that
g
is invariant under anything except
s_{3}
, so
\partial_{j}(g)=0
if
j\neq 3
. This implies, for example, that
\partial_{3}
kills
\partial_{23}(g)
, and that
\partial_{2}
kills
\partial_{3243}(g)
, etcetera.

We claim that

(94)

\partial_{123}(h_{i}(123)g)=h_{i-1}(1234)\partial_{23}(g)+h_{i}(234)\partial_{123}(g).

One proof is to apply the ordinary twisted Leibniz rule repeatedly using (
92
). After one step we obtain

\partial_{123}(h_{i}(123)g)=\partial_{12}\left(h_{i-1}(1234)g+h_{i}(124)\partial_{3}(g)\right).

The first term on the right-hand side is invariant under
s_{2}
, so it is killed by
\partial_{2}
. Thus we have

\partial_{123}(h_{i}(123)g)=\partial_{1}(\partial_{2}(h_{i}(124)\partial_{3}(g)))=\partial_{1}(h_{i-1}(1234)\partial_{3}(g)+h_{i}(134)\partial_{23}(g)).

Again the first term on the right-hand side is invariant under
s_{1}
, so it is killed by
\partial_{1}
. A final application of the twisted Leibniz rule to

gives (
94
). Essentially, this proof is by iterating the twisted Leibniz rule and arguing that the first term vanishes in every application but the last, because the first term is appropriately invariant. We call this the
easy invariance argument
.

Note that
\partial_{123}(g)
is invariant under everything but
s_{4}
. More generally,
\partial_{12\ldots a}(g)
is killed by
\partial_{j}
for all

. This is true when

since

. This is true when

and

. This is true when

because

and

.

By the easy invariance argument again, but with indices shifted and

replaced by
\partial_{123}(g)
, we have

(95)

\partial_{234}(h_{i}(234)\partial_{123}(g))=h_{i-1}(2345)\partial_{34123}(g)+h_{i}(345)\partial_{234123}(g).

This is how we treat the second term in the right side of (
94

Note that all computations above are unchanged by adding new variables to our complete symmetric polynomials which are untouched by any of the simple reflections used by the formula. For example, adding

in (

In this example, we call

an

Now we examine the first term in the right side of (

\partial_{23}(g)
is invariant under all simple reflections except

and
s_{4}

However, as

Overall, we see that

\displaystyle\partial_{(234)(123)}(h_{i}(123)g)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle h_{i}(345)\partial_{(234)(123)}(g)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle h_{i-1}(2345)\partial_{(34)(123)}(g)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle h_{i-2}(12345)\partial_{(34)(23)}(g).

The pattern is relatively straightforward.
Here’s the next one in the pattern:

(100)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle h_{i}(456)\partial_{(345)(234)(123)}(g)

\displaystyle+

6.3.
Reduction to the connected case

The previous section proves an atomic Leibniz rule under the assumption
W_{M}=\mathbf{S}_{n}
. Now we do the general case.

Let
W=\mathbf{S}_{n}
. An arbitrary atomic coset in
W
contains the longest element of the reducible Coxeter group
W_{M}=\mathbf{S}_{n_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathbf{S}_{n_{k}}
where
\sum n_{i}=n
. It is a coset for
(\widehat{s},\widehat{t})
, where
s
and
t
are simple reflections in the same irreducible component
\mathbf{S}_{n_{i}}
of
W_{M}
. We can prove polynomial forcing for an arbitrary atomic coset in type
A
if we can bootstrap the result from
\mathbf{S}_{n_{i}}
to
W_{M}
.

In this discussion, there is no difference between type
A
and a general Coxeter type. Thus let
M
be finitary, with
s,t\in M
and
w_{M}sw_{M}=t
. Let
{\mathtt{a}}
be the atomic
(\widehat{s},\widehat{t})
-coset containing
w_{M}
.

Now suppose that
M=C_{1}\sqcup C_{2}\sqcup\ldots\sqcup C_{k}
is a disjoint union of connected components (the simple reflections in
C_{i}
commute with those in
C_{j}
for
i\neq j
). Suppose without loss of generality that
s\in C_{1}
, and let
D=C_{2}\sqcup\ldots\sqcup C_{k}
. Then
t\in C_{1}
as well, and
t=w_{C_{1}}sw_{C_{1}}
. Let
{\mathtt{a}}^{\prime}
denote the atomic
(C_{1}\setminus s,C_{1}\setminus t)
-coset containing
w_{C_{1}}
. Then
{\mathtt{a}}
and
{\mathtt{a}}^{\prime}
are related by the operation
+D
described in
[
EK21
, §4.10]
.

Lemma 6.5
.

With the notation as above, polynomial forcing holds for
{\mathtt{a}}
if and only if it holds for
{\mathtt{a}}^{\prime}
.

Lemma 6.6
.

With the notation as above, an atomic Leibniz rule holds for
{\mathtt{a}}
if and only if it holds for
{\mathtt{a}}^{\prime}
.

Proof.

The proof is straightforward and left to the reader, but we wish to point out the available ingredients. Many basic properties of the operator
+D
are given in
[
EK21
, §4.10]
. There is a bijection between cosets
q<{\mathtt{a}}
and cosets
q^{\prime}<{\mathtt{a}}^{\prime}
, and also a bijection between their reduced expressions. Note that
\partial_{\overline{q}w_{\widehat{t}^{-1}}}=\partial_{\overline{q^{\prime}}w_{C_{1}\setminus t}^{-1}}
as operators
R\to R
. Finally, dual bases for the Frobenius extension
R^{M}\subset R^{M\setminus t}
can also be chosen as dual bases for the Frobenius extension
R^{C_{1}}\subset R^{C_{1}\setminus t}
.
∎

Theorem 6.7
.

The atomic Leibniz rule and polynomial forcing hold for any atomic coset in type
A_{n-1}
when
R=\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
.

Proof.

The restriction of the permutation realization to any
\mathbf{S}_{n_{i}}\subset\mathbf{S}_{n}
is a
W
-invariant enlargement of the permutation realization of
\mathbf{S}_{n_{i}}
.
Thus the result follows from the previous two lemmas, Theorem
6.4
, and Lemma
3.17
. ∎

Applying
Lemma

3.17
, we also deduce the atomic Leibniz rule for a host of other realizations, including when
R=\Bbbk[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
for any commutative ring
\Bbbk
.
