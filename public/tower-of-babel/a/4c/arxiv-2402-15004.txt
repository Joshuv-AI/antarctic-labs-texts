Repro Samples Method for a Performance Guaranteed Inference in General and Irregular Inference Problems
Min-ge Xie, Peng Wang

Abstract
Rapid advancements in data science require us to have fundamentally new frameworks to tackle prevalent but highly non-trivial “irregular” inference problems, to which the large sample central limit theorem does not apply. Typical examples are those involving discrete or non-numerical parameters and those involving non-numerical data, etc. In this article, we present an innovative, wide-reaching, and effective approach, called repro samples method , to conduct statistical inference for these irregular problems plus more. The development relates to but improves several existing simulation-inspired inference approaches, and we provide both exact and approximate theories to support our development. Moreover, the proposed approach is broadly applicable and subsumes the classical Neyman-Pearson framework as a special case. For the often-seen irregular inference problems that involve both discrete/non-numerical and continuous parameters, we propose an effective three-step procedure to make inferences for all parameters. We also develop a unique matching scheme that turns the discreteness of discrete/non-numerical parameters from an obstacle for forming inferential theories into a beneficial attribute for improving computational efficiency. We demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed general methodology using various examples, including a case study example on a Gaussian mixture model with unknown number of components. This case study example provides a solution to a long-standing open inference question in statistics on how to quantify the estimation uncertainty for the unknown number of components and other associated parameters. Real data and simulation studies, with comparisons to existing approaches, demonstrate the far superior performance of the proposed method.



Key words: Artificial (Monte-Carlo) data; Conformal inference; Discrete or non-numerical parameters; Gaussian mixture; Generative model; Inversion method;
Parametric and nonparametric settings

Introduction

The approaches of repeatedly creating
Monte-Carlo (artificial) data to
help assess uncertainty and make
statistical inference have proven to be an effective method in the literature;
see, e.g.,
Efron and Tibshirani 1993
;
Fishman 1996
;
Robert 2016
;
Hannig et al. 2016
,
and references therein.
Most of these approaches, however, rely on the large sample central limit theorem (CLT) or related works
to justify their inference validity.
Their applicability to more complicated problems, especially those involving discrete or non-numerical parameters and non-numerical data (e.g., the unknown number of clusters; models A, B, …;
architecture parameters (layers/number of neurons) in neural networks; space partitions in tree models; graphical network models; population ranks; etc.),
is limited.
In this article, we
develop a fundamentally new and wide-reaching artificial-sample-inspired inferential framework and provide comprehensive supporting theories that can be used
for such complicated problems plus more.
The proposed framework, called
repro samples method
,
does not need to rely on large sample theories or likelihood functions.
It is especially effective for difficult
irregular inference problems
.
Here, following
Wasserman et al. 2020
, the
irregular inference problems
refer to those “highly non-trivial” and “complex setups” to which the regularity assumptions (for large sample theories) do not apply.
Although our focus is mostly on finite-sample inference, the
proposed framework
has direct extensions to the situations where the large sample theorem does hold.

Suppose that
the sample data
\bm{Y}\in{\cal Y}

are generated from a model or a distribution

\bm{Y}|{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits}_{0}\sim F_{{\bf\theta}_{0}}(\cdot),
where

{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits}_{0}\in\Theta
is the true value of the model parameters that can either be numerical, or non-numerical, or a mixture of both.
The sample data
\bm{Y}
typically contain
n
observations
\bm{Y}=(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n})^{\top}
as in most publications, but in this paper we also
allow
\bm{Y}
to be a summary or function of
\bm{Y}
,
or even non-numerical types of data (e.g., image; voice; object data; etc.).
We assume only that we know how to simulate
\bm{Y}
data from
F_{\bf\theta}(\cdot)
, given
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits
.
Often,
\bm{Y}|{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits}\sim F_{{\bf\theta}}(\cdot)

can be re-expressed in the form of
generative algorithmic model
:

\bm{Y}=G({\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits},\bm{U}),

(1)

where
G(\cdot,\cdot)
is a known mapping from
\Theta\times{\cal U}\mapsto{\cal Y}
and
\bm{U}=(U_{1},\ldots U_{r})^{\top}\in{\cal U}
, for some
r>0
,
is a random vector
whose distribution is free of
\bm{\theta}
.
Let

\bm{y}_{obs}=G({\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits}_{0},\bm{u}^{rel})

be the realized observation and

\bm{u}^{rel}
be the corresponding (unobserved)
realization of
\bm{U}
.
The algorithmic model formulation (
1
)
is common in
the literature

(
Dalmasso et al. 2022
;
Robert 2016
;
Hannig et al. 2016
;
Martin and Liu 2015
, e.g.,)
.
It is very general. For instance, it can be a machine learning generative model or a differential equation based model. It also subsumes
the traditional statistical models specified using a (parametric) family of density functions.

The repro samples framework
relies on a simple yet fundamental idea: study the performance of artificial samples obtained by mimicking the sampling mechanism
of the observed data; then use
them to help
quantify inference uncertainty.
The intuition is that, if an artificial sample, say
\bm{y}^{*}
, generated using a
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits\in\Theta
can match well with the observed
\bm{y}_{obs}
,
then we cannot dismiss the possibility that such a
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits
equals
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{0}
.
In particular,
for any
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits\in\Theta
and
a copy of
\bm{u}^{*}\sim\bm{U}
, we can use
equation (
1
) to get
a copy of artificial sample
\bm{y}^{*}=G({\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits},\bm{u}^{*})
,
which we refer to in this paper as a
repro sample
.
When
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits=\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{0}
and
\bm{u}^{*}
matches
\bm{u}^{rel}
, the corresponding
\bm{y}^{*}
will match
\bm{y}_{obs}
.
The obstacle is that both
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{0}
and
\bm{u}^{rel}
are unknown.
However, we can use the fact that
\bm{u}^{rel}
and
\bm{u}^{*}
are both realizations from the same
\bm{U}
distribution (and also a so-called
nuclear mapping function
to be introduced in Section 2)
to first quantify
\bm{u}^{*}
. For the collection of
\bm{u}^{*}
’s that have a relative high possibility of being a potential candidate of
\bm{u}^{rel}
,
we find those
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits
values that can produce repro samples
\bm{y}^{*}=G(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits,\bm{u}^{*})
that match
\bm{y}_{obs}
. Our proposal is to use
such
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits
’s to recover and make inference for
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{0}
. The repro samples
method
is broadly applicable for any, including the complex irregular, inference problems.

In this paper, we formally formulate the above idea to develop a general framework to construct level-
\alpha
confidence sets,
\alpha\in(0,1)
, for parameters of interest in the generative model (
1
) and a generalized semiparametric model setup. The development covers both regular and irregular problems and also both finite and large sample settings.
We show that confidence sets constructed by the method have theoretically guaranteed frequency coverage rates. Furthermore, most inference problems in statistics applications and machine learning models often involve nuisance parameters and are complicated.
We further provide several theoretical developments on handling nuisance parameters, the use of a data-driven candidate set to reduce computing costs, among others.
We also show that the traditional Neyman-Pearson test procedure can be viewed as a special case of the proposed framework, in the sense that the confidence sets obtained by the repro samples method are
never worse than (i.e.,
either the same as or smaller than)
the confidence sets constructed by the Neyman-Pearson procedure.
This framework is effective and it
overcomes (or sidesteps) several drawbacks of several existing simulation-based inference approaches.

The
repro samples method
is particularly useful for irregular inference problems, which
are prevalent and “highly non-trivial”

(
Wasserman et al. 2020
;
Xie et al. 2009
, cf.,)
.
For these
problems,
point estimators (if exist) do not provide uncertainty quantification,
and the often-used bootstrap approaches lack theoretical support.
Bayesian methods may construct credible sets, but the sets are highly sensitive to the prior choices and their frequency coverage performance is typically poor even when the sample sizes are large
(
Hastie and Green 2012
;
Kass and Raftery 1995
)
. These difficulties are because the large sample CLT
and Bernstein-von-Mises (BvM) theorem do not apply in these irregular cases. On the contrary, the repro samples method
can be used to solve
the
difficult inference problems.
In this paper,
we specifically proposed and highlight an effective three-step procedure to handle irregular inference problems with model parameters
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits=(\eta,{\bm{\beta}}^{\top})^{\top}
, where
\eta
are discrete or non-numerical parameters and, when given
\eta
, the remaining parameters
\bm{\beta}
may
be handled by a regular approach.
This three-step approach
dissects a complicated irregular inference problem into manageable steps. Furthermore, the discrete nature of
\eta,
an obstacle in developing inference theories, transforms into a beneficial attribute within our repro samples framework, to greatly improve
computational and inference efficiency.

Major contributions and significance.

1.

We develop a novel and broadly applicable inferential framework to construct performance-guaranteed confidence sets
for parameters of interest under a general
generative model.
The proposed framework is likelihood-free and general.
In addition to the regular inference problems under
the conventional settings,
the method
is particularly useful for irregular inference problems and can effectively
handle discrete or non-numerical parameters and non-numerical data.
(Sections 2-6)

2.

We introduce a so-called
nuclear mapping function
to greatly increase the flexibility and utility of the proposed method.
A nuclear mapping function plays a similar role as a test statistic but is more general and does not need to be a test statistic. In the special case where the nuclear mapping function is a test statistic,
we show that
a confidence set obtained by the repro samples method is always better than (either the same as or smaller than) that obtained by inverting the Neyman-Pearson test procedure.
We obtain optimality results that an optimal Neyman-Pearson method correspond to optimal confidence sets by the repro samples method; we also provide examples that the repro samples method improves the result by a Neyman-Pearson method when it is not optimal. (Sections 2.1 & 6)

3.

Although the repro samples method is a simulation-inspired method,
it can provide explicit mathematical solutions without involving simulation in many cases.
In the cases when an explicit expression is unavailable, we develop effective algorithms to compute confidence sets using simulated
samples.
To reduce computing costs, we develop a novel data-driven approach, with supporting theories, to significantly reduce the search space
of discrete/non-numerical parameters, by utilizing a many-to-one mapping that is unique in repro samples.
For continuous parameters, we can use a quantile regression technique
similar to that described in
Dalmasso et al. 2022
. (Sections 2.2, 3.2 & Appendix)

4.

We develop a novel profiling method to handle nuisance parameters under the proposed repro samples framework.
Unlike the conventional ‘hybrid’ or ‘likelihood profiling’ methods that often rely on asymptotics
(
Chuang and Lai 2000
;
Dalmasso et al. 2022
, e.g.,)
, the proposed
method is developed based on
p
-values constructed under the repro samples framework, and
it
guarantees the finite sample coverage rates
of the constructed confidence sets for the target parameters. The approach is computationally efficient
and
effective for many complex irregular inference problems. (Sections 3.1 & 4)

5.

We provide a case study example to demonstrate the effectiveness of the repro samples method in handling irregular inference problems with varying
number of
parameters.
Specifically, we provide a solution to an open and “highly nontrivial” inference problem in statistics on how to quantify
the uncertainty in estimating the unknown number of the components (say
\tau_{0}
)
and also associated parameters in a Gaussian mixture model
(
Wasserman et al. 2020
;
Chen et al. 2012
, cf.)
.
Numerical studies, comparing with existing frequentist and Bayesian approaches,
show that the repro samples method is the only effective approach that can always cover
\tau_{0}
with the desired accuracy. (Section 4)

6.

The repro samples framework
stems from several simulation-based approaches across Bayesian, frequentist and fiducial (BFF) paradigms, namely, bootstrap,
approximate Bayesian computing (ABC), generalized fiducial inference (GFI) and inferential model (IM),
but it addresses or sidesteps several drawbacks of these existing approaches.
Moreover, it provides a unique perspective to bridge the foundation of statistical inference across BFF paradigms. As a purely frequentist approach, it is transformative both theoretically and practically and has unique strengths in many areas.
(Section 1 & Appendix)

Relation to other work
:
Beside
the classical Neyman-Pearson testing method (Item 2 above),
there are
several relevant
inferential
approaches in the literature. More details and additional discussions are provided in Appendix C.

Efron’s Bootstrap and related methods

are perhaps the most successful and broadly-used artificial-sample-based inference approach
to date.
The key justification is the so-called bootstrap central limit theorem

(
Singh 1981
;
Bickel and Freedman 1981
)

in which the randomness of resampling (multinomial-distributed) is matched with the sampling randomness of the
data (often not-multinomial distributed) using the large sample central limit theorem. Instead of resampling, the repro samples method directly generates artificial samples using
\bm{u}^{*}
.
It does not need any large sample theorems, and can effectively address many problems
that the bootstrap methods cannot.

Approximate Bayesian Computing (ABC)
methods
refer to a family of techniques used to approximate posterior distributions
of
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits
by bypassing direct likelihood evaluations
(
Csilléry et al. 2010
, e.g.,)
. Both ABC and repro samples methods
look for artificial samples that are similar to
\bm{y}_{obs}
, but the repro samples method utilizes many copies of artificial samples at each parameter value while ABC typically uses only one copy.
The repro samples method sidesteps two difficulties encountered in
an ABC method, i.e., requiring a) the summary statistic used in an ABC algorithm be sufficient
and b) a pre-specified approximation value
\epsilon\to 0
at a fast rate as sample size
n\to\infty

(
Li and Fearnhead 2018
)
.
Furthermore, the repro samples method is a
frequentist
method and
need not assume a prior distribution.

Generalized fiducial Inference (GFI)
is a generalization of Fisher’s fiducial method, which is understood in contemporary statistics as an “inversion method” to solve a pivot equation for model parameter
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits

(
Zabell 1992
;
Hannig et al. 2016
)
.
A GFI method can be
viewed as a constrained optimization method that limits artificial data within an
\epsilon
-neighborhood of
\bm{y}_{obs}
, and it relies on large-sample and the so-called fiducial Berstein-von-Mises theorem to justify its inference validity
(
Hannig et al. 2016
)
.
Different than the repro samples method, The GFI method has the same issue as the ABC method, requiring a pre-specified
\epsilon\to 0
at a fast rate, as sample size
n\to\infty
.
Furthermore,
the repro samples method
has a guaranteed finite-sample frequentist performance and can effectively handle irregular inference problems in which large-sample theorems do not apply.

Inferential Model (IM)
is a new framework to produce an epistemic “prior-free exact probabilistic inference” for unknown parameter
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{0}

(
Martin and Liu 2015
)
. A novel
aspect of the IM method
(beyond a typical fiducial procedure)
is that inferential uncertainty is quantified through
\bm{U}
in (
1
) using random sets.
The repro samples method also uses the distribution of
\bm{U}
for uncertainty
quantification, but
through a nuclear mapping function and a fixed level-
\alpha
set.
The repro samples method additionally stresses the actual matching of simulated
\bm{u}^{*}
with the unknown
\bm{u}^{rel}
,
leading to a novel method for obtaining a data-driven candidate set that is particularly useful for irregular inference problems.
Unlike IM, the repro samples method is fully a frequentist approach without needing
any additional probability system
(e.g.,
Dempster-Shafer calculus, etc.).
The repro samples methods can also cover
large sample inferences. They are less restrictive, more flexible, and easier to implement than the IM method.

Universal inference
is another novel framework developed for conducting performance-guaranteed
inferences
without regularity conditions
(
Wasserman et al. 2020
)
.
The method typically requires a tractable likelihood function and data splitting. It relies on a Markov inequality to justify its validity (testing size), at some expanse of power
(
Dunn et al. 2022
;
Tse and Davison 2022
)
.
The framework can handle irregular inference problems and is particularly effective for sequential testing problems
(
Ramdas et al. 2020
;
Xie 2023
)
.
The case study example of Gaussian mixture is also studied in
Wasserman et al. 2020
but, unlike the repro samples method, it cannot provide a two-sided confidence set for
\tau_{0}
.
The repro samples method
is a likelihood-free approach that is also effective for irregular inference problems. Unlike the universal inference method, it does not systemically suffer a loss of power while maintaining its inference validity.

Organization of the remaining sections

Section 2 develops a basic yet general framework of the repro samples method with supporting theories.
Section 3 provides further developments, including handling of nuisance parameters, developing a data-driven candidate set to improve computational efficiency, and developing an effective three-step procedure tailored
for irregular inference problems.
Section 4 is a case study example that addresses a long-standing open inference problem in a Gaussian mixture model
on how to quantify the estimation uncertainty of the unknown number of components in the mixture and make associate inference for other parameters.
Section 5 contains a real data analysis and simulation studies that demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed method. To better understand the scope of the proposed repro samples framework, we
compare it
with the classical Neyman-Pearson procedure in Section 6, showing that the repro samples method subsumes the classical method as a special case.
Section 7 provides further remarks and some future research directions.

A general inference framework by repro samples

Let
(\Omega,{\cal F},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space and
\bm{U}\in{\cal U}\subset\mathbb{R}^{r}
is a measurable
r\times 1
random vector
with
\{\bm{U}\leq\bm{u}\}=\{\omega\in\Omega:\bm{U}(\omega)\leq\bm{u}\}
.
For a given
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits^{*}
and a simulated
\bm{u}^{*}\sim\bm{U}
,
we call
\bm{y}^{*}=G({\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits}^{*},\bm{u}^{*})
an (artificial) repro sample of
\bm{y}_{obs}
and
\bm{u}^{*}
a repro copy of
\bm{u}^{rel}
.
Section 2.1 presents a basic yet general framework of a repro samples method,
including a nonparametric (semi-parametric) extension to which model (
1
) is not fully specified. Section 2.2 provides a generic algorithm to construct a confidence set for the cases
when an explicit mathematical expression is not available.

2.1

A general formulation of a repro samples method

Under model
(
1
),
the sampling uncertainty of
\bm{Y}
is solely determined by the uncertainty of
\bm{U}
, whose distribution is free of the unknown parameters
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{0}
.
As in
Martin and Liu 2015
we first focus on the easier task
of quantifying the uncertainty of
\bm{U}
; however
we use a single fixed level-
\alpha
Borel set
B_{\alpha}\subset{\cal U}
. Specifically, let
B_{\alpha}
be a set such that
\mathbb{P}\{\bm{U}\in B_{\alpha}\}\geq\alpha,
for some
0<\alpha<1
(e.g.,
\alpha=.95
or
.99
).
It follows that we have more than
\alpha
-level confidence that
\bm{u}^{rel}\in B_{\alpha}
for the realized unknown
\bm{u}^{rel}
corresponding to
\bm{y}_{obs}
.
We define

\Gamma_{\alpha}(\bm{y}_{obs})=\big\{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits:\exists\,\bm{u}^{*}\in B_{\alpha}\mbox{ s.t. }\bm{y}_{obs}=G({\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits},\bm{u}^{*})\big\}\subset\bm{\Theta},

(2)

where s.t. is an abbreviation for such that. That is, for a potential value
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits
, if there exists a
\bm{u}^{*}\in B_{\alpha}
such that the repro sample
{\bm{y}}^{*}=G({\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits},{\bm{u}}^{*})
matches with
\bm{y}_{obs}
(i.e.,
\bm{y}^{*}=\bm{y}_{obs}
), then we keep this
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits
in the set
\Gamma_{\alpha}(\bm{y}_{obs})
. Note that the observed sample
\bm{y}_{obs}
can be produced by the pair
({\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits},\bm{u}^{*})
with

\bm{u}^{*}
being an realization of
\bm{U}
, so we cannot rule out the possibility of
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits=\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{0}.

Since
\bm{y}_{obs}=G({\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits}_{0},\bm{u}^{rel})
, if

\bm{u}^{rel}\in B_{\alpha}
then
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{0}\in\Gamma_{\alpha}(\bm{y}_{obs})
.
Similarly,
under the model
\bm{Y}=G({\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits}_{0},\bm{U})
, we have
\left\{\bm{U}\in B_{\alpha}\right\}\subseteq\left\{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{0}\in\Gamma_{\alpha}(\bm{Y})\right\}
. Thus,

\mathbb{P}\left\{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{0}\in\Gamma_{\alpha}(\bm{Y})\right\}\geq\mathbb{P}\big\{\bm{U}\in B_{\alpha}\big\}\geq\alpha.

Thus, by construction, the set
\Gamma_{\alpha}(\bm{y}_{obs})
in (
2
)
is a level-
\alpha

confidence set for
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{0}
.

To expand the scope
of our framework,
we propose to replace the requirement
\mathbb{P}(\bm{U}\in B_{\alpha})\geq\alpha
by

\mathbb{P}\left\{T(\bm{U},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)\in B_{\alpha}(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)\right\}\geq\alpha,

(3)

for any fixed
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits
, where
T(\cdot,\cdot)
is a (given) mapping function from
{\cal U}\times\Theta\to

{\cal T}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{q}
, for some
q\leq n
.
Then, the constructed confidence set in (
2
) becomes

\Gamma_{\alpha}(\bm{y}_{obs})=\big\{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits:\exists\,\bm{u}^{*}\in{\cal U}\mbox{ s.t. }\bm{y}_{obs}=G({\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits},\bm{u}^{*}),\,T(\bm{u}^{*},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)\in B_{\alpha}(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)\big\}\subset\Theta.

(4)

The mapping function
T
, which we refer to as a nuclear mapping, adds much flexibility to the proposed repro-samples method and is necessary for many more complicated problems, as we will see in the rest of the paper.
For now, we assume that the nuclear mapping function
T
is given.
Further discussions on the choice of
T

will be included
in Sections
3
-
6
. At the outset, we state that
the role of the nuclear mapping
T
is similar to, but broader than, that of a test statistic in the classical Neyman-Pearson testing framework.

Theorem
1
below states that the set
\Gamma_{\alpha}(\bm{y}_{obs})
in (
4
) is a level-
\alpha
confidence set, including both finite-sample exact inference and potential (large sample or other) approximations.

Theorem 1
.

Assume model (
1
) holds with
\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{0}
. If inequality (
3
) holds exactly for any fixed
\bm{\theta}
, then the following inequality holds exactly

\mathbb{P}\left\{{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits}_{0}\in\Gamma_{\alpha}(\bm{Y})\right\}\geq\alpha\,\,\,\hbox{for}\,\,0<\alpha<1.

(5)

Furthermore, if the inequality (
3
) holds approximately
with
\mathbb{P}\big\{T(\bm{U},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)\in B_{\alpha}(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)\big\}\geq\alpha\{1+o(\delta^{{}^{\prime}})\}
, then (
5
) holds approximately
with
\mathbb{P}\left\{{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits}_{0}\in\Gamma_{\alpha}(\bm{Y})\right\}\geq\alpha\{1+o(\delta^{{}^{\prime}})\}
, for
0<\alpha<1
, where
\delta^{{}^{\prime}}>0
is
a small value
that may or may not
depend on sample size
n
.

A proof of the theorem is in Appendix A. In Theorem
1
, the
\delta^{{}^{\prime}}
may or may not depend on the sample size
n
.
In examples involving large sample
approximations,
\delta^{{}^{\prime}}
is often a function of
n
with
\delta^{{}^{\prime}}\to 0
as
n\to 0
. However, there are also examples in which
\delta^{{}^{\prime}}
does not involve
n
.
For example, suppose that
Y|\theta=\lambda\sim\text{Poisson}(\lambda)
. Then,
U=(Y-\lambda)/\sqrt{\lambda}\to N(0,1)
, when
\lambda
is large. So,
if we take
T(U,\lambda)=U
,
\mathbb{P}\{T(U,\lambda)\in B\}=\int_{{t}\in B}\phi({t})d{t}\{1+o(\lambda^{-1})\}
, for any Borel set
B
; thus
\delta^{{}^{\prime}}=\lambda^{-1}
.
Here,

\phi(t)
is the density function of
N(0,1)
.

To illustrate the proposed repro samples method, we consider below a binomial example.

Example 1
.

[Binomial sample with small
\theta_{0}
]
Assume
Y\sim Binomial(r,\theta_{0})
,
0<\theta_{0}<1
, and we observe
y_{obs}
.
We can re-express the model as
Y=\sum_{i=1}^{r}{\bf 1}{(U_{i}\leq\theta_{0})}
for
U_{1},\ldots,U_{r}\sim U(0,1)
.
It follows that

y_{obs}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}{\bf 1}{(u_{i}^{rel}\leq\theta_{0})},

\bm{u}^{rel}=(u_{1}^{rel},\ldots,u_{r}^{rel})

2.2

A generic Monte-Carlo algorithm

In many cases such as those of Examples 1 and A1, the distributions of the nuclear mapping
T({\bm{U}},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
are explicitly defined and their confidence sets
\Gamma_{\alpha}(\bm{y}_{obs})

have explicit forms. We do not need to use any Monte-Carlo method.
In more complex situations, however, the distribution of
T({\bm{U}},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
is not readily available and we may need to use a Monte-Carlo method to obtain
\Gamma_{\alpha}(\bm{y}_{obs})
. A generic Monte-Carlo algorithm is as below:

Algorithm 1

A generic Monte-Carlo algorithm

1:

For a given value of
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits\in\Theta
,

(a) Use a Monte-Carlo method to
a level-
\alpha
Borel set
B_{\alpha}(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
in (
3
).

(b) Check whether there exists a
\bm{u}^{*}\in{\cal U}
such that

{\bm{y}_{obs}}=G({\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits},\bm{u}^{*})
and
T(\bm{u}^{*},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)\in B_{\alpha}(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
. If both of the above criteria are satisfied, keep the
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits
.

2:

Collect all kept
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits
to form a level-
\alpha
confidence set
\Gamma_{\alpha}(\bm{y}_{obs})
.

For Step 1 (a),
we can typically simulate many copies of
{\bm{u}^{s}}\sim\bm{U}
, the collection of which forms a set
{\cal V}
.
We may compute
\{T({\bm{u}^{s}},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits),{\bm{u}^{s}}\in{\cal V}\}
, for a given
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits\in\Theta
, and derive an empirical distribution of
T(\bm{U},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
. Based on the empirical distribution, we can then get a level-
\alpha
Borel set
B_{\alpha}(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
.
In particular,
if
T(\bm{u},{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits})
is a scalar, we can directly compute its empirical distribution
F_{\cal V}(t)=

\sum_{\bm{u}^{s}\in{\cal V}}I\big(T(\bm{u}^{s},{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits})\leq t\big)\big/|{\cal V}|
and use the (upper/lower) quantiles of
F_{\cal V}(t)
to get a level-
\alpha
interval as the Borel set
B_{\alpha}({\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits})
. When
T(\bm{u},{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits})
is a vector in
{\cal T}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{q}
,
we may use some development in the data depth literature
and follow
Liu et al. 2022

to construct
the Borel set
B_{\alpha}({\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits})
. Specifically, let
D_{{\cal S}_{\theta}}(\bm{t})
be the empirical depth function for any
\bm{t}\in\cal T
that is computed based on the Monte-Carlo points in
{\cal S}_{\theta}=\{T(\bm{u}^{s},{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits})
,
\bm{u}^{s}\in{\cal V}\}
. Then,
F_{{\cal V}|D}(t)=

\sum_{\bm{u}^{s}\in{\cal V}}I\big(D_{{\cal S}_{\theta}}(T(\bm{u}^{s},{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits}))\leq t\big)\big/|{\cal V}|
is the empirical cumulative distribution function of
D_{{\cal S}_{\theta}}(T(\bm{u}^{s},{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits})).

By Lemma 1 (a) of
Liu et al. 2022
, the level-
\alpha
(empirical) central region

B_{\alpha}({\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits})=\{\bm{t}:F_{{\cal V}|D}(D_{{\cal S}_{\theta}}(\bm{t}))\geq 1-\alpha\}
is a level-
\alpha
Borel set on
{\cal T}
. Here, the empirical depth function
D_{{\cal S}_{\theta}}(\cdot)
can be computed, for example, by R package ddalpha
(
Pokotylo et al. 2019
)
.
Alternatively, we may also get an (empirical) central region

B_{\alpha}({\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits})
on
{\cal T}
using a more recent development of empirical center-outward quantiles (developed based on optimal transport mapping). See
Hallin et al. 2021
for further details.

We note that, in appearance, the generic algorithm needs to screen through every
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits\in\Theta
when
T(\cdot,\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
is not a pivot quantity and its distribution depends on
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits
. The computation cost may be expensive.
Fortunately, in most situations, we can avoid such an exhaustive screen search of all possible values of
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits
. In this paper in particular, we will describe two approaches to help mitigate this potential computing issue, one in Section
3.2
and the other in Appendix B-II. The first one is to significantly reduce
the search space from
\Theta
to a much smaller subset of
\Theta
. This approach is particularly effective
for discrete or non-numerical target parameters, a full development of which utilizes a unique many-to-one mapping in the repro samples setup and
is provided in Section
3.2
. The second approach is for the case when
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits\in\Theta
is continuous and
T(\bm{u}^{s},{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits})
is a smooth function in
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits
. In this case, we can
utilize the developments of nonparametric quantile regression
to obtain the set
B_{\alpha}(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
in Step 1(a)
while avoiding grid search of continuous
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits\in\Theta
.
Due to space limitations, a detailed description of the second approach is only provided in Appendix B-II. A similar computing method using nonparametric quantile regression techniques is also developed in
Dalmasso et al. 2022
for inverting a generic Neyman test to obtain a confidence set.

3

Repro samples method in presence of nuisance parameters and for irregular inference problems

Most inference problems in contemporary statistics and data science are very complicated, often involving nuisance parameters and many times even irregular discrete or non-numerical parameters.
In this section, we discuss how to use the repro sample method to tackle several challenges on handling nuisance parameters and on making inference in irregular problems concerning discrete or non-numerical parameters. For ease of presentation, we partition the parameters by
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits=(\eta,{\bm{\beta}}^{\top})^{\top}
; but with a slight abuse of notation,
\eta
and
\bm{\beta}
have different meanings in different subsections.
In Section 3.1,
\eta
denotes the target parameter and
\bm{\beta}
are the nuisance parameters. We present a general profiling method to define a nuclear mapping function for making inference of
\eta
while controlling the impact of the nuisance parameters
\bm{\beta}
. Sections 3.2 - 3.3
focus on a commonly-seen setting of irregular inference problems, where
\eta\in{\it\Upsilon}
are discrete or non-numeric parameters in a parameter space
{\it\Upsilon}
and
\bm{\beta}
are remaining parameters whose inference can be handled
when given
\eta
.
Section
3.2
provides a unique many-to-one mapping approach that is inherent in a repo samples method to get a data-dependent candidate set in
{\it\Upsilon}
to drastically reduce the searching space of
\eta
. Section
3.3
presents an effective three-step procedure to make inference for
\eta
and
\bm{\beta}
and jointly for
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits=(\eta,{\bm{\beta}}^{\top})^{\top}
, respectively. Note that, for different values of
\eta
, the length of the parameters
\bm{\beta}
may change. The issues associated with the changing length (thus changing the dimension of the parameter space for
\bm{\beta}
) add another level of complication for our inference tasks and make the problems “non-trivial”
(
Wasserman et al. 2020
)
.

3.1

Repro sample method in presence of nuisance parameters

Among the large number of literature on handling nuisance parameters,
profiling and conditional inference methods remain to be
two major techniques

(e.g.,
Cox and Reid 1987
;
Davison 2003
, and references therein)
.
These techniques typically
lead to a test statistic, say
\widetilde{T}(\bm{y},\eta)
, that does not involve
\bm{\beta}
in its expression, but the distribution of
\widetilde{T}(\bm{Y},\eta)=

\widetilde{T}(G({\small(\eta,{\bm{\beta}}^{\top})^{\top}},\bm{U}),\eta)
still often depends on the unknown nuisance parameter
\bm{\beta}
and thus
not readily available. To handle this problem and get the distribution of
\widetilde{T}(\bm{Y},\eta)

Reid and Cox 2015

In this subsection,
we we develop a
profile method for the repro samples method. Our development does not require a large sample or a plug-in estimator, and it can
guarantee the finite sample performance of our inference.

Let

For instance, we can use

used to make a joint inference for
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits=(\eta,{\bm{\beta}}^{\top})^{\top}

\bm{y}=G(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits,\bm{u})

\Gamma_{\alpha^{\prime}}(\bm{y})
be a level-
\alpha^{\prime}

\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits

4

9

T_{a}(\bm{u},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)

let us consider another copy of parameter vector

Theorem 3

.

The profile nuclear mapping function
T_{p}(\bm{u},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
in (
12
) is defined through
\nu(\cdot;\eta,\bm{\beta},\widetilde{\bm{\beta}})
in (
11
).
If
\nu(\cdot;\eta,\bm{\beta},\widetilde{\bm{\beta}})
has no explicit expression,
Lemma
2
below suggests that we can use a Monte-Carlo method to get
\nu(\cdot;\eta,\bm{\beta},\widetilde{\bm{\beta}})
for each given
(\eta,\bm{\beta},\widetilde{\bm{\beta}})
.

Lemma 2
.

Let
D_{{\cal S}_{\theta}}(\bm{t})
be the empirical data depth function
(
Liu et al. 1999
)
that is computed based on the Monte-Carlo points in
{\cal S}_{\theta}
, where

{{\cal S}_{\theta}}=\{T_{a}(\bm{u}^{s},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits),\bm{u}^{s}\in{\cal V}\}
is the Monte-Carlo set defined in Algorithm 1 for a given
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits=(\eta,{\bm{\beta}}^{\top})^{\top}
. Let
F_{\mathcal{V}|D}(s)=\frac{1}{|{\cal V}|}\sum_{\bm{u}^{s}\in{\cal V}}I\big\{D_{{\cal S}_{\theta}}\big(T_{a}(\bm{u}^{s},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)\big)\leq s\big\}
be the empirical cumulative distribution function of

D_{{\cal S}_{\theta}}\big(T_{a}(\bm{u}^{s},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)\big).
Then,

\nu(\bm{u};\eta,\bm{\beta},\widetilde{\bm{\beta}})=\inf\nolimits_{\{\bm{u}^{*}:G(\tilde{\theta},\bm{u}^{*})=G(\theta,\bm{u})\}}\left\{1-F_{\mathcal{V}|D}\left(D_{{\cal S}_{\tilde{\theta}}}\big(T_{a}(\bm{u}^{*},\tilde{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}}\nolimits)\big)\right)\right\},

where
\tilde{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}}\nolimits=(\eta,\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}^{\top})^{\top}.

A
proof is provided in Appendix A. Using the lemma, we can evaluate

T_{p}(\bm{u},(\eta,{\bm{\beta}}^{\top})^{\top})
in (
13
).
Specifically, for a given
(\bm{u},(\eta,{\bm{\beta}}^{\top})^{\top})
,
\nu(\bm{u};\eta,\bm{\beta},\widetilde{\bm{\beta}})
in Lemma
2
is a function of
\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}
only. We can use (
12
) and a build-in optimization function in R (or another computing package) to obtain
T_{p}(\bm{u},(\eta,{\bm{\beta}}^{\top})^{\top})
. An illustration of this approach is in the case study example in Section 4, where we use it to construct a confidence set of (
13
) for the unknown number of components in a Gaussian mixture model.

3.2

Space reduction for discrete or non-numerical parameters

In this subsection,
the target parameter
\eta\in{\it\Upsilon}
is discrete or non-numerical,
and
\bm{\beta}
are
nuisance parameters of any types. We take advantage of an inherited “many-to-one” mapping to get a data-dependent candidate set
to drastically reduce
\eta
’s search space of
{\it\Upsilon}
.
By doing so, the discrete nature of
\eta,
a hurdle for many large-sample-based regular inference methods, becomes
an advantage for computation and theory derivation in our development.

We first note that
\bm{y}_{obs}=G((\eta_{0},\bm{\beta}_{0}^{\top})^{\top},\bm{u}^{rel}),

so we can rewrite
\eta_{0}
as a solution of an optimization problem

\eta_{0}=\arg\min\nolimits_{\eta}\min\nolimits_{\bm{\beta}}\|\bm{y}_{obs}-G((\eta,\bm{\beta}^{\top})^{\top},\bm{u}^{rel})\|
or, more generally,

\eta_{0}=\arg\min\nolimits_{\eta}\min\nolimits_{\bm{\beta}}L\big(\bm{y}_{obs},G((\eta,\bm{\beta}^{\top})^{\top},\bm{u}^{rel})\big),

(14)

where
L\big(\bm{y},\bm{y}^{\prime}\big)
is a continuous loss function that measures the difference between two copies of data
\bm{y}
and
\bm{y}^{\prime}
. However, since we do not observe the unknown
\bm{u}^{rel}
, we replace
\bm{u}^{rel}
with a repro copy
\bm{u}^{*}
in (
14
). It leads to a function that maps a value
\bm{u}^{*}\in{\cal U}
to a value
\eta^{*}\in{\it\Upsilon}
,

\eta^{*}=\arg\min\nolimits_{\eta}\min\nolimits_{\bm{\beta}}L\big(\bm{y}_{obs},G((\eta,\bm{\beta}^{\top})^{\top},\bm{u}^{*})\big).

(15)

In our setting,
{\cal U}
is often uncountable and
{\it\Upsilon}
is countable, so the mapping in (
15
) is “many-to-one”; i.e., many
\bm{u}^{*}\in{\cal U}
correspond to a single one
\eta^{*}\in{\it\Upsilon}
.

We are particularly interested in the
subset
S=\big\{\bm{u}^{*}:\eta_{0}=\arg\min_{\eta}\min_{\bm{\beta}}L\big(\bm{y}_{obs},G((\eta,

\bm{\beta}^{\top})^{\top},\bm{u}^{*})\big)\big\}

\subset{\cal U}
, where for any
\bm{u}^{*}\in S
the mapping in (
15
) produces
\eta^{*}=\eta_{0}
. Because

\bm{u}^{rel}\in S
, we know that
S\not=\emptyset
.
Now, let us assume
S\subset{\cal U}
is a nontrivial set,
i.e.,
\mathbb{P}\big\{\bm{U}\in S\big|\bm{y}_{obs}\big\}>0
has a positive probability. We can then use a Monte-Carlo method to obtain a candidate set
\widehat{\it\Upsilon}=\widehat{\it\Upsilon}(\bm{y}_{obs})

such that
\eta_{0}\in\widehat{\it\Upsilon}(\bm{y}_{obs})
with a high (Monte-Carlo) probability.
The idea is to simulate a sequence of
\bm{u}^{s}\sim\bm{U}
, say,

{\cal V}=

\big\{\bm{u}_{1}^{s},\ldots,

\bm{u}_{N}^{s}\big\}
. When
N=|{\cal V}|
is large enough, we have
{\cal V}\cap S\not=\emptyset
and thus
\eta_{0}\in\widehat{\it\Upsilon}_{{\cal V}}(\bm{y}_{obs})
, with a high (Monte-Carlo) probability. Specifically, we define our candidate set
\widehat{\it\Upsilon}=\widehat{\it\Upsilon}(\bm{y}_{obs})
as

\widehat{\it\Upsilon}_{{\cal V}}(\bm{y}_{obs})=\left\{\eta^{*}=\arg\min\nolimits_{\eta}\min\nolimits_{\bm{\beta}}L\big(\bm{y}_{obs},G((\eta,\bm{\beta}^{\top})^{\top},\bm{u}^{s})\big)\,\,\big|\,\,\bm{u}^{s}\in{\cal V}\right\}.

(16)

Formally,
let

\bm{U}^{*}
be an independent copy of
\bm{U}
.
We assume
the following condition holds:

(N1)

For
any
\bm{u}^{rel}\in\mathcal{U}
, there exists a
neighborhood of
\bm{u}^{rel}
, say
S_{\cal N}(\bm{u}^{rel})
,
such that

S_{\cal N}(\bm{u}^{rel})\subseteq S=\big\{\bm{u}^{*}:\eta_{0}=\arg\min\nolimits_{\eta}\min\nolimits_{\beta}L\big(G((\eta_{0},\bm{\beta}_{0}^{\top})^{\top},\bm{u}^{rel}),G((\eta,\bm{\beta}^{\top})^{\top},\bm{u}^{*})\big)\big\}

and
\mathbb{P}_{\bm{U}^{*},\bm{U}}\left\{\bm{U}^{*}\in S_{\cal N}(\bm{U})\right\}\geq c_{\cal N}>0
,
for a positive number
c_{\cal N}
.

Here,
\mathbb{P}_{\bm{U}^{*},\bm{U}}(\cdot)
refers to the join distribution of
(\bm{U}^{*},\bm{U})
.
Although the neighborhood
S_{\cal N}
and the number
c_{\cal N}
are problem specific, Condition (N1) is often satisfied when
L(\cdot,\cdot)
is a continuous loss function and
|{\cal U}|
is uncountable.
The following lemma states that,
under Condition (N1) and as
|{\cal V}|\to\infty
,

\widehat{\it\Upsilon}_{{\cal V}}(\bm{Y})

in (
16
) contains
\eta_{0}
with a high probability.
Here,
\mathbb{P}_{\bm{U},{\cal V}}(\cdot)
refers to the joint distribution of
\bm{U}
and
|{\cal V}|
copies of Monte-Carlo
\bm{U}^{*}
’s in
{\cal V}
, and
\bm{Y}
is generated with
\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{0}=(\eta_{0},\bm{\beta}_{0}^{\top})^{\top}.

A proof of the lemma
is in Appendix A.

Lemma 3
.

If Condition (N1) holds,
then
\mathbb{P}_{\bm{U},{\cal V}}\big\{\eta_{0}\not\in\widehat{\it\Upsilon}_{{\cal V}}(\bm{Y})\big\}\leq(1-c_{\cal N})^{|{\cal V}|}.

In Theorem
4
below, the probability
\mathbb{P}_{\bm{U}|{\cal V}}(\cdot)
refers to the condition distribution of
\bm{U}
given the Monte-Carlo set
{\cal V}
and the “in probability” statement is regarding to the Monte-Carlo randomness of
{\cal V}
. Theorem
4

contains a clear
frequentist statement:
if we use the proposed Monte-Carlo method to obtain the candidate set
\widehat{\it\Upsilon}_{{\cal V}}(\bm{Y})
, we have a near
100\%
chance (a Monte-Carlo probability statement regarding the Monte-Carlo set
{\cal V}
) that
\eta_{0}\in\widehat{\it\Upsilon}_{{\cal V}}(\bm{Y})
and in addition
the coverage provability of the repro sample confidence set
\Gamma_{\alpha}^{\prime}(\bm{Y})=\Gamma_{\alpha}(\bm{Y})\cap\{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits:\eta\in\widehat{\it\Upsilon}_{{\cal V}}(\bm{Y})\}
is
at least
\alpha
(the regular frequentist statement regarding
\bm{Y}
or
\bm{U}
). Here again,
\bm{Y}=G(\bm{U},(\eta_{0},\bm{\beta}_{0}^{\top})^{\top})
is generated with
\bm{\theta}_{0}=(\eta_{0},\bm{\beta}_{0}^{\top})^{\top}.
A proof of the theorem is in Appendix A.

Theorem 4
.

Suppose Condition (N1) holds and
\widehat{\it\Upsilon}(\bm{y}_{obs})
is defined in (
16
). Then, (a) there exists a positive number
c
such that we have, in probability,

\mathbb{P}_{\bm{U}|{\cal V}}\big\{\eta_{0}\in\widehat{\it\Upsilon}_{{\cal V}}(\bm{Y})\big\}=1-o_{p}\big(e^{-c|{\cal V}|}\big)
.
(b) Let
\Gamma_{\alpha}^{\prime}(\bm{Y})=\Gamma_{\alpha}(\bm{Y})\cap\{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits:\eta\in\widehat{\it\Upsilon}_{{\cal V}}(\bm{Y})\}
where
\Gamma_{\alpha}(\bm{Y})
is defined in Theorem
1
or
2
, then

\mathbb{P}_{\bm{U}|{\cal V}}\big\{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{0}\in\Gamma_{\alpha}^{\prime}(\bm{Y})\big\}\geq\alpha-o_{p}\left(e^{-c|{\cal V}|}\right)
, in probability.

In Theorem
4
, the term

o_{p}\left(e^{-c|{\cal V}|}\right)
tends to
0
as the size of the Monte-Carlo set
|{\cal V}|\to\infty
and the results hold for any fixed (data) sample size
n<\infty
; so they are finite sample results.
We will further discuss and elaborate Condition (N1) and Theorem
4
in Section
4
.

3.3

A three step procedure for irregular inference problems

In many irregular inference problems, the model parameters
\bm{\theta}=(\eta,\bm{\beta}^{\top})^{\top}
involve both discrete or non-numerical parameters
\eta
and the remaining (often more standard) parameters
\bm{\beta}
whose length may depend on
\eta
.
In this subsection, we propose a three-step procedure to dissect this difficult irregular inference problem into manageable steps. For notation simplicity, we focus on the case of
\bm{\theta}=(\eta,\bm{\beta}^{\top})^{\top}
and making inference for
\eta
,
\bm{\beta}
and jointly
(\eta,\bm{\beta}^{\top})^{\top}
, respectively. A more general case of
\bm{\theta}=(\eta,\bm{\beta}^{\top},\bm{\varsigma}^{\top})^{\top}
with additional unknown nuisance parameters
\bm{\varsigma}
is discussed at the end of the subsection. Illustrations and further discussions of the three step procedure, including an example of the more general case, are provided in the case study example of
Gaussian mixture models in
Section
4
.

The proposed three steps are:

Step

1: [Inference of
\eta_{0}
] Use a repro samples method to construct a candidate set
\widehat{\it\Upsilon}_{\mathcal{V}}(\bm{y}_{obs})

and then a level-
\alpha
confidence set
\Xi_{\alpha,1}(\bm{y}_{obs})
for
\eta_{0}
, treating
\bm{\beta}
as nuisance parameters;

Step

2: [Inference of
\bm{\beta}_{0}
] For a given
\eta\in\Xi_{\alpha,1}(\bm{y}_{obs})
or
\eta\in\widehat{\it\Upsilon}_{\mathcal{V}}(\bm{y}_{obs})
, use either a repro samples method or a regular inference approach to construct a level-
\alpha
confidence set
\Xi_{\alpha,2}(\bm{y}_{obs})
for
\bm{\beta}_{0}
.

Step

3: [Joint Inference of
(\eta_{0},\bm{\beta}_{0}^{\top})^{\top}
]
Construct a level-
\alpha
joint confidence set, say
\Xi_{\alpha,3}(\bm{y}_{obs})
, for
\bm{\theta}_{0}=(\eta_{0},\bm{\beta}_{0}^{\top})^{\top}
based on the results in Steps 1 and 2.

In Step 1, we first use the development in Section 3.2 and formula (
16
) to obtain a data dependent candidate set
\widehat{\it\Upsilon}_{\mathcal{V}}(\bm{y}_{obs})
.
Then, we treat parameters
\bm{\beta}
as nuisance parameters and use the development in Section 3.1 and formula (
13
) to construct a set in
\it\Upsilon
:

\displaystyle\Xi_{\alpha,1}(\bm{y}_{obs})=\big\{\eta:\exists\,\bm{u}^{*}\in{\cal U}\,\text{and}\,\bm{\beta},

\displaystyle\qquad\text{ s.t. }{\footnotesize\begin{pmatrix}\eta\\
\bm{\beta}\end{pmatrix}}\in\Theta,\bm{y}_{obs}=G\left({\footnotesize\begin{pmatrix}\eta\\
\bm{\beta}\end{pmatrix}},\bm{u}^{*}\right),\,T_{p}\left(\bm{u}^{*},{\footnotesize\begin{pmatrix}\eta\\
\bm{\beta}\end{pmatrix}}\right)\leq\alpha\big\}\bigcap\widehat{\it\Upsilon}_{\mathcal{V}}(\bm{y}_{obs}),

(17)

where
T_{p}\left(\bm{u}^{*},{\footnotesize\begin{pmatrix}\eta\\
\bm{\beta}\end{pmatrix}}\right)
is defined following (
12
) for discrete
\eta
. Because we already have
\widehat{\it\Upsilon}_{\mathcal{V}}(\bm{y}_{obs})\subset{\it\Upsilon}
, we limit ourselves to check only those
\eta\in\widehat{\it\Upsilon}_{\mathcal{V}}(\bm{y}_{obs})
. Corollary
2
at the end of this subsection states that
\Xi_{\alpha,1}(\bm{y}_{obs})
is a level-
\alpha
confidence set for
\eta_{0}
.

In Step 2, for each
\eta\in\widehat{\it\Upsilon}_{\mathcal{V}}(\bm{y}_{obs})
, we treat it as given so the only parameters in this part is
\bm{\beta}
. By using the repro samples approach in Section 2 (e.g., formula (
4
)) or using a regular inference approach, we can construct a representative set of
\bm{\beta}_{0}
, given a
\eta\in\widehat{\it\Upsilon}_{\mathcal{V}}(\bm{y}_{obs})
:

\Gamma_{\alpha}^{[\eta]}(\bm{y}_{obs})=\big\{\bm{\beta}:\exists\,\bm{u}^{*}\in{\cal U}\mbox{ s.t. }\bm{y}_{obs}=G^{[\eta]}(\bm{\beta},\bm{u}^{*}),\,T^{[\eta]}(\bm{u}^{*},\bm{\beta})\in B_{\alpha}^{[\eta]}(\bm{\beta})\big\}\subset{\cal B}.

(18)

Here, to highlight
\eta
is given, we denote
G^{[\eta]}(\bm{\beta},\bm{u}^{*})=G((\eta,\bm{\beta}^{\top})^{\top},\bm{u}^{*})
,
T^{[\eta]}(\bm{u}^{*},\bm{\beta})=T(\bm{u}^{*},(\eta,\bm{\beta}^{\top})^{\top})
and
B_{\alpha}^{[\eta]}(\bm{\beta})=B_{\alpha}((\eta,\bm{\beta}^{\top})^{\top})
.
If
\eta=\eta_{0}
,

\Gamma_{\alpha}^{[\eta])}(\bm{y}_{obs})
is a level-
\alpha
confidence set of
\bm{\beta}_{0}
. But we do not know whether the given
\eta
is
\eta_{0}
,
so we instead refer to
\Gamma_{\alpha}^{(\eta)}(\bm{y}_{obs})
in (
18
) as a representative set of
\bm{\beta}_{0}
.
To get a level-
\alpha
confidence set for
\bm{\beta}_{0}
with an unknown
\eta_{0}
and to accommodating the uncertainty of estimating
\eta_{0}
, we consider the combined set:

\displaystyle\Xi_{\alpha,2}(\bm{y}_{obs})=\cup_{\bm{\eta}\in\widehat{\it\Upsilon}(\bm{y}_{obs})}\Gamma_{\alpha}^{[\eta]}(\bm{y}_{obs})

\displaystyle\qquad=\big\{\bm{\beta}:\exists\,\bm{u}^{*}\in{\cal U}\mbox{ s.t. }\bm{y}_{obs}=G^{[\eta]}(\bm{\beta},\bm{u}^{*}),\,T^{[\eta]}(\bm{u}^{*},\bm{\beta})\in B_{\alpha}^{[\eta]}(\bm{\beta}),\eta\in\widehat{\it\Upsilon}_{\mathcal{V}}(\bm{y}_{obs})\big\}.

(19)

Corollary
2
at the end of the section suggests that
\Xi_{\alpha,2}(\bm{y}_{obs})
constructed in (
3.3
) is a level-
\alpha
confidence set for
\bm{\beta}_{0}
.

Alternative to (
3.3
), if the size
|\widehat{\it\Upsilon}_{\mathcal{V}}|
is large and we have many union terms in (
3.3
), we can instead replace
\eta\in\widehat{\it\Upsilon}_{\mathcal{V}}(\bm{y}_{obs})
with
\eta\in\Xi_{\alpha^{\prime},1}(\bm{y}_{obs})
, where
1>\alpha^{\prime}>\alpha
and
\Xi_{\alpha,1}(\bm{y}_{obs})
is a level-
\alpha^{\prime}
confidence set from
\eta_{0}
obtained using Step 1. We then construct a representative set of
\bm{\beta}
, say
\Gamma_{\alpha^{\prime\prime}}^{[\eta]}(\bm{y}_{obs})
, as in (
18
) but of the level
\alpha^{\prime\prime}=1-(\alpha^{\prime}-\alpha)>\alpha
instead of
\alpha
. An example is
\alpha=.95
and
\alpha^{\prime}=\alpha^{\prime\prime}=.975
. Now, the set
\Xi_{\alpha,2}(\bm{y}_{obs})
in (
3.3
) is replaced by

\displaystyle\Xi_{\alpha,2}(\bm{y}_{obs})=\cup_{\bm{\eta}\in\Xi_{\alpha,1}(\bm{y}_{obs})}\Gamma_{\alpha^{\prime\prime}}^{[\eta]}(\bm{y}_{obs})

\displaystyle\qquad=\big\{\bm{\beta}:\exists\,\bm{u}^{*}\in{\cal U}\mbox{ s.t. }\bm{y}_{obs}=G^{[\eta]}(\bm{\beta},\bm{u}^{*}),\,T^{[\eta]}(\bm{u}^{*},\bm{\beta})\in B_{\alpha^{\prime\prime}}^{[\eta]}(\bm{\beta}),\bm{\eta}\in\Xi_{\alpha^{\prime},1}(\bm{y}_{obs})\big\}.

(20)

Corollary
2
at the end of the section suggests that
\Xi_{\alpha,2}(\bm{y}_{obs})
constructed in (
3.3
) is also a level-
\alpha
confidence set for
\bm{\beta}_{0}
.
Note that, since
\widehat{\it\Upsilon}(\bm{y}_{obs})
can be viewed as a level-
100\%
confidence set for
\eta_{0}
, (
3.3
) is a special case of (
3.3
) with
\alpha^{\prime}=1
and
\alpha^{\prime\prime}=\alpha
.

We remark that, since the length of the parameters
\bm{\beta}
may be different for different
\eta
’s, the parameter space
{\cal B}
of
\bm{\beta}
, which is also the space that the representative set
\Gamma_{\alpha^{\prime\prime}}^{[\eta]}(\bm{y}_{obs})
is in,
may have different dimensions for different
\eta
’s. Thus,
\Xi_{\alpha,2}(\bm{y}_{obs})
defined in (
3.3
) and (
3.3
) may be a union of sets in spaces of different dimensions.
See Figure 1 of
Wang et al. 2022
for an illustration, where a joint confidence set of the true regression coefficients
\bm{\beta}_{\tau_{0}}
in a (sparse) high dimensional linear model
is a union (collection) of level-
\alpha^{\prime\prime}
ellipsoids
\Gamma_{\alpha^{\prime\prime}}^{[\eta]}(\bm{y}_{obs})
of different dimensions of the models in
\Xi_{\alpha^{\prime},1}(\bm{y}_{obs})
.

In Step 3, the construction of a joint confidence set for
\bm{\theta}_{0}=(\eta_{0},\bm{\beta}_{0}^{\top})^{\top}

is similar to that of (
3.3
), but the set is a subset in
\Theta={\it\Upsilon}\otimes{\cal B}
, instead of
{\cal B}
; thus we use a product instead of a union to construct the joint confidence set:

\displaystyle\Xi_{\alpha,3}(\bm{y}_{obs})=\Xi_{\alpha^{\prime},1}(\bm{y}_{obs})\otimes\Gamma_{\alpha^{\prime\prime}}^{[\eta]}(\bm{y}_{obs})

(21)

\displaystyle=\big\{\bm{\theta}=(\eta,\bm{\beta}):\exists\,\bm{u}^{*}\in{\cal U}\mbox{ s.t. }\bm{y}_{obs}=G^{[\eta]}(\bm{\beta},\bm{u}^{*}),\,T^{[\eta]}(\bm{u}^{*},\bm{\beta})\in B_{\alpha^{\prime\prime}}^{[\eta]}(\bm{\beta}),\bm{\eta}\in\Xi_{\alpha^{\prime},1}(\bm{y}_{obs})\big\}.

Here, the product
\otimes
refers to that an element in
{\it\Upsilon}
and a (corresponding) element in
{\cal B}
jointly form an element in
\Theta
.
Also,
(\alpha^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime\prime})
and other terms involved are defined as in (
3.3
).
Corollary
2
below suggests that
\Xi_{\alpha,3}(\bm{y}_{obs})
is a level-
\alpha
confidence set of
\bm{\theta}_{0}=(\eta_{0},\bm{\beta}_{0}^{\top})^{\top}
.

We formally formulate the coverage of the confidence sets proposed above in the following corollary, and we provide the proof of the corollary in Appendix A.

Corollary 2
.

Under the setup of this subsection and assuming the conditions in
Theorems
1
and
4
hold, we have (a)
\mathbb{P}\{\eta_{0}\in\Xi_{\alpha,1}(\bm{Y})\}\geq\alpha-o_{p}(e^{-c_{1}|{\cal V}|});

(b)
\mathbb{P}\{\bm{\beta}_{0}\in\Xi_{\alpha,2}(\bm{Y})\}\geq\alpha-

o_{p}(e^{-c_{2}|{\cal V}|});
(c)
\mathbb{P}\{\bm{\theta}_{0}\in\Xi_{\alpha,3}(\bm{Y})\}\geq\alpha-o_{p}(e^{-c_{3}|{\cal V}|}).

Here,
c_{i}>0
is some constant for
i=1,2,3
.

Finally, we want to comment that the three step procedure can be extended to a more general case of
\bm{\theta}_{0}=(\eta_{0},\bm{\beta}_{0}^{\top},\bm{\varsigma}_{0}^{\top})^{\top}
, for which we need to slightly modify the formulations in (
18
) - (
21
).
Here, the inference targets are
\eta_{0}
,
\bm{\beta}_{0}
and
(\eta_{0},\bm{\beta}_{0}^{\top})^{\top}
and
\bm{\varsigma}_{0}
are additional nuisance parameters. We can have a similar Corollary
2
. Due to the space limitation, the modified formulas and the corresponding proofs are not included in this section, but we instead illustrate them using specific examples in the case study Section
4
on Gaussian mixture models. For instance, in Section
4.3
we consider the inference problem of

(\tau_{0},\mu_{j}^{(0)})
, for a
given
j
,
1\leq j\leq\tau_{0}
, where
\tau_{0}
is the
unknown number of mixture components and
\mu_{j}^{(0)}
is the location parameter of the
j
th component. In the example,
\eta_{0}=\tau_{0}
,
\bm{\beta}_{0}=\mu_{j}^{(0)}
and
\bm{\varsigma}_{0}=

(\bm{M}_{\tau_{0}},\bm{\mu}_{(-j)}^{(0)},\bm{\sigma}_{0})
, where

\bm{M}_{\tau_{0}}
is the membership assignment matrix,
(\bm{\mu}_{0},\bm{\sigma}_{0})
are the location and scale parameters of the
\tau_{0}
components,
and

\bm{\mu}_{(-j)}^{(0)}
is
\bm{\mu}_{0}
minus
\mu_{j}^{(0)}
. Further details are in Section
4
.
Another example is in the high dimensional linear model example of
Wang et al. 2022
, where a repro samples method is used to construct a confidence set for a single coefficient, say
\bm{\beta}_{j}
corresponding to covariate
X_{j}
. There,
\eta_{0}
is the true sparse model,
\bm{\beta}_{0}=\beta_{j}^{(0)}
and
\bm{\varsigma}_{0}
is the remaining regression
p-1
coefficients plus the error variance.

Wang et al. 2022
compared the method with the state-of-art debiased methods
(
Zhang and Zhang 2014
;
Javanmard and Montanari 2014
)
and demonstrated the repro samples method has superior empirical performance, especially when the true
\beta_{j}^{(0)}\not=0
.

4

Case study example: Inference for Gaussian mixture model with unknown number of components

The Gaussian mixture model has a long history and is commonly used in statistics and data science; however
the task of
making inference for the number of components, say
\tau_{0}
, when it is unknown remains to be a “highly non-trivial” and challenging task
(
Wasserman et al. 2020
, cf.,)
.
Although there exist several procedures to obtain point estimators of
\tau_{0}
, the question of assessing the uncertainty in these estimations remains open.
The difficulty is is due to the fact that the unknown
\tau_{0}
is a discrete natural number
and the standard tools relying on the large sample central limit theorem are no longer applicable to the estimators of
\tau_{0}
.
In this section, we demonstrate how a repro samples method can solve this open question
by constructing a finite sample level-
\alpha
confidence set for the unknown
\tau_{0}
. In addition, we provide a set of well-performed confidence sets for the location and scale parameters while accommodating the unknown
\tau_{0}
, which is also an unaddressed inference problem in statistics to the best of our knowledge.

Consider a Gaussian mixture model with

Y_{i}\sim N\big(\mu_{k},\sigma_{k}^{2}\big),i=1,\ldots,n,

where
k
indicates one of the
\tau
models in
{\cal M}=\{N\big(\mu_{j},\sigma_{j}^{2}\big),

j\in\{1,\ldots,\tau\}\}
.
The model parameters are
{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits}=(\tau,\bm{M}_{\tau},{\bm{\mu}}_{\tau},\bm{\sigma}_{\tau}^{2})^{\top}
, where
\bm{\mu}_{\tau}=(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{\tau})^{\top},\bm{\sigma}_{\tau}=(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{\tau})^{\top}
and
{\bm{M}}_{\tau}

=({\bm{m}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{m}}_{n})^{\top}
is the unobserved

n\times\tau
membership assignment matrix with
{\bm{m}}_{i}=(m_{i1},\ldots,m_{i\tau})^{\top}
and
m_{ik}
being the indicator of whether
Y_{i}
is from the
k
th distribution
N(\mu_{k},\sigma_{k}^{2})
.
We denote the corresponding true values as
\tau_{0}
,
\bm{M}_{0}=({\bm{m}}^{(0)}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{m}}^{(0)}_{n})^{\top}

=

\big((m^{(0}_{11},\ldots,m^{(0)}_{1\tau})^{\top},\dots,(m^{(0)}_{n1},

\ldots,

m^{(0)}_{n\tau})^{\top}\big)^{\top}
,

{\bm{\mu}}_{0}=(\mu_{1}^{(0)},\ldots,\mu_{{}_{\tau_{0}}}^{(0)})^{\top}
,
\bm{\sigma}_{0}=(\sigma^{(0)}_{1},\dots,\sigma^{(0)}_{\tau_{0}})^{\top}
and
{\bm{\theta}}_{0}

=

(\tau_{0},\bm{M}_{0},{\bm{\mu}}_{0},

\bm{\sigma}_{0})^{\top}
, respectively. We are interest in making inference for the unknown

\tau_{0}
and also the location and scale parameters
\mu_{j}^{(0)}
and
\sigma_{j}^{(0)}
,
j=1,\ldots,\tau_{0}
.

We can re-express
Y_{i}
as
Y_{i}=\sum_{k=1}^{\tau}m_{ik}(\mu_{k}+\sigma_{k}U_{i})
, for
U_{i}\sim N(0,1)
. In a vector form,

\displaystyle{\bm{Y}}={\bm{M}}{\bm{\mu}}+\hbox{diag}(\bm{U})\bm{M}{\bm{\sigma}}={\bm{M}}{\bm{\mu}}+\hbox{diag}(\bm{M}{\bm{\sigma}})\bm{U},

(22)

where
{\bm{Y}}=(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n})^{\top}

and
\bm{U}=(U_{1},\ldots,U_{n})^{\top}
.
The realization of (
22
) with true
\bm{\theta}_{0}
is

{\bm{y}_{obs}}={\bm{M}}_{0}{\bm{\mu}}_{0}+\,\hbox{diag}({\bm{u}}^{rel}){\bm{M}}_{0}\bm{\sigma}_{0}.

An alternative
setup
Y_{i}\sim\sum_{k=1}^{\tau}p_{k}N(\mu_{k},\sigma_{k}^{2})
further assumes that
{\bm{m}}_{i}=(m_{i1},\ldots,m_{i\tau})^{\top}
is a random draw from multinomial distribution
\text{Multinomial}(\tau;p_{1},\ldots,

p_{\tau})
with unknown proportion parameters
(p_{1},\ldots,p_{\tau})
,
\sum_{k=1}^{\tau}p_{k}=1

(
Chen et al. 2012
, cf.,)
.
In this case,

\bm{M}_{0}
is the realized membership assignment
matrix that generates
\bm{y}_{obs}
.

It is known in the literature that there is an identifiability issue in a mixture model; i.e., multiple sets of parameters could possibly generate the same data
(
Liu and Shao 2003
)
. To account for this identifiability
issue,
we re-define the true parameters as those that correspond to the ones with the smallest
\tau
, i.e.,

\displaystyle(\tau_{0},\bm{M}_{0},{\bm{\mu}}_{0},\bm{\sigma}_{0}^{2})={\arg\min}_{\{(\tau,\bm{M}_{\tau},{\bm{\mu}}_{\tau},\bm{\sigma}^{2})|{\bm{M}}_{\tau}{\bm{\mu}}_{\tau}={\bm{M}}_{0}{\bm{\mu}}_{0},\,{\bm{M}}_{\tau}{\bm{\sigma}}_{\tau}={\bm{M}}_{0}{\bm{\sigma}}_{0}\}}\tau.

(23)

Here,
\bm{M}_{0}
and
{\bm{\mu}}_{0}
on the right-hand side are the true parameter values that generate
\bm{y}^{obs}.
With a slight abuse of notation, we still use the same notations on the left-hand side to denote the ones
with the smallest
\tau
.
We further assume that
(\tau_{0},\bm{M}_{0},{\bm{\mu}}_{0},\bm{\sigma}_{0}^{2})
defined in (
23
)
is unique and

0<\sigma_{\min}\leq\sigma_{\max}<\infty,

where
\sigma_{\min}=\|\bm{\sigma}_{0}\|_{0}
and
\sigma_{\max}=\|\bm{\sigma}_{0}\|_{\infty}.

Thus, among the
\tau_{0}
components, no
\sigma_{k}^{2}

can completely dominate another
\sigma_{j}^{2}
, for any
j\not=k
. Finally, the inference procedure we develop is for the set of parameters defined in (
23
).

Once again we remark
that the inference problems associated with the Gaussian mixture model are nontrivial. First, the unknown
\tau_{0}
is discrete and the question on how to quantify the uncertainty in estimating
\tau_{0}
is still not addressed. Second, the
sizes of the remaining
parameters vary for different values of
\tau
and there are both types:

(\bm{\mu}_{\tau},\bm{\sigma}_{\tau})
are continuous and

\bm{M}_{\tau}
are discrete and potentially high-dimensional. The questions on how to make inference for
\bm{\mu}_{0}
and
\bm{\sigma}_{0}
while accommodating the uncertainty in estimating
\tau_{0}
remain to be elusive as well.
In this Section, we address these open problems using the repro samples method.
In Section 4.1, we extend the approach introduced in Section
3.1
to get a performance-guaranteed confidence set of
\tau_{0}
.
In Section 4.2, we use a many-to-one mapping described in Section
3.2
to construct
a candidate set for
(\tau,\bm{M}_{\tau})
.
In Section 4.3, we follow the recommendation of Section
3.3
to obtain a confidence set for each component’s location and scale parameter, i.e.,

\mu_{j}^{(0)}
and
\sigma_{j}^{(0)}
,
j=1,\ldots,\tau_{0}
, respectively. Specific results tailored to the Gaussian mixture models are provided in each of the three subsections.

4.1

Confidence set for unknown number of components
\tau_{0}

To make inference for
\tau_{0}
, we need to handle the nuisance parameters
(\bm{M}_{\tau},\bm{\mu}_{\tau},\bm{\sigma}_{\tau}).
In the Gaussian mixture model, when given
(\tau,\bm{M}_{\tau})

which can be obtained by projecting
\bm{Y}
onto the space expanded by

(\bm{\mu}_{\tau},\bm{\sigma}^{2}_{\tau})
. In the first part of the subsection, we use the profiling method described
in Section
3.1
(letting
\eta=\tau
and
\bm{\beta}=\bm{M}_{\tau}
) to handle the nuisance parameters
\bm{M}_{\tau}
.

First, let us assume that we are able to obtain a nuclear mapping function
T_{a}(\bm{U},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)

and the corresponding Borel set
B_{\alpha}
that satisfies

3
.
Now, following the approach described in Section
3.1
,
for a sample data

,
we test whether it is from

,
where

Then, the (profile) nuclear mapping function for
\tau
is

\displaystyle T_{p}(\bm{u},\bm{\theta})=\widetilde{T}_{p}(\bm{y},\tau)=\min\nolimits_{\widetilde{\bm{M}}_{\tau}}\tilde{\nu}(\bm{y},\tau,\widetilde{\bm{M}_{\tau}})=\min\nolimits_{\widetilde{\bm{M}}_{\tau}}\inf\nolimits_{\alpha^{\prime}}\{\alpha^{\prime}:\widetilde{T}(\bm{y},(\tau,\widetilde{\bm{M}_{\tau}}))\in B_{\alpha^{\prime}}\}.

(24)

Following Lemma
1
and (

),
we construct a confidence set for

\displaystyle\Xi_{\alpha}(\bm{y}_{obs})

\displaystyle=\big\{\tau:\exists\bm{u}^{*}\mbox{ and }(\bm{\mu}_{\tau},\bm{\sigma}_{\tau},\bm{M}_{\tau})\mbox{ s.t. }\bm{y}_{obs}=\bm{M}_{\tau}\bm{\mu}_{\tau}+\hbox{diag}(\bm{M}_{\tau}{\bm{\sigma}_{\tau}})\bm{u}^{*},T_{p}(\bm{u}^{*},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)\leq\alpha\big\}

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt\bm{y}^{*}=\bm{M}_{\tau}\bm{\mu}_{\tau}+\hbox{diag}(\bm{M}_{\tau}{\bm{\sigma}_{\tau}})\bm{u}^{*}\big\}

\displaystyle=\resizebox{23791065}{}{$\big\{\tau:\exists\bm{u}^{*}\mbox{ and }(\bm{\mu}_{\tau},\bm{\sigma}_{\tau},\bm{M}_{\tau})\mbox{ s.t. }\bm{y}_{obs}=\bm{M}_{\tau}\bm{\mu}_{\tau}+\hbox{diag}(\bm{M}_{\tau}{\bm{\sigma}_{\tau}})\bm{u}^{*},\widetilde{T}_{p}(\bm{y}_{obs},\tau)\leq\alpha\big\}$}

The second equality follows from (

), and the fourth equality
holds since we can always find a

that matches

for any

by setting

below states that

Corollary 3
.

Let
T_{a}(\bm{u},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)=\widetilde{T}(\bm{y},(\tau,{\bm{M}_{\tau}}))
for
\bm{y}=\bm{M}_{\tau}\bm{\mu}+\hbox{diag}(\bm{M}_{\tau}{\bm{\sigma}})\bm{u}
, for a set of fixed
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits=(\tau,{\bm{M}_{\tau}},\bm{\mu},\bm{\sigma})
. Suppose
\mathbb{P}(T_{a}(\bm{U},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)\in B_{\alpha})\geq\alpha
for some Borel set
B_{\alpha}
for any given
\bm{\theta}.
If
\widetilde{T}_{p}(\bm{y},\tau)
is defined following from (
24
), then
\Xi_{\alpha}(\bm{y}_{obs})
in (
25
) is a level
\alpha
confidence set for
\tau_{0}
, i.e.
\mathbb{P}(\tau_{0}\in\Xi_{\alpha}(\bm{Y}))\geq\alpha
for a random sample
\bm{Y}
that is generated with the set
\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{0}=(\tau_{0},{\bm{M}_{0}},\bm{\mu}_{0},\bm{\sigma}_{0})
.

We now turn to the task of finding a nuclear mapping
T_{a}(\bm{u},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)=\widetilde{T}(\bm{y},(\tau,{\bm{M}_{\tau}}))
with
\bm{y}=

\bm{M}_{\tau}\bm{\mu}+\hbox{diag}(\bm{M}_{\tau}{\bm{\sigma}})\bm{u}
and a Borel set
B_{\alpha}
such that
\mathbb{P}(T_{a}(\bm{U},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)\in B_{\alpha})\geq\alpha
, for any given
\bm{\theta}.

To do so, let
\hat{\tau}(\bm{y})
be an
estimator of
\tau
;
e.g., we use in our paper a BIC-based estimator

\displaystyle\hat{\tau}(\bm{y})

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{\tau\in{\it\Upsilon}}\left[\left\{-2\max\nolimits_{(\tau,\bm{M}_{\tau},{\bm{\mu}},\bm{\sigma})}\log\ell(\bm{M}_{\tau},{\bm{\mu}},\bm{\sigma}|\bm{y})\right\}+2\tau\log(n)\right],

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{\tau\in{\it\Upsilon}}\min_{\bm{M}_{\tau}}\left\{2\sum\nolimits_{k=1}^{\tau}n_{k}\log\left(\|(I-\bm{H}_{\tau}^{(k)})\bm{y}^{(k)}\|\right)-\sum\nolimits_{k=1}^{\tau}n_{k}\log n_{k}+2\tau\log(n)\right\},

where

\ell(\tau,\bm{M}_{\tau},{\bm{\mu}},\bm{\sigma}|\bm{y})=\sum\nolimits_{k=1}^{\tau}\{-n_{k}\log(\sigma^{(k)})-\frac{1}{2(\sigma^{(k)})^{2}}\|\bm{y}^{(k)}-\mu^{(k)}\|^{2}\}
is the log-likelihood function of
(\tau,\bm{M}_{\tau},{\bm{\mu}},\bm{\sigma})
given
\bm{y}
. Here,
\bm{H}_{\tau}^{(k)}=\bm{H}_{\tau}[\mathcal{I}_{k},\mathcal{I}_{k}]
is the submatrix of
\bm{H}_{\tau}={\bm{M}}_{\tau}({\bm{M}}_{\tau}^{\top}{\bm{M}}_{\tau})^{-1}{\bm{M}}_{\tau}^{\top}
corresponding to the
k
th component, and
{\bm{y}}^{(k)}=\bm{y}[\mathcal{I}_{k}]
is the corresponding subvector of
\bm{y}
, where
\mathcal{I}_{k}=\{i:m_{ik}=1\}
is the index set for component
k
.
In the next paragraph, we define our nuclear mapping function
T_{a}(\bm{u},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)=\widetilde{T}(\bm{y},(\tau,{\bm{M}_{\tau}}))
through this point estimator
{\hat{\tau}}(\bm{Y})
for a given
(\tau,\bm{M}_{\tau})
. At the same time, we take care of the complication of the unknown nuisance parameters
(\bm{\mu}_{\tau},\bm{\sigma}_{\tau})
.

Suppose temporally that we can compute
P_{{\bm{Y}}}(\bar{\tau})=\mathbb{P}_{{\bm{Y}}}\{\hat{\tau}({\bm{Y}})=\bar{\tau}\}
,
for any
\bar{\tau}\in{\cal T}
, where
\mathbb{P}_{{\bm{Y}}}\{\cdot\}

refers the probability of the random sample
\bm{Y}
that is generated using
\bm{U}
and the set of fixed
\bm{\theta}=(\tau,{\bm{M}_{\tau}},\bm{\mu},\bm{\sigma})
.
Then, following the practice and idea of defining a
p
-value, we can assess how ‘extreme’ the realized
\hat{\tau}({\bm{y}})
is,
i.e.,

{\cal P}(\bm{y})=\sum_{\{\bar{\tau}:\,P_{{\bm{Y}}}(\bar{\tau})>P_{{\bm{Y}}}(\hat{\tau}(\bm{y}))\}}P_{{\bm{Y}}}(\bar{\tau}).

We expect the distribution of
{\cal P}(\bm{Y})
is closely related to
U(0,1)
. A complication is that the nuisance parameters
(\bm{\mu}_{\tau},\bm{\sigma}_{\tau})
are unknown, we cannot directly compute the distribution
P_{{\bm{Y}}}(\bar{\tau})
or simulate
\bm{Y}=\bm{M}_{\tau}\bm{\mu}_{\tau}+\hbox{diag}(\bm{M}_{\tau}{\bm{\sigma}_{\tau}})\bm{U}
when only given
(\tau,\bm{M}_{\tau})
.
However, when
(\tau,\bm{M}_{\tau})
is given, we have sufficient statistics
\big(\mathbf{a}_{\tau}(\bm{y}),

\mathbf{b}_{\tau}(\bm{y})\big)

for the unknown
(\bm{\mu}_{\tau},\bm{\sigma}_{\tau})
, where
\mathbf{a}_{\tau}(\bm{y})=(\bm{M}_{\tau}^{\top}\bm{M}_{\tau})^{-1}\bm{M}_{\tau}^{\top}\bm{y}
and
\mathbf{b}_{\tau}(\bm{y})=\big(\|(I-\bm{H}_{\tau}^{(1)})\bm{y}^{(1)}\|,

\cdots,\|(I-\bm{H}_{\tau}^{(\tau)})\bm{y}^{(\tau)}\|\big)^{T}
are their point estimators with an observed sample set
\bm{y}
. Thus, we can generate a repro sample
\bm{Y}^{\prime}
using
(\tau,M_{\tau},\mathbf{a}_{\tau}(\bm{y}),\mathbf{b}_{\tau}(\bm{y}))
instead. Specifically, we simulate an independent copy
{\bm{U}}^{\prime}
from the distribution of
\bm{U}

and let
{\bm{Y}}^{\prime}=\bm{M}_{\tau}\mathbf{a}_{\tau}(\bm{y})+\hbox{diag}\left\{\bm{M}_{\tau}\mathbf{b}_{\tau}(\bm{y})\right\}\mathbf{c}_{\tau}({\bm{U}^{\prime}})
with
\mathbf{c}_{\tau}(\bm{U}^{\prime})=\big(\frac{\{(I-\bm{H}_{\tau}^{(1)})\bm{U}^{\prime(1)}\}^{T}}{\|(I-\bm{H}_{\tau}^{(1)})\bm{U}^{\prime(1)}\|},\cdots,

\frac{\{(I-\bm{H}_{\tau}^{(\tau)})\bm{U}^{\prime(\tau)}\}^{T}}{\|(I-\bm{H}_{\tau}^{(\tau)})\bm{U}^{\prime(\tau)}\|}\big)^{T}
and
\bm{U}^{\prime(k)}=\bm{U}^{\prime}[\mathcal{I}_{k}].

We define our nuclear mapping function
T_{a}(\bm{u},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
as

\displaystyle T_{a}(\bm{u},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)=\widetilde{T}(\bm{y},(\tau,{\bm{M}_{\tau}}))=\sum\nolimits_{\{\bar{\tau}:P_{{\bm{Y}}^{\prime}}(\bar{\tau})>P_{{\bm{Y}}^{\prime}}(\hat{\tau}(\bm{y}))\}}P_{{\bm{Y}}^{\prime}}(\bar{\tau}).

(26)

Since
\mathbf{c}_{\tau}({\bm{U}^{\prime}})\sim\mathbf{c}_{\tau}(\bm{U})
and
\mathbf{c}_{\tau}(\bm{U})
is independent of
(\mathbf{a}_{\tau}(\bm{Y}),\mathbf{b}_{\tau}(\bm{Y})),
the repro samples

{\bm{Y}}^{\prime}
and
{\bm{Y}}

have the same conditional distribution

{\bm{Y}}^{\prime}\big|\{\mathbf{a}_{\tau}(\bm{Y}^{\prime}),\mathbf{b}_{\tau}(\bm{Y}^{\prime})\}=\{\mathbf{a}_{\tau}(\bm{y}),\mathbf{b}_{\tau}(\bm{y})\}\sim\bm{Y}\big|\{\mathbf{a}_{\tau}(\bm{Y})\mathbf{b}_{\tau}(\bm{Y})\}=\{\mathbf{a}_{\tau}(\bm{y}),\mathbf{b}_{\tau}(\bm{y})\}.

Based on this fact, we can prove the theorem below; A proof is provided in Appendix A.

Theorem 5
.

, Given a set of
\bm{\theta}
and
T_{a}(\bm{u},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
defined in (
26
), we have

\mathbb{P}(T_{a}(\bm{U},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)\leq\alpha)\geq\alpha.

By Theorem
5
and (
26
),

\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{T}(\bm{Y},(\tau,{\bm{M}_{\tau}}))\in B_{\alpha})\geq\alpha
, for
B_{\alpha}=[0,\alpha].

It follows that the function

T_{p}(\bm{u},\bm{\theta})

in (
24
) can be simplified to be
T_{p}(\bm{u},\bm{\theta})=\min_{\widetilde{\bm{M}}_{\tau}}

\inf_{\alpha^{\prime}}

\{\alpha^{\prime}:\widetilde{T}(\bm{y},(\tau,\widetilde{\bm{M}_{\tau}}))\leq\alpha^{\prime}\}=\min_{\widetilde{\bm{M}}_{\tau}}\widetilde{T}(\bm{y},

(\tau,\widetilde{\bm{M}_{\tau}})).

Thus, the confidence set in (

) becomes

\Xi_{\alpha}(\bm{y}_{obs})=\big\{\tau:\min\nolimits_{{}_{{\bm{M}}_{\tau}}}\widetilde{T}(\bm{y}_{obs},(\tau,{\bm{M}_{\tau}}))\leq\alpha\big\}.

(27)

To obtain (
27
), it only
suffices to compute

for any given

We propose to use a Monte-Carlo method to compute

and collect them to form a set

. For each

\bm{y}^{s}={\bm{M}}_{\tau}\mathbf{a}_{\tau}(\bm{y}_{obs})+\hbox{diag}\{{\bm{M}}_{\tau}\mathbf{b}_{\tau}(\bm{y}_{obs})\}\mathbf{c}_{\tau}(\bm{u}^{s})

and

. Then

is
\widehat{P}_{\mathcal{V}}(\bar{\tau})=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{V}|}\sum_{\bm{u}^{s}\in\mathcal{V}}

I(\hat{\tau}(\bm{y}^{s})=\bar{\tau})
, where

{\bm{Y}}^{\prime}=\bm{M}_{\tau}\mathbf{a}_{\tau}(\bm{y}_{obs})

for

. It follows that
when

is large enough, we can approximate the nuclear statistic by

Theorem 6
.

Suppose
n-\tau_{0}>4.
Let the distance metric between
\bm{U}
and
\bm{U}^{*}
be
\rho(\bm{U},\bm{U}^{*})=\frac{\bm{U}^{T}\bm{U}^{*}}{\|\bm{U}\|\|\bm{U}^{*}\|},
then for any arbitrarily small
\delta>0,
there exist
\gamma_{\delta}>0,c>0
and also an interval of positive width
\Lambda_{\delta}=[\frac{\gamma_{\delta}^{1.5}}{\log(n)/n},

\frac{\log\{0.5C^{2}_{\min}\sigma_{\max}\gamma_{\delta}/\sigma_{\min}+1\}}{2\tau_{0}\log(n)/n}]
such that when
\lambda\in\Lambda_{\delta},

aa

(a)
\mathbb{P}_{\bm{U}|{\cal V}}\{(\tau_{0},\bm{M}_{0})\in\widehat{\it\Upsilon}_{{\cal V}}(\bm{Y})\}

\geq 1-\delta-o_{p}(e^{-c|\mathcal{V}|})
, and

aa
(b)
\mathbb{P}_{\bm{U}|{\cal V}}\{\tau_{0}\in\Xi^{\prime}_{\alpha}(\bm{Y})\}\geq\alpha-\delta-o_{p}(e^{-c|\mathcal{V}|}).

Here, a random sample
\bm{Y}
that is generated with the set
\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{0}=(\tau_{0},{\bm{M}_{0}},\bm{\mu}_{0},\bm{\sigma}_{0})
.
Also,
\Xi^{\prime}_{\alpha}(\cdot)
is defined in (
30
) and
C_{\min}={\min}_{\small\{\bm{M}_{\tau}:\,|\tau|\leq|\tau_{0}|,\tau\neq\tau_{0}\}}\|(\bm{I}-\bm{M}_{\tau})\bm{M}_{0}\bm{\mu}_{0}\|>0
.

Theorem
6
states that we can recover both
\tau_{0}
and
\bm{M}_{0}
with a high certainty, if we have an unlimited computing resource that allows
|\mathcal{V}|
going to infinity. Nevertheless, getting each element in
\bm{M}_{0}
correctly is a much harder task, requiring a larger
|\mathcal{V}|
, than the task of recovering
\tau_{0}
. This is because a repro sample
\bm{y}^{s}
generated by a wrong
{\bm{M}}_{\tau}
can very well produce the same estimate of the number of components as that by using
\bm{M}_{0}
, if
{\bm{M}}_{\tau}
is not too different from
\bm{M}_{0}
.
In practice,
our computing power is limited so we focus only the task of making inference for
\tau_{0}
. Our numerical studies in Section
5
suggest that the confidence set for
\tau_{0}
in (
30
) can often achieve its desirable coverage rate empirically.

4.3

Inference for cluster mean
\mu_{k}^{(0)}
and standard deviation
\sigma_{k}^{(0)}

The repro samples method also offers further developments and a novel approach to make inference for the location and scale parameters
\bm{\mu}_{0}
and
\bm{\sigma}_{0}
with an unknown
\tau_{0}.
To our knowledge, this inference problem is not fully addressed even when
\tau_{0}
is known, let alone the situation when
\tau_{0}
is unknown.
We face several
challenges for the inference problem.
First, the lengths of the parameters
{\bm{\mu}}_{\tau}
and
\bm{\sigma}_{\tau}
vary with
\tau
. When
\tau\not=\tau_{0}
, the elements of the vectors
{\bm{\mu}}_{\tau}
and
{\bm{\sigma}}_{\tau}
do not necessarily lineup with those of the target parameters
{\bm{\mu}}_{0}
and
\bm{\sigma}_{0}
at all.
Thus, to make an inference for
\bm{\mu}_{0}
or
\bm{\sigma}_{0}
when
\tau_{0}
is unknown, we need to consider it jointly with the unknown discrete parameter
\tau_{0}
. Furthermore, even if
\tau_{0}
is given, the membership assignment matrix
\bm{M}_{0}
is a set of discrete nuisance parameters and this matrix often cannot be fully well estimated, especially for those at the overlapping tails of two neighboring components.
In this subsection,
we provide an inference method under the repro samples framework to address these problems.
To simplify our presentation, our development focuses only on making inference
for each mixture component’s mean
\mu_{k}^{(0)}
and standard deviation
\sigma_{k}^{(0)}
. Or, more specifically, we make a joint inference for each
(\tau_{0},\mu_{k}^{(0)})
and
(\tau_{0},\sigma_{k}^{(0)})
, for
k=1\dots\tau_{0}
. Other inference problems concerning the location and scale parameters
\bm{\mu}_{0}
and
\bm{\sigma}_{0}
can be handled in a similar fashion.

At the outset, we follow the three-step procedure proposed in Section
3.3
to tackle the problem. Here, the first step is already done in Sections
4.1
and
4.2
, so we directly get into Steps 2 and 3. Specifically, for each unique
(\tau^{*},\bm{M}^{*})
in the candidate set
\widehat{\it\Upsilon}_{{\cal V}}(\bm{y}_{obs})
, we first obtain a level-
\alpha
representative interval for
\mu_{k}
and
\sigma_{k}
, say,
(a_{k}^{M^{*}},b_{k}^{M^{*}})
and
(q_{k}^{M^{*}},r_{k}^{M^{*}})
, for
k=1,\ldots,\tau^{*}
, respectively.
We then follow the discussion in Section
3.3
and construct a confidence set for
(\tau_{0},\mu_{k}^{(0)})
and
(\tau^{0},\sigma_{k}^{(0)})
, respectively,

\Xi^{(\mu_{k})}(\bm{y}_{obs})=\bigcup\nolimits_{\tau^{*}\in\widehat{\it\Upsilon}^{(\tau)}_{{\cal V}}(\bm{y}_{obs})}\{\tau^{*}\}\times(a^{*}_{k},b^{*}_{k})\,\,\hbox{and}\,\,\Xi^{(\sigma_{k})}(\bm{y}_{obs})=\bigcup\nolimits_{\tau^{*}\in\widehat{\it\Upsilon}^{(\tau)}_{{\cal V}}(\bm{y}_{obs})}\{\tau^{*}\}\times(q^{*}_{k},r^{*}_{k}),

where
(a_{k}^{*},b_{k}^{*})
=
\big(\inf_{\{\bm{M}^{*}:(\tau^{*},\bm{M}^{*})\in\widehat{\it\Upsilon}_{{\cal V}}(\bm{y}_{obs})\}}a_{k}^{M^{*}},\allowbreak\sup_{\{\bm{M}^{*}:(\tau^{*},\bm{M}^{*})\in\widehat{\it\Upsilon}_{{\cal V}}(\bm{y}_{obs})\}}b_{k}^{M^{*}}\big)
,
(q_{k}^{*},r_{k}^{*})
=
\big(\inf_{\{\bm{M}^{*}:}

{}_{(\tau^{*},\bm{M}^{*})\in\widehat{\it\Upsilon}_{{\cal V}}(\bm{y}_{obs})\}}q_{k}^{M^{*}},\,\sup_{\{\bm{M}^{*}:(\tau^{*},\bm{M}^{*})\in\widehat{\it\Upsilon}_{{\cal V}}(\bm{y}_{obs})\}}r_{k}^{M^{*}}\big)
,
and
\widehat{\it\Upsilon}^{(\tau)}_{{\cal V}}(\bm{y}_{obs})
=
\{\tau^{*}:(\tau^{*},\bm{M}^{*})\in\widehat{\it\Upsilon}_{{\cal V}}(\bm{y}_{obs})

\mbox{for some }\bm{M}^{*}\}
is the collection of unique
\tau^{*}
’s in
\widehat{\it\Upsilon}_{{\cal V}}(\bm{y}_{obs}).

From Corollary
2
in Section
3.3
, if

(a_{k}^{*},b_{k}^{*})
is a valid level-
\alpha
confidence set for
\mu_{k}^{(0)}
when
\tau^{*}=\tau_{0}
,
then
\Xi^{(\mu_{k})}(\bm{y}_{obs})
is a level-
\alpha
confidence set for
(\tau_{0},\mu_{k}^{(0)}).
Similarly,
\Xi^{(\sigma_{k})}(\bm{y}_{obs})
is a level-
\alpha
confidence set for
(\tau^{0},\sigma_{k}^{(0)})
.

The remaining task is to obtain the representative sets
(a_{k}^{M^{*}},b_{k}^{M^{*}})
and
(q_{k}^{M^{*}},r_{k}^{M^{*}})
, given
(\tau^{*},\bm{M}^{*})\in\widehat{\it\Upsilon}_{{\cal V}}(\bm{y}_{obs}).

If one set of these
(a_{k}^{M^{*}},b_{k}^{M^{*}})
and
(q_{k}^{M^{*}},r_{k}^{M^{*})})
cover
\mu_{k}^{(0)}
and
\sigma_{k}^{(0)}
with probability
\alpha,

then
(a^{*}_{k},b^{*}_{k})
and
(q_{k}^{*},r_{k}^{*})
are valid confidence sets for
\mu_{k}^{(0)}
and
\sigma_{k}^{(0)}
respectively when
\tau^{*}=\tau_{0}.

By Theorem
4
, with enough computing power allowing
|{\cal V}|\to\infty
, we can always capture
(\tau_{0},\bm{M}_{0})
in the candidate set
\widehat{\it\Upsilon}_{{\cal V}}(\bm{y}_{obs})
thus
(a^{*}_{k},b^{*}_{k})
and
(q_{k}^{*},r_{k}^{*})
are valid confidence sets. In practice with limited computing resources, we can well recover
\tau_{0}
in the candidate set
\widehat{\it\Upsilon}_{{\cal V}}(\bm{y}_{obs})
(as evidenced in our empirical study in Section
5
) but it might be hard to exactly recover
\bm{M}_{0}
.
To counter this issue,
we propose a robust version of
(a_{k}^{M^{*}},b_{k}^{M^{*}})
and
(q_{k}^{M^{*}},r_{k}^{M^{*}})
that only requires
\bm{M}^{*}
to be reasonably close to
\bm{M}_{0}
for
(a_{k}^{M^{*}},b_{k}^{M^{*}})

to cover
\mu_{k}^{(0)}
and
(q_{k}^{M^{*}},r_{k}^{M^{*}})
to cover
\sigma_{k}^{(0)}
with the desirable rate.
There are many
\bm{M}^{*}
’s that are close to
\bm{M}_{0}
, so in practice we can use a far smaller
\mathcal{V}
to achieve the desirable coverage rate.

Specifically, for a given
(\tau^{*},\bm{M}^{*})\in\widehat{\it\Upsilon}_{{\cal V}}(\bm{y}_{obs})
, let
{\cal D}_{k}^{*}=\{y_{i}:m^{*}_{ik}=1,i=1,\dots,n_{k}\}
be the data points assigned to the
k
component,
1\leq k\leq\tau^{*}
.
We may use the classical sign test for the population median to get a representative set for the
k
th component’s
location parameter
\mu_{k}
; It happens to be equivalent to a special case of the repro sample approach developed for nonparametric quantile inference in Example B1-B2
of Appendix B:

(a_{k}^{\bm{M}^{*}},b_{k}^{\bm{M}^{*}})=\big\{\mu:a_{L}(.5)\leq\sum\nolimits_{y_{i}\in{\cal D}_{k}^{*}}I(y_{i}\leq\mu)\leq a_{U}(.5)\big\}.

Here,
a_{L}(.5)
and
a_{U}(.5)
are level-
\alpha
lower and upper bounds defined in Example
1
with
r=|{\cal D}_{k}^{*}|
and the quantile
\zeta=\frac{1}{2}
.
Further details and discussions are provided
Appendix B.

Under the assumption that

y_{i}\in{\cal D}_{k}^{*}
are from
N(\mu_{k},\sigma_{k}^{2})
,
a consistent robust estimator for the scale parameter
\sigma_{k}
is
\hat{\sigma}_{k}={\it MAD}/\Psi^{-1}(.5)
, where the median absolute deviation
{\it MAD}=

{\it med}\{|y_{i}-M_{k}|:y_{i}\in{\cal D}_{k}^{*}\}
,
M_{k}={\it med}\{y_{i}:y_{i}\in{\cal D}_{k}^{*}\}
and
\Psi(t)=P(|Z_{j}-M_{z}|<t)
is the cumulative distribution function of
|Z_{j}-M_{z}|
,
j=1,\ldots,|{\cal D}_{k}^{*}|
. Here,

Z_{j}\overset{iid}{\sim}N(0,1)
and
M_{z}=

{\it med}\{Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{|{\cal D}_{k}^{*}|}\}
.
In the form of generalized data generating equation (
8
), we have

\sum\nolimits_{y_{i}\in{\cal D}_{k}^{*}}I\{|y_{i}-M_{k}|\leq{\small\Psi^{-1}(.5)}\sigma\}-\sum\nolimits_{j=1}^{|{\cal D}_{k}^{*}|}U^{\prime}_{j}=0,

where
U^{\prime}_{j}=I\{|Z_{j}-M_{z}|\leq{\small\Psi^{-1}(.5)}\}
are dependent Bernoulli
(\frac{1}{2})
variables. Since the distribution of
\sum\nolimits_{j=1}^{|{\cal D}_{k}^{*}|}U^{\prime}_{j}
can be simulated, we can get
a Borel interval
B_{\alpha}=[a^{\prime}_{L},a^{\prime}_{U}]
,
with

(a_{L}^{\prime},

a_{U}^{\prime})=\arg\min_{\{(l,r):P(l<\sum\nolimits_{j=1}^{|{\cal D}_{k}^{*}|}U^{\prime}_{j}<r)\geq\alpha\}}|r-l|
. Then, given
(\tau^{*},\bm{M}^{*})
, a representative set of
\sigma_{k}
is:

(q_{k}^{\bm{M}^{*}},r_{k}^{\bm{M}^{*}})=\big\{\sigma_{k}:a^{\prime}_{L}\leq\sum\nolimits_{y_{i}\in{\cal D}_{k}^{*}}I(|y_{i}-M_{k}|\leq{\small\Psi^{-1}(.5))}\sigma_{k})\leq a^{\prime}_{U}\big\}.

Further details are provided in Appendix B.

Numerical studies: Real and simulated data

5.1

Real data analysis: red blood cell SLC data

The data set consists of red blood cell sodium-lithium countertransport (SLC) activity measurements from 190 individuals
(
Dudley et al. 1991
)
.
The SLC measurement is known to be correlated with blood pressure, and considered as an essential cause of hypertension by some researchers.

Roeder 1994
and
Chen et al. 2012
analyzed the same SLC data set
using a Gaussian mixture model and tested the unknown number of components
H_{0}:\tau_{0}=k

Roeder 1994
concluded that

\sigma_{j}^{2}

Chen et al. 2012
concluded instead the smallest

We use the repro samples approach developed in Section

to re-analyze the SLC data and and provide

We first obtain a
95% confidence set for the discrete unknown number of components
\tau_{0}
.
To do so we first obtain a candidate a candidate set for
(\tau_{0},\bm{M}_{0})

(
28

as the covariate using the R package flexmix
(
Grün and Leisch 2007
)

30

The
\tau_{0}

that both hypothesis
\tau_{0}=2
and
\tau_{0}=3
are plausible. In genetics, a two-component mixture distribution corresponds to a simple dominance model and a three-component corresponds to an additive model

An advantage of our proposed method is that

or

.

The simulation study can further demonstrate the importance of having a confidence set for
\tau_{0}
, as evidenced by the inferior performance of point estimations. For example, using the same simulated data, the classical BIC method recovers the true value of

(out of 200 repetitions) when the true
\tau_{0}=4.
The results are not surprising, since with a strong penalty term the BIC point estimator is often biased towards smaller
\tau
values in practice, even though the BIC estimator is known to be a consistent estimator.
These two extremely low rates of correctly estimating the true
\tau_{0}
highlight the challenge of the traditional point estimation method in our simulation examples, and more generally the risk of using just a point estimation in a Gaussian mixture model. Indeed, this result highlights the critical need to quantify the estimation uncertainty through a confidence set.

Additionally, we also illustrate the advantage of our proposed confidence set over the existing one-sided hypothesis testing approaches in
Roeder 1994
and
Chen et al. 2012
, both of which
advocate the use of the smallest
\tau_{0}
that can not be rejected.

\tau_{0}=3
and we perform the PLR test of

versus

\tau_{0}=4
and we perform the PLR test on
H_{0}:\tau_{0}=3
versus
H_{1}:\tau_{0}=4
,
we fail 93% times to reject
H_{0}:\tau_{0}=3
. These results suggest that most of the time, the PLR test prefers a wrong
\tau
over the true one. Furthermore, inverting either of the one-sided tests does not produce an as useful confidence set, since the upper bound of the set is
\infty
. In conclusion, the proposed confidence set by the repro samples method is a more intact approach to conducting inference on
\tau_{0}
compared to the one that uses only a hypothesis testing method.

Nuclear mapping by a test statistic and connection to classical Neyman-Pearson method

In a classical Neyman-Pearson testing problem
H_{0}:\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{0}=\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits
versus
H_{1}:\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{0}\not=\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits
, we often construct a test statistic, say
\widetilde{T}(\bm{y}_{obs},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
, and assume that we know the distribution of
\widetilde{T}(\bm{Y},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
for
\bm{Y}=G(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits,\bm{U})
generated under
H_{0}:\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{0}=\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits
.
Under the classical framework, we can derive
a level-
\alpha
acceptance region
A_{\alpha}(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)=\big\{\bm{y}_{obs}\big|\widetilde{T}(\bm{y}_{obs},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)\in B_{\alpha}(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)\big\}
, where the set
B_{\alpha}(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
satisfies
\mathbb{P}\big\{\widetilde{T}(\bm{Y},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)\in B_{\alpha}(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)\big\}\geq\alpha
. Then, by the property of test duality (i.e., the so-called Neyman Inversion method), a level-
\alpha
confidence set is

\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\alpha}(\bm{y}_{obs})=\left\{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits:\bm{y}_{obs}\in A_{\alpha}(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)\right\}=\left\{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits:\widetilde{T}(\bm{y}_{obs},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)\in B_{\alpha}(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)\right\}.

Now suppose our nuclear mapping
T(\bm{u},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)

for a potential
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits
is defined through
this test statistic by the formula below:

T(\bm{U},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)=\widetilde{T}(\bm{Y},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)=\widetilde{T}(G({\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits},\bm{U}),\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits),

(31)

where
\bm{Y}=G(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits,\bm{U})
using the same
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits
. Since
\mathbb{P}\left\{T(\bm{U},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)\in B_{\alpha}(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)\right\}=\mathbb{P}\big\{\widetilde{T}(\bm{Y},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)\in B_{\alpha}(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)\big\}

\geq\alpha
for the same
B_{\alpha}(\cdot)
, a level-
\alpha
confidence set of (
4
) by the repro samples method is

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\alpha}(\bm{y}_{obs})=\left\{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits:\exists\bm{u}^{*}\in{\cal U}\mbox{ s.t. }\bm{y}_{obs}=G({\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits},\bm{u}^{*}),\widetilde{T}(G({\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits},\bm{u}^{*}),\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)\in B_{\alpha}(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)\right\}.

Lemma
4
below suggests that this
\Gamma_{\alpha}(\bm{y}_{obs})
is always smaller than or equal to the confidence set
\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\alpha}(\bm{y}_{obs})
by the Neyman method; i.e.,

\Gamma_{\alpha}(\bm{y}_{obs})\subseteq\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\alpha}(\bm{y}_{obs})
.
See Appendix A for a proof.

Lemma 4
.

If the nuclear mapping function is defined through a test statistic as in (
31
), then we have
\Gamma_{\alpha}(\bm{y}_{obs})\subseteq\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\alpha}(\bm{y}_{obs})
. The two sets are equal

\Gamma_{\alpha}(\bm{y}_{obs})=\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\alpha}(\bm{y}_{obs})
, when
\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\alpha}(\bm{y}_{obs})\subseteq\big\{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits:\bm{y}_{obs}=G({\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits},\bm{u}^{*}),\exists\,\bm{u}^{*}\in{\cal U}\big\}
.

We note that it is possible that
\Gamma_{\alpha}(\bm{y}_{obs})
is strictly smaller than

\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\alpha}(\bm{y}_{obs})
; i.e.,
\Gamma_{\alpha}(\bm{y}_{obs})\subsetneq\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\alpha}(\bm{y}_{obs})
. The following is such an example where
\Gamma_{\alpha}(\bm{y}_{obs})
is strictly smaller than

\widetilde{\Gamma}_{\alpha}(\bm{y}_{obs})
.

Example 2
.

Suppose
\bm{y}_{obs}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})^{\top}
is a set of samples from the model

Y_{i}=\theta_{0}+U_{i},\qquad U_{i}\sim U(-1,1),\quad i=1,\ldots,n.

Here,
\Theta=(-\infty,+\infty)
,
{\cal Y}=(-\infty,\infty)
.
An unbiased point (and also
\sqrt{n}
-consistent) estimator
is
\bar{Y}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}
. Suppose we
use the test statistic
\widetilde{T}(\bm{y}_{obs},\theta)=

\bar{y}-\theta
. Since
n\{(\bar{Y}-\theta)

+1\}/2=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{U_{i}+1}{2}
follows an Irwin-Hall distribution when
\theta
is the true value
\theta_{0}
,
by the classical testing method a level-
95\%
confidence interval is
\widetilde{\Gamma}_{.95}(\bm{y}_{obs})=(\bar{y}-\frac{2}{n}q_{.975}+1,\bar{y}+\frac{2}{n}q_{.975}-1)
. Here,
q_{.975}
is the

quantile of the Irwin-Hall distribution.

\Gamma_{.95}(\bm{y}_{obs})=\big\{\theta:y_{i}=\theta+u_{i}^{*},i=1,\ldots,n;\,\bar{y}\in(\theta-\frac{2}{n}q_{.975}+1,\theta+\frac{2}{n}q_{.975}-1);\exists\bm{u}^{*}\in(-1,1)^{n}\big\}=\left\{\theta:\theta\in\cap_{i=1}^{n}(y_{i}-1,y_{i}+1)\right\}\cap\widetilde{\Gamma}_{.95}(\bm{y}_{obs})

As an illustration, consider the case

We note that a nuclear mapping function does not need to be a test statistic, but
if it is defined through a test statistics as in (

\Gamma(\bm{y}_{obs})
by the repro samples method is more desirable, since by Lemma

\Gamma(\bm{y}_{obs})

Corollary 4
.

(a) If the test statistic
\widetilde{T}(\bm{y}_{obs},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)

\widetilde{\Gamma}(\bm{y}_{obs})
is a level-

\Gamma(\bm{y}_{obs})
by the corresponding repro sample method is also a level-
\alpha

(b) If the test statistic
\widetilde{T}(\bm{y}_{obs},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
corresponds to the uniformly most powerful unbiased test and
\widetilde{\Gamma}(\bm{y}_{obs})
is a level-
\alpha

\Gamma(\bm{y}_{obs})
by the corresponding repro sample method is also a level-
\alpha
UMA unbiased confidence set.

We note that the repro samples method is more general and flexible than the often-recommended likelihood inference approach. Typical hypothesis testing framework often involves finding a pivotal quantity and its distribution, and the most commonly used pivotal quantity is perhaps the likelihood ratio test statistics (LRT)
(
Reid and Cox 2015
, cf.,)
.
It is well known that the negative two log-LRT statistic is asymptotically
\chi^{2}
-distributed and a test developed based on the LRT statistic is asymptotically efficient
(
Casella and Berger 1990
, p381)
under some regularity
conditions. When these regularity conditions hold, one can define the nuclear mapping function through the LRT statistic and our repro samples method enjoys the same large sample optimality. When
these regularity conditions do not hold and the chi-square approximation fails, the repro samples method can achieve improved performance over the LRT-based inference, even though by the Neyman-Pearson Lemma the LRT is uniformly most powerful for a simple-versus-simple test. Below is one such example.

Concluding remarks and future research

This paper develops a repro samples method to provide an innovative, flexible, and efficient framework for making inferences for a broad range of problems, including many previously challenging open problems.
The proposed framework utilizes (explicitly or implicitly) artificially generated samples that mimic the observed data to quantify inference uncertainty, and we provide both finite and approximate (large sample) theories to support the framework.
Further developments and discussions cover topics such as the effective handling of nuisance parameters, the use of a data-driven candidate set to enhance computing efficiency, and examining
the relationship and advantages over the classical hypothesis testing procedures, along with the associated optimality results. To construct the confidence sets, in many cases, the repro samples method provides explicit mathematical solutions, while in other scenarios, it employs Monte-Carlo approaches to attain numerical solutions.
A notable strength of the repro samples framework is that it does not rely on the likelihood or large sample CLT for inference developments. Furthermore, the framework is flexible, making it particularly effective for a diverse set of challenging inference problems, including those irregular problems that involve discrete or non-numerical inference targets.

The article also includes a case study example of Gaussian mixture models to demonstrate the use of the repro samples procedure. The classic
Gaussian mixture models have a long history and are prevalent in statistics and data science. Although the models may appear simple, the associated inference problems are highly non-trivial and challenging, particularly when the number of components is unknown. This is because it involves a mixed type (discrete and continuous) of unknown parameters, many of which have varying lengths. In the case study example, we provide a comprehensive solution for the long-standing, important, yet still open inference problems using the repro samples method. Theoretical derivations specifically
tailored to the Gaussian mixture models are provided.
Moreover, numerical studies in a real-data setting and comparisons with existing approaches demonstrate that the repro samples method is the only effective approach that can consistently cover model parameters (both discrete and continuous types) with the desired coverage rates.
Another example of addressing open challenging problems is demonstrated in
Wang et al. 2022
, in which the authors provide the first comprehensive method to incorporate model selection uncertainty in inference problems for high dimensional linear models. In particular, the authors have developed confidence sets for the unknown true (sparse) model, the regression coefficients, or jointly for both the true model and the coefficients with guaranteed finite-sample performances, providing a full picture of inference uncertainties covering model space, regression coefficients, and more.

Indeed, by considering broad types of parameters and models,
the repro samples method
significantly extends the scope and reach of statistical inference. Besides the two classical case study examples mentioned in the previous paragraph,
The repro samples method can also be used to provide interpretable inference for many complex black-box machine learning models. For instance, it can be used in a tree model to provide a confidence set for the underlying ‘true’ tree model that generates observed data, and using the confidence tree set to develop a principled random forest (that only ensemble trees in a confidence set). Preliminary results have demonstrated both theoretical and numerical advantages over the regular ensemble tree methods (e.g., the standard random forest method, etc.). The repro samples method can further be used to construct a confidence set for the architecture parameters (layer and vertices) of the smallest neural network model(s) that can generate observed data.
Due to space limitation, these results will be reported in separate research papers. Finally, the data
\bm{y}^{obs}
mentioned in this paper can also be non-numerical (e.g., graphical network data; voice data; image data; etc). In this case, we can still apply the repro sample method to make inference for such data, as long as we still have a generative model and a metric to measure certain conformity among the non-numerical data. This is also a future research direction. We anticipate that the repro samples method can potentially serve as a stepping stone for establishing theoretical foundations in data science and for providing informed statistical inference and prediction in many machine learning problems.

The assumed algorithmic model (
1
) or its extension (
8
) encompass most models assumed in the current literature. Nevertheless,
it is still of interest to investigate the impact if the assumed model (
1
) or (
8
) is misspecified; for example, the distribution of
\bm{U}
is unknown or the form
G(\cdot)
is misspecified. We first note that, in some cases, it is possible to estimate the distribution of
\bm{U}
. For instance, in the high dimensional sparse linear model studies in
Wang et al. 2022
,it is possible to use their model candidate set to get a supper model, use the super model to get residuals, and then use the residuals to estimate the distribution of
\bm{U}
. Alternatively, instead of assuming Gaussian errors in the linear model, we can also assume the error distribution is from a more general family, i.e.,
\propto e^{-|u_{i}|^{\eta_{1}}/\eta_{2}}
, and then estimate the additional parameters
\eta_{1}>0
and
\eta_{2}>0
together with other model parameters.
In cases when the form of
G(\cdot,\cdot)
is unknown, the target parameter can perhaps be defined as the parameter in the misspecified model family that is closest to the true model that generated the observed data. Relevant research at this front includes model calibration
(
Smith 2007
, e.g.,)

and variational Bayes
(
Wang and Blei 2019
, e.g.,)
. Similar to all model-based approaches, model robustness against misspecification is an important research topic here as well.
It is certainly an interesting future topic to be further investigated.

Finally, most of our theoretical developments are centered on showing the validity of the approaches. Section
6
shows that the repro samples development subsumes the classical
Neyman procedure in the sense that
a confidence set obtained by our repro samples method is always better than (either the same as or smaller than) that obtained by inverting the Neyman test procedure. If a confidence set obtained by the classical Neyman procedure is optimal, our repro samples method can also get the same optimal set.
Furthermore, the repro samples development borrows the inversion idea that is deeply rooted in a fiducial method, and such an inversion method does not lose any information and often enjoys “good finite-sample performance”
(
Hannig et al. 2016
)
. Nevertheless, the choice of the nuclear mapping function and its associated are problem specific and have impact on the size of confidence sets.
Further research on these efficiency issues is also an interesting research topic.
Due to space limitations, we will carry out the research in a separate paper.

B-I Two additional examples: Nonparametric quantile inference and robust method for contaminated Gaussian data

Example B2

(Robust method for contaminated Gaussian data)

Suppose the observations

B-II Techniques to improve computing efficiency

In this subsection, we describe two techniques that may be used to improve the computing efficiency of the generic Algorithm 1. The first technique is using the quantile regression method for the situation when the target parameter
\theta
is continuous. The second one specifies a condition under which a data-dependent candidate set can be used.

B-II-1 Use of quantile regression to find
B_{\alpha}(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)

When the target parameter(s) are discrete, we develop in Sections 3.2 and 4.2 an effective method to significantly reduce the search space of the discrete parameters. Here, we consider the case when the target parameters are continuous. We discuss how to utilize the continuity and quantile regression techniques to side-step the issues of (grid) search of the entire parameter space.

Without loss of generality, we first assume that our parameter of interest is
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits
in a continuous parameter space
\Theta
and we do not have any nuisance
parameters.
We further assume that

T(\bm{U},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
is a mapping from

{\cal U}\times\Theta\to{\cal T}\in\mathbb{R}
,
and it is continuous in
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits
. In this case, we can use the following technique based on quantile regression to improve the computation efficiency of the repro samples method in Algorithm 1 of Section 2.2

•

Let
\pi(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
be a proposal distribution that has no-zero probability over the range of
\Theta
. In addition to
|{\cal V}|
copies of
\bm{u}^{s}\sim\bm{U}
, we simulate
|{\cal V}|
copies of
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits^{s}\sim\pi(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
. We then fit a level-
\alpha
quantile regression model with
t^{s}=T(\bm{u}^{s},{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits}^{s})
as response and
{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits^{s}}
as covariates. We output the conditional level-
\alpha
quantile, say
\widehat{C}(\alpha|\theta))
, via regression of
t^{s}
on
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits^{s}
.
The Borel set
B_{\alpha}(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
for our repro samples method is either
(-\infty,\widehat{C}(\alpha|\theta))
or
(\widehat{C}((1-\alpha)/2|\theta),\widehat{C}(1-(1-\alpha)/2|\theta))
.

This method is similar to Algorithm 1 of
Dalmasso et al. 2022
in which the authors use a quantile regression to get a level-
(1-\alpha)
rejection region for a Neyman-Pearson test problem. The difference is that the
t^{s}
in
Dalmasso et al. 2022
is computed from a test statistic while our
t^{s}
is from a nuclear mapping function. Ours is simpler since we do not need to generate any data points
\bm{y}^{s}=G(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits^{s},\bm{u}^{s})
, especially in cases when
\bm{y}^{s}
is expensive to generate.

Dalmasso et al. 2022
showed that the output conditional level-
\alpha
quantile (say,
\widehat{C}(\alpha|\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits))
via regression of

on
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits^{s}
is a level-
(1-\alpha)
critical value, if the quantile regression estimator is consistent

(
Koenker et al. 1994
;
Takeuchi et al. 2006
)
, as
|{\cal V}|\to\infty
. Similarly, in our case, if the quantile regression estimator is consistent, then
B_{\alpha}(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)=(-\infty,\widehat{C}(\alpha|\theta))
or

3

Using the quantile regression method,
we only need to calculate total
|{\cal V}|
copies of

|{\cal V}|
copies of

\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits

The method often significantly improves our computational efficiency.
Here, we require that

{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits}
to ensure the consistency of quantile regression estimators.
The method holds for any

\pi(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)>0
for all
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits\in\Theta

Practically, we often pick a proposal distribution

When

\alpha

Finally, suppose
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits=(\eta,{\bm{\beta}}^{\top})^{\top}

Specifically, suppose we have simulated

constructing a data-dependent candidate set (a subset of

\widehat{\Theta}(\bm{y}_{obs})

Here,

\bm{y}_{obs}
.
While there can be different ways to obtain this pre-screened candidate set, a key question is to overcome the potential problem of “double usage” of the observed data.
In particular,
after constructing this candidate set

\alpha

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\alpha}^{\prime}(\bm{y}_{obs})=\widehat{\Theta}(\bm{y}_{obs})\cap\Gamma_{\alpha}(\bm{y}_{obs})

\displaystyle\qquad=\big\{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits:\exists\,\bm{u}^{*}\in{\cal U},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits\in\widehat{\Theta},\,\hbox{s.t.}\,\bm{y}_{obs}=G_{z}({\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits},\bm{u}^{*}),\,T(\bm{u}^{*},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)\in B_{\alpha}(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)\big\}.

(B2)

Here, the “double usage” refers to the fact that we use the observed data in constructing both the candidate set
\widehat{\Theta}(\bm{y}_{obs})

To achieve our goal, a necessary condition that we impose on our data-dependent candidate set is

\mathbb{P}\big\{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{0}\in\widehat{\Theta}(\bm{Y})\big\}=1-o(\delta^{\prime}),

(B3)

where

\alpha

\theta

Corollary 5
.

Let
\bm{Y}=G(\bm{U},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{0})

\mathbb{P}\big\{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{0}\in\Gamma_{\alpha}^{\prime}(\bm{Y})\big\}\geq\alpha-o(\delta^{\prime}).

By the fact that

we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big\{T(\bm{U},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{0})\in B_{\alpha}(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{0})\big\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\big\{T(\bm{U},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{0})\in B_{\alpha}(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{0}),\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{0}\in\widehat{\Theta}\big\}+\mathbb{P}\big\{T(\bm{U},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{0})\in B_{\alpha}(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{0}),\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{0}\not\in\widehat{\Theta}\big\}

Appendix C: A Brief Review and Comparison with Existing and Relevant Inference Procedures

The repro samples method is a (pure) frequentist approach, but
its development inherits some key ideas from several
existing inferential procedures
across Bayesian, fiducial, and frequentist (BFF) paradigms.
For readers’ convenience, we first provide in Appendix C-I a brief review of several relevant BFF inferential approaches. We then provide in Appendix C-II a comparative discussion to highlight the strengths and uniqueness of the proposed repro samples method. We also describe a role that the repro samples method can play in bridging across BFF inference approaches.

C-I A Brief Review of Existing and
Relevant BFF procedures

The repro samples method is a framework that utilizes artificial sample sets for inference, and there are several relevant artificial sample-based inferential approaches across the BFF paradigms. Several relevant and important ones are briefly reviewed below.

(i) Approximate Bayesian Computing (ABC)

refers to a family of techniques used to approximate posterior densities of
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits
by bypassing direct likelihood evaluations
(
Rubin 1984
;
Tavaré et al. 1997
;
Csilléry et al. 2010
;
Peters et al. 2012
, cf.)
.
A basic version of the so-called rejection ABC algorithm has the following [a] - [c] steps:

[Step a]
Simulate an artificial model parameter
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits^{*}
from a prior distribution
\pi(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
and, given the simulated
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits^{*}
, simulate an artificial data set
{\bf y}^{*}

(e.g., set
{\bf y}^{*}=G(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits^{*},{\bf u}^{*})
for a simulated
{\bf u}^{*}
);

[Step b]
If

{\bf y}^{*}\approx{\bf y}_{obs}
,
we collect the artificial parameter
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits^{*}
;

[Step c]
Repeat Steps [a] and [b] to obtain a large set of
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits^{*}
.

ABC is in fact
a Bayesian inversion method,
since keeping
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits^{*}
that has the match
{\bf y}^{*}\approx{\bf y}_{obs}
is equivalent to keep the
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits^{*}
that solves
G(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits,{\bf u}^{*})\approx{\bf y}_{obs}
.

Operationally,
because it is difficult to match
{\bf y}^{*}\approx{\bf y}_{obs}
, the ABC method instead suggests to match

|S({\bf y}_{obs})-S({\bf y}^{*})|\leq\epsilon
for a pre-chosen summary statistic
S(\cdot)
and tolerance
\epsilon>0
. The kept
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits^{*}
form
an ABC posterior,
p_{\epsilon}(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits|\bm{y}_{obs})
.
If
S(\cdot)

is a sufficient statistic, then the ABC posterior
p_{\epsilon}(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits|\bm{y}_{obs})\to p(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits|\bm{y}_{obs})
,
the target posterior distribution, as
\epsilon\to 0
at a fast rate
(
Barber et al. 2015
;
Li and Fearnhead 2018
)
.
To improve its computing efficiency, this basic ABC algorithm has been extended to, for instance, more complex ABC-Markov Chain Monte Carlo (ABC-MCMC) type of algorithms, but the key matching and Bayesian inversion ideas remain.
Note that, the ABC method seeks to match each single artificial sample copy
\bm{y}^{*}
with the observed data
\bm{y}_{obs}
through a summary statistic and a preset tolerance level
\epsilon
to form a rejection rule.

There are two remaining issues unsolved in the ABC method
(
Li and Fearnhead 2018
;
Thornton et al. 2023
, cf.,)
. First, When
S(\cdot)

is not sufficient,
p_{\epsilon}(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits|\bm{y}_{obs})\not\to p(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits|\bm{y}_{obs})
.
In this case,
there is no guarantee that an ABC posterior is a Bayesian posterior, thus
the interpretation of the inferential result by the ABC posterior is unclear.
However, it is not possible to
derive a sufficient statistic when the likelihood is intractable. Thus, the requirement that
S(\cdot)
is sufficient places a limitation on the practical use of the ABC method (as a likelihood-free inference approach).
Second, the ABC method requires the preset threshold value
\epsilon\to 0
at a fast rate, leading to a degeneracy of computing efficiency. This is because the acceptance probability in Step [b] goes to
0
for a very small
\epsilon\to 0
,
In practice, there is no clear-cut choice for an appropriate
\epsilon
to balance the procedure’ computational efficiency and inference validity. It
remains to be an unsolved question in the literature
(
Li and Fearnhead 2018
, e.g.,)
.

(ii) Generalized fiducial inference (GFI)
is a generalization of Fisher’s fiducial method, which is understood in contemporary statistics as an inversion method to solve a pivot equation for model parameter
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits

(
Zabell 1992
;
Hannig et al. 2016
)
.
The GFI method extends the inversion approach from a pivot to actual data; i.e., it solves for
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits^{*}
from

{\bf y}_{obs}=G(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits,{\bf u}^{*})
for artificially generated
{\bf u}^{*}
’s. However, since a solution is not always possible, the GFI method proposes to
consider an optimization under an
\epsilon
-approximation:

\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{\epsilon}^{*}={\rm argmin}_{\theta\in\{\theta:\,\,||{\bf y}_{obs}-G(\theta,{\bf u}^{*})||^{2}\leq\epsilon\}}||{\bf y}_{obs}-G({\theta},{\bf u}^{*})||^{2}

(C1)

and let
\epsilon\to 0
at a fast rate
(
Hannig et al. 2016
)
.
The GFI method many times has a guaranteed statistical (frequentist) performance, provided the sample size
n\to\infty
and
G
is smooth/differentiable in
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits

(
Hannig 2009
;
Hannig et al. 2016
)
. Different than the ABC method that uses the rejection sampling approach, the GFI uses an optimization to approximately solve the equation
\bm{y}_{obs}=G(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits,\bm{u}^{*})
within a
\epsilon
-constrained set in
\Theta
.
Operationally, the
GFI method suffers the same computational issue as in the ABC method on the choice of the pre-specified
\epsilon
, since,
as
\epsilon\to\infty
at a fast rate, equation (
C1
) may not have a solution.

(iii) Efron’s bootstrap and other related artificial sampling methods
, in which many copies of artificial data are generated, are a popular way to help quantify uncertainty in complicated estimation problems in statistics. Let
\hat{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{y}}_{obs})

be a point estimator of the true parameter
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{0}
and
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits^{*}=\hat{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits}(\bm{y}^{*})
be the corresponding bootstrap estimator calculated using a bootstrap sample
\bm{y}^{*}
.
The key validity justification of bootstrap methods relies on the so-called bootstrap central limit theorem (CLT)
(
Singh 1981
;
Bickel and Freedman 1981
, e.g.,)
:

\hat{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits}(\bm{Y}^{*})-\hat{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{y}}_{obs})|\hat{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{y}}_{obs})\sim\hat{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{Y})-\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{0}|\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{0},

in which the (multinomial distributed) uncertainty in resampled artificial data
\bm{Y}^{*}
asymptotically matches the (often non-multinomial distributed) uncertainty in
\hat{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}}\nolimits(\bm{Y})
inherited from the target random sample
\bm{Y}
.
When the CLT does not apply (e.g., the parameter space
\Theta
is a discrete set), the bootstrap methods are not supported by theory and often perform poorly.

Furthermore, the repro samples method is also related the so-called Inferential Model (IM) that seeks to produce (epistemic) probabilistic inference for
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{0}
:

(iv) Inferential model (IM)
is an attempt to develop a general framework for “prior-free exact probabilistic inference”
(
Martin and Liu 2015
)
. As described in
Martin and Liu 2015
and under the model assumption (
1
), an IM procedure include three steps:

•

[A-step]
Associate
(\bm{Y},\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits)
with the unobserved auxiliary statistic
\bm{U}
, i.e.,
\bm{Y}=G({\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits},{\bm{U}})
;

[P-Step]
Predict the unobserved auxiliary statistic
\bm{U}
with a random set
{\cal S}
with distribution
{\mathbb{P}}_{\cal S}
;

[C-step]
Combine observed
\bm{y}_{obs}
with random set
{\cal S}
into a new data-dependent random set

\Theta_{\bm{y}_{obs}}({\cal S})=\bigcup_{\bm{u}\in{\cal S}}\left\{\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits:\bm{y}_{obs}=G(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits,\bm{u})\right\}
.

A novel aspect of the IM development that is different than a typical fiducial procedure is that the uncertainty quantification of
\bm{Y}
is through the parameter-free
\bm{U}
using a random set in [P-step]. This separation removes the impact of parameter
\bm{\theta}
and makes the task of uncertainty quantification easier.
In order to fully express the outcomes in probabilistic forms, the IM development needs to use a system of imprecise probability known as Dempster-Shafer Calculus.
The outcomes of an IM algorithm are the so-called plausible and belief functions (or lower and upper probability functions) for
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{0}
, which can be used for frequentist inference. The plausible and belief functions are akin to the so-called upper or lower confidence distributions
(
Thornton and Xie 2024
, e.g.,)
, although the IM method is not considered by most (including Martin and Liu) as a frequentist development.

In addition to the four reviewed methods, the repro samples also has some connections to the universal inference
(
Wasserman et al. 2020
)
and conformal prediction
(
Shafer and Vovk 2008
)
approaches, which be further discussed in the next subsection.

C-II Repro samples method and Connections to BFF Inferential Approaches

The repro samples method borrows two important ideas from the aforementioned BFF procedures. First, the repro samples method utilizes artificial data samples to help quantify the uncertainty of statistical inference. This idea has been used in the bootstrap, ABC, GFI, and Monte-Carlo hypothesis testing procedures, among others. A key aspect of utilizing artificial data samples for inference in these procedures is the common attempt
to match (in a certain way) the artificial data with the observed sample. See
Thornton and Xie 2024
for a more elaborated discussion. This matching is done by directly comparing either the value or distribution of the artificial
\bm{y}^{*}
(or its summary statistic) with that of
\bm{y}_{obs}
(or its summary statistic). The proposed repro samples method fully utilizes the artificial samples and the matching idea to both develop inference and solve computational questions. In addition, the repro samples method also borrows an idea from the IM development to quantify the inference uncertainty by first assessing the uncertainty of the unobserved
\bm{u}^{rel}
. These two important ideas have guided us to develop both finite and asymptotic inferences under the frequentist framework without the need to use any likelihood or decision theoretical criteria.
We consider that the repro samples method is a further development (generalization and refinements) of these aforementioned existing BFF procedures.

In the remaining of this subsection, we provide some comparative discussions, comparing the repro samples method with: (i) the ABC and GFI methods; (ii) the IM method; (iii) the universal inference method; (iv) the conformal prediction approach; and (v) the Monte-Carlo hypothesis testing procedure.
We further describe the role that the repro samples method can play in bridging across BFF inference approaches.

(i) Repro samples method versus ABC and GFI methods.

Both the GFI and repro samples approach highlight the matching of equation
\bm{y}_{obs}=G(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits,\bm{u}^{*})
and the fact that when
\bm{u}^{*}
matches or is close to
\bm{u}^{rel}
, the solution of
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits
from the equation is equal or close to the true
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{0}
. The difference lies in that the GFI approach compares each single copy of artificial sample
\bm{y}^{*}=G(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits,\bm{u}^{*})
with the single copy of the observed
\bm{y}_{obs}
, so does the ABC method. In the Monte-Carlo implementation of the repro samples method (e.g., Algorithm 1), however, we compare the single copy of the observed
\bm{y}_{obs}
with multiple copies of
\bm{y}^{s}=G(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits,\bm{u}^{s})

(or, more accurately, the realized
T(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits,\bm{u}^{rel})
with multiple copies of
T(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits,\bm{u}^{s})
’s), for
\bm{u}^{s}\in{\cal V}
, where
{\cal V}
is the collection of many copies of
\bm{u}^{s}
. By comparing observed sample
\bm{y}_{obs}
(or realized
\bm{u}^{rel}
) with many copies of artificially generated
\bm{y}^{s}=G(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits,\bm{u}^{s})
(or
\bm{u}^{s}\in{\cal V}
), it allows us to use a confidence level
\alpha
to calibrate the uncertainty thus side-step the difficult issue in both GFI and ABC methods on how to appropriately preset the
threshold
\epsilon
. Furthermore, the use of the nuclear mapping adds much flexibility to the repro samples method. Unlike the ABC method, the often inconvenient requirement of using a sufficient statistic to ensure inference validity is not needed for the repro samples method. Additionally, the repro samples method is a frequentist method. It does not need to make the additional model assumption of a prior distribution. It also avoids the potential philosophical issues that are often associated with fiducial inference on its interpretations.

(ii) Repro samples method versus IM method.

The repro samples and IM methods both promote first quantifying the uncertainty of
\bm{U}
and use it to help address the overall uncertainty inherited in the sampling data. To produce a level
\alpha
confidence set, the repro sample method uses a single fixed Borel set and a
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits
-dependent nuclear mapping function to help quantify the uncertainty in
\bm{U}
. Moreover, the repro samples method is a fully frequentist approach
developed using only the standard probability tool. The IM method, on the other hand, attempts to achieve a higher level goal of producing a prior-free probabilistic inference for the unknown parameter
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits
. To achieve this goal, it requires to use random sets and a complex imprecise probability system known as Dempster-Shafer calculus.
Furthermore, the IM method focuses on finite sample inference, while the repro samples can be used for both finite and large sample inferences. In addition, the introduction and promotion of the so-called nuclear mapping function in the repro samples method allows it to greatly extend the scope of the inferential framework,
making it more flexible and effective for many complex inference problems in both statistics and data science.
Finally, the repro samples method emphasizes the actual matching of simulated artificial
\bm{u}^{*}
with the (unknown) realized
\bm{u}^{rel}
as well. The development of candidate set for discrete parameter
\eta
in Section
3.2
is rooted in the fact that when
\bm{u}^{*}
is equal or in a neighborhood of
\bm{u}^{rel}
can give us the same
\eta_{0}
in the many-to-one mapping function (
15
). This matching scheme cannot be easily adapted in an IM approach.

(iii) Repro samples method versus universal inference approach.
Universal inference
(
Wasserman et al. 2020
)
is a framework recently developed to provide performance-guaranteed inferences
without regularity conditions.
The universal inference method typically requires a tractable likelihood function and data splitting. It relies on a Markov inequality to justify its validity (testing size), at some expanse of power
(
Dunn et al. 2022
;
Tse and Davison 2022
)
.
The framework can handle irregular inference problems and is particularly effective for sequential testing problems
(
Ramdas et al. 2020
;
Xie 2023
)
.
The case study example of Gaussian mixture is also studied in
Wasserman et al. 2020
but, unlike the repro samples method, it cannot provide a two-sided confidence set for the unknown number of components
\tau_{0}
. The repro samples method
is a likelihood-free approach that is also effective for irregular inference problems, and it does not systemically suffer a loss of power while maintaining inference validity.

(iv) Repro samples method versus conformal prediction method.

Conformal prediction is a popular nonparametric prediction method used to construct a level-
\alpha
confidence set for a future (unobserved) observation, say
y_{new}
, given observed

n
exchangeable data points

, then the value

is conformal with
\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}\}

at the
\alpha
-level. The collection of such

values forms a level-
\alpha
predictive set of
y_{new}
. In our case, we are interested in
a fixed unknown parameter

\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{0}
, instead of a random

y_{new}
. Nevertheless, we can use the same conformal concept to intuitively explain the Monte-Carlo repro samples approach (cf., Algorithm
1
in Section

):
For a potential value
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits
of
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits_{0}
, we
generate
|{\cal V}|
sets of artificial data
\bm{y}^{s}=G(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits,\bm{u}^{s})
(or, more accurately,
|{\cal V}|
copies of
T(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits,\bm{u}^{s})
’s), for
\bm{u}^{s}\in{\cal V}
; If the

\bm{y}_{obs}
is conformal with the
|{\cal V}|
artificial data sets
\bm{y}_{s}
’s (or, more accurately, if there exists an
\bm{u}^{*}\in{\cal U}
such that
\bm{y}_{obs}=G(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits,\bm{u}^{*})
and the value

is conformal with multiple copies of
T(\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits,\bm{u}^{s})

\bm{u}^{s}\in{\cal V}

we collect the
\mathop{\theta\kern-4.49997pt\hbox{$\theta$}}\nolimits

Finally, as an artificial-sample-based inference procedure, the repro samples method plays a role in bridging across BFF inference approaches.

Artificial-sample-based inference and a bridge of BFF paradigms
. There have been several recent developments on the foundation of statistical inference across Bayesian, fiducial, and frequentist (BFF) paradigms.
Reid 2024
and
Thornton and Xie 2024
provide comprehensive overviews on several recent BFF research developments at the foundation level. Based on the development of confidence distribution,
Thornton and Xie 2024
also explores the aspect of making inferences through matching artificial samples with observed data across BFF procedures. It argues that the matching of simulated artificial randomness with the sampling randomness inherited from a statistical model provides a unified
bridge to connect BFF inference procedures.
Here, the artificial randomness includes, for example, bootstrap randomness in bootstrap, MCMMC randomness in a Bayesian analysis, the randomness of
\bm{U}^{*}
in fiducial procedures such as the
GFI and IM methods.
The repro samples method is another development that seeks to align simulated artificial randomness with the sampling randomness. By doing so we can effectively measure and quantify the uncertainty in our statistical inferential statements.
The repro samples method can be used to address many difficult inference problems, especially those involving discrete parameter space and also those where large sample CLT does not apply. It provides a further advancement on the foundation of statistical inference to meet the growing need for ever-emerging data science.
