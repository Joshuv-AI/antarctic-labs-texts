Multi-objective optimization of the magnetic wiping process in dip-coating
Fabio Pino 1,2, Benoit Scheid 2, Miguel A. Mendez 1

Abstract
Electromagnetic wiping systems allow to pre-meter the coating thickness of the liquid metal on a moving substrate. These systems have the potential to provide more uniform coating and significantly higher production rates compared to pneumatic wiping, but they require substantially larger amounts of energy. This work presents a multi-objective optimization accounting for (1) maximal wiping efficiency (2) maximal smoothness of the wiping meniscus, and (3) minimal Joule heating. We present the Pareto front, identifying the best wiping conditions given a set of weights for the three competing objectives. The optimization was based on a 1D steady-state integral model, whose prediction scales according to the Hartmann number (Ha). The optimization uses a multi-gradient approach, with gradients computed with a combination of finite differences and variational methods. The results show that the wiping efficiency depends solely on Ha and not the magnetic field distribution. Moreover, we show that the liquid thickness becomes insensitive to the intensity of the magnetic field above a certain threshold and that the current distribution (hence the Joule heating) is mildly affected by the magnetic field’s intensity and shape.

1
Introduction

Hot-dip galvanization consists in coating a steel substrate with a thin layer of zinc to prevent corrosion
(
Kistler and Schweizer 1997
)
. The substrate usually takes the form of a large strip, dipped and then withdrawn from a bath of molten zinc. The liquid excess is removed via a wiping system. In galvannealing, the strip is heated for a few seconds after the wiping region in an induction furnace (annealing furnace) when the zinc is still liquid. Thereby, the iron in the steel and the zinc coating diffuse into each other, forming a zinc-iron inter-metallic compound with a composition of approximately
90\%
iron and
10\%
zinc in the bulk
(
Goodwin 2013
;
Center 2014
)
.

The wiping is usually performed through impinging gas jets in a process called jet wiping
(
Gosset and Buchlin 2006
;
Hocking et al. 2011
)
. The process is simple and economically attractive, but it develops hydrodynamic instabilities when the substrate speed exceeds 2 or 3 m/s, depending on the wiping strength. These instabilities include splashing of the wiped liquid
(
Gosset and Buchlin 2006
)
and oscillations of the impinging jet, which results in coating undulations
(
Gosset et al. 2019
;
Mendez et al. 2019
;
Barreiro-Villaverde et al. 2021
)
.

An alternative to jet wiping is electromagnetic wiping, extensively investigated by
Bregand et al. 2010
. These systems are based on the ElectroMagnetic Brake (EMB) principle, that is the use of electromagnetic forces to control the thickness of the liquid coating
(
Ernst et al. 2009
;
Lloyd-Jones, C and Barker, HA and Worner 1998
;
Dumont et al. 2011
)
. This is achieved using a strong magnetic field produced by a permanent magnet or high-frequency induction generators. The relative motion of the liquid film and the magnetic field create an induced current in the liquid film, which generates a downward Lorentz force that wipes the excess of liquid. These systems have been considered as “pre-wiping” devices in lines that target very large substrate speeds
(
Lloyd-Jones, C and Barker, HA and Worner 1998
)
.
Despite offering promising performances and coating uniformity at large production rates, the major challenges in their large-scale deployment are the energy requirements and the possible overheating of the liquid zinc, which increases the risks of promoting iron-zinc phase growth. The design of an electromagnetic system must thus strike a balance between the contrasting objectives of having sufficient “wiping capabilities” while minimizing overheating and energy consumption.

This work investigates the trade-off between these competing goals using multi-objective optimization. Specifically, we present the Pareto front, defining the optimum according to three criteria: (1) wiping capabilities, (2) Joule heating and (3) smoothness of the wiping liquid meniscus. The optimization is based on a simple steady-state model, neglecting surface tension. The remaining of the article is structured as follows. Section
4
presents the simplified model used in the optimization; this is based on the steady-state streamwise momentum balance of the liquid film with the magnetic effect expressed by the Hartmann number. Section
5
reports on the methods for computing the relevant first-order sensitivity of the problem, which combined variational methods and finite differences depending on the size of the parameter vector. Section
6
defines the constrained multi-objective optimization problem and the solution method based on a multi-gradient descent. Finally, conclusions and perspectives are reported in Section
8
.

2
Problem description

We consider liquid zinc with density
\rho=6570
kg/m
3
, dynamic viscosity
\mu=3.5\times 10^{-3}
Pa
\cdot
s, kinematic viscosity
\nu=\mu/\rho
, surface tension
\sigma=0.7
N/m and electrical conductivity
\sigma_{M}=0.028\times 10^{8}
S/m. These properties are taken at around 500 Celsius degrees as in
Giordanengo et al. 2000
. Figure
1
shows the schematic of the process: a metal strip is withdrawn from a bath of molten zinc at a constant speed
U_{p}
under the effect of gravity
g
, wiped by permanent magnets (light blue circle) and then heated in a galvannealing furnace (green box).

The reference system is centered on the substrate, with the positive streamwise axis
x
pointing downwards, the wall-normal axis
y
towards the free surface, and the cross-stream axis
z
in the spanwise direction. We assume a two-dimensional velocity vector field within the liquid, given by
\mathbf{V}=[u(x,y),v(x,y),0]^{T}
. The flow is bounded by the substrate at
y=0
, and by the free surface
h
, with the streamwise flow rate
q
defined as:

q=\int_{0}^{h}u(y)\;dy.

(1)

The magnetic field generated by the actuators is given by the vector:

\mathbf{B}=[0,-B(x),0]^{T},

(2)

where
B(x)
is always positive since, for simplicity, we are only considering the negative pole. The relative motion of the liquid zinc with respect to the external magnetic field generates an induced current in the bulk. Neglecting the electric potential difference, this is given by the vector:

\mathbf{J}=\mathbf{V}\times\mathbf{B}=[0,0,j_{z}]^{T}=[0,0,-\sigma_{M}uB],

(3)

The integraction of the induced current with the external magnetic field results in a downward Lorentz force
\mathbf{F}_{L}
defined as the cross product of
\mathbf{B}
and
\mathbf{J}
:

\mathbf{F}_{L}=\mathbf{J}\times\mathbf{B}=[F_{L},0,0]^{T},

(4)

with
F_{L}=\sigma_{M}uB^{2}
.

3
Scaling quantities

To recast the governing equations in nondimensional form, we scale the flow velocity by the substrate velocity
U_{p}
and lengths by the liquid film thickness resulting from the steady state viscous-gravity balance
(
Mendez et al. 2021
)
:

[u]=U_{p}\qquad\qquad[h]=\sqrt{\frac{\nu U_{p}}{g}}\,,

(5)

where the
[\cdot]
denotes reference quantities. Based on
[u]
and
[h]
, we obtain the scale for the flow rate as:

[q]=[u][h]=\sqrt{\frac{\nu U_{p}^{3}}{g}}

(6)

We scale the streamwise coordinate
x
assuming long-wave conditions:

[x]=[h]/\epsilon,

(7)

with
\epsilon\ll 1.

We define the magnetic field and the induced current reference quantities with their maximum values:

[B]=sup(B(x))\qquad\qquad[J]=\sigma_{M}U_{p}[B],

(8)

We define the power density
P_{D}
reference quantity as:

[P_{D}]=U_{p}\rho g.

(9)

The non-dimensional group governing the system is the Hartman number:

\mbox{\text{Ha}}=\sqrt{\frac{\sigma_{M}[B]^{2}U_{p}}{\rho g}},

(10)

which weighs the importance of electromagnetic to gravitational forces. In the following, we use the hat notation
\hat{a}=a/[a]
for the non-dimensional quantities and the shorthand notation
\partial_{x}h=\partial h/\partial_{x}
for partial derivatives.

4
Simplified model

In line with classical simplified engineering models for the jet wiping
(
Thornton and Graff 1976
;
Tuck 1983
)
, we assume a gently sloped interface (
\partial_{x}h\sim 0
)
(
Mendez et al. 2021
)
, a unidimensional flow (
v(x,y)=0
), without surface tension
(
Dumont et al. 2011
)
and without pressure gradient along the wall-normal and the streamwise direction. The governing equation is given by the steady-state streamwise momentum balance, which in non-dimensional form reads:

\partial_{\hat{y}\hat{y}}\hat{u}+1-\mbox{\text{Ha}}^{2}\hat{B}^{2}\hat{u}=0,

(11)

where the first term is the viscous stress, the second the gravitational forces, and the last the Lorentz force. The non-dimensional magnetic field
\hat{B}
is modelled as a Gaussian along the streamwise direction, centred in
\hat{x}=0
with standard deviation
\gamma
:

\hat{B}=\exp\Big(-\frac{\hat{x}^{2}}{2\gamma^{2}}\Big).

(12)

We impose a no-slip boundary condition at the wall, and a shear-free boundary condition at the free surface
(
Kalliadasis et al. 2011
, Chapter 2)
:

\hat{u}(\hat{y}=0)=-1,\qquad\qquad\partial_{\hat{y}}\hat{u}(\hat{y}=\hat{h})=0.

(13)

The solutions of (
11
) with boundary conditions (
13
) is given by:

\begin{split}\hat{u}=\left(1+\frac{1}{\mbox{\text{Ha}}^{2}\hat{B}^{2}}\right)&\Big(\tanh{(\mbox{\text{Ha}}\hat{h}\hat{B})}\sinh{(\mbox{\text{Ha}}\hat{y}\hat{B})}-\cosh{(\mbox{\text{Ha}}\hat{y}\hat{B})}\Big)+\frac{1}{\mbox{\text{Ha}}^{2}\hat{B}^{2}},\end{split}

(14)

as shown by
Dumont et al. 2011
. Integrating the streamwise velocity over the liquid film thickness, we obtain a relation between
\hat{q}
and
\hat{h}
:

\begin{split}\hat{q}=g(\hat{h};\mbox{\text{Ha}}\hat{B})=&\frac{1}{\mbox{\text{Ha}}^{3}\hat{B}^{3}}\Big\{\mbox{\text{Ha}}\,,\hat{h}\hat{B}-\tanh{(\mbox{\text{Ha}}\,\hat{h}\hat{B})}\left(\mbox{\text{Ha}}^{2}\hat{B}^{2}+1\right)\Big\}.\end{split}

(15)

:
Relation between the nondimensional flow rate
\hat{q}
and liquid film thickness
\hat{h}
for different value of the nondimensional magnetic field
\hat{B}
.

For
\mbox{\text{Ha}}\rightarrow 0
, (
14
) and (
15
) retrieve the relations without wiping
(
Mendez et al. 2021
)
. Figure
2
plots (
15
) for a fixed
Ha
at a fixed
\hat{x}
location for different values of the magnetic field
\hat{B}
. In wiping conditions,
\hat{B}
is varying along
\hat{x}
with a minimum
\hat{B}=0
far from the actuator and a maximum
\hat{B}=1
right at the pole location (
\hat{x}=0
). The thickness
\hat{h}
reaches a minimum at
\hat{x}=0
. Its value is found by solving
\partial_{\hat{h}}g(\hat{h};\mbox{\text{Ha}},\hat{B}=1)=0
, which yields:

\hat{h}_{min}=\frac{1}{\mbox{\text{Ha}}}\arccsc\Big(\sqrt{\frac{1}{\mbox{\text{Ha}}^{2}+1}}\Big).

(16)

The flow rate
\hat{q}_{opt}
is obtained injecting (
16
) in (
15
). The liquid film profile
\hat{h}(\hat{x})
is given by solving the equation:

\hat{q}_{opt}-g(\hat{h}(\hat{x});\mbox{\text{Ha}},\hat{B}(\hat{x}))=0,

(17)

at different locations along the stream-wise coordinate
\hat{x}
. In this work, we consider
N=100
equispaced points in a domain
\hat{x}\in[-10,10]
.

5
Sensitivity derivatives

The gradient-based multiobjective optimization carried out in this work requires the computation of the jacobian (variation) of the state variables
\hat{h}
,
\hat{q}
,
\hat{u}(\hat{y})
and
\hat{j}
with respect to the magnetic wiping parameters
\mathbf{p}=[\mbox{\text{Ha}},\hat{B}]^{T}
:

\partial_{\mathbf{p}}\hat{h}(\hat{x}),\qquad\qquad\partial_{\mathbf{p}}\hat{u}(\hat{y}),\qquad\qquad\partial_{\mathbf{p}}\hat{q},\qquad\qquad\partial_{\mathbf{p}}\hat{j}(\hat{x},\hat{y})\,.

(18)

For a given
Ha
and
\hat{B}(x)
, the gradient
\partial_{\mathbf{p}}\hat{h}
was computed with finite differences using the python library
scipy.optimize.approx_fprime
on a set of
N
equispaced points:

\hat{h}(\hat{x}_{i})=\frac{\hat{h}(\hat{x}_{i};\mathbf{p}+\delta)-\hat{h}(\hat{x}_{i};\mathbf{p})}{\delta},

(19)

with
\delta=10^{-8}
.

The total variation of
\hat{u}
(
d\hat{u}/d\mathbf{p}
) is given by:

\frac{d\hat{u}}{d\mathbf{p}}=\partial_{\mathbf{p}}\hat{u}+\partial_{\hat{h}}\hat{u}\partial_{\mathbf{p}}\hat{h}\,,

(20)

where
\partial_{\hat{h}}\hat{u}
was obtained differentiating (
14
):

\partial_{\hat{h}}\hat{u}=\left(1+\frac{1}{\mbox{\text{Ha}}^{2}\hat{B}^{2}}\right)\frac{\mbox{\text{Ha}}\hat{B}}{\cosh{(\mbox{\text{Ha}}\hat{h}\hat{B})}^{2}}\sinh{(\mbox{\text{Ha}}\hat{y}\hat{B})}\,.

(21)

The sensitivity gradient
\partial_{\mathbf{p}}\hat{u}(\hat{y})
was calculated using a variational method
(
Calver and Enright 2017
)
. This consists in differentiating (
11
) with respect to
\mathbf{p}
, which leads to a system for the sensitive gradients given by:

\displaystyle\partial_{\mathbf{p}}\frac{d\mathbf{u}(\hat{y})}{d\hat{y}}\,=\,\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{u}}\mathbf{S}_{\mathbf{p}}+\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{p}}\,,

(22)

where
\mathbf{u}=[u_{1},u_{2}]^{T}=[\hat{u}(\hat{y}),\partial_{\hat{y}}\hat{u}(\hat{y})]^{T}
, and
\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{u}}
and
\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{p}}
are the Jacobian matrices with respect to
\mathbf{u}
and
\mathbf{p}
respectively:

\mathbf{F_{u}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
\mbox{\text{Ha}}^{2}\hat{B}^{2}&0\end{pmatrix}\,\qquad\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{p}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
2\mbox{\text{Ha}}\hat{B}^{2}u_{1}&2\mbox{\text{Ha}}^{2}\hat{B}u_{1}\end{pmatrix},

(23)

and
\mathbf{S_{p}}
is the matrix of sensitivity derivatives of
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
:

\mathbf{S_{p}}=\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{\mbox{\text{Ha}}}u_{1}&\partial_{\hat{B}}u_{1}\\
\partial_{\mbox{\text{Ha}}}u_{2}&\partial_{\hat{B}}u_{2}.\end{pmatrix}.

(24)

Solving (
11
) and (
22
) with boundary conditions:

\begin{split}u_{1}(\hat{y})|_{\hat{y}=0}=-1,\quad u_{2}(\hat{y})|_{\hat{y}=\hat{h}},=0,\quad\partial_{\mbox{\text{Ha}}}u_{1}|_{\hat{y}=0}=0,\\
\partial_{\hat{B}}u_{1}|_{\hat{y}=0}=0,\quad\partial_{\mbox{\text{Ha}}}u_{2}|_{\hat{y}=\hat{h}}=0,\quad\partial_{\hat{B}}u_{2}|_{\hat{y}=\hat{h}}=0.\end{split}

(25)

gives the vector of sensitivity derivatives
\partial_{\mathbf{p}}\hat{u}=[\partial_{\mbox{\text{Ha}}}\hat{u},\partial_{\hat{B}}\hat{u}]^{T}
.

The variation of the flow rate
\partial_{\mathbf{p}}\hat{q}
is obtained as:

\frac{d\mathbf{q}}{d\mathbf{p}}=\frac{d}{d\mathbf{p}}\int_{0}^{\mathbf{h}}\,\hat{u}(\hat{y}^{\prime})\,d\hat{y}^{\prime}=\hat{u}(\hat{h})\partial_{\mathbf{p}}\hat{h}+\int_{0}^{\hat{h}}\,\partial_{\mathbf{p}}\hat{u}(\hat{y}^{\prime})\,dy^{\prime}.

(26)

Based on its definition in (
3
), the current density sensitivities are given by:

\partial_{\mbox{\text{Ha}}}\hat{j}=-\partial_{\mbox{\text{Ha}}}\hat{u}(\hat{y})\hat{B}(\hat{x}),\qquad\qquad\partial_{\hat{B}}\hat{j}=-\hat{u}(\hat{y})-\partial_{\hat{B}}\hat{u}(\hat{y})\hat{B}(\hat{x}).

(27)

6
Multiobjectives optimization

We set up a constrained multiobjectives minimization problem problem for the parameters
Ha
and
\gamma
, controlling the intensity and the shape of the magnetic field distribution. The constraints impose that
\mbox{\text{Ha}}<10
, above which the magnetic field becomes generally overly large, a positive standard deviation
\gamma>0
, and a negative flow rate
\hat{q}<0
(that is some liquid is withdrawn from the plate):

\boxed{\begin{aligned} \min_{\mbox{\text{Ha}},\gamma}\quad&J(\mbox{\text{Ha}},\gamma)\\
\textrm{s.t.}&\gamma>0,\,\,\mbox{\text{Ha}}<10\,\,\hat{q}<0\\
\end{aligned}}

(28)

The cost function
J
adds up the contribution of three terms, weighting the wiping strength (
J_{1}
), the smoothness of the free surface (
J_{2}
) and the Joule heating due to the induced currents (
J_{3}
):

J(Ha,\gamma)=J_{1}+J_{2}+J_{3}\,,

(29)

where:

\begin{split}J_{1}=-\hat{q},\quad J_{2}=\frac{1}{2}\int\limits_{x_{1}}\limits^{x_{2}}\Big(\partial_{\hat{x}\hat{x}}\hat{h}(\hat{x})\Big)^{2}\;d\hat{x},\quad J_{3}=\frac{1}{2}\int\limits_{x_{1}}\limits^{x_{2}}\int\limits_{0}\limits^{\hat{h}}\mbox{\text{Ha}}^{2}\hat{j}(\hat{x},\hat{y})^{2}\;d\hat{y}d\hat{x}\,.\end{split}

(30)

Reducing the term
J_{1}
requires reducing the flow rate and hence the liquid film thickness. Reducing the term
J_{2}
requires reducing the curvature of the wiping meniscus, hence producing smoother transition from the thin film solution at
x\rightarrow-\infty
and the thick solution at
x\rightarrow\infty
. A smoother transition implies smoother curvature of the flow streamlines within the film, smaller velocity gradients and thus a more stable film. The second derivative was computed by differentiating a fourth-order spline, which interpolated the solution in all the grid points.

The term
J_{3}
is related to the Joule heating due to the induced current
\mathbf{J}
. In its differential form, the heating power
P_{D}
is equal to the dot product of the current density vector
\mathbf{{J}}
times the electric field
\mathbf{E}
. Since the current density and the electric field are linked by the relation
\mathbf{E}=\mathbf{J}\sigma_{M}
, this yields the following expression for
P_{D}
:

P_{D}=\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E}=\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{J}/\sigma_{M}=||\mathbf{J}||^{2}/\sigma_{M}\,,

(31)

which in non-dimensional form reads (see also
Smolentsev et al. 1997
and
Mao et al. 2008
):

\hat{P}_{D}=\frac{\sigma_{M}U_{p}^{2}[B]^{2}}{[P_{D}]}\hat{j}^{2}=\mbox{\text{Ha}}^{2}\hat{j}^{2}.

(32)

6.1
Multi-Gradient Descent method

The constrained optimization problem (
28
) is solved using the Stochastic Multi-Subgradient Descent Algorithm (SMSGDA) method
(
Milojkovic et al. 2019
)
. This consists in updating the value of the parameters, starting from an initial value
\mathbf{p}_{init}
, in a gradient descent fashion:

\mathbf{p}^{\{i+1\}}=\mathbf{p}^{\{i\}}-\eta\nabla_{\mathbf{p}}J(\mathbf{p})

(33)

where the superscript
{i}
indicates the number of optimization iterations,
\eta
is a small number controlling the parameter update and
\nabla_{\mathbf{p}}J(\mathbf{p})
is the common descent vector computed as described in the following. This vector is given by the linear combination of the gradient of the ith objective function
\nabla_{\mathbf{p}}J_{i}(\mathbf{p})=[\partial_{\mbox{\text{Ha}}}J_{i},\partial_{\gamma}J_{i}]^{T}
(see
A
for more details):

\nabla_{\mathbf{p}}J(\mathbf{p})=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\,\alpha_{i}\nabla_{\mathbf{p}}J_{i}(\mathbf{p}),

(34)

where
\alpha_{i}
are the weighting coefficients. The values of these weights are obtained by solving a quadratic optimization problem at each optimization iteration:

\min_{\alpha,\cdots,\alpha_{n}}\quad\Big[||\sum_{i=1}^{n}\,\alpha_{i}\nabla_{\mathbf{p}}J_{i}(\mathbf{p})||^{2}\;|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\,\alpha_{i}=1,\alpha_{i}\geq 0\Big]\\

(35)

where
||\cdot||
is the Euclidean norm.
Due to the different scales of the objective functions, the gradients can have significantly different norms along the two objectives, hence causing these two to dominate on the other one. To mitigate this risk, we normalize the gradients as follows:

\nabla_{\mathbf{p}}\hat{J}_{i}(\mathbf{p})=\frac{\nabla_{\mathbf{p}}J_{i}(\mathbf{p})}{J_{i}(\mathbf{p}_{init})}\,,

(36)

where
\nabla_{\mathbf{p}}\hat{J}_{i}(\mathbf{p})
is the normalized gradient vector,
\nabla_{\mathbf{p}}J_{i}(\mathbf{p})
is the non-normalized gradient and
J_{i}(\mathbf{p}_{init})
is the initial loss related to the initial parameter guess
\mathbf{p}_{init}
.

The optimization is concluded when the necessary optimality Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) condition is met. This reads:

\alpha,\cdots,\alpha_{n}\geq 0\qquad,\sum_{i=1}^{n}\,\alpha_{i}=1\qquad,\sum_{i=1}^{n}\,\alpha_{i}\nabla_{\mathbf{p}}J_{i}(\mathbf{p})=0\,.

Every solution satisfying these conditions is called Pareto Stationary
(
Désidéri 2012
)
. To find the Pareto optimal solutions, we run several simulations starting from different
\mathbf{p}_{init}
until they converged on the point of the Pareto front.

Conclusion

In this work, we presented a multi-objective optimization for electromagnetic wiping in galvannealing coating processing. The optimization balances the need to reduce the film thickness while preserving a smooth wiping meniscus and minimizing the Joule heating. The problem was solved via a multi-gradient descent optimization, providing the Pareto front in the objectives’ and parameters’ spaces.

What stands out from this analysis is that the liquid film overheating and the steepness of the wiping meniscus increase for
Ha>3
. Depending on the coating requirements, one can choose solutions favouring one objective over the other. This result could pave the way to a hybrid solution where the jet wiping is supported by magnetic wiping in the pre-wiping or a pre-heating phase before the galvannealing furnace. This would represent a good compromise between wiping efficiencies and an increase in the temperature of the liquid, which would reduce the effort of the galvannealing furnace in creating the Fe-Zn alloy.
