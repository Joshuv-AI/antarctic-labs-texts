Multiplicative and additive compounds via Kronecker products and Kronecker sums Thanks: This research was partly supported by research grant 407/19 from the Israel Science Foundation.
Ron Ofir, Michael Margaliot

Abstract
Compound matrices play an important role in many fields of mathematics and have recently found new applications in systems and control theory. However, the explicit formulas for these compounds are non-trivial and not always easy to use. Here, we derive new formulas for the multiplicative and additive compounds of a matrix using Kronecker products and sums. This provides a new approach to matrix compounds based on the well-known and powerful theory of Kronecker products and sums. We demonstrate several applications of these new formulas, including deriving a new expression for the additive compound of the product of two matrices.

II-A

Compound matrices

Definition 1

Fix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
and
k\in\{1,\dots,\min\{n,m\}\}

k
-multiplicative compound matrix of

\binom{n}{k}\times\binom{m}{k}

k
-minors of
A

For example,
for

A=\begin{bmatrix}a_{11}&a_{12}\\
a_{21}&a_{22}\\
a_{31}&a_{32}\end{bmatrix}

and

A^{(2)}=\begin{bmatrix}\det\begin{bmatrix}a_{11}&a_{12}\\
a_{21}&a_{22}\end{bmatrix}\\
\det\begin{bmatrix}a_{11}&a_{12}\\
a_{31}&a_{32}\end{bmatrix}\\
\det\begin{bmatrix}a_{21}&a_{22}\\
a_{31}&a_{32}\end{bmatrix}\end{bmatrix}.

Definition
1

implies that
(A^{(k)})^{T}=(A^{T})^{(k)}
,

A^{(1)}=A
, and if
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
then
A^{(n)}=\det(A)
.
If

is diagonal, that is,

then Definition

implies that

is also diagonal, with

In particular,
the
k
-multiplicative compound of the

identity matrix is
(I_{n})^{(k)}=I_{r}
, with
r:=\binom{n}{k}
.

More generally,

-multiplicative compound of a square matrix

has a “multiplicative” spectral property. If the eigenvalues of
A

\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}\in\mathbb{C}
then the eigenvalues of

are all the
\binom{n}{k}
products:

\lambda_{i_{1}}\dots\lambda_{i_{k}},\quad 1\leq i_{1}<\dots<i_{k}\leq n.

The term “multiplicative compound” is justified by the
Cauchy-Binet theorem that asserts that for any
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
,
B\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times p}
and
k\in\{1,\dots,\min\{n,m,p\}\}
, we have

(AB)^{(k)}=A^{(k)}B^{(k)}.

(1)

Note that for

and

, this reduces to
\det(AB)=\det(A)\det(B)
. Furthermore,

A
is square and invertible then applying (

implies that

is also invertible, and
(A^{(k)})^{-1}=(A^{-1})^{(k)}

II-B

Kronecker product

The Kronecker product of two matrices
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
and
B\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}
is

A\otimes B:=\begin{bmatrix}a_{11}B&\dots&a_{1m}B\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
a_{n1}B&\dots&a_{nm}B\end{bmatrix}.

(9)

Note that
A\otimes B\in\mathbb{R}^{(np)\times(mq)}
.
We list several properties of the Kronecker product that will be used below (see
[
14
, Ch. 4]

for proofs and more details).
The product is distributive, that is,

\displaystyle A\otimes(B+C)

\displaystyle=A\otimes B+A\otimes C,

\displaystyle(B+C)\otimes A

\displaystyle=B\otimes A+C\otimes A,

and associative, that is,

(A\otimes B)\otimes C=A\otimes(B\otimes C).

Transposition is distributive over the Kronecker product, that is,

(A\otimes B)^{T}=A^{T}\otimes B^{T}.

The Kronecker product also satisfies the following mixed-product property: if
A,B,C,D
are matrices such that the products
AC
and
BD
are well-defined then

(A\otimes B)(C\otimes D)=(AC)\otimes(BD).

(10)

The Kronecker product is useful when studying linear equations with matrix variables and, in particular, in rewriting them as equations with vector variables. In particular, for
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
,

B\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
,
and
C,X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
,
the matrix equation

AX+XB=C

is equivalent to the equation

\left((I_{m}\otimes A)+(B^{T}\otimes I_{n})\right)\vect(X)=\vect(C).

(11)

The Kronecker product also has a useful property with respect to the matrix exponential. If
A_{1},A_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
then (
10
) implies that the matrices
A_{1}\otimes I_{n}
and
I_{n}\otimes A_{2}
commute, so

\displaystyle\exp(A_{1}\otimes I_{n}+I_{n}\otimes A_{2})

\displaystyle=\exp(A_{1}\otimes I_{n})\exp(I_{n}\otimes A_{2})

\displaystyle=(\exp(A_{1})\otimes I_{n})(I_{n}\otimes\exp(A_{2}))

\displaystyle=\exp(A_{1})\otimes\exp(A_{2}),

(12)

where the last equation follows from using (
10
) again.

More generally, for

A_{1},\dots,A_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, we have

\exp\left(A_{1}\otimes I_{n}\otimes\dots\otimes I_{n}+I_{n}\otimes A_{2}\otimes\dots\otimes I_{n}+I_{n}\otimes\dots\otimes I_{n}\otimes A_{s}\right)=\exp(A_{1})\otimes\dots\otimes\exp(A_{s}),

(13)

where every Kronecker product includes
s
terms.

The “
k
th Kronecker power”, denoted
A^{\otimes k}
,
is defined inductively
by

A^{\otimes k}:=A^{\otimes(k-1)}\otimes A,

with
A^{\otimes 1}:=A
.
If
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
with eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}
then the eigenvalues of
A^{\otimes k}
are all the
n^{k}
products

\lambda_{i_{1}}\dots\lambda_{i_{k}},\text{ with }i_{\ell}\in\{1,\dots,n\}.

Note that this set includes in particular all the eigenvalues of
A^{(k)}
.

The next example demonstrates
how the Kronecker product naturally appears
in the multiplicative
compound of a block matrix.

Example 1

Consider the matrix
B=\operatorname{diag}(d_{1},\dots,d_{4})
.
Then a direct calculation gives

B^{(2)}=\operatorname{diag}(d_{1}d_{2},d_{1}d_{3},d_{1}d_{4},d_{2}d_{3},d_{2}d_{4},d_{3}d_{4}).

Note that if we write
B
as a block matrix
B=\operatorname{diag}(B_{1},B_{2})
,
with
B_{1}=\operatorname{diag}(d_{1},d_{2})
and
B_{2}=\operatorname{diag}(d_{3},d_{4})
, then

B^{(2)}=\operatorname{diag}(B_{1}^{(2)},B_{1}\otimes B_{2},B_{2}^{(2)}).

II-C

The Kronecker sum

The Kronecker sum of two square matrices
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n},B\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
is the
(nm)\times(nm)
matrix

A\oplus B:=A\otimes I_{m}+I_{n}\otimes B.

With this notation, (
II-B
) becomes

\exp(A_{1}\oplus A_{2})=\exp(A_{1})\otimes\exp(A_{2}),

and (
11
) becomes

(B^{T}\oplus A)\vect(X)=\vect(C).

(14)

The next example demonstrates
how the Kronecker sum naturally appears
in the additive compound of a block matrix.

Example 2

Consider the matrix
B=\operatorname{diag}(d_{1},\dots,d_{4})
.
Then a direct calculation gives

(I_{4}+\varepsilon B)^{(2)}=\operatorname{diag}(s_{1}s_{2},s_{1}s_{3},s_{1}s_{4},s_{2}s_{3},s_{2}s_{4},s_{3}s_{4}),

where
s_{i}:=1+\varepsilon d_{i}
, and thus (
5
) with
n=4
and
k=2
gives

B^{[2]}=\operatorname{diag}(d_{1}+d_{2},d_{1}+d_{3},d_{1}+d_{4},d_{2}+d_{3},d_{2}+d_{4},d_{3}+d_{4}).

Writing
B
as a block matrix
B=\operatorname{diag}(B_{1},B_{2})
,
with
B_{1}=\operatorname{diag}(d_{1},d_{2})
and
B_{2}=\operatorname{diag}(d_{3},d_{4})
yields

B^{[2]}=\operatorname{diag}(B_{1}^{[2]},B_{1}\oplus B_{2},B_{2}^{[2]}).

For
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}

the “
k
th Kronecker sum” of
A
is defined by

A^{\oplus k}:=A\otimes I_{n}\otimes\dots\otimes I_{n}+I_{n}\otimes A\otimes I_{n}\otimes\dots\otimes I_{n}+\dots+I_{n}\otimes\dots\otimes I_{n}\otimes A,

(15)

where every product includes
k
terms.
For example,
A^{\oplus 1}=A
, and
A^{\oplus 2}=A\oplus A
.

If
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
with eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}
then the eigenvalues of
A^{\oplus k}
are all the
n^{k}
sums

\lambda_{i_{1}}+\dots+\lambda_{i_{k}},\text{ with }i_{\ell}\in\{1,\dots,k\}.

Note that this set includes in particular all the eigenvalues of
A^{[k]}
.

\displaystyle M_{n,n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{(j_{1},\dots,j_{n})\in S(1,\dots,n)}\sigma(j_{1},\dots,j_{n})(e^{j_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes e^{j_{n}})(\begin{bmatrix}e^{1}&\dots&e^{n}\end{bmatrix}^{(n)})^{T}

\displaystyle=\sum_{(j_{1},\dots,j_{n})\in S(1,\dots,n)}\sigma(j_{1},\dots,j_{n})(e^{j_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes e^{j_{n}}),

and

\displaystyle L_{n,n}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}e^{1}&\dots&e^{n}\end{bmatrix}^{(n)}(e^{1}\otimes\dots\otimes e^{n})^{T}

\displaystyle=(e^{1})^{T}\otimes\dots\otimes(e^{n})^{T}.

III-B

Properties of the matrices
M_{n,k}
and
L_{n,k}

The matrices
M_{n,k}
and
L_{n,k}
play an important role in this paper, so we begin by analyzing some of their properties.
It is useful to rewrite
M_{n,k}
and
L_{n,k}
in another form.
To do this
requires the following definition.

Definition 4

Let
w^{1}(n,k),\dots,w^{r}(n,k)
denote the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{r}
. Let
v^{1}(n,k),\dots,v^{n^{k}}(n,k)
denote the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n^{k}}
.

To simplify the notation, we will usually just write
w^{i}
and
v^{i}
, omitting the dependence on
n,k
, but it is important to remember that the dimension of these vectors depends on
n,k
.
For example, for
n=4
and
k=2
, we have that
r=\binom{4}{2}=6
,
w^{1},\dots,w^{6}

denote the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{6}
,
n^{k}=16
, and
v^{1},\dots,v^{16}

denote the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{16}
.

Lemma 1

The matrices
M_{n,k}
and
L_{n,k}
satisfy

\displaystyle M_{n,k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})\in Q(n,k)}\sum_{(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})\in S(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})}\sigma(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})v^{\ind^{R}_{n,k}(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})}(w^{\ind^{Q}_{n,k}(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})})^{T},

(17)

\displaystyle L_{n,k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})\in Q(n,k)}w^{\ind^{Q}_{n,k}(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})}(v^{\ind^{R}_{n,k}(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})})^{T}.

(18)

Proof:

It is shown in
Lemma
7
in the Appendix that

\ind^{R}_{n,k}(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})=\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}(i_{\ell}-1)n^{k-\ell}+1.

Combining this with the definition of the Kronecker product implies that
(x^{1}\otimes\dots\otimes x^{k})_{\ind^{R}_{n,k}(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})}=x^{1}_{i_{1}}\dotsm x^{k}_{i_{k}}
.
In particular, for the canonical basis

e^{1},\dots,e^{n}
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have

(e^{i_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes e^{i_{k}})_{\ind^{R}_{n,k}(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})}=e^{i_{1}}_{i_{1}}\dotsm e^{i_{k}}_{i_{k}}=1
, so

e^{i_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes e^{i_{k}}=v^{\ind^{R}_{n,k}(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})}.

(19)

Similarly, by the definition of the multiplicative compound, for any
(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})\in Q(n,k)
, we have that
\begin{bmatrix}e^{i_{1}}&\dots&e^{i_{k}}\end{bmatrix}^{(k)}=w^{\ind^{Q}_{n,k}(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})}
.
Substituting these expressions in Definition
3
completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 2

The matrices
M_{n,k}
and
L_{n,k}
satisfy

L_{n,k}M_{n,k}=I_{r}.

(20)

Proof:

We begin by analyzing
\image(M_{n,k})
.
Fix an index
p\in\{1,\dots,r\}
,
and consider

\displaystyle y:

\displaystyle=M_{n,k}w^{p}

\displaystyle=\sum_{(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})\in Q(n,k)}\sum_{(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})\in S(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})}\sigma(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})v^{\ind^{R}_{n,k}(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})}(w^{\ind^{Q}_{n,k}(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})})^{T}w^{p}.

Let
(i_{1}^{\prime},\dots,i_{k}^{\prime})
be the sequence in
Q(n,k)
such that
\ind^{Q}_{n,k}(i_{1}^{\prime},\dots,i_{k}^{\prime})=p
. Then

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle=\sum_{(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})\in S(i_{1}^{\prime},\dots,i_{k}^{\prime})}\sigma(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})v^{\ind^{R}_{n,k}(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})}.

Thus, the multiplication by
M_{n,k}
“duplicates”
the single one in entry
p
of

to all the
k!
entries with indices
\ind^{R}_{n,k}(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})
,
(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})\in S(i_{1}^{\prime},\dots,i_{k}^{\prime})
, in

, each
with either a plus or a minus sign (see Fig.

Now,

\displaystyle L_{n,k}y

\displaystyle=\sum_{(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})\in Q(n,k)}w^{\ind^{Q}_{n,k}(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})}(v^{\ind^{R}_{n,k}(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})})^{T}\sum_{(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})\in S(i_{1}^{\prime},\dots,i_{k}^{\prime})}\sigma(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})v^{\ind^{R}_{n,k}(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})}

\displaystyle=w^{\ind^{Q}_{n,k}(i_{1}^{\prime},\dots,i_{k}^{\prime})}\sigma(i^{\prime}_{1},\dots,i^{\prime}_{k})

\displaystyle=w^{p}.

Since the index
p
is arbitrary, this proves (
20
).

Remark 1

Recall that a matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is called skew-symmetric if
A+A^{T}=0
. The half-vectorization of such a matrix, denoted
\vech(A)
, is the column vector obtained by stacking the entries below the main diagonal of
A
column by column.
For example, for the
3\times 3
skew-symmetric matrix

A_{3}=\begin{bmatrix}0&a_{12}&a_{13}\\
-a_{12}&0&a_{23}\\
-a_{13}&-a_{23}&0\end{bmatrix},

(26)

we have

\vech(A_{3})=\begin{bmatrix}-a_{12}&-a_{13}&-a_{23}\end{bmatrix}^{T}.

For an
n\times n
skew-symmetric matrix
A
,
the matrices
M_{n,2}
and
L_{n,2}
relate between vectorizations and half-vectorizations of

:

\vect(A)=M_{n,2}\vech(A),\text{ and }\vech(A)=L_{n,2}\vect(A).

(27)

For example, for the case
n=3
using
M_{3,2}
in (
4
) gives

\displaystyle M_{3,2}\vech(A_{3})

\displaystyle=M_{3,2}\begin{bmatrix}-a_{12}&-a_{13}&-a_{23}\end{bmatrix}^{T}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}0&-a_{12}&-a_{13}&a_{12}&0&-a_{23}&a_{13}&a_{23}&0\end{bmatrix}^{T}

\displaystyle=\vect(A_{3}).

The relations (
27
)
have been used in
[
6
]
to study skew-symmetric solutions of matrix ODEs of the form
\dot{X}=AX+XA^{T}
, where
A
and
X
are block matrices.

Remark 2

Similar matrices, sometimes referred to as duplication and elimination matrices, also appear when considering vectorizations and half-vectorizations of
symmetric
matrices, see for example
[
18
]
.

III-C

Algebraic expressions for the
k
-multiplicative compound

We can now state one of the main results in this section. This result allows to express the
k
-multiplicative compound of an
n\times k
matrix using a sum of Kronecker products. The matrices
L_{n,k}
and
M_{n,k}
link the two representations.

Theorem 1

For any
x^{1},\dots,x^{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
k\leq n
, we have

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}x^{1}&\dots&x^{k}\end{bmatrix}^{(k)}

\displaystyle=L_{n,k}\sum_{(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})\in S(1,\dots,k)}\sigma(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})(x^{j_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes x^{j_{k}}),

(28)

and

\displaystyle M_{n,k}\begin{bmatrix}x^{1}&\dots&x^{k}\end{bmatrix}^{(k)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})\in S(1,\dots,k)}\sigma(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})(x^{j_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes x^{j_{k}}).

(29)

Example 5

For
k=2
Theorem
1
becomes

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}x^{1}&x^{2}\end{bmatrix}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=L_{n,2}(x^{1}\otimes x^{2}-x^{2}\otimes x^{1}),

and

\displaystyle M_{n,2}\begin{bmatrix}x^{1}&x^{2}\end{bmatrix}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=x^{1}\otimes x^{2}-x^{2}\otimes x^{1}.

Intuitively speaking, this can be explained as follows.
The vector
\begin{bmatrix}x^{1}&x^{2}\end{bmatrix}^{(2)}\in\mathbb{R}^{\binom{n}{2}}
is the set of all the two
2\times 2
minors of
\begin{bmatrix}x^{1}&x^{2}\end{bmatrix}
. The entries of the vector
(x^{1}\otimes x^{2}-x^{2}\otimes x^{1})\in\mathbb{R}^{n^{2}}
also include all these minors, and multiplication by
the matrices
L_{n,2}
and
M_{n,2}
transforms one vector into the other.

Proof:

Let

z:=\sum_{(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})\in S(1,\dots,k)}\sigma(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})(x^{j_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes x^{j_{k}}).

We begin by considering the particular case
the where every
x^{i}
is a canonical vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, that is,
\begin{bmatrix}x^{1}&\dots&x^{k}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}e^{i_{1}}&\dots&e^{i_{k}}\end{bmatrix}
.
We consider three sub-cases.

Case 1.

Suppose that there exist two indices
i_{p}
,
i_{q}
with
p\not=q
and
i_{p}=i_{q}
. Then
\begin{bmatrix}x^{1}&\dots&x^{k}\end{bmatrix}^{(k)}=0
.
Any term
x^{j_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes x^{j_{k}}
includes
x^{i_{p}},x^{i_{q}}
, and permuting between these two vectors gives another term in the sum
z
, but with an opposite sign. Thus,
z=0
. So in this case, equations (
28
) and (
29
) hold.

Case 2.

Suppose that
x^{1}=e^{i_{1}},\dots,x^{k}=e^{i_{k}}
, with

i_{1}<\dots<i_{k}.

(30)

Then

\begin{bmatrix}x^{1}&\dots&x^{k}\end{bmatrix}^{(k)}=w^{\ind^{Q}_{n,k}(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})}.

Consider

\displaystyle L_{n,k}z

\displaystyle=\sum_{(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})\in Q(n,k)}w^{\ind^{Q}_{n,k}(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})}(v^{\ind^{R}_{n,k}(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})})^{T}\sum_{(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})\in S(1,\dots,k)}\sigma(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})v^{\ind^{R}_{n,k}(i_{j_{1}},\dots,i_{j_{k}})}

\displaystyle=\sigma(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})w^{\ind^{Q}_{n,k}(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})}

\displaystyle=w^{\ind^{Q}_{n,k}(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})},

where the last step follows from (
30
).
Thus, (
28
) holds. To verify (
29
), note that
M_{n,k}\begin{bmatrix}x^{1}&\dots&x^{k}\end{bmatrix}^{(k)}
is equal to

\displaystyle\sum_{(p_{1},\dots,p_{k})\in Q(n,k)}\sum_{(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})\in S(p_{1},\dots,p_{k})}\sigma(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})v^{\ind^{R}_{n,k}(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})}(w^{\ind^{Q}_{n,k}(p_{1},\dots,p_{k})})^{T}w^{\ind^{Q}_{n,k}(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})\in S(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})}\sigma(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})v^{\ind^{R}_{n,k}(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})},

implying that (
29
) holds.

Case 3.

Suppose that
x^{1}=e^{i_{1}},\dots,x^{k}=e^{i_{k}}
, with
i_{j}\not=i_{\ell}
for all
j\not=\ell
.
Then there exists a permutation
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{k}
of
i_{1},\dots,i_{k}
such that
\ell_{1}<\dots<\ell_{k}
.
Then
\begin{bmatrix}x^{1}&\dots&x^{k}\end{bmatrix}^{(k)}=\sigma(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})\begin{bmatrix}e^{\ell_{1}}&\dots&e^{\ell_{k}}\end{bmatrix}^{(k)}
. Let
s:=\sigma(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})
.
By Case 2,

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}x^{1}&\dots&x^{k}\end{bmatrix}^{(k)}

\displaystyle=s\begin{bmatrix}e^{\ell_{1}}&\dots&e^{\ell_{k}}\end{bmatrix}^{(k)}

\displaystyle=sL_{n,k}\sum_{(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})\in S(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{k})}\sigma(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})(x^{j_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes x^{j_{k}}),

and this gives

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}x^{1}&\dots&x^{k}\end{bmatrix}^{(k)}

\displaystyle=L_{n,k}\sum_{(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})\in S(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})}\sigma(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})(x^{j_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes x^{j_{k}}),

so (
28
) holds. A similar argument shows that (
29
) also holds.
This completes the proof of Theorem
1

when every
x^{i}
is a standard basis vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For arbitrary vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
the proof follows from the multilinearity of the multiplicative compound and of the Kronecker product.
∎

The next result describes a useful relation between Kronecker products and multiplicative compounds.

Theorem 2

For any
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
and
k\in\{1,\dots,\min\{n,m\}\}
, we have

A^{\otimes k}M_{m,k}=M_{n,k}A^{(k)}.

(31)

Proof:

Pick arbitrary
x^{1},\dots,x^{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and let
y:=\begin{bmatrix}x^{1}&\dots&x^{k}\end{bmatrix}^{(k)}
. Then using (
29
) and the mixed product rule
yields

\displaystyle A^{\otimes k}M_{m,k}y

\displaystyle=A^{\otimes k}\sum_{(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})\in S(1,\dots,k)}\sigma(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})(x^{j_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes x^{j_{k}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})\in S(1,\dots,k)}\sigma(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})(Ax^{j_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes Ax^{j_{k}}).

Using (
29
) again and the Cauchy-Binet theorem gives

\displaystyle A^{\otimes k}M_{m,k}y

\displaystyle=M_{n,k}\begin{bmatrix}Ax^{1}&\dots&Ax^{k}\end{bmatrix}^{(k)}

\displaystyle=M_{n,k}A^{(k)}\begin{bmatrix}x^{1}&\dots&x^{k}\end{bmatrix}^{(k)}

\displaystyle=M_{n,k}A^{(k)}y,

and since
x^{1},\dots,x^{k}
are arbitrary, this proves (
31
).
∎

The next result uses the expressions derived above
to provide a formula for the
k
-multiplicative compound of a matrix
A
using the
k
th Kronecker power of
A
.

Theorem 3

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
, and fix
k\in\{1,\dots,\min\{n,m\}\}
. Then

A^{(k)}=L_{n,k}A^{\otimes k}M_{m,k}.

(32)

Proof:

Multiply both sides of (
31
) by
L_{n,k}
from the left and apply (
20
).
∎

For example, for
k=2
, Eq. (
32
) gives

A^{(2)}=L_{n,2}\left(A\otimes A\right)M_{m,2}.

Remark 3

As a “sanity check”, we show that the expression in Theorem
3
implies the Cauchy-Binet theorem.
Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
and
B\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times p}
, and fix
k\in\{1,\dots,\min\{n,m,p\}\}
. Theorem
3
gives

\displaystyle A^{(k)}B^{(k)}

\displaystyle=L_{n,k}A^{\otimes k}M_{m,k}L_{m,k}B^{\otimes k}M_{p,k}.

By Theorem
2
,
B^{\otimes k}M_{p,k}=M_{m,k}B^{(k)}
,
and using this and (
20
) gives

A^{(k)}B^{(k)}=L_{n,k}A^{\otimes k}M_{m,k}B^{(k)}.

Using Theorem
2
again yields

A^{(k)}B^{(k)}=L_{n,k}A^{\otimes k}B^{\otimes k}M_{p,k}
,
and (
10
) and Theorem
3

give

\displaystyle A^{(k)}B^{(k)}

\displaystyle=L_{n,k}\left((AB)^{\otimes k}\right)M_{p,k}

\displaystyle=(AB)^{(k)}.

III-D

Algebraic expression for the
k
-additive compound

In this section, we derive
a formula for the
k
-additive compound using
the
k
th Kronecker sum defined in (
15
).
The starting point is the following result that is an
analogue of Theorem
2

to additive compounds.

Theorem 4

For any
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, and any
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, we have

A^{\oplus k}M_{n,k}=M_{n,k}A^{[k]}.

(33)

Proof:

Fix arbitrary
x^{1},\dots,x^{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and let
y:=\begin{bmatrix}x^{1}&\dots&x^{k}\end{bmatrix}^{(k)}
.
Then

\displaystyle A^{\oplus k}M_{n,k}y

\displaystyle=A^{\oplus k}M_{n,k}\begin{bmatrix}x^{1}&\dots&x^{k}\end{bmatrix}^{(k)}

\displaystyle=A^{\oplus k}\sum_{(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})\in S(1,\dots,k)}\sigma(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})(x^{j_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes x^{j_{k}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})\in S(1,\dots,k)}\sigma(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})\left((Ax^{j_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes x^{j_{k}})+\dots+(x^{j_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes Ax^{j_{k}})\right)

\displaystyle=M_{n,k}\left(\begin{bmatrix}Ax^{1}&\dots&x^{k}\end{bmatrix}^{(k)}+\dots+\begin{bmatrix}x^{1}&\dots&Ax^{k}\end{bmatrix}^{(k)}\right),

and using (
8
) yields
A^{\oplus k}M_{n,k}y=M_{n,k}A^{[k]}y
.
∎

The next result provides a formula for the
k
-additive compound using the
k
th Kronecker
sum.

Theorem 5

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, and fix
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. Then

A^{[k]}=L_{n,k}A^{\oplus k}M_{n,k}.

(34)

Proof:

Multiply both sides of (
33
) from the left by
L_{n,k}
and use (
20
).
∎

Remark 4

Since
(I_{n})^{\otimes\ell}=I_{n^{\ell}}
, we can also write (
34
) as

A^{[k]}=L_{n,k}\left(A\otimes I_{n^{k-1}}+I_{n}\otimes A\otimes I_{n^{k-2}}+\dots+I_{n^{k-1}}\otimes A\right)M_{n,k}.

(35)

Example 6

For
k=2
Eq. (
34
) gives

\displaystyle A^{[2]}

\displaystyle=L_{n,2}\left(A\otimes I_{n}+I_{n}\otimes A\right)M_{n,2}

(36)

\displaystyle=L_{n,2}(A\oplus A)M_{n,2}.

For
k=n
,
Eq. (
34
)
gives

\displaystyle A^{[n]}=L_{n,n}\left(A\otimes I_{n}\otimes\dots\otimes I_{n}+I_{n}\otimes A\otimes\dots\otimes I_{n}+\dots+I_{n}\otimes\dots\otimes I_{n}\otimes A\right)M_{n,n},

where every Kronecker product includes
n
terms,
and
using the expressions in Example
3

and (
10
) yields

\displaystyle A^{[n]}

\displaystyle=(e^{1})^{T}\otimes\dots\otimes(e^{n})^{T}\left(A\otimes I_{n}\otimes\dots\otimes I_{n}+\dots+I_{n}\otimes\dots\otimes I_{n}\otimes A\right)

\displaystyle\times\sum_{(j_{1},\dots,j_{n})\in S(1,\dots,n)}\sigma(j_{1},\dots,j_{n})(e^{j_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes e^{j_{n}})

\displaystyle=((e^{1})^{T}Ae^{1})\otimes((e^{2})^{T}e^{2})\otimes\dots\otimes((e^{n})^{T}e^{n})+\dots+((e^{1})^{T}e^{1})\otimes\dots\otimes((e^{n-1})^{T}e^{n-1})\otimes((e^{n})^{T}Ae^{n})

\displaystyle=a_{11}+\dots+a_{nn}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}(A),

where we used the fact that for any
(j_{1},\dots,j_{n})\in S(1,\dots,n)\setminus\{(1,2,\dots,n)\}
, the Kronecker product will have elements of the form
(e^{i})^{T}e^{j}
with
i\neq j
.

Remark 5

Consider the Lyapunov-like matrix ODE

\dot{X}(t)=AX(t)+X(t)A^{T},

(37)

with
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and
X:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
. By the vectorization property of the Kronecker product (see (
14
)), we may rewrite (
37
) in vector form as

\vect(\dot{X})=(A\oplus A)\vect(X).

(38)

Suppose in addition that
X(t)
is skew-symmetric for all
t
. Multiplying (
38
) on the left by
L_{n,2}
and using
Remark
1
and (
36
)
gives

\displaystyle\vech(\dot{X})

\displaystyle=L_{n,2}(A\oplus A)\vect(X)

\displaystyle=L_{n,2}(A\oplus A)M_{n,2}\vech(X)

\displaystyle=A^{[2]}\vech(X),

and this recovers
[
6
, Eq. (8)]
(see also
[
5
, Section 3.1]
).

IV
Applications

The formulas for compounds using Kronecker products and sums allow using the well-known theory of Kronecker products to analyze matrix compounds of any order
k
. We demonstrate this using several applications.

IV-A

A new proof for the expression for the entries of
A^{[k]}

Theorem
5
can be used to provide a simpler proof of a known explicit expression for the entries of the additive compound
A^{[k]}
in terms of the entries of
A

[
26
]
. To do so, first recall that, by definition, every entry of the multiplicative compound
A^{(k)}
corresponds so some
k\times k
minor of
A
, that is, every entry of
A^{(k)}
corresponds to rows of
A
with indices

(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})\in Q(n,k)
and columns of
A
with indices
(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})\in Q(m,k)
. Since
A^{[k]}
is defined via the
k
-multiplicative compound, the same holds for
A^{[k]}
as well.

Lemma 3

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, and fix
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. Let
z
denote the entry of
A^{[k]}
corresponding to
\alpha=(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})\in Q(n,k)
and
\beta=(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})\in Q(n,k)
, that is,

z=(A^{[k]})_{\ind^{Q}_{n,k}(i_{1},\dots,i_{k}),\ind^{Q}_{n,k}(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})}
. Then

z=\begin{cases}a_{i_{1}i_{1}}+\dots+a_{i_{k}i_{k}},&\text{if }i_{p}=j_{p}\text{ for all }p,\\
(-1)^{\ell+m}a_{i_{\ell}j_{m}},&\text{if exactly $k-1$ of the indices of $\alpha$ coincide with those of~$\beta$, with $i_{\ell}\not=j_{m}$ },\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(39)

Proof:

This result was proven in
[
26
]
. We show that the representation of compounds using Kronecker products and sums
can be used to derive a simpler proof.
By Theorem
5
,

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{bmatrix}e^{i_{1}}&\dots&e^{i_{k}}\end{bmatrix}^{(k)}\right)^{T}L_{n,k}A^{\oplus k}M_{n,k}\begin{bmatrix}e^{j_{1}}&\dots&e^{j_{k}}\end{bmatrix}^{(k)},

and applying (
3
) gives

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle=(e^{i_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes e^{i_{k}})^{T}(A\otimes I_{n}\otimes\dots\otimes I_{n}+\dots+I_{n}\otimes\dots\otimes I_{n}\otimes A)

\displaystyle\times\sum_{(p_{1},\dots,p_{k})\in S(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})}\sigma(p_{1},\dots,p_{k})(e^{p_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes e^{p_{k}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{(p_{1},\dots,p_{k})\in S(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})}\sigma(p_{1},\dots,p_{k})\left(a_{i_{1}p_{1}}\delta_{i_{2}p_{2}}\dots\delta_{i_{k}p_{k}}+\dots+\delta_{i_{1}p_{1}}\dots\delta_{i_{k-1}p_{k-1}}a_{i_{k}p_{k}}\right),

(40)

where we used the mixed product property and the fact that
(e^{i})^{T}Ae^{j}=a_{ij}
. Note that every product of
\delta_{ij}
s in (
IV-A
) includes
k-1
terms.
We consider three cases.

If
i_{p}=j_{p}
for
p=1,\dots,k
then (
IV-A
) gives

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle=\sum_{(p_{1},\dots,p_{k})\in S(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})}\sigma(p_{1},\dots,p_{k})\left(a_{i_{1}p_{1}}\delta_{i_{2}p_{2}}\dots\delta_{i_{k}p_{k}}+\dots+\delta_{i_{1}p_{1}}\dots\delta_{i_{k-1}{p}_{k-1}}a_{i_{k}p_{k}}\right)

\displaystyle=a_{i_{1}i_{1}}+\dots+a_{i_{k}i_{k}}.

If exactly
k-1
of the indices of
\alpha
coincide with those of
\beta
, with
i_{\ell}\not=j_{m}
,
then the only term that may be nonzero in the sum in (
IV-A
) is

\delta_{i_{1}p_{1}}\dots\delta_{i_{\ell-1}p_{\ell-1}}a_{i_{\ell}p_{\ell}}\delta_{i_{\ell+1}p_{\ell+1}}\dots\delta_{i_{k}p_{k}},

and this term will indeed be nonzero when
(p_{1},\dots,p_{k})

is a permutation of
\beta=(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})
such that
p_{\ell}=j_{m}
. Since the entries of
\beta
and
\alpha=(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})
coincide except for
i_{\ell}\not=j_{m}
, we conclude that in this case

z=\sigma(p_{1},\dots,p_{k})a_{i_{\ell}j_{m}}=(-1)^{\ell+m}a_{i_{\ell}j_{m}}.

If
\alpha
and
\beta
differ by two or more indices then the fact
that every product of
\delta_{ij}
s in (
IV-A
) includes
k-1
terms implies that
z=0
. This completes the proof of (
39
).
∎

Note that the proof above demonstrates how the representation of the compounds using Kronecker products and sums allows proving results about
k
-compounds, for any order
k
, using only properties of the Kronecker product and sum.

IV-B

Generalizing an identity for
2\times 2
matrices to the
n\times n
case

It is well-known and straightforward to prove that for any
A,B\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
, we have the identity

\det(A+B)=\det(A)+\det(B)+\operatorname{tr}(A)\operatorname{tr}(B)-\operatorname{tr}(AB).

The next result uses Theorem
3
to generalize
this identity to matrices with an arbitrary dimension
n
.

Theorem 6

Let
A,B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
with
n\geq 2
. Then

(A+B)^{(2)}=A^{(2)}+B^{(2)}+A^{[2]}B^{[2]}-(AB)^{[2]}.

(41)

Proof:

By Theorem
3
,

\displaystyle(A+B)^{(2)}

\displaystyle=L_{n,2}(A+B)^{\otimes 2}M_{n,2}

\displaystyle=L_{n,2}(A\otimes A+A\otimes B+B\otimes A+B\otimes B)M_{n,2}

\displaystyle=A^{(2)}+L_{n,2}(A\otimes B+B\otimes A)M_{n,2}+B^{(2)}.

Using the easy to verify identity

\displaystyle A\otimes B+B\otimes A

\displaystyle=(A\otimes I_{n}+I_{n}\otimes A)(B\otimes I_{n}+I_{n}\otimes B)-\left((AB)\otimes I_{n}+I_{n}\otimes(AB)\right)

\displaystyle=A^{\oplus 2}B^{\oplus 2}-(AB)^{\oplus 2},

gives

\displaystyle(A+B)^{(2)}

\displaystyle=A^{(2)}+B^{(2)}+L_{n,2}A^{\oplus 2}B^{\oplus 2}M_{n,2}-L_{n,2}(AB)^{\oplus 2}M_{n,2}.

(42)

Applying Theorem
4
and Lemma
2
yields

\displaystyle(A+B)^{(2)}

\displaystyle=A^{(2)}+B^{(2)}+L_{n,2}A^{\oplus 2}M_{n,2}B^{[2]}-L_{n,2}M_{n,2}(AB)^{[2]}

\displaystyle=A^{(2)}+B^{(2)}+L_{n,2}M_{n,2}A^{[2]}B^{[2]}-(AB)^{[2]}

\displaystyle=A^{(2)}+B^{(2)}+A^{[2]}B^{[2]}-(AB)^{[2]},

and this completes the proof.
∎

Example 7

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n},r:=\binom{n}{2}
. By Theorem
6
,

\displaystyle(I_{n}+\varepsilon A)^{(2)}

\displaystyle=(I_{n})^{(2)}+(\varepsilon A)^{(2)}+(I_{n})^{[2]}(\varepsilon A)^{[2]}-(\varepsilon A)^{[2]}

\displaystyle=I_{r}+\varepsilon^{2}A^{(2)}+2I_{r}\varepsilon A^{[2]}-\varepsilon A^{[2]},

so

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}(I_{n}+\varepsilon A)^{(2)}|_{\varepsilon=0}

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}(I_{r}+\varepsilon^{2}A^{(2)}+\varepsilon A^{[2]})|_{\varepsilon=0}

\displaystyle=A^{[2]},

as expected.

IV-C

A new formula for the
k
-additive compound of a product of matrices

The Cauchy-Binet Theorem asserts that
(AB)^{(k)}=A^{(k)}B^{(k)}
, that is, the multiplicative compound of a product of two matrices decomposes into a product of two matrices, each depending only on one of the two original matrices. A more difficult problem is to decompose
(AB)^{[k]}
using terms that depend only on
A
or only on
B
.
For the case
k=2
such a decomposition appears in
[
5
]
, but the method there seems difficult to extend to
k>2
. We use the
representation of the compounds using Kronecker products to decompose
(AB)^{[k]}
in the desired form.

We introduce more notation. For
n>1
,
k,i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, and a vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, define a matrix
H_{n,k,i}(v)\in\mathbb{R}^{(n^{k-1})\times(n^{k})}
by

H_{n,k,i}(v):=I_{n}\otimes\dots\otimes I_{n}\otimes v^{T}\otimes I_{n}\otimes\dots\otimes I_{n},

(43)

where the product includes
k
terms,
with
v^{T}
appearing in the
i
th
place. For example,

H_{n,k,1}(v):=v^{T}\otimes I_{n}\otimes I_{n}\otimes\dots\otimes I_{n},

Theorem 7

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
and
B\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, and fix
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
.
Let
w^{i}
,
i=1,\dots,m
,
denote the
i
th column of
A
, and let

(v^{j})^{T}
,
j=1,\dots,m
, denote the
j
th row of
B
. Note that
w^{i},v^{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then

(AB)^{[k]}=L_{n,k}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}(-1)^{\ell+1}H_{n,k,\ell}((w^{i})^{T})H_{n,k,1}(v^{i})M_{n,k}.

(44)

Proving this requires several auxiliary results. The first result shows that for any
i
the product
H_{n,k,i}(v)M_{n,k}
is equal to
H_{n,k,1}(v)M_{n,k}
, up to a sign change.

Lemma 4

The matrix
H_{n,k,i}(v)
satisfies

H_{n,k,i}(v)M_{n,k}=(-1)^{i+1}H_{n,k,1}(v)M_{n,k},

(45)

Proof:

Fix an index
s\in\{1,\dots,k\}
.
To simplify the notation,
denote
Z:=H_{n,k,s}(v)M_{n,k}
.
Using (
3
) and the mixed product property gives

\displaystyle Z

\displaystyle=\sum_{(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})\in Q(n,k)}\sum_{(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})\in S(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})}\sigma(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})(e^{j_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes e^{j_{s-1}}\otimes v^{T}e^{j_{s}}\otimes e^{j_{s+1}}\otimes\dots\otimes e^{j_{k}})

\displaystyle\times\left(\begin{bmatrix}e^{i_{1}}&\dots&e^{i_{k}}\end{bmatrix}^{(k)}\right)^{T}.

Since
v^{T}e^{j_{s}}=v_{j_{s}}
is a scalar, we get

\displaystyle Z

\displaystyle=\sum_{(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})\in Q(n,k)}\sum_{(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})\in S(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})}\sigma(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})(v^{T}e^{j_{s}}\otimes e^{j_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes e^{j_{s-1}}\otimes e^{j_{s+1}}\otimes\dots\otimes e^{j_{k}})

\displaystyle\times\left(\begin{bmatrix}e^{i_{1}}&\dots&e^{i_{k}}\end{bmatrix}^{(k)}\right)^{T}

\displaystyle=(v^{T}\otimes I_{n}\otimes\dots\otimes I_{n})

\displaystyle\times\sum_{(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})\in Q(n,k)}\sum_{(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})\in S(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})}\sigma(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})(e^{j_{s}}\otimes e^{j_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes e^{j_{s-1}}\otimes e^{j_{s+1}}\otimes\dots\otimes e^{j_{k}})

\displaystyle\times\left(\begin{bmatrix}e^{i_{1}}&\dots&e^{i_{k}}\end{bmatrix}^{(k)}\right)^{T},

and using the definition of
M_{n,k}
proves (
45
).
∎

The next result considers
(AB)^{[k]}
in the particular case where
A=v
and
B=w^{T}
, with
v,w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Lemma 5

Let
v,w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and fix
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. Then

(vw^{T})^{[k]}=L_{n,k}\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}(-1)^{\ell+1}H_{n,k,\ell}(w^{T})H_{n,k,1}(v)M_{n,k}.

Proof:

Theorem
5

gives

\displaystyle(wv^{T})^{[k]}

\displaystyle=L_{n,k}(wv^{T})^{\oplus k}M_{n,k}

\displaystyle=L_{n,k}(wv^{T}\otimes I_{n}\otimes\dots\otimes I_{n}+\dots+I_{n}\otimes\dots\otimes I_{n}\otimes wv^{T})M_{n,k},

where every Kronecker product in the brackets includes
k
terms. Using the mixed product property gives

\displaystyle(wv^{T})^{[k]}

\displaystyle=L_{n,k}\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}H_{n,k,\ell}(w^{T})H_{n,k,\ell}(v)M_{n,k},

(46)

and applying Lemma
4
completes the proof.
∎

We can now prove Theorem
7
.

Proof:

Let
w^{i}
denote the
i
th column of
A
, and let

(v^{i})^{T}
denote the
i
th row of
B
. Note that
w^{i},v^{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then

AB=\sum_{i=1}^{m}w^{i}(v^{i})^{T}
, so
(AB)^{[k]}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}(w^{i}(v^{i})^{T})^{[k]}
.
Applying Lemma
5
completes the proof.
∎

The next result specializes Theorem
7
to the case
k=2
.

Corollary 1

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
and
B\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
.
Let
w^{i}
denote the
i

, and let

(v^{i})^{T}
denote the

. Then

(AB)^{[2]}=M_{n,2}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{m}(H_{n,2,1}(w^{i}))^{T}H_{n,2,1}(v^{i})M_{n,2}.

Proof:

By Theorem

,

\displaystyle(AB)^{[2]}

\displaystyle=L_{n,2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{\ell=1}^{2}(-1)^{\ell+1}H_{n,2,\ell}((w^{i})^{T})H_{n,2,1}(v^{i})M_{n,2}

\displaystyle=L_{n,2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}(H_{n,2,1}((w^{i})^{T})-(H_{n,2,2}((w^{i})^{T}))H_{n,2,1}(v^{i})M_{n,2}.

(47)

P:=L_{n,2}(H_{n,2,1}((w^{i})^{T})-(H_{n,2,2}((w^{i})^{T}))
. Then

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle=\sum_{(i_{1},i_{2})\in Q(n,2)}\begin{bmatrix}e^{i_{1}}&e^{i_{2}}\end{bmatrix}^{(2)}(e^{i_{1}}\otimes e^{i_{2}})^{T}(w^{i}\otimes I_{n}-I_{n}\otimes w^{i})

Thus,

\displaystyle P^{T}

\displaystyle=(w^{i}\otimes I_{n})^{T}\sum_{(i_{1},i_{2})\in Q(n,2)}(e^{i_{1}}\otimes e^{i_{2}}-e^{i_{2}}\otimes e^{i_{1}})\left(\begin{bmatrix}e^{i_{1}}&e^{i_{2}}\end{bmatrix}^{(2)}\right)^{T}

\displaystyle=H_{n,2,1}(w)M_{n,2},

V
Discussion

Compound matrices play an important role in many fields of applied mathematics, and have recently found many applications in systems and control theory
[
8
]
. However, the standard expressions for these compounds are not always easy to use. For example, the compounds of a block matrix destroy the block structure.
We derived new formulas for matrix compounds using Kronecker products and sums. This allows to use the well-established theory of Kronecker products and sums in analyzing matrix compounds. We demonstrated several applications of these new formulas.

An important problem
in systems and control theory
is deriving tractable expressions for the compounds of block matrices.
For example, the block matrix may be the Jacobian
J
of a nonlinear dynamical system composed of an interconnection of several sub-systems, and tractable expressions for
J^{[k]}
may be used to prove
k
-contraction based on properties of the sub-systems.
An interesting direction for further research is to combine our results with
the existing theory of Kronecker products of block matrices
and their applications to large-scale systems
[
15
,
27
]
.

V-A

Expressions for
\ind^{Q}
and
\ind^{R}

We detail expressions for
\ind^{Q}
and
\ind^{R}
. These expressions are not necessary for deriving the main results in this paper, and are included here for completeness.
The starting point is
a recursive expression for
\ind^{Q}_{n,k}
.

Lemma 6

Fix an integer
n>1
and
k\in\{2,\dots,n\}
. Then for any sequence
1\leq i_{1}<\dots<i_{k}\leq n
, we have

\ind^{Q}_{n,k}(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})=\binom{n}{k}-\binom{n-i_{1}+1}{k}+\ind^{Q}_{n-i_{1},k-1}(i_{2}-i_{1},\dots,i_{k}-i_{1}),

(48)

with

\ind^{Q}_{n,1}(i_{1})=i_{1}.

(49)

Note that the solution of (
48
) and (
49
) is

\ind^{Q}_{n,k}(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})=i_{k}+\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\left(\binom{n-\sum_{\ell=1}^{j-1}i_{\ell}}{k-j+1}-\binom{n-\sum_{\ell=1}^{j}i_{\ell}+1}{k-j+1}-i_{j}\right).

(50)

Proof:

Eq. (
49
) is obvious. For
k>1
, there are in total
\binom{n}{k}
sequences in
Q(n,k)
, and in
\binom{n-i_{1}+1}{k}
sequences the first entry is larger than or equal to
i_{1}
. Hence,
\ind^{Q}_{n,k}(i_{1},i_{1}+1,i_{1}+2,\dots,i_{1}+k-1)=\binom{n}{k}-\binom{n-i_{1}+1}{k}
. Therefore,

\ind^{Q}_{n,k}(i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{k})=\binom{n}{k}-\binom{n-i_{1}+1}{k}+\ind^{Q}_{n-i_{1},k-1}(i_{2}-i_{1},\dots,i_{k}-i_{1}),

and this completes the proof.
∎

The next result provides an expression for
\ind^{R}_{n,k}
.
For a set
S
, let
|S|
denote the cardinality of
S
.

Lemma 7

Fix an integer
n>1
and
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. Then for any sequence
(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})\in R(n,k)
, we have

\ind^{R}_{n,k}(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})=\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}(i_{\ell}-1)n^{k-\ell}+1.

(51)

Proof:

For
k=1
, Eq. (
51
) gives
\ind^{R}_{n,1}(i_{1})=(i_{1}-1)n^{0}+1=i_{1}
, which is obviously true. Suppose that (
51
) is true for
k-1
. There are
(i_{1}-1)|R(n,k-1)|
sequences in
R(n,k)
with the first element strictly smaller than
i_{1}
, so

\displaystyle\ind^{R}_{n,k}(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})

\displaystyle=(i_{1}-1)|R(n,k-1)|+\ind^{R}_{n,k-1}(i_{2},\dots,i_{k})

\displaystyle=(i_{1}-1)n^{k-1}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{k-1}(i_{\ell+1}-1)n^{k-1-\ell}+1

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}(i_{\ell}-1)n^{k-\ell}+1,

where we used the fact that the cardinality of
R(n,k)
is
|R(n,k)|=n^{k}
.
∎

For example, for
k=n
and the sequence
(1,2,\dots,n)
Eq. (
51
)
gives

\displaystyle\ind^{R}_{n,n}(1,\dots,n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}(\ell-1)n^{n-\ell}+1

\displaystyle=\frac{n^{n}-n}{(n-1)^{2}}.

(52)
