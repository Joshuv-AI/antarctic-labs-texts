L ∞ L^{\infty} -sizes of the spaces Siegel cusp forms of degree n n via Poincaré series
Soumya Das

Abstract
. We prove the conjectures on the ( L ∞ L^{\infty} )-sizes of the spaces of Siegel cusp forms of degree n n , weight k k , for any congruence subgroup in the weight aspect as well as for all principal congruence subgroups in the level aspect, in particular. This size is measured by the size of the Bergman kernel of the space. More precisely we show that the aforementioned size is ≍ n k 3 ​ n ​ ( n + 1 ) / 4 \asymp_{n}k^{3n(n+1)/4} . Our method uses the Fourier expansion of the Bergman kernel, and has wide applicability. We illustrate this by a simple algorithm. We also include some of the applications of our method, including individual sup-norms for small weights and the non-vanishing of Poincaré series of exponential type in higher degrees.

1.
Introduction

The sup-norm problem for automorphic forms has seen a lot of activity in the recent years. Obtaining estimates of eigenfunctions of the Laplace-Beltrami operator in terms of the Laplace eigenvalues on a Riemannian manifold has a long history, germinating from classical analysis. We mention the case of
X
being compact and locally symmetric space, for which Sarnak proved
[
39
]
the generic bound:

(1.1)

\displaystyle\|\phi\|_{\infty}=\sup_{x\in X}|\phi(x)|\ll\lambda_{\phi}^{\tfrac{\dim X-r}{4}}

where
r
denotes the rank of
X
. Similar results are also known in the non-compact setting when one restricts the domain to compact subsets (cf.
[
11
]
).
For number theorists, the main concern is to solve the problem for arithmetic quotients i.e. for symmetric spaces of the form
\Gamma\backslash G
where
G
is a Lie group and
\Gamma
rises from arithmetic input, e.g.
\Gamma
may be a congruence subgroup of some kind. In this arithmetic setting, due to the availability of a nice family of Hecke operators, one can exploit non-trivial relations among them to implement an ‘amplification method’ to improve upon the bounds for an eigenform that one obtains generally over arbitrary manifolds via classical analysis. The first example of this approach, when
X=\Gamma\backslash\mathbf{H}
with
\Gamma\subseteq\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{R})
being a co-compact subgroup arising from the unit group of a quaternion algebra over
\mathbf{Q}
, was shown by Iwaniec and Sarnak
[
25
]
. Their result improves upon (
1.1
), and shows that

\|\phi\|_{\infty}\ll_{\epsilon}\lambda_{\phi}^{5/24+\epsilon}

for any
\epsilon>0
. Note that here
\dim X=2,r=1
and so the generic bound had exponent
1/4
.

In a similar theme as mentioned above, this paper will focus on (scalar-valued weight
k
) holomorphic modular forms on the Siegel upper half space of degree
n\geq 1
which are automorphic under
\mathrm{Sp}_{n}(\mathbf{Z})
, see
[
30
]
, and also Section
2
fo precise definitions. These can be embedded into the space of “weight-
k
” Siegel-Maaßforms with Laplace eigenvalue
\displaystyle\frac{nk}{4}(n-k+1)
under the weight
k
Laplace operator, see
[
32
]
for more details. The corresponding sup-norm problem, as discussed above, thus becomes one on the non-compact quotient
\mathrm{Sp}_{n}(\mathbf{Z})\backslash\mathbf{H}_{n}
.
We will briefly discuss some of the known results of the problem in the “weight-aspect” in this setting. For the level aspect, or Laplace eigenvalue aspects, we refer the reader to the works
[
38
]
,
[
28
]
.

For the case
n=1
, the best possible bound for the sup-norm of a cuspidal,
L^{2}
-normalised Hecke eigenform
f
on
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{Z})
is due to H. Xia
[
46
]
. put
\displaystyle\|f\|_{\infty}:=\sup_{z=x+iy\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{Z})\backslash\mathbf{H}}y^{k/2}|f(z)|
. Then it is shown in
[
46
]
that:
k^{1/4-\epsilon}\ll_{\epsilon}\|f\|_{\infty}\ll_{\epsilon}k^{1/4+\epsilon}
for any
\epsilon>0
.
This has now been established for congruence subgroups in
[
28
]
by using suitable theta kernels.
While
[
42
]
shows that for
f
as described above, but of half integer weight
k\geq 5/2
contained in the Kohnen plus space of level
4
, one has the bound

\|f\|_{\infty}\ll_{\epsilon}k^{3/7+\epsilon}
.

One can also consider the average version of the above problem – i.e., consider the sup-norm problem over an orthonormal basis of the space in question,– and here we might expect better and accurate results. Apart from its own interest, such average results are crucial in implementing the “amplification-method” to extract a non-trivial bound for a single
f
.
As we will see below, this average version is tractable in the case of elliptic modular forms, but ceases to become so for
n>1
.

1.1.
The sup-norm problem for holomorphic cusp forms in higher degrees

Whereas there are plenty of results for the arithmetic quotients of
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{Z})
, very few such are available for the higher dimensional spaces.
We now focus our attention to the particular case of our interest: the Siegel modular group
\mathrm{Sp}_{n}(\mathbf{Z})
and its subgroups. For a cofinite group
\Gamma\subset\mathrm{Sp}_{n}(\mathbf{Z})
, there are two distinct sup-norm problems to consider, each of which is in a nascent stage.

1.1.1.
The
L^{2}
-size of the space via the Bergman kernel

Let
S^{(n)}_{k}(\Gamma)
denote the space of holomorphic Siegel cusp form on
\Gamma\subset\mathrm{Sp}_{n}(\mathbf{Z})
, which is co-finite, i.e. the volume of
\Gamma\backslash\mathbf{H}_{n}
is finite. In this case we want
to obtain the
correct

L^{\infty}
size (i.e., genuine asymptotics, instead of bounds) of the space
S^{(n)}_{k}(\Gamma)
as
k\to\infty
as defined below.
Let
B_{k}(\Gamma)
denote an orthonormal basis for the space
S^{(n)}_{k}(\Gamma)
, and put (our Petersson norms are not volume normalized, see (
2.11
))

(1.2)

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}_{k,\Gamma}(Z):=\sum\nolimits_{F\in B_{k}(\Gamma)}\det(Y)^{k}|F(Z)|^{2},\ (Z\in\mathbf{H}_{n});\quad\sup(S^{(n)}_{k}(\Gamma)):=\sup\nolimits_{Z\in\mathbf{H}_{n}}\mathbb{B}_{k,\Gamma}(Z).

Its easy to check that the quantity
\det(Y)^{k}|F(Z)|^{2}
is invariant under
\Gamma
, that
\mathbb{B}_{k,\Gamma}(Z)
is independent of the choice of the orthonormal basis, and thus
\sup(S^{(n)}_{k}(\Gamma))
depends only on
n,k
and
\Gamma
. We wish to study its size in terms of these parameters – and call
\sup(S^{(n)}_{k}(\Gamma))
the “
L^{2}
–size” or simply the “size” of the space
S^{(n)}_{k}(\Gamma)
. The goal of the paper is to prove optimal bounds for this “size” of
S^{(n)}_{k}(\Gamma)
.

We expect (see also
[
15
]
) that,

Conjecture 1.1
.

As
k\to\infty
,

(1.3)

\displaystyle\sup\nolimits_{Z\in\mathbf{\mathbf{H}_{n}}}\mathbb{B}_{k,\Gamma}(Z)\asymp_{n,\Gamma}k^{3n(n+1)/4}.

We now assume that
\Gamma
is a congruence subgroup of level
N
. In the level aspect, we expect that,

Conjecture 1.2
.

With
k
fixed, as the level
N
of
\Gamma\to\infty
,

(1.4)

\displaystyle\sup\nolimits_{Z\in\mathbf{\mathbf{H}_{n}}}\mathbb{B}_{k,\Gamma}(Z)\asymp_{n,k}1.

It is of course nice if one can obtain a hybrid (i.e. uniform in various of these aspects (weight, degree, level)) result (cf.
[
33
]
). The genesis of the above conjectures for us are the corresponding lower bounds, which can be obtained without much difficulty, see Section
6.1
and the known result for the
n=1
case from
[
46
]
,
[
20
]
. In the “compact” setting (i.e., either in the co-compact case or the restriction to a compact domain) one can essentially work out the expected “sizes” by computing the contribution of the term corresponding to the identity in the geometric side of the Bergman kernel (cf. the work of Cogdell-Luo
[
12
]
). This is reminiscent of the computation of the dimension of spaces from trace formulae, see e.g., the remarkable work of
[
43
]
.
In the non-compact setting, this is not the case, but perhaps the “parabolic” contribution gives the answer. It does not seem that these conjectures (which concern arithmetic subgroups of
\mathrm{Sp}_{n}(\mathbf{Z})
) are stated anywhere in the literature pertaining to the complex geometry of the Siegel modular variety.
But we want to emphasize that in the average situation one would really expect the exact size in terms of the parameters and not merely lower or upper bounds. Perhaps an asymptotic formula holds with appropriate error terms in the above conjectures.

1.1.2.
Sup-norm bounds for an eigenform

To obtain bounds going beyond what is implied by the above inequalities for a single newform
F
, and ultimately reaching the limit (inspired by average lower bounds or dimension considerations) we conjecture that in the weight aspect (cf.
[
6
]
for level one Ikeda lifts):

Conjecture 1.3
.

If
F
is an eigenfunction of all Hecke operators, as
k\to\infty
,

(1.5)

\displaystyle\left\lVert F\right\rVert_{\infty}:=\sup\nolimits_{Z\in\mathbf{\mathbf{H}_{n}}}\det(Y)^{k/2}|F(Z)|\ll k^{\frac{n(n+1)}{8}}.

Similarly in the
N
aspect (here
\Gamma=\Gamma_{0}^{(n)}(N)
) we conjecture that,

Conjecture 1.4
.

With
F,\Gamma
as above, as
N\to\infty
,

(1.6)

\displaystyle\left\lVert F\right\rVert_{\infty}\ll_{k}N^{-\frac{n(n+1)}{4}}.

Some comments are in order on how the above conjectures fit together. We only illustrate this for level
1
. Namely, squaring and adding (
1.6
) over an orthonormal Hecke-basis, one gets

\sup\nolimits_{Z\in\mathbf{\mathbf{H}_{n}}}\mathbb{B}_{k,\Gamma}(Z)\ll k^{\frac{n(n+1)}{4}}\cdot\dim S^{n}_{k}\ll k^{\frac{n(n+1)}{4}}\cdot k^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}=k^{3n(n+1)/4},

since
\dim S^{n}_{k}=O(k^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}})
with the implied constant depending only on
n
. See
[
3
, Chapter 2, Theorem 4.5]
for level
1
and
[
43
]
for an asymptotic formula in the case of principal congruence subgroups. Essentially
[
43
]
says that the dimension is proportional to the index of the discrete subgroup – this is what we have used in the case of
\Gamma_{0}^{(n)}(N)
as well – using the index formula from
[
29
, (1)]
. With this in mind, same argument as above applies to the level case as well.

1.2.
Some known results in degree
n\geq 2

Among the few prior results on this topic, one had the papers
[
15
]
, where the
n=2
weight aspect case, i.e., Conjecture

The idea was to use the Fourier expansion “high in the cusp”, and the geometric side of the Bergman kernel near the boundary of the fundamental domain. However, we note here that in order to handle the Fourier expansion, one needs “uniform in
k
” bounds for the Fourier coefficients
a_{F}(T)
of a cusp form

. Nothing much is known about this when

[

However these objects are only tractable when

Hybrid

, and realizing the Bergman kernel as a limit of the corresponding heat-kernel. Bounds for the heat-kernel then provides bounds for the Bergman kernel.

1.3.
Main results

We now state the main results of the article. In line with the Conjecture
1.5
above, this essentially confirms (
1.5
) (up to
\epsilon
).

Theorem 1.5

(Weight aspect)
.

Let
k\geq 2n+2
and
\Gamma\subset\mathrm{Sp}_{n}(\mathbf{Z})
be any congruence subgroup of level
N
, for any
n\geq 1
. Let
\epsilon>0
. Then one has

(1.7)

\mathbf{c}_{\Gamma}\,k^{\frac{3n(n+1)}{4}}\ll_{n}\sup(S^{(n)}_{k}(\Gamma))\ll_{n,\epsilon}k^{\frac{3n(n+1)}{4}}\,N^{\frac{n^{2}}{2}+\epsilon},

where
\mathbf{c}_{\Gamma}
is a constant depending only on
\Gamma
, and moreover
\mathbf{c}_{\Gamma}\gg_{n}1
for most familiar congruence subgroups – e.g. for
\Gamma=\Gamma^{(n)}_{0}(N),\Gamma^{(n)}_{1}(N),\Gamma^{(n)}(N),\Gamma^{(n),0}(N)
or for any
\Gamma
whose “maximal width at the cusp
\infty
” is
1
; or
when
N\asymp_{n}1
.

For the definition of the “maximal width at the cusp
\infty
”, see (
2.12
).

We note that Theorem
1.5
should hold for any ‘arithmetic’ subgroup of
\mathrm{Sp}_{n}(\mathbf{R})
, see Remark
3.8
. We however stick to congruence subgroups, as they are of primary interest, and no further new ideas are necessary to handle the arithmetic groups. The upper bound in Theorem
1.5
is proven in Section
3
, and the lower bounds in Section
6
.

Coming to the condition on the weight in Theorem
1.5
, note that in the classical theory of Siegel modular forms, see e.g.
[
30
,
19
]
, one usually encounters ‘large’ weights (commonly
k\geq 2n
) to ensure absolute convergence of various objects – be it Eisenstein/Poincaré series or for the validity of higher dimensional sums and integrals. Of course one can adopt the so-called ‘Hecke’s trick’ to treat the smaller weights in some cases; and this in turn would perhaps improve the conditions on weights in the results of this paper. In the later parts of this paper we include a different approach on the ‘small weights’, see Subsection
1.7
.
Also, for using comparison of ‘sum versus integral’ (cf.
[
10
]
) in higher dimensions one has to be careful in this respect.

Next, in Theorem
1.6
given below, we prove a hybrid version of the Conjectures (i) above, for all the principal congruence subgroups.
The reader may notice that while Theorem
1.5
is weaker in the level aspect than that of Theorem
1.6
, it covers all familiar congruence subgroups, and is readily applicable to a wide variety of situations.
Indeed, the proof of Theorem
1.5
is simpler than that of Theorem
1.6
, uses a ‘soft’ approach mentioned above, and probably will find use elsewhere. In addition, some of the key calculations in the proof of Theorem
1.5
are crucially used in Section
5
.

Theorem 1.6

(Hybrid aspect)
.

Let
\Gamma^{(n)}(N)\subset\mathrm{Sp}_{n}(\mathbf{Z})
be the principal congruence subgroup of level
N
. Then for all
k>2n+2
, one has the uniform bound

(1.8)

\sup(S^{(n)}_{k}(\Gamma))\asymp_{n}k^{\frac{3n(n+1)}{4}}.

We prove a slightly stronger result, see Theorem
4.1
, from which Theorem
1.6
follows immediately. The advantage with principal congruence subgroups is that there is only one conjugate (of itself) and the congruence
c_{g}\equiv 0\bmod N
holds for all elements of the group. This can be leveraged suitably to kill the level dependence in Theorem
1.5
. Probably a more conceptual argument, still within the current framework, can be found. Our calculations simplify if we assume that the group of translations look like
\omega\cdot\mathrm{Sym}_{n}(\mathbf{Z})
at some cusp. This may not hold for all congruence subgroups when
n\geq 2
, see Remark
2.1
.

There are two main ideas behind the success of this method over that in
[
15
]
. As discussed above, the first one is the use of Poincaré series for all the conjugate subgroups of
\Gamma
– this allows one to work only in the convenient level one fundamental domain
\mathcal{F}^{(n)}_{1}
and completely dispenses with the need to work with the Petersson trace formula, something which is rather hard to handle anyway in higher degrees, see the introduction of
[
15
]
.
The second one roughly stems from the fact that Poincaré series
P_{G_{1}}
for a group
G_{1}
is in some sense ‘smaller’ than one for a bigger group
G_{2}\supseteq G_{1}
as the number of summands is higher for
G_{2}
. This feature is utilized actually for majorants of these objects, thereby reducing the bulk of the calculations to
\mathrm{Sp}_{n}(\mathbf{Z})
itself.

When the weight is large enough, of the order of the level at a
\log
scale (i.e.,
\log k\gg\log N
), we can prove the desired bound for all congruence subgroups.

Theorem 1.7

(Hybrid aspect)
.

Let
\Gamma\subset\mathrm{Sp}_{n}(\mathbf{Z})
be any congruence subgroup of level
N
. Let
\epsilon>0
. Then for all
k>\max\{(n+1)^{2},N^{3+\epsilon}\}
, one has the uniform bound

(1.9)

\mathbf{c}_{\Gamma}\,k^{\frac{3n(n+1)}{4}}\ll_{n}\sup(S^{(n)}_{k}(\Gamma))\ll_{n}k^{\frac{3n(n+1)}{4}}.

Note that the constraint here is of a different kind than the above Theorems: the weight has to be large compared to the level, and not only to the degree.
The proof follows by piecing together various bounds on the Bergman kernel that one obtains while proving Theorem
1.5
and depends on bounding various sums over symmetric matrices in a bit different manner than that in Theorem
1.5
. See Subsection
8.2
.

1.4.
Co-compact case

When the fundamental domain
\Gamma\backslash\mathbf{H}_{n}
is compact and
k\geq 2n
, one has the bound

(1.10)

\displaystyle\sup(S^{n}_{k}(\Gamma))\ll_{n}k^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}.

This bound is expected from complex geometry, see
[
33
]
and the references therein.
This is in spirit worked out in
[
12
]
, even though they only consider compact regions in the co-finite setting. One can however get this bound by observing
[
15
, eqn. (8.2)]
. Since
[
15
, eqn. (8.3)]
(Godement’s theorem) notes that the absolute majorant of the Bergman kernel for
\mathrm{Sp}_{n}(\mathbf{Z})
converges and gives the bound in (
1.10
), all co-compact subgroups
\Gamma
produce the same bound. Indeed, in
[
30
, p. 81, paragraph 2, last line]
it is noted that Godement’s theorem is valid for all discontinuous subgroups of
\mathrm{Sp}_{n}(\mathbf{Z})
. Hence in the rest of the paper, we focus only on the case of congruence subgroups.

1.5.
Remarks about further generalizations and applicability

1.5.1.
Vector-valued cusp forms

It is clear that the method of this paper generalizes without much issue to vector-valued Siegel cusp forms. We have not included it solely because it is not clear what is the explicit relationship of some of the constants e.g.
c(\rho),H(\rho)
(for the spaces
S_{\rho}(\Gamma)
, cf.
[
22
]
) that arise via the reproducing formula of the Bergman kernel or the Gamma function for
\rho
with the spectral parameters. We could not find a suitable reference for this. It seems that the Cartan Seminars
2
2

2

https://www.numdam.org/actas/SHC/
provide the only accessible references, where Godement comments that when
n=2
, these constants may be calculated explicitly, but probably not in higher degrees. Thus one needs to understand this and formulate a suitable conjecture for
S_{\rho}(\Gamma)
.
This being granted, all one needs, is to bound the relevant Poincaré series (say in level one, vector-valued) in Siegel’s fundamental domain as:

(1.11)

\displaystyle P_{T,\Gamma}(Z)\ll_{n}1\quad\quad(Z\in\mathcal{F}^{(n)}_{1}),

in a suitable norm of the underlying vector space. This is no different than our present situation!
After this, it is rather easy to obtain the desired bound (cf. Subsection
7
(3)) – and this is indeed our overall strategy in this paper.

1.5.2.
Half-integral weights, Hilbert cusp forms etc.

However the treatment of half-integral weights is verbatim the same, one could include them at the cost of more standard technicalities and notation. We choose not to do this, and leave it to the interested reader to work this out. In particular, we note that the present arguments do not rely on any kind of Atkin-Lehner-Hecke theory or specific arithmetic decomposition of congruence subgroups (cf.
[
2
, Appendix]
) to facilitate bounds in convenient regions. For arbitrary congruence subgroups such information may not be available at present anyway. However the arithmeticity of the subgroups that we consider is important.
The method generalizes to Hilbert modular forms with some work, and also to automorphic forms on other tube domains in principle. Hermitian or orthogonal modular forms are definitely cases of interest here, and it would be worthwhile to try these cases.

1.6.
Lower bounds

The other point that we want to emphasize, is about the lower bounds. We believe that the lower bounds are crucial in the problem on average – as this not only implies the
correct
size of the relevant space; but also aids in setting up the conjectural upper bounds for various spaces where the dimension formulae are not available. Indeed this was the way in which the conjectures for
\mathrm{Sp}_{n}(\mathbf{Z})
and its subgroups were obtained in
[
15
]
. Of course in level one, an asymptotic dimension formula is known, see e.g.
[
43
]
. As far as the author is aware, such formulae is not known for arbitrary congruence subgroups in a usable form.
The respective lower bounds were easy to establish for
\mathrm{Sp}_{n}(\mathbf{Z})
(cf.
[
15
, Section 7.1]
) – the presence of levels create some trouble, since the fundamental domain is more complicated – and delicate arguments are required. In many interesting cases, such lower bounds can be obtained, see Section
6
. These rely on a result about the “soft” asymptotic formula for the Fourier coefficients of Siegel Poincaré series of higher degrees, see Theorem
5.1
. Since the explicit formula for such Fourier coefficients is not available for higher degrees, our method in this paper plays a vital role in the proof.

1.7.
Small weights

We now make some remarks about the so-called small weights, say when
k\approx n
and the level is one. Here the dimension of
S^{n}_{k}
is just a function of
n
; and also since the implied constants in Theorem
1.5
, Theorem
1.6
depend on
n
, there is not much to do in the weight aspect. For other congruence subgroups, only the level aspect is relevant and of course, more subtle. In Theorem
9.2
we show that for any
L^{2}
-normalised
G\in S^{n}_{k}(\Gamma)
for
k\geq\frac{n}{2}
satisfies the bound:

(1.12)

\left\lVert G\right\rVert_{\infty}\ll_{n}k^{\frac{3n(n+1)}{8}}

where we assume the bound
\sup(S^{n}_{\kappa}(\Gamma))\ll_{n}\kappa^{\frac{3n(n+1)}{4}}
which the expected from (
1.3
) and (
1.4
), on
any
congruence subgroup
\Gamma
for
large enough
weights
\kappa
– by a norm interpolation technique using Hölder’s inequality. The condition on the weight is the best possible, as anything lower would not admit cusp forms. Again this method is quite general.

1.8.
Applications of our method

As immediate applications of our method, we can shed some light on the rather hard conjecture on the non-vanishing of Siegel Poincaré series (of exponential type). We essentially can show that given any set of pairwise
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbf{Z})
inequivalent matrices
\{T_{1},\ldots,T_{h}\}\subset\Lambda_{n}
, the corresponding Poincaré series
\{P_{T_{1}},\ldots,P_{T_{h}}\}
on
\Gamma^{(n)}_{0}(N)
are linearly independent provided
k
is large enough, depending on the traces of these matrices. This generalizes and improves upon the main results of
[
7
]
where the same statement was proved for infinite families of weights
k
in arithmetic progressions modulo suitable primes, for
\Gamma=\Gamma^{(n)}_{0}(N)
via congruences between modular forms. See Corollary
5.6
and Corollary
5.8
. Our results also generalize (and reprove when
n=2
) some of the results in
[
44
]
to higher degrees.

We also work out an uniform bound for the Bergman kernel
\mathbb{B}_{k,\Gamma}(Z)
in the Siegel’s fundamental domain for
\Gamma
, see Corollary
8.2
.

There are are further applications of our method to the problem of mass equidistribution on average on the Siegel modular variety, as well as to the bounds on certain
L
-series arising from cusp forms – these will be treated in a forthcoming paper. It is however not clear what our method implies for the various subspaces of
lifts
, e.g. see
[
1
]
,
[
2
]
for Saito-Kurokawa lifts. It would be interesting to investigate this further.

that the size of
S_{2}(\Gamma_{0}(N))
is
O(1)
.
When
k>2
, our argument is just a few lines, whereas when
k=2
, we first prove a large sieve inequality for
arbitrary
congruence subgroups and apply it to prove the bound
O(\log N)
for the size of
S_{2}(\Gamma)
, where the level of
\Gamma
is
N
. We could not find this result in the literature. Moreover treating all congruence subgroups is crucial for our approach to work, as we work with all the conjugates of
\Gamma
. See Theorem
10.4
(A), (B). It seems that the calculations in
[
2
]
, adapted for
k=2
, might be able to show the bound
O(1)
(using the conditional summation of Poincaré series), but we could not be successful in this regard.

We give two methods for proving the large sieve inequality.
The first one is Duke-Iwaniec’s method
[
18
]
of covering suitable subsets of
\mathbf{H}
of finite volume by finitely many copies of the fundamental domain. The second one is due to Duke-Friedlander-Iwaniec
[
17
]
via the Petersson formula – here we feel that the situation is rather subtle, since for an arbitrary congruence subgroup one does not have complete control over all the arithmetic functions at hand. For example, we have to assume a certain uniform bound for the Kloosterman sums
S_{\Gamma}(m,n;c)
, see Hypothesis
10.3
. Its likely that a suitable variant of our results also hold when
k=1
. Also one might try to consider the large sieve machinery for higher degrees – for arbitrary congruence subgroups this seems to be difficult. For
\Gamma^{(n)}_{0}(N)
, there may be some hope, and this is under consideration by the author.

Notation and setting

2.1.
Siegel modular forms

For basic facts about Siegel modular forms we refer to
[
3
]
,
[
19
]
or

[
30
]
. The symplectic group
\mathrm{Sp}_{n}(\mathbf{R})
defined as

\mathrm{Sp}_{n}(\mathbf{R})=\{g=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in M({2n},\mathbf{R})\mid M^{t}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0_{n}&-1_{n}\\
1_{n}&0_{n}\end{smallmatrix}\right)M=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0_{n}&-1_{n}\\
1_{n}&0_{n}\end{smallmatrix}\right)\},

acts on Siegel’s half-space
\mathbf{H}_{n}
defined as

{\mathbf{H}_{n}:=\{Z=Z^{t}\in M(n,\mathbf{C})\mid(Z-\overline{Z})/2i\text{ is positive definite}\},}

in the usual way by
g\langle Z\rangle=(AZ+B)(CZ+D)^{-1}
and
on functions
F:{\mathbf{H}}_{n}\longrightarrow{\mathbf{C}}
by the weight
k
“stroke”operator:

(F\mid_{k}g)(Z)=\det(CZ+D)^{-k}F(g\langle Z\rangle)\qquad(g=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\mathrm{Sp}(n,\mathbf{R}),k\in\mathbf{Z}_{\geq 0}).

Throughout the paper we put
\Gamma_{n}=\mathrm{Sp}_{n}(\mathbf{Z})=\mathrm{Sp}_{n}(\mathbf{R})\cap M(2n,\mathbf{Z})
.
For
g\in\Gamma_{n}
, we also write
g=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a_{g}&b_{g}\\
c_{g}&d_{g}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
. We write
\displaystyle J(g,Z):=\det(c_{g}Z+d_{g})
as the automorphy factor. We also put
\mathbf{H}:=\mathbf{H}_{1}
.

Let
\Gamma
be a congruence subgroup of
\Gamma_{n}
. For a congruence subgroup
\Gamma
, the level
N
is defined to be the smallest positive integer such that
\Gamma^{(n)}(N)\subset\Gamma
. Among specific subgroups of
\Gamma_{n}
, we will mainly consider the following congruence subgroups of
\Gamma_{n}
.

(2.1)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{0}^{(n)}(N)

\displaystyle:=\{\left(\begin{smallmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\Gamma_{n}:C\equiv 0\bmod N\};

(2.2)

\displaystyle\Gamma^{(n),0}(N)

\displaystyle:=\{\left(\begin{smallmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\Gamma_{n}:B\equiv 0\bmod N\};

(2.3)

\displaystyle\Gamma^{(n),0}_{0}(N)

\displaystyle:=\Gamma_{0}^{(n)}(N)\cap\Gamma^{(n),0}(N);

(2.4)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{1}^{(n)}(N)

\displaystyle:=\{\left(\begin{smallmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\Gamma_{n}:C\equiv 0\bmod N,A\equiv 1_{n}\bmod N\}

(2.5)

\displaystyle\Gamma^{(n)}(N)

\displaystyle:=\{\left(\begin{smallmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\Gamma_{n}:B,C\equiv 0\bmod N,A,D\equiv 1_{n}\bmod N\}.

A holomorphic function
F
on

{\mathbf{H}}_{n}
is called a modular form for
\Gamma
of weight
k
if it satisfies the transformation law

F\mid_{k}\gamma=F\qquad(\gamma=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\Gamma),

with the additional condition of being holomorphic at the cusps when
n=1
.
We denote the space of all such functions by
M^{n}_{k}(\Gamma)
.

2.2.
Translations, Fourier expansion and cusp forms

Let
\mathrm{Sym}_{n}(\mathbf{Z})
denote the group of all
n\times n
symmetric matrices over
\mathbf{Z}
.
We denote by

(2.6)

\Gamma_{\infty}=\{\pm\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1_{n}&S\\
0_{n}&1_{n}\end{smallmatrix}\right)\mid S=S^{t}\in\mathscr{S}_{\Gamma}\};\quad(\mathscr{S}_{\Gamma}\subset\mathrm{Sym}_{n}(\mathbf{Z})\text{ is a subgroup})

the subgroup consisting of the translations in
\displaystyle\Gamma
, and
\Gamma_{0,\infty}=\{g\in\Gamma\mid c_{g}=0_{n}\}
. We further let

(2.7)

\mathscr{U}(\Gamma)=\{m(U)\in\Gamma\mid U\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbf{Z})\}\quad(m(U)=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}U^{t}&0\\
0&U^{-1}\end{smallmatrix}\right)),

be the subgroup of the embedded copy of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbf{Z})
inside
\Gamma
. Further, we define the Siegel parabolic subgroup

(2.8)

\Gamma_{0,\infty}:=\{g\in\Gamma\mid c_{g}=0\},

and the subgroup of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbf{Z})
embedded in
\Gamma
by

(2.9)

\mathscr{U}_{\Gamma}:=\{U\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbf{Z})\mid\left(\begin{smallmatrix}U^{t}&0\\
0&U^{-1}\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\Gamma\}\quad(=m^{-1}(\mathscr{U}(\Gamma))).

Note that
\mathscr{U}_{\Gamma_{n}}=\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbf{Z})
. Note that every element
g\in\Gamma_{0,\infty}
can be uniquely represented, in the above notation, as
g=m(U)g_{1}
, where
g_{1}\in\Gamma_{0,\infty}\backslash\Gamma
.

The lattice dual (w.r.t. to the trace pairing) to
\mathscr{S}_{\Gamma}
will be denoted as
\Lambda^{*}_{\Gamma}
, i.e.,
\displaystyle\Lambda^{*}_{\Gamma}=\{T=T^{t}\in M_{n}(\mathbf{Q})\mid\mathrm{tr}\,(TS)\in\mathbf{Z}\,\text{for all }S\in\mathscr{S}_{\Gamma}\}
. It consists of matrices
T
such that
\displaystyle t_{ij}\in\frac{1}{2n_{ij}}\mathbf{Z}
. For each
(i,j)
, the positive integer
n_{ij}
is defined to be the generator of the subgroup of
\mathbf{Z}
consisting of the integers
s_{ij}
, as
S=(s_{pq})
varies in
\mathscr{S}_{\Gamma}
.
When there is no confusion we drop the dependence on
\Gamma
from the notation. We denote by
\Lambda_{\Gamma}:=\{T\in\Lambda^{*}_{\Gamma}\mid T>0\}
.

Every
F\in M^{n}_{k}(\Gamma)
can be expressed by a Fourier series of the form

(2.10)

F(Z)=\sum\nolimits_{T\in\Lambda^{*}_{\Gamma},T\geq 0}a_{F}(T)e(TZ);

and the ‘denominator’ of
T
is
\omega
, i.e., it is the smallest positive integer such that
T\in\frac{1}{\omega}\Lambda^{*}_{n}
(cf. (
2.12
)), where
\Lambda_{n}
denotes the usual half-integral positive semi-definite matrices of size
n
. It is in this sense
\omega
may be interpreted as the ‘maximal-width of the cusp’ at
\infty
of
\Gamma
. We also refer to the
n_{ij}
as the cusp width at
(i,j)
. See the next section.

For each
g\in\Gamma_{n}
, the modular form
F|_{k}g
on
g^{-1}\Gamma g
has a Fourier expansion like (
2.10
), and if for all such
g
, the Fourier expansion of
F|_{k}g
is supported only on
\Lambda_{g^{-1}\Gamma g}
, i.e., on the cone of the positive definite half-integral matrices, we say that
F
is a cusp form.
Let
S^{n}_{k}(\Gamma)
denote the space of holomorphic Siegel cusp forms on
\Gamma
of scalar weight
k
.

If
F,G\in S^{(n)}_{k}(\Gamma)
, we define their Petersson inner product, denoted by
\langle F,G\rangle=\langle F,G\rangle_{\Gamma}
by the integral pairing

(2.11)

\displaystyle\langle F,G\rangle=\int_{\Gamma\backslash\mathbf{H}_{n}}F(Z)\overline{G(Z)}\det(Y)^{k}d\mu(Z),

where
d\mu(Z)=dX\,dY\det(Y)^{-\frac{n+1}{2}}
is the invariant volume element on
\mathbf{H}_{n}
and
\Gamma\backslash\mathbf{H}_{n}
denotes any fundamental domain for the action of
\Gamma
on
\mathbf{H}_{n}
.

2.3.
Cusp configuration

Let us discuss a bit more about the cusp at
\infty
. Here there are two quantities which are relevant: the index and the “maximal width at
\infty
” (and analogously at other cusps):

(2.12)

\displaystyle|\mathscr{S}_{\Gamma}|:=|\mathrm{Sym}_{n}(\mathbf{Z})\colon\mathscr{S}_{\Gamma}|=\prod\nolimits_{i\leq j}n_{ij}(\Gamma),\quad\omega=\omega_{\Gamma}:=\mathrm{lcm.}_{i\leq j}(n_{ij}).

We call the collection
\mathcal{C}(\Gamma):=\{n_{ij}(\Gamma)\}
to be the cusp-width configuration for
\Gamma
at the cusp
\infty
.

For the principal congruence subgroup
\Gamma^{(n)}(N)
(see Section
2.1
), things simplify a bit: one has the exact description
\mathscr{S}_{\Gamma}=N\cdot\mathrm{Sym}_{n}(\mathbf{Z})
,
|\mathscr{S}_{\Gamma}|=N^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}
and
\omega_{\Gamma}=N
. Unfortunately not all congruence subgroups have this simple description as the example below shows, unlike when
n=1
. Clearly
\omega_{\Gamma}|N
for all
\Gamma
. Moreover for at least one conjugate
G
of
\Gamma
, one must have
\omega_{G}=N
.

Remark 2.1
.

In this remark we want to show that the cusp-width configuration at
\infty
may not be constant, i.e.,
n_{ij}(\Gamma)
are not constant multiples of a fixed integer.
We show this for some conjugates of
\Gamma:=\Gamma^{(2)}_{0}(p)
as examples.

We take a coset representative
g=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{smallmatrix}\right)
in
\Gamma\backslash\Gamma_{n}
. We can always assume without loss
C
is non-singular (cf. the proof of Corollary
8.2
), but we won’t use this. We just assume
C
is upper-triangular – and will choose specific representatives, in this form soon.

Let
G=g^{-1}\Gamma g
.
For
G
, let
\displaystyle n_{11}=t_{1},n_{12}=t_{3},n_{22}=t_{2}
. Then the generators of the lattice
\mathscr{S}_{G}
are
S_{1}=t_{1}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
,
S_{3}=t_{3}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&0\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)
and
S_{2}=t_{2}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
respectively. Then
t_{j}
are the smallest positive integers such that

(2.13)

\displaystyle\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&S_{j}\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in G,\text{ that is }g\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&S_{j}\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)g^{-1}\in\Gamma^{(2)}_{0}(p).

A calculation shows that
CS_{j}C^{t}\equiv 0\bmod p
for any
C
as above. Letting
C=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}c_{1}&c_{2}\\
0&c_{4}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
, this means

(2.14)

\displaystyle t_{1}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}c^{2}_{1}&0\\
0&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)\equiv 0\bmod p,\,t_{3}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2c_{1}c_{2}&c_{1}c_{4}\\
c_{1}c_{4}&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)\equiv 0\bmod p,\,t_{2}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}c^{2}_{2}&c_{2}c_{4}\\
c_{2}c_{4}&c^{2}_{4}\end{smallmatrix}\right)\equiv 0\bmod p.

One can immediately translate these into divisibility conditions

(2.15)

\displaystyle\frac{p}{t_{1}}\mid c_{1}^{2},\quad\frac{p}{t_{3}}\mid(2c_{1}c_{2},c_{1}c_{4}),\quad\frac{p}{t_{2}}\mid(c_{2}^{2},c_{4}^{2},c_{2}c_{4}).

Now we recall the explicit set of coset representatives in question. These can be of three types (see e. g.
[
9
]
,
[
2
]
). More precisely, for
0\leq j\leq 2
put

(2.16)

w_{j}=\begin{pmatrix}0_{j}&0&-1_{j}&0\\
0&1_{n-j}&0&0_{n-j}\\
1_{j}&0&0_{j}&0\\
0&0_{n-j}&0&1_{n-j}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{j}&b_{j}\\
c_{j}&d_{j}\end{pmatrix}.

Further, for
A\in\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathbf{Z})
and
B\in M_{2}(\mathbf{Z})
, we put

\displaystyle m(A):=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}A^{t}&0\\
0&A^{-1}\end{smallmatrix}\right)\text{ and }n(B):=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&B\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)
. Note that
m(A)
of this paper is
m(A^{t})
in
[
2
]
.

Let
\mathcal{R}(j)
denote the set of matrices of the form
w_{j}n(B_{j})m(A^{t})
, where
B_{j}
and
A
are as in
[
8
]
(lifts of matrices in
\mathrm{Sym}_{j}(\mathbf{F}_{p})
and
\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathbf{F}_{p})/P_{2,j}
to
\mathrm{Sp}_{2}(\mathbf{Z})
, respectively). Then a set of right coset representatives for
\Gamma^{(2)}_{0}(p)\backslash\mathrm{Sp}_{2}(\mathbf{Z})
is given by

(2.17)

\Gamma^{(2)}_{0}(p)\backslash\mathrm{Sp}_{2}(\mathbf{Z})=\bigcup\nolimits_{j=0}^{2}\mathcal{R}(j).

One may take representatives for
\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathbf{F}_{p})/P_{2,j}
to be

(2.18)

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\\
\end{pmatrix},\quad\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&x\\
\end{pmatrix}\,(x\in\mathbf{F}_{p}).

We need to find
C=c_{\gamma}
for each of the
\gamma\in\cup_{j}\mathcal{R}(j)
, and apply (
2.15
) to them.

\mathcal{R}(0)
corresponds to identity, i.e.
\Gamma^{(2)}_{0}(p)
, so here
C=0
and all the
t_{i}=1
. For
\mathcal{R}(2)

C=Id.
which means all the
t_{i}=p
. In the case of
\mathcal{R}(1)
, with
c_{1}
as in (

), one finds that
C=c_{1}A^{t}\in\{\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a_{1}&a_{2}\\
0&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)\}
with
(a_{1},a_{2})=(1,0),(0,-1)
. This gives in the first case
t_{1}=p
and no conditions on
t_{2},t_{3}
– so by minimality

. Similarly for the remaining case one finds that
t_{1}=1=t_{3},t_{2}=p

(1,1,1)
or
(1,1,p)
or
(p,1,1)
or
(p,p,p)

2.4.
Siegel Poincaré series

For a
T\in\Lambda_{\Gamma}
, we define the Poincaré series by

(2.19)

\displaystyle P_{T}(Z)=P_{\Gamma;T}(Z):=\sum\nolimits_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{\infty}\backslash\Gamma}e(TZ)\mid_{k}\gamma,

which is an element of
S^{n}_{k}(\Gamma)
provided
k>2n+1
.

For
T\in\Lambda_{\Gamma},F\in S^{n}_{k}(\Gamma)
, one has the formula, which is proved by the usual unfolding method (see e.g.
[
13
, Theorem 8.2.3 (c)]
for the case
n=1
)

(2.20)

\displaystyle\langle F,P_{T;G}\rangle=\frac{|\mathscr{S}_{\Gamma}|\,c_{n,k}\,a_{F}(T)}{\det(T)^{k-\frac{n+1}{2}}},

where we have put

(2.21)

\displaystyle c_{n,k}=\pi^{\frac{n(n-1)}{4}}(4\pi)^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}-nk}\Gamma_{n}\left(k-\frac{n+1}{2}\right).

Let us further define the constants

(2.22)

\displaystyle a_{n,k}

\displaystyle:=2^{-n(n+3)/2}\pi^{-n(n+1)/2}\prod\nolimits_{v=1}^{n}{\frac{\Gamma(k-\frac{v-1}{2})}{\Gamma(k-\frac{v+n}{2})}}\asymp_{n}k^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}},

(2.23)

\displaystyle b_{n,k}

\displaystyle:=(2\sqrt{\pi})^{-n(n-1)/2}(-2\pi i)^{-nk}\prod\nolimits_{\nu=0}^{n-1}\Gamma(k-\nu/2).

Note that our constant
a_{n,k}
differs from that in
[
30
]
by a factor of
(2i)^{nk}
due to different normalization. Our setting matches with that of
[
12
]
. Further we record here the relationship between the
L^{2}
-average of the Fourier coefficients of cusp form in an orthonormal basis and the Fourier coefficients of Siegel Poincaré series:

(2.24)

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{F\in\mathscr{B}(\Gamma)}\,|a_{F}(T_{0})|^{2}=c_{n,k}^{-1}\,\frac{|\mathscr{S}_{\Gamma}|^{-1}\,\det(T_{0})^{-\frac{n+1}{2}}}{\Gamma_{n}(k-\frac{n+1}{2})}\cdot p_{T_{0}}(T_{0}).

We remind the reader that (
2.24
) is derived by writing
P_{T}
as a linear combination of
F\in\mathscr{B}(\Gamma)
, using orthogonality and (
2.20
).

Next, we note the following Lipschitz formula (see e.g.
[
30
, p. 89]
) relative to sub-lattices (for
k>n
)

(2.25)

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{S\in\mathscr{S}_{\Gamma}}\det(Z+S)^{-k}=\frac{b_{n,k}^{-1}}{|\mathscr{S}_{\Gamma}|}\sum\nolimits_{T\in\Lambda_{\Gamma}}\det(T)^{k-\frac{n+1}{2}}\,e(TZ),

which comes from the Poisson summation formula along with holomorphy in
Z
and the Köcher’s principle if
n\geq 2
. These formulae together leads to the familiar geometric side of the Bergman kernel – which we mention below, for the seemingly lack of an explicit reference for arbitrary
\Gamma
. The main point is that one needs to be sure about the level dependence in the formulae explicitly. First we recall that the Bergman kernel is defined by

(2.26)

\displaystyle B_{k,\Gamma}(Z,W):=\sum\nolimits_{F\in\mathscr{B}_{k,\Gamma}}F(Z)\overline{F(W)},

where
\mathscr{B}_{k,\Gamma}
denotes an orthonormal basis of
S^{n}_{k}(\Gamma)
.

Then one can write, using (
2.20
) and (
2.25
) successively to get a geometric expression for the Bergman kernel (see e.g.
[
30
, p. 90, first display]
):

(2.27)

\displaystyle B_{k,\Gamma}(Z,W)

\displaystyle=c_{n,k}^{-1}\frac{1}{|\mathscr{S}_{\Gamma}|}\,\sum_{T\in\Lambda_{\Gamma}}\det(T)^{k-\frac{n+1}{2}}P_{T;\Gamma}(Z)e(-T\overline{W})

(2.28)

\displaystyle=a_{n,k}b_{n,k}^{-1}\sum_{g\in\Gamma_{\infty}\backslash\Gamma}J(g,Z)^{-k}\sum_{S\in\mathscr{S}_{\Gamma}}\det\left(\frac{g(Z)-\overline{W}+S}{2i}\right)^{-k}

(2.29)

\displaystyle=\frac{a_{n,k}}{2}\sum_{g\in\Gamma}\det\Big(\frac{Z-\overline{W}}{2i}\Big)|^{(n)}_{k}g.

The extra
2^{-1}
differs from that in
[
30
]
since in
[
30
]
, the sum over
g
is taken
\bmod{\{\pm 1\}}
.
We will use the following notation for the invariant kernel:

(2.30)

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}_{k,\Gamma}(Z,W)=\frac{a_{n,k}}{2}\,\det(Y)^{k/2}\det(V)^{k/2}\,B_{k,\Gamma}(Z,W).

We put
B_{k,\Gamma}(Z):=B_{k,\Gamma}(Z,Z)
,
\mathbb{B}_{k,\Gamma}(Z):=\mathbb{B}_{k,\Gamma}(Z,Z)
etc..

In view of future applications, we define, for any congruence subgroup
\Gamma\subset\Gamma_{n}
and any
\mathscr{Y}>0
,
T\in\Lambda_{n}
,

(2.31)

\displaystyle H_{\Gamma}(T,\mathscr{Y}):=\sum\nolimits_{U\in\mathscr{U}_{\Gamma}}\exp(-2\pi\mathrm{tr}\,\,T\mathscr{Y}[U]).

One might think of
H_{\Gamma}(T,\mathscr{Y})
, say for
\Gamma=\Gamma_{n}
as a sub-series of the usual theta series
\displaystyle\vartheta(iV)
where for
Z\in\mathbf{H}_{n}
, we put
\displaystyle\vartheta(Z)=\sum\nolimits_{X\in\mathbf{Z}^{n\times n}}e(Z[X])
. For future usage, we record here the simple property:

(2.32)

\displaystyle H_{\Gamma}(T,\mathscr{Y})\leq H_{\Gamma^{\prime}}(T,\mathscr{Y}),

wherever
\Gamma\subseteq\Gamma^{\prime}
.

3.
Proof of the Theorem
1.5
: weight aspect – upper bounds

We will first prove the statement for the group
\Gamma_{n}
and then reduce the case of arbitrary
\Gamma
to it. This proof is shorter than the hybrid-level aspect, and we believe is worth presenting separately.

3.1.
The case
\Gamma=\Gamma_{n}

We know from (
2.30
) that

(3.1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}_{k,\Gamma_{n}}(Z)=\frac{a_{n,k}}{2}\,\det(Y)^{k}B_{k}(Z).

We appeal to the Fourier expansion of the
B_{k}(Z,W)
, and put
Z=W
to get

(3.2)

\displaystyle B_{k}(Z)=b_{n,k}^{-1}\sum\nolimits_{T\in\Lambda_{n}}\det(T)^{k-\frac{n+1}{2}}P_{T}(Z)e(-T\overline{Z}).

First, we note that without loss, one can assume that
Z\in\mathcal{F}^{(n)}_{1}
which implies in particular that
Y\gg 1
and is Minkowski-reduced.

Next, note that it is enough to show that for
Z\in\mathcal{F}^{(n)}_{1}
one has

(3.3)

\displaystyle P_{T}(Z)\ll_{n}1.

Indeed, if we assume this, then

(3.4)

\displaystyle B_{k}(Z)\ll b_{n,k}^{-1}\sum\nolimits_{T}\det(T)^{k-\frac{n+1}{2}}\exp(-2\pi\mathrm{tr}\,TY).

We know via Lipschitz’s formula that for
k>n
and any
Y_{0}>0
,

(3.5)

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{S\in\mathrm{Sym}_{n}(\mathbf{Z})}\det(iY_{0}+S)^{-k}=b_{n,k}^{-1}\sum\nolimits_{T}\det(T)^{k-\frac{n+1}{2}}\exp(-2\pi\mathrm{tr}\,TY_{0}).

Quoting from
[
15
, Lemma 6.2]
, which is valid for any
Z\in\mathbf{H}_{n}
, we get, with
Z=iY_{0}
that

(3.6)

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{S}\det(iY_{0}+S)^{-k}\ll k^{\frac{n(n+1)}{4}}\det(Y_{0})^{-k}.

Finally, gathering together (
3.1
), (
3.4
) and (
3.6
) we arrive at the desired bound:

(3.7)

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}_{k}(Z)\ll a_{n,k}\cdot k^{\frac{n(n+1)}{4}}\asymp k^{\frac{3n(n+1)}{4}}.

It therefore remains to show (
3.3
), i.e.,
P_{T}(Z)\ll 1
. Recall from the definition (
2.19
) that

(3.8)

\displaystyle P_{T}(Z)=\sum\nolimits_{g\in\Gamma_{\infty}\backslash\Gamma_{n}}J(g,Z)^{-k}e(Tg(Z)).

Such a
g\in\Gamma_{n}
can be uniquely written as
g=m(U)\gamma
, where
U\in\mathscr{U}_{\Gamma_{n}}
and
\gamma\in\Gamma_{0,\infty}
. Then it follows that,

(3.9)

\displaystyle P_{T}(Z)=\sum\nolimits_{\gamma\in(\Gamma_{n})_{{0,\infty}}\backslash\Gamma_{n}}J(\gamma,Z)^{-k}\sum\nolimits_{U}\det(U)^{k}e(TU^{t}\gamma(Z)U).

Let us discuss some heuristics about the function
H_{\Gamma_{n}}(T,\mathscr{Y})
defined in (
2.31
).
Via Poisson summation, the theta series

\vartheta(Z)=\det(-Z)^{-\frac{n}{2}}\vartheta(-Z^{-1})

Proposition 3.1
.

For
\mathscr{Y}>0
,
T\in\Lambda_{n}

\displaystyle H_{\Gamma_{n}}(T,\mathscr{Y})\ll_{D,n}\frac{1}{\det(\mathscr{Y})^{D}}

Let us assume Proposition

for the moment and finish the proof of Theorem
1.5
. Namely, from (

\displaystyle P_{T}(Z)\ll\sum\nolimits_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{0,\infty}\backslash\Gamma_{n}}|J(\gamma,Z)|^{-k}H_{\Gamma_{n}}(T,Y_{\gamma}),

where

\displaystyle H_{\Gamma_{n}}(T,Y_{\gamma})\ll\det(Y_{\gamma})^{-n/2-\epsilon}=\det(Y)^{-n/2-\epsilon}|\det(c_{\gamma}Z+d_{\gamma})|^{n+2\epsilon}.

Using this in (

(3.12)

\displaystyle P_{T}(Z)\ll\sum\nolimits_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{0,\infty}\backslash\Gamma_{n}}|\det(c_{\gamma}Z+d_{\gamma})|^{-k+n+2\epsilon}\ll 1,

for any

[

and choose

1.5

\square

3.2.
Proof of Proposition
3.1

It remains to prove Proposition
3.1
.
Since
\mathrm{tr}\,T\mathscr{Y}[U]=\mathrm{tr}\,T[U^{t}]\mathscr{Y}
, by absolute convergence, and because the desired bounds depend only on
\det(\mathscr{Y})
, clearly we can assume that both
T
and
\mathscr{Y}
are reduced. That
T
can be assumed to be Minkowski-reduced (see
[

, Chapter I, Section 2]
) of course also follows from the definition of

in level one. Then

(3.13)

\displaystyle H_{\Gamma_{n}}(T,\mathscr{Y})\ll\sum\nolimits_{U}e(-c_{n}\mathrm{tr}\,\,\mathrm{diag}(T)\mathscr{Y}[U^{t}])\ll\sum\nolimits_{U}e(-c_{n}\mathrm{tr}\,\mathscr{Y}[U^{t}])=H_{\Gamma_{n}}(1_{n},c_{n}\mathscr{Y}),

where
\mathrm{diag}(T)
denotes the matrix consisting of the diagonal elements of
T
.

For a matrix
M
, let
M_{j}
denote its
j
-th column. Then for some constant
c_{n}
depending only on
n
,

(3.14)

\displaystyle H_{\Gamma_{n}}(1_{n},c_{n}\mathscr{Y})

\displaystyle\leq\sum\nolimits_{U}\exp(-c_{n}\mathrm{tr}\,\,U^{t}U\cdot\mathrm{diag}(\mathscr{Y}))

(3.15)

\displaystyle=\sum\nolimits_{U}\exp(-c_{n}\,\sum\nolimits_{j=1}^{n}v_{j}\cdot U_{j}^{t}U_{j})\leq\prod_{j=1}^{n}\big(\sum\nolimits_{U_{j},\mathrm{g.c.d.}(U_{j})=1}\exp(-c_{n}\,\sum_{j=1}^{n}v_{j}\cdot U_{j}^{t}U_{j})\big),

where

varies over “primitive” integral vectors in
\mathbf{Z}^{n}
, and
where

are the diagonal elements of
\mathscr{Y}
. We look at each sum over
U_{j}
. More generally, it is easy to check that for any number

,

(3.16)

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{X\neq 0}\exp(-a\cdot X^{t}X)\ll_{n}a^{-n/2-\epsilon},

because we can use the inequality

for

and note that

.
Since
\mathscr{Y}
is also reduced, in effect, we get from (

\displaystyle H_{\Gamma_{n}}(1_{n},c_{n}\mathscr{Y})\ll_{\epsilon,n}\det(\mathscr{Y})^{-n/2-\epsilon},

thereby finishing the proof.
\square

3.3.
Proof of Theorem
1.5
for general congruence groups

Let
\Gamma\subset\Gamma_{n}
be one such congruence subgroup. If the left coset representatives are
\{\gamma_{j}\}
, then a fundamental domain can be chosen to be
\mathcal{F}_{\Gamma}=\cup_{j}\gamma_{j}\mathcal{F}^{(n)}_{1}
. Therefore it is enough to bound
\mathbb{B}_{k}(\gamma_{j}(Z))
for all
j
for
Z\in\mathcal{F}^{(n)}_{1}
. In other words we have to bound

(3.18)

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{F\in\mathscr{B}(\Gamma)}\det(Y)^{k}|F|_{k}\gamma_{j}(Z)|^{2}=\sum\nolimits_{H\in\mathscr{B}(\gamma_{j}^{-1}\Gamma\gamma_{j})}\det(Y)^{k}|H(Z)|^{2},

for

. The equality in (

) follows since
F\mapsto F|g
is an isometry from
S^{(n)}_{k}(\Gamma)
to
S^{(n)}_{k}(g^{-1}\Gamma g)
for any

, and
g=\gamma\gamma_{j}
for some

.

Remark 3.2
.

We note here that the for any
g\in\Gamma_{n}
, one has
\sup(S^{(n)}_{k}(\Gamma))=\sup(S^{(n)}_{k}(g^{-1}\Gamma g))
.

We will require an elementary lemma in many of our arguments in the rest of the paper.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
A\subseteq B
be subgroups of
\Gamma_{n}
and
A_{1}\subset A
,
B_{1}\subset B
be subgroups such that
A\cap B_{1}=A_{1}
. Then one has the natural inclusion
A_{1}\backslash A\hookrightarrow B_{1}\backslash B
.

We would now check that the same idea of using the Fourier expansion of the Bergman kernel and bounding the Poincaré series on
\mathcal{F}^{(n)}_{1}
as in the previous Subsection also works here. We next look at the Fourier expansion of
B_{k,G}(Z,W)
for every conjugate
G
of
\Gamma
. Recall from (
2.19
) the definition of the Poincaré series
P_{T,G}
for
G
at the cusp
\infty
.

If we put
G=\gamma_{j}^{-1}\Gamma\gamma_{j}
, then we can apply Lemma
3.3
with

. Indeed Lemma
3.3
applies in this setting since
G\cap\Gamma_{n,\infty}=G_{\infty}
.
We fix a set of coset representatives
\{g_{j}\}_{j}
for
G_{\infty}\backslash G
. Then the above says that
\{g_{j}\}_{j}
also occurs as a subset of coset representatives of
\Gamma_{n,\infty}\backslash\Gamma_{n}
, and we always work with this choice.

Next we take term-wise absolute values in (
3.8
), use the above remark, and positivity, to obtain

(3.19)

\displaystyle P_{T;G}(Z)\ll\sum\nolimits_{g\in\Gamma_{n,\infty}\backslash\Gamma_{n}}|J(g,Z)|^{-k}e(-2\pi\mathrm{tr}\,\,\mathcal{T}Y_{g}/\omega).

Here we also use the fact that
T
has ‘denominator’
\omega
, i.e.
\omega T=\mathcal{T}\in\Lambda_{n}
, see Section
2
.

Even after taking absolute values, the summands are invariant under the translation groups: namely

We bounded the level

) in (
3.12
) with the only difference that in (

instead of
Y
. Therefore,

(3.20)

\displaystyle P_{T;G}(Z)\ll\big(\frac{\omega^{n}}{\det(Y)}\big)^{\frac{n}{2}+\epsilon}.

Coming back to our desired objective: we recall

(3.21)

\displaystyle B_{k,G}(Z)=|\mathscr{S}_{G}|^{-1}\,b_{n,k}^{-1}\sum\nolimits_{T}\det(T)^{k-\frac{n+1}{2}}P_{T;G}(Z)e(-T\overline{Z}),

which gives, after using (

(3.22)

\displaystyle B_{k,G}(Z)

\displaystyle\ll\big(\frac{\omega^{n}}{\det(Y)}\big)^{\frac{n}{2}+\epsilon}\,|\mathscr{S}_{G}|^{-1}\,b_{n,k}^{-1}\sum\nolimits_{\mathcal{T}\in\Lambda_{G}}\det(T)^{k-\frac{n+1}{2}}\exp(-2\pi\mathcal{T}Y)

\displaystyle\ll k^{\frac{n(n+1)}{4}}\,\big(\frac{\omega^{n}}{\det(Y)}\big)^{\frac{n}{2}+\epsilon}

if we invoke the bound for the

) from Lemma

. Then, the relation

(3.24)

\mathbb{B}_{k,G}(Z)\ll k^{\frac{3n(n+1)}{4}}\,\big(\frac{\omega^{n}}{\det(Y)}\big)^{\frac{n}{2}+\epsilon}

on all of
\mathcal{F}^{(n)}_{1}
.
This finishes the proof of Theorem
1.5
, i.e., the
k
-aspect upper bound of the conjecture for all congruence subgroups of
\Gamma_{n}
, upto the proof of Lemma

\square

We will need the following lemmas, which is an adaptation of

to the present situation. As far as possible, we keep the notation as it is in

Lemma 3.4
.

For
Y
Minkowski reduced,
c=c_{n}>0
, depending only on
n
, we can bound the size of the set

(3.25)

\#\{T\in\Lambda_{G}|\mathrm{tr}\,(TY)\leq ck\}\ll_{n,c}|\mathscr{S}_{G}|\cdot k^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}\det(Y)^{-\frac{n+1}{2}}.

Proof.

This is the analogue of
[
15
, Lemma 4.8]
.
Since
Y
is reduced, we can assume that it is diagonal: this is because
Y_{D}\ll_{n}Y
. Put
T=(t_{i,j})
, and
t_{i}:=t_{i,i}
for

(3.26)

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\,(TY)=\sum_{i}t_{i}y_{i}\ll\mathrm{tr}\,(TY)\ll k\implies 1\leq t_{i}\ll k/y_{i}.

Since
T>0

From this, it follows that the number of choices for

is, up to a constant depending only on

\ll_{n}\prod_{i}\frac{n_{ii}k}{y_{i}}\cdot\prod_{i<j}\frac{n_{ij}k}{(y_{i}y_{j})^{1/2}}=\frac{|\mathscr{S}_{G}|\cdot k^{n+n(n-1)/2}}{\det(Y)^{1+(n-1)/2}}=\frac{|\mathscr{S}_{G}|\cdot k^{n(n+1)/2}}{\det(Y)^{(n+1)/2}}

if we refer to (
2.12

∎

Lemma 3.5
.

Let

be Minkowski-reduced and put, for a congruence subgroup

(3.27)

q_{k}(Y):=\sum_{T\in\Lambda_{G}}p(T)^{1/2}\,\det(Y)^{k/2}\,\exp(-2\pi\mathrm{tr}\,(TY)),

(3.28)

p(T)^{1/2}\ll_{n}\frac{(4\pi)^{nk/2}k^{\alpha}\det(T)^{k/2-\beta}}{\Gamma(k)^{n/2}}.

Then we have the bound (for any

(3.29)

q_{k}(Y)\ll_{n,\epsilon}|\mathscr{S}_{G}|\cdot\left(\left(\frac{k^{n}}{\det(Y)}\right)^{(n+1)/2-\beta}\cdot k^{\frac{n}{4}+\alpha}+\exp(-c_{0}k^{\epsilon})\det(Y)^{-(n+1)/2}\right),

Proof.

The proof is based on showing how one can adapt the arguments given in
[
15
]
to the present situation.
We will indicate how to modify
[
15
, Prop. 3.7]
to this effect. It relies on two inputs:

(i) The analytic part, via which we truncate the sum over

to a finite support via the growth of the Gamma function and decay of the exponential factor in (

with respect to

in the support.

Of these, a moment’s reflection into the proof of
[

, Prop. 3.7]
presented in loc. cit. shows that it carries over mutatis mutandis. It requires no specific information about

(3.30)

q_{k}(Y)\ll_{n}\left(\sum_{T\in\mathcal{C}(Y)}{1}+\sum_{T\notin\mathcal{C}(Y)}{\prod_{j}M(\lambda_{j})}\right)k^{\alpha+\frac{n}{4}}\det(T)^{-\beta},

where

are the eigenvalues of

\displaystyle M(x):=\left(\frac{4\pi x}{k}\right)^{k/2}\exp((k-4\pi x)/2)
; and

(3.31)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(Y):=\left\{T\in\Lambda_{G}\;\middle|\;\text{ all eigenvalues of }TY\text{ are of magnitude }\frac{k}{4\pi}+O(k^{\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon})\right\}.

The contribution from the first sum is as it is in
[

(3.32)

\displaystyle\sum_{T\in\mathcal{C}(Y)}k^{\alpha+\frac{n}{4}}\det(T)^{-\beta}\ll_{n}\,\#\mathcal{C}(Y)\cdot\det(Y)^{\beta}k^{-n\beta}k^{\alpha+\frac{n}{4}}.

Now we treat this as a counting problem on

is contained in the set

\{T\mid\mathrm{tr}\,(TY)\asymp\frac{nk}{4\pi}\}
where the implied

3.4

from Lemma

(3.33)

\displaystyle\sum_{T\in\mathcal{C}(Y)}k^{\alpha+\frac{n}{4}}\det(T)^{-\beta}\ll_{n}\,|\mathscr{S}_{G}|\cdot\left(\frac{k^{n}}{\det(Y)}\right)^{(n+1)/2-\beta}\cdot k^{\frac{n}{4}+\alpha},

The treatment of the “tail” part, i.e. the second sum

(3.34)

\sum\nolimits_{T\notin\mathcal{C}(Y)}{\prod\nolimits_{j}M(\lambda_{j})}\cdot k^{\alpha+\frac{n}{4}}\det(T)^{-\beta}\ll_{n}|\mathscr{S}_{G}|\cdot\exp\left(-c_{0}k^{\epsilon}\right)\det(Y)^{-\frac{n+1}{2}}

We now let

4.
Congruence subgroups: proof of Theorem
1.6

Our aim is to prove the following statement, from which Theorem
1.6
follows trivially.

Theorem 4.1
.

Suppose
k\geq 2n+2
, and that for all the (finitely many) conjugates
G
of
\Gamma
of level
N
, the following conditions hold.

(i)
G\subset\Gamma^{(2)}_{0}(\omega_{G})
,

(ii) the index of
\mathscr{U}_{\Gamma^{(n)}(N)}
in
\mathscr{U}_{G}
(cf. notation in (
2.9
)) is
O_{n}(1)
.

Then
\sup(S^{n}_{k}(\Gamma))\ll_{n}k^{\frac{3n(n+1)}{4}}
.

Since our assumption about the ‘maximal’ width of the cusps is the same for all these conjugates, it is enough to just work with
\Gamma
and then apply it to its conjugates. Our basic strategy will be the same as that of Theorem
1.5
. But we will treat the estimate of
P_{T,\Gamma}
differently. Namely we will isolate the contribution of those
g\in\Gamma_{\infty}\backslash\Gamma
in the definition of
P_{T,\Gamma}
for which
c_{g}=0
and handle the rest separately. On these elements
|J(g,Z)|
equals the minimum value
1
as
Z
varies in
\mathcal{F}^{(n)}_{1}
, so the factors
J(g,Z)^{-k}
attain their maximum value.
We state the necessary ingredients in two lemmas.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\Gamma\subset\Gamma_{n}
be a congruence subgroup with maximal cusp-width
\omega=\omega_{\Gamma}
. Suppose that
\Gamma\subset\Gamma^{(2)}_{0}(\omega)
.
For any
A\geq 0
depending only on
n
, and for
k>2n+1+A
,

(4.1)

\displaystyle P_{T;\Gamma}(Z)=\sum_{g\in\Gamma_{\infty}\backslash\Gamma,\,c_{g}=0}e(Tg(Z))+O_{A}(\omega^{-A}),\quad\quad(Z\in\mathcal{F}^{(n)}_{1}).

Proof.

We write
T=\omega\mathcal{T}
with
\mathcal{T}\in\Lambda_{n}
.
It is enough to consider the terms with
c_{g}\neq 0
. For these terms we have the following:

\displaystyle\sum_{c_{g}\neq 0,\,g\in\Gamma_{0,\infty}\backslash\Gamma}J(g,Z)^{-k}\sum_{U\in\mathscr{U}_{\Gamma}}e(\frac{1}{\omega}\mathcal{T}[U]g(Z))\leq\sum_{c_{g}\neq 0,\,g\in(\Gamma_{n})_{0,\infty}\backslash\Gamma_{n}}|J(g,Z)|^{-k}\sum_{U\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbf{Z})}e(-\frac{2\pi}{\omega}\,\mathcal{T}[U]Y_{g}),

where we have used the embedding from Lemma
3.3
and have put
Y_{g}:=\mathrm{Im}g(Z)
. The condition
c_{g}\neq 0
is stable under the aforementioned embedding. If we look at (
2.31
), then the expression in the RHS of the above inequality is simply bounded by

(4.2)

\displaystyle\sum_{c_{g}\neq 0}|J(g,Z)|^{-k}H_{\Gamma_{n}}(\mathcal{T},Y_{g}/\omega)

(4.3)

\displaystyle\ll\max\{|J(g,Z)|^{-A}\colon c_{g}\neq 0\}\sum\nolimits_{c_{g}\neq 0}|J(g,Z)|^{-k+A}H_{\Gamma_{n}}(\mathcal{T},Y_{g}/\omega)

(4.4)

\displaystyle\ll\max\{|J(g,Z)|^{-A}\colon c_{g}\neq 0\}\,\omega^{D}\,\sum\nolimits_{c_{g}\neq 0}|J(g,Z)|^{-k+A+D};

where in (
4.4
), we have applied Proposition
3.1
with
\mathscr{Y}=Y\gg 1
, and take
D>n/2
depending only on
n
. We would choose
A,D
as a functions of
n
only.

Since
Z\in\mathcal{F}^{(n)}_{1}
, the sum over
g
converges and is
O(1)
as soon as

(cf.
[
15
, p. 24]
).
Next, we look at each summad in (
4.4
). For these summands, since we have
c_{g}\neq 0
, i.e.
\mathrm{rank}(c_{g})\geq 1
, and
Y\gg 1
, the idea is that, in essence, we can
‘extract’ a power of
N
from the one we factorized by
J(g,Z)
. More precisely, let
\mathrm{rank}(C)=r\geq 1
. Then from Siegel
[
40
]
(see also
[
14
, Lemma 3.1]
)
we can find
U,W\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbf{Z})
and
C_{1},D_{1}
of size

such that

(4.5)

\displaystyle(UC,UD)=\left(\begin{pmatrix}C_{1}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}W^{t},\begin{pmatrix}D_{1}&0\\
0&I_{n-r}\end{pmatrix}W^{-1}\right).\quad\quad(\mathrm{rank}(C_{1})=r)

Using this we see that

(4.6)

\displaystyle|\det(CZ+D)|

\displaystyle=\left|\det\left(\begin{pmatrix}C_{1}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}Z_{0}[W]+\begin{pmatrix}D_{1}&0\\
0&I_{n-r}\end{pmatrix}\right)\right|

(4.7)

\displaystyle=\left|\det(C_{1}Z[W]_{*}+D_{1})\right|,

where
Z[W]_{*}
is the leading
r\times r
sub-matrix of
Z[W]

5.1.

A similar argument gives the following corollary about non-vanishing of Poincaré series from Theorem

For

(of exponential type) are non-zero.

Not much is known – one has partial results from

[

on this topic. For the author, this question for Poincaré series of exponential type (which show up often in number theory) remains an outstanding open question in the classical theory of modular forms. For other Poincaré series of ‘polynomial’ type, positive results are known – see e.g.
[

]
for results on this topic.

; and the asymptotic holds in each case provided the conditions stated in the lemma hold.

Remark 5.7

In

]
, where congruences between modular forms modulo

were used to prove the following theorem for
k
restricted to specific families of arithmetic progressions (depending on
p
) when

is even. Such progressions, even though completely explicit, however do not cover all but finitely many positive integers. Our result is valid for all large enough weights and can obviously be formulated for general congruence subgroups. We state it on
\Gamma_{n}
for convenience.

Corollary 5.8
.

Let
\{T_{1},\ldots,T_{h}\}\subset\Lambda_{n}
be a
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbf{Z})
-inequivalent subset. Then the Poincaré series
\{P_{T_{1}},\ldots,P_{T_{h}}\}
on
\Gamma_{0}^{(n)}(N)
are linearly independent provided
k\gg_{n}\max\{\mathrm{tr}\,T_{j}\colon 1\leq j\leq h\}
.

Proof.

Since the maximal width of the cusp at
\infty
for
\Gamma_{0}^{(n)}(N)
is
1
, we can apply the case (i) of Theorem
5.1
to the Poincaré series in question.
We show that the matrix
\mathscr{P}:=(p_{ij})
with
p_{ij}:=p_{T_{j}}(T_{i})
,
1\leq i,j\leq n
, has maximal rank. For this we expand
\det(\mathscr{P})
according to Leibnitz rule: except for the diagonal product
p_{11}\cdots p_{nn}
, all other terms are easily seen to be
O\big(\exp(cy_{0}\max_{j}{\mathrm{tr}\,(T_{j})})y_{0}^{-(n^{2}+1)/2-k/2n}\big)
. We choose

(any

should work). For

large enough as in the statement of the corollary we see that each such term is
O(2^{-k/4})
. Similarly the product
p_{11}\cdots p_{nn}=M+O(2^{-k/4n})
for some
M\gg 1
. This finishes the proof.
∎

Corollary

6.

To begin with, note that if
\Gamma_{1}\subseteq\Gamma_{2}\subseteq\Gamma_{n}
be two subgroups of finite index, then one has the inequality obtained by embedding one space into the other:

(6.1)

\mathbb{B}_{\Gamma_{2}}(Z)\leq|\Gamma_{2}\colon\Gamma_{1}|\mathbb{B}_{\Gamma_{1}}(Z),

where
|\Gamma_{2}\colon\Gamma_{1}|
denotes the index of
\Gamma_{1}
in
\Gamma_{2}
. Thus we immediately obtain

(6.2)

\displaystyle\sup(S^{(n)}_{k})|\Gamma_{n}\colon\Gamma|^{-1}\ll_{n}\sup(S^{(n)}_{k}(\Gamma)).

for any congruence subgroup
\Gamma
.

Our next goal is to remove the index factor in the denominator (in many cases). At this point we want to (again) remind the reader about the issue of not having an usable bound on the Fourier coefficients of Siegel Poincaré series of higher degrees on
\Gamma_{n}
, let alone congruence subgroups. In the context of obtaining lower bounds on
\sup(S^{(n)}_{k}(\Gamma))
, this was overcome in
[
15
, Sec. 7.1]
by using only ‘soft’ asymptotic properties of these Fourier coefficients as
k\to\infty
. This point is relevant in what follows about getting lower bounds on
\sup(S^{n}_{k}(\Gamma))
. See also Theorem
5.1
in Section
5
in this regard, which allows us to control the range of validity of the weight
k
in an an asymptotic formula for the Fourier coefficients of Siegel Poincaré series explicitly.

In the next Subsection we prove the lower bounds claimed in Theorem
1.5
, Theorem
1.6
and Theorem
1.7
. In the case of congruence subgroups, in the weight aspect, (

) is enough. In the level aspect things are somewhat subtle, and we need more care.

We now outline a general strategy that should work for all congruence subgroups. We present it as an algorithm. At the end of this section we complete the proof of Theorem
4.1
as per this algorithm.

7.1.
The Algorithm

(0)

Let
\Gamma

\Gamma_{n}

Set up the conjecture for the correct size of the

(1)

from (

(2)

(3)

Show that

\displaystyle\det(Y)^{k}\,B_{k,G}(Z)\ll|\mathscr{S}_{G}|^{-1}\,b_{n,k}^{-1}\sum\nolimits_{T\in\Lambda_{G}}\det(T)^{k-\frac{n+1}{2}}\exp(-2\pi\,TY)\ll k^{\frac{n(n+1)}{4}}.

Repeat the Steps (1)–(3) for all conjugates of
\Gamma

\displaystyle m(U)\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1_{n}&\mathscr{S}_{G}\\

0&1_{n}\end{smallmatrix}\right)\text{ i.e. }U\mathscr{S}_{G}U^{t}\subset\mathscr{S}_{G};

this places congruence restrictions on the elements

.
For simplicity we assume that that

,
N
odd and
n_{11},n_{12},n_{22}
are pairwise co-prime. Then one arrives, in particular, at the following conditions:

(7.3)

\displaystyle n_{11}|u_{12},\,n_{22}|u_{21},\,n_{12}|(u_{11}u_{21},u_{12}u_{22})\quad\quad(U=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}u_{11}&u_{12}\\
u_{21}&u_{22}\end{smallmatrix}\right)).

G\subset\Gamma^{(2)}_{0}(\omega_{G})

We write

(7.5)

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\,\,T[U]=t_{1}\mathcal{U}_{1}+t_{2}\mathcal{U}_{2}+t_{12}\mathcal{U}_{12}\geq(\sqrt{t_{1}\mathcal{U}_{1}}-\sqrt{t_{2}\mathcal{U}_{2}})^{2}+(\sqrt{t_{1}t_{2}}-|t_{12}|)\sqrt{\mathcal{U}_{1}\mathcal{U}_{2}}.

Whenever

Namely, we will get

Here

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{1}\geq n_{2}\sqrt{n_{12}},\,\mathcal{U}_{2}\geq n_{1}\sqrt{n_{12}}.

\displaystyle H_{G}(T,1_{n})\ll\sum_{U}\exp(-2\pi\,(\mathcal{U}_{1}\mathcal{U}_{2})^{1/4})\ll\sum_{(u_{11},u_{21})}(u^{2}_{11}+u^{2}_{21})^{-3}\cdot\sum_{(u_{12},u_{22})}(u^{2}_{12}+u^{2}_{22})^{-3}\ll 1.\hfill\square

8.1.

Corollary 8.2

For any any congruence subgroup
\Gamma

We can find coset representatives
\{\gamma\}
of
\Gamma
(of level

such that for all

are non-singular. This is because given any set of representatives
\{\tilde{\gamma}\}

\displaystyle\gamma\equiv\tilde{\gamma}\bmod N;\quad\gamma\equiv\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1_{n}\\

9.
The small weights

In the Section we will deal with the so called small weights:
k\geq n/2
. For better exposition we feel that the case
n=1
should be first discussed.

It is clear that the strategy of the earlier Sections would not work here, as the relevant Poincaré series do not converge absolutely. One can however use Hecke’s trick e.g. to construct such series
P_{2,\Gamma}
for the weight
k=2
. One can write down their Fourier expansion (cf.
[
41
]
), but there is some issue about bounds on Kloosterman sums on general congruence subgroups uniform in all parameters, see Hypothesis
10.3
. Anyway for higher degrees this is definitely a big challenge.

Since our main objective here is not to obtain explicit Fourier expansions of Poincaré series (cf. earlier Sections, where we did not use the Fourier expansion at all), we approach this issue differently. We will now propose various remedies to this situation.

We may use holomorphic weight increasing differential operators, and obtain information about the sizes of their Fourier coefficients on small weights from those on bigger weights. The information may be even worse than Hecke’s bound, but perhaps this would be sufficient as we need bounds only in the region
\mathcal{F}^{(n)}_{1}
. This kind of argument was also used in
[
15
]
. For instance, one may use the Serre derivative for weights
k\geq 1
:

(9.1)

\displaystyle\vartheta_{k}\colon S_{k}(\Gamma)\rightarrow S_{k+2}(\Gamma);\quad f\mapsto\theta f-\frac{k}{12}f\cdot E_{2},

where
\theta f=q\frac{d}{dq}f
and
E_{2}
is the holomorphic quasimodular form of weight
2
. If one knows the explicit image (in wieght
4
) of
P_{2,\Gamma}
under
\vartheta_{2}
; one can recursively solve for the coefficients of
P_{2,\Gamma}
in terms of that of the image. This information may then be used to bound the Poincaré series of small weight. Namely for the coefficients
a(n)
of the small weight Poincaré series, one obtains the recursion:
na(n)=\sum_{m<n}u(a(n))
for some function
u
. Also the
L^{2}
norms may be handled via ‘weight-increasing’ or ‘decreasing’ Maaß operators. See
[
45
]
for a description of this ‘image’, however here
k=2
is not considered. For higher degrees one may use suitable Rankin-Cohen operators.

Here we offer the following solution to the question about small weights for any fixed cusp form. The bounds are what one would get at best from the corresponding average bounds. This may be thought of as a ‘weight-embedding’ technique as compared to the ‘level-embedding’ technique of
[
17
]
.

Hypothesis 9.1
.

We assume that
\sup(S^{n}_{\kappa}(\Gamma))\ll_{n}\kappa^{2\alpha}
(
\kappa\gg_{n}1
,
\alpha\geq 0
depending only on
n
) where the implied constant depends only on the complementary set of parameters if we consider weight or level or the hybrid aspects respectively. We take
\alpha=0
in the hybrid aspect.

Theorem 9.2
.

Let
k\in\mathbf{Z}
satisfy
k\geq\frac{n}{2}
and
\Gamma\in\Gamma_{n}
be any congruence subgroup. With the Hypothesis
9.1
, for any
L^{2}
-normalised
G\in S^{n}_{k}(\Gamma)
, one has the bound

(9.2)

\displaystyle\left\lVert G\right\rVert_{\infty}\ll_{n}\kappa^{\alpha}.

The implied constants in each case depend on the complementary set of parameters.

Notice that strictly speaking, only the level aspect makes sense for weights
k\ll n
.

Proof.

The proof is based on an interpolation method using the various
L^{p}
norms. Fix any non-zero
F\in S^{n}_{k}(\Gamma)
. We do not normalize by the Petersson norm yet.
For ease of notation, let us put
f(Z)=\det(Y)^{k/2}F(Z)
.
Let
m\geq 1
. We first note that

(9.3)

\displaystyle\sup\nolimits_{\mathbf{H}_{n}}|f|^{m}=(\sup\nolimits_{\mathbf{H}_{n}}|f|)^{m}.

We choose
m\in\mathbf{N}
large enough so that
mk=\kappa
is bigger than the weights for which Hypothesis
9.1
holds. Note that we can, and will assume that
m
depends only on
n
, since our previous results are valid for all
\kappa\gg_{n}1
. (e.g. Theorem
1.5
, Theorem
1.6
are valid for
\kappa\gg n
etc.)
Thus using Hypothesis
9.1
for the weight
\kappa
,

(9.4)

\displaystyle|f|^{m}\ll(km)^{\alpha}\left\lVert f^{m}\right\rVert_{2}\ll_{n}k^{\alpha}\left(\int|f|^{2m}\right)^{1/2}\ll_{n}k^{\alpha}\left\lVert f\right\rVert^{m}_{2m}.

We now use Hölder’s inequality in the form (see, e.g.
[
4
]
)

(9.5)

\displaystyle\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{2m}\leq\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{2}^{1/m}\cdot\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}^{1-1/m}.

This gives us

(9.6)

\displaystyle|f|^{m}\ll k^{\alpha}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{2}\cdot\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}^{m-1},

which in turn implies, by taking supnorms,

(9.7)

\displaystyle\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}\ll k^{\alpha}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{2},

finishing the proof of the theorem, by taking
G=F/\left\lVert F\right\rVert_{2}
.
∎

10.
Appendix: the case
n=1

In this Section we show how our ideas in this paper play out successfully for elliptic modular forms. For weights
k>2
, the proof is especially short – and for the more subtle case
k=2
, we develop a version of large sieve inequality for Fourier coefficients for
arbitrary
congruence subgroups – something which seems not available in the literature. We do need this result for arbitrary congruence subgroups, since given a congruence subgroup
\Gamma
of level
N
, we have to simultaneously work with all its conjugates. This will lead to a bound
O(\log q)
for the size of
S_{2}(\Gamma)
, for most
\Gamma
of level
q
. This is slightly off the expected bound
O(1)
, but is sufficient for analytic applications. We follow
[
17
]
and
[
18
]
for this purpose.

10.1.
The case
n=1
and
k>2

This is the easy case and can be dealt with immediately following the ideas already discussed in this paper.

Theorem 10.1
.

For any congruence subgroup of
\Gamma\subset\Gamma_{1}
and
k>2
, one has
\displaystyle\sup(S_{k}(\Gamma))\asymp 1
, with the implied constant absolute.

Proof.

The basic pathway of the proof of is essentially contained in the proof of Theorem
1.5
. We keep the same setting as that used loc. cit.
Here there is no sum over
U
, and we simply note that on the piece
\gamma_{j}\mathcal{F}^{(n)}_{1}
, with
z\in\mathcal{F}_{1}
and
G=\gamma_{j}^{-1}\Gamma\gamma_{j}
, the bound

(10.1)

\displaystyle B_{k,G}(z)

\displaystyle\leq\omega^{-k}\,b_{1,k}^{-1}\sum\nolimits_{n}n^{k-1}|P_{n;G}(z)|e(-2\pi ny/\omega).

The remaining task is to show that
P_{n;G}(z)\ll 1
for
z\in\mathcal{F}_{1}
, with an absolute implied constant, which is obvious if we just majorize by the majorant
O(\sum_{c,d}|cz+d|^{-3})
, which is absolutely bounded since
k\geq 3
and
y\gg 1
. We now invoke (
3.6
) to finish the proof.
∎

10.2.
The case
n=1
and
k=2

Let
\Gamma\subset\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{Z})
be a congruence subgroup of level
q
and let
\omega
be the width of the cusp
\infty
of
\Gamma
. The bound
O(\log q)
for square-free levels
q
and when
\Gamma=\Gamma_{0}(q)
was obtained in
[
34
]
, which was improved to
O(1)
in
[
27
]
.
In the large sieve inequality that we want, it turns out that the relevant parameter here is
\omega
and not
q
. It seems this inequality is not available in the literature, yet this is crucial for us.
With this in mind, we proceed as in
[
26
, Sec. 5.4]
as follows. We define the normalized Fourier coefficients of
f=\sum_{n\geq 1}a_{f}(n)e(nz/\omega)\in S_{2}(\Gamma)
by

(10.2)

\displaystyle a(f,n)=\frac{a_{f}(n)}{(4\pi n)^{1/2}}.

For any sequence
a=(a_{n})
of complex numbers, we further define the linear form

(10.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(f,a)=\sum\nolimits_{n\leq K}a_{n}a(f,n).

We next quote the formula for the Fourier coefficients of (analytically continued) Poincaré series of weight
2
(see
[
41
]
,
[
35
]
). Let us define the Kloosterman sum for
\Gamma
at the cusp
\infty
(for
c\geq 1
)by

(10.4)

\displaystyle S_{\Gamma}(m,n;c)=\sum\nolimits_{d\in D(c)}e\big(\frac{m\bar{d}+nd}{c\omega}\big)

be the Kloosterman sum at the cusp
\infty
for
\Gamma
. Here
D(c)=\{d\in\mathbf{Z}\mid(c,d)=(c_{\gamma},d_{\gamma})\text{ for some }\gamma\in\Gamma;d\in[0,c\omega]\}
.

Proposition 10.2
.

(10.5)

\displaystyle p_{m}(n)=2\delta(m,n)-\frac{4\pi}{\omega}(\frac{n}{m})^{1/2}\sum_{c=1}^{\infty}\frac{S_{\Gamma}(m,n;c)}{c}J_{1}\big(\frac{4\pi\sqrt{mn}}{c\omega}\big),

where
J_{1}(x)
denotes the Bessel function of the first kind.

We have the Petersson trace formula in this setting:

(10.6)

\sum\nolimits_{f\in\mathscr{B}_{2}(\Gamma)}\overline{a(f,m)}a(f,n)=\frac{2}{\omega^{2}}\big(\frac{m}{n}\big)^{1/2}\,p_{m}(n).

Hypothesis 10.3
.

We make the following assumption about the general Kloosterman sum:

(10.7)

\displaystyle S_{\Gamma}(m,n;c)\ll(c\omega)^{1/2+\epsilon}(m,n,c\omega)^{1/2}.

It is known from Goldfeld-Sarnak (
[
23
]
) that
\displaystyle\limsup_{c\to\infty}|S_{\Gamma}(m,n;c)|c^{-1}\leq 1/2
, and from Petersson (
[
36
]
) that
S_{\Gamma}(m,n;c)\ll_{\epsilon}c^{1/2+\epsilon}
with the implied constant not depending on
n
. With these evidences, it seems likely that the above hypothesis may be proved from known techniques.

We want to offer two proofs of the desired large sieve inequality – one via the argument of Duke
[
18
]
and the other via that of Duke-Friedlander-Iwaniec
[
17
]
. Of these, the former has two advantages over the latter: most importantly, it does not rely on Hypothesis
10.3
on Kloosterman sums and secondly it gives a slightly better bound. We nevertheless include both approaches: this not only serves as a cross check for the result, which is a bit different in terms of a scaling factor than that for the Hecke congruence subgroups
\Gamma_{0}(q)
, but also perhaps provides a different perspective applicable to other situations.

Proposition 10.4
.

Let
\Gamma\subset\Gamma_{0}(q)
be any congruence subgroup of level
q
, and
\omega=\omega_{\Gamma}
be the width of the cusp at
\infty
.

(A) Then with the above notation, one has the bound

(10.8)

\sum\nolimits_{f\in\mathscr{B}_{2}(\Gamma)}|\mathcal{L}(f,a)|^{2}\ll\frac{1}{\omega^{2}}\big(\log K+O(\frac{K}{q\omega})\big)\left\lVert a\right\rVert^{2}.

(B) In addition, assume Hypothesis
10.3
. Then,

(10.9)

\sum\nolimits_{f\in\mathscr{B}_{2}(\Gamma)}|\mathcal{L}(f,a)|^{2}\ll\frac{1}{\omega^{2-\epsilon}}\big(1+O(\frac{K}{q\omega})\big)\left\lVert a\right\rVert^{2}.

Proof of (A).

By the duality of the large sieve (see e.g.
[
18
]
), it is enough to prove for any
f\in\mathscr{B}_{2}(\Gamma)
that

(10.10)

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{n\leq K}|a_{f}(n)|^{2}\ll\frac{K}{\omega^{2}}\big(1+O(\frac{K}{q\omega})\big).

Then Theorem
10.4
, which is for the normalised Fourier coefficients
a(f,n)\asymp a_{f}(n)n^{-1/2}
, follows from (
10.10
) by partial summation.

We will only be brief and mention the important points. We start with

(10.11)

\displaystyle\omega\sum\nolimits_{n\leq K}|a_{f}(n)|^{2}\exp(-4\pi ny/\omega)=\int_{0}^{\omega}|f(x+iy)|^{2}dx.

Keeping in mind that
k=2
here, we arrive at the inequality

(10.12)

\displaystyle\omega Y\int_{1}^{\infty}\exp(-4\pi KYy/\omega)dy\cdot\sum\nolimits_{n\leq K}|a_{f}(n)|^{2}\leq\int_{Y}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\omega}|f(z)|^{2}dxdy.

It we let
P_{Y}:=\{z=x+iy\in\mathbf{H}\mid 0<x\leq\omega,\,y>Y\}
, then as in
[
18
]
, we can write that

(10.13)

\displaystyle\int_{P_{Y}}|f(z)|^{2}dxdy\leq\max_{z\in P_{Y}}\#\{\gamma\in\Gamma\mid\gamma(z)\in P_{Y}\}\langle f,f\rangle

because
P_{Y}
can be covered by finitely many translates of the fundamental domain
\mathcal{F}_{\Gamma}
, which we assume to be without loss inside the strip
\{0<\mathrm{Re}(z)\leq\omega\}
(recall that one has
\Gamma_{\infty}=<\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&\omega\mathbf{Z}\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)>
). The count of
\gamma
goes through without any change as only imaginary parts of
z,\gamma(z)
play a role. We get

(10.14)

\displaystyle\max_{z\in P_{Y}}\#\{\gamma\in\Gamma\mid\gamma(z)\in P_{Y}\}\ll 1+3q^{-1}Y^{-1}.

At this point, choosing
Y:=\omega K^{-1}
finishes the proof of (A).
∎

Proof of (B).

We will closely follow the argument given in
[
26
, Sec. 5.4]
, and mention only the relevant points. Opening the square on the LHS of (
10.9
) and using (
10.5
), (
10.6
), we get

(10.15)

\displaystyle\sum_{f\in\mathscr{B}_{2}(\Gamma)}|\mathcal{L}(f,a)|^{2}=\frac{2}{\omega^{2}}\bigg(2\left\lVert a\right\rVert^{2}+\sum_{c\geq 1}\frac{1}{c\omega}{\underset{m,n\leq K}{\sum\sum}}\overline{a_{m}}a_{n}\,S_{\Gamma}(m,n;c)\,J_{1}\big(\frac{4\pi\sqrt{mn}}{c\omega}\big)\bigg)

Analogously as in
[
26
, Sec. 5.4]
we arrive at

(10.16)

\displaystyle|{\underset{m,n\leq K}{\sum\sum}}\overline{a_{m}}a_{n}\,S_{\Gamma}(m,n;c)|\leq c\omega{\underset{m\equiv n\bmod{c\omega}}{\sum\sum}}\overline{a_{m}}a_{n}\leq(c\omega+K)\left\lVert a\right\rVert^{2}.

Here we have used the fact that the sets
\{d\in D(c,\Gamma)\mid d\bmod{c\omega}\}
and
\{a\in D(c,\Gamma)\mid a\bmod{c\omega}\}
are in bijection via
\gamma\mapsto\gamma^{-1}
.
Assuming the bound from Hypothesis
10.3
and arguing as in [DFI], we also get the bound (this bound is relevant only for
k=2
)

(10.17)

\displaystyle|{\underset{m,n\leq K}{\sum\sum}}\overline{a_{m}}a_{n}\,S_{\Gamma}(m,n;c)|\leq(c\omega)^{1/2+\epsilon}K\left\lVert a\right\rVert^{2}.

When
K\leq\omega
, we use the Taylor series for the Bessel function and (
10.16
) to get that the summand in (
10.15
) as a function of
c
is

\displaystyle O\big(\frac{K}{c^{2}\omega^{2}}(c\omega+K)\left\lVert a\right\rVert^{2}

)
,
which we use to bound the contribution of
c\leq K
.
Whereas if we use (
10.17
), the same quantity as above is

\displaystyle O\big(\frac{K}{c^{2}\omega^{2}}(c\omega)^{1/2+\epsilon}K\left\lVert a\right\rVert^{2}\big)
,
which we use to bound the contribution of
c>K
.
The rest of the argument proceeds as in
[
26
, Sec. 5.4]
and as long as
q\omega\geq K
and the bound reads
\displaystyle O(\omega^{-2+\epsilon})
.

When
q\omega<K
, one resorts to a level embedding technique, which is a bit subtle here. For a prime
p
, we take
\Gamma^{\prime}:=\Gamma^{0}(p)\cap\Gamma
, and note that
[\Gamma:\Gamma^{\prime}]\leq p+1
from the natural inclusion
\Gamma^{\prime}\backslash\Gamma\hookrightarrow\Gamma^{0}(p)\backslash\Gamma_{1}
. We further assume
(p,\omega)=1
, so that the width of
\Gamma^{\prime}
at
\infty
is
p\omega
. We choose
p
such that
K<pq\omega\ll K
and one note that

(10.18)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{f\in\mathscr{B}_{2}(\Gamma)}|\mathcal{L}(f,a)|^{2}\leq(p+1)\sum_{f\in\mathscr{B}_{2}(\Gamma^{\prime})}|\mathcal{L}(f,a)|^{2}\ll\frac{1}{p^{2}\omega^{2-\epsilon}}(p+1)\ll\frac{1}{\omega^{2-\epsilon}}\big(1+O(\frac{K}{q\omega})\big)\left\lVert a\right\rVert^{2},

as desired. This finishes the proof.
∎

Theorem 10.5
.

Let
\Gamma\subset\Gamma_{0}(q)
be any congruence subgroup of level
q
. Then one has the bound
\sup(S_{2}(\Gamma))\ll\log q
.

Proof.

The proof is immediate from the large sieve inequality Proposition
10.4
(A). In view of the Algorithm
7.1
, is enough to prove that
\mathbb{B}_{G}(z)\ll 1
for all conjugates
G
of
\Gamma
and for all
z\in\mathcal{F}_{1}
.

First of all, using the trivial bound for the Kloosterman sums
S_{\Gamma}(m,n;c)
one can easily prove a bound

\displaystyle a(f,n)\ll n^{D}
for some absolute
D>0
. We can then truncate the Fourier expansion at
K=\delta q\log q
for appropriate
\delta
depending only on
D
, for the normalized Fourier coefficients
a(f,n)
for any
f\in\mathscr{B}_{2}(\Gamma)
, via the Petersson formula. E.g., see
[
34
]
where arithmetic new-old basis decomposition and Deligne’s bound were used. We don’t need any of them. The proof is finished by noting that

(10.19)

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}_{G}(z)\leq\sum\nolimits_{f\in\mathscr{B}_{2}(\Gamma)}y^{2}\left|\left(\sum\nolimits_{n\leq K}+\sum\nolimits_{n>K}\right)a_{f}(n)e(nz/\omega)\right|^{2}.

We define the sequence
a=(a_{n})
by
a_{n}:=\sqrt{n}e(nz/\omega)
and put
\omega=\omega_{G}
. Then we can write,

(10.20)

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}_{G}(z)\leq\sum\nolimits_{f\in\mathscr{B}_{2}(\Gamma)}y^{2}|\mathcal{L}(f,a)|^{2}+\sum\nolimits_{f\in\mathscr{B}_{2}(\Gamma)}y^{2}|\sum\nolimits_{n>K}a_{f}(n)e(nz/\omega)|^{2}

Let us call the first and second sums above as I and II respectively. Then

(10.21)

\displaystyle\mathrm{I}\ll\frac{y^{2}}{\omega^{2}}(1+\frac{K}{q\omega})\sum_{n\leq K}n\exp(-4\pi ny/\omega)\ll(1+\frac{K}{q\omega})\ll\log q.

A bound for the
n
-sum is precisely worked out on
[
34
, p. 662]
with
y
replaced with
y/\omega
,
N
with
\omega
. This works mutatis mutandis since
\omega|N
. We require
\delta>1/4\pi
. We do not repeat the details.

For the sum II, one actually has an exponential decay as it can be compared with the incomplete Gamma function. Note that for us
y\gg 1
.

(10.22)

\displaystyle y\sum\nolimits_{n>K}a_{f}(n)e(nz/\omega)\leq y\sum\nolimits_{n>K}a_{f}(n)\exp(-2\pi ny/\omega)\ll y\sum\nolimits_{n>K}n^{2D}\exp(-2\pi ny/\omega).

Recall (see e.g.
[
21
]
) that the incomplete Gamma function, for
a>1
, defined by
\displaystyle\Gamma(a,x):=\int_{x}^{\infty}t^{a-1}e^{-x}dx
satisfies the bound

(10.23)

\displaystyle\Gamma(a,x)\leq x^{a-1}e^{-x}\left(1-\frac{a-1}{x}\right)^{-1},\quad x>a-1.

Recall that
K=\delta q\log q
for suitable
\delta>0
. We thus have

(10.24)

\displaystyle y\sum\nolimits_{n>K}a_{f}(n)e(nz/\omega)

\displaystyle\ll y\cdot\left(\frac{\omega}{y}\right)^{D+1}\cdot\Gamma\left(D+1,\frac{K}{\omega}\right)

(10.25)

\displaystyle\ll q^{D+1}K^{D}\exp\left(-\frac{K}{\omega}\right)\ll q^{3D}\exp\left(-\frac{K}{q}\right)\ll q^{3D}\exp(-\delta\log q)\ll 1

for
\delta\gg D
. Since
D
was an absolute constant, this finishes the proof if we combine the bounds in (
10.21
) and (
10.25
).
∎
