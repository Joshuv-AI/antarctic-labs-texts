Nonlinear conditions for ultradifferentiability: a uniform approach Thanks: AR was supported by FWF-Project P 32905-N, DNN and GS by FWF-Project P 33417-N
David Nicolas Nenning, Armin Rainer, and Gerhard Schindl

Abstract
. Recent work showed that a theorem of Joris (that a function f f is smooth if two coprime powers of f f are smooth) is valid in a wide variety of ultradifferentiable classes 𝒞 \mathcal{C} . The core of the proof was essentially 1 1 -dimensional. In certain cases a multidimensional version resulted from subtle reduction arguments, but general validity, notably in the quasianalytic setting, remained open. In this paper we give a uniform proof which works in all cases and dimensions. It yields the result even on infinite dimensional Banach spaces and convenient vector spaces. We also consider more general nonlinear conditions, namely general analytic germs Φ \Phi instead of the powers, and characterize when Φ ∘ f ∈ 𝒞 \Phi\circ f\in\mathcal{C} implies f ∈ 𝒞 f\in\mathcal{C} .

1.
Introduction

A function
f
is smooth provided that two relatively prime powers or, equivalently, two consecutive powers of
f
are smooth,
by a theorem of Joris
[
4
]
.
In the recent paper
[
17
]
Thilliez showed that this result carries over to
Denjoy–Carleman classes of Roumieu type and,
by refining Thilliez’s method, we proved in
[
11
]
that it is valid in a wide variety of ultradifferentiable classes.
See the introduction in
[
11
]
for more on the historical development.

In this analysis the dimension of the domain of
f
had some substantial significance.
The proof in dimension one was based on holomorphic approximation
and utilized tools of complex analysis in one variable.
Multidimensional versions could be obtained only by some subtle reduction arguments (i.e., variants of Boman’s theorem
[
1
]
and
Beurling-to-Roumieu reduction).
Consequently, general validity of the result in all dimensions remained open in cases, most notably the quasianalytic case,
where the suitable reduction tools are not available.
See the table in the introduction of
[
11
]
for a summary of the known results
(in that paper the result in question was called property (
\mathscr{D}
)).

In the present paper we prove general validity in all dimensions and all cases.
In fact, the uniformity of our proof allows us to extend the result even to infinite dimensional Banach spaces and convenient vector spaces.
The crucial observation is that, in arbitrary dimension, holomorphic approximation in dimension one
can be replaced by uniform unidirectional holomorphic approximation combined with the polarization inequality.

Having proved that
\Phi\circ f\in\mathcal{C}
implies
f\in\mathcal{C}
for
\Phi(t)=(t^{j},t^{j+1})
,
j\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
, and
\mathcal{C}
a suitable ultradifferentiable class, it
is natural to ask what other
\Phi
have this property. This question was studied by
[
2
]
and
[
5
]
in the
C^{\infty}
-setting.
By combining slightly adjusted arguments of
[
5
]
with our result, we obtain a full characterization of the analytic germs

\Phi:(\mathbb{K},0)\to(\mathbb{K}^{n},0)
(where
\mathbb{K}
is
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
) having this property in terms of the support of the Taylor series of
\Phi
.
For example, it shows that
f\in\mathcal{C}
provided that
f^{2}+f^{3}\in\mathcal{C}
and
f^{p}\in\mathcal{C}
, where
p
is any positive integer.

The outline of the paper is the following.
Our main result is Theorem
1.1
.
For its formulation we first need to define ultradifferentiable classes (Section
1.1
) and
specify the required assumptions (Section
1.2
).
The proof of Theorem
1.1
is given in Section
2
, and
in Section

Finally, in Section

we prove the characterization alluded to above.

1.1.
Ultradifferentiable classes

Let
\mathfrak{M}
be a family of positive sequences
M=(M_{k})_{k\geq 0}
.
Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be an open subset.
The class
\mathcal{E}^{\{\mathfrak{M}\}}(U)
consists, by definition, of all complex valued functions
f\in C^{\infty}(U)
with the following property:
for all compact subsets
K\subseteq U
there exist
M\in\mathfrak{M}
and
\rho>0
such that

\|f\|^{M}_{K,\rho}:=\sup_{x\in K}\sup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{\|f^{(k)}(x)\|_{L^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{C})}}{\rho^{k}M_{k}}<\infty.

(1)

Moreover,
\mathcal{E}^{(\mathfrak{M})}(U)
consists of all complex valued functions
f\in C^{\infty}(U)
such that
(
1
) holds for all compact
K\subseteq U
, all
M\in\mathfrak{M}
, and all
\rho>0
.
Here
f^{(k)}
denotes the Fréchet derivative of order
k
of
f
and

\|f^{(k)}(x)\|_{L^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{C})}:=\sup\{|f^{(k)}(x)(v_{1},\ldots,v_{k})|:\|v_{i}\|\leq 1\}

is the operator norm.
In view of the polarization inequality (cf.
[
7
, 7.13.1]
)

\sup_{\|v\|\leq 1}|d_{v}^{k}f(x)|\leq\|f^{(k)}(x)\|_{L^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{C})}\leq(2e)^{k}\sup_{\|v\|\leq 1}|d_{v}^{k}f(x)|,

(2)

where
d_{v}^{k}f(x):=\partial_{t}^{k}f(x+tv)|_{t=0}
, we can equivalently use

\sup_{x\in K}\sup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\sup_{\|v\|\leq 1}\frac{|d_{v}^{k}f(x)|}{\rho^{k}M_{k}}<\infty

(3)

instead of (
1
) in the definition of

\mathcal{E}^{\{\mathfrak{M}\}}(U)
and
\mathcal{E}^{(\mathfrak{M})}(U)
.

The global classes
\mathcal{B}^{\{\mathfrak{M}\}}(U)
and
\mathcal{B}^{(\mathfrak{M})}(U)
are defined by taking the supremum over all
x\in U

in (
1
) (that is
\|f\|^{M}_{U,\rho}<\infty
) or equivalently (
3
).
We shall also need the Banach space
\mathcal{B}^{M}_{\rho}(U):=\{f\in C^{\infty}(U):\|f\|^{M}_{U,\rho}<\infty\}
.

The classes
\mathcal{E}^{\{\mathfrak{M}\}}
and
\mathcal{B}^{\{\mathfrak{M}\}}
are said to be of
Roumieu type
,

\mathcal{E}^{(\mathfrak{M})}
and
\mathcal{B}^{(\mathfrak{M})}
of
Beurling type
.
By convention, we use
[\cdot]
as a placeholder for
\{\cdot\}
and
(\cdot)
.

1.2.
Admissible weight matrices

We need to impose some conditions on
\mathfrak{M}
.

Definition
.

A family
\mathfrak{M}
of positive sequences
M=(M_{k})_{k\geq 0}
is said to be a
weight matrix
if

\mathfrak{M}
is totally ordered with respect to the pointwise order relation on sequences, and
each
M\in\mathfrak{M}
is log-convex with
M_{0}=1\leq M_{1}
and
M_{k}^{1/k}\to\infty
.
A positive sequence
M
with these properties is called a
weight sequence
.

With
M
we associate the sequence
m
given by
k!\,m_{k}=M_{k}
.
Assuming that
m_{k}^{1/k}\to\infty
we consider the function

h_{m}(t):=\inf_{k\in\mathbb{N}}m_{k}t^{k},\quad\text{ for }t>0,\quad\text{ and }\quad h_{m}(0):=0,

which is increasing, continuous on
[0,\infty)
, positive for
t>0
and equals
1
for large
t
.
For
t>0
we put

\displaystyle\overline{\Gamma}_{m}(t)

\displaystyle:=\min\{k:h_{m}(t)=m_{k}t^{k}\}

and

\displaystyle\underline{\Gamma}_{m}(t)

\displaystyle:=\min\Big\{k:\frac{m_{k+1}}{m_{k}}\geq\frac{1}{t}\Big\}.

Trivially,
\underline{\Gamma}_{m}\leq\overline{\Gamma}_{m}
.
Equality
\overline{\Gamma}_{m}=\underline{\Gamma}_{m}
holds,
if
m
is log-convex.

For positive sequences
M
,
N
we set

\operatorname{mg}(M,N):=\sup_{j,k\geq 0,\,j+k\geq 1}\left(\frac{M_{j+k}}{N_{j}N_{k}}\right)^{1/(j+k)}\in(0,\infty].

Note that
\operatorname{mg}(M,M)<\infty
is the condition (M.2) of Komatsu
[
6
]
often called
moderate growth
.

Definition
.

A weight matrix
\mathfrak{M}
satisfying
m_{k}^{1/k}\to\infty
for all
M\in\mathfrak{M}
is
called

•

\{\text{\it admissible}\}
or
R-admissible
if

–

\forall M\in\mathfrak{M}\;\exists N\in\mathfrak{M}\;\exists C\geq 1\;\forall t>0:\overline{\Gamma}_{n}(Ct)\leq\underline{\Gamma}_{m}(t)
,

–

\forall M\in\mathfrak{M}\;\exists N\in\mathfrak{M}:\operatorname{mg}(M,N)<\infty.

•

(\text{\it admissible})
or
B-admissible
if

–

\forall M\in\mathfrak{M}\;\exists N\in\mathfrak{M}\;\exists C\geq 1\;\forall t>0:\overline{\Gamma}_{m}(Ct)\leq\underline{\Gamma}_{n}(t)
,

–

\forall M\in\mathfrak{M}\;\exists N\in\mathfrak{M}:\operatorname{mg}(N,M)<\infty.

By our convention,
[\text{admissible}]
stands for
\{\text{admissible}\}
(i.e. R-admissible) in the Roumieu case and

(\text{admissible})
(i.e. B-admissible) in the Beurling case.

Remark
.

In the terminology of
[
11
]
a weight matrix is
[\text{admissible}]

if and only if it is
[\text{regular}]
and has
[\text{moderate growth}]
.

These are the minimal requirements needed for the tools used in our proof; see also
Remark
4.3
in which the necessity of these assumptions is discussed.
Note that [admissibility] of the weight matrix
\mathfrak{M}
neither implies nor obstructs
quasianalyticity of the corresponding class
\mathcal{E}^{[\mathfrak{M}]}
. In fact, (non-)quasianalyticity
plays no role in our analysis.

1.3.
Main results

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\mathfrak{M}
be an [admissible] weight matrix.
Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be an open subset.
Then a function
f:U\to\mathbb{C}
belongs to

\mathcal{E}^{[\mathfrak{M}]}(U)
if
f^{j},f^{j+1}\in\mathcal{E}^{[\mathfrak{M}]}(U)
for some positive integer
j
.

If we apply the theorem to the weight matrix which consists but of a single weight sequence
M

(with
m_{k}^{1/k}\to\infty
,
\overline{\Gamma}_{m}(Ct)\leq\underline{\Gamma}_{m}(t)
for some
C\geq 1
, and
\operatorname{mg}(M,M)<\infty
),
then we immediately get a version for Denjoy–Carleman classes.

As another special case we obtain the following result for Braun–Meise–Taylor classes
\mathcal{E}^{[\omega]}
.
For their definition and why it is a special case of Theorem
1.1
we refer the reader to
[
11
, Section 4.5]

and the references therein.
By a
weight function
we mean a continuous increasing function
\omega:[0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,\infty)

such that

•

\omega(2t)=O(\omega(t))
as
t\rightarrow\infty
,

•

\omega(t)=o(t)
as
t\rightarrow\infty
,

•

\log(t)=o(\omega(t))
as
t\rightarrow\infty
,

•

t\mapsto\omega(e^{t})=:\varphi_{\omega}(t)
is convex on
[0,\infty)
.

Corollary 1.2
.

Let
\omega
be a concave weight function.
Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be an open subset.
Then a function
f:U\to\mathbb{C}
belongs to

\mathcal{E}^{[\omega]}(U)
if
f^{j},f^{j+1}\in\mathcal{E}^{[\omega]}(U)
for some positive integer
j
.

Actually, for a given weight function
\omega
, the conclusion of Corollary
1.2
holds
if and only if

\omega
is equivalent to a concave weight function,
as we shall see in Remark
4.3
. Two weight functions
\omega_{i}
,
i=1,2
, are said to be
equivalent
if

\omega_{1}(t)=O(\omega_{2}(t))
and
\omega_{2}(t)=O(\omega_{1}(t))
as
t\to\infty

which holds if and only if

\mathcal{E}^{[\omega_{1}]}=\mathcal{E}^{[\omega_{2}]}
.

3.
On Banach spaces and on convenient vector spaces

3.1.
Ultradifferentiable classes of functions on Banach spaces

Let
E
be a Banach space and
U\subseteq E
an open subset.
Let
\mathfrak{M}
be a weight matrix.
For
M\in\mathfrak{M}
and
\rho>0
we consider
\mathcal{B}^{M}_{\rho}(U)=\{f\in C^{\infty}(U,\mathbb{C}):\|f\|^{M}_{U,\rho}<\infty\}

as well as

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}^{\{\mathfrak{M}\}}(U)

\displaystyle:=\{f\in C^{\infty}(U,\mathbb{C}):\;\exists M\in\mathfrak{M}\;\exists\rho>0:\|f\|^{M}_{U,\rho}<\infty\}\quad\text{ and}

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}^{(\mathfrak{M})}(U)

\displaystyle:=\{f\in C^{\infty}(U,\mathbb{C}):\;\forall M\in\mathfrak{M}\;\forall\rho>0:\|f\|^{M}_{U,\rho}<\infty\},

where
\|f\|^{M}_{U,\rho}
is defined by (
1
) (with
\mathbb{R}^{d}
replaced by
E
).
Furthermore, we consider the local classes

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\{\mathfrak{M}\}}(U)

\displaystyle:=\{f\in C^{\infty}(U,\mathbb{C}):\;\forall K\subseteq_{cp}U\;\exists M\in\mathfrak{M}\;\exists\rho>0:\|f\|^{M}_{K,\rho}<\infty\}\quad\text{ and}

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{(\mathfrak{M})}(U)

\displaystyle:=\{f\in C^{\infty}(U,\mathbb{C}):\;\forall K\subseteq_{cp}U\;\forall M\in\mathfrak{M}\;\forall\rho>0:\|f\|^{M}_{K,\rho}<\infty\}.

Let
B(a,r):=\{x\in E:\|x-a\|<r\}
be the open ball with center
a
and radius
r
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
f:U\to\mathbb{C}
be smooth. Then:

(1)

f\in\mathcal{E}^{\{\mathfrak{M}\}}(U)
if and only if

\;\forall a\in U\;\exists M\in\mathfrak{M}\;\exists\rho>0\;\exists r>0:f|_{B(a,r)}\in\mathcal{B}^{M}_{\rho}(B(a,r)).

(18)

(2)

f\in\mathcal{E}^{(\mathfrak{M})}(U)
if and only if

\;\forall a\in U\;\forall M\in\mathfrak{M}\;\forall\rho>0\;\exists r>0:f|_{B(a,r)}\in\mathcal{B}^{M}_{\rho}(B(a,r)).

(19)

Proof.

(1)
That (
18
) is sufficient for
f\in\mathcal{E}^{\{\mathfrak{M}\}}(U)
is easily seen.
Fix a compact subset
K\subseteq U
.
For each
a\in K
we get
M_{a}\in\mathfrak{M}
,
\rho_{a}>0
, and a small ball
B(a,r_{a})
such that

f|_{B(a,r_{a})}\in\mathcal{B}^{M_{a}}_{\rho_{a}}(B(a,r_{a}))
.
Now
K
is still covered by finitely many of the balls and we find
\|f\|^{M}_{K,\rho}<\infty
with

M:=\max M_{a}
and
\rho:=\max\rho_{a}
, where the maxima are taken over the corresponding
a
.

Let us show that
f\in\mathcal{E}^{\{\mathfrak{M}\}}(U)
implies (
18
).
If (
18
) does not hold, then
there exists
a\in U
such that

\displaystyle\;\forall M\in\mathfrak{M}\;\forall\rho>0\;\forall r>0\;\forall C>0\;\exists x\in B(a,r)\;\exists k\in\mathbb{N}:\|f^{(k)}(x)\|_{L^{k}(E,\mathbb{C})}\geq C\rho^{k}M_{k}.

By
[
3
, Lemma 2.5]
, we may assume that
\mathfrak{M}
is indexed by
\mathbb{N}
in the following way:

\mathfrak{M}=\{M^{(\ell)}:\ell\in\mathbb{N}\}
and
M^{(\ell_{1})}\leq M^{(\ell_{2})}
if and only if
\ell_{1}\leq\ell_{2}
.
In particular, taking
C=\rho=n
,
r=\tfrac{1}{n}
and
M=M^{(n)}
, we find

\displaystyle\;\forall n\in\mathbb{N}\;\exists x_{n}\text{ with }\|x_{n}-a\|<\tfrac{1}{n}\;\exists k_{n}\in\mathbb{N}:\|f^{(k_{n})}(x_{n})\|_{L^{k_{n}}(E,\mathbb{C})}\geq n^{k_{n}+1}M^{(n)}_{k_{n}}.

For the compact set
K:=\{x_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\cup\{a\}

this means that
\|f\|^{M}_{K,\rho}=\infty
for all choices of
M\in\mathfrak{M}
and
\rho>0
,
contradicting
f\in\mathcal{E}^{\{\mathfrak{M}\}}(U)
.

(2)
That (
19
) is sufficient for
f\in\mathcal{E}^{(\mathfrak{M})}(U)
is again easy to check.
For the necessity assume that (
19
) does not hold.
Then
there exist
a\in U
,
M\in\mathfrak{M}
, and
\rho>0
such that

\displaystyle\;\forall r>0\;\forall C>0\;\exists x\in B(a,r)\;\exists k\in\mathbb{N}:\|f^{(k)}(x)\|_{L^{k}(E,\mathbb{C})}\geq C\rho^{k}M_{k}.

Taking
C=n
and
r=\tfrac{1}{n}
we get

\displaystyle\;\forall n\in\mathbb{N}\;\exists x_{n}\text{ with }\|x_{n}-a\|<\tfrac{1}{n}\;\exists k_{n}\in\mathbb{N}:\|f^{(k_{n})}(x_{n})\|_{L^{k_{n}}(E,\mathbb{C})}\geq n\,\rho^{k_{n}}M_{k_{n}}.

Thus
\|f\|^{M}_{K,\rho}=\infty
for
K:=\{x_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\cup\{a\}
,
contradicting
f\in\mathcal{E}^{(\mathfrak{M})}(U)
.
∎

3.2.
Theorem

holds on Banach spaces

Let
E
be a Banach space and
U\subseteq E

Let

Then Theorem

and Proposition

remain valid for functions

(with literally the same proofs).
Note that the classical Joris theorem is valid on Banach spaces by Boman’s theorem.

Theorem 3.2
.

(and thus Corollary

) hold if

is an open subset of a Banach space

.

Proof.

We treat Roumieu and Beurling case separately.

Roumieu case.
Assume that

.
Fix
a\in U
; without loss of generality we may assume that
a
is the origin in

.
By Lemma
3.1
, there exist
M\in\mathfrak{M}
,
\rho>0
, and

such that

Then

.
By R-admissibility of
\mathfrak{M}
,
we find sequences

satisfying the assumptions of Proposition

which implies that the restriction of

Then

.

Beurling case.
Assume that

,

, and

; we may again assume that

By B-admissibility of

, there are sequences

as required in Proposition

with

.

be the constant from Proposition

.

, there exists

4.
More general nonlinear conditions

As seen above the map

\Phi(t)=(t^{j},t^{j+1})
, where
j
is some positive integer, has the property

\Phi\circ f\in\mathcal{E}^{[\mathfrak{M}]}\implies f\in\mathcal{E}^{[\mathfrak{M}]},

(20)

provided that
\mathfrak{M}
is an [admissible] weight matrix.
Here
t
is a complex or a real variable.
Equivalently, the map
\Phi(t)=(t^{p},t^{q})
, where
p
and
q
are positive coprime integers,
has property (
20
); indeed, all integers
j\geq pq
have the form
j=ap+bq
with
a,b\in\mathbb{N}
.

The question arises which maps
\Phi
have the property (
20
) for all continuous
f
. From now on
we take continuity of
f
as a basic assumption.
(The continuity of
f
was not an issue in Theorem
1.1
, since one of the powers is necessarily odd and hence
has a global continuous inverse.)
This problem was investigated in
[
2
]
and in
[
5
]

in the
C^{\infty}
-setting. In the latter article the smooth germs
\Phi:(\mathbb{R},0)\to(\mathbb{R}^{n},0)

(and
\Phi:(\mathbb{C},0)\to(\mathbb{C}^{n},0)
which possess a complex Taylor series, cf.
[
5
, p.204]
)
with the property that
\Phi\circ f\in C^{\infty}
implies
f\in C^{\infty}
for all continuous germs
f
with
f(0)=0

were characterized in terms of a condition on the support of the formal Taylor series of
f
.
They were called
pseudo-immersive
germs.

The proof is based (similar to most proofs in
[
2
]
) on a reduction to the
case
\Phi(t)=(t^{p},t^{q})
with
\operatorname{gcd}(p,q)=1
which is mostly algebraic and hence applies to our situation with marginal adjustments.
We thus obtain a characterization of the
analytic
germs
\Phi
satisfying (
20
)
for all continuous germs
f
with
f(0)=0
by the same condition on the support of the Taylor series.
Note that [admissibility] of the weight matrix
\mathfrak{M}
implies that
\mathcal{E}^{[\mathfrak{M}]}
contains all analytic maps
and is stable under composition (by Corollaries 3.3 and 3.5 and Proposition 1.1 in
[
3
]
).
See also Remark
4.4
.

4.1.
A necessary condition

Let
\mathbb{K}
be
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
.
Let
\Phi:(\mathbb{K},0)\to(\mathbb{K}^{n},0)
be the germ of a non-zero analytic map.
Then
\Phi
is represented by its convergent Taylor series

\Phi(t)=\sum_{1\leq k<N}a_{k}t^{n_{k}},\quad\text{ where }a_{k}\in\mathbb{K}^{n}\setminus\{0\}\text{ for all }k,

1\leq n_{1}<n_{2}<\cdots
,
and
1\leq N\leq\infty
. That means that
\{n_{1},n_{2},\ldots\}
is the
support
of the power series
\Phi(t)
.

If (
20
) holds for all continuous germs
f
with
f(0)=0
, then
\operatorname{gcd}(n_{1},n_{2},\ldots)=1
.
Indeed, if there are integers
p\geq 2
and
\ell_{k}
such that
n_{k}=p\ell_{k}
for all
k
,
then the power series
\sum_{k}a_{k}x^{\ell_{k}}
is convergent, defines an analytic germ
\Psi
so that
\Phi(t)=\Psi(t^{p})
,
and it is easy to find a continuous germ
f\not\in C^{1}
with
f(0)=0
such that
\Phi\circ f
is analytic;
cf.
[
5
, Theorem 2]
.

4.2.
A characterization

We will see that the condition
\operatorname{gcd}(n_{1},n_{2},\ldots)=1
is also sufficient for (
20
)
at least up to equivalence of analytic germs.
Two analytic germs
\Phi,\Psi:(\mathbb{K},0)\to(\mathbb{K}^{n},0)
are said to be
equivalent
if there exist
germs of analytic diffeomorphisms
u:(\mathbb{K}^{n},0)\to(\mathbb{K}^{n},0)
and
v:(\mathbb{K},0)\to(\mathbb{K},0)
such that

u\circ\Phi\circ v=\Psi
.
Equivalent germs either both satisfy or do not satisfy (
20
).
Any non-zero analytic germ
\Phi:(\mathbb{K},0)\to(\mathbb{K}^{n},0)
is
equivalent to a germ whose first component is a positive power.
So it is no restriction to assume
\Phi_{1}(t)=t^{p}
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\Phi=(\Phi_{1},\ldots,\Phi_{n}):(\mathbb{K},0)\to(\mathbb{K}^{n},0)
be an analytic germ such that

\Phi_{1}(t)=t^{p}

and let
\{n_{1},n_{2},\ldots\}
be the support of the power series
\Phi(t)
.
Then the following conditions are equivalent:

(1)

Let
\mathfrak{M}
be an [admissible] weight matrix.
If
f:(\mathbb{R}^{d},0)\to(\mathbb{K},0)
is a function germ and
\Phi\circ f
is of class
\mathcal{E}^{[\mathfrak{M}]}
, then

f
is of class
\mathcal{E}^{[\mathfrak{M}]}
.

(2)

Let
\omega
be a concave weight function.
If
f:(\mathbb{R}^{d},0)\to(\mathbb{K},0)
is a function germ and
\Phi\circ f
is of class
\mathcal{E}^{[\omega]}
, then

f
is of class
\mathcal{E}^{[\omega]}
.

(3)

\operatorname{gcd}(n_{1},n_{2},\ldots)=1
.

Proof.

Since (2) is a special case of (1) (cf.
[
11
, Section 4.5]
) and the
necessity of (3) for (2) follows from the discussion above,
it remains to show that (3) implies (1).
This is achieved by adjusting the arguments in the proofs of Theorems 3 and 3’ in
[
5
]
;
actually the proof simplifies a bit, since all series involved in the arguments are convergent.

We may assume that
p\geq 2
and
n=2
.
In fact, if
p=1
there is nothing to prove and if
n>2
then one
can find real constants
\gamma_{i}
such that the support of the power series

(t^{p},\gamma_{2}\Phi_{2}(t)+\cdots+\gamma_{n}\Phi_{n}(t))
satisfies (3).

Thus we may assume that
\Phi(t)=(t^{p},\varphi(t))
, where
\varphi(t)=\sum_{1\leq k<N}a_{k}t^{n_{k}}

with
a_{k}\in\mathbb{K}\setminus\{0\}
for all
k
.
Then (3) takes the form
\operatorname{gcd}(p,n_{1},n_{2},\ldots)=1
.
The idea is to show that there exist a positive integer
q
and analytic germs
\alpha_{j}
at
0
such that

t^{1+pq}=\sum_{j=0}^{p-1}\alpha_{j}(t^{p})\varphi(t)^{j}.

(21)

Then (1) follows from Theorem
1.1
(by plugging
f
into (
21
)).

We will sketch how to establish (
21
) following the steps in
[
5
]
;
the main difference is that in
[
5
]
all involved series are formal (power or Laurent) series
so that at the end an application of Borel’s lemma and some handling of the flat terms is required.

Let
z
be an indeterminate. Recall that
\mathbb{K}\{z\}
denotes the ring of convergent power series in
z
with coefficients in
\mathbb{K}
.
The convergent power series

\varphi(z)=\sum_{1\leq k<N}a_{k}z^{n_{k}}
has the form

\varphi(z)=\varphi_{0}(z^{p})+z\varphi_{1}(z^{p})+\cdots+z^{p-1}\varphi_{p-1}(z^{p})

where
\varphi_{j}(z^{p})\in\mathbb{K}\{z^{p}\}
for all
j=0,\ldots,p-1
.
Introducing another independent indeterminate
x
, set

S(x):=\varphi_{0}(z^{p})+x\varphi_{1}(z^{p})+\cdots+x^{p-1}\varphi_{p-1}(z^{p})\in\mathbb{K}\{z^{p}\}[x].

Furthermore, define

\displaystyle G_{kj}(x):=S(x)^{j}x^{kp},\quad j=0,1,\ldots,p-1,~k=0,1,\ldots,(p-1)^{2}.

Let
\mathbb{K}\{\!\{z\}\!\}
denote the field of fractions of
\mathbb{K}\{z\}
. It is easy to see that

\mathbb{K}\{\!\{z\}\!\}=\{z^{n}g(z):n\in\mathbb{Z},\,g(z)\in\mathbb{K}\{z\}\}
.
We look for nontrivial
a_{kj}\in\mathbb{K}\{\!\{z^{p}\}\!\}
such that

\sum_{j=0}^{p-1}\sum_{k=0}^{(p-1)^{2}}a_{kj}G_{kj}(x)=xH(x^{p})

with
H(x^{p})\in\mathbb{K}\{\!\{z^{p}\}\!\}[x^{p}]
. This amounts to an underdetermined homogeneous system of linear equations
over the field
\mathbb{K}\{\!\{z^{p}\}\!\}
; see
[
5
, p.201]
for details.
So we find

a_{kj}\in\mathbb{K}\{\!\{z^{p}\}\!\}
for
j=0,1,\ldots,p-1
,
k=0,1,\ldots,r
, and some
r\geq 0
, not all zero,
and
H(x^{p})\in\mathbb{K}\{\!\{z^{p}\}\!\}[x^{p}]
such that

\sum_{j=0}^{p-1}\sum_{k=0}^{r}a_{kj}G_{kj}(x)=xH(x^{p}).

Regrouping the terms on the left-hand side, we obtain

A_{j}(x^{p})\in\mathbb{K}\{\!\{z^{p}\}\!\}[x^{p}]
,
j=0,1,\ldots,p-1
, not all zero,
such that

\sum_{j=0}^{p-1}A_{j}(x^{p})S(x)^{j}=xH(x^{p}).

(22)

Taking
r
as small as possible, at least one of the series
A_{0}(z^{p})
, … ,
A_{p-1}(z^{p})
,
which are obtained by replacing
x^{p}
with
z^{p}
, is non-zero and also
H(z^{p})
is non-zero.
Indeed, if
H(z^{p})=0
then (
22
) shows that
\varphi(z)=S(z)
is algebraic over

\mathbb{K}\{\!\{z^{p}\}\!\}\subseteq\mathbb{K}(\!(z^{p})\!)\subseteq\mathbb{C}(\!(z^{p})\!)

of degree at most
p-1
; here
\mathbb{K}(\!(z)\!)
is the field of formal Laurent series in
z
with coefficients in
\mathbb{K}
.
The automorphism
\sigma
of
\mathbb{C}(\!(z)\!)
induced by
z\mapsto\xi z
, where
\xi=e^{2\pi i/p}
, leaves invariant
\mathbb{C}(\!(z^{p})\!)
and
generates the cyclic group
\langle\sigma\rangle
.
Thus
\varphi(z)
(as an element of
\mathbb{C}(\!(z)\!)
) is invariant under a non-trivial subgroup of
\langle\sigma\rangle
,
say
\langle\sigma^{b}\rangle
with
1\leq b\leq p-1
and
b|p
.
Then

\sum_{1\leq k<N}a_{k}z^{n_{k}}=\sum_{1\leq k<N}a_{k}\xi^{bn_{k}}z^{n_{k}}

which implies
\xi^{bn_{k}}=1
for all
k
, since all
a_{k}\neq 0
.
But that means that
p/b
divides all
n_{k}
, contradicting
\operatorname{gcd}(p,n_{1},n_{2},\ldots)=1
.
Cf.
[
5
, p.202]
.

Multiplying (
22
) by a suitable power of
z^{p}
, we may suppose that the coefficients of
A_{j}
and
H

belong to
z^{p}\mathbb{K}\{z^{p}\}
.
Substituting
z
for
x
, we find

\sum_{j=0}^{p-1}A_{j}(z^{p})\varphi(z)^{j}=zH(z^{p})

with
A_{j}(z^{p})
and
H(z^{p})
belonging to
z^{p}\mathbb{K}\{z^{p}\}
. Since
H(z^{p})\neq 0
,
there exist
q\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
,
c\in\mathbb{K}\setminus\{0\}
, and
H_{1}(z^{p})\in\mathbb{K}\{z^{p}\}
such that

H(z^{p})=z^{pq}(c+z^{p}H_{1}(z^{p}))

from which it is easy to conclude (
21
).
∎

Remark 4.2
.

Let
\Phi
be as in Theorem
4.1
with
\operatorname{gcd}(n_{1},n_{2},\ldots)=1

and assume that
\mathfrak{M}
is an [admissible] weight matrix.
Then the map
\mathcal{E}^{[\mathfrak{M}]}\ni\Phi\circ f\mapsto f\in\mathcal{E}^{[\mathfrak{M}]}
in (1) takes bounded sets to bounded sets.
This follows from Remark
2.5
and (
21
), since the superposition operator
g\mapsto h\circ g
is bounded,
even continuous, among

\mathcal{E}^{[\mathfrak{M}]}
(in finite dimensions), provided that
\mathcal{E}^{[\mathfrak{M}]}
is stable under composition;
see
[
12
, Theorem 4.13]
.

Remark 4.3
.

Let
g
be a continuous germ at
0\in\mathbb{R}
such that
g(0)=1
.
Then
f:=1/g
and
h:=f-1
are continuous germs and
h(0)=0
.
Consider the analytic germ
\varphi(t)=\frac{1}{1+t}-1=\sum_{k\geq 1}(-1)^{k}t^{k}
.
Then
\varphi\circ h=g-1
.
Any analytic germ
\Phi:(\mathbb{R},0)\to(\mathbb{R}^{n},0)
that has
\varphi
as a component evidently satisfies the condition
4.1
(3).
Now if
g
is of class
\mathcal{E}^{[\mathfrak{M}]}
and (
20
) holds, then

h
is of class
\mathcal{E}^{[\mathfrak{M}]}
and consequently also
f=1/g
.
That means that (
20
) for analytic
\Phi
implies that
\mathcal{E}^{[\mathfrak{M}]}
is
inverse-closed
.

In view of
[
3
, Theorem 4.8]
we infer that concavity of the weight function
\omega
(up to equivalence)
is a necessary condition
for the conclusions of Corollary
1.2
and Theorem
4.1
(2).

Next we want to discuss implications of the conclusions of Theorem
1.1
and Theorem
4.1
(1)
and compare them with the assumption of admissibility for the weight matrix
\mathfrak{M}
;
our goal is to convince the reader that admissibility is not only sufficient but also “close to” necessary.
Suppose that

m_{k}^{1/k}\to\infty\quad\text{ for all }M\in\mathfrak{M};

(23)

this guarantees that
\mathcal{E}^{[\mathfrak{M}]}
contains the real analytic class.
Consider also the conditions

\displaystyle\begin{split}\;\forall M\in\mathfrak{M}\;\exists N\in\mathfrak{N}&:\sup_{k}\Big(\frac{M_{k+1}}{N_{k}}\Big)^{1/k}<\infty\qquad\text{ in the Roumieu case,}\\
\;\forall M\in\mathfrak{M}\;\exists N\in\mathfrak{N}&:\sup_{k}\Big(\frac{N_{k+1}}{M_{k}}\Big)^{1/k}<\infty\qquad\text{ in the Beurling case,}\end{split}

(24)

which characterize stability under derivation of
\mathcal{E}^{[\mathfrak{M}]}
.

Fact i:

Assuming (
24
), the conclusions of Theorem
1.1
and Theorem
4.1
(1) imply that
\mathcal{E}^{[\mathfrak{M}]}
is stable under composition.

Indeed, we saw that (
20
) for analytic
\Phi
entails that
\mathcal{E}^{[\mathfrak{M}]}
is inverse-closed which in turn implies (see
[
12
, Theorem 4.9 and 4.11]

and
[
13
]
)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\;\forall M\in\mathfrak{M}\;\exists N\in\mathfrak{N}&:\sup_{j\leq k}\frac{m_{j}^{1/j}}{n_{k}^{1/k}}<\infty\qquad\text{ in the Roumieu case,}\\
\;\forall M\in\mathfrak{M}\;\exists N\in\mathfrak{N}&:\sup_{j\leq k}\frac{n_{j}^{1/j}}{m_{k}^{1/k}}<\infty\qquad\text{ in the Beurling case.}\end{split}

(25)

Now (
25
), in conjunction with (
24
),
yields that
\mathcal{E}^{[\mathfrak{M}]}
is stable under composition (
loc.​​ cit.
).
Conversely, since
\mathcal{E}^{[\mathfrak{M}]}
contains the real analytic class (thanks to (
23
)),
stability under composition entails inverse-closedness and hence (
25
).
Note that (
24
) follows from the moderate growth part in the definition
of an [admissible] weight matrix.

Fact ii:

Assuming (
23
) and (
24
),
the other part in the definition of admissibility, i.e. the control of
\overline{\Gamma}_{n}
by
\underline{\Gamma}_{m}
(resp.
\overline{\Gamma}_{m}
by
\underline{\Gamma}_{n}
),
also implies stability under composition.

This follows from almost analytic extension (see
[
3
, Remark 2.7]
).
But the property “control of
\overline{\Gamma}_{n}
by
\underline{\Gamma}_{m}
” seems not to be
substantially
stronger than stability under composition.
This is evidenced by Fact iii and Fact iv.

Fact iii:

For any weight function
\omega
,
the class
\mathcal{E}^{[\omega]}
being stable under composition implies the existence of a weight matrix

\mathfrak{M}
with
\mathcal{E}^{[\omega]}=\mathcal{E}^{[\mathfrak{M}]}
such that

m
is log-convex and thus

\overline{\Gamma}_{m}=\underline{\Gamma}_{m}
for all
M\in\mathfrak{M}
.

See
[
3
, Theorem 4.8]
and
[
15
, Proposition 3]
);
in this case (
23
) and moderate growth (hence (
24
)) are automatic.

Fact iv:

If a general weight matrix
\mathfrak{M}
fulfills (
23
), (
25
), and

\displaystyle\begin{split}\;\forall M\in\mathfrak{M}\;\exists N\in\mathfrak{N}&:\sup_{k}\frac{m_{k}}{m_{k-1}}n_{k}^{-1/k}<\infty\qquad\text{ in the Roumieu case,}\\
\;\forall M\in\mathfrak{M}\;\exists N\in\mathfrak{N}&:\sup_{k}\frac{n_{k}}{n_{k-1}}m_{k}^{-1/k}<\infty\qquad\text{ in the Beurling case,}\end{split}

(26)

then
\mathfrak{M}
admits the desired control of
\overline{\Gamma}_{n}
by
\underline{\Gamma}_{m}
.

Indeed, in the Roumieu case, for given
M\in\mathfrak{M}
there exist
L,N\in\mathfrak{M}
and constants
C_{i}>0
such that for all
j\leq k
,

\frac{m_{j}}{m_{j-1}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:qr}}}{{\leq}}C_{1}\ell_{j}^{1/j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:almostincreasing}}}{{\leq}}C_{2}n_{k}^{1/k}\leq C_{3}\frac{n_{k}}{n_{k-1}};

the last inequality it true for every weight sequence.
It follows that
\overline{\Gamma}_{n}(C_{3}t)\leq\underline{\Gamma}_{m}(t)
for all
t>0
(see
[
14
, Lemma 3.3]
).
The Beurling case
is analogous.

Remark 4.4
.

In principle, one can apply the above method also to the more general case that
\Phi
is the germ of a
\mathcal{E}^{[\mathfrak{M}]}
-mapping,
but the ramification
z^{p}
causes a loss of regularity and, since the series are formal,
an ultradifferentiable Borel lemma must be used
which introduces flat terms whose handling again entails a loss of regularity.
