Abstract
. In this article we develop new methods for exhibiting convex semialgebraic sets that are not spectrahedral shadows. We characterize when the set of nonnegative polynomials with a given support is a spectrahedral shadow in terms of sums of squares. As an application of this result we prove that the cone of copositive matrices of size n ≥ 5 n\geq 5 is not a spectrahedral shadow, answering a question of Scheiderer. Our arguments are based on the model theoretic observation that any formula defining a spectrahedral shadow must be preserved by every unital ℝ {\mathbb{R}} -linear completely positive map R → R R\to R on a real closed field extension R R of ℝ {\mathbb{R}} .

1.
Introduction

A set
S\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is called a

spectrahedron

if

S=\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\mid A_{0}+A_{1}x_{1}+\dots+A_{n}x_{n}\succeq 0\}

for some
k\times k
real symmetric matrices
A_{0},A_{1},\dots,A_{n}
. Here
A\succeq 0
says that the real symmetric matrix
A
is positive semidefinite, and the expression

A_{0}+A_{1}x_{1}+\cdots+A_{n}x_{n}\succeq 0
is called a
linear matrix inequality (LMI) of size
k
.
Note that spectrahedra are convex and
semialgebraic
, i.e.,
definable over
{\mathbb{R}}
by a first-order formula in the language of real-closed fields (i.e., the language of ordered rings).

There has been ample interest in representing convex semialgebraic sets
K\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
as a
spectrahedral shadow
. This amounts to finding real symmetric matrices
A_{0},\ldots,A_{n},B_{1},\ldots,B_{m}
of the same size such that

K=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\mid\exists y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\colon A_{0}+x_{1}A_{1}+\cdots+x_{n}A_{n}+y_{1}B_{1}+\cdots{+}y_{m}B_{m}\succeq 0\}.

The interest stems primarily from the fact that there are efficient methods for approximately optimizing a linear function over such a set.
It was shown by Helton and Nie
[
11
]
that every convex semialgebraic set satisfying certain smoothness and curvature conditions is a spectrahedral shadow. Moreover, Scheiderer proved that every convex semialgebraic set in the plane is a spectrahedral shadow
[
30
]
. On the other hand, not every convex semialgebraic set is a spectrahedral shadow. The first counterexamples were constructed by Scheiderer
[
31
]
. Further examples were found in
[
8
,
4
]
, both using similar techniques as Scheiderer in his seminal work.

Recent efforts also focus on finding a representation as spectrahedral shadow with several LMIs of a bounded size.
For instance Averkov
[
2
]
showed that the cone of
k\times k
real symmetric positive semidefinite matrices has no semidefinite extended representation
with LMIs of size less than
k
(for a formal definition, see
Section

2
).
A generalization of this result in terms of convex geometry was given by Saunderson
[
29
]
. On the positive side, Scheiderer
[
32
]
proved that for every closed convex semialgebraic set
K\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
LMIs of size two are sufficient.

Our approach to these questions is based on an easy consequence of the Tarski principle for real closed fields. The question whether a given semialgebraic set
K\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is a spectrahedral shadow is equivalent to the question whether
K
has a primitive positive definition over the structure
\mathfrak{A}
with domain
\mathbb{R}
and with a relation
S\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
for every spectrahedron
S
.
By the Tarski principle for real closed fields, we may
equivalently study this question over real-closed field extensions
R
of
\mathbb{R}
instead of
\mathbb{R}
.
More precisely, if
\phi
is a formula in the language of real closed fields that defines
K
,
let
K^{*}
be the set defined by
\phi
over
R
. Likewise,
let
{\mathfrak{A}}^{*}
denote
the structure with domain
R
which contains for every formula
\psi
that defines a spectrahedron over
{\mathbb{R}}

the relation defined by
\psi
over
R
.
Then
K
has a primitive positive definition in
{\mathfrak{A}}
if and only if
K^{*}
has a primitive positive definition in
\mathfrak{A}^{*}
.
A map
h\colon R\to R

preserves
a relation
K\subseteq R^{n}

if
(h(a_{1}),\dots,h(a_{n}))\in K
whenever
(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\in K
.
We use the easy fact that
K^{*}
does not have an existential positive definition in
{\mathfrak{A}}^{*}
(and hence in particular no primitive positive definition) if
there exists a map
h\colon R\to R
that preserves all the relations of
{\mathfrak{A}}^{*}
but not
K^{*}
. In
Appendix

B
we discuss this approach in more detail and prove a converse of the last statement.

We show that a map
L\colon R\to R
preserves
{\mathfrak{A}}^{*}
if and only if it is unital,
{\mathbb{R}}
-linear, and completely positive in the sense of Operator theory. Thus, in order to prove that
K
is not a spectrahedral shadow, it suffices to exhibit a unital,
{\mathbb{R}}
-linear, and completely positive map
L\colon R\to R
on a real closed field extension
{\mathbb{R}}\subseteq R
that does not preserve the set defined by
\phi
.
In this way we prove that the set of copositive matrices of size
n
is not a spectrahedral shadow whenever
n\geq 5
, a family of convex semialgebraic sets that resisted Scheiderer’s efforts, see
[
31
, Question 5.2]
.
A real symmetric matrix
A
is
copositive
if
x^{\top}Ax\geq 0
for every non-negative real vector
x
.
Note that for
n\leq 4
it follows from the main result in
[
7
]
that this set is a spectrahedral shadow.

Note that convexity is not only preserved by conjunction (i.e., intersection) and existential quantification (i.e., projections), but also by universal quantification.
In the light of the results mentioned above one may ask whether every convex semi-algebraic set has a definition using a formula from
positive first-order logic
over the structure formed by all spectrahedra. Positive first-order logic is the extension of existential positive logic by universal quantification (so only negation from first-order logic is prohibited).
Our proof shows that the considered convex semialgebraic sets are not definable from spectrahedra even in this more expressive logic. The reason is that the map
L\colon R\to R
that we construct is
surjective
.
Hence, we may combine the Tarski principle with the easy direction of Lyndon’s preservation theorem (see, e.g., Corollary 10.3.5 in
[
13
]
)
to conclude that for example the set of copositive matrices does not have a definition from spectrahedra in positive first-order logic.

Apart from the Tarski principle the only ingredient to our proof is a separation theorem (
3.7
) that follows from the results in
[
21
]
. We will give a self-contained proof in
Appendix

A
.
In particular, we do not make use of the theory developed in
[
31
]
. Our result on copositive matrices is a consequence of the following more general result:

Theorem

(see
3.15
below)
.

Let
S\subset{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{n}
a finite set and let
\operatorname{P}_{+}(S)
the set of all nonnegative polynomials
with coefficients from
{\mathbb{R}}

whose support is contained in
S
. Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

\operatorname{P}_{+}(S)
is a spectrahedral shadow.

(2)

There exists an integer
d>0
such that
p(x_{1}^{d},\ldots,x_{n}^{d})
is a sum of squares for all
p\in\operatorname{P}_{+}(S)
.

Finally, in
Section

4
we reprove Averkov’s above mentioned result in a similar fashion. Here we use results about positive definite maps
[
15
]
.

We now give a brief outline of our proof strategy. We first observe in
Section

2
that a map
L\colon R\to R
for some real closed field extension
R
of
{\mathbb{R}}
preserves
{\mathfrak{A}}^{*}
if and only if
L
is unital,
{\mathbb{R}}
-linear, and completely positive in the sense that applying
L
entry-wise to a positive semidefinite symmetric matrix over
R
gives a positive semidefinite matrix. When
R=R^{\prime}[[\epsilon^{\Lambda}]]
is the field of Hahn series with value group
\Lambda
and coefficients in another real closed field
R^{\prime}
, we consider maps
L_{f}\colon R\to R
of the form
L_{f}\left(\sum_{a\in\Lambda}c_{a}\epsilon^{a}\right)=\sum_{a\in\Lambda}f(a)c_{a}\epsilon^{a}
for functions
f\colon\Lambda\to R^{\prime}
. On the other hand, extending
f
linearly, we obtain a linear functional
T_{f}\colon R^{\prime}[\Lambda]\to R^{\prime}
on the group ring of
\Lambda
. The crucial insight of
Section

2
is that
L_{f}
is completely positive whenever
T_{f}
is positive on every nonzero square in
R^{\prime}[\Lambda]
. In
Section

3
we focus on the case
\Lambda={\mathbb{Q}}^{n}
. Elements of the group ring
R^{\prime}[{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}]
can be regarded as functions on
(R^{\prime}_{>0})^{n}
. If such a function takes only nonnegative values but is not a sum of squares in
R^{\prime}[{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}]
, then it can be separated by a linear functional
T_{f}
from the cone of sums of squares after a base change to a larger real closed field by
3.7
. The corresponding map
L_{f}
is then completely positive but does not preserve a certain convex cone of nonnegative polynomials. Applying this construction to the so-called Horn polynomial proves that the cone of copositive matrices of size
n\geq 5
is not a spectrahedral shadow.

2.
k
-positive maps

In this section let
R
always be a real closed field extension of
{\mathbb{R}}
.
Let
\Gamma_{k}
be the set of all first-order formulas in the language of real closed fields with parameters from
{\mathbb{R}}
that express
a linear matrix inequality of size
k
with coefficients from
{\mathbb{R}}
,
and let
\Gamma=\cup_{k=1}^{\infty}\Gamma_{k}
. Let
{\mathfrak{A}}_{k}
be the relational structure with domain
{\mathbb{R}}
whose relations consist of the sets defined by the formulas in
\Gamma_{k}
. We follow standard terminology in model theory, see, e.g.,
[
13
,
33
]
. For the convenience of the reader, we have collected the relevant concepts in Appendix
B.1
. The signature of
\mathfrak{A}_{k}
should not be confused with the signature of real closed fields. If a set
K
has a primitive positive definition over
{\mathfrak{A}}_{k}
then we say that
K
has a
semidefinite extended formulation with LMIs of size
k
, following the terminology of Averkov
[
2
]
.

We say that a map
h\colon R\to R

preserves
a formula in the language of real closed fields if it preserves the relation defined by the formula over
R
.
The map
h
is called
unital
if it preserves the formula
x=1
, and for
R^{\prime}\subseteq R
it is called
R^{\prime}
-linear
if it preserves the formula
z=\lambda\cdot x+\mu\cdot y
for all
\lambda,\mu\in R^{\prime}
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}
. A map
L\colon R\to R
is called
k
-positive
if for every
k\times k
symmetric positive semidefinite matrix
A=(a_{ij})_{i,j}
with
a_{ij}\in R
the matrix
L(A):=(L(a_{ij}))_{i,j}
is positive semidefinite as well. For technical reasons, we declare every map
L\colon R\to R
to be
0
-positive. If
L
is
k
-positive for every
k
, then, following the nomenclature of operator theory (see, e.g.,
[
6
, §1.5]
), we say that
L
is
completely positive
.

Remark 2.2
.

Note that if
L\colon R\to R
is
k
-positive, then it is also
k^{\prime}
-positive for all
k^{\prime}\in\{0,\ldots,k\}
since principal submatrices of positive semidefinite matrices are again positive semidefinite.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}
.
A map
L\colon R\to R
preserves all formulas in
\Gamma_{k}
(respectively
\Gamma
)
if and only if
L
is unital,
{\mathbb{R}}
-linear, and
k
-positive (respectively completely positive).

Proof.

Assume that
L
preserves all formulas in
\Gamma_{k}
. The formula
x=1
is equivalent to

{\begin{pmatrix}x-1\end{pmatrix}\succeq 0\wedge\begin{pmatrix}1-x\end{pmatrix}\succeq 0.}

Thus
L(1)=1
. A similar argument shows that
z=\lambda\cdot x+\mu\cdot y
is in
\Gamma_{k}
for all
\lambda,\mu\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
To prove that
L
is
k
-positive,
note that a symmetric matrix
A=(a_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq k}
is positive semidefinite if and only if

(a_{11},a_{12},\dots,a_{1k},a_{22},a_{23},\dots,a_{{kk}})
satisfies
the linear matrix inequality

\sum_{1\leq i\leq j\leq k}x_{ij}E_{ij}\succeq 0
where
E_{ij}
has entry
1
at position
i,j
and at position
j,i
and entry
0
at all other positions. Since
L
preserves this formula,
we have that
L(A)\succeq 0
whenever
A\succeq 0
.

Conversely, assume that
L
is unital,
{\mathbb{R}}
-linear, and
k
-positive. Consider
k\times k
real symmetric matrices
A_{0},A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}
.
Let

K=\{x\in R^{n}\mid A_{0}+x_{1}A_{1}+\cdots+x_{n}A_{n}\succeq 0\}

and let
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in K
. Then
L(x)=(L(x_{1}),\ldots,L(x_{n}))
is also in
K
. Indeed, by the properties of
L
we have

\displaystyle A_{0}+L(x_{1})A_{1}+\cdots+L(x_{n})A_{n}

\displaystyle=L(A_{0}+x_{1}A_{1}+\cdots+x_{n}A_{n})\succeq 0.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
R\subset R^{\prime}
a field extension and
L\colon R^{\prime}\to R^{\prime}
be an
R
-linear map. Let
A
be an
m\times n
matrix with entries in
R^{\prime}
. Let
M
and

be
a\times m
and

matrices with entries in

. Then we have

.

Proof.

This follows directly from

-linearity.
∎

Now let
\Lambda
a divisible ordered abelian group. We consider
H=R[[\epsilon^{\Lambda}]]
,
the field of Hahn series
over
R
with value group
\Lambda
and the valuation

given by
v\left(\sum_{e\in\Lambda}c_{e}\epsilon^{e}\right)=\min\{e\in\Lambda\mid c_{e}\neq 0\}
.
Since

is real closed

1
, page 218, (2)]

x\leq y\Leftrightarrow\exists z(z^{2}+x=y)
.
Further, let
{\mathfrak{o}}
the valuation ring of

is a local ring with residue field

, the maximal ideal consisting of all elements with positive valuation. We denote by
\pi\colon{\mathfrak{o}}\to R

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
L\colon H\to H
be
R
-linear and
(k-1)
-positive
and suppose that for all
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\in H_{>0}
with
v(a_{1})<\cdots<v(a_{k})
the matrix
L(a\cdot a^{\top})
, where
a=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})^{\top}
, is positive semidefinite. Then
L
is
k
-positive.

Proof.

Let
B
be a
k\times k
symmetric positive semidefinite matrix over
H
. Then

B=\sum_{i=1}^{k}b_{i}\cdot b_{i}^{\top}

for some column vectors
b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}\in H^{k}
. Since
L
is
R
-linear, we have

L(B)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}L(b_{i}\cdot b_{i}^{\top}).

Since the sum of positive semidefinite matrices is positive semidefinite, it therefore suffices to show that
L(a\cdot a^{\top})
is positive semidefinite for all
a=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})^{\top}
with
a_{1},\dots,a_{k}\in H
.
Since
L
is
(k-1)
-positive, we may assume that
a_{i}\neq 0
for all
i
. Let
M\in\operatorname{{\mathbf{GL}}}_{n}(R)
.
The matrix
L(a\cdot a^{\top})
is positive semidefinite if and only if

ML(a\cdot a^{\top})M^{\top}
is positive semidefinite, which
equals

L((Ma)\cdot(Ma)^{\top})

by
2.4
. Thus, after choosing
M
to be a suitably signed permutation matrix, we may assume that
a_{1}\geq a_{2}\geq\cdots\geq a_{k}>0
. This implies that
v(a_{1})\leq\cdots\leq v(a_{k})
. Now, after choosing
M
to be a product of suitable elementary matrices, we can make each inequality strict.
∎

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
A
be a symmetric
n\times n
matrix with entries in
{\mathfrak{o}}
. Then the following holds:

(1)

If
A=(a_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq n}
is positive semidefinite, then
\pi(A)=(\pi(a_{ij}))_{1\leq i,j\leq n}
is positive semidefinite.

(2)

If
\pi(A)
is positive definite, then
A
is positive definite.

Proof.

We use the well-known fact that a symmetric matrix is positive semidefinite (positive definite) if and only if all its principal minors are nonnegative (positive, respectively); see, e.g.,
[
14
, Theorem 7.2.5]
).
Let
x\in{\mathfrak{o}}
with
x\geq 0
. Since every nonnegative element in
{\mathfrak{o}}
has a square root in
{\mathfrak{o}}
, we have that

\pi(x)\in R
has a square root as well and hence
\pi(x)\geq 0
. If
B
is a principal submatrix of
A
, then
\det(\pi(B))=\pi(\det(B))
.
Therefore, if every principal minor of
A
is nonnegative, the same is true for every principal minor of
\pi(A)
. This shows (1). For proving (2) assume that
A
is not positive definite. Then there is a principal minor of
A
which is nonpositive. The same minor of
\pi(A)
will also be nonpositive showing that
\pi(A)
is not positive definite.
∎

. We have to show that the matrix

is positive semidefinite, where
a=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})^{\top}
. Let
b=(\epsilon^{-v(a_{1})},\ldots,\epsilon^{-v(a_{k})})^{\top}
and
B=b\cdot b^{\top}
. Since
B
is positive semidefinite of rank one with nonzero entries, the matrix
L_{f}(a\cdot a^{\top})
is positive semidefinite if and only if its Hadamard product
C
with
B
is positive semidefinite. As above one calculates that
\pi(C)
is the matrix
(f(v(a_{i})+v(a_{j})))_{1\leq i,j\leq k}
which is positive definite since
f
is
k
-positive definite and
v(a_{1}),\dots,v(a_{k})
are pairwise different. Thus,
C
is positive definite by
2.6
(2). Therefore,
L_{f}(a\cdot a^{\top})
is positive semidefinite and
(1)
follows from
2.9
.
∎

Remark 2.14
.

Another point of view on positive semidefinite functions can be taken by looking at the group ring
R[\Lambda]
. The group ring
R[\Lambda]
can be identified with the subring of
H=R[[\epsilon^{\Lambda}]]
consisting of series with finite support. Now any function
f\colon\Lambda\to R
can be extended linearly to an
R
-linear functional
T_{f}\colon R[\Lambda]\to R
. An element of
R[\Lambda]
is of the form
a=\sum_{i=1}^{k}c_{i}\epsilon^{a_{i}}
for
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
(c_{1},\dots,c_{k})\in R^{k}
, and
(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})\in\Lambda^{k}
. Then we have

\displaystyle T_{f}(a^{2})

\displaystyle=T_{f}\left(\sum_{i,j=1}^{k}c_{i}c_{j}\epsilon^{a_{i}+a_{j}}\right)=\sum_{i,j}^{k}c_{i}c_{j}f(a_{i}+a_{j})

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}c_{1}&\ldots&c_{k}\end{pmatrix}\cdot(f(a_{i}+a_{j}))_{1\leq i,j\leq k}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}c_{1}\\
\vdots\\
c_{k}\end{pmatrix}.

From this we see
that
f
is positive semidefinite (positive definite) if and only if
T_{f}(a^{2})\geq 0
(
T_{f}(a^{2})>0
, respectively) for all
a\in R[\Lambda]\setminus\{0\}
.

For the rest of this section we focus on the case
\Lambda={\mathbb{R}}
.
Let
{\mathcal{H}}({\mathbb{C}})
be the ring of holomorphic functions on
{\mathbb{C}}
.
We have a homomorphism of
{\mathbb{R}}
-algebras

\Psi\colon{\mathbb{R}}[\Lambda]\to{\mathcal{H}}({\mathbb{C}})
defined as follows.
For
a\in{\mathbb{R}}
, set
\Psi(\epsilon^{a})

to be the holomorphic function
z\mapsto\exp(az)
and extend linearly to all of
{\mathbb{R}}[\Lambda]
.

Lemma 2.15
.

Let
g\in{\mathbb{R}}[\Lambda]
. If
\Psi(g)
vanishes on
[a,b]
for
a<b
, then
g=0
as element of
{\mathbb{R}}[\Lambda]
. In particular, the homomorphism
\Psi
is injective.

Proof.

Let
g=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\epsilon^{a_{i}}
for some
c_{i},a_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
i=1,\ldots,n
, with
a_{1}<\cdots<a_{n}
.
The identity theorem from complex analysis implies that
\Psi(g)
vanishes on all of
{\mathbb{C}}
. Thus we have
\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\exp(a_{i}z)=0
for all
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
. Linear independence of characters now implies that
c_{i}=0
for
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
because the functions
{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}},\,t\mapsto\exp(a_{i}t)
for
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
are pairwise different characters.
∎

This enables us to construct positive definite functions
{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
.

Example 2.16
.

Let
\Lambda={\mathbb{R}}
and
\mu
a measure on
{\mathbb{R}}
with
\int\exp(ax)d\mu(x)<\infty
for all
a\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Then the functional

T\colon{\mathbb{R}}[\Lambda]\to{\mathbb{R}},\,g\mapsto\int\Psi(g)(x)d\mu(x)

clearly satisfies
T(g^{2})\geq 0
for all
g\in{\mathbb{R}}[\Lambda]
. If we choose for instance
\mu
to be the pushforward to
{\mathbb{R}}
of the Lebesgue measure on some compact interval
[a,b]
with
a<b
, then we even have
T(g^{2})>0
for every
g\in{\mathbb{R}}[\Lambda]\setminus\{0\}
. Indeed, for any
g\in{\mathbb{R}}[\Lambda]
with
T(g^{2})=0
the function
\Psi(g)
must vanish on
[a,b]
, which implies
g=0
by
2.15
.
Let
f\colon{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
defined by
f(a)=T(\epsilon^{a})
for
a\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Since
T
is
{\mathbb{R}}
-linear, we have
T=T_{f}
and thus
f
is positive definite by
2.14
.

Corollary 2.17
.

Let
\Lambda
be a divisible ordered abelian group whose dimension as a
{\mathbb{Q}}
-vector space is at most
2^{\aleph_{0}}
. Further, let
R
be a real closed field extension of
{\mathbb{R}}
. Then there exists an
R
-linear map
T\colon R[\Lambda]\to R
which satisfies
T(g^{2})>0
for every
g\in R[\Lambda]\setminus\{0\}
.

Proof.

By
2.16
there exists a positive definite map
f\colon{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
.
By our assumptions on
\Lambda
there is an injective group homomorphism
\iota_{1}\colon\Lambda\to{\mathbb{R}}
. Let
\iota_{2}\colon{\mathbb{R}}\to R
be the inclusion map. Then the map
\bar{f}=\iota_{2}\circ f\circ\iota_{1}\colon\Lambda\to R
is also positive definite.
By
2.14
the map
T_{\bar{f}}\colon R[\Lambda]\to R
has the desired properties.
∎

3.
The group ring
R[{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}]

From now on let
R
be a real closed field (not necessarily an extension of
{\mathbb{R}}
). We fix a total order on
{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}
that is translation-invariant, for instance the lexicographical ordering. As in
2.14
we consider the group ring
R[{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}]
as the subring of
R[[\epsilon^{{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}}]]
of series with finite support. For
a=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}
we write

\epsilon^{a}=\epsilon_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots\epsilon_{n}^{a_{n}}.

By plugging in positive elements from
R
for
\epsilon_{i}
we can regard elements from
R[{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}]
as functions on the positive orthant
R_{>0}^{n}
.
Clearly, every element of
R[{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}]
that can be written as a sum of squares of elements of
R[{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}]
is nonnegative on
R_{>0}^{n}
. We will see in
3.4
that the converse to this is not true. We will consider the polynomial ring
R[\underline{x}]=R[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
as subring of
R[{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}]
by replacing
x_{i}
by
\epsilon_{i}
.

Remark 3.1
.

Evaluating elements
f\in R[{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}]
at negative numbers is in general not well defined since the exponents can have even denominator. Similarly, a polynomial can be a sum of squares of elements from the group ring
R[{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}]
without being nonnegative on all of
R^{n}
. For example,
\epsilon\in R[\epsilon]
takes negative values on
R
, but equals
(\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}})^{2}
.

Remark 3.2
.

A polynomial
p\in R[\underline{x}]
is a sum of squares of elements in
R[{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}]
if and only if
p(x_{1}^{m},\dots,x_{n}^{m})
is a sum of squares of polynomials from
R[\underline{x}]
for some
m\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}
. Indeed, negative exponents cannot appear in the sum of squares decomposition since lowest degree terms cannot cancel out.

Example 3.3
.

Consider the Motzkin polynomial

M=x_{3}^{6}-3x_{1}^{2}x_{2}^{2}x_{3}^{2}+x_{1}^{2}x_{2}^{4}+x_{1}^{4}x_{2}^{2}\in R[x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}].

It follows from the inequality of arithmetic and geometric means that
M
is nonnegative on
R^{3}

(since
\frac{1}{3}x_{1}^{4}x_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{3}x_{1}^{2}x_{2}^{4}+\frac{1}{3}x_{3}^{6}\geq x_{1}^{4/3}x_{2}^{2/3}x_{1}^{2/3}x_{2}^{4/3}x_{3}^{6/3}
)
but it is not a sum of squares of polynomials from
R[\underline{x}]

[
18
]
. However,
M(x_{1}^{2},x_{2}^{2},x_{3}^{2})
is a sum of squares of polynomials
[
28
, Example 5.3]
:

M(x_{1}^{2},x_{2}^{2},x_{3}^{2})=2\cdot(x_{1}^{3}x_{2}^{3}-x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}^{4})^{2}+(x_{1}^{4}x_{2}^{2}-x_{1}^{2}x_{2}^{4})^{2}+(x_{3}^{6}-x_{1}^{2}x_{2}^{2}x_{3}^{2})^{2}.

This shows that
M
is a sum of squares of elements of
R[{\mathbb{Q}}^{3}]
.

Example 3.4
.

Consider the
Horn polynomial

h=(x_{1}+x_{2}+x_{3}+x_{4}+x_{5})^{2}-4(x_{1}x_{2}+x_{2}x_{3}+x_{3}x_{4}+x_{4}x_{5}+x_{5}x_{1}).

It was proven in
[
10
, p. 335]
that
h
is nonnegative on the positive orthant. However, for any integer
k>0
the polynomial
h(x_{1}^{k},\ldots,x_{5}^{k})
is not a sum of squares of elements of
R[{\underline{x}}]
. This was shown in
[
26
, §4]
by adapting the proof from
[
10
]
for the case
k=2
.
This shows that
h
is not a sum of squares of elements of
R[{\mathbb{Q}}^{5}]
.

For the sake of completeness, we repeat the argument here. Assume
h
is a sum of squares in
R[{\mathbb{Q}}^{5}]
. Then for some integer
d>0
the polynomial

H_{d}=h(x_{1}^{{d}},\ldots,x_{5}^{{d}})=g_{1}^{2}+\dots+g_{m}^{2}

is a sum of squares of homogeneous polynomials
g_{j}
of degree
d
(since highest and lowest degree terms cannot cancel out). Restricting
H_{d}
to
x_{i}=x_{i+1}=0
we obtain

(x_{i+2}^{d}-x_{i+3}^{d}+x_{i+4}^{d})^{2}

for
i\in\{1,\dots,5\}
where the indices are taken modulo
5
. Thus, for
j\in\{1,\dots,m\}
the restriction of
g_{j}
to
x_{i}=x_{i+1}=0
must be a scalar multiple of
x_{i+2}^{d}-x_{i+3}^{d}+x_{i+4}^{d}
.
Indeed, if

(x_{i+2}^{d}-x_{i+3}^{d}+x_{i+4}^{d})^{2}=g_{1}^{\prime 2}+\dots+g_{m}^{\prime 2}

for
g_{j}^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}[x_{i+2},x_{i+3},x_{i+4}]
, then each
g_{j}^{\prime}
must vanish on the real zero set of
x_{i+2}^{d}-x_{i+3}^{d}+x_{i+4}^{d}
. The latter is Zariski dense in the complex zero set which implies by Hilbert’s Nullstellensatz that
g_{j}^{\prime}
is divisible by the irreducible polynomial
x_{i+2}^{d}-x_{i+3}^{d}+x_{i+4}^{d}
.
Letting
c_{i}
be the coefficient of
x_{i}^{d}
in
g_{j}
, this shows that
c_{i}=-c_{i+1}
. Since
5
is an odd number, this actually implies that
c_{1}=\cdots=c_{5}=0
. In particular, we have that
g_{j}(1,0,\ldots,0)=0
for
j\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
. However, since
H_{d}(1,0,\ldots,0)\neq 0
, this is a contradiction.

Example 3.5
.

The Horn polynomial considered in
3.4
is a special case of the following more general construction. Recall that a
stable set
of a graph
G=(V,E)
is a subset of elements of
V
that are pairwise not adjacent to each other. The
stability number

\alpha(G)
of
G
is the largest size of a stable set of
G
. By a theorem of Motzkin–Straus
[
19
]
the quadratic form associated to the symmetric matrix

Q(G)=\alpha(G)(I+A)-J

is nonnegative on the positive orthant
[
23
, Example 3.163]
, i.e., the matrix
Q(G)
is
copositive
. Here,
A
is the adjacency matrix of
G
and
J
is the all-ones matrix. The Horn polynomial corresponds to the matrix
Q(C_{5})
where
C_{5}
is the five-cycle. The argument from
3.4
can in fact be applied to any odd cycle of size at least five.

We also note that nonnegative elements in
{\mathbb{R}}[{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}]
cannot be distinguished from sums of squares by an
{\mathbb{R}}
-linear functional
{\mathbb{R}}[{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}]\to{\mathbb{R}}
.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
T\colon{\mathbb{R}}[{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}]\to{\mathbb{R}}
be an
{\mathbb{R}}
-linear functional with
T(a^{2})\geq 0
for all
a\in{\mathbb{R}}[{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}]
and let
p\in{\mathbb{R}}[{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}]
be nonnegative on
R_{>0}^{n}
. Then
T(p)\geq 0
.

Proof.

In the case
n=1
the statement of
[
3
, Proposition 5.10, page 211]
says that for every such
T
there is a measure
\mu
on
[0,\infty)
such that
T(g)=\int gd\mu
. This shows in particular that
T(p)\geq 0
.
The case
n>1
then follows from
[
3
, Theorem 5.4, page 204]
.
∎

However, if we replace the target field by some real closed extension field, then separation by a linear functional is always possible. The following theorem follows from the results of
[
21
]
. We will give self-contained proofs in
Appendix

A
.

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
p\in R[{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}]
not be a sum of squares of elements of
R[{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}]
. Then there exists a real closed field extension
R^{\prime}\supset R
and an
R^{\prime}
-linear functional
T\colon R^{\prime}[{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}]\to R^{\prime}
such that for every
a\in R^{\prime}[{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}]\setminus\{0\}
we have

T(a^{2})>0
and
T(p)<0
.

Proof.

By
A.3
there exists a real closed field extension
R^{\prime}\supset R
and an
R^{\prime}
-linear functional
T^{\prime}\colon R^{\prime}[{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}]\to R^{\prime}
such that for every
a\in R^{\prime}[{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}]
we have
T^{\prime}(a^{2})\geq 0
and
T^{\prime}(p)<0
. Further, by
2.17
there exists an
R^{\prime}
-linear map
T_{0}\colon R^{\prime}[{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}]\to R^{\prime}
that satisfies
T_{0}(a^{2})>0
for all
0\neq a\in R^{\prime}[{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}]
. Let
\lambda\in R^{\prime}
be such that
\lambda>0
and
\lambda|T_{0}(p)|<|T^{\prime}(p)|
. Then
T=\lambda T_{0}+T^{\prime}
has the desired properties.
∎

Now we are ready to formulate and prove our main result.

Definition 3.8
.

Let
S\subset{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{n}
be a finite set and
R
a real closed field. We denote by
\operatorname{P}(S,R)
the (finite dimensional)
R
-vector space of all polynomials in
n
variables and coefficients in
R
whose support is contained in
S
. We further denote by
\operatorname{P}_{+}(S,R)
the convex cone of all
p\in\operatorname{P}(S,R)
such that
p(x)\geq 0
for all
x\in R^{n}
. Finally, we denote by
\Sigma(S,R)
the convex cone of all
P\in\operatorname{P}(S,R)
that are a sum of squares of polynomials (with coefficients in
R
). If we do not specify the real closed field, then we work over the real numbers, i.e., we write
\operatorname{P}(S)=\operatorname{P}(S,{\mathbb{R}})
,
\operatorname{P}_{+}(S)=\operatorname{P}_{+}(S,{\mathbb{R}})
, and
\Sigma(S)=\Sigma(S,{\mathbb{R}})
.

Remark 3.9
.

Note that
\operatorname{P}_{+}(S,R)
is the set of all tuples
(c_{s})_{s\in S}\subset R^{S}
such that

\forall x_{1}\cdots\forall x_{n}\colon\,\sum_{s\in S}c_{s}\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{s_{i}}\geq 0

holds true over
R
. This is a formula in the language of real closed fields without parameters.
Such a formula also exists for
\Sigma(S,R)
. Indeed, let
T_{1}={\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{n}\cap\operatorname{conv}(S)
and
T_{2}={\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{n}\cap(\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{conv}(S))
. Every element of
\Sigma(S,R)
is a sum of squares of elements of
\operatorname{P}(T_{2},R)
, i.e., lies in the conical hull of the set

\{q^{2}\mid q\in\operatorname{P}(T_{2},R)\}\subset\operatorname{P}(T_{1},R).

Then by Carathéodory’s theorem for the conical hull every such element can be written as a sum of
|T_{1}|=\dim(\operatorname{P}(T_{1},R))
many squares.
Therefore, we have that
\Sigma(S,R)
is the set of all
p\in\operatorname{P}(S,R)
for which there exist
c_{i,t}\in R
, for
i\in\{1,\dots,|T_{1}|\}

and
t\in T_{2}
, such that

p=\sum_{i=1}^{|T_{1}|}\left(\sum_{t\in T_{2}}c_{i,t}\prod_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}^{t_{j}}\right)^{2}.

This shows that
\Sigma(S,R)
is defined by a formula in the language of real closed fields
without parameters.

Remark 3.10
.

For all finite sets
S
the set
\Sigma(S)
is a spectrahedral shadow
[
22
, §6.3.1, page 263]
.

Proposition 3.11
.

Let
S\subset{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{n}
be a finite set. Assume that there exists a real closed field
R
and
p\in\operatorname{P}_{+}(S,R)
such that
p
is not a sum of squares of elements from
R[{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}]
. Then
\operatorname{P}_{+}(S)
is not a spectrahedral shadow.

Proof.

Without loss of generality we may assume that
R
contains
{\mathbb{R}}
.
By
3.7
there exists a real closed field extension
R^{\prime}\supset R
and an
R^{\prime}
-linear functional
T\colon R^{\prime}[{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}]\to R^{\prime}
such that
T(a^{2})>0
for all
a\in R^{\prime}[{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}]\setminus\{0\}
and
T(p)<0
. Let
f\colon{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}\to R^{\prime}
defined by
f(w)=T(\epsilon_{1}^{w_{1}}\cdots\epsilon_{n}^{w_{n}})\in R^{\prime}
for
w\in{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}
. By the
R^{\prime}
-linearity of
T
we then have
T=T_{f}
as in
2.14
. As
T(a^{2})>0
we have that
f
is positive definite (see
2.14
). After replacing
T
by
\frac{1}{T(1)}\cdot T
, we can further assume that
f(0)=1
. Therefore, we have that
L_{f}\colon R^{\prime}[[\epsilon^{{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}}]]\to R^{\prime}[[\epsilon^{{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}}]]
is completely positive by
2.13
,
{\mathbb{R}}
-linear by construction, and unital because
f(0)=1
. The polynomial
q=p(\epsilon_{1}x_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{n}x_{n})
with coefficients in
R[[\epsilon^{{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}}]]

is nonnegative on
R^{\prime}[[\epsilon^{{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}}]]^{n}
. By construction we have

L_{f}(q)(\epsilon_{1}^{-1},\ldots,\epsilon_{n}^{-1})=T_{f}(p)<0.

Thus,
L_{f}
preserves all formulas in
\Gamma
by
2.3
but not the formula defining
\operatorname{P}_{+}(S,R^{\prime}[[\epsilon^{{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}}]])
.
This shows that
\operatorname{P}_{+}(S)
is not a spectrahedral shadow.
∎

A first-order formula is called
positive

if it is built from atomic formulas by existential and universal quantification, conjunction, and disjunction.
That is, only negation from first-order logic is forbidden.

Proposition 3.12
.

Let
S\subset{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}_{\geq 0}
and
p\in\operatorname{P}_{+}(S,R)
be as in
3.11
. Then

\operatorname{P}_{+}(S)
does not have a positive definition over the relational structure whose relations consist of all spectrahedra and the inequality relation
\neq
.

Proof.

Note that by
2.10

the map
L_{f}\colon R^{\prime}[[\epsilon^{{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}}]]\to R^{\prime}[[\epsilon^{{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}}]]
constructed in the proof of
3.11

is surjective. It follows from the easy direction of the Lyndon preservation theorem for positive first-order logic (see, e.g., Corollary 10.3.5 in
[
13
]
) and the Tarski principle for real-closed fields (similarly as outlined for existential positive logic in the introduction) that
the convex semi-algebraic set
\operatorname{P}_{+}(S)
does not have a definition by a positive formula over spectrahedra.
In fact, the map
L_{f}
is also injective and hence preserves
\neq
, and so
\operatorname{P}_{+}(S)
is inexpressible even if we additionally allow the use of
\neq
.
∎

Remark 3.13
.

If full first-order logic is permitted, then the graph of multiplication can already be defined from fairly restricted sets of relations over the reals
[
24
,
17
]
(and in combination with the graph of addition we then obtain all semialgebraic relations). In particular, it is easy to see that all semialgebraic relations can be defined with first-order formulas and parameters from the graph of addition and the spectrahedron defined by
y\geq x^{2}
.

Let
R
be a real closed field,
d>0
an integer,
S\subset{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{n}
a finite set and
s\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
.
We consider the following isomorphisms of
R
-vector spaces:

\displaystyle\psi_{d}\colon

\displaystyle\operatorname{P}(S,R)\to\operatorname{P}(d\cdot S,R),

\displaystyle p(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\mapsto p(x_{1}^{d},\ldots,x_{n}^{d}),

\displaystyle\varphi_{s}\colon

\displaystyle\operatorname{P}(S,R)\to\operatorname{P}(S,R),

\displaystyle p(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\mapsto p(s_{1}x_{1},\ldots,s_{n}x_{n}).

Further, we define

\Sigma_{d}(S,R)=\bigcap_{s\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}}\varphi_{s}(\psi_{d}^{-1}(\Sigma(d\cdot S,R))).

Again we write
\Sigma_{d}(S)=\Sigma_{d}(S,{\mathbb{R}})
.

Lemma 3.14
.

The following hold true for all integers
d>0
:

(1)

\Sigma_{d}(S,R)
can be defined by a formula in the language of real closed fields without parameters.

(2)

\Sigma_{d}(S,R)\subseteq\Sigma_{i\cdot d}(S,R)
for all integers
i>0
.

(3)

\Sigma_{d}(S,R)\subseteq\operatorname{P}_{+}(S,R)
.

(4)

\Sigma_{d}(S)
is a spectrahedral shadow.

Proof.

Part
(1)
follows from
3.9
and the fact that
\psi_{d}
and
\varphi_{s}
are first-order definable without parameters in the language of real closed fields. If a polynomial
p
is a sum of squares, then
p(x_{1}^{i},\ldots,x_{n}^{i})
is also a sum of squares. This shows
(2)
. Let
p\in\Sigma_{d}(S,R)
and
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in R
. To show
(3)
, we have to show that
p(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\geq 0
. For
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, let
s_{i}\in\{\pm 1\}
be such that
s_{i}x_{i}\geq 0
. Then we let
y_{i}=\sqrt[d]{s_{i}x_{i}}
. We have
q=\psi_{d}(\varphi_{s}^{{-1}}(p))\in\Sigma(d\cdot S,R)\subseteq\operatorname{P}_{+}(d\cdot S,R)
. Therefore,

0\leq q(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})=(\varphi_{s}^{{-1}}(p))(s_{1}x_{1},\ldots,s_{n}x_{n})=p(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}).

This shows
(3)
. We finally have that
\Sigma_{d}(S)
is a spectrahedral shadow as the intersection of finitely many spectrahedral shadows.
∎

Theorem 3.15
.

Let
S\subset{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{n}
be a finite set. The following are equivalent:

(1)

\operatorname{P}_{+}(S)
is a spectrahedral shadow.

(2)

For every real closed field
R
and every polynomial
p\in\operatorname{P}_{+}(S,R)
, there exists
d>0
such that
p(x_{1}^{d},\ldots,x_{n}^{d})
is a sum of squares.

(3)

There exists
d>0
such that for every real closed field
R
and every polynomial
p\in\operatorname{P}_{+}(S,R)
the polynomial
p(x_{1}^{d},\ldots,x_{n}^{d})
is a sum of squares.

(4)

There exists
d>0
such that for every polynomial
p\in\operatorname{P}_{+}(S)
the polynomial
p(x_{1}^{d},\ldots,x_{n}^{d})
is a sum of squares.

(5)

\operatorname{P}_{+}(S)
has a positive definition over the relational structure whose relations consist of all spectrahedra and the inequality relation
\neq
.

Proof.

The implication
(1)\Rightarrow(2)
follows from
3.11
and
3.2
.
Now assume
(2)
. We first claim that

\operatorname{P}_{+}(S,R)=\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}\Sigma_{k}(S,R).

The inclusion
\supseteq
is
3.14
(3). Let
p\in\operatorname{P}_{+}(S,R)
. By assumption, for all
s\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
there is
k_{s}>0
such that
\psi_{k_{s}}(\varphi_{s}(p))\in\Sigma(k_{s}\cdot S,R)
. Therefore, letting
k=\prod_{s\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}}k_{s}
we have
\psi_{k}(\varphi_{s}(p))\in\Sigma(k\cdot S,R)
and thus
p\in\Sigma_{k}(S,R)
.

Both
\operatorname{P}_{+}(S,R)
and each
\Sigma_{k}(S,R)
can be defined by a formula without parameters. Thus, letting
{\mathbb{R}}^{*}
be an
\aleph_{1}
-saturation of
{\mathbb{R}}
, we have
\operatorname{P}_{+}(S,{\mathbb{R}}^{*})=\Sigma_{d}(S,{\mathbb{R}}^{*})
for some
d>0
by
[
27
, Theorem 2.2.11]
. By the Tarski principle we thus have
\operatorname{P}_{+}(S,R)=\Sigma_{d}(S,R)
for every real closed field
R
. This shows
(3)
.

The direction
(3)\Rightarrow(4)
is trivial. Finally, assume
(4)
which implies
\operatorname{P}_{+}(S)=\Sigma_{d}(S)
. This shows
(1)
by
3.14
(4) and we have that
(1)
—
(4)
are equivalent.

It is clear that
(1)
implies
(5)
and
3.12
shows that
(5)
implies
(2)
.
∎

Corollary 3.18

The set of copositive matrices of size

For the rest of this section we summarize the current knowledge on whether
\operatorname{P}_{+}(S)
is a spectrahedral shadow for a given finite set

.

Example 3.19

(Hilbert)
.

Let
S\subset{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{n}
a finite set and
\Delta_{\leq d}
the set of all
s\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{n}
such that
|s|\leq d
.
Hilbert
[

]
proved that every element of
\operatorname{P}_{+}(S)
is a sum of squares if we are in one of the following cases:

(1)

;

(2)

n=1
;

(3)

n=2
and
S\subseteq\Delta_{\leq 4}
.

Thus, in all these cases
\operatorname{P}_{+}(S)
is a spectrahedral shadow.

Example 3.20

(Fawzi, Scheiderer)
.

Let
S\subset{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{n}
a finite set which is
downward closed
in the sense that if
s^{\prime}\leq s
(coordinate-wise) and

, then

. Then by
[
8
, Theorem 3]
the set
\operatorname{P}_{+}(S)
is a spectrahedral shadow if and only if
\operatorname{P}_{+}(S)=\Sigma(S)
. Thus, for every nonnegative polynomial which is not a sum of squares, letting

be the downward closure of its support, we obtain an instance where
\operatorname{P}_{+}(S)
is not a spectrahedral shadow.
Scheiderer
[
31
]
used a related criterion in several examples. A generalization of
[
8
, Theorem 3]
to finite dimensional spaces of polynomials not necessarily spanned by monomials is given in
[

, Theorem 2.14]
.

Example 3.21

(SONC)
.

A finite subset

is called

if its convex hull
\operatorname{conv}(A)\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is a

-dimensional simplex whose vertices

are in

and

for some

in the relative interior of
\operatorname{conv}(A)
. For any finite

the cone of

is defined as

\textrm{SONC}(S)=\sum_{A\subseteq S\textrm{ a circuit set}}\operatorname{P}_{+}(A).

Averkov
[
2
, §6]
showed that

has a semidefinite extended representation with LMIs of size at most two. Without the bound on the LMI sizes this also follows from a result by Reznick
[

,
26
]
which implies that for every

the polynomial

is a sum of squares for

. In particular, we have that
\operatorname{P}_{+}(S)
is a spectrahedral shadow whenever
\operatorname{P}_{+}(S)=\textrm{SONC}(S)
. By definition this is the case if
S
is a circuit set. Equality is also straightforward to see if every element of

is a vertex of
\operatorname{conv}(S)
. More cases of equality can be deduced from
[
20
, Theorem 11]
and
[
9
, Theorem 8.1]
.

The proof of
3.18
shows the existence of a set

of size
\frac{1}{2}n(n+1)
, for
n\geq 5
, such that
\operatorname{P}_{+}(S)
is not a spectrahedral shadow. For

this means
|S|=15
. Moreover, Scheiderer
[

, Remark 4.21]
constructs such an

with

.
We do not know the smallest size of a set

such that
\operatorname{P}_{+}(S)
is not a spectrahedral shadow. The following shows that any such set must satisfy

.

Corollary 3.22
.

Let
S\subset{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{n}
with
|S|\leq 5
. Then
\operatorname{P}_{+}(S)
is a spectrahedral shadow.

Proof.

Consider the convex hull
K=\operatorname{conv}(S)\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and let
N
be the number of vertices of
K
. First suppose that
N\leq 2
. Then there is a finite subset
T\subset{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
and
v\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{n}
,
w\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
such that
S=\{v+k\cdot w\mid k\in T\}
and such that the entries of
w
do not have a nontrivial common divisor. If
\operatorname{P}_{+}(S)\neq\{0\}
we can further assume that
v\in 2{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{n}
. Then the vector space isomorphism

\operatorname{P}(T)\to\operatorname{P}(S),\,p\mapsto\underline{x}^{v}\cdot p(\underline{x}^{w})

identifies
\operatorname{P}_{+}(T)
with
\operatorname{P}_{+}(S)
. Since every univariate nonnegative polynomial is a sum of squares, the set
\operatorname{P}_{+}(T)
, and hence
\operatorname{P}_{+}(S)
, is a spectrahedral shadow.

In the case
N\geq 3
we claim that
\operatorname{P}_{+}(S)=\textrm{SONC}(S)
. This implies the claim since
\textrm{SONC}(S)
is a spectrahedral shadow by
[
2
, §6]
, see also
3.21
. First assume that
K
is a simplex. Since
|S|\leq 5
, there are at most two elements of
S
that are not vertices of
K
. Hence
\operatorname{P}_{+}(S)=\textrm{SONC}(S)
follows from
[
20
, Theorem 11(1)]
(applied to the trivial partition of
S
). Now assume that
K
is not a simplex. We first note that
N\geq 3
implies that
\dim(K)\geq 2
. Therefore, since
K
is not a simplex, we even have
N\geq 4
. Thus there is at most one element of
S
that is not a vertex of

and we can apply
[
20
, Theorem 11(2)]
(again to the trivial partition).
∎

4.
Separating
\Gamma_{k}
from
\Gamma_{k+1}

The following criteria for a function
f\colon{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
to be
k
-positive definite or semidefinite are due to Karlin
[
15
]
.

Lemma 4.1
.

For
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}
let
f\in C^{2k-2}({\mathbb{R}})
and for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
consider the Hankel matrix
H_{f}(x)=(f^{(i+j-2)}(x))_{1\leq i,j\leq k}
of order
k
.

(1)

If
f
is
k
-positive semidefinite, then
H_{f}(x)
is positive semidefinite for every
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

(2)

If
H_{f}(x)
is positive definite for every
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
, then
f
is
k
-positive definite.

Proof.

Part (1) follows from applying suitable transformations to the matrix

(f(x_{i}+x_{j}))_{i,j}

for
x_{1}<\cdots<x_{k}
and then letting all
x_{i}
converge to the same value
x
as it is done in
[
15
, §2.1]
.
Part (2) follows from
[
15
, Thm. 2.6]
applied to the kernel
K(x,y)=f(x+y)
.
∎

This criterion allows us to exhibit a function
{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
that is
k
-positive definite, but not
k+1
-positive semidefinite. First we make some observations on the Hankel matrices
H_{f}(x)
.

Remark 4.2
.

By definition the Hankel matrix
H_{f}(x)
is linear in
f
.

For the rest of the section let
0<a_{1}<\cdots<a_{k}
and
g(x)=\sum_{m=1}^{k}\exp(a_{m}x)
.
Let
\bar{g}(x)=g(-x)
.
For any
\epsilon>0
consider the function
f_{\epsilon}\colon{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
given by
f_{\epsilon}(x)=g(x)+\bar{g}(x)-\epsilon
.

Lemma 4.3
.

For any
\epsilon>0
the function
f_{\epsilon}
is not
(k+1)
-positive semidefinite.

Proof.

Let
{p}_{m}(x)=\exp(a_{m}x)
for
m\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
. The Hankel matrix
H_{{p}_{m}}(x)
of order
k+1
equals the rank one matrix

\exp(a_{m}x)\cdot v_{m}^{\top}\cdot v_{m}

where
v_{m}=(1,a_{m},\ldots,a_{m}^{k})
for
m\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
.
Let
w\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k+1}
be a nonzero vector in the orthogonal complement of the subspace spanned by
v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}
. We claim that
w^{\top}
is in the kernel of
H_{g}(x)
for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Indeed, we have for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
that

H_{g}(x)\cdot w^{\top}=\sum_{m=1}^{k}H_{{p}_{m}}(x)\cdot w^{\top}=\sum_{m=1}^{k}\exp(a_{m}x)\cdot v_{m}^{\top}\cdot\underbrace{v_{m}\cdot w^{\top}}_{=0}=0.

Further, we claim that the first component of
w
is nonzero. Indeed, assume otherwise and let
w^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
be such that
w=(0,w^{\prime})
. Then
w^{\prime}
is in the orthogonal complement of the vectors

(a_{1},\ldots,a_{1}^{k}),\ldots,(a_{k},\ldots,a_{k}^{k}).

However, these vectors are linearly independent because
a_{1},\dots,a_{k}
are pairwise different and nonzero. This is a contradiction to our assumption that
w
and thus
w^{\prime}
is nonzero. Therefore, we have shown that the first component of
w
is nonzero. Without loss of generality, we may assume that it equals
1
. For
m\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
let
b_{m}=-a_{m}
and
u_{m}=(1,b_{m},\ldots,b_{m}^{k})
. Then, as above, we compute

wH_{\bar{g}}(x)w^{\top}=\sum_{m=1}^{k}\exp(-a_{m}x)\cdot\langle u_{m},w\rangle^{2}

which tends to zero for
x\to\infty
because
a_{1},\dots,a_{k}
are positive. Let
x_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}
be such that
wH_{\bar{g}}(x)w^{\top}<\epsilon
for all
x>x_{0}
. Then we have for all
x>x_{0}
that

wH_{f_{\epsilon}}w^{\top}=wH_{\bar{g}}w^{\top}-\epsilon<0.

By
4.1
this proves the claim.
∎

Lemma 4.4
.

For sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
the function
f_{\epsilon}
is
k
-positive definite.

Proof.

Let
H_{g}(x)
and
H_{\bar{g}}(x)
be Hankel matrices of order
k
. They are
positive linear combinations of positive semidefinite rank one matrices so they are positive semidefinite for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
. We can write
H_{g}(x)=A+B(x)
where

A=\sum_{m=1}^{k}v_{m}^{\top}\cdot v_{m}
,
B(x)=\sum_{m=1}^{k}(\exp(a_{m}x)-1)\cdot v_{m}^{\top}\cdot v_{m}
and
v_{m}=(1,a_{m},\ldots,a_{m}^{k-1})
.
Similarly, we can write
H_{\bar{g}}(x)=A^{\prime}+B^{\prime}(-x)
where

A^{\prime}=\sum_{m=1}^{k}u_{m}^{\top}\cdot u_{m}
,
B^{\prime}(x)=\sum_{m=1}^{k}(\exp(-a_{m}x)-1)\cdot u_{m}^{\top}\cdot u_{m}
and
u_{m}=(1,-a_{m},\ldots,(-a_{m})^{k-1})
.
We note that when
x\geq 0
we have that
B(x)
is positive semidefinite because
\exp(a_{m}x)\geq 1
for
x\geq 0
and similarly
B^{\prime}(-x)
is positive semidefinite when
x\leq 0
. In any case,
A
and
A^{\prime}
are positive definite since the vectors
v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}
resp.
u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}
are linearly independent. Thus, it suffices to choose
\epsilon>0
in such a way that both
A-\epsilon\cdot\delta_{1}\cdot\delta_{1}^{\top}
and
A^{\prime}-\epsilon\cdot\delta_{1}\cdot\delta_{1}^{\top}
are positive definite, where
\delta_{1}
is the first unit vector, since
\epsilon\cdot\delta_{1}\cdot\delta_{1}^{\top}
is the Hankel matrix of the constant function
\epsilon
. Indeed, for
x\geq 0
we have that

H_{f_{\epsilon}}(x)=(A-\epsilon\cdot\delta_{1}\cdot\delta_{1}^{\top})+B(x)+H_{\bar{g}}(x)

is positive semidefinite. Similarly, for
x\leq 0
, we have that

H_{f_{\epsilon}}(x)=(A^{\prime}-\epsilon\cdot\delta_{1}\cdot\delta_{1}^{\top})+B^{\prime}(-x)+H_{{g}}(x)

is positive semidefinite.

∎

For sufficiently small
\epsilon>0

f_{\epsilon}
has the desired properties.

Appendix A
Real closed separation

We follow the line of arguments from
[
21
]
. We start with a lemma that is well-known which we include for the sake of completeness.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
R
be a real closed field and
I
some finite index set. Let
V_{1},V_{2}
be finite-dimensional
R
-vector spaces. Let
\alpha_{i}\colon V_{1}\to V_{2}
,
i\in I
, be some homogeneous polynomial maps with the property that no non-trivial positive linear combination of images of the
\alpha_{i}
is trivial, meaning that

\sum_{j=1}^{r}\lambda_{j}\alpha_{i_{j}}(v_{j})=0

for
r\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
i_{j}\in I
and
\lambda_{j}>0
implies that all
v_{j}\in V_{i_{j}}
are zero. Then

C:={\rm conv}({\cup_{i\in I}\alpha_{i}(V_{1})})

is a closed convex cone. Moreover, if
b\not\in C
, then there exists an
R
-linear map
\phi\colon V_{2}\to R
, such that
\phi(c)\geq 0
for
c\in C
and
\phi(b)<0
.

Proof.

After choosing bases we can assume that
V_{1}=R^{d}
and
V_{2}=R^{n}
.
Let

S=\{v\in R^{d}\mid||v||_{2}=1\}

the unit sphere with respect to the euclidean norm.
Since each
\alpha_{i}
is homogeneous, we have that
C
is the conical hull of
\cup_{i\in I}\alpha_{i}(S)
. In particular, it is a convex cone. Thus it remains to prove that
C
is closed.
Let
m=|I|
. We consider the map

\Phi:S^{m\cdot n}\times\Delta_{m\cdot n}\to R^{n},\,(v_{11},\ldots,v_{mn},\lambda_{11},\ldots,\lambda_{mn})\mapsto\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{ij}\alpha_{i}(v_{ij})

where
\Delta_{mn}\subset R^{mn}
is the standard simplex. The image
B\subset R^{n}
of
\Phi
is semialgebraically compact as the image of a semialgebraically compact set under a continuous semialgebraic map. We claim that

\Sigma=\{(v,b)\in R^{n}\times B\mid\exists\lambda\geq 0:v=\lambda\cdot b\}

is a closed subset of
R^{n}\times B
. Indeed, it can be written as

\Sigma=\{(v,b)\in R^{n}\times B\mid\forall i,j:(v_{i}b_{j}=v_{j}b_{i})\wedge(v_{i}b_{i}\geq 0)\}

since
0\not\in B
by assumption. Since
B
is semialgebraically compact, the projection of
\Sigma
onto the first coordinate, which equals to
C
by Carathéodory’s theorem for the conical hull, is closed as well.
Now the conical separation theorem implies the additional statement.
∎

Let
R
be a real closed field and
A
a commutative
R
-algebra. For
g_{1},\ldots,g_{r}\in A
we denote

{\rm U}_{A}(g_{1},\ldots,g_{r})=\{\alpha\in\operatorname{Sper}(A)\mid g_{1}(\alpha)>0,\ldots,g_{r}(\alpha)>0\}

where
\operatorname{Sper}(A)
is the
real spectrum
of
A
, see for example
[
5
, §7]
. We further consider the
quadratic module

{\rm QM}_{A}(g_{1},\ldots,g_{r})
, i.e., the set of all

s_{0}+s_{1}g_{1}+\cdots+s_{r}g_{r}\in A

where the
s_{i}\in A
are sum of squares. We say that
A
is
real reduced
if
\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}^{2}=0
implies
a_{1}=\cdots=a_{k}=0
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Theorem A.2
.

Let
R
be a real closed field and let
A
a real reduced commutative
R
-algebra. Let
b,g_{1},\ldots,g_{r}\in A
be such that the following holds:

(1)

b\not\in{\rm QM}_{A}(g_{1},\ldots,g_{r})
,

(2)

{\rm U}_{A}(g_{1},\ldots,g_{r})
is Zariski dense in
\operatorname{Sper}(A)
.

Then there exists a real closed field
R^{\prime}
containing
R
and an
R^{\prime}
-linear map

\phi\colon A^{\prime}=R^{\prime}\otimes_{R}A\to R^{\prime}

such that
\phi(1\otimes b)<0
and
\phi(c)\geq 0
for all
c\in{\rm QM}_{A^{\prime}}(g_{1},\ldots,g_{r})
.

Proof.

Since
A
is real reduced, we have that
\operatorname{Sper}(A)\neq\emptyset
. If
g_{i}=0
for some
i\in\{1,\dots,r\}
, then
{\rm U}_{A}(g_{1},\ldots,g_{r})=\emptyset
contradicting our second assumption. Therefore, all the
g_{i}
are nonzero.
In order to simplify notation we further let
g_{0}=1
.
Let
H
be a finite-dimensional
R
-subspace of
A
containing
b,g_{0},\ldots,g_{r}
. Consider the set

C(H):=\left\{\sum_{i=0}^{r}g_{i}\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{k_{i}}a_{ij}^{2}\mid k_{i}\in\mathbb{N},a_{ij}\in H\right\}.

Note that
C(H)
is a convex cone inside the finite-dimensional subspace

H^{(2)}:={\rm span}_{R}\{g_{i}a_{1}a_{2}\in A\mid a_{1},a_{2}\in H,\,0\leq i\leq r\}
and
b\in H^{(2)}
but
b\not\in C(H).
Since
A
is real-reduced and by condition
(2)
, we can apply
A.1
to the maps

\alpha_{i}:H\to H^{(2)},\,a\mapsto a^{2}\cdot g_{i}.

Therefore, there exists an
R
-linear map
\phi_{H}\colon H^{(2)}\to R
such that
\phi_{H}(c)\geq 0
for all
c\in C(H)
and
\phi_{H}(b)<0
. Let
\phi^{\prime}_{H}\colon A\to R
be an arbitrary linear extension of
\phi_{H}
to
A
. Consider an ultrafilter
\mathcal{U}
on the set of finite-dimensional subspaces of
A
, containing the filter generated by sets of the form
S_{H}:=\{H^{\prime}\mid H\subset H^{\prime}\}
for
H
a finite-dimensional subspace, and consider the ultra-product

R^{\prime}:=\prod{}^{\mathcal{U}}R
which is again a real closed field. There is a natural
R
-linear map
\phi^{\prime}\colon A\to R^{\prime}
induced by all
\phi^{\prime}_{H}
for
H
as above.
Consider the
R^{\prime}
-linear map

\phi:=1_{R^{\prime}}\otimes_{R}\phi^{\prime}\colon R^{\prime}\otimes_{R}A\to R^{\prime}.

It is clear by construction that
\phi(1\otimes b)<0
.
We claim that
\phi
is nonnegative on
{\rm QM}_{A^{\prime}}(g_{1},\ldots,g_{r})
. It suffices to show that
\phi
is nonnegative on elements of the form
a^{2}g_{k}
for
a\in R^{\prime}\otimes_{R}A
and
0\leq k\leq r
. We write
a=\sum_{j=1}^{l}x_{j}\otimes y_{j}
with
x_{j}\in R^{\prime}
and
y_{j}\in A.
Let
H
be a finite-dimensional subspace of
A
containing
b,g_{0},\ldots,g_{r}
and
y_{1},\dots,y_{l}
. Consider the matrix

(\phi(1\otimes g_{k}y_{i}y_{j}))_{i,j=1}^{{l}}=(\phi^{\prime}(g_{k}y_{i}y_{j}))_{i,j=1}^{{l}}\in M_{{l}}(R^{\prime}).

For
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{l})\in R^{\prime l}
and
y:=(g_{k}y_{i}y_{j})_{i,j=1}^{l}
we get

\phi(a^{2}g_{k})=x\phi^{\prime}(y)x^{\top}
, so that it is sufficient to show that
\phi_{H}(y)\in M_{{l}}(R)
is positive semi-definite for all such
H
. However, this is clear since for
x^{\prime}\in R^{l}
, we have

x^{\prime}\phi_{H}(y)x^{\prime\top}=\phi_{H}((x^{\prime}_{1}y_{1}+\cdots+x^{\prime}_{l}y_{l})^{2}\cdot g_{k})\geq 0

by construction.
This finishes the proof.
∎

Corollary A.3
.

Let
R
be a real closed field and let
A
a real reduced commutative
R
-algebra. Let
b\in A
not a sum of squares.
Then there exists a real closed field
R^{\prime}
containing
R
and an
R^{\prime}
-linear map

\phi\colon R^{\prime}\otimes_{R}A\to R^{\prime}

which is nonnegative on sums of squares such that
\phi(1\otimes b)<0
.

Remark A.4
.

Let
X\subset R^{n}
be some algebraic set and
A=R[X]
its coordinate ring, i.e., the ring of all polynomial functions
X\to R
. Then
A
is real reduced and the second condition of
A.2
is equivalent to the geometric condition that

\{a\in X\mid g_{1}(a)>0,\ldots,g_{r}(a)>0\}

is Zariski dense in
X
.

Appendix B
Preservation Theorems

The approach to study definable sets via structure-preserving maps can be used to obtain exact characterisations of existential positive, positive, and first-order definability.
There are various formulations of such preservation theorems. Our formulation in Theorem
B.1
(in Section
B.2
)
is closest to the setting of this article and we have been unable to find it in the literature, but it should be considered to be known.

B.1.
Basic Terminology

A
signature

\tau
is a set of
function symbols
and
relation symbols
. Each symbol is equipped with an arity
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
. A
\tau
-structure

\mathfrak{A}
consists of a set
A
(the
domain
) and

•

for each function symbol
f\in\tau
of arity
k
an operation
f^{\mathfrak{A}}\colon A^{k}\to A
, and

•

for each relation symbol
R\in\tau
of arity
k
a relation
R\subseteq A^{k}
.

Note that the arity might in particular be
0
; function symbols of arity
0
are also called
constants
.
We denote structures by
\mathfrak{A}
,
\mathfrak{B}
,
\mathfrak{C}
, and so on, and the corresponding underlying sets by the corresponding
roman letters
A
,
B
,
C
, and so on.
If
\mathfrak{A}
and
\mathfrak{B}
are structures with the same domain and
\mathfrak{A}
is obtained from
\mathfrak{B}
by dropping some of the relations and operations, then
\mathfrak{A}
is called a
reduct
of
\mathfrak{B}
, and
\mathfrak{B}
is called an
expansion
of
\mathfrak{A}
.

In the usual way we define
\tau
-terms
inductively; constant symbols from
\tau
are
\tau
-terms, variables are
\tau
-terms, and a function symbol of arity
k
applied to a sequence of
\tau
-terms of length
k
is a
\tau
-term. If
t
is a
\tau
-term, we write
t(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
if all the variables that appear in
t
are from
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
.
An
atomic
\tau
-formula
is
an expression of the form

•

\bot
(which stands for
false
), or

•

t_{1}=t_{2}
where
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
are
\tau
-terms, or

•

R(t_{1},\dots,t_{k})
where
R\in\tau
is a relation symbol of arity
k
and
t_{1},\dots,t_{k}
are
\tau
-terms.

A
first-order
\tau
-formula
is built from atomic
\tau
-formulas in the usual inductive manner, using conjunction
\wedge
, disjunction
\vee
, negation
\neg
, universal quantification
\forall
, and existential quantification
\exists
. If
\phi
is a first-order formula, we write
\phi(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
if all the variables that appear
free
in
\phi
(i.e., that are not bound by a universal or existential quantifier; for formal details, see
[
13
]
) are from
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
. Formulas without free variables are called
sentences
.
If
\phi(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
is a
\tau
-formula and
a_{1},\dots,a_{k}\in A
we define

\mathfrak{A}\models\phi(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
if

(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})

satisfies

\phi
in
\mathfrak{A}
(see
[
13
]
for a precise definition).
A
\tau
-formula
\phi(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
defines over
\mathfrak{A}
a relation
R\subseteq A^{k}
, namely

\big\{(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})\in A^{k}\mid\mathfrak{A}\models\phi(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})\big\}.

An
\tau
-formula
\phi(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
defines over
\mathfrak{A}
an operation
f\colon A^{k-1}\to A
if
\phi
defines over
\mathfrak{A}
the
graph
of
f
, i.e., the relation

\{(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})\in A^{k}\mid f(a_{1},\dots,a_{k-1})=a_{k}\}.

We say that
\mathfrak{A}
is a
first-order reduct
of
\mathfrak{B}
if
\mathfrak{A}
is a reduct of an expansion of
\mathfrak{B}
by relations and operations that are first-order definable in
\mathfrak{B}
.

B.2.
Preservation Theorems

A first-order formula is called
existential positive
if it is built without negation and universal quantification. Note that every existential positive formula is equivalent to a disjunction of
primitive positive formulas
, i.e., formulas of the form

\exists x_{1},\dots,x_{k}(\psi_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge\psi_{m})

where
\psi_{1},\dots,\psi_{m}
are atomic formulas.

Theorem B.1
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}
be a structure.
Then
\mathfrak{A}
has an elementary extension
\mathfrak{A}^{*}
such that the following holds.
If
\mathfrak{B}
is a first-order reduct of
\mathfrak{A}
and
K\subseteq A^{k}
is first-order definable over
\mathfrak{A}
, then

•

K
has an existential positive definition in
\mathfrak{B}
if and only if

K^{*}
is preserved by all endomorphisms of
\mathfrak{B}^{*}
;

•

K
has a positive definition in
\mathfrak{B}
if and only if

K^{*}
is preserved by all surjective endomorphisms of
\mathfrak{B}^{*}
;

•

K
has a first-order definition in
\mathfrak{B}
if and only if
K^{*}
is preserved by all automorphisms of
\mathfrak{B}^{*}
.

Here,
\mathfrak{B}^{*}
is the first-order reduct of
\mathfrak{A}^{*}
defined by the same formulas as the first-order reduct
\mathfrak{B}
of
\mathfrak{A}
,
and
K^{*}
is the relation defined over
\mathfrak{A}^{*}
by the same first-order formula that defines
K
over
\mathfrak{A}
.

We prove Theorem
B.1
in Section
B.4
.

Remark B.2
.

In our setting, the real-closed field
{\mathbb{R}}
plays the role of
\mathfrak{A}
.
For the results in Section
3
we then
work with the first-order reduct

\mathfrak{B}
of
\mathfrak{A}
that contains all spectrahedra as relations, to show that specific relations
K
with a first-order definition in
\mathfrak{A}
do not have a positive definition in
\mathfrak{B}
.
In Section
4
we work with the first-order reduct
\mathfrak{B}
of
\mathfrak{A}
that contains all relations that are defined by LMIs of size
k
to show that some specific relations
K
with a first-order definition in
\mathfrak{A}
do not have a positive definition in
\mathfrak{B}
. In both settings, Lemma
2.3
is a statement about the endomorphisms of
\mathfrak{B}^{*}
.

The elementary extension
\mathfrak{A}^{*}
of
\mathfrak{A}
in
B.1
will be a so-called
special model
(see, e.g.,
[
13
]
). In the context of preservation theorems often
saturated structures
are used.
Special structures are more technical to define than saturated structures. However, the existence of saturated structures requires the generalised continuum hypothesis which we would like to avoid.

B.3.
Saturated and Special Structures

For
n\geq 0
, an

n
-type
of a theory
T
is a set
p

of formulas with free variables
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
such that
p\cup T

is satisfiable.
An
n
-type over a structure
\mathfrak{A}
is an
n
-type of the first-order theory of
{\mathfrak{A}}
;
note that we do not admit parameters in these formulas (i.e., we allow constants to appear in the formulas of the type only if they belong to the signature).
An
n
-type
p
of
\mathfrak{A}
is
realised
in
\mathfrak{A}
if there exist
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\in A
such that
\mathfrak{A}\models\phi(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})
for each
\phi\in p
.
For an infinite cardinal
\kappa
, a structure
\mathfrak{A}
is
\kappa
-saturated
if,
for all
\beta<\kappa
and expansions
\mathfrak{A}^{\prime}
of
\mathfrak{A}
by at most
\beta
constants, every
1
-type of
\mathfrak{A}^{\prime}
is realised
in
\mathfrak{A}^{\prime}
. We say that an infinite structure
\mathfrak{A}
is
saturated
if it is
|A|
-saturated.
If
\lambda
is a cardinal, then
\lambda^{+}
denotes the successor cardinal of
\lambda
.

Theorem B.3

(Theorem 4.3.12 in
[
16
]
)
.

Let
\tau
be a signature and
\lambda\geq|\tau|
.
Then every
\tau
-structure
\mathfrak{B}
has a
\lambda^{+}
-saturated
elementary extension of cardinality
\leq|B|^{\lambda}
.

So if we assume the Generalised
Continuum Hypothesis, then there
are saturated models of size
\kappa^{+}
for all cardinals
\kappa
(Corollary 10.2.4 in
[
13
]
).

Definition B.4
.

A structure
\mathfrak{A}
of cardinality
\kappa\geq\omega
is

special
if
\mathfrak{A}
is the union of an elementary chain
(\mathfrak{B}_{\lambda})_{\lambda<\kappa}
and each
\mathfrak{B}_{\lambda}
is
\lambda^{+}
-saturated.

Saturated structures are special, because we can write a saturated structure

\mathfrak{A}
of cardinality
\kappa

as
(\mathfrak{B}_{\lambda})_{\lambda<\kappa}
where

\mathfrak{B}_{\lambda}=\mathfrak{A}
; since
\mathfrak{A}
is
\lambda^{+}
-saturated for all
\lambda<\kappa
, this proves that
\mathfrak{A}
is special. The converse is not true; see
[
13
, Exercises 6 to 8 on page 514]
. The advantage of special structures is that they always exist, independently from set-theoretic assumptions.

Theorem B.5

(Theorem 10.4.2. in
[
13
]
)
.

Every infinite structure has a special elementary extension.

B.4.
Proof of the Preservation Theorems

The following is at the heart of the proof of
B.1
.

Theorem B.6
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}
and
\mathfrak{B}
be special structures of the same signature and cardinality.

•

If every first-order sentence that is true in
\mathfrak{A}
is true in
\mathfrak{B}
, then there exists an isomorphism between
\mathfrak{A}
and
\mathfrak{B}
.

•

If every positive sentence that is true in
\mathfrak{A}
is true in
\mathfrak{B}
, then there exists a surjective homomorphism from
\mathfrak{A}
to
\mathfrak{B}
.

•

If every existential positive sentence that is true in
\mathfrak{A}
is true in
\mathfrak{B}
, then there exists a homomorphism from
\mathfrak{A}
to
\mathfrak{B}
.

Proof.

The first statement can be shown by a back and forth argument, and is Theorem 10.4.4. in
[
13
]
.
The other two statements can be shown analogously.
∎

Lemma B.7

(see Theorem 10.4.5. in
[
13
]
)
.

First-order reducts of special structures are special.

Proof of
B.1
.

In each of the three statements, the forward direction is immediate, since
homomorphisms preserve existential positive formulas, surjective homomorphisms preserve positive formulas, and automorphisms preserve first-order formulas. Moreover, if
K
has a certain definition in
\mathfrak{B}
, then
K^{*}
has the corresponding definition in
\mathfrak{B}^{*}
since all the involved structures and sets are first-order definable in
\mathfrak{A}
and
\mathfrak{A}^{*}
, respectively, and
\mathfrak{A}
and
\mathfrak{A}^{*}
have the same first-order theory.

The three statements are well-known if
\mathfrak{A}
is finite (see, e.g.,
[
25
]
) so let us assume that
\mathfrak{A}
is infinite.
Let
\mathfrak{A}^{*}
be a special elementary extension of
\mathfrak{A}
which exists by
B.5
,
and let
S
be the first-order theory of
\mathfrak{A}
(and
\mathfrak{A}^{*}
).
Let
\mathfrak{B}
be a first-order reduct of
\mathfrak{A}
with signature
\tau
and let
K\subseteq A^{k}
be first-order definable over
\mathfrak{A}
.
Let
\mathfrak{B}^{*}
be the reduct of
\mathfrak{A}^{*}
and
K^{*}\subseteq(A^{*})^{k}
be as in the statement of the theorem. By

B.7
we have that
\mathfrak{B}^{*}
is special as well.

To show the converse of the first statement, suppose that
K^{*}
is preserved by all endomorphisms of
\mathfrak{B}^{*}
.
Let
\Psi
be the set of all existential positive

\tau
-formulas
\psi(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})

that hold on all tuples from
K^{*}
in
\mathfrak{B}^{*}
.
Let
\underline{a}
be a tuple satisfying

\Psi
in
\mathfrak{B}^{*}
. Note that if there is no such tuple,
then
\phi
must be unsatisfiable in
\mathfrak{B}
. In this case,
\phi
is equivalent to the existential positive formula
\bot
and we are done.

Let
U
be the set of all primitive positive
\tau
-formulas
\theta(\underline{x})
such that
\mathfrak{B}^{*}\models\neg\theta(\bar{a})
. For any first-order
\tau
-formula
\eta

let
T(\eta)
be the first-order formula
obtained from
\eta
by replacing each atomic subformula of
\eta
by its first-order definition over
\mathfrak{A}^{*}
.
We claim that

S\cup\{T(\neg\theta)\mid\theta\in U\}\cup\{\phi\}

is satisfiable. Otherwise, by the compactness theorem of first-order logic there would be a finite subset
V
of
U
such that
S\cup\{T(\neg\theta)\mid\theta\in V\}\cup\{\phi\}
is unsatisfiable.
Since
\neg T(\theta)=T(\neg\theta)

we get that
S\cup\{\phi\}
implies
\bigvee_{\theta\in V}T(\theta)
.
Thus,
\psi:=\bigvee_{\theta\in V}\theta
is an existential positive formula such that
S\cup\{\phi\}\models T(\psi)
.
But then
\psi\in\Psi
and
\mathfrak{B}^{*}\models\psi(\underline{a})
.
This is in contradiction to the assumption that
\mathfrak{B}^{*}\models\neg\theta(\underline{a})
for all
\theta\in U
.

Since
\mathfrak{A}^{*}
is a special model of
S
, the
n
-type

\{T(\neg\theta(\bar{x}))\mid\theta\in U\}\cup\{\phi(\bar{x})\}
is realised by some
b\in(A^{*})^{k}
in
\mathfrak{A}^{*}
.
Let
c_{1},\dots,c_{k}
be new constant symbols
and let
\mathfrak{C}
be the
\tau\cup\{c_{1},\dots,c_{k}\}
-expansion of
\mathfrak{B}^{*}
where
\underline{c}^{\mathfrak{C}}=\underline{b}
.
Let
\mathfrak{D}
be the
\tau\cup\{c_{1},\dots,c_{k}\}
-expansion of
\mathfrak{B}^{*}
where
\underline{c}^{\mathfrak{C}}=\underline{a}
.
Clearly,
\mathfrak{C}
and
\mathfrak{D}
are special as well.
Every primitive positive
(\tau\cup\{c_{1},\dots,c_{k}\})
-sentence
\theta

that is true in
\mathfrak{D}
is also
true in
\mathfrak{C}
: otherwise, if
\theta
were false in
\mathfrak{C}
, then write
\theta
as
\theta(\underline{c})
for
\theta\in U
,
in contradiction to the assumption that
\mathfrak{D}\models\{\neg\theta(\underline{c})\;|\;\theta\in U\}
.
Hence, since
\mathfrak{C}
is special and by
B.6
,
there exists a homomorphism from
\mathfrak{D}
to

\mathfrak{C}
. This homomorphism maps
\underline{b}
to
\underline{a}
.
Since
\underline{b}
satisfies
\phi
in
\mathfrak{A}^{*}
we have that

\underline{b}\in K^{*}
, and since
K^{*}
is preserved by endomorphisms of
\mathfrak{B}^{*}
, we have
\underline{a}\in K^{*}
.
We conclude that
S\cup\{T(\theta)\mid\theta\in\Psi\}\cup\{\neg\phi\}
is unsatisfiable, and again by compactness there exists a finite subset
\Psi^{\prime}
of

\Psi
such that
S\cup\{T(\theta)\mid\theta\in\Psi^{\prime}\}\cup\{\neg\phi\}
is unsatisfiable. Note that
\psi:=\bigwedge\Psi^{\prime}
is
an existential positive formula
and
S\models\forall\underline{x}(T(\psi)\Leftrightarrow\phi)

which is what we wanted to show.

The converse directions for statement two and three of the theorem can be shown analogously.
∎
