Numerical method for the equilibrium configurations of a Maier-Saupe bulk potential in a Q-tensor model of an anisotropic nematic liquid crystal Journal: Journal of Computational Physics
Cody D. Schimming, Jorge Viñals, Shawn W. Walker

Abstract
We present a numerical method, based on a tensor order parameter description of a nematic phase, that allows fully anisotropic elasticity. Our method thus extends the Landau-de Gennes 𝐐 \mathbf{Q} -tensor theory to anisotropic phases. A microscopic model of the nematogen is introduced (the Maier-Saupe potential in the case discussed in this paper), combined with a constraint on eigenvalue bounds of 𝐐 \mathbf{Q} . This ensures a physically valid order parameter 𝐐 \mathbf{Q} (i.e., the eigenvalue bounds are maintained), while allowing for more general gradient energy densities that can include cubic nonlinearities, and therefore elastic anisotropy. We demonstrate the method in two specific two dimensional examples in which the Landau-de Gennes model including elastic anisotropy is known to fail, as well as in three dimensions for the cases of a hedgehog point defect, a disclination line, and a disclination ring. The details of the numerical implementation are also discussed.

2
Liquid Crystal Theory

We briefly review in this section the Landau-de Gennes theory for a nematic phase, as well as the more microscopic Maier-Saupe theory. Consider an anisotropic LC molecule which is uniaxial, with orientation described by the unit vector
\mathbf{p}
. The Maier-Saupe potential between two molecules
i
and
j
is a contact interaction of the form
-\kappa\left((\mathbf{p}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{p}_{j})^{2}-1/3)\right)
, where
\kappa
is the interaction constant
[
1
]
. In the isotropic phase, the thermal average of
\mathbf{p}
is zero, while it is nonzero in the nematic phase. The Landau-de Gennes theory of a nematic phase is formulated instead in terms of a mesoscopic order parameter, the symmetric, traceless tensor
\mathbf{Q}
. In the isotropic phase
\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{0}
. In the nematic phase,
\mathbf{Q}
is nonzero. A uniaxial nematic phase corresponds to two of the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{Q}
being equal, and a biaxial phase to the general case. Note that although the molecules themselves are uniaxial, the distribution of local orientations may itself be uniaxial or biaxial.

2.1
Landau-de Gennes Theory

Let
\mathbf{\Xi}
be the set of symmetric, traceless
3\times 3
matrices. Given a tensor-valued function
\mathbf{Q}:\Omega\to\mathbf{\Xi}

\Gamma

[
44

\begin{split}E[\mathbf{Q}]&:=\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{W}(\mathbf{Q},\nabla\mathbf{Q})\,d\mathbf{x}+\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\psi(\mathbf{Q})\,d\mathbf{x}\\
&\qquad+\eta_{\Gamma}\int_{\Gamma}f_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{Q})\,dS(\mathbf{x})-\int_{\Omega}\chi(\mathbf{Q})\,d\mathbf{x},\end{split}

(1)

\begin{split}\mathcal{W}(\mathbf{Q},\nabla\mathbf{Q})&:=\frac{1}{2}\Big(L_{1}|\nabla\mathbf{Q}|^{2}+L_{2}|\nabla\cdot\mathbf{Q}|^{2}+L_{3}(\nabla\mathbf{Q})^{T}\mathrel{\hbox{\hskip 1.3889pt\hskip-1.3889pt\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 1.38889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\hbox{$\cdot$}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\raisebox{2.44444pt}{\hbox{$\cdot$}}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip 1.38889pt}}\hskip-1.3889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\raisebox{-2.44444pt}{\hbox{$\cdot$}}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip 1.3889pt}}\nabla\mathbf{Q},\\
&\qquad\qquad+L_{4}\nabla\mathbf{Q}\mathrel{\hbox{\hskip 1.3889pt\hskip-1.3889pt\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 1.38889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\hbox{$\cdot$}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\raisebox{2.44444pt}{\hbox{$\cdot$}}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip 1.38889pt}}\hskip-1.3889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\raisebox{-2.44444pt}{\hbox{$\cdot$}}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip 1.3889pt}}(\mathbf{e}\cdot\mathbf{Q})+L_{*}\nabla\mathbf{Q}\mathrel{\hbox{\hskip 1.3889pt\hskip-1.3889pt\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 1.38889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\hbox{$\cdot$}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\raisebox{2.44444pt}{\hbox{$\cdot$}}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip 1.38889pt}}\hskip-1.3889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\raisebox{-2.44444pt}{\hbox{$\cdot$}}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip 1.3889pt}}[(\mathbf{Q}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{Q}]\Big),\end{split}

where

\begin{split}|\nabla\mathbf{Q}|^{2}:=(\partial_{k}Q_{ij})^{2},\quad|\nabla\cdot\mathbf{Q}|^{2}:=(\partial_{j}Q_{ij})^{2},\quad(\nabla\mathbf{Q})^{T}\mathrel{\hbox{\hskip 1.3889pt\hskip-1.3889pt\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 1.38889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\hbox{$\cdot$}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\raisebox{2.44444pt}{\hbox{$\cdot$}}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip 1.38889pt}}\hskip-1.3889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\raisebox{-2.44444pt}{\hbox{$\cdot$}}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip 1.3889pt}}\nabla\mathbf{Q}&:=(\partial_{j}Q_{ik})(\partial_{k}Q_{ij}),\\
\nabla\mathbf{Q}\mathrel{\hbox{\hskip 1.3889pt\hskip-1.3889pt\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 1.38889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\hbox{$\cdot$}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\raisebox{2.44444pt}{\hbox{$\cdot$}}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip 1.38889pt}}\hskip-1.3889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\raisebox{-2.44444pt}{\hbox{$\cdot$}}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip 1.3889pt}}(\mathbf{e}\cdot\mathbf{Q}):=e_{jkl}Q_{ji}\partial_{l}Q_{ki},\quad\nabla\mathbf{Q}\mathrel{\hbox{\hskip 1.3889pt\hskip-1.3889pt\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 1.38889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\hbox{$\cdot$}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\raisebox{2.44444pt}{\hbox{$\cdot$}}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip 1.38889pt}}\hskip-1.3889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\raisebox{-2.44444pt}{\hbox{$\cdot$}}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip 1.3889pt}}[(\mathbf{Q}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{Q}]&:=Q_{lk}(\partial_{l}Q_{ij})(\partial_{k}Q_{ij}).\end{split}

2.2
Landau-de Gennes bulk potential

The bulk potential
\psi
is a double-well type of function that is given by

\begin{split}\psi(\mathbf{Q})=K-\frac{A}{2}\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{Q}^{2})-\frac{B}{3}\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{Q}^{3})+\frac{C}{4}\left(\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{Q}^{2})\right)^{2}.\end{split}

(7)

Above,
A
,
B
,
C
are material parameters such that
A
,
B
,
C
are positive;
K
is a convenient constant to ensure
\psi\geq 0
. Stationary points of
\psi
are either uniaxial or isotropic
\mathbf{Q}
-tensors
[
52
]
.

This potential was introduced to describe the vicinity of the isotropic-nematic phase transition, which is weakly first order. Therefore the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{Q}
are small. However, the same potential is used to describe systems deep inside the nematic phase, while not providing for any constraint on the eigenvalues. It is known that they can leave their physically admissible range in some circumstances. For example, consideration of an elastically anisotropic phase
K_{1}\neq K_{3}
requires that
L_{*}\neq 0
. In this case, the energy
E[\mathbf{Q}]
is unbounded below for any choice of physical parameters
[
31
,
32
]
, a divergence that is related to the absence of a constraint on the eigenvalues. The computational approach that we present here is precisely designed to remedy this problem.

3
Self Consistent Mean Field Theory

3.1
Macroscopic order parameter

We review the singular bulk potential introduced in
[
53
,
31
]
. The goal is to have a bulk potential that correctly controls the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbf{\Xi}
, where
\mathbf{\Xi}
is the set of symmetric, traceless
3\times 3
matrices. Note that
\mathbf{\Xi}
is spanned by a set of five basis matrices
\{\mathbf{E}^{k}\}_{k=1}^{5}

[
54
]
.

The first step is to introduce a definition of the macroscopic order parameter (or mesoscopic field, under the assumption of local equilibrium), given by

\begin{split}\mathbf{Q}=\int_{S^{2}}\left(\mathbf{p}\otimes\mathbf{p}-\frac{1}{3}\mathbf{I}\right)\rho(\mathbf{p})\,dS(\mathbf{p}),\end{split}

(8)

where
\rho\in\mathcal{P}
is the equilibrium probability distribution of the LC molecules given by statistical mechanics, i.e.

\begin{split}\mathcal{P}:=\left\{\rho\in L^{1}(S^{2};\mathbb{R})\mid~\rho\geq 0,\quad\int_{S^{2}}\rho(\mathbf{p})\,dS(\mathbf{p})=1\right\}.\end{split}

(9)

Note that
\mathbf{Q}
as defined is a thermal average. Therefore the minimization discussed in Sec.
3.2
at fixed
\mathbf{Q}
needs to be understood in a mean field sense. Note also that in the case of a non uniform configuration, we will assume that the same definition is valid so that an order parameter field
\mathbf{Q}(\mathbf{x})
is defined from the local distribution
\rho(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{x})
.

Equation (
8
) implies that the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{Q}
, denoted
\lambda_{i}\equiv\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{Q})
, satisfy

\begin{split}-\frac{1}{3}\leq\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{Q})\leq\frac{2}{3},~\text{ for }i=1,2,3,\qquad\sum_{i=1}^{3}\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{Q})=0.\end{split}

(10)

In numerical work involving the Landau-de Gennes energy, equilibrium configurations of
\mathbf{Q}
are obtained by energy minimization, where the energy functional
E(\mathbf{Q})
is
independent
of any probability distribution of the underlying orientation of uniaxial molecules. In other words, (
10
) is not guaranteed. In contrast, the potential function defined below in Eq. (
13
) provides an energetic penalty so that the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{Q}
satisfy the bounds in (
10
).

3.2
Self-consistent free energy

Let us define the entropy functional

\begin{split}S[\rho]=\int_{S^{2}}\rho(\mathbf{p})\ln\rho(\mathbf{p})\,dS(\mathbf{p}),\end{split}

(11)

and the intermolecular interaction kernel

\begin{split}K[\rho,\eta]=\int_{S^{2}}\int_{S^{2}}\left[(\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{q})^{2}-\frac{1}{3}\right]\rho(\mathbf{p})\eta(\mathbf{q})\,dS(\mathbf{p})\,dS(\mathbf{q}),\end{split}

(12)

where
\rho
and
\eta
are two probability distribution functions in
{\cal P}
. The Maier-Saupe Potential is defined as

\begin{split}I_{\mathrm{MS}}[\rho]=TS[\rho]-\kappa K[\rho,\rho],\end{split}

(13)

where
T>0
is temperature, and
\kappa>0
is a constant (we have omitted the Boltzmann constant
k_{B}
). With this definition,
I_{MS}
reduces to the thermodynamic free energy when the distribution
\rho
is the corresponding equilibrium probability distribution.

One, however, proceeds differently. Given a value of
\mathbf{Q}
(or locally, if a field
\mathbf{Q}(\mathbf{x})
is specified), we minimize
I_{\mathrm{MS}}[\rho]
over the space of probability distribution functions with the condition that
\mathbf{Q}
is given by Eq. (
8
).

It is straightforward to write the interaction energy solely as a function of
\mathbf{Q}
. We have

\begin{split}K[\rho,\rho]&=\int_{S^{2}}\mathbf{q}^{T}\left(\int_{S^{2}}\left[\mathbf{p}\otimes\mathbf{p}-\frac{1}{3}\mathbf{I}\right]\rho(\mathbf{p})\,dS(\mathbf{p})\right)\mathbf{q}\rho(\mathbf{q})\,dS(\mathbf{q})\\
&=\int_{S^{2}}\mathbf{q}^{T}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{q}\rho(\mathbf{q})\,dS(\mathbf{q})=\mathbf{Q}:\int_{S^{2}}\left[\mathbf{q}\otimes\mathbf{q}-\frac{1}{3}\mathbf{I}\right]\rho(\mathbf{q})\,dS(\mathbf{q})=\mathbf{Q}:\mathbf{Q}=|\mathbf{Q}|^{2},\end{split}

(14)

where we used the fact that
\mathbf{Q}
is traceless. Therefore, the energy term
in the Maier-Saupe free energy of a given configuration
\mathbf{Q}
is
simply
-\kappa|\mathbf{Q}|^{2}
.

The computation of the entropy for fixed
\mathbf{Q}
is more complex. As in other field theories, one needs to “invert” the relationship in (
8
), i.e., given
\mathbf{Q}
find the corresponding
\rho
that provides this value of
\mathbf{Q}
in equilibrium. Of course, this is ill-posed, so we must impose some additional conditions. We use a mean field assumption, according to which
\rho
minimizes
I_{\mathrm{MS}}[\rho]
over the admissible set, and define the corresponding mean field free energy as

\begin{split}\psi(\mathbf{Q})&:=\inf_{\rho\in\mathcal{A}_{\mathbf{Q}}}I_{\mathrm{MS}}[\rho],\\
&=T\inf_{\rho\in\mathcal{A}_{\mathbf{Q}}}S[\rho]-\kappa|\mathbf{Q}|^{2},\end{split}

(15)

where the admissible set is

\begin{split}\mathcal{A}_{\mathbf{Q}}:=\left\{\rho\in\mathcal{P}\mid\mathbf{Q}=\int_{S^{2}}\left[\mathbf{p}\otimes\mathbf{p}-\frac{1}{3}\mathbf{I}\right]\rho(\mathbf{p})\,dS(\mathbf{p})\right\}.\end{split}

(16)

Since
\mathbf{Q}
is fixed, we can focus on the entropy. Define

\begin{split}f(\mathbf{Q}):=\begin{cases}\inf_{\rho\in\mathcal{A}_{\mathbf{Q}}}S[\rho],&\text{if eigenvalues of }\mathbf{Q}\text{ satisfy \eqref{eqn:Q_eigenvalue_bounds}},\\
+\infty,&\text{else}.\end{cases}\end{split}

(17)

Then

\begin{split}\psi(\mathbf{Q})&=Tf(\mathbf{Q})-\kappa|\mathbf{Q}|^{2}.\end{split}

(18)

) for

(19)

over the “physical triangle” as in
[
52
]
. For the plots we set
\kappa/T=4
. As seen in the figure, when
\mathbf{Q}
gets close to the physical bounds the bulk potential diverges. For this value of
\kappa/T
, there are three minima corresponding to uniaxial states where the director is
\mathbf{n}
,
\mathbf{m}
, or
\mathbf{n}\times\mathbf{m}
. For
\kappa/T<3.4049
there is a single minimum at
\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{0}

Lemma 1
.

For any
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbf{\Xi}
, such that
-1/3<\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{Q})<2/3

Proof.

\mathbf{\Lambda}=\mathbf{R}^{T}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{R}

,

. Now define the following (generalized) function (singular measure)

\begin{split}\tilde{\rho}(\mathbf{p})=\sum_{k=1}^{3}\left(\lambda_{k}+\frac{1}{3}\right)\frac{\delta(\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{e}_{k})+\delta(\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{e}_{k})}{2},\quad\text{where}\quad\int_{S^{2}}\tilde{\rho}(\mathbf{p})\,dS(\mathbf{p})=1,\end{split}

(20)

where

, for

, is the Dirac delta function on

\begin{split}\int_{S^{2}}g(\mathbf{p})\delta(\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{a})\,dS(\mathbf{p})=g(\mathbf{a}).\end{split}

\begin{split}X_{ii}=\lambda_{i},~\text{ for }i=1,2,3,\quad\text{and}\quad X_{ij}=0,~\text{ for }i\neq j.\end{split}

In other words, we have

\begin{split}\mathbf{\Lambda}=\int_{S^{2}}\left(\mathbf{p}\otimes\mathbf{p}-\frac{1}{3}\mathbf{I}\right)\tilde{\rho}(\mathbf{p})\,dS(\mathbf{p}),\end{split}

(23)

i.e. it satisfies the constraint. Next, we replace

by a regularized version

\begin{split}\phi_{k}^{\epsilon}(\mathbf{p})=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2|A_{\epsilon}^{1}|},&\text{if }~|\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{e}_{k}|\geq 1-\epsilon,\\
0,&\text{if }~|\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{e}_{k}|<1-\epsilon,\end{cases}\end{split}

(24)

where

\begin{split}\tilde{\rho}^{\epsilon}(\mathbf{p})=a_{0}\sum_{k=1}^{3}\left(\lambda_{k}+\frac{1}{3}+a_{1}\right)\phi_{k}^{\epsilon}(\mathbf{p}),\end{split}

where

are constants. For convenience, define

\begin{split}r_{i}^{k}=\frac{1}{2|A_{\epsilon}^{1}|}\int_{+A_{\epsilon}^{k}\cup-A_{\epsilon}^{k}}p_{i}^{2}\,dS(\mathbf{p}),\quad r_{i}^{i}=1-O(\epsilon),\forall i,\quad r_{i}^{k}=O(\epsilon^{2}),\text{ for }i\neq k,\end{split}

and note that

which implies that

.
Now define
X_{ij}^{\epsilon}:=\int_{S^{2}}\left(\mathbf{p}\otimes\mathbf{p}-\frac{1}{3}\mathbf{I}\right)_{ij}\tilde{\rho}^{\epsilon}(\mathbf{p})\,dS(\mathbf{p})
, for
1\leq i,j\leq 3
, and compute:

\begin{split}X_{ii}^{\epsilon}&=a_{0}\sum_{k=1}^{3}\left(\lambda_{k}+\frac{1}{3}+a_{1}\right)\frac{1}{2|A_{\epsilon}^{1}|}\int_{+A_{\epsilon}^{k}\cup-A_{\epsilon}^{k}}\left(p_{i}^{2}-\frac{1}{3}\right)\,dS(\mathbf{p})\\
&=a_{0}\sum_{k=1}^{3}\left(\lambda_{k}+\frac{1}{3}+a_{1}\right)\left(r_{i}^{k}-\frac{1}{3}\right)\\
&=a_{0}\sum_{k\neq i}\left(\lambda_{k}+\frac{1}{3}+a_{1}\right)\left(r_{i}^{k}-\frac{1}{3}\right)+a_{0}\left(\lambda_{i}+\frac{1}{3}+a_{1}\right)\left(r_{i}^{i}-\frac{1}{3}\right).\end{split}

(28)

Since
r_{i}^{i}=r_{1}^{1}
for all
i
, and
r_{i}^{k}=r_{1}^{2}
for all
i\neq k
, we continue to simplify (
28
):

\begin{split}X_{ii}^{\epsilon}a_{0}^{-1}&=\left(r_{1}^{2}-\frac{1}{3}\right)\sum_{k\neq i}\left(\lambda_{k}+\frac{1}{3}+a_{1}\right)+\left(\lambda_{i}+\frac{1}{3}+a_{1}\right)\left(r_{1}^{1}-\frac{1}{3}\right)\\
&=\left(r_{1}^{2}-\frac{1}{3}\right)\left[\frac{2}{3}-\lambda_{i}+2a_{1}\right]+\left(\lambda_{i}+\frac{1}{3}+a_{1}\right)\left(r_{1}^{1}-\frac{1}{3}\right)\\
&=\lambda_{i}\left(r_{1}^{1}-r_{1}^{2}\right)+\left(\frac{1}{3}+a_{1}\right)\underbrace{\left(r_{1}^{1}+2r_{1}^{2}-1\right)}_{=0}\\
&=\lambda_{i}\left(r_{1}^{1}-r_{1}^{2}\right).\end{split}

(29)

Thus, letting
a_{0}:=\left(r_{1}^{1}-r_{1}^{2}\right)^{-1}=1+O(\epsilon)
, we get

\begin{split}X_{ii}^{\epsilon}=\lambda_{i},\quad\text{for}\quad i=1,2,3.\end{split}

(30)

In addition, for
i\neq j
, we see that

\begin{split}X_{ij}^{\epsilon}&=a_{0}\sum_{k=1}^{3}\left(\lambda_{k}+\frac{1}{3}+a_{1}\right)\frac{1}{2|A_{\epsilon}^{1}|}\int_{+A_{\epsilon}^{k}\cup-A_{\epsilon}^{k}}p_{i}p_{j}\,dS(\mathbf{p})=0,\end{split}

(31)

where the integral term drops by symmetry/cancellation. Note that (
30
) and (
31
) hold for any value of
a_{1}
. We must choose
a_{1}
such that

\begin{split}1&=\int_{S^{2}}\tilde{\rho}^{\epsilon}(\mathbf{p})\,dS(\mathbf{p})=a_{0}\sum_{k=1}^{3}\left(\lambda_{k}+\frac{1}{3}+a_{1}\right)=a_{0}\left(1+3a_{1}\right),\\
\Rightarrow\quad a_{1}&=\frac{1}{3}\left(\frac{1}{a_{0}}-1\right)=O(\epsilon),\end{split}

(32)

where
a_{1}
may be negative. Since
\lambda_{k}+1/3>0
, choosing
\epsilon>0
sufficiently small (but fixed), we see that
\lambda_{k}+(1/3)+a_{1}>0
for
k=1,2,3
. Therefore,
\tilde{\rho}^{\epsilon}(\mathbf{p})\geq 0
for all
\mathbf{p}
, and
\tilde{\rho}^{\epsilon}\in\mathcal{P}
. Moreover,

\begin{split}\mathbf{\Lambda}=\int_{S^{2}}\left(\mathbf{p}\otimes\mathbf{p}-\frac{1}{3}\mathbf{I}\right)\tilde{\rho}^{\epsilon}(\mathbf{p})\,dS(\mathbf{p}),\end{split}

(33)

and
\tilde{\rho}^{\epsilon}\in\mathcal{A}_{\mathbf{\Lambda}}
.
Finally, by rotating coordinates with
\mathbf{R}
, and defining
\hat{\rho}^{\epsilon}(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{p}):=\tilde{\rho}^{\epsilon}(\mathbf{p})
, (
33
) transforms into

\begin{split}\mathbf{Q}=\int_{S^{2}}\left(\hat{\mathbf{p}}\otimes\hat{\mathbf{p}}-\frac{1}{3}\mathbf{I}\right)\hat{\rho}^{\epsilon}(\hat{\mathbf{p}})\,dS(\hat{\mathbf{p}}),\end{split}

(34)

where
\hat{\rho}^{\epsilon}\in\mathcal{A}_{\mathbf{Q}}
. So
\mathcal{A}_{\mathbf{Q}}
is non-empty.
∎

The following result lays out the main aspects of
f
needed.

Theorem 2
.

Given
\mathbf{Q}
with
-1/3<\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{Q})<2/3
(for
i=1,2,3
), there exists a unique minimizer
\rho^{*}\in\mathcal{A}_{\mathbf{Q}}
to the optimization problem in (
17
). In other words,

\begin{split}f(\mathbf{Q})=\int_{S^{2}}\rho^{*}(\mathbf{p})\ln\rho^{*}(\mathbf{p})\,dS(\mathbf{p}),\end{split}

(35)

where

\begin{split}\rho^{*}(\mathbf{p})=\frac{\exp\left(\mathbf{p}^{T}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{p}\right)}{Z(\mathbf{A})},\quad Z(\mathbf{A})=\int_{S^{2}}\exp\left(\mathbf{p}^{T}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{p}\right)\,dS(\mathbf{p}),\end{split}

(36)

and
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbf{\Xi}
(symmetric, traceless) is the (unique) Lagrange multiplier for the constraint in (
16
). Moreover,
\mathbf{A}
satisfies the following non-linear equation, a requirement of mean field self-consistency:

\begin{split}\frac{1}{Z(\mathbf{A})}\frac{\partial Z(\mathbf{A})}{\partial\mathbf{A}}:\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{Q}:\mathbf{P},\quad\text{for all }\mathbf{P}\in\mathbf{\Xi}.\end{split}

(37)

Proof.

Step 1.
We show that the minimization problem is well-posed. From Lemma
1
, we know
\mathcal{A}_{\mathbf{Q}}
is non-empty. Moreover, the constraint in (
16
) is clearly convex, so
\mathcal{A}_{\mathbf{Q}}
is a convex set. In addition,
S[\cdot]
is a convex functional on
\mathcal{P}
, because
\rho\ln\rho
is a (strictly) convex function of
\rho
. Hence,
S[\cdot]
is weakly lower semi-continuous on
\mathcal{P}
. So, by standard theory from the calculus of variations, there exists a minimizer
\rho^{*}\in\mathcal{A}_{\mathbf{Q}}
, and it is unique by convexity.

Step 2.
Derive the Euler-Lagrange equations that characterize the minimizer. We will mainly proceed formally, but this can be made more rigorous with similar arguments as in
[
55
, Ch. 8]
. In order to account for the constraint in
\mathcal{A}_{\mathbf{Q}}
, define the
Lagrangian

\begin{split}L\left[\rho,\mathbf{A}\right]&:=S[\rho]+\mathbf{A}:\mathbf{C}\left[\rho\right]=\int_{S^{2}}\rho(\mathbf{p})\ln\rho(\mathbf{p})\,dS(\mathbf{p})+\mathbf{A}:\mathbf{C}\left[\rho\right],\\
\mathbf{C}\left[\rho\right]&:=\mathbf{Q}-\int_{S^{2}}\left(\mathbf{p}\otimes\mathbf{p}-\frac{1}{3}\mathbf{I}\right)\rho(\mathbf{p})\,dS(\mathbf{p})\in\mathbf{\Xi},\end{split}

(38)

where
\mathbf{A}
is a constant matrix in
\mathbf{\Xi}
. In order to account for the other constraints of
\rho
being a probability measure, let us parameterize it:

\begin{split}\rho(\mathbf{p})=\frac{e^{\omega(\mathbf{p})}}{Z(\omega)},\quad\text{where}\quad Z(\omega)=\int_{S^{2}}e^{\omega(\mathbf{p})}\,dS(\mathbf{p}),\end{split}

(39)

where
\omega:S^{2}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
is an “arbitrary” (measurable) function; thus,
\rho
is a probability measure for any
\omega
. We list some perturbation formulas that will be useful later. Let
\omega_{\epsilon}=\omega+\epsilon\eta
, where
\epsilon>0
is small, and
\eta
is an arbitrary measurable function (perturbation). Then, standard variational calculus gives

\begin{split}\delta_{\omega}(e^{\omega})(\eta)&:=\frac{d}{d\epsilon}\Big|_{\epsilon=0}e^{\omega+\epsilon\eta}=\eta e^{\omega},\quad\delta_{\omega}Z(\omega)(\eta)=\int_{S^{2}}\eta(\mathbf{p})e^{\omega(\mathbf{p})}\,dS(\mathbf{p}),\\
\delta_{\omega}\left(\frac{\omega}{Z(\omega)}\right)(\eta)&=\frac{\eta}{Z(\omega)}-\frac{\omega}{(Z(\omega))^{2}}\delta_{\omega}Z(\omega)(\eta)=\frac{1}{Z(\omega)}\left(\eta-\omega\int_{S^{2}}\eta(\mathbf{p})\rho(\mathbf{p})\,dS(\mathbf{p})\right)=\frac{1}{Z(\omega)}\left(\eta-\omega\bar{\eta}\right),\\
\delta_{\omega}\left(\frac{e^{\omega}}{Z(\omega)}\right)(\eta)&=\frac{\eta e^{\omega}}{Z(\omega)}-\frac{e^{\omega}}{(Z(\omega))^{2}}\delta_{\omega}Z(\omega)(\eta)=\rho(\mathbf{p})\left(\eta(\mathbf{p})-\bar{\eta}\right),\end{split}

(40)

where
\bar{\eta}=\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[\eta]
(the expected value with respect to
\rho
).

Step 3.
Next, we derive the KKT conditions for the optimal solution of the problem, in terms of
\omega
. So, we instead form the Lagrangian (
38
) in terms of
\omega
:
L\left[\omega,\mathbf{A}\right]:=S[\omega]+\mathbf{A}:\mathbf{C}\left[\omega\right]
. Computing the variation of the entropy, we have

\begin{split}\delta_{\omega}S[\omega](\eta)&=\int_{S^{2}}\delta_{\omega}\left(\frac{e^{\omega}}{Z(\omega)}\right)(\eta)\left[\omega(\mathbf{p})-\ln Z(\omega)\right]\,dS(\mathbf{p})+\int_{S^{2}}\rho(\mathbf{p})\left[\eta(\mathbf{p})-\frac{1}{Z(\omega)}\delta_{\omega}Z(\omega)(\eta)\right]\,dS(\mathbf{p})\\
&=\int_{S^{2}}\rho(\mathbf{p})\left(\eta(\mathbf{p})-\bar{\eta}\right)\left[\omega(\mathbf{p})+1-\ln Z(\omega)\right]\,dS(\mathbf{p})=\int_{S^{2}}\rho(\mathbf{p})\left(\eta(\mathbf{p})-\bar{\eta}\right)\omega(\mathbf{p})\,dS(\mathbf{p}),\end{split}

(41)

where the last equality is because of the definition of
\bar{\eta}
and the fact that
+1-\ln Z(\omega)
is a constant. Note that
\rho
satisfies (
39
).

Next, note that

\begin{split}\mathbf{A}:\mathbf{C}\left[\omega\right]=\mathbf{A}:\mathbf{Q}-\int_{S^{2}}\mathbf{p}^{T}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{p}\frac{e^{\omega(\mathbf{p})}}{Z(\omega)}\,dS(\mathbf{p}).\end{split}

(42)

Hence, the first variation of the constraint gives

\begin{split}\delta_{\omega}(\mathbf{A}:\mathbf{C}\left[\omega\right])(\eta)&=-\int_{S^{2}}\mathbf{p}^{T}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{p}\,\delta_{\omega}\left(\frac{e^{\omega}}{Z(\omega)}\right)(\eta)\,dS(\mathbf{p})=-\int_{S^{2}}\mathbf{p}^{T}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{p}\,\rho(\mathbf{p})\left(\eta(\mathbf{p})-\bar{\eta}\right)\,dS(\mathbf{p}).\end{split}

(43)

The first KKT condition is given by
\delta_{\omega}L\left[\omega,\mathbf{A}\right](\eta)=0
, for all admissible
\eta
. Therefore, by the above calculations, we obtain

\begin{split}0&=\delta_{\omega}L\left[\omega,\mathbf{A}\right](\eta)=\int_{S^{2}}\left[\omega(\mathbf{p})-\mathbf{p}^{T}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{p}\right]\rho(\mathbf{p})\left(\eta(\mathbf{p})-\bar{\eta}\right)\,dS(\mathbf{p}),\end{split}

(44)

for all admissible
\eta
. This implies that
\omega(\mathbf{p})-\mathbf{p}^{T}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{p}=c
, where
c
is any constant. So, from (
39
), we find

\begin{split}\rho(\mathbf{p})=e^{c}\frac{\exp\left(\mathbf{p}^{T}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{p}\right)}{Z(\omega)}=\frac{\exp\left(\mathbf{p}^{T}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{p}\right)}{Z(\mathbf{A})},\end{split}

(45)

where the
e^{c}
term cancels out. Since the minimizer is unique, we have proven (
36
). The second KKT condition simply recovers the constraint
\mathbf{0}=\partial L\left[\omega,\mathbf{A}\right]/\partial\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{C}\left[\omega\right]
.

Step 4.
Finally, the last step in the inversion is an equation to determine
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbf{\Xi}
. Starting from the relation
Z(\mathbf{A})=\int_{S^{2}}\exp\left(\mathbf{p}^{T}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{p}\right)\,dS(\mathbf{p})
, we first differentiate with respect
\mathbf{A}
but in the direction of general symmetric matrices, not necessarily trace free:

\begin{split}\frac{\partial Z(\mathbf{A})}{\partial\mathbf{A}}&=\int_{S^{2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{A}}\left(\mathbf{p}^{T}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{p}\right)\exp\left(\mathbf{p}^{T}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{p}\right)\,dS(\mathbf{p})=\int_{S^{2}}\left(\mathbf{p}\otimes\mathbf{p}\right)\exp\left(\mathbf{p}^{T}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{p}\right)\,dS(\mathbf{p})\\
&=\int_{S^{2}}\left(\mathbf{p}\otimes\mathbf{p}-\frac{1}{3}\mathbf{I}\right)\exp\left(\mathbf{p}^{T}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{p}\right)\,dS(\mathbf{p})+\frac{1}{3}\mathbf{I}\int_{S^{2}}\exp\left(\mathbf{p}^{T}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{p}\right)\,dS(\mathbf{p})\\
&=Z(\mathbf{A})\left[\int_{S^{2}}\left(\mathbf{p}\otimes\mathbf{p}-\frac{1}{3}\mathbf{I}\right)\rho(\mathbf{p})\,dS(\mathbf{p})+\frac{1}{3}\mathbf{I}\right]=Z(\mathbf{A})\left[\mathbf{Q}+\frac{1}{3}\mathbf{I}\right],\end{split}

(46)

where
\rho
satisfies (
45
). Thus, the multiplier
\mathbf{A}
satisfies the following equation

\begin{split}\frac{1}{Z(\mathbf{A})}\frac{\partial Z(\mathbf{A})}{\partial\mathbf{A}}&=\mathbf{Q}+\frac{1}{3}\mathbf{I}.\end{split}

(47)

Dotting (
47
) with an arbitrary “test” function in
\mathbf{\Xi}
, we get (
37
).
∎

We will also make use of these results for
f
. Note that the partition function (
39
) is a single particle partition function obtained by integration over
\mathbf{p}\in S^{2}
, but with
specified
values of the Lagrange multiplier
\mathbf{A}
. The fact that it simply involves a quadratic form of
\mathbf{p}
originates from the form of the constrain (
8
). In the mean field approximation considered, given
\mathbf{Q}
there is a unique
\mathbf{A}
, defined by (
47
), so that the corresponding
\rho
in (
39
) gives as average precisely
\mathbf{Q}
.

The following result illustrates additional properties of
f(\mathbf{Q})
,
including the simultaneous diagonalization of
\mathbf{Q}
and
\mathbf{A}
, which can be
useful for numerical implementation purposes.

Corollary 3
.

The function
f(\mathbf{Q})
is a strictly convex function of
\mathbf{Q}
. In addition, any
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbf{\Xi}
, and the corresponding unique
\mathbf{A}
coming from solving the constrained minimization problem in Theorem
2
, are diagonalized by the
same
orthogonal matrix
\mathbf{R}
, i.e.

\mathbf{\Lambda}=\mathbf{R}^{T}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{R},\quad\mathbf{\Pi}=\mathbf{R}^{T}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{R},

(48)

where
\mathbf{\Lambda}=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3})
and
\mathbf{\Pi}=\mathrm{diag}(\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\pi_{3})
, where
\{\pi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{3}
are the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{A}
.

Moreover, the Lagrange multiplier
\mathbf{A}
can be characterized as the optimal solution of the dual problem. In other words, define

W\left(\mathbf{A}\right):=\ln\left(Z(\mathbf{A})\right)-\mathbf{Q}:\mathbf{A},

(49)

where
W\left(\cdot\right):\mathbf{\Xi}\to\mathbb{R}
is a strictly convex function (but not uniformly strictly convex). Then, the optimal Lagrange multiplier
\mathbf{A}
from Theorem
2
is the unique minimizer of (
49
) over the set of symmetric, traceless matrices, i.e. an unconstrained minimization problem.

Proof.

Convexity.
Let
\mathbf{Q}_{0},\mathbf{Q}_{1}\in\mathbf{\Xi}
with eigenvalues satisfying (
10
), and let
\rho_{i}
be the minimizer in (
35
) corresponding to
\mathbf{Q}_{i}
, for
i=0,1
. Set
\mathbf{Q}(t)=\mathbf{Q}_{0}(1-t)+\mathbf{Q}_{1}t
for all
t\in[0,1]
, and also define
\rho(\cdot,t):=\rho_{0}(\cdot)(1-t)+\rho_{1}(\cdot)t
. Then, since
S[\rho]
is a strictly convex functional of
\rho
, for all
0<t<1
we have

\begin{split}f(\mathbf{Q}(t))&=\min_{\rho\in\mathcal{A}_{\mathbf{Q}(t)}}S[\rho]\leq S[\rho(\cdot,t)]<S[\rho_{0}](1-t)+S[\rho_{1}]t\\
&=\left(\min_{\rho\in\mathcal{A}_{\mathbf{Q}_{0}}}S[\rho]\right)(1-t)+\left(\min_{\rho\in\mathcal{A}_{\mathbf{Q}_{1}}}S[\rho]\right)t=f(\mathbf{Q}_{0})(1-t)+f(\mathbf{Q}_{1})t,\end{split}

(50)

which verifies the strict convexity of
f(\cdot)
.

Simultaneous diagonalization.
Given
\mathbf{Q}
, let
\mathbf{A}
and
\rho
be the optimal solution of the constrained minimization problem, and let
\mathbf{R}
be the orthogonal matrix such that
\mathbf{\Pi}=\mathbf{R}^{T}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{R}
where
\mathbf{\Pi}
is a diagonal matrix. Then, using the change of variable
\mathbf{p}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{q}
, we get

\begin{split}Z(\mathbf{A})\mathbf{Q}&=\int_{S^{2}}\left(\mathbf{p}\mathbf{p}^{T}-\frac{1}{3}\mathbf{I}\right)\exp\left(\mathbf{p}^{T}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{p}\right)\,dS(\mathbf{p})=\int_{S^{2}}\left(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{q}\mathbf{q}^{T}\mathbf{R}^{T}-\frac{1}{3}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{R}^{T}\right)\exp\left(\mathbf{q}^{T}\mathbf{R}^{T}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{q}\right)\,dS(\mathbf{q})\\
&=\mathbf{R}\left[\int_{S^{2}}\left(\mathbf{q}\mathbf{q}^{T}-\frac{1}{3}\mathbf{I}\right)\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{3}\pi_{i}q_{i}^{2}\right)\,dS(\mathbf{q})\right]\mathbf{R}^{T},\end{split}

(51)

i.e.

\begin{split}\mathbf{R}^{T}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{R}&=\frac{1}{Z(\mathbf{A})}\int_{S^{2}}\left(\mathbf{q}\mathbf{q}^{T}-\frac{1}{3}\mathbf{I}\right)\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{3}\pi_{i}q_{i}^{2}\right)\,dS(\mathbf{q}).\end{split}

(52)

Now, note that for
i\neq j
, there holds

\begin{split}0=\int_{S^{2}}\underbrace{q_{i}q_{j}}_{\text{odd}}\underbrace{\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{3}\pi_{i}q_{i}^{2}\right)}_{\text{even}}\,dS(\mathbf{q}).\end{split}

(53)

That means
\mathbf{R}^{T}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{R}
must be diagonal. Since matrix diagonalization (with orthogonal matrices) is unique,
\mathbf{R}^{T}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{R}\equiv\mathbf{\Lambda}
.

The dual minimization problem.
Let
\mathbf{A}^{*}
be the optimal Lagrange multiplier from (
37
); it is clear that
\mathbf{A}^{*}
solves the first order condition for (
49
):

0=\frac{\partial W\left(\mathbf{A}\right)}{\partial\mathbf{A}}:\mathbf{P}=\frac{1}{Z(\mathbf{A})}\frac{\partial Z(\mathbf{A})}{\partial\mathbf{A}}:\mathbf{P}-\mathbf{Q}:\mathbf{P},\quad\text{for all }\mathbf{P}\in\mathbf{\Xi}.

(54)

Next, we compute the Hessian of
W\left(\mathbf{A}\right)
for any
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbf{\Xi}
:

\begin{split}\mathbf{T}:\frac{\partial^{2}W\left(\mathbf{A}\right)}{\partial\mathbf{A}^{2}}:\mathbf{P}&=\frac{1}{Z(\mathbf{A})}\mathbf{T}:\frac{\partial^{2}Z(\mathbf{A})}{\partial\mathbf{A}^{2}}:\mathbf{P}-\frac{1}{(Z(\mathbf{A}))^{2}}\left(\frac{\partial Z(\mathbf{A})}{\partial\mathbf{A}}:\mathbf{T}\right)\left(\frac{\partial Z(\mathbf{A})}{\partial\mathbf{A}}:\mathbf{P}\right),\end{split}

(55)

where

\begin{split}\frac{1}{Z(\mathbf{A})}\frac{\partial Z(\mathbf{A})}{\partial\mathbf{A}}:\mathbf{P}&=\int_{S^{2}}\mathbf{p}^{T}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{p}\,\rho(\mathbf{p})\,dS(\mathbf{p})=\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[\mathbf{p}^{T}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{p}],\\
\frac{1}{Z(\mathbf{A})}\mathbf{T}:\frac{\partial^{2}Z(\mathbf{A})}{\partial\mathbf{A}^{2}}:\mathbf{P}&=\int_{S^{2}}\left(\mathbf{p}^{T}\mathbf{T}\mathbf{p}\right)\left(\mathbf{p}^{T}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{p}\right)\rho(\mathbf{p})\,dS(\mathbf{p})=\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[\left(\mathbf{p}^{T}\mathbf{T}\mathbf{p}\right)\left(\mathbf{p}^{T}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{p}\right)],\end{split}

(56)

for all
\mathbf{P},\mathbf{T}\in\mathbf{\Xi}
; note that
\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[\cdot]
is the expected value with respect to
\rho
, which is determined by
\mathbf{A}
.
To show strict convexity, we must verify that
\partial_{\mathbf{A}}^{2}W\left(\mathbf{A}\right)
is positive definite for all
\mathbf{A}
:

\begin{split}\mathbf{P}:\frac{\partial^{2}W\left(\mathbf{A}\right)}{\partial\mathbf{A}^{2}}:\mathbf{P}&=\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[\left(\mathbf{p}^{T}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{p}\right)^{2}]-\left(\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[\mathbf{p}^{T}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{p}]\right)^{2}=\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[\left(\mathbf{p}^{T}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{p}-\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[\mathbf{p}^{T}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{p}]\right)^{2}],\end{split}

(57)

which is the covariance of
\mathbf{p}^{T}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{p}
with respect to
\rho
(which depends on
\mathbf{A}
) and is always positive semi-definite. If (
57
) were identically zero, then that would imply that some marginal distribution of
\rho
is a Dirac delta. But this is not possible given the form of
\rho
in (
45
) so long as
\mathbf{A}
is finite. Therefore,
\frac{\partial^{2}W\left(\mathbf{A}\right)}{\partial\mathbf{A}^{2}}
is positive definite for all
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbf{\Xi}
such that
|\mathbf{A}|<\infty
. This means that
W\left(\cdot\right)
is strictly convex.
∎

4
Minimizing the Landau-de Gennes Energy

The free energy minimization of the self consistent free energy (
18
) shares many elements with minimization procedures of the Landau-de Gennes free energy. We summarize known results concerning the later here, and emphasize the differences with the proposed method.

4.1
Existence of a Minimizer

The admissible space for
\mathbf{Q}
when seeking a minimizer is

\begin{split}\mathbb{V}(\mathbf{P}):=\left\{\mathbf{Q}\in H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbf{\Xi})\mid\mathbf{Q}|_{\Gamma_{D}}=\mathbf{P}\right\},\end{split}

(58)

where
\mathbf{P}\in H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbf{\Xi})
. Note that
\mathbf{\Xi}
is spanned by a set of five orthonormal basis matrices
\{\mathbf{E}^{k}\}_{k=1}^{5}
. The set
\Gamma_{D}\subset\Gamma
is where strong anchoring is imposed, i.e.
\mathbf{Q}|_{\Gamma}=\mathbf{Q}_{D}\in H^{1}(\Gamma;\mathbf{\Xi})
, where
\psi(\mathbf{Q}_{D}(\mathbf{x}))<\infty
for all
\mathbf{x}\in\Gamma
.
The weak anchoring function
\mathbf{Q}_{\Gamma}
is taken in
L^{2}(\Gamma;\mathbf{\Xi})
, with
\psi(\mathbf{Q}_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{x}))<\infty
for all
\mathbf{x}\in\Gamma
.
The minimization problem for the LdG energy functional (
1
) is

\begin{split}\min_{\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{V}(\mathbf{Q}_{D})}E[\mathbf{Q}],\end{split}

(59)

Existence of a minimizer requires the energy to be bounded from below. The following theorem
[
36
, Lem. 4.1]
establishes this result for the
L_{1}
,
L_{2}
, and
L_{3}
terms only.

Theorem 4
.

Let
a\left(\cdot,\cdot\right):H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbf{\Xi})\times H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbf{\Xi})\to\mathbb{R}
be the symmetric bilinear form defined by

\begin{split}a\left(\mathbf{P},\mathbf{T}\right)=\int_{\Omega}L_{1}\nabla\mathbf{P}\mathrel{\hbox{\hskip 1.3889pt\hskip-1.3889pt\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 1.38889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\hbox{$\cdot$}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\raisebox{2.44444pt}{\hbox{$\cdot$}}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip 1.38889pt}}\hskip-1.3889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\raisebox{-2.44444pt}{\hbox{$\cdot$}}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip 1.3889pt}}\nabla\mathbf{T}+L_{2}(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{P})\cdot(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{T})+L_{3}(\nabla\mathbf{P})^{T}\mathrel{\hbox{\hskip 1.3889pt\hskip-1.3889pt\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 1.38889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\hbox{$\cdot$}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\raisebox{2.44444pt}{\hbox{$\cdot$}}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip 1.38889pt}}\hskip-1.3889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\raisebox{-2.44444pt}{\hbox{$\cdot$}}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip 1.3889pt}}\nabla\mathbf{T}\,d\mathbf{x}.\end{split}

(60)

Then
a\left(\cdot,\cdot\right)
is bounded. If
L_{1}
,
L_{2}
,
L_{3}
satisfy

\begin{split}0<L_{1},\quad-L_{1}<L_{3}<2L_{1},\quad-\frac{3}{5}L_{1}-\frac{1}{10}L_{3}<L_{2},\end{split}

(61)

then there is a constant
C>0
such that
a\left(\mathbf{P},\mathbf{P}\right)\geq C|\mathbf{P}|^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}
for all
\mathbf{P}\in H^{1}(\Omega)
. Moreover, if
|\Gamma_{D}|>0
, then there is a constant
C^{\prime}>0

a\left(\mathbf{P},\mathbf{P}\right)\geq C^{\prime}\|\mathbf{P}\|^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}

\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{V}(\mathbf{0})

We also have the bilinear form
b\left(\cdot,\cdot\right):H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbf{\Xi})\times H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbf{\Xi})\to\mathbb{R}
and trilinear form
c\left(\cdot,\cdot;\cdot\right):H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbf{\Xi})\times H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbf{\Xi})\times H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbf{\Xi})\to\mathbb{R}

\begin{split}b\left(\mathbf{P},\mathbf{T}\right)=\frac{L_{4}}{2}\int_{\Omega}\nabla\mathbf{P}\mathrel{\hbox{\hskip 1.3889pt\hskip-1.3889pt\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 1.38889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\hbox{$\cdot$}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\raisebox{2.44444pt}{\hbox{$\cdot$}}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip 1.38889pt}}\hskip-1.3889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\raisebox{-2.44444pt}{\hbox{$\cdot$}}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip 1.3889pt}}(\mathbf{e}\cdot\mathbf{T})+\nabla\mathbf{T}\mathrel{\hbox{\hskip 1.3889pt\hskip-1.3889pt\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 1.38889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\hbox{$\cdot$}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\raisebox{2.44444pt}{\hbox{$\cdot$}}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip 1.38889pt}}\hskip-1.3889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\raisebox{-2.44444pt}{\hbox{$\cdot$}}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip 1.3889pt}}(\mathbf{e}\cdot\mathbf{P})\,d\mathbf{x},\end{split}

(62)

\begin{split}c\left(\mathbf{T},\mathbf{P};\mathbf{Q}\right)=\frac{L_{*}}{2}\int_{\Omega}\Big\{&\nabla\mathbf{P}\mathrel{\hbox{\hskip 1.3889pt\hskip-1.3889pt\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 1.38889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\hbox{$\cdot$}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\raisebox{2.44444pt}{\hbox{$\cdot$}}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip 1.38889pt}}\hskip-1.3889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\raisebox{-2.44444pt}{\hbox{$\cdot$}}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip 1.3889pt}}[(\mathbf{T}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{Q}]+\nabla\mathbf{P}\mathrel{\hbox{\hskip 1.3889pt\hskip-1.3889pt\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 1.38889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\hbox{$\cdot$}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\raisebox{2.44444pt}{\hbox{$\cdot$}}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip 1.38889pt}}\hskip-1.3889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\raisebox{-2.44444pt}{\hbox{$\cdot$}}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip 1.3889pt}}[(\mathbf{Q}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{T}]\\
+&\nabla\mathbf{T}\mathrel{\hbox{\hskip 1.3889pt\hskip-1.3889pt\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 1.38889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\hbox{$\cdot$}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\raisebox{2.44444pt}{\hbox{$\cdot$}}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip 1.38889pt}}\hskip-1.3889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\raisebox{-2.44444pt}{\hbox{$\cdot$}}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip 1.3889pt}}[(\mathbf{P}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{Q}]+\nabla\mathbf{T}\mathrel{\hbox{\hskip 1.3889pt\hskip-1.3889pt\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 1.38889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\hbox{$\cdot$}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\raisebox{2.44444pt}{\hbox{$\cdot$}}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip 1.38889pt}}\hskip-1.3889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\raisebox{-2.44444pt}{\hbox{$\cdot$}}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip 1.3889pt}}[(\mathbf{Q}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{P}]\\
+&\nabla\mathbf{Q}\mathrel{\hbox{\hskip 1.3889pt\hskip-1.3889pt\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 1.38889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\hbox{$\cdot$}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\raisebox{2.44444pt}{\hbox{$\cdot$}}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip 1.38889pt}}\hskip-1.3889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\raisebox{-2.44444pt}{\hbox{$\cdot$}}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip 1.3889pt}}[(\mathbf{P}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{T}]+\nabla\mathbf{Q}\mathrel{\hbox{\hskip 1.3889pt\hskip-1.3889pt\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 1.38889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\hbox{$\cdot$}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\raisebox{2.44444pt}{\hbox{$\cdot$}}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip 1.38889pt}}\hskip-1.3889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\raisebox{-2.44444pt}{\hbox{$\cdot$}}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip 1.3889pt}}[(\mathbf{T}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{P}]\Big\}d\mathbf{x}.\end{split}

(63)

Next, consider the following sub-part of the energy (
1
):

\begin{split}\widetilde{E}[\mathbf{Q}]&:=\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{W}(\mathbf{Q},\nabla\mathbf{Q})\,d\mathbf{x}+\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\psi(\mathbf{Q})\,d\mathbf{x},\\
&\equiv\frac{1}{2}a\left(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{Q}\right)+\frac{1}{2}b\left(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{Q}\right)+\frac{1}{6}c\left(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{Q};\mathbf{Q}\right)+\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\psi(\mathbf{Q})\,d\mathbf{x}.\end{split}

(64)

Combining Theorem
4
with the form of the energy in
(
1
) and other basic results (see

[
36
, Lem. 4.2, Thm. 4.3]
for instance) we arrive at the
following result.

Theorem 5

(existence of a minimizer)
.

Suppose that
\mathbf{Q}_{D}
and
\Gamma_{D}
are defined as above and that
\chi
is a bounded linear functional on
\mathbb{V}(\mathbf{Q}_{D})
.
Let
\widetilde{E}
be given by (
64
), where
\psi
is given by (
18
). Furthermore, assume
T>0
, let
L_{4}
be bounded, and assume that

\widetilde{L}_{1}:=L_{1}-\max\left(\frac{L_{*}}{3},-\frac{3}{2}L_{*}\right),

(65)

and
\widetilde{L}_{1}
,
L_{2}
,
L_{3}
satisfy (
61
) with
L_{1}
replaced by
\widetilde{L}_{1}
.
Then
\widetilde{E}
has a minimizer in the space
\mathbb{V}(\mathbf{Q}_{D})
, whose eigenvalues are
strictly within
the physical limits (
10
) almost everywhere.
Furthermore,
E
in (
1
), with
\psi
given by (
18
), has a minimizer in
\mathbb{V}(\mathbf{Q}_{D})
, whose eigenvalues are
strictly within
the physical limits (
10
) almost everywhere.

Proof.

When
L_{4}=L_{*}=0
, the result follows from
[
36
, Lem. 4.2, Thm. 4.3]
. Otherwise, consider the case where
L_{*}
is positive (the negative case is similar). Consider the constrained admissible set

\mathcal{A}:=\{\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{V}(\mathbf{Q}_{D})~|-1/3\leq\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{Q})\leq 2/3,\text{ for }i=1,2,3\},

(66)

and note that
\mathcal{A}
is a closed, convex set. Since the minimum eigenvalue of
L_{1}\mathbf{I}+L_{*}\mathbf{Q}
(on
\mathcal{A}
) is
\widetilde{L}_{1}
, using Theorem
4
, we have that
\widetilde{E}
satisfies the bound

\begin{split}\widetilde{E}[\mathbf{Q}]&\geq\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\widetilde{L}_{1}|\nabla\mathbf{Q}|^{2}+L_{2}(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{Q})^{2}+L_{3}(\nabla\mathbf{Q})^{T}\mathrel{\hbox{\hskip 1.3889pt\hskip-1.3889pt\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 1.38889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\hbox{$\cdot$}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\raisebox{2.44444pt}{\hbox{$\cdot$}}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip 1.38889pt}}\hskip-1.3889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\raisebox{-2.44444pt}{\hbox{$\cdot$}}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip 1.3889pt}}\nabla\mathbf{Q}\,d\mathbf{x}\\
&+\frac{L_{4}}{2}\int_{\Omega}\nabla\mathbf{Q}\mathrel{\hbox{\hskip 1.3889pt\hskip-1.3889pt\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 1.38889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\hbox{$\cdot$}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\raisebox{2.44444pt}{\hbox{$\cdot$}}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip 1.38889pt}}\hskip-1.3889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\raisebox{-2.44444pt}{\hbox{$\cdot$}}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip 1.3889pt}}(\mathbf{e}\cdot\mathbf{Q})\,d\mathbf{x}+\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\psi(\mathbf{Q})\,d\mathbf{x}\\
&\geq\frac{C}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\mathbf{Q}|^{2}\,d\mathbf{x}+\frac{L_{4}}{2}\int_{\Omega}\nabla\mathbf{Q}\mathrel{\hbox{\hskip 1.3889pt\hskip-1.3889pt\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 1.38889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\hbox{$\cdot$}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\raisebox{2.44444pt}{\hbox{$\cdot$}}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip 1.38889pt}}\hskip-1.3889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\raisebox{-2.44444pt}{\hbox{$\cdot$}}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip 1.3889pt}}(\mathbf{e}\cdot\mathbf{Q})\,d\mathbf{x}+\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\psi(\mathbf{Q})\,d\mathbf{x},\end{split}

(67)

for all
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathcal{A}
, for some constant
C>0
. Furthermore, one can show

\begin{split}\widetilde{E}[\mathbf{Q}]&\geq\frac{1}{2}\left(C-\zeta_{0}\right)\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\mathbf{Q}|^{2}\,d\mathbf{x}-\frac{C^{\prime}}{\zeta_{0}}\int_{\Omega}|\mathbf{Q}|^{2}\,d\mathbf{x}+\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\psi(\mathbf{Q})\,d\mathbf{x},\end{split}

(68)

for any
\zeta_{0}>0
where
C^{\prime}>0
is some bounded constant. Choosing
\zeta_{0}=C/2
, we get

\begin{split}\widetilde{E}[\mathbf{Q}]&\geq\frac{C}{4}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\mathbf{Q}|^{2}\,d\mathbf{x}+\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\widetilde{\psi}(\mathbf{Q})\,d\mathbf{x},\end{split}

(69)

where
\widetilde{\psi}(\mathbf{Q}):=Tf(\mathbf{Q})-(\kappa+2\epsilon^{2}C^{\prime}/C)|\mathbf{Q}|^{2}
. Thus,
\widetilde{E}[\mathbf{Q}]
is clearly bounded below by a coercive energy on
\mathcal{A}
. By standard calculus of variations
[
56
,
57
]
, there exists a minimizer,
\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}
, of
\widetilde{E}[\cdot]
in
\mathcal{A}
. Moreover,
f(\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}})<\infty
almost everywhere, meaning the eigenvalues of
\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}
are strictly within the physical limits almost everywhere. The same holds true for
E[\cdot]
.
∎

4.2
Gradient Flow

We look for an energy minimizer using a gradient flow strategy
[
40
,
37
,
41
,
35
]
applied to the energy (
1
).
Let
t
represent “time” and suppose that
\mathbf{Q}\equiv\mathbf{Q}(\mathbf{x},t)
satisfies an evolution equation such that
\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathbf{Q}(\cdot,t)=:\mathbf{Q}_{*}
is a local minimizer of
E
, where
\mathbf{Q}(\mathbf{x},0)=\mathbf{Q}_{0}
, and
\mathbf{Q}_{0}\in\mathbb{V}(\mathbf{Q}_{D})
is the initial guess for the minimizer. The tensor
\mathbf{Q}(\cdot,t)
evolves according to the following
L^{2}(\Omega)
gradient flow:

\begin{split}\left(\partial_{t}\mathbf{Q}(\cdot,t),\mathbf{P}\right)=-\delta_{\mathbf{Q}}E[\mathbf{Q};\mathbf{P}],\quad\forall\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{V}(\mathbf{0}),\end{split}

(70)

where
\left(\cdot,\cdot\right)
is the
L^{2}
inner product over
\Omega
. Formally, the solution of (
70
) will converge to
\mathbf{Q}_{*}
.

Remark 6
.

If
\psi
is the Landau-de Gennes bulk potential in (
7
), then (
70
) is essentially a tensor-valued Allen-Cahn equation. By the standard theory of parabolic PDEs
[
58
,
59
]
, it has a unique solution. The same result also holds when
\psi
is the singular bulk potential.

We use a numerical scheme for approximating (
70
) by first discretizing in time by minimizing movements
[
60
]
. Let
\mathbf{Q}_{k}(\mathbf{x})\approx\mathbf{Q}(\mathbf{x},k\delta t)
, where
\delta t>0
is a finite time-step, and
k
is the time index. Then (
70
) becomes a sequence of variational problems. Given
\mathbf{Q}_{k}
, find
\mathbf{Q}_{k+1}\in\mathbb{V}(\mathbf{Q}_{D})
such that

\begin{split}\left(\frac{\mathbf{Q}_{k+1}-\mathbf{Q}_{k}}{\delta t},\mathbf{P}\right)=-\delta_{\mathbf{Q}}E[\mathbf{Q}_{k+1};\mathbf{P}],\quad\forall\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{V}(\mathbf{0}),\end{split}

(71)

which is equivalent to

\begin{split}\mathbf{Q}_{k+1}=\argmin_{\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{V}(\mathbf{Q}_{D})}F(\mathbf{Q}),\quad F(\mathbf{Q}):=\frac{1}{2\delta t}\|\mathbf{Q}-\mathbf{Q}_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+E[\mathbf{Q}],\end{split}

(72)

and yields the useful property
F(\mathbf{Q}_{k+1})\leq F(\mathbf{Q}_{k})
. However, (
71
) is a
fully-implicit
equation and requires an iterative solution because of the non-linearities in
\mathcal{W}(\mathbf{Q},\nabla\mathbf{Q})
and
\psi(\mathbf{Q})
. As in the Landau-de Gennes case,
\psi(\mathbf{Q})
is non-convex
[
42
]
, so that we adopt a
convex splitting
method
[
61
,
41
,
62
]
. Setting
\psi_{c}(\mathbf{Q})=Tf(\mathbf{Q})
and
\psi_{e}(\mathbf{Q})=\kappa|\mathbf{Q}|^{2}
, we see that (
18
) already has the form of a convex splitting:

\begin{split}\psi(\mathbf{Q})&\equiv\psi_{c}(\mathbf{Q})-\psi_{e}(\mathbf{Q}),\end{split}

(73)

i.e.
\psi_{c}
and
\psi_{e}
are convex functions of
\mathbf{Q}
.

In computing (
71
), we treat
\psi_{c}
implicitly and
\psi_{e}
explicitly. Therefore, (
71
) becomes the following. Given
\mathbf{Q}_{k}
, find
\mathbf{Q}_{k+1}\in\mathbb{V}(\mathbf{Q}_{D})
such that

\begin{split}&\left(\frac{\mathbf{Q}_{k+1}-\mathbf{Q}_{k}}{\delta t},\mathbf{P}\right)+a\left(\mathbf{Q}_{k+1},\mathbf{P}\right)+b\left(\mathbf{Q}_{k+1},\mathbf{P}\right)+c\left(\mathbf{Q}_{k+1},\mathbf{P};\mathbf{Q}_{k+1}\right)\\
&\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\partial\psi_{c}(\mathbf{Q}_{k+1})}{\partial\mathbf{Q}}:\mathbf{P}\,d\mathbf{x}+\eta_{\Gamma}\int_{\Gamma}\frac{\partial f_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{Q}_{k+1})}{\partial\mathbf{Q}}:\mathbf{P}\,dS(\mathbf{x})\\
&=\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\partial\psi_{e}(\mathbf{Q}_{k})}{\partial\mathbf{Q}}:\mathbf{P}\,d\mathbf{x}+\int_{\Omega}\frac{\partial\chi(\mathbf{Q}_{k})}{\partial\mathbf{Q}}:\mathbf{P}\,d\mathbf{x},\quad\forall\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{V}(\mathbf{0}),\end{split}

(74)

where the right-hand-side of (
74
) is completely explicit. We then apply Newton’s method to solving (
74
).

Next, we approximate (
74
) by a finite element method, so we introduce some basic notation and assumptions in that regard. We assume that
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
is discretized by a conforming shape regular triangulation
\mathcal{T}_{h}=\{T_{i}\}
consisting of simplices, i.e. we define
\Omega_{h}:=\cup_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}T
. Furthermore, we define the space of continuous piecewise linear functions on
\Omega_{h}
:

\begin{split}\mathbb{M}_{h}(\Omega_{h}):=\left\{v\in C^{0}(\Omega_{h})\mid v|_{T}\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(T),~\forall T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\right\},\end{split}

(75)

where
\mathcal{P}_{k}(T)
is the space of polynomials of degree
\leq k
on
T
.

We discretize (
74
) by a
\mathcal{P}_{1}
approximation of the
\mathbf{Q}
variable denoted
\mathbf{Q}_{h}
. To this end, define

\begin{split}\mathbb{S}_{h}(\Omega_{h}):=\left\{\mathbf{P}\in C^{0}(\Omega_{h};\mathbf{\Xi})\mid\mathbf{P}=\sum_{i=1}^{5}q_{i,h}\mathbf{E}^{i},~q_{i,h}\in\mathbb{M}_{h}(\Omega_{h}),1\leq i\leq 5\right\},\end{split}

(76)

and let
\mathbf{Q}_{h}\in\mathbb{S}_{h}(\Omega_{h})
. Thus,
\mathbf{Q}_{h}=\sum_{i=1}^{5}q_{i,h}\mathbf{E}^{i}
, and
\mathbf{Q}_{h}\in H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbf{\Xi})
.

The fully discrete
L^{2}
-gradient flow now follows from (
74
), which we explicitly state. Given
\mathbf{Q}_{h,k}
, find
\mathbf{Q}_{h,k+1}\in\mathbb{S}_{h}(\Omega_{h})\cap\mathbb{V}(\mathcal{I}_{h}\mathbf{Q}_{D})
, where
\mathcal{I}_{h}
denotes the Lagrange interpolation operator, such that

\begin{split}&\left(\frac{\mathbf{Q}_{h,k+1}-\mathbf{Q}_{h,k}}{\delta t},\mathbf{P}\right)+a\left(\mathbf{Q}_{h,k+1},\mathbf{P}\right)+b\left(\mathbf{Q}_{h,k+1},\mathbf{P}\right)+c\left(\mathbf{Q}_{h,k+1},\mathbf{P};\mathbf{Q}_{h,k+1}\right)\\
&\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{I}_{h}\left(\frac{\partial\psi_{c}(\mathbf{Q}_{h,k+1})}{\partial\mathbf{Q}}\right):\mathbf{P}\,d\mathbf{x}+\eta_{\Gamma}\int_{\Gamma}\frac{\partial f_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{Q}_{h,k+1})}{\partial\mathbf{Q}}:\mathbf{P}\,dS(\mathbf{x})\\
&=\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\partial\psi_{e}(\mathbf{Q}_{h,k})}{\partial\mathbf{Q}}:\mathbf{P}\,d\mathbf{x}+\int_{\Omega}\frac{\partial\chi(\mathbf{Q}_{h,k})}{\partial\mathbf{Q}}:\mathbf{P}\,d\mathbf{x},\quad\forall\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{S}_{h}(\Omega_{h})\cap\mathbb{V}(\mathbf{0}).\end{split}

(77)

We iterate this procedure until some stopping criteria is achieved. As is the case with the Landau-de Gennes model, solving (
77
) at each time-step requires Newton’s method, i.e. we must compute the gradient and Hessian of the energy. In particular, we need to compute the gradient and Hessian of the singular bulk potential
\psi_{c}\equiv Tf
, as we detail in Section
5
.

5
Evaluating the Singular Bulk Potential

As in other self consistent mean field theories, the main difficulty with the
method is that
no explicit formula
for
f(\mathbf{Q})
is available.
Instead, one has to solve the
mean field self-consistency equation (
37
) numerically
for a given
\mathbf{Q}
. In the application of Newton’s method to the solution of
(
77
), we must compute
\partial\psi_{c}(\mathbf{Q})/\partial\mathbf{Q}
and
\partial^{2}\psi_{c}(\mathbf{Q})/\partial\mathbf{Q}^{2}
, evaluated at the
current guess of the solution
\mathbf{Q}_{h,k+1}
, where
\psi_{c}\equiv Tf
.
Since
\mathbf{Q}_{h,k+1}=\mathbf{Q}_{h,k+1}(\mathbf{x})
is spatially varying, this can
potentially be very expensive to compute. However, note the presence of the
Lagrange interpolation operator
\mathcal{I}_{h}
in
(
77
), i.e. see the term
\mathcal{I}_{h}\left(\partial\psi_{c}(\mathbf{Q}_{h,k+1})/\partial\mathbf{Q}\right)
. Hence,
f
, and
its derivatives, need only be evaluated at the finite element degrees-of-freedom
(or nodes) of the mesh. Moreover, the computation at each node is completely
independent of all other nodes, i.e. it is
embarrassingly parallel
.
Therefore, the numerical implementation of the singular bulk potential is
completely tractable.

There are two main steps involved in the determination of the bulk potential
f(\mathbf{Q})
and its derivatives: the calculation of the single particle partition function (
36
), and the solution of the mean field self consistency equation (
37
). We establish here some of the properties necessary for their calculation.

5.1
Differentiability

We require the gradient and Hessian of
f(\mathbf{Q})
in solving (
77
) via Newton’s method.

Proposition 7
.

Given
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbf{\Xi}
with eigenvalues that satisfy
-1/3<\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{Q})<2/3
, let
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbf{\Xi}
be the unique minimizer of (
49
). Then, there holds

\begin{split}f(\mathbf{Q})&=\mathbf{Q}:\mathbf{A}-\ln Z(\mathbf{A})=-W\left(\mathbf{A}\right),\\
\frac{\partial f(\mathbf{Q})}{\partial\mathbf{Q}}:\mathbf{P}&=\mathbf{A}:\mathbf{P},\quad\text{for all }\mathbf{P}\in\mathbf{\Xi},\\
\mathbf{T}:\frac{\partial^{2}f(\mathbf{Q})}{\partial\mathbf{Q}^{2}}:\mathbf{P}&=\left(\frac{\partial\mathbf{A}}{\partial\mathbf{Q}}:\mathbf{T}\right):\mathbf{P},\quad\text{for all }\mathbf{P},\mathbf{T}\in\mathbf{\Xi},\end{split}

(78)

where
\partial\mathbf{A}/\partial\mathbf{Q}:\mathbf{T}
is the unique solution of the linear system

\begin{split}\left(\frac{\partial\mathbf{A}}{\partial\mathbf{Q}}:\mathbf{T}\right):\frac{\partial^{2}W\left(\mathbf{A}\right)}{\partial\mathbf{A}^{2}}:\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{T}:\mathbf{P},\quad\text{for all }\mathbf{P}\in\mathbf{\Xi},\end{split}

(79)

for any
\mathbf{T}\in\mathbf{\Xi}
, where
\partial^{2}W\left(\mathbf{A}\right)/\partial\mathbf{A}^{2}
is the constant 4-tensor evaluated at
\mathbf{A}
.

Proof.

Let
\rho
be the probability distribution given by (
36
). Then, by (
35
),

\begin{split}f(\mathbf{Q})&=\int_{S^{2}}\rho(\mathbf{p})\ln\rho(\mathbf{p})\,dS(\mathbf{p})=\int_{S^{2}}\frac{\exp\left(\mathbf{p}^{T}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{p}\right)}{Z(\mathbf{A})}\left(\mathbf{p}^{T}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{p}-\ln Z(\mathbf{A})\right)\,dS(\mathbf{p}),\\
&=\mathbf{A}:\int_{S^{2}}\left(\mathbf{p}\otimes\mathbf{p}-\frac{1}{3}\mathbf{I}\right)\rho(\mathbf{p})\,dS(\mathbf{p})-\ln Z(\mathbf{A})=\mathbf{Q}:\mathbf{A}-\ln Z(\mathbf{A})=-W\left(\mathbf{A}\right),\end{split}

(80)

where we used (
8
) and (
49
). Next, using (
80
),

\begin{split}\frac{\partial f(\mathbf{Q})}{\partial\mathbf{Q}}:\mathbf{P}&=\mathbf{P}:\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{Q}:\mathbf{A}^{\prime}-\frac{1}{Z(\mathbf{A})}\frac{\partial Z(\mathbf{A})}{\partial\mathbf{A}}:\mathbf{A}^{\prime}=\mathbf{P}:\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{Q}:\mathbf{A}^{\prime}-\left(\mathbf{Q}+\frac{1}{3}\mathbf{I}\right):\mathbf{A}^{\prime}\\
&=\mathbf{P}:\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{Q}:\mathbf{A}^{\prime}-\mathbf{Q}:\mathbf{A}^{\prime}=\mathbf{A}:\mathbf{P},\end{split}

(81)

where
\mathbf{\Xi}\ni\mathbf{A}^{\prime}\equiv(\partial\mathbf{A}/\partial\mathbf{Q}):\mathbf{P}
and we used (
47
).

By differentiating (
81
), we clearly get the last line of (
78
). Thus, we need a characterization of
\partial\mathbf{A}/\partial\mathbf{Q}
. Recall that
\mathbf{A}
is the unique minimizer of (
49
), i.e.
\mathbf{A}
satisfies (
54
). So, we differentiate (
54
) with respect to
\mathbf{Q}
, in the direction
\mathbf{T}
:

\begin{split}\left(\frac{\partial\mathbf{A}}{\partial\mathbf{Q}}:\mathbf{T}\right):\frac{\partial^{2}W\left(\mathbf{A}\right)}{\partial\mathbf{A}^{2}}:\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{T}:\mathbf{P},\quad\text{for all }\mathbf{P}\in\mathbf{\Xi}.\end{split}

(82)

∎

Remark 8
.

The 4-tensor
\partial^{2}W\left(\mathbf{A}\right)/\partial\mathbf{A}^{2}
is positive definite, but the coercivity constant degrades as
|\mathbf{A}|\to\infty
.

5.2
Optimization Procedure

Given
\mathbf{Q}
, we describe a procedure to obtain the corresponding
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{Q})
, as well as its derivative with respect to
\mathbf{Q}
.

5.2.1
Solving the Euler-Lagrange Equation

Recall (
49
) and define the linear form
F_{\mathbf{Q}}(\mathbf{A};\mathbf{P})
to be the first variation of (
49
) with respect to
\mathbf{A}
:

F_{\mathbf{Q}}(\mathbf{A};\mathbf{P}):=\frac{1}{Z(\mathbf{A})}\frac{\partial Z(\mathbf{A})}{\partial\mathbf{A}}\cdot\mathbf{P}-\mathbf{Q}:\mathbf{P}.

(83)

Thus, given
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbf{\Xi}
, we want to find
\mathbf{A}
such that
F_{\mathbf{Q}}(\mathbf{A};\mathbf{P})=0
for all
\mathbf{P}\in\mathbf{\Xi}
. In other words, we want to find a zero of the non-linear function
F_{\mathbf{Q}}(\cdot;\mathbf{P})
. Hence, we apply Newton’s method.

For a given
\mathbf{A}
, define the bilinear form:

\begin{split}m_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{P},\mathbf{T})&:=\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{A}}F_{\mathbf{Q}}(\mathbf{A};\mathbf{P}):\mathbf{T}\\
&=\frac{1}{Z(\mathbf{A})}\mathbf{P}:\frac{\partial^{2}Z(\mathbf{A})}{\partial\mathbf{A}^{2}}:\mathbf{T}-\frac{1}{Z(\mathbf{A})}\left(\frac{\partial Z(\mathbf{A})}{\partial\mathbf{A}}:\mathbf{P}\right)\frac{1}{Z(\mathbf{A})}\left(\frac{\partial Z(\mathbf{A})}{\partial\mathbf{A}}:\mathbf{T}\right).\end{split}

(84)

Then Newton’s method is as follows.

1.

Initialize. Set
\mathbf{A}_{0}\in\mathbf{\Xi}
(can take the zero
3\times 3
matrix) and set
k=0
.

2.

While not converged, do:

(a)

Solve the following (linear) variational problem. Find
\delta\mathbf{A}_{k+1}\in\mathbf{\Xi}
such that

\begin{split}m_{\mathbf{A}_{k}}(\delta\mathbf{A}_{k+1},\mathbf{P})=-F_{\mathbf{Q}}(\mathbf{A}_{k};\mathbf{P}),\quad\forall\mathbf{P}\in\mathbf{\Xi}.\end{split}

(85)

(b)

Update. Set
\mathbf{A}_{k+1}:=\mathbf{A}_{k}+\delta\mathbf{A}_{k+1}
.

(c)

If
|\delta\mathbf{A}_{k+1}|
is less than some tolerance, then stop.

(d)

Else, set
k\leftarrow k+1
and return to Step (1).

Let
\mathbf{A}_{*}
be the solution, i.e.
F_{\mathbf{Q}}(\mathbf{A}_{*};\mathbf{P})=0
for all
\mathbf{P}\in\mathbf{\Xi}
. Let
\mathbf{A}_{*}^{\prime}(\mathbf{T})=(\partial\mathbf{A}/\partial\mathbf{Q}):\mathbf{T}
. We obtain
\mathbf{A}_{*}^{\prime}(\mathbf{T})
as the unique solution of the following variational problem. Find
\mathbf{A}_{*}^{\prime}(\mathbf{T})\in\mathbf{\Xi}
, for every
\mathbf{T}\in\mathbf{\Xi}
, such that

\begin{split}m_{\mathbf{A}_{*}}(\mathbf{A}_{*}^{\prime}(\mathbf{T}),\mathbf{P})=\mathbf{T}:\mathbf{P},\quad\forall\mathbf{P}\in\mathbf{\Xi}.\end{split}

(86)

5.2.2
Matrix-Vector Form

Recall the basis
\{\mathbf{E}^{k}\}_{k=1}^{5}
that spans
\mathbf{\Xi}
. We rewrite the Newton method in terms of this basis.

1.

Initialize. Let
\bm{\alpha}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{5}
, with
\bm{\alpha}_{0}=(\alpha_{0}^{1},...,\alpha_{0}^{5})
, such that
\mathbf{A}_{0}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{5}\alpha_{0}^{\ell}\mathbf{E}^{\ell}
. Can simply take
\bm{\alpha}_{0}=\mathbf{0}
. Set
k=0
.

2.

While not converged, do:

(a)

Compute. Let
\mathbf{b}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{5}
such that
b_{k}^{\ell}=-F_{\mathbf{Q}}(\mathbf{A}_{k};\mathbf{E}^{\ell})
for
\ell=1,...,5
. Moreover, let
\mathbf{H}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{5\times 5}
, i.e.
\mathbf{H}_{k}=[h_{k}^{ij}]_{i,j=1}^{5}
, such that
h_{k}^{ij}=m_{\mathbf{A}_{k}}(\mathbf{E}^{i},\mathbf{E}^{j})
for
1\leq i,j\leq 5
.

(b)

Solve for
\delta\bm{\alpha}_{k+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{5}
:

\begin{split}\mathbf{H}_{k}(\delta\bm{\alpha}_{k+1})=\mathbf{b}_{k}\end{split}

(87)

(c)

Update. Set
\bm{\alpha}_{k+1}:=\bm{\alpha}_{k}+\delta\bm{\alpha}_{k+1}
, and define
\mathbf{A}_{k+1}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{5}\alpha_{k+1}^{\ell}\mathbf{E}^{\ell}
.

(d)

If
|\delta\bm{\alpha}_{k+1}|
is less than some tolerance, then stop.

(e)

Else, set
k\leftarrow k+1
and return to Step (1).

Let
\mathbf{A}_{*}
be the solution, i.e.
F_{\mathbf{Q}}(\mathbf{A}_{*};\mathbf{P})=0
for all
\mathbf{P}\in\mathbf{\Xi}
. Let
\mathbf{A}_{*}^{\prime}(\mathbf{E}^{\ell})=(\partial\mathbf{A}/\partial\mathbf{Q}):\mathbf{E}^{\ell}
, for each
\ell=1,...,5
, and let
(\bm{\alpha}_{*}^{\prime})^{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{5}
be such that

\mathbf{A}_{*}^{\prime}(\mathbf{E}^{\ell})=\sum_{k=1}^{5}[(\bm{\alpha}_{*}^{\prime})^{\ell}\cdot\mathbf{e}_{k}]\mathbf{E}^{k}.

(88)

Next, let
\mathbf{H}_{*}
be the Hessian matrix corresponding to
\mathbf{A}_{*}
. Then, we obtain
\mathbf{A}_{*}^{\prime}(\mathbf{E}^{\ell})
by solving for
(\bm{\alpha}_{*}^{\prime})^{\ell}
the equation

\begin{split}\mathbf{H}_{*}(\bm{\alpha}_{*}^{\prime})^{\ell}=\mathbf{e}_{\ell},~\text{ for each }\ell=1,...,5.\end{split}

(89)

Note: this is a one time solve (there is no Newton iteration).

5.2.3
Computational Issues

The main difficulty associated with our method is that, contrary to the case of
the Landau-de Gennes energy (or the Oseen-Frank energy in the director
representation of the nematic), the energy density as a function of
\mathbf{Q}
is not
known explicitly. As in other self consistent field theories, practical
computation requires a numerical scheme. In this case, the
approximation involved in the evalulation of the free energy of a configuration
is due to both the Lagrange interpolation operator
\mathcal{I}_{h}
in
(
77
), and the fact that integrals on the unit
sphere
cannot be computed exactly
. Hence we must address the following
issues.

1.

The integrals must be approximated by quadrature, ideally a high order quadrature rule.

2.

The strict convexity of the functional in (
49
) degrades as
|\mathbf{A}|
becomes large, and this is exactly the situation that arises when the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{Q}
approach the physical limits in (
10
). Thus, any inaccuracies in the computations (e.g. the integrals) can adversely affect the convergence of the Newton method.

3.

Furthermore, when
\mathbf{A}
becomes large,
\exp\left(\mathbf{p}^{T}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{p}\right)
becomes extremely large. Even though we divide by
Z(\mathbf{A})
, there could be an intermediate overflow result or inaccuracies.

Therefore, we introduce the following modifications of the optimization method described earlier. Before computing any of the above, first do an eigen-decomposition of
\mathbf{Q}
.
If the eigenvalues are in the range:

\begin{split}-\frac{1}{3}+\delta_{0}\leq\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{Q})\leq\frac{2}{3}-\delta_{0},~\text{ for }i=1,2,3,\end{split}

(90)

for some
\delta_{0}>0
, then the plain Newton method above is sufficient (it converges in
\approx 5
iterations). Numerical experience indicates that
\delta_{0}=0.05
to
0.1
is adequate. If the eigenvalues are outside the range (
90
), then one must use a sufficiently accurate quadrature rule to ensure that the system in (
83
) is accurately approximated. In addition, a more robust optimization procedure should be used (e.g., the Broyden-Fletcher-Goldfarb-Shanno algorithm with a line search to ensure the objective function decreases) to account for possible numerical sensitivities. This is not difficult to implement since the problem size is small. However, we have not explored this possibility yet.

Next, as a general concern, the integrals should be computed using a shifting procedure. For example, consider the computation of

\begin{split}\frac{1}{Z(\mathbf{A})}\frac{\partial Z(\mathbf{A})}{\partial\mathbf{A}}&=\frac{1}{\int_{S^{2}}\exp\left(\mathbf{p}^{T}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{p}\right)\,dS(\mathbf{p})}\int_{S^{2}}\left(\mathbf{p}\otimes\mathbf{p}\right)\exp\left(\mathbf{p}^{T}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{p}\right)\,dS(\mathbf{p}),\end{split}

(91)

for a given
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbf{\Xi}
. Let
C_{0}=|\mathbf{A}|
. Then, (
91
) is equivalent to

\begin{split}\frac{1}{Z(\mathbf{A})}\frac{\partial Z(\mathbf{A})}{\partial\mathbf{A}}&=\frac{1}{\int_{S^{2}}\exp\left(\mathbf{p}^{T}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{p}-C_{0}\right)\,dS(\mathbf{p})}\int_{S^{2}}\left(\mathbf{p}\otimes\mathbf{p}\right)\exp\left(\mathbf{p}^{T}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{p}-C_{0}\right)\,dS(\mathbf{p}).\end{split}

(92)

The advantage of (
92
) over (
91
) is that, when
|\mathbf{A}|
is large, (
92
) will
not
result in an overflow calculation.

Lastly, all the integrals over the unit sphere have been approximated by Lebedev quadrature
[
63
]
. We ascertain the accuracy of the computation in section
6.1
. However, we note that they involve uniformly distributed points, so when the probability distribution
\rho
becomes very localized, the integration may fail. We have not encountered this problem in our numerical results below, but note that it would be possible to use an adaptive quadrature method instead. Indeed, one could adapt the quadrature rule depending on the performance of the Newton solve, or on how close the eigenvalues are to the physical limits.

6.3
Three dimensional configurations

Although the self consistent field theoretic method introduced might appear to lead to a more complex numerical implementation than the Landau-de Gennes theory, we show that even with modest computational resources it is possible to obtain defected configurations in three spatial dimensions. We consider three examples: a
m=+1

m=-1/2

\kappa/T=4

L_{1}=1
,
L_{2}=L_{3}=L_{4}=0
, and
L_{*}=3

[-5,5]^{3}
with a uniform tetrahedral mesh with
41\times 41\times 41
vertices. For the Saturn ring we use a cubic domain of size
[-30,30]^{3}
with a spherical cavity of radius
7.5
and a body-centered-cubic (bcc) mesh with
127108
vertices. For all computations, we use piecewise linear finite elements and a time step

\mathbf{Q}

7
Conclusions and discussion

The analysis of a new computational method to obtain equilibrium configurations of a nematic liquid crystal has been presented. The method, based on the Ball-Majumdar singular bulk potential, can overcome known limitations of the Landau-de Gennes theory in the case of elastically anisotropic media. We present selected numerical results demonstrating the convergence of the method in cases in which the Landau-de Gennes theory fails, and a study of prototypical three dimensional configurations that include both point and line topological defects. The code developed has been incorporated into the FELICITY finite element framework in order to facilitate adoption
[
64
,
65
]
.

The results shown have been obtained with a particular microscopic interaction model: the Maier-Saupe contact potential. This is a simple case to study since the resulting interaction energy is simply quadratic in
\mathbf{Q}
. However, the extension to more complex interaction energies is possible assuming one knows their explicit functional representation in terms of the mesoscale
\mathbf{Q}
. The eigenvalue constraint as introduced is captured in the entropy functional, which only depends on the definition of
\mathbf{Q}
, and hence is independent of the form of the interaction energy functional. Of course, the limitation of a mean field approximation remains as long as the energy only depends on the local statistical average of the molecular orientation
\mathbf{p}
. This is not expected to be a serious shortcoming as long as thermal fluctuations are negligible. This is the case in the majority of contemporary studies that focus on systems deep inside the nematic phase.

We have restricted our analysis to finding minimizers of the free energy functional, but the method can be readily extended to studies of nematodynamics (including hydrodynamic interactions). One simply needs to replace the Landau-de Gennes functional by the free energy computed from the singular potential. While there is an additional computational cost involved, it is not severe as demonstrated by our calculations of defect configurations in three dimensions, as long as one takes advantage of parallelism. The method can be therefore applied to the study of the temporal evolution of elastically anisotropic systems, including mass flows. Such a capability should be specially relevant to studies of nematic active matter in which the length of the molecular constituents, and the dependence of their elastic constants on the Debye length when charged, leads to strong elastic anisotropy.
