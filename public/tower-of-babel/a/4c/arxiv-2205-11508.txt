Contrastive and Non-Contrastive Self-Supervised Learning Recover Global and Local Spectral Embedding Methods
Randall Balestriero, Yann LeCun

Abstract
Self-Supervised Learning (SSL) surmises that inputs and pairwise positive relationships are enough to learn meaningful representations. Although SSL has recently reached a milestone: outperforming supervised methods in many modalities…the theoretical foundations are limited, method-specific, and fail to provide principled design guidelines to practitioners. In this paper, we propose a unifying framework under the helm of spectral manifold learning to address those limitations. Through the course of this study, we will rigorously demonstrate that VICReg, SimCLR, BarlowTwins et al. correspond to eponymous spectral methods such as Laplacian Eigenmaps, Multidimensional Scaling et al. This unification will then allow us to obtain (i) the closed-form optimal representation for each method, (ii) the closed-form optimal network parameters in the linear regime for each method, (iii) the impact of the pairwise relations used during training on each of those quantities and on downstream task performances, and most importantly, (iv) the first theoretical bridge between contrastive and non-contrastive methods towards global and local spectral embedding methods respectively, hinting at the benefits and limitations of each. For example, (i) if the pairwise relation is aligned with the downstream task, any SSL method can be employed successfully and will recover the supervised method, but in the low data regime, VICReg’s invariance hyper-parameter should be high; (ii) if the pairwise relation is misaligned with the downstream task, VICReg with small invariance hyper-parameter should be preferred over SimCLR or BarlowTwins.

Introduction

Self-Supervised Learning (SSL) is one of the most promising method to learn data representations that generalize across downstream tasks. SSL places itself in-between supervised and unsupervised learning as it does not require labels but does require knowledge of what makes some samples semantically close to others. Hence, where unsupervised learning relies on a collection of inputs
({\bm{X}})
, and supervised learning relies on inputs and outputs
({\bm{X}},{\bm{Y}})
, SSL relies on inputs and inter-sample relations
({\bm{X}},{\bm{G}})
that indicate semantic similarity akin to weak-supervision used in metric learning
(
Xing et al. 2002
)
.
The latter matrix
{\bm{G}}
is often constructed by augmenting
{\bm{X}}
through data-augmentations known to preserve input semantics
(
Kanazawa et al. 2016
;
Novotny et al. 2018
;
Gidaris et al. 2018
)
e.g. horizontal flip for an image, although recent methods have went away from DA by using videos from which consecutive frames can be seen as semantically equivalent
(
Sermanet et al. 2018
;
Kim et al. 2019
;
Xu et al. 2019
)
.

Although SSL originated decades ago
(
Bromley et al. 1993
)
, recent advances have pushed SSL performances beyond expectations
(
Chen et al. 2020
;
Misra and Maaten 2020
;
Caron et al. 2021
)
. Due to those rapid empirical advances, an urgent need for a principled theoretical understanding of those methods has emerged e.g. to understand how well the learned transformation transfer to different downstream tasks
(
Goyal et al. 2019
;
Ericsson et al. 2021
)
. Studies looking to provide a more fundamental and principled understanding of SSL mostly take one of the three following approaches: (i) studying the training dynamics and optimization landscapes in a linear network regime e.g. validating some empirically found tricks as necessary conditions for stable gradient dynamics
Wang et al. 2021
;
Tian et al. 2021
;
Wen and Li 2021
;
Pokle et al. 2022
;
Tian 2022
, (ii) studying the role of individual SSL components separately e.g. the projector and predictor networks
Hua et al. 2021
;
Jing et al. 2021
;
Bordes et al. 2021
;
Tosh et al. 2021
, or (iii) developing novel SSL criteria that often combine multiple interpretable objectives that a SSL model must fulfill
(
Arora et al. 2019
;
Nazi et al. 2019
;
Wang and Isola 2020
;
Shi et al. 2020
;
Zbontar et al. 2021
;
Bardes et al. 2021
)
. While those branches have led to novel understandings and even stem novel SSL methods, some fundamental questions remain open.

More recently and particularly relevant to our study,
Qiu et al. 2018
unified skip-gram/word2vec methods under the helm of matrix factorization. Without much effort, those results could be used to unify pretext-task SSL learning
(
Baevski et al. 2020
;
Bao et al. 2021b
;
He et al. 2021
)
(our focus is on joint-embedding SSL). Closer to our topic,
HaoChen et al. 2021
;
HaoChen et al. 2022
developed a study of SimCLR
(
Chen et al. 2020
)
by proposing a modified objective coined as Spectral Contrastive Loss based on graph representation of pairwise similarities. A perhaps more direct connection between video-based SSL and spectral methods (Laplacian Eigenmap in particular) can be found by combining the Spectral Inference Networks (SIN) of
Pfau et al. 2019
that generalize Slow Feature Analysis (SFA) to arbitrary linear operators, and the known relationship between SFA and Laplacian Eigenmap
(
Sprekeler 2011
)
. Although SIN was not framed within an SSL viewpoint e.g. SIN has a well-posed learned coordinate system while SSL methods coordinates can be arbitrarily rotated; those studies paved our way forward as
we propose in this paper a broad analysis that unify most existing SSL methods as variants of known spectral embedding methods, allowing us for the first time to provide provable design guildelines to practitioners in their choice of architecture and methods
. We summarize our unification results in
Fig.

. The instrumental results we obtain allow us to answer some long-standing questions such as:

Yes,

How does connecting SSL methods to spectral embedding methods improve our understanding and guide the design of novel SSL frameworks?
We demonstrate that contrastive and non-contrastive SSL corresponds to global and local spectral embedding methods respectively (
Sections

3.2
and
4.3
). From that, we easily identify the best use-cases for each of them e.g. contrastive methods aim to be metric preserving and shine with low-dimensional or high-dimensional but linear manifolds while non-contrastive shine with locally linear but globally nonlinear manifolds.

The natural symbiosis between SSL and spectral methods formulations that will be discovered throughout this study not only enables us to answer the above challenging questions but also provides practical guidelines to practitioners. One emblematic example is offered in

where we identify the situations for which different SSL methods should be preferred.

We summarize our contributions below:

1.

The DN representation

The linear representation

parameters obtained by minimizing
any

are obtained in closed-form, providing insights into the type of input statistics that a network parameters focus on to produce the optimal input representation (

and

3.

SSL methods employ diverse criterion that can be tied to eponymous spectral analysis methods both when employing a nonlinear DN as Laplacian Eigenmaps (VICReg,
Section

3.2
), ISOMAP (SimCLR/NNCLR,

), Canonical Correlation Analysis (BarlowTwins,

Eckart and Young 1936
;
Hestenes 1958
)
e.g.
{\bm{X}}={\bm{U}}_{x}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{x}{\bm{V}}^{T}_{x}
that are denoted as the left singular vectors
{\bm{U}}_{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
, the singular values
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times D}
and the right singular vectors
{\bm{V}}_{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times D}
of
{\bm{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times D}
. We will always specify as a lower-script and lower-case the matrix that is being decomposed (
x
in this case). It will also be convenient to only consider the left/right singular vectors whose associated singular values are
0
that we will denote as
\overline{{\bm{U}}}_{x}
and
\overline{{\bm{V}}}_{x}
respectively. Conversely, the left/right singular vectors whose associated singular values are
>0
will be denote as
\widehat{{\bm{U}}}_{x}
and
\widehat{{\bm{V}}}_{x}
respectively. Lastly, we will denote by
{\bm{\sigma}}_{z}
the vector of singular values such as
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{z}=\diag({\bm{\sigma}})
and without loss of generality and unless otherwise stated, this will always be in descending order.

Our goal in the following sections (
Section

3
for VICReg,
Section

4
for SimCLR/NNCLR, and
Section

5
for BarlowTwins) will be to find the optimal representations
{\bm{Z}}
of
{\bm{X}}
whilst tying those methods to their spectral embedding counterpart. Three surprising facts will emerge: (i) all existing methods recover exactly some flavors of famous spectral method, (ii) the spectral properties of the optimal representation
{\bm{Z}}
of
{\bm{X}}
can be obtained in closed-form, and (iii) from those properties, necessary and sufficient conditions can be obtained to bounds the downstream task error of those optimal SSL representations (
Section

6
).

3
VICReg Minimizes the Dirichlet Energy to Produce Smooth Signals on the Graph
{\bm{G}}
While Preventing Dimensional Collapse

We will first demonstrate in
Section

3.1
that the optimal VICReg representation can be obtained in closed form (
Theorem

1
) and that turning the VICReg optimization as a constrained problem recovers (Kernel) Laplacian Eigenmaps, an eponymous spectral embedding method
Section

3.2
. We then consider that same constrained problem but under a linear network regime for which we obtain the closed-form optimal network’s parameters (
Theorem

3

Section

3.3
).

3.1
Closed-Form Optimal Representation for VICReg

The first goal of this section is to build up some insights into VICReg by demonstrating how the invariance term corresponds to the Dirichlet energy of the signal
{\bm{Z}}
on the graph
{\bm{G}}
. Then, replacing the variance hinge loss at
1
with the squared loss at
1
as in
\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(1-\mathrm{Cov}({\bm{Z}})_{k,k}\right)^{2}
—notice that minimizing the latter implies minimizing the former— we obtain the closed-form optimal representation
{\bm{Z}}^{*}
of
{\bm{X}}
which turns out to be a function only of
{\bm{G}}
and the loss’ hyper-parameters.

From invariance to trace minimization.
As a first step, we propose to better understand the impact of VICReg’s invariance term (recall

3.2
VICReg Recovers Laplacian Eigenmaps in Feature Space and Kernel Locality Preserving Projection in Data Space

From the previous section
Theorems

1
and
8
we obtained that the ratio
\frac{\gamma}{\alpha}
entirely controls the mixing between the Laplacian matrix of a complete graph and the one from
{\bm{G}}
. In other words, this ratio controls the spectral properties of
{\bm{Z}}^{*}_{\alpha,\gamma}
by interpolating between the ones of
{\bm{I}}\frac{1}{N}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{T}
and the ones of
{\bm{L}}
(recall
Fig.

5
). The goal of this section is to study more precisely the scenario where
\alpha\gg\gamma
i.e. we prioritize the variance-covariance terms, in which case VICReg recovers Laplacian Eigenmaps (LE)
(
Belkin and Niyogi 2003
)
in feature space, and kernel Locality Preserving Projection (kLPP)
(
He and Niyogi 2003
)
in data space.

In feature space.
LE is a non-parametric method searching for a representation
{\bm{Z}}
by minimizing the following Brockett
(
Brockett 1991
)
optimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{{\bm{Z}}}\Tr\left({\bm{Z}}^{T}\left({\bm{D}}-{\bm{G}}\right){\bm{Z}}\right)\text{ s.t. }{\bm{Z}}^{T}{\bm{D}}{\bm{Z}}={\bm{I}},

(10)

with
{\bm{D}}
the diagonal degree matrix of
{\bm{G}}
(recall
Eq.

8
).
The relation between
Eq.

10
and VICReg comes by combining the following two facts. First, minimizing
Eq.

10
is done by taking the eigenvectors associated to the
K
smallest eigenvalues of
{\bm{I}}-{\bm{D}}^{-1}{\bm{G}}
(see
Liang et al. 2021
and Corollary 4.3.39 from
Horn and Johnson 2012
) yet, —LE disregards the eigenvector associated to the eigenvalue
0
— to produce the optimal LE solution denoted as
{\bm{Z}}^{*}_{\rm LE}
. Doing so,
Belkin and Niyogi 2003
produced a representation whose columns have
0
-mean since the only eigenvector with nonzero mean is the one associated to the eigenvalue
0
(details in
Section

A.8
). Second, VICReg employs a graph
{\bm{G}}
for which
{\bm{D}}=c{\bm{I}}
with
c=2
(positive pairs),
c=3
(positive tirplets, and so on. And thus, the eigenvectors associated to the nonzero eigenvalues of
{\bm{D}}-{\bm{G}}
or
{\bm{I}}-{\bm{D}}^{-1}{\bm{G}}
are the same i.e. the optimal solution of the LE problem minimizes the VICReg criterion with strict enforcement that the variance+covariance terms, denoted as
\mathcal{L}_{var},\mathcal{L}_{\rm cov}
hereon, are zero.

Theorem 2
.

Given a dataset
{\bm{X}}
and relation matrix
{\bm{G}}
, minimizing the VICReg loss (
Eq.

2
) with constraint that the variance and covariance loss are
0
(
\alpha,\beta
become irrelevant) as in

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\rm vic}({\bm{Z}}^{*}_{\rm LE})=\min_{{\bm{Z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times K}}\mathcal{L}_{\rm inv}({\bm{Z}})\text{ s.t. }\mathcal{L}_{\rm var}=0\text{ and }\mathcal{L}_{\rm cov}=0.

(Proof in
Section

A.8
.)

Hence, given a relation matrix
{\bm{G}}
, solving the LE problem is equivalent to solving a constrained VICReg problem. We ought to highlight however that a crucial part of LE lies in the design of that matrix
{\bm{G}}
, often found from a
k
-NN graph
(
Hautamaki et al. 2004
)
of the samples
{\bm{X}}
in the input space, while in SSL it is constructed from data-augmentations or given.

Going beyond LE, one can easily obtain that if
\left({\bm{D}}-{\bm{G}}\right)
is idempotent —which is true for common supervised and SSL scenarios as
{\bm{G}}
corresponds to a union of complete graphs— then LE also corresponds to Locally Linear Embedding
(
Roweis and Saul 2000
)
and so does VICReg.

In data space.
One difficulty arising from LE, and from non-parametric methods in general, is the ability to produce new representations
{\bm{z}}
for new data samples that were not present when solving
Eq.

10
. This led to the development of a two-step modeling process as

\displaystyle{\bm{x}}\;(\in\mathbb{R}^{D})\mapsto{\bm{h}}=\phi({\bm{x}})\;(\in\mathbb{R}^{S})\mapsto{\bm{z}}={\bm{W}}^{T}{\bm{h}}(\in\mathbb{R}^{K}),

with
S\gg K,{\bm{W}}\in\mathbb{R}^{S\times K}
, and where the first mapping ’s goal is to learn a generic input embedding that can be reused on new samples
{\bm{x}}
. To see this, let’s collect those mappings for all the training set into the
N\times S
matrix
\Phi
. With that, the LE problem in data space —known as the Kernel Locality Preserving Projection problem— becomes

\displaystyle\min_{\theta:{\bm{W}}^{T}\Phi^{T}{\bm{D}}\Phi{\bm{W}}={\bm{I}}}\Tr\left({\bm{W}}^{T}\Phi^{T}\left({\bm{D}}-{\bm{G}}\right)\Phi{\bm{W}}\right),

(11)

so that the original LE representation
{\bm{Z}}
can be obtained simply as
{\bm{Z}}=\Phi{\bm{W}}
. And more importantly, given a new sample
{\bm{x}}
, one can simply obtain the new representation via
{\bm{z}}={\bm{W}}^{T}\phi({\bm{x}})
.

Most existing solutions have been leveraging the Moore-Aronszajn theorem
(
Aronszajn 1950
)
and the fact that we are in a finite data regime to shift the question
what nonlinear and high-dimensional operator
\phi
should be used?
to the perhaps simpler question
what symmetric positive (semi-)definite matrix
\Phi\Phi^{T}
, i.e. what kernel, function should be used?

(
Bengio et al. 2003
;
Cheng et al. 2005
;
Tai et al. 2022
)
. Doing so,
{\bm{W}}^{T}\phi({\bm{x}})
can now be easily implemented e.g. as a Generalized Regression Network
(
Broomhead and Lowe 1988
;
Specht et al. 1991
)
. The crucial result of interest for our study is the following one that combine
Theorem

2
with a result from
He and Niyogi 2003
demonstrating the equivalence between LE in feature space and KLLE in data space.

Proposition 1
.

VICReg with variance/covariance constraint solves LE in embedding space and KLLE in input space (recall
Eq.

11
) employing a DN for
{\bm{W}}^{T}\circ\phi
.

Equipped with those results, we can now propose a practical result by obtaining in closed-form the optimal parameter weights that solve the constrained VICReg criterion with a linear network.

4.3
SimCLR is Akin to ISOMAP in Feature Space and Kernel ISOMAP in Input Space

We now propose to tie the SimCLR method along with its variants e.g. NNCLR to known

(

)
contrasting from VICReg which was tied to

In feature space.
Let’s first recall that ISOMAP is a variation of Multi-Dimensional Scaling (MDS)
(
Kruskal 1964
)
also known as Principal Coordinates Analysis. Classical MDS tries to learn embedding vectors that have similar pairwise distance (usually
\ell_{2}
) than the pairwise distance of the given input data. Often, MDS does this by using similarities instead of distances and thus by solving the following optimization problem
\min_{{\bm{Z}}}\|{\bm{G}}-{\bm{Z}}{\bm{Z}}^{T}\|_{F}^{2}
,
where
{\bm{G}}
is the Gram matrix of the inputs i.e.
{\bm{G}}={\bm{X}}{\bm{X}}^{T}
. At the most general level, ISOMAP simply corresponds to solving that same optimization problem but after redefining
{\bm{G}}
to better capture the geometric information of
{\bm{X}}
e.g. using the shortest path distance of the
k
-NN graph of
{\bm{X}}

(
Preparata and Shamos 2012
)
. The surprising result that we formalize below is that SimCLR and in particular NNCLR recover ISOMAP, the former by prescribing
{\bm{G}}
given the known positive pairs, and the latter by building a nearest neighbor graph.

Proposition 2
.

Using the settings of
Theorem

5
, SimCLR recovers a Generalized MDS akin to ISOMAP (and MDS iff
{\bm{G}}={\bm{X}}{\bm{X}}^{T}
). (Proof in
Section

A.12
.)

In data space with DNs.
From the above, we can extend
Proposition

2
but in input space, in a very similar way as was done in
Section

3.2
. In fact, originating in
Webb 1995
, there was a search to extend MDS, and ISOMAP to an input space formulation to solve the out-of-bag problem. In this setting, and taking MDS as an example, the original similarity matrix
{\bm{Z}}{\bm{Z}}^{T}
is replaced with
\Phi{\bm{W}}^{T}{\bm{W}}\Phi^{T}
using the same notations as in
Eq.

and already known relationship between those models, we obtain the following.

Proposition 3

(
(
Williams 2000
)
)
.

Whenever SimCLR recovers ISOMAP or MDS in feature space, it recovers kernel ISOMAP or kernel PCA
(
Schölkopf et al. 1998
)
in input space.

5.1

Recall from

2

with the correct parameter tuning, even on large models e.g. on the Imagenet dataset with modern deep learning architectures. Hence, we will leverage that loss function throughout this section.

Hence we consider that we are given representations
{\bm{z}}\triangleq f({\bm{x}})
from an input sample
{\bm{x}}
. Hence, we will denote the dataset representation as
{\bm{Z}}=[f({\bm{x}}_{1}),\dots,f({\bm{x}}_{N})]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times K}

where each
{\bm{y}}_{n}

Without loss of generality and to lighten our derivations, we will omit the bias vector
{\bm{b}}
, it can be learned as part of
{\bm{W}}
by adding
1
to the features of
{\bm{z}}_{n}
. Our loss function thus takes the following form
\mathcal{L}({\bm{W}})=\frac{1}{2}\|{\bm{Y}}-{\bm{Z}}{\bm{W}}\|_{F}^{2}
. To minimize this loss function, we will first find what matrices
{\bm{W}}
make the gradient of
\mathcal{L}
with respect to
{\bm{W}}
vanish. Hence, we need to find
{\bm{W}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times C}
such that

\displaystyle\nabla_{{\bm{W}}}\mathcal{L}=

\displaystyle\mathbf{0}\iff-{\bm{Z}}^{T}({\bm{Y}}-{\bm{Z}}{\bm{W}})=\mathbf{0}\iff{\bm{Z}}^{T}{\bm{Y}}={\bm{Z}}^{T}{\bm{Z}}{\bm{W}}.

If we assumed
{\bm{Z}}
to be full rank, we would directly recover the usual least square solution
{\bm{W}}^{*}=({\bm{Z}}^{T}{\bm{Z}})^{-1}{\bm{Z}}^{T}{\bm{Y}}

{\bm{Z}}

that fulfill
\nabla_{{\bm{W}}}\mathcal{L}=\mathbf{0}
regardless of the properties of
{\bm{Z}}
and
{\bm{Y}}
.
To that end, we first start by using the SVD of

—which always exists— to reformulate the above equality (see derivations in
Sections

A.2

A.3

\displaystyle{\bm{Z}}^{T}{\bm{Y}}={\bm{Z}}^{T}{\bm{Z}}{\bm{W}}\iff

\displaystyle{\bm{W}}\in\left\{({\bm{V}}_{z}{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}_{z}{\bm{U}}_{z}^{T}+\overline{{\bm{V}}}_{z}{\bm{M}}){\bm{Y}}:{\bm{M}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times C}\right\},

(21)

with

0\text{ otherwise}\end{cases},

hence

is a

6.3
Contrastive and Non-Contrastive Learning can all be Optimal

We now demonstrate in this section that any representation

.

Given a dataset
{\bm{X}}
and relation matrix

6.4
Non-Contrastive Methods Should be Preferred: Best and Worst Downstream Task Error Bounds

We demonstrated in
Section

6.3
that all SSL methods can be optimal to solve a task at ahdn as long as the spectral properties of
{\bm{G}}
and
{\bm{Y}}
are aligned. However, this is rarely the case in practical scenarios, and it thus becomes crucial to understand the behavior of the learned representation
{\bm{Z}}
on downstream task and if it varies with different SSL methods. First, we propose the following bound which represent the best and worst case downstream performances as a function of the rank of
{\bm{G}}
which is mostly a result of applying the Eckart-Young-Mirsky theorem. That is, we look at all the possible similarity matrices
{\bm{G}}
of rank
R
and see, given a task
{\bm{Y}}
what is the best achievable performance if
{\bm{G}}
correctly encoders the data geometry, and what is the worse possible performance if
{\bm{G}}
is “orthogonal” to the correct data geometry. For clarity and without loss of generality we assume here that
\rank({\bm{Y}})\leq K
as otherwise no method would produce an optimal representation in general and
K<N
as otherwise we are in the kernel regime.

Theorem 10
.

Given fixed inputs
{\bm{X}}
the lower and upper-bound over all possible matrices
{\bm{G}}
of rank
R
of the downstream task training performances (with fixed
{\bm{Y}}
) are given by

\displaystyle\sum_{i=R}^{K}({\bm{\Sigma}}_{y}^{2})_{i,i}\leq\left(\min_{{\bm{W}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times C}}\mathcal{L}({\bm{W}},{\bm{Z}}^{*}_{\rm SimCLR}({\bm{G}}))-\min_{{\bm{W}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times C}}\mathcal{L}({\bm{W}},{\bm{Z}}^{*}_{\rm VICReg}({\bm{G}}))\right)\leq\|{\bm{Y}}\|_{F}^{2},

and are tight. Hence one should prefer VICReg, then BarlowTwins and finally SimCLR to maximize the downstream task performances.

The above result is a direct consequence of SimCLR forcing the representation to have the same rank as
{\bm{G}}
while VICReg always enforce a full-rank representation. And although this difference becomes irrelevant with correct
{\bm{G}}
(recall
Section

6.3
) it becomes an important distinctive attribute between SSL methods when
{\bm{G}}
is not optimal, which concerns most practical scenarios.

7
Conclusions

We provided in this study a unifying analysis of the major self-supervised learning methods covering VICReg (
Section

3
), SimCLR (
Section

4
) and BarlowTwins (
Section

5
). In doing so, we were able to not only tie each of those methods and their variants to common spectral embedding techniques, but we were also able to find the commonalities between all those methods. Among the many insights that we obtained, the most crucial one is that whenever the similarity matrix
{\bm{G}}
is correctly defined with respect to a downstream task, any of those methods will produce an ideal representation that will perfectly solve the task at hand. In short, there is no benefit of one method versus any other. In the more realistic regime where
{\bm{G}}
might be misaligned with the downstream task, VICReg with lower invariance regularization hyper-parameter should be preferred. In that regime, the representation will include all the information from
{\bm{G}}
while preserving full-rank and thus allowing for the representation to be usable for other downstream task that are not encoded within
{\bm{G}}
. This is in contrast with BarlowTwins and SimCLR that collapse the representation to embed all information about
{\bm{G}}
and nothing else.

At a more general level, we were able to parallel the contrastive versus non-contrastive dichotomy in SSL to the global versus local methods in spectral methods respectively. This led to further highlights into the strengths and weakness of each. For example, global approaches (contrastive SSL) tend to give a more faithful representation of the data’s global structure as the embedding aims to be metric-preserving. On the other hand, the local approaches (non-contrastive) provide useful embedding on a broader range of manifolds, whose local geometry is close to Euclidean, but whose global geometry may not be
(
Silva and Tenenbaum 2002
)
.

Beyond those results, we hope that the ties provided in this paper will stem a plurality of future work. One example would be to leverage the connection between SSL methods and spectral embedding methods to port existing results and techniques from one field to the other. In fact, such spectral methods have fallen short when dealing with high-dimensional datasets such as Imagenet. However, SSL methods have risen to become state-of-the-art on those datasets. The only difference between those lies in how
{\bm{G}}
is constructed.

\displaystyle\alpha\left(\left\|\frac{1}{N}({\bm{Z}}^{*}_{\alpha,\gamma})^{T}{\bm{M}}{\bm{Z}}^{*}_{\alpha,\gamma}\right\|_{F}^{2}+K-2\|{\bm{\lambda}}^{\prime}_{\alpha,\gamma}\|_{2}^{2}\right),

now one should recall that

and that each of those eigenvector that has nonzero singular value has

mean since

\displaystyle({\bm{M}}-\frac{\gamma}{\alpha}{\bm{L}}){\bm{v}}

\displaystyle=\lambda{\bm{v}}

\displaystyle\iff{\bm{M}}({\bm{I}}-\frac{\gamma}{\alpha}{\bm{L}}){\bm{M}}{\bm{v}}

\displaystyle=\lambda{\bm{v}}

0

\displaystyle\implies\mathbf{1}^{T}{\bm{M}}({\bm{I}}-\frac{\gamma}{\alpha}{\bm{L}}){\bm{M}}{\bm{v}}

\displaystyle\implies 0

hence we obtain the following simplifications

\displaystyle\alpha\left(\left\|\frac{1}{N}({\bm{Z}}^{*}_{\alpha,\gamma})^{T}{\bm{M}}{\bm{Z}}^{*}_{\alpha,\gamma}\right\|_{F}^{2}+K-2\|{\bm{\lambda}}^{\prime}_{\alpha,\gamma}\|_{2}^{2}\right)

A.8
Proof of VICReg Recovering Laplacian Eigenmaps (
Theorem

2
)

This section takes on proving the first key result of our study that thoroughly tie VICReg to the known local spectral embedding method Laplacian Eigenmap. The only difference would be that in our case the graph
{\bm{G}}
is given from an SSL viewpoint and not constructed from a k-NN graph and geodesic distance estimate as in LE. We already know from
Section

A.5
that the invariance term of VICReg corresponds to the Trace term (times
2
) that LE tries to minimize. The only thing that we have to show to have the equivalent between the two is that the constraint
{\bm{Z}}^{T}{\bm{D}}{\bm{Z}}={\bm{I}}
is equivalent to the one that imposes an exact minimization of the variance and covariance terms. To see that, first notice (or recall from
Section

A.7
) that the variance+covariance term can be expressed as
\|\frac{1}{N}{\bm{Z}}^{T}{\bm{M}}{\bm{Z}}-{\bm{I}}\|_{F}^{2}
.
Now, recall that the minimizer of LE consists in taking the
[2:K+1]
eigenvectors of the Laplacian matrix
{\bm{I}}-{\bm{D}}^{-1}{\bm{G}}
which is equivalent (up to a rescaling of the eigenvalues) to taking the eigenvectors of
{\bm{D}}-{\bm{G}}
as long as
{\bm{D}}
is isotropic. We also already saw that those eigenvectors either have zero mean and nonzero eigenvalue, or have arbitrary means and zero eigenvalue. As LE only considers the eigenvectors with nonzero eigenvalues, it is direct to see that those eigenvectors are centered. This translates to
\|\frac{1}{N}{\bm{Z}}^{T}{\bm{M}}{\bm{Z}}-{\bm{I}}\|_{F}^{2}=\|\frac{1}{N}{\bm{Z}}^{T}{\bm{Z}}-{\bm{I}}\|_{F}^{2}
.
Hence, up to a rescaling, as long as
D
is isotropic (
D=c{\bm{I}}
) which is always the case in SSL, enforcing
\mathcal{L}_{\rm var}=0,\mathcal{\rm cov}=0
is equivalent to
{\bm{Z}}^{T}{\bm{Z}}=\frac{N}{c}{\bm{I}}
.

\displaystyle\sum_{i\not=j}d(f_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{i}),f_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{j})){\bm{W}}_{i,j}+\tau\sum_{i\not=j}{\bm{W}}_{i,j}(\log({\bm{W}}_{i,j})-1)+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}(\sum_{j\not=i}W_{i,j}-1)+\sum_{i}\beta_{i}{\bm{W}}_{i,i}

\displaystyle\begin{cases}d(f_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{i}),f_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{j}))+\tau\log({\bm{W}}_{i,j})+\lambda_{i}\iff i\not=j\\
\beta_{i}\iff i=j\end{cases}

\displaystyle\sum_{j\not=i}{\bm{W}}_{i,j}-1

\displaystyle\implies\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\beta{i}}=

\displaystyle{\bm{W}}_{i,i},

Recovering SimCLR with Cosine Similarity.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}{\bm{X}}^{T}{\bm{G}}{\bm{X}}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{c=1}^{C}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}_{c}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{c}}\frac{1}{N_{c}}{\bm{x}}_{i}{\bm{x}}_{j}^{T}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{c=1}^{C}N_{c}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}_{c}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{c}}\frac{1}{N_{c}^{2}}{\bm{x}}_{i}{\bm{x}}_{j}^{T}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{c=1}^{C}N_{c}\frac{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}_{c}}{\bm{x}}_{i}}{N_{c}}\frac{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{c}}{\bm{x}}_{j}^{T}}{N_{c}}

\displaystyle\sum_{c=1}^{C}\frac{N_{c}}{N}{\bm{\mu}}_{c}{\bm{\mu}}_{c}^{T},

recovering the between (inter-cluster) variance

with
{\bm{\mu}}_{c}
the center of class

we directly have that

\displaystyle\max_{{\bm{W}}}\frac{|{\bm{W}}^{T}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{b}{\bm{W}}|}{|{\bm{W}}^{T}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{w}{\bm{W}}|},

which is known as the Fisher criterion. Whenever
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{w}
is invertible (if not, then the original data can be projected to a lower dimensional subspace without loss on information that would ensure that
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{w}
is invertible) then Fisher’s criterion is maximized leading to
{\bm{W}}
being the solution to the generalized eigenvalue problem

\displaystyle{\bm{W}}^{*}=

\displaystyle\argmax\frac{|{\bm{W}}^{T}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{b}{\bm{W}}|}{|{\bm{W}}^{T}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{w}{\bm{W}}|}=\text{top eigenvectors of: }({\bm{\Sigma}}_{w})^{-1}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{b}.

Notice that the generalized eigenvalue problem is exactly

\displaystyle{\bm{X}}^{T}{\bm{G}}{\bm{X}}{\bm{W}}=\diag(\lambda){\bm{X}}^{T}({\bm{I}}-{\bm{G}}){\bm{X}}{\bm{W}},

The eigenvalues of

A.16
Proof of BarlowTwins in the Linear Regime (
Theorem

7
)

The tie between CCA and LDA is not new. In fact, if one considers one dataset to be the samples and the other to be the class labels (binary variables for two-class problems or a variation of one-hot encoding for multi-class problems) then CCA and LDA are equivalent
[
Bartlett 1938
,
Hastie et al. 1995
]
. But in our case, we do not explicitly use the labels but the views of different class. Hence, the goal of this section is to first recover the analytical parameters of BarlowTwins in the linear regime through CCA and then to demonstrate that the CCA-LDA connection also persists in the case when the views are both obtained from per-class samples i.e. exactly fitting the BarlowTwins scenario.

There exists many different ways to formulate the CCA problem. At the most simple level, one aims to sequentially learn pairs of filters that produce maximally correlated features as in

\displaystyle\max_{{\bm{w}}_{\rm a},{\bm{w}}_{\rm b}}\frac{\langle{\bm{X}}_{\rm a}{\bm{W}}_{\rm a},{\bm{X}}_{\rm b}{\bm{W}}_{\rm b}\rangle}{\|{\bm{X}}_{\rm a}{\bm{W}}_{\rm a}\|_{2}\|{\bm{X}}_{\rm b}{\bm{W}}_{\rm b}\|_{2}},

which can easily be recognized to be the diagonal element of the BarlowTwins loss that we aim to maximize in the linear regime i.e. the cosine similarity between a column of
{\bm{Z}}_{\rm a}
and the same column of
{\bm{Z}}_{\rm b}
. To find the corresponding filters, which are in general not assume to be identical, one transforms the above problem to a constrained optimization problem exactly as done with eigenvalue problems with the Rayleigh quotient. We thus obtain the following

\displaystyle\max_{{\bm{w}}_{\rm a},{\bm{w}}_{\rm b}}\langle{\bm{X}}_{\rm a}{\bm{W}}_{\rm a},{\bm{X}}_{\rm b}{\bm{W}}_{\rm b}\rangle\text{ s.t. }\|{\bm{X}}_{\rm a}{\bm{W}}_{\rm a}\|_{2}=1\text{ and }\|{\bm{X}}_{\rm b}{\bm{W}}_{\rm b}\|_{2}=1,

since rescaling of the weights does not impact the learned filters, which can be transformed into a Lagrangian function as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\langle{\bm{X}}_{\rm a}{\bm{W}}_{\rm a},{\bm{X}}_{\rm b}{\bm{W}}_{\rm b}\rangle-\lambda_{1}(\|{\bm{X}}_{\rm a}{\bm{W}}_{\rm a}\|_{2}-1)-\lambda_{2}(\|{\bm{X}}_{\rm b}{\bm{W}}_{\rm b}\|_{2}-1),

which has the following partial derivatives using the
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\rm ab}
notations for the cross-products

\displaystyle\nabla_{{\bm{w}}_{\rm a}}\mathcal{L}=

\displaystyle{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\rm ab}{\bm{w}}_{\rm b}-2\lambda_{1}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\rm aa}{\bm{w}}_{\rm a}

\displaystyle\nabla_{{\bm{w}}_{\rm b}}\mathcal{L}=

\displaystyle{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\rm ba}{\bm{w}}_{\rm a}-2\lambda_{2}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\rm bb}{\bm{w}}_{\rm b}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\lambda_{1}}=

\displaystyle{\bm{w}}_{\rm a}^{T}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\rm aa}{\bm{w}}_{\rm a}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\lambda_{2}}=

\displaystyle{\bm{w}}_{\rm b}^{T}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\rm bb}{\bm{w}}_{\rm b},

the first thing to notice is that setting those (first two) to zero will lead to
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}
which we thus denote as a single
\lambda
parameter since

\displaystyle{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\rm ab}{\bm{w}}_{\rm b}-2\lambda_{1}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\rm aa}{\bm{w}}_{\rm a}=0\implies{\bm{w}}_{\rm a}^{T}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\rm ab}{\bm{w}}_{\rm b}-2\lambda_{1}{\bm{w}}_{\rm a}^{T}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\rm aa}{\bm{w}}_{\rm a}=0\implies\lambda_{1}=\frac{1}{2}{\bm{w}}_{\rm a}^{T}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\rm ab}{\bm{w}}_{\rm b}

\displaystyle{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\rm ba}{\bm{w}}_{\rm a}-2\lambda_{2}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\rm bb}{\bm{w}}_{\rm b}=0\implies{\bm{w}}_{\rm b}^{T}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\rm ba}{\bm{w}}_{\rm a}-2\lambda_{2}{\bm{w}}_{\rm b}^{T}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\rm bb}{\bm{w}}_{\rm b}=0\implies\lambda_{2}=\frac{1}{2}{\bm{w}}_{\rm b}^{T}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\rm ba}{\bm{w}}_{\rm a},

and thus we can simplify the original system using a single Lagrangian multiplier to have th following system

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{r}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\rm ab}{\bm{w}}_{\rm b}-2\lambda{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\rm aa}{\bm{w}}_{\rm a}=0\\
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\rm ba}{\bm{w}}_{\rm a}-2\lambda{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\rm bb}{\bm{w}}_{\rm b}=0\end{array}\Big\}\begin{array}[]{r}{\bm{w}}_{\rm a}=\frac{1}{2\lambda}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\rm aa}^{-1}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\rm ab}{\bm{w}}_{\rm b}\\
(\frac{1}{4}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\rm bb}^{-1}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\rm ba}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\rm aa}^{-1}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\rm ab}-\lambda^{2}{\bm{I}}){\bm{w}}_{\rm b}=\mathbf{0}\end{array}\Big\},

where it is clear that one obtains
{\bm{w}}_{\rm b}
by solving the generalized eigenvalue problem and then finds
{\bm{w}}_{\rm a}
accordingly and this can be done by remove the
\frac{1}{4}
factor and replacing
2\lambda
by
\lambda
. For specific implementations of the above, we direct the reader to
Healy 1957
,
Ewerbring and Luk 1989
,
Hardoon et al. 2004
.
