Manuel González, Fernando León-Saavedra, María Pilar Romero de la Rosa

Abstract
A continuous linear operator L L defined on the space of entire functions ℋ ⁡ ( ℂ ) \mathcal{H}({\mathbb{C}}) is said to be an extended λ \lambda -eigenoperator of the differentiation operator D D provided D ​ L = λ ​ L ​ D DL=\lambda LD . Here we fully characterize when an extended λ \lambda -eigenoperator of D D is supercyclic, it has a hypercyclic subspace or it has a supercyclic subspace.



1
Introduction

A (continuous, linear) operator
T
acting on a separable Fréchet space
\mathcal{X}
is said to be
hypercyclic
if there exists
f\in\mathcal{X}
such that
\{T^{n}f\}_{n\geq 1}
is dense in
\mathcal{X}
.
In this case we say that the vector
f
is hypercyclic for
T
. To say that
T
is
supercyclic
means that there exists a vector
x\in\mathcal{X}
such that the projective orbit
\{\lambda T^{n}x\,\,:\,\,n\in\mathbb{N},\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\}
is dense in
\mathcal{X}
.
The operator
T
is said to have a
hypercyclic (supercyclic) subspace
if there exists an infinite dimensional closed subspace whose non-zero vectors are hypercyclic (supercyclic) for
T
. The study of hypercyclic (supercyclic) subspaces is a mainstream research line in the theory of hypercyclic operators (see
[
11
]
Chapter 10,
[
2
]
Chapter 8,
[
18
]
), and its interest come from the invariant subspace problem.

Over a century ago, Birkhoff
[
5
]
proved that the translation operator is hypercyclic on
\mathcal{H}({\mathbb{C}})
endowed with the compact open topology. Later, MacLane
[
16
]
proved that the differentiation operator is also hypercyclic. These seem to be the first examples of hypercyclic operators. Godefroy and Shapiro

[
9
]
unified the results of Birkhoff and MacLane by showing that each non-scalar operator
A
(i.e.,
A\neq\lambda I
) commuting with
D
is hypercyclic.

An operator
T
is said to
\lambda
-commute with
A
provided
AT=\lambda TA
, where
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
. If
T\neq 0
, then
T
is called an
extended
\lambda
-eigenoperator
of
A
with
extended eigenvalue

\lambda
.
Extended
\lambda
-eigenoperators appear in the proof of an extension of Lomonosov’s famous result, which was obtained independently by Scott Brown and Kim, Moore and Pearcy
[
7
,
12
]
. They proved that if an operator has a compact extended
\lambda
-eigenoperator then it has a non-trivial hyperinvariant subspace.
The determination of the extended
\lambda
-eigenoperators plays an important role in the study of an operator, and some properties, like hypercyclicity, are transferred in some way to the commutant (see
[
9
]
).

In this paper we study how the hypercyclic properties are transferred to the
\lambda
-commutant. Some results in
[
3
]
reveal that the hypercyclic properties for the extended
\lambda
-eigenoperators of
D
enjoy a rich structure.

For the differentiation operator
D
on
\mathcal{H}({\mathbb{C}})
, Bonilla and Grosse-Erdmann studied how frequent hypercyclicity is transferred to the commutant of
D
(see
[
6
]
). Recall that an operator
T
on a Fréchet space
\mathcal{X}
is said to be
frequently hypercyclic
if there exists a vector
x\in\mathcal{X}
such that for each nonempty open subset
U
of
\mathcal{X}
the set

N_{U}=\{n\in\mathbb{N}\,:\,T^{n}x\in U\}

has lower density in
\mathbb{N}
.
The study in
[
15
]
reveals that frequent hypercyclicity is not always transferred to the
\lambda
-commutant.

It has also been studied when an operator that commutes with
D
has an hypercyclic subspace. S. Shkarin
[
21
]
began by proving that
D
has a hypercyclic subspace.
Partial results were obtained by H. Petterson
[
20
]
and completed by Q. Mennet
[
17
]
, obtaining the so called Petersson-Menet-Shkarin’s result which asserts that each non-scalar operator commuting with
D
has a hypercyclic subspace. Thus the following question arises:

Question 1.
Assume that
L
is an extended
\lambda
-eigenoperator of

have a hypercyclic subspace?

In strong contrast with Petersson-Menet-Shkarin’s result, an extended

-eigenoperator

It is known from

2
Some preliminary results

Here we introduce some tools we will use along the paper. Let
\mathcal{X}
be a separable Fréchet space. Next result is a version of the Hypercyclicity Criterion discovered by Bès-Peris (see Theorem 3.24 in
[
11
]
).

Theorem 2.1

(Hypercyclicity criterion for sequences)
.

Let
(T_{n})
be a sequence of operators acting on
\mathcal{X}
. Suppose that there exist two dense subsets
X_{0},Y_{0}
of
\mathcal{X}
, a subsequence
(T_{n_{k}})
and a sequence of maps
S_{k}:Y_{0}\to\mathcal{X}
satisfying:

i)

T_{n_{k}}x_{0}\to 0
for all
x_{0}\in X_{0}
.

ii)

S_{k}y_{0}\to 0
and

iii)

T_{n_{k}}S_{k}y_{0}\to y_{0}
for all
y_{0}\in Y_{0}
.

Then there exists
x\in\mathcal{X}
such that the set
\{T_{n_{k}}x:k\in\mathbb{N}\}
is dense in
\mathcal{X}
.

We say that an operator
T
on
\mathcal{X}

satisfies the Hypercyclicity Criterion for the sequence
(n_{k})
if the hypothesis of Theorem
2.1
holds for the sequence of powers
(T^{n})
, and the sequence
(n_{k})
.

To obtain an infinite dimensional closed subspace of hypercyclic vectors, we need a stronger Hypercyclicity Criterion involving an infinite dimensional closed subspace
M_{0}
where we can control the orbits. Such a result appeared firstly in
[
10
]
in the Banach space setting. Here we will use the following Fréchet space extension by Petersson
[
20
]
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
(T_{n})
be a sequence of operators on a Fréchet space
\mathcal{X}
with a continuous norm. If
(T_{n})
satisfies the Hypercyclicity Criterion for a subsequence
(n_{k})
of
\mathbb{N}
and there exists an infinite dimensional closed subspace
M_{0}\subset\mathcal{X}
such that
T_{n_{k}}f\to 0
for all
f\in M_{0}
, then there is an infinite dimensional closed subspace
M_{1}
such that for every
x\in M_{1}\setminus\{0\}
the orbit
\{T_{n_{k}}x\}
is dense in
\mathcal{X}
.

The existence of the subspace
M_{0}
in the previous Theorem can be weakened, requiring only a control of the sequence of operators in a sequence of closed subspaces of decreasing infinite dimension. This weakening was proved firstly in the Banach space setting in
[
13
, Theorem 20]
. We will use the following extension for Fréchet spaces discovered by Q. Menet (see
[
17
]
, Theorem 1.11 and Remark 1.12).

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
T
be an operator on
\mathcal{X}
satisfying the Hypercyclicity Criterion for
(n_{k})
, and let
(\rho_{n})
be an increasing sequence of seminorms defining the topology of
\mathcal{X}
. If there exists a decreasing sequence of infinite dimensional closed subspaces
(M_{j})
such that, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we can find
C_{n}>0
and
m(n),k(n)\in\mathbb{N}
so that for each
j\geq k(n)
and
x\in M_{j}
,

\rho_{n}(T^{n_{j}}x)\leq C_{n}\rho_{m(n)}(x),

then
T
has a hypercyclic subspace.

Remark 2.4
.

Theorem
2.3
remains true replacing the sequence
(T^{n_{k}})
by a sequence of operators
(T_{n_{k}})
(see
[
17
, Theorem 1.11 and Remark 1.12]
).

To prove that a hypercyclic operator on Banach spaces does not have a hypercyclic subspace, it suffices to inspect its essential spectrum (see
[
10
]
). For sequences of operators on Banach spaces, the result by González-León-Montes (
[
10
]
) was refined by León-Muller in
[
13
]
(Theorem 22 and Corolary 23). For sequences of operators defined on Fréchet spaces this problem is more delicate and much more complex. The following sufficient condition to prove the nonexistence of hypercyclic subspaces was discovered by Q. Menet in
[
17
]
(Theorem 2.2 and Corollary 2.3).

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
T
be an operator on a Fréchet space
\mathcal{X}
with a continuous norm. Assume that there exist a sequence of seminorms
(\rho_{n})
defining the topology of
\mathcal{X}
and
N\geq 1
such that for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there exist
C_{n}>1
, a closed subspace
N_{n}
of finite codimension such that

\rho_{N}(T^{n}x)\geq C_{n}\rho_{n}(x)\textrm{ for $x\in N_{n}$.}

Then
T
has no hypercyclic subspace.

3
Hypercyclic subspaces for extended eigenoperators of
D

We begin by describing some results obtained in
[
3
]
where it was characterized when an extended
\lambda
-eigenoperator of
D
is hypercyclic. First of all, the extended
\lambda
-eigenoperators of
D
were described as follows:

Proposition 3.1
.

(
[
3
, Proposition 2.3]
)

Let
L
be an operator on
\mathcal{H}({\mathbb{C}})
.
Then
DL=\lambda LD
for some
0\neq\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
if an only if

L=R_{\lambda}\phi(D)
,
with
R_{\lambda}f(z)=f(\lambda z)
for
z\in\mathbb{C}
and
\phi
an entire function of exponential type.

From Proposition
3.1
, it was derived that if
\phi
has no zeros, then the operator
R_{\lambda}\phi(D)
is a multiple of a composition operator induced by an affine endomorphisms:
C_{\lambda,b}f(z)=f(\lambda z+b)
. The main result in
[
3
]
characterizes when
R_{\lambda}\phi(D)
is hypercyclic:

Theorem 3.2
.

(
[
3
]
)

For an operator
L
on
\mathcal{H}({\mathbb{C}})
satisfying
DL=\lambda LD
,
\lambda\neq 1
, the following conditions are equivalent:

1.

L
is hypercyclic.

2.

L
satisfies the Hypercyclicity Criterion.

3.

|\lambda|\geq 1
and
L
is not a multiple of the operator
C_{\lambda,b}f(z)=f(\lambda z+b)
.

Now we can restate our first main result as follows:

Main result 1.
Let
L=R_{\lambda}\phi(D)
be an extended
\lambda
-eigenoperator of
D
. Then the following conditions are equivalent:

1.

L
has an hypercyclic subspace.

2.

\phi
has infinitely many zeros and
|\lambda|\geq 1
, or
\phi
has a non-empty finite number of zeros and
|\lambda|=1
.

The proof splits into several cases which we will study separately. Next we consider the case when
\lambda
is a root of the unity.

Theorem 3.3
.

Assume that
\lambda
is a root of the unity. If
DL=\lambda LD
and
L
is not a multiple of
C_{\lambda,b}
then
L
has a hypercyclic subspace.

Proof.

If
\lambda^{n_{0}}=1
for some
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
then
R_{\lambda}^{n_{0}}=I
. Since

L=R_{\lambda}\phi(D)
is not a multiple of
C_{\lambda,b}
,
\phi
has some zero,
L^{n_{0}}
is not a multiple of the identity and
L^{n_{0}}
commutes with
D
.
Thus, since by Menet-Petterson-Shkarin’s result (see
[
17
,
20
,
21
]
) each non-scalar operator commuting with the differentiation operator has a hypercyclic subspace, we get that
L^{n_{0}}
has a hypercyclic subspace. Hence
L
has a hypercyclic subspace as we desired.
∎

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
L=R_{\lambda}\phi(D)
be an extended
\lambda
-eigenoperator of
D
. If
L
is hypercyclic and
\phi
has infinitely many zeros then
L
has a hypercyclic subspace.

Proof.

By Theorem
3.2
,
L
satisfies the Hypercyclicity Criterion for some subsequence
(n_{k})
of
\mathbb{N}
.
If
a
is a zero of
\phi
then
Le^{az}=\varphi(a)e^{a\lambda z}=0
.
Since
\phi
has infinitely many zeros,
\textrm{Ker}(L)
is infinite dimensional. Since the sequence
(L^{n})
converges trivially to zero on
\textrm{Ker}(L)
, by Theorem
2.2

L
has a hypercyclic subspace as we desired.
∎

Proposition
3.4
reduces our problem to the case that
\phi
is an entire function of exponential type which only has finitely many zeros.
By Hadamard factorization Theorem,
\phi(z)=P(z)e^{bz}
, where
P(z)
is a polynomial. This case includes the operators
T_{\lambda,b}
studied in
[
1
]
.
By Theorem
3.2
, it is sufficient to study the case
|\lambda|\geq 1
.
Since the existence of a hypercyclic subspace is invariant under similarity, our next result simplifies the problem.

Proposition 3.5
.

Set
\lambda\neq 1
. The operator
R_{\lambda}P(D)e^{bD}
is similar to
R_{\lambda}P(D)
.

Proof.

Set
\alpha=\frac{b}{1-\lambda}
. It is easy to check that
e^{-\alpha D}R_{\lambda}=R_{\lambda}e^{-\lambda\alpha D}
. Hence

e^{\alpha D}R_{\lambda}P(D)e^{\alpha D}=R_{\lambda}e^{-\lambda\alpha D}P(D)e^{\alpha D}=R_{\lambda}P(D)e^{\alpha(1-\lambda)D}=R_{\lambda}P(D)e^{bD},

and the result is proved.
∎

For
f(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}z^{k}\in\mathcal{H}({\mathbb{C}})
, we define

\rho_{M}(f)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}|a_{k}|M^{k}
.
The family of seminorms
\{\rho_{M}:M>0\}
induces the natural topology of
\mathcal{H}({\mathbb{C}})
.

The case
\lambda=1
of the following result was proved by Menet
[
17
]
.

Theorem 3.6
.

If
L=R_{\lambda}P(D)
with
|\lambda|=1
and
P
is a non-constant polynomial, then
L
has an hypercyclic subspace.

Proof.

By Theorem
3.2
,
L=R_{\lambda}P(D)
satisfies the Hypercyclicity Criterion for some subsequence
(n_{k})
, (moreover by
[
15
]
, the operator
L
satisfies the Hypercyclicity Criterion for the full sequence of natural numbers).

Let us denote
\omega=\lambda^{-1}
and
P(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{d}b_{k}z^{k}
, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
we write

P(\omega z)P(\omega^{2}z)\cdots P(\omega^{n}z)=\sum_{k=0}^{nd}b_{k}^{(n)}z^{k}

and set
\widetilde{C_{n}}=\max\{|b_{k}^{(r)}|\,:\,1\leq r\leq n\,,\,1\leq k\leq dr\}
.
Then

\displaystyle(R_{\lambda}P(D))^{n}(z^{s})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle R_{\lambda}P(D)\cdots R_{\lambda}P(D)(z^{s})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P(\omega D)\cdots P(\omega^{n}D)R_{\lambda}^{n}(z^{s})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{nd}b_{k}^{(n)}[s(s-1)\cdots(s-k+1)]\lambda^{ns}z^{s-k}.

Thus, if
|z|=M\geq 1
,

|(R_{\lambda}P(D))^{n}(z^{s})|\leq\sum_{k=0}^{nd}|b_{k}^{(n)}|[s(s-1)\cdots(s-k+1)]M^{s-k}\leq\widetilde{C_{n}}nds^{nd}M^{s}.

Clearly, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
we can select
M_{n}>0
such that

\widetilde{C_{n}}ndx^{dn}\leq 2^{x}

for
x\geq M_{n}
. We consider a strictly increasing sequence
(n_{j})
in
\mathbb{N}
with
n_{j+1}\geq M_{n_{j}}
. Thus if
s\geq j+1
then

\widetilde{C_{n_{j}}}n_{j}dn_{s}^{n_{j}d}\leq 2^{n_{s}}.

(1)

If
f\in N_{j}=\overline{\textrm{linearspan}}\{z^{n_{s}}\,:\,s\geq j+1\}
, then
f(z)=\sum_{s=j+1}a_{s}z^{n_{s}}
and

\displaystyle\rho_{M}(L^{n_{j}}f)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rho_{M}\left(L^{n_{j}}\left(\sum_{s=j+1}^{\infty}a_{s}z^{n_{s}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rho_{M}\left(\sum_{s=j+1}^{\infty}a_{s}L^{n_{j}}z^{n_{s}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{s=j+1}^{\infty}|a_{s}|n_{j}d\widetilde{C_{n_{j}}}n_{s}^{n_{j}}M^{n_{s}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{s=j+1}|a_{s}|(2M)^{n_{s}}=\rho_{2M}(f),

where the last inequality follows from equation (
1
). Thus Theorem
2.3
is fulfilled if we consider the sequence of seminorms
(\rho_{n})
and we take
m(n)=2n
,
C_{n}=k(n)=1
. Thus if
p(D)
is not a multiple of the identity and
|\lambda|=1
then
L=R_{\lambda}P(D)
has a hypercyclic subspace, as we desired to prove.
∎

The most intriguing case is when
|\lambda|>1
and
\phi
has a finite number of zeros; or just
\phi(D)=a_{d}D^{d}+\cdots a_{0}I
is a polynomial. The main idea of the proof is basically to show that the significant term of the action of
L^{n}
(on finite codimensional subspaces) is concentrate on
a_{d}D^{d}
. If we set

N_{n}=\{f\in\mathcal{H}({\mathbb{C}})\,:\,f(0)=f^{\prime}(0)=\cdots=f^{(n)}(0)=0\},

by bounding the derivative on a line, it is simple to show that there exist
c>0
and
r>0
such that for any
f\in N_{d}

\rho_{r}(Lf)\geq c|\lambda|^{d}\rho_{r}(f^{(d)}(\lambda z)).

However this idea of boundedness is not enough for the asymptotic inequality that is needed. We stress here that the following asymptotic formula relies on taking finite codimensional subspaces
N_{n}
for
n
large enough.

Lemma 3.7
.

Suppose that
L=R_{\lambda}P(D)
with
P
a polynomial of degree
d\geq 1
. Set
M>0
. There exist a constant
c>0
and an increasing sequence
(m_{n})\subset\mathbb{N}
such that for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
h\in N_{m_{n}}

\rho_{M}(L^{n}h)\geq c^{n}1\cdot|\lambda|^{d}\cdot|\lambda|^{2d}\cdots|\lambda|^{(n-1)d}\rho_{M}(h^{(nd)}(\lambda^{n}z)).

(2)

Proof.

We can assume that
L=P(D)R_{\lambda}
and
P(D)=\sum_{k=0}^{d}a_{k}D^{k}
.

For
n=1
, set
c>0
satisfying
|a_{d}|>c
, and let
h\in N_{m}
with
m
(large enough) to be determined later. We write

\rho_{M}(Lh)\geq\rho_{M}(a_{d}D^{d}R_{\lambda}h)-\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}|a_{k}|\rho_{M}(D^{k}R_{\lambda}h).

If
h(\lambda z)=\sum_{n=m}^{\infty}h_{n}z^{n}
, let us check the above formula in each
z^{p}
for
p\geq m
. Since

\xi_{M,d}(p)=\frac{|a_{0}|M^{d}+|a_{1}|pM^{d-1}+\dots+|a_{p-1}|p\cdots(p-d+2)M}{p(p-1)\cdots(p-d+1)}\to 0

as
p\to\infty
, there exists
m_{1}>0
such that for
p\geq m_{1}
,
|a_{d}|-\xi_{M,d}(p)>c
. Thus

\frac{p\cdots(p-d+1)}{M^{d}}\left[|a_{d}||h_{p}|M^{p}-\xi_{M,d}(p)|h_{p}|M^{p}\right]\geq cp\cdots(p-d+1)|h_{p}|M^{p-d},

and for each
f\in N_{m_{1}}
we get

\displaystyle\rho_{M}(Lh)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\rho_{M}(a_{d}D^{d}R_{\lambda}h)-\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}|a_{k}|\rho_{M}(D^{k}R_{\lambda}h)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle c\rho_{M}(D^{d}h(\lambda z))=c|\lambda|^{d}\rho_{M}(h^{(d)}(\lambda z)).

The proof for arbitrary
n
is similar: if
L^{n}=p(D)p(\omega D)\cdots p(\omega^{n-1}D)R_{\lambda}^{n}
, then

\displaystyle\rho_{M}(L^{n}h)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rho_{M}(p(D)p(\omega D)\cdots p(\omega^{n-1}D)R_{\lambda}^{n}h)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1\cdot|\omega|^{d}\cdots|\omega|^{d(n-1)}\rho_{M}(\widehat{P}(D)R_{\lambda}^{n}h),

where
\widehat{P}(D)
is a polynomial whose leader term is
|a_{d}|^{n}D^{nd}
. Thus, by arguing as in the first step, there exists
m_{n}
such that for any
h\in N_{m_{n}}
we get

\displaystyle\rho_{M}(L^{n}h)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1\cdot|\omega|^{d}\cdots|\omega|^{d(n-1)}\rho_{M}(\widehat{P}(D)R_{\lambda}^{n}h)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle c^{n}1\cdot|\omega|^{d}\cdots|\omega|^{d(n-1)}\rho_{M}(D^{nd}R_{\lambda}^{n}h)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c^{n}1\cdot|\omega|^{d}\cdots|\omega|^{d(n-1)}|\lambda|^{nd}\cdots|\lambda|^{nd}\rho_{M}(h^{(nd)}(\lambda^{n}z))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c^{n}|\lambda|^{d}|\lambda|^{2d}\cdots|\lambda^{nd}|\rho_{M}(h^{(nd)}(\lambda^{n}z)),

and the result is proved.
∎

Remark 3.8
.

For future reference, we remark that the asymptotic inequality
(
2
) remains true for any subsequence
(r_{n})
of
(m_{n})
. This fact will be crucial in the proof of Theorem
5.5
.

The following result completes the proof of Main result 1.
Surprisingly there are hypercyclic extended
\lambda
-eigenoperators of
D
such that all closed subspaces of hypercyclic vectors have finite dimension.

Theorem 3.9
.

If
L=R_{\lambda}\phi(D)
with
|\lambda|>1
and
\phi
has finitely many zeros, then each closed subspace of hypercyclic vectors for
L
is finite dimensional.

Proof.

We can assume that
L=P(D)R_{\lambda}
with
P(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{d}b_{k}z^{k}
non-constant. By applying Lemma
3.7
there exist a constant
c>0
and an increasing sequence
(m_{n})\subset\mathbb{N}
with
m_{n}\geq nd
such that for any
h\in N_{m_{n}}

\rho_{1}(L^{n}h)\geq c^{n}1\cdot|\lambda|^{d}\cdot|\lambda|^{2d}\cdots|\lambda|^{(n-1)d}\rho_{1}(h^{nd)}(\lambda^{n}z)).

(3)

Let us consider the seminorms
p_{0}(h)=\max_{|z|\leq 1}|h(z)|
, and

p_{n}(h)=\max_{|z|\leq|\lambda|^{n/4}}|h(z)|.

Then
(p_{n})
is an increasing sequence of seminorms that defines the original topology of
\mathcal{H}({\mathbb{C}})
.
Moreover, since
m_{n}\geq nd
, for
h\in N_{m_{n}}
we get

\displaystyle\rho_{1}(h^{(nd)}(\lambda^{n}z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{|z|\leq 1}|h^{(nd)}(\lambda^{n}z)|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{|w|\leq|\lambda|^{n}}|h^{(nd)}(w)|

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\max_{|w|\leq|\lambda|^{n/4}}|h^{nd)}(w)|

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle|\lambda|^{-dn^{2}/4}\max_{|w|\leq|\lambda|^{n/4}}|h(w)|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\lambda|^{-dn^{2}/4}p_{n}(h).

By setting
C_{n}=c^{n}|\lambda|^{\frac{dn(n-1)}{2}}|\lambda|^{-dn^{2}/4}
and using the above inequality in equation (

), for

we obtain

p_{0}(L^{n}h)\geq c^{n}|\lambda|^{\frac{dn(n-1)}{2}}|\lambda|^{-n^{2}/4}p_{n}(h)=C_{n}p_{n}(h)

with

. Thus, by Theorem
2.5
,

has no hypercyclic subspaces.
∎

4
Supercyclic extended eigenoperators of
D

Let
L
be an operator on
H(\mathbb{C})
satisfying
DL=\lambda LD
with
\lambda\neq 1
. In this section we study when
L
is supercyclic.
In this direction, it was proved in
[
4
, Theorem 3.6]
that no multiple of
C_{\lambda,b}
is supercyclic for
\lambda\neq 1
. The study of hypercyclicity of the extended
\lambda
-eigenoperators of
D
defined by:
T_{\lambda,b}f=f^{\prime}(\lambda z+b)
were studied in
[
1
,
8
,
14
]
. However, the supercyclicty of such operators, as far we know, has not been studied.
Our second main result solves this question and provides a complete answer to Question 2 in the introduction. Surprisingly enough, the characterization depends of the value of
\phi
at the origin.

Let us denote by
HC
(and
SC
) the subset of all hypercyclic operators (supercyclic operators) defined on
\mathcal{H}({\mathbb{C}})
.

Main result 2.
Assume that
L=R_{\lambda}\phi(D)
is an extended
\lambda
-eigenoperator of
D
. The following conditions are equivalent:

1.

L\in SC\setminus HC
.

2.

|\lambda|<1
,
\phi(0)=0
.

We point out that if
L=R_{\lambda}\phi(D)
with
\lambda\neq 1
and
\phi
has no zeros, then (see Proposition 2.3
[
3
]
)
L
is a multiple of
C_{\lambda,b}
for some
b\in\mathbb{C}
, hence by Bernal-Bonilla-Calderón
[
4
]
,
L
is not supercyclic. According to the results in
[
3
]
, the only extended
\lambda
-eigenoperator which are not hypercyclic and could be supercyclic are the extended
\lambda
-eigenoperators associated to
|\lambda|<1
. Thus, we can concentrate on the cases with
|\lambda|<1
and
\phi^{-1}\{0\}\neq\emptyset
.

The proof of the second main theorem splits into two cases. First we analyze the case
\varphi(0)=0
and
|\lambda|<1
, which includes the Aron-Markose operators. And then we study the case
|\lambda|<1
and
\phi(0)\neq 0
, in which surprisingly the extended
\lambda
-eigenoperators of
D
are not supercyclic.

Theorem 4.1
.

If
L=R_{\lambda}\phi(D)
with
|\lambda|<1
and
\phi(0)=0
, then
L
is supercyclic.

Proof.

It is enough to find a sequence of real numbers
(\lambda_{n})
such that the sequence of operators
(\lambda_{n}L^{n})
satisfies the conditions of Theorem
2.1
.

We write
\phi(z)=z^{m}\psi(z)
with
\psi(0)\neq 0
, and set
A_{\lambda}=R_{\lambda}\psi(D)
, so that
L=A_{\lambda}D^{m}
. Since
\psi(0)\neq 0
, the polynomials of degree less or equal than
n
form an invariant subspace for
A_{\lambda}
. Moreover, the operator
A_{\lambda}
has a triangular matrix representation with diagonal entries
\psi(0)\lambda^{k}
,
k=0,1,\cdots,n
; hence the eigenvalues of the matrix are simple. Let
p_{k}
(
0\leq k\leq n
) be the polynomial of degree
k

such that
A_{\lambda}p_{k}=\psi(0)\lambda^{k}p_{k}
for
k\geq 0
.
Since
DR_{\lambda}=\lambda R_{\lambda}D
we get:

\displaystyle L^{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle R_{\lambda}D^{m}\psi(D)\cdots R_{\lambda}D^{m}\psi(D)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda^{m}\lambda^{2m}\cdots\lambda^{(k-1)m}R_{\lambda}\psi(D)\cdots R_{\lambda}\psi(D)D^{km}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda^{m}\lambda^{2m}\cdots\lambda^{(k-1)m}A_{\lambda}^{k}D^{km}.

Set
\lambda_{k}=(\lambda^{m}\cdots\lambda^{m(k-1)})^{-1}
and let us denote
T_{k}=\lambda_{k}L^{k}
.
Since
L^{k}p(z)=0
for
km>\textrm{deg}(p)
, the sequence
(\lambda_{k}L^{k})
converges pointwise to zero on the set of polynomials
X_{0}=\textrm{linearspan}\{p_{k}(z)\,\,:\,k\geq 0\}
, which is dense in
\mathcal{H}({\mathbb{C}})
.
Let
V
be the complex Volterra operator defined by

Vf(z)=\int_{0}^{z}f(\xi)d\xi,\quad(z\in\mathbb{C}).

Since
LV^{m}p_{k}=A_{\lambda}p_{k}=\psi(0)\lambda^{k}p_{k}
, we can define

S_{k}p_{n}=\frac{V^{mk}p_{n}}{(\psi(0)\lambda^{n})^{k}},

and we extend
S_{k}
to
X_{0}
by linearity. It is easy to check that
\lambda_{k}T_{k}S_{k}=\textrm{Id}_{X_{0}}
.

Finally, for
n_{0}
fixed,
S_{k}p_{n_{0}}(z)\to 0
uniformly on compact subsets; hence
S_{k}
converges pointwise to zero on
X_{0}
. Thus, by Theorem
2.1
, there exists
f\in\mathcal{H}({\mathbb{C}})
such that
\{\lambda_{k}L^{k}f\}_{k\geq 0}
is dense in
\mathcal{H}({\mathbb{C}})
, hence
L
is supercyclic.
∎

The proof of the next result completes the characterization of supercyclicity for extended
\lambda
-eigenoperators of
D
.
We highlight here the difficulty of proving the non-supercyclicity of an operator.

Theorem 4.2
.

Assume that
|\lambda|<1
and
\phi(0)\neq 0
then the operator
L=R_{\lambda}\phi(D)
is not supercyclic.

Proof.

Assume that
f\in\mathcal{H}({\mathbb{C}})
is supercyclic for
L
.
Thus, given

g(z)=e^{z}
, there exists an increasing subsequence of natural numbers
\{n_{k}\}
and a sequence of scalars
\{\lambda_{n_{k}}\}\subset\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
such that

\lambda_{n_{k}}(L^{n_{k}}f)(z)\to e^{z}\quad\textrm{as}\quad n_{k}\to\infty

uniformly on compact subsets of
\mathbb{C}
. Moreover, since
D^{m}e^{z}=e^{z}
, for each
m\in\mathbb{N}

\lambda_{n_{k}}D^{m}(L^{n_{k}}f)(z)\to e^{z}\quad\textrm{as}\quad n_{k}\to\infty

uniformly on compact subsets of
\mathbb{C}
. In particular,

\lambda_{n_{k}}(D^{m}L^{n_{k}}f)(z_{0})\to e^{z_{0}}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\lambda_{n_{k}}(L^{n_{k}}f)(0)\to 1\quad\textrm{as}\quad n_{k}\to\infty.

(4)

From (
4
) we get that

\frac{(D^{m}L^{n_{k}}f)(z_{0})}{(L^{n_{k}}f)(0)}\to e^{z_{0}}\quad\textrm{as}\quad\textrm{as}\quad n_{k}\to\infty.

(5)

This unusual idea of applying
D^{m}
to the previous sequence is crucial and central in the proof. It really simplifies everything.
We will show that we can choose
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\frac{(D^{m}L^{k}f)(z_{0})}{(L^{k}f)(0)}\rightarrow 0\quad k\to\infty,

(6)

which contradicts (
5
).

To show (
6
) we will use two different integral representations of the operator
L
. It was shown in
[
3
, Theorem 3.3]
that there exists a Borel measure
\mu
with compact support in
\mathbb{C}
such that

(L^{k}f)(z)=\int\cdots\int f\left(\lambda^{k}z+\lambda^{k-1}w_{1}+\cdots+w_{k}\right)d\mu(w_{k})\cdots d\mu(w_{1}).

Now, since the support of
\mu
is contained in some disc
D(0,R)
and

|\lambda^{k}z+\lambda^{k-1}w_{1}+\cdots+w_{k}|\leq M(|z|)=|\lambda|^{k}|z|+\frac{1-|\lambda|^{k}}{1-|\lambda|}R

for
|z|<r
, we get that each element in the argument of
f
lies in the disk
D(0,M(r))
. Therefore, for
f\in\mathcal{H}({\mathbb{C}})
, if
|z|\leq r
then

|L^{k}f(z)|\leq\sup_{|z^{\prime}|=M(r)}|f(z^{\prime})|\|\mu\|^{k},

where
\|\mu\|
denotes the total variation of the measure
\mu
.
On the other hand, since
DL=\lambda LD
, for
|z|\leq r
we get

|(D^{m}L^{k}f)(z)|=|\lambda|^{mk}|(L^{k})D^{m}f(z)|\leq|\lambda|^{mk}\|\mu\|^{k}\max_{|z|=M(r)}|D^{m}f(z)|.

(7)

Next we obtain lower estimates of
|(L^{k}f)(0)|
. Indeed, let us observe that if
\omega=\lambda^{-1}
then

\displaystyle L^{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle R_{\lambda}\phi(D)R_{\lambda}\phi(D)\cdots R_{\lambda}\phi(D)

(8)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\phi(\omega D)\phi(\omega^{2}D)\cdots\phi(\omega^{k}D)R_{\lambda}^{k}.

(9)

We consider the sequence of entire functions
\Phi_{k}(z)=\phi(\omega z)\cdots\phi(\omega^{k}z)
with series expansions

\Phi_{k}(z)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{a_{m}^{(k)}}{m!}z^{m},

(10)

where
a_{m}^{(k)}=\Phi^{(m)}(0)
can be obtained by Leibnitz’s rule. Therefore

\displaystyle\left|\Phi_{k}^{(m)}(0)\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|[\phi(\omega z),\ldots,\phi(\omega^{k}z)]^{(m)}(0)\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{h_{1}+\ldots+h_{k}=m}\binom{m}{h_{1},\ldots,h_{k}}\prod_{t=1}^{k}(\phi(\omega^{t}z))^{(h_{t})}(0)\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{h_{1}+\ldots+h_{k}=m}\binom{m}{h_{1},\ldots,h_{k}}\prod_{t=1}^{k}|\omega^{th_{t}}(\phi^{(h_{t})}(0))|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{\rm C}^{m}(|\omega|+\ldots+|\omega|^{k})^{m},

with
C=\sup_{t\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}}|\phi^{(t)}(0)|
. Thus, for each
f\in\mathcal{H}({\mathbb{C}})

L^{k}f(z)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{a_{m}^{(k)}}{{m!}}D^{m}R_{\lambda}^{k}f(z)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{a_{m}^{(k)}}{{m!}}\lambda^{km}f^{(m)}(\lambda^{k}z).

Using the estimates of
a_{m}^{(k)}
previously obtained we get:

|L^{k}f(0)|=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{|a_{m}^{(k)}|}{{m!}}|\lambda^{km}||f^{(m)}(0)|\leq\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{K^{m}C^{m}}{m!}|f^{(m)}(0)|,

with
K
an upper bound of the bounded sequence
(|\lambda|^{k}[|\omega|+\cdots+|\omega|^{k}])_{k}
.

Thus, if
f=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{f^{(m)}(0)}{m!}z^{m}
is a supercyclic vector for
L
, we decompose
f(z)=(P_{m_{0}}f)(z)+(T_{m_{0}}f)(z)
, here
(P_{m_{0}}f)(z)
is the Taylor polynomial of degree
m_{0}
and
(T_{m_{0}}f)(z)
is the tail of the series.
By the above estimates we known that there exist
m_{0}
which depends only on
f
such that

|L^{k}(T_{m_{0}}f)(0)|\leq 1/4

(11)

uniformly on
k
.

On the other hand, we consider
L
acting on the invariant subspace
\{1,z,\cdots,z^{m_{0}}\}
. On such invariant subspace
L
is triangular with diagonal entries
\phi(0)\lambda^{k}
,
k=0,1,\cdots,m_{0}
. Since supercyclicity is invariant by multiplication by nonzero scalars, we can suppose without loss that
\phi(0)=1
.
Since the eigenvalues are different,
\{1,p_{1}(z),\cdots,p_{m_{0}}(z)\}
is a basis of the space
\mathcal{P}_{m_{0}}[z]
of polynomials of degree less or equal than
m_{0}
.
Therefore, there exist scalars
c_{0},c_{1},\cdots,c_{m_{0}}
such that
(P_{m_{0}}f)(z)=c_{0}1+c_{1}p_{1}(z)+\cdots+c_{m_{0}}p_{m_{0}}(z)
. Moreover, if
L
were supercyclic, then there would be a dense subset of supercyclic vectors. Thus,
we can suppose without loss that
c_{0}\neq 0
. Moreover since a non-zero multiple of a supercyclic vector is also supercyclic, we can suppose that
c_{0}=1
.
Therefore

L^{k}(P_{m_{0}}f)(z)=1+c_{1}\lambda^{k}p_{1}(z)+\cdots+c_{m_{0}}\lambda^{m_{0}k}p_{m_{0}}(z),

which implies that there exist
k_{0}
such that fo any
k\geq k_{0}
,

|c_{1}\lambda^{k}p_{1}(0)+\cdots+c_{m_{0}}\lambda^{m_{0}k}p_{m_{0}}(0)|<1/4.

That is:

|L^{k}P_{m_{0}}f(0)|\geq 3/4

(12)

for all
k\geq k_{0}
. Thus, by selecting
m
such that

|\lambda|^{m}<\|\mu\|
and using the estimates (
7
), (
11
) and (
12
), we get:

\displaystyle\left|\frac{(D^{m}L^{k}f)(z_{0})}{(L^{k}f)(0)}\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\frac{(D^{m}L^{k}f)(z_{0})}{|L^{k}P_{m_{0}}f(0)+L^{k}T_{m_{0}}f(0)|}\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{|\lambda|^{mk}\|\mu\|^{k}\max_{|z|=M(r)}|D^{m}f(z)|}{3/4-1/4}\rightarrow 0

as
k\to\infty
, which yields the desired result.
∎

5
Supercyclic subspaces

Let us donote by
HC_{\infty}
(respectively
SC_{\infty}
) the subset of operators having a closed infinite dimensional subspace whose non-zero elements are hypercyclic (respec. supercyclic).
In this section we characterize the extended
\lambda
-eigenoperators of
D
which belong to the subset
SC_{\infty}\setminus HC_{\infty}
.

If
|\lambda|=1
we know that
L=R_{\lambda}\phi(D)
has a hypercyclic subspace. Also, if
0<|\lambda|<1
and
\phi(0)\neq 0
then
L=R_{\lambda}\phi(D)
is not supercyclic. Finally, if
|\lambda|>1
and
\phi
has infinite zeros then
L=R_{\lambda}\phi(D)
has a hypercyclic subspace. Thus, the results in Sections
3
and
4
allow us to focus the study in the following cases: 1)
0<|\lambda|<1
and
\phi(0)=0
; and 2)
|\lambda|>1
and
\phi
has a finite number of zeros. We fully cover both cases obtaining the following characterization:

Main result 3.

Let
L
be an extended
\lambda
-eigenoperator of
D
,
\lambda\neq 1
. The following conditions are equivalent:

1.

L\in SC_{\infty}\setminus HC_{\infty}
.

2.

0<|\lambda|<1
and
\phi(0)=0
.

The proof of the next result uses some of the ideas applied in Proposition
3.4
and Theorem
3.6
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Assume that
L=R_{\lambda}\phi(D)
.
If
0<|\lambda|<1
and
\phi(0)=0
, then
L\in SC_{\infty}\setminus HC_{\infty}
.

Proof.

If
\phi(0)=0
then
\phi(z)=z^{m}\psi(z)
with
\psi(0)\neq 0
. By taking
\lambda_{k}=(\lambda^{m}\lambda^{2m}\cdots\lambda^{(k-1)m})^{-1}
as in the proof of Theorem
4.1
, we get that the sequence
(\lambda_{k}L^{k})
satisfies the Hypercyclicity Criterion (Theorem
2.1
).

If
\phi
has infinitely many zeros, then
M_{0}=\textrm{ker}(L)
is an infinite dimensional closed subspace and, for
f\in M_{0}
,
\lambda_{n}L^{n}f\to 0
uniformly on compact subsets.
Then by Theorem
2.2
we obtain that the sequence
\lambda_{n}L^{n}
has a hypercyclic subspace. Hence
L
has a supercyclic subspace.

Now, let us suppose that
\phi
has a finite number of zeros and
0<|\lambda|<1
. By Proposition
3.5
we can suppose without loss that
L=R_{\lambda}P(D)
with
P
a non-constant polynomial.

Now, we will rescue some ideas of Theorem
3.6
.
We set
\omega=\lambda^{-1}
, and we denote
P(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{d}b_{k}z^{k}
. For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
we write

P(\omega z)P(\omega^{2}z)\cdots P(\omega^{n}z)=\sum_{k=0}^{nd}b_{k}^{(n)}z^{k}.

Let us denote
\widetilde{C_{n}}=\max\{|b_{k}^{(r)}|\,:\,1\leq r\leq n\,,\,1\leq k\leq r\}
.
Then

\lambda_{n}(R_{\lambda}P(D))^{n}(z^{s})=\sum_{k=0}^{nd}b_{k}^{(n)}[s(s-1)\cdots(s-k+1)]\frac{\lambda^{ns}}{\lambda^{m}\cdots\lambda^{(n-1)m}}z^{s-k}.

Thus, for
|z|=M
, we get

|\lambda_{n}(R_{\lambda}P(D))^{n}(z^{s})|\leq\widetilde{C_{n}}nds^{nd}\left|\frac{\lambda^{ns}}{\lambda^{m}\cdots\lambda^{(n-1)m}}\right|M^{s}.

For each
n
we can find
M_{n}>0
such that, for
x\geq M_{n}
,

ndx^{dn}\leq 2^{x}\quad\textrm{ and }\quad\widetilde{C_{n}}\left|\frac{\lambda^{nx}}{\lambda^{m}\cdots\lambda^{(n-1)m}}\right|\leq 1.

Thus, we can construct inductively a strictly increasing sequence
(n_{j})
in
\mathbb{N}
such that
n_{j+1}\geq M_{n_{j}}
and, for
s>j
,

n_{j}dn_{s}^{n_{j}d}\leq 2^{n_{s}}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\widetilde{C_{n_{j}}}\left|\frac{\lambda^{n_{j}n_{s}}}{\lambda^{m}\cdots\lambda^{(n_{j}-1)m}}\right|\leq 1.

(13)

Hence, using the estimates (
13
), if
f\in N_{j}=\overline{\textrm{linearspan}}\{z^{n_{s}}\,:\,s>j\}
we have
f(z)=\sum_{s=j+1}a_{s}z^{n_{s}}
and

\displaystyle\rho_{M}(\lambda_{n_{j}}L^{n_{j}}f)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rho_{M}\left(L^{n_{j}}\left(\sum_{s=j+1}^{\infty}a_{s}z^{n_{s}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{s=j+1}|a_{s}|(2M)^{n_{s}}=\rho_{2M}(f).

Thus, the conditions of Theorem
2.3
are fulfilled if we consider the sequence of seminorms
\rho_{n}(f)
, and the sequences
m(n)=2n
and
C_{n}=k(n)=1
. Since the sequence of operators
(\lambda_{n}L^{n})
satisfies the Hypercyclicity Criterion (see Theorem
4.1
),
(\lambda_{n}L^{n})
has a hypercyclic subspace. Hence
L
has a supercyclic subspace as we desired to prove.
∎

Now, we turn our attention to the case
L=R_{\lambda}\phi(D)
with
|\lambda|>1
and
\phi
has a finite (non empty) number of zeros. We proved in Theorem
3.9
that in such a case all closed subspaces of hypercyclic vectors for
L
has finite dimension. And the question now is the following: by relaxing hypercyclicity by supercyclicity, could we obtain an infinite dimensional closed subspace
M_{1}
such that
x\in M_{1}\setminus\{0\}
is supercyclic for
L
?

We discard the case in which
\phi(z)
has no zeros, because in such a case
L=R_{\lambda}\phi(D)
is a multiple of the composition operator
C_{\lambda,b}f(z)=f(\lambda z+b)
, which is not supercyclic.
Thus we focus our attention in the case that
\phi
has a finite (nonempty) number of zeros, and by Proposition
3.5
again we can suppose that
L=R_{\lambda}P(D)
with
P
a non constant polynomial.

Let us point out that proving that an operator is not supercyclic is a more complicated task than proving that it is, as we can see in the work of A. Montes and H.N. Salas (
[
18
]
), the proofs of the non-existence of supercyclic subspaces are even more sophisticated. In our case, the refinements are enhanced by Fréchet spaces context and the structure of the involved operators.

We will use the following lemma, whose proof can be founded in
[
11
, Lemma 10.39]
, and we refer to
[
19
]
for a proof in the Banach space setting.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
X
be a Fréchet space,
F
a finite-dimensional subspace of
X
,
\rho
a continuous seminorm on
X
and
\varepsilon>0
. Then there exists a closed subspace
H
of finite codimension such that for any
x\in F
and
y\in H

\rho(x+y)\geq\max\left\{\frac{\rho(x)}{1+\varepsilon},\frac{\rho(y)}{2+\varepsilon}\right\}.

Our argument hinges on the following computations.

Lemma 5.3
.

Assume that
m>nd
. If
N_{n}=\{f\in\mathcal{H}({\mathbb{C}})\,:\,f^{k)}(0)=0,k\leq m-1\}
, then for any
f\in N_{n}
:

\rho_{1}(f^{(nd)}(\lambda^{n}z))\geq\frac{m!}{(m-nd)!}|\lambda|^{m\frac{n}{2}-n^{2}d}\rho_{|\lambda|^{n/2}}(f)

(14)

Proof.

If
f\in N_{m}
,
f(z)=\sum_{p=m}^{\infty}a_{p}z^{p}
, then

\displaystyle\rho_{1}(f^{(nd)}(\lambda^{n}z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{p=m}^{\infty}|a_{p}|p(p-1)\cdots(p-nd+1)|\lambda|^{n(p-nd)}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\lambda|^{n^{2}d}}\sum_{p=m}^{\infty}|a_{p}|\frac{p!}{(p-nd)!}(|\lambda|^{n/2}|\lambda|^{n/2})^{p}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{|\lambda|^{mn/2}}{|\lambda|^{n^{2}d}}\frac{m!}{(m-nd)!}\rho_{|\lambda|^{n/2}}(f)

which proves inequality (
14
) as we desired.
∎

Lemma 5.4
.

Assume that
L=R_{\lambda}P(D)
, with
d=\textrm{grad}(P)
.
For each
f\in\mathcal{H}({\mathbb{C}})
,
there is a constant
B>0
such that

|L^{n}f(0)|\leq B^{n}\frac{((d+1)n-1)!}{(n-1)!}|\lambda|^{d}|\lambda|^{2d}\cdots|\lambda|^{(n-1)d}\rho_{1}(f).

(15)

Proof.

Observe that

L^{n}=R_{\lambda}P(D)\cdots R_{\lambda}P(D)=\widehat{P_{n}(D)}R_{\lambda}^{n},

and
\widehat{P_{n}(z)}=P(\omega z)\cdots P(\omega^{n}z)
. If we write
P(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{d}p_{k}z^{k}
and denote
B=\max_{k}|p_{k}|
, then

\widehat{P_{n}}(z)=\sum_{m=0}^{nd}\frac{a_{m}^{(n)}}{m!}z^{m},

and using Leibnitz’s formula we obtain

a_{m}^{(n)}=\widehat{P_{n}}^{m)}(0)=\sum_{h_{1}+\cdots+h_{n}=m}\binom{m}{h_{1}\cdots h_{n}}\prod_{t=1}^{n}(\omega^{th_{t}}p_{h_{t}}h_{t}!),

from which we get

\displaystyle|L^{n}f(0)|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{nd}\frac{1}{m!}\sum_{h_{1}+\cdots+h_{n}=m}\binom{m}{h_{1}\cdots h_{n}}\prod_{t=1}^{n}(|\omega|^{th_{t}}|p_{h_{t}}|h_{t}!)|D^{m}R_{\lambda}^{n}f(0)|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{nd}\frac{1}{m!}\sum_{h_{1}+\cdots+h_{n}=m}m!|\lambda|^{nm-h_{1}-\cdots-nh_{n}}B^{n}|f^{(m)}(0)|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle B^{n}|\lambda|^{d}\cdots|\lambda|^{(n-1)d}\sum_{m=0}^{nd}\binom{m+n-1}{m}|f^{(m)}(0)|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle B^{n}|\lambda|^{d}\cdots|\lambda|^{(n-1)d}\frac{(nd+n-1)!}{(n-1)!}\sum_{m=0}^{nd}\frac{|f^{(m)}(0)|}{m!}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle B^{n}|\lambda|^{d}\cdots|\lambda|^{(n-1)d}\frac{(nd+n-1)!}{(n-1)!}\rho_{1}(f).

as promised.
∎

Now let us prove the main result that complete the Table
3
.

Theorem 5.5
.

Assume that
L=R_{\lambda}p(D)
and
|\lambda|>1
. Then all closed subspaces of supercyclic vectors for
L
have finite dimension.

Proof.

We want to show that in any infinite dimensional closed subspace
M\subset\mathcal{H}({\mathbb{C}})
there exists
f\in M\setminus\{0\}
such that

\frac{|L^{n}f(0)|}{\rho_{1}(L^{n}f)}\to 0\quad\textrm{as}\quad n\to\infty.

(16)

Thus
f
is not supercyclic for
L
, hence
L
has no supercyclic subspace.

The sequence of seminorms
p_{n}(\cdot)=\rho_{|\lambda|^{;}{n/2}}(\cdot)
induces the topology of
\mathcal{H}({\mathbb{C}})
.
Let
N_{n}=\{f\in\mathcal{H}({\mathbb{C}})\,:\,f^{k}(0)=0;\,k=1,\cdots,n-1\}
, and let
(m_{n})_{n}
be the increasing sequence obtained in Lemma
3.7
.Thus there is a constant
c>0
such that for each
h\in N_{m_{n}}
:

\rho_{1}(L^{n}h)\geq c^{n}1\cdot|\lambda|^{d}\cdot|\lambda|^{2d}\cdots|\lambda|^{(n-1)d}\rho_{1}(h^{nd)}(\lambda^{n}z)).

(17)

By Remark
3.8
, we can assume without loss that
m_{n}>dn
, otherwise we select a convenient subsequence of
(m_{n})
. The idea of the proof is to construct a sequence
f_{n}\in M\cap N_{m_{n}}
satisfying

p_{n}(f_{n})=\frac{1}{n^{2}}
for
n\geq 0
and

L^{n}f_{n}\in H_{n-1}
for
j=1,...,n
.

Here
H_{n-1}
is the finite codimensional subspace guaranteed by Lemma
5.2
associated to
F_{n-1}=\textrm{span}\{T^{j}f_{i}\,:i\leq n-1,j\leq n\}
.

Indeed, we take
f_{1}\in M\setminus\{0\}
. Then
f_{1}
is supercyclic for
L
, and since
p_{1}(f_{1})\neq 0
, we can suppose that
p_{1}(f_{1})=1
.
Let us consider the subspace
F_{1}=\textrm{span}\{T^{j}f_{1}\,:\,j=1,2\}
of finite dimension and the corresponging subspace
H_{1}
of finite codimension guaranteed by Lemma
5.2
.

Assume that
f_{1},\cdots,f_{n-1}
have been already been constructed. Let us consider

F_{n-1}=\textrm{span}\{T^{j}f_{i}\,:i\leq n-1,j\leq n\}
and by
H_{n-1}
the corresponding finite codimension subspace guaranteed by Lemma
5.2
.

Since
N_{m_{n}}
is of finite codimension, there exists
f_{n}\in M\setminus\{0\}\cap N_{m_{n}}
, such that
T^{n}f_{n}\in H_{n-1}
. Again
f_{n}
is supercyclic and
p_{n}(f_{n})\neq 0
, so we can suppose that
p_{n}(f_{n})=\frac{1}{n^{2}}
.

We claim that
f=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}f_{k}
is the function in
M\setminus\{0\}
that we are looking for. Indeed, since
f_{n}\in M\setminus\{0\}
,
p_{n}(f_{n})=1/n^{2}
and
(p_{n})
is an increasing sequence of seminorms defining the topology of
\mathcal{H}({\mathbb{C}})
, the series

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}f_{k}
defines an entire function

.

Now, with this construction in hand, we estimate the denominator of (

).
First we show that
\rho_{1}(L^{n}f)\geq\frac{1}{(\varepsilon+1)(\varepsilon+2)}\rho_{1}(L^{n}f_{n})

\displaystyle\rho_{1}(L^{n}f)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rho_{1}\left(L^{n}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}f_{j}\right)+L^{n}\left(\sum_{j=n+1}^{\infty}f_{j}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon}\rho_{1}\left(L^{n}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}f_{j}\right)+L^{n}f_{n}\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon}\frac{1}{2+\varepsilon}\rho_{1}\left(L^{n}f_{n}\right)

For inequality (

L^{n}(\sum_{j=1}^{n}f_{j})\in F_{n}
and

and

Next, since

) and (

\displaystyle\rho_{1}(L^{n}f)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon}\frac{1}{2+\varepsilon}\rho_{1}\left(L^{n}f_{n}\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{c^{n}1\cdot|\lambda|^{d}\cdot|\lambda|^{2d}\cdots|\lambda|^{(n-1)d}}{(1+\varepsilon)(2+\varepsilon)}\rho_{1}(f_{n}^{nd)}(\lambda^{n}z)).

According to Proposition
5.3
, since

,
we incorporate inequality (

\displaystyle\rho_{1}(L^{n}f)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{c^{n}1\cdot|\lambda|^{d}\cdot|\lambda|^{2d}\cdots|\lambda|^{(n-1)d}}{(1+\varepsilon)(2+\varepsilon)}\frac{|\lambda|^{m_{n}n/2}}{|\lambda|^{n^{2}d}}\frac{m_{n}!}{(m_{n}-nd)!}\rho_{|\lambda|^{n/2}}(f_{n})

\displaystyle\frac{c^{n}1\cdot|\lambda|^{d}\cdot|\lambda|^{2d}\cdots|\lambda|^{(n-1)d}}{(1+\varepsilon)(2+\varepsilon)}\frac{|\lambda|^{m_{n}n/2}}{|\lambda|^{n^{2}d}}\frac{m_{n}!}{(m_{n}-nd)!}\frac{1}{n^{2}}.

Since

\displaystyle|L^{n}f(0)|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|L^{n}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}f_{j}\right)(0)\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle B^{n}\frac{((d+1)n-1)!}{(n-1)!}|\lambda|^{d}|\lambda|^{2d}\cdots|\lambda|^{(n-1)d}\rho_{1}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}f_{j}\right)

Therefore, if we compute (

\displaystyle\frac{|L^{n}f(0)|}{\rho_{1}(L^{n}f)}

\displaystyle\leq

Choosing a subsequence of
