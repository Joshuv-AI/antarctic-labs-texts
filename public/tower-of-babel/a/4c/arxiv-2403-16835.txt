Diffraction Tomography for a Generalized Incident Field
Clemens Kirisits clemens.kirisits@univie.ac.at, Noemi Naujoks noemi.naujoks@univie.ac.at, Otmar Scherzer otmar.scherzer@univie.ac.at

Abstract
Diffraction tomography is an inverse scattering technique used to reconstruct the spatial distribution of the material properties of a weakly scattering object. The object is exposed to radiation, typically light or ultrasound, and the scattered waves induced from different incident field angles are recorded. In conventional diffraction tomography, the incident wave is assumed to be a monochromatic plane wave, an unrealistic simplification in practical imaging scenarios. In this article, we extend conventional diffraction tomography by introducing the concept of customized illumination scenarios, with a pronounced emphasis on imaging with focused beams. We present a new forward model that incorporates a generalized incident field and extends the classical Fourier diffraction theorem to the use of this incident field. This yields a new two-step reconstruction process which we comprehensively evaluate through numerical experiments.

1. Introduction

The task of reconstructing the internal structure of an object from scattered radiation has traditionally been known as the inverse scattering problem
[
5
]
. In this context, an object is exposed to a specific form of radiation, the resulting scattered waves are recorded, and the scattering potential is reconstructed from these recordings. Diffraction tomography (DT) provides a solution to the inverse scattering problem.

It is typically based on either the
Born
or the
Rytov approximation
, which simplifies the relationship between scattered wave and scattering potential by assuming that the object is only weakly scattering
[
17
,
24
,
26
,
36
]
. This simplification allows DT to offer explicit solutions to the inverse scattering problem, enabling fast implementation and making it attractive for real-world applications. Examples include
optical diffraction tomography
for visualizing living biological cells
[
35
]
via tomographic phase microscopy, and medical ultrasound imaging, known as
ultrasound computed tomography
, particularly used for breast imaging
[
26
,
31
,
32
]
.

E. Wolf laid the mathematical foundation for DT in
[
36
]
. His central result is the
Fourier diffraction theorem
. In a two-dimensional context, it states that the Fourier-transformed measurements of the scattered wave are equal to the 2D Fourier transform of the scattering potential on a semicircle. Incorporating a collection of measurements taken at different incident field angles generates more data in Fourier space, enabling the scattering potential to be extracted through a technique known as
filtered backpropagation
, see
[
6
,
17
,
18
,
36
]
.

The applicability of the Fourier diffraction theorem, and thus the reconstruction process in conventional DT, relies on the assumption that the incident wave is a monochromatic plane wave. However, this assumption is violated in various imaging applications. For instance, in ultrasound imaging the width of the transducer limits the emitted sound wave to a beam. More significantly, new devices provide high flexibility in controlling the shape of the wave sent into the body, which opens up the possibility to experiment with new beam designs. In this way, ultrasound devices can, for instance, emit focused beams to a region of interest in the human body to achieve a better resolution in the far field
[
21
]
.

Numerous efforts have been undertaken to extend the foundational concept of DT to alternative illumination scenarios. For instance, A. Devaney and G. Beylkin presented in
[
7
]
how the scattered field in ultrasound imaging setups with arbitrarily configured source and receiver surfaces can be related to the conventional plane-wave forward problem. In their research, they present reconstruction algorithms for both plane wave and cylindrical wave illumination. Furthermore, Synthetic Aperture Diffraction Tomography (SADT), initially introduced by
[
25
]
, provides reconstruction from scattered waves that are acquired by using two parallel single-element transducers (i.e. point sources) moving along a straight line. This methodology does not afford the capability to rotate the incident illumination around the object. Subsequently, SADT was further developed in
[
30
]
, specifically within the context of breast ultrasound tomography. This approach includes a toroidal array transducer that encircles the breast and allows for simultaneously transmitting plane waves from different directions and receiving the resulting scattered waves.

Contributions

Motivated by applications in ultrasound tomography, this article is devoted to extending DT to accommodate arbitrary incident field designs. We place particular emphasis on focused beams, which are commonly used in various tomographic contexts. A comparison of the experimental setup of conventional DT with the new beam DT approach is visualized in
Figure 1
.

The fundamental concept is to model a generalized incident field via superposition of monochromatic plane waves, each characterized by different propagation direction and amplitude
[
3
,
11
]
. We present a customized Fourier diffraction theorem, serving as the basis for reconstructing the scattering potential from a set of detected scattered waves. The measurements are obtained by rotating the beam around the object and capturing the resulting scattered waves along a fixed measurement line in front of the object. While the original Fourier diffraction theorem provides a
direct
relation between the measurements and the scattering potential in Fourier space, this modified version involves an integral operator acting on the Fourier-transformed scattering potential. As a result, it establishes an
indirect
relation, requiring the reconstruction of the scattering potential to be divided into two separated inversion steps: First, we invert the arising integral operator to extract the Fourier data of the scattering potential using truncated singular value decomposition. Subsequently, the scattering potential is reconstructed via Fourier inversion in the second step.

In addition to the theoretical findings presented in this article, we perform numerical experiments to assess our proposed method.

Outline

The article is organized as follows: We start with presenting the mathematical concept for the beam DT approach in
Section 2
. It contains the formulation of a generalized incident field and the introduction of the underlying wave propagation model based on the Born approximation. Subsequently, we delve into the analysis of this forward model in the Fourier domain by deriving an extension of the Fourier diffraction theorem. With this fundament, we turn our attention to the inverse problem in
Section 3
, where we introduce a two-step technique for extracting the scattering potential from the measurements. Thereafter, we discuss in
Section 4
the numerical implementation of our developed method that is subsequently validated by means of numerical examples in
Section 5
. This article ends with a conclusion of our results in
Section 6
.

2. Forward Model

In this section, we provide an overview of the mathematical model that serves as the foundation for our tomographic reconstruction. The concrete model under investigation follows the standard assumptions of DT by assuming that the acquired measurement data contain information about both the amplitude and phase of the scattered waves.
Furthermore, we operate under the assumption of scalar wave propagation. Hence, the wave propagation models are the same, regardless the motivation stems from acoustic or electromagnetic imaging modalities, making our results adaptable and applicable in both imaging techniques.

2.1. Conceptual setup

Although we are interested in realistic imaging applications, i.e. reconstructing three-dimensional objects, this article deals with an experimental setup in
\mathds{R}^{2}
. This limitation is justified by the fact that the two-dimensional theory directly can be applied to three-dimensional objects that vary only slightly along one of the dimensions. Furthermore, images are often built using single slices of the object.

The object we intend to quantify tomographically is assumed to be enclosed by the disk
\mathcal{B}_{r_{s}}(\mathbf{0})\coloneqq\{\mathbf{r}\in\mathds{R}^{2}:\left\|\mathbf{r}\right\|<r_{s}\}
centered at
\mathbf{0}
with radius
r_{s}
. It is described by the local refractive index
n:\mathds{R}^{2}\to\mathds{C}
with
n(\mathbf{r})=1
on the complement of
\mathcal{B}_{r_{s}}(\mathbf{0})
.

Further, let
\lambda
denote the wavelength and
k_{0}=\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}
the wave number. The function

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{r})=k_{0}^{2}\left[n(\mathbf{r})^{2}-1\right],\qquad\mathbf{r}\in\mathds{R}^{2}

(1)

defines the
scattering potential
of the object
[
36
]
and is the quantity we aim to reconstruct. Note that
f
differs only in
\mathcal{B}_{r_{s}}
from
\mathbf{0}
such that we observe

\displaystyle\operatorname{supp}(f)\subseteq\mathcal{B}_{r_{s}}\subset(-r_{s},r_{s})^{2}

by construction. An incident wave
u^{\text{inc}}
that propagates through the object induces a scattered wave
u^{\text{sca}}
.
In our experiment,
u^{\text{inc}}
is rotated around the object, while the scattered wave is recorded on a fixed measurement line
\{\mathbf{r}\in\mathds{R}^{2}:r_{2}=r_{M}\}
, with
r_{M}>r_{s}
. See
1b
for an illustration of the experimental setup.

2.2. A generalized incident field

Under the assumption that the incident field
u^{\text{inc}}
is represented by a time-harmonic solution of the wave equation, it satisfies the Helmholtz equation

\displaystyle\Delta u^{\text{inc}}(\mathbf{r})+k_{0}^{2}u^{\text{inc}}(\mathbf{r})=0,\qquad\mathbf{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}

(2)

in free space, see
[
5
, section 3.3]
. In conventional diffraction tomography, the incident wave is assumed to be the simplest solution of
Equation 2
, that is a monochromatic plane wave

u^{\text{inc}}_{\mathbf{s_{0}}}(\mathbf{r})\coloneqq e^{ik_{0}\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{s}_{0}},

(3)

propagating in direction
\mathbf{s}_{0}\in\mathbb{S}^{1}\coloneqq\{\mathbf{s}\in\mathds{R}^{2}:\left\|\mathbf{s}\right\|=1\}
. To extend DT to the use of arbitrary illumination scenarios satisfying
Equation 2
, we suggest modeling the incident field as a superposition of monochromatic plane waves having different propagation directions

\displaystyle u^{\text{inc}}(\mathbf{r})=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}a(\mathbf{s})e^{ik_{0}\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{s}}ds,

(4)

where
ds=ds(\mathbf{s})
is the standard line element and the function
a\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
specifies the amplitude of the individual plane waves arriving from each given direction
\mathbf{s}\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
. The Hilbert space
L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
is defined as the space of all square integrable functions
a:\mathbb{S}^{1}\to\mathbb{C}
. The technique of expanding a complex wave field into a superposition of plane waves having the same frequency but different propagating directions has its origin in the field of Fourier optics and is referred to as
angular spectrum representation
, see
[
3
,
22
]
or
[
11
, section 3.10]
.

The function
a\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
, which we refer to as
beam profile
hereafter, can be chosen almost arbitrarily. This flexibility is what allows
Equation 4
to accommodate a wide range of incident fields.
In the theoretical part of this work, we refrain from constraining ourselves to a particular beam profile. Nevertheless, we will now introduce the concept of focused beams, as they hold relevance in a variety of tomographic applications.

Example 2.1
.

Suppose the object is illuminated from the top, so that only downward propagating directions
\mathbf{s}=(s_{1},s_{2})\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
,
s_{2}<0
are considered. Then, we define a
Gaussian beam
to be the wave of the form in
Equation 4
having a Gaussian profile

\displaystyle a(\mathbf{s})=\begin{cases}e^{-As_{1}^{2}},\quad&s_{2}<0,\\
0,\quad&s_{2}>0,\end{cases}

(5)

where the parameter
A>0
affects the beam waist, that is the diameter in the focus.

The term
Gaussian beam
has its origin in optics and its construction according to
Equation 4
together with
Equation 5
can be found for instance in
[
1
]
or
[
22

. In

an illustration for different amplitudes
A>0
is given. Increasing
A
narrows the Gaussian profile, resulting in a wider beam waist.

2.3. The Born approximation

The total field
u^{\text{tot}}
is composed additively of the incident and the scattered wave

u^{\text{tot}}=u^{\text{inc}}+u^{\text{sca}}.

(6)

Considering time harmonic waves, it satisfies the reduced wave equation
[
5
, Section 2.1]

\displaystyle\Delta u^{\text{tot}}(\mathbf{r})+k_{0}^{2}n^{2}(\mathbf{r})u^{\text{tot}}(\mathbf{r})=0,\qquad\mathbf{r}\in\mathds{R}^{2}.

(7)

For the formulation of our forward model, we need to calculate the scattered wave. Inserting
Equation 2
in
Equation 7
leads to

\displaystyle\Delta u^{\text{sca}}(\mathbf{r})+k_{0}^{2}u^{\text{sca}}(\mathbf{r})=-f(\mathbf{r})(u^{\text{inc}}(\mathbf{r})+u^{\text{sca}}(\mathbf{r})).

(8)

To ensure uniqueness for the scattered wave, we consider in the following only outgoing waves satisfying the
Sommerfeld radiation condition
, see
[
34
,
5
]
.

The inverse problem of determining
f
from
u^{\text{sca}}
is inherently non-linear.
However, if the refractive index distribution
n
does not deviate much from the homogeneous background, the scattering effect is weak enough so that multiple scattering can be ignored. In that case one can assume
|u^{\text{sca}}|\ll|u^{\text{inc}}|
, which allows to replace
Equation 8
by

\displaystyle(\Delta+k_{0}^{2})u(\mathbf{r})=-f(\mathbf{r})u^{\text{inc}}(\mathbf{r}),

(9)

where
u
is the
Born approximation

[
36
]
to the scattered field.
The unique outgoing solution of
Equation 9
can be obtained by means of convolution

\displaystyle u(\mathbf{r})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}G(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})f(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})u^{\text{inc}}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})d\mathbf{r}^{\prime},\qquad\mathbf{r}\in\mathds{R}^{2},

(10)

with the outgoing fundamental solution

G(\mathbf{r})=\frac{i}{4}H_{0}^{(1)}(k_{0}\left\|\mathbf{r}\right\|),

where
H_{0}^{(1)}
defines the zeroth order Hankel function of the first kind
[
5
, Section 2.2]
.

Remark 2.2
.

The validity of the Born approximation is limited to weakly scattering objects. In
[
4
,
17
,
33
]
a detailed classification of objects satisfying this assumption is provided. There, they also discuss an alternative way to linearize
Equation 8
via
Rytov approximation

[
8
]
and provide a comparison of the approximations.

2.5. A Fourier diffraction relation

Next, regarding the reconstruction of
f
from a set of scattering experiments, it is convenient to examine the forward model in the spatial frequency domain, the
k
-space
. In doing so, we introduce some notation.

Let
\mathcal{F}
denote the
N
-dimensional Fourier transform

\mathcal{F}\phi(\mathbf{k})=(2\pi)^{-\frac{N}{2}}\int_{\mathds{R}^{N}}\phi(\mathbf{r})e^{-i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}}d\mathbf{r},\quad\mathbf{k}\in\mathds{R}^{N}.

Moreover, for a frequency
k\in(-k_{0},k_{0})
we define

\displaystyle\kappa(k)\coloneqq\sqrt{{k_{0}^{2}-k^{2}}},

(12)

and introduce the wave vector

\displaystyle\mathbf{h}(k)\coloneqq(k,\kappa(k))^{\intercal}\in\mathds{R}^{2}.

(13)

In conventional DT, the well-known Fourier diffraction theorem lays the theoretical foundation for reconstructing the scattering potential. This theorem establishes a relation between the detected scattered wave and the scattering potential in
k
-space. Detailed explanations of this fundamental result can be found for instance in
[
36
]
,
[
17
, section 6.3]
, or
[
27
, section 3.3]
. However, this theorem requires that the scattered wave is induced by the illumination of the object with a plane wave. In our approach, the scattered wave results from illuminating the object with a superposition of plane waves. As a consequence, we obtain the following adapted Fourier diffraction relation.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
f\in L^{2}(\mathds{R}^{2})
with
\operatorname{supp}(f)\subset\mathcal{B}_{r_{s}}
and
a\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
. Then, for
k\in(-k_{0},k_{0})
and with the definitions introduced above, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}\big(u\big|_{r_{2}=r_{M}}\big)(k)=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\frac{ie^{i\kappa(k)r_{M}}}{\kappa(k)}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}a(\mathbf{s})\mathcal{F}f({\bf h}(k)-k_{0}\mathbf{s})ds.

(14)

Proof
:

In order to simplify notation for this proof we set
v=u(\cdot,r_{M})
and
v_{\mathbf{s}}=u_{\mathbf{s}}(\cdot,r_{M})
, where
u_{\mathbf{s}}
is the scattered wave induced by the incident plane wave
u^{\text{inc}}_{\mathbf{s}}(\mathbf{r})=e^{ik_{0}\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{s}}
.

According to the classical Fourier diffraction theorem, see
[
17
,
27
,
36
]
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}v_{\mathbf{s}}(k)=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\frac{ie^{i\kappa(k)r_{M}}}{\kappa(k)}\mathcal{F}f({\bf h}(k)-k_{0}\mathbf{s})

for
(k,\mathbf{s})\in(-k_{0},k_{0})\times\mathbb{S}^{1}
. Since
\mathcal{F}f
is bounded we can multiply with
a
and integrate over
\mathbb{S}^{1}
to obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}a(\mathbf{s})\mathcal{F}v_{\mathbf{s}}(k)ds=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\frac{ie^{i\kappa(k)r_{M}}}{\kappa(k)}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}a(\mathbf{s})\mathcal{F}f({\bf h}(k)-k_{0}\mathbf{s})ds

(15)

for
k\in(-k_{0},k_{0}).
It remains to show that the left-hand side is equal to that of
Equation 14
. This is essentially a consequence of the superposition principle,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}a(\mathbf{s})u_{\mathbf{s}}(\mathbf{r})ds=u(\mathbf{r}),

(16)

for all
\mathbf{r}\in\mathds{R}^{2}
, which can be verified using
Equation 10
. Note, however, that we cannot directly change the order of integration on the left-hand side of
Equation 15
, because
\mathcal{F}v_{\mathbf{s}}
is in general a distributional Fourier transform (as is
\mathcal{F}\big(u\big|_{r_{2}=r_{M}}\big)=\mathcal{F}v
on the left-hand side of
Equation 14
). Recall that the Fourier transform of a tempered distribution
w\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathds{R})
is the unique
\mathcal{F}w\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathds{R})
satisfying
\mathcal{F}w[\phi]=w[\mathcal{F}\phi]
for all Schwartz functions
\phi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathds{R})
, see for instance
[
12
,
13
]
.

Note that
v
and
v_{\mathbf{s}}
are smooth by virtue of
Equation 10
and because
G
is smooth on
\mathds{R}\setminus\{0\}
. In addition they are bounded, since
H^{(1)}_{0}(\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|)=O(\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|^{-1/2})
for
\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|\to\infty
, see
[
28
,
(10.2.5)
]
. It follows that
v,v_{\mathbf{s}}
belong to the space of tempered distributions
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathds{R})
.

Now consider a test function
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathds{R})\subset\mathcal{S}(\mathds{R})
with support in
(-k_{0},k_{0})
. Then, since
1/\kappa
is integrable on
(-k_{0},k_{0})
, it follows from
Equation 15
that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathds{R}}\phi(k)\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}a(\mathbf{s})\mathcal{F}v_{\mathbf{s}}(k)ds\,dk=i\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\int_{\mathds{R}}\phi(k)\frac{e^{i\kappa(k)r_{M}}}{\kappa(k)}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}a(\mathbf{s})\mathcal{F}f({\bf h}(k)-k_{0}\mathbf{s})ds\,dk.

Applying Fubini’s theorem and the distributional definition of the Fourier transform we find

\displaystyle\int_{\mathds{R}}\phi(k)\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}a(\mathbf{s})\mathcal{F}v_{\mathbf{s}}(k)ds\,dk

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}a(\mathbf{s})\int_{\mathds{R}}\phi(k)\mathcal{F}v_{\mathbf{s}}(k)dk\,ds=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}a(\mathbf{s})\mathcal{F}v_{\mathbf{s}}[\phi]ds=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}a(\mathbf{s})v_{\mathbf{s}}[\mathcal{F}\phi]ds

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}a(\mathbf{s})\int_{\mathds{R}}v_{\mathbf{s}}(x)\mathcal{F}\phi(x)dx\,ds.

Another application of Fubini’s theorem combined with
Equation 16
yields

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathds{R}}\mathcal{F}\phi(x)\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}a(\mathbf{s})v_{\mathbf{s}}(x)ds\,dx=\int_{\mathds{R}}\mathcal{F}\phi(x)v(x)\,dx=v[\mathcal{F}\phi]=\mathcal{F}v[\phi].

Summarizing, we have shown that

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}v[\phi]=i\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\int_{\mathds{R}}\phi(k)\frac{e^{i\kappa(k)r_{M}}}{\kappa(k)}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}a(\mathbf{s})\mathcal{F}f({\bf h}(k)-k_{0}\mathbf{s})ds\,dk

for every test function
\phi
supported in
(-k_{0},k_{0})
. Therefore, on the interval
(-k_{0},k_{0})
we can identify
\mathcal{F}v=\mathcal{F}\big(u\big|_{r_{2}=r_{M}}\big)
with the function given by the right-hand side of
Equation 14
.

\square

While
Section 2.5
is an adaption of the classical Fourier diffraction theorem, there is a decisive difference: Instead of having
direct
access to
\mathcal{F}f
, now the measurements only provide information about averages of
\mathcal{F}f
taken over circles and weighted by the beam profile.

2.6. Rotating the incident field

The data presented in
Equation 14
correspond to a scenario in which the object is illuminated once and the scattered field is measured at a line below the object. However, this configuration does not provide sufficient data for a complete reconstruction of the scattering potential
f
, which is a two-dimensional complex-valued function. In response to the demands of imaging applications, particularly those involving the movement of the emitting device around the object, we address this limitation by rotating the incident field, see also
1b
. We introduce the rotated incident field

u^{\text{inc}}_{\theta}(\mathbf{r})\coloneqq u^{\text{inc}}(R_{\theta}\mathbf{r})=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}a(R^{\intercal}_{\theta}\mathbf{s})e^{ik_{0}\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{s}}ds,

where the matrix

\displaystyle R_{\theta}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\
\sin\theta&\cos\theta\\
\end{pmatrix}

specifies the counter clockwise rotation through the angle
\theta\in[-\pi,\pi]
. The corresponding scattered wave
u_{\theta}(\mathbf{r})
satisfies

\displaystyle(\Delta+k_{0}^{2})u_{\theta}(\mathbf{r})=-f(\mathbf{r})u^{\text{inc}}_{\theta}(\mathbf{r}).

For each rotation of the incident field a measurement is conducted. Hereby, we assume that the detector is kept in the same position for every experiment.
Now, we define the full set of data in
k
-space by

m(k,\theta)\coloneqq-\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}i\kappa(k)e^{-i\kappa(k)r_{M}}\mathcal{F}u_{\theta}(k,r_{M})

(17)

which we assume to be given for all
k\in(-k_{0},k_{0})
and all
\theta\in[-\pi,\pi]
.
According to
Section 2.5
they are related to the scattering potential
f
via

m(k,\theta)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}a(R^{\intercal}_{\theta}\mathbf{s})\mathcal{F}f(\mathbf{h}(k)-k_{0}\mathbf{s})ds.

(18)

Using a parametrization of
\mathbf{s}\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
in terms of polar coordinates

\mathbf{s}(\varphi)=(\cos\varphi,\sin\varphi)^{\intercal},\quad\varphi\in[-\pi,\pi)

(19)

turns
Equation 18
into

m(k,\theta)=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}a(\mathbf{s}(\varphi-\theta))\mathcal{F}f(\mathbf{h}(k)-k_{0}\mathbf{s}(\varphi))d\varphi,\qquad k\in(-k_{0},k_{0}),\ \theta\in[-\pi,\pi]

(20)

which lays the groundwork for the process of determining
f
.

3. Inverse problem

Based on our results derived up to this point we now address the
inverse problem
. It consists in extracting the scattering potential
f
from
m
via
Equation 20
. This relation is not direct. However, given our measurement configuration, which involves rotating the incident field, we can solve this problem through a two-step inversion process:

1.)

In the first step, the Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}f(\mathbf{h}(k)-k_{0}\mathbf{s}(\varphi))
is to be identified for all
k\in(-k_{0},k_{0})
and
\varphi\in[-\pi,\pi)
from
Equation 20
.

2.)

Subsequently, in the second step, the scattering potential
f
can be extracted via Fourier inversion.

In the following subsections, we discuss both inversion steps separately and present an explicit reconstruction formula afterward.

3.1. Identification of the data in
k
-space

The aim of this section is to infer the
k
-space data
\mathcal{F}f(\mathbf{h}(k)-k_{0}\mathbf{s}(\varphi))
for all
k\in(-k_{0},k_{0})
and
\varphi\in[-\pi,\pi)
from
Equation 20
.
For this purpose, we use the dependency of the measurements on the rotation angles
\theta\in[-\pi,\pi]
and consider the frequency
k\in(-k_{0},k_{0})
to be fixed. Then,

g(k,\cdot)\coloneqq{}\mathcal{F}f(\mathbf{h}(k)-k_{0}\mathbf{s}(\cdot))\in L^{2}\left([-\pi,\pi]\right),

because
\mathcal{F}f
is smooth.

Moreover, with the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we obtain that also

m(k,\cdot)\in L^{2}\left([-\pi,\pi]\right).

For this reason, we introduce the following integral operator

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}:L^{2}\left([-\pi,\pi]\right)\to L^{2}\left([-\pi,\pi]\right),\qquad\mathcal{A}g(\theta)=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}a(\varphi-\theta)g(\varphi)d\varphi,

(21)

whose integral kernel is defined as a translation of the beam profile
a\in L^{2}([-\pi,\pi])
. As in
Equation 21
we identify
a
with a
2\pi
-periodic function on
\mathds{R}
from now on and refrain from explicitly writing the parametrization. This operator is in general not self-adjoint since we do not impose
a(\varphi)=\overline{a(-\varphi)}
. From the fact that
a\in L^{2}([-\pi,\pi])
it follows directly that
\mathcal{A}
is compact, see
[
23
, theorem 3.4]
.

Accordingly, the problem presented in
Equation 20
can be reformulated as a system of (operator) equations

\mathcal{A}g(k,\cdot)=m(k,\cdot),\qquad k\in(-k_{0},k_{0}).

(22)

Each equation leads due to the compactness of
\mathcal{A}
to an
ill-posed
problem. For a comprehensive treatment of such problems, we refer to
[
9
,
19
,
23
]
. For simplicity of notation, we refrain from writing the dependency of the functions
g
and
m
on the frequency
k\in(-k_{0},k_{0})
in this subsection.

A foundational tool for the analysis of a non-selfadjoint compact linear operator is the theory of
singular systems
. We recall from
[
9
, section 2.2]
the following definition:

Definition 3.1
.

Let
K:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
be a compact operator between two Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}
and let
K^{*}:\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{X}
denote the adjoint of
K
. The set
\left\{\left(\sigma_{n};v_{n},u_{n}\right):n\in\mathds{N}_{0}\right\}
defines a
singular system
of
K
if
\sigma_{n}>0
,
\{(\sigma_{n}^{2},v_{n}):\ n\in\mathds{N}_{0}\}
forms an eigensystem to
K^{*}K
and
u_{n}=\frac{Kv_{n}}{\left\|Kv_{n}\right\|}.
Here, the singular values can be written down in decreasing order with multiplicity, i.e.
\sigma_{1}\geq\sigma_{2}\geq\dots>0
.

To determine a singular system for
\mathcal{A}
as defined in
Equation 21
, we introduce for
g,h\in L^{2}([-\pi,\pi])
the inner product

\langle g,h\rangle\coloneqq\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}g(\varphi)\overline{h(\varphi)}d\varphi,

that induces a norm

\left\|g\right\|^{2}=\langle g,g\rangle.

The adjoint operator
\mathcal{A}^{\ast}
of
\mathcal{A}
is given by

\mathcal{A}^{*}:L^{2}\left([-\pi,\pi]\right)\to L^{2}\left([-\pi,\pi]\right),\qquad(\mathcal{A}^{*}m)(\varphi)=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\overline{a(\varphi-\theta)}m(\theta)d\theta.

Note that the functions

e_{n}:[-\pi,\pi]\to\mathds{C},\qquad e_{n}(\varphi)\coloneqq e^{-in\varphi},\quad n\in\mathds{Z}

form a complete orthonormal system in
L^{2}\left([-\pi,\pi]\right)
equipped with the above defined inner product. Herewith, a singular system can be characterized as follows:

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
a\in L^{2}([-\pi,\pi])
and

a_{n}=\langle a,\overline{e_{n}}\rangle,\quad n\in\mathds{Z}

(23)

be its non-zero Fourier coefficients.
Then, the set

\displaystyle\left\{\left(2\pi\left|a_{n}\right|;e_{n},\frac{a_{n}}{|a_{n}|}e_{n}\right):n\in\mathds{Z}\right\}

(24)

forms a singular system of
\mathcal{A}
.

Proof
:

First, we expand the integral kernel as a Fourier series

a(\varphi-\theta)=\sum_{n\in\mathds{Z}}a_{n}\overline{e_{n}(\varphi-\theta)}=\sum_{n\in\mathcal{\mathds{Z}}}a_{n}\overline{e_{n}(\varphi)}e_{n}(\theta)

(25)

for almost every
\varphi\in[-\pi,\pi)
. Then, by exploiting
Equation 25
, we calculate

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{A}e_{n}\right)(\theta)=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}a(\varphi-\theta)e_{n}(\varphi)d\varphi=\sum_{l\in\mathds{Z}}a_{l}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e_{n}(\varphi)\overline{e_{l}(\varphi)}d\varphi e_{l}(\theta)=2\pi\sum_{l\in\mathds{Z}}a_{l}e_{l}(\theta)\delta_{nl}=2\pi a_{n}e_{n}(\theta),

and, therefore, together with
Equation 25
, we get

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{A}^{*}\mathcal{A}e_{n}\right)(\varphi)

\displaystyle=2\pi a_{n}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\overline{a(\varphi-\theta)}e_{n}(\theta)d\theta=2\pi a_{n}\sum_{l\in\mathds{Z}}\overline{a_{l}}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e_{n}(\theta)\overline{e_{l}(\theta)}d\theta e_{l}(\varphi)

\displaystyle=4\pi^{2}a_{n}\sum_{l\in\mathds{Z}}\overline{a_{l}}e_{l}(\varphi)\delta_{nl}=4\pi^{2}\left|a_{n}\right|^{2}e_{n}(\varphi)

for all
n\in\mathds{Z}
. Hence,
4\pi^{2}\left|a_{n}\right|^{2}>0,\ n\in\mathds{Z}
are eigenvalues of
\mathcal{A}^{*}\mathcal{A}
. Further, we have

\left\|\mathcal{A}e_{n}\right\|=2\pi\left|a_{n}\right|\left\|e_{n}\right\|=2\pi\left|a_{n}\right|

so that in total
Equation 24
forms a singular system of
\mathcal{A}
according to
Section 3.1
.

\square

Using this singular system, the operator
\mathcal{A}
can be diagonalized as

\mathcal{A}g=2\pi\sum_{n\in\mathds{Z}}a_{n}\langle g,e_{n}\rangle e_{n},\qquad g\in L^{2}([-\pi,\pi]).

(26)

According to
[
9
, theorem 2.8]
, the singular system provides a series representation for the
best-approximate solution
of
\mathcal{A}g=m
, which is defined as the unique least-squares solution of minimal norm, that is,

\mathcal{A}^{\dagger}m\coloneqq\argmin\left\{\left\|g\right\|\colon g\in L^{2}([-\pi,\pi]),\left\|\mathcal{A}g-m\right\|=\inf_{h}\left\|\mathcal{A}h-m\right\|\right\}.

Theorem 3.3
.

Suppose that
m\in L^{2}([-\pi,\pi])
satisfies the Picard condition

\sum_{n\in\mathds{Z}}\left(\frac{\left|\langle m,e_{n}\rangle\right|}{\left|a_{n}\right|}\right)^{2}<\infty.

(27)

Then,

\mathcal{A}^{\dagger}m=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{n\in\mathds{Z}}\frac{\langle m,e_{n}\rangle}{a_{n}}e_{n}.

(28)

This result demonstrates the ill-posed nature of the problem which is characterized by the fact that the singular values
|a_{n}|
tend to zero: The condition
Equation 27
, which ensures the existence of a least-squares solution, is satisfied if the coefficients
\langle m,e_{n}\rangle
decay fast enough relative to the values
a_{n}
. Moreover, in the presence of perturbations in the input data
m
, we can deduce from
Equation 28
that the instability of the inverse problem is also characterized by the decay of the coefficients
a_{n}
. This is why the choice of the integral kernel
a\in L^{2}([-\pi,\pi])
can influence the
degree of ill-posedness
. For further details see
[
9
, sect. 2.2]
,
[
19
, sect. 15.4]
or
[
23
, sect. 3.3]
.

However, it is straightforward to apply a regularization scheme that filters out the data associated with small singular values. To achieve this, we consider the
truncated singular value decomposition
(TSVD)
[
9
, section 4.1]

\mathcal{A}^{\dagger}_{N}m\coloneqq\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{n=-N}^{N}\frac{\langle m,e_{n}\rangle}{a_{n}}e_{n},\qquad N\in\mathds{N}

(29)

for some
N\in\mathds{N}
. Here, the truncation level
N
acts as a regularization parameter, that should be chosen such a way that all noise-dominated singular value coefficients are neglected. In the numerical part of this article, see
Section 5.1
, we specify this parameter by inspecting the discrete Picard criterion as it is proposed and studied in
[
15
]
. As an alternative regularization scheme it is also straightforward to apply Tikhonov-type regularization which corresponds to a multiplication of the summands in
Equation 28
with some damping coefficients. Anyhow, we restrict ourselves to the regularization via
Equation 29
and refer to
[
9
,
20
]
for a detailed discussion of the regularization strategies.

3.2. Frequency coverage and Fourier inversion

According to the analysis of the integral operator
\mathcal{A}
, the
k
-space data
\mathcal{F}f(\mathbf{h}(k)-k_{0}\mathbf{s}(\varphi))
are now approximated by (
\mathcal{A}^{\dagger}_{N}m)(k,\varphi)
for all
k\in(-k_{0},k_{0})
and all
\varphi\in[-\pi,\pi).

Based on this, we intend to finally quantify the target function
f
via Fourier inversion. In doing so, we introduce the set

\mathcal{U}\coloneqq\left\{(k,\varphi)\in\mathds{R}^{2}:k\in(-k_{0},k_{0}),\ \varphi\in[-\pi,\pi)\right\}

and define the map

T:\mathcal{U}\to\mathds{R}^{2},\qquad T(k,\varphi)\coloneqq\mathbf{h}(k)-k_{0}\mathbf{s}(\varphi)=\begin{pmatrix}k-k_{0}\cos\varphi\\
\kappa(k)-k_{0}\sin\varphi\end{pmatrix}

(30)

that traces out the domain in
k
-space that is covered by the measurements. Under the assumption that
\mathcal{F}f(T(k,\varphi))
can be determined exactly from
m
for all
(k,\varphi)\in\mathcal{U}
, we have access to
\mathcal{F}f
on the domain

T(\mathcal{U})=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}k-k_{0}\cos\varphi\\
\kappa(k)-k_{0}\sin\varphi\end{pmatrix}\in\mathds{R}^{2}:\ k\in(-k_{0},k_{0}),\ \varphi\in[-\pi,\pi)\right\}\subset\mathds{R}^{2}

(31)

in
k
-space which is depicted in
Figure 4
. Every plane wave angle
\varphi\in[-\pi,\pi)
provides data about
\mathcal{F}f
along a semicircle with radius
k_{0}
. For
\varphi\in[-\pi,0]
these semicircles fill the half disk of radius
2k_{0}
in the upper half plane except for the disks of radius
k_{0}
around
(\pm k_{0},0)^{\intercal}
that are captured by
\varphi\in(0,\pi)
. In total, a frequency coverage of the form depicted in
4c
is obtained. Note that this coverage is bounded by

\left\|T(k,\varphi)\right\|\leq 2k_{0}

such that the determination of
f
via Fourier inversion using the available frequencies on
T(\mathcal{U})\subset\mathds{R}^{2}
in
k
-space provides us a
low-pass filtered
approximation of the scattering potential

f(\mathbf{r})\approx\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{T(\mathcal{U})}\mathcal{F}f(\mathbf{y})e^{i\mathbf{y}\cdot\mathbf{r}}d\mathbf{y}.

(32)

T
. Therefore, we introduce the

\operatorname{Card}(T^{-1}(\mathbf{y}))
of
T
, where
\operatorname{Card}
is the counting measure.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
T
be defined as in
Equation 30
. Then, the integral in
Equation 32
can be determined via

\begin{split}\int_{T(\mathcal{U})}\mathcal{F}f(\mathbf{y})e^{i\mathbf{y}\cdot\mathbf{r}}d\mathbf{y}=\int_{\mathcal{U}}\mathcal{F}f(T(k,\varphi))e^{iT(k,\varphi)\cdot\mathbf{r}}\frac{\left|\det(\nabla T(k,\varphi))\right|}{\operatorname{Card}(T^{-1}(T(k,\varphi)))}d(k,\varphi),\quad\mathbf{r}\in\mathds{R}^{2},\end{split}

(33)

where

\det(\nabla T(k,\varphi))=k_{0}\left(\frac{k}{\kappa(k)}\sin\varphi-\cos\varphi\right),\quad(k,\varphi)\in\mathcal{U}

(34)

is the Jacobian determinant of
T
.

Proof
:

The map
T
is differentiable and it is straightforward to calculate the Jacobian matrix

\displaystyle\nabla T(k,\varphi)=\begin{pmatrix}1&k_{0}\sin\varphi\\
-\frac{k}{\kappa(k)}&-k_{0}\cos\varphi\end{pmatrix}

and hence the determinant (
34
).
As it is shown below in
Lemma 3.5
the Banach indicatrix
\operatorname{Card}(T^{-1}(\cdot))
is finite almost everywhere and vanishes nowhere in
T(\mathcal{U})
. For this reason,
we can write

\int_{T(\mathcal{U})}\mathcal{F}f(\mathbf{y})e^{i\mathbf{y}\cdot\mathbf{r}}d\mathbf{y}=\int_{T(\mathcal{U})}\mathcal{F}f(\mathbf{y})e^{i\mathbf{y}\cdot\mathbf{r}}\frac{\operatorname{Card}(T^{-1}(\mathbf{y}))}{\operatorname{Card}(T^{-1}(\mathbf{y}))}d\mathbf{y}.

Then, applying a change of variables according to
[
2
, theorem 5.8.30]
for the integrand

\mathbf{y}\mapsto\mathcal{F}f(\mathbf{y})e^{i\mathbf{y}\cdot\mathbf{r}}/\operatorname{Card}(T^{-1}(\mathbf{y}))

gives

\int_{T(\mathcal{U})}\mathcal{F}f(\mathbf{y})e^{i\mathbf{y}\cdot\mathbf{r}}d\mathbf{y}=\int_{\mathcal{U}}\mathcal{F}f(T(k,\varphi))e^{iT(k,\varphi)\cdot\mathbf{r}}\frac{\big|\det(\nabla T(k,\varphi))\big|}{\operatorname{Card}(T^{-1}(T(k,\varphi)))}d(k,\varphi).

\square

Finally, using this integral transform in
Equation 32
together with the data in

\mathbf{r}\in\mathds{R}^{2}
the

\boxed{f(\mathbf{r})\approx f^{\text{bp}}_{N}(\mathbf{r})\coloneqq\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathcal{U}}(\mathcal{A}^{\dagger}_{N}m)(k,\varphi)e^{iT(k,\varphi)\cdot\mathbf{r}}\times\frac{\left|\det(\nabla T(k,\varphi))\right|}{\operatorname{Card}(T^{-1}(T(k,\varphi)))}d(k,\varphi)}

(35)

of our reconstruction problem, where the Jacobian determinant is given via
Equation 34
. Hence, in the context of

, the term
backpropagation
refers to the computation of the low-pass filtered Fourier inversion using the available Fourier data
\mathcal{A}^{\dagger}_{N}m
, which must first be extracted from the original data
m
using TSVD. To actually calculate
Equation 35

\operatorname{Card}(T^{-1}(\mathbf{y}))
for
\mathbf{y}\in T(\mathcal{U})

Lemma 3.5
.

For almost every
(k,\varphi)\in\mathcal{U}

\displaystyle\operatorname{Card}\left(T^{-1}(T(k,\varphi))\right)=\begin{cases}2,&-\pi\leq\varphi<0,\\
1,&0\leq\varphi<\pi.\end{cases}

Proof
:

Let
\mathbf{y}\in T(\mathcal{U})
, i.e.
\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{h}(k)-k_{0}\mathbf{s}(\varphi)
for a

.

k_{0}\mathbf{s}(\varphi)\in k_{0}\mathbb{S}^{1}

\mathbf{h}(k)=(k,\kappa(k))^{\intercal}\in k_{0}\mathbb{S}_{+}^{1}=\{\mathbf{z}\in k_{0}\mathbb{S}^{1}:z_{2}>0\}

\operatorname{Card}(T^{-1}(\mathbf{y}))

such that
\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b}

and that

4. Numerical implementation

We have now intensively studied DT for a generalized incident field and presented the components needed for a reconstruction of the scattering potential from a set of scattering experiments. The aim of this section is now to transfer this theoretical knowledge into practice by providing the numerical framework that will subsequently be used for numerical experiments.

As
f
is assumed to be compactly supported within
\mathcal{B}_{r_{s}}
, we discretize the object domain for
M\in 2\mathds{N}
in terms of

\mathcal{R}_{M}\coloneqq\frac{2r_{s}}{M}\mathcal{I}_{M}^{2}\subset[-r_{s},r_{s}]^{2},

where

\mathcal{I}_{M}\coloneqq\left\{-\frac{M}{2}+j:j=0,\dots,M-1\right\}

are equidistant grid points. Furthermore, we introduce for
D\in 2\mathds{N}
the set of plane wave angles

S_{D}\coloneqq\frac{2\pi}{D}\mathcal{I}_{D}\subset[-\pi,\pi)

that is used for modeling the incident wave and herewith the grid

\mathcal{U}_{M,D}\coloneqq\left\{(k,\varphi):\ k\in\frac{2k_{0}}{M}\mathcal{I}_{M},\ k<k_{0},\ \varphi\in S_{D}\right\}.

In the remainder of this paper, for simplicity, we also use the set
S_{D}
to specify the rotation angles of the incident field. The sampling set in
k
-space is specified by

\mathcal{Y}_{M,D}\coloneqq T(\mathcal{U}_{M,D}).

4.1. Simulation of measurements

For numerical experiments, we generate synthetic data by applying the forward model to a known scattering potential
f
. Applying a quadrature on the uniform grid
S_{D}
to
Equation 20
yields

m(k,\theta)\approx\frac{2\pi}{D}\sum_{\varphi\in S_{D}}a(\varphi-\theta)\mathcal{F}f(T(k,\varphi)),\qquad(k,\theta)\in\mathcal{U}_{M,D}.

(39)

Here, the Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}f
of our phantom
f
is in general analytically not known. We therefore calculate an approximation via the two-dimensional
nonuniform discrete Fourier transform
(NDFT)
[
29
, section 7.1]

\mathcal{F}f(\textbf{y})\approx\frac{1}{2\pi}\left(\frac{2r_{s}}{M}\right)^{2}\sum_{\textbf{r}\in\mathcal{R}_{M}}f(\textbf{r})e^{-i\textbf{r}\cdot\textbf{y}}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\left(\frac{2r_{s}}{M}\right)^{2}\sum_{\textbf{j}\in\mathcal{I}_{M}^{2}}f\left(\frac{2r_{s}}{M}\textbf{j}\right)e^{-i\frac{2r_{s}}{M}\textbf{j}\cdot\textbf{y}},\quad\textbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}_{M,D}.

(40)

Then, by inserting
Equation 39
in
Equation 40
we obtain for known
f
with unknown Fourier transform the simulated measurement data

5.1. Evaluation of the TSVD and choice of the truncation level

As already pointed out in

5.3. Reconstruction with conventional DT

In
Section 2.4
a comparison of the forward model of conventional DT and focused beam DT was provided. We observed that the measurements differ depending on the chosen imaging scenario and therefore developed a new reconstruction technique for DT that takes customized incident fields as opposite to only plane wave imaging into account. At last, it is our intention to numerically quantify the impact of this new DT approach adapted to the illumination scenario. In doing so, we illuminate the test sample using a Gaussian beam and reconstruct with conventional DT, i.e. backpropagation without performing TSVD.

The incident beam waves for different amplitudes
A
together with the conventional DT solutions are presented in
Figure 11
. We observe that the reconstruction quality deteriorates as
A
decreases, i.e. as the plane wave assumption is violated by focusing. In summary, when dealing with an illumination scenario that deviates from the plane wave assumption, such as (focused) beam illumination, it is essential to account for this in the forward model and, consequently, in the reconstruction.

6. Conclusion

In this article, we studied the imaging problem of DT, considering arbitrary illumination scenarios modeled by a weighted superposition of monochromatic plane waves. Motivated by application in ultrasound tomography, we specifically introduced a focused beam as a representative imaging scenario that deviates from plane wave illumination. Based on the Born approximation we derived an adapted forward model to the new illumination concept. Then, we deduced from conventional DT an adapted Fourier diffraction theorem, serving as the basis for quantifying the scattering potential from the detected scattered waves. In contrast to the original theorem
[
36
]
, this adaption did not provide a direct relation between the measurements and the Fourier-transformed scattering potential. Therefore, the backpropagation of the scattering potential was divided into two parts: We first inverted the arising integral operator in this Fourier diffraction relation by calculating the TSVD. Subsequently, we extracted the scattering potential using a low-pass filtered Fourier inversion.

In the practical implementation of this approach, we assessed its performance. It became evident that the choice of the beam profile plays a crucial role in determining the accuracy and stability of the first inversion step and hence of the backpropagation. In particular, our findings revealed that focusing is disadvantageous for the stability of the solution. Nonetheless, we demonstrated that when dealing with an imaging scenario that deviates too much from a plane wave, such as focusing, conventional DT reconstruction is not sufficient and that our approach is to be preferred.
