Abstract
. We construct a Wick-type deformation quantization of contact metric manifolds. The construction is fully canonical and involves no arbitrary choice. Unlike the case of symplectic or Poisson manifolds, not every classical observable on a general contact metric manifold can be promoted to a quantum one due to possible obstructions to quantization. We prove, however, that all these obstructions disappear for Sasakian manifolds.

Primary 53D55; Secondary 53D35

1.
Introduction

In the recent paper
[
8
]
, we formulated the problem of deformation quantization for contact manifolds and solved it using a modification of Fedosov’s method
[
9
]
. The main novelty compared to the case of symplectic or Poisson manifolds is that not every classical observable – a Reeb-invariant function on a contact manifold – admits quantization. As a result, the algebra of quantum observables may well be smaller than the algebra of classical ones. The study of obstructions to quantization yielded some new invariants of contact manifolds.

One can regard the above quantization as a contact counterpart of the Weyl-type deformation quantization of symplectic manifolds.
This is the most natural quantization scheme if no extra geometric structure is assumed on symplectic or contact manifolds. In many interesting situations, however, symplectic manifolds come equipped with (integrable) Lagrangian polarizations, which are important, for example, for their physical interpretation as phase spaces of mechanical systems. An invariant Lagrangian polarization is also an integral part of the geometric quantization method. In the most favorable case, there is a pair of transverse Lagrangian polarizations, which may be complex and mutually conjugate. The last situation is
best exemplified by (almost) Kähler manifolds with their holomorphic and antiholomorphic polarizations. By definition, a Wick quantization of a Kähler manifold is a quantization adapted to the underlying holomorphic structure, see Sec.
5
. There is a large body of literature devoted to various aspects of and approaches to the problem, starting with the pioneering works of Berezin
[
1
,
2
]
. In Fedosov’s approach, the Wick deformation quantization of Kähler manifolds was in-depth studied in
[
4
,
20
,
6
,
11
,
13
]
.

The odd-dimensional counterpart of almost Kähler geometry is known as contact metric geometry. This can be defined as the geometry of contact manifolds endowed with compatible Riemannian metrics. Incorporating a metric structure in the fabric of contact geometry allows one to polarize a (maximally nonintegrable) contact distribution into holomorphic and antiholomorphic parts. In this paper, we use this polarization
to formulate a Wick-type deformation quantization of contact metric manifolds.
As in
[
8
]
, our construction largely relies on Fedosov’s method. The rich geometry of contact metric manifolds allows for several types of integrability conditions to be imposed. These lead to three special contact metric geometries: K-contact, CR, and Sasakian; the last one is the intersection of the first two. All these integrability conditions play an important part in the Wick deformation quantization developed below. In particular, we show that no obstructions arise for the Wick deformation quantization of Sasakian manifolds and the property of being K-contact ensures the absence of the first obstruction to quantization.

To conclude this introduction, we list the basic differences between the Weyl- and Wick-type deformation quantizations of contact manifolds. First, the contact metric manifolds enjoy a natural contact connection, making the Wick quantization completely canonical. The absence of a canonical connection on general contact manifolds introduces an inevitable arbitrariness in the Weyl quantization. Second, the obstructions to the Weyl quantization of classical observables start from the second order in the deformation parameter, while in the Wick quantization, they arise already in the first order. Third, the differential operators that control the leading obstructions to quantization are completely different: they are third-order for Weyl quantization and first-order for Wick.

2.
Contact metric geometry

We follow the definitions and conventions of the classical papers
[

,

,

[

One can regard contact manifolds as odd-dimensional analogues of symplectic ones. Recall that a
1
-form
\lambda
on a (
2m+1
)-dimensional manifold
M
is called
contact
if

(2.1)

\lambda\wedge(d\lambda)^{m}\neq 0

at each point of
M
. The pair
(M,\lambda)
is called a
contact manifold
. Geometrically, Eq. (
2.1
) means that the
contact distribution

\mathcal{C}=\ker\lambda
on

is ‘maximally nonintegrable’. As a consequence, each contact manifold carries the canonical volume form
v=\lambda\wedge(d\lambda)^{m}
and an exact presymplectic structure defined by the
2
-form
\omega=d\lambda
of rank
2m
.
The kernel of the presymplectic form
\omega
, called
characteristic distribution
, is generated by the
Reeb vector field

\xi
which is chosen to satisfy

(2.2)

i_{\xi}\omega=0\,,\qquad i_{\xi}\lambda=1\,.

This gives the canonical splitting

TM=\mathcal{C}\oplus L_{\xi}
,
where
L_{\xi}
is the trivial line bundle generated by
\xi
.

It is known that every contact manifold admits a Riemannian metric
g
and a tensor field
\phi
of type
(1,1)
that satisfy the following relations:

(2.3)

\begin{array}[]{l}\lambda(X)=g(X,\xi)\,,\qquad\omega(X,Y)=g(X,\phi(Y))\,,\\[8.53581pt]
g(\phi(X),\phi(Y))=g(X,Y)-\lambda(X)\lambda(Y)\,,\qquad\phi^{2}=-I+\xi\otimes\lambda\,,\\[8.53581pt]
\phi(\xi)=0\,,\qquad\lambda\circ\phi=0\,.\end{array}

Here,
X
and
Y
are arbitrary vector fields on
M
and we treat the
(1,1)
-tensor field
1

1

In
[
16
]

\phi

, but this name did not stick.

\phi

Every contact metric manifold carries a canonical
Jacobi structure

[
12

\xi
and the bivector
\pi

(2.4)

(\alpha\wedge\beta)(\pi)=\bar{g}(\alpha,\phi^{t}(\beta))\,.

Here,
\alpha
and
\beta
are arbitrary
1

\bar{g}

\phi

(2.5)

[\pi,\pi]_{SN}=2\pi\wedge\xi\,,\qquad[\xi,\pi]_{SN}=0\,.

The subscript SN refers to the Schouten–Nijenhuis bracket on poly-vector fields.
Alternatively, using the aforementioned volume form

\pi
as dual to the form
\lambda\wedge\omega^{m-1}
. This shows that the Jacobi structure
(\xi,\pi)
is completely determined by the contact structure alone

Given a Jacobi structure, one can make the space of smooth functions on
M
into a Lie algebra for the following bracket:

(2.6)

\{f,g\}=(df\wedge dg)(\pi)+fdg(\xi)-gdf(\xi)\,.

This is called the
Jacobi bracket
of functions

It should be noted that (

) is not a Poisson bracket as it fails to satisfy the Leibniz identity:

\{f,gh\}-\{f,g\}h-g\{f,h\}=ghdf(\xi)\,.

Notice, however, that the Jacobi bracket gives rise to a Poisson algebra structure on the commutative subalgebra of
\xi

C^{\infty}_{\xi}(M)\subset C^{\infty}(M)
.

By a deformation quantization of a contact manifold
(M,\lambda)
we mean a deformation quantization of the Poisson algebra
\big(C^{\infty}_{\xi}(M),\{\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,\}\big)
.
A precise definition will be given in the next section.

In order to develop the differential geometry of contact metric manifolds one usually introduces one or another adapted connection. By definition, a
contact metric connection

\nabla
is an affine connection on
M
that parallelizes the contact metric structure:

(2.7)

\nabla\lambda=0\,,\quad\nabla\xi=0,\quad\nabla\omega=0\,,\quad\nabla g=0\,,\quad\nabla\phi=0\,.

The definition is somewhat redundant since the first three relations follow from the last two
[
19
, Lem. 4.1]

It is known that a contact metric connection always exists and can be constructed as follows
[
17
, Thm. 11]
. Let
\nabla^{g}
denote the Levi-Civita connection that is compatible with the metric
g
. In each local coordinate system, define a
(1,2)
-tensor
S
by the expression

(2.8)

S^{i}_{jk}=-\frac{1}{2}\phi^{i}_{l}\nabla^{g}_{k}\phi^{l}_{j}-\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{j}\nabla^{g}_{k}\xi^{i}+\xi^{i}\nabla^{g}_{k}\lambda_{j}\,.

Using (
2.3
) one can readily verify that the new affine connection
\nabla=\nabla^{g}+S
meets all conditions (
2.7
). As usual, the commutator of covariant derivatives evaluated on a vector field
v

(2.9)

[\nabla{}_{k},\nabla{}_{l}]v^{i}=R^{i}_{jkl}v^{j}+T^{j}_{kl}\nabla_{j}v^{i}\,,

gives the curvature
R^{i}_{jkl}
and the torsion tensor
T^{i}_{jk}=S^{i}_{jk}-S^{i}_{kj}
. The latter is always nonzero. Indeed, it follows from the first identity in (
2.7

(2.10)

\omega_{ij}=\lambda_{k}T_{ji}^{k}\,.

The tensor
S
is known as the

of a metric connection
\nabla=\nabla^{g}+S
. The following easily verified identity shows that the torsion of a metric connection contains the same information as its potential:

(2.11)

S_{ij}^{k}=\frac{1}{2}T^{k}_{ij}+\frac{1}{2}(g_{im}T^{m}_{jn}+g_{jm}T^{m}_{in})g^{nk}\,.

Let us also introduce the covariant torsion and curvature tensors

(2.12)

T_{kij}=\omega_{kn}T^{n}_{ij}\,,\qquad R_{klij}=\omega_{kn}R^{n}_{lij}\,.

They enjoy the following symmetry properties:

(2.13)

\qquad R_{klij}=R_{lkij}=-R_{klji}\,,\qquad T_{kij}+T_{ijk}+T_{jki}=0\,.

Let
\hat{\phi}
denote the restriction of the endomorphism
\phi:TM\rightarrow TM

\hat{\phi}^{2}=-I
; and hence,
\phi
induces an almost complex structure on
\mathcal{C}
. Therefore, the complexification of the vector bundle
\mathcal{C}
splits into the two transverse and mutually conjugate subbundles composed of
\pm i

\hat{\phi}

(2.14)

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{C}}=\mathcal{P}\oplus\bar{\mathcal{P}}\,,\qquad\mathcal{P}\cap\bar{\mathcal{P}}=0\,,\\[8.53581pt]
\mathcal{P}=\{X-i\hat{\phi}X\;|\;\forall X\in\mathcal{C}\}\,,\qquad\bar{\mathcal{P}}=\{X+i\hat{\phi}X\;|\;\forall X\in\mathcal{C}\}\,.\end{array}

This splitting equips
M
with an
almost CR structure
of hyper surface type, which can be viewed as an odd-dimensional counterpart of the almost complex structure

The abbreviation CR means either ‘Cauchy–Riemann’ or ‘Complex-Real’.
. Furthermore, the presence of the positive definite Levi form

(2.15)

L_{\lambda}(X,Y)=\omega(\phi(X),Y)=g(X,Y)\qquad\forall X,Y\in\mathcal{C}\,,

allows one to identify this almost CR structure as
strongly pseudoconvex

[

]
. An almost CR structure is called integrable (or just CR structure) if the holomorphic subbundle
\mathcal{P}
is involutive, i.e.,
[\mathcal{P},\mathcal{P}]\subset\mathcal{P}
. The last condition amounts to the vanishing of the
(1,2)
-tensor field

(2.16)

Q_{ij}^{k}=\nabla^{g}_{j}\phi_{i}^{k}+\xi^{k}\phi_{i}^{n}\nabla^{g}_{j}\lambda_{n}+\phi^{k}_{n}\nabla^{g}_{j}\xi^{n}\lambda_{i}\,.

This result is due to Tanno

For the purposes of deformation quantization, we also introduce the dual vector bundles
\mathcal{C}^{\ast}
and
\mathcal{P}^{\ast}
. The sections of the former are naturally identified with the
1
-forms on
M
that annihilate the Reeb vector field
\xi
. To characterise the latter it is convenient to equip the complexified cotangent bundle
T^{\ast}_{\mathbb{C}}M
with the Hermitian form

(2.17)

h(\alpha,\beta)=\bar{g}(\alpha,\beta+i\phi^{t}(\beta))=i\pi(\alpha,\beta)+\bar{g}(\alpha,\beta)\,,

where
\alpha
and
\beta
are arbitrary
1
-forms. Let
\hat{h}
denote the restriction of
h
onto
\mathcal{C}^{\ast}
. Then
\mathcal{P}^{\ast}\subset\mathcal{C}^{\ast}_{\mathbb{C}}

coincides with the right kernel of
\hat{h}
, that is,
\hat{h}(-,\mathcal{P}^{\ast})=0
. In view of Hermiticity the antiholomorphic distribution
\bar{\mathcal{P}}^{\ast}
generates then the left kernel of
\hat{h}
. The contact metric connection (
2.7
) clearly respects
h
. We will refer to the Hermitian form (
2.17
) as the
Wick tensor
.

Finally, a contact metric structure
(\phi,\xi,\lambda,g,)
is said to be
K
-contact
if
\xi
is a Killing vector of the associated metric
g
, i.e.,
\mathcal{L}_{\xi}g=0
. If, in addition, the corresponding CR structure is integrable (
Q=0
), then the contact metric manifold is called
Sasakian
. It is known
[
18
, Thm. 11]
that the property of being Sasakian is equivalent to the identity

(2.18)

2\nabla^{g}_{k}\omega_{ij}=\lambda_{i}g_{kj}-\lambda_{j}g_{ki}\,.

In dimension
3
, the holomorphic distribution
\mathcal{P}
, being one-dimensional, is automatically integrable. Therefore every
3
-dimensional K-contact manifold is Sasakian.

3.
Wick deformation quantization of contact metric manifolds

As mentioned in the previous section, by the deformation quantization of a contact metric manifold
M
we understand the quantization of the Poisson algebra
(C^{\infty}_{\xi}(M),\{\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,\})
associated with the canonical Jacobi structure on
M
. It is remarkable that the Jacobi
bracket
\{\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,\}
is determined by the contact structure alone and in no way involves the associated metric. The general definition of the Weyl deformation quantization of contact manifolds was formulated in our recent paper
[
8
]
.
The presence of the canonical Hermitian form (
2.17
) on
M
suggests the following definition of Wick deformation quantization.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\nu
be a formal deformation parameter and
C_{\mathbb{C}}^{\infty}(M)
be the commutative algebra of smooth complex-valued functions on
M
.
By a
Wick deformation quantization
of a contact metric manifold
(M;\phi,\xi,\lambda,g)
we understand a pair
(\ast,\Delta)
, where
\ast
and
\Delta
are, respectively, bilinear and linear operators on
C_{\mathbb{C}}^{\infty}(M)[\![\nu]\!]
of the form

a\ast b=ab+\frac{\nu}{2}h(da,db)+\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\nu^{k}C_{k}(a,b)\,,\qquad\Delta a=\xi a+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\nu^{k}\Delta_{k}a\,.

It is also assumed that

(1)

C_{k}
and
\Delta_{k}
are differential operators on
C^{\infty}(M)
, which are extended to
C_{\mathbb{C}}^{\infty}(M)[\![\nu]\!]
by
\mathbb{C}[\![\nu]\!]
-linearity;

(2)

the elements of the subspace
\ker\Delta
form an associative algebra w.r.t. the
\ast
-product, that is,

\Delta(a\ast b)=0\quad\mbox{and}\quad(a\ast b)\ast c=a\ast(b\ast c)

whenever
\Delta a=\Delta b=\Delta c=0
.

The pair
(\ker\Delta,\ast)
is called the
algebra of quantum observables
.

An important property of the operators
\ast
and
\Delta
is their
locality
, i.e., the possibility of restriction on functions
defined in any open domain
U\subset M
. By abuse of language, we will refer to this as the restriction of the operators
\ast
and
\Delta
onto the domain
U
.

In order to construct the Wick deformation quantization above we will follow Fedosov’s approach
[
9
]
. Let
U
be a coordinate chart on
M
with coordinates
\{x^{i}\}
and denote by
\{y^{i}\}
the fibre coordinates on the tangent bundle
TU
w.r.t. the natural frame
\{\partial/\partial x^{i}\}
. For each point
x\in M
define the
formal Wick algebra

W_{x}
as the space spanned by the formal power series with complex coefficients

(3.1)

a(\nu,y)=\sum_{k,l=0}^{\infty}\nu^{k}a_{k,i_{1}\cdots i_{l}}y^{i_{1}}\cdots y^{i_{l}}

endowed with the
\circ
-product

(3.2)

(a\circ b)(y)=\exp\left(\frac{\nu}{2}h^{ij}(x)\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial y^{i}\partial z^{j}}\right)a(y)b(z)\Big|_{z=y}\,,

h^{ij}(x)
being the components of the Wick tensor at
x
. The
\circ
-product is known to be associative, but not commutative. Taking the union of algebras
W_{x}
, we obtain the bundle of formal Wick algebras
W
, whose space of sections will be denoted by
\mathcal{W}
. Prescribing the formal variables
y
’s and
\nu
the degrees
1
and
2
, respectively, makes
\mathcal{W}
into an
\mathbb{N}
-graded algebra, that is,
\mathcal{W}=\bigoplus_{n\geq 0}\mathcal{W}_{n}
. We refer to this grading as the
\nu
-degree
.
Associated with the
\nu
-degree is the descending filtration

{F}^{k}\mathcal{W}\supset{F}^{k+1}\mathcal{W}\,,\qquad{F}^{k}\mathcal{W}=\bigoplus_{n\geq k}\mathcal{W}_{n}\,,\qquad k=0,1,\ldots\,,

which defines topology and convergence in the space of formal power series.
Sometimes we will use another natural
\mathbb{N}
-grading on the vector space
{\mathcal{W}}
, which is given by the polynomial degree in
y
’s. We refer to it as
y
-degree
and write
{\mathcal{W}}=\bigoplus_{n\geq 0}{\mathcal{W}}_{(n)}
. Unlike the
\nu
-degree, the
y
-degree does not respect the
\circ
-product.

The most interesting to us will be the subalgebra
\mathcal{W}^{\xi}\subset\mathcal{W}
of
\xi
-transverse sections of
W
. This is defined as

(3.3)

\mathcal{W}^{\xi}\ni a\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\xi^{i}\frac{\partial a}{\partial y^{i}}=0\,.

Tensoring the algebra
\mathcal{W}
with the exterior algebra
\Lambda(M)=\bigoplus_{n\geq 0}\Lambda_{n}(M)
of differential forms on
M
, we obtain the bigraded algebra
\mathcal{W}\otimes\Lambda
, the second grading being the form degree. We will use the same symbol
\circ
for multiplication in the tensor product algebra.
Let us also introduce the commutator of two elements of
\mathcal{W}\otimes\Lambda
by

(3.4)

[a,b]=a\circ b-(-1)^{nm}b\circ a

for all
a\in\mathcal{W}\otimes\Lambda_{n}
and
b\in\mathcal{W}\otimes\Lambda_{m}
. The commutator makes the space
\mathcal{W}\otimes\Lambda
into a graded Lie algebra w.r.t. the form degree. It is easy to see that the centre of this Lie algebra is given by
\Lambda(M)[\![\nu]\!]
. The algebra
\mathcal{W}\otimes\Lambda
is endowed with the differential
\delta
of bidegree
(-1,1)
:

(3.5)

\delta=dx^{i}\wedge\frac{\partial}{\partial y^{i}}\,,\qquad\delta^{2}=0\,.

It is clear that

(3.6)

\delta(a\circ b)=\delta a\circ b+(-1)^{n}a\circ\delta b\qquad\forall a\in\mathcal{W}\otimes\Lambda_{n}\,,\quad\forall b\in\mathcal{W}\otimes\Lambda\,.

Upon restriction onto the differential subalgebra
\mathcal{W}^{\xi}\otimes\Lambda
one can write the differential
\delta
as

(3.7)

\delta a=\frac{i}{\nu}[\omega_{ij}y^{i}dx^{j},a]\qquad\forall a\in\mathcal{W}^{\xi}\otimes\Lambda\,.

In order to describe the cohomology of the differential
\delta
it is convenient to split
\Lambda(M)
into the direct sum

(3.8)

\Lambda=\Lambda^{\xi}\oplus\Lambda^{\lambda}\,,\qquad\Lambda^{\xi}=\{a\in\Lambda\,|\,i_{\xi}a=0\}\,,\qquad\Lambda^{\lambda}=\{\lambda a\,|\,\forall a\in\Lambda\}\,.

Since the differential
\delta
leaves invariant the subspaces
\Lambda^{\xi}
and
\Lambda^{\lambda}
, the groups of
\delta
-cohomology

H_{m,n}(\mathcal{W}\otimes\Lambda)=\mathrm{ker}(\delta:\mathcal{W}_{m}\otimes\Lambda_{n}\rightarrow\mathcal{W}_{m-1}\otimes\Lambda_{n+1})/\delta(\mathcal{W}_{m+1}\otimes\Lambda_{n-1})

split as
H_{m,n}(\mathcal{W}\otimes\Lambda)=H_{m,n}(\mathcal{W}\otimes\Lambda^{\xi})\oplus H_{m,n}(\mathcal{W}\otimes\Lambda^{\lambda})
.

Proposition 3.2
.

With the above notation

\begin{array}[]{l}H_{0,0}(\mathcal{W}^{\xi}\otimes\Lambda)\simeq H_{0,0}(\mathcal{W}^{\xi}\otimes\Lambda^{\xi})\simeq C_{\mathbb{C}}^{\infty}(M)[\![\nu]\!]\,,\\[8.53581pt]
H_{0,1}(\mathcal{W}^{\xi}\otimes\Lambda)\simeq H_{0,1}(\mathcal{W}^{\xi}\otimes\Lambda^{\lambda})\simeq C_{\mathbb{C}}^{\infty}(M)[\![\nu]\!]\,,\\[8.53581pt]
H_{m,n}(\mathcal{W}^{\xi}\otimes\Lambda)=0\,,\quad\forall m>0\quad\mbox{or}\quad n>1\,.\end{array}

For the sake of completeness we reproduce the proof of this statement from
[
8
]

Proof.

The
(1,1)
-tensor
P^{i}_{j}=\delta^{i}_{j}-\xi^{i}\lambda_{j}
defines an orthogonal projection in the tangent bundle
TM
:

P^{2}=P\,,\qquad\mathrm{Tr}P=2m\,,

P^{i}_{j}=\pi^{ik}\omega_{kj}\,,\quad\lambda_{i}P^{i}_{j}=0\,,\quad P^{i}_{j}\xi^{j}=0\,,\quad P^{k}_{i}\omega_{kj}=\omega_{ij}\,,\quad\pi^{ik}P^{j}_{k}=\pi^{ij}\,.

Define the operator
\delta^{\ast}:\mathcal{W}^{\xi}_{m}\otimes\Lambda_{n}\rightarrow\mathcal{W}^{\xi}_{m+1}\otimes\Lambda_{n-1}
as

\delta^{\ast}=y^{i}P_{i}^{j}i_{\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{j}}}\,.

It is easy to see that
(\delta^{\ast})^{2}=0
,
\delta^{\ast}\lambda=0
, and

(3.9)

(\delta\delta^{\ast}+\delta^{\ast}\delta)a=(m+n)a-\lambda i_{\xi}a\qquad\forall a\in\mathcal{W}^{\xi}_{m}\otimes\Lambda_{n}\,.

Notice that
\delta^{\ast}
is not a derivation of the
\circ
-product, but it maps
\xi
-transverse forms of
{\mathcal{W}}^{\xi}\otimes\Lambda
to
\xi
-transverse. Now define the operator

(3.10)

\delta^{-1}:\mathcal{W}^{\xi}_{m}\otimes\Lambda_{n}\rightarrow\mathcal{W}^{\xi}_{m+1}\otimes\Lambda_{n-1}

by the relations

(3.11)

\delta^{-1}a^{\prime}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{1}{m+n}\delta^{\ast}a^{\prime}\,,&m+n>0\,;\\
0\,,&m=n=0\,;\end{array}\right.\qquad\delta^{-1}a^{\prime\prime}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{1}{m+n-1}\delta^{\ast}a^{\prime\prime}\,,&m+n>1\,;\\
0\,,&m+n=1\,;\end{array}\right.

for all
a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{W}_{(m)}\otimes\Lambda_{n}^{\xi}
and
a^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{W}_{(m)}\otimes\Lambda^{\lambda}_{n}
. Writing
a=a^{\prime}+a^{\prime\prime}
, one can check that

(3.12)

(\delta\delta^{-1}+\delta^{-1}\delta)a=a-\Pi a\,,

where the operator
\Pi
acts as

\Pi a=a(x,0,0,\nu)+\lambda(i_{\xi}a)(x,0,0,\nu)\qquad\forall\,a=a(x,y,dx,\nu)\in\mathcal{W}^{\xi}\otimes\Lambda\,.

Clearly,
\Pi^{2}=\Pi
. Eq. (
3.12

\delta

\mathrm{ker}(1-\Pi)
spanned by the sums
a+\lambda b
with
a,b\in C_{\mathbb{C}}^{\infty}(M)[\![\nu]\!]
. On the other hand, the elements of the form
a+\lambda b
are obviously nontrivial cocycles and the proposition follows.

d_{\nabla}(a\circ b)=d_{\nabla}a\circ b+(-1)^{n}a\circ d_{\nabla}b\qquad\forall a\in{\mathcal{W}}\otimes\Lambda_{n}\,,\quad\forall b\in{\mathcal{W}}\otimes\Lambda\,.

Define the elements

(3.15)

T=\frac{1}{2}T_{kij}y^{k}dx^{i}\wedge dx^{j}\in{\mathcal{W}}^{\xi}_{1}\otimes\Lambda_{2}\quad\mbox{and}\quad R=\frac{1}{4}R_{ijkl}y^{i}y^{j}dx^{k}\wedge dx^{l}\in{\mathcal{W}}^{\xi}_{2}\otimes\Lambda_{2}

4.
Quantum observables

In this section, we will examine more closely the space of quantum observables
\ker\Delta
. In particular, we will consider the implications of a contact metric manifold being K-contact.

To study the solution space of the equation
\Delta a=0
, we expand the operator
\Delta
and an element
a\in C^{\infty}_{\mathbb{C}}(M)[\![\nu]\!]
in
powers of
\nu
:

(4.1)

\Delta=\xi+\nu\Delta_{1}+\nu^{2}\Delta_{2}+\cdots\,,\qquad a=a_{0}+\nu a_{1}+\nu^{2}a_{2}+\cdots\,.

On substituting these back into the equation, we get an infinite sequence of relations, which start as

(4.2)

\xi a_{0}=0\,,\qquad\Delta_{1}a_{0}+\xi a_{1}=0\,,\quad\ldots

The first equation says that
a_{0}
is a
\xi
-invariant function on
M
.
As mentioned in Sec.
2
, the
\xi
-invariant functions form a closed Poisson algebra
C^{\infty}_{\xi}(M)
w.r.t. the Jacobi bracket (
2.6
). It is natural to call

\big(C^{\infty}_{\xi}(M),\{\cdot\,,\,\cdot\}\big)
the
algebra of classical observables
. We thus conclude that the
\nu
-expansion of any quantum observable starts from a classical one. The higher powers of
\nu
are ‘quantum corrections’ we need to add to a
classical observable
a_{0}
to promote it at the quantum level. The corrections are to be found successively from the chain of equations (
4.2
).
The solvability of these equations is not ensured in advance. It depends on a particular contact metric structure and a classical observable
a_{0}
to be quantized. Therefore, in contrast to the deformation quantization of Poisson manifolds, not every classical observable on a contact manifold admits quantization.
We say that a classical observable
a_{0}\in C^{\infty}_{\xi}(M)
is
quantizable
if system (
4.2
) admits a solution. Otherwise, we speak about obstructions to quantization.
The first obstruction comes with the second equation in (
4.2
). It says that the function
-\Delta_{1}a_{0}
, defined by a classical observable
a_{0}
, must be a gradient of some other function
a_{1}\in C^{\infty}(M)
along the Reeb vector field
\xi
.
Recall that
\Delta_{1}
is a second-order differential operator defined by Eq. (
3.29
). After lengthy, albeit straightforward calculations, one can bring this operator into the form

(4.3)

\Delta_{1}=-\frac{1}{4}(\mathcal{L}_{\xi}g^{ij})\nabla_{i}\nabla_{j}+\frac{1}{12}(\mathcal{L}_{\xi}g^{ij})(\nabla^{g}_{i}\omega_{jk})\pi^{kn}\nabla_{n}\,.

The following proposition is now obvious.

Proposition 4.1
.

On every K-contact manifold,
\Delta_{1}=0
.

Thus, every classical observable on a K-contact manifold can be promoted to a quantum one up to the first order in
\nu
(e.g. setting
a_{1}=0
). Using the physics language, we can say that every classical observable on a K-contact manifold is quantizable in semi-classical approximation.

In the general case, the operator
\Delta_{1}
is different from zero, so the equation
\xi a_{1}+\Delta_{1}a_{0}=0
may present a real obstruction to quantization.
To better understand this obstruction it is useful to rewrite the operator (
4.3
) in terms of the metric connection:

(4.4)

\Delta_{1}=-\frac{1}{4}(\mathcal{L}_{\xi}g^{ij})\nabla^{g}_{i}\nabla^{g}_{j}-\frac{1}{4}(\mathcal{L}_{\xi}g^{ij})S_{ij}^{k}\nabla^{g}_{k}+\frac{1}{12}(\mathcal{L}_{\xi}g^{ij})(\nabla^{g}_{i}\omega_{jk})\pi^{kn}\nabla^{g}_{n}\,.

(The operator is supposed to act on scalar functions.) Next, one can find that

(4.5)

(\mathcal{L}_{\xi}g^{ij})S_{ij}^{k}\nabla^{g}_{k}=\frac{1}{2}(\mathcal{L}_{\xi}g^{ij})(\nabla^{g}_{i}\omega_{jk})\pi^{kn}\nabla^{g}_{n}+\frac{1}{2}(\mathcal{L}_{\xi}g^{ij})(\mathcal{L}_{\xi}g_{ij})\nabla_{\xi}^{g}\,.

The standard formulas for the commutator of the Lie and covariant derivatives,

(4.6)

[\mathcal{L}_{\xi},\nabla_{i}]f=0\,,\qquad[\mathcal{L}_{\xi},\nabla_{i}^{g}]\alpha_{j}=-({}^{g}\!R^{k}_{jni}\xi^{n}+\nabla^{g}_{i}\nabla^{g}_{j}\xi^{k})\alpha_{k}\,,

allows us to write

(4.7)

(\mathcal{L}_{\xi}g^{ij})\nabla^{g}_{i}\nabla^{g}_{j}=[\mathcal{L}_{\xi},\mathbb{L}]-g^{ij}[\mathcal{L}_{\xi},\nabla^{g}_{i}]\nabla^{g}_{j}=[\mathcal{L}_{\xi},\mathbb{L}]+({}^{g}\!R^{k}_{nlm}g^{nm}\xi^{l}+g^{nm}\nabla_{n}^{g}\nabla_{m}^{g}\xi^{k})\nabla_{k}^{g}\,,

where
{}^{g}\!R_{nlm}^{k}
is the Riemann curvature tensor and

(4.8)

\mathbb{L}=g^{nm}\nabla_{n}^{g}\nabla_{m}^{g}

is the Laplace operator on scalar functions. Combining all the parts together, we finally get

(4.9)

-4\Delta_{1}=[\mathcal{L}_{\xi},\mathbb{L}]+\frac{1}{2}(\mathcal{L}_{\xi}g_{ij})(\mathcal{L}_{\xi}g^{ij})\nabla_{\xi}^{g}+\nabla_{\zeta}\,.

Here we introduced the vector field

(4.10)

\zeta=\Big({}^{g}\!R^{i}{}_{nkm}g^{nm}\xi^{k}+g^{nm}\nabla^{g}_{n}\nabla^{g}_{m}\xi^{i}+\frac{1}{6}(\mathcal{L}_{\xi}g^{nm})(\nabla^{g}_{n}\omega_{mk})\pi^{ki}\Big)\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}\,.

The commutator in (
4.9
) suggests to look for the first quantum correction in the form

(4.11)

a_{1}=\frac{1}{4}\mathbb{L}a_{0}+\frac{1}{4}a_{1}^{\prime}\,,

where
a^{\prime}_{1}
satisfies the first-order differential equation

(4.12)

\xi a_{1}^{\prime}=\zeta a_{0}\,.

Below, we state some necessary conditions for this equation to have a solution. Recall that integral curves of the Reeb vector field
\xi
are called
characteristics
of a contact metric manifold
M
. The function
a_{0}
, being
\xi

The most interesting are closed characteristics, that is, periodic orbits of the Reeb flow
3
3

3

The famous Weinstein’s conjecture states that every compact manifold

[
3

.

(4.13)

\Psi_{\gamma}[a_{0}]:=\int_{\gamma}\lambda\zeta a_{0}=0\,.

We call

(associated with a closed characteristic

Suppose now that the contact metric manifold

(a,b)=\int_{M}v{a}b\,.

Here

\xi(f^{\prime}(a_{0})a_{1})=\zeta f(a_{0})\,.

Since the volume form is

\Phi_{f}[a_{0}]:=\int_{M}v\zeta f(a_{0})=0\,.

We call this functional the

implies an obstruction to quantization. Define the

\chi:=\mathrm{div}_{v}\zeta=\nabla_{i}^{g}\zeta^{i}\,.

The equality takes place because the canonical and Riemannian volume forms on

Integrating by parts in (

Eqs. (

5.
Sasakian manifolds and Wick property

Now is the time to explain the adjective ‘Wick’ in the name of the star product above. First, we recall the definition of the Wick deformation quantization on complex manifolds. Let

\ast
-product, that is, a formal associative deformation of the pointwise multiplication of complex-valued functions on

-product to

This result is quite expected, given the analogy between Kähler and Sasakian geometries. Less obvious is the fact that any classical observable on a Sasakian manifold is quantizable.

Let us start with some auxiliary constructions and statements. First, we define the following two subspaces in the

The subspaces

Lemma 5.2

On every Sasakian manifold

Notice that

(5.19)

g_{ij}h^{in}h^{jm}=h^{ni}h^{mj}g_{ij}=0\,,\qquad\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}h^{in}h^{jm}=h^{ni}h^{mj}\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}=\xi^{n}\xi^{m}\,.

Now we are ready to show that
\delta^{-1}T\in\mathrm{R}
. Indeed,

(5.20)

(\delta^{-1}T\circ b)|_{y=0}=\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{\nu}{2}\right)^{2}\lambda(g_{ij}-\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j})h^{in}h^{jm}\left.\frac{\partial^{2}b}{\partial y^{n}\partial y^{m}}\right|_{y=0}=0

for all
b\in{\mathcal{W}}^{\xi}\otimes\Lambda
. In the same way, one can see that
\delta^{-1}T\in\mathrm{L}
. Therefore,
\delta^{-1}T\in\mathrm{A}
.

∎

As mentioned in Sec.
2
, every Sasakian manifold carries a CR structure. This is given by the integrable (anti)holomorphic distribution (
2.14
). Let
U
be an open domain in
M
. A complex-valued function
a\in C_{\mathbb{C}}^{\infty}(U)
is said to be
CR-holomorphic
(resp.
CR-antiholomorphic
) if it is annihilated by all vector fields belonging to the antiholomorphic distribution
\bar{\mathcal{P}}|_{U}
(resp.
\mathcal{P}|_{U}
). By
\mathbb{C}[\![\nu]\!]
-linearity, these definition extends to the space
C_{\mathbb{C}}^{\infty}(U)[\![\nu]\!]
. Of particular interest to us will be CR-(anti)holomorphic functions that are
\xi
-invariant. It is easy to see that any
\xi
-invariant CR-holomorphic (resp. -antiholomorphic) on
U
function
a
satisfies the equations

(5.21)

h^{ji}\frac{\partial a}{\partial x^{i}}\Big|_{U}=0\qquad\Big(\mbox{resp.}\quad\frac{\partial a}{\partial x^{i}}h^{ij}\Big|_{U}=0\Big)\,.

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
U
be an open domain in a Sasakian manifold
M
. Consider the restriction of
\ast
and
\Delta
on
U\subset M
. Then any
\xi
-invariant CR-holomorphic (resp. -antiholomorphic) function
a
on
U
is a quantum observable, i.e.,
\Delta a=0
. Moreover, the Wick property holds

(5.22)

c\ast a=c\cdot a\qquad(\mbox{resp.}\quad a\ast c=a\cdot c)

for any quantum observable
c
on
U
.

Proof.

The quantum lift (
3.26
) of a CR-holomorphic function
a
is determined by the Fedosov connection (
3.19
), where
r
is found recursively from Eq. (
3.23
). By Lemmas
5.1
–
5.3
,
\delta^{-1}(T+R)\in\mathrm{A}
. Since
d_{\nabla}
and
\delta^{-1}
leave invariant the
\circ
-subalgebra
\mathrm{A}
, iteration of Eq. (
3.23
) gives a unique solution
r
belonging to
\mathrm{A}
. Let us expand both
r
and
Qa
according to the
\nu
-degree:

(5.23)

r=r_{2}+r_{3}+r_{4}+\cdots\,,\qquad Qa=a+a_{1}+a_{2}+\cdots\,.

The expansion for
Qa
follows from the recurrence relations

(5.24)

a_{1}=\delta^{-1}d_{\nabla}a\,,\qquad a_{n+1}=\delta^{-1}\Big(d_{\nabla}a_{n}+\frac{i}{\nu}\sum_{k=1}^{n}[r_{n-k+2},a_{k}]\Big)\,,\quad n=1,2,\ldots

We claim that
a_{1}\in\mathrm{L}
. Indeed,
a_{1}=y^{i}\nabla_{i}a
and

(5.25)

(c\circ a_{1})|_{y=0}=\frac{\nu}{2}\frac{\partial c}{\partial y^{j}}h^{ji}\frac{\partial a}{\partial x^{i}}\Big|_{y=0}=0\qquad\forall c\in{\mathcal{W}}^{\xi}\otimes\Lambda\,,

as
a
is
\xi
-invariant and CR-holomorphic on
U
. Since
r_{k}\in\mathrm{A}
and the
\mathrm{A}
-bimodule
\mathrm{L}
is invariant under the action of
d_{\nabla}
and
\delta^{-1}
, Eq. (
5.24
) implies that all
a_{k}\in\mathrm{L}
.

By Theorem
3.5
,
Qa
is a flat section of
{\mathcal{W}}_{D}
iff

(5.26)

\Delta a=i_{\xi}\Pi DQa=(D_{\xi}Qa)|_{y=0}=0\,.

Explicitly,

(5.27)

\Delta a=\xi a-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\xi^{i}\frac{\partial a_{k}}{\partial y^{i}}\Big|_{y=0}+i_{\xi}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\Big(d_{\nabla}a_{k}+\frac{i}{\nu}[r,a_{k}]\Big)\Big|_{y=0}=0\,.

Here the first summand vanishes due to the
\xi
-invariance of
a
, the first sum is zero as all
a_{k}
’s are
\xi
-transversal, and
the second sum goes to zero as an element of
\mathrm{L}
.

It remains to prove the Wick property (
5.22
). Let
c
be an arbitrary quantum observable, then

(5.28)

c\ast a=\Pi(Qc\circ Qa)=c\cdot a+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(Qc\circ a_{k})|_{y=0}=c\cdot a

because all
a_{k}\in\mathrm{L}
. The proof for CR-antiholomorphic functions is analogous.

∎

It follows from the theorem above that for every CR-holomorphic function
a
and every CR-antiholomorphic function
b
on
U\subset M
, the product
b\ast a=b\cdot a
is a quantum observable, that is,
\Delta(ab)=0
. On the other hand, the products of
\xi
-invariant CR-holomorphic and -antiholomorphic functions generate the whole space of
\xi
-invariant functions on
U
. This means that the operator
\Delta
, being local, must be of the form
\Delta=B\xi
for some formal differential operator
B=1+\mathcal{O}(\nu)
. Thus, we arrive at

Corollary 5.5
.

On every Sasakian manifold
M
, all classical observables – the elements of
C^{\infty}_{\xi}(M)
– are quantizable without any quantum corrections.

This remarkable property distinguishes Sasakian manifolds from other contact metric manifolds and conforms nicely to their intuitive understanding as odd-dimensional analogues of Kähler manifolds.
