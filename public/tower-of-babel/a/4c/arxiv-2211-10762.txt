Continuous sparse domination and dimensionless weighted estimates for the Bakry Riesz vector
K. Domelevo, S. Petermichl, K.A. Škreb

Abstract
We present a fundamentally new proof of the dimensionless L p \textup{L}^{p} boundedness of the Bakry-Riesz vector on manifolds with bounded geometry. Our proof has the significant advantage that it allows for a much stronger conclusion than previous arguments, namely that of some new dimensionless weighted estimates with optimal exponent. Part of the importance of this task lies in the novelty of the techniques: we develop the self-similarity argument known as ‘sparse domination’ in the setting of uniformly integrable càdlàg Hilbert space valued martingales and extend the domination to a process with infinite memory. We provide a range of optimal weighted estimates and weak type estimates for these stochastic processes. Previous geometric Riesz transform estimates relied on Bellman functions and did not provide this range of weighted estimates. The development of sparse domination in this probabilistic setting and its use for high dimensional problems is new.

A short summary

In this paper, we are primarily interested in dimensionless weighted and unweighted norm estimates of the Riesz vector on manifolds as well as related stochastic processes that are used to model these operators. These are loosely tied to the concept of differential subordination.

[

[

]

Z
by Li is defined via an auxiliary martingale
Y
that is differentially subordinate to
X
, but
Z
is not a martingale. (Here,
X
models
f
and an expectation of
Z
models
Rf
.) The process
Z
is defined via
Z_{0}=0
and evolves according to the stochastic ODE

\mathrm{d}Z_{t}=(V_{t}-a\Id)Z_{t}\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{d}Y_{t}.

(The sign of the operator
(V_{t}-a\Id)
is negative and it will be explained later how it relates to the curvature of the space.) The ODE above defines a process with memory, while classical sparse type arguments are self similar and resist to dominating objects with memory. A new type of domination procedure comes into play to overcome this difficulty, relying on the specific form of the ODE. Another needed tool is a weak type estimate of the maximal operator of
Z
. This is the only part of our proof that uses a (simple) Bellman function. Similar processes have been considered by Bañuelos-Osekowski
[
6
]

The sparse domination technique has witnessed considerable efforts in the last several years and has been used to prove numerous new results in harmonic analysis, using sparse operators defined on cubes. These cannot give dimensionless estimates and are often not adapted to the non-doubling case. Our use of stochastic processes and stopping times with a continuous parameter to tackle problems in geometric harmonic analysis is entirely new. One can deduce, in general, from such a domination a dimensionless bound. The sparse operators are particularly well suited for working with weights, which is why this so obtained dimensionless estimate also holds in the weighted setting.

2
Bakry Riesz transforms on manifolds.

Let
(M,g)
be a complete Riemannian manifold with metric
g
and of dimension

n
. Let
\Delta
be the non-positive Laplace-Beltrami operator,
\nabla

the gradient operator, and
\nu
the volume measure on
(M,g)
such that

\mathrm{d}\nu(x)=\sqrt{\det g(x)}\mathrm{d}x.

Let moreover
\mu_{\varphi}
be a
weighted volume measure on
M
with

\mathrm{d}\mu_{\varphi}(x)=e^{-\varphi(x)}\mathrm{d}\nu(x),

where
\varphi(x)\in\mathcal{C}^{2}(M)
. The weighted Laplacian

\Delta_{\varphi}
with respect to
\mu_{\varphi}
on
M
is defined for any function

f
by

\Delta_{\varphi}f:=\Delta f-\nabla\varphi\cdot\nabla f.

We assume that
\mu_{\varphi}(M)<\infty
and by normalizing, we may assume without
loss of generality that
\mu_{\varphi}
is actually a probability measure. The
Bakry-Emery curvature tensor associated with
\Delta_{\varphi}
is defined by

\operatorname{Ric}_{\varphi}=\operatorname{Ric}+\nabla^{2}\varphi,

where
\operatorname{Ric}
denotes the Ricci curvature tensor on
M
and
\nabla^{2}\varphi
the Hessian of
\varphi
with respect to the Levi-Civita connection
on
(M,g)
.

All over this paper, we assume that the Bakry-Emery curvature is bounded from below, i.e. there exists a non-negative constant
a
such that

\operatorname{Ric}_{\varphi}\geqslant-a.

This condition ensures that the manifold is stochastically complete and hence Brownian motion does not explode in finite time. We denote by
\vec{R}_{\varphi}^{a}
the Bakry Riesz transform defined as

\vec{R}_{\varphi}^{a}=\nabla\circ(a\Id-\Delta_{\varphi})^{-1/2},

where
\Id
denotes the identity. When
a=0
, we will denote the Riesz transforms just by
\vec{R}_{\varphi}
. It was proved already by Bakry
[
3
]
that for any
p>1
there exists a universal constant
C_{p}

such that for any function
f\in C_{0}^{\infty}(M)
, there holds

\|\vec{R}_{\varphi}^{a}f\|_{\textup{L}^{p}(\mu)}\leqslant C_{p}\|f\|_{\textup{L}^{p}(\mu)}.

However, the estimate did not have the asymptotic in
p
one would have expected. The more recent works mentioned earlier provided the correct growth in
p
near the end points.

2.1
Probabilistic representation of the Bakry Riesz vector on manifolds.

For this application, the familiar setting of continuous path processes is sufficient. Readers not familiar are encouraged to draw the relevant definitions from the next chapter, where they are rigorously given in greater generality. For the time being, we recall that the martingale
Y
is said to be
differentially subordinated
to the martingale
X
if the process
([X,X]_{t}-[Y,Y]_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
is non-negative and non-decreasing in
t
, where the bracket
[\cdot,\cdot]
denotes the usual quadratic covariation process for real or vector valued stochastic processes.

Let
B^{M}_{t}
be the diffusion process on
M
with generator
\Delta_{\varphi}
and initial distribution
\mu
obeying the stochastic differential equation

\mathrm{d}B^{M}_{t}=U_{t}\mathrm{d}W_{t}-\nabla\varphi(B^{M}_{t})\mathrm{d}t,

where
W_{t}
is the Brownian motion on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and

U_{t}:T_{B_{0}^{M}}M\to T_{B_{t}^{M}}M

denotes the stochastic parallel transport on
M
along the trajectory
\{B^{M}_{s},0\leqslant s\leqslant t\}
. Let further
B_{t}
be the one-dimensional Brownian motion started at
y>0
with the normalisation
E[(B_{t})^{2}]=2t
such that its generator is
\partial^{2}/\partial y^{2}
.
Following
[
36
,
25
,
24
]
, there exists a diffusion process

\tilde{B}_{t}=(B^{M}_{t},B_{t})

on
(M,\mathbb{R}^{+})
– the so-called
background radiation process
– associated to the generator
\Delta_{\varphi}+\partial^{2}/\partial y^{2}
and with the initial distribution
\mu\otimes\delta_{y}
.

Using a martingale approach, one can represent the Riesz vector
\vec{R}_{\varphi}^{a}
via a probabilistic representation. In the literature, it first appeared in
[
25
]
, where the Riesz transform was defined on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. In
[
1
]
Arcozzi extended this formula to compact Lie groups and spheres. In
[
32
]
,
[
33
]
X.-D. Li presented a new formula adapted to complete Riemannian manifolds. The representation formula of the Riesz vector in this setting for a complete manifold with
\Ric_{\varphi}\geqslant-a
is as follows

-\frac{1}{2}(\vec{R}_{\varphi}^{a}f)(x)=\lim_{y\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}_{y}\left[e^{-a\tau}M_{\tau}\int_{0}^{\tau}e^{as}M_{s}^{-1}\nabla Q^{a}f(B^{M}_{s},B_{s})\mathrm{d}B_{s}|B^{M}_{\tau}=x\right],

(1)

where
y
is the starting point of the random walk and

•

Q^{a}f(x,y)=e^{-y\sqrt{a\Id-\Delta_{\varphi}}}f(x)
is the Poisson extension;

•

\tau=\inf\{t>0:B_{t}=0\}
is the stopping time upon hitting the boundary of the upper half space;

•

M_{t}
is the solution to the matrix-valued stochastic differential equation

\mathrm{d}M_{t}=V_{t}M_{t}\mathrm{d}t,\ \ \ M_{0}=\Id,

for some adapted and continuous process
(V_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
taking values in the set of symmetric and non-positive
n\times n
matrices and arises from the geometry of the manifold.

Equivalently, one can rewrite this formula as

-\frac{1}{2}(\vec{R}_{\varphi}^{a}f)(x)=\lim_{y\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}_{y}\left[Z_{\tau}|B^{M}_{\tau}=x\right],

(2)

where
Z_{t}
is a semi-martingale defined thanks to the auxiliary martingale
Y_{t}
as follows

\displaystyle Y_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\nabla Q^{a}f(B^{M}_{s},B_{s})\mathrm{d}B_{s},

\displaystyle Z_{t}=e^{-at}M_{t}\int_{0}^{t}e^{as}M_{s}^{-1}\mathrm{d}Y_{s},

where
(Y_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
is by construction differentially subordinate to
(X_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
defined as

X_{t}=Q^{a}f(B_{0}^{M},y)+\int_{0}^{t}(\nabla,\partial_{y})Q^{a}f(B^{M}_{s},B_{s})(U_{s}\mathrm{d}W_{s},\mathrm{d}B_{s}).

We will also define the auxiliary process

\widetilde{X}_{t}:=Q^{a}f(B^{M}_{t},B_{t})=X_{t}+a\int_{0}^{t}Q^{a}f(B^{M}_{s},B_{s})\mathrm{d}s,

where the second equality follows by Itô formula (as can be seen in
[
32
]
). By denoting

A_{t}=a\int_{0}^{t}Q^{a}f(B^{M}_{s},B_{s})\mathrm{d}s,

(3)

we can write

\widetilde{X}_{t}=X_{t}+A_{t}

and observe that
(\widetilde{X}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
is in fact a submartingale. Indeed, since
(A_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
is an increasing FV process and
(X_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
is a martingale, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\widetilde{X}_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{s})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}(X_{t}+A_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{s})\geqslant\mathbb{E}(X_{t}+A_{s}|\mathcal{F}_{s})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}(X_{t}|F_{s})+\mathbb{E}(A_{s}|\mathcal{F}_{s})=X_{s}+A_{s}=\widetilde{X}_{s}.

For the theorems we wish to prove, it will be sufficient to consider
f\geqslant 0
and thus
X
,
\widetilde{X}\geqslant 0
.

Remark 1
.

For the non-negative curvature case, i.e.
a=0
, the representation formula (
1
) reduces to

-\frac{1}{2}(\vec{R}_{\varphi}f)(x)=\lim_{y\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}_{y}\left[M_{\tau}\int_{0}^{\tau}M_{s}^{-1}\nabla Qf(B^{M}_{s},B_{s})\mathrm{d}B_{s}|B^{M}_{\tau}=x\right],

where
Qf(x,y)=e^{-y\sqrt{-\Delta_{\varphi}}}f(x)
. In this case obviously
A_{t}=0
and
X_{t}=\widetilde{X}_{t}
for every
t\geqslant 0
. In particular, that means that the process
(\widetilde{X}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
is a martingale.

(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathfrak{F},\mathbbm{P})
be a complete filtered

a
filtration that is right continuous, where
\mathcal{F}_{0}
contains all

\mathcal{F}
null sets. Let
X
and

be uniformly integrable adapted
càdlàg martingales with values in a separable Hilbert space that are
in a relation of differential subordination according to Bañuelos-Wang

7
]

[

]

[
13
,
14
,
15
]
in the discrete time setting.

Definition 1
.

Y
is called differentially subordinate to
X
if
[X,X]_{t}-[Y,Y]_{t}
is
non-negative and non-decreasing in
t
. In this case we also call the ordered pair
(X,Y)
differentially subordinate.

For the definition of the square bracket process and its properties, see for example
Dellacherie-Meyer
[
18
]
or Protter
[
42
]
. Notice
that in particular
[X,X]_{0}=|X_{0}|^{2}
so that differential subordination
of

with respect to

implies
|Y_{0}|^{2}\leqslant|X_{0}|^{2}
.
Recall that for any stopping time
\tau
the stopping sigma algebra is

\mathcal{F}_{\tau}=\{\Lambda\in\mathcal{F}:\Lambda\cap\{\tau\leqslant t\}\in\mathcal{F}_{t}\}.

To adapt the concept of a sparse set (usually of dyadic intervals), we make the following definition:

Definition 2
.

An increasing sequence
\{T^{j}\}_{j\geqslant 0}
of stopping times with nested sets
E_{j}=\{T^{j}<\infty\}
is called sparse if

\forall A^{j}\subset E_{j},A^{j}\in\mathcal{F}_{T^{j}}{\text{ there holds }}\mathbbm{P}(A^{j}\cap E_{j+1})\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\mathbbm{P}(A^{j}).

Notice that we do not have the simple plateaus as in the discrete time case and as it turns out, this definition using the sparseness localised to each subset measurable in the coarsest filtration is the correct replacement.

Definition 3
.

If
\{T^{j}\}_{j\geqslant 0}
is a sparse sequence and
E_{j}=\{T^{j}<\infty\}
its associated sequence of nested sets, we call

\mathcal{S}:X\mapsto\mathcal{S}(X)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}|X|_{T^{j}}\chi_{E_{j}}

a sparse operator.

In cases, where some of the processes involved lose their martingale structure, this definition has to be slightly modified. In this context, for a non-negative process
\widetilde{X}=(\widetilde{X}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
we call the operator

S(\widetilde{X})=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}(\widetilde{X}|\mathcal{F}_{T^{j}})\chi_{E_{j}}

(4)

a
sparse operator
associated to
\{T^{j}\}_{j\geqslant 0}
. In the above definition we denote by
\widetilde{X}=\widetilde{X}_{\infty}
also the closure of
\widetilde{X}
.

Observe that if the process
\widetilde{X}=X
is also a non-negative martingale, the definitions coincide.

Definition 4
.

The maximal function associated with
X
is

X^{\ast}=\sup_{t\geqslant 0}|X_{t}|.

We shall throughout this text denote by
the same letters also the closures of the martingales that arise.

Last, we will often refer to the weight’s probabilistic characteristic:

Definition 5
.

We call a positive uniformly integrable martingale
w
a weight. The
quantity
Q_{p}(w)
below is the
A_{p}
characteristic of the weight
w
. If

is finite, then we say

.

Q_{p}(w)=\sup_{\tau}\left\|\mathbbm{E}\left[\left(\frac{w_{\tau}}{w}\right)^{\frac{1}{p-1}}\middle|\,\mathcal{F}_{\tau}\right]^{p-1}\right\|_{\infty}=\sup_{\tau}\left\|w_{\tau}u^{p-1}_{\tau}\right\|_{\infty},

where the supremum runs over adapted stopping times

and where
we write

.

If necessary, we may put a superscript to emphasise the filtration:

.

4
Main results

We use a sparse operator with continuous stopping times, dominating the martingale
Y_{t}
as well as Li’s process
Z_{t}
whose projection is the Riesz vector. Instead of cutting the domain into cubes, we are therefore able to use the Poisson extension itself, thus resulting in clean dimensionless estimates.

The estimate we aim to show will be a consequence of a sparse domination of the stochastic processes
Y_{t}
and
Z_{t}
(see
[
31
,
29
]
). In the case of
Y_{t}
the proof is relatively close to the argument of Lacey
[
29
]
but in the case of
Z_{t}
, the object is not a martingale, so the sparse domination is different and one key of the proof relies on the weak-
L^{1}
estimate for the maximal function of the studied stochastic operator. A delicate new domination method also comes into play in a second step, utilising the defining stochastic differential equation.
We do not aim at the fullest generality here, keeping our goal in mind, an estimate for the Riesz vector. Here are the results concerning the Riesz vector:

We get a dimensionless unweighted estimate in
\textup{L}^{p}
spaces for the Riesz vector on manifolds with curvature bounded from below (Theorem
1
). The first proofs of this result are recent
[
16
]
,
[
33
]
,
[
6
]
and all based on a form of a Bellman function. All these cited Bellman proofs give a better numeric estimate than our proof, but as mentioned earlier, our proof extends (for free) to the weighted case, as long as the curvature is non-negative, which the previous proofs do not. Our
L^{p}
estimates are linear in
p
, which means proportional to
(p-1)^{-1}
when

and to

Theorem 1

(
\textup{L}^{p}
estimate for the Riesz vector)
.

Suppose that
M
is a complete Riemannian manifold without boundary and
\Ric_{\varphi}\geqslant-a
for some
a\geqslant 0
. Then for all
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(M)
,
p\in(1,\infty)
, we have the following dimension-free estimate

\|\vec{R}_{\varphi}^{a}f\|_{\textup{L}^{p}(T^{*}_{x}M)}\leqslant C_{p}\|f\|_{\textup{L}^{p}(M)},

(5)

where
C_{p}>0
is a constant depending only on
p
proportional to
(p-1)^{-1}
when
p\to 1
and to
p
when
p\to\infty
.

Our approach allows us to change the measure and also get dimensionless weighted estimates in
\textup{L}^{p}
spaces for the Riesz vector on manifolds with non-negative curvature. For the Euclidean setting, we refer to
[
20
]
. For the case of manifolds, such an estimate was previously only known for the case
p=2
and it was also based on a form of a certain Bellman function (see
[
17
]
). A priori the weight has to be globally in
\textup{L}^{2}
so as to be able to define the
flow characteristic

\tilde{Q}_{p}(w)=\sup_{x,y}(Q(w))(x,y)(Q(w^{-\frac{1}{p-1}}))^{p-1}(x,y).

The collection of weights for which this characteristic is finite is denoted
\tilde{A}_{p}
.
There is also a natural way to extend the class of the weights to resemble more the classical case allowing local
L^{1}
weights. In this case we require that constants are integrable in the manifold
M
equipped with the measure
d\mu_{\varphi}
so as to prove the theorem for cut weights, such as in
[
17
]
, that are in
L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}\cap\textup{L}^{2}
and then define the characteristic by a limiting procedure and deduce the theorem. See
[
17
]
for a detailed exposition in the case
p=2
.

Theorem 2

(Weighted
\textup{L}^{p}
estimate for the Riesz vector for non-negative curvature)
.

Suppose that
M
is a complete Riemannian manifold without boundary and
\Ric_{\varphi}\geqslant 0
. Then for all
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(M)
,
p\in(1,\infty)
, and
w\in\widetilde{A}_{p}
, we have the following dimension-free estimate

\|\vec{R}_{\varphi}f\|_{\textup{L}^{p}(T^{*}_{x}M,w)}\leqslant\tilde{C}_{p}\widetilde{Q}_{p}(w)^{\max\{1,\frac{1}{p-1}\}}\|f\|_{\textup{L}^{p}(M,w)},

(6)

where
\tilde{C}_{p}>0
is again a constant depending only on
p
with the expected end point behavior.

5
Maximal Function of
X

In this section we prove Theorem
5
via the modification of a simple and direct
domination argument for the maximal function. See for example the argument by Lerner
[
30
]
in a different context. Notice that the obtained norm estimate is the same as that in
Buckley’s text
[
11
]
on homogeneous spaces. Buckley’s proof enjoyed an extension to some martingales with certain restrictive homogeneity
conditions in the presence of jumps - this was needed because of the failure of the openness of the
A_{p}
class in the general context. The argument here
does not rely on the openness condition of the
A_{p}
classes and is therefore providing the estimate in full generality and in addition recovers the correct growth with the
A_{p}
characteristic for the norm estimate. The argument consists of a trajectory-wise domination of the maximal operator of
X
and the use of Doob’s inequality. We write
u=w^{\frac{1}{p-1}}
for the dual weight and recall that the
A_{p}
characteristic is
\sup_{\tau}\|u_{\tau}^{p-1}w_{\tau}\|_{\infty}
.
We also introduce the notations

\mathbbm{E}[\,\cdot\;w]=\mathbbm{E}_{w}[\,\cdot\,]\mathbbm{E}[w],

(11)

\cdot_{\,\tau,w}=\mathbbm{E}_{w}[\,\cdot\;|\,\mathcal{F}_{\tau}],

(12)

X_{u}^{\ast}(\omega)=\sup_{t}|X_{t,u}(\omega)|.

(13)

There holds for all
t\geqslant 0
and all
p>1

\displaystyle|X_{t}|^{p-1}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\left(\mathbbm{E}\left[|X|\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]\right)^{p-1}\ =\left(\mathbbm{E}\left[|X|u^{-1}u\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]\right)^{p-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\mathbbm{E}_{u}\left[|X|u^{-1}\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]\right)^{p-1}\left(\mathbbm{E}\left[u\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]\right)^{p-1}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle Q_{p}(w)\left(\mathbbm{E}\left[w\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]\right)^{-1}\left(\mathbbm{E}_{u}\left[|X|u^{-1}\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]\right)^{p-1}.

Now observe that

\displaystyle\left(\mathbbm{E}_{u}\left[|X|u^{-1}\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]\right)^{p-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbbm{E}\left[\left(\mathbbm{E}_{u}[|X|u^{-1}\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{t}]\right)^{p-1}|\,\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\mathbbm{E}\left[\left(\left(|X|u^{-1}\right)_{u}^{\ast}\right)^{p-1}w^{-1}w\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbbm{E}_{w}\left[\left(\left(|X|w\right)_{u}^{\ast}\right)^{p-1}w^{-1}\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]\mathbbm{E}\left[w\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{t}\right].

Then get for all
t

|X_{t}|^{p-1}\leqslant Q_{p}(w)\left(\left(\left(|X|u^{-1}\right)^{\ast}_{u}\right)^{p-1}w^{-1}\right)^{\ast}_{w},

and therefore

(X^{\ast})^{p}\leqslant\left(Q_{p}(w)\left(\left(\left(|X|u^{-1}\right)^{\ast}_{u}\right)^{p-1}w^{-1}\right)^{\ast}_{w}\right)^{\frac{p}{p-1}}.

Thus

\displaystyle\mathbbm{E}[(X^{\ast})^{p}w]

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle Q_{p}(w)^{\frac{p}{p-1}}\mathbbm{E}\left[\left(\left(\left(\left(|X|u^{-1}\right)^{\ast}_{u}\right)^{p-1}w^{-1}\right)^{\ast}_{w}\right)^{p^{\prime}}w\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Q_{p}(w)^{\frac{p}{p-1}}\mathbbm{E}_{w}\left[\left(\left(\left(\left(|X|u^{-1}\right)^{\ast}_{u}\right)^{p-1}w^{-1}\right)^{\ast}_{w}\right)^{p^{\prime}}\right]\mathbbm{E}[w]

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle Q_{p}(w)^{\frac{p}{p-1}}\left(\frac{p^{\prime}}{p^{\prime}-1}\right)^{p^{\prime}}\mathbbm{E}_{w}\left[(((|X|u^{-1})^{\ast}_{u})^{p-1}w^{-1})^{p^{\prime}}\right]\mathbbm{E}[w]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Q_{p}(w)^{\frac{p}{p-1}}\left(\frac{p^{\prime}}{p^{\prime}-1}\right)^{p^{\prime}}\mathbbm{E}\left[(((|X|u^{-1})^{\ast}_{u})^{p-1}w^{-1})^{p^{\prime}}w\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Q_{p}(w)^{\frac{p}{p-1}}\left(\frac{p^{\prime}}{p^{\prime}-1}\right)^{p^{\prime}}\mathbbm{E}\left[((|X|u^{-1})^{\ast}_{u})^{p}u\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Q_{p}(w)^{\frac{p}{p-1}}\left(\frac{p^{\prime}}{p^{\prime}-1}\right)^{p^{\prime}}\mathbbm{E}_{u}\left[((|X|u^{-1})^{\ast}_{u})^{p}\right]\mathbbm{E}[u]

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle Q_{p}(w)^{\frac{p}{p-1}}\left(\frac{p^{\prime}}{p^{\prime}-1}\right)^{p^{\prime}}\left(\frac{p}{p-1}\right)^{p}\mathbbm{E}_{u}\left[(|X|u^{-1})^{p}\right]\mathbbm{E}[u]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Q_{p}(w)^{\frac{p}{p-1}}\left(\frac{p^{\prime}}{p^{\prime}-1}\right)^{p^{\prime}}\left(\frac{p}{p-1}\right)^{p}\mathbbm{E}\left[(|X|u^{-1})^{p}u\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Q_{p}(w)^{\frac{p}{p-1}}\left(\frac{p^{\prime}}{p^{\prime}-1}\right)^{p^{\prime}}\left(\frac{p}{p-1}\right)^{p}\mathbbm{E}\left[|X|^{p}w\right].

Raising to the power
1/p
gives the desired estimate in Theorem
5
with
C_{p}=\frac{p^{p^{\prime}}}{p-1}
.

Sparse domination of the process

.

In this section we prove Theorem
3
. Without loss of generality
X
has non-zero closure and
\|X\|_{1}>0
.
We will use the following preliminary weak type estimate due to Wang

[
47
]
.

Theorem 9

(Wang)
.

Let
Y
differentially subordinate to
X
then for all
\lambda>0
there holds

\mathbbm{P}(\{\omega\in\Omega:(|Y|+|X|)^{\ast}(\omega)\geqslant\lambda\})\leqslant\frac{2}{\lambda}\|X\|_{1}.

Notice that it trivially implies

Corollary 2

(Wang)
.

Let
Y
differentially subordinate to
X
then for all
\lambda>0
there holds

\mathbbm{P}(\{\omega\in\Omega:Y^{\ast}(\omega)\vee X^{\ast}(\omega)\geqslant\lambda\})\leqslant\frac{2}{\lambda}\|X\|_{1}.

It also implies the following.

Lemma 2
.

Let
Y
differentially subordinate to
X
then for all
A\in\mathcal{F}_{0}
and for all
\lambda>0
there holds

\mathbbm{P}(\{\omega\in A:Y^{\ast}(\omega)\vee X^{\ast}(\omega)>\lambda|X|_{0}(\omega)\})\leqslant\frac{2}{\lambda}\mathbb{P}(A).

Proof.

Let us write

\tilde{X}=\chi_{A\cap\{|X|_{0}>0\}}X/|X|_{0}\text{ and }\tilde{Y}=\chi_{A\cap\{|X|_{0}>0\}}Y/|X|_{0}.

Notice first that
|X|_{0}
is measurable in
\mathcal{F}_{0}
. So the pair
(\tilde{X},\tilde{Y})
are adapted martingales under differential subordination.
Further, notice that almost surely
|X|_{0}(\omega)=0\Rightarrow|X|_{t}(\omega)=0
for all
t\geqslant 0
. In particular, all future
increments of
X
are zero and thus
|X_{t}(\omega)|\vee|Y_{t}(\omega)|=0
for all
t>0
. Thanks to this and Wang’s Theorem we can estimate for all
\lambda>0

\displaystyle\mathbbm{P}(\{\omega

\displaystyle\in A:Y_{t}^{\ast}\vee X_{t}^{\ast}>\lambda|X|_{0}\})

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{P}(\{\omega\in A\cap\{|X|_{0}>0\}:Y_{t}^{\ast}\vee X_{t}^{\ast}>\lambda|X|_{0}\})

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{P}(\{\omega\in\Omega:\tilde{Y}_{t}^{\ast}\vee\tilde{X}_{t}^{\ast}>\lambda\})

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{2}{\lambda}\|\tilde{X}\|_{1}=\frac{2}{\lambda}\mathbbm{E}|\tilde{X}|

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\lambda}\mathbbm{E}\left[\frac{\chi_{A\cap\{|X|_{0}>0\}}}{|X|_{0}}\mathbbm{E}[|X|\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{0}]\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\lambda}\mathbb{P}(A).

∎

Stopping procedure.

The first step for the proof of Theorem
3
is the stopping procedure.
Let us select an increasing sequence
\{T^{j}\}_{j\geq 0}
of stopping times associated to the pair
X
and
Y
inductively.
To do so, start with

T^{0}(\omega)=\inf\{t>0:|X|_{t}(\omega)>0\},

that is,

T^{0}(\omega)=0
if

and
\infty
otherwise. Set

E_{0}=\{T^{0}<\infty\}=\{\omega\in\Omega:|X|_{0}(\omega)>0\}.

Notice that since
\|X\|_{1}>0
we have
\mathbb{P}(E_{0})>0
and
|X|_{T_{0}}>0
on
E_{0}
.
Let us define
\mathfrak{F}^{0}=(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{0})_{t\geqslant 0}
by
\mathcal{F}^{0}_{t}=\mathcal{F}_{T^{0}\vee t}

and consider the martingales

Y^{0}_{t}=\chi_{E_{0}}Y_{t}\text{ and }X^{0}_{t}=\chi_{E_{0}}X_{t}.

Now we proceed with the iteration. Assume
n\geqslant 1
.
Assume we have filtrations
\mathfrak{F}^{0},...,\mathfrak{F}^{n-1}
, an increasing sequence of
stopping times
T^{0},...,T^{n-1}
with associated nested sets
E_{0},...,E_{n-1}
measurable in
\mathcal{F}_{0},...,\mathcal{F}_{n-1}
respectively and pairs of martingales

(X^{0},Y^{0}),...,(X^{n-1},Y^{n-1})
under differential subordination.

We set the stopping time

by

T^{n}(\omega)=\inf\{t>0:Y^{n-1}_{t}(\omega)\vee X^{n-1}_{t}(\omega)>4|X|_{T^{n-1}}(\omega)\}.

Notice that
|X|_{T^{n-1}}(\omega)=0
implies
|X|_{t}(\omega)=0
for all

. In particular, all future

X
are zero and thus

for all
t>T^{n-1}
. There holds thus
T^{n}(\omega)=\infty
if
|X|_{T^{n-1}}(\omega)=0
, and

E_{n}=\{T^{n}<\infty\}=\{\omega\in\Omega:Y^{n-1\ast}(\omega)\vee X^{n-1\ast}(\omega)>4|X|_{T^{n-1}}(\omega)\}.

Let
the filtration

. Observe that

.

Choice of the iterates.

We now take care of the foot of the next pair of martingales, particularly in the presence of a jump at a stopping time.
By differential subordination we can compare the jumps almost surely

In particular, one sets

if

. Define now the new iterates

given

as

\displaystyle Y_{t}^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\chi_{E_{n}}\left(\mathbbm{E}[Y^{n-1}\,|\,\mathcal{F}^{n}_{t}]-Y^{n-1}_{T^{n}}+r_{T^{n}}X^{n-1}_{T^{n}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\chi_{E_{n}}\left(r_{T^{n}}X^{n-1}_{T^{n}}+\int^{t\vee T^{n}}_{T^{n}_{+}}\mathrm{d}Y^{n-1}_{u}\right)

and

\displaystyle X_{t}^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\chi_{E_{n}}\mathbbm{E}[X^{n-1}\;|\;\mathcal{F}^{n}_{t}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\chi_{E_{n}}\left(X^{n-1}_{T^{n}}+\int^{t\vee T^{n}}_{T^{n}_{+}}\mathrm{d}X^{n-1}_{u}\right).

By induction, these are martingales with respect to
\mathfrak{F}^{n}
and
Y^{n}
is
differentially subordinate to
X^{n}
.

We prove that the resulting selection

is sparse. Let

.
Let

with

. Then

We now prove the domination estimate. Indeed, we show that for all

In this section we will prove the weak type estimate, Lemma

Proof of Lemma

.

This proof is modelled after the exposition in Bañuelos-Osekowski
[

]
. We extend it to tackle the presence of jumps as well as the use of the nonnegative submartingale
\widetilde{X}
instead of the martingale
X
. We aim to show (
7
):

\mathbb{P}\left((|Z_{t}|+|\widetilde{X}_{t}|)^{*}\geqslant\lambda\right)\leqslant 2\lambda^{-1}\|\widetilde{X}\|_{1}.

It suffices to show the inequality for
\lambda=1
. Indeed, we can replace
\widetilde{X}_{t}
and
Z_{t}
in the above inequality with
\lambda^{-1}\widetilde{X}_{t}
and
\lambda^{-1}Z_{t}
to get (
7
).

To show the inequality for
\lambda=1
, define functions
V,U:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
by

V(x,y)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-2|x|&\mbox{when }|x|+|y|<1,\\
1-2|x|&\mbox{when }|x|+|y|\geqslant 1,\end{array}\right.

and

U(x,y)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}|y|^{2}-|x|^{2}&\mbox{when }|x|+|y|<1,\\
1-2|x|&\mbox{when }|x|+|y|\geqslant 1.\end{array}\right.

Let us first observe that everywhere
V\leqslant U
. When
|x|+|y|\geqslant 1
this is obvious. In the case
|x|+|y|<1
we see that
V\leqslant U\Leftrightarrow-2|x|\leqslant|y|^{2}-|x|^{2}\Leftrightarrow(|x|-1)^{2}\leqslant|y|^{2}+1
but with
0\leqslant|x|<1
and
|y|\geqslant 0
we have
(|x|-1)^{2}\leqslant 1
and
|y|^{2}+1\geqslant 1
.

Define the stopping time

T=\inf\{t\geqslant 0:|\widetilde{X}_{t}|+|Z_{t}|\geqslant 1\}

so that
|\widetilde{X}_{T}|+|Z_{T}|\geqslant 1
and
|\widetilde{X}_{t}|+|Z_{t}|<1
for

. We will prove that

\mathbb{E}U(\widetilde{X}_{T},Z_{T})\leqslant 0.

(17)

Here is how we deduce the weak-type estimate (
7
) from (
17
). We have
V\leqslant U
everywhere and
\mathbb{E}U(\widetilde{X}_{T},Z_{T})\leqslant 0
. Therefore

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\geqslant\mathbb{E}U(\widetilde{X}_{T},Z_{T})

\displaystyle\geqslant\mathbb{E}V(\widetilde{X}_{T},Z_{T})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}(V(\widetilde{X}_{T},Z_{T})\chi_{\{|\widetilde{X}_{T}|+|Z_{T}|\geqslant 1\}})+\mathbb{E}(V(\widetilde{X}_{T},Z_{T})\chi_{\{|\widetilde{X}_{T}|+|Z_{T}|<1\}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}((1-2|\widetilde{X}_{T}|)\chi_{\{|\widetilde{X}_{T}|+|Z_{T}|\geqslant 1\}})+\mathbb{E}((-2|\widetilde{X}_{T}|)\chi_{\{|\widetilde{X}_{T}|+|Z_{T}|<1\}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(|\widetilde{X}_{T}|+|Z_{T}|\geqslant 1)-2\mathbb{E}|\widetilde{X}_{T}|,

from which we deduce

\mathbb{P}((|\widetilde{X}_{t}|+|Z_{t}|)^{*}\geqslant 1)\leqslant 2\|\widetilde{X}\|_{1},

which is exactly (
7
) for
\lambda=1
.

In order to prove (
17
), we will split as follows

\mathbb{E}U(\widetilde{X}_{T},Z_{T})=\mathbb{E}(U(\widetilde{X}_{T},Z_{T})\chi_{\{T=0\}})+\mathbb{E}(U(\widetilde{X}_{T},Z_{T})\chi_{\{T>0\}})

Case

.

First we handle the case when

. For such
\omega
where
T=0

and
U(\widetilde{X}_{0},Z_{0})=1-2|\widetilde{X}_{0}|
and so since

so that

. For

we have thus

and so

\mathbb{E}(U(\widetilde{X}_{T},Z_{T})\chi_{\{T=0\}})=\mathbb{E}(U(\widetilde{X}_{0},Z_{0})\chi_{\{T=0\}})\leqslant 0.

Case
T>0
.

Now we take care of the
\omega
where
T>0
. By simple calculations on the derivatives of

, we check that for

we have

\displaystyle\partial_{x}U(x,y)

\displaystyle=-2x,

(18)

\displaystyle\partial_{y_{i}}U(x,y)

\displaystyle=2y_{i},

(19)

\displaystyle\partial^{2}_{xx}U(x,y)

\displaystyle=-2,

(20)

\displaystyle\partial^{2}_{xy_{i}}U(x,y)

\displaystyle=0,

(21)

\displaystyle\partial^{2}_{y_{i}y_{j}}U(x,y)

\displaystyle=2\delta_{ij},

(22)

where

On
\{T>0\}
, the process
(\widetilde{X}_{t},Z_{t})
evolves in the set

, in the interior of which the function

is twice differentiable, which means that we have the following Itô formula

U(\widetilde{X}_{T},Z_{T})=I_{0}+I_{1}+I_{2}+I_{3},

(23)

with

\displaystyle I_{0}

\displaystyle=U(\widetilde{X}_{0},Z_{0})

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{0_{+}}^{T}\partial_{x}U(\widetilde{X}_{s},Z_{s})\mathrm{d}\widetilde{X}_{s}+\sum_{i}\int_{0_{+}}^{T}\partial_{y_{i}}U(\widetilde{X}_{s},Z_{s})\mathrm{d}Z^{i}_{s}

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0_{+}}^{T}\partial^{2}_{xx}U(\widetilde{X}_{s},Z_{s})\mathrm{d}[\widetilde{X},\widetilde{X}]_{s}+\sum_{i}\int_{0_{+}}^{T}\partial^{2}_{xy_{i}}U(\widetilde{X}_{s},Z_{s})\mathrm{d}[\widetilde{X},Z^{i}]_{s}

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\sum_{j}\int_{0_{+}}^{T}\partial^{2}_{y_{i}y_{j}}U(\widetilde{X}_{s},Z_{s})\mathrm{d}[Z^{i},Z^{j}]_{s},

\displaystyle I_{3}=

\displaystyle\sum_{0<s\leqslant T}\Big(U(\widetilde{X}_{s},Z_{s})-U(\widetilde{X}_{s_{-}},Z_{s_{-}})-\partial_{x}U(\widetilde{X}_{s_{-}},Z_{s_{-}})\Delta X_{s}

\displaystyle\qquad\quad-\sum_{i}\partial_{y_{i}}U(\widetilde{X}_{s_{-}},Z_{s_{-}})\Delta Y^{i}_{s}

\displaystyle\qquad\quad-\frac{1}{2}\partial^{2}_{xx}U(\widetilde{X}_{s_{-}},Z_{s_{-}})|\Delta\widetilde{X}_{s}|^{2}-\sum_{i}\partial^{2}_{xy_{i}}U(\widetilde{X}_{s_{-}},Z_{s_{-}})\Delta\widetilde{X}_{s}\Delta Z^{i}_{s}

where the last sum is over jumping times.

.

Finally, since

\mathbb{E}(U(\widetilde{X}_{T},Z_{T})\chi_{\{T>0\}})\leqslant 0.

and that

t>0
. Thanks to Remark
2
we see that
Z_{t}(\omega)
also remains
0
. This means, the part

\{\mathbb{E}(\widetilde{X}|\mathcal{F}_{0})=0\}
takes no effect on the estimate we seek to prove, which allows us to deduce the claim from estimate (
28
).
∎

Proof of Theorem
6
.

Now that we have an appropriate distributional inequality (
27
), we are ready to establish a sparse domination.
We consider for the moment continuous path processes. To prove (
8
), we will prove that for all
t\geqslant 0
we have

|Z_{t}|\leqslant 4S(\widetilde{X}).

(29)

To do so, we will successively construct a sequence of filtrations and an increasing sequence of stopping times
(T^{j})_{j=0}^{\infty}
to determine the sparse domination.

The key of the proof, besides the weak type estimate established in Lemma
1
and Corollary
3
, relies on recursivity in order to construct the stopping times. The construction of the sparse domination differs from the one in the case of
Y
because the operator we want to estimate is a perturbation of
Y
(which is differentially subordinate to
X
).
Indeed, the iteration procedure produces certain difficulties concerning the foot of the iterated processes. The difficulty arises because
Z
is a process with infinite memory and governed by a stochastic ODE taking into account the value of
Z
. We cannot just go ahead and set its foot back to 0 or to anything else, that will allow us to use the weak type estimate in Lemma
1
. Instead, we build an array of processes that we dominate in a different way, making use of the homogeneous part of the ODE via Remark
2
.

Now, we begin with the domination. First let
\mathfrak{F}^{0}=\mathfrak{F}
, i.e.
\mathcal{F}^{0}_{t}=\mathcal{F}_{t}
and let

Y_{t}^{0}=Y_{t}
,
\widetilde{X}_{t}^{0}=\widetilde{X}_{t}
,
Z_{t}^{0}=Z_{t}
. Also we will put
T^{0}=0
and hence
E_{0}=\{T^{0}<\infty\}=\Omega
.

Define the set

E_{1}=\{\omega\in\Omega:Z^{0*}(\omega)\vee\widetilde{X}^{0*}(\omega)>4\mathbb{E}(\widetilde{X}|\mathcal{F}_{0})(\omega)\}.

Obviously,
Y^{0}
,
\widetilde{X}^{0}
and
Z^{0}
satisfy the assumptions of Corollary
3
,

so we can apply it with
\lambda=4
to estimate
\mathbb{P}(E_{1})
and more precisely if
A_{0}\in\mathcal{F}_{0}
then

\mathbb{P}(A_{0}\cap E_{1})\leqslant\dfrac{2}{4}\mathbb{P}(A_{0})=\dfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{P}(A_{0}).

We can also associate a stopping time

T^{1}(\omega)=\inf\{t>0:|Z_{t}^{0}(\omega)|\vee\widetilde{X}_{t}^{0}(\omega)>4\mathbb{E}(\widetilde{X}|\mathcal{F}_{0})(\omega)\},

which is by definition almost surely finite in
E_{1}
.
Now if
t\in[0,T^{1})
then by definition of
T^{1}
we have

|Z^{0}_{t}(\omega)|\chi_{[T^{0},T^{1})}(t)\chi_{E_{0}}(\omega)\leqslant 4\mathbb{E}(\widetilde{X}|\mathcal{F}_{0})(\omega)\chi_{E_{0}}(\omega).

Indeed, more is true. We define the process
\widetilde{Z}^{0}_{t}
to obey
\widetilde{Z}^{0}_{0}=0
and

\mathrm{d}\widetilde{Z}^{0}_{t}=(V_{t}-a\Id)\widetilde{Z}^{0}_{t}\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{d}Y_{t}\chi_{[0,T^{1})}.

Thanks to Remark
2
, we know that once
T^{1}
is reached and
\mathrm{d}Y
has been stopped, the ODE above becomes homogeneous with initial condition
Z_{T^{1}}
and its solution does not increase in norm. For the process
\widetilde{Z}^{0}
we have therefore an estimate not only for times
t<T^{1}
but for all times
t\geqslant 0
, namely:

|\widetilde{Z}^{0}_{t}(\omega)|\leqslant 4\mathbb{E}(\widetilde{X}|\mathcal{F}_{0})(\omega)\chi_{E_{0}}(\omega).

When
T^{1}=\infty
we are done, because on
E_{0}\setminus E_{1}
our sparse estimate is complete. For the next step, we work on
E_{1}=\{T^{1}<\infty\}
.
We define the filtration
\mathfrak{F}^{1}=(\mathcal{F}^{1}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}:=(\mathcal{F}^{0}_{t\vee T^{1}})_{t\geqslant 0}=(\mathcal{F}_{t\vee T^{1}})_{t\geqslant 0}
and consider the following adapted processes

\displaystyle Y_{t}^{1}=\chi_{E_{1}}\Big(0+\int_{T^{1}_{+}}^{t\vee T^{1}}\mathrm{d}Y_{s}\Big)=\chi_{E_{1}}(Y_{t\vee T^{1}}-Y_{T^{1}}),

\displaystyle\widetilde{X}_{t}^{1}=\chi_{E_{1}}\Big(\widetilde{X}_{T^{1}}+\int_{T^{1}_{+}}^{t\vee T^{1}}\mathrm{d}\widetilde{X}_{s}\Big),

with similar definitions for
X^{1}
and
A^{1}
. Notice that
Y^{1}
is differentially subordinate to
X^{1}
.
Define
Z^{1}
adapted to
\mathfrak{F}^{1}
by its initial value
Z^{1}_{0}=0
and satisfying for
t>0
the stochastic differential equation

\mathrm{d}Z^{1}_{t}=(V_{t_{-}}-a\Id)Z^{1}_{t_{-}}\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{d}Y^{1}_{t}

and the set

E_{2}=\{\omega\in E_{1}:Z^{1*}(\omega)\vee\widetilde{X}^{1*}(\omega)>4\mathbb{E}(\widetilde{X}|\mathcal{F}_{T^{1}})(\omega)\}

with its corresponding stopping time

T^{2}(\omega)=\inf\{t>T^{1}:|Z_{t}^{1}(\omega)|\vee\widetilde{X}_{t}^{1}(\omega)>4\mathbb{E}(\widetilde{X}|\mathcal{F}_{T^{1}})(\omega)\}.

Observe that the newly defined processes
X^{1}
,
Y^{1}
,
A^{1}
,
\widetilde{X}^{1}
and
Z^{1}
again satisfy the assumptions of Lemma
1
and Corollary
3
(but now with respect to the filtration
\mathfrak{F}^{1}
). It is important to notice that in particular
|Z^{1}_{0}|\leqslant|\widetilde{X}^{1}_{0}|
so we can apply the weak type estimate (
27
) to obtain

\mathbb{P}(E_{2})\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{P}(E_{1}).

Moreover, applying (
27
) to the set
A=A_{1}
, we get for every
A_{1}\subseteq E_{1}
,
A_{1}\in\mathcal{F}_{T^{1}}

\mathbb{P}(A_{1}\cap E_{2})=\mathbb{P}(\{\omega\in A_{1}:Z^{1*}(\omega)\vee\widetilde{X}^{1*}(\omega)>4\mathbb{E}(\widetilde{X}^{1}|\mathcal{F}_{T^{1}})(\omega)\})\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{P}(A_{1}).

Since
Z_{t}^{1}
has support on
E_{1}
, by definition of
T^{2}
we have for
t<T^{2}

|Z_{t}^{1}|\leqslant 4\mathbb{E}(\widetilde{X}|\mathcal{F}_{T^{1}})\chi_{E_{1}}.

Finally, we define the process
\widetilde{Z}^{1}_{t}
on all of
\Omega
via its initial value 0 as well as the ODE

\mathrm{d}\widetilde{Z}^{1}_{t}=(V_{t_{-}}-a\Id)\widetilde{Z}^{1}_{t_{-}}\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{d}Y^{1}_{t}\chi_{[T^{1},T^{2})}.

Using Remark
2
we have
\widetilde{Z}^{1}_{t}=0
outside
E_{1}
. We also have
\widetilde{Z}^{1}_{t}=0
for
t<T^{1}
and non-increasing after
T^{2}
. Clearly
\widetilde{Z}^{1}_{t}
is identical to
Z^{1}
up until
T^{2}
. So we have

|\widetilde{Z}^{1}_{t}|\leqslant 4\mathbb{E}(\widetilde{X}|\mathcal{F}_{T^{1}})\chi_{E_{1}}.

and the estimate holds for all
t
.
If
T^{2}=\infty
then we are done, because the sparse estimate is complete for
E_{0}\setminus E_{2}
. Otherwise, for the next step, we work on
E_{2}=\{T^{2}<\infty\}
, defining processes with a superscript 2 as before.

Repeating the procedure inductively delivers processes
\widetilde{Z}^{n}_{t}
all with initial value 0 and satisfying the ODE

\mathrm{d}\widetilde{Z}^{n}_{t}=(V_{t_{-}}-a\Id)\widetilde{Z}^{n}_{t_{-}}\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{d}Y^{n}_{t}\chi_{[T^{n},T^{n+1})}.

These processes are defined on all of
\Omega
, and satisfy for all
t\geqslant 0

|\widetilde{Z}^{n}_{t}|\leqslant 4\mathbb{E}(\widetilde{X}|\mathcal{F}_{T^{n}})\chi_{E_{n}}.

To finish, we sum the ODEs to obtain
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\widetilde{Z}_{0}^{n}=0
and

\mathrm{d}\Big(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\widetilde{Z}^{n}\Big)_{t}=(V_{t_{-}}-a\Id)\Big(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\widetilde{Z}^{n}\Big)_{t_{-}}\mathrm{d}t+\Big(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\chi_{[T^{i},T^{i+1})}\Big)\mathrm{d}Y_{t},

where the last term is simply
\mathrm{d}Y_{t}
.
The infinite sums are justified because
\mathbb{P}(E_{n})\to 0
.
By uniqueness of the solution to the original initial value problem, we have

Z_{t}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\widetilde{Z}_{t}^{n}.

We can now deduce the domination

|Z_{t}|\leqslant 4\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}(\widetilde{X}|\mathcal{F}_{T^{n}})\chi_{E_{n}}

as claimed.

∎

7.2.1
Changes in the presence of jumps

We already have a weak type estimate in the presence of jumps. The additional changes required to the domination itself are similar to what we have seen in the martingale case with jumps. We show that
|Z_{t}|\leqslant 8S(\widetilde{X})
. It is important to notice that
Y
has a jump
Y_{T}-Y_{T_{-}}
at time
T
if and only if
Z
does with
Z_{T}-Z_{T_{-}}=Y_{T}-Y_{T_{-}}
. As before, there exists an operator
r_{T}
of norm at most 1 so that
r_{T}(X_{T}-X_{T_{-}})=|X_{T}-X_{T_{-}}|
. Therefore, if a jump arises at the stopping time
T^{j}
, we may lose control of the size of
Z
. This is counter acted in writing

Z_{T^{j}}=Z_{T^{j}_{-}}+(Z_{T^{j}}-Z_{T^{j}_{-}})=Z_{T^{j}_{-}}+(Y_{T^{j}}-Y_{T^{j}_{-}}).

Now, using differential subordination

|Z_{T^{j}}|\leqslant|Z_{T^{j}_{-}}|+|X_{T}-X_{T_{-}}|=|Z_{T^{j}_{-}}|+r_{T^{j}}(X_{T^{j}}-X_{T^{j}_{-}}).

Before
T^{j}
, we have domination on
|Z_{T^{j}_{-}}|
and
r_{T^{j}}X_{T^{j}_{-}}
.
The remaining term
r_{T^{j}}X_{T^{j}}
is put with the new iterates
X^{j},Y^{j},Z^{j}
as follows:

\displaystyle X^{j}_{t}=\chi_{E_{j}}\Big(X_{T^{j}}+\int_{T^{j}_{+}}^{t\vee T^{j}}\mathrm{d}X_{s}\Big),

\displaystyle Y^{j}_{t}=\chi_{E_{j}}\Big(r_{T^{j}}X_{T^{j}}+\int_{T^{j}_{+}}^{t\vee T^{j}}\mathrm{d}Y_{s}\Big),

\displaystyle Z^{j}_{t}=\chi_{E_{j}}\Big(r_{T^{j}}X_{T^{j}}+\int_{T^{j}_{+}}^{t\vee T^{j}}\mathrm{d}Z_{s}\Big).

The remaining part of the argument remains the same.

estimates

In this section we provide
L^{p}
estimates for the process
Z
.

Proof of Theorem
7
.

This follows from the sparse domination in Theorem
6
and the corresponding estimates for the sparse operator
S(\widetilde{X})
. For every
p>1
there exists
C_{p}^{\prime}>0
such that

\|S(\widetilde{X})\|_{\textup{L}^{p}}\leqslant C_{p}^{\prime}\|\widetilde{X}\|_{\textup{L}^{p}}.

(30)

Moreover, assuming that the weight
w
is a positive, uniformly integrable submartingale, the estimate

\|S(\widetilde{X})\|_{\textup{L}^{2}(w)}\leqslant\tilde{C}_{2}^{\prime}Q_{2}^{\mathcal{F}}(w)\|\widetilde{X}\|_{\textup{L}^{2}(w)}

(31)

also holds for some constant
\tilde{C}_{2}^{\prime}>0
.

We omit the proof of (
30
) and (
31
) as they are almost the same as the proof of the estimate for the sparse operator in the martingale case. Namely for (
30
) the proof without weight, and for (
31
) the proof with the weights
w_{T^{j}}
sampled at stopping times
T^{j}
replaced by the projections
\mathbb{E}(w|\mathcal{F}_{T^{j}})
. Observe that if
a=0
, then
w
and
\widetilde{X}
are actually martingales so we get exactly the same estimate as before.

Now (
8
), (
30
) and (
31
) obviously imply (
9
) and (
10
) which proves the theorem.
∎

8
Estimates for the Riesz vector

The proof of Theorems
1
and
2
now follows standard arguments.

We start with the proof of (
6
) in Theorem
2
. Since here
\Ric_{\varphi}\geqslant 0
(i.e.
a=0
), as we mentioned before, both
\widetilde{X}=X
and the weight
w
are martingales. Hence we are able to use the martingale extrapolation theorem from
[
20
]
. Using this theorem for
r=2
we extrapolate from (
31
) and obtain weighted
L^{p}
estimates for
S(\widetilde{X})

\|S(\widetilde{X})\|_{\textup{L}^{p}(w)}\leqslant\tilde{c}_{p}^{\prime}Q_{p}^{\mathcal{F}}(w)^{\max\{1,\frac{1}{p-1}\}}\|\widetilde{X}\|_{\textup{L}^{p}(w)},

where
Q_{p}^{\mathcal{F}}(w)
is the
A_{p}
characteristic of the weight
w
defined on the filtered space as

Q_{p}^{\mathcal{F}}(w)=\sup_{\tau}\big\|\mathbb{E}(w|\mathcal{F}_{\tau})\mathbb{E}(u|\mathcal{F}_{\tau})^{p-1}\big\|_{\infty}.

In the above expression the supremum runs over adapted stopping times
\tau
and by
u
we denote the dual weight satisfying
u^{p}w=u
.

Obviously this estimate along with the sparse domination (
8
) implies a weighted
\textup{L}^{p}
estimate for
Z

\|Z^{*}\|_{\textup{L}^{p}(w)}\leqslant\tilde{c}_{p}Q_{p}^{\mathcal{F}}(w)^{\max\{1,\frac{1}{p-1}\}}\|\widetilde{X}\|_{\textup{L}^{p}(w)},

(32)

for some constant
\tilde{c}_{p}>0
.

Now by taking the probabilistic representation of the Riesz transform (
2
), one can write

\displaystyle\|\vec{R}_{\varphi}f\|^{p}_{\textup{L}^{p}(w)}

\displaystyle=\int_{M}|\vec{R}_{\varphi}f|^{p}w(x)d\mu_{\varphi}(x)

\displaystyle=2^{p}\lim_{y\to\infty}\int_{M}\big|\mathbb{E}_{y}\left[Z_{\tau}|B^{M}_{\tau}=x\right]\big|^{p}w(x)d\mu_{\varphi}(x)

\displaystyle\leqslant 2^{p}\lim_{y\to\infty}\int_{M}\mathbb{E}_{y}\big(|Z_{\tau}|^{p}|B^{M}_{\tau}=x\big)w(x)d\mu_{\varphi}(x)

\displaystyle=2^{p}\lim_{y\to\infty}\int_{M}\mathbb{E}_{y}\left[|Z_{\tau}|^{p}w_{\tau}|B^{M}_{\tau}=x\right]d\mu_{\varphi}(x).

(33)

Here in the third line we applied the Jensen’s inequality to the conditional expectation, while in the fourth line we used that for every
\omega
such that
B^{M}_{\tau}(\omega)=x
we have
w_{\tau}(\omega)=w(x)
.

Since
d\mu_{\varphi}(x)
is the invariant measure with respect to
B^{M}_{t}
, i.e.
d\mu_{\varphi}(x)
is counting how many trajectories arrive at
x
, we have

d\mu_{\varphi}(x)=d\mathbb{P}(B^{M}_{\tau}=x)

so we can write (
33
) as

\displaystyle 2^{p}\lim_{y\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left(\mathbb{E}_{y}\left[|Z_{\tau}|^{p}w_{\tau}|B^{M}_{\tau}=x\right]\right)

\displaystyle=2^{p}\lim_{y\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\big(|Z_{\tau}|^{p}w_{\tau}\big)

\displaystyle=2^{p}\lim_{y\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}|Z_{\tau}(\omega)|^{p}w_{\tau}(\omega)d\mathbb{P}(\omega)

\displaystyle=2^{p}\lim_{y\to\infty}\|Z_{\tau}\|^{p}_{\textup{L}^{p}(w)}.

Now we use the estimate
(
32
)
to get

\displaystyle 2^{p}\lim_{y\to\infty}\|Z_{\tau}\|^{p}_{\textup{L}^{p}(w)}

\displaystyle\leqslant 2^{p}\tilde{c}_{p}^{p}\lim_{y\to\infty}Q_{p}^{\mathcal{F}(y)}(w)^{\max\{p,\frac{p}{p-1}\}}\|\widetilde{X}\|^{p}_{\textup{L}^{p}(w)}

\displaystyle\leqslant 2^{p}\tilde{c}_{p}^{p}\lim_{y\to\infty}Q_{p}^{\mathcal{F}(y)}(w)^{\max\{p,\frac{p}{p-1}\}}\|Qf(B^{M}_{\tau},B_{\tau})\|_{\textup{L}^{p}(w)}^{p}

\displaystyle\leqslant\tilde{C}_{p}\tilde{Q}_{p}(w)^{\max\{p,\frac{p}{p-1}\}}\|f\|_{\textup{L}^{p}(w)}^{p},

for some constant
\tilde{C}_{p}>0
depending only on
p
. Combining all of the above we finally get

\|\vec{R}_{\varphi}f\|^{p}_{\textup{L}^{p}(w)}\leqslant\tilde{C}_{p}\tilde{Q}_{p}(w)^{\max\{p,\frac{p}{p-1}\}}\|f\|_{\textup{L}^{p}(w)}^{p},

which by taking the
p
-th root gives us exactly (
6
).

Notice that the sparse domination itself depends upon the used filtration (and hence
y
). Here the norm
\|\widetilde{X}\|_{\textup{L}^{p}(w)}
is taken at
t=\infty
, which is
\tau
in our stopped processes. We also use the fact that for
\omega
such that
(B^{M}_{\tau}(\omega),B_{\tau}(\omega))=(x,0)
we have

Qf(B^{M}_{\tau}(\omega),B_{\tau}(\omega))=Qf(x,0)=f(x)

and hence
\|Qf(B^{M}_{\tau},B_{\tau})\|_{\textup{L}^{p}(w)}=\|f\|_{\textup{L}^{p}(w)}
. Also
Q^{\mathcal{F}^{(y)}}_{p}(w)
is the
A_{p}
characteristic that corresponds to the filtration when
B_{0}=y
and
\tilde{Q}_{p}(w)
is the Poisson flow characteristic. When the weight
w
is a martingale, those two characteristics are comparable (for more details see
[
20
]
).

In the negative curvature case, i.e.
a>0
, we don’t have the needed comparability anymore and that is why we are not able to prove the weighted estimate (
6
) using this method. By using the same method we could get some sort of weighted estimate, but the Poisson flow characteristic
\tilde{Q}_{p}(w)
should be replaced with some different characteristic which would be comparable to the
A_{p}
characteristic
Q^{\mathcal{F}^{(y)}}_{p}(w)
.

On the other hand, unweighted estimates from Theorem
1
hold even in the case when
a>0
(negative curvature). Estimate (
5
) is proved the same way as (
6
) just by omitting the weight
w
.

9
Examples

The setting we are working on is very general and covers a lot of classical examples. In this section we are only going to mention a few of them.

(1)

(Euclidean space) Taking
M=\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\varphi=0
, we get
\Ric_{\varphi}=0
and
M_{t}=\textup{Id}
for all
t\geqslant 0
. This way one can retrieve estimates for the classical Riesz transforms
R_{i}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}(-\Delta)^{-1/2}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

(2)

(Gauss space) Taking
M=\mathbb{R}^{n}
and

\varphi(x)=\frac{\|x\|^{2}}{2}+\frac{n}{2}\log(2\pi),

we get
\mu_{\varphi}=\gamma_{n}
(Gaussian measure) and
M_{t}=e^{-t}\textup{Id}
for all
t\geqslant 0
. This gives us the estimates for the Riesz transform associated with the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck operator
\Delta_{\varphi}=\Delta-x\cdot\nabla
on
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\gamma_{n})
.

(3)

(Bessel setting) By taking
M=(0,\infty)
and
\varphi(x)=\ln(x^{-2\alpha})
for some
\alpha\geqslant 0
we get

\Delta_{\varphi}=\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+\frac{2\alpha}{x}\frac{d}{dx}=-B_{\alpha},

where
B_{\alpha}
is the Bessel operator considered by Muckenhoupt and Stein (
[
37
]
). The measure is now
d\mu_{\varphi}(x)=x^{2\alpha}dx
and
\Ric_{\varphi}=\frac{2\alpha}{x^{2}}\geqslant 0
. Hence we also retrieve estimates for the Riesz transform
R_{\alpha}
associated with
B_{\alpha}
defined as
R_{\alpha}=\frac{d}{dx}B_{\alpha}^{-1/2}
.

Bessel-Riesz transforms in higher dimensions were considered in
[
8
]
. Given a multi-index
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{n})

(\alpha_{i}\geqslant 0)
one defines the
n
-dimensional Bessel differential operator as

B_{\alpha}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{i}^{2}}-\frac{2\alpha_{i}}{x_{i}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\right)

and the Riesz transforms associated with
B_{\alpha}
as

R_{\alpha,i}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}(B_{\alpha})^{-1/2}.

Now by taking
M=(0,\infty)^{n}
and
\varphi(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ln(x^{-2\alpha_{i}})
we see that again we get

\Delta_{\varphi}=-B_{\alpha}\quad\text{and}\quad d\mu_{\varphi}(x)=x^{2\alpha}dx,

where
x^{2\alpha}
denotes
x_{1}^{2\alpha_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{2\alpha_{n}}
. Therefore we also get dimensionless estimates for the vector Riesz transform
R_{\alpha}
on
(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n},x^{2\alpha}dx)
.
