On the Asymptotic Nonnegative Rank of Matrices and its Applications in Information Theory
Yeow Meng Chee, Quoc-Tung Le, Hoang Ta

Abstract
In this paper, we study the asymptotic nonnegative rank of matrices, which characterizes the asymptotic growth of the nonnegative rank of fixed nonnegative matrices under the Kronecker product. This quantity is important since it governs several notions in information theory such as the so-called exact Rényi common information and the amortized communication complexity . By using the theory of asymptotic spectra of V. Strassen (J. Reine Angew. Math. 1988), we define formally the asymptotic spectrum of nonnegative matrices and give a dual characterization of the asymptotic nonnegative rank. As a complementary of the nonnegative rank, we introduce the notion of the subrank of a nonnegative matrix and show that it is exactly equal to the size of the maximum induced matching of the bipartite graph defined on the support of the matrix (therefore, independent of the value of entries). Finally, we show that two matrix parameters, namely rank and fractional cover number, belong to the asymptotic spectrum of nonnegative matrices.



1
Introduction

The nonnegative rank of a nonnegative matrix
A
of size
m\times n
, denoted by
\nrank(A)
, is the smallest integer number
r
such there exist two nonnegative matrices
B
and
C
of size
m\times r
and
r\times n
respectively such that
A=BC
(
BC
is the usual matrix product between
B
and
C
). The notion of nonnegative rank can be found in various fields such as combinatorial optimization
[
Yan91
]
and communication complexity
[
Kus97
,
Lov90
,
LS+09
,
RY20
]
. For instances, in two parties communication complexity, two players compute a boolean function
f:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\{0,1\}
on inputs
(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
where each party holds only one argument (either
x
or
y
). In this setting, the communication complexity
D(f)
of the function
f
is the minimum number of exchanging bits to evaluate
f
. Results in
[
Lov90
,
LS+09
]
show that the logarithm of the nonnegative rank of the associated matrix
M_{f}
of size
|\mathcal{X}|\times|\mathcal{Y}|
defined by
M_{f}(x,y)=f(x,y)
is a lower bound for the communication complexity, i.e.,
\log\nrank(M_{f})\leq D(f)
. Another application of nonnegative rank is in combinatorial optimization. In
[
Yan91
]
, it is shown that the minimum number of linear inequalities representing a given polytope can be determined by the nonnegative rank of a particular matrix. This quantity plays an important role in solving linear programming because the complexity of interior-point methods depends on the number of linear inequalities. Finally, it is demonstrated in
[
Zha12
,
JSWZ13
]
that the minimum amount of information exchanged by two parties in order to sample from the bivariate probability distribution
p(x,y)=A_{x,y}
represented by a matrix
A
(assuming
A
is normalized so that
\sum_{x,y}A_{x,y}=1
) can be determined by the nonnegative rank of
A
.

The asymptotic nonnegative rank, which is defined as the asymptotic growth of the nonnegative rank of a matrix under Kronecker product (see Equation (
1
) for its mathematical definition), is equally important since it is also related to many quantities in information theory and communication complexity such as the
exact Rényi common information

[
YT20
]
and the
amortized communication complexity

[
FKNN95
]
. However, in comparison to the nonnegative rank, the asymptotic counterpart is not equally well-studied.
For this reason, in this paper, we make several contributions to the study of asymptotic nonnegative rank: first, we give a dual characterization of this notion as well as additional results that relate to it via the theory of asymptotic spectra. Historically, this theory was developed by V. Strassen in
[
Str88
]
(see also the exposition in
[
WZ22
, Part I]
) to understand the arithmetic complexity of matrix multiplication (see, e.g.,
[
BCS97
]
). More recently, the theory of asymptotic spectra has been applied to a range of other problems in combinatorics
[
Zui19
]
, quantum information
[
LZ20
,
FFHT23
]
, circuit complexity
[
RZ22
]
and other domains
[
JV19
,
Fri21
,
Vra22
]
. Two related notions to the nonnegative rank and the asymptotic nonnegative rank,
the nonnegative subrank
and its asymptotic version, are also introduced and studied.

1.1
Notations and Definitions

We define some basic notions that will be used in the rest of this paper. Let
A\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{m_{A}\times n_{A}},B\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{m_{B}\times n_{B}}
be two nonnegative matrices, the
direct sum

A\oplus B
and
Kronecker product

A\otimes B
are defined respectively by:

\displaystyle A\oplus B\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}A&0\\
0&B\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{(m_{A}+n_{A})\times(m_{B}+n_{B})},\quad

\displaystyle A\otimes B\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}A_{1,1}B&\ldots&A_{1,n_{A}}B\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
A_{m_{A},1}B&\ldots&A_{m_{A},n_{A}}B\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{m_{A}m_{B}\times n_{A}n_{B}}\,,

where
A_{i,j}
is the value of
A
at the index
(i,j)
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, denote
\mathrm{I}_{n}
the identity matrix of size
n
and
\mathrm{GL}^{+}(n):=\{A\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n\times n}\mid A^{-1}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n\times n}\}
a subgroup of
\mathrm{GL}(n)
whose elements and their inverses are nonnegative matrices.

Given a set
S
equipped with a binary operator
\cdot:S\times S
, we say
(S,\cdot)
is a
commutative monoid
if:

(1)

The operator
\cdot
is associative:
(a\cdot b)\cdot c=a\cdot(b\cdot c),\forall a,b,c\in S
.

(2)

The operator
\cdot
is commutative:
a\cdot b=b\cdot a,\forall a,b\in S
.

(3)

There exists an identity element
0
:
a\cdot 0=0\cdot a=a,\forall a\in S
.

For a nonnegative matrix
A
,
\mathrm{supp}(A):=\{(i,j):A_{i,j}>0\}
the support of
A
is the set of indices whose coefficients in
A
is strictly positive. In this work,
\mathrm{supp}(A)
has two interpretations: either a subset of the index set or a binary matrix of the same size as
A
whose coefficients at
(i,j)
are equal to
1
if
(i,j)\in\mathrm{supp}(A)
and
0
otherwise.

Asymptotic nonnegative rank

The nonnegative rank is sub-multiplicative under Kronecker product. Indeed, given two nonnegative matrices
A,B
, suppose that
A=UV^{T}
and
B=XY^{T}
, where
U,V,X,Y
are nonnegative matrices, then
A\otimes B=(U\otimes X)(V\otimes Y)^{T}
. Therefore
\nrank(A\otimes B)\leq\nrank(A)\nrank(B)
. In general,
\nrank
is not multiplicative since there exists
A\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{4\times 4}
such that
\nrank(A)=4
and
\nrank(A\otimes A)=15\neq\nrank(A)^{2}

[
BKLT13
]
. For more similar examples, we refer to
[
BKLT13
,
VGGT16
]
).
Therefore, the
asymptotic nonnegative rank
is interesting to consider. Mathematically, the asymptotic nonnegative rank of nonnegative matrices is defined as follows.

\asynrank(A)\coloneqq\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\nrank(A^{\otimes n})^{1/n}\,.

(1)

Since the sequence
\nrank(A^{\otimes})^{1/n}
is sub-multiplicative, by Fekete’s lemma
[
Fek23
]
, we can prove that
\asynrank(A)
is well-defined and the limit can be replaced by the infimum over
n
. Namely,

\displaystyle\asynrank(A)=\inf_{n}\nrank(A^{\otimes n})^{1/n}\,.

We also note that the approximated version of the asymptotic nonnegative rank was studied in
[
BJLP17
]
. In that work, the authors showed that this notion captures the
common information

[
Wyn75
]
.

1.2
Applications of asymptotic nonnegative rank

The asymptotic nonnegative rank can be interpreted as the
exact Rényi common information of order zero

[
YT20
]
. This information is defined based on correlated sources (joint distribution), which is represented as a nonnegative matrix with normalized rows and columns. Specifically, given correlated sources
\pi_{XY}
over
X\times Y
. The exact Rényi common information of order zero, denoted by
T_{Ex}^{0}(\pi_{XY})
, is the minimum common randomness rate for exact generation of the target distribution, with the constraint that common randomness can only be compressed using fixed-length codes. In
[
YT20
]
, the authors showed that this information can be characterized by asymptotic nonnegative rank of
\pi_{XY}
. More precisely, they proved
T_{Ex}^{0}(\pi_{XY})=\log\asynrank(\pi_{XY})
. For more information about the exact Rényi common information, we refer to
[
YT22
, Section 5 and Section 7]
.

In addition, the asymptotic nonnegative rank can be used as a lower bound for the
amortized communication complexity
. Unlike the original communication complexity (that we briefly introduce in the precedent paragraph), this model is interested in the minimum number of exchanging bits required to calculate a boolean function
f
multiple times (on different inputs
{(x_{i},y_{i})\mid i=1,\ldots,m}
). A trivial upper bound for this quantity is
m
times the complexity of calculating
f
once. In other words, we obtain this complexity by simply calculating
f(x_{i},y_{i})
separately, one after another. However, it turns out that in many computational models, the amortized communication complexity is smaller than this natural upper bound. It implies that the gain of complexity by performing calculation in batch can be significant. A notable example can be found in
[
FKNN95
]
, where the author constructs a specific function
f
whose communication complexity
D(f)
and its amortization
\bar{D}(f)
are totally different, i.e.,
\bar{D}(f)/m<D(f)
. Using the result
\log\nrank(M_{f})\leq D(f)
of
[
Lov90
]
, we can prove a similar amortized counterpart, which states:
\log\asynrank(M_{f})\leq\bar{D}(f)/m
(remind that
M_{f}
is of size
|\mathcal{X}|\times|\mathcal{Y}|,M_{f}(x,y)=f(x,y)
). Therefore, the asymptotic nonnegative rank
\asynrank
is important for the study of amortized communication complexity.

1.3
Contribution

In this paper, we consider the asymptotic spectra theory in the context of asymptotic nonnegative rank. Our main contributions are:

1.

By introducing a nonnegative matrix semiring
\mathbb{M}_{+}
with two operators:
\oplus
(the direct sum) and
\otimes
(the Kronecker product) and a
preorder

\leqk
(cf. Definition
2.1
), we define
\mathbf{X}(\mathbb{M}_{+},\leqk)
, the
asymptotic spectrum of
\mathbb{M}_{+}
. The set
\mathbf{X}(\mathbb{M}_{+},\leqk)
consists of
monotone
semiring homomorphisms
\phi
from the set of nonnegative matrices
\mathbb{M}_{+}
to the set of nonnegative numbers
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, i.e., if
A\leqk B
, then
\phi(A)\leq\phi(B)
(
A,B
are two nonnegative matrices). In fact,
\mathbf{X}(\mathbb{M}_{+},\leqk)
is the nonnegative matrix version of other existing asymptotic spectra such as the asymptotic spectrum of tensors
[
Str88
]
, graphs
[
Zui19
]
and LOCC transformations
[
JV19
]
, which are studied in the literature.

2.

The definition of
\mathbf{X}(\mathbb{M}_{+},\leqk)
allows us to establish another characterization of the asymptotic nonnegative rank of a matrix
A
in Theorem
3.7
, which states:

\asynrank(A)=\max_{\phi\in\mathbf{X}(S,\leqk)}\phi(A)

3.

Similar to previous works on asymptotic spectra, we define and study two quantities: the
subrank
(
\subnonrank
) and
asymptotic subrank
(
\asympsubnonrank
) of a nonnegative matrix. While
\subnonrank
is a natural lower bound of
\nrank
,
\asympsubnonrank
is the dual of
\asynrank
. In this work, we prove that
\subrank(A)
is exactly equal to the induced matching number of the bipartite graph defined on the support of
A
(see Theorem
3.9
) and thus, independent of the coefficients of
A
. Moreover, similar to the asymptotic nonnegative rank, the asymptotic subrank can also be characterized by using
\mathbf{X}(\mathbb{M}_{+},\leqk)
(cf. Theorem
3.7
) as:

\asympsubnonrank(A)=\min_{\phi\in\mathbf{X}(S,\leqk)}\phi(A)

4.

Since
\mathbf{X}(\mathbb{M}_{+},\leqk)
allows us to characterize precisely
\asynrank
and
\asympsubnonrank
, we study in detail the set
\mathbf{X}(\mathbb{M}_{+},\leqk)
. Our investigation of
\mathbf{X}(\mathbb{M}_{+},\leqk)
consists of showing its two elements: the rank function and the fractional cover number (cf. Section
4
). We note that the construction of explicit elements in the asymptotic spectrum of a specific instance is nontrivial. For example, the construction of nontrivial (universal) elements in the asymptotic spectrum of tensors has been an open problem for more than thirty years until it was solved by recent work in
[
CVZ18
]
. In addition, we also showed that for all
\phi\in\mathbf{X}(\mathbb{M}_{+},\leqk)
, for all
non-degenerate
lower triangular nonnegative matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, we have:
\phi(A)=\min(m,n)
.

Organization

The paper is organized as follows: In Section
2
, we briefly present the theory of asymptotic spectra of Strassen. We then introduce the asymptotic spectrum on nonnegative matrices and give a dual characterization of the asymptotic nonnegative rank of nonnegative matrices in Section
3
. Finally, in Section
4
, we show two elements in the asymptotic spectrum of nonnegative matrices.

2
Asymptotic spectra and Strassen’s spectral theorem

In this section, we present briefly the abstract theory of asymptotic spectra which was introduced by V. Strassen
[
Str88
]
. For a more complete presentation, we refer the readers to
[
Str88
,
Zui18
,
WZ22
]
.

Let
(S,+,\cdot,0_{S},1_{S})
be a commutative semiring, i.e., a set
S
equipped with a binary addition operation
+
, a binary multiplication
\cdot
, and having two elements
0_{S},1_{S}\in S
such that for all
a,b,c\in S
:

(1)

(S,+)
is a commutative monoid with the identity element
0_{S}
.

(2)

(S,\cdot)
is a commutative monoid with the identity element
1_{S}
.

(3)

\cdot
distributes over
+
:
a\cdot(b+c)=(a\cdot b)+(a\cdot c)

(4)

0_{S}\cdot a=0_{S}
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
we denote the sum of
n
elements
1_{S}
, i.e.,
1_{S}+\dots+1_{S}\in S
by
n_{S}
. A
preorder

\leqslant
on
S
is a relation on
S
such that for all
a,b,c\in S

1)

\leqslant
is reflexive:
a\leqslant a

2)

\leqslant
is transitive:
a\leqslant b
and
b\leqslant c
implies
a\leqslant c
.

Definition 2.1
.

A preorder
\leqslant
on
S
is a
Strassen preorder
if

(i)

For all
n,m\in\mathbb{N}

n\leq m
iff
n_{S}\leqslant m_{S}
in
S
.

(ii)

For all
a,b,c,d\in S
if
a\leqslant b
and
c\leqslant d
, then
a+c\leqslant b+d
and
a\cdot c\leqslant b\cdot d
.

(iii)

For all
a,b\in S,b\neq 0
there is
r\in\mathbb{N}
such that
a\leqslant r_{S}\cdot b
.

Let
S
be a commutative semiring and let
\leqslant
be a Strassen preorder on
S
. Throughout the paper, we will use
\leq
to denote the usual order on
\mathbb{R}
. Let
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be the semiring of nonnegative real numbers. A semiring homomorphism from
S
to
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
(denoted by
\operatorname{Hom}\left(S,\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\right)
) is a map
\phi:S\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
such that:

(1)

\phi(a\cdot b)=\phi(a)\phi(b)

(2)

\phi(a+b)=\phi(a)+\phi(b)

(3)

\phi(1_{S})=1
.

Let
\mathbf{X}(S,\leqslant)
be the set of
\leqslant
-monotone semiring homomorphisms from
S
to
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, i.e:

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}(S,\leqslant)\coloneqq\left\{\phi\in\operatorname{Hom}\left(S,\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\right):\forall a,b\in S,a\leqslant b\Rightarrow\phi(a)\leq\phi(b)\right\}\,,

we note that for every
\phi\in\mathbf{X}(S,\leqslant)
holds
\phi(1_{S})=1
and thus
\phi(n_{S})=n
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. We call
\mathbf{X}(S,\leqslant)
the
asymptotic spectrum
of
(S,\leqslant)
and elements of
\mathbf{X}(S,\leqslant)

spectral points
.

Let
a\in S
, the rank of
a
is defined as
\mathrm{R}(a)\coloneqq\min\{n\in\mathbb{N}:a\leqslant n_{S}\}
. The subrank of
a
is defined as
\mathrm{Q}(a)\coloneqq\max\{n\in\mathbb{N}:n_{S}\leqslant a\}
. The asymptotic subrank and asymptotic rank of
a
are defined as

\asymrank(a)\coloneqq\lim_{N\to\infty}\rankk(a^{N})^{1/N}\text{ and }\asymsubrank(a)\coloneqq\lim_{N\to\infty}\subrank(a^{N})^{1/N}

(2)

From Fekete’s lemma, the limits in (
2
) indeed exist and can be replaced by an infimum and a supremum, that is,

\displaystyle\asymrank(a)=\inf_{N}\rankk(a^{N})^{1/N}\text{, and }\asymsubrank(a)=\sup_{N}\subrank(a^{N})^{1/N}\,.

The notions of asymptotic rank and asymptotic subrank have been extensively studied in various fields. For instance, in
[
Str88
]
, the author showed that the asymptotic rank of matrix multiplication tensors corresponds to the complexity of matrix multiplication, more examples can be found in
[
WZ22
]
. Beside that, the notion of asymptotic subrank for tensors can be utilized to establish upper bounds for several combinatorial problems, such as capset and sunflower problems
[
CVZ18
]
, as well as the Shannon capacity of hypergraphs
[
CFTZ22
]
. Furthermore, research has also focused on exploring structures of the set of nonnegative real numbers obtained from the asymptotic rank or asymptotic subrank of complex tensors, as seen in
[
BDR22
,
CGZ22
,
GZ23
]
.

In
[
Str88
]
, the author showed that the asymptotic rank and asymptotic subrank have the following dual characterization in terms of the asymptotic spectrum.

Theorem 2.2

(
[
Str88
, Th. 3.8]
and
[
Zui18
, Cor. 2.14]
)
.

Let
S
be a commutative semiring and let
\leqslant
be a Strassen preorder on
S
. For any
a\in S
such that
1_{S}\leqslant a
and
2_{S}\leqslant a^{k}
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we have that

\displaystyle\asymrank(a)=\max_{\phi\in\mathbf{X}(S,\leqslant)}\phi(a)\text{, and }\asymsubrank(a)=\min_{\phi\in\mathbf{X}(S,\leqslant)}\phi(a)\,.

The theory of asymptotic spectra, as well as Theorem
2.2
, was first applied to study the complexity of the matrix multiplication problem
[
Str88
,
Str91
]
. Subsequently, in
[
Zui19
,
LZ20
]
, the authors used this theory to introduce the (quantum) asymptotic spectrum of graphs, allowing them to establish a dual characterization for the (quantum) Shannon capacity on graphs. In the context of quantum information theory,
[
JV19
]
used this theory to provide a characterization of the converse error exponents for asymptotic entanglement transformations. In the following section, we will build a commutative semiring for the set of nonnegative matrices in which the theory of asymptotic spectra can be applied. This will allow us to establish a characterization for the asymptotic nonnegative rank.

3
Asymptotic spectrum of nonnegative matrices and duality

3.1
Asymptotic spectrum of nonnegative matrices

In this section, our goal is to apply the theory of the previous section to give the dual characterization of the asymptotic nonnegative rank of matrices. We remind the readers that
\otimes
denotes the Kronecker product,
\oplus
denotes the direct sum of matrices, and
\mathrm{I}_{n}
denotes the identity matrix of size
n\times n
.

A natural semiring can be defined by considering the set of nonnegative matrices equipped with the binary operators
\oplus
and
\otimes
. However, the Kronecker product
\otimes
is not commutative. Therefore, in the following construction, rather than working with the set of nonnegative matrices, we will work with its
quotient
space where
\oplus
and
\otimes
are commutative. In fact, we will introduce an equivalence relation in which equivalent elements share the same nonnegative rank, and the induced operators
\oplus/\otimes
on the corresponding quotient space are commutative. This allows us to bypass the technical requirement and apply the theory of asymptotic spectra.

Denote by
\mathbb{M}(m,n)
the set of all nonnegative matrices of size
m\times n
, for two nonnegative matrices
A,B\in\mathbb{M}(m,n)
, we write
A\cong B
if there is a pair of matrices
(X,Y)\in\mathrm{GL}^{+}(m)\times\mathrm{GL}^{+}(n)
such that
A=XBY^{\top}
(or equivalently
B=X^{-1}A(Y^{-1})^{\top}
). It is clear that
\cong
is an equivalence relation since if
A=X_{1}BY_{1}^{T},B=X_{2}CY_{2}^{\top}
, then
A=(X_{1}X_{2})C(Y_{1}Y_{2})^{\top}
with
X_{1}X_{2}\in\mathrm{GL}^{+}(m),Y_{1}Y_{2}\in\mathrm{GL}^{+}(n)
. In addition, the relation
\cong
enjoys other nice properties, as shown in Lemma
3.1
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Given four matrices
A,B\in\mathbb{M}(m_{1},n_{1})
,
C,D\in\mathbb{M}(m_{2},n_{2})
such that
A\cong B,C\cong D
, we have:

1.

\nrank(A)=\nrank(B)
(and
\nrank(C)=\nrank(D)
).

2.

A\oplus C\cong B\oplus D
.

3.

A\otimes C\cong B\otimes D
.

Proof.

Since
A\cong B
, so there are
X\in\mathrm{GL}^{+}(m_{1})
and
Y\in\mathrm{GL}^{+}(n_{1})
such that
XAY^{T}=B
. This implies that
\nrank(A)=\nrank(B)
since
X,Y
are invertible matrices.

Since
C\cong D
, so there are
U\in\mathrm{GL}^{+}(m_{2})
and
V\in\mathrm{GL}^{+}(n_{2})
such that
UCV^{T}=D
. We have
(X\oplus U)(A\oplus C)(Y\oplus V)^{T}=B\oplus D
, therefore
A\oplus C\cong B\oplus D
since
X\oplus U\in\mathrm{GL}^{+}(m_{1}+m_{2})
and
Y\oplus V\in\mathrm{GL}^{+}(n_{1}+n_{2})
. Similarly, one has
(X\otimes U)(A\otimes C)(Y\otimes V)^{T}=B\otimes D
, this implies
A\otimes C\cong B\otimes D
.

∎

Let
A\in M(m_{A},n_{A})
and
B\in M(m_{B},n_{B})
, we say
A
and
B
are
equivalent
, and write
A\sim B
, if there are zero matrices
A_{0}=0\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{a_{1}\times a_{2}}
and
B_{0}=0\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{b_{1}\times b_{2}}
such that
A\oplus A_{0}\cong B\oplus B_{0}
. In fact,
\sim
is also an equivalence relation and elements in the same equivalence class shares a common nonnegative rank, as shown below.

Lemma 3.2
.

The binary relation
\sim
is an equivalence one. Moreover, the nonnegative rank is invariant under
\sim
.

Proof.

We first prove that
\sim
is an equivalence class. Since the reflexivity and symmetry of
\sim
is trivial, we focus on proving the transitivity of
\sim
, i.e, for three matrices
A,B,C
, if
A\sim B,B\sim C
, then
A\sim C
. Indeed, by definition of
\sim
, there exists zero matrices
A_{0},B_{0},B_{1},C_{0}
such that:

A\oplus A_{0}\cong B\oplus B_{0},\qquad B\oplus B_{1}\cong C\oplus C_{0}

Therefore,

(A\oplus A_{0})\oplus B_{1}\cong(B\oplus B_{0})\oplus B_{1},\qquad(B\oplus B_{1})\oplus B_{0}\cong(C\oplus C_{0})\oplus B_{0},

using Lemma
3.1
. Moreover,
B_{0}\oplus B_{1}=B_{1}\oplus B_{0}
since both are zero matrix of the same sizes. This implies
B\oplus B_{1}\oplus B_{0}=B\oplus B_{0}\oplus B_{1}
. Using the fact that
\cong
is an equivalence relation, we have:

A\oplus(A_{0}\oplus B_{1})\cong C\oplus(C_{0}\oplus B_{0})

Notice that both
A_{0}\oplus B_{1}
and
C_{0}\oplus B_{0}
are zero matrices. This proves the first claim.

Moreover, the direct sum of a matrix with a zero matrix does not affect the nonnegative rank. Therefore, if
A\sim B
, then
\nrank(A)=\nrank(A\oplus A_{0})=\nrank(B\oplus B_{0})=\nrank(B)
. This concludes the proof.
∎

Let
\mathbb{M}_{+}
be the set of
\sim
-equivalence classes of nonnegative matrices (which is now well-defined thanks to Lemma
3.2
) , we define the addition and multiplication on
\mathbb{M}_{+}
as follow

\displaystyle[A]\oplus[B]

\displaystyle=[A\oplus B]

\displaystyle[A]\otimes[B]

\displaystyle=[A\otimes B]\,.

where
[A]
denotes the equivalence class of the matrix
A
. These two operations are also well-defined, as shown in the following lemma.

3.2
Asymptotic nonnegative rank and asymptotic nonnegative subrank

From Subsection

\leqk
is a Strassen preorder. Thus, similar to Section

\displaystyle\nrank(A)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\min\{r\in\mathbb{N}:A\leqk\mathrm{I}_{r}\}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\max\{q\in\mathbb{N}:\mathrm{I}_{q}\leqk A\}\,.

Similarly to the asymptotic nonnegative rank, the

\displaystyle\asympsubnonrank(A)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\subnonrank(A^{\otimes n})^{1/n}=\sup_{n}\subnonrank(A^{\otimes n})^{1/n}\,.

Using the abstract theory of Strassen, the asymptotic spectrum of nonnegative matrices can be used to characterize the asymptotic nonnegative rank and the asymptotic nonnegative subrank.

Let

4
Some elements in the asymptotic spectrum of nonnegative matrices

From Theorem
3.7
, an explicit description of the asymptotic spectrum of nonnegative matrices is desired to understand the asymptotic nonnegative rank. However, the abstract theory of asymptotic spectra in Section
2
does not give an explicit construction the elements of
\mathbf{X}(\mathbb{M}_{+},\leqk)
. Previous works for graphs and tensors, thus, are devoted to show some elements in their asymptotic spectrum. Namely, for the (quantum) asymptotic spectrum of graphs, several spectral points were introduced in
[
Zui19
,
LZ20
,
GGL22
,
GL20
]
such as Lovász theta number
[
Lov79
]
. In the case of tensors,
[
Str91
]
provided a universal spectral point for the asymptotic spectrum of oblique tensors (oblique is a strict subset of all tensors). Recently, in
[
CVZ18
]
constructed an infinite family of the asymptotic spectrums of tensors over complex field. We recall that known all elements of the asymptotic spectrum of tensors can be used to determine the complexity of the matrix multiplication problem. In the same spirit, in this section, we show two elements that belong asymptotic spectrum of nonnegative matrices
\mathbf{X}(\mathbb{M}_{+},\leqk)
introduced in Section
3
, namely rank and fractional cover number. In addition, in the last subsection, we show that the asymptotic spectrum of lower triangular nonnegative matrices with non-zero values on their diagonal contains exactly a single point.

4.1
Rank

We first remind readers of the definition of rank of a matrix.

Definition 4.1
.

Given a matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, the rank of the matrix
A
(denoted as
\mathrm{rank}(A)
) is the largest integer
r
such that there exists an invertible square submatrix of
A
of size
r\times r
.

The following lemma shows that
\mathrm{rank}
is indeed a spectral point of
\mathbf{X}(\mathbb{M}_{+},\leqk)
.

Lemma 4.2
.

The matrix
rank
is an element of the asymptotic spectrum
\mathbf{X}(\mathbb{M}_{+},\leqk)
.

Proof.

Matrix rank is well-known to be multiplicative under
\otimes
and additive under
\oplus
. Also clearly
\mathrm{rank}(\mathrm{I}_{n})=n
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Finally, it holds for any two matrices
X,Y
that if
A=XBY
, using the fact that
\mathrm{rank}(AB)\leq\min\{\mathrm{rank}(A),\mathrm{rank}(B)\}
, then
\mathrm{rank}(A)\leq\mathrm{rank}(B)
. In particular, this is true if
X,Y
are nonnegative matrices, which proves rank is monotone.
∎

4.2
Fractional cover number

The fractional cover number is a parameter of nonnegative matrices introduced by Lovász
[
Lov75
]
to study the chromatic number of uniform hypergraphs. Furthermore, in
[
KKN95
]
, the authors showed that this parameter gives a characterization for amortized
nondeterministic communication complexity. In this subsection, we show that the fractional cover number is a spectral point of
\mathbf{X}(\mathbb{M}_{+},\leqk)
.

We first remind readers of the definition of fractional cover number. Let
A\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{m\times n}
be a nonnegative matrix, a subset
X\times Y\subseteq[m]\times[n]
is called monochromatic rectangle of the matrix
A
if
X\times Y\subseteq\mathrm{supp}(A)
. Let
R_{1},\dots,R_{k}
be all monochromatic rectangle of
A
. We define the
fractional cover number
of
A
, denoted
F(A)
, as follows

minimize:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}\tau(R_{i})

s.t

\displaystyle\sum_{i:(a,b)\in R_{i}}\tau(R_{i})\geq 1\text{ for all }(a,b)\in\mathrm{supp}(A)\,

\displaystyle 0\leq\tau(R_{i})\leq 1\,\text{ for all }i\in[k]\,.

The dual of the above linear program is given by:

maximize:

\displaystyle\sum_{(a,b)\in\mathrm{supp}(A)}\mu(a,b)

s.t

\displaystyle\sum_{(a,b)\in R_{i}}\mu(a,b)\leq 1\text{ for all }i\in[k]\,

\displaystyle 0\leq\mu(a,b)\leq 1\,\text{ for all }(a,b)\in\mathrm{supp}(A)\,.

In
[
Lov75
]
, the author showed that the fractional cover number has the following properties.

(i)

Multiplicative:
F(A\otimes B)=F(A)F(B)
.

(ii)

Additive:
F(A\oplus B)=F(A)+F(B)
.

It is easy to verify that
F(\mathrm{I}_{n})=n
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. To prove that
F
is a spectral point, it is sufficient to prove its monotonicity with respect to the preorder
\leqk
, that is, if
A\leqk B
then
F(A)\leq F(B)
. To do this, we first recall another characterization of fractional cover.

We begin by reminding the notions of
t
-fold coverings
and
t
-fold covering number
of nonnegative

minimize:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}\tau(R_{i})

\displaystyle\sum_{i:(a,b)\in R_{i}}\tau(R_{i})\geq t\text{ for all }(a,b)\in\mathrm{supp}(A)\,

\displaystyle\tau(R_{i})\in\mathbb{N}\,\text{ for all }i\in[k]\,.

In particular,

is simply a linear relaxation of the integer programming definition of

. It is clear that

is subadditive in its subscript, that is,

\displaystyle F_{s+t}(A)\leq F_{s}(A)+F_{t}(A),

because the sum of

-fold coverings. By Fekete’s lemma, we can define the

\displaystyle F^{*}(A)\coloneqq\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{F_{t}(A)}{t}=\inf_{t}\frac{F_{t}(A)}{t}\,.

Proposition 4.3

(
[

]
)
.

For any nonnegative matrix

, the fractional cover number is equal to the asymptotic fractional cover number, that is,
F^{*}(A)=F(A)

Proof.

The proof can be found in

, but we do it here for self-containedness.

Let
R:=\{R_{1},\dots,R_{k}\}
be the set of all monochromatic rectangles of
A
. The fractional cover

can be described in the following compact form:

\displaystyle\text{minimize: }\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}

(8)

\displaystyle\text{s.t. }\quad Mx

\displaystyle\geq 1

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle\geq 0\,.

where
M
is a binary matrix dependent on the set
R
.

First we prove
F(A)\leq F^{*}(A)
. Given an arbitrary positive integer
a
, let
\{R_{1},\dots,R_{v}\}
be the smallest
a
-fold covering of

and let

the vector encoding the number of times

appears in this covering. Then
Mx\geq a,x\geq 0
. This implies that

. Thus

is a feasible solution of the linear program (

), hence

. Since this holds for all positive integers

.

Next we prove that

that yields the minimum value of the linear program (

). Let

by choosing a monochromatic rectangle

with multiplicity

. Therefore

. By definition of

.∎

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
A,B,C
be nonnegative matrices, if

then
F(A)\leq\min(F(B),F(C))
.

Proof.

It is sufficient to prove that

. We will show the former, i.e.

. The latter can be demonstrated similarly.

In this proof, we represent a monochromatic rectangle by a rank-one binary matrix. In fact, given a subset
X\times Y\subset[m]\times[n]

1_{X}\times 1_{Y}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{m\times n}

1_{X}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{m}

(resp.

If

Let
\{R_{1},\dots,R_{k}\}

t
-fold covering of

We define

and

). We prove that the set

. It is immediate that

. To do so, we first demonstrate that

is a monochromatic rectangle of

. Indeed, we use the following property of nonnegative matrices: for

\mathrm{supp}(MN)=\mathrm{supp}(\mathrm{supp}(M)\mathrm{supp}(N)),

(9)

where

is determined by the product of supports of

and
N
(in their binary matrix forms). This claim only holds for nonnegative matrices and it is incorrect for general matrices.

First, it is clear that

\displaystyle R^{\prime}_{i}=\mathrm{supp}(R_{i}C)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{supp}(\mathrm{supp}(R_{i})\mathrm{supp}(C))

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:supportrelation}}{=}\mathrm{supp}(BC)=\mathrm{supp}(A).

Moreover,

4.3
Asymptotic spectrum of triangular nonnegative matrices

As defined in Section
3
, asymptotic spectrum is a
\leqk
monotone semiring homomorphism from
S
to
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
that satisfies the constraint
\phi(\mathrm{I}_{n})=n,\forall n\in\mathbb{N},\forall\phi\in\mathbf{X}(\mathbb{M}_{+},\leqk)
. In other words, the set of
\{\phi(\mathrm{I}_{n})\mid\phi\in\mathbf{X}(\mathbb{M}_{+},\leqk)\}
collapses to a single value
n
. In this section, we prove that this phenomenon also happens to the set of lower (square) triangular matrices with nonzero diagonal.

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{m\times n}
be a nonnegative matrix, we call the matrix
A
is a
nonnegative rectangle lower triangular
matrix if
A_{i,j}=0
for all
i>j
.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{m\times n}
be a rectangle lower triagular nonnegative matrix. Define
V=\{i\leq\min\{m,n\}:A_{i,i}\neq 0\}
, then
\asympsubnonrank(A)\geq|V|
.

Proof.

Let
d=|V|
and let
N
be any multiple of
d
. Our proof is constructive, i.e, for any
N
multiple of
d
, we can find a
diagonal
submatrix of
A^{\otimes N}
whose coefficients on the diagonal are nonzero of size
d_{N}
such that
\lim_{N\to\infty}d_{N}^{1/N}=d
. Our argument is valid since Proposition
3.9
assure that
\subnonrank(A^{\otimes N})\geq d_{N}
.

We argue that one can assume that the matrix
A
is square
A
, i.e.
m=n
. Indeed, if
A
is not square, one can take the principle submatrix
B
of size
p\times p
of the matrix
A
where
p=\min(m,n)
and prove that
\asympsubnonrank(B)\geq|V|
. Since
\asympsubnonrank(A)\geq\asympsubnonrank(B)
(by Proposition
3.9
), we have:
\asympsubnonrank(A)\geq|V|
.

Hence, in the remaining proof, we assume
m=n
and the size of the matrix
A^{\otimes N}
is thus
n^{N}\times n^{N}
. In this proof, we will use
\mathcal{I}:=\{0,\ldots,n^{N}-1\}
as the set of row (or column) index of
A^{\otimes N}
. It is convenient to represent each element of
\mathcal{I}
in
n
-base (or radix), i.e, given
i\in\mathcal{I}_{R}
, we write
i=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{N}),0\leq i\leq n-1
to implies:
i=\sum_{k=1}^{N}i_{k}n^{k-1}
. This representation is useful since the coefficient at the index
(i,j)
of
A^{\otimes N}
has the form:

A^{\otimes N}_{i,j}=\prod_{k=1}^{N}A_{i_{k},j_{k}}.

(10)

Define
S(N)
the subset of
\mathrm{supp}(A^{\otimes N})

on the diagonal
such that for all
(i,i)\in S(N),i=(i_{1},\dots,i_{N})\in S(N)
, we have:

\displaystyle S(N)\coloneqq\{(i,i)\in\mathrm{supp}(A^{\otimes N})\mid|\{k\in[N]:i_{k}=p\}|=N/d\text{ for all }p\in V\}

In words, if
(i,i)\in S(N)
, then the
n
-basis representation of
i
contains exactly
N/d
digits
p
for every
p\in V
. The size of
S(N)
is thus,

|S(N)|=\binom{N}{N/d,\dots,N/d}\geq\frac{d^{N}}{(N+1)^{d}}\,.

The inequality follows from the fact that the largest multinomial coefficient is the central one, that is,
\binom{N}{a_{1},\dots,a_{d}}\leq\binom{N}{\frac{N}{d},\dots,\frac{N}{d}}
and the number of possible partitions of
N
into
d
parts is at most
(N+1)^{d}
. This proves
\asympsubnonrank(A)\geq\lim_{N\to\infty}|S(N)|^{1/N}=d
. Thus, it is sufficient to show that the
principle
submatrix of
A
restricted to indices
S(N)
is a diagonal matrix with nonzero coefficients in its diagonal.

Consider two indices
i,j\in S(N)
with
i=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{N}),j=(j_{1},\ldots,j_{N})
and
i\neq j
. Since
\sum_{k=1}^{N}i_{k}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}y_{k}=(N/d)\sum_{p\in V}p
, there must exist an index
\ell\leq N
such that
i_{\ell}>j_{\ell}
. By Equation
10
, we deduce that
A_{i,j}=0
(since
A_{i_{\ell},j_{\ell}}=0
). In addition,
A_{i,i}=\prod_{k=1}^{N}A_{i_{k},i_{k}}\neq 0,\forall i\in S(N)
because
i_{k}\in V
. That shows elements on the diagonal are nonzero. That concludes the proof.
∎

Denote
T\subseteq\mathbb{M}_{+}
is a subsemiring which generated by all lower triangular matrices with nonzero entries on diagonal under operation
\oplus
and
\otimes
. From Lemma
4.8
, the asymptotic spectrum
\mathbf{X}(T,\leqk)
contains only a single point. It is a corollary of Lemma

.

Corollary 4.9
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{m\times n}
be a lower rectangular triangular nonnegative matrix with nonzero on its diagonal. Then
\phi(A)=\min(m,n)
for any
\phi\in\mathbf{X}(\mathbb{M}_{+},\leqk)
.

Proof.

Let
d=\min(m,n)
, since

with nonzero on its diagonal, it implies
\mathrm{rank}(A)=\nrank(A)=d
, therefore
\asynrank(A)\leq d
. By Lemma
4.8
one has
\asympsubnonrank(A)\geq d

(which states

∎

Appendix A
Proof of technical lemmas

Theorem A.1
.

For every pair of nonnegative matrices
(X,Y)
, we have:

\nrank(X\oplus Y)=\nrank(X)+\nrank(Y)

Proof.

Let
m=\nrank(X)
and
n=\nrank(Y)
. By definition, there exist sets of pairs of nonnegative vectors:
\{(a_{i},b_{i})\mid i=1,\ldots,m\}
and
\{(c_{i},d_{i})\mid i=1,\ldots,n\}
such that:

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}b_{i}^{\top},\qquad Y

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}d_{i}^{\top}

(11)

To prove the equality, it is sufficient to show that
\nrank(X\oplus Y)\leq m+n
and
\nrank(X\oplus Y)\geq m+n
. Both inequalities are shown below:

1.

Proof of
\nrank(X\oplus Y)\leq m+n
: This claim is trivial because:

X\oplus Y=\begin{pmatrix}X&0\\
0&Y\end{pmatrix}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\bar{a}_{i}\bar{b}_{i}^{\top}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bar{c}_{i}\bar{d}_{i}^{\top}

where
\bar{a}_{i}=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}a_{i}\\
0\end{smallmatrix}\right]
and
b_{i}=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}b_{i}\\
0\end{smallmatrix}\right],\forall i=1,\ldots,m
, the concatenation of vectors
a_{i},b_{i}
with a zero vector respectively. Similarly,
\bar{c}_{i}=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0\\
c_{i}\end{smallmatrix}\right]
and
\bar{d}_{i}=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0\\
d_{i}\end{smallmatrix}\right],\forall i=1,\ldots,n
. Since
\bar{a}_{i},\bar{b}_{i},\bar{c}_{i},\bar{d}_{i}
are nonnegative by construction, we have:
\nrank(X\oplus Y)\leq m+n
.

2.

Proof of
\nrank(X\oplus Y)\geq m+n
: Assume that
\nrank(X\oplus Y)=\ell
. By definition of nonnegative rank, there exists a set of pairs of nonnegative vectors
(x_{i},y_{i})
such that:

X\oplus Y=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}x_{i}y_{i}^{\top}

(12)

Note that the “top right” and the “bottom left” of
X\oplus Y
are zero matrices, we must have either:
\mathrm{supp}(x_{i}y_{i}^{\top})\subseteq\mathrm{supp}(\left[\begin{smallmatrix}X&0\\
0&0\end{smallmatrix}\right])
or
\mathrm{supp}(x_{i}y_{i}^{\top})\subseteq\mathrm{supp}(\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0&0\\
0&Y\end{smallmatrix}\right])
. As a consequence, each pair
(x_{i},y_{i})
can be written either:

\displaystyle(x_{i},y_{i})

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{bmatrix}\bar{x}_{i}\\
0\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}\bar{y}_{i}\\
0\end{bmatrix}\right)

(13)

\displaystyle\text{ or }\quad(x_{i},y_{i})

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{bmatrix}0\\
\bar{x}_{i}\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}0\\
\bar{y}_{i}\end{bmatrix}\right)

(14)

where
(\bar{x}_{i},\bar{y}_{i})
are nonnegative vectors.

One can thus partition the set
\{1,\ldots,\ell\}
into two sets
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
such that
i\in\mathcal{X}
(resp.
i\in\mathcal{Y}
) if
(x_{i},y_{i})
has the form in Equation (
13
) (resp. Equation (
14
)). To make Equation (
12
), it is necessary that:

X=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{X}}\bar{x}_{i}\bar{y}_{i}^{\top},\qquad Y=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{Y}}\bar{x}_{i}\bar{y}_{i}^{\top}

By definition of nonnegative rank, we have:
|\mathcal{X}|\geq m,|\mathcal{Y}|\geq n
. Thus,
\ell=|\mathcal{X}|+|\mathcal{Y}|=m+n
. This concludes the proof. ∎
