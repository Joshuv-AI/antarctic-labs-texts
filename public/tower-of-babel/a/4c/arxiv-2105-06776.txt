Abstract
. Diophantine approximation is traditionally the study of how well real numbers are approximated by rationals. We propose a model for studying Diophantine approximation in an arbitrary totally bounded metric space where the rationals are replaced with a countable hierarchy of “well-spread” points, which we refer to as abstract rationals . We prove various Jarník–Besicovitch type dimension bounds and investigate their sharpness.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Diophantine approximation in a metric space

Let
(F,d)
be a non-empty totally bounded metric space. For
q\in\mathbb{N}
, define
abstract rationals of level
q
by fixing a maximal
(1/q)
-separated set
P(q)\subseteq F
. By
(1/q)
-
separated
we mean that for all
p_{1},p_{2}\in P(q)
,
d(p_{1},p_{2})\geqslant 1/q
and by
maximal
we mean it is impossible to add another point to the set and maintain the separation. Since
F
is totally bounded,
P(q)
is a finite set for all
q
. One should think of
P(q)
as the set of rationals with denominator
q
inside
F
. We refer to the set
\cup_{q}P(q)
as a set of
abstract rationals
, noting that it is immediately a countable dense subset of
F
. For
t>0
let

\displaystyle F_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{x\in F:\text{ i.o. for some $p\in P(q)$ }\,d(p,x)\leqslant 1/q^{t}\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigcap_{N}\bigcup_{q>N}\bigcup_{p\in P(q)}B(p,1/q^{t})

where
B(x,r)
generally denotes the closed ball centred at
x\in X
with radius
r>0
. Clearly, if
t\leqslant 1
, then
F_{t}=F
and so we are interested in
t>1
. Computing (or estimating) the size (e.g. Hausdorff dimension) of
F_{t}
describes “how much of
F
can be far away from a chosen set of well spread out points”.

Classical Diophantine approximation may be cast in this language. For
example, if
F=[0,1]
with the Euclidean distance and

P(q)=\mathbb{Z}/q\cap[0,1]
are the rationals with denominator

q
, then
F_{t}
is the set of
t
-well-approximable real numbers in

[0,1]
. In this setting, Dirichlet’s theorem tells us that

F_{2}=[0,1]
, the Borel–Cantelli lemma tells us that
F_{t}
is a
null set for all
t>2
, and the Jarník–Besicovitch theorem gives

\dim_{\textup{H}}F_{t}=2/t

for all
t\geqslant 2
, where
\dim_{\textup{H}}
denotes the Hausdorff
dimension. That is, the exponent
t_{0}=2
is a natural
threshold beyond which it is sensible to study the dimensions of

F_{t}
. Our aim here is to establish analogous results in the general
setting of abstract rationals.

The inspiration for studying abstract rationals comes from problems
of
intrinsic
Diophantine approximation. In such problems,
one has a subset
X
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and the goal is to understand
how well its points can be approximated by rational points which
also lie on
X
. The set
X
can for example be taken to be a
manifold
[
FKMS18
]
or a fractal set
[
BFR11
]
. Questions on
intrinsic Diophantine approximation on fractals have attracted much
attention and there have been many interpretations and answers to the
now-classical Mahler’s problem: ‘How well can irrationals in the
Cantor set be approximated by rationals in the Cantor set?’
Interpretations include random rational points
[
BD16
]
, intrinsic
rationals
[
FMS18
,
FS14
,
LSV07
,
S21
]
, and dynamical rationals

[
B19
,
AB
]
.

In Section
2
we provide analogues to the
Jarník–Besicovitch theorem in metric spaces using the language of
abstract rationals. It turns out that the problem is subtle and the
answer depends on how the abstract rationals are chosen. Despite the
generality of the notion of abstract rationals, the bounds we prove in
this general set-up are sharp in many cases of particular
interest. For example, Theorem
2.1
below demonstrates that the
upper bound for the dimension value in the Jarník–Besicovitch
theorem holds in much more generality, replacing “
2
” with
“
\dim_{\textup{P}}F+1
” where
\dim_{\textup{P}}
denotes the

packing
dimension. We also show in Proposition
2.2

that in general one cannot replace “
2
” with
“
\dim_{\textup{H}}F+1
” or “
2\dim_{\textup{H}}F
”, for example.
In order to estimate
\dim_{\textup{H}}F_{t}
in general we naturally
encounter several different notions of fractal dimension, which we
recall in the following subsection.

We obtain stronger dimension bounds in the special case when
F
is Ahlfors–David regular, see Theorem
2.7
. In Section
3
, during the investigation of the
sharpness of the bounds in the Ahlfors–David regular case, we
encounter the notions of
Dirichlet exponent
and
Dirichlet
spectrum
. By Dirichlet exponent we mean the threshold
t_{0}
after
which
F_{t}
does not (essentially) contain a ball. The
definition is a natural extension of the classical Dirichlet
exponent (
t_{0}=2
) to the setting of abstract rationals. We find
that in general the Dirichlet exponent of
F
depends on the choice
of abstract rationals, so we are occasioned to consider the
collection of possible Dirichlet exponents for a given
F
—the
Dirichlet spectrum of
F
. The main result of Section
2
and the mass transference principle of Beresnevich and
Velani
[
BV06
]
allow us to bound the Dirichlet spectrum and
reveal an interplay between Dirichlet exponents and the dimensions
of
F_{t}
. In studying that interplay, we find that the dimension
bounds from Theorem
2.7
are
sharp.

1.2.
Dimension theory

An important problem in the study of fractal objects is to characterise their dimension. There are many ways to define dimension and the relationship between the notions is especially interesting, and relevant to us. See
[
F14
,
F20
]
for more background on dimension theory and fractal geometry.

Given
s\geqslant 0
, the
s
-dimensional Hausdorff measure
of a set
X
in a metric space is defined by

\mathcal{H}^{s}(X)=\lim_{r\to 0}\,\inf\bigg\{\sum_{i}\lvert U_{i}\rvert^{s}:\{U_{i}\}_{i}\text{ is a countable $r$-cover of $X$}\bigg\}.

The
Hausdorff dimension
of
X
is then

\dim_{\textup{H}}X=\inf\Big\{s\geqslant 0:\mathcal{H}^{s}(X)=0\Big\}=\sup\Big\{s\geqslant 0:\mathcal{H}^{s}(X)=\infty\Big\}.

A subtle feature of the Hausdorff dimension is that sets of different sizes may be used in the covers and their contribution is weighted accordingly. The (upper) box dimension is a simpler notion of dimension which avoids this subtlety. The
upper box dimension
of
X
is

\overline{\dim}_{\textup{B}}X=\limsup_{r\to 0}\,\frac{\log N_{r}(X)}{-\log r},

where
N_{r}(X)
is maximum cardinality of an
r
-separated subset of
X
. If the
\limsup
is really a limit then we refer to the box dimension, written
\dim_{\text{B}}X
. Note that if
X
is not totally bounded then
N_{r}(X)
may be infinite.

The packing dimension of
X
may be defined via packing measure in an analogous way to the Hausdorff dimension, but we use a common alternative formulation in terms of the upper box dimension. We define the
packing dimension
of
X
by

\dim_{\textup{P}}X=\inf\left\{\sup_{i}\overline{\dim}_{\textup{B}}X_{i}\ :\ X=\bigcup_{i}X_{i}\right\}.

It should be clear from the definitions that

\dim_{\textup{H}}X\leqslant\dim_{\textup{P}}X\leqslant\overline{\dim}_{\textup{B}}X.

Finally, the
lower dimension
of
X
is defined by

\displaystyle{\@index{\@indexphrase{lower dimension}}}\dim_{\textup{L}}X\ =\ \sup\Bigg\{\ s\geqslant 0

\displaystyle:

there exists a constant
C>0
such that,

for all
0<r<R\leqslant|X|
and
x\in X
,

\displaystyle\hskip 76.82243pt\text{$N_{r}\big(B(x,R)\cap X\big)\ \geqslant\ C\bigg(\frac{R}{r}\bigg)^{s}$ }\Bigg\}

where
|X|
denotes the diameter of
X
. The lower dimension is the natural dual to the Assouad dimension and identifies the “thinnest part” of the space. If
X
is closed, then

\dim_{\textup{L}}X\leqslant\dim_{\textup{H}}X.

See
[
F20
, Chapter 3]
for more background on the lower dimension, including its relevance in a different area of Diophantine approximation, e.g.
[
F20
, Section 14.2]
.

2.
Main results

2.1.
General bounds

Our first result provides general bounds for the dimension of
F_{t}
which hold independently of how the abstract rationals are chosen. The general lower bound assumes compactness of
F
. If
F
is not compact, then this lower bound does not hold. For example, if
F=[0,1]\cap\mathbb{Q}
, then the packing dimension of
F=F_{1}
is
0
and the lower dimension is
1
.

Theorem 2.1
.

For all
t\geqslant 1

\dim_{\textup{H}}F_{t}\leqslant\frac{\dim_{\textup{P}}F+1}{t}

and, if
F
is compact,

\dim_{\textup{P}}F_{t}\geqslant\frac{\dim_{\textup{L}}F}{t}.

We begin with the general upper bound. Let
b>\dim_{\textup{P}}F
and let

F=\bigcup_{i}F_{i}

be a countable decomposition of
F
with the property that
\overline{\dim}_{\textup{B}}F_{i}<b
for all
i
. Temporarily fix an index
i
. We write

d(p,F_{i})=\inf\{d(p,x):x\in F_{i}\}.

For all
N\geqslant 1

\bigcup_{q>N}\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\in P(q):\\
d(p,F_{i})\leqslant 1/q\end{subarray}}\{B(p,1/q^{t})\}

provides a
2/N^{t}
-cover of
F_{t}\cap F_{i}
. Therefore, for some constant
C_{1}\geqslant 1
depending on
F
,
i
and
b
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{s}_{2/N^{t}}(F_{t}\cap F_{i})

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\sum_{q>N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\in P(q):\\
d(p,F_{i})\leqslant 1/q\end{subarray}}|B(p,1/q^{t})|^{s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{q>N}(\#\{p\in P(q):d(p,F_{i})\leqslant 1/q\})(2/q^{t})^{s}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle 2^{s}C_{1}\sum_{q\geqslant 1}q^{b-ts}<\infty

provided
s>\frac{b+1}{t}
. For such
s

\mathcal{H}^{s}(F_{t})\leqslant\sum_{i}\mathcal{H}^{s}(F_{t}\cap F_{i})=0

and the desired result follows.

Turning our attention to the general lower bound, let
0<l<\dim_{\textup{L}}F
(we may assume the lower dimension is strictly positive). Fix a rapidly increasing sequence of integers
q_{k}
satisfying

(1)

q_{1}q_{2}\cdots q_{k}\leqslant\log(q_{k+1})

and construct a nested decreasing sequence of compact sets
E_{k}\subseteq F_{t}
by setting
E_{1}=B(z,q_{1}^{-t})
with
z\in F
chosen arbitrarily and then letting
E_{k}
be the union of the closed balls
B(p,q_{k}^{-t})
with
p\in P(q_{k})
which lie completely inside
E_{k-1}
. The sets
E_{k}
are compact since
F
is compact and so by the Cantor intersection theorem

E=\bigcap_{k}E_{k}\subseteq F_{t}

with
E
non-empty and compact. We show
\dim_{\textup{P}}E\geqslant l/t
, which completes the proof. Let
\mu
be a probability measure supported on
E
constructed by assigning unit mass to
E_{1}
and then, given a
k
-level cylinder
B(p,q_{k}^{-t})
used in the construction of
E_{k}
, redistribute the mass of
B(p,q_{k}^{-t})
equally among the
(k+1)
-level cylinders lying inside. Since the
q_{k}
are rapidly increasing and
P(q_{k})
is
(1/q_{k})
-separated, using the definition of lower dimension, we may assume that there are at least

\left(\frac{q_{k}^{-t}}{q_{k+1}^{-1}}\right)^{l}

(k+1)
-level cylinders inside every
k
-level cylinder. For each
k
, consider
r=q_{k}^{-t}
. It follows that any ball
B(x,r)
with
x\in E
intersects at most one
k
-level cylinder. Recall that the centres of the
k
-level cylinders are
(1/q_{k})
-separated. Therefore

(2)

\displaystyle\mu(B(x,r))

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\left(\frac{q_{1}^{-t}}{q_{2}^{-1}}\right)^{-l}\left(\frac{q_{2}^{-t}}{q_{3}^{-1}}\right)^{-l}\cdots\left(\frac{q_{k-1}^{-t}}{q_{k}^{-1}}\right)^{-l}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle q_{1}^{tl}(q_{2}\cdots q_{k-1})^{tl-l}(q_{k}^{-1})^{l}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\left(\log(q_{k})\right)^{tl}(q_{k}^{-1})^{l}\qquad\text{by \eqref{rapid}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{|\log r|}{t}\right)^{tl}r^{l/t}.

This proves that for all
x\in E
, the upper local dimension of
\mu
at
x
is at least
l/t
. Applying standard estimates for packing dimension coming from local dimension of measures, e.g.
[
F97
, Proposition 2.3]
, this proves that
\dim_{\textup{P}}E\geqslant l/t
as required.
∎

2.2.
Sharpness of general bounds

2.2.1.
Sharpness of upper bound

Comparing the upper bound from Theorem
2.1
with the classical
Jarník–Besicovitch theorem, one might ask if the packing dimension can be
replaced by the Hausdorff dimension, or indeed if an upper bound of
the form
c\dim_{\textup{H}}F/t
is possible for some uniform constant

c
. The following proposition shows that this is not possible and
demonstrates that the packing dimension should appear in bounding the
Hausdorff dimension of
F_{t}
.

Proposition 2.2
.

For all
t>2
, there exists a compact set
F\subseteq[0,1]
and a choice of abstract rationals such that

\dim_{\textup{P}}F=1

and

\dim_{\textup{H}}F_{t}=\dim_{\textup{H}}F=2/t=\frac{\dim_{\textup{P}}F+1}{t}.

In particular we cannot replace the packing dimension by the Hausdorff dimension in Theorem
2.1
.

Let
E_{t}
denote the set of
t
-well-approximable numbers in
[0,1]
in the classical setting. Then

\dim_{\textup{H}}E_{t}=2/t

and
\mathcal{H}^{2/t}(E_{t})=\infty
. This is the Jarník–Besicovitch theorem, but we emphasise the infinitude of the Hausdorff measure which we need for what follows, see
[
BBDF09
, Theorem 11]
. By
[
F14
, Theorem 4.10]
there exists a compact subset
E_{t}^{*}\subseteq E_{t}
such that

\dim_{\textup{H}}E_{t}^{*}=2/t.

Let

F=E_{t}^{*}\cup\{p/q:p\in\mathbb{Z},\,q\in\mathbb{N},\,|p/q-x|\leqslant 1/q\text{ for some }x\in E_{t}^{*}\}.

Then
F
is compact and

\dim_{\textup{H}}F=\dim_{\textup{H}}E_{t}=2/t.

The abstract rationals
P(q)
are then chosen to be
P(q)=\mathbb{Z}/q\cap F
. By construction
E_{t}^{*}\subseteq E_{t}\cap F=F_{t}
and so

\dim_{\textup{H}}F_{t}=\dim_{\textup{H}}F=2/t.

Moreover, by Theorem
2.1
,

2/t=\dim_{\textup{H}}F_{t}\leqslant\frac{\dim_{\textup{P}}F+1}{t}

which forces
\dim_{\textup{P}}F=1
, completing the proof.
∎

The next result is along similar lines but this time the construction works for arbitrary choices of abstract rationals.

Proposition 2.3
.

For all integers
t\geqslant 2
, there exists a compact set
F\subseteq[0,1]
such that

F_{s}=F

for all
1\leqslant s<t
and all choices of abstract rationals
\mathcal{P}=\bigcup_{q}P(q)
. In particular, for all
t\geqslant 1
,

\sup_{F}\inf_{\mathcal{P}}\frac{\dim_{\textup{H}}F_{t}}{\dim_{\textup{H}}F}=1

where the supremum is taken over compact sets
F\subseteq[0,1]
with
\dim_{\textup{H}}F>0
and the infimum is taken over all choices of abstract rationals for
F
.

Consider an iterated function system (IFS) type construction which alternates between
\{x\mapsto x/2,x\mapsto(x+1)/2\}
and
\{x\mapsto x/2\}
. See
[
F14
]
for background on IFS theory. Let
N(k)
be the number of times we choose the first IFS in the first
k
levels. Then it is standard that

\dim_{\textup{H}}F=\liminf_{k\to\infty}N(k)/k

and

\dim_{\textup{P}}F=\limsup_{k\to\infty}N(k)/k.

Let
k_{n}
be a very rapidly increasing sequence of integers, e.g.
k_{n}=n^{n}
. Fix an integer
t\geqslant 2
and construct
F
inductively by using the first IFS
k_{n}
times, followed by the second IFS
(t-1)k_{n}
times. This is repeated in blocks of
tk_{n}
. Then

\dim_{\textup{H}}F=1/t<1=\dim_{\textup{P}}F.

Consider
x\in F
and especially the cylinder
E
of level

M=t\sum_{n=1}^{N}k_{n}

containing
x
. (Just after the
N
th long run of the second IFS). This cylinder has size
2^{-M}\leqslant 2^{-tk_{N}}
. There are very large holes to the left and right of this cylinder, of size

>2\cdot 2^{-k_{N}\Delta}

where
\Delta>1
can be chosen arbitrarily provided
N
is large enough. Due to the presence of these holes, there must exist
p\in P(q)\cap E
for some
q
with
1/q\geqslant 2^{-k_{N}\Delta}
. This ensures that the whole set
F
is contained in the

2^{-tk_{N}}=\left(2^{-k_{N}\Delta}\right)^{t/\Delta}\leqslant(1/q)^{t/\Delta}

neighbourhood of
P(q)
for infinitely many
q
. This ensures that

F_{t/\Delta}=F

for all
\Delta>1
.
∎

2.2.2.
Sharpness of lower bound

Comparing the lower bound from Theorem
2.1
with the classical
Jarník–Besicovitch theorem, one might ask if the lower dimension
can be replaced by the Hausdorff dimension, or indeed if a lower bound
of the form
(\dim_{\textup{L}}F+1)/t
or
c\dim_{\textup{H}}F/t
is
possible for some uniform constant
c
. The following proposition
shows that these bounds are not possible in general and demonstrates
that
F_{t}
(
t>1
) can be very small, even countable, if the abstract
rationals are “far away” from the large parts of
F
.

Proposition 2.4
.

There exists a compact set
F\subseteq[0,1]
with
\dim_{\textup{H}}F=\dim_{\textup{P}}F>0
and such that, for some choice of abstract rationals,
F_{t}
is countable for all
t>1
and, for another choice of abstract rationals,

\dim_{\textup{H}}F_{t}>0

for all
t>1
.

Let
F_{0}
be the middle third Cantor set (see
[
F14
, Example 4.5]
) and
F
be
F_{0}\cup C
where
C
is the countable set consisting of a single point in the centre of each complementary interval of
F_{0}
. Clearly
\dim_{\textup{H}}F=\dim_{\textup{P}}F=s=\log_{3}2>0
. We choose the first set of abstract rationals such that
\cup_{q}P(Q)=C
. More precisely, for
q\geqslant 1
, let
C_{k}\subseteq C
be the set of centres of complementary intervals of length
3^{-k}
where
k
is chosen such that
3^{-(k-1)}/2\leqslant 1/q<3^{-(k-2)}/2
. Then choose
P(q)
to be a maximal
(1/q)
-separated subset of
C
which contains
C_{k}
. Note that
P(q)
is a maximal
(1/q)
-separated subset of
F
by construction. Fix
t>1
,
q\in\mathbb{N}
and
p\in P(q)
and let
k
be as above. Then

B(p,q^{-t})\subseteq B(p,3^{-t(k-2)}/2^{t})=\{p\}

for sufficiently large
q
. More precisely, once
q
is large enough so that
k
is large enough to guarantee

3^{-t(k-2)}/2^{t}<3^{-k}/2.

It follows that
F_{t}=C
which is countable and, in particular,

\dim_{\textup{H}}F_{t}=\dim_{\textup{P}}F_{t}=0.

For the second choice of abstract rationals, choose
P(q)
such that it contains a maximal
(1/q)
-separated subset of
F_{0}
, which we denote by
P_{0}(q)\subseteq F_{0}
. Let
F_{0,t}=(F_{0})_{t}\subseteq F_{0}
be the set of points in
F_{0}
which are i.o.
t
-approximable by abstract rationals
P_{0}(q)
. Then, since
F_{0}
is
s
-Ahlfors–David regular, it follows from Theorem
2.7
, which we prove later, that

\dim_{\textup{H}}F_{t}\geqslant\dim_{\textup{H}}F_{0,t}\geqslant s/t>0

as required.
∎

The next result is an upgrade of the previous example to show that the lower bound
\dim_{\textup{L}}F/t
is sharp in a stronger sense.

Proposition 2.5
.

For all
0\leqslant L\leqslant H\leqslant 1
, there exists a compact set
F\subseteq[0,1]
with

\dim_{\textup{L}}F=L

and

\dim_{\textup{H}}F=\dim_{\textup{P}}F=H

such that for some choice of abstract rationals,

\dim_{\textup{H}}F_{t}=L/t=\frac{\dim_{\textup{L}}F}{t}

for all
t>1
.

This proposition is proved in the same way as Proposition
2.4
but with the middle third cantor set replaced by a central (self-similar) cantor set of dimension
H
and with the single points placed in the complementary intervals replaced by scaled copies of a fixed set
E
with
\dim_{\textup{L}}E=\dim_{\textup{H}}E=L
. The set
E
could be another central cantor set, for example. The scaling is done such that the diameter of a scaled copy is much smaller than the length of the complementary interval it is placed in. The abstract rationals are chosen to be contained in the countably many copies of
E
and within each copy
E^{\prime}
of
E
must be chosen such that

(3)

\dim_{\textup{H}}E^{\prime}_{t}=\frac{\dim_{\textup{H}}E^{\prime}}{t}.

The details work the same, but the challenge is in finding a way to choose the set
E
and abstract rationals to satisfy (
3
). We choose
E
to be an
L
-Ahlfors–David regular set with the abstract rationals chosen as in Proposition
3.4
, which we prove later.
∎

There are many other ways in which one could investigate sharpness of Theorem
2.1
. For example, our results above are pointwise in the sense that for a given
t
a set
F
is constructed. It may be interesting to consider if one can construct sets witnessing sharpness for all
t\geqslant 1
simultaneously. Another question of sharpness which we have not answered is if the
Hausdorff
dimension of
F_{t}
can be estimated from below in general.

Question 2.6
.

Is it true that for all
t\geqslant 1

\dim_{\textup{H}}F_{t}\geqslant\frac{\dim_{\textup{L}}F}{t}?

2.3.
The Ahlfors–David regular case

It turns out we can say more when
F
is an Ahlfors–David regular metric space. Recall that a compact set
F
is
s
-Ahlfors–David regular for some
s\geqslant 0
if there exists a constant
C>0
such that

r^{s}/C\leqslant\mathcal{H}^{s}(B(x,r))\leqslant Cr^{s}

for all
0<r<1
and all
x\in F
. In this case it is not hard to see that

\dim_{\textup{L}}F=\dim_{\textup{H}}F=\dim_{\textup{P}}F=\overline{\dim}_{\textup{B}}F=s

and that
F
is doubling, see
[
F20
, Theorem 6.4.1]
.

Theorem 2.7
.

Suppose
F
is a compact
s
-Ahlfors–David regular. For all
t\geqslant 1

\frac{s}{t}\leqslant\dim_{\textup{H}}F_{t}\leqslant\frac{s+1}{t}.

Given the general bounds, all that remains to establish is

\dim_{\textup{H}}F_{t}\geqslant\frac{s}{t}

which we prove now. The strategy is similar to the general lower bound, except that we need to control the measure at all scales, not just a sequence of scales. Let
E
and
\mu
be as in the proof of Theorem
2.1
. Given
r\in(0,q_{1}^{-t})
, let
k
be chosen uniquely such that

q_{k+1}^{-1}\leqslant r<q_{k}^{-1}.

Since
t\geqslant 1
is fixed and the
q_{k}
are rapidly increasing, we may assume that either

(4)

q_{k+1}^{-1}\leqslant r<q_{k}^{-t}\leqslant q_{k}^{-1}

or

(5)

q_{k+1}^{-1}<q_{k}^{-t}\leqslant r<q_{k}^{-1}.

Consider the first case (
4
). There is a uniform constant
C_{3}
such that an arbitrary ball
B(x,r)
may intersect
C_{3}(r/q_{k+1}^{-1})^{s}
many
(k+1)
-level cylinders. This uses the fact that
P(q_{k+1})
is
(1/q_{k+1})
-separated and the fact that
F
is
s
-Ahfors-David regular. Moreover, each
(k+1)
-level cylinder carries mass at most

(6)

q_{1}^{ts}(q_{2}\cdots q_{k})^{s(t-1)}(q_{k+1}^{-1})^{s}

by following the argument above (
2
). Therefore

\displaystyle\mu(B(x,r))

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle C_{3}(r/q_{k+1}^{-1})^{s}q_{1}^{ts}(q_{2}\cdots q_{k})^{s(t-1)}(q_{k+1}^{-1})^{s}\qquad\text{by \eqref{dunkel}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C_{3}q_{1}^{ts}(q_{2}\cdots q_{k})^{s(t-1)}\,r^{s}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle C_{3}\left(\log(q_{k})\right)^{ts}q_{k}^{s(t-1)}\,r^{s}\qquad\text{by \eqref{rapid}}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle C_{3}|\log r|^{ts}\left(r^{-1/t}\right)^{s(t-1)}\,r^{s}\qquad\text{by \eqref{case1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C_{3}|\log r|^{ts}r^{s/t}.

Now consider the second case (
5
). Since
F
is Ahlfors–David regular it is doubling and, therefore, there exists a uniform constant
C_{4}
such that an arbitrary ball
B(x,r)
intersects at most
C_{4}
many
k
-level cylinders. This uses the fact that the centres of the
k
-level cylinders are
(1/q_{k})
-separated. Therefore, using (
6
) to estimate the mass of a
k
-level cylinder,

\displaystyle\mu(B(x,r))

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle C_{4}q_{1}^{ts}(q_{2}\cdots q_{k-1})^{s(t-1)}(q_{k}^{-1})^{s}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle C_{4}\left(\log(q_{k})\right)^{ts}(q_{k}^{-1})^{s}\qquad\text{by \eqref{rapid}}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle C_{4}|\log r|^{ts}r^{s/t}\qquad\text{by \eqref{case2}.}

Combining the two cases, we have proved that for an arbitrary
x\in E
and
r\in(0,q_{1}^{-t})

\mu(B(x,r))\leqslant\max\{C_{3},C_{4}\}|\log r|^{ts}r^{s/t}

and the result follows by the mass distribution principle, see
[
F14
, Chapter 4]
.
∎

3.
Dirichlet spectrum of Ahlfors–David regular spaces

Notice that in the classical setting of Diophantine approximation on

[0,1]
with
P
defined by

P(q)=\left\{0,1/q,2/q,\dots,1\right\},\qquad(q\geqslant 1),

we have
s=1
and the
upper
bound in Theorem
2.7

gives the correct dimension of
F_{t}
, namely,

\dim_{\textup{H}}F_{t}=2/t
, the dimension given in the
Jarník–Besicovitch theorem. Therefore probing sharpness of the
lower bound seems like the most pressing concern.

Notice also that in the classical setting, we have

\displaystyle 2=\sup\left\{d:F_{d}=[0,1]\right\}=\inf\left\{d:\Leb(F_{d})=0\right\}

\displaystyle=\sup\left\{d:F_{d}\textrm{ contains a ball}\right\}=\sup\left\{d:F_{d}\textrm{ essentially contains a ball}\right\}.

As a generalisation of this, define the following: for a given
s
-Ahlfors–David
regular set
F
and choice
P
of abstract rationals in
F
, define the

Dirichlet exponent

\displaystyle d(P)

\displaystyle=\sup\left\{d:F_{d}\textrm{ contains a ball up to $\mathcal{H}^{s}$-measure $0$}\right\}

\displaystyle=\sup\left\{d:(\exists\textrm{ball }B\subset F)\quad\mathcal{H}^{s}(B\setminus F_{d})=0\right\}

and the
Dirichlet spectrum

\sigma(F)=\left\{d(P):P\textrm{ is a choice of abstract rationals in }F\right\}.

We can then ask the following.

Question 3.1
.

For a given
s
-Ahlfors–David-regular
F
, what is the nature of
the set
\sigma(F)
, and to what extent does the formula

\dim_{\textup{H}}F_{t}=\frac{d(P)s}{t}\qquad(t\geqslant d(P))

hold for a given choice
P
of abstract rationals in
F
?

In exploring this question, we will find, in particular, that the
dimension bounds in Theorem
2.7
are sharp. First, let us report
what Theorem
2.7
and the Mass Transference Principle say
immediately about Question
3.1
.

Proposition 3.1
.

If
F
is a locally compact,
s
-Ahlfors–David-regular metric space, then

\sigma(F)\subset[1,1+1/s]
, and for any choice
P
of
abstract rationals in
F
we have

\dim_{\textup{H}}F_{t}\geqslant\frac{d(P)s}{t}\qquad(t\geqslant d(P)).

Clearly, since for any
P
one has
F=F_{1}
, we have
d(P)\geqslant 1
. On the other hand, suppose
\mathcal{H}^{s}(F_{d})>0
. Then

\dim_{\textup{H}}F_{d}\geqslant s
, which, by Theorem
2.7
,
implies that
s\leqslant(s+1)/d
, hence
d\leqslant 1+1/s
. Taking
supremum over all such
d
shows that
d(P)\leqslant 1+1/s
, so we
have shown
d(P)\in[1,1+1/s]
for all
P
.

Now suppose
d<d(P)\leqslant t
. It follows that
F_{d}

essentially contains a closed ball
F^{\prime}=B(x^{\prime},r)\subset F
, and we
may suppose that
\mathcal{H}^{s}(S(x^{\prime},r))=0
, where
S(x^{\prime},r)

denotes the sphere of radius
r
centered at
x^{\prime}
. (After all, at
most countably many members of the uncountable disjoint union

F^{\prime}=\bigcup_{0\leqslant\rho\leqslant r}S(x^{\prime},\rho)
may have positive
measure, so if we must, we may shrink
r
until
S(x^{\prime},r)
has zero
measure.) The ball
F^{\prime}
is itself an
s
-Ahlfors–David-regular
metric space. Define a hierarchy of points
P^{\prime}=P\mid_{F^{\prime}}
in

F^{\prime}
by

P^{\prime}(q)=\begin{cases}P(q)\cap F^{\prime}&\textrm{if }P(q)\cap F^{\prime}\neq\emptyset\\
x^{\prime}&\textrm{if }P(q)\cap F^{\prime}=\emptyset.\end{cases}

Note that as soon as
q
is large enough we have

P^{\prime}(q)=P(q)\cap F^{\prime}
, hence
P^{\prime}(q)
is
(1/q)
-separated but it may
not be maximal. That is,
P^{\prime}
may not constitute abstract rationals
in
F^{\prime}
as we have defined the term. Still, let us use
F_{t}^{\prime}
to
denote all the points in
F^{\prime}
that are
t
-approximable by

P^{\prime}
. Notice then that

(7)

(F_{d}\cap F^{\prime})\setminus F_{d}^{\prime}\subset S(x^{\prime},r),

for, if
x\in F_{d}
and
d(x^{\prime},x)<r
, then for all
q
large enough,
every
p\in P(q)
with
d(x,p)\leqslant q^{-d}
must lie in
F^{\prime}
. All
such
p
are in
P^{\prime}(q)
, so we have
x\in F_{d}^{\prime}
. Importantly for
us, (
7
) implies that

\mathcal{H}^{s}(F^{\prime}\setminus F_{d}^{\prime})=0
.

For any
t\geqslant d(P)
, we have
F_{t}^{\prime}\subset F_{t}
. Also, since

\mathcal{H}^{s}(F^{\prime}\setminus F_{d}^{\prime})=0
, we have that for any ball

B\subset F^{\prime}
,

\mathcal{H}^{s}(F_{d}^{\prime}\cap B)=\mathcal{H}^{s}(B).

So the mass transference principle
[
BV06
, Theorem 3]
implies
that for any ball
B\subset F^{\prime}
we have

\mathcal{H}^{ds/t}(F_{t}^{\prime}\cap B)=\mathcal{H}^{ds/t}(B)=\infty.

Therefore
\dim_{\textup{H}}F_{t}^{\prime}\geqslant ds/t
, hence

\dim_{\textup{H}}F_{t}\geqslant ds/t
. Since
d<d(P)
was arbitrary, the
result follows.
∎

Remark
.

We could of course ask Question
3.1
when
F
is not
Ahlfors–David regular. There, Theorem
2.1
would guarantee that

1\leqslant d(P)\leqslant\frac{\dim_{\textup{P}}F+1}{\dim_{H}F}.

However, clearly no such dimension formula as the one in
Question
3.1
holds in general, even with
s
replaced
by
\dim_{\textup{H}}F.

We can now refine Question
3.1
by asking what kind of
subset
\sigma(F)\subset[1,1+1/s]
is. The next results exhibit
cases where
\sigma(F)=[1,1+1/s]
. We will also show that for
certain spaces
F
and any
d\in\sigma(F)
, there exist abstract
rationals witnessing
d(P)=d
such that
\dim_{\textup{H}}F_{t}=ds/t

for all
t\geqslant d
. That is, the full spectrum
\sigma(F)
can be
realized by abstract rationals satisfying a general
Jarnik–Besicovitch theorem.

In particular, we show that the bounds from Theorem
2.7
are
indeed sharp and, moreover, the dimensions of
F_{t}
can depend on the
choice of abstract rationals, even in the Ahlfors–David regular
setting.

Proposition 3.2
.

For
F=[0,1]
we have
\sigma(F)=[1,2]
and for any
d\in\sigma(F)

there is a choice
P
of abstract rationals with
d(P)=d
such that

\dim_{\textup{H}}F_{t}=\frac{d(P)}{t}\qquad(t\geqslant d(P)).

The case
d(P)=2
is achieved by the classical setting of the
Jarník–Besicovitch theorem so it remains to consider

d\in[1,2)
. We first prove the case
d=1
, which provides a
roadmap to the other cases.

For each
q\in[2^{k},2^{k+1})
let

P(q)=\left\{\frac{a}{2^{k}}:a=0,\dots,2^{k}\right\}

be the rationals with denominator
2^{k}
. Note that
P(q)
is a
maximal
(1/q)
-separated set in
F
. In this setting we have

d(P)=1
, which in particular shows
1\in\sigma(F)
. This will follow from Proposition
3.1
together with the upper bound for the dimension of
F_{t}
which we prove now. The lower bound

\dim_{\textup{H}}F_{t}\geqslant\frac{1}{t}\qquad(t\geqslant 1),

already follows from Theorem
2.7
. Note that

F_{t}\subset\bigcup_{k\geqslant 1}\bigcup_{a=0}^{2^{k}}\left[\frac{a}{2^{k}}-\frac{1}{2^{tk}},\frac{a}{2^{k}}+\frac{1}{2^{tk}}\right].

For all
\tau>1/t
we have that

\sum_{k\geqslant 1}\sum_{a=0}^{2^{k}}\left\lparen\frac{1}{2^{tk-1}}\right\rparen^{\tau}\ll\sum_{k\geqslant 1}2^{(1-\tau t)k},

which converges. (The notation
\ll
means that
\leqslant
holds up to
a positive absolute constant factor.) This implies

\mathcal{H}^{\tau}(F_{t})<\infty
and
\dim_{\textup{H}}F_{t}\leqslant\tau
. Therefore

\dim_{\textup{H}}F_{t}\leqslant 1/t
.

Now suppose
d\in(1,2)
and let
\alpha>1
be such that

d=1+1/\alpha
. For
q\in\mathbb{N}
with

k^{\alpha}\leqslant q<(k+1)^{\alpha}
, let

P(q)=\left\{\frac{a}{\lfloor k^{\alpha}\rfloor}:a=0,\dots,\lfloor k^{\alpha}\rfloor\right\}.

We claim that
d(P)=d
. In fact, this is a consequence of the
recently proved Duffin–Schaeffer conjecture
[
KM
]
, which states that if

\psi(n):\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}
is such that

\sum_{q}\psi(q)\varphi(q)=\infty,

then for almost every
x\in[0,1]
there are infinitely many
p/q\in\mathbb{Q}
such that

\left\lvert x-\frac{p}{q}\right\rvert<\psi(q).

Here and throughout
\varphi
is the is the Euler totient function. Consider the function

\psi(q)=\begin{cases}q^{-d}&\textrm{if }q=\left\lfloor k^{\alpha}\right\rfloor,k\in\mathbb{N}\\
0&\textrm{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Then

\displaystyle\sum_{q}\psi(q)\varphi(q)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\frac{1}{\lfloor k^{\alpha}\rfloor^{d}}\varphi(\lfloor k^{\alpha}\rfloor)

\displaystyle\geqslant\sum_{k}\frac{\varphi(\lfloor k^{\alpha}\rfloor)}{k^{\alpha+1}}

\displaystyle\geqslant\sum_{k}\frac{\varphi(\lfloor k^{\alpha}\rfloor)}{k^{\alpha+1}}

\displaystyle\gg\sum_{k}\frac{1}{k\log\log k^{\alpha}}=\infty,

this last estimate coming from the growth estimate

\varphi(n)\gg n/\log\log n

[
HW
]
. We may then conclude that
for almost every
x\in[0,1]
there are infinitely many
q
such that

x
is within
q^{-d}
of some
p\in P(q)
. That is,
F_{d}
has full
measure, hence
d(P)\geqslant d
. On the other hand, if we had defined

\psi
by
q^{-d^{\prime}}
for some
d^{\prime}>d
, then the above sum would have
converged, and the Borel–Cantelli lemma would imply that
F_{d^{\prime}}

had measure
0
. This shows that
d(P)=d
, as claimed.

Proposition
3.1
now gives

\dim_{\textup{H}}F_{t}\geqslant\frac{d(P)}{t}

for every
t\geqslant d
. The corresponding upper bound comes from a
cover of
F_{t}
by intervals as in the previous case. Here the
relevant series is

\sum_{k\geqslant 1}\sum_{a=0}^{\lfloor k^{\alpha}\rfloor}\left\lparen k^{-\alpha t}\right\rparen^{\tau}\ll\sum_{k\geqslant 1}k^{\alpha(1-t\tau)},

which converges as long as

\tau>\frac{1+1/\alpha}{t}=\frac{d}{t},

which leads to the upper bound and establishes

(8)

\dim_{\textup{H}}F_{t}=\frac{1+1/\alpha}{t}=\frac{d(P)}{t}.

Since
d\in(1,2)
was arbitrary, we are done.
∎

Proposition 3.3

Let

In particular,

and

Since

F_{t}\subset\bigcup_{q\geqslant 3^{m}}\bigcup_{p\in P(q)}B(p,q^{-t})\subset\bigcup_{q\geqslant 3^{m}}\bigcup_{p\in P(q)}B(p,3^{-nt})

The corresponding sum of diameters, with

(iii)
The intermediate case.

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geqslant 1}\sum_{q=3^{n}}^{3^{n}+2^{k-n}}\sum_{p\in P(q)}\text{diam}\left\lparen B(p,q^{-t})\right\rparen^{\tau}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geqslant 1}\sum_{q=3^{n}}^{3^{n}+2^{k-n}}\#P(q)q^{-t\tau}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{n\geqslant 1}2^{k}3^{-nt\tau}

, therefore

\dim_{\textup{H}}F_{t}\leqslant ds/t
and we are done.

The above result holds for more general Ahlfors–David regular sets than just the middle third Cantor set. Since it is especially useful to have examples with different dimensions having minimal Dirichlet exponent, see Proof of Proposition
2.5
, we record the following partial extension.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
F
be the central Cantor set having dimension

s=-\log 2/\log\lambda
, where
\lambda\in(0,1/2)
. Then there is
a choice
P
of abstract rationals such that

\dim_{\textup{H}}F_{t}=s/t
for all
t\geqslant 1
.

The proof of this statement is essentially the same as the proof of Proposition
3.3
(i). Using the analogous notation, for
\lambda^{-n}\leqslant q<\lambda^{-n-1}
let

P(q)=E_{n}^{L}\cup\left\{\textrm{enough endpoints of $E_{n}$ to get maximality}\right\}.

Notice that
2^{n-1}\leqslant P(q)\leqslant 2^{n}
. Notice that
P(\lambda^{-n})=E_{n}^{L}
and every point of
F
is in the
\lambda^{n}
-neigbourhood of every
P(\lambda^{-n})
, so that
d(F)\geq 1
.

What is more, for any
m
,

F_{t}\subset\bigcup_{n\geq m}\bigcup_{p\in P(\lambda^{-n})}[p-\lambda^{-nt},p+\lambda^{-nt}].

This implies that

\mathcal{H}^{\tau}(F_{t})\leq\sum_{m\geq n}2^{n}\lambda^{-nt\tau},

which converges if
\tau>s/t
. This implies two things: for
t>1
, we immediately obtain
\dim_{\textup{H}}F_{t}<\dim_{\textup{H}}F
so that
d(F)=1
. Further, we also see that
\dim_{\textup{H}}F_{t}\leq s/t
. Since Theorem
2.7
tells us that

\dim_{\textup{H}}F_{t}\geqslant s/t
, we have finished the proof of the theorem.
