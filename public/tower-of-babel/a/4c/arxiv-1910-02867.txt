Characterization of the equality of weak efficiency and efficiency on convex free disposal hulls
Naoki Hamada, Shunsuke Ichiki

Abstract
. In solving a multi-objective optimization problem by scalarization techniques, solutions to a scalarized problem are, in general, weakly efficient rather than efficient to the original problem. Thus, it is crucial to understand what problem ensures that all weakly efficient solutions are efficient. In this paper, we give a characterization of the equality of the weakly efficient set and the efficient set, provided that the free disposal hull of the domain is convex. By using this characterization, we obtain various mathematical applications. As a practical application, we show that all weakly efficient solutions to a multi-objective LASSO with mild modification are efficient.



1.
Introduction

The aim of multi-objective optimization is to find efficient solutions to a given problem.
In order to do so, various scalarization techniques have been developed so far (see for example
[
17
,
4
,
15
,
14
,
26
,
6
,
21
]
).
Nevertheless, there is no scalarization method that ensures for a wide variety of problems that all solutions optimal to scalarized problems are efficient to the original problem.
In general, scalarization methods only ensure that their solutions are
weakly
efficient to the original problem, which means users may waste computation resources for finding inefficient, undesirable solutions.
Thus, it is crucial to understand conditions that the weak efficiency coincides with the efficiency.

In the literature, the relationship between the weak efficiency and the efficiency has been investigated.
In some cases, the set of weakly efficient solutions to a given problem can be described as the union of the sets of efficient solutions to its subproblems
[
10
,
24
,
12
,
2
]
.
This property was named the
Pareto reducibility

[
18
]
and further investigated
[
19
,
20
,
9
]
.
Some relationships of the weak efficiency and the efficiency on quasi-convex problems are collected in
[
11
,
5
]
.
However, the equality between the weak efficiency and the efficiency, both of which are of the
original
problem (rather than subproblems), is still unclear.

In this paper, we give a characterization of the equality of the set of weakly efficient solutions and the set of efficient solutions, provided that the free disposal hull
[
1
]
of the image of an objective mapping is convex
(see
proposition

2.3
in
section

2
).
This claim is derived from our main theorem (
Theorem

2.1
in
section

2
), which gives a similar characterization of the equality of the weakly efficient set and the efficient set on a partially ordered Euclidean space without objective functions.
Furthermore,
proposition

2.3
yields various mathematical applications (see
corollaries

6.1
,
6.2
,
6.3
and
6.4
in
section

6
).
Moreover, as a practical application of
proposition

2.3
, we show that all weakly efficient solutions to a multi-objective LASSO with mild modification are efficient.

This paper is organized as follows.
First, in

2.
Preliminaries and the statements of the main results

Unless otherwise stated, it is not necessary to assume that mappings are continuous.
Throughout this paper, we set

\displaystyle M=\set{1,\ldots,m},

where
m
is a positive integer.
We denote a nonempty subset of
M
by
I
.
Let

(resp.,

) for all

The inequality

y_{i}\leq y_{i}^{\prime}
for all

and there exists

y_{j}<y^{\prime}_{j}
.

Let

be a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
Let
\M_{I}Y
(resp.,
\WM_{I}Y
) be the set consisting of all elements

such that there does not exist any element
y\in Y
satisfying
y\lneq_{I}y^{\prime}
(resp.,
y<_{I}y^{\prime}
).
For simplicity, set
\M Y=\M_{M}Y
and
\WM Y=\WM_{M}Y
.
Then, the set
\M Y
(resp.,
\WM Y
) is called the
efficient set
(resp., the
weakly efficient set
) of
Y
.

For a subset
Z
of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, the set
Z+\mathbb{R}^{m}_{\geq 0}
is called the
free disposal hull
of
Z
(denoted by
\FDH Z
), where

\mathbb{R}^{m}_{\geq 0}=\Set{(y_{1},\ldots,y_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}{y_{1}\geq 0,\ldots,y_{m}\geq 0}.

For details on free disposal hulls, see
[
1
]
.
A subset
Z
of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
is said to be

if
tx+(1-t)y\in Z
for all
x,y\in Z
and all
t\in[0,1]
.

The main theorem of this paper is the following.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
Y
be a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
If the free disposal hull of
Y
is convex, then the following
(\alpha)
and
(\beta)
are equivalent:

(\alpha)

\WM Y=\M Y
.

(\beta)

\displaystyle\bigcup_{\emptyset\neq I\subseteq M}\M_{I}Y\subseteq\M Y
.

Remark 2.2
.

As in the proof of
theorem

2.1
, the hypothesis that the free disposal hull of

is convex is used only in the proof of
(\beta)\Rightarrow(\alpha)
(see
section

4.2
).
In the proof of
(\alpha)\Rightarrow(\beta)
of
theorem

2.1
, it is not necessary to assume that the free disposal hull of
Y
is convex (see
section

4.1
).

Now, in order to state
proposition

2.3
, we will prepare some definitions.
Let
f=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}):X\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a mapping, and
I=\Set{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}}

(i_{1}<\cdots<i_{k})
be a nonempty subset of
M
, where
X
is a given set and

is the number of the elements of
I
.
Let

be the mapping defined by
f_{I}=(f_{i_{1}},\ldots,f_{i_{k}})
.
A point

is called an

(resp., a

) to the following multi-objective optimization problem:

\displaystyle\minimize_{x\in X}f_{I}(x)=(f_{i_{1}}(x),\ldots,f_{i_{k}}(x)),

if

(resp.,

By

(resp.,

Namely,

\displaystyle\E(f_{I},X)

\displaystyle=f^{-1}(\M_{I}f(X)),

\displaystyle\WE(f_{I},X)

\displaystyle=f^{-1}(\WM_{I}f(X)).

It is well known that a solution to a weighting problem is a weekly efficient solution (for example, see
[
15

).
On the other hand, a solution to a weighting problem is not necessarily an efficient solution.
For a given mapping

, if
\WE(f,X)=\E(f,X)
, then a solution to weighting problem is always an efficient solution.
Therefore, characterizations of
\WE(f,X)=\E(f,X)
are useful and significant.

As an application of
theorem

2.1
to multi-objective optimization problems, we have the following, which can be easily shown by setting
Y=f(X)
in
theorem

2.1
.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
f=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}):X\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a mapping, where
X
is a given set.
If the free disposal hull of
f(X)
is convex, then the following
(\alpha)
and
(\beta)
are equivalent:

(\alpha)

\WE(f,X)=\E(f,X)
.

(\beta)

{\displaystyle\bigcup_{\emptyset\neq I\subseteq M}\E(f_{I},X)\subseteq\E(f,X)}
.

Proof of

In the case

Hence, in what follows, we will consider the case

4.1.
Proof of
(\alpha)\Rightarrow(\beta)

Let
I
be a nonempty subset of
M
.
Then, it is clearly seen that

\M_{I}Y\subseteq\WM_{I}Y\subseteq\WM Y.

By
(\alpha)
, we get

Thus, we have

\Box

4.2.
Proof of
(\beta)\Rightarrow(\alpha)

It is sufficient to show that

.
Let
y^{*}=(y^{*}_{1},\ldots,y^{*}_{m})\in\WM Y
be an arbitrary element.
Set

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=\Set{(y_{1}-y^{*}_{1},\ldots,y_{m}-y^{*}_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}{(y_{1},\ldots,y_{m})\in\FDH Y},

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=\Set{y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}{y<_{M}0}.

Here, note that
0=(0,\ldots,0)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
in the above description of

.
Then, we will have

by contradiction.
Suppose that
A\cap B\neq\emptyset
.
Then, there exist
y^{\prime}\in Y
and

satisfying

.
Since

, we get

This contradicts

Hence, we have

.

In the following lemma,

Lemma 4.1

(Separation theorem
[

]
)
.

Let
D_{1}
and

be nonempty convex subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
satisfying

.
Then, there exist
a=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}

(a\neq 0)

(1)

For any

, we have
\left\langle a,y\right\rangle\geq b
.

(2)

For any

, we have
\left\langle a,y\right\rangle\leq b
.

Note that
A
and
B
are nonempty convex subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
Hence, by

4.1
, there exist
a=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}

(a\neq 0)
and

such that the following both assertions hold.

(1’)

For any
y\in A
, we have
\left\langle a,y\right\rangle\geq b
.

(2’)

For any
y\in B
, we have
\left\langle a,y\right\rangle\leq b
.

Then, we will show that

.
Since
0=(0,\ldots,0)\in A

Namely, we get

Since

We will show that

Suppose that there exists an element

Let

\dfrac{\left(\sum_{j=1,j\neq i^{\prime}}^{m}\left|a_{j}\right|\right)+1}{a_{i^{\prime}}}&\text{if $i=i^{\prime}$}.\end{cases}

This contradicts (2’).
Hence, it follows that

Now, set

Notice that

Set

Let

Since

5.
Preliminaries for applications of
proposition

2.3

Let
X
be a convex subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
A function
f:X\to\mathbb{R}
is said to be
convex
if

f(tx+(1-t)y)\leq tf(x)+(1-t)f(y)

for all
x,y\in X
and all
t\in[0,1]
.
A function
f:X\to\mathbb{R}
is said to be
strongly convex
if there exists
\alpha>0
satisfying

f(tx+(1-t)y)\leq tf(x)+(1-t)f(y)-\frac{1}{2}\alpha t(1-t)\left\|x-y\right\|^{2}.

for all
x,y\in X
and all
t\in[0,1]
, where
\left\|x-y\right\|
denotes the Euclidean norm of
x-y
.
For details on convex functions and strongly convex functions, see
[
16
]
.
A mapping
f=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}):X\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
is said to be
convex
(resp.,
strongly convex
) if every
f_{i}
is convex (resp., strongly convex).

First, we give the following well-known result.
For the sake of the readers’ convenience, we also give the proof.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
X
be a convex subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
f:X\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a convex mapping.
Then, the free disposal hull of
f(X)
is convex.

Proof of
lemma

5.1
.

Let
y=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{m})
,
\widetilde{y}=(\widetilde{y}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{y}_{m})\in\FDH f(X)
be arbitrary points and
t\in[0,1]
be an arbitrary element.
Then, there exist
x\in X
(resp.,
\widetilde{x}\in X
) and
z=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{\geq 0}
(resp.,
\widetilde{z}=(\widetilde{z}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{z}_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{\geq 0})
such that
y=f(x)+z
(resp.,
\widetilde{y}=f(\widetilde{x})+\widetilde{z}
).
Let
i
be an arbitrary integer satisfying
1\leq i\leq m
.
Since
f_{i}
is convex, we have

\displaystyle f_{i}(tx+(1-t)\widetilde{x})\leq tf_{i}(x)+(1-t)f_{i}(\widetilde{x}),

where
f=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{m})
.
Since
z_{i}\geq 0
and
\widetilde{z}_{i}\geq 0
for any
i=1,\ldots,m
, we also get

\displaystyle tf_{i}(x)+(1-t)f_{i}(\widetilde{x})\leq t(f_{i}(x)+z_{i})+(1-t)(f_{i}(\widetilde{x})+\widetilde{z}_{i})=ty_{i}+(1-t)\widetilde{y}_{i}.

Hence, we obtain

(5.1)

\displaystyle f_{i}(tx+(1-t)\widetilde{x})\leq ty_{i}+(1-t)\widetilde{y}_{i}.

Since we have
eq.

5.1
for any integer
i
satisfying
1\leq i\leq m
, it follows that
ty+(1-t)\widetilde{y}\in\FDH f(X)
.
∎

In the following, for two sets
U,V
, and a subset

, the restriction of a given mapping

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
f:X\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a mapping, where
X
is a given set.
Let
I=\Set{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}}

(i_{1}<\cdots<i_{k})
be a nonempty subset of

, where

is the number of the elements of

.
If
f_{I}|_{\E(f_{I},X)}:\E(f_{I},X)\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
is injective, then we have
\E(f_{I},X)\subseteq\E(f,X)
.

Proof of
lemma

5.2
.

Suppose that there exists an element
x\in\E(f_{I},X)
such that
x\not\in\E(f,X)
.
Then, there exists an element
y\in X
(
y\neq x
) satisfying
f_{i}(y)\leq f_{i}(x)
for any
i\in M
.
Since
I\subseteq M
, it follows that
f_{i}(y)\leq f_{i}(x)
for any

.
Since

, we get
f_{I}(x)=f_{I}(y)
.
Therefore, we have

.
This contradicts the assumption that
f_{I}|_{\E(f_{I},X)}:\E(f_{I},X)\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
is injective.
∎

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
X
be a convex subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
f:X\to\mathbb{R}^{m}

Proof of

.

Suppose that
f|_{\E(f,X)}:\E(f,X)\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
is not injective.
Then, there exist
x,y\in\E(f,X)

Let

Since

\alpha_{i}>0
satisfying

f_{i}(tx+(1-t)y)\leq tf_{i}(x)+(1-t)f_{i}(y)-\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{i}t(1-t)\left\|x-y\right\|^{2}

for the points
x,y\in\E(f,X)
and all
t\in[0,1]
.
Set
t=\frac{1}{2}
.
Then, we get

f_{i}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)\leq\frac{f_{i}(x)+f_{i}(y)}{2}-\frac{\alpha_{i}}{8}\left\|x-y\right\|^{2}.

Since
f|_{\E(f,X)}(x)=f|_{\E(f,X)}(y)
, we have

f_{i}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)\leq f_{i}(x)-\frac{\alpha_{i}}{8}\left\|x-y\right\|^{2}.

Since
x\neq y
and
\alpha_{i}>0
, it follows that

f_{i}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)<f_{i}(x).

This contradicts
x\in\E(f,X)
.
∎

6.
Mathematical applications of
proposition

2.3

In this section, as mathematical applications of
proposition

2.3
, we give
corollaries

6.1
,
6.2
,
6.3
and
6.4
.

First,
proposition

2.3
gives a characterization of the equality of the weak efficiency and the efficiency for possibly nonconvex problems having a convex image
f(X)
as follows:

Corollary 6.1
.

Let
f=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}):X\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a mapping, where
X
is a given set.
If
f(X)
is convex, then the following
(\alpha)
and
(\beta)
are equivalent:

(\alpha)

\WE(f,X)=\E(f,X)
.

(\beta)

{\displaystyle\bigcup_{\emptyset\neq I\subseteq M}\E(f_{I},X)\subseteq\E(f,X)}
.

Proof of
corollary

6.1
.

Since
f(X)
is convex, it is clearly seen that the free disposal hull of
f(X)
is also convex.
Thus, by
proposition

2.3
, we have
corollary

6.1
.
∎

Unfortunately, it is not easy to check the convexity of the image of a given mapping which is possibly nonconvex.
A more workable condition ensuring this characterization is the convexity of a given mapping itself.

Corollary 6.2
.

Let
X
be a convex subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
f=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}):X\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a convex mapping.
Then, the following
(\alpha)
and
(\beta)
are equivalent:

(\alpha)

\WE(f,X)=\E(f,X)
.

(\beta)

{\displaystyle\bigcup_{\emptyset\neq I\subseteq M}\E(f_{I},X)\subseteq\E(f,X)}
.

Proof of
corollary

6.2
.

Since
f
is convex, by
lemma

5.1
, the free disposal full of
f(X)
is convex.
Therefore, by
proposition

2.3
, we get
corollary

6.2
.
∎

As a direct consequence, the characterization is valid for convex programming problems.

Corollary 6.3
.

Let
X
be a convex subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
f=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}):X\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a convex mapping.
Let
g_{1},\ldots,g_{\ell}
be convex functions of
X
into
\mathbb{R}
, where
\ell
is a positive integer.
Set

\Omega=\Set{x\in X}{g_{1}(x)\leq 0,\ldots,g_{\ell}(x)\leq 0}.

Then, the following
(\alpha)
and
(\beta)
are equivalent:

(\alpha)

\WE(f|_{\Omega},\Omega)=\E(f|_{\Omega},\Omega)
.

(\beta)

{\displaystyle\bigcup_{\emptyset\neq I\subseteq M}\E((f|_{\Omega})_{I},\Omega)\subseteq\E(f|_{\Omega},\Omega)}
.

Proof of
corollary

6.3
.

Since
g_{1},\ldots,g_{\ell}
are convex functions, it is clearly seen that
\Omega
is convex.
Since the mapping
f|_{\Omega}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
is convex, by
corollary

6.2
, we get
corollary

6.3
.
∎

Let
f=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}):X\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a mapping, where
X
is a set.
Then,
x^{*}\in X
is called a
strictly efficient solution
if there does not exist
x\in X

(x\neq x^{*})
such that
f_{i}(x)\leq f_{i}(x^{*})
for all
i=1,\ldots,m
.
We denote the set of all strictly efficient solutions to the problem minimizing
f
by
\SE(f,X)
.

Corollary 6.4
.

Let
X
be a convex subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
f:X\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a strongly convex mapping.
Then, we have

\displaystyle\WE(f,X)=\E(f,X)=\SE(f,X).

Proof of
corollary

6.4
.

Since
f|_{\E(f,X)}
is injective by
lemma

5.3
, it is not hard to see that
\E(f,X)=\SE(f,X)
.

Now, we will show that
\WE(f,X)=\E(f,X)
.
Since
f
is strongly convex, by
lemma

5.1
, the set
\FDH f(X)
is convex.
Thus, by
proposition

2.3
, in order to show that
\WE(f,X)=\E(f,X)
, it is sufficient to show

(6.1)

\bigcup_{\emptyset\neq I\subseteq M}\E(f_{I},X)\subseteq\E(f,X).

Let
I
be any nonempty subset of
M
.
Since
f_{I}|_{\E(f_{I},X)}:\E(f_{I},X)\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
is strongly convex, by
lemma

5.3
, the mapping
f_{I}|_{\E(f_{I},X)}
is injective, where
k
is the number of the elements of
I
.
By
lemma

5.2
, we have
\E(f_{I},X)\subseteq\E(f,X)
.
Thus, we obtain
eq.

6.1
.
∎

7.
A practical application

In this section, as a practical application of
proposition

2.3
, we show that all weakly efficient solutions to a multi-objective LASSO with mild modification are efficient.
The LASSO is a sparse modeling method that is originally proposed as a single-objective optimization problem
[
22
]
and sometimes treated as a multi-objective one
[
3
]
.
Let us consider a linear regression model:

\displaystyle y=\theta_{1}x_{1}+\theta_{2}x_{2}+\dots+\theta_{n}x_{n}+\xi,

where
x_{i}
and
\theta_{i}

(i=1,\ldots,n)
are a predictor and its coefficient,
y
is a response to be predicted, and
\xi
is a Gaussian noise.
Given a matrix
X
with
m
rows of observations and
n
columns of predictors and a row vector
y
of
m
responses, the (original) LASSO regressor is the solution to the following problem:

(7.1)

\minimize_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}g_{\lambda}(\theta):=\frac{1}{2m}\left\|X\theta-y\right\|^{2}+\lambda\left|\theta\right|,

where
\left|\;\cdot\;\right|
is the
\ell_{1}
-norm and
\lambda
is a user-specified positive number to force the solution to be sparse (i.e., the optimal
\theta
contains many zeros).
Note that with
\lambda=0
, the problem
eq.

7.1
reduces to the ordinary least squares (OLS) regression.
Choosing an appropriate value for
\lambda
requires repeated solution of
eq.

7.1
with varying
\lambda
, which is the most time-consuming part of this method.

To find a good solution without such a costful hyper-parameter search, the problem
eq.

7.1
is sometimes reformulated as a multi-objective one whose efficient solutions are optimal solutions to the original problem
eq.

7.1
with different
\lambda
’s (for example, see
[
3
]
).
We treat the OLS term and the regularization term as individual objective functions:

f_{1}(\theta)=\frac{1}{2m}\left\|X\theta-y\right\|^{2},\quad f_{2}(\theta)=\left|\theta\right|.

In the multi-objective problem of minimizing
f=(f_{1},f_{2})
, the equality
\WE(f,\mathbb{R}^{n})=\E(f,\mathbb{R}^{n})
does not necessarily hold (for example, if
X
is a zero matrix, then
\WE(f,\mathbb{R}^{n})=\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\E(f,\mathbb{R}^{n})=\set{(0,\ldots,0)}
).

To avoid such a corner case, we consider a modified version of multi-objective LASSO:

(7.2)

\begin{split}\minimize_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\ &\widetilde{f}(\theta):=(\widetilde{f}_{1}(\theta),\widetilde{f}_{2}(\theta))\\
\text{where }&\widetilde{f}_{i}(\theta)=f_{i}(\theta)+\varepsilon f_{2}(\theta)\quad(i=1,2).\end{split}

In
eq.

7.2
, we assume that
\varepsilon
is a positive real number.
Note that
\widetilde{f}
in
eq.

7.2
is a non-differentiable mapping that is convex but never strongly convex.
As a practical application of
proposition

2.3
, we can show the following.

Theorem 7.1
.

In
eq.

7.2
, we have

\E(\widetilde{f},\mathbb{R}^{n})=\WE(\widetilde{f},\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Proof of
theorem

7.1
.

Since
\widetilde{f}
is convex, it is sufficient to show that
\E(\widetilde{f_{i}},\mathbb{R}^{n})\subseteq\E(\widetilde{f},\mathbb{R}^{n})
for
i=1,2
by
corollary

6.2
, which is one of the applications of
proposition

2.3
.
Since
\widetilde{f_{2}}
has the unique minimizer
(0,\ldots,0)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have
\E(\widetilde{f_{2}},\mathbb{R}^{n})\subseteq\E(\widetilde{f},\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
In order to show
\E(\widetilde{f_{1}},\mathbb{R}^{n})\subseteq\E(\widetilde{f},\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we prepare the following.

Lemma 7.2
.

Let
\theta^{\ast},\widetilde{\theta}^{\ast}
be elements of
\E(\widetilde{f_{1}},\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
Then, we have

\displaystyle\left\|X\theta^{\ast}-y\right\|^{2}=\|X\widetilde{\theta}^{\ast}-y\|^{2}\mbox{ and }\left|\theta^{\ast}\right|=|\widetilde{\theta}^{\ast}|.

Proof of
lemma

7.2
.

It is sufficient to consider the case
\theta^{\ast}\not=\widetilde{\theta}^{\ast}
.
Since
\widetilde{f_{1}}
is convex, there exists a mapping
c=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}):[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
given by

\displaystyle c(t)=(a_{1}t+b_{1},\ldots,a_{n}t+b_{n}),\ \ c(0)=\theta^{*},\ \ c(1)=\widetilde{\theta}^{*}

such that
c(t)
is the minimizer of
\widetilde{f}_{1}
for all
t\in[0,1]
, where
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n},b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
and
a_{i}\not=0
for at least one index
i
.
Set

\displaystyle N

\displaystyle=\set{1,\ldots,n},

\displaystyle\widetilde{N}

\displaystyle=\set{i\in N}{\mbox{$a_{i}=0$ and $b_{i}=0$}}.

Since
\widetilde{N}\subsetneq N
, we set
N\setminus\widetilde{N}=\set{i_{1},\ldots,i_{\rho}}
.
Since
c
is continuous, it is not hard to see that there exists an open interval
I

(\subseteq[0,1])
such that for any
k=1,\ldots,\rho
, either one of the following two holds:

(1)

We have
c_{i_{k}}(t)>0
for all
t\in I
.

(2)

We have
c_{i_{k}}(t)<0
for all
t\in I
.

Set
X=(x_{ij})_{1\leq i\leq m,1\leq j\leq n}
and
y=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{m})
.
Then, the composition
\widetilde{f}_{1}\circ c:I\to\mathbb{R}
is expressed by

\displaystyle(\widetilde{f}_{1}\circ c)(t)=\frac{1}{2m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\rho}x_{j,i_{k}}(a_{i_{k}}t+b_{i_{k}})-y_{j}\right)^{2}+\varepsilon\sum_{k=1}^{\rho}q_{i_{k}}(a_{i_{k}}t+b_{i_{k}}),

where

\displaystyle q_{i_{k}}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1&\mbox{ if $c_{i_{k}}(t)>0$ for all $t\in I$},\\
-1&\mbox{ if $c_{i_{k}}(t)<0$ for all $t\in I$}.\end{cases}

By calculation, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}(\widetilde{f}_{1}\circ c)}{dt^{2}}(t)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\rho}x_{j,i_{k}}a_{i_{k}}\right)^{2},

where
t\in I
.
Since
\widetilde{f}_{1}\circ c:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
is a constant function, we have

(7.3)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\rho}x_{j,i_{k}}a_{i_{k}}=0

for all
j=1,\ldots,m
.

For simplicity, set
h(\theta)=\left\|X\theta-y\right\|^{2}
.
Then, we have

\displaystyle(h\circ c)(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\rho}x_{j,i_{k}}\left(a_{i_{k}}t+b_{i_{k}}\right)-y_{j}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\rho}x_{j,i_{k}}a_{i_{k}}\right)t+\sum_{k=1}^{\rho}x_{j,i_{k}}b_{i_{k}}-y_{j}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\rho}x_{j,i_{k}}b_{i_{k}}-y_{j}\right)^{2}

for all
t\in[0,1]
.
The last equality above is obtained by
eq.

7.3
.
Hence, since
h\circ c:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
is a constant function, we obtain

\displaystyle h(\theta^{\ast})=(h\circ c)(0)=(h\circ c)(1)=h(\widetilde{\theta}^{\ast}).

Since
\widetilde{f}_{1}(\theta^{\ast})=\widetilde{f}_{1}(\widetilde{\theta}^{\ast})
, we also have
|\theta^{\ast}|=|\widetilde{\theta}^{\ast}|
.
∎

Now, let
\theta\in\E(\widetilde{f}_{1},\mathbb{R}^{n})
be an arbitrary element.
Suppose that
\theta\not\in\E(\widetilde{f},\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
Then, there exists
\widetilde{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\widetilde{f}_{1}(\widetilde{\theta})=\widetilde{f}_{1}(\theta)
and
\widetilde{f}_{2}(\widetilde{\theta})<\widetilde{f}_{2}(\theta)
.
Thus, we have
\theta,\widetilde{\theta}\in\E(\widetilde{f}_{1},\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
|\widetilde{\theta}|<|\theta|
.
This contradicts
lemma

7.2
.
Hence, we obtain
\theta\in\E(\widetilde{f},\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
∎

8.
Conclusion

In this paper, we have given a characterization of the equality of weak efficiency and efficiency when the free disposal hull of the domain is convex.
By this fact, we have presented four classes of optimization problems where this characterization holds, including convex problems.
As a practical application, we have also shown that all the weakly efficient solutions to a multi-objective LASSO defined by
eq.

7.2
are efficient.
We expect that the scope of the multi-objective reformulation discussed in
section

7
is not limited to the LASSO.
The same idea may be applied to a wide range of sparse modeling methods, including the group lasso
[
25
]
, the fused lasso
[
23
]
, the graphical lasso
[
7
]
, the smooth lasso
[
8
]
, the elastic net
[
27
]
, etc.
