Invariance of Gaussian RKHSs under Koopman operators of stochastic differential equations with constant matrix coefficients
Friedrich Philipp, Manuel Schaller, Karl Worthmann, Sebastian Peitz, Feliks Nüske

Abstract
. We consider the Koopman operator semigroup ( K t ) t ≥ 0 (K^{t})_{t\geq 0} associated with stochastic differential equations of the form d ​ X t = A ​ X t ​ d ​ t + B ​ d ​ W t dX_{t}=AX_{t}\,dt+B\,dW_{t} with constant matrices A A and B B and Brownian motion W t W_{t} . We prove that the reproducing kernel Hilbert space ℍ C \mathbb{H}_{C} generated by a Gaussian kernel with a positive definite covariance matrix C C is invariant under each Koopman operator K t K^{t} if the matrices A A , B B , and C C satisfy the following Lyapunov-like matrix inequality: A ​ C 2 + C 2 ​ A ⊤ ≤ 2 ​ B ​ B ⊤ AC^{2}+C^{2}A^{\top}\leq 2BB^{\top} . In this course, we prove a characterization concerning the inclusion ℍ C 1 ⊂ ℍ C 2 \mathbb{H}_{C_{1}}\subset\mathbb{H}_{C_{2}} of Gaussian RKHSs for two positive definite matrices C 1 C_{1} and C 2 C_{2} . The question of whether the sufficient Lyapunov-condition is also necessary is left as an open problem.



1. Introduction

The Koopman operator
[
6
]
of a (stochastic) dynamical system is a linear operator which is defined on a linear function space of so-called
observables
. For deterministic systems, it is simply defined as a composition operator with the flow
F
of the system, i.e.,
(K^{t}f)(x)=f(F(x,t))
. For stochastic systems,
(K^{t}f)(x)
is defined as the conditional expectation of
f(F(\cdot,t))
, given that the trajectory starts at
x
. Since the (generally unknown) Koopman operator is linear and provides complete information about the (expected) process behavior, it is very appealing to approximate this operator using sample data in the fashion of modern machine learning, see e.g. the monograph
[
9
]
and the references therein.

One of the most popular methods for Koopman operator learning is certainly Extended Dynamic Mode Decomposition (EDMD)
[
20
]
, a data-driven approach propagating a finite number of pre-defined observable functions along the flow, which results in a well-interpretable surrogate model for analysis, prediction, and control. EDMD has been successfully applied in a number of highly relevant applications such as molecular dynamics
[
16
]
,
turbulent flows
[
3
]
,
neuroscience
[
2
]
, or climate prediction
[
1
]
, just to name a few. Rigorous error analyses for EDMD have been conducted in
[
10
,
22
,
11
,
13
]
.

Since kernel methods play an important role in approximation theory and machine learning, it is not surprising that there exist kernel-based approaches for Koopman operator learning, one of them being a variant of EDMD, called kernel EDMD (kEDMD)
[
21
,
4
]
. Kernel-based methods have been rigorously analyzed in, e.g.,
[
15
,
14
,
8
,
7
]
and in
[
5
]
for deterministic systems, where uniform error bounds have been provided. As it turns out in the analysis, for providing error bounds on Koopman approximations in the kernel-based setting it is highly beneficial if the reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) generated by the kernel at hand is invariant under the Koopman operator. Therefore, an important task in kernel-based Koopman operator learning is to figure out which kernels and which systems are a good match in the sense that the generated RKHS is invariant under the Koopman operator of the system.

In this article, we consider the invariance of Gaussian reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces under the Koopman operator of dynamical systems driven by a stochastic differential equation (SDE) of the form
dX_{t}=AX_{t}\,dt+BdW_{t}
with constant coefficient matrices
A
and
B
, where
(A,B)
is controllable and
A
is Hurwitz. To be more precise, we prove that the RKHS
\mathbb{H}_{C}
generated by the Gaussian kernel
k(x,y)=\exp(-\|C^{-1}(x-y)\|^{2})
with a positive definite matrix
C
is Koopman-invariant if the matrices
A
,
B
, and
C
satisfy the Lyapunov-like inequality
AC^{2}+C^{2}A^{\top}\leq 2BB^{\top}
, see Theorem
2.1
. It is left open whether this sufficient condition is also necessary. In the course of proving Theorem
2.1
, we also prove that
\mathbb{H}_{C_{1}}\subset\mathbb{H}_{C_{2}}
holds for two positive definite matrices
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
if and only if
C_{1}^{2}\geq C_{2}^{2}
, see Proposition
3.3
. This fact is well known
[
18
]
for the scalar case, where
C_{1},C_{2}\in(0,\infty)
, but—to the best of the authors’ knowledge—seems to be unknown in the general matrix case.

The paper is arranged as follows. In the next section, we briefly introduce the reader to the Koopman operator of SDEs and present the main result, Theorem
2.1
. In Section
3
, we consider Gaussian kernels and their corresponding RKHSs and make use of the auxiliary results therein in Section
4
which is dedicated to the proof of Theorem
2.1
.

2. Setting and main result

Recall that a pair of matrices
(A,B)
with
A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and
B\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times m}
,
m\leq d
, is called
controllable
if
(B,AB,\ldots,A^{d-1}B)
has full rank
d
. The notion is due to the fact that a controlled system
\dot{x}(t)=Ax(t)+Bu(t)
can be steered from any state
x(0)=x_{0}
in any time
t>0
to any state
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
by a control function
u
if and only if
(A,B)
is controllable. Also recall that a square matrix
A
is called
Hurwitz
if the real parts of the eigenvalues of
A
are all negative. This implies that
\|e^{At}\|\leq Me^{-\omega t}
for all
t\geq 0
with some constants
M,\omega>0
.

Let us consider a
d
-dimensional stochastic differential equation with constant matrix coefficients

\displaystyle dX_{t}=AX_{t}\,dt+B\,dW_{t},

(2.1)

where
W_{t}
is
m
-dimensional Brownian motion,
m\leq d
, and
A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
,
B\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times m}
. Here, we assume that
(A,B)
is controllable and that
A
is Hurwitz. The solution process
X_{t}
emerging from the SDE (
2.1
) is also called a
d
-dimensional
Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process
. It has the unique stationary distribution

d\mu(x)=(2\pi)^{-d/2}(\det\Sigma)^{-1/2}\exp(-\tfrac{1}{2}x^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}x)\,dx,

where
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is the positive definite matrix

\Sigma=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{As}BB^{\top}e^{A^{\top}s}\,ds.

Note that the limit
\Sigma
exists since
A
is Hurwitz and that
\Sigma
is positive definite thanks to controllability of
(A,B)
. In linear control theory, the matrix
\Sigma
is called the
controllability Gramian
and is a solution of the matrix equation
AX+XA^{\top}=-BB^{\top}
.

The solution process
X_{t}
is a time-homogeneous Markov process. Recall that every such process has a so-called Markov transition kernel
\rho_{t}(x,F)=\mathbb{P}(X_{t}\in F\,|\,X_{0}=x)
, where
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
F
is a Borel set. Here, the transition kernel is absolutely continuous w.r.t. Lebesgue measure, and its density is given by (see
[
19
]
)

\displaystyle\rho_{t}(x,dy)

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-d/2}(\det\Sigma(t))^{-1/2}\exp\big(-\tfrac{1}{2}(y-e^{At}x)^{\top}\Sigma(t)^{-1}(y-e^{At}x)\big)\,dy,

where

\Sigma(t)=\int_{0}^{t}e^{As}BB^{\top}e^{A^{\top}s}\,ds.

Again,
\Sigma(t)
is positive definite as
(A,B)
is controllable.

The
Koopman operator

K^{t}:L^{2}(\mu)\to L^{2}(\mu)
at time
t\geq 0
corresponding to the SDE (
2.1
) is defined by

\displaystyle(K^{t}f)(x):=\mathbb{E}[f(X_{t})\,|\,X_{0}=x]=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(y)\,\rho_{t}(x,dy),\qquad f\in L^{2}(\mu).

(2.2)

It is well known (see, e.g.,
[
15
, Proposition 2.8]
) that
(K^{t})_{t\geq 0}
forms a strongly continuous semigroup of contractions on
L^{2}(\mu)
.

Here, we consider Gaussian kernels on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with a positive definite covariance matrix
C\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, i.e.,

k^{C}(x,y)=\exp\big[-(x-y)^{\top}C^{-2}(x-y)\big]=\exp\big[-\|C^{-1}(x-y)\|^{2}\big].

Note that for
C=\sigma\cdot I_{d}
,
\sigma>0
, we obtain the usual RBF kernel
k^{\sigma}(x,y)=\exp(-\|x-y\|^{2}/\sigma^{2})
. By
\mathbb{H}_{C}
we denote the RKHS generated by the kernel
k^{C}
. The Hilbert space norm on
\mathbb{H}_{C}
will be denoted by
\|\cdot\|_{C}
. For
\sigma>0
we simply write
\mathbb{H}_{\sigma}
and
\|\cdot\|_{\sigma}
instead of
\mathbb{H}_{\sigma I}
and
\|\cdot\|_{\sigma I}
, respectively.

The main result of this note reads as follows.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
C\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be a symmetric positive definite matrix such that

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}\big(AC^{2}+C^{2}A^{\top}\big)\,\leq\,BB^{\top}.

(2.3)

Then the RKHS
\mathbb{H}_{C}
is invariant under each Koopman operator
K^{t}
,
t\geq 0
, associated with the SDE (
2.1
), and we have

\|K^{t}f\|_{C}\leq e^{t\cdot\operatorname{Tr}(-A)/2}\cdot\|f\|_{C},\qquad f\in\mathbb{H}_{C}.

Let us briefly discuss the Lyapunov-like condition (
2.3
) on
C
.

Remark 2.2
.

(a) Since
A
is assumed to be Hurwitz, for every positive definite matrix
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
there exists a positive definite matrix
P\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
such that
AP+PA^{\top}=-Q
. Hence, the set of positive definite matrices
C
satisfying the matrix inequality (
2.3
) is not empty. For example,
C=\alpha\Sigma^{1/2}
is a solution for every
\alpha>0
.

(b) If
m=d
and
B
is invertible, then for any given positive definite matrix
C
there exists
\tau>0
such that
\tau C
satisfies (
2.3
). Indeed, if this was not the case, there would exist a sequence
(x_{n})\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\frac{1}{n}\langle(AC^{2}+C^{2}A^{\top})x_{n},x_{n}\rangle>\|B^{\top}x_{n}\|^{2}
. It is no restriction to assume
\|x_{n}\|=1
and
x_{n}\to x
as
n\to\infty
for some
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\|x\|=1
. But then, the above implies
B^{\top}x=0
and hence
x=0
, a contradiction.

(c) It is not clear whether the condition (
2.3
) is also necessary for Koopman invariance of
\mathbb{H}_{C}
. We discuss this at the end of Section
4
.

Corollary 2.3
.

Let
\sigma>0
and assume that
\frac{1}{2}(A+A^{\top})\leq\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}BB^{\top}

(
e.g., if
A
is dissipative
1
1

1

Recall that a matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is called
dissipative
if
A+A^{\top}\leq 0
.
)
. Then the RKHS
\mathbb{H}_{\sigma}
is invariant under each Koopman operator
K^{t}
,
t\geq 0
, associated with the SDE (
2.1
), and we have

\|K^{t}f\|_{\sigma}\leq e^{t\cdot\operatorname{Tr}(-A)/2}\|f\|_{\sigma},\qquad f\in\mathbb{H}_{\sigma}.

We conclude this section by tying up some notations. First of all, we agree on the convention that a positive definite kernel is always symmetric, i.e.,
k(x,y)=k(y,x)
, and real-valued. Also, the term “positive definite” only refers to real, symmetric matrices. Let
X
be a set. For
x\in X
and a positive definite kernel
k
on
X
, set
k_{x}(y):=k(x,y)
,
y\in X
. That is,
x\mapsto k_{x}
is the canonical feature map of the kernel
k
. For two positive definite kernels
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
on
X
we write
k_{1}\preceq k_{2}
if

\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}k_{1}(x_{i},x_{j})\leq\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}k_{2}(x_{i},x_{j})

for any choice of
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\alpha_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
, and
x_{j}\in X
,
j=1,\ldots,n
. We also write
V\!\lhook\joinrel\xrightarrow{\hskip 2.84544ptc\hskip 2.84544pt}W
(continuous embedding) for two normed vector spaces
V
and
W
to indicate that
V\subset W
and the identity map from
V
to
W
is continuous, i.e., if there exists
K>0
such that
\|v\|_{W}\leq K\|v\|_{V}
for all
v\in V
.

3. Some properties of Gaussian kernels

In this section, we collect the statements on Gaussian kernels that are utilized in the proof of Theorem
2.1
. The next lemma shows that
\mathbb{H}_{C}
can be regarded as a dense subspace of
L^{2}(\mu)
and that the
\mathbb{H}_{C}
-norm is stronger than the
L^{2}(\mu)
-norm on
\mathbb{H}_{C}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
C\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be positive definite. Then the RKHS
\mathbb{H}_{C}
is densely and continuously embedded in
L^{2}(\mu)
.

Proof.

The fact that
\mathbb{H}_{C}\!\lhook\joinrel\xrightarrow{\hskip 2.84544ptc\hskip 2.84544pt}L^{2}(\mu)
is the first part of
[
17
, Theorem 4.26]
. Moreover, if
f\in\mathbb{H}_{C}
and
f=0

\mu
-a.e., then
f(x)=0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
as
f
is continuous and the density
d\mu/dx
is positive. Therefore, we may regard
\mathbb{H}_{C}
as a subspace of
L^{2}(\mu)
. Concerning the density of
\mathbb{H}_{C}
in
L^{2}(\mu)
, we have to show that the integral operator
K:L^{2}(\mu)\to\mathbb{H}_{C}
, defined by

Kf(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}k^{C}(x,y)f(y)\,d\mu(y),\qquad f\in L^{2}(\mu),

has trivial kernel in
L^{2}(\mu)
, see
[
17
, Theorem 4.26]
. So, let
f\in L^{2}(\mu)
such that
Kf=0
, i.e.,

(\phi*\psi)(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\underbrace{\exp(-\|C^{-1}(x-y)\|^{2})}_{=\phi(x-y)}\cdot\underbrace{f(y)\exp(-\|Py\|^{2})}_{=\psi(y)}\,dy=0,\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

where
P=(2\Sigma)^{-1/2}
. Applying the Fourier transform to
\phi*\psi
, we obtain
\widehat{\phi}(\omega)\cdot\widehat{\psi}(\omega)=0
for a.e.
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. But
\widehat{\phi}(\omega)>0
for all
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, hence
\widehat{\psi}=0
and thus
\psi=0
.
∎

The following lemma is well-known. We will apply it in the proof of Proposition
3.3
below, which can be seen as a generalization of Lemma
3.2
to the multi-dimensional case.

Lemma 3.2
.

For
\sigma>0
, consider the Gaussian kernel
k^{\sigma}
on
\mathbb{R}
. If
\sigma_{1}\geq\sigma_{2}>0
, then
\mathbb{H}_{\sigma_{1}}\!\lhook\joinrel\xrightarrow{\hskip 2.84544ptc\hskip 2.84544pt}\mathbb{H}_{\sigma_{2}}
with
\|f\|_{\sigma_{2}}\leq(\frac{\sigma_{1}}{\sigma_{2}})^{1/2}\|f\|_{\sigma_{1}}
for
f\in\mathbb{H}_{\sigma_{1}}
, and
k^{\sigma_{1}}\preceq\frac{\sigma_{1}}{\sigma_{2}}k^{\sigma_{2}}
.

Proof.

The first part is
[
18
, Corollary 6]
, and the second follows from Aronszajn’s inclusion theorem, Theorem
A.1
.
∎

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
C_{1},C_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be positive definite. Then
\mathbb{H}_{C_{1}}\!\lhook\joinrel\xrightarrow{\hskip 2.84544ptc\hskip 2.84544pt}\mathbb{H}_{C_{2}}
if and only if
C_{1}^{2}\geq C_{2}^{2}
. In this case, we have

\|f\|_{C_{2}}\leq(\tfrac{\det C_{1}}{\det C_{2}})^{1/2}\|f\|_{C_{1}},\qquad f\in\mathbb{H}_{C_{1}},

and
k^{C_{1}}\preceq\frac{\det C_{1}}{\det C_{2}}\cdot k^{C_{2}}
.

Proof.

The proof of Proposition
3.3
is divided into three parts.

1.
Denote by
I=I_{d}
the
d\times d
-identity matrix. We first prove that for a positive diagonal matrix
D\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
we have
\mathbb{H}_{D}\!\lhook\joinrel\xrightarrow{\hskip 2.84544ptc\hskip 2.84544pt}\mathbb{H}_{I}
if and only if
D\geq I
. For this, let
D=\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{d})
with
\sigma_{i}>0
for all
i=1,\ldots,d
. Observe that for
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
we have (for the tensor product of kernels see (
A.1
))

\displaystyle k^{D}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\exp\big(-(x-y)^{\top}D^{-2}(x-y)\big)=\exp\Big(-\sum_{k=1}^{d}\sigma_{k}^{-2}(x_{k}-y_{k})^{2}\Big)=\prod_{k=1}^{d}\exp\Big[-\frac{(x_{k}-y_{k})^{2}}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}\Big]

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=1}^{d}k^{\sigma_{k}}(x_{k},y_{k})=(k^{\sigma_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes k^{\sigma_{d}})(x,y),

where the
x_{k}
and
y_{k}
are the Cartesian coordinates of
x
and
y
, respectively.

Hence, if
D\geq I
(i.e.,
\sigma_{i}\geq 1
for all
i=1,\ldots,d
), Lemmas
A.2
and
3.2
imply

k^{D}=k^{\sigma_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes k^{\sigma_{d}}\preceq\sigma_{1}k^{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\sigma_{d}k^{1}=\sigma_{1}\dots\sigma_{d}\cdot k^{I}=\det D\cdot k^{I},

and Aronszajn’s inclusion theorem, Theorem
A.1
, implies
\mathbb{H}_{D}\!\lhook\joinrel\xrightarrow{\hskip 2.84544ptc\hskip 2.84544pt}\mathbb{H}_{I}
.

Conversely, let
\mathbb{H}_{D}\!\lhook\joinrel\xrightarrow{\hskip 2.84544ptc\hskip 2.84544pt}\mathbb{H}_{I}
and suppose that
\sigma_{1}<1
. By Theorem
A.1
, we have
k^{D}\preceq\alpha k^{I}
with some
\alpha>0
. By setting
x_{k}=y_{k}=0
for
k=2,\ldots,d
, we see that
k^{D}(x,y)=k^{\sigma_{1}}(x_{1},y_{1})
and
k^{I}(x,y)=k^{1}(x_{1},y_{1})
. Hence, we have
k^{\sigma_{1}}\preceq\alpha k^{1}
. On the other hand,
1>\sigma_{1}
and Lemma
3.2
imply
k^{1}\preceq\sigma_{1}^{-1}k^{\sigma_{1}}
. This implies
\mathbb{H}_{\sigma_{1}I}=\mathbb{H}_{I}
, which contradicts
[
18
, Corollary 7 ii)]
.

2.
Next, we prove that for a positive definite matrix
C\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
we have
\mathbb{H}_{C}\!\lhook\joinrel\xrightarrow{\hskip 2.84544ptc\hskip 2.84544pt}\mathbb{H}_{I}
if and only if
C\geq I
. For this, let
U\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be an orthogonal matrix such that
C=U^{\top}DU
with a diagonal matrix
D\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. Then
k^{C}=k^{D}\circ U
and
k^{I}=k^{I}\circ U
. If
C\geq I
, then

k^{C}=k^{D}\circ U\preceq\det D\cdot k^{I}\circ U=\det C\cdot k^{I}.

Conversely, if
\mathbb{H}_{C}\!\lhook\joinrel\xrightarrow{\hskip 2.84544ptc\hskip 2.84544pt}\mathbb{H}_{I}
, then
k^{C}\preceq\alpha k^{I}
with some
\alpha>0
, which implies
k^{D}\preceq\alpha k^{I}
and hence
D\geq I
, which is equivalent to
C\geq I
.

3.
Finally, let
C_{1},C_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be arbitrary symmetric positive definite matrices. Note that
C_{1}^{2}\geq C_{2}^{2}
is equivalent to
C:=(C_{2}^{-1}C_{1}^{2}C_{2}^{-1})^{1/2}\geq I
. Furthermore, note that

\displaystyle k^{C}(x,y)=k^{C_{1}}(C_{2}x,C_{2}y)\qquad\text{and}\qquad k^{I}(x,y)=k^{C_{2}}(C_{2}x,C_{2}y),\qquad x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(3.1)

Therefore, if
C_{1}^{2}\geq C_{2}^{2}
, by the second part of the proof,

k^{C_{1}}=k^{C}\circ C_{2}^{-1}\preceq\det C\cdot(k^{1}\circ C_{2}^{-1})=\tfrac{\det C_{1}}{\det C_{2}}\cdot k^{C_{2}},

which yields
\mathbb{H}_{C_{1}}\!\lhook\joinrel\xrightarrow{\hskip 2.84544ptc\hskip 2.84544pt}\mathbb{H}_{C_{2}}
. Conversely, if
\mathbb{H}_{C_{1}}\!\lhook\joinrel\xrightarrow{\hskip 2.84544ptc\hskip 2.84544pt}\mathbb{H}_{C_{2}}
, then there exists
\alpha>0
such that
k^{C_{1}}\preceq\alpha k^{C_{2}}
. In view of (
3.1
), this gives
k^{C}\circ C_{2}^{-1}=k^{C_{1}}\preceq\alpha k^{C_{2}}=\alpha k^{I}\circ C_{2}^{-1}
and hence
k^{C}\preceq\alpha k^{I}
. By part 2, this implies
C\geq I
.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

Note that
C_{1}\geq C_{2}
does in general not imply
C_{1}^{2}\geq C_{2}^{2}
for symmetric positive definite matrices
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
. On the other hand
C_{1}\geq C_{2}
implies
C_{1}^{1/2}\geq C_{2}^{1/2}
and
C_{1}^{-1}\leq C_{2}^{-1}
.

We conclude this section with the following lemma.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
C_{1},C_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be positive definite and
z,w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}k_{z}^{C_{1}}(y)\cdot k_{w}^{C_{2}}(y)\,dy=\frac{\pi^{d/2}}{\det(C_{1}^{-2}+C_{2}^{-2})^{1/2}}\cdot k^{C}(z,w),

where
C=(C_{1}^{2}+C_{2}^{2})^{1/2}
.

Proof.

Let
M:=(C_{1}^{-2}+C_{2}^{-2})^{1/2}
. We have
k_{z}^{C_{1}}(y)k_{w}^{C_{2}}(y)=e^{-L(y)}
,
where

\displaystyle L(y)

\displaystyle=(y-z)^{\top}C_{1}^{-2}(y-z)+(y-w)^{\top}C_{2}^{-2}(y-w)

\displaystyle=y^{\top}(C_{1}^{-2}+C_{2}^{-2})y-2\big(C_{1}^{-2}z+C_{2}^{-2}w\big)^{\top}y+z^{\top}C_{1}^{-2}z+w^{\top}C_{2}^{-2}w

\displaystyle=\|My\|^{2}-2\big\langle M^{-1}\big(C_{1}^{-2}z+C_{2}^{-2}w\big),My\big\rangle+\|C_{1}^{-1}z\|^{2}+\|C_{2}^{-1}w\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\big\|My-M^{-1}(C_{1}^{-2}z+C_{2}^{-2}w)\big\|^{2}-K(z,w),

where

\displaystyle K(z,w)

\displaystyle=\big\|M^{-1}(C_{1}^{-2}z+C_{2}^{-2}w)\big\|^{2}-\|C_{1}^{-1}z\|^{2}-\|C_{2}^{-1}w\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\big\langle(C_{1}^{-2}+C_{2}^{-2})^{-1}(C_{1}^{-2}z+C_{2}^{-2}w),(C_{1}^{-2}z+C_{2}^{-2}w)\big\rangle-\|C_{1}^{-1}z\|^{2}-\|C_{2}^{-1}w\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\big\langle z+M^{-2}(C_{2}^{-2}w-C_{2}^{-2}z),C_{1}^{-2}z+C_{2}^{-2}w\big\rangle-\|C_{1}^{-1}z\|^{2}-\|C_{2}^{-1}w\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\langle z,C_{2}^{-2}w\rangle+\big\langle C_{2}^{-2}(w-z),(C_{1}^{-2}+C_{2}^{-2})^{-1}(C_{1}^{-2}z+C_{2}^{-2}w)\big\rangle-\|C_{2}^{-1}w\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\langle z,C_{2}^{-2}w\rangle+\big\langle C_{2}^{-2}(w-z),w+M^{-2}(C_{1}^{-2}z-C_{1}^{-2}w)\big\rangle-\|C_{2}^{-1}w\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\big\langle C_{2}^{-2}(w-z),M^{-2}C_{1}^{-2}(z-w)\big\rangle=\big\langle(C_{2}^{2}M^{2}C_{1}^{2})^{-1}(w-z),(z-w)\big\rangle

\displaystyle=-\langle(C_{1}^{2}+C_{2}^{2})^{-1}(z-w),(z-w)\big\rangle=-(z-w)^{\top}C^{-2}(z-w).

Thus, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}k_{z}^{C_{1}}(y)\cdot k_{w}^{C_{2}}(y)\,dy=e^{K(z,w)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\exp(-\|My-M^{-1}(C_{1}^{-2}z+C_{2}^{-2}w)\|^{2})\,dy=k^{C}(z,w)\cdot\frac{\pi^{d/2}}{\det M},

and the lemma is proved.
∎

4. Proof of Theorem 1.1

We first prove the following proposition on the action of the Koopman operator of (
2.1
) on
\mathbb{H}_{C}
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
t\geq 0
, let
C\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be symmetric positive definite, and define

\displaystyle C_{t}=\big[e^{-At}(C^{2}+2\Sigma(t))e^{-A^{\top}t}\big]^{1/2}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\tau_{t}=\frac{\det C}{(\det(C^{2}+2\Sigma(t)))^{1/2}}.

(4.1)

Then the Koopman operator
K^{t}
, associated with the SDE (
2.1
) and defined in (
2.2
), maps
\mathbb{H}_{C}
boundedly into
\mathbb{H}_{C_{t}}
with norm not exceeding
\tau_{t}^{1/2}
.

Proof.

Let
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. By Lemma
3.5
, we have

\displaystyle(K^{t}k_{z}^{C})(x)

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-d/2}(\det\Sigma(t))^{-1/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}k_{z}^{C}(y)\exp\big(-\tfrac{1}{2}(y-e^{At}x)\Sigma(t)^{-1}(y-e^{At}x)\big)\,dy

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-d/2}(\det\Sigma(t))^{-1/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}k_{z}^{C}(y)k_{e^{At}x}^{(2\Sigma(t))^{1/2}}(y)\,dy

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-d/2}(\det\Sigma(t))^{-1/2}\frac{\pi^{d/2}}{\det(C^{-2}+(2\Sigma(t))^{-1})^{1/2}}k^{(C^{2}+2\Sigma(t))^{1/2}}(z,e^{At}x)

\displaystyle=\frac{(\det\Sigma(t))^{-1/2}}{\det(2C^{-2}+\Sigma(t)^{-1})^{1/2}}\cdot k^{C_{t}}_{e^{-At}z}(x)=\tau_{t}\cdot k^{C_{t}}_{e^{-At}z}(x),

hence,
K^{t}k_{z}^{C}=\tau_{t}\cdot k^{C_{t}}_{e^{-At}z}\in\mathbb{H}_{C_{t}}
. Therefore, if
f=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}k^{C}_{x_{j}}
with
\alpha_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
and
x_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
(
j=1,\ldots,n
), we obtain

\displaystyle\|K^{t}f\|_{C_{t}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\Bigg\|\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}K^{t}k^{C}_{x_{j}}\Bigg\|_{C_{t}}^{2}=\tau_{t}^{2}\Bigg\|\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}k^{C_{t}}_{e^{-At}x_{j}}\Bigg\|_{C_{t}}^{2}=\tau_{t}^{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}k^{C_{t}}(e^{-At}x_{i},e^{-At}x_{j})

\displaystyle=\tau_{t}^{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}k^{(C^{2}+2\Sigma(t))^{1/2}}(x_{i},x_{j})\leq\tau_{t}^{2}\cdot\frac{\det(C^{2}+2\Sigma(t))^{1/2}}{\det C}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}k^{C}(x_{i},x_{j})=\tau_{t}\|f\|_{C}^{2},

where the inequality is due to Proposition
3.3
.

This shows that
K^{t}
maps
\mathbb{H}_{0,C}=\operatorname{span}\{k^{C}_{x}:x\in\mathbb{R}\}\subset\mathbb{H}_{C}
boundedly into
\mathbb{H}_{C_{t}}
. Since
\mathbb{H}_{0,C}
is dense in
\mathbb{H}_{C}
, it follows that
K^{t}|_{\mathbb{H}_{0,C}}
extends to a bounded operator
T:\mathbb{H}_{C}\to\mathbb{H}_{C_{t}}
. In order to see that
Tf=K^{t}f
for
f\in\mathbb{H}_{C}
, let
(f_{n})\subset\mathbb{H}_{0,C}
such that
f_{n}\to f
in
\mathbb{H}_{C}
. Then
K^{t}f_{n}=Tf_{n}\to Tf
in
\mathbb{H}_{C_{t}}
. Since
\mathbb{H}_{C}\!\lhook\joinrel\xrightarrow{\hskip 2.84544ptc\hskip 2.84544pt}L^{2}(\mu)
, we have
f_{n}\to f
in
L^{2}(\mu)
and thus
K^{t}f_{n}\to K^{t}f
in
L^{2}(\mu)
. Also,
K^{t}f_{n}\to Tf
in
L^{2}(\mu)
. Hence,
K^{t}f=Tf

\mu
-a.e. on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. But as both
K^{t}f
and
Tf
are continuous and
\mu
is absolutely continuous w.r.t. Lebesgue measure with a positive density, we conclude that
K^{t}f=Tf\in\mathbb{H}_{C}
.
∎

We are now in the position to prove the main result of this note—that the RKHS
\mathbb{H}_{C}
is invariant under the Koopman operator of the SDE (
2.1
) if the Lyapunov-like condition (
2.3
) on the interplay of the matrices
A
,
B
, and
C
is satisfied.

Proof of Theorem
2.1
.

Let
C_{t}
and
\tau_{t}
be defined as in Proposition
4.1
. We prove that
C_{t}^{2}\geq C^{2}
for all
t\geq 0
if any only if (
2.3
) is satisfied. For
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
t\geq 0
we have

\displaystyle f_{x}(t)

\displaystyle:=\langle C_{t}^{2}x,x\rangle=\langle e^{-At}(C^{2}+2\Sigma(t))e^{-A^{\top}t}x,x\rangle=\|Ce^{-A^{\top}t}x\|^{2}+2\langle e^{-At}\Sigma(t)e^{-A^{\top}t}x,x\rangle

\displaystyle=\|Ce^{-A^{\top}t}x\|^{2}+2\Big\langle\Big(\int_{0}^{t}e^{-As}BB^{\top}e^{-A^{\top}s}\,ds\Big)x,x\Big\rangle=\|Ce^{-A^{\top}t}x\|^{2}+2\int_{0}^{t}\big\|B^{\top}e^{-A^{\top}s}x\big\|^{2}\,ds.

Moreover,

\displaystyle\dot{f}_{x}(t)

\displaystyle=\big\langle Ce^{-A^{\top}t}x,\tfrac{d}{dt}Ce^{-A^{\top}t}x\big\rangle+\big\langle\tfrac{d}{dt}Ce^{-A^{\top}t}x,Ce^{-A^{\top}t}x\big\rangle+2\big\|B^{\top}e^{-A^{\top}t}x\big\|^{2}

\displaystyle=-\big\langle AC^{2}e^{-A^{\top}t}x,e^{-A^{\top}t}x\big\rangle-\big\langle C^{2}A^{\top}e^{-A^{\top}t}x,e^{-A^{\top}t}x\big\rangle+2\big\|B^{\top}e^{-A^{\top}t}x\big\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\big\langle\big[2BB^{\top}-(AC^{2}+C^{2}A^{\top})\big]e^{-A^{\top}t}x,e^{-A^{\top}t}x\big\rangle.

Note that
f_{x}(0)=\|Cx\|^{2}
. Hence, if (
2.3
) holds, we have
\dot{f}_{x}(t)\geq 0
for all
t\geq 0
and all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which yields
C_{t}^{2}\geq C^{2}
for all
t\geq 0
. Conversely, if
C_{t}^{2}\geq C^{2}
for all
t\geq 0
, i.e.,
f_{x}(t)\geq f_{x}(0)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and all
t\geq 0
, then
\dot{f}_{x}(0)\geq 0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which is (
2.3
).

We may thus apply Proposition
3.3
to conclude that
\mathbb{H}_{C_{t}}\!\lhook\joinrel\xrightarrow{\hskip 2.84544ptc\hskip 2.84544pt}\mathbb{H}_{C}
with
\|f\|_{C}^{2}\leq(\frac{\det C_{t}}{\det C})\|f\|_{C_{t}}^{2}
for
f\in\mathbb{H}_{C_{t}}
and all
t\geq 0
. Thus,

\displaystyle\|K^{t}f\|_{C}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\det C_{t}}{\det C}\|K^{t}f\|_{C_{t}}^{2}\leq\tau_{t}\frac{\det C_{t}}{\det C}\|f\|_{C}^{2}=\det e^{-At}\|f\|_{C}^{2}=e^{t\cdot\operatorname{Tr}(-A)}\|f\|_{C}^{2},

which concludes the proof of the theorem.
∎

Appendix A
Some background on RKHS theory

Theorem A.1

(Aronszajn’s inclusion theorem,
[
12
, Theorem 5.1]
)
.

Let
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
be two symmetric positive definite kernels on a set
X
. Denote their corresponding RKHS’s by
\mathbb{H}_{1}
and
\mathbb{H}_{2}
, respectively. Then
\mathbb{H}_{1}\subset\mathbb{H}_{2}
if and only if
\mathbb{H}_{1}\!\lhook\joinrel\xrightarrow{\hskip 2.84544ptc\hskip 2.84544pt}\mathbb{H}_{2}
. Moreover, for
c>0
we have
\mathbb{H}_{1}\lhook\joinrel\xrightarrow{\hskip 2.84544ptc\hskip 2.84544pt}\mathbb{H}_{2}
with
\|f\|_{2}\leq c\|f\|_{1}
for
f\in\mathbb{H}_{1}
if and only if
k_{1}\preceq c^{2}k_{2}
.

Given two symmetric positive definite kernels
k_{X}
on
X
and
k_{Y}
on
Y
, we define the
tensor product
(cf.
[
12
, Definition 5.12]
)
k_{X}\otimes k_{Y}:(X\times Y)^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
of
k_{X}
and
k_{Y}
by

\displaystyle(k_{X}\otimes k_{Y})\big((x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\big)=k_{X}(x,x^{\prime})\cdot k_{Y}(y,y^{\prime}).

(A.1)

It follows from the positive semi-definiteness of the Schur product of two positive semi-definite matrices (see, e.g.,
[
12
, Theorem 4.8]
) that
k_{X}\otimes k_{Y}
is a symmetric positive definite kernel on
X\times Y
.

Lemma A.2
.

Let
k_{X},k_{X}^{\prime}
be kernels on
X
and
k_{Y},k_{Y}^{\prime}
kernels on
Y
and assume that
k_{X}\preceq k_{X}^{\prime}
and
k_{Y}\preceq k_{Y}^{\prime}
. Then also
k_{X}\otimes k_{Y}\preceq k_{X}^{\prime}\otimes k_{Y}^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Denote the RKHS corresponding to
k_{X}
,
k_{X}^{\prime}
,
k_{Y}
, and
k_{Y}^{\prime}
by
\mathbb{H}_{X}
,
\mathbb{H}_{X}^{\prime}
,
\mathbb{H}_{Y}
, and
\mathbb{H}_{Y}^{\prime}
, respectively. Let
(e_{s})_{s\in S}
and
(f_{t})_{t\in T}
be orthonormal bases of
\mathbb{H}_{Y}
and
\mathbb{H}_{X}^{\prime}
, respectively. Then we have (
x_{1},x_{2}\in X
,
y_{1},y_{2}\in Y
)

k_{Y}(y_{1},y_{2})=\sum_{s\in S}e_{s}(y_{1})e_{s}(y_{2})\qquad\text{and}\qquad k_{X}^{\prime}(x_{1},x_{2})=\sum_{t\in T}f_{t}(x_{1})f_{t}(x_{2})

with pointwise convergence, see
[
12
, Theorem 2.4]
. Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\alpha_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
,
(x_{j},y_{j})\in X\times Y
,
j=1,\ldots,n
. Then

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}(k_{X}\otimes k_{Y})\big((x_{i},y_{i}),(x_{j},y_{j})\big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}k_{X}(x_{i},x_{j})k_{Y}(y_{i},y_{j})

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\in S}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}[\alpha_{i}e_{s}(y_{i})][\alpha_{j}e_{s}(y_{j})]k_{X}(x_{i},x_{j})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s\in S}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}[\alpha_{i}e_{s}(y_{i})][\alpha_{j}e_{s}(y_{j})]k_{X}^{\prime}(x_{i},x_{j})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}k_{X}^{\prime}(x_{i},x_{j})k_{Y}(y_{i},y_{j})

\displaystyle=\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}[\alpha_{i}\,f_{t}(x_{i})][\alpha_{j}\,f_{t}(x_{j})]k_{Y}(y_{i},y_{j})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}k_{X}^{\prime}(x_{i},x_{j})k_{Y}^{\prime}(x_{i},y_{j})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}(k_{X}^{\prime}\otimes k_{Y}^{\prime})\big((x_{i},y_{i}),(x_{j},y_{j})\big),

which was to be proven.
∎
