Quantitative Uniform Stability of the Iterative Proportional Fitting Procedure
George Deligiannidis , Valentin De Bortoli , Arnaud Doucet

Abstract
We establish that the iterates of the Iterative Proportional Fitting Procedure, also known as Sinkhorn’s algorithm and commonly used to solve entropy-regularised Optimal Transport problems, are stable w.r.t. perturbations of the marginals, uniformly in time. Our result is quantitative and stated in terms of the 1-Wasserstein metric. As a corollary we establish a quantitative stability result for Schrödinger bridges.



Keywords—
entropy regularized optimal transport; Schrödinger bridge; Iterative Proportional Fitting Procedure; Sinkhorn algorithm; particle filtering.

1
Introduction

The basic problem of Optimal Transport (OT) (see
Villani, 2009
for a broad overview),
in its modern formulation introduced by
Kantorovich, 1942
,
is to find a
coupling
of two distributions
\mu,\nu
that minimises

\inf_{\pi\in\mathbf{T}(\mu,\nu)}\int\|x-y\|^{2}\mathrm{d}\pi(x,y),

OT
(\mu,\nu)

where
\mathbf{T}(\mu,\nu)
denotes the collection of probability measures with
marginals
\mu,\nu
and the Euclidean distance
\|x-y\|
may be replaced by any
other metric or cost function
c(x,y)
. OT provides a theoretical framework for
analysis in the space of probability measures and has deep connections with many
branches of mathematics including partial differential equations and
probability. Beyond its intrinsic interest, OT has recently become an extremely
important tool for data science and machine learning, finding numerous
applications in fields as diverse as imaging, computer vision or natural
language processing
(
Peyré and Cuturi, 2019
)
.

This ubiquity of OT in modern applications is largely due to the computational tractability of the
Entropy-Regularised
Optimal Transport problem

\inf_{\pi\in\mathbf{T}(\mu,\nu)}\int\|x-y\|^{2}\mathrm{d}\pi(x,y)+\varepsilon\operatorname{KL}\left(\pi|\mu\otimes\nu\right),

OT
{}_{\varepsilon}(\mu,\nu)

which is equivalent to the
static Schrödinger bridge
, a problem
going back to
Schrödinger, 1931
, see
Equation

5

in Section
3
. Here
\operatorname{KL}\left(\pi|\rho\right)
denotes the

Kullback–Leibler divergence
between the probability measures
\pi
and

\rho
, defined as

\operatorname{KL}\left(\pi|\rho\right)=\begin{cases}\int\log(\frac{\mathrm{d}\pi}{\mathrm{d}\rho}(x))\mathrm{d}\pi(x),&\pi\ll\rho,\\
+\infty,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(2)

The great interest in
OT
{}_{\varepsilon}(\mu,\nu)
, as explained in the seminal paper of
Cuturi, 2013
,
stems from its amenability to the
Iterative Proportional
Fitting Procedure
(IPFP), see
Equation

8
below. The theoretical properties of IPFP, also known as the Sinkhorn algorithm,
have been investigated in numerous works, and are therefore fairly well
understood.

Due to its computational tractability,
OT
{}_{\varepsilon}(\mu,\nu)
has been
used in applications as an approximation to
OT
(\mu,\nu)
. Rigorous
justification of this approximation has been the subject of intense research
recently. Indeed it has been established, see e.g.
Cominetti and San Martín, 1994
;
Mikami, 2004
;
Léonard, 2012
;
Carlier et al., 2017
,
that as the regularisation parameter
\varepsilon\to 0
, the solution of

OT
{}_{\varepsilon}(\mu,\nu)
converges to that of
OT
(\mu,\nu)
.

More recently however, Schrödinger bridges and entropy-regularised OT are
being studied for their own sake, finding applications in control, computational
statistics and machine learning, see e.g.
Bernton et al., 2019
;
Chen et al., 2021
;
Corenflos et al., 2021
;
De Bortoli et al., 2021
;
Huang et al., 2021
;
Li et al., 2020
;
Vargas et al., 2021
. In these applications, the entropy
regularisation may be a desirable feature rather than an approximation, and the
main source of error is the fact that the marginal distributions are typically
intractable and often approximated by empirical versions. It is then desirable
that as the number of samples increases, this error vanishes. For example, a
quantitative version of this statement, can then be used to establish that the
differentiable particle filter proposed in
Corenflos et al., 2021

converges as the sample size increases, for any
\varepsilon>0
, thus strengthening
the analysis of
Corenflos et al., 2021
which requires

\varepsilon_{N}\to 0
as
N\to\infty
to ensure consistency towards the true optimal
filter.

This is the question we study in this paper. In particular we establish the
stability of the IPFP and of the solution of the corresponding Schrödinger
bridge problem w.r.t. perturbations of the marginals.

For standard OT, a classical argument
using compactness and cyclical monotonicity guarantees a qualitative version of this result, see e.g.
(
Villani, 2009
, Theorem 5.23, Corollary 5.23)
. Quantitative versions of this result appeared much more recently, at least in the case of quadratic costs, in
Mérigot et al., 2020
,
Li and Nochetto, 2021
,
Delalande and Merigot, 2021
. In particular it is established that the optimal transport plans, or maps in the case of absolutely continuous measures, is Hölder continuous in the marginals, with exponent
1/2
w.r.t. the marginals.
It is also known that the exponent
1/2
is the best possible, see
Gigli, 2011
.

For entropy-regularised OT and the static Schrödinger bridge problem, the
first qualitative result appeared very recently in
Ghosal et al., 2021
,
based on a version of cyclical monotonicity for entropy-regularised OT
introduced by
Bernton et al., 2021
. In the quantitative direction,

Luise et al., 2019
establish Lipschitz continuity of the potentials
w.r.t. the marginals, measured in the total variation metric, which is too
strong to capture the situation where the marginals are being approximated by
empirical versions. For
smooth
cost functions,
Luise et al., 2019

also establish that the sample complexity of learning the potentials is of order

n^{2}
, leveraging results from
Genevay et al., 2019
on the regularity of
potentials and the duality between Maximum Mean Discrepancy type metrics and
Sobolev spaces. However, if one is interested in learning the Schrödinger
bridge the situation is more complicated; the Wasserstein-1 distance between two
couplings is lower bounded by the distance of the marginals and so the results
by
Fournier and Guillin, 2015
imply that the sample complexity of learning the
Schrödinger bridge must scale at least as
n^{d}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

We present here the first, to the best of our knowledge, quantitative stability
result for entropy-regularised OT. In particular, this follows from a stronger
result, namely the uniform in time stability of IPFP, that is the Sinkhorn
iterates, w.r.t. the marginal distributions. We think this result is of particular importance for practical applications as IFPF is typically used for a finite number of iterations to approximate the Schrödinger bridge. One interesting fact is that in
contrast to the standard OT problem, the solution of the entropy-regularised
problem is Lipschitz continuous, in the Wasserstein metric, w.r.t. the
marginals. However, as the regularisation parameter
\varepsilon
vanishes, the
Lipschitz constant blows up as expected by the Hölder continuity of the OT
plan.

The recent paper by
Eckstein and Nutz, 2021
, which appeared a
couple of months after the first version of the present manuscript, uses very
interesting methods, completely different to the ones in the present paper, to
establish the quantitative stability of the Schrödinger bridge w.r.t. the
marginals measured in the Wasserstein distance. On the one hand, the setting
of
Eckstein and Nutz, 2021
is more general than ours, as it does not
require compactness. On the other hand,
Eckstein and Nutz, 2021
only
establish the stability of the Schrödinger bridge instead of the full iterates of IPFP and Hölder continuity w.r.t. the marginals with exponent
1/2
.

3
Main results

Let
(\mathsf{X},d_{\mathsf{X}})
,
(\mathsf{Y},d_{\mathsf{Y}})
be two compact metric spaces and
write
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}
for their respective Borel
\sigma
-algebras. We will use
d

to denote the metric for both
\mathsf{X},\mathsf{Y}
when the context allows. Let

\pi_{0}\in\mathscr{P}(\mathsf{X}),\pi_{1}\in\mathscr{P}(\mathsf{Y})
. We begin by recalling the
Iterative Proportional Fitting Procedure (IPFP) solving the following Schrödinger bridge problem

\mathbb{P}^{\star}\in\operatorname*{arg\,min}\{\operatorname{KL}(\mathbb{P}|\mathbb{Q})\,:\;\mathbb{P}\in\mathscr{P}(\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{Y}),\;\mathbb{P}_{0}=\pi_{0}\;,\mathbb{P}_{1}=\pi_{1}\},

(5)

where
\mathbb{Q}\in\mathscr{P}(\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{Y})
is a reference measure
admitting a density w.r.t.
\rho_{0}\otimes\rho_{1}
, with
\rho_{0}\in\mathscr{P}(\mathsf{X})
equivalent to
\pi_{0}
, and
\rho_{1}\in\mathscr{P}(\mathsf{Y})
, equivalent to
\pi_{1}
; that is for any

(x,y)\in\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{Y}

\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}/\mathrm{d}(\rho_{0}\otimes\rho_{1})(x,y)=K(x,y)=\exp[-c(x,y)].

(6)

In the case where

\mathsf{X}=\mathsf{Y}
, we have that Problem (
5
) with the choice

c(x,y)=\|x-y\|^{2}/\varepsilon
is equivalent to
OT

(
μ
,
ν
)

ε

, see e.g.

(
Peyré and Cuturi, 2019
, Remark 4.2)
.
First, we give a sufficient condition to ensure that the solution of (
5
) exists and is unique.
The proof of this proposition is a straightforward consequence of
(
Csiszár, 1975
, Corollary 3.2)
.

Proposition 1
.

Assume that
\operatorname{KL}\left(\pi_{i}|\rho_{i}\right)<+\infty
for
i\in\{0,1\}
and that

c\in\mathrm{C}(\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{Y},\mathbb{R})
. Then there exists a unique solution to
(
5
).

The following proposition, see

(
Peyré and Cuturi, 2019
, Proposition 4.2)
for instance, ensures that we can
assume without loss of generality that
\rho_{0}=\pi_{0}
and
\rho_{1}=\pi_{1}
.

Proposition 2
.

Assume that
\operatorname{KL}\left(\pi_{i}|\rho_{i}\right)<+\infty
for
i\in\{0,1\}
and that

c\in\mathrm{C}(\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{Y},\mathbb{R})
. Let
\mathbb{P}^{\star}
solution of
(
5
) with
\mathbb{Q}
given by (
6
) and

\hat{\mathbb{P}}^{\star}
the solution of (
5
) with
\mathbb{Q}

such that for any
(x,y)\in\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{Y}

\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}/\mathrm{d}(\pi_{0}\otimes\pi_{1})(x,y)=K(x,y).

(7)

Then
\mathbb{P}^{\star}=\hat{\mathbb{P}}^{\star}
.

As a consequence, for the rest of this paper, we assume that
\rho_{0}=\pi_{0}

and
\rho_{1}=\pi_{1}
. In order to solve (
5
) we
consider the IPFP sequence which iteratively solves each half-bridge problem,

i.e.
we define
(\mathbb{P}^{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{2n+1}=\operatorname*{arg\,min}\{\operatorname{KL}\left(\mathbb{P}|\mathbb{P}^{2n}\right)\,:\;\mathbb{P}\in\mathscr{P}(\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{Y}),\mathbb{P}_{0}=\pi_{0}\},

(8)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{2n+2}=\operatorname*{arg\,min}\{\operatorname{KL}\left(\mathbb{P}|\mathbb{P}^{2n+1}\right)\,:\;\mathbb{P}\in\mathscr{P}(\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{Y}),\mathbb{P}_{1}=\pi_{1}\},

(9)

with
\mathbb{P}^{0}=\mathbb{Q}
and where we recall that
\mathbb{P}_{0},\mathbb{P}_{1}
denote the
marginals of the joint distribution
\mathbb{P}
. Note that
(\mathbb{P}^{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

is uniquely defined if
c\in\mathrm{C}(\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{Y},\mathbb{R})
, see
(
Csiszár, 1975
, Theorem
3.1)
. For discrete or compact spaces

Chen et al., 2016
;
Altschuler et al., 2017
;
Franklin and Lorenz, 1989

Assume that

For any
\pi_{0},\hat{\pi}_{0}\in\mathscr{P}(\mathsf{X})
,

\pi_{1},\hat{\pi}_{1}\in\mathscr{P}(\mathsf{Y})

and

(\pi_{0},\pi_{1})

\mathbf{W}_{1}(\mathbb{P}^{n},\hat{\mathbb{P}}^{n})\leq C\left\{\mathbf{W}_{1}(\pi_{0},\hat{\pi}_{0})+\mathbf{W}_{1}(\pi_{1},\hat{\pi}_{1})\right\},

(10)

with

C=\mathrm{e}^{17\|c\|_{\infty}}\{1+15\operatorname{Lip}(c)(\mathfrak{d}_{\mathsf{X}}+\mathfrak{d}_{\mathsf{Y}})\}.

(11)

Section

3
, establishing the quantitative stability of the
Schrödinger bridge, can be obtained by making minor modifications to the proof

Corollary 4

Assume that
c\in\operatorname{Lip}(\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{Y},\mathbb{R})

For any
\pi_{0},\hat{\pi}_{0}\in\mathscr{P}(\mathsf{X})
,

\pi_{1},\hat{\pi}_{1}\in\mathscr{P}(\mathsf{Y})

Remark 5
.

Although the constants are far from sharp, Lipschitz continuity in the marginals is the best one can hope. Indeed, for any
\mathbb{P}\in\mathbf{T}(\pi_{0},\pi_{1})
,

\hat{\mathbb{P}}\in\mathbf{T}(\hat{\pi}_{0},\hat{\pi}_{1})
we have that

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}_{1}(\mathbb{P},\hat{\mathbb{P}})

\displaystyle=\sup\left\{\int_{\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{Y}}f(x,y)\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(x,y)-\int_{\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{Y}}f(x,y)\mathrm{d}\hat{\mathbb{P}}(x,y)\,:\;f\in\operatorname{Lip}_{1}(\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{Y})\right\}

\displaystyle\geq\sup\left\{\int_{\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{Y}}f(x)\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(x,y)-\int_{\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{Y}}f(x)\mathrm{d}\hat{\mathbb{P}}(x,y)\,:\;f\in\operatorname{Lip}_{1}(\mathsf{X})\right\}\geq\mathbf{W}_{1}(\pi_{0},\hat{\pi}_{0}),

(14)

and a similar calculation shows that
\mathbf{W}_{1}(\mathbb{P},\hat{\mathbb{P}})\geq\min\{\mathbf{W}_{1}(\pi_{0},\hat{\pi}_{0}),\mathbf{W}_{1}(\pi_{1},\hat{\pi}_{1})\}
. In the case where
\hat{\pi}_{0},\hat{\pi}_{1}
, are empirical versions of
\pi_{0},\pi_{1}
respectively with
n
samples, the Lipschitz continuity in the marginals and the results by
Fournier and Guillin, 2015
also imply a sample complexity of

for learning the Schrödinger bridge when
\mathsf{X}=\mathsf{Y}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

4
Proof

The proof is divided into four parts. First, we recall that the IPFP sequence is
associated with a sequence of potentials. In
Section

4.1
we
show quantitative regularity and boundedness properties for these
potentials. The boundedness is due to a reparameterization by

Carlier and Laborde, 2020
. Then, in
Section

4.2
we recall a
contraction property and show useful Lipschitz properties of the potentials
w.r.t. the Hilbert–Birkhoff metric. We then turn to the proof of the uniform
quantitative stability of the potentials w.r.t. this metric in

Section

4.3
. Finally, in
Section

4.4
we show
how uniform quantitative bounds on the potentials translate into bounds onto
probability measures which concludes the proof.

4.1
Regularity properties of the potentials

In this section, we fix
\pi_{0}\in\mathscr{P}(\mathsf{X})
and
\pi_{1}\in\mathscr{P}(\mathsf{Y})
and
let
(\mathbb{P}^{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
the IPFP sequence given by (
8
). The
IPFP sequence can be described by a corresponding sequence of (measurable)
potentials
(\tilde{f}_{n},\tilde{g}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that for any

n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\tilde{f}_{n}:\ \mathsf{X}\to\left(0,+\infty\right)
,

\tilde{g}_{n}:\ \mathsf{Y}\to\left(0,+\infty\right)
and
\tilde{f}_{0}=\tilde{g}_{0}=1
,
see
(
Csiszár, 1975
, Theorem 3.1)
.

Proposition 6
.

For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
(x,y)\in\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{Y}
we have

\displaystyle(\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}^{2n}/\mathrm{d}\pi_{0}\otimes\pi_{1})(x,y)=\tilde{f}_{n}(x)K(x,y)\tilde{g}_{n}(y),

(15)

\displaystyle(\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}^{2n+1}/\mathrm{d}\pi_{0}\otimes\pi_{1})(x,y)=\tilde{f}_{n+1}(x)K(x,y)\tilde{g}_{n}(y),

(16)

\displaystyle\textstyle{\tilde{f}_{n+1}(x)=\left(\int_{\mathsf{Y}}K(x,y)\tilde{g}_{n}(y)\mathrm{d}\pi_{1}(y)\right)^{-1},}

(17)

\displaystyle\textstyle{\tilde{g}_{n+1}(y)=\left(\int_{\mathsf{X}}K(x,y)\tilde{f}_{n+1}(x)\mathrm{d}\pi_{0}(x)\right)^{-1}.}

(18)

For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
a_{n}>0
and
(x,y)\in\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{Y}
we have also

(\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}^{2n}/\mathrm{d}\pi_{0}\otimes\pi_{1})(x,y)=(a_{n}\tilde{f}_{n}(x))K(x,y)(\tilde{g}_{n}(y)/a_{n}).

(19)

In other words, the measure
\mathbb{P}^{2n}
is invariant w.r.t. rescaling of the
potentials
\tilde{f}_{n}
and
\tilde{g}_{n}
. This observation is at the core of
the work of
Carlier and Laborde, 2020
which proves the geometric
convergence of the IPFP w.r.t. the
\mathrm{L}^{p}
metric for bounded costs.
For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\tilde{\varphi}_{n}=\log(\tilde{f}_{n})
and

\tilde{\Psi}_{n}=\log(\tilde{g}_{n})
and let

a_{n}=\exp[-\int_{\mathsf{X}}\tilde{\varphi}_{n}(x)\mathrm{d}\pi_{0}(x)]
. Finally, for any

n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\varphi_{n}=\tilde{\varphi}_{n}+\log(a_{n})
and

\Psi_{n}=\tilde{\Psi}_{n}-\log(a_{n})
. Similarly, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
we define

f_{n}=\exp[\varphi_{n}]\;,\hskip 18.49988ptg_{n}=\exp[\Psi_{n}]\;.

(20)

The log-potentials
(\varphi_{n},\Psi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
can be computed
recursively using the following proposition.

Proposition 7
.

For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
(x,y)\in\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{Y}
we have

\displaystyle\textstyle{\varphi_{n+1}(x)=-\log\left\{\int_{\mathsf{Y}}K(x,y)\exp[\Psi_{n}(y)]\mathrm{d}\pi_{1}(y)\right\}}

(21)

\displaystyle\hskip 18.49988pt\hskip 18.49988pt\hskip 18.49988pt\textstyle{+\int_{\mathsf{X}}\log\{\int_{\mathsf{Y}}K(x,y)\exp[\Psi_{n}(y)]\mathrm{d}\pi_{1}(y)\}\mathrm{d}\pi_{0}(x),}

(22)

\displaystyle\textstyle{\Psi_{n+1}(y)=-\log\left\{\int_{\mathsf{X}}K(x,y)\exp[\varphi_{n+1}(y)]\mathrm{d}\pi_{0}(x)\right\},}

(23)

\displaystyle(\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}^{2n}/\mathrm{d}(\pi_{0}\otimes\pi_{1}))(x,y)=\exp[\varphi_{n}(x)+\Psi_{n}(y)]K(x,y).

(24)

Recall that for any
x,y\in\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{Y}
we have
K(x,y)=\exp[-c(x,y)]
.
Using
(
Carlier and Laborde, 2020
, Lemma 3.1)
we have the following result.

Proposition 8
.

For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have

\max(\|\varphi_{n}\|_{\infty},\|\Psi_{n}\|_{\infty})\leq 3\|c\|_{\infty}
.

We now establish the Lipschitz property of these potentials under the assumption that the cost function
c
is Lipschitz; this is automatically satisfied in the case where

and

Proposition 9
.

Assume that
c\in\operatorname{Lip}(\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{Y},\mathbb{R})

\max\{\operatorname{Lip}(\varphi_{n}),\operatorname{Lip}(\Psi_{n})\}\leq\operatorname{Lip}(c).

(25)

Proof.

Using
Section

4.1
and the fact that

c\in\operatorname{Lip}(\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{Y},\mathbb{R})
, we have for any
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathsf{X}

\displaystyle\varphi_{n+1}(x)-\varphi_{n+1}(x^{\prime})

(26)

\displaystyle=\log\left\{\int_{\mathsf{Y}}K(x^{\prime},y)\exp[\Psi_{n}(y)]\mathrm{d}\pi_{1}(y)/\int_{\mathsf{Y}}K(x,y)\exp[\Psi_{n}(y)]\mathrm{d}\pi_{1}(y)\right\}

(27)

\displaystyle=\log\left\{\int_{\mathsf{Y}}\exp[-c(x,y)+c(x,y)-c(x^{\prime},y)+\Psi_{n}(y)]\mathrm{d}\pi_{1}(y)\right\}

(28)

\displaystyle\hskip 18.49988pt-\log\left\{\int_{\mathsf{Y}}\exp[-c(x,y+\Psi_{n}(y)]\mathrm{d}\pi_{1}(y)\right\}

(29)

\displaystyle\leq\log\left\{\int_{\mathsf{Y}}\exp[-c(x,y)+\operatorname{Lip}(c)d_{\mathsf{X}}(x,x^{\prime})+\Psi_{n}(y)]\mathrm{d}\pi_{1}(y)\right\}

(30)

\displaystyle\hskip 18.49988pt-\log\left\{\int_{\mathsf{Y}}\exp[-c(x,y)+\Psi_{n}(y)]\mathrm{d}\pi_{1}(y)\right\}

(31)

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{Lip}(c)d_{\mathsf{X}}(x,x^{\prime}).

(32)

Similarly we obtain that for any

\displaystyle\Psi_{n+1}(y^{\prime})-\Psi_{n+1}(y)

4.2
The Hilbert–Birkhoff metric, contraction and Lipschitz properties

We now recall basic properties of the Hilbert–Birkhoff metric; see
Lemmens and Nussbaum, 2014
;
Kohlberg and Pratt, 1982
;
Bushell, 1973
for a review. Let
\mathsf{E}
be a real vector space and

\mathsf{K}
a cone in this vector space,
i.e.

\mathsf{K}
is convex,
\mathsf{K}\cap(-\mathsf{K})=\{0\}
and
\lambda\mathsf{K}\subset\mathsf{K}
for any

\lambda\geq 0
. In what
follows, we let
\mathsf{C}
be a part of the cone
i.e.
for any
x,y\in\mathsf{C}
there
exist
\alpha,\beta\geq 0
such that
\alpha x-y\in\mathsf{K}
and

\beta y-x\in\mathsf{K}
. In addition, assume that
\mathsf{C}
is convex and that
for any
\lambda>0

\lambda\mathsf{K}\subset\mathsf{K}
. In this case we have for
any
x,y\in\mathsf{C}
that

M(x,y)=\inf\{\beta\geq 0\,:\;\beta y-x\in\mathsf{K}\}>0.

(41)

Similarly we define for any
x,y\in\mathsf{C}

m(x,y)=\sup\{\alpha\geq 0\,:\;x-\alpha y\in\mathsf{K}\}.

(42)

Note that
m(x,y)=M(y,x)^{-1}>0
. Finally, the Hilbert–Birkhoff metric is
defined for any
x,y\in\mathsf{C}
by

d_{H}(x,y)=\log(M(x,y)/m(x,y)).

(43)

By
(
Lemmens and Nussbaum, 2014
, Lemma 2.1)
,
d_{H}
is a metric on
\mathsf{C}/\sim
the
space
\mathsf{C}
quotiented by the equivalence relation:
x\sim y
if there exists

\lambda>0
such that
y=\lambda x
. In particular, if
\|\cdot\|

is a norm on
\mathsf{V}
then letting

\tilde{\mathsf{C}}=\{x\in\mathsf{C}\,:\;\|x\|=1\}
, we have that

(\tilde{\mathsf{C}},d_{H})
is a metric space.

Let
(\mathsf{V},\|\cdot\|)
and
(\mathsf{V}^{\prime},\|\cdot\|^{\prime})
be two normed
real vector space and
\mathsf{K}\subset\mathsf{V}
,
\mathsf{K}^{\prime}\subset\mathsf{V}^{\prime}
two cones. In
addition, let
\mathsf{C}
and
\mathsf{C}^{\prime}
be convex parts of
\mathsf{K}
and

\mathsf{K}^{\prime}
respectively, such that for any
\lambda>0
,
\lambda\mathsf{C}\subset\mathsf{C}
and

\lambda\mathsf{C}^{\prime}\subset\mathsf{C}^{\prime}
. Let
u:\ \mathsf{V}\to\mathsf{V}^{\prime}
be a linear mapping
such that
u(\mathsf{C})\subset\mathsf{C}^{\prime}
. The projective diameter of
u
is given by

\Delta(u)=\sup\{d_{H}(u(x),u(y))\,:\;x,y\in\tilde{\mathsf{C}}\}.

(44)

Similarly, we also define the Birkhoff contraction ratio of
u

\kappa(u)=\sup\{\kappa\,:\;d_{H}(u(x),u(y))\leq\kappa d_{H}(x,y),x,y\in\tilde{\mathsf{C}}\}.

(45)

Using the Birkhoff contraction theorem
(
Birkhoff, 1957
;
Bauer, 1965
;
Hopf, 1963
)
we have that

\kappa(u)\leq\tanh(\Delta(u)/4).

(46)

In order to use the Birkhoff contraction theorem, we collect the following basic
facts on cones in function spaces.

Proposition 12
.

Let
\mathsf{Z}
be a compact space.
\mathsf{F}=\left[0,+\infty\right)^{\mathsf{Z}}
is a cone and

\tilde{\mathsf{F}}=\mathrm{C}(\mathsf{Z},\left(0,+\infty\right))
is a convex part of
\mathsf{F}
such that
for any
\lambda>0
,
\lambda\tilde{\mathsf{F}}\subset\tilde{\mathsf{F}}
. In addition, we have
that for any
f,g\in\tilde{\mathsf{F}}

(47)

In this case, we have that for any
f,g\in\tilde{\mathsf{F}}
,
d_{H}(f,g)
is the
oscillation of
\log(f/g)
. In what follows, we introduce key mappings which
allow us to compute the IPFP potential
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and

\displaystyle\textstyle{g_{n+1}(y)=\left(\int_{\mathsf{X}}K(x,y)f_{n+1}(x)\mathrm{d}\pi_{0}(x)\right)^{-1}.}

(50)

Let
\pi_{0}\in\mathscr{P}(\mathsf{X})
and
\pi_{1}\in\mathscr{P}(\mathsf{Y})

d_{H}(f_{n+1},\hat{f}_{n+1})\leq\tanh(\left\|c\right\|_{\infty})d_{H}(f_{n},\hat{f}_{n})+4\operatorname{Lip}(c)\mathrm{e}^{8\left\|c\right\|_{\infty}}(\mathbf{W}_{1}(\pi_{0},\hat{\pi}_{0})+\mathbf{W}_{1}(\pi_{1},\hat{\pi}_{1})).

(88)

Since
d_{H}(f_{0},\hat{f}_{0})=0
we have that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}

\displaystyle d_{H}(f_{n+1},\hat{f}_{n+1})

\displaystyle\textstyle{\leq 4\operatorname{Lip}(c)\mathrm{e}^{8\left\|c\right\|_{\infty}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\tanh(\left\|c\right\|_{\infty})^{k}(\mathbf{W}_{1}(\pi_{0},\hat{\pi}_{0})+\mathbf{W}_{1}(\pi_{1},\hat{\pi}_{1}))}

(89)

\displaystyle\textstyle{\leq 4\operatorname{Lip}(c)\mathrm{e}^{8\left\|c\right\|_{\infty}}(1-\tanh(\|c\|_{\infty}))^{-1}(\mathbf{W}_{1}(\pi_{0},\hat{\pi}_{0})+\mathbf{W}_{1}(\pi_{1},\hat{\pi}_{1}))}

(90)

\displaystyle\textstyle{\leq 2\operatorname{Lip}(c)\mathrm{e}^{8\left\|c\right\|_{\infty}}(1+\mathrm{e}^{2\left\|c\right\|_{\infty}})(\mathbf{W}_{1}(\pi_{0},\hat{\pi}_{0})+\mathbf{W}_{1}(\pi_{1},\hat{\pi}_{1}))}

(91)

\displaystyle\textstyle{\leq 4\operatorname{Lip}(c)\mathrm{e}^{10\left\|c\right\|_{\infty}}(\mathbf{W}_{1}(\pi_{0},\hat{\pi}_{0})+\mathbf{W}_{1}(\pi_{1},\hat{\pi}_{1})).}

(92)

Similarly, we get that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}

\textstyle{d_{H}(g_{n},\hat{g}_{n}})\leq 4\operatorname{Lip}(c)\mathrm{e}^{10\left\|c\right\|_{\infty}}(\mathbf{W}_{1}(\pi_{0},\hat{\pi}_{0})+\mathbf{W}_{1}(\pi_{1},\hat{\pi}_{1})).

(93)

Combining (
74
), (
92
) and (
93
) concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma 16
.

For any
\pi_{0},\hat{\pi}_{0}\in\mathscr{P}(\mathsf{X})
,

\pi_{1},\hat{\pi}_{1}\in\mathscr{P}(\mathsf{Y})
let
(f_{n},g_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and

(\hat{f}_{n},\hat{g}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
the rescaled IPFP potential associated
with
(\pi_{0},\pi_{1})
, respectively
(\hat{\pi}_{0},\hat{\pi}_{1})
and given by
(
48
). Then, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
there exist

x_{n}^{\dagger}\in\mathsf{X}
and
y_{n}^{\dagger}\in\mathsf{Y}
such that

\displaystyle|f_{n}(x_{n}^{\dagger})g_{n}(y_{n}^{\dagger})/(\hat{f}_{n}(x_{n}^{\dagger})\hat{g}_{n}(y_{n}^{\dagger}))-1|\leq 4\operatorname{Lip}(c)\mathrm{e}^{10\left\|c\right\|_{\infty}}(\mathbf{W}_{1}(\pi_{0},\hat{\pi}_{0})+\mathbf{W}_{1}(\pi_{1},\hat{\pi}_{1})).

(94)

Proof.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and

\Delta:=4\operatorname{Lip}(c)\mathrm{e}^{10\left\|c\right\|_{\infty}}(\mathbf{W}_{1}(\pi_{0},\hat{\pi}_{0})+\mathbf{W}_{1}(\pi_{1},\hat{\pi}_{1})).

Using that
\mathbb{P}^{2n}(\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{Y})=\hat{\mathbb{P}}^{2n}(\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{Y})=1
, we have

\displaystyle\textstyle{\int_{\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{Y}}\{f_{n}(x)g_{n}(y)/(\hat{f}_{n}(x)\hat{g}_{n}(y))-1\}\hat{f}_{n}(x)\hat{g}_{n}(y)\mathrm{d}\hat{\pi}_{0}(x)\mathrm{d}\hat{\pi}_{1}(y)}

\displaystyle\hskip 18.49988pt\hskip 18.49988pt\hskip 18.49988pt=\textstyle{\int_{\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{Y}}f_{n}(x)g_{n}(y)\mathrm{d}\hat{\pi}_{0}(x)\mathrm{d}\hat{\pi}_{1}(y)}-1

\displaystyle\hskip 18.49988pt\hskip 18.49988pt\hskip 18.49988pt=\textstyle{\int_{\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{Y}}f_{n}(x)g_{n}(y)\mathrm{d}\hat{\pi}_{0}(x)\mathrm{d}\hat{\pi}_{1}(y)-\int_{\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{Y}}f_{n}(x)g_{n}(y)\mathrm{d}\pi_{0}(x)\mathrm{d}\pi_{1}(y)}.

4.1

\operatorname{Lip}(f_{n}g_{n})\leq\|f_{n}\|_{\infty}\operatorname{Lip}(g_{n})+\|g_{n}\|_{\infty}\operatorname{Lip}(f_{n})\leq 2\operatorname{Lip}(c)\mathrm{e}^{6\|c\|_{\infty}}.

Combining this result, (

\mathbf{W}_{1}(\pi_{0}\otimes\pi_{1},\hat{\pi}_{0}\otimes\hat{\pi}_{1})\leq\mathbf{W}_{1}(\pi_{0},\hat{\pi}_{0})+\mathbf{W}_{1}(\pi_{1},\hat{\pi}_{1})

\displaystyle\left|\textstyle{\int_{\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{Y}}\{f_{n}(x)g_{n}(y)/(\hat{f}_{n}(x)\hat{g}_{n}(y))-1\}\mathrm{d}\hat{\mathbb{P}}^{2n}(x,y)}\right|

(99)

\displaystyle\hskip 18.49988pt\leq 2\operatorname{Lip}(c)\mathrm{e}^{6\|c\|_{\infty}}\mathbf{W}_{1}(\pi_{0}\otimes\pi_{1},\hat{\pi}_{0}\otimes\hat{\pi}_{1})

(100)

\displaystyle\hskip 18.49988pt\leq 2\operatorname{Lip}(c)\mathrm{e}^{6\|c\|_{\infty}}(\mathbf{W}_{1}(\pi_{0},\hat{\pi}_{0})+\mathbf{W}_{1}(\pi_{1},\hat{\pi}_{1}))<\Delta/2.

(101)

Therefore

\displaystyle 1-\Delta/2\leq\textstyle{\int_{\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{Y}}f_{n}(x)g_{n}(y)/(\hat{f}_{n}(x)\hat{g}_{n}(y))\mathrm{d}\hat{\mathbb{P}}^{2n}(x,y)\leq 1+\Delta/2.}

(102)

Assume that for any
(x,y)\in\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{Y}
we have that

|f_{n}(x)g_{n}(y)/(\hat{f}_{n}(x)\hat{g}_{n}(y))-1|>\Delta.

(103)

Combining this with (
102
) there exist

(x_{n}^{+},y_{n}^{+})\in\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{Y}
and
(x_{n}^{-},y_{n}^{-})\in\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{Y}

\displaystyle f_{n}(x_{n}^{+})g_{n}(y_{n}^{+})/(\hat{f}_{n}(x_{n}^{+})\hat{g}_{n}(y_{n}^{+}))>1+\Delta,\hskip 9.24994ptf_{n}(x_{n}^{-})g_{n}(y_{n}^{-})/(\hat{f}_{n}(x_{n}^{-})\hat{g}_{n}(y_{n}^{-}))<1-\Delta,

(104)

whence it follows that
\Delta<1
.
Therefore, by
Theorem

15
we get that

\displaystyle d_{H}(f_{n}g_{n},\hat{f}_{n}\hat{g}_{n})

(105)

\displaystyle\hskip 18.49988pt\geq\log(f_{n}(x_{n}^{+})g_{n}(y_{n}^{+})/(\hat{f}_{n}(x_{n}^{+})\hat{g}_{n}(y_{n}^{+})))-\log(f_{n}(x_{n}^{-})g_{n}(y_{n}^{-})/(\hat{f}_{n}(x_{n}^{-})\hat{g}_{n}(y_{n}^{-})))

\displaystyle\hskip 18.49988pt>\log((1+\Delta)/(1-\Delta))\geq 2\Delta\geq d_{H}(f_{n}g_{n},\hat{f}_{n}\hat{g}_{n}),

(107)

which is absurd.

Proof of

17

(126)

Therefore, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}_{1}(\mathbb{P}^{2n},\hat{\mathbb{P}}^{2n})

\displaystyle=\sup\{\textstyle{\int_{\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{Y}}F(x,y)\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}^{2n}(x,y)-\int_{\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{Y}}F(x,y)\mathrm{d}\hat{\mathbb{P}}^{2n}(x,y)}\,:\;F\in\mathrm{Lip}\}

(127)

\displaystyle=\sup\{\textstyle{\int_{\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{Y}}F(x,y)\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}^{2n}(x,y)-\int_{\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{Y}}F(x,y)\mathrm{d}\hat{\mathbb{P}}^{2n}(x,y)}\,:\;F\in\mathrm{Lip}^{\star}\}

(128)

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{e}^{17\|c\|_{\infty}}\{1+15\operatorname{Lip}(c)(\mathfrak{d}_{\mathsf{X}}+\mathfrak{d}_{\mathsf{Y}})\}(\mathbf{W}_{1}(\pi_{0},\hat{\pi}_{0})+\mathbf{W}_{1}(\pi_{1},\hat{\pi}_{1})).\qed

(129)

Proof of
Section

3
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Using

\displaystyle d_{H}(f_{n+1}g_{n+1},f_{n}g_{n})

Section

4.3

\int_{\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{Y}}[f_{n+1}(x)g_{n+1}(y)/(f_{n}(x)g_{n}(y))]K(x,y)f_{n}(x)g_{n}(y)\mathrm{d}\pi_{0}(x)\mathrm{d}\pi_{1}(y)=1.

. Combining this
with (
131
), we get that there exist

(x^{+}_{n},y^{+}_{n}),(x^{-}_{n},y^{-}_{n})\in\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{Y}

\Delta<1

\displaystyle d_{H}(f_{n+1}g_{n+1},f_{n}g_{n})

This is absurd, hence there exists
(x^{\star},y^{\star})\in\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{Y}

\displaystyle\hskip 18.49988pt\leq\left|\log\left(f_{n+1}(x^{\star})g_{n+1}(y^{\star})/(f_{n}(x^{\star})g_{n}(y^{\star}))\right)\right|+d_{H}(f_{n+1}g_{n+1},f_{n}g_{n})

(138)

\displaystyle\hskip 18.49988pt\leq 2\kappa^{n}\left[d_{H}(f_{1},f_{0})+d_{H}(g_{1},g_{0})\right].

Combining this result,
Section

4.1
and the fact that for any

and

\displaystyle|f_{n+1}(x)g_{n+1}(y)-f_{n}(x)g_{n}(y)|

(140)

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathrm{e}^{6\|c\|_{\infty}}\kappa^{n}\left[d_{H}(f_{1},f_{0})+d_{H}(g_{1},g_{0})\right].

(141)

F\in\operatorname{Lip}_{1}(\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{Y},\mathbb{R})
,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathsf{X}\times\mathsf{Y}}F(x,y)f_{n+1}(x)g_{n+1}(y)K(x,y)\mathrm{d}\pi_{0}(x)\mathrm{d}\pi_{1}(y)

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt\leq 2(\mathfrak{d}_{\mathsf{X}}+\mathfrak{d}_{\mathsf{Y}})\mathrm{e}^{7\|c\|_{\infty}}\kappa^{n}\left[d_{H}(f_{1},f_{0})+d_{H}(g_{1},g_{0})\right].

(146)

\displaystyle\leq C\left\{\mathbf{W}_{1}(\pi_{0},\hat{\pi}_{0})+\mathbf{W}_{1}(\pi_{1},\hat{\pi}_{1})\right\}+\mathbf{W}_{1}(\mathbb{P}^{\ast},\mathbb{P}^{n})+\mathbf{W}_{1}(\hat{\mathbb{P}}^{n},\hat{\mathbb{P}}^{\ast}).

We conclude upon letting
