Port-Hamiltonian Dynamic Mode Decomposition
Riccardo Morandin † and Jonas Nicodemus ⋆ and Benjamin Unger ⋆

Abstract
. We present a novel physics-informed system identification method to construct a passive linear time-invariant system. In more detail, for a given quadratic energy functional, measurements of the input, state, and output of a system in the time domain, we find a realization that approximates the data well while guaranteeing that the energy functional satisfies a dissipation inequality. To this end, we use the framework of port-Hamiltonian (pH) systems and modify the dynamic mode decomposition, respectively operator inference, to be feasible for continuous-time pH systems. We propose an iterative numerical method to solve the corresponding least-squares minimization problem. We construct an effective initialization of the algorithm by studying the least-squares problem in a weighted norm, for which we present the analytical minimum-norm solution. The efficiency of the proposed method is demonstrated with several numerical examples.



Keywords:
dynamic mode decomposition, port-Hamiltonian systems, system identification, dissipation inequality, passivity, knowledge-driven realization

37J06,37M99,65P10,93A30,93B30,93C05

1.
Introduction

Incorporating prior knowledge into modern learning architectures becomes increasingly important in several applications. Such knowledge-driven or physics-informed approaches
[
26
,
39
]
exploit expert knowledge during the learning process, either by optimizing only over a suitable set of candidate functions, or by using physical information in the cost functional. In our work, we deal with data from physical systems with the goal of identifying a linear dynamical system, which we refer to as a realization that approximates the data as well as possible with respect to the Frobenius norm. In more detail, assume that we have measurements
(u(t_{i}),x(t_{i}),y(t_{i}))\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
at time instances
t_{i}
for
i=0,\ldots,M
. Then, we want to determine matrices
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
,
B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
,
C\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, and
D\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
such that the data can be approximately recovered by the linear time-invariant system

(1.1)

\displaystyle\dot{x}

\displaystyle=Ax+Bu,

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle=Cx+Du.

Since we assume that the data is based on a physical process, we want to ensure that the realization satisfies a dissipation inequality, i.e., that the rate of change of the energy associated with the system is bounded by the externally supplied energy. We thus incorporate physical knowledge by prescribing the (quadratic) energy functional

(1.2)

\mathcal{H}(x)\vcentcolon=\tfrac{1}{2}x^{\mathsf{T}}Hx

with symmetric positive definite matrix
H\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
. Our main goal is then to determine the matrices in (
1.1
) such that any solution of (
1.1
) satisfies the dissipation inequality

(1.3)

\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{H}(x(t))\leq y(t)^{\mathsf{T}}u(t)

for any
t
. One of the main advantages of requiring the learned model to satisfy a dissipation inequality is that whenever the model is coupled with another passive model via a
power-conserving or dissipative interconnection, then the coupled model is also passive. Moreover, since the Hamiltonian also serves as a Lyapunov function, we are guaranteed that the identified system is stable (independent of the underlying physical system and the quality of the measurements). Our framework can thus be used to guarantee the physical behavior of coupled first-principle and purely data-inferred dynamical systems. To achieve this goal, we use the framework of
port-Hamiltonian
(
pH
) systems
[
24
,
50
]
and modify the
dynamic mode decomposition
(
DMD
)
[
41
,
49
,
28
]
and
operator inference
(
OI
)
[
35
]
accordingly. Our main contributions are the following:

(i)

Since
DMD
is designed to compute a discrete-time dynamical system, we follow
[
27
,
30
]
and present a definition of a discrete-time
pH
system in
section

2.2
, which is motivated from the structure-preserving time-discretization of a continuous-time
pH
system. The corresponding modified
DMD
optimization problem is formulated in
3.1
.

(ii)

Although
3.1
is convex and solvable, a closed-form solution formula is not immediately available. Instead, we propose an iterative method (
Algorithm

1
) combining the result of a skew-symmetric Procrustes problem
[
15
]
with a projected fast gradient method for a positive semidefinite Procrustes problem
[
17
]
.

(iii)

For an efficient initialization of
Algorithm

1
, we consider a weighted Frobenius norm, where we weight the problem according to the relevant information in the data, see the forthcoming
Section

3.3
. The analytic minimum-norm solution of the weighted problem is then presented in
Theorem

3.7
and used, up to some modification, as initialization for our iterative method.

Literature review

The construction of a realization of the form (

) from data is a well-studied subject with many popular approaches. We mention the eigensystem realization algorithm
[

]
, the Loewner framework
[

[

, neural ordinary differential equations
[

]
,

[

]
, and

[
28
]
. Introducing expert knowledge to these approaches is not a novel idea and may even be the key idea to quantifying the error between the realization and the true physical system, cf.
[
22
,
23
]
. Exploiting expert knowledge in the identification of linear systems is reported in various applications and methods. For instance, the specific structure of mechanical systems is exploited in a vector fitting framework in
[
51
]
. The authors of
[
42
,
43
]
exploit the fact that certain wave-type phenomena can be represented with delay equations to construct accurate linear time-invariant surrogate models from data. In the context of the Navier-Stokes equation, the specific structure of the (semi-discretized) equations is exploited in an

[
7

[
48

47

. Similar ideas are discussed in the context of

in
[
4
]
by restricting the discrete-time iteration matrix to specific manifolds. In essence, our method relies on a similar idea but with a specific structure of the iteration matrix not discussed in
[
4
]
.

As detailed above, we use the framework of

systems to achieve our goal of identifying a system that satisfies a dissipation inequality. In contrast to our approach, most existing results on learning linear time-invariant

44

46

to approximately mimic an

. Instead of directly identifying a

14

We mention that many of the identification methods discussed above are closely related to intrusive structure-preserving model order reduction methods for

[
37
]
, tangential interpolation
[

,

,

]
, generalized balancing

,

,

-and

1.3.
Notation

We use the symbols
\mathbb{N}
,
\mathbb{R}
,
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
to denote the positive integers, the real numbers, the set of columns vectors with
n\in\mathbb{N}
real entries, and the set of
n\times m
real matrices, respectively. For a matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
we denote its transpose with
A^{\mathsf{T}}
and its Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse with
A^{\dagger}
. A matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is called symmetric (skew-symmetric), if
A=A^{\mathsf{T}}
(
A=-A^{\mathsf{T}}
). The decomposition of a squared matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
into its skew-symmetric resp. symmetric part is denoted as

\Sym(A)=\tfrac{1}{2}(A+A^{\mathsf{T}}),\qquad\mathrm{resp.}\qquad\Skew(A)=\tfrac{1}{2}(A-A^{\mathsf{T}}).

The sets of all
n\times n
symmetric positive definite and symmetric positive semi-definite matrices with real entries are denoted with
\mathcal{S}^{n}_{\succ}
and
\mathcal{S}^{n}_{\succeq}
.
The projection
\mathcal{P}_{\succeq}(A)
of a matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
onto
\mathcal{S}^{n}_{\succeq}
is given by

\mathcal{P}_{\succeq}(A)=\Xi\diag(\Lambda_{+},0,0)\Xi^{\mathsf{T}},

where
\Sym(A)=\Xi\diag(\Lambda_{+},\Lambda_{-},0)\Xi^{\mathsf{T}}
is the ordered eigendecomposition of the symmetric part of
A
. Here,
\Lambda_{+}
and
\Lambda_{-}
are the diagonal matrices containing the positive and negative eigenvalues, respectively.
The set of nonsingular matrices of size
n\times n
is denoted with
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
.
The Stiefel manifold of
n\times r
dimensional matrices with real entries is denoted by

\mathrm{St}(n,r)\vcentcolon=\left\{U\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}\mid U^{\mathsf{T}}U=I_{r}\right\},

where
I_{r}
denotes the
r\times r
identity matrix.
Furthermore, given
\Omega\in\mathcal{S}^{n}_{\succeq}
we denote the weighted Frobenius semi-norm as

(1.4)

\left\|A\right\|_{\Omega}\vcentcolon=\sqrt{\trace{(A^{\mathsf{T}}\Omega A)}}.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Dynamic Mode Decomposition and Operator Inference

Assume data triples
(u_{i},x_{i},y_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}
(
DMD
) or data quadruples
(u_{i},x_{i},\dot{x}_{i},y_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}
(
OI
) for
i=0,\ldots,M
of a dynamical system available, which may be obtained from measurements of real phenomena or the simulation of a model. The goal of (input-output)
DMD

[
38
,
2
]
or
OI

[
35
]
is to find matrices
\widetilde{A},\widetilde{B},\widetilde{C},\widetilde{D}
of suitable size minimizing

(2.1)

\min_{\widetilde{A},\widetilde{B},\widetilde{C},\widetilde{D}}\sum_{i=0}^{M-1}\|\Delta x_{i}-\widetilde{A}x_{i}-\widetilde{B}u_{i}\|_{2}^{2}+\|y_{i}-\widetilde{C}x_{i}-\widetilde{D}u_{i}\|_{2}^{2},

where
\Delta x_{i}\vcentcolon=\dot{x}_{i}
if we assume derivative information of the state to be available, and
\Delta x_{i}\vcentcolon=x_{i+1}
, otherwise. The goal of solving (
2.1
) is to determine a linear system of the form

(2.2)

\displaystyle\Delta\widetilde{x}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{A}\widetilde{x}+\widetilde{B}u,

\displaystyle\widetilde{y}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{C}\widetilde{x}+\widetilde{D}u,

that best approximates the data (in the sense of (
2.1
)), where
\Delta
denotes the differentiation operator with respect to time if we are in the continuous time setting described by
OI
, and
\Delta
denotes the forward shift in the discrete-time setting used in
DMD
.
This is conveniently achieved by introducing the matrices

\displaystyle Z_{0}\vcentcolon=\begin{bmatrix}x_{0}&\ldots&x_{M-1}\\
u_{0}&\ldots&u_{M-1}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{(n+m)\times M}\qquad\text{and}\qquad Z_{1}\vcentcolon=\begin{bmatrix}\Delta x_{0}&\ldots&\Delta x_{M-1}\\
y_{0}&\ldots&y_{M-1}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{(n+p)\times M}

and studying the equivalent problem

(2.3)

\min_{\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{(n+p)\times(n+m)}}\left\|Z_{1}-\mathcal{A}Z_{0}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}},

whose solution, conformably partitioned, yields the matrices
\widetilde{A},\widetilde{B},\widetilde{C}
, and
\widetilde{D}
. The minimum norm solution
\mathcal{A}^{\star}
of (
2.3
) is given by the Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse of the data, i.e.,
\mathcal{A}^{\star}=Z_{1}Z_{0}^{\dagger}
. We emphasize that this is the unique solution of (
2.3
) if and only if
r\vcentcolon=\rank(Z_{0})=n+m
. The pseudo-inverse can be computed efficiently using the
singular value decomposition
(
SVD
). In more detail, set
r\vcentcolon=\rank(Z_{0})
and let
Z_{0}=V\Sigma W^{\mathsf{T}}
with
V\in\mathrm{St}(n+m,r)
,
\Sigma\in\mathrm{GL}_{r}(\mathbb{R})
,
W\in\mathrm{St}(M,r)
denote the skinny
SVD
of
Z_{0}
. Then,
Z_{0}^{\dagger}=W\Sigma^{-1}V
. Note that from a numerical point of view, we truncate singular values below a given tolerance to ensure that the inverse
\Sigma^{-1}
, respectively the associated linear system, can be computed accurately. Such a truncation is equivalent to adding a regularization term to the minimization problem (
2.3
), see
[
9
]
for further details.

Remark 2.1
.

If no measurements of the derivative are available, then a classical finite difference approach of the form

(2.4)

\displaystyle\Delta x_{i}\approx\dot{\hat{x}}_{i}\vcentcolon=\frac{x_{i+1}-x_{i}}{\delta_{i}},

may be used as an approximation, where
\delta_{i}
is the time step between the snapshots. This is a common approach in the literature, see, e.g.,
[
35
]
, for which a convergence result for
\delta_{i}\to 0
is available
[
35
, Thm. 1]
. Throughout this manuscript, we do not assume
\dot{x}_{i}
available, and hence work with the approximation (
2.4
), instead.

The method can be further extended to include dimensionality reduction, which is then called
reduced
OI

[
35
]
. There, a low dimensional basis for the state space must is constructed. Afterwards, the data is projected onto this low-dimensional basis. Finally, (
2.1
) is solved for the reduced data, which results in a
reduced order model
.
The low dimensional basis generation can be achieved, for instance, by applying the
SVD
to the state data, i.e., let
X=V\Sigma W
denote the skinny
SVD
of
X
. Then the reduced basis
\Phi
of size
r
can be defined as the first
r
columns of
V
.

2.2.
Port-Hamiltonian system

As outlined in the introduction,
pH
systems are a promising modeling tool that generalize the notion of Hamiltonian systems to allow for interaction with the environment. Since the aim of
DMD
is to produce a linear system, we review the
pH
framework only for linear dynamics and refer the reader for a more general definition to
[
50
,
31
]
.

Definition 2.2

(Linear time-invariant
pH
system)
.

Assume that we have given a quadratic function
\mathcal{H}(x)=\tfrac{1}{2}x^{\mathsf{T}}\widehat{H}x
, called the
Hamiltonian
, with
\widehat{H}\in\mathcal{S}^{n}_{\succ}
and a suitable factorization
\widehat{H}=E^{\mathsf{T}}Q
with
E,Q\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
. Then the system

(2.5)

\begin{bmatrix}E\dot{x}\\
y\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}Qx\\
u\end{bmatrix},\qquad\begin{bmatrix}A&B\\
-C&-D\end{bmatrix}=\mathcal{J}-\mathcal{R},

with
\mathcal{J}=-\mathcal{J}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{(n+m)\times(n+m)}
and
\mathcal{R}\in\mathcal{S}^{n+m}_{\succeq}
is called a
pH
system
.

In
Definition

2.2
, the matrix
\mathcal{J}
represents the conservation of energy, while
\mathcal{R}
describes dissipation.
Note that the specific structure requires the input and output dimension to match and directly implies that
pH
systems are stable and passive, and the Hamiltonian serves as a Lyapunov function. In more detail, any
pH
system together with its Hamiltonian satisfies the dissipation inequality (
1.3
). Conversely, any passive linear time-invariant system, i.e., any system for which a quadratic Hamiltonian exists such that the system with this Hamiltonian satisfies the dissipation inequality (
1.3
), has a
pH
representation, cf.
[
5
]
.
Every
pH
system (
2.5
) can be equivalently written as

(2.6a)

\displaystyle E\dot{x}

\displaystyle=(J-R)Qx+(G-P)u,

(2.6b)

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle=(G+P)^{\mathsf{T}}Qx+(S-N)u,

with
J=-J^{\top}
,
N=-N^{\top}
, and

\mathcal{R}=\begin{bmatrix}R&P\\
P^{\mathsf{T}}&S\end{bmatrix}\in\mathcal{S}^{n+m}_{\succeq}.

For further details on the properties that are encoded within this structure we refer to
[
6
]
.
Multiplication of (
2.6a
) from the left with
Q^{\mathsf{T}}
, see
[
31
, Sec. 4.3]
, and introducing the matrices

\displaystyle\widehat{J}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=Q^{\mathsf{T}}JQ,

\displaystyle\widehat{R}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=Q^{\mathsf{T}}RQ,

\displaystyle\widehat{G}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=Q^{\mathsf{T}}G,

\displaystyle\widehat{P}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=Q^{\mathsf{T}}P,

\displaystyle\widehat{S}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=S,

\displaystyle\widehat{N}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=N

allows us to rewrite (
2.6
) as

(2.7a)

\displaystyle\widehat{H}\dot{x}

\displaystyle=(\widehat{J}-\widehat{R})x+(\widehat{G}-\widehat{P})u,

(2.7b)

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle=(\widehat{G}+\widehat{P})^{\mathsf{T}}x+(\widehat{S}-\widehat{N})u,

which is linear in the system matrices. Due to the congruence transformation, we immediately conclude
\widehat{J}=-\widehat{J}^{\mathsf{T}}
,
\widehat{N}=-\widehat{N}^{\mathsf{T}}
and

\widehat{\mathcal{R}}=\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{R}&\widehat{P}\\
\widehat{P}^{\mathsf{T}}&\widehat{S}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathcal{S}^{n+m}_{\succeq}.

Remark 2.3
.

Let us emphasize that from a mathematical perspective, the choice of the energy functional yielding to a
pH
system is generally not unique. Indeed, any solution of the corresponding Kalman-Yakubovich-Popov inequality can be used as the Hessian of the energy; see
[
5
]
for further details. Furthermore, recent results detail that the choice of the energy functional characterizes how easy it is to approximate the system
[
12
]
.

To understand
pH
systems in the framework of
DMD
, we need to find a discrete-time analogue of (
2.7
). Since symplectic Gauss-Legendre collocation methods are able to preserve the underlying Dirac structure of the
pH
system
[
27
,
30
]
, and
DMD
is able to recover the original dynamics if the
DMD
approximation is based on a discretization with a 1-stage Runge-Kutta method
[
20
]
, we use the implicit midpoint rule for the time-discretization.
More precisely, for a constant step size
\delta_{t}>0
, the continuous dynamics (
2.7a
) are replaced with the discrete dynamics

\widehat{H}\tfrac{x_{i+1}-x_{i}}{\delta_{t}}=(\widehat{J}-\widehat{R})\tfrac{x_{i+1}+x_{i}}{2}+(\widehat{G}-\widehat{P})\tfrac{u_{i+1}+u_{i}}{2},

with
x_{i}\approx x(i\delta_{t})
, and
u_{i}\vcentcolon=u(i\delta_{t})
. Note that with respect to our goal to generate a discrete-time
pH
system from data, we have replaced
u((i+\tfrac{1}{2})\delta_{t})
from the implicit midpoint rule with the approximation

u((i+\tfrac{1}{2})\delta_{t})\approx\tfrac{u_{i+1}+u_{i}}{2}.

It is natural to also replace the continuous output equation (
2.6b
) with its discrete counterpart using
y_{i}\approx y(i\delta_{t})
. To preserve port-Hamiltonian structure, we take the average of consecutive output equations arriving at

\tfrac{y_{i+1}+y_{i}}{2}=(\widehat{G}+\widehat{P})^{\mathsf{T}}\tfrac{x_{i+1}+x_{i}}{2}+(\widehat{S}-\widehat{N})\tfrac{u_{i+1}+u_{i}}{2}.

The previous analysis motivates the following definition.

Definition 2.4

(discrete-time
pH
system)
.

Consider sequences of states
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, inputs
u_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, outputs
y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and a constant time step
\delta_{t}>0
.
We call a system of the form

(2.8a)

\displaystyle\widehat{H}\tfrac{x_{i+1}-x_{i}}{\delta_{t}}

\displaystyle=(\widehat{J}-\widehat{R})\tfrac{x_{i+1}+x_{i}}{2}+(\widehat{G}-\widehat{P})\tfrac{u_{i+1}+u_{i}}{2},

(2.8b)

\displaystyle\tfrac{y_{i+1}+y_{i}}{2}

\displaystyle=(\widehat{G}+\widehat{P})^{\mathsf{T}}\tfrac{x_{i+1}+x_{i}}{2}+(\widehat{S}-\widehat{N})\tfrac{u_{i+1}+u_{i}}{2},

a
discrete-time
pH
system
with quadratic Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}(x)\vcentcolon=\tfrac{1}{2}x^{\mathsf{T}}\widehat{H}x
if and only if
\widehat{J}=-\widehat{J}^{\mathsf{T}}
,
\widehat{N}=-\widehat{N}^{\mathsf{T}}
, and
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\widehat{R}&\widehat{P}\\
\widehat{P}^{\mathsf{T}}&\widehat{S}\end{smallmatrix}\right]\in\mathcal{S}^{n+m}_{\succeq}
.

Remark 2.5
.

The discrete-time
pH
system (
2.8
) is not in the standard form of a discrete-time dynamical system. Nevertheless, assuming
\delta_{t}
to be sufficiently small, defining the matrices
\widehat{A}\vcentcolon=\widehat{J}-\widehat{R}
,
\widehat{B}\vcentcolon=\widehat{G}-\widehat{P}
,
\widehat{C}\vcentcolon=(\widehat{G}+\widehat{P})^{\mathsf{T}}
, and
\widehat{D}\vcentcolon=\widehat{S}-\widehat{N}
, and assuming a consistent initialization of the output, i.e.,
y_{0}=\widehat{C}x_{0}+\widehat{D}u_{0}
, then (
2.8
) can be rewritten as

(2.9)

\displaystyle x_{i+1}

\displaystyle=(\tfrac{1}{\delta_{t}}\widehat{H}-\tfrac{1}{2}\widehat{A})^{-1}(\tfrac{1}{\delta_{t}}\widehat{H}+\tfrac{1}{2}\widehat{A})x_{i}+\tfrac{1}{2}(\tfrac{1}{\delta_{t}}\widehat{H}-\tfrac{1}{2}\widehat{A})^{-1}\widehat{B}(u_{i+1}+u_{i}),

\displaystyle y_{i}

\displaystyle=\widehat{C}x_{i}+\widehat{D}u_{i}.

While the equation (
2.9
) can be useful, since it represents
x_{i+1}
explicitly in terms of
x_{i}
and of the output variables, the
pH
structure of the original system is not evident.
Because of that, we prefer working with the system (
2.8
) instead.

Let us emphasize that (
2.8
) can be equivalently written as

(2.10)

\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{H}\tfrac{x_{i+1}-x_{i}}{\delta_{t}}\\
-\tfrac{y_{i+1}+y_{i}}{2}\end{bmatrix}=(\widehat{\mathcal{J}}-\widehat{\mathcal{R}})\begin{bmatrix}\tfrac{x_{i+1}+x_{i}}{2}\\
\tfrac{u_{i+1}+u_{i}}{2}\end{bmatrix},

where
\widehat{\mathcal{J}}=-\widehat{\mathcal{J}}^{\mathsf{T}}
and
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}\in\mathcal{S}^{n+m}_{\succ}
. Defining
\Delta\mathcal{H}_{i}\vcentcolon=\tfrac{1}{\delta_{t}}(\mathcal{H}(x_{i+1})-\mathcal{H}(x_{i}))
and observing

\Delta\mathcal{H}_{i}=(\tfrac{x_{i+1}+x_{i}}{2})^{\mathsf{T}}\widehat{H}(\tfrac{x_{i+1}-x_{i}}{\delta_{t}})

immediately yields the following discrete version of the dissipation inequality (
1.3
).

Lemma 2.6
.

(Discrete-time dissipation inequality)
Any discrete-time
pH
system in the form of (
2.8
) satisfies the discrete-time dissipation inequality

(2.11)

\Delta\mathcal{H}_{i}\leq(\tfrac{y_{i+1}+y_{i}}{2})^{\mathsf{T}}(\tfrac{u_{i+1}+u_{i}}{2}).

Remark 2.7
.

To simplify our presentation, we use a constant time step
\delta_{t}
throughout this manuscript. Nevertheless, it is straightforward to use a variable time step in all what follows.

3.
Port-Hamiltonian Dynamic Mode Decomposition

In this section, we discuss a variant of
DMD
that allows to construct a continuous-time
pH
system from discrete-time data.

3.1.
The port-Hamiltonian Dynamic Mode Decomposition problem

If the data
(x_{i},u_{i},y_{i})
at hand are obtained from a physical system, we expect the real system to have a
pH
representation and thus want to reflect that in our time-discrete realization. Following our concise definition of a discrete-time
pH
system (
2.10
), we are thus interested in solving the following problem.

Problem 3.1

(Port-Hamiltonian Dynamic Mode Decomposition)
.

Consider data points
(x_{i},u_{i},y_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
for
i=0,1,\ldots,M
and define the matrices

\displaystyle\dot{\hat{X}}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\tfrac{1}{\delta_{t}}\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}-x_{0}&\ldots&x_{M}-x_{M-1}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times M},

\displaystyle\hat{X}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\tfrac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}+x_{0}&\ldots&x_{M}+x_{M-1}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times M},

\displaystyle\hat{U}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\tfrac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}u_{1}+u_{0}&\ldots&u_{M}+u_{M-1}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times M},

\displaystyle\hat{Y}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\tfrac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}y_{1}+y_{0}&\ldots&y_{M}+y_{M-1}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times M}.

Given any matrix
\widehat{H}\in\mathcal{S}^{n}_{\succ}
(defining the Hamiltonian), and a reduced basis
\Phi\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times\tilde{r}}
, find matrices
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}},\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\tilde{n}\times\tilde{n}}
with
\tilde{n}\vcentcolon=\tilde{r}+m
that solve

(3.1)

\min\left\|\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}-(\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}})\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}\qquad\text{such that $\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}=-\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{\tilde{n}\times\tilde{n}}$ and $\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}\in\mathcal{S}^{\tilde{n}}_{\succeq}$},

where
\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}\vcentcolon=\begin{bmatrix}\Phi^{\mathsf{T}}H\Phi\Phi^{\mathsf{T}}\dot{\hat{X}}\\
-\hat{Y}\end{bmatrix}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}\vcentcolon=\begin{bmatrix}\Phi^{\mathsf{T}}\hat{X}\\
\hat{U}\end{bmatrix}
. If no dimension reduction is applied, then we set
\tilde{r}=n
and
\Phi=I_{n}
.

Remark 3.2
.

Note that the structural properties of
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}
are preserved under the transformation with
\Phi
, since it is a congruence transformation.

Using standard arguments, it is easy to establish that the minimization problem (
3.1
) is convex and solvable. Moreover, let us emphasize that by solving
3.1
, we directly learn the matrices of a continuous-time
pH
system without requiring further postprocessing. Moreover,
3.1
includes two important special cases. First, suppose we know a-priori that no dissipation is involved. In that case, we can set
\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}=0
, and the minimization problem (
3.1
) reduces to a skew-symmetric Procrustes problem, which can be solved analytically. We refer to
[
15
,
4
]
and the forthcoming
Section

3.2
. Second, if the system has no input and output, i.e., we have
m=0
, then the task reduces to the identification of a dissipative Hamiltonian system, i.e., we want to solve

(3.2)

\min\left\|\widehat{H}\dot{\hat{X}}-\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{J}-\widetilde{R}\end{bmatrix}\hat{X}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}\qquad\text{such that $\widetilde{J}=-\widetilde{J}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{\tilde{n}\times\tilde{n}}$ and $\widetilde{R}\in\mathcal{S}^{\tilde{n}}_{\succeq}$}.

Remark 3.3
.

If instead of prescribing the Hamiltonian and thus the matrix
\widehat{H}
, one also wants to determine
\widehat{H}\in\mathcal{S}^{n}_{\succ}
from the data, then we observe that (
3.2
) is not a suitable formulation, since in this case (
3.2
) is not solvable. To see this, notice that
\mathcal{S}^{n}_{\succ}
is an open set. Hence, for any
\varepsilon>0
, set
\widetilde{J}=\widetilde{R}=0\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and
\widehat{H}=\smash{\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2\|\dot{\hat{X}}\|_{\mathrm{F}}}I_{n}}
. Then
\widehat{H}\in\mathcal{S}^{n}_{\succ}
,
\smash{\widetilde{J}}=-\smash{\widetilde{J}^{\mathsf{T}}}
,
\smash{\widetilde{R}}\in\mathcal{S}^{n}_{\succeq}
and we have

\left\|\widehat{H}\dot{\hat{X}}-\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{J}-\widetilde{R}\end{bmatrix}\hat{X}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}=\bigg\|\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2\|\dot{\hat{X}}\|_{\mathrm{F}}}\dot{\hat{X}}\bigg\|_{\mathrm{F}}=\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}<\varepsilon,

implying that the infimum of (
3.2
) is zero with infimizer
H=\widetilde{J}=\widetilde{R}=0
.

3.2.
An iterative algorithm for the pHDMD problem

Having established the existence of a solution for
3.1
, we will now derive a numerical algorithm to solve the minimization problem (
3.1
). In
[
16
]
, a
fast gradient method
(
FGM
), cf.
[
33
, p. 90]
, for the nearest stable matrix to a given matrix is proposed, which is formulated similarly as
3.1
. The
FGM
is an optimal first-order method for convex optimization, which means no other first-order method can converge faster while using the same first-order information. However, we notice that if we already have a guess for
\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}
, then
3.1
simplifies to the skew-symmetric Procrustes problem

(3.3)

\min\left\|\mathcal{Z}_{1}-\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}\mathcal{T}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}\qquad\text{such that $\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}=-\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{\tilde{n}\times\tilde{n}}$},

where
\mathcal{Z}_{1}\vcentcolon=\mathcal{Z}+\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}\mathcal{T}
. Fortunately, the solution of (
3.3
) can be computed analytically as detailed in the following theorem taken as a special case of
[
15
, Lem. 2.1]
, so we propose to include this knowledge into the iterative algorithm to achieve acceleration.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
\mathcal{T},\mathcal{Z}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{\tilde{n}\times M}
and let
V\Sigma W^{\mathsf{T}}=\mathcal{T}
denote the
SVD
of
\mathcal{T}
with

\Sigma=\begin{bmatrix}\Sigma_{1}&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix},\qquad\text{with}\ \Sigma_{1}=\diag(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{r})\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}\ \text{and}\ r=\rank(T).

Define
\Phi=[\phi_{ij}]\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
via
\phi_{ij}=\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}^{2}+\sigma_{j}^{2}}\qquad\text{for}\ i,j=1,\ldots,r
. Then

(3.4)

\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}=V^{\mathsf{T}}\begin{bmatrix}\Phi\odot\left(2\Skew(Z_{1}\Sigma_{1})\right)&-\Sigma_{1}^{-1}Z_{3}^{\mathsf{T}}\\
Z_{3}\Sigma_{1}^{-1}&\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}_{4}\end{bmatrix}V

is a solution of (
3.3
) for any skew-symmetric matrix
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}_{4}\in\mathbb{R}^{(\tilde{n}-r)\times(\tilde{n}-r)}
, where

\displaystyle Z_{1}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}I_{r}&0\end{bmatrix}V\mathcal{Z}_{1}W^{\mathsf{T}}\begin{bmatrix}I_{r}&0\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r},

\displaystyle Z_{3}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}0&I_{n-r}\end{bmatrix}V\mathcal{Z}_{1}W^{\mathsf{T}}\begin{bmatrix}I_{r}&0\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(\tilde{n}-r)\times r}.

The solution is unique if, and only if,
\rank(T)=\tilde{n}
.

Proof.

The proof follows along the proof of
[
15
, Lem. 2.1]
.
∎

On the other hand if
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}
is given,
3.1
simplifies to a symmetric positive definite Procrustes problem

(3.5)

\min\left\|\mathcal{Z}_{2}-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}\mathcal{T}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}\qquad\text{s.t. $\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}\in\mathcal{S}^{\tilde{n}}_{\succeq}$},

where
\mathcal{Z}_{2}=\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}\mathcal{T}-\mathcal{Z}
.
Algorithmic solutions for this problem are available
[
17
]
, for instance a
FGM
.
By modifying this algorithm to allow a
\mathcal{Z}_{2}
depending on the optimal solution

of the skew-symmetric Procrustes problem (

.

Remark 3.5
.

In general, we cannot guarantee convergence of

, by using a reinitialization with a standard gradient step and a backtracking line search. By doing so, classical convergence results can be obtained since our objective function is convex and solvable.

We notice in our numerical experiments that the performance of the algorithm strongly depends on the initialization and may need many iterations to converge if a poor initialization is used. We thus study a particular initialization strategy in the next subsection by analyzing
3.1
in a weighted norm.

3.3.
A weighted pHDMD problem

A different but related problem arises when the Frobenius norm of the
3.1
is replaced with the weighted Frobenius seminorm introduced in (
1.4
), where
\mathcal{T}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathcal{T}
is used as the semi-definite weighting matrix.

Problem 3.6

(Weighted Input-Output port-Hamiltonian Dynamic Mode Decomposition)
.

For given data
\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{\tilde{n}\times\tilde{M}}
, solve the optimization problem

(3.6)

\min\left\|\mathcal{T}^{\mathsf{T}}(\mathcal{Z}-(\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}})\mathcal{T})\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}\qquad\text{such that $\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}=-\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{\tilde{n}\times\tilde{n}}$ and $\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}\in\mathcal{S}^{\tilde{n}}_{\succeq}$}.

Two remarks are in order, first we immediately notice that by solving the weighted problem we solve the original problem, but weighted according to the relevant information in the data.
Second, using the definition of the data matrices from
3.1
, we observe that the
(i,i)
entry of the matrix
\mathcal{T}^{\mathsf{T}}(\mathcal{Z}-(\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}})\mathcal{T})
reads

\displaystyle e_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}(\mathcal{T}^{\mathsf{T}}(\mathcal{Z}-(\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}})\mathcal{T})e_{i}

\displaystyle=e_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}(\hat{X})^{\mathsf{T}}\widehat{H}\dot{\hat{X}}e_{i}-e_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\hat{U}^{\mathsf{T}}\hat{Y}e_{i}+e_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\begin{bmatrix}(\hat{X})^{\mathsf{T}}&\hat{U}^{\mathsf{T}}\end{bmatrix}\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}\begin{bmatrix}\hat{X}\\
\hat{U}\end{bmatrix}e_{i}

\displaystyle=\Delta\mathcal{H}_{i}-(\tfrac{y_{i+1}+y_{i}}{2})^{\mathsf{T}}(\tfrac{u_{i+1}+u_{i}}{2})+e_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\begin{bmatrix}(\hat{X})^{\mathsf{T}}&\hat{U}^{\mathsf{T}}\end{bmatrix}\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}\begin{bmatrix}\hat{X}\\
\hat{U}\end{bmatrix}e_{i},

which resembles the power-balance equation corresponding to the discrete-time dissipation inequality (
2.11
). In particular, the weighted problem (
3.6
) uses the dissipation inequality as part of the cost-functional to determine the dissipative component, for which we need a good initialization.

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
\mathcal{T},\mathcal{Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{\tilde{n}\times\tilde{M}}
and let
V_{1}\Sigma_{1}W_{1}^{\mathsf{T}}=\mathcal{T}
denote the skinny
SVD
of
\mathcal{T}
, i.e.,
V_{1}\in\mathrm{St}(\tilde{n},r)
,
\Sigma_{1}\in\mathrm{GL}_{r}(\mathbb{R})
, and
W_{1}\in\mathrm{St}(\tilde{M},r)
, where
r\vcentcolon=\rank(\mathcal{T})
. Moreover, let
W_{2}
complement
W_{1}
to an orthogonal matrix, i.e.,
\begin{bmatrix}W_{1}&W_{2}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathrm{St}(\tilde{M},\tilde{M})
.
Define

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{1}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\Sigma_{1}V_{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathcal{Z}W_{1},

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{2}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\Sigma_{1}V_{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathcal{Z}W_{2},

and

(3.7)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}^{\star}\vcentcolon=V_{1}\Sigma_{1}^{-1}\Skew(\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{1})\Sigma_{1}^{-1}V_{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{\star}\vcentcolon=V_{1}\Sigma_{1}^{-1}\mathcal{P}_{\succeq}(-\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{1})\Sigma_{1}^{-1}V_{1}^{\mathsf{T}}.

Then,
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}^{\star}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{\star}
are the unique minimum-norm minimizers of (
3.6
) with

(3.8)

\left\|\mathcal{T}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathcal{Z}-\mathcal{T}^{\mathsf{T}}(\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}^{\star}-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{\star})\mathcal{T}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=\left\|\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{2}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\left\|\Lambda_{+}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2},

where
\Lambda_{+}
is the diagonal matrix which contains the positive eigenvalues of
\Sym(\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{1})

Remark 3.8
.

The two terms on the right-hand side of (
3.8
) can be interpreted as follows. The first term is only present if we have too much data to fit, i.e., if
\mathcal{T}
has more columns than rows. Then, in general, no linear system can perfectly capture the data, and the corresponding error contribution is
\left\|\smash{\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{2}}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}
. On the other hand, due to the specific
pH
structure, not every linear system can be written as a
pH
system. This potential deviation corresponds to the second error term given by
\left\|\Lambda_{+}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}
.

Before we present the proof, we first illustrate
Theorem

3.7
with an academic toy example and need some further preliminary results that provide the best-fit in the Frobenius norm within the class of skew-symmetric and symmetric positive semi-definite matrices.

Example 3.9
.

Consider
\mathcal{Z}=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}-1&2\\
\phantom{-}2&-1/2\end{smallmatrix}\right]
and
\mathcal{T}=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&2\end{smallmatrix}\right]
.
We immediately notice that we can choose
V_{1}=W_{1}=I_{2}
and
\Sigma_{1}=\mathcal{T}
. We thus obtain
\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{1}=\Sigma_{1}V_{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathcal{Z}W_{1}=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}-1&\phantom{-}2\\
\phantom{-}4&-1\end{smallmatrix}\right]
and thus

\Sym(\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{1})=\begin{bmatrix}-1&\phantom{-}3\\
\phantom{-}3&-1\end{bmatrix}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Skew(\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{1})=\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\
1&\phantom{-}0\end{bmatrix}.

Then, the ordered eigendecomposition of
\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{\mathrm{sym}}
is given by,

\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{\mathrm{sym}}=\Xi\Lambda\Xi^{\mathsf{T}}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}&\phantom{-}\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\\
\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}&-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}2&\phantom{-}0\\
0&-4\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}&\phantom{-}\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\\
\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}&-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\end{bmatrix}.

Thus, the unique minimizers are given by

(3.9)

\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}^{\star}=\begin{bmatrix}0&-\frac{1}{2}\\
\frac{1}{2}&\phantom{-}0\end{bmatrix}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{\star}=\begin{bmatrix}\phantom{-}2&-1\\
-1&\phantom{-}\tfrac{1}{2}\end{bmatrix}.

The minimal value of the optimization is given by

\left\|\mathcal{T}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathcal{Z}-\mathcal{T}^{\mathsf{T}}(\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}^{\star}-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{\star})\mathcal{T}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}=\left\|\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\
1&1\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}=2=\left\|\Lambda_{+}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}.

For the proof of
Theorem

3.7
we need the following technical result, taken from
[
16
, Lem. 7]
, see also
[
21
]
.

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
Z\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
, then the minimization problem

(3.10)

\min\left\|Z-(J-R)\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}\qquad\text{such that $J=-J^{\mathsf{T}}$ and $R\in\mathcal{S}^{r}_{\succeq}$}.

is solved by
J^{\star}\vcentcolon=\Skew(Z)
and
R^{\star}\vcentcolon=\mathcal{P}_{\succeq}(-Z)
.

Proof of
Theorem

3.7
.

Let
\mathcal{T}=V\Sigma W^{\mathsf{T}}
denote the singular value decomposition of
\mathcal{T}
, with partitioning

(3.11a)

\displaystyle V

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}V_{1}&V_{2}\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle\Sigma

\displaystyle=\diag(\Sigma_{1},0),

\displaystyle W

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}W_{1}&W_{2}\end{bmatrix}

such that
\Sigma_{1}\in\mathrm{GL}_{r}(\mathbb{R})
, where
r=\rank(\mathcal{T})
. In particular, we obtain
\mathcal{T}=V_{1}\Sigma_{1}W_{1}^{\mathsf{T}}
. Let
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}=-\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\tilde{n}\times\tilde{n}}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}\in\mathcal{S}^{\tilde{n}}_{\succeq}
. Define

(3.11b)

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}_{11}&-\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}_{21}^{\mathsf{T}}\\
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}_{21}&\phantom{-}\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}_{22}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=V^{\mathsf{T}}\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}V,

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{11}&\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{21}^{\mathsf{T}}\\
\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{21}&\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{22}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=V^{\mathsf{T}}\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}V

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{Z}_{11}&\mathcal{Z}_{12}\\
\mathcal{Z}_{21}&\mathcal{Z}_{22}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=V^{\mathsf{T}}\mathcal{Z}W

Note that throughout the proof we work with congruence transformations of
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}
, which preserve the skew-symmetry and the positive semi-definiteness.
We then obtain

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{T}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathcal{Z}-\mathcal{T}^{\mathsf{T}}(\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}})\mathcal{T}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|\Sigma V^{\mathsf{T}}\mathcal{Z}W-\Sigma V^{\mathsf{T}}(\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}})V\Sigma\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|\begin{bmatrix}\Sigma_{1}\mathcal{Z}_{11}&\Sigma_{1}\mathcal{Z}_{12}\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}\Sigma_{1}(\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}_{11}-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{11})\Sigma_{1}&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{1}-\Sigma_{1}(\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}_{11}-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{11})\Sigma_{1}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\left\|\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{2}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}.

We immediately notice that
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}_{21},\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}_{22},\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{21}
, and
\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{22}
do not influence the objective function and can thus be chosen arbitrarily (provided that
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}_{22}=-\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}_{22}^{\mathsf{T}}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}\in\mathcal{S}^{\tilde{n}}_{\succeq}
). For our further construction we set them to zero, in agreement with (
3.7
). It thus suffices to minimize over all skew-symmetric matrices
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}_{11}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
and all
\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{11}\in\mathcal{S}^{r}_{\succeq}
.
Thus, using
Lemma

3.10
, we obtain

\displaystyle\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}=-\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}^{\mathsf{T}},\\
\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}\in\mathcal{S}^{\tilde{n}}_{\succeq}\end{subarray}}\left\|\mathcal{T}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathcal{Z}-\mathcal{T}^{\mathsf{T}}(\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}})\mathcal{T}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}_{11}=-\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}_{11}^{\mathsf{T}},\\
\widetilde{R}_{11}\in\mathcal{S}^{r}_{\succeq}\end{subarray}}\left\|\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{1}-\Sigma_{1}(\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}_{11}-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{11})\Sigma_{1}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\left\|\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{2}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\min_{\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{11}\in\mathcal{S}^{r}_{\succeq}}\left\|\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{1}-\Sigma_{1}(\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}_{11}^{\star}-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{11})\Sigma_{1}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\left\|\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{2}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\min_{\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{11}\in\mathcal{S}^{r}_{\succeq}}\left\|\Sym(\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{1})+\Sigma_{1}\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{11}\Sigma_{1}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\left\|\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{2}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|\Sym(\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{1})+\Sigma_{1}\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{11}^{\star}\Sigma_{1}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}+\left\|\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{2}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=\left\|\Lambda_{+}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\left\|\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{2}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}.

It remains to show that
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}^{\star}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{\star}
are the minimizers with minimal norm. To this end, let
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}=-\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\tilde{n}\times\tilde{n}}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}\in\mathcal{S}^{\tilde{n}}_{\succeq}
be further minimizers of (
3.6
). Then

\left\|\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}=\left\|W^{\mathsf{T}}\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}W\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}=\left\|\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}_{11}&-\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}_{12}^{\mathsf{T}}\\
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}_{21}&\phantom{-}\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}_{22}\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}.

Lemma

3.10
implies
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}_{11}=\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}_{11}^{\star}
, and thus

\left\|\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}=\left\|\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}_{11}^{\star}&-\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}_{12}^{\mathsf{T}}\\
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}_{21}&\phantom{-}\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}_{22}\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}\geq\left\|\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}_{11}^{\star}&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}=\left\|W\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}^{\star}W\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}=\left\|\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}^{\star}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}.

A similar argument shows
\left\|\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}\geq\left\|\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{\star}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}
, which completes the proof.
∎

3.4.
Relation between the optimization problems

In this subsection we discuss the relation between the original
pHDMD
optimization
3.1
and the weighted
3.6
discussed in the previous section. We immediately obtain the following result, which showcases that whenever the data is sufficiently rich, then the cost functional of the projected optimization problem (
3.6
) provides an upper bound for the original minimization problem (
3.1
).

Lemma 3.11
.

For given
\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{\tilde{n}\times\tilde{M}}
with
\rank(\mathcal{T})=\tilde{n}
there exists a constant
c>0
such that for every
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}},\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\tilde{n}\times\tilde{n}}
we have

(3.12)

\left\|\mathcal{Z}-(\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}})\mathcal{T}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq c\left\|\mathcal{T}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathcal{Z}-\mathcal{T}^{\mathsf{T}}(\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}})\mathcal{T}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{T}=V\Sigma W^{\mathsf{T}}
denote the singular value decomposition of
\mathcal{T}
. We then have
\mathcal{T}^{\dagger}=W\Sigma^{\dagger}V^{\mathsf{T}}
. Define
c\vcentcolon=\left\|\smash{\mathcal{T}^{\dagger}}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}=\left\|\smash{\Sigma^{\dagger}}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}
. Using
\rank{\mathcal{T}}=\tilde{n}
, we conclude
\Sigma^{\dagger}\Sigma=I_{\tilde{n}}
. Let
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}},\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\tilde{n}\times\tilde{n}}
. Then

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{Z}-(\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}})\mathcal{T}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}

\displaystyle=\left\|VV^{\mathsf{T}}(\mathcal{Z}-(\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}})\mathcal{T})\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}=\left\|(\mathcal{T}^{\mathsf{T}})^{\dagger}\mathcal{T}^{\mathsf{T}}(\mathcal{Z}-(\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}})\mathcal{T})\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|(\mathcal{T}^{\mathsf{T}})^{\dagger}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}\left\|\mathcal{T}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathcal{Z}-\mathcal{T}^{\mathsf{T}}(\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}})\mathcal{T}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}},

which completes the proof.
∎

If
\mathcal{T}
has not full row rank, i.e.,
\rank(\mathcal{T})<\tilde{n}
, then we cannot expect to obtain a similar result, in particular if we use the minimum norm-minimizers from
Theorem

3.7
. The main reason for this behavior is that in this case, using the notation as in (
3.11
), we project out the data corresponding to
\mathcal{Z}_{21}
and
\mathcal{Z}_{22}
. While the latter corresponds, similarly as
\mathcal{Z}_{12}
to too much data, the contribution
\mathcal{Z}_{21}
corresponds to the components
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}_{21}-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{21}
, which are set zero in
Theorem

3.7
. In more detail, using the notation as in (
3.11
), we obtain for
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}=-\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\tilde{n}\times\tilde{n}}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}\in\mathcal{S}^{\tilde{n}}_{\succeq}

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{Z}-(\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}})\mathcal{T}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}

\displaystyle=\left\|\begin{bmatrix}V_{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\\
V_{2}^{\mathsf{T}}\end{bmatrix}\mathcal{Z}\begin{bmatrix}W_{1}&W_{2}\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}V_{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\\
V_{2}^{\mathsf{T}}\end{bmatrix}(\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}})\begin{bmatrix}V_{1}&V_{2}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\Sigma_{1}&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}

\displaystyle=\left\|\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{Z}_{11}&\mathcal{Z}_{12}\\
\mathcal{Z}_{21}&\mathcal{Z}_{22}\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}_{11}-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{11}&-\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}_{21}^{\mathsf{T}}-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{12}^{\mathsf{T}}\\
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}_{21}-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{21}&\phantom{-}\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}_{22}-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{22}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\Sigma_{1}&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}

\displaystyle=\left\|\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{Z}_{11}-(\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}_{11}-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{11})\Sigma_{1}&\mathcal{Z}_{12}\\
\mathcal{Z}_{21}-(\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}_{21}-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{21})\Sigma_{1}&\mathcal{Z}_{22}\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}.

Thus, whenever we find
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}_{21},\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{21}
such that
\mathcal{Z}_{21}\Sigma_{1}^{-1}=\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}_{21}-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{21}
we only have to ensure that
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}
remains skew-symmetric and
\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}
remains symmetric positive semi-definite. A simple way to achieve this is via the choice

(3.14)

\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}_{21}^{\star}\vcentcolon=\mathcal{Z}_{21}\Sigma_{1}^{-1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{21}^{\star}=0.

In view of
Algorithm

1
, where we only require an initialization for
\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}
, we can directly use the result of
Theorem

3.7
. Nevertheless, in our numerical experiments, we observe that the initialization with
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}^{\star}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{\star}
with the modification from (
3.14
) already yields promising results such that they can be used even without a further application of
Algorithm

1
. However, in general these are not the optimal choices as the next example illustrates.

Example 3.12
.

Consider again
Example

3.9
with the minimizers of the weighted problem
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}^{\star}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{\star}
as presented in (
3.9
).
We obtain
\left\|\mathcal{Z}-(\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}^{\star}-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{\star})\mathcal{T}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}=\sqrt{\frac{5}{2}}
for the original
pHDMD
minimization problem. Nevertheless, for

\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}^{(1)}=\begin{bmatrix}0&-\frac{1}{5}\\
\frac{1}{5}&\phantom{-}0\end{bmatrix}

we obtain
\left\|\mathcal{Z}-(\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}^{(1)}-\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{\star})\mathcal{T}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}=\sqrt{\frac{200}{105}}<\sqrt{\frac{5}{2}}

5.
Conclusions

We have developed a physics-informed system identification and dimensionality reduction algorithm that uses time-domain samples of the input, state, and output to infer a linear continuous-time dynamical system. Our algorithm is physics-informed in the sense that for a prescribed energy functional, the identified system is guaranteed to satisfy a dissipation inequality along any solution of the system. Hence, the identified system is guaranteed to be stable and passive independently of the data used for the identification. To achieve this goal, we present a generalization of dynamic mode decomposition and operator inference to port-Hamiltonian systems. The resulting method is an iterative algorithm based on a fast gradient method. For the initialization, we study a weighted problem, where we use the dominant information of the data as a weighting factor. For the weighted problem, we derive the analytical solution and detail in the numerical examples that the initialization is close to the optimum.
