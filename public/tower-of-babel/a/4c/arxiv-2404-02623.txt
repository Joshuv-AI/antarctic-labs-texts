Self-similar intermediate asymptotics for first-order mean field games
Sebastian Munoz

Abstract
We study the intermediate asymptotic behavior of solutions to the first-order mean field games system with a local coupling, when the initial density is a compactly supported function on the real line, and the coupling is of power type. Addressing a question that was left open in [ 6 ] , we prove that the solutions converge to the self-similar profile. We proceed by analyzing a continuous rescaling of the solution, and identifying an appropriate Lyapunov functional. We identify a critical value for the parameter of the coupling, which determines the qualitative behavior of the functional, and the well-posedness of the infinite horizon system. Accordingly, we also establish, in the subcritical and critical cases, a second convergence result which characterizes the behavior of the full solution as the time horizon approaches infinity. We also prove the corresponding results for the mean field planning problem. A large part of our analysis and methodology apply just as well to arbitrary dimensions. As such, this work is a major step towards settling these questions in the higher-dimensional setting.



Keywords:
self-similar solutions; continuous rescaling; Lyapunov functional; intermediate asymptotics; degenerate elliptic equations; intrinsic scaling; displacement convexity; Lagrangian coordinates; free boundary; mean field games; Hamilton-Jacobi equations; continuity equation.

Introduction

The goal of this paper is to determine the asymptotic behavior of the solutions to the first-order mean field games system (MFG), expressed as:

\begin{cases}-u_{t}+\frac{1}{2}u_{x}^{2}=m^{\theta}&(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}\times(0,T),\\
m_{t}-(mu_{x})_{x}=0&(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}\times(0,T).\\
m(x,0)=m_{0}(x)&x\in\mathbb{R},\end{cases}

(MFG)

where
\theta>0
, and
m_{0}:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
is a continuous, compactly supported function.

From a modeling perspective, the solution
(u,m)
can be interpreted as the Nash equilibrium of a differential game with a large number of agents. The function
m:\mathbb{R}\times[0,T]\to[0,\infty)
may be interpreted as the density of players at time
t
and position
x
, whereas
u(x,t)
is the optimal cost for a generic player at time
t
, at the position
x
, that is,

u(x,t)=\inf_{\beta\in H^{1}((t,T)),\,\beta(t)=x}\quad\int_{t}^{T}\left(\frac{1}{2}|\dot{\beta}(s)|^{2}+m(\beta(s),s)^{\theta}\right)ds+u(\beta(T),T).

(1.1)

In addition to prescribing the initial density of agents, the system (
MFG
) will be supplemented by a terminal condition, which may either be

u(x,T)=c_{T}m^{\theta}(x,T),

(TC)

for some
c_{T}>0
, or

m(x,T)=m_{T}(x),

(P)

where
m_{T}:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
satisfies the compatibility condition

\int_{\mathbb{R}}m_{T}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}m_{0}.

(1.2)

The problem with a prescribed terminal cost for the agents such as (
TC
) is typical in the theory of MFG. On the other hand, the problem with a prescribed terminal density (
P
) is known as the planning problem, and is related to the theory of optimal transport (see
[
6
, Sec. 1]
).

The system (
MFG
) has been previously studied in a more general setting, and in arbitrary dimensions, when the right hand side of the Hamilton–Jacobi equation (HJ) in (
MFG
) is allowed to be an arbitrary strictly increasing function
f(m)
. This study was started by P.-L. Lions in his lectures at Collège de France
[
18
]
, where he observed that the monotonicity of
f
creates a regularization effect in the region
\{m>0\}
which allows one to reformulate the problem as a single elliptic equation in
u
, in the space-time variables. This point of view was developed and extended in
[
21
,
22
,
23
]
. In arbitrary dimensions, the solution is known to be smooth under the blow-up assumption

\lim_{m\rightarrow 0^{+}}f(m)=-\infty,

(1.3)

provided that the marginals
m_{0},m_{T}
are (strictly) positive, say for positive measures on a compact domain (e.g. on the flat torus) or for Gaussian-like measures on the whole space. It was also established in
[
21
]
that, for the one-dimensional case, assumption (
1.3
) may be removed, thus requiring only the positivity of the marginals.

The case of compactly supported marginals on the whole space is more challenging, largely due to the loss of ellipticity at the interface where
m
vanishes, and was recently studied for the first time by P. Cardaliaguet, A. Porretta, and the author in
[
6
]
.

Indeed, it was shown in
[
6
]
that the power-type nonlinearity
f(m)=m^{\theta}
gives rise to finite speed of propagation, leading to the formation of a free boundary. The regularity of the solution and the geometry of its free boundary were then thoroughly examined. Furthermore, the authors exhibited a family of self-similar solutions
(\mathcal{U}_{a},\mathcal{M}_{a})
, where
a>0
, and the density
\mathcal{M}_{a}
is given, for
(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}\times(0,\infty)
, by

\mathcal{M}_{a}(x,t)=t^{-\alpha}M_{a}(x/t^{\alpha}),\,\,\,M_{a}(\eta)=\left(R_{a}-\frac{\alpha(1-\alpha)}{2}\eta^{2}\right)_{+}^{1/\theta},\,\,\,\,\,\alpha=\frac{2}{2+\theta},

(1.4)

with
R_{a}>0
being the unique constant such that

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(R_{a}-\frac{\alpha(1-\alpha)}{2}\eta^{2}\right)_{+}^{1/\theta}d\eta=a.

The function
\mathcal{M}_{a}
evolves from a Dirac mass into a compactly supported measure, and is analogous to the well-known source-type solutions for the porous medium equation
[
25
]
. The accompanying value function
\mathcal{U}_{a}
is given, in the support of
\mathcal{M}_{a}
, by the formula

\mathcal{U}_{a}(x,t)=t^{2\alpha-1}U_{a}(x/t^{\alpha})+z(t),\quad U_{a}(\eta)=-\frac{\alpha}{2}\eta^{2},\quad z^{\prime}(t)=-R_{a}t^{2\alpha-2}.

(1.5)

There is ample experimental, computational, and analytical evidence showing that self-similar solutions determine the asymptotic behavior of a large number of evolution problems. The purpose of this work is to determine whether this is the case for the solutions to (
MFG
), a question that was left open in
[
6
]
. Our main result answers this question in the affirmative, for arbitrary
\theta>0
. We refer to Section
2
for the assumptions (
2.1
), (
2.2
), (
2.4
), (
2.5
), and (
2.7
).

Remark 1.2
.

We now explain what is meant in Theorem

[

Remark 1.3
.

Both

A follow-up question to Theorem

\begin{cases}-u_{t}+\frac{1}{2}u_{x}^{2}=m^{\theta}&(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}\times(0,\infty),\\
m_{t}-(mu_{x})_{x}=0&(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}\times(0,\infty),\\

(MFGI)

In the simpler case of solutions in a compact domain with strictly positive density, where there is no free boundary and the solutions are smooth, this analysis was carried out in

Remark 1.5
.

In view of Theorem

and the preceding comments, we henceforth refer to the range

Remark 1.6

We explain here the non-uniqueness statement regarding the planning problem. It is a general fact (see
[
6

)–(

Additionally, for the critical case

(see Theorems

In fact, while the statement of Theorem

We prove that, when

We further elaborate on our proof strategy. We use the method of continuous rescaling to analyze a zoomed version of
(u,m)
, in which the scaling factors are indexed by a continuous parameter

, representing the ‘new time’. While this approach is new in the context of MFG, it is a classical idea that been applied successfully to study the asymptotics of many evolution problems (see, for instance,
[
12
,
15
]
). To determine the correct zooming factors, we observe that for any

, the pair of functions
(x,t)\mapsto\lambda^{\alpha}m(\lambda^{\alpha}x,t\lambda)
and
(x,t)\mapsto\lambda^{1-2\alpha}u(\lambda^{\alpha}x,\lambda t)
still satisfy (
MFG

a=\int_{\mathbb{R}}m(\cdot,t)
preserved. Crucially, in the case of the self-similar solution
\mathcal{M}_{a}
, the function itself remains invariant under this rescaling. Accordingly, we define the continuous rescaling
(v,\mu)
, of (
MFGI

\mu(\eta,\tau)=t^{\alpha}m(x,t),\,v(\eta,\tau)=t^{1-2\alpha}u(x,t),\quad(\eta,\tau)\in\mathbb{R}\times\left(-\infty,\log(T)\right],

(1.16)

where

t=e^{\tau},\,\,x=t^{\alpha}\eta.

(1.17)

Under this transformation, the pair
((\mathcal{U}_{a})_{x}(x,t),\mathcal{M}_{a}(x,t))
becomes the stationary profile
(U_{a}^{\prime}(\eta),M_{a}(\eta))
. Accordingly, (
1.12
)–(
1.13

\tau\to\infty

(v_{\eta},\mu)
converges to
(U_{a}^{\prime}(\eta),M_{a}(\eta))
. To show this, we analyze the system of PDE satisfied by

, together with an appropriately chosen Lyapunov functional. We will see that, in particular, the qualitative behavior of this functional is determined by whether
\theta
is in the subcritical, critical, or supercritical range.

We present the formal computations behind this analysis in Section
3

whose algebraic rate of growth as

is the sharp one, in the sense that it is attained by the self-similar solution. We carry this out in Section

)–(
TC

)–(

The case of dimensions
d>1

We now discuss the extent to which our arguments may be used to study the asymptotic behavior of (
MFG
) in dimensions larger than
1
. This is a delicate matter. On one hand, the strongest regularity results of
[
6
]
, of which this paper is a sequel, are currently quite far from being extended to
d>1
, with major obstacles such as the lack of a continuity result for
m
, and of a gradient estimate for
u
that does not require
m_{0}
to be positive. We use these regularity results here in full force, in order to obtain the uniform convergence result (
1.7
). Therefore, the case
p=\infty
of Theorem
1.1
currently appears to be out of reach for higher dimensions.

However, the range
p\in[1,\infty)
is a different matter. Indeed, a major portion of the analysis of the infinite horizon system and the continuous rescaling, developed in Section
3
, involves energy computations which remain valid in any dimension. While there are still significant regularity issues, we believe that this paper paves the way to prove an analogue of Theorem
1.1
in arbitrary dimensions, and this will be explored in upcoming work.

\gamma_{L},\gamma_{R}\in W^{2,\infty}(0,T)

such that
\gamma_{L}<\gamma_{R}
, and, for every
t\in(0,T),

\{m(\cdot,t)>0\}=(\gamma_{L}(t),\gamma_{R}(t)).

(2.8)

The support of
m
is convex, with the curves
\gamma_{L}
and
\gamma_{R}
satisfying
\ddot{\gamma}_{L}>0,\ddot{\gamma}_{R}<0
. Furthermore, if
u
solves (
MFG
)–(
TC
), then the support is expanding in time, with
\dot{\gamma}_{L}<0,\dot{\gamma}_{R}>0.

A fundamental tool in the analysis of (
MFG
) is the Lagrangian flow
\gamma:(a_{0},b_{0})\times[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
. This is simply the flow of optimal trajectories associated to the HJ equation, and can be defined, for each
x\in(a_{0},b_{0}),
as the solution to the differential equation

\begin{cases}\dot{\gamma}(x,\cdot)=-u_{x}(\gamma(x,\cdot),\cdot),\\
\gamma(x,0)=x.\end{cases}

(2.9)

The next result we cite here contains the main properties of the Lagrangian flow, its relation to the free boundary curves, and, crucially, its exact rate of growth (
[
6
, Thm. 1.1, Thm. 1.4]
). To specify the rate of growth, we define

{d}(t)=\begin{cases}t&\text{if }u\text{ solves \eqref{mfg}--\eqref{tc}},\\
\text{dist}(t,\{0,T\})&\text{if }u\text{ solves \eqref{mfg}--\eqref{pp}.}\end{cases}

(2.10)

Theorem 2.4
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
2.3
, let
(u,m)
be a solution to (
MFG
)–(
TC
), or to (
MFG
)–(
P
), and let
\gamma_{L},\gamma_{R}
be its free boundary curves. The Lagrangian flow
\gamma
is well defined
on
(a_{0},b_{0})\times[0,T]
, and

\gamma\in W^{1,\infty}((a_{0},b_{0})\times(0,T))\cap C_{\emph{\text{loc}}}^{2,\overline{\alpha}}((a_{0},b_{0})\times[0,T]),\,\,\,\gamma_{L}=\gamma(a_{0},\cdot),\,\gamma_{R}=\gamma(b_{0},\cdot).

(2.11)

Furthermore, we have, for
(x,t)\in(a_{0},b_{0})\times(0,T)
,

\gamma_{x}(x,t)=\frac{m_{0}(x)}{m(\gamma(x,t),t)},

(2.12)

and there exists
C>0
such that

|\gamma(x,t)|\leq C(1+{d}(t)^{\alpha}),

(2.13)

\frac{1}{C}(1+{d}(t)^{\alpha})\leq\gamma_{x}(x,t)\leq C(1+{d}(t)^{\alpha}),

(2.14)

where
C=C(C_{0},|a_{0}|,|b_{0}|)
in the case of problem (
MFG
)–(
TC
), and
C=C(C_{0},C_{1},|a_{0}|,|b_{0}|,|a_{1}|,|b_{1}|)
in the case of problem (
MFG
)–(
P
).

Finally, we state a version of the so-called displacement convexity formula, which is a versatile tool for obtaining energy estimates. In the context of MFG, displacement convexity was first studied in
[
16
,
14
]
, but it is a classical topic in the theory of optimal transport (see
[
20
]
).

Lemma 2.5
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
2.3
, let
(u,m)
be a solution to (
MFG
)–(
TC
) or (
MFG
)–(
P
), and let
p\in(0,1)\cup(1,\infty)
.
Then, for every
\zeta\in C^{\infty}_{c}(0,T)
, with
\zeta\geq 0
, we have

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(m^{p}u_{xx}^{2}+4\theta(\theta+p)^{-2}(m^{\frac{\theta+p}{2}})_{x}^{2})\zeta(t)dxdt\leq\frac{1}{p(p-1)}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}}m^{p}\ddot{\zeta}(t)dxdt.

(2.15)

In particular, the map
t\mapsto(p(p-1))^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}}m^{p}(x,t)dx
is convex.

Proof.

For smooth solutions, the fact that (
2.15
) holds with equality is precisely the displacement convexity formula (
[
6
, Prop. 3.6]
). Therefore, the task at hand is to explain how to handle the limited regularity. Consider, for
R>0
, the MFG system with Neumann boundary conditions

\begin{cases}-u_{t}+\frac{1}{2}u_{x}^{2}=m^{\theta}&(x,t)\in[-R,R]\times(0,T),\\
m_{t}-(mu_{x})_{x}=0&(x,t)\in[-R,R]\times(0,T),\\
u_{x}(-R,t)=u_{x}(R,t)=0,&t\in[0,T].\end{cases}

(2.16)

It was shown in
[
6
, Thm. 4.3]
that one may uniformly approximate
(u,m)
by a sequence of smooth solutions
(u_{n},m_{n})
to (
2.16
), for a fixed value of
R
, as long as
R
is sufficiently large for the rectangle
[-R,R]\times[0,T]
to contain the support of
m
. Furthermore,
u_{n}\to u
in
C^{2}_{\text{loc}}(\{m>0\})
, so that, in particular,

m^{p}u_{xx}^{2}\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}m_{n}^{p}((u_{n})_{xx})^{2}.

(2.17)

The displacement convexity formula, which is valid in this smooth setting, reads:

\frac{d^{2}\varphi_{n}}{dt^{2}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}(m_{n}^{p}(u_{n})_{xx}^{2}+4\theta(\theta+p)^{-2}(m_{n}^{\frac{\theta+p}{2}})_{x}^{2}))dx,\quad\varphi_{n}(t)=\frac{1}{p(p-1)}\int_{-R}^{R}m_{n}^{p}(x,t)dx.

(2.18)

In view of (
2.17
), the result follows from Fatou’s lemma by integrating (
2.18
) against
\zeta
and letting
n\to\infty
.
∎

3
Intermediate asymptotics of the infinite horizon system

In this section, we will study the asymptotic behavior of compactly supported solutions to the infinite horizon system:

\begin{cases}-u_{t}+\frac{1}{2}u_{x}^{2}=m^{\theta}&(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}\times(t_{0},\infty),\\
m_{t}-(mu_{x})_{x}=0&(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}\times(t_{0},\infty),\end{cases}

(3.1)

where
t_{0}>0
. In order to present some of the main ideas in a simple setting, we will postpone the discussion of well-posedness and regularity of solutions to this system. Instead, we will prove a convergence result under the assumption that a solution
(u,m)
exists, and that its continuous rescaling
(v,\mu)
has sufficient regularity, uniformly in time. Specifically, our main goal in this section is to prove the following statement.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
(u,m)\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}\times(t_{0},\infty))\times C(\mathbb{R}\times[t_{0},\infty))
satisfy the first equation of (
3.1
) in the classical sense, and the second equation in the distributional sense, with
m\geq 0
. Assume that there exist functions
\gamma_{L},\gamma_{R}\in W^{1,\infty}_{\emph{loc}}((t_{0},\infty))
such that (
2.8
) holds for every
t\in(t_{0},\infty)
, and let
a=\int_{\mathbb{R}}m(\cdot,t_{0})dx>0
. Let
(v,\mu)
be the continuous rescaling (
1.16
) of
(u,m)
, and let
\tau_{0}=\log(t_{0})
. Assume further that, for some constant
\rho>0
,

\mu\in\emph{BUC}(\mathbb{R}\times(\tau_{0},\infty)),\quad\sup_{\tau_{1}\in(\tau_{0}+\rho,\infty)}\|\mu^{\theta}\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}\times(\tau_{1}-\rho,\tau_{1}+\rho)))}<\infty,

(3.2)

and that there exists a constant
R\geq R_{a}
such that

\emph{supp}(\mu)\subset[-R,R]\times\left[\tau_{0},\infty\right),\quad Dv\in C^{1}(\{\mu>0\})\cap L^{\infty}((-R,R)\times(\tau_{0},\infty)).

(3.3)

Then, for every
p\in[1,\infty]
,

\lim_{t\to\infty}t^{\alpha(1-\frac{1}{p})}\left\|m(\cdot,t)-\mathcal{M}_{a}(\cdot,t)\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R})}=0,

(3.4)

\lim_{t\to\infty}t^{2-\alpha(1+\frac{1}{p})}\left\|m(\cdot,t)\left|u_{x}(\cdot,t)-(\mathcal{U}_{a})_{x}(\cdot,t)\right|^{2}\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R})}=0,

(3.5)

\lim_{t\to\infty}t^{2-\alpha(1+\frac{1}{p})}\left\|m(\cdot,t)\left|u_{t}(\cdot,t)-(\mathcal{U}_{a})_{t}(\cdot,t)\right|\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R})}=0.

(3.6)

The significance of this result will become clear in Section
4
, where we show that its regularity assumptions are indeed satisfied by the continuous rescaling
(v^{T},\mu^{T})
of the solutions
(u^{T},m^{T})
to (
MFG
)–(
TC
) or (
MFG
)–(
P
), uniformly in
T
. As a result, any subsequential limit as
T\to\infty
of
(u^{T},m^{T})
must be a solution
(u,m)
to (
3.1
) which satisfies the hypotheses, and hence the conclusion, of Proposition
3.1
(see Proposition
5.2
).

3.1
Basic properties of the continuous rescaling

For clarity, we restate the precise definition of the continuous rescaling.

Definition 3.2
.

Given a solution
(u,m)
to (
3.1
), we define the continuous rescaling
(v,\mu)
as follows. For
(\eta,\tau)\in\mathbb{R}\times\left[\tau_{0},\infty\right):=\mathbb{R}\times\left[\log(t_{0}),\infty\right),
we set

t=e^{\tau},\,\,x=t^{\alpha}\eta,

(3.7)

and we define

\mu(\eta,\tau)=t^{\alpha}m(x,t),\,v(\eta,\tau)=t^{1-2\alpha}u(x,t).

(3.8)

We begin by reducing the proof of Proposition
3.1
to the equivalent problem of showing that
(v_{\eta},\mu)
converges to the stationary profile
(U^{\prime}_{a},M_{a})
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
(u,m)
and
(v,\mu)
be as in Proposition
3.1
. Then (
3.4
) and (
3.5
) are equivalent, respectively, to

\lim_{\tau\to\infty}\left\|\mu(\cdot,\tau)-M_{a}(\cdot)\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R})}=0,\,\,\,\text{ and }\,\,\,\lim_{\tau\to\infty}\left\|\mu(\cdot,\tau)\left|v_{\eta}-U^{\prime}_{a}(\cdot)\right|^{2}\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R})}=0.

(3.9)

Proof.

The key point is that the continuous rescaling of the self-similar solution
\mathcal{M}_{a}
is precisely the stationary profile
M_{a}
. Indeed, changing variables according to (
3.7
), we have

t^{\alpha(p-1)}\left\|m(\cdot,t)-\mathcal{M}_{a}(\cdot,t)\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R})}^{p}=t^{\alpha(p-1)}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|m(x,t)-t^{-\alpha}M_{a}(x/t^{-\alpha})|^{p}dx\\
=\int_{\mathbb{R}}|t^{\alpha}m(t^{\alpha}\eta,t)-M_{a}(\eta)|^{p}d\eta=\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\mu(\eta,\tau)-M_{a}(\eta)|^{p}d\eta=\left\|\mu(\cdot,\tau)-M_{a}(\cdot)\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R})}^{p},

(3.10)

which proves the first equivalence of (
3.9
). The other equivalence is obtained in the same way.
∎

The next step will be to obtain the system of equations satisfied by the continuous rescaling
(v,\mu)
. In fact, recalling (
1.5
), we want to show that, in
\{\mu>0\}
,
v_{\eta}\to U^{\prime}_{a}\equiv-\alpha\eta
. As such, it will be convenient to define

w(\eta,\tau)=v(\eta,\tau)+\frac{\alpha}{2}\eta^{2},

(3.11)

and to rewrite the problem as a system in
(w,\mu)
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
(u,m)
and
(v,\mu)
be as in Proposition
3.1
, and define
w
by (
3.11
). Then the pair
(w,\mu)
satisfies

\begin{cases}-w_{\tau}+\frac{1}{2}w_{\eta}^{2}=\mu^{\theta}+\frac{\alpha(1-\alpha)}{2}\eta^{2}+(2\alpha-1)w&(\eta,\tau)\in\mathbb{R}\times(\tau_{0},\infty),\\
\mu_{\tau}-(\mu w_{\eta})_{\eta}=0&(\eta,\tau)\in\mathbb{R}\times(\tau_{0},\infty),\end{cases}

(3.12)

where the first equation is taken in the classical sense, and the second equation is taken in the sense of distributions. Furthermore, in the set
\{\mu>0\}
, the second equation is also satisfied in the classical sense.

Proof.

We write
u(x,t)=t^{2\alpha-1}v(\eta,\tau),
and, using the fact that
u\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}\times(\tau_{0},\infty))
we compute

u_{t}=t^{2\alpha-2}((2\alpha-1)v+v_{\tau}-\alpha v_{\eta}),

(3.13)

u_{x}=t^{\alpha-1}v_{\eta}.

(3.14)

Substituting these findings in the first equation of (
3.1
), we obtain

-v_{\tau}+\frac{1}{2}v_{\eta}^{2}+\alpha\eta v_{\eta}=\mu^{\theta}+(2\alpha-1)v.

(3.15)

The first equation of (
3.12
) then follows by substituting (
3.11
) in (
3.15
).

We now show that the second equation of (
3.12
) holds in the distributional sense. Let
\zeta\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}\times(\tau_{0},\infty))
, and define
\psi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}\times(t_{0},\infty))
by

\psi(x,t)=\zeta(x/t^{\alpha},\log(t))
.
Note that

\psi_{t}(x,t)=(-\alpha xt^{-\alpha-1})\zeta_{\eta}+\zeta_{\tau}t^{-1},

(3.16)

\psi_{x}(x,t)=\zeta_{\eta}t^{-\alpha}.

(3.17)

Using (
3.14
) and (
3.11
), we also have

w_{\eta}(\eta,\tau)=t^{1-\alpha}u_{x}(x,t)+\alpha\eta,

(3.18)

and, by (
3.7
),

d\eta d\tau=t^{-1-\alpha}dxdt.

(3.19)

Hence, we obtain from (
3.16
), (
3.17
), (
3.18
) and (
3.19
) that

\int_{\tau_{0}}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\mu\zeta_{\tau}-\mu w_{\eta}\zeta_{\eta})d\eta d\tau\\
=\int_{t_{0}}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}}t^{\alpha}m(x,t)\big(\zeta_{\tau}(x/t^{\alpha},\log(\tau))-(t^{1-\alpha}u_{x}(x,t)+\alpha x/t^{\alpha})\zeta_{\eta}(x/t^{\alpha},\log(t)\big)t^{-1-\alpha}dxdt\\
=\int_{t_{0}}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(m\psi_{t}-mu_{x}\psi_{x})dxdt.

(3.20)

The right hand side vanishes, since
m
satisfies the second equation of (
3.1
) in the distributional sense. Finally, we observe from (
3.15
) that, since
u\in C^{2}(\{\mu>0\}),
we have
\mu\in C^{1}(\{\mu>0\})
, and so the second equation of (
3.12
) must also be satisfied classically in
\{\mu>0\}
.
∎

We now take advantage of (
3.12
) to get an interior regularity estimate for
w
in
\{\mu>0\}
, by computing the elliptic equation satisfied by
w
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
(u,m)
and
(v,\mu)
be as in Proposition
3.1
, and define
w
by (
3.11
). Let
\delta>0
, and assume that
(\eta_{1},\tau_{1})\in\mathbb{R}\times(\tau_{0},\infty)
is such that

\mu(\eta,\tau)\geq\delta,\quad(\eta,\tau)\in(\eta_{1}-4\delta,\eta_{1}+4\delta)\times(\tau_{1}-4\delta,\tau_{1}+4\delta):=\mathcal{R}.

(3.21)

Then there exists
C=C(R,\|\mu\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}\times(\tau_{0},\infty))},\|Dv\|_{L^{\infty}(\{\mu>0\})},\theta,\theta^{-1},\delta^{-1})

such that

\|Dw\|_{C^{1,1/2}([\eta_{1}-\delta,\eta_{1}+\delta]\times\left[\tau_{1}-\delta,\tau_{1}+\delta]\right)}\leq C.

(3.22)

Proof.

Noting that (
3.12
) is a first order MFG system (albeit with
w
dependence in the HJ equation), and that the equations are satisfied classically when
\mu>0,
we follow the standard computation of the elliptic equation of
w
(see
[
18
,
22
]
). Namely, solving for
\mu
in the first equation of (
3.12
), and substituting in the second equation, one obtains the quasilinear, degenerate elliptic equation

-\text{tr}(A(w_{\eta},w_{\tau})D^{2}w)+\alpha(1-\alpha)\eta w_{\eta}-(1-2\alpha)w_{\eta}^{2}+(1-2\alpha)w_{\tau}=0,

(3.23)

where

A(p,s)=\begin{pmatrix}w_{\eta}^{2}+\theta\mu^{\theta}&-w_{\eta}\\
-w_{\eta}.&1\end{pmatrix}

(3.24)

This matrix is positive, with

\text{tr}(A)=1+w_{\eta}^{2}+\theta\mu^{\theta},\quad\det(A)=\theta\mu^{\theta}.

(3.25)

Thus,
\lambda I\leq A\leq\Lambda I
in
\mathcal{R}
, where
\lambda
,
\Lambda
depend only on
\|\mu\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}\times(\tau_{0},\infty))},\delta^{-1},\theta,\theta^{-1},\|Dw\|_{L^{\infty}(\{\mu>0\})}
. Note also that, in view of (
3.11
) and (
3.3
),
\|Dw\|_{L^{\infty}(\{\mu>0\})}
is bounded in terms of
\|Dv\|_{L^{\infty}(\{\mu>0\})}
and
R
.
Additionally, any vertical translation of
w
satisfies (
3.23
). Thus, since the conclusion (
3.22
) is also invariant under vertical translation, we may assume that

\|w\|_{W^{1,\infty}(\{\mu>0\})}\leq K_{1}=K_{1}(R,\|Dv\|_{L^{\infty}(\{\mu>0\})}).

(3.26)

Writing,
S_{\delta}=[\eta_{1}-\delta,\eta_{1}+\delta]\times[\tau_{1}-\delta,\tau_{1}+\delta]
, the interior
C^{1,\beta}
estimates for quasilinear elliptic equations (
[
13
, Thm. 13.6]
) imply that there exist
\beta\in(0,1)
and
K_{2}>0
, depending only on
K_{1}
,
\delta^{-1}
,
R
, and
\Lambda/\lambda
such that

\|w\|_{C^{1,\beta}(S_{2\delta})}\leq K_{2}.

(3.27)

Next, the interior Schauder estimates
[
13
, Thm. 6.2]
imply that there exists
K_{3}>0
, depending only on
K_{1},K_{2},R,\Lambda,\lambda^{-1}
, and
\beta
such that

\|w\|_{C^{2,\beta}(S_{\delta})}\leq K_{3}.

(3.28)

Thus, (
3.27
) holds with
\beta=1/2
and constant
K_{3}
, which, a posteriori, means (
3.28
) must also hold with
\beta=1/2
and some constant
K_{4}
.

Remark 3.6
.

The estimate of Lemma 3.5 is entirely local, so it only requires
(w,\mu)
to solve (
3.12
) on
(\eta_{1}-4\delta,\eta_{1}+4\delta)\times(\tau_{1}-4\delta,\tau_{1}+4\delta).
In particular, the same conclusion holds if
(u,m)
is merely a solution to the finite time horizon problem (
MFG
).

∎

3.2
Analysis of a Lyapunov functional

We define

F(s)=\frac{s^{\theta+1}}{\theta+1},\quad s\in[0,\infty),

(3.29)

and consider, given a solution
(w,\mu)
to (
3.12
), the functional

{\mathcal{E}}_{w,\mu}(\tau)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\mu|w_{\eta}|^{2}-\left(F(\mu)-F(M_{a})-\left(R_{a}-\frac{\alpha(1-\alpha)}{2}\eta^{2}\right)(\mu-M_{a})\right)\right)d\eta,\quad\tau\in\left[\tau_{0},\infty\right).

(3.30)

We note that, by construction,
{\mathcal{E}}_{w,\mu}\equiv 0
when
(v_{\eta},\mu)
is the self-similar profile
(U^{\prime}_{a},M_{a})
.

In order to understand the asymptotics of the continuous rescaling
(w,\mu)
, we will first analyze
{\mathcal{E}}_{w,\mu}
. As it turns out, the qualitative behavior of
{\mathcal{E}}_{w,\mu}
is dependent on the parameter
\theta
, with
\theta=2
being the critical value (see (
3.32
), and Propositions
3.9
and
3.10
below).

Lemma 3.7
.

Under the assumptions of Proposition
3.1
, let
(w,\mu)
be given according to Definition
3.2
and (
3.11
), and let
{\mathcal{E}}_{w,\mu}:\left[\tau_{0},\infty\right)\to\mathbb{R}
be defined by (
3.30
). Let
K>0
be a constant such that, for every
\tau_{1}\in(\tau_{0}+\rho,\infty)
,

\|\mu^{\theta}\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}\times(\tau_{1}-\rho,\tau_{1}+\rho))}\leq K.

(3.31)

Then
{\mathcal{E}}_{w,\mu}\in C^{1,1/2}(\left[\tau_{0},\infty)\right)
, with

\frac{d}{d\tau}{\mathcal{E}}_{w,\mu}(\tau)=\frac{\theta-2}{\theta+2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mu w_{\eta}^{2}d\eta,

(3.32)

and

\|{\mathcal{E}}_{w,\mu}\|_{H^{2}((\tau_{1}-\rho,\tau_{1}+\rho))}\leq C,

(3.33)

where
C=C(K,R,\|\mu\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}\times(\tau_{0},\infty))},\|Dw\|_{L^{\infty}(\{\mu>0\})})
.

Proof.

Let
\tau_{0}<b<c<\infty
. For each
\varepsilon>0,
let
h\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
satisfy
0\leq h\leq 1
,
h\equiv 1
in
[2\varepsilon,1-2\varepsilon],

h\equiv 0
outside of
[\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]
, and
|h^{\prime}|\leq K_{1}/\varepsilon
. Let
\gamma_{L}<\gamma_{R}
be the (Lipschitz) free boundary curves of
m
, so that

\beta_{L}(\tau)=t^{-\alpha}\gamma_{L}(e^{\tau}),\quad\beta_{R}(\tau)=t^{-\alpha}\gamma_{R}(e^{\tau}),\quad\tau\in\left[\tau_{0},\infty\right)

are the (Lipschitz) free boundary curves for
\mu
, that is,

\{\mu(\cdot,\tau)>0\}=(\beta_{L}(\tau),\beta_{R}(\tau)),\quad\tau\in\left[\tau_{0},\infty\right).

Set

\varphi(\eta,\tau)=h\left((x-\beta_{L}(\tau))/(\beta_{R}(\tau)-\beta_{L}(\tau))\right),\quad(\eta,\tau)\in\mathbb{R}\times[b,c].

Note that
\varphi
is compactly supported in
\{\mu>0\}
, and Lipschitz, with

|D\varphi|\leq K_{b,c}/\varepsilon,

for some constant
K_{b,c}>0
depending on
b,c,\beta_{L},\beta_{R},
and
K_{1}
.

We recall that (
3.12
) holds classically in
\{\mu>0\}
. Multiplying the first and second equations in (
3.12
) by
\mu_{\tau}
and
v_{\tau}
, respectively, and adding the two resulting equations, we obtain

\mu_{\tau}\frac{1}{2}w_{\eta}^{2}-(\mu w_{\eta})_{\eta}w_{\tau}=\mu^{\theta}\mu_{\tau}+\frac{\alpha(1-\alpha)}{2}\eta^{2}\mu_{\tau}+(2\alpha-1)w\mu_{\tau}.

Integrating against
\varphi,
using the fact that
w\in C^{2}(\{\mu>0\})
, and integrating by parts, we obtain

\int_{b}^{c}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau}\left(\frac{1}{2}\mu w_{\eta}^{2}-\left(\frac{\mu^{\theta+1}}{\theta+1}+\frac{\alpha(1-\alpha)}{2}\eta^{2}\mu\right)\right)\varphi d\eta d\tau=\int_{b}^{c}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(2\alpha-1)w\mu_{\tau}\varphi d\eta d\tau+E_{1},

(3.34)

where

E_{1}=\int_{b}^{c}\int_{\mathbb{R}}-\mu w_{\eta}w_{\tau}\varphi_{\eta}d\eta d\tau.

On the other hand,

\int_{b}^{c}\int_{\mathbb{R}}w\mu_{\tau}\varphi d\eta d\tau=\int_{b}^{c}\int_{\mathbb{R}}w(\mu w_{\eta})_{\eta}\varphi d\eta d\tau=\int_{b}^{c}\int_{\mathbb{R}}-\mu w_{\eta}^{2}\varphi d\eta d\tau+E_{2},

(3.35)

where

|E_{2}|=\left|\int_{b}^{c}\int_{\mathbb{R}}-w\mu w_{\eta}\varphi_{\eta}d\eta d\tau\right|\leq C_{b,c,w}\int_{b}^{c}\varepsilon^{-1}\int_{\text{dist}(\eta,\{\beta_{L}(\tau),\beta_{R}(\tau)\})\leq\varepsilon}\mu=o(1)

as
\varepsilon\to 0,
by the continuity of
\mu
, which vanishes at
\eta=\beta_{L}(\tau)
and
\eta=\beta_{R}(\tau)
. Similarly,

E_{1}=\int_{a}^{b}\int_{\mathbb{R}}-\mu w_{\eta}w_{\tau}\varphi_{\eta}=o(1),

and

\int_{b}^{c}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\mu w_{\eta}^{2}-\left(\frac{\mu^{\theta+1}}{\theta+1}+\frac{\alpha(1-\alpha)}{2}\eta^{2}\mu\right)\right)\varphi_{\tau}d\eta d\tau=o(1).

Thus, (
3.34
) and (
3.35
) yield

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\mu w_{\eta}^{2}-\left(\frac{\mu^{\theta+1}}{\theta+1}+\frac{\alpha(1-\alpha)}{2}\eta^{2}\mu\right)\right)\varphi d\eta\Bigg|_{b}^{c}=(1-2\alpha)\int_{b}^{c}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mu w_{\eta}^{2}\varphi d\eta d\tau+o(1).

(3.36)

Noting that
\varphi\to\chi_{\{\mu>0\}}
pointwise as
\varepsilon\to 0
, we conclude by dominated convergence that

\frac{d}{d\tau}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\mu w_{\eta}^{2}-\left(F(\mu)+\frac{\alpha(1-\alpha)}{2}\eta^{2}\mu\right)\right)d\eta=(1-2\alpha)\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mu w_{\eta}^{2}d\eta.

(3.37)

On the other hand, we clearly have

\frac{d}{d\tau}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(F(M_{a}(\eta))+\frac{\alpha(1-\alpha)}{2}\eta^{2}M_{a}(\eta)\right)d\eta=0,

(3.38)

and, since
\mu
and
M_{a}
have the same mass,

\frac{d}{d\tau}\int_{\mathbb{R}}R_{a}(\mu-M_{a})d\eta=\frac{d}{d\tau}0=0.

(3.39)

Adding (
3.37
), (
3.38
), and (
3.39
), we obtain (
3.32
). A posteriori, it follows from the the continuity of
\mu
and the interior
C^{1}
regularity of
w
that the right hand side of (
3.32
) is continuous, which means that
{\mathcal{E}}_{w,\mu}\in C^{1}(\left[\tau_{0},\infty\right))
.
Through similar test function arguments, and using (
3.31
), the following formal computations also hold:

\small\frac{d}{d\tau}\int_{\mathbb{R}}F(\mu)d\eta=\int_{\mathbb{R}}F^{\prime}(\mu)\mu_{\tau}d\eta=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mu^{\theta}(\mu w_{\eta})_{\eta}d\eta=\int_{\mathbb{R}}-\theta\mu^{\theta}\mu_{\eta}w_{\eta}d\eta=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\theta(F(\mu))_{\eta}w_{\eta}d\eta\in L^{2}_{\text{loc}}((\tau_{0},\infty)),

(3.40)

and

\small\frac{d}{d\tau}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{2}\eta^{2}\mu d\eta=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{2}\eta^{2}(\mu w_{\eta})_{\eta}d\eta=\int_{\mathbb{R}}-\eta w_{\eta}\mu d\eta\in L^{\infty}((\tau_{0},\infty)).

(3.41)

Thus, (
3.37
), (
3.40
), and (
3.41
) yield

\frac{d}{d\tau}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{2}\mu w_{\eta}^{2}=(1-2\alpha)\int_{\mathbb{R}}\big(\mu w_{\eta}^{2}-\theta(F(\mu))_{\eta}w_{\eta}-\alpha(1-\alpha)\eta w_{\eta}\mu\big)d\eta.

(3.42)

The first and third terms on the right hand side of (
3.42
) are bounded, and the second term is in
L^{2}_{\text{loc}}((\tau_{0},\infty))
by (
3.31
). Thus, (
3.32
) combined with (
3.42
) yields the
H^{2}\hookrightarrow C^{1,1/2}
regularity of
{\mathcal{E}}_{w,\mu}
, and the quantitative claim (
3.33
) follows from (
3.31
).
∎

4
Estimates for the continuous rescaling

Starting with this section, we will focus on proving the main results of the paper exclusively for the terminal cost problem (

Proposition 4.5

Proof.

0<f(m_{0})_{x}(a_{0})\|\gamma_{x}(\cdot,t)\|_{\infty}^{-(1+\theta)}\leq\ddot{\gamma}_{L}(t)\leq f(m_{0})_{x}(a_{0})\|\gamma_{x}(\cdot,t)^{-1}\|_{\infty}^{1+\theta}.

Proposition 4.6

4.2
Exact rates for the value function and its gradient

We focus now on obtaining the optimal rates for the value function
u
and its first derivatives. It will be useful to remark that, since
u\in W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R}\times(0,T))\cap C^{1}(\mathbb{R}\times(0,T))
is a classical solution to the HJ equation in (
MFG
), it satisfies the dynamic programming principle. That is, for
(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}\times(0,T)
and
0<h<T-t,

u(x,t)=\inf_{\beta\in H^{1}((t,t+h)),\,\beta(t)=x}\quad\int_{t}^{t+h}\left(\frac{1}{2}|\dot{\beta}(s)|^{2}+m(\beta(s),s)^{\theta}\right)ds+u(\beta(t+h),t+h).

(4.12)

We define, for
s\leq t
,

S(m,s,t)=\text{supp}(m)\cap(\mathbb{R}\times[s,t]),

and begin by estimating the oscillation of
u
inside the support of
m
.

Proposition 4.7

(Optimal rate for the oscillation of
u
)
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
2.3
, let
(u,m)
be the solution to (
MFG
)–(
TC
). For every
\delta>0
, there exists a constant
K=K(C_{0},|a_{0}|,|b_{0}|,\delta^{-1})
such that, for every
t_{0}\in[\delta,T/2]
,

\underset{S\left(m,\frac{t_{0}}{2},2t_{0}\right)}{\emph{osc}}(u)\leq Kt_{0}^{2\alpha-1}.

(4.13)

Proof.

In view of Theorem
2.3
, we know that
u\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}\times(0,T))
, and

S:=S\left(m,\frac{t_{0}}{2},2t_{0}\right)=\left\{(x,t):\frac{t_{0}}{2}\leq t\leq 2t_{0},\gamma_{L}(t)\leq x\leq\gamma_{R}(t)\right\}.

(4.14)

We claim that

\max_{S}u=\max_{S\cap\{t=t_{0}/2\}}u,\,\,\text{ and }\,\,\,\min_{S}u=\min_{S\cap\{t=2t_{0}\}}u.

(4.15)

To see this, we switch to Lagrangian coordinates, setting
\overline{u}(x,t)=u(\gamma(x,t),t)
. In these coordinates, the set
S
becomes the rectangle
[a_{0},b_{0}]\times[t_{0}/2,2t_{0}].
Therefore, we infer from (
2.9
), (
2.11
), and (
MFG
) that

\overline{u}_{t}(x,t)=u_{t}(\gamma(x,t),t)+\gamma_{t}(x,t)u_{x}(\gamma(x,t),t)=u_{t}-u_{x}^{2}=-m^{\theta}-\frac{1}{2}u_{x}^{2}\leq 0,

which implies (
4.15
). Now, given
(x,t_{0}/2)\in S\cap\{t=t_{0}/2\}
and
(y,2t_{0})\in S\cap\{t=2t_{0}\}
, we let
\beta:[t_{0}/2,2t_{0}]\to\mathbb{R}
be the linear function joining
(x,t_{0}/2)
and
(y,2t_{0})
. As a result of (
4.7
) and (
4.14
), and recalling that
t_{0}\geq\delta>0,
we have, for some constant
C>0
depending on
\delta
,

|\dot{\beta}|=\frac{|y-x|}{t_{0}}\leq Ct_{0}^{\alpha-1}.

From Proposition
4.3
, recalling (
4.4
), and using
\beta
as a competitor in (
4.12
), we infer that, up to increasing the value of
C
,

u(x,t_{0}/2)\leq\int_{\frac{t_{0}}{2}}^{2t_{0}}\left(\frac{1}{2}|\dot{\beta}(s)|^{2}+m(\beta(s),s)^{\theta}\right)ds+u(y,2t_{0})\leq Ct_{0}^{2\alpha-1}+u(y,2t_{0}).

Thus, (
4.13
) follows from (
4.15
).
∎

We turn our attention to obtaining the optimal rate for the gradient. We will first reduce the problem to estimating
u_{x}
on the region
\{m>0\}
, where
u
is known to be smooth and its oscillation has now been optimally quantified. This reduction is done through optimal control arguments that exploit the convex shape of the support.

To make these geometric arguments more transparent, we first characterize the optimal trajectories of (
1.1
). Of course, in the region where the density is positive, these trajectories are uniquely given by the (smooth) Lagrangian flow
\gamma
, according to Theorem
2.4
. The following proposition describes the remaining trajectories (up to interchanging
\gamma_{L}
and
\gamma_{R}
).

Proposition 4.8

(Identification of the optimal trajectories in the vanishing set)
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
2.3
, let
(u,m)
be the solution to (
MFG
)–(
TC
), and let
\gamma_{L}
be its left free boundary curve. Let

l(s)=\gamma_{L}(T)+\dot{\gamma}_{L}(T)(s-T),\quad s\in[0,T]

be the supporting line for the decreasing, convex function
\gamma_{L}
at
t=T
. Assume that
(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}\times(0,T),
with
x\leq\gamma_{L}(t)
, and let
\beta:[t,T]\to\mathbb{R}
be an optimal trajectory for (
1.1
). Then
\beta\in C^{1}([t,T])
is uniquely determined as follows:

1.

If
x\leq\gamma_{L}(T)
, then
\beta
is the straight line
\dot{\beta}\equiv 0
.

2.

If
\gamma_{L}(T)<x\leq l(t)
, then
\beta
is the straight line joining the points
(x,t)
and
(\gamma_{L}(T),T)
.

3.

If
l(t)<x\leq\gamma_{L}(t)
, then there exists
t^{*}\in(t,T)
such that

\beta(s)=\begin{cases}x-u_{x}(x,t)(s-t)&\,\,s\in[t,t^{*}],\\
\gamma_{L}(s)&\,\,s\in[t^{*},T].\end{cases}

Proof.

We will tacitly use the fact that, from Theorem
2.3
, the function
\gamma_{L}
is strictly convex and strictly decreasing on
(0,T)
. The
C^{1}([t,T))
regularity of
\beta
follows from the fact that
u\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}\times(0,T))
, and from the optimality condition for (
4.12
), namely that
\beta
solves the differential equation

\begin{cases}\dot{\beta}(s)=-u_{x}(\beta(s),s)&\,\,s\in(t,T),\\
\beta(t)=x.\end{cases}

(4.16)

The
C^{1}
regularity up to
t=T
will follow later from the explicit form of
\beta
. Assume that
(x_{1},t_{1})\in\mathbb{R}\times(0,T)
is such that
x_{1}<\gamma_{L}(t_{1})
. Then, since
u\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}\times(0,T)),
is a classical solution to the HJ equation

-u_{t}+\frac{1}{2}u_{x}^{2}=0

near
(x_{1},t_{1})
, we must have
u\in C^{1,1}_{\text{loc}}
at such points, with bounds that may degenerate near the interface (see
[
19
, Thm 15.1]
). As a result, any characteristic curve passing through
(x_{1},t_{1})
is locally a straight line. In particular, (
4.16
) has a unique solution at such points, and the minimizer
\beta(\cdot)
is a uniquely determined line segment until (and if) it hits the interface
\gamma_{L}
. More precisely,
\beta
is determined on the interval
(t,t^{*})
as the linear function with slope

\dot{\beta}(s)=-u_{x}(x,t),\quad s\in[t,t^{*}),

where

t^{*}=\sup\{q\in(t,T):m(\beta(s),s)=0\text{ for every }s\in(t,q)\}.

We also reiterate that, by Theorem
2.4
, the optimal trajectories in
\{m>0\}\cap(\mathbb{R}\times[0,T])
are uniquely determined by the (smooth) Lagrangian flow
\gamma
. In particular, such trajectories never touch the free boundary. For this reason,

\beta(s)\leq\gamma_{L}(s),\,\,\,s\in[t,T].

(4.17)

We claim that if
t^{*}<T
, then
\beta\equiv\gamma_{L}
on
[t^{*},T]
. Indeed, otherwise there would be a maximal time
t^{**}\in[t^{*},T)
such that
\beta\equiv\gamma_{L}
on
[t^{*},t^{**}]
. From (
4.17
) and the
C^{1}
regularity of
\beta
, we see that
\dot{\beta}(t^{**})=\dot{\gamma}_{L}(t^{**})
. By the definition of
t^{**}
and the convexity of
\gamma_{L}
,
\beta
must then be a straight line on
[t^{**},T],
which yields

\int_{t^{**}}^{T}\left(\frac{1}{2}|\dot{\beta}|^{2}+m(\beta(s),s)^{\theta}\right)ds+c_{T}m^{\theta}(\beta(T),T)=\int_{t^{**}}^{T}\frac{1}{2}|\dot{\gamma}_{L}(t^{**})|^{2}ds>\int_{t^{**}}^{T}\frac{1}{2}|\dot{\gamma}_{L}(s)|^{2}ds\\
=\int_{t^{**}}^{T}\left(\frac{1}{2}|\dot{\gamma}_{L}(s)|^{2}+m(\gamma_{L}(s),s)^{\theta}\right)ds+c_{T}m^{\theta}(\gamma_{L}(T),T),

contradicting the optimality of
\beta
.

Summing up, we have so far shown the following characterization: the minimizer
\beta\in C^{1}([t,T])
satisfies
\beta\leq\gamma_{L}
, it is a straight line on the interval
[t,t^{*}]
, and coincides with
\gamma_{L}
on the (possibly empty) interval
(t^{*},T]
. We now proceed to the proof of the lemma, in each of the three cases.

First, assume that
x\leq\gamma_{L}(T)
. Then, by the monotonicity of
\gamma_{L}
, we have
m(x,s)=0
for
s\in[t,T]
. Thus, the minimal cost
0
in (
1.1
) must be
0
, which is achieved if and only if
\dot{\beta}\equiv 0
.

Next, assume that
\gamma_{L}(T)<x\leq l(t).
Note that we must have
\beta(T)=\gamma_{L}(T)
. Otherwise,
\beta
would intersect the (constant) optimal trajectory
s\mapsto\gamma_{L}(T)
at some interior time
s<T
, which is impossible by the previously explained local uniqueness. Thus, it is enough to show that
\beta
is a straight line, that is, that
t^{*}=T
. But note that, by the strict convexity of
\gamma_{L}
,

\dot{\beta}(t)=\dot{\beta}(t^{*})=\dot{\gamma}_{L}(t^{*})<\dot{\gamma}_{L}(T)=\dot{l}(t),

unless
t^{*}=T
. Thus,
\beta\leq l<\gamma_{L}
on
[t,t^{*}]
, unless
t^{*}=T
. However, by definition of
t^{*}
,
\beta(t^{*})=\gamma_{L}(t^{*})
, so
t^{*}=T
, as wanted.

Finally, assume that
l(t)<x\leq\gamma_{L}(t)
. We just need to show that
\beta
is not a straight line. As in the previous case, we must have
\beta(T)=\gamma_{L}(T)
. But the linear function
l_{1}:[t,T]\to\mathbb{R}
connecting
(x,t)
and
(\gamma_{L}(T),T)
has slope

\dot{l}_{1}(t)=\frac{\gamma_{L}(T)-x}{T-t}<\frac{\gamma_{L}(T)-l(t)}{T-t}=\dot{l}(t)=\dot{\gamma}_{L}(T).

Thus,
l_{1}>\gamma_{L}
near
s=T
, which means, by (
4.17
), that
\beta\neq l_{1}
. Hence,
\beta
is not a straight line, as wanted.

∎

Having identified the optimal trajectories, we can now readily bound the space derivative of
u
outside of
\{m>0\}
, in terms of the speed of the free boundary curves.

Corollary 4.9
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
2.3
, let
(u,m)
be the solution to (
MFG
)–(
TC
). Then, for every
(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}\times(0,T)
such that
m(x,t)=0
, we have

|u_{x}(x,t)|\leq\max(|\dot{\gamma}_{L}(t)|,|\dot{\gamma}_{R}(t)|).

(4.18)

Proof.

By symmetry, we may assume that
x\leq\gamma_{L}(t)
. Let
\beta
be the optimal trajectory for (
1.1
). Note that, by (
4.16
),
u_{x}(x,t)=-\dot{\beta}(t)
. The result now follows from the convexity of
\gamma_{L}
. Indeed, by Proposition
4.8
, there are three possibilities:

1.

\beta
is constant, in which case
u_{x}(x,t)=0
.

2.

\beta
is a straight line, with the negative slope

\dot{\beta}(t)=\frac{\gamma_{L}(T)-x}{T-t}\geq\frac{\gamma_{L}(T)-\gamma(t)}{T-t}>\dot{\gamma}_{L}(t).

3.

\beta
is a straight line on some interval
[t,t^{*}]
, with the negative slope

\dot{\beta}(t)=\dot{\beta}(t^{*})=\dot{\gamma}_{L}(t^{*})\geq\dot{\gamma}_{L}(t).

∎

To complete the estimates of this subsection, we employ a localized version of the standard gradient estimate (
[
22
, Thm. 3.9]
,
[
6

to obtain the sharp algebraic rate of decay as

Proposition 4.10

(Optimal rate for the gradient of

)
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
2.3
, let
(u,m)
be the solution to (
MFG
)–(
TC
). For each

and each

Proof.

Since the estimate is allowed to degenerate as

, so that

. First note that, in view of the first equation of (

, and (

. We will use the fact that,

S:=S(m,t_{0}/2,2t_{0})=\{(x,t):t_{0}/2\leq t\leq t_{0},\gamma_{L}(t)\leq x\leq\gamma_{R}(t)\},\quad\omega=\underset{S}{\text{osc}}(u),\quad\tilde{u}=u-\min_{S}u,

(4.21)

on

(x,t)\in S

in

t\in(t_{0}/2,2t_{0})

or

) and Corollary
4.9
. Hence, we may assume that
\gamma_{L}(t)<x<\gamma_{R}(t)

so that, writing

k=\varepsilon/t_{0}^{2\alpha},

|u_{xx}|=\frac{\varepsilon}{t_{0}^{2\alpha}}|\tilde{u}|\leq k\omega.

Thus, through direct computation, we obtain from (

), (

), and (

Thus, (

L_{u}\left(-\zeta_{1}\right)\leq\frac{8At_{0}^{2\alpha}}{(t-t_{0}/2)^{4}}.

(4.31)

For a fixed choice of

we require

4.4
Exact rates for the modulus of continuity of the density

In this subsection, we explain how the intrinsic scaling arguments of
[
6
, Sec. 4.3.1]
can be adapted to our purposes. The goal here is to obtain a Hölder continuity estimate for
m
which decays sufficiently fast with time, so as to guarantee uniform continuity of the continuous rescaling
\mu

We begin by discussing the underlying ideas behind these Hölder estimates. The starting point is to switch to Lagrangian coordinates and consider the function

p(x,t)=m(\gamma(x,t),t)^{\theta},\quad(x,t)\in[a_{0},b_{0}]\times[0,T].

(4.56)

It was shown in
[
6
, Lem. 3.5]
that

satisfies the divergence form, degenerate elliptic equation,

-\left((\gamma_{x})^{-1}p_{x}\right)_{x}-\left(\gamma_{x}(\theta p)^{-1}p_{t}\right)_{t}=0,

(4.57)

where
\gamma_{x}>0
is the space derivative of the Lagrangian flow (
2.9
).
The main point now, which was exploited in
[
6
]
, is that, thanks to the fact that the
\gamma_{x}
is bounded above and below (for bounded times), we can quantify in a precise way how the equation degenerates near the free boundary: as
x
approaches
\{a_{0},b_{0}\}
, the time diffusion blows up like
p(x,t)^{-1}
in the time direction, while the space diffusion remains bounded above and below. This quantification is, of course, intrinsic, since it depends on the value of the solution itself. However, if one considers appropriate intrinsic subdomains, Hölder continuity estimates may be obtained with the classical arguments of E. De Giorgi (
[
9
]
), which are valid for uniformly elliptic equations in divergence form having bounded and measurable coefficients. The method of intrinsic scaling is due to E. DiBenedetto (
[
10
,
11
]
, see also
[
24
]
).

The key additional observation that we will utilize here is that the long time behavior of the degeneracy can also be quantified. Indeed, in view of (
2.14
), the diffusion in (
4.57
) is of the order of
t^{-\alpha}
in the space direction, and
t^{\alpha}p(x,t)^{-1}
in the time direction. This time-dependence must be carefully tracked for the resulting continuity estimates to remain valid for large times, for the height–normalized density
\mu=t^{\alpha}m
, in the zoomed-out variables
(\eta,\tau)=(xt^{\alpha},\log(t))
.

[

Proof.

We first show existence, and begin by explaining why, for this subcritical range of

satisfies (

\tilde{u}(x,t)=\inf_{\beta\in H^{1}((t,t_{1})),\,\beta(t)=x}\int_{t}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{2}|\dot{\beta}(s)|^{2}+m(\beta(s),s)^{\theta}\right)ds,

(5.36)

which uniquely characterizes the value function in terms of

5.3
The critical case
\theta=2

When
\theta=2
, the quantitative results of Proposition
3.10
break down. The global uniqueness results from the previous section fail as well, because the limiting solution
u
may, in principle, be unbounded both as
t\to 0^{+}
and as
t\to\infty
(see (
1.5
) and (
4.46
)).

Our strategy here will be to insist on uniquely characterizing the subsequential limits of
(u^{T},m^{T})
, at least in the region
\{m>0\}
, through a partial well-posedness result. The unbounded behavior of
u
as
t\to\infty
will be addressed by exploiting the unique scaling of the case
\theta=2
, which is precisely the correct one to leverage the convergence results of Section
3
in the uniqueness argument.
The lack of regularity up to
t=0
, on the other hand, will be addressed by weakening the definition of solution, as follows (cf.
[
3
,
5
,
17
]
). We denote by
{\mathcal{P}}_{1}(\mathbb{R})
the space of probability measures on
\mathbb{R}
which have a finite first order moment, equipped with the Monge-Kantorovich distance
d_{1}
.

Definition 5.6
.

We say that
(u,m)\in W^{1,\infty}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R}\times(0,\infty))\times C(\mathbb{R}\times(0,\infty))
is a weak solution to (

) if
m\in C([0,T],{\mathcal{P}}_{1}(\mathbb{R}))
, the first equation holds in the viscosity sense, the second equation holds in the distributional sense, and
m(\cdot,0)=m_{0}
holds in the
d_{1}
sense.

We can now show the main convergence result for the critical case.

Theorem 5.7
.

Assume that
m_{0}
satisfies (
2.1
) and (
2.2

. There exists a weak solution

m^{T}\to m\,\,\text{ uniformly on compact subsets of }\,\,\mathbb{R}\times\left[0,\infty\right),

(5.37)

(5.38)

where

is the solution to (

(u,m)
satisfies, for every

, and
a=\int_{\mathbb{R}}m_{0}>0

5.23

5.25

Furthermore,

is the unique weak solution to (

Proof.

As in the proof of Proposition
5.3
, since the conclusion is invariant under vertical translations of

according to (

It remains to show uniqueness, as well as the limits (

Let

To treat the lack of regularity at

\int_{\mathbb{R}}(m(\cdot,t)-\tilde{m}(\cdot,t))(u(\cdot,t)-\tilde{u}(\cdot,t))+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\frac{1}{2}(m+\tilde{m})|u_{x}-\tilde{u}_{x}|^{2}+(m^{\theta}-\tilde{m}^{\theta})(m-\tilde{m})\right)=0.

Let

\lim_{\tau\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}}w(\eta,\tau)(\mu(\eta,\tau)-M_{a}(\eta))d\eta=\lim_{\tau\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\tilde{w}(\eta,\tau)(\tilde{\mu}(\eta,\tau)-M_{a}(\eta))d\eta=0.

(u,m)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}w(\eta,\tau)(\mu(\eta,\tau)-M_{a}(\eta))d\eta=t^{2\alpha-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}}u(x,t)(m(x,t)-\mathcal{M}_{a}(x,t)))dx,

\int_{\mathbb{R}}(m(\cdot,t)-\tilde{m}(\cdot,t))(u(\cdot,t)-\tilde{u}(\cdot,t))\\

6
The planning problem

In this final section, we explain the necessary modifications to the preceding arguments in order to prove the main results for the case of the planning problem (

)–(
P
). The changes needed are relatively small. A minor point is that the estimates of Theorem
2.4
now involve the function
{d}
defined by (

. This is not an issue, however, since our results concern the intermediate asymptotic behavior in which

. Besides this, there are two main difficulties.

The first difficulty is the fact that the free boundary curves are no longer known to be monotone. This issue is readily addressed in Lemma
6.1
below, by showing that monotonicity still holds in the interval

where

is a small constant.

The second difficulty is that, while the function
u
in (

)–(
TC
) is globally unique,
u
in (

)–(
P
) is only unique (up to a constant) in the set
\{m>0\}
. For this reason, the optimal trajectories outside the support of
m
cannot be characterized in this case as they were in Proposition
4.8
. Instead, we must argue that, given any solution
(\tilde{u},m)
there exists a sufficiently well-behaved solution
(u,m)
that agrees with
\tilde{u}
in the support of

. This will be explained in Proposition
6.2
below.

Lemma 6.1

This is the step where the planning problem differs the most from (

)–(

, if necessary, outside of the support of

\Omega_{1}=\{0\leq t\leq t^{*},\,\gamma_{L}(t^{*})\leq x\leq\gamma_{L}(t)\},\quad\Omega_{2}=\{0\leq t\leq t^{*},\,\,x\leq\gamma_{L}(t^{*})\},

(6.13)
