Abstract

1.
Introduction

Harmonic maps
are the critical points of the
energy functional

(1.1)

E(\phi)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{M}\,|d\phi|^{2}\,dv_{M}\,\,,

where
\phi:M\to N
is a smooth map from a compact Riemannian
manifold
(M^{m},g)
to a Riemannian
manifold
(N^{n},h)
. In particular,
\phi
is harmonic if it is a solution of the Euler-Lagrange system of equations associated to (
1.1
), i.e.,

(1.2)

-d^{*}d\phi={\trace}\,\nabla d\phi=0\,\,.

The left member of (
1.2
) is a vector field along the map
\phi
or, equivalently, a section of the pull-back bundle
\phi^{-1}TN
: it is called
tension field
and denoted
\tau(\phi)
. In addition, we recall that, if
\phi
is an
isometric immersion
, then
\phi
is a harmonic map if and only if it defines a
minimal submanifold
of
N
(see
[
7
,
8
]
for background).

A related topic of growing interest is the study of
biharmonic maps
. As suggested in
[
8
]
,
[
9
]
, these maps, which provide a natural generalisation of harmonic maps, are defined as the critical points of the
bienergy functional

E_{2}(\phi)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{M}\,|d^{*}d\phi|^{2}\,dv_{M}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{M}\,|\tau(\phi)|^{2}\,dv_{M}\,\,.

There have been extensive studies on biharmonic maps. We refer to
[
5
,
11
,
13
,
15
,
20
,
22
]
for an introduction to this topic.
We observe that, obviously, any harmonic map is trivially biharmonic and an absolute minimum for the bienergy. Therefore, we say that a biharmonic map is
proper
if it is not harmonic and, similarly, a biharmonic isometric immersion is
proper
if it is not minimal.

Our paper is devoted to the study of the second variation of the bienergy functional. In order to introduce this topic, it is convenient to recall some basic facts about the generalised Jacobi operator
I_{2}^{\phi}(V)
and the definition of index and nullity. More specifically, let
\phi:M\to N
be a biharmonic map. We shall consider a two-parameter smooth variation
\left\{\phi_{t,s}\right\}

(-\varepsilon<t,s<\varepsilon,\,\phi_{0,0}=\phi)
and denote by
V,W
their associated vector fields:

(1.3)

\displaystyle V(x)=\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\right|_{t=0}\phi_{t,0}(x)\in T_{\phi(x)}N

\displaystyle W(x)=\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\right|_{s=0}\phi_{0,s}(x)\in T_{\phi(x)}N\,.

Note that
V
and
W
are sections of
\phi^{-1}TN
. The
Hessian
of the bienergy functional
E_{2}
at its critical point
\phi
is defined by

H(E_{2})_{\phi}(V,W)=\left.\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t\partial s}\right|_{(t,s)=(0,0)}E_{2}(\phi_{t,s})\,.

The following theorem was obtained by Jiang and translated by Urakawa
[
11
]
:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\phi:M\to N
be a biharmonic map between two Riemannian manifolds
(M^{m},g)
and
(N^{n},h)
, where
M
is compact. Then the Hessian of the bienergy functional
E_{2}
at a critical point
\phi
is given by

H(E_{2})_{\phi}(V,W)=\left(I_{2}^{\phi}(V),W\right)=\int_{M}\langle I_{2}^{\phi}(V),W\rangle\,dv_{M}\,\,,

where
I_{2}^{\phi}=\,:\mathcal{C}\left(\phi^{-1}TN\right)\to\mathcal{C}\left(\phi^{-1}TN\right)
is a semilinear elliptic operator of order
4
.

When the context is clear, we shall just write
I_{2}
instead of
I_{2}^{\phi}
.

Next, we want to give an explicit description of the operator
I_{2}
. To this purpose, let
\nabla^{M},\nabla^{N}
and
\nabla^{\phi}
be the induced connections on the bundles
TM,TN
and
\phi^{-1}TN
respectively. Then the
rough Laplacian
on sections of
\phi^{-1}TN
, denoted by
\overline{\Delta}
, is defined by

\overline{\Delta}=d^{*}d=-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Big\{\nabla^{\phi}_{e_{i}}\nabla^{\phi}_{e_{i}}-\nabla^{\phi}_{\nabla^{M}_{e_{i}}e_{i}}\Big\}\,\,,

where
\{e_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
is a local orthonormal frame field tangent to
M
.

Let
\mathbb{S}^{n}(R)
denote the Euclidean
n
-dimensional sphere of radius
R
. We shall write
\mathbb{S}^{n}
when
R=1
. In the present paper, we shall only need the explicit expression of
I_{2}(V)
in the case that the target manifold is
\mathbb{S}^{n}
. This useful formula, which was first given in
[
19
]
and can be deduced from a more general result in
[
11
]
, is the following:

(1.4)

\displaystyle I_{2}(V)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\overline{\Delta}^{2}V+\overline{\Delta}\left({\rm trace}\langle V,d\phi\cdot\rangle d\phi\cdot-|d\phi|^{2}\,V\right)+2\langle d\tau(\phi),d\phi\rangle V+|\tau(\phi)|^{2}V

\displaystyle-2\,{\rm trace}\langle V,d\tau(\phi)\cdot\rangle d\phi\cdot-2\,{\rm trace}\langle\tau(\phi),dV\cdot\rangle d\phi\cdot-\langle\tau(\phi),V\rangle\tau(\phi)

\displaystyle+{\rm trace}\langle d\phi\cdot,\overline{\Delta}V\rangle d\phi\cdot+{\rm trace}\langle d\phi\cdot,\left({\rm trace}\langle V,d\phi\cdot\rangle d\phi\cdot\right)\rangle d\phi\cdot

\displaystyle-2|d\phi|^{2}\,{\rm trace}\langle d\phi\cdot,V\rangle d\phi\cdot+2\langle dV,d\phi\rangle\tau(\phi)-|d\phi|^{2}\,\overline{\Delta}V+|d\phi|^{4}V\,,

where
\cdot
denotes trace with respect to a local orthonormal frame field on
M
.

Next, we recall from the general theory that, since
M
is compact, the spectrum

\mu_{1}<\mu_{2}<\ldots<\mu_{i}<\ldots

of the generalised Jacobi operator
I_{2}(V)
associated to the bienergy is
discrete
and tends to
+\infty
as
i
tends to
+\infty
. We denote by
\mathcal{V}_{i}
the eigenspace associated to the eigenvalue
\mu_{i}
. Then we can define the biharmonic
index
of
\phi
as follows (see, for instance,
[
24
]
):

(1.5)

{\rm Index}_{2}(\phi)=\sum_{\mu_{i}<0}\dim(\mathcal{V}_{i})\,.

The biharmonic
nullity
of
\phi
is defined as the dimension of the kernel of
I_{2}
:

(1.6)

{\rm Null}_{2}(\phi)=\dim\left\{V\in\mathcal{C}\left(\phi^{-1}TN\right)\,:\,I_{2}(V)=0\right\}=\dim\left({\rm Ker}(I_{2})\right)\,.

We say that a biharmonic map
\phi:M\to N
is
stable
if
{\rm Index}_{2}(\phi)=0
. As pointed out in
[
8
]
, this definition has to be regarded as a notion of
second order stability
. This notion has a geometric meaning, that is it does not depend on the variation
\{\phi_{t}\}
but only on the associated vector field
V=\partial\phi_{t}/\penalty\partial t\big|_{t=0}
. On the other hand, we know that for a variation
\{\phi_{t}\}
with associated vector field
V
which belongs to
{\rm Ker}(I_{2})
, it may happen that

(1.7)

\left.\frac{d^{\ell}}{dt^{\ell}}\right|_{t=0}E_{2}(\phi_{t})=0\,,\quad\ell=1,\ldots,k-1\,;\quad\left.\frac{d^{k}}{dt^{k}}\right|_{t=0}E_{2}(\phi_{t})<0\,\quad{\rm for\,some\,even}\,\,k\geq 4\,.

If (
1.7
) occurs, then the function
E_{2}(\phi_{t})
has a local maximum at
t=0
.

In general, the study of the second variation of the bienergy functional is a complicated task and there are not many papers dealing with computations and estimates of the index and nullity of some proper biharmonic submanifolds in the Euclidean unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{n}
(for instance, see
[

13

A natural first step is to investigate the second variation of a proper biharmonic submanifold of

which lies in the small hypersphere

\displaystyle\Phi:\mbox{${\mathbb{T}}$}\quad

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}^{4}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{5}

Let

\begin{array}[]{llll}{\rm(i)}&{\rm Index}_{2}(\Phi)&=&1\,;\\

In Section

We observe that the Clifford torus

In this context, we shall study a more general composition

The first step is to derive an explicit expression for the operator

\Phi

More specifically, using Cartesian coordinates
y=\left(y^{1},y^{2},y^{3},y^{4},y^{5}\right)

{\mathbb{R}}^{5}

(2.1)

V_{\gamma}=Y_{\gamma}(\Phi)\,;\quad V_{\vartheta}=Y_{\vartheta}(\Phi)\,;\quad V_{\nu}=Y_{\nu}(\Phi)\,;\quad V_{\eta}=Y_{\eta}(\Phi)\,,

(2.2)

\begin{array}[]{lll}Y_{\gamma}&=&2\left(-y^{2},y^{1},0,0,0\right)\left(=\left(-2y^{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial y^{1}}+2y^{1}\frac{\partial}{\partial y^{2}}\right)\right)\,;\\

provides an orthonormal basis of

at each point of the image of
\Phi

\mathbb{S}^{4}

By way of summary, we conclude that each section

can be written as

(2.3)

where

and

Our first goal is to compute

4.

-harmonic index and nullity of

So far, we computed the biharmonic index and the biharmonic nullity of

.
The aim of the first part of this section is to carry out a detailed study of a natural sub-class of variations, i.e., variations of the form
\Phi_{s,t}=i\circ\varphi_{s,t}
. As a completion of this analysis, in the second part of the section we shall compute the
p
-harmonic index and nullity of
\varphi

Now, more generally, let
\tilde{\Phi}=\tilde{\varphi}\circ i
, where
\tilde{\varphi}:M^{m}\to\mathbb{S}^{n-1}(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}})
,
m\geq 1,\,n\geq 3
, is a minimal immersion and
i:\mathbb{S}^{n-1}(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}})\to\mathbb{S}^{n}
, is the biharmonic small hypersphere. The map
\tilde{\Phi}
is proper biharmonic and we shall determine a general

condition which ensures that the second variation of
\tilde{\Phi}
is nonnegatively defined on
\mathcal{C}\big(\tilde{\varphi}^{-1}T\mathbb{S}^{n-1}(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}})\big)

To this purpose, we study
2
-parameter variations

\tilde{\Phi}_{s,t}=i\circ\tilde{\varphi}_{s,t}\,,

where

\tilde{\varphi}_{s,t}:M^{m}\to\mathbb{S}^{n-1}(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}})

is a
2
-parameter variation of
\tilde{\varphi}
.

Let
V,W
be vector fields associated to
\tilde{\Phi}_{s,t}
as in (
1.3
). We denote the subspace of these vector fields as

\mathcal{W}^{\tilde{\varphi}}\subset\mathcal{C}\big(\tilde{\Phi}^{-1}(T\mathbb{S}^{n})\big)

(note that, geometrically, there is a natural identification between
\mathcal{W}^{\tilde{\varphi}}
and
\mathcal{C}\big(\tilde{\varphi}^{-1}T\mathbb{S}^{n-1}(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}})\big)
).
Now,

E_{2}\left(\tilde{\Phi}_{s,t}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{M}\left|\tau\left(\tilde{\Phi}_{s,t}\right)\right|^{2}dV_{M}

and we observe that, in this case,

(4.1)

\left|\tau\left(\tilde{\Phi}_{s,t}\right)\right|^{2}=\left|di\big(\tau(\tilde{\varphi}_{s,t})\big)+{\rm trace}\nabla di(d\tilde{\varphi}_{s,t}\cdot,d\tilde{\varphi}_{s,t}\cdot)\right|^{2}=|\tau\big(\tilde{\varphi}_{s,t}\big)|^{2}+|d\tilde{\varphi}_{s,t}|^{4}

where the last equality is a consequence of the fact that

is an isometric immersion and the two terms are orthogonal.

As a consequence of (
4.1
), for all

we have:

(I_{2}^{\tilde{\Phi}}(V),W)=(I_{2}^{\tilde{\varphi}}(V),W)+2(J^{\tilde{\varphi}}_{(4)}(V),W)=((J^{\tilde{\varphi}})^{2}(V),W)+2\,(J^{\tilde{\varphi}}_{(4)}(V),W)\,,

where

is the Jacobi operator associated to the
p
-energy and we write
J^{\tilde{\varphi}}
for

.

Now, let
\pi:\mathcal{C}\big(\tilde{\Phi}^{-1}(T\mathbb{S}^{n})\big)\to\mathcal{W}^{\tilde{\varphi}}

(4.2)

I_{2}^{\tilde{\Phi},\pi}(V)=\pi\big(I_{2}^{\tilde{\Phi}}(V)\big)=(J^{\tilde{\varphi}})^{2}(V)+2\,J^{\tilde{\varphi}}_{(4)}(V)\quad\forall V\in\mathcal{W}^{\tilde{\varphi}}\,.

We want to study the quadratic form

(4.3)

H(E_{2})_{\tilde{\Phi}}(V,W)=(I_{2}^{\tilde{\Phi},\pi}(V),W)\quad\forall\,V,W\in\mathcal{W}^{\tilde{\varphi}}\,.

Our general result in this context is:

Theorem 4.1
.

Suppose that no eigenvalue of
J^{\tilde{\varphi}}
belongs to the open interval
(-2m,0)
. Then the quadratic form (
4.3
) is nonnegatively defined, i.e.,

(4.4)

(I_{2}^{\tilde{\Phi},\pi}(V),V)\geq 0\quad\forall\,V\in\mathcal{W}^{\tilde{\varphi}}\,.

Proof.

We know that

(4.5)

\displaystyle J^{\tilde{\varphi}}(V)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\overline{\Delta}V+{\rm trace}\Big({\rm Riem}^{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}(1/\penalty\sqrt{2})}\big(d\tilde{\varphi}(\cdot),V\big)d\tilde{\varphi}(\cdot)\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\overline{\Delta}V+{\rm trace}\Big(2\big[-\langle d\tilde{\varphi}(\cdot),d\tilde{\varphi}(\cdot)\rangle V+\langle V,d\tilde{\varphi}(\cdot)\rangle d\tilde{\varphi}(\cdot)\big]\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\overline{\Delta}V+{\rm trace}\Big(2\big[-mV+V^{\top}\big]\Big)\,,

where, for
V\in\mathcal{W}^{\tilde{\varphi}}
,
V^{\top}
and
V^{\perp}
are respectively the tangent and the normal component.

The expression of
J^{\tilde{\varphi}}_{(p)}
was computed in
[
18
]
for a generic harmonic map
\tilde{\varphi}
. Because, in our context,
\tilde{\varphi}
is a minimal immersion, (1.9) of
[
18
]
simplifies and gives:

(4.6)

J^{\tilde{\varphi}}_{(p)}(V)=(p-2)m^{\frac{p-4}{2}}\,d^{*}\big(\langle dV,d\tilde{\varphi}\rangle d\tilde{\varphi}\big)+m^{\frac{p-2}{2}}\,J^{\tilde{\varphi}}(V)\,.

Now, using (
4.6
) with

(4.7)

Since
\tilde{\varphi}
is an immersion, there exists a unique vector field
\xi\in\mathcal{C}(TM)
such that

We prove that

(4.8)

\Big(d^{*}\big(\langle dV,d\tilde{\varphi}\rangle d\tilde{\varphi}\big),V\Big)=\int_{M}({\rm div}\xi)^{2}dV_{M}\,.

Indeed, let
P\in M
be arbitrarily fixed. We use a geodesic frame field
\{X_{i}\}
around
P
. Then, at

we have:

\displaystyle\langle dV,d\tilde{\varphi}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\langle\nabla_{X_{i}}^{\tilde{\varphi}}(V^{\top}+V^{\perp}),d\tilde{\varphi}(X_{i})\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Big\{\langle\nabla^{M}_{X_{i}}\xi,X_{i}\rangle+\langle B(X_{i},\xi),X_{i}\rangle

\displaystyle+\langle\nabla^{\perp}_{X_{i}}V^{\perp},X_{i}\rangle-\langle A_{V^{\perp}}(X_{i}),X_{i}\rangle\Big\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Big\{\langle\nabla^{M}_{X_{i}}\xi,X_{i}\rangle-\langle A_{V^{\perp}}(X_{i}),X_{i}\rangle\Big\}

\displaystyle{\rm div}(\xi)-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\langle B(X_{i},X_{i}),V^{\perp}\rangle

\displaystyle{\rm div}(\xi)-m\langle H,V^{\perp}\rangle={\rm div}(\xi)\,,

where

and

\Big(d^{*}\big(\langle dV,d\tilde{\varphi}\rangle d\tilde{\varphi}\big),V\Big)=\int_{M}\left(\langle dV,d\tilde{\varphi}\rangle\right)^{2}dV_{M}=\int_{M}({\rm div}\xi)^{2}dV_{M}

Now, we can end the proof. Let

and from this the conclusion of Theorem
4.1
follows immediately.
∎

and from this

\frac{(J(V_{a}),V_{a})}{(V_{a},V_{a})}=-\frac{4}{3}>\mu_{1}\,.

We deduce that the Hessian is negatively defined on
\mathcal{W}_{{\rm conf}}
, but we have
\mathcal{W}_{{\rm conf}}\cap\mathcal{W}_{\mu_{1}}=\{\vec{0}\}
.

By contrast, we have verified that the Hessian of
\Phi
is positively defined on the subspace determined by the conformal vector fields on
\mathbb{S}^{4}
.

The proof of Theorem
4.3
has shown that
J^{\varphi}
admits the negative eigenvalue
\mu=4-4\sqrt{2}\in(-4,0)
. Therefore, in this case, the hypothesis of Theorem
4.1
is not verified. Thus, in this case, the study of the quadratic form (
4.3
) must be carried out directly. The result of this investigation is:

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
H(E_{2})_{\Phi}(V,W)
be the quadratic form defined as in (
4.3
).
Then

(I_{2}^{\Phi,\pi}(V),V)\geq 0\quad\forall\,V\in\mathcal{W}^{\tilde{\varphi}}\,.

Proof.

We know from Theorem
1.2
that Index
{}_{2}(\Phi)=1
and
I_{2}^{\Phi}(V_{\eta})=\tilde{\mu}V_{\eta}
, with
\tilde{\mu}=-16<0
. We argue by contradiction and assume that
I_{2}^{\Phi,\pi}
has a negative eigenvalue. Then there exists
V^{*}\in\mathcal{W}^{\varphi}
such that
I_{2}^{\Phi,\pi}(V^{*})=\mu^{*}V^{*}
, with
\mu^{*}<0
. Because both
V^{*}
and
I_{2}^{\Phi}(V^{*})
are orthogonal to
V_{\eta}
, it is easy to conclude that
I_{2}^{\Phi}
would be negatively defined on the
2
-dimensional subspace spanned by
V^{*}
and
V_{\eta}
, a fact which contradicts Theorem
1.2
and completes the proof of the proposition.
∎

5.
Equivariant Index and Nullity

In Theorems
1.2
and
3.1
we obtained a complete description of the second variation of
\Phi:\mbox{${\mathbb{T}}$}\to\mathbb{S}^{4}
. From these results it appears that there exist essentially two geometrically significant directions, that is
V_{\eta}
, which determines the index, and
V_{\nu}
, which is the only direction of
{\rm Ker}I_{2}
which is not generated by a Killing field either of the ambient or of the domain.

The Clifford torus
\Phi(\mbox{${\mathbb{T}}$})
in
\mathbb{S}^{4}
is a
G
-invariant submanifold, with
G={\rm SO}(2)\times{\rm SO}(2)
. The main aim of this section is to show that the key directions
V_{\eta},\,V_{\nu}
could also be determined by a direct analysis in the orbit space
\mathbb{S}^{4}/\penalty G
.

It is convenient to consider the following family of maps:

\displaystyle\Phi_{\eta,\nu}:\mathbb{S}^{1}(R_{1})\times\mathbb{S}^{1}(R_{2})

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}^{4}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\times{\mathbb{R}}

(5.1)

\displaystyle\left(\gamma,\vartheta\right)

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle\Big((\sin\eta\sin\nu)e^{i\gamma},(\sin\eta\cos\nu)e^{i\vartheta},\cos\eta\Big)\,,\;0\leq\gamma,\vartheta\leq 2\pi\,.

In a map of the type (
5
), we assume that
R_{1},R_{2}>0
are fixed.

A map of the type (
5
) is
G
-equivariant, i.e.,
\Phi_{\eta,\nu}(gx)=g\Phi_{\eta,\nu}(x)
for all
x\in\mbox{${\mathbb{T}}$},\,g\in G
(here
G
acts naturally on
{\mathbb{T}}
, and on the first
4
coordinates of
{\mathbb{R}}^{4}
concerning the target).

In fact, the family of all the maps of the type (
5
) form the set
\Sigma
of the symmetric points of
C^{\infty}(\mbox{${\mathbb{T}}$},\mathbb{S}^{4})
with respect to the action of
G
(see
[
23
]
). In our case,
\Sigma
is
2
-dimensional and the tangent space to
\Sigma
at a symmetric point
\Phi_{\eta,\nu}
is generated by

\left\{\frac{\partial\Phi_{\eta,\nu}}{\partial\eta}\,,\frac{\partial\Phi_{\eta,\nu}}{\partial\nu}\right\}\,.

The orbit space
\mbox{${\mathbb{T}}$}/\penalty G
is just a point, while the orbit space
\mathcal{Q}=\mathbb{S}^{4}/\penalty G
is naturally identified with the spherical sector
0\leq\eta\leq\pi
,
0\leq\nu\leq\pi/\penalty 2
.

By way of summary, at the level of orbit spaces, the map
\Phi_{\eta,\nu}
can be identified with the point
(\eta,\nu)\in\mathcal{Q}
. To proceed further, we need to compute the bienergy function.

To this purpose, we observe that the tension field of a map of type (
5
) can be computed using

\tau(\Phi_{\eta,\nu})=-\Delta\Phi_{\eta,\nu}+\big|d\Phi_{\eta,\nu}\big|^{2}\Phi_{\eta,\nu}

and, writing
M
for
\mathbb{S}^{1}(R_{1})\times\mathbb{S}^{1}(R_{2})
, that leads us to

E_{2}(\Phi_{\eta,\nu})=\frac{1}{2}\int_{M}\big|\tau(\Phi_{\eta,\nu})\big|^{2}\,dV_{M}=\int_{M}\,\hat{E}_{2}(\eta,\nu)\,dV_{M}\,,

where, setting
c=1/\penalty(32R_{1}^{4}R_{2}^{4})
,

\displaystyle\hat{E}_{2}(\eta,\nu)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c\Big\{\big(5R_{1}^{4}-2R_{1}^{2}R_{2}^{2}+5R_{2}^{4}+(3R_{1}^{4}+2R_{1}^{2}R_{2}^{2}+3R_{2}^{4})\cos(2\eta)\big)\sin^{2}\eta

\displaystyle-2(R_{1}^{2}-R_{2}^{2})^{2}\cos(4\nu)\sin^{4}\eta+2(R_{1}^{4}-R_{2}^{4})\cos(2\nu)\sin^{2}(2\eta)\Big\}\,.

We shall call
\hat{E}_{2}(\eta,\nu)
the
reduced bienergy function
. We point out that, alternatively, the explicit expression of
\tau(\Phi_{\eta,\nu})
can also be obtained using the reduced energy function and the fact that
\tau(\Phi_{\eta,\nu})
is tangent to
\Sigma
at
\Phi_{\eta,\nu}
(see
[
17
]
).

Now, we are in the cohomogeneity zero case of the reduction theory introduced in
[
12
]
. Then, according to
[
4
, Proposition 2.5]
, the map
\Phi_{\eta,\nu}
is biharmonic if and only if
(\eta,\nu)
is a critical point of
\hat{E}_{2}
, that is

(5.2)

\frac{\partial\hat{E}_{2}}{\partial\eta}(\eta,\nu)=0\quad{\rm and}\quad\frac{\partial\hat{E}_{2}}{\partial\nu}(\eta,\nu)=0\,.

Moreover, the map
\Phi_{\eta,\nu}
is an isometric immersion if and only if

(5.3)

R_{1}=\sin\eta\sin\nu\quad{\rm and}\quad R_{2}=\sin\eta\cos\nu\,.

As we are looking for proper biharmonic immersions which are not congruent, we can assume that
0<\eta,\nu<\pi/\penalty 2
. Then we check that the only possibility to satisfy both (
5.2
) and (
5.3
) is

\eta^{*}=\frac{\pi}{4}\,,\quad\nu^{*}=\frac{\pi}{4}

which give
R_{1}=R_{2}=1/\penalty 2
and so
\Phi_{\eta^{*},\nu^{*}}=\Phi
. The counterparts of
V_{\eta},\,V_{\nu}
in the orbit space can be described as follows. Let

\pi:\mathbb{S}^{4}\to\big(\mathcal{Q},\sin^{2}\eta\,d\nu^{2}+d\eta^{2}\big)

be the canonical projection. Then
V_{\eta},\,V_{\nu}
are horizontal with respect to
\pi
and

(5.4)

d\pi(V_{\eta})=\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}\,,\quad d\pi(V_{\nu})=\sqrt{2}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\nu}\,.

Now, we can proceed to the study of the
equivariant second variation
, a notion which was introduced in
[
16
]
.

To this purpose, we compute the Hessian matrix of
\hat{E}_{2}
at the critical point
(\eta^{*},\nu^{*})
. Setting
R_{1}=R_{2}=(1/\penalty 2)
, we obtain

\left[\begin{array}[]{rr}\displaystyle{\frac{\partial^{2}\hat{E}_{2}}{\partial\eta^{2}}}&\displaystyle{\frac{\partial^{2}\hat{E}_{2}}{\partial\eta\partial\nu}}\\
&\\
\displaystyle{\frac{\partial^{2}\hat{E}_{2}}{\partial\eta\partial\nu}}&\displaystyle{\frac{\partial^{2}\hat{E}_{2}}{\partial\nu^{2}}}\end{array}\right]_{(\eta^{*},\nu^{*})}=\left[\begin{array}[]{rr}-16&0\\
0&0\end{array}\right]\left(=\frac{1}{{\rm Vol}(M)}\left[\begin{array}[]{rr}(I_{2}(V_{\eta}),V_{\eta})&\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(I_{2}(V_{\nu}),V_{\eta})\\
&\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(I_{2}(V_{\eta}),V_{\nu})&\frac{1}{2}(I_{2}(V_{\nu}),V_{\nu})\end{array}\right]\right)\,.

Therefore, this analysis in the orbit space at the critical point
(\eta^{*},\nu^{*})
tells us that the
equivariant index
of
\Phi
is equal to
1
(eigenvalue
\mu_{1}=-16
, unit eigenvector
\partial/\penalty\partial\eta
). Moreover, also the
equivariant nullity
of
\Phi
is equal to
1
(unit eigenvector
\sqrt{2}\,\partial/\penalty\partial\nu
). These results, together with (
5.4
), suggest that in this example the orbit space analysis displays all the significant second variation features of
\Phi
.

Remark 5.1
.

The method of this section can be extended to other examples. For instance, one could apply these arguments to the
{\rm SO}(\ell+1)\times{\rm SO}(\ell+1)
-invariant proper biharmonic immersions
\Phi_{\ell}:\mathbb{S}^{\ell}\big(\frac{1}{2}\big)\times\mathbb{S}^{\ell}\big(\frac{1}{2}\big)\to\mathbb{S}^{2\ell+2}
,
\ell\geq 2
. Of course, it would be nice to be able to exclude, when
\ell\geq 2
, that there exist directions different from
V_{\eta},\,V_{\nu}
which are geometrically significant for the study of the second variation of
\Phi_{\ell}
(index or nullity).

Remark 5.2
.

In the case of Theorem
4.3
the submanifold
\varphi(\mbox{${\mathbb{T}}$})
is again
{\rm SO}(2)\times{\rm SO}(2)
-invariant and the orbit space is
1
-dimensional. In this example, there are
4
geometrically significant directions which determine the index (see (
4.10
)), but it is not possible to recover this
4
-dimensional subspace by carrying out a simplified analysis in the orbit space.

Remark 5.3
.

We point out that, in our example
\Phi:\mbox{${\mathbb{T}}$}\to\mathbb{S}^{4}
,
the normal bundle has dimension equal to two
and so it is interesting to compare this situation with the recent results proved by Ou concerning the normal stability of certain proper biharmonic
hypersurfaces
of the Euclidean sphere (see
[
21
]
).
