Synchronization in cilia carpets and the Kuramoto model with local coupling: breakup of global synchronization in the presence of noise
Anton Solovev, Benjamin M. Friedrich

Abstract
Carpets of beating cilia represent a paradigmatic example of self-organized synchronization of noisy biological oscillators, characterized by traveling waves of cilia phase. We present a multi-scale model of a cilia carpet, which comprises realistic hydrodynamic interactions between cilia computed for a chiral cilia beat pattern from unicellular Paramecium and active noise of the cilia beat. We demonstrate an abrupt loss of global synchronization beyond a characteristic noise strength. We characterize stochastic transitions between synchronized and disordered dynamics, which generalizes the notion of phase slips in pairs of coupled noisy phase oscillators. Our theoretical work establishes a link between the two-dimensional Kuramoto model of phase oscillators with mirror-symmetric oscillator coupling and detailed models of biological oscillators with asymmetric, chiral interactions.



Motile cilia are hair-like extensions of biological cells that beat periodically like a whip.
Collections of cilia on the surface of microorganisms or human airways
beat in a coordinated fashion as a traveling wave
– similar to a Mexican wave in a soccer stadium – to facilitate efficient fluid transport.
This emblematic example of synchronization of noisy biological oscillators attracted attention
from physicists and mathematicians for more than hundred years,
yet important question remain open,
including the impact of active noise on wave synchronization,
which has so far not been systematically studied for cilia carpets.
Recently, the authors proposed a multi-scale model of a cilia carpet
Solovev and Friedrich 2022
,
which in this work is advanced to include active noise of the cilia beat.
We show that global synchronization in the form of traveling waves of cilia phase
is abruptly lost at a characteristic noise strength,
and provide evidence for local synchronized patches in large systems.
We connect our findings to the celebrated Kuramoto model.

.1
Introduction

Motile cilia and flagella are noisy biological oscillators:
the regular bending waves of these slender cell appendages exhibit
small, but measurable phase fluctuations,
causing frequency jitter

Polin et al. 2009
;
Ma et al. 2014
;
Wan and Goldstein 2014
.
This active noise induces phase slips in pairs of synchronized cilia,
as observed in the bi-ciliate green alga
Chlamydomonas
,
where one cilium occasionally executes an extra beat
Goldstein et al. 2009
;
Goldstein et al. 2011
.
In fact, phase slips in pairs of cilia are well described by the famous Adler equation
of two coupled noisy phase oscillators
Adler 1946
;
Stratonovich 1967
.

On epithelial tissues, such as in mammalian airways,
carpets of thousands of cilia can spontaneously synchronize into
so-called metachronal waves, i.e., traveling waves of cilia phase
Sanderson and Sleigh 1981
.
The coordinated beating of cilia in these cilia carpets
pumps mucus and thus clears pathogens from the airways.
Moreover, cilia carpets that exhibit metachronal synchronization are found on the surface of model organisms,
e.g., green alga colonies or unicellular
Paramecium

Machemer 1972
;
Brumley et al. 2012
.
In these systems, the traveling waves of cilia phase have a well-defined direction
relative to the common direction of the effective stroke of the beating cilia
Machemer 1972
;
Knight-Jones 1954
.
In many species, including
Paramecium
,
the cilia beat consists of an approximately planar effective stroke during which the cilium is approximately straight,
and a recovery stroke, during which the cilium moves in a counter-clockwise fashion close to the surface,
see also Fig.
1
(a).
These chiral bending waves of a beating cilium reflect its internal molecular architecture,
which comprises a chiral distribution of molecular motors.
The common direction of the effective stroke in a cilia carpet is set by a global polarity of the ciliated tissue
that aligns the bases of cilia (and additionally by directed flows during tissue development)
Guirao et al. 2010
.

Hydrodynamic interactions between cilia have been identified as one of the
key mechanisms for cilia coupling underlying synchronization

Taylor 1952
;
Brumley et al. 2014
.
If exposed to moderate external flows,
the timing of the cilia beat pattern changes,
while the sequence of shapes assumed by the cilium during its beat cycle is altered only a little

Goldstein et al. 2009
;
Klindt et al. 2016
;
Pellicciotta et al. 2020
.
Importantly, if many cilia beat together, the fluid flow generated by one cilium will
either increase or decrease the hydrodynamic friction at another cilium nearby,
depending on the specific geometry of that cilium pair
Vilfan and Jülicher 2006
;
Solovev and
Friedrich 2021a
.
This change in hydrodynamic load subsequently changes the phase speed by which the other cilium progresses along its beat cycle and can cause synchronization

Brumley et al. 2014
;
Geyer et al. 2013
;
Klindt et al. 2016
.

A rich theoretical literature on synchronization in cilia carpets
can be broadly classified as either
detailed numerical simulations based on mechanistic models that describe the generation of cilia bending waves

Gueron and Levit-Gurevich 1999
;
Elgeti and Gompper 2013
;
Stein and Shelley 2019
;
Chakrabarti et al. 2021
,
or minimal models, where beating cilia are idealized, e.g., as orbiting spheres

Vilfan and Jülicher 2006
;
Guirao and Joanny 2007
;
Niedermayer et al. 2008
;
Uchida and Golestanian 2011
;
Wollin and Stark 2011
;
Friedrich and Jülicher 2012
;
Friedrich 2016
;
Pellicciotta et al. 2020
;
Meng et al. 2021
.
What is missing are models of intermediate complexity
that faithfully account for the specific, chiral shape of the cilia beat,
yet still allow to average over many stochastic realizations and
systematically screen key parameters such as the noise strength of active cilia fluctuations.

The periodic sequence of cilia shapes during one beat cycle can be regarded as a limit cycle

Ma et al. 2014
;
Wan and Goldstein 2014
;
Werner et al. 2014
.
This limit cycle can be uniquely parametrized by a phase variable of the cilia beat
if we require that phase speed should be constant in the absence of perturbations and noise
Pikovsky et al. 2003
.
Correspondingly, we can describe each beating cilium as a phase oscillator
Ma et al. 2014
;
Wan and Goldstein 2014
,
and a cilia carpet as an array of phase oscillators.

Here, we built on a recently established multi-scale model of a cilia carpet
Solovev and Friedrich 2022

to systematically study the impact of active noise in arrays of hydrodynamically coupled cilia.
The underlying modeling framework termed
Lagrangian Mechanics of Active Systems

describes beating cilia as effective phase oscillators,
where phase is used as a generalized coordinate,
whose dynamics is determined by a balance of conjugate active and passive forces
Solovev and
Friedrich 2021a
.
Using this stochastic model, we address in particular the co-existence of multiple synchronized states,
corresponding to traveling wave solutions with different wave vectors,
and their prevalence in the presence of noise.
We show how the asymmetric coupling arising from a chiral cilia beat
results in a dominant wave with non-trivial wave vector as long as the noise strength remains below a characteristic noise strength.
Above this characteristic noise strength,
global synchronization is lost,
but dynamic domains of local synchronization may persist,
analogous to the two-dimensional Kuramoto model
Rouzaire and Levis 2021
;
Sarkar 2021
.
Our work links an extensive literature on the Kuramoto model with local coupling and noise
Shinomoto and Kuramoto 1986
;
Dörfler and Bullo 2014
;
Sarkar 2021

(or quenched frequency disorder
Sakaguchi et al. 1987
;
Strogatz and Mirollo 1988
;
Acebrón et al. 2005
;
Lee et al. 2010
;
Ottino-Löffler and Strogatz 2016
)
to more detailed models of cilia carpets with asymmetric oscillator coupling.

.2
Cilia carpet model

In a recent publication
Solovev and Friedrich 2022
,
we had put forward a multi-scale model of a cilia carpet,
using an experimentally measured cilia beat pattern and detailed hydrodynamic computations,
albeit without accounting for active phase fluctuations of the cilia beat yet.
For the convenience of the reader,
we briefly review this multi-scale cilia carpet model
and then show how active noise of cilia beating can be naturally incorporated into this model.

We consider a carpet of
N
cilia positioned on a regular triangular lattice of base points
\mathbf{x}_{j}

in a rectangular domain with periodic boundary conditions,
see Fig.
1
(d).
Each cilium is described as a phase oscillator whose phase
\varphi_{i}
advances by
2\pi
on each cycle.
This phase variable
\varphi_{i}
parameterizes a periodic sequence of three-dimensional cilia shapes,
previously measured for
Paramecium

Machemer 1972
;
Naitoh and Sugino 1984
, see Fig.
1
(a).
We can consider
\varphi_{i}
as a generalized coordinate
in the sense of Lagrangian mechanics
Solovev and
Friedrich 2021a
,
introduce conjugate forces, and derive an equation of motion
in terms of a force balance of generalized forces
(similar to Onsager’s variational principle
Doi 2011
;
Wang et al. 2021
).
We first define a generalized hydrodynamic friction force
P_{i}
for each cilium,
conjugate to its phase variable
\varphi_{i}
.
Specifically, this generalized friction force can be computed as the surface integral

P_{i}=\int_{\mathcal{S}_{i}}\!d^{2}\mathbf{x}\,\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})\cdot\partial\mathbf{x}/\partial\varphi_{i}
,
where

\mathcal{S}_{i}
is the surface of cilium
i
,

\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})
is the surface density of hydrodynamic friction forces arising from the active motion of the cilia, and

\partial\mathbf{x}/\partial\varphi_{i}
is the local velocity at which a point
\mathbf{x}
on cilium
i
moves
if
\varphi_{i}
where to change at unit rate (
principal shape change
)
Solovev and
Friedrich 2021a
.
We can compute
\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})

using the Stokes equation of low Reynolds number hydrodynamics
(which is admissible because short-range interaction dominate, see
Wei et al. 2019
;
Solovev and
Friedrich 2021a
and the discussion in
Solovev and Friedrich 2022
),
see also Fig.
1
(b).
Due to the linearity of the Stokes equation,
the generalized hydrodynamic friction forces are themselves linear in the generalized velocities
\dot{\varphi}_{j}

\displaystyle P_{i}(\boldsymbol{\Phi},\dot{\boldsymbol{\Phi}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\Gamma_{ij}(\boldsymbol{\Phi})\,\dot{\varphi}_{j}

\displaystyle\approx\Gamma_{ii}(\varphi_{i})\,\dot{\varphi}_{i}+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\Gamma_{ij}(\varphi_{i},\varphi_{j})\,\dot{\varphi}_{j}\quad,

(1)

where,

\boldsymbol{\Phi}=(\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N}

is the
N
-component phase vector,

\Gamma_{ij}
are the generalized hydrodynamic friction coefficients
which characterize
hydrodynamic interactions
between cilia
i
and
j
for
i\neq j
,
and
\Gamma_{ii}
characterizes
self-friction
.

Generalized hydrodynamic friction coefficients
may in general depend on the phase of every cilium
\varphi_{j}
,
but not on the generalized velocities
\dot{\varphi_{j}}
.
In the second line of the Eq. (
.2
),
we made a pairwise interaction approximation by averaging out the (weak) dependence
of
\Gamma_{ij}

on the non-essential variables
\varphi_{k}
with
k\neq i,j
.
Likewise, we averaged out the dependence of
\Gamma_{ii}
on
\varphi_{k}
for
k\neq i
.
We additionally restricted the sum of these hydrodynamic interactions to cilia
j
from a neighborhood
\mathcal{N}_{i}
in the vicinity of cilium
i
, because hydrodynamic interactions with cilia outside of this neighborhood turned out to be weak,
see
Solovev and
Friedrich 2021a
for details.

Hydrodynamic friction corresponds to a continuous dissipation of energy at a rate

\sum_{j}P_{j}\dot{\varphi}_{j}
. This energy is provided by active processes within each cilium,
which we can coarse-grain into an active driving force
Q_{i}=Q_{i}(\varphi_{i})

that drives the beat of cilium
i
.
At low Reynolds numbers,
we have a
force balance

between the generalized friction force
P_{i}
and
this active driving force
Q_{i}

Q_{i}(\varphi_{i})=P_{i}(\boldsymbol{\Phi},\dot{\boldsymbol{\Phi}})\quad,\quad i=1,\ldots,N\quad.

(2)

The active driving force
Q_{i}(\varphi_{i})
of each cilium
is uniquely determined by a reference condition
that the phase speed of a cilium should be constant
in the absence of perturbations or noise
(i.e.,
\dot{\varphi}_{i}=\omega_{0}
, while
\dot{\varphi}_{k}=0
for all
k\neq i
),
which yields
Q_{i}(\varphi_{i})=\omega_{0}\,\Gamma_{ii}(\varphi_{i})
.
Inverting the force balance
Eq. (
2
)
gives the
equation of motion

\dot{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}=\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}\cdot\mathbf{Q}
,
where
\mathbf{Q}=(Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{N})
is the vector of active driving forces.

This equation of motion implies a
load response
of beating cilia, i.e.,
individual cilia beat slower (or faster) if external flows increase (or decrease) the hydrodynamic friction force acting on them.
The relative change in the phase speed of cilium
i
induced by the motion of cilium
j

is given by the ratio

\Gamma_{ij}(\varphi_{i},\varphi_{j})/\Gamma_{ii}(\varphi_{i})
,
which is a function of the respective phases,
see Fig.
1
(c).
Previously, similar models were shown to be in quantitative agreement
with the measured load response in the green alga
Chlamydomonas

Klindt et al. 2016

as well as cilia bundles exposed to external flows
Pellicciotta et al. 2020
.

.3
Active noise

To account for active phase noise of beating cilia
Goldstein et al. 2009
;
Ma et al. 2014
,
we augment the equation of motion
independent Gaussian white noise terms
\xi_{j}(t)

to the equation of each
\dot{\varphi}_{j}
,

\dot{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}=\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\Phi})\cdot\mathbf{Q}(\boldsymbol{\Phi})+\Xi(t),

(3)

where
\Xi(t)=(\xi_{1}(t),\ldots,\xi_{N}(t))
is a noise vector
with independent Gaussian white noise components
\xi_{i}(t)
and

\langle\xi_{i}(t)\xi_{j}(t^{\prime})\rangle=2D\,\delta_{ij}\,\delta(t-t^{\prime})

with noise strength
D
.

.4
Kuramoto order parameter

We consider perfect traveling waves
that are compatible with the periodic boundary conditions of the cilia carpet model

\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\mathbf{k}}(t):\varphi_{j}(t)=\omega_{\mathbf{k}}\,t-\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{j}\quad;

(4)

here,
\mathbf{k}
is a wave vector corresponding to one of the
N
reciprocal lattice points in the Brillouin zone of the cilia lattice
Solovev and Friedrich 2022
.

We introduce a generalized Kuramoto order parameter
by generalizing a previous definition for oscillator chains
(
Gupta et al. 2014
, Eq. (160))

r_{\mathbf{k}}(\boldsymbol{\Phi})=N^{-1}\left|{\textstyle\sum_{j}}\exp i(\varphi_{j}+\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{j})\right|\quad.

(5)

Note that

r_{\mathbf{k}}(\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\mathbf{k}})=1
,
but

r_{\mathbf{k}}(\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\mathbf{m}})=0
for
\mathbf{m}\neq\mathbf{k}
.

In the absence of noise with
D=0
,
the cilia carpet model exhibits traveling wave solutions

\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\mathbf{k}}^{\ast}

for each admissible wave vector
\mathbf{k}
,
which are not exactly equal to the perfect waves
\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\mathbf{k}}
, but close
Solovev and Friedrich 2022
.
For these so-called
metachronal wave solutions
, we have

r_{\mathbf{k}}(\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\mathbf{k}}^{\ast})\approx 1
.
Several wave solutions are linearly stable to small perturbations.
Below, Fig.
3
(a) shows for each stable wave solution
\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\ast}_{\mathbf{k}}

the maximal real part of the dimensionless Lyapunov exponents,
corresponding to the slowest decaying perturbation mode
(with relaxation time
\tau\approx-T_{0}/\max\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda_{j}
, where
T_{0}=2\pi/\omega_{0}
is the intrinsic period of the cilia beat).
Despite this multi-stability of wave solutions,
the majority of trajectories with random initial conditions converges to a dominant wave
{\mathbf{k}_{\mathrm{I}}}

Solovev and Friedrich 2022
, shown in Fig.
1
(d).

In the presence of noise with
D>0
,
the Kuramoto order parameter
\mathbf{r}_{\mathbf{k}_{\mathrm{I}}}
for the dominant wave
{\mathbf{k}_{\mathrm{I}}}
fluctuates.
Fig.
2
(a) shows the stochastic dynamics of
\mathbf{r}_{\mathbf{k}_{\mathrm{I}}}(t)
for individual trajectories
for two values of the noise strength
D
just below and above a characteristic noise strength

D_{c}=0.18\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}
, respectively.
(The ensemble had started from random initial conditions and had been equilibrated until
the ensemble-mean
\overline{r}_{{\mathbf{k}_{\mathrm{I}}}}
converged.)
We observe a qualitative change of the steady-state distribution

p(r_{\mathbf{k}_{\mathrm{I}}})
of the order parameter at this characteristic noise strength,
with most values of
r_{\mathbf{k}_{\mathrm{I}}}
close to
1
for
D<D_{c}
,
but much smaller values for
D>D_{c}

(with
r_{\mathbf{k}_{\mathrm{I}}}\sim N^{-1/2}
for
D\rightarrow\infty
).
Accordingly, we define
a
synchronized
regime by
the inequality
r_{\mathbf{k}_{\mathrm{I}}}(\boldsymbol{\Phi})>r^{\ast}
,
and a
disordered
regime by
r_{\mathbf{k}_{\mathrm{I}}}(\boldsymbol{\Phi})<r^{\ast}
,
where we chose the threshold
r^{*}=2^{-1/2}

(qualitative results are independent of the choice of
r^{\ast}
).
We observe stochastic transitions between these two regimes.
Fig.
2
(b) shows a typical example of
the synchronized and the disordered regime, respectively,
in terms of the deviation of cilia phases from the perfect wave
\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\mathbf{k}_{\mathrm{I}}}
.

We computed residence times
t_{\text{res}}
of individual trajectories in the synchronized regime,
i.e., the time intervals between a consecutive entrance and escape of the synchronized regime
Gammaitoni et al. 1998
.
The residence times
t_{\text{res}}
follow a broad distribution:
we distinguish a regime of brief excursions into the synchronized regime
where residence times follow a power-law distribution (blue; fitted exponent
\approx{-}1.75
),
and a second regime of long residence times,
which are distributed according to an exponential distribution

\sim\exp(-\beta\,t_{\mathrm{res}})
(orange),
as expected from escape-rate theory, see Fig.
2
(c).
The effective escape rate
\beta
strongly depends on noise strength
D
,
see Fig.
2
(d).
Note that we could not explore
\beta(D)
for a wider range of
D

because transitions became very rare otherwise:
for strong noise,
trajectories
rarely enter the synchronized regime,
while for weak noise,
they rarely escape.

.5
Noise-induced breakdown of global synchronization

Fig.
3
(a)
shows the linear stability of all possible wave solutions
\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\ast}_{\mathbf{k}}

in the absence of noise
Solovev and Friedrich 2022
.
For each wave,
we determined the
synchronized fraction
for this wave in the presence of noise,
i.e., the time fraction that trajectories spend in the synchronized regime for this wave
defined by mutually exclusive conditions
r_{\mathbf{k}}>2^{-1/2}
,
see Fig.
3
(b).
For weak noise, results closely resemble the relative sizes of basins-of-attraction
previously determined in the absence of noise
Solovev and Friedrich 2022
.
In particular, most trajectories spend most of their time near the dominant wave
{\mathbf{k}_{\mathrm{I}}}
,
consistent with
{\mathbf{k}_{\mathrm{I}}}
’s large basin-of-attraction.
Near the characteristic noise strength
D_{c}
identified in Fig.
2
,
the synchronized fractions abruptly drop to almost zero for all
\mathbf{k}
.
Concomitantly, we observe a decline of spatial and temporal correlations.
Fig.
3
(c) shows the
spatial correlation function

S(\mathbf{d})
of cilia dynamics
(similar to a structure factor in condensed matter physics
Chaikin et al. 1995
)
for different noise strengths
D
, where

S(\mathbf{d})=\left|\langle\exp{i[\varphi(\mathbf{x}_{j}+\mathbf{d},t)-\varphi(\mathbf{x}_{j},t)]}\rangle\right|\quad.

(6)

Note that for a perfect traveling wave
\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\mathbf{k}}
from Eq. (
4
),
we would have
S(\mathbf{d})\equiv 1
for all distance vectors
\mathbf{d}
.
For weak noise,

S(\mathbf{d})
slowly decays as a function of cilia distance
d=|\mathbf{d}|
,
yet with a decay length that exceeds system length, corresponding to global synchronization.
Above the characteristic noise strength
D_{c}\approx 0.18\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}
,

S(\mathbf{d})
is strongly reduced,
reflecting a loss of global synchronization.
Among the three principal lattice directions,
spatial correlations are slightly higher along the direction approximately parallel to the wave front of the dominant wave.

Fig.
3
(d) shows the
temporal correlation function

C(\Delta t)=\left|\langle\exp i[\varphi_{j}(t+\Delta t)-\varphi_{j}(t)]\rangle\right|\quad.

(7)

For an isolated cilium with equation of motion

\dot{\varphi}_{j}=\omega_{0}+\xi_{j}(t)
,
we would have
C(\Delta t)=\exp(-D\,t)

Ma et al. 2014
.
For a cilium in a cilia carpet,

C(\Delta t)
displays instead an initial fast decay,
reflecting fluctuations of
\varphi_{j}
of finite amplitude around the reference wave

\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\ast}_{\mathbf{k_{\mathrm{I}}}}(t)
,
followed by a slow decay,
reflecting phase diffusion of the global phase with effective diffusion coefficient
D/N

(for small
D
, see SM text).
For weak noise,
C(\Delta t)
displays damped oscillations,
which are consistent with predictions from linear stability analysis:
the slowest decaying perturbation of
\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\mathbf{k}_{\mathrm{I}}}^{\ast}

has a Lyapunov exponent with non-zero imaginary part
\mathrm{Im}\,\lambda\neq 0
,
which implies that perturbations will decay in a spiral-like fashion
with angular frequency approximately
|\mathrm{Im}\,\lambda|/T_{0}
.
This frequency indeed matches
the frequency of the apparent oscillations of
C(\Delta t)
,
representing an example of noise-induced oscillations.

Generalized Kuramoto models

The most striking difference between the cilia carpet model and the Kuramoto model with local sinusoidal coupling is the fact that the dominant synchronized state is
a traveling wave
\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\mathbf{k}_{\mathrm{I}}}^{\ast}\approx\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\mathbf{k}_{\mathrm{I}}}
with nontrivial wave vector
{\mathbf{k}_{\mathrm{I}}}\neq{\boldsymbol{0}}
for the cilia carpet,
in contrast to in-phase synchronization in the Kuramoto model.
This is a consequence of the chiral cilia beat pattern, which results in asymmetric hydrodynamic interactions.
Intriguingly, we can introduce a modified Kuramoto model with asymmetric coupling

\displaystyle\dot{\varphi_{i}}=\omega_{0}+\frac{K}{6}\sum_{|\mathbf{x}_{j}-\mathbf{x}_{i}|=a}\sin\left[\varphi_{j}-\varphi_{i}-\mathbf{k}\cdot(\mathbf{x}_{j}-\mathbf{x}_{i})\right]\quad.

(9)

Note that the coupling in Eq. (
9
) is
not mirror-symmetric in the sense that the coupling
acts differently on mirror-symmetric neighbors.
The variable transformation
\varphi_{j}\mapsto\varphi_{j}-\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{j}

maps solutions of Eq. (
8
) to solutions of Eq. (
9
):
this maps not only
\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\mathbf{m}}(t)
to
\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\mathbf{m}+\mathbf{k}}(t)
,
but also all trajectories in the vicinity of
\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\mathbf{m}}(t)
to
trajectories in the vicinity of
\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\mathbf{m}+\mathbf{k}}(t)
.
Hence, the linear stability and the basins-of-attractions
of the wave solution
\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\mathbf{m}}(t)
of Eq. (

correspond exactly to the linear stability and the basins-of-attraction
of the wave solution
\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\mathbf{m}+\mathbf{k}}(t)
of Eq. (
9
).
This implies that Fig.
3
applies with minor modifications
also to the modified Kuramoto model, Eq. (
9

in Fig.

not be centered at the in-phase synchronized state with
\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{0}
anymore

\mathbf{k}
;
in Fig.
3

\mathbf{k}
;
the spatial and temporal correlations shown in Fig.

because these correlation functions quantify order irrespective of the dominant wave.

More generally, we can consider

\displaystyle\dot{\varphi_{i}}

\displaystyle=\omega_{0}+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}C_{ij}(\varphi_{i},\varphi_{j})\quad,

(10)

with generic coupling functions
C_{ij}(\varphi_{i},\varphi_{j})

and a set of of neighbors

for each oscillator

We introduce the spatial mirror operation

j\mapsto{\overline{\jmath}}
such that
\mathbf{x}_{j}-\mathbf{x}_{i}=-(\mathbf{x}_{\overline{\jmath}}-\mathbf{x}_{i})

In Eq. (

which implies

C_{ij}(\varphi_{i},\varphi_{j})=C_{{\overline{\jmath}}i}(\varphi_{i},\varphi_{j})

A special case of Eq. (

would be a

coupling functions that are invariant under a global shift of phase
\varphi_{i}\mapsto\varphi_{i}+\delta

In this case, Eq. (

If the coupling is not phase-invariant, but weak,

;
Meng et al. 2021

This allows to average the coupling functions over one cycle of

which yields a phase-invariant effective coupling and

This is indeed what we find for the cilia carpet model.

Two properties of the generic model of coupled oscillators given by Eq. (

allow us to highlight differences between the cilia carpet model and the classical Kuramoto model, see Fig.

(i)

odd coupling:

for all

\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\mathbf{k}}

(ii)

mirror-symmetry:

C_{ij}(\varphi_{i},\varphi_{j})=C_{i{\overline{\jmath}}}(\varphi_{i},\varphi_{j})

for all

with this condition, we can map any trajectory

A comment on symmetries:
the matrix of hydrodynamic interaction coefficients

\Gamma_{ij}(\boldsymbol{\Phi})
is symmetric due to Onsager reciprocity

But the individual coefficients are not symmetric in their arguments,

The odd part of phase-averaged coupling functions

In this minimal model of a cilia carpet in terms of hydrodynamically coupled rotors,
the stability criterion is invariant under

reflecting an effectively mirror-symmetric coupling in the sense of condition (ii).

.9
Kuramoto model for large systems

Numerical simulations show that
D_{c}
depends weakly on system length
L

for the two-dimensional Kuramoto model.
A heuristic argument suggests
D_{c}\sim 1/\ln L

Pikovsky et al. 2003
, see SM text.

In the thermodynamic limit of infinite system size,
Kuramoto models with
local
, short-range coupling
exhibit a conventional order-disorder transition as a function of noise strength
only in three- or higher-dimensional systems, but not in two-dimensional systems
Shinomoto and Kuramoto 1986
;
Sarkar 2021
.
This is essentially a consequence of the famous Mermin-Wagner theorem from statistical physics,
which rules out global order in two-dimensional systems with local coupling and continuous symmetries

Mermin and Wagner 1966
.
In fact, we can map the two-dimensional Kuramoto model with local sinusoidal coupling [Eq. (
8
)]
to the classical XY model of interacting spins in the plane with polar angles
\theta_{j}

by switching to a co-rotating frame
\theta_{j}=\varphi_{j}-\omega_{0}t

Rouzaire and Levis 2021
;
Sarkar 2021
.
Noise excites long-wavelength perturbation modes with long relaxation times
(so-called
Goldstone modes
),
which rules out global synchronization in two-dimensional, infinitely extended systems.
These systems exhibit additional unusual features:
the spatial correlation function decays algebraically (with non-universal, parameter-dependent exponent);
moreover, topological defects can be easily excited in these systems
(and are in fact favored by entropy in the XY model).
Topological defects can form pairs below a characteristic noise strength,
corresponding to a Kosterlitz-Thouless transition, i.e., an unconventional phase transition.
In contrast, the Kuramoto model with
global
, all-to-all coupling exhibits
a conventional second-order phase transition as a function of noise strength
Kuramoto 2003
;
Strogatz 2000
;
Son and Hong 2010
;
Dörfler and Bullo 2014
.
Thus, there is a stark difference between Kuramoto models with global coupling
and two-dimensional models with local coupling,
previously studied for quenched frequency disorder
Sakaguchi et al. 1987
;
Strogatz and Mirollo 1988
;
Lee et al. 2010
;
Ottino-Löffler and Strogatz 2016

and noise
Shinomoto and Kuramoto 1986
;
Sarkar 2021
.
Models of cilia carpets
Hong et al. 2020
ignoring this difference have limited predictive power.

Based on the correspondence between the cilia carpet model and the two-dimensional Kuramoto model
(as suggested by Fig.
3

and demonstrated by the mapping from Eq. (
8
) to Eq. (
9
)),
we expect that large carpets of coupled noisy cilia
likewise do not exhibit global synchronization, but instead local synchronized domains.

.10
Discussion

We investigated the role of active noise on metachronal synchronization in cilia carpets
using a multi-scale model.
Non-trivial synchronized states in the form of traveling waves persist even in the presence of noise,
featuring one dominant wave whose synchronized fraction exceeds those of all other possible waves.
Beyond a characteristic noise strength,
global synchronization is lost even in finite systems,
characterized by a rapid decline of spatial and temporal correlations.
Close to the characteristic noise strength,
we observe stochastic transitions between the synchronized and the disordered regime,
which one may regard as a high-dimensional analogue of phase-slips in pairs of noisy oscillators

Stratonovich 1967
;
Goldstein et al. 2009
.

We highlighted similarities and differences of synchronization in cilia carpets
and the two-dimensional Kuramoto model of phase oscillators:
the cilia carpet model and the Kuramoto model
both exhibit a loss of global synchronization in finite systems beyond a characteristic noise strength.
As a small difference, the decline of spatial and temporal correlations
as a function of noise strength is more abrupt in the cilia carpet model.
Generally,
the temporal correlation function of a single oscillator features first a rapid decay,
which reflects small-amplitude fluctuations of this oscillator around a synchronized state,
followed by a slow decay, which reflects slow phase diffusion of a global phase associated to this synchronized state.
In addition, in the cilia carpet model,
we observe a peculiar oscillatory component of the temporal correlation function,
which reflects slow, noise-induced oscillations of the system’s dynamics.
The period of these slow oscillations matches the imaginary part of the Lyapunov exponent
of the slowest-decaying perturbation mode of the dominant synchronized state, which becomes excited by noise.

The decline of global synchronization beyond a characteristic noise strength can be intuitively understood as follows:
in a limit of weak oscillator coupling and weak noise, one can linearize the dynamics around the dominant synchronized state.
This yields fundamental perturbation modes,
each of which has a characteristic relaxation time,
and whose dynamics is approximately described by a relaxation process driven by active noise
(Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process), see SM text for details.
Importantly, the relaxation time of the slowest-decaying perturbation mode diverges as
\sim L^{2}

with system length
L
.
This can be analytically derived for the Kuramoto model with local coupling
and has been numerically shown for the cilia carpet model
Solovev and Friedrich 2022
.
Thus, in larger systems perturbation modes with longer wave-length and longer relaxation times appear

Sakaguchi et al. 1987
;
Pikovsky et al. 2003
.
Assuming that a global synchronized state exists,
one can compute the fluctuations of an individual oscillator around this synchronized state
as a sum over all perturbation modes,
and thus arrive at a self-consistency condition
that the amplitude of these fluctuations is small in a globally synchronized state.
This heuristic argument suggests a condition for global synchronization in a two-dimensional system of system length
L

in terms of the noise strength
D
as
D<D_{c}(L)

with a characteristic noise strength
D_{c}
that depends weakly on system length
L
as
D_{c}\sim 1/\ln L
.
As a consequence, global synchronization is not possible in two-dimensional, infinitely-extended systems,
even for arbitrarily small noise strengths.
If a noise strength
D
satisfies

D_{c}(L_{1})\ll D\ll D_{c}(L_{2})
,
we may thus expect local synchronization on length scales
L_{2}
,
but not anymore on length scales
L_{1}
.
Indeed, previous simulations of the two-dimensional Kuramoto model
showed examples of such local synchronization
Sarkar 2021
.

Despite the similarities,
there is one striking difference between the cilia carpet and the classical Kuramoto model:
in the cilia carpet, mirror-symmetry is broken:
This broken mirror-symmetry in the cilia carpet model manifests itself most clearly in a dominant wave with non-trivial wave vector
(which is rotated counter-clockwise relative to a reference direction
set by the direction of the effective stroke of the cilia beat).
In fact, the linear stability of different synchronization states with respective wave vectors
\mathbf{k}

is symmetric under a reflection
\mathbf{k}\rightarrow-\mathbf{k}
for the Kuramoto model
with mirror-symmetric coupling,
but highly asymmetric for the cilia carpet,
where Lyapunov exponents have the most negative real part close to the dominant wave. The broken mirror-symmetry of the emergent dynamics in the cilia carpet model is a result of the chiral cilia beat pattern.

Our analysis became possible by a multi-scale simulation approach
Friedrich and Jülicher 2012
;
Polotzek and Friedrich 2013
;
Solovev and
Friedrich 2021a
,
which describes a carpet of beating cilia as an array of phase oscillators,
with direction-dependent coupling functions that were calibrated
from detailed hydrodynamic simulations using a measured cilia beat pattern from
Paramecium

Machemer 1972
;
Naitoh and Sugino 1984
.
Our approach allows to systematically vary the strength of active phase fluctuations,
whereas a previous study addressing synchronization in cilia carpets
employed a stochastic hydrodynamic simulation algorithm
and thus included noise only implicitly
Elgeti and Gompper 2013
.

For technical reasons, cilia spacing in our model (
a{=}18\,\mu\mathrm{m}
)
is larger than in real cilia carpets (
2\,\mu\mathrm{m}

Machemer 1972
),
similar to the dilute limit considered in most theoretical studies.
Therefore, we underestimate hydrodynamic interactions.
Correspondingly, we consider lower noise strengths,
corresponding to quality factors
Q_{c}=\omega_{0}/(2D_{c})
that are approximately 10–30 times larger than previous measurements with quality factors in the range
Q=25
–
100

Goldstein et al. 2011
;
Ma et al. 2014
.
Real cilia carpets are characterized also by
quenched disorder of cilia position,
dispersity of intrinsic beat frequency,
and non-perfect alignment of cilia
Guirao et al. 2010
,
which should reduce the regularity of emergent metachronal waves,
in addition to active frequency jitter spotlighted here.
In the presence of these additional sources of disorder,
we expect qualitatively similar behavior,
albeit with lower characteristic noise strength (for the same cilia density).
In conclusion,
the dynamics of a cilia carpet in the presence of active noise
is similar to that of a two-dimensional Kuramoto model, likewise displaying local but not global synchronization,
with the crucial difference that the dominant synchronization state is not the in-phase synchronized state,
but a so-called metachronal, traveling wave with non-trivial wave vector.

C.2
Synchronized fraction

We computed the synchronized fraction near a traveling wave solution
\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\ast}_{\mathbf{k}}

with wave vector
\mathbf{k}

as the fraction of time that simulated trajectories spent in the vicinity of the corresponding wave, quantified as
r_{\mathbf{k}}(\boldsymbol{\Phi})>r^{*}
.
Here, we used the threshold
r^{*}=\sqrt{2}/2
.
This choice of threshold is motivated as follows:
the order parameters
r_{\mathbf{k}}
are
closely related to Fourier transform on the lattice;
therefore,
\sum_{k\in\mathbbm{K}}r_{\mathbf{k}}(\boldsymbol{\Phi})^{2}=1

as a consequence of Plancherel’s theorem.
Hence,
r_{\mathbf{k}}>\sqrt{2}/2
guarantees
r_{\mathbf{m}}<\sqrt{2}/2
for all
\mathbf{m}\in\mathbbm{K}/\{\mathbf{k}\}
,
i.e., the choice of threshold ensures that the neighborhoods are mutually disjoint.
Note that these neighborhoods comprise only a tiny (
<10^{-10}
) fraction of phase space volume.

D.3
Kuramoto model with nearest-neighbor sinusoidal coupling in
d
dimensions in the presence of noise

One can consider a Kuramoto model of noisy phase oscillators with identical frequencies
on a hyper-cubic lattice in
d
-dimensional space (with lattice spacing
a
and lattice positions
\mathbf{x}_{i}
).
We assume local nearest-neighbor coupling, i.e., each oscillator with phase variable
\varphi_{i}
is coupled to its
m=2d
nearest neighbors (enumerated by an index set
\mathcal{N}_{i}
) with total coupling strength
K
,

\dot{\varphi}_{i}=\omega_{0}-\frac{K}{m}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\sin(\varphi_{i}-\varphi_{j})+\xi_{i}\quad.

(S3)

Here,
\xi_{i}(t)
denotes uncorrelated Gaussian white noise with

\langle\xi_{i}(t)\xi_{j}(t^{\prime})\rangle=2D\,\delta_{ij}\,\delta(t-t^{\prime})
.
We impose periodic boundary conditions.

In the absence of noise,
linear stability analysis yields a set of fundamental perturbation modes
Solovev and Friedrich 2022

\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\mathbf{m}}:\delta_{j}=\varepsilon\exp(-i\,\mathbf{m}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{j})\text{ for }\mathbf{m}\in\mathbbm{K}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}\}\quad,

(S4)

the corresponding dimensionless Lyapunov exponents read

\lambda_{\mathbf{m}}=-\frac{KT_{0}}{m}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\cos(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{ij})\left[1-\cos(\mathbf{m}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{ij})\right]\quad,

(S5)

where we used short-hand
\mathbf{x}_{ij}=\mathbf{x}_{j}-\mathbf{x}_{i}
.

In the limit of weak noise,
D\ll K
,
phases fluctuate around
\overline{\varphi}
and
we can linearize Eq. (
S3
)

\dot{\varphi}_{i}=\omega_{0}-\frac{K}{m}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\left(\varphi_{i}-\varphi_{j}\right)+\xi_{i}\quad.

(S6)

We introduce the discrete set of Fourier modes

\widetilde{\varphi}_{\mathbf{k}}=a^{d}\,\sum_{j}\varphi_{j}(t)\exp(-i\,\mathbf{x}_{j}\cdot\mathbf{k})\quad,

(S7)

where
k\in\mathbbm{K}
is an element of the first Brillouin zone of the reciprocal lattice.
We introduce short-hand
\mathbbm{K}^{\ast}=\mathbbm{K}\setminus\{{\boldsymbol{0}}\}
,
noting that the wave modes
\mathbf{k}={\boldsymbol{0}}
will behave different.
The inverse Fourier transform reads

\varphi_{j}(t)=V^{-1}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbbm{K}}\widetilde{\varphi}_{\mathbf{k}}(t)\exp(i\,\mathbf{x}_{j}\cdot\mathbf{k})\quad,

(S8)

where
V=a^{d}\,N
is the
d
-dimensional volume of the system
and
N=\Pi_{i=1}^{d}N_{i}
the number of oscillators
with system size
N_{1}\times\ldots\times N_{d}
.
By conjugation,
\widetilde{\varphi}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}}=\widetilde{\varphi}_{-\mathbf{k}}
.
The Fourier coefficients are closely related to the Kuramoto order parameters
r_{\mathbf{k}}
introduced in Eq. (
5
)
as
r_{\mathbf{k}}\approx V^{-1}|\widetilde{\varphi}_{-\mathbf{k}}|=V^{-1}|\widetilde{\varphi}_{\mathbf{k}}|
,
provided
D\ll K
, hence
|\varphi_{j}|\ll 1
.

D.4
Dynamics of Fourier modes

The Fourier-transform of the linearized dynamics, Eq. (
S6
),
shows that each Fourier mode
\widetilde{\varphi}_{\mathbf{k}}
fluctuates as an independent complex Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process around zero

\frac{d}{dt}\widetilde{\varphi}_{\mathbf{k}}=-\frac{1}{\tau_{\mathbf{k}}}\,\widetilde{\varphi}_{\mathbf{k}}+\xi_{\mathbf{k}}(t)\text{ for }\mathbf{k}\in\mathbbm{K}^{\ast}\quad,

(S9)

where

\xi_{\mathbf{k}}(t)
is isotropic complex Gaussian white noise with

\langle\xi_{\mathbf{k}}(t)\xi^{\ast}_{\mathbf{m}}(t+\Delta t)\rangle=2\sigma^{2}V\,\delta_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{m}}\,\delta(t-t^{\prime})
,
where
\sigma^{2}=Da^{d}
.
Note
\langle\xi_{\mathbf{k}}(t)\xi_{\mathbf{k}}(t+\Delta t)\rangle=0
, because contributions from the real and imaginary part of
\widetilde{\varphi}_{\mathbf{k}}
exactly cancel.
The relaxation times
\tau_{\mathbf{k}}
read

\tau_{\mathbf{k}}=\frac{m}{2K}\left(d-\sum_{n=1}^{d}\cos(ak_{n})\right)^{-1}\quad.

(S10)

The derivation of
\tau_{\mathbf{k}}
uses Eq. (
S6
) and the shift theorem of the discrete Fourier transform.
The cross-correlation of the Fourier coefficients is thus given by

\displaystyle\langle\widetilde{\varphi}_{\mathbf{k}}(t)\widetilde{\varphi}^{\ast}_{\mathbf{m}}(t+\Delta t)\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathrm{var}(\widetilde{\varphi}_{\mathbf{k}})\,\delta_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{m}}\,\exp(-|\Delta t|/\tau_{\mathbf{k}}),

(S11)

with variance

\mathrm{var}(\widetilde{\varphi}_{\mathbf{k}})=\sigma^{2}V\,\tau_{\mathbf{k}}\quad.

(S12)

The Fourier mode with
\mathbf{k}={\boldsymbol{0}}
represents an exception,
and is described by a diffusion process with drift

\frac{d}{dt}\widetilde{\varphi}_{\boldsymbol{0}}=\omega_{0}V+\xi_{\boldsymbol{0}}(t)\quad,

(S13)

where

\xi_{\boldsymbol{0}}(t)
is Gaussian white noise with

\langle\xi_{\boldsymbol{0}}(t)\xi_{\boldsymbol{0}}(t+\Delta t)\rangle=2\sigma^{2}V\,\delta(t-t^{\prime})
.
This zeroth Fourier mode is closely related to the
global phase

\overline{\varphi}=\sum_{j}\varphi_{j}/N
as

\overline{\varphi}=V^{-1}\,\widetilde{\varphi}_{\boldsymbol{0}}\quad.

(S14)

The phase correlation function of the global phase reads
Ma et al. 2014

C_{\overline{\varphi}}(\Delta t)=|\langle\exp i\,[\overline{\varphi}(t+\Delta t)-\overline{\varphi}(t)]\rangle|\\
=\exp\left(-\frac{D}{N}|\Delta t|\right)\quad,

(S15)

i.e.,
\overline{\varphi}
exhibits effective phase diffusion with effective diffusion coefficient
D/N

(in addition to its deterministic drift with
\langle\overline{\varphi}(t+\Delta t)-\overline{\varphi}(t)\rangle=\omega_{0}\Delta t
).

We are interested in the deviations

\delta_{j}=\varphi_{j}-\overline{\varphi}

of the phases of the individual oscillators from the global phase.
For the autocorrelation function of these deviations, we find

\displaystyle\langle\delta_{j}(t)\delta_{j}(t+\Delta t)\rangle

\displaystyle=V^{-2}\!\!\!\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbbm{K}^{\ast}}\!\!\!\mathrm{var}(\widetilde{\varphi}_{\mathbf{k}})\,\exp(-|\Delta t|/\tau_{\mathbf{k}})\quad,

(S16)

with relaxation times
\tau_{\mathbf{k}}
given in Eq. (
S10
).

D.5
Temporal correlations

Similarly, we can compute the phase correlation function of
\delta_{j}
.
We first note the phase correlation function (also called moment-generating function, characteristic function, circular autocorrelation function)
of an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process
\delta(t)
given by

\dot{\delta}=-\delta/\tau+\xi
,

\langle\xi(t)\xi(t^{\prime})\rangle=2D\,\delta(t-t^{\prime})
,
with relaxation time
\tau
and variance
D\tau
as
1
1

1

For a proof, note that we write
x(t+\Delta t)-x(t)=A+B
as a sum of two independent random variables,

A=x(t)[\exp(-\Delta t/\tau)-1]
,
and
B=\int_{t}^{t+\Delta t}\!dt^{\prime}\,\xi(t^{\prime})\,\exp[-(t+\Delta t-t^{\prime})/\tau]
,
where
A
and
B
are normal distributed random variables with zero mean and respective variances,

D\tau\,[1-\exp(-\Delta t/\tau)]^{2}
and
D\tau\,[1-\exp(-2\Delta t/\tau)]
.
Since
\langle\exp i\,\Xi\rangle=\exp[-\langle\Xi^{2}\rangle/2]

for normally distributed random variables with zero mean,
we conclude

\langle\exp i\,[x(t+\Delta t)-x(t)]\rangle=\langle\exp iA\rangle\cdot\langle\exp iB\rangle\\
=e^{-D\tau\,[1-\exp(-\Delta t/\tau)]^{2}/2}\cdot e^{-D\tau[1-\exp(-2\Delta t/\tau)]/2}\quad,

from which the assertion follows.

C_{\delta}(\Delta t)=|\langle\exp i\,[\delta(t+\Delta t)-\delta(t)]\rangle|=\\
\exp\left(-D\tau\left[1-\exp\left(-\frac{|\Delta t|}{\tau}\right)\right]\right)\quad.

(S17)

This phase correlation function converges to a non-zero limit value

\lim_{\Delta t\to\infty}C_{\delta}(\Delta t)=\exp{\left(-D\tau\right)}\quad.

(S18)

We now compute the phase correlation function of
\delta_{j}
.
Without loss of generality, it suffices to consider

\delta_{0}
at the origin
\mathbf{r}_{0}={\boldsymbol{0}}
due to the translation symmetry of the oscillator lattice.
Since
\delta_{0}(t)
is a sum of independent complex Ornstein-Uhlenbeck processes,
namely the
\widetilde{\varphi}_{\mathbf{k}}(t)
,
it follows that the circular autocorrelation function of
\delta_{0}

is a product of their respective circular autocorrelation functions

C^{\prime}(\Delta t)=|\langle\exp i\,[\delta_{0}(t+\Delta t)-\delta_{0}(t)]\rangle|=\\
\prod_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbbm{K}^{\ast}}\exp\left(-\frac{D}{N}\,\tau_{\mathbf{k}}\left[1-\exp\left(-\frac{|\Delta t|}{\tau_{\mathbf{k}}}\right)\right]\right)\quad.

(S19)

Because global phase
\overline{\varphi}
and phase deviation
\delta_{0}
are independent random variables,
the phase correlation function

C(\Delta t)=|\langle\exp i\,[\varphi_{j}(t+\Delta t)-\varphi_{j}(t)]\rangle|

of

\varphi_{0}=\overline{\varphi}+\delta_{0}

is given as the product of
C_{\overline{\varphi}}(\Delta t)
and
C_{\delta}(\Delta t)
.
We thus understand the behavior of temporal correlations on short and long time-scales:

•

Short-time dynamics.

For short times,
\Delta t\lesssim\tau_{\mathbf{k}}
,

C(0,\Delta t)
will rapidly decay to a limit value

C_{\infty}=\Pi_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbbm{K}^{\ast}}\exp(-D\tau_{\mathbf{k}})=\exp[-\mathrm{var}(\delta_{j})]

due to fluctuations of individual phases around the global phase
\overline{\varphi}
,
where

\mathrm{var}(\delta_{j})=V^{-2}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbbm{K}^{\ast}}\mathrm{var}(\widetilde{\varphi}_{\mathbf{k}})

is the variance of
\delta_{j}
.
This decay is a superposition of exponentials with a spectrum of relaxation time-scales given
by
\tau_{\mathbf{k}}
for
\mathbf{k}\in\mathbbm{K}^{\ast}
.

•

Long-time dynamics.

For long times,
\Delta t\gg\tau_{\mathbf{k}}
,
C(0,\Delta t)
decays as

C(0,\Delta t)\approx R_{\infty}\,\exp[-(D/N)\Delta t]
,
reflecting diffusion of the global phase
\overline{\varphi}
.

In summary,
the temporal correlation function of the phase
\varphi_{j}

is characterized by an initial fast decay due to fluctuations of individual phases around the global phase,
and a subsequent slow decay due to phase diffusion of the global phase itself

\boxed{C(\Delta t)\approx\underbrace{C^{\prime}(\Delta t)}_{\text{fast}}\,\underbrace{\exp\left(-D|\Delta t|/N\right)}_{\text{slow}}}\quad,

(S20)

where

C^{\prime}(0)=1
, and

C^{\prime}(\Delta t)\rightarrow C_{\infty}=\exp[-\mathrm{var}(\delta_{0})]
for
\Delta t\gg\tau=\max\tau_{\mathbf{k}}
.

Appendix E
Continuum limit

(cf. Pikovsky et al.,
(
Pikovsky et al. 2003
, Chapter 11)
)

Following
Pikovsky et al. 2003
,
we consider an oscillator lattice with weak isotropic nearest-neighbor coupling,
and characterize noise-induced fluctuations in a continuum limit.
Specifically,
we consider a
d
-dimensional lattice
\Lambda\subset\mathbbm{R}^{d}
with lattice spacing
a
,
where identical noisy phase oscillators with phase variables
\varphi_{i}
,
intrinsic frequency
\omega_{0}
and noise strength
D

are located at nodes
\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\Lambda
.
Each oscillator is coupled to its
2d
nearest neighbors (enumerated by an index set
\mathcal{N}_{i}
),
with a generic
2\pi
-periodic coupling function
c
and small coupling strength
\varepsilon>0

\dot{\varphi}_{i}=\omega_{0}+\varepsilon\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}c(\varphi_{i}-\varphi_{j})+\xi_{i}\quad.

(S25)

Here,
\xi_{i}(t)
denotes uncorrelated Gaussian white noise with

\langle\xi_{i}(t)\xi_{j}(t^{\prime})\rangle=2D\,\delta_{ij}\,\delta(t-t^{\prime})
.
We perform a continuum limit
a\rightarrow 0

with corresponding renormalization of coupling and noise strength,
\varepsilon
and
D
, respectively,
such that
\gamma=\varepsilon a^{2}
and
\sigma^{2}=Da^{d}
remain constant.
We obtain a partial differential equation for the phase field
\varphi(\mathbf{x},t)
,
valid for long-wave perturbations of long-wave solutions
3
3

3

For a rigorous derivation of Eq. (
S26
),
one should restrict the analysis to phase fields that vary only on long length scales
d\gg a
,
and subsequently perform a Taylor expansion to second order in the small dimensionless parameter
a/d
.
In the theory of stochastic partial equations, Eq. (
S26
) is known as a stochastic heat equation (for
\beta=0
),
whose solutions are (almost)
\alpha
-Hölder continuous, hence the differential operator
\nabla^{2}
is well defined.

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\varphi(\mathbf{x},t)=\omega_{0}+\alpha\nabla^{2}\varphi(\mathbf{x},t)+\beta\left[\nabla\varphi(\mathbf{x},t)\right]^{2}+\xi(\mathbf{x},t)\quad.

(S26)

Here,
\xi(\mathbf{x},t)
denotes uncorrelated Gaussian white noise
4
4

4

Discretization of the stochastic partial differential equation Eq. (
S26
)
should yield again the system of coupled stochastic differential equations Eq. (
S25
)
with appropriately rescaled coupling and noise strengths.
To show this,
we introduce the discrete set of variables

\varphi_{i}(t)=a^{-d}\int_{V_{i}}\!d\mathbf{x}\,\varphi(\mathbf{x},t)

that average the continuous field
\varphi(\mathbf{x},t)

over a
d
-cube unit cell
V_{i}
centered at the lattice point
\mathbf{x}_{i}
.
Indeed, the stochastic part

\Xi_{i}=\int_{t}^{t+\Delta t}dt^{\prime}\int_{V_{i}}\!d\mathbf{x}\,\xi(\mathbf{x},t)

of the increment

\varphi_{i}(t+\Delta t)-\varphi_{i}(t)

is a normally distributed random variable with
zero mean,
\langle\Xi_{i}\rangle=0
, and variance

\langle\Xi_{i}\Xi_{j}\rangle=a^{-2}\,2\sigma^{2}\,\Delta t\,a^{d}=2D\,\Delta t
,
which equals
\int_{t}^{t+\Delta t}\!dt^{\prime}\,\xi_{i}(t)
