CLT for Linear Spectral Statistics in High-Dimensional Random Effects Models
Ran Xie, Iain Johnstone

Abstract
We study sample covariance matrices arising from multi-level components of variance. Thus, let B n = 1 N ​ ∑ j = 1 N T j 1 / 2 ​ x j ​ x j T ​ T j 1 / 2 B_{n}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}T_{j}^{1/2}x_{j}x_{j}^{T}T_{j}^{1/2} , where x j ∈ ℝ n x_{j}\in\mathbbm{R}^{n} are i.i.d. standard Gaussian, and T j = ∑ r = 1 k l j ​ r 2 ​ Σ r T_{j}=\sum_{r=1}^{k}l_{jr}^{2}\Sigma_{r} are n × n n\times n real symmetric matrices with bounded spectral norm, corresponding to k k levels of variation. As the matrix dimensions n n and N N increase proportionally, we show that the linear spectral statistics (LSS) of B n B_{n} have Gaussian limits. The CLT is expressed as the convergence of a set of LSS to a standard multivariate Gaussian after centering by a mean vector Γ n \Gamma_{n} and a covariance matrix Λ n \Lambda_{n} which depend on n n and N N and may be evaluated numerically. Our work is motivated by the estimation of high-dimensional covariance matrices between phenotypic traits in quantitative genetics, particularly within nested linear random-effects models with up to k k levels of randomness. Our proof builds on the Bai-Silverstein [ BS04 ] martingale method with some innovation to handle the multi-level setting.

Approach and prior work

We aim to establish a central limit theorem for the difference between the LSS of
B_{n}
and that of its deterministic equivalent
\tilde{B}_{n}
. The latter is deterministic, it can be computed from

,
j=1,\ldots,N

3

Informally, we prove the existence of sequences of biases
\mu_{n}
and variances
\sigma_{n}
, such that

\displaystyle\sigma_{n}^{-1}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(f(\lambda_{i})-f(\tilde{\lambda}_{i})\right)-\mu_{n}\right]

converges to a standard normal distribution. Here,
\tilde{\lambda}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{\lambda}_{n}
denote the eigenvalues of
\tilde{B}_{n}
. Moreover, we generalize our proof to establish the asymptotic normality for an arbitrary finite list of functions
\{f_{\nu}\}

For sample covariance matrices corresponding to
B_{n}
as in (
1
) with all
T_{j}\equiv T
, Bai and Silverstein
[

]
established the CLT of linear spectral statistics using a three-step strategy:

\bullet

Reduce problem to showing the CLT for scaled Stieltjes transform

\displaystyle M_{n}(z)

\displaystyle=\text{Tr}(B_{n}-z)^{-1}-\text{Tr}(\tilde{B}_{n}-z)^{-1}

\displaystyle=M_{n}^{1}(z)+M_{n}^{2}(z),

where

\displaystyle M_{n}^{1}(z)

\displaystyle=\text{Tr}(B_{n}-z)^{-1}-\mathbbm{E}\text{Tr}(B_{n}-z)^{-1},

\displaystyle M_{n}^{2}(z)

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{E}\text{Tr}(B_{n}-z)^{-1}-\text{Tr}(\tilde{B}_{n}-z)^{-1}.

\bullet

Prove CLT for the centralized term
M_{n}^{1}(z)
via the Martingale Central Limit Theorem.

\bullet

Compute the limit of the deterministic term
M_{n}^{2}(z)
.

This strategy has since been extensively adapted to show CLT of linear spectral statistics for broader classes of large sample covariance matrices. For information-theoretic type sample covariance matrix
S_{n}=(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}L_{1}XL_{2}+A)(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}L_{1}XL_{2}+A)^{T}

L_{1}

L_{2}

[
Hac+12
]
established the CLT for log-determinant statistics, a special class of linear spectral statistics. For

with deterministic
A
,

[
BNY20
]
showed a CLT for linear spectral statistics corresponding to general functions
f
.
[

]

B_{n}
as in (
1
) with
T_{j}=l_{j}^{2}\Sigma
, which can be viewed as corresponding to a variance component estimator within linear random-effects models with a single level of randomness.

Our contributions

We generalize the three-way strategy outlined above in three directions: Firstly, we adapt the strategy to accommodate a matrix
B_{n}
of type (
1
) having
k
levels of variation with a more complicated spectral distribution than previously studied. As we shall detail in Section
3
,
[
FJ19
]
constructed the deterministic equivalent Stieltjes transform of
B_{n}
building upon the solution to a system of
2k
equations, which plays a key role in our construction of the limiting biases and variances. To the best of our knowledge, in past literature, the CLT has been established for sample covariance matrices with Stieltjes transforms determined by a system of no more than two equations
[
BLP19
]
, which corresponds to
k=1
in our context for Gaussian random variables.

Secondly, we establish a CLT for the deviation of the linear spectral statistics calculated using the empirical spectral distribution of
B_{n}
, relative to its deterministic equivalent, instead of to the limiting spectral distribution
[
BS04
,
BLP19
]
. In this formulation, we shall construct the bias
\mu_{n}
and covariance
\sigma_{n}
for the CLT using the deterministic equivalent matrix
\tilde{B}_{n}
. This approach enables computation of
\mu_{n}
and
\sigma_{n}
based on finite sample quantities without assuming the convergence of the aspect ratio
n/N
or the empirical spectral distributions of
\Sigma_{r}
and
L_{r}
. Previous studies such as
[
BNY20
,
Hac+12
]
have also established the CLT with respect to the deterministic equivalent, but have provided only limited analysis of the limiting bias. Specifically, they demonstrated that the bias asymptotically vanishes for complex Gaussian random variables. Our research focuses on real Gaussian random variables, for which the limiting bias persists.

Thirdly, our proof of the tightness of
M_{n}^{1}
simplifies significantly by utilizing the concentration results from
[
GZ00
]
, applied to the bulk distribution of
B_{n}
.

Finally, we propose a method to numerically evaluate the limiting bias and covariance in the central limit theorem, building upon an iterative algorithm proposed in
[
FJ19
]
and the trapezoidal rule
[
TW14
]
.

2.3
Overview of proof

As outlined in the introduction, our proof generalizes a three-step strategy developed by Bai and Silverstein
[
BS04
]
. This approach consists of three main components:

(a)

finite-dimensional CLT of the centralized term
M_{n}^{1}(z)
, as formalized in equation (
40
);

(b)

tightness of
M_{n}^{1}(z)
as in (
77
);

(c)

convergence of the deterministic term
M_{n}^{2}(z)
as in (
81

In addressing the first component, we establish the CLT by applying the martingale central limit theorem. Initially, we write

as a series of martingale differences and demonstrate that this sequence exhibits light-tailed behavior, thereby satisfying condition (ii) of Theorem
E.1
. The primary distinction in our approach concerns the calculation of limiting covariance. This task is challenging due to the complexity of matrix
B_{n}
defined in (
1
). Specifically, our calculations are reduced to evaluating the limits of the normalized traces:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\text{Tr}(\mathbbm{E}_{j}D_{j}^{-1}(z_{1})\Sigma_{r}\mathbbm{E}_{j}D_{j}^{-1}(z_{2})\Sigma_{s}),\quad{\forall r,s=1,\ldots,k}.

(13)

Above,

, and

denotes the conditional expectation with respect to the
\sigma
-field generated by

. This deviates from the formulations in
[
BS04
]
and
[
BLP19
]
, where the focus was on:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\text{Tr}(\mathbbm{E}_{j}D_{j}^{-1}(z_{1})\Sigma\mathbbm{E}_{j}D_{j}^{-1}(z_{2})\Sigma),

with
B_{n}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Sigma^{1/2}x_{i}x_{i}^{T}\Sigma^{1/2}

As a result, we derive asymptotic equivalents for the normalized traces as delineated in equation (

2.5
Application to multivariate linear random-effects models

Genetic covariance matrices are central to the study of
multivariate quantitative genetics, providing insights into how traits genetically covary with each other
due to pleiotropy and linkage disequilibrium. Accurate estimation of these matrices in the context of linear random effects models is fundamental to predicting organism response to
selection and understanding evolutionary limits. Nested random-effects models, in particular, have gained prominence for their applicability in improving selective breeding programs, notably within the poultry industry.

Consider a nested random-effects model with
n_{s}
samples and
p

Y\in\mathbbm{R}^{n_{s}\times p}
are modeled by a
k
-level Gaussian random-effects model

\displaystyle Y=U_{1}\alpha_{1}+\ldots+U_{k}\alpha_{k}.

(14)

Above, each
\alpha_{r}\in\mathbbm{R}^{F_{r}\times p}
represents the
r
-th level of random effect, with i.i.d. rows each distributed as
N(0,\Sigma_{r})
. Each
U_{r}
denotes the deterministic membership matrix that assigns each individual to the corresponding group of random effect in the
r
-th level. For instance, in the first level of randomness, if the
i
th individual is assigned to the
j
th group, then
U_{1}[i,j]=1
, and
U_{1}[i,l]=0
when
l\neq j
. By the nested nature of the model, these membership matrices satisfy

\displaystyle F_{1}\leq...\leq F_{k}\leq n_{s},

\displaystyle\text{col}(U_{1})\subset\ldots\subset\text{col}(U_{k}).

For simplicity, here we omit possible fixed effects. In this context, the genetic covariance of interest is a known linear combination
\Sigma_{1},\ldots,\Sigma_{k}

[
Fal96
]
. We are interested in estimating its eigenvalue distribution, which contains important information on evolutionary dynamics, e.g. null space dimension, largest eigenvalues, etc. To achieve this goal, one possible approach is to formulate parametric assumptions on the eigenvalue distributions of each
\Sigma_{r}
, and obtain method of moments estimators of these parameters based on the sum of squares matrix

\displaystyle B_{p}=\frac{1}{F_{1}}Y^{T}U_{1}(U_{1}^{T}U_{1})^{-1}U_{1}^{T}Y.

(15)

In particular, if we model the eigenvalue distributions of
\Sigma_{1},\ldots,\Sigma_{k}
as parameterized by
\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{l}
for a positive integer
l
, we can obtain method of moments estimators from a mapping between the parameters and moments of
B_{p}
in the form of

\displaystyle(\hat{\tau}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\tau}_{l})=\mathcal{F}(\text{Tr}B_{p},\ldots,\text{Tr}B_{p}^{l}).

(16)

We refer to a working manuscript
[
JWX
]
for the detailed expression of the mapping
\mathcal{F}
.

Here we show how
B_{p}
may be written in the form (
1
) with
(n,N)=(p,F_{1})
so that Theorem
2.2
may be applied. By nature of membership matrices, we have that
U_{1}^{T}U_{1}
is diagonal. Under the nested model, we further have that
U_{1}^{T}U_{r}
has full row rank and that
U_{1}^{T}U_{r}U_{r}^{T}U_{1}
is diagonal and positive definite. Additionally, considering a matrix
A\in\mathbbm{R}^{m_{1}\times m_{2}}
,
m_{1}<m_{2}
and a standard Gaussian vector
x\in\mathbbm{R}^{m_{2}}
, there exists a standard Gaussian vector
z\in\mathbbm{R}^{m_{1}}
such that
Ax\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scriptsize\mbox{d}}}}{{=}}(AA^{T})^{1/2}z
.
As a result, by Gaussianity of the random effects,

\displaystyle(U_{1}^{T}U_{1})^{-1/2}U_{1}^{T}U_{r}\alpha_{r}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scriptsize\mbox{d}}}}{{=}}L_{r}X_{r}\Sigma_{r}^{1/2},

where
L_{r}=(U_{1}^{T}U_{1})^{-1/2}(U_{1}^{T}U_{r}U_{r}^{T}U_{1})^{1/2}
is diagonal and positive definite, and
X_{r}\in\mathbbm{R}^{F_{1}\times p}
has i.i.d. standard Gaussian entries. Thus, we can express the sum of squares matrix as

\displaystyle B_{p}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scriptsize\mbox{d}}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{F_{1}}{\sum_{r,s=1}^{k}\Sigma_{r}^{1/2}}X_{r}^{T}L_{r}L_{s}X_{s}\Sigma_{s}^{1/2}.

(17)

Let
T_{f}=\sum_{r=1}^{k}l_{rf}^{2}\Sigma_{r}
and denote the
f
th row of
X_{r}
by
x_{rf}
, then by the independence of the random effects there further exist i.i.d. standard Gaussian vectors

x_{f}\in\mathbbm{R}^{p}
such that
\sum_{r=1}^{k}l_{rf}\Sigma_{r}^{1/2}x_{rf}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scriptsize\mbox{d}}}}{{=}}T_{f}^{1/2}x_{f}
. Therefore, we can alternatively express
B_{p}
as

\displaystyle B_{p}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scriptsize\mbox{d}}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{F_{1}}\sum_{f=1}^{F_{1}}T_{f}^{1/2}x_{f}x_{f}^{T}T_{f}^{1/2},\quad T_{f}=\sum_{r=1}^{k}l_{rf}^{2}\Sigma_{r}.

(18)

Thus, from a central limit theorem on the LSS of
B_{p}
such as the moments
(\text{Tr}B_{p},\ldots,\text{Tr}B_{p}^{l})
, we can apply the Delta method to recover limiting results on the method of moments estimators from the mapping defined in (
16
).

By condition of tightness in Theorem 8.2 and 12.3 in Billingsley

First, building upon the analyses in

Lemma 4.3

(Concentration of the spectral measure of

)
.

For functions f such that

is lipschitz, we have

\displaystyle\mathbbm{P}\left(\left|\int fdF_{n}-\mathbbm{E}\int fdF_{n}\geq\epsilon\right|\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{C_{\epsilon}n^{2}}{|g|_{L}^{2}}\right),

(78)

Using this concentration result, we establish the tightness of

as follows:

Lemma 4.4
.

On

, the stochastic process

\left\{M_{n}^{1}(z)=n(m_{n}(z)-\mathbbm{E}[m_{n}(z)])\bigg|z\in C_{0}\right\}

Proof.

When
\text{Im}z>v_{0}
, by definition of tightness we would like to prove that for an positive
\epsilon
,
\eta>0
, there exists
\delta\in\left[0,1\right]
such that, for any
|z_{1}-z_{2}|\leq\delta
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbbm{P}\left[\left|M_{n}^{1}(z_{1})-M_{n}^{1}(z_{2})\right|>\epsilon\right]<\eta.

Define a function
\tilde{f}(\lambda)=(\lambda-z_{1})^{-1}-(\lambda-z_{2})^{-1}
, then

Let

are

Lipschitz. Therefore, applying Lemma

, we have the bound

\displaystyle\mathbbm{P}\left[\left|M_{n}^{1}(z_{1})-M_{n}^{1}(z_{2})\right|>\epsilon\right]=\mathbbm{P}\left[n\left|\int\tilde{f}dF_{n}-\mathbbm{E}\int\tilde{f}dF_{n}\right|>\epsilon\right]<2\exp{\left(-\frac{C_{\epsilon}v_{0}^{8}}{\delta^{2}}\right)}.

We conclude the proof by taking

Proof of Lemma

Before proceeding with the proof, we note that our proof is self-contained, it simplifies the justification for the truncation step described by
[
BS04
]
, adapting it from moments assumptions to the Gaussian assumption.

Under Assumption
2.1
, by Gaussianity, we have
\mathbbm{E}|x_{11}|^{4}=3<\infty
. For
m=1,2,\ldots
, find
n_{m}
(
n_{m}>n_{m-1}
) satisfying

\displaystyle m^{4}\mathbbm{E}\left[|x_{11}|^{4}\mathbbm{1}[|x_{11}|\geq\sqrt{n}/m]\right]<2^{-m}.

for all

. Define
\delta_{n}^{\prime}=1/m
for all

(=1 for
n<n_{1}
). Then, as
n\rightarrow 0
,
\delta_{n}^{\prime}\rightarrow 0
and

\displaystyle\delta_{n}^{\prime-4}\mathbbm{E}\left[|x_{11}|^{4}\mathbbm{1}[|x_{11}|\geq\delta_{n}^{\prime}\sqrt{n}]\right]\rightarrow 0.

Define

, then the following conditions hold:

\displaystyle\delta_{n}\rightarrow 0,\quad\delta_{n}n^{1/4}\rightarrow\infty,\quad\delta_{n}^{-4}\mathbbm{E}\left[|x_{11}|^{4}\mathbbm{1}[|x_{11}|\geq\delta_{n}\sqrt{n}]\right]\rightarrow 0.

(98)

Let
\check{x}_{ji}=x_{ji}\mathbbm{1}_{|x_{ji}|\leq\delta_{n}\sqrt{n}}
, and
\check{B}_{n}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}T_{j}^{1/2}\check{x}_{j}\check{x}_{j}^{T}T_{j}^{1/2}
, then

\displaystyle\mathbbm{P}(B_{n}\neq\check{B}_{n})

\displaystyle\leq nN\mathbbm{P}(|x_{11}|\geq\delta_{n}\sqrt{n})

\displaystyle=nN\mathbbm{E}\left[\mathbbm{1}[|x_{11}|\geq\delta_{n}\sqrt{n}]\right]

\displaystyle\leq nN\mathbbm{E}\left[\frac{|x_{11}|^{4}}{\delta_{n}^{4}n^{2}}\mathbbm{1}[|x_{11}|\geq\delta_{n}\sqrt{n}]\right]

\displaystyle\leq K\delta_{n}^{-4}\mathbbm{E}\left[|x_{11}|^{4}\mathbbm{1}[|x_{11}|\geq\delta_{n}\sqrt{n}]\right]\rightarrow 0

for some constant
K
. In other words, for any function
f
satisfying the assumptions in Theorem
2.2
, we have

\displaystyle\int fdG_{n}(x)=\int fd\check{G}_{n}(x)+o_{p}(1),

(99)

where
\check{G}_{n}
is analogue of
G_{n}
with the matrix
B_{n}
replaced by
\check{B}_{n}
.

Now, let
\hat{x}_{ji}=\check{x}_{ji}/\sigma_{n}
, where

\displaystyle\sigma_{n}^{2}

\displaystyle=\text{Var}(\check{x}_{ji})=\mathbbm{E}|\check{x}_{ji}|^{2}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{2\delta_{n}\sqrt{n}\varphi(\delta_{n}\sqrt{n})}{\Phi(\delta_{n}\sqrt{n})-\Phi(-\delta_{n}\sqrt{n})}\rightarrow 1

follows from Gaussianity. Above,
\varphi
and
\Phi
respectively represent the probability density function and cumulative density function of the standard normal distribution. Equation (
98
) yields

\displaystyle\mathbbm{E}|\hat{x}_{11}|^{4}=\frac{\mathbbm{E}|\check{x}_{11}|^{4}}{\sigma_{n}^{4}}=\frac{\mathbbm{E}|{x}_{11}|^{4}+o(\delta_{n}^{4})}{\sigma_{n}^{4}}=3+o(1).

Collecting the terms gives

\displaystyle\hat{x}_{ji}<\delta_{n}\sqrt{n},\quad\mathbbm{E}[\hat{x}_{ji}]=0,\quad\mathbbm{E}|\hat{x}_{ji}|^{2}=1,\quad\mathbbm{E}|\hat{x}_{ji}|^{4}=3+o(1).

Let
\hat{B}_{n}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}T_{j}^{1/2}\hat{x}_{j}\hat{x}_{j}^{T}T_{j}^{1/2}=\sigma_{n}^{-2}\check{B}_{n}
and similarly define
\hat{G}_{n}
. Denote the
i
th smallest eigenvalue of a PSD matrix
A
by
\lambda_{i}^{A}
, then

\displaystyle\left|\int fd\check{G}_{n}(x)-\int fd\hat{G}_{n}(x)\right|

\displaystyle\leq K\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\lambda_{i}^{\check{B}_{n}}-\lambda_{i}^{\hat{B_{n}}}|

\displaystyle\leq K(\sigma_{n}^{2}-1)\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\lambda_{i}^{\hat{B}_{n}}|

\displaystyle\leq Kn\frac{2\delta_{n}\sqrt{n}\varphi(\delta_{n}\sqrt{n})}{(\Phi(\delta_{n}\sqrt{n})-\Phi(-\delta_{n}\sqrt{n}))}\lambda_{\max}^{\hat{B}_{n}}.

Above,

is the upper bound of
f^{\prime}
, and by the property (
27

is almost surely bounded by a deterministic finite value. Finally, direct calculations show

\displaystyle\delta_{n}\sqrt{n}\varphi(\delta_{n}\sqrt{n})=C\frac{1}{\delta_{n}^{5}n^{3/2}}\frac{(\delta_{n}^{2}n)^{3}}{e^{\delta_{n}^{2}n}}\rightarrow 0.

As a result, together with equation (
99
), we arrive at the conclusion

\displaystyle\int fdG_{n}(x)=\int fd\hat{G}_{n}(x)+o_{p}(1).

\displaystyle(C+\tau rr^{*})^{-1}-C^{-1}=\tau\frac{C^{-1}rr^{*}C^{-1}}{1+\tau r^{*}C^{-1}r}.

Using the spectral decomposition
B=\sum\lambda_{j}q_{j}q_{j}^{*}
with
\lambda_{j}\in\mathbbm{R}
and
r_{j}=q_{j}^{*}r
, we have

\displaystyle\lVert C^{-1}r\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=r^{*}(B-\bar{z}I)^{-1}(B-{z}I)^{-1}r=\sum_{j}\frac{r_{j}^{2}}{|\lambda_{j}-z|^{2}},

\displaystyle\text{Im}r^{*}C^{-1}r

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}r_{j}^{2}\text{Im}\frac{1}{\lambda_{j}-z}=(\text{Im}z)\sum_{j}\frac{r_{j}^{2}}{|\lambda_{j}-z|^{2}}.

Hence

\displaystyle\left|\text{tr}\left[C^{-1}-(C+\tau rr^{*})^{-1}\right]A\right|=\left|\frac{\tau r^{*}C^{-1}AC^{-1}r}{1+\tau r^{*}C^{-1}r}\right|\leq\frac{\lVert A\rVert\lVert C^{-1}r\rVert^{2}}{|\text{Im}r^{*}C^{-1}r|}\leq\frac{\lVert A\rVert}{\text{Im}z}.

∎

Lemma E.3

(Lemma 0.13 from
[
BLP19
]
)
.

Let
M
be a nonrandom matrix, for each
j=1,...,n
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbbm{E}|\text{Tr}D_{j}^{-1}M-\mathbbm{E}\text{Tr}D_{j}^{-1}M|^{2}\leq C\lVert M\rVert^{2}.

Proof.

Following a similar argument as in (
42
), we have

\displaystyle\text{Tr}D^{-1}M-\mathbbm{E}\text{Tr}D^{-1}M

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbbm{E}_{j}\text{Tr}D^{-1}M-\mathbbm{E}_{j-1}\text{Tr}D^{-1}M

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}(\mathbbm{E}_{j}-\mathbbm{E}_{j-1})\text{Tr}(D^{-1}-D_{j}^{-1})M,

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j=1}^{N}(\mathbbm{E}_{j}-\mathbbm{E}_{j-1})\beta_{j}(z)r_{j}^{T}D_{j}^{-1}MD_{j}^{-1}r_{j}.

By the mutual independence of
(\mathbbm{E}_{j}-\mathbbm{E}_{j-1})\beta_{j}(z)r_{j}^{T}D_{j}^{-1}MD_{j}^{-1}r_{j}
and the basic algebraic fact that
(x+y)^{2}\leq 2(x^{2}+y^{2})
, we further have

\displaystyle\mathbbm{E}|\text{Tr}D^{-1}M-\mathbbm{E}\text{Tr}D^{-1}M|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbbm{E}|(\mathbbm{E}_{j}-\mathbbm{E}_{j-1})\beta_{j}(z)r_{j}^{T}D_{j}^{-1}MD_{j}^{-1}r_{j}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 4\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbbm{E}|\beta_{j}(z)r_{j}^{T}D_{j}^{-1}MD_{j}^{-1}r_{j}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 8\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbbm{E}|\beta_{j}(z)|^{2}|r_{j}^{T}D_{j}^{-1}MD_{j}^{-1}r_{j}-N^{-1}\text{Tr}D_{j}^{-1}MD_{j}^{-1}T_{j}|^{2}

\displaystyle+8\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbbm{E}|N^{-1}\text{Tr}D_{j}^{-1}MD_{j}^{-1}T_{j}|^{2}.

Together with Lemma
3.4
and the boundedness of
\beta_{j}
established in Lemma
4.1
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbbm{E}|\text{Tr}D^{-1}M-\mathbbm{E}\text{Tr}D^{-1}M|^{2}\leq C\lVert M\rVert^{2},

for some constant
C
.
Applying the same arguments for
D_{j}(z)
,
j=1,\ldots,N
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\mathbbm{E}|\text{Tr}D_{j}^{-1}M-\mathbbm{E}\text{Tr}D_{j}^{-1}M|^{2}\leq C\lVert M\rVert^{2}.

∎

Lemma E.4

(Equation (1.15) in
[
BS04
]
)
.

Let
x_{1}=(x_{1i})_{1}^{n}
be a vector with i.i.d. entries satisfying
\mathbbm{E}x_{11}=0
,
\mathbbm{E}x_{11}^{2}=\sigma^{2}
,
\mathbbm{E}x_{11}^{4}<\infty
, then for
n\times n
matrices
A=(a_{ij})
and
B=(b_{ij})
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbbm{E}(x_{1}^{T}Ax_{1}-\text{Tr}A)(x_{1}^{T}Bx_{1}-\text{Tr}B)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\mathbbm{E}|x_{11}|^{4}-3\sigma^{4})\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{ii}b_{ii}+\sigma^{4}(\text{Tr}AB+\text{Tr}AB^{T})+(\sigma^{2}-1)^{2}\text{Tr}A\text{Tr}B.

Under Assumption
3.3
, if additionally
B=B^{T}
, the above relationship simplifies to

\displaystyle\mathbbm{E}(x_{1}^{T}Ax_{1}-\text{Tr}A)(x_{1}^{T}Bx_{1}-\text{Tr}B)=2\text{Tr}AB+o(1)\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{ii}b_{ii}.

Proof.

Since
\mathbbm{E}x^{T}Ax=\sigma^{2}\text{Tr}A
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbbm{E}(x_{1}^{T}Ax_{1}-\text{Tr}A)(x_{1}^{T}Bx_{1}-\text{Tr}B)=\mathbbm{E}(x_{1}^{T}Ax_{1})(x_{1}^{T}Bx_{1})-2\sigma^{2}\text{Tr}A\text{Tr}B+\text{Tr}A\text{Tr}B.

It remains to study

\displaystyle\mathbbm{E}(x_{1}^{T}Ax_{1})(x_{1}^{T}Bx_{1})=\sum_{ijkl}a_{ij}b_{kl}\mathbbm{E}x_{1i}x_{1j}x_{1k}x_{1l}.

Denote each summand by
C_{ijkl}
, then by independence, the sum reduces to

\displaystyle\mathbbm{E}(x_{1}^{T}Ax_{1})(x_{1}^{T}Bx_{1})=T_{ii}+T_{ij}+T_{ik}+T_{il},

where

\displaystyle T_{ii}=\sum_{i=j=k=l}C_{ijkl}=\mathbbm{E}x_{1i}^{4}\sum_{i}a_{ii}b_{ii},

\displaystyle T_{ij}=\sum_{i=j\neq k=l}C_{ijkl}=\sigma^{4}\sum_{i\neq k}a_{ii}b_{kk}=\sigma^{4}(\text{Tr}A\text{Tr}B-\sum_{i}a_{ii}b_{ii}),

\displaystyle T_{ik}=\sum_{i=k\neq j=l}C_{ijkl}=\sigma^{4}\sum_{i\neq j}a_{ij}b_{ij}=\sigma^{4}(\text{Tr}AB^{T}-\sum_{i}a_{ii}b_{ii}),

\displaystyle T_{il}=\sum_{i=l\neq j=k}C_{ijkl}=\sigma^{4}\sum_{i\neq j}a_{ij}b_{ji}=\sigma^{4}(\text{Tr}AB-\sum_{i}a_{ii}b_{ii}).

∎
