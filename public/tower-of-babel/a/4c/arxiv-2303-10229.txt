Abstract
We study the minimum number of distinct distances between point sets on two curves in 𝐑 3 \mathbf{R}^{3} . Assume that one curve contains m m points and the other n n points. Our main results: (a) When the curves are conic sections, we characterize all cases where the number of distances is O ⁡ ( m + n ) O(m+n) . This includes new constructions for points on two parabolas, two ellipses, and one ellipse and one hyperbola. In all other cases, the number of distances is Ω ⁡ ( min ⁡ { m 2 / 3 ​ n 2 / 3 , m 2 , n 2 } ) \Omega(\min\{m^{2/3}n^{2/3},m^{2},n^{2}\}) . (b) When the curves are not necessarily algebraic but smooth and contained in perpendicular planes, we characterize all cases where the number of distances is O ⁡ ( m + n ) O(m+n) . This includes a surprising new construction of non-algebraic curves that involve logarithms. In all other cases, the number of distances is Ω ⁡ ( min ⁡ { m 2 / 3 ​ n 2 / 3 , m 2 , n 2 } ) \Omega(\min\{m^{2/3}n^{2/3},m^{2},n^{2}\}) .

Introduction

Erdős
[
7
]
introduced his distinct distances problem in 1946, which has motivated significant mathematical developments in the fields of discrete geometry and additive combinatorics. For a given
n
, the problem asks for the minimum number of distinct distances spanned by
n
points in
\mathbf{R}^{2}
. Given a finite point set
\mathcal{P}
, we define
D(\mathcal{P})
as the number of distinct distances spanned by pairs of
\mathcal{P}^{2}
. The distinct distances problem asks for
\min D(\mathcal{P})
, where the minimum is over all sets
\mathcal{P}\subset\mathbf{R}^{2}
of
n
points. Erdős discovered such a set
\mathcal{P}
that satisfies
D(\mathcal{P})=O(n/\sqrt{\log n}).

The problem was almost completely resolved by Guth and Katz
[
8
]
, who proved that every set
\mathcal{P}
of
n
points satisfies
D(\mathcal{P})=\Omega(n/\log n)
.

The above is one of many distinct distances problems, most of which were also introduced by Erdős (for example, see Sheffer
[
14
]
).
In a

distinct distances problem, we have two point sets
\mathcal{P}_{1}
and
\mathcal{P}_{2}
and are interested in the distinct distances spanned by the pairs of
\mathcal{P}_{1}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}
.
We denote this number as

.
In one family of bipartite distinct distances problems, we assume that there exist curves

Theorem 1.3

(Few distances between conics)
.

Let
C_{1},C_{2}\subset\mathbf{R}^{3}
be non-degenerate conic sections.

(a) Assume that
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are either perpendicular circles, aligned circles, CPO parabolas, or matching curves. Then
(C_{1},C_{2})
spans few distances.

(b) If
(C_{1},C_{2})
is not one of the configurations from part (a), then
(C_{1},C_{2})
spans many distances.

We note that, as in the cases of
\mathbf{R}^{2}
and circles in
\mathbf{R}^{3}
, every conic configuration spans either few or many distinct distances.

Theorem
1.3
does not include the case where at least one of the curves is a line. This case is significantly simpler to study, as shown in the proof of the following result.
Throughout this work, the word

That is, cylinders are the surfaces that are obtained by translating, rotating, and scaling

The

Let

Our technique.

Our analysis begins with the proof of Mathialagan and Sheffer
[
12
]
, but significantly generalizes this proof. We now briefly describe one of the main tools of our analysis.

We say that a set
S\subset\mathbf{R}^{3}
is an
arc
if there exists a smooth bijective map
\gamma:(0,1)\to S
.
We also say that
\gamma
is a
parameterization
of
S
.
For more information, such as the definition of smoothness, see Section
2
.
We extend the properties of spanning many distances and spanning few distances to pairs of arcs in the straightforward way.
That is, instead of considering point sets of two curves, we consider point sets on two arcs.

Consider arcs
A_{1},A_{2}\subset\mathbf{R}^{3}
with respetive parameterizations
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}
.
For
s\in(0,1)
, we denote the three coordinates of
\gamma_{i}(s)
as
\gamma_{i,x}(s)
,
\gamma_{i,y}(s)
, and
\gamma_{i,z}(s)
.
The
distance function
of
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
is

\rho(s,t)=(\gamma_{1,x}(s)-\gamma_{2,x}(t))^{2}+(\gamma_{1,y}(s)-\gamma_{2,y}(t))^{2}+(\gamma_{1,z}(s)-\gamma_{2,z}(t))^{2}.

(5)

The distance function receives a point from each arc, using the parameterizations
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
.
The function returns the square of the distance between those two points.

A function
f:\mathbf{R}^{2}\to\mathbf{R}

is of a special form
if there exist open intervals
I_{1},I_{2},I_{3}\subset\mathbf{R}
and smooth functions
\phi_{1}:I_{1}\to\mathbf{R},\phi_{2}:I_{2}\to\mathbf{R},\phi_{3}:I_{3}\to\mathbf{R}
that satisfy the following: For every
s\in I_{1}
and
t\in I_{2}
, we have that
\phi_{1}(s)+\phi_{2}(t)\in I_{3}
and that

f(s,t)=\phi_{3}(\phi_{1}(s)+\phi_{2}(t)).

(6)

The following lemma is a generalization of a lemma of Mathialagan and Sheffer

Lemma 1.7
.

Let
A_{1},A_{2}\subset\mathbf{R}^{3}
be arcs with respective parametrizations
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}
.
Let
\rho(s,t)
be the distance function of
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
.
Then at least one of the following holds.

(a) The configuration

spans many distances.

(b) The function

After proving Lemma
1.7
, it remains to study when the distance function
\rho(s,t)
is of a special form.
To do that, we rely on the following
derivative test
.
Here
\rho_{s}
and
\rho_{t}
are the first partial derivative of
\rho
according to
s
and

Lemma 1.8

(Derivative test)
.

Let
\rho:(0,1)^{2}\to\mathbf{R}
be a smooth function of a special form, such that
\rho_{s}\not\equiv 0
and
\rho_{t}\not\equiv 0
in every open neighborhood in
\mathbf{R}^{2}
. Then there exists an open neighborhood in
\mathbf{R}^{2}
in which

\frac{\partial^{2}(\ln|\rho_{s}/\rho_{t}|)}{\partial s\partial t}\equiv 0.

In our proofs, we study the expression from Lemma
1.8

\rho(s,t)
is the distance fuction from (

Then the contrapositive of Lemma

Theorem 1.9

Open problems.

One main remaining open problem is to characterize all pairs of curves in
\mathbf{R}^{3}
that span few distances.
With the above in mind, we propose the following question:

True or false? If two planar curves in
\mathbf{R}^{3}
span few distances, then the planes that contain the curves are either parallel or perpendicular.

If the above statement is true, then Theorem
1.5
and Lemma
3.1
characterize all pairs of
planar
curves in
\mathbf{R}^{3}
that span few distances.

Recently, Solymosi and Zahl
[
20
]
improved the bound for the number of distinct distances between two lines from
\Omega(\min\{m^{2/3}n^{2/3},m^{2},n^{2}\})
to
\Omega(\min\{m^{3/4}n^{3/4},m^{2},n^{2}\})
.
It seems plausible that the bounds of Theorem
1.1
, Theorem
1.2
, and the current work could also be improved in a similar way.
Such a potential improvement would not replace the proofs of the current work.
Instead, it would be added on top of our proofs, to amplify the bounds that are obtained.

We also suggest studying distinct distances in
\mathbf{R}^{3}
between points on two curves that are not necessarily algebraic.
Theorem
6.1
states that, when the two curves are contained in perpendicular planes, the only cases with few distances and a curve that is not algebraic are

•

one of the curves is a line and the other is contained in a parallel or perpendicular plane.

•

matching log-circles.

•

periodic functions that involve a sine or a cosine.

The third bullet point is not covered by Theorem
6.1
, since a periodic curve may not be the union of finitely many arcs and points.
In other words, problem is

Are there pairs of curves in
\mathbf{R}^{3}
that span few distances, are not contained in perpendicular plane, not periodic, not lines, and at least one is not algebraic?.

In this section, we briefly survey notation and results from real algebraic geometry.

For polynomials
f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}\in\mathbf{R}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]
, the
variety
defined by
f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}
is

\mathbf{V}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{k})=\left\{p\in\mathbf{R}^{d}\ :\ f_{1}(p)=f_{2}(p)=\cdots=f_{k}(p)=0\right\}.

We say that a set
U\subset\mathbf{R}^{d}
is a variety if there exist
f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}\in\mathbf{R}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]
such that
U={\bf v}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{k})
.
While not true over some other fields, in
\mathbf{R}^{d}
every variety can be defined using a single polynomial.
A variety
U
is
irreducible
if there do not exist two varieties
U_{1},U_{2}\subsetneq U
such that
U=U_{1}\cup U_{2}
.
The
dimension
of an irreducible variety
U
, denoted
\dim U
, is the largest integer
d_{U}
for which there exist non-empty irreducible varieties
U_{0},U_{1},\ldots,U_{d_{U}}
such that

U_{0}\subsetneq U_{1}\subsetneq\ldots\subsetneq U_{d_{U}}=U.

We now mention two classic results about intersections of varieties in
\mathbf{R}^{d}
.

Theorem 2.1

(Bezout’s theorem)
.

Let
f
and
g
be polynomials in
\mathbf{R}[x,y]
of degrees
k_{f}
and
k_{g}
, respectively. If
f
and
g
do not have common factors then
\mathbf{V}(f)\cap\mathbf{V}(g)
consists of at most
k_{f}\cdot k_{g}
points.

Theorem 2.2

(Milnor–Thom
[
11
,
21
]
)
.

Let
f_{1},...,f_{m}\in\mathbf{R}[x_{1},...,x_{d}]
be of degree at most
k
. Then the number of connected components of
\mathbf{V}(f_{1},...,f_{m})
is at most

k(2k-1)^{d}.

We observe the following simple corollary of Theorem
2.2
.

Corollary 2.3
.

Let
C\subset\mathbf{R}^{3}
be a curve and let
U\subset\mathbf{R}^{3}
be a variety. If
C\cap X
is infinite then
C\subset X
.

Proof.

We set
W=C\cap U
and assume that
W
is infinite. By definition,
W
is a variety.
If
\dim W=0
, then this variety has infinitely many connected components.
Since this contradicts Theorem
2.2
, we have that
\dim W\geq 1
.
Since
C
is irreducible and one-dimensional, we conclude that
C=W\subset X
.
∎

Lemma

.

Let

Proof.

By definition, there exist open intervals

and smooth
\phi_{1}:I_{1}\to\mathbf{R},\phi_{2}:I_{2}\to\mathbf{R}
,
\phi_{3}:I_{3}\to\mathbf{R}

By the assumptions on

in which

and
\rho_{t}
are never zero.
In

\displaystyle\rho_{s}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial[\phi_{3}(\phi_{1}(s)+\phi_{2}(t))]}{\partial s}=\phi_{3}^{\prime}(\phi_{1}(s)+\phi_{2}(t))\cdot\phi_{1}^{\prime}(s),

\displaystyle\rho_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial[\phi_{3}(\phi_{1}(s)+\phi_{2}(t))]}{\partial t}=\phi_{3}^{\prime}(\phi_{1}(s)+\phi_{2}(t))\cdot\phi_{2}^{\prime}(t).

With these equations, we get that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}(\ln|\rho_{s}/\rho_{t}|)}{\partial s\partial t}=\frac{\partial^{2}(\ln|\phi_{1}^{\prime}(s)/\phi_{2}^{\prime}(t)|)}{\partial s\partial t}=\frac{\partial^{2}(\ln|\phi_{1}^{\prime}(s)|-\ln|\phi_{2}^{\prime}(t)|)}{\partial s\partial t}=0,

For

5

In this section, we study configurations that span few distances. To show that a configuration spans few distances, we show that the distance function is of a special form and then apply Lemma

Recall that, in this work, we only consider right circular cylinders.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\ell\subset\mathbf{R}^{3}
be a line in

and let
C

be a curve contained in a cylinder centered around

We translate and rotate the space so that

becomes the

is contained in a cylinder of radius one around

In this case,

it must be a circle contained in a plane orthogonal to

be an arc of

In Lemma

Lemma 6.2

For nonzero

We consider a unit vector in the direction of

.

•

If

is an ellipse then
\gamma_{2}(t)=(p,q,r)+\frac{t^{2}-1}{t^{2}+1}\mathbf{v}_{1}+\frac{2t}{t^{2}+1}\mathbf{v}_{2}
.

As before, these parameterizations might exclude a single point of

.

Let
\rho(s,t)
be the distance function of

and
\gamma_{2}(t)

By Lemma

, either

is of a special form or

spans many distances.
We assume that we are in the case where

is of a special form.
If
\rho_{s}
or
\rho_{t}
is identically zero in an open neighborhood, then Lemma
6.3
implies that
C_{1}
or
C_{2}
is a line.
Since this contradicts the assumption of the theorem,
\rho_{s}
and
\rho_{t}
are not identically zero in any open neighborhood.

By the above, we may apply Lemma
1.8
with
\rho(s,t)
.
The lemma implies that there exists an open neighborhood in
\mathbf{R}^{2}
in which

\frac{\partial^{2}(\ln|\rho_{s}/\rho_{t}|)}{\partial s\partial t}\equiv 0.

(16)

A careful reader might complain that Lemma
1.8
is defined for pararmeterizations with the domain
(0,1)
,
while the above parameterizations assume
s,t\in\mathbf{R}
.
It is easy to address this technicality.
For example, we can obtain half of the curve by composing the above parameterization with
h(r)=1/r
, where
r\in(0,1)
.
The other half is obtained by composing the parameterization with
h(r)=-1/r
.
By observing (
6
), we note that having a special form is invariant under replacing
s
with
\pm 1/s
and
t
with
\pm 1/t
.

The left side of (
16
) is a rational function.
To see that, we set
h(s,t)=\rho_{s}/\rho_{t}
.
Since
\rho(s,t)
is a rational function in
s
and
t
, so is
h(s,t)
.
For each point
(s,t)
that satisifies
\rho_{s}(s,t)\neq 0
and
\rho_{t}(s,t)\neq 0
, there exists an open neighbourhood where
|h(s,t)|=c\cdot h(s,t)
, with
c\in\{-1,1\}
. In either case,

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\log|h(s,t)|=\frac{1}{c\cdot h(s,t)}\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(c\cdot h(s,t))=\frac{h_{t}(s,t)}{h(s,t)}.

We write
\mathbf{v}_{1}=(x_{1},y_{1},z_{1})
and
\mathbf{v}_{2}=(x_{2},y_{2},z_{2})
.
Then the left side of (
16
) is a rational function in
s
and
t
whose coefficients are rational functions in
c,p,q,r,x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2},z_{1},z_{2}
.
We denote the numerator of this rational function as
\rho_{N}(s,t)
.

By (
16
), the polynomial
\rho_{N}(s,t)
is identically zero in an open neighborhood of
\mathbf{R}^{2}
.
That is, all the coefficients of
\rho_{N}(s,t)
are zero.
We use Wolfram Mathematica to compute
\rho_{N}(s,t)
.
We then show that, when we are not in one of the special configurations that span few distances, it is impossible for all the coefficients of
\rho_{N}(s,t)
to be zero simultaneously.
This contradiction implies that
(C_{1},C_{2})
spans many distances, as asserted.
For the Mathematica computations, see Appendices
A
and
B
.
∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem
1.3
.
We first recall the statement of this result.

Theorem
1.3
(Few distances between conics)

Let
C_{1},C_{2}\subset\mathbf{R}^{3}
be non-degenerate conic sections.

(a) Assume that
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are either perpendicular circles, aligned circles, CPO parabolas, or matching curves. Then
(C_{1},C_{2})
spans few distances.

(b) If
(C_{1},C_{2})
is not one of the configurations from part (a), then
(C_{1},C_{2})
spans many distances.

Proof.

Part (a) is immediate by combining Lemmas
3.1
,
5.3
,
5.4
, and
5.5
.
Part (b) is immediate from Lemma
7.1
.
∎

\displaystyle=a\left(x-\frac{b}{2a}y\right)^{2}+b\left(x-\frac{b}{2a}y\right)y+cy^{2}
