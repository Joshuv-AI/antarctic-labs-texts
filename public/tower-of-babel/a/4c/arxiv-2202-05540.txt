Necessary and sufficient conditions for identifiability in the admixture model
Jan van Waaij

Abstract
We consider M M SNP data from N N individuals who are an admixture of K K unknown ancient populations. Let Π s ​ i \Pi_{si} be the frequency of the reference allele of individual i i at SNP s s . So the number of reference alleles at SNP s s for a diploid individual is distributed as binomial ⁡ ( 2 , Π s ​ i ) \binomial(2,\Pi_{si}) . We suppose Π s ​ i = ∑ k = 1 K F s ​ k ​ Q k ​ i \Pi_{si}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}F_{sk}Q_{ki} , where F s ​ k F_{sk} is the allele frequency of SNP s s in population k k and Q k ​ i Q_{ki} is the proportion of population k k in the ancestry of individual i i . I am interested in the identifiability of F F and Q Q , up to a relabelling of the ancient populations. Under what conditions, when Π = F 1 ​ Q 1 = F 2 ​ Q 2 \Pi=F^{1}Q^{1}=F^{2}Q^{2} are F 1 F^{1} and F 2 F^{2} and Q 1 Q^{1} and Q 2 Q^{2} equal? I show that the anchor condition ( Cabreros & Storey 2019 ) on one matrix together with an independence condition on the other matrix is sufficient for identifiability. I will argue that the proof of the necessary condition in Cabreros & Storey 2019 is incorrect, and I will provide a correct proof, which in addition does not require knowledge of the number of ancestral populations. I will also provide abstract necessary and sufficient conditions for identifiability. I will show that one cannot deviate substantially from the anchor condition without losing identifiability. Finally, I show necessary and sufficient conditions for identifiability for the non-admixed case.

Introduction

We consider the following model (see for instance
Cabreros & Storey 2019
;
Garcia-Erill & Albrechtsen 2020
). There are
N
individuals,
M
diallelic sites and
K
ancestral populations. Let
Q
be the
K\x N
matrix of of admixture proportions, so fraction
Q_{ki}
of individual
i
’s genome comes from population
k
. Note that the
i
-th column of
Q
,

, is a probability vector,
i\in\left\{1,\ldots,N\right\}
. That is,
Q_{ki}\geq 0
for all
k\in\left\{1,\ldots,K\right\}
and
i\in\left\{1,\ldots,N\right\}
and
\sum_{k=1}^{K}Q_{ki}=1
, for all
i\in\left\{1,\ldots,N\right\}
. Let
F
be a real
M\x K
matrix of ancestral SNP frequencies. So
F_{sk}\in[0,1]
, for all

and

.
So fraction
F_{sk}
of ancestral population

\Pi_{si}=(FQ)_{si}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}F_{sk}Q_{ki}.

The observed genotype

of a (diploid) individual is the number (0,1, or 2) of reference variants at SNP

G_{si}\mid\Pi_{si}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{independent}}}{{\sim}}\binomial(2,\Pi_{si}).

(1)

It follows that
\Pi
is identifiable, and the law of

\Pi

(F^{1},Q^{1}),(F^{2},Q^{2})
so that
\Pi=F^{1}Q^{1}=F^{2}Q^{2}
, the data cannot decide between
(F^{1},Q^{1})
or
(F^{2},Q^{2})
. In this case, we cannot say how much individual

inherits from ancient population
k
. Is it
Q_{ki}^{1}
or
Q_{ki}^{2}
?

Of course, identifiability is not the same as consistency (i.e. if one has estimators
\widehat{F},\widehat{Q}
, do they converge to the true

Q
). But consistency requires identifiability. It is therefore important to work with models that are identifiable.

As far as I know
Cabreros & Storey 2019
is the only study that considers identifiability for the admixture model. They introduce the so-called “anchor condition” on either
F
or
Q
, as a sufficient condition for identifiability. They require linearly independent columns for
F
and linearly independent rows for
Q
, respectively. The anchor condition on
F
means, that for every
k\in\left\{1,\ldots,K\right\}
there is a row
s
, so that
F_{s\ell}=0
when
\ell\neq k
, and
F_{sk}>0
. The anchor condition on
Q
means, that for every
k\in\left\{1,\ldots,K\right\}
there is a column
i
so that
Q_{\ell i}=0
when
\ell\neq k
, and
Q_{ki}=1
, because the columns of
Q
sum to one.

So if there is an anchor for
k
at SNP
s
(so
F_{sk}>0
and
F_{s\ell}=0
when
\ell\neq k
), then if an individual has the reference allele at SNP
s
, then it inherits for sure from population
k
. If an individual
i
is an anchor for
k
(so
Q_{ki}=1
and
Q_{\ell i}=0
for
\ell\neq k
), then it stores pure information from ancient population
k
. See also
Cabreros & Storey 2019
, page 1014
. I refer to
Arora et al. 2013
, page 2
for a discussion of the anchor condition in topic modelling.

The anchor condition first appeared in
Donoho & Stodden 2004
in the context of non-negative matrix decomposition. Under the anchor condition they provide an algorithm to calculate the matrix decomposition of a non-negative matrix in two non-negative matrices of a given rank. The authors use the term separability condition instead of anchor condition. The anchor condition was first used in the context of topic modelling in
Arora et al. 2012
, who also introduce this term, and in the context of admixture models by
Cabreros & Storey 2019
.

I will argue that the proof of identifiability with the anchor condition in
Cabreros & Storey 2019
is not correct. In this study I treat identifiability in a mathematical rigorous way. I will give a correct proof for identifiability with the “anchor” condition (explained below) of
Cabreros & Storey 2019
and I will discuss abstract necessary and sufficient conditions. In contrast to
Cabreros & Storey 2019
I allow the number of ancient populations to be unknown.

Identifiability

Because

and
F^{2}

As for any permutation

Let

The matrices

Let

and

and the columns of

.

We define an equivalence relation on the pairs

. We say that
(F^{1},Q^{1})
is equivalent to
(F^{2},Q^{2})
when

and
F_{2}
have the same number of columns

(which is then the number of rows of

and
Q^{2}
) and there is a permutation

and the rows of

,

. In this case

.
When

and

Definition 1
.

A model subset
\sM\subseteq\bigcup_{K=1}^{\infty}\sF_{K}\x\sQ_{K}

if for every

.

Let
\sQ_{K}^{\text{an}}
be the subset of
\sQ_{K}
of all matrices

there is an

, so that

for all

. Note that
\sQ_{K}^{\text{an}}
is empty when

. We say that matrices

satisfy the anchor condition on

, for some

Let
\sF_{K}^{\text{an}}\subseteq\sF_{K}
be the set of matrices so that for each
k\in\left\{1,\ldots,K\right\}
, there is an
s\in\left\{1,\ldots,M\right\}
so that for all

and

. Note that
\sF_{K}^{\text{an}}
is empty when

. We say that matrices

. A SNP

so that

, for some
\delta>0
, is called an anchor SNP.

Let us determine independence conditions on the matrices. Let
\sF_{K}^{\text{in}}
be the subset of
\sF_{K}
of all matrices

so that
F_{\star,1}-F_{\star,K},\ldots,F_{\star,K-1}-F_{\star,K}
are linearly independent vectors. Note that
\sF_{K}^{\text{in}}
is empty when

.
Let
\sQ_{K}^{\text{in}}
be the subset of
\sQ_{K}
of all matrices
Q\in\sQ_{K}
so that the rows of

are linearly independent. Note that
\sQ_{K}^{\text{in}}
is empty when
K>N
.
The independence condition on the columns of

is slightly lighter than the independence condition on the rows of
Q
. This is because we use that the other matrix in the pair has rows that sum to one. This basically reduces the dimension by one.

2.1
The argument of
Cabreros & Storey 2019

Let
\widetilde{\sF}_{K}^{\text{in}}
be all
F\in\sF_{K}
so that all columns of
F
are linearly independent (i.e. all rank
K
matrices in
\sF_{K}
). Note that
\widetilde{\sF}_{K}^{\text{in}}\subseteq\sF_{K}^{\text{in}}
.

Cabreros & Storey 2019
assure that for given (known)

,
\sF_{K}^{\text{an}}\x\sQ_{K}^{\text{id}}
and
\widetilde{\sF}_{K}^{\text{id}}\x\sQ_{K}^{\text{an}}
are identifiable models. They “proof” identifiability for the anchor condition on

is similar.

The “proof” of
Cabreros & Storey 2019
is as follows. First they suppose that for
(F,Q)
in the model, when
\Pi=FQ
you may assume that

has the form

F=\begin{pmatrix}D\\
A\end{pmatrix},

(2)

where

is a diagonal matrix with positive entries and
A
are the
M-K
bottom rows of
F
. Next they argue that when
\Pi^{\prime}
is the submatrix of
\Pi
formed from the first

\Pi
, then

. Finally, the fact that the rows of

are linearly independent, uniquely identifies

.

Although this argument sounds convincing, it is wrong. We have to show that when
(F^{1},Q^{1}),(F^{2},Q^{2})
are in the model, so that
\Pi=F^{1}Q^{1}=F^{2}Q^{2}
that
(F^{1},Q^{1})\sim(F^{2},Q^{2})
. To write

in the form

2
, one has to permute the rows of

, and hence also the rows of
\Pi
. However, if after a permutation of the rows of

,

is of the form

2
, then under the same permutation

is not necessarily of this form, let alone that
D^{1}=D^{2}
(where
D^{1}
and

are defined similarly as

).
At the very least, this should be proven.
Doing a different permutation of the rows of

and

would also not work, as after the transformation, the resulting

do not establish identifiability.

Although
\sF_{K}^{\text{an}}\x\sQ_{K}^{\text{id}}
and
\widetilde{\sF}_{K}^{\text{id}}\x\sQ_{K}^{\text{an}}
are identifiable models (according to
theorems

4
and
2
below),
Cabreros & Storey 2019
’s argument for identifiability is not correct.

2.2
Sufficient conditions for identifiability

In this section I give a correct proofs for identifiability with an anchor condition on one matrix and an independence matrix on the other matrix. I will allow for a slightly weaker condition on the independence of the columns of
F
compared with
Cabreros & Storey 2019
. Futhermore, I don’t require knowledge of
K
. In
theorems

4
and
2
I show that
\bigcup_{K=1}^{\infty}\sF_{K}^{\text{in}}\x\sQ_{K}^{\text{an}}=\bigcup_{K=1}^{(M+1)\wedge N}\sF_{K}^{\text{in}}\x\sQ_{K}^{\text{an}}
and
\bigcup_{K=1}^{\infty}\sF_{K}^{\text{an}}\x\sQ_{K}^{\text{in}}=\bigcup_{K=1}^{M\wedge N}\sF_{K}^{\text{an}}\x\sQ_{K}^{\text{in}}
are identifiable models. In
theorems

5
and
3
I show that the independence requirements are necessary. In the same theorems I show that we cannot deviate from the anchor condition too much without loosing identifiability. Necessary and sufficient conditions are discussed in
section

3

Although the proofs of
theorems

2

The reader may familiarise him- or herself with the theory of convex sets in

Let

Q_{\star i}^{1}=e_{k}
and
Q_{\star i}^{2}=e_{\pi(k)}
. So
(F^{1},Q^{1})\sim(F^{2},Q^{2})
.
∎

The following theorem shows that under the non-admixability assumption, the sets
\sF^{\text{d}}_{K}
and
\sQ^{\text{ua}}_{K}
cannot be enlarged so that their product space is still an identifiable model.

Theorem 10
.

Let
K\geq 2
and
N>K
. Let
F\in\sF_{K}
be such so that there are (at least) two identical columns. Then there are
Q^{1},Q^{2}\in\sQ_{K}^{\text{ua}}
so that
FQ^{1}=FQ^{2}
, but
(F,Q^{1})\not\sim(F,Q^{2})
.

On the other hand, let
K\geq 2
and
N\geq 1
, and let
Q\in\sQ_{K}\backslash\sQ_{K}^{\text{ua}}
be so that every column of
Q
is of the form
e_{i}
, but for some
k
,
e_{k}
is not a column of
Q
. Then for every
F\in\sF^{\text{ua}}_{K}
there is a
F^{2}\in\sF^{\text{ua}}_{K}
so that
FQ=F^{2}Q
, but
(F,Q)\not\sim(F^{2},Q)
.

Proof.

Let
k,\ell\in\left\{1,\ldots,K\right\},k\neq\ell
so that the
k
th and
\ell
th column of
F
are identical. Consider

\displaystyle Q^{1}=

\displaystyle(I,e_{k},\ldots,e_{k}),

\displaystyle Q^{2}=

\displaystyle(I,e_{\ell},\ldots,e_{\ell}),

then
FQ^{1}=FQ^{2}
, but
(F,Q^{1})\not\sim(F,Q^{2})
.

For the second statement, let
F^{2}\in\sF_{K}^{\text{d}}
be any matrix so that
F^{2}_{\star\ell}=F_{\star\ell}
for all
\ell\neq k
, and
F^{2}_{\star k}
is a column different to all columns in
F
. Then
FQ=F^{2}Q
, as
e_{k}
is not a column in
Q
, but
(F,Q)\not\sim(F^{2},Q)
.
∎

So the unadmixed case is easy: provided
N>K
, our sufficient conditions are also necessary. So we see here again the phenomenon that shrinking the sets from
\sQ_{K}^{\text{in}}
to
\sQ_{K}^{\text{an}}
to
\sQ_{K}^{\text{ua}}
allows us to enlarge the sets of allowable
F\in\sF_{K}
, from
\sF_{K}^{\text{an}}
to
\sF_{K}^{\text{in}}
to
\sF_{K}^{\text{d}}
.

Appendix A
Convex sets

This section develops the theory on convex sets required for the proofs.
A convex set is a subset of a real linear space so that for every
x,y\in C
and
\lambda\in[0,1]
,
\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y\in C
.
Let
S
be a non-empty set of a real vector space. Then we define the convex set
generated
by
S
as

\co(S)=\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{N}\lambda_{k}x_{k}:N\in\NN,x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\in S,\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{N}\geq 0,\sum_{k=1}^{N}\lambda_{k}=1\right\}.

(3)

A.1
Unique decompositions

In this subsection I am interested under what conditions the elements of
\co(S)
have a unique convex decomposition in terms of the elements of
S
. So when
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{K},\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{K}\geq 0
and
\sum_{k=1}^{K}\lambda_{k}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mu_{k}=1
, and
\sum_{k=1}\lambda_{k}x_{k}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mu_{k}x_{k}
,
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\in S
, is then also
\lambda_{i}=\mu_{i}
for all
i
? I’ll develop precise necessary and sufficient conditions for this.

Definition 11
.

Let
C
be the convex hull of
v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}
. An element
v\in C
has a unique convex combination of elements
v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}
when
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{m},\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{m}\geq 0,\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mu_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}=1
and
v=\lambda_{1}v_{1}+\ldots+\lambda_{m}v_{m}=\mu_{1}v_{1}+\ldots+\mu_{m}v_{m}
impies
\lambda_{i}=\mu_{i}
, for all
i=1,\ldots,m
.

Lemma 12
.

Let
V
be a real vector space. Let
k\in\NN
. Let
v_{1},\ldots,v_{k+1}\in V
and let
C
be the convex hull of
v_{1},\ldots,v_{k+1}
.
Then each element of
C
has a unique convex combination of elements of
v_{1},\ldots,v_{k+1}
if and only if
v_{1}-v_{k+1},\ldots,v_{k}-v_{k+1}
are linearly independent.

Proof.

First we prove that when
v_{1}-v_{k+1},\ldots,v_{k}-v_{k+1}
are linearly independent, that each element of
C
has a unique convex combination of elements
v_{1},\ldots,v_{k+1}
.

Let
v\in C
and let
v=\lambda_{1}v_{1}+\ldots+\lambda_{k+1}v_{k+1}=\mu_{1}v_{1}+\ldots+\mu_{k+1}v_{k+1}
be convex combinations of
v
. Then, making use of the fact that
\lambda_{1}+\ldots+\lambda_{k+1}=\mu_{1}+\ldots+\mu_{k+1}=1
,
v-v_{k+1}=\lambda_{1}(v_{1}-v_{k+1})+\ldots+\lambda_{k+1}(v_{k+1}-v_{k+1})=\mu_{1}(v_{1}-v_{k+1})+\ldots+\mu_{k+1}(v_{k+1}-v_{k+1})
,
so

\lambda_{1}(v_{1}-v_{k+1})+\ldots+\lambda_{k}(v_{k}-v_{k+1})=\mu_{1}(v_{1}-v_{k+1})+\ldots+\mu_{k}(v_{k}-v_{k+1}).

It follows from the fact that
v_{1}-v_{k+1},\ldots,v_{k}-v_{k+1}
are linearly independent, that
\lambda_{i}=\mu_{i}
for all
i=1,\ldots,k
. Finally,
\lambda_{k+1}=1-\lambda_{1}-\ldots-\lambda_{k}=1-\mu_{1}-\ldots-\mu_{k}=\mu_{k+1}
. So
v
has a unique convex combination.

For the proof in the other direction, suppose
v_{1}-v_{k+1},\ldots,v_{k}-v_{k+1}
are not linearly independent. We will show, that there is an element in the convex hull of
v_{1},\ldots,v_{k+1}
that does not have a unique convex combination.

From the linear dependence of
v_{1}-v_{k+1},\ldots,v_{k}-v_{k+1}
follows that there are
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k}
, not all zero, so that
\alpha_{1}(v_{1}-v_{k+1})+\ldots+\alpha_{k}(v_{k}-v_{k+1})=0
.
Let
I=\left\{i:\alpha_{i}>0\right\}
and
J=\left\{i:\alpha_{i}\leq 0\right\}
. So

\sum_{i\in I}\alpha_{i}(v_{i}-v_{k+1})=\sum_{i\in J}-\alpha_{i}(v_{i}-v_{k+1}).

As at least one
\alpha_{i}\neq 0
,
i\in\left\{1,\ldots,k\right\}
, at least one of
\sum_{i\in I}\alpha_{i}
or
\sum_{i\in J}-\alpha_{i}
is positive, and both are non-negative. Let
M=\max\left\{\sum_{i\in I}\alpha_{i},\sum_{i\in J}-\alpha_{i}\right\}>0
. Let
\beta=M-\sum_{i\in I}\alpha_{i}
and
\gamma=M-\sum_{i\in J}-\alpha_{i}
. Note that
\beta,\gamma\geq 0
, and that

\beta+\sum_{i\in I}\alpha_{i}=\gamma+\sum_{i\in J}-\alpha_{i}=M
. As
v_{k+1}-v_{k+1}=0
, we have

\frac{\beta}{M}(v_{k+1}-v_{k+1})+\sum_{i\in I}\frac{\alpha_{i}}{M}(v_{i}-v_{k+1})=\frac{\gamma}{M}(v_{k+1}-v_{k+1})+\sum_{i\in J}\frac{-\alpha_{i}}{M}(v_{i}-v_{k+1}).

Using that

\frac{\beta}{M}+\sum_{i\in I}\frac{\alpha_{i}}{M}=\frac{\gamma}{M}+\sum_{i\in J}\frac{-\alpha_{i}}{M}=1
, adding
v_{k+1}
on both sides gives

\frac{\beta}{M}v_{k+1}+\sum_{i\in I}\frac{\alpha_{i}}{M}v_{i}=\frac{\gamma}{M}v_{k+1}+\sum_{i\in J}\frac{-\alpha_{i}}{M}v_{i}.

As
I
and
J
are disjoint, and at least one of
\alpha_{i}\neq 0
, it follows that this are two different convex combinations of
v_{1},\ldots,v_{k+1}
of the same element
\frac{\beta}{M}v_{k+1}+\sum_{i\in I}\frac{\alpha_{i}}{M}v_{i}
.
∎

Lemma 13
.

Let
V
be a vector space, and
C
the convex hull of
v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}\in V
. When
v\in C
has two different convex combinations of
v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}
, then
v
has infinitely many convex combinations of
v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}
.

Proof.

Suppose

v=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}v_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mu_{i}v_{i}

are two different convex combinations of
v
. So for some
i_{0}\in\left\{1,\ldots,m\right\}
,
\lambda_{i_{0}}\neq\mu_{i_{0}}
. Let
\alpha\in[0,1]
. Note that

v=\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\alpha\lambda_{i}+(1-\alpha)\mu_{i})v_{i}=:\sum_{i=1}^{m}\nu_{i}(\alpha)v_{i},

is also a convex combination of
v
. When
\alpha_{1}\neq\alpha_{2}
,
\nu_{i_{0}}(\alpha_{1})-\nu_{i_{0}}(\alpha_{2})=(\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2})(\lambda_{i_{0}}-\mu_{i_{0}})\neq 0
. Hence there are infinitely many convex combinations of
v
.
∎

Definition 14
.

Let
C
be a convex set. A convex combination

v=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}v_{i}

is open when for all
i\in\left\{1,\ldots,m\right\}
,
\lambda_{i}>0
.

Definition 15
.

Let
V
be a real vector space and let
v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}\in V
. We define the open convex set generated by
v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}
to be the set of all open convex combinations of
v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}
.

Note that when
V=\re^{n}
and
m>2
, then the open convex set is also open in the topological sense of the word. This is not the case when
m=1
.

Remark 16
.

To distinguish between the open convex hull and the “usual” convex hull, we call the later sometimes the “closed convex hull”.

Lemma 17
.

Let
V
be a real vector space and let
C^{\circ}
be the open convex set generated by
v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}\in V
. Let
C
be the convex set generated by
v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}
. Then
C^{\circ}
is convex and
\emptyset\neq C^{\circ}\subseteq C
.

Proof.

It is obvious that
C^{\circ}
is contained in the convex set generated by
v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}
. We have that
(1/m)v_{1}+\ldots+(1/m)v_{m}\in C^{\circ}
, so
C^{\circ}
is not empty.

Let
v=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}v_{i},w=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mu_{i}v_{i}\in C^{\circ}
,
\lambda_{i},\mu_{i}>0
for all
i
. Let
\alpha\in[0,1]
. Then

\alpha v+(1-\alpha)w=\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\alpha\lambda_{i}+(1-\alpha)\mu_{i})v_{i}.

Note that
\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\alpha\lambda_{i}+(1-\alpha)\mu_{i})=1
, and
\alpha\lambda_{i}+(1-\alpha)\mu_{i}>0
, for all
i\in\left\{1,\ldots,m\right\}
. Hence
\alpha v+(1-\alpha)w\in C^{\circ}
. So
C^{\circ}
is convex.
∎

Lemma 18
.

Let
V
be a vector space and let
C^{\circ}
(resp.
C
) be the open (resp. closed) convex set generated by
v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}\in V
.
The following statements are equivalent:

(i)

There is an element
v\in C
that does not have a unique convex combination of
v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}
.

(ii)

Every element of
v\in C^{\circ}
does not have a unique convex combination of
v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}
.

(iii)

For every element
v\in C^{\circ}
there are infinitely many convex combinations of
v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}
.

Proof.

Obviously, (iii)
\implies
(ii). As
C^{\circ}
is not empty and contained in
C
(
lemma

17
), (ii)
\implies
(i). The implication (ii)
\implies
(iii) follows from
lemma

13
. We are only left to prove (i)
\implies
(ii). Let
v\in C
be an element so that

v=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}v_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mu_{i}v_{i}

are two different convex combinations of
v
.
Note that

0=\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\lambda_{i}-\mu_{i})v_{i}.

Let
w\in C^{\circ}
have an open convex combination

w=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\nu_{i}v_{i}.

Let
\alpha=\min_{i}\nu_{i}>0
. As
\lambda_{i}-\mu_{i}\geq-1
,
\nu_{i}+\alpha(\lambda_{i}-\mu_{i})\geq 0
, for all
i
, and
\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\nu_{i}+\alpha(\lambda_{i}-\mu_{i}))=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\nu_{i}+\alpha\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\lambda_{i}-\mu_{i})=1+0=1
.
So

w=\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\nu_{i}+\alpha(\lambda_{i}-\mu_{i}))v_{i}.

is another convex combination of
w
, because for at least one
i\in\left\{1,\ldots,m\right\},\lambda_{i}\neq\mu_{i}
and
\alpha>0
.
∎

A.2
Convex hulls

In this subsection, I am interested in the smallest subsets
S
of a convex set
C
, so that
S
generates
C
(in formula’s
C=\co(S)
). It turns out that not every convex set
C
has a smallest set
S
that generates
C
, and even in cases where it happens, there is not always a unique decomposition in terms of the elements of
S
.

Definition 19
.

Let
S
be a subset of a vector space, and let
C
be the convex space generated by
S
. We call
S
minimal, when for every
x\in S
,
C\neq\co(S\backslash\left\{x\right\})
.

Not every convex set has a minimal generating set.

Example 20
.

Consider the real numbers
\re
, which is a convex set, and let
S\subseteq\re
be a set that generates
\re
.
First note that
S
is infinite, as otherwise
r=\max_{x\in S}|x|<\infty
and
\co(S)\subseteq[-r,r]\neq\re
. It follows that there are
x,y,z\in S
so that
x<y<z
. Note that
y
is a convex combination of
x
and
z
, so
S\backslash\left\{y\right\}
also generates
\re
. So
S
is not minimal. We chose
S
arbitrary, so
\re
has no minimal generating set.

However, if
C
is generated by a finite set
S
, then there exists a minimal set.

Lemma 21
.

Let
S
be a finite non-empty subset of a real vector space. Let
C=\co(S)
. Then
C
has a minimal set.

Proof.

Define
S_{0}=S
and until
S_{i}
is minimal, set
S_{i+1}=S_{i}\backslash\left\{x\right\}
, where
x\in S_{i}
is an element so that
C=\co(S_{i})=\co(S_{i}\backslash\left\{x\right\})
, which exist when
S_{i}
is not minimal. As
S_{0}
is finite, and
S_{i+1}
has one element less than
S_{i}
, this algorithm is destined to terminate after
i_{0}\geq 0
steps. Note that
S_{i_{0}}
is not empty and
C=\co(S_{i_{0}})
and
S_{i_{0}}
is minimal.
∎

Definition 22
.

Let
C
be convex and
x\in C
. We call
x
extreme, when there are no
y,z\in C
,
y\neq z
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
so that
x=\alpha y+(1-\alpha)z
.

Example 23
.

In
C=\left\{(x,y)\in\re^{2}:0\leq x\leq 1,0\leq y\leq 1\right\}
,
(0,0),(0,1),(1,0)
and
(1,1)
are extreme points.

Lemma 24
.

Let
C
be a convex set generated by a minimal set
S
. Then
S
is the set of all extrema of
C
.

Proof.

For an extremum
x\in C
, there are no
y,z\in C,y\neq z
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
so that
x=\alpha y+(1-\alpha)z
. So
\co(S\backslash\left\{x\right\})
does not contain
x
. Hence
x\in S
.

Suppose
x\in S
is not extreme. Then there are
y,z\in C,x\neq y
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
so that
x=\alpha y+(1-\alpha)z
. Then there are mutually different elements
x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}\in S
,
m\geq 1
, so that
y,z
are convex combinations

y=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\beta_{i}x_{i},\en z=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\gamma_{i}x_{i}.

Note that we can choose this
x_{i}
so that at least one of
\beta_{i}
or
\gamma_{i}
is positive, for every
i\in\left\{1,\ldots,m\right\}
.
So

x=\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\alpha\beta_{i}+(1-\alpha)\gamma_{i})x_{i}.

If all
x_{i}\neq x
, then
x
is a convex combination of other elements of
S
, and so
C=\co(S\backslash\left\{x\right\})
, so
S
is not minimal. Contradiction. So
x
is equal to some
x_{i}
.
After relabelling, if necessary, we may assume
x=x_{1}
. As
y\neq z
, either
\beta_{1}<1
or
\gamma_{1}<1
, or both. In particular
m\geq 2
. We already assumed that
\beta_{1}>0
or
\gamma_{1}>0
. So
0<\alpha\beta_{1}+(1-\alpha)\gamma_{1}<1
. So

(1-(\alpha\beta_{1}+(1-\alpha)\gamma_{1}))x=\sum_{i=2}^{m}(\alpha\beta_{i}+(1-\alpha)\gamma_{i})x_{i}.

Note that
\sum_{i=2}^{m}(\alpha\beta_{i}+(1-\alpha)\gamma_{i})=1-(\alpha\beta_{1}+(1-\alpha)\gamma_{1})
, so

x=\sum_{i=2}^{m}\frac{\alpha\beta_{i}+(1-\alpha)\gamma_{i}}{1-(\alpha\beta_{1}+(1-\alpha)\gamma_{1})}x_{i},

is a convex combination of elements from
S\backslash\left\{x\right\}
. So
C=\co(S\backslash\left\{x\right\})
, so
S
is not minimal. Contradiction. As this were all posibilities, it follows that all elements of
S
are extreme.
∎

However, a set of extreme points does not necessarily generate the convex set.

Example 25
.

Consider

C=\left\{(x,y)\in\re^{2}:x^{2}+y^{2}\leq 1,\text{ when }x,y\geq 0,\text{ otherwise }x^{2}+y^{2}<1\right\}.

Then
C
is convex, and the set of extreme points is

E=\left\{(x,y):x^{2}+y^{2}=1,x\geq 0,y\geq 0\right\}.

But
C
is not generated by
E
.

A corollary to
lemma

24
is

Corollary 26
.

A convex set
C
has at most one minimal set.

Proof.

If
C
has a minimal set
S
, then
S
is the set of extrema. So
S
is uniquely determined.
∎

So when a minimal set exists, it is unique, which allows us to speak about the minimal set.

Lemma 27
.

When
C
is a convex set generated by its set
E
of extrema, then
E
is the minimal set.

Proof.

Suppose
E
is not minimal, then there is an
x\in E
so that
C
is generated by
E\backslash\left\{x\right\}
. So there are
x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}\in E\backslash\left\{x\right\}
and
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{m}>0
,
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}=1
so that
x=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}x_{i}
. But then
x
is not extreme. Contradiction.
∎

A corollary to
lemmas

24
and
27
is

Corollary 28
.

Let
C
be a convex set generated by
S\subseteq C
. Then
S
is minimal if and only if
S
is the set of all extreme points.

As a corollary to
lemmas

21
and
26
we have that every finitely generated convex set has a unique minimal set.

Corollary 29
.

Let
S
be a finite non-empty subset of a real linear space. Then
\co(S)
has a unique minimal set.

However, not every element in a convex set
C
generated by a minimum set
S
has a unique decomposition in elements in
S
.

Example 30
.

Take for instance
C=\left\{(x,y)\in\re^{2}:x^{2}+y^{2}\leq 1\right\}
, which has minimal set
S=\left\{(x,y)\in\re^{2}:x^{2}+y^{2}=1\right\}
. Then

(0,0)=\frac{1}{2}(-1,0)+\frac{1}{2}(1,0)\en(0,0)=\frac{1}{2}(0,-1)+\frac{1}{2}(0,1).

However, if
C
is generated by
S=\left\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}\right\}
and every element in
C
has a unique decomposition in terms of elements of
S
, then
S
is minimal:

Lemma 31
.

Let
C
be a convex set generated by
S=\left\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}\right\}
. If every element in
C
has a unique decomposition in terms of
S
, then
S
is a minimal set.

Proof.

Let
x_{j}\in S
. Suppose there are
y,z\in C
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
so that

x_{j}=\alpha y+(1-\alpha)z.

Then
y
and
z
have convex decompositions

y=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\beta_{i}x_{i},\en z=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\gamma_{i}x_{i}.

So

x_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\alpha\beta_{i}+(1-\alpha)\gamma_{i})x_{i},

is a convex decomposition of
x_{j}
in terms of
x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}
.
As the convex decompositions are unique,
\beta_{i}=\gamma_{i}=0
for all
i\neq j
, and
\beta_{j}=\gamma_{j}=1
, so
y=z
, so
x_{j}
is extreme. So by
corollary

28

S
is a minimal set.
∎

A corollary to
lemmas

12
and
31
is

Corollary 32
.

Let
v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}
be vectors in a vectors space and let
C
be the convex space generated by
v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}
. When
v_{1}-v_{k},\ldots,v_{k-1}-v_{k}
are linearly independent, then
v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}
are extreme points of
C
and
\left\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}\right\}
is a minimal set.

Appendix B
Cones

In this section, I develop a theory similar for cones as I did for the convex spaces. A cone is subset
K
of a real vector space so that for all
x,y\in K
, and
\alpha\geq 0
, also
\alpha x
and
x+y
are in
K
and if
-K=\left\{-x:x\in K\right\}
, then
K\cap(-K)=\left\{0\right\}
. The cone generated by a set
S
is the set

\cone(S)=\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{N}\alpha_{k}x_{k}:N\in\NN,\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{N}\geq 0\right\},

provided
\cone(S)\cap(-\cone(S))=\left\{0\right\}
. I am again interested when every element of
\cone(S)
can be written as a unique decomposition

\sum_{k=1}^{N}\alpha_{k}x_{k}

of elements
x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\in S
. I am also interested in smallest subsets
S\subseteq K
that generate
K
.

The following definition is taken from
Kalauch & van 2018
, Definition 1.1.1
.

Definition 33
.

A wedge
K
is a subset of a vector space so that when
x,y\in K
then also
x+y\in K
and if
\alpha\geq 0
then also
\alpha x\in K
. If, additionally,
-K=\left\{-x:x\in K\right\}
, and
K
satisfies
K\cap(-K)=\left\{0\right\}
, then we call
K
a cone.

Definition 34
.

Let
A
be a subset of a vector space. We define the wedge generated by
A
as the set

\displaystyle\we(A)=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}x_{i}:n\in\NN,\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}\geq 0,x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in A\right\}.

Note that
A
is a wedge. If additionally
\we(A)
is a cone, then we say that
\cone(A):=\we(A)
is the cone generated by
A
.

Note that the wedge generated by the empty set is
\left\{0\right\}
.

Definition 35
.

Let
A
be a subset of a real vector space. Let
x\in\we(A)
. Then
x
has a unique decomposition in terms of
A
, if for mutually different elements
x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}\in A
, and arbitrary
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n},\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n}\geq 0
,

\displaystyle x=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}x_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\beta_{i}x_{i},

implies
\alpha_{i}=\beta_{i}
, for all
i\in\left\{1,\ldots,n\right\}
.

Lemma 36
.

Let
A
be a subset of a real linear space. Then the following are equivalent:

1.

0\in\we(A)
does not have a unique decomposition,

2.

every element in
\we(A)
does not have a unique decomposition.

Proof.

Obviously, 2 implies 1. Now assume 1. Then there is an
N\in\NN
and there are positive scalars
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{N}
and
x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\in A
so that

0=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\lambda_{k}x_{k}.

Let
x\in\we(A)
. Then there are
M\in\NN
and
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{M}
,
y_{1},\ldots,y_{M}\in A
so that

x=\sum_{k=1}^{M}\mu_{k}y_{k}.

Then

x=x+0=\sum_{k=1}^{M}\mu_{k}y_{k}+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\lambda_{k}x_{k}

is another representation of
x
.
∎

Lemma 37
.

If
0\in\we(A)
has a uniquely decomposition in terms of
A
, then
\we(A)
is a cone.

Proof.

Let
x\in\we(A)\cap(-\we(A))
. So
x,-x\in\we(A)
. Let

\displaystyle x=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}x_{i},

\displaystyle-x=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\beta_{i}x_{i},

\displaystyle\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n},\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n}\geq 0,\quad x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in A

be decompositions of
x
and
-x
.
It follows that

\displaystyle 0=x+-x=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\alpha_{i}+\beta_{i})x_{i}

is the unique decomposition of 0. So
\alpha_{i}+\beta_{i}=0
for all
i\in\left\{1,\ldots,n\right\}
. So
\alpha_{i}=\beta_{i}=0
for all
i\in\left\{1,\ldots,n\right\}
. So
x=0
. So
\we(A)
is a cone.
∎

Lemma 38
.

Let
A
be a subset of a real vector space. Then every element in
\we(A)
has a unique decomposition in terms of
A
if and only if all elements in
A
are linearly independent.

Proof.

Suppose the elements in
A
are linearly independent, then every element in
\we(A)
has a unique decomposition.
Suppose
A
is not linearly independent. So there are elements
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}\in\re\backslash\left\{0\right\}
,
n\geq 1
, and
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in A
so that

\displaystyle 0=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}x_{i}.

Define
I=\left\{i:\lambda_{i}>0\right\}
and
J=\left\{i:\lambda_{i}<0\right\}
. At least one of
I
or
J
is not empty, so

\displaystyle x:=\sum_{i\in I}\lambda_{i}x_{i}=\sum_{i\in J}(-\lambda_{i})x_{i}

in
\we(A)
has no unique decomposition.
∎

So when every element in
\we(A)
has a unique decomposition in terms of
A
, then
\we(A)=\cone(A)
.

Definition 39
.

Let
K
be a wedge and let
x\in K\backslash\left\{0\right\}
. We call
x
an extreme point when for
y,z\in K\backslash\left\{0\right\}
,
x=y+z
implies
y=\alpha x,z=\beta x
for some
\alpha,\beta>0
.

Remark 40
.

When
x
is an extreme point, then for every
\alpha>0
,
\alpha x
is also an extreme point.

Definition 41
.

Let
A
be a subset of a real linear space. We call
A
minimal, when for every
x\in A
,
\we(A\backslash\left\{x\right\})\neq\we(A)
.

Every wedge that is generated by a finite set has a minimum set.

Lemma 42
.

Let
K
be a wedge generated by a finite set
A
. Then
K
has a minimal set.

Proof.

Let
A_{0}=A
. Inductively, for
i\geq 0
, if
A_{i}
is not minimal, then
A_{i}
is not empty, and there is an
x\in A_{i}
so that
\we(A_{i}\backslash\left\{x\right\})=\we(A_{i})
. In this case define
A_{i+1}
. If
A_{i}
is minimal, set
i_{0}=i
and terminate the procedure. As
A_{i+1}
has one element less than
A_{i}
this algorithm eventually terminates after
i_{0}\geq 0
finite steps and
\we(A_{i_{0}})=K
and
A_{i_{0}}
is minimal.
∎

Lemma 43
.

Let
A
be a minimal set that generates a cone, then every element of
A
is an extremum.

If
A
is an nonempty subset of a real linear space, then for every extreme element
x\in\we(A)
, there is some
\alpha>0
, so that
\alpha x\in A
.

Proof.

Let
A
be a minimal set. Note that for all
x,y\in A
,
x\neq y
:
x
is nonzero and, for every
\alpha>0
,
y\neq\alpha x
, as otherwise
A
is not minimal.

Let
x\in A
and suppose that for some
y,z\in\cone(A)
,
x=y+z
. Then there are
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n},\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n}\geq 0
, with
\alpha_{i}+\beta_{i}>0
, and
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in A
,
n\geq 1
, so that

\displaystyle y=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}x_{i},

\displaystyle z=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\beta_{i}x_{i},

so

\displaystyle x=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\alpha_{i}+\beta_{i})x_{i}.

If
n=1
, then
\alpha_{1}+\beta_{1}=1
, and
x
is extreme. Suppose
n\geq 2
.
If for all
i,
and for all
\alpha>0
,
x_{i}\neq\alpha x
, then
x\in\we(A\backslash\left\{x\right\})
, so
A
is not minimal. Contradiction.
So, for some
i
,
x_{i}=\gamma x
for certain
\gamma>0
. After relabelling, if necessary, we may assume
i=1
. So

\displaystyle(1-(\alpha_{1}+\beta_{1})\gamma)x=\sum_{i=2}^{n}(\alpha_{i}+\beta_{i})x_{i}.

If
(1-(\alpha_{1}+\beta_{1})\gamma)>0
, then

\displaystyle x=\sum_{i=2}^{n}\frac{\alpha_{i}+\beta_{i}}{1-(\alpha_{1}+\beta_{1})\gamma}x_{i}.

and
\we(A)=\we(A\backslash\left\{x\right\})
, so
A
is not minimal. Contradiction. If
(1-(\alpha_{1}+\beta_{1})\gamma)<0
, then

\displaystyle-x=\sum_{i=2}^{n}\frac{\alpha_{i}+\beta_{i}}{(\alpha_{1}+\beta_{1})\gamma-1}x_{i}.

so
\we(A)
is not a cone. Contradiction.
If
(1-(\alpha_{1}+\beta_{1})\gamma)=0
, then

\displaystyle 0=\sum_{i=2}^{n}(\alpha_{i}+\beta_{i})x_{i}

As
x_{2}\neq 0
and
\alpha_{2}+\beta_{2}>0
, it follows that
n\geq 3
. So

\displaystyle-x_{2}=\sum_{i=3}^{n}\frac{\alpha_{i}+\beta_{i}}{\alpha_{2}+\beta_{2}}x_{i}.

So
\we(A)
is not a cone. Contradiction. As these were all possibilities, we conclude that
x
is extreme.

Let
x\in\we(A)
be extreme. Then for all
y,z\in\we(A)
,
x=y+z
implies that
y=\alpha x,z=\beta x
for some
\alpha,\beta\geq 0
. So
\we(A\backslash\left\{\alpha x:\alpha>0\right\})
does not contain
x
. So for some
\alpha>0
,
\alpha x\in A
.
∎

Lemma 44
.

Let
K
be a cone. Let
A
be a set of all extreme elements, so that for all
x,y\in A,x\neq y
, we have that
x\neq\alpha y
, for all
\alpha>0
. If
K
is generated by
A
, then
A
is minimal.

Proof.

Suppose
A
is not minimal, then for some
x\in A
and for some
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in A\backslash\left\{x\right\}
, and
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}>0
,

x=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}x_{i}.

As
x_{i}\neq\gamma x
for all
\gamma>0
and
i\in\left\{1,\ldots,n\right\}
, it follows that
x
is not extreme. Contradiction. So
A
is minimal.
∎

Let

Lemma 46
.

Let
K
be a cone that is generated by minimal sets

and

, so

Lemma 47
