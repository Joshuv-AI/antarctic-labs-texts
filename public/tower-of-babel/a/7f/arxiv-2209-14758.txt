On k k -clusters of high-intensity random geometric graphs
Mathew D. Penrose, Xiaochuan Yang

Abstract
Let k , d k,d be positive integers. We determine a sequence of constants that are asymptotic to the probability that the cluster at the origin in a d d -dimensional Poisson Boolean model with balls of fixed radius is of order k k , as the intensity becomes large. Using this, we determine the asymptotics of the mean of the number of components of order k k , denoted S n , k S_{n,k} in a random geometric graph on n n uniformly distributed vertices in a smoothly bounded compact region of R d R^{d} , with distance parameter r ⁡ ( n ) r(n) chosen so that the expected degree grows slowly as n n becomes large (the so-called mildly dense limiting regime). We also show that the variance of S n , k S_{n,k} is asymptotic to its mean, and prove Poisson and normal approximation results for S n , k S_{n,k} in this limiting regime. We provide analogous results for the corresponding Poisson process (i.e. with a Poisson number of points). We also give similar results in the so-called mildly sparse limiting regime where r ⁡ ( n ) r(n) is chosen so the expected degree decays slowly to zero as n n becomes large.



1
Overview

1.1
Introduction and background

The
Poisson blob model
(PBM) of overlapping spheres centred on
random points in space, also known as the
Gilbert graph
,
is perhaps the simplest model of random
clustering in a continuous space. Cluster formation is governed
entirely by spatial proximity of particles, and their locations
are governed by complete spatial randomness with no interactions, i.e. a
homogeneous spatial Poisson process.
The
random geometric graph
(RGG) is obtained by restricting the PBM to
a finite window, or in an alternate version, by specifying the number of particles in the window.

In the present paper we investigate these models at high intensity, i.e. with
a high density of particles relative to the range at which connections form.
For high densities, one expects most of the particles to lie in a single giant cluster, but the spatial randomness means that from time to time smaller
clusters may also be seen. We consider here clusters of fixed order
k
.
We provide precise asymptotics on the frequency of these clusters
as the intensity increases, and moreover describe the fluctuations of the number of clusters in a window that grows in size simultaneously
with the growth of the intensity; the window needs to be large enough to
observe clusters of order
k
. We also describe the limiting internal spatial
distribution of the
k
-clusters (suitably rescaled) at high intensity,
and show it is governed by an interesting energy functional defined on
configurations of
k
points.

Formally, the
RGG based on a random sample

{\cal X}
of points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(with
d\in\mathbb{N}
)
is the graph
G({\cal X},r)
with vertex set
{\cal X}
and with an edge between each pair
of points distant at most
r
apart, in the Euclidean metric, for a specified
distance parameter
r>0
. Such graphs are important in a variety
of applications (see
[
23
]
), such as wireless communications
(see
[
3
]
)
and
topological data analysis (TDA) (see
[
5
,
6
]
).

The PBM is given by a union of balls of equal radius, say
\frac{1}{2}
, centred
a homogeneous Poisson process
{\cal H}_{\lambda}
of intensity
\lambda
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
The clusters of the PBM correspond to
those of
the graph
G({\cal H}_{\lambda},1)
,
which is often called the
Gilbert graph
.
The terminology ‘Poisson blob model’ is used in e.g.

[
1
,
28
]
.
The PBM is a special case of the

Poisson Boolean model

with balls of fixed radius. This
is a fundamental model of spatial clustering and continuum percolation in
stochastic geometry; see

[
10
]
,
[
13
]
,
[
18
]
,
[
21
]
,

[
29
]
,
[
31
]
.

In this paper we consider, for fixed
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
the
number of components of order
k
(i.e., having

k
vertices) of the graph
G({\cal X},r)
,
here denoted
K_{k,r}({\cal X})
,
where
{\cal X}
is either a random sample of
n
points,
denoted
{\cal X}_{n}
,
uniformly distributed over a compact set

A
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}

with a smooth (in fact,
C^{1,1}
) boundary
or the corresponding Poisson process, denoted
{\cal P}_{n}
. In particular,
we investigate asymptotic properties of
K_{k,r}({\cal X}_{n})

and
K_{k,r}({\cal P}_{n})
for large
n
with

r=r(n)
specified and decaying to zero according
to a certain limiting regime (see (
1.1
) and
(
1.2
) below).
In the special case
k=1
,
K_{1,r}({\cal X})
is the number of isolated
vertices in
G({\cal X},r)
.

Some limiting regimes have been considered already.
In the
thermodynamic
limiting regime with
nr^{d}
held constant,

K_{k,r}({\cal X}_{n})

is known to grow proportionately to
n
, with a strong law of large numbers
(LLN)
(see
[
23
, Theorem 3.15]
) and there are central limit theorems
(CLTs) both for
K_{k,r}({\cal X}_{n})
(see
[
23
, Theorem 3.14]
)
and for
K_{k,r}({\cal P}_{n})
(see
[
23
, Theorem 3.11]
).
There is also a strong LLN for
K_{k,r}({\cal X}_{n})
in
the
sparse
regime with
nr^{d}\to 0
, subject to some further conditions
on
r(n)
; see
[
23
, Theorem 3.19]
.
Within the sparse regime,
when
n(nr^{d})^{(k-1)}\to\infty
but
n(nr^{d})^{k}\to 0
,
a CLT for
K_{k,r}({\cal X}_{n})
can be derived from
[
23
, Theorem 3.5]

and the fact that the second condition implies that the number
of components of order larger than
k
vanishes, in probability.
In fact the results in
[
23
]

are stated for the number of components isomorphic to some specified
connected graph
\Gamma
,
but results for
K_{k,r}({\cal X}_{n})
or

K_{k,r}({\cal P}_{n})
can then be obtained by summing over all possible

\Gamma
with
k
vertices. More recently,
[
15
]

gives a large deviations principle for the
k
-component
count in the sparse regime when also
n(nr^{d})^{k-1}\to\infty
.

In the
logarithmic regime
we take

n\theta r^{d}=b\log n
for some constant
b
,
where
\theta
denotes the volume of the unit radius ball in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Provided

and

b
exceeds the critical value

(where
{\rm Vol}
denotes Lebesgue measure),
it is known that with probability tending to 1 as
n\to\infty
,

G({\cal X}_{n},r)
is fully connected
so that
K_{k,r}({\cal X}_{n})=0
(see
[
23
, Theorem 13.7]
).

The main limiting regime for
r=r(n)
that we consider here is to make
the assumptions

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}(nr^{d})

\displaystyle=\infty;

(1.1)

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}((\theta nr^{d})/\log n)

\displaystyle<\min((2/d),1){\rm Vol}(A).

(1.2)

We call this the
intermediate
or

mildly dense
regime because the average
vertex degree is of order
\Theta(nr^{d})
and therefore grows
to infinity as
n
becomes large, but only slowly in this regime.
Our intermediate regime includes the logarithmic regime for small
values of the constant
b
;
we discuss the significance of the upper bound in (
1.2
) later on.

While asymptotics for the number of
k
-clusters
in continuum clustering models of this sort are a classical object
of study

[
23
,
12
,
13
,
11
,
31
,
28
]
, there has also beeen renewed
interest
more recently, e.g.
[
15
]
, often motivated by
TDA, where topological properties
of a simplicial complex based on the sample of points are used to
try to understand those of the underlying space. The sum
\sum_{k}K_{k,r}({\cal X})

is the
total number of components
in
G({\cal X},r)
, and this
is of interest in TDA as one of the Betti numbers
[
5
,
6
,
16
]

In TDA the number of components (and other Betti numbers) are of
interest in the whole
range of values of
r
, not just in the sparse or thermodynamic limiting
regime. One can also imagine other situations where the dense limit is
of interest: for example the structure of impurities in a gas that
gets more and more compressed, or that of isolated communities
in a society that becomes more and more interconnected.
However, most of the existing work on the limit theory
is concerned with the
sparse or thermodynamic limit; in the TDA context, this is noted in
the last paragraph of
[
6
, Section 2.4.1]
.
It seems well worth building our understanding of the mildly dense limit
as well.

In a companion paper
[
27
]
we investigate the limiting behaviour of
the total number of components in the mildly dense limiting regime.
It turns out that this quantity is dominated by the first term in
the sum, namely
K_{1,r}({\cal X})
(which is also the case in the sparse limit,
as observed long ago in
[
12
]
). The results in

[
27
]
rely on those in the present paper, in particular
the variance asymptotics in Propositions

4.1
and
4.3
and
the Poisson approximation results in Theorem
5.1
.
While it is the special case
k=1
of our results here that are most
relevant to
[
27
]
, one can imagine some algorithm whereby all singletons
are removed from the sample, and one is interested in the number of remaining
clusters. In this case we would expect the 2-clusters to dominate, and
if these too were removed, the 3-clusters would dominate, and so on. So it
seems natural to investigate the behaviour of
k
-clusters for general
k
.

1.2
Summary of results

Regarding the Poisson blob model, the number of components of order
k

in
G({\cal H}_{\lambda},1)
will be infinite. Instead, we consider the
probability
p_{k}(\lambda)
that an inserted point at the origin

o
lies in a component of order
k
of
G({\cal H}_{\lambda}\cup\{o\},1)
.
This can be interpreted, loosely speaking, as the proportion
of vertices in
G({\cal H}_{\lambda},1)
that lie in components of order
k
.

Our first result, Theorem
2.1
,
determines the asymptotic behaviour of

p_{k}(\lambda)
for large
\lambda
and fixed
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
It says that
p_{k}(\lambda)\sim\alpha_{k}\lambda^{(1-k)(d-1)}e^{-\theta\lambda}
as
\lambda\to\infty
,
where
\alpha_{1}:=1
and for
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
\alpha_{k+1}:=(1/k!)\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{k}}e^{-g({\bf z})}d{\bf z}
, where

g({\bf z}):=\int_{\cup_{i=1}^{k}B(\frac{1}{2}z_{i},\|z_{i}\|/2)}\|x\|^{1-d}dx,\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ {\bf z}=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k})\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{k}.

Here and elsewhere, for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
r>0
we write
B(x,r)
or
B_{r}(x)

for
\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|y-x\|\leq r\}
,
and
\|\cdot\|
denotes the Euclidean norm on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
This result is both of interest in its
own right, and important for understanding the asymptotics
for number of components of order
k
in RGGs in the mildly dense regime.

In Theorem
2.2
, we shall provide
a result on the large-
\lambda

limiting distribution of the (rescaled) points of
the
component containing the origin,
given that it is of order
k+1
. Namely, their joint density
is given by
e^{-g({\bf z})}
, normalized to a probability density function.
We have not seen the ‘energy functional’
g(\cdot)
in the literature before.

We now describe our main results on finite RGGs. Let

A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be compact with smooth boundary (in the
C^{1,1}
sense
that we define later), and let

X_{1},X_{2},\ldots
be independent identically distributed random
d
-vectors,
uniformly distributed over
A
.
For
n\in\mathbb{N}
set

{\cal X}_{n}:=\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\}
, which is a binomial point process
(see e.g.
[
18
]
).
Also, for
n\in(0,\infty)

let
Z_{n}
be a Poisson random variable with mean
n
,
independent of
(X_{1},X_{2},\ldots)
, and set

{\cal P}_{n}:={\cal X}_{Z_{n}}
. Then
(see
[
18
, Proposition 3.5]
)

{\cal P}_{n}

is a Poisson point process in
A
with intensity measure
(n/{\rm Vol}(A))dx

(in this case
n
does not need to be an integer).

Our results are concerned with the variables
S_{n,k}:=K_{k,r}({\cal X}_{n})
and

S^{\prime}_{n,k}:=K_{k,r}({\cal P}_{n}
), with
r=r(n)
given, satisfying
(
1.1
) and (
1.2
) unless stated otherwise.
For now we present them as asymptotic results as
n\to\infty

with
k
fixed, but the precise statements of these results
later on come with bounds on the rates of convergence.

As first-order results we shall give the asymptotic
behaviour of
\mathbb{E}[S_{n,k}]
and
\mathbb{E}[S^{\prime}_{n,k}]
.
Setting
I_{n,k}:=\mathbb{E}[S^{\prime}_{n,k}]
, we show in Theorem

(with

\displaystyle I_{n,k}\sim k^{-1}\alpha_{k}n(nr^{d}/{\rm Vol}(A))^{(1-k)(d-1)}\exp(-(\theta/{\rm Vol}(A))nr^{d})

Turning to second-order results, we shall

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}[S_{n,k}]\sim\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}[S^{\prime}_{n,k}]\sim I_{n,k}\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ {\rm as}\penalty\ n\to\infty.

\displaystyle I_{n,k}^{-1/2}(S^{\prime}_{n,k}-I_{n,k})\overset{{\cal D}}{\longrightarrow}N(0,1);\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ I_{n,k}^{-1/2}(S_{n,k}-\mathbb{E}[S_{n,k}])\overset{{\cal D}}{\longrightarrow}N(0,1).

Finally, in Section

It is natural to ask whether our results on

and
S^{\prime}_{n,k}

generalize to non-uniform distributions. To answer this, we shall present
these results in greater generality. We shall assume
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots

are independent random
d
-vectors having a common probability distribution

\nu
with density
f
, supported by
A
(so that
\mathbb{P}[X_{i}\in dx]=\nu(dx)=f(x)dx

We then define
{\cal X}_{n}
and
{\cal P}_{n}
as already described.

For all of our results on the mildly dense limiting regime,
we shall assume that
f
is continuous on
A
,
and that
f_{0}>0
, where we set

f_{0}:=\inf_{x\in A}f(x);\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ f_{1}:=\inf_{x\in\partial A}f(x);\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ f_{\rm max}:=\sup_{x\in A}f(x),

. By compactness of

; moreover

.
In the special case where

,
we have

; we call this the

.

In the general (non-uniform) case, instead of (

) we assume

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}((\theta nr^{d})/\log n)<\frac{1}{\max(f_{0},d(f_{0}-f_{1}/2))},

) in the uniform case.
This condition may be understood as follows. Suppose

A ‘back-of-the envelope’ calculation suggests
that the mean number of components of order

(which we shall call

.

If

is less than the right hand side

.

) in the uniform case,
and we leave this as possible

S_{n,k}
and

) and (

),
except
for the

).

Remark 1.1
.

1.

As alluded to earlier, in the thermodynamic or
sparse limiting regime, it is of interest
to further distinguish between different
components of order
k
, according to which
of the possible graphs
\Gamma
they are
isomorphic to. In the mildly dense limiting regime
considered here, this
is perhaps of less interest, because of the
compression

phenomenon already noted in
[
1
]
; for
fixed
k
and large
n
, nearly all
of the components of order
k
are likely to
be compressed into balls of diameter less than

r
(in fact, less than
\delta r
for any
fixed
\delta>0
), and therefore
fully connected. In other words, nearly all
of the components
of order
k
are likely to be cliques.

2.

It seems plausible
that one could derive a result on the
limiting within-cluster spatial distribution of points in
k
-clusters
of the RGG in the mildly dense limit,
suitably rescaled,
using Theorem
2.2
, but we do not do so here.

In
[
28
]
the mean volume of
k
-clusters of the PBM at finite intensities
is investigated, in particular providing an expansion in
\lambda
for

\lambda
small. We would expect Theorem

2.2
to be useful for understanding the limiting behaviour
of the mean volume of
k
-clusters
for
\lambda
large.

3.

To prove our Gaussian approximation results, we shall use the
local dependence methods of Chen and Shao

[
8
]
to deal
with
S^{\prime}_{n,k}
and those of Chatterjee

[
7
]
to deal with
S_{n,k}
.
For the Poisson approximation, we shall use the coupling bounds from
e.g. Lindvall
[
20
]
for
S_{n,k}
and from Penrose

[
25
]
for
S^{\prime}_{n,k}
.
All of these approximation bounds
are obtained by a blend of Stein’s method and stochastic analysis of
general Poisson or binomial point process functionals. For a
systematic account of the subject, we refer the reader
to
[
30
,
4
,
9
,
22
,
18
]
, and to
[
2
]

for some recent developments about the methodology.

4.

Our results hold for all
d\geq 2
, and also for
d=1
with the sole
exception of our result on Poisson approximation for
S_{n,k}

(Proposition
5.6
); we conjecture that even this result could
be proved by other means for

. Many of our results seem to be new
even for the special case with

(i.e., the isolated vertex count) and/or

.

1.3
Notation

We use the following notation for asymptotics.
Given

,
and

,
we write

, and

,

.
Here, the limit is taken either as

We denote the origin in

Proposition 3.2

(Mean

If

asymptotics for
I_{n,k}
. However we do have the following,
which formalises back-of-the-envelope calculations
suggesting
I_{n,k}\approx n\exp(-n\theta f_{0}r_{n}^{d})
.

Theorem 3.8

(Limiting behaviour of
I_{n,k}
in the non-uniform
case)
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Then

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\left((nr^{d})^{-1}\log(I_{n,k}/n)\right)=-\theta f_{0}.

(3.11)

Proof.

By Lemma
3.1
, given
f_{0}^{+}>f_{0}
we have
for large
n
that
I_{n,k}\geq n\exp(-n\theta f_{0}^{+}r^{d})
,
so that
\log(I_{n,k}/n)\geq-n\theta f_{0}^{+}r^{d}
, and hence

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\left((nr^{d})^{-1}\log(I_{n,k}/n)\right)\geq-\theta f_{0}.

For a bound the other way, note that
\nu(B_{r}(x))\geq\theta f_{0}r^{d}

for
x\in A^{(r)}
. Also using Lemma
3.7
,
given
f_{1}^{-}<f_{1}
we have for
n
large enough that

\nu(B_{r}(x))\geq(\theta/2)f_{1}^{-}r^{d}
for all
x\in A
. Therefore
using the last part of Lemma
3.7
, we have
that

\displaystyle I_{n,k}\leq

\displaystyle n\nu(A^{(r)})(nf_{\rm max}\theta((k-1)r)^{d})^{k-1}\exp(-n\theta f_{0}r^{d})

\displaystyle+O(nr(nr^{d})^{k-1}\exp(-n(\theta/2)f_{1}^{-}r^{d})).

(3.12)

The second term in the right-hand side of (
3.12
),
divided by the first term,
is bounded by a constant times

r\exp(n\theta r^{d}(f_{0}-f^{-}_{1}/2))
, and using
(
3.1
) we can show this tends to zero, provided

f_{1}^{-}
is chosen close enough to
f_{1}
.
Hence by (
3.12
)
we can deduce that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\left((nr^{d})^{-1}\log(I_{n,k}/n)\right)\leq-\theta f_{0},

and hence (
3.11
).
∎

4
Variance asymptotics and laws of large numbers

In this section,
we show that the variance of the
k
-cluster count

S_{n,k}
and its expectation are asymptotically equivalent, with explicit rates, and likewise for
S^{\prime}_{n,k}
.
As mentioned in Section
1
, this is enough to yield
weak laws of large numbers for
S_{n,k}
and
S^{\prime}_{n,k}
but we shall
also derive
concentration results which yield strong laws of large numbers.

4.1
Variance asymptotics in the Poisson setting

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Then there exists
c>0
such that as
n\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}[S^{\prime}_{n,k}]=\mathbb{E}[S^{\prime}_{n,k}](1+O(e^{-cnr^{d}}))=I_{n,k}(1+O(e^{-cnr^{d}})).

Proof.

Since
S^{\prime}_{n,k}(S^{\prime}_{n,k}-1)
is the number of ordered
pairs of distinct
k
-clusters in
G({\cal P}_{n},r)
,
with
h(\cdot):=h_{r}(\cdot)
as given at (
1.7
),
we can write
S^{\prime}_{n,k}(S^{\prime}_{n,k}-1)
as follows

\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi,\psi\subset{\cal P}_{n},|\varphi|=|\psi|=k}h(\varphi)h(\psi)\mathbbm{1}\{({\cal P}_{n}\setminus\varphi\setminus\psi)\cap B_{r}(\varphi\cup\psi)=\varnothing,\dist(\varphi,\psi)>r\}.

Indeed,
for distinct
k
-subsets
\psi,\varphi
of
{\cal P}_{n}
, their distance must be larger than
r
in order that they are both connected components of order
k
, for otherwise
\psi
and
\varphi
are connected with
|\psi\cup\varphi|>k
.
Thus by the multivariate Mecke equation

\mathbb{E}[(S^{\prime}_{n,k})^{2}]-\mathbb{E}[S^{\prime}_{n,k}]
equals

\displaystyle\frac{n^{2k}}{k!k!}\int_{A^{k}}\int_{A^{k}}h({\bf x})h({\bf y})\exp(-n\nu(B_{r}({\bf x},{\bf y})))\mathbbm{1}\{\dist({\bf x},{\bf y})>r\}\nu^{k}(d{\bf y})\nu^{k}(d{\bf x}),

where
B_{r}({\bf x},{\bf y}):=B_{r}({\bf x})\cup B_{r}({\bf y})
.
We compare this integral with

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[S^{\prime}_{n,k}]^{2}=\frac{n^{2k}}{k!k!}\int_{A^{k}}\int_{A^{k}}h({\bf x})h({\bf y})\exp(-n[\nu(B_{r}({\bf x}))+\nu(B_{r}({\bf y}))])\nu^{k}(d{\bf y})\nu^{k}(d{\bf x}),

which comes from (
3.3
).
Observe that
\nu(B_{r}({\bf x},{\bf y}))=\nu(B_{r}({\bf x}))+\nu(B_{r}({\bf y}))

whenever
\dist({\bf x},{\bf y})>2r
.
Therefore
|\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}[S^{\prime}_{n,k}]-\mathbb{E}[S^{\prime}_{n,k}]|\leq J_{1,n}+J_{2,n}
,
where

\displaystyle J_{1,n}

\displaystyle:=\frac{n^{2k}}{k!k!}\int_{A^{k}}\int_{A^{k}}h({\bf x})h({\bf y})\exp(-n\nu(B_{r}({\bf x},{\bf y})))\mathbbm{1}\{\dist({\bf x},{\bf y})\in(r,2r]\}\nu^{k}(d{\bf y})\nu^{k}(d{\bf x});

(4.1)

\displaystyle J_{2,n}

\displaystyle:=\frac{n^{2k}}{k!k!}\int_{A^{2k}}h({\bf x})h({\bf y})\exp(-n[\nu(B_{r}({\bf x}))+\nu(B_{r}({\bf y}))])\mathbbm{1}\{\dist({\bf x},{\bf y})\leq 2r\}\nu^{2k}(d({\bf x},{\bf y})).

(4.2)

We estimate
J_{1,n}
in Lemma
4.2
below.
For
J_{2,n}
, note that by Lemma
3.7
there exists
n_{0}

such that for all
n\geq n_{0}
and all

{\bf y}\in A^{k}

we have
\nu(B_{r}({\bf y}))\geq(\theta/3)f_{0}r^{d}
.
Hence for
{\bf x}\in A^{k}
we have

\frac{n^{k}}{k!}\int_{A^{k}}h({\bf y})\exp(-n\nu(B_{r}({\bf y})))\mathbbm{1}\{\dist({\bf x},{\bf y})\leq 2r\}\nu^{k}(d{\bf y})=O((nr^{d})^{k}\exp(-n(\theta/3)f_{0}r^{d})),

and hence

J_{2,n}=O(I_{n,k}(nr^{d})^{k}\exp(-nf_{0}(\theta/3)r^{d}))
, which
is
O(I_{n,k}\exp(-nf_{0}(\theta/4)r^{d}))
.
Combined with Lemma
4.2
, this completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
J_{1,n}
be given by (
4.1
).
There exists
c>0
such that

J_{1,n}=O(e^{-cnr^{d}}I_{n,k})
as
n\to\infty
.

Proof.

Let us write
{\bf x}\prec{\bf y}

if there exists

y\in\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{k}\}
such that all points of

\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\}
precede
y
in the lexicographic ordering
(or in other words,
the rightmost entry of
{\bf x}
lies to the
left of the rightmost entry of
{\bf y}
).
Since the integrand in
J_{1}

is symmetric in
{\bf x}
and
{\bf y}
,
J_{1}
is twice the same expression
with the inner integral restricted to
{\bf y}
satisfying
{\bf x}\prec{\bf y}
.

Suppose
{\bf x},{\bf y}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{k}
with
h({\bf x})=h({\bf y})=1

and
\dist({\bf y},{\bf x})\in(r,2r]
.
These conditions imply that
{\bf y}\in(B_{2kr}(x_{1}))^{k}
, where
we write
{\bf x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
.
Thus, if also
{\bf x}\in(A^{(9kr)})^{k}

then
{\bf y}\in(A^{(7kr)})^{k}
,
and if moreover
{\bf x}\prec{\bf y}
,
by considering the right half of the ball of radius
r
centred
on the right-most entry of
{\bf y}
, we see that

\nu(B_{r}({\bf y})\setminus B_{r}({\bf x}))\geq(\theta f_{0}/2)r^{d}
.
Therefore since

B_{r}({\bf x},{\bf y})=B_{r}({\bf x})\cup(B_{r}({\bf y})\setminus B_{r}({\bf x}))

and this is a disjoint union,

\displaystyle J_{1,n}\leq\frac{2n^{k}}{k!}\int_{(A^{(9kr)})^{k}}h({\bf x})\exp(-n\nu(B_{r}({\bf x})))a({\bf x})\nu^{k}(d{\bf x})+z_{n},

where

\displaystyle a({\bf x}):=\frac{n^{k}}{k!}\int_{A^{k}}e^{-(\theta f_{0}/2)nr^{d}}h({\bf y})\mathbbm{1}\{\dist({\bf x},{\bf y})\in(r,2r],{\bf x}\prec{\bf y}\}\nu^{k}(d{\bf y}),

(4.3)

and
z_{n}
is a remainder term from
{\bf x}
near
\partial A

(to be dealt with later).

As already mentioned, if
{\bf x},{\bf y}\in A^{k}
with
h({\bf x})=h({\bf y})=1
and

r<\dist({\bf x},{\bf y})\leq 2r
, then
{\bf y}\in(B_{2kr}(x_{1}))^{k}
, and
consequently

\displaystyle a({\bf x})\leq\frac{(nf_{\rm max})^{k}}{k!}e^{-(\theta f_{0}/2)nr^{d}}\theta^{k}(2kr)^{kd}=O((nr^{d})^{k}e^{-(\theta f_{0}/2)nr^{d}})=O(e^{-(\theta f_{0}/3)nr^{d}}),

uniformly over
{\bf x}\in A^{k}
. Therefore by
virtue of (
3.3
), we have

J_{1,n}-z_{n}=O(e^{-(\theta f_{0}/3)nr^{d}}I_{n,k})
.

For the remainder term, note that given
f_{1}^{-}<f_{1}
,
by Lemma
3.7
and the assumed continuity of
f|_{A}
,
there exists
n_{0}
such
that for all
n\geq n_{0}
and
all
{\bf x}\in A^{k}\setminus(A^{(9r)})^{k}
, we
have
\nu(B_{r}({\bf x}))\geq n(\theta f_{1}^{-}/2)r^{d}
.
Also if
{\bf x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\in A^{k}\setminus(A^{(9kr)})^{k}
then at least one of
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}

lies in
A\setminus A^{(9kr)}
. Introducing a factor of
k
we
can assume this is
x_{1}
, and hence
deduce that

\displaystyle|z_{n}|\leq\frac{2(nf_{\rm max})^{2k}}{(k-1)!}\int_{A\setminus A^{(9kr)}}(\theta(2kr)^{d})^{2k-1}dx_{1}e^{-n\theta f_{1}^{-}r^{d}/2}=O(nr(nr^{d})^{2k-1}e^{-n\theta f_{1}^{-}r^{d}/2}).

Combined with (
3.4
) this shows that

z_{n}/I_{n,k}=O(r\exp(n\theta r^{d}(f_{0}^{+}-f_{1}^{-}/2)))
,
for any
f_{0}^{+}>f_{0}
.
Using (
3.1
), we can choose
\varepsilon>0
,
f_{0}^{+}>f_{0}
and
f_{1}^{-}<f_{1}

in such a way that this upper bound implies
z_{n}/I_{n,k}=O(rn^{(1/d)-\varepsilon})
,
and using (
3.1
) again we see that this is
O(e^{-cnr^{d}})
for
some
c>0
. Thus
J_{1,n}=O(e^{-cnr^{d}}I_{n,k})
for some
c>0
.
∎

4.2
Variance asymptotics in the binomial case

Proposition 4.3
.

There exists
c>0
such that as
n\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}[S_{n,k}]=\mathbb{E}[S_{n,k}](1+O(e^{-cnr^{d}})).

(4.4)

Proof.

Let
\prec
denote the lexicographic ordering on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Write

S_{n,k}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}
, here taking

\displaystyle\xi_{i}:=\mathbbm{1}\{|{\cal C}_{r}(X_{i},{\cal X}_{n})|=k,X_{i}\prec y\penalty\ \forall y\in{\cal C}_{r}(X_{i},{\cal X}_{n})\setminus\{X_{i}\}\}.

(4.5)

Then

\mathbb{E}[S_{n,k}]=n\mathbb{E}[\xi_{1}]
, and

\mathbb{E}[S_{n,k}^{2}]=n\mathbb{E}[\xi_{1}^{2}]+n(n-1)\mathbb{E}[\xi_{1}\xi_{2}],

so that
\mathbb{E}[S_{n,k}^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[S_{n,k}]+n(n-1)\mathbb{E}[\xi_{1}\xi_{2}]
.
Hence,

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}[S_{n,k}]-\mathbb{E}[S_{n,k}]=-n\mathbb{E}[\xi_{1}\xi_{2}]+n^{2}(\mathbb{E}[\xi_{1}\xi_{2}]-\mathbb{E}[\xi_{1}]\mathbb{E}[\xi_{2}]).

(4.6)

In view of
Proposition
3.2

we need to show the right hand
side of (
4.6
) is
O(e^{-cnr^{d}}I_{n,k})
.

For
{\bf x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{k}
,
recall from (
1.7
)
that
h^{*}_{r}({\bf x})
is the indicator variable
of the event that
G(\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\},r)
is connected
and moreover
x_{1}\prec x_{i}
for
i=2,\ldots,k
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\xi_{1}]=\binom{n-1}{k-1}\int_{A^{k}}h^{*}_{r}({\bf x})(1-\nu(B_{r}({\bf x})))^{n-k}\nu^{k}(d{\bf x}).

(4.7)

Consider the first term in the right hand side of (
4.6
).
Observe that if
\xi_{1}=\xi_{2}=1
then

{\cal C}_{r}(X_{2},{\cal X}_{n})\cap B_{r}({\cal C}_{r}(X_{1},{\cal X}_{n}))=\varnothing
.
Hence, using the bound
1-t\leq e^{-t}

for all
t\geq 0
and writing
B_{r}({\bf x},{\bf y}):=B_{r}({\bf x})\cup B_{r}({\bf y})
, we have that
n\mathbb{E}[\xi_{1}\xi_{2}]
equals

\displaystyle n\binom{n-2}{k-1}\binom{n-1-k}{k-1}\int_{A^{k}}\int_{(A\setminus B_{r}({\bf x}))^{k}}h_{r}^{*}({\bf x})h_{r}^{*}({\bf y})(1-\nu(B_{r}({\bf x},{\bf y})))^{n-2k}\nu^{k}(d{\bf y})\nu^{k}(d{\bf x})

(4.8)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2n^{2k-1}}{(k-1)!^{2}}\int_{A^{k}}\int_{(A\setminus B_{r}({\bf x}))^{k}}h_{r}^{*}({\bf x})h_{r}^{*}({\bf y})\exp(-n\nu(B_{r}({\bf x},{\bf y})))\nu^{k}(d{\bf y})\nu^{k}(d{\bf x}),

(4.9)

provided
n
is large enough so that
e^{2k\nu(B_{r}({\bf x},{\bf y}))}\leq 2
for all
{\bf x},{\bf y}\in A^{k}
.

Let
\alpha<\min(f_{0},f_{1}/2)
. Using Lemma
3.7
,
we have for all large enough
n
and all

{\bf x}\in A^{k}
,
{\bf y}\in(A\setminus B_{2r}({\bf x}))^{k}
that
\nu(B_{r}({\bf x},{\bf y}))\geq\nu(B_{r}({\bf x}))+\theta\alpha r^{d}
.
Hence for such
({\bf x},{\bf y})
the integrand in (
4.9
) is bounded by

h^{*}_{r}({\bf x})h^{*}_{r}({\bf y})e^{-n\nu(B_{r}({\bf x}))}e^{-\alpha\theta nr^{d}}
. Since

\int_{A^{k}}h^{*}_{r}({\bf y})\nu^{k}(d{\bf y})=O(r^{d(k-1)})
, and
I_{n,k}=\frac{n^{k}}{(k-1)!}\int_{A^{k}}h^{*}_{r}({\bf x})e^{-n\nu(B_{r}({\bf x}))}\nu^{k}(d{\bf x})

by the multivariate Mecke formula,

\displaystyle n^{2k-1}\int_{A^{k}}\int_{(A\setminus B_{2r}({\bf x}))^{k}}h_{r}^{*}({\bf x})h_{r}^{*}({\bf y})e^{-n\nu(B_{r}({\bf x},{\bf y}))}\nu^{k}(d{\bf y})\nu^{k}(d{\bf x})=O((nr^{d})^{k-1}e^{-n\theta\alpha r^{d}}I_{n,k}).

On the other hand, if
{\bf y}\in A^{k}\setminus(A\setminus B_{2r}({\bf x}))^{k}
,
and
h_{r}^{*}({\bf y})=1
, then
{\bf y}\in(B_{2kr}(x_{1}))^{k}
, and hence

\int_{A^{k}\setminus(A\setminus B_{2r}({\bf x}))^{k}}h_{r}^{*}({\bf y})\nu^{k}(d{\bf y})=O(r^{dk})
, and
\nu(B_{r}({\bf x},{\bf y}))\geq\nu(B_{r}({\bf x}))
, so that

\displaystyle n^{2k-1}\int_{A^{k}}\int_{A^{k}\setminus(A\setminus B_{2r}({\bf x}))^{k}}h_{r}^{*}({\bf x})h_{r}^{*}({\bf y})e^{-n\nu(B_{r}({\bf x},{\bf y}))}\nu^{k}(d{\bf y})\nu^{k}(d{\bf x})=O(r^{d}(nr^{d})^{k-1}I_{n,k}),

which is
O(e^{-cnr^{d}}I_{n,k})
for some
c>0
by (
3.1
).
Thus the first term in the right hand side of (
4.6
)
is
O(e^{-cnr^{d}}I_{n,k})
for some
c>0
.

Now consider the second term in the right hand side of (
4.6
).
Given
n
, for
{\bf x},{\bf y}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{k}

let
p_{\bf x}=\nu(B_{r}({\bf x}))
and
p_{{\bf x},{\bf y}}:=\nu(B_{r}({\bf x},{\bf y}))
.
By (
4.7
) and (
4.8
),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\xi_{1}\xi_{2}]-\mathbb{E}[\xi_{1}]\mathbb{E}[\xi_{2}]=\int_{A^{k}}\int_{(A\setminus B_{2r}({\bf x}))^{k}}\left[\binom{n-2}{k-1}\binom{n-1-k}{k-1}(1-p_{\bf x}-p_{\bf y})^{n-2k}\right.

\displaystyle\left.-\binom{n-1}{k-1}^{2}(1-p_{\bf x})^{n-k}(1-p_{\bf y})^{n-k}\right]h^{*}_{r}({\bf x})h^{*}_{r}({\bf y})\nu^{k}(d{\bf y})\nu^{k}(d{\bf x})

\displaystyle+\binom{n-2}{k-1}\binom{n-1-k}{k-1}\int_{A^{k}}\int_{(A\setminus B_{r}({\bf x}))^{k}\setminus(A\setminus B_{2r}({\bf x}))^{k}}h^{*}_{r}({\bf x})h^{*}_{r}({\bf y})(1-p_{{\bf x},{\bf y}})^{n-2k}\nu^{k}(d{\bf y})\nu^{k}(d{\bf x})

\displaystyle-\binom{n-1}{k-1}^{2}\int_{A^{k}}\int_{A^{k}\setminus(A\setminus B_{2r}({\bf x}))^{k}}h^{*}_{r}({\bf x})h^{*}_{r}({\bf y})(1-p_{\bf x})^{n-k}(1-p_{\bf y})^{n-k}\nu^{k}(d{\bf y})\nu^{k}(d{\bf x}).

(4.10)

Note that
p_{\bf x}\leq kf_{\rm max}\theta r^{d}
for all
{\bf x}\in A^{k}
.
Writing
p
for
p_{\bf x}
and
q
for
p_{\bf y}
, and using the fact that
as
n\to\infty
with
k
fixed,

\binom{n-1}{k-1}^{2}\div\left[\binom{n-2}{k-1}\binom{n-1-k}{k-1}\right]=1+O(n^{-1}),

we estimate
the integrand in the first term in the right hand side of (
4.10
)
as follows:

\displaystyle\binom{n-2}{k-1}\binom{n-1-k}{k-1}(1-p-q)^{n-2k}-\binom{n-1}{k-1}^{2}(1-p-q+pq)^{n-k}

\displaystyle=\binom{n-2}{k-1}\binom{n-1-k}{k-1}(1-p-q)^{n-k}\left[(1-p-q)^{-k}-(1+O(n^{-1}))\left(1+\frac{pq}{1-p-q}\right)^{n-k}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{n^{2k-2}}{(k-1)!^{2}}\exp\left((n-k)\log(1-p-q)\right)\times[(n-k)pq+O(r^{d})+O(n^{-1})]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{n^{2k-1}}{(k-1)!^{2}}e^{-np}(kf_{\rm max}\theta)^{2}r^{2d}e^{-nq}(1+o(1)).

(4.11)

By Lemma
3.7
, for all large enough
n
and all
{\bf y}\in A^{k}

we have
q=p_{\bf y}\geq(f_{0}\theta/3)r^{d}
. Multiplying the
expression at (
4.11
) by
h^{*}_{r}({\bf x})h^{*}_{r}({\bf y})
and integrating over

{\bf y}\in(A\setminus B_{2r}({\bf x}))^{k}
yields an expression bounded by

\displaystyle c^{\prime}n^{k}(nr^{d})^{k-1}\exp(-(f_{0}\theta/3)nr^{d})\exp(-np_{\bf x})r^{2d}h^{*}_{r}({\bf x}),

for some constant
c^{\prime}
independent of
n
.
Recalling
I_{n,k}=\frac{n^{k}}{(k-1)!}\int e^{-np_{\bf x}}h^{*}_{r}({\bf x})\nu^{k}(d{\bf x})
, we find that the absolute value of the
first term in the right hand side of (
4.10
), multiplied
by
n^{2}
, is bounded above by

\displaystyle c^{\prime}(k-1)!(nr^{d})^{k+1}I_{n,k}\exp(-(f_{0}\theta/3)nr^{d})=O(I_{n,k}\exp(-(f_{0}\theta/4)nr^{d})).

We turn to the second term in the right hand side of (
4.10
).
By the bound
1-t\leq e^{-t}
, for large
n
this term, multiplied
by
n^{2}
, is bounded by

\frac{2n^{2k}}{(k-1)!^{2}}\int_{A^{k}}\int_{(A\setminus B_{r}({\bf x}))^{k}\setminus(A\setminus B_{2r}({\bf x}))^{k}}h_{r}^{*}({\bf x})h_{r}^{*}({\bf y})\exp(-n\nu(B_{r}({\bf x},{\bf y})))\nu^{k}(d{\bf x})\nu^{k}(d{\bf y}).

By
the multivariate Mecke formula, the last
displayed expression equals
twice the expected number of ordered pairs
(\varphi,\psi)

of distinct
k
-clusters
of
G({\cal P}_{n},r)
with
\dist(\varphi,\psi)<2r
.
Hence by a further application of the multivariate Mecke formula,
it is precisely equal to
2J_{1,n}
, where
J_{1,n}

was defined at (
4.1
). Therefore by Lemma
4.2
,
the second term in the right hand side of (
4.10
), multiplied
by
n^{2}
, is
O(e^{-cnr^{d}}I_{n,k})
for some
c>0
.

We turn to the last term in the right hand side of (
4.10
).
For large enough
n
, by Lemma
3.7
we have

p_{\bf y}\geq f_{0}(\theta/3)r^{d}
and also

(1-p_{\bf x})^{-k}(1-p_{\bf y})^{-k}\leq 2

for all
{\bf x},{\bf y}\in A
. Then

\displaystyle n^{2+2(k-1)}\int_{A^{k}}\int_{A^{k}\setminus(A\setminus B_{2r}({\bf x}))^{k}}h^{*}_{r}({\bf x})h^{*}_{r}({\bf y})(1-p_{\bf x})^{n-k}(1-p_{\bf y})^{n-k}\nu^{k}(d{\bf y})\nu^{k}(d{\bf x})

\displaystyle\leq cn^{2k}r^{dk}\int_{A^{k}}h^{*}_{r}({\bf x})e^{-np_{\bf x}}e^{-(f_{0}\theta/3)nr^{d}}\nu^{k}(d{\bf x})

\displaystyle=I_{n,k}\times O((nr^{d})^{k}e^{-f_{0}(\theta/3)nr^{d}}),

which is
O(I_{n,k}\exp(-(f_{0}\theta/4)nr^{d}))
.
Thus there exists
c>0
such that all terms in the right hand side
of (
4.10
), multiplied by
n^{2}
, are

O(e^{-cnr^{d}}I_{n,k})
. Therefore the right hand side of (
4.6
)
is
O(e^{-cnr^{d}}I_{n,k})
, as required.
∎

4.3
LLNs and concentration results for
S_{n,k}
and
S^{\prime}_{n,k}

A sequence of random variables
(\xi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is said to

converge completely
to a constant
C
(written

\xi_{n}\overset{c.c.}{\longrightarrow}C
) as
n\to\infty
if
\mathbb{P}[|\xi_{n}-C|>\varepsilon]
is summable in
n

for any
\varepsilon>0
. In particular,
complete convergence implies almost sure convergence for any sequence of random variables defined on the same probability space. We
now prove
S_{n,k}/I_{n,k}\overset{c.c.}{\longrightarrow}1

Proof of Theorem

.

By definition

.
Hence by Lemma

,
given any

Partition

Enumerate the cubes in the partition that intersect

as

Then

By the assumption (

),
we may assume

Now consider

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}[|S_{n,k}-\mathbb{E}[S_{n,k}]|\geq ne^{-nf_{0}^{+}\theta r^{d}}].

Then (

can be proved in the same manner as (

It is immediate from (

In the uniform case,

5
Asymptotic distribution of the
k
-cluster count

To present Poisson and normal approximation results for
S_{n,k}

and
S^{\prime}_{n,k}
, we recall three notions of distance
which measure the proximity between the distributions
of real-valued random variables
X,Y
.
The Kolmogorov distance is defined by

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{K}}(X,Y):=\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}}|\mathbb{P}[X\leq z]-\mathbb{P}[Y\leq z]|

The Wasserstein-1 distance is defined by

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{W}}(X,Y):=\sup_{h\in\mathrm{Lip}_{1}}|\mathbb{E}[h(X)]-\mathbb{E}[h(Y)]|,

where
\mathrm{Lip}_{1}
denotes the family of Lipschitz continuous functions with Lipschitz constant at most one. The total variation distance is defined by

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{TV}}(X,Y):=\sup_{A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})}|\mathbb{P}[X\in A]-\mathbb{P}[Y\in A]|,

where the supremum is taken over all Borel measurable subsets of
\mathbb{R}
.
If
X,Y
both take values in
\mathbb{Z}
, then this is the
same as
(1/2)\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}|\mathbb{P}[X=n]-\mathbb{P}[Y=n]|
.
Convergence in any of the three
aforementioned distances implies convergence in distribution.

In this section we prove the following result, concerning the asymptotic
distributions of
S_{n,k}
(the number of
k
-clusters in
G({\cal X}_{n},r)
) and

S^{\prime}_{n,k}
(the number of
k
-clusters in
G({\cal P}_{n},r)
),
in the mildly dense regime.
Recall from (
3.3
)
that
\mathbb{E}[S^{\prime}_{n,k}]=I_{n,k}
.
Recall that
Z_{t}
denotes a Poisson variable with mean
t
,
and let
\mathcal{N}
denote a standard normal random variable.

Theorem 5.1

(Distributional results on the
k
-cluster count)
.

Suppose that
r=r(n)
satisfies (
3.1
), and
set
b:=\limsup_{n\to\infty}(n\theta r^{d}/\log n)
. Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
There exists a finite
c>0
such that for all large enough
n
,

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{TV}}(S^{\prime}_{n,k},Z_{I_{n,k}})\leq e^{-cnr^{d}},

(5.1)

and if
d\geq 2
then for all large enough
n
,

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{TV}}(S_{n,k},Z_{\mathbb{E}[S_{n,k}]})\leq e^{-cnr^{d}},

(5.2)

Moreover (for all

we have

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{K}}(\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}[S^{\prime}_{n,k}]^{-1/2}(S^{\prime}_{n,k}-I_{n,k}),\mathcal{N})=O(n^{\varepsilon+(bf_{0}-1)/2});

(5.3)

and

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{W}}(\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}[S_{n,k}]^{-1/2}(S_{n,k}-\mathbb{E}[S_{n,k}]),\mathcal{N})=O(n^{\varepsilon+(3bf_{0}-1)/2}),

Also if

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{K}}(\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}[S_{n,k}]^{-1/2}(S_{n,k}-\mathbb{E}[S_{n,k}]),\mathcal{N})\leq e^{-cnr^{d}},

Remark 5.2
.

)

.
When

)

5.1

Our main tool for proving the Poisson approximation result (

Lemma 5.3

(

Let

\displaystyle W:=F({\cal P}_{n}):=\sum_{\psi\subset{\cal P}_{n},|\psi|=k}g(\psi,{\cal P}_{n}\setminus\psi).

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{TV}}(W,Z_{\mathbb{E}[W]})\leq\frac{\min(1,\mathbb{E}[W]^{-1})}{k!}\int\mathbb{E}[|U_{\bf x}-V_{\bf x}|]p({\bf x})\mu^{k}(d{\bf x}).

(5.6)

We restate the first Poisson approximation result (

)

in the following proposition.
Recall that
Z_{t}
denotes a Poisson variable with mean

.

Proposition 5.4
.

Let

. Then
there exist finite constants

Proof.

In view of (

g(\psi,\varphi):=h_{r}(\psi)\mathbbm{1}\{\varphi\cap B_{r}(\psi)=\varnothing\}.

For
{\bf x}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{k}
,
with

Define

\displaystyle V_{\bf x}:=\sum_{\varphi\subset{\cal P}_{n}\setminus B_{r}({\bf x}),|\varphi|=k}g(\varphi,{\cal P}_{n}\setminus B_{r}({\bf x})\setminus\varphi).

There are two sources of contribution to the change

of

-clusters of

\displaystyle|U_{\bf x}-V_{\bf x}|\leq\xi_{1}({\bf x})+\xi_{2}({\bf x}).

(5.7)

Combining this with (

), any factors of the form
e^{-n\nu(B_{r}({\bf x}))}

arising in moment estimates no longer tend to zero, but remain bounded above
by 1.

Next we have

\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}[S^{\prime}_{n,k}]=I_{n,k}(1+O(nr^{d})^{k}).

To see this, follow the proof of Proposition
4.1
.
In the sparse regime, it can be seen directly from (
4.1
)
and (
4.2
)
that both
J_{1,n}
and

J_{2,n}
are
O(n^{2k}r^{d(2k-1)})
, which is
O((nr^{d})^{k}I_{n,k})
.

Next we have

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{TV}}(S^{\prime}_{n,k},Z_{I_{n,k}})=O((nr^{d})^{k}).

(6.3)

For this, we can follow the proof of Proposition
5.4
.
We now have
\mathbb{E}[\xi_{1}({\bf x})]=O((nr^{d})^{k})
, uniformly over
{\bf x}
.
Likewise
\mathbb{E}[\xi_{2}({\bf x})]=O((nr^{d})^{k})
.

For
S_{n,k}
, we consider only the case
k\geq 2
in the sparse limiting
regime, since isolated vertices are not rare events.
We claim that if
k\geq 2
then

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}[S_{n,k}]=\mathbb{E}[S_{n,k}](1+O(nr^{d})^{k-1}).

(6.4)

This is proved by following the proof of Proposition

4.3
. The expression at (
4.9
) is
O(n^{2k-1}r^{d(2k-2)})

which is
O(I_{n,k}(nr^{d})^{k-1})
. In the penultimate line of
(
4.11
) the last factor (in square brackets) now simplifies
to
O(n^{-1})
so the expression in (
4.11
) is
O(n^{2k-3})
.
Hence the first term in the right hand side of (
4.10
),
multiplied by
n^{2}
, is now
O(n^{2k-1}r^{d(2k-2)})=O((nr^{d})^{k-1}I_{n,k})
.
It can be seen directly that
the second and third terms in the right hand side of (
4.10
),
multiplied by
n^{2}
, are
O(n^{2k}r^{d(2k-1)})

which is
O((nr^{d})^{k}I_{n,k})
.
Combining these estimates
shows that
\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}S_{n,k}-\mathbb{E}S_{n,k}=O((nr^{d})^{k-1}I_{n,k})=O((nr^{d})^{k-1}\mathbb{E}S_{n,k})
, as claimed.

Next we claim that
by following the proof of Proposition
5.6
, one can show

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{TV}}(S_{n,k},Z_{\mathbb{E}S_{n,k}})=O((nr^{d})^{k-1}).

(6.5)

Indeed, for each
i\in[6]
we have
\mathbb{E}[N_{i}]=O((nr^{d})^{j})

with
j=j(i)\in\{k-1,k,k+1\}
. For
N_{6}
this can now be
seen directly without using the more involved
proof of Lemma
5.8
. In particular, the restriction to
d\geq 2

is not needed for (
6.5
).

By a similar argument to the proof of
(
5.5
), using (
6.5
) and (
6.4
),
we can obtain that

d_{\mathrm{K}}((\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}S_{n,k})^{-1/2}(S_{n,k}-\mathbb{E}S_{n,k}),\mathcal{N})=O(\max((nr^{d})^{k-1},(n(nr^{d})^{k-1})^{-1/2})).

Using (
6.3
) instead of (
6.5
) we can similarly
obtain that

d_{\mathrm{K}}((\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}S^{\prime}_{n,k})^{-1/2}(S^{\prime}_{n,k}-\mathbb{E}S^{\prime}_{n,k}),\mathcal{N})=O(\max((nr^{d})^{k},(n(nr^{d})^{k-1})^{-1/2})).
