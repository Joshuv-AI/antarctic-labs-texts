Accountability and Motivation
Liqun Liu

Abstract
We build a formal model that examines how different policymaking environments shape career-concerned officials’ reform decisions and implementation. When career concerns are strong, officials will inefficiently initiate reforms to signal to the central government that they are congruent. To improve the quality of reform policymaking, the central government must hold officials accountable to policy outcomes. We demonstrate that the central government can exercise this accountability by requiring officials to publicize policy outcomes while maintain secrecy on implementation details. In this situation, officials can signal their congruence only through a desirable policy outcome, so they are highly motivated to carry out a reform well. We also demonstrate that the accountability on policy outcomes is infeasible under alternative policymaking environments. We apply the results to China’s recent practice in decentralized reform policymaking. Keywords: Accountability; Motivation; Pandering; Transparency

Introduction

There is a widespread perception that decentralized policymaking, especially those regarding reform decisions, is a cause of China’s splendid economic growth since the Opening-up era. Under a hierarchical political system, decentralized policymaking confers at least two benefits compared with command and control. First, it allows the central government to diversify the risk embedded in reform decisions (e.g.,
Heilmann 2008b
;
Heilmann et al. 2013
). Second, it motivates policy innovation as well as discipline local officials to act in the public interest (e.g.,
Qian and Weingast 1997
). Indeed, China’s indigenous and successful policymaking methodology “proceeding from point to surface”– successful local initiatives can diffuse into the entire country – seems to corroborate with these scholarly wisdom. Should we therefore conclude that decentralization is the last word on China’s reform policymaking?

Before answering this question, we note several stylized facts that overshadow the optimism of decentralization. One is the ambiguous cadre promotion rule faced by local officials during reform policymaking. According to
Wang and Yang 2021
, it is the reform outcome rather than the reform decision that matters in local officials’ promotion. However, even if we do not account for the patronage network effects (e.g.,
Jiang 2018
), a desirable reform outcome seems unlikely to be local officials’ only ladder for success. Thanks to the short tenure (typically less than 3 years), local officials often move elsewhere before the outcomes of their policy initiatives are realized
(
Teets and Hasmath 2020
;
Zhu and Zhang 2016
)
.
This creates incentives for local officials to engage in the wasteful “face innovation”; that is, reform merely for the appearance of reform
(
Teets 2015
)
.

In fact, if local officials differ in competence, or if rival factions at the center have divided opinions about reform decisions
(
Cai and Treisman 2006
)
, then a “face innovation” has substantial signaling value for local officials’ future careers.

Another fact is the heterogeneous motives underlying local officials’ reform decisions. Local officials typically have different policy or ideological preferences in carrying out a reform, despite they are subject to the same promotion system
(
Teets et al. 2017
)
. For example, China’s market-oriented reforms often encountered resistance by conservative local policymakers
(
Cai and Treisman 2006
;
Heilmann 2008b
)
. For example, local actors may functionally deviate from central commands
(
Heilmann et al. 2013
;
Tsai 2006
;
Van Aken and Lewis 2015
)
. This alludes to the possibility that even a good reform may fail when it is left at the wrong hands.

The other fact concerns China’s nonnegligible institutional constraints in monitoring the implementation of policies. For example,
Wong 2012
notices that China’s auditing staffs are inadequately trained and low in numbers. Such a constraint can embolden local officials to engage in face innovation. To tackle deviations and ensure implementation loyalty, the central government recently requires local officials to report upward for approval before acting. But this measure can be costly, as it overloads local officials with extra administrative burdens
(
Teets et al. 2017
;
Chen 2017
)
. More importantly, local officials are discouraged from experimenting new ideas.
Chen and Göbel 2016
show that the number of reported policy experiments has declined from 100 in 2008 to around 50 in 2013, before plummeting to less than 10.

So, a salient challenge to China’s reform policymaking is how to hold local officials accountable for their reform decisions, implementation, and policy outcomes. By accountability, we mean the ability for the central government to credibly reward or punish
local officials if their policymaking meets or fails a set of explicit and implicit standards. The award can be monetary, such as an enlarged financial budget; or it can be political, such as a cadre promotion to higher offices. This brings us several research questions: which accountability is desirable for the sake of policymaking? How can the central government implement the desirable kind of accountability? Under what conditions should the central government recentralize its policymaking?

To answer these questions, we build a formal model of policymaking with career concerns. The model includes a local official and the central government. Prior to the game starts, the central government chooses to what extent it oversees the policymaking. Then, the local official decides between maintaining the status quo policy and initiating a reform; in the event of a reform, he also chooses the amount of costly implementation effort. Compared with the status quo, the reform is “risky”: it may fail either because it is a bad decision by nature, or because it is poorly implemented by the local official. Put differently,
“choosing well” and “implementing well” are complementary to a successful reform. The local official observes the nature of a reform, whereas the central government does not. Moreover, the local official does not necessarily share the same policy preference with the central government: with a positive probability, the official is a “congruent type,” who prefers a successful reform to the status quo to a failed reform just as the principal; with the complementary probability, the official is a “noncongruent type” who prefers the status quo to any reform outcome. At the end of the policymaking, the central government decides whether to promote the official or not conditional on available information.

Through equilibrium analysis, we identify instances in which
local official’s career concerns can hurt reform policymaking. Generally speaking, when career concerns are strong, the local official will use a reform decision to signal his congruence with the central government, regardless of whether the reform is actually good or bad. Since the ‘‘appearance’’ of reform matters, policymaking is distorted in two ways: many bad reforms are wrongly initiated, and many good reforms are badly implemented. Thus, to achieve high-quality policymaking, it seems necessary to hold the local official accountable for policy outcomes. That is, the central government should credibly commit to promoting (punishing) local officials who can successfully (unsuccessfully) implement a reform. However, committing to such an incentive scheme can be extremely difficult.

We show that if the central government merely observes the reform decision, or if it observes the reform decision, implementation details, and policy outcomes, then whether a reform succeeds or not does not affect the local official’s promotion. In other words, the accountability for policy outcomes fails in these circumstances. The idea is that in evaluating the local official, the central government places far more weights on observed actions than policy outcomes, which are nothing but the mechanical output induced by these actions. Accordingly, the local official’s attention is directed to appearing congruent rather than pursuing successful policymaking. This result explains why we observe many demotivated local officials and unsustainable face innovations.

Next, we demonstrate that career concerns can also benefit reform policymaking, provided that the central government chooses the right institution design. Notably, we show that the central government can hold local officials accountable for policy outcomes with “semi-transparency”: officials are required to publicize policy outcomes while maintain secrecy on implementation details (prior to a promotion decision is made).
To see this, policy outcomes will convey useful information about local officials’ policy motives when some parts of their actions are concealed from the central government. Since a congruent type is more motivated than a noncongruent type to carry out good policies, the central government will update positively (negatively) from a desirable (undesirable) policy outcome. This means that in equilibrium, the central government can credibly reward a successful reform as well as punish an unsuccessful one. Anticipating this, the local official becomes very diligent to implement a reform whenever possible, as a successful reform confers both political and policy payoffs. We show that under a wide range of circumstances, semi-transparency best serves the central government’s policy interest.
Substantively, the central government may want to extend the policy autonomy granted to local officials, and evaluate them on the basis of performance. Stricter controls,
such as requiring real time disclosure of policy progress and dispatching teams of monitors, can be counterproductive.

By studying the formal model and its extensions, we revisit two classic debates in policymaking. Regarding whether the central government should delegate reform decisions to local policymakers, our results resonate with the insights of delegation theory (e.g.,
Holmstrom 1977
and
Bendor et al. 2001
): if the benefit from informed policymaking outweighs the loss from being unable to implement its favorable policies, the central government should delegate policymaking. Importantly, we find that if the central government lacks the capacity to enforce implementation loyatlty, then centralization is strictly dominated. Regarding whether technical decisions should be left to accountable or nonacountable officials, our results complement
Maskin and Tirole 2004
. They show that nonaccountable officials are more motivated than nonaccountable officials to acquire costly
information about the right decision. We show that under the right incentive schemes, accountable officials may be more motivated than nonaccountable officials to exert costly implementation efforts.

Holmström 1979
has shown that under mild assumptions, a principal in a contractual relationship with an agent almost always benefits from observing more. Since then, scholars demonstrate that more information can be welfare reducing.

Parameter restrictions.

Now, we impose parameter restrictions to highlight the agent’s motivation problem. A noncongruent type averts reform policies due to antagonistic policy dispositions or ideological preferences. Nonetheless, such a type is willing to engage in face innovation for career purposes. We suppose

. At least within the context of China’s reform policymaking, the central government almost always observes whether a reform decision has been carried out locally.

Solution concept.
The solution concept is Perfect Bayesian Equilibrium (PBE). It requires the principal to form
beliefs about agent’s type
t

8
8

8

For example, if the principal does not observe the reform details and consequences, an equilibrium exists in which the agent maintains the status quo, supported by the off-path belief that whoever reforms is noncongruent. This equilibrium is nonetheless unsatisfying, because a congruent agent always benefits more from a reform than a noncongruent type. The divinity refinement rules out such a possibility.
Loosely speaking, the divinity criterion requires that whenever an unexpected action occurs, it comes from the type of agent who is most likely to benefit from it.

Analysis

In this section, we first compare the agent’s equilibrium behaviors in policymaking under three information structures. After that, we analyze the principal-optimal information structure. We conclude with discussing a few extensions.

Now, we compare the strategic interactions under different information structures. Generally, career concerns give rise to pandering incentives. More transparency deters the noncongruent type from signaling congruence via costly means, which makes it easier for the principal to sort types. However, if career concerns are overwhelmingly strong (
R>c+v
), then more transparency may not improve sorting.

Importantly, we do not observe a monotone pattern between transparency and the quality of policymaking. In fact, if motivating implementation plays a central role in policymaking, the principal should back away from full or no transparency. To provide an intuition for this result, note that the (distribution of) policy outcome is fully determined by the agent’s set of actions. When these actions are observed, the induced policy outcomes do not convey any additional information about the agent’s private type. Thus, under
Full-Transparency
and
Non-Transparency
, the principal is unlikely to condition her promotion decision on the policy outcomes. This means that only under
Semi-Transparency
can the agent’s policy and political payoffs be linked.

FT

FT
),
two kinds of distortion may arise: the congruent type undersupplies the implementation effort to save on the cost, and the noncongruent type inefficiently chooses the reform decision. So, if face innovation is not a terrible problem compared with the status quo (
\underline{e}
is large), or if the agent is sufficiently likely to be congruent (
\pi

Conversely, if face innovation is doomed to fail (
\underline{e}

\pi

\overline{e}
.

Before proceeding, we relate our results to prior works. Existing theories debate about whether “transparency on action” or “transparency on consequence” will improve the quality of policymaking.
Prat 2005
shows that transparency on consequence almost always improves policymaking, because it encourages career-concerned political agent to choose the best course of actions.
Fox and Van Weelden 2012
show that the result is sensitive to the cost structure of policy mistakes. Under certain conditions, it is never optimal to have transparency on both action and consequences. One key assumption driving the theoretical results is that one cannot perfectly deduce the agent’s action from the policy outcomes. Thus, making actions unobservable can reduce the agent’s incentive to signal smartness by pandering to the ex ante popular action.

However, their results do not fully carry through to reform policymaking, particularly in the China context. One obvious reason is that the central government can almost always observe the reform decisions. More importantly, from reform policymaking, one can always deduce whether a reform has taken place from any one of the three potential outcomes “successful reform,” “unsuccessful reform,” and “status quo.” Since transparency on outcome is almost always a part of the optimal institution design, the discussion about “transparency on the reform decision” is redundant. So, in the context of reform policymaking, the real focus seems to be whether the central government should allow for transparency on implementation. In fact, the “System of Requesting Instructions and Submitting Reports” is designed for this purpose.

3.3
Discussions

In this section, we revisit several key assumptions of the baseline model.

Alternative information structure

In the baseline model, we omit a plausible information structure where the principal observes the policy decision and implementation efforts, but not the consequences. As we have explicated in Section 3.1, the observed actions rather than their induced policy outcomes contain all relevant information for the principal’s promotion decision. In this situation, the agent faces exactly the same policy and career incentives as under
Full-Transparency
. Thus, studying this information structure would be repetitive. For policy outcomes to be relevant for the promotion decision, a necessary condition is that the principal cannot observe the agent’s entire set of actions.

Generality

We keep the baseline model parsimonious to deliver the core insights. In the Supplementary Appendix, we study a more general version of the model, featuring an imbalanced prior for the state
\omega
, a continuum of effort
e
with a convex cost, and the agent is imperfectly informed about the state. We show that, under benign conditions, motivation is made plausible only under
Semi-Transparency
, for reasons we have explicated in the baseline model. Moreover, in terms of welfare, the principal favors
Semi-Transparency
if motivating implementation is overwhelmingly important. This extension indicates that our results are indeed robust.

4
Policy Implications

In this section, we discuss the policy implications of the model.

4.1
Delegation vs centralization

Since 2017, China has started to limit the policy discretion of local governments. While this practice is meant to ensure implementation loyalty, scholars are concerned about the possibility that the ongoing recentralization process may eventually reverse to command and control. Historically, China’s policy experimentation had taken a top-down approach. For example,
Hongqi
, a political theory journal published by the Chinese Communist Party, wrote “All local efforts were to be unified in executing every new policy or ideological clue that came from the Party Center” in 1959 (quoted in
Heilmann 2008a
). To examine how such a command and control practice may affect the quantity and quality of policy innovation in China, we add “centralization” as a possible institution design to the baseline model. Under centralization, the (less informed) central government mandates the policy choice between a reform and the status quo, whereas local officials implement the policy. To make the results more general, we suppose that the reform is good by nature with probability
p
(i.e.,
P(\omega=G)=p
).

Since the central government does not observe the state of a reform
\omega
, it enacts policies depending on whether the prior on
\omega
favors a reform or the status quo.
So, even if policies can be perfectly implemented by local officials, centralization prevents the central government from adapting the policy to the state
\omega
. Thus, the central government should delegate reform decisions whenever informed policymaking really matters. Such an observation resonates with numerous scholarly works in the theory of delegation (e.g.,
Holmstrom 1980
;
Bendor et al. 2001
).

More importantly, in the event of a reform, centralization may prevent the central government from eliciting sufficient implementation efforts from local officials. Such demotivation happens because local officials cannot signal their loyalty or competence by executing mandated actions from higher offices. When asked to implement a reform in a situation where the efforts are unverifiable,
local officials are high likely to engage in face innovation, which is clearly suboptimal for policymaking. Indeed, within a hierarchical political system, the central government can resolve the shirking problem by strictly monitoring and auditing policy implementation. However, to put such a draconian measure into practice, the central government has to assemble a team of monitors with necessarily policy expertise to identify the deviants, which can be quite costly
(
Wong 2012
)
.

By contrast, delegation with a moderate control can hold local officials accountable to policy outcomes. Notably, the local official behaves as an almost-perfect agent under
Semi-Transparency
: he chooses policies responsively to the state of a reform, and implements a reform diligently whenever necessary. The only downside is that sometimes the central government may lose control over a bad type who undersupplies effort in a reform. If this issue is minor, either because most of local officials embrace reforms, or because the loss of control over the bad types is not serious, then the central government will benefit from delegating reform decisions.

4.2
Accountability and motivation

Another policy debate has to do with whether policymaking should be left to accountable or nonaccountable officials. According to
Maskin and Tirole 2004
,
nonaccountable officials are more motivated than accountable officials to acquire costly information about the optimal decision.
It so happens because accountable officials can lose jobs if they choose the informed decision rather than the popular decision, whereas nonaccountable officials are immune from this career concern. Thus, allocating decision-making powers to nonaccountable officials can be preferable if motivating information acquisition matters in policymaking.

Our analysis complements the findings of
Maskin and Tirole 2004
about motivation. Specifically, we demonstrate that accountability makes motivating implementation possible. Unaccountable officials are unlikely to implement a reform at the social-optimal level, as they often privately bear the cost of implementation efforts. By contrast, accountable officials who are up for election or promotion are
more willing to exert implementation efforts. In the baseline model, we show how the central government can encourage implementation by committing to a performance-based cadre promotion rule that links local officials’ policy and political payoffs. Substantively, if local officials already know how to choose well, accountability provides an incentive to motivate their implementation of policies.

We summarize the findings in the next Proposition:

Proposition 3
.

1.

If informed policymaking matters, or if monitoring is costly, then
centralization hurts policymaking.

2.

If the central government prioritizes on motivating reform implementation, then reform decisions should be allocated to accountable officials.

5
Conclusion

China’s decentralized reform policymaking, or “experimentation under hierarchy,” is often seen as the driver to China’s growth during the past decades. Recently, as China’s central government started to tighten the grip over local governments, the number of policy innovations has declined sharply. Motivated by these facts, we build a formal model that examines how different policymaking environments shape
career-concerned officials’ reform decisions and implementation. We find that under benign conditions, the central government achieves the best policy outcome by exercising a moderate level of control over local officials. That is, the central government should delegate reform decisions to the more informed local officials, back away from knowing the details of implementation, and evaluate local officials solely based on the policy outcomes. Such a “semi-transparent” policymaking environment enables the central government to commit to a performance-based promotion rule, thereby providing local officials with high-powered incentives to implement a reform policy well. We also show that the motivation effect cannot exist when the central government uses an alternative policymaking environment. Our findings are robust to a number of extensions.

Our results contribute to the theory of policymaking in several ways. First, it sheds new light on the classic debate between delegation versus centralization. We show that if uninformed policymaking is more serious a problem than the loss of control over misaligned local officials, or if the central government cannot monitor the implementation of reform at a low cost, then delegation is preferred. Second, we identify another potential benefit of accountability compared with nonaccountability: it makes motivating implementation possible. By exercising a moderate level of control, the central government can provide local officials with the right incentives to exert substantial implementation efforts. Third, we identify different accountabilities under different institution designs. We show that policymaking is surely distorted if local officials are only accountable to their policy decisions. Depending on whether motivating implementation or minimizing policy risks should be prioritized, the central government may or may not want to back away from full transparency to exercise accountability on policy outcomes.

How should we relate the theoretical results to China’s reform policymaking? One straightforward interpretation seems an advocacy for relaxing the control over local officials. As is well known, in the realm of politics, motivating local officials to work hard by committing to an explicit incentive contract can be difficult. In this paper, we demonstrate that the central government can credibly commit to a performance-based cadre promotion rule, provided that it allows for more local autonomy in policymaking. So, our results lend further support to delegation: when motivation is indispensable for policymaking, it is often a good idea for the central government to maintain an arm’s length relationship with local officials.
