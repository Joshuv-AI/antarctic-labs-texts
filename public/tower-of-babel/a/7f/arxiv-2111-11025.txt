On immersed boundary kernel functions: a constrained quadratic minimization perspective
Amneet Pal Singh Bhalla

Abstract
In the immersed boundary (IB) approach to fluid-structure interaction modeling, the coupling between the fluid and structure variables is mediated using a regularized version of Dirac delta function. In the IB literature, the regularized delta functions, also referred to IB kernel functions, are either derived analytically from a set of postulates or computed numerically using the moving least squares (MLS) approach. Whereas the analytical derivations typically assume a regular Cartesian grid, the MLS method is a meshless technique that can be used to generate kernel functions on complex domains and unstructured meshes. In this note we take a viewpoint that IB kernel generation, either analytically or via MLS, is a constrained quadratic minimization problem. The extremization of a constrained quadratic function is a broader concept than kernel generation and there are well-established numerical optimization techniques to solve this problem. For example, we show that the constrained quadratic minimization technique can be used to generate one-sided (anisotropic) IB kernels and/or to bound their values.



1
Introduction

The immersed boundary (IB) method was originally proposed by Peskin to model blood flow around heart valves
[
1
]
. In the IB formulation, the fluid occupying the entire computational domain is described by Eulerian variables, whereas
the immersed structure that is allowed to freely cut the background Eulerian grid is described by Lagrangian variables. The presence of the immersed structure leads to an additional body force term in the momentum equation. The Lagrangian structure moves with the local fluid velocity and satisfies the no-slip boundary condition on the fluid-structure interface. The Eulerian-Lagrangian coupling in the IB formulation is mediated using regularized integral kernels. Specifically, IB forces defined on the marker points are
spread
to the Eulerian grid from the Lagrangian structure, and conversely, the fluid velocity is
interpolated
from the Eulerian mesh to the marker points. The use of an isotropic and regularized IB kernel
diffuses
the fluid-structure interface to nearby grid cells and this smearing of the interface is proportional to the width of the kernel. This also implies that the (usual) isotropic IB kernels couple the structural degrees of freedom to fluid variables on both sides of the interface.

In the literature, IB kernels have been derived either analytically or computed numerically via the moving least squares (MLS) approach. Peskin
[
2
]
used a set of postulates to derive analytical expressions for several IB kernels such as the four-, five-, and six-point function. These postulates aim to achieve several desirable features in an IB kernel, such as polynomial reproducing conditions (also known as the discrete moment conditions), grid translational invariance property, and compact kernel support. More recently, Bao et al.
[
3
]
used a weaker second moment condition to derive improved versions of the standard five-, and six-point IB kernels. A weaker second moment condition allowed the authors to remove the negative tails of the standard five- and six-point kernels, and to enhance their gird translational invariance property. A main limitation of the analytical approach is the assumption of a regular Cartesian grid and the tensor-product form of the kernel function in dimensions higher than one. To circumvent these limitations, Vanella and Balaras introduced the MLS approach to generate IB kernel functions
[
4
]
. Although the authors in
[
4
]
demonstrated the IB/MLS approach on a regular Cartesian grid, the technique is easily extendable to unstructured grid-based IB methods as shown by Krishnan et al.
[
5
]
and Saadat et al.
[
6
]
. In this work we take a viewpoint that IB kernel generation is a constrained quadratic
minimization problem and both analytical and MLS formulations can be described using the language of optimization.

In the prior IB/MLS works
[
4
,
7
,
8
,
5
,
9
,
6
,
10
]
, a standard formulation of the moving least squares method was used to transform a cubic spline weight function into a regularized IB kernel function that satisfies zeroth- and first-order discrete moment conditions. The standard MLS formulation minimizes the weighted
L^{2}
-norm of the difference between the interpolant and the given data values. A lesser known formulation of the moving least squares problem (in the IB literature) is the Backus-Gilbert theory, which was developed in the context of geophysics
[
11
]
. In the Backus-Gilbert formulation, polynomial reproducing conditions are used in the MLS problem definition. However, both formulations ultimately lead to the same IB kernel function as shown in this work. The connection between the two MLS formulations was first pointed out by Bos and Šalkauskas in
[
12
]
and re-iterated here in the context of constrained quadratic minimization. We also show that the constrained quadratic minimization approach can be used to generate discrete moment conditions satisfying anisotropic IB kernels that effectively couple structural degrees of freedom to fluid variables on only one side of the fluid-structure interface. This can help to reduce spurious feedback forcing and “leakage" that are typically observed in the diffuse-interface IB models. Furthermore, the constrained quadratic minimization method can be used to bound the weights of the IB kernels.

In the following sections, we first review the two formulations of the moving least squares problem and describe them using the language of optimization. Next, we review the set of postulates used by Peskin to derive the four-point IB kernel and frame it into the language of constrained quadratic minimization. Finally, we describe methods to generate one-sided and/or bounded IB kernels and demonstrate this process using a two-dimensional example.

2
Moving least squares method

2.1
The standard formulation

The standard formulation of the moving least squares method considers the following
approximation
problem. Given a (column) vector of data values
\bm{\mathrm{g}}=(g(\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{1}),\ldots,g(\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{N}))^{\intercal}
, defined on
N
distinct data sites
\bm{\mathcal{X}}=\{\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{N}\}
by a (smooth) function
g
, find the quasi-interpolant
{\mathcal{P}}g
to
g

{\mathcal{P}}g(\bm{\mathrm{x}})=\sum_{j=1}^{m}c_{j}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})p_{j}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})=\bm{\mathrm{p}}^{\intercal}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\bm{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}),

(1)

at an
evaluation
point
\bm{\mathrm{x}}
using the polynomial approximation space
\mathbb{P}=\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{m}\}
. Typically,
m\ll N
and the coefficient vector
\bm{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
in Eq. (
1
) is found by minimizing the weighted
L^{2}
-norm of the error function
J(\bm{\mathrm{c}})

J(\bm{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))=\sum_{i=1}^{N}W(\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{i},\bm{\mathrm{x}})[\bm{\mathrm{p}}^{\intercal}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{i})\bm{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})-g(\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{i})]^{2},

(2)

with respect to
\bm{\mathrm{c}}
. In the equation above,
W(\bm{\mathrm{x}})=W(\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{i},\bm{\mathrm{x}})
is a given weight function which decreases in magnitude away from the evaluation point and is taken to be non-negative. The error function
J
is minimized using the
essential
condition of extremum,
\frac{\partial J}{\partial\bm{\mathrm{c}}}=\bm{\mathrm{0}}
, which leads to the normal equation of the form

\sum_{j=1}^{m}c_{j}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\left<p_{j},p_{k}\right>_{W(\bm{\mathrm{x}})}=\left<g,p_{k}\right>_{W(\bm{\mathrm{x}})},\quad k=1,\ldots,m.

(3)

Here,
\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>_{W(\bm{\mathrm{x}})}
denotes the weighted inner product of two quantities. If
\bm{\mathrm{\mathcal{A}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
denotes the polynomial matrix with entries
\bm{\mathrm{\mathcal{A}}}_{ij}=p_{i}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{j}),i=1,\ldots,m,j=1,\ldots,N
, and
\bm{\mathrm{\mathcal{W}}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})=\text{diag}\left(\bm{\mathrm{W}}\right)
is the diagonal matrix containing weights
(with
\bm{\mathrm{W}}=\left[W(\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{1},\bm{\mathrm{x}}),\dots,W(\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{N},\bm{\mathrm{x}})\right]
as the main diagonal of
\bm{\mathrm{\mathcal{W}}}
), then in matrix notation Eq. (
3
) reads as

\bm{\mathrm{\mathcal{G}}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\bm{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})=\bm{\mathrm{g}}_{p}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}).

(4)

Here,
\bm{\mathrm{\mathcal{G}}}
is the positive-definite Gram matrix
\bm{\mathrm{\mathcal{G}}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})=\bm{\mathrm{\mathcal{A}}}\bm{\mathrm{\mathcal{W}}}\bm{\mathrm{\mathcal{A}}}^{\intercal}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
and
\bm{\mathrm{g}}_{p}
is the right-hand side vector
\bm{\mathrm{g}}_{p}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})=\bm{\mathrm{\mathcal{A}}}\bm{\mathrm{\mathcal{W}}}\bm{\mathrm{g}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Note that the coefficient vector
\bm{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})=\bm{\mathrm{\mathcal{G}}}^{-1}\bm{\mathrm{g}}_{p}
, Gram matrix
\bm{\mathrm{\mathcal{G}}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
, and the right hand side vector
\bm{\mathrm{g}}_{p}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
, all depend on the evaluation point
\bm{\mathrm{x}}
. Consequently, the normal Eq. (
4
) is solved anew each time the evaluation point is changed or “moved."

In the context of the immersed boundary method, the standard MLS formulation is used to interpolate velocity from Eulerian grid nodes to a Lagrangian marker point and to spread IB force from a Lagrangian marker point to the surrounding Eulerian nodes
[
4
]
. This is achieved by considering a particular Lagrangian marker point as the current evaluation point and the surrounding Eulerian grid nodes (to the Lagrangian point) as the distinct data sites that provide velocity or force data. The IB/MLS kernel function
\bm{\mathrm{\Psi}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})=\left(\psi_{1}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}),\ldots,\psi_{N}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\right)^{\intercal}
, also known as the
generating function
, is obtained by combining Eqs. (
1
) and (
3
)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}g(\bm{\mathrm{x}})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\psi_{i}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})g(\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{i})=\bm{\mathrm{g}}^{\intercal}\bm{\mathrm{\Psi}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})

\displaystyle=\bm{\mathrm{p}}^{\intercal}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\bm{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})=\bm{\mathrm{g}}^{\intercal}\bm{\mathrm{\mathcal{W}}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\bm{\mathrm{\mathcal{A}}}^{\intercal}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\bm{\mathrm{\mathcal{G}}}^{-1}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\bm{\mathrm{p}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}).

(5)

Therefore, from the equation above we get

\bm{\mathrm{\Psi}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})=\bm{\mathrm{\mathcal{W}}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\bm{\mathrm{\mathcal{A}}}^{\intercal}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\bm{\mathrm{\mathcal{G}}}^{-1}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\bm{\mathrm{p}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}).

(6)

Comparing the two forms of the quasi-interpolant
{\mathcal{P}}g
in Eqs. (
1
) and (
6
), it is clear that the latter form employs a kernel function
\bm{\mathrm{\Psi}}=\bm{\mathrm{\Psi}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
that depends only on the evaluation point
\bm{\mathrm{x}}
. In contrast, the former form uses a coefficient vector
\bm{\mathrm{c}}=\bm{\mathrm{\mathcal{G}}}^{-1}\bm{\mathrm{g}}_{p}
, which depends on both the evaluation point
\bm{\mathrm{x}}
and the data values
\bm{\mathrm{g}}
, i.e.,
\bm{\mathrm{c}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}};\bm{\mathrm{g}})
. For IB methods, it is therefore more convenient to work with
\bm{\mathrm{\Psi}}
directly, as it allows the same kernel function to be used in both velocity interpolation and force spreading operations. It can be shown that using the same IB kernel function in the two operators conserve energy during Lagrangian-Eulerian coupling
[
2
]
.

Although, it is not explicitly enforced in the error minimization of
J
, the standard MLS method will reproduce the polynomial basis functions
p_{1},\ldots,p_{m}
, i.e.,

{\mathcal{P}}p_{j}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})=\bm{\mathrm{p}}^{\intercal}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\bm{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}};\bm{\mathrm{p}}_{j})=\bm{\mathrm{p}}_{j}^{\intercal}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{i})\bm{\mathrm{\Psi}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})=p_{j}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}),\quad\quad\forall p_{j}\in\mathbb{P}.

(7)

We shall prove this property of standard MLS formulation in Sec.
2.2
.

Next, we show that the standard MLS formulation can be viewed as a constrained quadratic minimization problem of the form

\text{Problem A}=\begin{cases}\min\limits_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}&J\;=\;\frac{1}{2}\,\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{\intercal}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\bm{\mathrm{\mathcal{G}}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\bm{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\\
\text{subject to:}&\bm{\mathrm{\mathcal{G}}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\bm{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})=\bm{\mathrm{g}}_{p}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}).\end{cases}

(8)

Note that Problem A is a minimization problem because
\bm{\mathrm{\mathcal{G}}}
is a (symmetric) positive-definite matrix. The above constrained optimization problem can be solved by introducing the Lagrangian function
{\mathcal{L}}
, with the help of a Lagrange multiplier
\bm{\mathrm{\lambda}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}

{\mathcal{L}}=\frac{1}{2}\,\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{\intercal}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\bm{\mathrm{\mathcal{G}}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\bm{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})-\bm{\mathrm{\lambda}}^{\intercal}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})[\bm{\mathrm{\mathcal{G}}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\bm{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})-\bm{\mathrm{g}}_{p}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})].

(9)

Using the
essential
conditions of extrema,
\frac{\partial{\mathcal{L}}}{\partial\bm{\mathrm{c}}}=\bm{\mathrm{0}}
and
\frac{\partial{\mathcal{L}}}{\partial\bm{\mathrm{\lambda}}}=\bm{\mathrm{0}}
, we get the following equalities

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{\mathcal{G}}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\bm{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})

\displaystyle=\bm{\mathrm{\mathcal{G}}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\bm{\mathrm{\lambda}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}),

(10)

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{\mathcal{G}}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\bm{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})

\displaystyle=\bm{\mathrm{g}}_{p}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}),

(11)

respectively. Solving the above two equations, we obtain
\bm{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})=\bm{\mathrm{\lambda}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})=\bm{\mathrm{\mathcal{G}}}^{-1}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\bm{\mathrm{g}}_{p}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
. Hence, the solutions to the standard MLS formulation and Problem A are the same.

2.2
The Backus-Gilbert formulation

The Backus-Gilbert formulation of the moving least squares method seeks the quasi-interpolant of the type

{\mathcal{P}}g(\bm{\mathrm{x}})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}g(\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{i})\psi_{i}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}),

(12)

that satisfies the polynomial reproducing conditions

\sum_{i=1}^{N}p(\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{i})\psi_{i}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})=p(\bm{\mathrm{x}}),\quad\quad\forall p\in\mathbb{P}.

(13)

The polynomial reproduction constraints correspond to the
discrete moment conditions
for the function
\psi_{i}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
. Eq. (
13
) in matrix form reads as
\bm{\mathrm{\mathcal{A}}}\bm{\mathrm{\Psi}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})=\bm{\mathrm{p}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
, in which
\bm{\mathrm{\mathcal{A}}}
is the same polynomial matrix as defined in the previous section
2.1
. The desired generating function
\bm{\mathrm{\Psi}}
in the Backus-Gilbert formulation is obtained by solving the following constrained quadratic minimization problem:

\text{Problem B}=\begin{cases}\min\limits_{\bm{\mathrm{\Psi}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}&J\;=\;\frac{1}{2}\,\bm{\mathrm{\Psi}}^{\intercal}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\bm{\mathrm{\mathcal{W}}}^{-1}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\bm{\mathrm{\Psi}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\\
\text{subject to:}&\bm{\mathrm{\mathcal{A}}}\bm{\mathrm{\Psi}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})=\bm{\mathrm{p}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}).\end{cases}

(14)

The solution to Problem B is obtained by minimizing the Lagrangian function
{\mathcal{L}}
, given by

{\mathcal{L}}=\frac{1}{2}\,\bm{\mathrm{\Psi}}^{\intercal}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\bm{\mathrm{\mathcal{W}}}^{-1}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\bm{\mathrm{\Psi}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})-\bm{\mathrm{\lambda}}^{\intercal}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})[\bm{\mathrm{\mathcal{A}}}\bm{\mathrm{\Psi}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})-\bm{\mathrm{p}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})],

(15)

in which
\bm{\mathrm{\lambda}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the Lagrange multiplier. The Lagrangian
{\mathcal{L}}
is minimized by using the essential conditions of extrema,
\frac{\partial{\mathcal{L}}}{\partial\bm{\mathrm{\Psi}}}=\bm{\mathrm{0}}
and
\frac{\partial{\mathcal{L}}}{\partial\bm{\mathrm{\lambda}}}=\bm{\mathrm{0}}
, which yields the following equalities

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{\mathcal{W}}}^{-1}\bm{\mathrm{\Psi}}-\bm{\mathrm{\mathcal{A}}}^{\intercal}\bm{\mathrm{\lambda}}

\displaystyle=\bm{\mathrm{0}},

(16)

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{\mathcal{A}}}\bm{\mathrm{\Psi}}-\bm{\mathrm{p}}

\displaystyle=\bm{\mathrm{0}}.

(17)

respectively. Solving the above two equations, we obtain

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{\lambda}}

\displaystyle=\left(\bm{\mathrm{\mathcal{A}}}\bm{\mathrm{\mathcal{W}}}\bm{\mathrm{\mathcal{A}}}^{\intercal}\right)^{-1}\bm{\mathrm{p}}=\bm{\mathrm{\mathcal{G}}}^{-1}\bm{\mathrm{p}},

(18)

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{\Psi}}

\displaystyle=\bm{\mathrm{\mathcal{W}}}\bm{\mathrm{\mathcal{A}}}^{\intercal}\bm{\mathrm{\lambda}}=\bm{\mathrm{\mathcal{W}}}\bm{\mathrm{\mathcal{A}}}^{\intercal}\left(\bm{\mathrm{\mathcal{A}}}\bm{\mathrm{\mathcal{W}}}\bm{\mathrm{\mathcal{A}}}^{\intercal}\right)^{-1}\bm{\mathrm{p}}=\bm{\mathrm{\mathcal{W}}}\bm{\mathrm{\mathcal{A}}}^{\intercal}\bm{\mathrm{\mathcal{G}}}^{-1}\bm{\mathrm{p}}.

(19)

Comparing Eqs. (
19
) and (
6
), it is clear that both formulations of the moving least squares method yield the same quasi-interpolant
{\mathcal{P}}g
to function
g
. Since the polynomial reproduction constraints are directly included in the quadratic function minimization used to solve Problem B, the equivalence of the two formulations implies that the standard moving least squares method also reproduces the polynomial basis functions.

In the IB literature, constant and linear polynomials are typically employed in the moving least squares formulation. Defining the polynomials relative to the evaluation point
\bm{\mathrm{x}}=(\bar{x},\bar{y},\bar{z})
, the set of basis functions
\mathbb{P}
for IB problems consists of

\mathbb{P}=\{1,x-\bar{x},y-\bar{y},z-\bar{z}\}.

(20)

Note that if the weight function
W(\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{i},\bm{\mathrm{x}})
already satisfies the discrete moment conditions on the support of the IB/MLS kernel, then the constrained quadratic minimization would result in a trivial solution
\bm{\mathrm{\Psi}}=\bm{\mathrm{\mathcal{W}}}
. Consequently, the product
\bm{\mathrm{\mathcal{A}}}^{\intercal}\bm{\mathrm{\lambda}}
in Eq. (
19
) produces a vector whose entries are all one.

3
Peskin’s IB kernel

If the IB simulations are performed on a regular Cartesian grid (which is often the case), then Peskin’s delta functions can be used instead of the IB/MLS kernels described earlier. Peskin’s delta functions satisfy the polynomial reproducing conditions, can be expressed compactly in a piecewise analytical manner, and furthermore, they do not explicitly depend upon a particular evaluation/marker point. The latter property is due to the fact that Peskin’s delta functions are constructed in terms of relative distance between the data site and the evaluation point, i.e,
\psi=\psi(|\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{i}-\bm{\mathrm{x}}|)
and
\psi\neq\psi(\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{i},\bm{\mathrm{x}})
.

Taking the example of widely popular four-point delta function
\psi_{4}
from the IB literature, we list the four postulates used by Peskin for its construction below:

\displaystyle(i)\text{ Zeroth moment:}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\sum_{i=1}^{N}\psi_{4}(|\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{i}-\bm{\mathrm{x}}|)=1,

\displaystyle(ii)\text{ Even-odd:}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\sum_{i\;\text{even}}\psi_{4}(|\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{i}-\bm{\mathrm{x}}|)=\sum_{i\;\text{odd}}\psi_{4}(|\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{i}-\bm{\mathrm{x}}|)=\frac{1}{2},

\displaystyle(iii)\text{ First moment:}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{i}-\bm{\mathrm{x}})\psi_{4}(|\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{i}-\bm{\mathrm{x}}|)=0,

\displaystyle(iv)\text{ Sum-of-squares:}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\sum_{i=1}^{N}\psi_{4}^{2}(|\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{i}-\bm{\mathrm{x}}|)=\left(\frac{3}{8}\right)^{d}.\quad\quad(d\text{ denotes the spatial dimension})

Note that the even-odd condition in the second postulate implies the zeroth moment condition (or the constant reproducing condition), and therefore, the first equation is not explicitly used in deriving
\psi_{4}
. The first moment condition in the third postulate is the linear polynomial reproducing condition, whereas the sum-of-squares condition in the last postulate is a weaker condition on grid translational invariance.

Similar to the IB/MLS kernel formulation, Peskin’s four-point delta function can be formulated as a constrained quadratic minimization problem. The minimization problem reads as

\text{Problem C}=\begin{cases}\min\limits_{\bm{\mathrm{\Psi}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}&J\;=\;\frac{1}{2}\,\bm{\mathrm{\Psi}}^{\intercal}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\bm{\mathrm{\mathcal{W}}}^{-1}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\bm{\mathrm{\Psi}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\\
\text{subject to:}&(\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{i}-\bm{\mathrm{x}})^{\intercal}\bm{\mathrm{\Psi}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})=\bm{\mathrm{0}},\\
&\bm{\mathrm{1}}_{\rm even}^{\intercal}\bm{\mathrm{\Psi}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})=\frac{1}{2},\\
&\bm{\mathrm{1}}_{\rm odd}^{\intercal}\bm{\mathrm{\Psi}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})=\frac{1}{2},\\
&\bm{\mathrm{\Psi}}^{\intercal}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\bm{\mathrm{\Psi}}=\left(\frac{3}{8}\right)^{d},\end{cases}

(21)

in which
\bm{\mathrm{1}}_{\rm even}
is a (column) vector containing the values one and zero at even- and odd-indexed data sites, respectively.
\bm{\mathrm{1}}_{\rm odd}
is defined analogously. Note that Problem C has a nonlinear equality constraint, which would require a nonlinear optimizer, such as the interior point algorithm
[
13
]
or the sequential quadratic programming technique
[
14
]
.

4
One-sided and bounded IB kernels

Next, we show that the constrained quadratic minimization approach can help to generate IB kernels that effectively couple structural degrees of freedom to fluid variables on only one side of the fluid-structure interface. Such interpolation kernels have been termed “one-sided IB kernels" by Bale et al.
[
15
]
, who have recently proposed them. The one-sided IB kernels help to reduce spurious feedback forcing and internal flows that are typically observed in IB models that use isotropic kernel functions which couple the structure to fluid degrees of freedom on both sides of the interface
[
15
]
. However, such kernels have large positive and negative weights, which can lead to numerical instability over time. To mitigate the numerical instability the authors in
[
15
]
mollified the one-sided IB kernel weights at the expense of the first moment condition. In contrast, here we show that the numerical values of the one-sided kernel function can be bounded, e.g., made positive or constrained within a smaller range using the constrained quadratic minimization approach, while still satisfying the first moment condition.

Summary and Conclusions

In this note we took a viewpoint that IB kernel generation, either analytically or via MLS, is a constrained quadratic minimization problem. The extremization of a constrained quadratic function is a broader concept than kernel generation and there are well-established numerical optimization techniques to solve this problem. We also reviewed the two formulations of the moving least squares problem and described them using the language of optimization. Further, the set of postulates used by Peskin to derive the four-point IB kernel was also described using this language. Finally, we described methods to generate one-sided and/or bounded IB kernels and demonstrated the process using a two-dimensional example. Four constrained quadratic minimization problems were described, namely, the Problem A, B, C, and D. All but Problem C involved linear constraints and can be solved using efficient optimization techniques such as the quadratic programming technique. For Problem C, a nonlinear optimizer, such as the interior point algorithm or the sequential quadratic programming technique is required.
