Performance boost of a collective qutrit refrigerator
Dmytro Kolisnyk 1,2, Gernot Schaller 2

Abstract
A single qutrit with transitions selectively driven by weakly-coupled reservoirs can implement one of the world’s smallest refrigerators. We analyze the performance of N N such fridges that are collectively coupled to the reservoirs. We observe a quantum boost, manifest in a quadratic scaling of the steady-state cooling current with N N . As N N grows further, the scaling reduces to linear, since the transitions responsible for the quantum boost become energetically unfavorable. Fine-tuned inter-qutrit interactions may be used to maintain the quantum boost for all N N and also for not-perfectly collective scenarios.

While for these background noise

This can for example be exploited in the collective charging of quantum batteries

Also finite-stroke quantum thermodynamic cycles have been analyzed with collective quantum working fluids

Our generic starting point is a total Hamiltonian of the form

\displaystyle\hat{H}=\hat{H}_{S}+\sum_{\nu}\hat{S}^{\nu}\otimes\hat{B}^{\nu}+\sum_{\nu}\hat{H}_{B}^{\nu}

(1)

that is composed of a system part
\hat{H}_{S}
, different reservoirs
\hat{H}_{B}^{\nu}
and the corresponding interactions written as products of bath operators
\hat{B}^{\nu}
that couple to different system operators
\hat{S}^{\nu}
.
In this paper, we will consider three reservoirs
\nu\in\{c,h,w\}
that we label cold, hot, and work (hottest) reservoirs, respectively.
These reservoirs are modeled by standard harmonic oscillator baths
\hat{H}_{B}^{\nu}=\sum_{k}\omega_{k\nu}\hat{b}_{k\nu}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{k\nu}
throughout.

An introductory example for the system could be a single three-level model (qutrit) with ground state
\left|0\right>
, first excited state
\left|1\right>
, and most excited state
\left|2\right>
, for which we could write

\hat{H}_{S}=\Delta\left|2\right>\left<2\right|+\delta\left|1\right>\left<1\right|
,
where
\Delta>\delta>0
are the excitation energies and we have gauged the ground state energy to zero.
We consider the case where the coupling between system and reservoir
drives the individual system transitions exclusively, e.g. for the single qutrit via

\hat{S}^{c}=[\left|0\right>\left<1\right|+\left|1\right>\left<0\right|]
,

\hat{S}^{h}=[\left|0\right>\left<2\right|+\left|2\right>\left<0\right|]
, and

\hat{S}^{w}=[\left|1\right>\left<2\right|+\left|2\right>\left<1\right|]
, compare Fig.
1
bottom left panel.
In the appropriate regime (reservoirs held in thermal states with inverse temperatures obeying
\beta_{c}>\beta_{h}>\beta_{w}
and the cooling condition
\beta_{h}\Delta>\beta_{w}(\Delta-\delta)+\beta_{c}\delta
), the single-qutrit model implements a quantum absorption refrigerator (QAR):
A device that implements stochastic cooling by on average absorbing heat from the coldest (
c
) and hottest (
w
) reservoirs and dumping the waste heat into the intermediate temperature (
h
) reservoir.
We provide more details on the working principles of this configuration and also for the case where the work and cold reservoirs are exchanged (as in Fig.
1
top left panel)
in App.
A
.

In this paper, we will instead as system consider
N
identical qutrits

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{S}

\displaystyle=\Delta\hat{N}_{\Delta}+\delta\hat{N}_{\delta}\,,

(2)

where
\hat{N}_{\Delta}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\left|2\right>\left<2\right|\right)_{i}
and
\hat{N}_{\delta}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\left|1\right>\left<1\right|\right)_{i}
count the total number of large and small excitations present in the system, and the introductory example is reproduced for
N=1
.
The reservoirs are assumed to drive individual transitions as before, but the coupling to the
i
th qutrit may in principle depend on its position.
Thus, we assume as system coupling operators the multi-qutrit operators

\displaystyle\hat{S}^{c}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\left[h_{i}^{c}\left(\left|1\right>\left<0\right|\right)_{i}+{\rm h.c.}\right]\equiv\hat{S}^{c}_{+}+\hat{S}^{c}_{-}\,,

\displaystyle\hat{S}^{h}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\left[h_{i}^{h}\left(\left|2\right>\left<0\right|\right)_{i}+{\rm h.c.}\right]\equiv\hat{S}^{h}_{+}+\hat{S}^{h}_{-}\,,

\displaystyle\hat{S}^{w}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\left[h_{i}^{w}\left(\left|2\right>\left<1\right|\right)_{i}+{\rm h.c.}\right]\equiv\hat{S}^{w}_{+}+\hat{S}^{w}_{-}\,,

(3)

where
\hat{S}^{\nu}_{-}=(\hat{S}^{\nu}_{+})^{\dagger}
.
The dependence of the coupling on the qutrit is encoded in the coefficients
h_{i}^{\nu}\in\mathbb{C}
, and furtheron we will denote the limit
h_{i}^{\nu}\to 1
as the
collective limit
, for which we write
\hat{J}^{\nu}=\lim\limits_{h_{i}^{\nu}\to 1}\hat{S}^{\nu}
and

\hat{J}^{\nu}_{\pm}=\lim\limits_{h_{i}^{\nu}\to 1}\hat{S}^{\nu}_{\pm}
.
Analogous to permutationally invariant many-qubit systems, where one can generalize single-qubit Pauli matrices – the generators of
su(2)
– to large spin operators (see e.g.
wang2002a
for potential applications), we can also define the collective generalizations of the generators of
su(3)
.
We denote them by lower indices

\displaystyle\hat{J}_{\alpha}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{\lambda}_{i}^{\alpha}\,,

(4)

where
\hat{\lambda}_{i}^{\alpha}
denotes the Gell-Mann matrix
\hat{\lambda}^{\alpha}
(with
1\leq\alpha\leq 8
) acting on the
i
th qutrit (with
1\leq i\leq N
).
In the collective limit, we can express the system coupling operators by the large qutrit operators, specifically we have

\hat{S}_{\pm}^{c}\to\hat{J}_{\pm}^{c}=\hat{J}_{6}\pm\mathrm{i}\hat{J}_{7}
,

\hat{S}_{\pm}^{h}\to\hat{J}_{\pm}^{h}=\hat{J}_{4}\pm\mathrm{i}\hat{J}_{5}
, and

\hat{S}_{\pm}^{w}\to\hat{J}_{\pm}^{w}=\hat{J}_{1}\pm\mathrm{i}\hat{J}_{2}
,
see App.
B
.
To represent the problem efficiently, it is advantageous to use common eigenstates of
\hat{J}_{3}
,
\hat{J}_{8}
and the collective Casimir operators of
su(3)
like
\hat{C}_{2}=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{8}\hat{J}_{\alpha}^{2}
.
We provide some example states in App.
C
.

The question we address here is whether one can observe superradiant performance boosts

analogous to results for interacting qubits
manzano2019a
;
kloc2021a
also for this system of non-interacting qutrits.

II
Methods

We aim at the perturbative treatment of the system-reservoir interaction (i.e., the
\hat{B}^{\nu}
operators) and a description of the system by master equations.
Depending on the microscopic implementation of (
1
), a non-perturbative treatment may require modifications to the global Hamiltonianian
nataf2010a
.
As in (
2
) all qutrits are identical and do not interact, the transformation into the interaction picture is straightforward as shown in Eq. (
D.1
).
This facilitates the derivation of these master equations.

First, we consider the Redfield-II master equation
redfield1965
(Lamb-shift omitted, see App.
D.1
for details)

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{i}\left[\hat{H}_{S},\rho(t)\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{\nu}\frac{\gamma_{\nu}(-\Omega_{\nu})}{2}\left(\left[\hat{S}^{\nu}_{+}\rho,\hat{S}^{\nu}\right]+\left[\hat{S}^{\nu},\rho\hat{S}^{\nu}_{-}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{\nu}\frac{\gamma_{\nu}(+\Omega_{\nu})}{2}\left(\left[\hat{S}^{\nu},\rho\hat{S}^{\nu}_{+}\right]+\left[\hat{S}^{\nu}_{-}\rho,\hat{S}^{\nu}\right]\right)\,,

(5)

where
\gamma_{\nu}(\omega)=\Gamma_{\nu}(\omega)[1+n_{\nu}(\omega)]\geq 0
is the product of the reservoir spectral coupling density
\Gamma_{\nu}(\omega)
and the Bose distribution
n_{\nu}(\omega)=[e^{\beta_{\nu}\omega}-1]^{-1}
, evaluated at the system excitation frequencies
\Omega_{c}=\delta>0
,
\Omega_{h}=\Delta>0
, and
\Omega_{w}=\Delta-\delta>0
.
Since we analytically continue the spectral coupling density as an odd function
\Gamma_{\nu}(-\omega)=-\Gamma_{\nu}(+\omega)
, we can express
\gamma_{\nu}(-\Omega_{\nu})=\Gamma_{\nu}(\Omega_{\nu})n_{\nu}(\Omega_{\nu})\geq 0
.
The generator (
II
) need in general not preserve the positivity of the density matrix
breuer2002
and need not be thermodynamically consistent
thingna2012a
.
For example, it may not exactly conserve the sum of all stationary energy currents leaving the reservoirs for non-vanishing couplings, such artifacts are of higher order than the accuracy Redfield approach though, cf. Fig.
7
in App.
D.1
.
Nevertheless, it has for selective systems been shown to approach the true quantum dynamics in the appropriate regimes very well
hartmann2020a
;
landi2022a
, such that we use it as benchmark approach here.

Second, we consider the Lindblad-Gorini-Kossakowski-Sudarshan (LGKS) master equation
lindblad1976a
;
gorini1976a
(Lamb-shift neglected, see App.
D.2
for details)

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{i}\left[\hat{H}_{S},\rho(t)\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{\nu}\gamma_{\nu}(+\Omega_{\nu})\left[\hat{S}^{\nu}_{-}\rho\hat{S}^{\nu}_{+}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{\hat{S}^{\nu}_{+}\hat{S}^{\nu}_{-},\rho\right\}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{\nu}\gamma_{\nu}(-\Omega_{\nu})\left[\hat{S}^{\nu}_{+}\rho\hat{S}^{\nu}_{-}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{\hat{S}^{\nu}_{-}\hat{S}^{\nu}_{+},\rho\right\}\right]\,,

(6)

which unconditionally preserves all density matrix properties and is thermodynamically consistent also for non-equilibrium reservoirs
schaller2014
.

Third, in the perfectly collective limit (
\hat{S}^{\nu}_{\pm}\to\hat{J}^{\nu}_{\pm}
) and for a completely symmetric initial state like e.g.

\displaystyle{\left|\Psi_{\rm rep}^{\rm vac}\right>=\left|0\right>\otimes\ldots\otimes\left|0\right>\equiv\left|0;0\right>}

(7)

the perfect permutational symmetry is preserved (i.e., formally the evolution is constrained to the subspace with largest Casimir operator eigenvalue).
The other permutationally completely symmetric states of this subspace with
M
large and
m
small excitations can be obtained by acting with
the collective raising operators on the vacuum state

\left|M;m\right>\propto(J^{h}_{+})^{M}(J^{c}_{+})^{m}\left|0;0\right>
.
This generates e.g. the state with
N
small and no large excitations
\left|0;N\right>=\left|1\ldots 1\right>
and the state with
N
large and no small excitations

C

In particular, the states in this subspace are non-degenerate and thereby

their populations obey a Pauli-type rate equation of the form

\displaystyle\dot{P}_{Mm}

\displaystyle=\sum_{M^{\prime}m^{\prime}}R_{Mm,M^{\prime}m^{\prime}}P_{M^{\prime}m^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\qquad-\sum_{M^{\prime}m^{\prime}}R_{M^{\prime}m^{\prime},Mm}P_{Mm}\,,

(8)

where
P_{Mm}\equiv\left<M;m\right|\rho\left|M;m\right>
and the transition rate from
\left|M^{\prime};m^{\prime}\right>
to
\left|M;m\right>
is given by
R_{Mm,M^{\prime}m^{\prime}}=\sum_{\nu}\Big[\gamma_{\nu}(+\Omega_{\nu}){\left|\left<M;m\right|\hat{J}^{\nu}_{-}\left|M^{\prime};m^{\prime}\right>\right|}^{2}+\gamma_{\nu}(-\Omega_{\nu}){\left|\left<M;m\right|\hat{J}^{\nu}_{+}\left|M^{\prime};m^{\prime}\right>\right|}^{2}\Big]\geq 0

This rate equation can be directly obtained by evaluating (
II

D.3

As Pauli rate equations can be obtained from microscopically derived LGKS equations in special cases (they always result for non-degenerate

To obtain the coefficients in Eq. (

\displaystyle\hat{J}^{h}_{+}\left|M;m\right>

\displaystyle=\sqrt{(N-M-m)(M+1)}\left|M+1;m\right>\,,

\displaystyle\hat{J}^{c}_{+}\left|M;m\right>

\displaystyle=\sqrt{(N-M-m)(m+1)}\left|M;m+1\right>\,,

\displaystyle\hat{J}^{w}_{+}\left|M;m\right>

\displaystyle=\sqrt{(M+1)m}\left|M+1;m-1\right>\,,

\displaystyle\hat{J}^{h}_{-}\left|M;m\right>

\displaystyle=\sqrt{(N-M-m+1)M}\left|M-1;m\right>\,,

\displaystyle\hat{J}^{c}_{-}\left|M;m\right>

\displaystyle=\sqrt{(N-M-m+1)m}\left|M;m-1\right>\,,

\displaystyle\hat{J}^{w}_{-}\left|M;m\right>

\displaystyle=\sqrt{M(m+1)}\left|M-1;m+1\right>\,.

(9)

Finally, we also use a coarse-grained rate equation

III
Results

In the collective and steady-state limits of the fully symmetric case (where the stationary solutions of LGKS (
II
) and Pauli (
II
) equations are identical), we can establish (see App.
G
), that the stationary currents are tightly coupled

\displaystyle\bar{I}_{E}^{w}=\frac{\Delta-\delta}{\delta}\bar{I}_{E}^{c}\,,\qquad\bar{I}_{E}^{h}=-\frac{\Delta}{\delta}\bar{I}_{E}^{c}\,,

(12)

and that the
N
-qutrit QAR is subject to the same cooling condition as a single qutrit QAR (see App.
D.3
)

\displaystyle\beta_{h}\Delta

\displaystyle>\beta_{w}(\Delta-\delta)+\beta_{c}\delta\,.

(13)

Together with the inherent assumption
\beta_{c}>\beta_{h}>\beta_{w}
, this defines an operational cooling regime.
Due to the tight-coupling, we only quantify the cooling current below.

Our main result however is that, within appropriate regimes, the collective features of our model can support a quadratic scaling of the stationary cooling current with the number of qutrits
N
, see Fig.
2
.
There, we approach the problem with methods of different complexity like the full Redfield treatment, the LGKS treatment, the rate equation treatment, and the coarse-grained rate equation treatment.

We see that all methods coincide for the case
N=1
(bottom left), where a simple rate equation treatment is sufficient (see App.

For larger but still small
N
, all methods applicable for a fully collective coupling predict a faster-than-linear growth of the current with the number of qutrits
N
, manifesting the quantum boost of the working fluid.
In App.
H
we emphasize the quantum nature of this effect.
This is seen both in LGKS (orange and green filled symbols) and Redfield (orange and green hollow symbols) approaches.
The perfect agreement of LGKS and Redfield approaches (solid versus hollow symbols) demonstrates that in the considered weak-coupling regime, the effect is not just a consequence of the secular approximation.
Furthermore, for collective couplings we also see that the cooling current in the fully symmetric subspace (orange symbols, generated with ladder operators from state (
7
)) is larger than than that originating from a subspace with second largest Casimir operator eigenvalue (green symbols), which we constructed by acting with collective ladder operators
\hat{J}^{\nu}_{\pm}
on the representative state

\displaystyle\left|\Psi_{\rm rep}\right>

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\Big[e^{\mathrm{i}2\pi 0/N}\left|0\ldots 01\right>+e^{\mathrm{i}2\pi 1/N}\left|0\ldots 010\right>

\displaystyle\qquad+\ldots+e^{\mathrm{i}2\pi(N-1)/N}\left|10\ldots 0\right>\Big]\,,

(14)

until no new orthogonal vectors were found (analogous to the discussion in App.
C
).

As one may expect for quantum features, the quadratic boost is fragile in some aspects:
First, we observe for large
N
a crossover to linear scaling (filled orange circles and blue triangles), which we can link to the fact that for fixed temperatures and increasing
N
, the most productive cycles with
m\approx M\approx N/3
are no longer populated significantly (insets display stationary populations for
N=8
and
N=80
).
Similiar inhibitions of superradiant behaviour for large
N
have been observed elsewhere
vogl2011a
;
niedenzu2018a
.
For the limit of an infinitely hot work reservoir (blue triangles), this can even be understood analytically (dashed magenta curves, see App.
D.4
).
Second, a more severe restriction appears when we relax the assumption of collective couplings by allowing for random phases in the coupling operators (red symbols).
Then, the Liouvillian no longer decouples the subspaces of different Casimir operator eigenvalues, and already for weak deviations from the collective limit, the steady-state current no longer scales quadratically.

In this limit, the steady state is close to a product of single-qutrit states, see App.
H
, such that
major parts of the steady state populate less productive subspaces.

When the deviations from the collective limit are small, at least two time scales will emerge: A fast one describes the evolution within the subspaces of constant Casimir operator eigenvalue, and the slow one(s) will describe the leakage between the subspaces of different Casimir operator eigenvalues.
Thus, initializing the working fluid in a permutationally symmetric state like
\left|0;0\right>
and operating the device only for a finite time, between these two time scales, may sufficiently populate states near
\left|N/3;N/3\right>
and then still yield a quantum boost.
However, to stabilize the quantum enhancement at steady state and to resist small perturbations, fine-tuned interactions may be required, as exemplified at the end of this section.

To analyze the device from the thermodynamic perspective, we note that LGKS and the derived rate equations are thermodynamically fully consistent.
At steady state, the first law just implies that the stationary energy currents add to zero
\sum_{\nu}\bar{I}_{E}^{\nu}=0
– see Eq. (
12
), and the second law implies that the stationary irreversible entropy production rate is non-negative
\bar{\sigma}_{\mathrm{i}}=-\sum_{\nu}\beta_{\nu}\bar{I}_{E}^{\nu}\geq 0

schaller2014
.
As specifically in the symmetric subspace the currents are tightly coupled (
12
), the coefficient of performance of the device is given by

\kappa\equiv\frac{\bar{I}_{E}^{c}}{\bar{I}_{E}^{w}}\Theta(\bar{I}_{E}^{c})=\frac{\delta}{\Delta-\delta}\Theta(\bar{I}_{E}^{c})
.
One can check that in the regions of cooling functionality (
\bar{I}_{E}^{c}>0
), the coefficient of performance is bound by its Carnot value via the condition that
\bar{\sigma}_{\mathrm{i}}\geq 0
.
Tighter bounds can be obtained by considering the thermodynamic uncertainty relation
barato2015a
;
pietzonka2016a
;
gingrich2016a

\displaystyle\bar{\sigma}_{\mathrm{i}}\frac{\bar{S}_{I_{E}^{\nu}}}{(\bar{I}_{E}^{\nu})^{2}}\geq 2\,.

(15)

Using that all currents are tightly coupled, we use it to lower-bound the fluctuations for e.g. the cooling current as

\displaystyle\bar{S}_{I_{E}^{c}}\geq\frac{\delta\bar{I}_{E}^{c}}{\beta_{h}\Delta-\beta_{c}\delta-\beta_{w}(\Delta-\delta)}\,,

(16)

which proves that the fluctuations inherit the super-extensive scaling of the current (if present).
As the eigenvalues of the Pauli rate equation cannot scale faster than
N^{2}
, we also find that the fluctuations cannot grow faster than
N^{2}
.
Therefore, the relative fluctuations
saryal2021a

\sqrt{\bar{S}_{I_{E}^{c}}}/\bar{I}_{E}^{c}

are also affected by this bound.
Numerically, we find that the fluctuations approximately follow the scaling of the current.
Then, it follows that these are suppressed strongest in the regime of quadratic current and noise scaling.

Due to the tight-coupling relations (
12
), we found the relative fluctuations to be alike for all reservoirs
mohanta2022a
.

Thus, if in practice one would like a device with a large cooling power, a look at
Fig.
3
suggests that our device should be operated at
\beta_{c}\gtrapprox\beta_{h}
.

We find that this quotient is smallest at the other boundary of the cooling region, where

By adding specifically taylored interactions to the system, we can energetically favor the maximally symmetric subspace and also the cycle with the maximum current.
One example for such an interaction could be

\displaystyle=\alpha_{C}\left[\frac{N(N+3)}{3}-\hat{C}_{2}\right]+\alpha_{P}\Bigg[\left(\frac{N}{3}-\hat{N}_{\Delta}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\left(\frac{N}{3}-\hat{N}_{\delta}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{N}{3}-\hat{N}_{\Delta}\right)\left(\frac{N}{3}-\hat{N}_{\delta}\right)\Bigg]

IV
Conclusions

We analyzed an ensemble of
N
identical qutrits for its suitability as a QAR using different approaches.
The most striking effect is a quadratic scaling of the output (cooling current) with the number of qutrits for a permutationally symmetric configuration and collective system-reservoir couplings.
This collective phenomenon can be understood analogously to Dicke superradiant decay of two level systems – here put to use in an engine by collective couplings.
In contrast to quantum computers subjected to decoherence, the performance of the QAR is only reduced to linear (classical) scaling for too large
N
(changing other parameters may increase the critical size) or for a not-perfectly-collective coupling.
For non-identical qutrits we would expect a similar breakdown
gerry2022a
.
Compared to a qubit implementation
kloc2021a
, we want to summarize some advantages of collective qutrits:
First, the initialization in the proper subspace requires just a permutationally symmetric state (no entangled states like in
kloc2021a
) as e.g.
\left|0\ldots 0\right>
.
Second, to see the speedup, we do not require fine-tuned inter-qutrit interactions (although this can be used to stabilize the setup against perturbations).
Third, in contrast to the interacting qubit system, the (non-interacting) multi-qutrit system only provides three transition frequencies, such that a selective driving appears easier to implement.

On the technical side, we have confirmed the effect with a variety of methods.
Using a bosonization technique is helpful to compute the transition rates analytically.
While the behaviour of the collective limit can be well understood with analytic approximations and allowed us to treat fairly large systems, treating the non-collective limit requires significant resources.
We have benchmarked our results with considering also the non-LGKS Redfield master equation.
We found that LGKS and Redfield results did not differ much in the considered weak-coupling regime.
Beyond stronger system-reservoir coupling strengths, one may also expect larger deviations outside the tight-coupling regime, where the reservoirs no longer drive the individual transitions.

As an outlook, we think it could be interesting to consider laser-driven QARs
kalaee2021a
;
kalaee2021b
or investigate the fluctuations of energy currents beyond the tight-coupling regime
jaseem2022a
or for detuned levels
holubec2019a
in greater detail.
One may also be tempted to address the strong-coupling regime beyond phenomenologic models
mu2017a
, e.g. using reaction coordinates
restrepo2018a
.
This however should be performed while maintaining a lower spectral bound on the global Hamiltonian for all coupling strengths and
N
, in this case we expect modifiations to Eq. (
1
)
nataf2010a
.

Appendix A
A single three-level QAR

For the case of a single qutrit
N=1
one may derive a rate equation

\dot{\vec{P}}=R\vec{P}
for the occupation probabilities
\vec{P}=(P_{0},P_{1},P_{2})^{\rm T}

of these states (Redfield, LGKS, and Pauli rate equations then yield the same dynamics for these probabilities).

For the normal configuration shown in Fig.
1
bottom left panel, where the cold reservoir drives transitions between the lowest two states
\left|0\right>\leftrightarrow\left|1\right>
, the hot between lowest and highest
\left|0\right>\leftrightarrow\left|2\right>
, and the work reservoir transitions between the two excited states
\left|1\right>\leftrightarrow\left|2\right>
,
the rate matrix becomes

\displaystyle R^{+}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}R_{00}^{+}&\Gamma_{c}(1+n_{c})&\Gamma_{h}(1+n_{h})\\
\Gamma_{c}n_{c}&R_{11}^{+}&\Gamma_{w}(1+n_{w})\\
\Gamma_{h}n_{h}&\Gamma_{w}n_{w}&R_{22}^{+}\end{array}\right)\,,

where
n_{\nu}\equiv n_{\nu}(\Omega_{\nu})
and the negative diagonal elements are fixed by demanding vanishing of the column sums (this implements overall probability conservation).
In the rate matrix, we can clearly distinguish the contributions of the individual reservoirs.
From this, the energy current entering from, for example, the cold reservoir can be obtained by standard techniques:
One may compute the current via
I_{E}^{c}(t)=\sum_{ij}(E_{i}-E_{j})R_{ij,c}^{+}P_{j}(t)
, where
R_{c}^{+}=R^{+}|_{\Gamma_{h}=\Gamma_{w}=0}
is the cold reservoir rate matrix.
Alternatively,
one may introduce energy counting fields
R^{+}\to R^{+}(\chi)
by replacing
\Gamma_{c}(1+n_{c})\to\Gamma_{c}(1+n_{c})e^{-\mathrm{i}\delta\chi}
and
\Gamma_{c}n_{c}\to\Gamma_{c}n_{c}e^{+\mathrm{i}\delta\chi}
in the off-diagonal elements of the rate matrix and compute the current via
I_{E}^{c}(t)=-\mathrm{i}(1,1,1)(\partial_{\chi}R^{+}(\chi))|_{\chi=0}\vec{P}(t)
, see also App.
F
.
With this formalism, it is possible to compute also higher cumulants of the distribution of energy transfers
[
73
]
.
In the limit of
n_{w}\to\infty
, the long-term (stationary) current reduces to

\displaystyle\bar{I}_{E+}^{c,\infty}=\frac{\Gamma_{c}\Gamma_{h}\delta(n_{c}-n_{h})}{\Gamma_{c}(1+3n_{c})+\Gamma_{h}(1+3n_{h})}\,.

(22)

For the case where cold and work reservoir are exchanged
E_{0}=0
,
E_{1}=\Delta-\delta
,
E_{2}=\Delta
depicted in Fig.
1
top left panel,
the rate matrix is given by

\displaystyle R^{-}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}R_{00}^{-}&\Gamma_{w}(1+n_{w})&\Gamma_{h}(1+n_{h})\\
\Gamma_{w}n_{w}&R_{11}^{-}&\Gamma_{c}(1+n_{c})\\
\Gamma_{h}n_{h}&\Gamma_{c}n_{c}&R_{22}^{-}\end{array}\right)\,,

and an analogous calculation leads in the case of
n_{w}\to\infty
to a slightly different cooling current

\displaystyle\bar{I}_{E-}^{c,\infty}=\frac{\Gamma_{c}\Gamma_{h}\delta(n_{c}-n_{h})}{\Gamma_{c}(2+3n_{c})+\Gamma_{h}(2+3n_{h})}\,.

(26)

Both currents have the same cooling condition

\displaystyle n_{c}>n_{h}\qquad\Leftrightarrow\qquad\beta_{c}<\beta_{h}\Delta/\delta\,,

(27)

which – together with
\beta_{c}>\beta_{h}
–
defines an operational window for cooling the coldest reservoir.
This is a sufficient and necessary condition for
n_{w}\to\infty
and
N=1
.

For finite
n_{w}
, the calculations are a bit lengthier but fully analogous.
One obtains for both configurations the sufficient and necessary cooling condition

\displaystyle\beta_{w}(\Delta-\delta)+\beta_{c}\delta<\beta_{h}\Delta\,,

(28)

which reduces to the previous condition as
\beta_{w}\to 0
.
Together with the underlying assumption
\beta_{c}>\beta_{h}
, this defines the bounds on the window of cooling also for larger
N
(compare Fig.
3
in the main text).

For a single qutrit, we can express the system Hamiltonian as

\displaystyle=(\Delta-\delta)\frac{\hat{\lambda}^{3}}{2}+(\Delta+\delta)\frac{\hat{\lambda}^{8}}{2\sqrt{3}}+(\Delta+\delta)\frac{\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}{3}\,,

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{\lambda}^{h}_{\pm,i}=\hat{J}_{4}\pm\mathrm{i}\hat{J}_{5}\,,

\displaystyle\hat{J}^{c}_{\pm}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{\lambda}^{c}_{\pm,i}=\hat{J}_{6}\pm\mathrm{i}\hat{J}_{7}\,,

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{8}\hat{J}_{\alpha}^{2}\,,

\displaystyle\hat{C}_{3}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\beta\gamma}{\rm Tr}\left\{\left\{\hat{J}_{\alpha},\hat{J}_{\beta}\right\}\hat{J}_{\gamma}\right\}\hat{J}_{\alpha}\hat{J}_{\beta}\hat{J}_{\gamma}

will be automatically conserved, to all orders in the system-reservoir interaction Hamiltonian.

7
),
we can constrain ourselves to the subspace of

Appendix D
Derivation of the evolution equations

D.1
Redfield master equation

For multiple weakly-coupled reservoirs, the dissipators act additively, such that for a single-coupling operator per reservoir

\hat{H}_{I}^{\nu}=\hat{S}^{\nu}\otimes\hat{B}^{\nu}
, the Redfield-II master equation
[
53
]
can be readily written as

\displaystyle\dot{\mbox{\boldmath$\rho$}}

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{\infty}d\tau\sum_{\nu}C_{\nu}(+\tau)\left[\mbox{\boldmath$\hat{S}^{\nu}$}(t),\mbox{\boldmath$\hat{S}^{\nu}$}(t-\tau)\mbox{\boldmath$\rho$}(t)\right]

\displaystyle\qquad-\int_{0}^{\infty}d\tau\sum_{\nu}C_{\nu}(-\tau)\left[\mbox{\boldmath$\rho$}(t)\mbox{\boldmath$\hat{S}^{\nu}$}(t-\tau),\mbox{\boldmath$\hat{S}^{\nu}$}(t)\right]\,,

(43)

where we have denoted the interaction picture by bold symbols and introduced the reservoir correlation function
C_{\nu}(\tau)={\rm Tr_{B}}\left\{e^{+\mathrm{i}\hat{H}_{B}^{\nu}\tau}\hat{B}^{\nu}e^{-\mathrm{i}\hat{H}_{B}^{\nu}\tau}\hat{B}^{\nu}\bar{\rho}_{B}^{\nu}\right\}
.
In particular for the non-interacting system Hamiltonian (
2
) and coupling operators (
I
), the transformation into the interaction picture can be made very explicit

\displaystyle e^{+\mathrm{i}\hat{H}_{S}t}\hat{S}^{c}_{\pm}e^{-\mathrm{i}\hat{H}_{S}t}

\displaystyle=e^{\pm\mathrm{i}\delta t}\hat{S}^{c}_{\pm}\,,

\displaystyle e^{+\mathrm{i}\hat{H}_{S}t}\hat{S}^{h}_{\pm}e^{-\mathrm{i}\hat{H}_{S}t}

\displaystyle=e^{\pm\mathrm{i}\Delta t}\hat{S}^{h}_{\pm}\,,

\displaystyle e^{+\mathrm{i}\hat{H}_{S}t}\hat{S}^{w}_{\pm}e^{-\mathrm{i}\hat{H}_{S}t}

\displaystyle=e^{\pm\mathrm{i}(\Delta-\delta)t}\hat{S}^{w}_{\pm}\,.

(44)

Thus, writing the Redfield equation in the Schrödinger picture, we get (compare e.g.
[
78
]
)

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{i}\left[\hat{H}_{S},\rho(t)\right]-\sum_{\nu}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\tau C_{\nu}(+\tau)\left[\hat{S}^{\nu},\mbox{\boldmath$\hat{S}^{\nu}$}(-\tau)\rho(t)\right]

\displaystyle\qquad-\sum_{\nu}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\tau C_{\nu}(-\tau)\left[\rho(t)\mbox{\boldmath$\hat{S}^{\nu}$}(-\tau),\hat{S}^{\nu}\right]

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{i}\left[\hat{H}_{S},\rho(t)\right]-\sum_{\nu}\int_{0}^{\infty}C_{\nu}(+\tau)e^{-\mathrm{i}\Omega_{\nu}\tau}d\tau\left[\hat{S}^{\nu},\hat{S}^{\nu}_{+}\rho\right]

\displaystyle\qquad-\sum_{\nu}\int_{0}^{\infty}C_{\nu}(+\tau)e^{+\mathrm{i}\Omega_{\nu}\tau}d\tau\left[\hat{S}^{\nu},\hat{S}^{\nu}_{-}\rho\right]

\displaystyle\qquad-\sum_{\nu}\int_{0}^{\infty}C_{\nu}(-\tau)e^{-\mathrm{i}\Omega_{\nu}\tau}d\tau\left[\rho\hat{S}^{\nu}_{+},\hat{S}^{\nu}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad-\sum_{\nu}\int_{0}^{\infty}C_{\nu}(-\tau)e^{+\mathrm{i}\Omega_{\nu}\tau}d\tau\left[\rho\hat{S}^{\nu}_{-},\hat{S}^{\nu}\right]\,.

(45)

To simplify this expression, one may express the integral prefactors by inserting the Fourier transform of the correlation function
C_{\nu}(\tau)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\gamma_{\nu}(\omega)e^{-\mathrm{i}\omega\tau}d\omega
and afterwards invoking the Sokhotskij-Plemelj theorem

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{+\mathrm{i}\omega\tau}d\tau=\frac{1}{2}\delta(\omega)+\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2\pi}{\cal P}\frac{1}{\omega}\,,

(46)

where
{\cal P}
denotes the Cauchy principal value.
This allows to write the integrals in terms of Hermitian (real) and anti-Hermitian (imaginary) parts, e.g.

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}C_{\nu}(+\tau)e^{+\mathrm{i}\Omega_{\nu}\tau}d\tau

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{\nu}(+\Omega_{\nu})+\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{\nu}(+\Omega_{\nu})\,,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}C_{\nu}(-\tau)e^{+\mathrm{i}\Omega_{\nu}\tau}d\tau

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{\nu}(-\Omega_{\nu})-\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{\nu}(-\Omega_{\nu})\,,

(47)

and analogously for the terms with
\Omega_{\nu}\to-\Omega_{\nu}
.
In the equations above, the functions on the r.h.s. are then the even and odd Fourier transforms of the reservoir correlation functions

\displaystyle\gamma_{\nu}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\int C_{\nu}(\tau)e^{+\mathrm{i}\omega\tau}d\tau\,,

\displaystyle\sigma_{\nu}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\int C_{\nu}(\tau){\rm sgn}(\tau)e^{+\mathrm{i}\omega\tau}d\tau\,.

(48)

The Lamb-shift parts
\sigma_{\nu}(\pm\Omega_{\nu})
are negligible in comparison to
\hat{H}_{S}
, such that it is common practice to neglect them.
Eventually, this transforms Eq. (
D.1
) into Eq. (
II
) in the main text.
The Redfield equation conserves trace and hermiticity, but not necessarily the positivity of the density matrix.
For general operators
\hat{O}
one can rewrite it in terms of expectation values

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left<\hat{O}\right>

\displaystyle=+\mathrm{i}\left<\left[\hat{H}_{S},\hat{O}\right]\right>

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{\nu}\frac{\gamma_{\nu}(-\Omega_{\nu})}{2}\Big[\left<\left[\hat{S}^{\nu},\hat{O}\right]\hat{S}_{+}^{\nu}\right>

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\left<\hat{S}^{\nu}_{-}\left[\hat{O},\hat{S}^{\nu}\right]\right>\Big]

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{\nu}\frac{\gamma_{\nu}(+\Omega_{\nu})}{2}\Big[\left<\left[\hat{S}^{\nu},\hat{O}\right]\hat{S}_{-}^{\nu}\right>

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\left<\hat{S}^{\nu}_{+}\left[\hat{O},\hat{S}^{\nu}\right]\right>\Big]\,.

(49)

Thus, it is easy to see that the above equation conserves all operators that commute with both
\hat{H}_{S}
and
\hat{S}^{\nu}
, i.e., if
[\hat{O},\hat{H}_{S}]=[\hat{O},\hat{S}^{\nu}]=0
, one also has

{\rm Tr}\left\{\hat{O}\dot{\rho}\right\}=0
.
This is the case in the collective limit, where
\hat{S}^{\nu}\to\hat{J}^{\nu}
e.g. for the Casimir operators
\hat{O}\to\hat{C}_{i}
.

Apart from the Lamb-shift terms, the spectral coupling densities of the reservoirs are always evaluated at their transition frequencies
\delta
,
\Delta
, and
\Delta-\delta
, respectively.
Thus, for our calculations in the main text (where the Lamb-shift is neglected), the actual form of the spectral coupling density is not relevant.
To confirm that the Lamb-shift contributions are indeed negligible, we employed a special model for the spectral coupling density.
Using

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\nu}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{\nu}\frac{\omega}{\epsilon_{\nu}}\frac{\delta_{\nu}^{4}}{(\omega^{2}-\epsilon_{\nu}^{2})^{2}+\delta_{\nu}^{4}}\,,

(50)

the correlation function
C_{\nu}(\tau)
as well as the Lamb-shift contribution

\displaystyle\sigma_{\nu}(\omega)=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\pi}{\cal P}\int\frac{\gamma_{\nu}(\omega^{\prime})}{\omega-\omega^{\prime}}d\omega^{\prime}

(51)

may be computed analytically (but yielding extremely lengthy expressions).
To compare with our calculations in the main text, we used resonant reservoirs with
\epsilon_{c}=\delta
,
\epsilon_{h}=\Delta
and
\epsilon_{w}=\Delta-\delta

and computed the Redfield current in Fig.
2
with Lamb-shift corrections included (setting
\delta_{\nu}=\epsilon_{\nu}
).
For the other parameters chosen as in Fig.
2
we found the effects of the Lamb-shift contributions to be negligible.

Similar to the rate equation discussion from App.
A
, the cold reservoir energy current may now be computed using different options:
Taking the view of the system perspective, one may obtain it by computing the system energy balance from (
II
).
The energy current entering from the cold reservoir then becomes

\displaystyle I_{E,S}^{c}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma_{c}n_{c}}{2}{\rm Tr}\left\{\hat{H}_{S}\left(\left[\hat{S}^{c}_{+}\rho,\hat{S}^{c}\right]+\left[\hat{S}^{c},\rho\hat{S}^{c}_{-}\right]\right)\right\}

(52)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\Gamma_{c}(1+n_{c})}{2}{\rm Tr}\left\{\hat{H}_{S}\left(\left[\hat{S}^{c},\rho\hat{S}^{c}_{+}\right]+\left[\hat{S}^{c}_{-}\rho,\hat{S}^{c}\right]\right)\right\}\,,

and analogously for the other reservoirs.
Alternatively, we may microscopically derive energy counting fields
[
79
,
78
,
53
]
, which takes the perspective of energy leaving the reservoir
and yields a tilted Liouvillian
{\cal L}\to{\cal L}(\chi)
.
In Eq. (
II
) in the main text, this would effectively lead to the replacements

\left(\left[\hat{S}^{c},\rho\hat{S}^{c}_{+}\right]+\left[\hat{S}^{c}_{-}\rho,\hat{S}^{c}\right]\right)\to\left(\hat{S}^{c}\rho\hat{S}^{c}_{+}e^{-\mathrm{i}\delta\chi}+\hat{S}^{c}_{-}\rho\hat{S}^{c}e^{-\mathrm{i}\delta\chi}-\rho\hat{S}^{c}_{+}\hat{S}^{c}-\hat{S}^{c}\hat{S}^{c}_{-}\rho\right)

and the analogous one

\left(\left[\hat{S}^{c}_{+}\rho,\hat{S}^{c}\right]+\left[\hat{S}^{c},\rho\hat{S}^{c}_{-}\right]\right)\to\left(\hat{S}^{c}_{+}\rho\hat{S}^{c}e^{+\mathrm{i}\delta\chi}+\hat{S}^{c}\rho\hat{S}^{c}_{-}e^{+\mathrm{i}\delta\chi}-\hat{S}^{c}\hat{S}^{c}_{+}\rho-\rho\hat{S}^{c}_{-}\hat{S}^{c}\right)
.
The current (and noise) can then be computed by the methods in App.
F
.
This yields for the current leaving the cold reservoir

\displaystyle I_{E}^{c}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\Gamma_{c}\delta(1+n_{c})}{2}{\rm Tr}\left\{\hat{S}^{c}\rho\hat{S}^{c}_{+}+\hat{S}^{c}_{-}\rho\hat{S}^{c}\right\}

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{\Gamma_{c}\delta n_{c}}{2}{\rm Tr}\left\{\hat{S}^{c}_{+}\rho\hat{S}^{c}+\hat{S}^{c}\rho\hat{S}^{c}_{-}\right\}\,.

(53)

In general, we have
I_{E}^{\nu}\neq I_{E,S}^{\nu}
for the Redfield equation.
Moreover, while by construction the stationary currents for the system add to zero
\sum_{\nu}\bar{I}_{E,S}^{\nu}=0
, this is only approximately true in the Redfield approach for the energy currents leaving the reservoir.
In Fig.
7
we plot the sum of energy currents leaving the reservoirs (i.e., the deviation from the first law at steady state) versus the coupling strengths to the reservoirs (assumed equal).
One can see that the deviation from energy conservation becomes smaller when the coupling strength is reduced.
Moreover, it scales with
\Gamma^{2}
(the Redfield approach is accurate to order
\Gamma
), such this apparent violation can be considered a truncation artifact
and does not indicate a violation of the first law in the weak-coupling regime, where for our model Redfield and LGKS solutions agree.

Double-logarithmic plot of the sum of all stationary Redfield energy currents leaving the reservoirs versus the coupling strength
\Gamma
for
N=6
qutrits. The apparent violation of the first law scales as
\Gamma^{2}
(dashed line), which is beyond the accuracy of the Redfield equation.
Parameters:
\Gamma=\Gamma_{c}=\Gamma_{h}=\Gamma_{w}
,

,
n_{c}=10
,

and
n_{w}=100
.

D.2
Secular master equations

Under a secular approximation, Eq. (
D.1
) simplifies into

\displaystyle\dot{\mbox{\boldmath$\rho$}}

\displaystyle\approx-\sum_{\nu}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\tau C_{\nu}(+\tau)e^{-\mathrm{i}\Omega_{\nu}\tau}\left[\hat{S}^{\nu}_{-},\hat{S}^{\nu}_{+}\mbox{\boldmath$\rho$}(t)\right]

\displaystyle\qquad-\sum_{\nu}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\tau C_{\nu}(+\tau)e^{+\mathrm{i}\Omega_{\nu}\tau}\left[\hat{S}^{\nu}_{+},\hat{S}^{\nu}_{-}\mbox{\boldmath$\rho$}(t)\right]

\displaystyle\qquad-\sum_{\nu}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\tau C_{\nu}(-\tau)e^{+\mathrm{i}\Omega_{\nu}\tau}\left[\mbox{\boldmath$\rho$}(t)\hat{S}^{\nu}_{-},\hat{S}^{\nu}_{+}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad-\sum_{\nu}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\tau C_{\nu}(-\tau)e^{-\mathrm{i}\Omega_{\nu}\tau}\left[\mbox{\boldmath$\rho$}(t)\hat{S}^{\nu}_{+},\hat{S}^{\nu}_{-}\right]

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{i}\sum_{\nu}\left[\frac{\sigma_{\nu}(-\Omega_{\nu})}{2\mathrm{i}}\hat{S}^{\nu}_{-}\hat{S}^{\nu}_{+}+\frac{\sigma_{\nu}(+\Omega_{\nu})}{2\mathrm{i}}\hat{S}^{\nu}_{+}\hat{S}^{\nu}_{-},\mbox{\boldmath$\rho$}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{\nu}\gamma_{\nu}(+\Omega_{\nu})\left[\hat{S}^{\nu}_{-}\mbox{\boldmath$\rho$}\hat{S}^{\nu}_{+}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{\hat{S}^{\nu}_{+}\hat{S}^{\nu}_{-},\mbox{\boldmath$\rho$}\right\}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{\nu}\gamma_{\nu}(-\Omega_{\nu})\left[\hat{S}^{\nu}_{+}\mbox{\boldmath$\rho$}\hat{S}^{\nu}_{-}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{\hat{S}^{\nu}_{-}\hat{S}^{\nu}_{+},\mbox{\boldmath$\rho$}\right\}\right]\,.

(54)

Back in the Schrödinger picture, we thus have

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{i}\left[\hat{H}_{S}+\sum_{\nu}\frac{\sigma_{\nu}(-\Omega_{\nu})}{2\mathrm{i}}\hat{S}^{\nu}_{-}\hat{S}^{\nu}_{+}+\frac{\sigma_{\nu}(+\Omega_{\nu})}{2\mathrm{i}}\hat{S}^{\nu}_{+}\hat{S}^{\nu}_{-},\mbox{\boldmath$\rho$}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{\nu}\gamma_{\nu}(+\Omega_{\nu})\left[\hat{S}^{\nu}_{-}\rho\hat{S}^{\nu}_{+}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{\hat{S}^{\nu}_{+}\hat{S}^{\nu}_{-},\rho\right\}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{\nu}\gamma_{\nu}(-\Omega_{\nu})\left[\hat{S}^{\nu}_{+}\rho\hat{S}^{\nu}_{-}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{\hat{S}^{\nu}_{-}\hat{S}^{\nu}_{+},\rho\right\}\right]\,,

(55)

which upon neglecting the Lamb shift
\sigma_{\nu}(\pm\Omega_{\nu})
reduces to Eq. (
II
) from the main text.
This equation is of LGKS form and thermalizes the system when all reservoirs are at the same equilibrium temperature.
Furthermore, when evaluating only the populations of
\rho
in the system energy eigenbasis for situations where the system Hamiltonian is non-degenerate (at least within a conserved subspace), we obtain the simple Pauli-type rate equation as exemplified in App.
D.3
.

In the collective limit, the associated effective non-Hermitian Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H_{\rm eff}=\hat{H}_{S}-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\sum_{\nu}\left[\Gamma_{\nu}(1+n_{\nu})\hat{J}^{\nu}_{+}\hat{J}^{\nu}_{-}+\Gamma_{\nu}n_{\nu}\hat{J}^{\nu}_{+}\hat{J}^{\nu}_{-}\right]

(56)

is diagonal in the maximum symmetry subspace
H_{\rm eff}\left|M;m\right>=\lambda_{Mm}\left|M;m\right>
.
Its eigenvalues can be calculated analytically via (
II
), and their imaginary part – related to the waiting time distribution
[
80
]
between any two jump events
[
81
]
– indicates that for fixed thermal reservoirs
n_{\nu}
and coupling strengths
\Gamma_{\nu}
, the waiting time is minimized for
M\approx m\approx N/3
(maximum engine activity).
Specifically, from the imaginary part of

\displaystyle\lambda_{\frac{N}{3},\frac{N}{3}}=\frac{N}{3}(\Delta+\delta)-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\frac{N}{3}\left(\frac{N}{3}+1\right)\left[\sum_{\nu}\Gamma_{\nu}(1+2n_{\nu})\right]

(57)

we can conclude that the lifetime of the states participating in the central cycle scales as
\tau\propto N^{-2}
.

The currents can now be computed in analogy to the previous section, i.e., either by the energy flowing into the system from the cold reservoir

\displaystyle I_{E,S}^{c}

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{c}(1+n_{c}){\rm Tr}\left\{\hat{H}_{S}\left[\hat{S}^{c}_{-}\rho\hat{S}^{c}_{+}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{\hat{S}^{c}_{+}\hat{S}^{c}_{-},\rho\right\}\right]\right\}

\displaystyle\qquad+\Gamma_{c}n_{c}{\rm Tr}\left\{\hat{H}_{S}\left[\hat{S}^{c}_{+}\rho\hat{S}^{c}_{-}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{\hat{S}^{c}_{-}\hat{S}^{c}_{+},\rho\right\}\right]\right\}\,,

(58)

or by assessing the energy flow out of the cold reservoir with counting fields
\hat{S}^{c}_{-}\rho\hat{S}^{c}_{+}\to\hat{S}^{c}_{-}\rho\hat{S}^{c}_{+}e^{-\mathrm{i}\delta\chi}

and
\hat{S}^{c}_{+}\rho\hat{S}^{c}_{-}\to\hat{S}^{c}_{+}\rho\hat{S}^{c}_{-}e^{+\mathrm{i}\delta\chi}
and then using the methods of Sec.
F
.
The current leaving the cold reservoir can then be obtained via
I_{E}^{c}(t)=-\mathrm{i}{\rm Tr}\left\{{\cal L}^{\prime}(0)\vec{\rho}(t)\right\}
and becomes

\displaystyle I_{E}^{c}

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{c}\delta n_{c}{\rm Tr}\left\{\hat{S}^{c}_{+}\rho\hat{S}^{c}_{-}\right\}-\Gamma_{c}\delta(1+n_{c}){\rm Tr}\left\{\hat{S}^{c}_{-}\rho\hat{S}^{c}_{+}\right\}\,.

(59)

In contrast to the Redfield case discussed above, we find
I_{E}^{\nu}=I_{E,S}^{\nu}
, such that we can unambiguously write the first law of thermodynamics as

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}{\rm Tr}\left\{\hat{H}_{S}\rho\right\}=\sum_{\nu}I_{E}^{\nu}(t)\,.

(60)

With using Spohn’s inequality
[
82
]
, one can also establish the second law of thermodynamics

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}{\rm Tr}\left\{-\rho\ln\rho\right\}-\sum_{\nu}\beta_{\nu}I_{E}^{\nu}(t)\geq 0\,.

(61)

These bound the coefficient of performance of the QAR by its Carnot value.

D.3
Pauli rate equation

For the fully symmetric case, the derivation of a rate equation becomes particularly simple:
We can formally insert a diagonal density matrix in the fully symmetric subspace

\displaystyle\rho=\sum_{M,m}P_{Mm}\left|M;m\right>\left<M;m\right|\,,

(62)

into (
II
), which with the help of Eq. (
II
) yields

\displaystyle\dot{P}_{Mm}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{c}(-\delta)(N-M-m+1)mP_{M,m-1}

\displaystyle\qquad-\gamma_{c}(-\delta)(N-M-m)(m+1)P_{M,m}

\displaystyle\qquad+\gamma_{c}(+\delta)(N-M-m)(m+1)P_{M,m+1}

\displaystyle\qquad-\gamma_{c}(+\delta)(N-M-m+1)mP_{M,m}

\displaystyle\qquad+\gamma_{h}(-\Delta)(N-M-m+1)MP_{M-1,m}

\displaystyle\qquad-\gamma_{h}(-\Delta)(N-M-m)(M+1)P_{M,m}

\displaystyle\qquad+\gamma_{h}(+\Delta)(N-M-m)(M+1)P_{M+1,m}

\displaystyle\qquad-\gamma_{h}(+\Delta)(N-M-m+1)MP_{M,m}

\displaystyle\qquad+\gamma_{w}(-\Delta+\delta)M(m+1)P_{M-1,m+1}

\displaystyle\qquad-\gamma_{w}(-\Delta+\delta)(M+1)mP_{M,m}

\displaystyle\qquad+\gamma_{w}(+\Delta-\delta)(M+1)mP_{M+1,m-1}

\displaystyle\qquad-\gamma_{w}(+\Delta-\delta)M(m+1)P_{M,m}\,.

(63)

This is precisely the Pauli-type rate equation (
II
) in the main text,
where we note that rates outside allowed states do naturally vanish.
In particular, we can identify the transitions that increase the system energy as

\displaystyle R_{(M,m+1),(M,m)}

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{c}n_{c}\cdot(N-M-m)(m+1)\,,

\displaystyle R_{(M+1,m),(M,m)}

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{h}n_{h}\cdot(N-M-m)(M+1)\,,

\displaystyle R_{(M+1,m-1),(M,m)}

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{w}n_{w}\cdot m(M+1)\,,

(64)

while transitions that decrease it are always a bit larger due to local detailed balance

\displaystyle R_{(M,m),(M,m+1)}

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{c}(1+n_{c})\cdot(N-M-m)(m+1)\,,

\displaystyle R_{(M,m),(M+1,m)}

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{h}(1+n_{h})\cdot(N-M-m)(M+1)\,,

\displaystyle R_{(M,m),(M+1,m-1)}

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{w}(1+n_{w})\cdot m(M+1)\,.

(65)

Currents can be obtained in analogy to the case
N=1
discussed in App.
A
:
Either one computes them via the energy balance of the system which, for a system subject to the rate equation
\dot{P}_{i}=\sum_{ij}\sum_{\nu}\left[R_{ij}^{\nu}P_{j}-R_{ji}^{\nu}P_{i}\right]
with
R_{ij}^{\nu}
denoting the transition rate between system energy eigenstate
j\to i
triggered by reservoir
\nu
, generically yields the expression

I_{E}^{\nu}=\sum_{ij}(E_{i}-E_{j})R_{ij}^{\nu}P_{j}
.
Alternatively, one may also insert counting fields (or use the ones from the LGKS description), which effectively corresponds to the replacement

\Gamma_{c}n_{c}\to\Gamma_{c}n_{c}e^{+\mathrm{i}\delta\chi}
and
\Gamma_{c}(1+n_{c})\to\Gamma_{c}(1+n_{c})e^{-\mathrm{i}\delta\chi}
in the off-diagonal matrix elements of the rate matrix and then use the methods of App.
F
.
As with the previous section, these two approaches are equivalent and thermodynamically consistent.

For multi-qutrit systems the exact computation of the steady state is cumbersome already for the collective limit and a Pauli rate equation (
II
), such that we do not provide analytic results for the currents.
However, in App.
G
we perform a cycle decomposition to establish tight-coupling relations between the currents.

D.4
Coarse-grained rate equation

For the case where the work reservoir is infinitely hot
n_{w}\to\infty
, the associated green transitions in Fig.
1
become dominant,
and along them the populations become approximately equal (but remain still conditioned on the total number of excitations).
This allows us to coarse-grain
[
83
,
59
]
the populations by summarizing all states with same total number of excitations

\displaystyle Q_{n}=\sum_{M,m}\delta_{M+m,n}P_{M,m}\,.

(66)

As the states connected by work reservoir transitions equilibrate much faster in this limit, we have for their conditional probability

\displaystyle\lim_{n_{w}\to\infty}\left.\frac{P_{M,m}}{Q_{n}}\right|_{M+m=n}=\frac{1}{n+1}\,.

(67)

Summing over the respective states of Eq. (
II
) allows us to obtain a coarse-grained one-dimensional Markovian rate equation

\displaystyle\dot{Q}_{n}

\displaystyle={\sum_{M,m}\delta_{M+m,n}}\Big[\gamma_{c}(-\delta)(N-M-m+1)m\frac{P_{M,m-1}}{Q_{n-1}}Q_{n-1}

\displaystyle\qquad-\gamma_{c}(-\delta)(N-M-m)(m+1)\frac{P_{M,m}}{Q_{n}}Q_{n}

\displaystyle\qquad+\gamma_{c}(+\delta)(N-M-m)(m+1)\frac{P_{M,m+1}}{Q_{n+1}}Q_{n+1}

\displaystyle\qquad-\gamma_{c}(+\delta)(N-M-m+1)m\frac{P_{M,m}}{Q_{n}}Q_{n}

\displaystyle\qquad+\gamma_{h}(-\Delta)(N-M-m+1)M\frac{P_{M-1,m}}{Q_{n-1}}Q_{n-1}

\displaystyle\qquad-\gamma_{h}(-\Delta)(N-M-m)(M+1)\frac{P_{M,m}}{Q_{n}}Q_{n}

\displaystyle\qquad+\gamma_{h}(+\Delta)(N-M-m)(M+1)\frac{P_{M+1,m}}{Q_{n+1}}Q_{n+1}

\displaystyle\qquad-\gamma_{h}(+\Delta)(N-M-m+1)M\frac{P_{M,m}}{Q_{n}}Q_{n}

\displaystyle\qquad+\gamma_{w}(-\Delta+\delta)M(m+1)\frac{P_{M-1,m+1}}{Q_{n}}Q_{n}

\displaystyle\qquad-\gamma_{w}(-\Delta+\delta)(M+1)m\frac{P_{M,m}}{Q_{n}}Q_{n}

\displaystyle\qquad+\gamma_{w}(+\Delta-\delta)(M+1)m\frac{P_{M+1,m-1}}{Q_{n}}Q_{n}

\displaystyle\qquad-\gamma_{w}(+\Delta-\delta)M(m+1)\frac{P_{M,m}}{Q_{n}}Q_{n}\Big]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n_{w}\to\infty}}{{\approx}}R_{n,n+1}^{\rm cg}Q_{n+1}+R_{n,n-1}^{\rm cg}Q_{n-1}

\displaystyle\qquad-[R_{n-1,n}^{\rm cg}+R_{n+1,n}^{\rm cg}]Q_{n}\,,

(68)

where
n\in\{0,1,\ldots,N\}
denotes the total number of excitations and we have inserted the conditional probabilities.
In the coarse-grained rates, the mesostate-internal transitions due to the work reservoir cancel out, whereas the other reservoirs determine the
transitions between mesostates

\displaystyle R_{n,n+1}^{\rm cg}

\displaystyle=\sum_{M,m}\frac{\delta_{M+m,n}}{n+2}\Big[\Gamma_{c}(1+n_{c})(N-M-m)(m+1)

\displaystyle\qquad+\Gamma_{h}(1+n_{h})(N-M-m)(M+1)\Big]\,,

\displaystyle R_{n,n-1}^{\rm cg}

\displaystyle=\sum_{M,m}\frac{\delta_{M+m,n}}{n}\Big[\Gamma_{c}n_{c}(N-M-m+1)m

\displaystyle\qquad+\Gamma_{h}n_{h}(N-M-m+1)M\Big]\,,

(69)

which evaluate to Eq. (
II
) in the main text.
Note that the case
R_{0,-1}^{\rm cg}=0
has to be treated separately as the state with zero excitations does not need to be coarse-grained.
As a sanity check, we remark that for
N=1
, the proper coarse-grained rates (which one gets by applying an analogous procedure to the rate matrices in Sec.
A
) for a single QAR are reproduced.
We note that the resulting effective rates for cold and hot reservoir no longer obey local detailed balance, which allows energy to flow out of the cold reservoir.
The simple tri-diagonal form of this effective rate equation has the advantage that the stationary state can be computed analytically, since it obeys

\displaystyle\bar{Q}_{n}=\frac{R_{n,n-1}^{\rm cg}}{R_{n-1,n}^{\rm cg}}\frac{R_{n-1,n-2}^{\rm cg}}{R_{n-2,n-1}^{\rm cg}}\cdot\ldots\cdot\frac{R_{1,0}^{\rm cg}}{R_{0,1}^{\rm cg}}\bar{Q}_{0}\,,\qquad\sum_{n}\bar{Q}_{n}=1\,.

(70)

Making the ratios explicit with (
II
), we can determine
\bar{Q}_{0}
from the normalization condition and from that write the stationary cooling current with
\bar{n}=\frac{\Gamma_{c}n_{c}+\Gamma_{h}n_{h}}{\Gamma_{c}+\Gamma_{h}}
and
\alpha_{\bar{n}}=\frac{\bar{n}}{\bar{n}+1}
and
R_{ij}^{c}\equiv R_{ij}^{\rm cg}|_{\Gamma_{h}\to 0}

\displaystyle\bar{I}_{E}^{c}

\displaystyle=\delta\sum_{n=1}^{N}R_{n,n-1}^{c}\bar{Q}_{n-1}-\delta\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}R_{n,n+1}^{c}\bar{Q}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma_{c}\delta}{2}\sum_{n=0}^{N}\Big[(n+2)(N-n)n_{c}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-n(N-n+1)(1+n_{c})\Big]\bar{Q}_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma_{c}\Gamma_{h}\delta}{2(\Gamma_{c}+\Gamma_{h})}(n_{c}-n_{h})f_{N}(\bar{n})\,,

\displaystyle f_{N}(\bar{n})

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{g_{N}(\bar{n})+\alpha_{\bar{n}}^{N+1}h_{N}(\bar{n})}{\bar{n}+1-\alpha_{\bar{n}}^{N+1}\left[2+\bar{n}+N\right]}\,,

\displaystyle g_{N}(\bar{n})

\displaystyle\equiv 2(N-3\bar{n})(\bar{n}+1)\,,

\displaystyle h_{N}(\bar{n})

\displaystyle\equiv N^{2}+(5+4\bar{n})N+6(\bar{n}+1)^{2}\,.

(71)

In the current, only the term
(n_{c}-n_{h})
can turn negative, such that we recover the original cooling condition for
n_{w}\to\infty
given in (
27
).
Apart from that, the scaling factor
f_{N}(\bar{n})>0
can be analyzed for various limits:
First of all, we have
f_{1}(\bar{n})=\frac{2}{1+3\bar{n}}
, which yields the same current as (
22
).
Second, for very large
N\gg\bar{n}
, we can drop the terms with powers of
\alpha_{\bar{n}}<1
, such that
f_{N}(\bar{n})\to 2N
, and the current eventually will scale just linearly
\bar{I}_{E}^{c}\approx\frac{\Gamma_{c}\Gamma_{h}\delta}{\Gamma_{c}+\Gamma_{h}}(n_{c}-n_{h})N
.
Third, for
N\ll\bar{n}
we have
f_{N}(\bar{n})\to\frac{N(N+3)}{6\bar{n}}
, and we maintain a quadratic scaling for the current

\bar{I}_{E}^{c}\approx\frac{\Gamma_{c}\Gamma_{h}\delta(n_{c}-n_{h})}{12(\Gamma_{c}n_{c}+\Gamma_{h}n_{h})}N(N+3)
.
The crossover system size
N^{*}
between these regimes can be found by simply equating the limits and yields
N^{*}=12\bar{n}-3
.
These limits can be seen well in Fig.
2
(dashed magenta curves).
The crossover between two scaling regimes is thus quite analogous to previous results for collective qubit systems
[
60
]
.
We can see that the current becomes maximal when
n_{h}\to 0
(which can be reached by
\Delta\to\infty
and then implies
n_{c}\to\frac{\Gamma_{c}+\Gamma_{h}}{\Gamma_{c}}\bar{n}
).
Then, we can numerically maximize
(n_{c}-n_{h})f_{N}(\bar{n})\to\frac{\Gamma_{c}+\Gamma_{h}}{\Gamma_{c}}\bar{n}f_{N}(\bar{n})
as a function of
\bar{n}
only.
The position of this maximum is for large
N
roughly at
\bar{n}\approx N/6
, and the current scales quadratically in
N
at this maximum.

D.5
Interacting Pauli rate equation

Pauli rate equations can also be derived for interactions present in the system Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{S}
.
If these interactions can be expressed by the permutationally symmetric operators
\hat{C}_{2}
,
\hat{N}_{\delta}
and
\hat{N}_{\Delta}
(or equivalently by
\sum_{\alpha}\hat{J}_{\alpha}^{2}
,
\hat{J}_{3}
and
\hat{J}_{8}
) like in Eq. (
III
), it follows that the same eigenstates can be used for the representation of the problem, and only the eigenvalues change.
Labelling the eigenstates of
\hat{H}_{S}
with the multi-index
i
, the Pauli rate equation will then generically have the structure
[
2
]

\displaystyle\dot{P}_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\nu}\sum_{j}\left[R_{ij}^{\nu}P_{j}-R_{ji}^{\nu}P_{i}\right]\,,

\displaystyle R_{ij}^{\nu}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{\nu}(E_{j}-E_{i}){\left|\left<i\right|\hat{S}^{\nu}\left|j\right>\right|}^{2}\,.

(72)

The inherent local detailed balance for the transition rates then exponentially suppresses excitations into undesired Casimir subspaces and undesired excitation numbers, whereas relaxation from the excited eigenvalues down to the desired most productive cycle is still possible.
Thus, for
N=3k+1
with integer
k
and sufficiently large penalty parameters
\beta_{\nu}\alpha_{C/P}\gg 1
, we can neglect the excited states and constrain our considerations to the states from the maximum symmetry (Casimir) sector with
\left<\hat{C}_{2}\right>=N(N+3)/3
:

\left|k;k\right>
,
\left|k;k+1\right>
, and
\left|k+1;k\right>
.
These three states then have the energies

\displaystyle E_{0}

\displaystyle=k(\Delta+\delta)+\alpha_{P}/3\,,

\displaystyle E_{1}

\displaystyle=k\Delta+(k+1)\delta+\alpha_{P}/3\,,

\displaystyle E_{2}

\displaystyle=(k+1)\Delta+k\delta+\alpha_{P}/3\,,

(73)

such that the penalty
\alpha_{P}
cancels out in their differences.
As the eigenstates
\left|M;m\right>
remain the same, the quadratic enhancement from Eq. (
II
) is preserved, and we obtain the current (
22
) with
\Gamma_{\nu}\to\Gamma_{\nu}\left(\frac{N+2}{3}\right)^{2}
, leading to the corresponding enhancement (
18
) in the main text.

Appendix E
Bosonization

The
8
operators
\{\hat{J}_{3},\hat{J}_{8},\hat{J}^{h}_{+},\hat{J}^{h}_{-},\hat{J}^{c}_{+},\hat{J}^{c}_{-},\hat{J}^{w}_{+},\hat{J}^{w}_{-}\}
inherit a closed algebra from the associated single-qutrit versions

\displaystyle\left[\hat{J}_{3},\hat{J}^{h}_{+}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\hat{J}^{h}_{+}}{2}\,,\;\left[\hat{J}_{3},\hat{J}^{c}_{+}\right]=-\frac{\hat{J}^{c}_{+}}{2}\,,\;\left[\hat{J}_{3},\hat{J}^{w}_{+}\right]=\hat{J}^{w}_{+}\,,

\displaystyle\left[\hat{J}_{8},\hat{J}^{h}_{+}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{3}\hat{J}^{h}_{+}}{2}\,,\;\left[\hat{J}_{8},\hat{J}^{c}_{+}\right]=\frac{\sqrt{3}\hat{J}^{c}_{+}}{2}\,,

\displaystyle\left[\hat{J}^{h}_{+},\hat{J}^{h}_{-}\right]

\displaystyle=\sqrt{3}\hat{J}_{8}+\hat{J}_{3}\,,\;\left[\hat{J}^{c}_{+},\hat{J}^{c}_{-}\right]=\sqrt{3}\hat{J}_{8}-\hat{J}_{3}\,,

\displaystyle\left[\hat{J}^{w}_{+},\hat{J}^{w}_{-}\right]

\displaystyle=2\hat{J}_{3}\,,

\displaystyle\left[\hat{J}^{h}_{+},\hat{J}^{c}_{-}\right]

\displaystyle=\hat{J}^{w}_{+}\,,\;\left[\hat{J}^{h}_{+},\hat{J}^{w}_{-}\right]=-\hat{J}^{c}_{+}\,,

\displaystyle\left[\hat{J}^{c}_{+},\hat{J}^{w}_{+}\right]

\displaystyle=-\hat{J}^{h}_{+}\,.

(74)

Other independent (which do not follow from Hermitian conjugation) commutators just vanish.

E.1
Four-mode Holstein-Primakoff transform

The above commutation relations can be realized with four bosonic modes with annihilation operators
\hat{a}_{\Delta}
,
\hat{a}_{\delta}
,
\hat{b}_{\Delta}
, and
\hat{b}_{\sigma}
obeying the usual bosonic commutation relations by using a generalization
[
84
]
of the Holstein-Primakoff-transform

\displaystyle\hat{J}_{3}

\displaystyle=\hat{b}_{\sigma}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{\sigma}+\frac{1}{2}\hat{b}_{\Delta}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{\Delta}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\hat{a}_{\Delta}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{\Delta}-\hat{a}_{\delta}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{\delta}\right)-\frac{N_{b}}{2}\,,

\displaystyle\hat{J}_{8}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\hat{b}_{\Delta}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{\Delta}+\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\left(\hat{a}_{\Delta}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{\Delta}+\hat{a}_{\delta}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{\delta}\right)-\frac{N_{a}}{\sqrt{3}}-\frac{N_{b}}{2\sqrt{3}}\,,

\displaystyle\hat{J}^{h}_{+}

\displaystyle=\hat{a}_{\Delta}^{\dagger}\sqrt{N_{a}-\hat{a}_{\Delta}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{\Delta}-\hat{a}_{\delta}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{\delta}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\hat{b}_{\Delta}^{\dagger}\sqrt{N_{b}-\hat{b}_{\Delta}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{\Delta}-\hat{b}_{\sigma}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{\sigma}}\,,

\displaystyle\hat{J}^{c}_{+}

\displaystyle=\hat{a}_{\delta}^{\dagger}\sqrt{N_{a}-\hat{a}_{\Delta}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{\Delta}-\hat{a}_{\delta}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{\delta}}+\hat{b}_{\Delta}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{\sigma}\,,

\displaystyle\hat{J}^{w}_{+}

\displaystyle=\hat{a}_{\Delta}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{\delta}-\hat{b}_{\sigma}^{\dagger}\sqrt{N_{b}-\hat{b}_{\Delta}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{\Delta}-\hat{b}_{\sigma}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{\sigma}}\,.

(75)

Here, the integer numbers
N_{a}\geq 0
and
N_{b}\geq 0
determine the physically admissable states, i.e., they have to be adjusted to match the behaviour of the collective qutrit operators such as e.g. the Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{S}

\displaystyle=\Delta\left[\hat{a}_{\Delta}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{\Delta}+\hat{b}_{\Delta}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{\Delta}+\hat{b}_{\sigma}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{\sigma}+\frac{N-N_{a}-2N_{b}}{3}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+\delta\left[\hat{a}_{\delta}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{\delta}-\hat{b}_{\sigma}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{\sigma}+\frac{N-N_{a}+N_{b}}{3}\right]\,.

(76)

In particular, the Fock states
\left|M,m,Q,q\right>
with
M
,
m
,
Q
, and
q
denoting the eigenvalues of
\hat{a}_{\Delta}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{\Delta}
,
\hat{a}_{\delta}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{\delta}
,
\hat{b}_{\Delta}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{\Delta}
, and
\hat{b}_{\sigma}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{\sigma}
, respectively, are physically admissable when

\displaystyle[(N_{a}=M+m)\lor(Q=0)]\land

\displaystyle[(N_{b}=Q+q)\lor(M=0)]\land

\displaystyle[(q=0)\lor(m=0)]\,.

(77)

For example, the special case
Q=q=N_{b}=0
fulfils these conditions and admits an even simpler representation of the algebra with just two bosonic modes, which we discuss below, and that corresponds to the fully symmetric subspace discussed in the main text.
For the subspace with second largest Casimir operator eigenvalue one has to use the four-boson representation instead.
As one always has
N_{a}+N_{b}\leq N
and
0\leq M+m\leq N_{a}
as well as
0\leq Q+q\leq N_{b}
, it follows that largest Clebsch-Gordan coefficients (and therefore the largest currents) may originate from the fully symmetric subspace.

E.2
Two-mode Holstein-Primakoff transform

When
Q=q=N_{b}=0
, the transformation requires only two bosonic modes
[
85
,
76
]
with a non-negative integer
N_{a}

\displaystyle\hat{J}_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\hat{a}_{\Delta}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{\Delta}-\hat{a}_{\delta}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{\delta}\right)\,,

\displaystyle\hat{J}_{8}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\left(\hat{a}_{\Delta}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{\Delta}+\hat{a}_{\delta}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{\delta}\right)-\frac{N_{a}}{\sqrt{3}}\,,

\displaystyle\hat{J}^{h}_{+}

\displaystyle=\hat{a}_{\Delta}^{\dagger}\sqrt{N_{a}-(\hat{a}_{\Delta}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{\Delta}+\hat{a}_{\delta}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{\delta})}\,,

\displaystyle\hat{J}^{c}_{+}

\displaystyle=\hat{a}_{\delta}^{\dagger}\sqrt{N_{a}-(\hat{a}_{\Delta}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{\Delta}+\hat{a}_{\delta}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{\delta})}\,,

\displaystyle\hat{J}^{w}_{+}

\displaystyle=\hat{a}_{\Delta}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{\delta}\,,

(78)

and analogously for the lowering operators.
It turns out that the fully symmetric subspace can be covered by the choice
N_{a}=N
(number of qutrits).
Then, the fully symmetric states discussed in the main text are equivalent to the Fock state representation with just two bosonic modes

\left|M;m\right>\equiv\left|M,m,0,0\right>
.

The system Hamiltonian (
E.1
) then simply assumes the form
\hat{H}_{S}=\Delta\hat{a}_{\Delta}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{\Delta}+\delta\hat{a}_{\delta}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{\delta}
.
As a sanity check, representing the Casimir operator in terms of the bosons we get the maximum eigenvalue valid for the fully symmetric subspace

\left<\hat{C}_{2}\right>=\frac{N(N+3)}{3}
.
From the bosonic properties it is then straightforward to compute the Clebsch-Gordan coefficients (
II
) of the symmetric subspace discussed in the main text.

E.3
Master equation for large
N

The master equation (
II
) can in the symmetric subspace and for large
N
such that
\left<a_{\nu}^{\dagger}a_{\nu}\right>\ll N
be simplified by expanding the roots in (
E.2
), which yields

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{i}\left[\Delta\hat{a}_{\Delta}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{\Delta}+\delta\hat{a}_{\delta}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{\delta},\rho\right]

(79)

\displaystyle\qquad+N\Gamma_{c}(1+n_{c})\left[\hat{a}_{\delta}\rho\hat{a}_{\delta}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{\hat{a}_{\delta}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{\delta},\rho\right\}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+N\Gamma_{c}n_{c}\left[\hat{a}_{\delta}^{\dagger}\rho\hat{a}_{\delta}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{\hat{a}_{\delta}\hat{a}_{\delta}^{\dagger},\rho\right\}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+N\Gamma_{h}(1+n_{h})\left[\hat{a}_{\Delta}\rho\hat{a}_{\Delta}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{\hat{a}_{\Delta}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{\Delta},\rho\right\}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+N\Gamma_{h}n_{h}\left[\hat{a}_{\Delta}^{\dagger}\rho\hat{a}_{\Delta}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{\hat{a}_{\Delta}\hat{a}_{\Delta}^{\dagger},\rho\right\}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+\Gamma_{w}(1+n_{w})\left[\hat{a}_{\delta}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{\Delta}\rho\hat{a}_{\Delta}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{\delta}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{\hat{a}_{\Delta}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{\delta}\hat{a}_{\delta}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{\Delta},\rho\right\}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+\Gamma_{w}n_{w}\left[\hat{a}_{\Delta}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{\delta}\rho\hat{a}_{\delta}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{\Delta}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{\hat{a}_{\delta}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{\Delta}\hat{a}_{\Delta}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{\delta},\rho\right\}\right]\,.

In absence of the work reservoir
\Gamma_{w}\to 0
, we find that relaxation to the steady state is therefore scaling with
Nt

\displaystyle\left<\hat{a}_{\delta}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{\delta}\right>_{t}=N_{\delta}^{0}e^{-N\Gamma_{c}t}+n_{c}(1-e^{-N\Gamma_{c}t})\,,

(80)

and analogously for the hot reservoir.
The above also manifests superradiant decay as the relaxation time is inversely proportional to
N
.
However, the above master equation is valid only in regimes where
\left<a_{\nu}^{\dagger}a_{\nu}\right>\ll N
, such that we cannot describe the regime of boosted cooling power with it.

Appendix F
Full Counting Statistics

The starting point for the determination of currents and their fluctuations is a generalized (or tilted) Liouvillian (or rate matrix) equation of the form

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}={\cal L}(\chi)\rho\,,

(81)

where
\rho
is the vectorized part of interest of the density matrix (this could be the complete system density matrix or just the populations of a relevant subspace) and
{\cal L}(\chi)
the matrix representing the corresponding part of the Redfield or LGKS dissipator or of the rate matrix that depends on the counting field
\chi
.
In the vectorized space, the trace is computed via
{\rm Tr}\left\{\rho\right\}=\vec{\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}^{\rm T}\cdot\vec{\rho}
, and trace conservation then implies
that
{\rm Tr}\left\{{\cal L}(0)\sigma\right\}=0
for any operator
\sigma
.

The moments of the conjugate variable
n
to the counting field can be obtained by taking derivatives of the moment-generating function

\displaystyle M(\chi,t)={\rm Tr}\left\{e^{{\cal L}(\chi)t}\rho_{0}\right\}

(82)

via
\left<n^{\alpha}\right>_{t}=(-\mathrm{i}\partial_{\chi})^{\alpha}M(\chi,t)|_{\chi=0}
.
Analogously, one may obtain cumulants from the cumulant-generating function

C(\chi,t)=\ln M(\chi,t)
by acting with the corresponding derivatives on it.
In the long-term limit and for systems with a unique stationary state, one can show that
C(\chi,t)\to\lambda(\chi)t
, where
\lambda(\chi)
is the dominant eigenvalue of
{\cal L}(\chi)
(the one with largest real part, that fulfils
\lambda(0)=0
).
We are interested in the long-term limit of the lowest two cumulants of the current, i.e., in the current
\bar{I}=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{d}{dt}\left<n\right>_{t}=-\mathrm{i}\lambda^{\prime}(0)
and the noise
\bar{S}_{I}=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{d}{dt}\left[\left<n^{2}\right>_{t}-\left<n\right>_{t}^{2}\right]=-\lambda^{\prime\prime}(0)
.
Unfortunately, for our problems, the dominant eigenvalue is not analytically known.
Second order perturbation theory for non-Hermitian matrices is rather non-trivial
[
86
]
and for larger
N
numerical differentiation
[
78
]
is not stable.
Therefore, to compute the stationary current and noise, we use a different approach
[
53
]
, derived from the counting statistics of time-dependent conductors
[
87
]
.
Trace conservation implies that the stationary current can be obtained via

\displaystyle\bar{I}=-\mathrm{i}{\rm Tr}\left\{{\cal L}^{\prime}(0)\bar{\rho}\right\}\,,

(83)

where
\bar{\rho}
is the solution to the equation
{\cal L}(0)\bar{\rho}=0
normalized to
{\rm Tr}\left\{\bar{\rho}\right\}=1
(i.e., the steady state).
With that, we can also compute the stationary noise

\displaystyle\bar{S}_{I}=-{\rm Tr}\left\{{\cal L}^{\prime\prime}(0)\bar{\rho}\right\}-2\mathrm{i}{\rm Tr}\left\{{\cal L}^{\prime}(0)\bar{\sigma}\right\}\,,

(84)

where the auxiliary variable
\bar{\sigma}
is the solution to the equation

{\cal L}(0)\bar{\sigma}=\mathrm{i}{\cal L}^{\prime}(0)\bar{\rho}+\bar{I}\bar{\rho}
normalized to
{\rm Tr}\left\{\bar{\sigma}\right\}=0
.

Appendix G
Tight-coupling of energy currents

The Pauli rate equation (
II
) has the form

\dot{P}_{i}=\sum_{\nu}\sum_{j}\left(R_{ij}^{\nu}P_{j}-R_{ji}^{\nu}P_{i}\right)
,
where
P_{i}
are occupation probabilities of energy eigenstate
i
(which in our state corresponds to the state
\left|M;m\right>
of the fully symmetric sector) and
\nu\in\{c,h,w\}
labels the reservoirs.
We can split the stationary populations into the contributions from the individual cycles that couple to state
i

\displaystyle\bar{P}_{i}=\sum_{\cal C}\bar{P}_{i}^{\cal C}\,,

(85)

where e.g. in Fig.
1
right panel the corner states take part in just one cycle, states on the facets take part in three cycles, and states in the interior take part in six cycles.
Then, the steady-state condition can be rewritten as

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{c}n_{c}\sum_{ij}h^{c}_{i}h^{c*}_{j}{\rm Tr}\left\{\left(\left|1\right>\left<0\right|\right)_{i}\left(\left|0\right>\left<1\right|\right)_{j}\bar{\rho}\right\}

will be additive in the number of qutrits: For such a steady state, in the above formula only the terms with
