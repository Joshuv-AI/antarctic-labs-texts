A Local Gaussian Process Regression Approach to Frequency Response Function Estimation footnoteinfo
Xiaozhu Fang, Yu Xu, Tianshi Chen

Abstract
Frequency response function (FRF) estimation is a classical subject in system identification. In the past two decades, there have been remarkable advances in developing local methods for this subject, e.g., the local polynomial method, local rational method, and iterative local rational method. The recent concentrations for local methods are two issues: the model order selection and the identification of lightly damped systems. To address these two issues, we propose a new local method called local Gaussian process regression (LGPR). We show that the frequency response function locally is either analytic or resonant, and this prior knowledge can be embedded into a kernel-based regularized estimate through a dot-product kernel plus a resonance kernel induced by a second-order resonant system. The LGPR provides a new route to tackle the aforementioned issues. In the numerical simulations, the LGPR shows the best FRF estimation accuracy compared with the existing local methods, and moreover, the LGPR is more robust with respect to sample size and noise level.

1
Introduction

Frequency response function (FRF) estimation in the frequency domain is a classic subject in the field of system identification, see e.g.
Ljung (1999)
;
Pintelon and Schoukens (2012)
. The early methods for FRF estimation include the classic spectral estimation method and the empirical transfer function estimate (ETFE), see e.g.
Ljung (1999)
. In the past two decades, there have been remarkable advances in developing new methods for this subject, see
Schoukens et al. (2018)
for a review. Among these methods, the local polynomial method (LPM)
(
Schoukens et al., 2009
)
, local rational method (LRM)
(
McKelvey and Guérin, 2012
)
, and iterative local rational method (ILRM)
(
Geerardyn, 2016
)
have gained significant attention due to their capability to almost reject the leakage. The pros and cons of the above local methods have been debated recently
(
Schoukens et al., 2018
;
Pintelon et al., 2020
)
.

For the LPM, since polynomials are employed as basis functions within each sliding window, the model order selection issue is unavoidable. The order of polynomials is often set to be 2
(
Schoukens et al., 2018
)
, or tuned by minimizing a criterion, e.g., the modified minimum
description length (MDL)
(
Pintelon et al., 2020
)
. Moreover, the identification of lightly damped systems is a critical issue for the LPM because the FRFs of lightly damped systems have resonance peaks, which are not smooth and are hard to capture by the LPM if the number of frequency bins is not much within the sliding window. In contrast, the LRM often gives better performance than the LPM by using a local rational model within each sliding window. The LRM also has the model order selection issue, and additionally, its noise variance estimate is biased due to the Levy approximation, especially for a low signal-to-noise ratio (SNR)
(
Geerardyn, 2016
, Guideline 3.1)
. As for the ILRM, it has less bias in the noise variance estimate than the LRM, but it involves nonconvex optimizations with severe local minima issues
(
Verbeke and Schoukens, 2019
)
. The success of the ILRM highly depends on the dedicated model order selection and the starting point of the optimization, where
Pintelon et al. (2020)
suggested using the result of LRM as the starting point. To summarize, none of the existing local methods has shown consistent superiority over others.

In contrast with the above local methods, the Gaussian process regression (GPR) is a global method that makes use of all data points in the FRF estimation. Moreover, the GPR is a kernel-based nonparametric method: on the one hand, the prior knowledge about the FRF to be estimated can be embedded in the estimated FRF by designing a suitable kernel, e.g.,
Latarie and Chen (2016)
, and on the other hand, the model complexity of the estimated FRF can be tuned continuously by tuning the hyper-parameters through e.g. the empirical Bayes method.

In this paper, we are interested in developing a new local method inspired by the global GPR.
More specifically,

•

we first propose a kernel-based regularized version of the LPM and call it the local regularized polynomial method
(LRPM). If the system is not lightly damped, then
the coefficients of the polynomial basis functions are absolutely summable, and then the diagonal (DI) kernel in
Chen et al. (2012)
is introduced to embed this prior knowledge;

•

we then show that the LRPM can be extended to a more general local GPR (LGPR). Particularly, we show that, for lightly damped systems, since their FRFs locally can be either analytic or resonant, a dot-product (DP) kernel
(
Smola et al., 2000
)
and a resonance kernel induced by a second-order resonant system can be designed to incorporate such prior knowledge, respectively.

•

we finally demonstrate the validity of the LGPR with the DP plus one resonance (DPpR1) kernel through numerical simulations, compared with the existing local methods.

The remaining part of this paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, FRF estimation and the LPM are briefly introduced. The main derivations of the LRPM and LGPR are shown in Section
3
and Section
4
, respectively. In Section
5
, the proposed method is tested by simulations.

Background and Problem Formulation

2.1
Frequency Response Function Estimation

We consider the causal, stable, continuous-time, single-input single-output, and linear time-invariant system with output disturbed by filtered white noise:

\displaystyle y(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}u(t-\tau)g(\tau)d\tau+v(t),

\displaystyle v(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}e(t-\tau)h(\tau)d\tau,

Let the input and output be sampled at

\displaystyle X(k)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum\limits_{n=0}^{N-1}x(nT_{s})e^{-\frac{2\pi jnk}{N}}.

(2)

For white Gaussian noise

The frequency domain correspondent of (

\displaystyle Y(k)=G(j\omega_{k})U(k)+T(j\omega_{k})+V(k),

\displaystyle V(k)=H(j\omega_{k})E(k),\ \omega_{k}=2\pi k/NT_{s},\ k=0,1,\cdots,\left\lfloor N/2\right\rfloor,

2.2
Existing Local Methods

Recently, it started to be clear that there is plenty of structure in
T(j\omega_{k})
, and some local methods have been proposed for the FRF estimation, see
Schoukens et al. (2018)
. Specifically, the local methods use low-order parametric models
G_{k}(j\omega;\theta_{k})
and
T_{k}(j\omega;\theta_{k})
to approximate
G(j\omega)
and
T(j\omega)
in the local window,

\displaystyle[j\omega_{k-\ell},\cdots,j\omega_{k-1},j\omega_{k},j\omega_{k+1},\cdots,j\omega_{k+\ell}],

where
j\omega_{k}
is any excited frequency in (
3
),
\theta_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{k}}
being the parameters to be estimated, and
n_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
being the dimension of
\theta_{k}
.
Then the predicted output in the local window is given by, with
r=-\ell,\ell+1,\cdots,\ell
,

\displaystyle\hat{Y}(k+r;\theta_{k})=G_{k}(j\omega_{r};\theta_{k})U(k+r)+T_{k}(j\omega_{r};\theta_{k}).

(4)

Then the parameters
\theta_{k}
can be estimated from the local data
Z_{k}
by minimizing an estimation criterion
\mathcal{V}
,

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}_{k}=\underset{\theta_{k}}{\argmin}\ \mathcal{V}(\theta_{k},Z_{k}),\hskip 9.24994ptZ_{k}=\{U(k+r),Y(k+r)\}_{r=-\ell}^{\ell}.

Finally, the central of the estimated local models is remained for the final estimate, i.e.,

\displaystyle\hat{G}(j\omega_{k})=G_{k}(0;\hat{\theta}_{k}),\hskip 9.24994pt\hat{T}(j\omega_{k})=T_{k}(0;\hat{\theta}_{k}),

(6)

and the noise variance estimates are given by

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{k}=\frac{1}{2\ell+1-n_{k}}\sum\limits_{r=-\ell}^{\ell}|Y(k+r)-\hat{Y}(k+r;\hat{\theta}_{k})|^{2}.

(7)

The existing local methods use different local models and estimation criteria.
The local polynomial method (LPM) chooses polynomials as the local models and squared local output error (LOE) as the estimation criterion:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}G_{k}(j\omega_{r};\theta_{k})=B^{c}_{k}(j\omega_{r}),\hskip 9.24994ptT_{k}(j\omega_{r};\theta_{k})=I^{c}_{k}(j\omega_{r}),\\
\theta_{k}=[b^{c}_{0,k},\cdots,b^{c}_{n_{b,k},k},i^{c}_{0,k},\cdots,i^{c}_{n_{i,k},k}]^{T},\\
n_{k}=n_{b,k}+n_{i,k}+2,\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{\text{LOE}}(\theta_{k},Z_{k})=\sum\limits_{r=-\ell}^{\ell}|Y(k+r)-\hat{Y}(k+r;\theta_{k})|^{2},

(11)

where
X^{c}_{k}(j\omega)
with
X=B,A,I
denote the polynomials with parameters
x_{n,k}^{c}
and polynomial order
n_{x,k}

\displaystyle X^{c}_{k}(j\omega)=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{n_{x,k}}x^{c}_{n,k}(\alpha\omega)^{n},\text{ with }x_{n,k}^{c},\alpha\in\mathbb{C},

(12)

with
\alpha
being a constant.
The local rational method (LRM) chooses rational functions as the local models and the local levy (LL) method as the estimation criterion:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}G_{k}(j\omega_{r};\theta_{k})=\frac{B^{c}_{k}(j\omega_{r})}{A^{c}_{k}(j\omega_{r})},\hskip 9.24994ptT_{k}(j\omega_{r};\theta_{k})=\frac{I^{c}_{k}(j\omega_{r})}{A^{c}_{k}(j\omega_{r})},\\
\theta_{k}\!=\![a^{c}_{1,k},\!\cdots\!,a^{c}_{n_{a,k},k},b^{c}_{0,k},\!\cdots\!,b^{c}_{n_{b,k},k},i^{c}_{0,k},\!\cdots\!,i^{c}_{n_{i,k},k}]^{T},\\
n_{k}\!=\!n_{a,k}+n_{b,k}+n_{i,k}+2,\hskip 9.24994pta_{0,k}^{c}\!=\!1,\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{\text{LL}}(\theta_{k},Z_{k})\!=\!\sum\limits_{r=-\ell}^{\ell}|A^{c}_{k}(j\omega_{r})|^{2}|Y(k+r)\!-\!\hat{Y}(k+r;\theta_{k})|^{2}.

(16)

The iterative local rational method (ILRM) chooses rational functions (
2.2
) as the local models and the LOE method (
11
) as the estimation criterion.

The comparison of the above local methods has been discussed in
Verbeke and Schoukens (2019)
;
Schoukens et al. (2018)
;
Pintelon et al. (2020)
.
In short, the LPM has difficulties in identifying lightly damped systems, while the LRM and ILRM do not. Moreover, the LRM has a biased noise variance estimate due to the LL method (
16
), while the ILRM involves a nonconvex optimization and suffers from severe local minima issues. It was suggested in
Pintelon et al. (2020)
to use the result of the LRM as the starting point of the optimization for the ILRM.

2.3
Problem Statement

The model order selection is essential for the existing local methods. How to choose the polynomial orders can be heuristic, e.g.
n_{a,k},n_{b,k}
, and
n_{i,k}
are suggested to be
2

(
Schoukens et al., 2018
)
, or the polynomial orders can be tuned by minimizing a criterion, e.g. modified minimum description length (MDL)
(
Pintelon et al., 2020
)
as follows:

\displaystyle\underset{n_{a,k},n_{b,k},n_{i,k}}{\argmin}\quad\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{k}e^{\log(4\ell+2)\frac{n_{k}}{2\ell+1-n_{k}-2}}

(17)

where
n_{a,k}=0
for the LPM.
In this paper, we first develop a kernel-based regularized version of the LPM and call it the local regularized polynomial method (LRPM). The LRPM can tune the model order in a continuous manner.

On the other hand, the LRPM also has difficulties in identifying lightly damped systems; to tackle that, we propose the local Gaussian process regression (LGPR), as an extension of the LRPM.

Kernel Design for the LRPM

Let

\displaystyle\left[\begin{matrix}\mathfrak{R}[\theta_{k}]\\

3.2.1
Finding True

The task of kernel design now becomes the parametrization of the real-valued kernel matrix

However, the true

3.3
What if Assumption
3.1
is invalid?

For lightly damped systems, the resonance poles are too close to the imaginary axis, and thus Assumption
3.1
is invalid at some
\omega_{k}
, and the power series in Theorem
3.1
do not converge. Thus,
the LRPM (and also the LPM) cannot be applied to lightly damped systems.

To tackle lightly damped systems, the polynomial models in the LPM are often replaced by rational function models, e.g., the LRM and ILRM. As an alternative, we
extend the LRPM to the local Gaussian process regression (LGPR).

4
Local Gaussian Process Regression

The LGPR is straightforward by employing the GPR
(
Latarie and Chen, 2016
, Thm. 3)
in the local window. Nonetheless, we prefer to derive the LGPR as an extension of the LRPM because this extension relationship would be important for the kernel design in Sec.
4.2
.

4.1
The LGPR: an Extension of the LRPM

It follows from Prop.
3.1

Proposition 4.1

4.2
Kernel Design from the LRPM to the LGPR

The extension relationship between the LRPM and LGPR implies that all kernels for the LRPM can be transformed into the kernels for the LGPR via (
36
).

Proposition 4.2

(Dot-Product Kernel)

Substituting the

DI kernel (
35
) into (
36
), we obtain

\displaystyle\Gamma_{G_{k}}^{\text{DP}}\!=\!\sum\limits_{n=0}^{n_{b,k}}(\alpha_{G}\lambda^{n}\!+\!\beta_{G}\kappa^{n})\phi^{n}(\phi^{n})^{H},

(40)

\displaystyle C_{G_{k}}^{\text{DP}}\!=\!\sum\limits_{n=0}^{n_{b,k}}(\alpha_{G}\lambda^{n}\!-\!\beta_{G}\kappa^{n})\phi^{n}(\phi^{n})^{T}

(41)

where
\phi^{n}
is given in (
18
), and (
40
) takes the form of the dot-product (DP) kernel, aka. the power series kernel, see e.g.
(
Smola et al., 2000
;
Zwicknagl, 2009
)
. Further, if
n_{b,k}\rightarrow\infty
, then (
40
) converges to

\displaystyle\begin{matrix}{M_{G_{k}}}^{\text{DP}}(\eta_{k})\\
{M_{T_{k}}}^{\text{DP}}(\eta_{k})\end{matrix}:\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}&\Gamma_{G_{k}}^{\text{DP}}=\frac{\alpha_{G}}{1-\lambda\phi^{1}(\phi^{1})^{T}}+\frac{\beta_{G}}{1-\kappa\phi^{1}(\phi^{1})^{T}}\\
&C_{G_{k}}^{\text{DP}}=\frac{\alpha_{G}}{1-\lambda\phi^{1}(\phi^{1})^{T}}-\frac{\beta_{G}}{1-\kappa\phi^{1}(\phi^{1})^{T}}\\
\end{array}\right.

where
\eta_{k}=[\alpha_{G},\lambda,\beta_{G},\kappa]
, and the convergence holds because all entries of
\lambda^{1/2}\phi^{1}
and
\kappa^{1/2}\phi^{1}
are within the unit disk.

Henceforth, the DP kernel is referred to as (
4.2
) by default, and the DP kernel (
4.2
) embeds the prior knowledge of analytic smoothness, i.e., the local function is analytic within the unit disk centering at
j\omega_{k}
.

4.3
Kernel Design for Lightly Damped Systems

On the other hand, the LGPR can immediately use the existing kernels from the global GPR, e.g., the diagonal-correlated (DC) kernel
(
Latarie and Chen, 2016
, Eq. (53))
, and the DC kernel plus multiple resonance kernels (DCpRn)
(
Hallemans et al., 2022
, Eq. (A.3))
, with

\Gamma_{G_{k}}(j\omega,j\omega^{\prime})=C_{G_{k}}(j\omega,-j\omega^{\prime})
,

\displaystyle\Gamma_{G_{k}}^{\text{DC}}(j\omega,j\omega^{\prime})=\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\frac{1}{\beta\!+\!j\omega\!-\!j\omega^{\prime}}\bigg(\frac{1}{\alpha\!+\!\beta/2\!+\!j\omega}\!+\!\frac{1}{\alpha\!+\!\beta/2\!-\!j\omega^{\prime}}\bigg)

(44)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{G_{k}}^{\text{DCpRn}}(j\omega,j\omega^{\prime})\!=\!\Gamma_{G_{k}}^{\text{DC}}(j\omega,j\omega^{\prime})\!+\!\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Gamma_{G_{k}}^{\text{R,i}}(j\omega,j\omega^{\prime}),

(45)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{G_{k}}^{\text{R,i}}(j\omega,j\omega^{\prime})\!=\!\frac{\gamma_{1,i}^{2}(j\omega+\beta_{1,i})(-j\omega^{\prime}+\beta_{1,i})+\gamma_{2,i}^{2}\beta_{2,i}^{2}}{((j\omega+\beta_{1,i})^{2}+\beta_{2,i}^{2})((-j\omega^{\prime}+\beta_{1,i})^{2}+\beta_{2,i}^{2})},

(46)

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt\beta_{1,i}>0,\beta_{2,i},\gamma_{1,i},\gamma_{2,i}\geq 0,i=1,\cdots,n,

where
\eta_{k}=[\lambda,\alpha,\beta]
for the DC kernel (
44
) and
\eta_{k}=[\lambda,\alpha,\beta,\{\beta_{1,i},\beta_{2,i},\gamma_{1,i},\gamma_{2,i}\}_{i=1}^{n}]
for the DCpRn kernel (
45
), and (
46
) is named the resonance kernel with Rn indicating the sum of
n
resonance kernels.
It is shown in
Hallemans et al. (2022)
that the DCpRn kernel (
45
) has a better performance than the DC kernel (
44
), but it has too many hyper-parameters, leading to difficulties in the hyper-parameter estimation.

However, the local FRF is more likely to have only one resonance, and, by exploiting the partial fractional decomposition (PFD), the vicinity of this resonance can be captured by a second-order resonant system.
Recall that the DP kernel (
4.2
) embeds a stronger smoothness than the DC kernel (
44
). Then we have the following proposition.

Proposition 4.3

(DCpR1 Kernel)

The DCpR1 kernel, (
45
) with
n=1
, can be used in the LGPR to handle lightly damped systems.

Proposition 4.4

(DPpR1 Kernel)

The DPpR1 kernel, i.e., the sum of the DP kernel (
4.2
) and R1 kernel (
46
), can be used in the LGPR to handle lightly damped systems.

The DC kernel (
44
) and the DP kernel (
4.2
) have close performances for the FRF estimations, while their noise variance estimations may have a large difference. This is related to the overfitting issue:
the DC kernel (
44
) can be reduced to an identity kernel
I_{4\ell+2}
when
\beta\rightarrow 0
and
\alpha\rightarrow\infty
, implying both the transient term and the noise term in (
37
) are identity kernels
I_{4\ell+2}
. Consequently, the noise would be mixed with the transient and thus underestimated. In this regard, the DP kernel (
4.2
) is superior to the DC kernel (
44
).

6
Conclusion

This work proposes a local Gaussian process regression and new kernels for FRF estimation. The proposed local Gaussian process regression (LGPR) provides a new route to tackle the model order selection and the identification of lightly damped systems. It is shown in the simulations that the LGPR has the best FRF estimation accuracy in terms of the mean square error (MSE), and the LGPR is more robust to different sample sizes and noise levels.
