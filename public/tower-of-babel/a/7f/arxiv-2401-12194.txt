Poincaré inequality and Quantitative De Giorgi method for hypoelliptic operators Thanks: AL is supported by the Cambridge Trust. CM is supported by ERC grant MAFRAN
Francesca Anceschi, Helge Dietert, Jessica Guerand, Amélie Loher, Clément Mouhot, Annalaura Rebucci

Abstract
. We propose a systematic elementary approach based on trajectories to prove Poincaré inequalities for hypoelliptic equations with an arbitrary number of Hörmander commutators, both in the local and in the non-local case. Our method generalises and simplifies the method introduced in [ 8 ] in the local case with one commutator, and extended in [ 13 ] to the non-local case with one commutator. It draws inspiration from the paper [ 17 ] , although we use different trajectories. We deduce Harnack inequalities and Hölder regularity along the line of the De Giorgi method. Our results recover those in [ 2 ] in the local case, and are new in the non-local case.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
The question at hand

This paper is devoted to the regularity theory of hypoelliptic
equations with rough coefficients. We consider equations with an
arbitrary number of commutators to obtain regularity in all
directions, in the sense of Hörmander
[
9
]
, and whose
diffusive part is either local (second-order) or non-local
(fractional order). Beyond their structural intrinsic importance,
such equations naturally appear in financial models (see

Section

1.5
).

We consider an unknown function
f=f(x,t)
satisfying equations of the form

(1.1)

\partial_{t}f+{\mathcal{B}}f+{\mathcal{A}}^{*}{\mathcal{A}}f=0

where
{\mathcal{B}}
is a first-order differential operator and

{\mathcal{A}}
is a differential, or fractional differential operator in
some of the directions
x^{(0)}
of
x
. We assume no regularity
on the coefficients of the diffusive part
{\mathcal{A}}
. Then
provided that
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
satisfy the Hörmander
commutator condition
[
9
]
, one expects the following

Poincaré inequality
to hold for weak non-negative
sub-solutions:

\int_{Q_{+}}\left(f-\frac{1}{|Q_{-}|}\int_{Q_{-}}f\right)_{+}\lesssim\int_{\Omega}|{\mathcal{A}}f|

where
Q^{-},Q^{+}
are two disjoint cylinders in the open domain

\Omega
so that
Q^{+}
is a time-translation of
Q^{-}
into the
future. In this paper we prove such an inequality with a
quantitative control on the constant for a large class of
equations of the type (
1.1
). The main novelty is
the method itself, but the result is
also new when
\mathcal{A}
is non-local and more than one commutator is
involved.

The proof is based on the combination of a method introduced
in
[
8
]
and the explicit construction of
appropriate new trajectories inspired partly
from
[
17
]
. The Poincaré inequality is a
powerful integral way to measure the oscillation of the
sub-solution, and its use in this context goes back
to
[
16
,
15
]
. We deduce quantitatively the De Giorgi
lemmas, the Hölder regularity for weak solutions, and the (weak)
Harnack inequality for sub-solutions following the methodology
recently developed
in
[
8
,
13
]
.

Examples of equations

When

Kolmogorov equation

(1.2)

\partial_{t}f+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f=\nabla_{v}\cdot\left[A\nabla_{v}f\right]

When the number of commutators

\partial_{t}f+x^{(\kappa-1)}\cdot\nabla_{x^{(\kappa)}}f+x^{(\kappa-2)}\cdot\nabla_{x^{(\kappa-1)}}f+\dots+v\cdot\nabla_{x^{(1)}}f=\nabla_{v}\cdot\left[A\nabla_{v}f\right]

,

\displaystyle\forall\,(x^{(\kappa)},x^{(\kappa-1)},\dots,x^{(1)},v,t)

\displaystyle\Lambda^{-1}\leq A(x^{(\kappa)},x^{(\kappa-1)},\dots,x^{(1)},v,t)\leq\Lambda.

We denote

for notational consistency.

In the fractional (non-local) case and with
\kappa=1

(1.6)

\partial_{t}f+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f=\left(-\Delta_{v}\right)^{\frac{\beta}{2}}\cdot\left[a\left(-\Delta_{v}\right)^{\frac{\beta}{2}}f\right]

on the unknown
f=f(x,v,t)
depending on
x,v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and

t\in{\mathbb{R}}
, with
\beta\in(0,1)
and a scalar function

that
is measurable and so that, for some
\Lambda\geq 1
,

(1.7)

a(x,v,t)\in[\Lambda^{-1},\Lambda].

When the number of commutators
\kappa\geq 2
, the prototypical
non-local equation is

(1.8)

\partial_{t}f+x^{(\kappa-1)}\cdot\nabla_{x^{(\kappa)}}f+x^{(\kappa-2)}\cdot\nabla_{x^{(\kappa-1)}}f+\dots+v\cdot\nabla_{x^{(1)}}f=\left(-\Delta_{v}\right)^{\frac{\beta}{2}}\cdot\left[a\left(-\Delta_{v}\right)^{\frac{\beta}{2}}f\right]

on the unknown
f
which depends on

x^{(\kappa)},x^{(\kappa-1)},\dots,x^{(1)},x^{(0)}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,

t\in{\mathbb{R}}
, and where the function
a
is measurable and so that,
for some
\Lambda\geq 1
,

(1.9)

\forall\,(x^{(\kappa)},x^{(\kappa-1)},\dots,x^{(1)},v,t)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{(\kappa+1)d+1},\quad\Lambda^{-1}\leq a(x^{(\kappa)},x^{(\kappa-1)},\dots,x^{(1)},v,t)\leq\Lambda.

In fact, our results still apply when the fractional diffusion
operator on the right hand side of (
1.6
)
and (
1.8
) is replaced by the more general
nonlocal operator

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}f\left(x^{(\kappa)},\dots,x^{(1)},x^{(0)},t\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\left[f\left(x^{(\kappa)},\dots,x^{(1)},w,t\right)-f\left(x^{(\kappa)},\dots,x^{(1)},x^{(0)},t\right)\right]K\left(x^{(\kappa)},\dots,x^{(1)},x^{(0)},w,t\right){\,\mathrm{d}}w

1.3.
General assumptions (H)

We denote the variable
z:=(x,t)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N+1}
with
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
and

x=(x^{(\kappa)},\dots,x^{(1)},x^{(0)})
with each

x^{(i)}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{i}}

d_{i}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}

d_{0}\geq d_{1}\geq\dots\geq d_{\kappa}\geq 1

We consider (
1.1
) with operators
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}

as follows. The operator
{\mathcal{B}}
is given by

{\mathcal{B}}:=({\mathsf{B}}x)\cdot\nabla_{x},

where the
N\times N
matrix
{\mathsf{B}}
has the form (
\mathbb{O}
denotes a
block matrix of zeros)

(1.12)

{\mathsf{B}}:=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{O}&{\mathsf{B}}_{\kappa}&\mathbb{O}&\cdots&\cdots&\mathbb{O}\\
\vdots&\mathbb{O}&{\mathsf{B}}_{\kappa-1}&\mathbb{O}&\cdots&\mathbb{O}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\mathbb{O}&\ddots&\mathbb{O}&\mathbb{O}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&{\mathsf{B}}_{2}&\mathbb{O}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\mathbb{O}&{\mathsf{B}}_{1}\\
\mathbb{O}&\mathbb{O}&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\mathbb{O}\end{pmatrix},

with
d_{i}\times d_{i-1}
blocks
{\mathsf{B}}_{i}
which are constant
matrices of rank
d_{i}
.

The operator
{\mathcal{A}}
is a linear operator acting only on the
variable
x^{(0)}
and
\beta
-homogeneous in this variable with

\beta\in(0,1]
and it satisfies the following pointwise
control from above and integral control from below, given

\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}({\mathbb{R}}^{N})
:

(1.13)

\|{\mathcal{A}}\varphi\|_{\infty}\lesssim\|\nabla_{(0)}\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}^{\beta}\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}^{1-\beta},\quad\int\left|{\mathcal{A}}\varphi\right|{\,\mathrm{d}}z\gtrsim\int\left|(-\Delta_{v})^{\beta/2}\varphi\right|{\,\mathrm{d}}z.

The transport operator
\mathcal{T}:=\partial_{t}+{\mathcal{B}}

naturally defines the non-commutative group of

hyper-Galilean transformations
, that generalises the
Galilean transformations:

(1.14)

(\tilde{x},\tilde{t})\circ(x,t)=(x+\exp(t{\mathsf{B}})\tilde{x},t+\tilde{t}),\hskip 14.22636pt(x,t),\ (\tilde{x},\tilde{t})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N+1}.

This transformation leaves the operator
\mathcal{T}
invariant:

(\mathcal{T}f)(\tilde{z}\circ z)=\mathcal{T}[f(\tilde{z}\circ\cdot)](z)
.

The scaling properties of
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
naturally
define the
hypoelliptic dilations
for
r>0
:

(1.15)

\delta_{r}:=\textrm{diag}\left(r^{1+2\kappa\beta}\mathbb{I}_{d_{\kappa}},\dots,r^{1+2\beta}\mathbb{I}_{d_{1}},r\mathbb{I}_{d_{0}},r^{2\beta}\right)

and we have the relation

({\mathcal{T}}+{\mathcal{A}}^{*}{\mathcal{A}})[f\circ\delta_{r}]=r^{2\beta}\big[\left({\mathcal{T}}+{\mathcal{A}}^{*}{\mathcal{A}}\right)f\big]\circ\delta_{r}.

These transformations allow to define the
hypoelliptic
cylinders

\displaystyle Q_{r}(\tilde{z}):=\tilde{z}\circ\left(\delta_{r}\left(Q_{1}\right)\right)\quad\text{ with }\quad Q_{1}:=B_{1}\times\ldots\times B_{1}\times(-1,0]\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{N+1}.

The (local) Kolmogorov equation (
1.2
) corresponds to

\kappa=1
,
d_{0}=d_{1}=d
,

(x^{(1)},x^{(0)})=(x,v)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,

{\mathcal{A}}:=\sqrt{A}\nabla_{v}
with a
d\times d
-matrix

A=A(x,v,t)
measurable symmetric so that

A\in[\Lambda^{-1},\Lambda]
, and the matrix

(1.16)

{\mathsf{B}}:=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{O}&\mathbb{I}_{d}\\
\mathbb{O}&\mathbb{O}\end{pmatrix}.

The (local) higher-order Kolmogorov equation (
1.4
)
corresponds to
d_{i}=d
for all
i
,

(x^{(\kappa)},\dots,x^{(1)},x^{(0)})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{(\kappa+1)d}
,

{\mathcal{A}}:=\sqrt{A}\nabla_{(0)}
with a
d\times d
-matrix

A=A(x^{(\kappa)},\dots,x^{(1)},x^{(0)},t)
measurable symmetric
so that
A\in[\Lambda^{-1},\Lambda]
, and the matrix

{\mathsf{B}}:=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{O}&\mathbb{I}_{d}&\mathbb{O}&\ldots&\mathbb{O}\\
\mathbb{O}&\mathbb{O}&\mathbb{I}_{d}&\mathbb{O}&\vdots\\
\mathbb{O}&\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\mathbb{O}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\mathbb{I}_{d}\\
\mathbb{O}&\mathbb{O}&\ldots&\ldots&\mathbb{O}\end{pmatrix}.

The fractional Kolmogorov equations (

)
and (
1.8

1.4.
Main result

Let us first give the definitions of weak sub-solutions and sub/super-solutions used in the results.

Definition 1
.

Let
\Omega=\Omega_{x}\times\Omega_{t}=\Omega_{(N-1)}\times\cdots\times\Omega_{(1)}\times\Omega_{(0)}\times\Omega_{t}
be an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
.
A function
f
is a
locally integrable weak sub-solution
of (
1.1
) if
f
and
{\mathcal{A}}f
are in
L^{1}_{loc}(\Omega)
and satisfies

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f+{\mathcal{B}}f+{\mathcal{A}}^{*}{\mathcal{A}}f\leq 0,

in the distributional sense for non-negative test functions
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
.
A function
f
is a
sub-solution
of (
1.1
) if
f
is a weak sub-solution in
L^{\infty}(\Omega_{t};L^{2}(\Omega_{x}))\cap L^{2}(\Omega_{(N-1)}\times\cdots\times\Omega_{(1)}\times\Omega_{t};H^{\beta}(\Omega_{(0)}))
and satisfies

(1.17)

\partial_{t}f+{\mathcal{B}}f\in L^{2}(\Omega_{(N-1)}\times\cdots\times\Omega_{(1)}\times\Omega_{t};H^{-\beta}(\Omega_{(0)})).

A function
f
is a
super-solution
of (
1.1
) if
-f
is a
sub-solution
.

Remark 2
.

The condition (
1.17
) is used in
[
7
]
and it is weaker than the condition

\partial_{t}f+{\mathcal{B}}f\in L^{2}(\Omega),

used in
[
19
]
. In the definition of sub-solution, one could also consider a third condition, only valid for the local case, that reads as follows:

\forall G:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\textrm{ in }C^{2}\textrm{ with }G^{\prime}\geq 0,G^{\prime\prime}\geq 0\textrm{ both bounded, then }G(f)\textrm{ is a weak sub-solution}.

This condition was introduced in
[
8
]
and is weaker than the other two listed in the definition above. Indeed, it allows for instance to consider
f=f(t)=\mathbf{1}_{t\leq 0}
as a sub-solution which explains the fact that the cylinders are disjoint in the following theorems.

(1)

(3)

It is likely that the approach can be extended to the

(4)

(5)

1.5.
Motivation

Kolmogorov equations appear in the theory of stochastic
processes: equation (
1.2
) was studied by
Kolmogorov in 1934
[
11
]
when
A
is the identity
matrix to understand the “time-integrated Brownian motion”. The
equation is then the Kolmogorov forward equation for the
process

{\rm d}V_{t}={\rm d}W_{t},\quad{\rm d}X_{t}=V_{t}{\,\mathrm{d}}t.

Kolmogorov obtained explicit formula for its fundamental solution
in
[
11
]
which inspired the seminal
work
[
9
]
. Integrating twice in time the Brownian motion
would lead to equation (
1.4
) with
\kappa=2
and
A

the identity matrix. Due to this connection to stochastic
processes, several mathematical models involving linear and nonlinear Kolmogorov type equations have also appeared in finance, and in particular equations of
type (
1.4
) appear in various models for pricing of
path-dependent financial instruments. For
example the equation for
P=P(S,A,t)

(1.21)

\partial_{t}P+\tfrac{1}{2}{\sigma}^{2}S^{2}\partial^{2}_{S}P+(\log S)\partial_{A}P+r(S\partial_{S}P-P)=0,\qquad S>0,\,A,t\in\mathbb{R},

arises in the Black and Scholes option pricing problem

\begin{cases}{\rm d}S_{t}=\mu S_{t}{\,\mathrm{d}}t+{\sigma}S_{t}{\,\mathrm{d}}W_{t},\\
{\rm d}A_{t}=S_{t}{\,\mathrm{d}}t,\end{cases}

where
{\sigma}
is the volatility of the stock price
S
,
\mu
is the
interest rate of a riskless bond and
P=P(S,A,t)
is the price
of the Asian option depending on the price of the stock
S
, the
geometric average
A
of the past price and the time to maturity

t
. For a more exhaustive treatment of the applications of
Kolmogorov operators to finance and to stochastic theory, we
refer to the monograph
[
18
]
by Pascucci.

Let us close this paragraph by mentioning that analogously,
in the non-local case when
a=1
,
or equivalently when
\mathcal{A}
corresponds to
the fractional Laplacian, the stochastic
process underlying this fractional diffusion is a Lévy process.

1.6.
Strategy of proof

The starting point is the method developed
in
[
8
]
for proving the Poincaré inequality
using trajectories and deducing the second Lemma of De Giorgi and
the weak Harnack inequality.

The strategy to obtain a Poincaré inequality for the local case
with one commutator in
[
8
]
can be summarised as
follows. The first step is to mollify the characteristic function
of
Q^{-}
, by adding an error term

\displaystyle\int_{Q^{+}}\left(f-\langle f\rangle_{Q^{-}}\right)_{+}{\,\mathrm{d}}z\leq

\displaystyle\int_{z_{+}\in Q^{+}}\left(\int_{z_{-}\in Q^{-}}\left[f(z_{+})-f(z_{-})\right]\varphi_{\varepsilon}(w,y){\,\mathrm{d}}z_{-}\right)_{+}{\,\mathrm{d}}z_{+}

\displaystyle+\varepsilon^{2d}\|f\|_{L^{2}(Q_{R})}

with
0\leq\varphi_{\varepsilon}\leq 1
smooth,
\varepsilon>0
,

z_{+}=(x,v,t)\in Q^{+}
and
z_{-}=(y,w,s)\in Q^{-}
. The second step
consists in constructing piecewise affine trajectories following
the two vector fields

\mathcal{T}:=\partial_{t}+v\cdot\nabla_{x}
and

We then want to integrate by parts the

\displaystyle(v,x,t)
