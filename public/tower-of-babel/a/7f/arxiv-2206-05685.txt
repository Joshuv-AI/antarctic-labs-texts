A simple derivation of moiré-scale continuous models for twisted bilayer graphene
Éric Cancès, Louis Garrigue, David Gontier

Abstract
We provide a formal derivation of a reduced model for twisted bilayer graphene (TBG) from Density Functional Theory. Our derivation is based on a variational approximation of the TBG Kohn-Sham Hamiltonian and asymptotic limit techniques. In contrast with other approaches, it does not require the introduction of an intermediate tight-binding model. The so-obtained model is similar to that of the Bistritzer-MacDonald (BM) model but contains additional terms. Its parameters can be easily computed from Kohn-Sham calculations on single-layer graphene and untwisted bilayer graphene with different stackings. It allows one in particular to estimate the parameters w AA w_{\rm AA} and w AB w_{\rm AB} of the BM model from first-principles. The resulting numerical values, namely w AA = w AB ≃ 126 w_{\rm AA}=w_{\rm AB}\simeq 126 meV for the experimental interlayer mean distance are in good agreement with the empirical values w AA = w AB = 110 w_{\rm AA}=w_{\rm AB}=110 meV obtained by fitting to experimental data. We also show that if the BM parameters are set to w AA = w AB ≃ 126 w_{\rm AA}=w_{\rm AB}\simeq 126 meV, the BM model is an accurate approximation of our reduced model.



Moiré materials
Andrei et al. 2021
;
Carr et al. 2020
have attracted a lot of interest in condensed matter physics since, notably, the experimental discovery of Mott insulating and nonconventional superconducting phases
Cao et al. 2018
in twisted bilayer graphene (TBG) for specific small twist angles
\theta
. In particular, the experiments reported in
Cao et al. 2018
were done with
\theta\simeq 1.1^{\circ}
. For such small twist angles, the moiré pattern is quite large and typically contains of the order of 11,000 carbon atoms. In addition, the system is aperiodic (incommensurate), except for a countable set of values of
\theta
. All this makes brute force first-principle calculations extremely challenging.

Most theoretical investigations on TBG rely on continuous models
Mele 2010
;
Bistritzer and MacDonald 2011a
;
Bistritzer and MacDonald 2011b
;
Lopes dos Santos et al. 2012
such as the Bistritzer-MacDonald (BM) model
Bistritzer and MacDonald 2011b
, an effective continuous periodic model describing low-energy excitations in TBG close to half-filling. The BM Hamiltonian is a self-adjoint operator on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathbb{C}^{4})
given by

H^{\rm BM}_{\theta}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}v_{\rm F}\bm{\sigma}_{-\theta/2}\cdot(-i\nabla)&\bm{V}(k_{\theta}{\mathbf{x}})\\
\bm{V}(k_{\theta}{\mathbf{x}})^{*}&v_{\rm F}\bm{\sigma}_{\theta/2}\cdot(-i\nabla)\end{array}\right),

(1)

where
v_{\rm F}
is the Fermi velocity in single-layer graphene,
\bm{\sigma}_{\pm\theta/2}=e^{\mp i\frac{\theta}{4}\sigma_{3}}(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})e^{\pm i\frac{\theta}{4}\sigma_{3}}
are rotated Pauli matrices, and
k_{\theta}=\frac{8\pi}{3a_{0}}\sin(\theta/2)
, with
a_{0}
the single-layer graphene lattice constant. The function
\bm{V}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}^{2}
is quasiperiodic at the so-called moiré scale (see Section
II.4
for details) and depends on two empirical parameters
w_{\rm AA}
and
w_{\rm AB}
describing interlayer coupling in AA and AB stacking respectively. A rigorous mathematical derivation of the BM model from a tight-binding Hamiltonian whose parameters satisfy suitable scaling laws, was recently proposed in
Watson et al. 2022
.
A simplified chiral BM model, obtained by setting
w_{\rm AA}=0
, was notably used in
Tarnopolsky et al. 2019
;
Becker et al. 2021
;
Watson and Luskin 2021
to prove the existence of perfectly flat bands at the Fermi level for a sequence of so-called “magic” angles. From a physical point of view, the existence of partially occupied almost flat bands in the single-particle picture may reflect the presence of localized strongly correlated electrons, and provide a possible explanation of the experimentally observed superconducting behavior
Po et al. 2018
. An alternative approach to using effective models at the moiré scale is to develop atomic-scale models and efficient computational methods adapted to incommensurate 2D heterostructures
Trambly de Laissardière et al. 2012
;
Jung and MacDonald 2014
;
Carr et al. 2017
;
Carr et al. 2018a
;
Le and Do 2018
.

This article is concerned with the derivation of BM-like effective models directly from Density Functional Theory (DFT). In contrast with other approaches
Bistritzer and MacDonald 2011b
;
Moon and Koshino 2013
;
Carr et al. 2019
;
Fang et al. 2019
;
Koshino and Nam 2020
;
Bernevig et al. 2021
;
Watson et al. 2022
, our derivation does not involve an intermediate tight-binding model and is based on real-space (not momentum-space) computations.

I
Approximation of the Kohn-Sham Hamiltonian for TBG

I.1
Single-layer graphene

We denote the position variable by
{\mathbf{r}}=({\mathbf{x}},z)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
where
{\mathbf{x}}=(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
z\in\mathbb{R}
are respectively the longitudinal (in-plane) and transverse (out-of-plane) position variables. Let
V
be the Kohn-Sham potential for a single-layer graphene in the horizontal plane
z=0
. The space group of graphene is
{\rm Dg80}={\rm D}_{6h}\ltimes\mathbb{L}
(p6/mmm), so
V
has the honeycomb symmetry and is
{\mathbb{L}}
-periodic, where
\mathbb{L}=\mathbb{Z}{\mathbf{a}}_{1}+\mathbb{Z}{\mathbf{a}}_{2}
and

{\mathbf{a}}_{1}=a_{0}\begin{pmatrix}1/2\\
-\sqrt{3}/2\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad{\mathbf{a}}_{2}=a_{0}\begin{pmatrix}1/2\\
\sqrt{3}/2\end{pmatrix}.

Here,
a_{0}=\sqrt{3}a
is the graphene lattice constant (
a\simeq 0.142
nm
\simeq 2.68
bohr is the carbon-carbon nearest neighbor distance). We set
\Omega:=\mathbb{R}^{2}/\mathbb{L}
the Wigner-Seitz cell.

Figure 1
:
Single-layer graphene. Above: atomic positions of the carbon atoms (A and B sublattices) in the physical space, and lattice vectors
{\mathbf{a}}_{1}
and
{\mathbf{a}}_{2}
. Below: reciprocal lattice vectors
{\mathbf{a}}_{1}^{*}
and
{\mathbf{a}}_{2}^{*}
, and first Brillouin zone in momentum space; the positions of the
{\mathbf{K}}
and
{\mathbf{K}}^{\prime}
Dirac points are also indicated.

The single-layer graphene Kohn-Sham Hamiltonian reads

H^{(1)}:=-\frac{1}{2}\Delta+V.

(2)

We denote by
H_{\mathbf{k}}^{(1)}
its Bloch fibers. Recall that these operators have domains representing the
{\mathbf{k}}
–quasiperiodic boundary conditions
\Phi({\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{R}},z)=e^{i{\mathbf{k}}\cdot{\mathbf{R}}}\Phi({\mathbf{x}},z)
for all
{\mathbf{R}}\in\mathbb{L}
. The map
{\mathbf{k}}\mapsto H_{\mathbf{k}}^{(1)}
is
{\mathbb{L}^{*}}
-periodic, where
{\mathbb{L}^{*}}
is the dual lattice of
\mathbb{L}
. Explicitly,
{\mathbb{L}^{*}}={\mathbf{a}}_{1}^{*}\mathbb{Z}+{\mathbf{a}}_{2}^{*}\mathbb{Z}
with

{\mathbf{a}}_{1}^{*}=\sqrt{3}k_{D}\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{3}/2\\
-1/2\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad{\mathbf{a}}_{2}^{*}=\sqrt{3}k_{D}\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{3}/2\\
1/2\end{pmatrix},

where
k_{D}:=\frac{4\pi}{3\,a_{0}}
. At the special Dirac point

{\mathbf{K}}:=\tfrac{1}{3}({\mathbf{a}}_{1}^{*}+{\mathbf{a}}_{2}^{*})=k_{D}(1,0)^{T},

(3)

H^{(1)}_{\mathbf{K}}
has an eigenvalue of multiplicity
2
at the Fermi level
\mu_{F}
. We denote by
\Phi_{1}
and
\Phi_{2}
two corresponding eigenvectors, oriented so that
\langle\Phi_{1},(-{\rm i}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}})\Phi_{1}\rangle=\langle\Phi_{2},(-{\rm i}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}})\Phi_{2}\rangle=(0,0)^{T}
, and

\displaystyle\langle\Phi_{1}, (-{\rm i}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}})\Phi_{2}\rangle=v_{\rm F}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
-{\rm i}\end{pmatrix},

(4)

with
v_{F}>0
the Fermi velocity. We refer to the Supplementary Material
A
for an explanation of how to achieve such an orientation. Finally, we denote by
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
the periodic parts of
\Phi_{1}
and
\Phi_{2}
,
i.e.

u_{j}({\mathbf{r}}):=\Phi_{j}({\mathbf{r}}){\rm e}^{-{\rm i}{\mathbf{K}}\cdot{\mathbf{x}}}
.

I.2
Kohn-Sham model for TBG

We now consider two parallel layers of graphene, separated by a distance
d>0
, and with a twist angle
\theta
between the two. More precisely, we first place the top layer in the plane
z=\tfrac{d}{2}
and the bottom layer in the plane
z=-\tfrac{d}{2}
in AA stacking, and then rotate counterclockwise the top layer by
-\theta/2
and the bottom layer by
\theta/2
around the
z
-axis, placing the origin at the center of a carbon hexagon. We set

c_{\theta}:=\cos\frac{\theta}{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\varepsilon_{\theta}:=2\sin\frac{\theta}{2}.

Note that
\varepsilon_{\theta}\sim\theta
in the small-angle limit. We denote by
R_{\theta}
the 2D rotation matrix of angle
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
, specifically

R_{\theta}:=c_{\theta}\;{\mathbb{I}}_{2}-\varepsilon_{\theta}J,\quad\text{with}\quad J:=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix},

and we introduce the unitary operator

(U_{d,\theta}f)({\mathbf{x}},z):=f(R_{-\theta/2}^{*}{\mathbf{x}},z)=f\left(c_{\theta}{\mathbf{x}}-\tfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon_{\theta}J{\mathbf{x}},z-\tfrac{d}{2}\right).

The inverse of
U_{d,\theta}
is
U_{d,\theta}^{-1}=U_{d,\theta}^{*}=U_{-d,-\theta}
.

For twist angles
\theta
giving rise to a periodic structure at the moiré scale,
the TBG Kohn-Sham potential is a well-defined moiré-periodic function. It is unclear how to define a mean-field potential for incommensurate twist angles. This problem actually occurs for all infinite aperiodic systems (see
Cancès et al. 2013
for a mathematical definition of a mean-field model in an ergodic setting). Here, we assume that this potential exists, and can be approximated using the procedure in
Tritsaris et al. 2016
. We consider an approximate Kohn-Sham potential for the TBG of the form

V^{(2)}_{d,\theta}({\mathbf{x}},z):=(U_{d,\theta}V)({\mathbf{x}},z)+(U_{d,\theta}^{-1}V)({\mathbf{x}},z)+V_{{\rm int},d}(z).

Each component
U_{d,\theta}^{\pm 1}V
represents a layer of graphene shifted by
\pm\tfrac{d}{2}{\mathbf{e}}_{z}
, and twisted by an angle
\mp\theta/2
. The last term
V_{{\rm int},d}
is a correction which takes into account the relaxation of the Kohn-Sham potential due to interlayer coupling. This term is constructed as follows. For each disregistry vector
{\mathbf{y}}\in{\Omega}
, we denote by
V^{(2)}_{d,{\mathbf{y}}}
the mean-field Kohn-Sham potential for the configuration where the two layers are aligned (no twist angle) with the top one shifted by
{\mathbf{y}}
in the longitudinal direction. The potential
V_{{\rm int},d}(z)
is defined as the average

V_{{\rm int},d}(z)=\fint_{{\Omega}}V_{{\rm int},d,{\mathbf{y}}}(z){\rm d}{\mathbf{y}}

where
\fint_{\Omega}:=|\Omega|^{-1}\int_{\Omega}
, and with
V_{{\rm int},d,{\mathbf{y}}}(z)
defined by

\fint_{{\Omega}}\left(V^{(2)}_{d,{\mathbf{y}}}({\mathbf{x}},z)-V({\mathbf{x}},z+\tfrac{d}{2})-V({\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{y}},z-\tfrac{d}{2})\right){\rm d}{\mathbf{x}}.

In other words,
V_{\rm int,d}
is the mismatch between the interacting Kohn-Sham potential and the non-interacting one, averaged over all possible disregistries
{\mathbf{y}}\in\Omega
. Note that
V_{\rm int,d}(z)
only depends on the
z
–variable and satisfies
V_{{\rm int},d}(-z)=V_{{\rm int},d}(z)
.

In what follows, we study the approximate TBG Kohn-Sham Hamiltonian

H_{d,\theta}^{(2)}:=-\frac{1}{2}\Delta+V^{(2)}_{d,\theta}({\mathbf{x}},z).

(5)

Our goal is to derive a 2D reduced model describing the low-energy band structure around the Fermi level
\mu_{\rm F}
in the limit of small twist angles.

II
Reduced model

The potential
V^{(2)}_{d,\theta}
is of the form
V^{(2)}_{d,\theta}({\mathbf{x}},z)=v_{d}\left(c_{\theta}{\mathbf{x}},\varepsilon_{\theta}{\mathbf{x}},z\right)
, with

\displaystyle v_{d}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{X}},z):=

\displaystyle\;V({\mathbf{x}}-\tfrac{1}{2}J{\mathbf{X}},z-\tfrac{d}{2})+V({\mathbf{x}}+\tfrac{1}{2}J{\mathbf{X}},z+\tfrac{d}{2})+V_{{\rm int},d}(z).

The potential
v_{d}
is
\mathbb{L}
–periodic in the first variable
{\mathbf{x}}
, and
2J\mathbb{L}
-periodic in its second variable
{\mathbf{X}}
. In the limit
\theta\to 0
,
v_{d}\left(c_{\theta}{\mathbf{x}},\varepsilon_{\theta}{\mathbf{x}},z\right)
has a natural two-scale structure, so that
H_{d,\theta}^{(2)}
could be studied using adiabatic theory, semiclassical analysis, and/or homogenization theory. In this article however, we take a different route, and present a simple approximation scheme to derive an effective Hamiltonian describing electronic transport around the Fermi level.

II.1
Variational approximation of low-energy wavepacket dynamics

The main idea of our approach is to project the time-dependent Schrödinger equation

i\partial_{t}\Psi(t,{\mathbf{r}})=(H^{(2)}_{d,\theta}-\mu_{\rm F})\Psi(t,{\mathbf{r}}),\quad\Psi(0,{\mathbf{r}})=\Psi_{0}({\mathbf{r}})

(6)

onto the two-scale approximation space

\mathcal{X}_{d,\theta}:=\left\{(\alpha:\Phi)_{d,\theta},\;\alpha:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}^{4}\right\},

(7)

where we set

\left(\alpha:\Phi\right)_{d,\theta}({\mathbf{x}},z):=\sum_{\eta\in\{\pm 1\}\atop j\in\{1,2\}}\alpha_{\eta,j}\left(\varepsilon_{\theta}{\mathbf{x}}\right)(U_{d,\theta}^{\eta}\Phi_{j})({\mathbf{x}},z).

The trial functions in
\mathcal{X}_{d,\theta}
are linear combinations of four wave-packets, each one consisting of an envelope function
\alpha
oscillating at the moiré scale
\varepsilon_{\theta}^{-1}
multiplied by one of the two (translated and rotated) Bloch waves
\Phi_{1}
or
\Phi_{2}
of one of the two layers. Note that both the TBG approximation subspace
\mathcal{X}_{d,\theta}
and Hamiltonian
H_{d,\theta}^{(2)}
depend on the small parameter
\theta
.

Given an initial state of the form
\Psi_{0}=(\alpha^{0}:\Phi)_{d,\theta}
, the true solution
\Psi(t)
of (
6
) is expected to be close to
(\alpha(\varepsilon_{\theta}t):\Phi)_{d,\theta}
up to times of order
\varepsilon_{\theta}^{-1}
, where
\alpha(t)
satisfies
\alpha(t=0)=\alpha^{0}
, and solves the projected equation

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}\left\langle\left(\widetilde{\alpha}:\Phi\right)_{d,\theta},\left(\alpha(\varepsilon_{\theta}t):\Phi\right)_{d,\theta}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\quad=\left\langle\left(\widetilde{\alpha}:\Phi\right)_{d,\theta},\left(H_{d,\theta}^{(2)}-\mu_{F}\right)\left({\alpha}(\varepsilon_{\theta}t):\Phi\right)_{d,\theta}\right\rangle

(8)

for all
\widetilde{\alpha}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}^{4}
. The time variable has to be rescaled as
\tau:=\varepsilon_{\theta}t
in order to obtain wave-packet propagation with finite velocity at the moiré scale.

II.2
Formulation of the reduced model

It follows from tedious calculations detailed in Appendix
A
that if we let
\theta
go to zero in (
II.1
) for fixed trial smooth functions
\widetilde{\alpha}
and
\alpha
, we obtain the asymptotic equality

{\rm i}\partial_{\tau}\langle\widetilde{\alpha},\mathcal{S}_{d}\alpha(\tau)\rangle=\langle\widetilde{\alpha},\mathcal{H}_{d,\theta}\alpha(\tau)\rangle+O(\varepsilon_{\theta}^{\infty}),

(9)

where the overlap operator
\mathcal{S}_{d}
and the Hamiltonian operator
\mathcal{H}_{d,\theta}
act on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathbb{C}^{4})
, and are defined by

\mathcal{S}_{d}:=\begin{pmatrix}{\mathbb{I}}_{2}&\Sigma_{d}({\mathbf{X}})\\
\Sigma_{d}^{*}({\mathbf{X}})&{\mathbb{I}}_{2}\end{pmatrix}

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{d,\theta}

\displaystyle:=\begin{pmatrix}v_{\rm F}\bm{\sigma}_{-\theta/2}\cdot(-{\rm i}\nabla_{\mathbf{X}})&\varepsilon_{\theta}^{-1}{\mathbb{V}}_{d}({\mathbf{X}})\\
\varepsilon_{\theta}^{-1}{\mathbb{V}}_{d}({\mathbf{X}})^{*}&v_{\rm F}\bm{\sigma}_{\theta/2}\cdot(-{\rm i}\nabla_{\mathbf{X}})\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon_{\theta}^{-1}{\mathbb{W}}_{d}^{+}&0\\
0&\varepsilon_{\theta}^{-1}{\mathbb{W}}_{d}^{-}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle\quad+\begin{pmatrix}0&c_{\theta}J(-{\rm i}\nabla\Sigma_{d}({\mathbf{X}}))\cdot(-{\rm i}\nabla)\\
c_{\theta}J(-{\rm i}\nabla\Sigma_{d}^{*})({\mathbf{X}})\cdot(-{\rm i}\nabla)&0\end{pmatrix}-\frac{\varepsilon_{\theta}}{2}\nabla\cdot\left(\begin{pmatrix}{\mathbb{I}}_{2}&\Sigma_{d}({\mathbf{X}})\\
\Sigma_{d}^{*}({\mathbf{X}})&{\mathbb{I}}_{2}\end{pmatrix}\nabla\bullet\right).

(10)

The
2\times 2
matrix-valued functions
\Sigma_{d}({\mathbf{X}})
,
{\mathbb{V}}_{d}({\mathbf{X}})
and
{\mathbb{W}}_{d}({\mathbf{X}})
are given by

\displaystyle\left[\Sigma_{d}({\mathbf{X}})\right]_{jj^{\prime}}

\displaystyle:={\rm e}^{-{\rm i}J{\mathbf{K}}\cdot{\mathbf{X}}}(\!(u_{j},u_{j^{\prime}})\!)^{+-}_{d}({\mathbf{X}}),

\displaystyle\left[{\mathbb{V}}_{d}({\mathbf{X}})\right]_{jj^{\prime}}

\displaystyle:={\rm e}^{-{\rm i}J{\mathbf{K}}\cdot{\mathbf{X}}}(\!(\left(V+V_{\text{int},d}(\cdot+\tfrac{d}{2})\right)u_{j},u_{j^{\prime}})\!)_{d}^{+-}({\mathbf{X}}),

\displaystyle\left[{\mathbb{W}}_{d}^{\pm}({\mathbf{X}})\right]_{jj^{\prime}}

\displaystyle:=(\!(u_{j}\overline{u_{j^{\prime}}},V)\!)_{d}^{\pm\mp}({\mathbf{X}})+\left(W^{\pm}_{\text{int},d}\right)_{jj^{\prime}},

where for all
\mathbb{L}
-periodic functions
f
and
g
,

\displaystyle\left(\!\left(f,g\right)\!\right)_{d}^{\eta\eta^{\prime}}({\mathbf{X}}):=

\displaystyle\;\int_{\Omega\times\mathbb{R}}\overline{f\left({\mathbf{x}}-\eta\tfrac{1}{2}J{\mathbf{X}},z-\eta\tfrac{d}{2}\right)}\,g\left({\mathbf{x}}-\eta^{\prime}\tfrac{1}{2}J{\mathbf{X}},z-\eta^{\prime}\tfrac{d}{2}\right)\,{\rm d}{\mathbf{x}}\,{\rm d}z,

(11)

and where

\displaystyle\left(W^{\pm}_{\text{int},d}\right)_{jj^{\prime}}:=\int_{\Omega\times\mathbb{R}}(\overline{u_{j}}u_{j^{\prime}})({\mathbf{x}},z\mp\tfrac{d}{2})V_{{\rm int},d}(z){\rm d}{\mathbf{x}}\,{\rm d}z.

All these quantities can be computed from the single-layer potential
V
, the Bloch wave-functions
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
, and the Kohn-Sham correction potential
V_{{\rm int},d}
.

It is not obvious that the operator
\mathcal{H}_{d,\theta}
is Hermitian. It is however the case. For instance, one can check that
\overline{(\!(u,v)\!)^{+-}_{d}}({\mathbf{X}})=(\!(\overline{u},\overline{v})\!)^{+-}_{d}({\mathbf{X}})
, which proves that the matrices
{\mathbb{W}}^{+}_{d}({\mathbf{X}})
and
{\mathbb{W}}_{d}^{-}({\mathbf{X}})
are Hermitian. In addition, from the equality,
{\rm div}J\nabla=-\partial_{x_{1}x_{2}}^{2}+\partial_{x_{2}x_{1}}^{2}=0
, we see that the third matrix in the definition (
10
) of
\mathcal{H}_{d,\theta}
also defines an Hermitian operator.

The asymptotic equality (
9
) leads us to introduce the following effective model for the propagation of low-energy wave-packets in TBG:

{\rm i}\partial_{\tau}\left(\mathcal{S}_{d}\alpha\right)(\tau,{\mathbf{X}})=\left(\mathcal{H}_{d,\theta}\alpha\right)(\tau,{\mathbf{X}}).

(12)

At this stage, we have kept all the terms in
\mathcal{S}_{d}
and
\mathcal{H}_{d,\theta}
, and only thrown away the remainders of order
\varepsilon_{\theta}^{\infty}
. Indeed,
\varepsilon_{\theta}
should not be considered as the only small parameter in this problem. The interlayer coupling is also small, hence the operators
{\mathbb{W}}_{d}^{\pm}
can be considered to be small as well. The interplay between the two parameters
\varepsilon_{\theta}
and
w
(the interlayer characteristic interaction energy) is subtle and will be explored in a future work.

Remark 1
.

A similar approach can be used to derive an effective model for wave-packet propagation in monolayer graphene in an energy range close to the Fermi level and localized in the
K
-valley in momentum space. One obtains the effective Hamiltonian

\mathcal{H}^{(1)}_{\varepsilon}=v_{F}{\bm{\sigma}}\cdot(-{\rm i}\nabla)+\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon(-{\rm i}\nabla)^{2}

acting on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathbb{C}^{2})
. Neglecting the second term, we recover the usual massless Dirac operator. This effective model was rigorously derived in
Fefferman and Weinstein 2013
using other methods.

II.3
Translational covariance

For any
\mathbb{L}
-periodic functions
f
and
g
, the maps
\left(\!\left(f,g\right)\!\right)_{d}^{\pm\mp}({\mathbf{X}})
defined in (
11
) are
J{\mathbb{L}}
-periodic.
This suggests to introduce the rescaled moiré lattice

{\mathbb{L}}_{\rm M}:=J{\mathbb{L}}

which satisfies
\mathbb{L}_{\rm M}={\mathbf{a}}_{1,\rm M}\mathbb{Z}+{\mathbf{a}}_{2,\rm M}\mathbb{Z}
with lattice vectors
{\mathbf{a}}_{1,\rm M}=J{\mathbf{a}}_{1}
and
{\mathbf{a}}_{2,\rm M}=J{\mathbf{a}}_{2}
. Explicitly,

{\mathbf{a}}_{1,\rm M}=a_{0}\begin{pmatrix}-\sqrt{3}/2\\
-1/2\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad{\mathbf{a}}_{2,\rm M}=a_{0}\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{3}/2\\
-1/2\end{pmatrix}.

The corrresponding Wigner–Seitz cell is
\Omega_{\rm M}:=\mathbb{R}^{2}/\mathbb{L}_{\rm M}
, and its dual basis is given by
{\mathbf{a}}_{1,\rm M}^{*}=J{\mathbf{a}}_{1}^{*}
and
{\mathbf{a}}_{2,\rm M}^{*}=J{\mathbf{a}}_{2}^{*}
, that is

{\mathbf{a}}_{1,\rm M}^{*}=\sqrt{3}k_{\rm D}\begin{pmatrix}-1/2\\
-\sqrt{3}/2\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad{\mathbf{a}}_{2,\rm M}^{*}=\sqrt{3}k_{\rm D}\begin{pmatrix}1/2\\
-\sqrt{3}/2\end{pmatrix}.

We also introduce the vectors (see Fig.
3
)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{q}}_{1}:=k_{\textup{D}}\begin{pmatrix}0\\
-1\end{pmatrix}=\frac{1}{3}\left({\mathbf{a}}_{1,\rm M}^{*}+{\mathbf{a}}_{2,\rm M}^{*}\right),

\displaystyle{\mathbf{q}}_{2}:=k_{\textup{D}}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\\
\frac{1}{2}\end{pmatrix}=\frac{1}{3}\left(-2{\mathbf{a}}_{1,\rm M}^{*}+{\mathbf{a}}_{2,\rm M}^{*}\right)=R_{\frac{2\pi}{3}}{\mathbf{q}}_{1},

\displaystyle{\mathbf{q}}_{3}:=k_{\textup{D}}\begin{pmatrix}-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\\
\frac{1}{2}\end{pmatrix}=\frac{1}{3}\left({\mathbf{a}}_{1,\rm M}^{*}-2{\mathbf{a}}_{2,\rm M}^{*}\right)=R_{\frac{2\pi}{3}}{\mathbf{q}}_{2}.

These vectors satisfy
{\mathbf{q}}_{1}+{\mathbf{q}}_{2}+{\mathbf{q}}_{3}={\mathbb{0}}
and correspond to the
{\mathbf{K}}
–valley of the moiré Brillouin zone. Actually, we have
{\mathbf{q}}_{1}=J{\mathbf{K}}
.

Going back to our reduced model, we see that the diagonal elements
{\mathbb{W}}_{d}^{\pm}
are
\mathbb{L}_{M}
-periodic, while, for
{\mathbf{R}}_{M}\in\mathbb{L}_{M}
,

{\mathbb{V}}_{d}({\mathbf{X}}-{\mathbf{R}}_{M})={\rm e}^{{\rm i}{\mathbf{q}}_{1}\cdot{\mathbf{R}}_{M}}{\mathbb{V}}_{d}({\mathbf{X}}),

(13)

and similarly for
\Sigma_{d}
. Writing
{\mathbf{R}}_{M}=m_{1}{\mathbf{a}}_{1,M}+m_{2}{\mathbf{a}}_{2,M}
, we have
{\rm e}^{{\rm i}{\mathbf{q}}_{1}\cdot{\mathbf{R}}_{M}}=\omega^{(m_{1}+m_{2})}
, where we set
\omega:={\rm e}^{{\rm i}\frac{2\pi}{3}}
. Since
\omega^{3}=1
, our model is
3{\mathbb{L}}_{\rm M}
–periodic.

Thus, although the true moiré pattern generated by the superposition of two twisted honeycomb lattices is not periodic for a generic twist angle
\theta
, our reduced model is. In some sense, the moiré pattern looks
\varepsilon_{\theta}^{-1}{\mathbb{L}}_{\rm M}
-periodic at the mesoscopic scale
\varepsilon_{\theta}^{-1}
for
\theta\ll 1
(see Fig.
2
).

II.4
Comparison with the Bistritzer-MacDonald model

The BM Hamiltonian (
1
) can be written more explicitly
Bistritzer and MacDonald 2011b
;
Tarnopolsky et al. 2019
;
Watson et al. 2022
as

H^{\rm BM}_{\theta}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}v_{\rm F}\bm{\sigma}_{-\theta/2}\cdot(-{\rm i}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}})&\bm{V}(\varepsilon_{\theta}{\mathbf{x}})\\
\bm{V}(\varepsilon_{\theta}{\mathbf{x}})^{*}&v_{\rm F}\bm{\sigma}_{\theta/2}\cdot(-{\rm i}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}})\end{array}\right),

with

\bm{V}({\mathbf{X}}):=\begin{pmatrix}w_{\rm AA}\,G({\mathbf{X}})&w_{\rm AB}\,\overline{F(-{\mathbf{X}})}\\
w_{\rm AB}\,F({\mathbf{X}})&w_{\rm AA} \,G({\mathbf{X}})\end{pmatrix},

(14)

where
w_{\rm AA}
and
w_{\rm AB}

\displaystyle F({\mathbf{X}})

\displaystyle G({\mathbf{X}})

The BM potential satisfies, for all

Rescaling lengths and energies as

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{\rm BM}_{\theta}=\begin{pmatrix}v_{\rm F}\bm{\sigma}_{-\theta/2}\cdot(-{\rm i}\nabla_{\mathbf{X}})&\varepsilon_{\theta}^{-1}\bm{V}({\mathbf{X}})\\
\varepsilon_{\theta}^{-1}\bm{V}({\mathbf{X}})^{*}&v_{\rm F}\bm{\sigma}_{\theta/2}\cdot(-{\rm i}\nabla_{\mathbf{X}})\end{pmatrix}.

Going back to our model in (

1.

the matrix

2.

As for the last point, first-principle values of the BM parameters can be inferred from our reduced model by setting

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{3|\Omega_{\rm M}|}\int_{\Omega_{\rm M}}[{\mathbb{V}}_{d}]_{11}({\mathbf{X}})\overline{G({\mathbf{X}})}\,{\rm d}{\mathbf{X}},

\displaystyle w_{\rm AB}^{d}

IV
Conclusion and perspectives

We have proposed a simple direct space construction of an effective model for TBG at the moiré scale from DFT based on

•

an approximation of the TBG Kohn-Sham Hamiltonian following the method introduced in
Tritsaris et al. 2016
;

•

a variational approximation of the so-obtained Hamiltonian at the Dirac point
{\mathbf{K}}
around the Fermi level;

•

an asymptotic expansion valid for small twist angles
\theta
.

This effective model has a structure similar to the one of the Bistritzer-MacDonald model, but contains additional terms: a non-trivial overlap operator
\mathcal{S}_{d}
, intralayer effective potentials
{\mathbb{W}}_{d}^{\pm}
, more complicated interlayer effective potentials
{\mathbb{V}}_{d}
, and higher-order corrections. We show numerically that both models give rise to similar band diagrams, the main difference being that at the first magic angle, the almost flat bands are separated from the rest of the spectrum in our model, which is not the case in the BM model.

It is well established that atomic relaxation plays a key role in the electronic properties of TBG (see e.g.
Nam and Koshino 2017
and references therein), especially at very small twist angles
\theta<1^{\circ}
. We derived our reduced model (
12
) for an unrelaxed configuration, but it can be extended to take both in-plane and out-of-plane relaxation
Carr et al. 2018b
;
Carr et al. 2019
;
Cazeaux et al. 2020
into account. The derivation and numerical simulation of such a model is work in progress.

The same methodology can be applied to study the propagation of low-energy wave-packets localized in momentum space in both the
{\mathbf{K}}
and
{\mathbf{K}}^{\prime}
valleys. The approximation space then contains functions of the form

\left(\alpha:\Phi\right)_{d,\theta}({\mathbf{x}},z):=\sum_{p\in\{{\mathbf{K}},{\mathbf{K}}^{\prime}\}}\sum_{\eta\in\{\pm 1\}\atop j\in\{1,2\}}\alpha_{\eta,j,p}\left(\varepsilon_{\theta}{\mathbf{x}}\right)(U_{d,\theta}^{\eta}\Phi_{j}^{p})({\mathbf{x}},z).

For the unrelaxed configuration, it can be checked that the two valleys are uncoupled. Intervalley coupling may appear however when taking atomic relaxations into account.

Our goal is to identify the leading orders terms in (

{\mathbf{K}}

(S4)

Appendix B
Symmetries of our reduced model for TBG

B.0.1
Symmetries induced by the atomic configuration

We now record the symmetries of the operator induced by the specific atomic configuration of the TBG. These symmetries are valid for all
d>0
and
\theta>0
. First, we note that

\left[\mathcal{R}_{\tfrac{2\pi}{3}},U_{d,\theta}\right]=\left[\mathcal{P},U_{d,\theta}\right]=\left[\mathcal{C},U_{d,\theta}\right]=0

while
{\mathfrak{R}}U_{d,\theta}=U_{d,\theta}^{*}{\mathfrak{R}}
. We deduce that

\left[H_{d,\theta}^{(2)},\mathcal{R}_{\tfrac{2\pi}{3}}\right]=\left[H_{d,\theta}^{(2)},\mathcal{P}\right]=\left[H_{d,\theta}^{(2)},\mathcal{C}\right]=\left[H_{d,\theta}^{(2)},{\mathfrak{R}}\right]=0.

We now study how these commutation relations translate in our reduced model. Let us first focus on the
3
-fold rotation symmetry. Using (
S1
), we obtain that, for
\alpha:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}^{4}
, we have

\mathcal{R}_{\tfrac{2\pi}{3}}\left(\alpha:\Phi\right)_{d,\theta}=\left((\mathcal{R}_{\tfrac{2\pi}{3}}\alpha):(\mathcal{R}_{\tfrac{2\pi}{3}}\Phi)\right)_{d,\theta}=\left((\widetilde{\mathcal{R}_{\tfrac{2\pi}{3}}}\alpha):\Phi\right)_{d,\theta},

where we introduced the unitary operator
\widetilde{\mathcal{R}_{\tfrac{2\pi}{3}}}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathbb{C}^{4})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathbb{C}^{4})
defined by

\widetilde{\mathcal{R}_{\tfrac{2\pi}{3}}}\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{+,1}\\
\alpha_{+,2}\\
\alpha_{-,1}\\
\alpha_{-,2}\end{pmatrix}=\mathcal{R}_{\tfrac{2\pi}{3}}\begin{pmatrix}\omega\alpha_{+,1}\\
\omega^{2}\alpha_{+,2}\\
\omega\alpha_{-,1}\\
\omega^{2}\alpha_{-,2}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\omega&0&0&0\\
0&\omega^{2}&0&0\\
0&0&\omega&0\\
0&0&0&\omega^{2}\end{pmatrix}\mathcal{R}_{\tfrac{2\pi}{3}}\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{+,1}\\
\alpha_{+,2}\\
\alpha_{-,1}\\
\alpha_{-,2}\end{pmatrix}.

We deduce that

\left\langle\left(\widetilde{\alpha}:\Phi\right)_{d,\theta},\left(H_{d,\theta}^{(2)}-\mu_{F}\right)\left(\alpha:\Phi\right)_{d,\theta}\right\rangle=\left\langle\left((\widetilde{\mathcal{R}_{\tfrac{2\pi}{3}}}\widetilde{\alpha}):\Phi\right)_{d,\theta},\left(H_{d,\theta}^{(2)}-\mu_{F}\right)\left((\widetilde{\mathcal{R}_{\tfrac{2\pi}{3}}}\alpha):\Phi\right)_{d,\theta}\right\rangle,

and a similar formula for
\left\langle\left(\widetilde{\alpha}:\Phi\right)_{d,\theta},\left(\alpha:\Phi\right)_{d,\theta}\right\rangle
. Hence

\left[\mathcal{S}_{d},\widetilde{\mathcal{R}_{\tfrac{2\pi}{3}}}\right]=0,\quad\text{and}\quad\left[\mathcal{H}_{d,\theta},\widetilde{\mathcal{R}_{\tfrac{2\pi}{3}}}\right]=0.

These commutation relations give rises to symmetry properties on the entries of
\mathcal{S}_{d}
and
\mathcal{H}_{d,\theta}
. For
{\mathbb{V}}_{d}
, we have (the results are similar for
\Sigma_{d}
and
{\mathbb{W}}_{d}
)

\displaystyle[{\mathbb{V}}_{d}]_{11}(R_{\frac{2\pi}{3}}^{*}{\mathbf{X}})=[{\mathbb{V}}_{d}]_{11}({\mathbf{X}}),

\displaystyle[{\mathbb{V}}_{d}]_{12}(R_{\frac{2\pi}{3}}^{*}{\mathbf{X}})=\omega[{\mathbb{V}}_{d}]_{12}({\mathbf{X}}),

(S8)

\displaystyle[{\mathbb{V}}_{d}]_{21}(R_{\frac{2\pi}{3}}^{*}{\mathbf{X}})=\overline{\omega}[{\mathbb{V}}_{d}]_{21}({\mathbf{X}}),

\displaystyle[{\mathbb{V}}_{d}]_{22}(R_{\frac{2\pi}{3}}^{*}{\mathbf{X}})=[{\mathbb{V}}_{d}]_{22}({\mathbf{X}}).

(S9)

We now consider the
\mathcal{C}\mathcal{P}
symmetry. Using the definition of
\Phi_{2}
in (
S1
) we deduce that for
\alpha\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathbb{C}^{4})
,

(\mathcal{C}\mathcal{P})\left(\alpha:\Phi\right)_{d,\theta}=\left((\mathcal{C}\mathcal{P}\alpha):(\mathcal{C}\mathcal{P}\Phi)\right)_{d,\theta}=\left((\widetilde{\mathcal{C}\mathcal{P}}\alpha):\Phi\right)_{d,\theta},

where
\widetilde{\mathcal{C}\mathcal{P}}
is the anti-unitary of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathbb{C}^{4})
defined by

\widetilde{\mathcal{C}\mathcal{P}}\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{+,1}\\
\alpha_{+,2}\\
\alpha_{-,1}\\
\alpha_{-,2}\end{pmatrix}=(\mathcal{C}\mathcal{P})\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{+,2}\\
\alpha_{+,1}\\
\alpha_{-,2}\\
\alpha_{-,1}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0&(\mathcal{C}\mathcal{P})&0&0\\
(\mathcal{C}\mathcal{P})&0&0&0\\
0&0&0&(\mathcal{C}\mathcal{P})\\
0&0&(\mathcal{C}\mathcal{P})&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{+,1}\\
\alpha_{+,2}\\
\alpha_{-,1}\\
\alpha_{-,2}\end{pmatrix}.

We deduce as previously that
\left[\mathcal{S}_{d},\widetilde{\mathcal{C}\mathcal{P}}\right]=0
and
\left[\mathcal{H}_{d,\theta},\widetilde{\mathcal{C}\mathcal{P}}\right]=0
, from which we infer, for
{\mathbb{V}}_{d}
(the results are similar for
\Sigma_{d}
and
{\mathbb{W}}_{d}^{\pm}
)

\displaystyle\overline{[{\mathbb{V}}_{d}]_{11}(-{\mathbf{X}})}=[{\mathbb{V}}_{d}]_{22}({\mathbf{X}})\quad\mbox{and}\quad\overline{[{\mathbb{V}}_{d}]_{12}(-{\mathbf{X}})}=[{\mathbb{V}}_{d}]_{21}({\mathbf{X}}).

(S10)

Finally, we consider the mirror symmetry, coming from the
{\mathfrak{R}}
operator. Using the relation
{\mathfrak{R}}\Phi_{1}=-\Phi_{2}
, we get

({\mathfrak{R}})\left(\alpha:\Phi\right)_{d,\theta}=\left(({\mathfrak{R}}\alpha):({\mathfrak{R}}\Phi)\right)_{d,\theta}=\left((\widetilde{{\mathfrak{R}}}\alpha):\Phi\right)_{d,\theta},

with, setting
(\mathcal{M}f)({\mathbf{x}})=f(M{\mathbf{x}})
with
M=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}
,

\widetilde{{\mathfrak{R}}}\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{+,1}\\
\alpha_{+,2}\\
\alpha_{-,1}\\
\alpha_{-,2}\end{pmatrix}=-\mathcal{M}\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{-,2}\\
\alpha_{-,1}\\
\alpha_{+,2}\\
\alpha_{+,1}\end{pmatrix}=-\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0&\mathcal{M}\\
0&0&\mathcal{M}&0\\
0&\mathcal{M}&0&0\\
\mathcal{M}&0&0&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{+,1}\\
\alpha_{+,2}\\
\alpha_{-,1}\\
\alpha_{-,2}\end{pmatrix}.

Note that this time, since
{\mathfrak{R}}U_{d,\theta}=U_{d,\theta}^{*}{\mathfrak{R}}
, the mirror symmetry exchanges the top and bottom layers. From the relations
\left[\mathcal{S}_{d},\widetilde{{\mathfrak{R}}}\right]=0
and
\left[\mathcal{H}_{d,\theta},\widetilde{{\mathfrak{R}}}\right]=0
, we infer

\displaystyle[{\mathbb{V}}_{d}]_{11}(M{\mathbf{X}})=\overline{[{\mathbb{V}}_{d}]_{22}({\mathbf{X}})},\quad[{\mathbb{V}}_{d}]_{12}(M{\mathbf{X}})=\overline{[{\mathbb{V}}_{d}]_{12}({\mathbf{X}})},

\displaystyle[{\mathbb{V}}_{d}]_{21}(M{\mathbf{X}})=\overline{[{\mathbb{V}}_{d}]_{21}({\mathbf{X}})},\quad[{\mathbb{V}}_{d}]_{22}(M{\mathbf{X}})=\overline{[{\mathbb{V}}_{d}]_{22}({\mathbf{X}})},

and similar relations for
\Sigma_{d}
, as well as

\displaystyle[{\mathbb{W}}^{+}]_{11}(M{\mathbf{X}})=[{\mathbb{W}}^{-}]_{22}({\mathbf{X}}),\quad[{\mathbb{W}}^{+}]_{12}(M{\mathbf{X}})=[{\mathbb{W}}^{-}]_{21}({\mathbf{X}}),

\displaystyle[{\mathbb{W}}^{+}]_{21}(M{\mathbf{X}})=[{\mathbb{W}}^{-}]_{12}({\mathbf{X}}),\quad[{\mathbb{W}}^{+}]_{22}(M{\mathbf{X}})=[{\mathbb{W}}^{-}]_{11}({\mathbf{X}}).

B.0.2
Extra symmetries at the limit

In addition to the previously identified symmetries, extra symmetries appear due to the averaging process over all possible stacking. Indeed, we claim that

\mathcal{C}\left(\!\left(f,g\right)\!\right)_{d}^{\eta\eta^{\prime}}=\left(\!\left(g,f\right)\!\right)_{d}^{\eta^{\prime}\eta},\qquad\mathcal{C}\mathcal{P}\left(\!\left(f,g\right)\!\right)_{d}^{+-}=\left(\!\left(\mathcal{S}g,\mathcal{S}f\right)\!\right)_{d}^{+-},\qquad\left(\!\left(f,g\right)\!\right)_{d}^{+-}=\left(\!\left(\mathcal{C}\mathcal{P}g,\mathcal{C}\mathcal{P}f\right)\!\right)_{d}^{+-}.

(S11)

The first equality comes directly from the definition (
11
). To prove the second equality, we write

\displaystyle\overline{\left(\!\left(f,g\right)\!\right)_{d}^{+-}(-{\mathbf{X}})}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega\times\mathbb{R}}f({\mathbf{x}}+\tfrac{1}{2}J{\mathbf{X}},z-\tfrac{d}{2})\overline{g({\mathbf{x}}-\tfrac{1}{2}J{\mathbf{X}},z+\tfrac{d}{2})}\,{\rm d}{\mathbf{x}}\,{\rm d}z

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega\times\mathbb{R}}\overline{g({\mathbf{x}}-\tfrac{1}{2}J{\mathbf{X}},-z+\tfrac{d}{2})}f({\mathbf{x}}+\tfrac{1}{2}J{\mathbf{X}},-z-\tfrac{d}{2})\,{\rm d}{\mathbf{x}}\,{\rm d}z

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega\times\mathbb{R}}\overline{\mathcal{S}g({\mathbf{x}}-\tfrac{1}{2}J{\mathbf{X}},z-\tfrac{d}{2})}\mathcal{S}f({\mathbf{x}}+\tfrac{1}{2}J{\mathbf{X}},z+\tfrac{d}{2})\,{\rm d}{\mathbf{x}}\,{\rm d}z=\left(\!\left(\mathcal{S}g,\mathcal{S}f\right)\!\right)_{d}^{+-}({\mathbf{X}}).

The third equality follows from similar arguments.

From the first equality of (
S11
), we deduce that

\left[{\mathbb{W}}_{d}^{+}\right]_{11}({\mathbf{X}})=\left[{\mathbb{W}}_{d}^{-}\right]_{22}({\mathbf{X}}),\quad\text{and}\quad\left[{\mathbb{W}}_{d}^{+}\right]_{22}({\mathbf{X}})=\left[{\mathbb{W}}_{d}^{-}\right]_{11}({\mathbf{X}}),

and all these quantities are real-valued. Next, from the second equality of (
S11
), we obtain

\Sigma_{d}(-{\mathbf{X}})^{*}=\Sigma_{d}({\mathbf{X}}),\quad{\mathbb{V}}_{d}(-{\mathbf{X}})^{*}={\mathbb{V}}_{d}({\mathbf{X}}),\quad\text{and}\quad{\mathbb{W}}_{d}^{+}(-{\mathbf{X}})^{*}={\mathbb{W}}_{d}^{-}({\mathbf{X}}).

Finally, from the third equality, we get

\left[\Sigma_{d}\right]_{11}({\mathbf{X}})=\left[\Sigma_{d}\right]_{22}({\mathbf{X}}),\quad\left[{\mathbb{V}}_{d}\right]_{11}({\mathbf{X}})=\left[{\mathbb{V}}_{d}\right]_{22}({\mathbf{X}}),\quad\left[{\mathbb{W}}_{d}^{+}\right]_{12}({\mathbf{X}})=\left[{\mathbb{W}}_{d}^{-}\right]_{21}({\mathbf{X}}).

Appendix C
TBG Hamiltonians with nonlocal pseudopotentials

In the presence of norm-conserving pseudopotentials, the TBG approximate Kohn-Sham Hamiltonian contains an additional term originating from the nonlocal contribution to the pseudopotential, that is

H_{\theta,d}^{(2)}:=-\frac{1}{2}\Delta+V_{d,\theta}^{(2)}+U_{d,\theta}V_{\rm nl}U_{d,\theta}^{-1}+U_{d,\theta}^{-1}V_{\rm nl}U_{d,\theta}.

where
V_{\textup{nl}}
is the
{\mathbb{L}}
-periodic nonlocal component of the pseudopotential of single-layer graphene.
For the Goedeker-Teter-Hutter pseudopotential implemented in the DFTK software used in our numerical simulations,
V_{\rm nl}
is of the form

\displaystyle V_{\rm nl}=v_{0}\sum_{{\mathbf{R}}\in{\mathfrak{C}}}|\tau_{{\mathbf{R}}}\varphi\rangle\langle\tau_{{\mathbf{R}}}\varphi|,\qquad\tau_{{\mathbf{R}}}\varphi:=\varphi(\cdot-{\mathbf{R}}),

where
v_{0}>0
, and where
{\mathfrak{C}}:=\left({\mathbb{L}}+{\mathbf{b}}_{1}\right)\cup\left({\mathbb{L}}+{\mathbf{b}}_{2}\right)
denotes the locations of the Carbon atoms in graphene. Here,
{\mathbf{b}}_{1}:=\tfrac{2}{3}{\mathbf{a}}_{1}+\tfrac{1}{3}{\mathbf{a}}_{2}
,
{\mathbf{b}}_{2}=\tfrac{1}{3}{\mathbf{a}}_{1}+\tfrac{2}{3}{\mathbf{a}}_{2}
are the positions of the atom in the Wigner-Seitz cell, and
\varphi
is a radial normalized Gaussian function with variance similar to the characteristic radius of the carbon 1s orbital.

The functions
\Phi_{j}
satisfy

\left(-\frac{1}{2}\Delta+V+V_{\rm nl}-\mu_{\rm F}\right)\Phi_{j}=0.

Since
\varphi
is even while
\Phi_{j}
is odd with respect to the horizontal symmetry plane, we have
\langle\varphi,\Phi_{j}\rangle=0
, hence
V_{\rm nl}\Phi_{j}=0
, and
\Phi_{j}
also satisfies

\left(-\frac{1}{2}\Delta+V-\mu_{\rm F}\right)\Phi_{j}=0.

We need to compute
H^{(2)}_{\theta,d}(\alpha:\Phi)_{d,\theta}
. For the Laplacian part, we can still use (
23
), and the local part of the potential can still act directly on the Bloch modes. We now focus on the non-local part of the potential. We have

\displaystyle\bigg\langle(\widetilde{\alpha}:\Phi)_{d,\theta}\bigg|\left(U_{d,\theta}V_{\rm nl}U_{d,\theta}^{-1}+U_{d,\theta}^{-1}V_{\rm nl}U_{d,\theta}\right)\bigg|(\alpha:\Phi)_{d,\theta}\bigg\rangle

(S12)

\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{\eta,j}\sum_{\eta^{\prime},j^{\prime}}\sum_{\eta^{\prime\prime}}\langle\widetilde{\alpha}_{\eta,j}(\varepsilon_{\theta}\cdot)U_{d,\theta}^{\eta}\Phi_{j}|U_{d,\theta}^{\eta^{\prime\prime}}V_{\rm nl}U_{d,\theta}^{-\eta^{\prime\prime}}|\alpha_{\eta^{\prime},j^{\prime}}(\varepsilon_{\theta}\cdot)U_{d,\theta}^{\eta^{\prime}}\Phi_{j^{\prime}}\rangle

\displaystyle\quad=v_{0}\sum_{\eta,j}\sum_{\eta^{\prime},j^{\prime}}\sum_{\eta^{\prime\prime}}\sum_{{\mathbf{R}}}\langle U_{d,\theta}^{-\eta^{\prime\prime}}(\widetilde{\alpha}_{\eta,j}(\varepsilon_{\theta}\cdot)U_{d,\theta}^{\eta}\Phi_{j})|\tau_{{\mathbf{R}}}\varphi\rangle\langle\tau_{{\mathbf{R}}}\varphi|U_{d,\theta}^{-\eta^{\prime\prime}}(\alpha_{\eta^{\prime},j^{\prime}}(\varepsilon_{\theta}\cdot)U_{d,\theta}^{\eta^{\prime}}\Phi_{j^{\prime}})\rangle.

Again, since
\varphi
is even and
\Phi_{j}
is odd with respect to the horizontal symmetry plane, we have that for all
{\mathbf{R}},\eta,j
,

\langle\tau_{{\mathbf{R}}}\varphi|U_{d,\theta}^{-\eta}(\beta(\varepsilon_{\theta}\cdot)U_{d,\theta}^{\eta}\Phi_{j})\rangle=0.

Thus only the terms
\eta^{\prime\prime}=-\eta^{\prime}=-\eta
contribute to the sum, which reduces to

\displaystyle v_{0}\sum_{\eta}\sum_{j,j^{\prime}}\sum_{{\mathbf{R}}}\langle U_{d,\theta}^{\eta}(\widetilde{\alpha}_{\eta,j}(\varepsilon_{\theta}\cdot)U_{d,\theta}^{\eta}\Phi_{j})|\tau_{{\mathbf{R}}}\varphi\rangle\langle\tau_{{\mathbf{R}}}\varphi|U_{d,\theta}^{\eta}(\alpha_{\eta,j^{\prime}}(\varepsilon_{\theta}\cdot)U_{d,\theta}^{\eta}\Phi_{j^{\prime}})\rangle.

We now compute the above inner products. We have

\displaystyle\langle\tau_{{\mathbf{R}}}\varphi|U_{d,\theta}^{\eta}(\beta(\varepsilon_{\theta}\cdot)U_{d,\theta}^{\eta}\Phi_{j})\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\beta\left(\varepsilon_{\theta}R_{-\eta\frac{\theta}{2}}^{*}{\mathbf{x}}\right)\,\overline{\varphi({\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{R}},z)}\;\Phi_{j}\left(R_{-\eta\theta}^{*}{\mathbf{x}},z-\eta d\right)\,{\rm d}{\mathbf{x}}\,{\rm d}z

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\beta\left(\varepsilon_{\theta}R_{-\eta\frac{\theta}{2}}^{*}({\mathbf{y}}+{\mathbf{R}})\right)\;\overline{\varphi({\mathbf{y}},z)}\;\Phi_{j}\left(R_{-\eta\theta}^{*}({\mathbf{y}}+{\mathbf{R}}),z-\eta d\right)\,{\rm d}{\mathbf{y}}\,{\rm d}z

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\beta\left(\varepsilon_{\theta}R_{-\eta\frac{\theta}{2}}^{*}{\mathbf{R}}+\varepsilon_{\theta}R_{\eta\frac{\theta}{2}}{\mathbf{y}}\right)\;\overline{\varphi({\mathbf{y}},z)}\;\Phi_{j}\left(R_{-\eta\theta}^{*}{\mathbf{R}}+{\mathbf{y}},z-\eta d\right)\,{\rm d}{\mathbf{y}}{\rm d}z,

where we used that
\varphi
is radially symmetric in the last line. Since
\varphi
is localized near
{\mathbf{y}}=\mathbb{0}
, we perform a Taylor expansion of
\beta
in
{\mathbf{y}}
. This one takes the form

\beta\left(\varepsilon_{\theta}R_{-\eta\frac{\theta}{2}}^{*}{\mathbf{R}}+\varepsilon_{\theta}R_{-\eta\frac{\theta}{2}}{\mathbf{y}}\right)=\beta\left(\varepsilon_{\theta}R_{-\eta\frac{\theta}{2}}^{*}{\mathbf{R}}\right)+\varepsilon_{\theta}\nabla\beta\left(\varepsilon_{\theta}R_{-\eta\frac{\theta}{2}}^{*}{\mathbf{R}}\right)\cdot R_{-\eta\frac{\theta}{2}}{\mathbf{y}}+\ldots

We obtain a series in
\varepsilon_{\theta}

\langle\tau_{{\mathbf{R}}}\varphi|U_{d,\theta}^{\eta}(\beta(\varepsilon_{\theta}\cdot)U_{d,\theta}^{\eta}\Phi_{j})\rangle=\beta\left(\varepsilon_{\theta}R_{-\eta\frac{\theta}{2}}^{*}{\mathbf{R}}\right)F_{0,j}(R_{\eta\theta}^{*}{\mathbf{R}})+\varepsilon_{\theta}R_{-\eta\frac{\theta}{2}}^{*}\nabla\beta\left(\varepsilon_{\theta}R_{-\eta\frac{\theta}{2}}^{*}{\mathbf{R}}\right)\cdot G_{1,j}(R_{-\eta\theta}^{*}{\mathbf{R}})+\ldots

where

\displaystyle F_{0,j}({\mathbf{X}})

\displaystyle:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\overline{\varphi({\mathbf{y}},z)}\;\Phi_{j}\left({\mathbf{X}}+{\mathbf{y}},z-\eta d\right)\,{\rm d}{\mathbf{y}}\,{\rm d}z,

\displaystyle G_{1,j}({\mathbf{X}})

\displaystyle:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}{\mathbf{y}}\overline{\varphi({\mathbf{y}},z)}\;\Phi_{j}\left({\mathbf{X}}+{\mathbf{y}},z-\eta d\right)\,{\rm d}{\mathbf{y}}\,{\rm d}z.

We can go on with the Taylor expansion, but we stop here, as the leading order is already enough for numerical purpose (we show below that even the leading order term can be neglected). The leading order term of (
S12
) is therefore

\displaystyle v_{0}\sum_{\eta,j,j^{\prime}}\sum_{{\mathbf{R}}\in{\mathfrak{C}}}(\overline{\widetilde{\alpha_{\eta,j}}}\alpha_{\eta,j^{\prime}})\left(\varepsilon_{\theta}R_{-\eta\frac{\theta}{2}}^{*}{\mathbf{R}}\right)\left[\overline{F_{0,j}}F_{0,j^{\prime}}\right](R_{\eta\theta}^{*}{\mathbf{R}}).

Performing a Taylor expansion in
\varepsilon_{\theta}
of the

R_{\eta\theta}^{*}=c_{\theta}-2\varepsilon_{\theta}\eta J=1-2\varepsilon_{\theta}\eta J+O(\varepsilon_{\theta}^{2})
, we obtain, for

and
{\mathbf{R}}\in\mathbb{L}
,

\displaystyle F_{0,j}(R_{-\eta\theta}^{*}({\mathbf{R}}+{\mathbf{b}}_{s}))

\displaystyle\approx F_{0,j}\left(({\mathbf{R}}+{\mathbf{b}}_{s})-2\varepsilon_{\theta}\eta J({\mathbf{R}}+{\mathbf{b}}_{s})\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\overline{\varphi({\mathbf{y}},z)}\;\Phi_{j}\left({\mathbf{R}}+{\mathbf{b}}_{s}-2\varepsilon_{\theta}\eta J({\mathbf{R}}+{\mathbf{b}}_{s})+{\mathbf{y}},z-\eta d\right){\rm d}{\mathbf{y}}{\rm d}z

\displaystyle={\rm e}^{{\rm i}{\mathbf{K}}\cdot{\mathbf{R}}}\widetilde{F}_{0,s,j,\eta}(\varepsilon_{\theta}({\mathbf{R}}+{\mathbf{b}}_{s})),

with

\widetilde{F}_{0,s,j,\eta}({\mathbf{X}}):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\overline{\varphi({\mathbf{y}},z)}\;\Phi_{j}\left({\mathbf{b}}_{s}-2\eta J{\mathbf{X}}+{\mathbf{y}},z-\eta d\right){\rm d}{\mathbf{y}}{\rm d}z.

A similar expansion for the
\alpha
-functions gives, to leading order,

v_{0}\sum_{\eta,j,j^{\prime}}\sum_{s\in\{1,2\}}\sum_{{\mathbf{R}}\in\mathbb{L}}(\overline{\widetilde{\alpha_{\eta,j}}}\alpha_{\eta,j^{\prime}})\left(\varepsilon_{\theta}({\mathbf{R}}+{\mathbf{b}}_{s})\right)\left[\overline{\widetilde{F}_{0,s,j}}\widetilde{F}_{0,s,j^{\prime}}\right](\varepsilon_{\theta}({\mathbf{R}}+{\mathbf{b}}_{s})).

We recognize a Riemann sum. Therefore, at leading order, this term also equals (the superscript
-1
refers to the fact that only the leading order term was taken into account)

\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{\theta}^{2}}\sum_{\eta,j,j^{\prime}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(\overline{\widetilde{\alpha_{\eta,j}}}\alpha_{\eta,j^{\prime}})({\mathbf{X}})[{\mathbb{W}}_{d}^{\rm nl,-1}]_{jj^{\prime}}({\mathbf{X}}){\rm d}{\mathbf{X}},\quad\text{with}\quad[{\mathbb{W}}_{d}^{\rm nl,-1}]_{jj^{\prime}}:=v_{0}\sum_{s\in\{1,2\}}\overline{\widetilde{F}_{0,s,j}}\widetilde{F}_{0,s,j^{\prime}}.

This term modifies the effective Hamiltonian as follows:

\mathcal{H}_{d,\theta}^{\rm nl,-1}=\varepsilon_{\theta}^{-1}\left(\mathcal{V}_{d}+\mathcal{V}_{d}^{\rm nl,-1}\right)+c_{\theta}T_{d}+\varepsilon_{\theta}T_{d}^{(1)},

(S13)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{d}^{\rm nl,-1}

\displaystyle:=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}{\mathbb{W}}_{d}^{\rm nl,-1}&0\\
0&{\mathbb{W}}_{d}^{\rm nl,-1}\end{array}\right).

The remainder in the three-term expansion (
9
) valid for local (multiplicative) potentials is of order
\varepsilon_{\theta}^{\infty}
. In contrast, the non-local component of the pseudopotential gives rise to an infinite series of terms of orders
\varepsilon_{\theta}^{k}
for all
k\geqslant-1
. All these terms, including the leading order one are very small (
\sim 6\times 10^{-1}
meV, see Fig.
S2
).

Appendix D
Variation of the reduced quantities as a function of
d

Let us finally study the variations of the fields
\Sigma_{d}({\mathbf{X}})
,
{\mathbb{V}}_{d}({\mathbf{X}})
and
{\mathbb{W}}_{d}^{\pm}({\mathbf{X}})
as functions of
d
. Let us expand these three fields of interest as

\displaystyle\Sigma_{d}({\mathbf{X}})

\displaystyle=[u_{1}|u_{1}]_{d,0,0}\bm{S}^{(0)}({\mathbf{X}})+r_{\Sigma,d}({\mathbf{X}}),

\displaystyle{\mathbb{V}}_{d}({\mathbf{X}})

\displaystyle=w_{\rm AA}\bm{S}^{(0)}({\mathbf{X}})+[V_{d}u_{1}|u_{1}]_{d,-1,-1}\bm{S}^{(1)}({\mathbf{X}})+r_{{\mathbb{V}},d}({\mathbf{X}}),

\displaystyle{\mathbb{W}}_{d}^{+}({\mathbf{X}})

\displaystyle=W_{\text{int},d}^{+}{\mathbb{I}}_{2}+\bm{W}^{+}_{d}({\mathbf{X}})+r_{{{\mathbb{W}}},d}({\mathbf{X}}),

where
[u_{1}|u_{1}]_{d,0,0}
,
w_{\rm AA}=[V_{d}u_{1}|u_{1}]_{d,0,0}
,
[V_{d}u_{1}|u_{1}]_{d,-1,-1}
, and
W_{\text{int},d}^{+}
are real numbers, and where

\displaystyle\bm{S}^{(0)}({\mathbf{X}})

\displaystyle:=\begin{pmatrix}G({\mathbf{X}})&\overline{F(-{\mathbf{X}})}\\
F({\mathbf{X}})&G({\mathbf{X}})\end{pmatrix},\qquad\bm{S}^{(1)}({\mathbf{X}}):=\begin{pmatrix}G^{(1)}({\mathbf{X}})&\overline{F^{(1)}(-{\mathbf{X}})}\\
F^{(1)}({\mathbf{X}})&G^{(1)}({\mathbf{X}})\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle\bm{W}^{+}_{d}({\mathbf{X}})

\displaystyle:=\begin{pmatrix}\left|[\left|u_{1}\right|^{2}|V_{d}]_{d,0,1}\right|D({\mathbf{X}})&\left|[\overline{u_{1}}u_{2}|V_{d}]_{d,0,1}\right|\overline{Z(-{\mathbf{X}})}\\
\left|[\overline{u_{1}}u_{2}|V_{d}]_{d,0,1}\right|Z({\mathbf{X}})&\left|[\left|u_{1}\right|^{2}|V_{d}]_{d,0,1}\right|D({\mathbf{X}})\end{pmatrix},

with

\displaystyle G^{(1)}({\mathbf{X}})

\displaystyle:=e^{-i{\mathbf{q}}_{1}\cdot{\mathbf{X}}}\left(e^{i\left({\mathbf{a}}_{1}^{*}-{\mathbf{a}}_{2}^{*}\right)\cdot{\mathbf{X}}}+e^{i\left({\mathbf{a}}_{2}^{*}-{\mathbf{a}}_{1}^{*}\right)\cdot{\mathbf{X}}}+e^{-i\left({\mathbf{a}}_{1}^{*}+{\mathbf{a}}_{2}^{*}\right)\cdot{\mathbf{X}}}\right)

\displaystyle=e^{i2{\mathbf{q}}_{1}\cdot{\mathbf{X}}}+e^{i2{\mathbf{q}}_{2}\cdot{\mathbf{X}}}+e^{i2{\mathbf{q}}_{3}\cdot{\mathbf{X}}},

\displaystyle F^{(1)}({\mathbf{X}})

\displaystyle:=e^{-i{\mathbf{q}}_{1}\cdot{\mathbf{X}}}\left(\omega e^{i\left({\mathbf{a}}_{1}^{*}-{\mathbf{a}}_{2}^{*}\right)\cdot{\mathbf{X}}}+\omega^{2}e^{i\left({\mathbf{a}}_{2}^{*}-{\mathbf{a}}_{1}^{*}\right)\cdot{\mathbf{X}}}+e^{-i\left({\mathbf{a}}_{1}^{*}+{\mathbf{a}}_{2}^{*}\right)\cdot{\mathbf{X}}}\right)

\displaystyle=e^{i2{\mathbf{q}}_{1}\cdot{\mathbf{X}}}+\omega e^{i2{\mathbf{q}}_{2}\cdot{\mathbf{X}}}+\omega^{2}e^{i2{\mathbf{q}}_{3}\cdot{\mathbf{X}}},

\displaystyle D({\mathbf{X}})

\displaystyle:=\omega\left(e^{-i{\mathbf{a}}_{2}^{*}\cdot{\mathbf{X}}}+e^{i{\mathbf{a}}_{1}^{*}\cdot{\mathbf{X}}}+e^{i\left(-{\mathbf{a}}_{1}^{*}+{\mathbf{a}}_{2}^{*}\right)\cdot{\mathbf{X}}}\right)+\omega^{2}\left(e^{-i{\mathbf{a}}_{1}^{*}\cdot{\mathbf{X}}}+e^{i{\mathbf{a}}_{2}^{*}\cdot{\mathbf{X}}}+e^{i\left({\mathbf{a}}_{1}^{*}-{\mathbf{a}}_{2}^{*}\right)\cdot{\mathbf{X}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\omega\left(e^{i\left({\mathbf{q}}_{1}-{\mathbf{q}}_{3}\right)\cdot{\mathbf{X}}}+e^{i\left({\mathbf{q}}_{2}-{\mathbf{q}}_{1}\right)\cdot{\mathbf{X}}}+e^{i\left({\mathbf{q}}_{3}-{\mathbf{q}}_{2}\right)\cdot{\mathbf{X}}}\right)+\text{c.c.},

\displaystyle Z({\mathbf{X}})

\displaystyle:=\omega\left(e^{i{\mathbf{a}}_{2}^{*}\cdot{\mathbf{X}}}+e^{-i{\mathbf{a}}_{2}^{*}\cdot{\mathbf{X}}}\right)+\omega^{2}\left(e^{i{\mathbf{a}}_{1}^{*}\cdot{\mathbf{X}}}+e^{-i{\mathbf{a}}_{1}^{*}\cdot{\mathbf{X}}}\right)+e^{i\left({\mathbf{a}}_{1}^{*}-{\mathbf{a}}_{2}^{*}\right)\cdot{\mathbf{X}}}+e^{i\left(-{\mathbf{a}}_{1}^{*}+{\mathbf{a}}_{2}^{*}\right)\cdot{\mathbf{X}}}

\displaystyle=2\left(\cos\left(\left({\mathbf{q}}_{3}-{\mathbf{q}}_{2}\right)\cdot{\mathbf{X}}\right)+\omega\cos\left(\left({\mathbf{q}}_{3}-{\mathbf{q}}_{1}\right)\cdot{\mathbf{X}}\right)+\omega^{2}\cos\left(\left({\mathbf{q}}_{2}-{\mathbf{q}}_{1}\right)\cdot{\mathbf{X}}\right)\right).

The above four functions are expanded on the subdominant Fourier modes and fulfill the expected symmetries.

We infer from the data in Figure
S3
that for all values of
d
around the experimental average interlayer distance
d\simeq 6.45

\displaystyle\left|\!\left|r_{\Sigma,d}\right|\!\right|_{L^{2}}
