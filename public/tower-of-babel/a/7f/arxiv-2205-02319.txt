Critical Window of The Symmetric Perceptron
Dylan J. Altschuler

Abstract
. We study the critical window of the symmetric binary perceptron, or equivalently, random combinatorial discrepancy. Consider the problem of finding a ± 1 \pm 1 -valued vector σ \sigma satisfying ‖ A ​ σ ‖ ∞ ≤ K \|A\sigma\|_{\infty}\leq K , where A A is an α ​ n × n \alpha n\times n matrix with iid Gaussian entries. For fixed K K , at which constraint densities α \alpha is this constraint satisfaction problem (CSP) satisfiable? A sharp threshold was recently established by Perkins and Xu [ 29 ] , and Abbe, Li, and Sly [ 2 ] , answering this to first order. Namely, for each K K there exists an explicit critical density α c {\alpha_{c}} so that for any fixed ϵ > 0 \epsilon>0 , with high probability the CSP is satisfiable for α ​ n < ( α c − ϵ ) ​ n \alpha n<({\alpha_{c}}-\epsilon)n and unsatisfiable for α ​ n > ( α c + ϵ ) ​ n \alpha n>({\alpha_{c}}+\epsilon)n . This corresponds to a bound of o ⁡ ( n ) o(n) on the size of the critical window. We sharpen these results significantly, as well as provide exponential tail bounds. Our main result is that, perhaps surprisingly, the critical window is actually at most of order log ⁡ ( n ) \log(n) . More precisely, for a large constant C C , with high probability the CSP is satisfiable for α ​ n < α c ​ n − C ​ log ⁡ ( n ) \alpha n<{\alpha_{c}}n-C\log(n) and unsatisfiable for α ​ n > α c ​ n + C \alpha n>{\alpha_{c}}n+C . These results add the the symmetric perceptron to the short list of CSP models for which a critical window is rigorously known, and to the even shorter list for which this window is known to have nearly constant width.



1.
Introduction

Discrepancy arises as a fundamental quantity in combinatorics, functional analysis, geometry, and optimization. The
combinatorial discrepancy
of a matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{\alpha n\times n}
is given by the following optimization problem:

\mathrm{disc}(A)\coloneqq\min_{\sigma\in\left\{-1,+1\right\}^{n}}\|A\sigma\|_{\infty}\,.

In words, discrepancy is the task of finding some
\sigma\in\left\{\pm 1\right\}^{n}
that has small inner product in absolute value with all rows of
A
. This task is naturally encoded as a CSP (constraint satisfaction problem). Fix a positive parameter
K
and look for assignments
\sigma\in\left\{\pm 1\right\}^{n}
that satisfy each of the
\alpha n
constraints encoded by the rows of
A
:

(1)

\left\{\left|\left\langle A_{1},\sigma\right\rangle\right|\leq K\right\}\wedge\dots\wedge\left\{\left|\left\langle A_{\alpha n},\sigma\right\rangle\right|\leq K\right\}\,.

We say the problem is satisfiable if such an assignment
\sigma
exists. For fixed
K
, if we construct
Eq.

1
by adding one constraint at a time, how many constraints can we add before the equation becomes unsatisfiable?

Much recent work focuses on understanding, for random
A
, the parameters
K
and
\alpha
for which
Eq.

1
is satisfiable with positive probability. For random CSP, it is natural to suspect the existence of a sharp threshold
[
12
,
19
,
22
]
. A
sharp threshold
is said to occur if there exists some
{\alpha_{c}}
so that for any
\epsilon>0
, the probability that
Eq.

1
is satisfiable tends to one for
\alpha n<({\alpha_{c}}-\epsilon)n
and to zero for
\alpha n>({\alpha_{c}}+\epsilon)n
, as
n
grows. We now formally state the model of randomness that will be studied.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
\alpha\coloneqq\alpha(n)
. Say an
\alpha n\times n
matrix
A
is
(\alpha,n)
-Gaussian if it has iid normal entries with mean 0 and variance
1/n
.

The normalization is so that
\mathrm{disc}(A)
is on the constant scale.
We will only consider
\alpha\coloneqq\alpha(n)
tending to a finite, strictly postive limit (see
Section

2
for
\alpha\to 0
). The discrepancy of such matrices has gathered significant recent attention in the statistical physics community due to connections with the celebrated
Binary Perceptron
.

The binary perceptron is an idealized model of learning that has enjoyed almost six decades of intense study by the statistical physics community. While there is an extensive body of detailed physics predictions for the perceptron, these predictions have largely resisted rigorous mathematical treatment
[
16
,
21
,
27
,
33
,
39
,
10
]
. Aubin, Perkins, and Zdeborová
[
6
]
had the insight of interpreting combinatorial discrepancy as a “symmetric” analogue of the binary perceptron. Happily, discrepancy is more amenable to analysis and is conjectured to capture most of the interesting behaviours of the perceptron. This connection has already lead to several breakthroughs in both problems
[
1
,
2
,
20
,
28
,
29
]
.

In brief, the reduction between the SBP and discrepancy is as follows. We are interested in the set of parameters
(K,\alpha)
for which
Eq.

1
is satisfiable. One could equally well fix
\alpha
and ask for the smallest
K
, or else fix
K
and ask for the largest
\alpha
. The former is adopted in discrepancy and the latter for the SBP. Formally,

Definition 1.2

(Symmetric perceptron)
.

For a fixed parameter
K
, an instance of the associated
symmetric binary perceptron
(SBP) problem is given by a sequence of iid Gaussian vectors
\{a_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
with

a_{i}\sim N(0,n^{-1}\mathrm{I}_{n\times n})\,.

The
storage capacity
of the SBP is the random variable
\bm{\alpha_{*}}
defined as the largest
\alpha
so that
\alpha n\in\mathbb{Z}
and there exists
\sigma\in\left\{\pm 1\right\}^{n}
with

\max_{i\in[\alpha n]}\left|\left\langle a_{i},\sigma\right\rangle\right|\leq K\,.

The original binary perceptron model asks for
\sigma
to have positive, rather than small, inner product with rows. A simple but important observation (made formal in
Lemma

3.2
below) is the following.

Let
{K_{c}}
and
{\alpha_{c}}
denote the “typical values” of
\mathrm{disc}(A)
and
\bm{\alpha_{*}}
respectively. A bound on
|\mathrm{disc}(A)-{K_{c}}|
is equivalent to a bound on
|\bm{\alpha_{*}}-{\alpha_{c}}|
of the same order. Thus, fluctuation bounds on the capacity of the SBP or the discrepancy of a Gaussian matrix are equivalent.

As such, we discuss these quantities interchangeably. For matrices with iid Gaussian or Rademacher entries, the existence of a
coarse threshold
was shown by Aubin, Perkins, and Zdeborová
[
6
]
via the second moment method. They also noted the second moment method fails to give a sharp threshold and conjectured a sharp threshold should still occur. The failure of the second moment method to yield a sharp threshold—usually a difficult obstacle to overcome—is a key feature of many interesting CSP, including the binary perceptron. (In fact, the second moment method famously fails to even yield a coarse threshold for the binary perceptron). In an impressive series of works, Perkins and Xu
[
29
]
and Abbe, Li, and Sly
[
2
]
independently established the sharp threshold for the SBP, resolving the conjecture of
[
6
]
.

When a random CSP admits a sharp threshold, a next natural question is to identify the width of the critical window, sometimes also called the scaling window. Namely, fix some small constant
\delta>0
; as a function of
n
, how many constraints must be added for the probability that (
1
) is satisfiable to decrease from
1-\delta
to
\delta
? The following proposition summarizes what is known:

Proposition 1.3

(Sharp threshold
[
29
,
2
]
)
.

For any positive constants
K
,
\epsilon
, and
\delta
, there exists
{\alpha_{c}}\coloneqq{\alpha_{c}}(K)
(given explicitly in
Eq.

8
) so that for
A
drawn from the
(\alpha,n)
-Gaussian ensemble and
n
sufficiently large,

\displaystyle\alpha n<{\alpha_{c}}n-\epsilon n

\displaystyle\implies\mathbb{P}\left[\mathrm{disc}(A)>K\right]<\delta\,,

\displaystyle\alpha n>{\alpha_{c}}n+\epsilon n

\displaystyle\implies\mathbb{P}\left[\mathrm{disc}(A)<K\right]<\delta\,.

Equivalently, the capacity of the symmetric perceptron satisfies

\mathbb{P}\left[{\alpha_{c}}n-\epsilon n<\bm{\alpha_{*}}<{\alpha_{c}}n+\epsilon n\right]>1-2\delta

No further quantification has been established or even conjectured. Our main contribution is to narrow the current bound on the critical window from
o(n)
to
\mathcal{O}\left(\log(n)\right)
. Strikingly, the list of CSP for which the critical window is even known to be polynomially smaller than
n
, let alone constant, is very short. This is discussed further in
Section

2
below. We begin with a simplified, qualitative statement of our main result:

Theorem 1.4

(
Main result:
logarithmic critical window)
.

For any positive constants
K
and
\delta
, there exists an explicit constant
{\alpha_{c}}\coloneqq{\alpha_{c}}(K)
and some sufficiently large
c>0
so that for
A
drawn from the
(\alpha,n)
-Gaussian ensemble and
n
sufficiently large,

\displaystyle\alpha n<{\alpha_{c}}n-c\log(n)

\displaystyle\implies\mathbb{P}\left[\mathrm{disc}(A)>K\right]<\delta\,,

\displaystyle\alpha n>{\alpha_{c}}n+c

\displaystyle\implies\mathbb{P}\left[\mathrm{disc}(A)<K\right]<\delta\,.

Equivalently, the capacity of the symmetric perceptron satisfies

\mathbb{P}\left[{\alpha_{c}}n-c<\bm{\alpha_{*}}<{\alpha_{c}}n+c\log(n)\right]>1-2\delta

We actually prove a significant quantitative strengthening of this. Our main technical contribution gives sub-exponential tails on the fluctuations of discrepancy and directly implies
Theorem

1.4
.

Theorem 1.5

(
Fluctuations
: capacity)
.

Fix
K>0
, and let
\bm{\alpha_{*}}
be the storage capacity of the corresponding SBP. There exist positive constants
c
and
{\alpha_{c}}:={\alpha_{c}}(K)
depending only on
K
so that for any sufficiently large fixed
x
, the following holds for all sufficiently large
n
:

\mathbb{P}\left[\left|\bm{\alpha_{*}}-{\alpha_{c}}\right|>\frac{x\log(n)}{n}\right]\leq\exp\left\{-cx\log\left(n\right)\right\}\,.

In addition, there exist some other positive constants
c
and
C
depending only on
K
so that for all
n
sufficiently large,

\frac{c}{n}\leq\mathrm{Var}(\bm{\alpha_{*}})^{1/2}\leq\frac{C}{n}\log(n)\,.

Theorem

1.5
implies
Theorem

1.4
as an immediate corollary, as well as an analogous bound on discrepancy:

Theorem 1.6

(
Fluctuations
: discrepancy)
.

Fix
\alpha>0
and let
A
be from the
(\alpha,n)
-Gaussian ensemble. There exist positive constants
c
and
{K_{c}}:={K_{c}}(\alpha)
depending only on
\alpha
so that for any sufficiently large fixed
x
, the following holds for all sufficiently large
n
:

(2)

\mathbb{P}\left[\left|\mathrm{disc}(A)-{K_{c}}\right|>\frac{x\log(n)}{n}\right]\leq\exp\left\{-cx\log\left(n\right)\right\}\,.

In addition, there exist some other positive constants
c
and
C
depending only on
\alpha
so that for all
n
sufficiently large,

(3)

\frac{c}{n}\leq\mathrm{Var}(\mathrm{disc}(A))^{1/2}\leq\frac{C}{n}\log(n)\,.

Some remarks:

(1)

Existing general tools fall quite short of being able to capture when the critical window of a CSP has width polynomially smaller than
n
—let alone a window of constant width. See e.g. a result of Xu giving a sharp threshold for the (asymmetric) perceptron
[
41
]
based on Hatami’s theorem
[
22
]
, which establishes a sharp threshold sequence of width at most
n/\left(\log(\log(n))\right)^{1/10}
.

(2)

It is natural to compare discrepancy to the smallest singular value of a Gaussian matrix, since they solve somewhat similar minimization problems.
Theorem

1.5
shows discrepancy is significantly more concentrated, with fluctuations of order
n^{-1}
, whereas the smallest singular value of a Gaussian matrix follows the Tracy-Widom law with fluctuations of order
n^{-2/3}

[
34
]
.

(3)

Our lower-bound on the variance shows that the critical window is at least constant. C.f. random linear equations over a finite field, which can be written as a CSP with a degenerate window
[
26
,
24
]
. By “degenerate”, we mean that the critical window has size exactly one, as well as a deterministic location; this corresponds to random (regular) equations being solvable w.h.p. exactly until the number of equations
\alpha n
exceeds the number of variables
n
.

We conclude by highlighting a key intermediate result. Let us briefly introduce a standard perspective for studying CSP: counting solutions. Fix
\alpha
and define
Z_{K}\coloneqq Z_{K,\alpha}(A)
to be the set of solutions incurring discrepancy at most
K
.

Z_{K}\coloneqq\left\{x\in\left\{\pm 1\right\}^{n}:~\|Ax\|_{\infty}\leq K\right\},\quad\mathrm{disc}(A)=\inf\{K:~Z_{K}\neq\emptyset\}\,.

A natural prediction is that
\mathrm{disc}(A)
should concentrate around
{K_{c}}\coloneqq{K_{c}}(\alpha)
, defined as the smallest
K
for which
\mathbb{E}\left[|Z_{K}|\right]\geq 1
. Showing
|Z_{K}|
concentrates around its mean would verify this prediction. Markov’s inequality readily implies
\mathrm{disc}(A)>({K_{c}}-\epsilon)
with exponentially high probability, for any
\epsilon>0
. The coarse threshold of Aubin, Perkins, and Zdeborová
[
6
]
, which states
\mathrm{disc}(A)<({K_{c}}+\epsilon)
with positive probability for any
\epsilon>0
, corresponds to a variance bound:

(4)

\forall\epsilon>0,\quad 1<c\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[|Z_{K_{c}+\epsilon}|^{2}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[|Z_{K_{c}+\epsilon}|\right]^{2}}\leq C<\infty\,.

While
Eq.

4
implies that the variance of
|Z_{K}|
is small enough to obtain a coarse threshold, it also implies the variance of
|Z_{K}|
is too large to obtain a sharp threshold. Rather, it is
\log|Z_{K}|
that concentrates well. We will soon check that for any small positive constant
\epsilon
,
\log\mathbb{E}\left[|Z_{{K_{c}}+\epsilon}|\right]=\Theta\left(\epsilon n\right)
. The sharp threshold
Proposition

1.3
of
[
29
,
2
]
corresponds to the asymptotic bound that for any positive
\delta
, there exists large
M
with

(5)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left[\left|\log|Z_{{K_{c}}+\epsilon}|-\log\mathbb{E}\left[|Z_{{K_{c}}+\epsilon}|\right]\right|>M\log(n)\right]\leq\delta\,.

Our contribution is to give a non-asymptotic sharpening of
Eq.

5
in which
\epsilon
and
\delta
have explicit, near-optimal dependence on
n
. As a necessary intermediate step, we show a significant strengthening of
Eq.

4
. Namely, the second moment method yields a non-trivial bound even
exactly
down to the critical threshold
{K_{c}}
. This is new and perhaps surprising.

Theorem 1.7

(Coarse threshold, up to criticality)
.

Fix any
\alpha>0
and let
{K_{c}}:={K_{c}}(\alpha)
. There exists
C\coloneqq C(\alpha)>0
so that for all
n
sufficiently large,

(6)

\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[|Z_{K_{c},\alpha}|^{2}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[|Z_{K_{c},\alpha}|\right]^{2}}\geq C>0\,.

Consequently,

(7)

\mathbb{P}\left[\mathrm{disc}(A)\leq{K_{c}}\right]>C\,.

Since the second moment method can be carried out all the way to criticality, it is natural to suspect similar improvements are possible in
Eq.

5
. Indeed, this is the content of
Theorem

1.6
. We choose to refine the techniques of
[
29
]
because they naturally lead to strong tail bounds, albeit at the cost of an extra logarithm for the width of the critical window. It seems likely that the techniques of
[
2
]
can be used to show
\mathrm{disc}(A)={K_{c}}+\Theta\left(1/n\right)
, resolving this extra log. However, the techniques of
[
2
]
are asymptotic and do not easily lead to tail bounds.

Simultaneously providing strong tail bounds and capturing the exact size of fluctuations is left as an open question; we conjecture that our main result can be improved both by removing the logarithmic mismatch in
Eq.

3
, and also by giving subgaussian rather than subexponential tails in
Eq.

2
.

Conjecture 1.8
.

|\mathrm{disc}(A)-{K_{c}}|
is
1/n^{2}
-subgaussian.

Author’s note: after the appearance of this work, Sah and Sawhney
[
35
]
showed the lower tails of discrepancy are actually sub-exponential. For applications in discrepancy theory, analysis of the upper tails is essential and remains open.

2.
Previous Works

Critical windows of random CSP.

The previous sharp threshold results
[
2
]
and
[
29
]
on the symmetric perceptron correspond to a bound of
o(n)
on the width of the critical window. In the literature of random CSP, it is rare to find optimal, or even non-trivial, improvements over
o(n)
on the critical window. General tools from Fourier Analysis such as the sharp threshold sequence theorems of Friedgut and Bourgain
[
19
]
and Hatami
[
22
]
, are the best known bounds for most CSP (the perceptron
[
41
]
, k-SAT
[
15
]
, and k-coloring
[
3
]
, etc.). Even when applicable, the Friedgut-Bourgain sharp threshold theorem gives only the qualitative upper-bound
o(n)
, and Hatami’s theorem gives a slightly improved
n/\log(\log(n))
. It is possible that many of these standard CSP should have critical windows
polynomially
smaller than
n
.

We are only aware of two other CSP for which such an improvement has been shown: “constrained” XOR-SAT
[
30
]
, and 2-SAT
[
9
]
. Both enjoy very special structure: XOR-SAT is essentially a “matrix invertibility” problem, and its critical window is at most constant due to dimension-counting arguments. The 2-SAT model has critical window of size
\Theta\left(n^{2/3}\right)
, and the analysis employs from a series of clever reductions to a well-studied percolation problem.

Let us also clarify the difference between our result and sharp threshold results for “regular” models, e.g. random regular NAE-SAT. A random regular constraint satisfaction problem on
n
variables is a collection of clauses each involving
k
variables, with each variable in
d
clauses. The number of clauses must then be

nd/k\,.

Ding, Sly, and Sun showed for random regular NAE-SAT
[
14
]
that the largest value of
d
for which there are solutions concentrates around an explicit constant, with at most constant fluctuations. Since a constant change in
d
results in a change of order
n
to the number of clauses, this kind of sharp threshold for
d
seems closer to a qualitative sharp threshold rather than the type of critical window bound investigated here.

Finally, it is quite special that the symmetric perceptron enjoys such a small critical window: there are many sources of fluctuations that could preclude a CSP from having a constant-size critical window. For example, Wilson showed the critical window of the infamous K-SAT problem is at least
\Omega\left(\sqrt{n}\right)
. His argument proceeds by noting that the number of trivially satisfiable clauses is of order
n
with CLT-like fluctuations of order
\Theta\left(\sqrt{n}\right)
. Another example is the critical window of the binary perceptron, which is conjectured by Ding and Sun
[
16
]
to be of order
\Theta\left(\sqrt{n}\right)
. They posit that such large fluctuations should occur due to fluctuations in the barycenter of the solution set.

Coarse threshold.

The connection between discrepancy and the perceptron model was introduced by Aubin, Perkins and Zdeborová in 2019
[
6
]
. They established a
coarse threshold
via the a second moment method. More precisely, they showed—conditional on a numerical conjecture (see
Section

3
)—for any
\epsilon>0
, that
\mathrm{disc}(A)>{K_{c}}-\epsilon
with high probability and
\mathrm{disc}(A)<{K_{c}}-\epsilon
with positive probability. Their work also establishes the negative result that the second moment method does not suffice to prove a sharp threshold, but they nonetheless conjectured a sharp threshold (i.e. the upper-bound should hold with high, rather than positive, probability) .

This conjecture was proven by Abbe, Li, and Sly
[
2
]
, and Perkins and Xu
[
29
]
independently and simultaneously.

Sharp threshold (cycle counting).
Abbe, Li, and Sly used a “dense graph” analogue of the so-called small subgraph conditioning technique of Robinson and Wormald
[
32
]
. They established both the sharp threshold as well as an explicit asymptotic description of the number of solutions. The small subgraph conditioning method is a celebrated technique to correct the failure of the second moment method for counting objects in sparse random graphs. The idea is to explain the extra variance by explicitly quantifying how the number of cycles in a graph affects this count.

Interestingly, the techniques of
[
2
]
have a strong analogue in the literature of the SK (Sherrington-Kirkpatrick) model. A seminal paper of Aizenman, Lebowitz, and Ruelle
[
4
]
—written in 1987, actually predating the appearance of the small subgraph conditioning technique—established the asymptotic log-normal limit for the fluctuations in the free energy of the SK model at high temperatures, also using cycle counts in a dense graph, with many of the same objects appearing. The connection between the techniques of
[
4
]
and
[
32
]
were further investigated and made explicit in a recent work of Banerjee
[
7
]
that establishes a log-normal limit for a perturbation of the SK model.

Sharp threshold (cavity method).
Perkins and Xu independently and simultaneously resolved the sharp threshold conjecture for the SBP with different techniques. They adapted and refined an argument of Talagrand
[
38
]
originally used for the binary perceptron. The technique is a “rigorous cavity method”, in which martingale techniques are used to show concentration of the log-partition function as rows of the matrix are added. We discuss this method further in
Section

5
.

Sun and Nakajima
[
28
]
recently extended the method of Talagrand to give a sharp threshold
sequence
for wide class of random matrices, yielding comparable results to
[
2
,
29
]
in a broader setting, notably including the binary perceptron. (A sharp threshold sequence is a weaker notion of a sharp threshold in which the capacity concentrates around some
{\alpha_{c}}(n)
for each
n
, but
\left({\alpha_{c}}(n)\right)_{n=1}^{\infty}
may not converge to some value that is independent of
n
.)

Algorithms.
The foundational result of combinatorial discrepancy is Spencer’s “Six standard deviations suffice”
[
37
]
, which asserts for
any
square matrix
A
with entries in
[-n^{-1/2},n^{1/2}]
, deterministically
\mathrm{disc}(A)\leq 6
. Finding an efficient algorithm that achieves Spencer’s bound took almost 25 years. While a number of such algorithms were discovered in the last decade, and while many of these easily generalize beyond bounded entries to our setting of iid random entries (e.g.
[
11
]
), they are all far from achieving the optimal constant
{K_{c}}
. There are intimate connections between average-case complexity and the geometry of the “solution space” for optimization problems that heavily motivated the recent work on sharp thresholds for the perceptron. We refer the interested reader to
[
6
,
2
,
1
,
29
,
20
]
for further discussion.

Wide matrices.
The discrepancy of
\alpha n\times n
matrices with
\alpha\to 0
has applications in a surprising range of combinatorial optimization problems. The case of matrices with iid Bernoulli
(p)
entries is of particular interest due to practical reasons as well as connections with the long-standing Beck-Fiala conjecture. The second moment method readily gives a sharp threshold for Gaussian matrices
[
40
]
. However for Bernoulli matrices, there are fundamental obstacles to analyzing the second moment if
p\coloneqq p(n)
vanishes quickly. A sharp characterization of discrepancy was first established for large values of
p

[
23
,
31
,
8
,
17
,
18
,
25
]
, and eventually for all
p

[
5
]
.

3.
Preliminaries

3.1.
First Moment

Let us adopt the language of random Constraint Satisfaction Problems (CSP’s). For an
(\alpha,n)
-Gaussian matrix
A
, define the sublevel set
Z
by:

Z_{K,\alpha}\coloneqq\left\{x\in\left\{\pm 1\right\}^{n}:~\|Ax\|_{\infty}\leq K\right\},

so that

\mathrm{disc}(A)=\inf\{K:~Z_{K,\alpha}\neq\emptyset\}\,.

Then
Z_{K,\alpha}
is the set of solutions to the “discrepancy instance” with parameters
K
and
\alpha
given by the matrix
A
. The matrix
A
encodes a set of constraints: for each row
a_{i}
, a solution
x
needs to satisfy
|\left\langle x,a_{i}\right\rangle|\leq K
. It is natural to suspect that for fixed
\alpha
, the probability of
Z_{K,\alpha}
being empty undergoes a rapid transition from zero to one as
K
increases.

A standard first approach to understanding the value of an optimization problem is the first moment method. If
\mathbb{E}\left[|Z_{K,\alpha}|\right]
is vanishing, then by Markov’s inequality there are no solutions with high probability. This provides a lower bound on
\mathrm{disc}(A)
. For an
(\alpha,n)
-Gaussian matrix
A
, it is an elementary computation that for
W
a standard normal,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbb{E}\left[\left|Z_{K,\alpha}\right|\right]

\displaystyle=\log(2)+\alpha\log p_{K}\,,

\displaystyle p_{K}\coloneqq\mathbb{P}\left[|W|\leq K\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-K}^{K}e^{-w^{2}/2}dw\,.

For fixed
K
, we define
{\alpha_{c}}(K)
the “critical value” of
\alpha
so that the expected number of solutions is one:

(8)

{\alpha_{c}}(K)\coloneqq-\frac{\log(2)}{\log p_{K}}\,.

Conversely, for fixed
\alpha
, we define
{K_{c}}(\alpha)
as the unique solution to

{\alpha_{c}}(K_{c})=\alpha\,.

We end this section by showing that for
\alpha
even slightly greater than
\alpha_{c}
—or equivalently,
K
slightly smaller than
K_{c}
—the expected size of
Z_{K,\alpha}
is exponentially smaller than one. Applying Markov’s inequality yields an upper-bound on the capacity of the symmetric perceptron, completing the elementary half of
Theorem

1.5
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Consider an arbitrary fixed
\alpha>0
and let
{K_{c}}:={K_{c}}(\alpha)
. There exist positive constants
\epsilon
,
c
, and
C
depending only on
\alpha
so that for all
K>0
with
|K-{K_{c}}|<\epsilon
, we have:

(9)

c\leq\frac{\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbb{E}\left[|Z_{K,\alpha}|\right]}{K-{K_{c}}}\leq C\,.

Proof.

Let
{\alpha_{c}}:={\alpha_{c}}(K)
. Recall that
\mathbb{E}\left[|Z_{K,\alpha_{c}}|\right]=1
by construction of
\alpha_{c}
, so that

(10)

\mathbb{E}\left[|Z_{K,\alpha}|\right]=\left(p_{K}\right)^{n(\alpha-{\alpha_{c}})}\,,\quad\text{i.e.}\quad\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbb{E}\left[|Z_{K,\alpha}|\right]=(\log p_{K})(\alpha-{\alpha_{c}})\,.

For fixed
\alpha>0
,
{K_{c}}
is bounded away from 0. For
\epsilon
small enough,
K
is bounded from 0 also. It follows that
p_{K}
and
p_{{K_{c}}}
are bounded from zero as well, so that
\log p_{K}=:c_{K}
and the first two derivatives in
K
of
\log p_{K}
are bounded in absolute value by some constants depending only on
\alpha
. (We have eliminated dependence on
\epsilon
by recall that
\epsilon
is a constant depending only on
\alpha
). Thus, we have by Taylor’s expansion:

(11)

\alpha-\alpha_{c}=\log(2)\left(\frac{1}{\log p_{K}}-\frac{1}{\log p_{{K_{c}}(\alpha)}}\right)=c\left({K_{c}}-K\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(|{K_{c}}-K|^{2}\right)\,.

Combining with
Eq.

10
yields the result.
∎

We will repeatedly use
Eq.

11
to justify the idea that studying
\alpha
close to
\alpha_{c}
and
K
close to
{K_{c}}
are equivalent. It will be convenient extract a formal statement for later use.

Lemma 3.2

(Equivalence of perturbations to
\alpha
and
K
)
.

Let
\alpha:=\alpha(n)
and
K:=K(n)
be two sequences strictly bounded away from zero. There exist positive constants
\epsilon
,
c
, and
C
so that if

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\max\left(|K-{K_{c}}(\alpha)|,~|\alpha-{\alpha_{c}}(K)|\right)<\epsilon\,,

then for all
n
sufficiently large:

c\leq\frac{{\alpha_{c}}(K)-\alpha}{K-{K_{c}}(\alpha)}\leq C\,.

The following are immediate consequences of applying Markov’s inequality to
Proposition

3.1
and then invoking
Eq.

10
.

Lemma 3.3

(First moment method)
.

Fix
K>0
and let
{\alpha_{c}}:={\alpha_{c}}(K)
.
For any
y>0
,

(12)

\mathbb{P}\left[\bm{\alpha_{*}}n\geq{\alpha_{c}}n+y\right]\leq(p_{K})^{y}\,.

Alternatively, fix
\alpha>0
and let
{K_{c}}:={K_{c}}(\alpha)
. There exist positive constants
c
and
\epsilon
so that for all
y\in[0,\epsilon]
,

(13)

\mathbb{P}\left[(\mathrm{disc}(A)-{K_{c}})_{-}>y\right]<e^{-cny}\,.

Integrating the tail bound
Eq.

13
easily yields a one-sided variance bound:

Corollary 3.4
.

\mathbb{E}\left[(\mathrm{disc}(A)-{K_{c}})_{-}^{2}\right]^{1/2}=\mathcal{O}\left(n^{-1}\right)\,.

Proof of
Corollary

3.4
.

Integrating the tail bound
Eq.

13
for
y\in[0,\epsilon]
and noting that trivially
(\mathrm{disc}(A)-{K_{c}})_{-}\leq{K_{c}}
deterministically,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[(\mathrm{disc}(A)-{K_{c}})_{-}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\epsilon}2ye^{-cny}dy+({K_{c}})^{2}e^{-cn\epsilon}\leq\mathcal{O}\left(n^{-2}\right)\,.

∎

In summary, the first moment method applied to
|Z_{K,\alpha}|
shows
\mathrm{disc}(A)
cannot be much smaller than
{K_{c}}
. Far more difficult is showing
\mathrm{disc}(A)
is not much larger than
{K_{c}}
.

3.2.
Second Moment

We turn to the second moment method for an upper bound on
\mathrm{disc}(A)
. Fix
\alpha
and let
Z_{K}:=Z_{K,\alpha}
. If the variance of
|Z_{K}|
is small, then
\mathrm{disc}(A)
will concentrate around the first value
K
for which
\mathbb{E}\left[|Z_{K}|\right]\geq 1
.

Aubin, Perkins, and Zdeborová
[
6
]
established (originally contingent on a numerical hypothesis, later removed by
[
2
]
)

Theorem 3.5

(Coarse threshold;
[
6
]
)
.

Fix any
\alpha>0
and let
{K_{c}}:={K_{c}}(\alpha)
,
Z_{K}:=Z_{K,\alpha}
. There exist positive constants
1<C_{1}<C_{2}<\infty
so that for any
\epsilon>0
, for all
n
sufficiently large,

C_{1}\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[|Z_{{K_{c}}+\epsilon}|^{2}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[|Z_{{K_{c}}+\epsilon}|\right]^{2}}\leq C_{2}\,.

An upper bound on
\mathrm{disc}(A)
immediately follows. By the Paley-Zygmund inequality, for any constants
\alpha>0
and
\epsilon>0
,

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left[\mathrm{disc}(A)<{K_{c}}+\epsilon\right]>0\,.

However, since
C_{1}>1
,
Theorem

3.5
also establishes that the variance of
|Z_{K}|
is too large to directly show that
\mathrm{disc}(A)<{K_{c}}+\epsilon
with high probability. Of course,
|Z_{K}|
having large variance certainly does not imply that
\mathrm{disc}(A)
has large variance. A sharp threshold for
\mathrm{disc}(A)
only requires that the random variable
\mathbbm{1}\left(|Z_{K}|>0\right)
is well-concentrated.

Moving beyond the failure of the second moment method to establish a sharp threshold is often quite difficult, and indeed the results of Abbe, Li, and Sly
[
2
]
, and Perkins and Xu
[
29
]
required significant new ideas. Our work builds on
[
29
]
, which in turn builds on an idea of Talagrand
[
38
]
. Let us begin by formally stating the result of
[
29
]
that we wish to refine:

Theorem 3.6

(
[
29
]
, Theorem 9)
.

Fix
K>0
and
\epsilon>0
. Let
Z_{\alpha}:=Z_{K,\alpha}
and
{\alpha_{c}}:={\alpha_{c}}(K)
. For
\alpha\leq{\alpha_{c}}-\epsilon
and for every
\delta>0
, there exists
M=M(\delta,\epsilon,K)
so that

(14)

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left[\left|\log\left(\frac{\left|Z_{\alpha}\right|}{\mathbb{E}\left[\left|Z_{\alpha}\right|\right]}\right)\right|\geq M\log n\right]\leq\delta\,.

Similarly to
Theorem

3.5
, the original statement of
Theorem

3.6
was originally contingent on a numerical conjecture (
Eq.

17
below) that can be removed by the results of
[
2
]
. Recall

\mathbb{E}\left[|Z_{\alpha}|\right]=\left(p_{K}\right)^{n\left(\alpha_{c}-\alpha\right)}\,,

so that
\mathbb{E}\left[|Z_{\alpha}|\right]=\exp\left\{\Omega\left(\epsilon n\right)\right\}
is exponentially large. In contrast,
Theorem

3.6
yields that the fluctuations of
\log|Z_{\alpha}|
are at most polynomial, establishing that
|Z_{\alpha}|
is non-empty with high probability. The shortcoming we seek to improve is that
Theorem

3.6
only holds for
|\alpha-\alpha_{c}|
of constant order. In order to capture the critical window of the SBP, we need to show the bound of
Theorem

3.6
actually continues to hold even as
|\alpha-{\alpha_{c}}|
approaches order
1/n
. We begin with the important first step of showing that this improvement holds with positive probability.

Theorem 3.7

(Second moment method)
.

Fix positive
K>0
. There exist positive constants
1<C_{1}<C_{2}<\infty
, so that for
n
sufficiently large and any
\alpha\coloneqq\alpha(n)\leq{\alpha_{c}}(K)
,

(15)

C_{1}<\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[|Z_{K,\alpha}|^{2}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[|Z_{K,\alpha}|\right]^{2}}<C_{2}\,.

Consequently, for
A
an
(\alpha,n)
-Gaussian matrix (see
Definition

1.1
),

(16)

\mathbb{P}\left[\mathrm{disc}(A)\leq{K_{c}}(\alpha)\right]>C_{2}^{-1}\,.

Eq.

15
will be used as an important step in our strengthening of
Theorem

3.6
, and
Eq.

16
will be the main ingredient for our lower bound on the variance of
\mathrm{disc}(A)
. We prove
Theorem

3.7
in the next section.

In order to compute the second moment of
|Z_{K,\alpha}|
, we study a quantity we will call the
second moment profile function
(sometimes called in physics the “annealed free energy of pairs”). The second moment profile function is a measure of how much pairs of corners
(\sigma,\tau)
from the discrete hypercube contribute to the second moment
\mathbb{E}\left[\left|Z_{K,\alpha}\right|^{2}\right]
, as a function of the normalized Hamming distance
n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}(\sigma_{i}\neq\tau_{i})
.

Definition 3.8

(Pair probability)
.

For
X
and
Y
independent standard normals, let

q_{K}(\beta)\coloneqq\mathbb{P}\left[\left|\sqrt{\beta}X+\sqrt{1-\beta}Y\right|\leq K,~\left|\sqrt{\beta}X-\sqrt{1-\beta}Y\right|\leq K\right]\,.

Then
q_{K}(\beta)^{\alpha n}
is the probability that
\sigma
and
\tau
with
\left\langle\sigma,\tau\right\rangle=(2\beta-1)n
are both in
Z_{K,\alpha}
.

Definition 3.9

(Second moment profile function)
.

Let
H
be binary entropy; define the second moment profile function
F:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
by

F(\beta)\coloneqq F_{K,\alpha}(\beta)=H(\beta)+\alpha\log q_{K}(\beta)\,.

Figure 1.
The shape of the second moment profile function
F(\beta)
for
K=1
and various
\alpha
. (An identical plot has previously appeared in Figure 1a of
[
6
]
). Note that
F(\beta)
is symmetric around
\beta=1/2
;
F(1/2)=2F(0)=2F(1)
; and most importantly,
F
has a unique maximum at
\beta=1/2
for
\alpha\leq\alpha_{c}
.

Let us collect some trivial facts about
F
for intuition. First, Gaussian random variables which are orthogonal are also independent, so
q(1/2)=p^{2}
for any
K
and
\alpha
. Second, the functions
q(\beta)
and
H(\beta)
are symmetric around
\beta=1/2
, so
F
is as well. Finally, by construction of
{\alpha_{c}}
, for any
K
and
n
,

F_{K,{\alpha_{c}}(K),n}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)=F_{K,{\alpha_{c}}(K)}\left(0\right)=-\log(2)\,.

Regarding the shape of
F
, the following was conjectured and assumed in
[
6
]
and
[
29
]
(as well as an earlier version of
[
2
]
) in order for second moment method computations to be tractable:

Conjecture 3.10

(Shape of
F
; conjecture)
.

For all positive
K
and
\alpha
with
F_{K,\alpha}^{\prime\prime}(1/2)<0
, it further holds that
F\coloneqq F_{K,\alpha}
has a single critical point in
(0,1/2)
. In particular, for any
0\leq a\leq b\leq 1/2
,

(17)

\max_{a\leq\beta\leq b}F(\beta)\in\max\left\{F(a),~F(b)\right\}\,.

Compelling numerical evidence was supplied in
[
6
]
. Later, in
[
2
]
, a slightly weaker form of this conjecture was recovered, for
K>0
and
\alpha<{\alpha_{c}}
. The main difference is that for some
K
, it is only easy to rigorously establish that
F
is decreasing near
0
and increasing near
1/2
. In between, it is far easier to simply show that
F
is much less than
F(1/2)
—rather than controlling the number of critical points—which is more than enough for second moment method applications. We now collect the facts, rigorously established in
[
2
]
, that we will need:

Lemma 3.11

(Shape of
F

[
2
]
)
.

Recall
F\coloneqq F_{K,\alpha}
.

(1)

For any
\alpha
and
K

(18)

F^{\prime\prime}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)=4\left(-1+\frac{2}{\pi}\frac{\alpha K^{2}e^{-K^{2}}}{p^{2}}\right)\,.

(2)

Fix any
K>0
. There exists
\epsilon\coloneqq\epsilon(K)>0
sufficiently small so that for any
\alpha\leq{\alpha_{c}}(K)
,

(19)

F^{\prime\prime}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)<-\epsilon\,.

(3)

There exists
b\coloneqq b(K)>0
such that for all
\alpha\leq{\alpha_{c}}
,
F_{\alpha}(\beta)
is decreasing for
\beta\in[0,b]
.

(4)

Let
K>0
and
F_{{\alpha_{c}}}\coloneqq F_{K,{\alpha_{c}}(K)}
. There exists
\epsilon\coloneqq\epsilon(K)>0
such that, for any
x,y\in[0,1/2]
,

(20)

\max_{\beta\in[x,y]}F_{{\alpha_{c}}}(\beta)\leq\max\{F_{{\alpha_{c}}}(x),~F_{{\alpha_{c}}}(y),~F_{{\alpha_{c}}}(1/2)-\epsilon\}\,.

The proof of
Lemma

3.11
is deferred to the appendix, since it is directly reproduced from
[
2
]
with only trivial modifications. Upon first read, one could also simply assume
Eq.

17
.

3.3.
Sharp Threshold

We seek to boost the “positive probability” guarantee of
Theorem

3.7
into a “high probability” guarantee. Our goal is: if
\log\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\left|Z_{K,\alpha}\right|\right]\right)\gg\log n
, then
|Z_{r}|\gg 1
with high probability. Making this precise and also giving a quantitative tail bound, we will show:

Theorem 3.12

(Upper tail)
.

Fix
K>0
. There exists a constant
c>0
so that if

\alpha\coloneqq\alpha(n)={\alpha_{c}}(K)-xn^{-1}\log(n)

for a sufficiently large constant
x>0
, then for all
n
sufficiently large:

(21)

\mathbb{P}\left[|Z_{K,\alpha}|=0\right]<n^{-cx}\,.

Equivalently, for any
\alpha>0
there exists some constant
c>0
so that for any fixed
x
sufficiently large, for all
n
sufficiently large:

(22)

\mathbb{P}\left[\mathrm{disc}(A)>K_{c}+\frac{x\log(n)}{n}\right]<n^{-cx}\,.

It is fairly straightforward to control the variance of
\mathrm{disc}(A)
by integrating this tail bound. The only (small) complication is that our tail bound does not allow for
x
growing as a function of
n
.

Corollary 3.13
.

Fix
\alpha>0
and let
n
be sufficiently large. We have the one-sided variance bound

(23)

\mathbb{E}\left[(\mathrm{disc}(A)-{K_{c}})_{+}^{2}\right]^{1/2}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log(n)}{n}\right)\,.

Proof of
Corollary

3.13
.

A crude bound suffices. We can always upper-bound
\mathrm{disc}(A)
by
\|A\sigma\|_{\infty}
for some arbitrary
\sigma
. Recall that
A\sigma
is a centered Gaussian vector with an
\alpha n\times\alpha n
identity covariance matrix. It is easy to check by a direct computation the very sub-optimal bound:
\mathbb{E}\left[\|A\sigma\|_{\infty}^{4}\right]<n^{4}
. Define

U\coloneqq(\mathrm{disc}(A)-{K_{c}})_{+}\,.

Then for sufficiently large
x>0
, we have by Cauchy-Schwarz:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[U^{2}\right]^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[U^{2}\mathbbm{1}(U\leq xn^{-1}\log n)\right]^{1/2}+\mathbb{E}\left[U^{2}\mathbbm{1}(xn^{-1}\log n\leq U)\right]^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{x\log(n)}{n}+\mathbb{E}\left[\|A\sigma\|_{\infty}^{4}\right]^{1/4}\mathbb{P}\left[\mathrm{disc}(A)>{K_{c}}+xn^{-1}\log n\right]^{1/4}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{x\log(n)}{n}+n\left(n^{-cx/4}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log(n)}{n}\right)\,.

∎

4.
Second Moment Method

Here we establish that solutions exist up to criticality with positive probability.

Proof of
Theorem

3.7
.

Fix
K>0
and some sequence
\alpha\coloneqq\alpha(n)\leq{\alpha_{c}}
. Our goal is to upper bound the ratio

(24)

\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[|Z_{K,\alpha}|^{2}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[|Z_{K,\alpha}|\right]^{2}}=2^{-n}\sum_{s=0}^{n}\binom{n}{s}\left(\frac{q\left(\frac{s}{n}\right)}{p^{2}}\right)^{\alpha n}\,.

We claim that
Eq.

24
is monotone increasing in
\alpha
, so that if we establish the claim for
\alpha
set to equal
{\alpha_{c}}
, then the claim also follows for all
\alpha\leq{\alpha_{c}}
. Indeed, for any
\beta
, it easy to check that
p^{2}\leq q(\beta)
by a direct computation. Alternatively, if the reader is familiar with such tools, the Gaussian Correlation Inequality (e.g. Theorem 1 of
[
36
]
) also immediately yields
p^{2}\leq q(\beta)
. Indeed,
q(\beta)
is the probability that the Gaussian vector
A_{i}
is in the intersection of two origin-symmetric convex slabs:

\left\{a:|\left\langle a,\sigma\right\rangle|\leq K\right\}\cap\left\{a:|\left\langle a,\tau\right\rangle|\leq K\right\}\,,

where
\sigma
and
\tau
are corners of the discrete cube with Hamming distance
\beta n
.

So, it suffices to upper bound
Eq.

24
for
\alpha={\alpha_{c}}
. Note that for this choice of
\alpha
, the denominator of the left of
Eq.

24
is simply one. For convenience, we adopt the shorthand
F\coloneqq F_{K,{\alpha_{c}}(K),n}
.

Let
m\coloneqq{\alpha_{c}}n
be the number of rows in the matrix
A
. Consider
\beta_{s}\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{s}{2n}
. We would like to Taylor expand
q(\beta_{s})
; first note that
q(0)=p^{2}
. Next,

\displaystyle q^{\prime}(\beta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\partial_{\beta}\int_{x=-K}^{K}\int_{y=\frac{-K-(2\beta-1)x}{2\sqrt{\beta(1-\beta)}}}^{\frac{K-(2\beta-1)x}{2\sqrt{\beta(1-\beta)}}}\exp\left\{-\frac{x^{2}+y^{2}}{2}\right\}dydx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{\beta(1-\beta)}}e^{-\frac{K^{2}}{2(1-\beta)}}\left(e^{\frac{(2\beta-1)K^{2}}{2\beta(1-\beta)}}-1\right)\,.

Note that
q^{\prime}(1/2)=0
. Differentiating again and then evaluating at
\beta=1/2
yields:

\displaystyle q^{\prime\prime}(1/2)

\displaystyle=\frac{8e^{-K^{2}}K^{2}}{\pi}=4p^{2}\left(1-\mu_{2}\right)^{2}\,,

where we have adopted the following notation of
[
2
]
:

(25)

\mu_{2}\coloneqq p^{-1}\int_{-K}^{K}x^{2}e^{-x^{2}/2}\frac{dx}{\sqrt{2\pi}}=-p^{-1}\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}Ke^{-K^{2}/2}+1\,.

Thus, restricted to
|\beta_{s}|<\epsilon
for some small constant
\epsilon>0
depending only on
K
, Taylor’s expansion yields

(26)

q(\beta_{s})=p^{2}\left(1+\frac{(1-\mu_{2})^{2}(s^{2}/n)}{2n}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{s^{4}}{n^{4}}\right)\right)\,.

We are ready to bound
Eq.

24
. Split the sum into three annular regions and treat them separately. (For the first region, highly similar computations appear in e.g. Lemma 3.1 in
[
2
]
; Lemma 7 of
[
6
]
; or Lemma 3 of
[
5
]
.)

(1)

(
1/2-\delta\leq\beta\leq 1/2
) Recall
\beta_{s}\coloneqq 1/2+s/(2n)
. Let
\delta>0
be some arbitrarily small constant that we fix later. For
\delta
sufficiently small, we apply Stirling’s formula and
Eq.

26
. Denote for shorthand
B\coloneqq\sqrt{\alpha}(1-\mu_{2})/2
. Counting over even-valued
s
,

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{|s|\leq 2\delta n}2^{-n}\binom{n}{\frac{n+s}{2}}\left(\frac{q(\beta_{s})}{p^{2}}\right)^{m}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2}{n\pi}}\sum_{|s|\leq 2\delta n}\exp\left\{-\frac{s^{2}}{2n}\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(\delta\right)\right)\right\}\exp\left\{\frac{\alpha}{2}(1-\mu_{2})^{2}\left(\frac{s^{2}}{n}\right)\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(\delta\right)\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2}{n\pi}}\sum_{|s|\leq 2\delta n}\exp\left\{-\frac{s^{2}}{2n}\left(1-4B^{2}\right)\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(\delta\right)\right)\right\}e^{-2B^{2}}\,.

Since
\delta
can be taken arbitrarily small, it suffices to show
|B|
is strictly less than
1/2
, uniformly for sufficiently large
n
. This follows immediately by combining
Eq.

18
with
Eq.

25
. Indeed, by
Eq.

19
, there is some
\epsilon\coloneqq\epsilon(K)>0
so that

1-4B^{2}=-\frac{1}{4}F^{\prime\prime}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)>\epsilon>0\,.

Thus
|B|
is strictly bounded from
1/2
, yielding

(27)

I_{1}=\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(\delta\right)\right)e^{-2B^{2}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{1-4B^{2}}}\,.

(The factor of
2
from
\sqrt{2/(n\pi)}
was cancelled by the fact that we were only summing over even
s
). Taking
\delta
sufficiently small, we obtain for some implicit constant depending only on
K
that
I_{1}=\mathcal{O}\left(1\right)
.

(2)

(
\delta\leq\beta\leq 1/2-\delta
)
Let
I_{2}
be

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{s:~2\delta n\leq|s|<(1-2\delta)n}2^{-n}\binom{n}{\frac{n+s}{2}}\left(\frac{q(\beta_{s})}{p^{2}}\right)^{m}\,.

Applying Stirling’s formula,

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle<n(2^{-n}p^{-2m})\max_{\beta\in[\delta,\frac{1}{2}-\delta]}\exp\left\{nF(\beta)+\mathcal{O}\left(\log n\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=\max_{\beta\in[\delta,\frac{1}{2}-\delta]}\exp\left\{n\left(F(\beta)-F(1/2)\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\log n\right)\right\}\,.

It suffices to show the exponent is strictly negative and of order
n
on this interval. Applying
Eq.

20
, we have for some
\epsilon_{0}\coloneqq\epsilon_{0}(K,\delta)>0
,

\displaystyle\max_{\beta\in[\delta,1/2-\delta]}F(\beta)-F(1/2)

\displaystyle\leq\max\left\{F(\delta)-F(1/2),~F(1/2-\delta)-F(1/2),-\epsilon\right\}\leq-\epsilon_{0}\,.

Thus for some implicit constant depending only on
K
and
\delta
,
I_{2}=\exp\left\{-|\Theta\left(n\right)|\right\}
.

(3)

(
0\leq\beta<\delta
)
Define
I_{3}
, the contribution of the remaining terms, by

\displaystyle I_{3}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{|s|>(1-2\delta)n}2^{-n}\binom{n}{\frac{n+s}{2}}\left(\frac{q(\beta_{s})}{p^{2}}\right)^{m}\,.

By Stirling’s formula—and recalling we set
\alpha
to be
{\alpha_{c}}(K)
, where
{\alpha_{c}}
is given in
Eq.

8
—we also have the identity

(28)

I_{3}=\sum_{|s|>(1-2\delta)n}p^{-m}\exp\left\{nF(\beta_{s})+\mathcal{O}\left(\log n\right)\right\}\,.

Since we are able to take
\delta
an arbitrarily small constant in all the previous parts of this proof, let
\delta
be sufficiently small so that by
Lemma

3.11
,
F
is decreasing on
[0,\delta]
. The idea is to say
F(\beta)
is already extremely negative even for
\beta=1/n
. Intuitively, such a dramatic decay is possible due to the “frozen” nature of typical solutions or, equivalently, the fact that
F^{\prime}(\beta)\to-\infty
as
\beta\to 0
from above. Note the minor subtlety that
F
being decreasing does not directly imply that the summand of
I_{3}
is decreasing, due to the logarithmic error terms in Stirling’s formula. Conveniently,
F(1/n)
is negative enough to outweigh these lower-order corrections.

First computing the summand for
\beta=0
, since
q(0)=p
, we have:

\displaystyle 2^{-n}\binom{n}{0}\left(\frac{q(0)}{p^{2}}\right)^{m}=2^{-n}p^{-m}=1\,.

Next, in order to compute the summand for
\beta=1/n
, we claim: (c.f. Lemma 6 of
[
29
]
for a related result on the “planted” model)

(29)

\frac{q\left(1/n\right)}{q\left(0\right)}=\frac{q\left(1/n\right)}{p}\leq 1-\Omega\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\,.

Indeed, let
\beta\coloneqq 1/n
and define
\Delta\coloneqq c_{0}n^{-1/2}
for some new positive constant
c_{0}
that we fix to be sufficiently small later. We have by definition of
q
:

\displaystyle q(\beta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-K}^{K}e^{-y^{2}/2}\int_{\frac{-K+(1-2\beta)y}{2\sqrt{\beta(1-\beta)}}}^{\frac{K+(1-2\beta)y}{2\sqrt{\beta(1-\beta)}}}\exp\left\{-x^{2}/2\right\}dxdy

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-K}^{K-\Delta}e^{-y^{2}/2}dy+\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{K-\Delta}^{K}e^{-y^{2}/2}\int_{\frac{-K+(1-2\beta)y}{2\sqrt{\beta(1-\beta)}}}^{\frac{K+(1-2\beta)y}{2\sqrt{\beta(1-\beta)}}}\exp\left\{-x^{2}/2\right\}dxdy

\displaystyle\leq p_{K}-\Delta\left(\frac{e^{-K^{2}/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\right)+\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{K-\Delta}^{K}e^{-y^{2}/2}\int_{\frac{-K+(1-2\beta)(K-\Delta)}{2\sqrt{\beta(1-\beta)}}}^{\infty}\exp\left\{-x^{2}/2\right\}dxdy\,.

Examining the bounds of integration,

\displaystyle\frac{-K+(1-2\beta)(K-\Delta)}{2\sqrt{\beta(1-\beta)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{-2K\beta-\Delta}{2\sqrt{1/n}}\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\right)=-\frac{\Delta\sqrt{n}}{2}\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\right)\,.

Thus, we obtain in total:

\displaystyle q\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)

\displaystyle\leq p_{K}-\Delta\left(\frac{e^{-K^{2}/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\right)+\frac{1+\mathcal{O}\left(n^{-1}\right)}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{K-\Delta}^{K}e^{-y^{2}/2}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\int_{-\frac{c_{0}}{2}}^{0}\exp\left\{-x^{2}/2\right\}dx\right)dy

\displaystyle=p_{K}+\Delta\left(-\frac{e^{-K^{2}/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}+\frac{e^{-K^{2}/2}}{2\sqrt{2\pi}}+\Theta\left(c_{0}e^{-K^{2}/2}\right)\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)

\displaystyle\leq p_{K}-\Theta\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\,.

The last line follows by taking
c_{0}
sufficiently small. We have thus established the desired bound
Eq.

29
. Raising this inequality to the
m
’th power yields

q(1/n)^{m}\leq p^{m}\exp\left\{-\Omega\left(\sqrt{n}\right)\right\}\,,

which is far smaller than even what is needed. Indeed, returning to
Eq.

28
and recalling that
F
is strictly decreasing, as well as noting crudely that
H(1/n)<n^{-.9}
, we obtain:

\displaystyle I_{3}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=0}^{\delta n}p^{-m}\exp\left\{nF(\beta_{s})+\mathcal{O}\left(\log n\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\leq 2\left(1+\delta n\left(\frac{q(1/n)}{p}\right)^{m}\exp\left\{n^{.1}+\mathcal{O}\left(\log n\right)\right\}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\left(1+\exp\left\{-\Omega\left(\sqrt{n}\right)\right\}\right)\,.

Thus,
I_{3}=2+\mathrm{o}(1)
.

This completes our casework. Summing
I_{1}
,
I_{2}
, and
I_{3}
, we have shown that the second moment ratio (
24
) is
\mathcal{O}\left(1\right)
. By our previous considerations on the monotonicity of
Eq.

24
with respect to
\alpha
,
Eq.

15
is then established for all
\alpha\coloneqq\alpha(n)\leq{\alpha_{c}}
. By the Paley-Zygmund inequality,
Eq.

16
follows immediately and the lemma is complete.
∎

5.
Sharp Threshold

5.1.
Sketch of
Theorem

3.12

By
Lemma

3.2
(“equivalence of perturbations to
\alpha_{c}
and
K_{c}
”),
Eq.

21
implies
Eq.

22
. In order to establish
Eq.

21
, we need to refine
Theorem

3.6
of Perkins and Xu to hold even for
\alpha
allowed to vary with
n
. In particular, it should hold for
\alpha\leq\alpha_{0}\coloneqq{\alpha_{c}}-xn^{-1}\log(n)
. We also would like quantitative tail-bounds. Let us briefly survey the proof.

We follow an approach that Talagrand developed for the binary perceptron
[
38
]
. Talagrand’s approach was carefully adapted to the setting of the symmetric perceptron by Perkins and Xu
[
29
]
. The outline is to build a constraint satisfaction problem by adding one clause at a time, and then show the logarithm of the number of solutions concentrates via martingale arguments.

Reusing the notation of
[
29
]
for clarity of comparison, define a time-indexed process
(S_{t})_{t}
of the solutions to the first
t
rows:

(30)

S_{t}\coloneqq\{\sigma\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}:|(A\sigma)_{j}|\leq K,~\forall~1\leq j\leq t\},\quad\mathbb{E}\left[|S_{t}|\right]=2^{n}(p_{K})^{t}\,.

In terms of our previous notation,
S_{t}
is equal to
Z_{K,\alpha}
with
\alpha=t/n
. Denote the deviation of
\log|S_{t}|
from its mean by
Q_{t}
, which we write as a telescoping sum:

Q_{t}\coloneqq\log\left(\frac{|S_{t}|}{\mathbb{E}\left[|S_{t}|\right]}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{t}\left[\log\left(\frac{|S_{i}|}{\mathbb{E}\left[|S_{i}|\right]}\right)-\log\left(\frac{|S_{i-1}|}{\mathbb{E}\left[|S_{i-1}|\right]}\right)\right]\,.

Rewriting
Q_{t}
in terms of a martingale that we call
Y_{t}
,

Q_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{t}\log(1+Y_{i}),\quad Y_{t}\coloneqq\frac{1}{p}\left(\frac{|S_{t}|}{|S_{t-1}|}-p\right)\,.

Then, as long as
Y_{i}
is small for each
i\leq\alpha_{0}n
, we can use the Taylor Expansion

Q_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{t}Y_{i}-\frac{Y_{i}^{2}}{2}+\mathcal{O}\left(Y_{i}^{3}\right)\,.

The
Y_{i}
are centered and should be roughly
Y_{i}\asymp n^{-1/2}
, so that both the sum of the
Y_{i}
and the sum of the
Y_{i}^{2}
are constant order, and the sum of the
Y_{i}^{3}
is vanishing. We then expect
|Q_{t}|=\mathcal{O}\left(\log(n)\right)
with exponential tails, which would yield the theorem. Indeed,
\log\mathbb{E}\left[\left|S_{\alpha_{0}n}\right|\right]
is at least
\Omega\left(x\log(n)\right)
, so if we take
x
sufficiently large, then
|Q_{t}|\ll\log\mathbb{E}\left[\left|S_{t}\right|\right]
for all
t\leq\alpha_{0}n
with very high probability. This would imply that the fluctuations of
|S_{t}|
are much less than its mean, yielding the result. More precisely, we would have with high probability:

\displaystyle\log|S_{\alpha_{0}n}|

\displaystyle=\log\mathbb{E}\left[|S_{\alpha_{0}n}|\right]+\log\left(\frac{|S_{\alpha_{0}n}|}{\mathbb{E}\left[|S_{\alpha_{0}n}|\right]}\right)

\displaystyle=\log\mathbb{E}\left[|S_{\alpha_{0}n}|\right]+Q_{\alpha_{0}n}

\displaystyle\geq cx\log n-C\log n

\displaystyle\gg 0\,.

The last line is achieved by taking
x
sufficiently large, completing the theorem.

The analysis is by induction. The key idea for this induction is a geometric notion of regularity for
S_{t}
that asserts solutions are sufficiently “well-spread”. We begin by assuming for induction that
S_{t^{\prime}}
is
regular
for all
t^{\prime}\leq t
(precise definition given shortly). Then we follow with three estimates (c.f. Lemmas 11, 12, and 13 of
[
29
]
respectively). First, if
S_{t}
is regular, then
Y_{t+1}
enjoys good tail bounds. Second, if
Y_{t^{\prime}}
has good tails for all
t^{\prime}\leq t+1
, then martingale concentration yields exponential tail bounds for
Q_{t+1}
. Third, if
Q_{t+1}
is small, then
S_{t+1}
is also regular with good probability.

We emphasize that this structure was innovated in
[
38
]
and refined in
[
29
]
. We will directly use the first estimate (tail bound on
Y_{t}
) from
[
29
]
. For the second and third estimates, we also significantly borrow from the structure of the proofs in
[
29
]
, albeit with exponential improvements. This concludes our sketch.

5.2.
Proof of
Theorem

3.12
(c.f. Theorem 9 of
[
29
]
)

Let
S_{t}
,
Q_{t}
, and
Y_{t}
be as defined in the sketch. Fix
K>0
. All other constants in what follows will depend on
K
; we treat
K
as a universal constant and suppress this dependence in our notation. Fix
x
sufficiently large. Define
\alpha_{0}\coloneqq\alpha_{0}(n)
by
\alpha_{0}n\coloneqq{\alpha_{c}}n-x\log(n)
. We emphasize that no constants in what follows will depend implicitly on
x
. Recall our convention that
c
and
C
denote generic constants that may vary between lines; important constants will have a subscript.

In order to use the martingale structure of
Y_{t}
, define the filtration
\mathcal{F}_{t}
generated by revealing the first
t
rows of the matrix
A
. Define the conditional measure
\mathbb{P}_{t}\left[\cdot\right]
by

(31)

\mathbb{P}_{t}\left[\cdot\right]:=\mathbb{P}\left[\cdot\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]\,.

With this notation, we are ready to formally state our main definitions and lemmas.

Definition 5.1

(Regular)
.

We say
S_{t}\subset\{\pm 1\}^{n}
is
regular
if
S_{t}\neq\emptyset
and, for
\sigma_{t}^{(1)}
,
\sigma_{t}^{(2)}
drawn uniformly and independently (with replacement) from
S_{t}
,

\mathbb{P}_{t}\left[\left|\left\langle\sigma_{t}^{(1)},\sigma_{t}^{(2)}\right\rangle\right|>\sqrt{C_{r}n(x\log(n)+|Q_{t}|)}\right]\leq n^{-c_{r}x}\,,

where
C_{r}
and
c_{r}
are some positive universal constants.

The parameters
C_{r}
and
c_{r}
are explicitly chosen below in
Eq.

45
and
Eq.

50
respectively. Define two stopping times:
\tau_{S}
the first time
t
that
S_{t}
is not regular and
\tau_{Q}
the first time that
|Q_{t}|
is large. Also define the first time
\tau
that either of these “bad” events occurs. More precisely, for
C_{q}\in(0,1)
some small universal constant (explicitly chosen in
Eq.

43
below),

\displaystyle\tau_{S}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\min\left\{t:\,S_{t}\text{ is not regular}\right\}\,,

\displaystyle\tau_{Q}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\min\left\{t:\,|Q_{t}|>C_{q}x\log(n)\right\}\,,

\displaystyle\tau

\displaystyle\coloneqq\tau_{S}\wedge\tau_{Q}\,.

The first estimate of the induction is that if
S_{t}
is regular then
Y_{t+1}
is sub-exponential. Up to some trivial rewriting (we use
\tau_{Q}
to suppress
|Q|
), the following is exactly Lemma 11 of
[
29
]
.

Lemma 5.2

(
Y_{t+1}
is small;
[
29
]
, Lemma 11)
.

There exist positive constants
c
and
C
so that for any
t
, for all sufficiently large
n
and
y
,

(32)

\mathbbm{1}\left(\tau>t\right)\mathbb{P}_{t}\left[|Y_{t+1}|>Cy\sqrt{\frac{(1+C_{q}x)\log n}{n}}\right]\leq\exp\left\{-cy\right\}\,.

Next, we would like to say that
Q_{t}
is small for
t\leq\tau_{S}
. Only a first-moment bound on
|Q_{t}|
is given in
[
29
]
; the refinement we require is a bound on the moment-generating function. This will both allow us take
t
much closer to
{\alpha_{c}}
as well as yield strong tail bounds on
|S_{t}|
.

Lemma 5.3

(
Q_{t+1}
is small)
.

There exists a constant
c>0
so that for all sufficiently large
n
and any
t\leq\alpha_{0}n
,

(33)

\mathbb{P}\left[|Q_{t\wedge\tau}|>C_{q}x\log n\right]\leq n^{-cx}\,.

Assuming
S_{t}
is regular, these lemmas yield that
|Y_{t+1}|
and
|Q_{t+1}|
are small. The induction is complete if
|Q_{t+1}|
being small implies
S_{t+1}
is regular.

Lemma 5.4

(
S_{t+1}
is regular;
\tau
is large)
.

There exists a constant
c>0
so that for all sufficiently large
n
,

\mathbb{P}\left[\tau_{S}\leq\alpha_{0}n\right]<n^{-cx}\,.

We prove these lemmas in the next subsection. Let us first formally check that they imply
Theorem

3.12
, our desired bound on the upper tail of
\mathrm{disc}(A)
.

Proof of
Theorem

3.12
.

For convenience, define the constant
c_{K}:=-\log(p_{K})>0
. Since
K
is fixed,
c_{K}
may be treated as a universal constant. By
Eq.

10
,

(34)

\log\mathbb{E}\left[|S_{t}|\right]=c_{K}(\alpha_{c}n-t)\geq c_{K}x\log n,\quad\forall\,t\leq\alpha_{0}n\,.

We will eventually choose
C_{q}
explicitly (in
Eq.

43
) so that
C_{q}\leq c_{K}/2
. Thus, on the event
\left\{\tau_{Q}>t\right\}
, we have
|S_{t}|\gg 0
almost surely. Indeed, as sketched in
Section

5.1
,

\displaystyle\log|S_{t}|=Q_{t}+\log\mathbb{E}\left[|S_{t}|\right]\geq-C_{q}x\log n+c_{K}x\log n\geq\frac{c_{K}}{2}x\log n\,,

where the equality is by definition of
Q_{t}
and the first inequality holds almost surely on the event
\left\{\tau_{Q}>t\right\}
.

The theorem will follow if the event
\left\{\tau_{Q}>\alpha_{0}n\right\}
holds with very high probability. By
Lemma

5.4
, with probability at least
1-n^{-cx}
, we have
\left\{\tau_{S}>\alpha_{0}n\right\}
, i.e. the solution space is regular simultaneously for all
t\leq\alpha_{0}n
. Then, by
Lemma

5.3
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\tau_{Q}\leq\alpha_{0}n\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left[\tau_{S}\leq\alpha_{0}n\right]+\sum_{t=1}^{\alpha_{0}n}\mathbb{P}\left[\tau_{S}>\alpha_{0}n,\tau_{Q}=t\right]

\displaystyle\leq n^{-cx}+\sum_{t=1}^{\alpha_{0}n}\mathbb{P}\left[|Q_{t\wedge\tau}|>C_{q}x\log n\right]

\displaystyle\leq n^{-cx}\,.

In summary, we have shown:

\mathbb{P}\left[|Z_{K,\,{\alpha_{c}}n-x\log n}|=0\right]\leq\mathbb{P}\left[|S_{\alpha_{0}n}|\leq e^{\frac{c_{K}}{2}x\log n}\right]\leq\mathbb{P}\left[\tau_{Q}\leq\alpha_{0}n\right]\leq n^{-cx}\,.

∎

5.3.
Proofs of Lemmas

Proof of
Lemma

5.3
.

Fix
t\leq\alpha_{0}n
and define the event
E
that all the
Y_{i}
are bounded for
i\leq t\wedge\tau
:

E:=\bigcap_{i\leq t-1}~\left\{\mathbbm{1}_{\tau>i}|Y_{i+1}|<n^{-.4}\right\}\,.

By
Lemma

5.2
and union bound, the event
E
holds with probability at least
1-n^{-C_{E}}
for any (arbitrarily large) constant
C_{E}
. Let
\lambda\in(0,1)
, where
\lambda
does not vary with
n
and will be taken sufficiently small later; our goal is to show

(35)

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left\{\lambda|Q_{t\wedge\tau}|\right\}\mathbbm{1}_{E}\right]\leq n^{\lambda^{2}Cx}\,.

Let us simplify
Eq.

35
. Since
e^{Q_{t}}
is a martingale for all
t
by construction and
\lambda\in(0,1)
, the optional stopping theorem and Jensen’s inequality yield

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left\{\lambda Q_{t\wedge\tau}\right\}\right]\leq 1\,.

Thus, to establish
Eq.

35
, it suffices to show:

(36)

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left\{-\lambda Q_{t\wedge\tau}\right\}\mathbbm{1}_{E}\right]\leq n^{\lambda^{2}Cx}.

By a Taylor expansion, we have on the event
E
that

(37)

\exp\left\{-\lambda\log(1+Y_{i})\right\}\leq 1-C\lambda Y_{i}+C\lambda^{2}Y_{i}^{2},\quad\forall\,i\leq t\wedge\tau\,.

Additionally, by integrating the tail bound of
Lemma

5.2
, we obtain for some new positive constant
C>0
:

(38)

\mathbbm{1}_{\tau>i}\mathbb{E}_{i}\left[Y_{i+1}^{2}\right]\leq Cn^{-1}(1+C_{q}x)\log n,\quad\forall\,i\,.

Recall that
(Y_{i})_{i}
is a martingale under the filtration generated by revealing the rows of
A
. Combining
Eq.

37
and
Eq.

38
yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left\{-\lambda Q_{t\wedge\tau}\right\}\mathbbm{1}_{E}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left\{-\lambda Q_{(t-1)\wedge\tau}-\mathbbm{1}_{\tau>t-1}\lambda\log(1+Y_{t})\right\}\mathbbm{1}_{E}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left\{-\lambda Q_{(t-1)\wedge\tau}\right\}\mathbb{E}_{t-1}\left[\exp\left\{-\mathbbm{1}_{\tau>t-1}\lambda\log(1+Y_{t})\right\}\right]\mathbbm{1}_{E}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left\{-\lambda Q_{(t-1)\wedge\tau}\right\}\left(1-C\mathbbm{1}_{\tau>t-1}\lambda\mathbb{E}_{t-1}\left[Y_{t}\right]+C\mathbbm{1}_{\tau>t-1}\lambda^{2}\mathbb{E}_{t-1}\left[Y_{t}^{2}\right]\right)\mathbbm{1}_{E}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left\{-\lambda Q_{(t-1)\wedge\tau}\right\}\mathbbm{1}_{E}\left(1+\lambda^{2}\frac{C(1+C_{q}x)\log n)}{n}\right)\right]\,.

We have used that the linear term of
Y
in
Eq.

37
vanishes in expectation. Since
Q_{0}=0
by definition, we obtain the desired result (
36
) by an easy induction:

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left\{-\lambda Q_{t\wedge\tau}\right\}\mathbbm{1}_{E}\right]\leq\left(1+\lambda^{2}\frac{C(1+C_{q}x)\log n}{n}\right)^{t}\leq n^{\lambda^{2}C(1+C_{q}x)}\leq n^{\lambda^{2}Cx}\,,

where the last inequality follows for some new constant
C
, since
C_{q}
is an explicit universal constant independent of
x
, and
x
is assumed to be sufficiently large. Finally, fix
\lambda=\min\left\{C_{q}/(2C),~1/2\right\}
, so that
\lambda\in(0,1)
as promised. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[|Q_{t\wedge\tau}|>C_{q}x\log(n)\right]\leq n^{\lambda^{2}Cx}n^{-\lambda C_{q}x}+\mathbb{P}\left[E^{c}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\min\left\{n^{-C_{q}^{2}x/(2C)},~n^{-C_{q}x/2}\right\}+n^{-C_{E}}

\displaystyle=:n^{-cx}\,.

The last line follows by taking
C_{E}
arbitrarily large.
∎

Proof of
Lemma

5.4
.

By union bound and the tower property,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\tau\leq\alpha_{0}n\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{\alpha_{0}n}\mathbb{P}\left[\tau=t\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=1}^{\alpha_{0}n}\mathbb{P}\left[\tau_{S}=t,~\tau_{Q}>t\right]+\mathbb{P}\left[\tau_{Q}=t,~\tau>t-1\right]

\displaystyle\leq n^{-cx}+\sum_{t=1}^{\alpha_{0}n}\mathbb{P}\left[\tau_{S}=t,~\tau_{Q}>t\right]

The last line follows by
Lemma

5.3
. It then suffices to show each summand is bounded above by
n^{-cx}
. Formally, our goal is:

(39)

\mathbb{P}\left[\tau_{S}=t,~\tau_{Q}>t\right]\leq n^{-cx},\quad\forall t\,\leq\alpha_{0}n\,.

By
Lemma

3.11
, there exists some constant
c_{f}>0
so that for all
n
sufficiently large,

(40)

\max_{\beta\in[0,1/2-\lambda n^{-1/2}]}F_{K,\,t/n,\,n}(\beta)\leq-c_{f}\min\left\{\lambda^{2},\,\mathbb{E}\left[|Z_{K,\,t/n}\right]\right\}\,.

As a convenient short-hand, we write

R_{t}(\lambda)\coloneqq c_{f}(\lambda^{2}\wedge L_{t}),\quad L_{t}\coloneqq\log\left(\mathbb{E}\left[|Z_{K,t/n}\right]\right)\,.

Then, for two vectors
\sigma_{t}^{(1)}
and
\sigma_{t}^{(2)}
drawn uniformly and independently from
S_{t}
, it can easily be extracted from the proof of
Theorem

3.7
that:

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\left\{(\sigma_{t}^{(1)},\sigma_{t}^{(2)})\in S_{t}:~\left|\left\langle\sigma_{t}^{(1)},\sigma_{t}^{(2)}\right\rangle\right|\geq\lambda\sqrt{n}\right\}\right|\right]\leq\exp\left\{-R_{t}(\lambda)\right\}\mathbb{E}\left[|S_{t}|^{2}\right]\,.

In particular, since we showed in
Theorem

3.7
that there exists a positive constant
C
so that
\mathbb{E}\left[|S_{t}|^{2}\right]\leq C\mathbb{E}\left[|S_{t}|\right]^{2}
uniformly for all
t
,

(41)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{P}_{t}\left[\left|\left\langle\sigma_{t}^{(1)},\sigma_{t}^{(2)}\right\rangle\right|\geq\lambda\sqrt{n}\right]\frac{|S_{t}|^{2}}{\mathbb{E}\left[|S_{t}|\right]^{2}}\right]\leq C\exp\left\{-R_{t}(\lambda)\right\}\,.

We would now like to show an exponential tail bound on the overlap of a pair of solutions. Let us give this a short-hand name for convenience:

\mathcal{O}^{t}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left|\left\langle\sigma_{t}^{(1)},\sigma_{t}^{(2)}\right\rangle\right|\,.

Recall that by definition
\exp\left\{Q_{t}\right\}=|S_{t}|/\mathbb{E}\left[|S_{t}|\right]
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left\{\frac{R_{t}(\mathcal{O}^{t})}{2}+2Q_{t}\right\}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left\{\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{c_{f}\left\langle\sigma_{t}^{(1)},\sigma_{t}^{(2)}\right\rangle^{2}}{n}\wedge L_{t}\right)+2Q_{t}\right\}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}_{t}\left[\exp\left\{\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{c_{f}\left\langle\sigma_{t}^{(1)},\sigma_{t}^{(2)}\right\rangle^{2}}{n}\wedge L_{t}\right)\right\}\geq y\right]dy\frac{|S_{t}|^{2}}{\mathbb{E}\left[|S_{t}|\right]^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\exp\left\{L_{t}/2\right\}}\mathbb{P}_{t}\left[\exp\left\{\frac{c_{f}}{2n}\left\langle\sigma_{t}^{(1)},\sigma_{t}^{(2)}\right\rangle^{2}\right\}\geq y\right]dy\frac{|S_{t}|^{2}}{\mathbb{E}\left[|S_{t}|\right]^{2}}\right]\,.

From the second to the third line, we have conditioned on
S_{t}
. Applying the change of variables
z\coloneqq\sqrt{2\log(y)/c_{f}}
and then
Eq.

41
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left\{\frac{R_{t}(\mathcal{O}^{t})}{2}+2Q_{t}\right\}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\sqrt{L_{t}/c_{f}}}c_{f}ze^{c_{f}z^{2}/2}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{P}_{t}\left[\left|\left\langle\sigma_{t}^{(1)},\sigma_{t}^{(2)}\right\rangle\right|\geq z\sqrt{n}\right]\frac{|S_{t}|^{2}}{\mathbb{E}\left[|S_{t}|\right]^{2}}\right]dz

6.
Lower Bound on Fluctuations

Lemma 6.1
.

Fix positive
\alpha
and let
{K_{c}}:={K_{c}}(\alpha)
,
Z_{K}:=Z_{K,\alpha}
. There exists a positive constant
C
such that for
K
with

,

sufficiently large, and positive
\epsilon>0
sufficiently small:

(51)

\mathbb{P}\left[K-\frac{\epsilon}{n}\leq\mathrm{disc}(A)\leq K\right]<C\epsilon~\mathbb{E}\left[|Z_{K}|\right]\,.

As an easily corollary, we obtain a lower bound on the variance of
\mathrm{disc}(A)
, completing our main result
Theorem

1.5
. In fact, we obtain a lower bound on fluctuations, which is strictly stronger than a bound on the variance.

Definition 6.2

(Fluctuations)
.

We say that a sequence of random variables
X_{n}
has fluctuations at least of order
\sigma_{n}
if there exist constants
\delta>0
and
C>0
such that, for
n
sufficiently large and any
|a-b|>C\sigma_{n}
,

\mathbb{P}\left[a<X_{n}<b\right]<1-\delta\,.

Corollary 6.3
.

The fluctuations of
\mathrm{disc}(A)
are at least order
1/n
.

Proof of
Corollary

6.3
.

By
Theorem

3.7
, we know that for some
\delta>0

\mathbb{P}\left[\mathrm{disc}(A)\leq K_{c}(\alpha)\right]>\delta>0\,.

So, for any
a
and
b
possibly functions of
n
, with
K_{c}\leq a\leq b
, we have uniformly

\mathbb{P}\left[\mathrm{disc}(A)\in[a,b]\right]<1-\delta\,.

All that remains is the case that
a\coloneqq a(n)
and
b\coloneqq b(n)
with
a<b
and
a\leq K_{c}
. Assume
|a-b|\leq\epsilon/n
for some sufficiently small constant
\epsilon>0
we choose shortly. By definition of
K_{c}
as well as the expansion
Eq.

9
, we have for some
C>0
,

\mathbb{E}\left[|Z_{b}|\right]\leq Ce^{Cn(b-{K_{c}})_{+}}\leq C\exp\left\{C\epsilon\right\}\,.

By
Eq.

51
, for
\epsilon

\mathbb{P}\left[\mathrm{disc}(A)\in[a,b]\right]<C\epsilon\exp\left\{C\epsilon\right\}\leq\frac{1}{2}\,.

∎

Proof of
Lemma

6.1
.

Let
\Xi
be the set of solutions with discrepancy inside an
\epsilon/n
window around
K
, namely

\Xi\coloneqq\Xi_{K,\epsilon}\coloneqq\left(Z_{K-\epsilon/n}\right)^{c}\cap Z_{K}\,.

We will show
\Xi
is empty with positive probability by Markov’s inequality. Let
T_{i}(x)
denote the event that row
i
is
tight
, namely

T_{i}\coloneqq\{|(Ax)_{i}|\in I_{K,\epsilon}\},\quad I_{K,\epsilon}\coloneqq[K-\epsilon/n,~K]\,.

Correspondingly, define the number of tight rows
T
by

T(x)\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{\alpha n}\mathbbm{1}(T_{i}(x))\,.

Let
x
be an arbitrary vector in
\{\pm 1\}^{n}
. Conditioning on
T
and using the independence of the rows of
A
, we have for some sufficiently large
C_{0}\coloneqq C_{0}(\alpha)>0
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[|\Xi|\right]

\displaystyle=2^{n}\sum_{t=0}^{\alpha n}\mathbb{P}\left[x\in\Xi|T(x)=t\right]\mathbb{P}\left[T(x)=t\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2^{n}\sum_{t=1}^{\alpha n}\binom{{\alpha n}}{t}\mathbb{P}\left[(Ax)_{1}\in I_{K,\epsilon}\right]^{t}\mathbb{P}\left[\left|(Ax)_{1}\right|<K\right]^{{\alpha n}-t}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{n}\sum_{t=1}^{\alpha n}\binom{{\alpha n}}{t}\left(\frac{C_{0}\epsilon}{n}p_{K}\right)^{t}p_{K}^{{\alpha n}-t}\,.

where, as usual,
p_{K}=\mathbb{P}\left[|Z|\leq K\right]
for
Z
a standard Gaussian. Note that we are able to let
C_{0}
only depend on
\alpha
because
K
is bounded above by a constant, namely
2K_{c}
. Thus, by the binomial theorem, for some other sufficiently large
C\coloneqq C(\alpha)>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[|\Xi|\right]

\displaystyle\leq(2^{n}p_{K}^{\alpha n})\sum_{t=1}^{\alpha n}\binom{{\alpha n}}{t}\left(\frac{C_{0}\epsilon}{n}\right)^{t}1^{{\alpha n}-t}=\mathbb{E}\left[|Z_{K}|\right]\left(\left(1+\frac{C_{0}\epsilon}{n}\right)^{\alpha n}-1\right)\leq\mathbb{E}\left[|Z_{K}|\right]C\epsilon\,.

Note that
|\Xi|
is a non-negative integer, so the events
\left\{|\Xi|\leq 1/2\right\}
and
\left\{|\Xi|=0\right\}
are almost surely equal. Thus, Markov’s inequality applied to
|\Xi|
immediately yields the theorem.
∎
