Maxwell operator in a cylinder. Separation of variables
N. D. Filonov

Abstract
The Maxwell operator in a 3D cylinder is considered. The coefficients are assumed to be scalar functions depending on the longitudinal variable only. Such operator is represented as a sum of countable set of matrix differential operators of first order acting in L 2 ​ ( ℝ ) L_{2}(\mathbb{R}) . Based on this representation we give a detailed description of the structure of the spectrum of the Maxwell operator in two particular cases: 1) in the case of coefficients stabilizing at infinity; and 2) in the case of periodic coefficients.



Introduction

Let
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a bounded connected domain with Lipschitz boundary.
Let
\Pi=U\times\mathbb{R}
be a three-dimensional cylinder.
We study the self-adjoint Maxwell operator
{\cal M}
in the cylinder
\Pi

under the boundary conditions of perfect conductivity.
It is a matrix differential operator of first order with coefficients
\varepsilon(x)
,
\mu(x)
,
describing the dielectric and the magnetic permeabilities of the medium filling the cylinder.
The exact definition will be given below, see Definition
1.3
.
In this paper we assume that
\varepsilon
and
\mu
are scalar functions
(the medium in the cylinder is isotropic), bounded and positive definite,

0<\varepsilon_{0}\leqslant\varepsilon(x)\leqslant\varepsilon_{1},\qquad 0<\mu_{0}\leqslant\mu(x)\leqslant\mu_{1},

(0.1)

and that they depend on the longitudinal variable only,

\varepsilon(x)=\varepsilon(x_{3}),\qquad\mu(x)=\mu(x_{3}).

(0.2)

In this situation, we show that the Maxwell operator is an orthogonal sum of countable set
of matrix one-dimensional differential operators of first order in
L_{2}(\mathbb{R})
(see Theorem
4.1
below).
If the coefficients
\varepsilon
,
\mu
possess the second Sobolev derivatives,

\varepsilon,\mu\in W_{1,loc}^{2}(\mathbb{R}),\qquad\text{and moreover},\quad\sup_{s\in\mathbb{R}}\int_{s}^{s+1}\left(|\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}(t)|+|\mu^{\prime\prime}(t)|\right)dt<\infty,

(0.3)

then the square
{\cal M}^{2}
of the Maxwell operator is unitarily equivalent to the orthogonal sum
of countable set of Schrödinger operators

-d^{2}/dy^{2}+V_{k}(y)
on the real line (Theorem
6.6
below).
The Maxwell operator itself is unitarily equivalent to the orthogonal sum
of square roots of Schrödinger operators on the real line, taken with signs plus and minus
(Corollary
6.7
).
Note, that the potentials
V_{k}
can be explicitely calculated in terms of the coefficients
\varepsilon
,
\mu
,
and the eigenvalues
\lambda_{k}
,
\varkappa_{l}
of the Laplace operator of the Dirichlet or Neumann boundary problems
in the cross-section
U
(see (
6.19
), (
6.20
), (
6.21
) below).

In the precedent work
[
6
]
we have done partial separation of variables,
and we have shown that the square of the Maxwell operator
is unitarily equivalent to the orthogonal sum of four scalar elliptic operators of second order in the cylinder
\Pi
.
Now, we succeeded in complete separating of variables, and we reduce the problem
to the set of one-dimensional Shrödinger operators
that are very well studied.
Note also that in
[
6
]
we used the boundedness of the inverse operator
{\cal M}^{-1}

which needs the simple connectivity of the cross-section
U
.
In the present paper we do not need such condition.

This reduction to the Shrödinger operators allows us to describe the spectrum of the Maxwell operator
for different behaviour of the coefficients.
We consider two cases as natural examples.
In the first case, the coefficient stabilize at infinity,

\varepsilon(x_{3})\to\varepsilon_{*},\qquad\mu(x_{3})\to\mu_{*}\qquad\text{as}\quad x_{3}\to\pm\infty,

see Theorem
1.7
below.
In the second case, the coefficients are periodic along the axis of the cylinder,
see Theorem
1.8
below.

1
Formulation of the results

1.1
Functional spaces

Let
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a bounded connected domain,
\partial U\in\operatorname{Lip}
,
\Pi=U\times\mathbb{R}
.
We treat the boundary conditions in the definition of the Maxwell operator in the sense of integral identities.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
u\in L_{2}(\Pi,\mathbb{C}^{3})
.

\nu
) If
\operatorname{div}u\in L_{2}(\Pi)
then

\left.u_{\nu}\right|_{\partial\Pi}=0\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad\int_{\Pi}\langle u,\nabla\omega\rangle dx=-\int_{\Pi}\operatorname{div}u\,\overline{\omega}dx\quad\forall\ \omega\in W_{2}^{1}(\Pi).

\tau
) If
\operatorname{rot}u\in L_{2}(\Pi,\mathbb{C}^{3})
then

\left.u_{\tau}\right|_{\partial\Pi}=0\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad\int_{\Pi}\langle u,\operatorname{rot}z\rangle dx=\int_{\Pi}\langle\operatorname{rot}u,z\rangle dx\quad\forall\ z\in L_{2}(\Pi,\mathbb{C}^{3}):\operatorname{rot}z\in L_{2}(\Pi,\mathbb{C}^{3}).

Here
\langle\,.\,,\,.\,\rangle
is a standard scalar product in
\mathbb{C}^{3}
.

We need also several functional spaces.
Introduce the Hilbert space

H(\operatorname{rot})=\left\{u\in L_{2}(\Pi,\mathbb{C}^{3}):\operatorname{rot}u\in L_{2}(\Pi,\mathbb{C}^{3})\right\},

endowed with the norm

\|u\|_{H(\operatorname{rot})}^{2}=\int_{\Pi}\left(|\operatorname{rot}u|^{2}+|u|^{2}\right)dx,

and its subspace

H(\operatorname{rot},\tau)=\left\{u\in H(\operatorname{rot}):\left.u_{\tau}\right|_{\partial\Pi}=0\right\}.

The following fact is a simple corollary of the definitions.

Lemma 1.2
.

The set
C_{0}^{\infty}(\Pi,\mathbb{C}^{3})
is dense in
H(\operatorname{rot},\tau)
.

Proof.

Consider the operator
\operatorname{rot}_{0}
defined on
\operatorname{Dom}\operatorname{rot}_{0}=C_{0}^{\infty}(\Pi,\mathbb{C}^{3})
.
Its adjoint operator is the operator
(\operatorname{rot}_{0})^{*}=\operatorname{rot}
defined on

\operatorname{Dom}\operatorname{rot}=H(\operatorname{rot})
.
Therefore, the closure of the operator
\operatorname{rot}_{0}
is the operator
\overline{\operatorname{rot}_{0}}=(\operatorname{rot})^{*}

which acts also as the differential operation
\operatorname{rot}
on the domain
H(\operatorname{rot},\tau)

(see Definition
1.1
).
Therefore, the set
C_{0}^{\infty}(\Pi,\mathbb{C}^{3})
is dense in
H(\operatorname{rot},\tau)
with respect to the graph norm.

Now, let
\varepsilon
,
\mu
satisfy (
0.1
).
Introduce the subspaces of divergence-free fucntions

J(\varepsilon)=\{u\in L_{2}(\Pi,\mathbb{C}^{3},\varepsilon):\operatorname{div}(\varepsilon u)=0\},

J(\nu,\mu)=\{v\in L_{2}(\Pi,\mathbb{C}^{3},\mu):\operatorname{div}(\mu v)=0,\left.(\mu v)_{\nu}\right|_{\partial\Pi}=0\}.

Next, we introduce the spaces

\Phi(\tau,\varepsilon)=H(\operatorname{rot},\tau)\cap J(\varepsilon),\qquad\Phi(\nu,\mu)=H(\operatorname{rot})\cap J(\nu,\mu).

Definition 1.3
.

The Maxwell operator is defined in the Hilbert space
{\cal J}:=J(\varepsilon)\oplus J(\nu,\mu)
via the formula

{\cal M}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}E\\
H\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}i\varepsilon^{-1}\operatorname{rot}H\\
-i\mu^{-1}\operatorname{rot}E\end{array}\right)

on the domain

\operatorname{Dom}{\cal M}=\Phi(\tau,\varepsilon)\oplus\Phi(\nu,\mu).

It is easy to see that the Maxwell operator is self-adjoint,
{\cal M}={\cal M}^{*}
,
see for example
[
2
]
.
It has a block structure

{\cal M}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&R^{*}\\
R&0\end{array}\right).

Here the operators

R=-i\mu^{-1}\operatorname{rot},\qquad R^{*}=i\varepsilon^{-1}\operatorname{rot}

defined on

\operatorname{Dom}R=\Phi(\tau,\varepsilon),\qquad\operatorname{Dom}R^{*}=\Phi(\nu,\mu),

are mutually adjoint.
This structure yields that the operators
{\cal M}
and
-{\cal M}
are unitarily equivalent,
and the spectrum of the operator
{\cal M}
is symmetric with respect to zero
(see Lemma
4.2
below).

1.2
Laplace operator in the cross-section

Denote by
-\Delta_{\cal D}
(resp.
-\Delta_{\cal N}
) the Laplace operator in
U
with Dirichlet (resp. Neumann) boundary condition.
These are self-adjoint operators corresponding to the quadratic forms

\int_{U}|\nabla\varphi|^{2}dx_{1}dx_{2},\qquad\varphi\in\mathring{W}_{2}^{1}(U)

and

\int_{U}|\nabla\psi|^{2}dx_{1}dx_{2},\qquad\psi\in W_{2}^{1}(U)

respectively.
Cross-section
U
is a bounded domain with Lipschitz boundary,
so the spectra of both problems are discrete.
Denote by
\lambda_{k}
,
\varphi_{k}
(resp.
\varkappa_{l}
,
\psi_{l}
) the eigenvalues and the eigenfunctions of the Laplace operator
with Dirichlet (resp. Neumann) boundary condition,

-\Delta_{\cal D}\varphi_{k}=\lambda_{k}\varphi_{k},\qquad-\Delta_{\cal N}\psi_{l}=\varkappa_{l}\psi_{l},

0<\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}\leqslant\dots,\quad\lambda_{k}\to+\infty,\qquad 0=\varkappa_{1}<\varkappa_{2}\leqslant\dots,\quad\varkappa_{l}\to+\infty,

\psi_{1}\equiv\operatorname{const}
.
We choose eigenfunctions to be orthonormal,

\|\varphi_{k}\|_{L_{2}(U)}^{2}=1,\quad\|\nabla\varphi_{k}\|_{L_{2}(U)}^{2}=\lambda_{k},\qquad\|\psi_{l}\|_{L_{2}(U)}^{2}=1,\quad\|\nabla\psi_{l}\|_{L_{2}(U)}^{2}=\varkappa_{l}.

(1.1)

If the boundary
\partial U
is smooth then the eigenfunctions are classical solutions to the corresponding problems

\begin{cases}-\Delta\varphi_{k}=\lambda_{k}\varphi_{k},\\
\left.\varphi_{k}\right|_{\partial U}=0,\end{cases},\qquad\begin{cases}-\Delta\psi_{l}=\varkappa_{l}\psi_{l},\\
\left.\frac{\partial\psi_{l}}{\partial\nu}\right|_{\partial U}=0.\end{cases}

1.3
Results

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a bounded connected domain,
\partial U\in\operatorname{Lip}
,
\Pi=U\times\mathbb{R}
.
Let the coefficients
\varepsilon
,
\mu
be scalar real measurable functions satisfying (
0.1
) and (
0.2
).
Then the square
{\cal M}^{2}
of the Maxwell operator is unitarily equivalent to the orthogonal sum

\left(\bigoplus_{k=1}^{\infty}A^{el}_{k}\right)\bigoplus\left(\bigoplus_{k=1}^{\infty}A^{el}_{k}\right)\bigoplus\left(\bigoplus_{l=2}^{\infty}A^{m}_{l}\right)\bigoplus\left(\bigoplus_{l=2}^{\infty}A^{m}_{l}\right)\bigoplus\left(\bigoplus_{j=1}^{2N-2}A^{0}\right),

(1.2)

and

\sigma\left({\cal M}^{2}\right)=\left(\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}\sigma\left(A^{el}_{k}\right)\right)\bigcup\left(\bigcup_{l=2}^{\infty}\sigma\left(A^{m}_{l}\right)\right)\bigcup\sigma\left(A^{0}\right).

(1.3)

Here

A^{el}_{k}=-\frac{1}{\mu}\frac{d}{dz}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\frac{d}{dz}\right)+\frac{\lambda_{k}}{\varepsilon\mu},\qquad\operatorname{Dom}A^{el}_{k}=\left\{p\in W_{2}^{1}(\mathbb{R}):(\varepsilon^{-1}p^{\prime})^{\prime}\in L_{2}(\mathbb{R})\right\}

and

A^{0}=-\frac{1}{\mu}\frac{d}{dz}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\frac{d}{dz}\right),\qquad\operatorname{Dom}A^{0}=\left\{p\in W_{2}^{1}(\mathbb{R}):(\varepsilon^{-1}p^{\prime})^{\prime}\in L_{2}(\mathbb{R})\right\}

are self-adjoint operators in the space
L_{2}(\mathbb{R},\mu dz)
;

A^{m}_{l}=-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\frac{d}{dz}\left(\frac{1}{\mu}\frac{d}{dz}\right)+\frac{\varkappa_{l}}{\varepsilon\mu},\qquad\operatorname{Dom}A^{m}_{l}=\left\{q\in W_{2}^{1}(\mathbb{R}):(\mu^{-1}q^{\prime})^{\prime}\in L_{2}(\mathbb{R})\right\}

are self-adjoint operators in the space
L_{2}(\mathbb{R},\varepsilon dz)
;

N
is the number of connected components of the boundary
\partial U
;
if the cross-section
U
is simply connected, the last summand in (
1.2
) is absent.

Remark 1.5
.

The operator
A^{0}
coincides with the operator
A^{el}_{k}
after the substitution
\lambda_{k}\mapsto 0
.
One can take the operator
A^{m}_{l}
with substitution
\varkappa_{l}\mapsto 0
instead of it.
These operators are unitarily equivalent (see Remark
4.8
below).

Corollary 1.6
.

If the cross-section
U
is simply connected then there is a gap centered at zero in the spectrum of the Maxwell operator,

\sigma({\cal M})\subset\left(-\infty,-\sqrt{\frac{\varkappa_{2}}{\|\varepsilon\mu\|_{L_{\infty}}}}\right]\cup\left[\sqrt{\frac{\varkappa_{2}}{\|\varepsilon\mu\|_{L_{\infty}}}},+\infty\right).

Theorem 1.7
.

Let the conditions of Theorem
1.4
be fulfilled.
Assume moreover that there are two constants
\varepsilon_{*}
and
\mu_{*}
such that

\varepsilon-\varepsilon_{*}\in W_{1}^{2}(\mathbb{R}),\qquad\mu-\mu_{*}\in W_{1}^{2}(\mathbb{R}).

Then

1) there are no singular continuous component in the spectrum of the Maxwell oeprator,

\sigma_{sc}({\cal M})=\emptyset.

2) If the cross-section
U
is multiply connected then the absolute continuous spectrum fills the whole real line,

\sigma_{ac}({\cal M})=\mathbb{R}.

If the cross-section
U
is simply connected then

\sigma_{ac}({\cal M})=\left(-\infty,-\sqrt{\frac{\varkappa_{2}}{\varepsilon_{*}\mu_{*}}}\right]\cup\left[\sqrt{\frac{\varkappa_{2}}{\varepsilon_{*}\mu_{*}}},+\infty\right).

3) If

\varepsilon(z)\mu(z)\leqslant\varepsilon_{*}\mu_{*}\qquad\forall\ z\in\mathbb{R},

then there are no eigenvalues in the spectrum,

\sigma_{p}({\cal M})=\emptyset
.
If

\exists\ z_{0}\in\mathbb{R}:\quad\varepsilon(z_{0})\mu(z_{0})>\varepsilon_{*}\mu_{*},

then the operator
{\cal M}
has infinite (countable) set of eigenvalues.
In particular, there are infinitely many eigenvalues inside the continuous spectrum.

Theorem 1.8
.

Let the conditions of Theorem
1.4
be fulfilled.
Assume moreover that
\varepsilon,\mu\in W_{1,loc}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
and that they are periodic,

\varepsilon(z+a)=\varepsilon(z),\qquad\mu(z+a)=\mu(z).

(1.4)

Then the spectrum of the Maxwell operator is absolutely continuous,

\sigma({\cal M})=\sigma_{ac}({\cal M}),\qquad\sigma_{sc}({\cal M})=\emptyset,\qquad\sigma_{p}({\cal M})=\emptyset,

and the number of gaps in the spectrum is finite.

1.4
Comments

1.
It is interesting to compare the problem under consideration with the inverse situation.
The Maxwell operator in a cylinder with matrix-valued coefficients that depend
on the cross-sectional variables only was considered in
[
5
]
.
The spectrum of such operator is absolutely continuous.
If the cross-section is multiply connected then the spectrum fills the whole real line;
if the cross-section is simply connected then the spectrum has exactly one gap centered at zero.
Note that the dependence of the spectrum of the Maxwell operator in a cylinder on the topology
of the cross-section is well known in the case of smooth boundary and trivial coefficients

\varepsilon(x)\equiv\mu(x)\equiv 1\!\!\!\!1
(vacuum in the cylinder),
see
[
8
, §91]
.
In
[
5
]
this statement is generalised to the case of Lipschitz boundary
and non-trivial coefficients.

2.
Under the conditions of Theorem
1.7
we do not know
if the eigenvalues outside the continuous spectrum can occur.

3.
The common wisdom assumes that if a periodic problem admits a separation of variables
then the Bethe-Sommerfeld conjecture (the number of gaps in the spectrum is finite)
holds true.
Theorem
1.8
supports this point.

.

2
Separation of variables

2.1
Decompositions of the space
L_{2}(U,\mathbb{C}^{2})

If
U
is multiply connected we denote by
\Gamma_{j}
the connected components of its boundary,

\partial U=\cup_{j=1}^{N}\Gamma_{j}
.
Introduce the space of harmonic functions in
U
that are constant on each component of the boundary,

{\cal L}=\left\{\omega\in W_{2}^{1}(U):\Delta\omega=0,\left.\omega\right|_{\Gamma_{j}}=c_{j},j=1,\dots,N\right\}.

Clearly,
\dim{\cal L}=N
.
Put

{\cal H}^{0}=\left\{\nabla\omega:\omega\in{\cal L}\right\}.

The identical constant does not make a contribution here, so
\dim{\cal H}^{0}=N-1
.
Let us fix an orthonormal basis
\{\nabla\omega_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N-1}
in
{\cal H}^{0}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

If
\varphi\in\mathring{W}_{2}^{1}(U)
,
\eta\in W_{2}^{1}(U)
, then

\int_{U}\left(\partial_{1}\varphi\partial_{2}\eta-\partial_{2}\varphi\partial_{1}\eta\right)dx_{1}dx_{2}=0.

(2.1)

If
\omega\in{\cal L}
,
\eta\in W_{2}^{1}(U)
, then

\int_{U}\left(\partial_{1}\omega\partial_{2}\eta-\partial_{2}\omega\partial_{1}\eta\right)dx_{1}dx_{2}=0.

(2.2)

Proof.

Clearly, (
2.1
) holds for
\eta\in W_{2}^{1}(U)
and
\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(U)
.
By continuity it is also true for
\varphi\in\mathring{W}_{2}^{1}(U)
.

Fix a function
\omega\in{\cal L}
.
Represent it as a sum
\omega=\tilde{\omega}+\hat{\omega}
,
where
\tilde{\omega}\in C^{\infty}(\overline{U})

and
\tilde{\omega}
is constant in a neighbourhood of each component
\Gamma_{j}
of the boundary,

j=1,\dots,N
;
and
\hat{\omega}\in\mathring{W}_{2}^{1}(U)
.
Then

\int_{U}\left(\partial_{1}\hat{\omega}\partial_{2}\eta-\partial_{2}\hat{\omega}\partial_{1}\eta\right)dx_{1}dx_{2}=0

by (
2.1
).
Furthermore, denote by
\nu
the unit outward normal vector to the boundary.
Then

\int_{U}\left(\partial_{1}\tilde{\omega}\partial_{2}\eta-\partial_{2}\tilde{\omega}\partial_{1}\eta\right)dx_{1}dx_{2}=\int_{\partial U}\left(\partial_{1}\tilde{\omega}\,\nu_{2}-\partial_{2}\tilde{\omega}\,\nu_{1}\right)\eta\,dx_{1}dx_{2}=0,

due to the identity
\nabla\tilde{\omega}\equiv 0
in the neighbourhood of
\partial U
.
Now (
2.2
) follows.

Lemma 2.2
.

The set of vector-functions

\left\{\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\partial_{1}\varphi_{k}\\
\partial_{2}\varphi_{k}\end{array}\right)\right\}_{k=1}^{\infty},\qquad\left\{\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\partial_{2}\psi_{l}\\
-\partial_{1}\psi_{l}\end{array}\right)\right\}_{l=2}^{\infty},\qquad\left\{\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\partial_{1}\omega_{j}\\
\partial_{2}\omega_{j}\end{array}\right)\right\}_{j=1}^{N-1}

(2.3)

is an orthogonal basis in
L_{2}(U,\mathbb{C}^{2})
.
If the cross-section
U
is simply connected the third set is absent.

Proof.

Orthogonality.
Clearly,
\nabla\varphi_{k}\perp\nabla\varphi_{m}
and
\nabla\psi_{k}\perp\nabla\psi_{m}
in
L_{2}(U,\mathbb{C}^{2})
if
k\neq m
.
Lemma
2.1
implies that all functions
\nabla\varphi_{k}
and
\nabla\omega_{j}
are orthogonal to all functions

\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\partial_{2}\psi_{l}\\
-\partial_{1}\psi_{l}\end{array}\right)
.
Furthermore,

\int_{U}\left(\partial_{1}\varphi_{k}\partial_{1}\overline{\omega_{j}}+\partial_{2}\varphi_{k}\partial_{2}\overline{\omega_{j}}\right)dx_{1}dx_{2}=-\int_{U}\varphi_{k}\overline{\Delta\omega_{j}}dx_{1}dx_{2}=0

by definition of the space
{\cal H}^{0}
.

Completeness.
Assume that a vector-function
f\in L_{2}(U,\mathbb{C}^{2})
is orthogonal to all functions

\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\partial_{1}\varphi_{k}\\
\partial_{2}\varphi_{k}\end{array}\right)
,
k=1,2,\dots
, and

\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\partial_{2}\psi_{l}\\
-\partial_{1}\psi_{l}\end{array}\right)
,
l=2,3,\dots
.
Then

\int_{U}\left(f_{1}\partial_{2}\psi-f_{2}\partial_{1}\psi\right)dx_{1}dx_{2}=0\qquad\forall\ \psi\in W_{2}^{1}(U),

because
\{\psi_{l}\}_{l=1}^{\infty}
is a basis in
W_{2}^{1}(U)
, and
\psi_{1}\equiv\operatorname{const}
.
Put

\tilde{f}(x_{1},x_{2})=\begin{cases}f(x_{1},x_{2}),&\text{if}\ (x_{1},x_{2})\in U,\\
0,&\text{if}\ (x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus U.\end{cases}

Then

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left(\tilde{f}_{1}\partial_{2}\psi-\tilde{f}_{2}\partial_{1}\psi\right)dx_{1}dx_{2}=0\qquad\forall\ \psi\in W_{2}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2}),

and therefore, there is a function
\tilde{\omega}\in W_{2,loc}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
such that
\tilde{f}=\nabla\tilde{\omega}
.
Moreover, the function
\tilde{\omega}
is constant on the connected components of
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus U
.
This means that
f=\nabla\omega
where
\omega=\left.\tilde{\omega}\right|_{U}
, and

\omega\in W_{2}^{1}(U),\qquad\left.\omega\right|_{\Gamma_{j}}=\operatorname{const}\quad\forall\ j=1,\dots,N.

Finally, the condition
f\perp\nabla\varphi_{k}
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
implies
\operatorname{div}f=0
in
U
.
Thus,
\Delta\omega=0
,
\omega\in{\cal L}
and
f\in{\cal H}^{0}
.

By 90 degree rotation we obtain

Corollary 2.3
.

The set of vector-functions

\left\{\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\partial_{1}\psi_{l}\\
\partial_{2}\psi_{l}\end{array}\right)\right\}_{l=2}^{\infty},\qquad\left\{\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\partial_{2}\varphi_{k}\\
-\partial_{1}\varphi_{k}\end{array}\right)\right\}_{k=1}^{\infty},\qquad\left\{\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\partial_{2}\omega_{j}\\
-\partial_{1}\omega_{j}\end{array}\right)\right\}_{j=1}^{N-1}

(2.4)

is an orthogonal basis in
L_{2}(U,\mathbb{C}^{2})
.
If the cross-section
U
is simply connected the third set is absent.

Remark 2.4
.

Functions
\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\partial_{1}\omega_{j}\\
\partial_{2}\omega_{j}\end{array}\right)

solve the electrostatic problem in
U

\operatorname{curl}f=0,\qquad\operatorname{div}f=0,\qquad\left.f_{\tau}\right|_{\partial U}=0.

Functions
\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\partial_{2}\omega_{j}\\
-\partial_{1}\omega_{j}\end{array}\right)

solve magnetostatic problem in
U

\operatorname{curl}f=0,\qquad\operatorname{div}f=0,\qquad\left.f_{\nu}\right|_{\partial U}=0.

Here the boundary conditions
\left.f_{\tau,\nu}\right|_{\partial U}=0
understood
in the sence of integral identities analoguous to Definition
1.1

(see (
3.1
) below).

2.2
Key isomorphisms

Starting from this point we use the following agreement:
the indices
k
and
m
belong to
\mathbb{N}
,
index
l\geqslant 2
, index
j\in\{1,\dots,N-1\}
.

Introduce the auxiliary Hilbert spaces
{\cal H}_{el}
and
{\cal H}_{m}
.
Elements of
{\cal H}_{el}
are the sets of functions from
L_{2}(\mathbb{R})

\{a_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty},\quad\{b_{l}\}_{l=2}^{\infty},\quad\{c_{m}\}_{m=1}^{\infty},\quad\{d_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N-1}

with finite norm

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}|a_{k}(z)|^{2}+\sum_{l=2}^{\infty}\varkappa_{l}|b_{l}(z)|^{2}+\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}|c_{m}(z)|^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}|d_{j}(z)|^{2}\right)\varepsilon(z)\,dz.

Elements of
{\cal H}_{m}
are the sets of functions from

Lemma 3.1

Let

We understand the last equality in the sense analoguous to Definition

(3.1)

Then there is a sequence of functions

Proof.

Let us consider the operator

\operatorname{div}_{0}:L_{2}(U,\mathbb{C}^{2})\to L_{2}(U)
defined on

\operatorname{Dom}\operatorname{div}_{0}=C_{0}^{\infty}(U,\mathbb{C}^{2})

and acting as divergence,

.
This operator is densely defined and not closed.

Therefore, the closure of the operator

is the operator adjoint to nabla,

\overline{\operatorname{div}_{0}}=-\nabla^{*}=\operatorname{div}
.
The last operator is described just by the formula (
3.1
).
So, any function

can be approximated by the functions from

C_{0}^{\infty}(U,\mathbb{C}^{2})
in the graph norm of the operator
\operatorname{div}
.

Reduction to one-dimensional Schrödinger operator

Now, it is natural to transform the operators

6.1
Change of variables

Lemma 6.1
.

Let

\varepsilon\in W_{1,loc}^{2}(\mathbb{R})\cap L_{\infty}(\mathbb{R})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sup_{s\in\mathbb{R}}\int_{s}^{s+1}|\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}(t)|\,dt=:S<\infty.

Then
\varepsilon\in W_{\infty}^{1}(\mathbb{R})
.

Proof.

Let
s\in\mathbb{R}
.
Then

\varepsilon^{\prime}(s)=\int_{s-1}^{s}\left((t-s+1)\varepsilon^{\prime}(t)\right)^{\prime}dt=\int_{s-1}^{s}\left((t-s+1)\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}(t)+\varepsilon^{\prime}(t)\right)dt,

so

|\varepsilon^{\prime}(s)|\leqslant\int_{s-1}^{s}\left|\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}(t)\right|dt+\left|\varepsilon(s)-\varepsilon(s-1)\right|\leqslant S+2\|\varepsilon\|_{L_{\infty}},

and
\|\varepsilon^{\prime}\|_{L_{\infty}}\leqslant S+2\|\varepsilon\|_{L_{\infty}}
.

It is convenient to change the variable by the rule

z\mapsto y(z)=\int_{0}^{z}\sqrt{\varepsilon(s)\mu(s)}\,ds.

(6.1)

The function
y
strictly increases, and it is a bijection
\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
.
Introduce the functions

\tilde{\varepsilon}(y)=\varepsilon(z),\qquad\tilde{\mu}(y)=\mu(z).

(6.2)

We have

dy=\sqrt{\varepsilon(z)\mu(z)}\,dz,\qquad dz=\frac{dy}{\sqrt{\tilde{\varepsilon}(y)\tilde{\mu}(y)}}.

(6.3)

Lemma 6.2
.

Let the functions
\varepsilon
,
\mu
satisfy (

) and (
0.3
),
and the functions
\tilde{\varepsilon}
,

(6.4)

and

Inequalities (

Let us prove (

We have

\tilde{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(y)=\frac{\varepsilon^{\prime}(z)}{\sqrt{\varepsilon(z)\mu(z)}},

(6.6)

\tilde{\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}(y)=\frac{\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}(z)}{\varepsilon(z)\mu(z)}-\frac{\varepsilon^{\prime}(z)^{2}}{2\varepsilon(z)^{2}\mu(z)}-\frac{\varepsilon^{\prime}(z)\mu^{\prime}(z)}{2\varepsilon(z)\mu(z)^{2}}.

Thus,

\int_{t}^{t+1}|\tilde{\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}(y)|\,dy\leqslant C\left(\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_{0}\mu_{0}}}\right]+1\right)\left(\sup_{s\in\mathbb{R}}\int_{s}^{s+1}|\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}(z)|\,dz+\|\varepsilon^{\prime}\|_{L_{\infty}}^{2}+\|\varepsilon^{\prime}\|_{L_{\infty}}\|\mu^{\prime}\|_{L_{\infty}}\right).

8
Periodic coefficients

We will use the following well-known result (see for example
[
3

).

Theorem 8.1
.

Let
W
be a real-valued periodic function,

W\in L_{1,loc}(\mathbb{R}),\qquad W(y+b)=W(y).

Let
H=-\frac{d^{2}}{dy^{2}}+W(y)
be the self-adjoint operator in
L_{2}(\mathbb{R})

corresponding to the quadratic form

h[u]=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(|u^{\prime}(y)|^{2}+W(y)|u(y)|^{2}\right)dy,\qquad\operatorname{Dom}h=W_{2}^{1}(\mathbb{R}).

Then

1) the spectrum of the operator is absolutely continuous,

\sigma(H)=\sigma_{ac}(H),\qquad\sigma_{sc}(H)=\emptyset,\qquad\sigma_{p}(H)=\emptyset.

2) The spectrum has a band-gap structure,

\sigma(H)=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}[\alpha_{n},\beta_{n}],\qquad\beta_{n}\leqslant\alpha_{n+1}.

Here
\alpha_{n}
(resp.
\beta_{n}
) is the
n
-th eigenvalue of the operator
-\frac{d^{2}}{dy^{2}}+W(y)

in
L_{2}(0,b)
with periodic (resp. semiperiodic) boundary conditions if the number
n
is odd,
and conversely,
\alpha_{n}
(resp.
\beta_{n}
) is the
n
-th eigenvalue of the operator
-\frac{d^{2}}{dy^{2}}+W(y)

in
L_{2}(0,b)
with semiperiodic (resp. periodic) boundary conditions if the number
n
is even.
Moreover,

\displaystyle\alpha_{n}=\frac{\pi^{2}(n-1)^{2}}{b^{2}}+\frac{1}{b}\int_{0}^{b}W(y)\,dy+o(1),\quad n\to\infty,

\displaystyle\beta_{n}=\frac{\pi^{2}n^{2}}{b^{2}}+\frac{1}{b}\int_{0}^{b}W(y)\,dy+o(1),\quad n\to\infty.

Some gaps can vanish (i.e.
\beta_{n}=\alpha_{n+1}
for some
n
), but in any cases
the lengths of gaps tend to zero,
\alpha_{n+1}-\beta_{n}\to 0
.
