Derivatives and Integrals of Polynomials Associated with Integer Partitions


Abstract
Integer partitions express the different ways that a positive integer may be written as a sum of positive integers. Here we explore the analytic properties of a new polynomial f λ ​ ( x ) f_{\lambda}(x) that we call the partition polynomial for the partition λ \lambda , with the aim to learn new properties of partitions. We prove a recursive formula for the derivatives of f λ ​ ( x ) f_{\lambda}(x) involving Stirling numbers of the second kind, show that the set of integrals from 0 to 1 of a normalized version of f λ ​ ( x ) f_{\lambda}(x) is dense in [ 0 , 1 / 2 ] [0,1/2] , pose a few open questions, and formulate a conjecture relating the integral to the length of the partition. We also provide specific examples throughout to support our speculation that an in-depth analysis of partition polynomials could further strengthen our understanding of partitions.

A

\lambda

of positive integers, called the

of the partition. By convention, the only partition of size

is the empty partition with no parts. Although partitions are classical objects and simple to define, they are also complex additive structures with elusive analytic properties and are still a significant topic of study in number theory today
[
1
,
2
,
5
]
. Hardy, Rademacher, Ramanujan, and many other well known number theorists of the twentieth century made major breakthroughs in our understanding of partitions via the generating function of

[
8
,
A000041
]

n
. These results include the famous Hardy-Ramanujan asymptotic formula which was proven analytically using the circle method
[
4
]
:

p(n)\sim\frac{1}{4n\sqrt{3}}e^{\pi\sqrt{\frac{2n}{3}}}\hskip 7.11317pt\text{as }n\rightarrow\infty;

and the Ramanujan congruences which can be proven using the theory of modular forms
[
10
,
11

p(5n+4)\equiv 0\ \mbox{\rm(mod}\ 5\mbox{\rm)},\hskip 5.69046ptp(7n+5)\equiv 0\ \mbox{\rm(mod}\ 7\mbox{\rm)},\hskip 5.69046pt\text{and}\hskip 5.69046ptp(11n+6)\equiv 0\ \mbox{\rm(mod}\ 11\mbox{\rm)}

for all integers
n\geq 0
. Rademacher’s main contribution was an exact formula for

as an absolutely convergent infinite series involving Kloosterman sums and the
I
-Bessel function
[
9
]
. Most of the discoveries that have been made about partitions so far have resulted from studying

Recent work by the first author, Just, and Schneider
[
3
]
takes a different approach to studying partitions. They define a map from the set of all partitions to the set of natural numbers called the
supernorm
of a partition, previously called the Heinz number:
[
8
,
A305078
]
, which sheds more light on properties of partitions by relating them to the multiplicative structure of prime factorizations of integers. For notational convenience, their work utilizes frequency notation
\lambda=\langle 1^{m_{1}},2^{m_{2}},\dots,k^{m_{k}}\rangle
, where
m_{i}
is the multiplicity of the part

in the partition
\lambda
and
k
is the largest part of
\lambda
, as opposed to the traditional additive notation. The
supernorm
of a partition
\lambda=\langle 1^{m_{1}},2^{m_{2}},\dots,k^{m_{k}}\rangle
is defined by

\widehat{N}(\lambda)=\prod_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}^{m_{i}},

where

denotes the

\lambda

\ell(\lambda)

, the

(the product of the parts), and the supernorm

, among other partition statistics, Just
[
6

defined a polynomial to which one can apply calculus and analysis in order to analyze properties of partitions. To define this new polynomial, let
\lambda=\langle 1^{m_{1}},2^{m_{2}},\dots,k^{m_{k}}\rangle
be a partition written in frequency notation. We define the
partition polynomial

by

f_{\lambda}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}m_{i}x^{i}.

(1)

Differentiation and integration of the partition polynomial both have the potential to reveal certain properties of partitions. The most basic observations one can make in the direction of differentiation are that
f_{\lambda}(1)=\ell(\lambda)
and

. Does

give some generalization of the size or the length of
\lambda

Theorem 1
.

Given a partition
\lambda=\langle 1^{m_{1}},2^{m_{2}},\dots,k^{m_{k}}\rangle

f_{\lambda}^{(d)}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}i^{d}m_{i}x^{i-d}-\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}{d\brace j}x^{j-d}f_{\lambda}^{(j)}(x)

(2)

for all
0\leq d\leq k
, where
{d\brace j}
is the Stirling number of the second kind.

The

denotes the partition polynomial itself. Note that the second sum is empty when

for all

[

and
[

A008277
]

In addition to studying derivatives, one may also attempt to understand partitions further by studying integrals. Before taking integrals of partition polynomials, we first normalize them and restrict their domain as follows in order to more easily compare partitions. The
normalized partition polynomial

\hat{f}_{\lambda}:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
of the partition
\lambda=\langle 1^{m_{1}},2^{m_{2}},\dots,k^{m_{k}}\rangle
is defined by

\hat{f}_{\lambda}(x)=\frac{1}{\ell(\lambda)}\sum_{i=1}^{k}m_{i}x^{i}.

(3)

Some normalized partition polynomials.

It is straightforward to see that the integral of a normalized partition polynomial must have a value between 0 and 1/2 as illustrated in Figure

Theorem 2

The integrals of normalized partition polynomials are dense in

We provide more basic results on integrals of normalized partition polynomials in Section

Question 3
.

Is there a combinatorial interpretation of
{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}}\hat{f}_{\lambda}(x)\,dx
?

Question

which rely on asymptotic results. It would be desirable to prove a result for all partition sizes

that does not rely on asymptotics.

One could also ask whether certain types of partitions of fixed size

Conjecture 4

Let

, and let

denote the average of all integrals of normalized partition polynomials over all partitions of size

\ell

\mathrm{Avg}(n,1)\leq\mathrm{Avg}(n,2)\leq\cdots\leq\mathrm{Avg}(n,n).

Numerical computation confirms that Conjecture

for all

\mathrm{Avg}(n,2)\leq\mathrm{Avg}(n,3)

Theorem 5

Let

. Then
\mathrm{Avg}(n,1)\leq\mathrm{Avg}(n,2)
.

Note that

\ell

\ell

The proof of Theorem

Theorem 6

For sufficiently large

\mathrm{Avg}(n,2)\leq\mathrm{Avg}(n,3)

Proving Conjecture

Derivatives of partition polynomials

There are many open questions that arise when studying the derivatives of partition polynomials. It is clear that
f_{\lambda}^{(d)}(1)\geq 0
for all

, since all of the parts of partitions are positive and all of their multiplicities are nonnegative. It is also clear that evaluating the first few derivatives at

\displaystyle f_{\lambda}^{(0)}(1)

\displaystyle=\ell(\lambda),

\displaystyle f_{\lambda}^{(1)}(1)

\displaystyle=|\lambda|,

\displaystyle f_{\lambda}^{(2)}(1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}i^{2}m_{i}-{2\brace 1}|\lambda|,

\displaystyle f_{\lambda}^{(3)}(1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}i^{3}m_{i}-{3\brace 2}\sum_{i=1}^{k}i^{2}m_{i}-\left({3\brace 1}-{3\brace 2}{2\brace 1}\right)|\lambda|,

\displaystyle f_{\lambda}^{(4)}(1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}i^{4}m_{i}-{4\brace 3}\sum_{i=1}^{k}i^{3}m_{i}-\left({4\brace 2}-{4\brace 3}{3\brace 2}\right)\sum_{i=1}^{k}i^{2}m_{i}

\displaystyle\hskip 53.3489pt-\left({4\brace 1}-{4\brace 2}{2\brace 1}-{4\brace 3}{3\brace 1}+{4\brace 3}{3\brace 2}{2\brace 1}\right)|\lambda|.

It would be nice to find a combinatorial interpretation of the

Question 9

If
\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}
are any two unequal partitions, is it true that
f_{\lambda}^{(d)}(1)\neq f_{\lambda^{\prime}}^{(d)}(1)

Proposition 10

There exists a positive integer

with

Proof.

We use the pigeonhole principle to prove the proposition. Denote the number of partitions of

into

[

that

p(n,s)\sim\frac{n^{s-1}}{s((s-1)!)^{2}}

p(n,5)\sim n^{4}/2880
. It suffices to show that

is bounded by an asymptotically smaller expression. Suppose

is a partition of size
n

f_{\lambda}^{(2)}(1)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}i^{2}m_{i}-n\leq k^{2}n-n\leq n^{3}-n.

Since
(n^{3}-n)/p(n,5)\rightarrow 0
as

, there must be two unequal partitions of the same size and length 5 with equal second derivative evaluations.
∎

In fact, this argument holds for all

, so the second derivative evaluation fails to distinguish unequal partitions of the same size and length at least 5. One could also refine the question to ask how many derivatives are necessary in order to distinguish any two unequal partitions of fixed size
n
and length
s
.

Another interesting observation is that taking the derivative of the partition polynomial of one partition yields the partition polynomial of a different partition. Explicitly, if we define the partition
\lambda=\lambda^{(0)}=\langle 1^{m_{1}},2^{m_{2}},\dots,k^{m_{k}}\rangle
, then
f_{\lambda}^{(1)}(x)
is the partition polynomial of the new partition
\lambda^{(1)}

\lambda^{(1)}=\langle 1^{2m_{2}},2^{3m_{3}},\dots,(k-1)^{km_{k}}\rangle

\left(\lambda^{(d)}\right)_{0\leq d<k}
whose partition polynomials are related by differentiation:

\displaystyle\lambda^{(d)}

\displaystyle=\left\langle 1^{(d+1)!m_{d+1}/1!},2^{(d+2)!m_{d+2}/2!},\dots,(k-d)^{k!m_{k}/(k-d)!}\right\rangle\hskip 7.11317pt\text{for all }0\leq d<k.

(11)

Strictly speaking, these partitions also have parts of size zero which we exclude, and parts of negative size which occur with multiplicity zero. We have that for all
0\leq d<k
,

\ell\left(\lambda^{(d)}\right)=\sum_{i=d+1}^{k}\frac{i!}{(i-d)!}m_{i}\hskip 28.45274pt\text{and}\hskip 28.45274pt\left|\lambda^{(d)}\right|=\sum_{i=d+1}^{k}\frac{i!}{(i-d-1)!}m_{i}.

(12)

Therefore, the process of differentiation guarantees that the following relationship between the sizes and lengths of the partitions in the sequence (
11
) holds for all
1\leq d\leq k
:

\left|\lambda^{(d-1)}\right|=\ell\left(\lambda^{(d)}\right)+d!m_{d},

(13)

where
\ell\left(\lambda^{(k)}\right)=0
since
\lambda^{(k)}
is the empty partition.

Example 11
.

Let
\lambda=\langle 1^{1},2^{0},3^{3},4^{1}\rangle
. Then we have

\displaystyle\lambda^{(0)}

\displaystyle=\langle 1^{1},2^{0},3^{3},4^{1}\rangle,

\displaystyle\lambda^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\langle 1^{0},2^{9},3^{4}\rangle,

\displaystyle\lambda^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\langle 1^{18},2^{12}\rangle,

\displaystyle\lambda^{(3)}

\displaystyle=\langle 1^{24}\rangle.

We also see that
\ell\left(\lambda^{(0)}\right)=5
,

\displaystyle\left|\lambda^{(0)}\right|=\ell\left(\lambda^{(1)}\right)+1!m_{1}

\displaystyle=14,

\displaystyle\left|\lambda^{(1)}\right|=\ell\left(\lambda^{(2)}\right)+2!m_{2}

\displaystyle\left|\lambda^{(2)}\right|=\ell\left(\lambda^{(3)}\right)+3!m_{3}

4
Integrals of normalized partition polynomials

As Figure
1
suggests, there are a few properties that all normalized partition polynomials have in common. Namely, they all start at the point
(0,0)
, end at the point
(1,1)
, and are bounded above by the line
y=x
. In the following proposition, recall that normalized partition polynomials are defined as functions from
[0,1]
to
\mathbb{R}
.

Proposition 12
.

Let
\hat{f}_{\lambda}(x)
be a normalized partition polynomial. Then following properties hold.

1.

\hat{f}_{\lambda}(0)=0
and
f_{\lambda}(1)=1
.

2.

\hat{f}_{\lambda}(x)\leq x
.

Proof.

We omit the proof of part 1, and we prove part 2 here. Since
0\leq x\leq 1
, we have that
x^{i}\leq x
for all
i\geq 1
. Then we see that

\hat{f}_{\lambda}(x)=\frac{1}{\ell(\lambda)}\sum_{i=1}^{k}m_{i}x^{i}\leq\frac{1}{\ell(\lambda)}\sum_{i=1}^{k}m_{i}x.

The observation that
\sum_{i=1}^{k}m_{i}=\ell(\lambda)
completes the proof.
∎

Additionally, we can characterize completely the cases when
\hat{f}_{\lambda}
has more than two fixed points.

Proposition 13
.

Let
\hat{f}_{\lambda}
be a normalized partition polynomial. Then
\hat{f}_{\lambda}(x)=x
if and only if
m_{1}=\ell(\lambda)
; that is,
\lambda
only contains parts of size
1
.

Proof.

Suppose
m_{1}=\ell(\lambda)
. Then
\hat{f}_{\lambda}(x)=\frac{1}{\ell(\lambda)}\cdot\ell(\lambda)x=x
. Now suppose
m_{j}>0
for some
j>1
. Then, using the fact that
x^{j}<x
when
0<x<1
, we have that

\hat{f}_{\lambda}(x)=\frac{1}{\ell(\lambda)}\sum_{i=1}^{k}m_{i}x^{i}<\frac{1}{\ell(\lambda)}\sum_{i=1}^{k}m_{i}x=x.

This completes the proof.
∎

4.1
Integration basics

In this subsection, we show some basic integration results for normalized partition polynomials. We first show that integration on the interval
[0,1]
may be viewed as a finite sum of “harmonic-like” numbers.

Theorem 14
.

Let
\hat{f}_{\lambda}
be a normalized partition polynomial. Then we have that

\int_{0}^{1}\hat{f}_{\lambda}(x)\,dx=\frac{1}{\ell(\lambda)}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{m_{i}}{i+1}.

Proof.

We integrate directly to obtain

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\hat{f}_{\lambda}(x)\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{\ell(\lambda)}\sum_{i=1}^{k}m_{i}x^{i}\,dx=\frac{1}{\ell(\lambda)}\sum_{i=1}^{k}m_{i}\cdot\frac{x^{i+1}}{i+1}\bigg|_{0}^{1}=\frac{1}{\ell(\lambda)}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{m_{i}}{i+1}.

∎

We now obtain an elementary bound for integrals of normalized partition polynomials.

Proposition 15
.

Let
\hat{f}_{\lambda}
be a normalized partition polynomial. Then

0<\int_{0}^{1}\hat{f}_{\lambda}(x)\,dx\leq\frac{1}{2}.

Proposition
15
follows directly from Proposition
12
.

Proposition
13
and the discussion afterwards show that the right inequality in Proposition
15
is strict if a partition contains parts of size greater than
1
. Despite this, one can find a sequence of normalized partition polynomials for partitions with parts greater than
1
whose integrals approach
1/2
. We discuss this further in Section
5
.

To make the distinction between partitions with parts greater than
1
and those which only have parts of size
1
, we call the former
non-trivial
partitions.

4.2
Operations on partitions

In this subsection, unless otherwise stated,
\lambda
and
\gamma
are partitions whose multiplicities are given by the sequence
(a_{i})
and
(b_{i})
respectively. We denote the largest part of
\lambda
by
k_{\lambda}
and the largest part of
\gamma
by
k_{\gamma}
. We define the
sum

\lambda\oplus\gamma
as the partition with multiplicities
a_{i}+b_{i}
and largest part
\max\{k_{\lambda},k_{\gamma}\}
. In other words, we obtain the sum
\lambda\oplus\gamma
by combining all of the parts of
\lambda
and
\gamma
into one combined partition. This operation
\oplus
on partitions has been previously defined
[
1
]
, and Schneider more recently named this operation the
product
of two partitions as part of his multiplicative partition theory
[
12
, Def. 1.2.2]
.

The normalized partition polynomial of
\lambda\oplus\gamma
is given by

\hat{f}_{\lambda\oplus\gamma}(x)=\frac{1}{\ell(\lambda)+\ell(\gamma)}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k_{\lambda}}a_{i}x^{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{k_{\gamma}}b_{i}x^{i}\right).

The following proposition shows what happens when we integrate the normalized partition polynomial of the sum of two partitions.

Proposition 16
.

We have that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\hat{f}_{\lambda\oplus\gamma}(x)\,dx=\frac{\ell(\lambda)}{\ell(\lambda)+\ell(\gamma)}\int_{0}^{1}\hat{f}_{\lambda}(x)\,dx+\frac{\ell(\gamma)}{\ell(\lambda)+\ell(\gamma)}\int_{0}^{1}\hat{f}_{\gamma}(x)\,dx.

Proof.

We proceed by direct computation.

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\hat{f}_{\lambda\oplus\gamma}(x)\,dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\ell(\lambda)+\ell(\gamma)}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k_{1}}\frac{a_{i}}{i+1}+\sum_{i=1}^{k_{2}}\frac{b_{i}}{i+1}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\ell(\lambda)+\ell(\gamma)}\left(\ell(\lambda)\frac{1}{\ell(\lambda)}\sum_{i=1}^{k_{1}}\frac{a_{i}}{i+1}+\ell(\gamma)\frac{1}{\ell(\gamma)}\sum_{i=1}^{k_{2}}\frac{b_{i}}{i+1}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\ell(\lambda)+\ell(\gamma)}\left(\ell(\lambda)\int_{0}^{1}\hat{f}_{\lambda}(x)\,dx+\ell(\gamma)\int_{0}^{1}\hat{f}_{\gamma}(x)\,dx\right).

The result follows.
∎

A few important corollaries result from this proposition.

Corollary 17
.

We have that

\int_{0}^{1}\hat{f}_{\lambda\oplus\lambda}(x)\,dx=\int_{0}^{1}\hat{f}_{\lambda}(x)\,dx.

Proof.

By Proposition
16
, we have that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\hat{f}_{\lambda\oplus\lambda}(x)\,dx

\displaystyle=\frac{\ell(\lambda)}{\ell(\lambda)+\ell(\lambda)}\int_{0}^{1}\hat{f}_{\lambda}(x)\,dx+\frac{\ell(\lambda)}{\ell(\lambda)+\ell(\lambda)}\int_{0}^{1}\hat{f}_{\lambda}(x)\,dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\hat{f}_{\lambda}(x)\,dx+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\hat{f}_{\lambda}(x)\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\hat{f}_{\lambda}(x)\,dx.

∎

Corollary 18
.

The sum of two partitions of the same length has integral equal to the average of the individual integrals. Explicitly, if
\ell(\lambda)=\ell(\gamma)
, then we have that

\int_{0}^{1}\hat{f}_{\lambda\oplus\gamma}(x)\,dx=\frac{1}{2}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\hat{f}_{\lambda}(x)\,dx+\int_{0}^{1}\hat{f}_{\gamma}(x)\,dx\right).

The proof of Corollary
18
is similar to the proof of Corollary
17
. These results will be used to prove Theorem
2
in Section
5
through a binary search type argument.

5
Proof of Theorem
2

In order to prove Theorem
2
, it suffices to fix an arbitrary
c\in[0,1/2]
and to show that there exists a sequence of partitions
(\delta_{s})
such that

\lim_{s\to\infty}\int_{0}^{1}\hat{f}_{\delta_{s}}(x)\,dx=c.

We first provide constructive proofs of the
c=0
and
c=1/2
cases of Theorem
2
.

Lemma 19
.

There exists a sequence of non-trivial partitions
(\alpha_{s})
such that

\lim\limits_{s\to\infty}\int_{0}^{1}\hat{f}_{\alpha_{s}}(x)\,dx=0.

Proof.

Define the partition
\alpha_{s}
by
\langle 1^{1},2^{0},3^{0},\dots,s^{s-1}\rangle
. Then we observe that
\hat{f}_{\alpha_{s}}(x)=\frac{1}{s}(x+(s-1)x^{s})
, and

\lim\limits_{s\to\infty}\int_{0}^{1}\hat{f}_{\alpha_{s}}(x)\,dx=\lim\limits_{s\to\infty}\frac{1}{s}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{s-1}{s+1}\right)=0.

∎

A similar result holds for non-trivial partitions whose integral approaches
1/2
.

Lemma 20
.

There exists a sequence of non-trivial partitions
(\beta_{s})
such that

\lim\limits_{s\to\infty}\int_{0}^{1}\hat{f}_{\beta_{s}}(x)\,dx=1/2.

Proof.

Define the partition
\beta_{s}
by
\langle 1^{s-1},2^{0},3^{0},\dots,(s-1)^{0},s^{1}\rangle
. Then we have that
\hat{f}_{\beta_{s}}(x)=\frac{1}{s}((s-1)x+x^{s})
, and

\lim\limits_{s\to\infty}\int_{0}^{1}\hat{f}_{\beta_{s}}(x)\,dx=\lim\limits_{s\to\infty}\frac{1}{s}\left(\frac{s-1}{2}+\frac{1}{s+1}\right)=1/2.

∎

The following theorem, combined with Lemmas
19
and
20
, will give Theorem
2
immediately.

Theorem 21
.

Suppose that
\alpha
and
\beta
are partitions such that

\int_{0}^{1}\hat{f}_{\alpha}(x)\,dx=a\hskip 14.22636pt\text{and}\hskip 14.22636pt\int_{0}^{1}\hat{f}_{\beta}(x)\,dx=b.

If
0<a<b<1/2
and
\ell(\alpha)=\ell(\beta)
, then for every
c\in(a,b)
, there exists a sequence
(\delta_{s})
of partitions such that

\lim\limits_{s\to\infty}\int_{0}^{1}\hat{f}_{\delta_{s}}(x)\,dx=c.

Proof.

Let
\epsilon>0
be given. We construct
(\delta_{s})
recursively by first constructing two sequences
(\beta_{s})
and
(\alpha_{s})
of partitions such that each
\beta_{s}
approximates
c
from above and each
\alpha_{s}
approximates
c
from below. Define
\beta_{1}=\beta
,
\alpha_{1}=\alpha
, and
\delta_{1}=\alpha_{1}\oplus\beta_{1}
. The sequence
(\delta_{s})
will be defined by
\delta_{s}=\alpha_{s}\oplus\beta_{s}
.

Let
r\geq 1
. If
c={\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}}\hat{f}_{\delta_{r}}(x)\,dx
, then define
\delta_{s}=\delta_{r}
. Otherwise, we have one of the following two cases.

Case 1:

c<{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}}\hat{f}_{\delta_{r}}\,dx
. Define
\beta_{r+1}=\delta_{r}
and
\alpha_{r+1}=\alpha_{r}\oplus\alpha_{r}
. Since
\ell(\beta_{r+1})=2^{r+1}\ell(\alpha)=\ell(\alpha_{r+1})
, we see that

\int_{0}^{1}\hat{f}_{\delta_{r+1}}(x)\,dx=\frac{1}{2}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\hat{f}_{\beta_{r+1}}(x)\,dx+\int_{0}^{1}\hat{f}_{\alpha_{r+1}}(x)\,dx\right).

Case 2:

c>{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}}\hat{f}_{\delta_{r}}\,dx
. Define
\beta_{r+1}=\beta_{r}\oplus\beta_{r}
and
\alpha_{r+1}=\delta_{r}
. Since
\ell(\beta_{r+1})=2^{r+1}\ell(\alpha)=\ell(\alpha_{r+1})
, we see that

\int_{0}^{1}\hat{f}_{\delta_{r+1}}(x)\,dx=\frac{1}{2}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\hat{f}_{\beta_{r+1}}(x)\,dx+\int_{0}^{1}\hat{f}_{\alpha_{r+1}}(x)\,dx\right).

Note that by definition,

\int_{0}^{1}\hat{f}_{\delta_{s}}(x)\,dx=\frac{1}{2}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\hat{f}_{\beta_{s}}(x)\,dx+\int_{0}^{1}\hat{f}_{\alpha_{s}}(x)\,dx\right).

Since the integral for
\alpha_{s}
is less than the integral for
\beta_{s}
, we obtain the following inequality:

\int_{0}^{1}\hat{f}_{\alpha_{s}}(x)\,dx<\int_{0}^{1}\hat{f}_{\delta_{s}}(x)\,dx<\int_{0}^{1}\hat{f}_{\beta_{s}}(x)\,dx.

Now let
N>\log_{2}\left(\frac{b-a}{\epsilon}\right)
. The previous inequality, along with the fact that
c
is an upper bound for the integral for
\alpha_{s}
, yields the following for all
s\geq N
:

\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{1}\hat{f}_{\delta_{s}}(x)\,dx-c\right|

\displaystyle<\int_{0}^{1}\hat{f}_{\beta_{s}}(x)\,dx-\int_{0}^{1}\hat{f}_{\alpha_{s}}(x)\,dx\leq\frac{b-a}{2^{s}}\leq\frac{b-a}{2^{N}}<\epsilon.

Therefore,
\lim\limits_{s\to\infty}{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}}\hat{f}_{\delta_{s}}(x)\,dx=c
as desired.
∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem
2
.

Proof of Theorem
2
.

The edge cases
c=0
and
c=1/2
follow from Lemma
19
and Lemma
20
, respectively. Thus, it suffices to show that the result holds for
c\in(0,1/2)
. Define the partitions

\displaystyle\alpha_{s}=\langle 1^{1},2^{0},3^{0},\dots,(s-1)^{0},s^{s-1}\rangle\hskip 14.22636pt\text{and}\hskip 14.22636pt\beta_{s}=\langle 1^{s-1},2^{0},3^{0},\dots,(s-1)^{0},s^{1}\rangle.

Then we have that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\hat{f}_{\alpha_{s}}(x)\,dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{s}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{s-1}{s+1}\right),

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\hat{f}_{\beta_{s}}(x)\,dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{s}\left(\frac{s-1}{2}+\frac{1}{s+1}\right).

Since
\ell(\alpha_{s})=\ell(\beta_{s})
for all
s\in\mathbb{N}
, Theorem
21
shows that the result holds for all
c
with

\int_{0}^{1}\hat{f}_{\alpha_{s}}(x)\,dx<c<\int_{0}^{1}\hat{f}_{\beta_{s}}(x)\,dx.

For increasing
s
, these are nested intervals. The union of all such intervals is the set
(0,1/2)
. This proves Theorem
2
.
∎

Proofs of Theorems

Proof of Theorem
5
.

Recall that one may compute
\mathrm{Avg}(n,\ell)
by evaluating the integral of the sum of all partitions of
n
with
\ell
parts. It is straightforward to enumerate all partitions of
n
into one part and all partitions of
n
into two parts. Adding all of the parts together for
\ell=1
and
\ell=2
gives the partition
\lambda_{1}=\langle 1^{0},2^{0},\dots,n^{1}\rangle
for all
n
, and the partitions

\lambda_{2}^{\text{odd}}=\langle 1^{1},2^{1},\dots,(n-1)^{1},n^{0}\rangle

if
n
is odd or

\lambda_{2}^{\text{even}}=\langle 1^{1},2^{1},\dots,((n/2)-1)^{1},(n/2)^{2},((n/2)+1)^{1},\dots,(n-1)^{1},n^{0}\rangle

if
n
is even. Let
H_{n}
denote the
n
th Harmonic number. Then the averages are

\mathrm{Avg}(n,1)=\int_{0}^{1}\hat{f}_{\lambda_{1}}(x)\,dx=\frac{1}{n+1}

for all
n
; and

\displaystyle\mathrm{Avg}(n,2)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\hat{f}_{\lambda_{2}^{\text{odd}}}(x)\,dx=\frac{1}{2\cdot\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{3}+\cdots+\frac{1}{n}\right)=\frac{1}{2\cdot\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor}(H_{n}-1)

if
n
is odd, or

\displaystyle\mathrm{Avg}(n,2)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\hat{f}_{\lambda_{2}^{\text{even}}}(x)\,dx=\frac{1}{2\cdot\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{3}+\cdots+\frac{1}{n}+\frac{1}{n/2}\right)=\frac{1}{2\cdot\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor}\left(H_{n}-1+\frac{2}{n}\right)

if
n
is even. Noting that
\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor\leq\frac{n}{2}
, we see that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Avg}(n,2)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2\cdot\frac{n}{2}}(H_{n}-1)\geq\frac{1}{n+1}(H_{n}-1)\geq\mathrm{Avg}(n,1)

for all
n\geq 4
. One can check by hand that the result also holds for
n<4
. Thus, we have that
\mathrm{Avg}(n,1)\leq\mathrm{Avg}(n,2)
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
∎

Note in the above proof that we have the asymptotic expression
\mathrm{Avg}(n,2)\sim\ln(n)/n
as
n\rightarrow\infty
, since the
n
th harmonic number is asymptotic to
\ln(n)
.

Proof of Theorem
6
.

We will show that
\mathrm{Avg}(n,3)
is asymptotically bounded below by
2\ln(n)/n
. Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
\lambda_{3}
be the sum of all partitions of
n
into
3
parts. To get a lower bound on the multiplicity of each part in
\lambda_{3}
, let
1\leq i\leq n-2
, and note that the following sums give partitions of
n
into
3
parts:

\displaystyle 1+i+(n-(i+1))=2+i+(n-(i+2))=\cdots=(i-1)+i+(n-(2i-1)),

\displaystyle i+i+(n-2i)=i+(i+1)+(n-(2i+1))=\cdots=i+\left\lfloor\frac{n-i}{2}\right\rfloor+\left\lceil\frac{n-i}{2}\right\rceil.

Counting the number of times
i
appears, there are at least
i-1
instances in the first row and at least
\left\lfloor\frac{n-i}{2}\right\rfloor-i+1
in the second row. This gives a lower bound of

\displaystyle i-1+\left\lfloor\frac{n-i}{2}\right\rfloor-i+1

\displaystyle=\left\lfloor\frac{n-i}{2}\right\rfloor\geq\frac{n-i}{2}-1

parts of size
i
among all partitions of
n
into 3 parts. Define the function
g:[0,n-2]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by
g(x)=\frac{n}{2}-1-\frac{n/2}{n-2}x
. We use this function to obtain a lower bound for the integral of
f_{\lambda_{3}}
, as it always lies below the bound achieved above. We see that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Avg}(n,3)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\hat{f}_{\lambda_{3}}(x)\,dx

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\ell(\lambda_{3})}\int_{0}^{n-2}\frac{g(x)}{x+1}\,dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\ell(\lambda_{3})}\cdot\left(\left(\frac{n}{2}-1\right)\ln(n-1)-((n-2)-\ln(n-1))\cdot\frac{n/2}{n-2}\right)

\displaystyle\sim\frac{1}{\ell(\lambda_{3})}\cdot\frac{n}{2}\ln(n)

as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Also, as
n\rightarrow\infty
we have that
\ell(\lambda_{3})\sim 3\cdot\frac{n^{2}}{12}
, by a known asymptotic formula for the number of partitions of size
n
into 3 parts (see
[
7
]
). This yields

\displaystyle\mathrm{Avg}(n,3)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\ell(\lambda_{3})}\cdot\frac{n}{2}\ln(n)\sim\frac{1}{3\cdot\frac{n^{2}}{12}}\cdot\frac{n}{2}\ln(n)=\frac{2\ln(n)}{n}\sim 2\mathrm{Avg}(n,2)

as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Therefore,
\mathrm{Avg}(n,3)\geq\mathrm{Avg}(n,2)
for sufficiently large
n
, as desired.
∎
