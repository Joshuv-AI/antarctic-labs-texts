Work-Efficient Parallel Counting via Sampling
Hongyang Liu, Yitong Yin, Yiyao Zhang

Abstract
A canonical approach to approximating the partition function of a Gibbs distribution via sampling is simulated annealing. This method has led to efficient reductions from counting to sampling, including: • classic non-adaptive (parallel) algorithms with suboptimal total work [ DFK91 , Bez+08 ] ; • adaptive (sequential) algorithms with near-optimal total work [ SVV09 , Hub15 , Kol18 , HK24 ] . We present an algorithm that achieves both near-optimal total work and efficient parallelism, providing a reduction from counting to sampling with logarithmic depth and near-optimal work. As consequences, we obtain work-efficient parallel counting algorithms for several important models, including the hardcore and Ising models within the uniqueness regime.

Introduction

The relationship between counting and sampling is a fundamental topic in the theory of computing.
A classic result by Jerrum, Valiant and Vazirani
[
JVV86
]
established that
approximate counting and sampling are computationally equivalent up to polynomial time for all self-reducible problems.
A wide range of counting problems can be framed as computing the partition function of a Gibbs distribution,
for which significantly more efficient approximation algorithms have been developed by leveraging samples from the corresponding Gibbs distributions.

1.1
Partition function of Gibbs distribution

Gibbs distributions model systems composed of locally constrained random variables.
Formally, a Gibbs distribution is defined via the Hamiltonian function
H
as follows:

Definition 1.1
.

Let
\Omega
be a finite sample space and
h\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
be a positive integer.
Given a function
H:\Omega\rightarrow\left\{0,1,\ldots,h\right\}
,
the
Gibbs distribution

\pi_{\beta}
on
\Omega
at the inverse temperature
\beta\geq 0
, is defined as:

\forall x\in\Omega,\qquad\pi_{\beta}(x)\triangleq\frac{\exp\left[-\beta\cdot H(x)\right]}{Z(\beta)}

(1)

where the normalizing factor
Z(\beta)\triangleq\sum_{x\in\Omega}\exp\left[-\beta\cdot H(x)\right]
is called the
partition function
.

Remark 1.2
.

The function
H(\cdot)
in the above definition is commonly referred to as the
Hamiltonian function
.
Here, we focus on integer-valued Hamiltonian functions ranging from
0
to a positive integer
h
.
We make a mild assumption that the Hamiltonian function is guaranteed to take the value
0
for at least one configuration in its domain as this is the case for most models or can be simply met.
General integer-valued Hamiltonian functions can be handled by applying a normalization.
Also, our work can be directly extended to the continuous setting in
[
HK24
]
where
H(x)\in\left\{0\right\}\cup[1,h]
. We defer the discussion to
Appendix

A
.

A large number of counting problems can be represented as calculating partition functions.
To estimate the value of a partition function
Z(\beta)
, a standard approach is to estimate the ratio:

\displaystyle Q\triangleq\frac{Z(\beta_{\max})}{Z(\beta_{\min})},

(2)

where
\beta_{\max}=\beta(\text{resp. }\beta_{\min}=\beta)
and
\beta_{\min}<\beta_{\max}
is chosen such that
Z(\beta_{\min})(\text{resp. }Z(\beta_{\max}))
is either trivial or easy to calculate.
The value of
Q
in (
2
) can be estimated using the Monte Carlo method by drawing samples, which is characterized by the sampling oracle defined below.
The key contribution of our work is developing an efficient reduction from counting to sampling.

Definition 1.3
.

Given a Hamiltonian function
H:\Omega\rightarrow\left\{0,1,\ldots,h\right\}
on a sample space
\Omega
and an interval
[\beta_{\min},\beta_{\max}]
with
0\leq\beta_{\min}<\beta_{\max}
,
a
sampling oracle

\mathcal{O}
for the interval
[\beta_{\min},\beta_{\max}]
is a program that, upon each query with input
\beta\in[\beta_{\min},\beta_{\max}]
, returns a random sample
X\sim\pi_{\beta}
.

Remark 1.4
.

The above definition assumes exact samplers. However, in practice, the standard Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) method produces approximate samples.
This discrepancy can be addressed through a standard coupling argument (as discussed in
[
SVV09
, Remark 5.9]
), ensuring that the bias introduced by approximate sampling does not affect our main conclusions.

1.2
Graphic models

We introduce the hardcore model and the Ising model to better illustrate the counting problem and its connection to sampling.

Hardcore model.

The hardcore model is specified on a undirected graph
G=(V,E)
with vertex activity
\lambda\geq 0
. Let
n
be the number of vertices,
m
be the number of edges and
\Delta
be the maximum degree of the graph. A configuration
\sigma\in\left\{-1,+1\right\}^{V}
assigns each vertex
v\in V

\pm 1
-spin. We say a configuration
\sigma
indicates an independent set if for any edge
\left\{u,v\right\}\in e
, at least one of the following holds:
\sigma_{u}=-1
and
\sigma_{v}=-1
. The size of an independent set
n_{+}(\sigma)
is defined as the number of
+1
-spins. Let the sample space
\Omega
be the set of all configurations indicating an independent set. The Gibbs distribution
\pi^{\text{hardcore}}
is defined by

\forall\sigma\in\Omega,\quad\pi^{\text{hardcore}}(\sigma)\propto\lambda^{n_{+}(\sigma)},

and the partition function is
P(\lambda)=\sum_{\sigma\in\Omega}\lambda^{n_{+}(\sigma)}
. Observe that
\lambda^{n_{+}(\sigma)}=\exp\left[-\log(1/\lambda)\cdot n_{+}(\sigma)\right]
. Hence
\log(1/\lambda)
can be viewed as the inverse temperature
\beta

and

\lambda=1

As discussed, we need a trivial starting point and we estimate the ratio via sampling from the distribution within the interval. Under this setting, the natural starting point is
\lambda=0

.

Ising model.

The Ising model is another important graphic model defined on a undirected graph
G=(V,E)
with edge activity
\gamma\geq 0
and vertex activity
\lambda\geq 0
. The Ising model is defined on the sample space
\Omega=\left\{-1,+1\right\}^{V}
, such that the Gibbs distribution
\pi^{\text{Ising}}
is defined by,

\forall\sigma\in\Omega,\quad\pi^{\text{Ising}}(\sigma)\propto\gamma^{m(\sigma)}\lambda^{n_{+}(\sigma)},

where
m(\sigma)=\sum_{(u,v)\in E}\mathbb{1}[\sigma_{u}=\sigma_{v}]
denotes the number of monochromatic edges in
\sigma
and the partition function is
P(\gamma)=\sum_{\sigma\in\Omega}\gamma^{m(\sigma)}\lambda^{n_{+}(\sigma)}
. Calculating the partition function of the Ising model is of crucial significance in statistical physics. See
[
JS93
]
for a detailed discussion. We need a slight generalization of the Gibbs distribution for the partition function of the Ising model and we defer it to subsequent sections.

The hardness of sampling is characterized by the uniqueness condition. Specifically, the uniqueness conditions for the hardcore models and the Ising models are respectively given by
\lambda<\lambda_{c}(\Delta)\triangleq\frac{(\Delta-1)^{\Delta-1}}{(\Delta-2)^{\Delta}}
and
\gamma\in\left(\frac{\Delta-2}{\Delta},\frac{\Delta}{\Delta-2}\right)
. Under the uniqueness condition, optimal
O(n\log n)
mixing times for the Glauber dynamics, a simple and well-studied Markov chain, have been established for these models through a series of work. Beyond these regimes, either the Glauber dynamics is slow mixing or the sampling problem itself becomes computationally intractable. We thus investigate the counting problem within the uniqueness regime with the sampling procedure encapsulated by a sampling oracle for convenience.

1.3
Simulated annealing

The method of simulated annealing provides a systematic approach to estimate the ratio in (
2
) by sampling from Gibbs distributions using the sampling oracle defined in
Definition

1.3
.
The main intuition of the method is that the ratio is hard to estimate for inverse temperatures far from each other, but it can be well estimated for nearby inverse temperatures.
Hence a sequence of increasing inverse temperatures
\beta_{\min}=\beta_{1}<\beta_{2}<\cdots<\beta_{\ell}=\beta_{\max}
, known as the
cooling schedule
, is constructed (either adaptively based on the sampling outcomes, or non-adaptively), which allows for the combination of independent samples to form an estimate
\hat{Q}
of the ratio
Q=Z(\beta_{\max})/Z(\beta_{\min})
.

We denote the total number of queries to the sampling oracle as the total work of the algorithm.
Additionally, we are interested in the number of rounds the algorithm queries the oracle, noting that independent queries can be made within a single round.
Formally, we assume the concurrent-read, concurrent-write parallel random access machine (CRCW-PRAM) model with arbitrary write,
where an arbitrary value written concurrently is stored.
The depth of an algorithm in this model refers to the number of time steps required for its execution.
To compare different simulated annealing algorithms, we adopt the following notation:

Definition 1.5
.

Given a Gibbs distribution on a sample space
\Omega
, let
q\triangleq\ln Z(0)=\ln|\Omega|
.

Remark 1.6
.

Our definition of
q
differs from that in
[
Kol18
,
HK24
]
, where
q
is defined as
\log\frac{Z(\beta_{\min})}{Z(\beta_{\max})}
.
However, using this definition requires access to the Hamiltonian function
H
,
which is unsuitable for non-adaptive cooling schedules (the setting of our paper), since no prior knowledge of the target partition function
Z(\beta)
is available without queries.
Moreover, our definition of
q
aligns with that used in
[
SVV09
]
and simplifies applications such as the Ising model and the hardcore model, where
|\Omega|
is either easy to compute or can be easily upper-bounded.

Previous work on simulated annealing algorithms for estimating the partition function within a relative error
\varepsilon

Non-adaptive algorithms using

\tilde{O}(q^{2}\varepsilon^{-2})

•

Adaptive algorithms using

[

Here and above,

These algorithms suggest that adaptivity (i.e., being sequential) is crucial for reducing the total computational cost.
Indeed, since the seminal work of
[

Given this context, a fundamental open question naturally arises:

For counting via sampling, is it possible to be efficient in both depth and total work?

1.4
Our results

In this work, we answer this question affirmatively,
as formally stated by the following theorem.

Theorem 1.7

(Main result)
.

Fix any Hamiltonian function
H:\Omega\rightarrow\left\{0,1,\ldots,h\right\}
and let
q=\ln|\Omega|
.
For any given values of
0\leq\beta_{\min}<\beta_{\max}

assuming a sampling oracle
\mathcal{O}
for the interval
[\beta_{\min},\beta_{\max}]

We use
\mathbf{NC}

2.1
Chebyshev’s inequality

For random variable
X
with finite variance, we denote its expectation and variance respectively as
\mathbb{E}[X]
and
\mathop{\mathbb{V}}\nolimits[X]
. The relative variance of
X
is
\frac{\mathop{\mathbb{V}}\nolimits[X]}{\mathbb{E}^{2}[X]}
and we use the following notation for convenience:
\mathbb{S}[X]=\frac{\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]}{\mathbb{E}^{2}[X]}=1+\frac{\mathop{\mathbb{V}}\nolimits[X]}{\mathbb{E}^{2}[X]}
. Hence the well-known Chebyshev’s inequality follows.

\forall\varepsilon>0,\mathop{\mathbb{P}}\nolimits\left[|X-\mathbb{E}[X]|\geq\varepsilon\mathbb{E}[X]\right]\leq\frac{\mathop{\mathbb{V}}\nolimits[X]}{\varepsilon^{2}\mathbb{E}^{2}[X]}=\frac{\mathbb{S}[X]-1}{\varepsilon^{2}}.

2.2
Monotonicity and convexity of partition function

Let
z(\beta)=\ln Z(\beta)
denote the log-partition function. We have the result for its monotonicity and convexity.

Lemma 2.1
.

z(\beta)
is monotonically decreasing and convex on
\beta\geq 0

The result follows by simple calculations:

\displaystyle z^{\prime}(\beta)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{Z(\beta)}\sum_{x\in\Omega}H(x)\cdot\exp\left[-\beta\cdot H(x)\right]=-\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\pi_{\beta}}[H(X)]\leq 0,

\displaystyle z^{\prime\prime}(\beta)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\pi_{\beta}}[H^{2}(X)]-\mathbb{E}^{2}_{X\sim\pi_{\beta}}[H(X)]=\mathop{\mathbb{V}}\nolimits_{X\sim\pi_{\beta}}[H(X)]\geq 0.

In above, we observe that
z^{\prime}(\beta)=-\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\pi_{\beta}}[H(X)]\geq-h

3
Parallel Annealing Algorithm

We present a parallel algorithm for estimating the ratio
Q=Z(\beta_{\max})/Z(\beta_{\min})
for given inverse temperatures
\beta_{\min}<\beta_{\max}
,
assuming access to a sampling oracle
\mathcal{O}
for the interval
[\beta_{\min},\beta_{\max}]
.

The algorithm utilizes the simulated annealing method and consists of two key components:

•

A
cooling schedule
from
\beta_{\min}
to
\beta_{\max}
of length
\ell
is constructed.
This schedule is an increasing sequence of inverse temperatures
\mathcal{L}=(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{\ell})
, where

\beta_{\min}=\beta_{1}<\beta_{2}<\cdots<\beta_{\ell}=\beta_{\max}.

•

Given the cooling schedule
\mathcal{L}=(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{\ell})
,
independent samples for
\pi_{\beta_{i}}(1\leq i\leq\ell)
, are generated by the sampling oracle and combined into an
estimator
for
Q=Z(\beta_{\max})/Z(\beta_{\min})
.

The cooling schedule.

The construction of the cooling schedule
\mathcal{L}=(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{\ell})
can be
adaptive
: the choice of inverse temperature
\beta_{i}
may depend on the samples drawn according to the previous inverse temperatures
\beta_{j}
for
j<i
. Such adaptivity was a key to achieving near-linear cost for the annealing algorithm, as displayed in previous works
[
SVV09
,
Hub15
,
Kol18
,
HK24
]
.

Alternatively, we revisit
non-adaptive
cooling schedules, where the construction may depend on the values of
q=\ln|\Omega|
and
h
, but not directly on the Hamiltonian function
H:\Omega\rightarrow\left\{0,1,\ldots,h\right\}
.
Such non-adaptive schedules (e.g., the Chebyshev cooling schedule) were used in classical works such as
[
DFK91
,
Bez+08
]
, although they resulted in sub-optimal quadratic costs.

We prove the existence and efficiency of a non-adaptive cooling schedule that satisfies certain key properties.

Theorem 3.1
.

For any Hamiltonian function
H:\Omega\rightarrow\left\{0,1,\ldots,h\right\}
and letting
q=\ln|\Omega|
,
there exists a non-adaptive cooling schedule

0=\beta_{1}<\beta_{2}<\ldots<\beta_{\ell}=+\infty

of length
\ell=O(q\log^{2}h)
such that

z(\beta_{j-1})-z(\beta_{j})\leq\begin{cases}2&j=\ell,\\
1/\log h&j=2,...,\ell-1,\end{cases}

where recall that
z(\beta)=\ln Z(\beta)
represents the log-partition function.

Theorem

3.1
is proved by the following explicit construction of a non-adaptive cooling schedule.

Construction 3.2

(Non-adaptive cooling schedule)
.

Let

k=\left\lceil q\right\rceil,\quad\gamma=1+\frac{1}{q},\quad\text{and}\quad t=\left\lceil(1+q)(\ln h+\ln\ln h)\right\rceil.

The non-adaptive cooling schedule
0=\beta_{1}<\beta_{2}<\ldots<\beta_{\ell}=+\infty
is constructed as follows:

•

For
1\leq i\leq k+1
,

\beta_{i}=\frac{i-1}{h\ln h}.

•

For
i=k+2
or
i=k+3
,

\beta_{k+2}=\frac{\gamma+1}{2}\cdot\frac{k}{h\ln h},\qquad\beta_{k+3}=\gamma\cdot\frac{k}{h\ln h}.

•

For
k+3<i\leq k+3+(t-1)\left\lceil 2\gamma\left\lceil\ln h\right\rceil\right\rceil
,

\beta_{i}=\max\left\{\frac{k\gamma^{x_{i}+1}}{h\ln h},\frac{k\gamma^{x_{i}}}{h\ln h}+y_{i}\cdot\frac{k\gamma^{x_{i}}-k\gamma^{x_{i}-1}}{2h\ln h\left\lceil\ln h\right\rceil}\right\}.

where

x_{i}=\left\lfloor\frac{i-k-4}{\left\lceil 2\gamma\left\lceil\ln h\right\rceil\right\rceil}\right\rfloor+1\quad\text{ and }\quad y_{i}=i-k-3-\left\lceil 2\gamma\left\lceil\ln h\right\rceil\right\rceil(x_{i}-1).

•

For
i=\ell\triangleq k+4+(t-1)\left\lceil 2\gamma\left\lceil\ln h\right\rceil\right\rceil
,

\beta_{i}=+\infty.

It suffices to verify that the non-adaptive cooling schedule described in

Theorem

Remark 3.3
.

As discussed earlier, computing
q
exactly may be difficult, but it can be easily upper bounded.
Replacing

For instance, in the context of the hardcore model, we may abuse notation by letting

A key observation is that this simple non-adaptive cooling schedule is sufficient to support a good estimate of the ratio
Q=Z(\beta_{\max})/Z(\beta_{\min})

The classic Product Estimator (PE).

Given a cooling schedule
\mathcal{L}=(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{\ell})
and independent samples drawn according to the corresponding Gibbs distributions
\pi_{\beta_{i}}
,
a canonical estimator for the ratio
Q=Z(\beta_{1})/Z(\beta_{\ell})
is the so-called product estimator (PE).
Recall the telescopic product:

Q=\frac{Z(\beta_{\ell})}{Z(\beta_{1})}=\frac{Z(\beta_{2})}{Z(\beta_{1})}\cdot\frac{Z(\beta_{3})}{Z(\beta_{2})}\cdots\frac{Z(\beta_{\ell})}{Z(\beta_{\ell-1})}.

For each
1\leq i\leq\ell-1
, let
X_{i1},\ldots,X_{ir}
be
r
independent samples of
\pi_{\beta_{i}}
, where
r
is a parameter. The estimator is naturally defined as:

\displaystyle\hat{Q}=\prod_{i=1}^{\ell-1}\left(\frac{1}{r}\sum_{j=1}^{r}W_{ij}\right),\text{ where }W_{ij}=\exp\left[(\beta_{i}-\beta_{i+1})H(X_{ij})\right].

Observe that
\mathbb{E}[W_{ij}]=\frac{Z(\beta_{i+1})}{Z(\beta_{i})}
and
\mathbb{E}[\hat{Q}]=Q
.
Therefore,
\hat{Q}
is an unbiased estimator of
Q
.

an estimate
\hat{Q}
of

.

for

in parallel

do

1

for

The Paired Product Estimator (PPE).

The other well known estimator for

be

\displaystyle=W/V,

\displaystyle\text{ where }W

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r}\sum_{j=1}^{r}\prod_{i=1}^{\ell-1}\exp\left[\frac{\beta_{i}-\beta_{i+1}}{2}H(X_{ij})\right],

\displaystyle V

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r}\sum_{j=1}^{r}\prod_{i=1}^{\ell-1}\exp\left[\frac{\beta_{i+1}-\beta_{i}}{2}H(Y_{ij})\right].

Nevertheless, we have

Paired Product Estimator:
\texttt{PPE}(\mathcal{L},r)

This new estimator requires fewer samples but requires the cooling schedule to have narrower gaps between the log-partition functions of neighboring temperatures.
In prior work, such cooling schedules were generated in an adaptive fashion by sequential programs.

A key observation of us is that there is a non-adaptive cooling schedule (as in
theorem

3.1
) using which the PPE can also be fairly efficient.

Theorem 3.6
.

Given a cooling schedule
\mathcal{L}=\left(\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\ldots,\beta_{\ell}\right)
satisfying that

•

z(\beta_{i})-z(\beta_{i+1})\leq 1/\ln h
for any
i\in\left\{1,\ldots,\ell-1\right\}
,

•

\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\pi_{\beta_{\ell}}}[H(X)]\geq D
for a constant
D>0

the procedure
\texttt{PPE}(\mathcal{L},\left\lceil 36D^{-1}\varepsilon^{-2}\delta^{-1}\right\rceil)

returns an estimate
\hat{Q}
of
Z(\beta_{\ell})/Z(\beta_{1})
satisfying

\mathop{\mathbb{P}}\nolimits\left[(1-\varepsilon)\frac{Z(\beta_{\ell})}{Z(\beta_{1})}\leq\hat{Q}\leq(1+\varepsilon)\frac{Z(\beta_{\ell})}{Z(\beta_{1})}\right]\geq 1-\delta,

within one round of oracle calls and the total cost of
O(\ell\varepsilon^{-2}\delta^{-1})
.

Recall that
z(\beta)=\ln Z(\beta)
. Let
\kappa_{i}=z(\beta_{i})+z(\beta_{i+1})-2z(\frac{\beta_{i}+\beta_{i+1}}{2})
. For
0\leq i\leq\ell-1
,

\mathbb{E}[W_{ij}]=\frac{Z\left(\frac{\beta_{i}+\beta_{i+1}}{2}\right)}{Z(\beta_{i})},\quad\mathbb{E}[V_{ij}]=\frac{Z\left(\frac{\beta_{i}+\beta_{i+1}}{2}\right)}{Z(\beta_{i+1})},\quad\mathbb{S}[W_{ij}]=\mathbb{S}[V_{ij}]=\frac{Z(\beta_{i})Z(\beta_{i+1})}{Z^{2}\left(\frac{\beta_{i}+\beta_{i+1}}{2}\right)}=\mathrm{e}^{\kappa_{i}}.

Let
\kappa=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell-1}\kappa_{i}
. Since
W_{j}=\prod_{i=1}^{\ell-1}W_{ij}
and
V_{j}=\prod_{i=1}^{\ell-1}V_{ij}
, we have

\mathbb{E}[W_{j}]=\prod_{i=1}^{\ell-1}\frac{Z\left(\frac{\beta_{i}+\beta_{i+1}}{2}\right)}{Z(\beta_{i})},\quad\mathbb{E}[V_{j}]=\prod_{i=1}^{\ell-1}\frac{Z\left(\frac{\beta_{i}+\beta_{i+1}}{2}\right)}{Z(\beta_{i+1})},\quad\mathbb{S}[W_{j}]=\mathbb{S}[V_{j}]=\prod_{i=1}^{\ell-1}\mathrm{e}^{\kappa_{i}}=\mathrm{e}^{\kappa}

Furthermore, taking the average over the
r
trials, we have

\frac{\mathbb{E}[W]}{\mathbb{E}[V]}=\frac{Z(\beta_{\ell})}{Z(\beta_{1})}\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathbb{S}[W]=\mathbb{S}[V]=1+\frac{1}{r}\left(\mathrm{e}^{\kappa}-1\right).

Note that
\kappa
can be bounded using the following property established in
[

]
from the monotonicity and convexity of the log-partition function

Lemma 3.7

(Lemma 3.2 in
[
Hub15
]
)
.

For any
0\leq\beta_{1}\leq\beta_{2}
, let
\tilde{\kappa}=z(\beta_{1})+z(\beta_{2})-2z(\frac{\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}}{2})
,

\frac{-z^{\prime}(\beta_{1})}{-z^{\prime}(\beta_{2})}\geq\exp\left[\frac{2\tilde{\kappa}}{z(\beta_{1})-z(\beta_{2})}\right].

(4)

By

4

\displaystyle\kappa

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell-1}\kappa_{i}\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell-1}[z(\beta_{i})-z(\beta_{i+1})]\cdot\ln\frac{-z^{\prime}(\beta_{i})}{-z^{\prime}(\beta_{i+1})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\ln h}\cdot\ln\frac{-z^{\prime}(\beta_{0})}{-z^{\prime}(\beta_{\ell})}\leq\frac{1}{2\ln h}\cdot\ln\frac{h}{D}\leq\frac{1}{2}+\ln\frac{1}{D}.

Therefore,

and by the Chebyshev’s inequality,

The Non-Adaptive Cooling Schedule

It remains to formally prove
Theorem

3.1
, specifically by verifying that the non-adaptive cooling schedule constructed in

Theorem

3.1
.

A key finding of this paper is that a non-adaptive cooling schedule can achieve near-optimal total work.
Compared to the schedule in
[
Bez+08
]
, our construction in
3.2
reduces the gap between the log-partition functions of neighboring temperatures from a constant to

. It is clear that in
3.2
, the length satisfies that
\ell=k+4+(t-1)\left\lceil 2\gamma\left\lceil\ln h\right\rceil\right\rceil\leq 50q\log^{2}h

To motivate our construction, we first introduce a slight modification to the non-adaptive cooling schedule from
[

]

SVV09
]
. The schedule consists of two phases: one with linear growth intervals and another with exponential growth intervals.

Lemma 4.1
.

Consider the following cooling schedule
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}=\left(\tilde{\beta}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{\beta}_{\ell^{*}}\right)

We verify the properties as follows.

1.

If
\tilde{\beta}_{j}=\tilde{\beta}_{j-1}+\frac{1}{h\ln h}

\gamma\in\left(\frac{\Delta-2+\delta}{\Delta-\delta},\frac{\Delta-\delta}{\Delta-2+\delta}\right)
,

such that an approximate sample within
1/\textnormal{poly}(n)
total variation distance from the Gibbs distribution can be obtained within
O_{\delta}(\log\Delta\cdot\log n)
depth on
\tilde{O}(n+m)
processors.

It is clear that we always sample from the distributions within the uniqueness regime in the following proof of the applications. Thus, we assume a sampling oracle for convenience.

Recall that the sample space is the set of all
\left\{-1,+1\right\}^{V}
configurations indicating an independent set. Hence
q=\log\left|\Omega\right|\leq n
. Let
\hat{\lambda}
be the target parameter and we compute the partition function

P(\hat{\lambda})=\sum_{\sigma\in\Omega}\lambda^{n_{+}(\sigma)}.

•

If
\hat{\lambda}\leq 1
, we can directly formulate the corresponding Gibbs distribution when
\lambda\in[0,\hat{\lambda}]
.

\forall\sigma\in\Omega,\quad\pi^{\text{hardcore}}(\sigma)=\frac{\lambda^{n_{+}(\sigma)}}{Z}=\frac{\exp\left[-\log(1/\lambda)\cdot n_{+}(\sigma)\right]}{Z},

where
Z=\sum_{\sigma\in\Omega}\lambda^{n_{+}(\sigma)}=\sum_{\sigma\in\Omega}\exp\left[-\log(1/\lambda)\cdot n_{+}(\sigma)\right]
is the partition function. Here
n_{+}(\sigma)
is the Hamiltonian function with values in
\left\{0,\ldots,n\right\}
and
\beta=\log(1/\lambda)
is the inverse temperature. Observe that when
\lambda=0
(i.e.
\beta=+\infty
), the partition function
P(0)=Z(+\infty)=1
is trivial. Set
\beta_{\min}=\log(1/\hat{\lambda})
and
\beta_{\max}=+\infty
and
Algorithm

4
returns an estimate of
\hat{Q}=Z(+\infty)/Z(\log(1/\hat{\lambda}))=P(0)/P(\lambda)
. The final estimation is
\hat{Q}^{-1}
.

•

If
\hat{\lambda}>1
, we estimate the partition function in two steps. Firstly, we can obtain an estimate of
\hat{Q}_{1}=Z(+\infty)/Z(0)=P(0)/P(1)
as stated above. Secondly, for
\lambda\in(1,\hat{\lambda}_{c}]
, we modify the partition function as follows:

Z^{\prime}=\lambda^{-n}Z=\sum_{\sigma\in\Omega}\lambda^{n_{+}(\sigma)-n}=\sum_{\sigma\in\Omega}\exp\left[-\log\lambda\cdot(n-n_{+}(\sigma))\right],

and the Gibbs distribution is defined by

\forall\sigma\in\Omega,\quad\pi^{\text{hardcore}}(\sigma)=\frac{\lambda^{n_{+}(\sigma)}}{Z^{\prime}}=\frac{\exp\left[-\log\lambda\cdot(n-n_{+}(\sigma))\right]}{Z^{\prime}},

In this way,
n-n_{+}(\sigma)
is the Hamiltonian function with values in
\left\{0,\ldots,n\right\}
and
\beta=\log\lambda
is the inverse temperature. A minor challenge is that
c_{0}=\left|\left\{x\in\Omega\;\middle|\;n-n_{+}(\sigma)=0\right\}\right|\geq 1
is no longer guaranteed and our cooling schedule does not work for this case. However, this obstacle can be conquered with the help of a constant upper bound of the uniqueness threshold:
\hat{\lambda}<\lambda_{c}(\Delta)\leq 4
. Hence we construct an easy cooling schedule
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
with linear growth
1/(n\ln n)
from
0
to
\ln 4
which works for
Algorithm

4
and has length
O(n\log n)
:

0,\frac{1}{n\ln n},\frac{2}{n\ln n},\ldots,\ln 4.

Using this cooling schedule in
Algorithm

4
and setting
\beta_{\min}=0
,
\beta_{\max}=\log\hat{\lambda}
, we obtain an estimate of
\hat{Q}_{2}=Z^{\prime}(\log\hat{\lambda})/Z^{\prime}(0)=\hat{\lambda}^{-n}P(\hat{\lambda})/P(1)
. Our final estimation is
\hat{\lambda}^{n}\hat{Q}_{1}\hat{Q}_{2}
. The error rate can be easily controlled by replacing
\varepsilon
with
\varepsilon/3
and the success probability can be bounded by boosting the success probability
0.75
to
0.9
and applying a union bound.

Observe that the total
O(n\varepsilon^{-2}\log^{2}n)
samples in
Algorithm

4
can be obtained in the one round of oracle query and hence the depth is
O(\log\Delta\cdot\log n+\log\varepsilon^{-1})
if replacing the oracle with the parallel sampler. Combing
Lemma

5.1
, we have a
\mathbf{RNC}
counting algorithm with depth
O(\log n\cdot\log\Delta+\log\varepsilon^{-1})
on
\tilde{O}(nm\varepsilon^{-2})
processors.

For the Ising model, we introduce a slight generalization of the Gibbs distribution given by
[
GH20
]
. Let
\Omega
be a finite sample space and
h\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
be a positive integer. Given a function
H:\Omega\rightarrow\left\{0,1,\ldots,h\right\}
and nonnegative function
F:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, the generalized Gibbs distribution
\pi_{\beta}
on
\Omega
at the inverse temperature
\beta\geq 0
, is defined as:

\forall x\in\Omega,\quad\pi_{\beta}(x)\triangleq\frac{\exp\left[-\beta\cdot H(x)\right]\cdot F(x)}{Z(\beta)},

where
Z(\beta)=\sum_{x\in\Omega}\exp\left[-\beta\cdot H(x)\right]\cdot F(x)
is the partition function. We can straightforwardly apply
Algorithm

4
to estimate the partition function as the factor
F(x)
can be treated as
F(x)
copies of
x
for the standard Gibbs distribution. For more details, see
[
GH20
, Lemma 12]
.

Fix the vertex activity
\lambda\geq 0
and let the target edge activity be
\hat{\gamma}
. Note that we have the universal constant bound
1/3\leq\hat{\gamma}\leq 3
. Let the sample space
\Omega=\left\{-1,+1\right\}^{V}
. We estimate the partition function

P(\hat{\gamma})=\sum_{\sigma\in\Omega}\gamma^{m(\sigma)}\lambda^{n_{+}(\sigma)}.

Observe that when
\gamma=1
, the partition function
P(\gamma)=(1+\lambda)^{n}
is easy to calculate.

•

If
\gamma<1
, the Gibbs distribution is defined by

\forall\sigma\in\Omega,\pi^{\text{Ising}}(\sigma)=\frac{\gamma^{m(\sigma)}\lambda^{n_{+}(\sigma)}}{Z}=\frac{\exp\left[-\log(1/\gamma)\cdot m(\sigma)\right]\cdot\lambda^{n_{+}(\sigma)}}{Z},

where
m(\sigma)
is the Hamiltonian function whose value ranges from
0
to
m
and
\beta=\log(1/\gamma)
is the inverse temperature. Note that
q=\log Z(0)=n\log(1+\lambda)
. Similar to the hardcore model with
\lambda\geq 1
, we cannot guarantee that there exists a configuration
\sigma
with
m(\sigma)=0
but have a constant upper bound for the inverse temperature
\beta=\log(1/\gamma)\leq\ln 3
. Hence again we can design an easy cooling schedule with linear growth
1/(m\ln m)
and length
O(m\log m)
. Using this cooling schedule and setting
\beta_{\min}=0
and
\beta_{\max}=\log(1/\gamma)
, we apply
Algorithm

4
to obtain an estimate
\hat{Q}=Z(\log(1/\gamma))/Z(0)=P(\gamma)/P(1)
. The final estimate is
(1+\lambda)^{n}\hat{Q}
.

•

If
\gamma>1
, we modify the partition function

Z^{\prime}=\gamma^{-m}Z=\sum_{\sigma\in\Omega}\gamma^{m(\sigma)-m}\lambda^{n_{+}(\sigma)}=\sum_{\sigma\in\Omega}\exp\left[-\log\gamma\cdot(m-m(\sigma))\right]\cdot\lambda^{n_{+}(\sigma)},

and the Gibbs distribution is defined by

\forall\sigma\in\Omega,\quad\pi^{\text{Ising}}(\sigma)=\frac{\gamma^{m(\sigma)-m}\lambda^{n_{+}(\sigma)}}{Z^{\prime}}=\frac{\exp\left[-\log\gamma\cdot(m-m(\sigma))\right]\cdot\lambda^{n_{+}(\sigma)}}{Z^{\prime}}.

Here
m-m(\sigma)
is the partition function ranging from
0
to
m
and
\beta=\log\gamma
is the inverse temperature. Note that
q=\log Z^{\prime}(0)=n\log(1+\lambda)
and there always exists a configuration
\sigma
(e.g. all
+1
-spins) such that
m(\sigma)=0
. Therefore, setting
\beta_{\min}=0
and
\beta_{\max}=\log\gamma
and applying
Algorithm

4
directly, we obtain an estimate
\hat{Q}=Z^{\prime}(\log\gamma)/Z^{\prime}(0)=\gamma^{-m}P(\gamma)/P(1)
. The final estimation is
(1+\lambda)^{n}\gamma^{m}\hat{Q}
.

Note that we only sample from Gibbs distributions for Ising models within the uniqueness regime and the total
O(n\varepsilon^{-2}\log^{2}m)
samples can be obtained in one round. Combing
Lemma

5.1
, we have a
\mathbf{RNC}
counting algorithm with depth
O(\log n\cdot\log\Delta+\log\varepsilon^{-1})
on
\tilde{O}(nm\varepsilon^{-2})
processors.
∎
