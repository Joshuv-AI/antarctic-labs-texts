Convergence results of a nested decentralized gradient method for non-strongly convex problems
Woocheol Choi, Doheon Kim, Seok-Bae Yun

Abstract
. We are concerned with the convergence of NEAR-DGD + (Nested Exact Alternating Recursion Distributed Gradient Descent) method introduced to solve the distributed optimization problems. Under the assumption of the strong convexity of local objective functions and the Lipschitz continuity of their gradients, the linear convergence is established in [ 1 ] . In this paper, we investigate the convergence property of NEAR-DGD + in the absence of strong convexity. More precisely, we establish the convergence results in the following two cases: (1) When only the convexity is assumed on the objective function. (2) When the objective function is represented as a composite function of a strongly convex function and a rank deficient matrix, which falls into the class of convex and quasi-strongly convex functions. Numerical results are provided to support the convergence results.

Introduction

Recent years have seen a growing interest in developing algorithms to optimize a system in which several networked agents cooperate to minimize the aggregate cost function:

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}f(x):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}(x),

(1.1)

which often goes by the name of the distributed optimization in the literature.
Here
f_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a local objective function (or cost function of agent
i
) and
n
is the number of agents.
Such formulation is relevant in various situations where the resource has to be optimally allocated. Examples include multi-agent system
[
4
,
5
]
, wireless sensor networks
[
13
,
26
,
33
]
, and machine learning problems
[
6
,
9
,
29
,
37
]
, to name a few.

The system
(\ref{1.1})
is often studied as an equivalent decentralized optimization problem:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\min_{{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{np}}F({\bf x})\mbox{ under the constraint }~\big(W\otimes I_{p}\big){\bf x}={\bf x},\end{split}

where
F
denotes the aggregate cost function in (
1.1
):

F({\bf x})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}(x_{i}).

Here
I_{p}
is the
p\times p
identity matrix,
{\bf x}
denotes an
np
dimensional column vector made by stacking up
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}

(i=1,\cdots,n)
:

{\bf x}=(x_{1}^{\top},x_{2}^{\top},\cdots,x_{n}^{\top})^{\top},

and the consensus matrix
W
is the
n\times n
matrix that contains the information on the connectivity of the underlying network:

W=\begin{pmatrix}w_{11}&w_{12}&\cdots&w_{1n}\\
w_{21}&w_{22}&\cdots&w_{2n}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
w_{n1}&w_{n2}&\cdots&w_{nn}\end{pmatrix}.

(1.2)

For a
k\times l
matrix
A
and an
m\times n
matrix
B
, the Kronecker product
A\otimes B
is defined as the following
km\times ln
matrix:

\displaystyle A\otimes B=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}A_{11}B&\cdots&A_{1l}B\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
A_{k1}B&\cdots&A_{kl}B\end{array}\right)

where
A_{ij}B
denotes an
m\times n
block matrix. Throughout this paper, we assume the following properties for the consensus matrix:

•

W
is a doubly-stochastic
n\times n
matrix.

•

The directed graph associated to
W
is strongly connected, and has a self-loop at every vertex.

In Lemma
3.3
, we will show that the graph associated to
W^{\top}W
is connected, and so the eigenvalue
1
of
W^{\top}W
is simple and all other eigenvalues have modulus strictly less than 1. We let
\beta\in[0,1)
be the square root of the second largest eigenvalue of

, which is equal to the spectral norm of
W-\frac{1}{n}1_{n}1_{n}^{\top}
, where

is a column vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
whose entries are all
1
. We let

be the optimal objective value of (
1.1
) and denote by
{X}^{*}
the set of optimal solutions to (
1.1
) which is assumed to be nonempty, i.e.,

{X}^{*}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{p}~:~f(x)=f^{*}\}.

Several approaches have been suggested to solve the distributed optimization problem (
1.1
). Since the
literature is huge, we only review works that are directly related to the decentralized method.
The most common category of algorithms to solve the aforementioned distributed optimization (
1.1
) is
the distributed gradient descent (DGD), which is a gradient descent method with a weighted averaging operation using the consensus matrix in the non-gradient part. After the introduction of DGD in
[
20
]
(See also
[
36
]
), numerous extensions and modifications of this method have been suggested. For example, extension to the case where noise or randomness is present
[
14
,
17
,
30
,
35
]
, extension to optimizations on the network represented by an undirect graph
[
21
]
, constrained problems
[
25
]
, and quantization effect
[
24
]
to name a few. Nice overview of DGD can be found in
[
10
,
23
,
18
]
. For a comparison of the decentralized method and centralized method, we refer to
[
12

.

A common problem shared by the family of the distributed gradient methods
is that, when the step size is fixed
[
34
]
, the iteration does not lead to the exact optimizing solution, but only to a neighborhood of it. The exact optimizing solution can be reached only when a proper diminishing of the step size is accompanied.
Several attempts to overcome this drawback and to obtain the convergence to the exact optimizing solution with fixed time step have been suggested.
In
[
34
]
, a multi-step decentralized gradient descent is suggested by subtracting the
DGD of one step from the next.
The gradient tracking method was introduced in

[
27
,
15
,
22
]
where an auxiliary variable is introduced to track the difference of the gradients in a consensus setting.
In NEAR-DGD
+
method
[
1
,
2
]
, increasingly aggregate consensus process is imposed on each step to guarantee the convergence to the exact optimizing solution without reducing the step size. In
[
1
]
, the linear convergence was obtained for NEAR-DGD
+
when each local cost function

is strongly convex and smooth. In the current work, we extend the convergence estimate to the cases of convex functions when such strong convexity is missing. Specifically, we establish the convergence results in the following two cases: (1) When only the convexity is assumed on the objective function
f
. (2) When the objective function
f
is represented as a composite function of a strongly convex function and a rank deficient matrix, which falls into the class of convex and quasi-strongly convex functions.

2.
Main results

In this section, we describe the detail of NEAD DGD
+
and state the main results of this paper.

Definition 2.1
.

For given
\alpha\geq 0
, a function
\mathbf{f}:\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is called
\alpha
-strongly convex (convex if
\alpha=0
) if

\mathbf{f}(y)\geq\mathbf{f}(x)+\langle y-x,\nabla\mathbf{f}(x)\rangle+\frac{\alpha}{2}\|y-x\|^{2}\quad\forall~x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{p}.

Definition 2.2
.

We say that a function
\mathbf{f}:\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is called
L
-smooth for given
L>0
if
f
has Lipschitz continuous gradient with constant
L
, i.e.,

\|\nabla\mathbf{f}(x)-\nabla\mathbf{f}(y)\|\leq L\|x-y\|\quad\forall~x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{p}.

2.1.
NEAR-DGD
+

The Nested Exact Alternating Recursion Distributed Gradient Descent (NEAR-DGD
+
) was introduced in
[
1
,
2
]
to solve (
1.1
):

{\bf{\bf x}}_{k+1}=\big(W^{t(k)}\otimes I_{p}\big)\big({\bf x}_{k}-\mu\nabla F({\bf x}_{k})\big)\quad\forall~k\geq 0,

(2.1)

where
{\bf x}_{k}=(x_{1,k}^{\top},x_{2,k}^{\top},\cdots,x_{n,k}^{\top})^{\top}
and
\mu>0
. Here
\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{np}
denotes

\nabla F({\bf x}_{k})=\left(\nabla f_{1}(x_{1,k})^{\top},\cdots,\nabla f_{n}(x_{n,k})^{\top}\right)^{\top}

and the exponent
t(k)\in\mathbb{N}
indicates the number of consensus step.

To state convergence results, we introduce the averaging of any
np
-dimensional vector
\mathbb{x}
:

\displaystyle\bar{\bf x}=\frac{1}{n}\big(1_{n}1^{\top}_{n}\!\!\otimes I_{p}\big){\mathbb{x}}

(2.2)

where
1_{n}
is a column vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
whose entries are all
1
. In particular, we have

\displaystyle\bar{{\bf x}}_{k}=\frac{1}{n}\big(1_{n}1^{\top}_{n}\!\!\otimes I_{p}\big){\bf x}_{k}~~\mbox{ and }~~\overline{\nabla F}(\mathbf{x}_{k})=\frac{1}{n}\big(1_{n}1^{\top}_{n}\!\!\otimes I_{p}\big)\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{k}).

(2.3)

We note that

\bar{{\bf x}}_{k}=1_{n}\otimes\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i,k}\right)=1_{n}\otimes\bar{x}_{k}=(\bar{x}_{k}^{\top},\bar{x}_{k}^{\top},\cdots,\bar{x}_{k}^{\top})^{\top}.

Using these notation for averaged quantities, we derive the following averaged version of (
2.1
) which plays an important role as a useful intermediate step in the convergence analysis:

\bar{{\bf x}}_{k+1}=\bar{{\bf x}}_{k}-\mu\overline{\nabla F}({\bf x}_{k}),

(2.4)

where we used the property that
1_{n}^{\top}W=1_{n}^{\top}
coming from the column-stochasticity of the consensus matrix
W
.

Under the assumptions of strong convexity and Lipschitz continuity of the gradient of each function
f_{i}
, the linear convergence
of (
2.1
) with
t(k)=k
is established in
[
1
]
, i.e., there exist constants
C>0
,
D>0
and
\rho\in(0,1)
such that

\|{\bf x}_{k}-\bar{{\bf x}}_{k}\|\leq\beta^{k}D\quad\textrm{and}\quad\|{\bf\bar{x}}_{k}-{\bf x}_{*}\|\leq C\rho^{k}

for all
1\leq i\leq n
and
k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
.
We recall that
\beta\in[0,1)
is the spectral norm of
W-\frac{1}{n}1_{n}1_{n}^{\top}
.

In the current work, we are interested in the case where the strong convexity of the cost functions are missing. Precisely, we obtain the convergence results when either (1) the cost function is convex or (2) it is given in the form of the composite function of a strongly convex function and a linear function. We note that the objective function of the latter is convex and quasi-strongly convex.

2.2.
\bullet
Main result I: Convergence for convex functions

Our first results concern the case where only the convexity (not the strong convexity) is assumed on the cost function
f
. Throughout this paper,
\|\cdot\|
denotes the usual Euclidean norm. We also recall that
\beta\in[0,1)
is the spectral norm of
W-\frac{1}{n}1_{n}1_{n}^{\top}
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Suppose that the cost function
f
is convex and each local cost
f_{i}
is
L_{i}
-smooth
for some
L_{i}>0
. Assume that
\mu\in(0,2/L]
with
L=\max_{1\leq i\leq n}L_{i}
and the sequence
\{t(k)\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}}
satisfies
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\beta^{t(k)}<\infty
. Then, for any minimizer
x_{*}\in X^{*}
, we have

\|{\bar{\bf x}}_{k}-{\bf x}_{*}\|=O(1)\quad\textrm{and}\quad\|\bar{\mathbb{x}}_{k+1}-\mathbb{x}_{k+1}\|=O(\beta^{t(k)}),\quad k\to\infty,

(2.5)

where
\mathbb{x}_{*}:=1_{n}\otimes x_{*}=(x_{*}^{\top},x_{*}^{\top},\cdots,x_{*}^{\top})^{\top}
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Suppose that the cost function
f
is convex and each local cost
f_{i}
is
L_{i}
-smooth for some
L_{i}>0
. Assume that
\mu\in(0,1/L]
with
L=\max_{1\leq i\leq n}L_{i}
and the sequence
\{t(k)\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}}
satisfies
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\beta^{t(k)}<\infty
.
Then we have

f\Bigg(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\bar{x}_{k}\Bigg)-f^{*}=O\Big(\frac{1}{T}\Big)

for
T\in\mathbb{N}
.

In order to obtain the above results, we will find a recursive inequality for the sequence of vectors consisting of
\|\bar{\bf x}_{k}-{\bf x}_{*}\|
and
\|\bar{\bf x}_{k}-{\bf x}_{k}\|
. The recursive inequality at step
k
involves a multiplication with a
2\times 2
matrix depending on
\beta^{t(k)}
followed by an addition with a two-dimensional vector depending on
\beta^{t(k)}
. By obtaining a sharp bound on multiplications of those matrices, we first prove the uniform boundedness of the sequence. We then make use of the bound with the convexity of the cost function to derive the convergence result.

Remark 2.1
.

We note that obtaining estimates (
2.5
) is equivalent to obtaining similar estimates for
\|\bar{x}_{k}-x_{*}\|
and
\|x_{i,k}-\bar{x}_{k}\|
for
1\leq i\leq n
in view of the following relations:

\|\bar{\bf x}_{k}-{\bf x}_{*}\|=\sqrt{n}\|\bar{x}_{k}-x_{*}\|\quad\textrm{and}\quad\|\bar{\bf x}_{k}-{\bf x}_{k}\|=\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\|\bar{x}_{k}-x_{j,k}\|^{2}\Big)^{1/2}.

\bullet

Main result 2: Convergence for quasi-strongly convex functions.

Our second result is concerned with the case where the quasi-strong convexity is assumed on the cost function in place of the strong convexity. Especially, we shall consider the case when the quasi-convexity
arises from the composition of a strongly convex function and a rank-deficient matrix, which
appears ubiquitously in optimization problems or machine learning tasks. The following is the definition of the quasi-strongly convex function:

Definition 2.3

(
[
19
]
)
.

Continuously differentiable function
f
is called quasi-strongly convex
on set X if there exists a constant
\kappa_{f}>0
such that

f^{*}:=\min_{y\in X}f(y)\geq f(x)+\langle\nabla f(x),[x]-x\rangle+\frac{\kappa_{f}}{2}\|x-[x]\|^{2}

for all
x\in X
. Here
[x]
denotes the projection of
x
onto the optimal set

X^{*}:=\{x\in X:f(x)=f^{*}\}.

The projection
[x]
is well
defined if
X^{*}
is closed and convex (see From Theorem 1.5.5 in
[
8
]
). The following composite objective function constitutes a relevant example of quasi-strongly convex functions
[
16
]
:

f(x)={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(h_{i}^{\top}x-y_{i})^{2}=\|Hx-y\|^{2},}

(2.6)

where
x\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
,
h_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, and
y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
and

H=(h_{1},\cdots,h_{n})^{\top}\quad\textrm{and}\quad y=(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n})^{\top}.

Note that the above function
f
is strongly convex if
H
is full ranked, while
it is only quasi-strongly convex if
H
is not of full rank. In general, if
g:\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is an
\alpha
-strongly convex and
L
-smooth function and
H\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times p}
, then the function
f(x)=g(Hx)
is quasi-strongly convex.
Now we state our main result for quasi-strongly convex functions.

Theorem 2.3
.

Suppose that each local cost
f_{i}
is
L_{i}
-smooth for some
L_{i}>0
and the aggregate cost function
f:\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
takes the form
f(x)=g(Hx)
for an
\alpha
-strongly convex and
\mathbf{L}
-smooth function
g:\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with
H\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times p}
. Assume further that
f
has a minimizer and

D:=\sup_{x\in X^{*}}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\|\nabla f_{j}(x)\|^{2}\Big)^{1/2}<\infty.

(2.7)

If the stepsize
\eta>0
and the sequence
\{t(k)\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}}
satisfy

\mu\leq\frac{2C_{H}}{\mathbf{L}+\alpha}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\beta^{t(k)}<\infty,

then the discrepancy

\|\mathbb{x}_{k}-{[{\bar{\bf x}}_{k}]}\|

vanishes as
k\rightarrow\infty
, where we adopted the convention
[{\bar{\bf x}}_{k}]:=1_{n}\otimes[{\bar{x}}_{k}]
. Moreover we have

\big\|\mathbb{x}_{T+1}-[\bar{\bf x}_{T+1}]\big\|\leq C\Big(\max_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\lceil T/2\rceil\leq j\leq T}}\beta^{t(j)}\Big)+C\Big((1-C_{2}\mu)^{T/2}\Big),

where
C_{2}=\frac{2\mathbf{L}\alpha c_{H}}{\mathbf{L}+\alpha}
and constant
C>0
is independent of
T
.
Here
c_{H}
denotes a coefficient in the Hoffman inequality and
C_{H}=1/\|H\|_{2}^{2}
with
\|H\|_{2}:=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\setminus\{0\}}\|Hx\|_{2}/\|x\|_{2}
. Also,
\lceil z\rceil
is the least integer greater than or equal to z.

Remark 2.2
.

The assumption (
2.7
) holds if
H
is the identity matrix since
X^{*}
is a point set. In the Lemma
2.7
below, we prove that the assumption (
2.7
) is true for natural cases containing the example (
2.6
).

Remark 2.3
.

Hoffman coefficient
c_{H}

[
11
,
19
]
is defined by the constant that satisfies the following inequality

\|Hx-H[x]\|^{2}\geq c_{H}\|x-[x]\|^{2}

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. More general definitions are available, but this is sufficient for our purpose. The above inequality implies that
c_{H}\leq 1/C_{H}=\|H\|_{2}^{2}
since
\|Hx-H[x]\|\leq\|H\|_{2}\|x-[x]\|
.

Remark 2.4
.

In the above theorem, we note that
f
is a convex function either. Therefore the optimal set
X^{*}
is closed and convex, and so the projection
[x]
onto
X^{*}
is well defined. We remark that a quasi-strongly convex function need not be a convex function in general.

In order to prove the above result, we establish a coercivity estimate for the quasi-strongly convex functions of the composite form (see Lemma
5.1
). With the help of the coercivity estimate, we will derive a recursive inequality for the sequence of vectors consisting of
\|\bar{\bf x}_{k}-[\bar{\bf x}_{k}]\|
and
\|\bar{\bf x}_{k}-{\bf x}_{k}\|
. Then, as in the proof of Theorems
2.1
and

It will be an interesting problem to extend this convergence result to general quasi-strongly convex functions. We refer to the recent paper
[
19
]
where the linear convergence was obtained for gradient descent methods applied to quasi-strongly convex functions.

The results of above theorems are proved by analyzing the growth of the matrix norm appearing in the sequential inequality of the vectors
\|\bar{\bf x}_{k}-[\bar{\bf x}_{k}]\|
and
\|\bar{\bf x}_{k}-{\bf x}_{k}\|
. We also provide another approach for analyzing the sequential inequality of the vectors, which gives an improved estimate under weak assumptions on
\{t(k)\}
. In the approach, we first prove that the sequences are uniformly bounded and then use this fact to analyze two types of inequalities for the vectors, separately. We first find a sharp bound of
\|\bar{\bf x}_{k}-[\bar{\bf x}_{k}]\|
, and then use it to obtain a sharp bound of
\|\bar{\bf x}_{k}-{\bf x}_{k}\|
.

Before ending this section, we prove two lemmas regarding the cost functions in the setting of Theorem
2.3
.

Lemma 2.6
.

Suppose that each local cost
f_{i}
is
L_{i}
-smooth for some
L_{i}>0
and the aggregate cost function
f:\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
takes the form
f(x)=g(Hx)
for an
\alpha
-strongly convex and
\mathbf{L}
-smooth function
g:\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with
H\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times p}
. Then
L=\max_{1\leq i\leq n}L_{i}
satisfies the inequality
L\geq\alpha\|H\|_{2}^{2}
.

Proof.

The strong convexity of
g
implies

g(Hy)\geq g(Hx)+\langle\nabla g(Hx),(Hy-Hx)\rangle+\frac{\alpha}{2}\|Hy-Hx\|^{2}\quad\forall~x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}.

(2.10)

Since
f(x)=g(Hx)
and
\nabla f(x)=H^{\top}\nabla g(Hx)
, this yields that

f(y)\geq f(x)+\langle\nabla f(x),y-x\rangle+\frac{\alpha}{2}\|Hy-Hx\|^{2}.

On the other hand, the aggregate cost
f=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}
is
L
-smooth since each
f_{i}
is
L_{i}
-smooth and
L=\max_{1\leq i\leq n}L_{i}
. Therefore,

f(y)\leq f(x)+\langle\nabla f(x),y-x\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\|y-x\|^{2}.

Combining these two inequalities, we find

\alpha\|H(x-y)\|^{2}\leq L\|x-y\|^{2}\quad\forall~x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{m},

(2.11)

which implies
L\geq\alpha\|H\|_{2}^{2}
.
∎

Lemma 2.7
.

Suppose that each local cost
f_{i}
is
L_{i}
-smooth for some
L_{i}>0
and the aggregate cost function
f:\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
takes the form
f(x)=g(Hx)
for an
\alpha
-strongly convex and
\mathbf{L}
-smooth function
g:\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with
H\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times p}
. Assume one of the following statements holds:

(1)

The local cost
f_{i}
is bounded below for all
1\leq i\leq n
.

(2)

The local cost
f_{i}
satisfies
f_{i}(x)=f_{i}(x+v)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
v\in\textrm{Ker}(H)
and
1\leq i\leq n
.

Then we have

\sup_{x\in X^{*}}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\|\nabla f_{j}(x)\|^{2}\Big)^{1/2}<\infty.

(2.12)

Proof.

We assume that the first statement holds true. By a standard argument using the smoothness of
f_{i}
, we have

\min_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}f_{i}(y)\leq f_{i}\Big(x-\frac{1}{L_{i}}\nabla f_{i}(x)\Big)\leq f_{i}(x)-\frac{1}{2L_{i}}\|\nabla f_{i}(x)\|^{2}\quad\forall~x\in\mathbb{R}^{p},

which yields

\|\nabla f_{i}(x)\|^{2}\leq 2L_{i}\Big(f_{i}(x)-\min_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}f_{i}(y)\Big).

(2.13)

Since
f(x)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}(x)
is constant as the optimal value
f^{*}
for
x\in X^{*}
and each
f_{i}
is bounded below,

\sup_{x\in X^{*}}f_{i}(x)<\infty.

Combining this with (
2.13
) proves (
2.12
).

Next we assume the second statement. Take a point
x_{*}\in X^{*}
. Then, using (
2.10
) with
f(x)=g(Hx)
and
\nabla f(x)=H^{\top}\nabla g(Hx)
, we see that

X^{*}=\{x_{*}+v~:~v\in\textrm{Ker}(H)\}.

For all
v\in\textrm{Ker}(H)
, we have
f_{i}(x)=f_{i}(x+v)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
. Thus we have
\nabla f_{i}(x)=\nabla f_{i}(x+v)
, and so

\sup_{x\in X^{*}}\|\nabla f_{i}(x)\|=\sup_{v\in\textrm{Ker}(H)}\|\nabla f_{i}(x_{*}+v)\|=\|\nabla f_{i}(x_{*})\|<\infty.

This proves (
2.12
).
∎

In the above lemma, we remark that the statement
(2)
is trivially holds when
H
is given by an identity matrix since
\textrm{Ker}(H)=\{0\}
. This corresponds to the case that the aggregate cost
f
is a strongly convex function.

Preliminary results

In this section, we establish a proposition which will play a key role in the proofs of the main theorem. We begin with recalling the following well-known lemma (see e.g.
[
3
, Lemma 3.11]
).

Lemma 3.1
.

Assume that
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is
\alpha
-strongly convex and
L
-smooth. Then, for any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
we have

\langle\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y),x-y\rangle\geq\frac{L\alpha}{L+\alpha}\|x-y\|^{2}+\frac{1}{\alpha+L}\|\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)\|^{2}.

In the proofs of the main theorems, we will consider a sequence
\{(A_{k},B_{k})\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
defined in (
4.1
) and derive a recursive estimate for the sequence. Then, to obtain the desired results from the recursive estimate, we will use the following proposition.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
\{X_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
and
\{Y_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
be sequences of non-negative vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
\{M_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
be a sequence of
2\times 2
matrices. Assume that
\{X_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
,
\{Y_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
and
\{M_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
satisfy the following iterative relation:

X_{k+1}\leq M_{k}X_{k}+Y_{k}.

Suppose further that
\{Y_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
is a sequence of non-negative vectors such that
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\big\|Y_{k}\big\|<\infty
, and
\{M_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
takes the following form:

M_{k}=\begin{pmatrix}1&r\\
\alpha_{k}&\alpha_{k}\end{pmatrix}

for some fixed
r>0
and a sequence of non-negative real numbers
\{\alpha_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
satisfying
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{k}<\infty
.
Then, we have

(1)

The matrix
M_{b}M_{b-1}\dots M_{a}
is uniformly bounded for
0\leq a<b<\infty
.

(2)

\{X_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
is uniformly bounded.

Proof.

(1) We take
c=\max\{r,1\}
and define a weighted norm
\|\cdot\|_{c}
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
by

\|(x,y)^{\top}\|_{c}=|x|+c|y|\quad{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mbox{for }x,y\in\mathbb{R}}.

For a matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
, we define the operator norm by

\|M\|_{\mathcal{L}}=\sup_{v\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\|Mv\|_{c}}{\|v\|_{c}}.

We claim that

\|M_{k}\|_{\mathcal{L}}\leq 1+c\alpha_{k}.

To show this, we take an arbitrary
v=(x,y)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and compute

M_{k}v=\begin{pmatrix}1&r\\
\alpha_{k}&\alpha_{k}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}x+ry\\
\alpha_{k}x+\alpha_{k}y\end{pmatrix},

which has the weighted
L^{1}
norm as

\|M_{k}v\|_{c}=|x+ry|+c|\alpha_{k}x+\alpha_{k}y|\leq(1+c\alpha_{k})|x|+(r+c\alpha_{k})|y|.

Since
c=\max\{r,1\}
we have

r+c\alpha_{k}\leq c(1+c\alpha_{k})

so that

\begin{split}\|M_{k}v\|_{c}&\leq(1+c\alpha_{k})|x|+(r+c\alpha_{k})|y|\\
&\leq(1+c\alpha_{k})(|x|+c|y|)=(1+c\alpha_{k})\|v\|_{c}.\end{split}

This proves the claim. Using (
3
), for any
1\leq a<b<\infty
we obtain

\begin{split}\Big\|M_{b}M_{b-1}\dots M_{a}\Big\|_{\mathcal{L}}&\leq\prod_{k=a}^{b}\|M_{k}\|_{\mathcal{L}}\\
&\leq\prod_{k=a}^{b}(1+c\alpha_{k})\\
&\leq\prod_{k=a}^{b}e^{c\alpha_{k}}=\exp\Big(c\sum_{k=a}^{b}\alpha_{k}\Big).\end{split}

Combining this with the assumption that
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{k}<\infty
, we conclude that
\Big\|M_{b}M_{b-1}\dots M_{a}\Big\|_{\mathcal{L}}
is uniformly bounded for
0\leq a<b<\infty
.

(2) By induction, the iteration implies that

X_{k+1}\leq\Big(M_{k}M_{k-1}\dots M_{0}\Big)X_{0}+\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\Big(M_{k}M_{k-1}\dots M_{j+1}\Big)Y_{j}+Y_{k}.

(3.1)

This gives the desired uniform boundedness of
X_{k}
since
M_{b}M_{b-1}\dots M_{a}
is uniformly bounded and
\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\big\|Y_{j}\big\|<\infty
.
∎

We finish this section by proving that the two conditions on the consensus matrix
W
introduced earlier actually leads to
\beta\in[0,1)
. Its proof was sketched briefly in
[
28
]
. We provide a more detailed version of the proof for the readers’ convenience.

Lemma 3.3
.

[
28
]

Assume that the consensus matrix
W
satisfies the following:

•

W
is a doubly-stochastic
n\times n
matrix.

•

The directed graph associated to
W
is strongly connected, and has a self-loop at every vertex.

Then the following assertions hold:

(1)

The graph associated to
W^{\top}W
is connected and aperiodic. (Since
W^{\top}W
is symmetric, we may either interpret its associated graph as a strongly connected directed graph, or a connected undirected graph, depending on preference.)

(2)

The largest eigenvalue of
W^{\top}W
is
1
, and it is simple.

(3)

The spectral norm
\beta
of
W-\frac{1}{n}1_{n}1_{n}^{\top}
satisfies
\beta\in[0,1)
.

Proof.

(1) The assumption that the graph associated to
W
has a self-loop at every vertex, is equivalent to
w_{ii}>0
for all
1\leq i\leq n
. Let
w_{0}:=\frac{1}{2}\min\limits_{1\leq i\leq n}w_{ii}>0
and
\widetilde{W}:=W-w_{0}I_{n}
. Then all entries of
\widetilde{W}
are nonnegative, and the directed graph associated to
\widetilde{W}
is precisely the same as that of
W
. Note that

W^{\top}W=(\widetilde{W}+w_{0}I_{n})^{\top}(\widetilde{W}+w_{0}I_{n})\geq w_{0}\widetilde{W},

with the inequality being in entrywise sense. Hence the graph associated to
W^{\top}W
contains that associated to
\widetilde{W}
(or
W
) as its subgraph, which is indeed strongly connected and aperiodc.

(2) Since
W^{\top}W
is symmetric positive semidefinite, we can arrange its eigenvalues as
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{n}\geq 0
. The Perron-Frobenius theorem combined with (1) yields
1=\lambda_{1}>\lambda_{2}
.

(3) Since
W^{\top}W
is symmetric, it can be diagonalized by an orthogonal matrix, i.e.,

W^{\top}W=Q\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})Q^{\top},\quad Q:=[u_{1}|\dots|u_{n}],

with
u_{i}
being an eigenvector associated to
\lambda_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,n
. Note that
\lambda_{1}=1
and
u_{1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}1_{n}
, because
W
is doubly-stochastic. Hence

W^{\top}W=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}u_{i}u_{i}^{\top}=\frac{1}{n}1_{n}1_{n}^{\top}+\sum_{i=2}^{n}\lambda_{i}u_{i}u_{i}^{\top},

which yields

\displaystyle\left\|W-\frac{1}{n}1_{n}1_{n}^{\top}\right\|

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\mbox{the largest eigenvalue of }(W-\frac{1}{n}1_{n}1_{n}^{\top})^{\top}(W-\frac{1}{n}1_{n}1_{n}^{\top})}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\mbox{the largest eigenvalue of }W^{\top}W-\frac{1}{n}1_{n}1_{n}^{\top}}=\sqrt{\lambda_{2}}<1,

where the second equality follows using that
1_{n}1_{n}^{\top}W=1_{n}1_{n}^{\top}
and
W^{\top}1_{n}1_{n}^{\top}=1_{n}1_{n}^{\top}
since
W
is doubly-stochastic. The proof is finished.
∎

when the global objective function belongs to a class of convex and quasi-strongly convex functions. Namely, we consider the class of convex functions that arise from the composition of strongly convex function and possibly rank-deficient matrix. The following lemma provides a coercivity estimate for those functions which will be an essential tool for the convergence analysis.

Lemma 5.1
.

Assume the function
f:\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
takes the form
f(x)=g(Hx)
for
H\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times p}
and an
\alpha
-strongly convex and
L
-smooth function
g:\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. Then for some
c_{H}>0
, we have

\begin{split}&\langle\nabla f(x)-\nabla f([x]),x-[x]\rangle\\
&\qquad\geq\frac{{L}\alpha c_{H}}{L+\alpha}\|x-[x]\|^{2}+\frac{C_{H}}{L+\alpha}\|\nabla f(x)-\nabla f([x])\|^{2},\end{split}

where
c_{H}
is the coefficient of the Hoffman inequality
[
19
,
31
]
, and
C_{H}=1/\|H\|_{2}
with
\|H\|_{2}:=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\|Hx\|_{2}}{\|x\|_{2}}
.

Proof.

By applying Lemma
3.1
with the fact that
g
is strongly convex and smooth, we find

\begin{split}&\langle\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y),\,x-y\rangle\\
&\qquad=\langle H^{\top}\nabla g(Hx)-H^{\top}\nabla g(Hy),\,x-y\rangle\\
&\qquad=\langle\nabla g(Hx)-\nabla g(Hy),\,Hx-Hy\rangle\\
&\qquad\geq\frac{{L}\alpha}{L+\alpha}\|Hx-Hy\|^{2}+\frac{1}{L+\alpha}\|\nabla g(Hx)-\nabla g(Hy)\|^{2}.\end{split}

With the choice of
y=[x]
, we get

\begin{split}&\langle\nabla f(x)-\nabla f([x]),\,x-[x]\rangle\\
&\qquad\geq\frac{{L}\alpha}{L+\alpha}\|Hx-H[x]\|^{2}+\frac{1}{L+\alpha}\|\nabla g(Hx)-\nabla g(H[x])\|^{2},\end{split}

(5.1)

Then, we have from the Hoffman inequality
[
19
,
31
]
that

\|Hx-H[x]\|^{2}\geq c_{H}\|x-[x]\|^{2}

(5.2)

and from the definition of
f
that

\begin{split}\|\nabla g(Hx)-\nabla g(H[x])\|&\geq C_{H}\|A^{\top}\nabla g(Hx)-H^{\top}\nabla g(H[x])\|\\
&=C_{H}\|\nabla f(x)-\nabla f([x])\|.\end{split}

(5.3)

Inserting (
5.2
), (
5.3
) into (
5.1
), we get the desired result. ∎

We now turn to the proof of Theorem
2.3
.

5.1.
Proof of the Theorem
2.3

:
In this proof, we denote any positive constant that is independent of the number of iterations
k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
, by
C>0
. First we use (
2.11
) to compute

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|\bar{{x}}_{k+1}-{[\bar{x}_{k}]}\|&=\bigg\|\bar{{x}}_{k}-{[\bar{x}_{k}]}-\frac{\mu}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\nabla f_{j}(x_{j,k})\bigg\|\cr&=\bigg\|\bar{{x}}_{k}-{[\bar{x}_{k}]}-\frac{\mu}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\nabla f_{j}(\bar{x}_{k})+\frac{\mu}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Big(\nabla f_{j}(\bar{x}_{k})-\nabla f_{j}(x_{j,k})\Big)\bigg\|\cr&\leq\Big\|\bar{{x}}_{k}-{[\bar{x}_{k}]}-{\mu}\nabla f(\bar{x}_{k})\Big\|+\frac{\mu L}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\|\bar{x}_{k}-x_{j,k}\|\cr&\leq\Big\|\bar{{x}}_{k}-{[\bar{x}_{k}]}-{\mu}\nabla f(\bar{x}_{k})\Big\|+\frac{\mu L}{\sqrt{n}}\|\bar{{\bf x}}_{k}-{\bf x}_{k}\|.\\
\end{split}

(5.4)

For the first term of the last line, we use
{\nabla f}({[\bar{x}_{k}]})=0
to compute

\displaystyle\Big\|\bar{{x}}_{k}-{[\bar{x}_{k}]}-{\mu}\nabla f(\bar{x}_{k})\Big\|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}=}\|\bar{{x}}_{k}-{[\bar{x}_{k}]}\|^{2}-2\mu\langle\bar{{x}}_{k}-{[\bar{x}_{k}]},{\nabla f}(\bar{{x}}_{k})\rangle+\mu^{2}\|{\nabla f}(\bar{{x}}_{k})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad=\|\bar{{x}}_{k}-{[\bar{x}_{k}]}\|^{2}-2\mu\langle\bar{{x}}_{k}-{[\bar{x}_{k}]},\nabla f(\bar{{x}}_{k})-{\nabla f}({[\bar{x}_{k}]})\rangle

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\mu^{2}\|{\nabla f}(\bar{{x}}_{k})-{\nabla f}({[\bar{x}_{k}]})\|^{2}.

Then we apply Lemma
5.1
and use
0<\mu\leq\frac{2C_{H}}{\mathbf{L}+\alpha}
to bound the last line by

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\|\bar{{x}}_{k}-{[\bar{x}_{k}]}\|^{2}-2\mu\left(\frac{\mathbf{L}\alpha c_{H}}{\mathbf{L}+\alpha}\|\bar{x}_{k}-{[\bar{x}_{k}]}\|^{2}+\frac{C_{H}}{\mathbf{L}+\alpha}\|{\nabla f}(\bar{{x}}_{k})-{\nabla f}({[\bar{x}_{k}]})\|^{2}\right)\\
&\quad+\mu^{2}\|{\nabla f}(\bar{{x}}_{k})-{\nabla f}({[\bar{x}_{k}]})\|^{2}\\
&\quad\leq\left(1-2\mu\frac{{\mathbf{L}}\alpha c_{H}}{\mathbf{L}+\alpha}\right)\|\bar{{x}}_{k}-{[\bar{x}_{k}]}\|^{2}.\end{split}

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\|\bar{{x}}_{k}-{[\bar{x}_{k}]}-\mu{\nabla f}(\bar{{x}}_{k})\|\leq\sqrt{1-C_{2}\mu}\|\bar{{x}}_{k}-{[\bar{x}_{k}]}\|,

(5.5)

where

.
Inserting (

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|\bar{{x}}_{k+1}-{[\bar{x}_{k}]}\|\leq\sqrt{1-C_{2}\mu}\|\bar{{x}}_{k}-{[\bar{x}_{k}]}\|+\frac{\mu L}{\sqrt{n}}\|{\bf x}_{k}-\bar{{\bf x}}_{k}\|.\end{split}

This implies

\|\bar{{x}}_{k+1}-{[\bar{x}_{k+1}]}\|\leq\sqrt{1-C_{2}\mu}\|\bar{{x}}_{k}-{[\bar{x}_{k}]}\|+\frac{\mu L}{\sqrt{n}}\|{\bf x}_{k}-\bar{{\bf x}}_{k}\|

(5.6)

since
[x]
is the projection of
x
onto the minimizing set
X^{*}
.
On the other hand, we recall from (

\begin{split}\|{\bf x}_{k+1}-\bar{{\bf x}}_{k+1}\|\leq{(1+\mu L)}\beta^{t(k)}\|{\bf x}_{k}-\bar{\bf x}_{k}\|+{\mu\sqrt{n}L}\beta^{t(k)}\|\bar{x}_{k}-[\bar{x}_{k}]\|+{\mu D}\beta^{t(k)},\end{split}

(5.7)

where
D>0
is now defined as in (
2.7
).
If we put

A_{k}=\sqrt{n}\|\bar{{x}}_{k}-{[\bar{x}_{k}]}\|,\quad B_{k}=\|{\bf x}_{k}-\bar{{\bf x}}_{k}\|,

(5.8)

we get the following system of difference inequalities:

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{c}A_{k+1}\cr B_{k+1}\end{array}\right)\leq\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\sqrt{1-C_{2}\mu}&\mu L\cr{C_{0}}\beta^{t(k)}&{C_{0}}\beta^{t(k)}\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}A_{k}\cr B_{k}\end{array}\right)+{}\beta^{t(k)}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}0\cr\mu D\end{array}\right),

where
C_{0}=(1+\mu L)
.
We let
q=\sqrt{1-C_{2}\mu}
and define

Z_{k}=\begin{pmatrix}A_{k}\\
B_{k}\end{pmatrix},\quad Y_{k}={}\beta^{t(k)}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}0\cr\mu D\end{array}\right),\quad\textrm{and}\quad M_{k}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\sqrt{1-C_{2}\mu}&\mu L\cr{C_{0}}\beta^{t(k)}&{C_{0}}\beta^{t(k)}\end{array}\right).

Noting that

M_{k}=q\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&(\mu L)/q\cr({C_{0}}/q)\beta^{t(k)}&({C_{0}}/q)\beta^{t(k)}\end{array}\right)

and applying Proposition
3.2
, we see that

\|M_{b}M_{b-1}\dots M_{a}\|_{1}\leq{C}\,q^{b-a+1}

(5.17)

for all
1\leq a\leq b<\infty
. We now expand (
5.1
) as in (
3.1
) and apply (
5.17
) with the triangle inequality to deduce

\begin{split}\|Z_{T+1}\|&\leq\left\|\Big(M_{T}M_{T-1}\dots M_{1}\Big)Z_{0}+\sum_{j=0}^{T-1}\Big(M_{T}M_{T-1}\dots M_{j+1}\Big)Y_{j}+Y_{T}\right\|\\
&\leq Cq^{T}\|Z_{0}\|+C\sum_{j=0}^{T-1}q^{T-j}\|Y_{j}\|+\|Y_{T}\|\\
&\leq Cq^{T}\|Z_{0}\|+C\sum_{j=0}^{T}q^{T-j}\beta^{t(j)}.\end{split}

We estimate this further as

\begin{split}\|Z_{T+1}\|&\leq Cq^{T}\|Z_{0}\|+C\sum_{j=\lceil T/2\rceil}^{T}q^{T-j}\beta^{t(j)}+C\sum_{j=0}^{\lceil T/2\rceil-1}q^{T-j}\beta^{t(j)}\\
&\leq Cq^{T}\|Z_{0}\|+C\Big(\max_{\lceil T/2\rceil\leq j\leq T}\beta^{t(j)}\Big)\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}q^{k}+C\sum_{j=0}^{\lceil T/2\rceil-1}q^{T-j}\\
&\leq Cq^{T}\|Z_{0}\|+C\Big(\max_{\lceil T/2\rceil\leq j\leq T}\beta^{t(j)}\Big)\frac{1}{1-q}+\frac{Cq^{T/2}}{1-q}.\end{split}

Since

for

, the above estimate gives the desired estimate. The proof is finished.

6.
Convergence results for fixed steps of consensus

In this section, we provide another approach to analyze the sequential inequality (
5.1
). We first prove the uniform boundedness of the sequences from the inequality and then use it to analyze each of the two sequential inequalities in (
5.1
). This approach results in the improved convergence estimates of (
2.1
) under weaker condition on
t(k)
containing the case that
t(k)
is constant.

We recall from (
5.8
) the sequences defined by

A_{k}=\sqrt{n}\|\bar{{x}}_{k}-{[\bar{x}_{k}]}\|,\quad B_{k}=\|{\bf x}_{k}-\bar{{\bf x}}_{k}\|.

In the following lemma, we show that the sequences
\{A_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
and
\{B_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
are uniformly bounded.

Lemma 6.1
.

Fix
J\in\mathbb{N}
. Assume that
t(k)\geq J
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
and

\mu<\min\Big\{\frac{2C_{H}}{\mathbf{L}+\alpha},~\frac{C_{2}}{C_{2}+L(1+\sqrt{2})}\cdot\frac{1-\beta^{J}}{L\beta^{J}}\Big\},

where
C_{2}=\frac{2\mathbf{L}\alpha c_{H}}{\mathbf{L}+\alpha}
.
We take
\gamma=\frac{1-\sqrt{1-C_{2}\mu}}{\mu L}
and

R=\max\Big\{A_{0},~B_{0}/\gamma,~\frac{\mu D\beta^{J}}{\gamma-(\gamma+(1+\gamma)\mu L)\beta^{J}}\Big\}.

(6.1)

Then we have

A_{k}\leq R,\quad B_{k}\leq\gamma R

(6.2)

for all
k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
.

Remark 6.1
.

In the definition of
R
of the above lemma, we need the condition
(\gamma+(1+\gamma)\mu L)\beta^{J}<\gamma
, which is written as

\mu<\frac{\gamma(1-\beta^{J})}{(1+\gamma)L\beta^{J}}.

(6.3)

Noting that
\gamma=\frac{C_{2}}{L(1+\sqrt{1+C_{2}\mu})}
and
C_{2}\mu<1
, we have

\gamma>\frac{C_{2}}{L(1+\sqrt{2})}.

It yields that
\frac{\gamma}{1+\gamma}\geq\frac{C_{2}}{C_{2}+L(1+\sqrt{2})}
, and so the following condition

\mu<\frac{C_{2}}{C_{2}+L(1+\sqrt{2})}\cdot\frac{1-\beta^{J}}{L\beta^{J}}

is a sufficient condition for (
6.3
).

Proof of Lemma
6.1
.

We argue by an induction. Combining
t(k)\geq J
with (
5.6
) and (
5.7
), we have the following estimates

\begin{split}A_{k+1}&\leq\sqrt{1-C_{2}\mu}A_{k}+\mu LB_{k}\\
B_{k+1}&\leq\mu L\beta^{J}A_{k}+(1+\mu L)\beta^{J}B_{k}+\mu D\beta^{J}.\end{split}

(6.4)

Note that (
6.2
) holds for
k=0
by the definition of
R
. Assume that it holds that

A_{k}\leq R,\quad B_{k}\leq\gamma R

(6.5)

for a fixed value
k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
. Combining this with the first estimate of (
6.4
) yields

A_{k+1}\leq(\sqrt{1-C_{2}\mu}+\gamma\mu L)R=R.

We also use the second estimate of (
6.4
) with (
6.5
) to deduce

\begin{split}B_{k+1}&\leq\mu L\beta^{J}R+(1+\mu L)\beta^{J}\gamma R+\mu D\beta^{J}\\
&=(\mu L+\gamma(1+\mu L))\beta^{J}R+\mu D\beta^{J}\\
&\leq\gamma R,\end{split}

where the second inequality holds due to
R\geq\frac{\mu D\beta^{J}}{\gamma-(\mu L+\gamma(1+\mu L))\beta^{J}}
. This completes the inductive argument, and so the proof is completed.
∎

Remark 6.2
.

Notice that using the definition of
C_{2}
along with the inequality
c_{H}\leq\|H\|_{2}^{2}
(see Remark
2.3
) and
L\geq\alpha\|H\|_{2}^{2}
of Lemma
2.6
, we have

\begin{split}\gamma&=\frac{C_{2}}{L(1+\sqrt{1+C_{2}\mu})}\\
&\leq\frac{C_{2}}{2{L}}=\frac{2\mathbf{L}\alpha c_{H}}{2L(\mathbf{L}+\alpha)}\\
&\leq\frac{\alpha\|H\|_{2}^{2}\mathbf{L}}{L(\mathbf{L}+\alpha)}\\
&\leq\frac{\mathbf{L}}{\mathbf{L}+\alpha}<1.\end{split}

(6.6)

Using the uniform boundedness of the above lemma, we proceed to analyze the sequential estimates (
5.6
) and (
5.7
) further to derive a sharp bound on
A_{k}
and
B_{k}
. For this we shall use an elementary lemma given in the below.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
\beta>1
. Suppose that a sequence
\{v_{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}\subset\mathbb{R}
satisfies

v_{k+1}\leq\alpha v_{k}+\beta

(6.7)

for all
k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
. Then we have

v_{k}\leq\alpha^{k}v_{0}+\frac{\beta}{1-\alpha}.

Proof.

Using (
6.7
) inductively, we find

v_{k}\leq\alpha^{k}v_{0}+\beta\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\alpha^{j}<\alpha^{k}v_{0}+\frac{\beta}{1-\alpha}.

The proof is done.
∎

We derive a convergence for (
2.1
) when the number of the consensus steps
t(k)
is fixed in the following result, which corresponds to Theorem
2.4
.

Proof of Theorem
2.4
.

Using the bound of Lemma
6.1
with (
6.4
) and (
6.6
), we find

B_{k+1}\leq\beta^{J}B_{k}+\mu\beta^{J}(2LR+D).

By applying the above lemma, we deduce that

B_{k}\leq(\beta^{J})^{k}B_{0}+\frac{\mu\beta^{J}(2LR+D)}{1-\beta^{J}}.

Inserting this estimate into the first inequality of (
6.4
) yields

A_{k+1}\leq\sqrt{1-C_{2}\mu}A_{k}+\mu^{2}L\beta^{J}\frac{(2LR+D)}{1-\beta^{J}}+\mu L(\beta^{J})^{k}B_{0}.

We consider two sequences
v_{k}
and
w_{k}
such that

v_{k+1}=\sqrt{1-C_{2}\mu}\,v_{k}+\mu^{2}L\beta^{J}\frac{(2LR+D)}{1-\beta^{J}}

(6.8)

for
k\geq 1
with
v_{1}=A_{1}
, and

w_{k+1}=\sqrt{1-C_{2}\mu}\,w_{k}+\mu L(\beta^{J})^{k}B_{0}

(6.9)

for
k\geq 1
and
w_{1}=0
. Then it is easy to see that
A_{k}\leq v_{k}+w_{k}
for all
k\geq 1
.
Applying Lemma
6.2
to (
6.8
) yields

v_{k}\leq(\sqrt{1-C_{2}\mu})^{k-1}A_{1}+\frac{\mu^{2}}{1-\sqrt{1-C_{2}\mu}}\frac{L\beta^{J}(2LR+D)}{1-\beta^{J}}.

Also, noting that (
6.9
) implies that
w_{k+1}\leq w_{k}+\mu L(\beta^{J})^{k}B_{0}
, we deduce that

w_{k}\leq\sum_{l=1}^{k-1}\mu L(\beta^{J})^{l}B_{0}\leq\frac{\mu LB_{0}}{1-\beta^{J}}\beta^{J}.

Combining the above estimates with the fact that
A_{k}\leq v_{k}+w_{k}
, we get

A_{k}\leq(\sqrt{1-C_{2}\mu})^{k-1}A_{1}+\frac{\mu^{2}}{1-\sqrt{1-C_{2}\mu}}\frac{L\beta^{J}(2LR+D)}{1-\beta^{J}}+\frac{\mu LB_{0}}{1-\beta^{J}}\beta^{J}.

Using that
A_{1}\leq A_{0}+\mu LB_{0}
from (
6.4
), we obtain

A_{k}\leq(\sqrt{1-C_{2}\mu})^{k-1}(A_{0}+\mu LB_{0})+\frac{\mu L\beta^{J}}{1-\beta^{J}}\Big(\frac{\mu(2LR+D)}{1-\sqrt{1-C_{2}\mu}}+{B_{0}}\Big)

(6.10)

The proof is done.
∎

Remark 6.3
.

We notice that (
6.10
) gives a bound of the form

A_{k}\leq(\sqrt{1-C_{2}\mu})^{k-1}(A_{0}+\mu LB_{0})+O\Big(\frac{\mu L\beta^{J}}{1-\beta^{J}}\Big)

using that

\frac{\mu^{2}}{1-\sqrt{1-C_{2}\mu}}=\frac{\mu(1+\sqrt{1-C_{2}\mu})}{C_{2}}\leq\frac{2\mu}{C_{2}}.

We utilize the above argument to handle general step size
\{\beta(k)\}
to get the following result, which corresponds to Theorem
2.5
.

Proof of Theorem
2.5
.

Using (
5.7
), we have

\begin{split}B_{k+1}&\leq\mu L\beta^{t(k)}A_{k}+(1+\mu L)\beta^{t(k)}B_{k}+\mu D\beta^{t(k)}\\
&=\beta^{t(k)}B_{k}+\mu\beta^{t(k)}(LA_{k}+LB_{k}+D)\\
&\leq\beta^{t(k)}B_{k}+\mu(2LR+D)\beta^{t(k)},\end{split}

(6.11)

where we used Lemma
6.1
and (
6.6
) in the second inequality.
Since
t(k)\geq J
, we have

B_{k+1}\leq\beta^{J}B_{k}+\mu(2LR+D)\beta^{J}.

By applying Lemma
6.2
to the above inequality, we get

B_{k}\leq\beta^{kJ}B_{0}+\frac{\mu(2LR+D)\beta^{J}}{1-\beta^{J}}.

Inserting this estimate into (
6.11
), we get

\begin{split}B_{k+1}&\leq\beta^{kJ+t(k)}B_{0}+\mu(2LR+D)\beta^{t(k)}\Big(\frac{\beta^{J}}{1-\beta^{J}}+1\Big)\\
&=\beta^{(k+1)J}B_{0}+\frac{\mu(2LR+D)\beta^{t(k)}}{1-\beta^{J}}.\end{split}

Combining this with the first inequality of (
6.4
) yields

A_{k+1}\leq\sqrt{1-C_{2}\mu}A_{k}+\frac{\mu^{2}L(2LR+D)\beta^{t(k-1)}}{{1-\beta^{J}}}+\mu LB_{0}\beta^{kJ}

for
k\geq 1
.
Using this iteratively gives

\begin{split}A_{k+1}&\leq(\sqrt{1-C_{2}\mu})^{k}A_{1}+\frac{\mu^{2}L(2LR+D)}{1-\beta^{J}}\sum_{l=0}^{k-1}(\sqrt{1-C_{2}\mu})^{k-1-l}\beta^{t(l)}\\
&\quad+\mu LB_{0}\sum_{l=0}^{k-1}(\sqrt{1-C_{2}\mu})^{k-1-l}\beta^{(l+1)J}.\end{split}

(6.12)

Splitting the summation, we estimate

\begin{split}&\sum_{l=0}^{k-1}(\sqrt{1-C_{2}\mu})^{k-1-l}\beta^{t(l)}\\
&=\sum_{l=0}^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor-1}(\sqrt{1-C_{2}\mu})^{k-1-l}\beta^{t(l)}+\sum_{l=\lfloor k/2\rfloor}^{k-1}(\sqrt{1-C_{2}\mu})^{k-1-l}\beta^{t(l)}\\
&\leq(\sqrt{1-C_{2}\mu})^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}\sum_{l=0}^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor-1}\beta^{t(l)}+\frac{\beta^{t(\lfloor k/2\rfloor)}}{1-\sqrt{1-C_{2}\mu}}.\end{split}

Similarly,

\begin{split}&\sum_{l=0}^{k-1}(\sqrt{1-C_{2}\mu})^{k-1-l}\beta^{(l+1)J}\\
&=\sum_{l=0}^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor-1}(\sqrt{1-C_{2}\mu})^{k-1-l}\beta^{(l+1)J}+\sum_{l=\lfloor k/2\rfloor}^{k-1}(\sqrt{1-C_{2}\mu})^{k-1-l}\beta^{(l+1)J}\\
&\leq(\sqrt{1-C_{2}\mu})^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}\sum_{l=0}^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor-1}\beta^{(l+1)J}+\frac{\beta^{(\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1)J}}{1-\sqrt{1-C_{2}\mu}}.\end{split}

Combining the above two estimates in (
6.12

\begin{split}A_{k+1}&\leq(\sqrt{1-C_{2}\mu})^{k}A_{1}\\
&\quad+\frac{\mu^{2}L(2LR+D)}{1-\beta^{J}}\bigg((\sqrt{1-C_{2}\mu})^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}\sum_{l=0}^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor-1}\beta^{t(l)}+\frac{\beta^{t(\lfloor k/2\rfloor)}}{1-\sqrt{1-C_{2}\mu}}\bigg)\\
&\quad+\mu LB_{0}\bigg((\sqrt{1-C_{2}\mu})^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}\sum_{l=0}^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor-1}\beta^{(l+1)J}+\frac{\beta^{(\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1)J}}{1-\sqrt{1-C_{2}\mu}}\bigg).\end{split}

Using that
A_{1}\leq A_{0}+\mu LB_{0}
from (
6.4
), we obtain

\begin{split}A_{k+1}&\leq(\sqrt{1-C_{2}\mu})^{k}(A_{0}+\mu LB_{0})\\
&\quad+\frac{\mu L}{1-\sqrt{1-C_{2}\mu}}\bigg[\mu(2LR+D)\beta^{t(\lfloor k/2\rfloor)}+B_{0}\beta^{(\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1)J}\bigg]\\
&\quad+\mu L(\sqrt{1-C_{2}\mu})^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}\bigg[\mu(2LR+D)\sum_{l=0}^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor-1}\beta^{t(l)}+B_{0}\sum_{l=0}^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor-1}\beta^{(l+1)J}\bigg].\end{split}

The proof is done.
∎

7.
Numerical Results

In this section, we present numerical results which validate the convergence results obtained in this paper.

7.1.
Regression Problem

We perform the numerical test for the regression problem

f(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|h_{i}^{\top}x-y_{i}\|^{2}=\|Hx-y\|^{2},

where
x\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
,
h_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times s}
, and
y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
and

H=(h_{1},\cdots h_{n})^{\top}\quad\textrm{and}\quad y=(y_{1}^{\top},\cdots,y_{n}^{\top})^{\top}.

This function
f
is quasi-strongly convex, and it is strongly convex if and only if
\textrm{ker}(H)=\{0\}
. We recall that
\textrm{ker}(H)=\{0\}
is equivalent to
H^{\top}H
being invertible. Thus we may determine whether
f
is strongly convex or not, by computing
\det(H^{\top}H)
.

We present the simulation for the case when
f
is quasi-strongly convex but not strongly convex (
\textrm{ker}(H)\neq\{0\}
). In this case we let
X^{*}
be the set of minimizers of
f
, and for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
we denote by
[x]
the projection of
x
onto the set
X^{*}
. We measure
\|\bar{x}_{k}-[\bar{x}_{k}]\|
and plot this quantity in terms of the following measures (see Figure 1 below)

(1)

Number of iterations;

(2)

Cumulative cost of communication and computation, i.e., sum of the costs corresponding to the iteration numbers
0,1,2,\dots,k
.

Here the cost of communication and computation is defined by

Cost =
\#
Communications
\times c_{c}+\#
Computations
\times c_{g}
.

The above cost introduced in
[
1
]
concerns both the cost of communications and that of gradient computations with suitable weight
c_{c}>0
and
c_{g}>0
reflecting the background of the problem.

Figure 1.
Performance of NEAR-DGD
+
for the regression problem with respect to: (1) Log
10
of Number of iterations, (2) Log

8.
Conclusion

In this paper, we studied the convergence property of the NEAR-DGD
+
(Nested Exact Alternating Recursion Distributed Gradient Descent) method when the strong convexity assumption is missing. First, we obtained the
O(1/T)
convergence result when the cost function is convex and smooth. Secondly, we established the linear convergence for a class of quasi-strongly convex functions which are given as composition of a degenerate linear mapping and a strongly convex function. We also provided the numerical results supporting the theoretical convergence results. Extending our result to general quasi-strongly convex functions will be an interesting future work.
