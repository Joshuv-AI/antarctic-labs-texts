Interpolation by decomposable univariate polynomials
Joachim von zur Gathen, Guillermo Matera

Abstract
. The usual univariate interpolation problem of finding a monic polynomial f f of degree n n that interpolates n n given values is well understood. This paper studies a variant where f f is required to be composite, say, a composition of two polynomials of degrees d d and e e , respectively, with d ​ e = n de=n , and therefore d + e − 1 d+e-1 given values. Some special cases are easy to solve, and for the general case, we construct a homotopy between it and a special case. We compute a geometric solution of the algebraic curve presenting this homotopy, and this also provides an answer to the interpolation task. The computing time is polynomial in the geometric data, like the degree, of this curve. A consequence is that for almost all inputs, a decomposable interpolation polynomial exists.

For two univariate polynomials

With a suitable normalization, explained in Section

The standard univariate interpolation problem is well understood.
It asks for a monic interpolation polynomial

many constraints on values.
When we require

, then only the
d+e-1

We write

for an algebraic closure of

and

Problem 1.1
.

Given integers
d,e\geq 2
,

\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{d+e-1})\in{\mathbb{F}}^{d+e-1}

such that
f(\alpha_{i})=\beta_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq d+e-1
and
f=g\circ h
with
\deg g=d
as above?

Standard univariate interpolation at
n
points

(\alpha_{i},\beta_{i})
has a solution if
\alpha_{i}\neq\alpha_{j}
for
i\neq j
,
that is,
\prod_{i\neq j}(\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{j})
is nonzero.
But most polynomials are indecomposable: the decomposable ones form a subvariety of small dimension
within the space of all polynomials (Fact
3.1
).
Thus interpolating at
n
points and then decomposing will usually not furnish a solution.
Our parametrization provides a polynomial
R
(Definition
3.9
) such that
R(\alpha)\neq 0
guarantees
a decomposable interpolant.
Thus almost all such problems have a solution and Problem
1.1
a positive answer.

This issue arises, for example, in the black-box model of representing polynomials.
If it is expensive to calculate any single value of the polynomial—say by some experiment—then one may wish to minimize the number of values sufficient to identify the polynomial.
If, in addition, the polynomial is a priori known to be decomposable,
then our method provides the optimal number of values required.

The polynomial

\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccccc}\alpha_{i_{1}}-\alpha_{j}&\cdots&\alpha_{i_{1}}^{e}-\alpha_{j}^{e}\\

3.
The number of interpolants

We have shown with methods from
linear algebra how to solve the interpolation problem for special

\boldsymbol{\beta}

We use notions like varieties and morphism only over algebraically closed fields,
but they may be “defined over” some smaller field
{\mathbb{F}}
with algebraic closure
\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}
.
Standard notions and notations of algebraic geometry can be found in,
e.g., Kunz
[
25
]
or Shafarevich
[

]
.

We now set some notation for the decomposable polynomials as in (

).
We may assume all three polynomials
to be
monic (leading coefficient 1) and

0, so that the graph contains the origin). All other compositions

on the right) with linear polynomials (polynomials of degree 1);

[
13
]

Thus we consider for a proper divisor

\displaystyle\;\{f\in{{\mathbb{F}}}[X]\colon\deg f=n,f\text{ monic}\},

\displaystyle P_{e}^{*}({\mathbb{F}})=

\displaystyle\;\{h\in P_{e}({\mathbb{F}})\colon h\text{ original}\},

(3.1)

\displaystyle\gamma_{n,d}\colon

\displaystyle P_{d}({\mathbb{F}})\times P_{e}^{*}({\mathbb{F}})\rightarrow P_{n}({\mathbb{F}})\text{
with }\gamma_{n,d}(g,h)=g\circ h,

\displaystyle C_{n,d}({\mathbb{F}})=

\displaystyle\;\{f\in P_{n}({\mathbb{F}})\colon\exists\,g,h\in P_{d}({\mathbb{F}})\times P_{e}^{*}({\mathbb{F}})\;\;f=g\circ h\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\text{im }\gamma_{n,d},

\displaystyle C_{n}({\mathbb{F}})=

\displaystyle\bigcup_{{d\mid n}\atop{d\not\in\{1,n\}}}C_{n,d}({\mathbb{F}}).

We may endow

f=X^{n}+f_{n-1}X^{e-1}+\cdots+f_{0}

with the vector
(f_{n-1},\ldots,f_{0})
, and
C_{n}({\mathbb{F}})
is the
algebraic variety of decomposable polynomials. We drop the argument

{\mathbb{F}}
when it is clear from the context. When
n
is prime, then

C_{n}({\mathbb{F}})
is empty, and in the following we always assume
n
to be composite.
We have the following geometric description of
C_{n,d}
.

Fact 3.1

(von zur Gathen & Matera
[
15
, Theorem
2.2]
)
.

Let
d
be a proper divisor of
n
and
{\mathbb{F}}
be an algebraically closed field with

\text{char}({\mathbb{F}})
not dividing
d
. Then
C_{n,d}({\mathbb{F}})=~\mathrm{im}\ \gamma_{n,d}
as in (
3
) is a closed irreducible algebraic subvariety of

P_{n}({\mathbb{F}})
of dimension
d+e-1

and degree at most
d^{d+e-1}
.

In all of the following, we
assume that
\text{char}({\mathbb{F}})
does not divide
d
. Our goal is to show that
Problem
1.1
has an affirmative answer for a
suitably generic instance
(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})\in\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{d+e-1}\times\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{d+e-1}
, namely there exists an interpolation polynomial in

C_{n,d}
. For this purpose, we introduce the following incidence
variety:

(3.2)

\Gamma_{n,d}=\{(g,h,\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})\in P_{d}(\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,})\times P_{e}^{*}(\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,})\times\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{d+e-1}\times\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{d+e-1}\colon g\circ h(\boldsymbol{\alpha})=\boldsymbol{\beta}\}.

We have the following result.

Lemma 3.2
.

\Gamma_{n,d}
is defined over
{\mathbb{F}}
, irreducible of dimension
2(d+e-1)

and degree at most
n^{d+e-1}
.

Proof.

Let
\boldsymbol{A}=(A_{1},\ldots,A_{d+e-1})
,
\boldsymbol{B}=(B_{1},\ldots,B_{d+e-1})
,

\boldsymbol{G}=(G_{d-1},\ldots,G_{0})
and
\boldsymbol{H}=(H_{e-1},\ldots,H_{1})
be
vectors of indeterminates over
\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}
. We denote by
\boldsymbol{A}
and
\boldsymbol{B}
the coordinates of
\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{d+e-1}\times\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{d+e-1}
, and by
\boldsymbol{G}
and
\boldsymbol{H}
the coordinates of
P_{d}(\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,})\times P_{e}^{*}(\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,})
. Let

G,H\in{\mathbb{F}}[\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{H},X]
be the polynomials

G=X^{d}+G_{d-1}X^{d-1}+\cdots+G_{0},\quad H=X^{e}+H_{e-1}X^{e-1}+\cdots+H_{1}X,

and consider the
{\mathbb{F}}
-algebra morphism

\Phi\colon{\mathbb{F}}[\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{B},\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{H}]\to{\mathbb{F}}[\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{H}]

defined by
\Phi(B_{i})=(G\circ H)(A_{i})
for
1\leq i\leq d+e-1
. It is
clear that
\Phi
is surjective, and its kernel is

\displaystyle\mathrm{ker}\,\Phi

\displaystyle=\langle B_{1}-(G\circ H)(A_{1}),\ldots,B_{d+e-1}-(G\circ H)(A_{d+e-1})\rangle=\mathcal{I}(\Gamma_{n,d}),

where
\mathcal{I}(\Gamma_{n,d})\subset{\mathbb{F}}[\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{B},\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{H}]

denotes the ideal of polynomials vanishing on
\Gamma_{n,d}
. It
follows that

\overline{\Phi}:{\mathbb{F}}[\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{B},\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{H}]/\mathcal{I}(\Gamma_{n,d})\to{\mathbb{F}}[\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{H}]

is an isomorphism. Since
{\mathbb{F}}[\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{H}]
is a domain,

\Gamma_{n,d}
is irreducible, and its dimension agrees with the
Krull dimension of
{\mathbb{F}}[\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{H}]
, namely
2(d+e-1)
.

Since
\Gamma_{n,d}
is defined by
d+e-1
equations
of degree at most
n=de
, the Bézout inequality (see, e.g., Heintz

[
18
]
or Fulton
[
11
]
) implies the claimed upper bound on its degree.
∎

We have a natural projection

(3.3)

\pi\colon\Gamma_{n,d}\to\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{d+e-1}\times\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{d+e-1}.

In these terms, Problem
1.1
can be rephrased
as follows:

Problem 3.3
.

Given
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{d+e-1})\in\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{d+e-1}

with
\alpha_{i}\not=\alpha_{j}
for
i\not=j
, and

\boldsymbol{\beta}=(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{d+e-1})\in\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{d+e-1}
, is
the fiber
\pi^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
nonempty?

Our next result shows that Problem
3.3
has
a positive answer for most
(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})\in\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{d+e-1}\times\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{d+e-1}
.

Corollary 3.4
.

The projection mapping
\pi\colon\Gamma_{n,d}\to\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{d+e-1}\times\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{d+e-1}
is dominant. In particular, if
R^{(2)}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\neq 0
,
then the fiber

\pi^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
is of dimension zero.

Proof.

Lemma
3.2
shows that
\Gamma_{n,d}
is
irreducible of dimension
2(d+e-1)
. Consider
\pi\colon\Gamma_{n,d}\to\overline{\pi(\Gamma_{n,d})}
and observe that both
\Gamma_{n,d}

and
\overline{\pi(\Gamma_{n,d})}
are irreducible varieties, where

\overline{\pi(\Gamma_{n,d})}
is the Zariski closure of

\pi(\Gamma_{n,d})
in
\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{d+e-1}\times\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{d+e-1}
. For an

\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{d+e-1}
with
R^{(2)}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\neq 0
,
the fiber

\pi^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{0})
has dimension zero by Corollary
2.5
.
Therefore, the theorem on the dimension of fibers (see, e.g.,
[
30
, §6.3, Theorem
7]
) shows that

2(d+e-1)-\dim\overline{\pi(\Gamma_{n,d})}=\dim\Gamma_{n,d}-\dim\overline{\pi(\Gamma_{n,d})}\leq 0.

It follows that
2(d+e-1)\leq\dim\overline{\pi(\Gamma_{n,d})}
, that
is,
\overline{\pi(\Gamma_{n,d})}=\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{d+e-1}\times\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{d+e-1}
.
This proves the first assertion. Finally, taking into account that

\Gamma_{n,d}
and
\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{d+e-1}\times\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{d+e-1}
are both
irreducible varieties of dimension
2(d+e-1)
, the second assertion
is a standard consequence of the theorem on the dimension of fibers.
∎

We may paraphrase the statement of Corollary

3.4
in terms of the interpolation
problem in the following way: for any
(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
with
R^{(2)}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\neq 0
,
there is a finite positive number of solutions
f=g\circ h\in C_{n,d}
to the interpolation problem defined by

(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
. Now we discuss the possible number of
interpolants.

Since the projection

\pi\colon\Gamma_{n,d}\to\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{d+e-1}\times\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{d+e-1}

is a dominant mapping defined over
{\mathbb{F}}
, it induces a finite field
extension
{\mathbb{F}}(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{B})\hookrightarrow{\mathbb{F}}(\Gamma_{n,d})
between
the fields of rational functions on
\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{d+e-1}\times\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{d+e-1}

and on
\Gamma_{n,d}
defined over
{\mathbb{F}}
. The degree of this field
extension is the
degree of the morphism

\pi
and is denoted
by
\deg\pi
.

Assume that the field extension
{\mathbb{F}}(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{B})\hookrightarrow{\mathbb{F}}(\Gamma_{n,d})
is separable (in Proposition

3.7
below we prove this
assertion). By, e.g.,
[
18
, Proposition 1]
,

\#\pi^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})\leq\deg\pi
for any

(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})\in\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{d+e-1}\times\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{d+e-1}
with a
finite fiber, with equality in a Zariski open subset of

\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{d+e-1}\times\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{d+e-1}
. In particular, from Corollary

2.5
we deduce that

\deg\pi\geq S_{2}({d+e-1,d}).

We also have an easy upper bound.

Lemma 3.5
.

We have
\deg\pi\leq\deg C_{n,d}\leq d^{d+e-1}
.

Proof.

Let
(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})\in\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{d+e-1}\times\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{d+e-1}
with

\#\pi^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})=\deg\pi
. Then
\pi^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
is isomorphic to the zero-dimensional variety

\{f\in C_{n,d}\colon f(\alpha_{i})=\beta_{i}\ \text{ for }1\leq i\leq d+e-1\},

which is the intersection of
C_{n,d}
with an affine linear subspace
of
\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{n}
. The Bézout inequality (see, e.g.,

[
18
]
or
[
11
]
) implies the first
inequality, and the second one follows from Theorem

3.1
.
∎

Corollary
2.5
and Lemma

3.5
imply that

(3.4)

S_{2}({d+e-1,d})\leq\deg\pi\leq d^{d+e-1}.

To compare both sides of the inequality, we recall that

d!\,S_{2}(d+e-1,d)
is the number of surjective functions from

\{1,\ldots,d+e-1\}
to
\{1,\ldots,d\}
(see, e.g., Mezö
[
26
, page
17]
), while
d^{d+e-1}
is the total number of functions
from
\{1,\ldots,d+e-1\}
to
\{1,\ldots,d\}
.

Next we want to show that
\#\pi^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})=\deg\pi
for most inputs.
For this purpose, we have to understand
the ramification
locus of the projection
\pi\colon\Gamma_{n,d}\to\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{d+e-1}\times\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{d+e-1}
and the separability of the corresponding field extension. For
(g,h,\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})\in\pi^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
,
\pi
is
unramified

at
(g,h,\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
if the differential mapping

d_{(g,h,\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})}\pi\colon T_{(g,h,\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})}\Gamma_{n,d}\to T_{(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})}(\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{d+e-1}\times\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{d+e-1})
is
injective.

Since the polynomials
Q_{i}=(G\circ H)(A_{i})-B_{i}
with
1\leq i\leq d+e-1

defining the incidence variety
\Gamma_{n,d}
generate a radical
ideal, it follows that the tangent space
T_{(g,h,\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})}
of any

(g,h,\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})\in\Gamma_{n,d}
is the kernel of the
Jacobian matrix
(\partial\boldsymbol{Q}/(\partial(\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{H},\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{B}))(g,h,\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
of
\boldsymbol{Q}=(Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{d+e-1})
with
respect to
\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{H},\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{B}
at
(g,h,\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
.
Since
\pi
is the linear mapping defined by the projection to

\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{d+e-1}\times\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{d+e-1}
, it is
unramified at
(g,h,\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
if and only if the Jacobian
matrix
((\partial\boldsymbol{Q})/(\partial(\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{H}))(g,h,\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
is of full rank.

Our first result considers the special case
(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{0})
.

Lemma 3.6
.

For an
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
with
R^{(2)}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\neq 0
,
\pi
is unramified at any point
(g,h,\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{0})\in\pi^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{0})
.

Proof.

We let
q=d+e-1
and consider the
q\times q
Jacobian matrix

J=(\partial\boldsymbol{Q}/(\partial(\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{H}))(g,h,\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{0})
:

(3.5)

J=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccccc}h(\alpha_{1})^{d-1}&\cdots&h(\alpha_{1})&\!\!1&g^{\prime}(h(\alpha_{1}))\,\alpha_{1}^{e-1}&\cdots&g^{\prime}(h(\alpha_{1}))\,\alpha_{1}\\
\vdots&&\vdots&\!\!\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
h(\alpha_{d})^{d-1}&\cdots&h(\alpha_{d})&\!\!1&g^{\prime}(h(\alpha_{d}))\,\alpha_{d}^{e-1}&\cdots&g^{\prime}(h(\alpha_{d}))\,\alpha_{d}\\[4.30554pt]

\vdots&&\vdots&\!\!\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
h(\alpha_{q})^{d-1}&\cdots&h(\alpha_{q})&\!\!1&g^{\prime}(h(\alpha_{q}))\,\alpha_{q}^{e-1}&\cdots&g^{\prime}(h(\alpha_{q}))\,\alpha_{q}\end{array}\right).

From Corollary
2.5
we have
\#h(\boldsymbol{\alpha})=d
and by Lemma
2.2

that
h(\alpha_{i})\not=h(\alpha_{j})
for
1\leq i<j\leq d
, while each
of the remaining values
h(\alpha_{d+j})
agrees with an

h(\alpha_{i_{j}})
with
1\leq i_{j}\leq d
for
1\leq j\leq e-1
. As a
consequence, the upper-left
d\times d
submatrix
J_{1}
of
J
is
invertible. Further, by subtracting the
i_{j}
th row from the

(d+j)
th row of
J
for
1\leq j\leq e-1
, we obtain a
q\times q

matrix with the same rank as
J
and of the following shape:

\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}J_{1}&*\\
\hline\cr\boldsymbol{0}&\begin{array}[]{ccc}g^{\prime}(h(\alpha_{d+1}))\,(\alpha_{d+1}^{e-1}-\alpha_{i_{1}}^{e-1})&\cdots&g^{\prime}(h(\alpha_{d+1}))\,(\alpha_{d+1}-\alpha_{i_{1}})\\[4.30554pt]
\vdots&&\vdots\\
g^{\prime}(h(\alpha_{q}))(\alpha_{q}^{e-1}-\alpha_{i_{e-1}}^{e-1})&\cdots&g^{\prime}(h(\alpha_{q}))(\alpha_{q}-\alpha_{i_{e-1}})\end{array}\end{array}\right).

It follows that
J
is invertible if and only if this
(e-1)\times(e-1)
lower-right submatrix
J_{2}
is invertible.

Since
g
is monic polynomial of degree
d
vanishing on

h(\boldsymbol{\alpha})
and
\#h(\boldsymbol{\alpha})=d
, any
h(\alpha_{i})
is a
simple root of
g
. This implies that
g^{\prime}(h(\alpha_{d+i}))\not=0

for
1\leq i\leq e-1
. We conclude that
J_{2}
is invertible if and
only if the matrix

J_{3}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}\alpha_{d+1}^{e-1}-\alpha_{i_{1}}^{e-1}&\cdots&\alpha_{d+1}-\alpha_{i_{1}}\\[4.30554pt]
\vdots&&\vdots\\
\alpha_{q}^{e-1}-\alpha_{i_{e-1}}^{e-1}&\cdots&\alpha_{q}-\alpha_{i_{e-1}}\end{array}\right)

is nonsingular. Up to row and column permutations and signs,

J_{3}
is the matrix in (
2.6
).
(In the numbering of (
2.6
),
d+j
is in the same block
of the partition as
i_{j}
.)
Since
R^{(2)}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\neq 0
, it follows that

J_{3}
is nonsingular.
∎

In fact, the lemma’s statement and proof are valid for any
\boldsymbol{\beta}
with
\#\boldsymbol{\beta}\leq d
,
not just
\boldsymbol{\beta}=\boldsymbol{0}
,
using Proposition
2.3
instead of Corollary
2.5
.

Next we show that the field extension
{\mathbb{F}}(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{B})\hookrightarrow{\mathbb{F}}(\Gamma_{n,d})
is separable.
This will show that
\#\pi^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})=\deg\pi
for
any suitably generic
(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
.

Proposition 3.7
.

The field extension
{\mathbb{F}}(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{B})\hookrightarrow{\mathbb{F}}(\Gamma_{n,d})

is separable.

Proof.

The Jacobian
\boldsymbol{J}=\partial\boldsymbol{Q}/\partial(\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{H})\in{\mathbb{F}}[\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{H},\boldsymbol{A}]^{q\times q}

is the matrix in (
3.5
)
with
g,h,\alpha

replaced by vectors of indeterminates
\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{H},\boldsymbol{A}
.
Lemma
3.6
shows that
\det\boldsymbol{J}
does not vanish at certain points,
so in particular
\det\boldsymbol{J}\neq 0
as a polynomial.

In the proof of Lemma
3.2
it is shown that
the
{\mathbb{F}}
-algebra morphism
\overline{\Phi}\colon{\mathbb{F}}[\Gamma_{n,d}]\to{\mathbb{F}}[\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{H}]
, defined by
\overline{\Phi}(\overline{B}_{j})=(G\circ H)(A_{j})
for
1\leq j\leq d+e-1
, where
\overline{B}_{j}\in{\mathbb{F}}[\Gamma_{n,d}]={\mathbb{F}}[\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{B},\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{H}]/\mathcal{I}(\Gamma_{n,d})
is the coordinate function of

\Gamma_{n,d}
defined by
B_{j}
, is an isomorphism. As
\det\boldsymbol{J}
is a nonzero element of
{\mathbb{F}}[\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{H}]
,
we have
\overline{\Phi}^{-1}(\det{\boldsymbol{J}})\not=0
, and the fact that
{\mathbb{F}}[\Gamma_{n,d}]
is a
domain implies that
\overline{\Phi}^{-1}(\det{\boldsymbol{J}})
is
not a zero divisor of
{\mathbb{F}}[\Gamma_{n,d}]
. As a consequence,

\Gamma_{n,d}\cap\{\det{\boldsymbol{J}}\not=0\}
is a nonempty
Zariski open subset of
\Gamma_{n,d}
. In particular, the projection

\pi:\Gamma_{n,d}\to\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{d+e-1}\times\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{d+e-1}
is unramified at
any
(\boldsymbol{g},\boldsymbol{h},\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
in this set.

According to Iversen
[
21
, Proposition T.8]
, the extension

{\mathbb{F}}(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{B})\hookrightarrow{\mathbb{F}}(\Gamma_{n,d})
is separable if and
only if
\pi\colon\Gamma_{n,d}\to\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{d+e-1}\times\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{d+e-1}
is
unramified in a nonempty Zariski open subset of
\Gamma_{n,d}
,
which proves the proposition.
∎

Now
[
18
, Proposition 1]

shows that
\deg\pi=\max\#\pi^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
, where the
maximum is taken among all fibers with finite cardinality, and there exists a nonempty Zariski open
subset of
\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{d+e-1}\times\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{d+e-1}
such that

\#\pi^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})=\deg\pi
for all its elements.
Schost
[
29
, Proposition 3]
and its proof allow to describe more precisely
an open subset for the condition that

\#\pi^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})=\deg\pi
.

Fact 3.8
.

There exists a nonzero polynomial
R^{(3)}\in{\mathbb{F}}[\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{B}]
of total
degree at most
2\deg\pi\deg\Gamma_{n,d}
with the following
property: for any
(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})\in\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{d+e-1}\times\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{d+e-1}
with
R^{(3)}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})\not=0
, we have

\#\pi^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})=\deg\pi
.

We have introduced two polynomial conditions on
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
:
R^{(2)}
in (
2.7
) and

R^{(3)}
in the previous fact.
We now combine them into a single polynomial:

(3.6)

R=R^{(2)}R^{(3)}.

Then
R\in{\mathbb{F}}[\boldsymbol{A}]
is nonzero and from
(
2
),
(
3.4
), and Lemma
3.2
, we have

(3.7)

\deg R<(d+e)^{d+e-1}+2(d^{2}e)^{d+e-1}\leq 3(d^{2}e)^{d+e-1}.

Definition 3.9
.

For
d,e\geq 2
and a field
{\mathbb{F}}
, we call
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in{\mathbb{F}}^{d+e-1}

generic
if
R(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\neq 0
, and the
nonempty Zariski-open set
\mathcal{U}=\{R\neq 0\}\subset{\mathbb{F}}^{d+e-1}
consists of these generic
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
. For any
\boldsymbol{\beta}\in{\mathbb{F}}^{d+e-1}
, we also call
(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})

generic
.

We can now give a criterion under which
\pi
is unramified.

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})\in\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{d+e-1}\times\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{d+e-1}
be generic.
Then
\#\pi^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})=\deg\pi
and
\pi
is
unramified at all
(g,h,\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})\in\pi^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
.

Proof.

From Fact
3.8
,
we know that
\#\pi^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})=\deg\pi
.
Thus the restriction

\pi|_{\pi^{-1}(\mathcal{U})}\colon\pi^{-1}(\mathcal{U})\to\mathcal{U}
is
a finite morphism. Then for any
(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})\in\mathcal{U}
,
the Principle of conservation
of number (see, e.g., Danilov
[
9
, §5.7]
) asserts that

\deg\pi=\sum_{(g,h,\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})\in\pi^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})}\deg_{(g,h,\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})}\pi,

where

\deg_{(g,h,\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})}\pi=\dim_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathcal{O}_{\Gamma_{n,d},(g,h,\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})}/\pi^{*}(\mathfrak{m}_{(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})})\mathcal{O}_{\Gamma_{n,d},(g,h,\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})}),

\mathcal{O}_{\Gamma_{n,d},(g,h,\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})}
is the local
ring of rational functions on
\Gamma_{n,d}
defined over
{\mathbb{F}}
which
are well-defined at
(g,h,\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
and

\mathfrak{m}_{(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})}
is the maximal ideal of
rational functions on
\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{d+e-1}\times\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{d+e-1}
, defined over

{\mathbb{F}}
and vanishing at
(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
.

Now the fact that
\#\pi^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})=\deg\pi
implies
that
\deg_{(g,h,\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})}\pi=1
for any
(g,h,\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})\in\pi^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
. This means
that
\mathcal{O}_{\Gamma_{n,d},(g,h,\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})}/\pi^{*}(\mathfrak{m}_{(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})})\mathcal{O}_{\Gamma_{n,d},(g,h,\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})}
is isomorphic
to
{\mathbb{F}}
. It follows that

\pi^{*}(\mathfrak{m}_{(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})})=\mathfrak{m}_{(g,h,\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})}

for all the points
(g,h,\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})\in\pi^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
, which by,
e.g.,
[
9
, §5.2]
, implies that
\pi
is unramified at
all such points.
∎

4.
Homotopy for interpolation

Our general task is to find composite interpolating polynomials
for given generic
(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})\in{\mathbb{F}}^{d+e-1}\times{\mathbb{F}}^{d+e-1}
,
where
\text{char}({\mathbb{F}})
does not divide
d
.
Corollary
2.5
provides a solution for the special value
\boldsymbol{\beta}=\boldsymbol{0}
.
Our main tool is a homotopy connecting these two instances.

Recall that the interpolants for

(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{d+e-1},\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{d+e-1})
are the common zeros
(g,h)
of the polynomial system

(4.1)

(G\circ H)(\alpha_{i})-\beta_{i}=0\quad\text{ for }1\leq i\leq d+e-1,

where
G=X^{d}+G_{d-1}X^{d-1}+\cdots+G_{0}
and

H=X^{e}+H_{e-1}X^{e-1}+\cdots+H_{1}X
are univariate polynomials in

X
with
d+e-1
indeterminate coefficients
G_{d-1},\ldots,G_{0}
and

H_{e-1},\ldots,H_{1}
. The deformation describing the homotopy is the
variety
V^{*}\subset\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{d+e}
, defined over
{\mathbb{F}}
and determined by the
equations

(4.2)

(G\circ H)(\alpha_{i})-\beta_{i}S=0\quad\text{ for }1\leq i\leq d+e-1.

Here,
S
is a parameter whose values range over
\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}
. For
S=0
, we have
the special problem already solved, and
S=1
is our general task.
More precisely, we shall be interested in a curve
V\subset V^{*}

which consists of certain irreducible components over
{\mathbb{F}}
of the
variety
V^{*}
.
Since each equation in (
4.2
) has degree at most
d
,
the Bézout inequality then implies that

(4.3)

\delta=\deg V\leq\deg V^{*}\leq d^{d+e-1}.

The algorithm solving system (
4.1
)
may be divided into three main parts. First, we compute a bivariate
polynomial
m_{i}(S,T)
defining an irreducible plane curve
birationally equivalent to an irreducible component of
V
.
Then we extend the computation of the polynomial
m_{i}(S,T)
to the
computation of the birational inverse itself. Finally, we substitute 1 for
S
in the birational inverse computed in the
previous step and recover at least one solution to system
(
4.1
).
We use a representation of varieties called a
geometric
solution
or a
rational univariate representation
which is well suited for
algorithmic purposes (see, e.g., Rouillier
[
28
]
,
Giusti et al.
[
17
]
).

4.1.
Geometric solutions

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be indeterminates over
{\mathbb{F}}
and denote
\boldsymbol{X}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
. We start with the definition of a geometric
solution of a zero-dimensional variety defined over
{\mathbb{F}}
. Let
V=\{\boldsymbol{P}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{P}_{D}\}\subset\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{n}
be a zero-dimensional
reduced variety defined over
{\mathbb{F}}
, and
\mathcal{L}\in{\mathbb{F}}[\boldsymbol{X}]
a
linear form which separates the points of
V
, that is,

\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{P}_{i})\neq\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{P}_{j})
for
i\neq j
. A

geometric solution
of
V
consists of

•

\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in{\mathbb{F}}^{n}
so that the linear form
\mathcal{L}=\boldsymbol{\lambda}\cdot\boldsymbol{X}=\lambda_{1}X_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{n}X_{n}\in{\mathbb{F}}[\boldsymbol{X}]

separates the points of
V
,

•

the minimal polynomial
m_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=\prod_{1\leq i\leq D}(T-\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{P}_{i}))\in{\mathbb{F}}[T]
of
\mathcal{L}
in
V
, where

T
is a new variable,

•

polynomials
w_{1},\dots,w_{n}\in{\mathbb{F}}[T]
with
\deg w_{j}<D
for

1\leq j\leq n
such that

V=\{(w_{1}(\eta),\dots,w_{n}(\eta))\in\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{n}\colon\,\eta\in\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,},\,m_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(\eta)=0\}.

Thus
\boldsymbol{P}_{i}=\big(w_{1}(\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{P}_{i})),\ldots,w_{n}(\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{P}_{i})\big)
for
1\leq i\leq D
, and each
w_{j}
interpolates
on
D
pairwise distinct points
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{P}_{1}),\ldots,\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{P}_{D})
.

Next, we consider this notion for equidimensional varieties of
dimension one, that is, for curves, defined over
{\mathbb{F}}
. Let
n\geq 2
,
V\subset\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{n}
be a reduced variety defined over
{\mathbb{F}}
of pure
dimension
1
and degree
\delta
, and let
V=V_{1}\cup\cdots\cup V_{t}

be the decomposition over
{\mathbb{F}}
of
V
into irreducible components.
Suppose that the indeterminate
X_{1}
forms a separable transcendence
basis of all field extensions
{\mathbb{F}}\hookrightarrow{\mathbb{F}}(V_{i})
for
1\leq i\leq t
, that is, each
{\mathbb{F}}(X_{1})\hookrightarrow{\mathbb{F}}(V_{i})
is a finite
separable field extension. Denote by
D_{i}
its degree and let

D=\sum_{1\leq i\leq t}D_{i}
. In particular, the linear projection

\pi:V_{i}\to\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{1}
defined by
\pi(\boldsymbol{x})=x_{1}
is a dominant
morphism of degree
D_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq t
. The behavior of the
degree of a variety under linear maps (see, e.g.,
[
18
, Lemma
2]
) implies that

(4.4)

D=\sum_{1\leq i\leq t}D_{i}\leq\sum_{1\leq i\leq t}\deg V_{i}=\delta.

Let
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}=(\Lambda_{2},\ldots,\Lambda_{n})
be a vector of
new indeterminates. The extension
V_{{\mathbb{F}}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda})}
of
V
to

\overline{{\mathbb{F}}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda})}{}^{n}
is a variety of pure dimension
1

and its coordinate ring is isomorphic to

{\mathbb{F}}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda})\otimes_{{\mathbb{F}}}{\mathbb{F}}[V]
. Consider the generic linear form

(4.5)

\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\cdot\boldsymbol{X}^{*}=\Lambda_{2}X_{2}+\cdots+\Lambda_{n}X_{n}.

As the mapping
{\mathbb{F}}[V]\to{\mathbb{F}}[V_{1}]\times\cdots\times{\mathbb{F}}[V_{t}]
defined by

\xi\mapsto(\xi|_{V_{1}},\ldots,\xi|_{V_{t}})
is an isomorphism of

{\mathbb{F}}
-algebras, we identify
{\mathbb{F}}[V]
with

{\mathbb{F}}[V_{1}]\times\cdots\times{\mathbb{F}}[V_{t}]
. For
\xi\in{\mathbb{F}}[V]
, we denote its
restriction to
V_{i}
by
\xi^{(i)}
for
1\leq i\leq t
. Let

\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n}\in{\mathbb{F}}[V]
be the coordinate functions of
V

induced by
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
, and set
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}=\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}(\xi_{2},\ldots,\xi_{n})\in{\mathbb{F}}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda})\otimes_{{\mathbb{F}}}{\mathbb{F}}[V]
. Since
\xi_{1}^{(i)}
and

\widehat{\mathcal{L}}^{(i)}
are algebraically dependent over

{\mathbb{F}}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda})
, there exists an irreducible polynomial

m_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{(i)}\in{\mathbb{F}}[\boldsymbol{\Lambda},X_{1},T]
, separable with
respect to
T
, such that

m_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{(i)}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda},\xi_{1}^{(i)},\widehat{\mathcal{L}}^{(i)})=0

holds in
{\mathbb{F}}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda})\otimes_{{\mathbb{F}}}{\mathbb{F}}[V_{i}]
for
1\leq i\leq t
.
These polynomials are pairwise relatively prime, and defining

m_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}=\prod_{1\leq i\leq t}m_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{(i)},

we find the following identity in

{\mathbb{F}}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda})\otimes_{{\mathbb{F}}}{\mathbb{F}}[V]
:

(4.6)

m_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda},\xi_{1},\widehat{\mathcal{L}})=0.

From, e.g.,
[
29
, Proposition 1]
, we deduce the following
bounds:

•

\deg_{T}m_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}=D
,

•

\deg_{X_{1}}m_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}\leq\delta
,

•

\deg_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}m_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}\leq\delta
.

Taking partial derivatives with respect to
\Lambda_{j}
in (
4.6
), we deduce the identity

(4.7)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial m_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}{\partial\Lambda_{j}}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda},\xi_{1},\widehat{\mathcal{L}})+\xi_{j}\displaystyle\frac{\partial m_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}{\partial T}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda},\xi_{1},\widehat{\mathcal{L}})=0

in
{\mathbb{F}}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda})\otimes_{{\mathbb{F}}}{\mathbb{F}}[V]
for
2\leq j\leq n
. The
polynomial
\partial m_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}/\partial T
is nonzero, and the
resultant
\mathrm{Res}_{T}(m_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}},\partial m_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}/\partial T)
is also nonzero (see, e.g., Gímenez & Matera
[
16
, Lemma
3.2]
).

We take some
\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in{\mathbb{F}}^{n-1}
such that
\deg_{T}m_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda},X_{1},T)=D
and
\mathrm{Res}_{T}(m_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}},\partial m_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}/\partial T)(\boldsymbol{\lambda},X_{1})\not=0
. Then the linear form

\mathcal{L}=\boldsymbol{\lambda}\cdot\boldsymbol{X}^{*}
with
\boldsymbol{X}^{*}=(X_{2},\ldots,X_{n})
induces a primitive element of the separable field extension

{\mathbb{F}}(X_{1})\hookrightarrow{\mathbb{F}}(V_{i})
for
1\leq i\leq t
, and the minimal
polynomials of
\mathcal{L}
in the field extensions

{\mathbb{F}}(X_{1})\hookrightarrow{\mathbb{F}}(V_{i})
are pairwise relatively prime (see,
e.g.,
[
16
, Proposition 3.4]
). If
\ell
is the coordinate
function of
{\mathbb{F}}[V]
defined by
\mathcal{L}
, we then say that

\ell
is a
primitive element
of the extension

{\mathbb{F}}(X_{1})\hookrightarrow{\mathbb{F}}(V)
, where
{\mathbb{F}}(V)
is total ring of
fractions of
{\mathbb{F}}[V]
. Since
\deg_{T}m_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}=D
, it follows
that
m_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda},X_{1},T)
is the minimal polynomial of

\ell
in the extension
{\mathbb{F}}(X_{1})\hookrightarrow{\mathbb{F}}(V)
.
Setting

(4.8)

m_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(X_{1},T)=m_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda},X_{1},T),\quad v_{j}(X_{1},T)=-\frac{\partial m_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}{\partial\Lambda_{j}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda},X_{1},T)\text{ in }{\mathbb{F}}[X_{1},T]

and substituting
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
for
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
in (
4.6
)–(
4.7
), we obtain
the following identities of
{\mathbb{F}}[V]
:

(4.9)

m_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(\xi_{1},\ell)=0,\quad\frac{\partial m_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}{\partial T}(\xi_{1},\ell)\xi_{j}-v_{j}(\xi_{1},\ell)=0\ \ (2\leq j\leq n).

Thus the polynomials

m_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(X_{1},\mathcal{L})\text{ and }\frac{\partial m_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}{\partial T}(X_{1},\mathcal{L})X_{j}-v_{j}(X_{1},\mathcal{L})\ \ (2\leq j\leq n)

belong to
\mathcal{I}(V)\subset{\mathbb{F}}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]
.
They satisfy the bounds
\deg_{X_{1}}v_{j}\leq\delta

and
\deg_{T}v_{j}\leq D
for
2\leq j\leq n
.
Finally, the equations

m_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(X_{1},\mathcal{L})=0,\quad\frac{\partial m_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}{\partial T}(X_{1},\mathcal{L})X_{j}-v_{j}(X_{1},\mathcal{L})=0\quad(2\leq j\leq n),

constitute a system of equations for the variety
V
in the Zariski
dense open subset
V\cap\{(\partial m_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}/\partial T)(X_{1},\mathcal{L})\not=0\}
of
V
. This motivates the following
definition.

Definition 4.1
.

With assumptions as above, a
geometric solution
of
V

consists of the following items:

•

a linear form
\mathcal{L}=\boldsymbol{\lambda}\cdot\boldsymbol{X}^{*}\in{\mathbb{F}}[\boldsymbol{X}^{*}]

which induces a primitive element
\ell
of the extension

{\mathbb{F}}(X_{1})\hookrightarrow{\mathbb{F}}(V)
,

•

the minimal polynomial
m_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\in{\mathbb{F}}[X_{1}][T]
of
\ell
,

•

a generic
parametrization

v_{2},\ldots,v_{n}
of
V
by the
zeros of
m_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
, of the form

\frac{\partial m_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}{\partial T}(X_{1},T)\,X_{j}-v_{j}(X_{1},T)\quad(2\leq j\leq n),

with
v_{j}\in{\mathbb{F}}[X_{1}][T]
,

\deg_{T}v_{j}<D
,
\deg_{X_{1}}v_{j}\leq\delta
and
(\partial m_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}/\partial T)(X_{1},\mathcal{L})\,X_{j}-v_{j}(X_{1},\mathcal{L})\in\mathcal{I}(V)
for
j\leq n
.

We observe that the polynomial
m_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\in{\mathbb{F}}[X_{1},T]
can be
also defined as follows: consider the linear map

\pi_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\colon V\to\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{2},\quad\pi_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(\boldsymbol{x})=(x_{1},\boldsymbol{\lambda}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}^{*}).

The Zariski closure of
\pi_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(V)
is a plane curve of
degree at most
\delta
, which is indeed defined by

m_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(X_{1},T)=0
. Further, the projection

\pi_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
constitutes a birational equivalence between
V

and the plane curve
\mathcal{C}=\{m_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(X_{1},T)=0\}
,
namely there is a rational inverse of
\pi_{\lambda}
. More precisely,
let

\displaystyle V_{\mathrm{deg}}

\displaystyle=\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{n}:(\partial m_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}/\partial T)(x_{1},\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{x}))=0\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{deg}}

\displaystyle=\{\boldsymbol{(}x_{1},t)\in\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{2}:(\partial m_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}/\partial T)(x_{1},t)=0\}.

The fact that
\mathrm{Res}_{T}(m_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}},\partial m_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}/\partial T)\not=0
implies that
V\setminus V_{\mathrm{deg}}
and

\mathcal{C}\setminus\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{deg}}
are Zariski dense
open subsets of
V
and
\mathcal{C}
respectively. We have the
following result.

Fact 4.2

(Cafure & Matera
[
7
, Proposition
6.3]
)
.

\pi_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}|_{V\setminus V_{\mathrm{deg}}}:V\setminus V_{\mathrm{deg}}\to\mathcal{C}\setminus\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{deg}}

is an isomorphism of open sets defined over
{\mathbb{F}}
.

We remark that the inverse of
\pi_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}|_{V\setminus V_{\mathrm{deg}}}
is defined in the following way:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}\setminus\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{deg}}

\displaystyle\to V\setminus V_{\mathrm{deg}},

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{x},t)

\displaystyle\mapsto\left(x_{1},\frac{v_{2}(x_{1},t)}{(\partial m_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}/\partial T)(x_{1},t)},\ldots,\frac{v_{n}(x_{1},t)}{(\partial m_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}/\partial T)(x_{1},t)}\right).

4.2.
Power series
expansions

Let
n\geq 2
,
V\subset\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{n}
be a reduced variety defined over

{\mathbb{F}}
of pure dimension
1
and degree
\delta
, and let

V=V_{1}\cup\cdots\cup V_{t}
be the decomposition over
{\mathbb{F}}
of
V
into
irreducible components. Let
\mathcal{L}=\boldsymbol{\lambda}\cdot\boldsymbol{X}^{*}\in{\mathbb{F}}[\boldsymbol{X}^{*}]
be a linear form and
m_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}},v_{2},\ldots,v_{n}\in{\mathbb{F}}[X_{1}][T]
which form a geometric solution of
V
as in the
previous subsection.

V
is birationally equivalent to the plane curve

\mathcal{C}\subset\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{2}
defined by
m_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
. The fact
that
V=V_{1}\cup\cdots\cup V_{t}
, where each
V_{i}
is irreducible of
dimension 1, implies
\mathcal{C}
has a decomposition

\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}_{1}\cup\cdots\cup\mathcal{C}_{t}
, where each

\mathcal{C}_{i}
is defined by an irreducible factor
m_{i}
of

m_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
, so that the irreducible factorization of

m_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
in
{\mathbb{F}}[X_{1},T]
is of the form

m_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(X_{1},T)=\prod_{i=1}^{t}m_{i}(X_{1},T).

As the linear form
\mathcal{L}
is a primitive element of the
extension
{\mathbb{F}}(X_{1})\hookrightarrow{\mathbb{F}}(V)
, the factors
m_{i}
are
pairwise coprime with
\sum_{i=1}^{t}\deg_{T}m_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{t}D_{i}=D
.

Consider a single factor, say
m_{1}
, of
m_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
. A
representation of the zeros of
m_{1}
is provided by the well-known
Hensel lemma (see, e.g., Abhyankar
[
1
, Lecture 12]
). More
precisely, assume that

(4.10)

\mathrm{Res}_{T}\left(m_{1}(0,T),\frac{\partial m_{1}}{\partial T}(0,T)\right)\not=0.

Then there is a factorization

m_{1}=\prod_{i=1}^{D_{1}}(T-\sigma_{i})
in
\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}[\![X_{1}]\!][T],

where
\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}[\![X_{1}]\!]
denotes the ring of formal power series in

X_{1}
with coefficients in
\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}
, and
\sigma_{i}\not=\sigma_{j}
for

i\not=j
. We shall see that an approximation of order
2D\delta
of
a single power series, say
\sigma=\sigma_{1}
, suffices to compute

m_{1}
.

For this purpose, we observe that
m_{1}\in{\mathbb{F}}[X_{1},T]
can also be
characterized as the primitive polynomial
p\in{\mathbb{F}}[X_{1}][T]
of
minimal degree (up to nonzero multiples in
{\mathbb{F}}
) for which

p(X_{1},\sigma)=0
holds. Let
N=2D\delta
and let
\sigma_{N}\in{\mathbb{F}}[X_{1}]
be the power series
\sigma
truncated up to order
N+1
,
that is,
\sigma_{N}
is the polynomial of degree at most
N

congruent to
\sigma
modulo
X_{1}^{N+1}
. Our next result shows that

m_{1}
can be obtained as the solution of a suitable congruence
equation involving
\sigma_{N}
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
p\in{\mathbb{F}}[X_{1},T]
be
a polynomial with
\deg_{X_{1}}p\leq\delta
and
\deg_{T}p\leq D

satisfying the condition

(4.11)

p(X_{1},\sigma_{N})\equiv 0\mod X_{1}^{N+1}.

Then
m_{1}
divides
p
in
{\mathbb{F}}[X_{1},T]
.

Proof.

Let
p\in{\mathbb{F}}[X_{1},T]
be a solution of
(
4.11
) satisfying the conditions of the
lemma. The resultant
q\in{\mathbb{F}}[X_{1}]
of
p
and
m_{1}
with respect to

T
has degree at most
N
and belongs to the ideal generated by

m_{1}
and
p
. Since
m_{1}(X_{1},\sigma_{N})\equiv 0\mod X_{1}^{N+1}
and

p(X_{1},\sigma_{N})\equiv 0\mod X_{1}^{N+1}
by hypothesis, we deduce
that
q(X_{1})\equiv 0\mod X_{1}^{N+1}
. Therefore, the facts that
\deg q\leq N
and
q(X_{1})\equiv 0\mod X_{1}^{N+1}
imply
q=0
. In
particular,
m_{1}
and
q
have a nonconstant common factor in

{\mathbb{F}}(X_{1})[T]
. Taking into account the irreducibility of
m_{1}
in

{\mathbb{F}}(X_{1})[T]
and the Gauss lemma, we easily deduce the statement of
the lemma.
∎

Lemma
4.3
characterizes
m_{1}
as the
nonzero solution of (
4.11
) of minimal
degree in
T
, up to nonzero multiples in
{\mathbb{F}}
. We remark that the
argument of Lemma
4.3
holds
mutatis
mutandis
with any
M=2D^{\prime}\delta^{\prime}
instead of
N
, where
D^{\prime}
and

\delta^{\prime}
are upper bounds of
D
and
\delta
respectively.

5.
The homotopy curve

This section studies the set
V^{*}\subset\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{d+e}
of common
solutions of the equations (
4.2
)
and provides a homotopy between the solutions for
(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})

and for
(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{0})
.
We use throughout the notation given there and assume that
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
is generic.

We consider the projection
\pi_{S}\colon V^{*}\to\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{1}
with
\pi_{S}(\boldsymbol{g},\boldsymbol{h},s)=s
.

Lemma 5.1
.

\pi_{S}
is dominant.

Proof.

For
s\in\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}
, we write
\pi_{S}^{-1}(s)=W_{s}\times\{s\}
, where

W_{s}\subset\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{d+e-1}
is the set of solutions
(g,h)
of the system

(G\circ H)(\alpha_{i})-s\beta_{i}=0

\text{ for }1\leq i\leq d+e-1
, namely the
set of interpolants for the interpolation problem determined by

(\boldsymbol{\alpha},s\boldsymbol{\beta})
. By Corollary

3.4
,
any
(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\delta})\in\mathcal{U}
admits a nonempty finite set of
interpolants. As
(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
belongs to this dense Zariski open subset

\mathcal{U}\subset\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{2(d+e-1)}
, it follows
that the affine line

L_{(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})}=\{(\boldsymbol{\alpha},s\boldsymbol{\beta})\colon s\in\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{1}\}
has a
nonempty intersection with
\mathcal{U}
. In particular,

L_{(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})}\cap\mathcal{U}\}
is a nonempty Zariski open subset
of
L_{(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})}
, which proves that there is a nonempty
Zariski open subset
U_{S}=\{s\colon(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}s)\in L(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})\cap\mathcal{U}\}
of
\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{1}
,
so that the interpolation problem
determined by
(\boldsymbol{\alpha},s\boldsymbol{\beta})
for
s\in U_{S}
admits a finite positive
number of interpolants. In particular,
\pi_{S}^{-1}(s)
is nonempty
for any
s\in U_{S}
, which proves that
\pi_{S}
is dominant.
∎

Let
V^{*}=V_{1}\cup\cdots\cup V_{u}
be the decomposition of
V^{*}
into
irreducible components defined over
{\mathbb{F}}
. Since
\pi_{S}
is dominant,
we may assume that the restriction
\pi_{S}|_{V_{i}}\colon V_{i}\to\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{1}
of

\pi_{S}
to
V_{i}
is dominant for
1\leq i\leq t
and not dominant for

t+1\leq i\leq u
, with some
t\in\{1,\ldots,u\}
.
We now define the curve which describes our deformation:

(5.1)

V=V_{1}\cup\cdots\cup V_{t}\subset\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{d+e}.

Lemma 5.2
.

For
1\leq i\leq t
,
\dim V_{i}=1
.

Proof.

Fix
i
with
1\leq i\leq t
. The Theorem on the dimension of fibers
(see, e.g.,
[
30
, §6.3, Theorem 7]
) shows that

\dim V_{i}-1=\dim(\pi_{S}|_{V_{i}})^{-1}(s)

for
s\in{\mathcal{U}}_{S}
.
The proof of
Lemma
5.1
shows that
\pi_{S}^{-1}(s)
has
dimension 0 for generic
s\in\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{1}
, which implies that

\dim V_{i}-1=\dim(\pi_{S}|_{V_{i}})^{-1}(s)\leq 0.

Taking into account that
V\subset\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{d+e}
is defined by the

d+e-1
equations (
4.2
), it follows
that
\dim V_{i}\geq 1
for
1\leq i\leq u
. We conclude that

\dim V_{i}=1
.
∎

Next we give a more intrinsic definition of the curve
V
. Let

Q_{i}=(G\circ H)(\alpha_{i})-\beta_{i}S

\text{ for }1\leq i\leq d+e-1
, denote
\boldsymbol{Q}=(Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{d+e-1})
and let
J
be the Jacobian determinant of

\boldsymbol{Q}
with respect to
\boldsymbol{G}
and
\boldsymbol{H}
, that is,

J=\det(\partial\boldsymbol{Q}/\partial(\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{H})).

Let
\mathcal{I}\subset{\mathbb{F}}[\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{H},S]
be the ideal generated by

Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{d+e-1}
and let

\mathcal{I}:J^{\infty}=\{Q\in{\mathbb{F}}[\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{H},S]\colon\exists\,m\geq 0\textrm{ with }J^{m}Q\in\mathcal{I}\}

be the saturation of
\mathcal{I}
with respect to
J
.
Its variety
\mathcal{Z}(\mathcal{I}:J^{\infty})\subset{\mathbb{F}}^{d+e}
is the
Zariski closure of the locally closed set

\mathcal{Z}(\mathcal{I})\setminus\mathcal{Z}(J)
.

Lemma 5.3
.

V=\mathcal{Z}(\mathcal{I}:J^{\infty})
.

Proof.

For a component
V_{i}
with
i>t
we have
\dim\pi_{S}(V_{i})=0
.
Further, as
V_{i}
is irreducible, also
\pi_{S}(V_{i})
is irreducible,
so that
\pi_{S}(V_{i})=\{s_{i}\}
for a specific
s_{i}\in\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{1}
. The fiber

\pi_{S}^{-1}(s_{i})
is the set of common zeros in

\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{d+e-1}
of the polynomials
Q_{j}(\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{H},s_{i})

(1\leq j\leq d+e-1)
. For a given point
(\boldsymbol{g},\boldsymbol{h},s_{i})\in\pi_{S}^{-1}(s_{i})\cap V_{i}
, if
J(\boldsymbol{g},\boldsymbol{h},s_{i})
was nonzero, then
(\boldsymbol{g},\boldsymbol{h},s_{i})
would
be an isolated point of
V_{i}
, contradicting the fact that
\dim V_{i}\geq 1
. It follows that
J(\boldsymbol{g},\boldsymbol{h},s_{i})=0
for any
(\boldsymbol{g},\boldsymbol{h},s_{i})\in V_{i}
and
\mathcal{Z}(\mathcal{I}:J^{\infty})\subseteq V
.

On the other hand, let
1\leq i\leq t
. By Lemma
5.2
,
V_{i}

has dimension 1 and

\pi_{S}|_{V_{i}}\colon V_{i}\to\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{1}
is dominant. Since
V_{t+1}\cup\cdots\cup V_{u}
projects onto the finite set
\{s_{t+1},\ldots,s_{u}\}
, for

s\in\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{1}\setminus\{s_{t+1},\ldots,s_{u}\}
the fiber
\pi_{S}^{-1}(s)

does not intersect
V_{t+1}\cup\cdots\cup V_{u}
. Further, since

\pi_{S}^{-1}(1)=\pi^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})\times\{1\}
and

\#\pi^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})=\deg\pi
by Fact
3.8

and the genericity of
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathcal{U}
,
we conclude that

\#\pi_{S}^{-1}(s)=\deg\pi
for generic
s\in\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{1}
. In particular, by
Lemma
3.10
we have that
\pi
is unramified
at any
(\boldsymbol{g},\boldsymbol{h},\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\delta})\in\pi^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\delta})
with
(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\delta})\in\mathcal{U}
, which implies that
\pi_{S}
is unramified at any
(\boldsymbol{g},\boldsymbol{h},s)\in\pi_{S}^{-1}(s)
with
s
in the nonempty Zariski open subset

U_{S}
of
\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{1}
.

We pick some
s\in\pi_{S}(V_{i})\cap U_{S}

and
(\boldsymbol{g},\boldsymbol{h},s)\in\pi_{S}^{-1}(s)
.
Then
J(\boldsymbol{g},\boldsymbol{h},s)\not=0
, so that

V_{i}
is not contained in
\{J=0\}
. It
follows that the Zariski closure
V_{i}
of
V_{i}\setminus\{J=0\}
is
contained in the zero set
\mathcal{Z}(\mathcal{I}:J^{\infty})
,
which completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

With a slight abuse of notation, we also denote the
restriction of
\pi_{S}
to
V
as
\pi_{S}
, that is, we consider
the projection

\pi_{S}\colon V\to\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{1}\text{ with }\pi_{S}(\boldsymbol{g},\boldsymbol{h},s)=s.

As
V=V_{1}\cup\cdots\cup V_{t}
is the irreducible decomposition of
V

over
{\mathbb{F}}
and
\pi_{S}|_{V_{i}}\colon V_{i}\to\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{1}
is dominant for

1\leq i\leq t
, we define

\deg\pi_{S}=\sum_{1\leq i\leq t}\deg\pi_{S}|_{V_{i}}.

In the next corollary we collect several consequences of Lemma

.

Corollary 5.4
.

The following assertions hold:

(1)

the extension
{\mathbb{F}}(S)\hookrightarrow{\mathbb{F}}(V_{i})
is separable for
1\leq i\leq t
;

(2)

\deg\pi_{S}=\deg\pi
;

(3)

\mathcal{I}:J^{\infty}
is a radical ideal.

Proof.

Fix

with
1\leq i\leq t
.
Since

is not
contained in
\{J=0\}
,

has dimension zero, and

is a nonempty Zariski open subset of

.
Furthermore,
\pi_{S}|_{V_{i}}
is unramified precisely

According to

, the finite field extension

As a consequence, for

there exists a nonempty Zariski

of

such that

the other hand,

intersects properly each irreducible

∎

6.
An algorithm for decomposable interpolation

The algorithm for computing an interpolant
(g,h)
for the input

(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
with a generic
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
proceeds as
follows. Computing in
{\mathbb{F}}[\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{H},S]
is straightforward, and we
can also take its elements modulo the ideal
{\mathcal{I}}(V)
of the
homotopy curve
V
to
{\mathbb{F}}[V]={\mathbb{F}}[\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{H},S]/{\mathcal{I}}(V)
.
But testing whether an element of
{\mathbb{F}}[\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{H},S]
is zero in

{\mathbb{F}}[V]
amounts to an ideal membership test, which is a nontrivial
task. In order to circumvent this, we work with power series
expansions.
Recall the general assumption that
\text{char}({\mathbb{F}})
does not divide
d
.

From Section
2
, we find an interpolating
polynomial
(g_{0},h_{0})\in W_{0}
for
(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{0})
, so that

\boldsymbol{v}=(g_{0},h_{0},0)\in V
. Now
\boldsymbol{v}
actually lies on a single
irreducible component of
V
, say
\boldsymbol{v}\in V_{1}
. Furthermore,
S\in{\mathbb{F}}[\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{H},S]
is a local parameter on
V_{1}
at
\boldsymbol{v}
, so
that there is an injective morphism of
{\mathbb{F}}
-algebras
\tau\colon{\mathcal{O}}_{\boldsymbol{v}}\to{\mathbb{F}}[\![Y]\!]
from the local ring
{\mathcal{O}}_{\boldsymbol{v}}
of
V_{1}
at
\boldsymbol{v}
to the ring of power series in a new
variable
Y
. Since
\tau(S)=Y
generates this ring,
\tau
is
actually an isomorphism.

To simplify notation, we abbreviate
\boldsymbol{G}|\boldsymbol{H}=(G_{d-1},\ldots,G_{0},H_{e-1},\ldots,H_{1})
, a vector of
d+e-1
indeterminates. For
each
i<d
,
\gamma_{i}=\tau(G_{i})\in{\mathbb{F}}[\![Y]\!]
is a power
series, and similarly
\eta_{i}=\tau(H_{i})\in{\mathbb{F}}[\![Y]\!]
for
i<e
. We abbreviate again
\boldsymbol{\gamma}|\boldsymbol{\eta}=(\gamma_{d-1},\ldots,\gamma_{0},\eta_{e-1},\ldots,\eta_{1})
.

A Newton-Hensel iteration computes an approximation of

\boldsymbol{\gamma}|\boldsymbol{\eta}
to a sufficiently large precision
N
, that is,

\boldsymbol{\psi}\in({\mathbb{F}}[Y]/(Y^{N}))^{d+e-1}
with
\boldsymbol{\psi}\equiv\boldsymbol{\gamma}|\boldsymbol{\eta}\bmod Y^{N}
. For a randomly chosen
\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in{\mathbb{F}}^{d+e-1}
, we take the linear combination

\boldsymbol{\lambda}\cdot(\boldsymbol{\gamma}|\boldsymbol{\eta})=\sum_{1\leq i\leq d}\lambda_{i}\gamma_{d-i}+\sum_{d<i<d+e}\lambda_{i}\eta_{d+e-i}
, its
truncation
L=\sum_{1\leq i<d+e}\lambda_{i}\psi_{i}\in{\mathbb{F}}[Y]/(Y^{N})
and find the minimal polynomial
m\in{\mathbb{F}}[Y,T]
of

\boldsymbol{\lambda}\cdot\boldsymbol{\gamma}|\boldsymbol{\eta}
, which equals the polynomial of
minimal degree in
T
with
\deg m\leq d^{d+e-1}
such that
m(Y,L)\equiv 0\bmod Y^{N}
. Furthermore, we calculate
m^{\prime}((\boldsymbol{G}|\boldsymbol{H})_{i})

for each
i<e+d
, where
m^{\prime}=\partial m/\partial T
is the derivative of
m
. These data provide a geometric
solution of
V_{1}
, and it then remains to substitute
S=1
in them
to find the desired interpolation polynomial
(g,h)
for
(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
.

We sketch the whole algorithm as follows.

Algorithm 6.1
.

Input: Generic
(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})\in{\mathbb{F}}^{d+e-1}\times{\mathbb{F}}^{d+e-1}
, real
\epsilon
with
0<\epsilon<1
, access to a
finite set
{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq{\mathbb{F}}
with at least
32\epsilon^{-1}(d^{2}+de)^{2}d^{5(d+e-1)}
elements.

Output:
A composite interpolating polynomial
f
for

(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
as in Problem
1.1
, or “fail”.

(1)

Compute a point
(\boldsymbol{g}_{0},\boldsymbol{h}_{0})\in W_{0}
.

(2)

Set
M=d^{d+e-1}+1
and
N=2d^{2(d+e-1)}+1
. Compute truncated
power series approximations
\boldsymbol{\gamma}|\boldsymbol{\eta}\in({\mathbb{F}}[Y]/(Y^{N}))^{d+e-1}
for

\boldsymbol{G}|\boldsymbol{H}
, with
\lceil\log_{2}N\rceil
steps of the following
quadratic Newton-Hensel iteration for solving the equations
\boldsymbol{P}=(P_{1},\ldots,P_{d+e-1})=0
, where
P_{j}(\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{H})=(G\circ H)(\alpha_{j})-\beta_{j}\,S\in{\mathbb{F}}[G,H,S]
for
1\leq j\leq d+e-1
. The
initial value is
(\boldsymbol{g}_{0},\boldsymbol{h}_{0})
. One iteration step is given by
the Newton-Hensel operator

N_{\boldsymbol{P}}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\eta})=(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\eta})-(J_{\boldsymbol{P}}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\eta},Y))\bigg(\boldsymbol{P}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\eta},Y)^{\sf T}\bigg)\in{\mathbb{F}}[\![Y]\!]^{d+e-1},

where
J_{\boldsymbol{P}}=\partial\boldsymbol{P}/{\partial(G|H)}\in{\mathbb{F}}[G,H,S]^{(d+e-1)\times(d+e-1)}
is the Jacobian matrix of the
system and
\sf T
denotes transposition. Let

\psi\in({\mathbb{F}}[Y]/(Y^{N}))^{d+e-1}
be the resulting vector of power series
truncated up to order
N
.

(3)

Choose
\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\mathcal{S}^{d+e-1}
uniformly at random.
For
1\leq j<d+e
,
let

\boldsymbol{Z}^{(j)}=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{j-1},\lambda_{j}+\Lambda_{j},\lambda_{j+1},\ldots,\lambda_{d+e-1}),

where
\Lambda_{1},\ldots,\Lambda_{d+e-1}
are new indeterminates.
Compute

\displaystyle L^{(j)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{Z}^{(j)}\cdot\boldsymbol{\psi}=\sum_{1\leq i<d+e}\boldsymbol{Z}^{(j)}_{i}\psi_{i}\in{\mathbb{F}}[\Lambda_{j}][Y]/(Y^{N}),

\displaystyle(L^{(j)})^{2},\ldots,(L^{(j)})^{2M}\in{\mathbb{F}}[\Lambda_{j}][Y]/(Y^{N},\Lambda_{j}^{2}).

(4)

For each
j\in\{1,\ldots,d+e-1\}
, find by
binary search the smallest value
k_{j}
of
\ell

for which

(6.1)

\sum_{0\leq i\leq\ell}a_{i}^{(j)}(L^{(j)})^{i}\equiv 0\bmod(Y^{N},\Lambda_{j}^{2})

has a solution

with
\deg_{Y}a_{i}^{(j)}<M
for
i\leq\ell

and
a_{\ell}^{(j)}
monic. This corresponds to a system of linear
equations for the coefficients in
{\mathbb{F}}[\Lambda_{j}]/(\Lambda_{j}^{2})
of
the
a_{i}^{(j)}
’s. Compute such a solution
a^{(j)}_{0},\ldots,a^{(j)}_{k_{j}}
with
a^{(j)}_{k_{j}}
monic of minimal degree
M_{j}
.
[Then
\ell\leq 2M
and
M_{j}\leq 2M
for all
\ell
and
M_{j}

considered.]

(5)

For each
j\in\{1,\ldots,d+e-1\}
, let

\displaystyle m^{(j)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{0\leq i\leq k_{j}}a^{(j)}_{i}T^{i}\in{\mathbb{F}}[\Lambda_{j}][Y,T],\quad w^{(j)}=-\partial m^{(j)}/\partial\Lambda_{j}\in{\mathbb{F}}[\Lambda_{j}][Y,T].

Set

\displaystyle m

\displaystyle=m^{(1)}|_{\Lambda_{1}\leftarrow 0}\in{\mathbb{F}}[Y,T],\quad v_{j}=w^{(j)}|_{\Lambda_{j}\leftarrow 0}+Y\cdot(\partial m/\partial T)\in{\mathbb{F}}[Y,T].

[Then
\boldsymbol{\lambda},m,v_{1},\ldots,v_{d+e-1}
form a geometric
solution of
V_{1}
.]

(6)

Substitute 1 for
Y
in the polynomials
m,v_{1},\ldots,v_{d+e-1}
. Let
m_{1}=m(1,T)
. Compute polynomials
u,w_{1},\ldots,w_{d+e-1}\in{\mathbb{F}}[T]
of degrees less than
\deg m_{1}

that satisfy

u\equiv(m_{1}^{\prime})^{-1}\bmod m_{1},\quad w_{j}\equiv(m_{1}^{\prime})^{-1}\cdot v_{j}(1,T)\bmod m_{1}\ (1\leq j<d+e).

[Then
\boldsymbol{\lambda},m_{1},w_{1},\ldots,w_{d+e-1}\in{\mathbb{F}}[T]
form a
geometric solution of the zero-dimensional variety

(W_{1}\times\{1\})\cap V_{1}
.]

(7)

Find a root
\eta
of
m_{1}
in some extension field
{\mathbb{K}}
of
{\mathbb{F}}
and compute

If
(g\circ h)(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j})=\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}
for
1\leq j<d+e
,
then return

else return “fail”.

We next provide some subroutines used in Algorithm
6.1
and estimate error probabilities and cost.
For the latter, we only count arithmetic operations in
{\mathbb{F}}
and ignore the (small) cost
of Boolean operations and finding random elements.
Furthermore, we use the dense representation of (multivariate)
polynomials and mainly standard procedures for simplicity, except
that we denote by
{\sf M}(n)
the cost of multiplying two

, and by

operations. Thus we

(Le Gall
[

]
), and with the notation

for real
functions

and

, we have
{\sf M}\in O\,\,{\widetilde{}}\,(n)
. Possible
algorithmic improvements are discussed in Section

.

For the analysis of the algorithm, we assume throughout that
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in{\mathbb{F}}^{d+e-1}
is generic, as required.

Lemma 6.3

One step of the Newton-Hensel iteration in step

\pi
to

\overline{{\mathbb{F}}\!}{\,}^{2(d+e-1)}
. According to (
5.2
), their geometric
degrees are bounded as
D=\deg\pi\leq\delta=\deg\Gamma_{n,d}\leq d^{d+e-1}
. In step (
2
) of Algorithm

6.1
, the quantities
M
and
N
are defined so that
M>\delta\geq D
and
N>2\delta D
. These inequalities are all that is being
used about
M
and
N
in the lemmas above, and the algorithm would
also work with
D+1
and
2\delta D+1
in their place.

Within the algorithm, we avoid using the values of
\delta
and
D
because we do not know them.
But we could start the algorithm with some small guess at those values
and then keep on doubling until we actually find a solution
(g,h)
.
The running time then is polynomial in
\delta
.

More precisely, the runtime of this variant of our algorithm is

O\,\,{\widetilde{}}\,{((d+e)^{\omega}D\delta}+(D\delta)^{\omega}+(d+e)D^{2}\delta)

plus root finding for degree at most
D
. In particular, Lemma

6.7
can be modified so that for a
lucky choice of
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
, step (
4
) can be
performed with

O\,\,{\widetilde{}}\,((d+e)D^{\omega}\delta)\subset O\,\,{\widetilde{}}\,(d^{4(d+e-1)})

operations in
{\mathbb{F}}
. We briefly discuss below this modification.

The important papers of Lecerf
[
20
]
and van der Hoeven & Lecerf
[
19
]
present
algorithms for solving systems of polynomial equations, with running
times
O\,\,{\widetilde{}}\,{(B^{3})}
and
O\,\,{\widetilde{}}\,{(B^{2})}
, respectively, where
B

is the system’s Bézout number. The values
D
and
\delta

considered above satisfy
D\leq\delta\leq B
. Our approach can
approximate
D
and
\delta
by binary search, as described. In
cases where they are considerably smaller than
B
, the running time
presented here may be smaller than that of the cited works.

The paper of
[
19
]
assumes that their elimination encounters only radical ideals
and that the polynomial system forms a regular sequence.
In our case, the latter requires a saturation with respect to the Jacobian.
This has degree up to roughly
n^{d+e}
; see Figure
3.5
.
If this is added to the equations of Problem
3.3

via the Rabinovich trick, then it will increase the Bézout number by the stated factor.
Our approach circumvents this problem by using a starting value for the Newton iteration
where the Jacobian does not vanish.

For the characteristic
p
, we only demand that it does not divide
the degree
n
of the target polynomial.
[
20
]
is stated only
for characteristic zero.

We discuss here
[
20
]
and
[
19
]
, because they yield important progress
in general. But it does not seem that they are applicable to our problem.

7.1.
Hermite–Padé approximations to perform step (
4
) of Algorithm
6.1

In this section we briefly discuss a variant to perform step
(
4
) of Algorithm
6.1
. Recall that,
in such a step, for each
j\in\{1,\ldots,d+e-1\}
we find by binary
search the smallest values
k_{j}
of
\ell
and, given
k_{j}
, of

M_{j}
for
M^{\prime}
for which

(7.1)

\sum_{0\leq i\leq\ell}a_{i}^{(j)}(L^{(j)})^{i}\equiv 0\bmod(Y^{N},\Lambda_{j}^{2})

has a solution
a_{0}^{(j)},\ldots,a_{\ell}^{(j)}\in{\mathbb{F}}[\Lambda_{j}][Y]
with
\deg_{Y}a_{i}^{(j)}\leq\delta
for
i<\ell
,

\deg_{Y}a_{\ell}^{(j)}<M^{\prime}
and
a_{\ell}^{(j)}
monic. For this
purpose, we consider (
7.1
) as a system of linear
equations for the coefficients in
{\mathbb{F}}[\Lambda_{j}]/(\Lambda_{j}^{2})
of
the
a_{i}^{(j)}
’s, and compute such a solution
a^{(j)}_{0},\ldots,a^{(j)}_{k_{j}}
with
a^{(j)}_{k_{j}}=1
monic of degree
M_{j}^{\prime}
.

To find such a solution of (
7.1
), we interpret it
as a problem of Hermite-Padé
approximation, which can be solved applying an algorithm of Bostan et al.
[
6
]
, based
on fast linear-algebra algorithms for matrices of fixed displacement
rank (cf. Bini & Pan
[
4
]
,
Pan
[
27
]
).
More precisely, for a suitable ordering of the unknowns, the matrix

B_{j}
of system (
7.1
) is a block-Toeplitz matrix
with
\ell
blocks (see Cafure et al.
[
8
, Lemma 4.3]
; see also

[
6
, Lemma 17]
).

From
[
6
, Corollary 1]
it follows that, if there exist a
nonzero solution of (
7.1
), then one such solution
can be computed with
O\,\,{\widetilde{}}\,\big(\ell^{\omega-1}{\sf M}(\ell M^{\prime})\big)
arithmetic operations in
{\mathbb{F}}
and error probability at
most
1/(2\epsilon)
for any fixed
\epsilon>0
. The algorithms of

[
6
]
work over a field. We apply their results to
computations over
{\mathbb{F}}(\!(\Lambda_{j})\!)
, but perform calculations in

{\mathbb{F}}[\![\Lambda_{j}]\!]/(\Lambda_{j}^{2})
, which is all that we need. This
works because our conditions guarantee that all divisions are by
series which are invertible in
{\mathbb{F}}[\![\Lambda_{j}]\!]/(\Lambda_{j}^{2})
.
Thus computing in
{\mathbb{F}}[\Lambda_{j}]/(\Lambda_{j}^{2})
is sufficient.
Taking into account that at most
2\log_{2}(2D)
values of
\ell

and
2\log_{2}(2\delta)
values of
M^{\prime}
occur for
1\leq j\leq d+e-1
, we have the following result.

Lemma 7.1
.

For a lucky choice of
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
, step (
4
) of
Algorithm
6.1
can be computed with

O\,\,{\widetilde{}}\,\big((d+e)D^{\omega}\delta\big)
operations in
{\mathbb{F}}
and error
probability at most
1/(2\epsilon)
for any fixed
\epsilon>0
.

Next we analyze the probability of failure of the choice of the
vector
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
.

Proposition 7.2
.

Let
\mathcal{S}\subset{\mathbb{F}}
be a finite set with at least

64\epsilon^{-1}(d+e)D^{2}(\delta+2)^{\log\delta+3}
elements. A

\boldsymbol{\lambda}
chosen uniformly at random in
\mathcal{S}^{d+e-1}
is
lucky with probability at least
1-2\epsilon
.

Proof.

Arguing as in Lemma
6.5
, we analyze the denominators
in
{\mathbb{F}}[\Lambda_{j}]
which occur when using the algorithm of

[
6
]
to solve the Hermite-Padé approximation problem
(
7.1
) for
1\leq j\leq d+e-1
. This algorithm is an
adaptation of Kaltofen’s
Leading Principal Inverse
algorithm
(Kaltofen
[
22
]
,
[
23
]
). The algorithm
performs a recursive reduction of the computation of the inverse of
a “generic-rank-profile” square input matrix

A=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}A_{1,1}&A_{1,2}\\
A_{2,1}&A_{2,2}\\
\end{array}\right)

to that of the leading principal submatrix
A_{1,1}
and its Schur
complement
\Delta=A_{2,2}-A_{2,1}A_{1,1}^{-1}A_{1,2}
. The
divisions which arise during the execution of this recursive step
are related to the computation of
A_{1,1}^{-1}
and
\Delta^{-1}

and a routine of “compression” (cf.
[
4
, Problem
2.2.11.c]
) of the generators of matrices which are obtained
as certain products involving
A_{1,1}^{-1}
,
\Delta^{-1}
,

A_{1,2}
and
A_{2,1}
. The latter in turn requires the computation
of the inverses of certain submatrices of the products under
consideration.

Each entry of the matrix
B_{j}
of the linear system
(
7.1
) is a coefficient of a power
(L^{(j)})^{i}
,
which is therefore a polynomial in
\Lambda_{j}
of degree at most

i\leq\ell
. Since the generic-rank-profile matrix
A
is obtained
by multiplying the matrix
B_{j}
with suitable matrices with entries
in
{\mathbb{F}}
, we conclude that the entries of
A_{1,1}
are polynomials
of
{\mathbb{F}}[\Lambda_{j}]
of degree at most
\ell\leq D
, while the
numerators and denominators of the entries of
\Delta^{-1}
are
polynomials of
{\mathbb{F}}[\Lambda_{j}]
of degree at most
D(\delta+2)
.
Therefore, by a simple recursive argument it can be seen that the
numerators and denominators of all leading principal submatrices and
Schur complements which are inverted during the algorithm have
degrees bounded by
\mathcal{D}=D(\delta+2)^{\log\delta}
. This in
turn implies that the denominators arising during the compression
routine have degrees bounded by
3\mathcal{D}\delta
.

Taking into account that the algorithm of
[
6
]
for each
value of
\ell
and
M^{\prime}
consists of at most
\log_{2}\delta

recursive steps, and that each recursive step requires the inversion
of at most 4 matrices, we conclude that the product of all the
denominators arising during the algorithm has degree bounded by

8\mathcal{D}\delta\log_{2}\delta
. Since at most
2\log_{2}(2D)

values of
\ell
and
2\log_{2}(2\delta)
values of
M^{\prime}
occur for

1\leq j\leq d+e-1
, we conclude that the degree of all denominators
is bounded from above by

32(d+e)\mathcal{D}\delta\log_{2}^{3}(2\delta)
.

Finally, according to Lemma
5.5
,

\boldsymbol{\lambda}
must not annihilate a polynomial
R^{(4)}
of degree

2D^{2}
. The statement of the proposition readily follows.
∎

We recall from (
4.3
) and (
4.4
) that

D\leq\delta\leq d^{d+e-1}
.
Then from the above estimate, Lemma

7.1
and the error probability

1/(2\mu)
of the algorithm underlying Lemma

7.1
, we deduce the following result.

Theorem 7.3
.

Let
d,e\geq 2
,
0<\epsilon<1
,

{\mathbb{F}}
be a field of characteristic not dividing
d
and with at least

64\epsilon^{-1}(d+e)D^{2}(\delta+2)^{\log\delta+3}
elements,
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in{\mathbb{F}}^{d+e-1}
generic, and
\boldsymbol{\beta}\in{\mathbb{F}}^{d+e-1}
. Then a
solution
(g,h)
to Problem
1.1
can be
computed with probability at least
1-\epsilon
. It uses

O\,\,{\widetilde{}}\,{((d+e)^{\omega}D^{\omega}\delta)}
operations in
{\mathbb{F}}
plus
root finding for degree at most
D
.

Open question

Our algorithm uses time polynomial in the
geometric input
size
, namely the degree of the variety
\Gamma_{n,d}
in
(
3.2
) that encodes our problem. The entries of an output

(g,h)
lie in an extension field whose degree is bounded by
D\leq d^{d+e-1}
and our runtime estimate is polynomial in this bound.
It remains open how close this bound is to the actual minimal extension degree required;
some experiments might shed light on this, but we have not done this.

One may wonder whether time polynomial in the actual output size of an instance is possible,
or whether some particularly succinct representation of the output allows a faster solution.
The special cases treated in Section
2
even allow a solution
in time polynomial in the size of the input, consisting just of the
d+e-1
field elements that
form
\alpha
and
\beta
.
This cannot be expected in general.

Another approach would be to use a numerical instead of our algebraic homotopy.
We have not investigated this.
