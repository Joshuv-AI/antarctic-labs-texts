Abstract
In previous work we showed that a loop g : S 1 → SU ⁡ ( 2 ) g\colon S^{1}\to{\rm SU}(2) has a triangular factorization if and only if the loop g g has a root subgroup factorization. In this paper we present generalizations in which the unit disk and its double, the sphere, are replaced by a based compact Riemann surface with boundary, and its double. One ingredient is the theory of generalized Fourier–Laurent expansions developed by Krichever and Novikov. We show that a SU ⁡ ( 2 ) {\rm SU}(2) valued multiloop having an analogue of a root subgroup factorization satisfies the condition that the multiloop, viewed as a transition function, defines a semistable holomorphic SL ⁡ ( 2 , ℂ ) {\rm SL}(2,\mathbb{C}) bundle. Additionally, for such a multiloop, there is a corresponding factorization for determinants associated to the spin Toeplitz operators defined by the multiloop.

(a)

(a)

Linear triangular factorization

In the remainder of the paper,
\Sigma
denotes a connected compact Riemann surface with nonempty
boundary
S
. Let
\widehat{\Sigma}

denote the double, i.e.,

\displaystyle\widehat{\Sigma}=\Sigma^{*}\circ\Sigma,

where
\Sigma^{*}
is the adjoint of
\Sigma
, the surface
\Sigma

with the orientation reversed, and the composition is sewing along
the common boundary
S
. Let

denote the antiholomorphic
involution (or reflection) fixing

The singular cohomology group

is a real symplectic vector space with respect to wedge product.
There is a positive polarization of the complexification

\displaystyle H^{1}\big(\widehat{\Sigma},\mathbb{C}\big)=H^{1,0}\oplus H^{0,1},

where we concretely identify
H^{1,0}
with the complex
\text{genus}(\widehat{\Sigma})

of holomorphic differentials on
\widehat{\Sigma}
,
and
H^{0,1}
is the conjugate vector space of antiholomorphic differentials.
In this way we can think of
H^{1,0}
as the dual of
H^{0,1}
and vice versa.
There is also a Dolbeault isomorphism

\displaystyle H^{1}\big(\mathcal{O}\big(\widehat{\Sigma}\big)\big)\to H^{0,1}\big(\widehat{\Sigma}\big),

where
\mathcal{O}(\widehat{\Sigma})
denotes the
sheaf of holomorphic functions (see
[
6
, p. 45]
, but we will spell out what we need in (
2.1
)).

Let
H^{0}(S)
denotes the space of analytic functions on
S
(hence each such function can be extended to a holomorphic function in
an open neighborhood of
S
in
\widehat{\Sigma}
).
Using Mayer–Vietoris for the sheaf of holomorphic functions, corresponding to a slight thickening of the covering
of
\widehat{\Sigma}
by
\Sigma
and
\Sigma^{*}
, there is an exact sequence of vector spaces

\displaystyle 0\to H^{0}\big(\widehat{\Sigma}\big)\to H^{0}(\Sigma)\oplus H^{0}(\Sigma^{*})\to H^{0}(S)\to H^{0,1}\big(\widehat{\Sigma}\big)\to 0.

In terms of the Dolbeault isomorphism, the connecting map
H^{0}(S)\to H^{0,1}(\widehat{\Sigma})
is realized concretely as a map

\displaystyle H^{0}(S)\to H^{0,1}\big(\widehat{\Sigma}\big)\colon\ f\to\begin{cases}\overline{\partial}f_{1}&\text{in}\ \Sigma,\\
\overline{\partial}f_{2}&\text{in}\ \Sigma^{*},\end{cases}

(2.1)

where in the

category,

,
f_{1}
is smooth in (a slight open enlargement of)
\Sigma
, and
f_{2}
is smooth in (a slight
open enlargement of)
\Sigma^{*}
.

In reference to the following statements, recall that the space of complex hyperfunctions on an oriented
analytic one-manifold is the dual
of the space of complex analytic one forms on the one-manifold, and is denoted by
\operatorname{Hyp}(S)
;
see Appendix
A
(and
[
19
]
for the original reference).
The introduction of hyperfunctions is
possibly a distraction at this point of the paper. The point is that eventually we will want to
consider ‘transition functions’ with minimal regularity; all such functions can be viewed as
hyperfunctions, and the natural domain for connecting maps, such as (

), is a space of hyperfunctions.

Lemma 2.1
.

The connecting map (
2.1
)
is dual to the injective map

\displaystyle C^{\omega}\Omega^{1}(S,\mathbb{C})\leftarrow H^{1,0}\big(\widehat{\Sigma}\big)\equiv H^{1}\big(\widehat{\Sigma},\mathbb{R}\big)\leftarrow 0

given by restriction of a holomorphic differential to
S
. Consequently the
connecting map extends continuously to a surjective map

\displaystyle\operatorname{Hyp}(S)\to H^{0,1}\big(\widehat{\Sigma}\big)\equiv H^{1}\big(\widehat{\Sigma},\mathbb{R}\big)\to 0.

Proof.

We first show that the connecting map is dual to the restriction map.
Suppose that
f\in H^{0}(S)
(i.e., functions which are holomorphic in a neighborhood of
S
) and write
f=f_{1}-f_{2}

as in the realization of the connecting map (
2.1
),
f\to\overline{\partial}f_{j}
. Suppose also that

\omega\in H^{1,0}(\widehat{\Sigma})

\displaystyle(\overline{\partial}f_{j},\omega)=(f,\omega|_{S}).

Proof.

4.1
Proof of Theorem

We recall the statement of the theorem:

Theorem 4.1
.

Suppose

. If

\displaystyle g(z)=k_{1}^{*}(z)\left(\begin{matrix}e^{\chi(z)}&0\\
0&e^{-\chi(z)}\end{matrix}\right)k_{2}(z),

where

, and
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
are as in

and
(II.1)

then

, the holomorphic
G
bundle on
\widehat{\Sigma}

defined by

as a transition function, is semistable, and the associated bundle for the defining representation
has a sub-line bundle with an antiholomorphic reflection symmetry compatible with

.

Remark 4.2
.

In the following proof,
a_{1}
is a positive constant and hence
a_{1}^{*}=a_{1}
. We will write
a_{1}^{*}
at various points in the proof because in the next subsection we will want to note that the calculations are valid more generally when

is a function.

Proof.

In the proof we will use
the fact that, using a multiloop as a transition function, there is a bijective correspondence
between the double coset space

\displaystyle H^{0}\big(\Sigma^{0*},G\big)\backslash\operatorname{Hyp}(S,G)/H^{0}\big(\Sigma^{0},G\big)

and the set of isomorphism classes
of holomorphic
G
bundles on
\widehat{\Sigma}
, and that a smooth function defines a hyperfunction; see Appendix
A.3

By assumption
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
have ‘triangular
factorizations’ of the following forms:

\displaystyle k_{1}=\left(\begin{matrix}1&0\\
y^{*}+y_{0}&1\end{matrix}\right)\left(\begin{matrix}a_{1}&0\\
0&a_{1}^{-1}\end{matrix}\right)\left(\begin{matrix}\alpha_{1}&\beta_{1}\\
\gamma_{1}&\delta_{1}\end{matrix}\right),

and

\displaystyle k_{2}=\left(\begin{matrix}1&x^{*}+x_{0}\\

\gamma_{2}&\delta_{2}\end{matrix}\right).

Given these ‘triangular factorizations’ for
k_{1}

and
k_{2}
, we can derive a ‘triangular factorization’ for
g
. (Note: we have not developed
a general theory of triangular factorization in the context of this paper, so we are using this term somewhat
loosely.) To simplify notation,
let
X=a_{2}^{-2}(x+x_{0}^{*})
,
Y=a_{1}^{2}(y+y_{0}^{*})
, and
a=a_{1}^{*}a_{2}
. Then

\displaystyle g=\left(\begin{matrix}\alpha_{1}&\beta_{1}\\
\gamma_{1}&\delta_{1}\end{matrix}\right)^{*}\left(\begin{matrix}1&Y\\
0&1\end{matrix}\right)\left(\begin{matrix}ae^{\chi_{-}+\chi_{0}+\chi_{+}}&0\\
0&(ae^{\chi_{-}+\chi_{0}+\chi_{+}})^{-1}\end{matrix}\right)\left(\begin{matrix}1&X^{*}\\
0&1\end{matrix}\right)\left(\begin{matrix}\alpha_{2}&\beta_{2}\\
\gamma_{2}&\delta_{2}\end{matrix}\right)

\displaystyle\hphantom{g}{}=\left(\begin{matrix}\alpha_{1}^{*}&\gamma_{1}^{*}\\
\beta_{1}^{*}&\delta_{1}^{*}\end{matrix}\right)\left(\begin{matrix}e^{\chi_{-}}&0\\
0&e^{-\chi_{-}}\end{matrix}\right)\left(\begin{matrix}1&e^{-2\chi_{-}}Y\\
0&1\end{matrix}\right)\left(\begin{matrix}ae^{\chi_{0}}&0\\
0&(ae^{\chi_{0}})^{-1}\end{matrix}\right)\left(\begin{matrix}1&e^{2\chi_{+}}X^{*}\\
0&1\end{matrix}\right)

\displaystyle\hphantom{g=}{}\times\left(\begin{matrix}e^{\chi_{+}}&0\\
0&e^{-\chi_{+}}\end{matrix}\right)\left(\begin{matrix}\alpha_{2}&\beta_{2}\\
\gamma_{2}&\delta_{2}\end{matrix}\right).

The bundle
E(g)
defined by

as a transition

three factors, because of the remark at the beginning of the proof. The product of the middle three factors equals

\displaystyle\left(\begin{matrix}ae^{\chi_{0}}&B\\
0&(ae^{\chi_{0}})^{-1}\end{matrix}\right),

where

\displaystyle B=a^{-1}e^{-\chi_{0}-2\chi_{-}}Y+ae^{\chi_{0}+2\chi_{+}}X^{*}.

We claim that the rank two bundle defined by this transition function is semistable. Suppose otherwise.
Then there exists a sub-line bundle which has positive degree. The degree of this sub-line bundle is the negative of
the degree of a transition function
S\to{\rm GL}(1,\mathbb{C})
(see our conventions for transition functions
in Remark
A.1
). This means that there exists a factorization of the form

\displaystyle\left(\begin{matrix}ae^{\chi_{0}}&B\\
0&(ae^{\chi_{0}})^{-1}\end{matrix}\right)=g_{-}\left(\begin{matrix}\lambda&\beta\\
0&\lambda^{-1}\end{matrix}\right)g_{+}^{-1},

where

\gamma_{\pm}&\delta_{\pm}\end{matrix}\right)

are holomorphic
{\rm SL}(2,\mathbb{C})
-valued functions in
\Sigma
(
\Sigma^{*}
, respectively),
and the degree of
\lambda\colon S\to{\rm GL}(1,\mathbb{C})
is negative. This factorization implies

\displaystyle ae^{\chi_{0}}\gamma_{-}=\lambda^{-1}\gamma_{+}

and hence the line bundle defined by the transition function

has
a global holomorphic section. But this is impossible, because the degree of this transition function is positive (i.e., the degree
of the corresponding line bundle is negative).
This implies that
E(g)
is semistable. (Note that in general, a principal bundle is semistable if and only if the
corresponding adjoint bundle is semistable; however for
{\rm SL}(2,\mathbb{C})
it suffices to consider the defining representation, see
[
17

Because
\chi_{0}=-\chi_{0}^{*}
, and because

is a positive constant, the line bundle defined by the transition function

has antiholomorphic symmetry with respect to
R
. This completes the proof.
∎

Theorem 4.3

Suppose

For

5
Spin Toeplitz operators

A spin structure for a Riemann surface is the same thing as a choice of square root for
the canonical bundle,
\kappa
(by way of explanation, holomorphic sections of
\kappa
are holomorphic differentials).
We let
\kappa^{1/2}
(
\to\Sigma,\widehat{\Sigma},\Sigma^{*}
, depending on the surface) denote the choice of square root
(see
[
20
, Chapter 7]
for other points of view). There is a natural (pre-) Hilbert space structure on

\Omega^{0}\big(S,\kappa^{1/2}\big)\otimes\mathbb{C}^{2}
(
\mathbb{C}^{2}
valued spinors along
S
) given by

\displaystyle\langle\psi,\phi\rangle=\int_{S}\left(\psi,\overline{\phi}\right)_{\mathbb{C}^{2}},

where
\left(v,\overline{w}\right)_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}
denotes the standard Hermitian inner product on
\mathbb{C}^{2}
, so that the integrand
is a one density on
S
.

For the sheaf of holomorphic sections of
\kappa^{1/2}
, and (a slight open thickening of) the covering of
\widehat{\Sigma}
by

\Sigma
and
\Sigma^{*}
, there is a Mayer–Vietoris long exact sequence,

\displaystyle 0\to H^{0}\big(\widehat{\Sigma},\kappa^{1/2}\big)\to H^{0}\big(\Sigma,\kappa^{1/2}\big)\oplus H^{0}\big(\Sigma^{*},\kappa^{1/2}\big)

\displaystyle\hphantom{0}{}\to\Omega^{0}\big(S,\kappa^{1/2}\big)\to H^{1}\big(\widehat{\Sigma},\kappa^{1/2}\big)\to 0.

In terms of the
\overline{\partial}
operator,

H^{0}\big(\widehat{\Sigma},\kappa^{1/2}\big)=\operatorname{ker}(\overline{\partial})
and
H^{1}\big(\widehat{\Sigma},\kappa^{1/2}\big)=\operatorname{coker}(\overline{\partial})
.
The index of
\overline{\partial}
is zero.

When
\overline{\partial}
is invertible, a generic condition,
by taking Hilbert space completions, we obtain a Hilbert space polarization

\displaystyle L^{2}\Omega^{1/2}(S)\otimes\mathbb{C}^{2}=H_{+}\oplus H_{-},

where
L^{2}\Omega^{1/2}(S)
,
H_{+}
, and
H_{-}
denote the completions of
\Omega^{0}\big(S,\kappa^{1/2}\big)
,
H^{0}\big(\Sigma,\kappa^{1/2}\big)\otimes\mathbb{C}^{2}
, and
H^{0}\big(\Sigma^{*},\kappa^{1/2}\big)\otimes\mathbb{C}^{2}
, respectively.
When
\overline{\partial}
is not invertible, there are two distinct reasonable polarizations. The first possibility is that
H_{+}
is the completion of
\Omega^{0}\big(S,\kappa^{1/2}\big)\otimes\mathbb{C}^{2}
, and
H_{-}
is the orthogonal complement.
The second possibility is that
H_{-}
is the completion of
H^{0}\big(\Sigma^{*},\kappa^{1/2}\big)\otimes\mathbb{C}^{2}
and
H_{+}
is the orthogonal complement.

Given an essentially bounded matrix valued function
g\colon S\to\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{C}^{2})
,
there is an associated bounded multiplication operator

M_{g}
on
\Omega^{1/2}(S)\otimes\mathbb{C}^{2}
, and relative to a polarization as in the preceding paragraphs

\displaystyle M_{g}=\left(\begin{matrix}A(g)&B(g)\\
C(g)&D(g)\end{matrix}\right).

If
\Sigma=D
, the unit disk, then
A(g)
and
B(g)
are the classical (block) Toeplitz and Hankel operators associated to the symbol
g
(see
[
3
]
and
[
12
, Chapter 3]
). In general the basic qualitative properties of the operators
A(g)
and
B(g)
(which we
refer to as ‘spin Toeplitz and Hankel operators’, respectively) are the same
as in the classical case, because the projection to
H_{+}
differs from the classical projection by a smoothing operator.
For example (of most importance for our purposes), exactly as in
[
16
, Chapter 5]
, the map
g\to M_{g}
defines an embedding of loops into the Hilbert–Schmidt general linear
group; the precise statement is that there is an embedding

\displaystyle L^{\infty}\cap W^{1/2}(S;{\rm SL}(2,\mathbb{C}))\to{\rm GL}(H_{+}\oplus H_{-})_{(2)}.

(5.1)

The relevant Lie structure is described in
[
16
, Chapters 6 and 7]
.

Remark 5.1
.

The Krichever–Novikov theory, at least in principle, provides an explicit means to calculate spin Hankel and Toeplitz operators.
According to Krichever and Novikov (following the notation in
[
11
]
), for certain spin structures and for generic basepoints, there is
an (orthonormal) basis for
\mathcal{M}^{1/2}
(meromorphic spinors which are regular in the complement of the basepoints) of the form

\displaystyle\dots,\phi_{3/2},\phi_{1/2},\phi_{-1/2}\phi_{-3/2},\dots

(which in the classical case reduces to the usual basis

\displaystyle\dots,z^{2}(dz)^{1/2},z(dz)^{1/2},z^{0}(dz)^{1/2},z^{-1}(dz)^{1/2},\dots)

a basis for
\mathcal{M}^{0}
,
\{f_{k}\colon k\in\frac{g}{2}+\mathbb{Z}\}
(which in the classical case reduces to the usual basis),
and constants
Q
such that

Proof.

5.2

Our goal is to calculate the second determinant appearing on the right-hand side of the statement
in Theorem

as a multiplication operator on scalar valued functions. We first consider the simplest possibility,

Proposition 5.6
.

Suppose that
\chi=\chi_{+}+\chi_{-}
, where
\chi_{+}
is holomorphic in
\Sigma

and
\chi_{-}
is holomorphic in
\Sigma^{*}
. Then

(a)

\det(A(e^{\chi})A(e^{-\chi}))=\exp(\operatorname{tr}(B(\chi_{+})C(\chi_{-})))
,

(b)

if

valued, then this equals

\displaystyle\exp(-\operatorname{tr}(B(\chi_{+})B(\chi_{+})^{*}))=\exp\big({-}|B(\chi_{+})|^{2}_{\mathcal{L}_{2}}\big).

In the classical case
\Sigma=D
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}(B(\chi_{+})B(\chi_{+})^{*})=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n|\chi_{n}|^{2}.

Proof.

Because
A(e^{\chi})=A(e^{\chi_{-}})A(e^{\chi_{+}})
,
\det(A(e^{\chi})A(e^{-\chi}))
equals

\displaystyle\det\big(A(e^{\chi_{-}})A(e^{\chi_{+}})A(e^{-\chi_{-}})A(e^{-\chi_{+}})\big)=\det\big(e^{A(\chi_{-})}e^{A(\chi_{+})}e^{A(-\chi_{-})}e^{A(-\chi_{+})}\big).

The Helton–Howe formula (see
[
10

) implies that this equals
\exp(\operatorname{tr}([A(\chi_{-}),A(\chi_{+})]))
.
Now observe that

\displaystyle\begin{split}[A(\chi_{-}),A(\chi_{+})]&=A(\chi_{-})A(\chi_{+})-A(\chi_{+})A(\chi_{-})\\
&=A(\chi_{-})A(\chi_{+})-\left(A(\chi_{+}\chi_{-})-B(\chi_{+})C(\chi_{-})\right)=B(\chi_{+})C(\chi_{-}).\end{split}

This implies part (a).

For part (b) the only thing we need to comment on is the last equality. This follows from writing out the matrix
for
B
, relative to the standard basis. Up to a multiple, it also follows abstractly by the
{\rm SU}(1,1)
symmetry
of

and Schur’s lemma.
∎

Remark 5.7
.

It is obvious that the formula in (a) depends on the complex structure of

. Does it depend on the spin structure?

The shortcoming of the preceding proposition is that when
\text{genus}(\widehat{\Sigma})>0
, we have not accounted for
zero modes.

Proposition 5.8
.

\displaystyle\det\big(A(e^{\chi})A(e^{-\chi})\big)=\mathbf{c}(e^{\chi_{-}},e^{\chi_{0}})\mathbf{c}(e^{\chi_{0}},e^{\chi_{+}})\det\big(\big\{e^{-A(\chi_{+})},e^{A(\chi_{-})}\big\}\big)\det\big(A(e^{\chi_{0}})A(e^{-\chi_{0}})\big).

In the case that
\chi
has values in
i\mathbb{R}
, this equals

\displaystyle\det\big(\big|\big\{A(e^{-\chi_{0}}),e^{-A(\chi_{+})}\big\}\big|^{2}\big)\det\big(A(e^{\chi_{0}})A(e^{\chi_{0}})^{*}\big)\exp\big({-}\operatorname{tr}(B(\chi_{+})B(\chi_{+})^{*})\big),

where
\{A,B\}=ABA^{-1}B^{-1}
denotes the group commutator.

Proof.

\displaystyle A(e^{\chi})A(e^{-\chi})=e^{A(\chi_{-})}A(e^{\chi_{0}})e^{A(\chi_{+})}e^{-A(\chi_{-})}A(e^{-\chi_{0}})e^{-A(\chi_{+})}.

The right-hand side is of the form
xyzx^{-1}Yz^{-1}
. The conjugate of this by
z^{-1}

equals

\displaystyle z^{-1}xyzx^{-1}Y=\big\{z^{-1}x,y\big\}y\big\{z^{-1},x\big\}y^{-1}yY,

where we have temporarily assumed that

This implies

\displaystyle\det\big(A(e^{\chi})A(e^{-\chi})\big)=\det\big(\big\{e^{-A(\chi_{+})}e^{A(\chi_{-})},A(e^{\chi_{0}})\big\}\big)

\displaystyle\hphantom{\det\big(A(e^{\chi})A(e^{-\chi})\big) =}{}\times\det\big(\big\{e^{-A(\chi_{+})},e^{A(\chi_{-})}\big\}\big)\det\big(A(e^{\chi_{0}})A(e^{-\chi_{0}})\big)

\displaystyle\det\big(\big\{e^{-A(\chi_{+})}e^{A(\chi_{-})},A(e^{\chi_{0}})\big\}\big)\exp\big({-}\operatorname{tr}(B(\chi_{+})C(\chi_{-}))\big)\det\big(A(e^{\chi_{0}})A(e^{-\chi_{0}})\big).

This implies that the first determinant in the

In the classical case this determinant is nonvanishing. It would seem unlikely

exactly.

Appendix A
Hyperfunctions and holomorphic bundles

A.1
Hyperfunctions on a circle

Suppose that
X
is an oriented analytic compact
d
-manifold. Let

C^{\omega}\Omega^{d}(X)
denote the vector space of real analytic forms of order

d
on
X
. There is a standard topology on this space.
By definition the space of (real) hyperfunctions on
X
, denoted
\operatorname{Hyp}(X;\mathbb{R})
,
is the dual of this topological vector space; the
space of complex hyperfunctions is the complexification, denoted
\operatorname{Hyp}(X;\mathbb{C})
, or more simply

\operatorname{Hyp}(X)
(see
[
9
, p. 35]
).
In the case of
X=S^{1}
, any complex hyperfunction has a unique representation of the
form

\displaystyle f=f_{-}+f_{0}+f_{+},

where
f_{-}\in H^{0}(\Delta^{*},(\infty);\mathbb{C},0)
,
f_{0}\in\mathbb{C}
, and
f_{+}\in H^{0}(\Delta,0;\mathbb{C},0)

(this is how hyperfunctions were first introduced, see
[
19
]
).
The corresponding functional is given by

\displaystyle C^{\omega}\Omega^{1}\big(S^{1}\big)\to\mathbb{C}\colon\ \omega\to\int_{|z|=1-\epsilon}(f_{0}+f_{+})\omega+\int_{|z|=1+\epsilon}f_{-}\omega,

where
\omega=gdz
and
g
is complex analytic in a collar of

S^{1}
, for sufficiently small
\epsilon
.

An integrable function
f\colon S^{1}\to\mathbb{C}
naturally defines a hyperfunction by integrating an analytic form against
f

around
S^{1}
. Note that for the linear triangular decomposition for the Fourier series of
f
, where
f_{\pm}=\sum\limits_{\pm n>0}f_{n}z^{n}
,

are not
necessarily integrable on the circle, but they do define holomorphic functions in
\Delta
(
\Delta^{*}
, respectively).

A.2
Nonabelian hyperfunctions on a circle

Suppose that
G
is a simply connected complex Lie group, e.g.,

.

In
[
16
]
it is observed that the group of analytic loops,

of the identity consists of those loops which have a

\displaystyle g=g_{-}\cdot g_{0}\cdot g_{+},

(A.1)

where
g_{-}\in H^{0}(D^{*},(\infty);G,1)
,

,

\displaystyle H^{1}(D^{*},\mathfrak{g})\times G\times H^{1}(D,\mathfrak{g}),

The hyperfunction completion,

\displaystyle H^{1}(\Delta^{*},\mathfrak{g})\times G\times H^{1}(\Delta,\mathfrak{g})

\displaystyle\operatorname{Hyp}\big(S^{1},G\big)=\lim_{r\downarrow 1}H^{0}\big(\{1<|z|<r\},G\big)\times_{H^{0}(S^{1},G)}\lim_{r\uparrow 1}H^{0}\big(\{r<|z|<1\},G\big).

(

\displaystyle\operatorname{Hyp}\big(S^{1},G\big)=\bigsqcup_{\lambda\in\operatorname{Hom}(S^{1},T)}\Sigma^{\rm hyp}_{\lambda},\qquad\Sigma_{\lambda}^{\rm hyp}=H^{0}(\Delta^{*},G)\cdot\lambda\cdot H^{0}(\Delta,G).

The space

Remark A.1

Our convention for transition functions is the following.
A (holomorphic) section of

With this convention, for scalar transition functions, the degree of a line bundle is the negative of the degree of a corresponding

(
