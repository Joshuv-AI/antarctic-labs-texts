A new proof of the Pythagorean Theorem and generalization of the usual algebra norm
Fred Greensite

Abstract
. A new proof of the Pythagorean Theorem is presented, utilizing George Birkhoff’s version of the postulates of Euclidean geometry as incorporating ℝ \mathbb{R} . Extrapolation of the mechanism of proof leads to a generalization of the usual algebra norm associated with a real finite-dimensional unital associative algebra, and resulting novel computationally accessible algebra isomorphism invariants.



Phrased in modern terms, Decartes’ introduction of the Cartesian plane as emerging from the classical Euclidean plane entails construction of an isometric isomorphism between the intrinsic vector space resulting from the Euclidean plane with a distinguished origin, and
\mathbb{R}\oplus\mathbb{R}
augmented by the inherited Euclidean notion of length (the four postulate formulation of Euclidean geometry due to George Birkhoff
[
1
]
, which fundamentally incorporates
\mathbb{R}
, provides a rigorous environment for effecting that program). The isomorphism relies on the parallel postulate or its equivalent, and an important feature is that such an isomorphism can be constructed from
any
pair of perpendicular lines through a chosen origin in the Euclidean plane. We will present a proof of the Pythagorean Theorem emerging from that feature (ultimately, the proof can be classified as being based on the concept of isotropy). The proof is believed to be new - at least the idea of this proof is not found in the large compendium
[
2
]
, the more recent survey
[
3
]
, or the present version of the Wikipedia article “Pythagorean theorem”.

The proof exploits a decomposition of the points of Euclidean space as contained in the observation that, away from the origin, a nonnegative function
\ell:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
satisfies

(0.1)

(x,y)=\ell(x,y)\nabla\ell(x,y),

and

(0.2)

\ell(\nabla\ell(x,y))=1,

if and only if

\ell(x,y)=\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}.

Thus, to prove the Pythagorean Theorem from Birkhoff’s postulates of Euclidean geometry, it is sufficient to derive (
0.1
), (
0.2
) for the implicit Euclidean norm
\ell
.

More interesting than the proof itself, presented in Section 1 (as supported by the lemmas in Section 3), is extrapolation of the basic rationale to the setting of real finite-dimensional unital associative algebras - accomplished by mimicking the decomposition (
0.1
), (
0.2
). A preliminary presentation of this extrapolation is found in Section 2. The latter ultimately leads to a generalization of the usual notion of an algebra norm, and some novel computationally accessible algebra isomorphism invariants, as fully developed in
[
4
]
. In fact, the proof in Section 1 was originally inspired by the algebraic application rather than the other way around.

1.
Preparation and proof

Euclid’s proof relies on various definitions, postulates, and specifically cited earlier propositions, tailored to the needs of a synthetic geometry proof. Our proof relies on such prior things as well, except tailored to the needs of an analytic proof. They are introduced in the following preamble, and proofs of the cited lemmas are found in Section 3.

First of all, we assume Birkhoff’s postulates of Euclidean geometry (Section 3.1), combined with an understanding of
\mathbb{R}
as in Real Analysis, along with Differential Calculus in one dimension, as well as usual notions of limits, continuity, and vector spaces.

The original motivation for the Cartesian plane was that it can provide a convenient arena for computations relating to features of the classical Euclidean plane. These are often based on the nonnegative “Euclidean length function”
\ell
which specifies the length of a line segment from a distinguished (fixed) origin
O
to any other point
P
of the Euclidean plane (i.e., the Euclidean norm). Using parallelogram constructions, along with dilations/reflections of any line segment based at
O
, resulting from “multiplication” by members of
\mathbb{R}
, the Euclidean plane with origin is easily made into a vector space (Definition
3.1
). According to Lemma
3.1
, there is an isometric isomorphism between the Euclidean vector space and
\mathbb{R}\oplus\mathbb{R}
inheriting
\ell
, resulting from any two perpendicular lines through the
O
(this relies on the Postulate of Similarity, Section 3.1).

Now define a “direction” to be any point
C
of the unit circle around the origin. The directional derivative of
\ell
at
P
in reference to a given direction
C
can be defined in terms of the labeling of
O,P,C
by
(0,0),(x,y),(u,v)\in\mathbb{R}\oplus\mathbb{R}
as determined by the above isomorphism resulting from a selected pair of perpendicular lines intersecting at
O
(“a pair of orthogonal coordinate axes through the origin”). Thus, we let
D\ell(x,y;u,v)
indicate the above directional derivative value according to the usual definition based on vector space operations taking place in a one-dimensional subset of
\mathbb{R}\oplus\mathbb{R}
(Definition
3.2
). This value for the directional derivative of
\ell
at
P
in the direction
C
is invariant under a change to a different pair of orthogonal coordinate axes through the origin. That is because the spaces defined by these alternative sets of coordinate axes, each being isometrically isomorphic to the above Euclidean plane with distinguished origin vector space (“the Euclidean plane vector space”), are thus isometrically isomorphic to each other.
D\ell(P;C)
, as the directional derivative at
P
in direction
C
with respect to the Euclidean plane vector space, is thereby unambiguously defined.

Using the triangle inequality, Lemma
3.2
establishes that all directional derivatives of
\ell(x,y)
exist at
(x,y)\neq(0,0)
. Lemma
3.3
further proves that
\ell(x,y)
is continuously differentiable at
(x,y)\neq(0,0)
.
Lemma
3.3
might be considered the main subtlety of our program, as there is no prior analytic expression for the length of an arbitrary line segment, so the two dimensional continuity must be established using a “one-dimensional” argument. Its proof relies on the degree-1 positive homogeneity of
\ell(x,y)
, which (given the isomorphism) is an immediate consequence of the Postulate of Line Measure (Section 3.1).

Theorem 1.1

(
\Pi\Upsilon\Theta\text{A}\Gamma\text{OPA}\Sigma
)
.

The area of the square on the side of a right triangle opposite the right angle is equal to the sum of the areas of the squares on the other two sides.

Proof.

As explicitly derived in the proof of Lemma
3.4
, the Mean Value Theorem of one dimensional Calculus and the fact that
\ell(x,y)
is continuously differentiable at
(x,y)\neq(0,0)
together indicate that

(1.1)

D\ell(x,y;u,v)=D\ell(x,y;1,0)\,u+D\ell(x,y;0,1)\,v=\frac{\partial\ell(x,y)}{\partial x}u+\frac{\partial\ell(x,y)}{\partial y}v,

so that
\frac{\partial\ell(x,y)}{\partial x}
and
\frac{\partial\ell(x,y)}{\partial y}
are the directional derivatives of
\ell
at
(x,y)\neq(0,0)
, with respect to the directions associated with the first and second coordinate axes of an orthogonal coordinate system (these directions being determined by the direction of increasing values of
\mathbb{R}
on the respective coordinate axes). These coordinate axes result in a first isometric isomorphism according to Lemma
3.1
.

Thus, for fixed
(x,y)\neq(0,0)
,
D\ell(x,y;u,v)
is a linear functional for
(u,v)
now allowed to vary over all of
\mathbb{R}\oplus\mathbb{R}
. Evidently, this linear functional is the dual vector of
\left(\frac{\partial\ell(x,y)}{\partial x},\frac{\partial\ell(x,y)}{\partial y}\right)
. It follows that
D\ell(P;C)
is a linear functional for fixed
P\neq O
, with
C
now varying throughout the Euclidean plane vector space. In view of the isomorphism, the dual of this linear functional is the point
G
of the Euclidean plane vector space labeled by
\left(\frac{\partial\ell(x,y)}{\partial x},\frac{\partial\ell(x,y)}{\partial y}\right)
(i.e., the isometric isomorphism derived in Lemma
3.1
maps vectors to vectors and dual vectors to dual vectors). The point
G
as obtained from
D\ell(x,y;u,v)
via the isometric isomorphism is thereby invariant under a change to an alternative pair of orthogonal coordinate axes through the origin since, as observed in the penultimate paragraph prior to the theorem statement, the linear functional
D\ell(P;C)
as determined by
D\ell(x,y;u,v)
is invariant under this change.

Consequently, we can choose an alternative “second” pair of orthogonal coordinate axes through the origin (with coordinate axes and components of points distinguished by the symbol
′
), resulting in a second isometric isomorphism, such that
P
is on the positive portion of the first coordinate axis of the new coordinate system, so that the Eucldean plane vector space point
P
originally labeled by
(x,y)
is now labeled by
(x^{\prime},0)
, with
x^{\prime}>0
, and
G
is now labeled by
\left(\frac{\partial\ell(x^{\prime},0)}{\partial x^{\prime}},\frac{\partial\ell(x^{\prime},0)}{\partial y^{\prime}}\right)\equiv\big(D\ell(x^{\prime},0;1,0),D\ell(x^{\prime},0;0,1\big)
. But,

(1.2)

\left(\frac{\partial\ell(x^{\prime},0)}{\partial x^{\prime}},\frac{\partial\ell(x^{\prime},0)}{\partial y^{\prime}}\right)=(1,0).

To see this, first observe that
\frac{\partial\ell(x^{\prime},0)}{\partial x^{\prime}}\equiv D\ell(x^{\prime},0;1,0)=1
is an easy calculation from (
3.3
), since it only involves evaluation of
\ell
on the
x^{\prime}
-coordinate axis. Next recall that
\frac{\partial\ell(x^{\prime},0)}{\partial y^{\prime}}\equiv D\ell(x^{\prime},0;0,1)
. Writing (
1.1
) with respect to the
x^{\prime},y^{\prime}
coordinate system, we have
D\ell(x^{\prime},0;0,-1)=-D\ell(x^{\prime},0;0,1)
. And yet, according to (
3.3
),
D\ell(x^{\prime},0;0,1)=D\ell(x^{\prime},0;0,-1)
because, using the side-angle-side congruence,

\ell\big((x^{\prime},0)+\epsilon(0,1)\big)=\ell\big((x^{\prime},0)+\epsilon(0,-1)\big).

It follows that
D\ell(x^{\prime},0;0,1)=0
. Thus,
\frac{\partial\ell(x^{\prime},0)}{\partial y^{\prime}}=0
, and (
1.2
) is established.

We now observe that the right-hand-side of (
1.2
) is a point on the unit circle, and this point is evidently a positive multiple of
(x^{\prime},0)
(our new label for
P
). An isometric isomorphism maps the unit circle to the unit circle. Since the left-hand-side of (
1.2
) labels
G
under our second isometric isomorphism, we then see that
G
is a point on the unit circle, i.e.,
\ell(G)=1
. But with respect to our first orthogonal coordinate system, our first isometric isomorphism labels
G
as
\left(\frac{\partial\ell(x,y)}{\partial x},\frac{\partial\ell(x,y)}{\partial y}\right)
. To be consistent with the result of the second isomorphism, the first isomorphism must also label
G
by a point on the unit circle. That is,

(1.3)

\ell\left(\frac{\partial\ell(x,y)}{\partial x},\frac{\partial\ell(x,y)}{\partial y}\right)=1.

As noted above, (
1.2
) also indicates that, under our second isometric isomorphism,
G
(labeled by
\left(\frac{\partial\ell(x^{\prime},0)}{\partial x^{\prime}},\frac{\partial\ell(x^{\prime},0)}{\partial y^{\prime}}\right)
) is in fact labeled by a positive multiple of
(x^{\prime},0)
(i.e,
G
is labeled by a point on the ray from the origin through
(x^{\prime},0)
). With respect to our first isometric isomorphism, this means that
\left(\frac{\partial\ell(x,y)}{\partial x},\frac{\partial\ell(x,y)}{\partial y}\right)
is a positive multiple of
(x,y)
.
From the Postulate of Line Measure,
\ell
is a nonnegative degree-1 positive homogeneous function (i.e.,
\ell(\alpha(x,y))=\alpha\ell(x,y)\geq 0
, for
\alpha>0
). Consequently, with respect to the first pair of orthogonal coordinate axes through the origin, we have

(1.4)

\left(\frac{\partial\ell(x,y)}{\partial x},\frac{\partial\ell(x,y)}{\partial y}\right)=\left(\frac{x}{\ell(x,y)},\frac{y}{\ell(x,y)}\right),

since the right-hand-side (uniquely) is both a point on the unit circle while also being a positive multiple of
(x,y)
- as are now seen to be required for a labeling of
G
via an isometric isomorphism (the right-hand-side is obviously a positive multiple of
(x,y)
, and applying
\ell
to it followed by invocation of the homogeneity condition indicates satisfaction of (
1.3
)).

As regards (
1.4
), one-dimensional indefinite integration (anti-differentiation) can be applied to the equality of the first coordinates, and then one-dimensional indefinite integration can be independently applied to the equality of the second coordinates. Reconciliation of the two resulting expressions for
\ell(x,y)
in the context (
1.3
) and the required nonnegativity of
\ell
quickly leads to
\ell(x,y)=\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}
, from which the Pythagorean Theorem easily follows via an argument involving parallelograms to measure lengths of arbitrary line segments using
\ell
. ∎

2.
The proof’s structure leads to a generalization of the usual norm associated with a unital associative algebra and novel isomorphism invariants

Surprisingly, the form of the argument presented in this proof of the Pythagorean Theorem is quite robust as regards its potential for generalization. The climax of the proof centers on (
1.4
) and (
1.3
), which can be recast as,

(2.1)

s=\ell(s)\nabla\ell(s),

and,

(2.2)

\ell(\nabla\ell(s))=1,

for
s\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
not at the origin (with
n=2
, though we will not use that restriction going forward), where “
\nabla\ell(s)
” is simply shorthand for the ordered
n
-tuple of coordinate-wise one-dimensional derivatives as in the proof (and we assume the standard basis for
\mathbb{R}^{n}
throughout this discussion). Indeed, solution of (
2.1
) and (
2.2
) by themselves yields the Euclidean norm. But (
2.2
) is itself just a consequence of (
2.1
) and degree-1 positive homogeneity of
\ell(s)
(just apply
\ell
to both sides of (
2.1
) and invoke the homogeneity condition). And although (
2.1
) is derived from the postulates of Euclidean geometry, that equation is appealing on its own since given the degree-1 positive homogeneity constraint it seems to be a “simplest” expression for a relationship between
s
and
\ell(s)
. That is, the Euler Homogeneous Function Theorem states that

s\cdot\nabla\ell(s)=\ell(s)
(once again, “
\nabla\ell(s)
” is simply shorthand for the ordered
n
-tuple of coordinate-wise one-dimensional derivatives). For
\alpha>0
, replacing
s
by
\alpha s
then leads to

\alpha s\cdot\nabla\ell(\alpha s)=\ell(\alpha s)=\alpha\ell(s)=\alpha s\cdot\nabla\ell(s),

where
\nabla\ell(\alpha s)
means that
\nabla\ell
is evaluated at
\alpha s
. Equality of the above left-hand-side expression with the above right-hand-side expression implies,

\nabla\ell(\alpha s)=\nabla\ell(s).

So, although
s
and
\nabla\ell(s)
are both members of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, they behave very differently when
s
is replaced by
\alpha s
. On the other hand, for degree-1 positive homogeneous
\ell
, the expressions
s
and
\ell(s)\nabla\ell(s)

do
behave the same way when
s
is replaced by
\alpha s
(each expression simply being multiplied by
\alpha
). So what could be more natural than linking them via (
2.1
) (and thus forwarding the Euclidean norm)?

But without unduly compromising the simplicity of the rationale,
we could alternatively propose that (
2.1
) be replaced by

(2.3)

Ls=\ell(s)\nabla\ell(s),

for a linear transformation
L:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
, since we would still have
Ls
and
\ell(s)\nabla\ell(s)
behaving the same when
s
is replaced by
\alpha s
,
\alpha>0
. Applying the dot product with
s
to both sides of (
2.3
), the Euler Homogeneous Function Theorem then implies

(2.4)

s^{T}Ls=s\cdot Ls=\ell^{2}(s),

where
s^{T}
is the dual vector of
s
. According to (
2.3
),
L
must be a real symmetric matrix since
Ls
is a gradient (i.e., the right-hand-side of (
2.3
) is equal to
\nabla[\frac{1}{2}\ell^{2}(s)]
). According to (
2.4
),
L
must be positive semi-definite. From the Polarization Identity, it is seen that the above leads to arbitrary inner product spaces on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
- a simple generalization.

However, the above argument can easily be further generalized to the task of attaching a “norm” to the units of a unital associative algebra whose vector space of elements is
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. That is, the basic rationale of the argument is easily modified to allow the new feature of the inverse of an element to influence selection of this norm - something not explicitly addressed by the well-recognized “usual norm” of a finite-dimensional associative algebra as the determinant of an element’s image under the left regular representation (e.g., the algebra’s norm as defined by Bourbaki
[
5
]
).

Thus, we wish to exploit the existence of an element’s inverse to aid in construction of a “norm-like” real degree-1 positive homogeneous function, again denoted as
\ell(s)
, on the units
s
of the algebra. By analogy with the vector space argument in the first two paragraphs of this section, one recognizes that
s^{-1}
and
\nabla\ell(s)
are members of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
if
s
is a unit of the algebra, and
\nabla\ell(\alpha s)=\nabla\ell(s)
for
\alpha>0
if degree-1 positive homogeneity is again mandated. While the expressions
s^{-1}
and
\nabla\ell(s)
thereby behave differently when
\alpha s
replaces
s
, this time it is
s^{-1}
and
\frac{\nabla\ell(s)}{\ell(s)}
that behave the same under that replacement, in that both expressions are simply multiplied by
\frac{1}{\alpha}
- mirroring the situation that pertains to the first paragraph of this section. So now, based on that latter treatment (and its “minimalist” approach for attaching a norm to a vector space), we might be tempted to equate
s^{-1}
and
\frac{\nabla\ell(s)}{\ell(s)}
- except that the latter is the gradient of
\log\ell(s)
, while
s^{-1}
is in general not a gradient (i.e., in the context where the vector space of elements
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is taken to be Euclidean). That problem resolves if we can find a symmetric matrix
L:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
Ls^{-1}

is
a gradient, i.e., satisfies the exterior derivative condition,

(2.5)

d\left([Ls^{-1}]\cdot ds\right)=d\left([s^{-1}]^{T}Lds\right)=0.

The collection of matrices satisfying (
2.5
) is an abelian group under addition - in fact, a vector space. A member
L
of this space is called an “uncurling metric”. This allows us to propose,

(2.6)

Ls^{-1}=\|{\bf 1}\|^{2}\frac{\nabla\ell(s)}{\ell(s)}=\left(\frac{\|{\bf 1}\|^{2}}{\ell(s)}\right)\nabla\ell(s),

where the multiplicative identity of the algebra is denoted by
{\bf 1}
, and
\|{\bf 1}\|^{2}\equiv{\bf 1}\cdot{\bf 1}
(again, the standard basis assumed throughout this discussion). The reason for introducing
\|{\bf 1}\|^{2}
will be made clear shortly. Given the inversion operation
s\rightarrow s^{-1}
and an uncurling metric
L
, one can provide the associated “unital norm”
\ell(s)
by integrating (
2.6
), at least in a simply connected neighborhood of
\mathbf{1}
that excludes non-units. The collection of an algeba’s unital norms is an abelian group with respect to product defined by the usual pointwise multiplication of functions. Note that the pairing of
L
with its associated
\ell(s)
characterizes the algebra’s multiplicative inversion operation,
s\rightarrow s^{-1}
, in the portion of the above neighborhood of
\mathbf{1}
that is orthogonal to the kernel of
L
.

But suppose we additionally require
\ell(s)
to be a degree-1 positive homogeneous function (as is an inner product space norm). In that case, the Euler Homogenous Function Theorem indicates that application of the dot product with
s
to both sides of (
2.6
) yields,

(2.7)

s^{T}Ls^{-1}=s\cdot Ls^{-1}=\|{\bf 1}\|^{2}.

Note that (
2.7
) must hold if
s
is replaced by
s^{-1}
as both are units, so on that basis alone it would already be expected that
L
must be symmetric. The format of (
2.7
) then suggests that
L
be considered a scalar product matrix. Under that interpretation, the meaning of constraint (
2.7
) is that multiplicative inverses
s,s^{-1}
must also behave inversely as vector space members with respect to scalar product
L
. We call
L
satisfying (
2.5
) and (
2.7
) a “normalized uncurling metric”, and call the associated
\ell(s)
a “special unital norm”. It is not hard to demonstrate that such a unital norm really is special, since it is not only degree-1 positive homogeneous but also respects the multiplicative inversion operation, i.e.,

\ell(s^{-1})=\big(\ell(s)\big)^{-1}.

Equation (
2.6
) is analogous to (
2.3
) (which we emphasize with the format of the second equality in (
2.6
)), and (
2.7
) is analogous to (
2.4
). But the marked difference is that for the “naked” vector space application (where (
2.3
) and (
2.4
) pertain),
L
can be any appropriately dimensioned real symmetric matrix (allowing for possibly complex-valued
\ell(s)
), while in the unital algebra application (where (
2.6
) and (
2.7
) pertain) the additionally required (
2.5
) very significantly constrains real symmetric
L
. It is this constraint (which allows for the further constraint (
2.7
)) that makes things interesting, because different algebras have very different uncurling metric groups, and these differences distinguish the algebras in novel ways. In particular, various features of an algebra’s group of uncurling metrics (a vector space) represent algebra isomorphism invariants. One of these invariants is simply the dimension of that space.

These novel isomorphism invariants are potentially significant since the goal of classifying algebras up to isomorphism equivalence is a so-called wild problem, e.g.,
[
6
,
7
,
8
]
(greatly contrasting with the isomorphism classification problem for the quadratic spaces introduced in the second paragraph of this section, which is completely solved by computing matrix signatures). That is, those invariants could be used to fashion coarser but more tractable algebra classification systems.

Finally, this program generalizes the usual algebra norm (the determinant of the element under the left regular representation) since the latter is closely related to a special case of what has been presented here. This can begin to be appreciated by observing that if the algebra in question is
M_{n}(\mathbb{R})
(keeping in mind that a real
n
-dimensional associative algebra is isomorphic to a subalgebra of
M_{n}(\mathbb{R})
), and
L
is taken to be the matrix transpose operation, then a (ultimately, the) solution to (
2.6
), (
2.7
) is given by
\ell(s)=[\text{det}(s)]^{\frac{1}{n}}
, where det
(s)
is the determinant of
s
. This is an easy consequence of Jacobi’s formula.

So, the following summarize some of the reasons for bothering with all of this.

•

The group of characterizations of an algebra’s multiplicative inversion operation
s\rightarrow s^{-1}
consisting of each pairing of an uncurling metric
L
with its associated unital norms
\ell(s)
via (
2.6
), leads to a generalization of the algebra norm.

•

Novel isomorphism invariants can be derived from the above group, and some of these invariants are always computationally straightforward.

•

The above invariants impact not only the question of whether two algebras are isomorphic but (in the context of a particular functor) also the more general question of whether two algebras are related by an epimorphism.

•

For some purposes, a parametrized symmetric matrix depiction of the group of uncurling metrics leads to an alternative to the explicitly ideal-based expression of algebra structure.

The above are developed in
[
4
]
, which also presents an alternative motivation to the one we have used here.

(u,v)

3.1.
Birkhoff’s postulates for plane Euclidean geometry, and the resulting “Euclidean plane with distinguished origin” as a vector space, with a homogeneous length function

Slightly reworded, Birkhoff’s postulates
[
1
]
entail the following.

(1)

“Postulate of Line Measure”: There is a bijection between
\mathbb{R}
and the set of points on any line, so that the length of any line segment is the absolute value of the difference of the real numbers corresponding to the segment’s endpoints.

(2)

“Point-Line Postulate”: There is one and only one line that contains any two given distinct points of the plane.

(3)

“Postulate of Angle Measure”: There is a bijection between the set of rays from a point
O
and the real numbers
(\mbox{mod}\,2\pi)
such that, for a line not containing
O
, continuously varying a point on the line implies continuous variation of the bijection-assigned real number value of the ray it lies on. The angle formed by a first ray from
O
assigned real value
\alpha_{1}
and a second ray from
O
assigned real value
\alpha_{2}
, is
\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2}
(mod
2\pi
).

(4)

“Postulate of Similarity”: If a triangle has two sides having lengths related to the lengths of two sides of another triangle by the same positive proportionality constant, and the absolute values of the angles formed by these two sides of each triangle are equal, then these are similar triangles, i.e., they have the same three angles up to a sign, and the lengths of the third side of the two triangles are related by the same proportionality constant.

Regarding the identification of points of the plane with points of
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
, we’ll let
[
1
]
speak for itself: “We are now prepared to define a rectangular coordinate system
(x,y)
with axes the directed perpendicular lines
Ox,Oy
intersecting in the origin
O
. Choose a system of numeration along
Ox,Oy
so that
O
is marked
0
on both lines, and the numbers increase algebraically in the positive direction. Drop the unique perpendiculars from
P
to
Ox
and
Oy

respectively meeting these lines at
Px
and
Py
. The rectangular coordinates of
P
are then the numbers

x_{P}
and
y_{P}
attached to
Px
and
Py
respectively. These are evidently
uniquely determined numbers.” This bijection, explicitly derived in
[
1
]
, is a straightforward consequence of Playfair’s “parallel axiom” (Theorem IX in
[
1
]
) - which follows from the Postulate of Similarity without use of the Pythagorean Theorem.

Birkhoff’s formulation of Euclidean geometry makes intrinsic use of
\mathbb{R}
. Accordingly, we take
\mathbb{R}
to be as understood in Real Analysis, and we also assume usual notions of limits and continuity, as well as Differential Calculus in one dimension.

We can now fashion a vector space with a nonnegative length function (i.e., norm) out of the Euclidean plane with origin
O
.

Definition 3.1
.

The Euclidean plane vector space is constituted as follows:

(1)

There is a chosen point
O
of the Euclidean plane termed “the origin”.

(2)

There is a length function
\ell
such that for
P
in the Euclidean plane we have
\ell(P)
as the length of the line segment from the origin to
P
, and we say that
P
has length
\ell(P)
.

(3)

For
P,P^{\prime}
in the Euclidean plane vector space that are not collinear with the origin
O
, we use the “parallel postulate” (Birkhoff’s Theorem IX
[
1
]
) to define
P+P^{\prime}
to be a third point
P^{\prime\prime}
in the Euclidean plane vector space as the point of intersection of two unique lines, the first line being the line through
P
parallel to the line segment from the origin to
P^{\prime}
, and the second line being the line through
P^{\prime}
parallel to the line segment from the origin to
P
. The case of points
O,P,P^{\prime}
being collinear is addressed using the Postulate of Line Measure: If
P
and
P^{\prime}
do not lie on the same ray from
O
, then
P^{\prime\prime}
has length given by the absolute value of the difference of the lengths of the two points, and resides on the ray from the origin through the point
P
or
P^{\prime}
as determined by which point has the greater length. If
P
and
P^{\prime}
do lie on the same ray from
O
, then
P^{\prime\prime}
has length equal to the sum of the lengths of
P
and
P^{\prime}
, and lies on the ray from the origin on which
P
and
P^{\prime}
lie.

(4)

Multiplication of a point
P
by
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
is defined so that, for
\alpha\neq 0
and
P
not at the origin,
\alpha P
is one of two points on the line containing the origin and
P
that have length
|\alpha|\ell(P)
. If
\alpha>0
, the point
\alpha P
is such that
P
and
\alpha P
lie on the same ray from the origin, and the new point is referred to as “parallel” to
P
. For
\alpha<0
, the point
\alpha P
is such that
P
and
\alpha P
do not lie on the same ray from the origin, and the new point is referred to as “antiparallel” to
P
. For
\alpha=0
, or if
P
is the origin, the point
\alpha P
is the origin.

It is easy to show that the Euclidean plane vector space as defined above is in fact a real vector space. Also, it follows from (2) and (4) in the above definition that
\ell
is a nonnegative degree-1 positive homogeneous function.

3.2.
The isometric isomorphism between the Euclidean plane vector space and
\mathbb{R}\oplus\mathbb{R}
with the inherited Euclidean length function

Lemma 3.1
.

Consider the bijection between the members of the Euclidean plane vector space and the members of
\mathbb{R}\oplus\mathbb{R}
, that results from a pair of orthogonal coordinate axes through the distinguished origin in the Euclidean plane. Suppose
\mathbb{R}\oplus\mathbb{R}
is endowed with a length function similarly notated to that of the Euclidean plane vector space, i.e.,
\ell:\mathbb{R}\oplus\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, is such that
\ell(x,y)\equiv\ell(P_{x,y})
where
P_{x,y}
is the member of the Euclidean plane vector space corresponding to
(x,y)
under the bijection. Then the bijection is an isometric isomorphism.

Proof.

Using the bijection referred to in the theorem statement, we can denote points of the Euclidean plane vector space as
P_{x,y}
, with
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}\oplus\mathbb{R}
. From the structure of the Euclidean plane vector space (Definition
3.1
), we can write
P_{x,y}=P_{x,0}+P_{0,y}
. Consequently,
P_{x_{1},y_{1}}+P_{x_{2},y_{2}}=P_{x_{1},0}+P_{0,y_{1}}+P_{x_{2},0}+P_{0,y_{2}}=P_{x_{1}+x_{2},0}+P_{0,y_{1}+y_{2}}=P_{x_{1}+x_{2},y_{1}+y_{2}}
. Notating the bijection as
B
, we then have

(3.1)

\displaystyle B(P_{x_{1},y_{1}}+P_{x_{2},y_{2}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle B(P_{x_{1}+x_{2},y_{1}+y_{2}})=(x_{1}+x_{2},y_{1}+y_{2})=(x_{1},y_{1})+(x_{2},y_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle B(P_{x_{1},y_{1}})+B(P_{x_{2},y_{2}}).

Furthermore, for
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

(3.2)

B(\alpha P_{x,y})=B(P_{\alpha x,\alpha y})=(\alpha x,\alpha y)=\alpha(x,y)=\alpha B(P_{x,y}),

where the first equality follows from the Postulate of Similarity.
Equations (
3.1
) and (
3.2
) indicate that
B
is a linear isomorphism from the Euclidean plane vector space to
\mathbb{R}\oplus\mathbb{R}
. By assumption,
\ell(B(P_{x,y}))=\ell(x,y)=\ell(P_{x,y})
. Consequently,
B
is an isometric isomorphism.
∎

Henceforth, when we write
\mathbb{R}\oplus\mathbb{R}
it is assumed to be the indicated vector space endowed with the inherited Euclidean length function
\ell
as in Lemma
3.1
.

3.3.
The length function
\ell(x,y)
is continuously differentiable on domains excluding
(0,0)

As indicated prior to the statement of Theorem
1.1
, a “direction” in the Euclidean plane with origin
O
is a point on the unit circle centered at
O
. Thus, a “direction”
(u,v)
in
\mathbb{R}\oplus\mathbb{R}
is a point on the corresponding unit circle, i.e.,
\ell(u,v)=1
.

Definition 3.2
.

The value of the
directional derivative of the length function

\ell:\mathbb{R}\oplus\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
at a point
(x,y)\neq(0,0)
with respect to direction
(u,v)
is given by

(3.3)

D\ell(x,y;u,v)\equiv\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\frac{\ell\big((x,y)+\epsilon(u,v)\big)-\ell(x,y)}{\epsilon}.

Lemma 3.2
.

Directional derivatives of
\ell
in all directions exist at each point exclusive of the origin.

Proof.

Using a parallelogram construction, the length of any line segment can be expressed using
\ell
and vector subtraction. That is, for two points
\mathbf{p},\mathbf{p}^{\prime}
in the Cartesian plane, the length of the line segment with endpoints
\mathbf{p},\mathbf{p}^{\prime}
is
\ell(\mathbf{p}^{\prime}-\mathbf{p})
.
The continuity of
\ell
immediately follows from the “reverse triangle inequality”
|\ell(\mathbf{p}^{\prime})-\ell(\mathbf{p})|\leq\ell(\mathbf{p}^{\prime}-\mathbf{p})
(a consequence of the triangle inequality).

Birkhoff’s Postulate of Line Measure assures that every non-origin point is a positive multiple of a direction.
Thereby, evaluation of (
3.3
) immediately indicates that the directional derivative exists in the directions parallel and antiparallel to
(x,y)\neq(0,0)
, and these values are respectively
+1
and
-1
.
Thus, consider the directional derivative in some other direction. We assert that the expression inside the limit in (
3.3
) is monotonic as a function of
\epsilon
. If that were not the case, since
\ell
is continuous, there would have to be two choices of
\epsilon
such that the expressions inside that limit are equal. We can call these choices
\epsilon^{\prime}
and
\frac{1}{\alpha}\epsilon^{\prime}
, with
\alpha\neq 1
. Suppose
\alpha>0
. Then since
\ell(\beta(x,y))=|\beta|\ell(x,y)
,
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
(from the Postulate of Line Measure), simple manipulation of the equality of those two expressions resulting from
\epsilon^{\prime}
and
\frac{1}{\alpha}\epsilon^{\prime}
leads to,

(3.4)

\left|\ell\big((x,y)+\epsilon^{\prime}(u,v)\big)-\ell\big(\alpha(x,y)+\epsilon^{\prime}(u,v)\big)\right|=|1-\alpha|\ell(x,y)\\
=\ell\left((1-\alpha)(x,y)\right)=\ell\Big([(x,y)+\epsilon^{\prime}(u,v)]-[\alpha(x,y)+\epsilon^{\prime}(u,v)]\Big).

But according to the reverse triangle inequality applied to the triangle with vertices given by
(0,0)
,
(x,y)+\epsilon^{\prime}(u,v)
, and
\alpha(x,y)+\epsilon^{\prime}(u,v)
, the left-hand-side of (
3.4
) must be strictly less than the right-hand-side, since
\alpha\neq 1
, and
(0,0)
,
(u,v)
, and
(x,y)
are not collinear by assumption. Thus, (
3.4
) is not possible, so the above monotonicity assertion is verified for
\alpha>0
. A very similar argument, except directly using the triangle inequality rather than the reverse triangle inequality, can be employed for the case
\alpha<0
.

Furthermore, the triangle inequality indicates that the expression inside the limit in (
3.3
) is bounded by the values of the the directional derivatives of
\ell
in the directions parallel and antiparallel to
(x,y)
. A monotone convergence argument then establishes the existence of the directional derivative.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

The directional derivative of
\ell
in a given fixed direction as a function over the points of the plane is continuous at each point exclusive of the origin.

Proof.

From the existence of directional derivatives in all directions at
(x,y)\neq(0,0)
(Lemma
3.2
), and the degree-1 positive homogeneity of
\ell
, it easily follows from the directional derivative expression (
3.3
) that a fixed-direction directional derivative of
\ell
at any point distinct from the origin depends only on the ray on which the point lies (i.e., it is constant on a ray). This means that to demonstrate continuity of the directional derivative at
(x,y)\neq(0,0)
, the argument need only evaluate the limit of the difference of the directional derivatives at
(x,y)
and
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
as the ray on which the latter point lies approaches the ray on which the former point lies - so it is sufficient to examine the case where
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
approaches
(x,y)
along the circle with center
(0,0)
containing
(x,y)
.
In fact, the derivative is continuous at
(x,y)
if and only if it is continuous at the point of the unit circle
\frac{(x,y)}{\ell(x,y)}
.

Thus, without loss we can assume that
(x,y)
and
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
are both points on the unit circle centered at the origin, so that it is then sufficient to show that the difference of their directional derivatives in a fixed direction
(u,v)
tends to zero as
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
approaches
(x,y)
along the unit circle. That is, we only need to show that the directional derivative in the fixed
(u,v)
-direction is a continuous function over the points of the unit circle. But, the directional derivative in a direction
(u,v)
for a point
(x,y)
on the unit circle depends only on the angle formed by
(x,y)
and
(u,v)
, since the directional derivative is coordinate system-independent, and the latter vectors both have unit length. Hence, the following symmetry pertains: demonstrating continuity of the directional derivative in a fixed direction as a function over all points of the unit circle, is equivalent to demonstrating continuity of the directional derivative taken at a fixed point of the unit circle as a function over all directions.

To demonstrate continuity of the latter function, we invoke Proposition 24 of book 1 of Euclid’s Elements, which states that if two sides of one triangle are congruent to two sides of a second triangle, and the [absolute value of the] included angle of the first triangle is larger than the [absolute value of the] included angle of the second triangle, then the third side of the first triangle is longer than the third side of the second triangle. Using Birkhoff’s Postulate of Angle Measure, we now label the rays from the origin via
\theta\in(-\pi,\pi]
, with
\theta=0
labeling the ray containing
(x,y)
. Applying the above Proposition 24 to the directional derivative expression (
3.3
), it is easily seen that the directional derivative at
(x,y)
as a function of the direction labeled by
\theta
is monotonic increasing for
\theta\in(-\pi,0]
and monotonic decreasing for
\theta\in[0,\pi]
. As a simple consequence of Darboux’s Theorem, this indicates that the directional derivative is continuous on each of these intervals. Since these agree on the overlap at
\theta=0
(where the directional derivative takes the value 1), the continuity of the directional derivative taken at a fixed point of the unit circle as a function over all directions is established. In view of the statement at the end of the last paragraph, this establishes the continuity of the directional derivative taken in a fixed direction over the non-origin points of the plane.
∎

3.4.
The directional derivative
D\ell(x,y;u,v)
has the form prescribed in the first sentence of the proof of Theorem
1.1

Suppose non-origin point
P
in the Euclidean plane vector space is labeled by
(x,y)
via the isometric isomorphism with the Cartesian coordinate plane. Lemma
3.2
states that all directional derivatives exist at
(x,y)
. Let
\frac{\partial\ell(x,y)}{\partial x}
and
\frac{\partial\ell(x,y)}{\partial y}
be the respective directional derivative values of
\ell
at
(x,y)
in the
(1,0)
and
(0,1)
directions. From the mean value theorem of one-dimensional Calculus,

(3.5)

\displaystyle\ell\big((x,y)+\epsilon(u,v)\big)-\ell(x,y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big[\ell\big((x,y)+\epsilon(u,0)\big)-\ell(x,y)\big]

\displaystyle+\big[\ell\big((x,y)+\epsilon(u,v)\big)-\ell\big((x,y)+\epsilon(u,0)\big)\big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\ell({\bf p}_{1})}{\partial x}u\epsilon+\frac{\partial\ell({\bf p}_{2})}{\partial y}v\epsilon,

where
{\bf p}_{1}
is in the line segment with endpoints
(x,y)
and
(x,y)+\epsilon(u,0)
, and
{\bf p}_{2}
is in the line segment with endpoints
(x,y)+\epsilon(u,0)
and
(x,y)+\epsilon(u,v)
, and by constructing an appropriate parallelogram we already know that the respective lengths of these line segments are just
u\epsilon
and
v\epsilon
since the line segments are parallel to the
x
-axis and
y
-axis and the values of
\ell
are known on these axes (i.e.,
\ell(a,0)=|a|
and
\ell(0,b)=|b|
, from the Postulate of Line Measure and Lemma
3.1
).

We know from Lemma
3.3
that the directional derivative of
\ell
in a fixed direction is continuous at
(x,y)\neq(0,0)
. Thus, dividing both sides of (
3.5
) by
\epsilon
and taking the limit as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
, we have established

Lemma 3.4
.

D\ell(x,y;u,v)=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\frac{\ell\left((x,y)+\epsilon(u,v)\right)-\ell(x,y)}{\epsilon}=\frac{\partial\ell(x,y)}{\partial x}u+\frac{\partial\ell(x,y)}{\partial y}v.
