Modified Neumann–Neumann methods for semi- and quasilinear elliptic equations Thanks: Version of August 9, 2026.
Emil Engström, Eskil Hansen

Abstract
The Neumann–Neumann method is a commonly employed domain decomposition method for linear elliptic equations. However, the method exhibits slow convergence when applied to semilinear equations and does not seem to converge at all for certain quasilinear equations. We therefore propose two modified Neumann–Neumann methods that have better convergence properties and require less computations. We provide numerical results that show the advantages of these methods when applied to both semilinear and quasilinear equations. We also prove linear convergence with mesh-independent error reduction under certain assumptions on the equation. The analysis is carried out on general Lipschitz domains and relies on the theory of nonlinear Steklov–Poincaré operators.



keywords

Nonoverlapping domain decomposition, Neumann–Neumann method, linear convergence, semi- and quasilinear elliptic equations

Introduction

We consider a bounded Lipschitz domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
for
d=2,3
, with boundary
\partial\Omega
, together with the quasilinear elliptic equation with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\nabla\cdot\alpha(x,u,\nabla u)+\beta(x,u,\nabla u)&=f&&\text{in }\Omega,\\
u&=0&&\text{on }\partial\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

(1)

We will refer to the equation as semilinear if
\alpha(x,y,z)=\tilde{\alpha}(x)z
. A common approach to generate numerical schemes for elliptic equations that can be implemented in parallel and only rely on local communication is to employ nonoverlapping domain decomposition methods.

The performance and convergence properties of these methods have been extensively studied for linear elliptic equations, as surveyed in
[
9
,
13
]
, but less is known for the methods applied to quasilinear elliptic equations. Convergence results for overlapping Schwarz methods have been derived in
[
6
,
8
,
11
,
12
]
. Note that
[
12
]
even considers degenerate quasilinear equations, i.e., when
\alpha(x,y,z)=\tilde{\alpha}(x,y,z)z
and
\tilde{\alpha}(x,y,z)=0
for some
y\neq 0
. For nonoverlapping decompositions we are only aware of a few studies that derive rigorous convergence results. In
[
1
]
the convergence of the Dirichlet–Neumann and Robin–Robin methods are analyzed for a one-dimensional quasilinear elliptic equation, and in our own paper
[
7
]
we prove convergence without order for the Robin–Robin method applied to degenerate quasilinear equations with a
p
-structure. Moreover, a modified Dirichlet–Neumann method for nonlinear equations is studied in
[
3
]
, and convergence is derived for one-dimensional problems. There are also some studies relating to quasilinear equations and decompositions with cross points
[
2
,
4
,
5
,
10
]
, but without convergence results.
We are unaware of any linear convergence results for nonoverlapping decompositions applied to quasilinear, or even semilinear, elliptic problems on general Lipschitz domains.

The basic nonoverlapping decompositions methods are the already mentioned Dirichlet–Neumann, Neumann–Neumann, and Robin–Robin methods. In the context of linear elliptic equations the Neumann–Neumann method has better convergence properties on non-symmetric domain decompositions compared to the Dirichlet–Neumann method. Furthermore, for linear equations and after a finite element discretization one can prove that the Neumann–Neumann method converges linearly with an error reduction constant
C
that is uniformly bounded with respect to the mesh width
h
, whereas the Robin–Robin method obtains a deteriorating constant of the form
C=1-\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{h})
. With these considerations in mind, the Neumann–Neumann method is a natural starting point for developing linearly convergent methods for more general elliptic equations.

In order to introduce the methods, we decompose the domain
\Omega
into two subdomains
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
such that

\overline{\Omega}=\overline{\Omega}_{1}\cup\overline{\Omega}_{2},\quad\Omega_{1}\cap\Omega_{2}=\emptyset\quad\text{and}\quad\Gamma=(\partial\Omega_{1}\cap\partial\Omega_{2})\setminus\partial\Omega,

where
\partial\Omega_{i}
denotes the boundary of
\Omega_{i}
and
\Gamma
is the interface separating the subdomains. The subdomains can also be replaced by two families of nonadjacent subdomains, which is required in order to obtain a parallel method. Two possible decompositions are illustrated in
fig.

1
.

Figure 1
:
An example of a domain decomposition with two subdomains
\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2}
(left), and one with a decomposition into two families of subdomains
\{\Omega_{1,\ell}\}
,
\{\Omega_{2,\ell}\}
(right).

The Neumann–Neumann method for the nonlinear equation
eq.

1
is then as follows. Let
s_{1},s_{2}>0
be method parameters and
\eta^{0}
an initial guess on
\Gamma
. For
n=0,1,2,\ldots,
find
(u_{1}^{n+1},u_{2}^{n+1},w_{1}^{n+1},w_{2}^{n+1})
such that

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\nabla\cdot\alpha(x,u^{n+1}_{i},\nabla u^{n+1}_{i})+\beta(x,u^{n+1}_{i},\nabla u^{n+1}_{i})&=f&&\text{in }\Omega_{i},\\
u^{n+1}_{i}&=0&&\text{on }\partial\Omega_{i}\setminus\Gamma,\\
u^{n+1}_{i}&=\eta^{n}&&\text{on }\Gamma,\text{ for }i=1,2,\\[5.0pt]
-\nabla\cdot\alpha(x,w^{n+1}_{i},\nabla w^{n+1}_{i})+\beta(x,w^{n+1}_{i},\nabla w^{n+1}_{i})&=0&&\text{in }\Omega_{i},\\
w^{n+1}_{i}&=0&&\text{on }\partial\Omega_{i}\setminus\Gamma,\\
\alpha(x,w^{n+1}_{i},\nabla w^{n+1}_{i})\cdot\nu_{1}&=&&\\
\alpha(x,u_{1}^{n+1},\nabla u_{1}^{n+1})\cdot\nu_{1}-\alpha(x,u_{2}^{n+1},\nabla u_{2}^{n+1})&\cdot\nu_{1}&&\text{on }\Gamma,\text{ for }i=1,2,\end{aligned}\right.

(2)

with
\eta^{n+1}=\eta^{n}-s_{1}\left.w_{1}^{n+1}\right|_{\Gamma}-s_{2}\left.w_{2}^{n+1}\right|_{\Gamma}
. Here,
\nu_{1}
denote the outwards pointing unit normal on
\partial\Omega_{1}
, and the computed quantities
(u_{1}^{n},u_{2}^{n},\eta^{n})
approximate
(u|_{\Omega_{1}},u|_{\Omega_{2}},u|_{\Gamma})
, respectively. We will refer to the last three equalities in
eq.

2
as the auxiliary problem.

However, the Neumann–Neumann method requires the solution of four nonlinear problems in each iteration, which is inefficient even for semilinear elliptic equations. The method also displays surprisingly poor convergence properties for degenerate quasilinear equations. For example consider the semilinear equation

-\Delta u+|u|u=f

(3)

and the
p
-Laplace equation

-\nabla\cdot(|\nabla u|^{p-2}\nabla u)+u=f,

(4)

both given on the unit square
\Omega=(0,1)^{2}
with a decomposition into two
L
-shaped subdomains
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
. The Neumann–Neumann method then requires a significantly larger amount of equation solves compared to other methods for the semilinear equation; see
fig.

2(a)
. Furthermore, it does not even generate a decreasing error with respect to the number of iterations for the
p
-Laplace equation; see
fig.

2(b)
. The details of the numerical experiments are given in
section

9
.

In order to obtain methods that require less solutions of nonlinear equations, we propose two modified Neumann–Neumann methods based on replacing the auxiliary problem with a linear one, i.e., we employ a linear preconditioner. For the first modified method we replace the auxiliary problem by

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta w^{n+1}_{i}&=0&&\text{in }\Omega_{i},\\
w^{n+1}_{i}&=0&&\text{on }\partial\Omega_{i}\setminus\Gamma,\\
\nabla w^{n+1}_{i}\cdot\nu_{1}&=\\
\alpha(x,u_{1}^{n+1},\nabla u_{1}^{n+1})\cdot\nu_{1}-\alpha(x,u_{2}^{n+1},\nabla u_{2}^{n+1})&\cdot\nu_{1}&&\text{on }\Gamma,\text{ for }i=1,2.\end{aligned}\right.

(5)

We also consider a second method where the auxiliary problem is iteration-dependent, in particular via the linearization of the auxiliary problem. For notational simplicity, we assume that
\alpha(x,y,z)=\alpha(x,z)
and
\beta(x,y,z)=\beta(x,y)
. We then introduce
J_{\alpha}
and
J_{\beta}
, the Jacobians of
\alpha
and
\beta
with respect to
z
and
y
, respectively. The second modified method is then given by replacing the auxiliary problem with

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\nabla\cdot\bigl(J_{\alpha}(x,\nabla u_{i}^{n+1})\nabla w_{i}\bigr)+J_{\beta}(x,u_{i}^{n+1})w_{i}&=0&&\text{in }\Omega_{i},\\
w^{n+1}_{i}&=0&&\text{on }\partial\Omega_{i}\setminus\Gamma,\\
J_{\alpha}(x,\nabla u^{n+1}_{i})\nabla w^{n+1}_{i}\cdot\nu_{1}&=\\
\alpha(x,\nabla u_{1}^{n+1})\cdot\nu_{1}-\alpha(x,\nabla u_{2}^{n+1})&\cdot\nu_{1}&&\text{on }\Gamma,\text{ for }i=1,2.\end{aligned}\right.

(6)

The efficiency gains of the modified Neumann–Neumann methods over the standard Neumann–Neumann method are illustrated in
fig.

2(a)
for our semilinear example. The modified methods even perform well for the degenerate
p
-Laplace equation; see
fig.

2(b)
.

Hence, there are two goals for this study. First, to provide numerical evidence of the advantages of the modified Neumann–Neumann methods for quasilinear equations, including degenerate ones. Secondly, to rigorously prove linear convergence for the modified methods when applied to semi- or quasilinear elliptic equations with domains and subdomains that are only assumed to be Lipschitz.

2
Preliminaries

Let
X
be a Hilbert space and
X^{*}
its dual. We use the notation
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{X^{*}\times X}
for the dual pairing, or simply
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
if the spaces are obvious from context. A (nonlinear) operator
G:X\rightarrow X^{*}
is said to be demicontinuous if
x^{n}\rightarrow x
in
X
implies that

\langle Gx^{n}-Gx,y\rangle\rightarrow 0\qquad\text{for all }y\in X

and
G
is said to be coercive if

\lim_{\|x\|_{X}\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\langle Gx,x\rangle}{\|x\|_{X}}=\infty.

Moreover, the operator
G
is uniformly monotone if there exists a
c>0
such that

\langle Gx-Gy,x-y\rangle\geq c\|x-y\|_{X}^{2}\quad\text{ for all }x,y\in X.

Note that a uniformly monotone operator
G
is also coercive, since

\frac{\langle Gx,x\rangle}{\|x\|_{X}}=\frac{\langle Gx-G(0),x-0\rangle}{\|x-0\|_{X}}+\frac{\langle G(0),x\rangle}{\|x\|_{X}}\geq c\|x\|_{X}-\|G(0)\|_{X^{*}}.

Finally, for a linear operator
P:X\rightarrow X^{*}
we say that
P
is symmetric if

\langle Px,y\rangle=\langle Py,x\rangle\quad\text{ for all }x,y\in X.

Also note that for a linear
P
the uniform monotonicity is equivalent to

\langle Px,x\rangle\geq c\|x\|_{X}^{2}\quad\text{ for all }x\in X.

For the analysis, we require the following geometric assumption.

Assumption 1
.

The domains
\Omega,\Omega_{i}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
are bounded and Lipschitz. The interface
\Gamma
and exterior boundaries
\partial\Omega\setminus\partial\Omega_{i}
are
(d-1)
-dimensional Lipschitz manifolds.

We denote the standard Sobolev spaces by
H^{1}
and
H^{1}_{0}
. We will also make use of the fractional Sobolev space
H^{1/2}
, see
[
13
, Appendix A.2]
for a proper definition. We denote our Sobolev spaces by

\displaystyle V

\displaystyle=H_{0}^{1}(\Omega),\quad V_{i}^{0}=H_{0}^{1}(\Omega_{i}),

\displaystyle V_{i}

\displaystyle=\{u\in H^{1}(\Omega_{i}):\,T_{i}u=0\},

\displaystyle\Lambda

\displaystyle=\{\mu\in L^{2}(\Gamma):\,E_{i}\mu\in H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega_{i})\},

with the usual norms for
V
and
V_{i}^{0}
, and

\displaystyle\|u\|_{V_{i}}

\displaystyle=\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{i})}+|u|_{V_{i}}=\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{i})}+\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{i})^{d}},

\displaystyle\|\mu\|_{\Lambda}

\displaystyle=\|E_{i}\mu\|_{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega_{i})}.

Here,
E_{i}:L^{2}(\Gamma)\rightarrow L^{2}(\partial\Omega_{i})
denotes the extension by zero and
T_{i}:V_{i}\rightarrow\Lambda
denotes the trace operator on the interface
\Gamma\subset\partial\Omega_{i}
. The trace operator is a bounded linear operator
[
7
, Lemma 4.4]
. We will also make use of the fact that
T_{i}
has a bounded linear right inverse, which we denote by
R_{i}:\Lambda\rightarrow V_{i}
. For more details, we refer to
[
7
, Section 4]
.

We assume the following structure on the equation
eq.

1
.

Assumption 2
.

The functions
x\mapsto\alpha(x,y,z)
and
x\mapsto\beta(x,y,z)
are measurable for almost all
y\in\mathbb{R}
and
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Moreover,
\alpha
and
\beta
satisfy the following conditions, where
h_{\ell}
are nonnegative functions in
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
.

•

The function
\alpha
is Lipschitz continuous in the variables
y
and
z
. That is,

|\alpha(x,y,z)-\alpha(x,y^{\prime},z^{\prime})|\leq h_{1}(x)\bigl(|z-z^{\prime}|+|y-y^{\prime}|\bigr)

for all
y,y^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R},\,z,z^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

•

The function
\beta
satisfies the local Lipschitz bound

|\beta(x,y,z)-\beta(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime})|\leq L(y,y^{\prime})|y-y^{\prime}|+h_{1}(x)|z-z^{\prime}|

for all
y,y^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R},\,z,z^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Here we assume that the Lipschitz constant
L
satisfies the growth bound

L(y,y^{\prime})\leq C\bigl(1+|y|^{p^{*}-2}+|y^{\prime}|^{p^{*}-2}\bigr),

(7)

where
p^{*}
is the Sobolev conjugate of
p=2
, i.e.,

p^{*}=\frac{2d}{d-2},

or if
d=2
, then we can take any
p^{*}\geq 2
.

•

The functions
\alpha
and
\beta
satisfy the uniform monotonicity

\displaystyle\bigl(\alpha(x,y,z)-\alpha(x,y^{\prime},z^{\prime})\bigr)\cdot(z-z^{\prime})+\bigl(\beta(x,y,z)-\beta(x,y^{\prime},z^{\prime})\bigr)(y-y^{\prime})

\displaystyle\quad\geq h_{2}(x)|z-z^{\prime}|^{2}-h_{3}(x)|y-y^{\prime}|^{2}

for all
y,y^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R},\,z,z^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Here,

\inf_{x\in\Omega}h_{2}(x)>C_{p}\sup_{x\in\Omega}h_{3}(x),

(8)

where
C_{p}
is the largest of the Poincaré constants of
\Omega
and
\Omega_{i}
.

•

The source term
f\in V^{*}
and can be written

\langle f,v\rangle=\langle f_{1},\left.v\right|_{\Omega_{1}}\rangle+\langle f_{2},\left.v\right|_{\Omega_{2}}\rangle\qquad\text{for all }v\in V,

with
f_{i}\in V_{i}^{*}
,
i=1,2
.

For sake of simplicity we will only derive convergence for our second method under the following restriction to semilinear equations.

Assumption 3
.

The functions
\alpha
and
\beta
satisfy the following conditions, where
h_{\ell}
are nonnegative functions in
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
.

•

The function
\alpha
is of the form
\alpha(x,y,z)=J_{\alpha}(x)z
with
J_{\alpha}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)^{d\times d}
. That is,
\alpha
is linear in
z
.

•

The function
\beta(x,y,z)=\beta(x,y)
is differentiable with respect to
y
and has measurable Jacobian
J_{\beta}:\Omega\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, which satisfies the bounds

\displaystyle|J_{\beta}(x,y)|

\displaystyle\leq h_{1}(x)+L(y),

\displaystyle|J_{\beta}(x,y)-J_{\beta}(x,y^{\prime})|

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{L}(y,y^{\prime})|y-y^{\prime}|

for all
y,y^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
. Here, we assume that the constants
L
and
\tilde{L}
satisfies the growth bounds

L(y)\leq C\bigl(1+|y|^{p^{*}-2}\bigr)\quad\text{and}\quad\tilde{L}(y,y^{\prime})\leq C\bigl(1+|y|^{p^{*}-3}+|y^{\prime}|^{p^{*}-3}\bigr),

respectively, where
p^{*}
is the Sobolev conjugate of
p=2
, i.e.,

p^{*}=\frac{2d}{d-2},

or if
d=2
, then we can take any
p^{*}\geq 2
.

•

The Jacobians
J_{\alpha}
and
J_{\beta}
satisfy the coercivity bound

J_{\alpha}(x)z^{\prime}\cdot z^{\prime}+J_{\beta}(x,y)(y^{\prime})^{2}\geq h_{2}(x)|z^{\prime}|^{2}-h_{3}(x)|y^{\prime}|^{2}

for all
y,y^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R},\,z,z^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
h_{2},h_{3}
are as in
eq.

8
.

.

Consider the semilinear reaction-diffusion equation

\alpha(z)=z
and

. This equation satisfies

,

, and

. Similarly, the equation satisfies

.

.

Consider the quasilinear equation with

\alpha(x,u,\nabla u)=\nabla u+\gamma(x)\sin(|\nabla u|)(1,\ldots,1)^{\mathrm{T}}

and
\beta=0
. This equation satisfies

assuming that
\|\gamma\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}
is small enough, but will not satisfy

since

.

3
Weak formulations

We define the operators
A:V\rightarrow V^{*}
and
A_{i}:V_{i}\rightarrow V_{i}^{*}
by

\displaystyle\langle Au,v\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\alpha(x,u,\nabla u)\cdot\nabla v+\beta(x,u,\nabla u)v\mathrm{d}x\qquad\text{and}

\displaystyle\langle A_{i}u_{i},v_{i}\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega_{i}}\alpha(x,u_{i},\nabla u_{i})\cdot\nabla v_{i}+\beta(x,u_{i},\nabla u_{i})v_{i}\mathrm{d}x,

respectively.

Lemma 1
.

Suppose that
1
and
2
hold. The operators
A_{i}
then satisfy the Lipschitz condition

\|A_{i}u-A_{i}v\|_{V_{i}^{*}}\leq L\bigl(\|u\|_{V_{i}},\|v\|_{V_{i}}\bigr)\|u-v\|_{V_{i}},

where
L
satisfies the growth bound

L\bigl(\|u\|_{V_{i}},\|v\|_{V_{i}}\bigr)\leq C\bigl(1+\|u\|_{V_{i}}^{p^{*}-2}+\|v\|_{V_{i}}^{p^{*}-2}\bigr).

Moreover, the operators
A_{i}
are uniformly monotone.

Proof.

Let

q=\frac{p^{*}}{p^{*}-2},

as in
2
. This implies that that

q^{*}=\frac{q}{q-1}=\frac{p^{*}}{2}.

It follows that for
f\in L^{q}(\Omega_{i})
and
g,h\in L^{p^{*}}(\Omega_{i})
we have the three term Hölder inequality

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{i}}|fgh|\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\|f\|_{L^{q}(\Omega_{i})}\Bigl(\int_{\Omega_{i}}|gh|^{q^{*}}\mathrm{d}x\Bigr)^{1/q^{*}}\leq\|f\|_{L^{q}(\Omega_{i})}\||g|^{q^{*}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{i})}^{1/q^{*}}\||h|^{q^{*}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{i})}^{1/q^{*}}

\displaystyle=\|f\|_{L^{q}(\Omega_{i})}\|g\|_{L^{2q^{*}}(\Omega_{i})}\|h\|_{L^{2q^{*}}(\Omega_{i})}=\|f\|_{L^{q}(\Omega_{i})}\|g\|_{L^{p^{*}}(\Omega_{i})}\|h\|_{L^{p^{*}}(\Omega_{i})}.

Using this, the regular Hölder inequality, and the Lipschitz continuity of
\alpha
and
\beta
yields

\displaystyle\bigl|\langle A_{i}u-A_{i}v,w\rangle\bigr|

\displaystyle\quad=\biggl|\int_{\Omega_{i}}\alpha(x,u,\nabla u)\cdot\nabla w-\alpha(x,v,\nabla v)\cdot\nabla w+\beta(x,u,\nabla u)w-\beta(x,v,\nabla v)w\mathrm{d}x\biggr|

\displaystyle\quad\leq\int_{\Omega_{i}}\bigl|\alpha(x,u,\nabla u)-\alpha(x,v,\nabla v)\bigr||\nabla w|+\bigl|\beta(x,u,\nabla u)-\beta(x,v,\nabla v)\bigr||w|\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad\leq\int_{\Omega_{i}}h_{1}|\nabla u-\nabla v||\nabla w|+\bigl(L(u,v)|u-v|+h_{1}|\nabla u-\nabla v|\bigr)|w|\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad\leq C|u-v|_{V_{i}}\|w\|_{V_{i}}+\|L(u,v)\|_{L^{q}(\Omega_{i})}\|u-v\|_{L^{p*}(\Omega_{i})}\|w\|_{L^{p*}(\Omega_{i})}.

The Lipschitz constant can now be estimated as follows

\displaystyle\|L(u,v)\|_{L^{q}(\Omega_{i})}

\displaystyle\leq C\|1+|u|^{p^{*}-2}+|v|^{p^{*}-2}\|_{L^{q}(\Omega_{i})}

\displaystyle\leq C\bigl(\|1\|_{L^{q}(\Omega_{i})}+\||u|^{p^{*}-2}\|_{L^{q}(\Omega_{i})}+\||v|^{p^{*}-2}\|_{L^{q}(\Omega_{i})}\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq C\bigl(1+\|u\|_{L^{p^{*}}(\Omega_{i})}^{p^{*}-2}+\|v\|_{L^{p^{*}}(\Omega_{i})}^{p^{*}-2}\bigr)

and by the Sobolev embedding
V_{i}\hookrightarrow L^{p*}(\Omega_{i})
we have that

\displaystyle\bigl|\langle A_{i}u-A_{i}v,w\rangle\bigr|\leq C\bigl(1+\|u\|_{V_{i}}^{p^{*}-2}+\|v\|_{V_{i}}^{p^{*}-2}\bigr)\|u-v\|_{V_{i}}\|w\|_{V_{i}}.

To prove uniform monotonicity we use the monotonicity of
\alpha,\beta
and
eq.

8
to get that

\displaystyle\langle A_{i}u-A_{i}v,u-v\rangle

\displaystyle\quad=\int_{\Omega_{i}}\Bigl(\alpha(x,u,\nabla u)\cdot\nabla(u-v)-\alpha(x,v,\nabla v)\cdot\nabla(u-v)

\displaystyle\qquad+\beta(x,u,\nabla u)(u-v)-\beta(x,v,\nabla v)(u-v)\Bigr)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad\geq\int_{\Omega_{i}}h_{2}(x)|\nabla u-\nabla v|^{2}-h_{3}(x)|u-v|^{2}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad\geq\inf_{x\in\Omega}h_{2}(x)\int_{\Omega_{i}}|\nabla u-\nabla v|^{2}\mathrm{d}x-\sup_{x\in\Omega_{i}}h_{3}(x)\int_{\Omega_{i}}|u-v|^{2}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad\geq\inf_{x\in\Omega_{i}}h_{2}(x)|u-v|_{V_{i}}^{2}-\sup_{x\in\Omega_{i}}h_{3}(x)\|u-v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{i})}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\geq c\|u-v\|_{V_{i}}^{2}.

The weak formulation of the quasilinear elliptic equation is to find
u\in V
such that
Au=f
in
V^{*}
, or equivalently,

\langle Au,v\rangle=\langle f,v\rangle\qquad\textrm{for all }v\in V.

(9)

Similarly, the weak formulation of the inhomogeneous problem on
\Omega_{i}
is the following: For
\eta\in\Lambda
find
u_{i}\in V_{i}
such that
T_{i}u_{i}=\eta
and

\langle A_{i}u_{i},v_{i}\rangle=\langle f,v_{i}\rangle\qquad\textrm{for all }v_{i}\in V_{i}^{0}.

(10)

This problem has a unique solution and defines a solution operator

F_{i}:\Lambda\rightarrow V_{i}:\eta\mapsto u_{i},

see e.g.
[
7
, Lemma 6.1]
.

Lemma 2
.

Suppose that
1
and
2
hold. The solution operators
F_{i}:\Lambda\rightarrow V_{i}
then satisfy the following local Lipschitz bound

\|F_{i}\eta-F_{i}\mu\|_{V_{i}}\leq L\bigl(\|F_{i}\eta\|_{V_{i}},\|F_{i}\mu\|_{V_{i}}\bigr)\|\eta-\mu\|_{\Lambda},

where
L
has the following growth

L\bigl(\|u\|_{V_{i}},\|v\|_{V_{i}}\bigr)\leq C\bigl(1+\|u\|_{V_{i}}^{p^{*}-2}+\|v\|_{V_{i}}^{p^{*}-2}\bigr).

Proof.

Let
w_{i}=(R_{i}\eta-R_{i}\mu)-(F_{i}\eta-F_{i}\mu)
and note that
T_{i}w_{i}=0
, which implies that
w_{i}\in V_{i}^{0}
. Therefore, by
eqs.

10
and
1
we get

\displaystyle c\|F_{i}\eta-F_{i}\mu\|^{2}_{V_{i}}

\displaystyle\leq\langle A_{i}F_{i}\eta-A_{i}F_{i}\mu,F_{i}\eta-F_{i}\mu\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle A_{i}F_{i}\eta-A_{i}F_{i}\mu,R_{i}(\eta-\mu)\rangle-\langle A_{i}F_{i}\eta-A_{i}F_{i}\mu,w_{i}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle A_{i}F_{i}\eta-A_{i}F_{i}\mu,R_{i}(\eta-\mu)\rangle-\langle f_{i}-f_{i},w_{i}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq L\bigl(\|F_{i}\eta\|_{V_{i}},\|F_{i}\mu\|_{V_{i}}\bigr)\|F_{i}\eta-F_{i}\mu\|_{V_{i}}\|R_{i}(\eta-\mu)\|_{V_{i}}

\displaystyle\leq L\bigl(\|F_{i}\eta\|_{V_{i}},\|F_{i}\mu\|_{V_{i}}\bigr)\|F_{i}\eta-F_{i}\mu\|_{V_{i}}\|\eta-\mu\|_{\Lambda}.

Dividing by
c\|F_{i}\eta-F_{i}\mu\|_{V_{i}}
gives the Lipshchitz bound.

4
The transmission problem and the Steklov–Poincaré formulation

The transmission problem is to find
(u_{1},u_{2})\in V_{1}\times V_{2}
such that

\left\{\begin{aligned} \langle A_{i}u_{i},v_{i}\rangle&=\langle f_{i},v_{i}\rangle&&\text{for all }v_{i}\in V_{i}^{0},\,i=1,2,\\
T_{1}u_{1}&=T_{2}u_{2},&&\\
\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{2}\langle A_{i}&u_{i},R_{i}\mu\rangle-\langle f_{i},R_{i}\mu\rangle=0&&\text{for all }\mu\in\Lambda.\end{aligned}\right.

(11)

The transmission problem is equivalent to the weak problem
eq.

9
, see
[
7
, Theorem 5.2]
. We define the nonlinear Steklov–Poincaré operators
S_{i}:\Lambda\rightarrow\Lambda^{*}
by

\langle S_{i}\eta,\mu\rangle_{\Lambda^{*}\times\Lambda}=\langle A_{i}F_{i}\eta-f_{i},R_{i}\mu\rangle_{V_{i}^{*}\times V_{i}}.

Remark 2
.

We will repeatedly use that the Steklov–Poincaré operators are independent of the choice of extension
R_{i}
. To see this consider arbitrary extensions
R_{i},\tilde{R}_{i}
such that
T_{i}R_{i}=T_{i}\tilde{R}_{i}=I
and let
w_{i}=R_{i}\mu-\tilde{R}_{i}\mu
. Then
T_{i}w_{i}=0
and therefore
w_{i}\in V_{i}^{0}
. It follows from the definition of
F_{i}
that

\langle S_{i}\eta,\mu\rangle=\langle A_{i}F_{i}\eta-f_{i},R_{i}\mu\rangle=\langle A_{i}F_{i}\eta-f_{i},w_{i}\rangle+\langle A_{i}F_{i}\eta-f_{i},\tilde{R}_{i}\mu\rangle=\langle A_{i}F_{i}\eta-f_{i},\tilde{R}_{i}\mu\rangle.

Writing out the definitions of the Steklov–Poincaré operators shows that the transmission problem can be reformulated as finding
\eta\in\Lambda
such that

S\eta=0\quad\textrm{in }\Lambda^{*}.

(12)

The solution to the transmission problem can then be recovered as
(u_{1},u_{2})=(F_{1}\eta,F_{2}\eta)
. Conversely, the solution to the Steklov–Poincaré equation can be recovered from the solution to the transmission problem
(u_{1},u_{2})
by setting
\eta=T_{1}u_{1}=T_{2}u_{2}
. For more details, see
[
7
, Lemma 6.2]
.

The Steklov–Poincaré operators inherit the properties of the operators
A_{i}
.

Theorem 3
.

Suppose that
1
and
2
hold. The Steklov–Poincaré operators
S_{i}:\Lambda\rightarrow\Lambda^{*}
then satisfy the local Lipschitz condition

\displaystyle\|S_{i}\eta-S_{i}\mu\|_{\Lambda^{*}}

\displaystyle\leq L\bigl(\|\eta\|_{\Lambda},\|\mu\|_{\Lambda}\bigr)^{2}\|\eta-\mu\|_{\Lambda},

where
L
satisfies the growth bound

L\bigl(\|\eta\|_{\Lambda},\|\mu\|_{\Lambda}\bigr)\leq C\bigl(1+\|\eta\|_{\Lambda}^{p^{*}-2}+\|\mu\|_{\Lambda}^{p^{*}-2}\bigr).

Moreover,
S_{i}
is uniformly monotone. The same result holds for
S
.

Proof.

Let
L(y,y^{\prime})
denote any function satisfying
eq.

7
, with constant possibly changing line to line. To prove the Lipschitz bound we employ
lemmas

1
and
2
to get

\displaystyle\bigl|\langle S_{i}\eta-S_{i}\mu,\lambda\rangle\bigr|=\bigl|\langle A_{i}F_{i}\eta-A_{i}F_{i}\mu,R_{i}\lambda\rangle\bigr|

\displaystyle\quad\leq L\bigl(\|F_{i}\eta\|_{V_{i}},\|F_{i}\mu\|_{V_{i}}\bigr)\|F_{i}\eta-F_{i}\mu\|_{V_{i}}\|R_{i}\lambda\|_{V_{i}}

\displaystyle\quad\leq L\bigl(\|F_{i}\eta\|_{V_{i}},\|F_{i}\mu\|_{V_{i}}\bigr)^{2}\|\eta-\mu\|_{\Lambda}\|R_{i}\lambda\|_{V_{i}}

\displaystyle\quad\leq L\bigl(\|\eta\|_{\Lambda},\|\mu\|_{\Lambda}\bigr)^{2}\|\eta-\mu\|_{\Lambda}\|\lambda\|_{\Lambda}.

The last inequality follows from the Lipschitz continuity of
F_{i}
since it implies that

\|F_{i}\eta\|_{V_{i}}\leq C(1+\|\eta\|_{\Lambda}).

For the uniform monotonicity, let
w_{i}=(R_{i}\eta-R_{i}\mu)-(F_{i}\eta-F_{i}\mu)
and note that
T_{i}w_{i}=0
, which implies that
w_{i}\in V_{i}^{0}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\langle S_{i}\eta-S_{i}\mu,\eta-\mu\rangle=\langle A_{i}F_{i}\eta-A_{i}F_{i}\mu,R_{i}\eta-R_{i}\mu\rangle

\displaystyle\quad=\langle A_{i}F_{i}\eta-A_{i}F_{i}\mu,F_{i}\eta-F_{i}\mu\rangle+\langle A_{i}F_{i}\eta-A_{i}F_{i}\mu,w_{i}\rangle

\displaystyle\quad=\langle A_{i}F_{i}\eta-A_{i}F_{i}\mu,F_{i}\eta-F_{i}\mu\rangle

\displaystyle\quad\geq c\|F_{i}\eta-F_{i}\mu\|_{V_{i}}^{2}\geq c\|T_{i}F_{i}\eta-T_{i}F_{i}\mu\|_{\Lambda}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad=c\|\eta-\mu\|_{\Lambda}^{2}.

The fact that the same holds for
S
follows since
S=S_{1}+S_{2}
.

Corollary 4
.

Suppose that
1
and
2
hold. Then the Steklov–Poincaré operators
S_{i},S
are bijective. In particular, the Steklov–Poincaré equation
eq.

12
has a unique solution
\eta\in\Lambda
.

Proof.

We will use the Browder–Minty theorem
[
14
, Theorem 26.A]
in order to prove bijectivity and must therefore show that
S_{i}
is demicontinuous, coercive, and monotone. Note that the uniform monotonicity derived in
theorem

3
directly yields that
S_{i}
is coercive and monotone. Demicontinuity follows from the local Lipschitz bound in
theorem

3
. To see this, let
\eta^{n}\rightarrow\eta
in
\Lambda
and note that
\|\eta^{n}\|_{\Lambda}\leq C
. Therefore

\|S_{i}\eta^{n}-S_{i}\eta\|_{\Lambda^{*}}\leq L\bigl(\|\eta\|_{\Lambda},\|\eta^{n}\|_{\Lambda}\bigr)^{2}\|\eta-\eta^{n}\|_{\Lambda}\leq C\|\eta-\eta^{n}\|_{\Lambda}\rightarrow 0,

as
\eta^{n}
tends to
\eta
. The same properties for
S
follow since
S=S_{1}+S_{2}
. Hence,
S_{i},S
are all bijective.

5
Domain decomposition methods

The domain decomposition methods can be equivalently formulated on the interface by writing
\eta^{n}=T_{i}u_{i}^{n+1}
, where
u_{i}^{n}
approximates
u_{i}=\left.u\right|_{\Omega_{i}}
, the solution of
eq.

11
. The interface iterates
\eta^{n}
then approximate the solution of
eq.

12
. The iterates of the domain decomposition methods can also be recovered as
u_{i}^{n+1}=F_{i}\eta^{n}
. The methods studied here have interface iterations of the form

\eta^{n+1}=\eta^{n}-sP^{-1}(S\eta^{n}).

By choosing
P^{-1}=s_{1}S_{1}^{-1}+s_{2}S_{2}^{-1}
for some parameters
s_{1},s_{2}>0
we get the standard nonlinear Neumann–Neumann method. We now propose modified Neumann–Neumann methods based on linear preconditioners
P
. We suggest two choices for the operator
P
that correspond to the methods
eqs.

5
and
6
.

Remark 3
.

The introduction of a third parameter
s
is redundant since we could replace
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
by
\tilde{s}_{1}=ss_{1}
and
\tilde{s}_{2}=ss_{2}
, respectively. However, this simplifies the notation of the proofs.

Method 1
.

The iteration
eq.

5
is equivalent to the interface iteration

\eta^{n+1}=\eta^{n}-sP^{-1}S\eta^{n},

(13)

with the operator
P:\Lambda\rightarrow\Lambda^{*}
defined as

P^{-1}=s_{1}P_{1}^{-1}+s_{2}P_{2}^{-1}.

Here,
s_{1},s_{2}>0
are method parameters and
P_{i}
is the Steklov–Poincaré operator corresponding to the Laplace equation on
\Omega_{i}
. That is, if
\hat{F}_{i}:\eta\mapsto u
denotes the solution operator to the problem

\left\{\begin{aligned} \langle\hat{A}_{i}u,v\rangle=\int_{\Omega_{i}}\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\mathrm{d}x&=0\quad\textrm{for all }v\in V_{i}^{0},\\
T_{i}u&=\eta,\end{aligned}\right.

then

\langle P_{i}\eta,\mu\rangle_{\Lambda^{*}\times\Lambda}=\langle\hat{A}_{i}\hat{F}_{i}\eta,R_{i}\mu\rangle_{V_{i}^{*}\times V_{i}}.

Method 2
.

The iteration
eq.

6
is equivalent to the interface iteration

\eta^{n+1}=\eta^{n}-sP(\eta^{n})^{-1}S\eta^{n},

(14)

with the operator
P(\nu):\Lambda\rightarrow\Lambda^{*}
defined as

P(\nu)^{-1}=s_{1}P_{1}(\nu)^{-1}+s_{2}P_{2}(\nu)^{-1}.

Here,
s_{1},s_{2}>0
are method parameters and
P_{i}(\nu)
is the Steklov–Poincaré operator corresponding to the linearization of
eq.

10
at
w_{i}=F_{i}\nu
. That is, if
\hat{F}_{i}(\nu):\eta\mapsto u
denotes the solution operator to the problem

\left\{\begin{aligned} \langle\hat{A}_{i}(w_{i})u,v\rangle=\int_{\Omega_{i}}J_{\alpha}(w_{i})\nabla u\cdot\nabla v+J_{\beta}(w_{i})uv\mathrm{d}x&=0\quad\textrm{for all }v\in V_{i}^{0},\\
T_{i}u&=\eta,\end{aligned}\right.

then

\langle P_{i}(\nu)\eta,\mu\rangle_{\Lambda^{*}\times\Lambda}=\bigl\langle\hat{A}_{i}\bigl(F_{i}(\nu)\bigr)\hat{F}_{i}(\nu)\eta,R_{i}\mu\bigr\rangle_{V_{i}^{*}\times V_{i}}.

6
Convergence analysis for MNN1

We will now prove convergence of
1
. We first require the following lemma.

Lemma 5
.

Let
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
be linear operators from a (real) Hilbert space
X
into
X^{*}
that are bounded, uniformly monotone, and symmetric. Then
P_{1}
,
P_{2}
, and

s_{1}P_{1}^{-1}+s_{2}P_{2}^{-1}

are bijective. Moreover, the linear operator
P=(s_{1}P_{1}^{-1}+s_{2}P_{2}^{-1})^{-1}
is also a bounded, uniformly monotone, and symmetric.

Proof.

First note that
P_{1}
,
P_{2}
, and
s_{1}P_{1}+s_{2}P_{2}
all have bounded inverses by the Lax–Milgram lemma. We verify that
P_{2}(s_{2}P_{1}+s_{1}P_{2})^{-1}P_{1}
is a left inverse to
s_{1}P_{1}^{-1}+s_{2}P_{2}^{-1}
by

\displaystyle\bigl(P_{2}(s_{2}P_{1}+s_{1}P_{2})^{-1}P_{1}\bigr)\bigl(s_{1}P_{1}^{-1}+s_{2}P_{2}^{-1}\bigr)

\displaystyle\quad=\bigl(P_{2}(s_{2}P_{1}+s_{1}P_{2})^{-1}P_{1}\bigr)\bigl(P_{1}^{-1}(s_{2}P_{1}+s_{1}P_{2})P_{2}^{-1}\bigr)=I

and similarly one can show that it is a right inverse. It follows immediately that the inverse, i.e.,
P
, is bounded. To show that
P
is uniformly monotone, let
\eta\in X
,
\sigma=P\eta
, and
\lambda_{i}=P_{i}^{-1}\sigma
. As
P_{i}
are uniformly monotone and bounded, and
P^{-1}=s_{1}P_{1}^{-1}+s_{2}P_{2}^{-1}
is bounded, one has

\displaystyle\langle P\eta,\eta\rangle

\displaystyle=\bigl\langle\sigma,P^{-1}\sigma\bigr\rangle=\bigl\langle\sigma,(s_{1}P_{1}^{-1}+s_{2}P_{2}^{-1})\sigma\bigr\rangle

\displaystyle=s_{1}\langle P_{1}\lambda_{1},\lambda_{1}\rangle+s_{2}\langle P_{2}\lambda_{2},\lambda_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle\geq c\bigl(\|\lambda_{1}\|_{X}^{2}+\|\lambda_{2}\|_{X}^{2}\bigr)

\displaystyle\geq c\bigl(\|P_{1}\lambda_{1}\|_{X^{*}}^{2}+\|P_{2}\lambda_{2}\|_{X^{*}}^{2}\bigr)=c\|\sigma\|_{X^{*}}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq c\|P^{-1}\sigma\|_{X}^{2}=c\|\eta\|_{X}^{2}.

Next, since
P_{i}
is symmetric, we have for all
\sigma,\rho\in X^{*}
that

\langle\sigma,P_{i}^{-1}\rho\rangle=\langle P_{i}P_{i}^{-1}\sigma,P_{i}^{-1}\rho\rangle=\langle P_{i}P_{i}^{-1}\rho,P_{i}^{-1}\sigma\rangle=\langle\rho,P_{i}^{-1}\sigma\rangle.

The fact that
P
is symmetric follows from the above, as

\displaystyle\langle P\eta,\mu\rangle

\displaystyle=\bigl\langle P\eta,P^{-1}P\mu\bigr\rangle

\displaystyle=s_{1}\bigl\langle P\eta,P_{1}^{-1}P\mu\bigr\rangle+s_{2}\bigl\langle P\eta,P_{2}^{-1}P\mu\bigr\rangle

\displaystyle=s_{1}\bigl\langle P\mu,P_{1}^{-1}P\eta\bigr\rangle+s_{2}\bigl\langle P\mu,P_{2}^{-1}P\eta\bigr\rangle

\displaystyle=\bigl\langle P\mu,P^{-1}P\eta\rangle=\langle P\mu,\eta\rangle.

Theorem 6
.

Let
X
be a (real) Hilbert space and
G:X\rightarrow X^{*}
a nonlinear operator that is uniformly monotone and satisfies the local Lipschitz condition

\|G\mu-G\lambda\|_{X^{*}}\leq L(\|\mu\|_{X},\|\lambda\|_{X})\|\mu-\lambda\|_{X},

where
L(\|\mu\|_{X},\|\lambda\|_{X})
is bounded for bounded
\|\mu\|_{X},\|\lambda\|_{X}
. Then for every
\chi\in X^{*}
there exists a unique solution
\eta\in X
to
G\eta=\chi
.

Moreover, let
P:X\rightarrow X^{*}
be a linear, bounded, uniformly monotone, and symmetric operator
and let
s>0
be small enough. The iteration

\displaystyle\eta^{n+1}=\eta^{n}+sP^{-1}(\chi-G\eta^{n})

then converges to
\eta
and satisfies the linear error estimate

\|\eta^{n}-\eta\|_{X}\leq CL^{n}\|\eta^{0}-\eta\|_{X}

for any
\eta^{0}\in X
.

Proof.

First observe that
G
is a bijection by the Browder–Minty theorem; compare with the proof of
corollary

4
. Hence, for every
\chi\in X^{*}
there exists a unique solution
\eta\in X
to
G\eta=\chi
. Next, we define the operator

K\mu=\mu+sP^{-1}(\chi-G\mu)

and write

\displaystyle\langle PK\mu-PK\lambda,K\mu-K\lambda\rangle=\langle P\mu-P\lambda-s(G\mu-G\lambda),\mu-\lambda-sP^{-1}(G\mu-G\lambda)\rangle

\displaystyle\quad=\langle P\mu-P\lambda,\mu-\lambda\rangle+s^{2}\langle G\mu-G\lambda,P^{-1}(G\mu-G\lambda)\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad-s\Bigl(\langle P\mu-P\lambda,P^{-1}(G\mu-G\lambda)\rangle+\langle G\mu-G\lambda,\mu-\lambda\rangle\Bigr)

\displaystyle\quad=I_{1}+s^{2}I_{2}-sI_{3}.

The second term
I_{2}
is estimated using the continuity of
P^{-1}
and
G
and the uniform monotonicity of
P
, which yields that

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle=\langle G\mu-G\lambda,P^{-1}(G\mu-G\lambda)\rangle

\displaystyle\quad\leq L\bigl(\|\mu\|_{X},\|\lambda\|_{X}\bigr)\|\mu-\lambda\|_{X}\|P^{-1}(G\mu-G\lambda)\|_{X}

\displaystyle\quad\leq C_{1}L\bigl(\|\mu\|_{X},\|\lambda\|_{X}\bigr)^{2}\|\mu-\lambda\|_{X}^{2}.

The third term
I_{3}
is estimated using the symmetry of
P
and the monotonicity of
G
, i.e.,

\displaystyle I_{3}=2\langle G\mu-G\lambda,\mu-\lambda\rangle\geq c\|\mu-\lambda\|_{X}^{2}.

We define

(\mu,\lambda)_{P}=\langle P\mu,\lambda\rangle

and note that this is an inner product which gives a norm
\|\cdot\|_{P}
that is equivalent to
\|\cdot\|_{X}
. The latter follows as
P
is bounded, uniformly monotone, and symmetric. Thus

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \|K\mu-K\lambda\|_{P}&=\langle PK\mu-PK\lambda,K\mu-K\lambda\rangle\\
&\leq\Bigl(1+s^{2}C_{1}L\bigl(\|\mu\|_{X},\|\lambda\|_{X}\bigr)^{2}-sc\Bigr)\|\mu-\lambda\|_{P}^{2}.\end{aligned}

(15)

For
r>0
we define the ball

D_{r}=\{\mu\in X:\|\mu-\eta\|_{P}\leq r\}

and then fix
r>0
large enough that
\eta^{0}\in D_{r}
. Since
L\bigl(\|\mu\|_{X},\|\lambda\|_{X}\bigr)
is bounded on
D_{r}
we can find
C_{2}(r)>0
such that
C_{1}L(\|\mu\|_{P},\|\lambda\|_{P})^{2}\leq C_{2}(r)
for all
\mu,\lambda\in D_{r}
. We can then choose
s>0
small enough that
1+s^{2}C_{2}(r)-sc<1
. This implies that, for
\mu\in D_{r}
, we have

\|K\mu-\eta\|_{P}=\|K\mu-K\eta\|_{P}\leq(1+s^{2}C_{2}(r)-sc)\|\mu-\eta\|_{P}\leq r(1+s^{2}C_{2}(r)-sc)<r,

which shows that
K\mu\in D_{r}
. Hence, by induction
\{\eta^{n}\}\subset D_{r}
as
\eta^{0}\in D_{r}
. Moreover, according to
eq.

15
,

\|K\mu-K\lambda\|_{P}\leq L\|\mu-\lambda\|_{P}

with
L<1
for all
\mu,\lambda\in D_{r}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\|\eta^{n}-\eta\|_{X}

\displaystyle\leq C\|\eta^{n}-\eta\|_{P}=C\|K\eta^{n-1}-K\eta\|_{P}\leq CL\|\eta^{n-1}-\eta\|_{P}

\displaystyle\leq CL^{n}\|\eta^{0}-\eta\|_{P}\leq CL^{n}\|\eta^{0}-\eta\|_{X},

which tends to zero as
n
tends to infinity.

Setting
(X,G,\chi)=(\Lambda,S,0)
in this abstract result immediately yields the convergence of
1
for quasilinear problems, after noting that
P
is bounded, uniformly monotone, and symmetric. Since
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
are the Steklov–Poincaré operators for the Laplace equation this follows from
[
9
, Chapter 4]
and
lemma

5
. In particular, the symmetry follows since
P_{i}
is independent of the choice of extension
R_{i}
and can be written

\langle P_{i}\eta,\mu\rangle=\langle\hat{A}_{i}\hat{F}_{i}\eta,R_{i}\mu\rangle=\langle\hat{A}_{i}\hat{F}_{i}\eta,\hat{F}_{i}\mu\rangle,

compare with
remark

2
. The fact that the method is well defined, i.e., the iteration has a unique solution at each step, is also a consequence of the above.

Corollary 7
.

Let
1
and
2
hold and suppose that
s_{1},s_{2}>0
are small enough. Then the iterates
\eta^{n}
of the interface iteration
eq.

13
converges linearly to the solution of
eq.

12
in
\Lambda
for any
\eta^{0}\in\Lambda
. Moreover the iterates
(u_{1}^{n+1},u_{2}^{n+1})=(F_{1}\eta^{n},F_{2}\eta^{n})
of
1
converges linearly to the solution of
eq.

11
in
V_{1}\times V_{2}
.

7
Convergence analysis for MNN2

We will now prove convergence of our second method for semilinear problems. We will use the following abstract convergence result.

Theorem 8
.

Let
X
be a (real) Hilbert space
G:X\rightarrow X^{*}
a nonlinear operator that is uniformly monotone and satisfies the local Lipschitz condition

\|G\mu-G\lambda\|_{X^{*}}\leq L(\|\mu\|_{X},\|\lambda\|_{X})\|\mu-\lambda\|_{X},

where
L(\|\mu\|_{X},\|\lambda\|_{X})
is bounded for bounded
\|\mu\|_{X},\|\lambda\|_{X}
. Then for every
\chi\in X^{*}
there exists a unique solution
\eta\in X
to
G\eta=\chi
.

Moreover, for any
\nu\in X
let
P(\nu):X\rightarrow X^{*}
be a symmetric operator such that the family of operators
P(\cdot)
satisfy

\displaystyle\|P(\nu)\mu\|_{X^{*}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}(\nu)\|\mu\|_{X}\qquad

\displaystyle\text{for all }\nu,\mu\in X,

\displaystyle\langle P(\nu)\mu,\mu\rangle

\displaystyle\geq c\|\mu\|_{X}^{2}\qquad

\displaystyle\text{for all }\nu,\mu\in X,

\displaystyle\bigl\|\bigl(P(\nu)-P(\mu)\bigr)\lambda\bigr\|_{X^{*}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}\bigl(\|\nu\|_{X},\|\mu\|_{X}\bigr)\|\nu-\mu\|_{X}\|\lambda\|_{X}\qquad

\displaystyle\text{for all }\nu,\mu,\lambda\in X,

where
C_{1}(\|\mu\|_{X}),C_{2}(\|\nu\|_{X},\|\mu\|_{X})
are bounded for bounded
\|\nu\|_{X}
,
\|\mu\|_{X}
. In particular,
P(\nu)
is bounded and uniformly monotone for all
\nu\in X
. Assume also that
\eta^{0}
is close enough to
\eta
and that
s>0
is small enough. Then the iteration

\displaystyle\eta^{n+1}=\eta^{n}+sP(\eta^{n})^{-1}(\chi-G\eta^{n})

converges to
\eta
and satisfies the linear error estimate

\|\eta^{n}-\eta\|_{X}\leq CL^{n}\|\eta^{0}-\eta\|_{X}.

Proof.

The fact that
G\eta=\chi
has a unique solution has already been proven in
theorem

8
. We define the operator
K(\nu)\mu=\mu+sP(\nu)^{-1}(\chi-G\mu)
and compute

\displaystyle\langle P(\nu)K(\nu)\mu-P(\nu)K(\nu)\lambda,K(\nu)\mu-K(\nu)\lambda\rangle

\displaystyle\quad=\bigl\langle P(\nu)\mu-P(\nu)\lambda-s(G\mu-G\lambda),\mu-\lambda-s\bigl(P(\nu)^{-1}(G\mu-G\lambda)\bigr)\bigr\rangle

\displaystyle\quad=\langle P(\nu)\mu-P(\nu)\lambda,\mu-\lambda\rangle+s^{2}\langle G\mu-G\lambda,P(\nu)^{-1}(G\mu-G\lambda)\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad-s\Bigl(\langle P(\nu)\mu-P(\nu)\lambda,P(\nu)^{-1}(G\mu-G\lambda)\rangle+\langle G\mu-G\lambda,\mu-\lambda\rangle\Bigr)

\displaystyle\quad=I_{1}+s^{2}I_{2}-sI_{3}.

The second term
I_{2}
is estimated using the continuity of
P(\nu)^{-1}
and
G
and the coercivity of
P
, which yields that

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle=\langle G\mu-G\lambda,P(\nu)^{-1}(G\mu-G\lambda)\rangle

\displaystyle\quad\leq L\bigl(\|\mu\|_{X},\|\lambda\|_{X}\bigr)\|\mu-\lambda\|_{X}\|P(\nu)^{-1}(G\mu-G\lambda)\|_{X}

\displaystyle\quad\leq C_{3}L\bigl(\|\mu\|_{X},\|\lambda\|_{X}\bigr)^{2}\|\mu-\lambda\|_{X}^{2}.

The third term
I_{3}
can be divided further using the symmetry of
P(\nu)
and estimated using the monotonicity of
G
, i.e.,

\displaystyle I_{3}=2\langle G\mu-G\lambda,\mu-\lambda\rangle\geq c\|\mu-\lambda\|_{X}^{2}.

We define the inner products

(\mu,\lambda)_{P(\nu)}=\langle P(\nu)\mu,\lambda\rangle

and note that they yield norms
\|\cdot\|_{P(\nu)}
that are equivalent to
\|\cdot\|_{X}
. Thus

\displaystyle\|K(\nu)\mu-K(\nu)\lambda\|_{P(\nu)}

\displaystyle=\langle P(\nu)K(\nu)\mu-P(\nu)K(\nu)\lambda,K\mu-K\lambda\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\Bigl(1+s^{2}C_{3}L\bigl(\|\mu\|_{X},\|\lambda\|_{X}\bigr)^{2}-sc\Bigr)\|\mu-\lambda\|_{P(\nu)}^{2}.

We define

D_{r}=\{\mu\in X:\|\mu-\eta\|_{P(\eta)}\leq r\},

where
\eta
is the unique solutionto
G\eta=\chi
. Let
R>0
. For
\mu,\lambda\in D_{R}
we can find
C_{4}=C_{4}(R)>0
such that
C_{3}L(\|\mu\|_{X},\|\lambda\|_{X})\leq C_{4}
and then choose
s>0
small enough such that
L:=1+s^{2}C_{4}-sc<1
. Moreover, we can find
C_{5}=C_{5}(R)>0
such that for all
\mu\in X,\nu\in D_{R}
we have

\frac{1}{C_{5}}\|\mu\|_{X}\leq\|\mu\|_{P(\nu)}\leq C_{5}\|\mu\|_{X}.

Then we can choose
r
small enough that
0<r<R
and

\bigl\|\bigl(P(\eta)-P(\nu)\bigr)\mu\bigr\|_{X^{*}}\leq\frac{1-\sqrt{L}}{\sqrt{L}C_{5}^{2}}\|\mu\|_{X}.

for
\nu\in D_{r},\mu\in X
. It follows that for
\nu\in D_{r},\mu\in X
we have

\displaystyle\|\mu\|_{P(\eta)}^{2}=\|\mu\|_{P(\nu)}^{2}+\langle P(\eta)\mu-P(\nu)\mu,\mu\rangle\leq\Bigl(1+\frac{1-\sqrt{L}}{\sqrt{L}}\Bigr)\|\mu\|_{P(\nu)}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{L}}\|\mu\|_{P(\nu)}^{2},

\displaystyle\|\mu\|_{P(\nu)}^{2}=\|\mu\|_{P(\eta)}^{2}+\langle P(\nu)\mu-P(\eta)\mu,\mu\rangle\leq\Bigl(1+\frac{1-\sqrt{L}}{\sqrt{L}}\Bigr)\|\mu\|_{P(\eta)}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{L}}\|\mu\|_{P(\eta)}^{2}.

For
\mu,\nu\in D_{r}
we also have that

\displaystyle\|K(\nu)\mu-\eta\|_{P(\eta)}

\displaystyle=\|K(\nu)\mu-K(\nu)\eta\|_{P(\eta)}\leq\frac{1}{L^{1/4}}\|K(\nu)\mu-K(\nu)\eta\|_{P(\nu)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1+s^{2}C_{4}-sc}{L^{1/4}}\|\mu-\eta\|_{P(\nu)}\leq\frac{1+s^{2}C_{4}-sc}{\sqrt{L}}\|\mu-\eta\|_{P(\eta)}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{L}r<r,

which shows that
K(\nu)\mu\in D_{r}
. Moreover, for all
\mu,\lambda,\nu\in D_{r}

\displaystyle\|K(\nu)\mu-K(\nu)\lambda\|_{P(\eta)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{L^{1/4}}\|K(\nu)\mu-K(\nu)\lambda\|_{P(\nu)}

\displaystyle\leq L^{3/4}\|\mu-\lambda\|_{P(\nu)}\leq\sqrt{L}\|\mu-\lambda\|_{P(\eta)}

with
L<1
.

Since
\eta^{n+1}=K(\eta^{n})\eta^{n}
and
\eta^{0}\in D_{r}
, as
\eta^{0}
is assumed to be sufficiently close to
\eta
, we have by induction that
\{\eta^{n}\}\subset D_{r}
. Moreover,

\displaystyle\|\eta^{n}-\eta\|_{X}

\displaystyle\leq C\|\eta^{n}-\eta\|_{P(\eta)}=C\|K(\eta^{n-1})\eta^{n-1}-K(\eta^{n-1})\eta\|_{P(\eta)}

\displaystyle\leq C\sqrt{L}\|\eta^{n-1}-\eta\|_{P(\eta)}

\displaystyle\leq CL^{n/2}\|\eta^{0}-\eta\|_{P(\eta)}\leq CL^{n/2}\|\eta^{0}-\eta\|_{X},

which tends to zero as
n
tends to infinity.

Corollary 9
.

Let
1
,
2
and
3
hold and suppose that
s_{1},s_{2}>0
are small enough and that
\eta^{0}
is close enough to
\eta
, the solution of
eq.

12
. Then the iterates
\{\eta^{n}\}
of the interface iteration
eq.

14
converges linearly to
\eta
in
\Lambda
. Moreover the iterates
(u_{1}^{n+1},u_{2}^{n+1})=(F_{1}\eta^{n},F_{2}\eta^{n})
of
2
converges linearly to the solution of
eq.

11
in
V_{1}\times V_{2}
.

Proof.

The properties of
S,S_{1},S_{2}
are acquired in
theorems

3
and
4
and it therefore only remains to show that the properties of
P(\nu)
hold. We will show that the linear operator
\hat{A}_{i}(w):V_{i}\rightarrow V_{i}^{*}

\langle\hat{A}_{i}(w)u,v\rangle=\int_{\Omega_{i}}J_{\alpha}(w)\nabla u\cdot\nabla v+J_{\beta}(w)uv\,\mathrm{d}x

is bounded, locally Lipschitz continuous, and uniformly monotone independently of
w
.

By
3
we have that

\displaystyle\bigl|\langle\hat{A}_{i}(w)u,v\rangle\bigr|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega_{i}}|J_{\alpha}(x)\nabla u\cdot\nabla v|+|J_{\beta}(x,w)uv|\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\|J_{\alpha}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{i})^{d\times d}}\|u\|_{V_{i}}\|v\|_{V_{i}}

\displaystyle\quad+\|J_{\beta}(\cdot,w)\|_{L^{q}(\Omega_{i})}\|u\|_{L^{p*}(\Omega_{i})}\|v\|_{L^{p*}(\Omega_{i})}

\displaystyle\leq C\|u\|_{V_{i}}\|v\|_{V_{i}}+\|1+|w|^{p^{*}-2}\|_{L^{q}(\Omega_{i})}\|u\|_{L^{p*}(\Omega_{i})}\|v\|_{L^{p*}(\Omega_{i})}

\displaystyle\leq C\|u\|_{V_{i}}\|v\|_{V_{i}}+C\bigl(1+\|w\|_{L^{p^{*}}(\Omega_{i})}^{p^{*}-2}\bigr)\|u\|_{L^{p*}(\Omega_{i})}\|v\|_{L^{p*}(\Omega_{i})}.

This follows by the same three term Hölder inequality as in the proof of
lemma

1
. The boundedness then follows from the Sobolev embedding
V_{i}\hookrightarrow L^{p*}(\Omega_{i})
.

Similarly, the Lipschitz continuity of
\hat{A}_{i}
follows from
3
and the Sobolev embedding
V_{i}\hookrightarrow L^{p*}(\Omega_{i})
, since

\displaystyle\bigl|\langle\hat{A}_{i}(w)u-\hat{A}_{i}(\tilde{w})u,v\rangle\bigr|\leq\int_{\Omega_{i}}\Bigl(|\bigl(J_{\alpha}(x)-J_{\alpha}(x)\bigr)\nabla u\cdot\nabla v|

\displaystyle\qquad+|\bigl(J_{\beta}(x,w)-J_{\beta}(x,\tilde{w})\bigr)uv|\mathrm{d}x\Bigr)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\|J_{\beta}(\cdot,w)-J_{\beta}(\cdot,\tilde{w})\|_{L^{q}(\Omega_{i})}\|u\|_{L^{p*}(\Omega_{i})}\|v\|_{L^{p*}(\Omega_{i})}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\|\tilde{L}(w,\tilde{w})(w-\tilde{w})\|_{L^{q}(\Omega_{i})}\|u\|_{L^{p*}(\Omega_{i})}\|v\|_{L^{p*}(\Omega_{i})}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\|1+|w|^{p^{*}-3}+|\tilde{w}|^{p^{*}-3}\|_{L^{q^{\prime}}(\Omega_{i})}\|w-\tilde{w}\|_{L^{p^{*}}(\Omega_{i})}\|u\|_{L^{p*}(\Omega_{i})}\|v\|_{L^{p*}(\Omega_{i})}

\displaystyle\quad\leq C(1+\|w\|_{L^{p^{*}}(\Omega_{i})}^{p^{*}-3}+\|\tilde{w}\|^{p^{*}-3}_{L^{p^{*}}(\Omega_{i})})\|w-\tilde{w}\|_{L^{p^{*}}(\Omega_{i})}\|u\|_{L^{p^{*}}(\Omega_{i})}\|v\|_{L^{p^{*}}(\Omega_{i})}.

Here,

q^{\prime}=\frac{p^{*}}{p^{*}-3}.

Finally, the uniform monotonicity follows from the same property of
J_{\alpha}
and
J_{\beta}
in
3
, as

\displaystyle\langle\hat{A}_{i}(w)u,u\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega_{i}}J_{\alpha}(x)\nabla u\cdot\nabla u+J_{\beta}(x,w)u^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\geq h_{2}(x)\int_{\Omega_{i}}|\nabla u|^{2}-h_{3}(x)|u|^{2}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\geq\inf_{x\in\Omega}h_{2}(x)\int_{\Omega_{i}}|\nabla u|^{2}\mathrm{d}x-\sup_{x\in\Omega_{i}}h_{3}(x)\int_{\Omega_{i}}|u|^{2}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\geq\inf_{x\in\Omega_{i}}h_{2}(x)|u|_{V_{i}}^{2}-\sup_{x\in\Omega_{i}}h_{3}(x)\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{i})}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq c\|u\|_{V_{i}}^{2}.

We now identify

\langle P_{i}(\nu)\eta,\mu\rangle=\bigl\langle\hat{A}_{i}\bigl(F_{i}(\nu)\bigr)\hat{F}_{i}(\nu)\eta,R_{i}\mu\bigr\rangle=\bigl\langle\hat{A}_{i}\bigl(F_{i}(\nu)\bigr)\hat{F}_{i}(\nu)\eta,\hat{F}_{i}(\nu)\mu\bigr\rangle,

compare with
remark

2
. This then yields that
P_{i}(\nu)
is bounded, locally Lipschitz continuous, uniformly monotone, and symmetric by arguing as in
theorem

3
.

Remark 4
.

The convergence result for
2
is somewhat weaker than the result for
1
, since we assume semilinearity of the equation and that the initial guess
\eta^{0}
is close enough to the solution
\eta
. However, in numerical experiments the method performs better and we expect that these extra assumptions are unnecessary in practice.

8
Discrete nonlinear domain decomposition methods

Let
V_{i}^{h}\subset V_{i}
and
\Lambda^{h}\subset\Lambda
be finite dimensional subsets and let
V_{i}^{h,0}=V_{i}^{h}\cap V_{i}^{0}
. Then we can define the discrete operators

A^{h}:V^{h}\rightarrow(V^{h})^{*}
and
A_{i}^{h}:V_{i}^{h}\rightarrow(V_{i}^{h})^{*}
by

\displaystyle\langle A^{h}u,v\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\alpha(x,u,\nabla u)\cdot\nabla v+\beta(x,u,\nabla u)v\mathrm{d}x\qquad\text{and}

\displaystyle\langle A_{i}^{h}u_{i},v_{i}\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega_{i}}\alpha(x,u_{i},\nabla u_{i})\cdot\nabla v_{i}+\beta(x,u_{i},\nabla u_{i})v_{i}\mathrm{d}x,

respectively. We assume the following compatibility relation between the spaces
V_{i}^{h},\Lambda^{h}
.

Assumption 4
.

The trace operators
T_{i}
map
V^{h}_{i}
into
\Lambda^{h}
and have bounded linear right inverses
R_{i}^{h}:\Lambda^{h}\rightarrow V_{i}^{h}
. We assume that
R_{i}^{h}
is bounded independently of
h
.

Typically we would have that
V_{1}^{h},V_{2}^{h},\Lambda^{h}
share the same grid points on the interface. In this case choosing
R_{i}^{h}
to be the discrete harmonic extension gives
h
-independent bounds under the assumption that
V_{i}^{h}
are finite element spaces on a regular family of triangulations, see
[
9
, Theorem 4.1.3]
.

The weak discrete problem is then to find
u^{h}\in V^{h}
such that

\langle A^{h}u^{h},v\rangle=\langle f,v\rangle\quad\textrm{for all }v\in V^{h}.

(16)

Moreover, the discrete transmission problem is to find
(u_{1}^{h},u_{2}^{h})\in V_{1}^{h}\times V_{2}^{h}
such that

\left\{\begin{aligned} \langle A_{i}^{h}u_{i}^{h},v_{i}\rangle&=\langle f_{i},v_{i}\rangle&&\text{for all }v_{i}\in V_{i}^{h,0},\,i=1,2,\\
T_{1}u_{1}&=T_{2}u_{2},&&\\
\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{2}\langle A_{i}^{h}&u_{i}^{h},R_{i}^{h}\mu\rangle-\langle f_{i},R_{i}^{h}\mu\rangle=0&&\text{for all }\mu\in\Lambda.\end{aligned}\right.

(17)

As for the continuous case, the weak problem and the transmission problem are equivalent with
u_{i}^{h}=\left.u^{h}\right|_{\Omega_{i}}
. Under
4
, there exists discrete solution operators
F_{i}^{h}:\Lambda^{h}\rightarrow V_{i}^{h}
that satisfy
T_{i}F_{i}^{h}\eta=\eta
and

\langle A_{i}^{h}F_{i}^{h}\eta,v\rangle=\langle f_{i},v\rangle\quad\textrm{for all }v\in V_{i}^{h,0}.

Thus, we can define discrete nonlinear Steklov–Poincaré operators
S_{i}:\Lambda^{h}\rightarrow(\Lambda^{h})^{*}
as

\langle S_{i}^{h}\eta,\mu\rangle=\langle A_{i}^{h}F_{i}^{h}\eta,R_{i}^{h}\mu\rangle-\langle f_{i},R_{i}^{h}\mu\rangle.

Defining
S^{h}=S_{1}^{h}+S_{2}^{h}:\Lambda^{h}\rightarrow(\Lambda^{h})^{*}
we can reformulate the discrete transmission problem as finding
\eta_{h}\in\Lambda^{h}
such that

S_{i}^{h}\eta_{h}=0\quad\text{in }(\Lambda^{h})^{*}.

(18)

Remark 5
.

The discrete Steklov–Poincaré operators are independent of the choice of extension
R_{i}^{h}
, i.e., for any
\tilde{R}_{i}^{h}:\Lambda^{h}\rightarrow V_{i}^{h}
such that
T_{i}\tilde{R}_{i}^{h}=I
we have

\langle S_{i}^{h}\eta,\mu\rangle=\langle A_{i}^{h}F_{i}^{h}\eta,R_{i}^{h}\mu\rangle-\langle f_{i},R_{i}^{h}\mu\rangle=\langle A_{i}^{h}F_{i}^{h}\eta,\tilde{R}_{i}^{h}\mu\rangle-\langle f_{i},\tilde{R}_{i}^{h}\mu\rangle,

compare with
remark

2
. In particular, this means that, in implementation, we can take
R_{i}^{h}
as the extension by zero to all interior grid points, since this is more efficient to compute.

The discrete nonlinear Steklov–Poincaré operators have the following properties. They follow by the same argument as in
theorem

3
.

Theorem 10
.

Let
1
,
2
and
4
hold. Then the discrete Steklov–Poincaré operators
S_{i}^{h}:\Lambda^{h}\rightarrow(\Lambda^{h})^{*}
satisfy the Lipschitz bound

\displaystyle\|S_{i}^{h}\eta-S_{i}^{h}\mu\|_{(\Lambda^{h})^{*}}

\displaystyle\leq L\bigl(\|\eta\|_{\Lambda},\|\mu\|_{\Lambda}\bigr)^{2}\|\eta-\mu\|_{\Lambda},

where
L
has the following growth

L\bigl(\|u\|_{\Lambda},\|v\|_{\Lambda}\bigr)\leq C\bigl(1+\|u\|_{\Lambda}^{p^{*}-2}+\|v\|_{\Lambda}^{p^{*}-2}\bigr).

Moreover,
S_{i}^{h}
are uniformly monotone, i.e.,

\langle S_{i}^{h}\eta-S_{i}^{h}\mu,\eta-\mu\rangle\geq c\|\eta-\mu\|_{\Lambda}^{2}.

The constants
c,C,L
are independent on the choice of the spaces
V_{i}^{h},\Lambda^{h}
. The same result holds for
S^{h}
. In particular, there exists a unique solution
\eta_{h}\in\Lambda^{h}
of
eq.

18
.

The Steklov–Poincaré interpretations of the discrete versions of
1
and
2
are

\displaystyle\eta_{h}^{n+1}=\eta_{h}^{n}-s(P^{h})^{-1}S^{h}\eta_{h}^{n}\qquad\text{and}

\displaystyle\eta_{h}^{n+1}=\eta_{h}^{n}-s(P^{h}(\eta_{h}^{n}))^{-1}S^{h}\eta_{h}^{n},

respectively. Here
P^{h}
and
P^{h}(\nu)
are the discrete versions of

in

. The following convergence result follows directly from
theorem

with the choice

.
